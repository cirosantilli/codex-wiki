The three contributions to the Frank elastic energy penalize distinct slow distortions of the nematic director. Nematic splay measures local spreading or convergence and is governed by K1​(∇⋅n)2/2. Nematic twist measures rotation around an axis perpendicular to the director, with cost K2​(n⋅∇×n)2/2. Nematic bend measures curvature of director integral lines; for a unit vector, (n⋅∇)n=−n×(∇×n), giving cost K3​∣n×∇×n∣2/2. These terms respect the head-tail symmetry of a nematic liquid crystal. Positive elastic constants oppose these distortions.
For the one-dimensional reduction assume lateral uniformity, so θ and ϕ depend only on z. A prime below means d/dz. Directly differentiating the nematic director gives
∇⋅n=cosθθ′,∇×n=(−ny′​,nx′​,0),n⋅∇×n=−cos2θϕ′.
(1)
The squared curl is sin2θθ′2+cos2θϕ′2. Subtracting the squared twist invariant gives
∣n×∇×n∣2=sin2θθ′2+sin2θcos2θϕ′2.
(2)
Consequently the one-dimensional twisted nematic energy has elastic coefficients
f(θ)=K1​cos2θ+K3​sin2θ,g(θ)=K2​cos4θ+K3​sin2θcos2θ.​
(3)
A normal electric field has E⋅n=E0​sinθ. There is a genuine factor-of-two inconsistency in the source: its initial functional places the electric term inside the overall factor 1/2, whereas its subsequent density and threshold use the full electric term. For the requested density and threshold, use the usual orientational coupling −ϵE02​sin2θ/(8π). Here ϵ is the dielectric anisotropy of a nematic, in the printed Gaussian-unit convention. If the first functional is instead read literally, this coupling is halved and the resulting threshold voltage is multiplied by 2​.
Put c=ϵE02​/(8π) and L=fθ′2/2+gϕ′2/2−csin2θ. Apply the Euler-Lagrange equations for two fields. Variation in ϕ, which does not appear explicitly, gives
(g(θ)ϕ′)′=0.​
(4)
Variation in θ gives (fθ′)′−fθ​θ′2/2−gθ​ϕ′2/2+2csinθcosθ=0, or
fθ′′+21​fθ​θ′2−21​gθ​ϕ′2+4πϵE02​​sinθcosθ=0.​
(5)
At θ=0, f=K1​, g=K2​, and fθ​=gθ​=0. With zero field, θ′=0 and ϕ′=π/(2L) satisfy both equations and the imposed azimuthal endpoints. Thus the quarter-turn planar texture is a stationary solution. Stationarity alone is not a claim of stability for arbitrary elastic constants.
To find the Fréedericksz transition, impose the additional strong planar anchoring θ(0)=θ(L)=0. Write α=π/(2L). The small-angle expansions are
f=K1​+(K3​−K1​)θ2+O(θ4),g=K2​+(K3​−2K2​)θ2+O(θ4).
(6)
The conserved gϕ′ and fixed total twist imply ϕ′=α+O(θ2) for a pure tilt perturbation. Linearizing the tilt Euler-Lagrange equation therefore gives
K1​θ′′+[4πϵE02​​−(K3​−2K2​)α2]θ=0.
(7)
Substitute θ=θ0​sin(πz/L). A nonzero neutral amplitude requires
4πϵEc2​​=L2π2​[K1​+4K3​−2K2​​].
(8)
Since Vc​=Ec​L, the quarter-turn nematic instability threshold is
Vc​=2π3/2K1​+41​(K3​−2K2​)​ϵ−1/2.​
(9)
This is the first zero-endpoint mode: higher sine modes replace K1​ by n2K1​ and cost more elastic energy. The quadratic energy change is 21​∫[K1​θ′2+{(K3​−2K2​)α2−ϵE02​/(4π)}θ2]dz, so the first mode has negative stiffness above this voltage. The result assumes ϵ>0 and K1​+(K3​−2K2​)/4>0. If the latter is negative the planar state is already unstable at zero field; for negative dielectric anisotropy of a nematic, a normal field opposes this tilt. The literal initial electric prefactor would give Vc,literal​=2​Vc​.
For reflective operation of the twisted nematic field effect, orient each polarizer's transmission axis along the adjacent planar nematic director. Light entering through the top polarizer initially has y linear polarization. At low voltage, adiabatic optical following in a twisted nematic rotates it through a quarter turn to x as it travels downward, so it passes the bottom polarizer and reaches the mirror. On returning, it follows the reverse twist back to y and exits the top polarizer: the pixel appears bright. At sufficiently high voltage, positive dielectric anisotropy of a nematic aligns most of the director with the normal field. The bulk no longer produces the quarter-turn optical rotation, so the crossed bottom polarizer blocks the incident light and the pixel appears dark. Elastic relaxation restores the bright twisted state after voltage is removed. The adiabatic requirement is more precisely 2π∣Δn∣/λ≫∣ϕ′∣, involving birefringence, not thickness alone. Voltage switches director orientation and hence the reflected brightness; it does not require the liquid crystal to emit light.

Set ζ=γ/m>0 and A=A′/m. Multiplying the Langevin equation by eζt and integrating gives
u(t)=u0​e−ζt+U(t),U(t)=∫0t​e−ζ(t−s)A(s)ds.​
(10)
The expected value of U is zero. For the printed dot-product force covariance, write CA​(s−s′)=ϕ(∣s−s′∣)/m2, so ∫CA​(y)dy=τ. Without any white-noise approximation the trace variance is
⟨∣U∣2⟩=∫0t​ds∫0t​ds′e−ζ(2t−s−s′)CA​(s−s′).
(11)
The intended short-correlation approximation replaces CA​(y) by τδ(y), where δ is the Dirac delta. It requires the force correlation time to be much shorter than ζ−1 and the observation time. Then
⟨∣U∣2⟩=2ζτ​(1−e−2ζt).​
(12)
At thermal equilibrium, the equipartition theorem gives m⟨∣u∣2⟩/2=3kB​T/2. Taking the long-time limit determines the vector noise normalization in inertial Langevin dynamics:
τ=m6ζkB​T​=m26γkB​T​.​
(13)
This factor six corresponds to the dot-product covariance across three components. If ϕ were defined for one component instead, its integrated strength would be 2γkB​T before dividing by m2, and the acceleration strength would be 2ζkB​T/m.
Finite rapid correlation is not literally a Dirac delta. For a stationary force and local drag, its exact limiting velocity trace is
⟨∣u∣2⟩∞​=2ζ1​∫−∞∞​CA​(y)e−ζ∣y∣dy.
(14)
This follows by splitting the double convolution into s≥s′ and s′≥s. Thus equipartition fixes the weighted integral, not just τ, unless the short-correlation approximation is made. For example, CA​(y)=τe−∣y∣/tc​/(2tc​) gives τ/[2ζ(1+ζtc​)]. The remaining explicit formulae use the intended continuous-time Gaussian white noise limit.
Assume isotropic jointly Gaussian noise, as appropriate to an isotropic bath. The component covariance is
⟨Ai​(s)Aj​(s′)⟩=3τ​δij​δ(s−s′)=m2ζkB​T​δij​δ(s−s′).
(15)
The scalar moment identities in the question must now be applied to a fixed Cartesian component X=Ui​, not to the magnitude of the vector. Its variance is
st2​=⟨Ui2​⟩=mkB​T​(1−e−2ζt).
(16)
In the 2n-fold integral for ⟨X2n⟩, the Isserlis theorem replaces the force product by a sum of complete pairings. Each pairing yields n identical two-time integrals, hence (st2​)n. Pair the first labelled factor in 2n−1 ways, then the first unpaired factor in 2n−3 ways, and continue. The Gaussian even-moment pairing count is
(2n−1)(2n−3)⋯1=(2n−1)!!=2nn!(2n)!​.
(17)
Odd force products vanish, so
⟨Ui2n+1​⟩=0,⟨Ui2n​⟩=(2n−1)!!(st2​)n.​
(18)
For completeness, summing the scalar moment-generating function gives
⟨eaUi​⟩=∑n=0∞​(2n)!a2n​(2n−1)!!(st2​)n=ea2st2​/2.
(19)
The same Gaussian process argument for every linear combination of components gives the vector characteristic function ⟨eik⋅U⟩=e−st2​∣k∣2/2. Inverting this Fourier transform yields the normalized multivariate Gaussian distribution
W(u,t;u0​)=[2πkB​T(1−e−2ζt)m​]3/2exp[−2kB​T(1−e−2ζt)m∣u−u0​e−ζt∣2​].​
(20)
The initial condition is a velocity Dirac delta as t↓0, and the long-time limit is the thermal velocity density. The converted TeX's exponent is damaged; the PDF has the squared norm displayed here. The scalar pairing formula cannot be interpreted as a vector-norm formula: the radial moments of an isotropic Gaussian vector instead give ⟨∣U∣2n⟩=(2n+1)!!(st2​)n in three dimensions.
Integrate the velocity once more. Interchanging the time integrals gives the integrated Ornstein-Uhlenbeck displacement
r(t)=r0​+ζ1−e−ζt​u0​+ζ1​∫0t​[1−e−ζ(t−s)]A(s)ds.​
(21)
For the stated fixed initial position and velocity,
⟨r(t)⟩=r0​+ζ1−e−ζt​u0​.​
(22)
Let R=r−⟨r⟩ and D=kB​T/γ. Its component covariance comes from integrating the squared noise kernel:
⟨Ri​Rj​⟩​=δij​ζ22ζkB​T/m​∫0t​(1−e−ζv)2dv=2Dδij​[t−ζ2(1−e−ζt)​+2ζ1−e−2ζt​].​
(23)
Therefore the total centered variance is
⟨∣R∣2⟩=6D[t−ζ2(1−e−ζt)​+2ζ1−e−2ζt​].​
(24)
The mean-square displacement from r0​ additionally contains ∣u0​∣2(1−e−ζt)2/ζ2. Confusing this deterministic drift contribution with centered variance changes the short-time conclusion.
For times longer than the force correlation time but ζt≪1, the mean motion is r0​+u0​t+O(t2) and the centered trace is 2ζ(kB​T/m)t3+O(t4). Thus a nonzero fixed initial velocity produces a leading ballistic mean-square displacement ∣u0​∣2t2; conditioned on u0​=0, the white-noise displacement variance instead starts as t3. If one averages over an independent equilibrium initial velocity, its covariance adds 3(kB​T/m)(1−e−ζt)2/ζ2, giving
⟨∣r−r0​∣2⟩eq​=6D[t−ζ1−e−ζt​]∼m3kB​T​t2.
(25)
This is the usual thermal ballistic regime, before velocity memory has relaxed. At ζt≫1, the conditional mean approaches r0​+u0​/ζ, while the centered trace is 6Dt−9D/ζ+O(e−ζt). Its leading behavior is diffusion with ⟨∣R∣2⟩∼6Dt and D=kB​T/γ, as successive displacement increments lose inertial memory. At times below a finite force correlation time the white-noise short-time expansion is not applicable; for a regular force covariance and fixed initial velocity the leading noise-induced displacement variance is of order t4.

The filament bending modulus has units force times length squared. The perpendicular drag coefficient in this equation is drag per unit length divided by velocity, so its units are force times time per length squared. Hence A/(ζω) has units length to the fourth power. Balancing oscillatory viscous drag ζωh against elastic force density Ah/ℓ4 gives the elastohydrodynamic penetration length
ℓ(ω)=(ζωA​)1/4,ω>0.​
(26)
Increasing frequency confines the response nearer the actuator. The chosen coefficient one in this characteristic length is a convention; the exact decay lengths acquire the root-dependent factors found below.

The overdamped filament bending equation is linear, so sinusoidal forcing has a sinusoidal steady response at the same frequency. Write h=h0​Re[eiωtF(η)] with η=x/ℓ. Substitution gives
iωζF=−ℓ4A​F(4).
(27)
Using the elastohydrodynamic penetration length from the previous part reduces this ordinary differential equation to
F(4)+iF=0.​
(28)
The boundary conditions become F(0)=1, F′′(0)=0, and decay of F and its derivatives at infinity. The derivative condition expresses zero bending moment at the driven end, rather than a clamped slope. This separation of variables constructs the periodic response; an unspecified initial shape can add a transient solution of the homogeneous evolution problem.

Set F=ekη. The characteristic polynomial is k4=−i, whose roots have arguments −π/8, 3π/8, 7π/8, and 11π/8. Only the last two have negative real part and decay at infinity. Write c=cos(π/8) and s=sin(π/8); the admissible roots are
kℓ​=−c+is,kr​=−s−ic.​
(29)
Thus F=aℓ​ekℓ​η+ar​ekr​η. Since kr​=ikℓ​, the boundary equations are aℓ​+ar​=1 and kℓ2​(aℓ​−ar​)=0. Therefore
aℓ​=ar​=21​,F(η)=21​(e(−c+is)η+e(−s−ic)η).​
(30)
Taking real parts gives the oscillatory bending of a moment-free semi-infinite filament
h(x,t)=2h0​​[e−cx/ℓcos(ωt+sx/ℓ)+e−sx/ℓcos(ωt−cx/ℓ)].​
(31)
The first phase is constant along paths with dx/dt=−ωℓ/s, so that contribution travels left. The second has dx/dt=ωℓ/c and travels right. Both are forced, attenuating patterns in an overdamped medium; they are not undamped inertial waves. Their amplitudes are equal at the driven end, but c≃0.924>s≃0.383 gives decay lengths ℓ/c≃1.08ℓ and ℓ/s≃2.61ℓ. Hence the right-travelling contribution dominates away from the end, because it decays more slowly; its amplitude relative to the left-travelling one grows as e(c−s)x/ℓ.
The envelopes in the sketch show this amplitude comparison independently of the instantaneous phase. At particular points a cosine can vanish or the two terms can interfere, so dominance refers to envelopes and the far-field response, not to a strict pointwise inequality at every time.

Use the arc length s and the unit tangent t=∂s​r of an inextensible filament. In the high-force state choose its positive longitudinal branch. The constraint gives
tz​=1−∣t⊥​∣2​=1−21​∣t⊥​∣2+O(∣t⊥​∣4).
(32)
The extension is z=∫0L​tz​ds=L−21​∫0L​∣t⊥​∣2ds+⋯. The curvature of a space curve obeys κ2=∣∂s​t∣2. Since ∂s​tz​=−t⊥​⋅∂s​t⊥​+⋯ is second order in small fluctuations, its squared contribution is fourth order. Thus the worm-like chain energy through quadratic order is
E=−fL+21​∫0L​[A∣∂s​t⊥​∣2+f∣t⊥​∣2]ds.​
(33)
Positive force suppresses transverse orientation fluctuations, while the filament bending modulus penalizes their spatial variation.
To apply the equipartition theorem without missing either component or a mode-counting factor, expand each component in a real orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions of −∂s2​, tα​(s)=∑q​aαq​wq​(s), with ∫wq​wq′​ds=δqq′​ and α=x,y. The quadratic energy is 21​∑α,q​(Aq2+f)aαq2​. Each real quadratic degree of freedom contributes kB​T/2, so
⟨aαq​aβq′​⟩=Aq2+fkB​T​δαβ​δqq′​.
(34)
For a chain long compared with the force-dependent correlation length, the interior is translation invariant and the mode sum per length becomes ∫dq/(2π). Equivalently, for the Fourier transform tα​(q)=∫e−iqstα​(s)ds,
⟨tα​(q)tβ​(q′)⟩=2πδ(q+q′)δαβ​Aq2+fkB​T​.
(35)
The negative and positive wavenumbers here represent the real-field mode count consistently, rather than two independent complex modes. Summing both transverse components gives the high-force worm-like chain elasticity
⟨∣t⊥​∣2⟩=2kB​T∫−∞∞​2πdq​Aq2+f1​=Af​kB​T​,
(36)
where the integral follows by setting q=f/A​u and using ∫−∞∞​(1+u2)−1du=π. Therefore
L⟨z⟩​=1−2Af​kB​T​=1−4Lp​fkB​T​​,Lp​=kB​TA​.​
(37)
Here Lp​ is the three-dimensional zero-force persistence length. The result requires fLp​≫kB​T, negligible backbone stretching, and a long-chain bulk approximation. It does not describe the eventual bond-stretching regime. For a continuum polymer description, the force-dependent length should also exceed the microscopic cutoff.
For comparison, a three-dimensional freely jointed chain has contour length L=Nb and independent link orientations. At force f, a link of polar angle ϑ has Boltzmann factor excosϑ with x=fb/(kB​T). Its angular canonical partition function is
Z1​=2π∫−11​exudu=4πxsinhx​.
(38)
The total canonical partition function is Z1N​. Differentiating with respect to force gives the force-extension of a three-dimensional freely jointed chain:
Nb⟨z⟩​=dxdlogZ1​​=cothx−x1​.
(39)
Consequently
1−L⟨z⟩​∼fbkB​T​for a freely jointed chain,1−L⟨z⟩​∼2Af​kB​T​for a worm-like chain.​
(40)
The extension deficits scale as f−1 and f−1/2 respectively. Inverting them gives force divergences proportional to the first and second inverse powers of the deficit. Freely jointed link rotations cost no bending energy; the worm-like chain instead has a continuum of coupled bending modes whose contributing wavelengths change with force. Setting a long-wavelength coarse-grained link length b=2Lp​ can match weak-force statistics, but does not make these high-force laws identical.
For the transverse tangent correlation of a stretched worm-like chain, use a separation r consistently: the source writes C(y) but integrates a separation denoted r. In the translation-invariant bulk the spatial average is redundant and
C(r)=⟨t⊥​(s)⋅t⊥​(s+r)⟩=2kB​T∫−∞∞​2πdq​Aq2+feiqr​.
(41)
To evaluate the integral without an assumed decay law, let G(r) be the inverse Fourier transform of (Aq2+f)−1. Then (−A∂r2​+f)G=δ(r). Away from zero, decay at both infinities requires G=Be−∣r∣/ξ with ξ=A/f​. Integrating across zero yields −A[G′(0+)−G′(0−)]=1, so B=1/(2Af​). Thus
C(r)=Af​kB​T​e−∣r∣/ξ,ξ=fA​​=fkB​TLp​​​.​
(42)
At r=0 this agrees with the two-component equipartition theorem calculation. The correlation length decreases with force and is distinct from the zero-force persistence length. The full tangent correlation has a nonzero aligned background; the exponentially decaying quantity here is specifically the transverse correlation.
For a finite open chain, the source's integral samples s+r beyond L and supplies no endpoint tangent conditions. An exact finite-length correlation is therefore not uniquely specified. The result above is the intended bulk form when L≫ξ. As one explicit finite convention, periodically extending the tangent fluctuations gives the periodic finite-length tangent correlation of a stretched worm-like chain
CL​(r)=∑j∈Z​C(r+jL)=Af​kB​T​sinh[L/(2ξ)]cosh[(L/2−dL​(r))/ξ]​,
(43)
where dL​(r) is the distance from zero around the periodic interval. Then CL​(0)=(kB​T/Af​)coth[L/(2ξ)], and the extension formula acquires this same finite-length factor. The bulk law is recovered as L/ξ→∞. This makes the endpoint assumption explicit rather than claiming an unspecified finite-chain correlation is exactly translationally invariant.
