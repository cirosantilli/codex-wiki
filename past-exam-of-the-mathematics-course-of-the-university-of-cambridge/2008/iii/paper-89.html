In seawater, freezing produces nearly salt-free solid and leaves salt in the interstitial liquid. The growing cover is therefore a mushy layer, with sea-ice crystal fabric determined by both crystal growth and the mechanical history. Crystallographic c-axes are perpendicular to the plates' basal planes; grain elongation and c-axis orientation must not be confused.
For first-year sea ice growing by congelation, heat loss first brings the surface water to its salinity-dependent freezing point. Small frazil ice crystals may form and collect into a thin continuous sheet, or a quiet surface can freeze directly; the resulting nilas provides the initial cover. Once the water surface is protected from strong agitation, heat transported by heat conduction upward through the sheet permits freezing onto its underside. Competitive growth favors grains with fast basal-plane growth directed downward, so grains with approximately horizontal c-axes crowd out less favorably oriented grains. The mature congelation ice consists predominantly of long vertical columns beneath a finer initial surface layer. Within and between these grains, the advancing cellular interface traps brine-bearing spaces. The growth sequence and crystallographic selection explain why the resulting texture is columnar rather than a random aggregate of floating crystals.
For pancake ice, wind and surface gravity waves prevent an uninterrupted ice sheet. Supercooled surface water produces suspended frazil ice; crystals accumulate into grease ice and then small coherent cakes. Wave-induced convergence, collisions and repeated accumulation of frazil increase cake size and thickness. Collisions and overwash deposit slush around the margins, building the characteristic raised rims. The crystal aggregate is predominantly granular, with much less systematic orientation than in congelation ice. A calmer interval can add a columnar basal layer, so a pancake need not be purely granular throughout. Observed pancake structures include frazil layers, basal congelation growth and rafted cakes.
In Antarctic consolidated pancake ice, the active edge initially follows this frazil-pancake route. As concentration rises and waves are attenuated, pancakes jam, raft and become frozen together, with interstitial frazil filling the gaps. The consolidated sheet retains overlapping cake outlines, stacked granular layers and an irregular underside, rather than acquiring the simple fabric of a single quiet-water-grown sheet. Later basal freezing can add congelation ice, but the granular fraction often remains large. Snow loading or overwash can flood the top; freezing this slush adds granular snow ice, a separate contribution from seawater-derived frazil. These mechanisms produce the heterogeneous sea-ice crystal fabric documented in cores from the Antarctic marginal ice zone. Quiet basal growth produces columnar ice; wave-driven aggregation and consolidation produce predominantly granular, layered ice.
The main efficient net desalination mechanisms require transport through the ice boundary. In gravity drainage of sea-ice brine, cold saline liquid is denser than the water below, so a connected permeable pore network permits downward flow and compensating entry of less saline seawater. Dense brine can collect into channels and convective drainage pathways. For a layer of depth H, a Darcy law estimate of drainage speed is uD​∼KΔρg/μ, giving a drainage time H/uD​ and showing directly why permeability of a porous medium and buoyancy matter. Warm, brine-rich ice drains much more readily than cold ice whose pores have become poorly connected. This is important during growth and can intensify as warming reconnects channels; an isolated sealed brine pocket cannot drain merely because its liquid is dense. The connectivity constraint is supported by sea-ice permeability measurements.
In meltwater flushing of sea ice, water supplied by surface snow melt or melt ponds has a hydraulic head above sea level and percolates down connected pores and cracks, displacing brine. It can remove salt rapidly in warm summer ice. Its limits are the supply and head of meltwater and pore connectivity; lateral runoff may reduce the amount available for vertical flushing. Gravity-driven drainage can act without surface melting, whereas flushing cannot.
Brine expulsion occurs when additional freezing and solid expansion pressurize an inclusion and force liquid into nearby passages. This is often internal redistribution: expelling brine from one inclusion does not desalinate the entire floe unless that liquid reaches the ocean. Migration of brine inclusions involves melting on a warmer wall and refreezing on the colder wall; molecular salt diffusion through liquid connections is also possible. These slow processes are far less effective at exporting bulk salt than connected advective drainage. Diffusion through the ice lattice itself is extremely slow. Likewise, excluding salt from each growing crystal is not by itself proof of salt loss from a whole ice-brine mixture. Desalination experiments and transport calculations distinguish net salt export from internal redistribution. Gravity drainage dominates desalination of permeable growing ice; meltwater flushing can dominate summer desalination; expulsion, inclusion migration and diffusion are comparatively ineffective without a boundary-crossing flow.

Let hi​,hs​ denote ice and snow thickness, let z increase downwards, and count external atmospheric fluxes as positive into the surface. The Maykut-Untersteiner model is a time-dependent column model, rather than a single thickness formula: heat conduction, heat storage, internal absorption of sunlight and moving freezing/melting boundaries must be solved together. In an ice-following description its energy equation can be written
ρi​DtDei​(T,S)​=∂z​(ki​(T,S)∂z​T)−∂z​I(z,t),ρs​cs​DtDTs​​=∂z​(ks​∂z​Ts​),
(1)
where ei​ is specific enthalpy, S is the prescribed ice salinity, and I is the downward penetrating shortwave flux. The material derivative includes coordinate motion if a moving numerical grid is used. A common attenuation form is I=I0​e−κz within a homogeneous ice layer, with the origin taken at that layer's top. The corresponding source is κI. Snow-covered cases have much less penetration; absorbed surface radiation must not also be counted as internal heating.
The liquid brine fraction contributes latent heat to the effective specific heat capacity: for the illustrative liquidus approximation ϕb​≃−μS/T with Celsius T<0 in the mixed-phase range 0<ϕb​<1, ceff​=∂T​ei​≃c0​+Lf​μS/T2. The precise ice conductivity and heat capacity are salinity- and temperature-dependent. This is why the full transient temperature profile need not be linear. At the snow-ice interface impose continuity of temperature and conductive heat flux; at the ice bottom impose the seawater freezing temperature Tf​.
Write I0​=i0​(1−α)FSW​ and define the atmospheric input absorbed at the surface by
QA​=(1−i0​)(1−α)FSW​+FLW,↓​−εσTsurf,K4​+FH​+FE​.
(2)
Here α is the surface albedo, σ is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant, and FH​,FE​ are signed sensible and latent turbulent heat fluxes. With upward conductive flux qc​=k∂z​T, the cold, nonmelting surface satisfies QA​+qc,s​=0. Once the surface reaches its melting temperature, constrain it there and use excess energy for ablation:
Ls​m˙s​=QA​+qc,s​≥0.
(3)
The mass-loss rate m˙s​ first removes snow at thickness rate m˙s​/ρs​; after the snow is gone it removes ice at rate mi​=m˙s​/ρi​. Snow thickness also changes through prescribed snowfall and any separately represented compaction or sublimation.
At the bottom, the Stefan condition equates latent heat released by growth to upward conductive removal minus upward ocean heat flux Fo​:
ρi​Lb​gb​=qc,b​−Fo​,h˙i​=gb​−mi​.​
(4)
Here gb​ is positive for basal freezing and negative for basal melting, and Lb​ is the appropriate effective latent heat. These equations, the incident fluxes and snowfall determine the seasonal thickness trajectory. Annual sea-ice thermodynamic equilibrium means a periodic temperature field and hi​(t+T)=hi​(t), so
∫0T​(gb​−mi​)dt=0.​
(5)
With constant Lb​, this is equivalently ∫(qc,b​−Fo​−ρi​Lb​mi​)dt=0. An “equilibrium thickness” must refer to the mean or a specified phase of this seasonal cycle.
A useful winter reduction neglects heat storage and solar penetration and treats each conductivity as constant. The two layers act as thermal resistances in series:
qc​≃hi​/ki​+hs​/ks​Tf​−Tsurf​​,ρi​Lb​h˙i​≃hi​/ki​+hs​/ks​Tf​−Tsurf​​−Fo​.​
(6)
This displays the dependence on snow and ice thickness, but is not a replacement for the full seasonally forced Maykut-Untersteiner model.
Increasing ocean heat flux subtracts directly from basal growth and increases bottom ablation. On a stable perennial branch it lowers annual equilibrium thickness, and sufficiently large input can eliminate a year-round ice cover. With zero oceanic input, removing this heat sink can allow much thicker thermodynamic ice; it does not remove the seasonal surface energy balance.
Additional annual snowfall has competing effects. Larger hs​/ks​ suppresses winter heat conduction and basal growth, favoring thinner ice. But persistent bright snow raises albedo, consumes energy when it melts and delays exposure of dark bare ice, suppressing summer ice ablation. The original column calculations show near compensation for moderate snowfall and substantial thickening when much more snow survives into summer. Thus annual equilibrium thickness need not decrease with snowfall, even though greater snow thickness at fixed winter temperatures always reduces the conductive growth rate. The balance also depends on snowfall timing and the treatment of snow-to-ice conversion. These seasonal responses are illustrated by the original model calculations.
Reducing surface albedo increases absorbed shortwave radiation. It promotes surface ablation, internal heating and, where sunlight reaches the ocean, basal ablation; the ice-albedo feedback reinforces the reduction in equilibrium ice thickness. Increasing albedo has the opposite effect. This sensitivity is strongest when sunlight is available and dark surfaces are exposed, rather than during polar night.
For the observed bottom melt, use ρi​≃917 kgm−3, Lf​≃3.34×105 Jkg−1 and approximately 122 days. The basal melt heat flux equivalent is
Fmelt​=Δtρi​Lf​Δh​≃122(86400)917(3.34×105)(2)​≃58 Wm−2≈60 Wm−2.​
(7)
The integrated latent heat is about 6.1×108 Jm−2. This is energy used for melting, not automatically the complete ocean-to-ice heat input: the basal balance gives Fo​=ρi​Lb​(−gb​)+qc,b​, with sensible heat and salinity corrections if needed.
A likely major source is solar heating of an unusually large open-water fraction. Dark water absorbs much more sunlight than snow-covered ice, and warm water transfers that stored heat to floe bottoms by mixing and lateral transport. Thin or ponded ice also transmits more sunlight. Wind-driven divergence and export expose additional water, while advection of warm Pacific or Atlantic water and mixing of subsurface heat can contribute regionally. The supplied melt amount alone cannot partition these sources. The 2007 Beaufort Sea heat-budget analysis found that locally absorbed solar heat was sufficient to account for the enhanced basal melt. The latent-heat equivalent is about 60 Wm−2; increased solar absorption by exposed ocean is a well-supported principal explanation.

Define g(x,h,t)dh as the fraction of horizontal ocean area covered by sea ice of thickness in [h,h+dh]. Allow a Dirac delta mass at h=0 for open water, so the whole-area distribution has ∫gdh=1; its first moment H=∫hgdh is ice volume per ocean area, rather than mean thickness conditional on ice being present. Let f=dh/dt be thermodynamic growth, v the horizontal ice velocity and ψ the mechanical redistribution of sea ice.
Conservation in a horizontal cell and a thickness interval gives horizontal flux vg and thickness-space flux fg. For h1​<h2​, their contribution is −∇⋅∫h1​h2​​vgdh−[fg]h1​h2​​; adding ∫h1​h2​​ψdh and shrinking the interval yields
∂t∂g​+∇⋅(vg)+∂h∂(fg)​=ψ.​
(8)
This is the sea-ice thickness distribution equation. Its distributional formulation must include creation and removal of open water when characteristics reach or leave h=0. On a positive-thickness smooth portion it is equivalently
DtDg​+f∂h​g=−g(∇⋅v+∂h​f)+ψ.
(9)
If a nonconservative form uses a redistribution function ψ​, then ψ​=ψ−g∇⋅v; the two definitions must not be interchanged.
With the open-water boundary flux accounted for consistently, whole-area normalization requires ∫ψdh=∇⋅v. Mechanical rearrangement preserves ice volume locally, so ∫hψdh=0. The volume moment consequently obeys
∂t​H+∇⋅(vH)=∫fgdh,​
(10)
where formation of new sea ice from the open-water category is included in the thermal term. Compression raises local volume per area through convergence, even though ridging does not create ice mass.
The three processes have distinct effects on the curve. Pure advection imports the distributions found upstream, so a fixed location may acquire a different mode or thick tail. Along an incompressible material trajectory, advection alone does not alter its distribution. Convergence and divergence also change area fractions through the horizontal flux, with the redistribution and open-water terms ensuring normalization.
Thermodynamic growth moves probability along thickness characteristics h˙=f(h,t), with g(h,t)dh=g(h0​,0)dh0​ when mechanics and horizontal divergence vanish. For example, winter conductive growth f=κ/h gives h2=h02​+2κt and
g(h,t)=g0​(h2−2κt​)h2−2κt​h​.
(11)
Thin ice grows faster, so the mode moves to larger thickness and neighboring thin-ice characteristics converge, sharpening the level-ice peak. Summer ablation moves classes left and creates open water when ice disappears; if melt rate depends on thickness, the shape changes as well.
Mechanical redistribution of sea ice removes area from thin or level ice and populates thick rafted or classes containing sea-ice pressure ridges, broadening the upper tail. It can also create sea-ice leads. A simple volume-conserving example, stacking a fraction r of an initial distribution into double-thickness ice, is
gmech​(h)=(1−r)g0​(h)+4r​g0​(h/2)+2r​δ0​(h).
(12)
The stacked component has area r/2 and unchanged volume r∫hg0​(h)dh; the freed area r/2 becomes open water. Thus mechanical thickening is not merely a translation of the entire curve.
The figure uses illustrative distributions, not measured data. The advection panel compares local ice with incoming thicker ice; the growth panel applies the thickness-characteristic formula; the mechanical panel uses r=0.4 and shows its open-water mass separately from the continuous density.
For the thick tail, take the common-shape sea-ice pressure ridge idealization to mean triangular profile sections with a common effective side slope s=tanδ, while peak thicknesses vary. Let n(H)dH count sea-ice pressure ridge peaks per track length with maximum thickness in [H,H+dH]. Above a level-ice baseline h0​, a sea-ice pressure ridge with H≥h contributes two flank lengths dh/s at thickness h; it contributes nothing at h>H. For a representative transect, summing these lengths gives the triangular ridge occupation identity
gtail​(h)=s2​∫h∞​n(H)dH.
(13)
Suppose n(H)=Be−λH for H≥h0​. Then
gtail​(h)=Ae−λh,A=sλ2B​,tanδ=Aλ2B​.​
(14)
Thus an exponential ridge-draft model, expressed in the same vertical coordinate as the profile, generates an exponential occupation tail with the same decay parameter. The peak-count distribution and the area-weighted thickness distribution are different distributions, so their amplitudes carry essential information.
If μ=∫h0​∞​n(H)dH is ridge line density and p=∫h0​∞​gtail​(h)dh is the tail's area fraction, the same result becomes
λ=⟨H⟩−h0​1​,tanδ=λp2μ​.​
(15)
The decay rate alone does not fix slope: sea-ice pressure ridge frequency or tail amplitude is also needed. If side slopes vary independently of peak height, replace 1/s by ⟨1/s⟩, giving an effective harmonic-mean slope. Measured along-track slopes differ from slopes normal to a ridge if the transect is oblique. Overlap, irregular shape and porosity limit this simple geometric model. Literally identical complete ridge cross-sections would have one height, so “common shape” here necessarily permits different ridge sizes.
Sea-ice pressure ridge spacings are positive and broadly distributed. A spatial Poisson process gives an exponential distribution of gaps, a useful simple approximation. Observed sea-ice pressure ridge spacings, however, often fit a shifted lognormal distribution better, with few very small spacings because of ridge geometry, identification rules and finite beam width. This differs from the successful exponential model for peak drafts. Observed ridge-spacing and draft distributions establish this distinction.
Sea-ice lead-width distributions contain very many narrow openings and a broad tail. A power law often describes widths over a resolved range better than one negative exponential; its exponents and apparent cutoffs depend on the lead definition, resolution and count-versus-area weighting. Sea-ice lead spacings may be approximately exponential over an intermediate range, but clustering produces excess close pairs and large gaps, so there is no universal independent-spacing law. Both behaviors were found in Greenland Sea and Eurasian Basin lead statistics. Advection imports thickness classes, thermodynamics transports them in h, and mechanics exchanges thin-ice area for ridged ice and leads; common-slope exponential ridge populations reproduce the exponential thick tail.

Let m=ρi​hi​+ρs​hs​ be the ice-plus-snow mass per horizontal area, let v be its velocity, let η be sea-surface elevation and let Σ be the depth-integrated internal stress tensor, with units Nm−1. With upward unit vector z^ and signed Coriolis parameter f, the sea-ice drift momentum balance per unit area is
m(DtDv​+fz^×v)=τa​+τw​+∇⋅Σ−mg∇η.​
(16)
The last term is the horizontal hydrostatic pressure force associated with sea-surface tilt. In components,
m(Dt​vx​−fvy​)m(Dt​vy​+fvx​)​=τa,x​+τw,x​+∂x​Σxx​+∂y​Σxy​−mg∂x​η,=τa,y​+τw,y​+∂x​Σyx​+∂y​Σyy​−mg∂y​η.​
(17)
The material derivative is Dt​=∂t​+v⋅∇. Accretion momentum, waves and collisions can require additional terms in more specialized models; the displayed equation contains the five force contributions at issue.
Use scalar quadratic drag laws
τa​=ρa​Ca​∣U−v∣(U−v),τw​=ρw​Cw​∣uw​−v∣(uw​−v).
(18)
Here U is the surface wind velocity and uw​ is water velocity. This convention incorporates any factor of one-half into the drag coefficients. Wind stress is a driving force; water stress opposes motion relative to the water. The Coriolis force is perpendicular to ice velocity and does no work. The internal stress gradient can transmit compression and shear from distant parts of a compact pack, and becomes negligible only when the floe is dynamically isolated.
For an order-of-magnitude comparison, take hi​∼2 m, m∼1800 kgm−2, U∼10 ms−1, relative water speed 0.15--0.20 ms−1, ∣f∣∼1.4×10−4 s−1, ρa​∼1.3, ρw​∼1025 kgm−3, Ca​∼1.3×10−3 and Cw​∼5×10−3. Then wind stress is about 0.17 Nm−2, water stress about 0.12--0.21 Nm−2, and the Coriolis force about 0.04--0.05 Nm−2. Sea-surface slope ∣∇η∣∼10−6--10−5 gives mg∣∇η∣∼0.02--0.2 Nm−2. Internal stress of order 104--105 Nm−1 varying over 100 km gives 0.1--1 Nm−2. Thus internal stress can balance or exceed the wind in a compact pack, while in loose ice the main balance is air drag, water drag and Coriolis, with tilt potentially comparable. These are regime estimates, not a universal ranking.
An acceleration of 0.2 ms−1 over a day gives only about 0.004 Nm−2, explaining the usefulness of a steady force balance over long periods. Over an hour the same change gives 0.1 Nm−2, so acceleration and inertial oscillations can matter after rapid forcing changes. If water is approximately geostrophic, fz^×uw​=−g∇η; the tilt term then combines with Coriolis into mfz^×(v−uw​) in the steady relative-motion equation, rather than constituting a negligible independent correction.
For the requested isolated-floe equilibrium, set internal stress to zero, take stationary water and a level sea surface, and initially neglect the floe velocity in air drag, since ∣v∣≪U. Put the wind along +x and define
F=ρa​Ca​U2,B=ρw​Cw​,C=mf,q=∣v∣,Jv=z^×v.
(19)
Steady sea-ice free drift satisfies Fx^=Bqv+CJv. Taking the squared magnitude eliminates the direction:
F2=B2q4+C2q2,q=[2B2C4+4B2F2​−C2​]1/2.​
(20)
A numerically equivalent expression is q2=2F2/[C2+C4+4B2F2​]. Inverting the two-by-two force relation gives
vx​=B2q2+C2FBq​,vy​=−B2q2+C2FC​,θ=arctan(ρw​Cw​qmf​).​
(21)
Here θ is the signed turning angle to the right of the wind. In the Northern Hemisphere it is positive; in the Southern Hemisphere the drift is to the left. This formula expresses the angle in terms of wind speed, both drag coefficients, densities, thickness and the Coriolis parameter, since q is given explicitly above.
When water drag dominates, q≃Uρa​Ca​/(ρw​Cw​)​ and θ is small. When Coriolis dominates, q≃F/∣mf∣ and the direction approaches a right angle to the wind. Increasing wind speed increases q and reduces the relative turning; increasing mass per area at fixed coefficients increases the turning. For the numerical example above, the formula gives q≃0.178 ms−1 and θ≃15.4∘ to the right, showing the familiar order of a few percent of wind speed and an appreciable deflection. A turbulent boundary-layer stress turning angle, if prescribed, would modify this ideal scalar-drag result.
For completeness, retain the ice velocity in the air stress. Set A=ρa​Ca​ and w=∣U−v∣. The exact steady scalar-drag equation becomes
(Aw+Bq)v+CJv=AwU.
(22)
Consequently
tanθ=Aw+BqC​,q2[(Aw+Bq)2+C2]=A2w2U2,w2=U2+q2−2Uqcosθ.​
(23)
These relations determine q,w and the turning angle without the small-ice-speed approximation. The explicit formula follows by neglecting Awv and replacing w by U in the wind forcing; the correction is small when both q/U and AU/(Bq) are small.
An iceberg has much larger mass per horizontal area than a thin ice floe. In this same flat-water idealization, holding the effective drag factors fixed, increasing m reduces the wind-driven speed and turns the trajectory further across the wind, approaching 90∘ in the strongly Coriolis-dominated limit. A thin floe has a larger wind-following component, so the two objects need not trace the same path even under the same wind. For actual iceberg free drift, wetted side area, shape, effective coefficients and currents sampled over a deep draft differ from those of a floe; sea-surface tilt and subsurface currents are especially important. Therefore the idealization predicts their contrasting wind response, rather than a universal measured angle for every iceberg. Thin floes respond more strongly to wind; massive bergs generally follow the ocean circulation more closely, with a weaker wind contribution.
