A phase diagram locates stable pure thermodynamic phases and their phase coexistence boundaries in a space of control parameters. For a concrete three-dimensional example, take the temperature, crystal-field parameter, and magnetic field of the Blume–Capel model. Its spins take values 0,±1; changing the cost of the zero-spin state can turn a continuous ordering transition into a discontinuous one. A local scalar description near the resulting tricritical point is the Landau-Ginzburg theory
F[M]=∫dDx[2K​∣∇M∣2+V(M)],V(M)=2r​M2+4u​M4+6v​M6−hM,K,v>0.
(1)
The three local control coordinates are (r,u,h), with r varying linearly with the thermal scaling field. For u>0, h=0, the transition at r=0 is continuous. For u<0, zero-field coexistence is at r=3u2/(16v), and the magnetization jumps between zero and ±−3u/(4v)​. The two loci meet at r=u=h=0, the tricritical point.
The full three-dimensional phase diagram has an ordered coexistence sheet at h=0, where the two opposite magnetizations exchange stability when h changes sign. On its three-phase line it meets two nonzero-field tricritical wings. These wings are first-order phase transition surfaces between minima of the same sign but different magnitude, terminating at ordinary thermodynamic critical points. Their tricritical wing critical edges are found by V′=V′′=V′′′=0:
Mc2​=−10v3u​,rc​=20v9u2​,hc​=25v6u2Mc​​(u<0).
(2)
Indeed V′′′=6uM+20vM3 fixes Mc2​, and substituting into V′′=0 and V′=0 gives rc​,hc​; V′′′′=−12u>0 ensures a stable critical quartic minimum. The two signs give the two wings. All transition surfaces and critical edges end together at the tricritical point. The following plot uses v=1; its wing surfaces come from the exact tricritical wing coexistence factorization, rather than schematic unconstrained surfaces.
The unheaded requests for critical exponents follow from the uniform equation of state
h=rM+uM3+vM5.
(3)
For an ordinary thermodynamic critical point, u>0 is fixed and r=At, A>0. At h=0, t<0, the small ordered minimum obeys M2∼−r/u, so the order-parameter critical exponent is β=1/2. Above the transition χ=(∂M/∂h)h=0​=1/r, giving the magnetic-susceptibility critical exponent γ=1. At r=0, h∼uM3, so the critical-isotherm exponent is δ=3. At the tuned tricritical point, u=0, instead M4=−r/v below the transition and h=vM5 on its critical isotherm, while the disordered susceptibility remains 1/r. Therefore
(β,γ,δ)ordinary​=(1/2,1,3),(β,γ,δ)tricritical​=(1/4,1,5).​
(4)
Below the transition the inverse susceptibility is r+3uM2+5vM4, giving 2∣r∣ in the ordinary limit and 4∣r∣ in the tricritical limit. Thus both sides give the same susceptibility power, with different amplitudes.
To derive the general Ginzburg criterion, tune away all lower even nonlinearities and let the leading stabilizer be vn​M2n/(2n), n≥2. The equation of state gives M02​≍∣r∣1/(n−1), and its curvature at the ordered saddle is 2(n−1)∣r∣, so ξ≍∣r∣−1/2. The fluctuations of the order parameter averaged over a correlation volume are estimated by its long-wavelength quadratic covariance:
⟨(δM)2⟩ξ​≍kB​T∫∣q∣≲ξ−1​(2π)DdDq​Kq2+c∣r∣1​≍ξ2−D.
(5)
The integral has a fixed dimensionless upper limit after q=p/ξ; this is a coarse-volume fluctuation estimate, not the ultraviolet-dominated point variance. Consequently the multicritical Ginzburg ratio is
M02​⟨(δM)2⟩ξ​​≍∣r∣(D−2)/2−1/(n−1).
(6)
The Landau approximation is asymptotically controlled by this test only when the ratio tends to zero. This gives the upper critical dimension
Dc​=n−12n​;Dc​=4 (n=2),Dc​=3 (n=3).​
(7)
The same threshold follows by giving M its Gaussian engineering dimension (D−2)/2: the coupling to M2n has scaling exponent D−n(D−2)=2n−(n−1)D, which becomes relevant below Dc​.
For D<Dc​, the fluctuation ratio diverges and the saddle calculation cannot predict the generic interacting critical exponents. At D=Dc​ the test is marginal, not a proof of changed power exponents: the ratio has zero thermal power and marginal interactions can produce logarithmic corrections. Thus the PDF's inclusion of equality in a blanket claim about wrong power exponents needs this qualification. The Landau-Ginzburg theory as a fluctuating field theory remains useful; what loses validity is its fluctuation-free Landau approximation.

An order parameter distinguishes phases by an equilibrium expectation value. For an Ising model, the local field M(x) is the coarse-grained magnetization, and the uniform value is M=N−1⟨∑r​σr​⟩. The spin inversion symmetry maps M to −M, so at zero field the Landau free energy contains even powers. Its disordered minimum is M=0; ordered pure thermodynamic phases have M=±M0​. Their sign is selected by taking the thermodynamic limit before sending h to zero. A symmetric mixture can have zero average without being a single disordered phase. The term −hM identifies h as the field conjugate to an order parameter.

A first-order phase transition exchanges distinct global minima of the Landau free energy; the order parameter jumps and a thermal crossing generically has latent heat. A continuous phase transition has minima merging continuously and a diverging correlation length. A local loss of stability at a spinodal point is not the equilibrium coexistence condition.
For the sextic even Landau potential, write y=M2. At a nonzero zero-field stationary point, r+uy+vy2=0. To coexist with M=0, also require ry/2+uy2/4+vy3/6=0. Eliminate r to obtain y=−3u/(4v) and r=3u2/(16v), which requires u<0. On this locus
V(M)=6v​M2(M2+4v3u​)2≥0,
(9)
proving that all three equal minima are globally stable. Their curvatures are 3u2/(16v) at zero and 3u2/(4v) at the nonzero minima, so the correlation length is finite when u<0 is fixed. For u>0, by contrast, the nonzero minimum approaches zero as r↑0, giving the ordinary continuous transition. At fixed u>0 and r<0, crossing h=0 exchanges the two ordered minima discontinuously even though the zero-field thermal transition at r=0 is continuous.

d OurBigBook.com
Universality classes identify systems with the same long-distance renormalization-group fixed point. Spatial dimension, order parameter symmetry and number of components, and the range of interactions help determine the equilibrium class. Microscopic lattice details and many local interactions become irrelevant operators under coarse-graining. Universal quantities include critical exponents, scaling functions after fixing metric normalizations, and suitable amplitude ratios. The critical temperature, bare couplings, absolute correlation-length and susceptibility amplitudes, analytic free-energy density backgrounds, and microscopic length scales are generally not universal. Dynamic universality can additionally depend on conservation laws, so it should not be inferred solely from a static scalar potential.

A critical exponent describes the leading singular power of an observable in a specified scaling limit. The order-parameter critical exponent describes the zero-field ordered branch; the magnetic-susceptibility critical exponent describes response to its conjugate field; and the critical-isotherm exponent describes response at the critical temperature. They are found in the Landau approximation by minimizing the potential and differentiating the resulting equation of state, as calculated in the question's root solution. For a leading M2n stabilizer the same calculation gives M≍(−r)1/(2n−2), χ≍r−1 and M≍h1/(2n−1). Thus β=1/(2n−2), γ=1 and δ=2n−1. Beyond the Landau approximation, the renormalization-group relevance exponents and anomalous dimension determine the singular powers; Question 2 derives their relations. The critical exponents refer to specified paths, and tuning to a higher multicritical point requires eliminating its lower relevant perturbations.

At a tricritical point, both r and u vanish, while v>0 stabilizes the Landau free energy. It separates ordinary continuous transitions for u>0 from first-order coexistence for u<0. The zero-field order-parameter jump shrinks to zero as u↑0, and the tricritical wings terminate there in the full (r,u,h) diagram. For r<0, u=0, minimizing gives M4=−r/v and
V(M0​)=−3v​∣r∣3/2​.
(10)
Two thermal derivatives give the heat-capacity critical exponent α=1/2, compared with the finite mean-field heat-capacity jump, α=0, at an ordinary quartic transition. The remaining tricritical mean-field critical exponents are (β,γ,δ)=(1/4,1,5), and the mean-field correlation-length critical exponent is ν=1/2. To observe tricritical scaling one must tune the quartic scaling field as well as the thermal field: a generic path with fixed u>0 instead has ordinary critical behavior. Its upper critical dimension is three, as the multicritical Ginzburg ratio shows.

Use βth​=1/(kB​T) for inverse temperature, to distinguish it from the order-parameter exponent. In a translation-invariant pure thermodynamic phase, the connected correlation function is
G(r)=⟨σr​σ0​⟩−⟨σr​⟩⟨σ0​⟩.
(11)
The subtraction is essential below the transition. Its exponential correlation length is defined by ξ−1=−limr→∞​r−1log∣G(r)∣, with direction specified if the lattice is anisotropic. Away from criticality, G(r) has a tail proportional to e−r/ξ times an algebraic prefactor. In the massive continuum scalar approximation that prefactor is r−(D−1)/2; at criticality the exponential cutoff disappears and G(r)≍r−(D−2+η) instead. The large-distance limit here is r≫ξ, as the PDF shows.
Let Ftot​=−βth−1​logZ. With the energy convention −h∑r​σr​, the magnetization per site and magnetic susceptibility are
M=−N1​∂h∂Ftot​​,χ=∂h∂M​=−N1​∂h2∂2Ftot​​.
(12)
Writing S=∑r​σr​, direct differentiation of the partition function gives ∂h​⟨S⟩=βth​(⟨S2⟩−⟨S⟩2). Thus the correlation-function susceptibility sum rule is
χ=Nβth​​r,r′∑​⟨σr​σr′​⟩c​=βth​r∑​G(r).​
(13)
In physical continuum coordinates the lattice sum becomes a−D∫dDrG(r), so omitting the site-density factor would change the normalization. A derivative with respect to the dimensionless source βth​h instead absorbs the explicit inverse-temperature factor.
An exact renormalization-group transformation with length factor b>1 can be defined by a nonnegative normalized blocking kernel Kb​(σ′,σ) satisfying ∑σ′​Kb​(σ′,σ)=1. A deterministic majority rule is one example; randomized tie-breaking preserves its normalization. Define the blocked energy, including its constant, by
e−βth​H(u′,σ′)−βth​N′C′=∑σ​Kb​(σ′,σ)e−βth​H(u,σ)−βth​NC,N′=N/bD.
(14)
Summing over σ′ proves Z(u′,C′,N′)=Z(u,C,N). All generated operators must be retained for this identity to remain exact; a finite-coupling truncation is an approximation. After p iterations, Np​=N/bpD and the spacing is ap​=bpa. Long-distance physics is preserved because this is a marginalization of microscopic probabilities, with observables and sources transformed along with the energy. It integrates short-distance variables rather than changing their effects on retained observables. Physical lengths remain fixed even though the correlation length in units of the new spacing is reduced by bp.
For clarity, now use dimensionless free energy per site F(u,C)=−N−1logZ=f(u)+βth​C. Exact equality of the partition functions gives
F(u0​,C0​)=b−pDF(up​,Cp​).
(15)
A constant microscopic energy shifts every blocked weight by the same factor, so C′=bDC+c(u). Substitution at one step yields the additive free-energy recursion under blocking
f(u)=b−Df(Rb​u)+g(u),g(u)=βth​b−Dc(u).
(16)
Induction now gives the requested relation
f(u0​)=b−pDf(up​)+j=0∑p−1​b−jDg(uj​).​
(17)
The function g is the identity-operator contribution to renormalization-group free energy: it records eliminated-mode entropy, integration constants and normalization factors. A free-energy function satisfying this inhomogeneous equation need not be purely singular until its analytic background has been subtracted.
At a renormalization-group fixed point, Rb​u∗​=u∗​. Linearize in scaling coordinates vi​ so vi′​=bλi​vi​. Here λi​ is a scaling exponent, not the discrete multiplier itself. A positive λi​ gives a relevant operator, a negative one an irrelevant operator, and zero a marginal operator. The critical surface is the stable manifold: tune every relevant scaling field to zero and the irrelevant coordinates flow toward u∗​. A repulsive renormalization-group trajectory leaves along a relevant direction; it can still have irrelevant coordinates contracting toward that manifold. The plot shows the h=0 section when temperature and field are relevant: t=0 is the critical line in this section, while an independent nonzero field is another direction away from criticality.
The inhomogeneous term cannot simply be discarded from the full free energy. If an analytic background A(u) solves A(u)=b−DA(Rb​u)+g(u), its subtraction leaves Fs​=f−A obeying homogeneous scaling. For example, in linear thermal and field coordinates, a source monomial gmn​tmhn is removed by
Amn​=1−bmλt​+nλh​−Dgmn​​.
(18)
When the denominator vanishes, the source is resonant and can instead produce a logarithm. Thus the homogeneous singular equation is justified after analytic subtraction when there is no relevant resonance affecting the leading singularity, no marginal logarithm that has been omitted, and no singular dependence on a dangerously irrelevant coupling. Merely saying that g is regular is insufficient to discard the whole weighted sum without this analysis.
Under these assumptions, with two relevant scaling fields t,h, choose p so that bpλt​∣t∣ is of order one. The scaling hypothesis for critical phenomena then gives
Fs​(t,h)=∣t∣D/λt​Φ±​(∣t∣λh​/λt​h​).
(19)
Writing Φ±​=f±​+I±​ recovers the PDF's notation:
F±​=∣t∣D/λt​[f±​(h/∣t∣λh​/λt​)+I±​].​
(20)
The sign labels the two matching branches t>0 and t<0, which enter different phases when the flow leaves the critical neighborhood. The constants I±​ retain a branch-dependent identity contribution or a chosen split of the scaling function. They are determined by the same matching problem, not independently adjustable nonuniversal free-energy offsets. An arbitrary analytic offset from C0​ belongs to the subtracted background. The formula describes the singular part; adding that background recovers the full physical free energy.
At h=0, restore the common metric factors as Fs,±​=Af​∣at​t∣D/λt​Φ±​(0). Their ratio at equal ∣t∣ is Φ+​(0)/Φ−​(0), proving the expected universal singular free-energy amplitude ratio because Af​ and at​ cancel. The ratio of full free energies including an arbitrary regular C0​ is not universal; zero amplitudes and logarithmic resonances also require separate treatment.
The critical exponents are obtained by rescaling and differentiating, not by identifying them with the discrete multipliers. The correlation length obeys ξ(t,h)=bpξ(bpλt​t,bpλh​h), hence ν=1/λt​. Two temperature derivatives of Fs​(t,0) give CV,s​≍∣t∣D/λt​−2, while one and two field derivatives give the ordered magnetization and susceptibility powers. Thus
ν=λt​1​,α=2−λt​D​,β=λt​D−λh​​,γ=λt​2λh​−D​,δ=D−λh​λh​​.​
(21)
For δ, instead set t=0 and choose the blocking scale by h, obtaining Fs​(0,h)≍∣h∣D/λh​ and then differentiate. A nonzero spontaneous ordered-branch derivative is assumed in the formula for β. In particular the hyperscaling relation is α=2−Dν​. Its physical assumption is an order-one singular free energy per correlation volume, with a single diverging length and no dangerous variable changing its volume scaling. Above the upper critical dimension, a stabilizing quartic coupling can invalidate that assumption even though the correlation length still scales.
For the last model, take the quadratic Hamiltonian exactly as visually printed: its source term is +hϕ, outside the half multiplying the gradient and mass terms. With κ>0 and m2>0, its connected quadratic covariance is proportional to
G(q)=κ−1q2+m21​=q2+κm2κ​.
(22)
The pole scale, or the massive Gaussian field correlation tail obtained by its Fourier transform, gives
ξ=(κm2)−1/2=κ​m1​(m>0).​
(23)
The uniform source shifts the mean to ⟨ϕ⟩=−h/m2 and does not change the connected covariance. Replacing h by −h matches the earlier magnetic source convention without changing any scaling index.
Define a Gaussian thinning transformation by retaining momenta ∣q∣<Λ/b, integrating the Gaussian shell, and rescaling
x′=x/b,ϕ′(x′)=b(D−2)/2ϕ<​(bx′).
(24)
The gradient coefficient is unchanged. Counting volume and field powers in the remaining action gives
κ′=κ,(m2)′=b2m2,h′=b(D+2)/2h.
(25)
Shell integration contributes only the additive determinant term. Thus λt​=2 when the thermal variable is proportional to m2, and λh​=(D+2)/2. Inserting these into the homogeneous index formulas gives the requested Gaussian critical exponents

∑x,μ​nx,μ​(θx+μ^​​−θx​)=−∑x​θx​∑μ​(nx,μ​−nx−μ^​,μ​).

In three dimensions the defects are vortex lines, and the dual constrained-current representation still holds but is not a gas of point charges with logarithmic interactions.
First take a connected simply connected planar lattice, with free outer angles, so no torus winding sectors are silently discarded. To see existence, integrate the rotated current along a dual path: the change around a closed dual path is the total divergence inside, hence zero. Summing all plaquette curls cancels each bond against its neighboring plaquette. This proves neutrality directly. Equivalently, restoring the common dual-height mode modulo its integer shift gives ∫01​dϕ0​e2πiϕ0​∑q, which vanishes unless the total charge is zero; only then is the inverse lattice Laplacian well-defined on the charge source.
On an infinite plane, the same condition is required for a localized finite-energy configuration with constant phase at infinity. Net charge Q produces θ∼Qargx far away, so its exterior action is
2β​∫aL​2πrdrr2Q2​=πβQ2log(L/a),
(37)
which diverges unless Q=0. With an open boundary the total charge instead equals the boundary winding, and the finite planar duality in part (b) can sum nonneutral configurations. Thus neutrality follows from periodicity or the stated finite-energy boundary condition, not from arbitrary boundaries. In three dimensions the analogous local constraint is that vortex lines cannot end in the bulk.

For ϵ>0, the quartic interaction has a nonzero limiting value λ∗​=O(ϵ) instead of disappearing under coarse-graining. Long-wavelength thermal fluctuations are therefore interacting, so the fluctuation-free Landau approximation cannot give the generic critical powers. ν=yt​1​=21​+12ϵ​+O(ϵ2).​
