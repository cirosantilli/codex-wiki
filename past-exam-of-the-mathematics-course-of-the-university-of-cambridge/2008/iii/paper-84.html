Assume the dense current lies on the bottom, Δρ>0, and its height is small compared with the confined aquifer depth w. This permits the hydrostatic approximation and neglect of leading-order resistance from the return flow of the original fluid. The dense-layer pressure anomaly on the basal seal is Δρgh. The along-layer Darcy flux, integrated over the current thickness, and the downward leakage flux are respectively
qx​=−μkΔρg​hhx​,qℓ​=μbλΔρg​h.
(1)
Volume conservation requires ϕht​+∂x​qx​=−qℓ​. Thus the porous gravity current with hydrostatic leakage through a basal seal obeys
ht​=D(hhx​)x​−Ωh,D=ϕμkΔρg​,Ω=ϕμbλΔρg​.​
(2)
Here permeability of a porous medium has units of area, and Ω has units of inverse time. The original PDF instead prints the sink coefficient λkΔρg/(μb). With λ defined there as a permeability, that coefficient has units of area per time and cannot multiply h to give ht​. It also omits the division of leakage by porosity. This is a genuine source error, not a Darcy law convention. The remaining parts solve the stated linear-drainage equation for a constant Ω>0; the physically consistent coefficient is the one above. Formally treating the printed coefficient as an independently prescribed inverse-time constant leaves those mathematical solutions unchanged.

A model must track polymer age as well as its position. Polymer enters during s∈[ta​,ta​+Δt] and activates at age τ. The first activation in laboratory time is ta​+τ, not τ measured from initial water injection. Assume activation permanently changes the local dynamic viscosity from μ to λμ, with λ>1, and neglect polymer diffusion, retention and shear-dependent rheology.
Let Xi​(t)=∫0t​ui​(r)dr be cumulative pore-fluid displacement. A fluid particle entering at time s has position Xi​(t)−Xi​(s) until it exits. At time t≥ta​+τ, the youngest activated polymer was injected at sy​=min(ta​+Δt,t−τ). The length of activated polymer still inside layer i is exactly
ℓi​(t)=[min{L,Xi​(t)−Xi​(ta​)}−max{0,Xi​(t)−Xi​(sy​)}]+​.
(14)
Set ℓi​=0 when t<ta​+τ. This accounts both for gradual activation of the slug and its later exit. Hydraulic resistances add in series, so delayed polymer diversion in parallel porous layers is described by
X˙i​(t)=ϕμ[L+(λ−1)ℓi​(t)]ki​ΔP​,Qi​(t)=μ[L+(λ−1)ℓi​(t)]Ai​ki​ΔP​.​
(15)

Qtot​=Q1​+Q2​,f2​=∑i​Ai​ki​/[L+(λ−1)ℓi​]A2​k2​/[L+(λ−1)ℓ2​]​.
(16)
The original displacement front reaches the distal well when Xi​(t)=L. Consequently the injected-fluid outflow is ∑i​Qi​(t)1Xi​(t)≥L​, and the complementary sum gives original-fluid production.
The mechanism is especially transparent if Δt<τ, so the entire slug is injected before activation begins, and it activates before leaving the high-permeability layer. Before activation, its lengths are ui0​Δt. While each complete activated slug remains in its layer, those lengths stay fixed and
Qi0​Qi​​=1+(λ−1)Δt/ti​1​,t1​=ta​,t2​=ts​.

The total discharge decreases during polymer occupancy and returns to its original value once the activated slugs leave. A fixed-total-injection-rate experiment would have a different pressure response and cannot be substituted for this fixed-pressure model.

Before the unmodified low-permeability breakthrough, the only original-fluid discharge is from layer 2. Polymer raises its resistance, so its rate is never greater than Q20​ at the same fixed pressure. Selective diversion therefore does not increase the instantaneous original-fluid discharge during this initial interval.
It can increase original-fluid production at a later specified time by delaying completion of the sweep. Let T2​ be the treated low-permeability breakthrough time. Integrating its interstitial velocity gives
T2​−ts​=∫0T2​​(1−u20​u2​(t)​)dt>0​
(18)
whenever activated polymer occupies that layer for a positive time before breakthrough. For ts​<t<T2​, the unmodified reservoir produces no original fluid, whereas the treated reservoir still produces Q2​(t)>0. Thus the strategy can give greater original-fluid flux at a given time after unmodified breakthrough, through delayed sweep rather than increased absolute slow-layer flow. A sufficient activation condition is ta​+τ<ts​ with a nonzero slug that has not already exited the slow layer. If polymer only affects layer 1 after its breakthrough and never affects layer 2 before its own breakthrough, this particular benefit does not occur. Integrated recovery over a chosen finite operating window must be distinguished from a comparison at one time.

After transverse equilibration, a localized release on a sufficiently long aquifer has the advection-diffusion heat-kernel solution
C(x,t)=4πDeff​t​M​exp[−4Deff​t(x−x0​−Ut)2​],​

A short pulse can therefore have an initially asymmetric or strongly distorted longitudinal shape before entering the averaged regime.

The K-epsilon turbulence model evolves turbulent kinetic energy k and its turbulent kinetic energy dissipation rate ϵ, rather than resolving turbulent fluctuations. It closes the Reynolds stress with an isotropic eddy viscosity, νT​=Cμ​k2/ϵ, and models transport of k and ϵ by gradient diffusion with coefficients νT​/σk​ and νT​/σϵ​. Production is tied to mean strain; dissipation-equation production and destruction are modeled terms with empirical constants. The characteristic velocity and length are k​ and k3/2/ϵ. These assumptions are primarily for high Reynolds number, and strongly anisotropic flow, sharp interfaces and molecular-viscosity-dominated regions need additional treatment. The dissipation equation is a closure, not a second exact energy-conservation law.

The dominant balance is convection of small turbulent energy and dissipation by the steady mean flow against their modeled turbulent diffusion. Diffusion supplies turbulent quantities from the turbulent side to the front; there is no local turbulent production in this problem. For q=2p−1, the energy-destruction term divided by its advection term is proportional to ξq−(p−1)=ξp. In the dissipation equation, the destruction term has exponent 2q−p, so its ratio to advection is likewise ξ(2q−p)−(q−1)=ξp. Both tend to zero for p>0. Thus advection balances turbulent diffusion, while both modeled destruction terms are asymptotically smaller.

Let P denote total gravitational potential energy, Pb​ the background potential energy of the adiabatically sorted density field, and A=P−Pb​ the available potential energy. Define B positive for reversible transfer from kinetic energy to P, ϵ as viscous kinetic-energy loss, F as imposed mechanical work, W as molecular transfer from internal energy to potential energy, and M as the irreversible conversion of A to Pb​. With boundary contributions removed, the stratified mixing energy budget is
K˙=F−B−ϵ,P˙=B+W,P˙b​=M+W,A˙=B−M.​
(38)
For an unforced flow set F=0. In an alternative notation, if Φd​=P˙b​ is the full diffusive increase of background energy and Φi​=W, then M=Φd​−Φi​ and A˙=B+Φi​−Φd​. This distinguishes irreversible density mixing from reversible buoyancy exchange. The internal reservoir obeys I˙=ϵ−W, so summing the kinetic, potential and internal budgets gives d(K+P+I)/dt=F as required by conservation of energy.

Keep the forcing ratio r=Lf​/Lρ​ fixed in (0,1). Then
U02​K∞​​=Lρ​Lv​​r1−r​,ϵB​=Lρ​Lv​​KU02​​.
(45)
For Lv​≪Lρ​, dissipation is strong at a given K and K∞​≪U02​ when r is not extreme. If the initial energy is of order U02​, it decays toward a small steady value and starts with a small instantaneous mixing efficiency. This is a regime of short velocity-fluctuation scales and efficient viscous loss relative to the modeled buoyancy conversion at that initial energy.
For Lv​≫Lρ​, dissipation is weak at that same energy and K∞​≫U02​. A moderate initial energy grows and initially has a large instantaneous mixing efficiency; increasing stored energy eventually makes dissipation comparable with the remaining forcing. The energy-relaxation time is of order Lv​/K∞​​, which grows as Lv​​ at fixed forcing and density lengths.
Within this closure, both limits have the same long-time loss fraction ηc,∞​=Lf​/Lρ​. The steady energy changes with Lv​ so as to compensate for its explicit effect on dissipation. Different initial conditions and finite observation windows give different transient efficiencies; one cannot infer a universal long-time efficiency from Lv​/Lρ​ alone. In a real experiment the length scales need not remain independent of the changing flow, so this conclusion belongs to the stated model.

Assume both openings have positive area, no imposed wind pressure, a light buoyant plume, and a Boussinesq approximation. Write S=πQs​ and Bs​=πFs​ for physical source volume and buoyancy flux. Include discharge coefficients CH​,C0​; if ideal openings are intended, set these to one. In a steady two-layer displacement ventilation state with interface height z∗​, let the floor inflow be q0​ and roof outflow qH​. Volume and buoyancy balances give
qH​=q0​+S=πQ(z∗​),gu′​=qH​Bs​​.
(56)
The hydrostatic pressure head across the warm upper layer must equal the two opening losses:
2C02​A02​q02​​+2CH2​AH2​qH2​​=gu′​(H−z∗​).
(57)
A positive unprocessed lower layer needs q0​>0. At the boundary where this layer disappears, z∗​=0, the plume can entrain no lower ambient fluid, so q0​=0, qH​=S and gu′​=Bs​/S. The critical condition is therefore S3=2CH2​AH2​Bs​H.
To determine the inequality, consider a fully processed room. Its steady reduced gravity is g′=Bs​/S. If p0​ is the interior-to-exterior pressure difference at the floor, source-volume blocking of fresh floor inflow requires p0​≥0. Both openings then exhaust, with
S=C0​A0​2p0​/ρ0​​+CH​AH​2(p0​/ρ0​+g′H)​.
(58)
The right-hand side is increasing in p0​ and has its minimum at zero. Consequently a fully processed state exists exactly when
S3≥2CH2​AH2​Bs​H,Qs3​≥π22CH2​AH2​Fs​H​.​
(59)
This is source-volume blocking of displacement ventilation. At equality floor inflow vanishes; above it the floor can also exhaust and no fresh lower-layer supply is needed. Below it, the floor pressure must be negative, admitting fresh fluid and supporting a positive lower layer. In fact the steady interface equation has a unique positive root: its loss side increases with z∗​ because Q(z∗​) increases, while its buoyancy-head side decreases.
The floor-opening area affects the positive-interface state and its approach, but drops out of the blocking threshold because floor flux is zero at the threshold. Equal splitting of pressure head, or using a source-free effective opening area at this transition, would miss this distinction. If the floor opening is sealed, the fresh-inflow argument no longer applies: a finite source with only roof exhaust instead cycles the room asymptotically as in the ceiling-opening case of part (c).
