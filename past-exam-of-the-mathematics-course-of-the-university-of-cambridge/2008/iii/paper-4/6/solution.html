A finite-dimensional group algebra has finite representation type of a group algebra when there are only finitely many isomorphism classes of finite-dimensional indecomposable modules. The definition concerns all dimensions, not merely finitely many modules in each fixed dimension.
Let eB​ be the block idempotent and choose a maximal Brauer pair (D,bD​): bD​ is a block idempotent of kCG​(D) with bD​BrD​(eB​)=0. The inertial index of a block is
e(B)=∣NG​(D,bD​):DCG​(D)∣,​
(51)
where NG​(D,bD​) is the stabilizer of the chosen pair. Changing the pair by conjugation does not alter this number. For cyclic D, it is a prime-to-p divisor of p−1. Here n≥1, as required for the order-p subgroup Q to exist.
Put H=NG​(Q). Since Q is characteristic in D, NG​(D)≤H, so Green correspondence applies. In fact the nonprojective modules in B have a particularly simple correspondence. If g∈/H, a nontrivial intersection D∩gD would contain the unique order-p subgroup of both cyclic groups, giving Q=gQ and hence g∈H, a contradiction. Thus every such intersection is trivial. The error modules in induction are projective. In restriction, an intersection H∩gD cannot contain a conjugate of a nontrivial subgroup of D by an element of H: such a conjugate contains Q, and that would again force gQ=Q. The general Green correspondence therefore pairs all indecomposable modules with nontrivial module vertex contained in a conjugate of D. In particular it pairs the nonprojective modules of B with the nonprojective modules of its Brauer correspondent b in H; the block-compatible form of the correspondence selects b. Explicitly,
M↓H​=f(M)⊕MY​,f(M)↑G=M⊕P,
(52)
where P is projective and the restriction error family is Y={H∩gD:g∈/H}. One should not replace this restriction error term by projectives without an additional argument about H.
For the final structure calculation we use the general cyclic-defect structure theorem, which is permitted here as a general fact: the basic algebra of a split block with cyclic defect group is a Brauer tree algebra. Its tree has e(B) edges, one simple module per edge, and exceptional multiplicity
m=e(B)∣D∣−1​.
(53)
For a one-edge tree the basic algebra presentation is k[t]/(tm+1). This is the local presentation of the tree algebra, not an assertion that an arbitrary block with one simple module is automatically uniserial.
With e(B)=1 the tree has one edge and m=pn−1. Thus the basic algebra of B is
A0​=k[t]/(tpn).
(54)
It has exactly one simple module, k=A0​/(t). Its ideals are precisely (ti): for any nonzero element, factor out its lowest power of t, after which the remaining factor is a unit. The regular projective therefore has the unique submodule chain
A0​⊃tA0​⊃⋯⊃tpn−1A0​⊃0,
(55)
with each consecutive factor one-dimensional and simple. A Morita equivalence from A0​ to B preserves simple modules, projective covers, submodule lattices and composition length. Hence
B has one simple module S,P(S) is uniserial of length pn.​
(56)
In the split setting one may also write B≅Mdimk​S​(A0​). The projective cover has pn composition factors all isomorphic to S, so its vector-space dimension is pndimk​S, not generally pn.
There is also an elementary reason why the uniserial conclusion follows once the one-simple-module conclusion and finite type are known. If the basic local algebra had dimJ/J2≥2, quotienting its radical square and all but two generators would give k[x,y]/(x,y)2. The two-dimensional parameter family from Question 3(b) would then give infinitely many indecomposables. Thus J/J2 is one-dimensional. Nakayama lemma makes J generated by one element t, so its powers yield a chain and the basic algebra is k[t]/(tr). The cyclic-defect calculation above identifies r=pn. This explains why finite representation type rules out branching in this projective cover.
