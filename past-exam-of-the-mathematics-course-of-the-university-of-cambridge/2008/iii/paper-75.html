Assume a<b. A Chebyshev system of dimension n+1 is a family of real continuous functions u0​,…,un​ for which every linear combination u=∑j=0n​cj​uj​ with coefficients not all zero has at most n distinct zeros in the interval. In particular the functions are linearly independent: an identically zero nontrivial combination would violate that bound. Multiplicities are not counted in this definition.
For distinct points x0​,…,xn​, form the evaluation matrix A=(uj​(xi​))i,j=0n​. If its determinant vanished, a nonzero vector c would satisfy Ac=0. Its associated combination would vanish at all n+1 points, contradicting the Chebyshev system property. Conversely, if the property failed, some nonzero coefficient vector would give a combination vanishing at n+1 distinct points. At those points Ac=0, so the evaluation matrix would be singular. We have proved the determinant criterion for a Chebyshev system:
Φ is a Chebyshev system⟺det(uj​(xi​))i,j=0n​=0 for every set of distinct xi​.​
(1)
This is also the existence-and-uniqueness condition for interpolation from the span of the functions: any prescribed n+1 data values give a uniquely solvable linear system.

The exponents are real, as required for this family of real continuous functions. We prove the exponential Chebyshev system property by induction on the number of functions. A single nonzero multiple of an exponential function has no zero, so the base case holds.
Suppose the result holds for n distinct real exponents, and consider a nontrivial combination F(x)=∑j=0n​cj​eλj​x. If it had n+1 distinct zeros, so would
g(x)=e−λ0​xF(x)=c0​+∑j=1n​cj​e(λj​−λ0​)x,
(2)
since the prefactor is everywhere positive. Applying Rolle's theorem between consecutive zeros gives at least n distinct zeros of
g′(x)=∑j=1n​cj​(λj​−λ0​)e(λj​−λ0​)x.
(3)
The n exponents in this derivative are distinct. By induction, a nontrivial combination of them has at most n−1 zeros. Therefore every coefficient cj​(λj​−λ0​) must be zero. Distinctness gives cj​=0 for j≥1. Then g=c0​ has a zero only if c0​=0, contradicting the original nontriviality. Consequently F has at most n zeros, and
(eλ0​x,…,eλn​x) is a Chebyshev system on [a,b].​
(4)

Let d be the dimension of a proposed real Chebyshev system on the circle, and regard its functions as continuous 2π-periodic functions on the real line. Choose lifted points θ0​<θ1​<⋯<θd−1​<θ0​+2π and set θd​=θ0​+2π. Move the points by
θi​(s)=(1−s)θi​+sθi+1​,0≤s≤1,0≤i<d.
(5)
For every s the points remain distinct on the circle. Indeed, each consecutive gap is a convex combination of positive gaps; the closing gap is also a convex combination of the original closing gap and θ1​−θ0​. The evaluation determinant
D(s)=det(uj​(θi​(s)))i,j=0d−1​
(6)
is therefore nonzero for every s by the determinant criterion for a Chebyshev system. It is continuous, so the intermediate value theorem forbids a sign change. At s=1, however, periodicity makes its rows the cyclic permutation of the initial rows, giving D(1)=(−1)d−1D(0). For even d this reverses the sign, a contradiction. Hence every such periodic Chebyshev system has odd dimension; none has even dimension. This determinant argument avoids assuming differentiability or simple zeros of the functions.

The Korovkin theorem states that if positive linear operators on continuous functions Ln​:C[0,1]→C[0,1] satisfy
∥Ln​ej​−ej​∥∞​⟶0,ej​(t)=tj,j=0,1,2,
(7)
then ∥Ln​f−f∥∞​→0 for every real continuous function f.
Let r=k−1≥2 and hn​=∣Δn​∣. For the B-splines in this question, Ni,n​≥0 and their sum is one on the basic interval. Thus the Schoenberg spline operator Vn​ is linear and positive. To verify the three test functions, suppress the index n and write the Marsden identity with ωi​(x)=∏j=1r​(x−ti+j​). Comparing the coefficients of xr,xr−1,xr−2 gives the monomial B-spline coefficients
1=∑i​Ni​(t),t=∑i​ξi​Ni​(t),t2=∑i​qi​Ni​(t),
(8)
where
ξi​=r1​∑j=1r​ti+j​,qi​=(2r​)1​∑1≤j<ℓ≤r​ti+j​ti+ℓ​.
(9)
The first coefficient is the partition of unity; the second is the Greville abscissa. Every internal knot and the sample point τi​ lie in [ti​,ti+k​], of width at most khn​. Consequently ∣τi​−ξi​∣≤khn​. Since all these numbers lie in [0,1], for any two internal knots u,v we also have
∣τi2​−uv∣≤∣τi​∣∣τi​−u∣+∣u∣∣τi​−v∣≤2khn​.
(10)
Averaging gives ∣τi2​−qi​∣≤2khn​. Nonnegative weights of sum one now yield
∥Vn​1−1∥∞​=0,∥Vn​e1​−e1​∥∞​≤khn​,∥Vn​e2​−e2​∥∞​≤2khn​.
(11)
Here the spline order k is fixed, so all three errors tend to zero. The Korovkin theorem therefore proves
∥Vn​f−f∥∞​⟶0for every f∈C[0,1].​
(12)
No special selection such as the Greville abscissae is required for the sample points. In fact the local-support error bound for a Schoenberg spline operator also gives ∥Vn​f−f∥∞​≤ω(f,khn​). Under the usual continuous-spline convention the operators have the codomain required by the stated theorem. If the permissive knot notation is taken to allow full interior multiplicities and hence discontinuous splines, the same conclusion in the uniform norm still follows from this direct bound, although each approximant need not then be continuous.

For a real continuous function on [−1,1], the Chebyshev alternation theorem gives a unique best uniform approximation pn∗​ from the space of polynomials of degree at most n. If f is not already in that space, a candidate p is best if and only if there exist ordered points x0​<⋯<xn+1​ and a sign σ∈{1,−1} with
f(xj​)−p(xj​)=σ(−1)j∥f−p∥∞​,0≤j≤n+1.
(13)
Thus a nonzero best error has at least n+2 alternating extrema of equal magnitude. If f is already a polynomial of degree at most n, the unique best approximant is f itself with zero error.
Existence does not require assuming that the infimum is attained: a minimizing sequence has uniformly bounded polynomial norms, and evaluation at n+1 fixed distinct nodes bounds its coefficients through an invertible interpolation matrix. A convergent coefficient subsequence then attains the infimum. The alternation criterion characterizes that minimum and gives uniqueness. In the following parts, n≥1 whenever En−1​ occurs.

A nontrivial example is fn​=Tn+1​, the Chebyshev polynomial characterized by Tn+1​(cosθ)=cos((n+1)θ). Its supremum norm is one. At the n+2 points cos(jπ/(n+1)), 0≤j≤n+1, its values alternate between 1 and −1; reversing their order gives increasing nodes with the same alternation.
The zero polynomial therefore satisfies the Chebyshev alternation theorem for degree at most n, so En​(fn​)=1. Since the smaller polynomial space is contained in the larger one, En−1​(fn​)≥1; the zero polynomial is feasible in the smaller space as well, giving the reverse inequality. Hence
fn​=Tn+1​,En−1​(fn​)=En​(fn​)=1.​
(14)
A constant function would also give equality with both errors zero, but this example shows that equality can occur at a positive approximation error.

Let p be the best uniform approximation to the exponential function from the degree-at-most-n−1 polynomial space. Suppose that En−1​(ex)=En​(ex). Then the same p is best in the larger space. Its error g(x)=ex−p(x) is nonzero, since the exponential is not a polynomial. The Chebyshev alternation theorem supplies n+2 ordered points where g has alternating signs. By the intermediate value theorem, g has at least one zero in each of the n+1 intervening intervals, hence at least n+1 distinct zeros.
Applying Rolle's theorem successively n times forces a zero of g(n). But degp≤n−1, so
g(n)(x)=ex>0
(15)
everywhere. This is a contradiction. Together with the automatic non-increase of best errors, it proves
En−1​(ex)>En​(ex),n≥1.​
(16)
The argument is an instance of strict polynomial error decrease from nonvanishing derivatives.

For each n, choose the best uniform approximation pn∗​. If its error is nonzero, take its n+2 alternating extrema. The intermediate value theorem supplies a point yj​ strictly between the jth and (j+1)st extrema such that f(yj​)=pn∗​(yj​), for 0≤j≤n. These n+1 points are distinct. The polynomial interpolation problem at them has a unique solution of degree at most n, so its solution is exactly pn∗​. This constructs the interpolation nodes from best uniform approximation.
If En​(f)=0, then f itself belongs to the polynomial space, and any n+1 distinct nodes give the same conclusion. In either case, take xn,j​=yj​ or the arbitrary nodes just described. Then
∥ℓn​(f)−f∥∞​=En​(f).
(17)
The Weierstrass approximation theorem gives En​(f)→0; alternatively, the bound derived in part 4(b), combined with uniform continuity, gives this convergence directly. Therefore
∥ℓn​(f)−f∥∞​⟶0.​
(18)
The nodes may depend on f and on n. The conclusion does not assert a universal sequence of interpolation grids working for every continuous target.

For a function on an interval, the modulus of continuity is ω(f,t)=sup∣x−y∣≤t​∣f(x)−f(y)∣, with the pair restricted to that interval. For a periodic function the equivalent definition is supθ,∣h∣≤t​∣g(θ+h)−g(θ)∣. The mean value theorem and ∣sinθ∣≤1 give
∣cos(θ+h)−cosθ∣≤∣h∣.
(19)
Both cosine values lie in [−1,1]. Hence for ∣h∣≤t,
∣f​(θ+h)−f​(θ)∣=∣f(cos(θ+h))−f(cosθ)∣≤ω(f,t).
(20)
Taking the supremum proves
ω(f​,t)≤ω(f,t).​
(21)
If the periodic modulus of continuity is defined using circular distance instead, choose lifts at that shortest distance and apply the same argument. The cosine substitution for polynomial approximation therefore does not enlarge this modulus.

The first Jackson theorem for periodic approximation states that an absolute constant CJ​ exists such that, for every continuous 2π-periodic function g and integer n≥1, there is a trigonometric polynomial qn​ of degree at most n satisfying
∥g−qn​∥∞​≤CJ​ω(g,1/n).
(22)
Apply it to the continuous periodic function g(θ)=f(cosθ), which is even. Replace the approximant by its even part
qnev​(θ)=21​(qn​(θ)+qn​(−θ)).
(23)
Since g(−θ)=g(θ), the triangle inequality gives ∥g−qnev​∥∞​≤∥g−qn​∥∞​. This symmetrization cancels all sine terms, so qnev​=∑j=0n​αj​cos(jθ). The Chebyshev polynomials satisfy Tj​(cosθ)=cos(jθ) and have degree j; the latter follows inductively from T0​=1, T1​=x and Tj+1​=2xTj​−Tj−1​. Therefore
pn​(x)=∑j=0n​αj​Tj​(x)
(24)
is an algebraic polynomial of degree at most n, and pn​(cosθ)=qnev​(θ). Because cosine maps onto [−1,1], the supremum norms agree exactly:
∥f−pn​∥[−1,1]​=∥g−qnev​∥T​.
(25)
Finally, the definition of the best error, periodic Jackson approximation, and part (a) give the successive bounds
En​(f)≤∥f−pn​∥∞​≤CJ​ω(g,1/n)≤CJ​ω(f,1/n).
(26)
Thus
En​(f)≤CJ​ω(f,1/n),n≥1.​
(27)
Neither the constant nor the choice of approximation space depends on f. The cosine substitution for polynomial approximation and even symmetrization are what preserve the required degree bound.

Set r=k−1, with k≥2 for the final linear-reproduction assertion, and use the normalized knot polynomial ψi​=ωi​/r!. Differentiate the Marsden identity r−j times with respect to the auxiliary variable x, for 0≤j≤r. This gives
j!r!​(x−t)j=∑i​ωi(r−j)​(x)Ni​(t),j!(x−t)j​=∑i​ψi(r−j)​(x)Ni​(t).
(28)
The Taylor polynomial expansion of an arbitrary polynomial p of degree at most r about x is exact:
p(t)=∑j=0r​j!p(j)(x)​(t−x)j.
(29)
Substitute the differentiated identity into each term and collect the B-spline coefficients. The result is
p(t)=∑i​λi​(p)Ni​(t),λi​(p)=j=0∑r​(−1)jp(j)(x)ψi(r−j)​(x).​
(30)
This is the normalized Marsden dual functional. There is no extra factorial outside the sum, because it is already included in ψi​.
To prove independence of x directly, differentiate the finite sum. The contribution from differentiating ψi​ in term j cancels the contribution from differentiating p in term j−1. Explicitly,
dxd​λi​(p)=∑j=0r​(−1)jp(j+1)ψi(r−j)​+∑j=0r​(−1)jp(j)ψi(r−j+1)​=0.
(31)
The two uncancelled endpoint terms vanish because p(r+1)=ψi(r+1)​=0. Thus the coefficient functional is constant in the auxiliary variable, as required.
The leading two terms of the knot polynomial give
ψi(r)​(x)=1,ψi(r−1)​(x)=x−r1​∑j=1r​ti+j​=x−ti∗​.
(32)
For a linear polynomial p(t)=A+Bt, only j=0,1 survive, whence
λi​(p)=p(x)−(x−ti∗​)p′(x)=A+Bti∗​=p(ti∗​).
(33)
The coefficient points ti∗​ are the Greville abscissae. Inserting these coefficients proves exact linear reproduction:
p(t)=i∑​p(ti∗​)Ni​(t)for every p∈P1​.​
(34)
All identities hold on the basic knot interval [tk​,tn+1​], including the endpoints with the standard continuous endpoint convention. Repeated knots cause no difficulty in these polynomial differentiations.

Write r=x−u∗. If u∗ is a best approximation, then for every v∈Un​ and real t, the competitor u∗+tv satisfies
∥r−tv∥2−∥r∥2=−2tRe(r,v)+t2∥v∥2≥0.
(35)
The linear coefficient must be zero, since otherwise choosing sufficiently small t of the appropriate sign makes this expression negative. Hence Re(r,v)=0. In a real inner product space this is the desired orthogonality; in a complex inner product space, apply the same argument to iv to show that the imaginary part also vanishes. Thus (r,v)=0 for all v∈Un​.
Conversely, suppose this orthogonality holds. Every competitor u∈Un​ has u−u∗∈Un​. The Pythagorean identity then gives
∥x−u∥2=∥r−(u−u∗)∥2=∥r∥2+∥u−u∗∥2≥∥r∥2.
(36)
Equality forces u=u∗. We have proved the characterization, as well as uniqueness:
u∗ is best⟺(x−u∗,v)=0 for every v∈Un​.​
(37)
This is the orthogonal projection onto a finite-dimensional subspace. Completeness of the ambient space is not needed. Existence follows, for example, by solving the invertible Gram matrix system in a basis of the finite-dimensional linear subspace.

The dual vectors are the Riesz dual basis in an inner product space, and lie in Un​ itself. Take the convention that the inner product is conjugate-linear in its first argument; in a real space the conjugations can simply be omitted. Since the uj​ form a basis, their Gram matrix G is positive definite and invertible. Let B=G−1 and put
uk​=∑ℓ=1n​bℓk​uℓ​.
(38)
The proposed vectors satisfy
(ui​,uk​)=∑ℓ​Giℓ​bℓk​=(GB)ik​=δik​.
(39)
They therefore give the unique dual family within Un​. Their own Gram matrix is
((uj​,uk​))j,k​=B∗GB.
(40)
The inverse of a Hermitian positive-definite matrix is Hermitian, so B∗=B. Using GB=I gives B∗GB=B. Consequently
(uj​,uk​)=bjk​.​
(41)
For a real space this is the familiar identity BTGB=B. If the opposite complex inner-product convention is adopted, conjugate the coefficients in the dual-vector formula; the stated identity remains the same. Requiring the dual vectors to lie in Un​ is essential: arbitrary additions perpendicular to Un​ preserve the pairings with ui​ but alter the Gram matrix of the dual vectors.

Use the standard nonnegative partition-normalized B-splines Nj​; their sum is at most one, and is exactly one for the complete clamped basis on [0,1]. The printed question introduces Mi​ only in its last matrix formula without defining it. The intended normalization is the unit-integral normalization of a B-spline:
ci​=∫01​Ni​(t)dt>0,Mi​(t)=ci​Ni​(t)​,∫01​Mi​(t)dt=1.
(42)
For supports wholly inside the interval, ci​=(ti+k​−ti​)/k. The positivity and partition convention, sometimes called L∞​ normalization, should not be confused with individually rescaling every spline to have maximum exactly one.
The characterization in part 1(a) gives the normal equations for the orthogonal projection:
(f−∑j​aj​Nj​,Ni​)L2​​=0,1≤i≤n.
(43)
Dividing each equation by ci​ yields
Ga=d,Gij​=(Mi​,Nj​)L2​​,di​=(Mi​,f)L2​​.
(44)
This is the mixed-normalization spline Gram matrix. It need not be symmetric, but G=D−1H with D=diag(ci​) and Hij​=(Ni​,Nj​)L2​​. The ordinary Gram matrix H is positive definite because the splines are linearly independent, so G is invertible and a=G−1d.
Since each Mi​ is nonnegative and has integral one,
∣di​∣≤∫01​Mi​(t)∣f(t)∣dt≤∥f∥∞​,∥d∥ℓ∞​​≤∥f∥∞​.
(45)
The induced matrix operator norm is the maximum absolute row sum, so
∥a∥ℓ∞​​≤∥G−1∥ℓ∞​→ℓ∞​​∥f∥∞​.
(46)
Finally, nonnegativity and the subpartition of unity for B-splines give
∣PS​f(t)∣=​∑j​aj​Nj​(t)​≤∥a∥ℓ∞​​∑j​Nj​(t)≤∥a∥ℓ∞​​.
(47)
Taking the supremum over t and then over ∥f∥∞​≤1 proves the maximum-norm bound for spline projection:
∥PS​∥∞​≤∥G−1∥ℓ∞​→ℓ∞​​.​
(48)
The formulas also establish linearity and boundedness of the projection on continuous inputs. Its range is exactly the finite-dimensional spline space S, since it fixes every spline in that space. Thus, when the splines are continuous, it is an operator C[0,1]→C[0,1] with range S; it is not onto all of C[0,1] as the printed wording says. For example, et cannot belong to a fixed finite-degree spline space because it is not a polynomial on any nonempty knot interval. The missing Mi​ normalization and this range correction are needed for a precise reading of the result.
