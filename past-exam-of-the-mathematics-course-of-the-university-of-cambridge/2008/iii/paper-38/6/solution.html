In a finite game, a profile of independent mixed strategies σ=(σ1​,…,σn​) is a Nash equilibrium if no player can improve their expected payoff by changing only their own mixed strategy. In symbols,
Ua​(σa​,σ−a​)≥Ua​(τa​,σ−a​)for every player a and every strategy τa​.
(31)
Because expectation is linear in a player's own mixed strategy, checking this inequality for all their pure strategies suffices. Nothing requires the players' payoffs to sum to zero.
For the symmetric finite game, write ei​(p)=e(i,p) and eˉ(p)=∑i​pi​ei​(p). Each ei​(p) is a polynomial in the opponents' independent probabilities and therefore continuous on the probability simplex Δ={p≥0:∑i​pi​=1}. Put
ri​(p)=max(0,ei​(p)−eˉ(p)),S(p)=∑i​ri​(p),Fi​(p)=1+S(p)pi​+ri​(p)​.
(32)
This continuous map has nonnegative coordinates summing to one, so it maps the compact convex set Δ into itself. The Brouwer fixed-point theorem gives p=F(p). At that fixed point ri​=pi​S. If S>0, every i in the strategy support has ri​>0 and therefore ei​(p)>eˉ(p). But
∑i​pi​(ei​(p)−eˉ(p))=0,
(33)
whereas every supported term would be strictly positive, a contradiction. Thus S=0, all pure payoffs are at most eˉ(p), and their weighted average equals eˉ(p). Every supported pure strategy is consequently a best response to the common opposing mixed strategy. The profile in which every player uses p is a symmetric Nash equilibrium. This proves the gain-map proof of symmetric equilibrium for any finite number of players, including boundary fixed points of the probability simplex.
In the least unique bid auction, let p be the common probability of bid 1. Bidding 1 wins only when the other two bids are both 2, and its winning payoff is 3−1=2. Bidding 2 wins only when the other two bids are both 1, and its winning payoff is 3−2=1. Hence the two expected payoffs are
e1​(p)=2(1−p)2,e2​(p)=p2.
(34)
Neither symmetric pure strategy profile is an equilibrium: against two bids of 1 a deviation to 2 earns 1 instead of 0, and against two bids of 2 a deviation to 1 earns 2 instead of 0. An interior symmetric equilibrium must make the two expected payoffs equal. Solving 2(1−p)2=p2 leaves just one root in (0,1), giving
p=2−2​ for bid 1,1−p=2​−1 for bid 2.​
(35)
The expected payoff to each player is p2=6−42​.
To count all nonsymmetric Nash equilibria, let pi​ be player i's probability of bidding 1. The difference between their expected payoffs from bids 1 and 2 is
Δi​=2(1−pj​)(1−pk​)−pj​pk​=2−2pj​−2pk​+pj​pk​.
(36)
The best response conditions are pi​=1 when Δi​>0, pi​=0 when Δi​<0, and arbitrary pi​ when Δi​=0.
For any 0≤t≤1, the profile (p1​,p2​,p3​)=(1,0,t) satisfies these conditions: Δ1​=2(1−t)≥0, Δ2​=−t≤0, and Δ3​=0. Thus every permutation of this profile is a Nash equilibrium. At t=0,1 these give the six pure profiles with at least one bid of each kind; for 0<t<1, one player mixes freely and the other two use opposite pure strategies.
There are no other nonsymmetric equilibria. If all three probabilities are interior, every Δi​ must vanish. Subtracting two such equations gives
Δ1​−Δ2​=(p1​−p2​)(2−p3​)=0.
(37)
Since 2−p3​>0, all probabilities are equal, yielding the symmetric solution already found. If exactly two players mix and the third uses bid 2, either mixer strictly prefers bid 1 because Δ=2(1−pj​)>0. If the third uses bid 1, either mixer strictly prefers bid 2 because Δ=−pj​<0. Both cases are impossible. If exactly one player mixes, their indifference requires the other players to use opposite bids. Finally, with no mixing, the all-equal pure profiles fail and the remaining six satisfy the best response conditions.
Consequently the complete Nash equilibrium set consists of the single symmetric mixed profile together with the six closed one-parameter families given by permutations of (1,0,t). Their endpoints overlap at the six pure equilibria. In particular,
there are uncountably infinitely many nonsymmetric equilibria.​
(38)
Counting only the six pure ones would miss the interior points of these families in the two-bid three-player least unique bid auction.
