(aL1​−bL3​)−λ=r(L1​−L2​)+s(L2​−L3​),r=32a+b−4​,s=3a+2b−5​.
(16)
The dominant weight multiplicity formula for sl3 gives zero unless r,s are nonnegative integers, and otherwise gives
dimR(a,b)λ​=1+min(a,b,r,s).
(17)
For completeness, this multiplicity is an actual count. In the sl3 interlacing character formula, use top row (a+b,b,0) and bottom entry s+3; the target diagonal exponents are (s+3,s+2,s). The middle row (P,Q) obeys P+Q=2s+5 and
max{b,2s+5−b,s+3}≤P≤min{a+b,2s+5}.
(18)
Each allowable integral P gives one Gelfand–Tsetlin basis vector. Subtracting the lower bound from the upper bound gives the minimum of a,s,r,r+3,b,s+2, which reduces to min(a,b,r,s). This proves the displayed multiplicity formula in the case at hand.
Since both summands have a nonzero weight space and their dimensions at λ sum to three, their multiplicities must be one and two. For multiplicity one, the minimum is zero. The cases a=0 and b=0 give (0,4+3j) and (5+3j,0); the remaining cases r=0 or s=0 give (1,2) and (3,1). Hence the full family is
C1​={R(0,4+3j),R(5+3j,0):j≥0}∪{R(1,2),R(3,1)}.

The minimum conditions exhaust all cases and each listed module has the stated multiplicity. All candidate unordered pairs are {U,V} with U∈C1​, V∈C2​​. There are infinitely many at this stage; the question imposes no upper bound on their highest weights.

Work with a complex semisimple Lie algebra and its root system R in the real span E of the roots of a root system. The Killing form induces a positive definite inner product on this real root space, after the usual identification with the dual of the real span of the coroots. For each root α, its Weyl reflection is
sα​(λ)=λ−⟨λ,α∨⟩α,α∨=(α,α)2α​.
(23)
The Weyl group is W=⟨sα​:α∈R⟩​. The structural facts being used are that these reflections preserve the finite root system, that the roots span E, and that the reflections in the simple roots already generate W. Thus W is finite: it acts faithfully as permutations of R. Equivalently, it is the normalizer of the Cartan subalgebra modulo its centralizer in the associated adjoint group.

Choose a positive system R+ and its simple roots Δ={α1​,…,αℓ​}. The corresponding fundamental Weyl chamber is the open cone
W={λ∈E:⟨λ,αi∨​⟩>0 for all i}.​
(24)
Its Closed dominant Weyl chamber replaces all strict inequalities by weak inequalities. The facts used here are that every positive root is a nonnegative integral combination of the simple roots, and that the Weyl chambers are the connected components left after removing the root-orthogonal hyperplanes. Hence the displayed inequalities describe one such component. The Weyl group permutes these components simply transitively, and every orbit in E meets the Closed dominant Weyl chamber in exactly one point. A point on a wall may have a nontrivial stabilizer, which is why uniqueness of the chamber point does not always mean uniqueness of the group element carrying it there.

A highest-weight vector is a nonzero weight vector v such that gα​v=0 for every positive root α. Its weight of a representation is a highest weight. For an irreducible finite-dimensional Lie algebra representation, this is the unique highest weight of a representation, and v generates the module; a reducible module can have several highest weights of different summands.
Here is an existence argument for every nonzero finite-dimensional module V. For each simple root, its root operators and coroot form an sl2 subalgebra associated with a root. The classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations shows that this coroot acts diagonalizably with integral eigenvalues. These commuting coroots span the Cartan subalgebra, so simultaneous diagonalization gives a finite weight-space decomposition
V=⨁λ​Vλ​.
(25)
Choose a real linear functional ℓ on the weight lattice span that is positive on every simple root, hence on every positive root, and choose a weight λ maximizing ℓ among the finitely many weights of V. If v∈Vλ​ and Xα​∈gα​, then
H(Xα​v)=Xα​Hv+[H,Xα​]v=(λ(H)+α(H))Xα​v,
(26)
so Xα​v belongs to Vλ+α​ if nonzero. For positive α, that would contradict maximality because ℓ(λ+α)>ℓ(λ). Thus every nonzero v∈Vλ​ is a highest-weight vector. Every nonzero finite-dimensional module therefore has a highest weight. The zero module has no nonzero vector or weight, so it is the vacuous exception to the literal unrestricted wording.

In the finite-dimensional complex semisimple Lie algebra setting implicit in this root-system construction, the given Cartan subalgebra and root spaces determine a reduced crystallographic root system R. The ordering divides R into positive roots and their negatives. The simple roots are the positive roots that cannot be written as a sum of two positive roots; a standard root-system fact is that they form a basis and that every positive root is a nonnegative integral combination of them.
Index the simple roots as α1​,…,αℓ​ and form the Cartan matrix
Aij​=⟨αj​,αi∨​⟩=(αi​,αi​)2(αi​,αj​)​.
(43)
For i=j, these entries are nonpositive integers and Aij​Aji​∈{0,1,2,3}. Construct one vertex per simple root and join distinct vertices by Aij​Aji​ lines. If root lengths differ, direct the arrow toward the shorter simple root. This labelled and oriented graph is the Dynkin diagram. Its unoriented Coxeter graph is admissible: normalizing the simple roots to unit length gives 4(vi​,vj​)2=Aij​Aji​. A general Lie algebra without the semisimple root-system hypotheses need not produce such a diagram.

A complex semisimple Lie algebra has zero centre, so its Adjoint representation is faithful. Its absolute Jordan decomposition is the unique expression x=xs​+xn​ with [xs​,xn​]=0, adxs​ diagonalizable and adxn​ nilpotent. Existence follows by applying part (ii) to the adjoint embedding, and uniqueness follows from uniqueness of the matrix Jordan–Chevalley decomposition. The representation-compatibility theorem states that for every finite-dimensional Lie algebra representation ρ, ρ(xs​) and ρ(xn​) are respectively the semisimple and nilpotent parts of ρ(x). Thus these parts are intrinsic to the Lie algebra, independent of a chosen matrix embedding, and their semisimplicity and nilpotence are preserved in every finite-dimensional representation.
