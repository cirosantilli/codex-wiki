Let L=Q(α), where α is an algebraic integer with monic minimal polynomial f∈Z[X], and put I=[OL​:Z[α]]. The Kummer-Dedekind theorem applies to a prime number p with p∤I. Write its reduction as a product of polynomial factors over Fp​:
f​=∏j=1r​gjej​​,
(1)
where the gj​ are distinct monic irreducible polynomials, choose monic integral lifts Gj​. Then the distinct prime ideals above p are pj​=(p,Gj​(α)), and
pOL​=j=1∏r​pjej​​,f(pj​/p)=deggj​.​
(2)
Thus the multiplicity of a factor is its ramification index of a prime ideal, and its degree is the residue degree. The hypothesis on the order in a number field is essential: one cannot infer prime ideal factorization from an arbitrary defining polynomial at a prime dividing its index. There is also a relative version over a base number field K: if OL​ and OK​[α] agree after localization at p, factor the minimal polynomial over OK​/p and use (p,Gj​(α)) with the same conclusions.

The polynomial discriminant satisfies
disc(f)=(−1)n(n−1)/2Res(f,f′),n=degf.
(3)
A polynomial has no repeated roots over the algebraic closure exactly when it is coprime to its derivative. Consequently the assumed squarefree reduction has nonzero resultant with its derivative, so p∤disc(f). On the other hand the power basis is a basis of the order in a number field Z[α], and the discriminant-index formula for an integral lattice gives
disc(f)=disc(1,α,…,αn−1)=[OL​:Z[α]]2dL​.
(4)
Here the field discriminant dL​ is an integer. Therefore
p∤[OL​:Z[α]].​
(5)
This is squarefree reduction excludes a prime from the power-basis index; its argument establishes the index condition before applying the Kummer-Dedekind theorem.

Put α=441/11 and L=Q(α). The Eisenstein criterion at 11 shows that X11−44 is an irreducible polynomial, so [L:Q]=11. Its derivative is 11X10, and the polynomial discriminant is
disc(X11−44)=−11114410=−2201121.
(6)
The discriminant-index formula for an integral lattice implies that the field discriminant has no prime divisor outside {2,11}. By the discriminant obstruction to ramification, every other prime is unramified.
To prove that neither candidate disappears through an index correction, let p lie above p∈{2,11} and normalize its valuation to have value group Z. If e=ep/p​, the relation α11=44 gives
11vp​(α)=evp​(44)={2e,e,​p=2,p=11.​
(7)
In both cases 11∣e. The fundamental identity for prime decomposition, ∑p∣p​ep/p​fp/p​=11, now forces exactly one prime ideal above each candidate, with e=11 and f=1. This is ramification forced by a coprime root valuation; at 2 it avoids an invalid direct application of the Eisenstein criterion, since 4∣44. Hence
The ramified rational primes are exactly 2 and 11; both are totally ramified.​
(8)

Write G=Gal(F/K) and fix a prime ideal q above p. Suppose there is another prime r above p outside the G-orbit of q. Distinct nonzero prime ideals in the ring of integers are distinct maximal ideals, hence pairwise comaximal. The Chinese remainder theorem supplies a∈OF​ satisfying a≡0(modr) and a≡1(modσq) for every σ∈G. Its field norm is
b=NF/K​(a)=∏σ∈G​σ(a)∈OK​.
(9)
The factor a puts b in r, hence in r∩OK​=p. But every σ(a) is congruent to 1 modulo q, since a≡1(modσ−1q). Thus b≡1(modq), contradicting b∈p⊂q. This proves transitivity of the Galois action on primes without any unramified hypothesis.
Let D=StabG​(q) be the decomposition group and H=Gal(F/L). Transitivity identifies the primes of F above p with the coset space G/D. Two such primes contract to the same prime in L exactly when they are in the same H-orbit: apply the transitivity just proved to the Galois extension F/L. Therefore primes in an intermediate field as double cosets gives
#{l⊂OL​:l∣p}=∣H\G/D∣=∣D\G/H∣.​
(10)
The second equality follows by inversion of representatives. It allows one to count D-orbits on the embeddings of L into F, represented by G/H. If H is normal, this simplifies to [G:HD]; the double-coset formulation also handles nonnormal intermediate fields.

Write α=λ1/5, M=Q(α) and E=Q(ζ5​). The Eisenstein criterion at λ gives [M:Q]=5, whereas the prime cyclotomic field degree gives [E:Q]=4. The splitting field F=E(α) is a Galois extension of E, and an automorphism fixing E sends α to ζ5b​α. Thus its Galois group embeds in C5​. It is nontrivial, since M cannot be contained in the degree-four field E. Therefore [F:E]=5 and [F:Q]=20.
Index the five roots by j∈F5​, with root ζ5j​α. Every automorphism has the form ζ5​↦ζ5u​, α↦ζ5b​α and acts on indices as j↦uj+b, with u∈F5×​. This is a faithful inclusion of G=Gal(F/Q) into the affine group over a finite field, and both groups have order 20, so it is an isomorphism. This is the affine Galois group of a prime-radical splitting field.
Let D be the specified decomposition group. Its nonidentity element is an involution. If its linear coefficient were 1, it would be a nontrivial translation, which has order 5. Hence it acts as j↦−j+b. There is exactly one fixed root, at j=b/2, and the other four roots form two pairs. By involution orbits in an affine group of odd prime degree, D has three orbits on the five roots. Those roots identify with G/Gal(F/M), so the double-coset formula gives three primes in M.
For E, the embeddings identify with G/Gal(F/E)≃F5×​. The image of D is {1,−1} and acts by multiplication, giving the two orbits {1,−1} and {2,−2}. Therefore
#{m∣p in M}=3,#{e∣p in E}=2.​
(11)
The orbit argument counts primes whether or not p is ramified; cyclicity of the given order-two decomposition group does not require that it be generated by an unramified Frobenius element.

For a positive integer N, a Dirichlet character ψ modulo N is a group homomorphism (Z/NZ)×→C×, extended periodically to the integers and set equal to zero on integers not coprime to N. Its nonzero values are roots of unity. For Res>1, its Dirichlet L-function is the absolutely convergent Dirichlet series
LN​(ψ,s)=n=1∑∞​nsψ(n)​=p∤N∏​(1−ψ(p)p−s)−1.​
(12)
Complete multiplicativity of ψ proves the Euler product by expanding each factor as a geometric series and applying unique integer factorization; absolute convergence justifies these rearrangements.
Suppose ψ is a nonprincipal Dirichlet character. Choose a unit u with ψ(u)=1. Multiplication by u permutes a complete residue system, so ∑a=1N​ψ(a)=ψ(u)∑a=1N​ψ(a), and the sum is zero. Consequently A(x)=∑n≤x​ψ(n) is bounded: complete periods contribute zero and the remaining incomplete period has at most N terms. The Abel summation formula gives, initially for Res>1,
LN​(ψ,s)=s∫1∞​A(x)x−s−1dx.
(13)
For Res>0 the integral converges, locally uniformly together with its derivatives in s, because on a compact set with Res≥δ>0 the differentiated integrands are bounded by constants times x−1−δ(1+logx)k. Thus holomorphy of a Dirichlet series from bounded partial sums proves
LN​(ψ,s) is holomorphic on Res>0.​
(14)
For a nonreal ψ, the character ψ2 is also nonprincipal. The supplied logarithmic Euler product yields, for real σ>1,
log(ζ(σ)3∣LN​(ψ,σ)∣4∣LN​(ψ2,σ)∣)=∑p​∑k≥1​kpkσ3+4Re(ψ(p)k)+Re(ψ(p)2k)​.
(15)
At a prime dividing N the numerator is 3. At every other prime put ψ(p)k=eiθ; then the numerator is 3+4cosθ+cos2θ=2(1+cosθ)2≥0. This Euler product positivity for L-function nonvanishing proves that the product inside the logarithm is at least one. If LN​(ψ,1)=0, its holomorphy would give LN​(ψ,σ)=O(σ−1). Meanwhile LN​(ψ2,σ) is bounded near one and the Riemann zeta function has a simple pole there. The product would therefore be O(σ−1) and tend to zero, a contradiction.
For a real nonprincipal ψ, suppose instead that LN​(ψ,1)=0. The product H(s)=ζ(s)LN​(ψ,s) would then be holomorphic on the whole half-plane Res>0, because the zero cancels the only zeta pole there. In Res>1 it has coefficients
H(s)=∑n≥1​bn​n−s,bn​=∑d∣n​ψ(d).
(16)
These are nonnegative zeta-times-real-L coefficients. They are multiplicative, and their prime-power values are k+1 if ψ(p)=1, one or zero according as k is even or odd if ψ(p)=−1, and one if ψ(p)=0. In particular bn​≥0 and bm2​≥1 for every m.
The Taylor series of the assumed holomorphic H about 2 converges in the disk ∣s−2∣<2. Termwise differentiation of its absolutely convergent Dirichlet series at 2 gives (−1)kH(k)(2)=∑n​bn​(logn)k/n2. Evaluate the Taylor series at s=1/2 and apply the Tonelli theorem to the resulting nonnegative terms:
H(1/2)=∑k≥0​k!(3/2)k​∑n≥1​n2bn​(logn)k​=∑n≥1​n1/2bn​​≥∑m≥1​m1​=∞.
(17)
This contradicts holomorphy at 1/2. The argument is the positive-coefficient mechanism of the Landau theorem for a Dirichlet series with nonnegative coefficients, with the needed step proved explicitly. Combining the real and nonreal cases gives the nonvanishing of a nonprincipal Dirichlet L-function at one:
LN​(ψ,1)=0for every nonprincipal ψ.​
(18)
Now let a be coprime to N. The Dirichlet theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions asserts that infinitely many prime numbers satisfy p≡a(modN). To prove it, separate the first-power terms in the given logarithmic Euler product:
logLN​(ψ,σ)=∑p∤N​pσψ(p)​+Rψ​(σ),σ>1.
(19)
For σ≥1, the remainder is bounded uniformly by
∣Rψ​(σ)∣≤∑p​∑k≥2​p−k≤∑n=2∞​n(n−1)1​=1.
(20)
For a nonprincipal character, holomorphy and nonvanishing near one give a local holomorphic logarithm. Along a short real interval to the right of one, this logarithm and the supplied continuous logarithm differ by a fixed integer multiple of 2πi. Hence the prime-character sum near one is bounded:
∑p∤N​ψ(p)p−σ=O(1),ψ=ψ0​.
(21)
For the principal Dirichlet character ψ0​,
LN​(ψ0​,σ)=ζ(σ)∏p∣N​(1−p−σ),∑p∤N​p−σ=logσ−11​+O(1).
(22)
The latter follows from the simple zeta pole and the bounded remainder. Finally Orthogonality of Dirichlet characters gives
∑p primep≡a(modN)​​p−σ=φ(N)1​∑ψmodN​ψ(a)​∑p∤N​ψ(p)p−σ=φ(N)1​logσ−11​+O(1).

Here φ is the Euler totient function. The right-hand side tends to infinity as σ↓1, whereas a finite set of primes would give a bounded left-hand side. Therefore
gcd(a,N)=1⟹infinitely many primes p≡a(modN).​

Realize the two-dimensional irreducible representation ρ as the standard representation of the symmetric group on
V={(z1​,z2​,z3​)∈C3:z1​+z2​+z3​=0},
(25)
with S3​ permuting coordinates. It is the complement of the trivial summand in the three-point permutation representation. For each prime the local factor of an Artin L-function is
Lp​(ρ,s)=det(1−p−sρ(Frobp​)∣VIp​​)−1,
(26)
where Ip​ is the inertia group and Frobenius means any lift of the residue-field Frobenius automorphism. On VIp​ the lift is independent of that choice. At an unramified prime, put t=p−s. The Local Artin factors of the standard representation of S3 are
Frobenius cycle type(1)(2)(3)(12)(123)​eigenvalues on V1,11,−1ζ3​,ζ32​​Lp​(ρ,s)(1−t)−2(1−t2)−1(1+t+t2)−1.​​
(27)
The polynomial discriminant of X3−3 is −243=−35, so only 3 can ramify in its splitting field. For p=3, the squarefree factorization of X3−3 modulo p determines the Frobenius cycle type on its roots.
At p=2,
X3−3≡(X+1)(X2+X+1)(mod2).
(28)
The quadratic is irreducible, so Frobenius is a transposition and
L2​(ρ,s)=1−2−2s1​=1+2−2s+2−4s+⋯.
(29)
Thus a2​=0, a4​=1 and a8​=0. At p=5,
X3−3≡(X−2)(X2+2X+4)(mod5).
(30)
The quadratic discriminant is 3(mod5), a nonsquare, so this is again a transposition. Consequently L5​(ρ,s)=(1−5−2s)−1 and a5​=0.
At p=7, the possible cubes are 0,1,−1, so X3−3 has no root modulo 7. Being cubic, it is irreducible; Frobenius is a three-cycle. Therefore
L7​(ρ,s)=1+7−s+7−2s1​=1−7−3s1−7−s​=1−7−s+O(7−3s),
(31)
and a7​=−1.
The ramified factor at 3 must be computed on inertia group invariants. Write α=31/3 and set π=(ζ3​−1)/α. Using (ζ3​−1)2=−3ζ3​ gives
π3=1+2ζ3​,π6=−3.
(32)
The Eisenstein polynomial X6+3 over Q3​ has degree six, so Q3​(π) is a totally ramified degree-six subfield of Q3​(ζ3​,α). The latter has degree at most [F:Q]=6, so the fields agree. This Eisenstein sextic presentation of the splitting field of X3 minus 3 over Q3 shows D3​=I3​=S3​. Since the only permutation-invariant vectors in C3 have all coordinates equal, their intersection with V is zero. Thus
VI3​=0,L3​(ρ,s)=1.​
(33)
In particular a3​=a9​=0; an unramified degree-two factor at 3 would give the wrong coefficients.
Finally the Euler product makes its Dirichlet series coefficients multiplicative for coprime indices. Its constant coefficient is a1​=1, and a6​=a2​a3​=0, a10​=a2​a5​=0. Every integer up to ten is now covered, giving
(a1​,a2​,a3​,a4​,a5​,a6​,a7​,a8​,a9​,a10​)=(1,0,0,1,0,0,−1,0,0,0).​
