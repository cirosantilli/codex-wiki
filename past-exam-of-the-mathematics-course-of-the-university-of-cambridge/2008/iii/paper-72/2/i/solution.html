The stated kinematics do not, by themselves, fix a galaxy's intrinsic axis ratio. The requested curve is the usual simplified oblate-rotator flattening proxy; its additional geometric and streaming conventions should be explicit. Put q=b/a=1−e and adopt the approximate moment prescription
q≃σx2​+vp2​σz2​​.
(13)
Here vp2​ is the ordered support assigned to one in-plane component. Using σz2​=(1−β)σx2​ and solving gives
σx​vp​​≃1−ee−β​​.​
(14)
In this prescription, isotropic random motions give the oblate isotropic rotator curve; positive β allows flattening with less rotation. A real solution requires 0≤e<1, β<1, and e≥β. Equality e=β gives no rotation. This is a useful diagnostic approximation, not an exact identity for arbitrary density distributions.
To see what the tensor virial theorem actually requires, take a finite stationary axisymmetric system with mass-weighted constant velocity dispersions and azimuthal streaming speed vaz​. Since ux​=−vaz​sinϕ, the azimuthal average has ⟨ux2​⟩=vaz2​/2. The xx and zz components give
−Wxx​=M(σx2​+vaz2​/2),−Wzz​=Mσz2​,
(15)
and therefore, with the oblate tensor-virial shape factor Q=Wxx​/Wzz​,
σx2​vaz2​​=2[(1−β)Q−1].​
(16)
For similar oblate self-gravitating density surfaces, write s=1−q2​. The ellipsoidal force coefficients, obtained by integrating the Newtonian force over those surfaces, are
A1​=s3qarccosq​−s2q2​,A3​=s22​−s32qarccosq​,Q=q2A3​A1​​.
(17)
The common radial integral in the stellar potential-energy tensor cancels, but these shape coefficients remain. At q=1/2, Q≃1.79, rather than 1/q=2. With β=0, the intrinsic azimuthal ratio is about 1.26, whereas the printed simplified ratio is 1. Recovering the latter uses both vp​=vaz​/2​ and Q≃1/q. Thus, if the printed v literally means intrinsic azimuthal speed, its claimed exact relation omits necessary model assumptions and a streaming normalization.
