The qualitative prediction of galactic chemical evolution is useful: later stellar populations tend to inherit more enriched gas, and old stellar populations can preserve low stellar metallicity. The closed-box metallicity distribution, however, predicts many more metal-poor long-lived dwarfs in the solar neighbourhood than are observed. This is the G-dwarf problem, also apparent with other long-lived tracers. A single well-mixed box cannot describe the different age and abundance distributions of the galactic disk, galactic bulge, and stellar halo of the Milky Way. Nor need its exponentially declining star formation rate match the history of ongoing disk star formation.
Prolonged relatively metal-poor galactic gas inflow supplies fuel while diluting the enrichment, allowing many stars to form after the earliest enrichment stage; pre-enrichment of the initial reservoir also removes a pristine tail. Galactic outflows lower the effective yield, but a constant well-mixed outflow alone still gives an exponential metallicity distribution function, and is not automatically a cure for the G-dwarf problem. Spatially varying star formation, radial transport and migration, and selection of local survivors complicate a comparison with one global box. A varying initial mass function or stellar yield changes the enrichment law. Finally, finite stellar lifetimes and Type Ia supernovae make iron enrichment delayed, so an observed iron-abundance distribution is not interchangeable with the instantaneous total-metal distribution. Gas exchange, spatially distinct populations, and delayed enrichment are essential qualifications to the closed-box prediction.
