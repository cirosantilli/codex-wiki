For the period lattice Λ=Zω1​+Zω2​, define the Weierstrass elliptic function by
℘(z)=z21​+∑0=ω∈Λ​((z−ω)21​−ω21​).
(1)
On a compact set away from Λ, the summand is O(∣ω∣−3) uniformly for sufficiently large ∣ω∣. The permitted lattice convergence estimate therefore gives Normal convergence of the Weierstrass elliptic-function series. Consequently ℘ is a meromorphic function, with a double pole of principal part (z−ω)−2 at each lattice point and no other poles. Termwise differentiation gives the normally and absolutely convergent series
℘′(z)=−2∑ω∈Λ​(z−ω)−3.
(2)
Pairing ω with −ω shows that ℘ is even and ℘′ is odd. Reindexing the absolutely convergent derivative series gives ℘′(z+λ)=℘′(z) for every λ∈Λ. Thus ℘(z+λ)−℘(z) is constant, with removable singularities at the lattice points. For either basis period λ=ω1​,ω2​, evaluate at z=−λ/2: evenness makes this constant zero. These two periods imply all periods in Λ. Hence ℘ is a nonconstant elliptic function with period lattice Λ.
To obtain the Laurent coefficients of the Weierstrass elliptic function, take 0<∣z∣<minω=0​∣ω∣ and expand
(z−ω)21​−ω21​=∑j≥1​(j+1)ωj+2zj​.
(3)
The expansion and lattice summation can be interchanged uniformly on smaller disks, using the same convergent majorant. The odd powers cancel in pairs. In terms of the lattice Eisenstein sums, the answer is
℘(z)=z−2+r≥1∑​(2r+1)G2r+2​(Λ)z2r=z−2+3G4​z2+5G6​z4+O(z6).​
(4)
Only the absolutely convergent lattice Eisenstein sums of weights at least four occur here; no conditionally defined G2​ is needed.
Put g2​=60G4​ and g3​=140G6​. The Laurent series just found gives
℘′(z)2−4℘(z)3=−60G4​z−2−140G6​+O(z2).
(5)
Therefore F=℘′2−4℘3+g2​℘+g3​ has no pole at zero, and its value there is zero. By periodicity it has no poles anywhere. An entire elliptic function is bounded on a closed fundamental parallelogram, hence on the plane, and is constant by Liouville theorem. Since F(0)=0, this proves the Weierstrass elliptic differential equation
℘′2=4℘3−g2​℘−g3​.
(6)
We still need to identify the three roots without assuming their distinctness. For a nonconstant elliptic function u, the argument principle applied to u′/u around a fundamental parallelogram makes the opposite-edge integrals cancel. Thus its zeros and poles, counted with multiplicity, have equal total number. In particular ℘−a has exactly two zeros in a period cell for every finite a.
Let hi​=ωi​/2, with ω3​=−ω1​−ω2​. Their classes are the three nonzero two-torsion points of a complex torus. Oddness and periodicity give ℘′(hi​)=−℘′(hi​), so ℘′(hi​)=0. Thus ℘−ei​, where ei​=℘(hi​), has a zero of multiplicity at least two at hi​, and the preceding count says its multiplicity is exactly two. If two of the half-period values of the Weierstrass elliptic function were equal, the same function would have at least four zeros, a contradiction. Evaluating the Weierstrass elliptic differential equation at the three half-periods now gives three distinct roots of its cubic. Consequently
℘′(z)2=4i=1∏3​(℘(z)−ei​).​
(7)
For the remaining identity, fix i and put Ai​=(ei​−ej​)(ei​−el​), where {i,j,l}={1,2,3}. The elliptic function
Hi​(z)=(℘(z+hi​)−ei​)(℘(z)−ei​)
(8)
has possible poles only at z=0,−hi​ modulo Λ. At each, the other factor has an exact double zero, so these poles are removable. Thus Hi​ is constant, and its limit at zero is ℘′′(hi​)/2. Differentiating the cubic differential equation first where ℘′=0, then extending the resulting identity holomorphically, gives ℘′′=6℘2−g2​/2. Since ei​ is a root of P(X)=X3−g2​X/4−g3​/4, we obtain
21​℘′′(hi​)=3ei2​−4g2​​=P′(ei​)=Ai​,℘(z+hi​)=ei​+℘(z)−ei​Ai​​.
(9)
This proves the Weierstrass half-period translation formula by pole cancellation. Set ui​=hi​/2=ωi​/4. Since ui​+hi​≡−ui​(modΛ), evenness gives ℘(ui​+hi​)=℘(ui​), and therefore
Ai​=(℘(ui​)−ei​)2.
(10)
The denominator here is nonzero: the only zero of ℘−ei​ modulo Λ is hi​, whereas ui​ is not congruent to hi​. Finally differentiate the Weierstrass half-period translation formula and use z−hi​≡z+hi​(modΛ) to obtain the Weierstrass quarter-period derivative identity
℘′(z)℘′(z−ωi​/2)​=−(℘(z)−ei​℘(ωi​/4)−ei​​)2.​
(11)
The equality holds wherever these quotients are initially defined, and hence as an identity of meromorphic functions at all remaining points.

For the full modular group Γ=SL2​(Z) and integer weight k, a modular form is a holomorphic function on the upper half-plane satisfying
f(cτ+daτ+b​)=(cτ+d)kf(τ)for all (ac​bd​)∈Γ,
(12)
and holomorphic at the cusp at infinity. Explicitly, periodicity under τ↦τ+1 gives a Fourier expansion of a modular form in q=e2πiτ, and holomorphy at the cusp means f=∑n≥0​an​qn. A cusp form has a0​=0. All cusps are equivalent for this group. The matrix −I forces f=(−1)kf, so every odd-weight modular form is zero.
There is a corresponding qualification to the displayed Eisenstein series formula: the Bernoulli-normalized formula is for even integers k≥4. For odd integers k>2, pairing (m,n) with (−m,−n) gives Gk​=0, while Bk​=0, so the printed expression divided by Bk​ is undefined. We prove the asserted nonzero formula in its even-weight range.
On each compact set in the upper half-plane, the real-linear map (m,n)↦mτ+n has a uniformly bounded inverse. Hence ∣mτ+n∣≥C(m2+n2)1/2 there. For k>2, lattice convergence gives locally uniform absolute convergence of Gk​, and therefore holomorphy. If γ=(ac​bd​)∈Γ, then
mγτ+n=cτ+d(ma+nc)τ+(mb+nd)​.
(13)
The map (m,n)↦(ma+nc,mb+nd) permutes the nonzero integer pairs. Reindexing proves the weight-k transformation law.
To compute the Fourier expansion of a normalized Eisenstein series, start from the cotangent partial-fraction expansion
πcot(πz)=z1​+∑n≥1​(z+n1​+z−n1​).
(14)
For Imz>0, the cotangent Fourier expansion is πcot(πz)=−πi−2πi∑r≥1​e2πirz. Differentiating both normally convergent expressions k−1 times gives
∑n∈Z​(z+n)−k=(k−1)!(−2πi)k​∑r≥1​rk−1e2πirz.
(15)
For even k, the terms with m=0 sum to 2ζ(k), and the terms with m<0 duplicate those with m>0. Substituting z=mτ in the last identity and collecting N=mr gives
Gk​(τ)=2ζ(k)+(k−1)!2(2πi)k​∑N≥1​σk−1​(N)qN,σk−1​(N)=∑d∣N​dk−1.
(16)
The rearrangement is absolutely convergent for every ∣q∣<1, since the divisor function grows at most polynomially.
For completeness, the normalization follows directly from the definition of the Bernoulli numbers. Near zero, the cotangent partial-fraction expansion gives
πcot(πz)=z−1−2∑r≥1​ζ(2r)z2r−1.
(17)
On the other hand πcot(πz)=πi+2πi/(e2πiz−1). Substitute t=2πiz in the generating function of the Bernoulli numbers. The term B1​=−1/2 cancels πi, and the higher odd terms vanish because t/(et−1)+t/2 is even. Comparison of Laurent coefficients proves the Euler evaluation of even zeta values
2ζ(k)=−k!Bk​(2πi)k​(k even, k≥2).
(18)
Thus
Gk​(τ)=2ζ(k)(1−Bk​2k​n≥1∑​σk−1​(n)qn),k≥4 even.​
(19)
This Fourier series also proves holomorphy at infinity, completing the modular form verification.
For the Ramanujan congruence modulo 691, the standard facts we use are that the modular discriminant is the weight-twelve cusp form Δ=q∏n≥1​(1−qn)24=∑n≥1​τ(n)qn, and that S12​=CΔ. The latter also follows from the valence formula for the modular group: a nonzero weight-twelve modular form has total weighted zero order one, so a cusp form whose coefficient of q vanishes would have cusp order at least two and must be zero. The product makes the Ramanujan tau function integer-valued.
Write Ek​=Gk​/(2ζ(k)). Using B4​=−1/30 and the supplied B12​=−691/2730, we have
E4​=1+240∑n≥1​σ3​(n)qn,E12​=1+69165520​∑n≥1​σ11​(n)qn.
(20)
Both E12​ and E43​ have weight twelve and constant term one. Their difference is a cusp form, so is a multiple of Δ. Its coefficient of q is 65520/691−720=−432000/691, which determines that multiple:
691E12​=691E43​−432000Δ.
(21)
The coefficients of E43​ are integers. Comparing the positive-index Fourier coefficients and reducing modulo 691 therefore gives 65520σ11​(n)≡−432000τ(n). Both numerical constants are congruent to 566 modulo 691, and gcd(566,691)=1. Cancellation proves
τ(n)≡σ11​(n)(mod691)(n≥1).​
(22)

The Hecke operators organize modular forms simultaneously by their transformation law and their Fourier coefficients. Work with Γ=SL2​(Z), Mk​ its space of weight-k modular forms, and Sk​ its cusp form subspace, with even k≥4. Odd weights give zero spaces. The normalization below makes the first coefficient of Tn​f equal to the nth coefficient of f, which is the key link between operators and arithmetic.
For A=(ac​bd​) with positive determinant, the determinant-normalized slash operator for modular forms is
(f∣k​A)(τ)=(detA)k/2(cτ+d)−kf(Aτ).
(23)
The automorphy factor identity proves (f∣k​A)∣k​B=f∣k​(AB). Positive scalar matrices act trivially. Let An​ be the integral matrices of determinant n>0. Left multiplication by Γ reduces their first column, by Bezout identity, to (a,0) with a>0; the determinant then gives d=n/a>0. A row shear makes 0≤b<d. These are unique determinant-n matrix representatives for Hecke operators, so define
Tn​f=nk/2−1∑A∈Γ\An​​f∣k​A=nk−1∑ad=n​d−k∑b=0d−1​f(daτ+b​).
(24)
Right multiplication by any element of Γ permutes these left cosets. The slash operator for modular forms identity therefore proves that Tn​f has the same modular transformation law as f. Each summand is holomorphic on the upper half-plane. Its cusp behavior follows explicitly from the coefficient calculation below, so Tn​ preserves both Mk​ and Sk​.
Insert f=∑r≥0​ar​qr in the sum. The finite sum over b is zero unless d∣r, in which case it is d. Writing r=dℓ leaves the term adℓ​qaℓ with multiplier nk−1d1−k=ak−1. Thus the Fourier coefficients of a composite-index Hecke operator are
am​(Tn​f)=a∣gcd(m,n)∑​ak−1amn/a2​(f).​
(25)
For m=0 use gcd(0,n)=n. In particular the constant coefficient becomes σk−1​(n)a0​, so cusp forms remain cusp forms, and
a1​(Tn​f)=an​(f),Tp​f=pk−1f(pτ)+p1​∑b=0p−1​f(pτ+b​)
(26)
for a prime number p. The absence of negative powers proves cusp holomorphy. Notice that no primality assumption is needed in the composite-index formula.
The Hecke multiplication relations can be derived directly, rather than assumed from the coset description. On Fourier series define auxiliary maps Up​(∑am​qm)=∑apm​qm and Vp​f(q)=f(qp). They need not separately preserve Mk​, but Up​Vp​=1. With c=pk−1, the coefficient formula gives
Tpr​=∑j=0r​cjVpj​Upr−j​,Tp​=Up​+cVp​.
(27)
Multiplying on the left by Up​+cVp​, the term j=0 in the Up​ sum contributes Upr+1​; the terms with j≥1 become cjVpj−1​Upr−j​. Together with the cVp​ sum this is exactly Tpr+1​+cTpr−1​. Hence the Hecke prime-power recurrence from q-shift operators is
Tp​Tpr​=Tpr+1​+pk−1Tpr−1​(r≥1),T1​=1.
(28)
For coprime m,n, substituting the coefficient formula twice gives a sum over a∣gcd(m,r) and b∣gcd(n,r) with coefficient (ab)k−1amnr/(ab)2​(f). The divisors a,b correspond uniquely to a divisor ab∣gcd(mn,r), so Tm​Tn​=Tmn​. The prime-power recurrence, by induction on the smaller exponent, gives
Tpr​Tps​=∑j=0min(r,s)​pj(k−1)Tpr+s−2j​.
(29)
Combining the coprime and prime-power formulas proves
Tm​Tn​=d∣gcd(m,n)∑​dk−1Tmn/d2​.​
(30)
In particular the Hecke operators commute, and the modular Hecke algebra is generated by the prime operators.
The analytic reason for simultaneous diagonalization is the Petersson inner product
⟨f,g⟩=∫Γ\H​f(τ)g(τ)​ykdμ,dμ=y2dxdy​,
(31)
which is positive definite on Sk​ and convergent because of exponential cusp decay. Here is the adjoint argument. For a matrix A, set HA​=Γ∩A−1ΓA. The left cosets in ΓAΓ are represented by Aγ with γ∈HA​\Γ. Unfolding this finite coset sum in the Petersson inner product gives
⟨∑γ∈HA​\Γ​f∣k​Aγ,g⟩=∫HA​\H​(f∣k​A)(τ)g(τ)​ykdμ.
(32)
Under z=Aτ, hyperbolic area is unchanged and the determinant factors in the slash operator for modular forms give
(f∣k​A)(τ)g(τ)​yk=f(z)(g∣k​A−1)(z)​(Imz)k.
(33)
The integration group becomes AHA​A−1=Γ∩AΓA−1. Folding this finite cover back to Γ\H identifies the adjoint with the correspondence for A−1. For A∈An​, the matrix adj(A)=nA−1 is again integral with determinant n; scalar matrices act trivially, and adjugation permutes the double cosets making up An​. The real normalizing factor nk/2−1 is unchanged. Thus this proves that Hecke operators are self-adjoint for the Petersson inner product:
⟨Tn​f,g⟩=⟨f,Tn​g⟩.
(34)
The valence formula for the modular group makes Sk​ a finite-dimensional vector space. Commuting self-adjoint operators on a finite-dimensional inner product space have a common orthogonal eigenbasis: diagonalize one operator, use commutativity to preserve each of its eigenspaces, and repeat within those spaces. Therefore Sk​ has a basis of Hecke eigenforms, with real Hecke operator eigenvalues.
If f is a common Hecke eigenform, say Tn​f=λn​f, its first Fourier coefficient cannot vanish: a1​=0 would imply an​=a1​(Tn​f)=λn​a1​=0 for every n. Normalize a1​=1. Then the Hecke operator eigenvalue λn​ is precisely the Fourier coefficient an​. The Hecke multiplication relations become
am​an​=∑d∣gcd(m,n)​dk−1amn/d2​,apr+1​=ap​apr​−pk−1apr−1​.
(35)
Consequently the coefficients are multiplicative at coprime indices, and their local generating function satisfies
∑r≥0​apr​Xr=1−ap​X+pk−1X21​.
(36)
This gives the Euler product of a Hecke eigenform
L(f,s)=p∏​(1−ap​p−s+pk−1−2s)−1​
(37)
in a half-plane of absolute convergence. The Fourier coefficient bound for a cusp form already supplies such a half-plane; Question 4 improves it to Res>(k+1)/2.
The L-function of a cusp form also has an analytic description independent of the Hecke eigenform condition. Termwise Mellin transform in an initial right half-plane gives
Λ(f,s)=(2π)−sΓ(s)L(f,s)=∫0∞​f(iy)ys−1dy.
(38)
At infinity the cusp form decays exponentially. Modular inversion gives f(i/y)=(iy)kf(iy), so at zero it is O(y−ke−2π/y). The integral therefore converges locally uniformly for every s, continuing Λ to an entire function. Changing y to 1/y proves
Λ(f,s)=ikΛ(f,k−s).​
(39)
For a normalized Hecke eigenform, the Euler product of a Hecke eigenform and this functional equation describe the same completed L-function of a cusp form.
There is also an integral arithmetic structure. One standard consequence of the level-one dimension formula and the valence formula for the modular group is the integral triangular basis of level-one modular forms: if k=12m+s with s∈{0,4,6,8,10,14}, choose Fs​=1,E4​,E6​,E42​,E4​E6​,E42​E6​ respectively. Then
ΔjE43(m−j)​Fs​,0≤j≤m,
(40)
form a basis of Mk​, and the terms 1≤j≤m form a basis of Sk​. Their first nonzero terms are qj with coefficient one and all coefficients are integers. Successive elimination of coefficients of q,q2,…,qm shows that these cusp basis elements generate exactly the lattice of rational cusp forms with integral Fourier coefficients. The composite-index coefficient formula preserves this lattice, so each Tn​ has an integral matrix on it. Its characteristic polynomial is monic with integral coefficients. This proves that Hecke eigenvalues are algebraic integers; self-adjointness also makes every root of that characteristic polynomial real. The field generated by the eigenvalues is a finite totally real number field: a finite set of operators spans the finite-dimensional rational modular Hecke algebra, and every conjugate of each eigenvalue remains a root of its real-rooted characteristic polynomial.
Two basic examples connect the operator theory to familiar modular forms. The normalized Eisenstein series Ek​ has coefficients proportional to σk−1​(n). At a prime number its coefficient formula uses
σk−1​(pm)+pk−1σk−1​(m/p)=(1+pk−1)σk−1​(m),
(41)
where the second term is zero unless p∣m. This follows by writing m=pru with p∤u and summing a finite geometric series. It proves Tp​Ek​=(1+pk−1)Ek​, and the Hecke multiplication relations give Tn​Ek​=σk−1​(n)Ek​. Meanwhile S12​=CΔ implies Tn​Δ=τ(n)Δ, because a1​(Δ)=1. Thus the Ramanujan tau function is multiplicative and satisfies τ(pr+1)=τ(p)τ(pr)−p11τ(pr−1). For example, τ(2)=−24 gives τ(4)=(−24)2−211=−1472. This illustrates how Hecke operators turn analytic Fourier coefficients into a coherent arithmetic system.

Use the primitive real-analytic Eisenstein series with exactly the normalization in the PDF. Its coset expression uses Γ∞​={±(10​n1​):n∈Z}, so primitive bottom rows differing by sign represent the same coset. The factor 1/2 in the primitive-pair sum accounts for this sign identification. Unlike the sum over all nonzero integer pairs, this Eisenstein series has no additional factor 2ζ(2w). Write w for the Rankin–Selberg integral parameter and s for the Dirichlet series parameter to keep their shift visible.
First establish an initial region where all rearrangements are absolute. The invariant norm of a modular form yk/2∣f(τ)∣ is bounded: it is Γ-invariant, continuous on a truncated fundamental domain, and tends to zero at the cusp. Integrating one period of the Fourier series gives
an​e−2πny=∫01​f(x+iy)e−2πinxdx,∣an​∣≤Cy−k/2e2πny.
(42)
With y=1/n, this proves the Fourier coefficient bound for a cusp form an​=O(nk/2), and similarly bn​=O(nk/2). Hence D(f,g,s) is initially absolutely convergent for Res>k+1.
For Rew>1, the primitive real-analytic Eisenstein series is locally uniformly convergent and holomorphic in w. On the standard fundamental domain it has at most polynomial growth in y, locally uniformly in this half-plane. These are the analytic properties of the Eisenstein series needed here; they can also be checked directly from its defining sum. For example, for real σ>1 and y≥1, majorize the primitive sum by the sum over all nonzero pairs and use
∑n∈Z​((mx+n)2+(my)2)−σ≤Cσ​((∣m∣y)−2σ+(∣m∣y)1−2σ)(m=0).
(43)
This bound follows by comparing a shifted one-dimensional sum with its integral and a largest summand. Summing over m=0 gives E(τ,σ)=O(yσ+y1−σ). For complex w, ∣E(τ,w)∣≤E(τ,Rew), and the estimates are uniform on compact parameter sets. Since f and g decay exponentially on the cusp end, the integral I(f,g,w) is therefore holomorphic throughout Rew>1 by dominated integration.
The product f(τ)g(τ)​yk is Γ-invariant: the two modular factors cancel the transformation Im(γτ)=y/∣cτ+d∣2. With the hyperbolic area dμ=dxdy/y2, Rankin–Selberg unfolding gives, initially for Rew>2,
I(f,g,w)​=∫Γ\H​fg​ykγ∈Γ∞​\Γ∑​(Imγτ)wdμ=∫Γ∞​\H​f(τ)g(τ)​yw+kdμ=∫0∞​∫01​f(x+iy)g(x+iy)​yw+k−2dxdy.​
(44)
Absolute unfolding is justified by replacing fg​ by ∣fg∣ and using the same finite fundamental-domain integral with E(τ,Rew). The subsequent termwise integrations can be justified particularly economically by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality: on each horizontal line,
∫01​∣f(x+iy)g(x+iy)∣dx≤(∑n≥1​∣an​∣2e−4πny)1/2(∑n≥1​∣bn​∣2e−4πny)1/2.
(45)
The coefficient bounds make the right side O(y−k−1) as y↓0, and it decays exponentially as y→∞. Multiplication by yRew+k−2 is integrable at zero when Rew>2. Thus the integral of the inner Fourier series may be evaluated first. Orthogonality of the exponentials gives
∫01​f(x+iy)g(x+iy)​dx=∑n≥1​an​bn​​e−4πny.
(46)
The Mellin transform of each exponential is
∫0∞​e−4πnyyw+k−2dy=(4πn)w+k−1Γ(w+k−1)​.
(47)
In the same initial region the sum of the absolute values of these integrals is finite by an​bn​​=O(nk). Consequently the exact Rankin–Selberg integral identity is
I(f,g,w)=(4π)w+k−1Γ(w+k−1)​D(f,g,w+k−1).​
(48)
In particular the two parameters are not equal: the Dirichlet series argument is shifted by k−1.
The holomorphy of I on Rew>1 now gives the required continuation:
D(f,g,s)=Γ(s)(4π)s​I(f,g,s−k+1),Res>k.​
(49)
The right side agrees with the original Dirichlet series on Res>k+1 and is holomorphic on the larger half-plane. No continuation of the Eisenstein series beyond Rew>1 is needed.
It is important to supply a further argument for absolute convergence: analytic continuation alone does not imply it. For real w>1, both E(τ,w) and ∣f(τ)∣2 are nonnegative, and their fundamental-domain integral is finite by the cusp estimates above. Tonelli theorem therefore unfolds I(f,f,w) even before knowing convergence of the Dirichlet series. On each fixed horizontal line, the Fourier series converges absolutely and uniformly, so integration in x gives
∫01​∣f(x+iy)∣2dx=∑n≥1​∣an​∣2e−4πny.
(50)
A second use of Tonelli theorem, now in y, proves the positive unfolding of a cusp-form square:
I(f,f,w)=(4π)w+k−1Γ(w+k−1)​∑n≥1​nw+k−1∣an​∣2​<∞.
(51)
Since every real s>k has s=w+k−1 with w>1, we have absolute convergence of D(f,f,s) for Res>k. Absolute convergence for complex s follows by taking its real part. In fact Cauchy-Schwarz inequality also gives absolute convergence of D(f,g,s) there from the two corresponding square sums.
Finally let σ>(k+1)/2 and choose k<β<2σ−1. The preceding square-coefficient bound and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality give
∑n≥1​∣an​∣n−σ≤(∑n≥1​∣an​∣2n−β)1/2(∑n≥1​n−(2σ−β))1/2<∞.
(52)
This proves absolute convergence of cusp-form L-series from square coefficients, and hence
L(f,s) converges absolutely for Res>2k+1​.​
(53)
Equivalently, the suggested elementary inequality works by choosing k−σ<α<σ−1: after multiplication by n−σ its two bounds sum to a convergent ∑n−(σ−α) and a convergent ∑∣an​∣2n−(σ+α). The existence of this interval is precisely 2σ>k+1.
