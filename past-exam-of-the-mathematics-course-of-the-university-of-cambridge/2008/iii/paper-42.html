In a parametric statistical model {Pθ​:θ∈Θ}, the likelihood function L(θ;y) measures the relative support given by the observed sample for different parameter values. Maximum likelihood estimation supplies a point estimate; the sampling distributions of suitable statistics calibrate significance tests and confidence intervals. A sufficient statistic retains all parameter-dependent information in the likelihood function, so one first seeks a reduction to such a statistic rather than discarding information arbitrarily.
The characteristic conditioning principle in Fisherian conditional inference is to calibrate inference conditional on the observed value of an ancillary statistic. With no nuisance parameter, a statistic A is ancillary when its law is the same for every θ. Its observed value can nevertheless describe the experimental configuration or precision. Conditional inference uses Lθ​(T∣A=a) for an informative statistic T, with a the observed ancillary value. Since the marginal law of A does not depend on θ, conditioning leaves the parameter-dependent likelihood factor unchanged, while potentially changing the appropriate repeated-sampling reference distribution. Conditioning on a continuous A means using a regular conditional distribution, rather than dividing by P(A=a).
With parameters (ψ,λ), where ψ is the target and λ a nuisance parameter, a fully ancillary statistic has a law independent of both. A partial ancillary statistic for ψ may have a law depending on λ but not on ψ. To eliminate λ by conditioning, one needs the actual conditional law of the informative statistic to be free of λ; partial ancillarity by itself does not guarantee this. For example, if independent counts have Poisson distributions with means λψ and λ(1−ψ), their total N has Poisson distribution with mean λ, independent of ψ. The first count conditional on N=m has binomial distribution with parameters (m,ψ). Conditioning removes the nuisance intensity λ exactly. More generally, nuisance-free pivotal quantities or conditional likelihoods can be used where available; there is no universal conditioning device that removes every nuisance parameter.
For a concrete example of non-uniqueness of maximal ancillary statistics, consider one observed pair (U,V)∈{0,1}2, with
Pθ​(0,0)=Pθ​(1,1)=2θ​,Pθ​(0,1)=Pθ​(1,0)=21−θ​,0<θ<1.
(1)
Both U and V have Bernoulli distribution with success probability 1/2, independent of θ, so each is an ancillary statistic. Their generated partitions are different and incomparable. Moreover, each is maximal: refining either two-point cell singles out a point whose probability is θ/2 or (1−θ)/2, which is parameter-dependent. Their joint statistic is not ancillary, since Pθ​(U=V)=θ. Thus there is no common finer ancillary partition that reconciles the two choices. The conditional laws differ as labelled experiments: Pθ​(V=U∣U)=θ and Pθ​(U=V∣V)=θ, but they condition on different observed information. This illustrates why “condition on a maximal ancillary” need not specify a unique procedure.

μ​=Yˉ=μ+σZˉ,σ=σsZ​,
(2)
and therefore
Ai​=σYi​−μ​​=sZ​Zi​−Zˉ​.​
(3)
For n≥2, sZ​>0 almost surely, because an independent sample from a density has probability zero of all observations coinciding. The right side depends only on a sample from the fixed base law, so the entire vector A has a law independent of (μ,σ): A is ancillary.​ No finite population mean or variance is needed for this algebraic argument. With one observation the displayed standardization is undefined, so at least two observations are implicitly required.
The pair (μ​,σ) together with A reconstructs the full sample through Yi​=μ​+σAi​. Thus conditional inference in a location-scale family uses
Lμ,σ​(μ​,σ∣A=a),​
(4)
where a is the observed residual configuration. Equivalently one can use the parameter-free conditional law of ((μ​−μ)/σ,σ/σ) given A=a. To see this explicitly, the centered residual subspace has dimension n−1, so its radial coordinate t=σ contributes a Jacobian determinant factor proportional to tn−2. The conditional density of μ​=m, σ=t>0 is therefore proportional to
tn−2σ−n∏i=1n​f0​(σm+tai​−μ​).
(5)
Setting u=(m−μ)/σ and v=t/σ gives a conditional density proportional to vn−2∏i​f0​(u+vai​), with no unknown parameter. The normalizing factor can depend on a, but not on μ or σ. These statements concern almost every attainable ancillary value.

βn​=(logn)2,αn​=2logn.​

Let the summands be independent and identically distributed random variables with mean μ, variance σ2>0, and standardized third cumulant γ1​=E[(Y1​−μ)3]/σ3. Define
Sn∗​=σn​∑i=1n​(Yi​−μ)​.
(30)
An Edgeworth expansion refines the central limit theorem using the higher cumulants of one summand. Some smoothness and moment assumptions are essential: finite variance alone does not supply a density expansion. A convenient sufficient set for the expansions here is a smooth density whose derivatives decay faster than every inverse power, together with Eeδ∣Y1​∣<∞ for some δ>0. These assumptions give all required moments, Fourier smoothing and the nonlattice characteristic-function condition. Less restrictive sufficient conditions are possible, but this set covers the logistic model below.
Write ϕ and Φ for the standard normal density and standard normal distribution function. The first-order density Edgeworth expansion is
pSn∗​​(x)=ϕ(x)[1+6n​γ1​​(x3−3x)]+O(n−1).​
(31)
The remainder is uniform on every fixed compact interval under the stated assumptions. To see the origin of the correction, for Z=(Y1​−μ)/σ the cumulant expansion of its characteristic function gives
nlogEeitZ/n​=−2t2​+6n​γ1​(it)3​+O(n−1).
(32)
Exponentiating and using Fourier inversion yields the displayed correction, since the inverse transform of (it)3e−t2/2 is −ϕ(3)(x)=(x3−3x)ϕ(x). The Probabilists' Hermite polynomial convention is being used: H2​(x)=x2−1 and H3​(x)=x3−3x.
The corresponding distribution function expansion is
FSn∗​​(x)=Φ(x)−6n​γ1​​(x2−1)ϕ(x)+O(n−1),​
(33)
uniformly on fixed compact intervals. The sign follows from dxd​[H2​(x)ϕ(x)]=−H3​(x)ϕ(x). These are expansions of both the density and distribution function; a uniform density error alone would not justify integrating an error bound over the entire real line without the accompanying tail control.
For a fixed α∈(0,1), write z=zα​=Φ−1(α). At leading order the proposed quantile expansion gives Φ(p0​(z))=Φ(z), and strict monotonicity gives p0​(z)=z. Substitute yα​=z+p1​(z)n−1/2+O(n−1) into the distribution function expansion. A Taylor expansion at z gives
FSn∗​​(yα​)=α+n​ϕ(z)​[p1​(z)−6γ1​​(z2−1)]+O(n−1).
(34)
Since ϕ(z)>0, the coefficient of n−1/2 must vanish. Thus the first Cornish-Fisher expansion coefficients are
p0​(z)=z,p1​(z)=6γ1​​(z2−1).​
(35)
For the logistic distribution, set Zi​=Yi​−θ. Its density q(z)=ez/(1+ez)2 satisfies q(−z)=q(z) and has exponentially decreasing tails. Symmetry and integrability give EZi​=0, and the supplied integral gives
EZi2​=2∫0∞​(1+ez)2z2ez​dz=3π2​.
(36)
Therefore EYi​=θ, Var(Yi​)=π2/3, and the central limit theorem gives
Tn​=π/3​n​(Yˉ−θ)​d​N(0,1).
(37)

Yˉ−3n​π​z+​≤θ≤Yˉ−3n​π​z−​.
(38)
Consequently the confidence interval

(39)
has limiting coverage Φ(z+​)−Φ(z−​)=1−α. Whether the finite endpoints are included makes no probability difference because the sample mean has a density.
The centered logistic distribution is symmetric, so γ1​=0. Its smooth density and exponentially decreasing derivatives satisfy the conditions stated above; in particular all moments exist and Fourier smoothing is available. The first correction in the Edgeworth expansion vanishes, leaving FTn​​(z)=Φ(z)+O(n−1) at each of the two fixed quantiles. Subtracting gives the stronger conclusion

An exponential dispersion family of order one has density or mass function, relative to a fixed dominating measure, of the form
p(y;θ,ϕ)=exp{ϕyθ−b(θ)​+c(y,ϕ)},
(41)
where θ is a scalar natural parameter, ϕ>0 is the dispersion parameter, and the support does not depend on θ. For regular families, b is twice differentiable on an open natural-parameter domain and b′′>0. Known observation weights w replace ϕ by ϕ/w. “Order one” refers to the scalar canonical statistic y, rather than an arbitrary vector of canonical statistics.
Differentiate the normalization ∫p(y;θ,ϕ)dy=1, or its discrete counterpart, assuming differentiation under the integral is valid. The first derivative gives E(Y−b′(θ))=0. The second derivative gives
0=E[ϕ2(Y−b′(θ))2​−ϕb′′(θ)​].
(42)
Consequently,
μ=EY=b′(θ),Var(Y)=ϕb′′(θ).​
(43)
The variance function expresses the second derivative in terms of the mean:
V(μ)=b′′((b′)−1(μ)),Var(Y)=ϕV(μ)/w.​
(44)
A generalized linear model specifies independent responses from an exponential dispersion family, a linear predictor ηi​=xiT​β, and a differentiable monotone link function relating it to the response mean by g(μi​)=ηi​. The family, variance function, covariate design, link function and dispersion specification together define the model. The canonical link function identifies the linear predictor with the natural parameter:
g(μ)=(b′)−1(μ),θi​=xiT​β.​
(45)
One advantage is that the likelihood equations become particularly simple. With known weights wi​ and common dispersion ϕ, the score function and negative Hessian matrix for β are
Uβ​=ϕ1​∑i​wi​xi​(Yi​−μi​),−ℓββ​=ϕ1​∑i​wi​V(μi​)xi​xiT​.
(46)
The latter does not explicitly depend on the responses, so it equals the expected Fisher information matrix at the same parameter. With full-rank design and positive variances the likelihood is strictly concave in β, simplifying optimization. These statements concern the coefficient block at a fixed dispersion value.
Three explicit examples, taking unit weights, are as follows.
For a normal distribution with mean μ and variance ϕ,
logp(y)=ϕyμ−μ2/2​−2ϕy2​−21​log(2πϕ).
(47)
Thus θ=μ, b(θ)=θ2/2, c(y,ϕ)=−y2/(2ϕ)−21​log(2πϕ), and V(μ)=1. Its canonical link function is g(μ)=μ​, the identity link.
For a Poisson distribution with mean μ>0 and support {0,1,…},
logp(y)=ylogμ−μ−log(y!).
(48)
Here ϕ=1, θ=logμ, b(θ)=eθ, and c(y,1)=−log(y!). Therefore b′(θ)=b′′(θ)=eθ, V(μ)=μ, and the Poisson canonical link is g(μ)=logμ​.
For a Bernoulli distribution with success probability μ∈(0,1),
logp(y)=ylog1−μμ​+log(1−μ),y∈{0,1}.
(49)
Taking ϕ=1, θ=log(μ/(1−μ)), b(θ)=log(1+eθ), and c(y,1)=0 gives the required form. Its mean and variance function are b′(θ)=eθ/(1+eθ)=μ and V(μ)=μ(1−μ). Its canonical link function is g(μ)=log(μ/(1−μ))​, the logit link. The fixed dispersion values in the Poisson and Bernoulli cases are part of those models; an arbitrary continuous dispersion is not being asserted for them.
To test H0​:βj​=0, first fit the unrestricted model and then the restricted model with that component fixed at zero, estimating all nuisance parameters, including dispersion when applicable, in each fit. If ϑ and ϑ denote their full fitted parameter vectors, the likelihood-ratio test uses
D=2{ℓ(ϑ)−ℓ(ϑ)}.​
(50)
For identifiable regular models, an interior null parameter and a fixed-dimensional full-rank design with increasing information, the Wilks theorem gives Dd​χ12​ under H0​. A level-α test rejects for D greater than the (1−α)-quantile of the chi-squared distribution with one degree of freedom.
Alternatively the Wald test uses W=β​j2​/Var(β​j​), with the variance obtained from the appropriate inverse full Fisher information matrix; it has the same limiting χ12​ null law. The score test fits only the restricted model. If λ collects its nuisance parameters, its efficient information is Ijj⋅λ​=Ijj​−Ijλ​Iλλ−1​Iλj​, and the statistic is Uj​(ϑ)2/Ijj⋅λ​(ϑ), again asymptotically χ12​. Accounting for the nuisance block is necessary rather than using the unadjusted diagonal information.

For ordinary homoscedastic fixed-design nonparametric regression, take observations Yi​=g(xi​)+ϵi​ at known distinct sites, with independent mean-zero errors of common variance. A degree-p local polynomial estimator at a target u solves the finite-dimensional weighted least squares problem
β​(u)=argminβ∈Rp+1​∑i​K(hxi​−u​)[Yi​−∑r=0p​βr​(xi​−u)r]2.
(51)
Here K is a nonnegative regression kernel, h>0 is the smoothing bandwidth, and sufficient local design rank ensures uniqueness. The estimate of the regression function is g​(u)=β​0​(u). Each target has its own locally fitted polynomial; this is not one global polynomial fit. The bandwidth controls the localization and the bias-variance tradeoff.
A natural cubic smoothing spline instead solves the global penalized problem
g​λ​=argminv∈H2[a,b]​{∑i​[Yi​−v(xi​)]2+λ∫ab​[v′′(x)]2dx},λ>0.
(52)
The Sobolev space here consists of functions with square-integrable weak derivatives through order two. The minimizer is a natural cubic spline with knots at the observation sites and linear tails outside the extreme sites. Equivalently one may perform the minimization on that finite-dimensional spline space: the minimum roughness property of the natural cubic spline interpolant shows that replacing a candidate by the natural spline with the same site values preserves the data loss and decreases the second-derivative penalty. The smoothing parameter λ controls curvature; it differs from the local bandwidth h.
For the interval-average observation problem, the printed upper limit in the loss is an indexing error: there are only n−1 responses and intervals, so a term with i=n would involve the undefined Yn​ and xn+1​. The coherent loss uses the upper limit n−1. Put Δi​=xi+1​−xi​ and define the linear observation functionals
Li​v=Δi​1​∫xi​xi+1​​v(x)dx,1≤i≤n−1.
(53)
The intended criterion on C1[a,b] is therefore
Sλ​(v)=∑i=1n−1​(Yi​−Li​v)2+λ∫ab​[v′(x)]2dx.
(54)
Unlike the ordinary cubic smoothing spline, this uses interval averages as data and a first-derivative penalty; the resulting spline degree will be two.
Fix any candidate v∈C1[a,b]. By the supplied interpolation property, there is a unique quadratic spline q∈C1[a,b] with knots x1​,…,xn​, constant on both exterior intervals, such that Li​q=Li​v for every i. Put d=v−q. Then
∫xi​xi+1​​d(x)dx=0(1≤i≤n−1).
(55)
On each interior knot interval q′′ is a constant ci​, and on the two exterior intervals q′=0. Integrate q′d′ by parts separately on the intervals. At every knot the boundary contributions cancel because both q′ and d are continuous; the contributions at a and b vanish because q′ is zero there. Thus
∫ab​q′(x)d′(x)dx=−∑i=1n−1​ci​∫xi​xi+1​​d(x)dx=0.
(56)
This orthogonality is the key to the quadratic smoothing spline for interval averages. Expanding the roughness penalty gives
∫ab​[v′(x)]2dx=∫ab​[q′(x)]2dx+∫ab​[d′(x)]2dx.
(57)
The data-fit terms are equal because the interval averages are equal. Consequently,
Sλ​(v)=Sλ​(q)+λ∫ab​[d′(x)]2dx≥Sλ​(q).​
(58)
Equality can occur only if d′=0 everywhere: the derivative is continuous and its squared integral is zero. Then d is constant, and any of its zero interval averages forces that constant to be zero. Therefore every minimizer must equal its matching quadratic spline q.
We conclude that the minimizer is a continuously differentiable quadratic spline with knots at the specified sites and constant tails on both exterior intervals. Existence and uniqueness also follow from the supplied interpolation property. The interval-average map identifies the finite-dimensional space of these quadratic splines with Rn−1, and the criterion becomes a strictly convex quadratic function of that average vector: its data-fit term is ∥Y−y∥2, while its penalty is nonnegative and quadratic. It has a unique minimizer, and the strict equality argument above proves uniqueness in the full function class.
