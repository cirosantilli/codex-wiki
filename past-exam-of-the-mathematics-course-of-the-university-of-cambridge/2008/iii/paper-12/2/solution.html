For a first-order system, it is important to specify the frame in which its coefficients are written. In the simple-pole convention intended here, a Fuchsian linear differential system has, at a finite singular point a,
A(z)=z−aRa​​+Ha​(z),Ha​ holomorphic near a.
(7)
The residue is the matrix Ra​=limz→a​(z−a)A(z), computed entry by entry. Such a point is a regular singular point: on a sector, the coefficient bound ∥A(z)∥≤C/∣z−a∣ and integration along rays give at most power growth of solutions. Intrinsically, a regular singular point means moderate power growth of all solutions on sectors, allowing logarithmic factors. This intrinsic definition is more general than requiring a simple coefficient pole in an arbitrary meromorphic frame.
For A(z)=R/z, a fundamental matrix on a chosen complex logarithm branch is
Φ(z)=exp(Rlogz).
(8)
Differentiating the matrix exponential verifies Φ′=RΦ/z. A counterclockwise circuit replaces logz by logz+2πi, so
Φ⟼ΦM,M=e2πiR,monodromy group ={Mn:n∈Z}.​
(9)
This is the residue and monodromy of a constant Fuchsian system. A change of solution basis conjugates M. Its eigenvalues are e2πiλ for the residue eigenvalues λ; a nontrivial Jordan block supplies the corresponding logarithmic terms in zR. The monodromy does not uniquely recover R, since integer shifts of residue eigenvalues can leave their exponentials unchanged.
The coordinate-independent coefficient is the matrix-valued one-form A(z)dz. With t=1/z, its coefficient is
A(t)=−t−2A(1/t).
(10)
Being Fuchsian at infinity means A(z)=O(z−1), while being ordinary there means A(z)=O(z−2). If the finite simple poles are a1​,…,as​, subtract their principal parts:
H(z)=A(z)−∑j=1s​z−aj​Rj​​.
(11)
Each entry of H is entire and tends to zero at infinity. Liouville's theorem therefore gives H=0. Expanding this partial fraction decomposition at infinity yields
A(z)=j=1∑s​z−aj​Rj​​,R∞​=−j=1∑s​Rj​.​
(12)
Thus the sum of all residues, including the residue at infinity of the one-form, is zero. If infinity is ordinary, R∞​=0 and the finite residues alone sum to zero. Treating A merely as a scalar function of z, without transforming dz, would give the wrong infinity convention.
Put ω=e2πi/3. The possible finite singular points of (B+zC)/(1−z3) are 1,ω,ω2, and
Rζ​=−3ζ2B+ζC​(ζ3=1).
(13)
A point ζ is removable exactly when B+ζC=0 as a matrix. At infinity,
A(t)=1−t3C+tB​,
(14)
which is holomorphic at t=0. Consequently the actual set is S={ζ:ζ3=1, B+ζC=0}, with {1,ω,ω2} the generic set. The finite residues sum to zero, as follows also from ∑ζ3=1​ζ−1=∑ζ3=1​ζ−2=0.
Conversely, suppose a Fuchsian linear differential system has its finite singular points among these three roots and has no singularity at infinity. Then Q(z)=(1−z3)A(z) extends to an entire matrix function, and ordinariness at infinity gives Q(z)=O(z). By the Cauchy estimate, each entry has zero derivatives of order two or higher, so Q(z)=B+zC. The same argument applies to any subset S, with additional cancellations at the omitted roots. This proves the requested normal form.
There is a genuine convention limitation: the normal form is not true under intrinsic regular singularity alone in an unrestricted frame. For example, if N=0 and N2=0, then A(z)=N/(z−1)2 has fundamental matrix I−N/(z−1). Its solutions have only power growth, so 1 is intrinsically a regular singular point, and infinity is ordinary. Nevertheless its coefficient has a double pole and cannot equal (B+zC)/(1−z3). The simple-pole convention makes the preceding residue and normal-form assertions correct.
