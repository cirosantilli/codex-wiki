Let g be the Riemannian metric underlying the Hermitian metric and J its compatible complex structure. The fundamental form of a Hermitian manifold is ω(u,v)=g(Ju,v). It is a real alternating form of type (1,1). At a point choose an orthonormal real coframe x1,y1,…,xn,yn adapted to J, with xj+iyj of type (1,0). Then
ω=∑j=1n​xj∧yj,dVg​=x1∧y1∧⋯∧xn∧yn=n!ωn​.​
(20)
The orientation here is the complex orientation. In coordinates for which g=∑j​((dxj)2+(dyj)2), the same convention reads ω=(i/2)∑j​dzj∧dzˉj.
Use the complex Hodge star operator, the complex-linear extension of the real Hodge star operator. With the pointwise Hermitian inner product linear in its first variable, its defining identity is
α∧∗βˉ​=⟨α,β⟩g​dVg​
(21)
for forms of the same total degree. It maps type (p,q) to (n−q,n−p) and satisfies ∗2=(−1)p+q in real dimension 2n. It commutes with complex conjugation. These conventions distinguish it from the conjugate-linear Hodge star α↦∗αˉ.
Write a top holomorphic-type form as η=aζ1∧⋯∧ζn, where ζj=xj+iyj. On each oriented two-plane, ∗xj=yj and ∗yj=−xj, so the two-dimensional star of ζj is −iζj. In taking the star of their product, regrouping complementary one-form factors across the n two-planes contributes (−1)n(n−1)/2. Thus the Hodge star on top holomorphic forms is
∗η=(−1)n(n−1)/2(−i)nη,∗η=(−1)n(n+1)/2inη.​
(22)
This is pointwise and requires neither holomorphicity of the coefficient a nor closedness of ω.
To compute the formal adjoint, take compactly supported forms α of degree k−1 and β of degree k. The Stokes theorem gives
∫X​dα∧∗βˉ​=(−1)k∫X​α∧d(∗βˉ​).
(23)
Since the degree of d(∗βˉ​) is 2n−k+1, the identity for ∗2 makes the right side equal to ∫X​α∧∗(−∗d∗β)​. Consequently the formal adjoint of d in even real dimension is d∗=−∗d∗. Splitting by type, −∗∂∗ lowers antiholomorphic degree, while −∗∂ˉ∗ lowers holomorphic degree. Orthogonality of distinct types therefore yields
∂ˉ∗=−∗∂∗,∂∗=−∗∂ˉ∗.​
(24)
The argument applies locally with compact support, and globally without support restrictions on a compact manifold without boundary.
The Dolbeault Laplacian is Δ∂ˉ​=∂ˉ∂ˉ∗+∂ˉ∗∂ˉ. A form is ∂ˉ-harmonic when it lies in its kernel. On a compact Hermitian manifold, the adjoint identities imply
⟨Δ∂ˉ​α,α⟩L2​=∥∂ˉα∥L22​+∥∂ˉ∗α∥L22​.
(25)
Thus harmonicity is equivalent to both first-order equations vanishing. The Dolbeault Hodge decomposition on a compact Hermitian manifold states that the harmonic space H∂ˉp,q​ is finite-dimensional and that smooth forms have the orthogonal direct-sum decomposition
Ωp,q(X)=H∂ˉp,q​⊕∂ˉΩp,q−1(X)⊕∂ˉ∗Ωp,q+1(X).
(26)
To deduce the identification with Dolbeault cohomology, let ∂ˉα=0 and decompose α=h+∂ˉu+∂ˉ∗v. The closedness of α makes it orthogonal to the entire image of ∂ˉ∗. Taking its inner product with ∂ˉ∗v and using the orthogonality of the decomposition gives ∥∂ˉ∗v∥2=0. Hence α=h+∂ˉu, so each cohomology class has a harmonic representative. If a harmonic form equals ∂ˉu, its squared norm is ⟨∂ˉ∗h,u⟩=0, so the representative is unique. We have proved
H∂ˉp,q​⟶H∂ˉp,q​(X),h⟼[h], is an isomorphism.​
(27)
For the complementary types, define Cα=∗αˉ. It maps (p,q) to (n−p,n−q) and is conjugate-linear. If α is harmonic, then ∂ˉα=0 and ∂(∗α)=0 by the adjoint formula. Conjugating the latter equation gives ∂ˉ(∗αˉ)=0. Also, with k=p+q,
∂ˉ∗(Cα)=−∗∂∗(∗αˉ)=−(−1)k∗∂αˉ=0,
(28)
since ∂αˉ=∂ˉα. Therefore C carries harmonic forms to harmonic forms, and C2=(−1)k makes it a bijection. This proves complementary Dolbeault harmonic types on a Hermitian manifold:
dimC​H∂ˉp,q​=dimC​H∂ˉn−p,n−q​.​
(29)
The metric supplies a natural conjugate-linear isomorphism. If a complex-linear isomorphism is desired, choose a basis hj​ of the first space and send it to the basis Chj​ of the second, extending linearly. This last choice is not canonical; no Kähler assumption is needed for either the dimension equality or the harmonic bijection.
