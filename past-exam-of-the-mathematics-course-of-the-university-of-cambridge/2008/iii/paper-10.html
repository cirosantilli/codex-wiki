The maximal condition on subgroups, denoted max, says that every ascending sequence H1​≤H2​≤⋯ of subgroups eventually stabilizes. Suppose a subgroup H were not finitely generated. Starting with the trivial subgroup, repeatedly choose an element of H outside the subgroup generated so far. This produces a strictly ascending sequence, contradicting max.
Conversely, suppose every subgroup is a finitely generated group. The union H=⋃i​Hi​ of an ascending sequence is a subgroup. A finite generating set of H lies in a single Hm​, since the sequence is ascending. Thus H=Hm​, and Hi​=Hm​ for all i≥m. We have proved
G has max⟺every subgroup of G is finitely generated.​
(1)
For the extension arguments, let N◃G and let π:G→G/N be the quotient map. If N and G/N are finitely generated groups, choose generators a1​,…,ar​ of N and lifts b1​,…,bs​ of generators of G/N. For every g∈G, its image is a word w(π(b1​),…,π(bs​)), so w(b1​,…,bs​)−1g∈N. Therefore
G=⟨a1​,…,ar​,b1​,…,bs​⟩,d(G)≤d(N)+d(G/N).
(2)
Here d denotes the minimal number of generators. If instead N and G/N satisfy the maximal condition on subgroups, take any H≤G. Its intersection H∩N and its image π(H) are finitely generated by the condition in those two groups. The exact sequence 1→H∩N→H→π(H)→1 and the preceding argument make H finitely generated. The equivalence already proved now gives max for G. Hence both finite generation and the maximal condition are preserved by group extensions.
An infinitely generated group of finite Prüfer rank is the additive group Q. A finite set of rational numbers lies in D−1Z for a common denominator D, and its generated subgroup is cyclic: after multiplying by D, use the greatest common divisor of the integer numerators. Every finitely generated subgroup of Q therefore needs at most one generator, while Z needs one. But no finitely generated subgroup contains 1/(2D) once its elements all lie in D−1Z. Thus
Q is not finitely generated and has Pru¨fer rank 1.​
(3)
If G has Prüfer rank r, every finitely generated subgroup of any subgroup K≤G is also a finitely generated subgroup of G, so rkP​(K)≤r. For a quotient G/N, let A=⟨gˉ​1​,…,gˉ​k​⟩ be finitely generated and choose lifts gi​∈G. The subgroup L=⟨g1​,…,gk​⟩ needs at most r generators and maps onto A, so d(A)≤d(L)≤r. This proves the rank bound for every subgroup and quotient.
Now suppose N and G/N have Prüfer ranks r and s. For finitely generated H≤G with H∩N also finitely generated, the extension generating argument gives
d(H)≤d(H∩N)+d(π(H))≤r+s.
(4)
The final request needs an argument even when H∩N is not finitely generated. Write H=⟨h1​,…,hk​⟩. Choose at most s elements x1​,…,xs​∈H whose images generate π(H), omitting unused entries if fewer are needed. For each hi​, choose a word wi​ in these xj​ with the same image and set
ni​=wi​(x1​,…,xs​)−1hi​∈H∩N.
(5)
The finitely generated subgroup K=⟨n1​,…,nk​⟩ of N has generators y1​,…,yr​, again allowing fewer. Each original hi​ belongs to ⟨x1​,…,xs​,y1​,…,yr​⟩, and all these new generators belong to H. Thus d(H)≤r+s without requiring finite generation of the full intersection. Taking the supremum proves the Prüfer rank bound for extensions:
rkP​(G)≤r+s.​
(6)

The free product G1​∗G2​ is a group with homomorphisms from G1​,G2​ such that any pair of homomorphisms Gi​→K extends uniquely to a homomorphism G1​∗G2​→K. Concretely its elements are reduced words with nonidentity syllables alternating between the factors. Multiply by concatenating and then combining adjacent syllables from the same factor, deleting identities. The empty word is the identity, and this normal form theorem for a free product also makes the factor maps injective.
The Klein combination theorem, in its ping-pong form, is as follows. Suppose two nontrivial subgroups A,B act on a set X, with disjoint nonempty subsets XA​,XB​, such that
aXB​⊆XA​(1=a∈A),bXA​⊆XB​(1=b∈B).
(7)
If at least one factor has at least three elements, then ⟨A,B⟩≅A∗B through the natural factor maps. The cardinality condition is needed in this version: two order-two groups can have the same image swapping the two domains.
Here is the proof. Assume ∣A∣≥3. A reduced word beginning and ending with an A-syllable sends XB​ into XA​ by successive application of the displayed inclusions, so cannot act as the identity. A word beginning and ending in B becomes one of the preceding type on conjugating by any nonidentity a∈A. If it begins in A and ends in B, choose a∈A different from both 1 and its first syllable. The word a−1wa remains reduced after combining its first two syllables and begins and ends in A, so again it is nonidentity. If it begins in B and ends in A, apply this last argument to its inverse. Every nonempty reduced word is covered, so the natural homomorphism A∗B→⟨A,B⟩ has trivial kernel. This proves the Klein combination theorem.
Apply it to the two cyclic groups generated by the given Möbius transformations. On X=R∪{∞} take
XA​={x:∣x∣>1}∪{∞},XB​={x:∣x∣<1}.
(8)
Their powers are
fm(x)=x+2m,gm(x)=2mx+1x​(m∈Z).
(9)
For m=0 and ∣x∣<1, ∣x+2m∣≥2∣m∣−∣x∣>1, so fmXB​⊆XA​. For x∈XA​, put 1/∞=0; then
gm(x)1​=2m+x1​,​2m+x1​​>1,
(10)
so gmXA​⊆XB​. The powers displayed also show that both transformations have infinite order. The Klein combination theorem consequently gives
⟨f,g⟩≅Z∗Z≅F2​.​
(11)
For the finite-index formula, realize the free group Fn​ as the fundamental group of the rose with n edges. A subgroup H of index i corresponds to a connected i-sheeted covering graph. Explicitly, its vertices are right cosets Hx and for each basis generator sj​ there is a directed edge Hx→Hxsj​ projecting to the corresponding rose edge. This graph is connected because basis words reach every coset, and the based loops correspond precisely to words in H.
The covering graph has i vertices and ni geometric edges: each positively oriented rose edge has one lift from each vertex. A maximal tree has i−1 edges, and the complementary edges freely generate its fundamental group. Thus H is a free group with the Nielsen–Schreier formula
rank(H)=ni−(i−1)=1+i(n−1).​
(12)
This uses the connected cover associated with H; no claim that arbitrary disconnected covers are connected is needed.
Finally the specified basis of Fn​ gives d(Fn​)≤n. Its abelianization is Zn, by the universal property of the free group, and reduction modulo two gives a quotient (Z/2Z)n. Images of any generating set must span this n-dimensional vector space, so it contains at least n members. Therefore d(Fn​)=n​, and the same argument applies to the free group H above.
Using exactly the unshifted definition printed in the paper, the unshifted rank gradients are determined by
[Fn​:H]d(H)​=n−1+i1​.
(13)
The supremum is n, attained for H=Fn​. For n≥1, mapping one basis generator onto a generator of Z/iZ and the others to zero provides subgroups of every positive index i. Hence the infimum is n−1, approached as i→∞:
upper gradient=n,lower gradient=n−1(n≥1).​
(14)
For the trivial free group F0​, both values are zero. Subtracting one from d(H) would change the upper answer; that is a different convention from the one requested here.

A finitely presented group is one isomorphic to F(X)/⟨⟨R⟩⟩, where X and R are finite, F(X) is the free group on X, and the double brackets mean normal closure. Equivalently, there is a surjection from a free group of finite rank onto G whose kernel is normally generated by finitely many words. The universal property of the free group extends choices of generator images uniquely, and quotienting imposes the chosen relations.
For the semidirect product, choose finite group presentations
N=⟨a1​,…,ar​∣R⟩,H=⟨b1​,…,bs​∣S⟩.
(15)
For each i,j, choose an a-word wji​ representing ϕ(bj​)(ai​). We claim that a finite presentation of a semidirect product is
N⋊ϕ​H≅⟨a1​,…,ar​,b1​,…,bs​∣R,S, bj​ai​bj−1​=wji​ (1≤i≤r,1≤j≤s)⟩.​
(16)
All relations hold in the desired semidirect product, so the presented group maps onto it. We must also show there are no extra elements in the kernel. For inverse conjugations, choose an a-word vji​ representing ϕ(bj​)−1(ai​). Conjugating this word by bj​ using the displayed relations gives an a-word representing ϕ(bj​)ϕ(bj​)−1(ai​)=ai​, so R proves bj−1​ai​bj​=vji​. Thus we can move both positive and negative b-letters to the right, rewriting every word as u(a)v(b). If its image in N⋊H is trivial, projection to H makes v trivial using S; its remaining image in N then makes u trivial using R. The kernel is trivial, proving the claim and finite presentability.
For a poly-infinite cyclic group, write its subnormal series as G=G0​▹G1​▹⋯▹Gn​=1 with infinite cyclic factors. Given a subgroup K≤G, intersect the series with K. The homomorphism K∩Gi​→Gi​/Gi+1​ has kernel K∩Gi+1​, so
(K∩Gi​)/(K∩Gi+1​)↪Gi​/Gi+1​≅Z.
(17)
Each factor is trivial or infinite cyclic; deleting repetitions gives a poly-infinite cyclic series for K. For an extension 1→N→E→Q→1 with N,Q poly-infinite cyclic groups, lift the series of Q to its full preimages in E and append the series of N. The lifted quotient factors are those of Q, and the lower factors are those of N. Normality at every consecutive step is preserved. This proves closure under both subgroups and extensions.
Finite presentability follows by induction on the length of a poly-infinite cyclic series. The trivial group is finitely presented. For a nontrivial group, its first proper term G1​ has a shorter series and G/G1​≅Z. Lift 1∈Z to an element t∈G. Its powers give an injective section of the quotient map, since tk maps to k. Hence G≅G1​⋊⟨t⟩. By induction G1​ is finitely presented, and ⟨t⟩≅Z has a finite presentation. The finite presentation of a semidirect product proved above completes the induction. Thus every poly-infinite cyclic group is finitely presented.
For the final example, take the dyadic affine Baumslag-Solitar group
G=⟨a,t∣tat−1=a2⟩≅Z[1/2]⋊Z,
(18)
where the generator of Z acts on the additive group Z[1/2] by multiplication by two. Here is a verification of this identification, rather than an assumption about the presentation. Put ai​=t−iati for i≥0. The relation gives ai+12​=ai​, so
⟨a0​⟩≤⟨a1​⟩≤⋯.
(19)
Their union A is abelian and is normal: conjugation by t−1 shifts ai​ to ai+1​, while conjugation by t shifts ai​ to ai−1​ for i≥1 and sends a0​ to a02​. Every word can consequently be written aim​tk for some i≥0 and m,k∈Z.
Let a act on the rational line by x↦x+1 and t by x↦2x. These affine transformations satisfy the relation, and aim​tk acts by x↦2kx+m/2i. It is the identity only when k=m=0. Thus the action is faithful and identifies A with all dyadic translations. We have the required series
G▹Z[1/2]▹1,G/Z[1/2]≅Z.​
(20)
Both factors are torsion-free abelian groups, and G is visibly finitely presented. But Z[1/2] is not finitely generated, since finite sets have bounded powers of two in their denominators. Every poly-infinite cyclic group has the maximal condition on subgroups: its factors Z have that condition, and Question 1 proves closure under extensions. Therefore G cannot be poly-infinite cyclic. This gives a finitely presented counterexample to replacing infinite cyclic factors by arbitrary torsion-free abelian factors.

Suppose G is generated by d elements. An index of a subgroup n gives a transitive action of G on n cosets. After labelling them by 1,…,n, this is a homomorphism G→Sn​, and the subgroup is the stabilizer of one label. There are at most (n!)d homomorphisms, since images of the generators determine the map, and at most n point stabilizers for each map. Thus there are finitely many subgroups of each specified finite index, with the coarse bound n(n!)d.
The finite residual is
RG​=⋂N◃G, [G:N]<∞​N.
(21)
It is equivalently the intersection of the kernels of all homomorphisms to finite groups. Intersecting all finite-index subgroups gives the same subgroup, since each contains its normal core, a normal subgroup of finite index. A residually finite group is one with RG​=1, equivalently every nonidentity element survives in a finite image.
A Hopfian group is one whose surjective endomorphisms are all injective, hence automorphisms. Let θ:G→G be surjective, retaining the finite-generation hypothesis. For each n, pulling back by θ sends a normal subgroup of index n to another normal subgroup of the same index. It is injective on this finite set: equality of two preimages implies equality of the subgroups because θ is surjective. Therefore it is a permutation. For every finite-index normal subgroup N, some m≥1 satisfies
θ−m(N)=N.
(22)
It follows that kerθ⊆kerθm⊆N. Intersecting over N proves finite residual contains surjective endomorphism kernels:
kerθ⊆RG​.​
(23)
If G is also a residually finite group, this forces every surjective endomorphism to have trivial kernel, proving that a finitely generated residually finite group is Hopfian.
For the specified Baumslag-Solitar group, use the convention [x,y]=xyx−1y−1 and write
ai​=tiat−i(i∈Z).
(24)
In the abelianization, the defining relation gives 2a=3a, so the image of a is zero, while t generates an unrestricted infinite cyclic group. Thus G/G′≅Z through the exponent sum of t. The kernel is generated by all ai​: any word can be rewritten as a product of conjugates of a followed by the final power of t. Alternatively a=[t,a2]∈G′, so all ai​∈G′, while killing them leaves a cyclic quotient. Therefore
G′=⟨tiat−i:i∈Z⟩.​
(25)
Define θ(a)=a2 and θ(t)=t. This respects the defining relation because
t(a2)2t−1=(ta2t−1)2=a6=(a2)3.
(26)
The image contains a2, t, and ta2t−1=a3, hence contains a=a3(a2)−1 and is all of G. It is not injective. Indeed
c=[tat−1,a]=tat−1ata−1t−1a−1
(27)
maps to [a3,a2]=1. But it is a reduced word in the HNN extension with associated subgroups ⟨a2⟩ and ⟨a3⟩. Its potential subwords ta±1t−1 do not have even exponent, and its potential subword t−1at does not have exponent divisible by three. It has no pinch in an HNN extension, so Britton's lemma gives c=1. We have explicitly exhibited a surjective, noninjective endomorphism.
It remains to treat the whole second commutator subgroup, not just this one kernel element. Set
K=⋃k≥1​kerθk.
(28)
These kernels form an increasing sequence of normal subgroups, so K is a normal subgroup. Every iterate is surjective, and the preceding finite residual argument gives K⊆RG​.
Conjugating the defining relation gives ai+12​=ai3​ for every integer i. Repeated substitution gives, for j>i,
aj2j−i​=ai3j−i​.
(29)
Also θk(ai​)=ai2k​. Thus when k≥j−i,
θk([ai​,aj​])=[ai2k​,aj2k​]=[ai2k​,ai3j−i2k−(j−i)​]=1.
(30)
Every pair of the generating elements of G′ therefore commutes modulo K. The image of G′ in G/K is abelian, which means G′′⊆K. This proves the full statement using iterated endomorphism kernels in BS(2,3):
G′′⊆k≥1⋃​kerθk⊆RG​.​
(31)

We construct an infinite residually finite torsion group using the Golod-Shafarevich infinitude criterion for pro-p groups. Its useful form says that a pro-p presentation on d generators with relators ri​ gives an infinite pro-p group whenever
1−dτ+∑i​τD(ri​)<0for some 0<τ<1.
(32)
Here D is the Magnus degree of a free pro-p group element: map each generator to 1+Xj​ in a noncommutative power-series algebra over Fp​, and take the lowest degree of w−1. The criterion permits infinitely many relators with a convergent weight sum. It is a consequence of the Golod-Shafarevich inequality for the completed group algebra; see Ershov's survey, Theorem 3.1.
Take p=2, d=2, and τ=3/4. Let F be the free pro-p group on a,b, and enumerate all ordinary finite words w1​,w2​,… in these generators and their inverses. Choose integers ei​ sufficiently large that
τ2ei​<2−i−2.
(33)
Form the quotient pro-p group
P=F/⟨⟨wi2ei​​:i≥1⟩⟩​,
(34)
where the bar means the closed normal closure. If the Magnus image of wi​ is 1+vi​, characteristic two gives
(1+vi​)2ei​−1=vi2ei​​,D(wi2ei​​)≥2ei​D(wi​)≥2ei​
(35)
for nonidentity wi​; identity relators contribute zero weight. It follows that
1−2τ+∑i​τD(wi2ei​​)≤1−23​+∑i≥1​2−i−2=−41​<0.
(36)
Thus the Golod-Shafarevich infinitude criterion for pro-p groups proves that P is infinite.
Let G=⟨aˉ,bˉ⟩ be the abstract subgroup generated by the two images inside P. This subgroup is dense because the two images topologically generate the quotient, and it is infinite: a finite subgroup of a Hausdorff profinite group is closed, so could not be dense in infinite P. Every element of G is the image of some ordinary word wi​, and the imposed relation makes its order divide 2ei​. Therefore G is a two-generated torsion group. Finally P is an inverse limit of finite 2-groups. Any nonidentity element of G is nonidentity in P and hence has a nonidentity image in one of those finite groups. Restricting that map to G proves that it is a residually finite group. We have obtained
G is finitely generated, infinite, residually finite, and every element has finite order.​
(37)
The abstract subgroup G is essential here: the argument establishes finite order for ordinary words, not for every topological limit in the ambient pro-p group.
There is no surjective homomorphism from this group to an infinite soluble group. In fact finitely generated soluble torsion groups are finite. To prove this, let S be finitely generated, soluble and torsion, and induct on its derived length. In the abelian case, finitely many generators of finite orders generate a finite group. In general the abelianization S/S′ is a finitely generated abelian torsion group, hence finite. Its kernel S′ therefore has finite index and is finitely generated by Schreier's lemma. Explicitly, for a finite coset transversal T containing 1 and a finite symmetric generating set X, the finitely many elements txtx−1 generate the finite-index subgroup, where the bar denotes the chosen representative of the coset. Now S′ is torsion and has smaller derived length, so induction makes it finite. Hence S is finite as well. Every quotient of G is finitely generated and torsion, so any soluble quotient must be finite.
