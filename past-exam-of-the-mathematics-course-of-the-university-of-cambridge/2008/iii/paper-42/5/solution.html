An exponential dispersion family of order one has density or mass function, relative to a fixed dominating measure, of the form
p(y;θ,ϕ)=exp{ϕyθ−b(θ)​+c(y,ϕ)},
(41)
where θ is a scalar natural parameter, ϕ>0 is the dispersion parameter, and the support does not depend on θ. For regular families, b is twice differentiable on an open natural-parameter domain and b′′>0. Known observation weights w replace ϕ by ϕ/w. “Order one” refers to the scalar canonical statistic y, rather than an arbitrary vector of canonical statistics.
Differentiate the normalization ∫p(y;θ,ϕ)dy=1, or its discrete counterpart, assuming differentiation under the integral is valid. The first derivative gives E(Y−b′(θ))=0. The second derivative gives
0=E[ϕ2(Y−b′(θ))2​−ϕb′′(θ)​].
(42)
Consequently,
μ=EY=b′(θ),Var(Y)=ϕb′′(θ).​
(43)
The variance function expresses the second derivative in terms of the mean:
V(μ)=b′′((b′)−1(μ)),Var(Y)=ϕV(μ)/w.​
(44)
A generalized linear model specifies independent responses from an exponential dispersion family, a linear predictor ηi​=xiT​β, and a differentiable monotone link function relating it to the response mean by g(μi​)=ηi​. The family, variance function, covariate design, link function and dispersion specification together define the model. The canonical link function identifies the linear predictor with the natural parameter:
g(μ)=(b′)−1(μ),θi​=xiT​β.​
(45)
One advantage is that the likelihood equations become particularly simple. With known weights wi​ and common dispersion ϕ, the score function and negative Hessian matrix for β are
Uβ​=ϕ1​∑i​wi​xi​(Yi​−μi​),−ℓββ​=ϕ1​∑i​wi​V(μi​)xi​xiT​.
(46)
The latter does not explicitly depend on the responses, so it equals the expected Fisher information matrix at the same parameter. With full-rank design and positive variances the likelihood is strictly concave in β, simplifying optimization. These statements concern the coefficient block at a fixed dispersion value.
Three explicit examples, taking unit weights, are as follows.
For a normal distribution with mean μ and variance ϕ,
logp(y)=ϕyμ−μ2/2​−2ϕy2​−21​log(2πϕ).
(47)
Thus θ=μ, b(θ)=θ2/2, c(y,ϕ)=−y2/(2ϕ)−21​log(2πϕ), and V(μ)=1. Its canonical link function is g(μ)=μ​, the identity link.
For a Poisson distribution with mean μ>0 and support {0,1,…},
logp(y)=ylogμ−μ−log(y!).
(48)
Here ϕ=1, θ=logμ, b(θ)=eθ, and c(y,1)=−log(y!). Therefore b′(θ)=b′′(θ)=eθ, V(μ)=μ, and the Poisson canonical link is g(μ)=logμ​.
For a Bernoulli distribution with success probability μ∈(0,1),
logp(y)=ylog1−μμ​+log(1−μ),y∈{0,1}.
(49)
Taking ϕ=1, θ=log(μ/(1−μ)), b(θ)=log(1+eθ), and c(y,1)=0 gives the required form. Its mean and variance function are b′(θ)=eθ/(1+eθ)=μ and V(μ)=μ(1−μ). Its canonical link function is g(μ)=log(μ/(1−μ))​, the logit link. The fixed dispersion values in the Poisson and Bernoulli cases are part of those models; an arbitrary continuous dispersion is not being asserted for them.
To test H0​:βj​=0, first fit the unrestricted model and then the restricted model with that component fixed at zero, estimating all nuisance parameters, including dispersion when applicable, in each fit. If ϑ and ϑ denote their full fitted parameter vectors, the likelihood-ratio test uses
D=2{ℓ(ϑ)−ℓ(ϑ)}.​
(50)
For identifiable regular models, an interior null parameter and a fixed-dimensional full-rank design with increasing information, the Wilks theorem gives Dd​χ12​ under H0​. A level-α test rejects for D greater than the (1−α)-quantile of the chi-squared distribution with one degree of freedom.
Alternatively the Wald test uses W=β​j2​/Var(β​j​), with the variance obtained from the appropriate inverse full Fisher information matrix; it has the same limiting χ12​ null law. The score test fits only the restricted model. If λ collects its nuisance parameters, its efficient information is Ijj⋅λ​=Ijj​−Ijλ​Iλλ−1​Iλj​, and the statistic is Uj​(ϑ)2/Ijj⋅λ​(ϑ), again asymptotically χ12​. Accounting for the nuisance block is necessary rather than using the unadjusted diagonal information.
