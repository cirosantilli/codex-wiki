In a parametric statistical model {Pθ​:θ∈Θ}, the likelihood function L(θ;y) measures the relative support given by the observed sample for different parameter values. Maximum likelihood estimation supplies a point estimate; the sampling distributions of suitable statistics calibrate significance tests and confidence intervals. A sufficient statistic retains all parameter-dependent information in the likelihood function, so one first seeks a reduction to such a statistic rather than discarding information arbitrarily.
The characteristic conditioning principle in Fisherian conditional inference is to calibrate inference conditional on the observed value of an ancillary statistic. With no nuisance parameter, a statistic A is ancillary when its law is the same for every θ. Its observed value can nevertheless describe the experimental configuration or precision. Conditional inference uses Lθ​(T∣A=a) for an informative statistic T, with a the observed ancillary value. Since the marginal law of A does not depend on θ, conditioning leaves the parameter-dependent likelihood factor unchanged, while potentially changing the appropriate repeated-sampling reference distribution. Conditioning on a continuous A means using a regular conditional distribution, rather than dividing by P(A=a).
With parameters (ψ,λ), where ψ is the target and λ a nuisance parameter, a fully ancillary statistic has a law independent of both. A partial ancillary statistic for ψ may have a law depending on λ but not on ψ. To eliminate λ by conditioning, one needs the actual conditional law of the informative statistic to be free of λ; partial ancillarity by itself does not guarantee this. For example, if independent counts have Poisson distributions with means λψ and λ(1−ψ), their total N has Poisson distribution with mean λ, independent of ψ. The first count conditional on N=m has binomial distribution with parameters (m,ψ). Conditioning removes the nuisance intensity λ exactly. More generally, nuisance-free pivotal quantities or conditional likelihoods can be used where available; there is no universal conditioning device that removes every nuisance parameter.
For a concrete example of non-uniqueness of maximal ancillary statistics, consider one observed pair (U,V)∈{0,1}2, with
Pθ​(0,0)=Pθ​(1,1)=2θ​,Pθ​(0,1)=Pθ​(1,0)=21−θ​,0<θ<1.

Both U and V have Bernoulli distribution with success probability 1/2, independent of θ, so each is an ancillary statistic. Their generated partitions are different and incomparable. Moreover, each is maximal: refining either two-point cell singles out a point whose probability is θ/2 or (1−θ)/2, which is parameter-dependent. Their joint statistic is not ancillary, since Pθ​(U=V)=θ. Thus there is no common finer ancillary partition that reconciles the two choices. The conditional laws differ as labelled experiments: Pθ​(V=U∣U)=θ and Pθ​(U=V∣V)=θ, but they condition on different observed information. This illustrates why “condition on a maximal ancillary” need not specify a unique procedure.
