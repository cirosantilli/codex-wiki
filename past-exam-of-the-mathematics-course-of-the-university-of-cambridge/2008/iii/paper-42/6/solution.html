For ordinary homoscedastic fixed-design nonparametric regression, take observations Yi​=g(xi​)+ϵi​ at known distinct sites, with independent mean-zero errors of common variance. A degree-p local polynomial estimator at a target u solves the finite-dimensional weighted least squares problem
β​(u)=argminβ∈Rp+1​∑i​K(hxi​−u​)[Yi​−∑r=0p​βr​(xi​−u)r]2.
(51)
Here K is a nonnegative regression kernel, h>0 is the smoothing bandwidth, and sufficient local design rank ensures uniqueness. The estimate of the regression function is g​(u)=β​0​(u). Each target has its own locally fitted polynomial; this is not one global polynomial fit. The bandwidth controls the localization and the bias-variance tradeoff.
A natural cubic smoothing spline instead solves the global penalized problem
g​λ​=argminv∈H2[a,b]​{∑i​[Yi​−v(xi​)]2+λ∫ab​[v′′(x)]2dx},λ>0.
(52)
The Sobolev space here consists of functions with square-integrable weak derivatives through order two. The minimizer is a natural cubic spline with knots at the observation sites and linear tails outside the extreme sites. Equivalently one may perform the minimization on that finite-dimensional spline space: the minimum roughness property of the natural cubic spline interpolant shows that replacing a candidate by the natural spline with the same site values preserves the data loss and decreases the second-derivative penalty. The smoothing parameter λ controls curvature; it differs from the local bandwidth h.
For the interval-average observation problem, the printed upper limit in the loss is an indexing error: there are only n−1 responses and intervals, so a term with i=n would involve the undefined Yn​ and xn+1​. The coherent loss uses the upper limit n−1. Put Δi​=xi+1​−xi​ and define the linear observation functionals
Li​v=Δi​1​∫xi​xi+1​​v(x)dx,1≤i≤n−1.
(53)
The intended criterion on C1[a,b] is therefore
Sλ​(v)=∑i=1n−1​(Yi​−Li​v)2+λ∫ab​[v′(x)]2dx.
(54)
Unlike the ordinary cubic smoothing spline, this uses interval averages as data and a first-derivative penalty; the resulting spline degree will be two.
Fix any candidate v∈C1[a,b]. By the supplied interpolation property, there is a unique quadratic spline q∈C1[a,b] with knots x1​,…,xn​, constant on both exterior intervals, such that Li​q=Li​v for every i. Put d=v−q. Then
∫xi​xi+1​​d(x)dx=0(1≤i≤n−1).
(55)
On each interior knot interval q′′ is a constant ci​, and on the two exterior intervals q′=0. Integrate q′d′ by parts separately on the intervals. At every knot the boundary contributions cancel because both q′ and d are continuous; the contributions at a and b vanish because q′ is zero there. Thus
∫ab​q′(x)d′(x)dx=−∑i=1n−1​ci​∫xi​xi+1​​d(x)dx=0.
(56)
This orthogonality is the key to the quadratic smoothing spline for interval averages. Expanding the roughness penalty gives
∫ab​[v′(x)]2dx=∫ab​[q′(x)]2dx+∫ab​[d′(x)]2dx.
(57)
The data-fit terms are equal because the interval averages are equal. Consequently,
Sλ​(v)=Sλ​(q)+λ∫ab​[d′(x)]2dx≥Sλ​(q).​
(58)
Equality can occur only if d′=0 everywhere: the derivative is continuous and its squared integral is zero. Then d is constant, and any of its zero interval averages forces that constant to be zero. Therefore every minimizer must equal its matching quadratic spline q.
We conclude that the minimizer is a continuously differentiable quadratic spline with knots at the specified sites and constant tails on both exterior intervals. Existence and uniqueness also follow from the supplied interpolation property. The interval-average map identifies the finite-dimensional space of these quadratic splines with Rn−1, and the criterion becomes a strictly convex quadratic function of that average vector: its data-fit term is ∥Y−y∥2, while its penalty is nonnegative and quadratic. It has a unique minimizer, and the strict equality argument above proves uniqueness in the full function class.
