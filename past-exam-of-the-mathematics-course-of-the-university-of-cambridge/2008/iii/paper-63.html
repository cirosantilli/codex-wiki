Use metric signature (+−−−) throughout this solution. Stress-energy conservation for the perfect fluid is ∇b​Tab=0. Expanding it and using metric compatibility gives

Let N=KaKa​​>0 on the fluid region and choose the Killing vector field K to be future-directed. The normalized four-velocity is
Ua=NKa​.​
(4)
The Killing equation implies both Kb∇b​N=0 and
Kb∇b​Ka​=−Kb∇a​Kb​=−21​∇a​(KbKb​).
(5)
Consequently its Killing-flow four-acceleration is
Ub∇b​Ua​=N21​Kb∇b​Ka​=−∇a​logN.
(6)
For a scalar, the Lie derivative is LK​p=Kb∇b​p. Thus the assumed stationarity of the pressure gives Ub∇b​p=0. These are the ingredients needed for hydrostatic equilibrium along a Killing vector; hypersurface orthogonality of K is not needed.

b OurBigBook.com
Substitute the Killing-flow four-acceleration and stationary pressure into the relativistic Euler equation. It gives
∇a​p=−(ρ+p)∇a​logN,N=KcKc​​.​
(7)
For the equation of state of blackbody radiation, p=ρ/3, this becomes dρ=−4ρdlogN. The barotropic hydrostatic first integral therefore gives, on each connected nonvacuum fluid region,
ρN4=C>0,ρ=C(KcKc​)−2.​
(8)
This is the stationary radiation density law. Combining it with the Stefan–Boltzmann law ρ∝T4 also gives the Tolman–Ehrenfest relation TN=constant.
At a regular finite free surface, a smooth Killing vector field has finite norm. Hence C/N4 cannot approach zero there. More globally, its norm has a finite upper bound on a compact regular fluid closure, which bounds the density away from zero whenever C>0. If the timelike norm instead tends to zero, the density diverges. Thus a radiation fluid has no regular free surface: a finite radiation-filled cavity needs a material wall supporting nonzero pressure, rather than an unsupported radiation-vacuum interface.

Take z positive outward and define the separation as upper minus lower position. The Newtonian potential of a point mass gives the acceleration a(z)=−∂z​ϕ=−GM/z2. Expanding its difference across a separation ζ≪z gives the tidal force equation
ζ¨​=a(z+ζ)−a(z)=a′(z)ζ+O(ζ2/z2)≃z32GM​ζ​.
(9)
The upper particle falls slightly less rapidly, so the separation increases. If the upper particle carries the electric charge and the electric field points outward, the extra relative acceleration is qE/m. Reversing the field or placing the charge on the lower particle changes its sign. Write this signed acceleration as ae​ and put k=2GM/z3. With initial relative velocity zero and nearly constant k and ae​, the ordinary differential equation has solution
ζ(t)=ζ0​cosh(k​t)+kae​​[cosh(k​t)−1].
(10)
For kt2≪1, the leading changes due to the tidal force and electric field are respectively
Δζg​≃z3GM​ζ0​t2,Δζe​≃2mqE​t2​
(11)
with the stated sign convention. Thus, for a nonzero fixed electric acceleration,
∣Δζe​∣∣Δζg​∣​≃∣qE∣z32mGM∣ζ0​∣​=∣qE∣2mg​z∣ζ0​∣​,g=z2GM​.
(12)
A laboratory of size L has ∣ζ0​∣≤L, so this ratio tends to zero as L→0. This is local tidal acceleration scaling: gravitational relative acceleration is proportional to separation, whereas the electric difference persists even at coincident positions.
The duration also matters. At fixed time, Δζg​→0 in absolute size, but Δζg​/ζ0​≃(GM/z3)t2 need not tend to zero. To keep the electrically displaced particles in the shrinking laboratory, take ∣ae​∣t2≲L; then ∣Δζg​∣/L≲GML/(z3∣ae​∣)→0. Alternatively a stationary terrestrial laboratory has a free-fall time of order L/g​, giving ∣Δζg​∣/∣ζ0​∣=O(L/z). Both make precise the small spacetime-region limit. An extended, long-running experiment can still measure arbitrarily small tidal forces.
The weak equivalence principle says that freely falling test particles with the same initial data have composition-independent motion. In particular, a freely falling frame removes the common gravitational acceleration of both particles. The electric accelerations depend on charge and cannot be removed for charged and uncharged particles simultaneously by the same change of frame.
The Einstein equivalence principle extends the freely falling laboratory statement to all local nongravitational experiments: their outcomes obey special relativity, with local Lorentz invariance and local position invariance. The strong equivalence principle also includes local gravitational experiments and bodies with significant gravitational binding energy. It asserts universality of their free fall and independence of local experimental outcomes from the laboratory's location and velocity. The two-particle experiment illustrates the local distinction between gravity and a nongravitational force; by itself it does not establish this stronger claim about self-gravitating bodies.
In a metric theory, the common freely falling trajectories are timelike geodesics of a Lorentzian metric. Normal coordinates make gab​=ηab​ and the Christoffel symbols zero at one point. Fermi normal coordinates do the same along a reference timelike geodesic. The residual effects are controlled by the Riemann curvature tensor through geodesic deviation, and enter the local metric at quadratic order in distance. Curvature is a physical tidal field, so no coordinate choice removes it over a finite region.
A relativistic theory should consequently couple matter universally to the metric tensor, recover nongravitational laws in local inertial frames and recover the Newtonian limit. General relativity implements this with the Einstein field equations; the contracted Bianchi identity is consistent with stress-energy conservation. The Equivalence principle motivates this geometric description but does not alone uniquely determine the field equations. Extra gravitational fields may satisfy the Einstein equivalence principle for ordinary matter while failing the strong equivalence principle because local gravitational measurements or self-gravitating trajectories depend on those fields.

For the full unit-radius two-dimensional de Sitter spacetime, the coordinate ranges are
t∈R,χ∈R/(2πZ).​
(27)
One can use 0≤χ<2π with endpoints identified. If the identification is omitted, the same line element describes the universal covering spacetime instead. The periodicity follows from the hyperboloid embedding
X0=sinht,X1=coshtcosχ,X2=coshtsinχ,(X0)2−(X1)2−(X2)2=−1.
(28)
The induced ambient Minkowski metric is precisely the given Lorentzian metric.
Using an affine parameter s, the geodesic Lagrangian is 21​(t˙2−cosh2tχ˙​2). The nonzero Christoffel symbols are
Γtχχ​=sinhtcosht,Γχtχ​=Γχχt​=tanht.
(29)
Thus the geodesic equations and their first integrals are
t¨+sinhtcoshtχ˙​2=0,χ¨​+2tanhtt˙χ˙​=0,​
(30)
J=cosh2tχ˙​=constant,t˙2−cosh2tJ2​=ε,ε∈{1,0,−1}.

Here ε=1 for unit-speed timelike geodesics, 0 for null geodesics and −1 for unit-speed spacelike geodesics. The conserved J is the geodesic conserved quantity from a Killing vector for the angular symmetry.
All geodesics of two-dimensional de Sitter spacetime can be written especially simply in the embedding. If P is the initial point and V the initial tangent, with P2=−1, P⋅V=0, V2=ε, the geodesic has normal ambient acceleration X′′=εX. Therefore
X(s)=⎩⎨⎧​Pcoshs+Vsinhs,P+sV,Pcoss+Vsins,​ε=1,ε=0,ε=−1.​
(32)
These curves stay in the two-plane spanned by P and V, preserve both constraints and have no tangential acceleration. They consequently solve the geodesic equation and exhaust all initial directions.
An isometry can move the chosen point to P=(0,1,0), namely (t,χ)=(0,0). For future-directed timelike geodesics take V=(coshα,0,sinhα), obtaining
sinht=coshαsinhs,tanχ=sinhαtanhs.
(33)
The continuous angular branch has ∣χ∣<π/2, and every real α gives a distinct timelike direction. The two null geodesics have V=(1,0,±1) and
sinht=s,tanχ=±s.
(34)
For spacelike geodesics take V=(sinhα,0,coshα), giving
sinht=sinhαsins,coshtcosχ=coss,coshtsinχ=coshαsins.
(35)
They are closed with period 2π in affine arclength and ∣t∣≤∣α∣. In particular, α=0 is the equatorial geodesic t=0. These three families, with reversals of parametrization allowed, are the requested complete set through the chosen point.
The conformal time substitution gives
T=2arctan(et)−2π​,sinT=tanht,cosT=secht,dtdT​=secht.

Null geodesics are straight lines χ=±T through the origin. The other families have the useful diagram equations
χ(T)=arcsin(tanhαsinT)for timelike geodesics,T(χ)=arcsin(tanhαsinχ)for spacelike geodesics.

For the complete observer χ=0, let d(χ,0)∈[0,π] be the shortest angular distance on the circle. Since light travels one unit of angular distance per unit conformal time, an event at (T,χ) can signal to the observer at some finite future time precisely when
d(χ,0)<2π​−T.​
(39)
Equality defines its future observer event horizon. Likewise an event can receive a signal emitted by the observer precisely when
d(χ,0)<T+2π​,​
(40)
and equality defines the past horizon. In the lift −π≤χ≤π, their branches are χ=±(π/2−T) and χ=±(T+π/2). The common communication region is the diamond ∣χ∣+∣T∣<π/2, the observer's static patch of de Sitter spacetime. These are the observer horizons in two-dimensional de Sitter spacetime. Every complete timelike observer has analogous horizons after a de Sitter isometry; they depend on the observer, rather than enclosing an intrinsic black-hole region.
Despite these horizons, each constant-T circle is a Cauchy hypersurface of the full spacetime: any inextendible causal curve crosses it exactly once. The global spacetime is therefore globally hyperbolic and has no Cauchy horizons. The conformal boundaries lie at infinite proper time for complete timelike geodesics and infinite affine distance for complete null geodesics, so their finite height in the diagram is not a physical incompleteness.
