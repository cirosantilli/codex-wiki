i OurBigBook.com
A kinematic redshift is the relativistic Doppler effect arising from relative motion of emitter and receiver. In a local inertial frame, recession decreases the received frequency and approach increases it. A cosmological redshift instead compares photons emitted and received by comoving observers at different cosmic times: expansion of the scale factor stretches their wavelengths. It exists even when both observers have zero peculiar velocity. Both are frequency ratios measured by observers, but a large cosmological redshift should not be inserted into a flat-spacetime radial-speed formula to define the galaxy's unique recession speed.

1+zB→A​=aB​aA​​=210​=5,zB→A​=4.​

The assumed events must be connected by a null geodesic, as when A is on the same radial photon path between B and Earth. A K correction accounts for observing a redshifted spectrum through a fixed photometric passband, rather than the corresponding emitted band. In the same R-band astronomical magnitude convention,
MR​=mR​−5log10​(dL​/10pc)−KR​.
(13)
The luminosity distance already incorporates bolometric dimming; the additional correction depends on spectral shape and band response. At z=3, observed R-band light at 660nm was emitted at 165nm, in the far ultraviolet. It is not a direct measurement of rest-frame R-band luminosity.
To fix the sign, let Lν​ be emitted spectral luminosity and approximate R as narrow. Counting photon energy, arrival time and the transformed frequency interval gives Fνo​​=(1+z)L(1+z)νo​​/(4πdL2​). Thus
KR​=−2.5log10​[(1+z)Lν​(νR​)Lν​((1+z)νR​)​],MR,ignored​−MR​=KR​.
(14)
For an ordinary old elliptical galaxy, the far-ultraviolet spectrum is weak relative to the optical, so typically Lν​(165nm)/Lν​(660nm)<1/4. Then KR​>0: neglecting it overestimates the numerical absolute magnitude and underestimates the R-band luminosity. For a young, unobscured star-forming galaxy, hot stars make the far-ultraviolet strong, with an approximately flat Lν​ spectrum often serving as a useful model. It gives KR​=−2.5log10​4≃−1.51: neglecting it underestimates the numerical absolute magnitude and overestimates the R-band luminosity. Strong dust extinction or an unusual spectrum can reverse this latter sign; a galaxy label alone does not prove an exact correction. The band-integrated convention is given in Hogg and collaborators' definition of the K-correction.

The neutral-hydrogen column density is NHI​=∫nHI​dℓ, with units cm−2. Define the neutral-hydrogen column-density distribution by
f(N,z)=∂N∂z∂2N​,dN=f(N,z)dNdz.
(23)
Here N counts absorption systems along a quasar sightline. A histogram per logarithmic column interval instead measures Nln(10)f(N,z); forgetting this factor changes the inferred slope. Another common convention uses the absorption distance X, with dX/dz=H0​(1+z)2/H(z), and fX​=f/(dX/dz).
At a fixed epoch, weak Lyman-alpha forest lines have an approximately falling power-law distribution, f(N,z)≃A(z)N−β with β≃1.4–1.5 over representative low-column ranges. Stronger absorbers deviate from one universal slope, and the normalization and fitted slopes depend on epoch and sample. The low-column fit is measured, for example, in Kim and collaborators' forest survey. The sketch distinguishes that empirical low-column trend from an illustrative higher-column continuation.
Ordinary Lyman-alpha forest lines cover roughly 1012≲NHI​≲1017.2cm−2. They are numerous resonant Lyman-alpha absorption features from intervening gas at different cosmological redshifts, collectively resembling a forest in a quasar spectrum. Many are optically thin at the ionization edge even when their line centers are saturated.
A Lyman limit system becomes optically thick at the 91.2nm hydrogen ionization edge. With σ912​≃6.3×10−18cm2, τ912​=NHI​σ912​≳1 requires
NHI​≳1.6×1017cm−2≃1017.2cm−2.
(24)
Its spectrum consequently has a strong continuum decrement below the redshifted Lyman edge. In a classification separating the highest columns, Lyman limit systems extend up to the damped-system boundary.
A damped Lyman-alpha system conventionally has NHI​≳2×1020cm−2. Its Lyman-alpha absorption is sufficiently strong that the Lorentzian wings of the Voigt profile are prominent. “Damped” refers to the finite radiative lifetime of the atomic excited state, not an inference that the gas has a high temperature. These systems trace substantial neutral reservoirs, usually associated with galaxies.

Use high-resolution quasar spectra to fit Voigt profiles to Lyman-alpha forest lines near z=3, where the transition appears near 121.6(1+3)≃486.4nm. Each fit estimates the neutral-hydrogen column density and the velocity-width parameter b. The one-dimensional Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution has variance kB​T/mH​; consequently the thermal optical-depth profile is proportional to exp[−(v−v0​)2/b2], with the thermal cutoff of Lyman-alpha line widths
bth2​=mH​2kB​T​,T=2kB​mH​bth2​​.​
(30)
For example, a purely thermal b=20kms−1 corresponds to T≃2.4×104K.
An observed broad line is not by itself a thermometer: peculiar velocities, bulk gradients, blending and instrumental resolution also broaden it. For independent Gaussian contributions, b2=bth2​+bnonthermal2​+binst2​. After modeling the instrumental response, an uncorrected total width gives an upper bound on the thermal temperature, not a lower bound. The narrowest lines at each column density are least affected by additional broadening. Their lower envelope in the b–NHI​ plane constrains the temperature-density relation of photoionized intergalactic gas, often written T=T0​(1+δ)γ−1.
Calibrate the conversion between column density, density and width with hydrodynamic models incorporating the cosmic ionizing background, pressure smoothing, resolution and selection. It estimates T0​ and the slope rather than assigning one temperature to every absorber. Where aligned metal lines trace the same gas, comparing species of different atomic mass can separately estimate a common nonthermal contribution because thermal b2 scales as m−1. The forest lower-envelope method and its numerical calibration are developed in Schaye and collaborators' temperature analysis.

Let nn​ and np​ count the available neutrons and protons immediately before their incorporation into nuclei. In the usual neutron-limited helium synthesis approximation, all surviving neutrons go into 4He and the remaining protons form 1H. Each helium nucleus contains two neutrons and two protons, so
nHe​=nn​/2,nH​=np​−nn​.
(31)
Using the equal-nucleon-mass approximation and neglecting trace nuclei and binding-energy corrections, the primordial helium mass fraction is
Yp​=nH​+4nHe​4nHe​​=np​+nn​2nn​​=2(1+nn​np​​)−1.​
(32)
The ratio is the pre-assembly nucleon ratio, not the number ratio of free hydrogen nuclei to helium nuclei afterward. The derivation assumes enough protons to bind all the neutrons, as in standard Big Bang nucleosynthesis. The familiar np​/nn​≃7 gives Yp​≃1/4 and hydrogen mass fraction Xp​≃3/4.

Under the usual interpretation that the extra force simply slows otherwise adiabatic expansion, without changing reaction rates or injecting particles, the primordial hydrogen mass fraction would be larger. A smaller Hubble parameter makes cooling slower, delays the end of the deuterium bottleneck, and permits more free-neutron decay after freeze-out before nuclear assembly. If the reduced expansion also keeps weak conversions effective longer, the lower eventual cosmological weak freeze-out temperature further reduces the neutron-to-proton ratio. Both effects lower the primordial helium mass fraction and raise the remaining hydrogen mass fraction.
For fixed initial neutron fraction xn,1​=nn​/(nn​+np​), the post-freeze-out estimate is xn​=xn,1​e−(tN​−t1​)/τn​, where tN​ is the onset of efficient nuclear assembly. Thus Yp​≃2xn​ decreases when cooling delays tN​. The extra time is the time to reach the nuclear-assembly temperature, not an increase in the specified clock interval 300−1 seconds. An unspecified force that also reheats the plasma, changes weak rates or causes recollapse would require additional information; the simple expansion-only interpretation is essential to this expectation.

A primordial light-element abundance determination seeks gas or stars with little subsequent chemical processing. The three abundances require different observations and corrections.
For helium, measure helium and hydrogen recombination emission in metal-poor extragalactic ionized regions. Correct line ratios for electron temperature, density, extinction, collisional excitation, underlying stellar absorption and unobserved ionization stages. Convert the number ratio y=nHe​/nH​ to helium mass fraction Y≃4y/(1+4y), allowing for heavy elements when necessary. Since stars produce helium and metals, extrapolate a sample toward zero gas-phase metallicity to infer Yp​. The sensitivity to atomic data and these corrections is illustrated by Peimbert and collaborators' helium determination.
For deuterium, fit isotope-shifted hydrogen and deuterium absorption lines in relatively simple, metal-poor quasar absorption systems. The deuterium feature is displaced by about −82kms−1 from the corresponding hydrogen line. Multiple transitions and velocity components help distinguish deuterium from unrelated hydrogen contamination. The fitted neutral-column ratio estimates D/H in predominantly neutral gas. Since stellar processing destroys deuterium, low-metallicity, minimally processed absorbers are favored; the technique is exemplified by Pettini and collaborators' deuterium measurement.
For lithium, measure the 7Li absorption feature near 670.8nm in warm, old, metal-poor halo stars. Stellar-atmosphere models convert line strength to Li/H. The Spite plateau supplies a nearly constant low-metallicity level, but stellar metallicity trends, atmospheric temperature scales, departures from local thermal equilibrium and stellar lithium depletion must be considered. Thus the photospheric level is not automatically the initial abundance. Ryan and collaborators' halo lithium measurements illustrate the measured abundance and chemical-evolution corrections.

In standard Big Bang nucleosynthesis, with the radiation content and reaction rates fixed, the main adjustable abundance parameter is the baryon-to-photon ratio η. The baryon-density consistency between nucleosynthesis and the microwave background uses
η10​=1010η≃274Ωb,0​h2,H0​=100hkms−1Mpc−1.
(33)
Increasing cosmological baryon density makes deuterium burn more efficiently, so D/H falls steeply. The primordial helium mass fraction grows only slowly, because its dominant control is the surviving neutron fraction. Near the observationally favored density, lithium-7 production, much of it initially as beryllium-7, rises with density. At lower densities the full lithium curve has a valley; the sketch focuses on the branch relevant to the baryon-density comparison.
For the historical meaning of “current” in this 2008 paper, representative 2007–2008 fits near η10​≃6 are
105D/H≃2.68(6/η10​)1.6,Yp​≃0.2483+0.0016(η10​−6),10107Li/H≃4.30(η10​/6)2.
(34)
These local fits, with nuclear-rate uncertainties, and representative D/H and lithium ranges are documented in Steigman's 2007 nucleosynthesis analysis. They are not a replacement for a nuclear network outside their calibrated range.
The cosmic microwave background acoustic peak pattern measures baryon loading at recombination, independently of the abundance measurements. For example, the five-year WMAP cosmological analysis gives Ωb,0​h2=0.02273±0.00062 from WMAP alone, corresponding to η10​≃6.23±0.17. For h≃0.7, this is Ωb,0​≃0.046. Galaxy-cluster baryon fractions combined with independent total-matter density provide another, more astrophysically model-dependent cross-check.
At η10​≃6.2, the fitted predictions are D/H ≃2.5×10−5, Yp​≃0.249, and 7Li/H≃4.6×10−10. A representative observed D/H range around (2.7±0.3)×10−5 overlaps the prediction; its inferred density η10​≃6.0±0.4 is compatible with the cosmic microwave background. The helium result Yp​=0.2477±0.0029 from the 2007 helium analysis also agrees. Other helium analyses had lower central values, illustrating why ionization and temperature systematics matter.
In contrast, a representative plateau estimate 12+log10​(7Li/H)≃2.1±0.1 corresponds to roughly (1.0–1.6)×10−10, a factor of several below the prediction at the same density. This is the cosmological lithium problem. The three abundance ranges therefore should not all be described as exact simultaneous agreement: deuterium and helium broadly support the independently measured baryon density, while lithium has a substantial discrepancy. The plotted ranges explicitly refer to the 2007–2008 comparison, not present-day parameter estimates.

c OurBigBook.com
The agreement of the main light-element abundances with a hot early nuclear-burning phase, and the independent baryon-density consistency between nucleosynthesis and the microwave background, gives strong support to the hot Big Bang framework. These measurements connect the first minutes to recombination, despite their very different temperatures, densities and observables. In particular, the large primordial helium mass fraction and residual deuterium are natural outputs of Big Bang nucleosynthesis, rather than arbitrary products of subsequent star formation.
The cosmological lithium problem prevents claiming that the simplest abundance calculation explains every observation without qualification. One must distinguish nuclear-rate uncertainties, stellar depletion, atmosphere and abundance systematics, and possible new particle physics. Thus the broad hot Big Bang framework is strongly supported, while lithium tests the completeness of its simplest nucleosynthesis and stellar-abundance interpretation. An unresolved lithium discrepancy alone does not erase the independent successes of deuterium, helium and the cosmic microwave background.
