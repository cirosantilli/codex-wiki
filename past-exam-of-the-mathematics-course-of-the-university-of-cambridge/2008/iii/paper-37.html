On the finite horizon [0,T], strict positivity gives equivalence of the restricted probability measures: for A∈FT​, Q(A)=EP​(ZT​1A​) vanishes if and only if P(A) vanishes. The standard change-of-measure theorem gives semimartingale invariance under equivalent measures: a process is a semimartingale under P on this horizon if and only if it is one under Q. The sample-path quadratic variation is unchanged, but the local-martingale and finite-variation parts generally change.
More explicitly, set N=∫Z−1dZ, the zero-start stochastic logarithm of the density. For every continuous P-local martingale L, the Girsanov theorem states that
LtQ​=Lt​−∫0t​Zs​d[L,Z]s​​=Lt​−[L,N]t​​
(26)
is a continuous Q-local martingale. Consequently, if the continuous semimartingale decomposition under P is X=X0​+L+V, its decomposition under Q is
X=X0​+LQ+(V+[L,N]).
(27)
The covariation correction is a continuous finite-variation process, locally well-defined because Z stays strictly positive. In the Brownian case, if dZt​=Zt​θt​dBt​, then
BtQ​=Bt​−∫0t​θs​ds
(28)
is a Brownian motion under Q. The inverse finite-horizon density is 1/Z, giving the reverse change of measure. These assertions apply on each horizon on which the density is strictly positive; they do not assert equivalence on a larger terminal sigma-algebra where the full density D may vanish.

A strong solution of a stochastic differential equation is constructed for the prescribed driving Brownian motion on the prescribed stochastic basis, adapted to the augmented filtration generated by that driver and the initial data, and satisfies the integral equation almost surely. For this equation it must be continuous, nonnegative and satisfy Zt​=z+∫0t​Zs​​dBs​. Pathwise uniqueness means that any two solutions on the same filtered space, with the same initial value and the same driving Brownian motion, are indistinguishable.
The standard global existence theorem for stochastic differential equations with Lipschitz coefficients cannot be applied to the square-root branching diffusion, because σ(x)=x​ is not Lipschitz at zero:
∣h−0∣∣σ(h)−σ(0)∣​=h​1​⟶∞(h↓0).
(37)
It is locally Lipschitz on (0,∞), which gives a solution only up to its possible approach to zero by that theorem. Thus the missing hypothesis is the Lipschitz bound at the absorbing boundary, not any failure of continuity. Failure of this sufficient criterion does not establish failure of strong existence or uniqueness; part (c) constructs strong existence directly.

(48)
