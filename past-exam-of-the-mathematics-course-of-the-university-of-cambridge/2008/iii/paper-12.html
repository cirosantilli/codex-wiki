Consider functions u(z,α) continuous on D(0,r)​×D(0,ε)​, jointly holomorphic in the interior, with u(0,α)=0 and ∥u∥∞​≤S/2. This is a closed subset of a Banach space in the supremum norm. The integral operator
(Tu)(z,α)=∫0z​A(ζ,u(ζ,α),α)dζ
(1)
is well defined on the straight segment and is jointly holomorphic. Indeed, it equals z∫01​A(tz,u(tz,α),α)dt, an integral of jointly holomorphic functions locally uniformly bounded in both variables. Convexity of the w-disc and the derivative bound give
∥Tu∥∞​≤rM<S/2,∥Tu−Tv∥∞​≤rL∥u−v∥∞​.
(2)
Thus the Banach fixed-point theorem gives a fixed point f(z,α). More explicitly, the successive integral iterates starting from zero converge uniformly, and locally uniform convergence of holomorphic functions makes their limit jointly holomorphic. Differentiating the integral equation gives the required complex ordinary differential equation and initial value. This proves local existence, uniqueness and joint holomorphic dependence on (z,α). Uniqueness among arbitrary local solutions follows first on a small disc where their values stay within ∣w∣<S, by the same contraction mapping estimate, and then throughout their common connected domain by the identity theorem.
For holomorphic dependence of ordinary differential equations on parameters in the initial value, fix an admissible initial value w∗​ and put u=f−w0​. The equation becomes
u′=A(z,u+w0​),u(0)=0.

For a first-order system, it is important to specify the frame in which its coefficients are written. In the simple-pole convention intended here, a Fuchsian linear differential system has, at a finite singular point a,
A(z)=z−aRa​​+Ha​(z),Ha​ holomorphic near a.
(7)
The residue is the matrix Ra​=limz→a​(z−a)A(z), computed entry by entry. Such a point is a regular singular point: on a sector, the coefficient bound ∥A(z)∥≤C/∣z−a∣ and integration along rays give at most power growth of solutions. Intrinsically, a regular singular point means moderate power growth of all solutions on sectors, allowing logarithmic factors. This intrinsic definition is more general than requiring a simple coefficient pole in an arbitrary meromorphic frame.
For A(z)=R/z, a fundamental matrix on a chosen complex logarithm branch is
Φ(z)=exp(Rlogz).
(8)
Differentiating the matrix exponential verifies Φ′=RΦ/z. A counterclockwise circuit replaces logz by logz+2πi, so
Φ⟼ΦM,M=e2πiR,monodromy group ={Mn:n∈Z}.​
(9)
This is the residue and monodromy of a constant Fuchsian system. A change of solution basis conjugates M. Its eigenvalues are e2πiλ for the residue eigenvalues λ; a nontrivial Jordan block supplies the corresponding logarithmic terms in zR. The monodromy does not uniquely recover R, since integer shifts of residue eigenvalues can leave their exponentials unchanged.
The coordinate-independent coefficient is the matrix-valued one-form A(z)dz. With t=1/z, its coefficient is
A(t)=−t−2A(1/t).
(10)
Being Fuchsian at infinity means A(z)=O(z−1), while being ordinary there means A(z)=O(z−2). If the finite simple poles are a1​,…,as​, subtract their principal parts:
H(z)=A(z)−∑j=1s​z−aj​Rj​​.
(11)
Each entry of H is entire and tends to zero at infinity. Liouville's theorem therefore gives H=0. Expanding this partial fraction decomposition at infinity yields
A(z)=j=1∑s​z−aj​Rj​​,R∞​=−j=1∑s​Rj​.​
(12)
Thus the sum of all residues, including the residue at infinity of the one-form, is zero. If infinity is ordinary, R∞​=0 and the finite residues alone sum to zero. Treating A merely as a scalar function of z, without transforming dz, would give the wrong infinity convention.
Put ω=e2πi/3. The possible finite singular points of (B+zC)/(1−z3) are 1,ω,ω2, and
Rζ​=−3ζ2B+ζC​(ζ3=1).
(13)
A point ζ is removable exactly when B+ζC=0 as a matrix. At infinity,
A(t)=1−t3C+tB​,
(14)
which is holomorphic at t=0. Consequently the actual set is S={ζ:ζ3=1, B+ζC=0}, with {1,ω,ω2} the generic set. The finite residues sum to zero, as follows also from ∑ζ3=1​ζ−1=∑ζ3=1​ζ−2=0.
Conversely, suppose a Fuchsian linear differential system has its finite singular points among these three roots and has no singularity at infinity. Then Q(z)=(1−z3)A(z) extends to an entire matrix function, and ordinariness at infinity gives Q(z)=O(z). By the Cauchy estimate, each entry has zero derivatives of order two or higher, so Q(z)=B+zC. The same argument applies to any subset S, with additional cancellations at the omitted roots. This proves the requested normal form.
There is a genuine convention limitation: the normal form is not true under intrinsic regular singularity alone in an unrestricted frame. For example, if N=0 and N2=0, then A(z)=N/(z−1)2 has fundamental matrix I−N/(z−1). Its solutions have only power growth, so 1 is intrinsically a regular singular point, and infinity is ordinary. Nevertheless its coefficient has a double pole and cannot equal (B+zC)/(1−z3). The simple-pole convention makes the preceding residue and normal-form assertions correct.

Write the rational function A in reduced form. We first exclude a finite pole in its dependent variable at a generic point (a,b). If this pole has order m≥1, the inverse equation
dwdz​=A(z,w)1​,z(b)=a,
(15)
has a holomorphic right-hand side near (a,b), with a zero of order m in w−b at z=a. Choosing a generic point avoids collisions of pole branches and zeros of the reduced numerator. The unique inverse solution has
z(w)−a=c(w−b)m+1+O((w−b)m+2),c=0.
(16)
To justify the leading order, first note z′(b)=0; if the first nonzero term has order ℓ, substitution in the inverse equation makes the right-hand side start at order m, since the change caused by z−a has order at least ℓ. Thus ℓ−1=m. Inverting produces an algebraic branch point of order m+1. Away from the fixed coefficient exceptions, a can be chosen throughout an open set on the pole curve; inverse solutions based at these different a give different initial values at a nearby ordinary point. These are movable singularities of a complex differential equation, contradicting the hypothesis. Hence the denominator has no genuine w-dependent factor: A is a polynomial in w, with rational functions of z as coefficients.
Let its degree in w be d. Use the dependent coordinate v=1/w on the Riemann sphere. Its equation is
v′=−v2A(z,1/v).
(17)
If d≥3, the right-hand side has a pole of order d−2 at v=0 for generic z. The same inverse-equation argument gives a movable algebraic branch point of order d−1. It follows that d≤2, so the equation is Riccati, including its linear and constant special cases:
f′=a(z)f2+b(z)f+c(z).​
(18)
The requirement about the whole dependent-variable Riemann sphere is essential: examining only finite values would miss the branching at f=∞ for degree at least three.
The linearization of a Riccati equation is particularly symmetric in the system
(f2​f1​​)′=(b/2−a​c−b/2​)(f2​f1​​).
(19)
On a simply connected domain where the coefficients are holomorphic, its solutions are holomorphic. Direct differentiation gives
(f2​f1​​)′=f22​(bf1​/2+cf2​)f2​−f1​(−af1​−bf2​/2)​=a(f2​f1​​)2+bf2​f1​​+c.
(20)
A nonzero initial vector never becomes the zero vector, by uniqueness for the linear differential equation, so the quotient is meromorphic. Choosing initial vector (f(z0​),1) represents every finite initial value, and (1,0) represents infinity. At fixed coefficient singularities, a single globally holomorphic vector solution need not exist; the assertion is local on ordinary domains, or on their appropriate continuation surface.
Finally, for any two finite solutions u,v of the Riccati equation,
(u−v)′=(a(u+v)+b)(u−v).
(21)
Use this identity on the four differences in
K=(f−j)(h−g)(f−g)(h−j)​.
(22)
The logarithmic derivative of this cross-ratio is
a(f+g+h+j−f−j−h−g)+b+b−b−b=0.
(23)
Thus K is constant wherever the expression is initially defined, and the identity extends as an identity of meromorphic functions. Distinctness of g,h,j is preserved at ordinary points by uniqueness, interpreted on the Riemann sphere. Solving the constant cross-ratio equation gives the Riccati cross-ratio superposition
f=K(h−g)−(h−j)K(h−g)j−(h−j)g​.​
(24)
For K=∞ this means f=j; K=0 gives g, and K=1 gives h. The constant is selected by the initial value of f, so the formula represents every solution, with its denominator zeros interpreted as poles.

A fixed singularity of a complex differential equation has a location determined by the coefficients, independently of the initial value. A movable singularity of a complex differential equation belongs to an individual continued solution and changes location when the initial value changes. For example, w′=w2 has the movable pole z=c in w=−1/(z−c). A fixed exceptional location need not be singular for every solution.
The precise first-order form of the Painlevé determinateness theorem needed here is as follows. Let w′=A(z,w) be rational in w, with coefficients meromorphic in z on the chosen independent-variable surface. Algebraic coefficients can be made single valued on their own surface. Exclude the fixed coefficient singularities and exceptional fibres where a reduced numerator and denominator vanish together, working also in the dependent coordinate v=1/w. If a solution is continued along an arc γ:[0,1)→U with endpoint z∗​=limt→1​γ(t) outside that exceptional set, then w(γ(t)) has a definite finite or infinite limit. Any singularity there is a pole or an algebraic branch point; there is no movable essential or logarithmic singularity. The standard rectifiable-arc formulation is included in this statement.
Here is a proof. First suppose a sequence along the arc has (zn​,wn​)→(z∗​,w∗​) at a point where the vector field is holomorphic. Choose a smaller closed bidisc about this point. Uniform bounds on the vector field and its dependent-variable derivative give, by the integral contraction mapping proof, one radius r>0 on which every solution starting sufficiently close to (z∗​,w∗​) exists and remains bounded. For large n, the solution through (zn​,wn​) is therefore defined on a fixed disc about z∗​. The entire sufficiently late tail of γ lies in that disc. Uniqueness identifies the original continued solution with this local solution throughout that tail, so the solution extends through z∗​ and has a limit. The same argument applies to an ordinary point in the reciprocal coordinate v=1/w.
If the limit did not exist, consider the cluster set K in the dependent-variable Riemann sphere:
K=⋂s<1​{w(γ(t)):s≤t<1}​.
(47)
Each closed tail is compact and connected, and these sets are nested; hence K is nonempty, compact and connected. The preceding paragraph excludes every ordinary point from K. At z∗​ the rational vector field has only finitely many nonordinary dependent values, in both coordinate charts: an entire fibre of singular points was excluded among the fixed exceptions. Thus K is a connected subset of a finite set, so it is a singleton. Compactness then forces convergence to its unique point, contradicting the assumed lack of a limit. This proves determinateness.
It remains to determine the local type rather than merely the limit. At a finite limiting value w∗​ which is a pole of the vector field, write the reduced equation as w′=P/Q. Since z∗​ is not exceptional, P(z∗​,w∗​)=0, and the inverse equation
dwdz​=P(z,w)Q(z,w)​
(48)
is holomorphic near (z∗​,w∗​). The holomorphic dependence of ordinary differential equations on parameters makes its local solution curves jointly holomorphic in w and their initial z values. In particular, the value at the transverse line w=w∗​ is a holomorphic first integral labelling these curves. The original graph has constant label; taking its endpoint limit shows that it lies on the inverse solution through (z∗​,w∗​). That inverse solution cannot be the vertical curve z≡z∗​: this would require Q(z∗​,w) to vanish identically near w∗​, an excluded fixed singular fibre. Consequently its power series has a first nonzero term,
z(w)−z∗​=c(w−w∗​)k+O((w−w∗​)k+1),c=0.
(49)
Here k≥2 at a vector-field pole. Factor the right side as (w−w∗​)k times a nonvanishing holomorphic function and take its local kth root. This gives a new dependent coordinate with nonzero derivative, and the holomorphic inverse function theorem expresses w as a convergent power series in (z−z∗​)1/k. Thus the singularity is an algebraic branch point. At an infinite limit, repeat the argument for v=1/w. An ordinary zero of v gives a pole of w, while a finite ramification of v gives an algebraic pole branch. This proves the asserted local types and the theorem.
For the given equation, the finite-coordinate rational field is
A(z,w)=(z+1)(w2−z2)w​.
(50)
The whole fibre z=−1 is singular in the displayed coefficient, and at (z,w)=(0,0) the reduced numerator and denominator vanish together. Hence the finite fixed exceptional set is {−1,0}. Away from these two locations, the only possible finite dependent limits at a singularity are w∗​=σz∗​, with σ=±1.
At such a point the inverse equation is
zw​=w(z+1)(w2−z2)​.
(51)
It is holomorphic when z∗​=0,−1 and w∗​=σz∗​. Its inverse solution satisfies zw​(w∗​)=0, and differentiation gives
zww​(w∗​)=(z∗​+1)(1+w∗2​z∗2​​)=2(z∗​+1).
(52)
Therefore
z−z∗​=(z∗​+1)(w−w∗​)2+O((w−w∗​)3),
(53)
and
w(z)=σz∗​±z∗​+1z−z∗​​​+O(z−z∗​).​
(54)
These are genuine square-root algebraic branch points. The inverse solutions can be based at arbitrary z∗​∈/{0,−1} and then continued to nearby ordinary initial points. Their distinct branch locations require different initial data, so these branch points are movable. The signs specify the two inverse branches near each of the two values w∗​=±z∗​.
There are no movable poles. Indeed the reciprocal equation is
v′=−(z+1)(1−z2v2)v3​.
(55)
For every finite z=−1 it is holomorphic at v=0, and v≡0 is its unique solution with zero initial value. A nonzero reciprocal solution therefore cannot reach zero there. (57)
On a small disc this is a contraction mapping of the closed ball ∥h−1/2∥∞​≤1/4: both its departure from 1/2 and its Lipschitz constant are O(r2). Its unique holomorphic fixed point gives z=w2/2+O(w4). Thus
w(z)=±2z​(1+O(z))
(58)
is a genuine branch at the fixed location zero. The zero solution itself extends through zero as a function, although the original displayed coefficient is undefined at (0,0).
The point −1 can produce a fixed nonalgebraic singularity. For an explicit example take z=−1+x real, 0<x<x0​<1, and w=iy with initial y>2. Put q=y2 and L=−logx. Then
dLdq​=q+(x−1)22q​.
(59)
As L increases, q increases, its derivative is bounded above by 2 and bounded below by a positive constant, and therefore the solution exists for all large L and q→∞. The derivative then tends to 2, so q/L→2. Consequently
w(z)2∼2log(z+1)(z↓−1).
(60)
This is unbounded but grows more slowly than every negative power of z+1. It cannot be a meromorphic pole even after a finite ramified substitution, demonstrating an actual fixed singularity with logarithmic growth. The determinateness theorem does not exclude such behavior at fixed exceptions.
Finally, on the independent-variable Riemann sphere use t=1/z. For finite w the transformed equation is
dtdw​=(1+t)(1−t2w2)tw​,
(61)
which is ordinary at t=0. At simultaneous z=∞, w=∞, however, the reciprocal field is
dtdv​=−(1+t)(v2−t2)tv3​,
(62)
with an indeterminate point at (t,v)=(0,0). Thus infinity is an additional fixed exceptional location on the sphere, rather than an unavoidable singularity of solutions with finite limiting value. There are solutions escaping to infinity there: for real initial data w(z0​)>z0​>0, the difference d=w−z stays positive, since its derivative becomes positive as d↓0. Moreover d′≤0 when d≥1, so 0<d≤max(1,d(z0​)). Such a solution continues for all positive z, with w=z+O(1)→∞.
In the finite plane, the fixed exceptional locations are 0,−1; all other solution singularities are movable square-root branches at w=±z, and there are no movable poles. On the sphere, infinity is also a fixed exception at the infinite dependent value. Being fixed or movable describes the base location, not merely whether the coefficient contains the factors w−z and w+z.
