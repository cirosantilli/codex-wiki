Fix Haar measure mG​ and use f​(χ)=∫G​f(x)χ(x)​dmG​(x). A positive-definite function p satisfies ∑i,j​zi​zj​​p(xi​−xj​)≥0 for every finite choice of points and coefficients. The Bochner theorem states that a continuous positive-definite function on a locally compact Hausdorff Abelian group has a unique representation
p(x)=∫G​χ(x)dμp​(χ),μp​≥0,μp​(G)=p(0)<∞,​
(23)
where μp​ is a Radon measure. Conversely, any finite positive Radon measure on the Pontryagin dual group has a continuous positive-definite inverse transform. The sign convention here is the one compatible with the displayed Fourier transform.
First note that the dual is a locally compact Hausdorff topological group. Its group operations are continuous in the compact-open topology proved in question 3. For local compactness, adjoin the zero functional to the character space of an algebra of L1(G). All its elements lie in the closed unit ball of L1(G)∗, and the equations φ(a∗b)=φ(a)φ(b) define a closed subset in the weak-star topology. The Banach-Alaoglu theorem makes that subset compact Hausdorff. Removing the zero functional gives an open, hence locally compact, subspace. This provides the setting for constructing the measure on the dual, without assuming a Fourier inversion theorem.
Let u∗(x)=u(−x)​ and let P be the finite sums of convolution squares u∗u∗, u∈Cc​(G). Each such square is continuous with compact support, and is positive-definite because
∑i,j​zi​zj​​(u∗u∗)(xi​−xj​)=∫G​∣∑i​zi​u(t+xi​)∣2dmG​(t)≥0.
(24)
Its transform is ∣u∣2, so every p∈P has p​≥0. Also p∗q∈P: convolution of two squares is (u∗v)∗(u∗v)∗, and finite sums distribute. Insert the Bochner representation of q into p∗q and use the Fubini theorem to obtain
(p∗q)(x)=∫G​χ(x)p​(χ)dμq​(χ).
(25)
The representing measure on the right is finite and positive. Uniqueness in the Bochner theorem and commutativity now give the Bochner consistency identity for convolution squares
p​μq​=q​μp​=μp∗q​.
(26)
For every compact K⊆G there is p∈P with p​>0 throughout K. Indeed, at any χ0​ choose u=χ0​a, where a≥0 is a nonzero member of Cc​(G). Then u(χ0​)=∫a>0, and continuity supplies a neighbourhood where it stays nonzero. A finite subcover of K and the sum of the corresponding squares give such a p.
For ψ∈Cc​(G), choose p positive in transform on its support and define
J(ψ)=∫G​p​(χ)ψ(χ)​dμp​(χ).
(27)
The quotient is set to zero away from the support; its denominator is bounded away from zero there. If q is another choice, the consistency identity, multiplied by ψ/(p​q​) on that compact support, shows that the integral is unchanged. Choosing one p for a union of supports proves linearity; positivity follows from positivity of μp​. On each fixed compact support it is bounded by μp​(K)/minK​p​ times the uniform norm. Moreover, for every p∈P and ψ∈Cc​(G), choosing q positive on the support of ψ gives
J(ψp​)=∫q​ψp​​dμq​=∫ψdμp​.
(28)
A nonzero square has μp​(G)=p(0)=∥u∥22​>0; a suitable nonnegative compactly supported ψ consequently makes this last expression positive. Thus J is nonzero.
To prove translation invariance, fix η∈G and modulate p by pη​(x)=η(x)p(x). This again belongs to P, since modulation takes u∗u∗ to (ηu)∗(ηu)∗. Direct calculation and Bochner uniqueness give
pη​​(χ)=p​(η−1χ),μpη​​=(Tη​)∗​μp​,Tη​(χ)=ηχ.
(29)
Hence, for ψη​(χ)=ψ(η−1χ), using pη​ as the denominator function and changing variables gives J(ψη​)=J(ψ). The Riesz representation on compactly supported continuous functions now produces a nonzero translation-invariant positive Radon measure ν on the dual. It is finite on compact sets, so it is Haar measure. This is the compatible dual Haar measure, denoted mG​=ν.
The identity for J(ψp​) says, by uniqueness of Radon measures,
dμp​=p​dν.
(30)
Thus p​∈L1(G,ν) and the Bochner representation immediately proves
p(x)=∫G​p​(χ)χ(x)dν(χ)(p∈P).
(31)
It also proves the formula for all finite complex linear combinations of these functions. The measure scale is fixed by this identity: at x=0, a nonzero square gives ∫∣u∣2dν=∥u∥22​.
A wider inversion class is f∈L1(G) with f​∈L1(G,ν). To prove this extension, choose nonnegative aU​∈Cc​(G) with integral one and support in a small identity neighbourhood U, and put pU​=aU​∗aU∗​. These functions form an approximate identity: they are nonnegative, have integral one and support in U−U. Also 0≤p​U​=∣aU​∣2≤1, and p​U​→1 uniformly on compact subsets of the dual. For this uniform convergence use equicontinuity of compact families of characters: evaluation is jointly continuous, as follows from the translation quotient in question 3, and a finite cover of a compact family gives uniform control near the identity.
Convolving the already established inversion formula for pU​ with f, the Fubini theorem gives
(f∗pU​)(x)=∫G​f​(χ)p​U​(χ)χ(x)dν(χ).
(32)
This is justified by ∥f∥1​μpU​​(G)<∞. Compact-set uniform convergence and an integrable-tail split imply
∫G​∣f​∣∣p​U​−1∣dν⟶0.
(33)
Explicitly, first choose a compact set outside which ∫∣f​∣ is small, use the bound ∣p​U​−1∣≤2 on that tail, and use uniform convergence on the compact remainder. This argument works for neighbourhood-indexed nets, without assuming metrizability. Consequently f∗pU​ converges uniformly to the continuous inverse integral. On the other hand, translation continuity in Lp on a locally compact group gives f∗pU​→f in L1; testing these two limits against Cc​(G) shows they agree almost everywhere. At every continuity point of the chosen representative of f, the shrinking-support approximate identity also converges pointwise to f(x). We obtain Fourier inversion on a locally compact abelian group:
f(x)=∫G​f​(χ)⟨x,χ⟩dmG​(χ),⟨x,χ⟩=χ(x),​
(34)
valid everywhere for continuous f∈L1(G) with integrable transform, and in general as equality with a continuous representative almost everywhere. This class includes all convolution squares above, and their linear span.
Finally, for arbitrary h∈Cc​(G), apply the inversion formula at zero to h∗h∗:
∫G​∣h(χ)∣2dν(χ)=(h∗h∗)(0)=∫G​∣h(x)∣2dmG​(x).
(35)
Thus the integral Fourier transform is linear and norm-preserving on Cc​(G). That space is dense in L2(G), so for f∈L2(G) choose hn​∈Cc​(G) with hn​→f in L2 and define Ff as the L2(G,ν) limit of hn​. The norm identity makes this limit exist and independent of the approximating sequence, and preserves linearity. This proves the Plancherel theorem for locally compact abelian groups in the requested form:
F:L2(G,mG​)⟶L2(G,mG​) is linear,∥Ff∥2​=∥f∥2​.​
(36)
On L1∩L2, choose the approximating sequence to converge in both norms; uniform convergence of the integral transforms and their L2 convergence show that this extension agrees almost everywhere with the original transform. For general L2 functions the extension is defined by norm limits, not by presuming that the pointwise integral exists.
