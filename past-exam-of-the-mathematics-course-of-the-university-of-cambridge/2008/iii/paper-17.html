Let F be the generator of the smooth flow. The Anosov flow property requires F to be nowhere zero and a continuous splitting of the tangent bundle
TN=Es⊕RF⊕Eu
(1)
which is invariant under Dϕt​. There must be constants C≥1 and κ>0 such that, for every x, every t≥0 and every vector in the indicated fibre,
∥Dϕt​ξs​∥≤Ce−κt∥ξs​∥,∥Dϕ−t​ξu​∥≤Ce−κt∥ξu​∥.​
(2)
The first inequality defines the Anosov stable bundle; the second defines the Anosov unstable bundle and is equivalent to uniform forward expansion there. The remaining one-dimensional direction is the flow direction. The norm can come from any Riemannian metric, since norms are uniformly equivalent on a closed manifold. The essential requirements are a continuous invariant splitting and estimates uniform in both the base point and time, rather than separate exponential rates along individual orbits.

Here is the quadratic-form criterion for an Anosov flow. Assume that the generator F is nowhere zero and form the transverse vector bundle
E=TN/RF.
(3)
Since Dϕt​F(x)=F(ϕt​x), the derivative induces a linear cocycle Pt​:Ex​→Eϕt​x​. Suppose there is a continuous nondegenerate indefinite quadratic form Qx​ on each fibre, with constant signature of a quadratic form, such that t↦Qϕt​x​(Pt​ξ) is differentiable and
DQx​(ξ)=dtd​​t=0​Qϕt​x​(Pt​ξ)
(4)
is continuous and positive definite on every transverse fibre. Then ϕt​ is an Anosov flow. An equivalent way to specify Q is a quadratic form on TN whose radical is exactly RF and whose induced form and derivative satisfy these conditions on the quotient.
On the closed manifold, compactness makes the positivity uniform: for some c>0, DQx​(ξ)≥c∥ξ∥2. Thus the positive and negative cones are strictly separated by the linearized flow, yielding uniform unstable and stable transverse subbundles and their invariant lifts. The positive index is the unstable dimension and the negative index is the stable dimension. Strict positivity must be imposed transversely; imposing it on the flow direction would be impossible because that direction is carried into itself.

Use the Levi-Civita connection to represent a tangent vector to the unit tangent bundle at (x,v) by the initial data (J(0),Dt​J(0)) of the Jacobi field generated by a variation of the unit-speed geodesic. The unit-speed constraint gives ⟨Dt​J(0),v⟩=0. Along the geodesic, write
J(t)=aγ˙​(t)+y(t)iγ˙​(t),Dt​J(t)=y˙​(t)iγ˙​(t).
(5)
Here a is constant, while the perpendicular Jacobi field equation is
y¨​+K(γ(t))y=0.
(6)
The flow direction is represented by (a,y,y˙​)=(1,0,0). Consequently (y,z)=(y,y˙​) are coordinates on the transverse quotient. Define its nondegenerate quadratic form by Q(y,z)=yz. Its signature of a quadratic form is (1,1), and
dtd​Q(y(t),z(t))=z(t)2−K(γ(t))y(t)2.
(7)
Since the Gaussian curvature is strictly negative and the surface is a closed manifold, there is k0​>0 with K≤−k0​. Hence
Q˙​≥min(1,k0​)(y2+z2).
(8)
The quadratic-form criterion for an Anosov flow now proves the geodesic flow is Anosov. This is the geodesic hyperbolicity from negative Gaussian curvature argument. If the surface is not oriented, the local choice of perpendicular unit vector changes both y and z in sign; their product is unchanged, so the quadratic form still exists globally.
When K=−1, the perpendicular solutions are y=Aet+Be−t. The pure contracting data have (y,z)=(B,−B) and the pure expanding data have (y,z)=(A,A). In the canonical coframe of a surface unit tangent bundle, H represents (iv,0) and V represents (0,iv), so
Es(x,v)=R(H−V),Eu(x,v)=R(H+V).​
(9)
Equivalently the Anosov stable bundle consists of pairs (η,−η) with η⊥v, and the Anosov unstable bundle consists of (η,η). The derivative multiplies these data by e−t and et, respectively, using parallel transport along the geodesic to identify the perpendicular directions. Together with RX they give the required invariant splitting.

The Livsic theorem says that for a topologically transitive Anosov flow on a closed manifold, a Hölder continuous function f is a flow coboundary if and only if it has zero integral over every periodic orbit. In the integral formulation there is a Hölder continuous function u satisfying
u(ϕt​x)−u(x)=∫0t​f(ϕs​x)dsfor all x,t.​
(10)
Equivalently, u is differentiable along the flow and Fu=f. The periodic obstruction is
ϕT​x=x⟹∫0T​f(ϕs​x)ds=0.
(11)
It is necessary because the left-hand side of the coboundary identity is zero at a period. The substantive theorem is its sufficiency. For a smooth flow and smooth f, the smooth regularity version gives a smooth u. Two such solutions differ by a constant: their difference is flow invariant and hence constant on a dense orbit and, by continuity, everywhere. This smooth version is the one needed in part (b).

Evaluate the smooth one-form on unit velocities to obtain f(x,v)=θx​(v). Its integral along a periodic orbit of the geodesic flow is exactly the one-form integral along the corresponding closed geodesic. Negative curvature makes this geodesic flow an Anosov flow; the geodesic flow of a connected closed negatively curved surface is also topologically transitive. Apply the smooth Livsic theorem to obtain u∈C∞(SM) with
Xu=θx​(v).
(12)
If there is more than one connected component, apply this argument to each component separately.
The vertical vector field of a surface unit tangent bundle differentiates rotation of v, so
VXu=θx​(iv),V2Xu=−θx​(v).
(13)
For each x, writing θx​(v)=Acosϑ+Bsinϑ shows explicitly that
∫02π​θx​(v)2dϑ=π(A2+B2)=∫02π​θx​(iv)2dϑ.
(14)
The fibre disintegration of Liouville volume of a surface geodesic flow is dμ=dAg​dϑ. Integrating the displayed equality over the base therefore gives ∥VXu∥L22​=∥Xu∥L22​. Substitution into the supplied Pestov identity for a geodesic flow yields
∥XVu∥L22​+∫SM​(−K)(Vu)2dμ=0.
(15)
Both terms are nonnegative and −K≥k0​>0. Thus Vu=0 everywhere. Since every unit-circle fibre is connected, u is independent of velocity and descends to a smooth function h on M: u=h∘π. Its geodesic derivative is
Xu(x,v)=dhx​(v).
(16)
Comparison with Xu=θx​(v), first on unit vectors and then on all vectors by linearity, proves
θ=dh.​
(17)
Thus the one-form is an exact differential form. This proves the degree-one geodesic X-ray transform conclusion directly from the given integral identity and the stated smooth coboundary theorem.

No: the round sphere supplies a counterexample. On the unit round S2⊂R3, restrict the smooth one-form
θ=x32​dx1​.
(18)
The antipodal map A(x)=−x satisfies A∗θ=−θ, since x32​ is unchanged and dx1​ changes sign. Every unit-speed closed geodesic is a great circle, with γ(t+π)=A(γ(t)). Therefore its two half-circle integrals cancel:
∫02π​θγ(t)​(γ˙​(t))dt=∫0π​(θ+A∗θ)γ(t)​(γ˙​(t))dt=0.
(19)
The same holds for all repeated traversals and either orientation.
However, its exterior derivative is
dθ=2x3​dx3​∧dx1​,
(20)
which is not zero on the sphere. For example, at p=(0,1/2​,1/2​) the tangent vectors u=(1,0,0) and w=(0,1/2​,−1/2​) give dθp​(u,w)=1. Hence θ is not a closed differential form, and cannot be an exact differential form, because d(dh)=0. This is the counterexample to geodesic one-form injectivity on the round sphere; periodic geodesic integrals alone do not imply exactness without an additional geometric hypothesis.

A simple Riemannian manifold has strictly convex boundary, no conjugate points, and a unique connecting geodesic depending smoothly on its endpoints. In particular its unit-speed geodesics are nontrapping. Write ν for the inward unit normal, and identify inward and outward parts of its boundary unit tangent bundle by
∂+​SM={(x,v):x∈∂M, ∣v∣g​=1, ⟨v,ν⟩≥0},∂−​SM={(x,v):⟨v,ν⟩≤0}.
(21)
The signs are a convention; here + means inward. If (x,v) is strictly inward, let τ(x,v)>0 be its first exit time. The geodesic scattering relation is
α(x,v)=ϕτ(x,v)​(x,v)=(γx,v​(τ),γ˙​x,v​(τ))∈∂−​SM.​
(22)
Thus it records both the exit point and the outward unit velocity, not just the exit point. On strictly outward vectors extend α by following the same geodesic flow backwards to its previous entry point, retaining the velocity orientation. On tangential vectors set α(x,v)=(x,v). The extended scattering relation is an involution on the full boundary unit bundle, since its forward and backward constructions undo one another. On a simple Riemannian manifold, the travel time equals the boundary distance function between the two endpoints; travel time is often recorded separately as lens data.

First recover the induced boundary metric from the boundary distance function. If c(t) is a smooth boundary curve with c(0)=x and c˙(0)=w∈Tx​∂M, the local metric expansion of distance gives
limt→0​t2dgi​​(x,c(t))2​=gi​(w,w).
(23)
One can obtain this expansion in local coordinates from the fact that the metric tends uniformly to its value at x; distances to nearby points have that constant metric's first-order norm. Equality of the two boundary distance functions makes the two quadratic values equal for every boundary-tangent w. Polarization therefore recovers the full bilinear induced metric:
h=g1​∣T∂M​=g2​∣T∂M​.
(24)
This does not yet identify the metrics on normal vectors or mixed tangent-normal pairs.
Let νi​ be the inward unit normal for gi​. The boundary normal coordinates
ci​(x,r)=expxgi​​(rνi​(x)),0≤r<ε,
(25)
are collar neighborhoods for a common sufficiently small ε. The Gauss lemma gives ci∗​gi​=dr2+hi​(r) with hi​(0)=h. Hence the local boundary-fixing map c2​∘c1−1​ already satisfies
g1​=(c2​∘c1−1​)∗g2​on TM∣∂M​,
(26)
because both pulled-back metrics there are dr2+h.
To justify a global diffeomorphism, rather than assume a local collar map extends, interpolate the positive metrics by gs​=(1−s)g1​+sg2​, 0≤s≤1. Their inward unit normals and exponential maps give smoothly varying collars
Cs​(x,r)=expxgs​​(rνs​(x)).
(27)
Compactness of [0,1]×∂M gives a uniform collar size after shrinking ε. On the image of each collar define the time-dependent vector field
Zs​(Cs​(x,r))=∂s​Cs​(x,r).
(28)
Multiply it by a smooth cutoff which is one for small r and zero before the collar's outer edge, and extend it by zero to the rest of M. This gives a smooth global time-dependent vector field. It vanishes on the boundary, because Cs​(x,0)=x for all s. Its flow Ψs​ exists for the full interval and fixes the boundary. For sufficiently small fixed r, the curve s↦Cs​(x,r) solves this flow equation, so uniqueness gives
Ψs​(c1​(x,r))=Cs​(x,r).
(29)
Here C0​=c1​ and C1​=c2​. Thus the final map ψ=Ψ1​ agrees with the local map from the g1​ collar to the g2​ collar near the boundary. In particular Dψ fixes tangential vectors and sends ν1​ to ν2​ there. Since the tangent-normal splittings are orthogonal and the normals have unit length,
ψ∣∂M​=id,g1​=ψ∗g2​ on TM∣∂M​.​
(30)
The construction only needs equality of the induced boundary metrics; the equal-distance hypothesis supplies that equality.

Let ρ(x,y) be the common boundary distance function, with x=y. For each metric, the simple Riemannian manifold property gives a unique unit-speed geodesic from x to y, smoothly depending on the endpoint pair. Denote its initial and terminal velocities by vi​,wi​.
The first variation of geodesic energy, or equivalently length variation at unit speed, gives the endpoint formula
dx​ρ(ξ)=−gi​(vi​,ξ),dy​ρ(η)=gi​(wi​,η)(ξ∈Tx​∂M, η∈Ty​∂M).
(31)
For completeness, if J is an endpoint variation along a geodesic, differentiating its length and integrating the covariant derivative by parts gives
δL=⟨J,γ˙​⟩​0L​−∫0L​⟨J,Dt​γ˙​⟩dt.
(32)
The integral vanishes by the geodesic equation, leaving precisely these two boundary terms and their opposite signs.
Because the full metrics agree on the boundary tangent spaces Tx​M, the induced boundary metric h, unit spheres and inward unit normals agree there. The displayed covectors therefore determine the same tangential velocity components for the two metrics:
(vi​)T​=−gradxh​ρ,(wi​)T​=gradyh​ρ.
(33)
Their normal components are fixed by unit length and the entry or exit sign. With inward unit normals νx​,νy​,
vi​=(vi​)T​+1−∣(vi​)T​∣h2​​νx​,wi​=(wi​)T​−1−∣(wi​)T​∣h2​​νy​.
(34)
Strict convexity makes these signs strict for distinct endpoints. Thus v1​=v2​ and w1​=w2​ for the same endpoint pair.
Now start with any strictly inward (x,v) and let its g1​ exit point be y. The g2​ geodesic joining x to this same y has the same initial velocity v, so uniqueness of the geodesic equation identifies it with the g2​ trajectory launched from (x,v). Its exit velocity is also the same. Hence
αg1​​=αg2​​.​
(35)
This is the boundary distance determines geodesic scattering argument. The extension to outward vectors follows by the inverse scattering relation, and tangential vectors are fixed for both metrics. Equality of the travel times follows as well, since both equal ρ(x,y).

Let π:SM→M be the unit tangent bundle projection. Write X for the geodesic flow generator and V for the vertical vector field of a surface unit tangent bundle, positively rotating v toward iv. The acceleration prescribed in the question changes only this velocity angle, so the surface thermostat generator is
F=X+λV.
(36)
It never vanishes: dπ(F)=v=0. Also F and V are linearly independent, since dπ(V)=0.
The vertical Maslov cycle of a surface thermostat is
ΛV​={((x,v),L):V(x,v)∈L}={((x,v),RF(x,v)⊕RV(x,v))}.​
(37)
There is exactly one such plane in each fibre, so ΛV​ is the image of a smooth section. A two-plane containing F corresponds to a line in the rank-two quotient T(SM)/RF; thus Λ(SM) is a projective bundle with fibre RP1. Its total dimension is four and the cycle has dimension three, making it a smooth hypersurface. In this two-dimensional transverse space the Maslov cycle means the projective line meeting the distinguished vertical line, which here is just that distinguished line itself in each fibre.

Let H be the horizontal lift of iv supplied by the canonical coframe of a surface unit tangent bundle. The frame (X,H,V) has commutators
[V,X]=H,[V,H]=−X,[X,H]=KV.
(40)
Since F=X+λV, the Lie bracket product rule gives
[F,V]=−H−(Vλ)V,[F,H]=−λF+(K−Hλ+λ2)V.
(41)
These formulas hold for every smooth λ, with no curvature or Anosov assumption.
Follow a tangent variation ξ(t)=Dϕt​ξ(0) and express it in the moving frame as
ξ(t)=a(t)F+y(t)H+z(t)V.
(42)
Pulling this identity back by Dϕ−t​ makes its left side constant. The derivative of the pulled-back frame vector B is the pulled-back bracket [F,B], so the coefficients satisfy
a˙=λy,y˙​=z,z˙=−(K−Hλ+λ2)y+(Vλ)z.
(43)
This derives the linearized transverse equation for a surface thermostat; its first equation in a disappears on the quotient by F.
Near a vertical plane, z=0, and the smooth projective coordinate r=y/z describes the plane as
L=RF+R(V+rH).
(44)
The vertical Maslov cycle of a surface thermostat is exactly r=0. Differentiating the quotient with the derived equations yields
r˙=1−(Vλ)r+(K−Hλ+λ2)r2.
(45)
Consequently

Fix an orientation and a positive volume form Ω. Define the divergence by the Lie derivative
LF​Ω=(divΩ​F)Ω.
(47)
A smooth function is a flow coboundary when it equals Fu for some smooth u. Every other positive volume form is ehΩ for a smooth h. The product rule for the Lie derivative gives
LF​(ehΩ)=eh(Fh+divΩ​F)Ω,divehΩ​F=divΩ​F+Fh.
(48)
Thus changing the reference volume form changes its divergence by a flow coboundary. If volume forms of either orientation are allowed, take the logarithm of the absolute ratio on each connected component; multiplying a reference form by a constant sign leaves its divergence unchanged.
If divΩ​F=Fu, the preceding product rule shows
LF​(e−uΩ)=0.
(49)
Conversely, if ehΩ is invariant, then divΩ​F=−Fh=F(−h) is a flow coboundary. Vanishing of the Lie derivative is equivalent to flow invariance, since ∂t​ϕt∗​Ω=ϕt∗​(LF​Ω). Hence
ϕt​ preserves a smooth volume form⟺divΩ​F is a smooth flow coboundary.​
(50)
The equivalence holds for every choice of reference volume form, proving the invariant volume criterion for a smooth flow. The smoothness requirement matters: a merely measurable transfer function does not give a smooth invariant volume form.

This is a Gaussian thermostat, with generator and angular acceleration
F=X+λV,λ(x,v)=⟨E(x),iv⟩.
(51)
The canonical fields X and V preserve Liouville volume of a surface geodesic flow. To check this directly, use the canonical coframe of a surface unit tangent bundle (α,β,ω), with dual frame (X,H,V) and
dα=ω∧β,dβ=α∧ω,dω=−Kα∧β.

divμ​F=divμ​X+λdivμ​V+Vλ=Vλ.

Use the following permitted kernel result, stated in the form needed here. For an Anosov flow generated by a smooth surface thermostat on a closed oriented Riemannian surface, if a smooth function u on SM satisfies
Fu=h∘π+ηx​(v),
(55)
where h is a smooth base function and η is a smooth one-form, then h=0 and η is an exact differential form. Conversely these two conditions give a smooth solution by taking a base primitive. This is the kernel of the thermostat X-ray transform in degrees zero and one. In particular it applies to Gaussian thermostats without an additional curvature condition; it is stronger than the magnetic or geodesic special case.
Put θ=E♭, so θx​(v)=⟨E(x),v⟩. Suppose the thermostat preserves a smooth volume form. Write it as ρμ with smooth ρ>0, choosing its sign separately on each component if necessary. By the invariant volume criterion for a smooth flow and the divergence computation in part (b),
0=divρμ​F=−θx​(v)+Flogρ.
(56)
Thus u=logρ is a smooth solution of Fu=θx​(v). Apply the stated kernel theorem with h=0 to obtain θ=df for a smooth base function f. The definition of the gradient then gives E=gradg​f.
Conversely, suppose E=gradg​f. Since dπ(F)=v, the vertical part has no effect on a base function and
F(f∘π)=dfx​(v)=⟨E(x),v⟩.
(57)
The divergence of a Gaussian thermostat and the volume-change formula therefore give
divef∘πμ​F=−⟨E,v⟩+F(f∘π)=0.
(58)
Consequently
E=gradg​f⟺ϕt​ preserves a smooth volume form,Ω=ef∘πμ is invariant.​
(59)
Only the necessary direction uses the Anosov kernel theorem; the displayed invariant density works for any potential Gaussian thermostat. In particular its exponent is positive, as required to cancel the negative divergence found in part (b).
