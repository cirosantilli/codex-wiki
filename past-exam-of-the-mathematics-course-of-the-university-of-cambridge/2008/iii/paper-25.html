Write A=E×{p}, B={p}×E and D=ΔE​. Their self-intersection numbers vanish: each factor curve has trivial normal bundle, and the self-intersection of the diagonal of a curve is 2−2g(E)=0. Each pair meets transversely once. Their intersection pairing is therefore
​A2B⋅AD⋅A​A⋅BB2D⋅B​A⋅DB⋅DD2​​=​011​101​110​​,det=2.
(1)
If a rational linear combination of their cycle classes vanished in H2(E×E,Q), intersecting it with A,B,D would multiply its coefficients by this invertible matrix and give zero. All coefficients must vanish, proving the three classes are linearly independent.
The cycle class map detects the Chow Künneth failure for an elliptic self-product. The codimension-one image of the external product of Chow classes consists of sums from CH1(E)⊗CH0(E) and CH0(E)⊗CH1(E). Here CH0(E)=Z[E], and every divisor's cohomology class is its degree of a divisor times the class of a point. Thus the image's cohomology is contained in the span of [A],[B]. The diagonal's independent class lies outside that span, so its Chow class lies outside the external-product image. Therefore the natural map of Chow groups is not surjective.

Embed Y in projective space and let H be its hyperplane Cartier divisor. Since f is surjective between integral surfaces, it is generically finite, with degree r>0. Its pullback of a Cartier divisor L=f∗H is a nef divisor, and the projection formula for algebraic cycles gives L2=rH2>0. The same formula gives L⋅C=H⋅f∗​C=0, because the curve is contracted to a point.
The Hodge index theorem for algebraic surfaces makes the intersection pairing on the Picard group of a surface have signature (1,ρ−1) on N1(X)R​. The orthogonal complement of any positive-square vector, including L, is negative definite. The numerical class of C is nonzero, since an ample divisor on X intersects every nonzero effective divisor positively. Thus the contracted curve has negative self-intersection conclusion applies:
C2<0.​

Let D be an effective divisor linearly equivalent to C. If C were not a component of D, all intersection multiplicities between its components and C would be nonnegative, contradicting D⋅C=C2<0. Hence D=C+D′ with D′ effective and linearly equivalent to zero. For an ample divisor H, this gives H⋅D′=0. Positivity of H on every irreducible component of a nonzero effective divisor forces D′=0. Therefore D=C​, illustrating the rigidity of a negative curve.

b OurBigBook.com
Use countability of negative curves. A smooth projective surface over C is a compact smooth manifold homotopy equivalent to a finite CW complex, so its integral cohomology group H2(X,Z) is finitely generated and therefore countable. Distinct irreducible curves C,D with C2<0 cannot have the same cohomology class: that would give C⋅D=C2<0, while distinct effective curves have nonnegative intersection multiplicities. Assigning a negative curve its cohomology class is thus injective, proving there are at most countably many negative curves.
For zero self-intersection, uncountable families do occur. On the product of two projective lines P1×P1, every fiber P1×{p} has self-intersection zero, since a different fiber is disjoint and has the same numerical equivalence of divisors class. The parameter p∈P1(C) varies over an uncountable set. Thus yes, there can be uncountably many such curves.

Let X⊂P3 have degree d≥3 and hyperplane class H. The adjunction formula gives KX​=(d−4)H. For a line L⊂X, the same formula on the smooth rational curve L yields (KX​+L)⋅L=−2, hence
L2=2−d<0.​
(3)
Thus all lines are negative curves, and part (b) makes their set countable. The Fano scheme of lines is a closed projective scheme of Gr(2,4). A positive-dimensional complex algebraic component would have uncountably many points, so this scheme is zero-dimensional. A projective scheme of finite type has only finitely many zero-dimensional points. This proves the finiteness of lines on a smooth surface of degree at least three.

Choose a projective embedding of the abelian surface X. By Bertini's theorem, a general hyperplane section C is a smooth projective curve that is connected. It is ample, so C2>0. Choose p∈C and translate the inclusion so it sends p to the identity of X. The universal property of the Jacobian variety then gives a homomorphism of abelian varieties
Φ:J(C)⟶X
(4)
whose composition with the based Abel map of an algebraic curve is this translated inclusion.
The image is a closed connected algebraic subgroup, since J(C) is projective, and it contains a translate of C. If the image had dimension one, that irreducible curve would be the image subgroup itself. A distinct coset is disjoint from it and has the same numerical divisor class: translation acts trivially on cohomology, being homotopic to the identity on the complex torus. Its self-intersection would therefore be zero, contradicting C2>0. The image has dimension two and is all of X. Thus Φ is surjective, as expressed by the principle that an ample curve on an abelian surface generates the surface.

b OurBigBook.com
The Noether–Lefschetz theorem says that a very general smooth degree-d surface in PC3​, for d≥4, has Picard group freely generated by its hyperplane class H. Here very general means outside a countable union of proper closed loci in the parameter space of surfaces.
If such a surface contained a smooth rational curve C, its divisor class would be mH for an integer m. Since H⋅C=md>0, one has m>0 and therefore C2=m2d>0. Part (a), however, gives C2=−2−(d−4)degC≤−2, a contradiction. Thus a very general surface of degree at least four has no smooth rational curve. The statement concerns smooth embedded curves; a singular curve can still have rational normalization.
