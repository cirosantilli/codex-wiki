The exterior algebra of a vector space V is Λ∗V∗=⨁r=0dimV​ΛrV∗, whose degree-r elements are alternating r-linear forms. Its exterior product satisfies α∧β=(−1)rsβ∧α for degrees r,s. For example, one-forms anticommute, so dxi∧dxi=0. A differential form of degree r on a smooth manifold is a smooth section of ΛrT∗M; in coordinates it is an alternating sum of products of r coordinate one-forms. Its exterior derivative increases degree by one, satisfies d2=0, and obeys
d(α∧β)=dα∧β+(−1)rα∧dβ.
(1)
The coordinate definition and pullback of a differential form are compatible, making differential forms suitable for coordinate-independent integration.
An orientation and a nondegenerate metric tensor define the Hodge star operator by α∧∗β=⟨α,β⟩volg​ for equal-degree forms. It maps degree r to degree d−r. If the metric has s negative directions, its square is ∗2=(−1)r(d−r)+s. Thus Hodge duality turns antisymmetric tensors into complementary-degree tensors, using the metric and orientation rather than merely the underlying vector space. The Generalized Stokes theorem says ∫M​dη=∫∂M​η, with the induced boundary orientation, for appropriate smooth forms and compactness or support assumptions. It generalizes the fundamental theorem of calculus, circulation version of Stokes theorem, and divergence theorem.
For the field calculation, choose signature (−,+,+) and a fixed orientation on Minkowski spacetime. Write a=δA, so δF=da. Symmetry of the metric pairing and the Hodge star operator gives δ(F∧∗F)=2da∧∗F. Applying the exterior derivative product rule,
da∧∗F=d(a∧∗F)+a∧d∗F,A∧da=F∧a−d(A∧a).
(2)
Since F∧a=a∧F, variation of the action is
δS=∫M​a∧(d∗F+kF)+∫∂M​(a∧∗F−2k​A∧a).
(3)
Fix the boundary data or use compactly supported variations. Arbitrary interior one-forms a then imply the bulk field equation
d∗F+kF=0.​
(4)
With B=∗F, the Lorentzian Hodge star operator squares to −1 on both one- and two-forms, hence F=−∗B. Thus the Maxwell–Chern–Simons one-form equation becomes
dB−k∗B=0,∗dB+kB=0.​
(5)
These two displayed equations are equivalent. Also dF=0 gives d∗B=0. For k=0 the latter follows from applying d to the field equation; for k=0 it must be retained as the identity inherited from F=dA. Using the opposite metric-sign convention changes the corresponding ∗2 sign; the signature-independent equation is d∗F+kF=0.
Under the Abelian gauge transformation A↦A+dΛ, the curvature F=dA and the one-form B are unchanged because d2Λ=0. The field equation is therefore exactly gauge-invariant. The kinetic term is unchanged, while the other term changes by
ΔS=2k​∫M​dΛ∧F=2k​∫M​d(ΛF)=2k​∫∂M​ΛF.​
(6)
The last equality uses dF=0 and the Generalized Stokes theorem. Thus the action density changes by a total derivative and the integrated action can change on a boundary. The source's statement that the action is not invariant needs this qualification: on a boundaryless spacetime, or when the boundary integral vanishes, the integrated action is invariant too. No such boundary restriction is needed for invariance of the bulk field equation.

For a Lie group G, Left translation on a Lie group and right translation on a Lie group are the diffeomorphisms Lg​(h)=gh and Rg​(h)=hg. Their differentials identify the tangent space at the identity with every tangent space. Translating a basis ea​ of the Lie algebra gives global left-invariant vector fields Ea​(g)=(dLg​)e​ea​ and right-invariant vector fields Fa​(g)=(dRg​)e​ea​. Left and right translations commute. If [ea​,eb​]=ccab​ec​, then
[Ea​,Eb​]=ccab​Ec​,[Fa​,Fb​]=−ccab​Fc​.
(7)
The opposite sign for the right-invariant frame is essential.
Define an affine connection ∇− by declaring the Ea​ parallel. Explicitly, ∇X−​(faEa​)=X(fa)Ea​. Its curvature form of a connection vanishes, since successive derivatives of the component functions satisfy the vector-field commutator identity. Its torsion tensor is
T−(Ea​,Eb​)=∇Ea​−​Eb​−∇Eb​−​Ea​−[Ea​,Eb​]=−[Ea​,Eb​].
(8)
Similarly, declaring the Fa​ parallel gives another flat affine connection ∇+, with T+(Fa​,Fb​)=−[Fa​,Fb​]. In a left-invariant frame the latter connection obeys
∇X+​Y=[X,Y].
(9)
To see this, express Ea​=FAdg​ea​​. Along gexp(teb​), differentiation of Adgexp(teb​)​ea​ gives Adg​[eb​,ea​], producing the displayed bracket. Consequently T+(X,Y)=[X,Y] for left-invariant X,Y. These are the flat translation connections on a Lie group. They have opposite torsion; if G is Abelian, both torsion tensors vanish and the two connections coincide. The phrase “with torsion” does not mean nonzero torsion for Abelian groups.
The average ∇0=(∇−+∇+)/2 is again an affine connection and satisfies ∇X0​Y=21​[X,Y] for left-invariant fields. Its torsion tensor is zero. With the curvature convention R(X,Y)Z=∇X​∇Y​Z−∇Y​∇X​Z−∇[X,Y]​Z, the Jacobi identity gives
R0(X,Y)Z​=41​([X,[Y,Z]]−[Y,[X,Z]])−21​[[X,Y],Z]=−41​[[X,Y],Z].​
(10)
Define the Ricci tensor by Ric(X,Y)=Tr(Z↦R(Z,X)Y). Since [[Z,X],Y]=adY​adX​Z, this gives
R0(X,Y)Z=−41​[[X,Y],Z],Ric0(X,Y)=−41​K(X,Y),​
(11)
where K(X,Y)=Tr(adX​adY​) is the Killing form. Thus the canonical torsion-free connection on a Lie group is generally curved. Its exact flatness criterion is [[g,g],g]=0: Abelian and two-step nilpotent groups are exceptions. A vanishing Ricci tensor alone does not prove flatness.
For a semisimple Lie algebra, the Killing form is nondegenerate. Its adjoint invariance gives K([X,Y],Z)+K(Y,[X,Z])=0, so translating it defines a bi-invariant pseudo-Riemannian metric gK​. For left-invariant fields,
(∇X0​gK​)(Y,Z)=−21​(K([X,Y],Z)+K(Y,[X,Z]))=0.
(12)
A metric-compatible torsion-free connection is uniquely the Levi-Civita connection; therefore ∇0 is its Levi-Civita connection. Moreover,
Ric(gK​)=−41​gK​.​
(13)
The special feature is a canonical nondegenerate metric and an Einstein manifold structure. The metric need not be positive definite: noncompact semisimple groups generally require indefinite signature. For compact semisimple groups, using −K gives a bi-invariant Riemannian metric with Ric=41​g−K​ instead.

A fiber bundle with base B, fiber F and structure group G acting on F from the left consists of a projection π:E→B and local trivializations ϕi​:π−1(Ui​)→Ui​×F. On overlaps they satisfy
ϕi​ϕj−1​(x,f)=(x,gij​(x)⋅f),gij​gjk​=gik​.
(14)
The smooth transition functions are valued in G. A principal bundle is the case F=G with the left multiplication action on itself; its transition functions multiply the group coordinate from the left.
In each principal chart define (x,h)⋅a=(x,ha). This defines a global right action because gij​(x)(ha)=(gij​(x)h)a: coordinate changes commute with right multiplication. It is free, and acts transitively on each fiber. Thus the claimed right action follows from the left transition convention rather than being an additional choice.
If s:B→P is a global section of a fiber bundle, define Φ:B×G→P by Φ(x,a)=s(x)a. Freeness and transitivity give exactly one a for every point in the fiber, so Φ is bijective and equivariant. In a local trivialization where s(x) has group coordinate hi​(x), the inverse group coordinate is hi​(x)−1h, proving smoothness of the inverse. Hence Φ is a principal-bundle trivialization. Conversely, the identity coordinate in a product trivialization gives s(x)=Φ(x,e). Therefore
P is trivial as a principal bundle⟺P has a global section.​
(15)
This is the principal bundle trivialization by a global section criterion. It is special to principal bundles; a section alone need not trivialize an arbitrary fiber bundle.
For the quotient examples, assume the Lie subgroups are closed when smooth homogeneous-space statements are intended. The map G→G/H1​ is a principal bundle with structure group H1​ and right action g⋅h=gh; G/H1​ is a homogeneous space for left multiplication by G. Similarly, G→H2​\G has free transitive left H2​ action on its fibers. With the usual principal right-action convention one writes g⋅h=h−1g. The quotient H2​\G is a homogeneous space for right multiplication by G. These quotients are groups only when the respective subgroups are normal. Nonclosed immersed Lie subgroups can produce non-Hausdorff quotients, so an arbitrary Lie subgroup does not automatically give a smooth Hausdorff base.
Left H2​ and right H1​ multiplication commute, so taking their orbit equivalence relations in either order gives the same double coset set
H2​\G/H1​=(H2​\G)/H1​=H2​\(G/H1​).
(16)
This equality does not assert that the double quotient is a manifold or that either stage is a principal bundle. The H2​×H1​ action (a,b):g↦agb−1 can have stabilizers: they correspond to H2​∩gH1​g−1. If the induced action is free and proper, its quotient is a smooth principal-bundle quotient; without those hypotheses orbit dimensions can change. For example, with G=SO(3) and both subgroups the rotations fixing the third axis, double cosets are parametrized by e3​⋅ge3​∈[−1,1]. Interior and endpoint stabilizers differ, so this is not a uniform free principal-bundle construction.
For a principal bundle P and a left G action on F, the associated bundle is
P×G​F=(P×F)/∼,(pa,f)∼(p,af),
(17)
with projection [p,f]↦π(p). A local principal section gives coordinates [si​(x),f]↔(x,f), and its changes give precisely the left action of the principal transition functions on F. A linear action on a vector space gives an associated vector bundle.
For a metric of signature (r,s) on an n-manifold, an orthonormal frame at x is an isometry u:Rr,s→Tx​M. The orthonormal frame bundle O(M,g)→M has structure group O(r,s), acting on the right by u⋅a=u∘a. Its associated bundle for the standard representation is the tangent bundle, since
O(M,g)×O(r,s)​Rn⟶TM,[u,v]⟼u(v)
(18)
is well defined: (ua)(v)=u(av). Every tangent vector has exactly one such associated equivalence class, and local frame coordinates show that this is a vector-bundle isomorphism. The full frame bundle works similarly with GL(n,R).
Finally, choose a basis of the Lie algebra of a Lie group G. Its left translates are a global frame, yielding the explicit tangent bundle trivialization
G×g⟶TG,(g,v)⟼(dLg​)e​v.​
(19)
This proves parallelization of a Lie group by left translations and triviality of the full frame bundle. Choosing an inner product at the identity and translating it gives a left-invariant Riemannian metric; an orthonormal basis translates to a global orthonormal frame. The global section criterion therefore trivializes the corresponding orthonormal frame bundle as well. For any other positive-definite metric on G, smoothly applying the Gram-Schmidt process to this global frame gives the same conclusion. Thus the tangent and frame bundles of every Lie group are trivial; the orthonormal statement is with respect to a chosen metric as specified above.

The given equation is a Maurer-Cartan equation, with the sign appropriate to the chosen invariant coframe. Write λ=(λij​) as an antisymmetric matrix of one-forms, so dλ=λ∧λ. Using the graded product rule for the exterior derivative,
d2λij​​=dλik​∧λkj​−λik​∧dλkj​=λiℓ​∧λℓk​∧λkj​−λik​∧λkℓ​∧λℓj​=0.​
(20)
The two sums coincide after exchanging their dummy indices k,ℓ. This is also associativity of matrix-valued wedge products of differential forms.
To make the relation to the Jacobi identity explicit, let θa be any independent coframe and write dθa=−21​cabc​θb∧θc. Its dual invariant vector fields satisfy [Eb​,Ec​]=cabc​Ea​, by the exterior derivative of a one-form evaluated on vector fields. Evaluating d2θa=0 on three basis fields gives the cyclic coefficient identity
cebc​caed​+cecd​caeb​+cedb​caec​=0.
(21)
These are exactly the components of [[Eb​,Ec​],Ed​]+[[Ec​,Ed​],Eb​]+[[Ed​,Eb​],Ec​]=0. Thus the structure constants obey the Jacobi identity.
For the real four-dimensional case, abbreviate a=λ12​, b=λ23​, c=λ31​, d=λ43​, e=λ41​, f=λ42​; here d without an argument is a one-form, whereas d(⋅) denotes the exterior derivative. Directly expanding the given equations gives
da=−b∧c−e∧f,dd=−e∧c−b∧f,​db=−c∧a+d∧f,de=−f∧a+d∧c,​dc=−a∧b−d∧e,df=−d∧b−a∧e.​
(22)
Define α1±​=a±d, α2±​=b±e, and α3±​=c±f. For example,
dα1+​=−b∧c−e∧f−e∧c−b∧f=−(b+e)∧(c+f).
(23)
The minus combination and the other two cyclic combinations give
dα1±​=−α2±​∧α3±​,dα2±​=−α3±​∧α1±​,dα3±​=−α1±​∧α2±​.​
(24)
The six forms are independent, since the original pairs are their half-sums and half-differences. Their dual vector fields Ei+​,Ei−​ consequently obey
[Ei±​,Ej±​]=ϵijk​Ek±​,[Ei+​,Ej−​]=0.
(25)
Each triple is a three-dimensional Lie algebra ideal with the SO(3) Lie algebra brackets; the two ideals commute and span the original six-dimensional algebra. This proves the real four-dimensional rotation algebra splitting
so(4)≅so(3)⊕so(3).​
(26)
This is a Lie-algebra statement. Globally SO(4) is a central quotient of SU(2)×SU(2); a direct product of the corresponding SO(3) groups is not asserted.

Identify the ambient real four-dimensional vector space with 2×2 real matrices by
X(u,v,x,y)=(u+xy−v​y+vu−x​),−detX=−u2−v2+x2+y2.
(27)
For A,B∈SL(2,R), the linear action X↦AXB−1 preserves this quadratic form. It gives a group homomorphism to the special orthogonal group of signature (2,2); its determinant on matrix space is (detA)2(detB)−2=1. The group SL(2,R) is connected: an oriented Gram–Schmidt factorization writes every element as a rotation times an upper triangular matrix with positive diagonal and determinant one, and each factor varies in a connected space. Thus the image lies in the identity component SO0​(2,2).
If AXB−1=X for every X, taking X=I gives A=B. A matrix commuting with every real 2×2 matrix is scalar, and its determinant-one condition then gives A=B=±I. The kernel is therefore exactly {(I,I),(−I,−I)}. Infinitesimally the action is X↦aX−Xb, with a,b traceless. If this vanishes for every X, then a=b and it is scalar and traceless, hence zero. The differential is injective; source and target Lie algebras each have dimension six, so it is an isomorphism. The image is therefore an open subgroup of the connected target, which must be the entire target. Consequently
SO0​(2,2)≅{(I,I),(−I,−I)}SL(2,R)×SL(2,R)​.​
(28)
The finite quotient is by the diagonal central subgroup, not by a sign in just one factor. The connected-component subscript is a genuine qualification of the source's displayed formula: the full SO(2,2) is disconnected and cannot be isomorphic to a quotient of the connected product.
Let the anti-de Sitter radius be ℓ>0. Its quadric is −u2−v2+x2+y2=−ℓ2, or detX=ℓ2. Thus the map X↦g=X/ℓ identifies this quadric smoothly and globally with SL(2,R). This is the group model of three-dimensional anti-de Sitter spacetime. The induced Lorentzian metric is bi-invariant, since the two matrix multiplication actions preserve the ambient quadratic form. At the identity, tangent matrices a,b are traceless and
gℓ​(a,b)=2ℓ2​Tr(ab).
(29)
Indeed, for a traceless matrix a, deta=−21​Tr(a2), and polarizing −det(ℓa) gives this metric. For sl2​(R) the Killing form is K(a,b)=4Tr(ab): in the basis H=diag(1,−1), E=(00​10​) and F=(01​00​), the brackets are [H,E]=2E, [H,F]=−2F, [E,F]=H, giving K(H,H)=8, K(E,F)=4 and the remaining independent pairings zero. Therefore gℓ​=ℓ2K/8.
The canonical torsion-free connection on a Lie group is the Levi-Civita connection of this bi-invariant pseudo-Riemannian metric. Its Ricci tensor, already calculated from the double bracket, is
Ric=−41​K=−ℓ22​gℓ​,R=−ℓ26​.​
(30)
Substitution into the vacuum Einstein field equations gives
Ric−21​Rgℓ​+Λgℓ​=(ℓ21​+Λ)gℓ​=0,Λ=−ℓ−2.​
(31)
Thus the group metric solves the three-dimensional vacuum equations with negative cosmological constant.
The antipodal map X↦−X is g↦−g, multiplication by the central element −I. Hence the antipodal quotient is PSL(2,R)=SL(2,R)/{±I}, with the same local curvature and cosmological constant. To see the global time issue, use
u=ℓcoshρcost,v=ℓcoshρsint,x=ℓsinhρcosφ,y=ℓsinhρsinφ.
(32)
The induced metric is
ds2=ℓ2(−cosh2ρdt2+dρ2+sinh2ρdφ2).
(33)
On the quadric t has period 2π; curves with fixed ρ,φ are closed timelike curves. The antipodal identification is (t,φ)∼(t+π,φ+π), and does not eliminate this problem. At ρ=0, even the half-period time shift closes after the antipodal quotient.
If global time is not identified, take the universal cover, with t∈R. The topology of SL(2,R) is S1×R2, as follows from the same rotation–triangular decomposition, so the universal cover is diffeomorphic to R3 and carries the lifted group structure SL(2,R)​. The metric and Einstein equation are unchanged locally. Here t is a global time function with timelike gradient, excluding closed causal curves. Unwrapped anti-de Sitter spacetime is the universal covering group, rather than the original embedded quadric or its antipodal quotient; retaining the antipodal time-shift identification would wrap time again.

A Poisson manifold is a smooth manifold with a smooth antisymmetric bivector π=21​πij∂i​∧∂j​ whose function bracket {f,g}=πij(∂i​f)(∂j​g) satisfies the Jacobi identity. The bracket is automatically a derivation in each argument. A symplectic manifold has a nondegenerate differential two-form ω satisfying dω=0, that is, a closed differential form; nondegeneracy forces its dimension to be even. It determines a nondegenerate Poisson structure. We use matrix convention π=−ω−1, so that ιXH​​ω=dH corresponds to XH​(f)={f,H}.
Examples of genuinely nonsymplectic Poisson geometries include the zero bracket on R3 and the Lie-Poisson bracket {xi​,xj​}=ϵijk​xk​ on R3. Their three-dimensional underlying manifold cannot carry a nondegenerate two-form. In the latter case the Poisson rank is two away from the origin and zero at the origin, with spheres and the origin as symplectic leaves. Even in even dimension a given Poisson structure need not come from a symplectic form: π=x∂x​∧∂y​ on R2 satisfies Jacobi but vanishes along x=0. The statement concerns this degenerate structure, not a claim that R2 admits no other symplectic structure.
For any candidate bivector, applying the Jacobi identity to coordinate functions gives the necessary condition
Jijk:=πiℓ∂ℓ​πjk+πjℓ∂ℓ​πki+πkℓ∂ℓ​πij=0.​
(34)
It is sufficient as well: expanding {f,{g,h}} and its cyclic companions cancels all terms with second derivatives by antisymmetry of π. What remains is Jijk(∂i​f)(∂j​g)(∂k​h). This is the coordinate Jacobi condition for a Poisson bivector.
For a symplectic form, differentiate ωπ=−I to obtain ∂ℓ​π=π(∂ℓ​ω)π. Substituting this into the cyclic expression gives
Jijk=−πiaπjbπkc(∂a​ωbc​+∂b​ωca​+∂c​ωab​)=0,
(35)
because dω=0. Thus the inverse symplectic structure always satisfies the Jacobi identity. Conversely, for a nondegenerate Poisson structure, multiplying by three inverse matrices proves closure of its associated two-form.
In momentum space, choose Hamiltonian H(p)=E(p) and the constant Poisson bivector
πij=eϵijk​Bk​,π=e(B1​∂p2​​∧∂p3​​+B2​∂p3​​∧∂p1​​+B3​∂p1​​∧∂p2​​).​
(36)
Its Poisson bracket is {f,g}=eB⋅(∇f×∇g). The Hamiltonian vector field therefore gives
p˙​i​={pi​,E}=eϵijk​Bk​∂pj​​E=e(v×B)i​,v=∇p​E,
(37)
exactly the prescribed motion. The sign here uses the signed charge e as printed; an electron's physical charge may be negative. Since the magnetic field is uniform, every derivative of πij vanishes, so every term in Jijk is zero. Jacobi holds independently of the dispersion function E. This is the momentum-space Poisson structure for a uniform magnetic field.
For eB=0 this bivector has rank two and C(p)=B⋅p is a Casimir function of a Poisson manifold: {C,f}=0 for every f. Its symplectic leaves are the planes C=c. For axes with B=B0​e3​, the leaf form is ω=(eB0​)−1dp1​∧dp2​ in our inverse-sign convention. Also E˙={E,E}=0, so trajectories on a Fermi surface lie on its intersections with these planes, typically curves. The energy surface is invariant for this Hamiltonian, but need not be a Poisson submanifold; the bracket has been defined on the whole momentum space. If eB=0, the Poisson structure and motion vanish.

A Lagrangian submanifold of a 2n-dimensional symplectic manifold (M,ω) is an n-dimensional submanifold L such that the pullback of ω to L is zero. Equivalently, each tangent space Tx​L is its own symplectic orthogonal. The dimension condition makes it maximally isotropic: in a nondegenerate alternating vector space no isotropic subspace has dimension greater than n.
In geometric quantization, a line bundle with a connection on a vector bundle whose curvature is proportional to ω/ℏ provides the initial quantum-state space. A real polarization foliates phase space locally by Lagrangian leaves; requiring sections to be covariantly constant along these leaves reduces their independent variables by half. The curvature restricts to zero on a Lagrangian leaf, allowing local flat sections. Globally its holonomy can impose the familiar Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization condition. Thus Lagrangian submanifolds describe choices of quantum configuration data and the support of semiclassical states, rather than requiring simultaneous sharp values of all position and momentum coordinates.
For the thermodynamic application, regard (S,V,N) as base coordinates qi and (T,−P,μ) as fiber coordinates pi​ on T∗R3. The canonical one-form on a cotangent bundle and the position-first symplectic form are
θ=pi​dqi=TdS−PdV+μdN,ω=−dθ=dS∧dT−dV∧dP+dN∧dμ.
(38)
The negative sign in the pressure coordinate is essential. A smooth potential M(S,V,N) determines the embedding
ιM​(S,V,N)=(S,V,N,MS​,MV​,MN​),
(39)
where the last three coordinates are ordered as (T,−P,μ): explicitly T=MS​, P=−MV​, and μ=MN​, so the second fiber coordinate is −P=MV​. Accordingly, in the actual pi​ coordinates the graph is
LM​={(S,V,N;pS​,pV​,pN​):(pS​,pV​,pN​)=(MS​,MV​,MN​)}.​
(40)
Projection to the base is the identity, so this is an embedded three-dimensional submanifold, even if the Hessian of M is degenerate. The first law of thermodynamics gives ιM∗​θ=dM. Hence
ιM∗​ω=−d(ιM∗​θ)=−d2M=0.​
(41)
It has half the dimension of the ambient cotangent bundle and is therefore Lagrangian. This proves the thermodynamic potential as a Lagrangian graph construction for every smooth choice of M, locally on any domain on which the potential is defined. No invertibility, extensivity or stability assumption is needed for this geometric conclusion.
In components the same vanishing follows from equality of mixed derivatives. The thermodynamic conjugate variables satisfy
TV​=−PS​,TN​=μS​,−PN​=μV​.
(42)
These cancel the three coefficients of the pulled-back two-form. They are the integrability relations behind the local thermodynamic potential, and demonstrate directly why the first-law graph is Lagrangian.
