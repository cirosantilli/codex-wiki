The structural facts used about a non-Archimedean local field are that it is complete for a normalized discrete valuation v:K×→Z, its valuation ring OK​={x:v(x)≥0} is a discrete valuation ring, its maximal ideal is mK​=πOK​ for a uniformizer π, and its residue field is a finite field k=Fq​. Polynomial operations are continuous for this valuation topology.
A simple-root version of Hensel lemma is: if f∈OK​[X], a1​∈OK​, f(a1​)∈mK​, and f′(a1​)∈/mK​, then there is a unique a∈OK​ with f(a)=0 and a≡a1​(modmK​).
Construct an​ with f(an​)∈πnOK​. Given an​, put an+1​=an​+πnbn​. Taylor expansion over the valuation ring gives
f(an​+πnbn​)≡f(an​)+πnbn​f′(an​)(modπn+1).
(1)
The omitted terms contain π2n, and 2n≥n+1 for n≥1. The residue of f′(an​) remains the nonzero residue of f′(a1​), so choose bn​ with
bn​​=−f(an​)/πn​f′(an​)​−1.
(2)
This makes f(an+1​)∈πn+1OK​. The congruences an+1​≡an​(modπn) make (an​) a Cauchy sequence; completeness gives its limit a∈OK​. Continuity and v(f(an​))≥n give f(a)=0, and the first congruence gives its specified residue class.
For uniqueness, if a,b are two roots in that class, polynomial division of their difference gives
0=f(a)−f(b)=(a−b)(f′(b)+(a−b)c),c∈OK​.
(3)
The second factor is a unit, since f′(b) is a unit and a−b∈mK​. Thus a=b. This proves both existence and uniqueness in the stated Hensel lemma.

Write the finite residue field as k=Fq​, with q=pf and residue characteristic p. Every aˉ∈k satisfies aˉq=aˉ. The polynomial F(X)=Xq−X has derivative qXq−1−1, whose reduction is −1. Hence Hensel lemma gives a unique lift [aˉ]∈OK​ such that
[aˉ]modmK​=aˉ,[aˉ]q=[aˉ].
(4)
This includes [0]=0 and [1]=1. If aˉ=0, its lift is a unit and satisfies [aˉ]q−1=1.
The product [aˉ][bˉ] also satisfies Xq=X and has residue aˉbˉ. Uniqueness therefore gives [aˉbˉ]=[aˉ][bˉ]. Reduction is a left inverse of this map, so the map is injective. Restricting to nonzero residues proves the requested multiplicative Teichmuller lift:
k×↪OK×​⊂K×,aˉ⟼[aˉ].​
(5)
Its image is the group of (q−1)st roots of unity in K. Indeed a root of unity of that order is a unit satisfying Xq=X, hence is the unique lift of its residue. The only finite-field fact used here is aq=a; for a=0 it follows from Lagrange's theorem in the multiplicative group of order q−1.

Now suppose charK=p>0. The Teichmuller lifts constructed in (b) are additive as well as multiplicative: if aq=a and bq=b, then the characteristic-p Frobenius identity gives (a+b)q=aq+bq=a+b. Uniqueness of the lift with prescribed residue yields [aˉ+bˉ]=[aˉ]+[bˉ]. Their set is therefore a finite coefficient field in positive-characteristic local fields, and reduction identifies it with k.
Choose a uniformizer π. For x∈OK​, let a0​ be the lift of its residue and write x=a0​+πx1​ with x1​∈OK​. Repeat with x1​, then x2​, and so on. This gives
x=∑j=0N​aj​πj+πN+1xN+1​,aj​∈k⊂OK​,quadxN+1​∈OK​.
(6)
The remainder tends to zero, and completeness gives x=∑j≥0​aj​πj. The expansion is unique: in a nonzero difference of two series the first different coefficient is a nonzero element of the coefficient field, hence a unit, and so gives exactly the valuation of that difference.
Consequently substitution t↦π defines a bijection k[[t]]→OK​. It is a ring homomorphism because the coefficient lift is a field homomorphism, polynomial operations agree before passage to limits, and multiplication is continuous. Every element of K becomes integral after multiplication by a sufficiently large power of π. Allowing finitely many negative powers therefore extends substitution to the field isomorphism
k((t))∼​K,j≥m∑​aj​tj⟼j≥m∑​aj​πj.​
(7)
It preserves the normalized discrete valuation, since the first nonzero coefficient determines the valuation on each side. The results used are the structural facts stated in (a), its proved Hensel lemma, the proved multiplicative lifting in (b), and the characteristic-p identity (a+b)q=aq+bq. No classification theorem for local fields is needed.

A Non-Archimedean absolute value on a field is a function ∣⋅∣:K→R≥0​ with ∣x∣=0 exactly for x=0, ∣xy∣=∣x∣∣y∣, and the ultrametric inequality ∣x+y∣≤max(∣x∣,∣y∣). It is nontrivial if some nonzero element has value different from one. Equivalent absolute values induce the same topology; for nontrivial absolute values this is equivalent to ∣x∣2​=∣x∣1c​ for some c>0.
Here is also a proof of the latter equivalence in the case needed. A positive power plainly preserves the open neighborhoods of zero. Conversely, if the topologies coincide, then ∣x∣1​<1 if and only if xn→0 in the first topology, hence if and only if ∣x∣2​<1. Choose a with 0<∣a∣1​<1. For every x=0, m∈Z and n≥1, whether ∣xn/am∣<1 agrees for the two values. Comparison with all rational numbers m/n therefore identifies the two ratios log∣x∣j​/log∣a∣j​. Thus ∣x∣2​=∣x∣1c​ with c=log∣a∣2​/log∣a∣1​>0.
Now let ∣⋅∣ be nontrivial and non-Archimedean on Q. Since ∣1∣=1 and every positive integer is a sum of ones, ∣n∣≤1 for every integer. If all nonzero integers had value one, multiplicativity would make the absolute value trivial on all rationals. Hence some integer has value less than one; its prime factorization shows that some prime p has ∣p∣<1.
There cannot be two distinct such primes p,q. A Bezout identity ap+bq=1 with a,b∈Z would give
1=∣ap+bq∣≤max(∣a∣∣p∣,∣b∣∣q∣)≤max(∣p∣,∣q∣)<1.
(8)
Thus every other prime has value one. Prime factorization of the numerator and denominator of x∈Q× now gives
∣x∣=∣p∣vp​(x)=p−cvp​(x)=∣x∣pc​,c=−logplog∣p∣​>0.
(9)
Conversely ∣x∣p​=p−vp​(x) is multiplicative and satisfies the ultrametric inequality, since vp​(x+y)≥min(vp​(x),vp​(y)). Distinct primes give inequivalent values: pm→0 for the p-adic absolute value but ∣pm∣q​=1 for q=p. We have proved the non-Archimedean part of Ostrowski theorem:
The nontrivial non-Archimedean absolute values on Q are ∣⋅∣pc​,p prime, c>0.​
(10)
There is exactly one equivalence class for each prime.

A basis of open sets in the p-adic integers is
a+pnZp​(a∈Zp​, n≥0).​
(11)
Indeed this is the set of x with vp​(x−a)≥n, equivalently ∣x−a∣p​<p−(n−1); these neighborhoods become arbitrarily small. Reduction identifies Zp​/pnZp​ with Z/pnZ, so each has index pn.
If a subgroup H≤(Zp​,+) is open, a neighborhood of zero of the displayed form lies inside H. Thus pnZp​⊆H for some n, and [Zp​:H]≤pn is finite.
Conversely suppose the abstract index is the finite integer m. The quotient is a finite abelian group of order m, so Lagrange's theorem gives mx∈H for every x∈Zp​. Write m=pru with p∤u. Multiplication by the p-adic unit u is a bijection of Zp​, its inverse being multiplication by u−1∈Zp​. Consequently
prZp​=mZp​⊆H.
(12)
The subgroup H is a union of open cosets of prZp​, and is therefore open. This proves the characterization of open subgroups of the p-adic integers:
H open⟺[Zp​:H]<∞.​
(13)
No prior assumption of closedness is required for the finite-index direction.

For an odd integer x=2a+1, x2=1+4a(a+1)≡1(mod8). The same conclusion holds for every 2-adic unit by reduction modulo eight. Thus an odd square must be congruent to one modulo eight.
Conversely let u∈Z2×​ with u≡1(mod8). For digit lifting for square roots of 2-adic units, start with x3​=1. If xn​ is odd and xn2​≡u(mod2n), consider
xn+1​=xn​+εn​2n−1,εn​∈{0,1}.
(14)
For n≥3,
xn+12​≡xn2​+εn​2nxn​(mod2n+1),
(15)
since the remaining term is divisible by 22n−2 and 2n−2≥n+1. The odd multiplier xn​ lets the choice of εn​ correct exactly the next binary digit. Thus xn+12​≡u(mod2n+1). The corrections have valuations tending to infinity, so completeness gives x=limxn​∈Z2×​ with x2=u.
The unit 3 is congruent to one modulo two but is not a square, and 5 is congruent to one modulo four but is not a square, by the necessary modulo-eight condition. Therefore
The smallest k is 3,u is a square ⟺ u≡1(mod8).​
(16)
Every nonzero P-adic number in Q2​ is uniquely 2mu with m∈Z and u a 2-adic unit. A square has even valuation, and the unit square criterion shows that the unit class is determined by its odd residue modulo eight. The four residues form C2​×C2​, generated by −1 and 5; the valuation parity supplies the third factor, generated by 2. Hence the square-class group of the 2-adic numbers is
Q2×​/(Q2×​)2≅C23​,basis [−1],[2],[5].​
(17)
Its eight classes are represented by 1,−1,5,−5,2,−2,10,−10. They are distinct: the first four have even valuation and distinct unit residues modulo eight, while multiplication by 2 gives odd valuation and the same four distinct unit residues.
In characteristic different from two, completing the square in a quadratic minimal polynomial makes every quadratic extension K(d​) for a nonsquare d∈K. If K(d​)=K(e​), write e​=a+bd​ and square. The coefficient 2ab must vanish. Since e is nonsquare, b=0, so a=0 and e/d=b2. Conversely a square ratio plainly gives the same field. Thus the seven quadratic extensions of the 2-adic numbers are exactly
Q2​(−1​), Q2​(5​), Q2​(−5​), Q2​(2​), Q2​(−2​), Q2​(10​), Q2​(−10​).​
(18)
The unique unramified extension in this list is Q2​(5​). To verify this, α=(1+5​)/2 is integral and satisfies X2−X−1=0. Its reduction X2+X+1 is irreducible over F2​, so its residue field contains F4​. The standard degree formula for finite extensions of complete discretely valued fields with perfect residue field is [L:K]=e(L/K)f(L/K). Here the field degree is two, so the residue degree is two and the ramification index is one.
Conversely an unramified quadratic extension has residue field F4​. A root there of X2+X+1 lifts by Hensel lemma to a root of X2−X−1, whose derivative is a unit. For this lift β, (2β−1)2=5, so the extension contains Q2​(5​) and must equal it. Every other field in the list is therefore ramified.
For the requested ramification groups, choose L=Q2​(2​) and π=2​. We use uniqueness of the extension of the valuation to a finite extension, together with the degree formula just stated. Since v2​(π)=1/2, the extension has ramification index two and residue degree one. Normalize vL​(π)=1, giving vL​(2)=2. Every element is a+bπ with a,b∈Q2​, and the two summands have valuations of opposite parity:
vL​(a+bπ)=min{2v2​(a), 2v2​(b)+1}.
(19)
Thus a+bπ is integral exactly when a,b∈Z2​, proving OL​=Z2​⊕Z2​π directly.
The nonidentity automorphism σ negates π. For any integral a+bπ,
σ(a+bπ)−(a+bπ)=−2bπ,
(20)
of valuation at least three, with equality when b is a unit. By the definition Gi​={σ:vL​(σx−x)≥i+1 for every x∈OL​}, the ramification in the quadratic extension generated by the square root of two is therefore
G1​=G2​=Gal(L/Q2​)≅C2​,Gi​=1 for every i≥3.​
(21)
The lower ramification break is two; the valuation used here is the integral normalization on L.

For the finite Galois extension, normalize the discrete valuation vL​ to have value group Z. The inertia group is
I=G0​=ker(G→Gal(kL​/kK​)).
(22)
The lower ramification numbering is
Gi​={σ∈G:vL​(σx−x)≥i+1 for every x∈OL​},i≥0.
(23)
The wild inertia group is P=G1​, and the tame inertia quotient is I/P. We will prove that P is a p-group, where p=charkK​, and that I/P is cyclic of order prime to p. Tame inertia here is a quotient, not a specified canonical subgroup complementary to P.
First reduce the inertia calculation to a totally ramified extension. Choose bˉ generating the finite residue extension kL​/kK​. Its minimal polynomial is separable. Lift that monic polynomial to f∈OK​[X], and use Hensel lemma in OL​ to obtain a root b with residue bˉ. Irreducibility of the reduction implies irreducibility of f over K, by the monic form of the Gauss lemma for polynomials over a discrete valuation ring. Thus K0​=K(b) has degree [kL​:kK​] and residue field kL​; the degree formula makes K0​/K unramified. An automorphism fixes b precisely when it fixes bˉ: the nontrivial direction is uniqueness in Hensel lemma. Hence I=Gal(L/K0​), and L/K0​ is totally ramified.
Use the permitted integral-generation fact OL​=OK0​​[πL​]. Every element of I fixes the coefficients in this expression. The identity σ(πLj​)−πLj​=(σπL​−πL​)∑h=0j−1​(σπL​)hπLj−1−h​ consequently proves the uniformizer criterion for lower ramification groups:
Gi​={σ∈I:vL​(σπL​−πL​)≥i+1},i≥0.
(24)
Necessity follows by testing x=πL​, and the displayed power identity proves sufficiency for every integral polynomial in πL​.
Define the uniformizer character of inertia
θ0​:I→kL×​,θ0​(σ)=σπL​/πL​​.
(25)
It is multiplicative: writing uτ​=τπL​/πL​, one has στπL​/πL​=σ(uτ​)(σπL​/πL​), and σ acts trivially on residue units. Its kernel is exactly vL​(σπL​−πL​)≥2, namely G1​. Changing the uniformizer to uπL​ leaves the residue character unchanged, since σ(u)/u​=1. Therefore
I/P↪kL×​.
(26)
Every finite subgroup of a field's multiplicative group is cyclic: if its exponent is m, every element is a root of Xm−1, so its order is at most m; a finite abelian group has an element of order its exponent, constructed by multiplying elements of maximal prime-power orders, so its order is at least m. Equality makes it cyclic. Here kL×​ has order ∣kL​∣−1, prime to p. We have proved
The tame inertia group G0​/G1​ is cyclic of order prime to p.​
(27)
To verify the assertion about wild inertia as well, for i≥1 define
θi​:Gi​→(kL​,+),θi​(σ)=πLi+1​σπL​−πL​​​.
(28)
Write σπL​=πL​+aπLi+1​ and τπL​=πL​+bπLi+1​. Composition gives στπL​≡πL​+(a+b)πLi+1​(modπLi+2​), because inertia fixes residues and i≥1. Thus θi​ is additive with kernel Gi+1​. Thus positive ramification quotients are elementary abelian: each Gi​/Gi+1​ is a subgroup of the additive finite field, hence an elementary abelian p-group.
Finally the filtration terminates: a nonidentity element of I cannot fix πL​, since it already fixes K0​ and OL​=OK0​​[πL​]. Its displacement has finite valuation, and there are only finitely many automorphisms. Iterating the finite p-group quotients gives that P is a p-group. It is normal in I and has prime-to-p quotient, so it is its unique Sylow p-subgroup, justifying the wild inertia terminology.

Put K=C((t)). This is a complete discretely valued field, with valuation ring C[[t]] and residue field C; its infinite residue field means it is not a locally compact local field. The finite-extension facts needed here are uniqueness of the extended valuation, completeness of the extension, and [L:K]=ef for complete discretely valued fields with perfect residue field. They apply in this characteristic-zero setting.
Let [L:K]=n. The finite residue extension of C must be C itself, so f=1 and e=n. Normalize vL​ integrally and choose a uniformizer π. Since vL​(t)=n, write t=uπn with u∈OL×​.
The residue of u has an nth root b0​∈C×. Apply Hensel lemma to Xn−u: its derivative at b0​ is the unit nb0n−1​ because the residue characteristic is zero. Hence there is v∈OL×​ with vn=u. Setting s=vπ gives
sn=t,vL​(s)=1.
(29)
The elements 1,s,…,sn−1 are linearly independent over K. In a nontrivial relation ∑i=0n−1​ai​si=0, the nonzero summands have valuations nvK​(ai​)+i, distinct modulo n, so exactly one has least valuation and cannot cancel. Since L has dimension n, this proves L=K(s).
The constants already embed C into OL​ with residue field C. Subtracting the residue coefficient and dividing by s repeatedly, exactly as in Question 1(c), expresses every integral element uniquely as ∑j≥0​cj​sj with cj​∈C. Completeness and the first-nonzero-coefficient valuation show that allowing finite negative powers gives
L≅C((s))=C((t1/n)),t=sn.​
(30)
This proof of finite extensions of the complex Laurent series field did not need a Galois hypothesis. All n distinct nth roots of unity lie in the constant field, and the substitutions s↦ζs preserve sn=t. They give all n automorphisms, so every such finite extension is cyclic Galois:
Gal(L/K)≅μn​≅Z/nZ.
(31)
Choose compatible roots sn​=t1/n in an algebraic closure, first choosing them successively along the factorial-degree tower and then taking powers to define the other roots, with sn​=smm/n​ whenever n∣m. The fields Kn​=K(sn​) are nested under divisibility. Every finite extension inside the closure equals one of them: its constructed root s of Xn−t differs from sn​ by a constant root of unity. Therefore their union is the entire algebraic closure. Explicitly, every algebraic element generates a finite extension, so lies in the union; any polynomial over the union has its coefficients in one finite extension, and its roots are still algebraic over K, hence also lie there.
For n∣m, restriction of automorphisms sends ζm​∈μm​ to ζmm/n​∈μn​. Using the compatible primitive roots exp(2πi/n), these are the reduction maps on exponent groups. Thus the absolute Galois group of the complex Laurent series field is
Gal(C((t))​/C((t)))≅nlim​​Z/nZ=Z≅ℓ prime∏​Zℓ​.​
(32)
The inverse limit carries its profinite topology; choosing compatible roots gives the displayed identification with the profinite completion of the integers.
