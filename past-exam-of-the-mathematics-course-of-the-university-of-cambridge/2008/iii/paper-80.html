Apply the exponential-symbol order criterion for a multistep method. The exact-solution residual of the linear multistep method is encoded by E(z)=ρ(ez)−zσ(ez); vanishing through degree p is equivalent to the order conditions for a linear multistep method through order p. Here ρ(1)=0, ρ′(1)=σ(1)=1−α, and direct Taylor expansion gives
E(z)=−241+α​z4−36017+13α​z5+O(z6).
(1)
For example, for n≥2 the coefficient of zn can be calculated as
n!2n−(1+α)​−(n−1)!32​(1−α)+2n−1(5+α)/12​.
(2)
For n=2,3 this is zero, verifying the lower conditions explicitly. The constant and linear coefficients also vanish for every α. Therefore the formal order is at least three for every α.
The fourth-degree term vanishes exactly when α=−1. For that value the fifth-degree coefficient is −1/90=0, so
p=3 if α=−1,p=4 precisely when α=−1.​
(3)
At α=1 these are formal residual conditions only: the characteristic polynomial has a double root at one, so the method is not convergent. Canceling its common polynomial factor would change the recurrence and give a different order calculation. This distinction is relevant to part (b).

Consistency was established in part (a). By the Dahlquist equivalence theorem, convergence additionally requires the root condition for a multistep method. Since ρ(w)=(w−1)(w−α), its roots lie in the closed unit disk with each unit-modulus root simple exactly for
−1≤α<1.​
(4)
The endpoint α=−1 is zero-stability although it has a simple parasitic root at minus one; α=1 has a repeated root at one and is excluded.
For every parameter in this convergent range, the leading coefficient of σ is (5+α)/12>0, while
σ(−1)=−(1−α)/3<0.
(5)
Since σ(w)→+∞ as w→−∞, it has a real root w∗​<−1. Its other real root lies between minus one and one because σ(1)=1−α>0, so the exterior root is simple.
On the Dahlquist test equation, the amplification polynomial of a multistep method is Pz​(w)=ρ(w)−zσ(w), with z=hλ. For ∣z∣→∞, the equivalent polynomial −Pz​/z=σ−ρ/z tends coefficientwise to σ, and its leading coefficient stays nonzero. Choose a small closed disk about w∗​ lying wholly outside the unit disk and containing no other root of σ. On its boundary ∣σ∣ has a positive minimum, while ∣ρ/z∣ is smaller for every sufficiently large ∣z∣. Rouché's theorem then puts one root of Pz​ inside this exterior disk for all such complex z.
That root violates the stability root condition for a multistep method. Hence there is a finite radius Rα​ beyond which no point belongs to the absolute stability domain:
Sα​⊆{z∈C:∣z∣≤Rα​},−1≤α<1.​
(6)
This proves boundedness in every complex direction, rather than only excluding A-stability. It is an instance of exterior roots of the derivative polynomial bound multistep stability.

For fixed real κ, consider initial data u0​∈L2(0,1) and a solution continuous into L2 at time zero, with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions for positive times. Existence, uniqueness and continuous dependence in this norm give Hadamard well-posedness.
To construct a solution, put v(x,t)=eκxu(x,t). Substitution gives
vt​=vxx​−κ2v,v(0,t)=v(1,t)=0.
(7)
Let bj​=2∫01​eκxu0​(x)sin(jπx)dx. The Fourier sine series solution is
u(x,t)=e−κxj=1∑∞​bj​sin(jπx)e−[(jπ)2+κ2]t.​
(8)
Multiplication by e±κx is bounded on L2(0,1). Completeness of the sine basis gives convergence to u0​ in L2 at time zero, while the exponential decay gives smoothness for positive times and the required equation and boundary values. For a classical solution continuous up to the endpoints at time zero, the usual initial-boundary compatibility conditions are additionally required.
For the energy method, integration by parts gives
21​dtd​∥u∥L22​​=Re∫01​u(uxx​+2κux​)dx=−∫01​∣ux​∣2dx+κ[∣u∣2]01​=−∥ux​∥L22​.​

∥u(t)∥L2​≤e−π2t∥u0​∥L2​,​
(10)
with the identical estimate for differences of initial data. This Dirichlet convection-diffusion contraction holds for every real κ, and its energy constant is independent of the drift strength.

For a Runge-Kutta method with real stage matrix A=(aij​) and weights bi​, define
M=diag(b)A+ATdiag(b)−bbT,mij​=bi​aij​+bj​aji​−bi​bj​.
(14)
The method has algebraic stability of a Runge-Kutta method if
bi​≥0 for every i,M is positive semidefinite.​
(15)
The matrix condition means zTMz≥0 for every real vector z. Its significance is nonlinear contractivity: an algebraically stable method, on well-defined stage solutions, is B-stable for a dissipative vector field satisfying ⟨f(t,y)−f(t,z),y−z⟩≤0. This is distinct from scalar A-stability, which concerns the linear test equation. The contractivity proof in part (b) explains the matrix criterion directly.

The stated hypotheses are precisely the algebraic criterion in part (a). To prove its stability content, compare two Runge-Kutta method steps for the same dissipative vector field. Let d=yn​−zn​ be the input difference, Di​=Yi​−Zi​ the stage differences, and Fi​=f(tn​+ci​h,Yi​)−f(tn​+ci​h,Zi​). Then
Di​=d+h∑j​aij​Fj​,d+​=d+h∑i​bi​Fi​.
(16)
Expanding the squared norm of d+​ gives
∥d+​∥2=∥d∥2+2h∑i​bi​⟨d,Fi​⟩+h2∑i,j​bi​bj​⟨Fi​,Fj​⟩.
(17)
In each term of the linear sum, replace d by Di​−h∑j​aij​Fj​. Symmetrizing the resulting double sum proves the Runge-Kutta contractivity identity:
∥d+​∥2−∥d∥2=2hi∑​bi​⟨Di​,Fi​⟩−h2i,j∑​mij​⟨Fi​,Fj​⟩.​
(18)
Dissipativity makes ⟨Di​,Fi​⟩≤0, and the weights are nonnegative. For the second term, positive semidefiniteness permits a factorization M=CTC, giving
∑i,j​mij​⟨Fi​,Fj​⟩=∑k​∥∑i​Cki​Fi​∥2≥0.
(19)
For h≥0 both contributions in the distance-change identity are nonpositive. Therefore ∥yn+1​−zn+1​∥≤∥yn​−zn​∥. This proves the contractivity assertion behind algebraic stability, usually called the Butcher contractivity theorem. It assumes the compared implicit stage equations have solutions; the matrix condition by itself should not be substituted for a separate stage-solvability argument.

For Fourier stability analysis, take spatial modes eimθ and put
D(θ)=ae−iθ+b+ceiθ,N(θ)=d+eeiθ,G(θ)=N(θ)/D(θ).

A bounded implicit step requires D to stay away from zero, and von Neumann stability analysis requires ∣G∣≤1 for every frequency. Expanding the squared moduli and using cos2θ=2cos2θ−1 gives
∣D∣2∣N∣2∣D∣2−∣N∣2​​=a2+b2+c2+2b(a+c)cosθ+2accos2θ,=d2+e2+2decosθ,=μ(μ−2)(μ−1)(μ+1)(1−cosθ)2.​​
(27)
Consequently, apart from denominator invertibility, the necessary and sufficient sign condition is
μ(μ−2)(μ−1)(μ+1)≥0.
(28)
Its real solution set is (−∞,−1]∪[0,1]∪[2,∞).
To check the denominator rather than assume it, note
ImD=(1−2μ)sinθ,D(0)=3,D(π)=1+2μ−2μ2.
(29)
If μ=1/2, a zero can occur only at zero or the Nyquist frequency. The zero-frequency value is nonzero, and the Nyquist zeros occur only at μ=(1±3​)/2, both outside the proposed stable intervals. At μ=1/2, D=9/4+(3/4)cosθ≥3/2, also nonzero. Continuity on the frequency circle now gives a positive lower bound on ∣D∣ for each allowed μ.
The implicit convolution operator is therefore boundedly invertible on the square-summable grid sequences. By the Plancherel theorem, ∣G∣≤1 gives
∥un+1∥ℓh2​​≤∥un∥ℓh2​​,μ∈(−∞,−1]∪[0,1]∪[2,∞).​
(30)
Outside these intervals the modulus difference is negative at every nonzero frequency where D is nonzero. There is an interval on which ∣G∣>1, giving arbitrarily large growth of powers of the step operator, so the method is unstable; a zero of D additionally makes the step ill posed.
For the physical choice k,h>0, the stable range is 0<μ≤1 or μ≥2; zero is the limiting zero-step case. The exact-shift values have unit gain and are included.

The necessary formulation uses a real Hilbert space V of functions satisfying the essential boundary conditions, a continuous symmetric bilinear form a, and a continuous linear functional ℓ. Assume constants C,c>0 with
∣a(v,w)∣≤C∥v∥V​∥w∥V​,a(v,v)≥c∥v∥V2​.​
(31)
The latter is coercivity, the positive-definiteness condition needed in the chosen complete space. On the domain of the strong differential operator, integration by parts and the boundary conditions identify a(v,w)=⟨Lv,w⟩; this form extends to V. Let ℓ(w)=⟨f,w⟩ for admissible forcing. Define
I(v)=a(v,v)−2ℓ(v),u is a weak solution if a(u,w)=ℓ(w) for every w∈V.​
(32)
When u has the regularity to belong to the strong operator domain, this is Lu=f with its boundary conditions. For complex spaces use a Hermitian form and −2Reℓ(v) to make the functional real.
Here is an existence proof by minimization. Continuity of ℓ and coercivity give I(v)≥c∥v∥V2​−2∥ℓ∥V∗​∥v∥V​, so m=infV​I is finite and a minimizing sequence vn​ is bounded. The quadratic parallelogram identity is
I((vn​+vj​)/2)=2I(vn​)+I(vj​)​−41​a(vn​−vj​,vn​−vj​).
(33)
Since its left side is at least m,
a(vn​−vj​,vn​−vj​)≤2(I(vn​)+I(vj​)−2m)⟶0.
(34)
Coercivity makes vn​ Cauchy in V. Completeness gives vn​→u∈V, and continuity of the form and forcing gives I(u)=m.
For every w∈V, differentiation along u+tw yields
0=dtd​I(u+tw)​t=0​=2a(u,w)−2ℓ(w).
(35)
Thus the Euler-Lagrange equation is exactly the stated weak solution equation. Conversely, if u satisfies it, expansion gives
I(v)−I(u)=a(v−u,v−u)≥c∥v−u∥V2​.​
(36)
This proves that the weak solution is the unique global minimum and that two weak solutions coincide. It proves the symmetric coercive case underlying the Lax-Milgram theorem directly, rather than taking existence as a theorem name. The framework is symmetry and coercivity in quadratic energy minimization.
The qualifications matter if positive definiteness is read more weakly. For a nonsymmetric operator the derivative of ⟨Lv,v⟩ uses (L+L∗)/2, so it need not give Lu=f. For example, on smooth periodic real functions L=1+∂x​ has ⟨Lv,v⟩=∥v∥22​, but the functional's equation is u=f, not u+ux​=f. Even a symmetric operator can be strictly positive without being coercive: on l2 sequence space, (Lv)n​=vn​/n with fn​=1/n would require a solution un​=1, which is not square summable. The associated functional has values −∑n=1N​1/n on vectors with the first N entries equal to one, so it has no minimum in that space. This is strict positivity without coercivity can fail variational solvability. Under the usual symmetric coercive meaning of positive definiteness, the proof above supplies every assertion requested.

Let Ω=(0,1)2 and V=H01​(Ω), the closure of smooth compactly supported functions in the Sobolev space H1. Its elements have zero boundary trace. Set
a(v,w)=∫Ω​∇v⋅∇wdx,ℓ(w)=∫Ω​fwdx,f∈L2(Ω).
(37)
The form is symmetric and continuous by Cauchy-Schwarz. For a smooth function with zero boundary values, v(x,y)=∫0x​vx​(s,y)ds gives ∥v∥L2​≤∥vx​∥L2​≤∥∇v∥L2​ after integration. Density extends this Poincaré inequality to H01​. Therefore the gradient norm makes V complete and equivalent to its usual H1 norm, with
a(v,v)=∥∇v∥22​≥21​∥v∥H12​,∣ℓ(w)∣≤∥f∥2​∥w∥2​≤∥f∥2​∥∇w∥2​.
(38)
All the hypotheses of part (a) hold. Green's identity identifies the weak solution of the Dirichlet problem as
∫Ω​∇u⋅∇wdx=∫Ω​fwdxfor every w∈H01​(Ω).​
(39)
For smooth u this is −∇2u=f with zero boundary values; in general it defines the unique weak solution without requiring pointwise second derivatives.
For the Ritz method, choose a finite-dimensional conforming space Vh​⊂H01​(Ω) with basis ϕ1​,…,ϕN​ and put uh​=∑j​Uj​ϕj​. Minimize I(uh​) in the coefficients. Its derivative in each basis direction gives the finite element method equations
j∑​Kij​Uj​=Fi​,Kij​=∫Ω​∇ϕj​⋅∇ϕi​dx,Fi​=∫Ω​fϕi​dx.​
(40)
The stiffness matrix is symmetric positive definite: for a nonzero coefficient vector U, the corresponding basis combination is nonzero and UTKU=∥∇uh​∥22​>0. Hence the Ritz system has a unique solution.
For example, triangulate the square and use continuous piecewise-affine nodal basis functions at the interior vertices, with boundary vertex values fixed to zero. On a triangle T of area AT​, let λi​ be its barycentric basis functions. If its counterclockwise vertices are (xi​,yi​), define βi​=yj​−yk​ and χi​=xk​−xj​ cyclically. Then ∇λi​=(βi​,χi​)/(2AT​), giving the directly assemblable element matrices and loads
KijT​=4AT​βi​βj​+χi​χj​​,FiT​=∫T​fλi​dx.​
(41)
If f is constant on T, FiT​=fT​AT​/3; otherwise integrate or quadrature the load. Assemble by adding element contributions with the same global vertex labels and eliminate prescribed boundary coefficients. Only overlapping basis supports interact, making K sparse. This gives the Ritz-Galerkin equivalence for a symmetric coercive form in a practical finite element form.

The Euclidean matrix norm is the induced operator norm ∥C∥2​=sup∥x∥2​=1​∥Cx∥2​. By the spectral theorem, a real symmetric matrix has A=QΛQT with Q orthogonal and Λ=diag(λ1​,…,λm​). Hence
etA=Qdiag(etλ1​,…,etλm​)QT.
(42)
Orthogonal transformations preserve the Euclidean norm, and the induced norm of a real diagonal matrix is the maximum absolute diagonal entry. Therefore, for t≥0,
∥etA∥2​=jmax​etλj​=etμ(A).​
(43)
This gives the requested inequality with equality. If an exponent β<μ(A) were to work with prefactor one for every t, choose a unit eigenvector for μ(A). Its image has length etμ(A)>etβ for every t>0, a contradiction. Thus μ(A) is the smallest possible exponent.
For symmetric matrices the spectral abscissa equals the Euclidean logarithmic norm. Symmetry is essential to this particular exact norm formula; a general non-normal matrix can have transient growth not controlled with prefactor one by its spectral abscissa.
