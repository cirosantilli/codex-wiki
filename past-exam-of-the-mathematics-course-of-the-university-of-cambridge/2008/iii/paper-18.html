The Excision theorem for singular homology states that if Z⊆A⊆X and Z⊆intX​A, inclusion induces isomorphisms Hq​(X∖Z,A∖Z;Z)≅Hq​(X,A;Z) for all q. For an open cover X=U∪V, its small-chain form says that the subcomplex of singular chains whose individual simplices lie in U or V computes H∗​(X). Concretely, repeated barycentric subdivision puts each finite chain in that subcomplex, and subdivision is chain-homotopic to the identity. There is then a short exact sequence of chain complexes
0⟶C∗​(U∩V)c↦(c,−c)​C∗​(U)⊕C∗​(V)(a,b)↦a+b​C∗{U,V}​(X)⟶0.
(1)
Its long exact sequence in homology, with excision identifying the last complex's homology with H∗​(X), is the Mayer–Vietoris sequence:
⋯⟶Hq​(U∩V)(i∗​,−j∗​)​Hq​(U)⊕Hq​(V)⟶Hq​(X)⟶Hq−1​(U∩V)⟶⋯.
(2)
The Real projective space RP1 is a circle. The projective plane has one cell in each dimension 0,1,2, with cellular homology boundary maps d2​=2 and d1​=0. Thus, writing Aq​=Hq​(RP2;Z), we have A0​=Z, A1​=Z/2 and Aq​=0 for q≥2. Cover the circle by two open arcs with a two-component intersection and multiply this cover by the projective plane. In the Mayer–Vietoris sequence the overlap-to-cover map is
Aq​⊕Aq​⟶Aq​⊕Aq​,(a,b)⟼(a+b,−a−b).
(3)
Its kernel and cokernel are each Aq​. Consequently 0→Aq​→Hq​(X)→Aq−1​→0 for q≥1. In degree one the quotient is free, so this sequence splits; in degree two it identifies the group directly with A1​. The integral homology of a circle times a real projective plane is therefore
Hq​(RP1×RP2;Z)=⎩⎨⎧​Z,Z⊕Z/2,Z/2,0,​q=0,q=1,q=2,q≥3.​​
(4)
Without a compactness restriction, the answer to the final existence question is yes: there is a noncompact oriented four-manifold. Explicitly, take
Y=S1×((S2×R)/((v,t)∼(−v,−t))).
(5)
The involution is free and reverses orientation on both S2 and R, hence preserves their product orientation. Its quotient is consequently an oriented three-manifold, the orientable total space of the orientation line bundle of the projective plane. The contraction [v,t]↦[v,(1−s)t] is a deformation retraction onto RP2, so Y≃X.
If a closed oriented manifold is intended, the answer instead is no. A closed connected oriented d-manifold has Hd​≅Z and no homology above d. Here the highest nonzero positive-dimensional homology is the torsion group H2​=Z/2, so no dimension can supply that required top fundamental class. The distinction is necessary because the printed wording does not require the oriented manifold to be closed or three-dimensional.

A simply connected space is path-connected and has trivial fundamental group, equivalently every based loop is null-homotopic. The Whitehead theorem says that a map of CW complexes inducing isomorphisms on all homotopy groups is a homotopy equivalence. Its homological Whitehead theorem form says that a map between simply connected CW complexes, in particular finite ones, is a homotopy equivalence if it induces isomorphisms on integral homology. The latter implication follows from the relative Hurewicz theorem and the ordinary Whitehead theorem; homotopy equivalences necessarily induce homology isomorphisms.
For the attached-cell space X, the cellular homology chain complex in positive degrees is
0⟶Z2(2  3)​Z0​Z⟶0.
(6)
Dualizing gives the cellular cohomology complex Z0​Zt↦(2t,3t)​Z2. The last map is injective and its image is primitive because gcd(2,3)=1. Its cokernel is Z, explicitly via (a,b)↦3a−2b. Hence
Hq(X;Z)={Z,0,​q=0,2,q=0,2.​​
(7)
The van Kampen theorem gives π1​(X)=⟨a∣a2=1, a3=1⟩=1. Moreover H2​(X)=ker(2  3) is generated by (3,−2). Since X is simply connected, the Hurewicz theorem gives π2​(X)≅H2​(X). Choose a map S2→X representing that generator. It induces an isomorphism on H2​, and on H0​, while all other reduced homology groups vanish on both sides. The homological Whitehead theorem proves X≃S2​. This is a special case of coprime two-cell attachments to a circle.
It is not homeomorphic to the sphere. Indeed, if it were, its embedded one-skeleton would be a Jordan circle in S2, with the two open cell interiors as its complementary regions. The Jordan curve theorem and its Schoenflies form identify the closures of those regions with discs. A characteristic map, homeomorphic on the interior of such a disc, has local degree +1 or −1 at an interior point; the boundary homomorphism in relative homology then makes its attaching map on the circle have degree +1 or −1. This contradicts degrees 2 and 3. This argument does not require choosing special covering representatives of the attaching maps.
Finally, two compact examples with the same additive cohomology are
T2andS1∨S1∨S2.​
(8)
Both have H0=Z, H1=Z2, H2=Z and zero higher cohomology, by their cellular chain complexes. But their fundamental groups are respectively Z2 and the nonabelian free group on two generators, so they cannot be homotopy equivalent. This illustrates that cohomology groups do not determine homotopy type.

For singular cochains a∈Cp(X;R) and b∈Cq(X;R), define the cup product on a singular (p+q)-simplex by
(a⌣b)(σ)=a(σ∣[v0​,…,vp​])b(σ∣[vp​,…,vp+q​]).
(9)
The identity δ(a⌣b)=δa⌣b+(−1)pa⌣δb makes this descend to cohomology. The induced product is associative, unital and graded-commutative: ab=(−1)pqba in degrees p,q.
For finite CW complexes, the integral cohomological Künneth theorem for finite cell complexes is the split short exact sequence
0⟶⨁p+q=n​Hp(X;Z)⊗Hq(Y;Z)×​Hn(X×Y;Z)⟶⨁p+q=n+1​Tor1Z​(Hp(X;Z),Hq(Y;Z))⟶0.
(10)
The splitting need not be natural. If the factor cohomology is torsion-free, the cohomological cross product is an isomorphism, with multiplication (a×b)(c×d)=(−1)∣b∣∣c∣ac×bd.
A genus-g closed oriented surface has cellular groups H0=Z, H1=Z2g, H2=Z, and no higher cohomology. Choose the positive orientation class ω and a symplectic basis ai​,bi​ of H1. The cohomology ring of a closed oriented surface is specified by
ai​aj​=bi​bj​=0,ai​bj​=δij​ω,bi​aj​=−δij​ω,ωH>0=0,​
(11)
with unit 1∈H0. These products follow by representing their Poincare duality classes by oriented handle curves: the ith meridian meets the ith longitude once positively, curves on distinct handles have zero intersection, and swapping two oriented curves reverses the intersection sign. In general, for closed oriented n-manifolds and transverse oriented submanifolds of codimensions p,q, the cup product of their Poincare-dual classes is the dual class of their oriented intersection. Complementary-dimensional intersections give the signed point count ⟨αβ,[M]⟩.
For Σ2​, the four curves A1​,B1​,A2​,B2​ give a1​b1​=a2​b2​=ω and all other pairings zero apart from their negatives in reversed order. Thus the degree-one intersection pairing on an oriented surface is two symplectic blocks, and the point class is dual to ω.
For Σ2​×Σ2​, let ai​,bi​,ω1​ come from the first factor and cj​,dj​,ω2​ from the second. The Künneth theorem gives their graded tensor-product ring, whose additive ranks in degrees 0,1,2,3,4 are
(1,8,18,8,1).​
(12)
The top orientation class is Ω=ω1​ω2​. The two fiber surfaces {p}×Σ2​ and Σ2​×{q} have dual classes ω1​,ω2​, intersect once positively and have zero self-intersection. The other sixteen degree-two classes are products of degree-one classes and are dual, up to the chosen orientation convention, to products of handle curves. Their intersections are encoded by
ω12​=ω22​=0,(ai​cj​)(bk​dl​)=−δik​δjl​Ω,(ai​dj​)(bk​cl​)=δik​δjl​Ω.
(13)
The minus sign in the first mixed formula comes from commuting cj​ past bk​. These classes together with the fiber classes give the intersection form of a product of two closed oriented surfaces, here nine hyperbolic blocks. Complementary degrees one and three are similarly paired, for example ai​(bk​ω2​)=δik​Ω; geometrically this pairs a three-dimensional product submanifold with a transverse loop. This exhibits the full duality, not just the additive groups.
The Lefschetz fixed-point theorem says that a self-map of a compact polyhedron with nonzero Lefschetz number has a fixed point, where L(f)=∑q​(−1)qtr(f∗​:Hq​(−;Q)→Hq​(−;Q)). A map homotopic to the identity has L(f)=χ, and Euler characteristic of a product gives
0=L(f)=(2−2g1​)(2−2g2​).
(14)
Consequently
g1​=1 or g2​=1.​
(15)
The product g1​g2​ itself is not determined by the printed assumptions. For every h≥0, translation by a nonzero torus point, times the identity on Σh​, is a fixed-point-free map of Σ1​×Σh​ homotopic to the identity, and its genus product is h. Thus the exact conclusion is the criterion for fixed-point-free self-maps homotopic to the identity on products of surfaces, rather than a single value of g1​g2​.

The integral Poincare duality theorem says that a closed connected oriented n-manifold has a fundamental class [M]∈Hn​(M;Z) for which cap product gives Hq(M;Z)≅Hn−q​(M;Z). The general Poincare duality with the orientation local system uses [M]∈Hn​(M;OM​) and gives
Hq(M;A) ⌢[M] ​Hn−q​(M;OM​⊗A)
(16)
for a local coefficient system A. Over F2​ it holds without orientability. For constant integral coefficients on a nonorientable connected closed manifold, Hn​=H0(M;OM​)=0, while Hn=H0​(M;OM​)=Z/2: the invariants of sign reversal in Z are zero and its coinvariants are Z/(a∼−a).
If the three-manifold is orientable, duality and the universal coefficient theorem for cohomology give
H2​(M;Z)≅H1(M;Z)≅Hom(H1​(M;Z),Z)≅Zb​.
(17)
For a nonorientable three-manifold, H3​=0 and H3=Z/2. The top-degree universal coefficient sequence therefore gives
ExtZ1​(H2​(M;Z),Z)≅Z/2.
(18)
Since the homology groups are finitely generated, their cyclic decomposition shows that this Ext group is isomorphic to the torsion subgroup of H2​. Thus the torsion is exactly Z/2, not the possibly larger torsion subgroup of H1​. Also odd-dimensional closed manifolds have zero Euler characteristic, by pairing complementary mod-two Betti numbers. If r is the free rank of H2​, it follows that 0=χ(M)=1−b+r, giving the second homology of a closed nonorientable three-manifold
H2​(M;Z)≅Zb−1⊕Z/2,b≥1.​
(19)
The inequality is forced by the nonnegative free rank b−1.
For the final graded-group construction, one always has K0=K3=K6=Z, since each of these is copied from H0 of a connected three-manifold. Suppose it were the cohomology of a closed six-manifold N. The group K0 makes N connected, and K6=Z makes it orientable: the nonorientable alternative has top integral cohomology Z/2, as above. Its third rational cohomology would then be one-dimensional. But the middle Poincare duality pairing on H3(N;Q) is nondegenerate and skew-symmetric by graded commutativity, hence must have even dimension. Equivalently, a generator u would satisfy u⌣u=−u⌣u, so its square in the torsion-free top group is zero and the entire rank-one pairing vanishes. This contradicts duality and the even rank of middle cohomology in dimension four k plus two. Therefore
K is never the integral cohomology of a closed six-manifold.​
(20)
Both existence questions have negative answers, independently of the other groups of the original three-manifold.

For closed connected oriented d-manifolds, the degree of a map between oriented manifolds is the integer defined by f∗​[M]=(degf)[N] in Hd​(N;Z). If y∈N has finite fiber {x1​,…,xr​}, choose disjoint small neighbourhoods Ui​ containing no other points of that fiber. The map of local relative homology groups
Hd​(Ui​,Ui​∖{xi​};Z)⟶Hd​(N,N∖{y};Z)
(21)
sends the local orientation generator at xi​ to di​ times the one at y. This integer is its local degree of a continuous map, independent of the small neighbourhood by excision. Excision also identifies Hd​(M,M∖f−1(y)) with the direct sum of the local groups, and the global fundamental class restricts to their orientation generators. Naturality of the maps to relative homology therefore proves the degree as a sum of local degrees:
degf=i=1∑r​degxi​​f.​
(22)
The empty fiber gives degree zero. For smooth maps at a regular value, a local degree is the sign of the derivative determinant, but general continuous maps can have larger local multiplicities.

Using complex coordinates near zero, Fk​(z)=zk for k>0 induces winding number k on a small circle, so its local relative homology multiplier is k. For k<0, Fk​(z)=z∣k∣ has local degree k, since conjugation reverses the winding. For zero, take F0​(z)=∣z∣2∈C: its zero fiber is the single point zero, while the image of a small circle is a single positive real point, so its local degree is zero. Equivalently a nearby nonreal target has no preimages.
Each of these maps is proper and hence extends continuously over the one-point compactifications to a map S2→S2 with zero as an isolated preimage of zero. Thus
deg0​Fk​=kfor every k∈Z.​
(23)
These are local degrees of arbitrary integer value. Taking Fk​ times the identity on Rd−2 gives the same local degrees for every d≥2. In dimension one, an isolated local degree is restricted to −1,0,1, so the all-integers assertion is realized in dimensions at least two.

Identify T2 with the product of two unit circles and use the unit sphere S2⊂R3. The surjective degree-zero sphere-to-torus map is
F(x,y,z)=(e2πix,e2πiy).​
(24)
Given any torus point, choose its two angle representatives with x,y∈[−1/2,1/2]. Then x2+y2≤1/2, so z=1−x2−y2​ completes a preimage on the sphere. Thus the map is surjective. The explicit homotopy Ft​(x,y,z)=(e2πitx,e2πity), 0≤t≤1, contracts it to a constant map. By homotopy invariance of mapping degree, degF=0​, despite surjectivity.

Choose an oriented embedded closed d-disc D in M and collapse M∖intD to a point. The quotient is D/∂D≅Sd, with orientation chosen to agree on the disc interior. The resulting collapse map of degree one onto a sphere is
q:M⟶M/(M∖intD)≅Sd,degq=1.​
(25)
Indeed, any target point other than the collapsed point has exactly one preimage, where the map is an orientation-preserving homeomorphism and has local degree +1. The degree as a sum of local degrees therefore gives global degree one. This is the usual connected, positive-dimensional setting for mapping degree; extra components, if allowed, may all be collapsed.

For k>0, the surface presentation
π1​(Σ2​)=⟨a1​,b1​,a2​,b2​∣[a1​,b1​][a2​,b2​]=1⟩
(26)
has an epimorphism to Z/k sending a1​ to 1 and the other generators to zero. Its kernel has index k. By the classification of connected covering spaces, it determines a connected k-sheeted cover p:Σ→Σ2​. Pull back the orientation, so each local sheet has degree +1 and degp=k. Counting lifts of cells gives χ(Σ)=kχ(Σ2​)=−2k, hence 2−2g=−2k and g=k+1.
For k<0, use the ∣k∣-sheeted cover and compose with an orientation-reversing diffeomorphism of Σ2​, for instance reflection in a symmetric embedded genus-two surface. By multiplicativity of mapping degree, the degree becomes −∣k∣=k. For k=0 take a constant map from the torus. Thus the arbitrary-degree maps to a surface of genus two can be chosen with
g(k)=∣k∣+1,Σ∣k∣+1​⟶Σ2​ of degree k(k∈Z).​

A real rank-d vector bundle p:E→M is a continuous family of d-dimensional real vector spaces, locally trivial by homeomorphisms p−1(U)≅U×Rd which are linear on every fiber. Transition matrices are continuous maps into GLd​(R). The Grassmannian Grk​(Rn) is the space of k-dimensional linear subspaces; its topology can be defined by identifying each plane W with its orthogonal projection matrix PW​. Local graph charts identify nearby planes with graphs of maps W0​→W0⊥​.
The tautological bundle is Etaut​={(W,v):v∈W}→Grk​(Rn). In graph charts a basis of W0​ gives a continuously varying basis of each graph, proving local triviality. The universal quotient bundle has fiber QW​=Rn/W. The map [v]↦(I−PW​)v identifies it continuously with the orthogonal-complement bundle W⊥, whose graph charts similarly supply local frames. Thus Qtaut​ is a rank-(n−k) quotient vector bundle.
For the given E→M, at each m choose a basis of Em​ and global sections of a vector bundle realizing its basis vectors, using the evaluation-surjectivity assumption. Those d sections remain independent on a neighbourhood of m, since the corresponding determinant in a local trivialization is nonzero at m and continuous. Compactness gives a finite cover by such neighbourhoods. Collect all the selected sections as s1​,…,sN​; they span every fiber. They define a fiberwise surjective vector bundle morphism
F:M×RN⟶E,Fm​(c1​,…,cN​)=∑j=1N​cj​sj​(m).
(28)
Its kernels have constant dimension N−d and form the kernel bundle of a surjective vector bundle morphism. More concretely, in a local frame of E the map has a continuous full-row-rank matrix A(m) and its kernel projection is
PkerFm​​=IN​−A(m)T(A(m)A(m)T)−1A(m).
(29)
This varies continuously and is unchanged by an invertible change of target frame. The Grassmannian as projection matrices therefore makes
ϕ(m)=kerFm​∈GrN−d​(RN)
(30)
a continuous map. The fiber quotient map induced by Fm​ is an isomorphism RN/kerFm​→Em​. The same local matrix formula gives continuously varying right inverses, so these fiber isomorphisms give a vector bundle isomorphism. This proves the classification of vector bundles by a universal quotient bundle:
E≅ϕ∗Qtaut​.​
(31)
The displayed equality in the classification statement is understood as bundle isomorphism, not literal equality of total spaces.
Finally, the universal quotient bundle need not have a nowhere-zero section. Take Gr1​(R2)=RP1. Its quotient identifies with W⊥, a Möbius line bundle. Write Wθ​=span(cosθ,sinθ), with θ modulo π. A section of the complement line has the form a(θ)(−sinθ,cosθ), and being single-valued requires a(θ+π)=−a(θ). If a is nonzero anywhere, the intermediate value theorem forces a zero between that point and its translate by π; if it is nowhere nonzero the conclusion is immediate. Hence this rank-one quotient bundle has no nowhere-zero section.

For a rank-r real vector bundle with a fiber metric, the Thom isomorphism theorem says that if the bundle is oriented over the coefficient ring R, there is a Thom class u∈Hr(D(E),S(E);R) restricting to the chosen generator on every fiber pair, and
Hq(B;R) π∗(−)⌣u ​Hq+r(D(E),S(E);R)
(32)
is an isomorphism. For R=F2​, every real vector bundle has the required orientation. The disk bundle retracts to B, so apply the pair long exact sequence and the Thom isomorphism to get the Gysin sequence of a sphere bundle
⋯→Hq−r(B;R)⌣e(E)​Hq(B;R)π∗​Hq(S(E);R)π!​​Hq−r+1(B;R)⌣e(E)​Hq+1(B;R)→⋯.​
(33)
The first map is multiplication by the Euler class of a vector bundle, the pullback to the zero section of the absolute image of u. The connecting map followed by inverse Thom isomorphism gives π!​.
Apply this over F2​ to the tautological real line bundle γ on RPn. Its unit sphere bundle is Sn, with projection sending a unit vector to its line. Put a=e2​(γ)=w1​(γ)∈H1(RPn;F2​), the mod-two Euler class of a real line bundle. For n≥1 the degree-zero pullback H0(RPn)→H0(Sn) is an isomorphism. Exactness therefore makes the subsequent transfer zero, and multiplication by a from H0 into H1 injective. In intermediate degrees the sphere cohomology vanishes, making each successive multiplication by a an isomorphism. At degree n, the map from Hn−1 into Hn is injective; the transfer Hn(Sn)→Hn(RPn) is surjective because Hn+1(RPn)=0. Since Hn(Sn)=F2​, that top group is one-dimensional too, and an is its nonzero generator. The same endpoint reasoning covers n=1. This gives the Gysin proof of mod-two projective-space cohomology and the mod-two cohomology ring of real projective space:
H∗(RPn;F2​)=F2​[a]/(an+1),∣a∣=1.​
(34)
For n=0 this says simply H∗(RP0)=F2​.
For a self-map of RP4, the induced map has f∗a=λa with λ∈F2​, so f∗aj=λjaj. The mod-two alternating trace is
L2​(f)=1+λ+λ2+λ3+λ4=1
(35)
for either value of λ. The alternating trace on homology equals that on the finite cellular chain complex; consequently this is the reduction modulo two of the integral Lefschetz number. That integer is nonzero, and the Lefschetz fixed-point theorem forces a fixed point. Thus every self-map of the real projective four-space has a fixed point, a case of the Lefschetz fixed-point property of even-dimensional real projective space.
For the odd-dimensional example, let J(x1​,x2​,x3​,x4​)=(−x2​,x1​,−x4​,x3​), so J2=−I. Its projectivization is
F([x1​:x2​:x3​:x4​])=[−x2​:x1​:−x4​:x3​].​
(36)
It is well-defined and continuous on RP3. A fixed projective point would have Jv=tv for a nonzero real vector and real t, forcing −v=J2v=t2v and therefore t2=−1, impossible. This is a fixed-point-free complex-structure map on odd-dimensional real projective space.
