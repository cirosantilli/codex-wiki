The forward implication is immediate: the localization of a module that is zero remains zero. Conversely, suppose M=0 and choose 0=x∈M. Its annihilator
J=AnnA​(x)={a∈A:ax=0}
(2)

M=0⟺Mm​=0 for every maximal ideal m.​
(3)
In the suggested cyclic description, Ax≅A/J and part (a) gives (Ax)m​=0; the same fraction argument embeds it in Mm​. This is localization detects zero elements, applied to an arbitrary module; finite generation is unnecessary.

Suppose f is injective. If fm​(x/s)=0, some t∈/m satisfies tf(x)=f(tx)=0. Injectivity gives tx=0, and the vanishing criterion in a module localization gives x/s=0. Hence every localized module homomorphism is injective.
Conversely, suppose all fm​ are injective. For x∈kerf, its image x/1 in Mm​ maps to zero and therefore is zero for every maximal ideal m. The annihilator argument in part (b) implies x=0. Thus kerf=0 and f is injective. We have proved that injectivity of a module map is detected at maximal localizations:
f injective⟺fm​ injective for every maximal m.​
(4)
Equivalently, exactness of localization gives (kerf)m​≅ker(fm​), and part (b) detects whether this kernel is zero.

Use the defining criterion that a flat module E makes u⊗1E​ injective for every injective module homomorphism u. By right exactness of the tensor product, preserving injections is equivalent to preserving every short exact sequence.
First assume E is flat over A, and fix a maximal ideal m. Let u:U↪V be an injection of Am​-modules. Regard it as an injection of A-modules. There are natural module isomorphisms
U⊗Am​​Em​≅U⊗A​E,V⊗Am​​Em​≅V⊗A​E.
(5)
For example, u0​⊗(e/s) maps to s−1u0​⊗e, with inverse u0​⊗e↦u0​⊗(e/1). These are well-defined because every s∈/m already acts invertibly on U. Under these isomorphisms the tensor map is u⊗A​1E​, which is injective by flatness. Thus Em​ is flat over Am​.
For the converse, assume every Em​ is flat over Am​ and take any injection u:U↪V of A-modules. By part (c), each um​ is injective. Since localization commutes with tensor products, the localization of u⊗A​1E​ identifies with
um​⊗Am​​1Em​​:Um​⊗Am​​Em​⟶Vm​⊗Am​​Em​.
(6)
It is injective by the assumed local flatness. Apply part (c) again, now to u⊗A​1E​, to conclude that it is injective globally. Since u was arbitrary, E is flat. Therefore
E is <flat> over A⟺Em​ is <flat> over Am​ for every maximal m.​
(7)
This proves flatness is local without any finite-generation hypothesis.

Take B=Z, N=Z/2Z, and the short exact sequence
0⟶Z×2​Z⟶Z/2Z⟶0.
(8)
Multiplication by two is injective, and the reduction map has image all of Z/2Z and kernel 2Z, proving exactness. Any module homomorphism from Z/2Z to Z is zero: its generator must map to an integer killed by two. On the other hand, a homomorphism from Z/2Z to itself is determined arbitrarily by the image of its generator, so its Hom group is Z/2Z. Applying the Hom functor therefore gives
0⟶0⟶0⟶Z/2Z⟶0.
(9)
This sequence is not exact at Z/2Z: the incoming image is zero while the outgoing kernel is the whole nonzero group. In particular, the identity of N cannot lift through the reduction map to a map N→Z.

For any injection u:U↪V, the direct sum decomposition F=P⊕Q gives natural module isomorphisms
U⊗A​F≅(U⊗A​P)⊕(U⊗A​Q),V⊗A​F≅(V⊗A​P)⊕(V⊗A​Q).
(10)
Under these, u⊗1F​ is the block-diagonal map (u⊗1P​)⊕(u⊗1Q​). It is injective because F is a flat module. If an element lies in the kernel of u⊗1P​, insert zero in the other component; injectivity of the whole map forces that element to be zero. Hence u⊗1P​ is injective for every u, which proves P is flat​. This establishes that direct summands of flat modules are flat, without a finite-generation assumption.

Here the Hom groups must be taken over A; the printed subscript B in this part has no defined coefficient ring. For an arbitrary module P, form the free module
F=⨁p∈P​Aep​,
(11)
and define a module homomorphism π:F→P by ep​↦p. Each element of F has finite support, so this prescription extends uniquely and is well-defined. It is surjective because every p∈P is the image of ep​. Thus every module is a quotient of a free module.
Let K=kerπ. The short exact sequence 0→K→Fπ​P→0 and the defining exactness condition for a projective module make
HomA​(P,F)⟶HomA​(P,P),s⟼πs
(12)
surjective. Lift 1P​ to a map s:P→F. Then πs=1P​, so s is injective. Every f∈F has the decomposition
f=(f−sπ(f))+sπ(f),
(13)
whose first term lies in K and second lies in s(P). If s(p)∈K, applying π gives p=0, so the intersection is zero. Consequently
F=K⊕s(P)≅K⊕P.​
(14)
This is a split short exact sequence and proves that projective modules are direct summands of free modules, including modules that are not finitely generated.

First show that free modules are flat. If F=⨁j∈J​Aej​, distributivity of the tensor product of modules gives
U⊗A​F≅⨁j∈J​U.
(15)
Explicitly, u⊗∑j​aj​ej​ maps to (aj​u)j​, and the inverse sends a finitely supported tuple (uj​)j​ to ∑j​uj​⊗ej​. For an injection u:U↪V, the tensor map is therefore the coordinatewise injection ⨁J​U↪⨁J​V. This proves flatness for any free rank.
By part (c), a projective module P is a direct summand of a free module F. The latter is flat by the preceding argument, and part (b) proves that its summand P is flat. Therefore every projective A-module is flat​. This proves projective modules are flat directly from the exactness and splitting definitions.

Choose a1​,…,ad​∈m whose classes form a basis of the cotangent space of a local ring m/m2, and put J=(a1​,…,ad​). Spanning gives m=J+m2. Thus the quotient module N=m/J satisfies N=mN. It is a finitely generated module, since A is a Noetherian ring.
Here is the needed form of the Nakayama lemma, with proof. Choose generators n1​,…,nr​ of N and write ni​=∑j​cij​nj​ with cij​∈m. For the column vector n, (I−C)n=0. Multiply by the adjugate to obtain det(I−C)n=0. Since det(I−C)≡1(modm), this determinant is a unit in the local ring A, so every ni​=0. Therefore N=0, proving m=J. Conversely, any generators of m must span its cotangent space, so at least d are needed. Hence
μ(m)=d,​
(16)
Here μ(M) denotes the minimal number of generators of a module M.
For the additional minimum, the maximal ideal m is itself m-primary, so it supplies an eligible ideal generated by d elements. Consequently
min{μ(Q):Q is m-primary}≤d.​
(17)
The minimum here is over primary ideals. A bound μ(Q)≤d for every m-primary ideal would be false. Indeed, for A=k[[u,v]] and m=(u,v), d=2 but Q=m2=(u2,uv,v2) is primary and needs three generators. To verify primaryness, if ab∈Q and a∈/Q, then b cannot be a unit, so b∈m and b2∈Q. The three displayed monomials form a basis of Q/mQ=m2/m3, proving the lower bound of three, while they already generate Q. This illustrates that primary ideals can need more generators than the maximal ideal.

A formal power series f=∑n≥0​an​xn is a unit exactly when its constant coefficient is a unit in A. If fg=1, constant coefficients give a0​b0​=1. Conversely, for invertible a0​, set
b0​=a0−1​,bn​=−a0−1​∑i=1n​ai​bn−i​(n≥1).
(18)
Then every positive-degree coefficient of fg is zero and its constant coefficient is one, so g=∑bn​xn is an inverse. This is a formal coefficient calculation and requires no convergence.
Since A is a local ring, its nonunits are exactly m. The nonunits of B are consequently the series with a0​∈m, which are exactly I=mB+xB: such a series is a0​+xg, and conversely an element of I has constant coefficient in m. The map B→k taking the constant coefficient modulo m is surjective with kernel I, so I is a maximal ideal. Every proper ideal consists of nonunits and hence lies in I, proving that I is the unique maximal ideal. Thus
B is local,Max(B)={I},B/I≅k.​
(19)
This proves the description of formal power series over a local ring.
To compute the cotangent space of a formal power series ring, expand
I2=m2B+xmB+x2B.
(20)
Define the k-linear map
Θ:(m/m2)⊕k⟶I/I2,(aˉ,bˉ)⟼a+bx(modI2).
(21)
Changing a lift a by m2 or a lift b by m changes the image by I2, so the map is well-defined. Every series in I is congruent modulo x2B⊆I2 to its first two coefficients, proving surjectivity. If a+bx∈I2, its constant coefficient lies in m2 and its linear coefficient lies in m, so both input classes are zero. This proves injectivity. Therefore
I/I2≅(m/m2)⊕k,dimk​(I/I2)=d+1.​
(22)
Finally, part (a) gives generators a1​,…,ad​ of m, so I=(a1​,…,ad​,x). Use the Krull height theorem in its lecture form: in a Noetherian ring, a prime minimal over an ideal generated by r elements has height at most r. The problem allows Noetherianity of B to be assumed. Apply the theorem to I itself, whose height is the Krull dimension of the local ring B. Hence dimB=ht(I)≤d+1​.

Since A is a regular local ring, dimA=d is finite. Choose a chain of prime ideals of length d,
p0​⊊p1​⊊⋯⊊pd​=m.
(23)
Such a chain exists because the supremum defining this finite integer Krull dimension is attained; a maximal-length chain in a local ring ends at its maximal ideal.
For each i, the ideal pi​ is finitely generated because A is Noetherian. This ensures that its extension pi​B consists precisely of series all of whose coefficients lie in pi​: if pi​=(b1​,…,br​), write each coefficient as ∑j​bj​cjn​ and gather them into the finitely many series ∑n​cjn​xn. Thus coefficient reduction gives
B/pi​B≅(A/pi​)[[x]].
(24)
The coefficient ring A/pi​ is an integral domain, and so is its formal power series ring: the first nonzero coefficient of a product is the product of the first nonzero coefficients of its factors. Hence each pi​B is prime. Also pi​B∩A=pi​, so the strict inclusions persist upon extension. Finally, x∈/mB, since its image in B/mB≅k[[x]] is nonzero. We obtain the prime chain
p0​B⊊⋯⊊pd​B=mB⊊mB+(x)=I

dimA[[x]]=d+1.​

Let S=A∖p, let S act on B through φ, and write pB for the extension of an ideal generated by φ(p). Since κ(p)=S−1(A/p), the fiber ring has the canonical presentations
C=B⊗A​κ(p)≅S−1(B/pB)≅S−1B/pS−1B.
(27)
The first isomorphism sends b⊗(aˉ/s) to φ(a)b/s modulo pB. Its inverse sends the class of b/s to b⊗(1ˉ/s). The tensor balancing relation, reduction modulo p and the fraction relations show that these maps are well-defined and inverse. Thus the description is canonical and does not depend on chosen representatives.
Suppose q is a prime ideal of B with φ−1(q)=p. Then pB⊆q and q is disjoint from φ(S). Its localization S−1q is a proper prime, since the quotient is the localization of the integral domain B/q. It contains pS−1B, and therefore gives a prime
q⟼Q=S−1q/pS−1Bin C.
(28)
Conversely, for a prime Q⊂C, take its contraction q under the ring homomorphism ψ:B→C, b↦b⊗1. Contraction preserves primality. Every φ(a) with a∈p maps to zero, while every φ(s) with s∈S maps to a unit and hence cannot belong to a proper prime. Thus φ−1(q)=p.
These constructions are inverse. For a prime disjoint from φ(S), the fraction b/1 belongs to its localized extension exactly when some s∈S has φ(s)b∈q; primality then implies b∈q. In the other direction, every element of C has a representative b/s, and membership in Q is equivalent to membership of b/1 because the denominator is a unit. Hence Q is recovered by extending its contraction and quotienting. We have proved the canonical fiber of the map on spectra correspondence:
{q∈SpecB:φ−1(q)=p} ≅ Spec(B⊗A​κ(p)).​
(29)
If a denominator maps to zero, the fiber ring is the zero ring and both sets are empty; the same argument includes this case.

Let q be a prime ideal of C. Its quotient ring D=C/q is a nonzero integral domain and a finite-dimensional K-vector space. For 0=a∈D, multiplication by a is an injective K-linear map D→D, because D has no zero divisors. An injective endomorphism of a finite-dimensional vector space is surjective. In particular ab=1 for some b∈D. Every nonzero element is therefore invertible, so D is a field and q is a maximal ideal.
To prove finiteness, select any r distinct maximal ideals m1​,…,mr​. Distinct maximal ideals are comaximal: mi​+mj​ strictly contains mi​, so must equal C. The Chinese remainder theorem gives a surjection
C⟶∏i=1r​C/mi​.
(30)
Here is the needed surjectivity explicitly. For each j=i, choose aij​∈mj​ with aij​≡1(modmi​), using comaximality. Then ei​=∏j=i​aij​ is one modulo mi​ and zero modulo the other selected ideals. Given any residue tuple represented by bi​, the element ∑i​bi​ei​ maps to that tuple. Thus the displayed map really is surjective.
Each nonzero field quotient has K-dimension at least one, so
r≤∑i=1r​dimK​(C/mi​)≤dimK​C.
(31)
There cannot be more than dimK​C maximal ideals, because otherwise one could select a finite list exceeding this bound. Consequently
SpecC=MaxSpecC,∣MaxSpecC∣≤dimK​C<∞.​
(32)
This proves the description of the spectrum of a finite-dimensional commutative algebra. If C=0, its spectrum is empty and the same conclusion holds.

Choose algebra generators b1​,…,bn​ for B over A. Because the extension is integral, each bi​ satisfies a monic equation
biei​​+ai,ei​−1​biei​−1​+⋯+ai,0​=0(ai,j​∈A).
(33)
Repeatedly reduce any exponent at least ei​ using this equation. Since the generators commute, every polynomial in them becomes an A-linear combination of the finitely many monomials
b1ν1​​⋯bnνn​​,0≤νi​<ei​.
(34)
Thus B is a finitely generated module over A, with at most D=∏i​ei​ generators. This proves the relevant subalgebra generated by finitely many integral elements assertion rather than merely invoking it.
For a fixed prime ideal p⊂A, form the fiber ring C=B⊗A​κ(p). Tensoring the finite generating list shows dimκ(p)​C≤D: its pure tensors with one span the fiber over the residue field κ(p). Part (b) therefore gives only finitely many primes in C. By the canonical bijection in part (a), these are precisely the primes of B contracting to p. Hence
#{q∈SpecB:A∩q=p}≤dimκ(p)​(B⊗A​κ(p))≤D<∞.​
(35)
No Noetherian hypothesis on A is needed. For an empty algebra-generating list, B=A and the empty product D=1 gives the same conclusion.
