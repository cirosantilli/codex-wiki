A sigma-algebra on Ω is a collection G⊆P(Ω) containing Ω, closed under complements relative to Ω, and closed under countable unions. These properties also give closure under countable intersections by De Morgan laws.
Let G=⋂r∈R​Fr​. Every constituent sigma-algebra contains Ω, so Ω∈G. If A∈G, its complement belongs to every Fr​, hence to G. Likewise, if Aj​∈G for all j, then ⋃j​Aj​ belongs to every Fr​. Thus G is a sigma-algebra. For an empty indexing set, the usual intersection convention gives G=P(Ω), which is also a sigma-algebra.
A union need not work. On Ω={1,2,3} take
F1​={∅,Ω,{1},{2,3}},F2​={∅,Ω,{2},{1,3}}.
(1)
Their union contains {1} and {2} but not {1,2}, so it is not a sigma-algebra. Put A=⋃r​Fr​ and let C be the collection of all sigma-algebras on Ω containing A. This collection is nonempty because it contains the power set P(Ω). By the preceding intersection argument,
σ(A)=H∈C⋂​H​
(2)
is a sigma-algebra containing every Fr​, and it is contained in every other such sigma-algebra. This proves existence and minimality.
For the independent random variables, define the tail sigma-algebra by
T=⋂m≥1​σ(Xm​,Xm+1​,…).
(3)
Fix a tail event A∈T. For each m, it belongs to σ(Xm+1​,Xm+2​,…), which is independent of Hm​=σ(X1​,…,Xm​). To justify independence of the generated sigma-algebras, first use independence of finite collections on cylinder events, then extend twice by the pi-lambda theorem. In particular, P(A∩C)=P(A)P(C) for every C∈Hm​ and every m.
The increasing union ⋃m​Hm​ is an algebra of sets generating H=σ(X1​,X2​,…). The collection of C∈H satisfying this factorization is a Dynkin system: it contains Ω, is closed under complements, and under countable disjoint unions. The pi-lambda theorem therefore extends the factorization to all C∈H. Since A∈H, taking C=A gives P(A)=P(A)2. Consequently,
P(A)∈{0,1}(A∈T).​

This is the Kolmogorov zero-one law, proved here directly.
For each fixed m, write Sn​=Sm−1​+∑j=mn​Xj​ for n≥m, with S0​=0. The random variable Sm−1​ is finite, so Sm−1​/n​→0. Adding a real sequence tending to zero changes neither the extended-real limit inferior nor the extended-real limit superior. Hence
liminfn​n​Sn​​=liminfn​n​∑j=mn​Xj​​,limsupn​n​Sn​​=limsupn​n​∑j=mn​Xj​​.
(5)
The quantities on the right are measurable with respect to σ(Xm​,Xm+1​,…): write the limit inferior as supN​infn≥N​ and the limit superior as infN​supn≥N​, using countable operations on measurable functions. Therefore both threshold events belong to every such sigma-algebra, and hence are tail events, for every real threshold, including when either limit is infinite.
The final recurrence claim is false under the printed hypotheses. For c>0, let εj​ be independent signs with equal probabilities for +1 and −1, and set Xj​=c4−(j−1)εj​. These random variables are independent, have symmetric distributions, and obey ∣Xj​∣≤c. Nevertheless, the geometric series gives, on every sample path,
∣Sn​∣≥c−∑j=2n​c4−(j−1)≥c−3c​=32c​>2c​.
(6)
Thus the proposed repeated-return event has probability zero, even though none of the increments is degenerate. The Kolmogorov zero-one law does not determine which of zero and one occurs.
An additional identical-distribution hypothesis makes the intended conclusion true. Here is a full proof of that qualified version, using the oscillation of a bounded centered iid random walk. Symmetry and integrability give EXj​=0, so Sn​ is a martingale for its natural filtration. If the common probability distribution is concentrated at zero, every Sn​ is zero and the conclusion is immediate. Otherwise choose δ>0 with P(∣X1​∣>δ)>0. The Borel-Cantelli lemmas then give ∣Xn​∣>δ infinitely often almost surely; thus Sn​ cannot converge to a finite limit.
For a>0, let Ta​=inf{n:Sn​≥a}. Before Ta​ the sum is below a, and at Ta​ it is at most a+c. The stopped martingale a+c−Sn∧Ta​​ is therefore nonnegative. A stopped integrable martingale remains a martingale at these bounded times, and a nonnegative martingale converges finitely almost surely by the martingale convergence theorem. On {Ta​=∞} this would force Sn​ to converge finitely, which has just been ruled out. Hence Ta​<∞ almost surely. Apply the same argument to −Sn​ and take a countable intersection over positive integer a. It follows that
limsupn​Sn​=+∞,liminfn​Sn​=−∞almost surely.

A path with bounded increments of magnitude at most c cannot make such a passage without visiting [−c/2,c/2]: jumping directly between its two open complementary half-lines would require an increment larger than c. There are infinitely many such visits. Thus with the added identical-distribution hypothesis, the intended answer is
P(∣Sn​∣≤c/2 infinitely often)=1.​

The real L2 space on a probability space consists of equivalence classes of measurable functions X:Ω→R satisfying E[X2]<∞, where two random variables represent the same element if they are equal almost surely. Its norm is
∥X∥2​=(E[X2])1/2.
(19)
Passing to equivalence classes makes this a genuine norm: ∥X∥2​=0 exactly when X=0 almost surely. The Minkowski inequality gives the triangle inequality.
Let (Xn​) be a Cauchy sequence in this norm. Choose an increasing sequence of indices nk​ such that
∥Xnk+1​​−Xnk​​∥2​≤2−k(k≥1).
(20)
This is possible by choosing nk​ beyond a Cauchy threshold for tolerance 2−k. Set Dk​=Xnk+1​​−Xnk​​. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality on a probability space gives E∣Dk​∣≤∥Dk​∥2​. By Tonelli theorem,
E[∑k≥1​∣Dk​∣]=∑k≥1​E∣Dk​∣≤∑k≥1​2−k<∞.
(21)
Thus the series of absolute differences is finite almost surely. Define X=Xn1​​+∑k≥1​Dk​ on this probability-one event, and give it the value zero on its complement. It is a measurable function, and Xnk​​→X almost surely.
For l>k, the Minkowski inequality gives
∥Xnl​​−Xnk​​∥2​≤∑j=kl−1​2−j.
(22)
Apply the Fatou lemma to the squared difference as l→∞. It follows that
∥X−Xnk​​∥2​≤∑j=k∞​2−j=21−k.
(23)
In particular X−Xn1​​∈L2, and therefore X∈L2 by the Minkowski inequality. This proves convergence in L2 of the subsequence. To recover the entire sequence, for a prescribed ε>0 choose a Cauchy threshold N for ε/2, then choose k with nk​≥N and ∥X−Xnk​​∥2​<ε/2. For every n≥N,
∥Xn​−X∥2​≤∥Xn​−Xnk​​∥2​+∥Xnk​​−X∥2​<ε.
(24)
Hence Xn​→X in L2​, proving completeness of Lp spaces for p=2 without assuming the desired completeness in the argument.

A filtration (Fn​)n≥0​ on a probability space is an increasing sequence of sigma-algebras contained in F: Fn​⊆Fn+1​. A real stochastic process (Xn​) is a martingale for this filtration if Xn​ is Fn​-measurable, E∣Xn​∣<∞, and
E[Xn+1​∣Fn​]=Xn​almost surely for every n.​
(25)
The first condition is adaptedness; the tower property of conditional expectation then gives E[Xn​∣Fm​]=Xm​ whenever m≤n.
The almost-sure martingale convergence theorem states that if a real martingale satisfies supn​E∣Xn​∣<∞, then some integrable random variable X∞​ satisfies Xn​→X∞​ almost surely. The more general almost sure submartingale convergence theorem needs only supn​E[Xn+​]<∞ for a submartingale. In particular, a nonnegative martingale converges to a finite integrable limit almost surely, because its expected value is constant. Mere boundedness in the L1 norm does not imply convergence in L1. The stronger uniformly integrable martingale convergence theorem gives both almost-sure convergence and convergence in L1 when the martingale is uniformly integrable.

A standard Brownian motion is a real stochastic process (Bt​)t≥0​ with B0​=0 almost surely, continuous sample paths almost surely, and independent increments such that Bt​−Bs​ has normal distribution N(0,t−s) whenever 0≤s<t. With its completed, right-continuous natural filtration (Ft​), its Strong Markov property is the following: for every almost-surely finite stopping time T, the process
Ws​=BT+s​−BT​,s≥0,
(54)
is a standard Brownian motion independent of FT​. Here the stopping-time sigma-algebra is
FT​={A∈F:A∩{T≤t}∈Ft​ for all t≥0}.
(55)
In particular, for every bounded FT​-measurable random variable H and bounded Borel functional G on the continuous path space,
E[HG((BT+s​−BT​)s≥0​)]=E[H]E[G((Bs​)s≥0​)].
(56)
Thus the independence is from the entire stopped past, not only from BT​, and is a statement about the whole future path, not just a single increment.
To prove the Brownian reflection principle without assuming that a level is hit in finite time, fix t>0, m>0, and set Tm​=inf{s≥0:Bs​≥m} and R=Tm​∧t. Continuity of the sample paths makes Tm​ a stopping time and gives BTm​​=m whenever Tm​<∞. The stopping time R is bounded. Reflect the path after R:
Bs​={Bs​,2BR​−Bs​,​s≤R,s>R.​
(57)
By the Strong Markov property, the future increments after R are an independent Brownian motion. Negating them preserves their probability distribution, by symmetry of their joint normal distributions. Hence the reflected process B has the same path law as B.
If Tm​>t, reflection changes nothing on [0,t] and Bt​=Bt​<m. If Tm​≤t, both paths first hit m at the same time and Bt​=2m−Bt​. Therefore, for x≤m, the following events agree path by path:
{Mt​≥m, Bt​≤x}={Bt​≥2m−x}.
(58)
Indeed, the right-hand endpoint is at least m, so the reflected path must have hit m before or at t; this also forces the original path to have done so. The reflection relation then makes the endpoint inequality equivalent to Bt​≤x. Equality cases at Tm​=t satisfy the same relation. Since Bt​ and Bt​ have the same probability distribution,
P(Mt​≥m, Bt​≤x)=P(Bt​≥2m−x)(m>0, x≤m).​
(59)
For t=0, both sides are zero, because B0​=M0​=0 and 2m−x≥m>0.
For t>0, set x=m in this identity. Continuity implies {Bt​>m}⊆{Mt​≥m}, and the normal distribution of Bt​ has no atom at m. Splitting according to whether Bt​≤m gives
P(Mt​≥m)=P(Bt​≥m)+P(Bt​>m)=2P(Bt​≥m)=P(∣Bt​∣≥m).
(60)
Both random variables are nonnegative. Their tails agree for all m>0, which determines their probability distributions, including the mass at zero. Thus the Brownian running maximum satisfies
Mt​=d∣Bt​∣(t≥0).​
(61)
For x>0 and t>0, continuity again gives {Tx​≤t}={Mt​≥x}. Since Bt​/t​ is a standard normal random variable, the distribution function of the Brownian first-passage time is
FTx​​(t)=2[1−Φ(t​x​)].
(62)
It tends to one as t→∞ and to zero as t↓0, proving almost-sure finiteness and absence of an atom at zero. For the standard normal random variable B1​,
P((B1​x​)2≤t)=P(∣B1​∣≥t​x​)=2[1−Φ(t​x​)].
(63)
The value of this ratio on the zero-probability event {B1​=0} may be defined arbitrarily. Matching the distribution functions proves
Tx​=d(B1​x​)2.​
(64)
Finally, differentiate the distribution function on t>0, using Φ′=ϕ and dtd​(x/t​)=−x/(2t3/2). The first-passage-time density is
fTx​​(t)=2π​t3/2x​exp(−2tx2​)(t>0),​
(65)
and is zero for t≤0. Its integral is one by the endpoint limits of FTx​​, so no missing mass at an infinite hitting time is present.
