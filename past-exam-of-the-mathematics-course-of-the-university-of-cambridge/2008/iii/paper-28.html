The Hasse theorem for elliptic curves states
∣#E(Fq​)−(q+1)∣≤2q​.​
(1)
We prove it by the positive degree of an isogeny. Let π be the q-power Frobenius isogeny, of degree q. Its fixed points are precisely E(Fq​), so they form the kernel of 1−π. Since the differential of π is zero, the differential of 1−π is the identity; thus 1−π is a separable isogeny. Its degree is the number of its geometric kernel points. Therefore
N:=#E(Fq​)=deg(1−π).
(2)
We use the standard degree parallelogram law, together with deg[m]=m2: degree, extended by zero at the zero endomorphism, is a positive quadratic form on the endomorphism ring of an elliptic curve. Put t=1+q−N, the Trace of Frobenius. Polarizing the degree form gives, for every pair of integers m,n,
deg([m]−[n]π)=m2−tmn+qn2≥0.
(3)
Indeed, the values on 1 and π are 1 and q, and the cross coefficient is fixed by deg(1−π)=1+q−t. These are standard degree facts about isogenies of elliptic curves, which suffice without an assumption about characteristic or ordinariness.
For n=0, division by n2 shows x2−tx+q≥0 at every rational x=m/n. Density of the rational numbers and continuity of this polynomial make it nonnegative on the real line. Evaluating at its minimum x=t/2 gives q−t2/4≥0, whence ∣t∣≤2q​. Since N=q+1−t, this proves the estimate. This is the degree-form proof of the Hasse bound; it proves both sides simultaneously by positivity of one degree form.

Let t=q+1−#E(Fq​). The stated characteristic equation for the Frobenius isogeny is
π2−[t]π+[q]=0.
(4)
Take α,β to be the complex roots of T2−tT+q. They satisfy α+β=t and αβ=q. By the Hasse bound, t2≤4q. If the inequality is strict, the roots are complex conjugates and have squared modulus q. In the equality case both roots are the same real number ±q​. Thus in every case
∣α∣=∣β∣=q​,β=q/α.
(5)
To obtain every extension-field count without assuming distinct roots, set tn​=tr(πn) and t0​=2. The dual isogeny satisfies ππ=[q] and π+π=[t]. Consequently the sums πn+πn=[tn​] satisfy
tn​=ttn−1​−qtn−2​,t0​=2,t1​=t.
(6)
Alternatively, apply additivity of the trace of an elliptic-curve endomorphism directly to the characteristic equation multiplied by πn−2. The sequence αn+βn has the same recurrence and initial values, so tn​=αn+βn by induction. This is the Frobenius trace recurrence, valid also at a repeated root.
The fixed points of πn are E(Fqn​), and 1−πn is again separable. Since degπn=qn, the trace-degree identity gives
#E(Fqn​)=deg(1−πn)=1+qn−tn​=1+qn−αn−βn.
(7)
Factoring and using β=q/α yields
#E(Fqn​)=(1−αn)(1−qnα−n),∣α∣=q​(n≥1).​
(8)
This is the elliptic-curve point count over a finite field formula and the Riemann hypothesis for an elliptic curve over a finite field. In particular the powers on both factors are necessary; they are damaged in the converted TeX but present in the original PDF.

To check smoothness, a singular point with all coordinates nonzero would satisfy 3x2=2yz, 3y2=2xz, 39z2=2xy. Multiplication would force 351=8, a contradiction. Indeed, a point in the kernel is represented by the formal group of an elliptic curve on the maximal ideal. A one-dimensional commutative formal group law over the p-adic integers is a power series F(X,Y)∈Zp​[[X,Y]] with
F(X,0)=X,F(0,Y)=Y,F(X,Y)=F(Y,X),
(19)
F(F(X,Y),Z)=F(X,F(Y,Z)).
(20)
Its linear part is X+Y, and it has a unique formal inverse i(T)=−T+O(T2) satisfying F(T,i(T))=0. A formal-group homomorphism is a series h(T)∈TZp​[[T]] satisfying h(F(X,Y))=F(h(X),h(Y)).
Define [n](T) by adding T to itself n times with the formal group law. Induction on the linear part gives
[n](T)=nT+O(T2).
(21)
Associativity and commutativity make [n] a formal-group homomorphism. Since p∤n, its linear coefficient is a unit in Zp​. We now construct its compositional inverse of a formal power series, rather than merely assert that the nonzero coefficient is sufficient.
Write [n](T)=nT+a2​T2+⋯ and seek g(T)=b1​T+b2​T2+⋯ with [n](g(T))=T. The degree-one coefficient gives b1​=n−1. At degree k≥2, the coefficient is nbk​ plus a polynomial in the already chosen b1​,…,bk−1​ and a2​,…,ak​. Division by the unit n uniquely chooses bk​∈Zp​. This recursively constructs g over the same ring. A right inverse is constructed in the same way; associativity of composition identifies it with g, so g∘[n]=[n]∘g=T.
To prove that g respects the group law, apply [n] to the two candidate series:
[n](F(g(X),g(Y)))=F(X,Y)=[n](g(F(X,Y))).
(22)
Cancel by composing with g. The result is F(g(X),g(Y))=g(F(X,Y)). Thus
[n]:F⟶F is an isomorphism over Zp​.​
(23)
This is the multiplication isomorphism of a formal group law, a special case of the invertible morphism criterion for formal group laws. If p∣n, its linear coefficient is not a unit, so the same isomorphism assertion would fail.

R=P−nQ0​

has zero reduction and belongs to E1​(L), the kernel of reduction of an elliptic curve.

A congruent number is a positive integer which occurs as the area of a right triangle with positive rational side lengths. Scaling a triangle by a nonzero rational factor multiplies its area by a rational square, so the essential parameter is its positive square class; integer parameters can be reduced to square-free integers. For example 6 is congruent by the triangle (3,4,5), and 5 is congruent by (3/2,20/3,41/6).
The connection with elliptic curves comes from the congruent number elliptic curve in the model
EN​:y2=x3−N2x.
(26)
From a rational point with y=0, define
a=​yx2−N2​​,b=​y2Nx​​,c=​yx2+N2​​.
(27)
Direct squaring gives a2+b2=c2, and its area is
2ab​=y2N∣x(x2−N2)∣​=N.
(28)
Conversely, from a positive rational triangle (a,b,c) of area N, put
x=Nbc+a​,y=2N2b2c+a​.
(29)
Substitution using c2=a2+b2 and ab=2N gives y2=x3−N2x and y=0. Thus
N congruent⟺EN​(Q) has a point with y=0.​
(30)
The standard rational torsion of a congruent number curve consists of O,(0,0),(N,0),(−N,0). One way to see the torsion restriction is to use good primes p≡3(mod4). The character sum cancels at x and −x, giving #EN​(Fp​)=p+1. For any odd prime ℓ, the Dirichlet theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions supplies good primes with p≡3(mod4) and p≡1(modℓ), so the reduction of torsion points on an elliptic curve excludes ℓ-torsion. Good primes p≡3(mod8) similarly bound the two-primary torsion by four, and the four displayed points give equality. Consequently the Mordell-Weil theorem turns the criterion into positive rank of EN​(Q). Arithmetic two-descent on an elliptic curve and local obstructions are therefore tools for the congruent number problem, and a nontorsion point gives infinitely many rational right triangles from a nontorsion point by taking multiples.
We now give elementary proofs for 1 and 2, including the descent steps. Recall the primitive Pythagorean parametrization
A=2mn,B=m2−n2,C=m2+n2,
(31)
where m>n>0 are coprime and of opposite parity. To derive it, in a primitive Pythagorean triple put the even leg at A. The relatively prime integers (C+B)/2,(C−B)/2 have product (A/2)2, so each is a square, say m2,n2. This gives the formulas and the parity condition. The area is mn(m−n)(m+n), and its four factors are pairwise coprime: their common divisors reduce to divisors of m,n or of two, while m±n are odd.
Area one is impossible. Clearing denominators of a hypothetical rational triangle of area one gives an integer triangle with square area. Dividing out its common factor leaves a primitive integer triangle whose area is still a rational square; an integer which is a rational square is an integer square. Choose such a triangle with smallest hypotenuse. Pairwise coprimality in the area product forces
m=r2,n=s2,m+n=u2,m−n=v2.
(32)
The positive integers u,v are odd and coprime. If m were even, then n would be odd and u2−v2=2s2 would be 2 modulo eight, whereas two odd squares have difference zero modulo eight. Hence m is odd, n is even and s is even.

a2+b2=2u2+v2​=m=r2,2ab​=8u2−v2​=4s2​.
(33)
Thus (a,b,r) is another integer right triangle with square area and hypotenuse r, strictly smaller than m2+n2. This contradicts the minimal choice and proves the Fermat right triangle theorem. In particular 1 is not a congruent number.
For area two we first establish the auxiliary descent fourth-power sum cannot be a square. Suppose x4+y4=z2 with positive integers, and choose a primitive solution with smallest z. Two odd fourth powers sum to 2 modulo sixteen, so after exchange x is odd and y is even. Parametrize the primitive triangle (x2,y2,z):
x2=m2−n2,y2=2mn,z=m2+n2.
(34)
The case m even, n odd makes x2≡3(mod4), so m is odd and n even. Coprimality then gives m=u2 and n=2v2. Consequently
x2+(2v2)2=(u2)2.
(35)
This second triangle is primitive, since x is odd and gcd(x,v)=1. Parametrize it as x=r2−s2, 2v2=2rs, u2=r2+s2, with r,s coprime. The equality v2=rs makes r=a2, s=b2. Thus
a4+b4=u2,
(36)
a new positive solution with smaller hypotenuse u<z=u4+4v4. This contradicts infinite descent, proving the auxiliary assertion.
Area two is also impossible. Clearing denominators and removing a common factor now gives a primitive integer triangle with area twice an integer square. Indeed, if an integer is 2(a/b)2 with gcd(a,b)=1, then b2 divides two, forcing b=1. Its area product would satisfy

(40)

Let K be a field, Ks​ a separable closure and GK​ its absolute Galois group. A discrete Galois module A is an abelian group with a continuous GK​-action, so each element has an open stabilizer. Galois cohomology uses continuous cochains. Its zeroth group is H0(K,A)=AGK​, and its first group is
H1(K,A)=Z1(K,A)/B1(K,A),
(41)
where
cστ​=cσ​+σcτ​,B1(K,A)={cσ​=σa−a:a∈A}.
(42)
Thus cocycles measure a failure of descent, and coboundaries account for changing a chosen lift. A short exact sequence of Galois modules gives a long exact sequence in group cohomology. Its connecting map sends an invariant element of a quotient to the cocycle σb−b of a chosen lift b; changing the lift changes this by a coboundary. These constructions provide the bridge between arithmetic divisibility and cohomology.
For Kummer theory, assume the characteristic does not divide n. The multiplicative exact sequence is
1⟶μn​⟶Ks×​,n,​Ks×​⟶1.
(43)
The final arrow is surjective because a root of Tn−a is separable for a=0. Hilbert's theorem 90 says H1(K,Ks×​)=0. Here is its usual finite-extension proof. For a multiplicative cocycle cσ​ on a finite Galois extension L/K, choose a∈L so that
A=∑σ∈Gal(L/K)​cσ​σ(a)=0.
(44)
Such an a exists by the Artin independence theorem. The cocycle identity gives τA=cτ−1​A. With b=A−1 this becomes cτ​=τb/b, a coboundary. Every continuous cocycle with values in Ks×​ is defined over a sufficiently large finite Galois extension, so the same result holds for the absolute group.
The long exact sequence in group cohomology now gives
H1(K,μn​)≅K×/(K×)n.​
(45)
The class of a maps to cσ​=σ(α)/α, where αn=a. This cohomological statement does not require μn​⊂K; it retains the actual Galois action on μn​. If all nth roots of unity do lie in K, the action is trivial, and after choosing a primitive root of unity the cocycles are characters into Z/nZ. Thus adjoining an nth root of a describes a cyclic extension of degree dividing n, and Kummer classes classify the corresponding characters. For n=2, this gives the familiar quadratic extensions and the square-class group of a field.
Now let K be a number field and E/K an elliptic curve. The multiplication-by-n morphism is a surjective isogeny on E(K), with kernel E[n]≅(Z/nZ)2. The Kummer exact sequence of an elliptic curve
0⟶E[n]⟶E(K)[n]​E(K)⟶0
(46)
yields
0⟶E(K)/nE(K)δ​H1(K,E[n])⟶H1(K,E)[n]⟶0.
(47)
Explicitly, choose Q with nQ=P and set δ(P)σ​=σQ−Q. This lies in E[n], satisfies the cocycle identity and is unchanged as a class if Q is replaced by another division point. If it is a coboundary σT−T for T∈E[n], then Q−T is rational over K and P=n(Q−T). Conversely such a rational division point makes the cocycle zero. This proves the injectivity of the Kummer map of an elliptic curve on E(K)/nE(K).
To prove the Weak Mordell-Weil theorem, it remains to prove that this particular cohomological image is finite. The full group H1(K,E[n]) need not be finite, so the injection alone is not enough. Choose a finite set S of finite places containing the bad-reduction places and those dividing n. At any other place, the construction in Question 3 works over the local field: reduce P, find an n-division point over a finite residue extension, lift it by the Hensel lemma, and correct its error by the inverse formal multiplication. Thus P has an n-division point over the maximal unramified extension. Choosing that lift locally makes the cocycle zero on inertia. For an arbitrary lift its restriction is a coboundary, so the cohomology class is unramified. Therefore the image lies in first Galois cohomology unramified outside a finite set:
δ(E(K)/nE(K))⊆HS1​(K,E[n]).
(48)
We prove the required finiteness of this group. Take a finite Galois extension L/K containing all of E[n] and μn​, and enlarge S to include any remaining ramification of L/K. Let SL​ be the places above it. The inflation-restriction exact sequence bounds the kernel of restriction to L by
H1(Gal(L/K),E[n]),
(49)
a finite group, since both its group of arguments and its group of values are finite. Over L the torsion module is constant and can be identified with μn2​, so Kummer theory gives
H1(L,E[n])≅(L×/(L×)n)2.
(50)
Restrictions of our unramified classes remain unramified outside SL​. If a Kummer class [a] is unramified at such a place v, its nth root can be taken in an unramified local extension. The extended valuation still has integer values, and nv(na​)=v(a). Hence v(a) is divisible by n. Each coordinate therefore lies in the S-unramified power class group
L(SL​,n)={[a]∈L×/(L×)n:v(a)≡0(modn) for v∈/SL​}.
(51)
This group is finite. Indeed, the ideal of a away from SL​ is an. Sending [a] to [a] yields the exact sequence
0⟶OL,SL​×​/(OL,SL​×​)n⟶L(SL​,n)⟶Cl(OL,SL​​)[n]⟶0.
(52)
For its kernel, a principal a lets us divide a by an nth power to obtain an SL​-unit. For surjectivity, an ideal class killed by n has an nth power which is principal away from SL​, giving such an a. The S-unit group is finitely generated and the ideal class group is finite; hence both end groups are finite, proving finiteness of S-unramified Kummer classes. Restriction has finite image in L(SL​,n)2 and finite kernel, so HS1​(K,E[n]) is finite. We conclude
#(E(K)/nE(K))<∞(n≥2),​
(53)
which is the Weak Mordell-Weil theorem and its Kummer-theoretic proof of the weak Mordell-Weil theorem.
This framework also defines the Selmer group of an elliptic curve by imposing that a class in H1(K,E[n]) lie in the local Kummer image at every completion. Its classes are unramified outside a fixed finite set, so the same finiteness proof applies. Its relation to rational points is
0⟶E(K)/nE(K)⟶Sel(n)(E/K)⟶Sha(E/K)[n]⟶0.
(54)
The Tate–Shafarevich group here measures the difference between locally soluble torsors and globally soluble ones. Finally, the weak theorem gives finite divisibility quotients; adding the height descent lemma and bounded-height finiteness proves the stronger Mordell-Weil theorem. Those height inputs are additional to the cohomological finiteness just established.
