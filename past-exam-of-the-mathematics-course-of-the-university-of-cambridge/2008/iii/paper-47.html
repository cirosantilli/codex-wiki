A weakly stationary process has finite second moments, a mean m=EXt​ independent of t, and a covariance depending only on the separation of the observations. Its autocovariance function and autocorrelation function are
γ(h)=E[(Xt+h​−m)(Xt​−m)],ρ(h)=γ(h)/γ(0),
(1)
where the autocorrelation requires γ(0)>0.
Write the autoregressive polynomial as A(z)=1−αz+αz2, so the autoregressive model is A(B)Xt​=ϵt​, with B the backshift operator. It is important to distinguish a weakly stationary process from a causal time series: weak stationarity alone does not require Xt​ to depend only on present and past white noise.
For a two-sided weakly stationary process, the two-sided stationary inverse of an autoregressive polynomial exists and is unique if A has no root on the unit circle. To find the excluded parameters, set z=eiλ. The imaginary part of A(z)=0 gives
αsinλ(2cosλ−1)=0.
(2)
The case α=0 is harmless. The possible frequencies are 0,π,±π/3. Since A(1)=1, A(−1)=1+2α and A(e±iπ/3)=1−α, the exceptional parameters are −1/2 and 1. Thus, with nondegenerate white noise, the literal two-sided answer is
α∈R∖{−21​,1}.​
(3)
For completeness, away from those values 1/A(z) has an absolutely summable Laurent series on an annulus containing the unit circle. Its bilateral coefficients define an L2-convergent linear filter of a stationary time series applied to ϵt​, giving the required solution. Applying the same inverse to the equation gives uniqueness. At an exceptional value, the spectral measure of a stationary time series would have to satisfy ∣A(eiλ)∣2μX​(dλ)=σ2dλ/(2π). A zero of A on the unit circle makes the resulting density nonintegrable near that zero, contradicting finite variance.
If the usual additional causal time series convention is intended, the causality root criterion for an autoregressive model requires the roots of A to lie outside the closed unit disk. Equivalently, the roots of u2−αu+α lie strictly inside it. The real quadratic stability conditions reduce to ∣α∣<1 and 1+2α>0, giving the narrower answer
−21​<α<1for the causal solution.​
(4)
The printed question does not explicitly impose this extra convention. Both answers have therefore been distinguished. If σ2=0, the exceptional parameters permit nonunique stationary homogeneous solutions rather than the nonexistence conclusion above.
At α=−1/12,
A(z)=(1−z/4)(1+z/3),A(z)1​=1−z/43/7​+1+z/34/7​.
(5)
Consequently the Wold representation is
Xt​=j=0∑∞​ψj​ϵt−j​,ψj​=73​(41​)j+74​(−31​)j.​
(6)
The coefficients are absolutely summable, and ψ0​=1. The past of X lies in the closed linear span of the past white noise; conversely ϵt​=A(B)Xt​ recovers that noise from present and past X. Thus ϵt​ is orthogonal to the past of X and is its one-step linear prediction error, as required for the Wold decomposition. There is no deterministic component.
Using orthogonality of the white noise, put H=∣h∣ and calculate the autocovariance by geometric sums:
γ(h)/σ2​=j≥0∑​ψj​ψj+H​=499​1−1/16(1/4)H​+4916​1−1/9(−1/3)H​+4912​1+1/12(1/4)H+(−1/3)H​=455192​(1/4)H+9154​(−1/3)H.​
(7)
Hence
γ(h)=4556σ2​[32(1/4)∣h∣+45(−1/3)∣h∣].​
(8)
In particular, γ(0)=66σ2/65 and γ(1)=−6σ2/65; these also satisfy the Yule-Walker equations for this autoregressive model.

A good pseudorandom number generator for Monte Carlo integration should have approximately uniform outputs, a period much longer than the intended run, and negligible detectable dependence both serially and in higher-dimensional tuples. It should be fast, reproducible from a seed, portable enough to reproduce a calculation, and numerically precise enough for the required tail probabilities. Values strictly inside (0,1) avoid singularities in logarithmic inverse transform sampling. Separate simulations also need well-separated streams or seeds, rather than accidentally identical streams. A pseudorandom number generator is deterministic; these properties mean that it imitates independent uniform distributions sufficiently well for the application, not that its outputs are literally independent.

For rate λ>0, the exponential distribution has cumulative distribution function F(x)=1−e−λx on x≥0. Inverse transform sampling gives −log(1−U)/λ; since 1−U is also uniform, an equivalent answer is
E=−logU/λ.​
(18)
For x≥0, P(E>x)=P(U<e−λx)=e−λx, verifying the exponential distribution directly.
The centered Laplace distribution with density (λ/2)e−λ∣x∣ can be obtained by multiplying E by an independent sign taking 1 and −1 with equal probabilities. Alternatively, its inverse transform sampling formula uses just one uniform variate:
L={log(2U)/λ,−log(2(1−U))/λ,​0<U<1/2,1/2≤U<1.​​
(19)
The first branch inverts the negative half of the cumulative distribution function and the second branch inverts the positive half. Add a location parameter if a noncentered Laplace distribution is wanted.
Correct uniformity is essential for each marginal distribution, and independence is essential for independent samples and for the sign construction. For example, setting every Ui​ equal to a single uniform U gives uniform marginals but identical transformed draws. Likewise, choosing a sign from the same uniform used for the magnitude can change the Laplace distribution unless the one-uniform inverse above is used.

The first algorithm uses independent normal distributions: generate Z1​,…,Zν​ with law N(0,1) and return
Y=j=1∑ν​Zj2​.​
(20)
For example, the Box-Muller transform generates two such normal variates from independent uniforms:
Z1​=−2logU​cos(2πV),Z2​=−2logU​sin(2πV).
(21)
To verify this, R2=−2logU has density e−s/2/2 and 2πV is an independent uniform angle. Converting the resulting polar density to Cartesian coordinates gives (2π)−1e−(z12​+z22​)/2, which factors into two independent normal distributions. Independent pairs therefore give the required sum, discarding a spare coordinate when ν is odd. Its moment-generating function is (1−2t)−ν/2, that of the chi-squared distribution.
A genuinely different algorithm uses exponential-envelope rejection sampling for a chi-squared variable. Put a=ν/2≥1. The target and proposal probability density functions are
f(y)=2aΓ(a)ya−1e−y/2​,g(y)=ν1​e−y/ν,y>0.
(22)
For a>1, differentiating log(f/g) gives (a−1)(1/y−1/ν), so the maximum is at y=ν. The envelope constant and normalized density ratio are
M=Γ(a)aae1−a​,Mg(y)f(y)​=exp((a−1)[log(y/ν)−y/ν+1]).
(23)
They remain valid at a=1, where M=1 and f=g. Repeatedly draw fresh independent U,V, put Y=−νlogU, and accept when
logV≤(a−1)[log(Y/ν)−Y/ν+1].​
(24)
The right side is nonpositive by logu≤u−1, so this is a legitimate acceptance probability. The joint probability of proposing and accepting in dy is g(y)f(y)/(Mg(y))dy=f(y)dy/M; conditional on acceptance the density is exactly f. This proves the rejection sampling algorithm, rather than only identifying the target law.
For ν=2 I prefer the second method: it reduces to a single exponential draw with no rejection. The first method is simple and attractive when normal draws are already available or ν is small. For larger ν, the second method avoids generating ν normals: its acceptance probability is 1/M, asymptotic to 2π​/(ea​) by Stirling's formula. Its expected number of proposals grows like ν​, rather than the linear number of normal draws in the first method. Both are exact ideal-uniform algorithms; actual runtime also depends on the implementation.

For Jackknife resampling, calculate the leave-one-out estimates θ(−i)​ by deleting observation i, and their average θJ​=n−1∑i​θ(−i)​. The Jackknife variance estimator is
VJ​=nn−1​i=1∑n​(θ(−i)​−θJ​)2.​
(28)
The factor n−1 compensates for the small differences between the overlapping samples. For sufficiently smooth statistics it estimates their sampling variance. The related estimated bias is (n−1)(θJ​−θ), leading to the jackknife bias correction nθ−(n−1)θJ​.
For the nonparametric bootstrap, replace the unknown distribution by its empirical distribution function
Fn​(t)=n1​i=1∑n​1{xi​≤t}.​
(29)
Independently sample n observations with replacement from the original observations, compute the estimator on that bootstrap sample, and repeat this for B independently generated bootstrap samples. If the resulting values are θ1∗​,…,θB∗​, estimate the conditional bootstrap variance by
VB​=B−11​b=1∑B​(θb∗​−θ∗)2.​
(30)
Its interpretation as an approximation to the original sampling variance relies on the bootstrap approximating the sampling law of the statistic; increasing B only reduces simulation error and cannot repair a failure of that approximation.

Negative covariance can be exploited through antithetic variates, but it must belong to an actual joint sampling scheme. Two separately independent samples do not inherit negative covariance merely because one can imagine a coupling of their marginal distributions. Interpret the data as h=min(n,m) independent matched pairs (Xi​,Yi​) with covariance σXY​<0, together with independent unmatched observations if the sample sizes differ. Other known pairing schemes can be handled by substituting their actual covariance of the two means.
Let A=X, B=Y, and put
VX​=σX2​/n,VY​=σY2​/m,C=Cov(A,B)=hσXY​/(nm).
(35)
For any fixed w, the combination wA+(1−w)B is an unbiased estimator of the common mean. Its variance is w2VX​+(1−w)2VY​+2w(1−w)C. Differentiation, or completing the square, gives the minimum-variance combination of unbiased sample means:
θ=w∗​A+(1−w∗​)B,w∗​=VX​+VY​−2CVY​−C​,Var(θ)=VX​+VY​−2CVX​VY​−C2​.​
(36)
With C<0, both weights are positive. This is efficiency among fixed linear unbiased combinations, not a universal claim about every possible estimator for arbitrary distributions F,G.
For an immediately implementable exactly unbiased estimator without known moments, use fixed w=n/(n+m), the pooled sample mean. Its variance is
(n+m)2nσX2​+mσY2​+2hσXY​​,
(37)
which shows directly the gain from negative covariance. For any fixed weight, estimate VX​,VY​ using sX2​/n,sY2​/m, and C using hsXY​/(nm), with sXY​ the usual unbiased sample covariance of the matched pairs. Substitute them into the variance quadratic. At least two matched pairs are needed to estimate their covariance nonparametrically.
If the unknown optimal weights are desired while retaining exact unbiasedness, a cross-fitted combination of unbiased sample means gives an algorithm using the supplied data. Split the independent pairs and unmatched observations into two folds, keeping each pair together and retaining observations of both types in each fold. Estimate the moments from fold 2 to choose the weight for the fold-1 sample means, adjusting the variance terms for fold 1's sample sizes; do the converse for fold 2. Clip estimated weights to (0≤w≤1), to keep them bounded when training moment estimates are unstable. Average the two held-out combinations with deterministic weights, for example one half each for balanced folds.
Conditional on its training fold, each held-out pair of sample means has expectation θ, and its weights sum to one. Each held-out combination, and hence their average, is therefore exactly an unbiased estimator. Under consistent moment estimation with increasing balanced folds, the weights approach w∗​ and recover its first-order variance advantage. In contrast, estimating a weight from the same data to which it is applied need not yield an unbiased estimator.
Estimate the variance of this fitted-weight procedure by a bootstrap that resamples matched pairs as blocks and unmatched observations separately, repeats the entire split/weight/combination procedure, and takes the sample variance of the resulting estimates. This preserves the negative covariance and includes variability of the fitted weights. Resampling the x and y values independently would erase the feature responsible for the improvement. The bootstrap estimate has its usual large-sample qualification; the fixed-weight plug-in variance formula above is the simpler alternative when weights are predetermined.

A systematic Gibbs sampler updates the coordinates of θ successively from their full conditional distributions. At iteration t, draw
θj(t+1)​∼π(θj​∣θ1(t+1)​,…,θj−1(t+1)​,θj+1(t)​,…,θp(t)​),j=1,…,p.
(38)
For a single coordinate, if the current state has distribution π, its untouched coordinates have the correct marginal law and the resampled coordinate has precisely the conditional law under π. Integrating marginal times conditional therefore recovers π. Each coordinate kernel preserves this invariant distribution, so their composition also preserves it. This proves the target of the Gibbs sampler; the whole systematic sweep need not be a reversible kernel.
Starting from a suitable state and assuming the required irreducibility and convergence conditions, discard an initial transient and retain subsequent states as a dependent sample from, or approximately from, π. In finite-state problems irreducibility and aperiodicity suffice; in continuous-state problems the corresponding positive recurrence and ergodicity conditions must hold. Successive draws are generally dependent, so Monte Carlo uncertainty must account for that dependence.

The expectation-maximization algorithm alternates conditional averaging over missing data with maximization over the parameter. Start at a parameter value θ(0) for which the observed likelihood function is positive. At iteration t, its E-step forms
Q(θ∣θ(t))=Eθ(t)​[logf(x,Z;θ)∣x],
(50)
using the current conditional law of Z but treating θ as the candidate parameter. Its M-step takes
θ(t+1)∈argθmax​Q(θ∣θ(t)).​
(51)
Repeat until the parameter or observed log-likelihood stabilizes. An increase of Q implies an increase of the observed log-likelihood: if qt​(z)=f(z∣x;θ(t)), then
ℓ(θ)−ℓ(θ(t))=Q(θ∣θ(t))−Q(θ(t)∣θ(t))+DKL​(qt​∥f(⋅∣x;θ)),
(52)
where Kullback-Leibler divergence is nonnegative. In general this monotonicity does not guarantee a global maximum; distinct initializations may approach different stationary points. The particular observed log-likelihood below is concave, which makes a stronger conclusion possible there.

ℓ(θ)=x1​log(1−θ)+x2​logθ+x3​log(2+θ)+C.

ℓ′′(θ)=−(1−θ)2x1​​−θ2x2​​−(2+θ)2x3​​<0.

Thus the maximum-likelihood estimator is unique, allowing a boundary maximum. To verify that the iteration actually finds it, let u(θ)=x3​θ/(2+θ) and F(θ)=(x2​+u(θ))/(x1​+x2​+u(θ)). Then F′(θ)=x1​u′(θ)/(x1​+x2​+u(θ))2≥0, and

(62)
If the maximum is interior, this identity moves each iterate towards it, and monotonicity of F prevents crossing it. The bounded monotone iterates converge; continuity makes their limit a fixed point, and strict concavity makes that fixed point the maximum. If the maximum is at zero or one, the sign of ℓ′ gives the same monotone convergence towards the appropriate endpoint.
