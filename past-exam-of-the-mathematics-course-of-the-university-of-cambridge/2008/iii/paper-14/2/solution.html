Assume first 1≤t≤n, and write d=n−t. The sharp nonuniform t-intersecting family bound is
∣A∣≤⎩⎨⎧​i=0∑q​(in​),2i=0∑q​(in−1​),​d=2q,d=2q+1.​​
(7)
We prove the bound by deriving the needed Kleitman diametric theorem. Identify subsets with binary words, with Hamming distance ∣A△B∣. Let D(n,d) be the two expressions above. A set family of Hamming diameter at most d<n has size at most D(n,d), as follows.
First apply downward coordinate compression: delete coordinate i from a member only when its deletion is not already present. This preserves size and cannot increase the Hamming diameter. The only possible increased distance would be between a newly lowered member A∖{i} and a retained member B containing i. The deletion B∖{i} must have belonged to the old set family, or B would have been lowered too. The old pair A,B∖{i} has precisely that increased distance, proving it was already allowed. Repeated downward compressions terminate in a down-set.
Next apply all elementary set shifts Sij​ with i<j. They preserve size and Hamming diameter. If shifting one member but retaining the other increased their distance, the retained member must contain j but not i; its shifted partner was already present, and the old pair with that partner has the same distance. These shifts preserve the down-set property: a subset of a shifted member either was already present or is obtained by shifting its corresponding old subset. For a retained member, a subset cannot disappear, because the shifted partner of that subset is also a subset of the member or of its retained shifted partner. Repeated shifts terminate, since the sum of the selected coordinate labels decreases whenever a change occurs. We now have a shifted down-set F of the same size and Hamming diameter.
Split it into sections on [n−1]:
F0​={A:n∈/A, A∈F},F1​={A:A∪{n}∈F}.
(8)
The first section has Hamming diameter at most d. We claim the second has Hamming diameter at most d−2. For X,Y∈F1​, downward closure gives X∖Y,Y∈F1​. Compare (X∖Y)∪{n} with Y∈F0​. Their distance is ∣X∪Y∣+1, so ∣X∪Y∣≤d−1<n−1. Choose i<n outside their union. Shiftedness puts X∪{i} in F0​, while Y∪{n} is a member of F. Their distance is ∣X△Y∣+2, proving the claim. For d<2 this argument forces F1​ to be empty.
The induction now gives
∣F∣≤D(n−1,d)+D(n−1,d−2)=D(n,d),
(9)
where negative-diameter set families are empty. The equality of the numerical expressions is Pascal's identity. The base case d=0 has at most one member. At the boundary d=n−1, at most one member of each complementary pair is allowed, giving 2n−1, which equals D(n,n−1) by binomial symmetry. For the remaining cases 1≤d≤n−2, both section bounds belong to smaller-dimensional induction cases. This completes the diametric proof.
For a t-intersecting set family,
∣A△B∣=∣A∪B∣−∣A∩B∣≤n−t=d,
(10)
so the theorem applies. For d=2q, equality is attained by all sets of size at least n−q, since two such sets intersect in at least n−2q=t elements. For d=2q+1, take all sets of size at least n−q, and also all (n−q−1)-sets avoiding one fixed point. Two of these smaller sets intersect in at least 2(n−q−1)−(n−1)=t points; a smaller and a larger set intersect in at least t; two larger sets intersect in at least t+1. The size of this set family is
∑i=0q​(in​)+(qn−1​)=2∑i=0q​(in−1​),
(11)
so it attains the odd bound. If t=0, the full power set is admissible; if t>n, the only admissible set family is empty.
Every maximal 1-intersecting set family attains 2n−1, for n≥1. Such a set family is an up-set, since adding a superset preserves set intersections. It contains [n] and omits the empty set. It contains at most one member of any complementary pair. If neither of two nonempty complements A,Ac were present, maximality would give a member B disjoint from A; upward closure would then include Ac, a contradiction. Thus it chooses precisely one member of each complementary pair, as in maximal intersecting families choose one member of every complementary pair.
Not every maximal 2-intersecting set family attains the bound. On four points, let A={A:{1,2}⊆A}. This set family has four members and is maximal: every missing set meets its member {1,2} in at most one point. The sharp bound is 1+4=5, attained by all sets of size at least three. This proves maximal intersection does not imply maximum size.
