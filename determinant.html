Determinant (det)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra Multilinear algebra
OurBigBook.com Words: 2k Articles: 22
The determinant assigns a scalar to a square matrix. For a linear map on the real plane, it measures signed area scaling: its absolute value tells how much area grows, and its sign records whether the orientation of a vector space is preserved or reversed. In three dimensions, the same idea measures signed volume. Over a field, a square matrix is an invertible matrix exactly when its determinant is nonzero. These are two views of one phenomenon: losing an independent direction collapses full-dimensional volume and makes the original input impossible to recover uniquely.[ref]
Start with an explicit matrix and its action on a vector:
A=(21​13​),A(xy​)=(2x+yx+3y​),det(ac​bd​)=ad−bc,detA=2⋅3−1⋅1=5.​
(38)
The columns are the images of the standard basis vectors: Ae1​=(2,1)T and Ae2​=(1,3)T. Consequently the unit square with corners (0,0),(1,0),(1,1),(0,1) becomes the parallelogram with corners (0,0),(2,1),(3,4),(1,3). Its area is five. Every small patch of this square undergoes the same linear map, so the factor describes the whole deformation, rather than just one specially chosen boundary. The matrix mixes horizontal and vertical components, but its determinant compresses that combined effect into one area factor.[ref]
Now exchange the columns:
Aswap​=(13​21​),detAswap​=1⋅1−2⋅3=−5.
(39)
The parallelogram occupies exactly the same region, but its generating vectors arrive in the opposite order. Moving from the first column to the second now turns clockwise rather than counterclockwise. Ordinary area remains five; the oriented volume in two dimensions changes sign. A negative determinant therefore signals reversed orientation of a vector space, not a negative amount of material. Keeping the sign makes successive reversals calculable: two reversals restore the original orientation of a vector space.
Compare a singular matrix:
C=(12​24​),detC=1⋅4−2⋅2=0,C(xy​)=(x+2y)(12​).
(40)
Its columns are linearly dependent: the second is twice the first. The unit square collapses onto the segment from (0,0) to (3,6), which has zero area despite nonzero length. Since C(−2,1)T=0, the kernel of a linear map contains a nonzero vector; inputs differing by that vector have identical outputs. The collapsed picture explains why an inverse matrix cannot exist.
For A, recovery is possible, and the complete matrix multiplication check is
A−1=51​(3−1​−12​),AA−1=51​(2⋅3+1(−1)1⋅3+3(−1)​2(−1)+1⋅21(−1)+3⋅2​)=(10​01​)=I.
(41)
Here I is the identity matrix. The inverse matrix restores the original square, shrinking its area by 1/5. Generally, the adjugate identity gives A−1=adj(A)/detA whenever the determinant is nonzero over a field; this is why the denominator matters.[ref]
The multiplicativity of the determinant describes two successive deformations. Matrix multiplication means that AB applies B first, then A. Take
B=(10​02​),AB=(2⋅1+1⋅01⋅1+3⋅0​2⋅0+1⋅21⋅0+3⋅2​)=(21​26​).
(42)
The first linear map doubles area; the second multiplies the resulting area by five. Thus the unit square passes through areas 1,2,10, with the last parallelogram bounded by (0,0),(2,1),(4,7),(2,6). Direct calculation agrees:
detB=2,det(AB)=2⋅6−2⋅1=10=detAdetB.
(43)
This explains physically why area factors multiply instead of adding. The general identity holds for square matrices of the same size, including singular matrices. If either stage loses a direction, later processing cannot recover the discarded information.[ref]
The same interpretation works in three dimensions without drawing a new picture. The diagonal matrix
D=​200​030​00−1​​,detD=2⋅3⋅(−1)=−6
(44)
sends the unit cube to a rectangular box with side lengths two, three, and one. Its ordinary volume is six. The minus sign comes from reversing the third direction, so the oriented volume is minus six. Replacing the last diagonal entry by zero flattens the cube into a planar region: its area may be positive, but its three-dimensional volume is zero. This distinction matters whenever a linear map changes shape: the determinant measures size in the full input dimension, rather than the size of whichever lower-dimensional object remains.
The determinant measures neither trace nor perimeter. For A, the trace happens also to be five, but the identity matrix in two dimensions has trace two and determinant one. The first parallelogram above has perimeter 2(5​+10​), compared with four for the unit square; boundary length follows a different rule. Nor does determinant one require every length to stay fixed: the diagonal matrix diag(2,1/2) preserves area while stretching one direction and compressing the other.
These properties make the determinant useful when coordinates obscure what a deformation does. A nonzero determinant certifies that linearly independent input directions remain distinguishable; its magnitude counts the area or volume represented by a transformed cell. That second role reappears in the change of variables formula, where the Jacobian determinant corrects the sizes of small cells. Signed volume also separates a deformation that reverses the orientation of a vector space from one that merely stretches it, even when both give identical ordinary volume factors.[ref]
For general n, regard the columns v1​,…,vn​ as ordered arguments. The determinant is the unique alternating multilinear map to the scalar field normalized by detI=1. The multilinearity of the determinant means linearity in each column separately, with all others held fixed:
det(…,αu+βv,…)=αdet(…,u,…)+βdet(…,v,…).
(45)
Alternation means equal columns give zero; exchanging columns negates the result. Normalization sets the oriented volume of the standard basis to one. These properties also hold in rows. For real three-dimensional columns, their determinant is the scalar triple product, and its absolute value is the enclosed volume. Over other fields, the algebraic definition still works even without a geometric notion of positive volume.[ref]
Separate linearity does not mean the determinant is a linear map on the whole matrix. Scaling every column of an n-by-n matrix by t gives det(tA)=tndetA. For our two-dimensional example, det(2A)=4detA=20: doubling both directions quadruples area. The power records how many independent directions are being scaled together.
The properties suggest practical computations. Adding a multiple of one column to another preserves the determinant: the extra term has repeated columns and vanishes. This is the algebra behind an area-preserving shear mapping. Alternatively, expansion along a row uses cofactors, each a matrix minor multiplied by a sign:
detA=∑j=1n​aij​(−1)i+jdetA(ij),
(46)
where A(ij) deletes row i and column j. The Leibniz formula for determinants supplies the general entry formula. Both calculations encode the same geometric invariant; the defining properties explain their structure without requiring memorization of a separate recipe for every size.[ref]
