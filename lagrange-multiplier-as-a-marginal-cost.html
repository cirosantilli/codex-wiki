Lagrange multiplier as a marginal cost (V′(c)=λ∗​(c))

The chosen quadratic objective function above gives more than an optimal allocation: its Lagrange multiplier predicts how much the best cost changes when the required total changes. For this example,
V(c)=32c2​,V′(c)=34c​=λ∗​(c).
(87)
This is an explicit derivative of a constrained value function and an instance of the envelope theorem. At c=3, one extra small unit of required output costs approximately 4 units per unit of output, in the chosen normalized model. Increasing c makes this equality-constrained minimization problem more expensive; it is not a gain from relaxing an upper resource limit. The sign and meaning depend on how the optimization constraint and optimization Lagrangian are written.[ref]
For an actual finite change δ, the cost difference is exactly
V(3+δ)−V(3)=4δ+32​δ2.
(88)
At δ=0.1, the linear approximation predicts 0.4, while the exact increase is 61/150≈0.406667. The extra 1/150 comes from the squared term in this polynomial, rather than numerical error. A Lagrange multiplier gives a local sensitivity analysis; treating it as a constant price over a large change generally loses the change in the optimum itself.
For a smooth optimizer z(c) of an equality problem g(z)=c, the short chain rule calculation explains the result: ∇g(z(c))⋅z′(c)=1 and ∇f(z(c))=λ(c)∇g(z(c)) imply df(z(c))/dc=λ(c). The differentiability and stationarity assumptions matter: an optimizer can fail to be unique or vary smoothly. The envelope theorem records when this interpretation is justified.
