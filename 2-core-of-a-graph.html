2-core of a graph (C2​(G))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Foundations of mathematics Graph theory
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 4
The 2-core is what remains of a finite simple graph after repeatedly removing every vertex with fewer than two surviving graph neighbours. It strips away dangling trees while keeping the part that can support itself under this local rule. Precisely, for an undirected graph G=(V,E), its 2-core is the unique maximum by set inclusion induced subgraph G[S] such that
degG[S]​(v)≥2for every v∈S.
(1)
The empty vertex set is allowed: the condition is then vacuous, without needing to define the minimum degree of the empty graph. "Maximum by inclusion" means that S contains every other vertex set satisfying the condition. It is stronger than merely saying that one chosen set cannot be enlarged: the 2-core simultaneously contains all possible qualifying induced subgraphs. In this particular family, every inclusion-maximal qualifying set does equal that unique maximum; the union argument in why the 2-core is unique explains why. [ref]
For a complete worked example, let ab denote the edge {a,b} and take
V={a,b,c,d,e,f,g,z},E={ab,bc,ca,cd,de,ef,dg}.
(2)
The triangle in a graph abc has a branching tail attached at c, and z is an isolated vertex. In alphabetical order the initial degrees of vertices are (2,2,3,3,2,1,1,0). Deleting f turns e into a leaf of a graph; deleting g leaves d with degree of a vertex two; deleting z changes no other degree of a vertex. Now delete e, which makes d a leaf of a graph, and finally delete d. Exactly the three edges of abc remain: C2​(G)=G[{a,b,c}].
A forest has empty 2-core: every nonempty finite forest has an isolated vertex or a leaf of a graph, so deletion cannot stop at a nonempty remainder. In particular, an isolated edge, an isolated vertex, a path graph, and the empty graph all have empty 2-core. Every cycle graph is its own 2-core. Two disjoint triangles in a graph also form their own 2-core, so a 2-core need not be a connected graph; attaching a tree to either triangle changes neither surviving triangle.
