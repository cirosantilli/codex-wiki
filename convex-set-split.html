Convex set ((1−t)x+ty∈C(0≤t≤1))

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Mathematical optimization
OurBigBook.com Words: 7k Articles: 107
A convex set permits every intermediate mixture of two of its points. If two choices meet its constraints, continuously averaging them also meets those constraints. This geometric property makes many feasible regions easier to explore: there is no forbidden gap along a straight move between feasible choices.
Formally, a subset C of a real vector space is convex when
x,y∈C,0≤t≤1⟹(1−t)x+ty∈C.
(1)
The parameter t traverses the entire line segment, including both endpoints. The definition does not require a closed subset, a bounded set, or a nonempty interior. The empty set, the whole vector space, and every affine subspace are convex; an open unit disk and a closed unit disk are both convex.[ref]
The triangle inequality verifies the disk example directly: if ∥x∥,∥y∥≤1, then ∥(1−t)x+ty∥≤(1−t)∥x∥+t∥y∥≤1. For the open disk, the last inequality is strict. The unit circle fails the same test: (1,0) and (−1,0) belong to it, but their midpoint (0,0) does not. A curved route along the unit circle connects the endpoints, so being path-connected is weaker than being a convex set.
The distinction between a convex set and a convex function matters. The former concerns where one may choose a point; the latter concerns how a value changes along line segments. Convex optimization combines both: averaging preserves feasibility, and a convex function obeys an upper bound by the corresponding average of endpoint values. A convex set alone does not make an arbitrary objective a convex function or eliminate its inferior local minima.
