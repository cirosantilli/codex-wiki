Ideal gas law (PV=NkB​T=nmol​RT)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Ideal gas
OurBigBook.com Words: 706 Articles: 1
The ideal gas law is the equation of state relating the absolute pressure P, volume V, particle count N, and absolute thermodynamic temperature T of a classical ideal gas:
PV=NkB​T=nmol​RT,N=nmol​NA​,R=NA​kB​.
(217)
Here kB​ is the Boltzmann constant, NA​ is the Avogadro constant, R is the molar gas constant, and nmol​ is the amount of substance in moles. The distinct symbol nmol​ avoids confusing amount of substance with the number density n=N/V, measured in m−3: the same law can be written P=nkB​T. Use absolute pressure, relative to a vacuum, rather than an unconverted gauge pressure. [ref]
The exact SI definitions of the Avogadro constant and Boltzmann constant give
R=(6.02214076×1023)(1.380649×10−23)Jmol−1K−1=8.31446261815324Jmol−1K−1.
(218)
This numerical value is exact; the applicability of the ideal gas model to a real gas is an approximation. [ref] [ref]
For nmol​=1mol, T=300K, and V=0.025m3, direct substitution gives
RT=2494.338785445972Jmol−1,P=Vnmol​RT​=99773.55141783888Pa≈99.774kPa.
(219)
At fixed temperature and particle count, doubling the volume to 0.050m3 halves the pressure to approximately 49.887kPa: this is Boyle's law. At fixed volume and particle count, doubling the temperature from 300K to 600K doubles the pressure to approximately 199.547kPa. These ratios use kelvins, not readings in the degree Celsius scale: 300K is 26.85∘C, and 600K is 326.85∘C.
The curved slices show Boyle's law at three temperatures; the straight slice shows proportionality to absolute temperature at one fixed volume. They are relations among equilibrium states, rather than a prescribed process history. Compression could hold the temperature fixed through heat transfer, or raise it during adiabatic compression. To calculate a process's heat or work, supplement the ideal gas law with the process constraint, the first law of thermodynamics, and the relevant internal energy or heat capacity information. The graph's extrapolation toward T=0 is mathematical; it does not assert that a real gas remains classical or gaseous there.
For a stellar mixture with fixed mean molecular weight, the ideal gas law can be written P=RρT/μ, where ρ is mass density and R is the stellar gas constant. It assumes negligible interaction corrections and a nondegenerate gas; excitation or ionization can change the particle count and thermodynamic response.
In his 1834 memoir on the motive power of heat, Émile Clapeyron combined gas laws into a relation between pressure, volume, and temperature while analysing heat engines (printed page 164). The equation above uses modern SI notation and absolute thermodynamic temperature; it is not a transcription of his historical temperature convention. [ref]
