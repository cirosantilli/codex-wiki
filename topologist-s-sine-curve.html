Topologist's sine curve (T=G)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Geometry and topology Connected space
OurBigBook.com Words: 283
Consider the graph of a function x↦sin(1/x), using the sine of the multiplicative inverse of x, together with a vertical closed interval:
G={(x,sin(1/x)):0<x≤1},L={0}×[−1,1],T=G∪L.
(32)
Give T the subspace topology from the Euclidean plane. The symbol × denotes a Cartesian product. The right endpoint (1,sin1)≈(1,0.841471) belongs to G. The continuous map x↦(x,sin(1/x)) sends the connected real interval (0,1] onto G. Hence G is connected, and its closure G=T is connected by closure of a connected set. Every height in [−1,1] is approached as x tends to zero; for example,
pn​=π/2+2πn1​,qn​=3π/2+2πn1​,sin(1/pn​)=1,sin(1/qn​)=−1(n=0,1,…).
(33)
For n=1, these inputs are p1​=2/(5π)≈0.127324 and q1​=2/(7π)≈0.090946. For any other height y∈[−1,1], choose θ≥1 with sinθ=y; then the sequence (1/(θ+2πn),y) lies in G and has limit of a sequence (0,y).
Nevertheless, T has exactly two path components, G and L. Within G, join inputs a,b∈(0,1] by the continuous path t↦((1−t)a+tb,sin(1/((1−t)a+tb))). Within L, use t↦(0,(1−t)c+td) for heights c,d∈[−1,1].
The obstruction to a continuous path between L and G is persistent oscillation, not a visible gap. Proof outline: suppose such a path exists, directed from L to G, and take its last time in L. Immediately afterwards its first coordinate x(t)>0 approaches zero and its second coordinate is sin(1/x(t)). The intermediate value theorem forces the first coordinate to pass through inputs of both displayed types arbitrarily close to that departure time. The second coordinate therefore takes both values 1 and −1 arbitrarily close to that time, contradicting continuity. [ref]
The sampled graph of a function illustrates the formulas; it does not prove connectedness or the failure of path connectedness. Taking a closure preserves connectedness, but this example shows that it can destroy path connectedness.
