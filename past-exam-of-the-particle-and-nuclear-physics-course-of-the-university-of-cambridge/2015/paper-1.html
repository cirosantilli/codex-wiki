A positive-parity K=0 nuclear rotational band in an even-even nucleus has J=0,2,4,… and excitation energies
EJ​=2Iℏ2​J(J+1).
(1)
The four nonzero levels give EJ​/[J(J+1)]=15.17,14.85,14.48,14.01 keV. These approximately constant coefficients, together with the even-spin positive-parity sequence, support a rotational interpretation. The roughly eight-percent decline means a constant-inertia rigid rotor is only an approximation: the effective moment of inertia increases along the sequence.
Using the lowest excited level gives
I=91 keV3ℏ2​=2.29×10−54 kgm2.​
(2)
Using the highest level instead gives 2.48×10−54 kgm2, showing the scale of the departure from a constant inertia. This is a comparison of nuclear rotational inertia relative to a rigid sphere, not evidence for an exactly spherical rotating intrinsic state.
For a conventional size estimate, explicitly take a uniform sharp radius R=r0​A1/3 with r0​≃1.2 fm and mass M≃Amu​. These are standard scale approximations, not additional measured properties of this isotope supplied by the paper. They give R≃6.70 fm and

Thus the fitted rotational inertia is substantially smaller than the rigid-body scale. Nuclear pairing provides a physical reason collective nuclear inertia need not equal the classical rigid-body value. The comparison's numerical radius dependence is explicit; it is not an independently determined radius.

A physical baryon is a colour singlet. Its three-quark colour factor is antisymmetric, proportional to ϵabc​∣abc⟩. In the hypothetical scalar-quark theory, the quarks obey Bose-Einstein statistics, so the complete three-particle state must be symmetric under exchange. At L=0 the spatial factor is symmetric, and three zero spins supply no nontrivial spin factor. The flavour factor must therefore be antisymmetric to cancel the antisymmetry of the colour factor.
There is exactly one completely antisymmetric state made from three flavour basis states: the flavour singlet of three scalar quarks,
∣F⟩=6​1​(∣uds⟩+∣dsu⟩+∣sud⟩−∣usd⟩−∣dus⟩−∣sdu⟩).
(4)
Equivalently, dim⋀3C3=1. A repeated flavour gives zero under complete antisymmetrization. Only one ground-state flavour singlet, containing uds and having JP=0+, remains. Keeping fermionic exchange antisymmetry while changing the spins to zero would contradict the assumed Spin-statistics theorem. The standard antisymmetric colour factor and the ordinary-quark flavour decomposition are documented in the quark-model review.[ref]

Evidence for quarks comes from several kinds of observation that test different aspects of their interpretation. The light meson and baryon spectra organize into approximate flavour multiplets with the charges and strangeness expected for qqˉ​ and qqq. In particular, the baryon octet and baryon decuplet encode the three light quark flavours and their spin couplings. This is an economical account of hadron quantum numbers, but spectroscopy alone would not establish that the constituents behave as localized scattering centers.
Deep inelastic scattering supplies that second test. In energetic electron–proton scattering the outgoing lepton measures the energy and momentum transferred through a virtual photon, while the broken-up target is summed over. Approximate Bjorken scaling means the structure functions depend primarily on x=Q2/(2P⋅q) rather than varying rapidly with the resolving momentum scale Q2. It indicates nearly pointlike constituents. In the leading spin-half parton model,
F2​(x)=x∑q​eq2​[q(x)+qˉ​(x)],F2​(x)=2xF1​(x).
(7)
The charge-squared weights and the second relation test the constituents' fractional charges and spin-half nature. Scaling violations reflect quark and gluon radiation rather than a failure of the constituent picture.[ref]
Particle jets give complementary final-state evidence. A high-energy quark produces a collimated spray of hadrons because confinement prevents it from emerging as an isolated particle. Back-to-back jets fit quark–antiquark production; three-jet events show hard gluon radiation. Their angular correlations test a spin-one radiated parton. This distinguishes constituents of hadrons from a literal observation of free fractional-charge particles.
Three independent arguments identify an additional threefold colour degree of freedom. First, the spin-aligned uuu state of a ground-state Δ++ would have symmetric space, spin and flavour factors. The Pauli exclusion principle requires an antisymmetric additional factor. The three-quark colour singlet supplies it. The same construction accommodates the spin-aligned sss state of the Ω− without altering its measured quantum numbers.
Second, the inclusive hadronic R ratio, away from thresholds and narrow resonances, is at leading electromagnetic order
R=σ(e+e−→μ+μ−)σ(e+e−→hadrons)​=Nc​∑q maccessible​eq2​.
(8)
Each quark flavour has Nc​ distinguishable colour final states. With Nc​=3, the predicted plateaux for three, four and five accessible flavours are 2, 10/3 and 11/3; without the factor three they would be a third as large. The measured inclusive normalization is therefore a colour-counting test, with radiative and threshold corrections included in precision comparisons.[ref]
Third, the neutral pion's two-photon amplitude contains a quark-loop colour sum, proportional to Nc​(eu2​−ed2​); its rate and the other evidence are consistent with three colours. Quantum chromodynamics identifies this degree of freedom as a gauge charge, with eight gluons. Observed hadrons are colour singlets; the evidence for colour is its quantitative and symmetry consequences, not visible colours or isolated coloured quarks.

The common energetic principle behind nuclear fission and nuclear fusion is the nuclear binding energy. If the products are more tightly bound in total than the reactants, their smaller rest mass releases
Q=​i∑​Mi​−f∑​Mf​​c2=f∑​Bf​−i∑​Bi​.​
(9)
The two binding-energy expressions assume that the same numbers of protons and neutrons are counted on both sides; emitted free nucleons have zero nuclear binding. The energy appears mainly as product kinetic energy, with radiation and subsequent decays contributing according to the reaction.
The binding energy per nucleon rises rapidly through light nuclei, has a broad maximum in the iron/nickel region, and decreases more slowly for heavier nuclei. Combining suitable light nuclei can therefore increase their total binding, whereas splitting a sufficiently heavy nucleus into moderately heavy fragments can do the same. This is an energy comparison, not a rule that every fusion or fission channel is exothermic: the actual nuclear masses fix the sign of Q.
In nuclear fission, the liquid-drop model illustrates the competition between attractive short-range nuclear cohesion and long-range proton repulsive Coulomb interaction. Deformation increases surface area and costs surface energy, but separating the charge can reduce nuclear Coulomb energy. Their competition creates a fission barrier. An excited nucleus may pass over the barrier; spontaneous fission can occur through quantum tunnelling. The resulting fragments are often excited and neutron-rich, so they may emit prompt neutrons and photons and undergo later beta decay. The typical total energy released by a heavy-nucleus fission is of order a few hundred MeV, rather than an electronic-scale energy. Its size follows from an approximately MeV-per-nucleon binding gain summed over a heavy nucleus. A neutron from one fission can induce another; a nuclear chain reaction is the repeated physical process, distinct from the energy-release condition for one event.
In nuclear fusion, the initial charged nuclei repel one another. Exothermicity does not remove this entrance Coulomb barrier. A reaction requires sufficient overlap of their wavefunctions, achieved by energetic encounters and quantum tunnelling. For nonresonant low-energy charged-particle fusion, a useful separation is
σ(E)=ES(E)​e−2πηC​,ηC​=vrel​Z1​Z2​αc​.
(10)
The astrophysical S factor isolates the nuclear part from much of the rapidly varying Coulomb penetration probability. In a thermal population, the competition between the decreasing high-energy population and increasing penetration produces the Gamow peak. This explains how fusion can proceed below the classical barrier without requiring the mean thermal energy to equal its height.
Both processes conserve total energy, charge, baryon number and angular momentum. Their very different entrance barriers and mass ranges explain why a favorable Q value by itself does not specify a reaction rate. Short-range attraction gives approximately saturated nuclear binding, while surface, Coulomb, symmetry and pairing terms account for the broad trend and its isotope-dependent departures.

The deuteron is the bound proton–neutron system. Its ground state has JP=1+ and isospin T=0, with binding energy approximately 2.225 MeV and no excited bound state. The small binding compared with characteristic nuclear energy scales produces an extended tail. With reduced mass μ≃mN​/2, the asymptotic radial wavefunction decays as e−κr, where
κ=ℏ2μB​​,κ−1≃4.3 fm.
(11)
The decay length is not a sharp deuteron radius or the force range. It illustrates weak binding outside the region of strong attraction.
The observed total spin is consistent with aligned nucleon spins, S=1, and a predominantly L=0 spatial state, 3S1​. A central attraction identical in both spin–isospin channels would not distinguish this channel from a spin-singlet proton–neutron channel. In fact the triplet channel binds while the corresponding singlet channel does not. A realistic nuclear force must therefore distinguish these spin–isospin channels, for example through a term Vσ​(r)σp​⋅σn​, whose eigenvalues are +1 and −3 in triplet and singlet states. The absence of a bound dineutron and diproton reinforces the need to distinguish channels; proton–proton Coulomb repulsion is an additional effect, not a complete explanation of the singlet nuclear interaction.
The clearest evidence against a purely central force is the nonzero electric quadrupole moment. A pure isotropic S-wave charge distribution would give zero. The deuteron quadrupole evidence for a tensor force instead points to a nuclear tensor force,
VT​(r)S12​,S12​=3(σp​⋅r)(σn​⋅r)−σp​⋅σn​.
(12)
It depends on how the spins point relative to the line joining the particles. It preserves total J but couples orbital angular momenta differing by two, producing
∣d⟩=ru(r)​∣3S1​⟩+rw(r)​∣3D1​⟩.
(13)
Both terms have JP=1+, and their interference permits a quadrupole moment. The 1939 theoretical analysis explicitly connected the measured quadrupole moment with such directional dependence.[ref]
The nuclear magnetic moment provides a second check. A pure aligned-spin S wave would give approximately μp​+μn​≃0.880μN​, close to but larger than the observed deuteron value approximately 0.857μN​. The discrepancy is consistent with non-S-wave structure and interaction-current corrections; it is not by itself a model-independent measurement of a D-state probability. The deuteron D-state admixture depends on the adopted Hamiltonian and electromagnetic current. The magnetic moments and mass differences used for these scales are tabulated physical constants.[ref]
A useful schematic interaction therefore contains central, spin-dependent and tensor terms, and possibly spin–orbit terms in other channels. Long-range one-pion exchange naturally supplies a tensor component, with its finite range set by the pion mass. The deuteron establishes an attractive triplet channel and a noncentral tensor interaction; a spin-independent central potential is insufficient.

In the Breit-Wigner resonance cross section, E is the total centre-of-momentum energy, E0​ the resonance energy, and p either incident particle's center-of-momentum momentum magnitude. The total decay width is Γ=ℏ/τ, while Γi​ and Γf​ are partial widths into the entrance and specified exit channels. Their ratios to Γ are the corresponding branching fractions. The factor g averages over incident spin states and sums over the resonance's magnetic substates.
In natural units, ℏ=c=1, the resonant partial-wave cross-sectional scale is an area proportional to the squared reduced de Broglie wavelength, 1/p2. Equivalently a partial-wave scattering amplitude carries one factor 1/p, and its squared modulus supplies 1/p2. In SI area units the same scale is (ℏ/p)2; if p is expressed in energy units, multiply the final inverse-energy-squared answer by (ℏc)2.
For unpolarized spin-half incident muons, the unpolarized resonance spin factor is
g=(2sμ−​+1)(2sμ+​+1)2J+1​=42J+1​.
(14)
Hence gZ​=3/4 and gH​=1/4. The outgoing tau spins do not supply another independent multiplicity here: their allowed final states are already included in Γf​.
Near a narrow resonance, treat the smooth prefactor p−2 and the partial widths as constant across the line. At the peak the denominator is Γ2/4. Half its height occurs when (E−E0​)2+Γ2/4=Γ2/2, namely at E=E0​±Γ/2. Thus the full width at half maximum is Γ within the Breit-Wigner narrow-line approximation. A varying prefactor or convolution with the beam-energy distribution can modify an observed line shape.
The production amplitude has a muon pair joined to a Higgs propagator through their Yukawa coupling, followed by the bottom-quark pair at a second Yukawa vertex. The separate H→bbˉ graph is a tree-level Higgs–fermion vertex. There are no tree-level Hgg or Hγγ vertices in the renormalizable Standard Model: their lowest nonzero graphs are loops. A quark triangle, dominated by the top contribution, produces two gluons. Charged-fermion triangles contribute to two photons; charged-W loops also contribute and are dominant in the Standard Model amplitude. Photon permutations and the W contact topology belong to the full amplitude; the diagram shows these topologies rather than claiming one triangle alone is the entire gauge-invariant W contribution.[ref]

The inequality mH​<2mW​ forbids two on-shell W bosons in a two-body final state. It does not forbid the four-fermion states reached through a virtual W propagator. The physical channel is
H⟶WW∗⟶4 fermions,​

Beta decay converts a neutron to a proton or vice versa through the weak interaction, leaving the mass number fixed:
n→p+e−+νˉe​,p→n+e++νe​.
(23)
Inside a nucleus the full initial and final nuclear masses determine whether either process is possible. Electron capture, p+e−→n+νe​, is the related bound-electron process. The emitted charged-lepton spectrum is continuous because energy is shared with the neutrino and nuclear recoil.
For allowed beta decay, the lepton wavefunctions' leading spatial terms are constant over the nucleus. A Fermi transition has ΔJ=0 and no parity change. A Gamow-Teller transition has ΔJ=0,1 and no parity change, excluding 0→0. Thus a same-parity transition with nonzero initial and final spins and ΔJ=0 can contain both contributions. First-forbidden transitions change parity and permit ∣ΔJ∣=0,1,2; the ∣ΔJ∣=2 case is unique forbidden beta decay. Higher forbiddenness requires higher powers of the small lepton momentum times nuclear size. “Forbidden” denotes suppression of the leading allowed operator, not an impossible decay.[ref]
Fermi's golden rule expresses a rate as a squared nuclear matrix element times leptonic phase space. Neglecting charged-lepton mass and Coulomb distortion, a constant allowed matrix element gives
λ∝∣M∣2∫0Q​E2(Q−E)2dE=30∣M∣2Q5​.
(24)
This is the approximate Sargent rule, λ∝Q5 for comparable allowed strengths; it is not an exact law across arbitrary transitions. Finite electron mass, the Fermi function, forbidden shape factors and nuclear structure change the comparison. A microscopic first-forbidden unique operator introduces additional momentum factors.
Using bare nuclear masses Mi​,Mf​, the kinetic-energy release is
Qβ−​=(Mi​−Mf​−me​)c2,Qβ+​=(Mi​−Mf​−me​)c2.
(25)
The daughter is different in the two cases: its charge is Z+1 for beta minus and Z−1 for beta plus. The relevant mass difference must exceed one electron mass. For neutral atomic masses, to leading order in small electronic binding corrections, the corresponding expressions are Qβ−​=(Miat​−Mfat​)c2 and Qβ+​=(Miat​−Mfat​−2me​)c2. Electron capture is energetically possible approximately when Miat​>Mfat​, with available shell binding energy and daughter excitation included. Nuclear masses and atomic masses must not be mixed.

The diagram fixes the Fermi strengths but not both axial strengths. Including them and proper Positron phase-space functions changes the numerical result. Assuming equal total allowed strengths instead of the Fermi-only ratio three would give approximately 0.64%; that alternative makes a different nuclear-structure assumption. The rate enhancement from removing the forbiddenness explains why the X branch can be appreciable despite its much smaller endpoint than the Ne ground-state branch.
