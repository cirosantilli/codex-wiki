All three processes are weak charged current transformations between neighboring nuclear isobars. Neglecting neutrino masses, and writing Ma​(A,Z) for a neutral atomic mass, their Q values are:
  • Beta-minus decay: a neutron converts to a proton, emitting an electron and an electron antineutrino,
(A,Z)⟶(A,Z+1)+e−+νˉe​,Qβ−​=Ma​(A,Z)−Ma​(A,Z+1)>0.
(1)
  • Beta-plus decay: a proton converts to a neutron, emitting a Positron and an electron neutrino,
(A,Z)⟶(A,Z−1)+e++νe​,Qβ+​=Ma​(A,Z)−Ma​(A,Z−1)−2me​>0.
(2)
  • Electron capture: a bound atomic electron is absorbed in the conversion of a proton to a neutron,
e−+(A,Z)⟶(A,Z−1)+νe​,QEC​=Ma​(A,Z)−Ma​(A,Z−1)>0.
(3)
These formulae use the paper's natural units. With energies in MeV, beta-plus decay requires an atomic mass-energy difference exceeding 2me​c2=1.022MeV. The extra electron-mass subtraction arises from using neutral atomic rather than bare nuclear masses. Electron capture can occur when the atomic mass-energy difference is positive but below this threshold. For a particular capture shell or excited daughter, the available energy must also cover the electron-hole and nuclear excitation energies, and the small nuclear recoil. Small atomic binding corrections are suppressed in the displayed threshold formulae.
The question's fixed-A family consists of nuclear isobars, rather than isotopes: isotopes have fixed Z. The semi-empirical mass formula gives a convex nuclear mass parabola as a function of Z at fixed A. For odd A, nuclear pairing does not split the family into two branches, so there is typically one beta-stable isobar, at the discrete minimum. Neighboring members can lower their mass by changing Z toward it.
For even A, nuclear pairing lowers even-even nuclei and raises odd-odd nuclei, producing two offset nuclear mass parabolas. There are often two beta-stable even-even isobars, separated by an odd-odd member to which neither can energetically decay. The number is not an exact universal rule: some even-A chains have one or more than two such minima against single beta decay, and a few light odd-odd nuclei are stable. Stability against single beta decay also does not guarantee stability against double beta decay.

Radiocarbon dating measures how long carbon-bearing material has been isolated from a carbon reservoir. Atmospheric cosmic rays produce secondary neutrons, which create carbon-14 chiefly through
714​N+n⟶614​C+p.
(4)
The carbon-14 enters atmospheric carbon dioxide and then living organisms through carbon exchange, including photosynthesis and food consumption. While exchange continues, the organism's 14C/12C ratio tracks the relevant environmental ratio approximately.
After exchange ceases, the remaining carbon-14 undergoes beta-minus decay,
614​C⟶714​N+e−+νˉe​,
(5)
while stable carbon-12 remains. With half-life T1/2​≃5730yr, the radioactive decay law gives
N14​(t)=N14​(0)e−λt,λ=T1/2​ln2​.
(6)
Thus measuring the isotope ratio R, or the radioactive activity per fixed amount of carbon, gives
t=ln2T1/2​​lnRR0​​=ln2T1/2​​lnaa0​​.​
(7)
Here a=λN14​ is compared at equal carbon content; R0​,a0​ describe the appropriate initial reservoir. Accelerator mass spectrometry counts the rare carbon-14 relative to stable carbon directly; a decay-counting method instead measures its radioactive activity.
The initial ratio is not perfectly constant. Atmospheric production changes and reservoir differences therefore require radiocarbon calibration, often using independently dated tree rings. Contamination and subsequent carbon exchange must also be controlled. The date measures closure of the carbon system, not automatically the date of every later event involving the object. The physical half-life is about 5730 years; conventional radiocarbon ages historically use 5568 years and must be calibrated before interpretation as calendar dates.[ref]

A ground-state baryon contains three quarks in a spatially symmetric L=0 wavefunction, giving positive parity. Each quark is a spin one-half fermion, so the complete wavefunction must be antisymmetric under interchange of its complete quark labels. A physical baryon is a colour singlet, with the antisymmetric colour factor
ψcolour​∝ϵabc​∣abc⟩.
(26)
Consequently the combined spin-and-flavour factor must be symmetric. The product notation in the question organizes the degrees of freedom; it need not mean that spin and flavour are separately unentangled. In particular the octet requires a correlated sum of products.
For the three light flavours, the flavour symmetry decomposition is
3⊗3⊗3=10S​⊕8M​⊕8M​⊕1A​.
(27)
The subscripts describe permutation symmetry, with M denoting mixed symmetry. The addition of angular momentum of three spin one-half particles gives
2⊗2⊗2=4S​⊕2M​⊕2M​.
(28)
The symmetric spin quartet has total J=3/2; the two spin doublets have J=1/2.
For J=3/2, the spin factor is symmetric, so the flavour factor must also be symmetric: this gives the spin-3/2 baryon decuplet. Its flavour content is four Δ states, three Σ∗ states, two Ξ∗ states, and one Ω state.
For J=1/2, the mixed-symmetry spin functions combine with the mixed-symmetry flavour functions to make one fully symmetric spin-flavour factor for each octet weight: this gives the spin-1/2 baryon octet, containing two nucleons, three Sigma baryons, one Lambda baryon, and two Xi baryons. The two mixed-symmetry octets in the raw flavour product are not two distinct ground-state octets; their permutation partners are correlated with the spin factors. An antisymmetric flavour singlet would require a fully antisymmetric spin factor to make a symmetric product, but three spin states drawn from a two-dimensional single-quark spin space have no such factor.
Equivalently, the symmetric three-particle representation of spin-flavour symmetry has dimension (38​)=56 and decomposes under flavour and spin into
56S​=(10,4)⊕(8,2),56=10×4+8×2.​
(29)
These are approximate multiplets: the larger strange quark mass breaks exact flavour symmetry and splits their energies.

The baryon hyperfine mass formula is a phenomenological constituent-quark model. The three constituent quark masses include effects of strong-interaction dressing and an approximate spin-independent confinement energy; they are not the small running quark masses in a short-distance Quantum chromodynamics calculation.
The residual correction has the form of a colour-magnetic spin-spin interaction. A nonrelativistic reduction of one-gluon exchange produces a contact contribution proportional to
αs​mi​mj​Si​⋅Sj​​δ3(ri​−rj​).
(30)
After taking its expectation in the common spatial wavefunction and the colour singlet, the colour factor, coupling, and contact density are absorbed into A′. Summing the three quark pairs gives the supplied formula. Positive A′ raises aligned-spin configurations relative to lower-spin configurations. In natural units, the spin angular momentum operators are dimensionless, so A′ has units of energy cubed, as in the given GeV3.

The charged pion has spin zero. In its rest frame, the outgoing muon or Positron and the neutrino travel in opposite directions. With a nearly massless left-helicity neutrino, total angular momentum zero requires the positive charged antilepton to have negative helicity. The weak charged current instead creates a massless positive antilepton with positive helicity. A nonzero charged-lepton mass supplies the required opposite-helicity component.
Thus the decay amplitude is proportional to mℓ​: this is helicity suppression. Explicitly, contracting the pion's axial-current matrix element, proportional to its four-momentum pπμ​, with the leptonic current and applying the Dirac equation leaves a factor mℓ​. The leptonic pseudoscalar decay width is, in the fπ​ convention,
Γ(π+→ℓ+νℓ​)=8πGF2​∣Vud​∣2fπ2​mπ​mℓ2​​(1−mπ2​mℓ2​​)2.
(41)
The phase space advantage of the lighter Positron cannot offset its much stronger helicity suppression. The common factors cancel in the pion electron-to-muon decay ratio:
Γμ​Γe​​=mμ2​me2​​[1−mμ2​/mπ2​1−me2​/mπ2​​]2≃1.28×10−4.​
(42)
This tree-level estimate explains why the muonic branch dominates by roughly four orders of magnitude. The paper's table gives an electronic branching fraction 1.230×10−4; electromagnetic corrections change the simple ratio by a few percent without changing the mechanism.

The table gives the Xi baryon mean lifetime τΞ0​=2.90×10−10s. At t1​=10−12s, an initially resting particle has survival probability
P(Ξ0 survives)=e−t1​/τΞ0​=0.99656.
(52)
Thus the most probable contents are one undecayed Ξ0. The initial momentum is unspecified. For a moving parent, time dilation replaces the denominator by γΞ​τΞ0​, making the survival probability at least as large. The times below are characteristic proper mean lifetimes; laboratory times depend on the particle velocities.
The principal first step is
Ξ0→Λπ0,BΞ​=0.99524.
(53)
It typically occurs on the 10−10s scale. The neutral pion almost immediately converts to two photons, with τπ0​=8.43×10−17s and Bγγ​=0.98823. The Lambda baryon has τΛ​=2.632×10−10s and two dominant routes.
In the charged-pion route,
Ξ0Λπ−μ−​→Λ+π0,π0→2γ,→p+π−,B=0.639,→μ−+νˉμ​,τπ−​=2.6033×10−8s,→e−+νˉe​+νμ​,τμ​≃2.197×10−6s.​
(54)
The muon lifetime is a standard additional input, not present in the supplied five table pages.[ref] The successive contents are approximately Λ+2γ, then p+π−+2γ, then p+μ−+νˉμ​+2γ. After a few muon lifetimes the dominant stable endpoint is
p+e−+νˉe​+νμ​+νˉμ​+2γ.​
(55)
The leading-path probability including the tabulated two-photon pion branch is
PA​≃(0.99524)(0.639)(0.98823)(0.999877)=0.6284.
(56)
Here the last factor is the tabulated dominant muonic charged-pion fraction; rare radiative corrections are being neglected in describing a fixed photon count. Before selecting pion decay channels, the probability of reaching the pπ−π0 history is 0.99524×0.639=0.63596.
In the neutral-pion route,
Ξ0→Λπ0,Λ→nπ0(B=0.358),π0π0→4γ.
(57)
This produces n+4γ by roughly the nanosecond scale. It is a long-lived intermediate state, not the final endpoint: the free neutron has τn​=879.4s in the original table and eventually undergoes beta-minus decay,
n→p+e−+νˉe​.
(58)
Thus after several thousand seconds the dominant stable endpoint is
p+e−+νˉe​+4γ,PB​≃(0.99524)(0.358)(0.98823)2=0.3480.​
(59)
Before selecting the two π0→2γ decays, the nπ0π0 history has probability 0.99524×0.358=0.35630. The familiar rough description is therefore a 64% charged-pion route and a 36% neutron route; including the specified leading pion branches gives approximately 62.8% and 34.8% for the two displayed endpoints. The remainder includes Dalitz decays and other rare channels, not a failure of probability conservation.
These are particle decay cascades, treating the separated final proton, electron, photons and neutrinos as stable asymptotic particles and neglecting subsequent collisions and rare extra radiation. An infinitely large box prevents escaping particles from being removed from the inventory. Neutrino labels specify their production flavours. Each lifetime describes a stochastic waiting time, so there is no finite sharp instant at which every unstable particle has certainly decayed.
