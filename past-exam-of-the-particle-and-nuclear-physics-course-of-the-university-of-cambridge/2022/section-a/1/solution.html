All three processes are weak charged current transformations between neighboring nuclear isobars. Neglecting neutrino masses, and writing Ma​(A,Z) for a neutral atomic mass, their Q values are:
  • Beta-minus decay: a neutron converts to a proton, emitting an electron and an electron antineutrino,
(A,Z)⟶(A,Z+1)+e−+νˉe​,Qβ−​=Ma​(A,Z)−Ma​(A,Z+1)>0.
(1)
  • Beta-plus decay: a proton converts to a neutron, emitting a Positron and an electron neutrino,
(A,Z)⟶(A,Z−1)+e++νe​,Qβ+​=Ma​(A,Z)−Ma​(A,Z−1)−2me​>0.
(2)
  • Electron capture: a bound atomic electron is absorbed in the conversion of a proton to a neutron,
e−+(A,Z)⟶(A,Z−1)+νe​,QEC​=Ma​(A,Z)−Ma​(A,Z−1)>0.
(3)
These formulae use the paper's natural units. With energies in MeV, beta-plus decay requires an atomic mass-energy difference exceeding 2me​c2=1.022MeV. The extra electron-mass subtraction arises from using neutral atomic rather than bare nuclear masses. Electron capture can occur when the atomic mass-energy difference is positive but below this threshold. For a particular capture shell or excited daughter, the available energy must also cover the electron-hole and nuclear excitation energies, and the small nuclear recoil. Small atomic binding corrections are suppressed in the displayed threshold formulae.
The question's fixed-A family consists of nuclear isobars, rather than isotopes: isotopes have fixed Z. The semi-empirical mass formula gives a convex nuclear mass parabola as a function of Z at fixed A. For odd A, nuclear pairing does not split the family into two branches, so there is typically one beta-stable isobar, at the discrete minimum. Neighboring members can lower their mass by changing Z toward it.
For even A, nuclear pairing lowers even-even nuclei and raises odd-odd nuclei, producing two offset nuclear mass parabolas. There are often two beta-stable even-even isobars, separated by an odd-odd member to which neither can energetically decay. The number is not an exact universal rule: some even-A chains have one or more than two such minima against single beta decay, and a few light odd-odd nuclei are stable. Stability against single beta decay also does not guarantee stability against double beta decay.
