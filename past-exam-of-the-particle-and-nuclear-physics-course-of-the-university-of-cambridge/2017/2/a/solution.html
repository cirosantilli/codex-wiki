What is measured. A monoenergetic electron beam is directed at a thin target. A movable magnetic spectrometer selects the scattering angle and outgoing momentum, separating elastic scattering from nuclear excitation and other backgrounds. Incident beam current and target thickness supply the normalization of the differential scattering cross-section. The Hofstadter nuclear electron scattering experiment is a classic implementation of this electromagnetic probe.
For a heavy, approximately spin-zero atomic nucleus, neglecting recoil and treating the electron in the Born approximation, the point-charge result is multiplied by the nuclear charge form factor:

Here q is the magnitude of the spatial momentum transfer in natural units. The cosine factor includes the electron spin. Dividing the elastic data by the point-charge factor isolates ∣F(q)∣2 within these approximations. Recoil, magnetic terms for targets with spin, radiative corrections and particularly Coulomb distortion in electron scattering must be included when significant.

A practical extraction fits a family of densities to all measured cross sections, rather than treating a few measured values of ∣F∣2 as a complete density reconstruction. Finite angular coverage, resolution and the missing sign information make this an inverse problem.
Observed pattern. Apart from the lightest nuclei, the central density is approximately constant; the size grows approximately as R≃1.2A1/3fm and there is a diffuse surface. The coefficient describes an equivalent sharp nuclear radius, rather than the root-mean-square radius itself. Heavy nuclear charge distributions are often described by the two-parameter Fermi nuclear charge distribution, ρ(r)=ρ0​/[1+exp((r−c)/a)], with half-density radius c scaling roughly as A1/3 and diffuseness a around 0.5fm. The surface thickness is then about 4aln3≃2.2fm. Shell effects, deformation and the lightest systems give departures from this smooth pattern. These qualitative features are established by the electron scattering density measurements.[ref]
Complementary probes. Transition energies in a muonic atom are especially sensitive to nuclear size because the heavy negative muon orbits close to the charge. Electronic isotope shifts, after separating nuclear-mass and finite-size contributions, measure changes in ⟨r2⟩. Both require atomic-structure calculations. Electromagnetic excitation and transition strengths constrain deformation and charge multipoles. Hadronic scattering can also constrain nuclear dimensions, but carries uncertainties from the strong interaction and probes matter rather than purely electric charge.
