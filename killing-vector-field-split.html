Killing vector field (K)

 Home Physics Branches of physics General relativity
OurBigBook.com Words: 3k Articles: 36
A Killing vector field describes a direction in which geometry has a continuous symmetry: moving points together along its flow of a vector field preserves the metric tensor, including its pairing of tangent vectors. Such a symmetry gives a geodesic conserved quantity from a Killing vector, often a component of momentum or angular momentum. The direction may vary with position: rotation provides the basic example.
On a Riemannian manifold or Lorentzian manifold with metric tensor g, a smooth vector field K is a Killing vector field when its Lie derivative of g vanishes. If Φs​ is its local flow of a vector field, the equivalent statements are
LK​g=0⟺Φs∗​g=g⟺∇(μ​Kν)​=0.
(1)
The middle equality uses the pullback of a covariant tensor: the local flow maps are isometries on their domains. The last equality is the Killing equation, using the Levi-Civita connection. The infinitesimal condition holds throughout an open domain and integrates to exact local isometries; it is stronger than making the first derivatives of g vanish at a single point. See Tong, section 4.3.1, equation (4.29).
For example, on the interval 0<x<1 with Euclidean metric dx2, K=∂x​ translates points by x↦x+s. This is a local isometry, but a point reaches the end of the interval in finite flow parameter. A local flow of a vector field can be incomplete; see Killing fields on incomplete manifolds need not generate global isometries. The full-plane translation and rotation examples under Euclidean Killing vector field instead have flows for every real s.
