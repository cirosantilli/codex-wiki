Trail in a graph

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Foundations of mathematics Graph theory Walk in a graph
OurBigBook.com Words: 269
A trail is a walk whose edge sequence e1​,…,em​ has no repetitions. Its vertices may repeat, including its first and last vertices. The zero-length walk is a trail because it repeats no edge. Every graph path is a trail, but the converse fails: the trail in the shared example is
B,BC,C,CA,A,AD,D,DE,E,EA,A,AF,F.
(17)
Its six edges BC,CA,AD,DE,EA,AF are pairwise distinct, although A occurs twice. Visiting a junction again and traversing a road segment again are different events.[ref]
In an undirected graph, traversing AB and then BA repeats the same edge; direction of travel does not give it a new identity. In a multigraph with distinct parallel edges p,q joining X and Y, the closed walk (X,p,Y,q,X) is a trail, whereas (X,p,Y,p,X) is not. Both have the same vertex sequence X,Y,X. In a directed graph, separate directed edges X→Y and Y→X may instead be used once each; record their identities and obey their directions. Thus testing a trail means checking the actual edge records, not merely checking consecutive endpoint labels.
For a finite graph, a trail has length at most ∣E∣; unrestricted walks can have arbitrarily large length whenever a positive-length closed walk exists, since it can be repeated. An Eulerian trail is the special trail that uses every edge, so its length is exactly ∣E∣. Our length-six trail omits AB and is therefore not an Eulerian trail. In a road-inspection model, a trail describes servicing its selected segments once while allowing returns to junctions; requiring service of every segment leads to the Eulerian trail question. If extra travel between serviced segments is necessary, the complete journey can be a walk even when each segment is serviced only once. This distinction keeps the service requirement separate from the route actually travelled.
