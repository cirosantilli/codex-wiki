For n≤x, write F(n)=F(x)−∫nx​F′(t)dt, multiply by f(n) and sum. Interchanging a finite sum with the integral gives the Abel summation formula
∑n≤x​f(n)F(n)=S(x)F(x)−∫1x​S(t)F′(t)dt.

Apply it with F(t)=t−s and S(t)=∑n≤t​an​. If σ>α, the boundary term is O(xα−σ)→0 and ∫1∞​∣S(t)t−s−1∣dt converges. Therefore the Dirichlet series converges and
A(s)=s∫1∞​S(t)t−s−1dt=s∑m≥1​Im​(s).
(8)
Choose C with ∣S(t)∣≤Ctα for t≥1. For σ>α+δ,
∣Im​(s)∣≤C∫mm+1​tα−σ−1dt≤Cm−1−δ.
(9)
The Weierstrass M-test proves uniform convergence of ∑Im​ on this entire open half-plane. For fixed s, the same estimate with its actual σ gives Im​(s)=O(mα−1−σ). Each Im​ is an entire function, since its integral has bounded positive t range and can be differentiated in s under an integrable bound on each compact set. Thus the locally uniform sum is holomorphic, and so is its product with s. This gives holomorphy from polynomial growth of arithmetic partial sums and proves holomorphy of A for Res>α. Multiplication by the unbounded factor s does not assert uniform convergence of A on a whole unbounded half-plane; local uniform convergence is what the holomorphy argument needs.
With an​=μ(n) and the hypothetical bound, Aμ​(s) is holomorphic for Res>3/4. On Res>1, the Euler product gives ζ(s)Aμ​(s)=1. The identity theorem extends this as a meromorphic identity throughout the connected half-plane Res>3/4. Hence ζ has no zeros in that half-plane: at any zero, the product with the holomorphic Aμ​ would be zero. The pole at one is not a zero; Aμ​ has a compensating zero there.

For x>0, (ex−1)−1=∑n≥1​e−nx. If σ=Res>1, the integral of the sum of absolute values is Γ(σ)∑n−σ<∞. Therefore interchange of sum and integral is justified, and the substitution u=nx gives

On the two unbounded rays of the specified Hankel contour, x≥π and 1/(ex−1) decays exponentially, dominating every fixed power xσ−1. The branch multiplier on the lower ray is a fixed factor e2πi(s−1). On the circular part, the chosen branch is continuous along the parametrized arc, and ez−1 never vanishes because its nonzero zeros have modulus at least 2π. Thus I(s) exists for every s. The estimates are uniform when s ranges over a compact set, including after differentiation in s, so I is an entire function.
Let C(s)=(e2πis−1)Γ(s). The given identity shows that I(s)/C(s) agrees with ζ(s) for Res>1 wherever the quotient is directly defined. It supplies the meromorphic continuation elsewhere wherever C is finite and nonzero, with removable points filled by limits. At a nonpositive integer −k, the simple zero of the exponential factor cancels the supplied gamma pole, giving
C(−k)=k!2πi(−1)k​.

At s=−k, the integrals on the two rays cancel because the integrand is single-valued. The remaining positively oriented circle has only the pole at zero inside it, so

For a short Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve with coefficients in the p-adic integers, a point with nonintegral x has valuations vp​(x)=−2m and vp​(y)=−3m for some integer m≥1: the term x3 strictly dominates Ax+B in the equation, so 2vp​(y)=3vp​(x). Its formal parameter T=−x/y therefore has valuation m≥1. These points, together with the identity, form the formal neighborhood E1​(Qp​) of the identity, with parameter in pZp​ and the elliptic formal group law over the p-adic integers. For a point with x∈Zp​, the equation gives y∈Zp​ too.
The formal neighborhood has no nontrivial torsion. Multiplication by an integer coprime to p has series [m](T)=mT+O(T2) and preserves ∣T∣p​, so any torsion there must have p-power order. If p≥3, part (i) gives ∣T∣p​≥p−1/(p−1)>p−1, contradicting T∈pZp​.
The printed hypotheses also allow p=2. In that case, a nontrivial torsion point in the formal neighborhood would have order a power of two and hence yield a point of order two in that neighborhood. But every nonidentity point of order two on a short Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve has y=0 and x a root of the monic cubic over the p-adic integers X3+AX+B. Such a root lies in the p-adic integers, so it cannot belong to the formal neighborhood, whose nonidentity points have nonintegral coordinates. This excludes the remaining case. This is the torsion-free formal subgroup for a short Weierstrass equation. Thus every affine torsion point satisfies
∣x∣p​≤1,∣y∣p​≤1.​
(29)
With good reduction, the reduction homomorphism E(Qp​)→E(Fp​) has kernel exactly this torsion-free formal neighborhood. Its restriction to the torsion subgroup is therefore injective, proving the required subgroup isomorphism. The short model over the p-adic integers is essential to the prime-two argument; no blanket torsion-injectivity assertion for arbitrary models is needed.

For an equation y2=x(x2+ax+b) with integer coefficients, its first-coordinate square class has a square-free representative dividing b, including a possible sign. Indeed, at a prime not dividing b, a negative valuation of x must be even because x3 dominates; a positive valuation must be even because x2+ax+b is a unit.
