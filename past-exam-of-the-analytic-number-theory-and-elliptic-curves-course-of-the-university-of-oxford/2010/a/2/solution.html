Use the convention f(1)=1 in the definition of a multiplicative arithmetic function. For a finite set of primes, multiplying the absolutely convergent local series gives the Dirichlet sum over integers supported on those primes, by unique prime factorization. If all primes up to Y are included, any missing integer is larger than Y, so the difference from the full series is bounded by ∑n>Y​∣f(n)∣n−σ, which tends to zero. This proves the Euler product in its absolute-convergence half-plane.
For coprime m,n, every divisor of mn is uniquely a product of a divisor of m and a divisor of n. Hence σα​(mn)=σα​(m)σα​(n). The squared modulus is also multiplicative when α=iθ.

Suppose now that ζ(1+iθ)=0. Necessarily θ=0, since s=1 is a pole. Complex conjugation gives a zero also at 1−iθ. In the numerator, the double pole at s=1 is canceled by those two zeros in the shifted factors. At s=1±iθ, the possible simple pole of a shifted factor is canceled by the double zero of ζ(s)2. Thus on Res>0 the only possible remaining poles come from zeros of ζ(2s). Since the Euler product excludes zeros for Re(2s)>1, these possible poles lie in 0<Res≤1/2. In particular f is regular for Res>1/2.
The assumed nonnegative-series principle now makes its Dirichlet series converge and equal f there. Its first coefficient is one and all coefficients are nonnegative, so f(σ)≥1 for real σ>1/2. But as σ↓1/2, the numerator remains finite while ζ(2σ)→+∞, forcing f(σ)→0. This contradiction proves
ζ(s) has no zero on Res=1.​
(12)
At the real point s=1 the statement is understood as a pole rather than a finite nonzero value. The pole-location argument did not assume the zero-free boundary before proving it.
