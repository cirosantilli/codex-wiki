Put U(T)=FX​(0,T). The formal group identity F(X,0)=X gives U(0)=1, so U is a unit in a ring in R[[T]], even if R has zero divisors.
A differential h(T)dT is invariant under formal translation if
h(F(X,T))FX​(X,T)=h(X)
(29)
as an identity of formal power series. Setting X=0 gives h(T)U(T)=h(0). Consequently every invariant differential of a formal group law, if it exists, must have the form h(0)U(T)−1dT. In particular there can be only one with constant coefficient one.
Existence follows directly from associativity. Differentiate
F(Z,F(X,T))=F(F(Z,X),T)
(30)
with respect to Z at zero, obtaining
U(F(X,T))=FX​(X,T)U(X).
(31)
Every displayed U is a unit in a ring, so
U(F(X,T))−1FX​(X,T)=U(X)−1.
(32)
This is precisely invariance of U(T)−1dT. Thus
ω=FX​(0,T)−1dT,{invariant differentials}=Rω.​
(33)
Normalization means its constant coefficient is one. This proves both existence and uniqueness over an arbitrary commutative ring, without division by any integer.
