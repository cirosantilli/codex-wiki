Use the convention f(1)=1 in the definition of a multiplicative arithmetic function. For a finite set of primes, multiplying the absolutely convergent local series gives the Dirichlet sum over integers supported on those primes, by unique prime factorization. If all primes up to Y are included, any missing integer is larger than Y, so the difference from the full series is bounded by ∑n>Y​∣f(n)∣n−σ, which tends to zero. This proves the Euler product in its absolute-convergence half-plane.
For coprime m,n, every divisor of mn is uniquely a product of a divisor of m and a divisor of n. Hence σα​(mn)=σα​(m)σα​(n). The squared modulus is also multiplicative when α=iθ.
At a prime put z=p−s and a=piθ, so ∣a∣=1. Summing by the two divisor exponents gives
∑k≥0​​∑j=0k​aj​2zk=(1−z)2(1−az)(1−a−1z)1−z2​.
(10)
For example, expand the square as ∑j,l≤k​aj−l and sum zk from k=max(j,l) to obtain this rational expression. It remains valid for a=1. Absolute convergence for σ>1 follows from ∣σiθ​(pk)∣≤k+1 and convergence of ∑p​p−σ. Multiplying the factors therefore proves
n≥1∑​ns∣σiθ​(n)∣2​=ζ(2s)ζ(s)2ζ(s+iθ)ζ(s−iθ)​.​
(11)
Suppose now that ζ(1+iθ)=0. Necessarily θ=0, since s=1 is a pole. Complex conjugation gives a zero also at 1−iθ. In the numerator, the double pole at s=1 is canceled by those two zeros in the shifted factors. At s=1±iθ, the possible simple pole of a shifted factor is canceled by the double zero of ζ(s)2. Thus on Res>0 the only possible remaining poles come from zeros of ζ(2s). Since the Euler product excludes zeros for Re(2s)>1, these possible poles lie in 0<Res≤1/2. In particular f is regular for Res>1/2.
The assumed nonnegative-series principle now makes its Dirichlet series converge and equal f there. Its first coefficient is one and all coefficients are nonnegative, so f(σ)≥1 for real σ>1/2. But as σ↓1/2, the numerator remains finite while ζ(2σ)→+∞, forcing f(σ)→0. This contradiction proves
ζ(s) has no zero on Res=1.​
(12)
At the real point s=1 the statement is understood as a pole rather than a finite nonzero value. The pole-location argument did not assume the zero-free boundary before proving it.

The vertical integral is absolutely convergent because the denominator is quadratic in the imaginary part. If x<1, move its line to the right: no poles are crossed, and the integral on Res=R is O(xR/R), tending to zero. If x>1, move left across the simple poles at 0,−1. Their residues are 1 and −x−1, and the far-left integral tends to zero. Horizontal segments vanish by their O(∣Ims∣−2) decay for a fixed pair of vertical lines. At x=1, dominated convergence on the original line gives the limiting value zero. Hence the kernel is 0 below one and 1−x−1 at or above one.
On a line c>2, absolute convergence permits interchanging the totient Dirichlet series and this integral. The nth term is xϕ(n) times the kernel at x/n, giving exactly ϕ(n)(x−n) for n≤x. Thus
S(x):=∑n≤x​ϕ(n)(x−n)=2πi1​∫c−i∞c+i∞​ζ(s)ζ(s−1)​s(s+1)xs+1​ds.

We first prove all three general torsion assertions before treating the lettered cases. If a finite point has ∣x∣p​>1, the cubic term dominates the integral lower terms, so vp​(x)=−2m, vp​(y)=−3m for an integer m≥1. Its parameter z=−x/y then lies in pZp​, with norm at most p−1. The integral Weierstrass model defines its formal group at infinity over Zp​: using w=−1/y gives w=z3+Azw2+Bw3, an integral power series starting in degree three.
The supplied torsion estimate excludes a nonzero torsion parameter for p≥3, since p−1<p−1/(p−1), and the lower bound increases for higher p-powers. For p=2 it excludes orders 2n with n≥2, because their bound is strictly above 1/2. The remaining possibility is order two, which would imply the finite point equals its negative and hence y=0. A nonintegral x cannot solve the monic cubic with integral coefficients, so this is impossible too. Thus every finite torsion point has integral x, and the equation then forces integral y.
For good reduction the reduction map is a group homomorphism whose kernel is this formal group at infinity. Its torsion-free kernel proves injectivity on torsion, giving a subgroup of the finite reduced group. Applying local integrality to a rational torsion point at every prime gives x,y∈Z.
If y=0, the double of a rational torsion point is finite and integral. The doubling formula
x(2P)=4y2(3x2+a)2​−2x
(27)
therefore implies y2∣(3x2+a)2. Also y2=x3+ax+b. Evaluating the supplied polynomial identity at the integral x now gives
y=0ory2∣4a3+27b2.​
(28)
This establishes the needed Nagell–Lutz theorem conclusions without assuming them in the lettered applications.

Modulo five the curve is y2=x3+x, with good reduction because 4a3+27b2≡4(mod5). It has one point above each of x=0,2,3 and no others, plus infinity, so its group has order four. Rational torsion therefore has order dividing four.
