absolute convergence depends only on σ=Res, since ∣an​n−s∣=∣an​∣n−σ. Let A be the set of real σ for which ∑∣an​∣n−σ converges. Comparison with a convergent member shows that A is upward closed. The hypotheses give a member of A and a real number outside A, so its infimum σ0​ is finite. If σ>σ0​, some τ∈A lies below σ, giving convergence by comparison; if σ<σ0​, convergence would contradict the definition of the infimum. Thus σ0​=infA is the abscissa of absolute convergence of a Dirichlet series. This argument leaves convergence on the boundary open.
Partition 1,…,n according to d=n/gcd(k,n). Writing k=(n/d)j identifies the part indexed by d∣n with the integers 1≤j≤d coprime to d. There are ϕ(d) of them. Counting the entire partition proves the totient divisor-sum identity
d∣n∑​ϕ(d)=n.​
(1)
We use the multiplication theorem for absolutely convergent Dirichlet series: their product is the Dirichlet series of the Dirichlet convolution of their coefficients. absolute convergence justifies rearrangement of the double sum. Here 0<ϕ(n)≤n, so its series converges absolutely for σ>2, and
ζ(s)∑n≥1​nsϕ(n)​=∑n≥1​ns∑d∣n​ϕ(d)​=∑n≥1​n1−s=ζ(s−1).
(2)
The Euler product implies ζ(s)=0 for σ>1. Division gives
n≥1∑​nsϕ(n)​=ζ(s)ζ(s−1)​(Res>2).​
(3)
We finally use the simple pole of the Riemann zeta function at one, of residue one, and 0<ζ(2)<∞. The quotient therefore tends to +∞ as real s↓2. This is the totient Dirichlet series and its absolute convergence boundary.

Work with the smooth projective cubic, including its points at infinity. For two points P,Q, draw their chord, using the tangent when they coincide, and let R be its third intersection counted with intersection multiplicity. Draw the line through R and o, again using the tangent if necessary, and let S be its third intersection. Define P+Q=S. When o is a flex, this is the familiar reflection of the first third-intersection point across the Weierstrass involution. The two-line construction also works when the chosen identity is not a flex.
To prove associativity without assuming it in the geometry, use divisor class. A nonsingular plane cubic has genus one. By the Riemann-Roch theorem, every divisor class of degree one contains exactly one effective divisor on an algebraic curve of degree one: for such a divisor on an algebraic curve D,
ℓ(D)−ℓ(KC​−D)=degD=1,
(16)
and deg(KC​−D)=−1, so ℓ(KC​−D)=0. Thus ℓ(D)=1, and the nonzero section is unique up to scalar. Over K, a degree-one effective divisor on an algebraic curve defined over K is a K-rational point. This also proves uniqueness of that point. In particular the map
ι:C(K)⟶Pic0(C),P⟼[P−o]
(17)
is injective and identifies these points with the corresponding degree-zero Picard group of a curve: to recover the point from a class, add [o] and take its unique effective representative. This is the elliptic curve group law from Riemann-Roch.
Let H denote the divisor class cut out by a line. The two lines in the chord-and-tangent group law give
P+Q+R∼H,R+o+S∼H.
(18)
Their difference gives P+Q∼o+S, hence
ι(S)=[P−o]+[Q−o].
(19)
Addition of divisor class is associative. Therefore both (P+Q)+R and P+(Q+R) map to [P−o]+[Q−o]+[R−o]. Injectivity of ι proves the chord-and-tangent group law is associative. The same argument gives identity o, commutativity and inverses, and covers tangencies through the use of intersection multiplicities. All constructions are defined over K.

The discriminant of y2=x3−9 is −16⋅27⋅81=−2437, so five and seven are primes of good reduction of an elliptic curve. Count solutions over their residue fields.
Modulo five the values of x3−9 for x=0,1,2,3,4 are 1,2,4,3,0. They yield 2,0,2,0,1 affine points, respectively, and the point at infinity gives #E(F5​)=6.

Equivalently one may use prime-to-residue-characteristic injection at both primes; it gives the same coprime bound. This is a two-prime bound on rational elliptic torsion.
