Chemical equilibrium

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics Chemical potential
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 27
At chemical equilibrium, a reversible chemical reaction has equal forward and reverse reaction rates, so its macroscopic composition stays constant. Individual molecules can keep changing identity: the reaction has not stopped. This dynamic balance is the definition described by the [ref]. Equal reaction rates also need not mean equal concentrations. A small population converting rapidly can balance a large population converting slowly.
This distinction gives two useful questions. Which composition is favored at the specified temperature and other constraints? Thermodynamics answers through chemical potentials and an equilibrium constant. How quickly does a prepared mixture approach that composition? Chemical kinetics answers through reaction rates. Knowing the destination does not determine the journey's duration. A composition that barely changes during observation may reflect extremely slow chemical kinetics, so constancy alone does not establish chemical equilibrium.
Consider the idealized elementary chemical reaction
A⇌B,kf​=0.3s−1,kr​=0.1s−1.
(13)
Assume a well-mixed closed system at fixed temperature and volume, with first-order law of mass action rates in both directions and no competing chemical reactions. Write a=[A] and b=[B] for concentrations in M=mol/L. The total a+b=C=1M is conserved because each conversion replaces one molecule by one molecule. This conservation is special to the chosen stoichiometry.
The forward and reverse reaction rates, measured as conversions per unit volume per unit time, are Jf​=kf​a and Jr​=kr​b. Hence
dtda​=−kf​a+kr​b=−(kf​+kr​)a+kr​C.
(14)
Setting this derivative to zero gives
aeq​=kf​+kr​kr​C​=0.25M,beq​=0.75M.
(15)
With a(0)=1M and b(0)=0, solving the ordinary differential equation yields
a(t)=(0.25+0.75e−0.4t/s)M,b(t)=(0.75−0.75e−0.4t/s)M.
(16)
At t=5s, these are approximately 0.3515M and 0.6485M. Initially Jf​=0.3M/s and Jr​=0. At chemical equilibrium, both are 0.075M/s, while the net reaction rate Jf​−Jr​ is zero. The persistent exchange is visible in the lower panel even after the upper panel has nearly flattened.
Why does chemical equilibrium also involve a free energy minimum? For a closed system at fixed temperature and pressure, without imposed non-expansion work, stable equilibrium minimizes Gibbs free energy over compositions allowed by conserved elemental inventories. See W. C. Carter, MIT thermodynamics notes, sections 2-1.14 and 2-1.19. This is constrained Gibbs minimization for chemical equilibrium: freely minimizing over unrelated species amounts would change the inventory. At fixed temperature and volume, the appropriate potential is Helmholtz free energy. In either setting, varying an allowed extent of reaction gives the corresponding free energy slope ∑i​νi​μi​, with molar chemical potentials and signed stoichiometric vector entries. An interior equilibrium has zero slope; stability requires a minimum. For A converting to B, this says μB​=μA​.
The beauty of this connection is that molecular exchange, a macroscopic composition, and a geometric minimum describe the same balance. In the ideal concentration model, let x=b/C and choose the standard molar chemical potential difference consistent with K=3. Up to a composition-independent constant, the fixed-volume Helmholtz free energy then obeys
nRTF​=(1−x)ln(1−x)+xlnx−xln3,
(17)
where n is the conserved total amount of substance and R the molar gas constant. Its derivative is ln[x/(1−x)]−ln3, vanishing at x=3/4. Its second derivative is 1/x+1/(1−x)>0 inside 0<x<1, so this balance is the unique minimum. The mixing entropy terms explain why the favored composition still contains A.
