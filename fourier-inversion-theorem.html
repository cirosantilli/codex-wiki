Fourier inversion theorem (F−1)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Fourier analysis
OurBigBook.com Words: 499 Articles: 4
The Fourier inversion theorem makes decomposition into frequencies reversible: a complete Fourier transform, including its wave phases, determines the original function up to equality almost everywhere. With the angular-frequency convention
f​(ξ)=∫Rn​e−ix⋅ξf(x)dx,
(62)
if f and f​ belong to the L1 space, then
f(x)=(2π)n1​∫Rn​eix⋅ξf​(ξ)dξ
(63)
almost everywhere. The right side is a continuous representative of f. If f itself is continuous, equality holds everywhere, as explained by the continuous version of Fourier inversion. The integrability hypotheses ensure that both displayed integrals are ordinary absolutely convergent integrals. For arbitrary elements of the L2 space, the Plancherel theorem instead supplies inversion in the L2 space norm.
Each factor eix⋅ξ is a Fourier mode; the inverse integral adds them with the complex weights f​(ξ)/(2π)n. The normalization is part of the recipe. In one dimension, writing ordinary frequency as ν=ξ/(2π) gives the equivalent pair
F(ν)=∫R​f(t)e−2πiνtdt,f(t)=∫R​F(ν)e2πiνtdν,
(64)
where F(ν)=f​(2πν). Reversing both exponential signs gives another equivalent convention, but the forward and inverse formulas must use opposite signs consistently.
For example, the Fourier transform of a Gaussian gives
f(x)=e−ax2,f​(ξ)=aπ​​e−ξ2/(4a),a>0.
(65)
Fourier inversion says that a weighted continuum of oscillations reconstructs this smooth Gaussian function. Narrowing the original Gaussian function by increasing a broadens its frequency spectrum. This is a concrete demonstration that a sharply localized shape needs a wider range of frequencies.
Inversion is what makes a frequency calculation useful: transform a function, solve the simpler problem there, then return to the original variables. A brief proof outline is to multiply f​(ξ) by e−ϵ∣ξ∣2, ϵ>0. The inverse integral becomes convolution with a normalized Gaussian function, an approximate identity. As ϵ tends to zero, dominated convergence gives the unregularized inverse integral, while the approximate identity recovers f almost everywhere. Joseph Fourier's Théorie analytique de la chaleur (1822), Chapter IX, article 417, contains a cosine double-integral representation antecedent to this modern formula; the hypotheses above are the modern statement.[ref]
