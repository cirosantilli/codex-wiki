Write a=[A], b=[B] and c=[C] for the three concentrations. The letters denote chemical species; the forward arrow represents association of one molecule of each reactant into one complex, and the reverse arrow represents dissociation. The positive constants k+​ and k−​ are the respective reaction rate constants. For an elementary chemical reaction, the law of mass action gives forward and reverse reaction rates
R+​=k+​ab,R−​=k−​c.
(1)
Every forward event removes one molecule of each reactant and adds one complex; a reverse event reverses those changes. Thus the reaction-rate equations are
a˙=−k+​ab+k−​c,b˙=−k+​ab+k−​c,c˙=k+​ab−k−​c.​
(2)
The units of k+​ are inverse concentration per unit time, whereas k−​ has units of inverse time. Adding the equations gives the independent conservation laws
a+c=a(0)+c(0),b+c=b(0)+c(0).​
(3)
In particular a−b is constant, but this is a consequence of those two conservation laws. The total a+b+c need not be constant: association changes the number of separate molecules.
For the general stoichiometry, take p,q to be positive integers. The net reaction rate and stoichiometric vector are
R=k+​apbq−k−​c,ν=(−p,−q,1)T.
(4)
Consequently
a˙=−pR,b˙=−qR,c˙=R.​
(5)
The factors p,q outside R count molecules consumed per event; the powers inside R express the law of mass action. The corresponding conservation laws are a+pc=constant and b+qc=constant. No factorial is required in this deterministic convention for the reaction rate constant.

Normalize the total channel density to one. For independent permissive gates in an ion channel, the probability that the M gate is open is m, and the probability that the H gate is open is h. The joint probability is the product of the appropriate open or closed probabilities, giving
Sij​=mi(1−m)1−ihj(1−h)1−j.​
(7)
In particular
S00​=(1−m)(1−h),S10​=m(1−h),S01​=(1−m)h,S11​=mh.
(8)
The original PDF really prints j, rather than 1−j, on the final factor. That expression fails normalization of a probability distribution and does not give the closed-H probability at j=0; the displayed correction is necessary. If Sij​ are literal densities with conserved total density N, multiply each expression by N.
The product additionally assumes initially independent events and independent gate transitions. The transition scheme alone permits initially correlated gates and does not force this factorization at every time. The all-closed initial preparation used below satisfies the required product assumption.

In the Hodgkin-Huxley model, V is the intracellular-minus-extracellular membrane potential, Cm​ is the membrane capacitance per unit area, and Iapp​ is the applied inward electric current per unit area. A space clamp makes V spatially uniform, leaving temporal ordinary differential equations. With outward ionic electric current positive, the capacitance balance is Cm​V˙=Iapp​−Ii​.
The variables m,n,h lie between zero and one. They describe sodium activation, potassium activation and availability against sodium inactivation, respectively. Thus larger h means less inactivation. Their voltage-dependent limiting values are m∞​,n∞​,h∞​, and τm​,τn​,τh​ are their positive relaxation time constants.
Each ionic term has the Ohm's law form conductance times driving potential. The sodium term has instantaneous electrical conductance gNa​m3h, the potassium term has instantaneous electrical conductance gK​n4, and the leak term has constant electrical conductance gL​. Here the g constants are maximal electrical conductances per area, and VNa​,VK​,VL​ are the corresponding reversal potentials. The factors m3h and n4 arise from independent permissive gates in an ion channel. A conducting channel carries zero net ionic electric current at its reversal potential.

Use the three standard reduction assumptions: m relaxes much faster than V,n,h and can be replaced by m∞​(V); the two slower gates have the same time constant, τn​(V)=τh​(V)=τ(V); and their limiting open fractions have a voltage-independent sum, n∞​(V)+h∞​(V)=K. The constant-sum assumption is essential: equality of two time constants by itself does not preserve a moving voltage-dependent sum.
For q=n+h, addition of the two gate ordinary differential equations gives
τ(V)q˙​=K−q.
(20)
The specified initial condition is q(0)=K. Uniqueness for this first-order linear differential equation gives q(t)=K, hence h=K−n for all time. The remaining two ordinary differential equations are
Cm​V˙=τ(V)n˙=​Iapp​−gNa​m∞​(V)3(K−n)(V−VNa​)−gK​n4(V−VK​)−gL​(V−VL​),n∞​(V)−n.​​
(21)
These assumptions give a planar reduction of the Hodgkin-Huxley model. The FitzHugh-Nagumo model keeps its fast activation and slow recovery variable geometry, approximating the voltage response by a cubic polynomial and the recovery response by a linear function after nondimensionalization. The polynomial equations in part (c) are a further model simplification; they are not an exact algebraic consequence of arbitrary original gate functions.

Assume ε>0. At an equilibrium, w=γv=f(v), so
v[(a−v)(v−1)−γ]=0.
(22)
The nonzero roots are
v±​=21+a±(1−a)2−4γ​​,w±​=γv±​.
(23)
Consequently, with γc​=(1−a)2/4, there are three distinct equilibria for 0<γ<γc​, two distinct equilibria for γ=γc​, and only the origin for γ>γc​. At equality the two nonzero equilibria coincide; for 0<γ<γc​ they both have a<v<1. Geometrically, γc​ is the maximum of the concave quadratic (a−v)(v−1).
Since f′(0)=−a, the Jacobian matrix at the origin is
J0​=(−a/εγ​−1/ε−1​),trJ0​=−εa​−1<0,detJ0​=εa+γ​>0.
(24)
Thus the origin is always asymptotically stable. Its eigenvalues are
λ±​=2ε−(a+ε)±(a−ε)2−4εγ​​.
(25)
Writing γN​=(a−ε)2/(4ε), the origin is a stable node for 0<γ<γN​ and a stable focus for γ>γN​. At γ=γN​>0 it is a degenerate node: the repeated negative eigenvalue has only one independent eigenvector, since the nonzero off-diagonal entry prevents J0​ being a scalar matrix. If a=ε, then γN​=0 and every permitted γ>0 gives a stable focus. This change from node to focus does not change stability and is not a Hopf bifurcation.

Take γ>γc​ and let ε→0+ with the other parameters fixed. The nullclines are the cubic polynomial graph w=f(v) and the line w=γv. In the phase plane, v increases below the cubic polynomial graph and decreases above it; w increases below the line and decreases above it. The fast ordinary differential equation, in time s=t/ε, freezes w and gives dv/ds=f(v)−w.
At w=0, the fast roots are 0,a,1. Their linear stability follows from f′(0)=−a<0, f′(a)=a(1−a)>0 and f′(1)=a−1<0. Thus a fixed 0<v∗<a falls towards the resting branch, whereas a fixed a<v∗<1 rises towards v=1. If v∗>1, the first fast segment instead settles down towards 1, followed by the same recovery excursion. The threshold a is the leading singular perturbation threshold; finite-ε corrections matter for initial values approaching a with ε.
Let
d=1−a+a2​,vL​=31+a−d​,vR​=31+a+d​.
(26)
These are the roots of f′(v)=0. The outer branches v<vL​ and v>vR​ of the critical manifold are attracting under fast motion; the middle branch is repelling. The full excursion has four stages.
  • Fast activation: nearly horizontal movement towards (1,0), on the t=O(ε) time scale, apart from the usual matching tails.
  • First slow phase: motion along w=f(v) from v=1 down to v=vR​. Here w rises and v slowly falls, because w˙=γv−f(v)>0 on the positive outer branch. This takes order-one time for fixed γ.
  • Fast repolarization: near the right fold point of a critical manifold, the attracting branch ends. With wR​=f(vR​) nearly constant, v drops to the negative root vJ​ of f(vJ​)=wR​. The jump takes the fast time scale; a thin neighborhood of the fold has an intermediate fold delay.
  • Second slow phase: motion along the negative attracting branch from vJ​ towards 0 from below, while w falls towards zero. This is the refractory recovery and has order-one duration to a fixed small cutoff; exact return to the equilibrium takes infinite time.
For this cubic polynomial, the landing point is explicit. Since f(v)−f(vR​)=−(v−vR​)2(v−vJ​), comparison of the quadratic coefficients gives
vJ​=1+a−2vR​=31+a−21−a+a2​​<0.​
(27)
Thus a graph of v(t) has a rapid rise, a slowly falling elevated plateau, a rapid fall through zero to a negative undershoot, and a slow approach back to zero. The figure shows both the phase plane and a computed time trace. The smaller initial perturbation stays near the resting branch and produces no elevated plateau.
The four-stage description concerns the singular limit. Close to a simple fold point of a critical manifold, balancing the local quadratic activation term with the slow drift gives Δv=O(ε1/3) and Δt=O(ε2/3) for fixed parameters. These layers shrink compared with the slow phases and do not alter the leading time integrals below.

In this two-pool calcium model, c is the cytosolic calcium ion concentration, and cs​ measures calcium in an intracellular store, such as the endoplasmic reticulum. The variables in these balances are expressed using compatible effective volumes, so internal transfers enter their two equations with equal and opposite signs. If actual compartment concentrations use different reference volumes, the corresponding volume ratio must first be included in their balances.
The flux J+​ pumps calcium from the cytosol into the store, J−​ releases it back, and ks​cs​ is passive store leakage back to the cytosol. The external input r supplies cytosolic calcium, while kc exports it from the biological cell. Thus k,ks​ have units of inverse time and all five fluxes have units of concentration per time. Their directions can be sketched as
exterior  r ​ cytosol c  J+​ ​ store cs​,store cs​  J−​+ks​cs​ ​ cytosol c  kc ​ exterior.
(35)
Adding the two ordinary differential equations verifies the total calcium balance
dtd​(c+cs​)=r−kc.​
(36)
Internal uptake, release and leakage redistribute calcium; only the exchanges across the cell membrane change this total. In particular any equilibrium must have c=r/k when k>0.

To justify existence of a periodic orbit of the full planar system, rather than just draw a formal cycle, first establish a compact trapping region. The positive quadrant is forward invariant: at u=0, u˙=μ+γδv/ε>0, and at v=0, v˙=u2/[ε(1+u2)]≥0. Moreover
v˙≤ε1−δv​.
(56)
Choose V>1/δ above the initial store value. Since f(u,v)≥−1−δV when 0≤v≤V,
u˙≤μ−u+εγ​(1+δV).
(57)
Thus the rectangle 0≤u≤U, 0≤v≤V is a positively invariant set if U>μ+γ(1+δV)/ε and the initial point lies inside it. Every forward solution is bounded. There are no equilibria on its axes for μ>0, and its sole interior equilibrium is a hyperbolic equilibrium point with both eigenvalues having positive real parts in the strict unstable range. A sufficiently small neighborhood of this repelling equilibrium has outward flow; deleting it leaves a compact trapping region with no equilibria. The Poincaré-Bendixson theorem then supplies a periodic orbit.
Hence periodic solutions exist throughout the strict unstable interval, interpreted with the finite-ε correction above. This argument establishes existence, without asserting uniqueness, the criticality of possible endpoint Hopf bifurcations, or absence of coexisting periodic orbits outside the interval.
