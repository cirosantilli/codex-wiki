In the Hodgkin-Huxley model, V is the membrane potential, the voltage inside the axon relative to outside. The space clamp assumption makes it spatially uniform. The gating variables lie between zero and one: m describes sodium activation, h describes availability against sodium inactivation, and n describes potassium activation. Thus large h means channels remain available, not that inactivation is strong.
The left side Cm​V˙ is capacitive electric current per unit membrane area. The imposed I is inward-positive applied electric current. The other terms are outward-positive ionic electric currents: sodium with electrical conductance gNa​m3h, potassium with conductance gK​n4, and a passive leak with conductance gL​. Each current is its conductance times the difference between V and the corresponding reversal potential VNa​, VK​ or VL​. The coefficients gNa​,gK​ are maximal conductances; the gate factors represent independent permissive gates in an ion channel.
For each gate, k∞​(V) is the equilibrium opening/availability fraction at fixed voltage and τk​(V)>0 its exponential relaxation time. The equation follows from two-state ion-channel gating: if k˙=αk​(V)(1−k)−βk​(V)k, then
k∞​=αk​+βk​αk​​,τk​=αk​+βk​1​.​
(1)
Voltage feedback couples these otherwise elementary gating equations to the membrane potential and produces action potentials.

Write M(v)=m3(v). In the physically relevant strip −vK​<v<1, 0<n<H, the zero-current nullcline satisfies
H−nn4​=γK​(v+vK​)(1−v)M(v)​.​
(2)
The left side increases strictly from zero to infinity, so there is exactly one nullcline height n=N(v) for each interior voltage. If M is positive at the endpoints, its curve joins (−vK​,H) to (1,0); when low-voltage activation is idealized as exactly zero, the limiting left branch can lie on v=−vK​.
The signs of the derivatives explain its folds:
gn​=4γK​(v+vK​)n3+(1−v)M(v)>0,
(3)
gv​=γK​n4+(H−n)M(v)−(1−v)(H−n)M′(v),N′(v)=−gv​/gn​.
(4)
At low voltage, potassium's approximately linear contribution dominates and gv​>0. During rapid sodium activation the last negative term can dominate, giving gv​<0. Once activation saturates, M′ becomes small and gv​>0 again. This folded conductance-model nullcline has the familiar shape: the curve has descending outer branches and an ascending middle branch in the (v,n) plane. Equivalently, an intermediate fixed n can give three zeros of g as voltage rises, with positive, negative and positive gv​ at those crossings.
The rapid-activation description permits this folded shape; exact fold positions depend on M,γK​,H,vK​. The figure supplies an explicit admissible sigmoid example. Its right panel shows g=0 as well as the recovery nullcline and an oscillating trajectory.

The voltage nullcline is g(v,n)=I∗ and the recovery nullcline is n=n∞​(v). In the relevant region gn​>0, so below the voltage curve v˙>0 and above it v˙<0. Below the recovery curve n˙>0, while above it n˙<0.
For small ϵ, the outer voltage branches with gv​>0 attract fast voltage motion; the middle branch with gv​<0 repels it. Arrange the intersections so the equilibrium is on that middle branch, with recovery increasing along the high-voltage attracting branch and decreasing along the low-voltage attracting branch. The trajectory then drifts towards a fold, jumps rapidly to the other attracting branch at nearly constant n, and repeats. This is a relaxation oscillation.
The local linear stability calculation supports the construction. At an equilibrium,
J=(−gv​/ϵn∞′​​−gn​/ϵ−1​),trJ=−gv​/ϵ−1,detJ=ϵgv​+gn​n∞′​​.
(5)
A sufficiently steep recovery curve gives positive determinant, while gv​<−ϵ gives positive trace. Hence the equilibrium is a repeller. The bounded physical rectangle can be chosen forward invariant, and a small neighborhood of the repeller removed to give a trapping annulus. The Poincaré-Bendixson theorem then supplies a periodic orbit. These conditions can be realized because the recovery function and the applied current control the intersection and its slope; oscillation is possible, not forced for every choice.
For the illustration, γK​=0.1, H=0.8, vK​=0.2, ϵ=0.01, I∗=0, M(v)=(1+e−25(v−0.2))−1 and n∞​(v)=0.8/(1+e−20v). The rectangle [−0.2,1]×[0,0.8] has inward-pointing flow, and the displayed numerical trajectory realizes the folded-branch mechanism.

The nullclines are w=g(v) and v=0. The two outer pieces of g have slope −1; the middle piece has positive slope
μ=β−α1−β+α​>0.
(6)
The curve is continuous, with folds (α,−α) and (β,1−β). The unique equilibrium is (0,−α/(β−α)). Its Jacobian matrix has trace μ/ϵ>0 and determinant 1/ϵ>0, so it is unstable. The outer branches attract the fast voltage motion.
Let wL​=−α and wR​=1−β. The hypothesis β−α<1 gives 0<wL​<wR​<1. The singular relaxation oscillation follows this route:
  • On the right branch v=1−w>0, w slowly increases from wL​ to wR​.
  • At w=wR​, the fast voltage jumps from v=β to v=β−1<α.
  • On the left branch v=−w<0, w slowly decreases from wR​ to wL​.
  • At w=wL​, voltage jumps from v=α to v=1+α>β.
The jumps are almost horizontal in the phase plane, because w changes negligibly on the fast O(ϵ) time. Below w=g(v), voltage increases; above it, voltage decreases; w increases on the right of v=0 and decreases on the left. These signs provide a closed trapping region around the indicated route for sufficiently small positive ϵ, with the unstable equilibrium excluded, giving a periodic orbit by the planar return-map or Poincaré-Bendixson theorem argument. The continuous piecewise linear field is locally Lipschitz, so solution uniqueness is available at the corners.

Let pa​,pv​,pLV​ be pressures in the arterial reservoir, venous reservoir and left ventricle. Constant vascular compliances Ca​,Cv​ relate volume changes to pressure changes. The ventricular elastance relation is
VLV​=V0​+ELV​(t)pLV​​,
(9)
where V0​ is the unstressed ventricular volume and ELV​(t)>0 is the time-dependent inverse compliance. Define the positive part [x]+​=max(x,0). The systemic, aortic-valve and mitral-valve flows are
Qc​=Rc​pa​−pv​​,Qa​=Ra​[pLV​−pa​]+​​,Qv​=Rv​[pv​−pLV​]+​​.
(10)
Each R is a positive hydraulic resistance. The aortic valve permits outflow only when ventricular pressure exceeds arterial pressure, and the mitral valve permits filling only when venous pressure exceeds ventricular pressure.
Mass conservation, with blood treated as incompressible, gives
Ca​p˙​a​=Qa​−Qc​,Cv​p˙​v​=Qc​−Qv​,dtd​(ELV​pLV​​)=Qv​−Qa​.
(11)
Substitution and multiplication of the first two equations by Rc​ give exactly
Rc​Ca​p˙​a​Rc​Cv​p˙​v​dtd​(pLV​/ELV​)​=−(pa​−pv​)+Ra​Rc​​[pLV​−pa​]+​,=(pa​−pv​)−Rv​Rc​​[pv​−pLV​]+​,=Rv​[pv​−pLV​]+​​−Ra​[pLV​−pa​]+​​.​​
(12)
Adding the three original balances gives zero total volume change. This is a three-compartment ventricular circulation model.

During isovolumetric contraction, ventricular pressure lies between the venous and arterial pressures, pv​<pLV​<pa​. Both valves are closed, so Qv​=Qa​=0 and ventricular volume remains at its end-filling value V+. Consequently
pLV​=ELV​(t)(V+−V0​).​
(13)
Here V+ is end-diastolic ventricular volume and V0​ is the zero-pressure/unstressed volume.
The arterial and venous reservoirs still exchange systemic blood:
p˙​a​=−Rc​Ca​pa​−pv​​,p˙​v​=Rc​Cv​pa​−pv​​.
(14)
If contraction is short compared with both Rc​Ca​ and Rc​Cv​, the fractional changes are small and
pa​≃pa+​,pv​≃pv+​,​
(15)
where the superscript values refer to the beginning of contraction. Thus pressure can rise rapidly in the left ventricle through increasing elastance while the other pressures are approximately fixed. This stage ends when pLV​ reaches pa​ and the aortic valve opens.

During ejection the mitral valve is closed, so Qv​=0. Adding the arterial and ventricular balances cancels aortic flow:
Rc​Ca​p˙​a​+Rc​dtd​(pLV​/ELV​)=−(pa​−pv​)≃−pa​,
(16)
where the last approximation assumes pv​≪pa​.
Model the rapid contraction as a switch to approximately constant systolic elastance ES​, with Cs​=1/ES​ the systolic ventricular compliance. A sufficiently small aortic-valve resistance makes pLV​≃pa​ after a short pressure-equalization transient. The systolic pressure must be high enough to open the valve, ES​(V+−V0​)>pa+​. During that transient neglect systemic runoff. Conservation of combined arterial and stressed ventricular volume gives
Ca​pa+​+V+−V0​=(Ca​+Cs​)p​a​,
(17)
so
p​a​≃Ca​+Cs​Ca​pa+​+V+−V0​​.​
(18)
This is an approximation across a rapid transient, not a discontinuity at finite valve resistance. The subsequent common pressure obeys Rc​(Ca​+Cs​)p˙​a​≃−pa​, giving
pa​(t)≃p​a​exp[−Rc​(Ca​+Cs​)t​].​
(19)
The source's RC​ in this expression denotes the same systemic resistance Rc​.
At the end of firing, the remaining ventricular volume is Vend​≃V0​+Cs​pa​(ΔtF​). The stroke volume is V+−Vend​, hence
SV≃V+−V0​−Ca​+Cs​Cs​​(Ca​pa+​+V+−V0​)exp[−Rc​(Ca​+Cs​)ΔtF​​].​
(20)
The assumptions are a closed mitral valve throughout ejection, negligible venous pressure, rapid valve equalization relative to systemic runoff, and nearly constant systolic elastance during the interval measured by ΔtF​. These give the ventricular ejection pressure and stroke-volume approximation.

A positive solution stays positive, since
u(t)=u(0)exp(∫0t​[1−u(s−τ)]ds).
(21)
Therefore the sign of u˙ is exactly the sign of 1−u(t−τ). Initially the given history below one makes u˙>0. If u crosses one upwards at time t0​, it continues growing until t0​+τ, because the feedback still sees earlier values below one. It reaches a maximum when the delayed value crosses one. Similarly, after a downward crossing at t1​, it continues decreasing until t1​+τ and reaches a minimum then.
This delayed reversal makes overshoots and undershoots possible. In a derivative sketch, positive lobes persist until one delay after upward crossings, followed by negative lobes persisting until one delay after downward crossings. Sustained oscillation depends on the delay; a short delay can instead give convergence to one. The example shown has τ=2>π/2 and a history identically 1/2.

Put u=1+x. Then x˙=−x(t−τ)−x(t)x(t−τ), so the linearization is x˙=−x(t−τ). A mode eλt gives the characteristic equation of a delay differential equation
λ=−e−λτ.​
(22)
For real λ≥0, the left side is nonnegative and the right side negative. The two real graphs therefore never meet there, as drawn in the figure. There is no nonoscillatory exponential instability. If stability is lost, the unstable eigenvalues must be a nonreal conjugate pair, producing oscillatory growth; absence of a positive real root alone does not imply stability.

For a purely imaginary root λ=iω with ω>0, separating real and imaginary parts gives
0=−cos(ωτ),ω=sin(ωτ).
(23)
Thus ω=1 and τ=π/2+2πm, m≥0. The first crossing is
τc​=π/2,λ=±i.​
(24)
For 0<τ<π/2 there is no root with nonnegative real part. Indeed, write λ=a+ib with a≥0. The real equation forces cos(bτ)≤0, hence ∣b∣τ≥π/2. But the imaginary equation gives ∣b∣≤e−aτ≤1, contradicting τ<π/2.
Differentiate the characteristic equation along the root:
dτdλ​=−1+τλλ2​,Redτdλ​​λ=i,τ=τc​​=1+τc2​1​>0.
(25)
The imaginary roots are simple because 1+iτc​=0, and they cross into the unstable half-plane as delay increases.
One can also check the nonlinear Hopf bifurcation directly. With θ=ωt and small amplitude a, substitute
x=acosθ+a2(51​cos2θ+101​sin2θ)+O(a3),ω=1+νa2+O(a3),τ=τc​+μa2+O(a3).

The constant and second harmonics at order a2 give the displayed coefficients. Put d=μ+τc​ν. At order a3, absence of resonant first harmonics requires d+1/20=0 and ν+3/20=0. Hence
