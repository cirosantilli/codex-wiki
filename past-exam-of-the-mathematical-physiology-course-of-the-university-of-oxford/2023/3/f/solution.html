On the descending part of the graph, mark the two tangency points where
g′(μ−​)=−1/γ=g′(μ+​),μ−​<μ+​.​
(52)
Choose the crossings that enclose the portion with g′(u)<−1/γ. For fixed μ strictly between these values and sufficiently small ε, the unique equilibrium is a repelling equilibrium by part (e). The endpoints here are leading singular limit values; at finite ε the boundaries instead solve fv​−γfu​=ε and approach these tangencies when the crossings are nondegenerate.
There is a necessary qualification: such an interval exists only when γ is large enough that the descending graph actually attains slopes below −1/γ. If M=maxu>0​[−g′(u)], one needs γM>1. The supplied graph has a very steep descending section, but an arbitrarily small positive γ need not satisfy this condition. For δ=0.001, numerical evaluation gives M≃511.49, so the leading threshold is γ≃0.0019551. A graph alone does not remove this parameter restriction.
The fast motion is especially informative. In fast time s=τ/ε,
dsdu​=ε(μ−u)−γf(u,v),dsdv​=f(u,v).
(53)
At leading order z=u+γv is constant, so fast paths are straight lines with slope dv/du=−1/γ. Along such a line, the fast eigenvalue at the nullcline is
fv​−γfu​=fv​(1+γg′(u)).
(54)
It is negative on the two outer attracting segments and positive on the intervening repelling segment. The tangencies defining μ±​ are consequently the fold points of a critical manifold when it is expressed in the coordinates (z,v). They are not generally the maximum and minimum of g itself.
For μ−​<μ<μ+​, slow motion on an attracting segment follows
(1+γg′(u))u˙=μ−u.​
(55)
On the left attracting segment u<μ−​ this moves towards increasing u; on the right attracting segment u>μ+​ it moves towards decreasing u. Each segment ends at a tangency and is followed by a fast jump along a line of slope −1/γ to the other attracting segment. This constructs the limiting relaxation oscillation. The figure uses γ=0.01, for which μ−​≃0.62970 and μ+​≃1.01157; the marked endpoints change with γ.
To justify existence of a periodic orbit of the full planar system, rather than just draw a formal cycle, first establish a compact trapping region. The positive quadrant is forward invariant: at u=0, u˙=μ+γδv/ε>0, and at v=0, v˙=u2/[ε(1+u2)]≥0. Moreover
v˙≤ε1−δv​.
(56)
Choose V>1/δ above the initial store value. Since f(u,v)≥−1−δV when 0≤v≤V,
u˙≤μ−u+εγ​(1+δV).
(57)
Thus the rectangle 0≤u≤U, 0≤v≤V is a positively invariant set if U>μ+γ(1+δV)/ε and the initial point lies inside it. Every forward solution is bounded. There are no equilibria on its axes for μ>0, and its sole interior equilibrium is a hyperbolic equilibrium point with both eigenvalues having positive real parts in the strict unstable range. A sufficiently small neighborhood of this repelling equilibrium has outward flow; deleting it leaves a compact trapping region with no equilibria. The Poincaré-Bendixson theorem then supplies a periodic orbit.
Hence periodic solutions exist throughout the strict unstable interval, interpreted with the finite-ε correction above. This argument establishes existence, without asserting uniqueness, the criticality of possible endpoint Hopf bifurcations, or absence of coexisting periodic orbits outside the interval.
