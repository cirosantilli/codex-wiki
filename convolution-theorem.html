Convolution theorem (f∗g​=f​g​)

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Analysis Fourier analysis Convolution
OurBigBook.com Words: 377 Articles: 4
For Lebesgue integrable functions f,g on R, use the Fourier transform convention f​(ξ)=∫R​f(x)e−2πixξdx. Then
f∗g​(ξ)=f​(ξ)g​(ξ).
(91)
Thus the integral combining all translated products becomes an ordinary multiplication at each frequency. Different Fourier transform normalizations can place a constant in this identity; fixing the convention prevents that ambiguity. Its use in filtering is illustrated by [ref].
For the indicator function q=1[0,1]​,
q​(ξ)=e−πiξsinc(ξ),sinc(ξ)=πξsin(πξ)​,
(92)
with sinc(0)=1. Therefore the triangular function q∗q has Fourier transform e−2πiξsinc2(ξ). The extra factor of the sinc function improves the large-frequency bound from a constant times 1/∣ξ∣ to one times 1/∣ξ∣2, matching the smoother output in the overlap picture. Fourier decay of repeated interval convolutions extends this calculation to further repetitions.
For a proof outline, Fubini's theorem permits exchanging the two integrals because the integral of ∣f(y)g(x−y)∣ over (x,y) is ∥f∥1​∥g∥1​<∞. Set u=x−y in the inner integral:
f∗g​(ξ)​=∫R​∫R​f(y)g(x−y)e−2πixξdydx=∫R​∫R​f(y)g(u)e−2πi(y+u)ξdudy=f​(ξ)g​(ξ).​
(93)
The factorization of the complex exponential is the mechanism behind the simplification. The discrete convolution version similarly turns coefficient sums into products of generating functions.
