Refractive index and wavelength (λ=λ0​/n)

For a linear stationary interface, phase matching at a planar wave interface preserves frequency: the incident and transmitted electric fields must oscillate together at each boundary point. Their phase velocities can differ, so their wavelengths differ. Writing ω=2πf, the definitions give
λ0​=fc​,λ=fvph​​=nλ0​​,k=λ2π​=nλ0​2π​.
(15)
This explains the compressed wavefront spacing in the figure without changing the time between crests at the interface. Moving interfaces or time-dependent material responses need not preserve frequency. [ref]
The notation n(λ0​) labels refractive index by vacuum wavelength; it does not mean the wavelength inside the material. Dependence on λ0​, equivalently on frequency, is optical dispersion. The field driving the material induces oscillating electric charges; their emitted electromagnetic fields combine with the applied field to change the resulting wave phase. This collective response motivates refractive index, rather than a picture of independently delayed little pieces of light. [ref]
To distinguish three speeds, track a wavefront for phase velocity, a narrow wave envelope for group velocity, and the earliest newly introduced disturbance for front velocity. For a transparent differentiable propagation branch,
vg​=(dωdk​)−1=n+ωdn/dωc​=ng​c​,ng​=n−λ0​dλ0​dn​.
(16)
The denominator is the group refractive index. It describes envelope transit when loss and reshaping are small. It need not equal n, and neither phase velocity nor a reshaped pulse peak generally measures signal arrival. The front velocity article explains the separate relativistic causality constraint and why a detector threshold is not an exact causal-front measurement.
