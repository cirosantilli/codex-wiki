Nondegenerate bilinear form (radL​B=radR​B={0})

 Home Mathematics Area of mathematics Algebra Linear algebra Bilinear form
OurBigBook.com Words: 1k Articles: 13
A bilinear form B is nondegenerate when its left radical and right radical are both zero: every nonzero vector can be detected by some pairing, whichever input slot it occupies. On a finite-dimensional vector space, either condition implies the other, and the following tests are equivalent:
B(v,w)=0 for every vV⟶V∗,w⟼B(−,w)detA​⟹w=0,is an isomorphism,=0.​
(28)
Here A is the matrix of a bilinear form, and V∗ is the dual space. The linear map w↦B(−,w) has matrix A in the chosen basis and its dual basis, so its kernel is the right radical. The map v↦B(v,−) has matrix AT. An invertible matrix has zero kernel on both sides, proving the finite-dimensional equivalence. In infinite dimensions, zero left radical and right radical imply these maps are injective, but do not generally make them surjective.[ref]
For the real symmetric bilinear form B((x,y),(s,t))=xs−yt, the determinant is −1. Its isotropic vector (1,1) has zero self-pairing but pairs to 1 with (1,0), so it is detected. The alternating bilinear form with matrix J has determinant 1 and detects every nonzero vector even though all self-pairings vanish. Neither zero self-pairings nor the absence of positive definiteness implies degeneracy.
Nondegeneracy is useful because it turns a linear functional into a unique vector. For A=diag(1,−1), the linear functional ℓ(s,t)=2s+3t is B((2,−3),(s,t)); solving for that representing vector amounts to solving an invertible system of linear equations. The analogous identification underlies bilinear orthogonal complements and the dual isomorphisms induced by a nondegenerate pairing.
