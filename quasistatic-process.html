Quasistatic process

 Home Physics Branches of physics Statistical physics Thermodynamics
OurBigBook.com Words: 899
A quasistatic process is an ideal limit in which a changing thermodynamic system passes through a sequence of states arbitrarily close to thermodynamic equilibrium. Its intermediate pressure, temperature and volume are therefore well defined and obey its equation of state. Driving must be slow compared with the system's own equilibration, rather than merely taking a long time on a clock. A reversible thermodynamic process additionally allows both system and surroundings to be restored without net change; friction can produce entropy even in a quasistatic process.[ref]
For a computed example, take a fixed amount of substance n of ideal gas in a sealed cylinder with a frictionless piston, in contact with a heat bath at absolute temperature T. Reduce the external load in infinitesimal steps so that the piston expands from V0​ to 2V0​. In the reversible limit, the gas pressure exceeds the external pressure by an infinitesimal amount during expansion, and the heat transfer occurs across an infinitesimal temperature difference. Exact mechanical equilibrium would give no driving force; the limiting imbalance supplies motion while preserving equilibrium intermediate states.[ref]
Along this reversible isothermal expansion, the ideal gas law gives
p(V)=VnRT​,nRT=1kJ,V0​=1L=10−3m3,p0​=V0​nRT​=1MPa.
(1)
Here R is the molar gas constant; specifying nRT fixes an energy scale, without choosing n and T separately. The initial and final equilibrium states are (V0​,p0​,T) and (2V0​,p0​/2,T). Count work delivered to the surroundings as positive. The reversible work is the shaded area beneath the pressure-volume curve:
Wout,rev​=∫V0​2V0​​p(V)dV=nRTln2≈0.693147kJ.
(2)
The integral of 1/V supplies the natural logarithm. Since internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on temperature, ΔU=0; the first law of thermodynamics, ΔU=Qin​−Wout​, gives Qin,rev​=0.693147kJ to the displayed precision. The heat bath replaces the energy delivered as work.[ref]
Now start from the same initial state, but abruptly lower the external pressure to the constant value pext​=p0​/2=0.5MPa. There is a finite initial pressure imbalance, so this is an irreversible expansion rather than a quasistatic process. Assume the piston begins and ends at rest, has no net change in stored energy, and has no external friction; relaxation occurs within the gas and its heat bath. After motion has settled and the heat bath has restored temperature T, mechanical equilibrium and the ideal gas law give Vf​=nRT/pext​=2V0​. The work delivered to the constant load is
Wout,load​=pext​(Vf​−V0​)=(0.5MPa)(1L)=0.5kJ,ΔU=0,Qin,load​=0.5kJ.
(3)
This work follows directly from load force times piston displacement, using dV=Adx for piston area A. Intermediate gas states need not have a single equilibrium pressure or temperature. In particular, neither p(V)=nRT/V at the bath temperature nor p(V)=pext​ describes the actual gas during this irreversible motion. The entropy accounting makes the difference precise. Because entropy is a state function, both expansions have the same gas entropy change, computed along the reversible isothermal expansion:
ΔSgas​=∫V0​2V0​​TδQrev​​=nRln2.
(4)
The thermal reservoir stays at T and loses heat Qin​=Wout​, so ΔSbath​=−Wout​/T. Assume the ideal load stores work without an entropy change. The combined entropy production is therefore
ΔStotal​=nRln2−TWout​​={0,nR(ln2−1/2)>0,​reversible expansion,constant external load.​
(5)
No numerical entropy unit is implied by nRT=1kJ alone: nR or T must also be specified. The lost work opportunity is TΔStotal​=0.193147kJ for this comparison. This calculation uses equilibrium endpoints and the reservoir's temperature, without assigning equilibrium intermediate states to the irreversible gas. [ref]
For another boundary choice, let the same ideal gas expand into an initially evacuated compartment of volume V0​ inside a rigid, insulated vessel of total volume 2V0​. Removing the partition causes free expansion: no load is moved and no heat crosses the outer boundary, so Wout​=Qin​=0 and ΔU=0. After equilibration the temperature is again T, the pressure is p0​/2, and the gas entropy has increased by nRln2. The outer vessel has fixed volume; the gas's accessible volume doubles. Restoring the initial gas state requires compression and removal of heat, changing the surroundings. Equal endpoints do not make free expansion reversible. [ref]
A slowly moving piston with dry friction supplies a different caution: its gas can remain arbitrarily close to thermodynamic equilibrium, yet rubbing still converts work into heat and produces entropy. If the frictional force has a nonzero slow-motion limit, its dissipated energy over a fixed travel does not vanish with speed. Reversing the motion reverses friction, so infinitesimally changing the external load cannot retrace the same operation and restore the surroundings. The heat capacity calculations additionally exclude friction and other dissipative work.[ref]
The usefulness of a quasistatic process is that it replaces detailed motion by a readable sequence of equilibrium states. An equation of state and an integral then turn a pressure-volume drawing into an energy budget. The beautiful distinction is that internal energy and entropy depend on the endpoints, while heat and work record how the change was performed. Reversible benchmarks expose how much work an irreversible device sacrifices, while entropy production explains why simply making it slower need not recover that opportunity.
