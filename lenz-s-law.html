Lenz's law

 Home Physics Branches of physics Electromagnetism Maxwell equations Faraday's law of induction
OurBigBook.com Words: 401 Articles: 2
Lenz's law expresses the minus sign in Faraday's law of induction: the induced electromotive force drives a passive resistive loop's electric current in the direction whose own magnetic field opposes the imposed change in magnetic flux. It opposes the change of flux, not invariably the external magnetic field. Choose an oriented surface and its positive boundary direction first, find the sign of dΦext​/dt, and then use the right-hand rule to translate the signed electric current into a physical direction. [ref].
In the fixed-loop example in Faraday's law of induction, out-of-page magnetic flux increases at 0.0060Wb/s. With counterclockwise positive, I=−0.0030A is clockwise, producing an in-page magnetic field through the loop. Reverse the ramp to B(t)=0.40T−(0.30T/s)t on the same one-second interval: now E=+0.0060V and I=+0.0030A. The counterclockwise electric current produces an out-of-page magnetic field, supporting the decreasing external magnetic flux. A steady out-of-page magnetic field with fixed loop geometry gives no induced electromotive force in either direction.
For the increasing-area example in motional electromotive force, clockwise electric current runs down the right-hand rod. Its magnetic force on a current-carrying wire points left, opposing the imposed motion to the right. Writing Imag​=Bℓv/R for the current magnitude, the power required to maintain constant speed is
Pdrive​=(Imag​ℓB)v=R(Bℓv)2​=Imag2​R=PJoule​.
(219)
Thus the sign agrees with conservation of energy: the external drive supplies the Joule heating. The magnetic forces do no work on individual charges; mechanical work by the drive supplies the electrical energy. This also explains the usefulness of electromagnetic braking of a sliding rod and the terminal speed of a falling conducting loop. [ref].
The sign of induced electromotive force alone does not determine an arbitrary circuit's instantaneous electric current. Retaining constant self-inductance L gives LdI/dt+RI=−dΦext​/dt, so a pre-existing electric current can persist while the induced drive opposes its change. The distinction matters in the lossless limit of magnetic loop braking. The resistive toy examples use L=0 as an approximation, not a general consequence of Lenz's law.
