Declination (δ)

 Home Physics Branches of physics Astrophysics Equatorial coordinate system
OurBigBook.com Words: 338
Declination δ is the signed angle from the reference celestial equator to a direction, measured along its hour circle: −90∘≤δ≤+90∘. Positive declination means celestial north, rather than necessarily north of the observer's zenith. A direction with δ=0 lies on the celestial equator; δ=+90∘ is the north reference celestial pole. Declination is an angle, not a height or a measure of Euclidean distance. [ref].
The connection to an observation follows from a dot product. In the elementary spherical-Earth, unrefracted model, an observer at geographical latitude ϕ has zenith direction z=(cosϕcosΘ,cosϕsinΘ,sinϕ) in the same reference axes. Since n⋅z=sina for altitude a, the cosine addition formula gives
sina=sinϕsinδ+cosϕcosδcosH.
(14)
At upper meridian transit, H=0, so aupper​=90∘−∣ϕ−δ∣. For the synthetic site ϕ=52∘ and synthetic direction δ=20∘, this yields aupper​=58∘. A direction with δ=70∘ instead reaches 72∘, north of the zenith; positive declination does not imply a crossing on the southern half of the celestial meridian. A negative computed upper altitude means the direction never rises above the geometric astronomical horizon at that site.
For an angular readout, define signed zenith distance zs​ on the upper celestial meridian, positive toward the south and negative toward the north. Then δ=ϕ−zs​. A synthetic southward readout zs​=32∘ at ϕ=52∘ recovers δ=20∘. An unsigned distance ∣zs​∣ alone would leave two possibilities, ϕ±∣zs​∣, when both are in range. A calibrated optical telescope must therefore record which side of the zenith it points toward. In this linear relation, a 1′ error in the assumed geographical latitude produces a 1′ error in inferred declination; averaging repeated readings cannot remove that shared offset. Near the astronomical horizon, uncorrected atmospheric refraction also invalidates the geometric readout.
The unit vector construction explains a subtle feature of sky maps. At fixed declination, changing right ascension traces a parallel whose radius on the unit sphere is cosδ. Its arc length is cosδ∣dα∣, with angles in radians; at δ=60∘, a 15∘ right ascension increment spans a 7.5∘ arc along that parallel. This arc is not generally the shortest great circle separation. At either celestial pole, all right ascension values represent the same direction: the coordinate singularity belongs to the coordinate grid. The unit vector remains regular there.
