Drude model

The Drude model explains finite electrical conductivity by balancing charge carrier acceleration in an electric field against loss of directed momentum through collisions. Its useful surprise is that continuous microscopic motion can produce a constant macroscopic current density: the applied electric field continually restores the directed momentum lost to the material. The simplest version has one mobile species, constant number density n, electric charge q, mass m>0, and momentum relaxation time τ>0, in a stationary, homogeneous, isotropic material. At zero magnetic field, its mean drift velocity and current density obey
mdtdvd​​=qE(t)−τmvd​​,J=nqvd​,τdtdJ​+J=σ0​E(t),σ0​=mnq2τ​.

For electrons, q=−e with positive elementary charge e, so σ0​=ne2τ/m. The electron drift velocity points against a constant electric field, whereas conventional current points along it. Random microscopic velocities can have zero mean without being zero individually; drift velocity is this population mean, rather than each particle's speed.[ref]
The same first-order linear differential equation predicts both Drude current relaxation after switching an electric field and the Drude frequency response to a sinusoidal electric field. Together they let a measured electrical conductivity be interpreted in terms of a density, a mass, and a time scale; a single constant-field measurement determines only their combination nq2τ/m.
The original 1900 paper Zur Elektronentheorie der Metalle by Paul Drude sought a common account of electrical transport and heat transfer using mobile charged particles and ideas from the kinetic theory of gases. Its introduction, especially printed pp. 568–569, explains that comparing different transport phenomena could constrain the populations of free and bound charged particles. The modern momentum relaxation time formulation above is a useful presentation of the model, not a verbatim translation of the original notation or its historical assumptions about mass.[ref]
