Nucleic acid double helix

 Home Chemistry Polymer Nucleic acid
OurBigBook.com Words: 368 Articles: 2
A nucleic acid double helix consists of two nucleic acid strands winding around a common axis. In the familiar DNA arrangement, sugar–phosphate backbones lie outside and paired nucleobases lie inside, stacked along the axis. The strands are antiparallel nucleic acid strands. The flattened base pairing ladder above displays partners and direction; winding that ladder into a helix adds spatial organization. RNA can also form double-helical paired regions, so the geometry is not exclusive to DNA. See Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: DNA structure.
The useful surprise is that a structure explains a copying principle. Complementary base pairs allow each DNA strand to specify the other's nucleic acid sequence; separate strands can each act as templates in DNA replication. This is a structural constraint, while the enzymes and stages of DNA replication supply the actual process. A nucleic acid sequence specifies letter order without, by itself, giving every detail of the three-dimensional helix.
On 25 April 1953, James Watson and Francis Crick published a model with two oppositely directed chains, outside phosphate groups, and specific base pairs. Their short paper explicitly connected this pairing to a possible copying mechanism, while describing the model as requiring further experimental tests. See the original [ref], with an accessible [ref].
Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling's paper in the same issue had a different role: it presented X-ray diffraction evidence from DNA fibres and its structural interpretation, including the helical character of the wetter B form and evidence for phosphate groups on the outside. They distinguished forms produced at different water contents and assessed compatibility with the Watson–Crick model. Read their original [ref]. The model and the experimental analysis are distinct contributions; a diffraction pattern is evidence constraining a molecular geometry, rather than a direct picture of letter-by-letter base pairing.
