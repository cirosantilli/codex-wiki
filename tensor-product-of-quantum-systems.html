Spin-boson model

Words: 167 Articles: 1

A spin-boson model couples a two-state quantum system to a collection of quantum harmonic oscillators. The oscillator modes provide environmental degrees of freedom that can exchange energy with the spin and change its reduced density matrix. One transverse-coupling convention is
H=2ℏΩ​σz​+∑j​ℏωj​(aj†​aj​+1/2)+σx​∑j​λj​(aj†​+aj​).
(1)
The choice of coupling axis matters: here the Pauli matrix σx​ flips the energy-basis spin states. Coupling only through σz​ has a different effect, since it commutes with the free spin Hamiltonian operator.

Golden-rule relaxation of a spin coupled to an oscillator bath (Γ↓↑​=Γ0​(nΩ​+1),Γ↑↓​=Γ0​nΩ​)

Words: 102
Spin-boson model OurBigBook.com
For transverse coupling with equal mode amplitudes λ, Fermi's golden rule and the oscillator creation operator and annihilation operator give
Γ↓↑​=Γ0​(nΩ​+1),Γ↑↓​=Γ0​nΩ​,Γ0​=ℏ22πλ2​g(Ω),
(2)
where the first index is the final spin and g is the density of states per unit angular frequency. Decay creates a quantum and carries the factor nΩ​+1; excitation annihilates one and carries nΩ​. For nonuniform resonant occupations, nΩ​ is their density-weighted mean.
With a thermal Bose-Einstein distribution, nΩ​/(nΩ​+1)=e−βℏΩ. The two-state master equation therefore has the Boltzmann distribution as its unique stationary populations when Γ0​>0. The population deviation relaxes at rate Γ0​(2nΩ​+1). If there is no resonant coupling, both rates vanish and stationarity does not determine the populations.

Relative-coordinate box spectrum of identical particles (Er,s​=8ma2π2ℏ2​(r2+s2))
Words: 140
Indistinguishable particles OurBigBook.com
For two equal-mass particles with orthonormal coordinates y±​=(x1​±x2​)/2​ confined to ∣y±​∣<a, the Hamiltonian is the sum of two width-2a infinite square well Hamiltonians. Exchange preserves y+​ and reverses y−​. The relative-coordinate box mode is even for odd index s and odd for even index s. Pure spatial bosonic exchange symmetry therefore allows odd s, while pure spatial fermionic exchange symmetry allows even s.
The bosonic ground mode is (r,s)=(1,1), while its first excited mode (2,1) is degenerate in energy with the fermionic ground mode (1,2). Angular spin degrees of freedom, if present, must be included in the full exchange symmetry rather than assigned the spatial rule automatically. The mean relative distance is ⟨∣y−​∣⟩=a(1/2−2/π2) in the lowest even mode and a/2 in the lowest odd mode, obtained by integrating the normalized squared cosine and sine. Multiplication by 2​ gives the particle separation.

The complete wavefunction of identical fermions changes sign on exchange of two particles, including their spatial and internal coordinates. The Pauli exclusion principle follows because an antisymmetrized product with two identical complete spin orbitals is zero. Antisymmetry concerns the total state; a spin-antisymmetric singlet state can have a symmetric spatial factor.
Fermi-Dirac statistics
Words: 227 Articles: 5
Fermion OurBigBook.com
The antisymmetric exchange statistics of identical fermions. Interchanging two one-particle labels reverses the total state, and fermionic creation operators anticommute. Occupation of a one-particle mode is therefore zero or one, giving the Pauli exclusion principle; thermal equilibrium leads to the Fermi-Dirac distribution. Color, flavor, spin and orbital factors must together have the required antisymmetry, even if one factor is symmetric.

Two-electron states in the first two shells of an isotropic oscillator
Words: 209 Articles: 1

The spatial ground orbital of a three-dimensional quantum harmonic oscillator has l=0 and energy 3ℏω0​/2; its first excited shell has l=1 and energy 5ℏω0​/2. Two identical electrons both in the ground orbital must form the singlet state, giving one state at total energy 3ℏω0​.
At energy 4ℏω0​, one electron occupies each shell. There are two ground-shell spin orbitals and six first-excited-shell spin orbitals, hence twelve Slater determinants. Equivalently, 0⊗1 gives L=1; symmetric spatial combinations with the singlet state give three states, while antisymmetric spatial combinations with the triplet states give nine. Distinct occupied orbitals allow both spatial exchange symmetries, so both spin sectors must be kept.

For the interaction λS⋅L built from the total two-electron spin and orbital angular momentum, set J=L+S. The spin-dot-product eigenvalue identity then gives the shift in the title. In the first excited two-electron oscillator manifold, the singlet has L=1,S=0,J=1 and zero shift with multiplicity three. The triplet has L=S=1, with J=0,1,2 shifts −2λℏ2,−λℏ2,+λℏ2 and multiplicities 1,3,5.
The multiplicities sum to twelve, and their weighted shift sums to zero. The specified interaction preserves both L2 and S2; it is distinct from summing separate single-electron spin-orbit interactions. Its shifts are exact within this model's invariant oscillator manifold, with weak coupling ensuring the original level ordering remains useful.

Contact shifts of two spin-one-half fermions in a harmonic trap

For two identical fermions of mass m in the same one-dimensional quantum harmonic oscillator, use center of mass X=(x1​+x2​)/2 and separation r=x1​−x2​. The corresponding oscillator masses are 2m and reduced mass m/2, with the same frequency ω. If N,n are their occupation numbers, the unperturbed energy is ℏω(N+n+1). Pauli exclusion principle
Words: 2k Articles: 19
Fermion OurBigBook.com
The Pauli exclusion principle says that each complete one-particle state can have fermionic occupation number zero or one. “Complete” includes spin and any other internal labels, as well as the spatial wavefunction. Thus two electrons can share a spatial orbital with opposite spin projections: these are two different spin orbitals. Equal energy alone does not mean equal quantum state, because degenerate energy levels contain several independent one-particle states.
This distinction explains how an electron shell can accommodate several electrons while exclusion still allows only one per complete one-particle state. It also makes a finite model of fermionic state counting surprisingly simple: two spatial orbitals with two spin choices each give four complete one-particle states, and exactly six two-electron basis states. The following diagram enumerates them; its boxes represent occupation numbers, not locations in space.
Pauli's Exclusion principle and quantum mechanics by Wolfgang Pauli is his retrospective account, delivered on 13 December 1946 for the 1945 Nobel Prize. On printed pp. 29–30, he describes proposing an extra two-valued electron property in autumn 1924 and formulating exclusion in 1925. The crucial clue linked counts of resolved energy eigenstates in alkali spectra under a magnetic field with the populations of closed electron shells. Resolving every label turned a complicated shell capacity into one electron per complete one-particle state. This historical route preceded the modern spin interpretation and the antisymmetric wavefunction formulation; it was not originally a deduction from a Slater determinant.

Spectroscopic origin of the Pauli exclusion principle
Words: 187
Pauli exclusion principle OurBigBook.com
In 1925, Wolfgang Pauli connected two puzzles: alkali metal doublets and their anomalous Zeeman effect, and the capacities of closed electron shells. His paper assigned the extra two-valued property to the optically active electron, rather than an angular momentum of the closed core. Its Paschen-Back effect classification used four quantum numbers; these historical labels should not be identified literally with modern n,ℓ,mℓ​,ms​. The exclusion rule forbade matching all four. This connected resolved one-electron states with closed-group populations. An additional test was the missing triplet counterpart of the equivalent two-s-electron ground state in alkaline earth metals. Pauli explicitly claimed counts of allowed states, not their energies or ordering.[ref]
The modern question is especially concrete: if a Quantum Hamiltonian has a lowest one-particle state, why cannot every electron occupy it? Minimizing energy answers which of the allowed quantum states is preferred; the Pauli exclusion principle determines which quantum states are allowed in the first place. Spectral lines then test energy differences between allowed multiparticle quantum states. A line count is not itself a direct count of occupied spin orbitals: transitions also depend on selection rules and the prepared quantum state populations. The observed anomalous Zeeman effect supplied constraints for the historical classification; the two-particle antisymmetrization below supplies the modern mathematical explanation of exclusion.
Counting spin orbitals in an electron shell (Nmax​(n)=2n2)
Words: 245
Pauli exclusion principle OurBigBook.com
Choose the one-particle states of a hydrogen atom, or a central-field description of a chemical atom, with labels
n=1,2,…,ℓ=0,1,…,n−1,mℓ​=−ℓ,…,ℓ,ms​=±21​.
(4)
The principal quantum number n identifies the electron shell. The orbital angular momentum label ℓ identifies the electron subshell; the magnetic quantum number mℓ​ labels 2ℓ+1 independent spatial orbitals. Each has two spin one-half along an axis choices, hence 2(2ℓ+1) spin orbitals. Applying the Pauli exclusion principle once per spin orbital gives
Nmax​(n)=∑ℓ=0n−1​2(2ℓ+1)=2(22n(n−1)​+n)=2n2.
(5)
These quantum numbers label wavefunctions and spin projections, not electron trajectories. They need not resolve exact energy eigenstates in the presence of spin-orbit interaction; changing to coupled angular momentum eigenstates preserves the count of independent one-particle states within this shell model.
For example, n=3 allows ℓ=0,1,2, conventionally 3s,3p,3d. Their spatial orbital counts are 1,3,5; their spin orbital counts are 2,6,10. The sum is eighteen. The algebra behind “one per complete state” is short: two-particle antisymmetrization gives u∧u=(u⊗u−u⊗u)/2​=0, and the zero vector cannot be a normalized quantum state. Opposite-spin electrons in the same spatial orbital have distinct complete states, so their antisymmetric product survives.
This supplies a structural link to the periodic table: finite electron subshell capacities make repeated patterns of occupied electron configurations possible. It does not predict the exact ground state electron configuration or energy of a many-electron chemical atom. Different electron subshells overlap in energy, and electron interactions change their ordering. In particular, the periodic table period lengths are not simply successive 2n2 values: the third periodic table period has eight chemical elements, although the full n=3 electron shell has eighteen available states. A capacity counts available spin orbitals; an actual filling order requires a Quantum Hamiltonian.

But attempting the same construction with v=u gives the zero vector:
A12​(∣u⟩⊗∣u⟩)=2∣u⟩⊗∣u⟩−∣u⟩⊗∣u⟩​=0.
(7)
The zero vector cannot be normalized to a quantum state. This is exclusion as cancellation of probability amplitudes, rather than a repulsive force. More generally, for normalized u,v the squared norm of the numerator divided by 2​ is 1−∣⟨u∣v⟩∣2; the expression has unit norm only when u,v are orthogonal. An overall quantum phase difference between u,v describes the same one-particle state and also gives zero. Pauli explicitly connects exclusion with a vanishing antisymmetric wavefunction.[ref]

A Slater determinant extends two-particle antisymmetrization to N fermions. Here xi​ contains position and spin or other internal variables. Distinct orthonormal one-particle states give the normalized wavefunction displayed in the title. Repeating a one-particle state repeats a column of the determinant, so the wavefunction vanishes. Exclusion versus particle position

Exclusion constrains complete one-particle states, not a collection of little spatial boxes. In particular, two opposite-spin electrons in the same spatial orbital have
Ψ(r1​,s1​;r2​,s2​)=ϕa​(r1​)ϕa​(r2​)2​χ↑​(s1​)χ↓​(s2​)−χ↓​(s1​)χ↑​(s2​)​.
(8)
The spatial factor is symmetric; the spin-one-half singlet state supplies the required antisymmetry. At r1​=r2​, this wavefunction need not vanish for opposite spin labels. For equal spin labels it does vanish. In a continuous position description, an exact coincident point has zero measure anyway; the useful comparison concerns joint probability densities or detection in finite regions. Equal positions do not imply equal complete quantum states.
The fermionic occupation numbers nα​=0,1 refer to a chosen orthonormal basis of one-particle states. A quantum superposition need not have definite occupation numbers in that basis, and an expectation value ⟨nα​⟩ can be fractional. For example, one electron in (u+v)/2​ has mean occupation number 1/2 in each of two orthogonal spin orbitals, without splitting into two particles. The canonical anticommutation relations give (aα†​)2=0 and make aα†​aα​ a projector, so its expectation value stays between zero and one in every normalized quantum state.
Zero-temperature filling of finite fermion levels (E0​(N)=∑α=1N​ϵα​)
Words: 687 Articles: 2
Pauli exclusion principle OurBigBook.com
For M complete energy eigenstates of a one-particle Quantum Hamiltonian for identical noninteracting fermions, order their energies ϵ1​≤⋯≤ϵM​. At fixed particle number 0≤N≤M, the Quantum Hamiltonian and the particle-number constraint are
H=∑α=1M​ϵα​nα​,∑α=1M​nα​=N,nα​∈{0,1}.
(9)
The ground state occupies the N lowest complete one-particle states. A short minimization argument suffices: moving an occupant from a higher energy to an empty lower energy decreases the total energy. This filling describes the ground state at temperature T=0; exclusion by itself also applies to excited quantum states and at positive temperature. The underlying thermal description is the Fermi-Dirac distribution.[ref]
If the last occupied energy belongs to a group of g one-particle states at the same energy and q of them must be occupied, with all lower states filled, the ground state subspace has dimension (qg​). Exclusion does not select a particular basis or a particular quantum superposition in that subspace. The factor of two for an electron spatial orbital assumes both spin projections are included; a model restricted to one spin projection has only one state per spatial orbital. A magnetic field can split the degenerate energy levels, and interactions can change the lowest-energy electron configuration. Thus this finite noninteracting filling rule is not a complete prescription for interacting chemical atoms.

Exclusion and the energy of two electrons in three spatial levels
Words: 372
Zero-temperature filling of finite fermion levels OurBigBook.com
Consider an original noninteracting model with three orthonormal spatial orbitals A,B,C at energies 0,Δ,3Δ, where Δ>0. Both spin projections are available at each energy, so there are six spin orbitals. The Quantum Hamiltonian is H=h⊗I+I⊗h, with no interaction between particles and no dependence on spin. Comparing its allowed ground states isolates the effect of fermionic exchange symmetry.
For two distinguishable spin-one-half particles, the spatial ground state is A⊗A and any of four independent two-spin states is possible: E0​=0 and ground-state degeneracy four. For two identical electrons, both can still occupy A, using A↑ and A↓, but there is just one allowed ground state: the symmetric spatial factor times the spin-one-half singlet state,
Ψ0​=A(r1​)A(r2​)2​∣↑↓⟩−∣↓↑⟩​,E0​=0.
(10)
Exclusion has reduced the ground-state degeneracy without raising the ground state energy.
Now restrict both particles to the same spin projection ↑. Distinguishable particles can still both occupy A↑, so E0​=0. Identical electrons must occupy different spatial orbitals; the lowest choice is A,B, with
Ψ↑↑​=2​A(r1​)B(r2​)−B(r1​)A(r2​)​∣↑↑⟩,E0​=Δ.
(11)
The spin factor is a spin-one-half triplet state, and the spatial factor is antisymmetric. These are ground states within the restricted parallel-spin sector, not the unrestricted two-electron ground state. Both restricted models have ground-state degeneracy one.
The first excited two-electron energy in the unrestricted model is Δ, with four independent quantum states: one occupant in A, one in B, and two spin choices for each. A basis adapted to particle exchange operators separates these into one spin-one-half singlet state with symmetric spatial factor and three spin-one-half triplet states with antisymmetric spatial factor. All four have equal energy in this model. Singlet versus triplet does not by itself determine an energy gap; an interaction or a spin-dependent term is needed to split them.
As a further count, the full two-electron space has dimension (26​)=15. Its energies in units of Δ and corresponding multiplicities are (0,1,2,3,4,6) and (1,4,1,4,4,1). For distinguishable spin-one-half particles the ordered tensor product instead has dimension 62=36, with multiplicities (4,8,4,8,8,4) at those same energies. These are exact model counts, not atomic measurements. The higher restricted ground state energy follows from removing forbidden quantum states; the Quantum Hamiltonian contains no additional “exclusion force”.
Fermionic atom antibunching

Pauli exclusion principle OurBigBook.com
The 2007 paper Comparison of the Hanbury Brown-Twiss effect for bosons and fermions by T. Jeltes and others compares polarized helium-3 fermions with helium-4 bosons. The chemical atoms occupy a metastable atomic level. Switching off magnetic trapping of neutral atoms releases them onto a microchannel plate detector with a delay-line anode, 63 cm below. Position and arrival-time readouts give three-dimensional pair separations by time-of-flight atom detection. About 1000 clouds per isotope supply histograms; normalization removes the broad cloud shape.

The same apparatus, matched temperatures near 0.5μK, nearly identical trapping potentials, negligible interactions, and magnetic field gradient checks of spin polarization support an exchange-statistics interpretation. Finite detector resolution suppresses contrast; trap switch-off and resolution uncertainties limit quantitative agreement. A diverging laser lens deepens the dip, but its aberrations preclude a precise lens-model test. This detects exchange correlations, rather than measuring occupation of one selected spin orbital.[ref]

Composite statistics of neutral atoms
Words: 107
Spin-statistics theorem OurBigBook.com
Exchanging two identical bound atoms exchanges their constituent fermions. An atom with mass number A and atomic number Z contains A nucleons and Z electrons, so its exchange sign is (−1)A+Z when its internal state is preserved. An odd count gives a composite fermion; an even count gives a composite boson. This is equivalently the integer-versus-half-integer total-spin distinction in the Spin-statistics theorem. For example, neutral lithium-6 has nine fermionic constituents and is a fermion, while neutral lithium-7 has ten and is a boson. Counting nucleons alone instead classifies the nucleus, not the neutral atom. This effective composite-particle description requires energies and separations where the bound atom remains intact.
