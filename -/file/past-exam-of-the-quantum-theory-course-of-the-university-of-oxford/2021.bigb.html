= Solution

For a <free particle>, the <Hamiltonian operator> is $H=P^2/(2m)$, where $P=-i\hbar\,d/dx$ is the <momentum operator>. The <time-independent Schrödinger equation> is therefore
$$
\boxed{-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2}=E\psi,\qquad \psi(x+2\pi)=\psi(x).}
$$
The full <stationary state> is $\Psi(x,t)=e^{-iEt/\hbar}\psi(x)$. The <periodic boundary condition> identifies $x$ and $x+2\pi$, giving a <particle on a circle> with <wavefunction normalization> over one period.

For a <momentum eigenstate> with <eigenvalue> $p$, the equation $-i\hbar\psi'=p\psi$ has solution $\psi(x)=Ce^{ipx/\hbar}$. The <periodic boundary condition> imposes $e^{2\pi ip/\hbar}=1$, so $p/\hbar\in\mathbb Z$. The <wavefunction normalization> gives $1=2\pi|C|^2$. Choosing a convenient <global phase> yields
$$
\boxed{\psi_n(x)=\frac{e^{inx}}{\sqrt{2\pi}},\qquad p_n=n\hbar,\qquad n\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
These <momentum eigenstates> form an <orthonormal basis>, the <Fourier basis> for the <particle on a circle>:
$$
\langle\psi_n,\psi_k\rangle=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}e^{i(k-n)x}\,dx=\delta_{nk}.
$$
Here $\delta_{nk}$ is the <Kronecker delta>.

Since $P\psi_n=n\hbar\psi_n$, applying the <Hamiltonian operator> gives
$$
H\psi_n=\frac{P^2}{2m}\psi_n=\frac{n^2\hbar^2}{2m}\psi_n.
$$
Thus the <energy eigenvalues> and corresponding full <stationary states> are
$$
\boxed{E_n=\frac{n^2\hbar^2}{2m},\qquad \Psi_n(x,t)=e^{-in^2\hbar t/(2m)}\psi_n(x).}
$$
The <ground state> is $\psi_0$ with <energy> zero and <quantum degeneracy> one. For each $n=1,2,\ldots$, the positive <energy level> $n^2\hbar^2/(2m)$ has <quantum degeneracy> two: its <energy eigenspace> is $\operatorname{span}\{\psi_n,\psi_{-n}\}$. This is the <free particle spectrum on a circle>.

The identity $\cos^2x=\tfrac12+\tfrac14e^{2ix}+\tfrac14e^{-2ix}$ gives the <momentum eigenstate> expansion
$$
\Psi(x,0)=A\sqrt{2\pi}\left(\frac12\psi_0+\frac14\psi_2+\frac14\psi_{-2}\right).
$$
Using the <orthonormal basis> from part (a), the <wavefunction normalization> is
$$
1=|A|^2(2\pi)\left(\frac14+\frac1{16}+\frac1{16}\right)=|A|^2\frac{3\pi}{4}.
$$
Hence $|A|=2/\sqrt{3\pi}$. The <wavefunction normalization> leaves a <global phase> free; the positive real choice used in the requested expansion gives
$$
\boxed{A=\frac2{\sqrt{3\pi}},\qquad \Psi(x,0)=\sqrt{\frac23}\left(\psi_0(x)+\frac12\psi_2(x)+\frac12\psi_{-2}(x)\right).}
$$
The two nonzero <eigenvalues> of the <momentum operator> have the same <energy eigenvalue> $E_\star=2\hbar^2/m$. Under <unitary time evolution>, their coefficients acquire the same <dynamical phase>. With $\Omega=E_\star/\hbar=2\hbar/m$,
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\Psi(x,t)&=\sqrt{\frac23}\left(\psi_0(x)+\frac{e^{-i\Omega t}}2\psi_2(x)+\frac{e^{-i\Omega t}}2\psi_{-2}(x)\right)\\
&=\frac{1+e^{-i\Omega t}\cos(2x)}{\sqrt{3\pi}}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
At $t=0$, $1+\cos(2x)=2\cos^2x$ recovers the initial <wavefunction>. Its <probability density>, useful below, is
$$
|\Psi(x,t)|^2=\frac{1+\cos^2(2x)+2\cos(2x)\cos(\Omega t)}{3\pi}.
$$

The <ground state> is the single <energy eigenstate> $\psi_0$. Its coefficient in the normalized <wavefunction> is $\sqrt{2/3}$, so the <Born rule> gives
$$
\boxed{\Pr(E=0)=\left|\sqrt{\frac23}\right|^2=\frac23.}
$$
This <probability> is independent of $t$ because <unitary time evolution> changes only the <dynamical phases> of the <energy eigenstates>.

The coefficient of the <momentum eigenstate> $\psi_2$ is $e^{-i\Omega t}/\sqrt6$. The <Born rule> therefore gives $\Pr(P=2\hbar)=1/6$. Conditional on this outcome, the <projective measurement> prepares the <post-measurement state> $e^{-i\Omega t}\psi_2$, equivalent to $\psi_2$ up to <global phase>.

Since $H\psi_2=(4\hbar^2/(2m))\psi_2$, the subsequent <energy> measurement has <conditional probability> one of giving the specified <energy eigenvalue>. The <probability> for the entire sequence is consequently
$$
\boxed{\Pr\left(P=2\hbar\ \text{then}\ E=\frac{4\hbar^2}{2m}\right)=\frac16\times1=\frac16.}
$$
Any intervening <unitary time evolution> under the same <Hamiltonian operator> preserves this <energy eigenstate>, up to <global phase>.

For a <position measurement>, the <Born rule> integrates the <probability density> over the chosen interval. The three terms from part (b) have integrals
$$
\int_0^{\pi/4}1\,dx=\frac\pi4,\qquad
\int_0^{\pi/4}\cos^2(2x)\,dx=\left[\frac x2+\frac{\sin4x}{8}\right]_0^{\pi/4}=\frac\pi8,
$$
and
$$
\int_0^{\pi/4}2\cos(2x)\cos(\Omega t)\,dx=\left[\sin(2x)\cos(\Omega t)\right]_0^{\pi/4}=\cos(\Omega t).
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\Pr\left(0\leq x\leq\frac\pi4\right)=\frac18+\frac1{3\pi}\cos\left(\frac{2\hbar t}{m}\right).}
$$
The oscillating term comes from <quantum interference> between the zero and positive <energy> components. The <probability> lies between $1/8-1/(3\pi)$ and $1/8+1/(3\pi)$, both within $[0,1]$; integrating the same <probability density> over the whole circle gives one.

The <parity operator> exchanges $\psi_n$ and $\psi_{-n}$. Their coefficients in the <wavefunction> are equal, so
$$
(R\Psi)(x,t)=\Psi(-x,t)=\frac{1+e^{-i\Omega t}\cos(-2x)}{\sqrt{3\pi}}=\Psi(x,t).
$$
Thus the <wavefunction> is a <parity operator> <eigenstate> with <eigenvalue> $+1$ at every time. The <orthogonal projection> onto the $-1$ <eigenspace> is $(I-R)/2$, which annihilates it. By the <Born rule>,
$$
\boxed{\Pr(R=-1)=0.}
$$

The <position operators> and <momentum operators> are <self-adjoint operators>. Using conjugate linearity of the <adjoint operator>, $(cA+dB)^*=\overline cA^*+\overline dB^*$, gives
$$
\boxed{a_j^*=\frac{m\omega X_j-iP_j}{\sqrt{2m\omega\hbar}}.}
$$
Write $D=\sqrt{2m\omega\hbar}$. Bilinearity and antisymmetry of the <commutator>, together with the <canonical commutation relations>, give $[P_j,X_k]=-i\hbar\delta_{jk}I$. Hence
$$
\begin{aligned}
D^2[a_j,a_k]&=im\omega[X_j,P_k]+im\omega[P_j,X_k]\\
&=-m\omega\hbar\delta_{jk}I+m\omega\hbar\delta_{jk}I=0,\\
D^2[a_j,a_k^*]&=-im\omega[X_j,P_k]+im\omega[P_j,X_k]\\
&=2m\omega\hbar\delta_{jk}I.
\end{aligned}
$$
The terms involving two <position operators> or two <momentum operators> vanish by the <canonical commutation relations>. Finally, reversing products under the <adjoint operator>, $(AB)^*=B^*A^*$, gives $[a_j,a_k]^*=-[a_j^*,a_k^*]$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{[a_j,a_k]=[a_j^*,a_k^*]=0,\qquad [a_j,a_k^*]=\delta_{jk}I.}
$$
Here $I$ is the <identity operator>. These are the relations for two independent pairs of <creation and annihilation operators>. The <operator algebra> in this question is understood on a common invariant <operator domain>, such as finite <linear combinations> of the <number states> constructed in part (c).

First apply the <commutator product rule> in the form $[AB,C]=A[B,C]+[A,C]B$:
$$
[N,a_j^*]=\sum_{k=1}^2\left(a_k^*[a_k,a_j^*]+[a_k^*,a_j^*]a_k\right)=a_j^*.
$$
Repeated use of the <commutator product rule> gives, for $n\geq1$,
$$
[N,(a_j^*)^n]=\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}(a_j^*)^r[N,a_j^*](a_j^*)^{n-1-r}
=n(a_j^*)^n.
$$
This is the <commutator with powers of an eigenoperator>. For $n=0$, the <commutator> with the <identity operator> is zero, agreeing with the formula.

Each $a_k^*a_k$ is unchanged by taking the <adjoint operator>, so $N^*=N$ in this algebra. Taking the <adjoint of a commutator> yields $[N,(a_j^*)^n]^*=[a_j^n,N]=na_j^n$. By antisymmetry,
$$
\boxed{[N,(a_j^*)^n]=n(a_j^*)^n,\qquad [N,a_j^n]=-na_j^n,\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots.}
$$
In particular, each <creation operator> raises the <number operator> <eigenvalue> by one, and each <annihilation operator> lowers it by one.

Inverting the definitions of the <creation and annihilation operators> gives
$$
X_j=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}(a_j+a_j^*),\qquad
P_j=-i\sqrt{\frac{m\omega\hbar}{2}}(a_j-a_j^*).
$$
Substituting in the <orbital angular momentum> $L$ gives
$$
L=-\frac{i\hbar}{2}\left[(a_1+a_1^*)(a_2-a_2^*)-(a_2+a_2^*)(a_1-a_1^*)\right].
$$
Operators belonging to distinct modes commute. Therefore the terms with two <annihilation operators> and those with two <creation operators> cancel, and $a_1^*a_2=a_2a_1^*$, $a_2^*a_1=a_1a_2^*$. The surviving terms give the <orbital angular momentum in oscillator ladder operators>:
$$
\boxed{L=i\hbar(a_1a_2^*-a_2a_1^*).}
$$

From part (a), $[N,a_j]=-a_j$ and $[N,a_j^*]=a_j^*$. The <commutator product rule> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
[N,a_1a_2^*]&=[N,a_1]a_2^*+a_1[N,a_2^*]=-a_1a_2^*+a_1a_2^*=0,\\
[N,a_2a_1^*]&=[N,a_2]a_1^*+a_2[N,a_1^*]=-a_2a_1^*+a_2a_1^*=0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{[N,L]=0.}
$$
Each term in $L$ transfers one excitation between the two modes, preserving the total <number operator> <eigenvalue>. This is an instance of the <oscillator bilinear commutator>; every <number operator> <eigenspace> is an <invariant subspace> of $L$.

The common <Fock vacuum> obeys $N\psi=\sum_j a_j^*(a_j\psi)=0$. Put $B=(a_1^*)^{n_1}(a_2^*)^{n_2}$. By the <commutator product rule> and part (a),
$$
[N,B]=n_1B+n_2B=(n_1+n_2)B.
$$
Moving $N$ through $B$ therefore gives
$$
NB\psi=BN\psi+[N,B]\psi=(n_1+n_2)B\psi.
$$
Dividing by $\sqrt{n_1!n_2!}$ proves
$$
\boxed{N\psi_{n_1,n_2}=(n_1+n_2)\psi_{n_1,n_2}.}
$$
The constructed <number state> is nonzero: the relation $a_j(a_j^*)^r\psi=r(a_j^*)^{r-1}\psi$ follows from $[a_j,a_j^*]=I$ and $a_j\psi=0$. Commuting different modes and successively moving each <creation operator> across the <inner product> yields
$$
\|B\psi\|^2=n_1!n_2!\,\|\psi\|^2=n_1!n_2!.
$$
Thus the given factorial denominator also gives unit <norm>, as expected for a <number state>.

Write $u=\psi_{1,0}=a_1^*\psi$ and $v=\psi_{0,1}=a_2^*\psi$. The <canonical commutation relations> for the <creation and annihilation operators> give
$$
a_1u=\psi,\qquad a_2u=0,\qquad a_1v=0,\qquad a_2v=\psi.
$$
For example, $a_1a_1^*\psi=(I+a_1^*a_1)\psi=\psi$. The expression for $L$ from part (b), with distinct modes reordered, then yields
$$
Lu=i\hbar v,\qquad Lv=-i\hbar u.
$$
Thus $\operatorname{span}\{u,v\}$ is an <invariant subspace>, and the <matrix> of $L$ in the ordered <orthonormal basis> $(u,v)$ is
$$
[L]_{(u,v)}=\hbar\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its <characteristic polynomial> is $\ell^2-\hbar^2$, giving the <eigenvalues> and normalized <eigenstates>
$$
\boxed{\ell_+=+\hbar,\qquad \chi_+=\frac{\psi_{1,0}+i\psi_{0,1}}{\sqrt2};\qquad
\ell_-=-\hbar,\qquad \chi_-=\frac{\psi_{1,0}-i\psi_{0,1}}{\sqrt2}.}
$$
Indeed, $L(u\pm iv)=\pm\hbar(u\pm iv)$ and $\|u\pm iv\|^2=2$ by the assumed <orthonormality>. Both <eigenstates> remain in the $N=1$ <eigenspace>.

The <angular momentum eigenstates> have $J^2$ <eigenvalue> $\hbar^2j(j+1)$, where the <angular momentum quantum number> is $j=0,\tfrac12,1,\tfrac32,\ldots$. For each $j$, the <magnetic quantum number> runs in unit steps from $-j$ to $j$. Here that <magnetic quantum number> is $+1/2$, so only half-integer $j$ are compatible:
$$
\boxed{\lambda=j(j+1),\qquad j=\frac12,\frac32,\frac52,\ldots,\qquad
\lambda\in\left\{\frac34,\frac{15}4,\frac{35}4,\ldots\right\}.}
$$
Integer $j$ cannot contain a $J_3$ <eigenvalue> $\hbar/2$. Applying the <angular momentum lowering operator> and using $[J_3,J_-]=-\hbar J_-$ gives
$$
J_3\varphi=J_3J_-\psi=(J_-J_3-\hbar J_-)\psi
=\boxed{-\frac\hbar2\varphi}.
$$
The lowered vector is nonzero, since the <adjoint operator> identity $J_-^*=J_+$ gives
$$
\|\varphi\|^2=\langle\psi,J_+J_-\psi\rangle
=\hbar^2\left(\lambda-\frac14+\frac12\right)
=\hbar^2\left(\lambda+\frac14\right)>0.
$$
Finally, $J_3$ is a <self-adjoint operator>, so its <eigenstates> for distinct <eigenvalues> are <orthogonal>. Explicitly,
$$
-\frac\hbar2\langle\varphi\mid\psi\rangle
=\langle J_3\varphi\mid\psi\rangle
=\langle\varphi\mid J_3\psi\rangle
=\frac\hbar2\langle\varphi\mid\psi\rangle,
$$
and hence $\boxed{\langle\varphi\mid\psi\rangle=0}$.

For $\lambda=3/4$, the <adjoint operator> relation $J_+^*=J_-$ and the given product identity imply
$$
\|J_+\psi\|^2=\langle\psi,J_-J_+\psi\rangle
=\hbar^2\left(\frac34-\frac14-\frac12\right)=0.
$$
A vector of zero <norm> is zero, so $J_+\psi=0$. Part (i) gives
$$
\boxed{\|\varphi\|^2=\hbar^2,\qquad \|\varphi\|=\hbar.}
$$
The <commutator> $[J^2,J_-]=0$ also gives $J^2\varphi=(3/4)\hbar^2\varphi$, while $J_3\varphi=-(\hbar/2)\varphi$. Applying the other product identity to this unnormalized vector,
$$
\|J_-\varphi\|^2=\langle\varphi,J_+J_-\varphi\rangle
=\hbar^2\left(\frac34-\frac14-\frac12\right)\|\varphi\|^2=0.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{J_+\psi=0,\qquad J_-\varphi=0.}
$$
These are the two <angular momentum ladder endpoints> of a <spin one-half> pair. In particular, $\psi_2=\varphi/\hbar$ has unit <norm>, and $J_+\psi_2=\hbar\psi_1$ because $J_+J_-\psi=\hbar^2\psi$.

In the ordered <orthonormal basis> $(\psi_1,\psi_2)$, the <Hamiltonian operator> has <matrix>
$$
H=\hbar\omega\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}=\hbar\omega\sigma_1,
$$
where $\sigma_1$ is a <Pauli matrix>. Its symmetric and antisymmetric <eigenvectors> give
$$
\boxed{E_+=+\hbar\omega,\qquad \chi_+=\frac{\psi_1+\psi_2}{\sqrt2};\qquad
E_-=-\hbar\omega,\qquad \chi_-=\frac{\psi_1-\psi_2}{\sqrt2}.}
$$
The <energy eigenstates> $\chi_\pm$ are normalized and <orthogonal>; each <energy eigenvalue> is nondegenerate. Their full <stationary states> are $e^{-i\omega t}\chi_+$ and $e^{i\omega t}\chi_-$. Since they form an <orthonormal basis>, the general solution to the <time-dependent Schrödinger equation> is
$$
\boxed{\Psi(t)=c_+e^{-i\omega t}\chi_++c_-e^{i\omega t}\chi_-,\qquad c_+,c_-\in\mathbb C.}
$$
For a normalized <quantum state>, $|c_+|^2+|c_-|^2=1$; the constants are determined by its initial <inner products> with $\chi_\pm$.

The initial <quantum state> is $\psi_1$, up to <global phase>, and $\psi_1=(\chi_++\chi_-)/\sqrt2$. Applying <unitary time evolution> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Psi(t)&=\frac1{\sqrt2}\left(e^{-i\omega t}\chi_++e^{i\omega t}\chi_-\right)\\
&=\cos(\omega t)\psi_1-i\sin(\omega t)\psi_2.
\end{aligned}
$$
Since $J_3\psi_1=(\hbar/2)\psi_1$ and $J_3\psi_2=-(\hbar/2)\psi_2$, the <Born rule> gives
$$
\boxed{\Pr\left(J_3(t)=+\frac\hbar2\right)=|\langle\psi_1,\Psi(t)\rangle|^2=\cos^2(\omega t).}
$$
The other outcome has <probability> $\sin^2(\omega t)$, verifying that the two <probabilities> sum to one. This is a <two-level quantum return probability>.

Continue with the initial <quantum state> $\psi_1$ from part (ii), and let $\Delta t=T/N$. Conditional on every preceding outcome being $+\hbar/2$, the <post-measurement state> after each ideal <projective measurement> is again $\psi_1$, up to <global phase>. Part (ii) therefore gives the same <conditional probability> $\cos^2(\omega\Delta t)$ for each successive outcome. Multiplying these <conditional probabilities> yields the <repeated projective-measurement survival probability>
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P_N(T)=\left[\cos^2\left(\frac{\omega T}{N}\right)\right]^N.}
$$
This multiplication uses the <quantum state> reset at each successful <projective measurement>; the outcomes need not be assumed statistically independent.

For fixed $T$, put $z=\omega T$. The <Taylor expansion> $\cos^2u=1-u^2+O(u^4)$ gives the <quadratic short-time quantum survival probability>. For sufficiently large $N$, taking logarithms gives
$$
\log\mathbb P_N(T)=N\log\left(1-\frac{z^2}{N^2}+O(N^{-4})\right)
=-\frac{z^2}{N}+O(N^{-3})\longrightarrow0.
$$
Here the remainder uses <Big O notation>. Exponentiating proves
$$
\boxed{\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb P_N(T)=1,\qquad
\mathbb P_N(T)=1-\frac{(\omega T)^2}{N}+O(N^{-2}).}
$$
This is the <quantum Zeno effect>: increasingly frequent ideal <projective measurements> inhibit the transition away from the initial <quantum state>. The departure <probability> in a short interval is quadratic in its duration, so $N$ intervals of duration $T/N$ produce a total leading departure <probability> proportional to $T^2/N$. Without intervening measurements, the return <probability> at time $T$ would be $\cos^2(\omega T)$. The result concerns obtaining $+\hbar/2$ at every measurement in the ideal limit of instantaneous measurements with <unitary time evolution> between them.
