= i

= Solution
{parent=i}

The <time-independent Schrödinger equation> is the <energy eigenvalue> equation for the <Hamiltonian operator>:
$$
\boxed{-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2}+V(x)\psi(x)=E\psi(x).}
$$
Here $\hbar$ is the reduced <Planck constant>. A corresponding stationary <wavefunction> evolves as $\Psi(x,t)=\psi(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar}$, which follows by substitution in the <time-dependent Schrödinger equation>. Its position <probability density> is time independent because the time factor has unit modulus. Physical <energy eigenstates> must also satisfy the system's <boundary conditions>; for a <bound state>, <wavefunction normalization> requires $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\psi(x)|^2\,dx=1$.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Define the reflected function $\phi(x)=\psi(-x)$. The <chain rule> gives $\phi'(x)=-\psi'(-x)$ and $\phi''(x)=\psi''(-x)$. Evaluating the <time-independent Schrödinger equation> for $\psi$ at $-x$ and using the <even function> identity $V(-x)=V(x)$ gives
$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\phi''(x)+V(x)\phi(x)=E\phi(x).
$$
Thus reflection preserves the <energy eigenvalue>. The same substitution $y=-x$ in the <norm> gives
$$
\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\phi(x)|^2\,dx=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\psi(-x)|^2\,dx=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\psi(y)|^2\,dy=1.}
$$
This is the action of the <parity operator> $R\psi(x)=\psi(-x)$, which is a <unitary operator>. For a reflection-symmetric physical problem it preserves the <Hamiltonian operator>'s <operator domain> as well as its differential expression.

= iii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

By <linearity> of the <time-independent Schrödinger equation>, the even and odd parts
$$
\psi_+(x)=\frac{\psi(x)+\psi(-x)}2,\qquad \psi_-(x)=\frac{\psi(x)-\psi(-x)}2
$$
each satisfy the same <energy eigenvalue> equation whenever they are nonzero. They obey $R\psi_+=\psi_+$ and $R\psi_-=-\psi_-$, so they have definite <parity>. At least one is nonzero, and $\psi=\psi_++\psi_-$. Each nonzero part can be normalized separately.

Therefore **a basis of stationary solutions can be chosen to have definite <parity>**, and solving the even and odd sectors loses no <energy eigenvalues>. This does not assert that every possible <quantum superposition> has definite <parity>; a superposition of the two sectors need not. For one-dimensional <bound states> in the usual regular setting, <nondegeneracy of one-dimensional bound states> further implies that each individual <energy eigenstate> has definite <parity>.

= Solution
{parent=i}

In either exterior region of the <finite square well>, the <time-independent Schrödinger equation> reduces to $\psi''=k^2\psi$, since $E<0$. Its general solution is a <linear combination> of $e^{kx}$ and $e^{-kx}$. On the left, $e^{-kx}$ grows as $x\to-\infty$ and cannot be a <square-integrable function>, so its coefficient must be zero. On the right, the excluded growing term is $e^{kx}$. Hence the surviving tails are $Ae^{kx}$ for $x\leq-a$ and $De^{-kx}$ for $x\geq a$.

Inside the well, $E+V_0>0$ and the equation becomes $\psi''=-\kappa^2\psi$, whose general solution is $B\cos\kappa x+C\sin\kappa x$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\psi(x)=\begin{cases}Ae^{kx},&x\leq-a,\\B\cos\kappa x+C\sin\kappa x,&-a<x<a,\\De^{-kx},&x\geq a.\end{cases}}
$$
The coefficients are initially independent; <wavefunction matching at a finite potential step> imposes the remaining relations. Here the paper's $k$ is the exterior decay constant and $\kappa$ is the interior oscillation constant.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For an <even function>, the interior sine term must vanish, because <sine> is odd and <cosine> is even. Comparing reflected exterior tails at any $x>a$ gives $De^{-kx}=Ae^{-kx}$. Hence the <parity> condition alone is
$$
\boxed{C=0,\qquad A=D.}
$$
It does not yet impose the matching condition on $B$. Continuity at $x=a$, when imposed in the next part, additionally gives $A=D=Be^{ka}\cos(\kappa a)$.

= iii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

At a finite jump in <potential energy>, the <wavefunction> and its first <derivative> must be continuous. A jump in the derivative would create a <Dirac delta function> in $\psi''$, for which the finite potential has no balancing term. For the even solution, matching at $x=a$ gives
$$
B\cos\beta=De^{-\alpha},\qquad -B\kappa\sin\beta=-kDe^{-\alpha},\qquad \alpha=ka,\quad\beta=\kappa a.
$$
A nonzero solution cannot have $B=0$. It also cannot have $\cos\beta=0$: the first condition would give $D=0$, while the second would force $B\kappa\sin\beta=0$, a contradiction. Division is therefore valid and yields
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\beta\tan\beta.}
$$
<Parity> makes the conditions at $x=-a$ identical to those already imposed. Finally the definitions of the decay and oscillation constants give
$$
\boxed{\alpha^2+\beta^2=a^2\left(-\frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}+\frac{2m(E+V_0)}{\hbar^2}\right)=\frac{2mV_0a^2}{\hbar^2}.}
$$
The circle relation fixes the well strength; the tangent relation selects its even <bound states>.

= iv
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Write $\rho=a\sqrt{2mV_0}/\hbar>0$. On the first tangent branch, $0<\beta<\pi/2$, the matching relations are equivalent to
$$
\rho^2=\beta^2(1+\tan^2\beta),\qquad \rho=\frac\beta{\cos\beta}.
$$
The function $g(\beta)=\beta/\cos\beta$ is continuous and strictly increasing there:
$$
g'(\beta)=\sec\beta\left(1+\beta\tan\beta\right)>0,\qquad \lim_{\beta\to0^+}g(\beta)=0,\qquad \lim_{\beta\to(\pi/2)^-}g(\beta)=\infty.
$$
The <intermediate value theorem> therefore gives a unique first-branch root for **every $\rho>0$**. At this root $\beta<\rho$ and $\alpha=\beta\tan\beta>0$, so the <energy eigenvalue>
$$
E=-\frac{\hbar^2\alpha^2}{2ma^2}
$$
obeys $-V_0<E<0$. This proves the <even bound state in every attractive square well> result.

\Image[2015-finite-well.png]
{title=Even-state matching in a <finite square well>: the rising tangent branch meets the decaying circle for shallow and deeper wells}

To verify all the requested properties, take the resulting even <wavefunction> with exterior tails written in terms of their boundary values:
$$
\psi(x)=B\begin{cases}\cos(\kappa x),&|x|<a,\\\cos\beta\,e^{-k(|x|-a)},&|x|\geq a.\end{cases}
$$
The matching equation makes both the values and first <derivatives> agree at the endpoints. The exterior tails are <square-integrable> because $k>0$, and the interior interval is finite. More explicitly,
$$
\|\psi\|^2=|B|^2\left[a+\frac{\sin(2\beta)}{2\kappa}+\frac{\cos^2\beta}{k}\right],
$$
so choosing $B$ as the positive inverse square root of the bracket gives <wavefunction normalization>. Thus **an even, continuously differentiable, normalizable stationary solution always exists**, however small the positive depth.

= Solution
{parent=a}

The <position operator> $X$ and <momentum operator> $P$ are <self-adjoint operators>, so the <adjoint operator> is
$$
\boxed{a_+=P+im\omega X.}
$$
Use the <canonical commutation relation> with the order of factors preserved:
$$
\begin{aligned}
a_+a_-&=(P+im\omega X)(P-im\omega X)\\
&=P^2+m^2\omega^2X^2+im\omega(XP-PX)\\
&=P^2+m^2\omega^2X^2-m\hbar\omega
=\boxed{2m\left(H-\frac12\hbar\omega\right)},\\
a_-a_+&=P^2+m^2\omega^2X^2-im\omega(XP-PX)\\
&=P^2+m^2\omega^2X^2+m\hbar\omega
=\boxed{2m\left(H+\frac12\hbar\omega\right)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Subtracting the first product from the second proves
$$
\boxed{[a_-,a_+]=2m\hbar\omega I.}
$$
These <creation and annihilation operators> are unscaled and carry the paper's phase convention; their <commutator> is not the unit-normalized oscillator commutator.

= Solution
{parent=i}

From $H=a_+a_-/(2m)+\hbar\omega/2$ and the <commutator product rule>,
$$
[H,a_+]=\frac1{2m}a_+[a_-,a_+]=\hbar\omega a_+,\qquad [H,a_-]=\frac1{2m}[a_+,a_-]a_-=-\hbar\omega a_-.
$$
Consequently, if $H\psi=E\psi$,
$$
\boxed{H(a_\pm\psi)=(E\pm\hbar\omega)(a_\pm\psi).}
$$
Thus the <creation operator> raises the <energy eigenvalue> by $\hbar\omega$, and the <annihilation operator> lowers it by $\hbar\omega$, **provided the resulting vector is nonzero**. The zero vector is not an <eigenstate>; in particular the <ground state> will be annihilated by $a_-$. The displayed operator identity holds even in that case.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The squared <norm> is obtained by moving the <adjoint operator> to the other side of the <inner product>:
$$
\|a_-\psi\|^2=\langle\psi,a_+a_-\psi\rangle
=2m\left\langle\psi,\left(H-\frac12\hbar\omega\right)\psi\right\rangle.
$$
For an <energy eigenstate>, this is
$$
\boxed{\|a_-\psi\|^2=2m\left(E-\frac12\hbar\omega\right)\|\psi\|^2.}
$$
Since a squared <norm> is nonnegative, every nonzero <energy eigenstate> has $E\geq\hbar\omega/2$. Moreover $a_-\psi=0$ exactly when $E=\hbar\omega/2$.

= iii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Write $E_0=\hbar\omega/2$. Repeated application of the <annihilation operator> lowers the <energy eigenvalue> in steps of $\hbar\omega$. The lower bound from part (ii) forces this process to stop. To see why it also quantizes the starting energy, write
$$
\frac{E-E_0}{\hbar\omega}=n+\delta,\qquad n\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\},\quad 0\leq\delta<1.
$$
The first $n$ lowering steps have positive squared <norms>. If $\delta>0$, the next step is also nonzero by part (ii), and its energy would be $E_0+(\delta-1)\hbar\omega<E_0$, contradicting the bound. Thus $\delta=0$, and
$$
\boxed{E_n=\left(n+\frac12\right)\hbar\omega,\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots.}
$$
Existence of the <ground state> can also be checked directly in the position representation. The equation $a_-\psi_0=0$ is
$$
-i\hbar\psi_0'-im\omega x\psi_0=0,
$$
whose solution is proportional to $e^{-m\omega x^2/(2\hbar)}$. This <Gaussian function> is <square-integrable>, so it gives an actual <ground state>. Its normalization can be chosen using the <Gaussian integral>.

All the displayed levels occur: for a nonzero state at $E_n$,
$$
\|a_+\psi_n\|^2=\langle\psi_n,a_-a_+\psi_n\rangle=2m\hbar\omega(n+1)\|\psi_n\|^2>0.
$$
Thus repeated raising creates a nonzero <energy eigenstate> at every $E_n$.

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Let $\psi$ have energy $E_n$ and put $\gamma=2m\hbar\omega$. The lowering <norm> formula gives, inductively,
$$
\|(a_-)^j\psi\|^2=\gamma^j\frac{n!}{(n-j)!}\|\psi\|^2,\qquad 0\leq j\leq n.
$$
In particular $(a_-)^n\psi$ is a nonzero <ground state>. By the assumed nondegeneracy, it equals $c\psi_0$ for some $c\ne0$.

It remains to show that raising this vector recovers $\psi$, rather than merely another state at the same energy. On an <energy eigenstate> at $E_n$, factorization gives $a_+a_-\psi=\gamma n\psi$. Iterating the factorization down the ladder yields
$$
(a_+)^n(a_-)^n\psi=\gamma^n n!\,\psi.
$$
Applying $(a_+)^n$ to $(a_-)^n\psi=c\psi_0$ therefore proves
$$
\boxed{\psi=\frac{c}{(2m\hbar\omega)^n n!}(a_+)^n\psi_0.}
$$
For $n=0$ this is the original ground-state assumption. This proves <nondegeneracy propagated by an oscillator ladder>: every energy <eigenspace> is one-dimensional if the <ground state> is nondegenerate.

= Solution
{parent=i}

If $a_-\psi\ne0$, it has energy $E-\hbar\omega$, distinct from the energy of $\psi$. <Eigenstates> of a <self-adjoint operator> with distinct <eigenvalues> are <orthogonal>. Explicitly,
$$
E\langle\psi,a_-\psi\rangle=\langle H\psi,a_-\psi\rangle=\langle\psi,H(a_-\psi)\rangle=(E-\hbar\omega)\langle\psi,a_-\psi\rangle,
$$
so $\langle\psi,a_-\psi\rangle=0$. If $a_-\psi=0$, the same conclusion is immediate.

Dividing by $\|\psi\|^2$ and using the definition of $a_-$ gives
$$
\mathbb E_\psi(P)-im\omega\mathbb E_\psi(X)=0.
$$
Both <expectation values> are real because $P$ and $X$ are <self-adjoint operators>. Its real and imaginary parts therefore vanish separately:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E_\psi(X)=0,\qquad \mathbb E_\psi(P)=0.}
$$
An oscillator <energy eigenstate> is centered in both position and momentum, despite having nonzero <quantum uncertainties>.

= ii
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Twice applying the <creation operator> or twice applying the <annihilation operator> changes the <energy eigenvalue> by $2\hbar\omega$, unless they give zero. The same <orthogonality> argument as in part (i) therefore gives
$$
\mathbb E_\psi(a_+^2)=0=\mathbb E_\psi(a_-^2).
$$
Also the factorization in part (a) gives
$$
\mathbb E_\psi(a_+a_-)=2m(E-\hbar\omega/2),\qquad \mathbb E_\psi(a_-a_+)=2m(E+\hbar\omega/2).
$$
Now $P=(a_++a_-)/2$ and $X=(a_+-a_-)/(2im\omega)$. Squaring in the correct operator order yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_\psi(P^2)&=\frac14\mathbb E_\psi(a_+^2+a_+a_-+a_-a_++a_-^2)=mE,\\
\mathbb E_\psi(X^2)&=-\frac1{4m^2\omega^2}\mathbb E_\psi(a_+^2-a_+a_--a_-a_++a_-^2)=\frac{E}{m\omega^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
By part (i), the means are zero, so these are the <variances>. Taking positive square roots gives the requested <standard deviations>:
$$
\boxed{\Delta_\psi(X)=\sqrt{\frac{E}{m\omega^2}},\qquad \Delta_\psi(P)=\sqrt{mE}.}
$$
These <harmonic-oscillator number-state second moments> also show equal mean <kinetic energy> and <potential energy>, each $E/2$. Their uncertainty product is $E/\omega=(n+\tfrac12)\hbar$, so the <ground state> saturates the <Heisenberg uncertainty relation>.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Introduce the reduced <radial wavefunction> $u(r)=r\psi(r)$. Multiplying the <radial Schrödinger equation for a central potential> by $-2mr/\hbar^2$ gives
$$
u''-\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^2}u+\frac{2mKZ}{\hbar^2r}u+\frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}u=0.
$$
For the decaying <bound state>, take $\alpha>0$ and put $u=f e^{-\alpha r}$. Differentiation yields
$$
u''=e^{-\alpha r}\left(f''-2\alpha f'+\alpha^2f\right).
$$
Since $2mE/\hbar^2=-\alpha^2$, the two constant multiples of $f$ cancel. Dividing by the nonzero exponential gives
$$
\boxed{f''-2\alpha f'-\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^2}f+\frac{2mKZ}{\hbar^2r}f=0.}
$$
This separates the large-radius exponential decay from the remaining <Coulomb bound state> equation.

= ii
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Apply the <Frobenius method> at the origin. The lowest power in $f''-\ell(\ell+1)f/r^2$ is $r^{c-2}$, with coefficient
$$
\bigl[c(c-1)-\ell(\ell+1)\bigr]a_0.
$$
The terms $-2\alpha f'$ and $2mKZf/(\hbar^2r)$ start at power $r^{c-1}$ and therefore cannot cancel a nonzero $r^{c-2}$ coefficient. Because $a_0\ne0$, the <indicial equation> is
$$
\boxed{c(c-1)-\ell(\ell+1)=0,\qquad (c-\ell-1)(c+\ell)=0.}
$$
Its roots are $c=\ell+1$ and $c=-\ell$. Since $\psi=r^{-1}f e^{-\alpha r}$, their leading behaviors are respectively $\psi\sim r^\ell$ and $\psi\sim r^{-\ell-1}$. The physical choice is
$$
\boxed{c=\ell+1.}
$$
This is the <Coulomb radial origin condition>. For $\ell\geq1$, the other branch is not even locally normalizable: its radial <probability> integral behaves as $\int_0^\varepsilon r^2r^{-2\ell-2}\,dr=\int_0^\varepsilon r^{-2\ell}\,dr$, which diverges.

For $\ell=0$, the singular behavior $1/r$ is locally <square-integrable> with the measure $r^2dr$, so local <wavefunction normalization> alone would not reject it. The ordinary three-dimensional <Coulomb potential> problem additionally requires the regular <boundary condition> $u(0)=0$. A $1/r$ branch violates that condition and has divergent <kinetic energy> integral $\int_0^\varepsilon r^2|\partial_r(1/r)|^2\,dr=\int_0^\varepsilon r^{-2}\,dr$. Thus it is excluded from the physical energy domain as well. The regular choice gives $u\sim r^{\ell+1}$ in every case.

= iii
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For brevity write $\gamma=mKZ/\hbar^2$. With the physical <indicial root> $c=\ell+1$, substitute $f=\sum_{n\geq0}a_nr^{n+\ell+1}$ into the equation from part (i). The coefficient of $r^{n+\ell}$ is
$$
\bigl[(n+\ell+2)(n+\ell+1)-\ell(\ell+1)\bigr]a_{n+1}+
\bigl[2\gamma-2\alpha(n+\ell+1)\bigr]a_n=0.
$$
The <indicial equation> has already canceled the still lower power $r^{\ell-1}$. Rearranging gives the <recurrence relation>
$$
\boxed{a_{n+1}=2a_n\frac{\alpha(n+\ell+1)-mKZ/\hbar^2}{(n+\ell+2)(n+\ell+1)-\ell(\ell+1)}.}
$$
Its denominator is $(n+1)(n+2\ell+2)>0$ for every $n\geq0$.

To impose <wavefunction normalization> at infinity, first suppose that the series never terminates. Then
$$
\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\sim\frac{2\alpha}{n},\qquad n\to\infty.
$$
This is the exponential-series growth associated with $e^{2\alpha r}$, which would overwhelm the factor $e^{-\alpha r}$ in $\psi$. A direct lower-bound argument makes the conclusion precise. All sufficiently late recurrence ratios are real and positive, so after a common <global phase> choice the tail coefficients have one sign. For any $0<\eta<\alpha$, there is an index $N$ such that
$$
\frac{|a_{n+1}|}{|a_n|}\geq\frac{2\alpha-\eta}{n+1},\qquad n\geq N.
$$
Induction then bounds the tail below by a positive multiple of
$$
r^{\ell+1}\sum_{n\geq N}\frac{[(2\alpha-\eta)r]^n}{n!}.
$$
The finitely many earlier terms form a <polynomial> and cannot cancel this exponential growth. Thus $|\psi|=r^{-1}e^{-\alpha r}|f|$ grows at least as a positive multiple of $r^\ell e^{(\alpha-\eta)r}$ for large $r$, contradicting <wavefunction normalization>. This proves that the regular series must terminate.

Since the denominator never vanishes, termination requires the numerator to vanish at some nonnegative integer $n$:
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac{mKZ}{(n+\ell+1)\hbar^2}.}
$$
Then $a_{n+1}=0$ and every later coefficient is zero. Consequently **$f$ is a polynomial of degree $n+\ell+1$**, with a factor $r^{\ell+1}$; equivalently,
$$
f(r)=r^{\ell+1}P_n(r),\qquad \psi(r)=r^\ell P_n(r)e^{-\alpha r},
$$
where $P_n$ has degree $n$. This is <series-termination quantization of the Coulomb radial equation>. If $N_{\rm p}=n+\ell+1$ denotes the <principal quantum number>, the associated <energy eigenvalues> are
$$
\boxed{E_{N_{\rm p}}=-\frac{mK^2Z^2}{2\hbar^2N_{\rm p}^2},\qquad N_{\rm p}=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
Here $n$ counts the degree of the remaining radial polynomial; it is not the principal <quantum number> itself.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

The smallest <energy eigenvalue> in part (iii) occurs at the smallest possible <principal quantum number>, $N_{\rm p}=1$. Since $n,\ell\geq0$, this forces $n=0$ and $\ell=0$. Therefore $f=a_0r$, and
$$
\boxed{\psi_0(r)=a_0e^{-\alpha_0r},\qquad \alpha_0=\frac{mKZ}{\hbar^2},\qquad E_0=-\frac{mK^2Z^2}{2\hbar^2}.}
$$
For the spherically symmetric <ground state>, the full three-dimensional <wavefunction normalization> uses $d^3x=r^2dr\,d\Omega$. Integrating over the <solid angle> gives
$$
1=4\pi|a_0|^2\int_0^\infty r^2e^{-2\alpha_0r}\,dr.
$$
Put $R=2\alpha_0r$ and use the supplied <factorial> integral:
$$
\int_0^\infty r^2e^{-2\alpha_0r}\,dr=\frac1{(2\alpha_0)^3}\int_0^\infty R^2e^{-R}\,dR=\frac{2!}{8\alpha_0^3}=\frac1{4\alpha_0^3}.
$$
Thus $|a_0|^2=\alpha_0^3/\pi$. Choosing the positive <global phase> convention gives
$$
\boxed{a_0=\left(\frac{\alpha_0^3}{\pi}\right)^{1/2}.}
$$
This is the <radial normalization of the hydrogen ground state> convention in which the angular factor is absorbed into the spherically symmetric function. If instead one writes $\Psi=R(r)Y_{00}$ with the normalized <spherical harmonic> $Y_{00}=1/\sqrt{4\pi}$, then $R(r)=2\alpha_0^{3/2}e^{-\alpha_0r}$. Both conventions describe the same normalized three-dimensional <quantum state>.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=v}

The <radial probability density> includes the spherical-shell volume factor:
$$
p(r)=4\pi r^2|\psi_0(r)|^2=4\alpha_0^3r^2e^{-2\alpha_0r},\qquad r\geq0.
$$
Consequently the radial <expectation value> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_{\psi_0}(r)&=4\alpha_0^3\int_0^\infty r^3e^{-2\alpha_0r}\,dr\\
&=4\alpha_0^3\frac{3!}{(2\alpha_0)^4}=\boxed{\frac3{2\alpha_0}=\frac{3\hbar^2}{2mKZ}.}
\end{aligned}
$$
Keeping $m$ and $K$ fixed, the <hydrogenic ground-state radius scaling> is therefore **$\langle r\rangle\propto1/Z$**: the $Z=2$ state has half the mean radius of the $Z=1$ state. Equivalently, with $a_*=\hbar^2/(mK)$, the mean radii are $3a_*/2$ and $3a_*/4$. For the physical electron-nucleus <Coulomb potential>, $a_*$ is the <Bohr radius> of the fixed-nucleus <hydrogen atom> model.

The exact $Z=2$ comparison in this one-electron model describes the <hydrogen-like ion> $\mathrm{He}^+$. A neutral <helium atom> has two <electrons>; its exact size also depends on their mutual <Coulomb interaction> and is not determined by this one-electron calculation. The stated half-radius conclusion is the prediction of the model supplied in the paper.
