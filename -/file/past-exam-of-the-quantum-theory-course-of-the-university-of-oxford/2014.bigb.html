= Solution
{parent=a}

The <time-dependent Schrödinger equation>, with the <Hamiltonian> for a particle of <mass> $m$ in a real <potential energy> $V$, is
$$
i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}=H\Psi,\qquad H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}+V(x).
$$
A <stationary state> is an <energy eigenstate>: its <wavefunction> evolves by a single <global phase>,
$$
\Psi(x,t)=\psi(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar},\qquad \boxed{-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi''+V\psi=E\psi.}
$$
Its <probability density> is independent of time. For a normalized <wavefunction>, the <probability density> and <probability current> are
$$
\boxed{\rho=\Psi^*\Psi,\qquad j=\frac{\hbar}{2mi}(\Psi^*\partial_x\Psi-\Psi\partial_x\Psi^*)=\frac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}(\Psi^*\partial_x\Psi).}
$$
Subtracting the <complex conjugate> of the <time-dependent Schrödinger equation>, multiplied by $\Psi$, from the original equation multiplied by $\Psi^*$ cancels the real <potential energy> terms and gives the <continuity equation> $\partial_t\rho+\partial_xj=0$. Thus $j$ measures signed probability flow in the positive $x$ direction.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Inside the <infinite square well>, the <time-independent Schrödinger equation> and <Dirichlet boundary conditions> are
$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi''=E\psi\quad(0<x<a),\qquad \psi(0)=\psi(a)=0,
$$
and the <wavefunction> vanishes outside the interval. For a nonzero state, <integration by parts> gives
$$
E\int_0^a|\psi|^2\,dx=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int_0^a|\psi'|^2\,dx>0.
$$
The strict inequality follows because a function with zero derivative and zero endpoint values is zero. Put $k=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$. The general solution is $A\sin kx+B\cos kx$; the first <boundary condition> gives $B=0$, and the second gives $ka=n\pi$, $n=1,2,\ldots$. Since $\int_0^a\sin^2(n\pi x/a)\,dx=a/2$, the normalized <energy eigenstates> and <eigenvalues> are
$$
\boxed{\psi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac2a}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a},\qquad E_n=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2},\quad n\geq1.}
$$
The normalization fixes the magnitude of the coefficient; its arbitrary <global phase> has been chosen real and positive.

= c
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The initial <quantum state> is the <equal superposition of the first two box states>, $(\psi_1+\psi_2)/\sqrt2$. The <orthonormality> of the two <energy eigenstates> makes its squared <norm> equal to one. Their separate <stationary state> phases give
$$
\boxed{\Psi(x,t)=\frac1{\sqrt a}\left[\sin\frac{\pi x}{a}e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}+\sin\frac{2\pi x}{a}e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}\right].}
$$
Write $\Omega=(E_2-E_1)/\hbar=3\pi^2\hbar/(2ma^2)$. The <probability density> is
$$
\rho(x,t)=\frac1a\left[\sin^2\frac{\pi x}a+\sin^2\frac{2\pi x}a+2\sin\frac{\pi x}a\sin\frac{2\pi x}a\cos\Omega t\right].
$$
Each squared sine integrates to $a/4$ on the left half. For the <quantum interference> term, the product-to-sum identity gives
$$
\int_0^{a/2}\sin\frac{\pi x}a\sin\frac{2\pi x}a\,dx=\frac12\int_0^{a/2}\left(\cos\frac{\pi x}a-\cos\frac{3\pi x}a\right)dx=\frac{2a}{3\pi}.
$$
Hence the <Born rule> gives
$$
\boxed{P_{\mathrm L}(t)=\int_0^{a/2}\rho(x,t)\,dx=\frac12+\frac4{3\pi}\cos\Omega t.}
$$
The <quantum interference> moves probability between the halves even though each component separately is a <stationary state>.

= d
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

At the midpoint the second sine vanishes, whereas the derivative of the first sine vanishes. Thus the <wavefunction> and its spatial derivative simplify to
$$
\Psi(a/2,t)=\frac{e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}}{\sqrt a},\qquad \partial_x\Psi(a/2,t)=-\frac{2\pi}{a^{3/2}}e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}.
$$
Substitution in the <probability current> gives
$$
\boxed{j(a/2,t)=\frac{2\pi\hbar}{ma^2}\sin\Omega t.}
$$
The <continuity equation>, integrated over the left half, gives $P_{\mathrm L}'(t)=j(0,t)-j(a/2,t)$. The <Dirichlet boundary condition> makes $j(0,t)=0$, and the preceding <probability> formula gives
$$
P_{\mathrm L}'(t)=-\frac{4\Omega}{3\pi}\sin\Omega t=-\frac{2\pi\hbar}{ma^2}\sin\Omega t=-j(a/2,t).
$$
**Positive midpoint current removes probability from the left half.** This is precisely the <midpoint current of a two-mode box state>.

= Solution
{parent=a}

The dimensionless coordinate for the <quantum harmonic oscillator> gives $d/dx=\sqrt{m\omega/\hbar}\,d/d\xi$. The <time-independent Schrödinger equation> therefore becomes
$$
-\psi_{\xi\xi}+\xi^2\psi=\frac{2E}{\hbar\omega}\psi.
$$
For $\psi=e^{-\xi^2/2}f$, direct <differentiation> yields
$$
\psi_{\xi\xi}=e^{-\xi^2/2}\left[f''-2\xi f'+(\xi^2-1)f\right].
$$
Cancelling the nonzero exponential reduces the equation to the <Hermite differential equation>
$$
f''-2\xi f'+(\epsilon-1)f=0,\qquad \boxed{\epsilon=\frac{2E}{\hbar\omega}.}
$$

= b
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Substitute the <power series> and collect each power of $\xi$:
$$
\sum_{k\geq0}\left[(k+2)(k+1)a_{k+2}+(\epsilon-1-2k)a_k\right]\xi^k=0.
$$
Matching the <power series> coefficients gives the <recurrence relation>
$$
\boxed{a_{k+2}=\frac{2k+1-\epsilon}{(k+2)(k+1)}a_k,\qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots.}
$$
The <even> and <odd> coefficients form separate sequences, determined by $a_0$ and $a_1$, respectively. For example, $a_2=(1-\epsilon)a_0/2$ and $a_3=(3-\epsilon)a_1/6$. These are the two independent <power series solutions of the Hermite differential equation>.

= c
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The issue is the <square-integrable> condition on the whole real line, not just decay at one end. Because the <quantum harmonic oscillator> potential is <even>, the even and odd parts of a square-integrable <wavefunction> are themselves square integrable and satisfy the same equation. It is therefore enough to examine each parity sequence separately.

For a nonterminating sequence, put $b_j=a_{2j+p}$, where $p=0$ or $1$. Its <recurrence relation> gives
$$
\frac{b_{j+1}}{b_j}=\frac{4j+2p+1-\epsilon}{(2j+p+2)(2j+p+1)}\sim\frac1j.
$$
Since $\epsilon$ is real for an <energy eigenvalue>, the ratio is eventually positive. After multiplying by one constant phase, the tail coefficients are all positive. Choose $0<\delta<1/2$. For sufficiently large $j$, the ratio is at least $(1-\delta)/(j+1)$, so for some $K>0$ the tail coefficients obey $b_j\geq K(1-\delta)^j/j!$. Consequently, for $\xi>0$, the magnitude of $f$ eventually exceeds a positive multiple of $\xi^p e^{(1-\delta)\xi^2}$ after subtraction of a fixed <polynomial>. Multiplication by $e^{-\xi^2/2}$ leaves exponential growth like $e^{(1/2-\delta)\xi^2}$, incompatible with the <square-integrable> condition. Thus each nonzero parity series must terminate. The parity argument also excludes cancellation between the two nonterminating sequences at both infinities. This proves <normalization forces Hermite-series termination>.

If the terminating <polynomial> has degree $n$, its top nonzero coefficient requires $2n+1-\epsilon=0$. Conversely, when $\epsilon=2n+1$, choosing the seed of parity $n$ nonzero makes the <recurrence relation> terminate exactly at degree $n$. The opposite seed must be zero, since that sequence does not terminate. Therefore
$$
\boxed{E_n=\left(n+\frac12\right)\hbar\omega,\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots.}
$$
The resulting <polynomials> are proportional to the physicists' <Hermite polynomials>, and $\psi_n\propto H_n(\xi)e^{-\xi^2/2}$ is normalizable because any <polynomial> times this <Gaussian function> is square integrable. The two parity seeds describe the full solution space of the second-order <differential equation>; the preceding argument therefore exhausts the normalizable <stationary states>. Each <eigenspace> has dimension one. General time-dependent <quantum states> can of course be <superpositions> of these <energy eigenstates>.

= Solution

For a normalized <wavefunction>, the <expectation value> of a function of <position> is
$$
\mathbb E_\psi(g(x))=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(x)|\psi(x)|^2\,dx,
$$
provided the integral exists. For an unnormalized state, divide this expression by $\int|\psi|^2dx$. Put $b=m\omega/\hbar>0$. The <ground state> has <probability density> $\sqrt{b/\pi}\,e^{-bx^2}$, which is <even>. Thus
$$
\mathbb E_{\psi_0}(|x|)=2\sqrt{\frac b\pi}\int_0^\infty xe^{-bx^2}\,dx=2\sqrt{\frac b\pi}\left[-\frac{e^{-bx^2}}{2b}\right]_0^\infty=\boxed{\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{\pi m\omega}}}.
$$
The zero mean <position> $\mathbb E_{\psi_0}(x)=0$ expresses symmetry, whereas this positive <expectation value> measures the mean distance from the centre.

= Solution

The <commutator> of two <linear operators> is $\boxed{[A,B]=AB-BA}$. Write $T^\dagger$ for the <adjoint operator>, using the <inner product> convention linear in its second argument. Since $A^\dagger=A$ and $B^\dagger=B$,
$$
[A,B]^\dagger=B^\dagger A^\dagger-A^\dagger B^\dagger=BA-AB=-[A,B].
$$
Thus the <commutator> is anti-self-adjoint. With $C=-i[A,B]$, conjugation of the scalar gives
$$
\boxed{C^\dagger=i[A,B]^\dagger=-i[A,B]=C.}
$$
All products are understood on the stated common <operator domain>; for physical unbounded <linear operators>, the state must lie in the relevant product domains and have the finite moments used below.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

A normalized <quantum state> satisfies $\langle\psi,\psi\rangle=1$. Its <expectation value> is $\boxed{\mathbb E_\psi(A)=\langle\psi,A\psi\rangle}$. The conjugate symmetry of the <inner product> and <self-adjointness> give
$$
\overline{\langle\psi,A\psi\rangle}=\langle A\psi,\psi\rangle=\langle\psi,A\psi\rangle,
$$
so the <expectation value> is real. If $A\phi=\lambda\phi$ with $\phi\ne0$, normalize $\phi$ by dividing by its <norm>. Then
$$
\lambda=\langle\phi/\|\phi\|,A\phi/\|\phi\|\rangle\in\mathbb R.
$$
**Every eigenvalue of a self-adjoint operator is real.**

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Let $a=\mathbb E_\psi(A)$, which is real. The dispersion, or <quantum uncertainty>, is the nonnegative <standard deviation>
$$
\boxed{\Delta_\psi(A)=\sqrt{\mathbb E_\psi(A^2)-\mathbb E_\psi(A)^2}=\|(A-aI)\psi\|.}
$$
Indeed, <self-adjointness> and normalization give
$$
\|(A-aI)\psi\|^2=\langle\psi,(A-aI)^2\psi\rangle=\mathbb E_\psi(A^2)-2a\mathbb E_\psi(A)+a^2=\mathbb E_\psi(A^2)-a^2.
$$
A squared <norm> is nonnegative, so the <square root> is defined. It vanishes precisely when $(A-aI)\psi=0$, namely when $\psi$ is an <eigenvector> of $A$. Conversely, an <eigenvector> with <eigenvalue> $\lambda$ has $a=\lambda$ and hence zero dispersion. Thus **zero dispersion is equivalent to being an eigenstate**.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Set $u=A\psi$, $v=B\psi$ and $z=\langle u,v\rangle$. The <self-adjointness> of $A,B$ implies
$$
z=\langle\psi,AB\psi\rangle,\qquad \overline z=\langle\psi,BA\psi\rangle,\qquad \mathbb E_\psi(C)=-i(z-\overline z)=2\operatorname{Im}z.
$$
The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> followed by $|z|^2\geq(\operatorname{Im}z)^2$ gives
$$
\mathbb E_\psi(A^2)\mathbb E_\psi(B^2)=\|u\|^2\|v\|^2\geq|z|^2\geq(\operatorname{Im}z)^2=\boxed{\frac14\bigl(\mathbb E_\psi(C)\bigr)^2}.
$$
Both inequalities must be equalities. The exact conditions are **$A\psi$ and $B\psi$ are linearly dependent, and $\operatorname{Re}\langle A\psi,B\psi\rangle=0$**. Equivalently, equality holds if either vector is zero; when both are nonzero it holds precisely when
$$
\boxed{B\psi=i\lambda A\psi\quad\text{for some real }\lambda\ne0.}
$$
The zero-vector cases matter: they saturate the inequality but cannot all be expressed by solving for $B\psi$ as a multiple of $A\psi$. The real-part condition can also be written $\mathbb E_\psi(AB+BA)=0$, using the <anticommutator>. This is the <uncentered second-moment commutator inequality>.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Centre the <position operator> and <momentum operator> by their real <expectation values>:
$$
A=X-x_0I,\quad B=P-p_0I,\qquad x_0=\mathbb E_\psi(X),\quad p_0=\mathbb E_\psi(P).
$$
Scalar multiples of the <identity operator> commute with every <linear operator>, so the <canonical commutation relation> gives
$$
[A,B]=[X,P]=i\hbar I,\qquad C=\hbar I,\qquad \mathbb E_\psi(C)=\hbar.
$$
The second moments of the centred <linear operators> are the <variances>, $\mathbb E_\psi(A^2)=\Delta_\psi(X)^2$ and $\mathbb E_\psi(B^2)=\Delta_\psi(P)^2$. Applying the preceding inequality and taking nonnegative square roots proves the <Heisenberg uncertainty relation>:
$$
\Delta_\psi(X)^2\Delta_\psi(P)^2\geq\frac{\hbar^2}4,\qquad \boxed{\Delta_\psi(X)\Delta_\psi(P)\geq\frac\hbar2.}
$$
**Subtracting the means is essential**: the uncentred second moments alone do not equal the <variances>. The <equality case of the Heisenberg uncertainty relation> requires the centred vectors to be imaginary multiples, as in the preceding equality conditions.
