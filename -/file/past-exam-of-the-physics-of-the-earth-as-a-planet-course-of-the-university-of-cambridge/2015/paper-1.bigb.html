A <fault plane> represents the surface across which the two sides of a fault slip. A real fault need not be perfectly planar; these angles can describe a local planar segment. Its <fault strike> is the azimuth, measured clockwise from north, of its horizontal intersection. The line itself has a $180^\circ$ ambiguity; choose the direction for which the fault dips to the right. Its <fault dip>, $\delta$, is the angle below horizontal measured perpendicular to that <fault strike>, from $0^\circ$ for horizontal to $90^\circ$ for vertical.

The <fault rake>, $\lambda$, is the angle of the relative slip <vector> within the <fault plane>. State the sign convention: if $\mathbf s$ is the chosen strike direction and $\mathbf d$ points down dip, use
$$
\boxed{\mathbf b=b(\cos\lambda\,\mathbf s-\sin\lambda\,\mathbf d).}
$$
Thus $\lambda=0$ is slip along $\mathbf s$, $+90^\circ$ is up-dip slip and $-90^\circ$ is down-dip slip. This distinguishes the direction in which the fault is tilted from the direction in which it actually moves.

The <hypocentre> is the point where rupture begins within the <Earth>. The <epicentre> is the point on the surface directly above it. The <hypocentre> need not coincide with the centroid of the final rupture. **Orientation uses strike and dip; motion additionally needs rake; <epicentre> and <hypocentre> locate the rupture's start.**<Earthquake Hazards Program Glossary by United States Geological Survey>[Entries “fault plane”, “strike”, “dip”, “hypocenter” and “epicenter”]{ref}

Assume a stable, lossless elastic body with the usual symmetry of the <elasticity tensor> and <boundary conditions> that eliminate the surface terms in <integration by parts>, such as a traction-free outer surface. Its elastic stiffness operator is then self-adjoint. When <density> varies spatially, the precise eigenproblem is generalized:
$$
\hat H\mathbf u_\alpha=\rho\omega_\alpha^2\mathbf u_\alpha,
\qquad
\langle\mathbf u_\alpha,\mathbf u_\beta\rangle_\rho
=\int_\Omega\rho\,\mathbf u_\alpha^*\cdot\mathbf u_\beta\,dV
=\delta_{\alpha\beta}.
$$
Here $\alpha$ abbreviates the pair of mode labels. Equivalently, $\rho^{-1}\hat H$ is a <self-adjoint operator> in the mass <inner product>. Stability makes $\omega_\alpha^2$ real and nonnegative. Modes at different <eigenvalues> are orthogonal, and an <orthonormal basis> can be selected within each degenerate eigenspace. Completeness permits expansion of a suitable <displacement field> in these <mass-weighted elastic normal modes>.

Insert the expansion and project onto $\mathbf u_\alpha^*$. Mass normalization diagonalizes both the <acceleration> term and the restoring term. For a symmetric <stress glut tensor> supported away from the boundary,
$$
\int_\Omega\mathbf u_\alpha^*\cdot(-\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\Gamma)\,dV
=\int_\Omega\boldsymbol\Gamma:\nabla\mathbf u_\alpha^*\,dV
=\int_\Omega\boldsymbol\Gamma:\mathbf e_\alpha^*\,dV.
$$
The symmetry of the <stress glut tensor> removes the antisymmetric part of the displacement gradient, leaving the <strain>. The result is **one independent forced oscillator per mode**, with the stated excitation integral. If the modes instead have mass norm $N_\alpha$, that integral is divided by $N_\alpha$. A source touching the boundary requires the corresponding surface forcing to be retained rather than silently dropping it.

Rigid translations and rotations can be zero-frequency modes of a free body. Their <strain> vanishes, so an internal symmetric <seismic moment tensor> does not excite them through this coupling. Attenuation, radiative boundaries or other non-self-adjoint effects require modifications of the simple conservative expansion.

For a mode of positive <frequency>, differentiation of the causal <convolution> gives
$$
\dot a(t)=\int_0^t\cos[\omega(t-s)]f(s)\,ds,
$$
$$
\ddot a(t)=f(t)-\omega\int_0^t\sin[\omega(t-s)]f(s)\,ds
=f(t)-\omega^2a(t).
$$
The first boundary term vanishes because $\sin0=0$; the second supplies $f(t)$ because $\cos0=1$. Both initial values vanish. This verifies the forced-oscillator equation and its <initial conditions>, including a step source in the piecewise or distributional sense.

The spatial <Dirac delta function> reduces the excitation to
$$
f_\alpha(t)=C_\alpha H(t-t_s),\qquad
C_\alpha=\mathbf M:\mathbf e_\alpha^*(\mathbf x_s).
$$
For an event at $t_s\ge0$, put $\tau=t-t_s$. Integrating the oscillator kernel from $t_s$ to $t$ gives the <normal-mode response to a step seismic moment>:
$$
a_\alpha(t)=\frac{C_\alpha}{\omega_\alpha^2}
[1-\cos(\omega_\alpha\tau)]H(\tau),
$$
$$
\boxed{\mathbf u(\mathbf x,t)=H(\tau)\sum_\alpha
\mathbf u_\alpha(\mathbf x)
\frac{\mathbf M:\mathbf e_\alpha^*(\mathbf x_s)}{\omega_\alpha^2}
[1-\cos(\omega_\alpha\tau)].}
$$
Non-unit mass normalization introduces the same factor $1/N_\alpha$ as above. Complex modes must be combined with their conjugate partners to produce a real displacement. The formal $\omega\to0$ limit is $a=C\tau^2H(\tau)/2$; for rigid-body modes here, $C=0$ because their <strain> is zero.

The step produces a static component plus continuing oscillations in this lossless model. It does not predict physical damping. **A point source is appropriate when rupture dimensions are small compared with the <wavelengths> being analysed and rupture duration is short compared with their <periods>.** Long-period observations of a relatively small <earthquake> can meet these conditions; high-frequency finite-fault directivity generally cannot. A finite source-time function replaces the ideal instantaneous step when its duration matters.

For <seismic source inversion>, collect calibrated, consistently filtered samples from all available stations and components into a data <vector> $\mathbf d$. For each trial source position and origin time, compute the corresponding <elastodynamic Green tensor> in an <Earth> model, including the instrument response used for the data. Write the symmetric <seismic moment tensor> in six independent symmetric basis <tensors>:
$$
\mathbf M=\sum_{j=1}^6m_j\mathbf B_j,
\qquad
\mathbf d=\mathbf G(\mathbf q)\mathbf m+\boldsymbol\epsilon,
\qquad \mathbf q=(x_s,y_s,z_s,t_s).
$$
Each column of $\mathbf G$ is the synthetic <seismogram> for one basis <tensor>. An off-diagonal basis <tensor> contains both symmetric entries, so the double contraction and its factors of two remain consistent.

A suitable objective is
$$
\chi^2(\mathbf q,\mathbf m)
=[\mathbf d-\mathbf G(\mathbf q)\mathbf m]^T
\mathbf C_d^{-1}[\mathbf d-\mathbf G(\mathbf q)\mathbf m],
$$
with noise <covariance> $\mathbf C_d$; a justified penalty can be added for <regularization>. At fixed $\mathbf q$, solve for the six entries of $\mathbf m$ by <weighted least squares>, preferably using a <QR decomposition> or <singular value decomposition> rather than explicitly inverting the matrix of the normal equations. Then search over the four nonlinear parameters using a coarse location/time grid followed by local refinement. **This <variable projection> reduces a ten-parameter search to a four-parameter nonlinear search with an inner six-parameter <linear> fit.** Merely having more than ten data samples does not ensure identifiability: the <design matrix> must have adequate rank and the network must resolve the source's radiation patterns.

Potential errors include inaccurate <velocity>, <density>, anisotropy and attenuation in the assumed <Earth> model; neglected finite rupture and source-time effects; timing, instrument calibration or component-orientation errors; correlated noise; uneven station coverage; and tradeoffs between depth, origin time and the moment <tensor>. Imposing a <double-couple seismic source> mechanism without evidence can hide genuine non-double-couple components. <Regularization> also changes the recovered source and should be reported.

Inspect residuals, singular values, recovery of synthetic sources and uncertainty under resampling or noise perturbations. Linearized <covariance> or a <posterior distribution> can quantify uncertainty within the assumed forward model, but does not automatically account for errors in that model. A good waveform fit alone is insufficient evidence that all ten parameters are uniquely recovered.

Take upward deflection as positive, use $k>0$, and write $\Delta\rho=\rho_m-\rho_c>0$. A positive added load depresses the plate, so $w$ is negative beneath it. Because bending moves the original surface while the load adds thickness, the final topography is **$e=s+w$**. For the cosine mode the horizontal biharmonic operator multiplies $w$ by $k^4$, giving
$$
(Dk^4+g\rho_m)w=-g\rho_cs.
$$
Eliminate $s=e-w$ to obtain
$$
\boxed{e=-\frac{Dk^4+g\Delta\rho}{g\rho_c}\,w.}
$$
The original loading equation contains $\rho_m$, whereas the relation after eliminating the added thickness contains $\Delta\rho$. These must not be interchanged prematurely.

At first order in the relief, represent a displaced <density> interface by a sinusoidal mass sheet. A sheet of surface-density <amplitude> $\Sigma$ at depth $z$ produces a downward gravity <amplitude> $2\pi G\Sigma e^{-kz}$ at the datum. This follows by solving the Fourier-transformed gravitational <Poisson equation> above and below the sheet: the decaying potential has <amplitude> $-2\pi G\Sigma e^{-k|z-z_0|}/k$, with the normal derivative jump fixed by the sheet mass.

The top interface contributes $\rho_ce$; the displaced <crust>–<mantle> interface contributes $\Delta\rho\,w$ at depth $t$. Thus
$$
\Delta g=2\pi G[\rho_ce+\Delta\rho\,w e^{-kt}].
$$
The second term is negative beneath a positive load: depression replaces denser <mantle> material by lighter <crust>. Substituting the deflection-to-topography ratio yields
$$
\boxed{\Delta g=Z(k)e,\qquad
Z(k)=2\pi G\rho_c\left[1-
\frac{e^{-kt}}{1+Dk^4/(g\Delta\rho)}\right].}
$$
This <flexural gravity admittance> combines mechanical compensation and attenuation of the buried interface's gravitational signal. It assumes small relief, a flat uniform thin plate, uniform <density> layers and the stated surface loading, without additional internal <density> anomalies. Here $t$ is the depth of the <crust>–<mantle> interface. It is not automatically the <effective elastic thickness> $T_e$; the latter enters through $D=ET_e^3/[12(1-\nu^2)]$.

Define $C=2\pi G\rho_c$ and $\ell_f=[D/(g\Delta\rho)]^{1/4}$. Then
$$
\frac{Z}{C}=1-\frac{e^{-kt}}{1+(k\ell_f)^4}.
$$
At long <wavelength>, the mass deficit at the lower interface nearly cancels the surface excess, and the gravity field does not resolve their vertical separation. For $t>0$,
$$
\boxed{Z\sim Ckt\longrightarrow0\quad(k\to0).}
$$
The first mechanical contribution is fourth order in $k$, although the exponential also contributes terms at second and third order. If the ideal interface depth were zero, the leading term would instead be $C(k\ell_f)^4$.

At short <wavelength>, bending resists the interface's deflection, and any remaining buried signal is attenuated. Consequently
$$
\boxed{Z\longrightarrow C=2\pi G\rho_c\quad(k\to\infty).}
$$
This is the uncompensated surface-sheet value. For nonnegative $D,t$ the approach is from below. With $\beta=D/(g\Delta\rho)$,
$$
\frac1C\frac{dZ}{dk}
=e^{-kt}\left[\frac{t}{1+\beta k^4}
+\frac{4\beta k^3}{(1+\beta k^4)^2}\right]\ge0.
$$
The scales $kt\sim1$ and $k\ell_f\sim1$ respectively mark gravitational depth filtering and flexural resistance; which appears first depends on $t/\ell_f$. Admittance has dimensions of inverse time squared. The formal $k\to\infty$ limit describes this model, not an assertion that thin-plate theory remains accurate at arbitrarily short <wavelength>.

New oceanic <lithosphere> is hot and mechanically weak. As it cools, the depth over which rock can sustain elastic <stress> on the loading timescale generally increases, so **<effective elastic thickness> tends to increase with plate age at the time of loading**. It is this mechanical state when the load is emplaced, together with subsequent relaxation, that matters; the present age alone is insufficient for an ancient load.

The <half-space cooling model> illustrates the thermal dependence. With age $\tau$, surface <temperature> $T_s$, initial interior <temperature> $T_m$ and constant <thermal diffusivity> $\kappa$,
$$
T(z,\tau)=T_s+(T_m-T_s)
\operatorname{erf}\left(\frac{z}{2\sqrt{\kappa\tau}}\right).
$$
A chosen <temperature> $T_*$ lies at
$$
\boxed{z_*=2\sqrt{\kappa\tau}\,
\operatorname{erf}^{-1}\left(\frac{T_*-T_s}{T_m-T_s}\right)
\ \propto\sqrt\tau.}
$$
If the base of the mechanically strong layer approximately follows such an isotherm, $T_e$ initially follows a similar age trend. Cooling models constrained by ocean-floor depth and heat flow provide the thermal motivation; their fitted thermal thickness must not be mistaken for measured <elastic thickness>.<An analysis of the variation of ocean floor bathymetry and heat flow with age by Barry Parsons and John G. Sclater>[1977, pages 803–806 and discussion of half-space versus finite-plate cooling]{ref}

In practice $T_e$ is an effective bending thickness rather than the depth of one universal <temperature>. Stress-dependent yielding, <grain size>, water content, composition, loading duration and reheating can alter it. Finite-plate cooling and basal heat input also limit indefinite square-root growth at old ages. Nor should $T_e$ be identified with either the thickness of the <crust> or the full seismically defined <lithosphere>.

Use the supplied surface <temperature>, $420^\circ\mathrm C\simeq693\,\mathrm K$, as the premise. With otherwise comparable material and <geothermal gradient>, this hotter boundary increases thermally activated <material creep> at shallow depth and places a chosen mechanical cutoff <temperature> closer to the surface. That effect alone would favour a thinner elastic layer than in a cool terrestrial oceanic plate.

<Water weakening of rocks> makes dryness act in the opposite direction. Water can enhance <material creep>; sufficiently dry material can remain strong at temperatures at which its wet equivalent relaxes. Experiments on dry diabase provide a concrete basis for a strong dry <crust> and possible mechanical coupling between <crust> and <mantle>, rather than requiring a weak crustal layer solely because the surface is hot.<High-temperature deformation of dry diabase, with application to crustal deformation on Venus by S. J. Mackwell and others>[1996, printed pages 793–794, dry strength and implications for crustal coupling]{ref}

For an illustrative steady conductive geotherm with <thermal conductivity> $k_T$ and <heat flux> $q$, a fixed strength cutoff gives
$$
T_e\sim\frac{k_T(T_*-T_s)}q.
$$
Therefore the comparison would scale as
$$
\frac{T_{e,V}}{T_{e,E}}\sim
\frac{k_{T,V}}{k_{T,E}}\frac{q_E}{q_V}
\frac{T_{*,V}-T_{s,V}}{T_{*,E}-T_{s,E}},
$$
where all temperatures are compared consistently and a dry rheology may have a higher effective cutoff $T_{*,V}$. This is a conditional strength-layer estimate, not a universal definition of $T_e$.

**<Venus> can retain appreciable <elastic thickness> despite its hot surface; <temperature> favours thinning, while dryness favours strengthening. The supplied facts alone determine neither a numerical $T_e$ nor a definite ordering relative to <Earth>'s oceanic plates.** The comparison requires heat flow, subsurface thermal history, material rheology, interior water content and loading timescale. Little atmospheric water is evidence relevant to the hypothesis of dry rocks, not a direct measurement of deep water activity.

**<Seismic surface-wave dispersion>.** <Rayleigh waves> combine vertical and longitudinal motion in the vertical plane of propagation; <Love waves> have horizontal transverse motion and can be guided by a low-shear-speed surface layer. A homogeneous elastic half-space supports a <Rayleigh wave> with a frequency-independent speed in the ideal elastic limit, but depth-dependent <Earth> structure makes the surface-wave <eigenfunctions> and their propagation speeds depend on <frequency>. This structural <dispersion> can occur even when each constituent material is itself nondispersive.

Distinguish <phase velocity> $c=\omega/k$ from <group velocity> $U=d\omega/dk$, which controls a narrow-band envelope in the appropriate weakly attenuating limit. Longer <periods> generally have deeper sensitivity, while shorter <periods> emphasize shallower structure. In a profile whose shear speed increases with depth this often makes long-period waves faster, but low-velocity zones, anisotropy and higher modes can change that trend. <Frequency> dependence need not be monotonic in every <Earth> model.

Measure phase delays or wave-packet arrival times across known source–receiver paths for many <periods>, accounting for source phase, cycle ambiguity, multipathing and mode identification. Compute predicted <dispersion> from trial elastic profiles and compare with observations. Linearization expresses the sensitivity schematically as
$$
\frac{\delta c(\omega)}{c(\omega)}=
\int\left[K_\beta(z,\omega)\frac{\delta\beta}{\beta}
+K_\alpha(z,\omega)\frac{\delta\alpha}{\alpha}
+K_\rho(z,\omega)\frac{\delta\rho}{\rho}\right]dz,
$$
where $\alpha,\beta$ are compressional and shear speeds. A regularized <inverse problem> then estimates depth structure; many crossing paths additionally provide <surface-wave tomography>. Joint <Love wave> and <Rayleigh wave> measurements help distinguish shear-wave polarizations and constrain radial <seismic anisotropy>. Finite bandwidth, depth averaging and correlated sensitivities mean that one <dispersion> curve does not uniquely determine a detailed <Earth> profile.

**<Seismic ray theory>, the <eikonal equation> and <Fermat's principle>.** In a smoothly varying isotropic medium, insert a high-frequency ansatz $u=A(\mathbf x)e^{i\omega[T(\mathbf x)-t]}$ into the scalar <wave equation>. The leading powers of <frequency> give
$$
\boxed{|\nabla T|^2=\frac1{c(\mathbf x)^2}.}
$$
The travel-time surfaces are <wavefronts> and rays are their normal trajectories, with <slowness> <vector> $\mathbf p=\nabla T$. The next-order equation transports <amplitude>, including geometrical spreading; attenuation is an additional material effect.

<Fermat's principle> says that the ray makes $\int ds/c$ stationary under path variations with fixed endpoints. Stationarity, rather than always the global minimum, permits multiple arrivals. Its Euler–Lagrange equation, with arclength $s$ and unit tangent $\hat{\mathbf t}$, is
$$
\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{\hat{\mathbf t}}c\right)
=\nabla\left(\frac1c\right).
$$
For horizontal layering, the horizontal <slowness> $p_h=\sin i/c$ is conserved, giving <Snell's law> at interfaces and smooth refraction between them. Here $i$ is measured from vertical. A turning point satisfies $p_hc(z)=1$; increasing speed with depth bends a downgoing ray back upwards. Travel-time tomography uses these ray integrals to infer spatial <slowness> variations.

The approximation requires <wavelength> to be small compared with structural variation scales. Caustics, turning-point <amplitudes>, diffraction, scattering and finite-frequency sensitivity require wave-based corrections. Thus <seismic ray theory> gives an economical geometric description and useful travel times, while surface-wave <dispersion> retains essential interference and boundary-mode physics.

**<Homologous temperature> and deformation.** <Homologous temperature> is $\Theta=T/T_m$, with both temperatures in <kelvin> and $T_m$ the appropriate pressure- and composition-dependent melting <temperature>. It measures proximity to melting more meaningfully than an absolute <temperature> alone. Pressure, composition and water can alter both $T_m$ and the flow law, so equal $\Theta$ does not imply identical rheology.

A useful thermally activated <material creep> law is
$$
\dot\epsilon=A\sigma^n d^{-p}
\exp\left[-\frac{E^*+PV^*}{RT}\right],
$$
with <stress> $\sigma$, <grain size> $d$, <activation energy> $E^*$ and <activation volume> $V^*$. Water activity can add another factor. Increasing <temperature> raises the rate at fixed <stress> when the activation enthalpy is positive, greatly lowering effective <viscosity>. Solid material can therefore flow on geological timescales well below melting.

<Diffusion creep> is approximately <linear> in <stress>: bulk-diffusion <Nabarro-Herring creep> has grain-size scaling $d^{-2}$, while grain-boundary <Coble creep> has $d^{-3}$ in their ideal regimes. <Dislocation creep> usually has a <stress> exponent greater than one and weaker grain-size dependence. Its effective <viscosity> scales as $\eta_{\rm eff}\propto\sigma^{1-n}$ at fixed <temperature>, so stronger forcing changes the apparent <viscosity>. At lower <homologous temperature>, brittle/frictional deformation or high-stress dislocation mechanisms can become important. The dominant process also depends on pressure and confinement; low <temperature> is not by itself a guarantee of brittle failure deep in a planet.

A <deformation mechanism map> compares the competing rates or strengths as functions of <stress> and <homologous temperature> at specified <grain size>, pressure and composition. Fine grains favour <diffusion creep>; larger <stress> favours nonlinear <dislocation creep>. There is no universal <temperature> dividing all these regimes. This sensitivity helps explain a strong outer <lithosphere> above a slowly flowing solid <mantle> and why modest thermal or water variations can reorganize deformation.

**Determining the actual planform of <mantle convection>.** The task is to infer the geometry of upwellings, downwellings and connecting flow in the present <Earth>, rather than to draw a preferred laboratory convection cell. Plate velocities and the distribution of subduction zones directly constrain surface motion and likely cold downwellings. Ridges, volcanism and hotspots provide further clues, but a ridge need not mark the centre of a deep plume, nor is every hotspot uniquely explained by one plume geometry.

<Seismic tomography> supplies three-dimensional <velocity> structure from body-wave travel times, waveforms and surface-wave <dispersion>. Fast slab-like structures can often be traced beneath subduction zones into the lower <mantle>. The 1997 global P-wave study reported long narrow lower-mantle structures connected with convergence and interpreted some slab segments as reaching the deepest <mantle>; that is evidence for deep circulation, not proof that every slab follows one route.<Evidence for deep mantle circulation from global tomography by R. D. van der Hilst and others>[Nature 386, pages 578–584; abstract on lower-mantle structures and mantle-wide flow]{ref} <Temperature> affects seismic speed, but composition, phase changes, water and anisotropy also matter, so <velocity> is not itself a flow map.

Gravity and the <geoid> constrain the spatially integrated <density> field and its induced boundary deformation. <Dynamic topography> supplies information about vertical stresses, after separating crustal structure, isostatic loads and thermal subsidence. <Seismic anisotropy> can constrain deformation and fabric orientations, subject to a mineral-physics relation between fabric and flow. Discontinuity topography and the <mantle>'s heat budget provide further tests. Each observation has a different sensitivity to <density>, <temperature> and <viscosity>, making their combination more useful than any one image.

Compare these observations with three-dimensional geodynamic models that solve creeping-flow force balance, mass conservation and thermal transport, including buoyancy from <temperature> and composition, realistic plate motions, <viscosity> structure and phase boundaries. Predict seismic observables, gravity, <geoid> and <dynamic topography> from the same model, and assess data coverage and resolution before interpreting a feature. Whole-mantle exchange, impeded or deflected slabs near transition zones, and broad deep anomalies can coexist; the evidence does not require identical stacked or single-layer cells everywhere. **The actual planform is constrained by joint seismic, gravitational, surface-kinematic and thermal evidence, with substantial nonuniqueness in its detailed flow and history.**
