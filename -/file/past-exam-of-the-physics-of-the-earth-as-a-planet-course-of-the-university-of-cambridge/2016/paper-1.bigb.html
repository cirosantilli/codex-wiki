= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $\hat x$ point to the equatorial meridian at zero <longitude>, $\hat y$ to the equator at $90^\circ$ E, and $\hat z$ to the north pole. Use a right-hand positive <Euler angular velocity vector>. Relative <rotations> add as <vectors>, not as geographic coordinates:
$$
\boldsymbol\omega_{B/C}=\boldsymbol\omega_{B/A}+\boldsymbol\omega_{A/C}=(5\hat x+12\hat y)\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{rad\,yr^{-1}}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{|\boldsymbol\omega_{B/C}|=13\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{rad\,yr^{-1}},\qquad (\phi,\lambda)=(0^\circ,67.380^\circ\mathrm E).}
$$
Here $\lambda=\tan^{-1}(12/5)$ is the <longitude> of the positive <Euler pole>, and its <latitude> $\phi$ is zero. The antipodal pole with the opposite angular-velocity sign is an equivalent convention; mixing a pole reversal with an unchanged sign would give the wrong motion.

At <latitude> $\phi$ and <longitude> $\lambda$, $\mathbf r=R(\cos\phi\cos\lambda,\cos\phi\sin\lambda,\sin\phi)$. Motion is $\mathbf v_{B/C}=\boldsymbol\omega_{B/C}\times\mathbf r$. A meridian boundary has horizontal normal $\hat e_\lambda=(-\sin\lambda,\cos\lambda,0)$, so its across-boundary <velocity> is
$$
\mathbf v_{B/C}\cdot\hat e_\lambda=-R\sin\phi\,(5\cos\lambda+12\sin\lambda)\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}.
$$
For a <transform fault> this must vanish along the whole meridian, not only at an isolated point. Thus $5\cos\lambda+12\sin\lambda=0$. Select the solution in the western hemisphere:
$$
\boxed{\lambda=-22.620^\circ=22.620^\circ\mathrm W.}
$$
The other solution is the antipodal meridian in the eastern hemisphere. Geometrically, the transform meridian is in a great-circle plane perpendicular to the <Euler angular velocity vector>. Its outgoing tangent direction at either pole, expressed in the fixed $x,y$ axes, is $\hat t_F=(12,-5)/13$.

= Solution

At the south pole $\mathbf r=-R\hat z$. Choose plate C as the instantaneous <reference frame>. Since $R=4\times10^9$ mm, the two components of relative angular <speed> give 20 and 48 mm/year. The plate <velocities> in the fixed tangent-plane axes are
$$
\mathbf v_C=(0,0),\qquad\mathbf v_A=(-48,0),\qquad\mathbf v_B=(-48,20)\quad\mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}.
$$
The <plate velocity triangle> therefore has sides
$$
\boxed{|\mathbf v_B-\mathbf v_A|=20,\quad|\mathbf v_A-\mathbf v_C|=48,\quad|\mathbf v_B-\mathbf v_C|=52\quad\mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}.}
$$
The physical boundary rays point along $+x$ for AB, $+y$ for AC and $(12,-5)$ for BC. Plate A occupies the quadrant between the first two rays, B the narrow wedge between AB and BC, and C the remainder. The stated overriding relations make AB and AC <subduction trenches>, while BC remains a <transform fault>: this is a <trench-trench-transform triple junction>. Put the <subduction> teeth on B's side of AB and A's side of AC, identifying B above A and A above C.

For kinematic stability there must be a single junction <velocity> $\mathbf U$ compatible with all three boundary-normal motions. Use the standard idealization that each trench's normal <velocity> is that of its <overriding plate>, with no independent rollback. AB is parallel to $x$, so $U_y=v_{B,y}=20$. AC is parallel to $y$, so $U_x=v_{A,x}=-48$. The transform requires $\mathbf U-\mathbf v_C$ to be parallel to $(12,-5)$, which is true for this intersection. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbf U_S=(-48,20)\,\mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}=\mathbf v_B\quad\text{in the C frame}.}
$$
**The southern <triple junction> is instantaneously kinematically stable.** It is stationary relative to B, migrates along AB relative to A, and migrates along BC relative to C. This compatibility result does not promise long-term stability if plate <rotations> or trench rollback change. Notice that the three relative <velocities> close even though the overriding order is not a statement that all three plates move toward one common stationary point.

= Solution

At the north pole $\mathbf r=+R\hat z$, so the <velocities> reverse:
$$
\mathbf v_C=(0,0),\qquad\mathbf v_A=(48,0),\qquad\mathbf v_B=(48,-20)\quad\mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}.
$$
The triangle still has side lengths 20, 48 and 52 mm/year. Across AB, A moves toward positive $y$ relative to B; across AC, A moves toward positive $x$ relative to C. Both boundaries therefore open and are <spreading ridges>, with full opening <speeds> 20 and 48 mm/year. Together with BC they form a <ridge-ridge-transform triple junction>.

In the symmetric normal-spreading model, a ridge's normal <velocity> is the mean normal <velocity> of its two plates. AB gives $U_y=(0-20)/2=-10$, and AC gives $U_x=(48+0)/2=24$. Their intersection lies on the BC transform <velocity> line:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf U_N=(24,-10)\,\mathrm{mm\,yr^{-1}}=\frac{\mathbf v_B+\mathbf v_C}{2}.}
$$
**This northern junction is also instantaneously kinematically stable.** The diagrams use the same fixed geocentric tangent axes at both poles; reversing <velocities> is not the same operation as reflecting a map drawn from an exterior observer's viewpoint.

Not every ridge-ridge-transform geometry works. For pure normal spreading, each ridge <velocity> line is the <perpendicular bisector> of the corresponding side of the <plate velocity triangle>. Their intersection is the triangle's <circumcenter>. A transform junction must also lie on the third side's line. A nondegenerate triangle's <circumcenter> lies there only at that side's midpoint, so the opposite angle is a right angle. Equivalently, $\big(\mathbf v_B-\mathbf v_A\big)\cdot\big(\mathbf v_C-\mathbf v_A\big)=0$. The two ridge directions are then perpendicular, as they are here. **RRF junctions are conditionally stable, with the right-angle ridge geometry in this symmetric pure-normal model; they are not all stable.** Oblique or asymmetric accretion changes the velocity-line assumptions and must be specified separately.

= Solution

Assume an Earth-like reversing planetary <magnetic field> and magnetizable crust that acquires <thermoremanent magnetization> on cooling. Alternating polarity intervals then produce <marine magnetic stripes> parallel to each <spreading ridge>, with matching age/polarity sequences on its two sides. AB has <half-spreading rate> 10 mm/year, whereas AC has <half-spreading rate> 24 mm/year. Thus the same polarity interval $\Delta t$ produces widths $10\Delta t$ and $24\Delta t$ mm when time is in years: the AC stripes are 2.4 times wider. Age increases away from each ridge. The transform produces no new crust; bands end or are displaced at it, rather than forming a third symmetric stripe sequence centred on BC. This is the remanent-magnetization/spreading mechanism developed from marine magnetic observations.<Magnetic Anomalies Over Oceanic Ridges by F. J. Vine and D. H. Matthews>[pp. 947–949, alternating remanent magnetization and the spreading interpretation]{ref}

A more precise local sketch can be made in the northern junction frame. Let $x,y$ be distances in kilometres along the fixed tangent axes and $t$ the crustal age in Myr; numerically, mm/year equals km/Myr. Relative to the moving junction, the <velocities> are $\mathbf w_A=(24,10)$, $\mathbf w_B=(24,-10)$ and $\mathbf w_C=(-24,10)$. In A the steady age field is $t_A=\min(x/24,y/10)$, since backwards advection first reaches either the AC or AB ridge. Equal-age bands therefore join as right-angle corners within A, with the corners migrating along the ray $(24,10)$. In the B wedge, $t_B=-y/10$ and the stripes meet the transform. On the C flank directly supplied from AC, $t_C=-x/24$; other parts of C carry older crust whose inherited pattern requires earlier boundary history.

\Image[paper-1-magnetic-stripes.png]
{title=Original conditional remanent-polarity sketch in the north-junction frame; reversal intervals are illustrative and unsupplied older crust is left unassigned}

The sketch displays <magnetization> polarity, not a computed total-field anomaly amplitude. Actual anomalies depend on the planetary field direction, source depth, <magnetization> strength and observation geometry. The paper supplies no field history, so a numerical reversal chronology or an absolute positive/negative anomaly sequence cannot be inferred. Without field reversals, ridge accretion alone does not imply alternating polarity stripes.

= Solution

**<gravity> and <surface topography>.** <gravity> responds to all <density> structure, while <surface topography> records both load and support. First specify the reference field and measurement height. <latitude> and planetary <rotation> affect normal <gravity>. At elevation $h$, the reference field decreases approximately by $2gh/R$; a <free-air gravity anomaly> restores this elevation effect but retains topographic <mass>. A <Bouguer gravity anomaly> additionally subtracts an approximate topographic slab contribution $2\pi G\rho_ch$. <terrain correction> replaces the unrealistic slab near valleys and ridges, and spherical geometry matters over large areas. A <density> choice is required for these reductions; a numerical crustal <density> is not provided here.

In <Airy isostasy>, <surface topography> of height $h$ and crustal <density> $\rho_c$ is compensated by a root of thickness $b$ replacing mantle of <density> $\rho_m>\rho_c$. Equal column <masses> give $\rho_ch=(\rho_m-\rho_c)b$. The positive <gravity> of the relief is partly cancelled by the negative <density> contrast of the root. At long wavelengths a compensated mountain can therefore have a modest free-air anomaly and a negative Bouguer anomaly. <Pratt isostasy> instead varies column <density> at a roughly common compensation depth. Neither model says that <surface topography> itself exerts no <gravity>.

Potential-field depth filtering makes wavelength crucial. In a planar Fourier approximation, a <density> interface at depth $z$ is attenuated by $e^{-kz}$ at horizontal <wavenumber> $k$. For a small Airy-compensated height perturbation and a compensation interface at depth $D$, the <gravity-topography admittance> is approximately
$$
Z_{\rm FA}(k)=2\pi G\rho_c(1-e^{-kD}),\qquad Z_{\rm B}(k)=-2\pi G\rho_ce^{-kD}.
$$
Long wavelengths allow strong cancellation between load and root. At short wavelengths the root's <gravity> is filtered out, so the observed near-surface <mass> can dominate even if the column is compensated. In a <flexural isostasy> model, an elastic <lithosphere> supports and spreads the load; for downward deflection $w$ and imposed load $q$, $D_f\nabla^4w+\Delta\rho g w=q$, with $D_f=ET_e^3/[12(1-\nu^2)]$. Thus $w_k=q_k/(D_fk^4+\Delta\rho g)$, and the effective elastic thickness controls the support scale.

Estimate admittance from cross-spectra of <gravity> and <surface topography>, and compare its wavelength dependence and coherence with local-compensation, elastic-flexure and subsurface-load models. A primary Atlantic study sampled ship-track free-air <gravity> and bathymetry and compared their Fourier-domain relationship with compensation models, illustrating this observational route rather than inferring a root from the sign of one <gravity> value.<The relationship between bathymetry and gravity in the Atlantic Ocean by Dan McKenzie and Carl Bowin>[pp. 1903–1915, ship-track sampling, spectral transfer estimates and wavelength-dependent compensation]{ref} Check navigation, instrument drift, terrain/<density> reductions, sediment and water loads, and independent seismic crustal constraints. Density-depth trade-offs and mantle dynamic support make the <inverse problem> nonunique; <surface topography> and <gravity> together constrain support, but do not uniquely map every <density> anomaly.

**<mantle viscosity>.** A second, independent route concerns deformation rates rather than static compensation. <glacial isostatic adjustment> follows loading and unloading of a deformable <Earth>: an ice sheet depresses the surface, and subsequent removal leaves time-dependent uplift, horizontal <displacement>, sea-level and <gravity> changes. Fit dated relative sea-level indicators and raised shorelines together with geodetic <displacement> rates and a reconstructed ice/water load history to a viscoelastic <Earth> model. Relative sea level includes solid-surface motion, changing <geoid> and redistribution of ocean water; treating it only as local vertical rock motion biases the inference.

For scale, a sinusoidal surface perturbation of <wavenumber> $k$ on a uniform incompressible viscous half-space relaxes under <gravity> with $\tau_k\sim2\eta k/(\rho g)$. A Maxwell material separately has a stress-relaxation scale $\tau_M=\eta/\mu$. These are different experiments and generally different times: rebound modes also involve <gravity>, spherical geometry, a layered mantle and elastic <lithosphere>. Fit a family of relaxation modes or a full load-history convolution, rather than equating every observed rebound time to $\eta/\mu$.

Use independent ice-margin, exposure-age and sea-level constraints to reduce the trade-off between ice history and <viscosity>; compare horizontal as well as vertical <GNSS> motions, and separate present ice loss, groundwater changes and tectonic signals from the older loading response. A primary global analysis jointly tested ice-history and mantle-response predictions against space-geodetic observations, rather than treating a local uplift rate as a unique <viscosity> measurement.<Space geodesy constrains ice age terminal deglaciation: The global ICE-6G_C (VM5a) model by W. R. Peltier and others>[abstract and pp. 450–487, joint ice-history/GIA model and space-geodetic constraints]{ref}

<postseismic relaxation> probes shallower or faster relaxation after a <stress> perturbation, but must be distinguished from afterslip and poroelastic deformation. Long-wavelength <geoid> and mantle-flow models provide complementary constraints when <density> structure is estimated independently from seismic structure and mineral physics; unknown density-to-velocity conversion and boundary <tractions> remain trade-offs. Laboratory creep experiments constrain constitutive laws as functions of <temperature>, <stress> and grain size, but require a large extrapolation to geological conditions. **Combine spatially and temporally distinct observations; there is no single directly measured <mantle viscosity> independent of depth, <rheology> and loading assumptions.**

**First-motion source mechanisms.** Locate an <earthquake> from arrival times and use a seismic <velocity> model to calculate each station's source takeoff direction. Measure the initial direct <P wave> polarity after checking instrument sign, component orientation, timing and phase identification. An upward trace at an instrument is not automatically a source compression without the propagation and component convention. Plot the inferred source radiation signs on a <focal sphere>, commonly using a lower-hemisphere equal-area projection, and seek nodal planes separating positive and negative observations.

For a shear dislocation with orthogonal unit slip and fault-normal <vectors> $\mathbf s,\mathbf n$, the <double-couple fault source> has $M=M_0(\mathbf s\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}+\mathbf n\mathbf s^{\mathsf T})$. Its far-field P radiation sign is that of
$$
A_P(\hat{\mathbf r})\propto\hat{\mathbf r}^{\mathsf T}M\hat{\mathbf r}=2M_0(\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\mathbf s)(\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\mathbf n).
$$
The two zeros are orthogonal nodal planes; the four quadrants alternate between outward and inward initial radiation. Their orientations and quadrant signs determine candidate strike, dip and rake. Normal, reverse and strike-slip mechanisms give different projected patterns. The <earthquake pressure and tension axes> are the negative and positive moment-tensor principal directions, respectively; they do not by themselves measure the complete regional <stress tensor>.

\Image[paper-1-focal-mechanisms.png]
{title=Original lower-hemisphere equal-area P-radiation polarity examples for ideal strike-slip, normal and reverse double couples}

There is a fundamental <fault-plane ambiguity>: exchanging $\mathbf s$ and $\mathbf n$ leaves the radiation tensor unchanged. First motions therefore give a fault plane and an auxiliary plane without saying which actually slipped. Use mapped rupture, <aftershock> geometry or independent geodetic <displacement> to select the physical fault where possible. Polarity signs alone do not measure scalar seismic moment, source duration or rupture extent. A poorly sampled <focal sphere> or many near-nodal observations can admit several mechanisms rather than one defensible beachball.

Propagate uncertainty in event depth, <velocity> gradients and takeoff angles, allow for mispicked or reversed polarities, and report the range of mechanisms consistent with the observations. A primary method did this by testing alternative locations and <velocity> models and validated its uncertainty estimates using noisy synthetic data and clusters of events with similar waveforms.<A New Method for Determining First-Motion Focal Mechanisms by Jeanne L. Hardebeck and Peter M. Shearer>[pp. 2264–2266 and abstract, first-motion construction, polarity/takeoff uncertainty and independent validation tests]{ref} Broadband waveforms and amplitude ratios add information, especially for non-double-couple sources, but they are additional measurements rather than a way to extract amplitude information from a sign-only data set.

**Radial <density> structure.** In an <isotropic> adiabatic material, the <seismic parameter> is
$$
\Phi(r)=\frac{K_S}{\rho}=\alpha(r)^2-\frac43\beta(r)^2.
$$
For a chemically homogeneous region whose background stratification is adiabatic, $d\rho/dP=\rho/K_S=1/\Phi$. Combine this with spherical hydrostatic balance and <mass> conservation to obtain the <Adams-Williamson equations>
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\rho}{dr}=-\frac{\rho g}{\Phi},\qquad\frac{dm}{dr}=4\pi r^2\rho,\qquad g=\frac{Gm}{r^2},\qquad\frac{dP}{dr}=-\rho g.}
$$
The inward <density> increase follows <compressibility> under the increasing <pressure>. A specified starting <density> and measured $\Phi(r)$ allow integration within a homogeneous adiabatic layer. Seismic <velocities> alone, however, do not determine that <density> normalization, since they contain ratios of moduli to <density>. Chemical boundaries, phase transitions, nonadiabatic thermal gradients and <anisotropy> require additional modeling; a composition jump is not obtained by blindly integrating the smooth relation across it.

Use travel times and surface waves to constrain elastic <velocities>, reflected/converted phases to locate interfaces, and normal-mode frequencies to constrain <density> as well as elastic structure through their sensitivity kernels. Impose independently known total <mass> and <moment of inertia>:
$$
M=4\pi\int_0^R\rho(r)r^2\,dr,\qquad I=\frac{8\pi}{3}\int_0^R\rho(r)r^4\,dr.
$$
Because these weight depth differently, they help distinguish admissible profiles, but two integrals alone do not specify an arbitrary radial function. Laboratory equations of state and mineral physics restrict composition, <compressibility> and <density> jumps. <pressure> and enclosed <mass> are matched across interfaces, while <density> may jump. In a liquid region $\beta=0$ and $\Phi=\alpha^2$, so the shear-wave absence constrains phase state without itself providing a direct <density> measurement.

The <Preliminary Reference Earth Model> is a concrete joint inversion of seismic travel times, <normal modes>, <mass> and inertia, including <density> and attenuation along with elastic structure. The original analysis combined these complementary data rather than deriving <density> uniquely from <P wave> <speed>.<Preliminary reference Earth model by Adam M. Dziewonski and Don L. Anderson>[abstract and pp. 297–300, data classes and coupled radial reference-model inversion]{ref} **A defensible radial <density> model combines hydrostatic/thermodynamic relations with seismic and global integral constraints, and states where homogeneity and adiabaticity fail.**

= Solution
{parent=a}

For uniform <density> $\rho>0$ and stiffness, linear momentum balance without <body force> is $\rho\ddot u_i=\partial_j\sigma_{ij}$. The minor elastic symmetries make the two symmetric-strain terms equal, so $\partial_j\sigma_{ij}=c_{ijkl}u_{k,lj}$. Write $s=ct-\hat p_jx_j$ and $u_i=a_if(s)$. Then $\ddot u_i=c^2a_if''$ and $u_{k,lj}=a_k\hat p_l\hat p_jf''$. Thus a nontrivial plane-wave profile satisfies
$$
\rho c^2a_i=c_{ijkl}\hat p_j\hat p_la_k.
$$
The density-normalized <elastic acoustic tensor> is consequently
$$
\boxed{B_{ij}=\frac1\rho c_{ikjl}\hat p_k\hat p_l,\qquad B_{ij}a_j=c^2a_i.}
$$
The alternative dummy-index form $B_{ik}=\rho^{-1}c_{ijkl}\hat p_j\hat p_l$ is identical. In ordinary conservative <elasticity>, major symmetry $c_{ikjl}=c_{jlik}$ gives $B_{ji}=B_{ij}$ after interchanging the dummy indices. This symmetry comes from a strain-energy <density>; it is an assumption of the ordinary elastic tensor, not a consequence of homogeneity alone.

For any such eigenpair the profile $f$ is arbitrary, since both sides carry the same $f''$. This nondispersive result uses linear, local, time-independent <elasticity> without attenuation or spatial dispersion. A profile with $f''=0$ is a trivial uniform or affine <displacement> field and cannot be used by itself to infer the wave-speed <eigenvalue> condition.

= Solution

A real symmetric $3\times3$ <matrix> has three real <eigenvalues> and a complete <orthonormal basis> of <eigenvectors>. For a stable elastic material, <strong ellipticity> makes $\mathbf a^{\mathsf T}B\mathbf a>0$ for every nonzero <polarization>, so its <eigenvalues> are positive squared wave <speeds>. There are therefore three orthogonal plane-wave <polarizations> in a given direction, with three potentially distinct <speeds> in an anisotropic medium. Degenerate <eigenvalues> allow arbitrary orthonormal combinations within their eigenspace; they do not remove <polarization> degrees of freedom. General anisotropic modes need not be exactly longitudinal or transverse.

For an <isotropic> medium let $\lambda=\kappa-2\mu/3$. Contract the <isotropic> stiffness with $\hat p_k\hat p_l$:
$$
B_{ij}=\frac1\rho\big[(\lambda+\mu)\hat p_i\hat p_j+\mu\delta_{ij}\big].
$$
Using
$$
\alpha^2=\frac{\kappa+4\mu/3}{\rho}=\frac{\lambda+2\mu}{\rho},\qquad\beta^2=\frac\mu\rho,
$$
gives
$$
\boxed{B_{ij}=(\alpha^2-\beta^2)\hat p_i\hat p_j+\beta^2\delta_{ij}.}
$$
For $\mathbf a\parallel\hat{\mathbf p}$ the longitudinal projector has <eigenvalue> one, so $B\mathbf a=\alpha^2\mathbf a$: this is the <P wave>. For either of two independent $\mathbf a\perp\hat{\mathbf p}$ the projector vanishes, so $B\mathbf a=\beta^2\mathbf a$: these are two degenerate transverse <S wave> <polarizations>. **An <isotropic> solid has one P <speed> and one S <speed>, but three <polarization> modes: one longitudinal and two transverse.** A fluid limit with zero <shear modulus> has no propagating <S wave>, so the positive-speed conclusion requires a solid with positive shear stiffness.

Use geometric rays for body-wave arrivals and a traction-free upper surface. A compact <isotropic explosion source> has moment tensor proportional to $\delta_{ij}$ and produces direct P body radiation in a homogeneous <isotropic> medium. Thus the basic near-offset arrival is the direct upper-layer <P wave>, with time $X/\alpha_1$. A pure explosion is not a shear fault: it does not automatically emit a direct <S wave> of comparable strength. Interfaces can nevertheless convert its P <energy> into SV motion, and the free surface can support <Rayleigh waves>.

Downward P <energy> reflects from the interface to give a PP <reflection> returning to the receiver; for a horizontal interface its ray is symmetric and its time is $\sqrt{X^2+4H^2}/\alpha_1$. Other transmitted P <energy> enters the faster half-space. At $\sin i_c=\alpha_1/\alpha_2$, a critical ray travels along the interface and feeds an upgoing <seismic head wave>, often labeled Pn in this simplified crustal model. Its ideal travel-time branch is
$$
t_{\rm head}=\frac{X}{\alpha_2}+\frac{2H\cos i_c}{\alpha_1}.
$$
It requires enough offset for the two inclined upper-layer legs, $X\ge2H\tan i_c$. Once $X$ exceeds the larger <first-arrival crossover distance> $X_c=2H\sqrt{(\alpha_2+\alpha_1)/(\alpha_2-\alpha_1)}$, this branch arrives before the direct upper-layer wave because it spends most of the path in faster material. A transmitted ray descending into an otherwise uniform infinite half-space does not return as a separate <reflection> without an additional reflector.

\Image[paper-1-seismic-phases.png]
{title=Original two-layer P-ray sketches and illustrative direct, reflected and critically refracted travel-time branches}

At oblique incidence, the bonded-interface conditions couple P and SV <polarizations>. A downgoing P ray can return as a converted SV <reflection> in the upper layer, with common horizontal slowness $p=\sin i_P/\alpha_1=\sin i_S/\beta_1$. There are transmitted P/SV branches and, after further interaction with the free surface or interface, mixed-mode <reflections> and <layer reverberations>. Conversion coefficients depend on <density> and impedance as well as wave <speeds>, and some vanish at normal incidence. The supplied inequalities do not order $\beta_2$ against $\alpha_1$, so one cannot universally draw a P-incident critical head ray travelling at the lower-layer S <speed>: that direct conversion construction requires $\beta_2>\alpha_1$. A shear head-wave phase can be present if the appropriate SV <energy> and ray conditions are available, but is not an inevitable direct explosion arrival from the stated data alone.

At later times a shallow explosion can excite dispersive Rayleigh-wave trains, with coupled P-SV motion decaying with depth. Short wavelengths mainly sample the slow upper layer, whereas long wavelengths penetrate the lower half-space; group and phase <speeds> need not be equal. Multiple reflected/converted body waves can overlap this coda. The shear-speed contrast also permits Love-wave modes structurally, but SH motion is decoupled from P-SV in this flat <isotropic> model. **An ideal <isotropic> explosion does not excite <Love waves> here; appreciable SH/Love <energy> would require source asymmetry, scattering or a broken symmetry of the model.**

Exact amplitudes, resolved arrival separation and attenuation cannot be calculated without <densities>, source spectrum, damping and instrument response. The qualitative robust features are a near-offset direct P arrival, reflected and converted phases, a faster-lower-layer P head wave that can become first at long range, reverberations and P-SV surface-wave <energy>. The ray diagram uses declared illustrative <speed> ratios solely to show the geometry; they are not additional supplied material constants.
