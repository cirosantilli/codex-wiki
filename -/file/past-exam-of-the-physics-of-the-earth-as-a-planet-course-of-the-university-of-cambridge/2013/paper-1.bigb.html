= Solution
{parent=a}

In the <oceanic plate cooling model>, $T$ is <temperature>, $z$ height above the plate base, $a$ plate thickness, and $t$ age since formation at the spreading axis. With <half-spreading rate> $v$, a parcel at distance $x$ has $t=x/v$. The surface and basal <temperatures> are $T_0$ and $T_1$ respectively. The positive integer $n$ labels a sine <diffusion> mode. For constant <thermal diffusivity> $\kappa$,
$$
\boxed{\tau_0=\frac{a^2}{\pi^2\kappa}.}
$$
Substituting one sine mode into $T_t=\kappa T_{zz}$ fixes this normalization; $a^2/\kappa$ itself is the <diffusion> timescale before the factor $\pi^2$.

**Source qualification.** The printed transient coefficients have the wrong sign for the intended initially hot plate. Indeed, in the interior,
$$
\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{2(-1)^n}{n\pi}\sin\frac{n\pi z}{a}=-\frac za.
$$
Thus the literal zero-age profile is $T_0+\Delta T(1-2z/a)$, where $\Delta T=T_1-T_0$, rather than $T_1$. It would even approach $T_0-\Delta T$ from inside the upper boundary. The intended hot-ridge cooling solution reverses the transient sign, using $2(-1)^{n+1}/(n\pi)$. Both versions have the same boundary <temperatures> and old-age limit. Part (b) gives both surface-flux expressions; the physical ridge-elevation and ridge-push calculations explicitly use the hot initial plate. This sign is present in the original PDF as well as the held transcription.

At the top, **$T(a,t)=T_0$**. Ocean water provides a cold <thermal reservoir> relative to <mantle> <temperatures>, so a fixed surface <temperature> is a useful idealization. The model does not resolve near-axis hydrothermal circulation or spatially variable bottom-water <temperatures>.

= Solution

At the ridge axis, **$T(z,0)=T_1$ for $0<z<a$** in the intended cooling model. Upwelling and magmatic <heat> supply continually bring hot material into the newly formed plate. The abrupt meeting of this hot interior with the cold upper boundary is an idealized corner discontinuity, resolved diffusively for $t>0$. The printed series fails this <initial condition>, as shown in the parent Solution.

At the bottom, **$T(0,t)=T_1$**. Convecting <mantle> is treated as maintaining the basal <temperature> and supplying <heat> to the plate. This is a prescribed thermal boundary at fixed thickness, not a claim that the real mechanical base is an impermeable material interface.

Far from the ridge, **$T(z,\infty)=T_0+\Delta T(1-z/a)$**. Transients have decayed and the <heat equation> reduces to $T_{zz}=0$. A finite basal <heat> supply sustains this <gradient>. Thermal thickness, seafloor depth and surface <heat> flux therefore approach finite limits rather than continuing the laws of the <half-space cooling model> indefinitely.

= b
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Take positive <heat> flux upwards, along increasing $z$, and denote <thermal conductivity> by $k$. <Fourier's law> gives $q_s=-kT_z|_{z=a}$. Since $\cos(n\pi)=(-1)^n$, <differentiation> of the literal printed expression gives
$$
\boxed{q_{s,\rm printed}(t)=\frac{k\Delta T}{a}
\left[1-2\sum_{n=1}^\infty e^{-n^2t/\tau_0}\right].}
$$
Its negative young-age flux is another sign of the unphysical initial profile. For the explicitly corrected hot initial plate, the <surface heat flux of a cooling plate> is instead
$$
\boxed{q_s(t)=\frac{k\Delta T}{a}
\left[1+2\sum_{n=1}^\infty e^{-n^2t/\tau_0}\right].}
$$
The old-age limit is $k\Delta T/a$. For $t\ll\tau_0$, replacing the Gaussian sum by its leading <integral> gives $q_s\sim k\Delta T/\sqrt{\pi\kappa t}$, recovering the <half-space cooling model>. Neither $k$ nor $\kappa$ is numerically specified, so the age-dependent flux remains symbolic as requested.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Use the intended hot-ridge state and a linear law of <thermal expansion> about <mantle> <density> $\rho_m$ at $T_1$. Let $s$ be depth below the old seafloor. Its excess <density> is
$$
\delta\rho(s)=\rho_m\alpha\Delta T(1-s/a),\qquad 0<s<a,
$$
and its excess column <mass> is $\int_0^a\delta\rho\,ds=\rho_m\alpha\Delta T a/2$. <Isostasy> requires equal <pressure> at a compensation depth below both columns. Lowering the old seafloor by $h$ replaces rock by water, reducing column <mass> by $(\rho_m-\rho_w)h$. Therefore the <isostatic ridge elevation in a plate model> is
$$
\boxed{h=\frac{\rho_m\alpha\Delta T a}{2(\rho_m-\rho_w)}
=3.90\times10^3\,\mathrm m=3.9\,\mathrm{km}.}
$$
Here $\alpha\Delta T=0.052$; the result is a first-order estimate and neglects sediments, crustal-composition differences and flexural support. If one instead insists on the literal printed zero-age profile, its column is denser than the old plate and the formal relative elevation reverses sign, $-3.9\,\mathrm{km}$ to this order. **That profile cannot describe the elevated hot ridge posited by the question.**

= Solution

<Ridge push> is a horizontal <pressure> <force> integrated with depth, equivalently a difference in gravitational <potential energy> per horizontal <area>. It is not the weight of a slab multiplied by the surface slope at one chosen point.

For an explicit compensated-column derivation, let $y$ measure depth below the ridge seafloor; the old seafloor is at $y=h$. Set $b=\rho_m\alpha\Delta T$. The hot column has greater <pressure>. Its <pressure> excess over the old column is
$$
\Delta p(y)=\begin{cases}
g(\rho_m-\rho_w)y,&0<y<h,\\
\dfrac{gb}{2a}(a-s)^2,&y=h+s,\quad0<s<a,\\
0,&y>h+a.
\end{cases}
$$
The second line follows by integrating $\delta\rho(s)=b(1-s/a)$ upwards from the compensation level. The two expressions agree at $y=h$ because $(\rho_m-\rho_w)h=ba/2$. Integrating this positive <pressure> excess gives the <ridge-push force in a plate model>, directed away from the ridge:
$$
F=\int\Delta p\,dy
=\frac{\rho_mg\alpha\Delta T a^2}{6}
+\frac{g(\rho_m-\rho_w)h^2}{2}.
$$
Consistently retaining only first order in <thermal expansion> gives
$$
\boxed{F\simeq2.60\times10^{12}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}.}
$$
If the topographic term is retained within the same linear-density column model, it adds $1.52\times10^{11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$, giving **$F\simeq2.75\times10^{12}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$**. This is a useful small correction, not a claim that all other second-order material effects have been included. Both values use the intended hot initial plate, not the inconsistent printed zero-age profile.

= Solution
{parent=e}

The depth-averaged added horizontal compressive <stress> is $\bar\sigma=F/a$. Resolving its <traction> on a plane dipping at angle $\delta$ gives the <fault shear stress from a plate-integrated force>,
$$
\bar\tau=\bar\sigma\sin\delta\cos\delta,
\qquad
\boxed{|\bar\tau|=\frac{F}{2a}\simeq1.30\times10^7\,\mathrm{Pa}=13\,\mathrm{MPa}
\quad(\delta=45^\circ).}
$$
Keeping the previous topographic correction gives about $13.8\,\mathrm{MPa}$. This is the mean additional shear from <ridge push>, assuming the <force> is transmitted through thickness $a$ and resolving the nonisotropic <stress> increment. An isotropic lithostatic <pressure> adds normal <traction> but no shear. The <force> <integral> alone does not specify the local <stress> at each point on the <fault> or establish failure.

**Oceanic <magnetic anomalies>.** Newly formed oceanic <crust> contains magnetic minerals. As it cools through their <magnetic blocking temperatures>, it acquires <thermoremanent magnetization> recording the ambient field polarity. Continuing <seafloor spreading> carries that record away from the ridge. <Geomagnetic reversals> therefore generate approximately paired <marine magnetic stripes> parallel to the ridge, with older <crust> farther from its axis. A <magnetometer> measures the superposed field above this magnetized material; subtracting a reference field reveals <magnetic anomalies>. The anomaly sign and shape depend on <magnetization> direction, latitude, source depth and geometry, so a positive measured anomaly is not universally identical to normal rock polarity.<Developing the theory by United States Geological Survey>[Sections Magnetic striping and polar reversals, and Seafloor spreading and recycling of oceanic crust]{ref}

Matching a sequence of stripes to an independently dated reversal chronology gives plate ages and spreading histories. A stripe of width $w$ formed over interval $\Delta t$ implies <half-spreading rate> $v=w/\Delta t$ under steady symmetric spreading; full separation <speed> is $2v$. Unequal widths on opposite flanks can reflect asymmetric spreading or later deformation. Alteration, remagnetization and overlapping magnetic sources complicate inversion. **The sequence correlation is stronger evidence than an isolated stripe or a field-strength peak.**

**<Rayleigh-Bénard convection>.** Consider a horizontal fluid layer of thickness $d$, hotter below by $\Delta T>0$. The conductive state is motionless with a linear <temperature> <gradient>. A rising warm parcel is buoyant, but <viscosity> resists its motion and thermal <diffusion> erases its <temperature> anomaly. In the <Boussinesq approximation>, <density> variations are retained in <buoyancy> while the flow is otherwise incompressible. The competing effects are measured by the <Rayleigh number> and <Prandtl number>,
$$
\operatorname{Ra}=\frac{g\alpha\Delta T d^3}{\nu\kappa},\qquad
\operatorname{Pr}=\frac\nu\kappa.
$$
For stress-free impermeable, fixed-temperature boundaries, linear stationary disturbances with horizontal <wavenumber> $q/d$ give
$$
\operatorname{Ra}(q)=\frac{(q^2+\pi^2)^3}{q^2}.
$$
Minimizing gives $q_c=\pi/\sqrt2$ and the <critical Rayleigh number for an isothermal layer>, $\operatorname{Ra}_c=27\pi^4/4\simeq657.5$. Two rigid no-slip, fixed-temperature boundaries instead give approximately $1708$; the critical value is not independent of <boundary conditions>.<Boundary layer turbulence by Massachusetts Institute of Technology>[Chapter 20, §20.4, critical value for two rigid isothermal plates]{ref}

Above onset, <buoyancy> can sustain overturning rolls and <heat> transfer exceeds pure conduction, with <Nusselt number> $\operatorname{Nu}>1$. Nonlinear advection eventually limits the disturbance; larger driving can produce time-dependent or turbulent states. The ideal Boussinesq problem does not require hexagonal cells: planform selection depends on boundaries and non-Boussinesq effects. <Convection> driven by <surface tension> at a <free surface> is a distinct mechanism. **An adverse <temperature> <gradient> supplies driving, but overcoming <diffusion> and <viscosity> requires a finite threshold.**

**<Earthquake> location.** The <hypocentre> is rupture initiation; its surface projection is the <epicentre>. Record calibrated arrival times at stations of known coordinates, identify <P wave> and <S wave> phases, and predict their <travel times> through a stated <velocity> model. <Earthquake location by arrival times> fits
$$
t_i=t_0+T_i(\mathbf x),
$$
for the three source coordinates and origin time $t_0$. <Linearization> supplies residual equations $\delta t_i=\delta t_0+\nabla T_i\cdot\delta\mathbf x$. A grid search or iterative <weighted least squares> inversion can use pick uncertainties, while station geometry controls whether those parameters are well constrained. Four independent arrival constraints are the minimum for four unknowns, not a guarantee of a reliable solution.

In a homogeneous medium, an arrival-time difference between an <S wave> and a <P wave> cancels origin time and estimates source-to-station distance,
$$
r=\frac{t_S-t_P}{1/v_S-1/v_P}.
$$
Several distances intersect to locate the event; real layered or three-dimensional <Earth> structure requires curved-ray <travel times>. Nearby stations and diverse takeoff angles help resolve depth. Clock errors, phase misidentification, poor azimuthal coverage and incorrect <velocities> introduce uncertainty or systematic bias. Closely spaced <earthquakes> can be relocated precisely relative to each other because common-path errors partly cancel, even when their absolute locations remain uncertain. **Report a location uncertainty and model assumptions, not merely a best-fit point.**

**<H. F. Reid>'s earthquake-cycle model.** A <fault> can remain locked while long-term tectonic motion slowly deforms the surrounding rocks. Elastic <strain> <energy> and <shear stress> accumulate. When resistance is overcome, rapid <fault slip> releases part of that stored <energy> as seismic waves, frictional <heat> and other irreversible <work>. The adjoining rocks partly recover their elastic distortion while retaining a permanent displacement discontinuity across the <fault>: this is <elastic rebound>. Surface markers initially crossing the <fault> become bent during loading and offset after slip.<Reid's Elastic Rebound Theory by United States Geological Survey>[Explanation of gradual strain accumulation, sudden release and the displaced fence; cites Reid's 1910 report, especially pp. 16–28]{ref}

A simple sketch is a slow rise of <stress> followed by a rapid <stress> drop, then renewed loading; the plate displacement continues throughout. Geodetic deformation, observed <fault> offsets and <earthquake> <stress> changes can test the picture. Actual cycles need not repeat with identical slips or times: creep, afterslip, <stress> transfer and heterogeneous <friction> complicate the idealization. The release need not remove all pre-existing <stress>. **<Elastic rebound> explains storage and release; it is not a deterministic <earthquake> clock.**

For <group velocity from a period-dependent phase velocity>, start with $k=\omega/c_n(\omega)$ rather than equating <group velocity> to <phase velocity>. Differentiating,
$$
\frac1{u_n}=\frac{dk}{d\omega}=\frac1{c_n}-\frac{\omega}{c_n^2}\frac{dc_n}{d\omega}.
$$
For the stated inverse-frequency term, $dc_n/d\omega=-a/\omega^2$, so
$$
\boxed{\frac{u_n}{c_n}=\frac1{1+a/(\omega c_n)}
=\frac{c_n}{c_n+a/\omega},\qquad
u_n=\frac{c_n^2}{c_n+a/\omega}<c_n.}
$$
The inequality assumes positive <frequency> and physical positive <phase velocity>, with $a>0$. The symbol $a$ here has units of acceleration and is unrelated to the plate thickness in Question 1.

= Solution

Write the oscillation <period> as $P=2\pi/\omega$, so locally $c_n=c_o+(a/2\pi)P$. The <first overtone> is the lowest curve above the fundamental, not the <fundamental mode> itself. Near $P=50\,\mathrm s$, Figure 1 gives approximately $c_1=5.13\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Representative nearby readings are about $4.96\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ at $40\,\mathrm s$ and $5.30\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ at $60\,\mathrm s$. Thus
$$
\left.\frac{dc_1}{dP}\right|_{50\,\mathrm s}\simeq0.017\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-2}},\qquad
\boxed{a\simeq2\pi(0.017)\simeq0.11\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-2}}
\simeq1.1\times10^2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}.}
$$
The local group-velocity estimate is
$$
\boxed{u_1\simeq\frac{5.13}{1+50(0.017)/5.13}
\simeq4.4\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
These are graphical estimates, roughly $a=0.11\pm0.02\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-2}}$ and $u_1=4.4\pm0.1\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, rather than high-precision fitted constants. The nearby straight-line approximation need not describe that mode over the whole plotted <period> range. No source curve is reproduced here.

= Solution

The <phase velocity> in Figure 1 is measured along the <Earth>'s surface. Spherical geometry makes the local horizontal phase <speed> at radius $r$ equal to $cr/R_E$. In the ray approximation, squared radial shear <wavenumber> is
$$
q_r^2=\omega^2\left[\frac1{\beta(r)^2}-\frac{R_E^2}{c^2r^2}\right].
$$
The <spherical surface-wave evanescence threshold> immediately below the discontinuity is therefore
$$
\boxed{c<5.08\frac{6371}{6371-410}
\simeq5.43\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
Equality is the grazing limit. Using $5.08\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ directly would omit the requested spherical correction. Immediately above the discontinuity the corresponding boundary is $4.87(6371/5961)\simeq5.20\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. Hence the interval $5.20<c<5.43\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ specifically describes propagation just above the jump and evanescence just below it. Slower modes can already be evanescent above $410\,\mathrm{km}$.

Intersecting the plotted branches with $5.43\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ gives the following approximate upper <periods>. Below each upper <period>, that branch is evanescent immediately beneath the discontinuity:

* <fundamental mode>: throughout the displayed $10$–$100\,\mathrm s$ range;
* first and second <overtones>: approximately $67$ and $38\,\mathrm s$;
* successive higher curves: approximately $27$, $21$, $18$, $15$, $13$ and $11\,\mathrm s$.

For these <overtones> the displayed ranges thus start near $10\,\mathrm s$ and end at their respective crossings. Any still higher curve already above the threshold at the lower plotted <period> has no evanescent interval within the displayed range. Readings of the closely spaced higher curves are only accurate to about a second, and the plot does not establish their continuation to unshown <periods>. Nor does the local inequality prove that a mode remains evanescent at every greater depth in an unspecified <Earth> model.

= Solution

Use the flat two-layer model, with thickness $h=30\,\mathrm{km}$, crustal shear <speed> $\beta_1=3.5\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ and <mantle> <speed> $\beta_2=4.5\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. For <SH-wave travel times in a crustal waveguide>, unfolding each reflected path gives all <reflection> orders at once:
$$
\boxed{t_D=\frac x{3.5},\qquad
t_m=\frac{\sqrt{x^2+(60m)^2}}{3.5},\quad m=1,2,\ldots,}
$$
with $x$ in kilometres and times in seconds. The direct wave begins at the origin; reflected orders have intercepts $60m/3.5=17.14m\,\mathrm s$ and approach the direct-wave slope at large distance.

The critical angle, measured from the vertical, is $i_c=\arcsin(3.5/4.5)\simeq51.1^\circ$. Associated critical <refraction> and its multiples give
$$
\boxed{t_{H,m}=\frac x{4.5}+2mh\sqrt{\beta_1^{-2}-\beta_2^{-2}}
=\frac x{4.5}+10.77m,\qquad
x\geq x_{c,m}=2mh\tan i_c\simeq74.25m\,\mathrm{km}.}
$$
Each head-wave line is tangent to its <reflection> curve at the critical distance. In particular the primary head wave exists before it becomes the first arrival; it overtakes the direct wave only around $170\,\mathrm{km}$.

The slope of a reflected travel-time curve is $p=dt_m/dx=\sin i/\beta_1$. For $x<x_{c,m}$, $p<1/\beta_2$, so the <mantle> supports outgoing shear radiation and successive reverberations leak <energy>. For $x>x_{c,m}$, $1/\beta_2<p<1/\beta_1$: the <mantle> field is evanescent and ideal interface <reflection> is total. These <leaking and trapped SH-wave reverberations> explain the annotations in the original travel-time drawing below. A reflected ray is not itself a separate Love mode: constructive interference of the trapped contributions, including <reflection> phase, selects the <normal modes>.

For a horizontally propagating mode with $\beta_1<c<\beta_2$, define vertical <wavenumbers>
$$
q=\omega\sqrt{\beta_1^{-2}-c^{-2}},\qquad
s=\omega\sqrt{c^{-2}-\beta_2^{-2}}.
$$
The free upper surface and welded interface impose the Love-wave <dispersion> condition
$$
\tan(qh)=\frac{\mu_2s}{\mu_1q},\qquad
qh=n\pi+\arctan\frac{\mu_2s}{\mu_1q},\quad n=0,1,2,\ldots,
$$
where $\mu_j=\rho_j\beta_j^2$ are shear moduli. Each phase-velocity branch falls towards $3.5\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ with increasing <frequency>. Higher branches join $4.5\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ at their low-frequency cutoffs. At cutoff $s\to0$, so
$$
\boxed{f_{c,n}=\frac{n}{2h\sqrt{\beta_1^{-2}-\beta_2^{-2}}}
\simeq0.0928n\,\mathrm{Hz}.}
$$
Thus the first three <overtones> have maximum <periods> about $10.77$, $5.39$ and $3.59\,\mathrm s$. The <fundamental mode> has $n=0$ and **no positive-frequency cutoff in this flat layer-over-half-space model**: it approaches $c=\beta_2$ as $f\to0$, with ever-deeper penetration. The phase-velocity sketch uses an explicitly illustrative equal-density choice, because the question gives <speeds> but no <density> or rigidity ratio. That missing ratio changes detailed branch curvature, not the cutoff values or the requested branch topology.

For the actual finite spherical <Earth>, the very-long-period continuation must instead be described by discrete toroidal <normal modes>, not a flat infinite <waveguide> at arbitrary <wavelength>. The flat-model answer therefore does not assert a continuum of arbitrarily low-frequency global nonrigid oscillations. A global lowest oscillation <frequency> cannot be computed from the supplied crust-and-half-space data alone.<Essentials of Geophysics: Seismology by R. D. van der Hilst>[§4.18, Love-wave trapping; §4.23, finite spherical Earth and discrete normal modes, especially printed pp. 184–187]{ref}
