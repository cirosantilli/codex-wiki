= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $Z=(Z_n)_{n\geq0}$ be a <Markov chain> with <transition kernel> $K$, and let $N$ be an independent <Poisson process> of constant rate $r>0$. A <pseudo-Poisson process> is $X_t=Z_{N_t}$. On the <Banach space> $B_b(E)$ of bounded <Borel measurable functions>, with the <supremum norm>, write
$$
(Kf)(x)=\int_E f(y)K(x,dy),\qquad K^0=I.
$$
Its <infinitesimal generator>, <transition semigroup>, and <resolvent operator> are respectively
$$
\boxed{L=r(K-I),\qquad D(L)=B_b(E),}
$$
$$
\boxed{P_t=e^{tL}=e^{-rt}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(rt)^n}{n!}K^n,}
$$
and, for $\lambda>0$,
$$
\boxed{R_\lambda=\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t}P_t\,dt
=\frac1{\lambda+r}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac r{\lambda+r}\right)^nK^n
=(\lambda I-L)^{-1}.}
$$
The <transition semigroup> averages $K^n$ against the <Poisson distribution> of the number of updates. Since $K$ is a <Markov operator>, $\|K\|\leq1$; both series converge in <operator norm>. Updates with $Z_{n+1}=Z_n$ are allowed and do not count as changes of the observed state. The degenerate rate $r=0$ gives $L=0$, $P_t=I$, and $R_\lambda=I/\lambda$.

**The displayed identity requires a correction to the process construction in the source.** In a <piecewise-deterministic Markov process>, the instantaneous <hazard function> is evaluated at the current flowing state $X_{u-}$, and the destination is sampled from $K_u(X_{u-},dy)$. Keeping the jump times and destinations of the original <Markov jump process> $Y$ instead evaluates both quantities at its last reset state $Y_{u-}$, which generally differs from $X_{u-}$. The source PDF confirms that these original jump times and destinations were retained. Consequently its definition does not in general produce the asserted <transition kernel>, or even a <Markov process> in the state variable $X$ alone.

For an explicit counterexample, take $a(t,x)=1$, $\alpha(x)=2+\tanh x$, $y_0=0$, and let every $K_t(x,dy)$ be the <standard normal distribution>, independently of $t,x$. These are smooth coefficients with bounded positive jump rate, so <nonexplosion> holds. In the literal construction, the first holding time has the <exponential distribution> of rate $2$. Thus the <probability distribution> of $X_t$ has an atom of mass $e^{-2t}$ at $t$. Paths with at least one jump contribute no atom there, because the reset has a <normal density>. The asserted identity instead assigns to this no-jump atom the mass
$$
\exp\left(-\int_0^t(2+\tanh u)\,du\right)=\frac{e^{-2t}}{\cosh t},
$$
which differs for every $t>0$.

Here is the full derivation for the intended <piecewise-deterministic Markov process>. Assume <nonexplosion>; smoothness alone does not guarantee it. Start at $(s,x)$, let $\tau$ be the first jump after $s$, and set
$$
z(u)=\varphi(s,x,u),\qquad H(u)=\int_s^u\alpha(z(r))\,dr.
$$
One precise construction draws an independent unit-rate <exponential random variable> $E$ and takes $\tau=\inf\{u\geq s:H(u)\geq E\}$, with $\inf\varnothing=\infty$. This implements a <hazard function> along the <flow of a vector field>, rather than a fixed exponential holding time at the last reset. Its <survival probability> and jump-time <probability density function> are
$$
S(u)=\mathbb P_{s,x}(\tau>u)=e^{-H(u)},\qquad
-\partial_u S(u)=\alpha(z(u))e^{-H(u)}.
$$
Equivalently, $S'=-\alpha(z)S$ with $S(s)=1$. On $\{\tau>t\}$, the terminal state is $z(t)$. Conditional on $\tau=u$, draw the new state $y$ from $K_u(z(u),dy)$ and restart the same construction from $(u,y)$. The <Markov property> and the <tower property of conditional expectation> therefore give the <first-jump decomposition of a piecewise-deterministic Markov process>
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
(P_{s,t}f)(x)
&=e^{-H(t)}f(z(t))\\
&\quad+\int_s^t\alpha(z(u))e^{-H(u)}
\int_{\mathbb R}(P_{u,t}f)(y)K_u(z(u),dy)\,du.
\end{aligned}}
$$
This proves the requested identity under the corrected construction, for every bounded <Borel measurable function> $f$. The two contributions partition according to whether the first jump occurs before $t$; later jumps are already contained in $P_{u,t}$.

For comparison, the literal construction does have a different renewal identity when started at a reset with $X_s=Y_s=x$. If $Q_{s,t}$ denotes only these reset-start operators, the same <conditional expectation> argument gives
$$
(Q_{s,t}f)(x)=e^{-\alpha(x)(t-s)}f(\varphi(s,x,t))
+\int_s^t\alpha(x)e^{-\alpha(x)(u-s)}(K_uQ_{u,t}f)(x)\,du.
$$
These $Q_{s,t}$ need not be the <transition operators> conditional on $X_s=x$ at an arbitrary time: that observation does not specify the hidden reset state. This distinction explains exactly why replacing only the between-jump paths is insufficient.

The identity for the <killed deterministic flow> follows directly from the <chain rule> and the <fundamental theorem of calculus>, independently of the construction issue in part (b). For a continuously differentiable $f$, put $z(t)=\varphi(s,x,t)$ and $H(t)=\int_s^t\alpha(\varphi(s,x,u))\,du$. Then
$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_t(P^\alpha_{s,t}f)(x)
&=e^{-H(t)}\bigl[f'(z(t))\partial_tz(t)-\alpha(z(t))f(z(t))\bigr]\\
&=e^{-H(t)}\bigl[a(t,z(t))f'(z(t))-\alpha(z(t))f(z(t))\bigr]\\
&=(P^\alpha_{s,t}L_t^\alpha f)(x).
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence, on such <test functions>,
$$
\boxed{\partial_tP^\alpha_{s,t}=P^\alpha_{s,t}L_t^\alpha,\qquad
L_t^\alpha f=a(t,\cdot)f'-\alpha f.}
$$
The time subscript on $L_t^\alpha$ is necessary because the <vector field> may depend on time; it is suppressed on the right-hand side of the source's displayed operator identity. The <killed deterministic flow> transports an observable along the <flow of a vector field> and multiplies it by the no-jump <survival probability>.

Use the corrected <piecewise-deterministic Markov process> from part (b). For a smooth <test function> $f$, its time-dependent <infinitesimal generator> is
$$
(L_tf)(x)=a(t,x)f'(x)+\alpha(x)\left[\int_{\mathbb R}f(y)m_t(x,y)\,dy-f(x)\right].
$$
Indeed, over an interval of length $h$, deterministic motion contributes $ha(t,x)f'(x)$, while a jump has <probability> $h\alpha(x)+o(h)$ and changes the conditional mean of $f$ by $K_tf(x)-f(x)$. Terms involving both drift and a jump contribute only to the remainder. Thus $P_{t,t+h}f=f+hL_tf+o(h)$.

The <Dynkin formula>, or this infinitesimal expansion integrated against the current <probability distribution>, gives the weak evolution identity
$$
\frac d{dt}\int_{\mathbb R}f(x)p_t(x)\,dx
=\int_{\mathbb R}(L_tf)(x)p_t(x)\,dx.
$$
Take $f\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb R)$, so <integration by parts> has no boundary contribution. Assuming the displayed jump integrals are finite, <Fubini's theorem> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{\mathbb R}a(t,x)f'(x)p_t(x)\,dx
&=-\int_{\mathbb R}f(x)\partial_x\bigl(a(t,x)p_t(x)\bigr)\,dx,\\
\int_{\mathbb R}\alpha(y)p_t(y)\int_{\mathbb R}f(x)m_t(y,x)\,dx\,dy
&=\int_{\mathbb R}f(x)\int_{\mathbb R}\alpha(y)m_t(y,x)p_t(y)\,dy\,dx.
\end{aligned}
$$
Consequently the <forward equation of a piecewise-deterministic Markov process> is
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\partial_tp_t(x)=(A_tp_t)(x)
&=-\partial_x\bigl(a(t,x)p_t(x)\bigr)-\alpha(x)p_t(x)\\
&\quad+\int_{\mathbb R}\alpha(y)m_t(y,x)p_t(y)\,dy.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Here $A_t=L_t^*$ is the formal <adjoint operator> acting on <probability density functions>. Its terms are transport, loss from $x$, and incoming mass from every $y$. In particular, the incoming rate is $\alpha(y)$, evaluated at the departure state. The operator denoted $A$ in the source is generally time-dependent. Because $\int m_t(y,x)\,dx=1$, the gain and loss integrals cancel; with vanishing transport flux at infinity, $\frac d{dt}\int p_t=0$.

**The deterministic initial state in part (b) does not actually yield an everywhere smooth full density.** For finite times the no-jump contribution is the positive measure
$$
\exp\left(-\int_0^t\alpha(\varphi(0,y_0,u))\,du\right)
\delta_{\varphi(0,y_0,t)},
$$
where $\delta_z$ is the <Dirac measure> at $z$. Smooth jump destination <probability density functions> cannot remove this positive atom. The density equation above is therefore conditional on the extra density hypothesis, for example with a suitable random initial <probability density function>. For the specified fixed initial state, write $\nu_t$ for the whole <probability measure> and use instead
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dt}\int f\,d\nu_t=\int L_tf\,d\nu_t,\qquad \nu_0=\delta_{y_0}.}
$$
This weak measure evolution includes both the atom and any continuous component. It becomes the displayed <partial differential equation> whenever $\nu_t(dx)=p_t(x)\,dx$ with the assumed regularity. It does not describe the literal hidden-reset construction without the correction in part (b).

For the <stochastic differential equation> $dX_s=b(s,X_s)\,ds+\Sigma(s,X_s)\,dB_s$, the <diffusion generator> on smooth functions is
$$
(\mathcal L_sg)(x)=\sum_jb_j(s,x)\partial_jg(x)
+\frac12\sum_{j,k}(\Sigma\Sigma^{\mathsf T})_{jk}(s,x)\partial_{jk}g(x).
$$
One form of the <Feynman-Kac formula> is the following. Suppose the <stochastic differential equation> is well posed and nonexplosive, $c\geq0$ is bounded, the terminal data $g$ and source $h$ are bounded, and $F$ is a <classical solution> bounded on finite time intervals of
$$
\partial_tF+\mathcal L_tF-cF+h=0,\qquad F(T,x)=g(x).
$$
With the regularity needed for the <Itô formula>, the <parabolic Feynman-Kac formula with a source> states
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
F(t,x)=\mathbb E_{t,x}\bigg[&e^{-\int_t^Tc(r,X_r)\,dr}g(X_T)\\
&+\int_t^Te^{-\int_t^sc(r,X_r)\,dr}h(s,X_s)\,ds\bigg].
\end{aligned}}
$$
Here $\mathbb E_{t,x}$ means expectation for the <diffusion process> started at $X_t=x$. Under hypotheses ensuring existence of a <classical solution>, this representation also identifies that solution uniquely in the stated bounded class. In the remaining parts, $c=h=0$, so $F(t,x)=\mathbb E_{t,x}g(X_T)$ solves the terminal-value <Kolmogorov backward equation> $F_t+\mathcal L_tF=0$. Complex terminal data are allowed by applying the real formula to their <real part> and <imaginary part> separately.

= Solution

The <diffusion generator> is $\mathcal L=\mu\partial_x+\tfrac12\sigma^2\partial_{xx}$. By the <Feynman-Kac formula>, the required <conditional characteristic function> solves
$$
F_t+\mu F_x+\tfrac12\sigma^2F_{xx}=0,\qquad F(T,x)=e^{iux}.
$$
Use <separation of variables> in the form $F(t,x)=e^{iux}h(t)$. Since $F_x=iuF$ and $F_{xx}=-u^2F$, the temporal factor satisfies the <ordinary differential equation>
$$
h'(t)+\left(iu\mu-\frac12\sigma^2u^2\right)h(t)=0,\qquad h(T)=1.
$$
Thus $h(t)=\exp[(iu\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2u^2)(T-t)]$, and
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[e^{iuX_T}\mid X_t=x]
=\exp\left(iu[x+\mu(T-t)]-\frac12u^2\sigma^2(T-t)\right).}
$$
This is bounded in absolute value by one, so it lies in the uniqueness class of the <Feynman-Kac formula>. As an independent check, integrating the <stochastic differential equation> gives $X_T=x+\mu(T-t)+\sigma(B_T-B_t)$. The independent <Brownian increments> have the <normal distribution> with mean zero and <variance> $T-t$, yielding exactly the same <characteristic function>. For $\sigma=0$, the <conditional distribution> is a point mass at $x+\mu(T-t)$, and the same boxed expression still applies.

Here the <diffusion generator> is $\mathcal L=ax\partial_x+\tfrac12\sigma^2\partial_{xx}$. The <Feynman-Kac formula> gives
$$
F_t+axF_x+\tfrac12\sigma^2F_{xx}=0,\qquad F(T,x)=e^{iux}.
$$
Try $F(t,x)=\exp[iu\alpha(t)x+\beta(t)]$, where $\alpha(T)=1$ and $\beta(T)=0$. Substitution and collection of the coefficients of $x$ and the constant term give
$$
\alpha'(t)+a\alpha(t)=0,\qquad
\beta'(t)=\frac12\sigma^2u^2\alpha(t)^2.
$$
The solutions of these <ordinary differential equations>, with the terminal conditions imposed, are
$$
\alpha(t)=e^{a(T-t)},\qquad
\beta(t)=-\frac12\sigma^2u^2\int_t^Te^{2a(T-s)}\,ds.
$$
For $\Delta=T-t$, define the conditional <variance>
$$
v_\Delta=\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^2}{2a}(e^{2a\Delta}-1),&a\ne0,\\
\sigma^2\Delta,&a=0.
\end{cases}
$$
Then the required <conditional characteristic function> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[e^{iuX_T}\mid X_t=x]
=\exp\left(iue^{a\Delta}x-\frac12u^2v_\Delta\right).}
$$
In particular, for $a\ne0$ this exponent is $iue^{a\Delta}x-\frac{\sigma^2u^2}{4a}(e^{2a\Delta}-1)$. The <variance> $v_\Delta$ is nonnegative for either sign of $a$, and its limit at $a=0$ is $\sigma^2\Delta$. Hence the solution is bounded in absolute value by one and the <Feynman-Kac formula> identifies it with the required expectation. The value $u=0$ gives one directly, including that case without dividing by $u$.

For an independent check, the <integrating factor> $e^{-as}$ and the <Itô product rule> give
$$
X_T=e^{a\Delta}x+\sigma\int_t^Te^{a(T-s)}\,dB_s.
$$
The <stochastic integral> has a <normal distribution> because its integrand is deterministic; the <Itô isometry> gives its <variance> as $v_\Delta$. For $a<0$ this is the <Ornstein-Uhlenbeck transition distribution>. The formula also covers the unstable case $a>0$, and degenerates to deterministic motion when $\sigma=0$.

= Solution

For $a<0$, set $v_\infty=-\sigma^2/(2a)>0$. The candidate <invariant probability law of a Markov process> is
$$
\boxed{\pi=\mathcal N\left(0,-\frac{\sigma^2}{2a}\right),\qquad
\pi(dx)=\sqrt{\frac{-a}{\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\left(\frac{ax^2}{\sigma^2}\right)\,dx.}
$$
To prove invariance rather than merely guess a limiting <probability distribution>, initialize $X_t\sim\pi$ independently of future <Brownian increments>. Integrating the <conditional characteristic function> from part (i) against this centered <normal distribution> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[e^{iuX_{t+\Delta}}]
&=e^{-u^2v_\Delta/2}\,\mathbb E[e^{iu e^{a\Delta}X_t}]\\
&=\exp\left[-\frac12u^2\left(v_\Delta+e^{2a\Delta}v_\infty\right)\right]
=e^{-u^2v_\infty/2},
\end{aligned}
$$
because $v_\Delta=v_\infty(1-e^{2a\Delta})$. The <uniqueness theorem for characteristic functions> implies $X_{t+\Delta}\sim\pi$ for every $\Delta\geq0$. This proves $\pi P_\Delta=\pi$, as required. The <time-homogeneous Markov property> then also makes the process with this <Ornstein-Uhlenbeck stationary initialization> a <strictly stationary process>.

Let $E$ be the underlying <Banach space>. By the <Laplace-transform formula for a semigroup resolvent>,
$$
R_\lambda f=\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda s}P_sf\,ds,\qquad
\|R_\lambda\|\leq\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda s}\,ds=\frac1\lambda.
$$
These are <Bochner integrals>, defined for every $f\in E$ and $\lambda>0$. The <contraction semigroup> bound and <semigroup property> give
$$
R_\lambda R_\mu f
=\int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda s-\mu t}P_{s+t}f\,dt\,ds.
$$
Interchanging and regrouping these <Bochner integrals> is justified by the integrable bound $e^{-\lambda s-\mu t}\|f\|$, whose integral is $\|f\|/(\lambda\mu)$. For $\lambda\ne\mu$, set $v=s+t$ and evaluate the inner scalar integral:
$$
\begin{aligned}
R_\lambda R_\mu f
&=\int_0^\infty\left(\int_0^ve^{-\lambda s-\mu(v-s)}\,ds\right)P_vf\,dv\\
&=\frac1{\mu-\lambda}\int_0^\infty(e^{-\lambda v}-e^{-\mu v})P_vf\,dv\\
&=\frac{R_\lambda f-R_\mu f}{\mu-\lambda}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Since $f$ was arbitrary, the <resolvent identity> follows:
$$
\boxed{R_\lambda-R_\mu=(\mu-\lambda)R_\lambda R_\mu.}
$$
For $\lambda=\mu$, both sides vanish, so that case holds as well.

For $\lambda\ne\mu$, apply the <resolvent identity> from part (i) in both orders:
$$
R_\lambda R_\mu=\frac{R_\lambda-R_\mu}{\mu-\lambda},\qquad
R_\mu R_\lambda=\frac{R_\mu-R_\lambda}{\lambda-\mu}.
$$
The two quotients are equal. For $\lambda=\mu$ the assertion is immediate. Hence
$$
\boxed{R_\lambda R_\mu=R_\mu R_\lambda\qquad(\lambda,\mu>0).}
$$
Thus <resolvent operators> from the same <strongly continuous semigroup> commute. This conclusion does not require commuting arbitrary <bounded linear operators>; it uses the fact that both arise from the same <semigroup generator>.

A second-order <nondivergence-form elliptic operator> on a domain $D\subseteq\mathbb R^d$ has the form
$$
\mathcal Af(x)=\sum_{j,k=1}^d a_{jk}(x)\partial_{jk}f(x)
+\sum_{j=1}^db_j(x)\partial_jf(x)+c(x)f(x).
$$
Only the symmetric part of $a$ affects the <Hessian matrix> term, so one may take $a(x)$ symmetric. The operator is a <uniformly elliptic operator> if there is a constant $\kappa>0$, independent of $x$, such that
$$
\boxed{\sum_{j,k=1}^da_{jk}(x)\xi_j\xi_k\geq\kappa|\xi|^2
\qquad(x\in D,\ \xi\in\mathbb R^d).}
$$
A common bounded-coefficient convention additionally requires an upper bound $\sum a_{jk}(x)\xi_j\xi_k\leq\Lambda|\xi|^2$ with $\Lambda<\infty$. The lower bound says that the principal <quadratic form> cannot degenerate in any direction or at any location.

For an explicit stochastic example, standard $d$-dimensional <Brownian motion> has <infinitesimal generator>
$$
\boxed{\mathcal A=\tfrac12\Delta,\qquad a_{jk}=\tfrac12\delta_{jk},\quad b=0,\quad c=0.}
$$
Its principal <quadratic form> is $|\xi|^2/2$, so it is <uniformly elliptic> with $\kappa=\Lambda=1/2$. More generally, a sufficiently regular <stochastic differential equation> $dX_t=b(X_t)\,dt+\Sigma(X_t)\,dB_t$ has <diffusion generator> $b\cdot\nabla+\tfrac12(\Sigma\Sigma^{\mathsf T}):D^2$; it is <uniformly elliptic> when $\Sigma\Sigma^{\mathsf T}$ has a uniformly positive lower bound. A conservative <diffusion process> has no zeroth-order term, since its <infinitesimal generator> annihilates constants.

= Solution

The convergence is in the <strong operator topology>: for each fixed $f\in E$, one must prove $\|\lambda R_\lambda f-f\|\to0$. First, the <contraction semigroup> bound gives
$$
\|\lambda R_\lambda f\|
\leq\int_0^\infty\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\|P_tf\|\,dt
\leq\|f\|.
$$
Thus **$\lambda R_\lambda$ is a contraction**. This is the useful scaling of the contraction estimate in the source's hint. Since $\int_0^\infty\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\,dt=1$,
$$
\lambda R_\lambda f-f
=\int_0^\infty\lambda e^{-\lambda t}(P_tf-f)\,dt.
$$
Fix $\varepsilon>0$. By <strong continuity>, choose $\delta>0$ so that $\|P_tf-f\|<\varepsilon$ for $0\leq t\leq\delta$. Split the integral there and use $\|P_tf-f\|\leq2\|f\|$ for the tail:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\|\lambda R_\lambda f-f\|
&\leq\varepsilon\int_0^\delta\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\,dt
+2\|f\|\int_\delta^\infty\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\,dt\\
&\leq\varepsilon+2\|f\|e^{-\lambda\delta}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Let $\lambda\to\infty$ and then $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\lambda R_\lambda f\longrightarrow f\text{ in }E\quad\text{for every }f\in E.}
$$
The distinction from <operator norm> convergence is essential. For example, the <multiplication semigroup> on $\ell^2(\mathbb N)$ defined by $(P_tf)_n=e^{-nt}f_n$ is a <strongly continuous semigroup> of contractions: coordinatewise convergence and <dominated convergence> applied to $\sum_n|f_n|^2$ prove <strong continuity>. Its <resolvent operator> is $(R_\lambda f)_n=f_n/(\lambda+n)$, and
$$
\|\lambda R_\lambda-I\|=\sup_{n\geq1}\frac n{\lambda+n}=1.
$$
Thus the claimed convergence cannot in general be read as convergence in <operator norm>.

= Solution

Take $f$ in the <generator domain> $D(L)$. The <semigroup commutation with its unbounded generator> implies $P_tf\in D(L)$ and
$$
\frac d{dt}P_tf=P_tLf=LP_tf.
$$
Indeed, applying the bounded operator $P_t$ to the difference quotient at zero gives the right derivative $P_tLf$; <strong continuity> gives the full orbit derivative for $t>0$. Now <integration by parts> in the <Bochner integral> yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
R_\lambda Lf
&=\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t}P_tLf\,dt
=\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t}\frac d{dt}P_tf\,dt\\
&=\left[e^{-\lambda t}P_tf\right]_0^\infty
+\lambda\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t}P_tf\,dt
=-f+\lambda R_\lambda f.
\end{aligned}
$$
The boundary term at infinity vanishes because $\|e^{-\lambda t}P_tf\|\leq e^{-\lambda t}\|f\|$. The derivative integral exists as a <Bochner integral>, since its integrand is bounded in <norm> by $e^{-\lambda t}\|Lf\|$.

Multiplying this identity by $\lambda$ gives
$$
\lambda(\lambda R_\lambda-I)f=\lambda R_\lambda(Lf).
$$
Part (b) applies to the fixed vector $Lf\in E$, so
$$
\boxed{\lambda(\lambda R_\lambda-I)f\longrightarrow Lf
\quad\text{in }E\qquad(f\in D(L)).}
$$
The bounded operators $L_\lambda=\lambda^2R_\lambda-\lambda I$ are the <Yosida approximation> of the <semigroup generator>. The proof only applies the unbounded operator $L$ to $f\in D(L)$; the contraction and limit arguments then act on the ordinary vector $Lf$.
