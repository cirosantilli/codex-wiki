= Enthalpy
{title2=$H=U+pV$}
{wiki}

Enthalpy is the <state function> obtained by adding <pressure> times <volume (thermodynamics)> to <internal energy>:
$$
H=U+pV.
$$
Here $U$ denotes <internal energy>, also written $E$ in some references. Like <internal energy>, <enthalpy> is measured in <joules> and is an <extensive variable>. Its definition packages the <internal energy> change and expansion <work> into one useful quantity for fixed-<pressure> calculations. It does not define a body's stored <heat>. https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/E02141/plain[IUPAC Gold Book, enthalpy]{ref}

For a fixed amount and composition of a <simple compressible system>, the relation between neighboring equilibrium states is
$$
dH=T\,dS+V\,dp.
$$
Here $T$ is absolute <temperature> and $S$ is <entropy>. Thus <entropy> and <pressure> are the natural variables of this <thermodynamic potential>, with <partial derivatives> $T=(\partial H/\partial S)_p$ and $V=(\partial H/\partial p)_S$. Fixed composition matters: changing composition adds further terms. The formula follows in one line from the equilibrium <first law of thermodynamics> identity:
$$
dH=dU+p\,dV+V\,dp=(T\,dS-p\,dV)+p\,dV+V\,dp=T\,dS+V\,dp.
$$
The cancellation of $p\,dV$ is the reason <enthalpy> replaces <volume (thermodynamics)> with <pressure> as a natural variable. This identity concerns equilibrium states; it does not identify $T\,dS$ with actual irreversible <heat> transfer. https://davidtong.org/teaching/statistical-physics/statmechhtml/S4#S4.SS4.SSS1[David Tong, Statistical Physics, section 4.4.1: enthalpy]{ref}

For a <closed system> undergoing a <quasistatic process> at constant <pressure>, require fixed amount and composition, only <pressure>-<volume (thermodynamics)> <work>, no <friction>, and unchanged bulk <kinetic energy> and <potential energy>. Then the actual <first law of thermodynamics> balance gives
$$
\delta Q=dU+p\,dV=dH-V\,dp,\qquad
dp=0\ \Longrightarrow\ Q_p=\Delta H.
$$
The symbol $Q_p$ means transferred <heat> for this fixed-<pressure> process. If the piston example in <heat> additionally meets these conditions, its $20\,\mathrm{kJ}$ of incoming <heat> equals $\Delta H$, while $\Delta U=15\,\mathrm{kJ}$ and $p\Delta V=W=5\,\mathrm{kJ}$. The example shows exactly what <enthalpy> adds to <internal energy>: the expansion <work> required at the imposed <pressure>.

Within a single <thermodynamic phase>, <heat capacity at constant pressure> gives
$$
C_p=\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial T}\right)_p,\qquad
\Delta H=\int_{T_1}^{T_2}C_p(T)\,dT
$$
with the amount and composition fixed. Constant $C_p$ gives $\Delta H=C_p\Delta T$; dividing by <mass> gives <specific heat capacity at constant pressure>. At equilibrium <melting>, <enthalpy> can instead change through <latent heat> while <temperature> stays fixed. The <sensible heat> example therefore adds two single-<thermodynamic phase> <enthalpy> changes and one <latent heat> contribution.

The usefulness of <enthalpy> lies in matching the imposed constraint: a fixed-<pressure> experiment naturally reports $\Delta H$, while fixed-<volume (thermodynamics)> heating with no other <work> reports $\Delta U$. Neither equality is universal. Additional <work> or changing <pressure> alters the relation between supplied <heat> and $\Delta H$; irreversible <entropy> production also prevents identifying actual $\delta Q$ with $T\,dS$. The separate <Joule-Thomson coefficient> branch uses <enthalpy> to describe throttling under its own assumptions.

= Stagnation enthalpy
{parent=Enthalpy}
{title2=$h_0=h+u^2/2$}

The sum of specific <enthalpy> and macroscopic <kinetic energy> per unit <mass>. In a steady adiabatic flow without shaft work, its change is determined by the change in external <potential energy>. At essentially constant external <potential energy>, it is conserved through a stationary nonradiating <shock wave> even though <entropy> increases.

= Enthalpy of mixing
{parent=Enthalpy}
{title2=$\Delta H_{\rm mix}$}

The mixture enthalpy minus the sum of the unmixed component enthalpies at the same <temperature> and pressure. Its sign does not alone determine miscibility, which depends on the full <Gibbs free energy>.

= Reaction enthalpy
{parent=Enthalpy}
{title2=$\Delta_rH$}

The <enthalpy> change per specified extent of a reaction. Scaling or reversing a <chemical equation> scales or reverses this quantity.

= Molar enthalpy
{parent=Enthalpy}
{title2=$h_{\rm m}=H/n$}

<Molar enthalpy> is <enthalpy> per <amount of substance> $n$, measured in $\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}$. With $u_{\rm m}=U/n$ and <molar volume> $v_{\rm m}=V/n$, $h_{\rm m}=u_{\rm m}+pv_{\rm m}$. Its fixed-<pressure> <temperature> derivative is the corresponding <molar heat capacity> $C_{p,\rm m}$, at fixed composition.

= Isenthalpic process
{parent=Enthalpy}
{wiki}

An <isenthalpic process> preserves <enthalpy> for a fixed amount of material, or preserves <specific enthalpy> between inlet and outlet when comparing a flowing <fluid> of fixed composition. It need not preserve <temperature> or <entropy>. An insulated throttle satisfying the <steady-flow energy balance> assumptions is an important example.

= Steady-flow energy balance
{parent=Enthalpy}
{title2=$\dot Q-\dot W_s=\dot m\,\Delta(h+w^2/2+gz)$}

For <steady flow> through a fixed <control volume> with one inlet and one outlet, the <first law of thermodynamics> and conservation of <mass> give
$$
\dot Q-\dot W_s=\dot m\left[h_2-h_1+\frac{w_2^2-w_1^2}{2}+g(z_2-z_1)\right].
$$
Here $\dot m$ is the <mass flow rate>, $\dot Q$ incoming <heat> per unit time, $\dot W_s$ outgoing non-flow <work> per unit time, $w$ the flow speed, $z$ elevation and $g$ <gravitational acceleration>. The <specific enthalpy> $h=u+p/\rho$ includes both the transported <internal energy> per unit <mass> $u$ and the <pressure> <work> needed to push <volume (thermodynamics)> through the boundary; $\rho$ is <mass density>. The inlet supplies that <pressure> <work>, while the outlet consumes it to displace the downstream <fluid>.<Thermodynamics and Propulsion>[§2.5.1–2.5.2, equation 2.11: mass conservation, transported enthalpy and the steady-flow energy equation]{ref}

For an insulated stationary throttle, require negligible <heat> transfer, no shaft or other non-flow <work>, and negligible changes in bulk <kinetic energy> and gravitational <potential energy>. Then $h_2=h_1$. With unchanged composition this is also equality of <molar enthalpy>. The upstream and downstream states must admit equilibrium <pressure>, <temperature> and property values, although the constriction itself is irreversible and can be far from <thermodynamic equilibrium>. Start-up storage of <energy> in the walls and <fluid>, heat leakage, or a large outlet speed invalidates the reduced balance. A nozzle can convert <specific enthalpy> into bulk <kinetic energy> even when it is insulated.

The same accounting for a fixed transported amount is $U_2-U_1=p_1V_1-p_2V_2$, hence $U_1+p_1V_1=U_2+p_2V_2$. This explains why dropping the two <pressure> <work> terms would give the wrong conserved quantity.<Thermodynamics and Statistical Mechanics by Richard Fitzpatrick/Joule-Thompson Throttling>[Fitzpatrick, Joule-Thompson Throttling, equations 6.167–6.174: inlet/outlet pressure work and enthalpy conservation]{ref}

= Joule-Thomson effect
{c}
{parent=Enthalpy}
{wiki=Joule–Thomson_effect}

The <Joule-Thomson effect> is the <temperature> response of a <fluid> flowing from higher to lower <pressure> through a valve or a plug of <porous medium>, when the throttle is insulated and the other <steady-flow energy balance> assumptions hold. Its equilibrium endpoints have equal <specific enthalpy>. The <Joule-Thomson coefficient> determines whether a small drop cools or warms; cooling is not universal.

\Image[joule-thomson-throttle-enthalpy-balance.png]
{id=joule-thomson-throttle-enthalpy-balance}
{title=<Enthalpy> accounting for an insulated <Joule-Thomson effect> throttle}
{description=Original schematic of the <steady-flow energy balance>, with $u$ denoting <internal energy> per unit <mass>. The arrows include inlet and outlet <pressure> <work>. The reduced balance requires negligible bulk <kinetic energy> and <potential energy> changes. No particular apparatus or measured <temperature> drop is depicted.}

A useful contrast is between three insulated expansions. In <free expansion> of a gas-only <closed system> into a <vacuum>, no external <work> occurs and $U_2=U_1$; an <ideal gas> returns to its initial equilibrium <temperature>. In a throttle, continuous flow carries <pressure> <work> across the boundary and preserves <specific enthalpy> under the stated assumptions; an <ideal gas> again has no endpoint <temperature> change. In a reversible <adiabatic expansion> of a loaded <closed system>, the <gas> supplies <work>, loses <internal energy> and cools, even if it is an <ideal gas>. That process preserves <entropy>; throttling generally produces <entropy>. For a small constant-<enthalpy> step, $dH=T\,dS+V\,dp=0$ gives $dS=-V\,dp/T>0$ when $dp<0$ and $T,V>0$. This equilibrium comparison computes endpoint <entropy> differences without supplying a reversible path through the plug.

The effect is useful in <refrigerators> because an appropriately prepared <real gas> can cool while passing through a simple valve. The inlet must lie in a suitable positive-<Joule-Thomson coefficient> region. The <Joule-Thomson inversion curve> makes that requirement precise; a pressure drop by itself is not a guarantee of refrigeration.

= Measuring the Joule-Thomson effect with a porous plug
{parent=Joule-Thomson effect}

= Measurement of the Joule-Thomson effect with a porous plug
{synonym}

The 2024 operating instructions for PHYWE's 04361-00 apparatus describe a frit between two gas chambers. A copper-coil <heat exchanger> first restores inlet <temperature> after cylinder-regulator cooling. An upstream manometer reads <gauge pressure>; the outlet stays at <atmospheric pressure>. Two NiCr-Ni <thermocouples> sit 2–5 mm from the frit. The digital meter's difference mode reports $0.01\,\mathrm K$ precision.<Joule-Thomson apparatus 04361-00, Operating instructions by PHYWE>[PHYWE, Joule-Thomson apparatus 04361-00, 2024 instructions, pp. 1–2, sections 2–4: heat exchanger, frit, manometer, probes and difference readout]{ref}

The manual specifies at most $1\,\mathrm{bar}$ <gauge pressure>, one hour of apparatus acclimatization, half an hour of meter warm-up, about 1.5 minutes of flow settling, then 10–15 seconds of averaging. It identifies a stagnant-gas zeroing error that shifts the fitted intercept and notes a maximum <nitrogen> signal of about $0.3\,\mathrm K$ in this setup.<Joule-Thomson apparatus 04361-00, Operating instructions by PHYWE>[PHYWE, 2024 instructions, p. 2, section 4, Hints concerning the experiment: pressure limit, equilibration, drift, averaging and zero compensation]{ref}

Define $x=p_1-p_2>0$ and $y=T_1-T_2$, so a small-drop fit is $y\simeq\mu_{\rm JT}x+c$. A constant sensor offset $c$ leaves the slope unchanged; pressure-dependent drift or <heat> leakage can alter it. The observable is the differential <temperature> slope across the plug, not cylinder-outlet cooling. Display precision alone does not establish accuracy or prove an <isenthalpic process>. This low-pressure, room-temperature apparatus does not map a complete <Joule-Thomson inversion curve>.

= Joule-Thomson coefficient
{c}
{parent=Enthalpy}
{title2=$\mu=(\partial T/\partial p)_H$}

The <Joule-Thomson coefficient> is the local <temperature> response to changing <pressure> at fixed <enthalpy> and fixed amount and composition:
$$
\mu_{\rm JT}=\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial p}\right)_H,
\qquad [\mu_{\rm JT}]=\mathrm{K\,Pa^{-1}}.
$$
It describes the equilibrium endpoints of a <Joule-Thomson effect> experiment meeting the <steady-flow energy balance> assumptions. With $\Delta p=p_2-p_1<0$, a sufficiently small drop gives $\Delta T\simeq\mu_{\rm JT}\Delta p$. Thus $\mu_{\rm JT}>0$ means cooling, $\mu_{\rm JT}<0$ warming, and $\mu_{\rm JT}=0$ no first-order change. At zero coefficient, a finite drop can still change <temperature>. For a chosen illustrative local value $\mu_{\rm JT}=0.20\,\mathrm{K\,bar^{-1}}$ and $\Delta p=-0.50\,\mathrm{bar}$, the linear prediction is $\Delta T=-0.10\,\mathrm K$; these are stipulated numbers, not measured gas properties.

For a fixed-composition <simple compressible system>, the measurable form is
$$
\mu_{\rm JT}
=\frac{T(\partial V/\partial T)_p-V}{C_p}
=\frac{V}{C_p}(T\alpha-1).
$$
Here $C_p$ is the whole sample's <heat capacity at constant pressure> and $\alpha$ its volumetric <coefficient of thermal expansion>; alternatively use <molar volume> and <molar heat capacity> consistently. An <ideal gas> has $\alpha=1/T$, so $\mu_{\rm JT}=0$. Its <enthalpy> depends only on <temperature>; positive <heat capacity at constant pressure> then makes any such finite throttle preserve <temperature>. A <real gas> can have either sign. For $C_p>0$, compare $T\alpha$ with one: the <thermal expansion> response decides the sign, not insulation alone.<Thermodynamics and Statistical Mechanics by Richard Fitzpatrick/Joule-Thompson Throttling>[Fitzpatrick, Joule-Thompson Throttling, equations 6.175–6.182: ideal-gas limit and coefficient]{ref}

A short derivation uses $dH=T\,dS+V\,dp$ and the <Maxwell relation> $(\partial S/\partial p)_T=-(\partial V/\partial T)_p$:
$$
dH=C_p\,dT+\left[V-T\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_p\right]dp.
$$
Setting $dH=0$ gives the coefficient. These are equilibrium <state function> identities, not a claim of a <reversible thermodynamic process> inside the throttle. For a finite drop, solve the endpoint <enthalpy> equality or integrate
$$
T_2-T_1=\int_{p_1}^{p_2}\mu_{\rm JT}(T(p),p)\,dp
$$
along the constant-<enthalpy> equilibrium curve. The coefficient can change sign during that integration, as the <Van der Waals throttling model> illustrates.

= Joule-Thomson inversion curve
{c}
{parent=Joule-Thomson coefficient}
{title2=$\mu_{\rm JT}(T,p)=0$}

The <Joule-Thomson inversion curve> is the set of equilibrium <temperature>–<pressure> states where the <Joule-Thomson coefficient> vanishes. For positive <heat capacity at constant pressure>, its condition is $T\alpha=1$, with $\alpha$ the <coefficient of thermal expansion>. The curve separates locally cooling and warming throttle regions. A finite <isenthalpic process> can cross it, so the inlet sign alone does not fix the total <temperature> change. An <ideal gas> has zero coefficient everywhere, rather than an isolated dividing curve.

= Joule-Thomson inversion temperature
{c}
{parent=Joule-Thomson inversion curve}
{title2=$T_{\rm inv}(p)$}

A <Joule-Thomson inversion temperature> is a <temperature> at which the <Joule-Thomson coefficient> is zero at a specified <pressure>. There can be more than one such <temperature> at that <pressure>; distinguish the upper and lower branches from a maximum or a dilute-gas limiting value.

= Van der Waals throttling model
{c}
{parent=Joule-Thomson coefficient}

A hypothetical <Van der Waals equation> model illustrates positive and negative <Joule-Thomson coefficient> regions and a finite throttle that first warms, then cools. Use the molar convention already distinguished in <Van der Waals equation>:
$$
p=\frac{RT}{v_{\rm m}-b_{\rm m}}-\frac{a_{\rm m}}{v_{\rm m}^2},
\qquad
u_{\rm m}=C_{V,\rm m}T-\frac{a_{\rm m}}{v_{\rm m}},
\qquad C_{V,\rm m}=\frac32R.
$$
Here $v_{\rm m}$ is <molar volume>, $R$ the <molar gas constant>, $u_{\rm m}$ <internal energy> per <mole>, and $C_{V,\rm m}$ the stipulated constant <molar heat capacity> at fixed <volume (thermodynamics)>. The second equation is an additional caloric assumption: an <equation of state> alone does not specify the <temperature> dependence of <internal energy>. It satisfies the equilibrium identity $(\partial u_{\rm m}/\partial v_{\rm m})_T=T(\partial p/\partial T)_{v_{\rm m}}-p=a_{\rm m}/v_{\rm m}^2$. The arbitrary <energy> reference is set to zero.

\Image[van-der-waals-throttling-inversion-model.png]
{id=van-der-waals-throttling-inversion-model}
{title=Hypothetical <Van der Waals throttling model>: <Joule-Thomson inversion curve> and finite <temperature> responses}
{description=All curves are original model calculations, not <nitrogen>, <carbon dioxide> or other gas measurements. Colors label the response along constant <molar enthalpy>; arrows point toward lower <pressure>. The displayed states are above the model <Van der Waals critical point> <temperature> and have positive <isothermal compressibility>. The purple path crosses the zero-coefficient curve and has a <temperature> maximum.}

Define dimensionless variables $r=v_{\rm m}/b_{\rm m}$, $\theta=Rb_{\rm m}T/a_{\rm m}$, $\pi=b_{\rm m}^2p/a_{\rm m}$ and $\eta=b_{\rm m}h_{\rm m}/a_{\rm m}$, where $h_{\rm m}$ is <molar enthalpy>. These differ from the critical-value scaling in <Van der Waals law of corresponding states>. The throttle calculation reduces to
$$
\pi=\frac{\theta}{r-1}-\frac1{r^2},\qquad
\eta=\frac32\theta+\pi r-\frac1r,
\qquad
\theta(r;\eta)=\frac{\eta+2/r}{3/2+r/(r-1)}.
$$
Thus a prescribed inlet fixes $\eta$, and solving the first and last equations at the outlet <pressure> gives the final <temperature>. For an inlet $(\theta_1,\pi_1)=(0.8,0.12)$ and outlet $\pi_2=0.025$, the model gives $\theta_2\simeq0.752287$, a decrease of $0.047713$. The inlet $(2.2,0.12)$ instead gives $\theta_2\simeq2.205786$, an increase of $0.005786$. The purple inlet $(1.45,0.30)$ initially warms but ends at $\theta_2\simeq1.437585$ for the same outlet <pressure>. These are reduced toy values, not kelvin readings or experimental evidence. No physical gas parameters were fitted.

For a short inversion derivation, implicit <differentiation> of the <Van der Waals equation> at fixed <pressure> gives
$$
\left(\frac{\partial v_{\rm m}}{\partial T}\right)_p
=\frac{R/(v_{\rm m}-b_{\rm m})}{RT/(v_{\rm m}-b_{\rm m})^2-2a_{\rm m}/v_{\rm m}^3}.
$$
Insert this into $T(\partial v_{\rm m}/\partial T)_p=v_{\rm m}$ and substitute back into the <equation of state>. The <Joule-Thomson inversion curve> is
$$
T_{\rm inv}=\frac{2a_{\rm m}}{Rb_{\rm m}}\left(1-\frac{b_{\rm m}}{v_{\rm m}}\right)^2,
\qquad
\theta_{\rm inv}=2(1-1/r)^2,
\qquad
\pi_{\rm inv}=\frac{2r-3}{r^2}.
$$
Positive inversion <pressure> requires $r>3/2$. Its maximum <pressure> is $\pi=1/3$ at $r=3$, while the upper dilute limit is $\theta_{\rm inv}\to2$. Using <Van der Waals critical point> values, the latter is $T_{\rm inv,max}/T_c=27/4$. The inversion locus depends on the <equation of state>; the chosen <molar heat capacity> additionally determines how much <temperature> changes along each finite throttle.<Thermodynamics and Statistical Mechanics by Richard Fitzpatrick/Joule-Thompson Throttling>[Fitzpatrick, Joule-Thompson Throttling, equations 6.188–6.190 and following paragraph: inversion locus, maximum temperature and limits of the van der Waals prediction]{ref}

The displayed region has $\theta>8/27$, above the model's critical <temperature>, and uses only $r>1$, $p>0$, $(\partial p/\partial v_{\rm m})_T<0$ and positive $C_{p,\rm m}$. Subcritical calculations require attention to <phase coexistence>, <metastability> and the <Maxwell construction>; an unstable homogeneous continuation is not an equilibrium throttle endpoint. Even on a stable branch, this <mean-field theory> with constant $C_{V,\rm m}$ is a qualitative model, not an accurate inversion map for a particular <real gas>. <Phase transitions>, changing composition, appreciable <heat> leakage or bulk <kinetic energy> changes require a different property calculation or <steady-flow energy balance>. At <phase coexistence>, the smooth single-<thermodynamic phase> coefficient formula cannot replace a balance involving the <phase fraction>.

= Virial criterion for Joule-Thomson cooling
{parent=Joule-Thomson coefficient}

For $p=k_BT[n+B_2(T)n^2]$, the coefficient is $\mu=N[TB_2'-B_2]/[C_p(1+2B_2n)]$. On the stable dilute branch it is positive precisely when $d(B_2/T)/dT>0$.

= Specific enthalpy
{title2=$h$}
{parent=Enthalpy}

Specific enthalpy is <enthalpy> per unit mass. For a simple compressible fluid of fixed composition, the <first law of thermodynamics> gives
$$
dh=T\,ds+\frac{dp}{\rho},
$$
where $T$ is <temperature>, $s$ is <specific entropy>, $p$ is <pressure>, and $\rho$ is <mass density>. Along an <isentropic flow>, $dh=dp/\rho$. For a <perfect gas> with constant <specific-heat ratio> $\gamma$, $h=\gamma p/[(\gamma-1)\rho]$.

= Polytropic enthalpy
{parent=Specific enthalpy}
{title2=$Q=(n+1)p/\rho$}

For $p=K\rho^{1+1/n}$ with $K,n>0$, the specific <enthalpy> relative to vacuum is $Q=(n+1)K\rho^{1/n}=(n+1)p/\rho$. It obeys $\nabla Q=\nabla p/\rho$ and $nD_tQ=-Q\nabla\cdot\mathbf u$. The vacuum reference is fixed when these equations are combined with the <polytropic equation of state>.

= Enthalpy Maxwell relation
{parent=Enthalpy}

Since $T=(\partial H/\partial S)_p$ and $V=(\partial H/\partial p)_S$, equality of mixed derivatives gives $(\partial T/\partial p)_S=(\partial V/\partial S)_p$.

= Heat capacity at constant pressure
{parent=Enthalpy}
{title2=$C_p=(\partial H/\partial T)_p$}

= Constant-pressure heat capacity
{synonym}

For a fixed-composition <closed system>, the <heat capacity> at fixed <pressure> is $C_p=(\partial H/\partial T)_p$, with <enthalpy> $H$. Under the <quasistatic process> assumptions in <heat capacity>, only <pressure>-<volume (thermodynamics)> <work> is permitted, so $dp=0$ gives $\delta Q=dH=C_p\,dT$. Thus supplied <heat> is $Q=\Delta H$, or $Q=C_p\Delta T$ within a single <thermodynamic phase> when $C_p$ is constant. For an <ideal gas>, the expansion <work> explains why this exceeds the required <heat> at fixed <volume (thermodynamics)> for the same <temperature> increase. Its per-unit-<mass> counterpart is <specific heat capacity at constant pressure>.

= Difference between constant-pressure and constant-volume heat capacities
{title2=$C_P-C_V$}
{parent=Heat capacity at constant pressure}

For a simple compressible system,
$$
C_P-C_V
=T\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P
\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V.
$$
This follows by expressing the entropy as a function of temperature and volume and using a Maxwell relation.

= Heat-capacity difference and mechanical stability
{parent=Difference between constant-pressure and constant-volume heat capacities}
{title2=$C_p-C_V=TV\alpha_V^2/\kappa_T\geq0$}

For a fixed-composition <simple compressible system>, a <Maxwell relation> gives $C_p-C_V=T(\partial p/\partial T)_V(\partial V/\partial T)_p$. At fixed <volume>, the differential <equation of state> gives $(\partial p/\partial T)_V=\alpha_V/\kappa_T$, proving the displayed result. Positive absolute <temperature> and positive <isothermal compressibility> imply a nonnegative difference. It vanishes when <thermal expansion> does, so strict inequality requires a nonzero expansion coefficient. This stability argument includes nonideal internal-energy changes, unlike a purely ideal-gas expansion-work explanation.

= Volume derivative form of the heat-capacity difference
{parent=Difference between constant-pressure and constant-volume heat capacities}
{title2=$C_p-C_V=[(\partial U/\partial V)_T+p](\partial V/\partial T)_p$}

For a fixed-composition simple compressible <thermodynamic system> with smooth <equations of state>, write the <internal energy> as $U(T,V)$ and the <enthalpy> as $H=U+pV$. Differentiating $H$ at constant <pressure> and applying the <multivariable chain rule> gives
$$
C_p=\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_p+p\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_p,\qquad\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_p=\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_V+\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_p.
$$
Subtracting the <heat capacity at constant volume> proves the displayed identity. For an <ideal gas>, $(\partial U/\partial V)_T=0$ and $(\partial V/\partial T)_p=Nk_B/p$, so $C_p-C_V=Nk_B$. For a nonideal material the <internal energy> change with <volume (thermodynamics)> contributes as well as expansion <work>.
