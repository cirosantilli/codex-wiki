= Solution
{parent=i}

An $A$-<module> is **completely reducible** if it is a <direct sum of modules> each of which is a <simple module>:
$$
V=\bigoplus_{j\in J}S_j.
$$
Here a <simple module> is nonzero and has no <submodules> other than zero and itself. This is precisely the definition of a <semisimple module>. The indexing set need not be finite unless $V$ has finite <dimension>; the zero <module> is included as the empty <direct sum of modules>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Construct the sum successively, retaining a <simple module> only when it adds a new <direct summand>. Suppose $S=V_1+\cdots+V_{j-1}$ is already expressed as a <direct sum of modules> selected from these <simple modules>. The intersection $S\cap V_j$ is a <submodule> of the <simple module> $V_j$, so it is either zero or all of $V_j$. In the latter case $S+V_j=S$. In the former case
$$
S+V_j=S\oplus V_j.
$$
Starting with $S=0$ and proceeding by <mathematical induction> gives a <direct sum of modules> equal to $V_1+\cdots+V_n$. Thus the sum is a <semisimple module>. The argument proves the useful <finite sum of simple submodules> lemma without claiming that the originally listed <submodules> are themselves independent.

= Solution

Since $A$ is a nonzero <associative algebra> of finite <dimension>, it has a nonzero <left ideal> $I$ of least positive <dimension>. Such a <minimal left ideal> is a <simple module> under left multiplication.

For $a\in A$, right multiplication defines the <module homomorphism>
$$
R_a:I\longrightarrow A,\qquad x\longmapsto xa.
$$
Its <kernel> is a <submodule> of $I$, so its <image> $Ia$ is either zero or a <simple module> <isomorphic> to $I$. Each $Ia$ is a <left ideal>, since $c(xa)=(cx)a$.

Consider
$$
J=\sum_{a\in A}Ia.
$$
It is a <left ideal>. It is also stable under right multiplication, because $(Ia)b=I(ab)$, so it is a <two-sided ideal>. Moreover $I=I1\subseteq J$, giving $J\ne0$. The assumption that $A$ is a <simple ring> therefore forces $J=A$.

Only finitely many summands are needed: express each member of a finite <basis> of $A$ as a finite sum of elements of the $Ia$, and collect the finitely many indices used. After discarding zero summands, $A$ is a finite sum of <simple modules>. Part (a)(ii) makes its <left regular module> a <semisimple module>. This is exactly the definition of a <semisimple algebra>, proving that a <finite-dimensional simple algebra is semisimple>. The finite <dimension> assumption is essential to this assertion about a <simple ring>.

Use the left $A$-<module> structure $c\cdot(a\otimes b)=ca\otimes b$ on the <tensor product of associative algebras>. Expanding each $b\in B$ in the <basis> $b_1,\ldots,b_m$ shows that every <tensor> can be written
$$
x=\sum_{j=1}^m a_j\otimes b_j
=\sum_{j=1}^m a_j\cdot(1\otimes b_j).
$$
Thus the displayed family generates the <module>.

For uniqueness, let $\beta_j:B\to k$ be the corresponding <dual basis>, with $\beta_j(b_i)=\delta_{ij}$. Applying the <linear map> $\operatorname{id}_A\otimes\beta_j$ to a <linear relation> $\sum_i a_i\otimes b_i=0$ gives $a_j=0$. Hence the family is <linearly independent in a module>, and
$$
\boxed{A\otimes_kB\cong A^{\oplus m}\quad\text{as left }A\text{-modules}.}
$$
In particular $1\otimes b_1,\ldots,1\otimes b_m$ is a <basis of a module>, with coefficients uniquely in $A$.

Write $x\in Z(A\otimes_kB)$ uniquely as $x=\sum_j a_j\otimes b_j$ using part (i). For each $a\in A$, the equality $(a\otimes1)x=x(a\otimes1)$ becomes
$$
\sum_j(aa_j-a_ja)\otimes b_j=0.
$$
The coefficient uniqueness from part (i) gives $aa_j=a_ja$ for every $a$, so each $a_j$ belongs to the <center of an associative algebra> $Z(A)$. Consequently $x\in Z(A)\otimes_kB$.

Choose a <basis> $z_1,\ldots,z_t$ of the <vector space> $Z(A)$ and rewrite $x=\sum_{i=1}^t z_i\otimes d_i$. For $b\in B$, commuting with $1\otimes b$ gives
$$
\sum_i z_i\otimes(bd_i-d_ib)=0.
$$
Extend the <linearly independent> family $z_i$ to a <basis> of $A$ and apply its <dual basis> to the first factor. It follows that $bd_i=d_ib$ for every $b$, hence $d_i\in Z(B)$. This proves $Z(A\otimes_kB)\subseteq Z(A)\otimes_k Z(B)$.

Conversely, if $z\in Z(A)$ and $w\in Z(B)$, then
$$
(z\otimes w)(a\otimes b)=za\otimes wb=az\otimes bw=(a\otimes b)(z\otimes w).
$$
The <pure tensors> $a\otimes b$ span the <tensor product>, so $z\otimes w$ is central, as is every sum of such <pure tensors>. Therefore the <center of a tensor product of associative algebras> is
$$
\boxed{Z(A\otimes_kB)=Z(A)\otimes_k Z(B).}
$$
These <tensor products> are identified with their natural <vector subspaces> of $A\otimes_kB$; extending <bases> justifies these embeddings over the <field> $k$.

Let $J\ne0$ be a <two-sided ideal> of $A\otimes_kB$. Choose a nonzero $x\in J$ of least possible <two-factor tensor rank> $n$, and take a shortest expression
$$
x=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\otimes b_i.
$$
Both families $(a_i)$ and $(b_i)$ are <linearly independent>: a <linear dependence> in either family would combine terms and shorten the expression. In particular $a_1,b_1\ne0$.

Because $A$ is a <simple ring>, the <two-sided ideal> generated by $a_1$ is $A$. Thus there are finitely many $u_j,v_j\in A$ with $\sum_j u_ja_1v_j=1$. Form
$$
y=\sum_j(u_j\otimes1)x(v_j\otimes1)
=\sum_{i=1}^n c_i\otimes b_i\in J,\qquad
c_i=\sum_j u_ja_iv_j,\qquad c_1=1.
$$
Since the $b_i$ are <linearly independent>, applying a <linear functional> selecting $b_1$ shows $y\ne0$. Its <two-factor tensor rank> is at most $n$ and hence, by the choice of $x$, exactly $n$. Consequently $c_1=1,c_2,\ldots,c_n$ are <linearly independent>.

Similarly, simplicity of $B$ gives finitely many $p_j,q_j\in B$ with $\sum_jp_jb_1q_j=1$. Now
$$
z=\sum_j(1\otimes p_j)y(1\otimes q_j)
=\sum_{i=1}^n c_i\otimes d_i\in J,\qquad
d_i=\sum_jp_jb_iq_j,\qquad d_1=1.
$$
The <linear independence> of the $c_i$ shows $z\ne0$, and minimality again makes its <two-factor tensor rank> exactly $n$. We have obtained
$$
\boxed{z=1\otimes1+\sum_{i=2}^n c_i\otimes d_i\in J\setminus\{0\}.}
$$
Both coefficient families in this expression are <linearly independent>. Retaining this additional property is the key to the next part. This is <coefficient normalization in an ideal of a tensor product>.

= Solution

Keep the nonzero $z$ of minimal <two-factor tensor rank> constructed in part (i). For every $a\in A$, its <commutator> with $a\otimes1$ lies in $J$ and equals
$$
(a\otimes1)z-z(a\otimes1)
=\sum_{i=2}^n(ac_i-c_ia)\otimes d_i.
$$
If this <tensor> were nonzero, its <two-factor tensor rank> would be at most $n-1$, contradicting minimality. Thus it is zero. The <linear independence> of $d_2,\ldots,d_n$ forces $ac_i=c_ia$ for every $a$ and $i\geq2$. Since $Z(A)=k$, each $c_i$ is a <scalar multiple> of $1$.

But $1,c_2,\ldots,c_n$ are <linearly independent>. Hence $n=1$, so $z=1\otimes1\in J$. A <two-sided ideal> containing the multiplicative identity is the entire <ring>; therefore $J=A\otimes_kB$.

We have proved the stronger result that $A\otimes_kB$ is a <simple ring>. It has finite <dimension> $(\dim_kA)(\dim_kB)$, so part (b) proves
$$
\boxed{A\otimes_kB\text{ is a semisimple ring}.}
$$
Part (c)(ii) also gives $Z(A\otimes_kB)=k$, so this proves that a <tensor product of central simple algebras> is again a <central simple algebra>.

The <character formula for an induced representation> is
$$
\boxed{\chi_{\operatorname{Ind}_H^GW}(g)
=\frac1{|H|}\sum_{\substack{x\in G\\x^{-1}gx\in H}}\chi_W(x^{-1}gx).}
$$
Equivalently, extend the <character> $\chi_W$ by zero outside the <subgroup> $H$ and sum over all $x\in G$. The factor $1/|H|$ accounts for passing from elements of $G$ to representatives of left <cosets> of $H$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

For each $h\in g^G\cap H$, count the solutions of $x^{-1}gx=h$. Once a solution $x_0$ is fixed, every solution is uniquely $cx_0$ with $c\in C_G(g)$, the <centralizer> of $g$. Thus each $h$ contributes $|C_G(g)|\chi_W(h)$ to the sum in part (a).

The <orbit-stabilizer theorem> for the <conjugation action> gives $|C_G(g)|=|G|/|g^G|$. The intersection $g^G\cap H$ is the disjoint union of the <conjugacy classes> $h_1^H,\ldots,h_\ell^H$, and the <character> $\chi_W$ is constant on each such <conjugacy class>. Hence
$$
\chi_V(g)=\frac{|C_G(g)|}{|H|}\sum_{h\in g^G\cap H}\chi_W(h)
=\boxed{\frac{|G|}{|H|}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{|h_i^H|}{|g^G|}\chi_W(h_i)}.
$$
If the intersection is empty, the empty sum gives zero. This is the <conjugacy-class formula for an induced character>.

Order the <conjugacy classes> of the <symmetric group> $S_4$ by representatives $1,(12),(12)(34),(123),(1234)$; their sizes are $1,6,3,8,6$. For a three-dimensional <rotation> through an <angle> $\theta$, the <eigenvalues> are $1,e^{i\theta},e^{-i\theta}$, so the <trace of a three-dimensional rotation> is $1+2\cos\theta$.

Under the identification with the <rotational symmetry group of a cube>, elements of order two give half-turns, elements of order three give turns through $\pm2\pi/3$, and elements of order four give turns through $\pm\pi/2$. A half-turn can have an axis through opposite edges or opposite faces, but both kinds have <trace> $-1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|rrrrr}
\text{representative}&1&(12)&(12)(34)&(123)&(1234)\\\hline
\text{class size}&1&6&3&8&6\\
\chi_C&3&-1&-1&0&1
\end{array}.}
$$
Its <character inner product> with itself is
$$
\langle\chi_C,\chi_C\rangle_{S_4}
=\frac{9+6+3+0+6}{24}=1.
$$
By <character orthogonality>, a nonzero <complex representation> has <character inner product> one with itself exactly when it is an <irreducible representation>. Therefore $C$ is an <irreducible representation> of <dimension> three.

For comparison useful in part (e), the <standard representation of the symmetric group> has <character> equal to the number of fixed letters minus one, namely $(3,1,-1,0,-1)$ in this order. Multiplication by the <sign representation> changes this <character> to $(3,-1,-1,0,1)$. Thus $C$ is the <tensor product> of the <standard representation of the symmetric group> with the <sign representation>.

The given <permutations> satisfy $r^4=s^2=1$ and $srs=r^{-1}$. The eight elements $r^j,r^js$ for $0\leq j<4$ are distinct: $s\notin\langle r\rangle$, whose only element of order two is $r^2=(13)(24)$. Thus $H$ is the order-eight <dihedral group>, and we may use the <matrices>
$$
R=\sigma(r)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
S=\sigma(s)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
They obey the same relations and describe a quarter-turn and a <reflection> of a <square>.

The <eigenvalues> of $R$ over the <complex numbers> are $i$ and $-i$, with respective <eigenlines> spanned by $(1,-i)$ and $(1,i)$. Since $S$ interchanges these <eigenlines>, neither is an <invariant subspace> for $H$. Any proper nonzero <subrepresentation> of this two-dimensional <complex representation> would be a line invariant under both $R$ and $S$, and an $R$-invariant line must be one of those <eigenlines>. None qualifies, so **$W$ is irreducible**.

For the calculation in part (e), its <character> is
$$
\chi_W(1)=2,\qquad \chi_W(r^2)=-2,\qquad
\chi_W(r)=\chi_W(r^3)=\chi_W(r^js)=0\quad(0\leq j<4).
$$
Indeed $R^2=-I$, while a quarter-turn or a <reflection> has <trace> zero. The <character inner product> $(2^2+(-2)^2)/8=1$ provides a second irreducibility check.

= Solution

In the order of <conjugacy classes> used in part (c), the elements of $H$ have the following types:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{type in }S_4&\text{elements in }H\\\hline
1&1\\
(12)&rs=(13),\ r^3s=(24)\\
(12)(34)&r^2=(13)(24),\ s=(12)(34),\ r^2s=(14)(23)\\
(123)&\text{none}\\
(1234)&r,\ r^3
\end{array}.
$$
By part (d), only $1$ and $r^2$ have nonzero <character> values. Formula (b), or equivalently the element-sum version proved there, therefore gives
$$
\chi_V(1)=[G:H]\dim W=3\cdot2=6,
\qquad
\chi_V((12)(34))=\frac{|C_G((12)(34))|}{|H|}(-2)=\frac88(-2)=-2,
$$
with zero on the other three <conjugacy classes>. Here the <centralizer> order is $24/3=8$. Thus $\chi_V=(6,0,-2,0,0)$.

Let $\varepsilon$ denote the <sign representation> and take the explicit two-dimensional $G$-<module>
$$
U=\mathbf1\oplus\varepsilon.
$$
Its <character> is $\chi_U=(2,0,2,2,0)$. The <character> of a <tensor product of group representations> is the pointwise product of their <characters>, because $\operatorname{tr}(M\otimes N)=\operatorname{tr}(M)\operatorname{tr}(N)$. Consequently
$$
\chi_{C\otimes U}
=(3,-1,-1,0,1)(2,0,2,2,0)
=(6,0,-2,0,0)=\chi_V.
$$
By <Maschke's theorem> both <complex representations> are <semisimple representations>, and <characters distinguish finite-dimensional complex semisimple representations>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{V\cong C\otimes(\mathbf1\oplus\varepsilon)
\cong C\oplus(C\otimes\varepsilon).}
$$
This is the <induction of the planar square representation to the symmetric group on four letters>.

The <dimension> of the <fixed-point subspace of a group action> is the multiplicity of the <trivial representation>. Its <character inner product> here is
$$
\dim V^G=\langle\chi_V,1\rangle_G
=\frac{6+3(-2)}{24}=0.
$$
Hence **$V^G=0$**. Equivalently, <Frobenius reciprocity> identifies this multiplicity with $\langle\chi_W,1_H\rangle_H=(2-2)/8=0$.

Let $\rho$ afford the <character> $\chi$, and let $m$ be the <order of a group element> $g$. Then $\rho(g)^m=I$, so its <minimal polynomial> divides $T^m-1$. Over the <complex numbers> this <polynomial> has distinct <polynomial roots>, since its <formal derivative> $mT^{m-1}$ does not vanish at any of its <polynomial roots>. Thus $\rho(g)$ is <diagonalizable>, with <eigenvalues> $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_d$ that are <roots of unity>.

Each $\lambda_j$ has <absolute value> one, so $\lambda_j^{-1}=\overline{\lambda_j}$. The <eigenvalues> of $\rho(g^{-1})=\rho(g)^{-1}$ are $\lambda_j^{-1}$, giving
$$
\boxed{\chi(g^{-1})=\sum_j\lambda_j^{-1}
=\sum_j\overline{\lambda_j}
=\overline{\chi(g)}.}
$$
This is the <character inversion identity>.

On the <complex vector space> $\operatorname{CF}(G)$ of <class functions>, define the <linear operator>
$$
Tf(g)=f(g^{-1}).
$$
This is a complex-<linear map> and satisfies $T^2=I$. It is well-defined on <class functions>, because <inversion of conjugacy classes> takes an entire <conjugacy class> $K$ to another <conjugacy class> $K^{-1}$.

Use first the <basis> consisting of the <indicator functions> $1_K$ of the <conjugacy classes>. The <linear operator> $T$ permutes this <basis> by $1_K\mapsto1_{K^{-1}}$. A <permutation matrix> has <trace> equal to the number of fixed <basis vectors>, so
$$
\operatorname{tr}T=\#\{K:K=K^{-1}\}.
$$
The right-hand side counts the <real conjugacy classes>.

By <character orthogonality> and completeness, the <irreducible characters> are another <basis> of $\operatorname{CF}(G)$. Part (a) gives $T\chi=\overline\chi$. The pointwise <complex conjugate> of an <irreducible character> is again an <irreducible character>: conjugate the representing <matrices>, and conjugate any hypothetical proper <invariant subspace> back to contradict irreducibility. Thus $T$ also permutes this <basis>, and
$$
\operatorname{tr}T=\#\{\chi\in\operatorname{Irr}(G):\chi=\overline\chi\}.
$$
Here $\chi=\overline\chi$ means exactly that $\chi$ is a <real-valued character>. Equality of the <trace> in these two <bases> proves
$$
\boxed{\#\{\text{real-valued irreducible characters}\}
=\#\{\text{real conjugacy classes}\}.}
$$
This is the <real character and real conjugacy class counting theorem>. The <linear operator> used in the proof is $f\mapsto f\circ\operatorname{inv}$; on the <irreducible character> <basis> it agrees with pointwise <complex conjugation>, although pointwise <complex conjugation> itself is not a complex-<linear map> on all <class functions>.

= Solution

Write $N=\langle a\rangle$. The relation $bab^{-1}=a^2$ lets every element be expressed as $a^ib^j$ with $0\leq i<7$ and $0\leq j<3$. There are 21 such expressions, so the assumption $|G|=21$ makes them distinct. In particular $a$ has order seven, $b$ has order three, and $N$ is a <normal subgroup>. This is the <nonabelian group of order 21>, the <semidirect product> $C_7\rtimes C_3$ with the action given by multiplication by two modulo seven.

Conjugation by $a$ fixes $N$, while conjugation by $b$ sends $a^i$ to $a^{2i}$. The nonidentity <conjugacy classes> inside $N$ are therefore
$$
K_1=\{a,a^2,a^4\},\qquad
K_2=\{a^3,a^5,a^6\}.
$$
For the other elements, direct calculation gives
$$
a^t(a^ib^j)a^{-t}=a^{i+(1-2^j)t}b^j.
$$
When $j=1$ or $2$, the coefficient $1-2^j$ is nonzero modulo seven, so letting $t$ vary gives every member of the <coset> $Nb^j$. These <cosets> cannot merge under <conjugation>, since $G/N$ is an <abelian group> and conjugation acts trivially on that <quotient group>. Thus the five <conjugacy classes> have representatives and sizes
$$
\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
\text{representative}&1&a&a^3&b&b^2\\\hline
\text{class size}&1&3&3&7&7
\end{array}.
$$

To find the <linear characters>, note that the <commutator subgroup> $G'$ contains $bab^{-1}a^{-1}=a$. The <quotient group> $G/N\cong C_3$ is abelian, so $G'\subseteq N$; hence $G'=N$. The <abelianization> is $C_3$. With $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$, its three <linear characters> are
$$
\lambda_j(a)=1,\qquad\lambda_j(b)=\omega^j,\qquad j=0,1,2.
$$
This exhausts the <linear characters> because <one-dimensional characters factor through the abelianization>.

For the remaining <irreducible characters>, put $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/7}$ and take the <linear characters> $\theta_t$ of $N$ defined by $\theta_t(a)=\zeta^t$. The six nontrivial <linear characters> form two orbits under conjugation, with exponent sets $\{1,2,4\}$ and $\{3,5,6\}$. Define the <induced characters>
$$
\psi=\operatorname{Ind}_N^G\theta_1,\qquad
\overline\psi=\operatorname{Ind}_N^G\theta_3.
$$
For $a^i\in N$, the <character formula for an induced representation> gives
$$
\psi(a^i)=\zeta^i+\zeta^{2i}+\zeta^{4i},\qquad
\overline\psi(a^i)=\zeta^{3i}+\zeta^{5i}+\zeta^{6i}.
$$
Both vanish outside $N$, since no element outside a <normal subgroup> can be conjugate into it. This is <induction from an abelian normal subgroup with a free character orbit>. For completeness, its irreducibility here follows directly from <Frobenius reciprocity> and <character orthogonality> on the <cyclic group> $N$:
$$
\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_G
=\langle\theta_1,\operatorname{Res}_N^G\psi\rangle_N
=\langle\theta_1,\theta_1+\theta_2+\theta_4\rangle_N=1.
$$
The same calculation with $\theta_3,\theta_5,\theta_6$ proves that $\overline\psi$ is an <irreducible character>.

Let
$$
\alpha=\zeta+\zeta^2+\zeta^4,\qquad
\beta=\zeta^3+\zeta^5+\zeta^6=\overline\alpha.
$$
The sum of the nontrivial seventh <roots of unity> gives $\alpha+\beta=-1$. Squaring and reducing exponents modulo seven gives
$$
\alpha^2=\alpha+2\beta=-\alpha-2.
$$
Thus $\alpha,\beta$ are the two <polynomial roots> of $T^2+T+2$, and
$$
\alpha=\frac{-1+i\sqrt7}{2},\qquad
\beta=\frac{-1-i\sqrt7}{2}.
$$
To fix the signs with our specified $\zeta$, observe that $\operatorname{Im}\alpha=\sin(2\pi/7)+\sin(3\pi/7)-\sin(\pi/7)>0$, using the increase of sine on $(0,\pi/2)$. These <Gaussian periods for seventh roots of unity> make the answer explicit.

The resulting <character table> is
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
&1&a&a^3&b&b^2\\\hline
\text{class size}&1&3&3&7&7\\\hline
\lambda_0&1&1&1&1&1\\
\lambda_1&1&1&1&\omega&\omega^2\\
\lambda_2&1&1&1&\omega^2&\omega\\
\psi&3&\alpha&\beta&0&0\\
\overline\psi&3&\beta&\alpha&0&0
\end{array}.}
$$
The two degree-three <irreducible characters> are distinct because $\alpha\ne\beta$. Together with the three <linear characters>, they exhaust the <irreducible characters>: the <sum of squares of irreducible degrees> is $3\cdot1^2+2\cdot3^2=21=|G|$. Equivalently, we have five distinct <irreducible characters> for the five <conjugacy classes>.

As a check on part (b), inversion interchanges $K_1$ and $K_2$, and also interchanges the two <conjugacy classes> outside $N$. Only the identity <conjugacy class> is real. Correspondingly $\lambda_0$ is the only <real-valued character> among the <irreducible characters>; $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ and $\psi,\overline\psi$ are two conjugate pairs.
