= Power-coordinate reduction of a singular Sturm-Liouville quotient
{parent=Sturm-Liouville theory}
{c}
{title2=$z=x^{3/2},\quad I(w)=\frac92\frac{\int_0^1(W')^2dz}{\int_0^1W^2dz}$}

The singular equation $2xw''-w'+\lambda x^2w=0$ becomes $W''+(2\lambda/9)W=0$ under $z=x^{3/2}$, $w(x)=W(z)$. Its zero-endpoint finite-<energy> quotient transforms as $2\int_0^1x^{-1/2}(w')^2dx/\int_0^1x^{1/2}w^2dx=(9/2)\int_0^1(W')^2dz/\int_0^1W^2dz$. The <Poincaré inequality> therefore proves the sharp minimum $9\pi^2/2$, attained by $w=\sin(\pi x^{3/2})$. This coordinate change turns an apparently singular weighted variational problem into the usual interval <energy> problem. The admissible eigenfunction is $O(x^{3/2})$, making the integration-by-parts <boundary term> $wx^{-1/2}w'$ vanish and both <energy> <integrals> converge.

= Ground-state representation of a Sturm-Liouville energy
{c}
{parent=Sturm-Liouville theory}
{title2=$\int(py'^2+qy^2-\lambda wy^2)=\int pu^2[(y/u)']^2$}

Suppose $u>0$ on an interval solves the <Sturm-Liouville equation> $-(pu')'+qu=\lambda wu$. Writing $y=uv$ and expanding derivatives gives
$$
py'^2+(q-\lambda w)y^2=pu^2v'^2+(puu'v^2)'.
$$
If the endpoint term $[pu'y^2/u]$ vanishes, integration yields the displayed identity. With $p>0$, it proves a lower bound $\lambda$ for the associated <Rayleigh quotient>, attained by $y=u$ when $u$ is admissible. For singular endpoints, establish the identity on compact subintervals and check the boundary limits; this is essential for the <Bessel differential equation> with $p=w=x$, $q=k^2/x$.

= Sturm-Liouville equation
{parent=Sturm-Liouville theory}
{c}
{title2=$-(py')'+qy=\lambda wy$}

A second-order differential equation in the displayed divergence form. Under the usual regular spectral assumptions, $p>0$, $w>0$, the coefficients have suitable regularity, and separated self-adjoint boundary conditions specify a <Sturm-Liouville problem>. Multiplying by $y$ and using zero endpoint values gives the <Rayleigh quotient> $\lambda=\int(py'^2+qy^2)dx/\int wy^2dx$ for a nonzero real <eigenfunction>. Sign conventions for the potential and eigenvalue vary; they must be read from the stated equation.

= Sturm oscillation theorem
{c}
{parent=Sturm-Liouville theory}

For a real one-dimensional <Sturm-Liouville problem> with separated self-adjoint boundary conditions and discrete simple spectrum, the eigenfunction for the $n$th eigenvalue has exactly $n$ interior zeros, numbering from zero. For a confining real <Schrödinger operator> on the line, the same ordering makes the ground state nodeless and the first excited state have one node. If the potential is even, uniqueness and reflection symmetry imply that these states are respectively even and odd.

= Drift-removing substitution for a Dirichlet Sturm-Liouville problem
{parent=Sturm-Liouville theory}

For $\mathcal Ly=-y''-2ay'$, setting $y=e^{-ax}v$ gives $\mathcal Ly=e^{-ax}(-v''+a^2v)$. Dirichlet conditions are unchanged. The weighted self-adjoint form is $-e^{-2ax}(e^{2ax}y')'$, with weight $e^{2ax}$. On an interval of length $L$, <eigenvalues> are $(n\pi/L)^2+a^2$ and <eigenfunctions> are $e^{-ax}\sin(n\pi x/L)$.

= Orthogonality of Sturm-Liouville eigenfunctions
{parent=Sturm-Liouville theory}
{title2=$\int_a^b w\overline{y_m}y_n\,dx=0$}

For a regular <self-adjoint> <Sturm-Liouville problem> with positive weight $w$ and self-adjoint boundary conditions, <eigenfunctions> with distinct <eigenvalues> are <orthogonal> in the weighted <inner product> $\int w\overline uv$. <Integration by parts> proves this by equating $\int\overline u Lv$ and $\int\overline{Lu}v$.

= Derivative orthogonality when the Sturm-Liouville potential vanishes
{parent=Orthogonality of Sturm-Liouville eigenfunctions}
{title2=$\int_a^bp\,y_n'y_m'\,dx=\lambda_n\int_a^bw\,y_ny_m\,dx$}

For the <Sturm-Liouville problem> $-(py_n')'=\lambda_nwy_n$, assume that the boundary term $[py_n'y_m]_a^b$ vanishes. One <integration by parts> gives the displayed identity. Distinct <eigenvalues> make the right side zero by <orthogonality of Sturm-Liouville eigenfunctions>, so the derivatives are orthogonal with weight $p$, while the original functions are orthogonal with weight $w$. This conclusion uses the absence of the $qy$ term: for a general potential the extra integral $-\int qy_ny_m$ need not vanish. For the <Chebyshev polynomial of the second kind> of degree $n-1$, the two weights are $w=\sqrt{1-x^2}$ and $p=(1-x^2)^{3/2}$.

= Gegenbauer polynomial
{title2=$C_n^\alpha(x)$}
{parent=Sturm-Liouville theory}
{c}
{wiki}

For $\alpha>0$, the Gegenbauer polynomial $C_n^\alpha$ is the degree-$n$ polynomial solution of
$$
(1-x^2)y''-(2\alpha+1)xy'+n(n+2\alpha)y=0.
$$
These polynomials are orthogonal on $(-1,1)$ with weight $(1-x^2)^{\alpha-1/2}$.

= Gegenbauer differential equation
{parent=Gegenbauer polynomial}
{c}

The Gegenbauer differential equation is the displayed Sturm–Liouville eigenvalue equation defining the <Gegenbauer polynomial>. Multiplication by $(1-x^2)^{\alpha-1/2}$ puts it in self-adjoint form.

= Roots of a Gegenbauer polynomial
{parent=Gegenbauer polynomial}
{c}

For $\alpha>0$, all $n$ roots of $C_n^\alpha$ are simple and lie in $(-1,1)$. If the product $q$ of its interior linear factors had degree below $n$, orthogonality would make $\int C_n^\alpha q w=0$, while $C_n^\alpha q=q^2(C_n^\alpha/q)$ has a constant strict sign away from finitely many points.

= Sturm-Liouville form
{c}
{parent=Sturm-Liouville theory}

A scalar second-order differential operator is in Sturm–Liouville form when it is written as
$$
Lu=-(p(x)u')'+q(x)u,
$$
possibly together with a positive weight in an eigenvalue problem.

= Sturm-Liouville operator
{c}
{parent=Sturm-Liouville form}
{title2=$L\xi=W^{-1}[-(P\xi\prime)\prime+Q\xi]$}

The weighted operator $L=W^{-1}[-(P\xi\prime)\prime+Q\xi]$, with real coefficients and $P,W>0$ in the interior, defines a <Sturm-Liouville problem>. Its realization as a <self-adjoint differential operator> requires a domain whose endpoint conditions annihilate the boundary form $[P(\eta\prime^*\xi-\eta^*\xi\prime)]$. Its <Rayleigh quotient> uses the weight $W$ in the denominator.

= Cubic-coordinate reduction of a singular Sturm-Liouville operator
{parent=Sturm-Liouville operator}
{title2=$L=-x^{-4}D^2+2x^{-5}D-a\ \longmapsto\ -D_z^2-a,\quad z=x^3/3$}

For $0<x<1$ and zero endpoint values, write $L=w^{-1}[-(p y')'+qy]$ with $p=x^{-2}$, $w=x^2$ and $q=-ax^2$. The change of variable $z=x^3/3$ has $dz=x^2dx$ and sends $y'=x^2Y_z$, $y''=2xY_z+x^4Y_{zz}$. Thus it is an isometry from the <weighted inner product> to the ordinary integral on $0<z<1/3$, and the apparent singular operator becomes $-Y_{zz}-aY$. Its <Dirichlet eigenvalues> are $9n^2\pi^2-a$ and its normalized <eigenfunctions> are $\sqrt6\sin(n\pi x^3)$, $n\geq1$. The singular endpoint is handled through this transformed regular domain; a zero eigenvalue occurs exactly when $a=9n^2\pi^2$.

= Flux-domain realization of a one-dimensional elliptic operator
{parent=Sturm-Liouville operator}
{title2=$D(L)=\{v\in H_0^1(0,1):pv'\in H^1(0,1)\}$}

For continuous $p>0$ and bounded real $q$, the form $a(u,v)=\int(pu'v'+quv)$ on the <zero-trace Sobolev space> defines the weak operator $L=-(pu')'+qu$. Its literal $L^2$ operator domain is the displayed flux domain. If a weak solution has right-hand side $f\in L^2$, then $(pu')'=qu-f\in L^2$, so it belongs to that domain. This avoids assuming that a merely differentiable coefficient has bounded derivative. With $p\in C^1$ bounded away from zero, the domain reduces to $H^2\cap H_0^1$.

= Lagrange identity for a Sturm-Liouville operator
{c}
{parent=Sturm-Liouville operator}
{title2=$uL(v)-vL(u)=[p(uv'-vu')]'$}

For $L(v)=(pv')'+av$, direct differentiation gives $uL(v)-vL(u)=[p(uv'-vu')]'$. The potential terms cancel. Integrating with common homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions> eliminates the endpoint term. If $L(u)=0$ and $L(v)=f$, this gives $\int uf=0$, a necessary <solvability condition> for the inhomogeneous equation.

= Vanishing endpoint coefficient and the Sturm-Liouville boundary form
{parent=Lagrange identity for a Sturm-Liouville operator}
{title2=$[p(\overline u v'-\overline{u'}v)]_a^b=0$}

For the real-coefficient <differential operator> $Mv=(pv')'+qv$, <integration by parts> gives
$$
\int_a^b(\overline uMv-\overline{Mu}v)\,dx=[p(\overline uv'-\overline{u'}v)]_a^b.
$$
If $p$ tends to zero at both endpoints and $u,v,u',v'$ remain bounded there, the boundary term vanishes without prescribing endpoint values. Thus regular endpoint behaviour can supply the domain condition that makes the expression symmetric. Endpoint degeneracy alone is insufficient for arbitrary singular functions: for $p=x(1-x)$ and $v=\log x$, one has $pv'=1-x\to1$ at zero. A <self-adjoint differential operator> requires a specified admissible domain, rather than only a formal differential expression.

A regular Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue problem has the form

+(p y')'+q y=-\lambda w y
$$
on a bounded interval with self-adjoint boundary conditions and positive weight $w$.

= Bounded solvability for the Legendre operator
{c}
{parent=Sturm-Liouville problem}
{title2=$((1-x^2)y')'=f,\quad\int_{-1}^1f=0$}

= Zero-mean compatibility for the Legendre operator
{c}
{synonym}

For $f\in C([-1,1])$, the equation $((1-x^2)y')'=f$ has a bounded solution on $(-1,1)$ if and only if $\int_{-1}^1f=0$. All bounded solutions differ by a constant. To prove necessity, integrate the equation once. The quantity $(1-x^2)y'$ has finite endpoint limits, and any nonzero endpoint limit would force logarithmic growth in $y$. Both limits must therefore vanish, yielding the zero-mean condition. For sufficiency set $F(x)=\int_{-1}^xf(t)dt$ and $y(x)=C+\int_0^x F(s)/(1-s^2)ds$. Zero mean implies $|F(s)|\leq\|f\|_\infty\min(1+s,1-s)$, so $y'$ is bounded and $y$ extends continuously to the endpoints. The homogeneous solutions are a constant and $\tfrac12\log((1+x)/(1-x))$, proving uniqueness up to a constant. This is the <Fredholm alternative> for the zero <eigenvalue> of this singular <Sturm-Liouville problem>.

The <eigenfunction expansion> in <Legendre polynomials> is $y=C-\sum_{k\geq1}P_k\int_{-1}^1fP_k/[k(k+1)\int_{-1}^1P_k^2]$. It follows by projecting onto the <eigenfunctions> with <eigenvalues> $-k(k+1)$. Completeness follows because the <Legendre polynomials> span the <polynomials>, which are dense by the <Weierstrass approximation theorem>.

Continuity only in the open interval requires more care. Even if $f$ is integrable and has zero mean, the primitive of $F/(1-x^2)$ must still be bounded at both ends. For example, $F(x)=1/\log(e/(1-x^2))$ has zero endpoint limits and absolutely integrable derivative $f=F'$, but $F/(1-x^2)$ has a divergent primitive. Thus zero mean alone is insufficient without endpoint control.

= Exponential ground state of a linearly weighted Sturm-Liouville problem
{parent=Sturm-Liouville problem}
{title2=$-(xy')'+\omega^2xy=\lambda y,\quad\lambda_0=\omega,\quad y_0=e^{-\omega x}$}

For $\omega>0$ on $x>0$, use the regular finite branch at zero, decay at infinity, and a finite quadratic-form energy. For trial functions with $[xy^2]_0^\infty=0$, <integration by parts> gives
$$
\int_0^\infty x(y'^2+\omega^2y^2)dx=\omega\int_0^\infty y^2dx+\int_0^\infty x(y'+\omega y)^2dx.
$$
The <Rayleigh quotient> is at least $\omega$, with equality exactly for a nonzero multiple of $e^{-\omega x}$. Direct substitution verifies that this is an <eigenfunction> with <eigenvalue> $\omega$, so the lower bound is attained. The zero coefficient $p(0)=0$ makes this a singular-endpoint <Sturm-Liouville problem>; regularity replaces a prescribed zero endpoint value. Continuous piecewise differentiable trials of finite form energy are allowed in the <Rayleigh-Ritz method>, even if their derivative has a jump away from the endpoint.

= No-log boundary condition at a linearly vanishing Sturm-Liouville endpoint
{parent=Sturm-Liouville problem}
{title2=$\lim_{x\downarrow a}p(x)y'(x)=0$}

Suppose $p(x)=c(x-a)+O((x-a)^2)$ with $c>0$, and the other coefficients in $(py')'+(\lambda w-q)y=0$ extend continuously to $a$, with $w(a)>0$. A bounded <eigenfunction> has $(py')'=O(1)$, hence $py'=C+O(x-a)$. A nonzero $C$ would give $y'\sim C/[c(x-a)]$ and an unbounded <logarithmic singularity>. Thus $C=0$, and the regular branch has bounded first <derivative>. The second local branch carries a logarithm. The title condition excludes that branch.

For two regular <eigenfunctions>, $p(\overline u v'-\overline{u'}v)\to0$. This removes the singular-end contribution to the <Lagrange identity for a Sturm-Liouville operator>. Together with a separated self-adjoint condition at the other endpoint, it selects the regular <self-adjoint differential operator> realization. The precise operator domain uses the maximal differential-operator domain and the no-log endpoint condition; boundedness of classical eigenfunctions is its convenient local expression, not a replacement for specifying an operator domain.

= Dirichlet-Robin eigenvalues on a finite interval
{parent=Sturm-Liouville problem}
{c}
{title2=$-y''=\lambda y,\quad y(0)=0,\quad y'(\ell)+ky(\ell)=0$}

For $\ell>0$ and $k>0$, the <Sturm-Liouville problem> with one <Dirichlet boundary condition> and one <Robin boundary condition> has positive <eigenvalues>. Indeed, <integration by parts> gives
$$
\lambda\int_0^\ell y^2\,dx=\int_0^\ell(y')^2\,dx+k y(\ell)^2>0
$$
for every nonzero <eigenfunction>. Let $z=\ell\sqrt\lambda$ and $\kappa=k\ell$. The <eigenfunctions> are proportional to $\sin(zx/\ell)$, with
$$
\tan z=-\frac z\kappa.
$$
For each $n\geq1$, there is exactly one root $z_n$ in $((n-1/2)\pi,n\pi)$: on that interval $\tan z+z/\kappa$ increases strictly from $-\infty$ to a positive value. There are no other positive roots. The apparent root $z=0$ gives only the zero sine function and is not an <eigenvalue>.

For fixed $k,\ell$, write $z_n=(n-1/2)\pi+\varepsilon_n$. The root equation becomes $\kappa\cot\varepsilon_n=(n-1/2)\pi+\varepsilon_n$, hence
$$
\varepsilon_n=\frac{\kappa}{(n-1/2)\pi}+O(n^{-3}),\qquad
\lambda_n=\frac{(n-1/2)^2\pi^2}{\ell^2}+\frac{2k}{\ell}+O(n^{-2}).
$$
An optional unit normalization uses $\int_0^\ell\sin^2(\sqrt{\lambda_n}x)\,dx=\ell/2+k/[2(k^2+\lambda_n)]$. The positive sign of the Robin boundary term matters: reversing it can introduce nonpositive modes, as in <nonpositive modes in a Dirichlet-Robin interval expansion>.

= Radial Dirichlet spectrum in three dimensions
{parent=Sturm-Liouville problem}
{title2=$\lambda_n=(n\pi/R)^2,\quad u_n(r)=\sin(n\pi r/R)/r$}

For the radial <Laplace operator> $L=-r^{-2}(r^2u')'$ on $0<r<R$, require a finite value at the origin and $u(R)=0$. The map $u\mapsto v=ru$ preserves the <norm> from $L^2((0,R),r^2dr)$ to $L^2(0,R)$ and sends $L$ to $-d^2/dr^2$. The regular endpoint condition becomes $v(0)=0$. The <self-adjoint> realization therefore corresponds to the ordinary <Dirichlet boundary conditions> for the one-dimensional second <derivative>. Its <eigenvalues> and <eigenfunctions> are displayed above, with $u_n(0)=n\pi/R$ understood by a <limit>. Their squared weighted <norm> is $R/2$. Completeness and <orthogonality> follow from the <Fourier sine series> on $(0,R)$.

= Inverse-square weighted Dirichlet spectrum
{parent=Sturm-Liouville problem}
{title2=$\lambda_n=1/4+[n\pi/\log(b/a)]^2$}

For $y''+\lambda x^{-2}y=0$ on $0<a<x<b$ with zero endpoint values, write $t=\log(x/a)$ and $y=\sqrt{x}\,w(t)$. The <Euler-Cauchy equation> becomes $w''+(\lambda-1/4)w=0$. Thus $y_n=\sqrt{x}\sin[n\pi\log(x/a)/\log(b/a)]$ and the exact spectrum is displayed above. Leading <WKB quantization> omits the constant $1/4$, while producing the same leading eigenfunction shape after quantization.

For a bounded domain $\Omega$, the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of $-\Delta$ has the variational characterization

= Sturm-Liouville eigenfunction expansion
{parent=Sturm-Liouville problem}
{c}

For a regular self-adjoint Sturm-Liouville problem, eigenfunctions are orthogonal in the weighted inner product
$$
\langle f,g\rangle_w=\int fgw,
$$
and the coefficient of $y_n$ is $\langle f,y_n\rangle_w/\langle y_n,y_n\rangle_w$.

= Boundary data in a Sturm-Liouville eigenfunction expansion
{parent=Sturm-Liouville eigenfunction expansion}
{title2=$c_n=\frac{\int u_nf-[p(u_ny'-u_n'y)]_a^b}{(\lambda_n+\mu)N_n}$}

Let $Ay=(py')'+qy$ have real <eigenfunctions> $u_n$ obeying $Au_n=\lambda_nwu_n$ and homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions>, with $N_n=\int_a^bwu_n^2>0$. Suppose $Ay+\mu wy=f$, with possibly nonzero endpoint values. The <Lagrange identity for a Sturm-Liouville operator> gives
$$
\int_a^bu_nf\,dx-(\lambda_n+\mu)\int_a^bwu_ny\,dx=[p(u_ny'-u_n'y)]_a^b.
$$
Division by $(\lambda_n+\mu)N_n$ gives the displayed coefficient whenever the denominator is nonzero. At <resonance in a differential equation>, equality of the forcing projection and the boundary term is the <Fredholm solvability condition>. The <Sturm-Liouville eigenfunction expansion> represents the interior solution in the <weighted inner product> sense; its zero endpoint terms do not reproduce nonzero <boundary traces> by pointwise substitution. A smooth lift of the endpoint data reduces the calculation to homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions>.

= Nonpositive modes in a Dirichlet-Robin interval expansion
{parent=Sturm-Liouville eigenfunction expansion}
{title2=$y(0)=0,\quad y'(1)=y(1)/\alpha,\quad\alpha>0$}

For $-y''=\lambda y$, positive <eigenvalues> have <eigenfunctions> $\sin(q_nx)$, where $\tan q_n=\alpha q_n$ and $q_n>0$. Their squared <norms> are $N_n=1/2-\sin(2q_n)/(4q_n)$. A zero <eigenvalue> occurs precisely at $\alpha=1$, with <eigenfunction> $x$. A negative <eigenvalue> occurs precisely at $0<\alpha<1$: it is $-\kappa^2$ with <eigenfunction> $\sinh(\kappa x)$, where $\tanh\kappa=\alpha\kappa$. There is exactly one such $\kappa$, since $\tanh\kappa/\kappa$ decreases strictly from $1$ to $0$. The <integration by parts> identity
$$
\langle y,-y''\rangle=\int_0^1|y'|^2dx-\frac{|y(1)|^2}{\alpha}
$$
and the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> show positivity for $\alpha>1$ and nonnegativity at equality. The complete expansion of a <square-integrable function> therefore includes the extra term $3x\int_0^1tf(t)dt$ when $\alpha=1$, or $\sinh(\kappa x)\int_0^1f(t)\sinh(\kappa t)dt/[\sinh(2\kappa)/(4\kappa)-1/2]$ when $\alpha<1$, in addition to $\sum_n[\int_0^1f(t)\sin(q_nt)dt/N_n]\sin(q_nx)$. Omitting that extra mode makes the system incomplete. In particular, $f=x$ at $\alpha=1$ is orthogonal to every positive sine mode and is an immediate counterexample to a sine-only expansion.

= Spectral Green function of a regular Sturm-Liouville problem
{parent=Sturm-Liouville eigenfunction expansion}
{title2=$G_\mu(x,\xi)=\sum_n y_n(x)y_n(\xi)/(\lambda_n-\mu)$}

Write $\mathcal A y=-(py')'+qy$ and $\mathcal A y_n=\lambda_n r y_n$, where $r$ is the positive weight. Normalize the real <eigenfunctions> using $\int r y_my_n=\delta_{mn}$. For $\mu$ outside the <spectrum>, applying $\mathcal A-\mu r$ to the displayed <Green function> expansion gives $r(x)\sum_n y_n(x)y_n(\xi)=\delta(x-\xi)$ by weighted completeness. This is the kernel with respect to ordinary source integration $d\xi$; using the weighted measure would change its normalization.

= Normalized Sturm-Liouville spectral Green kernel
{parent=Spectral Green function of a regular Sturm-Liouville problem}
{title2=$G_\mu(x,\xi)=w(\xi)\sum_n\frac{y_n(x)y_n(\xi)^*}{\lambda_n-\mu}$}

Let $L=w^{-1}[-(d/dx)(p\,d/dx)+q]$ be the regular self-adjoint operator of a <Sturm-Liouville problem>, with <eigenfunctions> orthonormal for the <weighted inner product> $\int w\overline uv$. For $\mu$ outside its <spectrum>, the displayed kernel satisfies $(L_x-\mu)G_\mu=\delta(x-\xi)$ with an ordinary, unweighted <Dirac delta>. Indeed weighted completeness gives $w(\xi)\sum_ny_n(x)y_n(\xi)^*=\delta(x-\xi)$. Thus $u(x)=\int G_\mu(x,\xi)f(\xi)d\xi$ solves $(L-\mu)u=f$. A kernel integrated against $w(\xi)d\xi$ instead omits the prefactor. For real coefficients, the weighted reciprocity relation is $G_\mu(x,\xi)/w(\xi)=G_\mu(\xi,x)/w(x)$ when the spectral parameter is real and outside the spectrum. A zero eigenvalue prevents inversion at $\mu=0$ without a forcing compatibility condition or a projected inverse.

= Odd reciprocal-square sum from a Dirichlet Green function
{parent=Spectral Green function of a regular Sturm-Liouville problem}
{title2=$\sum_{j\ge0}(2j+1)^{-2}=\pi^2/8$}

For $L=d^2/dx^2$ on $(0,\pi)$ with zero endpoints, the zero-parameter <Green function> is $-x_<(\pi-x_>)/\pi$. Its <eigenfunctions> are $\sqrt{2/\pi}\sin(nx)$ with eigenvalues $-n^2$. At $x=\xi=\pi/2$, the kernel is $-\pi/4$ and its expansion contains precisely the odd reciprocal squares, giving the displayed identity.

= Self-adjoint differential operator
{parent=Sturm-Liouville theory}
{wiki}

A differential operator is self-adjoint when integration by parts makes its inner products symmetric on its domain.

= Self-adjoint fourth-order scalar differential operator
{title2=$D^4+pD^2+p'D+r$}
{parent=Self-adjoint differential operator}

For real coefficient functions, the fourth-order scalar operator
$$
L=D^4+pD^2+qD+r
$$
is formally self-adjoint exactly when $q=p'$. Indeed, $(pD^2)^*=pD^2+2p'D+p''$ and $(qD)^*=-qD-q'$. Under <clamped boundary condition>[clamped boundary conditions], its quadratic form is
$$
\langle y,Ly\rangle=\int\left[(y'')^2-p(y')^2+ry^2\right]dx.
$$

= Lagrange identity for a fourth-order differential operator
{parent=Self-adjoint fourth-order scalar differential operator}
{c}

For $L=D^4+q(x)$ where $q$ is a real <continuous function> on $[a,b]$, <integration by parts> gives
$$
\int_a^b\bigl(\overline u Lv-\overline{Lu}v\bigr)\,dx
=\left[\overline u v'''-\overline{u'}v''+\overline{u''}v'-\overline{u'''}v\right]_a^b.
$$
The potential terms cancel, and differentiating the boundary expression verifies the identity. Under <clamped boundary conditions> $u=u'=v=v'=0$ at both endpoints, this form vanishes. Conversely, requiring its vanishing against every clamped test <function> forces the adjoint <function> and its first <derivative> to vanish at both endpoints, because the second and third test-<function> <derivatives> are independent endpoint traces. Thus, on a regular finite <interval> with the usual fourth-order Sobolev domain, the clamped realization is <self-adjoint>. The equality of domains is part of this conclusion; formal <self-adjointness> of the expression alone does not supply it.

= Self-adjoint differential equation
{parent=Self-adjoint differential operator}

A second-order scalar differential equation is in self-adjoint form when it can be written
$$
(p(x)y')'+q(x)y=0.
$$
Multiplication by $y$ and integration by parts then gives an energy identity.

= Solvability condition at a Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue
{parent=Self-adjoint differential operator}

If $L(y_0;\lambda_0)=0$ and $y_0,y$ satisfy homogeneous separated boundary conditions, the Lagrange identity gives
$$
\int y_0L(y;\lambda_0)\,dx
=\int yL(y_0;\lambda_0)\,dx=0.
$$
Thus $L(y;\lambda_0)=f$ can be solved only if $f$ is orthogonal to the null mode $y_0$.

= Fredholm solvability condition
{parent=Solvability condition at a Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue}

At a simple eigenvalue of a self-adjoint linear operator, an inhomogeneous equation is solvable only when its forcing is orthogonal to the null eigenfunction. In weakly nonlinear expansions, this condition determines the amplitude equation.

= Translation-mode solvability for a bistable front
{parent=Fredholm solvability condition}
{title2=$c_1=-\int U_0\prime h(U_0)/\int(U_0\prime)^2$}

Suppose $u_t=Du_{xx}+f_0(u)+\varepsilon h(u)$ has a balanced stationary front $U_0(z)$, where $z=(x-q)/\sqrt D$ and $U_0''+f_0(U_0)=0$. Take $\dot q/\sqrt D=\varepsilon c_1+\cdots$ and $u=U_0+\varepsilon U_1+\cdots$. At first order, $LU_1=-c_1U_0'-h(U_0)$ with $L=d^2/dz^2+f_0'(U_0)$. Differentiating the front equation proves $LU_0'=0$. Integration by parts against the decaying <translation> mode gives
$$
c_1=-\frac{\int_{-\infty}^\infty U_0'h(U_0)\,dz}{\int_{-\infty}^\infty(U_0')^2\,dz}.
$$
This is the <Fredholm solvability condition> determining the velocity, derived from the <self-adjoint differential operator> rather than assumed. An orthogonality condition or a fixed midpoint removes the freedom to add a multiple of $U_0'$ to $U_1$. For the <bistable cubic reaction-diffusion equation>, $h=-aU_0(1-U_0)$ gives $c_1=-\sqrt2a$, and its first-order forcing vanishes pointwise, so the phase-fixed shape correction is zero.

= Moving-defect locking of a cubic-quintic front
{parent=Translation-mode solvability for a bistable front}

At the <Maxwell balance for a scalar reaction-diffusion front>, let $q=3\alpha/4$, $\beta=\sqrt3\alpha/2$, and perturb the linear growth rate by $\varepsilon K\delta(x+cT)$, $T=\varepsilon t$. Projection onto the front's translation mode gives $X_T=-K\operatorname{sech}^2(\beta Z/2)$, where $Z=X+cT+\beta^{-1}\log q$. Hence $Z_T=c-K\operatorname{sech}^2(\beta Z/2)$. Finite locked positions exist for $0<c/K\le1$. For a strict inequality, the root with $KZ<0$ is stable and the other unstable; they meet in a <saddle-node bifurcation> at $c=K$. This is a leading-order <solvability condition>, not an exact finite-perturbation solution.

= Leading nonlinear eigenvalue shift in a Sturm-Liouville problem
{parent=Solvability condition at a Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue}

For $L(y;\lambda)=y^{m+1}$ near a normalized simple null mode $y_0$, put $y=\varepsilon y_0+O(\varepsilon^2)$ and $\lambda-\lambda_0=\varepsilon^m\mu$. Orthogonality to $y_0$ yields
$$
\mu=\int y_0^{m+2}\,dx+O(\varepsilon).
$$

= Amplitude dependence of a nonlinear eigenvalue correction
{parent=Leading nonlinear eigenvalue shift in a Sturm-Liouville problem}
{title2=$\nu(C)=C^{p-1}\nu(1)$}

Suppose a real simple leading <eigenfunction> is $y_0=C\phi$ and a first-order correction satisfies $L_0v=-\nu w y_0-y_0^p$, where $p\geq2$ is an <integer>, $L_0$ is <self-adjoint> with <null space> spanned by $\phi$, the <boundary conditions> are homogeneous and independent of the parameter, and $w>0$. The <Fredholm solvability condition> gives
$$
\nu(C)=-C^{p-1}\frac{\int\phi^{p+1}\,dx}{\int w\phi^2\,dx}.
$$
Indeed, <orthogonality> of the forcing to $\phi$ gives $\nu C\int w\phi^2+C^p\int\phi^{p+1}=0$, and $C\ne0$ permits division. Thus the numerical <eigenvalue> correction depends on the chosen leading amplitude; fixing an <eigenfunction> normalization is part of defining the nonlinear branch. This calculation assumes a perturbative branch and does not by itself establish that branch's existence.
