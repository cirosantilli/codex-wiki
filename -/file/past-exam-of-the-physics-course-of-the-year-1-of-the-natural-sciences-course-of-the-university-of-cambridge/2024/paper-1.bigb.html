= Solution
{parent=1}

The <surface tension> has dimensions $[\gamma]=MT^{-2}$, while $[\rho]=ML^{-3}$ and $[g]=LT^{-2}$. Consequently
$$
\left[\frac{\gamma}{\rho gd^n}\right]=\frac{MT^{-2}}{(ML^{-3})(LT^{-2})L^n}=L^{2-n}.
$$
Matching the dimension of height gives $2-n=1$, so **$n=1$**. <Dimensional analysis> fixes this exponent but cannot determine a dimensionless prefactor or a contact-angle dependence.

Using the convention $e^{i\omega t}$, the series <RLC circuit> has <complex impedance>
$$
Z=R+i\left(\omega L-\frac1{\omega C}\right),\qquad\omega=2\pi f.
$$
At the stated <frequency>, $\omega L=180.956\,\Omega$ and $1/(\omega C)=88.4194\,\Omega$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Im}Z=+92.5\,\Omega,\quad |Z|=533\,\Omega,\quad\arg Z=+10.0^\circ.}
$$
The vector is $(525,92.5)$ in the resistance–<reactance> plane. Positive <reactance> means that the net circuit is inductive and current lags voltage. With the alternative $e^{-i\omega t}$ convention the imaginary signs reverse, while the magnitude and physical <phase> relationship agree.

= Solution
{parent=3}

The string's no-slip condition gives $v=R\omega$. Conservation of <mechanical energy> from release to the stated height drop yields
$$
mgh=\frac12mv^2+\frac12I\frac{v^2}{R^2}.
$$
Solving for the spool's <moment of inertia>,
$$
\boxed{I=\frac{2mghR^2}{v^2}-mR^2,\qquad A=2mghR^2,\quad B=mR^2.}
$$
The axle does no work because its point of application is fixed, and the ideal string and axle introduce no losses. The spool <mass> $M$ is not needed to infer $I$ from this energy measurement.

The slit spacing is $d=(600\times10^3)^{-1}\,\mathrm m=1.6667\,\mu\mathrm m$. At normal incidence, the <diffraction grating> maxima satisfy $d\sin\theta_m=m\lambda$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\theta_1=\pm22.3^\circ,\qquad\theta_2=\pm49.4^\circ.}
$$
The third order would require $\sin\theta_3=3\lambda/d=1.1394>1$, so it cannot propagate. At fixed order and spacing, a longer <wavelength> requires larger $|\theta|$. **Green light has a larger first-order <diffraction> <angle> than blue light.**

= Solution
{parent=5}

At closest approach the <velocity> is tangential. The conserved specific <orbital energy> and <specific angular momentum> are
$$
\varepsilon=\frac{v_p^2}{2}-\frac{GM}{r_p}=-2.1972\times10^8\,\mathrm{m^2s^{-2}},\qquad\ell=r_pv_p.
$$
The negative energy gives an <elliptic orbit>. Its <semi-major axis> is
$$
a=-\frac{GM}{2\varepsilon}=3.0376\times10^{11}\,\mathrm m.
$$
Since $r_p+r_a=2a$,
$$
\boxed{r_a=2a-r_p=5.58\times10^{11}\,\mathrm m.}
$$
This treats the star as fixed, appropriate to $m\ll M$. Equivalently, equate the energy at the two turning points using $v_a=\ell/r_a$. The planet <mass> cancels; a circular-orbit relation would be inappropriate here.

After the push, the <forces> are weight $mg$ downward, <normal reaction> $N$ upward and sliding <friction> $F=\mu N$ opposite the <velocity>. There is no continuing applied push. The table has constant slope
$$
a=\frac{1.02-1.20}{1.50}=-0.120\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}.
$$
Vertical balance gives $N=mg$, and horizontal motion gives $ma=-\mu mg$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mu=\frac{0.120}{9.81}=0.0122.}
$$
The <friction> magnitude is $0.0300\,\mathrm N$.

The inclined parcel has <tension> $T$ up the slope, weight $mg$ vertically downward, <normal reaction> $N=mg\cos\theta$ and <friction> $\mu mg\cos\theta$ down the slope. The hanging <mass> has weight downward and <tension> upward. The ideal string makes the two <acceleration> magnitudes equal.

The <velocity> slope is **$a=2.4/1.5=1.60\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$**, up the track for the parcel and downward for the hanging <mass>. The <free-body diagrams> show gravity itself as one vector; its slope-parallel and normal components are used in the equations rather than counted as additional <forces>.

Let $m_p=0.250\,\mathrm{kg}$ and let $m_w$ be the hanging <mass>. Taking up-slope and downward respectively as positive,
$$
T-m_pg\sin\theta-\mu m_pg\cos\theta=m_pa,\qquad m_wg-T=m_wa.
$$
Eliminate the common <tension> to obtain
$$
\boxed{m_w=\frac{m_p\{a+g(\sin\theta+\mu\cos\theta)\}}{g-a}=0.130\,\mathrm{kg}.}
$$
Here $\theta=10^\circ$, $\mu=0.1$ and $a=1.60\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. The pulley changes the direction of the string <force>; in the ideal model it does not change its magnitude.

Immediately after the parcel leaves, the <spring> still stores $\tfrac12kx^2$, but only the $m_s=0.300\,\mathrm{kg}$ sledge is launched. Choose <gravitational potential> zero at the ramp base. Its initial position is $-x$, so <conservation of energy> to the apex gives

above the ramp base. Its upward travel from the compressed position is $H_s+x=2.56882\,\mathrm m$, a different distance from the height above the base.

= Solution

For actual complete bounce distances $D_1=4\,\mathrm m$ and $D_{n+1}=rD_n$, the <geometric series> gives
$$
\boxed{D_{\rm total}=\frac{4}{1-r}\,\mathrm m,\qquad0\leq r<1.}
$$
A natural intended estimate compares the observed first round trip with the loss-free round trip from the initially compressed position,
$$
D_{\rm ideal}=2(H_s+x)=5.13764\,\mathrm m.
$$
If the reduction factor is taken to be $r=D_1/D_{\rm ideal}=0.77857$, the answer is **$D_{\rm total}\simeq18.1\,\mathrm m$**. The first term is the actual 4 m, not the hypothetical loss-free distance.

This numerical inference needs the stated comparison assumption. Strictly, knowing the first actual bounce distance and saying subsequent distances share a ratio does not independently specify that ratio. A change in <spring> compression also means that an energy-loss fraction is not automatically the same as a vertical-distance ratio.

The collinear <relativistic velocity addition> formula is
$$
u=\frac{v+u'}{1+vu'/c^2}.
$$
With both <velocities> directed toward Earth,
$$
\boxed{u=\frac{0.6c+0.8c}{1+0.48}=\frac{35}{37}c=0.946c.}
$$
The denominator keeps the result below $c$. Adding the two speeds by the Galilean rule would give an inadmissible $1.4c$.

= b
{parent=7}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The separation simultaneous in the mothership frame at launch is the <length contraction> of the Earth-frame simultaneous separation:
$$
D'=\frac{2\,\mathrm{ly}}{\gamma_m}=1.60\,\mathrm{ly},\qquad\gamma_m=\frac1{\sqrt{1-0.6^2}}=1.25.
$$
In that frame Earth approaches the launch position at $0.6c$ while the lander travels toward Earth at $0.8c$. Their coordinate separation closes at $1.4c$, so
$$
\boxed{t'_{\rm arrival}=\frac{1.60\,\mathrm{ly}}{1.4c}=\frac87\,\mathrm{yr}=1.14\,\mathrm{yr}.}
$$
A closing rate between two objects can exceed $c$; it is not either object's speed in the other's rest frame. Simultaneity matters: simply transforming the Earth event at Earth-frame launch time would not give the simultaneous mothership-frame separation.

Equating the lander and return-signal positions gives
$$
\frac{35}{37}t_D=2-\left(t_D-\frac{25}{12}\right),\qquad\boxed{t_D=\frac{1813}{864}\,\mathrm{yr}=2.09838\,\mathrm{yr}.}

The central rod occupies the holes in the discs, so the disc masses must be annular rather than counted as solid discs overlapping the rod. Define $C=L_1\rho_1=2L_2\rho_2$. Then
$$
M=\pi r_1^2L_1\rho_1+2\pi(r_2^2-r_1^2)L_2\rho_2=\pi Cr_2^2.
$$
The axial <moment of inertia> is
$$
I=\frac\pi2L_1\rho_1r_1^4+\pi L_2\rho_2(r_2^4-r_1^4)=\frac\pi2Cr_2^4.
$$
Thus **$I=\tfrac12Mr_2^2$**, exactly the axial inertia of a uniform disc of outer radius $r_2$ and <mass> $M$. The cancellation uses the given <density>–length relation.

{parent=8}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The contact radius is $r_1$, so <rolling without slipping> gives $v=r_1\omega$. With $I=Mr_2^2/2$, the energy at the minimum of the stated parabolic motion model is
$$
Mgh_0=\frac12Mv_{\max}^2+\frac12\frac{Mr_2^2}{2}\frac{v_{\max}^2}{r_1^2}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{v_{\max}=\frac{2\sqrt{gh_0}}{\sqrt{(r_2/r_1)^2+2}}=0.148\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The corresponding spin <angular frequency> is **$\omega_{\max}=v_{\max}/r_1=14.8\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$**. It is the rod radius, not the disc radius, that relates translation and rotation; the discs determine the larger rotational inertia.

= Solution
{parent=c}

In the small-amplitude approximation, $\dot x$ is the speed along the track, and $y=Bx^2$ supplies the <gravitational potential>. The conserved energy is
$$
E=\frac M2\left(1+\frac{r_2^2}{2r_1^2}\right)\dot x^2+MgBx^2.
$$
Differentiating and extending through turning points by continuity gives
$$
\left(1+\frac{r_2^2}{2r_1^2}\right)\ddot x+2gBx=0.
$$
This is <simple harmonic motion> with $\omega_0^2=4gB/\{(r_2/r_1)^2+2\}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{f_0=\frac{\sqrt{gB}}{\pi\sqrt{(r_2/r_1)^2+2}}=0.235\,\mathrm{Hz}.}
$$
The <frequency> is lower than for a sliding point <mass> because energy is also stored in rotation.

For weak linear <damping>, write the amplitude envelope as $A(t)=A_0e^{-\omega_0t/(2Q)}$. The amplitude e-folding time is $2Q/\omega_0$, and one period is approximately $2\pi/\omega_0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{N=\frac Q\pi=31.8\text{ oscillations}.}

Energy, being proportional to amplitude squared, decays twice as rapidly in its exponent. Using its e-folding time instead would introduce a factor-of-two error.

$V(x)$ is the particle's <potential energy> as a function of position, and $E$ is its total energy <eigenvalue> in this <time-independent Schrödinger equation>. Solving it with the boundary conditions determines the allowed <stationary states> and energy levels.

The <wavefunction> is a <probability> amplitude: the <Born rule> gives position <probability> <density> $|\psi(x)|^2$, normalized to unit <integral> for one particle. $\psi$ itself need not be real or positive and is not a classical matter-density profile. The full stationary-state time dependence is $\Psi(x,t)=\psi(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar}$, leaving $|\Psi|^2$ time independent.

{parent=9}

= Solution
{parent=b}

For a nonzero integer mode, normalize the <wavefunction>:
$$
1=|A|^2\int_0^L\sin^2\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx=|A|^2\frac L2.
$$
Thus **$|A|=\sqrt{2/L}$**. Choosing an arbitrary overall <phase> makes $A=\sqrt{2/L}$ real and positive. The physical modes are conventionally indexed $n=1,2,\ldots$; $n=0$ would be the zero function and cannot be normalized.

\boxed{E_2=37.6\,\mathrm{eV}=6.02\times10^{-18}\,\mathrm J.}

The <density> is symmetric about $L/2$: $p(L-x)=p(x)$. Denote the <expectation> by $\mu=\int_0^Lxp(x)\,dx$. Substitution $u=L-x$ gives

For a symmetric finite well supporting this excited bound state, the <wavefunction> and its derivative match continuously at each finite potential step. Outside, where $V_{\rm wall}>E$, the solution decays exponentially with
$$
\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{2m(V_{\rm wall}-E)}}\hbar.

The <density> therefore extends into the classically forbidden region instead of dropping to zero at the walls. In <magnetostatics>, <Ampère's circuital law> is
$$
\oint_C\mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=\mu_0I_{\rm enclosed}.
$$
It relates magnetic circulation around a closed curve to the steady current through a spanning surface. It is especially useful when <symmetry> makes the tangential field constant on a chosen path: an infinite straight wire gives $B=\mu_0I/(2\pi r)$, while an ideal long <solenoid> gives approximately $B=\mu_0nI$ inside.

The <Biot-Savart law> gives the field contribution from the actual current geometry:
$$
\mathbf B(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\int\frac{d\boldsymbol\ell'\times(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|^3}.
$$
The <cross product> determines the direction by the <right-hand rule>. Integrating around a circular loop of radius $a$ gives $B=\mu_0I/(2a)$ at its centre. A finite wire segment can also be integrated directly, even though it lacks enough <symmetry> for an Ampère-loop shortcut. The full steady-current circuit includes a return path; a segment calculation specifies that segment's contribution.

{parent=10}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Place the wire on the $y$ axis, its endpoints at $-a$ and $b$, and the observation point at $(-d,0)$. With current along $+y$, $d\boldsymbol\ell'\times\mathbf r$ points out of the page. Its magnitude gives
$$
B=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\int_{-a}^b\frac{d\,dy}{(d^2+y^2)^{3/2}}=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi d}\left[\frac y{\sqrt{d^2+y^2}}\right]_{-a}^{b}.
$$
In the question's <angle> convention, $\cos\beta=b/\sqrt{d^2+b^2}$ and $\cos\alpha=-a/\sqrt{d^2+a^2}$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{B=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi d}(\cos\beta-\cos\alpha).}
$$
The minus sign in $\cos\alpha$ matters because $\alpha$ is the exterior obtuse <angle>.

For a regular hexagon of side $a$, the perpendicular centre-to-side distance is $d=\sqrt3a/2$. Each side subtends endpoint <angles> with $\cos\beta=1/2$ and $\cos\alpha=-1/2$. Its centre field is consequently $\mu_0I/(4\pi d)$.

All six contributions have the same normal direction, so
$$
\boxed{B_{\rm centre}=6\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi d}=\frac{\sqrt3\,\mu_0I}{\pi a}.}
$$
The direction follows the current's <right-hand rule>. Equal side lengths alone would not fix an arbitrary distorted hexagon's field; the usual regular-coil geometry is used here.

= Solution

The regular hexagon has area $A=6(\sqrt3a^2/4)=3\sqrt3a^2/2$. Choose $t=0$ with its positive normal parallel to the imposed field. The external <magnetic flux> is $\Phi=B_0A\cos\omega t$, so <Faraday's law of induction> gives
$$
\mathcal E=-\frac{d\Phi}{dt}=B_0A\omega\sin\omega t.
$$
Taking the stated resistance to determine the loop response,
$$
\boxed{I(t)=\frac{3\sqrt3B_0a^2\omega}{2R}\sin\omega t.}
$$
A different initial orientation adds a <phase>. The sign is with respect to the chosen loop orientation and obeys <Lenz's law>; reversal of that orientation reverses the signed current. This expression neglects <self-inductance>, as in the resistive-loop model.
