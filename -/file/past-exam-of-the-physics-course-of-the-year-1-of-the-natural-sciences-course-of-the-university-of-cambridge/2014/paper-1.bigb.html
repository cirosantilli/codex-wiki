= Solution

Take $q=\ddot x$ positive when the hanging <mass> descends and the other <mass> moves uphill. For the sliding block, <Newton's second law> gives $mg-T=mq$ and $T-Mg\sin\theta=Mq$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{q=\frac{g(m-M\sin\theta)}{m+M},\qquad
T=\frac{mMg(1+\sin\theta)}{m+M}.}
$$
For the <pulley coupled to a rolling disc>, tension and weight act through its centre; the contact normal is perpendicular to the slope. <Static friction> provides the <torque> required for <rolling without slipping>. Define signed $F$ down the slope, so $F>0$ for uphill <acceleration>.

\Image[paper-1-ia2014-disc-forces.png]
{title=Original force diagram for an uphill-accelerating disc: centre-applied tension and weight, contact normal and downslope static friction}

The rotational equation and rolling constraint give $Fa=I q/a$, hence $F=Mq/2$. The translational equations become $T-Mg\sin\theta-F=Mq$ and $mg-T=mq$, giving
$$
\boxed{q=\frac{2g(m-M\sin\theta)}{2m+3M},\qquad
T=\frac{mMg(3+2\sin\theta)}{2m+3M},\qquad
F=\frac{Mg(m-M\sin\theta)}{2m+3M}.}
$$
Thus **the threshold remains $m>M\sin\theta$**: the <moment of inertia> increases the effective <mass> but does not change the sign of the driving <force>. For downhill <acceleration> $F$ reverses. The <normal force> is $Mg\cos\theta$; <static friction> does no work at the stationary contact point and sufficient roughness is assumed.

= Solution
{parent=B8}

In standard configuration the frames are inertial, their corresponding axes are parallel, S$'$ moves at $v$ along the positive $x$ axis of S, and their origins coincide at $t=t'=0$. Here $(t,x,y,z)$ and $(t',x',y',z')$ label the same event, $c$ is the <speed of light> and $\gamma=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$.

A clock fixed in S$'$ has $\Delta x'=0$. The inverse time transformation gives
$$
\boxed{\Delta t=\gamma\Delta t'=\gamma\Delta\tau,}
$$
so the moving clock accumulates less <proper time> between its two ticks than the elapsed S-coordinate time.

Without <time dilation> the flight time from the given altitude would be $66.7\,\mathrm{\mu s}$, about $30.3$ rest-frame <mean lifetimes>, giving survival fraction only $e^{-30.3}\simeq6.8\times10^{-14}$ even at <speed> $c$. Relativistic <muons> can instead reach the ground with appreciable survival. Momentum-selected decay measurements test this clock-rate dependence experimentally.<Variation of the Rate of Decay of Mesotrons with Momentum by Bruno Rossi and David B. Hall>[Physical Review 59, 223 (1941), primary abstract: different momentum groups have different observed decay rates consistent with relativistic moving clocks]{ref}

A <mean-lifetime survival energy estimate> requires a mean flight time no greater than the dilated <mean lifetime>:
$$
\gamma\tau_0\gtrsim\frac Hc,\qquad
\boxed{\gamma\gtrsim30.3,\qquad E=\gamma m_\mu c^2\gtrsim3.21\,\mathrm{GeV}.}
$$
This corresponds to <survival probability> at least about $e^{-1}$. **There is no sharp minimum <energy> for an individual random decay.** For a required probability $p$, use $\exp[-H/(\gamma v\tau_0)]\ge p$. <Energy> loss in the atmosphere is neglected in this estimate. In the <muon>'s frame the atmospheric path is length-contracted to $H/\gamma\simeq660\,\mathrm m$; the ground reaches it in about $\tau_0$, giving the same <survival probability>.

For the two-body decay, use the <rest frame> of A. <Momentum> conservation makes B and the <photon> oppositely directed with equal <momentum> magnitude $p$. <Energy> conservation and the <energy-momentum relation> give
$$
m_Ac^2=E_B+pc,\qquad E_B^2=p^2c^2+m_B^2c^4.
$$
Substitution and cancellation of $E_B^2$ give
$$
\boxed{E_B=\frac{(m_A^2+m_B^2)c^2}{2m_A},\qquad
E_\gamma=\frac{(m_A^2-m_B^2)c^2}{2m_A}.}
$$
A nonzero emitted <photon> requires $m_A>m_B$.

About a specified axis, the <moment of inertia> is $I=\int r_\perp^2\,dm$, giving <rotational kinetic energy> $I\omega^2/2$ for fixed-axis rotation. With square coordinates centred on the plate,
$$
I_x=\frac{M}{l^2}\int_{-l/2}^{l/2}\int_{-l/2}^{l/2}y^2\,dx\,dy
=\boxed{\frac{Ml^2}{12}}.
$$
The <in-plane inertia isotropy of a square plate> follows using the <perpendicular axis theorem>, which gives $I_z=I_x+I_y=Ml^2/6$. For any in-plane central axis at angle $\phi$, the perpendicular in-plane axis has the same inertia by the square's $90^\circ$ rotational symmetry. Their sum is $I_z$, so **every such in-plane axis has $I=Ml^2/12$**.

The <parallel axis theorem> gives hinge inertia $I_H=Ml^2/3$. During the short <impulse> <gravity> is negligible, and the hinge <impulse> has no moment about the hinge. Therefore
$$
I_H\omega=K(l/2+a),\qquad
\boxed{v=\frac{\omega l}{2}=\frac{3K}{4M}\left(1+\frac{2a}{l}\right).}
$$
The <hinge impulse of an edge-pivoted square plate> follows from <linear momentum>: $J_H=Mv-K$. Taking its positive direction to be that of the applied <impulse>,
$$
\boxed{J_H=K\left(\frac{3a}{2l}-\frac14\right),\qquad
N=|J_H|.}
$$
Generally $K$ alone does not supply the centre-of-mass <momentum> compatible with rotation about the fixed hinge, so the hinge supplies the difference. Its <impulse> vanishes at the <centre of percussion>,
$$
\boxed{a=l/6.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=C10}

For sinusoidal <steady state> with convention $e^{i\omega t}$, <complex impedance> is the <voltage> <phasor> divided by the current <phasor>. Its modulus is the <amplitude> ratio and its argument is the voltage-current <phase difference>. Since $V_L=L\,dI/dt$,
$$
Z_L=i\omega L,\qquad Z_C=\frac1{i\omega C},\qquad Z_R=R.
$$
The original circuit is a <series RLC circuit> with output across the <inductor>. Its transfer function is
$$
\boxed{H(\omega)=\frac{V_2}{V_1}
=\frac{i\omega L}{R+i(\omega L-1/(\omega C))},\qquad
|V_2|=\frac{|V_1|\omega L}{\sqrt{R^2+(\omega L-1/(\omega C))^2}}.}
$$
For the original <capacitance>, the outputs are **$10.15\,\mathrm{mV}$ at $9\,\mathrm{MHz}$ and $4.65\,\mathrm{mV}$ at $10\,\mathrm{MHz}$**, a ratio $0.458$. The current <resonance> is $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})=8.967\,\mathrm{MHz}$, so the circuit favors the first transmitter.

The <resistor> <voltage> is in <phase> with the current; the <inductor> <voltage> leads it by $\pi/2$ and the <capacitor> <voltage> lags by $\pi/2$. Large reactive <voltages> therefore nearly cancel in their <phasor> sum. A large <inductor> <voltage> is consistent with a small supply <voltage>: it is **<inductor voltage magnification>, not <power gain>** in a passive circuit.

Reducing the <capacitance> raises $f_0$ to $9.452\,\mathrm{MHz}$, between the two transmitters. Direct evaluation gives **$7.02\,\mathrm{mV}$ and $7.22\,\mathrm{mV}$**, respectively. Their similar detunings give approximately equal <amplitudes>; they are not exactly equal, partly because the numerator of $H$ also depends on <frequency>.

The <two-tone output of a linear resonant circuit>, for coherent monochromatic sources of equal input <amplitude> and arbitrary fixed input <phases>, is
$$
V_{\rm out}(t)=A\cos(2\pi f_A t+\phi_A)+B\cos(2\pi f_B t+\phi_B).
$$
With $A\simeq B=V_o$, this is approximately
$$
\boxed{V_{\rm out}\simeq2V_o
\cos\!\left[\pi(f_B-f_A)t+\frac{\phi_B-\phi_A}{2}\right]
\cos\!\left[2\pi\frac{f_A+f_B}{2}t+\frac{\phi_A+\phi_B}{2}\right].}
$$
Thus a $9.5\,\mathrm{MHz}$ rapid <oscillation> has a <beat> envelope repeating in magnitude at $1\,\mathrm{MHz}$. The signed envelope cosine has <frequency> $0.5\,\mathrm{MHz}$. Unequal <amplitudes> leave a small nonzero envelope minimum; independently drifting transmitter <phases> make the beating drift. No new <frequency> is generated by this ideal linear circuit.

= Solution

For the <spring> oscillator,
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12m\dot x^2+\frac12kx^2\right)
=\dot x(m\ddot x+kx)=0,
$$
so **the total <mechanical energy> is conserved**.

For <U-tube liquid oscillation>, define positive $y$ as the fall of the left surface, equal to the rise of the right surface. The hydrostatic height difference is $2y$. Moving a volume $A\,dy$ against this height difference gives $dU=2\rho gAy\,dy$, hence
$$
\boxed{U=\rho gAy^2,\qquad K=\frac12\rho Al\dot y^2.}
$$
<Energy> conservation yields $\rho Al\dot y(\ddot y+2gy/l)=0$. Equivalently the net pressure <force> is $-2\rho gAy$, so the equation holds also at turning points:
$$
\boxed{\ddot y+\omega_0^2 y=0,\qquad
\omega_0=\sqrt{\frac{2g}{l}},\qquad
f_0=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{2g}{l}}.}
$$
With negligible added volume, $y(0)=0$, $\dot y(0)=0.015\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{y(t)=(2.395\,\mathrm{mm})\sin[(6.264\,\mathrm{s^{-1}})t],\qquad
f_0=0.997\,\mathrm{Hz},\quad T=1.003\,\mathrm s.}
$$
The graph starts at zero with positive slope and first reaches its maximum after $T/4$.

\Image[paper-1-ia2014-u-tube.png]
{title=Original U-tube displacement solutions: the requested frictionless oscillation and the viscously damped response with exponential envelopes}

For <viscously damped U-tube oscillation>, the total <damping> <force> is $-\beta l\dot y$, not $-\beta\dot y$. Consequently
$$
\ddot y+\Gamma\dot y+\omega_0^2y=0,\qquad
\Gamma=\frac{\beta}{\rho A}=0.6667\,\mathrm{s^{-1}},\qquad
\boxed{Q=\frac{\omega_0}{\Gamma}=9.40.}
$$
The underdamped initial-value solution is $y=(v_0/\omega_d)e^{-\Gamma t/2}\sin\omega_dt$, where $\omega_d=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\Gamma^2/4}=6.255\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. Setting its <amplitude> envelope equal to $0.1\,\mathrm{mm}$ gives
$$
\boxed{t=\frac2\Gamma\ln\!\left(\frac{v_0/\omega_d}{0.1\,\mathrm{mm}}\right)=9.53\,\mathrm s.}
$$
This is the decay of the envelope, not the first time the instantaneous displacement passes through $0.1\,\mathrm{mm}$.

= Solution
{parent=D12}

A <travelling wave>, for example $A\cos(kx-\omega t)$, has moving constant-phase surfaces. A <standing wave>, for example $2A\sin(kx)\cos(\omega t)$, has stationary <nodes> and can be formed from two equal counterpropagating waves.

For the <half-line finite well> with $E>V_0$, define $k=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$ and $q=\sqrt{2m(E-V_0)}/\hbar$. The hard wall requires $\psi=0$ for $x<0$ and $\psi(0)=0$, so the general allowed forms are
$$
\boxed{\psi(x)=\begin{cases}
0,&x<0,\\
A\sin(kx),&0<x<a,\\
Be^{iq(x-a)}+Ce^{-iq(x-a)},&x>a.
\end{cases}}
$$
At the finite step both $\psi$ and $\psi'$ are continuous:
$$
A\sin(ka)=B+C,\qquad Ak\cos(ka)=iq(B-C).
$$
There is no requirement to match the derivative to zero across the infinite wall.

For a <bound state> $0<E<V_0$, put $\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar>0$. Normalizability excludes the growing exponential, leaving $A\sin(kx)$ inside and $De^{-\kappa(x-a)}$ outside. Matching their logarithmic derivatives gives
$$
\boxed{k\cot(ka)=-\kappa,\qquad \tan(ka)=-k/\kappa.}
$$
The exterior expression is equivalent to $Ce^{-\kappa x}$ by rescaling its constant. Negative cotangent first occurs for $\pi/2<ka<\pi$; all higher allowed intervals also have $ka>\pi/2$. Thus $\lambda_1=2\pi/k<4a$. Since $k<\sqrt{2mV_0}/\hbar$, a normalizable <bound state> requires
$$
\boxed{V_0>\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{8ma^2}.}
$$
This is also sufficient: on the lowest negative-cotangent branch, the two matching sides cross whenever $a\sqrt{2mV_0}/\hbar>\pi/2$. **The printed non-strict inequality includes the threshold limit.** At equality $E=V_0$ and the exterior is not square-integrable, so it is not itself a <bound state>.

For the helium <mass> and <energy> scale given, the limiting width is
$$
\boxed{a_{\rm th}=\frac{\pi\hbar}{\sqrt{8m_{\rm He}V_0}}=1.14\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm m=0.114\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
Actual binding requires a width just larger than this threshold in the stated ideal potential.

Represent each wave by a complex <phasor>. Add <phasors> before squaring: two <amplitudes> give $I\propto|A_1+A_2e^{i\delta}|^2=A_1^2+A_2^2+2A_1A_2\cos\delta$. For $N$ equally illuminated narrow slits, adjacent <phasors> differ by $\delta=2\pi d\sin\theta/\lambda$, so
$$
S_N=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{in\delta}
=e^{i(N-1)\delta/2}\frac{\sin(N\delta/2)}{\sin(\delta/2)}.
$$
All <phasors> align when $\delta=2\pi m$, giving the <grating equation>
$$
\boxed{d\sin\theta_m=m\lambda,\qquad I_{\rm principal}=N^2I_1.}
$$
The nearest zero has adjacent-phase increment $2\pi/N$: the <phasor> polygon closes once. Thus $d\cos\theta_m\Delta\theta\simeq\lambda/N$, giving
$$
\boxed{\Delta\theta\simeq\frac{\lambda}{Nd\cos\theta_m}=\frac{\lambda}{W\cos\theta_m}.}
$$
The angular linearization is valid away from grazing directions; the underlying exact first-zero condition is $d(\sin\theta_{\rm zero}-\sin\theta_m)=\pm\lambda/N$.

For the <first subsidiary maximum of a large slit array>, use the stipulated $1.5$ <phasor> wraps: $N\delta/2\simeq3\pi/2$ relative to a principal maximum. Its <amplitude> is approximately $2N/(3\pi)$ times one slit's <amplitude>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{I_{\rm principal}}{I_{\rm subsidiary}}\simeq\frac{9\pi^2}{4}=22.2.}
$$
This uses the question's approximate maximum position, not the exact extremum of the slit-array <intensity>.

\Image[paper-1-ia2014-grating.png]
{title=Original phasor sums for a principal maximum, the adjacent zero and the approximate first subsidiary maximum; calculated large-array intensity shown below}

The <Rayleigh criterion> places one line's principal maximum at the other's first minimum. <Grating dispersion> then gives $|m|N\gtrsim\lambda/\delta\lambda=1.674\times10^6$. Combining with normal incidence, $|m|\lambda=d|\sin\theta|$, gives the <normal-incidence grating width bound>:
$$
\boxed{W\gtrsim\frac{\lambda^2}{\delta\lambda\,|\sin\theta|}
\ge\frac{\lambda^2}{\delta\lambda}=1.289\,\mathrm m.}
$$
The $1.29\,\mathrm m$ value is an ideal lower bound approached near grazing diffraction. A usable finite angle needs greater width; for example $\theta=60^\circ$ requires about $1.49\,\mathrm m$. The exact sine first-zero relation still gives the lower bound when the local angular approximation fails near grazing.

Such a coherently illuminated grating is very large and needs accurate groove placement and suitable collection optics; an unaided far-field screen would require distances much greater than $W^2/\lambda\sim2\times10^6\,\mathrm m$. Focusing optics avoid that screen distance but not the required <resolving power of a diffraction grating>. Real linewidth, slit width and aberrations can require more resolution. The unequal <isotope> abundances also make this ideal Rayleigh estimate less decisive than for equally bright lines.

= Solution

An inverse-square field spreads through area proportional to squared distance. More generally the <flux> contribution of a point source is proportional to the <solid angle> subtended by a surface element; the oriented <solid angles> sum to $4\pi$ for an enclosed source and zero for an exterior one. <Superposition> extends this to a <mass distribution>.

<Newton's law of universal gravitation> gives $\mathbf g=-GM\hat{\mathbf r}/r^2$ for a <point mass>. A surrounding sphere therefore has <flux> $-4\pi GM$, so
$$
\boxed{C=4\pi G,\qquad \oint\mathbf g\cdot d\mathbf S=-4\pi GM_{\rm enc}.}
$$
For the tunnel, approximate its water as an infinite uniform cylinder with $R=3.5\,\mathrm m$ and <density> $\rho=1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. The bob is at radial distance $r=R+1.5\,\mathrm m=5.0\,\mathrm m$. A coaxial <Gaussian surface> gives
$$
2\pi rL_cg_r=-4\pi G(\pi R^2\rho L_c),\qquad
|g_r|=\frac{2\pi G\rho R^2}{r}.
$$
For the <plumb-line deflection from cylindrical gravity>, horizontal equilibrium gives $d/L\simeq|g_r|/g$. Draining removes the attraction toward the tunnel, so the bob shifts away by
$$
\boxed{d\simeq\frac{2\pi G\rho R^2L}{rg}=5.23\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm m=0.523\,\mathrm{\mu m}.}
$$
The unchanged rock and ordinary <gravitational field> cancel in the before/after comparison; end effects and mechanical disturbances are neglected.

For the <tidal water loading of a spring balance>, approximate the extra water at high tide as an infinite plane slab of thickness $h=8\,\mathrm m$, with the suspended <mass> above it. The <gravitational field of an infinite plane sheet>, derived with a pillbox <Gaussian surface>, gives added downward field $\Delta g=2\pi G\rho h$. With initial <spring> extension $x_0=mg/k=1\,\mathrm m$,
$$
\boxed{\Delta x=\frac m k\Delta g=x_0\frac{2\pi G\rho h}{g}
=3.42\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm m=0.342\,\mathrm{\mu m}.}
$$
**The extension increases at high tide.** These estimates use $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ and the stated water-density approximation; salinity, finite estuary geometry and terrestrial tidal deformation alter a real measurement.

= Solution
{parent=a}

A field assigns a physical quantity to each point. <Gravitational field> is <force> per unit test <mass>; <electric field> is <force> per unit test charge. In a conservative, time-independent field,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F=-\nabla U,\qquad \Delta U=-\int\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r.}
$$
Write $U=m\Phi$ for gravitational potential $\Phi$, or $U=q\phi$ for <electric potential> $\phi$, giving $\mathbf g=-\nabla\Phi$ and $\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi$. A <point mass> has $U=-GMm/r$ with zero at infinity, while two point charges have $U=Qq/(4\pi\epsilon_0r)$. Only potential-energy differences matter; a constant can be added freely. The <mechanical energy> $K+U$ is conserved if no nonconservative <forces> do work. A magnetic <Lorentz force> $q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$ does no work on a point charge and generally cannot be represented by a position-only scalar <potential energy>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

<Faraday's law> relates induced <electromotive force> to the rate of change of <magnetic flux linkage>. For a coil with $N$ turns and a consistent right-hand orientation,
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E=-\frac{d(N\Phi_B)}{dt}.}
$$
<Flux> can change through field variation or circuit motion. <Lenz's law> is the minus sign: the induced current's field opposes the <flux> change, consistent with <energy> conservation. It opposes the change rather than necessarily opposing the original field.

For a linear fixed-geometry <inductor>, $N\Phi_B=LI$ and the self-induced <electromotive force> is $-L\,dI/dt$. The passive terminal <voltage> drop is instead $V=L\,dI/dt$. The stored magnetic <energy> is
$$
\boxed{U_B=\int_0^I LI'\,dI'=\tfrac12LI^2.}
$$
<Inductance> is measured in henries. Transformers use <mutual inductance>: changing one winding's current induces an <electromotive force> in another.

= Solution

For steady currents in vacuum, the <Biot-Savart law> sums the contributions of current elements:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\int
\frac{d\boldsymbol\ell'\times(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|^3}.}
$$
It is useful for known wire geometries; a circular loop of radius $a$ gives $B=\mu_0I/(2a)$ at its centre.

<Ampère's circuital law> in <magnetostatics> is
$$
\boxed{\oint\mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=\mu_0I_{\rm enc}.}
$$
With sufficient symmetry it finds fields directly: a long straight wire gives $B=\mu_0I/(2\pi r)$, while a long <solenoid> has interior $B\simeq\mu_0nI$ and a small exterior field away from its ends. Without symmetry, a circulation value alone does not determine the local field. For time-dependent <electric fields>, use the <Ampère-Maxwell equation> with the displacement-current term $\mu_0\epsilon_0\,d\Phi_E/dt$ as well as conduction current.

= Solution
{parent=d}

For a test <mass> moving around a fixed central <mass> $M$, <gravity> is central and conservative. <Specific angular momentum> $h=|\mathbf r\times\mathbf v|$ and <specific orbital energy> $\epsilon=v^2/2-GM/r$ are constant. The motion is planar and sweeps equal areas in equal times, $dA/dt=h/2$.

For a nonradial orbit, the inverse-square <force> gives a <conic section>,
$$
\boxed{r=\frac{h^2/(GM)}{1+e\cos\theta},\qquad
 e^2=1+\frac{2\epsilon h^2}{(GM)^2}.}
$$
Negative <energy> gives an <ellipse>, zero <energy> a <parabola> and positive <energy> a <hyperbola>. For an <ellipse> of <semimajor axis> $a$,
$$
\boxed{\epsilon=-\frac{GM}{2a},\qquad
P^2=\frac{4\pi^2a^3}{GM},\qquad
v^2=GM\left(\frac2r-\frac1a\right).}
$$
A <circular orbit> is the $e=0$ case, with $v=\sqrt{GM/r}$. Escape from radius $r$ requires $v\ge\sqrt{2GM/r}$ in the absence of further <forces>. For two comparable <masses>, apply the relative two-body equation with $GM$ replaced by $G(M+m)$, rather than treating one body as fixed.
