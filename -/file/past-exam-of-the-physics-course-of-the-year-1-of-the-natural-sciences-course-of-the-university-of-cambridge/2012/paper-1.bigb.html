= Solution

The <shell theorem> gives the <gravity of a concentric hollow sphere> as $\mathbf g(r)=-GM(<r)\widehat{\mathbf r}/r^2$. There is no enclosed <mass> at the cavity boundary, whereas the entire shell has <mass> $M=\tfrac{4\pi\rho}{3}[(2a)^3-a^3]=\tfrac{28\pi}{3}\rho a^3$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbf g(a)=0,\qquad
\mathbf g(2a)=-\frac{7\pi G\rho a}{3}\widehat{\mathbf r},\qquad
\mathbf g(4a)=-\frac{7\pi G\rho a}{12}\widehat{\mathbf r}.}
$$
Both nonzero fields point towards the centre. The field is continuous at each boundary; there is no surface <mass> sheet.

For <drum-frequency dimensional scaling>, let $f\propto r^p\sigma^qT^s$, where $\sigma$ is <surface mass density>. The dimensions are $[\sigma]=ML^{-2}$ and $[T]=MT^{-2}$, so matching <powers> gives $s=1/2$, $q=-1/2$ and $p=-1$:
$$
f=C\frac1r\sqrt{\frac T\sigma},\qquad
\boxed{\frac{f_{\rm new}}f=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}=\frac1{\sqrt2}.}
$$
The dimensionless <normal mode> constant $C$ is unchanged for the same circular shape and <boundary condition>. The doubled <tension> does not offset the doubled <radius>.

For a <mass change in a viscously damped spring oscillator>, use linear <viscous damping> $m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=mg$, holding the <spring constant> $k$ and <damping> coefficient $b$ fixed. Measuring $x$ from $mg/k$ removes the constant <force>. The envelope is $e^{-\gamma t}$, where
$$
\gamma=\frac b{2m}=\frac{\ln100}{30\,\mathrm s}=0.15351\,\mathrm{s^{-1}},\qquad
\omega_d=\sqrt{\frac km-\gamma^2}=\pi\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}.
$$
Doubling $m$ halves both $k/m$ and $\gamma$. Therefore
$$
\omega_{d,\rm new}^2=\frac{\omega_d^2}{2}+\frac{\gamma^2}{4},\qquad
\boxed{f_{\rm new}=0.35376\,\mathrm{Hz}\simeq0.354\,\mathrm{Hz},\qquad t_{1\%,\rm new}=60\,\mathrm s.}
$$
Neglecting <damping> in the <frequency> gives $0.5/\sqrt2=0.35355\,\mathrm{Hz}$, indistinguishable at the supplied precision. The release condition sets the <phase> and new initial <amplitude>, not these envelope ratios. A different mass-dependent drag law would require its <damping> coefficient to be specified.

To find the <magnitude uncertainty of a series RL impedance>, put $X=\omega L$. Then $z=|Z|=\sqrt{R^2+X^2}=168.003\,\Omega$ and
$$
dz=\frac Rz\,dR+\frac Xz\,dX.
$$
If the quoted errors are <independent> <standard uncertainties>, $\sigma_R=1\,\Omega$ and $\sigma_X=6\,\Omega$ give
$$
\sigma_z=\frac{\sqrt{R^2\sigma_R^2+X^2\sigma_X^2}}z=4.86\,\Omega,\qquad
\boxed{|Z|\simeq(168\pm5)\,\Omega.}
$$
If they denote strict bounds instead, the first-order bound is $(R\Delta R+X\Delta X)/z=5.42\,\Omega$. The source does not specify their statistical interpretation, so these two error conventions should not be conflated.

The <phase> step between adjacent slits is
$$
\delta=\frac{2\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda}\simeq\frac\pi3.
$$
For equal <amplitudes> $a_0$, the six unmodified contributions form a closed <regular hexagon> in the <complex plane>:
$$
S_0=a_0\sum_{j=0}^{5}e^{ij\pi/3}=0.
$$
<Reversing one slit at an array minimum> replaces slit $j$'s contribution by its negative, so $S=S_0-2a_0e^{ij\pi/3}$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{I_{\rm screen}=|S|^2=4|a_0|^2=4I.}
$$
This is independent of which slit is reversed. The result uses the far-field, narrow-slit and <small-angle approximations>; retaining $\sin\theta$ changes the <phase> step only by about $7\times10^{-7}$ radians.

= Solution

Let $s$ increase down the ramp. On a plane, <static friction> $F$ acts uphill. Translation and rotation give
$$
m\ddot s=mg\sin\theta-F,\qquad
Fa=I\dot\omega,\qquad \dot\omega=\ddot s/a.
$$
With $I=2ma^2/5$, elimination yields **$\ddot s=(5/7)g\sin\theta$**. <Static friction> does no work at the stationary contact point.

For a <sphere rolling in a symmetric groove>, the distance from the centre to the instantaneous contact axis AB is $h=a\sin(\phi/2)$, so $v=h\omega$. The <kinetic energy> and work-energy equation are
$$
K=\frac12\left(m+\frac I{h^2}\right)\dot s^2,\qquad
\left(m+\frac I{h^2}\right)\ddot s=mg\sin\theta.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\ddot s=\frac{5g\sin\theta}{5+2/\sin^2(\phi/2)}.}
$$
For the stated angles this is $5g/18$. Using $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ and a one-second descent,
$$
\boxed{v_g=2.725\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},\qquad
K_g=\frac12mv_g^2\left(1+\frac{2}{5\sin^2 45^\circ}\right)=33.4\,\mathrm J.}
$$
On the level floor the rolling <radius> becomes $a$, so an <energy-preserving change of rolling radius> gives $K_g=\tfrac7{10}mv_f^2$. Therefore
$$
v_f=\sqrt{\frac97}\,v_g=3.090\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},\qquad
\boxed{t_{\rm pins}=\frac{20\,\mathrm m}{v_f}=6.47\,\mathrm s.}
$$
The floor <speed> exceeds the groove <speed> because less of the same total <kinetic energy> is rotational. Holding the translational <speed> fixed at the transition would violate the stipulated <energy> balance with the new no-slip constraint.

= B8
{parent=Section B}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=B8}

For $m>0$, $E=\gamma_u mc^2$ and $\mathbf p=\gamma_um\mathbf u$, with $\gamma_u=(1-u^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$. Thus
$$
E^2-p^2c^2=\gamma_u^2m^2c^4(1-u^2/c^2)=m^2c^4.
$$
A <photon> obeys **$E=pc$**; $E=hf$ and $p=h/\lambda$ agree because $f\lambda=c$ in vacuum.

Take the incident <photon> along $+x$, and write $\beta=v/c$ for the boost. In the boosted frame the initial momenta are
$$
p'_{\gamma x}=mc\sqrt{\frac{1-\beta}{1+\beta}}=\gamma_vmc(1-\beta),\qquad
p'_{ex}=-\gamma_vm\beta c.
$$
Their sum vanishes when $1-2\beta=0$, giving **$v_{\rm ZM}=c/2$** and $\gamma_v=2/\sqrt3$. The initial <photon energy> there is $mc^2/\sqrt3$, and total <energy> is $\sqrt3mc^2$.

For <right-angle Compton scattering in the centre-of-momentum frame>, choose the outgoing ZM <photon> along $+y$. The <electron> has the opposite <momentum> $-p'\widehat{\mathbf y}$. <Conservation of energy> determines its magnitude:
$$
p'c+\sqrt{m^2c^4+p'^2c^2}=\sqrt3mc^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
p'=\frac{mc}{\sqrt3},\qquad E'_e=\frac{2mc^2}{\sqrt3}.
$$
Thus **$\mathbf u'_e=-(c/2)\widehat{\mathbf y}$**. Inverting the <velocity> transformation gives
$$
u_{ex}=\frac{u'_{ex}+v}{1+u'_{ex}v/c^2}=\frac c2,\qquad
u_{ey}=\frac{u'_{ey}}{\gamma_v(1+u'_{ex}v/c^2)}=-\frac{\sqrt3c}{4}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{u_e=\frac{\sqrt7}{4}c=0.6614c,\qquad
\theta_e=-\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)=-40.9^\circ.}
$$
The <electron> recoils below the incident direction for our choice of <photon> scattering above it; the reflected configuration reverses both transverse signs. Finally,
$$
\boxed{E_{\gamma,\rm lab}=\gamma_v(E'_\gamma+vp'_{\gamma x})=\frac23mc^2\simeq341\,\mathrm{keV}.}
$$
As a check, $E_{e,\rm lab}=4mc^2/3$, so the final energies sum to the initial $2mc^2$. The outgoing laboratory <photon> makes $60^\circ$ with $+x$.

= B9
{parent=Section B}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=B9}

For a fixed set of bodies in an <inertial frame>, total <linear momentum> is constant if the resultant external <force> vanishes. Summing <Newton's second law> cancels internal <force> pairs by <Newton's third law>. For an open subsystem, <momentum> carried across its boundary must also be included.

For an <equal-intake-fuel mass-loading rocket>, enclose the original craft and the stationary material collected during $dt$. Its initial <momentum> is $mu$. At the end, the craft has <mass> $m-\mu\,dt$ and the exhaust has <mass> $2\mu\,dt$. To first order,
$$
mu=(m-\mu\,dt)(u+du)+2\mu\,dt(u-u_0),
\qquad
\boxed{m\frac{du}{dt}=2\mu u_0-\mu u.}
$$
The first term is <rocket thrust>; the second is the <momentum> cost of collecting stationary matter. It vanishes in the conventional <rocket equation>, which therefore has no analogous fixed exhaust-speed ceiling in <Newtonian mechanics>. Here the <acceleration> vanishes at **$u_{\max}=2u_0$**; from rest it stays positive below that value while fuel remains. Relativistic limits are outside this model.

For constant $\mu$, <mass conservation> gives $m=m_i-\mu t$. Separating variables,
$$
\int_0^u\frac{dw}{2u_0-w}=\int_0^t\frac{\mu\,d\tau}{m_i-\mu\tau}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{2u_0-u}{2u_0}=\frac{m_i-\mu t}{m_i}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{u(t)=\frac{2\mu u_0}{m_i}t,\qquad \dot u=\frac{2\mu u_0}{m_i}.}
$$
The formal limiting <speed> would require $t=m_i/\mu$ and zero remaining craft <mass>. A positive <rocket dry mass> and finite carried fuel terminate the burn earlier. Maintaining nonzero collection rate even at rest is an idealization explicitly prescribed by the problem.

= Solution

The bob experiences weight $Mg$ vertically downward and a rod <force> towards the pivot. The tangential component of weight is $-Mg\sin\theta$, so
$$
ML\ddot\theta=-Mg\sin\theta\simeq-Mg\theta,\qquad
\boxed{\omega_0^2=g/L,\qquad T_0=2\pi\sqrt{L/g}.}
$$
The rod <force> supplies the radial <acceleration> and contributes no tangential <torque> about the pivot.

\Image[paper-1-ia2012-pendulum-forces.png]
{title=Original force diagram for a displaced pendulum, with radial and tangential directions}

To the same small-angle order, writing $\chi=\omega_0t+\phi$,
$$
U=MgL(1-\cos\theta)\simeq\frac12MgL\theta_0^2\cos^2\chi,\qquad
K=\frac12ML^2\dot\theta^2=\frac12MgL\theta_0^2\sin^2\chi.
$$
Thus **$K+U=MgL\theta_0^2/2$** is constant. Put $b=\dot\theta(0)$, a signed <angular velocity>. Then
$$
\boxed{\theta_0=\sqrt{\alpha^2+b^2/\omega_0^2},\qquad
\phi=\operatorname{atan2}(-b/\omega_0,\alpha)\pmod{2\pi}.}
$$
If the printed <angular speed> $\beta$ is intended as the signed rate, set $b=\beta$. If it means magnitude only, $b=\pm\beta$ gives two possible <phases>; the <amplitude> is unique. At zero <amplitude> the <phase> is arbitrary.

For a <finite-radius spherical pendulum bob>, the PDF dimension $L$ runs from pivot to <centre of mass>. The <parallel axis theorem> gives $I_P=ML^2+2Ma^2/5$. The restoring <torque> remains $-MgL\theta$, hence
$$
T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{ML^2+2Ma^2/5}{MgL}}
=T_0\sqrt{1+\frac{2a^2}{5L^2}}
\simeq T_0\left(1+\frac{a^2}{5L^2}\right).
$$
With $a=[3M/(4\pi\rho)]^{1/3}=0.02605\,\mathrm m$,
$$
\boxed{100\frac{T-T_0}{T_0}=0.01357\%\simeq0.0136\%.}
$$
The finite bob increases the inertia without changing the gravitational lever arm.

In sinusoidal <steady state>, <complex impedance> is the <voltage> <phasor> divided by the current <phasor>. Its <modulus> is the <amplitude> ratio and its <argument> is the <voltage> lead. With the $e^{i\omega t}$ convention, $I=dq/dt=i\omega CV$, so **$Z_C=1/(i\omega C)$**.

The PDF labels $V_1$ across $R_1$, not across the complete driven network. If $I$ is the current through $R_1$, then $V_1=IR_1$ and $I=Y_pV_2$, where
$$
Y_p=\frac1R+i\left(\omega C-\frac1{\omega L}\right).
$$
The <resistor-referenced parallel-resonator response> is
$$
\boxed{H(\omega)=\frac{V_2}{V_1}=\frac{R/R_1}{1+iR(\omega C-1/(\omega L))}.}
$$
Writing $b(\omega)=\omega C-1/(\omega L)$,
$$
|H|=\frac{R/R_1}{\sqrt{1+R^2b^2}},\qquad
\arg H=-\tan^{-1}(Rb).
$$
The maximum occurs at $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$, where **$|H|=R/R_1$ and the <phase> is zero**. Below <resonance> the <phase> is positive, tending to $+\pi/2$; above it the <phase> is negative, tending to $-\pi/2$. The <amplitude> tends to zero at both extremes: the <inductor> shorts the parallel network at low <frequency>, and the <capacitor> does so at high <frequency>. At <resonance> their reactive currents cancel, leaving only the <resistor> current.

\Image[paper-1-ia2012-parallel-response.png]
{title=Resistor-referenced parallel-resonator amplitude and phase, normalized without choosing circuit component values}

If instead $V_{\rm in}$ denotes the <voltage> across the complete circuit, its different transfer ratio is $V_2/V_{\rm in}=1/[1+R_1/R+iR_1b]$, with peak $R/(R+R_1)$. This distinction follows directly from the printed <voltage> endpoints.

For <free parallel RLC voltage decay>, measure $V=V_A-V_B$ and branch currents from A to B. Then
$$
I_C=C\dot V,\qquad I_R=V/R,\qquad
I_L(t)=I_L(0)+\frac1L\int_0^tV(s)\,ds.
$$
There is no external current, so <Kirchhoff's current law> gives $I_C+I_R+I_L=0$. Differentiating yields
$$
\boxed{C\ddot V+\frac1R\dot V+\frac1LV=0.}
$$
For $V\propto e^{st}$ the <characteristic equation> is $Cs^2+s/R+1/L=0$. Its <underdamped> roots give
$$
\boxed{\gamma=\frac1{2RC},\qquad
\omega=\sqrt{\frac1{LC}-\frac1{4R^2C^2}}.}
$$
The supplied inequality makes $\omega$ real. A real physical <voltage> has the general form $V=Ae^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega t+\phi)$, the <real part> of the printed complex expression. A general complex-valued solution uses both roots, $e^{-\gamma t}(C_+e^{i\omega t}+C_-e^{-i\omega t})$. The <inductor>'s initial current is fixed consistently by the undifferentiated current balance; it is not an arbitrary additional voltage-mode constant.

= Solution
{parent=D12}

Fixed endpoints require $k_n=n\pi/L$, with $n=1,2,\ldots$. Writing $c_s=\sqrt{T/\rho}$,
$$
\boxed{\omega_1=\frac{\pi c_s}{L},\qquad\omega_2=\frac{2\pi c_s}{L}.}
$$
For equal <amplitudes> $A$, the two requested profiles are
$$
y(x,0)=A[\sin(\pi x/L)+\sin(2\pi x/L)],\qquad
y(x,t_1)=-A\sin(2\pi x/L).
$$
The first is asymmetric, with an interior zero at $x=2L/3$; the second has its interior <node> at $L/2$. For <two-mode string recurrence>, <orthogonality> means the whole initial profile recurs only when both <cosine> coefficients equal one. Since $\omega_2=2\omega_1$,
$$
\boxed{t=\frac{2\pi n}{\omega_1},\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots.}
$$

\Image[paper-1-ia2012-string-modes.png]
{title=Two-mode string profiles and the small change produced by weak positive dispersion at the original quarter-period}

The <weak-stiffness string frequency shift> follows by expanding the supplied <dispersion relation>:
$$
\omega(k)=c_sk\left(1+\frac{\alpha k^2}{2c_s^2}\right)+O(\alpha^2),\qquad [\alpha]=\mathrm{m^4\,s^{-2}}.
$$
Higher <normal modes> acquire larger fractional <frequency> shifts. Let $\eta=\alpha k_1^2/c_s^2\ll1$ and retain $\omega_1^{(0)}=c_sk_1$ for the original ideal <frequency>. At the originally defined time $t_1=\pi/(2\omega_1^{(0)})$,
$$
\cos(\omega_1t_1)=-\frac{\pi\eta}{4}+O(\eta^2),\qquad
\cos(\omega_2t_1)=-1+O(\eta^2).
$$
Thus the $t=0$ sketch is unchanged, whereas the original quarter-period sketch gains a small negative fundamental component. If $t_1$ is redefined using the new fundamental <frequency>, its fundamental component is exactly zero and the second-mode change there is only second order. Both conventions agree that generic later shapes change because of relative <phase> drift.

The new <frequency> ratio is
$$
r=\frac{\omega_2}{\omega_1}=2\sqrt{\frac{1+4\eta}{1+\eta}}=2+3\eta+O(\eta^2).
$$
An exact recurrence now requires $\omega_1t=2\pi q$ and $\omega_2t=2\pi p$ for integers $p,q$. **If $r=p/q$ in lowest terms, the first recurrence is $2\pi q/\omega_1$; for irrational $r$ there is no exact finite recurrence.** Near-recurrences remain possible. Positive stiffness alone does not prove that the ratio is irrational.

Here $V(x)$ is <potential energy>, $E$ is the stationary-state total <energy eigenvalue>, and $\psi(x)$ is its spatial <wavefunction>. <Wavefunction normalization> gives $\int|\psi|^2dx=1$, and $|\psi|^2dx$ is the <probability> of finding the particle in $dx$.

A <bound state> requires $-V_0<E<0$. Inside the well the equation is $\psi''=-k^2\psi$, whereas outside it is $\psi''=q^2\psi$, with
$$
\boxed{k=\frac{\sqrt{2m(E+V_0)}}\hbar,\qquad q=\frac{\sqrt{-2mE}}\hbar.}
$$
The <ground state> is even and has no <node>, selecting $\cos(kx)$ inside. Normalizability selects decaying, not growing, exterior exponentials. Continuity of $\psi$ and $\psi'$ gives
$$
A\cos(ka/2)=Ce^{-qa/2},\qquad k\tan(ka/2)=q.
$$
The first <excited state>, if present, is odd: a <sine> inside and opposite-sign decaying tails outside. Matching gives $-k_1\cot(k_1a/2)=q_1>0$. On its first odd branch, $\pi/2<k_1a/2<\pi$, hence $k_1a>\pi$. Since $k_1^2<2mV_0/\hbar^2$, existence requires
$$
\boxed{V_0>\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}.}
$$
This is the <first odd bound-state threshold of a square well>. There is no excited <bound state> below that threshold; equality gives a non-normalizable zero-energy limiting state, not an extra <bound state>.

\Image[paper-1-ia2012-well-states.png]
{title=Original finite-well wavefunctions: an even nodeless ground state and an odd excited state in a deeper illustrative well}

For the numerical well,
$$
V_0=\frac{\hbar^2(k^2+q^2)}{2m}=8.04\,\mathrm{eV}\simeq8.0\,\mathrm{eV},\qquad
\boxed{E=-\frac{\hbar^2q^2}{2m}=-4.95\,\mathrm{eV}.}
$$
The <exterior probability of a symmetric finite-well state> includes both tails:
$$
\boxed{P_{\rm out}=2\int_{a/2}^{\infty}C^2e^{-2qx}\,dx
=\frac{C^2}{q}e^{-qa}=0.178\simeq17.8\%.}
$$
The threshold is $9.40\,\mathrm{eV}$, exceeding the well depth, so **there is no excited <bound state>**. The rounded supplied coefficients integrate to $0.9973$ rather than exactly one; this rounding does not change the quoted <probability> precision materially.

= Solution
{parent=E14}

For steady currents in vacuum, <Ampère's law> is $\oint\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf l=\mu_0I_{\rm enclosed}$. A rectangular loop with one long side inside an ideal long <solenoid> and the other outside gives $B\ell=\mu_0(n\ell)I$, because the exterior field is negligible and the perpendicular sides contribute zero. Thus **$B=\mu_0nI$**, approximately uniform away from the ends.

Write the <electron> <electric charge> as $q_e=-e$, with $e>0$. The <Lorentz force> is $\mathbf F=-e\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$; it does no work. For perpendicular motion, $mv^2/r_L=evB$ gives
$$
\boxed{\omega_c=\frac{eB}{m},\qquad r_L=\frac{mv}{eB}.}
$$
Here $\omega_c$ is the positive <angular speed>; the rotation sense is determined by the negative <electron> <electric charge>.

The parallel <velocity> is unaffected, while the perpendicular component rotates. Hence the path is a <helix> with $v_\parallel=v\cos\theta$ and $r_L=mv\sin\theta/(eB)$. For <on-axis entry into a solenoidal tube>, the transverse circle passes through that axis: its <guiding centre> is displaced by $r_L$, and its maximum distance from the tube axis is **$2r_L$**, not $r_L$.

\Image[paper-1-ia2012-electron-orbit.png]
{title=End-on electron orbit: the circle passes through the tube axis and reaches twice its Larmor radius from it}

Using the nonrelativistic approximation appropriate to $1\,\mathrm{keV}$,
$$
v=\sqrt{\frac{2E_0}{m}}=1.876\times10^7\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},\qquad
\boxed{t=\frac{l}{v\cos3^\circ}=4.27\,\mathrm{ns}.}
$$
The <cyclotron period> is $2\pi/\omega_c=0.893\,\mathrm{ns}$, so the flight lasts $4.783$ revolutions. The <electron> therefore crosses the axis again **four times** before leaving. At $3^\circ$, $2r_L=0.279\,\mathrm{mm}<a$. Wall clearance requires $2mv\sin\theta/(eB)\le a$, giving
$$
\boxed{\theta_m=\sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{eBa}{2mv}\right)=5.38^\circ.}
$$
The transit is longer than a full orbit, so the maximum transverse excursion really is sampled before exit.

For the <solenoidal time-of-flight energy window>, the prescribed time fixes $v_\parallel=l/t_0=v_0=\sqrt{2E_0/m}$, while $0\le v_\perp\le eBa/(2m)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{E_{\min}=E_0=1.000\,\mathrm{keV},\qquad
E_{\max}=E_0+\frac{e^2B^2a^2}{8m}=1.0088\,\mathrm{keV}.}
$$
These are total <kinetic energies>. The upper value is the ideal grazing limit; if touching the wall counts as rejection, it is a <supremum> approached from below.

= Solution
{parent=a}

An <electric dipole> consists of opposite charges separated by a small displacement, with <electric dipole moment> $\mathbf p=q\mathbf d$ directed from negative to positive. At distances large compared with its size,
$$
\Phi=\frac{\mathbf p\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r}}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^2},\qquad
\mathbf E=\frac{3(\mathbf p\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})\widehat{\mathbf r}-\mathbf p}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^3}.
$$
The net charge term vanishes; the field decays faster than a monopole field. In an imposed slowly varying <electric field>,
$$
\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf p\times\mathbf E,\qquad U=-\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E,\qquad
\mathbf F=\nabla(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E)
$$
for a permanent fixed dipole. A uniform field produces a <torque> but no net <force>. For a linear induced dipole $\mathbf p=\alpha\mathbf E$, the interaction <energy> is instead $-\alpha E^2/2$, because building the dipole also costs <energy>.

A small planar <current loop> has <magnetic dipole moment> $\boldsymbol\mu=IA\widehat{\mathbf n}$, with the normal fixed by the <right-hand rule>. Outside the source,
$$
\mathbf B=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r^3}[3(\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})\widehat{\mathbf r}-\boldsymbol\mu],\qquad
\boldsymbol\tau=\boldsymbol\mu\times\mathbf B,\qquad U=-\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B.
$$
<Magnetic field> lines form closed loops; a current-loop dipole does not require <magnetic monopoles>. **Dipole moments describe the leading directional field of a neutral localized source**, and their <torques> explain alignment in external fields.

For two isolated <point masses>, introduce total <mass> $M_T=M+m$, <reduced mass> $\mu=Mm/M_T$ and relative separation $r$. The relative <acceleration> is $\ddot{\mathbf r}=-GM_T\mathbf r/r^3$. Conserved <specific angular momentum> $h=|\mathbf r\times\dot{\mathbf r}|$ fixes a plane and a constant areal <velocity> $h/2$. The <specific orbital energy> is
$$
\varepsilon=\frac{\dot r^2}{2}+\frac{h^2}{2r^2}-\frac{GM_T}{r}.
$$
For $h\ne0$, the trajectory is a conic:
$$
\boxed{r(\varphi)=\frac{h^2/(GM_T)}{1+e\cos(\varphi-\varphi_0)},\qquad
e^2=1+\frac{2\varepsilon h^2}{G^2M_T^2}.}
$$
Negative <energy> gives an <ellipse>, including the circular limit $e=0$; zero <energy> gives a <parabola>; positive <energy> gives a <hyperbola>. Bound <ellipses> obey $\varepsilon=-GM_T/(2a_o)$ and
$$
T^2=\frac{4\pi^2a_o^3}{GM_T}.
$$
Thus <Kepler's laws of planetary motion> follow from <angular momentum conservation> and the <inverse-square force>. For a <circular orbit> of <radius> $r$, $v_c=\sqrt{GM_T/r}$, while escape from that <radius> requires $v_{\rm esc}=\sqrt{2GM_T/r}$. **<Energy> determines whether the orbit is bound; <angular momentum> also determines its shape.** These ideal results neglect extended-body perturbations, drag and relativistic corrections.

In <electrostatic equilibrium> a conductor has $\mathbf E=0$ throughout its bulk and constant <electric potential> on each connected component. Otherwise free charge would continue moving. <Gauss's law> then excludes bulk excess charge; it resides on surfaces. Just outside a conductor the tangential field vanishes and the normal jump is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E_{\rm out}=\frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0}\widehat{\mathbf n}}
$$
for a vacuum interface with outward normal $\widehat{\mathbf n}$. Surface charge rearranges in an external field to enforce these <boundary conditions>. Greater curvature often concentrates charge, but the actual distribution is a global boundary-value problem.

An empty closed cavity inside a conductor has constant potential and zero field, even in an external electrostatic field. If a charge $q$ is placed in the cavity, a <Gaussian surface> in the metal shows that the cavity wall carries total induced charge $-q$; its detailed distribution depends on geometry. An initially neutral isolated conductor then has $+q$ on its exterior surface. A grounded conductor can exchange charge with <Earth>, so this isolation condition no longer applies.

A useful example is a charge $q$ at height $d$ above an infinite grounded plane. In the physical half-space, the <method of images> replaces the induced charges by an image $-q$ at height $-d$, giving the attractive <force> $q^2/(16\pi\epsilon_0d^2)$. The image is a mathematical construction, not an actual charge behind the conductor. **Equipotential surfaces and <charge conservation> determine conductor fields together.**

= Solution

In vacuum, <Maxwell's equations> in SI units are
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho_q/\epsilon_0,\qquad
\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf B,\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t\mathbf E.
$$
The <displacement current> term makes the last equation consistent with the <charge continuity equation>. In a source-free region, take the curl of <Faraday's law> and use $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)-\nabla^2\mathbf E$. This gives
$$
\nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t^2\mathbf E,
\qquad \nabla^2\mathbf B=\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t^2\mathbf B.
$$
A <plane electromagnetic wave> therefore propagates at **$c=1/\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}$**. The divergence equations make both fields transverse; the curl equations give
$$
\mathbf B=\frac1c\widehat{\mathbf k}\times\mathbf E.
$$
The <electric fields> and <magnetic fields> are in <phase> and perpendicular to one another and to the propagation direction. Their <energy densities> are equal:
$$
u=\frac12\epsilon_0E^2+\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}=\epsilon_0E^2,\qquad
\mathbf S=\frac1{\mu_0}\mathbf E\times\mathbf B=uc\widehat{\mathbf k}.
$$
For electric-field peak <amplitude> $E_a$, the time-averaged <intensity> is $\langle S\rangle=\epsilon_0cE_a^2/2$. **Mutually coupled changing <electric fields> and <magnetic fields> transport <energy> through vacuum**, even where no charges or conduction currents are present.
