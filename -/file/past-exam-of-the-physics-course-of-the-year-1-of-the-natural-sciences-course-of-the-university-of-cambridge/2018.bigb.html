= Solution
{parent=1}

For the same massless <spring> of <spring constant> $k$, <Hooke's law> gives the vertical <simple harmonic motion> about the displaced <equilibrium> position:
$$
\nu_i=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m_i}},\qquad
R=\frac{m_1}{m_2}=\left(\frac{\nu_2}{\nu_1}\right)^2.
$$
Gravity shifts the <equilibrium> extension but does not change this <frequency>. Use the measured <sample means> in the ratio estimate. For small measurement errors, <logarithmic differentiation> gives
$$
\frac{dR}{R}=2\frac{d\nu_2}{\nu_2}-2\frac{d\nu_1}{\nu_1}.
$$
Assuming the two frequency estimates have <independent> errors, their <variances> add. Writing $\epsilon=\delta\nu/\overline\nu$ for their common fractional <standard uncertainty>, <monomial uncertainty propagation> yields
$$
\left(\frac{\sigma(R)}R\right)^2\simeq4\epsilon^2+4\epsilon^2,
\qquad
\boxed{\frac{\sigma(R)}R\simeq\sqrt8\,\frac{\delta\nu}{\overline\nu}.}
$$
This is a first-order uncertainty formula, rather than an exact identity for an arbitrary noisy ratio. It assumes that errors in the two measured <frequencies> have zero <covariance>.

There is a statistical distinction in the wording: the <standard deviation> of individual repetitions is not the <standard error> of their <sample mean>. If the supplied $\sigma(\nu_i)$ describes individual trials, the frequency means have errors $\sigma(\nu_i)/\sqrt n$, and the ratio formed from those means instead satisfies
$$
\boxed{\frac{\sigma(R_{\rm means})}{R_{\rm means}}\simeq
\sqrt{\frac8n}\,\frac{\delta\nu}{\overline\nu}.}
$$
Thus the printed formula describes either a single-pair ratio or a convention in which $\delta\nu$ already denotes the uncertainty of each averaged <frequency>. Under either consistent interpretation, <independent> repeated measurements reduce statistical error in proportion to $n^{-1/2}$. Halving it requires **$4n$ trials in total for each mass, or $3n$ additional trials**. Repetition alone does not reduce a fixed <systematic error>.

The spacecraft clock measures <proper time> $\tau$, while a clock at rest with Earth measures the launch-to-emission interval $t$. Neglect Earth's subsequent motion and gravity, as in the intended <special relativity> model. The <Lorentz factor> and <time dilation> give
$$
\gamma=\frac1{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}=\frac3{\sqrt8},\qquad
t=\gamma\tau=\frac3{\sqrt8}(12\,\mathrm h)=12.7279\,\mathrm h.
$$
In Earth's <inertial frame>, the spacecraft's <displacement> at emission is $d=vt$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{d=\frac{c\tau}{\sqrt8}=4.24264\ \text{light-hours}
\simeq4.58\times10^{12}\,\mathrm m.}
$$
Here $\tau=43200\,\mathrm s$ and the <speed of light> is $c=2.99792458\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The distance is evaluated at the emission event, using simultaneous Earth-frame endpoints, as required by <relativity of simultaneity>; the return signal has not yet reached Earth. A distance assigned on the spacecraft's simultaneous time slice is different: Earth has moved away at $c/3$ for $12\,\mathrm h$ in that frame, giving $c\tau/3=4$ light-hours. Specifying the <reference frame> is essential to the numerical answer.

= Solution

Use dimensions $\mathsf M,\mathsf L,\mathsf T$ for <mass>, <length> and <time>. The <reduced Planck constant>, <gravitational constant> and <speed of light> have dimensions
$$
[\hbar]=\mathsf M\mathsf L^2\mathsf T^{-1},\qquad
[G]=\mathsf L^3\mathsf M^{-1}\mathsf T^{-2},\qquad
[c]=\mathsf L\mathsf T^{-1}.
$$
For $l_p=\hbar^\alpha G^\beta c^\gamma$ to have the dimensions of a <length>, <dimensional analysis> requires
$$
\alpha-\beta=0,\qquad
2\alpha+3\beta+\gamma=1,\qquad
-\alpha-2\beta-\gamma=0.
$$
The first equation gives $\beta=\alpha$; the third gives $\gamma=-3\alpha$. Substitution in the second gives $2\alpha=1$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac12,\qquad\beta=\frac12,\qquad\gamma=-\frac32,
\qquad l_p=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar G}{c^3}}.}
$$
With $\hbar=1.054571817\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$, $G=6.67430\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}$ and $c=2.99792458\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, the <Planck length> is
$$
\boxed{l_p\simeq1.6163\times10^{-35}\,\mathrm m.}
$$
The proposed monomial fixes the dimensionless prefactor to one; <dimensional analysis> on its own cannot select an arbitrary dimensionless multiplier.

**First intensity minimum.** In <Fraunhofer diffraction>, the path difference between adjacent slits is $d\sin\theta$, so the relative <wave phase> is $\delta=2\pi d\sin\theta/\lambda_1$. Summing the $N$ equal <complex amplitudes> as a <geometric series> gives the <diffraction grating> interference factor
$$
I(\theta)\propto
\left|\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{ij\delta}\right|^2
=\frac{\sin^2(N\delta/2)}{\sin^2(\delta/2)}.
$$
The central maximum has $\delta=0$. Its nearest zero occurs at $N\delta/2=\pi$, where the denominator is nonzero. Thus
$$
\sin\theta_{\min}=\frac{\lambda_1}{Nd}
=\frac{640\times10^{-9}}{10(80\times10^{-6})}=8.00\times10^{-4},
\qquad
\boxed{|\theta_{\min}|=8.00\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{rad}=0.0458^\circ.}
$$
There is a symmetric first zero on each side of the incident direction. The narrow slits are treated as having a sufficiently broad <single-slit diffraction> envelope.

**Wavelength resolution and the missing order specification.** Adopt the <Rayleigh criterion>: the principal maximum of one <wavelength> lies at the adjacent minimum of the other in the same nonzero <diffraction order> $m$. For $\lambda_2>\lambda_1$ and $m>0$, those directions obey
$$
d\sin\theta=m\lambda_2
=\left(m+\frac1N\right)\lambda_1.
$$
Consequently the ideal <resolving power of a diffraction grating> is $\lambda_1/\Delta\lambda=mN$, giving
$$
\boxed{\Delta\lambda_{\min,m}=\frac{\lambda_1}{mN}
=\frac{64}{m}\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
The usual first-order reading therefore gives **$64\,\mathrm{nm}$**. Zero <diffraction order> cannot separate the two <wavelengths>, because both central maxima coincide.

The paper does not specify which <diffraction order> is observed, so $64\,\mathrm{nm}$ is not an order-independent minimum. In the ideal infinitely narrow-slit model, the largest usable order for this positive wavelength separation must satisfy $(m+1/N)\lambda_1\le d$. Since $d/\lambda_1=125$, this permits $m\le124$, giving the formal all-order result
$$
\boxed{\Delta\lambda_{\min,124}=0.516\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
That pair meets the criterion at $\theta\simeq83.1^\circ$. Order $125$ of $\lambda_1$ is at grazing incidence and the corresponding longer-wavelength peak cannot propagate. In a real device, finite slit width, weak high-order intensity and screen acceptance may prevent such an extreme order from being used; those data are absent. Thus the specified-order expression, and $64\,\mathrm{nm}$ when first order is intended, are the defensible resolution answers under the stated criterion.

For a <photon>, the <photon energy> formula and the vacuum relation $\nu=c/\lambda$ give $E_\gamma=hc/\lambda$. For an <electron>, the <de Broglie wavelength> gives the same <momentum> magnitude $p=h/\lambda$, but the nonrelativistic <kinetic energy> is $K_e=p^2/(2m_e)$. Using $h=6.62607015\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$, $m_e=9.10938\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $1\,\mathrm{eV}=1.602176634\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J$,
$$
E_\gamma=\frac{(6.62607015\times10^{-34})(2.99792458\times10^8)}{4.0\times10^{-9}}
=4.96611\times10^{-17}\,\mathrm J,
$$
$$
K_e=\frac{(6.62607015\times10^{-34})^2}{2(9.10938\times10^{-31})(4.0\times10^{-9})^2}
=1.50617\times10^{-20}\,\mathrm J.
$$
Thus, to the precision of the <wavelength>,
$$
\boxed{E_\gamma=3.1\times10^2\,\mathrm{eV},\qquad K_e=9.4\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm{eV}.}
$$
The electron answer is its <kinetic energy>, as required for the diffraction experiment; its <relativistic energy> would additionally include the approximately $511\,\mathrm{keV}$ <rest energy>. Since $K_e\ll m_ec^2$, the nonrelativistic approximation is well justified. Equal <wavelengths> imply equal <momenta>, not equal energies for particles with different <energy–momentum relations>.

Put the charged sheet at $z=0$ and the grounded plates at $z=\pm d/2$. By symmetry the <electric field> points away from the positive sheet with equal magnitude in the two gaps. A <Gaussian pillbox> crossing the sheet gives
$$
E_z(0^+)-E_z(0^-)=\frac\sigma{\varepsilon_0},\qquad
|E|=\frac\sigma{2\varepsilon_0}.
$$
The plate boundary conditions fix their <electric potentials> to zero. For the <charged sheet between grounded parallel plates>, the central potential is
$$
V_s=|E|\frac d2=\frac{\sigma d}{4\varepsilon_0},\qquad
\sigma=1.0\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{C\,m^{-2}},\quad d=1.0\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm m.
$$
With <vacuum permittivity> $\varepsilon_0=8.85419\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{F\,m^{-1}}$, $V_s\simeq28.24\,\mathrm V$. An <electron> has <electric charge> $-e$, so moving from a grounded plate to this positive potential decreases its <potential energy> by $eV_s$. <Conservation of energy> then gives
$$
\frac12m_ev^2=eV_s=\frac{e\sigma d}{4\varepsilon_0},
\qquad
\boxed{v=\sqrt{\frac{e\sigma d}{2\varepsilon_0m_e}}
\simeq3.2\times10^6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The unrounded value is $3.15\times10^6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, about $0.0105c$. Either plate gives the same <speed>. The ideal calculation neglects the electron's disturbance of the prescribed field and image-force effects at its release point, consistently with treating it as a <test particle>.

= Solution
{parent=7}

**Powered vertical flight.** Take upward <velocity> $v$ as positive, and let $v_e>0$ be the downward exhaust <speed> relative to the rocket. During $dt$ the rocket's <mass> changes by $dm<0$, and exhaust mass $-dm$ leaves with Earth-frame <velocity> $v-v_e$. Compare the <momentum> of the rocket plus this newly expelled exhaust before and after the interval:
$$
(m+dm)(v+dv)+(-dm)(v-v_e)-mv=-mg\,dt.
$$
Discard the second-order product $dm\,dv$. The result is $m\,dv+v_e\,dm=-mg\,dt$, or
$$
m\frac{dv}{dt}=-v_e\frac{dm}{dt}-mg.
$$
The positive <rocket thrust> is $-v_e\dot m$. Integrating with $v(0)=0$ and $m(0)=m_0$ gives the <rocket equation> with gravitational loss:
$$
\boxed{v(t)=v_e\ln\frac{m_0}{m(t)}-gt.}
$$
Using $d(mv)/dt$ for the rocket alone without the exhaust <momentum flux> would give the wrong equation for this open system.

**Orbital speed under the stated gravity assumption.** A <circular orbit> of radius $r_o=R_E+h_o=7.90\times10^6\,\mathrm m$ requires <centripetal acceleration> $v_o^2/r_o$. If the magnitude of <gravitational acceleration> is the stipulated constant $g=9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ even at the orbit,
$$
\frac{v_o^2}{r_o}=g,
\qquad
\boxed{v_o=\sqrt{gr_o}=8.80\times10^3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
This treats gravity as centrally directed for the orbital calculation, while retaining its constant magnitude. The subsequent flat-surface approximation treats its direction as locally vertical.

**Burnout height.** During the unpowered <projectile motion>, horizontal <velocity> is constant. At the top of the trajectory the vertical component vanishes, so the horizontal component must equal the orbital <speed> $v_o$. At burnout, therefore,
$$
v_{y,b}=v_o\tan30^\circ,\qquad
h_o-h_b=\frac{v_{y,b}^2}{2g}=\frac{v_o^2}{6g}.
$$
These follow either by integrating $\ddot y=-g$ or by the vertical <work-energy theorem>. Substituting the preceding constant-$g$ orbital result gives
$$
\boxed{h_b=h_o-\frac{R_E+h_o}{6}
=1.83\times10^5\,\mathrm m\simeq183\,\mathrm{km}.}
$$
The vertical component is initially positive, as required to reach the orbit after burnout.

**Alternative inverse-square interpretation.** A real Earth's exterior <Newtonian gravitational field> is not constant over a $1500\,\mathrm{km}$ altitude change. If $9.8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ instead means the surface value $g_0$, then <Newton's law of universal gravitation> gives $GM=g_0R_E^2$ and
$$
v_o=\sqrt{\frac{g_0R_E^2}{R_E+h_o}}=7.13\times10^3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.
$$
Using this orbital <speed> but the paper's constant-$g$ flat <projectile motion> approximation for the coast would give
$$
\boxed{h_b=1.50\times10^6-
\frac{R_E^2}{6(R_E+h_o)}=6.36\times10^5\,\mathrm m\simeq636\,\mathrm{km}.}
$$
This alternative changes the global constant-gravity assumption; it is not the same model as the $183\,\mathrm{km}$ result. An exact curved <Kepler orbit> coast would also abandon the prescribed flat trajectory. The printed assumptions support the constant-magnitude answers above, while the inverse-square values explain the different answer obtained from the usual physical Earth-orbit formula.

= Solution
{parent=8}

**Directions.** Choose $+x$ along the horizontal spindle from the pivot to the disk, and $+z$ vertically upward. With <lever arm> $\mathbf r=l\widehat{\mathbf x}$ and weight $\mathbf F_g=-mg\widehat{\mathbf z}$, the <torque> about the pivot is
$$
\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf r\times\mathbf F_g=mgl\widehat{\mathbf y}.
$$
Clockwise <precession> viewed from above has $\boldsymbol\Omega=-\Omega\widehat{\mathbf z}$. The relation $\boldsymbol\tau=\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf L$ therefore requires the spin <angular momentum> to point along $-x$, **from the disk toward the pivot**. The disk spins **clockwise when viewed from its outer face looking along the spindle toward the pivot**. Looking from the pivot reverses that apparent rotation. The <right-hand rule> fixes these signs unambiguously.

\Image[2018-ia-physics-q8-gyroscope.png]
{title=Top-view torque and angular-momentum directions, with the disk's spin viewed from its outer face}

The drawing shows an instantaneous top view; the disk's <angular momentum> and the horizontal spindle turn clockwise together. The pivot <force> has zero <torque> about the pivot.

**Rate of precession.** For steady horizontal motion the spin contribution has magnitude $L=I\omega$ and turns through $d\phi=\Omega\,dt$ in time $dt$. Its change is perpendicular to itself and has magnitude $dL=L\,d\phi$. Hence
$$
|\boldsymbol\tau|=\left|\frac{d\mathbf L}{dt}\right|=\Omega I\omega=mgl,
\qquad
\boxed{\Omega=\frac{mgl}{I\omega}.}
$$
This is the usual <slow steady gyroscope precession> relation. Any constant vertical <angular momentum> associated with steady precession has zero time derivative; the horizontal spin contribution supplies the changing component here. Transient <nutation> is excluded by the prescribed horizontal steady motion.

**Clock design.** Convert both rotation rates to <angular frequencies>:
$$
\Omega=\frac{2\pi}{60}\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}},\qquad
\omega=2\pi(300)\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}.
$$
For the <moment of inertia of a uniform disc>, $I=mR^2/2$, so the <mass> cancels:
$$
\boxed{l=\frac{\Omega R^2\omega}{2g}
=\frac{(2\pi/60)(0.10)^2(600\pi)}{2(9.8)}
=0.1007\,\mathrm m\simeq10.1\,\mathrm{cm}.}
$$

**Spin friction.** At fixed geometry, reducing $\omega$ increases $\Omega\propto1/\omega$. The <precession> period, $T_p=2\pi/\Omega$, therefore **decreases**. This trend holds while slow steady <precession> remains a suitable description; it cannot be extrapolated to a stopped disk.

**Counterweight.** To oppose the disk's gravitational <torque>, place the counterweight on the other side of the pivot, at $-l_c\widehat{\mathbf x}$. The net <torque> is
$$
\boldsymbol\tau=g(ml-m_cl_c)\widehat{\mathbf y}.
$$
A horizontal axle can remain fixed when this vanishes, giving
$$
\boxed{l_c=\frac{ml}{m_c}\quad\text{on the opposite side of the pivot}.}
$$
For the fixed spin direction already found, <counterbalanced gyroscope precession> has signed rate
$$
\Omega_z=-\frac{g(ml-m_cl_c)}{I\omega}.
$$
Moving the counterweight outward from the pivot first slows the clockwise motion, eliminates its driving <torque> at balance, and then reverses the steady motion to counterclockwise. A counterweight on the disk's side would add to the gravitational <torque>. These statements neglect axial spin <moment of inertia> of the counterweight and assume the stated horizontal motion, without a separately imposed precession at balance.

= Solution

**Momentum identity.** From the <Lorentz factor>, $v^2/c^2=1-\gamma^{-2}$. The relativistic <three-momentum> therefore satisfies
$$
p^2=\gamma^2m^2v^2=m^2c^2\gamma^2(1-\gamma^{-2}),
\qquad
\boxed{p^2=m^2c^2(\gamma^2-1).}
$$
Together with <relativistic energy> $E=\gamma mc^2$, this gives the <energy–momentum relation> $E^2-p^2c^2=m^2c^4$.

**Parent in the laboratory.** The <rest energy> is $m_0c^2=1\,\mathrm{GeV}$, while the total <relativistic energy> is $3\,\mathrm{GeV}$. Consequently
$$
\gamma_0=3,\qquad
\boxed{\frac{v_0}{c}=\sqrt{1-\frac19}=\frac{\sqrt8}{3}=0.942809.}
$$
Its <momentum> magnitude is $p_0c=\sqrt{3^2-1^2}\,\mathrm{GeV}=\sqrt8\,\mathrm{GeV}$.

**Daughters in the zero-momentum frame.** The <centre-of-momentum frame> is initially the parent's rest frame. <Conservation of momentum> gives $\mathbf p_1^*=-\mathbf p_2^*$. Equal daughter <rest masses> then imply equal <relativistic energies>. <Conservation of relativistic energy> gives
$$
E_X^*=\frac{m_0c^2}{2}=\frac12\,\mathrm{GeV},\qquad
\gamma_X=\frac{E_X^*}{m_Xc^2}
=\frac{m_0/2}{m_0/3}=\frac32.
$$
Their <speeds> are therefore
$$
\boxed{\gamma_X=\frac32,\qquad \frac{v_X^*}{c}
=\sqrt{1-\frac49}=\frac{\sqrt5}{3}=0.745356.}
$$
The two <velocities> are opposite, and $p_X^*c=\sqrt{(1/2)^2-(1/3)^2}\,\mathrm{GeV}=\sqrt5\,\mathrm{GeV}/6$. Their total <kinetic energy> is $m_0c^2-2m_Xc^2=m_0c^2/3$.

**Invariant of the complete system.** Before the decay, in the <centre-of-momentum frame>,
$$
\mathcal I_{\rm before}=(m_0c^2)^2-0=m_0^2c^4.
$$
After the <relativistic two-body decay>,
$$
\mathcal I_{\rm after}=(E_X^*+E_X^*)^2
-c^2|\mathbf p_X^*+(-\mathbf p_X^*)|^2
=(m_0c^2)^2.
$$
In the laboratory, <four-momentum conservation> fixes the summed daughter energy to $3\,\mathrm{GeV}$ and their summed <momentum> to the parent's $\mathbf p_0$. Thus the same calculation is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal I_{\rm before}=\mathcal I_{\rm after}
=(3\,\mathrm{GeV})^2-(\sqrt8\,\mathrm{GeV})^2
=1\,\mathrm{GeV}^2=m_0^2c^4.}
$$
The invariant is formed after summing the daughter <four-momenta>; adding their separate squared rest energies would omit the relative-motion contribution to the invariant <mass> of the pair.

= Solution

**Axial moment of inertia.** In the original diagram $R_1$ is the outer radius and $R_2$ the inner radius. For uniform <mass density> $\rho$, a coaxial annulus of thickness $dr$ has $dm=2\pi\rho Lr\,dr$. Therefore the <axial moment of inertia of a hollow cylinder> is
$$
I=\int_{R_2}^{R_1}r^2dm
=2\pi\rho L\int_{R_2}^{R_1}r^3\,dr
=\frac{\pi\rho L}{2}(R_1^4-R_2^4).
$$
Since $M=\pi\rho L(R_1^2-R_2^2)$, factorizing the fourth-power difference gives
$$
\boxed{I=\frac12M(R_1^2+R_2^2).}
$$
For a solid cylinder $R_2\to0$, giving **$I=MR_1^2/2$**. For a thin cylindrical shell with fixed <mass> and $R_1,R_2\to R$, **$I=MR^2$**. The latter limit agrees with every mass element being at radius $R$.

**Undamped rolling oscillations.** Let $x$ be axle <displacement> from the <spring> equilibrium. <Rolling without slipping> imposes $|\omega_{\rm roll}|=|\dot x|/R$. The sum of translational and <rotational kinetic energy> is
$$
T=\frac12m\dot x^2+\frac12I\frac{\dot x^2}{R^2}
=\frac12\left(m+\frac I{R^2}\right)\dot x^2.
$$
The <potential energy> is $U=kx^2/2$. Differentiating $T+U$ in the absence of <damping>, or applying the force and torque equations below, gives
$$
\left(m+\frac I{R^2}\right)\ddot x+kx=0.
$$
For the thin shell $I=mR^2$, the effective inertia is $2m$, and the <angular frequency> of the <simple harmonic motion> is
$$
\boxed{\omega_0=\sqrt{\frac{k}{2m}}.}
$$
Static contact <friction> supplies the required <torque> but does no work when the contact point is instantaneously stationary.

**Damped force balance.** Take rightward <force> as positive and counterclockwise rotation as positive. Write $f$ for the signed <static friction> at the bottom contact. Then
$$
m\ddot x=-kx-b\dot x+f,\qquad
I\alpha=fR,\qquad\alpha=-\frac{\ddot x}{R}.
$$
Eliminate $f=-I\ddot x/R^2$ and use $I=mR^2$:
$$
2m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=0,
\qquad
\boxed{\ddot x+B\dot x+Cx=0,\qquad B=\frac b{2m},\quad C=\frac k{2m}.}
$$
Here $b$ has units $\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$, so $B$ has units $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $C$ units $\mathrm{s^{-2}}$. The stated <drag force> is interpreted as acting through the axle, with no additional viscous <torque>. An oil bath could also exert such a torque physically, but its magnitude is not specified by this force-only model.

**Light damping.** Substitution of $z=Ae^{-pt}$ into the differential equation gives $p^2-Bp+C=0$. In the <underdamped> regime $B^2<4C$,
$$
p=\frac B2\pm i\sqrt{C-\frac{B^2}{4}}
=\frac b{4m}\pm i\omega_d,
\qquad
\omega_d=\sqrt{\frac{k}{2m}-\frac{b^2}{16m^2}}.
$$
Taking real linear combinations gives the general <damped harmonic oscillator> displacement
$$
\boxed{x(t)=e^{-bt/(4m)}[A_1\cos(\omega_dt)+A_2\sin(\omega_dt)].}
$$
The constants are fixed by the initial <displacement> and <velocity>. For example, release from rest at $x_0$ gives $A_1=x_0$ and $A_2=bx_0/(4m\omega_d)$. Light damping means $b^2\ll8mk$, in which case $\omega_d\simeq\omega_0$ and the envelope decays slowly compared with an oscillation.

= Solution
{parent=11}

**Complex impedance.** In sinusoidal steady state write physical voltage and <electric current> as real parts of $\widetilde V e^{i\omega t}$ and $\widetilde I e^{i\omega t}$. The <electrical impedance> $Z=\widetilde V/\widetilde I$ is a ratio of <phasors>; its magnitude gives the voltage-to-current amplitude ratio and its argument gives their phase difference. It is measured in <ohms>.

For an ideal <capacitor>, $q=Cv$ and $i=dq/dt=C\,dv/dt$. Consequently $\widetilde I=i\omega C\widetilde V$, giving
$$
\boxed{Z_C=\frac1{i\omega C}=-\frac i{\omega C}.}
$$
With this time convention the voltage lags the <electric current> by $\pi/2$.

**Bridge output.** The original diagram has $R_1$ and $C_1$ in parallel in the upper-left arm, and $R_2$ and $C_2$ in series in the lower-left arm. The point labelled $C$ is connected to the upper supply node. Set the lower supply node to zero, so $V_C=V_i$. Assuming the measured output draws negligible <electric current>, the two <voltage dividers> give
$$
V_A=V_i\frac{Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2},\qquad
V_B=V_i\frac{R_4}{R_3+R_4}.
$$
Their difference is therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{V_{AB}}{V_i}=\frac{Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2}-\frac{R_4}{R_3+R_4}.}
$$
The reactive-arm <electrical impedances> are
$$
Z_1=\left(\frac1{R_1}+i\omega C_1\right)^{-1},\qquad
Z_2=R_2+\frac1{i\omega C_2}.
$$

**Balance.** Setting $V_{AB}=0$ and cross-multiplying gives $R_3Z_2=R_4Z_1$. For this <Wien bridge>,
$$
\frac{Z_2}{Z_1}
=\left(R_2+\frac1{i\omega C_2}\right)\left(\frac1{R_1}+i\omega C_1\right)
=\frac{R_2}{R_1}+\frac{C_1}{C_2}
+i\left(\omega R_2C_1-\frac1{\omega R_1C_2}\right).
$$
The right-hand side must be the real number $R_4/R_3$. Equating imaginary and real parts gives
$$
\boxed{\omega=\frac1{\sqrt{R_1R_2C_1C_2}},\qquad
\frac{C_1}{C_2}=\frac{R_4}{R_3}-\frac{R_2}{R_1}.}
$$
Positive <capacitances> require $R_4/R_3>R_2/R_1$. In the equal-component case $R_1=R_2=R$ and $C_1=C_2=C$, this reduces to
$$
\boxed{\omega=\frac1{RC},\qquad R_4=2R_3.}
$$

**Band-pass output and phase.** Preserve the requested voltage polarity: $V_{AC}=V_A-V_C$. Since $V_C=V_i$,
$$
\frac{V_{AC}}{V_i}=\frac{Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2}-1
=-\frac{Z_1}{Z_1+Z_2}.
$$
Put $u=\omega RC>0$. The equal-component ratio is $Z_2/Z_1=2+i(u-u^{-1})$, so the <Wien bridge band-pass response> is
$$
\boxed{H(\omega)=\frac{V_{AC}}{V_i}
=-\frac1{3+i(u-u^{-1})}
=-\frac{iu}{1-u^2+3iu}.}
$$
At finite positive <frequency>, its imaginary part vanishes exactly when $u-u^{-1}=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\omega_r=\frac1{RC},\qquad H(\omega_r)=-\frac13.}
$$
The magnitude $|H|=[9+(u-u^{-1})^2]^{-1/2}$ is maximal there and tends to zero at both frequency extremes, as expected for a <band-pass filter>.

A convenient continuous, unwrapped <wave phase> is
$$
\boxed{\phi(\omega)=-\pi-\arctan\!\left(\frac{u-u^{-1}}3\right).}
$$
It decreases from $-\pi/2$ as $\omega\to0^+$, passes through $-\pi$ at $\omega_r$, and tends to $-3\pi/2$ as $\omega\to\infty$. A principal-value plot instead jumps from $-\pi$ to $+\pi$ at resonance; both represent the same <phasor>. Reversing the output to $V_{CA}$ would shift the phase by $\pi$ and make the resonant ratio $+1/3$, but that is a different output polarity from the one printed.

**Reflection and central spot.** At a boundary from lower to higher <refractive index>, the near-normal electric-field reflection coefficient is negative, $r=(n_1-n_2)/(n_1+n_2)$. This gives a $\pi$ <wave phase> reversal on reflection from the glass plate through the air film. Reflection at the lens's glass-to-air underside has no such reversal. These are consequences of the <Fresnel equations> for the reflected <electric field>.

At ideal contact the film thickness is zero, so the two reflected contributions have zero geometrical <optical path difference> but a relative $\pi$ phase reversal. They produce <destructive interference>, and the **central spot is dark**. This conclusion concerns the film reflection; other lens surfaces can add a weak background.

**Rings and their radii.** At radial distance $r$, let the air thickness be $t$. The two relevant rays reflect at the upper and lower film interfaces. The lower reflection accumulates an extra round-trip <optical path length> $2t$, plus the relative $\pi$ reflection shift. As $t$ increases away from contact, alternating constructive and destructive <thin-film interference> forms <Newton's rings>. At near-normal incidence, a bright ring satisfies
$$
\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(2t)+\pi=2\pi(m+1),\qquad
2t=\left(m+\frac12\right)\lambda,\qquad m=0,1,2,\ldots.
$$
The extended source can illuminate these localized film fringes: each incident contribution interferes with its own two reflected paths; unrelated source points need not be mutually coherent.

For the spherical lens surface, geometry gives $r^2+(R-t)^2=R^2$, so $r^2=2Rt-t^2$. Since $t\ll R$, $t\simeq r^2/(2R)$. Combining the equations yields
$$
\boxed{r_m=\sqrt{R\lambda\left(m+\frac12\right)}.}
$$
The $m=0$ bright ring is the first bright ring around the dark contact point.

**Curvature from ring diameters.** Writing $D_m=2r_m$, subtract the squared diameters to eliminate the unknown initial order:
$$
D_{m+20}^2-D_m^2=4R\lambda(20).
$$
Using <SI> units,
$$
\boxed{R=\frac{(7.1\times10^{-3})^2-(3.3\times10^{-3})^2}
{80(500\times10^{-9})}=0.988\,\mathrm m\simeq0.99\,\mathrm m.}
$$

**Liquid-filled film.** With film <refractive index> $n=1.4$, still smaller than both glass indices, there remains exactly one relative reflection phase reversal. The round-trip <optical path length> is now $2nt$, so
$$
r_m^2=\frac{R\lambda}{n}\left(m+\frac12\right).
$$
Here $\lambda=500\,\mathrm{nm}$ is the illuminating vacuum wavelength, approximately its air value, rather than its shorter value inside the liquid. The ring labelled $m=2$ therefore has diameter
$$
\boxed{D_2=2\sqrt{\frac{(0.988)(500\times10^{-9})(2+1/2)}{1.4}}
=1.878\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm m\simeq1.9\,\mathrm{mm}.}
$$
Filling the gap contracts each ring radius by $1/\sqrt n$ at fixed order and curvature.

**Equation and interpretation.** The supplied equation is the <time-independent Schrödinger equation> for a nonrelativistic particle with <mass> $m$ in a prescribed <potential energy> $V(x)$. Equivalently,
$$
\widehat H\psi=E\psi,\qquad
\widehat H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+V(x).
$$
It is an <eigenvalue> problem for the <Hamiltonian operator>. A stationary energy state has full <wavefunction> $\Psi(x,t)=\psi(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar}$. By the <Born rule>, $|\psi(x)|^2dx$ is the probability of finding the particle in a small interval $dx$, with <wavefunction normalization> $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\psi|^2dx=1$.

**Infinite barriers.** Infinite exterior <potential energy> excludes any finite-energy probability outside the interval. The <infinite square well> consequently has $\psi=0$ outside and the hard-wall boundary conditions
$$
\boxed{\psi(0)=\psi(a)=0.}
$$
The interior <wavefunction> must meet the zero exterior amplitude continuously. Its derivative need not be continuous across an infinite wall; imposing both zero value and zero derivative would eliminate every nonzero interior solution.

**Ground state.** Inside the well $V=0$, so $\psi''+k^2\psi=0$, with $k=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$. Its general solution is $A\sin kx+B\cos kx$. The left wall gives $B=0$, and the right wall gives $ka=n\pi$ for $n=1,2,\ldots$. Thus the discrete energy <eigenvalues> are $E_n=n^2\pi^2\hbar^2/(2ma^2)$. The smallest, $n=1$, is the <ground state>. Since
$$
\int_0^a\sin^2\frac{\pi x}{a}\,dx=\frac a2,
$$
its normalized <wavefunction>, choosing an irrelevant overall phase to make it positive inside, is
$$
\boxed{\psi_1(x)=\begin{cases}
\sqrt{2/a}\,\sin(\pi x/a),&0<x<a,\\
0,&x\le0\ \text{or}\ x\ge a,
\end{cases}\qquad E_1=\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}.}
$$

**Finite barriers.** For a finite step and constant particle <mass>, both $\psi$ and $\psi'$ are continuous at $x=0,a$. <Wavefunction matching at a finite potential step> follows by integrating the <Schrödinger equation> across a vanishingly small neighborhood of the step: a bounded potential cannot generate a finite jump in $\psi'$. The bound-state exterior solutions decay rather than vanish identically:
$$
\psi(x)\propto e^{\kappa x}\quad(x<0),\qquad
\psi(x)\propto e^{-\kappa(x-a)}\quad(x>a),\qquad
\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{2m(V_w-E)}}{\hbar}.
$$
Normalizability excludes the growing exterior exponentials. The equivalent interior boundary conditions are $\psi'(0)=\kappa\psi(0)$ and $\psi'(a)=-\kappa\psi(a)$; neither boundary value is zero for the nontrivial <ground state>.

The <ground state of a finite square well> remains symmetric about $a/2$ and has no nodes. It bends smoothly into exponential tails at both walls, extending over a larger region than the infinite-well state. Its maximum after normalization is lower for the deep well considered here. When $V_w\gg E$, the penetration length $1/\kappa$ is short, so the two shapes are close except near the walls and in the exterior tails.

\Image[2018-ia-physics-q13-well-states.png]
{title=Normalized infinite- and finite-well ground states, with smooth exponentially decaying tails for the finite well}

The finite curve is an illustrative exact matched solution with $V_w=10E_{1,\infty}$; no numerical wall height is specified by the question.

**Energy comparison.** The finite barriers relax the confinement, allowing a less rapidly varying interior <wavefunction> and therefore smaller <kinetic energy>. More explicitly, the even interior solution is proportional to $\cos[k(x-a/2)]$, and matching to the tails gives $k\tan(ka/2)=\kappa$. The lowest root lies at $0<ka<\pi$, while the infinite well has $ka=\pi$. Hence
$$
\boxed{E_{1,\rm finite}<\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}=E_{1,\infty}.}
$$
As $V_w\to\infty$, the tails disappear and the finite-well energy approaches the infinite-well value from below. This comparison uses the same zero of <potential energy> inside both wells.

**Field of discrete masses.** <Newton's law of universal gravitation> and <superposition> give the <gravitational field> away from the source points:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf g(\mathbf r)=-G\sum_{i=1}^N
M_i\frac{\mathbf r-\mathbf r_i}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_i|^3}.}
$$
It is the <force> per unit test <mass>, with units $\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}=\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, and points toward each positive source <mass>.

**Infinite circular cylindrical rod.** Let $b$ be the radius of the circular cross-section, so $A=\pi b^2$, and write $\lambda=\rho A$ for the <linear mass density>. Choose a coaxial cylindrical <Gaussian surface> with radius $r>b$ and length $L$. Translation and rotation symmetry make $\mathbf g=g_r(r)\widehat{\mathbf r}$. The end caps have zero field <flux> because their normals are axial; on the curved surface the outward-normal component is the constant negative value $g_r$. <Gauss's law for gravity> therefore gives
$$
\oint\mathbf g\cdot d\mathbf S=2\pi rL g_r
=-4\pi G(\rho AL),
\qquad
\boxed{\mathbf g(r)=-\frac{2G\rho A}{r}\widehat{\mathbf r}\quad(r>b).}
$$
The <gravitational field of an infinite uniform cylinder> inside the material instead has enclosed <mass> $\rho\pi r^2L$, giving $g_r=-2\pi G\rho r$. The exterior result assumes a circular cylindrical rod; area by itself would not specify the exact near field of a noncircular cross-section.

\Image[2018-ia-physics-q14-rod-orbits.png]
{title=Gaussian-cylinder cross-section for an infinite rod and the circular-speed curve of a finite line rod}

In the left panel the dashed circle is a transverse section of the coaxial <Gaussian surface>; the field points inward and the surface normals outward. Its side area is $2\pi rL$.

For an exterior <circular orbit>, <Newton's second law> equates the <force> required for <centripetal acceleration> to gravity:
$$
\frac{mv^2}{r}=m\frac{2G\rho A}{r},\qquad
\boxed{v=\sqrt{2G\rho A}=\sqrt{2G\lambda}.}
$$
The orbital <speed> is independent of radius and of the orbiting object's <mass>, for orbits outside the infinite rod.

**Finite rod.** Taking the supplied field as the <gravitational field of a finite uniform line segment>, the corresponding balance at radius $R$ is
$$
\frac{v^2}{R}=\frac{GM}{R\sqrt{R^2+a^2}},\qquad
\boxed{v(R)=\left(\frac{GM}{\sqrt{R^2+a^2}}\right)^{1/2}
=\frac{\sqrt{GM}}{(R^2+a^2)^{1/4}}.}
$$
Since $M=2a\rho A=2a\lambda$, its two limits are
$$
\boxed{R\ll a:\quad v\simeq\sqrt{\frac{GM}{a}}=\sqrt{2G\rho A},
\qquad R\gg a:\quad v\simeq\sqrt{\frac{GM}{R}}.}
$$
The first reproduces the infinite-rod result: a nearby observer sees a very long source. The second is the familiar <circular orbit> speed about a <point mass>, since a distant observer cannot resolve the rod's length. The dimensionless curve is
$$
\frac{v}{\sqrt{GM/a}}=[1+(R/a)^2]^{-1/4}.
$$
It starts at a constant plateau and decreases smoothly, approaching an $R^{-1/2}$ dependence far away. For $R>0$, $dv/dR=-\sqrt{GM}\,R/[2(R^2+a^2)^{5/4}]<0$, consistent with the sketch.

**Derivation of the finite-rod field.** Put the source along $-a<z<a$ on the $z$ axis. In its central perpendicular plane, an element $dm=\lambda\,dz$ is at distance $s=\sqrt{r^2+z^2}$. Its field magnitude is $G\lambda\,dz/s^2$, and its inward radial component has the extra factor $r/s$. The axial components cancel between $z$ and $-z$. Thus
$$
g_r=-G\lambda r\int_{-a}^a\frac{dz}{(r^2+z^2)^{3/2}}
=-G\lambda r\left[\frac{z}{r^2\sqrt{r^2+z^2}}\right]_{-a}^{a}
=-\frac{2aG\lambda}{r\sqrt{r^2+a^2}}.
$$
Replacing $2a\lambda$ by $M$ gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf g(r)=-\frac{GM}{r\sqrt{r^2+a^2}}\widehat{\mathbf r}.}
$$
The integral treats the source as a <line mass>. For a finite cylinder of nonzero cross-section, the displayed finite-rod formula is a thin-rod approximation, not an exact field for all $r$. Its near-rod limit should therefore be read as $b\ll R\ll a$, keeping the orbit outside the material; the apparent line-source singularity at $r=0$ does not describe the interior of a real cylinder.

An <electric dipole> is a localized pair of opposite <electric charges>, or more generally a neutral <electric charge> distribution with nonzero first electric moment. For charges $-q$ and $+q$ separated by <displacement> $\mathbf d$ directed from negative to positive, the <electric dipole moment> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf p=q\mathbf d,\qquad [p]=\mathrm{C\,m}.}
$$
A neutral <molecule> can have a permanent <electric dipole moment> when its positive and negative charge centres do not coincide. An applied <electric field> can also produce an induced <electric dipole moment> by distorting an initially nonpolar <electric charge> distribution.

At distance $r\gg|\mathbf d|$ in <vacuum>, expand the difference of the two point-charge potentials to first order in the separation. The leading <electric potential> is
$$
\Phi(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mathbf p\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}.
$$
The monopole term cancels because the net <electric charge> is zero. Taking $\mathbf E=-\nabla\Phi$ gives the <electric dipole field>
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(\mathbf r)=\frac{3(\mathbf p\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})\widehat{\mathbf r}-\mathbf p}
{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}.}
$$
On the dipole axis its magnitude is $2p/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3)$ and its direction is along $\mathbf p$; in the equatorial plane the magnitude is $p/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3)$ and the field points opposite to $\mathbf p$. The $r^{-3}$ decay is faster than the $r^{-2}$ field of an isolated <point charge>.

In a uniform external <electric field>, the opposite forces on the two charges cancel in their resultant but form a <couple>. Its <torque> is
$$
\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf p\times\mathbf E,\qquad
|\boldsymbol\tau|=pE\sin\theta.
$$
For a fixed permanent <electric dipole moment>, the interaction <potential energy> is
$$
\boxed{U=-\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E=-pE\cos\theta.}
$$
Differentiating with respect to orientation recovers the torque. Parallel alignment minimizes $U$ and is stable against a small rotation; antiparallel alignment maximizes $U$ and is unstable. Rotating a fixed dipole slowly from parallel to antiparallel requires external <work> $2pE$.

A nonuniform electrostatic <electric field> can also exert a net <force>. Expanding the two charge forces gives $\mathbf F\simeq(\mathbf p\cdot\nabla)\mathbf E$, which equals $\nabla(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E)$ for a fixed dipole in a curl-free electrostatic field. Thus a suitably aligned dipole is attracted toward stronger field. An induced <electric dipole moment> requires care in computing energy: when $\mathbf p=\alpha\mathbf E$ for constant isotropic <electric polarizability>, building the dipole costs energy and the effective interaction is $U=-\alpha E^2/2$, rather than substituting directly into the fixed-dipole formula. These field, torque and energy effects explain molecular orientation and <electric polarization>.

A <conservative vector field> has path-independent <work>: the integral $\int_A^B\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r$ depends only on its endpoints. Equivalently, the <line integral> around every closed path in its domain vanishes. One can then define a single-valued <potential energy> $U$ by
$$
U(B)-U(A)=-\int_A^B\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r,
\qquad
\boxed{\mathbf F=-\nabla U,\qquad W_{A\to B}=U(A)-U(B).}
$$
The arbitrary additive constant in $U$ has no effect on the <force>. A useful zero is often chosen at infinity for a localized attractive interaction.

A differentiable <conservative force> has $\nabla\times\mathbf F=0$ because the <curl> of a <gradient> vanishes. Conversely, a smooth curl-free field on a <simply connected> domain admits a global scalar potential. The domain condition matters: on the punctured plane, $\mathbf F=(C/r)\widehat{\boldsymbol\theta}$ has zero <curl> away from the missing origin but closed-loop work $2\pi C$ around it. It is not globally conservative there. The existence of a local potential does not establish a single-valued potential throughout a domain with holes.

The <work-energy theorem> gives $\Delta T=W$ for <kinetic energy> $T$. If all forces are conservative and $U$ has no explicit time dependence, then
$$
\Delta T=-\Delta U,\qquad
\boxed{T+U=\text{constant}.}
$$
This is <conservation of mechanical energy>. In one dimension, an <equilibrium> occurs at $dU/dx=0$; a local minimum with $U''>0$ is stable, while a maximum with $U''<0$ is unstable. Expanding about a minimum gives $U\simeq U_0+U''(x_0)(x-x_0)^2/2$, producing <simple harmonic motion> with $\omega^2=U''(x_0)/m$.

Examples include the <spring> force $F_x=-kx$ with $U=kx^2/2$, the near-surface gravitational <force> $-mg\widehat{\mathbf z}$ with $U=mgz$, and the central <Newtonian gravitational field> with $U=-GMm/r$. An electrostatic <force> on a fixed <electric charge> $q$ has $U=q\Phi$, where $\Phi$ is the <electric potential>.

Sliding <friction> provides a contrasting physical test: traversing a closed path still dissipates <mechanical energy>, since the frictional force opposes the local motion on both outward and return legs. Its work depends on path length and cannot be represented by a position-only $U$. Total <energy> can remain conserved when the resulting internal energy is included. Also, even if $\mathbf F=-\nabla U(\mathbf r,t)$ at each instant, explicit time dependence gives $d(T+U)/dt=\partial U/\partial t$; a static conservative-field argument alone does not guarantee constant mechanical energy in that situation.

= Solution

<Faraday's law of induction> relates an induced <electromotive force> to the rate of change of <magnetic flux>. Choose a positive orientation around a loop and its associated surface normal by the <right-hand rule>. For a stationary conducting loop,
$$
\Phi_B=\int_S\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf S,\qquad
\boxed{\mathcal E=\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=-\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}.}
$$
Unlike an electrostatic field, the induced <electric field> generally has nonzero <curl>; the differential form is $\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial\mathbf B/\partial t$. For a moving circuit, <magnetic flux transport for a moving circuit> includes the magnetic <Lorentz force> on the charges:
$$
\mathcal E=\oint(\mathbf E+\mathbf u\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\mathbf l
=-\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt},
$$
where $\mathbf u$ is the local wire <velocity>. Thus changing the field or moving a conductor can produce induction.

The minus sign is <Lenz's law>: an induced <electric current>, when a closed conducting path permits it, produces a <magnetic field> opposing the change of linked flux. It need not oppose the original field itself. For example, increasing flux through a loop produces a current tending to reduce that increase; decreasing the same flux reverses the current. The opposition is consistent with <conservation of energy>: external mechanical or electrical <work> is needed to maintain a change against its inductive reaction.

For a coil of $N$ turns, define the <magnetic flux linkage> $\Lambda=\sum_{j=1}^N\Phi_j$, reducing to $N\Phi_B$ when every turn links the same flux. For fixed geometry and a linear magnetic response, <self-inductance> $L$ is defined by $\Lambda=LI$, and the coil's induced <electromotive force> is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E_{\rm self}=-L\frac{dI}{dt},\qquad [L]=\mathrm H=\mathrm{Wb\,A^{-1}}.}
$$
The corresponding terminal voltage drop, with the <passive sign convention>, is $V_L=L\,dI/dt$. It opposes changes in <electric current>; it does not require the current itself to vanish.

Building the current from zero requires power $P=IV_L=LI\,dI/dt$. Integrating gives the energy stored by an ideal <inductor>:
$$
\boxed{U_B=\int_0^I LI'\,dI'=\frac12LI^2.}
$$
This energy is stored in the <magnetic field> and can be returned when the current falls. In a series <RL circuit> driven by a constant voltage $V_0$, <Kirchhoff's voltage law> gives $L\dot I+RI=V_0$. Starting with zero current, $I=(V_0/R)(1-e^{-tR/L})$, with <time constant> $L/R$. This makes the resistance to rapid current changes directly observable. Two coils also have <mutual inductance>: changing primary current induces secondary <electromotive force> $-M\,dI_1/dt$ with a polarity determined by the chosen winding orientations, the principle behind a transformer.

= Solution

For a light <test particle> of <mass> $m$ outside a spherical source of <mass> $M$, <Newton's law of universal gravitation> gives $\mathbf F=-GMm\widehat{\mathbf r}/r^2$. It is a <central force>, so its <torque> about the source vanishes and <angular momentum> $\mathbf L=m\mathbf r\times\mathbf v$ is conserved. The motion therefore lies in a fixed plane when $L\ne0$. Its <gravitational potential energy> is $-GMm/r$, and <conservation of mechanical energy> gives
$$
\epsilon=\frac{v^2}{2}-\frac{GM}{r}=\text{constant},\qquad
h=\frac Lm=|\mathbf r\times\mathbf v|=\text{constant}.
$$
Here $\epsilon$ is <specific orbital energy> and $h$ is <specific angular momentum>. A <Kepler orbit> has polar equation
$$
\boxed{r(\theta)=\frac{p}{1+e\cos\theta},\qquad
p=\frac{h^2}{GM},\qquad
 e^2=1+\frac{2\epsilon h^2}{(GM)^2}.}
$$
The angle is measured from periapsis. The <conic section> is an <ellipse> for $\epsilon<0$ and $0\le e<1$, a <parabola> for $\epsilon=0$ and $e=1$, and a <hyperbola> for $\epsilon>0$ and $e>1$. The circle is the $e=0$ elliptical case. The special case $h=0$ is radial motion and must be handled as a degenerate limit. Bound ellipses have the source at one focus, which is <Kepler's first law>.

For a bound orbit of <semi-major axis> $a$, $\epsilon=-GM/(2a)$. Combining this with the energy equation gives the <vis-viva equation>:
$$
\boxed{v^2=GM\left(\frac2r-\frac1a\right).}
$$
The particle therefore moves faster near periapsis and slower near apoapsis. Conservation of <angular momentum> also gives constant <areal velocity>, $dA/dt=h/2$, expressing <Kepler's second law>. Using the ellipse's area and $h^2=GMa(1-e^2)$ gives the <orbital period>
$$
\boxed{T^2=\frac{4\pi^2a^3}{GM},}
$$
which is <Kepler's third law>.

A <circular orbit> follows from <centripetal acceleration> $v_c^2/r=GM/r^2$, giving $v_c=\sqrt{GM/r}$ and $T=2\pi\sqrt{r^3/(GM)}$. The minimum <escape velocity> from radius $r$ follows from the marginally unbound condition $\epsilon=0$:
$$
\boxed{v_c=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}},\qquad v_{\rm esc}=\sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}=\sqrt2\,v_c.}
$$
These ideal orbits neglect <drag force>, other gravitating bodies and changes in the central source. For two comparable <masses>, the relative <two-body problem> uses $G(M+m)$ instead of $GM$. An orbiting object is continuously in <free fall>; the inward gravitational <force> changes its direction even when its <speed> is constant. These inverse-square results assume the physical variation of gravity with radius, rather than extending a near-surface constant-$g$ approximation to an entire orbit.
