= Solution
{parent=A1}

Measure <displacement> from each arrangement's gravitational <equilibrium>. The <series and parallel spring stiffness> follows by adding <forces> or extensions. In the parallel arrangement the two <spring> <forces> change by $kx$ each, giving equivalent stiffness $2k$. In series the common <force> $F$ extends each <spring> by $F/k$, so total extension is $2F/k$ and the equivalent stiffness is $k/2$. Hence
$$
\boxed{f_{\parallel}=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{2k}{m}},\qquad f_{\rm series}=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{2m}},\qquad\frac{f_{\parallel}}{f_{\rm series}}=2.}
$$
The corresponding <angular frequencies> are $2\pi f$. <gravity> shifts the <equilibrium> extensions but does not alter the small-oscillation stiffness of these ideal linear, massless <springs>.

= Solution
{parent=A2}

For a <circular orbit>, $v^2/r=GM/r^2$. The relation for <circular orbital speed from surface gravity> eliminates the planet's <gravitational parameter>: $GM=gR^2$. The orbital radius is $r=R+h=7.4\times10^6$ m, so
$$
\boxed{v=\sqrt{\frac{gR^2}{R+h}}=\sqrt{\frac{10(6.4\times10^6)^2}{7.4\times10^6}}\simeq7.44\times10^3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
This is about **7.4 km/s**, under the circular-orbit and spherical-Earth approximation. Using surface $g$ unchanged at orbital height would overestimate the <centripetal acceleration>; its role here is to determine $GM$.

= Solution
{parent=A3}

In the stipulated constant-speed model, the Earth-frame <speed> is $v=D/t=3.844\times10^8/(76\times3600)\simeq1.405\times10^3$ m/s. The Earth and Moon are stationary endpoints in this model, so their Earth-frame separation $D$ is the <proper length>. The spacecraft-frame separation, measured simultaneously in that frame, is $D'=D/\gamma=D\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}$. Thus
$$
D-D'=\frac{D(v/c)^2}{1+\sqrt{1-(v/c)^2}}\simeq\frac{Dv^2}{2c^2}\simeq4.22\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm m.
$$
**The spacecraft-frame <distance> is shorter by about 4.2 mm.** This compares simultaneous endpoint measurements in two frames, not the coordinate difference between a launch event and an arrival event. The <small-speed length contraction estimate> also avoids numerical cancellation when evaluating the tiny contraction.

= Solution
{parent=A5}

Using metres and SI <viscosity>, the pressure difference is $\Delta P=200$ Pa and the central flow estimate is
$$
Q=\frac{\pi(2.3\times10^{-3})^4(200)}{8(8.9\times10^{-4})(0.50)}=4.939\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-1}}.
$$
If the quoted uncertainties are independent <standard uncertainties>, the pressure-difference uncertainty is $\sigma_{\Delta P}=\sqrt{10^2+10^2}=14.14$ Pa. First-order propagation gives
$$
\left(\frac{\sigma_Q}{Q}\right)^2=\left(4\frac{0.1}{2.3}\right)^2+\left(\frac{0.3}{8.9}\right)^2+\left(\frac{0.01}{0.50}\right)^2+\frac{10^2+10^2}{200^2}=0.03678.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{Q=(4.94\pm0.95)\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-1}}=(4.94\pm0.95)\,\mathrm{mL\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The <radius sensitivity of Poiseuille-flow uncertainty> comes from the fourth-power dependence on radius. If the quoted errors instead mean independent worst-case bounds, the first-order absolute fractional bound is $4(0.1/2.3)+0.3/8.9+0.01/0.50+20/200=0.3276$, giving approximately $\pm1.62\times10^{-6}$ m³/s. That conservative bound and the <standard uncertainty> result are different conventions, not interchangeable errors. The supplied flow model presumes a <Newtonian fluid> and <laminar flow> that is fully developed; no <density> is given for an independent Reynolds-number check.

The dimensions are $[\rho]=ML^{-3}$, $[\sigma]=MT^{-2}$ and $[\lambda]=L$. Equating exponents in $LT^{-1}=M^{\alpha+\beta}L^{-3\alpha+\gamma}T^{-2\beta}$ yields
$$
\boxed{\alpha=-\frac12,\qquad\beta=\frac12,\qquad\gamma=-\frac12.}

Thus the model has $v_{\rm ph}\propto\sqrt{\sigma/(\rho\lambda)}$. Its common dimensionless factor cancels in the ratio:
$$
\boxed{\frac{v_{\rm water}}{v_{\rm mercury}}=\sqrt{\frac{0.073}{0.490}\frac{13600}{1000}\frac{5}{10}}=1.0065\simeq1.01.}
$$
They are nearly equal within this capillary-wave model. The assumed <power law> omits gravitational restoring <forces> and finite-depth effects; <dimensional analysis> cannot establish those omissions for an arbitrary real surface <wave>.

= Solution
{parent=B7}

An <impulse> is $\mathbf J=\int\mathbf F\,dt$, giving $\Delta\mathbf P=\mathbf J$. For a short localized <impulse> at <displacement> $\mathbf r$ from the <centre of mass>, the <angular impulse> is $\Delta\mathbf L_{\rm CM}=\mathbf r\times\mathbf J$; for a changing lever arm use its instantaneous value inside the <integral>. For a uniform solid sphere with <volume> <density> $\rho_V$, direct integration about the $z$ axis gives
$$
I=\rho_V\int_0^a r^4\,dr\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,d\theta\int_0^{2\pi}d\phi=\frac{8\pi}{15}\rho_Va^5=\boxed{\frac25Ma^2}.
$$
Take forward translation $u$ and forward-rolling clockwise spin $\Omega$ as positive. Neglect the table's tangential <impulse> during the much shorter cue impact. Immediately afterwards,
$$
\boxed{u_0=\frac{I_p}{M},\qquad\Omega_0=\frac{dI_p}{I}=\frac{5dI_p}{2Ma^2}.}
$$
The bottom contact point has slip <velocity> $s_0=u_0-a\Omega_0$. The <cue height for immediate rolling> is therefore **$d=2a/5$**.

For the <impulse-driven slipping-to-rolling transition of a sphere> at an arbitrary admissible impact height, let $J_f$ be the signed <friction> <impulse> accumulated before slipping ends. Translation and spin then obey $u_f=u_0+J_f/M$ and $\Omega_f=\Omega_0-aJ_f/I$. Impose $u_f=a\Omega_f$ and eliminate $J_f$:
$$
\boxed{u_f=\frac{u_0+(I/(Ma))\Omega_0}{1+I/(Ma^2)}=\frac{5I_p}{7M}\left(1+\frac da\right).}
$$
This expression handles both sliding directions. If $d<2a/5$, the contact point slips forward and <friction> slows the centre; if $d>2a/5$, excess spin makes it slip backward and <friction> accelerates the centre. <friction> still dissipates total <mechanical energy>. For $\mu_d>0$, $\dot s=-(7/2)\mu_dg\,\operatorname{sgn}s$, so the slipping time is $2|s_0|/(7\mu_dg)$. Its duration depends on <friction>, while the final <speed> does not. With zero <friction> no slipping-to-rolling transition occurs unless rolling was present from the outset.

{parent=Section B}

= Solution
{parent=B8}

<Newton's first law> identifies <inertial frames> in which a force-free body maintains constant <velocity>. The second is $\dot{\mathbf P}=\mathbf F_{\rm net}$ for a closed system; for fixed <mass> it becomes $m\mathbf a=\mathbf F$. The third gives equal and opposite pair <forces>. Summing the second law over a closed collection cancels internal <force> pairs, so total <momentum> is conserved when the resultant external <force> vanishes. A rocket alone is an open variable-mass subsystem, so one must include the <momentum> carried by exhaust before applying this reasoning.

Choose downward <velocity> as positive and write $M(t)=M_0-\alpha t$. During $dt$, exhaust <mass> $dm=\alpha dt$ leaves downward with <speed> $v+v_E$ in the surface frame. To first order, <momentum> balance for rocket plus this exhaust gives
$$
Mv+Mg\,dt=(M-dm)(v+dv)+dm(v+v_E),\qquad \dot v=g-\frac{\alpha v_E}{M_0-\alpha t}.
$$
Integration, with $v(0)=v_0$, yields
$$
\boxed{v(t)=v_0+gt+v_E\log\frac{M_0-\alpha t}{M_0}.}
$$
Setting $g=0$ gives the gravity-free result. This assumes a constant downward relative exhaust <speed>, constant mass-loss rate and $M_0-\alpha t>0$ throughout the burn; the negative logarithm supplies the upward thrust effect.

Height satisfies $\dot h=-v$. Put $u(t)=1-\alpha t/M_0$. Integrating the logarithm and choosing $h(0)=h_0$ gives
$$
\boxed{h(t)=h_0-v_0t-\frac12gt^2+\frac{M_0v_E}{\alpha}[u\log u-u+1].}
$$
Equivalently, the last term is $v_E[t+(M_0-\alpha t)\log u/\alpha]$. Differentiating either form recovers $-v$ and checks the sign.

For this <constant-rate retro-rocket landing>, at touchdown $u_L=3/4$ and $v_L=0$, hence
$$
\boxed{t_L=\frac{v_E\log(4/3)-v_0}{g}=12.481\,\mathrm s.}
$$
The fuel condition gives $\alpha=M_0/(4t_L)=200.30$ kg/s. Requiring $h(t_L)=0$ then gives
$$
\boxed{h_0=v_0t_L+\frac12gt_L^2-v_Et_L[1+3\log(3/4)]=275.4\,\mathrm m.}
$$
In this burn the downward <speed> decreases monotonically to zero: the initial thrust acceleration already exceeds <gravity> and increases as fuel is consumed. The result is the idealized vertical landing, without atmospheric drag, changing <gravity> or a separate terminal burn <phase>.

= Solution
{parent=B9}

For a particle of <rest mass> $m_r$, $E=\gamma m_rc^2$ and $p=\gamma m_rv$, with $\gamma=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$. The low-speed expansion is $E=m_rc^2+\tfrac12m_rv^2+O(m_rv^4/c^2)$, separating invariant <rest energy> from the nonrelativistic <kinetic energy> term.

Conserve the total laboratory <energy> and <momentum> at coalescence: $E_{\rm tot}=4mc^2$ and $p_{\rm tot}=2.4mc$. Since $V=c^2p_{\rm tot}/E_{\rm tot}$, **the combined particle moves at $0.6c$**. Its <rest mass> is $M=\sqrt{4^2-2.4^2}\,m=3.2m$. The incident particle is not of <mass> $m$: its stated <energy> and <momentum> imply $m_{\rm incident}=\sqrt{3^2-2.4^2}\,m=1.8m$. Thus
$$
\boxed{M=3.2m>m_{\rm incident}+m_{\rm target}=2.8m.}
$$
The extra $0.4mc^2$ is internal <rest energy> retained by the composite after a <perfectly inelastic collision>; total <energy> and <four-momentum> are conserved, while the sum of individual <rest masses> need not be.

The composite's <Lorentz factor> is $1.25$. For a rest-frame lifetime $\tau$, the corresponding laboratory time is $1.25\tau$ and its travel <distance> is
$$
\boxed{\ell=V\gamma\tau=0.75c\tau.}
$$
If $\tau$ denotes a mean radioactive lifetime, this is the mean flight <distance>, not a fixed <distance> for every decay. The separate frame diagrams below use the common positive collision axis.

= Solution

In the <laboratory frame> the incoming particle and the composite move along $+x$, while the target is initially at rest. In the <equal-energy two-photon decay of a moving particle>, zero total transverse <momentum> makes the two <photon> directions symmetric about $+x$. Each <photon> has <energy> $2mc^2$ and <momentum> magnitude $2mc$, so longitudinal <momentum conservation> gives $2(2mc)\cos\theta=2.4mc$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\theta=\arccos0.6=53.130^\circ,\qquad\text{photon opening angle}=2\theta=106.260^\circ.}
$$
Their momenta can be written $(1.2,\pm1.6)mc$. The opening <angle> is not $180^\circ$ in this frame, because the parent carried nonzero total <momentum>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Boost along $+x$ at $0.6c$ into the <centre-of-momentum frame>. The composite is stationary. The incoming particle has $p'_x=1.25(2.4-0.6\times3)mc=0.75mc$, while the target has $p'_x=-0.75mc$. After decay the two <photons> have energies $Mc^2/2=1.6mc^2$ and opposite momenta.

Equal laboratory <photon> energies require the rest-frame <photons> to have zero <momentum> component along the boost: $E_{\rm lab}=\gamma(E'+Vp'_x)$ must be equal for two opposite <photons>. Their directions are therefore **$+y$ and $-y$ in the centre-of-momentum diagram**, perpendicular to the incoming collision axis. Boosting those transverse <photons> gives exactly the laboratory momenta in part (a). A stationary composite has no direction-of-travel arrow in this frame.

= Solution
{parent=C10}

For the supplied linear meter model let $\omega_0^2=C/I$ and $2\gamma=\Phi_0^2/(RI)$. If the applied step is $i_s$, the settled deflection is $\theta_\infty=\Phi_0i_s/C$. An <underdamped> response overshoots and rings about that value; a critically damped response reaches it monotonically without ringing; an <overdamped> response is also monotone from these initial conditions but has a slower final approach. The original plot uses the common final value and natural-frequency time scale.

\Image[paper-1-meter-response.png]
{title=Original normalized meter step responses and the critically damped amplitude and phase response}

The <critical step response of a galvanometer> has $\gamma=\omega_0$ and repeated homogeneous root $-\omega_0$. Apply $\theta(0)=\dot\theta(0)=0$ to the general constant-force solution to obtain
$$
\boxed{\theta(t)=\theta_\infty[1-(1+\omega_0t)e^{-\omega_0t}],\qquad t\ge0.}
$$
Its <derivative> is $\theta_\infty\omega_0^2t e^{-\omega_0t}$, with the sign of the step. It is the fastest nonoscillatory response within this second-order family at fixed $\omega_0$, avoiding a needle that repeatedly crosses the reading or an unnecessarily slow <overdamped> settling.

For sinusoidal drive choose $e^{i\omega t}$ <phasors>. Writing $F_0=\Phi_0i_0/I$, the response <amplitude> is
$$
\Theta=\frac{F_0}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2+2i\gamma\omega},\qquad\theta(t)=|\Theta|\cos(\omega t+\phi),
$$
$$
|\Theta|=\frac{F_0}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+4\gamma^2\omega^2}},\qquad \boxed{\phi=-\operatorname{atan2}(2\gamma\omega,\omega_0^2-\omega^2).}
$$
For positive $F_0$, choose the continuous lag branch from zero to $-\pi$ as <frequency> increases; an ordinary single-argument arctangent without the quadrant would give the wrong high-frequency <phase>.

For the <critical galvanometer transfer function>, the denominator factors as $(\omega_0+i\omega)^2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\theta(t)=\frac{F_0}{\omega_0^2+\omega^2}\cos(\omega t+\phi),\qquad \phi=-2\arctan(\omega/\omega_0).}
$$
For $\omega\ll\omega_0$, the meter follows the quasi-static deflection $\Phi_0i_0/C$ with a small lag $\phi\simeq-2\omega/\omega_0$. For $\omega\gg\omega_0$, its <amplitude> is $F_0/\omega^2$ and its lag approaches $-\pi$.

The stated components give $\gamma=12.5$ s⁻¹ and, if critical, $\omega_0=12.5$ rad/s, or $f_0=1.99$ Hz. At 50 Hz, $\omega=314.16$ rad/s and
$$
\boxed{\frac{|\Theta(50\,\mathrm{Hz})|}{|\Theta(0)|}=\frac{12.5^2}{12.5^2+314.16^2}=1.58\times10^{-3},\qquad\phi=-175.44^\circ.}
$$
The needle barely responds compared with its DC sensitivity and its mean deflection for a pure sinusoid is zero in this linear model. **It is unsuitable for directly measuring 50-Hz <amplitude> or RMS current without an additional <rectifier> or another readout method.** The calculation treats $\Phi_0$ as the stipulated <torque>/back-emf coefficient of the given equation; it does not infer an unprovided winding geometry.

= Solution
{parent=C11}

Use $e^{i\omega t}$ <phasors>. The relation $V=L\dot I$ gives $Z_L=i\omega L$. The three branches form a <parallel RLC circuit> and share the source <voltage>. The source-current <phasor> and its real time dependence are
$$
\widetilde I_{\rm total}=V_0\left[\frac1R+i\left(\omega C-\frac1{\omega L}\right)\right],
$$
$$
I_{\rm total}(t)=V_0\left[\frac{\cos\omega t}{R}-\left(\omega C-\frac1{\omega L}\right)\sin\omega t\right].
$$
Consequently its <amplitude> is $V_0\sqrt{R^{-2}+(\omega C-1/(\omega L))^2}$ and its <phase> relative to the <voltage> is $\tan^{-1}[R(\omega C-1/(\omega L))]$. At low <frequency> it is inductive and lags toward $-\pi/2$; at high <frequency> it is capacitive and leads toward $+\pi/2$. The ideal <amplitude> diverges at either extreme and has a finite minimum between them; real component losses and source <electrical impedance> limit those asymptotes.

\Image[paper-1-parallel-resonance.png]
{title=Original normalized source-current amplitude, phase and resistor-to-source-current ratio for an illustrative ideal parallel RLC circuit}

Zero <phase> requires cancellation of <electrical susceptances>:
$$
\boxed{\omega_{\rm res}=\frac1{\sqrt{LC}},\qquad|I_{\rm total}|_{\rm res}=\frac{V_0}{R}\quad\text{(minimum)}.}
$$
The <inductor> and <capacitor> currents are nonzero, equal in magnitude and opposite in <phase>, while the <resistor> supplies the in-phase source current. <energy> oscillates between the reactive elements; dissipation occurs in the shunt <resistor>. A source-current maximum would describe a different, series-resonant circuit.

The specified ratio is
$$
\frac{|I_R|}{|I_{\rm total}|}=\frac1{\sqrt{1+R^2(\omega C-1/(\omega L))^2}}.
$$
It exceeds $1/\sqrt2$ when $|\omega C-1/(\omega L)|<1/R$. Define $b=1/(RC)$ and $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$. The two positive endpoints are $\omega_\pm=[\sqrt{b^2+4\omega_0^2}\pm b]/2$, so
$$
\boxed{\delta\omega=\omega_+-\omega_-=\frac1{RC},\qquad Q=\frac{\omega_0}{\delta\omega}=R\sqrt{\frac CL}.}
$$
The endpoints have geometric mean $\omega_0$. Increasing the parallel resistance reduces dissipative loading and narrows this band, in contrast with increasing the resistance of a <series RLC circuit>. Equivalently, $Q=\omega_0U_{\max}/P_{\rm loss}$, with $U_{\max}=CV_0^2/2$ and $P_{\rm loss}=V_0^2/(2R)$. The <parallel RLC bandwidth> here belongs to the explicitly defined current ratio, not a half-maximum rule applied to a source-current peak that does not exist.

= Solution
{parent=D12}

Here $V(x)$ is <potential energy>, $E$ is the stationary state's total <energy>, and $\psi(x)$ is its spatial <probability amplitude>. The <wave function> has time factor $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$; $|\psi|^2$ is a position <probability density> for a normalized state. Stationary plane-wave scattering solutions are idealizations described by probability-current ratios, or components of a normalizable <wave packet>.

Above an upward step, a classical particle is fully transmitted with reduced <speed>. Quantum mechanically the abrupt <wavelength> change generally produces both reflected and transmitted <amplitudes> even though both regions are classically allowed. With unit incident <amplitude> and no <wave> arriving from the right,
$$
\psi(x)=\begin{cases}e^{ik_1x}+r e^{-ik_1x},&x<0,\\t e^{ik_2x},&x>0,\end{cases}\qquad k_1=\frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar},\quad k_2=\frac{\sqrt{2m(E-V_0)}}{\hbar}.
$$
The reflected component has signed wavevector $-k_1$ and the same <wavenumber> magnitude as the incident component. A finite potential jump with constant <mass> and no delta interaction requires continuity of $\psi$ and $\psi'$:
$$
1+r=t,\qquad k_1(1-r)=k_2t,\qquad\boxed{r=\frac{k_1-k_2}{k_1+k_2},\quad t=\frac{2k_1}{k_1+k_2}.}
$$
The <probability current> $j=(\hbar/m)\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\psi')$ gives probability reflection and transmission $\mathcal R=|r|^2$ and $\mathcal T=(k_2/k_1)|t|^2$, with $\mathcal R+\mathcal T=1$. The <amplitude> $t$ may exceed one without violating conservation of total probability, because the transmitted group <speed> is smaller.

For $0<E<V_0$, let $\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar$. The bounded right-hand solution is $t e^{-\kappa x}$, an <evanescent wave> tail with <amplitude> penetration scale $1/\kappa$. It carries no outgoing <probability current> in an infinitely long forbidden half-space, so reflection is total. This differs from transmission through a finite <potential barrier>, where a second boundary can support <quantum tunnelling>.

For a downward step, replace $k_2$ by $\sqrt{2m(E+V_0)}/\hbar>k_1$. The same <boundary conditions> give a nonzero negative $r$, corresponding to a reflected <amplitude> with <phase> reversal. If reflection were forbidden, $r=0$ would require both $t=1$ and $k_1=k_2$, impossible for a nonzero step. Reflection therefore does not require a classical <potential barrier>; the discontinuity of <wavenumber> mismatch is enough.

For the specified <electron> energies, $k_2/k_1=\sqrt{40/100}=\sqrt{0.4}$, so the requested <amplitude> coefficients are
$$
\boxed{r=0.22515,\qquad t=1.22515.}
$$
The corresponding probability coefficients are approximately $\mathcal R=0.05069$ and $\mathcal T=0.94931$. <electron mass> cancels from these ratios; there is no need to invent an additional material <effective mass>.

= D13
{parent=Section D}
{c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=D13}

For a flexible uniform string with <linear mass density> $\rho$ and <tension> $T$, <dimensional analysis> gives $[T/\rho]=L^2T^{-2}$ and hence $c\propto\sqrt{T/\rho}$. The small-slope <force> balance $\rho\psi_{tt}=T\psi_{xx}$ fixes the factor to one:
$$
\boxed{c=\sqrt{T/\rho},\qquad \omega=ck.}
$$
Here $\rho$ has units kg/m, not the volume-density units used for a bulk fluid. The <phase> moves along the string at $c$, while a material point moves transversely with $\psi_t=A\omega\sin(kx-\omega t)$. Its maximum <speed> is **$A\omega$**, which is not the <phase velocity>. Small slope $Ak\ll1$ makes $A\omega\ll c$ in this model.

The <kinetic energy> and extra elastic <energy> of a segment are
$$
K=\frac12\int\rho\psi_t^2\,dx,\qquad U=\frac12\int T\psi_x^2\,dx.
$$
The second expression follows from extra <length> $\sqrt{1+\psi_x^2}dx-dx\simeq\psi_x^2dx/2$ against the pre-existing constant <tension>. Time averaging a harmonic <wave> gives $\langle\mathcal E_K\rangle=\rho A^2\omega^2/4$ and $\langle\mathcal E_U\rangle=TA^2k^2/4=\rho A^2\omega^2/4$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\varepsilon_{\rm av}=\frac12\rho A^2\omega^2.}
$$

At the driven endpoint the string pulls along its local tangent into $x>0$, with vertical component $T\psi_x$ for small slope. The point exerts the opposite <force> on the string, so the <driven-string endpoint power> is
$$
\boxed{P(0,t)=-T\psi_x(0,t)\psi_t(0,t).}
$$
For the outgoing <wave> $\psi=A\cos(kx-\omega t)$, at $x=0$ the <derivatives> are $\psi_x=Ak\sin\omega t$ and $\psi_t=-A\omega\sin\omega t$. Thus $P=TA^2k\omega\sin^2\omega t$ and
$$
\boxed{P_{\rm av}=\frac12TA^2k\omega=c\varepsilon_{\rm av}.}
$$
The <energy flux of a transverse string wave> follows by combining the <energy density> $\mathcal E=(\rho\psi_t^2+T\psi_x^2)/2$ and flux $P=-T\psi_x\psi_t$, which satisfy $\partial_t\mathcal E+\partial_xP=0$. For a pure right-going nondispersive <wave>, $P=c\mathcal E$ even instantaneously. <energy> is transported with the <wave>; a material string element merely oscillates about its unperturbed position.

{parent=E14}

In the Lorentz law, $q$ is signed electric charge, $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$ are the electric and <magnetic fields> at the bead, and the <velocity> in $q\mathbf V\times\mathbf B$ is its full laboratory <velocity>. Here $\mathbf V=v\hat{\mathbf r}+w\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}$. Since $\hat r\times\hat z=-\hat\theta$ and $\hat\theta\times\hat z=\hat r$,
$$
\boxed{F_{B,r}=qBw,\qquad F_{B,\theta}=-qBv,\qquad F_{B,z}=0.}
$$
The tangential component integrated around an instantaneous <circle> of nearly fixed radius gives
$$
\boxed{W_\theta=\int_0^{2\pi}F_{B,\theta}R\,d\theta=-2\pi RqvB.}
$$
This is the stated tangential <circulation> <work> expression in the $w\gg v$ limit. **It is tangential <circulation> <work>.** The <tangential circulation work versus particle work> distinction matters because the bead actually follows an expanding spiral. The radial <displacement> may be small, but its <force> is proportional to $w$, so its <power> is of the same order as the tangential <power>. In fact
$$
P_{B,r}=qBwv,\qquad P_{B,\theta}=-qBvw,\qquad\boxed{\mathbf F_B\cdot\mathbf V=0.}
$$
The cancellation expresses the fact that <magnetic forces do no work>. Ignoring radial <work> while retaining the first-order tangential effect would incorrectly conclude that a <magnetic field> supplies net particle <energy>.

The <electric field> giving the same tangential <force> would be $\boxed{\mathbf E_{\rm eq}=-vB\hat\theta}$. This is a force-equivalent field for the moving guide, not evidence that a real circulating <electric field> has appeared in the laboratory merely because the loop expands in a static <magnetic field>. An <electromotive force> is the tangential driving-force <circulation> per unit charge, with units of <energy> per charge. For a material circuit moving with local <velocity> $\mathbf v_{\rm loop}$,
$$
\mathcal E=\oint(\mathbf E+\mathbf v_{\rm loop}\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\mathbf l.
$$
Orient the ring counterclockwise with positive normal $+\hat z$. Its flux is $\Phi=B\pi R^2$, so $\dot\Phi=2\pi BRv$. The tangential <circulation> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E=W_\theta/q=-2\pi BRv=-\frac{d\Phi}{dt}.}
$$
It is the motional term that matters here. For a fixed loop, the <circulation> of the actual <electric field> instead follows the partial time <derivative> of the <magnetic field>. The distinction between moving-wire magnetic <forces> and induction in a stationary loop is essential to the flux rule.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume II by Richard Feynman and others>[II.17–1, equations (17.1)–(17.3) and the distinction between moving circuits and changing fields; II.17–2, limits on applying the flux rule]{ref}

For the <magnetic power balance on an expanding circular guide>, the guide must supply an additional inward reaction $-qBw\hat r$ opposing the radial magnetic <force>. With radial guide <speed> $v$, this contribution to its <power> on the bead is **$P_{\rm guide}^{(B)}=-qBvw$**, equal to the tangential magnetic <power>. The radial magnetic <power> is positive by the same amount, so the total magnetic <power> still vanishes; the mechanical exchange is associated with the <moving mechanical constraint>.

If “the ring's <work>” is meant as its full <work> on a finite-mass bead, the source statement needs a further qualification. For a frictionless bead of <mass> $m_b$ with constant imposed radial <speed>, its radial acceleration is $-w^2/R$, so the complete radial guide reaction and <power> are
$$
N_r=-\frac{m_bw^2}{R}-qBw,\qquad P_{\rm guide}=N_rv=-\frac{m_bvw^2}{R}-qBvw.
$$
The first term is present even when $B=0$ and describes the familiar change of tangential <kinetic energy> on an expanding guide. The requested $-qBvw$ is exactly the additional magnetic-field-dependent term, not that full reaction <power>. No bead <mass> or extra agency fixing $w$ is supplied, so it would be unjustified to assign a numerical total mechanical <power> or silently discard its centripetal contribution.

An inverse-square point-source field has constant flux through enclosing spheres because their <area> grows as $r^2$. In <electrostatics>, <Gauss's law> is $\oint_S\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf S=Q_{\rm enc}/\varepsilon_0$, or $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho_e/\varepsilon_0$. For a spherical charge distribution, <rotational symmetry> makes the exterior field radial and constant in magnitude on a concentric sphere, giving $\mathbf E=Q\hat r/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)$. Symmetry is needed to take the field outside the <surface integral>; <Gauss's law> alone does not make an arbitrary charge distribution spherical.

For <gravity> the corresponding relations are $\oint_S\mathbf g\cdot d\mathbf S=-4\pi GM_{\rm enc}$ and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf g=-4\pi G\rho_m$. A spherical <mass> has exterior field $\mathbf g=-GM\hat r/r^2$. Inside a uniform sphere, $M_{\rm enc}(r)=4\pi\rho_mr^3/3$, so $\boxed{\mathbf g=-(4\pi G\rho_m/3)r\hat r}$. Inside an empty spherical shell the enclosed <mass> is zero and symmetry gives zero field. These examples distinguish an inverse-square exterior field from a field inside distributed matter.

The law also treats other symmetries, but their field-distance dependence differs: an infinitely long <line charge> has cylindrical rather than spherical spreading and a $1/r$ field. Flux conservation and geometry must both be used, rather than assuming every <Gaussian surface> leads to an inverse-square answer.

A time-independent <conservative force> defines a <conservative vector field> on a domain when the <work> between two points is independent of path, equivalently when every closed-path <work> <integral> vanishes. Define a <potential energy> $U$ by $\boxed{\mathbf F=-\nabla U}$ and $U(B)-U(A)=-\int_A^B\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r$. The additive constant is arbitrary; only differences affect <work>. A vanishing <curl> is necessary, and is sufficient on a <simply connected domain> under the usual smoothness assumptions. Local zero <curl> by itself need not guarantee a single-valued global potential on a domain with holes.

For a test <mass> outside a point or spherical <mass>, $U=-GMm/r$ with zero at infinity reproduces the inward gravitational <force>. Near a surface with approximately uniform downward <gravity>, $U=mgz$ when $z$ increases upward. A linear <spring> has $U=kx^2/2$. In <electrostatics> the <electric potential> is <energy> per unit charge, so $U=qV$ and $\mathbf E=-\nabla V$; potential and <potential energy> have different units and roles.

For motion under a time-independent <conservative force>, $d(K+U)/dt=0$. <nonconservative forces> change the <mechanical energy> by their <work>, and an explicitly time-dependent potential can exchange <energy> with the external agency changing it. A time-varying <magnetic field> can give a circulating <electric field>, so not every <electric field> is electrostatic or conservative. Conversely, zero magnetic <work> on a charge does not turn a velocity-dependent magnetic <force> into an ordinary position-potential <force>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

For a <test particle> outside a spherical central <mass>, the gravitational <force> is central, so <angular momentum> is conserved and the orbit is planar. With <specific angular momentum> $h=r^2\dot\theta$ and <specific orbital energy> $\epsilon=\dot r^2/2+h^2/(2r^2)-GM/r$, the radial <effective potential> combines attraction with the <centrifugal barrier>. Its nonradial solutions are <conic sections>:
$$
\boxed{r(\theta)=\frac{p}{1+e\cos(\theta-\theta_0)},\qquad p=\frac{h^2}{GM},\quad e^2=1+\frac{2\epsilon h^2}{(GM)^2}.}
$$
For nonzero $h$, negative <energy> gives an <ellipse> with $0\le e<1$, zero <energy> a <parabolic Kepler orbit> and positive <energy> a <hyperbolic Kepler orbit>. The <circle> is the special case $e=0$. Exactly radial motion is a separate zero-angular-momentum case and may hit the central body; a mathematical point-mass trajectory is not automatically a physically noncolliding orbit.

Conservation of $h$ gives constant <areal velocity> $h/2$, so equal <areas> are swept in equal times. For a bound <ellipse> of <semimajor axis> $a$, $\epsilon=-GM/(2a)$ and $T^2=4\pi^2a^3/(GM)$. A <circular orbit> has <speed> $\sqrt{GM/r}$, while the minimum local <escape speed> is $\sqrt{2GM/r}$, obtained by setting the total <energy> to zero. These are the test-mass two-body results: a comparable second <mass> requires the relative-coordinate <gravitational parameter> $G(M+m)$, and nonspherical structure or drag can perturb the conic and its conserved quantities.

= Solution

In SI units, with microscopic <charge density> $\rho_e$ and <current density> $\mathbf J$, <Maxwell's equations> can be written
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac{\rho_e}{\varepsilon_0},\quad\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\quad\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},\quad\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}.}
$$
The first relates <electric flux> to charge. The second states that the net <magnetic flux> through a closed surface is zero. <Faraday's law> relates electric <circulation> to changing <magnetic flux>. The <Ampère-Maxwell equation> includes both conduction current and <displacement current>; the latter is essential to time-dependent fields and local <charge conservation>.

For fixed contours and spanning surfaces, the <integral> <curl> equations are $\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=-d\Phi_B/dt$ and $\oint\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf l=\mu_0I_{\rm enc}+\mu_0\varepsilon_0d\Phi_E/dt$. For a moving material contour the first must include the motional <force> term; its total emf is $\oint(\mathbf E+\mathbf v_{\rm loop}\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\mathbf l$. This prevents confusing a changing circuit <area> with a partial time <derivative> of a static <magnetic field>.

Taking the <divergence> of the <Ampère-Maxwell equation> and using electric <Gauss's law> gives $\partial_t\rho_e+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0$. In a source-free <vacuum>, combining the two <curl> equations gives $\nabla^2\mathbf E-\mu_0\varepsilon_0\partial_t^2\mathbf E=0$, and similarly for $\mathbf B$, predicting <electromagnetic waves> of <speed> $c=1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$. The <Lorentz force> law then specifies how these fields act on a charge. Macroscopic matter descriptions may instead use $\mathbf D$ and $\mathbf H$ with free sources and constitutive relations; those should not be silently mixed with the microscopic-source equations above.
