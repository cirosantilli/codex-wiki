= Solution
{parent=a1}

**The <ground-state energies> are $1.50\ \mathrm{eV}$ and $0.376\ \mathrm{eV}$, respectively.** For a <particle in a box> of width $L$, the <wave function> must vanish at both infinite walls. The lowest nonzero <standing wave> therefore has <wavelength> $2L$, giving the <kinetic energy> eigenvalue
$$
E_1=\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2m_eL^2}=\frac{h^2}{8m_eL^2}.
$$
Using the <electron mass> and converting <joules> to <electronvolts> gives
$$
\boxed{E_1(0.5\ \mathrm{nm})\simeq1.504\ \mathrm{eV},\qquad E_1(1\ \mathrm{nm})\simeq0.3760\ \mathrm{eV}.}
$$
Doubling the confinement length divides this <kinetic energy> by four. A zero <potential energy> inside the box does not imply zero total <energy>: a constant, zero-<momentum> <wave function> cannot satisfy both wall conditions unless it vanishes everywhere. This is a concrete manifestation of <quantum confinement> and the <Heisenberg uncertainty principle>.

= Solution
{parent=a2}

Write the proposed <Jeans mass> as $M_J=K v^aG^bP^c$, with dimensionless $K$. The dimensions of <speed>, the <gravitational constant> and <pressure> are
$$
[v]=\mathsf L\mathsf T^{-1},\qquad [G]=\mathsf L^3\mathsf M^{-1}\mathsf T^{-2},\qquad [P]=\mathsf M\mathsf L^{-1}\mathsf T^{-2}.
$$
Equating the exponents of <mass>, length and time gives
$$
-b+c=1,\qquad a+3b-c=0,\qquad -a-2b-2c=0.
$$
Thus $a=4$, $b=-3/2$ and $c=-1/2$, so the <pressure-confined Jeans mass> scales as
$$
\boxed{M_J=K\frac{v^4}{G^{3/2}P^{1/2}}.}
$$
A larger <speed of sound> gives stronger support against <gravitational collapse>; stronger confinement at fixed <speed of sound> lowers the characteristic unstable <mass>. **<Dimensional analysis> determines the scaling, not $K$**, which requires a particular cloud model and instability criterion.

= Solution
{parent=a3}

Use an <RC low-pass filter>: put the <resistor> in series with the input and the <capacitor> between the output node and the return conductor. Measure the output <voltage> across the <capacitor>, with negligible output loading. The <voltage divider> then has <transfer function>
$$
H(\omega)=\frac{1/(i\omega C)}{R+1/(i\omega C)}=\frac1{1+i\omega RC},\qquad |H|=\frac1{\sqrt{1+(\omega RC)^2}}.
$$
At the high <frequency>, the attenuation requirement is
$$
2\pi f_hRC>\sqrt{500^2-1},\qquad C>\frac{\sqrt{500^2-1}}{2\pi(20000)(330)}\simeq12.1\ \mu\mathrm F.
$$
At the low <frequency>, approximately unchanged <amplitude> means $2\pi f_lRC\ll1$, so
$$
\boxed{12.1\ \mu\mathrm F<C\ll482\ \mu\mathrm F.}
$$
The upper relation is an asymptotic design condition, because “approximately” specifies no numerical tolerance. For example, retaining at least $99\%$ of the low-<frequency> <amplitude> would sharpen the upper bound to approximately $68.7\ \mu\mathrm F$.

**A practical illustrative choice is $C=22\ \mu\mathrm F$.** Its <voltage gain> is approximately $0.99896$ at $1\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and $0.001096$ at $20\ \mathrm{kHz}$: the latter <amplitude> is reduced by a factor of about $912$. Its <cutoff frequency> is $1/(2\pi RC)\simeq21.9\ \mathrm{Hz}$.

= Solution

For an ideal air-gap <parallel-plate capacitor>, neglecting fringing, the <electric field> between oppositely charged plates is $E=\sigma/\epsilon_0=Q/(\epsilon_0 A)$. <Dielectric breakdown> therefore limits the magnitude of the <electric charge> on either plate to
$$
\boxed{Q_{\max}=\epsilon_0 A E_{\rm bd}=(8.854\times10^{-12})(0.03)^2(5\times10^6)\simeq3.98\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm C=39.8\ \mathrm{nC}.}
$$
The two plates carry $+Q$ and $-Q$; the quoted stored <electric charge> is the magnitude on one plate, rather than the net <electric charge> of the whole <capacitor>. The gap does not enter this ideal bound: $C=\epsilon_0 A/d$ and $V_{\max}=E_{\rm bd}d$ make $CV_{\max}$ independent of $d$. Edge enhancement of the <electric field> can lower the practical limit.

= Solution
{parent=a5}

Far from its ends, approximate the body by an infinite uniform <cylinder>. By cylindrical <symmetry>, the <Newtonian gravitational field> is radial. Applying <Gauss's law for gravity> to a coaxial cylindrical surface of radius $s$ and length $\ell$ outside the material gives
$$
g(s)\,2\pi s\ell=4\pi G\rho\pi R^2\ell,\qquad g(s)=\frac{2\pi G\rho R^2}{s}.
$$
At the surface, $R=50\ \mathrm m$, so
$$
\boxed{g(R)\simeq2\pi G\rho R\simeq4.19\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}.}
$$
The <gravitational acceleration> points inward toward the axis. Its small magnitude reflects both the small size and the modest <density>. The infinite-<cylinder> approximation uses the specified distance from both ends; a spherical approximation would discard the relevant geometry. No rotational contribution is included, since no rotation rate is supplied.

= Solution
{parent=a6}

The threshold must conserve both <energy> and <momentum>. For head-on <photons>, the total <energy> is $E_1+E_2$, while the total <momentum> has magnitude $|E_2-E_1|/c$. Their combined <invariant mass> $M$ satisfies
$$
M^2c^4=(E_1+E_2)^2-c^2\left(\frac{E_2-E_1}{c}\right)^2=4E_1E_2.
$$
At the threshold for <photon-photon pair production>, the <electron> and <positron> have no relative <momentum> in their <centre-of-momentum frame>, so $M=2m_e$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{E_{2,\min}=\frac{(m_ec^2)^2}{E_1}=\frac{(5.110\times10^5\ \mathrm{eV})^2}{10^{-3}\ \mathrm{eV}}\simeq2.61\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{eV}=261\ \mathrm{TeV}.}
$$
Simply equating the laboratory <energy> to $2m_ec^2$ would miss the very large laboratory <momentum>: the newly produced pair must carry it away.

= Solution
{parent=b7}

The <moment of inertia> about a specified axis is $I=\int s^2\,dm$, where $s$ is perpendicular distance from the axis. The <parallel axis theorem> states $I=I_{\rm cm}+md^2$ for two parallel axes separated by $d$, one passing through the <centre of mass> of a body of <mass> $m$.

It is easiest to calculate the eccentric disc by subtraction. Let $m_f$ be the <mass> of the unperforated disc and $m_h$ the removed <mass>. Uniform thickness and <density> give $m_h=m_f/4$ and $m_f-m_h=M$, hence $m_f=4M/3$ and $m_h=M/3$. The hole is tangent to the rim, so its centre, and therefore the shaft axis, lies $R/2$ from the original disc centre. About the shaft axis,
$$
I_{\rm perforated}=\frac{4M}{3}\left(\frac{R^2}{2}+\frac{R^2}{4}\right)-\frac12\frac M3\left(\frac R2\right)^2=\frac{23}{24}MR^2.
$$
The shaft contributes $I_{\rm shaft}=M(R/2)^2/2=MR^2/8$. Thus
$$
\boxed{I=I_{\rm perforated}+I_{\rm shaft}=\frac{13}{12}MR^2.}
$$
The motor's <torque> is $P/\omega$ at nonzero <angular velocity>, while the resisting <torque> is $-\gamma\omega$. The <rotational equation of motion> and its equivalent <energy balance> are
$$
I\dot\omega=\frac P\omega-\gamma\omega,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12I\omega^2\right)=P-\gamma\omega^2.
$$
At the <steady state>, input <power> equals dissipated <power>, giving
$$
\boxed{\omega_0=\sqrt{P/\gamma}.}
$$
For the acceleration, use $u=\omega^2$. Then $\dot u+(2\gamma/I)u=2P/I$ with $u(0)=0$, so
$$
u(t)=\omega_0^2(1-e^{-2\gamma t/I}),\qquad \boxed{t=-\frac I{2\gamma}\ln\left(1-\frac{\omega^2}{\omega_0^2}\right),\quad0\leq\omega<\omega_0.}
$$
The approach to $\omega_0$ takes infinite time. This <constant-power rotational acceleration> idealizes the start: $P/\omega$ diverges at rest, although the <energy balance> has a well-defined limiting solution. A real motor has finite starting <torque>.

= Solution

<Conservation of energy> states that the total <energy> of an isolated system is constant. In a mechanical problem with conservative <forces> and no dissipation, it gives $K+U=\text{constant}$. It often determines a <speed> or turning height directly, without solving the full time-dependent <equation of motion>. With external work or dissipation, use the corresponding <energy balance> instead.

After a drop leaves the wheel, neglecting air resistance, the conservative <force> of the <Newtonian gravitational field> acts. Put the origin at the wheel axis and choose positive horizontal displacement to the right. In the printed diagram $\theta$ is measured upward from the leftward horizontal radius, and rotation is clockwise. The release position and <velocity> are therefore
$$
(x_0,y_0)=(-r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta),\qquad (v_{x0},v_{y0})=(r\omega\sin\theta,r\omega\cos\theta).
$$
For upward release, $\cos\theta>0$. These conventions fix the signs used below.

= a
{parent=b8}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=a}

With zero <gravitational potential energy> at the wheel axis, the release <energy> and the <energy> at the apex of the <projectile motion> are
$$
E_{\rm release}=\frac12mr^2\omega^2+mgr\sin\theta,\qquad E_{\rm apex}=\frac12mr^2\omega^2\sin^2\theta+mgy_{\max}.
$$
Only the vertical <velocity> vanishes at the apex: the horizontal <kinetic energy> remains. Equating the two <energies> gives
$$
\boxed{y_{\max}(\theta)=r\sin\theta+\frac{r^2\omega^2}{2g}\cos^2\theta.}
$$

= b
{parent=b8}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Set $s=\sin\theta$. The maximum height becomes the concave <quadratic function>
$$
y_{\max}=rs+\frac{r^2\omega^2}{2g}(1-s^2),\qquad \frac{dy_{\max}}{ds}=r-\frac{r^2\omega^2}{g}s.
$$
Under the stated high-<angular-frequency> assumption, $r\omega^2>g$, its interior maximum is
$$
\boxed{\sin\theta_* =\frac g{r\omega^2},\qquad (x_*,y_*)=\left(-r\sqrt{1-\frac{g^2}{r^2\omega^4}},\frac g{\omega^2}\right).}
$$
Thus the optimum release lies on the upper-left quarter of the wheel for the printed clockwise rotation. The largest attained height is
$$
\boxed{Y(\omega)=\frac{r^2\omega^2}{2g}+\frac g{2\omega^2}.}
$$
If extended to $r\omega^2\leq g$, the maximum over release positions is the limiting height $r$ at the top of the rim. The interior solution is the relevant branch for reaching a ceiling above the wheel.

= c
{parent=b8}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

To avoid contact with the ceiling, require $Y(\omega)<h$. Writing $X=r^2\omega^2$ gives
$$
X^2-2ghX+g^2r^2<0,
$$
whose roots are $g(h\pm\sqrt{h^2-r^2})$. Because $h>r$, the lower root is less than $gr$, while the assumed branch has $X>gr$. The lower inequality is therefore automatic on this branch, leaving
$$
\boxed{r^2\omega^2<gh+g\sqrt{h^2-r^2}.}
$$
Equality is the first grazing contact. This branch check is essential: retaining both algebraic roots as independent lower and upper speed limits would introduce a spurious lower limit on avoiding the ceiling.

= d
{parent=b8}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

At first contact the globally highest trajectory just touches the ceiling at its apex. Its flight time to that point is $t_*=r\omega\cos\theta_*/g$. The horizontal displacement from the wheel axis is
$$
x_{\rm hit}=-r\cos\theta_*+r\omega\sin\theta_*\frac{r\omega\cos\theta_*}{g}=\cos\theta_*\left(-r+\frac{r^2\omega^2}{g}\sin\theta_*\right)=0.
$$
Hence **the first point struck is directly above the rotation axis**, independently of $h$. The release starts to the left of the axis, but the drop's horizontal travel exactly cancels that offset at the optimum apex. The first-contact <angular velocity> satisfies $r^2\omega_*^2=g(h+\sqrt{h^2-r^2})$.

= Solution

The <Michelson-Morley experiment> splits coherent <light> into two perpendicular arms, reflects the beams and recombines them to observe <interference fringes>. Rotating the apparatus interchanges each arm's orientation relative to a proposed stationary <luminiferous aether>. The historical experiment used multiple reflections to increase the effective optical path and a rotatable support to compare orientations.<On the Relative Motion of the Earth and the Luminiferous Ether by Albert Abraham Michelson and Edward W. Morley>[1887, printed pp. 333–341: optical arrangement, rotation and fringe-shift comparison]{ref}

\Image[paper-1-interferometer.png]
{title=Original schematic of perpendicular round-trip optical paths in a Michelson interferometer}

For equal one-way effective arm lengths $\ell$, a classical stationary-aether model gives
$$
t_\parallel=\frac\ell{c-v}+\frac\ell{c+v}\simeq\frac{2\ell}{c}\left(1+\frac{v^2}{c^2}\right),\qquad t_\perp=\frac{2\ell}{\sqrt{c^2-v^2}}\simeq\frac{2\ell}{c}\left(1+\frac{v^2}{2c^2}\right).
$$
A right-angle rotation should reverse their difference, producing a <fringe shift> of approximately $2\ell v^2/(\lambda c^2)$. The observed orientation-dependent shift was much smaller than the stationary-aether prediction. In <special relativity>, the apparatus rest frame has the same vacuum <speed of light> along both arms, so no such orientation-dependent shift is expected. The result supports <special relativity> but does not, by itself, uniquely establish every postulate or exclude every modified aether theory.

For the rocket, take its tail at $x'=0$ and nose at $x'=L_0$, with coincident frame origins when $t=t'=0$. Denote emission, reflection and return by $A,B,C$. In the rocket's <inertial frame>, their coordinates $(t',x')$ are
$$
\boxed{A:(0,L_0),\quad B:(L_0/c,0),\quad C:(2L_0/c,L_0).}
$$
The inverse <Lorentz transformation> is $x=\Gamma(x'+ut')$, $t=\Gamma(t'+ux'/c^2)$, where $\Gamma=(1-u^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$. Thus the same <spacetime events>, in coordinates $(t,x)$, are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
A&:\left(\Gamma uL_0/c^2,\ \Gamma L_0\right),\\
B&:\left(\Gamma L_0/c,\ \Gamma uL_0/c\right),\\
C&:\left(\Gamma(2L_0/c+uL_0/c^2),\ \Gamma(L_0+2uL_0/c)\right).
\end{aligned}}
$$
There is no requirement that emission have $t=0$ in this chosen laboratory coordinate system.

The nose-to-tail travel time is $\Delta t_{AB}=\Gamma L_0(1-u/c)/c$. If $L$ is the laboratory length measured at one laboratory time, a left-moving pulse and the right-moving tail close their initial separation at speed $c+u$. Therefore
$$
L=(c+u)\Delta t_{AB}=\Gamma L_0(1-u^2/c^2),\qquad \boxed{L=L_0/\Gamma.}
$$
This derives <length contraction> from the pulse's travel. Comparing the positions of the two endpoints at different laboratory times would not measure the rocket's laboratory length.

= Solution

After switching, <Kirchhoff's voltage law> gives the free <series RLC circuit> equation
$$
L\ddot q+R\dot q+q/C=0.
$$
Its characteristic roots are $-\gamma\pm i\omega_d$, where
$$
\boxed{\gamma=\frac R{2L},\qquad \omega_d=\sqrt{\frac1{LC}-\frac{R^2}{4L^2}}.}
$$
A real, nonzero oscillation <frequency> requires $R^2<4L/C$. For a passive <resistor>,
$$
\boxed{0\leq R<2\sqrt{L/C}\simeq894\ \Omega.}
$$
The endpoint $R=0$ is the undamped limit; strictly decaying oscillations have $0<R<894\ \Omega$. The critical endpoint itself does not have a nonzero $\omega_d$.

Let $Q=CV_0$ denote the initial <electric charge>, so $Q=30\ \mu\mathrm C$ and $\dot q(0)=0$. Distinguish $Q$ from the amplitude coefficient $q_0$ in an exponential-times-cosine representation: imposing zero initial <electric current> generally requires a nonzero phase.

Put $\omega_0^2=1/(LC)$. The initial conditions give
$$
q(t)=Qe^{-\gamma t}\left(\cos\omega_dt+\frac\gamma{\omega_d}\sin\omega_dt\right),\qquad V_C(t)=V_0e^{-\gamma t}\left(\cos\omega_dt+\frac\gamma{\omega_d}\sin\omega_dt\right).

$$
\boxed{I(t)=-\frac{V_0}{L\omega_d}e^{-\gamma t}\sin\omega_dt.}

For the stated <electrical resistance>,
$$
\gamma=25\ \mathrm{s^{-1}},\quad \omega_0\simeq447.21\ \mathrm{s^{-1}},\quad \omega_d\simeq446.51\ \mathrm{s^{-1}},\quad T_d\simeq14.07\ \mathrm{ms}.

**The <voltage> starts at $6\ \mathrm V$ with zero slope; the signed <electric current> starts at zero and initially becomes negative.** Its initial slope is $\dot I(0)=-V_0/L=-6\ \mathrm{A\,s^{-1}}$. Both oscillate with an $e^{-25t}$ envelope and a $40\ \mathrm{ms}$ amplitude <time constant>. = Solution

The two stored <energies> follow directly by squaring the expressions for <electric charge> and <electric current>:
$$
E_C=\frac12CV_0^2e^{-2\gamma t}\left(\cos\omega_dt+\frac\gamma{\omega_d}\sin\omega_dt\right)^2,\qquad E_I=\frac{V_0^2}{2L\omega_d^2}e^{-2\gamma t}\sin^2\omega_dt.
$$
Both are nonnegative, with alternating maxima. Initially all stored <energy> is in the <capacitor>; at a zero of $q$ it is entirely in the <inductor>. The total is
$$
E=\frac{q^2}{2C}+\frac12L\dot q^2,\qquad \boxed{\frac{dE}{dt}=\dot q\left(\frac qC+L\ddot q\right)=-R I^2\leq0.}
$$
**The total <energy> decreases monotonically, with a horizontal tangent whenever the <electric current> is zero.** Its envelope decays on the $1/(2\gamma)=20\ \mathrm{ms}$ scale, but the exact total is not a featureless exponential: the resistor's instantaneous loss varies within each oscillation. The figure in part (a) shows this gradual modulation of the downward slope as well as the component <energies>.

= Solution
{parent=c11}

Measure the mass's displacement $x$ from its static equilibrium height. This removes the constant spring extension supporting its <weight>. If the spring's top moves with the box, its incremental extension is $y_2-x$, so its incremental upward <force> is $k(y_2-x)$. The specified damping force is against the mass's velocity in the static frame. <Newton's second law> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=ky_2=ka_s\cos\omega t.}
$$
In absolute height coordinates an additional constant on the right expresses the spring's rest length, its fixed attachment height and <gravity>; translating to static equilibrium removes it. Replacing the given damping by $-b(\dot x-\dot y_2)$ would describe a different <base-excited oscillator>.

After the decaying transient, write the <steady-state response> as $x=A(\omega)\cos(\omega t-\Phi)$. Using a complex sinusoid gives
$$
\widetilde x=\frac{ka_s}{k-m\omega^2+ib\omega},
$$
so
$$
\boxed{A(\omega)=\frac{ka_s}{\sqrt{(k-m\omega^2)^2+b^2\omega^2}},\qquad \Phi(\omega)=\operatorname{atan2}(b\omega,k-m\omega^2).}
$$
For positive <frequency>, the phase lag lies between zero and $\pi$, passing through $\pi/2$ at $\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}$. For small $b$, the <frequency response> begins at $a_s$, develops a narrow <resonance> near $\omega_0$, and falls as
$$
A(\omega)\sim\frac{k a_s}{m\omega^2}\qquad(\omega\gg\omega_0).
$$
The precise amplitude-peak <frequency> is $\sqrt{k/m-b^2/(2m^2)}$ when that expression is positive.

\Image[paper-1-seismograph-response.png]
{title=Absolute and relative motion of the stated seismograph model, and the phase lag of its inertial mass}

The pen records the relative displacement $z=x-y_2$. Its complex <transfer function> is
$$
\frac{\widetilde z}{a_s}=\frac{m\omega^2-ib\omega}{k-m\omega^2+ib\omega}.
$$
At high <frequency>, $x$ is small while $z\simeq-y_2$. **The inertial mass then stays nearly stationary and the recorded relative motion reproduces the ground displacement, with reversed sign.** At low <frequency> the mass follows the box and their relative motion is small; near <resonance> the response is strongly frequency-dependent. Here “high” means compared with the suspension's natural <frequency>, rather than an absolute seismic-frequency threshold.

= D12
{parent=section-d}

= Solution
{parent=d12}

Comparing with the standard <wave equation> gives the shallow-water <wave speed>
$$
\boxed{c=\sqrt{gd}.}
$$
Twice differentiating the proposed <standing wave> multiplies its spatial factors by $-k_x^2$ and $-k_y^2$, and its temporal factor by $-\omega^2$. It solves the <wave equation> precisely when
$$
\boxed{\omega^2=gd(k_x^2+k_y^2).}
$$
To obtain the local <gravitational potential energy>, integrate through a water column of height $d+h$ and unit horizontal area:
$$
V(t)=\int_0^{d+h}\rho gz\,dz=\frac{\rho g}{2}(d+h)^2.
$$
The time averages are $\langle h\rangle=0$ and $\langle h^2\rangle=A^2\cos^2(k_xx)\cos^2(k_yy)/2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\langle V\rangle=\frac12\rho gd^2+\frac14\rho gA^2\cos^2(k_xx)\cos^2(k_yy),\quad c_1=\frac12\rho gd^2,\quad c_2=\frac14\rho gA^2.}
$$
The constant is retained because the specified zero of <gravitational potential> is the tank bottom. This is local time-averaged <potential energy>, not the sum of <potential energy> and <kinetic energy>.

The no-normal-flow wall conditions are <Neumann boundary conditions>. Differentiating the cosine factors at the opposite walls gives
$$
\boxed{k_x=\frac{m\pi}{a},\qquad k_y=\frac{n\pi}{b},\qquad m,n=0,1,2,\ldots.}
$$
Negative integers give the same cosine modes. The formal $m=n=0$ case has $\omega=0$: it is a uniform change of the mean level, not a finite-<frequency> oscillation. In a closed tank with fixed water volume even that mean-level change is excluded from the oscillatory disturbance.

Since $a<b$, the lowest finite <frequency> is the mode $(m,n)=(0,1)$:
$$
\boxed{h=A\cos(\pi y/b)\cos(\omega t),\qquad \omega=\frac\pi b\sqrt{gd}.}
$$
It is uniform across the shorter direction, has opposite-sign displacement <antinodes> at the two ends of the longer direction, and a displacement <node> midway along it.

\Image[paper-1-water-modes.png]
{title=Original mode shapes for a closed rectangular tank and an ideal basin with one open end}

For the open-ended <Bay of Fundy> model, approximate the ocean mouth as a displacement <node> and the closed head as an <antinode>. The fundamental is a <quarter-wave basin resonance>, so its <wavelength> is $4L$ and
$$
T=\frac{4L}{\sqrt{gd}},\qquad \boxed{d=\frac{16L^2}{gT^2}.}
$$
Taking a roughly $12$-hour <semidiurnal tide> gives $d\simeq79\ \mathrm m$. Using the principal lunar period of approximately $12\ \mathrm h\ 25\ \mathrm{min}$ gives $d\simeq73\ \mathrm m$. **The idealized estimate is therefore about $7\text{–}8\times10^1\ \mathrm m$.** The lunar period is half of a roughly $24$-hour $50$-minute lunar day.<About Harmonic Constituents by National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration>[Examples M2 and S2: two lunar peaks per 24 hours 50 minutes, compared with two solar peaks per 24 hours]{ref}

This estimate treats the mouth as an ideal open boundary and replaces the real basin by a uniform depth. The supplied large tidal height motivates <resonance>; it is not enough by itself to determine damping or an amplification factor in this model.

<Snell's law> is $n_1\sin\theta_1=n_2\sin\theta_2$, with angles measured from the interface normal. The <Huygens-Fresnel principle> explains it by constructing the transmitted <wavefront> from secondary wavelets: during the same time interval, distances travelled in the two media are proportional to $c/n_1$ and $c/n_2$. The geometry of the advancing plane <wavefront> then gives $\sin\theta_1/\sin\theta_2=(c/n_1)/(c/n_2)=n_2/n_1$.

For incidence from index $n$ into vacuum, the transmitted angle is $\alpha=\arcsin(n\sin\theta)$. The ray bends away from the normal, so its positive deviation is
$$
\boxed{\delta(\theta,n)=\arcsin(n\sin\theta)-\theta.}
$$
For $0<\theta<\theta_c=\arcsin(1/n)$,
$$
\frac{\partial\delta}{\partial\theta}=\frac{n\cos\theta}{\sqrt{1-n^2\sin^2\theta}}-1>0.
$$
Thus the graph rises monotonically from zero to a finite endpoint with a vertical tangent:
$$
\boxed{\theta_c=\arcsin(1/n),\qquad \delta_{\max}=\frac\pi2-\arcsin(1/n).}
$$
At the endpoint the transmitted ray grazes the interface. Beyond the <critical angle for total internal reflection>, no propagating transmitted ray exists and <total internal reflection> occurs.

Inside a <prism>, the two ray angles with the face normals sum to its apex angle. For the equilateral cross-section, they are $\theta$ and $\pi/3-\theta$. The entry deviation equals the corresponding exit deviation under ray reversal. Adding the two changes of direction therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\Delta(\theta)=\delta(\theta,n)+\delta(\pi/3-\theta,n).}
$$
This is symmetric about $\theta=\pi/6$. Symmetry alone gives a stationary point; to establish that it is a minimum, differentiate once more:
$$
\frac{\partial^2\delta}{\partial\theta^2}=\frac{n(n^2-1)\sin\theta}{(1-n^2\sin^2\theta)^{3/2}}>0
$$
within the transmitted-ray interval. The sum is therefore strictly convex, and
$$
\boxed{\theta_{\min}=\frac\pi6,\qquad \Delta_{\min}=2\arcsin(n/2)-\frac\pi3.}
$$
These expressions require $1<n<2$ for a non-grazing path through both faces; $n=2$ is a grazing limit, and $n>2$ admits no such two-face transmitted path. More explicitly, both internal angles must be at most $\theta_c$, which is possible only if $2\theta_c\geq\pi/3$.

\Image[paper-1-prism-deviation.png]
{title=Single-interface deviation and the symmetric minimum of an equilateral prism, illustrated for index 1.5}

Atmospheric hexagonal ice crystals can present pairs of faces with a $60^\circ$ angle, acting as small <prisms>. Their orientations turn the minimum-deviation cone around the Moon into an <optical halo>. No transmitted ray of this family is deflected less than the <minimum deviation of a prism>. Moreover, near the minimum, $\Delta-\Delta_{\min}$ is quadratic in the departure from the symmetric ray, so a range of incident directions is compressed into a small outgoing angular range. **This <caustic> makes the inner rim sharp and bright**, with the ray density decreasing farther outside. The common <22-degree halo> has an angular radius near $22^\circ$ about either the Sun or Moon, hence a diameter near $44^\circ$.<International Cloud Atlas by World Meteorological Organization>[Section 3.2.3.1.1: radius, central luminary, bright ring and darker interior]{ref}

For a steady thin-wire <electric current>, the <Biot-Savart law> is
$$
d\mathbf B=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\frac{d\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf R}{R^3},
$$
where $\mathbf R$ points from the source element to the field point and $d\boldsymbol\ell$ follows positive <electric current>. On a circular loop's axis the transverse contributions cancel by <symmetry>. The axial numerator is $a^2d\varphi$ and the distance is $\sqrt{a^2+z^2}$, giving
$$
B_z(z)=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{a^2\,d\varphi}{(a^2+z^2)^{3/2}},\qquad \boxed{B_z(z)=\frac{\mu_0I a^2}{2(a^2+z^2)^{3/2}}.}
$$
Its graph is positive and even, with maximum $\mu_0I/(2a)$ at the centre and a $|z|^{-3}$ far-field decay. Reversing the <electric current> reverses its sign.

For two equally oriented $N$-turn coils centred at $\pm D$, superposition gives
$$
B_{\rm ax}(z)=\frac{\mu_0NIa^2}{2}\left[\frac1{(a^2+(z-D)^2)^{3/2}}+\frac1{(a^2+(z+D)^2)^{3/2}}\right].
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{B_{\rm ax}(0)=\frac{\mu_0NIa^2}{(a^2+D^2)^{3/2}}.}
$$
This is an even differentiable function, so every odd derivative at zero vanishes, including the first and third. To check the second derivative explicitly, let $f(s)=(a^2+s^2)^{-3/2}$. Then
$$
f''(s)=\frac{3(4s^2-a^2)}{(a^2+s^2)^{7/2}},\qquad B_{\rm ax}''(0)=\frac{3\mu_0NIa^2(4D^2-a^2)}{(a^2+D^2)^{7/2}}.
$$
Thus **$2D=a$ makes the quadratic variation vanish**, which is the <Helmholtz coil> condition. The central value and leading axial variation are
$$
\boxed{B_0=\left(\frac45\right)^{3/2}\frac{\mu_0NI}{a},\qquad \frac{B_{\rm ax}(z)}{B_0}=1-\frac{144}{125}\left(\frac za\right)^4+O((z/a)^6).}
$$

\Image[paper-1-helmholtz-profile.png]
{title=An original comparison of axial field uniformity for several coil separations}

Near the centre, the coils' <magnetic field lines> are nearly straight and parallel to the axis. On the axis rotational <symmetry> makes $B_x=B_y=0$ exactly; in the central plane the radial components from the two coils cancel. More quantitatively, in the current-free central volume, axisymmetry and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$ imply
$$
B_r(r,z)=-\frac r2B_{\rm ax}'(z)+O(r^3),\qquad B_z(r,z)=B_{\rm ax}(z)-\frac{r^2}{4}B_{\rm ax}''(z)+O(r^4).
$$
The suppressed axial derivatives therefore also suppress transverse <magnetic fields> near the centre. The smallness applies locally compared with the coil radius; it is not a claim of an exactly uniform field throughout the apparatus.

In a fixed point-mass <Newtonian gravitational field>, a test <mass> $m$ experiences
$$
\mathbf F=-\frac{GMm}{r^2}\widehat{\mathbf r}.
$$
Because this is a <central force>, its <torque> about the attracting centre vanishes and <angular momentum> is conserved. The orbit lies in a fixed plane and sweeps out equal areas in equal times. Since the <force> is also conservative, the specific orbital <energy> is constant:
$$
\varepsilon=\frac12\dot r^2+\frac{j^2}{2r^2}-\frac{GM}{r},\qquad j=r^2\dot\varphi.
$$
The last two terms form an <effective potential>. They show how the angular-momentum barrier competes with attraction and determines the turning radii. For nonradial motion the orbit is a <conic section>:
$$
\boxed{r=\frac{p}{1+e\cos(\varphi-\varphi_0)},\qquad p=\frac{j^2}{GM},\qquad e^2=1+\frac{2\varepsilon j^2}{G^2M^2}.}
$$
Negative <energy> gives an <ellipse>, with $e<1$; zero <energy> gives a <parabola>; positive <energy> gives a <hyperbola>. The circular limit has $e=0$, $v_c=\sqrt{GM/r}$ and $\varepsilon=-GM/(2r)$. For a bound <ellipse> of semimajor axis $a$, $\varepsilon=-GM/(2a)$ and the <orbital period> is $T=2\pi\sqrt{a^3/(GM)}$. The local <escape speed> is $\sqrt{2GM/r}$. These are the point-mass, test-particle limits; extended mass distributions or noncentral perturbations change the orbit laws.

An <electric dipole> consists of opposite <electric charges> separated by a short displacement. Its <electric dipole moment> is $\mathbf p=q\mathbf d$, directed from negative to positive <electric charge>. At distances large compared with the separation, its leading <electric potential> and <electric field> in vacuum are
$$
\boxed{V(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mathbf p\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r}}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^2},\qquad \mathbf E(\mathbf r)=\frac{3(\mathbf p\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})\widehat{\mathbf r}-\mathbf p}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^3}.}
$$
The absence of net <electric charge> removes the monopole term; the dipole <electric potential> therefore falls as $r^{-2}$ rather than $r^{-1}$. On its axis the <electric field> is parallel to $\mathbf p$ with magnitude $2p/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^3)$; on the equatorial plane it points oppositely with half that magnitude.

In a uniform applied <electric field>, the two <forces> cancel as a net translation but form a couple. The <torque> and <potential energy> are
$$
\boxed{\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf p\times\mathbf E,\qquad U=-\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E.}
$$
Alignment parallel to the <electric field> is stable. A nonuniform <electric field> can also exert a net <force>, $\mathbf F=\nabla(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E)$ for a fixed permanent <electric dipole moment>. An induced <electric dipole> instead depends on the applied <electric field>, so its <energy> must account for the work needed to create it.

<Electromagnetic induction> is the production of an <electromotive force> by changing <magnetic flux> or by moving a conductor through a <magnetic field>. With the loop and surface orientations related by the <right-hand rule>, <Faraday's law of induction> is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E=\oint(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=-\frac{d}{dt}\int\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf S.}
$$
Here $\mathbf v$ is the local wire velocity. For a stationary loop, the induced <electric field> can have a nonzero closed-path integral; its differential form is $\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial\mathbf B/\partial t$. For a straight rod moving perpendicularly to itself and to a uniform <magnetic field>, the motional <electromotive force> has magnitude $B\ell v$.

The minus sign expresses <Lenz's law>: the induced <electric current> produces a <magnetic field> that opposes the change in linked <magnetic flux>. Pulling a conducting loop through a <magnetic field> therefore requires work against an electromagnetic resisting <force>; that work supplies the resulting heat or stored <energy>.

For an <inductor> with constant self-<inductance>, $\mathcal E=-L\,dI/dt$ and its stored <magnetic energy> is $LI^2/2$. A changing <electric current> in one coil can induce an <electromotive force> in another through <mutual inductance>, the operating principle of a <electrical transformer>. These examples connect the flux law, the direction of the response and <conservation of energy>.

The <Lorentz force> determines the motion of a <charged particle>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\mathbf p}{dt}=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B).}
$$
At nonrelativistic <speeds>, $\mathbf p=m\mathbf v$. The <electric field> does work at rate $q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v$, while the magnetic part of the <Lorentz force> is perpendicular to <velocity> and does no work. A uniform <electric field> gives constant <acceleration> $q\mathbf E/m$; with a transverse initial <velocity>, the trajectory is a <parabola>.

In a uniform <magnetic field>, the parallel <velocity> is constant, while the perpendicular component undergoes <circular motion>. The angular <cyclotron frequency>, orbit radius and helical pitch are
$$
\boxed{\Omega_c=\frac{|q|B}{m},\qquad r_L=\frac{mv_\perp}{|q|B},\qquad p_{\rm helix}=\frac{2\pi v_\parallel}{\Omega_c}.}
$$
The sign of $q$ determines the sense of rotation. For perpendicular uniform <electric fields> and <magnetic fields>, the guiding centre has the <electric drift> velocity
$$
\boxed{\mathbf v_D=\frac{\mathbf E\times\mathbf B}{B^2}.}
$$
Indeed, $\mathbf E+\mathbf v_D\times\mathbf B=0$, so the remaining motion is cyclotron rotation about that drifting centre, with possible motion along the <magnetic field>. The drift is independent of <electric charge> sign and <mass>. At relativistic <energies>, retain $\mathbf p=\Gamma m\mathbf v$ and the corresponding relativistic orbit radius rather than assuming constant nonrelativistic <acceleration>.
