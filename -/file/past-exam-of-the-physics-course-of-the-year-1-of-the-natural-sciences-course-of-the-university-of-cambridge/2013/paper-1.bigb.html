= Solution

For a <photon>, $E=pc$ and $p=h/\lambda$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{E_\gamma=\frac{hc}{\lambda}=2.48\times10^4\,\mathrm{eV}=24.8\,\mathrm{keV}.}
$$

= b
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <de Broglie wavelength> fixes the same <momentum> $p=h/\lambda$, but the nonrelativistic <electron> has $K=p^2/(2m_e)$:
$$
\boxed{K_e=\frac{h^2}{2m_e\lambda^2}=6.02\times10^2\,\mathrm{eV}.}
$$
Here $K_e/(m_ec^2)\simeq0.00118$, consistent with the stated nonrelativistic approximation. The <photon energy> and <electron> <kinetic energy> are not interchangeable.

= Solution
{parent=2}

For equal mean <mass densities>, $M_a/M_E=(r/R_E)^3$. Thus the asteroid's surface gravitational <acceleration> is $g_a=g_Er/R_E$ and its <escape speed> is
$$
v_{\rm esc}^2=\frac{2GM_a}{r}=\frac{2g_Er^2}{R_E},\qquad
\boxed{r_{\max}=v_J\sqrt{\frac{R_E}{2g_E}}.}
$$
An explicit estimate of jumping ability is an <Earth> jump height $H=0.50\,\mathrm m$, giving $v_J=\sqrt{2g_EH}\simeq3.1\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{r_{\max}=\sqrt{R_EH}\simeq1.8\,\mathrm{km}.}
$$
This is an order-of-magnitude estimate, scaling with assumed take-off <speed>; no unique human jump <speed> is supplied. A spherical nonrotating asteroid and negligible atmosphere are assumed.

= Solution
{parent=3}

Convert both radii to metres before evaluating the <moment of inertia>:
$$
I=\tfrac12m(a^2+b^2)=1.1770375\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}.
$$
Its linear differential is
$$
dI=\tfrac12(a^2+b^2)\,dm+ma\,da+mb\,db.
$$
For <independent> <standard uncertainties>, add the three contributions in quadrature:
$$
\sigma_I=\sqrt{[\tfrac12(a^2+b^2)\sigma_m]^2+(ma\sigma_a)^2+(mb\sigma_b)^2}
=2.81\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2},
\qquad
\boxed{I=(1.177\pm0.028)\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}.}
$$
If the quoted errors are instead bounds, their first-order absolute contributions add, giving **$I\simeq(1.177\pm0.042)\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}$**. Their statistical interpretation is not specified; the two conventions should remain distinct.

{parent=Section A}

= Solution
{parent=4}

Spherical symmetry makes the <electric field> radial and constant in magnitude on each concentric <Gaussian surface>. The enclosed charge is
$$
Q(r)=4\pi\int_0^r(as)s^2\,ds=\pi ar^4.
$$
<Gauss's law> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(r)=\frac{ar^2}{4\epsilon_0}\,\widehat{\mathbf r},\qquad
|\mathbf E|=\frac{|a|r^2}{4\epsilon_0}.}
$$
The direction is outward for $a>0$ and inward for $a<0$; the field vanishes at the centre. No exterior <boundary condition> changes this spherically symmetric interior result.

= Solution
{parent=5}

Write $K\propto m^\alpha R^\beta h^\delta$. Matching dimensions to $ML^2T^{-2}$ gives
$$
\delta=2,\qquad\alpha=-1,\qquad\beta=-2,
\qquad K=C\frac{h^2}{mR^2}.
$$
The dimensionless factor $C$ cancels when the same confinement geometry and state are scaled. Hence
$$
K_p=K_e\frac{m_e}{m_p}\left(\frac{R_e}{R_p}\right)^2,
\qquad
\boxed{K_p\simeq8.3\,\mathrm{MeV}.}
$$
Using $m_p/m_e\simeq1836$ gives this value. It remains small compared with the <proton> <rest energy>, so the nonrelativistic mass-radius scaling is self-consistent.

= Solution
{parent=B7}

In the <centre-of-momentum frame> the two equal <masses> have opposite <velocities>. Conservation of <kinetic energy> in an elastic collision fixes their common <speed>, so scattering changes their directions only. For incoming laboratory <velocities> $\mathbf v$ and $\mathbf0$, the frame <velocity> is $\mathbf V=\mathbf v/2$ and the incoming relative <velocities> are $\pm\mathbf v/2$. If the outgoing relative <velocities> are $\pm\mathbf w$, then $|\mathbf w|=v/2$ and the laboratory <velocities> are $\mathbf V\pm\mathbf w$. Their dot product is zero: **nonzero outgoing laboratory <velocities> are perpendicular**.

For this <smooth equal-disc oblique collision>, let $\mathbf n$ point from A to B at contact. The <impulse> acts only along $\mathbf n$; the tangential <velocity> of A is unchanged. With $\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf n=v\cos\theta$, equal <masses> exchange their normal <velocity> components:
$$
\mathbf v_{Af}=\mathbf v-v\cos\theta\,\mathbf n,\qquad
\mathbf v_{Bf}=v\cos\theta\,\mathbf n.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{v_{Af}=v\sin\theta,\qquad\angle(\mathbf v,\mathbf v_{Af})=\frac\pi2-\theta.}
$$
No rotation about a disc centre is generated by these central <impulses>. At exactly head-on impact A stops, so its outgoing direction is undefined; the angle formula then denotes a limiting direction.

\Image[paper-1-ia2013-disc-velocities.png]
{title=Original velocity-vector construction for an equal-mass elastic collision, before and after impact in the laboratory and centre-of-momentum frames}

For this <adhesive collision of two equal discs>, use the <parallel axis theorem> about the midpoint of the touching centres:
$$
I_{\rm pair}=2(\tfrac12ma^2+ma^2)=3ma^2,\qquad
\mathbf L_{\rm CM}=(-a\mathbf n)\mathbin\times m\mathbf v.
$$
Its magnitude is $mav\sin\theta$. Linear and <angular momentum> conservation give
$$
\boxed{\mathbf V_{\rm CM}=\frac{\mathbf v}{2},\qquad
|\Omega|=\frac{v\sin\theta}{3a},\qquad
K_f=mv^2\left(\frac14+\frac{\sin^2\theta}{6}\right).}
$$
The rotation has the sign of $\mathbf L_{\rm CM}$, clockwise for the printed geometry. The <energy> loss is $mv^2(1/4-\sin^2\theta/6)>0$. After impact the midpoint translates uniformly and the rigid pair rotates uniformly about it: there are no external horizontal <forces> or axial <torques>.

Rough unglued edges can transmit a tangential <impulse> and generate <angular momentum> about the individual disc centres. The centre-of-mass <velocity> and total <angular momentum> still have the values above, but individual translational <speeds>, <angular velocities> and <energy> loss require a <friction>/contact law and normal restitution. **Roughness alone does not imply permanent sticking or the adhesive result.**

= Solution
{parent=B8}

Take inertial axes parallel, with frame $S'$ moving at $+v\widehat{\mathbf x}$ relative to $S$ and origins coincident at zero time. The <Lorentz factor> is $\gamma_v=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$; $\Delta x,\Delta y,\Delta t$ are separations of the same pair of events in $S$, and primed quantities are their separations in $S'$. The transverse coordinates are unchanged. For two events on an inertial clock's worldline,
$$
c^2\Delta\tau^2=c^2\Delta t^2-|\Delta\mathbf r|^2.
$$
The <proper time> $\Delta\tau$ is that clock's elapsed time; uniform <speed> $w$ gives $\Delta t=\gamma_w\Delta\tau$. Coordinate intervals between spatially separated events must instead be Lorentz-transformed with their spatial separation included.

For the round trip in the station frame, the rendezvous distance is $ut_{\rm out}$, and the return has the same <speed> and distance. Thus $t_{\rm round}=2t_{\rm out}=10a/(\sqrt7c)$. Its departure and arrival both occur at the station, so $\Delta x=0$ for these endpoints:
$$
\boxed{\Delta t_{S,\rm round}=\frac{10}{\sqrt7}\,\mathrm{days}=3.780\,\mathrm{days},\qquad
\Delta t_{B,\rm round}=\gamma_v\Delta t_{S,\rm round}
=\frac{25}{2\sqrt7}\,\mathrm{days}=4.725\,\mathrm{days}.}
$$
This uses the return <speed> as measured by the station. The elapsed time aboard A, apart from an ideal instantaneous turn, would be $\Delta t_{S,\rm round}/\gamma_u$.

For the crash, A's station-frame <velocity> components are $(3c/5,\sqrt7c/5)$, while B's are $(3c/5,0)$. Here $\gamma_u=5/3$ and $\gamma_v=5/4$. <Four-momentum conservation>, with no separately escaping radiation, gives the wreckage totals in the station frame:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf P_S=mc\left(\frac74\widehat{\mathbf x}+\frac{\sqrt7}{3}\widehat{\mathbf y}\right),\qquad
E_S=\frac{35}{12}mc^2.}
$$
In B's original <inertial frame> the same totals are
$$
\boxed{\mathbf P_B=\frac{\sqrt7}{3}mc\,\widehat{\mathbf y},\qquad E_B=\frac73mc^2.}
$$
The invariant wreckage <mass> is $m\sqrt{14/3}$, exceeding $2m$ because <internal energy> remains in the wreckage. Its <momentum> cannot be specified without naming a frame; both natural frames have been given.

= Solution

For a fixed rotation axis with constant <moment of inertia>, the axial <torque> obeys $\tau=I\ddot\theta$, with $\tau=\sum(\mathbf r\times\mathbf F)\cdot\widehat{\mathbf e}_{\rm axis}$. For a <point mass> at radius $l$, the tangential equation is $F_t=ml\ddot\theta$; multiplying by $l$ gives $lF_t=ml^2\ddot\theta$, as required. Radial <forces> have zero moment about the axis.

Take increasing $\theta$ in the direction of the clapper's initial fall and let the contact <force> $N$ oppose that tangential motion. Initial equilibrium of the clapper gives
$$
Nl=mgl\sin\beta,\qquad\boxed{N_{\rm initial}=mg\sin\beta.}
$$
While contact persists the bell and clapper rotate together. Their respective offsets from the upward vertical are $\theta-\beta$ and $\theta$. Summing external gravitational moments eliminates their internal contact <force>:
$$
\boxed{\ddot\theta=\frac{Mgh\sin(\theta-\beta)+mgl\sin\theta}{Mk^2+ml^2}.}
$$
The clapper alone satisfies $ml^2\ddot\theta=mgl\sin\theta-Nl$. Hence for $m\ll M$,
$$
\boxed{N\simeq mg\left[\sin\theta-\frac{lh}{k^2}\sin(\theta-\beta)\right].}
$$
A nonadhesive contact cannot exert tension. The first zero of this expression on the released trajectory is the loss-of-contact angle. Write $q=lh/k^2=0.9375$. Then
$$
(1-q\cos\beta)\sin\theta_0+q\sin\beta\cos\theta_0=0,
\qquad
\boxed{\theta_0=111.9^\circ.}
$$
The second-quadrant root is reached from $\theta=\beta=30^\circ$; selecting the principal negative arctangent would choose the wrong branch. Indeed the exact contact <force> before taking $m/M\to0$ is $mMg[k^2\sin\theta-lh\sin(\theta-\beta)]/(Mk^2+ml^2)$, so its zero is unchanged.

= Solution
{parent=C10}

Measure $x$ from the static equilibrium position, so the constant gravitational <force> has already cancelled the equilibrium <spring> <force>. With stationary oil, <Newton's second law> gives $m\ddot x=-b\dot x-kx$. The characteristic exponents are $-\gamma\pm i\omega_1$, where
$$
\boxed{\gamma=\frac{b}{2m},\qquad\omega_1=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}-\frac{b^2}{4m^2}},\qquad b^2<4mk.}
$$
For the stipulated <phase> of the damped cosine,
$$
U(t)=\frac12kA^2e^{-2\gamma t}\cos^2(\omega_1t),\qquad
K(t)=\frac12mA^2e^{-2\gamma t}[\gamma\cos(\omega_1t)+\omega_1\sin(\omega_1t)]^2.
$$
At $t_n=2\pi n/\omega_1$, the <velocity> is not zero: $\dot x=-\gamma Ae^{-\gamma t_n}$. Thus
$$
E_n=\tfrac12(k+m\gamma^2)A^2e^{-2\gamma t_n},\qquad
\boxed{\frac{E_{n+1}}{E_n}=e^{-4\pi\gamma/\omega_1},\quad
\frac{E_n-E_{n+1}}{E_n}=1-e^{-4\pi\gamma/\omega_1}.}
$$
Expanding $e^{-z}$ at small $z=4\pi\gamma/\omega_1$ gives the requested light-damping loss $z$. The exact instantaneous <energy> also satisfies $dE/dt=-b\dot x^2\leq0$.

From the static extension, $k=mg/(0.20\,\mathrm m)=44.145\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$; the instantaneous drag measurement gives $b=0.10\,\mathrm{N\,s\,m^{-1}}$. Consequently $\gamma=0.05556\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $\omega_1=7.003\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. Two successive downward equilibrium crossings are one damped period apart, so the same <energy> ratio applies. At the first crossing $E=mv^2/2=1.125\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm J$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Delta E=1.07\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm J,\qquad
v_{\rm next}=v e^{-2\pi\gamma/\omega_1}=0.04757\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The <speed> has **decreased by $2.43\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}=0.243\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$**. These values use the exact decay factor, not the first-order approximation.

= Solution
{parent=C11}

In series the same current passes through both <resistors>, while their <voltage> drops add: $V=I(R_1+R_2)$. In parallel the same <voltage> drives currents $V/R_1$ and $V/R_2$, which add. Thus $R_s=R_1+R_2$ and $R_p^{-1}=R_1^{-1}+R_2^{-1}$.

Use <phasors> proportional to $e^{i\omega t}$. Then $Z_L=i\omega L$ and $Z_C=1/(i\omega C)$. The printed diagram places C in parallel with the series RL branch, so the input admittance is
$$
Y=\frac1Z=i\omega C+\frac1{R+i\omega L},\qquad
\operatorname{Re}Y=\frac{R}{R^2+\omega^2L^2},\quad
\operatorname{Im}Y=\omega\left(C-\frac{L}{R^2+\omega^2L^2}\right).
$$
Its nonzero zero-phase <frequency> exists for $R^2<L/C$:
$$
\boxed{\omega_1=\sqrt{\frac1{LC}-\frac{R^2}{L^2}}.}
$$
At zero <frequency>, $|Y|=1/R$ and $I_0=V_0/R$. At $\omega_1$, $R^2+\omega_1^2L^2=L/C$, giving
$$
\boxed{|Y(\omega_1)|=\frac{RC}{L},\qquad I_0(\omega_1)=\frac{V_0RC}{L}.}
$$
For $0<\omega<\omega_1$, $\operatorname{Im}Y<0$, so current lags <voltage>. At very high <frequency>, $Y\sim i\omega C$, so current leads by $\pi/2$. Zero <phase> need not coincide exactly with the minimum current magnitude in this lossy network.

The three measured limits determine the components in order:
$$
R=\frac{V_0}{I_{\rm DC}}=100\,\Omega,\qquad
C=\frac{\alpha}{V_0}=2.50\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm F,\qquad
L=\frac{V_0RC}{I_0(\omega_1)}=0.100\,\mathrm H,
$$
$$
\boxed{\omega_1=6.245\times10^3\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
For the changed resistance, the quoted $0.6\,\mathrm{kHz}$ is an ordinary <frequency>, so $\omega'=2\pi(600\,\mathrm{s^{-1}})$. Keeping L and C fixed gives
$$
\boxed{R'=\sqrt{\frac LC-\omega'^2L^2}=5.08\times10^2\,\Omega.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=D12}

Across a uniformly illuminated slit, the <phase difference> between its edges is $2\pi a\sin\theta/\lambda$. Integrating the complex <amplitude> across the slit gives the single-slit <diffraction> factor
$$
\frac{I(\theta)}{I(0)}=\left[\frac{\sin q}{q}\right]^2,\qquad q=\frac{\pi a\sin\theta}{\lambda}\simeq\frac{\pi a\theta}{\lambda}.
$$
Thus $\beta=\pi a/\lambda$ in the small-angle <amplitude> expression. The first noncentral zero has $q=\pi$, so $\theta_0\simeq\lambda/a$. At the stated approximate subsidiary maximum,
$$
\boxed{\frac{I(1.5\lambda/a)}{I(0)}\simeq\frac4{9\pi^2}=0.0450.}
$$
The exact first side maximum solves $\tan q=q$, giving $q\simeq4.493$ and <intensity> ratio $0.0472$; the requested approximation need not be mistaken for the exact maximum.

A <virtual object> is a point toward which incident rays are already converging and would meet if the lens were removed. With real-is-positive object/image distances it has $u<0$. The thin-lens equation gives $1/v=1/f-1/u$; as $u\to-\infty$, **$v\to f$**, so a distant <virtual object> images in the rear focal plane.

Without the lens, $y\simeq L\theta$ puts the central maximum at zero and the first dark fringes at $y=\pm L\lambda_0/a=\pm0.100\,\mathrm m$. Subsequent zeros are integer multiples, and weaker subsidiary maxima lie between them.

For the finite-distance pattern compression, take the old screen as the prospective image plane at $L=6\,\mathrm m$, with $u=-L$, and the new screen at $d=0.10\,\mathrm m$. Then
$$
\frac1f=\frac1d-\frac1L,\qquad
f=\frac{Ld}{L-d}=0.101695\,\mathrm m,\qquad
\boxed{R=(n-1)f=0.0610\,\mathrm m.}
$$
The plane surface has infinite <radius of curvature>. The transverse scale is $d/L=1/60$, yielding
$$
\boxed{y_{0,\rm new}=\frac dL y_{0,\rm old}=\frac{d\lambda_0}{a}=1.67\,\mathrm{mm}.}
$$
The finite-distance scaling can also be checked without treating an <intensity> pattern as a collection of geometrical point objects: the slit-to-screen Fresnel integral after a <thin lens> has quadratic coefficient $1/d-1/f=1/L$ and linear term $-y/d$. It therefore reproduces the old <diffraction> pattern at transverse coordinate $Y=Ly/d$, up to an overall <amplitude> and <phase>. If the old far-field plane is treated as infinitely distant, the equally useful leading approximation is $f=d$, $R=6.0\,\mathrm{cm}$; the finite-distance correction is only $1.7\%$.

= Solution

Substitution into the <wave equation> gives $\partial_x^2\psi+\partial_y^2\psi=-(k_x^2+k_y^2)\psi$ and $\partial_t^2\psi=-\omega^2\psi$. Therefore $\omega^2=c^2(k_x^2+k_y^2)$. The fixed edges at zero are automatically satisfied by the sine factors; the opposite edges require
$$
k_x=\frac{p\pi}{a},\quad k_y=\frac{q\pi}{b},\quad p,q=1,2,\ldots,\qquad
\boxed{f_{pq}=\frac c2\sqrt{\frac{p^2}{a^2}+\frac{q^2}{b^2}}.}
$$
Neither integer may vanish, because that would make the entire displacement zero.

The element's <mass> is $\rho\,dx\,dy$, and its <speed> is $-\omega A\sin(k_xx)\sin(k_yy)\sin(\omega t)$. Squaring and multiplying by half its <mass> gives the requested <energy> <density>. The two sine-squared spatial integrals are $a/2$ and $b/2$, so
$$
\boxed{K(t)=\frac{\rho ab\omega^2A^2}{8}\sin^2(\omega t),\qquad
\overline K=\frac{\rho ab\omega^2A^2}{16}.}
$$
There is no <dissipation>. The total <mechanical energy> equals the maximum <kinetic energy>, giving
$$
\boxed{U(t)=\frac{\rho ab\omega^2A^2}{8}\cos^2(\omega t).}
$$
Equivalently, integrate the small-slope tension <energy> $\rho c^2|\nabla\psi|^2/2$ and use the <dispersion relation>.

For $b=4a/5$, the two lowest <modes> are $(p,q)=(1,1)$ and $(2,1)$:
$$
\boxed{f_{11}=\frac{\sqrt{41}\,c}{8a},\qquad
f_{21}=\frac{\sqrt{89}\,c}{8a}.}
$$
The fundamental has one <antinode> at $(a/2,b/2)$ and no interior nodal line. The next <mode> has a <node> along $x=a/2$ and opposite-sign <antinodes> at $(a/4,b/2)$ and $(3a/4,b/2)$. Every boundary is a <node>.

\Image[paper-1-ia2013-membrane-modes.png]
{title=Original displacement maps for the two lowest modes of a rectangular membrane, showing nodal lines and signed antinodes}

The square fundamental has $f=c\sqrt2/(2\beta a)$. Equating it to $f_{11}$ gives
$$
\boxed{\beta=\sqrt{\frac{32}{41}}=0.88345.}
$$
At the same peak displacement <amplitude> and <frequency>, time-averaged <kinetic energy> is proportional to membrane area. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{\overline K_{\rm square}}{\overline K_{\rm rectangle}}
=\frac{\beta^2a^2}{a(0.8a)}=\frac{40}{41}.}
$$
The reciprocal ratio is $41/40$ if the rectangle is placed first.

= Solution
{parent=E14}

In <magnetostatics> <Ampère's circuital law> is $\oint\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf l=\mu_0I_{\rm enc}$. Its general vacuum version adds $\mu_0\epsilon_0\,d\Phi_E/dt$ on the right. <Faraday's law> is $\mathcal E=\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=-d\Phi_B/dt$ for a fixed loop. A circular Amperian path around a long straight wire gives
$$
\boxed{B(r)=\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r},}
$$
with direction given by the right-hand rule. The <force> on a wire element is $d\mathbf F=I\,d\mathbf l\times\mathbf B$; its magnitude is $I B\,dl\sin\chi$ and its direction follows this cross product.

Choose the straight-wire current rightward and the square below it, as printed. Its positive <magnetic field> there is into the page. With an into-page loop normal, the <magnetic flux linkage> is
$$
\Lambda=N\int_s^{s+a}\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}a\,dr=\mathcal M I,
\qquad
\boxed{\mathcal M=\frac{\mu_0Na}{2\pi}\ln\frac{s+a}{s}.}
$$
Here $\mathcal M$ denotes <mutual inductance>, not a <mass>. Choose positive coil current to produce an out-of-page field. Then its induced current in the resistance-dominated approximation is
$$
\boxed{i(t)=\frac{\mathcal M}{R}\frac{dI}{dt}
=\frac{\mu_0\omega NaI_0}{2\pi R}\ln\frac{s+a}{s}\cos(\omega t).}
$$
This reproduces the specified sign with the declared orientation. It neglects coil <self-inductance>, requiring $\omega L_{\rm coil}\ll R$, as implicit in the requested expression.

The nearest side contains N parallel current segments and is at a uniform distance $s$, so its <force> magnitude is
$$
\boxed{F_{\rm near}=\frac{\mu_0Na}{2\pi s}|I(t)i(t)|.}
$$
The two sides perpendicular to the wire have opposite currents and equal <magnetic fields> at corresponding distances; their <forces> cancel pairwise. The far side opposes the near-side <force> and has the smaller field. Thus
$$
\boxed{F_{\rm total}=\frac{\mu_0Na}{2\pi}|I(t)i(t)|
\left(\frac1s-\frac1{s+a}\right).}
$$
When $I$ and $i$ have the same sign under the chosen conventions, the near-side coil current opposes the straight-wire current and the total <force> is away from the wire; otherwise it is toward it. In particular the signed <force> alternates with $\sin(\omega t)\cos(\omega t)$. The coil is fixed, so these <forces> do no mechanical work on it. The mean <Joule heating> is
$$
\boxed{\overline P=R\overline{i^2}=\frac{\mathcal M^2\omega^2I_0^2}{2R}.}
$$

A <gravitational field> is <force> per unit test <mass>, $\mathbf g=\mathbf F/m$. <Newton's law of universal gravitation> gives the field of a <point mass> M:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf g=-\frac{GM}{r^2}\widehat{\mathbf r},\qquad
\Phi=-\frac{GM}{r},\qquad\mathbf g=-\nabla\Phi.}
$$
The potential is <potential energy> per unit <mass>, with zero at infinity for an isolated <mass>. Fields and potentials superpose. The potential difference fixes the work per unit <mass>, and a conservative <gravitational field> has zero circulation around a closed path.

For a continuous <mass distribution>, the gravitational Gauss law is $\oint\mathbf g\cdot d\mathbf S=-4\pi GM_{\rm enc}$. A spherical body therefore has the same exterior field as a <point mass> at its centre. Inside a uniform sphere of <density> $\rho$,
$$
\mathbf g=-\frac{4\pi G\rho}{3}\mathbf r.
$$
The field is continuous at its surface and vanishes at its centre. An empty spherical shell has zero interior field but a nonzero constant interior potential. Near a small region of <Earth>'s surface one may approximate $\mathbf g$ as uniform and the potential change as $g\Delta h$. Farther away its spatial <gradient> produces tidal relative <accelerations>. Escape to infinity from radius R requires <speed> $\sqrt{2GM/R}$ when rotation and other bodies are neglected.

The <Lorentz force> determines the trajectory:
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\mathbf p}{dt}=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B),\qquad
\frac{dE}{dt}=q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v.}
$$
Thus a <magnetic field> changes <momentum> direction but does no work; an <electric field> can change the particle's <kinetic energy>. In a uniform <electric field> with negligible <magnetic field> and nonrelativistic <speed>, $\mathbf a=q\mathbf E/m$ is constant. A transverse initial <velocity> then gives a <parabola>.

In a uniform <magnetic field>, the parallel <velocity> is unchanged and the perpendicular motion is circular. Nonrelativistically,
$$
r_L=\frac{mv_\perp}{|q|B},\qquad\omega_c=\frac{|q|B}{m};
$$
the combined trajectory is a helix, with circulation reversed when the charge sign reverses. Relativistically $r_L=p_\perp/(|q|B)$ and the gyrofrequency is $|q|B/(\gamma m)$.

For perpendicular uniform fields in the magnetic-dominated case, the drift <velocity> is $\mathbf v_d=\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/B^2$, which obeys $\mathbf E+\mathbf v_d\times\mathbf B=0$. A particle at this <velocity> is undeflected: the crossed-field <velocity selector> selects <speed> $E/B$ in the appropriate direction, <independent> of <mass> and charge. Radiation reaction and collisions are neglected in these ideal trajectories.

In vacuum the electric and <magnetic field> <energy> <densities> are
$$
\boxed{u_E=\frac{\epsilon_0E^2}{2},\qquad u_B=\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}.}
$$
For example, charging a <capacitor> requires $U=\int_0^Q(q/C)\,dq=Q^2/(2C)=CV^2/2$. A <parallel-plate capacitor> with $C=\epsilon_0A/d$ and $E=V/d$ then gives $U=\epsilon_0E^2Ad/2$, identifying the <electric field> <energy> <density>. Similarly an <inductor> stores $LI^2/2$; applying this to a long <solenoid> yields $B^2/(2\mu_0)$ times its interior <volume>.

Electromagnetic <energy> is transported by the <Poynting vector> $\mathbf S=\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/\mu_0$. Poynting's theorem,
$$
\frac{\partial(u_E+u_B)}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot\mathbf S=-\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E,
$$
expresses local <energy> conservation and transfer to matter. In a vacuum <plane wave> $E=cB$, so the two <energy> <densities> are equal and the <energy> <flux> has magnitude $c(u_E+u_B)$. For nondispersive linear media the corresponding reversible expressions are $\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf D/2$ and $\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf H/2$; they must not be applied unchanged to arbitrary dispersive or hysteretic materials.
