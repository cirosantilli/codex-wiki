= Solution

Take a representative tennis-ball <mass> of $m\simeq0.06\,\mathrm{kg}$. The <speed> is nonrelativistic, so the <de Broglie wavelength> is
$$
\boxed{\lambda=\frac hp\simeq\frac h{mv}=\frac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{0.06\times10}\simeq1.1\times10^{-33}\,\mathrm m.}
$$
This is an estimate because the <mass> was not specified. The <wavelength> is vastly smaller than any practicable aperture or obstacle separation: a <diffraction> angle of order $\lambda/d$ is unresolvably small for laboratory-scale $d$. Moreover, interactions with air, thermal radiation and the apparatus destroy observable coherence between macroscopically distinct paths. <Quantum mechanics> still applies; the small <wavelength> and rapid <decoherence> explain why ordinary experiments reveal classical trajectories rather than a measurable <interference> pattern.

Write $X=1/(\omega C)=200\,\Omega$. The magnitude of the <series RC impedance> is
$$
\boxed{Z_m=\sqrt{R^2+X^2}=\sqrt{100^2+200^2}=223.61\,\Omega.}
$$
For independent small errors interpreted as <standard uncertainties>, <propagation of uncertainty> gives
$$
u(Z_m)^2=\left(\frac R{Z_m}u_R\right)^2+\left(\frac{X^2}{Z_m}\frac{u_\omega}{\omega}\right)^2+\left(\frac{X^2}{Z_m}\frac{u_C}{C}\right)^2.
$$
The input uncertainties are $u_R=2\,\Omega$, $u_\omega/\omega=0.01$, and $u_C/C=0.01$. Thus $u(Z_m)^2=0.80+3.20+3.20=7.20\,\Omega^2$, and
$$
\boxed{|Z|=(223.6\pm2.7)\,\Omega\quad\text{with independent standard uncertainties}.}
$$
The fractional uncertainty is $1.20\%$. If the stated errors are instead worst-case bounds, their first-order contributions add in magnitude, giving approximately $4.47\,\Omega$, or $2.0\%$, rather than the quadrature result. Both conventions require the error interpretation to be specified.

For uniform Earth density, the <shell theorem> says that only <mass> within radius $r$ contributes to the <gravitational field> there. Since enclosed <mass> is proportional to $r^3$,
$$
g(r)=\frac{GM(r)}{r^2}=g(R)\frac rR.
$$
At depth $h$, $g_h/g_0=1-h/R$. A <simple pendulum> has small-<amplitude> period $P=2\pi\sqrt{l/g}$, so the <pendulum clock below a uniform-density surface> runs at the fraction $P_0/P_h=\sqrt{1-h/R}$ of its surface-calibrated rate. After a surface-clock hour, its lag is
$$
\boxed{\Delta t=3600\left[1-\sqrt{1-\frac{1000}{6.371\times10^6}}\right]\,\mathrm s\simeq0.283\,\mathrm s.}
$$
The mine clock is slower because gravity is weaker under the stated uniform-density assumption. The expansion $\Delta t\simeq3600h/(2R)$ gives the same result. This is a change in the mechanical pendulum period, not a relativistic clock effect; a realistic Earth density profile would require a different interior-gravity calculation.

Use distinct symbols for the dimensions of <mass>, length, time, temperature and amount of substance. The <molar gas constant> has dimensions $\mathsf M\mathsf L^2\mathsf T^{-2}\mathsf\Theta^{-1}\mathsf N^{-1}$, while the given $M$ is a <molar mass> with dimensions $\mathsf M\mathsf N^{-1}$. Matching dimensions in the proposed power law gives
$$
\alpha+\gamma=0,\qquad 2\alpha+\delta=0,\qquad -2\alpha=-1,\qquad -\alpha+\beta=0,
$$
with the amount-of-substance equation repeating $\alpha+\gamma=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac12,\quad\beta=\frac12,\quad\gamma=-\frac12,\quad\delta=-1,\qquad f\propto\frac1L\sqrt{\frac{RT}{M}}.}
$$
<Dimensional analysis> fixes these exponents but not a dimensionless multiplier. For example, an open-tube mode number and the gas's heat-capacity ratio enter the actual <resonance> <frequency> without changing the dimensions.

The original diagram has a horizontal bottle neck, so weight does not enter the axial <force> balance. Gauge pressure acts on the cork's end area and supplies outward <force> $F_g=\pi a^2P_g$. The radial contact <force> is the contact pressure times the cylindrical contact area, $N=2\pi a(l-x)P_c$. The limiting friction is $\mu N$, so outward sliding occurs when
$$
\pi a^2P_g>2\pi a(l-x)\mu P_c,\qquad \boxed{P_g>\frac{2(l-x)\mu P_c}{a}.}
$$
The assumption of equal static and <kinetic friction> coefficients removes a separate <force> drop when slipping starts.

Initially the pressure is marginal at $x=0$, so $\pi a^2P_g=2\pi al\mu P_c$. During extraction the contact length decreases, while the pressure <force> stays constant. The net <force> is therefore $F(x)=\pi a^2P_gx/l$. The cork's <mass> is $m_c=\rho\pi a^2l$, giving the <pressure-driven cork ejection> equation
$$
\boxed{\ddot x=b^2x,\qquad b^2=\frac{P_g}{\rho l^2}.}
$$
For $x(0)=0$ and $\dot x(0)=v_0>0$, solving the constant-coefficient equation gives
$$
\boxed{x(t)=c\sinh(bt),\qquad b=\frac1l\sqrt{\frac{P_g}{\rho}},\qquad c=\frac{v_0}{b}=v_0l\sqrt{\frac\rho{P_g}}.}
$$
The equilibrium is unstable: as the cork moves out, friction decreases and the unbalanced <force> grows.

Multiplying the equation of motion by $\dot x$ and integrating gives $\dot x^2=v_0^2+b^2x^2$. The contact ends at $x=l$, so
$$
\boxed{v_{\mathrm{exit}}^2=v_0^2+\frac{P_g}{\rho},\qquad v_{\mathrm{exit}}\longrightarrow\sqrt{\frac{P_g}{\rho}}\quad(v_0\to0^+).}
$$
Equivalently, the net <work> is $\int_0^lF(x)dx=\pi a^2P_gl/2$. Exactly zero initial <velocity> would leave the ideal marginal configuration stationary. The exit time $b^{-1}\operatorname{arsinh}(bl/v_0)$ diverges as $v_0\to0^+$, even though the limiting exit <speed> is finite.

The density is $500\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, so $v_{\mathrm{exit}}\simeq\sqrt{400000/500}=28.3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. For an ideal upward launch at this <speed>, <conservation of mechanical energy> gives height above the exit
$$
\boxed{h=\frac{v_{\mathrm{exit}}^2}{2g}=\frac{P_g}{2\rho g}\simeq40.8\,\mathrm m\approx41\,\mathrm m.}
$$
This neglects air resistance and the small gravitational <work> during extraction compared with the launch <energy>. Actual gas pressure need not stay constant, so it is an idealized estimate.

Adding a stopper that doubles the moving <mass>, while preserving the same pressured area and contact friction, leaves the extraction <work> unchanged. The squared exit <speed> and the ballistic height are therefore halved:
$$
\boxed{v_{\mathrm{stopper}}=\frac{v_{\mathrm{exit}}}{\sqrt2},\qquad h_{\mathrm{stopper}}=\frac h2\simeq20.4\,\mathrm m.}
$$

For particles in an <inertial frame>, total <momentum> and <angular momentum> about a fixed origin are
$$
\boxed{\mathbf P=\sum_i m_i\mathbf v_i,\qquad \mathbf L_O=\sum_i\mathbf r_i\times m_i\mathbf v_i.}
$$
The <momentum> derivative is the total external <force> when internal forces cancel pairwise. <Angular momentum> is conserved when the total external <torque> vanishes and the internal torques sum to zero, as for pairwise equal-and-opposite central forces. Equal-and-opposite internal forces alone need not cancel internal <torque> if their lines of action are different. An isolated system with these internal-<force> properties conserves both quantities; if fields carry <momentum>, the complete system must include them.

For a straight rocket, let its instantaneous remaining <mass> be $M$ and let $dM<0$ be ejected during a <velocity> increase $dv$. The exhaust's inertial <velocity> is $v-v_e$ to first order. <Conservation of momentum> gives
$$
Mv=(M+dM)(v+dv)+(-dM)(v-v_e),\qquad M\,dv=-v_e\,dM.
$$
Integrating from initial <mass> $m+m_f$ to dry <mass> $m$ yields the <rocket equation>
$$
\boxed{v=v_e\log\frac{m+m_f}{m}.}
$$
It is the rocket-plus-exhaust system that is isolated; the rocket alone is a variable-<mass> open system.

For the firework, the original diagram places one tangentially firing point rocket at the end of each radial rod. The <parallel axis theorem> changes each rod's central inertia to $ma^2/12+m(a/2)^2=ma^2/3$. Adding all rods and fueled rockets gives
$$
\boxed{I_i=3\left[\frac{ma^2}{3}+(m+m_f)a^2\right]=(4m+3m_f)a^2.}
$$
When fuel is exhausted, $I_f=4ma^2$.

Let $M_f$ now denote the total remaining fuel in all three rockets, so $I=4ma^2+a^2M_f$. Fuel at the rim co-rotates before ejection. Its backward tangential exhaust <velocity> is $a\omega-v_e$, and its <angular momentum> per unit <mass> about the axle is $a(a\omega-v_e)$. For an infinitesimal ejection,
$$
I\omega=(I+dI)(\omega+d\omega)+(-dM_f)a(a\omega-v_e).
$$
Since $dI=a^2dM_f$, the co-rotation terms cancel and leave $I\,d\omega=-av_e\,dM_f$. The <tangential-exhaust rotor with a fixed dry inertia> therefore obeys
$$
\omega_f=\frac{v_e}{a}\log\frac{I_i}{I_f},\qquad \boxed{\omega_f=\frac{v_e}{a}\log\beta,\quad \beta=\frac{4m+3m_f}{4m}=1+\frac{3m_f}{4m}.}
$$
Neglecting the exhaust's initial tangential motion would give an incorrect angular-<momentum> balance. The rod <mass> remains in the denominator throughout the burn.

After the burn, the opposing constant axle <torque> $G>0$ acts on the fixed inertia $I_f$. Its <work> over the remaining turn equals the initial <rotational kinetic energy>:
$$
G\Delta\theta=\frac12I_f\omega_f^2,\qquad \boxed{\Delta\theta=\frac{2ma^2\omega_f^2}{G}=\frac{2mv_e^2}{G}(\log\beta)^2.}
$$

Use the PDF's notation $f_0$ for the source-frame pulse <frequency>, and set $\beta=v/c$, $\gamma=(1-\beta^2)^{-1/2}$. Successive emissions occur at the same train-frame position and are separated by <proper time> $1/f_0$. The <Lorentz transformation> gives a station-frame emission interval $\Delta t_e=\gamma/f_0$ and displacement $v\Delta t_e$. For a receding beacon, the second pulse travels an extra distance $v\Delta t_e$, so
$$
\Delta t_r=\Delta t_e(1+\beta),\qquad \boxed{\frac f{f_0}=\frac1{\gamma(1+\beta)}=\sqrt{\frac{1-\beta}{1+\beta}}.}
$$
This derivation includes both <time dilation> and the change in light-travel time. Expanding for $\beta\ll1$ gives $f/f_0=1-\beta+O(\beta^2)$, recovering the stated nonrelativistic approximation.

Let $\lambda_0$ be the common <wavelength> of the identical lights at rest. The approaching and receding <relativistic Doppler effect> factors give
$$
\lambda_1=\lambda_0\sqrt{\frac{1-\beta}{1+\beta}},\qquad \lambda_2=\lambda_0\sqrt{\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}}.
$$
Their ratio and product therefore determine the <speed> and rest <wavelength> separately:
$$
\boxed{\beta=\frac{\lambda_2-\lambda_1}{\lambda_2+\lambda_1}=\frac{636-530}{636+530}=\frac1{11},\qquad v=0.0909c,}
$$
$$
\boxed{\lambda_0=\sqrt{\lambda_1\lambda_2}=580.6\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
No assumption about the rest color was needed.

The last observation requires a transverse-geometry convention. The general observer-frame ray formula is
$$
\boxed{\lambda=\gamma\lambda'_3(1-\beta\cos\theta),}
$$
where $\theta$ is the angle between source <velocity> and photon propagation **in the platform frame at emission**. Under the usual transverse-ray interpretation, $\theta=\pi/2$, so for $\beta=0.6$ and $\gamma=1.25$,
$$
\boxed{\lambda=\gamma\lambda'_3=500\,\mathrm{nm}\quad\text{for a platform-frame transverse ray}.}
$$
This is the standard transverse redshift from <time dilation>.

There is a genuine distinction between emission and reception at the instant of passing. A side-mounted source emitting perpendicularly in its own frame instead has $\cos\theta=\beta$ by <aberration of light>, giving $\lambda=\lambda'_3/\gamma=320\,\mathrm{nm}$. The same blue result occurs if “at the exact moment” means reception when the source's current position is at closest approach, with a small finite transverse offset. For offset $d$, reception and emission satisfy $t_r=t_e+\sqrt{v^2t_e^2+d^2}/c$. Setting $t_r=0$ gives $t_e=-\gamma d/c$ and $\cos\theta=\beta$; setting $t_e=0$ instead gives the transverse ray received at $t_r=d/c$. These limits remain different as $d\to0$.

\Image[2017-doppler-geometry.png]
{title=Emission at closest approach and reception at closest approach give different Doppler factors}

Thus **500 nm requires the transverse ray in the platform frame**; **320 nm applies to reception at closest approach or transverse emission in the train frame**. The wording does not distinguish these event/frame choices, so merely neglecting the source-observer separation is insufficient to select between them. The reusable derivation is <transverse Doppler shift and observation time>.

Expand the cloud's <potential energy> about a <stable equilibrium>: $U(z)=U(0)+U'(0)z+U''(0)z^2/2+\cdots$. Equilibrium gives $U'(0)=0$, stability gives $U''(0)=k>0$, and reflection symmetry also removes odd terms. For small displacements, $U-U(0)\simeq kz^2/2$ and $F=-dU/dz\simeq-kz$.

Including the electric and damping forces, <Newton's second law> is $m\ddot z=qE(t)-b\dot z-kz$. Dividing by $m$ gives the <Lorentz oscillator model>
$$
\boxed{\ddot z+2\gamma\dot z+\omega_0^2z=\frac qmE(t),\qquad \omega_0=\sqrt{\frac km},\quad \gamma=\frac b{2m}.}
$$
The specified convention uses $2\gamma$ as the damping coefficient per unit <mass>.

For the steady response, represent the field by $E_0e^{i\omega t}$ and the displacement by $\widetilde z e^{i\omega t}$, taking real parts for physical quantities. Substitution gives
$$
(\omega_0^2-\omega^2+2i\gamma\omega)\widetilde z=\frac{qE_0}{m},\qquad \widetilde z=\frac{qE_0/m}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2+2i\gamma\omega}.
$$
Define the nonnegative <amplitude> $z_0=|\widetilde z|$ and $\delta=\operatorname{atan2}(2\gamma\omega,\omega_0^2-\omega^2)$. Then
$$
\boxed{z_0=\frac{|q|E_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+4\gamma^2\omega^2}},\qquad \phi=\arg q-\delta\pmod{2\pi}.}
$$
For positive $q$, $\phi=-\delta$. For electrons $q<0$, the <phase of a negatively charged driven oscillator> is $\phi=\pi-\delta$: the low-<frequency> displacement is opposite to the field, rather than in <phase> with it. A signed <amplitude> could absorb this extra $\pi$, but the <phase> convention must then be stated. The induced dipole $p=qz$ has <phase> $-\delta$ for either charge sign. Transient free oscillations have been omitted because the question asks for steady state.

For the electron cloud, $q/m=-e/m_e$ and
$$
\omega_0=\sqrt{\frac{k/n}{m_e}}=\sqrt{\frac{100}{9.109\times10^{-31}}}=1.05\times10^{16}\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}.
$$
The visible range corresponds to $\omega=2\pi c/\lambda$ between $2.35\times10^{15}$ and $4.71\times10^{15}\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$, below this <resonance>. The resonant <wavelength> is about $180\,\mathrm{nm}$, in the ultraviolet.

The <acceleration> <amplitude> is $\omega^2z_0$. At fixed incident field <amplitude>, the scattered intensity is therefore proportional to
$$
\boxed{I_{\mathrm{sc}}(\omega)\propto\frac{\omega^4}{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+4\gamma^2\omega^2}.}
$$
Well below <resonance> and outside strong damping corrections, $z_0$ is nearly <frequency>-independent and this becomes the <Rayleigh scattering wavelength dependence> $I_{\mathrm{sc}}\propto\omega^4\propto\lambda^{-4}$. The shortest visible wavelengths are consequently scattered much more strongly into directions away from the direct solar beam than the longest ones, producing a blue sky. The simple fourth-power approximation gives a factor 16 between 400 and 800 nm at equal incident intensity. Retaining the undamped denominator with the given <resonance> raises that factor to about 22.7, since the upper end is not extremely far below <resonance>. The exact ratio needs the unspecified damping and incident solar spectrum; the stronger scattering of the blue end is the robust conclusion here.

{title=Bound-charge response below an ultraviolet resonance favors short visible wavelengths}

The model describes <frequency>-dependent scattering, not the detailed perceived color after solar-spectrum weighting and human visual response. It also explains why treating the electrons as free at visible frequencies would miss the bound-charge fourth-power limit.

Use the diagram's downward displacement $z$, measured from the unloaded plate position, and take $z(0)=0$. The <mechanical energy>, with gravitational potential zero at that reference, is
$$
\boxed{E=\frac12m\dot z^2+\frac12kz^2-mgz.}
$$
The loaded equilibrium follows from $kz_{\mathrm{eq}}=mg$, so its downward shift is $\boxed{z_{\mathrm{eq}}=mg/k}$.

The shock absorber's <force> is $-b\dot z$, hence its power is <force> times <velocity>: $dE/dt=-b\dot z^2\leq0$. Differentiating the <energy> gives $\dot z(m\ddot z+kz-mg)=-b\dot z^2$, which yields, with continuity through instants of zero <velocity>,
$$
\boxed{m\ddot z+b\dot z+kz=mg.}
$$
Set $\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}$ and $\gamma=b/(2m)$.

For release from rest, the three <step response of a damped oscillator> regimes are easy to compare after shifting to $z-z_{\mathrm{eq}}$. With light damping $0<\gamma<\omega_0$, write $\omega_d=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}$:
$$
\frac z{z_{\mathrm{eq}}}=1-e^{-\gamma t}\left(\cos\omega_dt+\frac\gamma{\omega_d}\sin\omega_dt\right).
$$
It overshoots and oscillates around the loaded position with a decaying envelope. At <critical damping>, $\gamma=\omega_0$, it becomes $z/z_{\mathrm{eq}}=1-(1+\gamma t)e^{-\gamma t}$, approaching monotonically from below. For heavy damping $\gamma>\omega_0$, set $\eta=\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}$ and replace the trigonometric bracket by $\cosh\eta t+(\gamma/\eta)\sinh\eta t$. This is also monotonic, but its slow exponential delays settling.

\Image[2017-damped-scale.png]
{title=Damping controls the scale's settling, while initial impact velocity can still cause overshoot}

<Critical damping> gives the fastest nonoscillatory settling from the unloaded rest condition, avoiding a repeatedly swinging reading without the sluggishness of <overdamping>. Its required coefficient is
$$
\boxed{b_{\mathrm{crit}}=2\sqrt{km}.}
$$
At <critical damping> the repeated characteristic root is $-\gamma$. Thus the general displacement under constant load is $(A+Bt)e^{-\gamma t}+C$. The equilibrium and initial conditions give $C=z_{\mathrm{eq}}$, $A=-z_{\mathrm{eq}}$ and $B-\gamma A=v_0$, hence
$$
\boxed{A=-\frac{mg}{k},\qquad B=v_0-\gamma\frac{mg}{k},\qquad C=\frac{mg}{k},\qquad \gamma=\sqrt{\frac km}.}
$$
Subtracting the final position gives $z-z_{\mathrm{eq}}=[-z_{\mathrm{eq}}+(v_0-\gamma z_{\mathrm{eq}})t]e^{-\gamma t}$. The exponential is positive and the bracket starts negative. It becomes positive at some $t>0$ if and only if its slope is positive. Therefore <critical-damping overshoot from initial velocity> requires
$$
\boxed{v_0>\gamma z_{\mathrm{eq}}=g\sqrt{\frac mk}=\frac g\gamma,}
$$
with $v_0$ positive downwards. At equality the scale still approaches from below. <Critical damping> prevents oscillatory modes, but does not guarantee a non-overshooting response to an arbitrarily large impact <velocity>.

= Solution

Here $V(x,y)$ is the <potential energy> and $E$ is the stationary <energy> <eigenvalue>. The <Schrödinger equation> is the <eigenvalue> equation for kinetic plus <potential energy>. Its boundary conditions select the allowed energies. For a normalized <wavefunction>, the <Born rule> assigns probability $|\psi(x,y)|^2dx\,dy$ to a small area, and $\int|\psi|^2dx\,dy=1$. A stationary state's full time dependence is $\psi(x,y)e^{-iEt/\hbar}$; its <probability density> is time-independent.

The infinite walls impose $\psi=0$ on all four boundaries. For a separated solution $\psi=X(x)Y(y)$ in the zero-potential interior,
$$
\frac{X''}{X}+\frac{Y''}{Y}=-\frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}.
$$
The first term depends only on $x$ and the second only on $y$, so each is constant, $-k_x^2$ and $-k_y^2$. Vanishing at $x=0$ and $y=0$ removes the cosine terms. Vanishing at the other boundaries gives
$$
\boxed{k_x=\frac{n_x\pi}{a},\qquad k_y=\frac{n_y\pi}{b},\qquad n_x,n_y=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
The normalized <two-dimensional infinite square well> <eigenbasis> and energies are
$$
\boxed{\psi_{n_xn_y}(x,y)=\frac2{\sqrt{ab}}\sin\frac{n_x\pi x}{a}\sin\frac{n_y\pi y}{b},\qquad E_{n_xn_y}=\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\frac{n_x^2}{a^2}+\frac{n_y^2}{b^2}\right).}
$$
Normalization follows from the two sine-squared integrals $a/2$ and $b/2$. Zero quantum numbers would give an identically zero function. These separated products form a basis, not every possible <wavefunction>: a general state is their <superposition>, and a degenerate <energy> eigenspace can have nonseparable stationary combinations of products with that same <energy>.

For $a=1\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $b=2\,\mathrm{nm}$, the ground state is $(1,1)$ and the nearest excitation is $(1,2)$, along the longer side. The next quantum along the shorter side costs more <energy>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\epsilon_{\min}=E_{12}-E_{11}=\frac{3\pi^2\hbar^2}{2m_eb^2}=4.52\times10^{-20}\,\mathrm J=0.282\,\mathrm{eV}.}
$$
This transition has nonzero dipole matrix element for an electric-field component along $y$. Light polarized strictly along $x$ would not drive it, so the minimum assumes an allowed <polarization> rather than an arbitrary fixed one.

For the requested degeneracy, keep $b>a$. The first two states are necessarily $(1,1)$ and $(1,2)$, since $E_{21}-E_{12}=3\pi^2\hbar^2(a^{-2}-b^{-2})/(2m)>0$. The third level is the smaller of $E_{21}$ and $E_{13}$: states with both indices at least two lie above $E_{21}$, and higher states on the $n_x=1$ sequence lie above $E_{13}$. Make these two candidates equal:
$$
\frac4{a^2}+\frac1{b^2}=\frac1{a^2}+\frac9{b^2},\qquad \boxed{b=\sqrt{\frac83}\,a=1.633\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
The <third-level degeneracy in a rectangular quantum box> then has lowest distinct energies $11/8$, $5/2$ and $35/8$ in units $\pi^2\hbar^2/(2m_ea^2)$, with multiplicities one, one and two. The third-level pair is exactly $(2,1)$ and $(1,3)$.

At the first two specified times, the displacement profiles are
$$
\boxed{y(x,0)=y_0\cos kx,\qquad y\left(x,\frac{\pi}{2\omega}\right)=y_0\sin kx.}
$$
The second curve is the first shifted right by $\pi/(2k)=\lambda/4$. More generally a point of fixed wave <phase> satisfies $\omega t-kx=\text{constant}$, so it moves along $x=(\omega/k)t+\text{constant}$. Hence the wave travels in the positive $x$ direction with
$$
\boxed{c=\frac\omega k.}
$$
This <phase velocity> is distinct from the local transverse <velocity> $\partial y/\partial t$ of a string element.

For the <force> derivation take a short segment between $x$ and $x+\Delta x$. Let the tangent angles to the positive $x$ axis be $\theta_L$ and $\theta_R$. The left <tension> pulls along the negative tangent and the right <tension> along the positive tangent. With small slopes and approximately constant <tension> magnitude $T$, the horizontal components cancel to leading order and
$$
F_y=T\sin\theta_R-T\sin\theta_L\simeq T[y_x(x+\Delta x)-y_x(x)]\simeq T\Delta x\,y_{xx}.
$$
The segment <mass> is $\rho\Delta x$, where $\rho$ is **<mass> per unit length**, not volume density. <Newton's second law> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\rho y_{tt}=Ty_{xx},\qquad y_{tt}=c^2y_{xx},\qquad c=\sqrt{\frac T\rho}.}
$$
The approximation neglects bending stiffness, damping and changes in <tension> caused by finite-<amplitude> stretching.

\Image[2017-string-waves.png]
{title=String-element forces, a right-moving sinusoid and two Gaussian-pulse initial conditions}

Converting the supplied density gives $\rho=0.020\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}}$, so
$$
c=\sqrt{\frac{1000}{0.020}}=223.6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},\qquad \boxed{f=\frac c\lambda=\frac{223.6}{0.5}=447\,\mathrm{Hz}.}
$$
For the pulse, define $g(x)=Ae^{-ax^2/2}$ with $a>0$. The given initial <velocity> is $-cg'(x)$, exactly the <velocity> associated with a right-moving displacement $g(x-ct)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{y(x,t)=A\exp\left[-\frac a2(x-ct)^2\right].}
$$
It moves right without changing shape in this nondispersive model. With the same initial displacement but zero initial transverse <velocity>, <D'Alembert's formula> instead gives the <Gaussian pulse splitting from zero initial velocity>
$$
\boxed{y(x,t)=\frac A2\left\{e^{-a(x-ct)^2/2}+e^{-a(x+ct)^2/2}\right\}.}
$$
The two half-<amplitude> pulses travel in opposite directions. Their initial velocities cancel, while their initial displacements add to the prescribed <Gaussian>. The two initial-value problems have different kinetic energies; splitting into half-height pulses does not contradict <energy> conservation. These forms assume a string long enough that reflections from its ends have not returned.

In <electrostatics>, <Gauss's law> is
$$
\boxed{\oint_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf A=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{enc}}}{\epsilon_0},\qquad \boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac\rho{\epsilon_0}.}
$$
The surface normal points outward. <Spherical symmetry> makes the field radial and constant in magnitude on a concentric <Gaussian> surface.

For a uniformly charged sphere, enclosed charge is $4\pi\rho_0r^3/3$ inside and $Q=4\pi\rho_0R^3/3$ outside. Thus the radial field is
$$
\boxed{E_r(r)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\rho_0r}{3\epsilon_0},&0\leq r\leq R,\\[4pt]\displaystyle\frac{\rho_0R^3}{3\epsilon_0r^2},&r\geq R.\end{cases}}
$$
Set potential zero at infinity. Outside, $V=Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r)$. Inside, integrate $dV/dr=-E_r$ from the known boundary value, obtaining
$$
\boxed{V(r)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\rho_0}{6\epsilon_0}(3R^2-r^2),&r\leq R,\\[4pt]\displaystyle\frac{\rho_0R^3}{3\epsilon_0r},&r\geq R.\end{cases}}
$$
The field rises linearly from zero to its maximum at $R$, then falls as $r^{-2}$. The potential falls quadratically from $\rho_0R^2/(2\epsilon_0)$ to its surface value and then as $r^{-1}$. Both are continuous at the surface; there is no separate sheet of surface charge.

\Image[2017-uniform-sphere.png]
{title=Radial field and potential of a uniformly charged solid sphere}

For the <electric field of a hydrogen ground-state electron cloud>, its enclosed charge is
$$
Q_e(r)=4\pi\int_0^r s^2\rho(s)\,ds=-\frac{4e}{a_0^3}\int_0^r s^2e^{-2s/a_0}\,ds.
$$
Writing $u=2s/a_0$ and using $\int u^2e^{-u}du=-e^{-u}(u^2+2u+2)$ gives
$$
Q_e(r)=-e\left[1-e^{-2r/a_0}\left(1+\frac{2r}{a_0}+\frac{2r^2}{a_0^2}\right)\right].
$$
The total electron-cloud charge tends to $-e$, as required. Dividing by $4\pi\epsilon_0r^2$ yields
$$
\boxed{E_{e,r}(r)=\frac e{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac{e^{-2r/a_0}-1}{r^2}+\frac{2e^{-2r/a_0}}{a_0r}+\frac{2e^{-2r/a_0}}{a_0^2}\right].}
$$
The expression is negative and therefore points inward. Its apparently singular terms cancel at small radius, leaving $E_{e,r}\simeq-er/(3\pi\epsilon_0a_0^3)$.

A distant external charge polarizes the neutral atom. Its <electric field> displaces the positive and negative charge distributions relative to one another, giving an induced dipole $\mathbf p=\alpha\mathbf E_{\mathrm{ext}}$ with static <electric polarizability> $\alpha>0$. The field is stronger on the nearer side, so the opposite-charge attraction exceeds the like-charge repulsion. This is <charge-induced dipole attraction>, not a net monopole charge on the atom.

Quantitatively, for external charge $Q=\pm e$ at separation $r$, the <polarization> <energy> is $U=-\alpha E_{\mathrm{ext}}^2/2$, hence
$$
\boxed{U(r)=-\frac{\alpha Q^2}{2(4\pi\epsilon_0)^2r^4},\qquad F_r=-\frac{dU}{dr}=-\frac{2\alpha Q^2}{(4\pi\epsilon_0)^2r^5}.}
$$
Here negative $F_r$ means decreasing separation. Reversing the external charge reverses the induced dipole but leaves this leading long-range <force> attractive and of the same magnitude. The factor one half accounts for the <energy> cost of inducing the dipole.

The supplied charge density describes the unperturbed atom. Adding its electron field to the nucleus field leaves $E_{\mathrm{atom}}=e e^{-2r/a_0}(r^{-2}+2/(a_0r)+2/a_0^2)/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$, an exponentially small residual because the cloud has an infinite tail. A completely frozen density would give only that tiny sign-dependent <force>. Allowing <polarization> is essential to obtain the dominant attractive power-law <force> for either sign of external charge when $r\gg a_0$.

A charge $q$ moving nonrelativistically in prescribed fields obeys the <Lorentz force law>
$$
\boxed{m\frac{d\mathbf v}{dt}=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B).}
$$
The magnetic part is perpendicular to <velocity>, so $d(mv^2/2)/dt=q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v$: the <electric field> can change <kinetic energy>, while the <magnetic field> alone changes direction without doing <work>.

In a uniform <electric field> with no <magnetic field>, <acceleration> is constant: $\mathbf v=\mathbf v_0+(q\mathbf E/m)t$ and $\mathbf r=\mathbf r_0+\mathbf v_0t+q\mathbf E t^2/(2m)$. The trajectory is a parabola when there is initial motion perpendicular to the field, and a line when motion is purely along it. Reversing the charge reverses the electric <acceleration>.

In a uniform <magnetic field> with no <electric field>, resolve <velocity> into parallel and perpendicular components. The parallel component is constant; the perpendicular component rotates at the <cyclotron frequency>
$$
\boxed{\omega_c=\frac{|q|B}{m},\qquad r_L=\frac{mv_\perp}{|q|B}.}
$$
The resulting path is a helix, or a circle if $v_\parallel=0$, with pitch $2\pi v_\parallel/\omega_c$. Charge sign determines its sense of rotation. These relations underlie magnetic bending and <mass>/charge analysis. At relativistic energies, use <momentum> $\mathbf p=\gamma m\mathbf v$; at fixed <energy> the <cyclotron frequency> becomes $|q|B/(\gamma m)$.

For uniform crossed fields $\mathbf E\perp\mathbf B$, the <velocity> $\mathbf v_D=\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/B^2$ satisfies $\mathbf E+\mathbf v_D\times\mathbf B=0$. The orbit is a gyromotion about a <guiding center> drifting with
$$
\boxed{\mathbf v_D=\frac{\mathbf E\times\mathbf B}{B^2},}
$$
independent of <mass> and charge sign. A <velocity selector> transmits particles with the appropriate direction and <speed> $E/B$ without deflection. If the <electric field> has a component along the <magnetic field>, that component accelerates the parallel motion instead. Nonuniform fields can add other drifts; the simple helix and uniform crossed-field expressions require the stated uniform-field assumptions.

A position-dependent <force> is a <conservative force> when its <work> depends only on endpoints. Equivalently, its <work> around any closed allowed path vanishes, and one can define a <potential energy> $U$ satisfying
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F=-\boldsymbol\nabla U,\qquad W_{A\to B}=U(A)-U(B).}
$$
The potential is defined up to an additive constant. A vanishing <curl>, $\boldsymbol\nabla\times\mathbf F=0$, is a local test; it guarantees a global single-valued potential on a <simply connected> region with suitable regularity. Holes or excluded singularities require checking the global path condition.

The <work-energy theorem> gives $dK/dt=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v$. For a static potential this implies $d(K+U)/dt=0$. If $U$ depends explicitly on time, then instead $d(K+U)/dt=\partial U/\partial t$ under that <force> alone. Friction and other nonconservative forces add their own power and can convert <mechanical energy> into heat.

Examples include the <gravitational potential energy> $-GMm/r$ outside a spherical <mass> and the electrostatic <energy> $U=qV$, where $\mathbf E=-\boldsymbol\nabla V$. For a point source charge $Q$, $U=qQ/(4\pi\epsilon_0r)$, giving attraction or repulsion through the sign of $qQ$. A spring has $U=kx^2/2$ and <force> $-kx$. An equilibrium has $\boldsymbol\nabla U=0$; a strict local minimum is stable against small displacements when the quadratic curvature is positive. Expanding about that minimum explains the harmonic restoring <force> used in many small-oscillation models.

Magnetic <force> on a moving point charge does no <work>, but it is <velocity>-dependent and is not generally a position-only <force> $-\boldsymbol\nabla U$. Likewise a time-varying <magnetic flux> induces a circulating <electric field> with $\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=-d\Phi_B/dt$, so that <electric field> cannot globally be represented solely by an electrostatic scalar potential. These distinctions prevent treating every field <force> as conservative simply because an <energy> description exists for the complete system.

For steady currents in vacuum, the <Biot-Savart law> gives the <magnetic field> by summing oriented current elements. With $\mathbf R=\mathbf r-\mathbf r'$ from the source to the observation point,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\mathbf J(\mathbf r')\times\mathbf R}{R^3}\,d^3r'.}
$$
For a thin wire it becomes $\mathbf B=(\mu_0I/4\pi)\int d\mathbf l'\times\widehat{\mathbf R}/R^2$. The cross product fixes the direction by the right-hand rule. Fields from different steady circuits add by <superposition>; the entire current path, including return wires where relevant, belongs in the calculation.

An infinite straight wire gives circular field lines of magnitude $\boxed{B=\mu_0I/(2\pi s)}$ at perpendicular distance $s$. This follows by integrating the transverse component of each element along the wire. A circular loop of radius $a$ has cancelling transverse components on its axis, leaving
$$
\boxed{B_z(z)=\frac{\mu_0Ia^2}{2(a^2+z^2)^{3/2}},\qquad B_z(0)=\frac{\mu_0I}{2a}.}
$$
Multiple coincident turns multiply the field by their number. Far from the loop this is the field of a magnetic dipole of moment $\boldsymbol\mu=I\pi a^2\widehat{\mathbf n}$. Integrating over many loops gives the approximately uniform interior field $B\simeq\mu_0nI$ of a long <solenoid>, with $n$ turns per unit length. Finite coils have fringing fields, and appropriately spaced coil pairs can produce a more uniform measurement region.

The law is useful for predicting fields of wires and coils where direct symmetry is insufficient, designing electromagnets and estimating stray fields. It is a magnetostatic law: steady charge flow satisfies charge continuity. An instantaneous application to rapidly changing currents neglects propagation and radiation; such problems require the time-dependent <Maxwell's equations> and retarded fields. A quasistatic approximation can be valid when circuit dimensions are small compared with the light-travel distance during the variation timescale.

In SI units, the microscopic <Maxwell's equations> for total charge density $\rho$ and current density $\mathbf J$ are
$$
\boxed{\boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac\rho{\epsilon_0},\qquad \boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\qquad \boldsymbol\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},\qquad \boldsymbol\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}.}
$$
The first is <Gauss's law>: <electric charge> sources <electric flux>. The second is <Gauss's law for magnetism>, expressing the absence of magnetic monopole sources in this theory. <Faraday's law> relates changing <magnetic flux> to circulating <electric field>. The <Ampère-Maxwell law> relates magnetic circulation to both material current and changing <electric flux>.

For fixed loops and surfaces, the integral versions are
$$
\oint_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf A=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{enc}}}{\epsilon_0},\quad \oint_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf A=0,
$$
$$
\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=-\frac d{dt}\int_S\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf A,\quad \oint_{\partial S}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf l=\mu_0I_S+\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac d{dt}\int_S\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf A.
$$
Surface and loop orientations are related by the right-hand rule. These forms follow from the divergence theorem and Stokes' theorem. In material media, equivalent macroscopic equations can separate free and bound sources using $\mathbf D$ and $\mathbf H$ together with constitutive relations; the displayed forms use total sources.

The displacement-current term is essential for <charge conservation>. Taking the divergence of the last differential equation and using <Gauss's law> gives $\partial_t\rho+\boldsymbol\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0$. It also makes magnetic circulation consistent for surfaces spanning the same loop through either a charging capacitor's wire or its dielectric gap.

In source-free vacuum the <curl> equations combine to give $\nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t^2\mathbf E$, with the same equation for $\mathbf B$. Thus
$$
\boxed{c=\frac1{\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}}.}
$$
A plane wave is transverse, with $\mathbf E$, $\mathbf B$ and propagation direction mutually perpendicular and $E=cB$. The fields transport <energy> with density $u=\epsilon_0E^2/2+B^2/(2\mu_0)$ and <Poynting vector> $\mathbf S=\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/\mu_0$. Together with the <Lorentz force law>, the equations connect charges, currents, forces, induction and electromagnetic radiation.
