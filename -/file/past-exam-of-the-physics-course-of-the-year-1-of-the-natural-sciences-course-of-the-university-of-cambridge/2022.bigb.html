= Solution
{parent=1}

By <dimensional analysis>, <power> has dimensions $\mathsf{M L^2 T^{-3}}$, <mass density> has dimensions $\mathsf{M L^{-3}}$, and <angular velocity> has dimensions $\mathsf{T^{-1}}$; <radians> are dimensionless. Thus
$$
\mathsf{M L^2 T^{-3}}=\mathsf{M^\alpha L^{\gamma-3\alpha}T^{-\beta}}.
$$
Matching the three independent dimensions gives $\alpha=1$, $\beta=3$ and $\gamma-3\alpha=2$, hence
$$
\boxed{\alpha=1,\quad\beta=3,\quad\gamma=5,\qquad P\propto\rho\omega^3D^5.}
$$
With unchanged <mass density>, <angular velocity> and the same performance coefficient (a <dimensionless quantity>), the <pump power affinity law> gives
$$
\frac{P_{\rm new}}{P_{\rm old}}=\left(\frac{D_{\rm new}}{D_{\rm old}}\right)^5=1.1^5=1.61051.
$$
The required <power> therefore **increases by $61.1\%$**. This uses the stipulated dependence on only these three quantities, with comparable pump geometry and performance; <dimensional analysis> alone does not guarantee that unrelated pumps have the same performance coefficient.

= Solution

The <relativistic kinetic energy> is the total <energy> minus the <rest energy>:
$$
\boxed{K=(\gamma-1)Mc^2,\qquad\gamma=\frac1{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}.}
$$
Here $\gamma$ is the <Lorentz factor> and $c$ the <speed of light>. The <binomial series>, with $b=v/c$, gives
$$
(1-b^2)^{-1/2}=1+\frac12b^2+\frac38b^4+O(b^6),\qquad K=\frac12Mv^2+\frac38M\frac{v^4}{c^2}+O\!\left(M\frac{v^6}{c^4}\right).
$$
For $v\ll c$, the leading term is the classical <kinetic energy> $Mv^2/2$. The ratio of the fourth-order term to that leading term is $3v^2/(4c^2)$. Consequently
$$
\frac34\frac{v^2}{c^2}=0.01\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{\frac vc=\sqrt{\frac{0.04}{3}}=0.11547\simeq11.5\%.}
$$
The condition concerns one term of the <binomial series>; it is not the condition that the entire relativistic correction be exactly $1\%$.

Let $x$ be signed displacement along the rails, measured from the centre. The diagram in the original paper shows a guide extending across the centre, so the signed coordinate can pass through zero. <Hooke's law> gives the radial <force> $-kx$. In the <rotating reference frame>, the radial <centrifugal force> is $+m\Omega^2x$. The <Coriolis force> is perpendicular to the rails and is balanced by their reaction; it has no radial component. Therefore <Newton's second law> gives
$$
m\ddot x=-kx+m\Omega^2x,\qquad\ddot x+\left(\frac km-\Omega^2\right)x=0.
$$
Equivalently, the radial component of <acceleration in polar coordinates> in the <inertial frame> is $\ddot x-\Omega^2x$. The <radial spring oscillator on a rotating guide> has <simple harmonic motion> only when $k/m-\Omega^2>0$, with
$$
\boxed{\omega_{\rm osc}=\sqrt{\frac km-\Omega^2},\qquad f_{\rm osc}=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac km-\Omega^2}.}
$$
The <angular frequency> is in $\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$ and the <frequency> in $\mathrm{Hz}$. The critical <angular speed> is
$$
\boxed{\Omega_{\rm crit}=\sqrt{k/m},\qquad 0\le\Omega<\Omega_{\rm crit}.}
$$
Strictly this is an upper bound, not an attained maximum for <oscillations>: at equality $\ddot x=0$, so a displaced object released from rest stays at its displaced position relative to the rails; above it, the central <equilibrium> is unstable.

= Solution

Write $a=0.020\,\mathrm m$ for the side length. In an <equilateral triangle>, the distance from centre to a vertex is $a/\sqrt3$; it is two thirds of the altitude $\sqrt3a/2$. By the <superposition principle>, the <electric potential> at the centre due to the three fixed <electrons> is
$$
\Phi(0)=-\frac{3e}{4\pi\varepsilon_0(a/\sqrt3)}.
$$
Here $e>0$ is the <elementary charge> and $\varepsilon_0$ the <vacuum permittivity>. The approaching <electron> has <electric charge> $-e$, so its <potential energy>, measured relative to infinity, is positive:
$$
U(0)=(-e)\Phi(0)=\frac{3\sqrt3e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0a}.
$$
The fixed sources do not move, and radiation is excluded. Hence <conservation of mechanical energy> gives
$$
\frac12m_ev_\infty^2=U(0),\qquad\boxed{v_\infty=\sqrt{\frac{6\sqrt3e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0m_ea}}.}
$$
Using $e=1.60218\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$, $m_e=9.10938\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$ for the <electron mass>, and $(4\pi\varepsilon_0)^{-1}=8.98755\times10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,C^{-2}}$ gives
$$
U(0)=5.99\times10^{-26}\,\mathrm J=3.74\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{eV},\qquad\boxed{v_\infty\simeq3.63\times10^2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
This <speed> is small enough to justify classical <kinetic energy>. There is an idealization in the word “stops”: along the line normal to the triangle through its centre, this threshold <energy> makes the <electron> approach the central <equilibrium> asymptotically. A smooth, conservative trajectory cannot reach an <equilibrium> with exactly zero <velocity> in finite time unless it was already there. The energy threshold, and therefore the requested initial <speed>, is nevertheless unambiguous.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Choose three mutually perpendicular axes through the same point, and label coordinates $x,y,z$. The <moments of inertia> of any <rigid body> are
$$
I_x=\int(y^2+z^2)\,dm,\qquad I_y=\int(z^2+x^2)\,dm,\qquad I_z=\int(x^2+y^2)\,dm.
$$
Subtracting gives
$$
I_x+I_y-I_z=2\int z^2\,dm\ge0.
$$
For a thin <planar lamina> lying in $z=0$, the right-hand side vanishes, proving the <perpendicular axis theorem>:
$$
\boxed{I_z=I_x+I_y.}
$$
The two in-plane axes and the perpendicular axis must intersect at one point; this need not be the <centre of mass>. For an extended three-dimensional <mass distribution>, the equality generally fails. The general <moment-of-inertia triangle inequalities> are
$$
\boxed{I_x+I_y\ge I_z,\quad I_y+I_z\ge I_x,\quad I_z+I_x\ge I_y,}
$$
with equality in the first precisely when all <mass> lies in the plane perpendicular to its $z$ axis, and cyclically for the others. None of these identities requires <principal axes>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let $I_\perp$ denote the <moment of inertia> normal to the disc and $I_d$ that about any central diameter. <Rotational symmetry> makes the two perpendicular diameter values equal. By the <perpendicular axis theorem>, $I_\perp=2I_d$. The intended <angular impulse> has magnitude $J=I_\perp\omega_0$; delivering that magnitude about a diameter instead gives $J=I_d\omega$. Thus
$$
I_d\omega=2I_d\omega_0,\qquad\boxed{\omega=2\omega_0.}
$$
These are <principal axes>, so <angular momentum> is parallel to <angular velocity> in both cases. Equal delivered <angular impulse> means equal magnitude of the change in <angular momentum>, not equal rotational <kinetic energy>; the actual rotational <kinetic energy> is twice the intended value.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Take $+x$ along the upper ship's initial <velocity>, and take $+y$ from the lower ship to the upper ship at closest approach. The upper and lower ships then have initial <velocities> $v_1\widehat{\mathbf x}$ and $-v_2\widehat{\mathbf x}$. Their equal <masses> put each at distance $l/2$ from the <centre of mass>, so
$$
\boxed{I_{\rm CM}=2m(l/2)^2=\frac12ml^2.}
$$
By <momentum conservation>, the <centre of mass> keeps moving with constant <velocity>
$$
\boxed{\mathbf V_{\rm CM}=\frac{v_1-v_2}{2}\widehat{\mathbf x}.}
$$
In that frame the ships have opposite <velocities> of magnitude $u=(v_1+v_2)/2$. Their initial <angular momentum about the centre of mass> is nonzero even though their trajectories were straight:
$$
\mathbf L=-ml\frac{v_1+v_2}{2}\widehat{\mathbf z},\qquad L=\frac{ml(v_1+v_2)}2.
$$
The tether exerts central internal <forces>, so <conservation of angular momentum> gives
$$
\boxed{\omega=\frac L{I_{\rm CM}}=\frac{v_1+v_2}{l}.}
$$
Each ship follows uniform <circular motion> of radius $l/2$ around the translating <centre of mass>, clockwise viewed from $+z$. More explicitly, put the initial centre at the origin and define
$$
\mathbf n(t)=\sin(\omega t)\widehat{\mathbf x}+\cos(\omega t)\widehat{\mathbf y},\qquad\mathbf r_{1,2}(t)=\mathbf V_{\rm CM}t\pm\frac l2\mathbf n(t).
$$
The <kinetic-energy decomposition about the centre of mass> verifies <conservation of mechanical energy> directly:
$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{\rm after}&=\frac12(2m)V_{\rm CM}^2+\frac12I_{\rm CM}\omega^2\\
&=\frac m4(v_1-v_2)^2+\frac m4(v_1+v_2)^2\\
&=\boxed{\frac12mv_1^2+\frac12mv_2^2=E_{\rm before}.}
\end{aligned}
$$
At closest approach the relative radial <velocity> is already zero, so connecting the tether requires no dissipative radial impulse. Subsequently its <tension> is perpendicular to each ship's <velocity> relative to the <centre of mass> and does no net <work> on the pair at fixed $l$.

Each ship needs <centripetal acceleration> $\omega^2(l/2)$, supplied entirely by the <tension>. Thus

\boxed{T=m\omega^2\frac l2=\frac{m(v_1+v_2)^2}{2l}=\frac{2L^2}{ml^3}.}

The result has units of <force>, $\mathrm N$.

= e
{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=e}

During <quasi-static tether contraction>, <momentum conservation> keeps $\mathbf V_{\rm CM}$ constant and <conservation of angular momentum> keeps $L$ constant. To leading order in the slow radial motion, the <kinetic energy> is
$$
E=mV_{\rm CM}^2+\frac{L^2}{2I_{\rm CM}}=mV_{\rm CM}^2+\frac{L^2}{ml^2}.
$$
Its <derivative> at fixed $L$, not fixed $\omega$, is
$$
\boxed{\frac{dE}{dt}=-\frac{2L^2}{ml^3}\dot l>0\quad(\dot l<0).}
$$
To compute the <power> supplied by the <tension>, use the instantaneous unit vector $\mathbf n$ from the lower to the upper ship. Their radial <velocities> relative to the <centre of mass> are $\pm(\dot l/2)\mathbf n$, and the <tension> forces are $\mp T\mathbf n$. Consequently
$$
\dot W=(-T\mathbf n)\cdot\left(\frac{\dot l}{2}\mathbf n\right)+(T\mathbf n)\cdot\left(-\frac{\dot l}{2}\mathbf n\right)=-T\dot l=\boxed{-\frac{2L^2}{ml^3}\dot l=\frac{dE}{dt}.}
$$
The translational contributions cancel between the two ships and the tangential contributions are zero. The contraction mechanism supplies positive <work>, increasing the rotational <kinetic energy>.

The slow-contraction approximation matters: for a prescribed smooth $l(t)$ with a taut tether, the exact <kinetic energy> and <tension> include radial terms,
$$
E_{\rm exact}=mV_{\rm CM}^2+\frac{L^2}{ml^2}+\frac m4\dot l^2,\qquad T_{\rm exact}=\frac{2L^2}{ml^3}-\frac m2\ddot l.
$$
These still satisfy $dE_{\rm exact}/dt=-T_{\rm exact}\dot l$. Thus the paper's displayed formula is the leading result for slow, smooth contraction, or is exact when the radial <kinetic energy> is constant; it is not an identity for every possible shrinking schedule.

The <mass> and surface <area> of the spherical droplet are $m=(4\pi/3)\rho r^3$ and $A=4\pi r^2$. Constant surface <mass accretion rate> $\beta$ means
$$
\dot m=4\pi r^2\beta=4\pi\rho r^2\dot r.
$$
Therefore $\dot r=\beta/\rho=\mu$, and the initial radius gives
$$
\boxed{r(t)=r_0+\mu t,\qquad\mu=\frac\beta\rho.}
$$
Here $[\beta]=\mathrm{kg\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}}$, $[\rho]=\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, and $[\mu]=\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. This <surface-fed growth of an accreting sphere> law assumes constant droplet <mass density>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Choose downward <velocity> $v$ positive, and assume the collected material is initially at rest in the cloud's <inertial frame>. This assumption is required for the equation in the question. During $dt$, <momentum conservation> for the droplet plus the material it collects gives
$$
(m+dm)(v+dv)=mv+mg\,dt,
$$
up to second-order increments. Thus the <variable-mass system> satisfies
$$
m\dot v+v\dot m=mg,\qquad\dot v+\frac{\dot m}{m}v=g.
$$
Since $m\propto r^3$, $\dot m/m=3\dot r/r=3\mu/(r_0+\mu t)$, giving
$$
\boxed{\frac{dv}{dt}+\frac{3\mu}{r_0+\mu t}v=g.}
$$
The notation $dv/dt$ is appropriate because $v$ here depends only on <time>. The extra term accounts for the <momentum> needed to accelerate newly collected <mass>; using $m\dot v=mg$ would omit it. For incoming material with downward <velocity> $u$, the more general equation is $m\dot v=mg+(u-v)\dot m$.

= Solution

Set $s=r_0+\mu t$. The proposed <velocity> and its <derivative> are
$$
v=\frac{g}{4\mu}\left(s-\frac{r_0^4}{s^3}\right),\qquad\dot v=\frac g4\left(1+\frac{3r_0^4}{s^4}\right).
$$
The remaining term in the <linear ordinary differential equation> is
$$
\frac{3\mu}{s}v=\frac{3g}{4}\left(1-\frac{r_0^4}{s^4}\right),
$$
so the sum is exactly $g$. Also $v(0)=g(r_0-r_0)/(4\mu)=0$, establishing both the equation and its <initial condition>:
$$
\boxed{v(t)=\frac g{4\mu}\left[(r_0+\mu t)-\frac{r_0^4}{(r_0+\mu t)^3}\right].}
$$
The <integrating factor> $(r_0+\mu t)^3$ explains where the expression comes from:
$$
\frac d{dt}(s^3v)=gs^3,\qquad s^3v=\frac{g}{4\mu}(s^4-r_0^4).
$$
The result has units of <velocity>. Its continuous $\mu\to0$ limit is $gt$, despite the apparent $1/\mu$ in the written expression.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Introduce the <dimensionless quantities> $\tau=\mu t/r_0$ and $w=\mu v/(gr_0)$. Then
$$
w(\tau)=\frac14\left[(1+\tau)-(1+\tau)^{-3}\right].
$$
The <Taylor series> at $t=0$ gives
$$
\boxed{v(t)=gt-\frac{3g\mu}{2r_0}t^2+\frac{5g\mu^2}{2r_0^2}t^3+O(t^4),\qquad t\ll r_0/\mu.}
$$
Thus the initial slope is $g$, as for <free fall>. At late <times>,
$$
\boxed{v(t)=\frac{gt}{4}+\frac{gr_0}{4\mu}-\frac{gr_0^4}{4\mu(r_0+\mu t)^3}\sim\frac{gt}{4},\qquad t\gg r_0/\mu.}
$$
The oblique <asymptote> is $v=gt/4+gr_0/(4\mu)$, approached from below. Direct differentiation gives
$$
\dot v=\frac g4\left[1+3(1+\tau)^{-4}\right]>0,\qquad\ddot v=-\frac{3g\mu}{r_0}(1+\tau)^{-5}<0.
$$
The <speed> increases monotonically with decreasing slope, from $g$ towards $g/4$. It does not reach a <terminal velocity> in this drag-free model. Without <mass> accumulation, and still without <drag>, a drop released from rest has $v=gt$: its <acceleration> remains $g$, whereas the growing drop continually shares its <momentum> with newly acquired material.

= Solution
{parent=e}

An upward <drag force> $F_D\ge0$ changes the <variable-mass system> equation to
$$
\dot v+\frac{3\mu}{r_0+\mu t}v=g-\frac{F_D}{m}.
$$
For an ordinary <drag force> increasing with positive <speed>, the new curve lies below the no-drag curve. It still starts with slope $g$ if $F_D=0$ at rest. The dashed curve illustrates <quadratic drag on a sphere with constant radial growth>, using $F_D=\tfrac12 C_D\rho_{\rm air}\pi r^2v^2$ with constant <drag coefficient> $C_D$ and air <mass density> $\rho_{\rm air}$. The equation for these <dimensionless quantities> is
$$
\frac{dw}{d\tau}=1-\frac{3w}{1+\tau}-\eta\frac{w^2}{1+\tau},\qquad\eta=\frac{3C_D\rho_{\rm air}gr_0}{8\rho\mu^2}>0.
$$
The figure uses $\eta=2$ solely to show the shape; it is not an additional datum of the paper.

There is a limitation to the usual <terminal velocity> sketch here. The examiner's commentary describes a curve approaching <terminal velocity>, but the paper maintains unbounded radius growth and specifies no drag law. A horizontal limit is justified if one additionally treats the radius as approximately fixed, or otherwise assumes a drag-to-mass ratio with a fixed limiting balance. With the growing radius and constant-$C_D$ <quadratic drag> just written, the late balance instead gives
$$
\boxed{w\sim\sqrt{\tau/\eta},\qquad v\sim\sqrt{\frac{8\rho g\mu}{3C_D\rho_{\rm air}}\,t}.}
$$
This curve flattens in the sense that its slope tends to zero, but its <speed> remains unbounded. Thus **a lower, more strongly flattening curve is warranted; a finite terminal speed requires an additional assumption**. The no-growth approximation gives the familiar finite value $v_T=\sqrt{8\rho gr/(3C_D\rho_{\rm air})}$ for a specified fixed radius.https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/natural_science_tripos_physics_e/response/2127325/attach/3/FOI%202022%20639%20Sullivan%20materials.pdf?cookie_passthrough=1#page=62[Examiner commentary, B7, released PDF page 62: the stated terminal-velocity expectation]{ref}

For <electric potential difference> and <electric current> varying as <sinusoids>, choose the <phasor> convention
$$
V(t)=\operatorname{Re}(\widetilde V e^{i\omega t}),\qquad I(t)=\operatorname{Re}(\widetilde I e^{i\omega t}).
$$
The <electrical impedance> is
$$
\boxed{Z(\omega)=\frac{\widetilde V}{\widetilde I},\qquad [Z]=\Omega.}
$$
Its <modulus> gives the voltage-to-current <amplitude> ratio, and its <complex argument> gives the voltage-minus-current <phase difference>. For ideal components,
$$
\boxed{Z_R=R,\qquad Z_C=\frac1{i\omega C}=-\frac{i}{\omega C},\qquad Z_L=i\omega L.}
$$
These follow from <Ohm's law> $V=RI$, the <capacitor> law $I=C\,dV/dt$, and the <inductor> law $V=L\,dI/dt$. In <phasor> notation a <time derivative> becomes multiplication by $i\omega$, turning the <linear ordinary differential equations> of the <electrical circuit> into algebraic equations. <Electrical impedances> add in a <series circuit>; their reciprocals, the <electrical admittances>, add in a <parallel circuit>. This computes both <amplitudes> and <phase differences> in the steady response to <sinusoids>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The original diagram has a <series circuit> containing $R$ and $L$, in parallel with $C$; it is not three separate parallel components. The <electrical admittance> is therefore
$$
Y=\frac1Z=\frac1{R+i\omega L}+i\omega C=\frac R{R^2+\omega^2L^2}+i\left[\omega C-\frac{\omega L}{R^2+\omega^2L^2}\right].
$$
For $R>0$, voltage and current are in phase when the <imaginary part> of $Y$ vanishes. Excluding the zero-<frequency> solution, division by $\omega$ gives
$$
C=\frac L{R^2+\omega_r^2L^2},\qquad\boxed{\omega_r=\sqrt{\frac1{LC}-\frac{R^2}{L^2}}.}
$$
A nonzero <lossy parallel coil resonance> exists only if $L>R^2C$. With $R=10\,\Omega$, $L=0.150\,\mathrm H$ and $C=1.00\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm F$,
$$
\omega_r=\sqrt{666666.7-4444.44}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}=\boxed{813.77\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}},\qquad\boxed{f_r=\frac{\omega_r}{2\pi}=129.52\,\mathrm{Hz}.}
$$
For zero <electrical resistance>, the ideal parallel <inductor>-<capacitor> cancellation occurs at
$$
\boxed{\omega_0=\frac1{\sqrt{LC}}=816.50\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}},\qquad f_0=129.95\,\mathrm{Hz}.}
$$
The fractional reduction relative to the zero-resistance coil is
$$
\boxed{\frac{f_0-f_r}{f_0}=1-\sqrt{1-\frac{R^2C}{L}}=0.00333891=0.3339\%.}
$$
At exactly $R=0$ and $\omega=\omega_0$, $Y=0$, so finite applied voltage draws zero net current in the ideal model. The quoted <frequency> is the limiting parallel-resonance value; the phase of an exactly zero current is undefined.

= Solution
{parent=d}

For a long <solenoid>, <solenoid inductance> obeys $L=\mu_0N^2A/\ell$, where $N$ is the number of turns, $A$ the cross-sectional <area> and $\ell$ the axial winding length. The natural reading of half as many turns occupying half the axial length gives
$$
N'=N/2,\qquad\ell'=\ell/2,\qquad L'=L/2.
$$
With unchanged wire material and gauge, halving the wire length also halves its <electrical resistance>: $R'=R/2$. With the total <capacitance> $C_T$ fixed, the new <angular frequency> satisfies
$$
\omega'^2=\frac2{LC_T}-\frac{R^2}{L^2}=2\omega_*^2+\frac{R^2}{L^2}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\frac{f'}{50\,\mathrm{Hz}}=\sqrt{2+\left[\frac{R}{L\omega_*}\right]^2}=1.43005,\qquad f'=71.50\,\mathrm{Hz}.}
$$
Neglecting the small resistive correction gives the estimate $\sqrt2$.

There is a documented alternative interpretation: if half the turns are distributed over the original axial length, then $L'=L/4$ while $R'=R/2$, giving
$$
\omega'^2=\frac4{LC_T}-\frac{4R^2}{L^2}=4\omega_*^2,\qquad\boxed{f'=2f_*=100\,\mathrm{Hz}.}
$$
This corresponds to treating “length of the winding” as wire length while preserving axial geometry. The <time-independent Schrödinger equation> is the <energy eigenvalue> equation $\widehat H\psi=E\psi$ for a particle of <mass> $m$. The <Hamiltonian operator> is the sum of the kinetic operator $-(\hbar^2/2m)d^2/dx^2$ and the prescribed <potential energy> $V(x)$. Here $\hbar$ is the <reduced Planck constant>. A corresponding <stationary state> is $\Psi(x,t)=\psi(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar}$. The <Born rule> identifies $|\Psi|^2=|\psi|^2$ as <probability density> for a state with <wavefunction normalization> $\int|\Psi|^2dx=1$; integrating it over an interval gives a position <probability>. A <plane wave> scattering state is not a <normalizable wavefunction> on the whole line, but its <probability currents> give the reflection and transmission fractions of an incident <wave packet>.

There is a notation discrepancy in the sources: the original PDF prints an unbarred $h^2$ in the kinetic term, whereas the local TeX uses $\hbar^2$. The standard physical equation uses the <reduced Planck constant> $\hbar=h/(2\pi)$, where $h$ is the <Planck constant>; that convention is used below. Reading the printed $h$ literally as the <Planck constant> would scale all lengths in this model by $2\pi$ relative to the standard answers, but would leave the amplitude ratios unchanged.

For $E>V_0$, both sides allow oscillatory <wavefunctions>. A classical particle always crosses the <potential step> and slows because its <kinetic energy> falls from $E$ to $E-V_0$. In <quantum mechanics> there is generally both reflection and transmission even though the particle is above the step. Define the positive <wavenumbers>
$$
k=\frac{\sqrt{2mE}}\hbar,\qquad q=\frac{\sqrt{2m(E-V_0)}}\hbar.
$$
With no wave incident from the right, write
$$
\psi(x)=\begin{cases}Ae^{ikx}+Be^{-ikx},&x\le0,\\Ce^{iqx},&x>0.\end{cases}
$$
A finite <potential step> with constant <mass> makes both the <wavefunction> and its first <derivative> continuous. The two <boundary conditions> at $x=0$ are
$$
A+B=C,\qquad k(A-B)=qC.
$$
Solving gives the reflected and transmitted <wavefunctions>
$$
\boxed{\psi_{\rm ref}(x)=A\frac{k-q}{k+q}e^{-ikx},\qquad\psi_{\rm tr}(x)=A\frac{2k}{k+q}e^{iqx}.}
$$
For the specified <electron> energies, $q/k=\sqrt{10/50}=1/\sqrt5$, so the relative <probability amplitudes> are
$$
\boxed{\frac BA=\frac{\sqrt5-1}{\sqrt5+1}=0.381966,\qquad\frac CA=\frac{2\sqrt5}{\sqrt5+1}=1.381966.}
$$
The transmitted <probability amplitude> exceeding one is compatible with <conservation of quantum probability>, since the outgoing <speed> is smaller. In fact the <probability current> $j=(\hbar/m)\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\psi')$ gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal R=\left|\frac BA\right|^2=0.145898,\qquad\mathcal T=\frac qk\left|\frac CA\right|^2=0.854102,\qquad\mathcal R+\mathcal T=1.}
$$
These are the <quantum reflection probability> and <transmission probability>, which must be distinguished from the requested relative <amplitudes>.

Classically, a particle with $0<E<V_0$ is completely reflected and never enters the raised region. In <quantum mechanics> the <wavefunction> penetrates the classically forbidden side as an <evanescent wave>. Define the <tunnelling decay constant>
$$
\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}}\hbar.
$$
Boundedness at positive infinity excludes $e^{+\kappa x}$, so
$$
\psi(x)=\begin{cases}Ae^{ikx}+Be^{-ikx},&x\le0,\\Ce^{-\kappa x},&x>0.\end{cases}
$$
Matching the <wavefunction> and its <derivative> gives
$$
\frac BA=\frac{k-i\kappa}{k+i\kappa},\qquad\frac CA=\frac{2k}{k+i\kappa}.
$$
The reflected <probability amplitude> has unit <modulus>, hence <quantum reflection probability> one. The decaying tail carries zero <probability current>: this semi-infinite <potential step> has no transmitted beam at infinity, unlike a finite <potential barrier>. The sketch shows an exact matched example rather than attempting to assign a single real value to a complex <wavefunction>.

\Image[2022-q9-step-penetration.png]
{title=Real and imaginary components of a matched <wavefunction> at a <potential step>, with <exponential decay> of the <probability density>}

Because the <probability density> decays as $|C|^2e^{-2\kappa x}$, the requested $1/e$ penetration distance is
$$
\boxed{d=\frac1{2\kappa}=\frac{\hbar}{2\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}}.}
$$
For a <proton>, take $m_p=1.67262\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}$, $V_0-E=10\,\mathrm{MeV}=1.60218\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm J$ and $\hbar=1.05457\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$. Then
$$
\boxed{d=7.20\times10^{-16}\,\mathrm m=0.720\,\mathrm{fm}.}
$$
The <wavefunction> amplitude decay length $1/\kappa=1.44\,\mathrm{fm}$ is twice this value. The nonrelativistic equation supplied in the paper is appropriate for this estimate since $10\,\mathrm{MeV}$ is small compared with the <proton> <rest energy>, approximately $938\,\mathrm{MeV}$. If the original PDF's unbarred $h$ is instead read literally as the <Planck constant>, the displayed differential equation gives $d_{\rm literal}=2\pi d=4.53\,\mathrm{fm}$. This is a valid calculation for that literal coefficient, but $0.720\,\mathrm{fm}$ is the physical estimate using the standard <Schrödinger equation>.

A <scanning tunneling microscope> brings a tip and surface, both <electrical conductors>, close enough to leave only a small <vacuum> gap. An applied <electric potential difference> makes occupied <electron> states on one side able to feed unoccupied states on the other. Although the gap is a classically forbidden <potential barrier>, <quantum tunnelling> through its finite width produces an <electric current>.

For an approximate <rectangular potential barrier> of gap width $d$, the <transmission probability>, and thus the <electric current> at fixed small bias, varies approximately as $e^{-2\kappa d}$. This strong distance dependence makes the device sensitive to changes in separation on the scale of a <chemical atom>. <Feedback control> moves the tip vertically to keep the <electric current> constant during a scan; the required height changes form an image of the surface, with contrast also depending on its electronic states. The finite gap permits transmitted <probability current>, whereas the semi-infinite step in part (b) does not.

For an infinite straight wire in <vacuum>, <Ampère's circuital law> around a circle of radius $R$ gives $B(2\pi R)=\mu_0I$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B=\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi R}\widehat{\boldsymbol\phi}.}
$$
Here $\mu_0$ is the <vacuum permeability> and the azimuthal direction follows the <right-hand rule> around the <electric current>. The <magnetic field> is measured in $\mathrm T$.

{parent=10}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Choose the <electric current> counterclockwise as viewed from $+z$; reversing it reverses the <magnetic field>. Parameterize the circular wire by
$$
\mathbf r'(\phi)=R(\cos\phi,\sin\phi,0),\quad d\mathbf l=R(-\sin\phi,\cos\phi,0)\,d\phi,\quad\mathbf s=(0,0,z)-\mathbf r'.
$$
The <cross product> in the <Biot-Savart law> is
$$
d\mathbf l\times\mathbf s=(Rz\cos\phi,Rz\sin\phi,R^2)\,d\phi,\qquad |\mathbf s|=\sqrt{R^2+z^2}.
$$
Consequently the transverse terms integrate to zero over $0\le\phi\le2\pi$, while the axial term is constant:
$$
\mathbf B(0,0,z)=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\int_0^{2\pi}(Rz\cos\phi,Rz\sin\phi,R^2)\,d\phi.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B(0,0,z)=\frac{\mu_0IR^2}{2(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\widehat{\mathbf z}.}
$$
The direction is $+z$ on both halves of the axis for this <electric current> orientation, including $z<0$; it is not “away from the plane” on both sides. The <right-hand rule> gives the same direction.

Read the original diagram as a clockwise arc carrying <electric current> from its upper endpoint to its lower endpoint, with tangent semi-infinite leads extending to the right. Their crossing is insulated, so there is no extra junction. Choose $+z$ out of the page. At the centre the arc produces a negative axial <magnetic field>, and each straight lead a positive one, by the <right-hand rule>.

For the arc, every element is at distance $R$, with $d\mathbf l$ perpendicular to its displacement to the centre. The <Biot-Savart law> therefore gives
$$
B_{z,\rm arc}=-\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi R}\int_{-\theta/2}^{\theta/2}d\phi=-\frac{\mu_0I\theta}{4\pi R}.
$$
For each straight lead, write $s\ge0$ for distance along the tangent measured from its endpoint. Its perpendicular distance from the centre is $R$, so
$$
B_{z,\rm lead}=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\int_0^\infty\frac{R\,ds}{(R^2+s^2)^{3/2}}=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi R}.
$$
The integral equals $1/R$ because an <antiderivative> is $s/[R\sqrt{R^2+s^2}]$. Adding both leads and the arc,
$$
B_z(0)=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi R}(2-\theta),\qquad\boxed{\theta=2\,\mathrm{rad}\simeq114.6^\circ.}
$$
Using the full infinite-wire <magnetic field> for either lead would incorrectly double its contribution.

= Solution
{parent=d}

To calculate <magnetic-field cancellation of an arc and tangent leads> away from the plane, put $a^2=R^2+h^2$. Each point on the arc is distance $a$ from $(0,0,h)$, and the axial component of $d\mathbf l\times\mathbf s$ still has magnitude $R^2d\phi$. Thus its axial <magnetic field> is
$$
\boxed{B_{z,\rm arc}(h)=-\frac{\mu_0I\theta R^2}{4\pi(R^2+h^2)^{3/2}}.}
$$
For either tangent lead the axial numerator remains $R\,ds$, while the cubed distance is $(R^2+h^2+s^2)^{3/2}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{B_{z,\rm lead}(h)=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\int_0^\infty\frac{R\,ds}{(a^2+s^2)^{3/2}}=\frac{\mu_0IR}{4\pi(R^2+h^2)}.}
$$
Adding both leads gives
$$
\boxed{B_z(h)=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi R}\left[\frac2{1+(h/R)^2}-\frac{\theta}{[1+(h/R)^2]^{3/2}}\right].}
$$
For $\epsilon=h/R\ll1$, a <binomial series> yields
$$
B_z(h)=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi R}\left[(2-\theta)+\left(-2+\frac32\theta\right)\epsilon^2+O(\epsilon^4)\right].
$$
The exact condition for cancellation of the axial component is
$$
\theta(h)=2\sqrt{1+\epsilon^2},\qquad\boxed{\theta(h)=2+\frac{h^2}{R^2}+O\!\left(\frac{h^4}{R^4}\right)\quad\text{in radians}.}
$$
For example, keeping $\theta=2$ would leave a small positive axial <magnetic field> proportional to $h^2/R^2$. Both contributions weaken with height, but the arc factor is $(1+\epsilon^2)^{-3/2}$ whereas the lead factor is $(1+\epsilon^2)^{-1}$. The arc therefore loses a greater fraction of its axial <magnetic field>. Increasing its angle compensates for this loss. Physically, the arc is all at the same finite radius, whereas distant elements of the semi-infinite leads undergo a smaller fractional change in distance on moving a little out of the plane.

The cancellation here concerns $B_z$ only; off the plane it does not assert that every component of the <magnetic field> vanishes. The small-height result also stays within the tangent-lead geometry shown in the original paper.
