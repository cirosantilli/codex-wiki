= Solution

For a <simple pendulum> in the <small-angle approximation>, the <oscillation period> is $T=2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$, so
$$
g=\frac{4\pi^2L}{T^2}.
$$
Interpret the quoted random timing error and length error as <standard uncertainties>, with <independent> errors and no additional <systematic error>. For $N$ <independent> measurements, the <standard error> of the <sample mean> of the period is $\sigma_{\overline T}/T=0.02/\sqrt N$. The length has relative <standard uncertainty> $\sigma_L/L=0.1/25.0=0.004$. By <propagation of uncertainty>,
$$
\left(\frac{\sigma_g}{g}\right)^2=\left(\frac{\sigma_L}{L}\right)^2+4\left(\frac{\sigma_{\overline T}}{T}\right)^2=(0.004)^2+\frac{(0.04)^2}{N}.
$$
Requiring a relative <standard uncertainty> no greater than $0.01$ gives
$$
N\ge\frac{0.04^2}{0.01^2-0.004^2}=19.0476\ldots,\qquad\boxed{N_{\min}=20\text{ measurements}.}
$$
For $N=20$, the combined relative <standard uncertainty> is $0.009798$, or $0.980\%$; $N=19$ gives $1.001\%$ and just fails. Repeating the timing does not reduce the fixed length <measurement uncertainty>. The stated “accuracy” is consequently a statistical uncertainty target, not a guarantee for every experimental outcome. This is an example of <repeated-timing uncertainty in a pendulum gravity measurement>.

The original diagram shows two equal balls, each of <mass> $m$, attached to the fixed upper pivot and light sliding collar by four equal rods. Each ball describes a horizontal circle of radius $r=L\sin\theta$. The pivot-to-collar distance is $2L\cos\theta$, so the upward collar displacement compresses the <spring> by
$$
\delta=2L(1-\cos\theta).
$$
Thus <Hooke's law> gives a downward spring <force> $F_s=k\delta$ on the collar. Let $T_u,T_l$ be the <tensions> in the upper and lower rods attached to one ball. Vertical <force> balance on the light collar and then on either ball gives
$$
2T_l\cos\theta=F_s,\qquad T_u\cos\theta-T_l\cos\theta=mg.
$$
The radial <force> on one ball supplies its <centripetal acceleration>:
$$
(T_u+T_l)\sin\theta=m\omega^2L\sin\theta.
$$
For $\theta\ne0$, adding the vertical-balance results and eliminating the rod <tensions> gives
$$
\boxed{\omega^2=\frac{mg+F_s}{mL\cos\theta}=\frac{g}{L\cos\theta}+\frac{2k}{m}\frac{1-\cos\theta}{\cos\theta}.}
$$
For the given <centrifugal governor>, $\cos30^\circ=\sqrt3/2$ and
$$
\delta=0.0535898\,\mathrm m,\qquad F_s=26.7949\,\mathrm N,\qquad \omega^2=133.988\,\mathrm{s^{-2}}.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\omega=11.58\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\simeq110.5\text{ revolutions per minute}.}
$$
The factor of two in the collar balance matters: both lower rods support the same light collar. The spring force is not an extra downward force of this full magnitude on each ball; its effect on each ball is transmitted through its own lower rod. The formula is the steady <spring-loaded centrifugal governor> balance.

Let the <cylinder> length be $\ell=0.020\,\mathrm m$, cross-sectional <area> $A$, and the fluid <mass density> $\rho_f$. At <floating equilibrium>, <Archimedes' principle> gives
$$
mg=\rho_f gA\frac\ell2,\qquad m=\rho_f A\frac\ell2.
$$
For a small downward displacement $z$ from this <equilibrium>, the displaced fluid <volume> increases by $Az$. The additional upward <buoyant force> is $\rho_fgAz$, and <Newton's second law> becomes
$$
m\ddot z=-\rho_fgAz,\qquad\ddot z+\frac{2g}{\ell}z=0.
$$
This is <simple harmonic motion>, with <angular frequency> $\omega=\sqrt{2g/\ell}$ and <oscillation period>
$$
\boxed{T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac\ell{2g}}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{0.020\,\mathrm m}{2(9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}})}}=0.2006\,\mathrm s\simeq0.20\,\mathrm s.}
$$
The result for <vertical oscillations of a floating cylinder> is independent of its cross-sectional <area> and fluid <mass density> because those quantities cancel using the known immersion fraction. It neglects <drag>, surface effects and the extra fluid inertia from the motion; the <cylinder> must remain partly immersed throughout the small motion.

For a <diffraction grating> with $N$ illuminated lines in order $p$, its ideal <spectral resolving power> is
$$
\mathcal R=\frac\lambda{\Delta\lambda}=pN.
$$
The two <spectral lines> have separation $\Delta\lambda=3.4\,\mathrm{nm}$ and mean <wavelength> $\overline\lambda=395.1\,\mathrm{nm}$. In first order, $p=1$, so the <Rayleigh criterion> requires
$$
N\gtrsim\frac{395.1}{3.4}=116.2.
$$
With line density $500\,\mathrm{mm^{-1}}$, the ideal continuous estimate for the illuminated width is
$$
W\simeq\frac{116.2}{500\,\mathrm{mm^{-1}}}=\boxed{0.2324\,\mathrm{mm}\simeq0.23\,\mathrm{mm}.}
$$
Rounding the required number of illuminated grating lines upward gives **$117$ lines**, conventionally an illuminated width $W\simeq117/(500\,\mathrm{mm^{-1}})=\boxed{0.234\,\mathrm{mm}}$. Using the longer <wavelength> rather than the mean also gives the same integer requirement. This is the ideal limit set by the <resolving power of a diffraction grating>; additional broadening from the illumination or observing apparatus could require a larger width.

The tunnel is taken thin enough that removal of its material does not significantly disturb spherical <symmetry>. For $a\le r\le2a$, the enclosed <mass> is
$$
M(r)=\frac{4\pi\rho}{3}(r^3-a^3).
$$
By the <spherical shell theorem>, the inward <gravitational acceleration> has magnitude
$$
g(r)=\frac{GM(r)}{r^2}=\frac{4\pi G\rho}{3}\left(r-\frac{a^3}{r^2}\right).
$$
The released <gravitational potential energy> is the positive <work> done by gravity while the <mass> falls from radius $2a$ to $a$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
U_A-U_B&=m\int_a^{2a}g(r)\,dr\\
&=\frac{4\pi G\rho m}{3}\left[\frac{r^2}{2}+\frac{a^3}{r}\right]_a^{2a}\\
&=\boxed{\frac{4\pi G\rho ma^2}{3}.}
\end{aligned}
$$
By <conservation of mechanical energy>,
$$
\frac12mv_B^2=U_A-U_B,\qquad\boxed{v_B=a\sqrt{\frac{8\pi G\rho}{3}}.}
$$
Inside the cavity, the <spherical shell theorem> gives zero <gravitational field>, so the <velocity> stays constant. The original figure places B and C at opposite ends of a cavity diameter, a distance $2a$ apart. Therefore
$$
\boxed{t_{BC}=\frac{2a}{v_B}=\sqrt{\frac3{2\pi G\rho}}.}
$$
The quantity $G\rho$ has units $\mathrm{s^{-2}}$, so this is a <time>. There are no numerical values for $a$ or $\rho$ in the paper. The transit <time> is independent of $a$ and the falling <mass> $m$. The cavity's <gravitational potential> is constant, not necessarily zero; only its <gradient> vanishes. The calculation is a <hollow-sphere gravitational transit>.

**Translation and rotation.** For particles constituting a <rigid body>, the total <linear momentum> is
$$
\mathbf P=\sum_i m_i\mathbf v_i=M\mathbf V_{\rm CM},\qquad\frac{d\mathbf P}{dt}=\sum_i\mathbf F_{i,\rm ext}.
$$
Thus the total external <force> governs the motion of the <centre of mass>. About that <centre of mass>, the <angular momentum> and applied <torque> are
$$
\mathbf L_{\rm CM}=\sum_i\mathbf r_i'\times m_i\mathbf v_i',\qquad\mathbf G_{\rm CM}=\sum_i\mathbf r_i'\times\mathbf F_{i,\rm ext},\qquad\frac{d\mathbf L_{\rm CM}}{dt}=\mathbf G_{\rm CM},
$$
where primes denote relative positions and <velocities>. The <derivative> of the vector is taken relative to nonrotating axes. The <inertia tensor> relates <angular momentum> to <angular velocity>, $\mathbf L_{\rm CM}=\mathsf I\boldsymbol\omega$. For rotation about a fixed unit direction $\mathbf n$, the <moment of inertia> is $I_{\mathbf n}=\int r_\perp^2\,dm=\mathbf n\cdot\mathsf I\mathbf n$; it measures the resistance to changing that rotation and gives rotational <kinetic energy> $I_{\mathbf n}\omega^2/2$. The simpler vector relation $\mathbf L=I\boldsymbol\omega$ applies when the rotation axis is a <principal axis>, not for every orientation of an arbitrary <rigid body>. These translational and rotational balances must be applied together; a nonzero <torque> does not replace the net-<force> equation.

**Gyroscope precession.** A rapidly spinning <gyroscope> has a large <angular momentum> approximately along its spin axis. The <torque> component parallel to this vector changes its magnitude, while the perpendicular component turns its direction. If the vector undergoes <precession> with <angular velocity> $\boldsymbol\Omega$, then over $dt$ its change is the infinitesimal rotation $d\mathbf L=(\boldsymbol\Omega\,dt)\times\mathbf L$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbf G=\frac{d\mathbf L}{dt}=\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf L.}
$$
This describes direction-changing <precession> at constant $|\mathbf L|$; any spin-up contribution must be added separately. For perpendicular <torque> and <angular momentum>, the precessional component perpendicular to $\mathbf L$ is $\boldsymbol\Omega_\perp=\mathbf L\times\mathbf G/L^2$. The familiar tendency of a supported <gyroscope> to turn sideways is this vector balance, not the absence of gravitational <force>.

**Wheel-lift threshold without the flywheel.** Let $h=0.50\,\mathrm m$ be the <centre of mass> height, $a=1.0\,\mathrm m$ half the wheel separation, and $R_b=10\,\mathrm m$ the bend radius. The required inward <force> is $F=Mv^2/R_b$, provided by <static friction>. Let $N_o,N_i$ be the summed outer and inner wheel <normal forces>. Vertical balance gives $N_o+N_i=Mg$. Moment balance about the <centre of mass> gives
$$
a(N_o-N_i)=h\frac{Mv^2}{R_b},\qquad N_i=\frac M2\left(g-\frac{hv^2}{aR_b}\right).
$$
The inner wheels lift when $N_i=0$. Thus the <vehicle rollover threshold> is
$$
\boxed{v_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{gaR_b}{h}}=14.01\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}=50.4\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}.}
$$
The stated <mass> cancels. This assumes the rigid geometry and sufficient <static friction> specified in the paper; the threshold is set by loss of contact, not by sliding.

**Engine flywheel.** The <moment of inertia of a uniform disc> gives
$$
I_f=\frac12M_fr_f^2=\frac12(50)(0.4)^2=4.00\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}.
$$
Its spin <angular speed>, spin <angular momentum>, and turn <angular speed> at $40\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}$ are
$$
\omega_f=4000\frac{2\pi}{60}=418.88\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}},\quad L_f=I_f\omega_f=1675.52\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}},\quad\Omega=\frac{40/3.6}{10}=1.1111\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}.
$$
The transverse flywheel axis is perpendicular to the vertical turn axis, so
$$
\boxed{G=\Omega L_f=1.862\times10^3\,\mathrm{N\,m}.}
$$
To specify its direction, use right-handed axes $+x$ forward, $+y$ to the car's left, and $+z$ upward, and consider a left turn. Then $\boldsymbol\Omega=+\Omega\widehat{\mathbf z}$. Choose the flywheel <angular momentum> to the car's right, $\mathbf L_f=-L_f\widehat{\mathbf y}$. The car must apply
$$
\mathbf G_{\rm on\ flywheel}=+\Omega L_f\widehat{\mathbf x},\qquad\boxed{\mathbf G_{\rm on\ car}=-\Omega L_f\widehat{\mathbf x}}
$$
to obtain the required <precession>. This <gyroscopic roll torque in a turning vehicle> opposes the outward roll tendency in a left turn. By the <right-hand rule>, the flywheel must turn **counterclockwise viewed from the car's right side toward its centre**, opposite to the rotation of wheels rolling forward. Reversing flywheel spin would aggravate the outward roll. Reversing the turn reverses both roll and gyroscopic <torques>, so the same flywheel spin remains helpful.

\Image[2021-q6-flywheel-torque.png]
{title=<Gyroscope> vector directions for a left-turning car and the flywheel spin needed to oppose outward roll}

For comparison, the outward inertial roll moment at the stated <speed> is $Mhv^2/R_b=6173\,\mathrm{N\,m}$, and the limiting gravitational restoring moment is $Mga=9810\,\mathrm{N\,m}$. The gyroscopic reaction is about $30\%$ of the former and $19\%$ of the latter, so it is appreciable for this large flywheel. If this ideal model is extended to include the favorable gyroscopic reaction in the wheel-lift condition, it gives $Mhv^2/R_b-L_fv/R_b=Mga$, or about $56.8\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}$ rather than $50.4\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}$. This quantifies a useful but finite increase in the rollover threshold.

**Frame definitions and relativistic effects.** Let $S$ be the <inertial frame> of Earth and the stationary asteroid, with $+x$ pointing toward the asteroid. Bob's <inertial frame> $S'$ moves at $+v$ relative to $S$. For two events at Bob's fixed position, $\Delta x'=0$, the inverse <Lorentz transformation> gives
$$
\Delta t=\gamma\Delta t',\qquad\gamma=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}.
$$
The elapsed <proper time> on Bob's clock is $\Delta\tau=\Delta t'$, so <time dilation> means $\Delta t=\gamma\Delta\tau$: the separation of his clock ticks is larger in Earth coordinates. For a rod at rest in $S'$ with <proper length> $L_0$, a length measurement in $S$ uses simultaneous endpoints, $\Delta t=0$. The direct spatial <Lorentz transformation> gives $L_0=\Delta x'=\gamma\Delta x$, hence <length contraction>:
$$
\boxed{L=L_0/\gamma.}
$$
The simultaneity condition is essential: the endpoint events used to measure length are different from the two clock-tick events used to demonstrate <time dilation>.

**Bob's speed.** Food consumption measures Bob's own <proper time>, $\tau_B=4\text{ months}=1/3\,\mathrm{yr}$. The Earth-frame distance is $D=1/4\,\mathrm{ly}$ and the Earth travel <time> is $D/v$. Consequently
$$
\tau_B=\frac{D}{\gamma v},\qquad\gamma\frac vc=\frac{D}{c\tau_B}=\frac34.
$$
Putting $\beta=v/c$, $\beta^2/(1-\beta^2)=9/16$, so
$$
\boxed{v=\frac35c=0.600c,\qquad\gamma=\frac54.}
$$
The Earth travel <time> is $5/12\,\mathrm{yr}=5$ months. Equivalently Bob measures the contracted Earth-asteroid separation $D/\gamma=0.20\,\mathrm{ly}$ and sees the asteroid approach at $0.60c$, taking $1/3\,\mathrm{yr}$ to reach him.

**Events A and B.** Set both coordinate origins at Event A. Alice reaches the asteroid at Event B, travelling at $u=2c/3$ in $S$, so
$$
\boxed{\Delta x=\frac14\,\mathrm{ly},\qquad\Delta t=\frac{D}{u}=\frac38\,\mathrm{yr}=4.5\text{ months}.}
$$
The <Lorentz transformations> into Bob's frame give
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta t'&=\frac54\left(\frac38-\frac35\frac14\right)\mathrm{yr}=\boxed{\frac9{32}\,\mathrm{yr}=3.375\text{ months}},\\
\Delta x'&=\frac54\left(\frac14-\frac35\frac38\right)\mathrm{ly}=\boxed{\frac1{32}\,\mathrm{ly}=0.03125\,\mathrm{ly}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $c(1\,\mathrm{yr})=1\,\mathrm{ly}$ has been used consistently. With the time-positive sign convention, the <Minkowski interval> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta s^2&=c^2\Delta t^2-\Delta x^2=\left(\frac9{64}-\frac4{64}\right)\mathrm{ly^2}=\boxed{\frac5{64}\,\mathrm{ly^2}},\\
\Delta s'^2&=c^2\Delta t'^2-\Delta x'^2=\left(\frac{81}{1024}-\frac1{1024}\right)\mathrm{ly^2}=\boxed{\frac5{64}\,\mathrm{ly^2}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the <Minkowski interval> is invariant. It is timelike, as expected for two events on Alice's subluminal <worldline>. Using the opposite metric sign convention would change both signs together, without changing invariance.

**Relative velocity and spacetime diagram.** Divide the differential spatial and temporal <Lorentz transformations> for the same trajectory:
$$
u_x'=\frac{dx'}{dt'}=\frac{\gamma(dx-v\,dt)}{\gamma(dt-v\,dx/c^2)}=\boxed{\frac{u_x-v}{1-vu_x/c^2}}.
$$
For Alice,
$$
\boxed{u'_{\rm Alice}=\frac{(2/3-3/5)c}{1-(2/3)(3/5)}=\frac19c.}
$$
The <spacetime diagram> uses horizontal $x'$ and vertical $ct'$. In these coordinates Bob's <worldline> is $x'=0$, Alice's is $x'=ct'/9$, and the asteroid's is
$$
x'_{\rm asteroid}=\frac D\gamma-vt'=\frac15\,\mathrm{ly}-\frac35ct'.
$$
Thus Alice has slope $d(ct')/dx'=9$, while the asteroid has slope $-5/3$. Their intersection is exactly B, $(x',ct')=(1/32,9/32)\,\mathrm{ly}$. The asteroid later reaches Bob at $(0,1/3)\,\mathrm{ly}$. These intercepts and gradients show the <relativistic velocity addition> geometrically.

The <force> law separates into two identical <harmonic oscillator equations>:
$$
m\ddot x=-kx,\qquad m\ddot y=-ky.
$$
For positive $m,k$, the general real solution is
$$
\boxed{x(t)=x_0\cos(\omega t+\phi_1),\qquad y(t)=y_0\cos(\omega t+\phi_2),\qquad\omega=\sqrt{k/m}.}
$$
Since $\operatorname{Re}(e^{i\alpha})=\cos\alpha$ by <Euler's formula>, these are exactly the stated <real parts> of <complex exponentials>. The <amplitudes> $x_0,y_0$ and <phases> $\phi_1,\phi_2$ are fixed by the four initial position and <velocity> components. This is two-dimensional <isotropic harmonic oscillator flow>: the same <angular frequency> acts in every direction.

= Solution

Put $\delta=\phi_2-\phi_1$. If both coordinate <amplitudes> are nonzero, eliminate the common <phase> to obtain
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{x}{x_0}\right)^2+\left(\frac{y}{y_0}\right)^2-2\frac{xy}{x_0y_0}\cos\delta=\sin^2\delta.}
$$
For $\sin\delta\ne0$, this is an <ellipse> centred at the origin. The following special cases exhaust the possible nontrivial shapes:

* $\delta=0$ or $\pi$ gives straight-line <simple harmonic motion>, including an axis-aligned line if one coordinate <amplitude> vanishes.
* $\delta=\pm\pi/2$ gives an axis-aligned <ellipse>; if additionally $x_0=y_0$, it is a <circle> traversed in one of the two orientations.
* Other <phase differences> give tilted <ellipses>.

The degenerate zero-<amplitude> solution is the stationary origin. There are no general multi-loop patterns here because the two <angular frequencies> are equal. Another useful description is $\mathbf r=\mathbf a\cos\omega t+\mathbf b\sin\omega t$, the image of a <circle> under a <linear map>.

= Solution

The <force> is conservative, with $\mathbf F=-\nabla U$ and <potential energy> zero at the origin:
$$
\boxed{U(t)=\frac k2[x(t)^2+y(t)^2]=\frac k2\left[x_0^2\cos^2(\omega t+\phi_1)+y_0^2\cos^2(\omega t+\phi_2)\right].}
$$
Released from rest at $(c,c)$, the trajectory has $x=y=c\cos\omega t$, giving
$$
\boxed{U(t)=kc^2\cos^2\omega t=\frac{kc^2}{2}(1+\cos2\omega t).}
$$
The right panel of the figure shows this nonnegative <oscillation>: it starts at a maximum $kc^2$, reaches zero at $t=\pi/(2\omega)$, and repeats after $\pi/\omega$. Its <frequency> is twice the coordinate <frequency>. <Conservation of mechanical energy> gives $K(t)=kc^2\sin^2\omega t$, so the missing <potential energy> is <kinetic energy>, not an energy loss.

The starting radius is $r_0=\sqrt2\,c$. In uniform <circular motion>, the required <centripetal acceleration> has magnitude $\omega^2r_0=kr_0/m$. The initial <velocity> must be tangent to the starting radius, with the orientation chosen anticlockwise:

\boxed{\mathbf v(0)=\omega\widehat{\mathbf z}\times(c,c,0)=(-\omega c,\omega c).}

The resulting trajectory is
$$
x=c(\cos\omega t-\sin\omega t),\qquad y=c(\cos\omega t+\sin\omega t).

It has constant $x^2+y^2=2c^2$, so
$$
\boxed{U(t)=kc^2\quad\text{for all }t.}
$$
This is the horizontal line in the right panel of the figure. The <kinetic energy> also equals $kc^2$, giving total <mechanical energy> $2kc^2$, twice that for release from rest at the same point.

= Solution
{parent=e}

Write $\gamma=b/(2m)$ and $\nu=\sqrt{\omega^2-\gamma^2}$, where $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}$ is the undamped <angular frequency>. The <linear drag> gives the vector <damped harmonic oscillator> equation
$$
\ddot{\mathbf r}+2\gamma\dot{\mathbf r}+\omega^2\mathbf r=0.
$$
For the original circular <initial conditions>, $\mathbf r_0=(c,c)$ and $\mathbf v_0=\omega(-c,c)$, the exact <underdamped> solution is
$$
\mathbf r(t)=e^{-\gamma t}\left[\mathbf r_0\cos\nu t+\frac{\mathbf v_0+\gamma\mathbf r_0}{\nu}\sin\nu t\right].
$$
Using $|\mathbf r_0|^2=2c^2$, $|\mathbf v_0|^2=2\omega^2c^2$ and $\mathbf r_0\cdot\mathbf v_0=0$ gives
$$
\boxed{U(t)=kc^2e^{-2\gamma t}\left[1+\frac\gamma\nu\sin2\nu t+\frac{2\gamma^2}{\nu^2}\sin^2\nu t\right].}
$$
This exact <potential energy> is useful because it satisfies the prescribed <initial conditions>, including $\dot U(0)=k\mathbf r_0\cdot\mathbf v_0=0$. In the stated weak-<damping> limit $\gamma\ll\omega$, $\nu\simeq\omega$ and the square bracket differs from one by a small oscillatory term. The leading approximation is therefore
$$
\boxed{U(t)\simeq kc^2e^{-bt/m}.}
$$
The radius is approximately $\sqrt2\,c\,e^{-bt/(2m)}$, so the trajectory is approximately a shrinking <logarithmic spiral>. The exactly exponential result would require slightly adjusted initial <velocity>; with the specified undamped circular <velocity>, the small modulation above is real. This is the <initially circular damped planar oscillator>.

For <overdamping>, $\gamma=b/(2m)>\omega$. Put $\alpha=\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega^2}>0$. Both <characteristic roots> $-\gamma\pm\alpha$ are negative real numbers. Replacing trigonometric functions by <hyperbolic functions> gives the exact trajectory
$$
\boxed{\mathbf r(t)=e^{-\gamma t}\left[\mathbf r_0\cosh\alpha t+\frac{\mathbf v_0+\gamma\mathbf r_0}{\alpha}\sinh\alpha t\right].}
$$
It starts from $(c,c)$ with tangent $(-\omega c,\omega c)$, bends anticlockwise toward the origin, and approaches it without repeated revolutions. The right panel gives a representative choice $\gamma=1.5\omega$; the paper does not specify one particular value of $b$ in this regime.

One can make the qualitative shape precise. In radial and tangential directions relative to the initial radius, the angle turned from the starting direction obeys
$$
\tan\Delta\phi(t)=\frac{\omega\sinh\alpha t}{\alpha\cosh\alpha t+\gamma\sinh\alpha t}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{\Delta\phi(\infty)=\arctan\frac\omega{\gamma+\alpha}<\frac\pi4.}
$$
For $c>0$, both coordinates stay positive, and the approach is along the fixed ray with $y/x\to(\gamma+\alpha+\omega)/(\gamma+\alpha-\omega)$. The initial <velocity> is tangential, so an immediately straight radial return would be incorrect. The absence of oscillatory <characteristic roots> prevents repeated revolutions. This is a <non-spiralling overdamped central oscillator trajectory>.

Use the original diagram's positive distances: $u$ from the object to the vertex, $d$ from the vertex to the image, and $R$ from the vertex toward the centre of curvature on the outgoing side. Let a paraxial <light ray> strike at height $Y$. Its incoming inclination is $\alpha\simeq Y/u$ above the <optical axis>, its outgoing inclination is $\beta\simeq Y/d$ below it, and the inward surface normal is inclined by $\theta\simeq Y/R$ below it. Thus the incidence and refraction angles relative to that normal are respectively $\alpha+\theta$ and $\theta-\beta$.

The local tangent-plane form of <Snell's law>, using the <small-angle approximation>, gives
$$
n_1(\alpha+\theta)=n_2(\theta-\beta),\qquad n_1\alpha+n_2\beta=(n_2-n_1)\theta.
$$
Substitute the three height-to-distance ratios and cancel $Y$:
$$
\boxed{\frac{n_1}{u}+\frac{n_2}{d}=\frac{n_2-n_1}{R}.}
$$
This <refraction at a spherical surface> result keeps only first-order ray angles. The surface sag shifts the height's axial reference only at higher order. Distances for other object, image or curvature orientations must be signed consistently; the displayed positive-distance form follows the particular orientation in the paper.

Keep the paper's $R_1,R_2>0$ as magnitudes for the two convex faces. With the standard signed-curvature convention measured to the right, the first surface has signed radius $+R_1$ and the second $-R_2$. This explains why the requested formula contains a sum of the two reciprocal magnitudes.

Let the axial lens thickness be $\ell$, and let $d_1$ be the distance from the first vertex to the intermediate focus that its refracted rays would reach without the second surface. The first <refraction at a spherical surface> gives
$$
\frac{n_1}{u}+\frac{n_2}{d_1}=\frac{n_2-n_1}{R_1}.
$$
At the second face those rays are still converging toward that intermediate focus, so its object distance is negative, $u_2=-d_2$, with $d_2=d_1-\ell$. Applying the signed spherical-surface equation again gives
$$
-\frac{n_2}{d_2}+\frac{n_1}{v}=\frac{n_1-n_2}{-R_2}=\frac{n_2-n_1}{R_2}.
$$
In the <thin lens> limit, $d_2\simeq d_1$. Adding cancels the intermediate-focus terms:
$$
\boxed{\frac1u+\frac1v=\left(\frac{n_2}{n_1}-1\right)\left(\frac1{R_1}+\frac1{R_2}\right).}
$$
The <focal length> is the image distance for parallel incident <light rays>, $u\to\infty$, hence the <lensmaker's equation> is
$$
\boxed{f=\left[\left(\frac{n_2}{n_1}-1\right)\left(\frac1{R_1}+\frac1{R_2}\right)\right]^{-1}.}
$$
The same surrounding <refractive index> $n_1$ is assumed on both sides. For $n_2>n_1$ this biconvex <thin lens> has positive <focal length>. With signed radii $R_a=R_1$, $R_b=-R_2$, the equivalent familiar expression is $1/f=(n_2/n_1-1)(1/R_a-1/R_b)$.

For steady <electric currents> in <vacuum>, <Ampère's circuital law> is
$$
\boxed{\oint_C\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf l=\mu_0 I_{\rm enclosed}.}
$$
Choose an oriented closed contour and a spanning surface; the positive enclosed <electric current> crosses that surface according to the <right-hand rule> for the contour. First use the source <symmetry> to establish the direction of $\mathbf B$ and which positions have the same magnitude. Then choose the contour so its useful segments are parallel to $\mathbf B$ with constant magnitude, and the remaining segments are perpendicular to $\mathbf B$ or have vanishing field contribution. The <line integral> reduces to the unknown magnitude times a known length or sum of lengths.

For example, cylindrical <symmetry> around an infinite wire allows a circular contour, giving $B(2\pi r)=\mu_0I$. A uniform <current sheet> instead calls for a rectangle straddling it. The law is always valid in this magnetostatic setting, but by itself does not determine the local <magnetic field> from an arbitrary enclosed current without these extra <symmetry> arguments. Here $\mu_0$ is the <vacuum permeability>.

= Solution

Choose $+x$ along the <electric current>, $+z$ upward normal to the sheet, and $+y$ to complete a right-handed frame. In the interior region far from its edges, treat the sheet as infinite in its plane. Its <surface current density> is
$$
\mathbf K=\frac Iw\widehat{\mathbf x}.
$$
Reflection and translational <symmetry> make the <magnetic field> parallel to the sheet, perpendicular to its current, and equal in magnitude with opposite direction above and below. Choose an <Ampère's circuital law> rectangle with length $s$ along $y$, straddling the full thickness. The two long sides each contribute $Bs$ with the same sign; on the vertical sides $\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf l=0$ since $\mathbf B$ is horizontal. The enclosed <electric current> is $Ks=Is/w$. Thus
$$
2Bs=\mu_0\frac Iw s,\qquad\boxed{B=\frac{\mu_0I}{2w}.}
$$
The <right-hand rule> fixes the directions:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B_{\rm above}=-\frac{\mu_0I}{2w}\widehat{\mathbf y},\qquad\mathbf B_{\rm below}=+\frac{\mu_0I}{2w}\widehat{\mathbf y}.}
$$
The <current density> inside the finite-thickness conductor is $\mathbf J=I/(wt)\,\widehat{\mathbf x}$, so integrating through thickness $t$ gives the same $\mathbf K$. These are exterior fields in the broad-sheet approximation, not exact finite-width edge fields.

Keep $+x$ along the upper-sheet current and $+z$ upward, putting the lower sheet at $z=0$ and upper sheet at $z=d$. Between them, the upper sheet's lower-side <magnetic field> is $+\mu_0I/(2w)\widehat{\mathbf y}$. The lower sheet carries current in $-x$, so its upper-side <magnetic field> is also $+\mu_0I/(2w)\widehat{\mathbf y}$. The <superposition principle> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B=\frac{\mu_0I}{w}\widehat{\mathbf y}.}
$$
Each perfect conductor is an <equipotential>. The circuit's <resistor> carries current from upper to lower, so <Ohm's law> fixes their <electric potential difference>:
$$
V_{\rm upper}-V_{\rm lower}=IR.
$$
The broad parallel faces and their constant potentials make the <electric field> perpendicular to them and independent of the coordinates along them, away from edges. There is no volume <electric charge> in the gap. Apply <Gauss's law> to a small pillbox wholly within the gap, with equal face <areas> $A$ at heights $z_1,z_2$. Its side flux is zero, so
$$
[E_z(z_2)-E_z(z_1)]A=\frac{Q_{\rm enclosed}}{\varepsilon_0}=0.
$$
Thus $E_z$ is independent of height as well. Since $V_{\rm upper}-V_{\rm lower}=-\int_0^dE_z\,dz$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E=-\frac{IR}{d}\widehat{\mathbf z},\qquad E=\frac{IR}{d}.}
$$
It points from the higher-potential upper sheet to the lower sheet. <Gauss's law> requires the planar <symmetry> argument: zero enclosed charge alone would not prove a uniform <electric field> in a general geometry. These combined fields characterize <parallel current sheets with a resistive termination>.

= Solution

For injected <velocity> $\mathbf v=v\widehat{\mathbf x}$, the <Lorentz force> is
$$
\mathbf F=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)=q\left(-\frac{IR}{d}+v\frac{\mu_0I}{w}\right)\widehat{\mathbf z}.
$$
For nonzero <electric charge> and nonzero operating current, the <velocity selector> condition is
$$
\boxed{v=\frac EB=\frac{Rw}{\mu_0d}.}
$$
The requested nonrelativistic <kinetic energy> is consequently
$$
\boxed{T=\frac m2\left(\frac{Rw}{\mu_0d}\right)^2.}
$$
Both <electric current> and <electric charge> cancel. Reversing the sign of $q$ reverses both component <forces> and preserves their cancellation. The dimensions are consistent because $R/\mu_0$ has units of <speed>, while $w/d$ is dimensionless. The assumed <nonrelativistic particle> regime requires $Rw/(\mu_0d)\ll c$; the problem supplies no values with which to evaluate a numerical <energy>.
