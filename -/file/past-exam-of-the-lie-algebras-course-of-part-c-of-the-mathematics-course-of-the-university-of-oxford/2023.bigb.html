Solutions to all three questions of the <Lie algebras course of Part C of the mathematics course of the University of Oxford> examination, sat on 6 June 2023. Source: https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/request_for_mathematics_examinat/response/3129786/attach/3/FOI%20202507%20740%20Data.pdf#page=307[original released PDF, bundle pages 307–309; paper pages 1–3]. Questions 1 and 2 occupy paper page 2, and Question 3 occupies paper page 3. Module C2.1 and formal paper code A12189W1 identify this Part C paper. The original question numbering is retained.

Throughout, $k$ is an <algebraically closed field> of <characteristic zero>. All <Lie algebras> and <vector spaces> are <finite-dimensional vector spaces> except the <Lie algebra representation> explicitly considered in Question 2(c). We use the <operator commutator> $[A,B]=AB-BA$ and the standard <sl2 triple> convention $[H,E]=2E$, $[H,F]=-2F$, $[E,F]=H$. The printed operators in Question 2(c)(i) have a sign inconsistency with that convention; its solution explains the correction. The displayed definition of the <symplectic group> on paper page 3 also lacks a closing set brace; its intended meaning is the group of invertible <symplectic linear maps>.

= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

Since the <general linear Lie algebra> $\mathfrak{gl}_1(k)$ has zero <Lie bracket>, identify it with $kz$. A <central extension of a Lie algebra> is a <short exact sequence>
$$
0\longrightarrow kz\xrightarrow{\iota}\widetilde{\mathfrak g}\xrightarrow{\pi}\mathfrak g\longrightarrow0
$$
of <Lie algebra homomorphisms> with $\iota(kz)$ contained in the <center of a Lie algebra> $Z(\widetilde{\mathfrak g})$. Thus $\pi$ is surjective, its <kernel> is precisely the indicated line, and that line commutes with all of $\widetilde{\mathfrak g}$.

Choose a <linear map> $s:\mathfrak g\to\widetilde{\mathfrak g}$ with $\pi s=\operatorname{id}$. Such a section exists by lifting a <basis>. Identify $\widetilde{\mathfrak g}$ with the <direct sum of vector spaces> $\mathfrak g\oplus kz$ by $(x,tz)\mapsto s(x)+t\iota(z)$. The difference $[s(x),s(y)]-s([x,y])$ belongs to the <kernel> of $\pi$, so there is a unique <bilinear map> $\alpha:\mathfrak g\times\mathfrak g\to k$ such that
$$
[s(x),s(y)]-s([x,y])=\alpha(x,y)\iota(z).
$$
It is an <alternating bilinear form>: $\alpha(x,x)=0$, equivalently $\alpha(x,y)=-\alpha(y,x)$ in <characteristic zero>. Taking the central component of the <Jacobi identity> gives the <Lie algebra two-cocycle> condition
$$
\alpha([x,y],w)+\alpha([y,w],x)+\alpha([w,x],y)=0\qquad(x,y,w\in\mathfrak g).
$$
Conversely, any <alternating bilinear form> satisfying this identity defines a <Lie algebra> by
$$
\boxed{[(x,tz),(y,uz)]=([x,y],\alpha(x,y)z).}
$$
Indeed its <Lie bracket> is an <alternating multilinear map> and a <bilinear map>, and the two components of its <Jacobi identity> are respectively the <Jacobi identity> in $\mathfrak g$ and the displayed <Lie algebra two-cocycle> condition. The line $kz$ is central, and projection to $\mathfrak g$ gives the required <central extension of a Lie algebra>.

For later use, replacing $s$ by $s'(x)=s(x)+t(x)\iota(z)$, where $t\in\mathfrak g^*$ is in the <dual space>, changes the <Lie algebra two-cocycle> to
$$
\boxed{\alpha'(x,y)=\alpha(x,y)-t([x,y]).}
$$
Thus changes of section add a <Lie algebra two-coboundary>, up to the conventional sign. A section that is a <Lie algebra homomorphism> has $\alpha'=0$ and gives a <direct sum of Lie algebras>.

Choose a <basis> $x,y$ of the two-dimensional <abelian Lie algebra> $\mathfrak a$, and lifts with the same names. Every <alternating bilinear form> is determined by $c=\alpha(x,y)$, and every such form is a <Lie algebra two-cocycle> because every <Lie bracket> in $\mathfrak a$ is zero. Thus every <central extension of a Lie algebra> has a <basis> $x,y,z$ with
$$
[x,y]=cz,\qquad [x,z]=[y,z]=0.
$$
If $c=0$, all <Lie brackets> vanish, so this is the <direct sum of Lie algebras> $\mathfrak a\oplus kz\cong k^3$. If $c\ne0$, replace the central <basis vector> by $z'=cz$. Then $[x,y]=z'$, which is the <Heisenberg Lie algebra> $\mathfrak h_3(k)$. This change of <basis> shows that every nonzero $c$ gives the same <Lie algebra isomorphism> class.

These two <Lie algebras> cannot be isomorphic: their <derived algebras> have <dimensions> zero and one, respectively, and a <Lie algebra isomorphism> preserves the <derived algebra>. Moreover, the <Heisenberg Lie algebra> cannot be a <direct sum of Lie algebras> $kz\oplus\mathfrak a$, since that <direct sum of Lie algebras> would have zero <Lie bracket>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\widetilde{\mathfrak a}\cong k^3\quad\text{or}\quad\mathfrak h_3(k).}
$$
Here “up to isomorphism” means the <Lie algebra isomorphism> class of the middle <Lie algebra>, allowing a change of the identifications at the ends of the <short exact sequence>. If instead <equivalence of central extensions of a Lie algebra> must induce the identity on both $kz$ and $\mathfrak a$, the parameter $c$ cannot change: the formula in part (a) has $t([x,y])=0$. That stricter notion has one class for each $c\in k$, rather than two. This distinction explains the convention required by the question.

First put the nonabelian two-dimensional <Lie algebra> in a convenient form. For any <basis> $p,q$, let $u=[p,q]=ap+bq\ne0$. Then $[p,u]=bu$ and $[q,u]=-au$. Since $(a,b)\ne(0,0)$, some <linear combination> $x$ of $p,q$ has $[x,u]=u$. In particular, $x,u$ are <linearly independent>. Taking $y=u$ gives a <basis> with
$$
[x,y]=y.
$$
This is the <affine Lie algebra of the line>.

Every <alternating bilinear form> on this <vector space> is
$$
\alpha_c(rx+sy,tx+uy)=c(ru-st)\qquad(c\in k).
$$
For any <alternating bilinear form> $\alpha$, the expression
$$
J_\alpha(v,w,t)=\alpha([v,w],t)+\alpha([w,t],v)+\alpha([t,v],w)
$$
is an <alternating trilinear form>: it is trilinear, and setting any two arguments equal makes the terms cancel. Every <alternating trilinear form> on a two-dimensional <vector space> vanishes, since any triple is <linearly dependent>. Hence every $\alpha_c$ satisfies the <Lie algebra two-cocycle> condition, and there are no others:
$$
\boxed{Z^2(\mathfrak s,k)=\{\alpha_c:c\in k\}\cong k,\qquad\alpha_c(x,y)=c.}
$$

For the <Lie algebra two-cocycle> $\alpha_c$ of part (i), the <central extension of a Lie algebra> has a <basis> $x,y,z$ and <Lie brackets>
$$
[x,y]=y+cz,\qquad[x,z]=[y,z]=0.
$$
Put $y'=y+cz$. This is still a <basis> together with $x,z$, and
$$
[x,y']=[x,y]+c[x,z]=y+cz=y'.
$$
Consequently $\langle x,y'\rangle$ is a <Lie subalgebra> isomorphic to the <affine Lie algebra of the line>. It commutes with $kz$ and has zero intersection with $kz$, so both summands are <ideals of a Lie algebra> and
$$
\boxed{\widetilde{\mathfrak s}\cong\mathfrak s\oplus kz:\quad\text{exactly one class, which is trivial}.}
$$
This also holds for the stricter <equivalence of central extensions of a Lie algebra>. Indeed, choose the <linear functional> $t$ with $t(x)=0$, $t(y)=c$. Since $[x,y]=y$ in $\mathfrak s$, the change of section in part (a) gives $\alpha'_c(x,y)=c-t(y)=0$. Thus every <Lie algebra two-cocycle> here is a <Lie algebra two-coboundary>, so $H^2(\mathfrak s,k)=0$ in <Lie algebra cohomology>. The explicit map from the split <central extension of a Lie algebra> sends $x$ to $x$, $y$ to $y+cz$, and $z$ to $z$; it fixes the central line and induces the identity on the <quotient Lie algebra>.

Let $A$ be a three-dimensional <vector space> with <basis> $p,q,z$. Define a <bilinear map> $\bullet:A\times A\to A$ by
$$
p\bullet q=z,\qquad q\bullet p=-z,
$$
with all other products of <basis vectors> zero. Equivalently,
$$
(ap+bq+cz)\bullet(a'p+b'q+c'z)=(ab'-ba')z.
$$
Every product belongs to $kz$, and every product with an element of $kz$ is zero. Hence both $(u\bullet v)\bullet w$ and $u\bullet(v\bullet w)$ vanish for every $u,v,w\in A$. This proves <associativity>, so $A$ is an <associative algebra>. The product is an <alternating multilinear map>, and each nested product in the <Jacobi identity> is zero, so the same product is a <Lie bracket>. It is nonzero because $p\bullet q=z\ne0$.

There is no multiplicative <identity element>: any candidate $1_A$ would satisfy $1_A\bullet z=0$, whereas an <identity element> would require $1_A\bullet z=z$. Thus
$$
\boxed{A=kp\oplus kq\oplus kz,\quad p\bullet q=z=-q\bullet p,\quad A^2=kz,\quad A^3=0.}
$$
This is an <associative Lie product>; its underlying <Lie algebra> is the <Heisenberg Lie algebra>.

For a <perfect Lie algebra> $\mathfrak s$, every element is a sum of <Lie brackets>. The <Adjoint representation> satisfies $\operatorname{ad}[x,y]=[\operatorname{ad}x,\operatorname{ad}y]$, and the <trace of a commutator> is zero. Therefore
$$
\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{ad}x)=0\qquad(x\in\mathfrak s).
$$
Some $\operatorname{ad}x$ must have a nonzero <eigenvalue>. Otherwise, over the <algebraically closed field> $k$, every $\operatorname{ad}x$ would be a <nilpotent linear map>, and the <Engel theorem> would imply that $\mathfrak s$ is a <nilpotent Lie algebra>. This contradicts perfection: the <lower central series of a Lie algebra> $\gamma_1=\mathfrak s$, $\gamma_{r+1}=[\mathfrak s,\gamma_r]$ would equal $\mathfrak s\ne0$ at every step instead of terminating.

Choose such an $x$. Since $[x,x]=0$, zero is an <eigenvalue> of $\operatorname{ad}x$. Its <trace> is zero and its <dimension> is three, so the remaining <eigenvalues> are $\lambda,-\lambda$ with $\lambda\ne0$. These three <eigenvalues> are distinct in <characteristic zero>, hence $\operatorname{ad}x$ is <diagonalizable>. Put $h=2x/\lambda$, and choose nonzero <eigenvectors> $e,f$ with
$$
[h,e]=2e,\qquad[h,f]=-2f.
$$
The zero <eigenspace> is $kh$, so $h,e,f$ form a <basis>. Applying the <Jacobi identity> gives
$$
[h,[e,f]]=[[h,e],f]+[e,[h,f]]=2[e,f]-2[e,f]=0.
$$
Thus $[e,f]=ch$ for some $c\in k$. If $c=0$, all <Lie brackets> of <basis vectors> lie in $\langle e,f\rangle$, contradicting $[\mathfrak s,\mathfrak s]=\mathfrak s$. Therefore $c\ne0$, and replacing $f$ by $f/c$ gives $[e,f]=h$.

We have constructed an <sl2 triple> that is a <basis>. The <linear map> to the <sl2 Lie algebra> sending
$$
e\longmapsto\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad h\longmapsto\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad f\longmapsto\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
$$
preserves all <Lie brackets> of <basis vectors> and is bijective. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathfrak s\cong\mathfrak{sl}_2(k).}
$$
This is the <classification of three-dimensional perfect Lie algebras> without appealing to a classification of all <simple Lie algebras>.

Take the <semidirect product of a Lie algebra and a module>
$$
L=\mathfrak{sl}_2(k)\ltimes k^2,\qquad[(X,u),(Y,v)]=([X,Y],Xv-Yu),
$$
where $k^2$ is the <defining representation> of the <sl2 Lie algebra>. To see directly that this is a <Lie algebra>, realize $(X,u)$ as the block <matrix> $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}X&u\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. These <matrices> are closed under the <operator commutator>, whose <Jacobi identity> holds in every <associative algebra>.

Using the standard <sl2 triple> $e,h,f$, its <Lie brackets> show $[\mathfrak{sl}_2,\mathfrak{sl}_2]=\mathfrak{sl}_2$: indeed $h=[e,f]$, $e=[h,e]/2$, and $f=-[h,f]/2$. Also $h=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$ is invertible on $k^2$. For any $u\in k^2$, put $v=h^{-1}u$; then
$$
[(h,0),(0,v)]=(0,u).
$$
It follows that the <derived algebra> contains both $\mathfrak{sl}_2\oplus0$ and $0\oplus k^2$, so $[L,L]=L$: this is a <perfect Lie algebra>.

However, $0\oplus k^2$ is a nonzero <ideal of a Lie algebra> with zero <Lie bracket>. It is therefore a <solvable Lie algebra> and is contained in the <solvable radical> of $L$. A <semisimple Lie algebra> has zero <solvable radical>, so
$$
\boxed{[L,L]=L,\qquad0\ne k^2\subseteq\operatorname{rad}L,\qquad\dim L=5.}
$$
This illustrates the <perfect semidirect product criterion>: the <Lie brackets> internal to the first summand generate it, while its action generates the second summand.

**There is a sign error in the printed assertion under the standard <sl2 triple> convention.** Work first with the operators exactly as printed. Since the $v_i$ form a <Hamel basis>, their prescribed images extend to <linear maps> on $V$; each vector is a finite <linear combination> of <basis vectors>.

For $i\geq1$, the <operator commutators> with $h$ are
$$
\begin{aligned}
[h,e]v_i&=\bigl(-2(i-1)(i-1)+2i(i-1)\bigr)v_{i-1}=2ev_i,\\
[h,f]v_i&=\bigl(-2(i+1)^2+2i(i+1)\bigr)v_{i+1}=-2fv_i.
\end{aligned}
$$
At $i=0$, the first identity holds because both sides vanish; the second holds because $hf(v_0)=-2v_1$ and $fh(v_0)=0$. On the other hand, for $i\geq1$,
$$
[e,f]v_i=i(i+1)v_i-i(i-1)v_i=2iv_i=-hv_i.
$$
At $i=0$, both $ef(v_0)=e(v_1)=0$ and $fe(v_0)=0$, so this identity also holds. Thus the actual relations are
$$
\boxed{[h,e]=2e,\qquad[h,f]=-2f,\qquad[e,f]=-h.}
$$
In particular, $[e,f]v_1=2v_1\ne-2v_1=hv_1$ in <characteristic zero>; the requested equality cannot hold as printed. Setting
$$
\boxed{E=-e,\qquad H=h,\qquad F=f.}
$$
corrects the sign and gives $[H,E]=2E$, $[H,F]=-2F$, $[E,F]=H$. These are the standard <sl2 triple> relations, so the standard <basis> of the <sl2 Lie algebra> acts on $V$ by $E,H,F$, giving a <Lie algebra representation>.

For completeness, these operators are <linearly independent>: applying $aE+bH+cF=0$ to $v_0$ gives $c=0$; applying it to $v_1$ then gives $b=0$; applying it to $v_2$ gives $a=0$. Hence the printed operators span a <Lie subalgebra> isomorphic to the <sl2 Lie algebra>, even though their printed normalization fails. Replacing the coefficient $i-1$ in the printed definition of $e(v_i)$ by $1-i$ is one equivalent way to repair the statement.

Use the corrected <Lie algebra representation> $E=-e$, $H=h$, $F=f$ from part (i). A <vector subspace> is invariant under $E,H,F$ exactly when it is invariant under the printed $e,h,f$, since changing the sign of a <linear map> does not change its <invariant subspaces>. Define
$$
W=\bigoplus_{i\geq1}kv_i.
$$
This is a nonzero proper <submodule>: $H$ preserves each $kv_i$, $F$ increases the index, $E(v_1)=0$, and $E(v_i)=-(i-1)v_{i-1}\in W$ for $i\geq2$.

We must rule out <submodules> consisting only of mixtures of <weight vectors>. Let $U\ne0$ be any <submodule>, and choose $u=\sum_{j\in S}a_jv_j\in U$ with $S$ finite and every $a_j\ne0$. The <eigenvalues> $-2j$ of $H$ are distinct. For $i\in S$, the <Lagrange interpolation polynomial>
$$
p_i(t)=\prod_{\substack{j\in S\\j\ne i}}\frac{t+2j}{-2i+2j}
$$
satisfies $p_i(H)u=a_iv_i$. Since $U$ is invariant under $H$, it is invariant under every <polynomial> in $H$, so $v_i\in U$.

If $i=0$, repeated applications of $F$ give $F^mv_0=m!v_m$, and $U=V$. If $i\geq1$, repeated applications of $E$ give a nonzero multiple of $v_1$, because the successive coefficients $-(i-1),\ldots,-1$ are nonzero in <characteristic zero>. Repeated applications of $F$ to $v_1$ then give every $v_j$ with $j\geq1$, so $W\subseteq U$.

Finally, if such a $U$ contains any vector outside $W$, that vector has a nonzero coefficient of $v_0$; the same <polynomial interpolation> extracts $v_0$, forcing $U=V$. Hence the complete list of <submodules> is $0,W,V$. In particular, $W$ is a <simple module>. The <quotient module> $V/W$ has <basis> $\overline v_0$; $E\overline v_0=H\overline v_0=0$ and $F\overline v_0=\overline v_1=0$. It is the one-dimensional <trivial Lie algebra representation>.

The <module extension> does not split. If $V=W\oplus U$ for a <submodule> $U$, then $U$ would be a one-dimensional <trivial Lie algebra representation>, since it maps isomorphically to $V/W$. But $\ker H=kv_0$ and $Fv_0=v_1\ne0$, so no such $U$ exists. Thus
$$
\boxed{\begin{gathered}W=\bigoplus_{i\geq1}kv_i,\qquad V/W\cong k_{\mathrm{triv}},\\\text{Submodules: }0,W,V;\qquad V\text{ is not semisimple}.\end{gathered}}
$$
Here <semisimple module> means a <direct sum of modules> that are <simple modules>, equivalently a module in which every <submodule> has a complementary <submodule>. This is the <nonsplit zero-weight Verma extension for sl2>: rescaling to $w_i=i!v_i$ gives $Fw_i=w_{i+1}$ and $Ew_i=i(1-i)w_{i-1}$. Its <infinite-dimensional vector space> is outside the hypotheses of the <Weyl complete reducibility theorem>.

The <normalizer of a Lie subalgebra> and the <centralizer of a Lie subalgebra> are
$$
N_{\mathfrak g}(\mathfrak a)=\{x:[x,\mathfrak a]\subseteq\mathfrak a\},\qquad C_{\mathfrak g}(\mathfrak a)=\{x:[x,\mathfrak a]=0\}.
$$
The second is always contained in the first. Conversely, let $x\in N_{\mathfrak g}(\mathfrak a)$ and $a\in\mathfrak a$. Then $[a,x]\in\mathfrak a$, and, because $\mathfrak a$ is an <abelian Lie algebra>,
$$
(\operatorname{ad}a)^2x=[a,[a,x]]=0.
$$
By the definition of a <toral subalgebra>, $\operatorname{ad}a$ is <diagonalizable>. A <diagonalizable> <linear map> $T$ satisfies $\ker T^2=\ker T$: in an <eigenbasis>, both <kernels> consist of exactly the <eigenvectors> whose <eigenvalue> is zero. Thus $[a,x]=0$. This holds for every $a\in\mathfrak a$, so $x\in C_{\mathfrak g}(\mathfrak a)$ and
$$
\boxed{N_{\mathfrak g}(\mathfrak a)=C_{\mathfrak g}(\mathfrak a).}
$$
This is the <normalizer-centralizer equality for a toral abelian subalgebra>; it does not require $\mathfrak g$ to be a <semisimple Lie algebra>.

We use the supplied <weight decomposition> $V=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\Psi(\mathfrak a)}V_\lambda$. There is a small distinction to justify: the definition of a <toral subalgebra> uses the <Adjoint representation>, while these <weight spaces> use the <defining representation>. For the <symplectic Lie algebra>, <adjoint semisimplicity in the symplectic Lie algebra> implies that each $a\in\mathfrak a$ is <diagonalizable> on $V$ as well. Here is a direct verification.

Define the <symplectic adjoint> $a^*$ by $\omega(av,w)=\omega(v,a^*w)$. The <symplectic Lie algebra> condition is $a^*=-a$. Write the <Jordan–Chevalley decomposition> in $\operatorname{End}(V)$ as $a=S+N$, with $S$ <diagonalizable>, $N$ a <nilpotent linear map>, and $SN=NS$. Uniqueness of the <Jordan–Chevalley decomposition>, applied to $a^*=-a$, gives $S^*=-S$ and $N^*=-N$. Thus $S,N\in\mathfrak{sp}(V)$. On $\operatorname{End}(V)$, $\operatorname{ad}S$ is <diagonalizable> by using a <matrix unit> <basis> in an <eigenbasis> of $S$, and $\operatorname{ad}N$ is a <nilpotent linear map>, since
$$
(\operatorname{ad}N)^m(T)=\sum_{j=0}^m(-1)^j\binom mjN^{m-j}TN^j
$$
vanishes for large $m$. These operators commute and preserve $\mathfrak{sp}(V)$, so their restrictions are the <Jordan–Chevalley decomposition> of $\operatorname{ad}a$. Because $\operatorname{ad}a$ is <diagonalizable>, its nilpotent part is zero: $\operatorname{ad}N=0$ on $\mathfrak{sp}(V)$.

To conclude that $N=0$ without an assumption on the <center of a Lie algebra>, observe that each map $T_v(w)=\omega(v,w)v$ lies in $\mathfrak{sp}(V)$. If $v\ne0$, its image is $kv$, because $\omega$ is a <nondegenerate bilinear form>. Since $N$ commutes with $T_v$, it preserves $kv$. Hence $Nv\in kv$ for every $v$, forcing $N$ to be a scalar <linear map>: compare its action on two <linearly independent> vectors and their sum. A scalar <nilpotent linear map> is zero. For $V=0$ the assertion is immediate. This proves the needed <diagonalizability>. Commuting <diagonalizable> <linear maps> admit <simultaneous diagonalization>, giving the displayed <weight decomposition>.

Now let $v\in V_\lambda$ and $w\in V_\mu$. For every $a\in\mathfrak a$, preservation of the <alternating bilinear form> gives
$$
0=\omega(av,w)+\omega(v,aw)=(\lambda(a)+\mu(a))\omega(v,w).
$$
If $\lambda+\mu\ne0$ as a <linear functional>, choose $a$ on which it is nonzero; then $\omega(v,w)=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\omega(V_\lambda,V_\mu)=0\quad\text{unless }\mu=-\lambda.}
$$
For $v\ne0$ in $V_\lambda$, the fact that $\omega$ is a <nondegenerate bilinear form> supplies a $w\in V$ with $\omega(v,w)\ne0$. Decomposing $w$ into <weight spaces>, only its $V_{-\lambda}$ component can contribute. Thus $V_{-\lambda}\ne0$, and $\Psi(\mathfrak a)=-\Psi(\mathfrak a)$. The same argument on both sides shows that $\omega:V_\lambda\times V_{-\lambda}\to k$ is a <perfect pairing>, so $\dim V_\lambda=\dim V_{-\lambda}$. On $V_0$, the restriction of $\omega$ is a <nondegenerate bilinear form> that is an <alternating bilinear form>, so $\dim V_0$ is even.

Choose one representative $\lambda_i$ from each pair of nonzero <weights of a representation>, for $1\leq i\leq r$. Here
$$
r=\frac{|\Psi(\mathfrak a)\setminus\{0\}|}{2}.
$$
The <linear map> $\mathfrak a\to k^r$ sending $a$ to $(\lambda_1(a),\ldots,\lambda_r(a))$ is injective: an element in its <kernel> acts by zero on every nonzero <weight space>, and also on $V_0$, so it is the zero element of $\mathfrak a\subseteq\operatorname{End}(V)$. Taking <dimensions> yields
$$
\boxed{\dim\mathfrak a\leq r=\frac{|\Psi(\mathfrak a)\setminus\{0\}|}{2}\leq n.}
$$
The final inequality follows because each pair contributes at least two to $\dim V=2n$. Equivalently, the nonzero <weights of a representation> span the <dual space> $\mathfrak a^*$; they need not be <linearly independent> at this stage.

\boxed{C_{\mathfrak{sp}(V)}(\mathfrak a)\cong\mathfrak{sp}(V_0)\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^r\mathfrak{gl}(V_{\lambda_i}).}
$$
To prove it, a <linear map> commuting with every $a\in\mathfrak a$ must preserve every <weight space>: if $v\in V_\lambda$, then $a(Tv)=T(av)=\lambda(a)Tv$. Conversely, preserving the <weight spaces> makes a <linear map> commute with the scalar action of every $a$ on each space.

On $V_0$, the <symplectic Lie algebra> condition says exactly that the restriction belongs to $\mathfrak{sp}(V_0)$. On $V_{\lambda_i}\oplus V_{-\lambda_i}$, use the <perfect pairing> from part (b) to identify $V_{-\lambda_i}$ with the <dual space> $V_{\lambda_i}^*$. The action $A_i$ on $V_{\lambda_i}$ can be arbitrary, but its action on $V_{-\lambda_i}$ must be $-A_i^*$, where $A_i^*$ is the <dual map>. Indeed,
$$
\omega(A_iv,w)+\omega(v,B_iw)=0\quad\text{for all }v,w
$$
determines $B_i=-A_i^*$ uniquely. There are no additional conditions between different pairs because $\omega$ vanishes between different pairs. Every choice of these blocks therefore gives an element of the <centralizer of a Lie subalgebra>. The block correspondence preserves <Lie brackets>, proving the claimed <direct sum of Lie algebras>.

The <general linear Lie algebra> $\mathfrak{gl}_m(k)$ is an <abelian Lie algebra> exactly when $m\leq1$: for $m\geq2$, the <matrix units> satisfy $[E_{11},E_{12}]=E_{12}\ne0$. A nonzero <symplectic vector space> has a <symplectic basis> and contains a two-dimensional <symplectic subspace>. The <symplectic Lie algebra> acting on that plane, extended by zero on its <symplectic orthogonal complement>, contains the <matrices> $H=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$ and $E=E_{12}$ with $[H,E]=2E\ne0$. Thus $\mathfrak{sp}(V_0)$ is an <abelian Lie algebra> exactly when $V_0=0$.

Since different summands in a <direct sum of Lie algebras> commute, we obtain the precise criterion
$$
\boxed{C_{\mathfrak{sp}(V)}(\mathfrak a)\text{ is abelian}\iff\begin{gathered}V_0=0,\\\dim V_\lambda=1\quad(0\ne\lambda\in\Psi(\mathfrak a)).\end{gathered}}
$$
Equivalently, there are $2n$ distinct nonzero <weights of a representation>, each with a one-dimensional <weight space>. The condition concerns multiplicities in the <defining representation>; it does not assert that $\dim\mathfrak a=n$ for every such $\mathfrak a$.

= Solution
{parent=d}
{c}

Use the standard structural fact that $\mathfrak{sp}(V)$ is a <semisimple Lie algebra> over an <algebraically closed field> of <characteristic zero>. If $\mathfrak h$ is a <Cartan subalgebra>, part (a)(ii) gives $C_{\mathfrak{sp}(V)}(\mathfrak h)=\mathfrak h$, and $\mathfrak h$ is an <abelian Lie algebra>. Part (c) therefore shows that its <weight spaces> occur as $n$ pairs of one-dimensional spaces, with no zero <weight space>.

Choose representatives $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$ of its nonzero <weights of a representation>. Pick $p_i\ne0$ in $V_{\lambda_i}$ and $q_i\ne0$ in $V_{-\lambda_i}$, scaling $q_i$ so that $\omega(p_i,q_i)=1$. Part (b) shows that all other pairings among different pairs are zero. Each individual <weight space> is one-dimensional and the <bilinear form> is alternating, so the ordered <basis>
$$
\mathcal B=(p_1,\ldots,p_n,q_1,\ldots,q_n)
$$
is a <symplectic basis>. Every $h\in\mathfrak h$ is a <diagonal matrix> in this <basis>, with entries $\lambda_1(h),\ldots,\lambda_n(h),-\lambda_1(h),\ldots,-\lambda_n(h)$.

Let $D_{\mathcal B}$ be the <Lie subalgebra> consisting of all <diagonal matrices>
$$
D_{\mathcal B}=\{\operatorname{diag}(t_1,\ldots,t_n,-t_1,\ldots,-t_n):t_i\in k\}.
$$
Each such <matrix> lies in the <symplectic Lie algebra> and commutes with $\mathfrak h$. Hence
$$
\mathfrak h\subseteq D_{\mathcal B}\subseteq C_{\mathfrak{sp}(V)}(\mathfrak h)=\mathfrak h,
$$
and $\mathfrak h=D_{\mathcal B}$. In particular, every <Cartan subalgebra> has <dimension> $n$; the $\lambda_i$ are a <basis> of $\mathfrak h^*$.

Carry out the same construction for $\mathfrak h'$, obtaining a <symplectic basis> $\mathcal B'=(p'_1,\ldots,p'_n,q'_1,\ldots,q'_n)$ with $\mathfrak h'=D_{\mathcal B'}$. The unique <linear map> $g$ sending $p_i$ to $p'_i$ and $q_i$ to $q'_i$ is invertible and preserves all pairings of <basis vectors>. By <bilinearity>, it preserves $\omega$ on all of $V$, so $g$ belongs to the <symplectic group> $\operatorname{Sp}(V)$. The <change of basis> gives
$$
\boxed{g\mathfrak h g^{-1}=gD_{\mathcal B}g^{-1}=D_{\mathcal B'}=\mathfrak h',\qquad g\in\operatorname{Sp}(V).}
$$
Thus <conjugacy of Cartan subalgebras of the symplectic Lie algebra> follows by explicitly matching their <symplectic bases>. If $n=0$, the <Lie algebra> is zero and the same conclusion holds with $g$ the identity on the zero <vector space>.
