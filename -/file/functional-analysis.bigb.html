= Functional analysis
{wiki}

Functional analysis brings <linear algebra> and <topology> together to study <vector spaces> of <functions> and the <linear operators> acting on them. A <function space> such as $C([0,1])$ has infinitely many independent directions: the <monomials> $1,x,x^2,\ldots$ are <linearly independent>. Finite <linear combinations> alone cannot describe all its <continuous functions>. A useful theory must also say when approximations converge, whether their limits belong to the chosen space, and which operations preserve that <norm convergence>. <Normed vector spaces>, <Banach spaces>, and <bounded linear operators> provide these three controls.https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-102-introduction-to-functional-analysis-spring-2021/4ae9a5af8622dba3aa37461b71d08407_MIT18_102s21_lec1.pdf[MIT 18.102, lecture 1: the infinite-dimensional viewpoint]{ref}

A <bounded linear operator> $T:X\to Y$ between <normed vector spaces> satisfies $\|Tf\|_Y\leq M\|f\|_X$ for every $f$, with one finite constant $M$. The least possible constant is its <operator norm>. By <linearity>, the same estimate controls approximation errors:
$$
\|Tf-Tg\|_Y\leq\|T\|\,\|f-g\|_X.
$$
This is exactly <continuity> for a <linear map> between <normed vector spaces>; neither space needs to be complete. It turns an input accuracy guarantee into an output accuracy guarantee, even when the input has infinitely many degrees of freedom.https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-102-introduction-to-functional-analysis-spring-2021/22bf2f774fb161776032491568148707_MIT18_102s21_lec2.pdf[MIT 18.102, lecture 2: boundedness, continuity, and the operator norm]{ref}

For a concrete <linear functional> on $C([0,1])$, take the <integral> $F(f)=\int_0^1 f(x)\,dx$. In the <supremum norm>,
$$
|F(f)|\leq\int_0^1|f(x)|\,dx\leq\|f\|_\infty.
$$
The constant function $f=1$ attains equality, so $\|F\|=1$. For $f(x)=x^2$, the output is $F(f)=1/3$; any approximation $g$ with $\|g-x^2\|_\infty\leq0.01$ therefore has $|F(g)-1/3|\leq0.01$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> also gives $|F(f)|\leq\|f\|_2\|1\|_2=\|f\|_2$ on this interval, again with equality at $1$. Thus integration remains a <bounded linear functional> under the <L2 norm>, although <point evaluation> behaves very differently.

The beauty of this viewpoint is that one estimate can justify an entire approximation procedure. Once a <sequence> converges in the chosen <norm>, every <bounded linear operator> carries its limit along. <Completeness> ensures that Cauchy approximations actually describe an element of the space. The choice of <norm> matters: it determines which errors are small and which measurements are stable. The examples below distinguish an average, which ignores sufficiently narrow peaks, from <point evaluation>, which detects their full height. For applications, this decides whether reducing an overall fitting error guarantees an accurate reported measurement. A small <L2 norm> of the error controls its <integral> on this interval, while it gives no such guarantee for a single sampled value.

= Superposition operator
{synonym}

The <Nemytskii operator> associated with a scalar function $f$ applies $f$ pointwise to a function. The domain and target <function spaces> must be specified; an arbitrary continuous $f$ need not preserve them. On a finite-measure domain, a bounded continuous $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ defines a continuous map $N_f:L^2\to L^2$ with $\|N_f(u)\|_2\leq\|f\|_\infty|\Omega|^{1/2}$. To prove continuity, extract an almost-everywhere convergent further subsequence from every $L^2$-convergent subsequence and apply the <dominated convergence theorem> to $|f(u_j)-f(u)|^2$. This forces convergence of the entire image sequence in $L^2$.

= Continuous embedding
{parent=Functional analysis}
{title2=$X\hookrightarrow Y$}

For <normed vector spaces> of functions on the same domain, a continuous embedding is an injective inclusion $X\subset Y$ satisfying $\|u\|_Y\leq C\|u\|_X$.

= Compact embedding
{parent=Functional analysis}
{title2=$X\hookrightarrow\hookrightarrow Y$}

A <compact embedding> of <Banach spaces> $X,Y$ is an injective <continuous linear map> $i:X\to Y$ that sends every <bounded set> to a <relatively compact subset>. Equivalently, every bounded <sequence> in $X$ has a <subsequence> converging in $Y$. One identifies $X$ with its image when $i$ is the natural inclusion. The <Rellich-Kondrachov theorem> supplies many <compact embeddings> between <Sobolev spaces> and <Lp spaces> on bounded <domains>.

= Sublinear operator
{parent=Functional analysis}

A nonnegative function-valued operator $S$ is sublinear if $S(f+g)\leq S(f)+S(g)$ and $S(cf)=|c|S(f)$ <almost everywhere>. This pointwise domination notion includes maximal <one-sided interval averaging operators> and differs from requiring an ordinary <linear map>.

= Weak-type operator
{parent=Sublinear operator}
{title2=$\mu\{|Sf|>\lambda\}\leq(C\|f\|_p/\lambda)^q$}

An operator has weak type $(p,q)$, with finite $q$, if the displayed bound holds for every input and every $\lambda>0$, with one constant independent of both. It controls the distribution of its output rather than requiring its strong $L^q$ <norm> to be finite.

= Strong Lp bound from weak L1 and L-infinity bounds
{parent=Weak-type operator}
{title2=$\|Sf\|_p^p\leq2^p q C_1C_\infty^{p-1}\|f\|_p^p$}

Let $S$ be a <sublinear operator> with positive constants and $\mu\{Sf>a\}\leq C_1\|f\|_1/a$ and $\|Sf\|_\infty\leq C_\infty\|f\|_\infty$. Split $f$ into its part above $a/(2C_\infty)$ and its bounded remainder. The latter contributes at most $a/2$, so the weak bound controls the superlevel measure by $2C_1a^{-1}\int_{|f|>a/(2C_\infty)}|f|$. Integrating this estimate with <layer cake representation> proves the displayed bound for $1<p<\infty$ and $q=p/(p-1)$. In particular it gives the strong $L^p$ estimate for the <Hardy-Littlewood maximal function>.

= Weak type with a normed domain
{parent=Weak-type operator}
{title2=$\lambda\{|Tf|>a\}\leq(C\|f\|_E/a)^q$}

For a <normed space> $E$ and $0<q<\infty$, an operator $T:E\to L^0$ is of weak type $(E,q)$ when the displayed distribution bound holds uniformly over $f\in E$ and $a>0$. No completeness of $E$ is needed. A <sublinear operator> has pointwise triangle domination and absolute homogeneity; its outputs may be understood modulo <almost everywhere> equality.

= Dense approximation under a weak-type maximal bound
{parent=Weak type with a normed domain}

Suppose <linear operators> $A_t$ and $A_0$ on a <normed space> satisfy $|A_tf|,|A_0f|\leq Sf$, where $S$ has <weak type with a normed domain>, and all relevant suprema are measurable. If $A_tg\to A_0g$ <almost everywhere> on a dense subspace, the same holds for every input. For a fixed approximation $g$, the limiting error is at most $2S(f-g)$. The measure where it exceeds $a$ is at most $(2C\|f-g\|/a)^q$, which tends to zero as the approximation improves. This is a convergence-to-a-specified-limit version of <almost-everywhere convergence from weak-type domination>.

= Weak type (1,1)
{parent=Weak-type operator}
{title2=$\mu\{Sf>\lambda\}\leq C\|f\|_1/\lambda$}

For a nonnegative <sublinear operator>, weak type $(1,1)$ is the displayed level-set bound for all integrable inputs and positive thresholds. It supplies an exceptional-set estimate for approximation errors; the <uncentered maximal weak-type inequality> is a central example.

= Almost-everywhere convergence from weak-type domination
{parent=Weak type (1,1)}

Suppose a family of <linear operators>, including its limiting operator, is pointwise dominated by a <sublinear operator> of <weak type (1,1)>. Convergence <almost everywhere> on an $L^1$-dense subspace then extends to every $L^1$ input. Approximate the input by errors of <norm> at most $2^{-2n}$. The weak estimate and the <Borel-Cantelli first lemma> make the corresponding domination errors tend to zero <almost everywhere>. <Linearity> bounds the difference between the full and approximating convergence errors by twice that domination error. This argument does not need an uncountable supremum to be measurable.

= Operator algebra
{parent=Functional analysis}
{wiki}

An <operator algebra> is an algebra of <linear operators>, with multiplication given by composition. In quantum examples it can be generated by creation and annihilation operators subject to the <canonical commutation relations> or <canonical anticommutation relations>. Its analytic realization requires specifying a common domain for unbounded operators.

= Quasi-local observable algebra
{parent=Operator algebra}
{title2=$\mathcal A=\overline{\bigcup_\Lambda\mathcal A_\Lambda}^{\|\cdot\|}$}

For a lattice system, finite-region observable algebras $\mathcal A_\Lambda$ embed into larger ones by tensoring with the identity. Their union has a norm closure called the <quasi-local observable algebra>, a <C-star algebra>. It describes finite-support observables and norm limits of such observables. An operator flipping every spin of an infinite lattice need not belong to it: successive finite-region flips are not norm-Cauchy. Restrictions to this algebra can therefore lose relative-phase information retained by increasingly nonlocal finite-system observables.

= Cyclic vector for an operator algebra
{parent=Operator algebra}
{title2=$\overline{A\Omega}=H$}

A <vector> is cyclic for an <operator algebra> when its orbit under that algebra has dense linear span. This is distinct from requiring the powers of one chosen operator to span the space. If the algebra is linear, its orbit itself is already a <vector> subspace.

= Separating vector for an operator algebra
{parent=Cyclic vector for an operator algebra}
{title2=$a\Omega=0\Longrightarrow a=0$}

= Separating vector
{synonym}

A <vector> is separating for an <operator algebra> when evaluation at that <vector> is injective. A cyclic <vector> for the <commutant of an operator algebra> is separating for the original algebra: if $a\Omega=0$, then $a$ vanishes on its dense commutant orbit.

= Tomita operator
{c}
{parent=Separating vector for an operator algebra}
{title2=$S_0(a\Omega)=a^*\Omega$}

For a <Von Neumann algebra> with a cyclic separating <vector>, this is a densely defined antilinear operator. It is closable: the commutant orbit lies in the domain of its antilinear adjoint and is dense. Write $S$ for its <closure> and $S=J\Delta^{1/2}$ for its polar decomposition. In finite dimension it is everywhere defined and invertible. For a tracial <vector>, $S_0$ is isometric and extends to an antiunitary involution.

= Modular operator
{parent=Tomita operator}
{title2=$\Delta=S^*S$}

The <positive operator> in the polar decomposition $S=J\Delta^{1/2}$ of the <Tomita operator>. In a matrix-algebra standard representation with cyclic <vector> $D>0$, it is $\Delta(X)=D^2XD^{-2}$. For a tracial <vector> it is the identity.

= Modular conjugation
{parent=Tomita operator}
{title2=$J$}

The antiunitary factor in the polar decomposition of the <Tomita operator>. In the finite-dimensional and tracial settings it satisfies $J^2=I$ and $JMJ=M'$. In a matrix-algebra standard representation by left multiplication, with positive invertible cyclic <vector> $D$, it acts by $J(X)=X^*$.

= Von Neumann algebra
{c}
{parent=Operator algebra}
{wiki}
{title2=$M=M''\subseteq B(H)$}

A <unital> <star-subalgebra> of the <bounded operators> on a <Hilbert space> that is closed in the <weak operator topology>, equivalently in the <strong operator topology>. The <Von Neumann double commutant theorem> makes these conditions equivalent to equality with the double <commutant of an operator algebra>. It is <norm> closed and hence a <C-star algebra>.

= Hilbert space module over a von Neumann algebra
{c}
{parent=Von Neumann algebra}

= Hilbert space module
{c}
{synonym}

Here a <Hilbert space module> means a <Hilbert space> with a <unital> star-representation of a <Von Neumann algebra>, rather than a Hilbert C-star module with an algebra-valued <inner product>. A closed <invariant subspace> is reducing because the algebra is closed under adjoints. Module equivalence means an intertwining unitary. The <polar decomposition of a bounded operator> converts an intertwiner into a unitary between its initial and final support subspaces.

= Schröder-Bernstein theorem for Hilbert space modules
{c}
{parent=Hilbert space module over a von Neumann algebra}

Two <Hilbert space modules> are unitarily equivalent if each embeds isometrically as a closed submodule of the other. For isometric intertwiners $u:H_1\to H_2$ and $v:H_2\to H_1$, let $D=H_1\ominus vH_2$ and $K=\bigoplus_{n\geq0}(vu)^nD$. These summands are orthogonal. The operator acting as $u$ on $K$ and as $v^*$ on $K^\perp$ is the required unitary onto $H_2$.

= Von Neumann factor
{c}
{parent=Von Neumann algebra}
{title2=$Z(M)=\mathbb CI$}

A <Von Neumann algebra> whose center consists exactly of scalar multiples of the identity. A <group von Neumann algebra> is a factor exactly when every nonidentity <conjugacy class> is infinite. A central operator has a square-summable coefficient <vector> constant on those classes; an infinite class must therefore have coefficient zero. Conversely, the sum of left translations over a finite nonidentity class is a nonscalar central operator.

= Group von Neumann algebra
{parent=Von Neumann algebra}
{title2=$L(\Gamma)=\lambda(\Gamma)''$}

For a discrete <group>, its <group von Neumann algebra> is generated on $\ell^2(\Gamma)$ by left translations $\lambda(g)\delta_h=\delta_{gh}$. The <vector> $\delta_e$ is cyclic and separating, and its <vector> functional is tracial. Its <modular conjugation> acts by $J\delta_g=\delta_{g^{-1}}$ with conjugated scalar coefficients. The commutant is the algebra generated by right translations $\rho(g)\delta_h=\delta_{hg^{-1}}$.

= Exponentially weighted supremum norm
{title2=$\|u\|_\alpha=\sup_te^{-\alpha t}\|u(t)\|$}
{parent=Functional analysis}

On continuous functions $u:[0,T]\to X$, the exponentially weighted supremum norm is $\|u\|_\alpha=\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}e^{-\alpha t}\|u(t)\|$. It is equivalent to the ordinary supremum norm and often turns a Volterra integral map into a <contraction mapping>.

= Operator topology
{parent=Functional analysis}
{wiki}

An operator topology specifies convergence of linear operators. Common choices include the norm, strong, and weak operator topologies.

= Strong operator topology
{parent=Operator topology}
{wiki}

Operators $T_n$ converge to $T$ in the strong operator topology when $T_nx\to Tx$ in norm for every vector $x$.

= Strong-star operator topology
{parent=Strong operator topology}
{title2=$T_i\xi\to T\xi,\quad T_i^*\xi\to T^*\xi\quad(\xi\in H)$}

= Strong-star topology
{synonym}

This topology on <bounded operators> is generated by the seminorms $(\|T\xi\|^2+\|T^*\xi\|^2)^{1/2}$. A net converges exactly when both it and its adjoint net converge in the <strong operator topology>. It is the topology used in the full-unit-ball version of the <Kaplansky density theorem>.

= Weak operator topology
{parent=Operator topology}
{wiki}

Operators $T_n$ converge to $T$ in the weak operator topology when $\langle T_nx,y\rangle\to\langle Tx,y\rangle$ for every pair of vectors $x,y$.

= Operator predual from matrix coefficients
{parent=Weak operator topology}

For a <Hilbert space> $H$, the linear span of the matrix-coefficient functionals $T\mapsto\langle Tx,y\rangle$ is a predual of $\mathcal B(H)$. The unit ball is compact in the resulting <weak operator topology> by the <Tychonoff theorem>, and the <compact norming dual-pair criterion> identifies $\mathcal B(H)$ with the dual of that span.

= Schauder basis
{c}
{parent=Functional analysis}
{wiki}

A Schauder basis of a <Banach space> $X$ is a sequence $(e_n)$ such that every $x\in X$ has a unique norm-convergent expansion $x=\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_ne_n$.

= Uniform partial-sum criterion for a Schauder basis
{parent=Schauder basis}
{title2=$\sup_n\|P_n\|<\infty\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x=\sum_n f_n(x)x_n$}

For a countable <minimal total family in a normed space> in a <Banach space>, let $(x_n,f_n)$ be its unique <biorthogonal system> and let $P_nx=\sum_{j\leq n}f_j(x)x_j$. Uniform boundedness of these projections implies $P_nx\to x$ for every $x$: approximate $x$ by a finite linear combination $y$, eventually fixed by $P_n$, and bound the error by $(1+\sup_n\|P_n\|)\|x-y\|$. Conversely, if every <vector> has a norm-convergent expansion in this family, applying each continuous $f_j$ shows that its coefficients must be $f_j(x)$. Thus $P_nx\to x$, so the projections are pointwise bounded. The <uniform boundedness principle> gives their uniform operator-norm bound. This proves the criterion and uniqueness of all expansions.

= Block basic sequence
{parent=Schauder basis}

Relative to a <Schauder basis> $(e_j)$, a block basic sequence consists of nonzero finite combinations supported on successive disjoint coordinate intervals. In the <l-p sequence space>, disjointly supported vectors $u_n$ of norm one satisfy $\|\sum a_nu_n\|_p^p=\sum|a_n|^p$. Their closed span is consequently isometric to the <l-p sequence space>.

= Small perturbation of a complemented block basis
{parent=Block basic sequence}

Suppose $(u_n)$ is a normalized <block basic sequence> in the <l-p sequence space>, $1\le p<\infty$, and $\sum_n\|x_n-u_n\|_p<1$. Choose norm-one <linear functionals> $\phi_n$ supported on the respective blocks with $\phi_n(u_n)=1$. The <bounded linear operator> $A=I+\sum_n\phi_n(\cdot)(x_n-u_n)$ is invertible by the <Neumann series>. The block projection $Qz=\sum_n\phi_n(z)u_n$ has norm at most one, and $AQA^{-1}$ projects onto the closed span of $(x_n)$. Thus that span is a <complemented subspace> isomorphic to the <l-p sequence space>.

= Block sequence
{parent=Schauder basis}

Relative to a <Schauder basis>, a block sequence consists of nonzero finitely supported vectors with successive disjoint supports: $\max\operatorname{supp}x_i<\min\operatorname{supp}x_{i+1}$. It is a <basic sequence>, with <basis constant> at most that of the original basis.

= Block subspace
{parent=Block sequence}

A block subspace is the closed linear span of an infinite <block sequence>. Passing to a block subspace preserves the order of supports and permits successive-vector constructions.

= Gowers Ramsey theorem for Banach spaces
{c}
{parent=Block subspace}

If an analytic family of finite normalized <block sequences> meets every infinite-dimensional block subspace, then after an arbitrarily prescribed coordinatewise perturbation it is strategically large in some block subspace. In the block game, one player chooses an infinite-dimensional block subspace at each turn and the other chooses a successive unit vector from it; strategically large means the vector player can force a finite initial segment into the family.

= Basis projection
{title2=$P_n$}
{parent=Schauder basis}

The nth basis projection associated with a <Schauder basis> is
$$
P_n\left(\sum_{k=1}^\infty a_ke_k\right)=\sum_{k=1}^na_ke_k.
$$

= Basis constant
{parent=Basis projection}

The basis constant of a <Schauder basis> is $\sup_n\lVert P_n\rVert$, where $P_n$ are its <basis projection>[basis projections]. Uniform boundedness makes this supremum finite.

= Coordinate functional of a Schauder basis
{title2=$e_n^*$}
{parent=Schauder basis}

The nth coordinate functional of a <Schauder basis> is the bounded <linear functional> $e_n^*(\sum_ka_ke_k)=a_n$.

= Dual sequence of a Schauder basis
{title2=$(e_n^*)$}
{parent=Coordinate functional of a Schauder basis}

The dual sequence consists of the coordinate functionals $(e_n^*)$ in $X^*$. It is always a <basic sequence>, though it need not span the entire dual space.

= Shrinking Schauder basis
{parent=Dual sequence of a Schauder basis}

A Schauder basis is shrinking when its <dual sequence of a Schauder basis>[dual sequence] is a Schauder basis of the whole dual space. Every Schauder basis of a <reflexive Banach space> is shrinking.

= Basic sequence
{parent=Schauder basis}
{wiki}

A basic sequence in a Banach space is a sequence that is a <Schauder basis> for its closed linear span.

= Rosenthal l1 theorem
{c}
{title2=$\ell^1$}
{parent=Basic sequence}

Every bounded sequence in a <Banach space> has either a <weakly Cauchy sequence> as a subsequence or a subsequence equivalent to the unit vector basis of $\ell^1$. The real-scalar proof represents vectors as continuous functions on a compact dual ball and uses infinite Ramsey theory to select independent lower and upper level sets when pointwise convergence cannot be obtained.

= Bourgain l1-index
{c}
{parent=Basic sequence}
{title2=$I_1(X)=\sup_{K\in\mathbb N}o(T_K(X))$}

For $K\geq1$, let $T_K(X)$ consist of finite normalized sequences satisfying $K^{-1}\sum|a_i|\leq\|\sum a_ix_i\|\leq\sum|a_i|$ for every scalar combination. Its tree order measures the depth of finite copies of the unit vector basis of $\ell^1$. A separable <Banach space> containing no copy of $\ell^1$ has a countable index because every such tree is closed and well-founded. Countable well-founded trees can be used to construct separable reflexive spaces with arbitrarily high index, precluding a separable reflexive universal space.

= Unconditional basic sequence
{parent=Basic sequence}

An unconditional basic sequence is a <Schauder basis> for its closed span whose expansions converge after arbitrary permutations or coordinate suppressions. Equivalently, the finite coordinate projections are uniformly bounded. Failure of this property gives pairs of disjointly supported unit vectors at arbitrarily small distance, obtained by separating a finite combination with a large coordinate projection.

= Unconditional tree index
{parent=Unconditional basic sequence}
{title2=$o(T_C(X))$}

Let $T_C(X)$ consist of finite normalized sequences for which every scalar combination and sign change satisfy $\|\sum_i\varepsilon_i a_ix_i\|\leq C\|\sum_i a_ix_i\|$. These inequalities give a closed <tree on a Polish space> when $X$ is separable. An infinite branch is an <unconditional basic sequence>. If there is no such sequence, each fixed-constant tree is well-founded and has countable <rank of a well-founded tree>. Consequently, finite unconditional configurations with one fixed constant and arbitrarily high countable ranks force an infinite unconditional sequence.

= Suppression-unconditional basic sequence
{parent=Unconditional basic sequence}
{title2=$\|\sum_{i\in A}a_ie_i\|\leq C\|\sum_i a_ie_i\|$}

A basic sequence is suppression-unconditional with constant $C$ if every coordinate deletion satisfies the displayed inequality. When $C=1$, deletion never increases the norm. Uniformly bounded finite-coordinate projections imply unconditional convergence of the basis expansions; for real scalars, the sign-change constant is at most $2C$.

= Spreading model
{parent=Basic sequence}
{title2=$\|\sum a_ie_i\|=\lim_{n_1\to\infty}\|\sum a_ix_{n_i}\|$}

A normalized <basic sequence> generates a spreading model when the norms of all finite scalar combinations converge as the first index tends to infinity, uniformly over increasing choices of the indices. The limiting norm on finitely supported scalar sequences is unchanged by increasing relabellings of coordinates. Completing this norm gives a <Banach space> with its canonical <basic sequence>. A diagonal application of the finite-colour infinite <Ramsey theorem> produces such a model from a subsequence of every normalized basic sequence.

= Basis selection theorem
{parent=Basic sequence}

If a bounded subset $K$ of a Banach space is bounded away from zero and zero lies in its <weak closure>, then $K$ contains a <basic sequence>. More generally, weak closure may be replaced by closure in any Hausdorff locally convex vector topology weaker than the weak topology.

= Reflexive space
{parent=Functional analysis}
{wiki}

A locally convex <topological vector space> is reflexive when its canonical evaluation map to its continuous bidual is an isomorphism with the specified strong dual topologies. For a <Banach space>, this reduces to surjectivity of the canonical map and gives a <reflexive Banach space>.

= Reflexive Banach space
{parent=Reflexive space}

A Banach space $X$ is reflexive when its canonical embedding $J_X:X\to X^{**}$ is surjective. Equivalently, its closed unit ball is <weakly compact set>[weakly compact].

= Evaluation onto the dual of a reflexive dual subspace
{parent=Reflexive Banach space}
{title2=$T:Z\twoheadrightarrow X^*,\quad(Tz)(x)=x(z)$}

If $Z$ is a <Banach space> and $X$ is a norm-closed reflexive <linear subspace> of $Z^*$, evaluation gives the displayed surjection. Restriction $R:Z^{**}\to X^*$ is <weak-star topology>[weak-star] continuous and maps the closed <unit ball> onto the target ball by the <Hahn-Banach theorem>. The <Goldstine theorem> then makes $T(B_Z)$ weak-star dense in $B_{X^*}$, where $T=R J_Z$. Reflexivity of $X$ makes this target <weak-star topology> equal to its <weak topology>. Convexity and the <Hahn-Banach separation theorem> convert weak density of this ball image to <norm> density.

To pass from density to surjectivity, approximate a desired $f$ by $Tz_1$ with $\|z_1\|\leq\|f\|$ and residual at most $\|f\|/2$. Repeat on each residual. The chosen <vectors> have summable <norms>, with sum at most $2\|f\|$, and the residuals tend to zero geometrically. Completeness of $Z$ produces $z=\sum_nz_n$ with $Tz=f$. Using any smaller residual ratio proves the infimum lifting <norm> equals $\|f\|$, although that infimum need not be attained. This argument proves onto-ness, rather than merely density of the evaluation functionals.

= Norm minimizer in a closed convex subset of a reflexive Banach space
{parent=Reflexive Banach space}
{title2=$\min_{x\in C}\|x\|$}

A nonempty norm-closed convex subset $C$ of a <reflexive Banach space> attains its distance to zero. For $d=\inf_C\|x\|$, the sets $C\cap(d+1/n)B_X$ are nonempty weakly closed subsets of one weakly compact ball, and form a decreasing family. Compactness gives a point in their intersection, of norm $d$. <Mazur theorem> supplies weak closedness of $C$ and the balls; no sequential-compactness theorem is required.

= Weak compactness characterization of reflexivity
{parent=Reflexive Banach space}

A <Banach space> is <reflexive Banach space>[reflexive] if and only if its closed unit ball is compact in the <weak topology>. One direction applies the <Banach--Alaoglu theorem> to the unit ball in the bidual; the converse follows because <Goldstine theorem> makes the canonical image of the unit ball weak-star dense, while weak compactness makes that image weak-star closed.

= Weak sequential compactness in a Hilbert space
{parent=Weak compactness characterization of reflexivity}
{title2=$\sup_j\|u_j\|_H<\infty\Longrightarrow u_{j_k}\rightharpoonup u$}

Every bounded sequence in a <Hilbert space> has a weakly convergent subsequence. The closed span of a sequence is separable; diagonal convergence of coordinates along an <orthonormal basis> gives a bounded limiting <linear functional>, represented by a vector of the <Hilbert space>. Applied to a bounded family of mollifications, this proves that their strong Lebesgue limit inherits the <weak derivative> bound.

= Weak sequential compactness of bounded sequences in a reflexive Banach space
{parent=Weak compactness characterization of reflexivity}

Every bounded sequence in a <reflexive Banach space> has a weakly convergent subsequence. This is the sequential form of weak compactness supplied by the <Eberlein-Smulian theorem>.

= Weakly compact operator
{parent=Functional analysis}

A linear operator $T:X\to Y$ is weakly compact when $T(B_X)$ is <relatively weakly compact set>[relatively weakly compact] in $Y$.

= Bidual characterization of weakly compact operators
{parent=Weakly compact operator}
{title2=$T^{**}(X^{**})\subseteq J_YY$}

A <bounded linear operator> $T:X\to Y$ between <Banach spaces> is a <weakly compact operator> exactly when its second adjoint takes values in the canonical copy of $Y$ inside $Y^{**}$. The <Goldstine theorem> proves necessity; the <Banach-Alaoglu theorem>, continuity of the second adjoint, and the <Mazur theorem> prove sufficiency.

= Weak compactness of an operator and its adjoint
{parent=Bidual characterization of weakly compact operators}
{title2=$T\text{ weakly compact}\Longleftrightarrow T^*\text{ weakly compact}$}

A <bounded linear operator> between <Banach spaces> is weakly compact exactly when its <Banach-space adjoint> is weakly compact. Equivalently, that adjoint is continuous from its domain's <weak-star topology> to its range's <weak topology>. For the reverse direction, the bidual condition applied to $T^*$ forces $T^{***}$ to annihilate every functional on $Y^{**}$ vanishing on $J_YY$; the <Hahn-Banach theorem> then places $T^{**}(X^{**})$ inside $J_YY$.

= Gantmacher theorem
{c}
{parent=Weakly compact operator}

For Banach spaces $X,Y$ and bounded $T:X\to Y$, the following are equivalent: $T$ is weakly compact; $T^{**}(X^{**})\subseteq J_Y(Y)$; and $T^*$ is weakly compact.

= Operator ideal of weakly compact operators
{title2=$\mathcal W(X,Y)$}
{parent=Weakly compact operator}

Weakly compact operators form a norm-closed vector subspace of $\mathcal B(X,Y)$ and have the ideal property: bounded compositions on either side of a weakly compact operator remain weakly compact.

= Pitt theorem

Pitt's theorem implies in particular that every bounded operator $c_0\to\ell^1$ is compact. Thus two nonreflexive Banach spaces can have only weakly compact operators between them.

= Function space
{parent=Functional analysis}
{wiki}

A function space is a collection of <functions> with common domain and codomain, often supplied with a <vector space> structure and a <norm> or <topology>. Examples include <L2 spaces> and <Sobolev spaces>.

= Space of continuous compactly supported functions
{parent=Function space}
{title2=$C_c(X)$}

For a locally compact Hausdorff space $X$, $C_c(X)$ consists of continuous functions whose <support> is compact. A <positive linear functional> on this space, bounded on each fixed compact support, is represented by a <Radon measure>. For a locally compact group with <Haar measure>, these functions are dense in $L^p$ for $1\leq p<\infty$.

= Skorokhod space
{parent=Function space}
{c}
{title2=$D([0,1],\mathbb R)$}

The Skorokhod space consists of real paths that are <right-continuous> and have a finite left limit at every positive time. Its $J_1$ topology allows small changes of time: $x_n\to x$ when there are increasing homeomorphisms $\lambda_n$ of $[0,1]$ such that $\|\lambda_n-\mathrm{id}\|_\infty\to0$ and $\|x_n\circ\lambda_n-x\|_\infty\to0$, where $\mathrm{id}$ denotes the identity time map. This topology aligns nearby jumps rather than insisting on uniform closeness at identical times. The step-path version of the <Donsker invariance principle> converges here to continuous <Brownian motion>; its polygonal version uses the ordinary <supremum norm> on continuous paths.

= Integral operator
{parent=Functional analysis}

An integral operator maps a function $f$ to a function of the form
$$
(Tf)(x)=\int K(x,y)f(y)\,dy.
$$
A bounded continuous kernel on a compact domain defines a bounded operator in the uniform norm.

= Singular integral operator
{parent=Integral operator}
{title2=$Tf(x)=\operatorname{PV}\int K(x,t)f(t)\,dt$}

A singular <integral> operator has a kernel whose ordinary <integral> can fail at points on the integration domain. A specified limiting prescription, such as a <Cauchy principal value>, can still define the operator by cancellation. For the <Hilbert transform> kernel $1/(t-x)$, subtracting $f(x)$ from a locally Hölder-<continuous> density leaves $[f(t)-f(x)]/(t-x)$ locally integrable; the constant part is interpreted by symmetric deletion. The limiting prescription and the class of allowed densities are part of the operator's definition, not optional details.

= Singular integral equation
{parent=Singular integral operator}
{title2=$a(t)f(t)+b(t)\operatorname{PV}\int_\Gamma K(t,\zeta)f(\zeta)d\zeta=c(t)$}

An integral equation with a kernel singular on the integration <contour> needs a prescribed limiting interpretation such as a <Cauchy principal value>. For the Cauchy kernel, the <Sokhotski–Plemelj theorem> converts a linear scalar equation into a <Riemann-Hilbert problem> for its two analytic boundary values. Normalization, endpoint regularity and the index of that jump determine homogeneous freedom and solvability.

{synonym}

= Finite-rank projection kernel

= Orthogonal polynomial projection kernel

For real <monic orthogonal polynomials> $p_j$ with squared weighted norms $h_j$ under a positive weight $w$, the <functions> $p_j\sqrt{w/h_j}$ form an <orthonormal set> for the unweighted reference measure. = Volterra integration operator
{c}
{synonym}

The Volterra integration operator on $L^2([0,1])$ is compact and injective, but its inverse is differentiation and is unbounded. Its singular values $2/((2n-1)\pi)$ tend to zero, making recovery of a function from noisy indefinite-integral data an ill-posed inverse problem.

= Adjoint of an integration operator anchored at zero
{parent=Volterra operator}
{title2=$Tf(t)=\int_0^t f(s)ds$}

On $L^2(-a,a)$, the <adjoint operator> is $T^*g(s)=\int_s^ag(t)dt$ for $s>0$, and $T^*g(s)=-\int_{-a}^sg(t)dt$ for $s<0$. These formulas follow by reversing the triangular integration order separately on the two half-intervals. Consequently $(T+T^*)g$ is constant on each half, with values $\int_0^ag$ and $-\int_{-a}^0g$. Applying this adjoint to a real weight transfers a weighted integral of an anchored function to an integral against its derivative.

= Volterra power-kernel integral
{parent=Volterra operator}
{c}
{title2=$(V_\beta f)(x)=\int_0^x(x-y)^{\beta-1}f(y)\,dy,\quad\beta>0$}

For nonnegative <Lebesgue measurable> $f$ on $(0,1)$, the displayed <Volterra power-kernel integral> is defined for every $0<x<1$ as a nonnegative extended <Lebesgue integral>. The factor is finite and continuous on the open integration <interval> $(0,x)$, and an endpoint value can be assigned arbitrarily. The result may initially be infinite; for example $f(y)=1/y$ makes every value infinite. At $\beta=1$, it reduces to the ordinary <Volterra operator>.

For joint <integration>, use the <integral kernel> $(x-y)^{\beta-1}$ on $0<y<x<1$ and zero elsewhere. This piecewise prescription avoids the singular diagonal and never raises a negative number to a noninteger power. The <L1 bound for a Volterra power-kernel integral> gives an integrable output, finite <almost everywhere>, whenever $f$ is integrable.

= L1 bound for a Volterra power-kernel integral
{parent=Volterra power-kernel integral}
{c}
{title2=$\|V_\beta f\|_1\leq\beta^{-1}\|f\|_1$}

For nonnegative integrable $f$, <Tonelli's theorem> applied on the triangle $0<y<x<1$ gives the exact identity
$$
\|V_\beta f\|_1=\int_0^1f(y)\left(\int_y^1(x-y)^{\beta-1}\,dx\right)dy
=\frac1\beta\int_0^1(1-y)^\beta f(y)\,dy\leq\frac1\beta\|f\|_1.
$$
For signed or complex integrable $f$, first apply the same calculation to $|f|$ and use the <triangle inequality for an integral>. The defining <integral> then exists absolutely for <almost every> $x$, and the same <L1 norm> bound holds. The constant $1/\beta$ is sharp: use $f_n=n\mathbf1_{(0,1/n)}$, whose <L1 norm> is one and whose weighted <integrals> above tend to $1/\beta$.

Finiteness at every point is stronger than this <L1 space> assertion. For $0<\beta<1$, take $x_0=1/2$, $\gamma=(1+\beta)/2$, and $f(y)=(x_0-y)^{-\gamma}$ on $(0,x_0)$, zero otherwise. It is integrable because $\gamma<1$, but $(V_\beta f)(x_0)=\infty$ because the power near $y=x_0$ is $\beta-1-\gamma=(\beta-3)/2<-1$. This is compatible with an integrable output after changing values on a <null set>.

= Mixed-boundary singular system of the Volterra operator
{parent=Volterra operator}
{title2=$\sigma_n=((n-\tfrac12)\pi)^{-1}$}

For the <Volterra integration operator> $Af(x)=\int_0^x f(t)dt$, its adjoint is $A^*g(x)=\int_x^1g(t)dt$. With $a_n=(n-\tfrac12)\pi$, the input singular vectors are $\sqrt2\cos(a_nx)$ and the output singular vectors $\sqrt2\sin(a_nx)$, with singular values $1/a_n$. = Spectral Tikhonov differentiation for the Volterra operator
{parent=Mixed-boundary singular system of the Volterra operator}
{c}
{title2=$x_\alpha=\sum_n\frac{a_n}{1+\alpha a_n^2}\langle y,v_n\rangle u_n$}

The <Tikhonov regularization> of the <Volterra integration operator> replaces the unbounded differentiation gain $a_n$ by $a_n/(1+\alpha a_n^2)$. The series converges in $L^2$ for every datum and $\alpha>0$. On the <range of the Volterra integration operator>, it converges to the <weak derivative> of the datum as $\alpha\downarrow0$. No pointwise differentiation of arbitrary noisy <L2 space> data is required.

= Differentiation by one-sided difference quotients
{parent=Volterra operator}
{title2=$D_h f(x)=\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}h\ \text{or}\ \frac{f(x)-f(x-h)}h$}

On $(0,1/2)$ use the forward difference and on $(1/2,1)$ the backward difference, with $0<h<1/2$. This defines a <bounded linear operator> on $L^2(0,1)$ and $\|D_h\|\leq\sqrt6/h$. For $f$ in the <range of the Volterra integration operator>, these differences are local averages of $f'$ and converge to $f'$ in $L^2$ by continuity of translations. Hence $D_h$ gives a <linear regularization> of differentiation. No pointwise evaluation of an arbitrary $L^2$ equivalence class is needed: translated functions are defined almost everywhere.

= Error bound for one-sided differentiation
{parent=Differentiation by one-sided difference quotients}
{title2=$\|D_h f^\delta-f'\|_2\leq\sqrt6\delta/h+\|f''\|_\infty h/2$}

For $f\in C^2[0,1]$ and $\|f^\delta-f\|_2\leq\delta$, two translated half-interval integrals have overlap multiplicity at most two. The inequality $|a-b|^2\leq2|a|^2+2|b|^2$ gives $\|D_h\|\leq\sqrt6/h$. Averaging $f'$ over the relevant interval and applying $|f'(x+t)-f'(x)|\leq\|f''\|_\infty|t|$ gives the second term. For this to estimate a <Moore–Penrose inverse of an operator>, also require $f(0)=0$; otherwise the derivative is still approximated but $K^\dagger f$ is not defined.

= Range of the Volterra integration operator
{parent=Volterra operator}
{title2=$\mathcal R(K)=\{f\in H^1(0,1):f(0)=0\}$}

For $Ku(y)=\int_0^y u(x)\,dx$ on the <Hilbert space> $L^2(0,1)$, every image has a <weak derivative> $f'=u$ and zero trace at zero. Conversely every such <Sobolev space> element is this integral of its <weak derivative>. The range is dense because it contains smooth functions supported in $(0,1)$, but it is not closed: a step function can be approximated in $L^2$ by continuous ramps and is not itself in $H^1$. The <Moore–Penrose inverse of an operator> has this range as its domain and differentiates there.

= Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem
{c}
{parent=Functional analysis}
{wiki}

Every positive linear functional $\varphi$ on $C(K)$ for a compact Hausdorff space $K$ has a unique representation
$$
\varphi(f)=\int_Kf\,d\mu
$$
by a finite regular positive Borel measure $\mu$. More generally, $C(K)^*$ is isometrically the space of finite regular complex Borel measures with the total-variation norm.

= Riesz representation on compactly supported continuous functions
{c}
{parent=Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem}
{title2=$I(f)=\int_X f\,d\mu$}

A <positive linear functional> on the <space of continuous compactly supported functions> of a locally compact Hausdorff space has a unique representing positive <Radon measure>. Positivity gives boundedness on fixed compact supports: choose a compactly supported continuous cutoff $p\geq1$ on the support $K$, then $|I(f)|\leq\|f\|_\infty I(p)$ for real $f$ supported in $K$. The resulting measure is finite on compact sets but need not have finite total mass.

= Cantor-space representation of positive functionals
{c}
{parent=Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem}
{title2=$\phi(f)=\int_\Omega f\,dP$}

Extend the <cylinder premeasure from a positive functional> to a <Borel probability measure> and use <cylinder-function density in Cantor space>. The integral and functional agree on cylinder <simple functions>, and both are continuous in the <supremum norm>, so they agree on all of $C(\Omega)$. Uniqueness on the generating cylinder algebra gives uniqueness of the Borel <measure>.

= Compact-metric representation by Cantor-space pullback

Given a continuous surjection $h:\Omega\to K$ from <Cantor space> to a nonempty <compact metric space>, the pullback $f\mapsto f\circ h$ is a unital <isometric embedding> of $C(K)$ into $C(\Omega)$. Transport a normalized <positive linear functional> to its image, take a <positive extension from a unital subspace of C(K)>, and represent that extension on Cantor space. The <pushforward measure> $h_*P$ then represents the original functional.

= Atomic approximation on finite-dimensional spaces of continuous functions
{parent=Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem}
{title2=$\left|\int f\,d\mu-\sum_it_if(w_i)\right|\leq\varepsilon\|f\|$}

A finite regular <complex measure> of total variation one can be approximated on a finite-dimensional <vector subspace> of continuous functions by a finite sum of phased point evaluations with $\sum_i|t_i|=1$. The <Krein-Milman theorem> and the <extreme points of the dual unit ball of C(K)> give weak-star approximation by convex combinations of phased point masses. A finite <norm> net of the <vector subspace> <unit ball> turns finitely many scalar approximations into one uniform estimate. Repeated nodes may be kept separate to retain the exact coefficient-magnitude sum.

= Stone-Weierstrass theorem
{c}
{parent=Functional analysis}
{wiki=Stone–Weierstrass_theorem}

A point-separating real subalgebra of $C(X)$ that contains the constants is uniformly dense when $X$ is compact Hausdorff. Polynomial approximation of the square root makes its closure a lattice; finite maxima and minima then turn pointwise interpolation into uniform approximation.

= Even-polynomial approximation with exact endpoint interpolation
{parent=Stone-Weierstrass theorem}
{title2=$b(t)=p(t^2),\quad b(0)=a(0),\ b(1)=a(1)$}

On $[0,1]$, polynomials in $t^2$ form a unital algebra closed under <complex conjugation> separating points, so the <Stone-Weierstrass theorem> makes them dense in the continuous complex functions. Their approximation can preserve both endpoint values exactly. Given $\|a-p(t^2)\|_\infty<\eta$, add $[a(0)-p(0)](1-t^2)+[a(1)-p(1)]t^2$. This is still an even <polynomial>, fixes both endpoints, and changes the approximation by at most $\eta$, since its correction is a <convex combination> of the endpoint errors. Taking $\eta<\varepsilon/2$ yields any prescribed uniform error $\varepsilon$.

= Even and odd polynomial density on a positive interval
{parent=Stone-Weierstrass theorem}
{title2=$\overline{\mathbb C[t^2]}=\overline{t\mathbb C[t^2]}=C([a,b]),\quad0<a<b$}

On a positive compact interval, both even-power and odd-power complex <polynomial> families are uniformly dense in the continuous-function algebra. The even family is a conjugation-closed unital algebra separating points, since squaring is injective on the interval; the <Stone-Weierstrass theorem> proves its density. Equivalently use the <homeomorphism> $t\mapsto t^2$ and <polynomial> approximation on $[a^2,b^2]$. For the odd family, given a target $f$, first approximate $f/t$ by an even <polynomial> $p(t^2)$. Then $tp(t^2)$ is odd and its error is at most $b$ times the first error. Division is continuous because the interval avoids zero. Thus the odd family can be dense even though it is not a unital <subalgebra>.

= Lattice approximation from two-point approximation
{parent=Stone-Weierstrass theorem}

Suppose a family $A\subseteq C(K,\mathbb R)$ on a <compact space> is closed under pointwise maximum and minimum. If a target $f$ can be approximated arbitrarily closely at each pair of points by a member of $A$, it can be approximated in the <supremum norm>. For a fixed first point, take finitely many pairwise approximants whose neighborhoods cover $K$ and take their maximum: it lies above $f-\varepsilon$ everywhere and below $f+\varepsilon$ near that first point. Take finitely many such first-point neighborhoods covering $K$ and take the minimum of their maxima. The result is within $\varepsilon$ of $f$ everywhere. Starting with tolerance $\varepsilon/2$ ensures a strict <uniform approximation> error smaller than $\varepsilon$.

= Weierstrass approximation theorem
{parent=Stone-Weierstrass theorem}
{c}
{wiki}

Every <continuous> <real-valued function> on a <compact interval> can be approximated by <polynomials> with arbitrarily small error everywhere on the interval. More precisely, if $a<b$ and $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ is <continuous>, then for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a <polynomial> $p$ with
$$
\|f-p\|_\infty=\sup_{x\in[a,b]}|f(x)-p(x)|<\varepsilon.
$$
The error is measured in the <supremum norm>, so one approximation works at every point. The degenerate interval $a=b$ needs only a <constant function>. A change of variable $t=(x-a)/(b-a)$ reduces the nondegenerate case to $[0,1]$.<The Weierstrass approximation theorem (Georgia Tech handout)>[The Weierstrass approximation theorem, Theorem 0.3, printed p. 2: uniform polynomial approximation on the unit interval]{ref}

This is useful because a <polynomial> is described by finitely many <coefficients> and can be evaluated with <addition> and <multiplication>. Its surprise is that the target need not be <differentiable>: the sharp corner of an <absolute value> function can be approximated by smooth <polynomials>. The <Bernstein approximation of a corner> below makes this visible. Approximating values, however, does not by itself approximate <derivatives>; <Weierstrass approximation does not preserve derivatives> gives a counterexample.

= Weierstrass approximation does not preserve derivatives
{c}
{parent=Weierstrass approximation theorem}

On $[0,1]$, the <polynomials> $p_n(x)=x^n/n$ converge in <supremum norm> to the zero <function>, since $\|p_n\|_\infty=1/n$. But $p_n'(1)=1$ for every $n$, whereas the zero <function> has left <derivative> zero at that endpoint. Thus <uniform convergence> of function values does not imply <uniform convergence> of <derivatives>. This does not contradict <uniform convergence of Bernstein polynomial derivatives>, which has a particular approximation construction and additional <differentiability> hypotheses.

= Polynomial recurrence for the absolute value
{parent=Weierstrass approximation theorem}
{title2=$p_{r+1}(x)=p_r(x)+(x^2-p_r(x)^2)/2$}

Starting with $p_0=0$ on $[-1,1]$, this <polynomial> recurrence gives $0\le p_r(x)\le |x|$ and $p_{r+1}(x)\ge p_r(x)$. Indeed the error satisfies $|x|-p_{r+1}=(|x|-p_r)[1-(|x|+p_r)/2]$. Its pointwise limit solves $p^2=x^2$, so it is $|x|$. <Dini's theorem> gives <uniform convergence>. Each <continuous> <piecewise linear function> is an affine function plus a finite <linear combination> of $(x-t)_+=(x-t+|x-t|)/2$. Scaling and translating the recurrence therefore gives <polynomial approximation> of every such function, and their density gives the <Weierstrass approximation theorem>.

= Bernstein polynomial
{parent=Weierstrass approximation theorem}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$B_nf$}

For a function $f:[0,1]\to\mathbb R$ and an <integer> $n\geq1$, its <Bernstein polynomial> is
$$
B_nf(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n f(k/n)\binom nk x^k(1-x)^{n-k}
       =\mathbb E[f(K/n)],\qquad K\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,x).
$$
The <Bernstein basis> supplies nonnegative weights whose sum is one. Equivalently, the weights are the <probabilities> of a <binomial distribution>, and the result is an <expected value>. At a given $x$, this averages the sampled values $f(k/n)$ with weights concentrated near $k/n=x$.<The Weierstrass approximation theorem (Georgia Tech handout)>[The Weierstrass approximation theorem, Lemmas 0.1–0.2 and Theorem 0.3, printed pp. 1–2: weights, binomial moments and polynomial construction]{ref}

The endpoints are exact: $B_nf(0)=f(0)$ and $B_nf(1)=f(1)$. Interior sampled points need not be interpolated. For example, <binomial distribution> moments give $B_n(x)=x$ and $B_n(x^2)=x^2+x(1-x)/n$. Thus every <affine function> is reproduced exactly, whereas $x^2$ is generally changed. <Bernstein polynomial degree preservation> is a statement about <degree of a polynomial>, not equality of the entire <polynomial>.

For a <continuous> $f$, the <uniform continuity> of $f$ and the <variance> $\operatorname{Var}(K/n)=x(1-x)/n\leq1/(4n)$ explain <uniform convergence>. Split the <expected value> of the error into $|K/n-x|<\delta$, where <uniform continuity> makes the change small, and its complement, whose <probability> is at most $1/(4n\delta^2)$ by <Chebyshev's inequality>. The <bounded function> $f$ bounds the remaining error. This proves convergence without assuming <differentiability>.

For $f\in C([0,1]^d)$, the tensor-product <Bernstein polynomial> is the <expected value> of $f(X_1/n,\ldots,X_d/n)$ for <independent random variables> $X_j\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,x_j)$. The same <uniform continuity> and coordinatewise <variance> estimates give <uniform convergence> for each fixed finite $d$.

= Bernstein approximation of a corner
{c}
{parent=Bernstein polynomial}
{title2=$f(x)=|x-1/2|$}

Take the <continuous> function $f(x)=|x-1/2|$ on $[0,1]$. It is <Lipschitz continuous> with <Lipschitz constant> one, but is not <differentiable> at $x=1/2$. For $n=2$, the sample values are $1/2,0,1/2$, so
$$
B_2f(x)=\frac12(1-x)^2+\frac12x^2,
\qquad B_2f(1/2)=\frac14\ne f(1/2).
$$
This is already a useful warning: the <Bernstein polynomial> is built from grid values but is not generally their <polynomial interpolation>.

= Lipschitz error bound for Bernstein approximation
{c}
{parent=Bernstein polynomial}
{title2=$\|B_nf-f\|_\infty\leq L/(2\sqrt n)$}

If $f$ is <Lipschitz continuous> on $[0,1]$ with <Lipschitz constant> $L$, then <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> and the <binomial distribution> <variance> give
$$
|B_nf(x)-f(x)|
\leq L\mathbb E|K/n-x|
\leq L\sqrt{\mathbb E[(K/n-x)^2]}
=L\sqrt{\frac{x(1-x)}n}
\leq\frac L{2\sqrt n}.
$$
This is an explicit error guarantee, rather than only an existence theorem. For the <Bernstein approximation of a corner>, choosing $n\geq2500$ guarantees <supremum norm> error at most $0.01$; $n>2500$ guarantees error strictly less than $0.01$. The guarantee is sufficient, not optimal.

For this specific target, the error reaches its maximum at $x=1/2$. Indeed the <derivative> of $B_nf$ is a weighted average of the slopes $n[f((k+1)/n)-f(k/n)]$, all in $[-1,1]$: see <derivatives of Bernstein polynomials>. Subtracting the target makes the error nondecreasing to the left of the corner and nonincreasing to its right. For even $n\geq2$, the symmetric <binomial distribution> at $x=1/2$ gives the exact value

$$
The last equality follows by pairing terms about $n/2$ and telescoping the <binomial coefficients>. It gives $1/4$ for $n=2$ and $3/16$ for $n=4$.

= Bernstein polynomial degree preservation
{c}
{parent=Bernstein polynomial}
{title2=$\deg B_np=\deg p\quad(\deg p\leq n)$}

For $K$ with a binomial distribution of parameters $n,x$, the Bernstein image of a monomial is $B_n(x^m)=\mathbb E(K/n)^m$. Expand $K^m$ in the monic <falling factorial> <basis>. Since $\mathbb E K^{\underline r}=n^{\underline r}x^r$, the <leading coefficient> becomes $n^{\underline m}/n^m$, which is nonzero for $m\leq n$. Thus an actual degree-$m$ <polynomial> retains its degree, even though its lower <coefficients> generally change.

= Derivatives of Bernstein polynomials
{parent=Bernstein polynomial}
{title2=$B_n^{(r)}f=(n)_r\sum_{j=0}^{n-r}\Delta_{1/n}^rf(j/n)p_{n-r,j}$}

Differentiating the Bernstein basis $p_{n,j}(x)=\binom njx^j(1-x)^{n-j}$ gives $p_{n,j}'=n(p_{n-1,j-1}-p_{n-1,j})$, with out-of-range terms zero. Telescoping coefficient differences and induction give the displayed formula, where $(n)_r=n!/(n-r)!$ and $0\le r\le n$. For $n>r$, set $g_{n,r}(t)=(n)_r\Delta_{1/n}^rf((n-r)t/n)$; then $B_n^{(r)}f=B_{n-r}g_{n,r}$. The rescaled argument compensates for the different sampling meshes.

= Uniform convergence of Bernstein polynomial derivatives
{parent=Derivatives of Bernstein polynomials}
{title2=$\|B_n^{(r)}f-f^{(r)}\|_\infty\to0$}

For fixed $r$ and $f\in C^r[0,1]$, uniform convergence of normalized <finite differences> to $f^{(r)}$, uniform continuity of $f^{(r)}$, and $(n)_r/n^r\to1$ imply $g_{n,r}\to f^{(r)}$ uniformly. Positivity and partition of unity make <Bernstein polynomials> contractions in the <supremum norm>. Consequently $\|B_{n-r}g_{n,r}-f^{(r)}\|_\infty\le\|g_{n,r}-f^{(r)}\|_\infty+\|B_{n-r}f^{(r)}-f^{(r)}\|_\infty\to0$. The differentiability assumption is essential; continuity alone does not define the target derivative.

= Bernstein falling-factorial identity
{c}
{parent=Bernstein polynomial}
{title2=$B_n(\prod_{r=0}^{m-1}(x-r/n))=\prod_{r=0}^{m-1}(1-r/n)x^m$}

At the sampling point $j/n$, the polynomial inside the <Bernstein polynomial> has value $n^{-m}j(j-1)\cdots(j-m+1)$. The <falling factorial> identity $j^{\underline m}\binom nj=n^{\underline m}\binom{n-m}{j-m}$ and the <binomial theorem> prove the displayed formula. For fixed $m$, both the input and its scalar factor tend uniformly to $x^m$ as $n$ grows. The <supremum norm> contraction of the <Bernstein polynomial> therefore gives $\|B_n(x^m)-x^m\|_\infty\le m(m-1)/n$ for $n\ge m$.

= Rounded Bernstein polynomial
{parent=Bernstein polynomial}
{title2=$B_n^*(f,x)$}

The rounded <Bernstein polynomial> replaces each coefficient $\binom nk f(k/n)$ in the unnormalized <basis> $x^k(1-x)^{n-k}$ by its <floor function>. It therefore has <integer> <coefficients> in the ordinary monomial <basis>. If $f(0),f(1)\in\mathbb Z$, only interior rounding errors remain and
$$
\|B_n^*f-B_nf\|_\infty\le\max\{3/(2n),(n-1)(3/4)^n\}\longrightarrow0\qquad(n\ge2).
$$
To prove this, bound each interior rounding error by one. On $[0,1/4]$, sum the resulting powers as a <geometric series> of ratio at most $1/3$ and maximize $x(1-x)^{n-1}$; reflect for $[3/4,1]$. On the middle interval each degree-$n$ product is at most $(3/4)^n$. <integer> endpoints are necessary for this convergence: a noninteger endpoint produces a fixed nonzero rounding error there.

= Bernstein monomial recurrence
{parent=Bernstein polynomial}
{c}
{title2=$B_n(x^m)$}

For $m\ge1$ and $n\ge2$, the <Bernstein polynomial> of the <monomial> $x^m$ satisfies
$$
B_n(x^m)=x\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\binom{m-1}{\ell}\frac{(n-1)^{m-1-\ell}}{n^{m-1}}B_{n-1}(x^{m-1-\ell}).
$$
It follows by applying $k\binom nk=n\binom{n-1}{k-1}$ and expanding $k=(k-1)+1$ with the <binomial theorem>. The coefficients sum to one; only the first tends to one as $n\to\infty$. Induction on $m$ proves <uniform convergence> of these monomial approximants, starting from $B_n(1)=1$.

= Inverse Bernstein approximation on a fixed-degree polynomial space
{parent=Bernstein polynomial}
{title2=$\|B_n^{-1}f-f\|_\infty\to0$}

On the fixed finite-dimensional space of polynomials of degree at most $d$, uniform convergence of $B_n$ on each monomial and <equivalence of norms in finite dimensions> imply $\varepsilon_n=\|B_n-I\|_{\rm op}\to0$. A <Neumann series> then gives $\|B_n^{-1}-I\|_{\rm op}\le\varepsilon_n/(1-\varepsilon_n)$ for sufficiently large $n$. Consequently the displayed convergence holds. A strictly positive polynomial has positive minimum on the compact interval, so its inverse Bernstein approximants are also strictly positive for all sufficiently large $n$.

= Bernstein basis
{c}
{parent=Bernstein polynomial}
{title2=$b_{k,n}(x)=\binom nkx^k(1-x)^{n-k}$}

These $n+1$ nonnegative polynomials form a basis of polynomials of degree at most $n$, and sum to one on $[0,1]$. For $0\le j\le n$,
$$
x^j=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{(k)_j}{(n)_j}b_{k,n}(x),
$$
where ratios for $j=0$ equal one and terms with $k<j$ vanish. The identity follows from $\binom nk(k)_j/(n)_j=\binom{n-j}{k-j}$ and the <binomial theorem>. It gives explicit basis coefficients using the <falling factorial>.

= Quadratic Bernstein coefficient bound
{parent=Bernstein basis}
{title2=$(|a_1|,|a_2|,|a_3|)\leq(1,3,1)$}

Write $p(x)=a_1x^2+2a_2x(1-x)+a_3(1-x)^2$ on $[0,1]$. Endpoint evaluation gives $a_1=p(1)$ and $a_3=p(0)$, while midpoint evaluation gives $a_2=2p(1/2)-[p(0)+p(1)]/2$. Thus $\|p\|_\infty\leq1$ implies $|a_1|,|a_3|\leq1$ and $|a_2|\leq3$. These bounds are sharp: $p(x)=8x^2-8x+1$ has <uniform norm> one and coefficients $(1,-3,1)$.

= Positive Bernstein coefficients for a strictly positive polynomial
{parent=Bernstein basis}

If a polynomial $f$ is strictly positive on $[0,1]$, take $g_n=B_n^{-1}f$. By <inverse Bernstein approximation on a fixed-degree polynomial space>, eventually $g_n>0$ on the interval. Therefore
$$
f(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk g_n(k/n)x^k(1-x)^{n-k}
$$
has strictly positive coefficients. The converse implication to nonnegativity follows immediately because each basis term is nonnegative. Strict positivity is essential for the general existence result: a nonzero polynomial vanishing at an interior point cannot have a nonnegative-coefficient representation in this basis, whose individual terms are positive throughout the open interval.

= Degree elevation of Bernstein coefficients
{parent=Bernstein basis}
{title2=$\beta_{k,n+1}=\frac{k}{n+1}\beta_{k-1,n}+\left(1-\frac{k}{n+1}\right)\beta_{k,n}$}

Write $f=\sum_k\beta_{k,n}b_{k,n}$ in the <Bernstein basis>. Representing the same polynomial at degrees $n$ and $n+1$ gives the displayed convex combinations for internal indices; the endpoint coefficients are copied. Therefore the minimum coefficient cannot decrease under degree elevation. In the unnormalized basis $x^k(1-x)^{n-k}$, the elevated coefficients are $c'_k=c_{k-1}+c_k$ with the corresponding endpoint convention, so nonnegativity certificates are preserved.

= Globally uniform limit of real polynomials
{parent=Stone-Weierstrass theorem}

A function $f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R$ is a uniform limit on all of $\mathbb R^n$ of real polynomials exactly when it is itself a polynomial. If $(p_i)$ converges uniformly, then $p_i-p_j$ is bounded on $\mathbb R^n$ for all sufficiently large $i,j$. A bounded real polynomial is constant, so a tail of the sequence differs from one fixed polynomial only by convergent constants.

= Open mapping theorem
{disambiguate=functional analysis}
{parent=Functional analysis}
{wiki}

= Open mapping theorem
{synonym}

A bounded surjective linear map between Banach spaces is open. Baire category first puts a ball in the closure of an image; iterative correction removes the closure.

= Operator range category dichotomy
{parent=Open mapping theorem (functional analysis)}

A <bounded linear operator> $T:X\to Y$ from a <Banach space> to a <normed space> either maps onto $Y$ or has <meagre set> image in $Y$. To prove this, write $T(X)=\bigcup_{n\geq1}T(n\overline B_X)$. If every $\overline{T(n\overline B_X)}$ has empty <interior>, each term is a <nowhere dense set>. Otherwise scaling and <geometric correction for approximate surjectivity> place a <ball> about zero inside $T(\overline B_X)$; the <vector subspace> $T(X)$ then equals $Y$. In particular a nonclosed operator image is a <meagre set>. A proper closed operator image is also a <meagre set>, so the closed/meagre alternatives need not be exclusive.

= Geometric correction for approximate surjectivity
{parent=Open mapping theorem (functional analysis)}
{title2=$\|Tx-y\|\le k\|y\|\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\|x_{\rm exact}\|\le R\|y\|/(1-k)$}

Let $T:E\to F$ be a <bounded linear operator>, $E$ a <Banach space>, and $F$ a <normed vector space>. Suppose each residual $r$ has an approximate preimage of norm at most $R\|r\|$ leaving error at most $k\|r\|$, where $0<k<1$. Correct successive residuals to get vectors $x_j$ with $\|x_j\|\le Rk^j\|y\|$. Their absolutely convergent sum in $E$ maps exactly to $y$, and its norm is at most $R\|y\|/(1-k)$. Completeness of $F$ is not needed for this argument.

= Completeness forced by uniformly bounded lifting
{parent=Geometric correction for approximate surjectivity}
{title2=$Tx=y,\quad\|x\|\le C\|y\|$}

If a <bounded linear operator> from a <Banach space> onto a <normed vector space> admits the displayed lift for every $y$, then its target is complete. Given a <Cauchy sequence>, choose a subsequence with summable successive differences. Lift those differences to a summable sequence in the source, and use its convergent sum to produce a limit of the target subsequence. The original Cauchy sequence has the same limit.

= Open mapping theorem for Fréchet spaces
{parent=Open mapping theorem (functional analysis)}
{title2=$A:E\twoheadrightarrow F\Longrightarrow A\text{ is open}$}

A continuous surjective <linear map> between <Fréchet spaces> is an <open map>. The <Baire category theorem> first shows that the closure of the image of every convex balanced zero-neighbourhood is a zero-neighbourhood: the target is the countable union of scalar multiples of this image, and an interior point of its closure can be translated to zero using convexity and symmetry.

To remove the closure, fix a domain zero-neighbourhood $U$ and choose convex balanced zero-neighbourhoods $U_n$ so that every series $\sum_nu_n$, $u_n\in U_n$, converges to a point of $U$. With an increasing defining sequence of <seminorms> $p_j$, one can ensure $p_j(u_n)<2^{-n}$ for $j\le n$ and make the finitely many <seminorms> defining $U$ summable within its bounds. Completeness gives convergence. Choose target zero-neighbourhoods $V_n\subset\overline{A(U_n)}$ shrinking to zero. For $y\in V_1$, choose $u_n\in U_n$ successively so that $y-A\sum_{j\le n}u_j\in V_{n+1}$. Density permits each correction. Continuity then gives $y=A\sum_nu_n\in A(U)$, proving openness.

= Dense open-unit-ball image criterion
{parent=Open mapping theorem (functional analysis)}

Let $T:X\to X$ be a <bounded linear operator> on a <Banach space>, and let $B$ be the open unit ball. If $B\subseteq\overline{T(B)}$, then $B\subseteq T(B)$. Scale the density statement and correct a target by a geometrically decreasing sequence of approximate preimages. Completeness makes their sum converge inside $B$, and continuity of $T$ makes its image equal the target.

= Bounded inverse theorem
{parent=open-mapping-theorem-functional-analysis}
{wiki}

A bounded bijective linear map between Banach spaces has a bounded inverse. This follows because an open bijection has continuous inverse.

= Diagonal operator on sequence space
{parent=Functional analysis}

A diagonal operator on $\ell^p$ acts coordinatewise as $(x_n)\mapsto(a_nx_n)$. It is bounded when $(a_n)$ is bounded, while its inverse on its image is bounded only when the nonzero $a_n$ are bounded away from zero.

= Unbounded inverse on a nonclosed operator range
{parent=Diagonal operator on sequence space}

The injective diagonal map $(x_n)\mapsto(x_n/n)$ on $\ell^2$ is bounded, but its inverse on the range is unbounded; the range is correspondingly not closed.

= Coercive operator
{parent=Functional analysis}

A bounded operator $T$ on a Hilbert space is coercive when $\operatorname{Re}\langle x,Tx\rangle\geq c\|x\|^2$ for some $c>0$. This estimate implies injectivity and bounds an inverse on the range by $1/c$.

= Nonlinear coercive operator
{parent=Coercive operator}
{title2=$\langle A(u),u-u_0\rangle/\|u-u_0\|\to+\infty$}

For a map $A:M\to X^*$ on a subset of a real <Banach space>, coercivity with respect to $u_0\in M$ means the displayed limit as $u\in M$ and $\|u-u_0\|\to\infty$. Here the brackets denote the <dual pairing>. This growth condition is useful in existence theorems for <monotone operators>. A linear Hilbert-space estimate $\langle Au,u\rangle\geq c\|u\|^2$, $c>0$, implies the condition at zero. Monotonicity alone does not: the zero operator is monotone and fails this growth condition on an unbounded space.

= Strict positivity without coercivity can fail variational solvability
{parent=Coercive operator}

On <l2 sequence space> $\ell^2$, let $(Lu)_n=u_n/n$ and $f_n=1/n$. This bounded <self-adjoint operator> has $\langle Lu,u\rangle>0$ for nonzero $u$, but the equation $Lu=f$ requires $u_n=1$, which is not in $\ell^2$. Taking the first $N$ coordinates equal to one makes $I(u)=-\sum_{n=1}^N1/n\to-\infty$. Thus strict positivity in isolation does not give existence in that <Hilbert space>; a <coercive bilinear form> and a continuous forcing functional in its norm are essential hypotheses for the usual minimization proof.

= Ellipticity of a bounded Hilbert-space operator
{parent=Coercive operator}
{title2=$\operatorname{Re}\langle Lv,v\rangle\geq\gamma\|v\|^2$}

In a variational <Hilbert space> setting, a bounded operator $L$ is elliptic or uniformly coercive when $\operatorname{Re}\langle Lv,v\rangle\geq\gamma\|v\|^2$ for a constant $\gamma>0$. On a real <Hilbert space> this condition depends on the symmetric part of $L$ and does not imply <self-adjointness>. It differs from the principal-symbol condition defining an <elliptic differential operator>.

= Graph of a linear operator

The graph of $T:X\to Y$ is the linear subspace $\Gamma(T)=\{(x,Tx):x\in X\}$ of $X\times Y$.

A linear operator $A:D(A)\subseteq X\to Y$ is closed when its graph is closed in $X\times Y$. Equivalently, if $x_n\to x$ and $Ax_n\to y$, then $x\in D(A)$ and $Ax=y$.

= Sectorial operator

A densely defined <closed operator> is sectorial of angle $\theta$ if its <spectrum> lies in $\{z:|\arg z|\leq\theta\}\cup\{0\}$ and, for every larger angle $\phi<\pi$, its <resolvent> outside that larger sector satisfies $\|(z-A)^{-1}\|\leq C_\phi/|z|$. For a <closed linear operator> $A$, the resolvent is the bounded inverse $(zI-A)^{-1}$ when $z$ lies in the <resolvent set of an operator>. This definition includes unbounded $A$. It specializes to the <resolvent of an element> when $A$ is an element of a <Banach algebra>.

= Stieltjes matrix resolvent
{c}
{parent=Resolvent of an operator}
{title2=$G_X(z)=(X-zI)^{-1}$}

For a <Hermitian matrix> $X$ and nonreal $z$, this is the negative of the usual <resolvent of an operator> convention $(zI-X)^{-1}$. Its normalized trace is the <Stieltjes transform of a measure> of the <empirical spectral measure> with kernel $(x-z)^{-1}$. Naming the convention prevents sign errors in self-consistency identities.

= Resolvent self-consistency defect
{parent=Stieltjes matrix resolvent}
{title2=$\varepsilon_N(z)=g_N(z)+(z+g_N(z))^{-1}$}

For a zero-diagonal real <symmetric matrix>, put $q_i=x_i^T(X^{(i)}-zI)^{-1}x_i$ and $g_N=N^{-1}\operatorname{Tr}(X-zI)^{-1}$. The <Schur complement formula for a diagonal resolvent entry> gives $g_N=-N^{-1}\sum_i(z+q_i)^{-1}$. Subtracting the comparison value $-(z+g_N)^{-1}$ gives $\varepsilon_N=N^{-1}\sum_i(q_i-g_N)/[(z+g_N)(z+q_i)]$. Upper-half-plane positivity and the <principal minor resolvent trace bound> control this defect by the average of $|q_i-g_N^{(i)}|$ plus a trace correction.

= Principal minor resolvent trace bound
{parent=Stieltjes matrix resolvent}
{title2=$|N^{-1}\operatorname{Tr}G_X-N^{-1}\operatorname{Tr}G_{X^{(i)}}|\leq c/(N|\operatorname{Im}z|)$}

The <eigenvalue interlacing> of a <Hermitian matrix> and a principal minor bounds the difference of their resolvent traces by a constant times $|\operatorname{Im}z|^{-1}$. When both traces are normalized by the original dimension $N$, the bound acquires $N^{-1}$. One proof writes the difference using the interlacing counting <functions>, whose difference is at most one, and bounds the integral of $|t-z|^{-2}$ by $\pi/|\operatorname{Im}z|$. This gives the admissible absolute constant $c=\pi$.

= Positive imaginary part of a Stieltjes matrix resolvent
{parent=Stieltjes matrix resolvent}
{title2=$\operatorname{Im}G_X(z)=\eta G_X(z)^*G_X(z)\quad(\eta=\operatorname{Im}z>0)$}

By an orthonormal eigenbasis, the imaginary part of the <Stieltjes matrix resolvent> has <eigenvalues> $\eta/|\lambda-z|^2>0$. Thus its normalized trace has positive imaginary part, and its quadratic form at any vector has nonnegative imaginary part. This yields $|z+g|\geq\eta$ and $|z+q|\geq\eta$ for the normalized trace $g$ and a quadratic form $q$ used in Schur-complement estimates.

= Graph norm

The graph norm of $A:D(A)\subseteq X\to Y$ is $\|x\|_A=\|x\|_X+\|Ax\|_Y$. If $X$ and $Y$ are <Banach spaces>, then $A$ is a <closed linear operator> exactly when its domain is complete in the graph norm.

= Completeness of a graph norm from a bounded right inverse
{parent=Graph norm}
{title2=$\ker T\text{ complete},\ TS=I,\ S\text{ bounded}\ \Longrightarrow\ (X,\|\cdot\|_T)\text{ complete}$}

Let $X$ be a <normed space>, $Y$ a <Banach space>, and $T:X\to Y$ a <linear map>. If its <kernel> is <complete> in the original <norm> and $S:Y\to X$ is a bounded <right inverse>, then the <graph norm> $\|x\|_T=\|x\|_X+\|Tx\|_Y$ is <complete>, even if $X$ with its original <norm> is incomplete or $T$ is unbounded.

For a <Cauchy sequence> $(x_n)$ in the <graph norm>, $y_n=Tx_n$ converges to $y\in Y$. The elements $w_n=x_n-Sy_n$ lie in the <kernel> and satisfy $\|w_n-w_m\|_X\leq(1+\|S\|)\|x_n-x_m\|_T$. Hence $w_n\to w\in\ker T$. The element $x=w+Sy$ satisfies $Tx=y$ and
$$
\|x_n-x\|_T\leq\|w_n-w\|_X+(1+\|S\|)\|y_n-y\|_Y\longrightarrow0.
$$

= Resolvent set of an operator

= Resolvent set

The resolvent set $\rho(A)$ consists of the complex numbers $z$ for which $zI-A:D(A)\to X$ is bijective and has a bounded inverse on $X$. This inverse is the <resolvent of an operator> $R(z,A)=(zI-A)^{-1}$.

= Closed graph theorem
{parent=Graph of a linear operator}
{wiki}

A linear map between Banach spaces is continuous if and only if its graph is closed.

= Separating space of a linear map
{parent=Closed graph theorem}
{title2=$S(T)=\{y:\exists x_n\to0,\ Tx_n\to y\}$}

For an everywhere-defined <linear map> $T:E\to F$ between <Banach spaces>, its separating space measures the possible limiting defect in continuity at zero. It is a <closed linear subspace>: linear combinations combine witnessing sequences, and diagonal choices with $\|x_j\|<1/j$ and $\|Tx_j-y_j\|<1/j$ handle a convergent sequence $y_j$ of defects. The <closed graph theorem> gives $T$ continuous exactly when $S(T)=\{0\}$, since a limit $(x_n,Tx_n)\to(x,y)$ has $y-Tx\in S(T)$.

= Gliding-hump continuity principle
{parent=Separating space of a linear map}
{title2=$U_nSP_n\text{ is continuous eventually},\quad P_n=T_1\cdots T_n$}

Let $S:E\to Y$ be linear between <Banach spaces>, let $T_n:E\to E$ be bounded, and let $U_n:Y\to Z_n$ be bounded. If $U_nSP_m$ is <continuous> whenever $m>n$, then $U_nSP_n$ is <continuous> for all sufficiently large $n$. After rescaling the <bounded operators>, assume their <norms> are at most one. If the conclusion failed, discontinuity would allow successively tiny <vectors> $x_j$ whose diagonal images dominate all previous contributions. Choose their <norms> also to control the <continuous> maps $U_{n_i}SP_{n_i+1}$ on subsequent tails. The convergent sum $x=\sum_jP_{n_j}x_j$ would then satisfy $\|U_{n_j}Sx\|>j$ despite $\|U_{n_j}Sx\|\le\|Sx\|$, a contradiction. The tail is factored through $P_{n_i+1}$ before applying $S$, so no <continuity> of $S$ is assumed.

= Closed graph theorem for Fréchet spaces
{parent=Closed graph theorem}
{title2=$\Gamma(T)\text{ closed}\Longrightarrow T\text{ continuous}$}

A <linear map> $T:E\to F$ between <Fréchet spaces> is continuous if its <graph of a linear operator> is closed in $E\times F$. The closed graph is itself a <Fréchet space>. Its first-coordinate projection is a continuous linear bijection onto $E$, so the <open mapping theorem for Fréchet spaces> makes the inverse continuous. Compose that inverse with the second-coordinate projection to obtain $T$. Completeness of both original spaces is part of the theorem.

= Closed graph theorem from the bounded inverse theorem
{parent=Closed graph theorem}

For a closed graph, the projection $\Gamma(T)\to X$ is a bounded bijection between Banach spaces. Its bounded inverse followed by projection to $Y$ is $T$.

= Closed-graph proof for a factored operator
{parent=Closed graph theorem}

If bounded $S:X\to Y$ is injective and $\operatorname{im}T\subseteq\operatorname{im}S$, then $R=S^{-1}T$ has closed graph: $x_n\to x$ and $Rx_n\to z$ imply $Sz=Tx=S(Rx)$ and hence $z=Rx$.

= Incomplete-domain counterexample to closed-graph factorization
{parent=Closed graph theorem}

On incomplete $c_{00}$ with the $\ell^2$ norm, let $S(x_n)=(x_n/n)$ and let $T$ be inclusion into $\ell^2$. Their images agree, but $S^{-1}T(e_n)=ne_n$ is unbounded.

= Continuity of a positive linear map into a dual space
{parent=Closed graph theorem}

Let $V$ be a real Banach space and $T:V\to V^*$ be linear with $T_v(v)\geq0$ for every $v$. If $v_n\to0$ and $T_{v_n}\to f$ in $V^*$, positivity of $T_{v_n+tw}(v_n+tw)$ and passage to the limit give
$$
0\leq t f(w)+t^2T_w(w)
$$
for every real $t$ and every $w$. Taking small $t$ of either sign forces $f(w)=0$. Thus the graph of $T$ is closed, and the <closed graph theorem> makes $T$ continuous.

= Closed-range criterion for an injection
{parent=Functional analysis}

A bounded injective map between Banach spaces is bounded below exactly when its image is closed, by the bounded inverse theorem applied to its image.

= Closed-range bound on the kernel complement
{parent=Closed-range criterion for an injection}
{title2=$\|Tv\|\ge b\|v\|\quad(v\perp\ker T)$}

For a <bounded linear operator> $T$ between <Hilbert spaces>, its range is closed exactly when there is $b>0$ with $\|Tv\|\ge b\|v\|$ for all $v\in(\ker T)^\perp$. The restriction to this <orthogonal complement> is a bounded bijection onto the range, so the <bounded inverse theorem> proves necessity. Conversely, the bound makes preimages of a <Cauchy sequence> of image points a <Cauchy sequence>, proving closedness by completeness.

= Sequential properness for a self-adjoint operator
{parent=Closed-range bound on the kernel complement}

A functional $F$ is weakly lower semicontinuous when $x_j\rightharpoonup x$ implies $F(x)\leq\liminf_jF(x_j)$. Convex integral functionals with suitable growth have this property, which lets weak limits of minimizing sequences remain minimizers.

= Minimum of a coercive convex functional on a Hilbert space
{parent=Weak lower semicontinuity}
{title2=$\min_{x\in K}F(x)$}

Let $K$ be a nonempty norm-closed <convex set> in a real <Hilbert space>, and let $F:K\to\mathbb R$ be continuous and a <convex function>. If $K$ is bounded, or $F(x)\to\infty$ as $x\in K$ tends to infinity in <norm>, then $F$ attains its <infimum>. Every <sublevel set> is bounded, norm-closed, and convex, hence contains the weak limits of its sequences by <norm-closed convex set is weakly closed>. A <minimizing sequence> in one fixed <sublevel set> has a <weakly convergent> subsequence by <weak sequential compactness in a Hilbert space>. Its limit lies in every <sublevel set> above the <infimum>. Since $F$ is real-valued, this also rules out <infimum> $-\infty$ and proves attainment. This is an instance of the <direct method in the calculus of variations>.

= Local Sobolev compactness gives lower semicontinuity of a nonnegative integral
{parent=Weak lower semicontinuity}

If $u_n\rightharpoonup u$ in $H^1(\mathbb R^d)$ and $F:\mathbb R\to[0,\infty)$ is continuous, then $\int F(u)\leq\liminf_n\int F(u_n)$. Start with a subsequence attaining the lower limit. The <Rellich-Kondrachov compactness theorem> gives strong local <L2 space> convergence along a diagonal subsequence, and a further subsequence converges almost everywhere. Its limit is $u$, by uniqueness of the local weak limit. Now apply the <Fatou lemma>. Nonnegativity is essential to this argument; <convexity> of $F$ is unnecessary. In particular $F(s)=1-\cos s$ is allowed despite its failure to be convex.

= Atkinson theorem
{c}
{parent=Fredholm operator}
{title2=$T\text{ Fredholm}\iff[T]\in\mathcal Q(H)^\times$}

A <bounded operator> is <Fredholm> if and only if it has a bounded two-sided inverse modulo <compact operators>. For the forward implication invert its restriction from the orthogonal kernel complement to its closed range and extend by zero. Conversely a parametrix makes the identity compact on the kernel, forcing finite dimension. A failure of a lower bound on the kernel complement would yield unit vectors $x_n$ with $Tx_n\to0$; the compact error then forces a norm-convergent subsequence, whose nonzero limit lies both in the kernel and its complement. This proves closed range; applying the other parametrix identity to the adjoint gives finite cokernel.

= Fredholm index
{c}
{parent=Fredholm operator}
{title2=$\operatorname{ind}T=\dim\ker T-\dim\operatorname{coker}T$}

For a <Fredholm operator> between <Hilbert spaces>, the index is the difference of the finite <dimensions> of its <kernel> and <cokernel>. Since the range is closed, its <cokernel> identifies with $\ker T^*$ by <orthogonal projection>. An invertible operator has index zero. Conversely, an index-zero <Fredholm operator> becomes invertible after adding a <finite-rank operator>: choose an isomorphism $J:\ker T\to\ker T^*$, extend it by zero on $(\ker T)^\perp$, and observe that $T+J$ maps the two summands bijectively onto $\operatorname{ran}T\oplus\ker T^*$.

= Equivariant Fredholm index
{parent=Fredholm index}
{title2=$\operatorname{ind}_G T=[\ker T]-[\operatorname{coker}T]$}

An equivariant <Fredholm operator> between <unitary representations> of a <compact group> has finite-dimensional invariant kernel and cokernel. Their formal difference is its index in the <representation ring of a compact group>. If domain and target decompose into irreducibles with finite multiplicities $m_\pi,m'_\pi$, Schur's lemma makes each intertwining block a matrix on the multiplicity spaces. Its rank cancels between kernel and cokernel, giving $\operatorname{ind}_G T=\sum_\pi(m_\pi-m'_\pi)[\pi]$, with finitely many nonzero terms.

= Local constancy of the Fredholm index
{parent=Fredholm index}

Near a <Fredholm operator>, split the domain into its kernel and orthogonal complement and the target into its range and orthogonal complement. The large invertible block stays invertible under small norm perturbations. Invertible block elimination reduces the perturbed operator to that block and a map between the finite-dimensional kernel and cokernel spaces. Its index is their dimension difference regardless of its rank. Thus the Fredholm set is open and its index is locally constant.

= Compact perturbation invariance of Fredholm operators
{parent=Fredholm operator}
{title2=$L\text{ Fredholm}\Longleftrightarrow L+K\text{ Fredholm}$}

For a <Fredholm operator> $L$ on a <Hilbert space>, invert its restriction from the kernel complement to its closed range, extending by zero on the range complement. The resulting bounded $B$ has $BL=I-P$ and $LB=I-Q$ with finite-rank projections. Adding a <compact operator> $K$ gives two-sided inverses modulo compact errors. Such a parametrix forces finite kernel, a lower bound on the kernel complement, closed range and finite adjoint kernel; the latter is the <cokernel>. Repeating with $-K$ proves the equivalence without assuming self-adjointness.

= Essential spectra of a closed operator

Several essential spectra distinguish failure of upper and lower semi-Fredholm properties. For $T=A-zI$, these failures are detected at zero by the positive self-adjoint operators $T^*T$ and $TT^*$.

= Essential spectrum in the Calkin algebra
{parent=Essential spectrum of a closed operator}
{title2=$\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(T)=\sigma_{\mathcal Q(H)}([T])$}

For a <bounded linear operator> on an infinite-dimensional complex <Hilbert space>, define the essential spectrum by failure of the <Fredholm> property for $T-\lambda I$. The <Atkinson theorem> identifies it with the spectrum in the <Calkin algebra>, hence it is compact and nonempty. On a finite-dimensional <Hilbert space> it is empty. For a bounded real diagonal operator on a separable infinite-dimensional space it equals the subsequential cluster set of the diagonal entries, including values of infinite multiplicity.

= Essential-spectrum invariance under relatively compact perturbations
{parent=Essential spectrum of a closed operator}
{title2=$\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(A+V)=\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(A)$}

Let $A$ be a <closed operator> and let $V:D(A)\to H$ be <compact> when the domain carries its <graph norm>. If $A+V$ is closed on the same domain, <compact perturbation invariance of Fredholm operators> applied to the maps $D(A)\to H$ proves invariance of the Fredholm <essential spectrum>. For self-adjoint operators this is also the <essential spectrum> obtained by deleting isolated finite-multiplicity <eigenvalues>. A bounded potential tending to zero at spatial infinity is relatively compact with respect to a second-order constant-coefficient operator: local <Rellich-Kondrachov compactness theorem> gives compactness on bounded intervals and the small tails give compactness globally.

= Weyl theorem for bounded compact perturbations
{c}
{parent=Essential spectrum of a closed operator}
{title2=$\Sigma_{\mathrm e}(L+K)=\Sigma_{\mathrm e}(L)$}

For bounded $L$ and compact $K$, use the Fredholm convention $\Sigma_{\mathrm e}(L)=\{z:L-zI\text{ is not Fredholm}\}$. Applying <compact perturbation invariance of Fredholm operators> to every shift proves the equality. This general theorem needs no self-adjointness; other definitions of <essential spectrum> for nonnormal operators must be distinguished.

= Essential spectrum of a bounded self-adjoint operator
{parent=Essential spectrum of a closed operator}
{title2=$\Sigma_{\mathrm e}(L)$}

For a bounded <self-adjoint operator> $L$ on a complex <Hilbert space>, define
$$
\Sigma_{\mathrm e}(L)=\{\lambda\in\mathbb R:\ker(L-\lambda I)\text{ is infinite-dimensional or }\operatorname{ran}(L-\lambda I)\text{ is not closed}\}.
$$
This equals the <spectrum of a bounded operator> after removing its isolated <eigenvalues> of finite multiplicity. To see isolation when the kernel is finite and the shifted range is closed, restrict the shifted operator to its kernel complement: it is invertible there by <image-kernel orthogonality for an adjoint>. A <Neumann-series perturbation> leaves nearby nonzero shifts invertible on that complement and on the finite kernel. The closed range must concern the shifted operator, not the original one.

= Weyl theorem for compact self-adjoint perturbations
{parent=Essential spectrum of a bounded self-adjoint operator}
{c}
{title2=$\Sigma_{\mathrm e}(L+K)=\Sigma_{\mathrm e}(L)$}

If $L$ is bounded and self-adjoint on a complex <Hilbert space> and $K$ is compact and self-adjoint, then
$$
\Sigma_{\mathrm e}(L+K)=\Sigma_{\mathrm e}(L).
$$
A <singular Weyl sequence> for $L$ stays singular for $L+K$, because <compact operators send weak convergence to norm convergence> and therefore $Kf_n\to0$. Applying the same argument with $-K$ proves the reverse inclusion. Finite-multiplicity isolated <eigenvalues> can move under such perturbations; the <essential spectrum of a bounded self-adjoint operator> is unchanged.

= Solvability complexity index
{title2=SCI}
{c}
{parent=Functional analysis}
{wiki}

The solvability complexity index counts nested limiting processes needed to compute a mathematical object from prescribed finite information. Arithmetic towers use finite arithmetic operations and comparisons at each stage.

= Computational problem in the SCI hierarchy
{parent=Solvability complexity index}

A computational problem is a quadruple $(\Xi,\Omega,\mathcal M,\Lambda)$: the primary set $\Omega$ contains the inputs, $\Lambda$ is the family of permitted evaluation functions, $(\mathcal M,d)$ is the output metric space, and $\Xi:\Omega\to\mathcal M$ is the problem function.

= General algorithm in the SCI hierarchy
{parent=Computational problem in the SCI hierarchy}

A general algorithm reads only finitely many evaluations for each input, its output depends only on their values, and another input giving those same values causes it to request the same finite information. No restriction is placed on the finite computation performed after reading the data.

= Arithmetic algorithm in the SCI hierarchy
{parent=General algorithm in the SCI hierarchy}

An arithmetic algorithm is a <general algorithm in the SCI hierarchy> whose finite computation uses only finitely many arithmetic operations and comparisons on the evaluated data.

= Inexact information in the SCI hierarchy
{parent=Computational problem in the SCI hierarchy}

In the $\Delta_1$ inexact-information model, a requested evaluation $\lambda(A)$ at precision $m$ may be replaced by any value within $2^{-m}$ of the exact value. An algorithm must work for every admissible stream of such approximations.

= Perfect measurement device for a dynamical system
{parent=Inexact information in the SCI hierarchy}

For a map $F:X\to X$, a perfect measurement device may query any $x\in X$ and any precision $m$, receiving $y$ with $d(F(x),y)\leq2^{-m}$. It is perfect in the information-theoretic sense: there is no sampling restriction and measurement error can be made arbitrarily small, although every terminating algorithm makes only finitely many queries.

= Tower of algorithms
{parent=Solvability complexity index}

A tower of algorithms of height $k$ is a family $\Gamma_{n_k,\ldots,n_1}$ of finite-information algorithms such that
$$
\Xi(A)=\lim_{n_k\to\infty}\cdots\lim_{n_1\to\infty}\Gamma_{n_k,\ldots,n_1}(A)
$$
for every input $A$. The <solvability complexity index> is the least possible height.

= Classical computational spectral problem
{parent=Solvability complexity index}

The classical computational spectral problem takes $\Omega=\mathcal B(\ell^2(\mathbb N))$, reads the matrix entries $\langle Ae_j,e_i\rangle$, and asks for $\operatorname{Sp}(A)$ in the <Hausdorff distance> or <Attouch--Wets topology>. Its <solvability complexity index> is three for general bounded operators.

= Finite-column decision problem
{parent=Solvability complexity index}

For a bi-infinite zero-one matrix, ask whether one uniform bound $D$ exists such that each column either contains fewer than $D$ ones or contains infinitely many ones in both directions. This decision problem has <solvability complexity index> three even for general algorithms and is a standard source of lower bounds by reduction.

= Verification in the SCI hierarchy
{parent=Solvability complexity index}

Verification means that a finite-stage output comes with a mathematically valid one-sided or two-sided error guarantee. Convergence in one limit alone does not provide such a certificate at any specified finite stage.

= Numerical range of an operator
{title2=$W(A)$}
{parent=Functional analysis}
{wiki}

The numerical range is $W(A)=\{\langle Ax,x\rangle:x\in D(A),\ \|x\|=1\}$.

= Numerical-range spectral enclosure
{parent=Numerical range of an operator}
{title2=$\sigma(T)\subseteq\overline{W(T)}$}

For a bounded <self-adjoint operator>, $z$ outside the closed numerical range yields $\|(T-z)u\|\geq\operatorname{dist}(z,\overline{W(T)})\|u\|$. The same inequality for $T-\overline z$ makes the range dense, and the first makes it closed. Thus $z$ is in the resolvent. The enclosure remains valid for general bounded Hilbert-space operators by the analogous adjoint numerical-range argument.

= Numerical-range endpoint approximate-eigenvalue theorem
{parent=Numerical-range spectral enclosure}
{title2=$\inf W(T),\sup W(T)\in\sigma_{\mathrm{ap}}(T)$}

For bounded <self-adjoint> $T$, put $B=\beta I-T\geq0$ with $\beta=\sup W(T)$. <Unit vectors> with $\langle Bu_n,u_n\rangle\to0$ satisfy $\|Bu_n\|^2\leq\|B\|\langle Bu_n,u_n\rangle$ by positive-form <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. Thus $\beta$ is an <approximate eigenvalue>. Applying the same argument to $T-\alpha I$ proves the result at $\alpha=\inf W(T)$.

= Numerical radius
{parent=Numerical range of an operator}
{title2=$w(T)=\sup_{\|u\|=1}|\langle Tu,u\rangle|$}

The radius is a scalar derived from the <numerical range of an operator>, rather than the range set itself.

For a bounded <linear operator> on a complex <Hilbert space>, the numerical radius is the supremum of the absolute values in its <numerical range of an operator>. It is a <norm> equivalent to the <operator norm>, with $w(T)\leq\|T\|\leq2w(T)$. For a self-adjoint operator the two <norms> agree.

The essential numerical range consists of limits $\lim\langle Av_n,v_n\rangle$ along unit vectors converging weakly to zero. It is stable under compact perturbations and may be empty for an unbounded operator.

= Sublinear functional

= Superlinear functional

In the functional-analytic sense used here, a superlinear functional is real valued, positively homogeneous and superadditive. Equivalently $-q$ is a <sublinear functional>. This definition concerns functional inequalities, rather than a comparison of asymptotic growth rates. Such a $q$ is concave, satisfies $q(0)=0$, and can serve as a lower support in the <sandwich form of the Hahn-Banach theorem>.

= Hahn-Banach theorem
{c}
{parent=Functional analysis}
{wiki=Hahn–Banach_theorem}

Every bounded linear functional on a linear subspace of a real normed space extends to the whole space without increasing its norm.

= Complex Hahn-Banach theorem
{parent=Hahn-Banach theorem}

A bounded complex-linear <linear functional> on a complex <linear subspace> of a <normed space> extends to the whole space with the same <operator norm>. To deduce this from the real <Hahn-Banach theorem>, extend the real part $u$ under the bound $\|\ell\|\|x\|$ on the underlying real <vector space>, then put $L(x)=u(x)-iu(ix)$. This is complex-linear and agrees with $\ell$ on its original domain. Given $x$, choose a complex number $a$ of modulus one with $aL(x)=|L(x)|$. Then $|L(x)|=\operatorname{Re}L(ax)=u(ax)\leq\|\ell\|\|x\|$. Restriction gives the reverse <operator norm> inequality.

= Convex domination form of the Hahn-Banach theorem
{parent=Hahn-Banach theorem}
{title2=$g|_W=f,\quad g\le\phi$}

A <linear functional> $f$ dominated by a finite real <convex function> $\phi$ on a <vector subspace> $W$ extends with the same domination to the whole real <vector space>. To add $v\notin W$, choose $g(v)$ between the supremum of $(f(w)-\phi(w-tv))/t$ and the infimum of $(\phi(w+sv)-f(w))/s$, over $w\in W$ and $s,t>0$. Convexity, applied to $(s(w_1-tv)+t(w_2+sv))/(s+t)$, makes every lower candidate at most every upper candidate. The candidates at $w=0,s=t=1$ bound the interval by finite numbers. Chain unions preserve domination, so <Zorn's lemma> gives a full extension. No homogeneity of $\phi$ is assumed.

= Sandwich form of the Hahn-Banach theorem
{parent=Hahn-Banach theorem}
{title2=$q\leq U\leq p$}

If $p$ is a <sublinear functional>, $q$ a <superlinear functional> and $q\leq p$ on a real vector space, there exists a linear functional $U$ between them. More generally, a linear functional $S$ on a subspace extends with this domination whenever $S(y)\leq p(x+y)-q(x)$ for all ambient $x$ and subspace $y$. In a one-dimensional extension the <admissible values for a sandwich extension> form a nonempty real interval. Positive homogeneity checks the extension on both signs of the new coordinate. Chain unions preserve linearity and the mixed domination, so <Zorn's lemma> gives a maximal extension, whose domain must be the whole space. Finally $x=0$ in the mixed inequality gives the upper bound; using the pair $(x,-x)$ gives the lower bound. Conversely, any linear $U$ between $q,p$ obeys the mixed inequality by $U(y)=U(x+y)-U(x)$.

= Admissible values for a sandwich extension
{parent=Sandwich form of the Hahn-Banach theorem}
{title2=$\sup_{x,y}[S(y)-p(x+y-z)+q(x)]\leq U(z)\leq\inf_{x,y}[-S(y)+p(x+y+z)-q(x)]$}

Suppose $S$ satisfies the mixed domination on a subspace $Y$, and $z\notin Y$. Apply that domination to $y+y'$ and $x+x'$, split the $p$ argument as $(x+y+z)+(x'+y'-z)$, and use superadditivity of $q$. This proves every lower candidate in the displayed interval is at most every upper candidate. The zero-vector candidates bound the lower supremum below by $-p(-z)$ and the upper infimum above by $p(z)$, so both endpoints are finite. Choosing any intervening value $a$ permits $U(y+tz)=S(y)+ta$. For $t>0$, scale the upper comparison by $t$; for $t<0$, scale the lower comparison by $-t$. Both give the required mixed domination, proving the interval characterizes the permitted extension values.

= Choice-free Hahn-Banach extension in a separable space
{parent=Hahn-Banach theorem}
{title2=$\widetilde T|_U=T,\quad\|\widetilde T\|=\|T\|$}

In a real <normed vector space> with a specified countable <dense subset>, a <bounded linear functional> on any <vector subspace> admits a norm-preserving extension without the <axiom of choice>. Add the dense vectors one at a time using the supremum endpoint in the <one-dimensional dominated extension of a real linear functional>. This makes the recursion deterministic. Extend from the resulting dense subspace by continuity, using the least index in the dense list which approximates a given vector to within $2^{-n}$.

= Separation of a point from a closed linear subspace
{parent=Hahn-Banach theorem}
{title2=$f(x_0)=1,\quad f|_Y=0$}

If $Y$ is a <closed linear subspace> of a Hausdorff <locally convex space> and $x_0\notin Y$, choose a continuous <seminorm> $q$ with $q(x_0-y)\geq1$ for all $y\in Y$, using a basic neighborhood disjoint from $Y$. The <linear functional> $g(y+tx_0)=t$ is bounded by $q$. The <Hahn-Banach theorem> extends it to a continuous <linear functional> with the displayed values. This is the linear-subspace form of separation, sharper than merely distinguishing the two sets.

= One-dimensional dominated extension of a real linear functional
{parent=Hahn-Banach theorem}
{title2=$\sup_m(g(m)-p(m-v))\leq c\leq\inf_m(p(m+v)-g(m))$}

A real <linear functional> $g$ on a <vector subspace>, dominated by a <sublinear function> $p$, can be extended across one new vector $v$ by setting $\widetilde g(m+tv)=g(m)+tc$. The displayed lower and upper bounds are compatible because $g(m+n)\leq p(m-v)+p(n+v)$. A value between them preserves domination for both signs of $t$. Chain unions and <Zorn's lemma> then yield the full <Hahn-Banach theorem>.

= Countable norming family
{title2=$\lVert x\rVert=\sup_n\varphi_n(x)$}

A real <separable Banach space> has a sequence $(\varphi_n)$ in its dual unit ball with $\lVert x\rVert=\sup_n\varphi_n(x)$ for every $x$. Choose a dense sequence $(u_n)$ in the unit sphere and apply the <Hahn-Banach theorem> to obtain $\varphi_n(u_n)=1$. In the complex case use $\sup_n\operatorname{Re}\varphi_n(x)$.

= Hahn-Banach distance formula

For a closed subspace $Y$ of a normed space $X$,
$$
d(x,Y)=\sup\{|f(x)|:f\in X^*,\ \lVert f\rVert\leq1,\ f|_Y=0\}.
$$
This is the dual norm formula in the quotient $X/Y$.

= Canonical embedding into the bidual
{parent=Hahn-Banach theorem}

For a normed space $X$, the map $J:X\to X''$ defined by
$$
(Jx)(f)=f(x)
$$
is a linear isometry. The easy inequality is $\|Jx\|\leq\|x\|$; Hahn--Banach extends the norm-one functional on $\operatorname{span}\{x\}$ that takes $x$ to $\|x\|$, proving the reverse inequality.

= Singular functional on L infinity
{parent=Hahn-Banach theorem}

Point evaluation at zero is bounded on $C([0,1])$ with the essential-supremum norm, and Hahn--Banach extends it to a bounded functional on $L^\infty([0,1])$. No $g\in L^1$ represents this extension: continuous functions supported in shrinking neighbourhoods of zero have value one at zero while their integrals against $g$ tend to zero. Hence $(L^\infty)'$ is strictly larger than $L^1$.

= Separation from an absorbing convex set
{parent=Hahn-Banach separation theorem}

If a <convex set> $E$ in a real <normed vector space> contains $B(0,\epsilon)$, every $x\notin E$ admits a <continuous linear functional> $T$ with $Tx\geq1\geq Te$ for all $e\in E$, even if $E$ is not closed. Its <Minkowski functional> $p(v)=\inf\{t>0:v\in tE\}$ is <sublinear functional>, satisfies $p(v)\leq\|v\|/\epsilon$, $p(e)\leq1$, and $p(x)\geq1$. Extend $tx\mapsto tp(x)$ by the dominated <Hahn-Banach theorem>. The resulting domination $T\leq p$ applied to $v$ and $-v$ makes $T$ continuous and supplies the separation inequalities.

= Geometric Hahn-Banach theorem for radially open sets
{parent=Hahn-Banach separation theorem}
{title2=$U\cap C=\varnothing\Longrightarrow U\subseteq\ker L,\quad L(C)<0$}

For a nonempty <radially open convex set> $C$ disjoint from a <vector subspace> $U$, there is a nonzero <linear functional> $L$ whose <kernel of a linear map> contains $U$ and avoids $C$. Choose $a\in C$ and the <Minkowski functional> $p$ of $C-a$. On $U+\mathbb Ra$, set $g(u+ta)=-t$. Disjointness implies $p(u-a)\geq1$, which gives $g\leq p$ for negative $t$; nonnegativity of $p$ gives it for nonnegative $t$. The algebraic <Hahn-Banach theorem> extends $g$ to $L\leq p$. For $x\in C$, $L(x)+1=L(x-a)<1$, so $L(x)<0$. Also $L(a)=-1$ and $L|_U=0$.

= Finite-dimensional interpolation form of Goldstine's theorem
{parent=Goldstine theorem}

= Lax-Milgram theorem
{c}
{parent=Functional analysis}

The theorem supplies solvability for a coercive <weak formulation>.

A <bounded bilinear form> $a$ on a real <Hilbert space> $H$ that is a <coercive bilinear form> represents every bounded <linear functional> uniquely: for each $\ell\in H'$, there is a unique $u\in H$ with $a(u,v)=\ell(v)$ for every $v\in H$.

For a proof, the <Riesz representation theorem> gives a <bounded linear operator> $T$ and a <vector> $g$ with $a(u,v)=\langle Tu,v\rangle$ and $\ell(v)=\langle g,v\rangle$. Coercivity and the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> give $\|Tu\|\geq\alpha\|u\|$, so $T$ is injective and its image is closed: convergence of $Tu_n$ makes $u_n$ a <Cauchy sequence>, whose limit supplies the image limit. If $v$ belongs to the <orthogonal complement> of the image, then $a(u,v)=0$ for every $u$; setting $u=v$ and using coercivity forces $v=0$. Thus the image is also dense and equals $H$. There is consequently a unique $u$ with $Tu=g$, and $\|u\|\leq\|\ell\|/\alpha$. The complex version uses a bounded sesquilinear form with $\operatorname{Re}a(u,u)\geq\alpha\|u\|^2$.

= Space of continuous functions on a compact space
{title2=$C(K)$}
{parent=Space of continuous functions vanishing at infinity}

For a <compact Hausdorff space> $K$, $C(K)$ consists of continuous scalar-valued functions with the <supremum norm>. It is a <Banach space> and, with complex scalars, pointwise multiplication and conjugation make it a commutative unital <C-star algebra>.

Why does <completeness> hold? A <Cauchy sequence> in the <supremum norm> is Cauchy at every point, hence has a pointwise limit. The uniform Cauchy estimate passes to that limit, giving <uniform convergence>; the <uniform limit theorem> makes the limit continuous. Thus the <norm> and the chosen regularity of the functions cooperate to keep every limit inside $C(K)$.

Changing to the <L2 norm> on $C([0,1])$ loses this property. For integers $n\geq2$, the <continuous functions> $h_n(x)=\min(1,\max(0,n(x-1/2)))$ approximate the <step function> $h(x)=0$ for $x\leq1/2$ and $h(x)=1$ for $x>1/2$. Direct integration gives
$$
\|h_n-h\|_2^2=\int_{1/2}^{1/2+1/n}\bigl(1-n(x-1/2)\bigr)^2\,dx=\frac1{3n}.
$$
The <triangle inequality> makes $(h_n)$ a <Cauchy sequence>, but no <continuous function> agrees with $h$ <almost everywhere>: continuity would force value zero on the whole left open interval and one on the whole right open interval. Hence this <normed space> is incomplete. The missing limit exists in <L2 space>, its <completion of a normed space>, showing exactly what the larger space adds.

= Continuous functions are meagre in L1
{parent=Space of continuous functions on a compact space}

The image of $C([0,1])$ in the <L1 space> $L^1(0,1)$ is a <meagre set>. The canonical map $J$ is a <bounded linear operator> from the <Banach space> $C([0,1])$ with its <supremum norm>, since $\|Jf\|_1\leq\|f\|_\infty$. It is not onto: the indicator of $(1/2,1)$ has no <continuous function> representative. Continuity would force values zero throughout $(0,1/2)$ and one throughout $(1/2,1)$, contradicting continuity at $1/2$. The <operator range category dichotomy> therefore proves the claim. Continuous ramps $f_n(t)=\min(1,\max(0,n(t-1/2)))$ also show directly that this image is not closed, since their <L1 norm> distance to that indicator is $1/(2n)$ for $n\geq2$.

= Planar logarithm factorization
{parent=Space of continuous functions on a compact space}
{title2=$C(K,\mathbb C^*)=\exp C(K)\times\langle z-a_U:U\text{ bounded complementary component}\rangle$}

{title2=$(Qf)_0=-f_0,\quad (Qf)_n=4^n(f_{n-1}/2-f_n)$}

On its maximal domain in $\ell^2$, put $g_n=2^nf_n$. Then $g_n-g_{n-1}=-2^{-n}(Qf)_n$, so $g_n$ has a finite limit $\ell_f$. Summation gives $\operatorname{Re}\langle Qf,f\rangle=-\tfrac12(|g_0|^2+|\ell_f|^2+\sum_{n\geq1}|g_n-g_{n-1}|^2)$. For $\alpha>0$, the resolvent equation is the recursion $g_n=(g_{n-1}+2^{-n}h_n)/(1+\alpha4^{-n})$, which produces a bounded $g_n$ and hence $f\in\ell^2$. The <Lumer-Phillips theorem> proves contraction-semigroup generation.

= Feller semigroup
{parent=Contraction semigroup}
{c}
{title2=$P_t1=1,\quad f\geq0\Longrightarrow P_tf\geq0$}

On a compact <metric space>, a conservative Feller semigroup is a <strongly continuous semigroup> of positive unital operators on $C(X)$. The <Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem> associates a <Markov kernel> to each operator. On noncompact spaces the standard definition uses $C_0(X)$; conservativity then refers to the corresponding kernels having mass one, even when $1\notin C_0(X)$.

= Symmetric Feller semigroup
{parent=Feller semigroup}
{title2=$\langle P_tf,g\rangle_{L^2(\mu)}=\langle f,P_tg\rangle_{L^2(\mu)}$}

A <Feller semigroup> symmetric in the space associated with an invariant measure has a self-adjoint contraction realization in $L^2(\mu)$. Its two-point kernel measure $\mu(dx)P_t(x,dy)$ is symmetric. This reversibility expresses its joint energy as an integral of products of increments.

= Power inequality for symmetric Markov energies
{parent=Joint energy of a symmetric Markov semigroup}
{title2=$\mathcal E(f^{q/2})\leq\frac{q^2}{4(q-1)}\mathcal E(f^{q-1},f)$}

For $a,b\geq0$ and $q>2$, <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> applied to the integral of $(q/2)s^{q/2-1}$ gives $(a^{q/2}-b^{q/2})^2\leq q^2( a^{q-1}-b^{q-1})(a-b)/(4(q-1))$. Integrating against the symmetric two-point kernel measure and taking the energy limit proves the displayed inequality.

= Carré du champ operator
{parent=Feller semigroup}
{wiki}
{title2=$\Gamma(f,g)=\tfrac12\bigl(L(fg)-fLg-gLf\bigr)$}

= Carré du champ
{synonym}

For a <Markov process> generator and suitable real functions, this bilinear expression records the local quadratic energy. <Positivity> of $\Gamma(f,f)$ follows by differentiating $P_t(f^2)\geq(P_tf)^2$. For $L=D^2-xD$ it is $f'g'$, giving the <Gaussian Dirichlet energy> after integration against the invariant measure.

= Generator square inequality
{parent=Carré du champ operator}
{title2=$L(f^2)\geq2fLf$}

If real $f$ and $f^2$ belong to the <generator domain> of a <Feller semigroup>, <Jensen inequality> gives $P_t(f^2)-(P_tf)^2\geq0$. This expression is zero at $t=0$, so its right <derivative> is nonnegative, proving the inequality. It is equivalent to <positivity> of the <carré du champ>.

= Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup
{parent=Contraction semigroup}
{c}
{title2=$P_t=e^{t(D^2-xD)}$}

The one-dimensional semigroup associated with the standard <Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process> has invariant standard <Gaussian measure> and generator $Lf=f''-xf'$. Its <Mehler formula for the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup> makes <positivity> and invariance explicit. The normalized <probabilists' Hermite polynomials> diagonalize it, with $P_th_n=e^{-nt}h_n$. It has <spectral gap> one and satisfies the sharp <Gaussian Poincaré inequality>.

= Ornstein-Uhlenbeck gradient commutation identity
{parent=Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup}
{c}
{title2=$(P_tf)'=e^{-t}P_t(f')$}

Differentiating the <Mehler formula for the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup> proves the identity for continuously differentiable functions with bounded <derivative>. Density extends it to the Gaussian Sobolev form domain. Together with a weighted <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>, it yields the <Ornstein-Uhlenbeck entropy dissipation identity> proof of the <Gaussian logarithmic Sobolev inequality>.

= Gaussian creation and annihilation operators
{parent=Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup}
{c}
{title2=$a^-=D,\quad a^+=x-D$}

On polynomials in $L^2$ of standard <Gaussian measure>, <Gaussian integration by parts> makes $D$ and $x-D$ adjoints, with commutator $[a^-,a^+]=I$. The <probabilists' Hermite polynomials> are obtained by applying $a^+$ repeatedly to $1$, and $a^-\operatorname{He}_n=n\operatorname{He}_{n-1}$. These give a Gaussian-space realization of <creation and annihilation operators>.

= Gaussian number operator
{parent=Gaussian creation and annihilation operators}
{c}
{title2=$N=a^+a^-=-D^2+xD$}

The normalized <probabilists' Hermite polynomials> diagonalize the Gaussian number operator with <eigenvalues> $n\geq0$. Its self-adjoint closure has domain $\sum n^2|c_n|^2<\infty$. Polynomial truncations are dense for its graph <norm>. The semigroup $e^{-tN}$ is the <Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup> and its energy form is the <Gaussian Dirichlet energy>.

= Gaussian Dirichlet energy
{parent=Gaussian number operator}
{c}
{title2=$\mathcal E_\gamma(f)=\int|f'|^2\,d\gamma=\sum_{n\geq1}n|c_n|^2$}

The energy form of the <Gaussian number operator> is the displayed squared <derivative> integral. Its form domain requires $\sum n|c_n|^2<\infty$, while the full <operator domain> requires $\sum n^2|c_n|^2<\infty$. It can be defined by $\lim_{t\downarrow0}t^{-1}(\|f\|_2^2-\langle f,P_tf\rangle)$ even when $Nf$ does not exist in $L^2$. This energy occurs in the <Gaussian Poincaré inequality> and <Gaussian logarithmic Sobolev inequality>.

= Mehler formula for the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup
{parent=Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup}
{c}
{title2=$P_tf(x)=\mathbb E f(e^{-t}x+\sqrt{1-e^{-2t}}Z)$}

Here $Z$ has the standard <normal distribution>. Applied to the generating function $e^{sx-s^2/2}$, the <Gaussian moment-generating function> replaces $s$ by $e^{-t}s$, proving the Hermite <eigenvalue> identity. <Positivity> and preservation of <Gaussian measure> then give the $L^2$ contraction and identify the full <Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semigroup> by polynomial density.

= Multiplication semigroup
{parent=Contraction semigroup}
{title2=$P_tf=e^{-tk}f$}

A nonnegative continuous multiplier $k$ defines a <contraction semigroup> on the <space of continuous functions vanishing at infinity> and on $L^2(\mu)$ by $P_tf=e^{-tk}f$. <Strong continuity> follows from <uniform convergence> on <compact sets> and small tails in $C_0$, or the <dominated convergence theorem> in $L^2$. The multiplier itself may be unbounded.

= Generator of a multiplication semigroup
{parent=Multiplication semigroup}
{title2=$Lf=-kf,\quad D(L)=\{f:kf\text{ belongs to the underlying space}\}$}

The <infinitesimal generator of a semigroup> for $P_tf=e^{-tk}f$ is multiplication by $-k$ on its maximal domain. The bound $|(1-e^{-tk})/t|\leq k$ proves the <norm> <derivative> when $kf$ is integrable; in $C_0$ use a compact-small-tail argument on $kf$. Conversely <norm> convergence identifies the pointwise <derivative> as $-kf$. Limits of graph pairs satisfy the same multiplication identity, proving it is a <closed operator>.

= Semigroup property
{parent=C0-semigroup}

The semigroup property is $U(t+s)=U(t)U(s)$ for nonnegative times, together with $U(0)=I$. It expresses that evolving for two consecutive intervals is equivalent to evolving for their total duration.

= Strong continuity
{parent=C0-semigroup}

An operator family $U(t)$ is strongly continuous when $t\mapsto U(t)x$ is norm-continuous for every vector $x$. This is continuity in the <strong operator topology>.

= Infinitesimal generator of a semigroup
{title2=$A$}
{parent=C0-semigroup}

= Semigroup generator
{synonym}

The infinitesimal generator is the generally unbounded operator
$$
Ax=\lim_{t\downarrow0}\frac{U(t)x-x}{t}
$$
on the vectors for which the norm limit exists.

= Yosida approximation
{c}
{parent=Infinitesimal generator of a semigroup}
{title2=$L_\lambda=\lambda^2(\lambda I-L)^{-1}-\lambda I$}

For a <contraction semigroup> with <semigroup generator> $L$, the bounded operators $L_\lambda$ approximate $L$ on its <generator domain>. The <Laplace-transform formula for a semigroup resolvent> and <strong continuity> give $\lambda R_\lambda g\to g$ for every vector $g$: split $\int_0^\infty\lambda e^{-\lambda t}(P_tg-g)dt$ into a short interval and an exponentially small tail. For $f\in D(L)$, <integration by parts> gives $R_\lambda Lf=\lambda R_\lambda f-f$, so $L_\lambda f=\lambda R_\lambda Lf\to Lf$. No <operator norm> convergence of $\lambda R_\lambda$ is implied.

= Semigroup commutation with its unbounded generator
{parent=Infinitesimal generator of a semigroup}
{title2=$T_tD(Z)\subseteq D(Z),\quad ZT_tf=T_tZf$}

For a <strongly continuous semigroup>, apply the bounded operator $T_t$ to the difference quotient defining its <semigroup generator>. The limit proves domain invariance and commutation on the generator domain. This is not an assertion that the unbounded generator acts on every vector.

= Generator of a strongly continuous semigroup is closed and densely defined
{parent=Infinitesimal generator of a semigroup}

The <Yosida averaging of a semigroup> gives $x_h=h^{-1}\int_0^hS(t)x\,dt\in D(A)$ with $x_h\to x$, proving that the <generator domain> is dense. For $x\in D(A)$, the <Bochner integral> identity $S(t)x-x=\int_0^tS(s)Ax\,ds$ holds. If $x_n\to x$ and $Ax_n\to y$, pass to the limit in this identity and divide by $t$. The <strongly continuous semigroup> property makes the resulting limit $y$, so $x\in D(A)$ and $Ax=y$. This proves that the <infinitesimal generator of a semigroup> is a <closed operator>.

= Generator domain
{title2=$D(A)$}
{parent=Infinitesimal generator of a semigroup}

The generator domain consists of the vectors whose semigroup orbit is right-differentiable at zero. It is a dense linear subspace, and the generator is closed.

= Semigroup restricted to its generator domain
{parent=Generator domain}

Equip $D(A)$ with its <graph norm>. The restrictions $U(t)|_{D(A)}$ form a <C0-semigroup> on this <Banach space>; its generator is $A$ restricted to $D(A^2)$.

= Hille-Yosida theorem
{c}
{parent=C0-semigroup}
{wiki=Hille–Yosida_theorem}

The Hille-Yosida theorem characterizes generators of exponentially bounded C0-semigroups by closedness, dense domain, a resolvent half-line, and uniform bounds on every positive resolvent power.

= Laplace-transform formula for a semigroup resolvent
{parent=Hille-Yosida theorem}

If $A$ generates a <C0-semigroup> satisfying $\|U(t)\|\leq Me^{\omega t}$, then for $\operatorname{Re}z>\omega$ its <resolvent operator> is
$$
(zI-A)^{-1}x=\int_0^\infty e^{-zt}U(t)x\,dt.
$$
Repeated differentiation gives $n!(zI-A)^{-(n+1)}x=\int_0^\infty t^ne^{-zt}U(t)x\,dt$.

= Yosida averaging of a semigroup
{c}
{parent=Hille-Yosida theorem}

For $x$ in the underlying Banach space,
$$
x_t=\frac1t\int_0^tU(s)x\,ds
$$
belongs to the generator domain, satisfies $Ax_t=[U(t)x-x]/t$, and converges to $x$ as $t\downarrow0$.

= Exponentially shifted semigroup
{parent=C0-semigroup}

If $U(t)$ has generator $A$, then $\widehat U(t)=e^{-\omega t}U(t)$ has generator $A-\omega I$. This shift converts a growth bound $Me^{\omega t}$ into the uniform bound $M$.

= Stable family of semigroup generators
{parent=C0-semigroup}

A time-indexed family $A(t)$ is stable with constants $M,\omega$ when products of its frozen semigroups obey
$$
\left\|e^{s_nA(t_n)}\cdots e^{s_1A(t_1)}\right\|
\leq Me^{\omega(s_1+\cdots+s_n)}
$$
for all nonnegative $s_j$. This condition controls products uniformly as the number of time slices grows.

= Dissipative operator
{parent=C0-semigroup}
{wiki}

= Dissipativity
{synonym}

On a Hilbert space, an operator $A$ is dissipative when $\operatorname{Re}(Ax,x)\leq0$ for every $x\in D(A)$. This is the infinitesimal form of norm contraction.

= Positive spectrum of a dissipative operator is adjoint point spectrum
{parent=Dissipative operator}
{title2=$\alpha\in\sigma(Z)\Longleftrightarrow\alpha\in\sigma_p(Z^*)\quad(\alpha>0)$}

For a closed densely defined <dissipative operator>, the estimate $\|(\alpha I-Z)f\|\geq\alpha\|f\|$ makes its range closed and its kernel trivial. Thus a positive spectral value means this range is proper, which is equivalent to a nonzero vector in $\ker(\alpha I-Z^*)$. Dissipativity is essential: a real multiplication operator may have positive continuous spectrum with no adjoint eigenvector.

= Dissipative Cayley-transform contraction
{parent=Dissipative operator}
{title2=$\|(I-hL/2)^{-1}(I+hL/2)\|_2\leq1$}

If $\operatorname{Re}\langle Lv,v\rangle\leq0$ in a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, $I-hL/2$ is invertible for every positive $h$. The relation $v_+-v_-=(h/2)L(v_++v_-)$ gives $\|v_+\|^2-\|v_-\|^2=(h/2)\operatorname{Re}\langle L(v_++v_-),v_++v_-\rangle\leq0$. Thus the <Crank-Nicolson method> contracts for such a linear system even when $L$ is a <non-normal matrix>. This is a direct energy result, not an unqualified inequality by a scalar rational function evaluated at a logarithmic norm.

= Maximal dissipative operator
{parent=Dissipative operator}

= Maximal dissipative
{synonym}

A maximal dissipative operator is dissipative and has no proper dissipative extension. Equivalently, for a densely defined dissipative operator, $\lambda I-A$ is onto for some positive $\lambda$.

{c}
{parent=Maximal dissipative operator}
{wiki=Lumer–Phillips_theorem}

The Lumer-Phillips theorem says that a densely defined operator generates a contraction C0-semigroup exactly when it is maximal dissipative.

= Strongly continuous unitary group
{parent=C0-semigroup}

A strongly continuous unitary group is a family $U(t)$ for all real $t$ that is strongly continuous, obeys the group law, and preserves the Hilbert norm. Its generator is skew-adjoint; conversely, every skew-adjoint operator generates such a group.

= Stone's theorem on one-parameter unitary groups
{c}
{parent=Strongly continuous unitary group}
{wiki}
{title2=$U_t=e^{itA},\qquad A=A^*$}

= Stone theorem for one-parameter unitary groups
{c}
{synonym}

Every <strongly continuous unitary group> has a unique possibly unbounded <self-adjoint operator> $A$ as its generator. Its domain consists of the <vectors> for which the <norm> limit of $(U_t\xi-\xi)/(it)$ exists as $t\to0$. Conversely, the <Borel functional calculus for a normal operator> constructs a <strongly continuous unitary group> from every <self-adjoint> $A$. Smooth averaging supplies a dense generator domain; Laplace integrals of $U_t$ and $U_{-t}$ prove that $A\pm i$ are surjective, which establishes self-adjointness.

= Skew-adjoint generator
{parent=Strongly continuous unitary group}

= Skew-adjoint
{synonym}

= Anti-self-adjoint
{synonym}

= Skew-adjoint operator
{synonym}

An operator $A$ is skew-adjoint when $A^*=-A$. Both $A$ and $-A$ are then maximal dissipative, and the generated group is unitary.

= Even rank of a skew-adjoint linear map
{parent=Skew-adjoint generator}
{title2=$\operatorname{rank}A\in2\mathbb N_0$}

On a finite-dimensional real <inner product space>, a <skew-adjoint operator> has even rank. In an orthonormal basis its <matrix> is skew-symmetric, so an invertible such matrix must have even dimension by $\det A=\det(-A)=(-1)^d\det A$. Moreover $\ker A=(\operatorname{im}A)^\perp$, so the image and kernel intersect trivially. The restriction of $A$ to its image is an invertible skew-adjoint map, and its dimension, the rank, is therefore even.

= Skew-adjoint nilpotent operators vanish
{parent=Skew-adjoint generator}
{title2=$A^*=-A,\ A^k=0\ \Longrightarrow\ A=0$}

On a finite-dimensional positive inner-product space, a skew-adjoint <nilpotent operator> is zero. In an orthonormal basis, $\|A\|_F^2=\operatorname{tr}(A^*A)=-\operatorname{tr}(A^2)=0$, since a <nilpotent matrix> has zero trace and so does its square. The <Frobenius norm> therefore vanishes. In particular, a nonzero nilpotent adjoint map rules out a <compact Lie algebra>.

= Abstract Cauchy problem
{parent=C0-semigroup}
{wiki}

An abstract Cauchy problem has the form $Z'(t)=AZ(t)+F(t)$ in a Banach space, where $A$ may be unbounded and generates the homogeneous evolution.

= Classical solution of an abstract Cauchy problem
{parent=Abstract Cauchy problem}
{title2=$u'=Lu+f$}

= Classical solutions of an abstract Cauchy problem
{synonym}

For a <closed operator> $L$ on a <Banach space> $H$, a positive-time classical solution belongs to $C([0,T],H)\cap C^1((0,T],H)$, takes values in $D(L)$ for positive time, and has $Lu\in C((0,T],H)$. It satisfies the <differential equation> and the initial condition in $H$. Classical regularity also at time zero requires the corresponding regularity of the initial datum, in particular $u_0\in D(L)$. For an <analytic semigroup> and <Hölder continuous> forcing, the <variation-of-constants formula> gives such a solution: subtracting the forcing at the current time turns the singular estimate $\|LS(r)\|\leq C/r$ into the integrable bound $Cr^{\theta-1}$.

= Nonautonomous abstract Cauchy problem
{parent=Abstract Cauchy problem}

A nonautonomous abstract Cauchy problem has the form $u'(t)=A(t)u(t)$, with a time-dependent generally <closed linear operator>. Under stability, common-domain, and regularity hypotheses, it is propagated by an <evolution family>.

= Evolution family
{title2=$U(t,s)$}
{parent=Nonautonomous abstract Cauchy problem}

= Solution operator for a nonautonomous evolution equation
{synonym}

An evolution family consists of bounded operators $U(t,s)$ for $s\leq t$ satisfying $U(s,s)=I$ and $U(t,r)U(r,s)=U(t,s)$. For sufficiently regular data it solves $\partial_tU(t,s)x=A(t)U(t,s)x$ and $\partial_sU(t,s)x=-U(t,s)A(s)x$.

= Frozen-generator product approximation
{parent=Evolution family}

For a partition $s=t_0<\cdots<t_N=t$, the frozen-generator approximation is
$$
U_N(t,s)=e^{(t_N-t_{N-1})A(t_{N-1})}\cdots e^{(t_1-t_0)A(t_0)}.
$$
Under the hypotheses of the nonautonomous generation theorem, these products converge strongly and uniformly on compact time triangles to the <evolution family>.

= Mild solution of an abstract Cauchy problem
{parent=Abstract Cauchy problem}

A mild solution need only be continuous and satisfy the integrated semigroup formula. It need not lie in the generator domain or be differentiable.

= Variation-of-constants formula
{parent=Mild solution of an abstract Cauchy problem}

If $A$ generates $U(t)$, the variation-of-constants formula is
$$
Z(t)=U(t)Z_0+\int_0^tU(t-s)F(s)\,ds.
$$
It is also called Duhamel's formula.

= Semilinear abstract Cauchy problem
{parent=Abstract Cauchy problem}

A semilinear abstract Cauchy problem has $Z'=AZ+N(Z)$, where the linear part generates a C0-semigroup and the nonlinear map is typically locally Lipschitz on the phase space.

= Local mild solution of a semilinear evolution equation
{parent=Semilinear abstract Cauchy problem}

A local mild solution is a fixed point of the nonlinear variation-of-constants map in $C([0,T];X)$. Local Lipschitz continuity of the nonlinearity gives existence and uniqueness for sufficiently small $T$.

= Blow-up alternative for a semilinear evolution equation
{parent=Local mild solution of a semilinear evolution equation}

For a locally Lipschitz semilinear evolution, a maximal mild solution either exists for all positive time or its phase-space norm becomes unbounded as the finite maximal time is approached.
