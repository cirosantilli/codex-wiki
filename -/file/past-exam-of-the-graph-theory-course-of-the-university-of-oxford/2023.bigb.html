= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

A <proper edge colouring> assigns colours to the <edges> of a <graph> so that distinct <edges> sharing a <vertex> have different colours. The <edge chromatic number> $\chi'(G)$ is the least number of colours in such an assignment. Equivalently, it is the least number of <matchings in a graph> whose disjoint union is the entire collection of <edges>.

<Vizing's theorem> states that a finite <simple graph> of <maximum degree> $\Delta$ satisfies
$$
\boxed{\Delta\leq\chi'(G)\leq\Delta+1.}
$$
The lower bound holds because all <edges> incident with a <vertex> of <vertex degree> $\Delta$ require different colours.

<Hall's marriage theorem> states that a finite <bipartite graph> with <vertex sets> $A,B$ has a <matching in a graph> saturating $A$ if and only if
$$
\boxed{|N(S)|\geq|S|\quad\text{for every }S\subseteq A,}
$$
where $N(S)\subseteq B$ is the <graph neighbourhood> of $S$. Here <matching saturation> means that each <vertex> of $A$ is incident with an <edge> of the <matching in a graph>; it does not require every <vertex> of $B$ to be met.

= Solution

{c}

Let $A_\Delta=\{a\in A:d_G(a)=\Delta\}$. Apply <Hall's marriage theorem> to the <induced subgraph> on $A_\Delta\cup B$, which is a <bipartite graph>. For any <subset> $S\subseteq A_\Delta$, <double counting> the <edges> from $S$ to its <graph neighbourhood> gives
$$
\Delta|S|=\sum_{a\in S}d_G(a)=e_G(S,N(S))
\leq\sum_{b\in N(S)}d_G(b)\leq\Delta|N(S)|.
$$
The equality uses the fact that every <neighbour> of $S$ lies in $B$, so all <edges> incident with $S$ have been counted. The final inequality uses the <maximum degree> bound on $B$. Since $\Delta>0$, division gives the <Hall condition> $|S|\leq|N(S)|$.

Consequently there is a <matching in a graph> $M_1$ saturating $A_\Delta$. If $A_\Delta$ is empty, use the empty <matching in a graph>. This proves the required <matching covering maximum-degree vertices in a bipartite graph> on the specified side; other <vertices> need not be met.

By part (i), choose a <matching in a graph> $M_1$ meeting all <vertices> of <vertex degree> $\Delta$ in $A$. Interchanging the two <vertex sets> gives a <matching in a graph> $M_2$ meeting all such <vertices> in $B$. Consider the spanning <subgraph> $H$ with <edges> $M_1\cup M_2$.

Every <vertex> has <vertex degree> at most two in $H$. An <edge> belonging to both <matchings in a graph> forms a whole two-<vertex> <connected component of a graph>, since neither endpoint can meet another <edge> of either <matching in a graph>. On every other nontrivial <connected component of a graph>, the <edges> alternate between $M_1$ and $M_2$, so the <connected component of a graph> is a <graph path> or an even <graph cycle>. We select one of the two <matchings in a graph> separately on each <connected component of a graph>.

On a <graph cycle>, either choice covers every <vertex>. On a <graph path> with an odd number of <edges>, the first and last <edges> belong to the same <matching in a graph>; choose it, and it covers every <vertex> of the <graph path>. For a common <edge>, choose that <edge>.

It remains to treat a <graph path> with a positive even number of <edges>. Its endpoints lie in the same side of the <bipartite graph>. Every internal <vertex> meets both <matchings in a graph>. If both endpoints lie in $A$, choose $M_1$: every endpoint of <vertex degree> $\Delta$ must already be met by $M_1$, by its defining property. If both endpoints lie in $B$, choose $M_2$ for the same reason. Thus the choice meets all <vertices> of <vertex degree> $\Delta$ in that <connected component of a graph>.

Finally, an isolated <vertex> of $H$ cannot have <vertex degree> $\Delta$ in $G$, since it would have been met by $M_1$ or $M_2$. The union of the selected <edges> over the <connected components of a graph> is a <matching in a graph>, because different <connected components of a graph> have disjoint <vertex sets>. It meets every <vertex> of <vertex degree> $\Delta$ in $G$. The useful mechanism is the <union of two matchings> and its alternating <connected components of a graph>.

Use <mathematical induction> on the <maximum degree> $\Delta$, including $\Delta=0$ as the base case. A <graph> of <maximum degree> zero has no <edges>, so zero colours suffice.

For $\Delta>0$, part (ii) supplies a <matching in a graph> $M$ meeting every <vertex> of <vertex degree> $\Delta$. Delete its <edges>, retaining all <vertices>. Each <vertex> formerly of <maximum degree> loses one incident <edge>; every other <vertex> already had <vertex degree> at most $\Delta-1$. Hence
$$
\Delta(G-M)\leq\Delta-1.
$$
The resulting <graph> is still a <bipartite graph>. By <mathematical induction>, it has a <proper edge colouring> using at most $\Delta-1$ colours. Give every <edge> of $M$ one new colour. Since $M$ is a <matching in a graph>, this produces a <proper edge colouring> of $G$ with at most $\Delta$ colours. The <edges> at a <vertex> of <maximum degree> require at least $\Delta$ colours, so
$$
\boxed{\chi'(G)=\Delta.}
$$
This is <Kőnig's line colouring theorem>.

For even $r\geq2$, take the <complete graph> $K_{r+1}$. It is an $r$-<regular graph> on an odd number of <vertices>. Every <matching in a graph> has at most $r/2$ <edges>, so $r$ colours in a <proper edge colouring> could cover at most $r^2/2$ <edges>. But
$$
|E(K_{r+1})|=\frac{r(r+1)}2>\frac{r^2}2.
$$
Therefore $\chi'(K_{r+1})>r$; <Vizing's theorem> gives **$\chi'(K_{r+1})=r+1$**. This includes the <graph triangle> when $r=2$.

For odd $r\geq3$, first construct a <graph> $H_r$ on $r+2$ <vertices>. Start with the <complete graph> on
$$
\{v,a,b,c_1,\ldots,c_{r-1}\},
$$
and delete the <edges> $va$, $vb$, and $c_1c_2,c_3c_4,\ldots,c_{r-2}c_{r-1}$. The pairing is possible because $r-1$ is even. In the initial <complete graph>, every <vertex> has <vertex degree> $r+1$. The distinguished <vertex> $v$ loses two <edges> and every other <vertex> loses exactly one, giving
$$
d_{H_r}(v)=r-1,\qquad d_{H_r}(x)=r\quad(x\ne v).
$$
Take two disjoint copies of $H_r$, with distinguished <vertices> $v,v'$, and add the <edge> $vv'$. The resulting <simple graph> $G_r$ is an $r$-<regular graph>. The added <edge> is the only <edge> between the two copies; each copy has the odd number $r+2$ of <vertices>.

Suppose an $r$-colour <proper edge colouring> existed. At each <vertex>, the $r$ incident <edges> would use all $r$ colours. Thus each colour would form a <perfect matching>. Any <perfect matching> must use $vv'$: without it, each odd-sized copy would have to be covered by internal pairs, which is impossible by <parity>. Therefore all $r$ colour classes would have to contain the same <edge> $vv'$, a contradiction. Hence <Vizing's theorem> again gives
$$
\boxed{\chi'(G_r)=r+1>r.}
$$
These constructions give <class two regular graphs> for every $r\geq2$. The odd-$r$ construction exhibits the <bridge obstruction to regular edge colouring>; $H_r$ is a <connected graph> because every <vertex> other than $a,b,v$ is adjacent to $v$, while $a,b$ are adjacent to those <vertices>.

For a finite <graph> $G$, its <chromatic polynomial> $p_G(x)$ is the <polynomial> whose value at each positive <integer> $q$ counts <proper vertex colourings> of $G$ from a fixed palette of $q$ labelled colours. Adjacent <vertices> must receive different colours, and unused colours are allowed.

For an <edge> $e=uv$, the <deletion-contraction recurrence for the chromatic polynomial> is
$$
\boxed{p_G(x)=p_{G-e}(x)-p_{G/e}(x).}
$$
Here $G-e$ deletes only the <edge> $e$, while $G/e$ performs <edge contraction>, identifying $u,v$ and removing $e$. Any parallel <edges> produced by <edge contraction> can be replaced by a single <edge>, since their constraints on <proper vertex colourings> are identical.

Indeed, the <proper vertex colourings> of $G-e$ in which $u,v$ differ are precisely those of $G$, while those in which $u,v$ agree correspond bijectively to <proper vertex colourings> of $G/e$. This proves the equality for every positive <integer> $q$, hence as a <polynomial identity>. Together with $p_G(x)=x^{|V(G)|}$ for a <graph> without <edges>, the recurrence also proves by <mathematical induction> that the counting function is a <polynomial>.

Let the nonempty <connected components of a graph> of $G$ be $G_1,\ldots,G_t$. A <proper vertex colouring> can be chosen separately on each <connected component of a graph>, since no <edge> joins different <connected components of a graph>. The <multiplication principle> gives, first at positive <integers> and then as a <polynomial identity>,
$$
p_G(x)=\prod_{j=1}^t p_{G_j}(x).
$$
It remains to justify that every factor has zero constant term. More generally, for every nonempty <simple graph> $H$, prove $p_H(0)=0$ by <mathematical induction> on the number of <edges>. If there are no <edges>, then $p_H(x)=x^{|V(H)|}$ with $|V(H)|\geq1$. Otherwise choose an <edge> $e$. Both $H-e$ and the simplified <edge contraction> $H/e$ are nonempty <graphs> with fewer <edges>. The <deletion-contraction recurrence for the chromatic polynomial> and <mathematical induction> give
$$
p_H(0)=p_{H-e}(0)-p_{H/e}(0)=0.
$$
Thus $p_{G_j}(x)=xq_j(x)$ for a <polynomial> $q_j$, and
$$
\boxed{p_G(x)=x^t\prod_{j=1}^t q_j(x),\qquad x^t\mid p_G(x).}
$$
For the empty <graph>, $t=0$ and $p_G(x)=1$, so the conclusion also holds. This is the <chromatic polynomial product over components>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Write $V=V(G)$ and $E=E(G)$. For $F\subseteq E$, let $c(F)$ denote the number of <connected components of a graph> of the spanning <subgraph> $(V,F)$, including isolated <vertices>. The <inclusion-exclusion principle>, applied to the unwanted events that the two endpoints of an <edge> receive the same colour, gives the <chromatic polynomial inclusion-exclusion formula>
$$
p_G(x)=\sum_{F\subseteq E}(-1)^{|F|}x^{c(F)}.
$$
To justify the exponent, imposing equal colours across all <edges> in $F$ forces a single colour on each <connected component of a graph> of $(V,F)$, with independent choices between its <connected components of a graph>. Thus the corresponding count at $q$ colours is $q^{c(F)}$. Equality for all positive <integers> proves the <polynomial identity>.

Fix an index $i$ in the stated range. A <subset> $F$ contributes to the coefficient of $x^{n-i}$ exactly when $n-c(F)=i$. If $(V,F)$ contains a <graph cycle> of length $s$, the hypothesis gives $s\geq\ell$. All its $s$ <vertices> lie in one <connected component of a graph>. For any <connected component of a graph> $D$, its contribution to $n-c(F)$ is $|V(D)|-1$, so
$$
n-c(F)=\sum_D(|V(D)|-1)\geq s-1\geq\ell-1.
$$
This contradicts $i\leq\ell-2$. Therefore every contributing $(V,F)$ is a <forest>. The <forest> identity $|F|=n-c(F)$ then gives $|F|=i$.

Conversely, every $i$-<edge> <subset> is a <forest>, since a <graph cycle> in it would have at most $i<\ell$ <edges>. It therefore has $c(F)=n-i$ and contributes exactly $(-1)^i$. There are $\binom mi$ such <subsets>, by the definition of a <binomial coefficient>. Consequently
$$
[x^{n-i}]p_G(x)=(-1)^i\binom mi,\qquad
\boxed{a_i=\binom mi\quad(0\leq i\leq\ell-2).}
$$
Here the coefficient notation is understood within the defined indices $0\leq i\leq n$; if the displayed range is extended past $n$, the coefficients beyond the constant term can be set to zero. For a <forest>, $m\leq n-1$, so the <binomial coefficients> there are also zero. The key reason <girth determines initial chromatic coefficients> is that a <graph cycle> can affect only exponents whose loss of <connected components of a graph> is at least $\ell-1$.

First reduce the <unicyclic graph> to its unique <graph cycle> $C$. Delete the <edges> of $C$ but retain every <vertex>. The remaining <graph> is a <forest>, since any surviving <graph cycle> would be a second one in $G$. Each of its <connected components of a graph> contains a <vertex> of $C$: follow a <graph path> to $C$ in the original <connected graph>, stopping at the first <vertex> of $C$; this initial segment uses no <edge> of $C$.

No such <connected component of a graph> contains two <vertices> of $C$. Otherwise, on the <tree> <graph path> between them, choose two successive visits to $C$. The segment between these visits has no internal <vertex> on $C$ and uses no <edge> of $C$. Combining it with either connecting arc of $C$ gives a second <graph cycle>, a contradiction. Thus the remaining <graph> consists of <trees>, each rooted at exactly one <vertex> of $C$.

After choosing a <proper vertex colouring> of $C$ from $q$ colours, colour these <rooted trees> away from their roots. Each of the $n-\ell$ off-<graph cycle> <vertices> has one already coloured <parent in a rooted tree>, leaving exactly $q-1$ choices. The <multiplication principle> therefore gives
$$
p_G(x)=(x-1)^{n-\ell}p_{C_\ell}(x).
$$
Equivalently, this repeatedly applies the <chromatic polynomial after attaching a leaf> formula.

To compute the <chromatic polynomial of a cycle>, the base case is the <graph triangle>:
$$
p_{C_3}(x)=x(x-1)(x-2)=(x-1)^3-(x-1).
$$
For $s\geq4$, deleting a <cycle graph> <edge> gives a <path graph> on $s$ <vertices>, while <edge contraction> gives $C_{s-1}$. The <chromatic polynomial> of that <path graph> is $x(x-1)^{s-1}$, by choosing the colour of its first <vertex> and then each successive <vertex>. Hence the <deletion-contraction recurrence for the chromatic polynomial> gives
$$
p_{C_s}(x)=x(x-1)^{s-1}-p_{C_{s-1}}(x).
$$
Using <mathematical induction>, substitute $p_{C_{s-1}}(x)=(x-1)^{s-1}+(-1)^{s-1}(x-1)$ to obtain
$$
p_{C_s}(x)=(x-1)^s+(-1)^s(x-1).
$$
Multiplying by the factor for the attached <trees> proves the <chromatic polynomial of a unicyclic graph> formula
$$
\boxed{p_G(x)=(x-1)^n+(-1)^\ell(x-1)^{n-\ell+1}.}
$$

A <graph> is a <k-connected graph> if it has at least $k+1$ <vertices> and deleting any <set> of fewer than $k$ <vertices>, together with their incident <edges>, leaves a <connected graph>.

The <vertex connectivity> $\kappa(G)$ is the largest such $k$. Equivalently, for a <graph> with at least two <vertices>, it is the smallest number of <vertices> whose deletion either disconnects the <graph> or leaves a single <vertex>. The latter convention gives $\kappa(K_n)=n-1$ for a <complete graph>. A disconnected <graph> has <vertex connectivity> zero; one usually also sets $\kappa(K_1)=0$.

For distinct nonadjacent <vertices> $x,y$, the <local connectivity> is
$$
\boxed{\kappa_G(x,y)=\min\{|S|:S\subseteq V(G)\setminus\{x,y\},\ \text{there is no }x\text{--}y\text{ path in }G-S\}.}
$$
Such a <set> $S$ is an $x$–$y$ <vertex separator>. By <Menger's theorem>, <local connectivity> also equals the maximum number of <internally vertex-disjoint paths> from $x$ to $y$. If no <graph path> exists already, the empty <vertex separator> gives zero.

= Solution

The <vertex separator> version of <Menger's theorem> says that, for distinct nonadjacent <vertices> $x,y$ of a finite <graph>, the maximum number of <internally vertex-disjoint paths> from $x$ to $y$ equals the minimum size of an $x$–$y$ <vertex separator>.

Use the following version of the <max-flow min-cut theorem>: in a finite directed <flow network> with nonnegative <integer> <flow network edge capacities>, the maximum <flow value> equals the minimum <cut capacity>, and a <maximum flow> can be chosen to have <integer> values on every directed <edge>. The last clause is the <integral max-flow theorem>.

Here is the reduction, including the correspondence with <vertex separators>. Put $N=|V(G)|$. Perform <vertex splitting> on each <vertex> $v\ne x,y$, replacing it by $v_{\rm in},v_{\rm out}$ with a directed <edge> $v_{\rm in}\to v_{\rm out}$ of <flow network edge capacity> one. Keep $x$ as the source and $y$ as the sink. For each original undirected <edge> $uv$, put directed <edges> from the output of $u$ to the input of $v$ and from the output of $v$ to the input of $u$, each of <flow network edge capacity> $N$; an unsplit endpoint uses $x$ or $y$ itself.

A family of $p$ <internally vertex-disjoint paths> gives a feasible integral <flow> of value $p$, by sending one unit down each <graph path>. Conversely, decompose an integral <flow> into unit source-to-sink <graph paths> and <directed cycles>, discarding the <directed cycles>. This decomposition can be obtained by following positive-<flow> directed <edges> and subtracting one unit along a source-to-sink <graph path> or a <directed cycle>, repeating until the <flow value> is exhausted. A capacity-one split <edge> can belong to at most one unit <graph path>. The projected <graph paths> in $G$ are therefore <internally vertex-disjoint paths>. Thus the largest number of such <graph paths> equals the maximum <flow value>.

Deleting a <vertex separator> $S$ corresponds to removing its split <edges>, of total <flow network edge capacity> $|S|$. This destroys every source-to-sink <graph path>; taking the source-reachable <vertices> after removal gives a <cut of a flow network> of <cut capacity> at most $|S|$. Thus the minimum <cut capacity> is no greater than the minimum <vertex separator> size.

For the reverse inequality, deleting all $N-2$ internal <vertices> separates $x,y$, because they are nonadjacent. Hence a <minimum cut> has <cut capacity> at most $N-2<N$ and cannot contain a directed <edge> of <flow network edge capacity> $N$. Its outgoing <edges> are therefore split <edges>. Let $S$ be the original <vertices> associated with them. Any $x$–$y$ <graph path> avoiding $S$ would lift to a source-to-sink <graph path> crossing this <cut of a flow network> without using an outgoing <edge>, an impossibility. Hence $S$ is a <vertex separator>, with size exactly the <cut capacity>. The two minima agree, and the <max-flow min-cut theorem> proves <Menger's theorem>.

Add two new <vertices> $s,t$ to $G$, joining $s$ to every <vertex> of $U$ and $t$ to every <vertex> of $W$, with no other new <edges>. The <vertices> $s,t$ are nonadjacent. Suppose $S\subseteq V(G)$ has $|S|<k$. Since $G$ is a <k-connected graph>, $G-S$ is a <connected graph>. Both $U\setminus S$ and $W\setminus S$ are nonempty because each original <set> has <cardinality> $k$. Choose $u\in U\setminus S$ and $w\in W\setminus S$; a <graph path> between them in $G-S$ extends to an $s$–$t$ <graph path> avoiding $S$.

Consequently no fewer than $k$ <vertices> can form an $s$–$t$ <vertex separator>. By <Menger's theorem>, there are at least $k$ <internally vertex-disjoint paths> from $s$ to $t$. There cannot be more than $k$, since $s$ has only $k$ <neighbours>.

Remove $s,t$ from these $k$ <graph paths>. The resulting <graph paths> in $G$ start at distinct <vertices> of $U$, end at distinct <vertices> of $W$, and are pairwise <vertex-disjoint paths>: any shared <vertex> would have been an internal <vertex> common to two of the original <graph paths>. Because there are exactly $k$ <graph paths>, they use every <vertex> of $U$ and $W$ as an endpoint. None can pass through another terminal internally, since that terminal already occurs as an endpoint of one of the <vertex-disjoint paths>. Thus there are $k$ <vertex-disjoint paths> from $U$ to $W$, with some pairing of the endpoints. This is also an immediate application of the <set version of Menger theorem>.

**No.** A counterexample works for every $k\geq2$. Take the <complete bipartite graph> $G=K_{k,k}$ with <vertex sets>
$$
A=\{a_1,\ldots,a_k\},\qquad B=\{b_1,\ldots,b_k\}.
$$
It is a <k-connected graph>: deleting fewer than $k$ <vertices> leaves at least one <vertex> in each side, and the remaining <complete bipartite graph> is a <connected graph>.

Choose
$$
U=\{b_1,a_1,\ldots,a_{k-1}\},\qquad
W=\{b_2,\ldots,b_k,a_k\},
$$
and order these <sets> so that $u_1=b_1$ and $w_1=b_2$. Order the other members arbitrarily. These are disjoint <sets> of <cardinality> $k$, and every <vertex> of $G$ is one of the specified endpoints.

If the prescribed $k$ <vertex-disjoint paths> existed, no one of them could have an internal <vertex>, because every <vertex> must serve as the endpoint of one of the <graph paths>. Thus each <graph path> would have to be a single <edge>. But $b_1,b_2$ lie in the same side of the <bipartite graph>, so there is no <edge> $b_1b_2$. This contradicts the requirement on $P_1$.

For $k=2$ this is the four-<vertex> <cycle graph>, with opposite <vertices> paired. The stronger property that every prescribed pairing can be realized is the <linked graph> property; <vertex connectivity> alone does not guarantee it.

We verify the <Hall condition> for $U$. Let $S\subseteq U$. If $S$ is empty, then $|N(S)|=0=|S|$. Suppose $0<|S|\leq n/2$. Choose $u\in S$. Its <graph neighbourhood> is contained in $N(S)$, so the <minimum degree> hypothesis gives
$$
|N(S)|\geq d_G(u)\geq n/2\geq|S|.
$$
If $|S|>n/2$, suppose for a contradiction that some <vertex> $w\in W\setminus N(S)$ exists. All <neighbours> of $w$ lie in $U\setminus S$, so
$$
d_G(w)\leq n-|S|<n/2,
$$
contradicting the <minimum degree> hypothesis. Thus $N(S)=W$ and $|N(S)|=n\geq|S|$.

Every <subset> $S\subseteq U$ satisfies the <Hall condition>. By <Hall's marriage theorem>, there is a <matching in a graph> saturating $U$. Since $|U|=|W|=n$, its $n$ <edges> also cover every <vertex> of $W$. It is therefore a **complete matching**, meaning a <perfect matching>. For $n=0$, the empty <matching in a graph> already has this property.
