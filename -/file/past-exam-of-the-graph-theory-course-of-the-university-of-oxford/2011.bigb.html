== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Ramsey number> $R(s,t)$ is the least <integer> $N$ such that every <graph> on $N$ <vertices> contains either a <clique> of size $s$ or an <independent set> of size $t$. Equivalently, every red-blue <colouring> of the <edges> of $K_N$ contains a red $K_s$ or a blue $K_t$.

Put $a=R(s-1,t)$, $b=R(s,t-1)$ and consider a <graph> on $a+b$ <vertices>. Fix a <vertex> $v$. If it has at least $a$ <neighbours>, their induced <graph> contains either a <clique> of size $s-1$, which together with $v$ gives a <clique> of size $s$, or an <independent set> of size $t$. Otherwise $v$ has at least $b$ nonneighbours. Their induced <graph> contains either a <clique> of size $s$, or an <independent set> of size $t-1$, which can be enlarged by $v$. Thus
$$
\boxed{R(s,t)\leq R(s-1,t)+R(s,t-1).}
$$
Since $R(2,3)=R(3,2)=3$, this gives $R(3,3)\leq6$. For the reverse bound, the <cycle graph>[five-cycle] has no <triangle in a graph>[triangle] and no <independent set> of size three: its <complement> is again a <cycle graph>[five-cycle], so it too is triangle-free. Hence the property fails on five <vertices> and
$$
\boxed{R(3,3)=6.}
$$

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <vertex> version of <Menger's theorem> states that for two distinct nonadjacent <vertices> $s,t$ of a finite <graph>, the <maximum> number of internally vertex-disjoint $s$-$t$ <graph paths> equals the <minimum> size of a <set> $S\subseteq V\setminus\{s,t\}$ whose deletion separates $s$ from $t$.
$$
\boxed{\max\{\text{internally disjoint }s\text{-}t\text{ paths}\}=\min\{|S|:s,t\text{ disconnected in }G-S\}.}
$$
Internally disjoint <graph paths> may share their endpoints but no other <vertex>. The nonadjacency hypothesis is needed in this separator formulation, since an <edge> $st$ cannot be destroyed by deleting other <vertices>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let the <bipartition> be $L\cup R$. Form an auxiliary <graph> by adding a new <vertex> $s$ adjacent to every <vertex> of $L$ and a new <vertex> $t$ adjacent to every <vertex> of $R$. The <vertices> $s,t$ are nonadjacent, so <Menger's theorem> applies.

A <matching> of size $m$ gives $m$ internally disjoint <graph paths> $s\ell rt$, one for each <matching> <edge> $\ell r$. Conversely, every $s$-$t$ <graph path> must use an <edge> of the original <bipartite graph>. Choose one such <edge> from each member of an internally disjoint family. Their endpoints are all distinct, so the chosen <edges> form a <matching>. Thus the <maximum> number of those <graph paths> is the <maximum matching> size.

An $s$-$t$ separator contained in $L\cup R$ is exactly a <vertex cover> of the original <graph>. Indeed, an uncovered <edge> $\ell r$ would leave the <graph path> $s\ell rt$, while removing a <vertex cover> removes every <edge> between $L$ and $R$ and hence every $s$-$t$ <graph path>. The <minimum> separator size is therefore the <minimum> <vertex cover> size. Menger gives
$$
\boxed{\nu(G)=\tau(G)\quad\text{for bipartite }G.}
$$
This is <Kőnig's theorem for bipartite matching>. The auxiliary construction gives the required deduction from <vertex-disjoint paths> rather than assuming a <matching> theorem in advance.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

<Hall's marriage theorem> says that a <bipartite graph> with parts $L,R$ has a <matching> saturating $L$ if and only if
$$
\boxed{|N(S)|\geq|S|\qquad\text{for every }S\subseteq L.}
$$
Necessity follows because the matched <neighbours> of <vertices> of $S$ are distinct and belong to $N(S)$.

For sufficiency, assume these <inequalities>. Let $K=U\cup W$ be any <vertex cover>, with $U\subseteq L$ and $W\subseteq R$. Every <neighbour> of $S=L\setminus U$ must belong to $W$, since otherwise its <edge> from $S$ is uncovered. Therefore $|W|\geq|N(S)|\geq|S|$, and $|K|\geq|U|+|S|=|L|$. The whole part $L$ is itself a <vertex cover> of size $|L|$. Part (ii) now gives a <matching> of size exactly $|L|$, so it saturates $L$.

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Choose a <spanning tree> of the <connectivity (graph theory)>[connected] <graph> and <root> it at $x$. List <vertices> in nonincreasing order of their <distance> from $x$ in this <tree>, breaking ties arbitrarily. The <root> has <distance> zero and is last, so $v_n=x$.

Every other <vertex> has a parent one step closer to the <root>. That parent is adjacent to it and appears later in the ordering. Hence
$$
\boxed{\forall i<n\ \exists j>i:\ v_iv_j\in E(H).}
$$
For a <graph> of one <vertex> the condition is vacuous and the same ordering works.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $\Delta=\Delta(G)$, which is at least two because $x$ has the two specified <neighbours>. Give $y$ and $z$ the same colour, say colour one; this is proper because they are nonadjacent.

Apply part (i) to the <connectivity (graph theory)>[connected] <graph> $G\setminus\{y,z\}$, with $x$ last. Colour its <vertices> greedily in the resulting order using a palette of $\Delta$ colours. Every <vertex> before $x$ has a <neighbour> later in this order, so at most $\deg(v)-1\leq\Delta-1$ of its <neighbours> have already received colours. At least one palette colour is available.

When $x$ is reached, all its <neighbours> are coloured, but the two <neighbours> $y,z$ have the same colour. Thus there are at most $\deg(x)-1\leq\Delta-1$ distinct forbidden colours. Colour $x$ with a remaining one. This constructs a proper <colouring> with
$$
\boxed{\chi(G)\leq\Delta(G).}
$$
The common colour on the two initial <vertices> replaces the usual need for an uncoloured <neighbour> at the last greedy step.

This is <greedy colouring with two precoloured neighbours>.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Take a proper <colouring> $c:V(G)\to\{1,\ldots,k\}$ and orient every <edge> from its lower-coloured endpoint towards its higher-coloured endpoint. Along a <directed path> the colour strictly increases, so such a <graph path> has at most $k$ <vertices>. A <directed cycle> is impossible for the same reason. Thus
$$
\boxed{D\text{ is acyclic and }\lambda(D)\leq k.}
$$

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For each <vertex> $v$, let $c(v)$ be the <maximum> number of <vertices> in a <directed path> ending at $v$. These numbers lie between one and $\ell$.

If $u\to v$ is an <edge>, a longest <graph path> ending at $u$ cannot already contain $v$, since that would give a <directed cycle> on appending $u\to v$. Appending it therefore creates a <graph path> ending at $v$, so $c(v)\geq c(u)+1$. Adjacent <vertices> consequently receive different colours. Hence
$$
\boxed{c\text{ is a proper colouring using at most }\ell\text{ colours}.}
$$
Acyclicity is exactly what justifies extending the <graph path> without repeating a <vertex>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Part (ii) shows that $\chi(G)\leq\lambda(D)$ for every <acyclic orientation> $D$, so $\chi(G)$ is at most their <minimum>. Part (i), applied to an optimal <colouring>, supplies an <acyclic orientation> with $\lambda(D)\leq\chi(G)$. Combining the <inequalities> gives
$$
\boxed{\chi(G)=\min_{D\text{ acyclic orientation of }G}\lambda(D).}
$$
The parameter counts <vertices> on a <graph path>; if <graph path> length were instead counted in <edges>, the corresponding <minimum> would be $\chi(G)-1$.

These constructions prove <chromatic number from acyclic longest paths>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Fix a proper <colouring> with exactly $k$ nonempty <colour classes>. For any class $C_i$, suppose every <vertex> in it misses at least one of the other $k-1$ colours in its <neighbourhood>. Recolour each of these <vertices> by one of its missing colours. No <edge> joins two <vertices> of $C_i$, so these simultaneous recolourings cannot conflict with one another, and the chosen colours do not conflict with <vertices> outside $C_i$.

This would eliminate colour $i$ and produce a $(k-1)$-colouring, contradicting optimality. Therefore each <colour class> contains a <vertex> adjacent to at least one <vertex> of every other colour. That <vertex> has degree at least $k-1$. Choosing one from each class gives $k$ distinct <vertices>, proving
$$
\boxed{\bigl|\{v:\deg(v)\geq k-1\}\bigr|\geq k.}
$$
For $k=1$, a nonempty <graph> already has a <vertex> of degree at least zero, so the conclusion holds as well.

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For disjoint nonempty <sets> $X,Y$, let $e(X,Y)$ count <edges> between them. Their <edge> density is
$$
\boxed{d(X,Y)=\frac{e(X,Y)}{|X||Y|}.}
$$
The pair is $\varepsilon$-regular if every $A\subseteq X$ and $B\subseteq Y$ with $|A|\geq\varepsilon|X|$, $|B|\geq\varepsilon|Y|$ satisfies $|d(A,B)-d(X,Y)|\leq\varepsilon$.

An $\varepsilon$-regular partition has disjoint classes $V_0,V_1,\ldots,V_k$ covering $V(G)$, with $|V_0|\leq\varepsilon|V(G)|$, equal positive sizes $|V_1|=\cdots=|V_k|$, and at most $\varepsilon k^2$ unordered pairs $(V_i,V_j)$ with $1\leq i<j\leq k$ that are not $\varepsilon$-regular. The <set> $V_0$ is exceptional and its pairs need not satisfy regularity.

<Szemerédi regularity lemma> states that for every $\varepsilon>0$ and <integer> $m_0\geq1$, there are <integers> $M,n_0$ such that every <graph> with $n\geq n_0$ <vertices> has such a partition with
$$
\boxed{m_0\leq k\leq M.}
$$
The bounds depend on $\varepsilon,m_0$, not on the <graph> order. Using a strict regularity <inequality> or a slightly different <constant> in the allowed pair count gives an equivalent conventional version; the displayed convention will be used below.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $B\subseteq X$ be the <vertices> with fewer than $(d/2)|Y|$ <neighbours> in $Y$. If $|B|\geq\varepsilon|X|$, regularity applies to the pair $(B,Y)$ and gives
$$
d(B,Y)\geq d(X,Y)-\varepsilon\geq d-\varepsilon\geq d/2.
$$
But averaging the defining strict degree <inequality> over $B$ gives $d(B,Y)<d/2$, a contradiction. Therefore
$$
\boxed{|B|<\varepsilon|X|.}
$$
Regularity controls the <neighbourhood> of almost every <vertex> because a large <set> of exceptional low-degree <vertices> would itself witness an irregular density.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Apply part (b) to $(X_1,X_2)$ and $(X_1,X_3)$. Fewer than $2\varepsilon|X_1|$ <vertices> fail at least one of the degree bounds. For any remaining <vertex> $v$, put $N_2=N(v)\cap X_2$ and $N_3=N(v)\cap X_3$. Then
$$
|N_2|\geq\frac d2|X_2|\geq\varepsilon|X_2|,\qquad
|N_3|\geq\frac d2|X_3|\geq\varepsilon|X_3|.
$$
Regularity of $(X_2,X_3)$ gives $d(N_2,N_3)\geq d-\varepsilon\geq d/2$. Every <edge> between $N_2$ and $N_3$ makes a distinct <triangle in a graph>[triangle] containing $v$. Hence $v$ belongs to at least
$$
\frac d2|N_2||N_3|\geq\frac{d^3}8|X_2||X_3|
$$
such <triangle in a graph>[triangles]. This proves the asserted bound on exceptional <vertices>.

There are at least $(1-2\varepsilon)|X_1|\geq|X_1|/2$ good <vertices>. Summing their <triangle in a graph>[triangle] counts counts each <triangle in a graph>[triangle] with one <vertex> in each cluster exactly once, at its $X_1$ <vertex>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\#\{\text{triangles in }G\}\geq\frac{d^3}{16}|X_1||X_2||X_3|.}
$$
There is no factor of three to divide out in this cross-cluster count.

This is the <regular triangle counting lemma> with explicit coarse constants.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Choose a small density threshold $d>0$, then $\varepsilon\leq\min(d/2,1/4)$ sufficiently small and a lower partition bound $m_0$ sufficiently large that
$$
2\varepsilon+\frac1{2m_0}+\frac d2<\eta.
$$
Apply the regularity lemma. Delete all <edges> incident with $V_0$, all <edges> within a class $V_i$, all <edges> between <irregular pairs>, and all <edges> between <regular pairs> of density below $d$. Their total is at most
$$
\varepsilon n^2+\frac{n^2}{2k}+\varepsilon n^2+\frac d2 n^2<\eta n^2.
$$
If a <triangle in a graph>[triangle] remains, its <vertices> lie in three distinct nonexceptional classes, all whose pairs were regular with original density at least $d$. Part (c) then forces at least $(d^3/16)t^3$ <triangle in a graph>[triangles] in the original <graph>, where $t$ is the common class size. Since $k\leq M$ and $\varepsilon\leq1/4$, we have $t\geq n/(2M)$, so this is at least $d^3n^3/(128M^3)$.

Choose $c=d^3/(256M^3)$ and take $n_0$ large enough for the regularity lemma. An original <graph> with at most $cn^3$ <triangle in a graph>[triangles] cannot leave any <triangle in a graph>[triangle] after these deletions. Thus **deleting at most $\eta n^2$ <edges> makes the <graph> triangle-free**. This is the <triangle removal lemma>; boundedly many large regular clusters turn any surviving <triangle in a graph>[triangle] into a positive-density family of original <triangle in a graph>[triangles].
