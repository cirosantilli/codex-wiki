Take $\widehat V\psi=V(x)\psi$ with real $V$, and $\widehat T=-(\hbar^2/2m)d^2/dx^2$. The <operator domain> matters: use sufficiently smooth <wavefunctions> for which the following boundary terms vanish at both infinities. A real multiplication operator gives
$$
\langle f,\widehat Vg\rangle=\int f^*Vg\,dx=\int(Vf)^*g\,dx=\langle\widehat Vf,g\rangle.
$$
Two applications of <integration by parts> give
$$
\langle f,\widehat Tg\rangle-\langle\widehat Tf,g\rangle
=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}[f^*g'-f'^*g]_{-\infty}^{\infty}=0.
$$
Thus the <potential energy> and <kinetic energy> operators obey the stated <Hermitian operator> identity on this common domain. For $D=d/dx$, one application of <integration by parts> instead gives
$$
\langle f,Dg\rangle=[f^*g]_{-\infty}^{\infty}-\langle Df,g\rangle=-\langle Df,g\rangle.
$$
The derivative is <anti-Hermitian>, rather than <Hermitian>; the minus sign cannot generally be removed. Multiplication by $-i\hbar$ makes the corresponding <momentum operator> Hermitian. These integrations establish the requested symmetry identities; full <self-adjointness> of an unbounded operator additionally requires equality of its domain with its adjoint's domain.

Let $\widehat Q\psi_n=q_n\psi_n$ with $\psi_n\ne0$. The <Hermitian operator> identity and conjugate linearity of the first slot of the <inner product> imply
$$
q_n\langle\psi_n,\psi_n\rangle
=\langle\psi_n,\widehat Q\psi_n\rangle
=\langle\widehat Q\psi_n,\psi_n\rangle
=q_n^*\langle\psi_n,\psi_n\rangle.
$$
The squared <norm> is positive, so $q_n=q_n^*$: every such <eigenvalue> is real.

For two <eigenfunctions>, apply the same <Hermitian operator> identity across the two states:
$$
q_n\langle\psi_m,\psi_n\rangle
=\langle\psi_m,\widehat Q\psi_n\rangle
=\langle\widehat Q\psi_m,\psi_n\rangle
=q_m\langle\psi_m,\psi_n\rangle.
$$
Hence $(q_n-q_m)\langle\psi_m,\psi_n\rangle=0$. Distinct <eigenvalues> therefore imply <orthogonal> <eigenfunctions>. Within a degenerate eigenspace, <orthogonality> is a possible basis choice, rather than a consequence for every pair of vectors.

Multiply the <eigenfunction expansion> by $\psi_m^*$ and integrate over the common domain. <Orthogonality> and <wavefunction normalization> give
$$
\int\psi_m^*(x)f(x)\,dx
=\sum_n c_n\int\psi_m^*(x)\psi_n(x)\,dx
=\sum_n c_n\delta_{mn}=c_m.
$$
Thus $\boxed{c_m=\langle\psi_m,f\rangle}$. For a normalized $f$, the <Born rule> identifies $|c_m|^2$ as the probability of this basis outcome.

The decisive observation is that $x$ is itself an <eigenfunction>:
$$
\widehat Qx=\frac d{dx}\left[(x^2-1)\frac{dx}{dx}\right]=2x.
$$
On the regular <Legendre polynomial> domain, integration by parts has endpoint factor $x^2-1$, so the boundary form vanishes for regular functions at $x=\pm1$. The interval alone is not an endpoint condition; regularity is the usual condition selecting the <Legendre polynomials>. Their <eigenvalues> are $n(n+1)$, and the $n=1$ normalized function is
$$
\psi_1(x)=\sqrt{\frac32}\,x,\qquad \int_{-1}^{1}|\psi_1|^2dx=1.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{x=\sqrt{\frac23}\,\psi_1(x),\qquad c_1=\sqrt{\frac23},\quad c_n=0\ (n\ne1).}
$$
Equivalently, the <Hermitian operator> identity gives $(q_n-2)c_n=0$, and nondegeneracy leaves only one possible nonzero coefficient. The unnormalized function $x$ has squared <norm> $2/3$, consistent with this coefficient.

The <quantum variational principle> states that the energy expectation of an admissible trial <wavefunction> is at least the <ground state> energy:
$$
\boxed{\frac{\langle f,\widehat Hf\rangle}{\langle f,f\rangle}\ge E_0.}
$$
For the discrete, complete <orthonormal basis> assumed here, expand in <Hamiltonian operator> eigenfunctions, $f=\sum_n c_n\phi_n$, with $\widehat H\phi_n=E_n\phi_n$ and $E_n\ge E_0$. Then
$$
\frac{\langle f,\widehat Hf\rangle}{\langle f,f\rangle}-E_0
=\frac{\sum_n|c_n|^2(E_n-E_0)}{\sum_n|c_n|^2}\ge0.
$$
Every summand is nonnegative. Equality holds precisely when all occupied components lie in the ground eigenspace. Thus minimizing trial energies supplies upper bounds on $E_0$, with exact equality if the trial family contains a <ground state>.

The <Pauli principle> requires the complete <wavefunction> of identical <fermions> to be antisymmetric under exchanging the full coordinates, including <spin>:
$$
\boxed{\Psi(x_2,x_1)=-\Psi(x_1,x_2),\qquad x_i=(\mathbf r_i,s_i).}
$$
It is the total spatial-and-spin state that changes sign; the spatial factor alone need not be antisymmetric.

= Solution

Let $a=\phi_{1s}$ and $b=\phi_{2s}$ be orthonormal <atomic orbitals>. The normalized spatial factors are
$$
\Phi_\pm(1,2)=\frac{a(1)b(2)\pm b(1)a(2)}{\sqrt2}.
$$
For the <singlet state>, use the antisymmetric spin factor
$$
\chi_{00}=\frac{\alpha(1)\beta(2)-\beta(1)\alpha(2)}{\sqrt2},\qquad \Psi_S=\Phi_+\chi_{00}.
$$
For the three components of the <triplet state>, use
$$
\chi_{11}=\alpha(1)\alpha(2),\quad
\chi_{10}=\frac{\alpha(1)\beta(2)+\beta(1)\alpha(2)}{\sqrt2},\quad
\chi_{1,-1}=\beta(1)\beta(2),\qquad \Psi_{T,M}=\Phi_-\chi_{1M}.
$$
Each total <wavefunction> obeys the <Pauli principle>. At coincident spatial coordinates,
$$
\Phi_-(\mathbf r,\mathbf r)=\frac{a(\mathbf r)b(\mathbf r)-b(\mathbf r)a(\mathbf r)}{\sqrt2}=0.
$$
Consequently the triplet pair <probability density> vanishes at coincidence, even for its $M=0$ component. This zero is stronger than the trivial statement that any exact point has zero integrated probability in a continuum.

= Solution

In <atomic units>, define the <Coulomb integral> and <exchange integral> by
$$
J=\iint\frac{|a(1)|^2|b(2)|^2}{r_{12}}\,d^3r_1d^3r_2,\qquad
K=\iint\frac{a^*(1)b(1)b^*(2)a(2)}{r_{12}}\,d^3r_1d^3r_2.
$$
Expanding the four products in the expectation of $r_{12}^{-1}$ yields
$$
\langle\Phi_\pm|r_{12}^{-1}|\Phi_\pm\rangle=\tfrac12(J+J\pm K\pm K)=J\pm K.
$$
The one-electron terms have the same value for these two states. Within this common-orbital approximation,
$$
E_S=\epsilon_{1s}+\epsilon_{2s}+J+K,\qquad
E_T=\epsilon_{1s}+\epsilon_{2s}+J-K,
$$
so $\boxed{E_S-E_T=2K}$. The <exchange integral> is nonnegative: it is the positive Coulomb quadratic form of the transition density $a^*b$, or equivalently has a nonnegative Fourier representation. The triplet is lower. Actual orbital relaxation and correlation make the common-orbital $2K$ an approximate physical splitting.

Keeping the same two spin states but incorrectly imposing <boson> exchange symmetry would require a symmetric total <wavefunction>. The spin-antisymmetric <singlet state> would then accompany $\Phi_-$, while the spin-symmetric <triplet state> would accompany $\Phi_+$. Thus
$$
E_S^{\rm hypothetical}=E_{\rm one}+J-K,\qquad
E_T^{\rm hypothetical}=E_{\rm one}+J+K,
$$
and $\boxed{E_T^{\rm hypothetical}-E_S^{\rm hypothetical}=2K}$. The ordering reverses: the triplet would be destabilized by $2K$ relative to the singlet. A genuinely spinless <boson> would have no singlet/triplet spin distinction; this comparison changes exchange symmetry while retaining the question's spin degrees of freedom.

If two <fermions> occupied the same complete <spin orbital> $u$, their antisymmetrized product would be
$$
\frac{u(1)u(2)-u(2)u(1)}{\sqrt2}=0.
$$
The zero vector cannot represent a normalized <quantum state>. This is the <Pauli exclusion principle>: at most one identical <fermion> occupies any complete one-particle state. Two <electrons> may occupy the same <spatial orbital> with opposite spins, because $a\alpha$ and $a\beta$ are different <spin orbitals>.

The $3d$ and $4s$ <atomic orbitals> of <chromium> are close enough in energy that electron-electron terms can overturn the simple filling order. The configuration $[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^54s^1$ permits five singly occupied $3d$ orbitals and a parallel-spin $4s$ electron. Relative to $3d^44s^2$, it removes the $4s$ pair and permits more parallel-spin exchange interactions, lowering the Coulomb-and-exchange contribution enough to offset the orbital promotion cost.

As qualitative bookkeeping, six parallel <electron spins> give $\binom62=15$ same-spin pairs, compared with $\binom52=10$ in high-spin $3d^44s^2$. The individual <exchange integrals> are unequal, so this is an explanation of the tendency, rather than a quantitative atomic energy calculation. The observed <ground state> configuration is $\boxed{[\mathrm{Ar}]\,3d^54s^1}$.<Energy levels of neutral chromium in the NIST Handbook>[Cr I ground level, 3d5(6S)4s, a7S, J=3, energy 0 cm−1]{ref}

The closed argon core contributes $L=S=0$. In the half-filled $3d^5$ shell, <Hund's rules> put one parallel-spin electron in each $m_\ell=-2,-1,0,1,2$ orbital. This gives $S_d=5/2$ and the orbital term $L_d=0$. Adding the parallel $4s$ electron, with $\ell=0$ and $s=1/2$, gives

For one <normal mode>, the fundamental is $v=0\to1$, with other modes initially in their ground levels. In the linear-dipole <harmonic approximation>, the transition moment is nonzero for $\Delta v=\pm1$, so a <vibrational fundamental> is normally much stronger than its overtones, provided the mode has <vibrational infrared activity>.

The first <vibrational overtone> is $v=0\to2$, with $\Delta v=2$. It lies near twice the fundamental wavenumber, generally slightly below it because of <anharmonicity>. The linear-dipole harmonic model forbids it; anharmonic wavefunctions or higher terms in the dipole expansion give a weak intensity. Thus it is generally weaker than the corresponding fundamental.

The second <vibrational overtone> is $v=0\to3$, with $\Delta v=3$, near three times the fundamental wavenumber. It requires higher-order departures from the linear-dipole harmonic model and is ordinarily weaker again. These are qualitative intensity trends; resonances can redistribute intensity between nearby vibrational states.

= Solution

A <hot band> starts from a thermally populated vibrationally excited state, for example $v=1\to2$. It can satisfy $\Delta v=1$ and need not have a small transition moment, but its lower-state population is reduced by the <Boltzmann distribution>:
$$
\frac{N_1}{N_0}\simeq\exp[-(E_1-E_0)/(k_BT)].
$$
It is therefore usually weak at low temperature and grows relative to the fundamental on heating. <Anharmonicity> shifts its frequency away from the $0\to1$ transition; in a polyatomic molecule, excitation in another mode can also produce a hot-band sequence.

= Solution

A useful vibration-rotation expression, with energies measured from the potential minimum, is
$$
\boxed{\frac{E(v,J)}{hc}=\widetilde\nu_e(v+\tfrac12)-\widetilde\nu_ex_e(v+\tfrac12)^2+B_vJ(J+1),\qquad
B_v\simeq B_e-\alpha_e(v+\tfrac12).}
$$
Here $v=0,1,\ldots$ is the vibrational quantum number, $J=0,1,\ldots$ the rotational angular-momentum quantum number, $\widetilde\nu_e$ the harmonic <spectroscopic wavenumber>, $x_e$ a dimensionless anharmonic coefficient, and $B_v$ the vibrational-state-dependent <rotational constant>. $B_e$ is its equilibrium value and $\alpha_e$ describes vibration-rotation coupling. All wavenumber constants use the same inverse-length units; $h$ is the <Planck constant> and $c$ the <speed of light>.

The elementary harmonic <rigid rotor> result follows by setting $x_e=\alpha_e=0$, with $\widetilde\nu_e=(2\pi c)^{-1}\sqrt{k/\mu}$ and $B_e=h/(8\pi^2cI)$, where $I=\mu r_e^2$. The molecule's electronic energy would add a common constant.

This is a <rovibrational spectrum>: the vibrational excitation is accompanied by quantized <molecular rotation>. The high-wavenumber R branch has $\Delta J=+1$, and the low-wavenumber P branch has $\Delta J=-1$. In the simplest common-$B$ approximation,
$$
\widetilde\nu_R(J)=\widetilde\nu_0+2B(J+1),\qquad
\widetilde\nu_P(J)=\widetilde\nu_0-2BJ.
$$
Consecutive lines within either branch are therefore separated by roughly $2B$, while the gap between P(1) and R(0) is roughly $4B$. There is no Q branch: for this diatomic parallel $\Sigma\to\Sigma$ transition, the electric-dipole rotational selection rule allows $\Delta J=\pm1$, and the $\Delta J=0$ line strength vanishes.

The intensity envelope comes from rotational populations, approximately $(2J+1)e^{-hcBJ(J+1)/(k_BT)}$, together with the rotational line strengths. The paired peaks arise from the two chlorine <isotopologues>, $\mathrm H^{35}Cl$ and $\mathrm H^{37}Cl$: their different <reduced masses> shift both rotational and vibrational spacings. The more abundant $^{35}\mathrm{Cl}$ gives the stronger member. The changing spacing away from the band centre reflects $B_1\ne B_0$ and, at higher $J$, centrifugal distortion; it is not evidence for additional vibrational fundamentals.

Let $Q_k$ be a vibrational <normal mode coordinate> with symmetry $\Gamma_k$. The harmonic vibrational ground state is totally symmetric; the one-quantum state transforms as $Q_k$. Integration over a symmetry-invariant domain annihilates any integrand lacking the <totally symmetric representation>. Thus a fundamental requires
$$
\Gamma(v'=1_k)\otimes\Gamma(\mu_\alpha)\otimes\Gamma(v''=0)
\supset A_1
$$
for some Cartesian component $\alpha$. For the one-dimensional irreducible representations used here, this means $\Gamma_k=\Gamma(x)$, $\Gamma(y)$ or $\Gamma(z)$. Equivalently, expanding the <electric dipole moment> gives
$$
\mu_\alpha=\mu_{\alpha,0}+\left(\frac{\partial\mu_\alpha}{\partial Q_k}\right)_0Q_k+\cdots;
$$
the derivative can be nonzero only if $Q_k$ and $\mu_\alpha$ have compatible symmetry. This is the symmetry criterion for <vibrational infrared activity>; it permits a transition without guaranteeing a large intensity.

= Solution

For operations ordered as $(E,C_2(z),\sigma_{xz},\sigma_{yz})$, the characters of the twelve Cartesian displacements are
$$
\chi_{3N}=(12,-2,4,2).
$$
Only atoms left fixed contribute: all four for $E$ and $\sigma_{xz}$, but only C and O for $C_2$ and $\sigma_{yz}$. Multiplying their counts by the Cartesian traces $3,-1,1,1$ gives these numbers. Subtract
$$
\chi_{\rm trans}=(3,-1,1,1),\qquad \chi_{\rm rot}=(3,-1,-1,-1)
$$
to obtain $\chi_{\rm vib}=(6,0,4,2)$. The <character table> reduction gives
$$
\boxed{\Gamma_{\rm vib}=3A_1+2B_1+B_2.}
$$
The dimensions sum to $6=3N-6$, as required. The $B_2$ mode is the out-of-plane wag with this axis convention.

In this <C2v point group> convention, $z\sim A_1$, $x\sim B_1$, and $y\sim B_2$. Therefore all six <normal modes> have symmetry-allowed infrared fundamentals: $\boxed{6\text{ IR features}}$, absent accidental coincidences or vanishing intensities.

For <Raman spectroscopy>, examine the <polarizability tensor>: $x^2,y^2,z^2\sim A_1$, $xz\sim B_1$, and $yz\sim B_2$; $xy\sim A_2$ is also Raman-allowed but no $A_2$ vibration occurs here. All six vibrations are therefore Raman-active too, giving $\boxed{6\text{ Raman features and }6\text{ IR/Raman coincidences}}$. The centrosymmetric mutual-exclusion rule does not apply to $C_{2v}$ <formaldehyde>.

The out-of-plane displacements in the wag transform as $y$, hence as $B_2$. A one-quantum combination of two distinct <normal modes> transforms as the product of their irreducible representations. The observed feature satisfies

Write $p_i$ for the orthonormal $2p_z$ <atomic orbital> on numbered site $i$, with every positive lobe pointing along $+z$. A normalized set of <symmetry-adapted linear combinations> is
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|l}
\text{symmetry}&\text{name}&\text{symmetry orbital}\\\hline
B_{1u}&O_+&(p_1+p_8)/\sqrt2\\
B_{1u}&C_+&(p_2+p_7)/\sqrt2\\
B_{1u}&R_+&(p_3+p_4+p_5+p_6)/2\\
B_{3g}&O_-&(p_1-p_8)/\sqrt2\\
B_{3g}&C_-&(p_2-p_7)/\sqrt2\\
B_{3g}&R_-&(p_3+p_4-p_5-p_6)/2\\
B_{2g}&X&(p_3-p_4+p_5-p_6)/2\\
A_u&Y&(p_3-p_4-p_5+p_6)/2
\end{array}
$$
Each has unit squared coefficient sum, and every distinct pair has zero <inner product>. The orbital representation therefore reduces to $\boxed{3B_{1u}+3B_{3g}+B_{2g}+A_u}$. For example, the fully symmetric site sum still has $B_{1u}$ orbital symmetry because a $p_z$ orbital itself changes sign under operations that reverse $z$. Ignoring that sign would assign the wrong representations.

Set $E=\alpha+\lambda\beta$, with the usual bonding convention $\beta<0$. In the <Hückel method>, the dimensionless matrix $M=(H-\alpha I)/\beta$ has diagonal entries one on oxygen sites 1 and 8, zero on carbon, and unit entries between bonded neighbours. In the bases $(O_+,C_+,R_+)$ and $(O_-,C_-,R_-)$ above, its blocks are
$$
M_{B_{1u}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\1&0&\sqrt2\\0&\sqrt2&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
M_{B_{3g}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\1&0&\sqrt2\\0&\sqrt2&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The one-dimensional $B_{2g}$ and $A_u$ blocks have $\lambda=1$ and $-1$, respectively. The <characteristic polynomial> of the $B_{1u}$ block factors as
$$
\det(\lambda I-M_{B_{1u}})=(\lambda-1)(\lambda^2-\lambda-3),
$$
so its three <molecular orbital> energies are
$$
\boxed{E_{B_{1u}}=\alpha+\beta,\qquad\alpha+\frac{1+\sqrt{13}}2\beta,\qquad\alpha+\frac{1-\sqrt{13}}2\beta.}
$$
For the $B_{3g}$ block the <matrix trace> is zero. Thus its three $\lambda$ values sum to zero, and the pair supplied in the paper gives the remaining one as $-(1.86-2.12)\simeq0.26$. Directly, its secular equation is $\lambda^3-4\lambda+1=0$, whose roots are approximately $1.860806$, $0.254102$ and $-2.114908$. The supplied pair is approximate, so $0.26$ from its trace and $0.2541$ from the exact block should not be mistaken for an additional physical splitting.

All eight energies, ordered from low to high for $\beta<0$, are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\lambda&\text{symmetry}&\text{neutral occupancy}\\\hline
2.302776&B_{1u}&2\\
1.860806&B_{3g}&2\\
1&B_{1u}&2\\
1&B_{2g}&2\\
0.254102&B_{3g}&0\\
-1&A_u&0\\
-1.302776&B_{1u}&0\\
-2.114908&B_{3g}&0
\end{array}
$$
The eight $\pi$ electrons fill the four lowest <molecular orbitals>. The equality at $\lambda=1$ is an accidental degeneracy of this parameterized model, not a multidimensional representation of $D_{2h}$.

The extra electron occupies the <lowest unoccupied molecular orbital> of the neutral molecule, the $B_{3g}$ orbital at the middle root $\lambda_L=0.25410169\ldots$ of $\lambda^3-4\lambda+1=0$. Write
$$
\psi_L=aO_-+bC_-+cR_-.
$$
The first and third rows of the $B_{3g}$ block give
$$
b=(\lambda_L-1)a,\qquad c=\frac{\sqrt2(\lambda_L-1)}{1+\lambda_L}a,\qquad
 a=\left[1+(\lambda_L-1)^2+\frac{2(\lambda_L-1)^2}{(1+\lambda_L)^2}\right]^{-1/2}.
$$
Choosing the overall phase so that site 1 is positive gives $(a,b,c)\simeq(0.664623,-0.495741,-0.559033)$. In the original site order,
$$
\boxed{\psi_L\simeq0.469959p_1-0.350542p_2-0.279516p_3-0.279516p_4
+0.279516p_5+0.279516p_6+0.350542p_7-0.469959p_8.}
$$
The squared coefficients sum to one. Within this fixed-orbital <Hückel orbital charge density> approximation, the added electron contributes site populations
$$
\Delta n_{1,8}\simeq0.220862\ \text{each},\qquad
\Delta n_{2,7}\simeq0.122880\ \text{each},\qquad
\Delta n_{3,4,5,6}\simeq0.078129\ \text{each}.
$$
Thus the oxygen sites $\boxed{1\text{ and }8}$ receive the largest added negative charge, each receiving an increment of approximately $-0.221e$. Including the four doubly occupied neutral orbitals gives an anion $\pi$ population of about $1.621$ on each oxygen, $0.870$ on sites 2 and 7, and $1.005$ on each remaining carbon. Thus oxygen also has the largest total $\pi$ population in this model. A coefficient's sign is an orbital phase, not the sign of its electric charge. Predicting an actual charge density would additionally require orbital relaxation and a population-analysis convention; the stated result is the requested orbital model.
