The <internal energy> is the population-weighted sum of the molecular <energy levels>:
$$
U=\sum_i n_i\varepsilon_i=\frac Nq\sum_i\varepsilon_i e^{-\varepsilon_i/kT}.
$$
At fixed <volume>, assume the <energy levels> themselves are temperature-independent. Differentiating the molecular <partition function> gives
$$
\left(\frac{\partial q}{\partial T}\right)_V
=\frac1{kT^2}\sum_i\varepsilon_i e^{-\varepsilon_i/kT},
\qquad\boxed{U=NkT^2\frac{\partial\log q}{\partial T}}.
$$
Choosing the zero of energy changes $U$ by the corresponding constant per molecule, so the ground-level convention must be consistent throughout.

Relative to the <ground state>, the <quantum harmonic oscillator> levels are $vhc\widetilde\omega$. Put $\theta_v=hc\widetilde\omega/k$. Summing the <geometric series> gives the <vibrational partition function> $q=(1-e^{-\theta_v/T})^{-1}$. Therefore the vibrational <internal energy> is
$$
\boxed{U=\frac{Nk\theta_v}{e^{\theta_v/T}-1}}
=\frac{Nhc\widetilde\omega}{e^{hc\widetilde\omega/kT}-1}.
$$
The omitted <zero-point energy> would add $Nhc\widetilde\omega/2$ if absolute oscillator energies were used.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

“Low” and “high” compare $kT$ with the vibrational spacing $hc\widetilde\omega$, not <temperature> with an arbitrary number of kelvin. For $T\ll\theta_v$, occupation of excited levels is exponentially suppressed and
$$
\boxed{U\sim Nk\theta_v e^{-\theta_v/T}\longrightarrow0}.
$$
This vanishing result uses the ground-relative energy zero. For $T\gg\theta_v$, expand $e^{\theta_v/T}-1\sim\theta_v/T$ to obtain
$$
\boxed{U\sim NkT}.
$$
This agrees with <equipartition>: the oscillator has one quadratic kinetic and one quadratic potential term, contributing $kT/2$ each. The corresponding limiting vibrational <heat capacity> is zero at low <temperature> and $Nk$ at high <temperature>.

Subtracting the $v=0$ energy in the <Morse oscillator> gives
$$
\frac{\varepsilon_v-\varepsilon_0}{hc}
=v\widetilde\omega-v(v+1)\widetilde\omega x.
$$
The first three relative wavenumbers are $0$, $213$, and $424\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$. Thus, with $\theta_1=213/0.695\ \mathrm K$ and $\theta_2=424/0.695\ \mathrm K$,
$$
\boxed{q_3(T)=1+e^{-\theta_1/T}+e^{-\theta_2/T}},\qquad
\boxed{q_3(100\ \mathrm K)=1.04891}.
$$
This is a three-level <partition function>, as stipulated, rather than an unrestricted sum of the anharmonic formula to arbitrarily large $v$.

For one mole, $Nk=R$, so the three-level <Morse oscillator> result is
$$
\boxed{U_m=RT^2\frac{q_3'}{q_3}=124.21\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}}.

$$
\boxed{U_{m,\mathrm{harm}}=\frac{R(215/0.695)}{e^{215/(0.695\cdot100)}-1}

$$
The specified anharmonic calculation is about $1.7\%$ higher. Anharmonicity lowers the first excitation spacings and increases excited-state occupation. Both results remain small because $100\ \mathrm K$ is well below the vibrational <temperature>. The comparison also mixes a three-level anharmonic truncation with an infinite harmonic sum; omitted higher Morse levels would make a further small positive correction at this <temperature>.

For identical, non-interacting molecules, the translational <canonical partition function> is $Q_N=q_{\rm trans}^N/N!$. <Stirling's approximation> gives the <Helmholtz free energy>
$$
A=-NkT\left[\log(q_{\rm trans}/N)+1\right].
$$
At fixed $V$, $q_{\rm trans}\propto T^{3/2}$, so differentiation gives $S=Nk[\log(q_{\rm trans}/N)+5/2]$. For one mole at the standard-state molar <volume>,
$$
\boxed{S_{m,\rm trans}^{\circ}=R\left[\log\left(\frac{q_{m,\rm trans}^{\circ}}{N_A}\right)+\frac52\right]}.
$$
Here the quoted molar translational quantity is per mole, and its ratio to $N_A$ is dimensionless. The derivative defining <entropy> is taken at fixed <volume> before inserting the standard-state <volume>; differentiating along a constant-pressure path would give the wrong additive constant.

A <potential energy surface> gives the molecular electronic energy, with nuclear repulsion, as a function of the nuclear coordinates under the <Born-Oppenheimer approximation>. Its minima describe stable configurations and its saddle regions help identify reaction barriers.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

A <reaction coordinate> parametrizes progress along a chosen path on the <potential energy surface>. It usually combines several bond lengths or angles; it need not be one literal bond distance or elapsed time.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

A <transition state> is the configuration or dividing-surface region associated with passage between reactant and product basins. In the elementary saddle-point picture it is a first-order saddle of the <potential energy surface>: stable against small displacements in transverse directions but unstable along the reaction direction. <Transition state theory> counts forward crossings and, in its simplest form, neglects recrossing.

Write $M=m_A+m_B$ and $\mu=m_Am_B/M$. An atom has no rotational or vibrational internal factor under the stated electronic assumption. The transition-state <moment of inertia> is $I=\mu R^2$, so the high-temperature <rigid rotor> result is
$$
q_{\rm rot,TS}=\frac{8\pi^2\mu R^2kT}{\sigma h^2}.
$$
The translational factors therefore give
$$
\frac{f'_{\rm TS}}{f_Af_B}
=\left(\frac{h^2}{2\pi\mu kT}\right)^{3/2}
\frac{8\pi^2\mu R^2kT}{\sigma h^2}.
$$
Substituting into the <transition state theory> expression and simplifying,
$$
\boxed{k_{\rm 2nd}=\frac{\pi R^2}{\sigma}
\sqrt{\frac{8kT}{\pi\mu}}\,
\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta\varepsilon_0^{\ddagger}}{kT}\right)}.
$$
For distinct atoms, $\sigma=1$. This is a number-density rate coefficient, with units of <volume> per time. A rate law in molar concentrations uses $N_Ak_{\rm 2nd}$. The derivation assumes a classical high-temperature rotor at the dividing surface and a transmission coefficient of one.

Identify the transition-state separation with contact, $R=r_A+r_B$, take distinct atoms so $\sigma=1$, and let $E_A=N_A\Delta\varepsilon_0^{\ddagger}$. Then $\pi R^2=\rho$ and $\Delta\varepsilon_0^{\ddagger}/kT=E_A/RT$, yielding
$$
\boxed{k_{\rm 2nd}=\rho\sqrt{\frac{8kT}{\pi\mu}}e^{-E_A/RT}}.
$$
The velocity factor is the mean relative speed from the <Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution>. Thus this simple <transition state theory> model reproduces the stated <collision theory> expression. Molecular orientation constraints, quantum transmission and reaction-surface recrossing can invalidate the equality outside these simplifying assumptions.

{parent=A9}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

For $T\ll T_F$, only <electrons> within an energy window of order $kT$ around the <Fermi energy> can change occupation appreciably, because of the <Pauli exclusion principle>. The active fraction is of order $kT/E_F=T/T_F$. Giving each of these <electrons> energy of order $kT$ estimates an excitation energy $U-U_0\sim N_ekT^2/T_F$, and therefore
$$
\boxed{C_V\sim\frac{2N_ekT}{T_F}}.
$$
Per mole of conduction <electrons>, $N_ek=R$, which is the quoted $2RT/T_F$. Per mole of metal atoms with $z$ model conduction <electrons> each, the <conduction-electron count in the molar electronic heat capacity> instead gives $2zRT/T_F$. The numerical factor is only approximate: the <Sommerfeld expansion> gives $C_V=(\pi^2/2)N_ekT/T_F$ for a three-dimensional free-electron gas.

= Solution
{parent=f}

Lattice vibrations contribute a <phonon> <heat capacity>. At temperatures well below the <Debye temperature>, its leading contribution is proportional to $T^3$, whereas the electronic part is proportional to $T$:
$$
\boxed{C_{V,m}\simeq\gamma T+\beta T^3}.
$$
The lattice term can therefore become important rapidly as <temperature> rises, even if the electronic term dominates sufficiently close to zero. At higher <temperature> the lattice contribution approaches the <Dulong-Petit law> value $3R$ per mole of atoms within the harmonic approximation. This is not a claim that the low-temperature $T^3$ law holds at all temperatures.

For an intrinsic, nondegenerate <semiconductor>, charge neutrality and the <Boltzmann approximation> to the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> give $n_i=\sqrt{N_CN_V}\exp[-E_g/(2kT)]$. Assume the two polymorphs have equal effective-density-of-states prefactors and retain the quoted <band gaps> at the <temperature> being estimated. Their carrier-density ratio is then
$$
\frac{n_{i,\beta}}{n_{i,\alpha}}\simeq
\exp\left(\frac{3.4-3.2}{2k_BT}\right),
$$
with $k_B$ expressed in eV per kelvin. At $77\ \mathrm K$,
$$
\boxed{n_{i,\beta}/n_{i,\alpha}\simeq3.51\times10^6}.
$$
Both absolute intrinsic densities are extremely small at this <temperature>; a large ratio does not imply that either material is a good intrinsic conductor. Defects or unintended dopants can dominate real “undoped” specimens.

Suitable substitutional donors include **Si on a Ga site** and **O on an N site**. The former replaces a group-III atom with a group-IV atom; the latter replaces a group-V atom with a group-VI atom. Each provides an extra valence <electron> relative to its host site. Si is a plausible substitutional cation-site dopant, and the compact oxygen atom is compatible with an anion site. Site compatibility matters: merely choosing an element with an extra <electron> without considering which sublattice it occupies is insufficient.

A standard substitutional acceptor choice is **Mg on a Ga site**: a group-II atom replaces group III, leaving one fewer valence <electron>. Its size is sufficiently compatible with the cation sublattice to make substitution plausible. This identifies the intended acceptor chemistry; actual <hole> density also depends on activation, incomplete ionization and compensation. Size and valence rules alone do not guarantee a shallow, fully ionized acceptor.

= Solution
{parent=c}

At equilibrium, <electrons> initially diffuse from the n side and <holes> from the p side. Recombination leaves ionized donors and acceptors in a <depletion region>, producing an internal electric field and <band bending>. The <Fermi level> is constant across the equilibrium <p-n junction>. With p material on the left, both band edges lie higher on the p side, as in the left panel.

\Image[paper-2-gan-junction.png]
{title=Equilibrium and forward-biased direct-gap p-n junction energy bands, with injection and photon emission}

Forward bias, with the p side positive relative to n, reduces the injection barrier. <Electrons> and <holes> enter the junction region and can recombine radiatively, emitting <photons> with energy close to the <band gap>. Under bias, separate <electron> and <hole> <quasi-Fermi levels> replace a single equilibrium <Fermi level>.

Both <GaN> polymorphs have a <direct band gap>, so a conduction-band <electron> and valence-band <hole> can recombine without a large crystal-momentum change. <Silicon> and <germanium> have <indirect band gaps>, requiring <phonon> assistance for near-edge radiative recombination, which makes efficient band-edge light emission much harder. A direct gap enables the process; defects, carrier confinement and competing nonradiative channels still determine device efficiency.

{parent=A10}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=e}

**<GaN> has a substantial band-gap advantage for high-temperature operation.** Its gap is larger than those of <GaAs> and <GaP>, suppressing thermally generated intrinsic carriers through $n_i\propto e^{-E_g/(2kT)}$. A doped junction therefore retains its intended carrier imbalance and can have lower thermally generated leakage over a wider <temperature> range, other factors being equal. The wide gap also supports emission at higher <photon> energies.

This is a materials-based expectation, not a guarantee for every finished device. Dopant activation and compensation, defects, contact stability, heat extraction and nonradiative recombination can set the practical limit. Increasing <temperature> also reduces carrier mobility and can broaden or quench light emission. The relevant advantage is reduced intrinsic excitation; a comparison of operating temperatures needs those other device properties too.
