Write $D_x=y\partial_z-z\partial_y$, $D_y=z\partial_x-x\partial_z$ and $D_z=x\partial_y-y\partial_x$. The <product rule> applied to a smooth test <function> $f$ gives
$$
[D_x,D_y]f=y\partial_xf-x\partial_yf=-D_zf.
$$
The mixed second <partial derivatives> cancel because they commute. Since the <orbital angular momentum> components are $\hat j_i=-i\hbar D_i$, their <commutator> is
$$
\boxed{[\hat j_x,\hat j_y]=(-i\hbar)^2(-D_z)=i\hbar\hat j_z.}
$$
This is one of the <orbital angular momentum commutation relations>; the <Planck constant> enters through the definition of each <linear operator>.

<Cyclic permutation> of the coordinates in the preceding <commutator> calculation gives the remaining <orbital angular momentum commutation relations>:
$$
\boxed{[\hat j_y,\hat j_z]=i\hbar\hat j_x,\qquad [\hat j_z,\hat j_x]=i\hbar\hat j_y.}
$$
Reversing the order of a <commutator> reverses its sign.

Use the <commutator derivation identity> $[A,BC]=[A,B]C+B[A,C]$ and the <orbital angular momentum commutation relations>. They give
$$
\begin{aligned}
[\hat j_z,\hat j_x^2]&=i\hbar(\hat j_y\hat j_x+\hat j_x\hat j_y),\\
[\hat j_z,\hat j_y^2]&=-i\hbar(\hat j_x\hat j_y+\hat j_y\hat j_x),\\
[\hat j_z,\hat j_z^2]&=0.
\end{aligned}
$$
The first two expressions cancel on addition, so
$$
\boxed{[\hat j_z,\hat{\mathbf j}^{\,2}]=0.}
$$
The same argument works for each component of the <angular momentum operator>.

The squared <angular momentum operator> and one selected component are <commuting operators>, so a complete set of common <eigenfunctions> can be chosen: for the $z$ component these are the usual $|j,m\rangle$ <angular momentum eigenstates>. **The squared <angular momentum> and one chosen component can have simultaneously sharp values. All three components cannot have simultaneously sharp values in a nonzero-$j$ state.** The possible measured component values are $m\hbar$, while the squared magnitude is $j(j+1)\hbar^2$. This concerns the <quantum state>, rather than imperfect experimental resolution. The <Robertson uncertainty relation>, for example,
$$
\Delta j_x\,\Delta j_y\geq\frac{\hbar}{2}|\langle\hat j_z\rangle|,
$$
quantifies the restriction; its right-hand side may vanish in some states, without making all three components simultaneously sharp.

<Separation of variables> makes the angular factor a <spherical harmonic>. Applying the <quantum Hamiltonian> gives
$$
\hat H(RY_{lm})=
Y_{lm}\left[-\frac{1}{2r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2R'\right)
+\frac{l(l+1)}{2r^2}R-\frac{R}{r}\right].
$$
Consequently $RY_{lm}$ is an <energy eigenfunction> **provided the radial factor satisfies**
$$
\boxed{-\frac{1}{2r^2}\frac{d}{dr}(r^2R')+
\left[\frac{l(l+1)}{2r^2}-\frac1r\right]R=ER.}
$$
This is the <radial Schrödinger equation for a central potential>, here with the attractive <Coulomb potential>. An arbitrary $R$ does not give an <eigenfunction>; regularity, decay and <wavefunction normalization> select admissible bound <quantum states>.

For a $2p$ <atomic orbital>, $l=1$. The radial factor obeys
$$
R'=Ne^{-r/2}(1-r/2),\qquad R''=Ne^{-r/2}(r/4-1).
$$
Substitution into the <radial Schrödinger equation for a central potential> gives
$$
-\frac12R''-\frac{R'}r+\frac{R}{r^2}-\frac Rr=-\frac18R.
$$
Thus the <energy eigenvalue> is
$$
\boxed{E=-\frac18\,E_{\rm h}\simeq-3.40\ \mathrm{eV}.}
$$
The <normalization constant> cancels; $E_{\rm h}$ is the <Hartree energy>.

The <quantum variational principle> says that every admissible nonzero trial <wavefunction> obeys
$$
\boxed{\widetilde E=
\frac{\langle\widetilde\Psi|\hat H|\widetilde\Psi\rangle}
{\langle\widetilde\Psi|\widetilde\Psi\rangle}\geq E_0,}
$$
where $E_0$ is the <ground-state energy>. Thus the <Rayleigh quotient> is an upper bound. Equality holds when the trial lies entirely in the <ground-state subspace>.

= Solution

Expand the trial in a complete <orthonormal eigenbasis> of the <quantum Hamiltonian>, $\widetilde\Psi=\sum_nc_n\phi_n$, with $\hat H\phi_n=E_n\phi_n$. <Orthonormality> gives
$$
\widetilde E=
\frac{\sum_n|c_n|^2E_n}{\sum_n|c_n|^2},\qquad
\widetilde E-E_0=
\frac{\sum_n|c_n|^2(E_n-E_0)}{\sum_n|c_n|^2}\geq0.
$$
Every summand in the numerator is nonnegative because $E_n\geq E_0$. This proves the <quantum variational principle> for the stated discrete expansion; the trial <energy> is a <weighted mean> of exact <energy eigenvalues>.

Inside the <particle in a box>, the <quantum Hamiltonian> is
$$
\boxed{\hat H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2},\qquad 0<x<a.}
$$
The infinite potential outside imposes the <Dirichlet boundary conditions> $\psi(0)=\psi(a)=0$ and a zero <wavefunction> outside the interval. These boundary conditions are part of the <Hamiltonian operator>, rather than an optional addition.

Measure $x$ from the <carbon> nucleus toward the <sulfur> nucleus. The definition of the <center of mass> gives
$$
x_{\rm cm}=\frac{m_{\rm C}\,0+m_{\rm S}r}{m_{\rm C}+m_{\rm S}}.

For an axis through the <center of mass> perpendicular to the bond, the point-mass <moment of inertia> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
I&=m_{\rm C}r_1^2+m_{\rm S}r_2^2\\
&=\frac{m_{\rm C}m_{\rm S}^2+m_{\rm S}m_{\rm C}^2}
{(m_{\rm C}+m_{\rm S})^2}r^2
=\boxed{\frac{m_{\rm C}m_{\rm S}}{m_{\rm C}+m_{\rm S}}r^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The prefactor is the <reduced mass> $\mu$; this is the standard reduction of a two-body <linear rigid rotor> to $I=\mu r^2$.

The <rotational state populations> start at $N_0/N_0=1$. Their $2J+1$ <statistical weight> factor initially favors higher $J$, while the <Boltzmann factor> eventually suppresses them, producing one maximum followed by a tail tending to zero. The physically allowed values occur at integer $J$; a continuous curve only guides the eye.

Set $\theta_{\rm rot}=hc\widetilde B/k_{\rm B}$, the <rotational temperature>. For a continuous nonnegative variable $J$,
$$
\frac{d}{dJ}\log\frac{N_J}{N_0}
=\frac{2}{2J+1}-\frac{\theta_{\rm rot}}T(2J+1).
$$
Equating this <derivative> to zero gives the <most populated rotational level>
$$
\boxed{J_*=\frac12\left(\sqrt{\frac{2T}{\theta_{\rm rot}}}-1\right)
=\sqrt{\frac{k_{\rm B}T}{2hc\widetilde B}}-\frac12.}
$$
The second <derivative> is $-4/(2J+1)^2-2\theta_{\rm rot}/T<0$, so this is a maximum when $J_*\geq0$. At lower temperatures the constrained continuous maximum is $J=0$. For the physical integer maximum compare the nearby allowed levels explicitly.

The <trigonal pyramidal molecular geometry> has **<C3v point group> symmetry**: identity, two rotations about the threefold axis, and three vertical mirror reflections. Choose $z$ along the threefold axis through <nitrogen> and the center of the hydrogen triangle.

In the <C3v point group>, the <nitrogen> valence <atomic orbitals> transform as
$$
\boxed{2s:A_1,\qquad 2p_z:A_1,\qquad (2p_x,2p_y):E.}
$$
The $s$ orbital is invariant under every <symmetry operation>; $p_z$ is also invariant because each operation preserves the $z$ coordinate. The other two components mix as a two-dimensional <irreducible representation>.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution

Use the classes $(E,2C_3,3\sigma_v)$ of the <C3v point group>. For the three hydrogen $1s$ <atomic orbitals>, the <character of a representation> counts orbitals fixed by each <symmetry operation>. Identity fixes three, a nontrivial threefold rotation fixes none, and a vertical mirror fixes one. Therefore
$$
\chi_{\rm H}=(3,0,1).
$$
The <character inner product> gives multiplicities
$$
n_{A_1}=\frac{3+3}{6}=1,\quad
n_{A_2}=\frac{3-3}{6}=0,\quad
n_E=\frac{2\times3}{6}=1.
$$
Thus **$\Gamma_{\rm H}=A_1\oplus E$**, with the $E$ contribution accounting for two independent <symmetry orbitals>.

Neglecting intersite <orbital overlap>, one normalized real set of <symmetry-adapted linear combinations> is
$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\chi_{A_1}&=(s_A+s_B+s_C)/\sqrt3,\\
\chi_{E,1}&=(2s_A-s_B-s_C)/\sqrt6,\\
\chi_{E,2}&=(s_B-s_C)/\sqrt2.
\end{aligned}}

The coefficient vectors are <orthonormal>. The first has equal phase at all three sites; the second opposes site $A$ to $B,C$; the third has zero amplitude at $A$ and opposite phases at $B,C$. Any orthonormal rotation of the two $E$ functions is equally valid. With nonzero intersite <orbital overlap>, normalization must instead use the overlap <matrix>.

The hydrogen $A_1$ <symmetry orbital> mixes with both <nitrogen> $2s$ and $2p_z$, giving three $a_1$ <molecular orbitals>. The two hydrogen $E$ functions mix with $(2p_x,2p_y)$, giving a bonding $e$ pair and an antibonding $e$ pair. Eight valence <electrons> fill the lower levels as
$$
\boxed{(2a_1)^2(1e)^4(3a_1)^2.}

This uses conventional numbering that reserves $1a_1$ for the omitted <nitrogen> core $1s$ orbital. The low $2a_1$ and $1e$ levels provide the principal N–H <bonding molecular orbitals>; the highest occupied $3a_1$ level is the mainly nitrogen <lone pair>, with some bonding admixture permitted by the pyramidal geometry. Empty $4a_1$ and $2e$ levels are antibonding. Symmetry fixes which orbitals can mix, rather than their quantitative energy separations.

The planar equilateral structure has **<D3h point group> symmetry**. In addition to the threefold axis, it has a horizontal mirror plane containing all four nuclei and three twofold axes lying in that plane.

For planar <ammonia>, the <nitrogen> valence <atomic orbitals> have symmetries $2s:A_1'$, $(2p_x,2p_y):E'$ and $2p_z:A_2''$. The hydrogen <symmetry orbitals> span $A_1'\oplus E'$. Consequently the in-plane orbitals form a bonding $a_1'$ orbital, a bonding $e'$ pair and their antibonding partners. \boxed{(2a_1')^2(1e')^4(1a_2'')^2.}

The nitrogen-centered <highest occupied molecular orbital> of <ammonia> supplies a <lone pair>; planar <borane> has an empty out-of-plane $p$-derived <lowest unoccupied molecular orbital>. A <symmetry orbital> is a <linear combination of atomic orbitals> transforming as a component of an <irreducible representation> of the molecular <point group>. A <molecular orbital> is a one-electron <wavefunction> for the molecule, obtained as an <eigenfunction> of the chosen effective one-electron <Hamiltonian operator>. Symmetry organizes the eigenproblem, but a symmetry label alone does not determine its coefficients.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

**A <symmetry orbital> is a <molecular orbital> when it is also an <eigenfunction> of the effective one-electron <Hamiltonian operator>.** If an <irreducible representation> occurs once in a complete invariant model space, a symmetry-invariant <Hamiltonian> acts as a scalar on that copy by <Schur's lemma>. Its basis functions are then degenerate <eigenfunctions>. When the same symmetry occurs in several copies, those copies can mix; one must diagonalize the corresponding block. Merely having a one-dimensional symmetry label is insufficient if other orbitals have that same label.

The **$z$ axis is a $C_2$ axis** through the centers of opposite cube faces: a half turn permutes atoms within each species. \begin{array}{c|c|c}
&\mathrm{Li}&\mathrm{Na}\\ \hline
A_1&(L_1+L_2+L_3+L_4)/2&(N_5+N_6+N_7+N_8)/2\\
T_{2x}&(-L_1+L_2+L_3-L_4)/2&(N_5-N_6-N_7+N_8)/2\\
T_{2y}&(L_1-L_2+L_3-L_4)/2&(N_5-N_6+N_7-N_8)/2\\
T_{2z}&(L_1+L_2-L_3-L_4)/2&(N_5+N_6-N_7-N_8)/2
\end{array}
$$
Each $T_2$ row takes its signs from the corresponding coordinate, so the three functions transform together like $(x,y,z)$. These coefficient vectors are <orthonormal> when intersite <orbital overlap> is neglected. A common sign reversal of any row is physically immaterial.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Use the stipulated common <Hückel Coulomb parameter> $\alpha$, an equal <Hückel resonance integral> $\beta$ along each cube edge, and zero coupling elsewhere; take an orthonormal site basis. Each lithium vertex has three sodium neighbors. The totally symmetric combinations therefore have the block
$$
H_{A_1}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&3\beta\\3\beta&\alpha\end{pmatrix}.
$$
For a coordinate-like $T_2$ combination, the sum of the three neighboring sodium coordinate signs equals the lithium coordinate sign. Thus each of the three equivalent blocks is
$$
H_{T_2}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\\beta&\alpha\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Their <eigenvalues> give the <cube nearest-neighbour Hückel model>:
$$
\boxed{E=\alpha+3\beta\ (a_1),\quad\alpha+\beta\ (t_2),\quad
\alpha-\beta\ (t_2),\quad\alpha-3\beta\ (a_1).}
$$
For the bonding convention $\beta<0$ these are in increasing energy order, with <statistical weights> $1,3,3,1$. The eight valence <electrons> occupy **$(a_1)^2(t_2)^6$**, filling the four bonding spatial <molecular orbitals>. Their total one-electron energy is $8\alpha+12\beta$. This is a deliberately simplified model: unequal atomic energies or unequal overlaps require a generalized calculation.
