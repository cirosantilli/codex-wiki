= Solution

With the physics convention $\langle\Phi|\Psi\rangle=\int\Phi^*\Psi\,\mathrm d\tau$, the <adjoint operator> is defined by
$$
\boxed{\langle\Phi|\hat A\Psi\rangle
=\langle\hat A^\dagger\Phi|\Psi\rangle.}
$$
This definition includes the operator domains and their <boundary conditions>. If $\hat A$ is a <Hermitian operator> on the common domain, then $\hat A^\dagger\Phi=\hat A\Phi$. Conjugate symmetry of the <inner product> consequently gives
$$
\langle\Phi|\hat A\Psi\rangle
=\langle\hat A\Phi|\Psi\rangle
=\langle\Psi|\hat A\Phi\rangle^*,
$$
which is the required integral identity.

The <boundary conditions> must make the boundary form from <integration by parts> vanish for every pair of allowed functions. For a Schrödinger kinetic operator in one dimension, this means
$$
\left[\Phi^*\Psi'-(\Phi')^*\Psi\right]_{\rm boundary}=0.
$$
Sufficient examples are decay of the functions and derivatives at spatial infinity, homogeneous hard-wall conditions $\Phi=\Psi=0$ at finite endpoints, or matching periodic conditions on both functions and their derivatives. The exact boundary form depends on $\hat A$; square integrability alone does not replace the domain requirement.

The unperturbed <Hamiltonian> satisfies
$$
\boxed{\hat H^{(0)}\Psi_i^{(0)}=E_i^{(0)}\Psi_i^{(0)}.}
$$
The supplied <eigenfunctions> can be chosen as an <orthonormal eigenbasis>, with $\langle\Psi_j^{(0)}|\Psi_i^{(0)}\rangle=\delta_{ji}$.

Insert the expansions into the <energy eigenvalue> equation $\hat H\Psi_i=E_i\Psi_i$. Through first order,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat H\Psi_i&=\hat H^{(0)}\Psi_i^{(0)}
+\lambda\big(\hat H^{(0)}\Psi_i^{(1)}+\hat H^{(1)}\Psi_i^{(0)}\big)+O(\lambda^2),\\
E_i\Psi_i&=E_i^{(0)}\Psi_i^{(0)}
+\lambda\big(E_i^{(0)}\Psi_i^{(1)}+E_i^{(1)}\Psi_i^{(0)}\big)+O(\lambda^2).
\end{aligned}
$$
The zeroth-order coefficients reproduce part (i). Equating the coefficients of $\lambda$ and moving them to one side gives
$$
\boxed{(\hat H^{(0)}-E_i^{(0)})\Psi_i^{(1)}
+(\hat H^{(1)}-E_i^{(1)})\Psi_i^{(0)}=0.}
$$
Terms involving $\hat H^{(2)}$ first enter at order $\lambda^2$ and do not affect this equation of <first-order nondegenerate perturbation theory>.

= Solution

Multiply the first-order equation by $\Psi_i^{(0)*}$ and integrate. The first term vanishes because <Hermitian conjugation> moves the unperturbed <Hamiltonian> onto the known bra:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle\Psi_i^{(0)}|(\hat H^{(0)}-E_i^{(0)})|\Psi_i^{(1)}\rangle
&=\langle\hat H^{(0)}\Psi_i^{(0)}|\Psi_i^{(1)}\rangle
-E_i^{(0)}\langle\Psi_i^{(0)}|\Psi_i^{(1)}\rangle\\
&=0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $E_i^{(0)}$ is real. The remaining equation is
$$
\langle\Psi_i^{(0)}|\hat H^{(1)}|\Psi_i^{(0)}\rangle
-E_i^{(1)}\langle\Psi_i^{(0)}|\Psi_i^{(0)}\rangle=0.
$$
Using <wavefunction normalization> therefore yields
$$
\boxed{E_i^{(1)}=\langle\Psi_i^{(0)}|\hat H^{(1)}|\Psi_i^{(0)}\rangle
=\int\Psi_i^{(0)*}\hat H^{(1)}\Psi_i^{(0)}\,\mathrm d\tau.}
$$
Thus the first-order <energy> coefficient is the perturbation's <expectation value> in the unperturbed <quantum state>.

For this <quantum harmonic oscillator>, $\hat H^{(1)}=x^4$. The <Gaussian integral> gives
$$
E_0^{(1)}=\frac1{\sqrt\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^4e^{-x^2}\,\mathrm dx

Project the first-order equation onto $\Psi_j^{(0)}$, with $j\ne i$. By <orthonormality> and the <Hermitian operator> property,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle\Psi_j^{(0)}|(\hat H^{(0)}-E_i^{(0)})|\Psi_i^{(1)}\rangle
&=(E_j^{(0)}-E_i^{(0)})c_{ij},\\
\langle\Psi_j^{(0)}|(\hat H^{(1)}-E_i^{(1)})|\Psi_i^{(0)}\rangle
&=H_{ji}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{c_{ij}=\frac{H_{ji}}{E_i^{(0)}-E_j^{(0)}}
=\frac{H_{ij}^*}{E_i^{(0)}-E_j^{(0)}}\qquad(j\ne i),}
$$
and
$$
\boxed{\Psi_i^{(1)}=\sum_{j\ne i}
\frac{\langle\Psi_j^{(0)}|\hat H^{(1)}|\Psi_i^{(0)}\rangle}
{E_i^{(0)}-E_j^{(0)}}\Psi_j^{(0)}.}
$$
The numerator order is important: the question defines $H_{ij}$ with $i$ on the bra, whereas projecting the correction onto state $j$ gives $H_{ji}$. The two coincide only when the relevant <matrix element> is real.

Excluding an $i$ component chooses $\langle\Psi_i^{(0)}|\Psi_i^{(1)}\rangle=0$, a permitted first-order phase and <wavefunction normalization> convention. A component parallel to $\Psi_i^{(0)}$ is not determined by the projected equation. Nondegeneracy ensures nonzero denominators; a small <energy> gap can still make the perturbative expansion inaccurate unless $|\lambda H_{ji}|\ll|E_i^{(0)}-E_j^{(0)}|$.

The <Pauli exclusion principle> requires the complete <electron> <wavefunction> to change sign on exchange of any two <electrons>, including both position and <spin> labels. Let $a=1s$ and $b=2s$ be normalized, orthogonal spatial <atomic orbitals>, and let $\alpha,\beta$ denote <spin> up and down.

The symmetric and antisymmetric spatial functions are
$$
\Phi_\pm=\frac{a(1)b(2)\pm b(1)a(2)}{\sqrt2}.
$$
The <singlet state> is
$$
\boxed{\Psi_{S=0}=\Phi_+\,
\frac{\alpha(1)\beta(2)-\beta(1)\alpha(2)}{\sqrt2}.}
$$
The three <triplet states> share the antisymmetric spatial factor:
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\Psi_{1,+1}&=\Phi_-\alpha(1)\alpha(2),\\
\Psi_{1,0}&=\Phi_-\frac{\alpha(1)\beta(2)+\beta(1)\alpha(2)}{\sqrt2},\\
\Psi_{1,-1}&=\Phi_-\beta(1)\beta(2).
\end{aligned}}
$$
Every factor and complete product is normalized. Here the subscripts are $(S,M_S)$; the spatial <electron configuration> is $1s2s$ in each case.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Let $P_{12}$ exchange the complete labels of the two <electrons>. The spatial factors obey $P_{12}\Phi_\pm=\pm\Phi_\pm$. The <singlet state> <spin> factor is antisymmetric, while each <triplet state> <spin> factor is symmetric. Thus in all four cases
$$
\boxed{P_{12}\Psi=-\Psi,}
$$
as required by the <Pauli exclusion principle>. In particular, the $M_S=0$ <triplet state> remains a triplet despite having opposite measured <spin> projections in each product term.

For a spin-independent <Hamiltonian> and the same fixed spatial <atomic orbitals>, direct evaluation gives
$$
E_{\rm singlet}=h_a+h_b+J_{ab}+K_{ab},\qquad
E_{\rm triplet}=h_a+h_b+J_{ab}-K_{ab},
$$
where $J_{ab}$ is the direct <Coulomb integral> and $K_{ab}>0$ is the <exchange integral> for the <helium> $1s,2s$ pair. Therefore
$$
\boxed{E_{\rm singlet}-E_{\rm triplet}=2K_{ab}>0.}
$$
The antisymmetric spatial <wavefunction> vanishes when the two positions coincide and reduces their Coulomb repulsion. The lower <energy> of the maximum-spin member of this fixed <electron configuration> is the first of <Hund's rules>.

In <LS coupling>, add all orbital and <spin> <angular momenta> separately:
$$
\mathbf L=\sum_a\mathbf l_a,\qquad
\mathbf S=\sum_a\mathbf s_a,\qquad
\mathbf J=\mathbf L+\mathbf S.
$$
The <quantum numbers> specify
$$
\begin{aligned}
L^2&\longrightarrow\hbar^2L(L+1),&L_z&\longrightarrow\hbar M_L,\\
S^2&\longrightarrow\hbar^2S(S+1),&S_z&\longrightarrow\hbar M_S,\\
J^2&\longrightarrow\hbar^2J(J+1).
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus $M_L=-L,\ldots,L$ and $M_S=-S,\ldots,S$. The <addition of angular momentum> rule gives
$$
\boxed{J=|L-S|,|L-S|+1,\ldots,L+S.}
$$
In a product basis with individual projection labels,
$$
\boxed{M_L=\sum_a m_{l,a},\qquad M_S=\sum_a m_{s,a}.}
$$
The magnitudes $L$ and $S$ are not generally the arithmetic sums of the individual magnitudes; they result from angular-momentum coupling. The sum rules for projections remain valid for every product component of a coupled <angular momentum eigenstate>.

An <electron configuration> specifies the occupied one-electron subshells and their populations, such as $1s^22s^22p^2$, without fixing how all orbital and <spin> <angular momenta> are coupled.

An <atomic term> groups states of a configuration with the same $L$ and $S$. An <atomic fine-structure level> additionally specifies $J$; <spin-orbit coupling> can split one term into several such levels. In zero external field, a level retains $2J+1$ magnetic substates.

The <term symbol> is
$$
\boxed{{}^{2S+1}L_J,}
$$
with letters $S,P,D,F,G,\ldots$ denoting $L=0,1,2,3,4,\ldots$. The superscript is the <spin multiplicity>. Without the $J$ subscript, ${}^{2S+1}L$ identifies an <atomic term>, rather than an individual fine-structure level.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Closed subshells have $L=S=0$, so only the open $p$ or $d$ subshell determines each <atomic term>. Apply <Hund's rules> in order: maximize $S$, maximize $L$ at that $S$, then choose the lowest $J$ below half filling or the highest $J$ above half filling.

For <carbon>, the two $2p$ <electrons> occupy different magnetic <atomic orbitals> with parallel <spins>, giving $S=1$. Their largest allowed $M_L$ at that <spin> is $1+0=1$, so $L=1$, a $P$ term. The $p^2$ subshell is less than half full, giving $J=|1-1|=0$. **The ground level is ${}^3P_0$.**

For <vanadium>, three parallel-spin $3d$ <electrons> give $S=3/2$. The largest $M_L$ compatible with the <Pauli exclusion principle> is $2+1+0=3$, hence $L=3$, an $F$ term. The $d^3$ subshell is less than half full, so $J=3-3/2=3/2$. **The ground level is ${}^4F_{3/2}$.**

For <cobalt>, the $d^7$ subshell is equivalent to three $d$ holes for the allowed $L,S$ terms, so its maximum-spin, maximum-$L$ term is again ${}^4F$. Explicitly, five spin-up <electrons> fill all $m_l$ values and two spin-down <electrons> can occupy $m_l=2,1$, giving $M_S=3/2$ and $M_L=3$. Since $d^7$ is more than half full, $J=3+3/2=9/2$. **The ground level is ${}^4F_{9/2}$.**

* The <atomic nuclei> are much heavier than <electrons>, so nuclear motion is slower. In the <Born-Oppenheimer approximation>, first hold the nuclear positions $\mathbf R$ fixed and solve
  $$
  \hat H_e(\mathbf r;\mathbf R)\psi_n(\mathbf r;\mathbf R)
  =U_n(\mathbf R)\psi_n(\mathbf r;\mathbf R).
  $$
* Approximate the complete <wavefunction> by $\Psi(\mathbf r,\mathbf R)=\psi_n(\mathbf r;\mathbf R)\chi_n(\mathbf R)$ and neglect the nuclear-derivative couplings involving $\nabla_{\mathbf R}\psi_n$ and $\nabla_{\mathbf R}^2\psi_n$.
* Nuclear motion then occurs on one electronic <potential energy> surface:
  $$
  \boxed{\left[\hat T_N+U_n(\mathbf R)+V_{NN}(\mathbf R)\right]\chi_n=E\chi_n.}
  $$
  Here $U_n$ excludes the nuclear repulsion $V_{NN}$, which must be included once.
* The mass separation makes the discarded coupling terms small when electronic <energy> gaps are sufficiently large; near a degeneracy or avoided crossing, a single-surface description can fail.

Differentiate the <Lennard-Jones potential>. Its minimum satisfies
$$
-12\sigma^{12}r^{-13}+6\sigma^6r^{-7}=0,
\qquad\boxed{r_e=2^{1/6}\sigma,\quad V(r_e)=-\alpha.}
$$
For <dilithium>, the two wells consequently have
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
(\sigma,\alpha)&r_e\text{ in bohr}&D_e\text{ in hartree}\\\hline
(3.4,0.06)&3.8164&0.060\\
(4.9,0.05)&5.5001&0.050
\end{array}
$$
**Under an additional same-orbital, Hund-type exchange comparison, assign $(3.4,0.06)$ to the triplet and $(4.9,0.05)$ to the singlet:** the lower-energy minimum is associated with the exchange-stabilized <triplet state>, as in part (a).

This assignment needs that extra orbital-parentage assumption. A scalar <Lennard-Jones potential> does not itself encode <spin>, and a common atomic dissociation limit does not specify a unique molecular electronic configuration or spatial symmetry. The two curves even cross at about $5.006$ bohr: neither lies below the other at every separation. Thus **the minima and well depths are determined unambiguously; the <spin> labels are a conditional qualitative inference**, rather than a unique consequence of the four supplied parameters. Neither “a triplet must always have a longer bond” nor a universal ordering of unrelated excited states is justified.

The resolved lines arise from simultaneous changes of vibration and rotation in <hydrogen cyanide>. A vibrational fundamental connects many populated lower rotational levels to different upper rotational levels, giving the fine structure of a <rovibrational spectrum>.

The prominent central <Q branch>, together with flanking <P branch> and <R branch> lines, identifies a perpendicular band. For linear HCN, this is the **doubly degenerate bending fundamental**, not either stretching fundamental. Bending creates a transition-dipole component perpendicular to the molecular axis; its <vibrational angular momentum> permits $\Delta J=0$ as well as $\Delta J=\pm1$.

The two perpendicular bending coordinates share one vibrational frequency. Their degeneracy is different from the series of resolved rotational transitions superimposed on that fundamental. The stretches have their <electric dipole transition moment> along the molecular axis and would give the usual parallel-band P/R pattern without this strong central Q feature.

Let $J$ label the lower rotational state and write the elementary band model as
$$
\widetilde\nu=\widetilde\nu_*+B'J'(J'+1)-B''J(J+1).
$$
Here $B'',B'$ are lower and upper <rotational constants>, in inverse centimetres; a constant term associated with upper-state <vibrational angular momentum> can be included in $\widetilde\nu_*$. The <P branch> has $J'=J-1$, and the <R branch> has $J'=J+1$.

In this model the six printed labels are assigned as
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c}
\widetilde\nu/\mathrm{cm}^{-1}&\text{branch label}\\\hline
709.04&P(1)\\
706.09&P(2)\\
703.15&P(3)\\
700.22&P(4)\\
720.91&R(2)\\
723.90&R(3)
\end{array}}
$$
The low-frequency series steps away from the central origin towards increasing lower $J$. The first two high-frequency R positions are approximately $714.96$ and $717.93\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$, so the labels at $720.91$ and $723.90$ correspond to $R(2)$ and $R(3)$; they are not the first two rotational transitions.

There is a physical limitation to taking this schematic model literally. A strict $l=0\to|l|=1$ bending band has $J'\geq1$, so a genuine $P(1)$ transition to $J'=0$ is forbidden. The printed $709.04$ feature has the formal $P(1)$ position of the elementary model. The numerical analysis below follows the printed line sequence; it does not infer a physically allowed $J'=0$ bending level from that feature.

Use <rovibrational spectroscopy> combination differences so that the band origin cancels. With the assignments above, $R(2)$ and $P(4)$ end at the same upper level $J'=3$, giving
$$
\widetilde\nu[R(2)]-\widetilde\nu[P(4)]
=B''\{4\cdot5-2\cdot3\}=14B''.
$$
Likewise $R(3)$ and $P(3)$ start at the same lower level $J=3$, so
$$
\widetilde\nu[R(3)]-\widetilde\nu[P(3)]
=B'\{4\cdot5-2\cdot3\}=14B'.
$$
Only the printed wavenumbers are needed:
$$
\boxed{B''=\frac{720.91-700.22}{14}=1.47786\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1},}
$$
$$
\boxed{B'=\frac{723.90-703.15}{14}=1.48214\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}.}
$$
Thus **$B'-B''\simeq0.00429\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$**. The <rotational constants> differ only slightly, so the average <moment of inertia> changes little on vibrational excitation. The upper value is slightly larger, corresponding to a slightly smaller effective <moment of inertia>. This sign is possible for a bending excitation; the intuition that excitation must always lengthen a diatomic bond is not a general rule for a polyatomic bend.

The rounded printed values are internally consistent: fitting all six positions to the same elementary model gives approximately $\widetilde\nu_*=711.9945\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$, $B''=1.47797\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ and $B'=1.48203\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$, with residuals below $0.002\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$. The displayed combination-difference values have more digits than their physical accuracy; the meaningful scale is about $1.48\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$.

For a linear polyatomic <molecule>, the first-order <polyatomic vibration-rotation interaction> is
$$
B_{\mathbf v}=B_e-\sum_i\alpha_i(v_i+d_i/2),
$$
where $d_i$ is the degeneracy of <normal mode> $i$. The bending mode has $d_2=2$, so the two measured states imply
$$
\alpha_2=B''-B'\simeq-0.00429\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1},\qquad
B_e=B''+\alpha_2+\frac{\alpha_1+\alpha_3}{2}.
$$
The two stretching zero-point contributions are not supplied. **The true all-mode equilibrium constant is therefore not uniquely determined by this band alone.** Neglecting those unknown stretching corrections gives the bend-only estimate
$$
\boxed{B_e^{(\rm bend)}\simeq2B''-B'=1.47357\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}.}
$$
Using $B=h/(8\pi^2cI)$ and converting inverse centimetres to inverse metres,
$$
I_e^{(\rm bend)}=\frac{6.62607015\times10^{-34}}
{8\pi^2(2.99792458\times10^8)(100)(1.47357)}
\simeq\boxed{1.900\times10^{-46}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}.}
$$
If the intended course approximation instead treats the observed vibration as a single nondegenerate oscillator with $B_v=B_e-\alpha_e(v+1/2)$, its extrapolation gives
$$
B_e^{(1D)}=\frac{3B''-B'}2=1.47571\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1},\qquad
I_e^{(1D)}=1.897\times10^{-46}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}.
$$
That is a useful effective one-coordinate estimate, but it should not be identified with an exact equilibrium constant of the doubly degenerate polyatomic bend. Both estimates give **$I\simeq1.90\times10^{-46}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$** at the accuracy warranted here.

The expanded feature resolves the <Q branch>: each line has $\Delta J=0$ but starts from a different lower rotational level. In the simple <rigid rotor> band model,
$$
\boxed{\widetilde\nu_Q(J)=\widetilde\nu_*+(B'_Q-B'')J(J+1).}
$$
Because the upper <rotational constant> is larger, the lines extend towards higher wavenumber. Their adjacent spacings are
$$
\widetilde\nu_Q(J+1)-\widetilde\nu_Q(J)=2(B'_Q-B'')(J+1),
$$
so they crowd together at the low-frequency edge and become increasingly separated. A strictly resolved bending-band analysis may distinguish the upper parity component sampled by Q lines from that sampled by P/R lines; it is not necessary to impose identical high-resolution constants on every branch in this sketch.

The intensity envelope reflects rotational state populations and the rotational transition strength. For this perpendicular <Q branch>, the usual rotational line-strength factor gives the approximate dependence
$$
\boxed{I_Q(J)\propto(2J+1)
\exp\!\left[-\frac{hcB''J(J+1)}{k_{\rm B}T}\right].}
$$
At small $J$, the increasing magnetic degeneracy makes the line intensity rise; at large $J$, <Boltzmann suppression> dominates. The maximum is therefore at an intermediate $J$. This is a population envelope across distinct transitions, rather than one intrinsically broad vibrational line.

Count the successive <Q branch> lines from the low-frequency edge, taking $J=1$ as the first allowed bend transition; the first lines are crowded and partly unresolved. $$
\boxed{T\simeq\frac{hcB''}{2k_{\rm B}}(2J_{\max}+1)^2.}

The alternating short and long bonds preserve fourfold, but not eightfold, rotations. Including the molecular plane and <inversion symmetry>, the planar structure belongs to **$D_{4h}$**, the <D4h molecular point group>.

Use the printed axes: the short bonds are $1$–$2$, $3$–$4$, $5$–$6$ and $7$–$8$, and the $x$ and $y$ axes pass through the midpoints of opposite short bonds. Interchanging short and long bonds is not a <molecular symmetry operation> of this geometry.

For the $p_z$ <atomic orbital> basis, only orbitals whose centres remain fixed contribute to a <character>. Each such orbital contributes $+1$ if its $z$ direction is preserved and $-1$ if it is reversed. In the standard <D4h molecular point group> class order,
$$
\begin{array}{c|rrrrrrrrrr}
&E&2C_4&C_2&2C_2'&2C_2''&i&2S_4&\sigma_h&2\sigma_v&2\sigma_d\\\hline
\chi_{p_z}&8&0&0&0&0&0&0&-8&0&0
\end{array}
$$
No nonidentity operation other than $\sigma_h$ leaves any <carbon> site fixed. Reflection in the molecular plane fixes all eight sites but reverses every $p_z$ function.

<Molecular character reduction> gives
$$
\boxed{\Gamma_{p_z}=A_{1u}+A_{2u}+B_{1u}+B_{2u}+2E_g.}
$$
The dimensions sum to $1+1+1+1+2\cdot2=8$. The two copies of $E_g$ are separate equivalent <irreducible representations> which may mix with one another; they do not force all four functions to have a common <energy>.

Write $p_j=p_{zj}$ and use the orthonormal basis of the <Hückel method>. The four normalized one-dimensional <symmetry-adapted linear combinations> are
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Phi_{A_{1u}}&=\frac{p_1-p_2+p_3-p_4+p_5-p_6+p_7-p_8}{\sqrt8},\\
\Phi_{A_{2u}}&=\frac{p_1+p_2+p_3+p_4+p_5+p_6+p_7+p_8}{\sqrt8},\\
\Phi_{B_{1u}}&=\frac{p_1-p_2-p_3+p_4+p_5-p_6-p_7+p_8}{\sqrt8},\\
\Phi_{B_{2u}}&=\frac{p_1+p_2-p_3-p_4+p_5+p_6-p_7-p_8}{\sqrt8}.
\end{aligned}
$$
For example, the $xz$ mirror plane exchanges sites $1\leftrightarrow6$, $2\leftrightarrow5$, $3\leftrightarrow4$, $8\leftrightarrow7$, without changing the sign of an individual $p_z$ orbital. The all-positive combination is unchanged by every vertical mirror but changes sign under $\sigma_h$, giving $A_{2u}$. The alternating combination changes sign under both sorts of vertical mirror, as required for $A_{1u}$. The two remaining sign patterns distinguish $B_{1u}$ from $B_{2u}$ in the printed axes. The signs refer to the upper $p_z$ lobes; the lower lobes have opposite <quantum phases>, and neither colour denotes an <electric charge>.

For a normalized coefficient vector $\mathbf c$, the <energy> is
$$
E=\alpha+2\sum_{\langle j,k\rangle}\beta_{jk}c_jc_k.
$$
Every site has one short-bond and one long-bond neighbour. Equivalently, apply the <Hückel method> <matrix> to any coefficient and read off its two neighbouring signs. The results are
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{symmetry}&E\\\hline
A_{1u}&\alpha-\beta_S-\beta_L\\
A_{2u}&\alpha+\beta_S+\beta_L\\
B_{1u}&\alpha-\beta_S+\beta_L\\
B_{2u}&\alpha+\beta_S-\beta_L
\end{array}}
$$
With negative resonance integrals and $|\beta_S|>|\beta_L|$, the $A_{2u}$ and $B_{2u}$ levels are occupied, while $B_{1u}$ and $A_{1u}$ are unoccupied in the closed-shell alternating-bond state.

Use the four supplied opposite-site combinations in the order $(\phi_1,\phi_2,\phi_3,\phi_4)$. Their <Hückel method> <matrix> is
$$
H_{E_g}=\begin{pmatrix}
\alpha&\beta_S&0&-\beta_L\\
\beta_S&\alpha&\beta_L&0\\
0&\beta_L&\alpha&\beta_S\\
-\beta_L&0&\beta_S&\alpha
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The negative corner is essential. For example, $\langle\phi_4|H|\phi_1\rangle$ includes the long bonds $4$–$5$ and $8$–$1$, each with opposite coefficient signs, giving $-\beta_L$.

Because the basis is orthonormal, the secular <determinant> is
$$
\boxed{\begin{vmatrix}
\alpha-E&\beta_S&0&-\beta_L\\
\beta_S&\alpha-E&\beta_L&0\\
0&\beta_L&\alpha-E&\beta_S\\
-\beta_L&0&\beta_S&\alpha-E
\end{vmatrix}=0.}
$$
It factors as $\big[(\alpha-E)^2-\beta_S^2-\beta_L^2\big]^2=0$, giving two doubly degenerate <energies>, $\alpha\pm\sqrt{\beta_S^2+\beta_L^2}$.

Fill four spatial <molecular orbitals> with the eight $\pi$ <electrons>. For negative $\beta_S,\beta_L$ and $|\beta_S|>|\beta_L|$, the occupied <energies> are $\alpha+\beta_S+\beta_L$, $\alpha+\beta_S-\beta_L$, and the pair at $\alpha-\sqrt{\beta_S^2+\beta_L^2}$. Therefore

$$
\boxed{E_\pi^{\rm uniform}=8\alpha+4(1+\sqrt2)\beta.}
$$
At equal bond lengths, the last two <electrons> occupy the frontier degeneracy at $E=\alpha$, so their distribution within that degenerate space does not change the bare <Hückel method> <energy>.

The uniform planar eight-electron ring has a partially filled frontier degeneracy. <Bond-length alternation> splits those levels, permitting the two frontier <electrons> to occupy the lowered orbital and opening a gap to the empty partner. This is the electronic mechanism for destabilizing the equal-bond planar geometry.

A controlled comparison keeps the mean resonance integral fixed. Put
$$
\beta_S=\beta(1+\delta),\qquad
\beta_L=\beta(1-\delta),\qquad \beta<0,\quad0<\delta<1.
$$
Using part (i), the change from the uniform ring is
$$
\Delta E_\pi=4\beta\delta
+4\sqrt2\,\beta\big(\sqrt{1+\delta^2}-1\big)
=4\beta\delta+2\sqrt2\,\beta\delta^2+O(\delta^4)<0.
$$
Thus **a small alternation lowers the electronic <energy>**, with a leading gain linear in $\delta$. An ordinary elastic cost starts quadratically, so the equal-bond planar state is unstable in this model. The equilibrium distortion requires those geometric and sigma-bond costs as well.

The eight $\pi$ <electrons> constitute a $4n$ cyclic count rather than the closed-shell $4n+2$ case associated with <aromaticity>. The result concerns the imposed planar geometry; it does not establish the unconstrained three-dimensional equilibrium shape of <cyclooctatetraene>.

For a displacement <group representation>, an atom contributes only if the operation leaves its site fixed. Its three Cartesian displacements then contribute the trace of the polar-vector transformation.

Take $z$ perpendicular to the ring and an in-plane $x$ axis through opposite <carbon> atoms. To avoid confusing the principal twofold rotation with the in-plane axes, write $C_2(z)$ for the former, $C_{2a}$ for an in-plane axis through atoms and $C_{2b}$ for one through opposite bond midpoints. The <carbon> displacement characters in the <D6h molecular point group> are
$$
\begin{array}{c|rrrrrr}
C&E&2C_6&2C_3&C_2(z)&3C_{2a}&3C_{2b}\\\hline
\chi_C&18&0&0&0&-2&0\\
iC&i&2S_3&2S_6&\sigma_h&3\sigma_d&3\sigma_v\\\hline
\chi_C(iC)&0&0&0&6&0&2
\end{array}
$$
A through-atom half-turn fixes two <carbon> sites and has Cartesian trace $-1$, giving $-2$. The molecular-plane reflection fixes all six carbons with trace $+1$, giving $6$. A through-atom vertical mirror fixes two sites with trace $+1$, giving $2$.

Using <molecular character reduction>, the 18-dimensional carbon-only result is
$$
\boxed{\Gamma_C=A_{1g}+A_{2g}+B_{2g}+E_{1g}+2E_{2g}
+A_{2u}+B_{1u}+B_{2u}+2E_{1u}+E_{2u}.}
$$
The six one-dimensional terms contribute six dimensions and the six copies of two-dimensional representations contribute twelve, totaling eighteen. The distinction between carbon-only and whole-molecule displacements is needed in part (ii).

Each <hydrogen> lies on the radial line through its attached <carbon>. Consequently every <molecular symmetry operation> fixes or permutes the <hydrogen> sites exactly as it does the <carbon> sites, and the Cartesian trace is the same. Thus $\Gamma_H=\Gamma_C$ and the complete benzene displacement <group representation> is **$\Gamma_{3N}=2\Gamma_C$**, of dimension $36$.

Subtract translations $A_{2u}+E_{1u}$ and rotations $A_{2g}+E_{1g}$. The full set of <normal modes> is
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\Gamma_{\rm vib}={}&2A_{1g}+A_{2g}+2B_{2g}+E_{1g}+4E_{2g}\\
&+A_{2u}+2B_{1u}+2B_{2u}+3E_{1u}+2E_{2u}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The one-dimensional components contribute $2+1+2+1+2+2=10$ coordinates. The two-dimensional components contribute $2(1+4+3+2)=20$, giving **$30=3(12)-6$ vibrational degrees of freedom**. There are twenty distinct symmetry-labelled fundamentals before accidental coincidences, because each doubly degenerate pair has one frequency.

Subtracting translations and rotations directly from the carbon-only result would instead give twelve carbon-skeleton coordinates. That is a six-site mechanical model, not all the <normal modes> of $\mathrm{C_6H_6}$. The <hydrogen> contribution is required for the actual molecule.

The transition strength depends on $|R_{ji}|^2$, so a necessary condition is that at least one Cartesian <electric dipole transition moment> is nonzero. An integral can be nonzero only if its integrand contains the <totally symmetric representation>:
$$
\Gamma_f^*\otimes\Gamma(\mu_a)\otimes\Gamma_i\supset A_{1g}.
$$
The harmonic vibrational <ground state> is totally symmetric, and a one-quantum state of <normal mode> $k$ transforms as $\Gamma^{(k)}$. For the real <irreducible representations> here, the fundamental is therefore symmetry-allowed precisely when
$$
\boxed{\Gamma^{(k)}\text{ occurs among the Cartesian dipole components.}}
$$
Equivalently, expand the <electric dipole moment> near equilibrium:
$$
\mu_a(\mathbf Q)=\mu_a(0)+\sum_k
\left(\frac{\partial\mu_a}{\partial Q_k}\right)_0Q_k+\cdots.
$$
The fundamental has $\langle1_k|Q_k|0_k\rangle\ne0$, so <vibrational infrared activity> requires a nonzero dipole derivative of the matching symmetry. In $D_{6h}$, $z\sim A_{2u}$ and $(x,y)\sim E_{1u}$, giving **$A_{2u}$ and $E_{1u}$ as the IR-active species**. Symmetry permission does not guarantee a large band intensity.

The <electronic transition> starts from $A_{1g}$, so the electric-dipole test reduces to whether the final electronic <irreducible representation> occurs among $A_{2u}+E_{1u}$. The excited $E_{1u}$ state matches the $(x,y)$ dipole components and is therefore **electric-dipole allowed**.

The $B_{2u}$ state matches neither $z$ nor $(x,y)$ and is **electric-dipole forbidden** at the equilibrium geometry. Both excited states are singlets, so the difference is spatial symmetry rather than the <spin> selection rule. Inversion parity alone does not decide the result: both final states are ungerade, but only the matching vector representation is allowed.

The initial <vibronic state> is $A_{1g}$ and the final one is $E_{1u}$, so an in-plane dipole component satisfies
$$
E_{1u}\otimes E_{1u}\otimes A_{1g}
=A_{1g}+A_{2g}+E_{2g}.

The product contains the <totally symmetric representation>. **The combined electronic–vibrational transition is therefore symmetry-allowed**, with an in-plane <electric dipole transition moment>.

A nonzero intensity still needs <vibronic coupling>. In a coordinate-independent separation, the forbidden electronic <matrix element> would remain zero. Its first derivative with respect to the $E_{2g}$ coordinate can instead be nonzero, and the vibrational factor $\langle1|Q|0\rangle$ supplies the required one-quantum transition. This is <vibrational activation of an electronic transition> and explains why the band can be weak.

A coordinate $Q_s$ of an $A_{1g}$ <normal mode> is unchanged by every <molecular symmetry operation>. Every harmonic-oscillator excitation $H_n(Q_s)e^{-Q_s^2/2}$ therefore also has symmetry $A_{1g}$, including odd values of $n$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{B_{2u}\otimes E_{2g}\otimes A_{1g}=E_{1u}}
$$
for any number of quanta in that totally symmetric coordinate. **The same electric-dipole permission survives every $A_{1g}$ excitation.**

This statement concerns symmetry. Actual progression intensities depend on vibrational <inner products>, equilibrium displacements and the coordinate dependence of the <electric dipole transition moment>; a permitted line need not have measurable intensity for every vibrational quantum number.
