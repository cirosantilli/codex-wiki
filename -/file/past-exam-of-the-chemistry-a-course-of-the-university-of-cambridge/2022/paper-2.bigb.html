= Solution
{parent=a}

Take the <energy> of an isolated, field-free gas atom as zero, and let $E_b>0$ be the field-independent <binding energy> at one <adsorption site>. The two adsorbed <spin> energies are
$$
\boxed{\varepsilon_\pm=-E_b\pm\mu_{\rm B}B.}
$$
Here $\mu_{\rm B}$ is the <Bohr magneton>. The lower state is stabilized by increasing $|B|$; the assignment of the two signs to names for the <spin> projections is a convention.

= b
{parent=A6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Put $x=\mu_{\rm B}B/(k_{\rm B}T)$. Summing the two <Boltzmann factors> gives the occupied-site internal <partition function>
$$
\boxed{z=e^{-\varepsilon_+/k_{\rm B}T}+e^{-\varepsilon_-/k_{\rm B}T}
=2e^{E_b/k_{\rm B}T}\cosh x.}
$$
If the single atom can occupy any of the $M$ identical sites, its complete surface <partition function> is
$$
\boxed{q_{\rm ads}=Mz=2M e^{E_b/k_{\rm B}T}\cosh x.}
$$
One also commonly calls the one-site factor $z$ itself $q_{\rm ads}$. Both conventions are valid, but the combinatorial factor in part (c) must use the same convention. We use the complete one-atom surface function $q_{\rm ads}=Mz$ below and give the equivalent one-site expression.

= c
{parent=A6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Use the ideal <Langmuir adsorption model>: identical independent sites, at most one atom per site, no lateral interactions, and no additional thermally active adsorbed degrees of freedom beyond the two <spin> states. Treat the atoms as indistinguishable and localized after <adsorption>; only site occupancy and <spin> choices distinguish configurations.

There are $\binom MN$ ways to select $N$ occupied sites. For any such selection, independent <spin> sums contribute $z^N$. Thus
$$
\boxed{Q_N=\binom MN z^N.}
$$
With $q_{\rm ads}=Mz$ as defined in part (b), this is
$$
\boxed{Q_N=q_{\rm ads}^{\,N}f(M,N),\qquad
f(M,N)=\frac{M!}{N!(M-N)!\,M^N},\quad0\leq N\leq M.}
$$
Equivalently, starting from labelled particles gives $M!/(M-N)!$ distinct assignments to different sites; division by $N!$ removes particle relabellings. The $M^{-N}$ removes the site multiplicity already included in each full one-particle function.

If $q_{\rm ads}$ denotes the one-site internal factor instead, **$f(M,N)=\binom MN$** and $Q_N=\binom MN q_{\rm ads}^{\,N}$. These are identical physical results, not different counting models. In particular, the complete-function convention gives $f(M,1)=1$ and hence $Q_1=q_{\rm ads}$.

= Solution
{parent=d}

For the adsorbed <lattice gas>, the <Helmholtz free energy> is
$$
A_{\rm ads}=-k_{\rm B}T\left[\ln\binom MN+N\ln z\right].
$$
Use <Stirling's approximation> with $M,N,M-N$ large, then differentiate at fixed $M,T,B$. Writing <surface coverage> as $\theta=N/M$ gives the <adsorption chemical potential>
$$
\boxed{\mu_{\rm ads}=k_{\rm B}T\ln\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}
-k_{\rm B}T\ln z
=-E_b-k_{\rm B}T\ln(2\cosh x)
+k_{\rm B}T\ln\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}.}
$$
The occupation ratio comes from site-mixing <entropy>; it is essential near saturation.

Let $N_g$ be the number of free atoms and $n_g=N_g/V$. In the field-free gas, both <spin> states have zero <energy>, so the one-atom <partition function> is $q_g=2V/\Lambda^3$, where $\Lambda$ is the <thermal de Broglie wavelength>. For a dilute <ideal gas>,
$$
Q_g=\frac{(2V/\Lambda^3)^{N_g}}{N_g!},\qquad
A_g\simeq N_gk_{\rm B}T\left[\ln\frac{n_g\Lambda^3}{2}-1\right].
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mu_g=k_{\rm B}T\ln\frac{n_g\Lambda^3}{2}
=k_{\rm B}T\ln\frac{p\Lambda^3}{2k_{\rm B}T}.}
$$
The last equality uses $p=n_gk_{\rm B}T$. The bulk <spin degeneracy> is two even though the bulk <magnetic field> vanishes: degeneracy is not the absence of <spin> states.

= Solution
{parent=e}

At <adsorption> equilibrium, $\mu_{\rm ads}=\mu_g$. The expressions from part (d) give
$$
\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}
=z\frac{p\Lambda^3}{2k_{\rm B}T}
=\frac{p\Lambda^3}{k_{\rm B}T}e^{E_b/k_{\rm B}T}\cosh x.
$$
Define
$$
\boxed{b(T,B)=\frac{\Lambda^3}{k_{\rm B}T}
 e^{E_b/k_{\rm B}T}\cosh\!\left(\frac{\mu_{\rm B}B}{k_{\rm B}T}\right).}
$$
Solving the occupation ratio yields
$$
\boxed{\theta=\frac{bp}{1+bp}.}
$$
The coefficient $b$ has units of inverse <pressure>, so $bp$ is dimensionless. The two <spin> states contribute a factor two on both sides of the <adsorption> equilibrium and cancel; retaining the surface factor but dropping the bulk degeneracy would incorrectly double $b$.

{parent=A6}

= Solution
{parent=f}

At fixed <temperature>,
$$
\boxed{b(B)=b(0)\cosh x,\qquad x=\mu_{\rm B}B/(k_{\rm B}T).}
$$
Thus <magnetic-field-dependent Langmuir adsorption> is even in $B$ and increases with $|B|$. At fixed nonzero gas <pressure>, stronger field increases <surface coverage>; in an isotherm plot, the half-coverage <pressure> $p_{1/2}=1/b$ moves to lower <pressure>. The saturation coverage remains one because the number of sites is unchanged.

For weak fields, with $\theta_0=b(0)p/[1+b(0)p]$,
$$
\theta(B)=\theta_0+\frac12\theta_0(1-\theta_0)x^2+O(x^4).
$$
There is no linear small-field change. At large $|x|$, $\cosh x\sim e^{|x|}/2$, and at fixed $p>0$ the coverage approaches one. The field lowers the <free energy> of the surface <spin> ensemble relative to the unsplit bulk; simply averaging the two surface energies to $-E_b$ would miss this equilibrium effect.

<Chemical equilibrium> for $\mathrm M\rightleftharpoons\mathrm{M^+}+\mathrm{e^-}$ requires
$$
\mu_{\rm M}=\mu_{\rm M^+}+\mu_e.
$$
Insert the given <chemical potential> form and define the positive <ionization energy>
$$
E_I=\varepsilon_{\rm M^+}+\varepsilon_e-\varepsilon_{\rm M}.
$$
After collecting logarithms,
$$
k_{\rm B}T\ln\left(\frac{f_{\rm M}c_{\rm M^+}c_e}
{c_{\rm M}f_{\rm M^+}f_e}\right)+E_I=0,
$$
so
$$
\frac{c_{\rm M^+}c_e}{c_{\rm M}}
=\frac{f_{\rm M^+}f_e}{f_{\rm M}}e^{-E_I/k_{\rm B}T}.
$$
Define a dimensionless concentration <equilibrium constant> using a standard <number density> $c^\circ$:
$$
\boxed{K_c=\frac{(c_{\rm M^+}/c^\circ)(c_e/c^\circ)}{c_{\rm M}/c^\circ}
=\frac{f_ef_{\rm M^+}}{f_{\rm M}}\frac1{c^\circ}e^{-E_I/k_{\rm B}T}.}
$$
Each $f_i$ is a <molecular partition function density>, with units of inverse volume. Consequently $f_ef_{\rm M^+}/f_{\rm M}$ has density units and division by $c^\circ$ makes $K_c$ dimensionless. All ground-state energies must use the same <energy> reference.

{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

For monatomic species, there are no molecular rotational or vibrational factors. Assume excited electronic states are negligible, so the internal partition sums are the ground-state degeneracies. A free <electron> has two <spin> states, giving
$$
f_e=2\left(\frac{2\pi m_ek_{\rm B}T}{h^2}\right)^{3/2},\qquad
f_{\rm M}=g_{\rm M}\left(\frac{2\pi m_{\rm M}k_{\rm B}T}{h^2}\right)^{3/2},
$$
with the analogous expression for the ion. Since losing one <electron> changes the atomic mass negligibly, $(m_{\rm M^+}/m_{\rm M})^{3/2}\simeq1$. Hence
$$
\boxed{K_c=\left(\frac{2\pi m_ek_{\rm B}T}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}
\frac1{c^\circ}\frac{2g_{\rm M^+}}{g_{\rm M}}e^{-E_I/k_{\rm B}T}.}
$$
This is the ground-state form of the <Saha ionization equation>. Its assumptions include a dilute, nondegenerate ideal mixture, negligible interparticle interactions, thermal and <chemical equilibrium>, no additional ionization stages, and consistent treatment of internal degeneracies. Nuclear-spin factors may be omitted when they cancel between the atom and ion; they must not be counted on only one side.

= Solution
{parent=c}

For fixed degeneracies and <ionization energy>,
$$
\frac{\mathrm d\ln K_c}{\mathrm dT}
=\frac{3}{2T}+\frac{E_I}{k_{\rm B}T^2}>0.
$$
Therefore **$K_c$ increases with <temperature>** in this model. Ionization costs positive <energy>, so it is suppressed at low <temperature> by its <Boltzmann factor>. At higher <temperature> that penalty becomes less important.

Ionization also creates an additional independently translating particle and more ways to distribute the particles and their momenta. This favours the ionized mixture through its translational and mixing <entropy>, consistent with the $T^{3/2}$ prefactor and increasing ionization at high <temperature>.

= Solution

Let $\alpha$ be the <degree of ionization>. Conservation of nuclei and <charge neutrality>, with no pre-existing <electrons> or other ions, give
$$
c_{\rm M}=(1-\alpha)c_{\rm tot},\qquad
c_{\rm M^+}=c_e=\alpha c_{\rm tot}.
$$
Substitution into the definition of $K_c$ gives
$$
\frac{\alpha^2}{1-\alpha}=y,\qquad
 y=\frac{K_cc^\circ}{c_{\rm tot}}.
$$
The physical root lies between zero and one:
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac{\sqrt{y^2+4y}-y}{2}
=\frac{2}{1+\sqrt{1+4/y}}.}
$$
The second form avoids subtracting nearly equal terms at large $y$. The limits are $\alpha\simeq\sqrt y$ when $y\ll1$ and $\alpha\simeq1-1/y$ when $y\gg1$. The dependence on initial <number density> matters: $K_c$ alone, without $c_{\rm tot}$ and the standard-state convention, does not specify the ionized fraction.

For hydrogen, the electronic ground state has $g_{\rm H}=2$, while the proton has no electronic degeneracy, $g_{\rm H^+}=1$. For sodium, the neutral $3s\,{}^2S_{1/2}$ ground state has $g_{\rm Na}=2$ and the closed-shell ion has $g_{\rm Na^+}=1$. Ignoring hyperfine structure and consistently cancelling nuclear-spin factors, **$2g_{\rm M^+}/g_{\rm M}=1$ for both**.

At $5000\ \mathrm K$,
$$
k_{\rm B}T=0.43087\ \mathrm{eV},\qquad
\left(\frac{2\pi m_ek_{\rm B}T}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}
\simeq8.537\times10^{26}\ \mathrm m^{-3}.
$$
It is convenient to calculate the density $D=K_cc^\circ$, which avoids choosing an unnecessary numerical value of the standard density. The results are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
& D/\mathrm m^{-3}&y=D/c_{\rm tot}&\alpha\\\hline
\mathrm H&1.672\times10^{13}&1.672\times10^{-9}&4.088\times10^{-5}\\
\mathrm{Na}&6.176\times10^{21}&0.6176&0.5356
\end{array}
$$
Thus **hydrogen is about $0.00409\%$ ionized, whereas sodium is about $53.6\%$ ionized** in the stated ground-state ideal-gas model. The large difference is dominated by the smaller sodium <ionization energy> in the exponential. The weak-ionization approximation is excellent for hydrogen but would be inaccurate for sodium, so the quadratic solution is needed there.

= Solution

Assume a dilute classical <ideal gas> of $N$ indistinguishable, noninteracting particles, with translational states dense enough to replace their sum by a continuum integral. Separate translation from all internal degrees of freedom. Then
$$
Q_{\rm trans}=\frac{q_{\rm trans}^{\,N}}{N!},\qquad
q_{\rm trans}=\frac V{\Lambda^3},\qquad
\Lambda=\frac h{\sqrt{2\pi mk_{\rm B}T}}.
$$
Using <Stirling's approximation> in the <Helmholtz free energy>,
$$
A_{\rm trans}=-Nk_{\rm B}T\left[\ln\frac{V}{N\Lambda^3}+1\right].
$$
Differentiate at fixed $N,V$. Since $\Lambda^{-3}\propto T^{3/2}$,
$$
S_{\rm trans}=-\left(\frac{\partial A_{\rm trans}}{\partial T}\right)_{N,V}
=Nk_{\rm B}\left[\ln\frac{V}{N\Lambda^3}+\frac52\right].
$$
Only after this derivative set $N=N_A$ and $V=RT/p^\circ$ for one mole at <standard pressure>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{S^\circ_{\rm trans,m}}R
=\ln\left[\left(\frac{2\pi mk_{\rm B}T}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}\frac{RT}{p^\circ}\right]
-\ln N_A+\frac52.}
$$
This is the translational <Sackur-Tetrode equation>. The $N!$ factor is essential for the correct extensive <entropy>; replacing $V$ by a temperature-dependent standard molar volume before taking the fixed-volume derivative would give the wrong constant term.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For independent internal rotational states, the rotational factor in the many-molecule <partition function> is $q_{\rm rot}^N$. The indistinguishability factor has already been included in the translational part and is not applied a second time to the internal factor. Thus
$$
A_{\rm rot}=-Nk_{\rm B}T\ln q_{\rm rot},\qquad
\frac{S_{\rm rot,m}}R=\ln q_{\rm rot}
+T\left(\frac{\partial\ln q_{\rm rot}}{\partial T}\right).
$$
A nonlinear <rigid rotor> has three rotational quadratic degrees of freedom; in the high-temperature limit, $q_{\rm rot}\propto T^{3/2}$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{S_{\rm rot,m}=R\left(\ln q_{\rm rot}+\frac32\right).}
$$
This is the <rotational entropy> contribution, with the appropriate <rotational symmetry number> already inside $q_{\rm rot}$. It has no ideal-gas <pressure> dependence.

In the orthogonal nearest-neighbour <tight-binding model>,
$$
\alpha_s=\langle s_j|H|s_j\rangle,\qquad
\beta_s=\langle s_j|H|s_{j+1}\rangle.
$$
The on-site integral $\alpha_s$ sets the band centre, while the <hopping integral> $\beta_s$ describes coupling between neighbouring orbitals. With a consistent phase convention, the bonding combination at the zone centre has $\beta_s<0$.

For <p orbitals> directed along the chain, use
$$
\boxed{E_p(k)=\alpha_p+2\beta_p\cos(ka),\qquad \beta_p>0.}
$$
Here every positive $p$ lobe points in the same spatial direction. Neighbouring equal-sign coefficients then bring opposite-sign facing lobes together and make the zone-centre combination antibonding; the alternating zone-boundary combination is bonding. Thus the usual axial $p$ hopping has the opposite sign to $s$ hopping. <Orbital phase and the sign of one-dimensional hopping> must be treated consistently; replacing only $\alpha_s$ by $\alpha_p$ while silently keeping the same physical hopping sign would give the wrong band ordering.

Write $t_s=|\beta_s|$ and $t_p=|\beta_p|$, so
$$
E_s=\alpha_s-2t_s\cos(ka),\qquad
E_p=\alpha_p+2t_p\cos(ka).
$$
The labelled key points of the two <dispersion relations> are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
k&E_s&E_p\\\hline
0&\alpha_s-2t_s&\alpha_p+2t_p\\
\pi/(2a)&\alpha_s&\alpha_p\\
\pi/a&\alpha_s+2t_s&\alpha_p-2t_p
\end{array}

The $s$ band rises from centre to boundary and the axial $p$ band falls. The separated-band assumption requires $\alpha_p-\alpha_s>2(t_s+t_p)$. The upper-left panel shows a representative pair, with the other panels supplying the requested comparisons in part (b).

The <energy-band widths> are the difference between their extrema:
$$
\boxed{W_s=4t_s=4|\beta_s|,\qquad W_p=4t_p=4|\beta_p|.}

For the separated bands, the top of the $s$ band and bottom of the $p$ band are both at $k=\pi/a$. Hence

\boxed{E_g^{\min}=\alpha_p-\alpha_s-2(t_s+t_p).}

It is a **direct <band gap> at the zone boundary**, because both limiting states have the same <wavevector>. This result uses the opposite signs of the $s$ and axial-$p$ hoppings.

= Solution
{parent=i}

The opposite spatial parity of the <s orbital> and axial <p orbital> gives opposite signs for left and right $s$–$p$ hopping. With real $\beta_{sp}$, one consistent Bloch-basis convention gives
$$
H(k)=\begin{pmatrix}
E_s(k)&2i\beta_{sp}\sin(ka)\\
-2i\beta_{sp}\sin(ka)&E_p(k)
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The resulting energies are
$$
\boxed{E_\pm=\frac{E_s+E_p}{2}
\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{E_s-E_p}{2}\right)^2+4\beta_{sp}^2\sin^2(ka)}.}
$$
Thus <s-p mixing in a one-dimensional tight-binding chain> lowers the lower branch and raises the upper branch wherever the coupling is nonzero. For separated atomic levels, the lower branch remains mainly $s$ and the upper mainly $p$, with increasing admixture where their uncoupled energies approach.

The upper-right panel of the figure in part (a)(ii) compares the mixed curves with dashed unmixed curves. The mixing vanishes at both $k=0$ and $\pi/a$, so those endpoints remain unchanged. If the unmixed curves approach or cross in the interior, the nonzero coupling produces an <avoided crossing>.

With mixing and $\gamma=|\beta_{sp}|$, the equal-on-site-energy result is
$$
\boxed{E_\pm(k)=\pm2\sqrt{t^2\cos^2(ka)+\gamma^2\sin^2(ka)}.}

For the regime $0<\gamma<t$ considered in part (iii), the crossing becomes an <avoided crossing> with gap $4\gamma$ at $k=\pi/(2a)$. The lower band has its maximum there and the upper band its minimum. Their orbital characters exchange smoothly across this region, with equal $s/p$ weights at its centre. The lower-right panel gives the sketch.

The zero-mixing limit recovers the crossing of case (1). A useful special limiting case is $\gamma=t$, where the square root is independent of <wavevector> and both bands are flat at $E=\pm2t$. This flat-band limit is outside the strict $\gamma<t$ assumption made in part (iii).

= Solution

Use $t=|\beta|$ and $\gamma=|\beta_{sp}|<t$. Direct substitution gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c}
k&E_{k,1}&E_{k,2}\\\hline
0&+2t&-2t\\
\pi/(2a)&+2\gamma&-2\gamma\\
\pi/a&+2t&-2t
\end{array}}
$$
The upper branch falls to a minimum at the middle point and rises symmetrically; the lower branch rises to a maximum and falls symmetrically. These are the lower-right curves in the figure in part (a)(ii).

At $k=0$ the lower state is pure $s$ and the upper state pure $p$. At $k=\pi/a$ the assignments are reversed. At $k=\pi/(2a)$ the two states have equal orbital weights because their diagonal energies coincide and the off-diagonal coupling is nonzero.

More explicitly, for the lower band and $u=ka$, define $D=\sqrt{t^2\cos^2u+\gamma^2\sin^2u}$. Then
$$
\boxed{w_s^{(-)}=\frac12\left(1+\frac{t\cos u}{D}\right),\qquad
w_p^{(-)}=1-w_s^{(-)}.}
$$
The upper-band weights are interchanged. For zero mixing, the energies still reduce to the crossing limit, but the orbital weights at the exact crossing are not uniquely fixed by <energy>. These normalized squared amplitudes quantify the smooth change of orbital character; the Bloch-basis phase in the off-diagonal matrix element does not change them.

A typical <intrinsic semiconductor> has a nearly full <valence band> ending at $E_v$ and a nearly empty <conduction band> starting at $E_c$, separated by
$$
\boxed{E_g=E_c-E_v>0.}
$$
At finite <temperature>, the equilibrium <Fermi level> lies near the middle of the gap. In the nondegenerate parabolic-band approximation,
$$
\boxed{E_F=\frac{E_c+E_v}{2}+\frac{k_{\rm B}T}{2}\ln\frac{N_v}{N_c},}
$$
where $N_c,N_v$ are the two <effective density of states> values. Equal densities of states put it exactly at midgap; unequal masses or valley multiplicities shift it slightly. The left panel gives the requested energy-level sketch, with doped cases for comparison.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

In a <nondegenerate semiconductor>, the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> reduces near the conduction edge to $f(E)\simeq e^{-(E-E_F)/k_{\rm B}T}$. The corresponding dilute hole probability near the valence edge gives
$$
n=N_c e^{-(E_c-E_F)/k_{\rm B}T},\qquad
p=N_v e^{-(E_F-E_v)/k_{\rm B}T}.
$$
An intrinsic, neutral sample has $n=p=n_i$. Multiplying the two expressions therefore yields
$$
\boxed{n_i=\sqrt{N_cN_v}\,e^{-E_g/(2k_{\rm B}T)}.}
$$
Each excitation creates an <electron-hole pair>; <charge neutrality> is why the carrier concentration has half the gap in its activation exponent. The <electrical conductivity> is
$$
\sigma=e(n\mu_e+p\mu_h)
=e(\mu_e+\mu_h)\sqrt{N_cN_v}\,e^{-E_g/(2k_{\rm B}T)}.
$$
Thus **$\sigma\propto e^{-E_g/(2k_{\rm B}T)}$ to leading exponential accuracy**. The prefactor contains the mobilities and the density-of-states <temperature> dependence; for fixed parabolic masses, $\sqrt{N_cN_v}\propto T^{3/2}$.

Measure conductivity in the intrinsic regime and plot $\ln\sigma$ against $1/T$. If the prefactor is constant or its <temperature> dependence has been corrected, a slope $m$ gives
$$
\boxed{E_g=-2k_{\rm B}m.}
$$
Use the intrinsic branch rather than a donor freeze-out or extrinsic branch. A temperature-dependent gap or mobility changes the apparent activation <energy>, so a precision extraction must account for those prefactors.

<Arsenic> has five valence <electrons>, one more than <silicon>. As a substitutional <donor impurity>, it supplies a weakly bound extra <electron> which is largely thermally released into the <conduction band> at room <temperature>, leaving an ionized positive donor.

Consequently **the mobile <electron> density increases far above the intrinsic value**, so the conductivity increases substantially even at ppm dopant levels. The crystal remains macroscopically neutral: fixed donor charge compensates the added mobile charge.

= Solution

There are three useful <temperature> regimes.

At low <temperature>, <dopant freeze-out> keeps most donor <electrons> bound. Warming ionizes donors and increases conductivity through the rising mobile-electron concentration. For an uncompensated dilute donor system, negligible holes and <spin degeneracy> two, <charge neutrality with partial donor ionization> gives
$$
\frac{n^2}{N_D-n}=\frac{N_c}{2}e^{-E_b/k_{\rm B}T}.
$$
Deep in freeze-out, $n\ll N_D$, so
$$
n\simeq\sqrt{\frac{N_DN_c}{2}}e^{-E_b/(2k_{\rm B}T)}.
$$
The activation scale is set by the shallow donor binding, rather than the whole <band gap>.

In the extrinsic regime, donors are largely ionized and $n\simeq N_D$ is nearly constant. The <temperature> dependence of conductivity then mainly follows the <electron mobility>. Increasing <phonon> scattering can make conductivity fall as <temperature> rises, even though donors remain ionized; impurity scattering can give a different mobility trend at lower <temperature>.

At sufficiently high <temperature>, thermally generated <electron>–hole pairs overwhelm the dopant population. **The intrinsic, gap-activated increase in conductivity returns.** The boundaries depend on donor concentration, compensation and scattering, so no one exponential describes the entire doped-sample curve.

<Gallium> has three valence <electrons>, one fewer than silicon, and acts as an <acceptor impurity>. A neutral acceptor can be described as a negatively charged centre binding an <electron hole>. Thermal release of these holes gives a <p-type semiconductor>.

At low <temperature>, the bound hole can absorb far-IR photons in transitions between acceptor levels. The simplest scalar <hydrogenic acceptor state> model predicts
$$
E_n=-\frac{R_A^*}{n^2},\qquad
R_A^*=R_H\frac{m_h^*/m_e}{\epsilon_r^2},
$$
so one expects **a discrete bound-hole absorption series followed by a photoionization continuum**. In that idealization, the first lines would be at $3R_A^*/4$, $8R_A^*/9$ and $15R_A^*/16$, with threshold $R_A^*$.

The hole <effective mass> is not supplied, so the <electron> mass $0.2m_e$ cannot be reused to give numerical Ga line positions. In a real <valence band>, multiple hole bands, anisotropic masses and spin-orbit effects can split and complicate the series; acceptor spectra are generally less accurately described by one scalar hydrogenic ladder.<Optical Properties of Solids by Mildred S.
