In <Q-switching>, hold the <optical resonator>'s <quality factor> low while pumping builds <population inversion>, then rapidly reduce the loss. The stored excitation produces a large <optical gain>, the intracavity <light intensity> grows, and <stimulated emission> depletes the <population inversion> until the <optical gain> falls below the <laser threshold>. The resulting <optical pulse> is a transient discharge of stored excitation.

Its duration depends on the high-<quality-factor> <photon lifetime>, initial <population inversion>, saturation dynamics and switching time. For example, a simple <laser rate equation> model is
$$
\dot S=(g(N)-\tau_c^{-1})S,\qquad
\dot N=R_p-N/\tau_u-g(N)S,
$$
where $S$ is intracavity <photon number>, $N$ is the available inversion in compatible units, $R_p$ is the pump rate and $\tau_c$ is the <photon lifetime>. During the short discharge, pumping and upper-state decay can often be neglected. The build-up and depletion rates determine the width; there is no universal equality between the width and $\tau_c$. The initially stored <energy> and the output coupling also matter. Typical <Q-switching> produces nanosecond-scale <optical pulses>.

In <mode locking>, fixed relative <phases> between many longitudinal <normal modes> produce constructive <interference> during a short part of each <optical resonator> round trip. The round-trip time sets the repetition period. The available coherent <gain bandwidth>, the number of locked <normal modes>, the <spectral phase>, and residual <group velocity dispersion> determine the <optical pulse> duration.

For $M$ consecutive equally weighted <normal modes> of <angular frequency> spacing $\Delta\omega$, their <wave amplitude> sum is proportional to
$$
\frac{\sin(M\Delta\omega t/2)}{\sin(\Delta\omega t/2)}.
$$
Its central peak has width of order $1/(M\Delta\omega)$, while its repetition period is $2\pi/\Delta\omega$. A <transform-limited pulse> therefore becomes shorter as more coherent <gain bandwidth> is used. This mechanism can give picosecond or femtosecond <optical pulses>: it does not require depletion of the whole stored <population inversion> during each peak, as <Q-switching> does. <Mode locking> generally accesses a much shorter <time-bandwidth product> scale than the gain-discharge time of <Q-switching>.

Use the complex <electric field> convention $e^{-i\omega t}$ and take the locked <wave amplitudes> $a(\omega_p)$ to be real and nonnegative after extracting their common <phase>. Linear <superposition> gives
$$
E(t)=\sum_p a(\omega_p)e^{-i\omega_pt+i\phi_0}
=e^{-i(\omega_{\rm ce}t-\phi_0)}\sum_p a(\omega_p)e^{-ip\Delta\omega t}.
$$
The leading factor has unit modulus, so it does not change the <light intensity>. The <triangle inequality> bounds the remaining modulus by $\sum_pa(\omega_p)$. Consecutive occupied <normal modes> attain this bound together at
$$
\boxed{t_m=mT,\qquad T=\frac{2\pi}{\Delta\omega},\qquad m=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
There is also a peak at $t=0$. The <electric field> obeys $E(t+T)=e^{-i\omega_{\rm ce}T}E(t)$, although the <light intensity> is periodic.

The <carrier-envelope phase> specifies where the carrier oscillation lies relative to the maximum of an <optical pulse>'s envelope. With $s=t-t_m$ and $E=F(s)e^{-i\omega_0s+i\psi_m}$, the signed complex-field <phase> at the envelope maximum is $\psi_m$. Consequently,
$$
\boxed{\psi_{m+1}-\psi_m=-\omega_{\rm ce}T
=-\frac{2\pi\omega_{\rm ce}}{\Delta\omega}\pmod{2\pi}.}
$$
If instead the real <electric field> is written $F(s)\cos(\omega_0s+\phi_{{\rm CE},m})$, then $\phi_{{\rm CE},m}=-\psi_m$ and the conventionally quoted slip is $\phi_{\rm slip}=+2\pi\omega_{\rm ce}/\Delta\omega$. These are the same physical shift with opposite phase definitions.

The <carrier-envelope phase> matters for few-cycle <optical pulses>, when the largest instantaneous <electric field> affects strong-field dynamics, for <high harmonic generation>, and for stabilizing an <optical frequency comb>. An ordinary cycle-averaged <light intensity> measurement does not determine it.

Put $b=\Delta\omega_b>0$ and $s=t-t_m$. At the peak time, $e^{-ip\Delta\omega t_m}=1$, so the exact comb sum becomes
$$
E(t_m+s)=Ae^{i\phi_0-i\omega_{\rm ce}t_m}
\sum_p e^{-[(\omega_p-\omega_0)/b]^2}e^{-i\omega_ps}.
$$
For many occupied <normal modes>, $b\gg\Delta\omega$, replace the sum by an <integral> with density $1/\Delta\omega$. Setting $x=\omega-\omega_0$ and evaluating the <Fourier transform of a Gaussian> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
E(t_m+s)&\simeq\frac{A}{\Delta\omega}e^{i\phi_0-i\omega_{\rm ce}t_m}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-[(\omega-\omega_0)/b]^2}e^{-i\omega s}\,d\omega\\
&=\frac{A\sqrt\pi b}{\Delta\omega}
 e^{-b^2s^2/4}e^{-i\omega_0s}e^{i\phi_0-i\omega_{\rm ce}t_m}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the isolated-<optical-pulse> approximation has
$$
\boxed{F(s)=\frac{A\sqrt\pi\,\Delta\omega_b}{\Delta\omega}
 e^{-(\Delta\omega_b s/2)^2},\qquad
\psi_m=\phi_0-\omega_{\rm ce}t_m.}
$$
The <optical pulse> has a <Gaussian function> envelope and no <chirp>. Its carrier <angular frequency> is $\omega_0$, and its <light intensity> <full width at half maximum> is $2\sqrt{2\ln2}/b$. Successive envelope maxima have different <carrier-envelope phases>, consistent with <1/b>.

**Approximation in the printed assertion.** A discrete <optical frequency comb> gives a train, not one exact isolated <Gaussian pulse>. Applying the <Poisson summation formula> to the ideal sum over all <integers> instead gives
$$
E(t)=\frac{A\sqrt\pi b}{\Delta\omega}
\sum_{j\in\mathbb Z}e^{-b^2(t-jT)^2/4}
 e^{-i\omega_0(t-jT)}e^{i\phi_0-i\omega_{\rm ce}jT}.
$$
Near $t_m$, the $j=m$ term dominates when $bT\gg1$. The other terms are exponentially small but not identically zero. A single <Gaussian function> used globally would decay between $0$ and $T$, whereas the exact <light intensity> has equal maxima there; this supplies a concrete reason the isolated form cannot be an exact global identity. The extension to negative <frequencies> is the usual narrow-positive-band analytic-field approximation when $\omega_0\gg b$.

In <second-harmonic generation>, the quadratic <nonlinear polarization> contains products of pairs of comb components. Their <angular frequencies> are
$$
\omega_p+\omega_q=2\omega_{\rm ce}+(p+q)\Delta\omega,
$$
including $2\omega_p$ when $p=q$. Original comb components have <angular frequencies> $\omega_r=\omega_{\rm ce}+r\Delta\omega$, hence
$$
(\omega_p+\omega_q)-\omega_r
=\omega_{\rm ce}+(p+q-r)\Delta\omega.
$$
When the doubled low-<frequency> region overlaps the original high-<frequency> region, choosing $r=p+q$ gives the <beat> at $|\omega_{\rm ce}|$. More generally the nearest positive <beat> has <angular frequency>
$$
\boxed{\omega_{\rm beat,min}
=\min_{k\in\mathbb Z}|\omega_{\rm ce}+k\Delta\omega|,}
$$
which is $|\omega_{\rm ce}|$ for the stipulated small offset. For $0\leq\omega_{\rm ce}<\Delta\omega$ it is $\min(\omega_{\rm ce},\Delta\omega-\omega_{\rm ce})$, provided the corresponding teeth are in the overlap.

In <comb self-referencing>, overlap the doubled red part and the original blue part on a <photodiode>. The <beat> measures the <carrier-envelope offset frequency>. Feedback on an intracavity dispersion control or pump setting can set $\omega_{\rm ce}=0$ modulo $\Delta\omega$, giving $\boxed{\phi_{\rm slip}=0\pmod{2\pi}}$. A phase-sensitive lock or calibrated approach to zero resolves the sign ambiguity of an unsigned <beat> measurement.<Carrier-Envelope Phase Control of Femtosecond Mode-Locked Lasers and Direct Optical Frequency Synthesis by David J. Jones and others>[2000, comb self-referencing and stabilization of the pulse-to-pulse carrier phase]{ref}

The coherent <optical spectrum> must span an octave, $\omega_{\max}\gtrsim2\omega_{\min}$, with sufficient overlapping spectral strength after <second-harmonic generation>. This calls for very short broadband <optical pulses>, or coherent external spectral broadening. The two branches must preserve <optical coherence>, overlap in time and <polarization>, and have adequate <phase matching>; bandwidth alone does not guarantee a detectable <beat>.

Use the <complex beam parameter> convention
$$
\frac1q=\frac1{R_b}-i\frac{\lambda}{\pi w^2},\qquad q_{\rm waist}=iz_R.
$$
Here $R_b$ is the <wavefront> curvature, distinguished from the mirror curvature $R$. In an unfolded propagation coordinate, a concave mirror has the <ray transfer matrix> $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\-2/R&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$: a ray at height $x$ changes direction by $-2x/R$. The return to the original plane is therefore
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}1&L\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-2/R&1\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}1&L\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}1-2L/R&2L(1-L/R)\\-2/R&1-2L/R\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The <Gaussian beam> transformation $q'=(Aq+B)/(Cq+D)$ gives
$$
\boxed{q'=\frac{(1-2L/R)iz_R+2L(1-L/R)}{1-2L/R-2iz_R/R}.}
$$
For $R=2L$, $M=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&L\\-1/L&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ and $q'=-L^2/q=iL^2/z_R$. The returning <Gaussian beam> consequently has a <beam waist> at the same plane, with
$$
\boxed{z_R'=L^2/z_R.}
$$
This alone does not make it the same <Gaussian beam>; equal <Rayleigh ranges> require $z_R=L$.

Adding the plane mirror requires a self-consistent <optical resonator> <normal mode>, $q'=q=iz_R$. Since $A=D$, the fixed-point equation reduces to $Cq^2=B$, giving
$$
\boxed{z_R=\sqrt{L(R-L)}=\sqrt{LR(1-L/R)}.}
$$
For a finite stable <Gaussian beam>, $0<L<R$. The same formula remains valid at $R=2L$, where self-consistency selects $z_R=L$. At the geometrical hemispherical limit $R=L$ it predicts $z_R=0$; that marginal boundary has no finite-width stable fundamental <Gaussian beam>. Thus the source's word “hemispherical” is used for the plano-concave family, including the near-hemispherical case below.

Propagate from one <beam waist> to the other, using the <optical lens> <ray transfer matrix>:
$$
M=P(v)L(f)P(u)
=\begin{pmatrix}1-v/f&u+v-uv/f\\-1/f&1-u/f\end{pmatrix}.
$$
<Optical mode matching> requires $q_A=iz_A$ to transform to $q_B=iz_B$. Multiplying the denominator across gives
$$
iz_B(1-u/f-iz_A/f)=(1-v/f)iz_A+u+v-uv/f.
$$
Equating real and imaginary parts and multiplying by $f$ yields
$$
\boxed{v(u-f)+z_Az_B=uf,\qquad
vz_A-z_B(u-f)=z_Af.}
$$
Set $\delta=u-f$. The second equation gives $v=f+(z_B/z_A)\delta$. Substituting this into the first cancels the terms $f\delta$:
$$
\frac{z_B}{z_A}\delta^2+z_Az_B=f^2.
$$
Consequently the two algebraic branches are
$$
\boxed{\delta=\pm\sqrt{\frac{z_A}{z_B}f^2-z_A^2},\qquad
u=f\pm z_A\sqrt{\frac{f^2}{z_Az_B}-1},\qquad
v=f\pm z_B\sqrt{\frac{f^2}{z_Az_B}-1}.}
$$
Equivalently, $u=f+\delta$. The signs in $u$ and $v$ are the same. Real solutions require $\boxed{f^2\geq z_Az_B}$; a converging <optical lens> therefore needs $f\geq\sqrt{z_Az_B}$. At equality $u=v=f$. For the stated arrangement, additionally require $u,v\geq0$ and that the chosen <optical lens> position is accessible outside both <optical resonators>. A negative $v$ is an algebraic virtual-waist solution, not a downstream <beam waist>.

For one electromagnetic <normal mode>, $a$ and $a^\dagger$ are the <annihilation operator> and <creation operator>, with <commutator> $[a,a^\dagger]=I$. Their action on normalized <number states> is
$$
a|n\rangle=\sqrt n\,|n-1\rangle,\qquad
a^\dagger|n\rangle=\sqrt{n+1}\,|n+1\rangle.
$$
They remove or add one <photon>, whose <energy> is $\hbar\omega$. The <vacuum state> $|0\rangle$ is normalized and obeys $a|0\rangle=0$. It has no <photons>, but retains <zero-point energy> $\hbar\omega/2$ for the <quantum harmonic oscillator> <Hamiltonian> $H=\hbar\omega(a^\dagger a+1/2)$. The <coherent state> $|\alpha\rangle=D(\alpha)|0\rangle$ is a displaced <vacuum state>.

Set $A=\alpha a^\dagger$ and $B=-\alpha^*a$. Their <commutator> is
$$
[A,B]=- |\alpha|^2[a^\dagger,a]=|\alpha|^2I.
$$
It commutes with both operators, so the stated <Baker--Campbell--Hausdorff formula> terminates:
$$
\boxed{D(\alpha)=e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}e^{\alpha a^\dagger}e^{-\alpha^*a}.}
$$
In particular $e^{-\alpha^*a}|0\rangle=|0\rangle$. Expanding the remaining exponential and using $(a^\dagger)^n|0\rangle=\sqrt{n!}|n\rangle$ gives the <number state> expansion
$$
\boxed{|\alpha\rangle=e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{\alpha^n}{\sqrt{n!}}|n\rangle.}
$$
The orthonormality of the <number states> gives
$$
\boxed{P_n=e^{-|\alpha|^2}\frac{|\alpha|^{2n}}{n!}.}
$$
This is a <Poisson distribution> with parameter $\mu=|\alpha|^2$. Its probabilities sum to $e^{-\mu}e^\mu=1$. The phase of $\alpha$ affects the <coherent state>'s relative <probability amplitudes> but not its <photon number> probabilities. At $\alpha=0$ the distribution is concentrated at $n=0$.

The <number operator> is $\hat N=a^\dagger a$. The <number state> expansion gives $a|\alpha\rangle=\alpha|\alpha\rangle$ by shifting the summation index; taking the adjoint gives $\langle\alpha|a^\dagger=\alpha^*\langle\alpha|$. Thus the <expectation value> is
$$
\overline N=\langle a^\dagger a\rangle=|\alpha|^2=\mu.
$$
For the second moment, use $aa^\dagger=a^\dagger a+I$:
$$
\hat N^2=a^\dagger aa^\dagger a=(a^\dagger)^2a^2+a^\dagger a.
$$
Its <expectation value> in the <coherent state> is $\langle\hat N^2\rangle=|\alpha|^4+|\alpha|^2$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\overline N=|\alpha|^2,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(N)=\langle\hat N^2\rangle-\overline N^2=|\alpha|^2,\qquad
\sigma_N=|\alpha|.}
$$
The <variance> equals the mean, exactly as for the <Poisson distribution> found in <3/b>. For $\mu>0$ the fractional <standard deviation> is $1/\sqrt\mu$, the usual <photon shot noise> scaling. For the <vacuum state>, both the mean <photon number> and its <variance> vanish; this does not imply zero <optical quadrature> fluctuations. At $\mu=0$ the fractional fluctuation is undefined.

For the normalized <coherent state>, $\langle a\rangle=\alpha$ and $\langle a^\dagger\rangle=\alpha^*$. The dimensionless <electric field> and <magnetic field> <optical quadratures> consequently have <expectation values>
$$
\boxed{\langle\mathcal E\rangle=\alpha+\alpha^*=2\operatorname{Re}\alpha,\qquad
\langle\mathcal B\rangle=-i(\alpha-\alpha^*)=2\operatorname{Im}\alpha.}
$$
The physical <electric field> and <magnetic field> strengths have the corresponding proportionality factors and mode functions. In free evolution replace $\alpha$ by $\alpha e^{-i\omega t}$ to recover the classical oscillation.

Expanding the squared operators cancels the $a^2$ and $(a^\dagger)^2$ terms:
$$
\mathcal E^2+\mathcal B^2=2(aa^\dagger+a^\dagger a)=4\hat N+2I.
$$
On the other hand, $(\alpha+\alpha^*)^2+[-i(\alpha-\alpha^*)]^2=4|\alpha|^2$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\langle\mathcal E^2+\mathcal B^2\rangle=4|\alpha|^2+2,\qquad
\langle\mathcal E\rangle^2+\langle\mathcal B\rangle^2=4|\alpha|^2.}
$$
The difference is the sum of the <variances>, not an extra mean field. Indeed $\operatorname{Var}(\mathcal E)=\operatorname{Var}(\mathcal B)=1$, independent of $\alpha$. The <commutator> $[\mathcal E,\mathcal B]=2iI$ requires $\sigma_{\mathcal E}\sigma_{\mathcal B}\geq1$, and the <coherent state> attains equality. The residual two units are the <vacuum fluctuations> of the two <optical quadratures>.

<Zero-point fields> are the irreducible <electromagnetic field> fluctuations in the <vacuum state>. Each <normal mode> has ground <energy> $\hbar\omega/2$. Although $\langle\mathcal E\rangle=\langle\mathcal B\rangle=0$ in the <vacuum state>, <3/d> gives $\langle\mathcal E^2\rangle=\langle\mathcal B^2\rangle=1$. These are quantum fluctuations, not a classical random coherent field with a known instantaneous value.

Two consequences are the <Lamb shift>, where coupling to the quantized <electromagnetic field> changes atomic <energy levels>, and <spontaneous emission>, where an excited <chemical atom> can emit a <photon> into an initially empty <normal mode>. In the latter process the <creation operator> has a nonzero <matrix element> $\langle1|a^\dagger|0\rangle=1$; stimulated-plus-spontaneous factors have the form $n+1$, which does not vanish at $n=0$. Descriptions splitting <spontaneous emission> into <vacuum fluctuations> and radiation reaction depend on operator-ordering conventions; the measurable total rate does not. <Zero-point fields> do not mean that the <vacuum state> contains a nonzero mean <photon number>.

The five <DiVincenzo criteria> are: a scalable collection of well-characterized <qubits>; preparation in a known fiducial <quantum state>; relevant coherence times long compared with <quantum gate> times; a <universal quantum gate set>; and qubit-specific <quantum measurement>. The criterion is useful control of the whole computation, including errors, rather than merely observing two resolved levels.<The Physical Implementation of Quantum Computation by David P. DiVincenzo>[Section III, numbered requirements 1–5; Section IV's two communication requirements are additional]{ref}

For **trapped ions**, the implementation is:

* **Scalable characterized <qubits>.** Two long-lived <hyperfine structure> levels or an optical ground/metastable pair in each <ion> encode $|0\rangle,|1\rangle$. An <ion trap> confines individually addressed <ions>. Extending a chain adds <qubits>, but increasing motional complexity, addressing errors and heating limit straightforward scaling; segmented traps and transport can help.
* **Initialization.** <Optical pumping> prepares the internal <quantum state>. <Doppler cooling> and, where required, <resolved sideband cooling> prepare the shared motion used for <quantum gates>.
* **Coherence.** Long-lived internal levels give coherence much longer than controlled pulse times. Magnetic noise, laser noise, scattering and motional heating cause <quantum decoherence>; suitable clock-state encodings and control reduce these errors.
* **Universal control.** Resonant optical, microwave or <Raman transitions> give arbitrary one-<qubit> rotations. Shared motional <normal modes>, coupled through the <Coulomb force>, mediate entangling <two-qubit gates>. Together these supply a <universal quantum gate set>.
* **Readout.** State-selective cycling <fluorescence>, often with <electron shelving>, produces a bright/dark signal resolved for individual <ions>.

For **trapped neutral atoms**, the corresponding implementation is:

* **Scalable characterized <qubits>.** Long-lived ground-state <hyperfine structure> levels encode <qubits> in an <optical lattice> or an array of <optical tweezers>. Large arrays are feasible, but random loading, vacancies, atom loss and individual addressing must be controlled.
* **Initialization.** Cooling plus <optical pumping> prepares the internal <quantum state>. Filling or rearranging traps prepares a usable register; motional cooling is needed when the chosen <quantum gate> depends on motion.
* **Coherence.** Weak environmental coupling of neutral ground-state <chemical atoms> allows long coherence. Trap-light scattering, differential light shifts and magnetic noise limit it; switching traps or choosing suitable encodings can reduce these effects.
* **Universal control.** Microwave or <Raman transitions> implement one-<qubit> rotations. Ground-state <chemical atoms> do not have the ions' long-range <Coulomb force>, so that motional-bus construction cannot simply be copied. Controlled collisions or <Rydberg blockade> can nevertheless supply entangling <two-qubit gates>.
* **Readout.** State-dependent <fluorescence> or state-selective removal followed by imaging identifies a chosen <qubit>. Optical resolution, cross-talk and atom loss constrain repeated measurements.

Thus neither platform is forbidden from meeting a criterion in principle. Weak neutral-ground-state interactions make entangling control harder, but claiming that trapped neutral atoms cannot implement a <universal quantum gate set> would be false. Gate-model <quantum algorithms> using <Rydberg blockade> had already been demonstrated in a programmable neutral-atom processor by 2022.<Multi-qubit entanglement and algorithms on a neutral-atom quantum computer by T. M. Graham and others>[Nature 604, 457–462 (2022), abstract and controlled-phase gate implementation]{ref}

Let $x\in\{00,01,10,11\}$ and let $f(x)$ be one only at the marked input. A three-<qubit> implementation of <Grover's algorithm> starts with $|00\rangle|1\rangle$, applies a <Hadamard gate> to each <qubit>, queries the reversible <quantum oracle> $U_f:|x,y\rangle\mapsto|x,y\mathbin\oplus f(x)\rangle$, applies the <Grover diffusion operator> $D=2|s\rangle\langle s|-I$ to the first two <qubits>, and measures those two <qubits> in the <computational basis>. Here $|s\rangle=\tfrac12\sum_x|x\rangle$. The full three-wire <quantum circuit> is the composition
$$
\boxed{|00\rangle|1\rangle
\xrightarrow{H^{\otimes3}}|s\rangle|-\rangle
\xrightarrow{U_f}(O_f|s\rangle)|-\rangle
\xrightarrow{D\otimes I}|x_*\rangle|-\rangle
\xrightarrow{\text{measure first two}}x_*.}
$$
\Image[2022-grover-search-circuits.png]
{title=Three- and two-qubit implementations of <Grover's algorithm> for four inputs}

Here $|-\rangle=(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$. Since $X|-\rangle=-|-\rangle$, <phase kickback> gives $U_f(|x\rangle|-\rangle)=(-1)^{f(x)}|x\rangle|-\rangle$. The third <qubit> remains separable and unchanged. A direct <marked-state phase oracle> $O_f|x\rangle=(-1)^{f(x)}|x\rangle$ therefore replaces the three-<qubit> query using only two <qubits>:
$$
\boxed{|00\rangle\xrightarrow{H\otimes H}|s\rangle
\xrightarrow{O_f}O_f|s\rangle\xrightarrow{D}|x_*\rangle
\xrightarrow{\text{measure}}x_*.}
$$
For the explicit choice $f(x)=1$ only at $x=11$, $O_f$ is a <controlled-Z gate>. Immediately after it,
$$
O_f|s\rangle=\tfrac12(|00\rangle+|01\rangle+|10\rangle-|11\rangle).
$$
The mean of these four <probability amplitudes> is $\overline a=1/4$. The <Grover diffusion operator> maps each <probability amplitude> $a_x$ to $2\overline a-a_x$. The three unmarked <probability amplitudes> become zero and the marked one becomes one. A single query consequently finds $11$ with <probability> one.

A gate-level implementation of $D$ is $H^{\otimes2}(2|00\rangle\langle00|-I)H^{\otimes2}$. Up to an irrelevant <global phase>, it is $H^{\otimes2}X^{\otimes2}\,\mathrm{CZ}\,X^{\otimes2}H^{\otimes2}$. For any other chosen marked word, conjugate the <controlled-Z gate> by <Pauli X gates> on precisely the positions where the marked word has a zero.

In <liquid-state NMR quantum computing>, use two distinguishable spin-$1/2$ <atomic nuclei> in one molecule as the two <qubits>. Their different resonance <frequencies> permit selective radiofrequency pulses for one-<qubit> rotations. $$
H_J=2\pi\hbar J I_zS_z=\frac{\pi\hbar J}{2}Z_1Z_2.
$$
Assuming $J>0$, free evolution for $t=1/(2J)$ implements $U_J=e^{-i\pi Z_1Z_2/4}$. Define $R_z(\theta)=e^{-i\theta Z/2}$. Then

[R_z(-\pi/2)\otimes R_z(-\pi/2)]U_J.}

\begin{aligned}
|\Psi_1\rangle&=\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}|\phi\rangle,\\
|\Psi_2\rangle&=\frac{|0\rangle|\phi\rangle+|1\rangle U|\phi\rangle}{\sqrt2},\\
|\Psi_3\rangle&=\frac12\left[|0\rangle(I+U)|\phi\rangle+|1\rangle(I-U)|\phi\rangle\right].
\end{aligned}
$$
Apply <5/b> to the last line. Since $U^\dagger U=I$ by <unitarity>,
$$
\begin{aligned}
P_0&=\tfrac14\langle\phi|(I+U^\dagger)(I+U)|\phi\rangle
=\tfrac12\left[1+\operatorname{Re}\langle\phi|U|\phi\rangle\right],\\
P_1&=\tfrac14\langle\phi|(I-U^\dagger)(I-U)|\phi\rangle
=\tfrac12\left[1-\operatorname{Re}\langle\phi|U|\phi\rangle\right].
\end{aligned}
$$
For the <Pauli Z gate> observable on the first <qubit>, the two <eigenvalues> are $+1$ and $-1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\langle Z\rangle=P_0-P_1=\operatorname{Re}\langle\phi|U|\phi\rangle.}
$$
As a check, $U=I$ returns outcome zero certainly, while $U=-I$ returns one certainly. The <controlled unitary gate> makes this relative <phase> measurable even though multiplying an isolated system's <quantum state> by $-1$ is only a <global phase>.

Starting at port 1, the first <beam splitter> encounter sends amplitude $c$ straight out through port 0 and stores amplitude $-c$ towards M2. A return from M2 enters port $1'$; it sends the stored amplitude times $-c$ out through port 1 and times $c$ towards M1. A return from M1 enters port $0'$; it sends amplitude times $c$ out through port 0 and times $c$ towards M2. Both internal continuations carry $+c$, so two additional encounters multiply the surviving amplitude by $+c^2$.

With the fixed mirror and propagation phases absorbed as in <6/a>, the nonzero coefficients of the outgoing <single-photon state> are

\boxed{\begin{aligned}

c_{0,2j+1}&=-2^{-(2j+1)/2},&&j=1,2,3,\ldots,\\
c_{1,2j}&=2^{-j},&&j=1,2,3,\ldots,

and $c_{0,2j}=c_{1,2j+1}=0$. The first nonzero terms are $c_{0,1}=1/\sqrt2$, $c_{1,2}=1/2$, $c_{0,3}=-1/(2\sqrt2)$ and $c_{1,4}=1/4$. Distinct exit events are orthogonal because they differ in output port or arrival-time bin. This requires the incident wavepacket to resolve the delay. Summing the squared moduli as <geometric series> gives
$$
\boxed{P(0)=\sum_{j=0}^\infty2^{-(2j+1)}=\frac23,\qquad
P(1)=\sum_{j=1}^\infty2^{-2j}=\frac13.}
$$
Their sum is one. After $n$ encounters the probability of still being inside is $2^{-n}$, which tends to zero, so no trapped component is omitted.

The source drawing places the delay line in the M2 arm. A visit to M2 crosses that line in both directions. If $\tau$ is its **one-way** delay, then an exit after $n$ encounters has crossed it $2\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ times:

The two paths selected by D0's time gate are $L$ on the outward journey followed by $S$ on the return, and $S$ outward followed by $L$ on the return. They have the same total delay and fixed propagation phase, so their <probability amplitudes> interfere.

For the first history, Alice applies $e^{i\phi_A}$ to the late component, while Bob's return-time modulation is not encountered in the return delay arm. For the second, Alice's early component is unmodulated and Bob applies $e^{i\phi_B}$ in the return delay arm. Bob must time his modulation to the return passage, leaving the outward reference preparation unchanged; this is implicit in the stated encoding. Each enabled modulator is traversed twice, so its $\pi/2$ single-pass <phase> becomes the stipulated $\pi$.

Include the amplitude $c$ to enter BS1 through BS2, and the amplitude of modulus $c$ to reach D0 through BS2 on return. Both histories therefore contain six 50:50 <beam splitter> factors. Ignoring one common unobservable <global phase>,
$$
\mathcal A_{D0}=-c^6(e^{i\phi_A}+e^{i\phi_B})
=-\frac18(e^{i\phi_A}+e^{i\phi_B}).
$$

$$
\boxed{P(D0\mid\phi_A,\phi_B)
=\frac{1+\cos(\phi_A-\phi_B)}{32}
=\frac1{16}\cos^2\!\frac{\phi_A-\phi_B}{2}.}

For the allowed values $0,\pi$, this is $1/16$ when $\phi_A=\phi_B$ and zero otherwise. Encode $0$ by phase zero and $1$ by phase $\pi$. Alice and Bob choose their bits independently and keep their choices private. Bob announces the trial numbers with a D0 click over an authenticated public channel. For each retained trial, <6/c> guarantees that their bits agree. In the ideal symmetric setup, the two equal-phase cases $00$ and $11$ give the same click probability, so announcing success does not itself reveal which shared bit was used.

The matching-phase cases occur with <probability> $1/2$, and a matching case succeeds with <probability> $1/16$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{P(\text{retained trial})=\frac1{32},\qquad
\mathbb E[K_{\rm raw}]=\frac N{32}.}
$$
For independent ideal trials, the raw-key length has a <binomial distribution> with <standard deviation> $\sqrt{31N}/32$. These are bits before sacrificing test positions or performing <privacy amplification>.

A missing click does **not** certify unequal phases: the same phases also fail to click with <probability> $15/16$. With uniform choices,
$$
P(\phi_A=\phi_B\mid\text{no click})=\frac{15}{31},\qquad
P(\phi_A\ne\phi_B\mid\text{no click})=\frac{16}{31}.
$$
Thus a no-click trial supplies neither a known equal bit nor a known complemented bit. This is why it cannot be retained by the direct error-free sifting rule. The source's “cannot use” should be understood in this sense: the events are not statistically independent, so an absolute claim that they contain no information would be false.

To obtain a secret final key, reveal and discard a random subset of retained bits to test the observed error rate, then apply reconciliation and <privacy amplification> as required by a security analysis of the actual implementation. Resolving which time path a <photon> took removes the accepted histories' <quantum coherence> and can turn destructive <interference> into false D0 clicks, providing a simple disturbance test. Equal-phase click correlations alone are not a proof of security against every attack, particularly in the presence of losses and device imperfections. The supplied ideal model fixes the sifted rate $N/32$; it does not fix a positive secure final length without an adversary model and observed parameters.
