== 1
{scope}

=== Solution

Treat $|a\rangle=|0\rangle$ and $|b\rangle=|1\rangle$ as an isolated <ground-state hyperfine qubit>. Their transition angular frequency is $\omega_0=2\pi\times320.7\,\mathrm{MHz}$. The radiofrequency <magnetic field> couples them through an allowed <magnetic dipole transition>. After choosing its phase reference, take the two-level laboratory <Hamiltonian operator> to be
$$
H(t)=-\frac{\hbar\omega_0}{2}Z+\hbar\Omega\cos(\omega t)X,
$$
where $X,Z$ are <Pauli operators>. Here $\Omega$ is defined by the on-resonance rotating-frame off-diagonal matrix element $\hbar\Omega/2$.

Use a <quantum rotating frame> and the <rotating-wave approximation>, valid for $\Omega,|\delta|\ll\omega_0$, with <Rabi detuning> $\delta=\omega_0-\omega$. Ignoring off-resonant coupling to other levels gives
$$
H_R=\frac\hbar2(\Omega X-\delta Z).
$$
At resonance $\delta=0$, the <unitary time evolution> is
$$
U_R(t)=\cos\frac{\Omega t}{2}I-i\sin\frac{\Omega t}{2}X,
\qquad
|\psi_R(t)\rangle=\cos\frac{\Omega t}{2}|a\rangle-i\sin\frac{\Omega t}{2}|b\rangle.
$$
Thus the <Born rule> gives coherent <Rabi oscillations>,
$$
\boxed{P_a(t)=\cos^2\frac{\Omega t}{2},\qquad P_b(t)=\sin^2\frac{\Omega t}{2}.}
$$
The stated amplitudes are in the rotating frame; the laboratory carrier phases are tracked by the drive reference.

These logical states are orthogonal, individually resolvable <hyperfine levels> in the same electronic <ground state>. They avoid fast optical decay, can be coherently coupled by radiofrequency control, and have a state-selective optical readout through $|c\rangle$. The excited level $|c\rangle$ is useful for <fluorescence> detection rather than for the storage superposition in this model. Long lifetimes alone do not guarantee a useful qubit: control isolation and adequate <quantum coherence> are also required.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

A resonant <Rabi pi pulse> satisfies $\Omega T_\pi=\pi$. The supplied $T_\pi=0.256\,\mathrm s$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\Omega=\frac\pi{0.256\,\mathrm s}\simeq12.27\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}},\qquad
\frac\Omega{2\pi}=1.953125\,\mathrm{Hz}.}
$$
The ideal rotating-frame operation is $-iX$, a <Pauli X gate> up to <global phase>.

Both logical states belong to the electronic ground level. A fast <electric dipole transition> between them is forbidden, and spontaneous radiative relaxation at a hyperfine radiofrequency is weak. The optical readout field is off during the coherent pulse. Neglecting relaxation is therefore consistent with a population lifetime much longer than $0.256\,\mathrm s$. Magnetic noise, collisions and technical dephasing are additional limitations; the ideal coherent calculation assumes their effects are small over this interval.

At detuning $\delta$, define $\Omega_{\rm eff}=\sqrt{\Omega^2+\delta^2}$. The same two-level <Hamiltonian operator> gives
$$
P_b(t)=\frac{\Omega^2}{\Omega^2+\delta^2}
\sin^2\left(\frac{t\sqrt{\Omega^2+\delta^2}}2\right).
$$
For an accurate fixed-duration $\pi$ rotation, the detuning must be small compared with the <Rabi angular frequency>. Writing $\delta\nu=\delta/(2\pi)$,
$$
\boxed{|\delta\nu|\ll\frac\Omega{2\pi}=1.95\,\mathrm{Hz},}
$$
a fractional mismatch much smaller than $6.1\times10^{-9}$ relative to the $320.7\,\mathrm{MHz}$ carrier. This is an accuracy scale, not an assertion that a particular nonzero detuning gives a perfect gate.

The <small-detuning error of a Rabi pi pulse> quantifies a chosen tolerance. For an initial $|a\rangle$ and the resonant pulse duration,
$$
1-P_b(T_\pi)=\left(\frac\delta\Omega\right)^2+O\!\left((\delta/\Omega)^4\right).
$$
A population-transfer error at most $\epsilon$ consequently requires approximately **$|\delta\nu|\lesssim1.95\sqrt\epsilon\,\mathrm{Hz}$**; for example, **about $0.20\,\mathrm{Hz}$ for one percent error**. Full gate calibration also includes pulse area and drive phase, beyond this population-transfer criterion.

== 3
{scope}

=== Solution

In the ideal <state-selective fluorescence detection> model, driving the $a\leftrightarrow c$ optical transition produces a bright record only for $a$, while $b$ is dark. A sufficiently discriminating pulse therefore measures the basis $\{|a\rangle,|b\rangle\}$. The optical cycle is assumed to return population to the appropriate logical state without leakage. Ignoring the recorded outcome removes the off-diagonal <quantum coherence> between $a$ and $b$.

Take $n$ equally spaced measurements at $t_j=jT_\pi/n$, including the final time. Between checks the resonant drive rotates through angle $\Omega T_\pi/n=\pi/n$. The probability of changing basis state in one interval is
$$
p=\sin^2\frac\pi{2n}.
$$
After each ideal <projective measurement>, the populations therefore follow the symmetric two-state <Markov chain>
$$
\begin{pmatrix}P_a'\\P_b'\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}1-p&p\\p&1-p\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}P_a\\P_b\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The population difference is multiplied by $1-2p=\cos(\pi/n)$ at each step. Starting in $a$, the <nonselective two-level Zeno dynamics> gives
$$
\boxed{P_b(T_\pi)=\frac{1-\cos^n(\pi/n)}2
=\frac{\pi^2}{4n}+O(n^{-2})\longrightarrow0.}
$$
For $n=64$, the exact final population is **$P_b\simeq0.03712$**, while the requested large-$n$ estimate is **$\pi^2/256\simeq0.03855$**, about **four percent**. Thus the transition that an uninterrupted pi pulse would complete is strongly inhibited.

If “leaving” instead means departing from $a$ at least once in the complete measurement record, use the <repeated projective-measurement survival probability>. The probability that every check returns $a$ is
$$
P_{\rm all\,a}=\cos^{2n}\frac\pi{2n},\qquad
1-P_{\rm all\,a}\simeq0.03782\quad(n=64).
$$
It has the same leading estimate $\pi^2/(4n)$, but is not exactly the final $b$ population: a trajectory can leave $a$ and subsequently return. Keeping these events separate also distinguishes postselected records from an experiment that measures only final populations.

The mechanism is the <quadratic short-time quantum survival probability>: each short interval has transition probability of order $(T_\pi/n)^2$, so $n$ checks leave only an order-$1/n$ total transition probability. The <quantum Zeno effect> here requires effective, sufficiently fast measurements; a finite-duration optical probe is a physical interaction whose imperfections must be included when comparing with data.

The <beryllium-ion quantum Zeno experiment of 1990> used the same ground-state transition and a finite-duration optical probing sequence, followed by a calibrated ensemble <fluorescence> readout. Its measured final populations motivate the nonselective model; the instantaneous projections here idealize those physical pulses.
