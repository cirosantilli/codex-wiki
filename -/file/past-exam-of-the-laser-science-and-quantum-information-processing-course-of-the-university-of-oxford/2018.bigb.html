== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Write the optical system's spectral response as $A(\omega)e^{i\phi(\omega)}$. Expanding its <spectral phase> around the pulse's central <angular frequency> gives
$$
\phi(\omega)=\phi(\omega_0)+\phi^{(1)}(\omega_0)(\omega-\omega_0)+\frac12\phi^{(2)}(\omega_0)(\omega-\omega_0)^2+\cdots.
$$
The <group delay> is $\phi^{(1)}=d\phi/d\omega$, with units of time. The linear <spectral phase> term delays the pulse envelope by this amount. The <group delay dispersion> is $\phi^{(2)}=d^2\phi/d\omega^2$, with units of time squared. It makes the <group delay> <frequency> dependent: $t_g(\omega)\simeq\phi^{(1)}+\phi^{(2)}(\omega-\omega_0)$. Positive <group delay dispersion> delays higher <frequencies> more and gives positive <chirp>. A nonzero quadratic <spectral phase> broadens an initially transform-limited pulse; applied to an already chirped pulse, a suitable opposite sign can compress it.

In <chirped pulse amplification>, an ultrashort seed pulse first passes through a dispersive stretcher, often a grating-based system. This correlates <frequency> with arrival time and increases pulse duration while approximately preserving pulse <energy>, thereby lowering peak <intensity>. An optical amplifier can then increase pulse <energy> without exposing the gain medium to the original short pulse's enormous peak <intensity>, reducing optical damage and unwanted nonlinear <phase> accumulation. A final dispersive compressor, typically using gratings, cancels the accumulated <spectral phase> to recover a short, high-energy pulse. The essential components are therefore a short-pulse source, stretcher, amplifier and matched compressor; spectral gain bandwidth and higher-order <dispersion> limit the achievable compression.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The temporal <intensity> is proportional to the squared field modulus:
$$
I(t)=I_0e^{-2at^2}.
$$
Its half-maximum points satisfy $2at^2=\ln2$, so the <intensity> <full width at half maximum> is
$$
\boxed{\tau=\sqrt{\frac{2\ln2}{a}}.}
$$
With the chosen $e^{-i\omega t}$ convention, the instantaneous <angular frequency> is minus the time derivative of the field's argument. Hence
$$
\boxed{\omega(t)=\omega_0+2bt.}
$$
A localized Gaussian requires $\operatorname{Re}\Gamma=a>0$. It is positively chirped precisely when $d\omega/dt=2\operatorname{Im}\Gamma>0$, or $b>0$; $b=0$ gives no chirp and $b<0$ gives negative chirp.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $d=\delta t$. At the port that is bright for zero delay, the ideal 50:50 <Michelson interferometer> gives
$$
E_{\mathrm{out}}(t)=\frac12[E(t)+E(t-d)].
$$
Each arm has field <amplitude> one half after two beam-splitter interactions. For $|d|\ll\tau$, approximate both real Gaussian envelopes by $E_0e^{-at^2}$ while retaining their rapidly varying <phases>. Put $A=\omega_0t+bt^2$ and $B=\omega_0(t-d)+b(t-d)^2$. The identity $(e^{-iA}+e^{-iB})/2=e^{-i(A+B)/2}\cos[(A-B)/2]$ gives
$$
\boxed{E_{\mathrm{out}}(t)\simeq E_0e^{-at^2}\cos\!\left[bd\left(t-\frac d2+\frac{\omega_0}{2b}\right)\right]e^{-i[\omega_0t+\psi(t)]},}
$$
where
$$
\boxed{\psi(t)=bt^2-bdt+\frac{bd^2}{2}-\frac{\omega_0d}{2}.}
$$
A delay-dependent constant overall <phase> can be shifted by a port convention, but the expression above follows directly from the specified bright-port sum.

The output <intensity> is a Gaussian envelope modulated by a squared cosine:
$$
I_{\mathrm{out}}(t)\simeq I_0e^{-2at^2}\cos^2\!\left[bd(t-d/2)+\omega_0d/2\right].
$$
The modulation maxima are separated by
$$
\boxed{\Delta t\simeq\frac{\pi}{b|d|}.}
$$
This is the spacing of resolved adjacent pulse peaks when the envelope changes little over a modulation period, requiring $b|d|\tau\gg1$. The source's assumption $|d|\ll\tau$ alone does not ensure several visible peaks: a weakly chirped pulse may show only one broad maximum. At $d=0$ the output is the original pulse and the modulation-spacing formula has no finite-peak interpretation. The figure compares the exact bright-port field sum with the small-delay approximation.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

A time delay $d$ multiplies the spectral field by $e^{i\omega d}$. The bright-port <transfer function> is therefore
$$
H(\omega)=\frac{1+e^{i\omega d}}2=e^{i\omega d/2}\cos\frac{\omega d}{2},\qquad
|H(\omega)|^2=\cos^2\frac{\omega d}{2}.
$$
The spectral transmission maxima have <angular frequency> spacing $\Delta\omega=2\pi/|d|$. For a strongly chirped pulse the local frequency-time mapping is $\omega(t)=\omega_0+2bt$. Thus the filter's adjacent transmitted <frequency> bands emerge at times separated by
$$
\boxed{\Delta t=\frac{\Delta\omega}{d\omega/dt}=\frac{\pi}{b|d|},}
$$
as obtained directly from the temporal fields. The spectral <transfer function> itself is exact; mapping its features directly onto time requires a sufficiently strong chirp and overlapping envelopes.

As $|d|$ approaches the pulse duration, the two envelopes overlap only partly and the small-delay formula fails. For $|d|\gg\tau$, the selected port contains two separate replicas of field <amplitude> $E_0/2$, each with one quarter of the input pulse <energy>. Their combined <energy> is half the input; the other output port carries the remainder. There is no temporal overlap or corresponding time-resolved <interference>. A sufficiently resolving spectrometer can still reveal fine spectral fringes, whose spacing decreases as $1/|d|$.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Represent a paraxial ray by its height and forward slope $(x,\theta)^{\mathsf T}$, with $\theta=dx/dz$. Free-space propagation leaves the slope unchanged and increases height by $L\theta$, giving
$$
\boxed{P(L)=\begin{pmatrix}1&L\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
A <thin lens> leaves the ray height unchanged. A parallel incident ray at height $x$ must emerge toward the focal point, so its slope changes by $-x/f$. By linearity, the same increment applies to any paraxial incident slope:
$$
\boxed{F(f)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-1/f&1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
These <ray transfer matrices> have determinant one in air and compose in reverse <matrix> order to the physical order of traversal.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the forward-slope convention established in part a, with $x'=Ax+B\theta$ and $\theta'=Cx+D\theta$, where $AD-BC=1$. Assume $B\ne0$. Solve for the endpoint slopes:
$$
\theta=\frac{x'-Ax}{B},\qquad \theta'=\frac{Dx'-x}{B}.
$$
<Fermat's principle> makes the optical path stationary against variations at internal interfaces. Consequently only endpoint terms survive when its endpoints change. For optical path $l(x,x')$, these are
$$
\frac{\partial l}{\partial x}=-\theta=\frac{Ax-x'}B,\qquad
\frac{\partial l}{\partial x'}=\theta'=\frac{Dx'-x}B.
$$
Integrate the first equation with respect to $x$, then match the second and impose $l(0,0)=L_0$. The resulting <ABCD optical path generating function> is
$$
\boxed{l(x,x')=L_0+\frac{Ax^2+Dx'^2-2xx'}{2B}.}
$$
Thus the geometrical kernel defined in the source would have the form
$$
\boxed{K(x,x')=\frac{\exp\!\left\{ik\left[L_0+(Ax^2+Dx'^2-2xx')/(2B)\right]\right\}}{L_0+(Ax^2+Dx'^2-2xx')/(2B)}.}
$$
**The printed minus sign is inconsistent with forward propagation under these conventions.** A concrete counterexample is free space: $A=D=1$, $B=L>0$, $C=0$. Its true geometrical path is
$$
\sqrt{L^2+(x'-x)^2}=L+\frac{(x'-x)^2}{2L}+O\!\left((x'-x)^4/L^3\right),
$$
whereas the printed expression predicts a path shorter than $L$ for nonzero transverse separation. For example, $L=1\,\mathrm m$, $x=0$, $x'=1\,\mathrm{mm}$ gives $1\,\mathrm m+0.5\,\mu\mathrm m$, rather than the printed $1\,\mathrm m-0.5\,\mu\mathrm m$. Reversing the slope convention would also reverse the free-space $B$ entry and must be done consistently; it cannot justify the minus sign with $P(L)$ from part a.

The printed form can be recovered by explicitly using the opposite slope coordinate $\widetilde\theta=-dx/dz$. Then $\widetilde B=-B$, free space has $\widetilde B=-L$, and the same physical path is $l=L_0-(Ax^2+Dx'^2-2xx')/(2\widetilde B)$. Thus the sign can be a convention choice, but the printed minus sign must not be combined with the positive-$B$ free-space <ray transfer matrix> used here.

For physical field propagation, the paraxial <diffraction> kernel retains this quadratic optical-path <phase> while using the leading spreading <amplitude>. In one transverse dimension the normalized <linear canonical transform> is
$$
E(x')=\frac{e^{ikL_0}}{\sqrt{i\lambda B}}\int\exp\!\left[\frac{ik}{2B}(Ax^2+Dx'^2-2xx')\right]E(x)\,dx,
$$
with a consistent square-root branch and $\lambda=2\pi/k$. The source's prefactor and $1/l$ kernel are a schematic one-dimensional version of the <Fresnel-Kirchhoff diffraction integral>, not this normalized general-system propagator. The quadratic denominator is normally replaced by its leading value when making the Fresnel approximation. When $B=0$, propagation is an imaging limit rather than an ordinary integral with division by $B$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For the shown sequence of free space, <thin lens>, and free space, multiply the <ray transfer matrices> in traversal order:
$$
M=P(L_2)F(f)P(L_1)=\begin{pmatrix}
1-L_2/f&L_1+L_2-L_1L_2/f\\-1/f&1-L_1/f
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
To obtain an ordinary <Fourier transform> of an arbitrary input field, neither an input quadratic <phase> nor an output quadratic <phase> may remain. Thus $A=D=0$, requiring
$$
\boxed{L_1=L_2=f,\qquad M=\begin{pmatrix}0&f\\-1/f&0\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Using the physically consistent kernel derived in part b gives
$$
\boxed{E(x')=\frac{e^{ikL_0}}{\sqrt{i\lambda f}}\int E(x)e^{-i2\pi xx'/(\lambda f)}\,dx
=\frac{e^{ikL_0}}{\sqrt{i\lambda f}}\widehat E\!\left(\frac{x'}{\lambda f}\right).}
$$
The spatial Fourier coordinate is $x'/(\lambda f)$. If the source's schematic integral normalization is retained instead, set its slowly varying denominator to $L_0$ to obtain the same <Fourier transform> with overall prefactor $ik e^{ikL_0}/(2\pi L_0)$. Keeping the source's incorrect quadratic sign would reverse the Fourier coordinate. These overall-normalization conventions do not alter the focal-plane placement, but an unapproximated transverse-dependent denominator would not give an exact <Fourier transform>.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Take $\sigma_z|e\rangle=|e\rangle$ and $\sigma_z|g\rangle=-|g\rangle$. In the resonant <Jaynes-Cummings model>, the interaction connects only $|e,n\rangle$ and $|g,n+1\rangle$. Both have free <energy> $\hbar\omega(n+1/2)$, and their interaction <matrix> is $\hbar g\sqrt{n+1}\,\sigma_x$. Therefore
$$
H_n=\hbar\omega(n+1/2)I+\hbar g\sqrt{n+1}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Exponentiating this two-state block and applying it to $|e,n\rangle$ yields
$$
\boxed{|\psi_n(t)\rangle=e^{-i\omega(n+1/2)t}\left[\cos(g\sqrt{n+1}\,t)|e,n\rangle-i\sin(g\sqrt{n+1}\,t)|g,n+1\rangle\right].}
$$
The populations undergo <Rabi oscillations> at <angular frequency> $2g\sqrt{n+1}$. The common <phase> in this fixed-$n$ block does not affect its <probabilities>, but it must be retained when blocks with different <photon numbers> are superposed.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Let the initial field be the normalized <coherent state> $|z\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n|n\rangle$, where $c_n=e^{-|z|^2/2}z^n/\sqrt{n!}$. The atom is again initially excited. Linearity of the <Schrödinger equation> gives
$$
\boxed{|\Psi(t)\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^\infty c_ne^{-i\omega(n+1/2)t}\left[\cos(g\sqrt{n+1}\,t)|e,n\rangle-i\sin(g\sqrt{n+1}\,t)|g,n+1\rangle\right].}
$$
The different <photon numbers> have different <Rabi frequencies>, so the state generally becomes entangled between atom and field. It cannot in general be replaced by one Rabi-oscillating atomic state multiplied by an unchanged <coherent state>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Project the field onto the five-photon <number state>. The excited-atom contribution comes from the initial $n=5$ block, while the ground-atom contribution comes from the initial $n=4$ block. The unnormalized conditional atomic state is
$$
|\widetilde\chi_5\rangle=c_5e^{-i11\omega t/2}\cos(g\sqrt6\,t)|e\rangle-i c_4e^{-i9\omega t/2}\sin(g\sqrt5\,t)|g\rangle.
$$
Thus the five-count <probability> and normalized post-measurement state are
$$
\boxed{P_5=|c_5|^2\cos^2(g\sqrt6\,t)+|c_4|^2\sin^2(g\sqrt5\,t),\qquad
|\chi_5\rangle=\frac{|\widetilde\chi_5\rangle}{\sqrt{P_5}}.}
$$
The relative free-evolution <phase> is physically retained; it is absent only in an explicitly rotating atomic frame. The conditional state is defined when $P_5>0$. In particular, an initial vacuum cannot produce five photons in this one-atom model, so that conditioning event has <probability> zero when $z=0$.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The conditional <probability> of the atomic ground state is the squared ground-state <amplitude> divided by the five-photon event <probability>:
$$
\boxed{P(g\mid5)=\frac{|c_4|^2\sin^2(g\sqrt5\,t)}{|c_5|^2\cos^2(g\sqrt6\,t)+|c_4|^2\sin^2(g\sqrt5\,t)}.}
$$
For $z\ne0$, $|c_5|^2=|z|^2|c_4|^2/5$, giving the equivalent expression
$$
\boxed{P(g\mid5)=\frac{5\sin^2(g\sqrt5\,t)}{|z|^2\cos^2(g\sqrt6\,t)+5\sin^2(g\sqrt5\,t)}.}
$$
It applies only when the conditioning denominator is nonzero. The distinct $\sqrt5$ and $\sqrt6$ factors reflect the two different initial photon-number blocks.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

For $\bar n=|z|^2\gg1$, the <Poisson distribution> of <photon number> has width $\sqrt{\bar n}\ll\bar n$. Initially the atom therefore undergoes nearly classical <Rabi oscillations> at <angular frequency> approximately $2g\sqrt{\bar n}$. However, the atomic inversion is a sum over slightly different <frequencies>:
$$
\langle\sigma_z(t)\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^\infty e^{-\bar n}\frac{\bar n^n}{n!}\cos(2g\sqrt{n+1}\,t).
$$
The components dephase, causing collapse of the visible <Rabi oscillations> on a time scale of order $1/g$; a Gaussian photon-number approximation gives an initial envelope approximately $e^{-g^2t^2/2}$. Their discrete <phases> later rephase, giving revivals near
$$
\boxed{t_{\mathrm{rev}}\simeq\frac{2\pi\sqrt{\bar n}}g.}
$$
The revival estimate follows by requiring the <phase> difference of neighbouring photon-number components to advance by $2\pi$. These <Jaynes-Cummings collapse and revival> effects arise from atom-field <entanglement> and photon-number discreteness. Large <amplitude> makes the initial classical description accurate over many Rabi periods, but does not eliminate quantum collapse and revival at all later times.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

Four complementary classical features of a large-amplitude <coherent state> are:

* It is an eigenstate of the <annihilation operator>, $a|z\rangle=z|z\rangle$. Normally ordered field <amplitudes> therefore behave like a classical complex <amplitude>; normalized <photon subtraction> leaves the same state.
* Free harmonic evolution preserves its form, $|z\rangle\to|ze^{-i\omega t}\rangle$. The mean <electric field> follows a classical sinusoidal wave with an <amplitude> and <phase> set by $z$.
* Its <photon number> has <Poisson distribution>, with $\Delta n/\bar n=1/\sqrt{\bar n}$ and $g^{(2)}(0)=1$. Relative <intensity> fluctuations become small at large <amplitude>.
* Its <optical quadratures> $X=(a+a^\dagger)/2$ and $P=(a-a^\dagger)/(2i)$ have minimum, balanced uncertainties, $\Delta X=\Delta P=1/2$ and $\Delta X\Delta P=1/4$. The fixed vacuum-scale fluctuation becomes small relative to the large phase-space displacement.

These properties identify a particular classical oscillation, rather than a state of definite <photon number>. Absolute quantum fluctuations remain even when their relative size is small.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <universal quantum gate set> can approximate any unitary operation on any finite register, to arbitrary requested accuracy, using a finite sequence of its gates on the register. Unobservable overall <global phase> is ignored. Approximation matters: a fixed finite alphabet has only countably many finite circuits, whereas there are uncountably many exact <unitary matrices>.

The <Hadamard gate> $H$ and <T gate> generate a dense set of single-qubit operations, but cannot themselves entangle different <qubits>. Adding the <controlled-NOT gate> gives a universal multi-qubit set. More generally, an available two-qubit gate must be genuinely entangling: it must take at least one product input to an entangled output. A product of local unitaries, or a <SWAP gate> composed with local unitaries, cannot provide this resource. Together with arbitrary single-qubit control, an entangling two-qubit gate on a connected interaction network supplies universal register control.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Since $\sigma_\phi^2=I$, the <rotation gate> is
$$
\boxed{R_\phi(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\theta/2)&-i e^{-i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)\\-i e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)&\cos(\theta/2)\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
For two chronological $\pi$ pulses, later operations multiply on the left:
$$
\boxed{R_{\phi_2}(\pi)R_{\phi_1}(\pi)=-\begin{pmatrix}e^{-i(\phi_2-\phi_1)}&0\\0&e^{i(\phi_2-\phi_1)}\end{pmatrix}=-R_z[2(\phi_2-\phi_1)].}
$$
Thus equatorial pulses generate arbitrary rotations about the $z$-axis. The minus sign is an overall <global phase>.

For the other chronological sequence, $T^4=Z$, and
$$
\boxed{H T^4=HZ=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}=R_y(\pi/2).}
$$
This equality has no additional overall <phase> under the standard definitions of $H$ and $T$. Rotations about two nonparallel axes, such as $x$ and $y$, generate all single-qubit operations by an <Euler angles> decomposition. Equivalently, the available equatorial rotations include $R_y(\pi/2)$ and arbitrary $R_z$ rotations constructed above; for example $H=R_y(\pi/2)Z$ and $T=e^{i\pi/8}R_z(\pi/4)$. Hence these rotations provide a universal single-qubit set. Overall <phases> do not affect any <probability> or isolated-register observable; a <phase> controlled by an additional <qubit> would instead be relative and could not be discarded without accounting for it.

The minimum number of distinct fixed rotation gates for approximate universality is **two**. One fixed gate and its powers generate a commuting subgroup and cannot approximate all noncommuting single-qubit operations. Two suitable equatorial gates suffice: for example $R_x(\pi/\sqrt2)$ and $R_y(\pi/\sqrt3)$. Each has an angle irrational relative to $2\pi$, so its powers densely approximate rotations about its axis; combining the two supplies the Euler decomposition. Noncommutation alone is insufficient: two Clifford quarter-turns can generate only a finite subgroup. If continuous angles are regarded as available controls, two nonparallel rotation axes likewise suffice, now for exact Euler synthesis.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For $d$-dimensional unitaries, $\operatorname{tr}(U^\dagger U)=d$. The relative propagator $W=U^\dagger V$ is unitary, and its eigenvalues lie on the unit circle. The triangle inequality gives $|\operatorname{tr}W|\leq d$, with equality precisely when all eigenvalues have the same <phase>, or $V=e^{i\gamma}U$. Therefore the specified <trace-overlap gate fidelity> lies between zero and one, is insensitive to <global phase>, and equals one exactly when the implemented gate has the same physical action as the target. It compares propagators independently of any particular input state; it is not the identically defined quantity as average state or average gate fidelity.

For target $R_x(\pi)$ and actual $R_x[\pi(1+\epsilon)]$, the relative propagator is $R_x(\pi\epsilon)$. Its trace is $2\cos(\pi\epsilon/2)$, so
$$
\boxed{\mathcal F_1=\left|\cos\frac{\pi\epsilon}{2}\right|=1-\frac{\pi^2}{8}\epsilon^2+O(\epsilon^4),\qquad \alpha=\frac{\pi^2}{8}.}
$$
The absolute value is unnecessary in a sufficiently small neighbourhood of zero error, but belongs in the exact expression.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

An ideal $\pi$ pulse is $-i\sigma_\phi$. Since $\sigma_\phi\sigma_{2\phi}\sigma_\phi=\sigma_x$, the chronological three-pulse sequence has propagator
$$
\boxed{R_\phi(\pi)R_{2\phi}(\pi)R_\phi(\pi)=i\sigma_x.}
$$
It implements the <Pauli X gate> up to <global phase>, for every $\phi$.

For this <three-pulse amplitude-error compensation>, the leading error in the supplied fidelity expansion vanishes when $1+2\cos\phi=0$, giving
$$
\boxed{\phi=\frac{2\pi}{3}\quad\text{or}\quad\frac{4\pi}{3}\pmod{2\pi}.}
$$
These choices minimize the leading small-angle-error infidelity. Substituting $\cos\phi=\cos2\phi=-1/2$ into the fourth-order term gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal F_3=1-\frac{3\pi^4}{128}\epsilon^4+O(\epsilon^6).}
$$
The fourth-order coefficient is nonzero, so this three-pulse sequence improves the single pulse's quadratic infidelity to quartic infidelity, rather than sixth-order infidelity.

An exact check is available. With $x=\pi\epsilon/2$, multiplication of the three imperfect <rotation gates> at either optimum gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal F_3=\left|\cos x\left(1+\frac12\sin^2x\right)\right|.}
$$
Its <Taylor expansion> has precisely the fourth-order coefficient above. The improvement concerns the same systematic fractional angle error on all three pulses; other errors need not cancel.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Take $|0\rangle$ to have $\sigma_z=+1$ and use angular-frequency <gyromagnetic ratios>. Neglecting interspin coupling, the <Hamiltonian> of the <Zeeman energy> is
$$
\boxed{H=-\frac{\hbar B}{2}(\gamma_1 Z_1+\gamma_2 Z_2).}
$$
For $\gamma_1=\gamma_2=\gamma$, define $\epsilon=\hbar\gamma B/(k_BT)$. The exact thermal <density matrix> in the order $|00\rangle,|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle$ is
$$
\rho_{\mathrm{th}}=\frac{\operatorname{diag}(e^\epsilon,1,1,e^{-\epsilon})}{4\cosh^2(\epsilon/2)}.
$$
At high <temperature>, $|\epsilon|\ll1$, expand both numerator and normalization to obtain
$$
\boxed{\rho_{\mathrm{th}}=\frac14\operatorname{diag}(1+\epsilon,1,1,1-\epsilon)+O(\epsilon^2),\qquad \epsilon=\frac{\hbar\gamma B}{k_BT}.}
$$
For positive $\gamma$, $|00\rangle$ is the most populated state; a negative <gyromagnetic ratio> reverses that population preference under this basis convention.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For the lower-qubit controlled rotation, the control is the upper <qubit> and only the subspace $|10\rangle,|11\rangle$ rotates. Write $u=\cos(\theta/2)$ and $v=\sin(\theta/2)$. Unitary conjugation of its diagonal populations gives $cu^2+dv^2$ and $cv^2+du^2$, together with off-diagonal coherences. The ideal crush gradient stipulated in the source removes those coherences without changing populations. Therefore
$$
\boxed{c'=\frac{c+d+\cos\theta(c-d)}2,\qquad d'=\frac{c+d-\cos\theta(c-d)}2,\qquad \alpha=\cos\theta.}
$$
The other two populations remain $a,b$. This <controlled-transfer gate> is not unitary as a complete operation because it includes <dephasing>.

For an upper-qubit target, the lower <qubit> is the control: the mixed subspace is $|01\rangle,|11\rangle$. Thus
$$
\boxed{(a,b,c,d)\longmapsto\left(a,\frac{b+d+\cos\theta(b-d)}2,c,\frac{b+d-\cos\theta(b-d)}2\right).}
$$
The population $a$ of $|00\rangle$ is unaffected in either direction because its control bit is zero. The <dephasing> step also leaves this diagonal population unchanged, so any sequence of these controlled-transfer gates preserves $a$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the high-temperature state consistently to first order in $\epsilon$. Applying the lower-target <controlled-transfer gate> with $\cos\theta_1=1/3$ gives
$$
\rho_1=\frac14\operatorname{diag}\left(1+\epsilon,1,1-\frac\epsilon3,1-\frac{2\epsilon}3\right).
$$
The upper-target gate with $\cos\theta_2=0$ then equalizes the second and fourth populations. The result is
$$
\boxed{\rho_{\mathrm{pp}}=\frac14\operatorname{diag}\left(1+\epsilon,1-\frac\epsilon3,1-\frac\epsilon3,1-\frac\epsilon3\right)
=(1-p)\frac I4+p|00\rangle\langle00|,\qquad p=\frac\epsilon3.}
$$
This is a <pseudo-pure state>; the identity part is unchanged by unitary gates and does not contribute to traceless <NMR> signals.

For $n$ equal, weakly polarized <spins>, the high-temperature expansion is $\rho\simeq2^{-n}[I+(\epsilon/2)\sum_j Z_j]$. The all-zero population is $a_0=2^{-n}(1+n\epsilon/2)$. Preserving this largest population and averaging the other $2^n-1$ populations gives
$$
\boxed{p_n=\frac{2^na_0-1}{2^n-1}\simeq\frac{n\epsilon}{2(2^n-1)}.}
$$
Thus the signal-bearing effective purity falls approximately as $n/2^n$ at fixed small per-spin <polarization>. The linear expansion requires $n|\epsilon|\ll1$. More generally, if the exact single-spin <polarization> is $r=\tanh(\epsilon/2)$, the same population-preserving averaging gives $p_n=[(1+r)^n-1]/(2^n-1)$, which still decreases exponentially for fixed $0<r<1$. This scaling concerns high-temperature ensemble preparation, not an impossibility theorem for every initialization method.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

In heteronuclear <liquid-state NMR quantum computing>, different <gyromagnetic ratios> give well-separated resonance <frequencies>. A resonant transverse radio-frequency pulse selects one nucleus; its <phase> sets the rotation axis in the transverse plane and its duration and <amplitude> set the rotation angle. Detuning, free precession and rotating-frame <phase> changes supply $z$ rotations. For homonuclear <spins>, differing <chemical shifts> can provide <frequency> selectivity, but selective pulses must be narrow enough not to rotate the other <spin>. Exactly magnetically equivalent <spins> cannot be independently addressed by a spatially uniform radio-frequency field merely because they are labelled as different <qubits>.

In the weak-coupling regime, the secular <NMR scalar coupling> is
$$
H_J=2\pi\hbar J I_{1z}I_{2z}=\frac{\pi\hbar J}{2}Z_1Z_2,
$$
with $J$ in hertz. Free coupling evolution for $1/(2|J|)$ gives an entangling propagator $\exp[-i\operatorname{sgn}(J)\pi Z_1Z_2/4]$. Suitable single-qubit $z$ rotations convert it to a <controlled-Z gate>, and Hadamard conjugation on the target converts controlled-$Z$ to a <controlled-NOT gate>. Refocusing pulses remove unwanted Zeeman evolution and unwanted couplings. This construction applies to resolved, weakly coupled homonuclear <spins> as well as heteronuclear <spins>; strongly coupled or equivalent <spins> need a different control analysis.

These controls supply a universal gate set on a finite addressable connected register, but conventional liquid-state molecular <NMR> is not an architecture that scales straightforwardly to arbitrary register size. A finite chemical-shift bandwidth causes spectral crowding as more <spins> and coupling-split transitions are added; increasingly selective pulses become slow relative to coherence times, and complicated coupling networks require extensive refocusing. The exponentially small pseudo-pure-state signal further limits the complete ensemble scheme. Formal unitary universality therefore does not establish scalable physical addressability, gate speed or initialization.

==== Solution

One implementation uses <type-II phase matching> for <spontaneous parametric down-conversion> in a nonlinear birefringent crystal pumped by a coherent laser. A pump photon produces two lower-frequency photons of orthogonal <polarizations>, subject to <energy> and momentum <phase> matching. Collect two spatial modes in which the alternatives “horizontal photon in Alice's mode and vertical photon in Bob's mode” and the reversed assignment are indistinguishable. Compensation crystals, filters and matched optical paths erase polarization-dependent timing and spectral information. The <amplitudes> then add coherently to give a state proportional to $|HV\rangle+e^{i\varphi}|VH\rangle$; a birefringent <phase> element tunes $\varphi$.

The essential requirement is coherent indistinguishability of the two assignments. Producing two orthogonally polarized photons with information revealing which assignment occurred would instead give a mixture without the required <polarization> <entanglement>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Taking the <partial trace> over either photon of the pure pair state gives
$$
\boxed{\rho_A=\rho_B=\frac12I.}
$$
The two <Schmidt coefficients> are both $1/\sqrt2$, so the <entanglement entropy> is one bit for every $\varphi$. A local <phase> gate on Alice maps $|\Psi^0\rangle$ to $|\Psi^\varphi\rangle$, proving local-unitary equivalence. In strict four-vector Bell-basis terminology only $\varphi=0,\pi$ give the standard $|\Psi^+\rangle,|\Psi^-\rangle$; every <phase> nevertheless gives a maximally entangled Bell-type state.

The eigenpolarizations of the requested observable are
$$
|+_\alpha\rangle=\cos\frac\alpha2|H\rangle+\sin\frac\alpha2|V\rangle,\qquad
|-_\alpha\rangle=-\sin\frac\alpha2|H\rangle+\cos\frac\alpha2|V\rangle.
$$
A <polarizing beam splitter> with its transmitted <polarization> axis at angle $\alpha/2$ to horizontal separates these two orthogonal states. Assign outcome $+1$ to its first detector and $-1$ to the other. Equivalently, use a fixed horizontal/vertical splitter preceded by a <half-wave plate> whose fast axis is at $\alpha/4$; the diagram shows this equivalent implementation. Bob uses $\beta/2$ for his splitter axis, or $\beta/4$ for the preceding half-wave plate.

The pair correlations are $\langle Z\otimes Z\rangle=-1$, $\langle X\otimes X\rangle=\cos\varphi$, and $\langle Z\otimes X\rangle=\langle X\otimes Z\rangle=0$. Expanding the two observables therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\langle\sigma_\alpha\otimes\sigma_\beta\rangle=-\cos\alpha\cos\beta+\cos\varphi\sin\alpha\sin\beta.}
$$
The half-angle between the Bloch observable parameter and the physical linear-polarization analyser angle is essential.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For a deterministic local hidden variable, write the four possible measurement results as $q,r,s,t\in\{\pm1\}$. Its Bell combination is
$$
b=q(s-t)-r(s+t).
$$
If $s=t$, the first bracket vanishes and the second is $\pm2$; if $s=-t$, the second vanishes and the first is $\pm2$. Thus $b=\pm2$ for every hidden variable. Averaging over any local hidden-variable distribution gives $|\mathcal B|\leq2$, and in particular the requested upper bound.

For the specified settings, the correlation formula in part b gives
$$
\langle QS\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt2},\quad
\langle RS\rangle=\langle RT\rangle=-\frac{\cos\varphi}{\sqrt2},\quad
\langle QT\rangle=-\frac1{\sqrt2}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\mathcal B=\sqrt2(1+\cos\varphi).}
$$
This violates the local bound precisely when
$$
\boxed{\cos\varphi>\sqrt2-1,\qquad
|\varphi-2\pi m|<\arccos(\sqrt2-1)\simeq1.14372\ \text{rad},\quad m\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
The maximum is $2\sqrt2$ at $\varphi=0$ modulo $2\pi$. The state remains maximally entangled at other <phases>, even when this fixed choice of real-plane analyser settings gives no violation. Local <phase> compensation or adapted measurement settings can recover the optimum.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The <phase averaging of a polarization Bell state> gives the ensemble <density matrix>
$$
\rho_{\varphi_0}=\frac12\left[|01\rangle\langle01|+|10\rangle\langle10|+s\bigl(|01\rangle\langle10|+|10\rangle\langle01|\bigr)\right],\qquad
s=\frac{\sin\varphi_0}{\varphi_0},
$$
with $s=1$ by continuity at $\varphi_0=0$. This follows from averaging $e^{i\varphi}$ over $[-\varphi_0,\varphi_0]$. An observable's <expectation> is linear in the <density matrix>, so averaging the pure-state Bell function is equivalent to evaluating the mixed-state <expectation>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathcal B_{\varphi_0}=\sqrt2\left(1+\frac{\sin\varphi_0}{\varphi_0}\right).}
$$
Violation requires $\sin\varphi_0/\varphi_0>\sqrt2-1\simeq0.41421$. The supplied graph gives the first crossing near $2.1$ radians; solving more accurately gives
$$
\boxed{0\leq\varphi_0<2.09275\ldots\ \text{rad}.}
$$
The inequality is strict at the upper endpoint. On $(0,\pi)$ the sinc function decreases to zero, so this is its only crossing in that interval. For $\varphi_0\geq\pi$, $|\sin\varphi_0/\varphi_0|\leq1/\pi<\sqrt2-1$, excluding later violation windows. The plotted Bell signal shows the same threshold without copying the source illustration.
