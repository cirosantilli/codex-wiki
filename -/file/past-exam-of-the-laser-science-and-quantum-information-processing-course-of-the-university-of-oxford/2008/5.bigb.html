== 1
{scope}

=== Solution

For a normalized <pure state>, expand $|\psi\rangle=\sum_m c_m|m\rangle$ in an <orthonormal basis> of <eigenvectors> of the measured <Hermitian operator>. The <Born rule> assigns outcome $m$ the <probability> $p_m=|c_m|^2$. Under the ideal <projective measurement> update, a nondegenerate outcome leaves $|m\rangle$, up to a <global phase>. The sum of the outcome probabilities is one, and the <expectation value> is $\sum_m m|c_m|^2$; a single trial reports one eigenvalue, not that average.

If $M|m\rangle=m|m\rangle$, then
$$
m\langle m|m\rangle=\langle m|M|m\rangle
=\overline{\langle m|M|m\rangle}
=\overline m\langle m|m\rangle,
$$
since $M=M^\dagger$. A nonzero <eigenvector> has positive squared <norm>, so **$m$ is real**. For two eigenvectors,
$$
\langle n|M|m\rangle=m\langle n|m\rangle
=\langle Mn|m\rangle=n\langle n|m\rangle.
$$
Thus $(m-n)\langle n|m\rangle=0$: **distinct eigenvalues have orthogonal eigenvectors**.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

The overlap $\langle0|+\rangle=1/\sqrt2$ is nonzero, whereas <perfect discrimination of pure states requires orthogonality>. Consequently no single-copy <quantum measurement> can identify both preparations with certainty. After a nondegenerate <projective measurement>, the <loss of preparation information after a nondegenerate projective measurement> prevents further measurements from recovering the missing label: conditional on the first outcome, the post-measurement state is the same for every compatible initial hypothesis. Repeating the same basis gives that outcome again; changing basis probes the collapsed state. Fresh, independently prepared copies would be additional resources.

In the <computational basis>, input $|0\rangle$ always gives zero and remains $|0\rangle$. Input $|+\rangle$ gives zero or one with probability $1/2$, leaving the corresponding basis state. Combining these likelihoods with equal prior <probabilities>, the <Bayesian update after a projective measurement> gives
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|cc}
\text{outcome}&P(\text{outcome})&P(\text{input }0\mid\text{outcome})&P(\text{input }+\mid\text{outcome})\\\hline
0&3/4&2/3&1/3\\
1&1/4&0&1
\end{array}.
$$
For example, <Bayes' theorem> gives $P(0\text{ input}\mid0\text{ outcome})=(1/2)/(3/4)=2/3$. **Outcome one certifies input $|+\rangle$; outcome zero is ambiguous.** Both inputs that give outcome zero leave exactly the same post-measurement state $|0\rangle$.

In the <Hadamard basis>, input $|+\rangle$ always gives $+$, while input $|0\rangle$ gives $+$ or $-$ equally. Therefore
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|cc}
\text{outcome}&P(\text{outcome})&P(\text{input }0\mid\text{outcome})&P(\text{input }+\mid\text{outcome})\\\hline
+&3/4&1/3&2/3\\
-&1/4&1&0
\end{array}.
$$
**Outcome minus certifies input $|0\rangle$; outcome plus is ambiguous.** Guessing the more likely input after the ambiguous outcome gives success probability **$3/4$** for either of these two fixed-basis measurements.

== 3
{scope}

=== Solution

Put $q=\cos^2(\pi/8)=(2+\sqrt2)/4$ and $1-q=\sin^2(\pi/8)$. In the <computational basis>, input $|a\rangle$ has outcome probabilities $(q,1-q)$, while $|b\rangle$ has $(1-q,q)$. The marginal outcomes are equally likely; the <Bayesian posterior> probabilities are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|cc}
\text{outcome}&P(\text{outcome})&P(a\mid\text{outcome})&P(b\mid\text{outcome})\\\hline
0&1/2&q&1-q\\
1&1/2&1-q&q
\end{array}.
$$
Guess $a$ after zero and $b$ after one. Each guess is correct with probability **$q\simeq0.853553$** and wrong with probability **$1-q\simeq0.146447$**. Neither outcome is certain evidence for one input.

For a real state $|\psi_\theta\rangle=\cos\theta|0\rangle+\sin\theta|1\rangle$, the <Born rule> in the <Hadamard basis> gives $P(\pm\mid\theta)=(1\pm\sin2\theta)/2$. Both angles $\pi/8$ and $3\pi/8$ have $\sin2\theta=1/\sqrt2$. Thus both preparations give $P(+)=q$ and $P(-)=1-q$. By <Bayes' theorem>, **either outcome leaves the two input hypotheses equally likely**. The biased outcome frequencies carry no information about which state was prepared; the best success probability with this measurement is **$1/2$**.

== 4
{scope}

=== Solution

The <Bloch vectors> of $|a\rangle$ and $|b\rangle$ are
$$
\mathbf r_a=(1/\sqrt2,0,1/\sqrt2),\qquad
\mathbf r_b=(1/\sqrt2,0,-1/\sqrt2).
$$
They share the same $x$ component and differ along $z$. The <equal-prior discrimination of qubit states> therefore selects the $Z$ axis: it maximizes the separation of their projected outcome probabilities. This also explains why the $X$ measurement in part 3 is uninformative.

For $|0\rangle$ and $|+\rangle$, the Bloch vectors are $(0,0,1)$ and $(1,0,0)$. Their difference points along $\mathbf n=(-1,0,1)/\sqrt2$. Hence <Carl W. Helstrom>'s <Helstrom measurement for the zero and plus states> measures
$$
\boxed{M_{\rm opt}=\frac{Z-X}{\sqrt2},\qquad
P_\pm=\frac{I\pm M_{\rm opt}}2.}
$$
Guess $|0\rangle$ after $P_+$ and $|+\rangle$ after $P_-$. An explicit <orthonormal basis> for these projectors is
$$
|h_+\rangle=\cos(\pi/8)|0\rangle-\sin(\pi/8)|1\rangle,\qquad
|h_-\rangle=\sin(\pi/8)|0\rangle+\cos(\pi/8)|1\rangle.
$$
The positive measurement axis is tilted by $\pi/4$ from $+z$ towards $-x$ on the <Bloch sphere>; the corresponding state-vector rotation angle is $-\pi/8$.

{title=Bloch-sphere cross-sections for the two equal-prior discrimination problems}
{description=Solid arrows are the candidate-state <Bloch vectors>. Dashed lines show the optimal measurement axes. The first pair has identical $x$ projections; the second is separated most strongly along $(z-x)/\sqrt2$.}

For completeness, if a projective measurement guesses $0$ along a unit Bloch axis $\mathbf n$, its average success is $1/2+(n_z-n_x)/4$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> bounds $n_z-n_x\le\sqrt2$, with equality at the axis above. The <Holevo–Helstrom theorem> shows this optimum also holds among general <POVMs>. Thus
$$
\boxed{P_{\rm success}^{\rm opt}=\frac12\left(1+\frac1{\sqrt2}\right),\qquad
P_{\rm error}^{\rm opt}=\frac12\left(1-\frac1{\sqrt2}\right).}
$$
