== 1
{scope}

=== Solution

The <beta barium borate> crystal supplies <spontaneous parametric down-conversion>: a weak <ultraviolet> pump can produce a pair of lower-frequency <photons>, with their frequencies and <wavevectors> selected by <conservation of energy> and <phase matching>. A suitably aligned and compensated source makes the two polarization alternatives coherent, giving the stated <Bell state>. The crystal alone does not guarantee that every collected pair has that state.

The two <polarizing beam splitters> route <horizontal polarization> and <vertical polarization> to different ports. Take $H$ from arm $a$ to the trigger $T$ and $V$ to arm $c$. The ordinary <beam splitter> in arm $b$ is a lossless, polarization-independent 50:50 splitter, with the unused input in the <vacuum state>. It splits <probability amplitudes>, rather than measuring which output a photon takes. The <half-wave plate> changes the relative phase of its principal polarizations by $\pi$; orient it so that a $V$ photon in $c$ becomes $(H+V)/\sqrt2$ in $c'$, up to a common phase. Finally, <photodetectors> absorb arriving <photons> and supply electrical clicks. They need not resolve <photon number> for the ideal four-photon, fourfold-coincidence calculation: a click at every detector already requires one photon at each.

Choose the relative <wave phases> so that the final <polarizing beam splitter> sends $H_{c'}$ to $H_2$, $V_{c'}$ to $V_1$, $H_d$ to $H_1$ and $V_d$ to $V_2$. Such phase conventions must be used consistently: an uncompensated relative phase would give a phase-shifted <GHZ state>.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

Use the <two-pair component of polarization-entangled down-conversion> to account for identical <bosons> in the same collected modes. Write $a_H^\dagger$ for a <creation operator> in mode $a$ with <horizontal polarization>, and similarly for the other modes. For the plus <Bell state>, the pair-creation operator is $K^\dagger=a_H^\dagger b_V^\dagger+a_V^\dagger b_H^\dagger$. The normalized four-photon state is
$$
|\Psi_2\rangle=\frac{(K^\dagger)^2}{2\sqrt3}|0\rangle
=\frac{|2H_a,2V_b\rangle+|H_aV_a,H_bV_b\rangle+|2V_a,2H_b\rangle}{\sqrt3}.
$$
These are normalized <Fock states>. In particular, $(a_H^\dagger)^2|0\rangle=\sqrt2|2H_a\rangle$; omitting this factor would misnormalize the four-photon input.

The first <polarizing beam splitter> sends $a_H^\dagger$ to $T_H^\dagger$ and $a_V^\dagger$ to $c_V^\dagger$. Only the middle term can give exactly one trigger photon and leave another photon in $c$. In the first term both $a$ photons reach $T$ and none reaches $c$; in the last there is no trigger. Thus the relevant, still unnormalized component is
$$
\frac1{\sqrt3}T_H^\dagger c_V^\dagger b_H^\dagger b_V^\dagger|0\rangle.
$$

For the selected paths of the <half-wave plate> and <beam splitter>, take
$$
c_V^\dagger\longmapsto\frac{c_H^{\prime\dagger}+c_V^{\prime\dagger}}{\sqrt2},\qquad
b_\sigma^\dagger\longmapsto\frac{d_\sigma^\dagger+e_\sigma^\dagger}{\sqrt2},\quad\sigma=H,V.
$$
Here $e$ labels the output towards $D_3$. An immaterial common reflection phase has been absorbed in the mode definitions. Keeping one photon at $D_3$ leaves
$$
\frac{T_H^\dagger}{2\sqrt6}
(c_H^{\prime\dagger}+c_V^{\prime\dagger})
(d_H^\dagger e_V^\dagger+d_V^\dagger e_H^\dagger)|0\rangle.
$$
At the final <polarizing beam splitter>, the $H_{c'}H_d$ alternative yields one $H$ at each of $D_1,D_2$, while $V_{c'}V_d$ yields one $V$ at each. The two opposite-polarization alternatives send both photons to the same detector and fail fourfold <coincidence counting>. The accepted component is therefore
$$
\frac1{2\sqrt6}|H\rangle_T
\bigl(|HHV\rangle_{123}+|VVH\rangle_{123}\bigr).
$$
After conditioning and <normalization>, the three detected output modes have
$$
\boxed{|\mathrm{GHZ}\rangle_{123}=\frac{|VVH\rangle+|HHV\rangle}{\sqrt2}.}
$$
The <optical mode matching> must erase any timing, frequency or other record that distinguishes the two alternatives. Otherwise the same polarization populations could describe an incoherent <mixed quantum state>, rather than this <quantum superposition>. The <Bouwmeester three-photon GHZ experiment> uses this principle. The <photonic GHZ preparation by fourfold coincidence> is a measurement in the coincidence basis: detecting all four photons projects and consumes them. A click at $T$ by itself does not herald a guaranteed freely available three-photon state.

== 3
{scope}

=== Solution

The <optical success probability of a fourfold GHZ source> depends on the photon-number statistics of the assumed two-pair input. Let $q$ be the probability that arm $a$ contains one $H$ and one $V$, given two pairs. The <beam splitter> then puts one of the two $b$ photons into each output with probability $1/2$, and the final polarization-parity selection succeeds with probability $1/2$. With ideal <photodetectors>,
$$
\boxed{P(\text{fourfold}\mid\text{two pairs})=\frac q4.}
$$
For the normalized, fully overlapping <two-pair component of polarization-entangled down-conversion> used above, $q=1/3$, so the squared norm of the accepted component gives **$P=1/12$**. Treating the two emissions instead as independently labelled <Bell pairs> gives the commonly used bookkeeping estimate $q=1/2$ and **$P=1/8$**. This is a different pair-statistics approximation; it must not be obtained by dropping labels from an input state without renormalizing it. Genuine <GHZ state> coherence additionally requires erasing information about the pair of origin.

Let $P_2$ be the two-pair probability per pulse and $f$ the pulse <repetition rate>. The coincidence rate is **$R=fP_2q/4$**. With the usual order-of-magnitude estimate $P_2\simeq p^2$, the supplied $f=10^6\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $p=10^{-3}$ give $fP_2\simeq1\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. The fully overlapping model gives **$R\simeq0.083\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$**, about one event per twelve seconds; independent-pair bookkeeping gives **$R\simeq0.125\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$**, about one per eight seconds. Thus the estimate is of order **$10^{-1}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$** under these idealizations.

An exact prefactor cannot follow from the single-pair probability alone. For example, the <pair-number statistics of polarization-entangled down-conversion> in a single collected mode pair give $P_2\simeq3p^2/4$ at weak gain, and hence **$R\simeq fp^2/16=0.0625\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$** with $q=1/3$. Multiple temporal or spectral modes change those statistics. Real collection losses and detector efficiencies further reduce the rate; they are excluded by the ideal calculation.
