== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <laser relaxation oscillation> is the damped exchange between stored upper-level excitation and intracavity <photon number> after a small perturbation of a lasing <steady state>. Extra <photons> increase stimulated depletion of the upper level; the resulting smaller gain then reduces the <photon> population. Pumping subsequently replenishes the inversion, so the two populations overshoot their equilibrium values before damping settles them.

<Laser spiking> is the corresponding large-signal sequence of intense output pulses, often during switch-on. Excitation first accumulates while the optical field is small. Once gain exceeds loss, rapid <stimulated emission> releases this stored <energy> in a spike, depleting the inversion below threshold. Recovery can produce further spikes. This is distinct from an externally imposed <Q-switching>[Q-switching] cycle, although both draw on stored excitation.

Many solid-state gain media have upper-state lifetimes far longer than the cavity <photon lifetime>. The optical <polarization> relaxes rapidly, leaving the two slow <Laser rate equation>[rate-equation] variables of a class-B <laser>. A long-lived population reservoir and a much faster <photon> response make underdamped recovery and pronounced startup spiking common. The criterion and <frequency> follow from the <Linearization of a dynamical system>[linearization] in part c; they are not universal consequences of the word “solid-state”.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $v_g\simeq c/n_r$ be the light's <group velocity> in the fibre, with unit mode overlap and a uniform transverse mode area. The <fibre-laser gain-volume rate equations> follow because the upper-level <density> is defined per doped volume, whereas the <photon> <density> is defined per full cavity volume. <Stimulated emission> therefore removes upper-level ions at rate $v_g\sigma_{21}N_2n$ per doped volume, but supplies cavity <photons> at the volume-averaged rate $fv_g\sigma_{21}N_2n$. Thus
$$
\dot N_2=S-v_g\sigma_{21}N_2n-\frac{N_2}{\tau_2},\qquad
\dot n=\left(fv_g\sigma_{21}N_2-\frac1{\tau_c}\right)n.
$$
At threshold the stimulated <photon> growth rate balances the <photon> loss rate, giving
$$
\boxed{N_{\mathrm{th}}=\frac1{fv_g\sigma_{21}\tau_c}\simeq\frac{n_r}{fc\sigma_{21}\tau_c}.}
$$
Using this relation yields the stated equations, including the essential $1/f$ in the ion-depletion term:
$$
\dot N_2=S-\frac1f\frac{N_2}{N_{\mathrm{th}}}\frac n{\tau_c}-\frac{N_2}{\tau_2},\qquad
\dot n=\left(\frac{N_2}{N_{\mathrm{th}}}-1\right)\frac n{\tau_c}.
$$
Above threshold, the nonzero stationary <photon> <density> requires $N_{2,\mathrm{ss}}=N_{\mathrm{th}}$. The remaining stationary equation then gives
$$
\boxed{r=\frac{S\tau_2}{N_{\mathrm{th}}}=\frac S{S_{\mathrm{th}}},\qquad
n_{\mathrm{ss}}=(r-1)f\frac{\tau_c}{\tau_2}N_{\mathrm{th}},\qquad S_{\mathrm{th}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{th}}}{\tau_2}.}
$$
The parameter $r$ is the pump rate in units of its lasing-threshold value. The above-threshold branch requires $r>1$; below threshold the ideal stationary solution is $n=0$, $N_2=S\tau_2$. <Spontaneous emission> into the lasing mode has been omitted in this deterministic model, so an exactly zero initial field would need a seed or noise to start, even when the zero-field branch is unstable.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $N_2=N_{\mathrm{th}}+u$ and $n=n_{\mathrm{ss}}+w$. Keeping terms linear in the perturbations gives
$$
\frac d{dt}\begin{pmatrix}u\\w\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}-r/\tau_2&-1/(f\tau_c)\\f(r-1)/\tau_2&0\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}u\\w\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Equivalently,
$$
\ddot w+\frac r{\tau_2}\dot w+\frac{r-1}{\tau_c\tau_2}w=0.
$$
The characteristic exponents are
$$
s_\pm=-\frac r{2\tau_2}\pm i\sqrt{\frac{r-1}{\tau_c\tau_2}-\frac{r^2}{4\tau_2^2}}.
$$
Consequently the above-threshold <steady state> has damped <oscillations> precisely when
$$
\boxed{\tau_2>\frac{r^2}{4(r-1)}\tau_c,\qquad r>1.}
$$
Their ordinary <frequency> is
$$
\boxed{f_{\mathrm{ro}}=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{r-1}{\tau_c\tau_2}-\frac{r^2}{4\tau_2^2}}
\simeq\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{r-1}{\tau_c\tau_2}}.}
$$
The approximation neglects the damping correction when $r^2\tau_c\ll4(r-1)\tau_2$. The exponential envelope decays on time $2\tau_2/r$. The doped-volume fraction cancels out of this linear <frequency> once the pump is expressed through $r$. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-974-fundamentals-of-photonics-quantum-electronics-spring-2006/18f30fad63a62ef4dd894d3752b55a60_chapter7.pdf[MIT 6.974, Chapter 7, <laser> dynamics and relaxation <oscillations>]{ref}

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The upper-state <density> is clamped near threshold once the <laser> operates, so excess pumping mainly increases <photon> <density> and output <power>. For approximately pump-independent absorption efficiency, $r=P_p/P_{p,\mathrm{th}}$, and both $P_{\mathrm{out}}$ and $f_{\mathrm{ro}}^2$ should rise approximately linearly with $P_p-P_{p,\mathrm{th}}$.

**The left ordinate is the square of the <frequency>**, in $\mathrm{kHz}^2$, rather than <frequency> in kHz. Reading the original triangles gives a threshold near $12$–$13\ \mathrm{mW}$ and a slope about $1.0\ \mathrm{kHz}^2/\mathrm{mW}$. A representative straight-line fit to visually read marker centers gives $P_{p,\mathrm{th}}\simeq12.2\ \mathrm{mW}$ and $A=d f_{\mathrm{ro}}^2/dP_p\simeq1.02\times10^6\ \mathrm{Hz}^2/\mathrm{mW}$. Therefore
$$
A\simeq\frac1{4\pi^2\tau_c\tau_2P_{p,\mathrm{th}}},\qquad
\boxed{\tau_c\simeq\frac1{4\pi^2(0.013)(12.2)(1.02\times10^6)}
\simeq1.6\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm s.}
$$
The displayed precision is limited by graph reading and scatter; a lifetime around $0.15\ \mathrm{\mu s}$ is the meaningful result. The output squares have a compatible threshold and an approximately constant slope above it.

The round-trip time is
$$
t_{\mathrm{rt}}=\frac{2n_rL}{c}=\frac{2(1.46)(7)}c\simeq6.82\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm s.
$$
If mirror transmission is the only appreciable passive loss, <energy> decays by $R_1R_2=e^{-t_{\mathrm{rt}}/\tau_c}$ per round trip. For $R_1=1$,
$$
\boxed{R_2=e^{-t_{\mathrm{rt}}/\tau_c}\simeq0.65,}
$$
a <power> <reflectance> of roughly 65%. The exact exponential is preferable to replacing $1-R_2$ by $t_{\mathrm{rt}}/\tau_c$, since the inferred round-trip loss is not tiny. If distributed fibre loss is appreciable, the lifetime instead fixes the total round-trip retention; without a separate loss measurement, the bare mirror <reflectance> is not uniquely determined and is at least this retention factor.

=== a

==== Solution

Measure the <phase> relative to propagation through the same geometrical thickness of air. If the <lens>'s central thickness is $t_0$, its spherical surface gives
$$
t(x)=t_0-\left[R-\sqrt{R^2-x^2}\right]
\simeq t_0-\frac{x^2}{2R},\qquad |x|\ll R.
$$
The optical-path excess is $(n-1)t(x)$, so the <phase> imparted by the <lens> is
$$
\boxed{\phi(x)=k(n-1)t_0-\frac{k(n-1)x^2}{2R}.}
$$
The constant part is a spatially uniform <phase>. The physically important <lens> transmission is therefore $T_f(x)=\exp[-ikx^2/(2f)]$ with
$$
\boxed{f=\frac R{n-1}.}
$$
One way to identify the focus is to append propagation to the on-axis point a distance $f$ away. Its path <phase> has the paraxial contribution $+kx^2/(2f)$, which cancels the <lens> <phase>. Thus all incident portions of a plane wave add with the same <phase> at that point. The diagram below shows the thickness change and both Fourier-transform arrangements.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Apply the first <lens>, propagate, and apply the second <lens>. Omitting only common prefactors and uniform <phases>,
$$
E_{\mathrm{out}}(x)\propto\int dx'\,
\exp\!\left[-\frac{ikx^2}{2f}+\frac{ik(x-x')^2}{2L}-\frac{ikx'^2}{2f}\right]E_{\mathrm{in}}(x').
$$
The coefficients of both $x^2$ and $x'^2$ vanish if and only if $f=L$. The remaining cross term is $-ikxx'/L$, so
$$
\boxed{f=L,\qquad E_{\mathrm{out}}(x)\propto\int dx'\,e^{-i(kx/L)x'}E_{\mathrm{in}}(x')
=\widetilde E_{\mathrm{in}}\!\left(\frac{kx}{L}\right).}
$$
The Fourier convention here is $\widetilde E(q)=\int e^{-iqx}E(x)dx$. Finite clear apertures would truncate the field and limit the spatial-frequency range; the ideal relation assumes the paraxial field fits within both <optical lens>[lenses].

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Free propagation is <convolution> with a chirp, $K_L(x)=C_Le^{ikL}e^{ikx^2/(2L)}$. The <convolution theorem> therefore turns it into multiplication by its <Fourier transform>. Complete the square:
$$
\frac{kx^2}{2L}-qx=\frac{k}{2L}\left(x-\frac{Lq}{k}\right)^2-\frac{Lq^2}{2k}.
$$
The <Gaussian integral> then gives
$$
\widetilde E(q,L)=C_L\sqrt{\frac{2\pi iL}{k}}\,
 e^{ikL-iLq^2/(2k)}\widetilde E(q,0).
$$
Thus the wavevector-dependent <phase> is
$$
\boxed{\varphi(k_\perp)=kL-\frac{Lk_\perp^2}{2k},}
$$
up to an overall <phase> convention; after removing the carrier it is $-Lk_\perp^2/(2k)$. This also follows from $k_z=\sqrt{k^2-k_\perp^2}\simeq k-k_\perp^2/(2k)$.

**<Normalization> qualification:** the source keeps a two-dimensional-style prefactor while integrating over only one transverse coordinate. With its literal $C_L=ik/(2\pi L)$, there is an additional $q$-independent factor, rather than solely a unit-modulus multiplier. A properly normalized one-dimensional <Fresnel propagator> has $C_L=\sqrt{k/(2\pi iL)}$, which cancels the square-root factor above and makes propagation a pure Fourier-domain <phase>. For example, the literal source kernel multiplies a plane-wave Fourier mode by a factor of magnitude $\sqrt{k/(2\pi L)}$, not one. The spatially dependent <phase> and the proportional Fourier-transform results remain unchanged. <Oscillations and Waves by Richard Fitzpatrick>[Wave optics, two-dimensional Fourier optics: optical-path expansion and Fourier-domain diffraction]{ref}

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Place the input plane one <focal length> before a single positive <lens> and the output plane one <focal length> after it. The <lens> maps each incident transverse spatial <frequency> to its corresponding point in the back focal plane. Referencing the input to the front focal plane removes the otherwise present output <phase curvature>, giving an exact scaled <Fourier transform> within the same paraxial model.

A short <Ray transfer matrix>[ray-matrix] check makes the geometry explicit. With free propagation $P(f)$ and <lens> <matrix> $L(f)$,
$$
\boxed{P(f)L(f)P(f)=
\begin{pmatrix}1&f\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-1/f&1\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}1&f\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}0&f\\-1/f&0\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The vanishing diagonal entries are the Fourier-plane condition; the output is proportional to $\widetilde E_{\mathrm{in}}(kx/f)$. The final panel of the original diagram in part a shows the two focal distances. A <lens> with input immediately on its surface and output at the back focal plane would generally leave a quadratic output <phase>; the front-focal-plane choice avoids it.

=== a

==== Solution

Let $p=|\alpha|^2$, so $|\beta|^2=1-p$. The <projective measurement> has outcome 0 with <probability> $p$ and leaves the state $|0\rangle$; outcome 1 has <probability> $1-p$ and leaves $|1\rangle$. Alice knows her actual result, so her conditional description is a <pure state>. Her <fidelity of quantum states> with the initial state is $p$ after outcome 0 or $1-p$ after outcome 1. Its outcome average is $p^2+(1-p)^2$.

Bob knows the preparation but not the outcome, so his <density matrix> is the nonselective postmeasurement state
$$
\boxed{\rho_B=p|0\rangle\langle0|+(1-p)|1\rangle\langle1|,\qquad
F_B=\langle\psi|\rho_B|\psi\rangle=p^2+(1-p)^2.}
$$
It is mixed for $0<p<1$ and pure only at the endpoints. The relative <phase> has disappeared because the classical outcome record is unavailable to him.

The <undisclosed qubit measurement outcome with an unspecified preparation prior> requires care: Charlene has no specified prior over preparations. Consequently the information given does not determine a unique posterior <probability> for the two outcomes. Under a symmetric ignorance or maximum-entropy prior, her best assignment is
$$
\boxed{\rho_C=I/2,\qquad F_C=1/2.}
$$
More generally, if her prior mean of $|\alpha|^2$ is $q$, her assignment is $\rho_C=\operatorname{diag}(q,1-q)$ and its <fidelity of quantum states> with the actual initial state is $pq+(1-p)(1-q)$. Knowing that a measurement occurred guarantees diagonality in its basis, but does not by itself prove $q=1/2$. Different descriptions here reflect different available records, rather than different physical outcomes. <Foundations I: States and Ensembles by John Preskill>[Sections 2.3–2.4, density matrices, ensembles and measurement information]{ref}

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the common $D_{3/2}$ level to subtract the two upper-state energies. The indicated transition satisfies
$$
\frac1\lambda=\frac1{397\ \mathrm{nm}}+\frac1{850\ \mathrm{nm}}-\frac1{866\ \mathrm{nm}},
\qquad
\boxed{\lambda\simeq393.6\ \mathrm{nm}.}
$$
The value inherits the rounding of the supplied <wavelengths>. It is shorter than 397 nm because $P_{3/2}$ lies above $P_{1/2}$.

A static electric potential alone cannot confine a charge stably in all three dimensions in a charge-free region. An RF quadrupole <ion trap> uses a rapidly alternating <electric field> to provide effective radial confinement; DC endcap fields can supply axial confinement. Near a stable equilibrium, the <Paul trap secular motion>[secular motion] sees an approximately harmonic pseudopotential. It has quantized vibrational levels $E=\sum_i\hbar\omega_i(n_i+1/2)$, with separate <normal modes> along the principal directions. RF <Paul trap micromotion> is superposed on this slower secular <oscillation>. For several <trapped ions>, Coulomb interactions instead give collective <normal modes>. <Nobel Lecture: Superposition, entanglement, and raising Schrödinger’s cat by David J. Wineland>[Sections III–IV, trapped-ion confinement and quantized motion]{ref}

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The two $D$ states are metastable, so a <quantum superposition> can retain its relative <phase> much longer than one involving a rapidly decaying $P$ state. In the stipulated transition diagram they have no allowed downward <Electric dipole transition>[electric-dipole transition]; real weaker decay channels limit, rather than eliminate, this lifetime advantage. Their distinct energies permit resolved state-selective optical control.

Both $D$ levels couple to $P_{3/2}$. Apply coherent beams near 850 nm and 854 nm, detuned from that common intermediate level by a large <Rabi detuning> $\Delta_R$. Absorption from one beam and <stimulated emission> into the other transfer population between the two $D$ states without appreciably populating the short-lived intermediate level. Tune the <frequency> difference to their splitting, including differential AC Stark shifts. In the far-detuned limit,
$$
\boxed{\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}\simeq\frac{\Omega_{850}\Omega_{854}^*}{2\Delta_R},\qquad
P_{D_{5/2}}(t)=\sin^2\!\left(\frac{|\Omega_{\mathrm{eff}}|t}{2}\right)}
$$
for an initially pure $D_{3/2}$ state on two-photon resonance. The controllable relative optical <phase> sets the rotation axis; the large <Rabi detuning> suppresses spontaneous-scattering errors. Suitable <polarizations> and a chosen pair of magnetic sublevels are required in an actual experiment.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Use state-dependent <fluorescence>. With 397 nm light on $S_{1/2}\leftrightarrow P_{1/2}$ and an 866 nm repumper on $D_{3/2}\leftrightarrow P_{1/2}$, an ion initially in $D_{3/2}$ is brought into a bright cycling manifold. The repumper prevents population trapping in $D_{3/2}$. An ion in $D_{5/2}$ remains dark while the 854 nm light is off, since it has no allowed link to this cycle. A detected bright or dark record therefore measures the <qubit> in its logical basis by <electron shelving>.

To reset, apply 854 nm light to empty the shelved state, and use 397 nm plus repumping for <Doppler cooling> and internal-state preparation. <Doppler cooling> alone generally does not reach the <ground state>. In the resolved-sideband and <Lamb-Dicke regime>, drive the <Motional sideband>[red motional Raman sideband]
$$
|D_{3/2},n\rangle\longrightarrow|D_{5/2},n-1\rangle
$$
and then optically repump to $D_{3/2}$ with little change in motional occupation. Each cycle removes approximately one vibrational quantum. The $n=0$ state is dark to this red sideband because its <matrix> element contains $\sqrt n$. Repeat with appropriate <wavevector> projections and <frequency> differences for all modes to be cooled, then optically pump into the selected $D_{3/2}$ sublevel. Small recoil during repumping is necessary for a high ground-state <probability>. <Nobel Lecture: Superposition, entanglement, and raising Schrödinger’s cat by David J. Wineland>[Sections III–IV, fluorescence readout and resolved-sideband control of ion motion]{ref}

==== Solution

Use the time-ordered network $\operatorname{CNOT}_{1\to2}$, $\operatorname{CNOT}_{2\to1}$, $\operatorname{CNOT}_{1\to2}$. On bits $(a,b)$ its successive values are
$$
(a,b)\longmapsto(a,a\oplus b)\longmapsto(b,a\oplus b)\longmapsto(b,a).
$$
Thus the network exchanges the two <Computational basis>[computational-basis] states. Since the gates are linear, this basis proof also establishes <SWAP gate> on arbitrary quantum states.

The <Bell states> $|\Phi^\pm\rangle=(|00\rangle\pm|11\rangle)/\sqrt2$ and $|\Psi^\pm\rangle=(|01\rangle\pm|10\rangle)/\sqrt2$ obey
$$
\boxed{S|\Phi^+\rangle=|\Phi^+\rangle,\quad S|\Phi^-\rangle=|\Phi^-\rangle,\quad
S|\Psi^+\rangle=|\Psi^+\rangle,\quad S|\Psi^-\rangle=-|\Psi^-\rangle.}
$$
The antisymmetric <spin singlet state> alone has <eigenvalue> $-1$. Its standalone sign is a global <phase>; a <Fredkin gate> can make that sign observable relative to the control branch, as in part d.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Control all three CNOTs of the <SWAP gate> decomposition by <qubit> 1. This gives, in temporal order,
$$
T_{1,2\to3},\qquad T_{1,3\to2},\qquad T_{1,2\to3}.
$$
When the first <qubit> is 0, all three <Toffoli gates> do nothing. When it is 1, the three lower-wire CNOTs implement <SWAP gate>. The middle panel of the original diagram shows this controlled network.

Only one <Toffoli gate> is actually needed. An equivalent circuit is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{CNOT}_{3\to2},\qquad T_{1,2\to3},\qquad\operatorname{CNOT}_{3\to2}.}
$$
For input $(c,a,b)$, the first gate forms $a\oplus b$ on wire 2. The second changes wire 3 to $b'=b\oplus c(a\oplus b)$, and the final gate gives wire 2 the value $a'=a\oplus c(a\oplus b)$. For $c=0$ this is $(a,b)$; for $c=1$ it is $(b,a)$. The last panel displays the circuit.

The <Hamming-weight obstruction to Fredkin-only Toffoli synthesis> means that a network of <Fredkin gates> alone cannot implement a <Toffoli gate> on the same three wires: each <Fredkin gate> preserves <Computational basis>[computational-basis] <Hamming weight>, whereas <Toffoli gate> maps $110$ to $111$. The same obstruction holds if fixed ancillary bits must be returned unchanged. Conservative classical computation can use extra encoded wires and input-dependent garbage, but that is not an ancilla-free coherent three-qubit <Toffoli gate> operation. The resources and output encoding would need to be specified before making a different universality claim.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Use basis order $000,001,010,011,100,101,110,111$, with the ancilla first, and put $x=e^{i\phi_1}$, $y=e^{i\phi_2}$. Initially the ancilla is at the north pole of the <Bloch sphere>. The other two <qubits> lie on its equator at azimuths $\phi_1,\phi_2$, with <Bloch vectors> $(\cos\phi_j,\sin\phi_j,0)$.

The four dotted cuts, in left-to-right order, have the explicit normalized vectors
$$
\boxed{v_0=\frac12(1,y,x,xy,0,0,0,0)^{\mathsf T},}
$$
$$
\boxed{v_1=\frac1{2\sqrt2}(1,y,x,xy,1,y,x,xy)^{\mathsf T},}
$$
$$
\boxed{v_2=\frac1{2\sqrt2}(1,y,x,xy,1,x,y,xy)^{\mathsf T},}
$$
$$
\boxed{v_3=\frac14(2,x+y,x+y,2xy,0,y-x,x-y,0)^{\mathsf T}.}
$$
The first <Hadamard gate> copies the lower-register amplitudes into two control branches; the <Fredkin gate> exchanges them only in the second branch; the final <Hadamard gate> adds and subtracts these branches.

Summing the squared magnitudes of the first four entries of $v_3$ gives
$$
P_0=\frac{8+2|x+y|^2}{16}=\frac34+\frac14\cos(\phi_1-\phi_2).
$$
Meanwhile $\langle\phi_2|\phi_1\rangle=(1+e^{i(\phi_1-\phi_2)})/2$, so
$$
\boxed{P_0=\frac{1+|\langle\phi_2|\phi_1\rangle|^2}{2}.}
$$
Identical input <qubits> give $P_0=1$; orthogonal equatorial inputs give $P_0=1/2$. The factor one half is an ancilla-interference effect, not a direct <projective measurement> of the lower <qubits> individually.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For any joint lower-register state, the <probability> is $P_0=(1+\langle S\rangle)/2$, where $S$ is its exchange operator. The Bell-state <eigenvalues> from part a therefore give
$$
\boxed{P_0(\Phi^+)=P_0(\Phi^-)=P_0(\Psi^+)=1,\qquad P_0(\Psi^-)=0.}
$$
For the <spin singlet state>, controlled exchange adds a minus sign to the control-1 branch, so the final <Hadamard gate> puts the ancilla in $|1\rangle$. For the three symmetric states the ancilla returns to $|0\rangle$.

The product-state overlap formula cannot be applied to arbitrary entangled lower inputs. All four <Bell states> have identical one-qubit reduced states $I/2$, yet the <spin singlet state> result differs maximally. The network measures exchange symmetry of the joint state, rather than <fidelity of quantum states> inferred from its two marginals.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

<Wave plates> and <polarization> <phase> shifters implement deterministic single-qubit <unitaries>: a suitable quarter-wave/half-wave/quarter-wave sequence realizes an arbitrary <polarization> rotation. A deterministic entangling gate on two independently encoded <photons> needs an effective interaction or additional resources that make one <photon>'s logical action conditional on the other. For example, a sufficiently strong conditional <phase> followed by Hadamards on the target implements <CNOT gate>. Ordinary passive <linear optics> alone does not provide this deterministic interaction on independent <polarization> <qubits>. The practical difficulty is that optical nonlinearities at the single-photon level are weak and commonly accompanied by loss or noise; auxiliary <photons>, measurements and feed-forward trade determinism for resource overhead.

The <polarizing beam splitter> eigenpolarizations at angle $\alpha$ are the orthonormal states
$$
\boxed{|T_\alpha\rangle=\cos\alpha|H\rangle+\sin\alpha|V\rangle,\qquad
|R_\alpha\rangle=-\sin\alpha|H\rangle+\cos\alpha|V\rangle.}
$$
The first is perfectly transmitted and the second perfectly reflected, from either input port. Port-dependent reflection <phases> can be fixed by a consistent optical <phase> convention or compensated locally; they do not alter the transmitted/reflected basis.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Before the gates, write the two <Bell states> as
$$
|\Phi^+\rangle_{12}|\Phi^+\rangle_{34}
=\frac12\sum_{a,b=0}^1|a\rangle_1|a\rangle_2|b\rangle_3|b\rangle_4.
$$
The <CNOT gate> sends the last bit to $b\oplus a$. Projection onto outcome 0 on <qubit> 4 therefore requires $b=a$. After the <Hadamard gate> on <qubit> 2, projection onto its outcome 0 contributes $1/\sqrt2$ for either remaining value of $a$. The unnormalized surviving state is
$$
\frac{|00\rangle_{13}+|11\rangle_{13}}{2\sqrt2}=\frac12|\Phi^+\rangle_{13}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{|\psi\rangle_{13}=|\Phi^+\rangle_{13},\qquad P(00_{24})=\frac14.}
$$
This is <entanglement swapping>: <photons> 1 and 3 become entangled even though the joint measurement acts only on their original partners.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For <heralded Bell-pair projection with two polarization parity filters>, a <Hadamard gate> followed by <Computational basis>[computational-basis] detection is a measurement in the diagonal <polarization> basis $|\pm\rangle=(|H\rangle\pm|V\rangle)/\sqrt2$. It can therefore be realized directly by a <polarizing beam splitter> at $45^\circ$ with a <photodetector> in each output: its transmitted and reflected clicks label $+$ and $-$. No separate <Hadamard gate> plate is required for this measurement.

Conditioning on one <photon> in each output of the first <polarizing beam splitter> implements the even $Z$-parity filter
$$
\Pi_Z^{25}=|00\rangle\langle00|+|11\rangle\langle11|=\frac{I+Z_2Z_5}{2}.
$$
The $45^\circ$ <polarizing beam splitter> similarly implements the even $X$-parity filter $\Pi_X^{46}=(I+X_4X_6)/2$. These are ideal coherent filters, with the optical <phases> chosen consistently. The all-zero <photodetector> record projects the upper outputs onto $\langle++|_{25}$ and the lower outputs onto $\langle00|_{46}$. The two effective bras are
$$
\langle++|\Pi_Z=\frac{\langle00|+\langle11|}{2}=\frac{\langle\Phi^+|}{\sqrt2},\qquad
\langle00|\Pi_X=\frac{\langle00|+\langle11|}{2}=\frac{\langle\Phi^+|}{\sqrt2}.
$$
Therefore the unnormalized retained state is
$$
\begin{aligned}
|\chi\rangle_{13}
&=\frac12\langle\Phi^+|_{25}\langle\Phi^+|_{46}
\bigl(|\Phi^+\rangle_{12}|\Phi^+\rangle_{34}|\Phi^+\rangle_{56}\bigr)\\
&=\frac18|\Phi^+\rangle_{13}.
\end{aligned}
$$
For example, the simultaneous Bell contractions require the three pair bits to coincide, leaving only the $000$ and $111$ terms of the original pair expansion. Each surviving $|00\rangle_{13}$ or $|11\rangle_{13}$ amplitude is $1/(8\sqrt2)$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{|\psi\rangle_{13}=|\Phi^+\rangle_{13},\qquad
P(0000\text{ and valid output paths})=\frac1{64}.}
$$
This <probability> is conditioned on production of all three input pairs, but not yet on successful <polarizing beam splitter> routing. The routing <probability> itself is $\langle\Pi_Z^{25}\Pi_X^{46}\rangle=1/4$: each nonidentity expectation vanishes on the relevant initial reduced state. Thus, if the question instead regards the routing <postselection> as already performed,
$$
\boxed{P(0000\mid\text{valid output paths})=\frac{1/64}{1/4}=\frac1{16}.}
$$
These two denominators describe different conditioning. Neither includes the <probability> that the three crystals actually emit the required pairs, <photodetector> inefficiency or undesired multipair events.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

A herald tells the experimenter when an entangled resource has actually been prepared while leaving the two resource <photons> available for subsequent operations. This permits synchronization, conditional storage or switching, and feed-forward. Direct down-conversion emission is probabilistic; inferring that a pair existed by destructively detecting both <photons> does not leave that same pair available as an input resource.

The second network uses passive optical elements, auxiliary <entanglement> and detection rather than a strong deterministic <photon>–<photon> <CNOT gate> interaction. Its smaller success rate can be acceptable if trials are inexpensive and successful events are reliably announced. Heralding is still probabilistic, not deterministic on demand; losses, dark counts and multiple-pair emission can give false or degraded heralds. The ideal $1/64$ result assumes the specified input pairs and valid detection model, so it should not be confused with a complete laboratory production efficiency.

== 6
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use $a|n\rangle=\sqrt n|n-1\rangle$, $a^\dagger|n\rangle=\sqrt{n+1}|n+1\rangle$, $\sigma_z|e\rangle=|e\rangle$ and $\sigma_z|g\rangle=-|g\rangle$. Only one interaction term acts on each of the two <quantum states>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathcal H|e,n\rangle=\hbar\left(n\omega+\frac{\omega_0}{2}\right)|e,n\rangle
+\hbar g\sqrt{n+1}|g,n+1\rangle,}
$$
$$
\boxed{\mathcal H|g,n+1\rangle=\hbar\left((n+1)\omega-\frac{\omega_0}{2}\right)|g,n+1\rangle
+\hbar g\sqrt{n+1}|e,n\rangle.}
$$
The interaction exchanges one atomic excitation with one field quantum. The square-root factor is the photon-number enhancement of the transition <matrix> element.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The total excitation operator $\mathcal N=a^\dagger a+(\sigma_z+1)/2$ commutes with the <Hamiltonian>. Each sector with $\mathcal N=n+1\geq1$ is therefore spanned by $|e,n\rangle,|g,n+1\rangle$, and no term connects it to a sector of different <excitation number>.

In that ordered basis, with the source's <Rabi detuning> $\Delta=\omega-\omega_0$,
$$
\boxed{\mathcal H_n=\hbar\omega\left(n+\frac12\right)I
+\hbar\begin{pmatrix}-\Delta/2&g\sqrt{n+1}\\g\sqrt{n+1}&\Delta/2\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The first term is the common sector <energy>; the remaining diagonal entries record the bare <Rabi detuning> and the off-diagonal terms exchange the excitation.

The <isolated ground sector of the Jaynes-Cummings model> is an important exception to a statement that the *entire* Hilbert space consists of such pairs: $|g,0\rangle$ has no partner and is annihilated by both interaction terms. It is a one-dimensional invariant sector with <energy> $-\hbar\omega_0/2$. The full decomposition is this one-dimensional sector plus all the two-dimensional sectors above.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Subtract the common sector <energy> and call the remaining <eigenvalue> $\hbar\lambda$. Its characteristic equation is
$$
\lambda^2-\frac{\Delta^2}{4}-g^2(n+1)=0.
$$
The dressed-state energies are consequently
$$
\boxed{E_{\pm n}=\hbar\omega\left(n+\frac12\right)\pm\frac\hbar2\Omega_n,
\qquad\Omega_n=\sqrt{\Delta^2+4g^2(n+1)}.}
$$
On resonance their splitting is $2\hbar|g|\sqrt{n+1}$; far off resonance the <eigenstates> approach the uncoupled <quantum states> with small dispersive <energy> shifts. The <eigenvalue> splitting is an angular-frequency difference $\Omega_n$, so an ordinary <frequency> would be $\Omega_n/(2\pi)$.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The requested bare labels require $n\geq1$. Their four energies are
$$
E_{e,n-1}=\hbar\omega(n-1)+\frac{\hbar\omega_0}{2},\quad
E_{g,n-1}=\hbar\omega(n-1)-\frac{\hbar\omega_0}{2},
$$
$$
E_{e,n}=\hbar\omega n+\frac{\hbar\omega_0}{2},\quad
E_{g,n}=\hbar\omega n-\frac{\hbar\omega_0}{2}.
$$
Among these four, the pair that is fully present in one interacting sector is $|e,n-1\rangle,|g,n\rangle$. Its center is $C=\hbar\omega(n-1/2)$ and its dressed branches are
$$
\boxed{E_{\pm,n-1}=C\pm\frac\hbar2\sqrt{(\omega-\omega_0)^2+4g^2n}.}
$$
Their endpoint values at $\omega_0=0$ and $2\omega$ are the same, $C\pm\hbar\sqrt{\omega^2+4g^2n}/2$. At $\omega_0=\omega$ they are $C\pm\hbar|g|\sqrt n$. The <avoided crossing> therefore has resonant gap
$$
\boxed{E_{+,n-1}-E_{-,n-1}=2\hbar|g|\sqrt n.}
$$
At the three horizontal-axis landmarks, the bare energies divided by $\hbar\omega$ are respectively
$$
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
&\omega_0/\omega=0&1&2\\\hline
|e,n-1\rangle&n-1&n-1/2&n\\
|g,n-1\rangle&n-1&n-3/2&n-2\\
|e,n\rangle&n&n+1/2&n+1\\
|g,n\rangle&n&n-1/2&n-1
\end{array}
$$
which specifies the vertical-axis labeling without assigning an unstated numerical $g$. The original plot below uses illustrative $n=3$ and $g/\omega=0.08$ and shows this complete central doublet together with all four bare lines.

\Image[2017-jaynes-cummings-avoided-crossing.png]
{title=Four bare levels and the complete dressed doublet contained among them in the <Jaynes-Cummings model>}

**Sector qualification:** the outer displayed state $|e,n\rangle$ couples to $|g,n+1\rangle$, outside the requested bare set. Likewise $|g,n-1\rangle$ couples to $|e,n-2\rangle$ if $n\geq2$; for $n=1$ it is the isolated $|g,0\rangle$. Thus the four-state set cannot be diagonalized as a closed four-dimensional block. If branches associated with these outer states are also desired, complete those neighboring sectors and use the formula in part c with indices $n$ and $n-2$.

Finally, “adjacent <dressed states>” within the shown resonant pair have the gap just boxed. Adjacent *rungs* of the dressed ladder are not equally spaced: at resonance,
$$
\boxed{E_{\pm,n}-E_{\pm,n-1}=\hbar\omega\pm\hbar|g|(\sqrt{n+1}-\sqrt n).}
$$
When neighboring doublets remain ordered, the gap between their nearest branches is $\hbar\omega-\hbar|g|(\sqrt{n+1}+\sqrt n)$. This distinguishes the within-doublet Rabi splitting from differences between adjacent excitation sectors.
