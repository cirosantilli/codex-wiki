An $R$-<module> $M$ is a <finitely generated module> if there are $m_1,\ldots,m_s\in M$ such that every element of $M$ has the form $r_1m_1+\cdots+r_sm_s$ with $r_i\in R$. Equivalently, **$M=Rm_1+\cdots+Rm_s$**, or $M$ is a <quotient module> of $R^s$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The <structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain> applies because every <Euclidean domain> is a <principal ideal domain>. In its cyclic decomposition form, every <finitely generated module> has a decomposition
$$
\boxed{M\cong R^r\oplus R/(d_1)\oplus\cdots\oplus R/(d_t),\qquad d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_t,}
$$
where the $d_i$ are nonzero nonunits. The nonnegative integer $r$ and the <invariant factors of a finitely generated module> $d_i$ are unique, with each $d_i$ determined up to multiplication by a unit. The zero module corresponds to no summands. Equivalently, factor the $d_i$ into powers of prime elements and use the <Chinese remainder theorem> to obtain a direct sum of cyclic prime-power <torsion modules> and a free summand.

Give $V$ a $k[t]$-<module> structure by $t\cdot v=\alpha v$. A finite <vector-space basis> generates it as a <module>. It is a <torsion module>: the <Cayley-Hamilton theorem> gives a nonzero polynomial annihilating all of $V$. Since $k[t]$ is a <Euclidean domain>, cyclic decomposition gives
$$
V\cong\bigoplus_{j=1}^s k[t]/(f_j),\qquad f_1\mid\cdots\mid f_s,
$$
with monic nonconstant <invariant factors of a linear operator> $f_j$ and no free summand. On $k[t]/(f_j)$, use the <basis> $1,t,\ldots,t^{\deg f_j-1}$. Multiplication by $t$ then has the <companion matrix> $C(f_j)$, because the relation $f_j(t)=0$ expresses the last power in terms of earlier ones. Concatenating these <bases> yields
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{diag}(C(f_1),\ldots,C(f_s)),}
$$
the <rational canonical form> of $\alpha$. In particular, its <minimal polynomial> is $f_s$ and its <characteristic polynomial> is $\prod_jf_j$.

A <simple module> is a nonzero <module> with no <submodules> other than zero and itself. Equivalently, **every nonzero element generates the entire <module>**: $Am=M$ whenever $m\ne0$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

A <composition series of a module> is a finite chain
$$
0=M_0\subsetneq M_1\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq M_r=M
$$
of <submodules> whose factors $M_j/M_{j-1}$ are <simple modules>. A finite-dimensional <module> has such a series by induction on its <dimension>, choosing a maximal proper <submodule> at each step.

The <Jordan–Hölder theorem for modules> states that **any two <composition series of a module> have the same length and the same simple factors up to <isomorphism> and reordering**. Thus the multiset of <composition factors> is an invariant of the <module>, even when the series itself is not unique.

{parent=iii}

The general <Schur's lemma> says that a homomorphism $f:M\to N$ between <simple modules> over any unital <ring> is either zero or an <isomorphism>. Indeed, $\ker f$ is a <submodule> of $M$ and $\operatorname{im}f$ is a <submodule> of $N$. If $f\ne0$, simplicity gives $\ker f=0$ and $\operatorname{im}f=N$. In particular, $\operatorname{End}_R(M)$ is a <division ring>.

For a complex <algebra> and a finite-dimensional simple <module> $M$, the stronger conclusion is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{End}_R(M)=\mathbb C\,\mathrm{id}_M.}
$$
For $f\in\operatorname{End}_R(M)$, the <fundamental theorem of algebra> gives an <eigenvalue> $\lambda\in\mathbb C$. The endomorphism $f-\lambda I$ has nonzero <kernel>, so the general <Schur's lemma> forces it to be zero. Consequently a homomorphism between two such simple <modules> is zero if they are nonisomorphic, and their homomorphism space is one-dimensional if they are isomorphic.

The finite-dimensional qualification is essential for this scalar conclusion. For example, the <field> $\mathbb C(t)$ is a complex <algebra>, simple as a <module> over itself, and its endomorphisms include multiplication by $t$, which is not a complex scalar.

Let $M$ be a nonzero <simple module> for $A=k[X]/(X^3)$ and let $N$ be the action of $X$. Since $X$ is central, $NM$ is a <submodule>, and $N^3=0$. If $NM=M$, iterating would give $N^3M=M$, impossible. Thus $NM=0$.

The action therefore factors through $A/(X)\cong k$. A simple <module> over a <field> is one-dimensional: the span of any nonzero vector is already a nonzero <submodule>. Conversely, $k$ with $X$ acting as zero is simple. Hence **there is exactly one simple <module> up to <isomorphism>**, namely
$$
\boxed{A/(X)\cong k,\qquad X\cdot v=0.}
$$
This argument covers all simple <modules>, without assuming their finite dimensionality in advance.

On a finite-dimensional $A$-<module> $M$, the actions of $X$ and $Y$ are endomorphisms $P,Q$ satisfying $PQ-QP=I_M$. Taking <traces> and using $\operatorname{tr}(PQ)=\operatorname{tr}(QP)$ gives

0=\operatorname{tr}(PQ-QP)=\operatorname{tr}(I_M)=\dim_kM\quad\text{in }k.

Since $k$ has <characteristic zero>, this forces $\dim_kM=0$. Thus **the zero <module> is the only finite-dimensional <module>** for this <Weyl algebra>. The obstruction is the <Weyl algebra has no nonzero finite-dimensional modules>.

The <group algebra> $kG$ is the <vector space> with <basis> $G$, whose elements are formal sums $\sum_{g\in G}a_gg$ with $a_g\in k$. Extend the <group> multiplication bilinearly:
$$
\left(\sum_ga_gg\right)\left(\sum_hb_hh\right)=\sum_{g,h}a_gb_h(gh).
$$
This is an associative unital $k$-<algebra>, with multiplicative identity the <group> identity, and $\dim_k kG=|G|$.

= Solution

Given a <group representation> $\rho:G\to\operatorname{GL}_n(k)$, define an action on $k^n$ by
$$
\boxed{\left(\sum_ga_gg\right)\cdot v=\sum_ga_g\rho(g)v.}
$$
It is bilinear. The identity acts as $I_n$, and
$$
\left(\sum_ga_g\rho(g)\right)\left(\sum_hb_h\rho(h)\right)=\sum_{g,h}a_gb_h\rho(gh),
$$
because $\rho$ is a <group homomorphism>. Hence the product action agrees with successive action, proving the <module> axioms. Thus every <group representation> extends uniquely by linearity to a $kG$-<module>.

A <semisimple module> is a direct sum of <simple modules>. A finite-dimensional <semisimple algebra> is one whose left regular <module> is semisimple; equivalently, all its finite-dimensional <modules> are semisimple, or every <submodule> of a finite-dimensional <module> has a complementary <submodule>.

The <Maschke theorem> states that, for a finite <group> $G$ and a <field> $k$ with $|G|\ne0$ in $k$, every finite-dimensional $kG$-<module> is semisimple, so $kG$ is a <semisimple algebra>. The assumption is equivalent to saying that the <characteristic of a field> does not divide $|G|$. The averaging construction makes a projection onto a <submodule> equivariant by replacing $P$ with $|G|^{-1}\sum_{g\in G}gPg^{-1}$.

Let $w=\sum_{g\in G}g$. This is nonzero since its <basis> coefficients are all one, and $gw=w$ for every $g\in G$. Hence $kw$ is a one-dimensional left ideal of $kG$.

If the regular <module> is semisimple, $kw$ is a direct summand. Let $\pi:kG\to kw$ be the corresponding $kG$-linear projection, so $\pi(w)=w$. Write $\pi(1)=cw$ for $c\in k$. Then
$$
w=\pi(w)=\sum_{g\in G}\pi(g)=\sum_{g\in G}g\pi(1)=c|G|w.
$$
Because $w\ne0$, $c|G|=1$ in $k$. Thus
$$
\boxed{|G|\ne0\text{ in }k.}
$$
Together with the <Maschke theorem>, this gives the exact <semisimple algebra> criterion for a <group algebra>.

For the <Abelian> case of <Cauchy theorem for groups>, use induction on $|G|$. Choose $a\ne e$. If $p$ divides $m=|a|$, then $a^{m/p}$ has order $p$. Otherwise $\langle a\rangle$ is a nontrivial <normal subgroup> of order prime to $p$, and $G/\langle a\rangle$ has smaller order still divisible by $p$. Induction gives a coset of order $p$; any lift $b$ has order divisible by $p$, because the order of its <image> divides its own order. A suitable power of $b$ then has order $p$. This uses no classification of finite <Abelian groups>.

For a finite <p-group>, the <class equation> is
$$
|G|=|Z(G)|+\sum_{[g]\ne\{z\}}[G:C_G(g)],
$$
where the sum is over noncentral <conjugacy classes>. Each index is a positive power of $p$, hence divisible by $p$. Therefore $p$ divides $|Z(G)|$, and the <center of a group> is nontrivial.

If $|G|=p^n$ with $n>1$ and $Z(G)\ne G$, this is already a proper nontrivial <normal subgroup>. If $Z(G)=G$, the <group> is <Abelian>; the established case of <Cauchy theorem for groups> gives a subgroup of order $p$, which is normal and proper. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{No group of order }p^n\ (n>1)\text{ is simple}.}
$$

= Solution

If $|G|=p^am$ with $p\nmid m$, a <Sylow p-subgroup> has order $p^a$. The <Sylow theorems> say that such subgroups exist; every <p-subgroup> is contained in one; they are all conjugate; and their number $n_p$ divides $m$ and satisfies $n_p\equiv1\pmod p$.

Suppose $|G|=p^2q$ with distinct primes and $G$ were simple. A <Sylow subgroup> of order $q$ has order $q$, and $n_q>1$ divides $p^2$, so $n_q=p$ or $p^2$. If $n_q=p$, conjugation gives a nontrivial homomorphism $G\to S_p$. Its <kernel> is normal and, by simplicity, trivial. Hence $p^2q$ would divide $p!$ by <Lagrange's theorem>, impossible because $p!$ has only one factor of $p$.

If $n_q=p^2$, distinct <Sylow subgroups> of order $q$ meet only in the identity and together contain $p^2(q-1)$ nonidentity elements. Only $p^2$ elements of $G$ remain, including the identity. Every <Sylow p-subgroup> lies entirely in that remaining set and itself has $p^2$ elements. They must therefore all be the same subgroup, making it normal, again a contradiction. Thus **no group of order $p^2q$ is simple**. If $p=q$, the preceding <p-group> argument applies.

For distinct $p,q$, a finite <Abelian group> is the <direct product of groups> of its primary subgroups. A group of order $p^2$ is either cyclic or has every nonidentity element of order $p$, in which case choosing two independent elements gives $C_p\times C_p$. Consequently the possibilities are
$$
\boxed{C_{p^2q},\qquad C_p\times C_{pq}\quad(p\ne q).}
$$
If the primes coincide, the <fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups> gives instead $C_{p^3}$, $C_{p^2}\times C_p$, and $C_p^3$. This classification is used only for the requested list, not for the proof above.

First establish the two general claims about <polycyclic groups>. Intersect a subnormal cyclic-factor series $(G_i)$ with $H\leq G$. Then $H\cap G_i\trianglelefteq H\cap G_{i+1}$, and the inclusion induces an <injective> homomorphism
$$
\frac{H\cap G_{i+1}}{H\cap G_i}\longrightarrow\frac{G_{i+1}}{G_i}.
$$
A subgroup of a <cyclic group> is cyclic, so this is a cyclic-factor <subnormal series> for $H$, after omitting repetitions. This proves <subgroups of polycyclic groups are polycyclic>.

To prove the generator bound, choose $g_i\in G_i$ whose coset generates $G_i/G_{i-1}$ whenever that factor is nontrivial. Inductively $G_i=\langle G_{i-1},g_i\rangle$, so $G=\langle g_1,\ldots,g_n\rangle$. This is the <generator bound from a cyclic-factor series>: **at most $n$ generators suffice**.

For the first example, take $G=C_2^m$ with its coordinate flag
$$
0<C_2<C_2^2<\cdots<C_2^m.
$$
It has length exactly $m$ and each factor is $C_2$, so all factors are cyclic.

In a finite <group>, choose a maximal chain of <normal subgroups> from $1$ to $G$. Finiteness ensures such a chain exists, and maximality leaves no <normal subgroup> of $G$ strictly between successive terms. This is a <chief series>.

The <infinite cyclic group has no chief series>. In any finite chain starting at zero, the first nonzero subgroup is $n\mathbb Z$. But $2n\mathbb Z$ is a nonzero proper subgroup of it and is normal in $\mathbb Z$. Thus zero is not maximal among the normal subgroups in that first term, contradicting the definition.

For any <chief series>, $G_{n-1}$ is a maximal proper <normal subgroup> of $G$. The <subgroup correspondence theorem> identifies normal subgroups of $G/G_{n-1}$ with normal subgroups of $G$ containing $G_{n-1}$. Consequently **the top chief factor is simple**.

Other <chief factors> need only have no proper nonzero subgroup invariant under the action of the whole <group>. In $A_4$,
$$
\boxed{1<V_4<A_4}
$$
is a <chief series>, where $V_4=\{1,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}$. Conjugation in $A_4$ permutes the three involutions transitively, so none of its three order-two subgroups is normal in $A_4$. Thus $V_4$ is a minimal nontrivial <normal subgroup>; the top quotient is $C_3$. But the bottom <chief factor> $V_4/1\cong C_2\times C_2$ is **not simple**, since it has proper order-two normal subgroups as an abstract <group>.

A complex <character of a representation> of a finite <group> $G$ is the function
$$
\boxed{\chi(g)=\operatorname{tr}(\rho(g)),}
$$
where $\rho:G\to\operatorname{GL}(V)$ is a finite-dimensional complex <group representation>. The <character> is a <class function> because <trace> is unchanged by conjugation. Its degree is $\chi(1)=\dim V$.

= Solution

The <row orthogonality relations for a character table> assert that distinct complex <irreducible characters> are orthogonal and each has squared <norm> one:
$$
\langle\chi_i,\chi_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}.
$$
Using the linearity and conjugate-linearity of the <character inner product>,
$$
\langle\psi,\psi\rangle=\sum_{i,j}\lambda_i\overline{\lambda_j}\langle\chi_i,\chi_j\rangle=\sum_i|\lambda_i|^2.
$$
Since the multiplicities are nonnegative integers, this is
$$
\boxed{\langle\psi,\psi\rangle=\sum_i\lambda_i^2.}
$$

By the <Maschke theorem>, the restricted <representation> is a direct sum of irreducible $H$-<modules>. The <row orthogonality relations for a character table> identify the multiplicity of $\psi_i$ as $d_i=\langle\chi\mathord\downarrow_H,\psi_i\rangle_H$. Therefore
$$
\chi\mathord\downarrow_H=\sum_i d_i\psi_i,\qquad d_i\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}.
$$
Using the preceding <character inner product> identity and the irreducibility of $\chi$,
$$
\sum_i d_i^2=\frac1{|H|}\sum_{h\in H}|\chi(h)|^2\leq\frac1{|H|}\sum_{g\in G}|\chi(g)|^2=\frac{|G|}{|H|}.
$$
This is the <character restriction norm bound>. When $[G:H]=2$, the nonzero sum of squares of integer multiplicities is either one or two. Hence **the restriction is irreducible, or is the sum of two distinct <irreducible characters>**, each with multiplicity one. This proves the requested <index-two character restriction dichotomy> directly; normality of an index-two subgroup is a general <group theory> fact.

Use the column order $1,(123),(12)(34),(12345),(13452)$ in $A_5$. The two restrictions of degree four coincide and equal $\theta_4=(4,1,0,-1,-1)$; those of degree five coincide and equal $\theta_5=(5,-1,1,0,0)$. The <centralizer> orders give
$$
\langle\theta_4,\theta_4\rangle=\frac{16}{60}+\frac13+\frac15+\frac15=1,\qquad
\langle\theta_5,\theta_5\rangle=\frac{25}{60}+\frac13+\frac14=1.
$$
Thus both are <irreducible characters>. The remaining given restriction is $\eta=(6,0,-2,1,1)$, and
$$
\langle\eta,\eta\rangle=\frac{36}{60}+\frac44+\frac15+\frac15=2.
$$
It splits as two distinct <irreducible characters>. Neither is $1$, $\theta_4$ or $\theta_5$, since their <character inner products> with $\eta$ are zero. A finite <group> has as many complex <irreducible characters> as <conjugacy classes>, so these five exhaust them. The sum of squares of irreducible degrees is $|A_5|=60$. The first three degrees contribute $1+16+25=42$, leaving $18$ for the two constituents, whose degrees sum to six. Hence both have degree three.

Write one constituent as $\theta=(3,x,y,u,v)$. All five <conjugacy classes> are closed under inversion, so all its values are real: $\chi(g^{-1})=\overline{\chi(g)}$. Orthogonality with $1$, $\theta_4$ and $\theta_5$ gives
$$
\frac3{60}+\frac x3+\frac y4+\frac{u+v}5=0,\qquad
\frac{12}{60}+\frac x3-\frac{u+v}5=0,\qquad
\frac{15}{60}-\frac x3+\frac y4=0.
$$
Solving gives $x=0$, $y=-1$ and $u+v=1$. The squared <norm> one gives $9/60+1/4+(u^2+v^2)/5=1$, so $u^2+v^2=3$ and $uv=-1$. Thus $u,v$ are the roots of $t^2-t-1$, namely $\varphi=(1+\sqrt5)/2$ and $\bar\varphi=(1-\sqrt5)/2$. Here the bar labels the other real root, rather than complex conjugation. The other constituent is $\eta-\theta$, so the two last values are interchanged.

The <character table of the alternating group A5> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|rrrrr}
&1&(123)&(12)(34)&(12345)&(13452)\\\hline
1&1&1&1&1&1\\
\theta_3&3&0&-1&\varphi&\bar\varphi\\
\theta'_3&3&0&-1&\bar\varphi&\varphi\\
\theta_4&4&1&0&-1&-1\\
\theta_5&5&-1&1&0&0
\end{array}.}
$$
The <conjugacy class> sizes are $1,20,15,12,12$. A <normal subgroup> is a union of entire <conjugacy classes> containing the identity, and its order divides $60$ by <Lagrange's theorem>. A nontrivial such union has size at least $13$; the only proper divisors of $60$ at least $13$ are $15,20,30$. Subtracting the identity would require a sum of some of $20,15,12,12$ equal to $14,19,29$, respectively, none of which is possible. Thus **$A_5$ is simple**.
