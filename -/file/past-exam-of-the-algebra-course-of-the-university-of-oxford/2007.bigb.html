A left <module> over the unital $K$-<algebra> $A$ is a $K$-<vector space> $V$ with a $K$-bilinear action $A\times V\to V$ satisfying $(ab)v=a(bv)$ and $1v=v$. For a vector $v$, the map $A\to V$, $a\mapsto av$, is an $A$-<module homomorphism>. Its <kernel> is $\operatorname{Ann}(v)$; explicitly this is a <linear subspace> and $b(av)=(ba)v=0$ for $a\in\operatorname{Ann}(v)$, so it is a <left ideal>.

If $V=Av$, this homomorphism is <surjective>, and the <module isomorphism> theorem gives $V\cong A/\operatorname{Ann}(v)$. Conversely the quotient by a <left ideal> is generated by $1+I$. Thus **<cyclic modules> are exactly the quotients $A/I$ by <left ideals>**.

For the first example set $e=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,0)$. Right multiplication by $e$ keeps the first two columns and zeroes the third. Every <matrix> with third column zero equals its product with $e$. Hence **$V=Ae$ is cyclic**, generated by $e$.

A <simple module> is nonzero and has no <submodules> other than zero and itself. A <semisimple module> is a <direct sum> of simple <submodules>; the zero <module> is allowed as the empty sum.

For (1)$\Rightarrow$(2), argue by <dimension>. Choose a minimal nonzero <submodule> $S$, which is simple, and split $V=S\oplus C$ using (1). The property passes to $C$: for $U\subseteq C$, write $V=U\oplus D$; then $C=U\oplus(C\cap D)$. Apply induction to $C$ and append $S$. The zero-dimensional case starts the induction. The implication (2)$\Rightarrow$(3) is immediate. For (3)$\Rightarrow$(2), repeatedly append a simple <submodule> not contained in the sum already chosen. Its intersection with that sum is zero by simplicity. <Dimension> strictly increases, so after finitely many steps their <direct sum> is $V$.

For (2)$\Rightarrow$(1), write $V=\bigoplus_iS_i$ and fix $U$. Choose a sum $C$ of a maximal subset of these $S_i$ whose sum is disjoint from $U$. If $U+C\ne V$, some $S_j$ is not contained in $U+C$, so simplicity gives $S_j\cap(U+C)=0$. Then $C+S_j$ is a larger <direct sum> still disjoint from $U$, a contradiction. Hence $V=U\oplus C$.

Condition (1) implies (1*). Conversely suppose (1*) and let $W$ be the sum of all simple <submodules>. If $W\ne V$, finite <dimension> supplies a maximal proper <submodule> $M$ containing $W$. By (1*), $V=M\oplus C$. Projection to the simple quotient $V/M$ identifies the nonzero complement $C$ with a <simple module>. Thus $C\subseteq W\subseteq M$, contradicting the <direct sum>. Consequently $W=V$, which is (3). This proves **all four conditions are equivalent**.

For the concrete complement, $x^2=x$. <Orthogonal idempotents> $x$ and $1-x$ give $A=Ax\oplus A(1-x)$: the sum follows from $1=x+(1-x)$, and right multiplication by $x$ shows their intersection is zero. In explicit <matrix> form,
$$
\boxed{U=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&\lambda a\\b&\lambda b\end{pmatrix}:a,b\in K\right\},\qquad C=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0&c\\0&d\end{pmatrix}:c,d\in K\right\}=A(1-x).}
$$
The decomposition of $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$ uses second-column remainders $b-\lambda a,d-\lambda c$ in $C$.

Each $M_i$ is an <ideal> because the <algebra> is commutative. For $m\in M_i$, the product $me_j$ belongs both to $M_i$ and $M_j$, so is zero when $j\ne i$. Multiplying $1=\sum_je_j$ by $m$ therefore gives $m=me_i$. In particular $e_i^2=e_i$, different $e_i$ have zero product, and
$$
\boxed{M_i=Ae_i,\qquad e_i^2=e_i,\qquad e_ie_j=0\ (i\ne j).}
$$
For any <idempotent> $e$, the set $Ae$ is closed under multiplication and $e$ acts as its identity. With $f=1-e$ the map $a\mapsto(ae,af)$ is an <algebra> isomorphism $A\to Ae\times Af$: multiplication is componentwise because $ef=0$, and its inverse sends $(u,v)$ to $u+v$.

The $A$-<submodules> of $Ae$ are exactly its <ideals> as a unital <algebra>, since acting by $a$ on $Ae$ is the same as acting by $ae$. A nonzero commutative unital <algebra> is a <simple module> over itself exactly when every nonzero principal <ideal> is the whole <algebra>, which is equivalent to every nonzero element having an inverse. Thus **a nonzero component $Ae$ is simple exactly when it is a <field>**. If $e=0$, both properties fail under the nonzero-<field> convention.

A semisimple regular <module> is a finite <direct sum> of simple <ideals>. Applying the preceding decomposition gives a finite direct product of <fields>. Conversely the coordinate <ideals> of a finite product of <fields> are <simple modules>, so its regular <module> is semisimple. The <fields> are finite extensions of $K$, because $A$ has finite $K$-<dimension>.

For a generator $g$ of the <cyclic group> of order three, evaluation $X\mapsto g$ gives a surjection $\mathbb Q[X]\to\mathbb QG$ with <kernel> $(X^3-1)$, by division and independence of $1,g,g^2$. The factors $X-1$ and $X^2+X+1$ are coprime, and the quadratic is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$. The <Chinese remainder theorem> yields
$$
\boxed{\mathbb QG\cong\frac{\mathbb Q[X]}{(X^3-1)}\cong\mathbb Q\times\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3}).}
$$
The explicit map sends $g$ to $(1,\zeta_3)$, where $\zeta_3=(-1+\sqrt{-3})/2$.

An $R$-<module isomorphism> $T:V_\alpha\to V_\beta$ is a $K$-linear isomorphism satisfying $T\alpha=\beta T$, by applying it to multiplication by $X$. Conversely that identity implies $Tf(\alpha)=f(\beta)T$ for every <polynomial>, so a similarity is an $R$-<module isomorphism>.

The cyclic decomposition theorem over the <principal ideal domain> $R=K[X]$ states that any <finitely generated module> is
$$
R^r\oplus R/(d_1)\oplus\cdots\oplus R/(d_s),\qquad d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_s,
$$
with nonunit monic invariant factors, uniquely determined, and free rank $r$ uniquely determined. A finite-dimensional $K$-space has $r=0$. In each cyclic summand the basis $1,X,\ldots,X^{\deg d_i-1}$ makes multiplication by $X$ the <companion matrix> of $d_i$. Their block diagonal sum is the <rational canonical form>.

Here the two <polynomials> are already in divisibility order. With the companion convention having ones below the diagonal,
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{RCF}(\alpha)=\operatorname{diag}\left(\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\1&0&-3\\0&1&3\end{pmatrix}\right).}
$$
Integer coefficients are interpreted in $K$, so this remains valid in characteristics two and three.

In the cyclic case there is one companion block $C(d)$. Its defining <polynomial> is both characteristic and minimal: the vector represented by $1$ is cyclic and a <polynomial> annihilates it exactly when divisible by $d$. Thus **the <minimal polynomial> is read directly from that block**.

The <eigenvalues> in $K$ are precisely the roots in $K$ of the companion <polynomial> $d$; over an <algebraic closure> take all its roots. Thus the form determines their root set, but **an explicit list generally requires factoring or solving $d$ and cannot be read off by inspection**. This is the relevant distinction in the qualification “with no further calculations”. Over a general <field> the <polynomial> may have no roots in $K$ at all, and its diagonal entries are not its <eigenvalues>. If “determinable” means only uniquely specified rather than immediately readable, all the information is of course fixed by the <matrix>, including this root set.

For $d(X)=X^n+a_{n-1}X^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0$, the <determinant> of its <companion matrix> is $(-1)^na_0$. Therefore

In the cyclic quotient $R/(d)$, the <kernel> is formed by residue classes of <polynomials> $h$ such that $d\mid Xh$. If $X\nmid d$, coprimality forces $d\mid h$, so the <kernel> is zero. If $d=Xe$, divisibility is equivalent to $e\mid h$, and the residue classes of multiples of $e$ modulo $Xe$ form a one-dimensional $K$-space. Hence
$$
\boxed{\dim\ker\alpha=\begin{cases}0,&d(0)\ne0,\\1,&d(0)=0.\end{cases}}
$$
This too is immediately readable in the cyclic case, even if zero is a repeated root. The zero-dimensional <module> has <kernel> <dimension> zero separately.

For $|G|=p^rm$ with $p\nmid m$, Sylow's theorem gives subgroups of order $p^r$, says that every $p$-subgroup lies in a conjugate of one of them, and that all such <Sylow subgroups> are conjugate. Their number $n_p$ divides $m$ and satisfies $n_p\equiv1\pmod p$. A <group> is soluble when its <derived series>, obtained by repeatedly taking the <commutator subgroup>, reaches the identity. Equivalently it has a subnormal series with abelian factors.

For a <group> of order $p^2$, the <class equation> shows its center is nontrivial: noncentral conjugacy classes have sizes divisible by $p$. If the center had order $p$, the quotient by the center would be cyclic; a <group> with cyclic central quotient is abelian, since representatives are powers of one element times central elements. This would make the center the whole <group>. Thus **every <group> of order $p^2$ is abelian**.

Now consider $|G|=pq^a$ with $0\leq a\leq2$. Cases with a normal <Sylow subgroup> are soluble: that subgroup has prime or prime-square order and is abelian, and its quotient also has such order and is abelian. This gives a subnormal series with abelian factors.

If $p<q$, the Sylow number $n_q$ divides $p$ and is $1$ modulo $q$, so is one. If $p>q$ and $a\leq1$, $n_p$ divides $q^a<p$, so again is one. For $p>q$ and $a=2$, a nontrivial $n_p$ can only be $q^2$, since $q<p$. The congruence would force $p\mid(q-1)(q+1)$, hence $p\mid q+1$. Since $p>q$, this means $p=q+1$, and primality forces $(p,q)=(3,2)$.

In the remaining order-twelve case, either a <Sylow subgroup> is already normal, or there are four <Sylow subgroups> of order three. Those have pairwise trivial intersection and contribute eight distinct elements of order three. Only four elements remain, including the identity. Every <Sylow subgroup> of order four lies entirely among those four elements, so it is unique and normal. It and its quotient are abelian. Therefore **all <groups> of the required orders are soluble**, by elementary Sylow arguments alone.

A <composition series> is a chain $1=G_0\triangleleft G_1\triangleleft\cdots\triangleleft G_r=G$ with simple nontrivial factors. Every <finite group> has one, by recursively choosing a maximal proper <normal subgroup>. The <Jordan-Hölder theorem> states that any two such series have the same length and the same multiset of simple factors up to isomorphism.

For a proof induct on $|G|$. Let $M,N$ be the penultimate terms in two series. If $M=N$, apply induction there. Otherwise their maximal normality gives $MN=G$. Set $D=M\cap N$. The second isomorphism theorem gives
$$
M/D\cong G/N,\qquad N/D\cong G/M,
$$
so these quotients are simple. Choose a <composition series> of $D$ and extend it once to $M$ and once to $N$. Induction in the smaller <groups> $M,N$ compares these with the respective original prefixes. Appending $G/M$ or $G/N$ gives in both cases the factors of $D$ together with $G/M,G/N$. This completes the proof, including the case $D=1$.

**Exactly one:** take $S_5$. Its only nontrivial proper <normal subgroup> is $A_5$. Indeed a normal $N$ has $N\cap A_5$ either $1$ or $A_5$ by the supplied simplicity. In the first case $N$ injects into $S_5/A_5\cong C_2$, so a nontrivial $N$ would be a <normal subgroup> of order two and hence central. But $S_5$ has trivial center: conjugating every transposition trivially forces preservation of each unordered pair, and therefore of every point. Thus the unique series is $1\triangleleft A_5\triangleleft S_5$.

**Exactly two:** take $A_5\times C_2$. A <normal subgroup> containing $A_5\times1$ is that subgroup or the whole product. For a <normal subgroup> $N$ meeting $A_5\times1$ trivially, commutators of its elements with $A_5\times1$ vanish; its first coordinates therefore lie in $Z(A_5)=1$. Hence $N\subseteq1\times C_2$. The two nontrivial proper <normal subgroups> are exactly the two factors, each simple, so the two series pass respectively through $A_5\times1$ and $1\times C_2$.

**More than two:** take $C_{120}$. A <cyclic group> has exactly one subgroup of each divisor order, and composition steps have prime indices. The orders of those indices are the distinct permutations of $2,2,2,3,5$, giving
$$
\boxed{5!/3!=20\text{ composition series}.}
$$
All three example <groups> have order $120$.

An extension of $A$ by $B$ is a <short exact sequence> $1\to A\to G\to B\to1$. It splits if the quotient map has a homomorphic section. Equivalently the embedded $A$ has a subgroup complement isomorphic to $B$, and $G$ is the corresponding <semidirect product>.

For $t\notin K$ and $a\in K$, both $t$ and $ta$ lie outside $K$, so $t^2=(ta)^2=1$. It follows that $tat=a^{-1}$, and $t=t^{-1}$ gives the claimed <conjugation> identity. <Conjugation> is an <automorphism> of $K$, so inversion is an <automorphism> there. Comparing the image of $ab$ with the product of the images gives $b^{-1}a^{-1}=a^{-1}b^{-1}$. Therefore **$K$ is abelian**.

Conversely inversion is an <automorphism> of any <abelian group> $A$, of order dividing two. Form the <semidirect product> $G=A\rtimes C_2$ with multiplication
$$
(a,\varepsilon)(b,\delta)=(ab^{(-1)^\varepsilon},\varepsilon+\delta),\qquad\varepsilon,\delta\in\mathbb Z/2.
$$
For completeness associativity follows because $b\mapsto b^{(-1)^\varepsilon}$ is an <automorphism> and these automorphisms compose according to addition modulo two; $(1,0)$ is the identity and inverses follow from this formula. The subgroup $H=A\times\{0\}$ is normal with quotient $C_2$, and every outside element satisfies $(a,1)^2=(aa^{-1},0)=1$. Thus **the <generalized dihedral group> supplies the required extension**, including when $A$ is infinite or has elements of order two.

The degree-sum formula for <irreducible characters> gives
$$
\boxed{|G|=1^2+1^2+1^2+3^2+3^2=21.}
$$
The number of <linear characters> is the order of the <abelianization>, so **$|G/G'|=3$** and $|G'|=7$. The intersection of the kernels of all <linear characters> is $G'$. Consequently $g_4,g_5$ are nonidentity elements of this order-seven subgroup and have order seven. The images of $g_2,g_3$ under a displayed <linear character> have order three, so their orders are divisible by three. <Lagrange's theorem> leaves three or twenty-one; an element of order twenty-one would make $G$ cyclic, contradicting its degree-three <irreducible characters>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{(|g_2|,|g_3|,|g_4|,|g_5|)=(3,3,7,7).}
$$
The center is trivial. A nontrivial proper center would have order three or seven, giving a cyclic quotient and hence an <abelian group>, again a contradiction. Thus a <centralizer> of a nonidentity element is proper. It has order three for an order-three element, and seven for an order-seven element. The class sizes are therefore $1,7,7,3,3$.

Column orthogonality says $\sum_i|\chi_i(g)|^2=|C_G(g)|$. For $g_2,g_3$, the three <linear characters> already contribute three, so the remaining two entries must both vanish. Orthogonality with the identity column says $\sum_i\chi_i(1)\overline{\chi_i(g)}=0$ for $g\ne1$. At $g_4$ this gives $1+\alpha+\chi_5(g_4)=0$, so $\chi_5(g_4)=\beta$, using $\alpha+\beta=-1$. Similarly $\chi_5(g_5)=\alpha$. Thus the completed table is
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
&e&g_2&g_3&g_4&g_5\\\hline
\text{class size}&1&7&7&3&3\\
\chi_1&1&1&1&1&1\\
\chi_2&1&\omega&\omega^2&1&1\\
\chi_3&1&\omega^2&\omega&1&1\\
\chi_4&3&0&0&\alpha&\beta\\
\chi_5&3&0&0&\beta&\alpha
\end{array}}
$$
Here $\beta=\overline\alpha$, $|\alpha|^2=|\beta|^2=2$ and $\alpha\beta=2$. These relations, together with $1+\omega+\omega^2=0$, verify all weighted row orthogonality relations and the <centralizer> column norms.
