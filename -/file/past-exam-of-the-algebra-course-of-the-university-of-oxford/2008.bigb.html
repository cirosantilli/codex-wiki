An <algebra homomorphism> is a $K$-<linear map> preserving multiplication, and preserving the unit under the unital convention. An <algebra isomorphism> is a bijective <algebra homomorphism>; its inverse then preserves the same operations.

**A <commutative> domain <algebra> may map into a <noncommutative> target:** take $A=K$, $B=K\times M_2(K)$, and $\varphi(\lambda)=(\lambda,\lambda I_2)$. This is even an injective unital homomorphism. The matrix factor makes the target <noncommutative> over every <field>, since $E_{12}E_{21}\ne E_{21}E_{12}$.

**A <noncommutative> <algebra> may have a <commutative> quotient:** take $A=K\times M_2(K)$, $B=K$, and $\varphi(\lambda,M)=\lambda$. This is a surjective unital homomorphism. Neither requested change of commutativity can occur for an isomorphism, since an isomorphism transfers the identity $ab=ba$ in both directions.

For the matrix classifications, write $E_{ij}$ for a <matrix unit> and $N=E_{12}+E_{23}$. Then $N^2=E_{13}$ and $N^3=0$. The complete square-zero sets and commutativity results are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
 &\text{commutative?}&\{\alpha:\alpha^2=0\}\\\hline
A_1&\text{yes}&\{0\}\\
A_2&\text{yes}&KE_{12}+KE_{13}\\
A_3&\text{yes}&KE_{13}\\
A_4&\text{no}&KE_{12}\\
A_5&\text{yes}&KE_{13}+KE_{23}.
\end{array}
$$
Indeed $A_1$ is diagonal. In $A_2$ and $A_5$ the two off-diagonal generators have all products zero, so multiplication has the form $(x,u)(x',u')=(xx',xu'+x'u)$. The diagonal entries of a square force $x=0$, after which the off-diagonal part squares to zero. In $A_3=K[N]$, a square-zero element first has scalar coefficient zero and then $(yN+zN^2)^2=y^2N^2$, forcing $y=0$. This argument works also in characteristic two. In $A_4$, a square first forces both diagonal parameters to vanish, leaving precisely $KE_{12}$. Noncommutativity follows from $E_{22}E_{12}=0$ but $E_{12}E_{22}=E_{12}$.

The coordinate map $xI+yE_{12}+zE_{13}\mapsto xI+yE_{13}+zE_{23}$ is an isomorphism $A_2\to A_5$, by the common multiplication formula. Every other pair is nonisomorphic: $A_4$ is the only <noncommutative> member, and among the <commutative> members the dimensions of the square-zero sets are respectively zero, two, one, and two. These sets are vector spaces in these examples, and an <algebra isomorphism> must carry one onto the other. Thus **the only distinct isomorphic pair is $A_2\cong A_5$**.

A <composition series> is a chain $0=V_0\subsetneq V_1\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq V_r=V$ of submodules with every factor $V_i/V_{i-1}$ simple; its length is $r$. Suppose $0\subset S\subset V$ is one of length two, and put $T=V/S$.

For any nonzero proper <submodule> $U$, either $U\cap S=S$ or $U\cap S=0$, by simplicity of $S$. In the first case, simplicity of $V/S$ forces $U=S$. In the second, the quotient map embeds $U$ nontrivially into $T$, so $U\cong T$ and its <image> is all of $T$. Hence $V=S\oplus U$, and $V/U\cong S$. Thus every nonzero proper <submodule> is simple and has simple quotient; every <composition series> has length two.

The <Jordan-Hölder theorem> states that any two <composition series> of a finite-length module have equal length and the same multiset of simple factors, up to isomorphism and order. The preceding argument proves this for length two: either $U=S$, giving factors $S,T$, or $U\ne S$, giving factors $T,S$.

The map $A\to S$, $a\mapsto ax$, is a surjective <module homomorphism> with <kernel> $I=\operatorname{Ann}_A(x)$. The <first isomorphism theorem for modules> gives $A/I\cong S$ as left modules; $I$ need only be a <left ideal>. Every <simple module> is cyclic, since any nonzero $x$ generates a nonzero <submodule>. As a quotient of the finite-dimensional regular module $A$, it is finite-dimensional. Extend $I$ to a <composition series> of $A$; its simple quotient $A/I$ is one of the factors. Jordan-Hölder shows that its isomorphism type belongs to the fixed finite list of factors of any one <composition series> of $A$. Hence **there are finitely many simple-module isomorphism types**.

For the final assertion let $W$ be the <image> of $\psi$. Its <kernel> is $\bigcap I_j$. We prove $W=\prod S_j$ by induction on $k$. It is true for $k=1$. Projection onto the first $k-1$ factors is onto by induction. Its <kernel> on $W$ is a <submodule> of $S_k$, so is zero or $S_k$. If zero, $W\cong\prod_{j<k}S_j$. But its projection onto $S_k$ is onto, making $S_k$ a <composition factor> of that product, contrary to Jordan-Hölder and the pairwise distinct types. Thus the <kernel> is all of $S_k$. Surjectivity on the first factors together with this <kernel> proves $W$ is the entire product. The first isomorphism theorem now gives
$$
\boxed{A/\bigcap_{j=1}^kI_j\cong S_1\times\cdots\times S_k}
$$
as left $A$-modules, without asserting that the left ideals are two-sided.

A <simple module> is a nonzero module with no proper nonzero <submodule>. For a finite-dimensional simple complex module $S$, any <endomorphism> $\varphi$ has a complex <eigenvalue> $\lambda$. The nonzero <kernel> of $\varphi-\lambda I$ is an $A$-<submodule>, hence all of $S$. Thus **$\varphi=\lambda I$**, proving <Schur's lemma>.

More generally a nonzero map between two simple modules is an isomorphism, because its <kernel> is zero and its <image> is the whole target. The <endomorphism> <algebra> of $S_1\oplus S_2$ is the block <algebra> with entries $\operatorname{Hom}_A(S_j,S_i)$. For nonisomorphic summands its off-diagonal entries vanish and its diagonal entries are scalars, giving $\mathbb C\times\mathbb C$. For isomorphic summands, choose one fixed isomorphism to identify them; every block is scalar and block composition is matrix multiplication, giving $M_2(\mathbb C)$.

A finite-dimensional <algebra> is a <semisimple algebra> when its <left regular module> is a <direct sum> of simple modules. The complex <Artin-Wedderburn theorem> gives $A\cong\prod_iM_{d_i}(\mathbb C)$, with the multiset of positive integers $d_i$ uniquely determined. Its <dimension> is $\sum_i d_i^2$. For <dimension> eight only sizes one and two can occur; the number of size-two blocks is zero, one, or two. Thus there are **exactly three isomorphism types**:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb C^8,\quad M_2(\mathbb C)\times\mathbb C^4,\quad M_2(\mathbb C)\times M_2(\mathbb C).}
$$
They are distinct by the uniqueness clause, or by their numbers of one-dimensional simple modules. If $G$ is nonabelian, $\mathbb CG$ is <noncommutative> because its group elements form a basis, so the first type is excluded. The augmentation $\sum a_gg\mapsto\sum a_g$ provides a one-dimensional <simple module>, excluding the third type. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\mathbb CG\cong\mathbb C^4\times M_2(\mathbb C).}
$$
There is one <simple module> for each matrix block, of <dimension> the block size, so exactly four are one-dimensional. Every nonabelian group of order eight therefore has this same complex <group algebra>.

The <character> of a finite-dimensional complex representation is $\chi_\rho(g)=\operatorname{Tr}\rho(g)$; it is irreducible if the representation is irreducible. The <character inner product> is
$$
(\alpha,\beta)=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\overline{\alpha(g)}\beta(g).
$$
Distinct irreducible characters are orthogonal and each has norm one. For a <permutation representation>, the diagonal entry of the permutation matrix at a basis vector is one exactly when that vector is fixed. Its <trace> therefore counts fixed points.

Let $r$ be rotation through $\pi/2$. Let $s_x,s_y$ be reflections in the coordinate axes, and $s_d,s_{d'}$ reflections in the two diagonals. The eight values are
$$
\begin{array}{c|rrrrrrrr}
g&e&r&r^2&r^3&s_x&s_y&s_d&s_{d'}\\\hline
\chi_\Omega(g)&4&0&0&0&0&0&2&2.
\end{array}
$$
Only diagonal reflections fix vertices, two each. Hence
$$
\boxed{(1,\chi_\Omega)=\frac{4+2+2}{8}=1,\qquad(\chi_\Omega,\chi_\Omega)=\frac{16+4+4}{8}=3.}
$$

Complete reducibility and <character> <orthogonality> imply that the squared multiplicities sum to three. The trivial constituent has multiplicity one, so the remaining two units come from two distinct nontrivial constituents, each with multiplicity one. Their degrees add to $4-1=3$, so they are two and one. Thus
$$
\boxed{\chi_\Omega=1+\chi+\eta,\qquad\chi(1)=2,\quad\eta(1)=1.}
$$
The two-dimensional constituent is the complexification of the geometric action on the plane. Its <trace> is $2$ on the identity, $-2$ on the half-turn, zero on the quarter-turns, and zero on every reflection. Its <character> norm is $(4+4)/8=1$, proving it irreducible. Alternatively, the span of the two coordinate functions on the vertex set is a two-dimensional invariant subspace of the permutation module with this geometric action. This identifies the requested representation rather than merely its degree.

Write $C=\langle c\rangle$ with $|c|=n$. For each divisor $k$ the <subgroup> $\langle c^{n/k}\rangle$ has order $k$. Conversely, if $H$ has order $k$, Lagrange gives $k\mid n$, and every $c^j\in H$ satisfies $(c^j)^k=1$, so $n\mid jk$. Therefore $n/k\mid j$ and $H\subseteq\langle c^{n/k}\rangle$. Equality follows from the common order. Thus **there is exactly one <subgroup> for each divisor of $n$**.

For the Abelian assertion, take a surjection $\mathbb Z^n\to A$ and let $L$ be the inverse <image> of $B$. Every <subgroup> of $\mathbb Z^n$ has at most $n$ generators, by induction: its projection onto the first coordinate is either zero, reducing to $\mathbb Z^{n-1}$, or $d\mathbb Z$ for some $d>0$. In the latter case choose one element with first coordinate $d$; subtracting its multiples reduces every element to the <kernel>, generated by at most $n-1$ elements by induction. The base case is the elementary description of subgroups of $\mathbb Z$ by their least positive element. Images of these generators generate $B$, proving the claim.

In $S_6$, the commuting disjoint transpositions $(12),(34),(56)$ generate $C_2^3$. This is a three-dimensional <vector space> over $\mathbb F_2$, so it requires three generators and cannot be generated by two.

For each $n\geq3$, take $G=S_{2n+2}$, generated by $(12)$ and the cycle $(1\,2\,\ldots\,2n+2)$. Its <subgroup> generated by
$$
(12),(34),\ldots,(2n+1\,2n+2)
$$
is $C_2^{n+1}$. It requires $n+1$ generators by the same <dimension> argument, giving **a two-generated group with a <subgroup> requiring more than $n$ generators**.

A <Sylow subgroup> for the <prime> $p$ is a maximal $p$-<subgroup>; in a <finite group> of order $p^am$ with $p\nmid m$ it has order $p^a$. <Sylow theorems> assert existence, containment of every $p$-<subgroup> in a Sylow $p$-<subgroup>, conjugacy of all Sylow $p$-subgroups, and the counting conditions $n_p\mid m$, $n_p\equiv1\pmod p$.

Since $N$ is a <normal subgroup>, $NP$ is a <subgroup> and
$$
[N:N\cap P]=[NP:P]\mid[G:P].
$$
The final index is <prime> to $p$. Since $N\cap P$ is a $p$-<subgroup>, its index in $N$ being <prime> to $p$ means it contains the entire $p$-part of $|N|$. Hence **$N\cap P$ is Sylow in $N$**.

The quotient $PN/N$ is a $p$-group, being the <image> of $P$, and
$$
[G/N:PN/N]=[G:PN]\mid[G:P].
$$
Its index is <prime> to $p$, so **$PN/N$ is Sylow in $G/N$**.

If $P\leq A$ and $Q\leq B$ are Sylow $p$-subgroups, their product has the full $p$-part of $|A\times B|$, hence is Sylow. Conversely, for any Sylow $R\leq A\times B$, its two projections are $p$-subgroups and lie in some Sylows $P,Q$. Then $R\subseteq P\times Q$, and equality follows by order. Distinct pairs give distinct subgroups because their projections recover $P$ and $Q$. Thus
$$
\boxed{n_p(A\times B)=n_p(A)n_p(B)=mn.}
$$

Define $G^{(0)}=G$ and $G^{(i+1)}=[G^{(i)},G^{(i)}]$. A group is a <soluble group> if this <derived series> reaches the identity after finitely many steps. Its <derived length> is the least $d$ with $G^{(d)}=1$.

Let $V=\{1,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}\leq A_4$. This <Klein four group> is a <normal subgroup> and Abelian; $A_4/V$ has order three, so $A_4'\subseteq V$. The group $A_4$ is nonabelian, so its derived <subgroup> is nontrivial. Conjugation by a three-cycle permutes the three nonidentity elements of $V$ transitively. Since the derived <subgroup> is a <normal subgroup>, containing any one forces it to contain all three. Thus $A_4'=V$, and $V'=1$. Therefore **$A_4$ has <derived length> two**.

If $H\leq G$, every <commutator> in $H$ is one in $G$, and induction gives $H^{(i)}\subseteq G^{(i)}$ for all $i$. Thus a <subgroup> of a <solvable group> is solvable, with no larger <derived length>.

The sign map gives $S_4'\subseteq A_4$. The <commutator> $[(12),(23)]$ is a three-cycle. Normality of $S_4'$ supplies every conjugate three-cycle, and three-cycles generate $A_4$. Hence $S_4'=A_4$, so the complete <derived series> is
$$
\boxed{S_4\supset A_4\supset V\supset1.}
$$
Every inclusion is strict, proving solvability and <derived length> three.

For the preliminary index assertion, the <coset action> gives a homomorphism $G\to S_n$. Its <kernel> is a <normal subgroup> and cannot be all of $G$, because the action is transitive on $n>1$ points. Simplicity makes the <kernel> trivial. The <sign homomorphism> of this faithful action must be trivial: otherwise simplicity would identify $G$ with $C_2$, impossible for a transitive action of degree at least three. Thus $G$ embeds in $A_n$, and Lagrange gives
$$
\boxed{|G|\mid n!/2.}
$$
A group of order $112=16\cdot7$ has a Sylow two-<subgroup> of index seven. If the group were simple, the preceding result would give $112\mid7!/2=2520$. This fails because $112$ is divisible by $16$ while $2520$ has only three factors of two. Thus **there is no <simple group> of order $112$**.

For order $56$, Sylow gives $n_7\in\{1,8\}$. If it is one, the order-seven Sylow is a <normal subgroup>. Otherwise its eight Sylows of order seven intersect only at the identity, giving $8\cdot6=48$ nonidentity elements of order seven. Only eight elements remain including the identity. Every Sylow two-<subgroup> has order eight and consists entirely of these remaining elements. Hence all Sylow two-subgroups coincide and the order-eight <subgroup> is a <normal subgroup>. Either case gives a nontrivial proper <normal subgroup>, so **no group of order $56$ is simple**.

For order $105=3\cdot5\cdot7$, Sylow's congruence and divisibility conditions give
$$
n_5\in\{1,21\},\qquad n_7\in\{1,15\}.
$$
If neither <Sylow subgroup> were a <normal subgroup>, there would be $21\cdot4=84$ elements of order five and $15\cdot6=90$ elements of order seven. Distinct subgroups of either <prime> order intersect only at the identity, and elements of the two orders are disjoint. This would give at least $84+90+1=175$ elements in a group of order $105$, a contradiction. Thus **a <subgroup> of order five or seven is a <normal subgroup>**, and the group is not simple.
