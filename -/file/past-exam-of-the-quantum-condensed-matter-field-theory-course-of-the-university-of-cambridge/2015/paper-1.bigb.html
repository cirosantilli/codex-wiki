= Solution
{parent=a}

Use $\hbar=1$ and $S_n^\pm=S_n^x\pm iS_n^y$. Operators on different sites commute, and expanding the two products gives $S_n^+S_{n+1}^-+S_n^-S_{n+1}^+=2(S_n^xS_{n+1}^x+S_n^yS_{n+1}^y)$. Therefore the <quantum XX chain> has
$$
\boxed{H=-\frac J2\sum_{n=1}^N(S_n^+S_{n+1}^-+S_n^-S_{n+1}^+)-h\sum_{n=1}^NS_n^z.}
$$
Each exchange moves a reversed <spin> between neighbours, conserving the total number of reversed <spins>. The factor of one half follows from defining $S^\pm$ without a normalizing square root.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <spin commutation relations> give $[S_{\rm tot}^z,S_n^\pm]=\pm S_n^\pm$. Using the product rule for a <commutator>,
$$
[S_{\rm tot}^z,S_n^+S_{n+1}^-]
=S_n^+S_{n+1}^--S_n^+S_{n+1}^-=0,
$$
and the conjugate term also vanishes. The longitudinal field commutes with every $S_n^z$, so **$[H,S_{\rm tot}^z]=0$**. The <Hamiltonian> is invariant under the global <spin rotation> $U(\theta)=e^{-i\theta S_{\rm tot}^z}$: its <continuous symmetry> group is $U(1)$. Simultaneous <energy> and total-magnetization <eigenstates> can be chosen, and evolution preserves each fixed-magnetization sector. Local <magnetization> can still flow between sites; generally $[H,S_n^z]\ne0$. In the <fermion> <representation> the conserved number will be $N_f=N/2-S_{\rm tot}^z$.

Use the <canonical anticommutation relations> $\{c_n,c_m^\dagger\}=\delta_{nm}$ and $\{c_n,c_m\}=0$. In particular, $n_m=c_m^\dagger c_m$ is a <projector>, so $(1-2n_m)^2=1$. Define $K_n=\prod_{m<n}(1-2n_m)$. Then $K_n^\dagger=K_n$, $K_n^2=1$, and $K_n$ commutes with $c_n$ and $c_n^\dagger$. Taking the adjoint of the given raising operator gives $S_n^-=c_n^\dagger K_n$. Hence
$$
[S_n^+,S_n^-]
=K_nc_nc_n^\dagger K_n-c_n^\dagger K_n^2c_n
=c_nc_n^\dagger-c_n^\dagger c_n
=1-2n_n=2S_n^z.
$$
Also $[n_n,c_n]=-c_n$ and $[n_n,c_n^\dagger]=c_n^\dagger$ imply $[S_n^z,S_n^\pm]=\pm S_n^\pm$. For different sites, exchanging two <fermion> operators contributes one minus sign and commuting an operator through the other site's parity string contributes another; their product is positive. Thus distinct <spin> algebras commute. **The string supplies precisely the signs required for the <Jordan–Wigner transformation>.** The <fermion> vacuum corresponds to <spin> up, while one occupied site corresponds to <spin> down; this convention fixes subsequent signs.

For a bulk bond, $K_{n+1}=K_n(1-2n_n)$ and $c_n(1-2n_n)=-c_n$. Reordering the remaining different-site operators then gives
$$
S_n^+S_{n+1}^-=c_{n+1}^\dagger c_n,\qquad
S_n^-S_{n+1}^+=c_n^\dagger c_{n+1}\quad(n<N).
$$
The periodic <spin> <boundary condition> requires separate attention. Set $P=\prod_{n=1}^N(1-2n_n)=(-1)^{N_f}$. The end bond becomes $S_N^+S_1^-=-P c_1^\dagger c_N$, with the conjugate relation for its adjoint. Consequently the exact finite-ring <Hamiltonian> is
$$
\boxed{H=-\frac{hN}{2}+h\sum_{n=1}^Nn_n
-\frac J2\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}(c_n^\dagger c_{n+1}+c_{n+1}^\dagger c_n)
+\frac{JP}{2}(c_N^\dagger c_1+c_1^\dagger c_N).}
$$
<fermion parity> is conserved, so diagonalize in a sector $P=\pm1$. There the end bond can be written like every other bond provided $c_{N+1}=-Pc_1$. This <parity-dependent boundary condition of the Jordan-Wigner chain> is **antiperiodic for even <fermion> number and periodic for odd <fermion> number**. Simply assuming periodic <fermions> in both sectors changes the finite-size spectrum.

= Solution

In a fixed fermion-parity sector use lattice spacing one and the unitary <discrete Fourier transform>
$$
c_n=\frac1{\sqrt N}\sum_k e^{ikn}c_k,\qquad
c_k=\frac1{\sqrt N}\sum_n e^{-ikn}c_n.
$$
The allowed momenta satisfy $e^{ikN}=-P$: they are $k=2\pi(\ell+1/2)/N$ for $P=+1$ and $k=2\pi\ell/N$ for $P=-1$, with $N$ distinct values modulo $2\pi$. The hopping and number sums become $\sum_k2\cos k\,c_k^\dagger c_k$ and $\sum_kc_k^\dagger c_k$. Thus
$$
\boxed{H=E+\sum_k\epsilon_k c_k^\dagger c_k,\qquad
E=-hN/2,\qquad\epsilon_k=h-J\cos k.}
$$
Here $E$ is the <energy> of the all-up <spin> configuration, not generally the ground-state <energy>. The dispersion has minimum $h-J$ at $k=0$, maximum $h+J$ at $k=\pi$, and positive curvature at its minimum. Since the <fermions> do not interact, each mode can be occupied zero or one <time>. A negative-energy occupation lowers the many-body <energy>, so in the <thermodynamic limit>
$$
|\mathrm{gs}\rangle=\prod_{|k|<k_F}c_k^\dagger|0\rangle,
\qquad k_F=\arccos(h/J),\quad 0<h<J.
$$
This is a filled <Fermi sea> containing many <fermions>; it is not obtained by putting a single particle into the band minimum. For $h>J$ every mode has positive <energy> and the vacuum is the <ground state>. Its <spin> interpretation is complete longitudinal <polarization>. At $h=J$ the zero-momentum one-particle excitation first becomes gapless.

For an exact finite ring use <parity-consistent filling of a free-fermion band>. In each <momentum> grid fill negative-energy levels, check that their particle number has the chosen parity, and if necessary toggle a mode with the smallest $|\epsilon_k|$. Compare the two sector energies. A zero-energy mode allows degenerate occupations. This restores the physical <spin> <Hilbert space> without changing the stated band.

= Solution
{parent=f}

Because $S_{\rm tot}^z=N/2-N_f$, the exact finite-size answer is $m_N=1/2-N_f^{\rm gs}/N$, with $N_f^{\rm gs}$ determined by the parity-consistent filling above. For the thermodynamic <magnetization of the quantum XX chain>, the occupied fraction is the <length> of the negative-energy interval divided by the Brillouin-zone <length>:
$$
\frac{N_f}{N}\longrightarrow\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-k_F}^{k_F}dk
=\frac{k_F}{\pi}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{m_z(h)=\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\frac12-\frac1\pi\arccos(h/J)
=\frac1\pi\arcsin(h/J),&0\le h\le J,\\[3pt]
\displaystyle\frac12,&h\ge J,
\end{cases}\qquad h_c=J.}
$$
The zero-field limit has zero <magnetization> and the high-field limit has all <spins> up. The finite-ring curve consists of <magnetization> steps approaching this continuous limit. Strictly above $h_c$ the fully polarized <ground state> is unique; exactly at $h_c$ a zero-energy spin-flip state can be degenerate with it. This finite-size qualification does not alter the saturation field or limiting <magnetization> density.

The <Holstein–Primakoff transformation> converts a <spin> algebra into bosonic operators while retaining the finite physical <spin> <Hilbert space>. With $[a_n,a_m^\dagger]=\delta_{nm}$, write
$$
S_n^z=S-a_n^\dagger a_n,\qquad
S_n^+=\sqrt{2S-a_n^\dagger a_n}\,a_n,\qquad
S_n^-=a_n^\dagger\sqrt{2S-a_n^\dagger a_n}.
$$
The <Holstein–Primakoff occupation constraint> is $0\le n_n\le2S$. Acting on $|n\rangle$, the raising <matrix> element is $\sqrt{n(2S-n+1)}$ and the lowering element is $\sqrt{(n+1)(2S-n)}$. Their squared difference is $2(S-n)$, reproducing $[S^+,S^-]=2S^z$. Operator ordering inside the square roots is essential. For low <boson> occupation relative to $S$, expand them to obtain $S^+\simeq\sqrt{2S}\,a$ and $S^-\simeq\sqrt{2S}\,a^\dagger$. Keeping quadratic terms gives the <linear spin-wave approximation>; higher terms describe <magnon> interactions. Large $S$ helps control the expansion, but a low-dimensional <infrared divergence> can invalidate it even at large finite $S$.

For the <ferromagnet> choose $H_F=-J\sum_n\mathbf S_n\cdot\mathbf S_{n+1}$, $J>0$, with unit lattice spacing and $\hbar=1$. Expand around the all-up state. A longitudinal bond contributes $-JS^2+JS(n_n+n_{n+1})$, and the transverse exchange contributes $-JS(a_n^\dagger a_{n+1}+a_{n+1}^\dagger a_n)$. After a <Fourier transform>,
$$
H_F^{(2)}=-JNS^2+\sum_k\omega_F(k)a_k^\dagger a_k,
\qquad\boxed{\omega_F(k)=2JS(1-\cos k)\simeq JS k^2.}
$$
These are <ferromagnetic magnons>. The polarized vacuum is an exact <ground state> of the full ferromagnetic <Hamiltonian>: every bond is in its maximum <total-spin sector>, minimizing that bond <energy>. It therefore has no quantum zero-point reduction of its <magnetization>. The one-magnon dispersion is also exact, although interacting many-magnon states need not be free <bosons>. Rotating the <polarization> generates the maximum-total-spin ground-state multiplet. Selecting a definite direction in a thermodynamic symmetry-breaking description breaks $SU(2)$ to $U(1)$; the two transverse <spin> components form a canonical pair, producing a single quadratic <type-B Goldstone boson>. A small aligning field gaps it by the Zeeman <energy>.

At nonzero <temperature> the one-dimensional <ferromagnet> is different: its thermal <magnon> number contains $\int dk\,[e^{\beta JS k^2}-1]^{-1}$, with infrared behaviour $\int dk\,T/k^2$. The divergent <magnetization> reduction signals the absence of finite-temperature <long-range order> in an infinite short-range isotropic chain, consistent with the <Mermin-Wagner theorem>. It does not disallow the polarized zero-temperature <ground states> just constructed.<Absence of Ferromagnetism or Antiferromagnetism in One- or Two-Dimensional Isotropic Heisenberg Models by N. D. Mermin and H. Wagner>[abstract and finite-temperature theorem]{ref}

For the <antiferromagnet> use $H_{AF}=+J\sum_n\mathbf S_n\cdot\mathbf S_{n+1}$, still $J>0$, on an even bipartite chain. The classical reference state alternates up and down. Rotate one sublattice through $\pi$ so that both local quantization axes point along their reference <spins>. In unrotated physical components, to leading order $S_A^z=S-a^\dagger a$, $S_B^z=-S+b^\dagger b$, $S_A^+\simeq\sqrt{2S}\,a$ and $S_B^+\simeq\sqrt{2S}\,b^\dagger$. Choosing Fourier phases using the actual site <positions> gives
$$
H_{AF}^{(2)}=-JNS^2+2JS\sum_{k\in\mathrm{RBZ}}
\left[a_k^\dagger a_k+b_k^\dagger b_k+
\cos k\,(a_kb_{-k}+a_k^\dagger b_{-k}^\dagger)\right].
$$
The reduced <Brillouin zone> has $M=N/2$ momenta and <length> $\pi$. The pair creation and annihilation terms express the fact that an antiparallel reference product state is not an <eigenstate>. A bosonic <Bogoliubov transformation> with $u_k=\cosh\theta_k$, $v_k=\sinh\theta_k$, $\tanh2\theta_k=\cos k$ diagonalizes each two-mode block. With the sign convention $a_k=u_k\alpha_k-v_k\beta_{-k}^\dagger$ and $b_{-k}=u_k\beta_{-k}-v_k\alpha_k^\dagger$, the result is
$$
H_{AF}^{(2)}=E_{AF}^{(2)}+\sum_{k\in\mathrm{RBZ}}\omega_{AF}(k)
(\alpha_k^\dagger\alpha_k+\beta_k^\dagger\beta_k),
$$
$$
\boxed{\omega_{AF}(k)=2JS\sqrt{1-\cos^2 k}=2JS|\sin k|\simeq2JS|k|,}
\qquad E_{AF}^{(2)}=-JNS^2+\sum_{k\in\mathrm{RBZ}}[\omega_{AF}(k)-2JS].
$$
Thus the harmonic antiferromagnetic modes have linear, rather than quadratic, long-wave dispersion. In an unfolded Brillouin-zone description the same result is represented by zeros at both zero and the antiferromagnetic <wavevector>; these are not extra independent degrees of freedom beyond the two-sublattice accounting.

The Bogoliubov vacuum has no $\alpha$ or $\beta$ excitations but contains original <spin> <bosons>, with $v_k^2=\tfrac12(|\sin k|^{-1}-1)$. Its <staggered magnetization> is $m_s=S-M^{-1}\sum_{k\in\mathrm{RBZ}}v_k^2$. This <quantum depletion of Néel order> diverges logarithmically at small $k$ in one dimension. **Linear spin-wave theory therefore does not establish a stable ordered antiferromagnetic <ground state> for this chain.** Its nominal gapless dispersion is a result of expanding around an invalid ordered reference, not a general proof of the exact excitation spectrum. For the nearest-neighbour isotropic chain, integer-spin systems have a Haldane gap, whereas half-integer-spin systems are gapless; the spin-one-half chain in particular has fractional <spin> excitations rather than a simple dilute gas of ordered-state <magnons>. This distinction requires physics beyond the harmonic expansion, including the topological term in the continuum theory.<Nonlinear Field Theory of Large-Spin Heisenberg Antiferromagnets: Semiclassically Quantized Solitons of the One-Dimensional Easy-Axis Néel State by F. Duncan M. Haldane>[pp.1153–1156, integer versus half-integer spin]{ref}

For one coordinate with <Hamiltonian> $H=\hat p^2/(2m)+V(\hat x)$, the propagator is $K(x_f,t_f;x_i,t_i)=\langle x_f|e^{-iHT/\hbar}|x_i\rangle$, where $T=t_f-t_i>0$. Divide $T$ into $M$ intervals $\delta t$, use the <Trotter product formula> to separate kinetic and potential evolution, and insert complete <position> and <momentum> bases at each slice. The <momentum> integral is Gaussian:
$$
\langle x_{j+1}|e^{-i\delta t\hat p^2/(2m\hbar)}|x_j\rangle
=\int\frac{dp_j}{2\pi\hbar}\exp\left[\frac i\hbar p_j(x_{j+1}-x_j)-\frac{i\delta t}{2m\hbar}p_j^2\right]
=\left(\frac m{2\pi i\hbar\delta t}\right)^{1/2}
 e^{im(x_{j+1}-x_j)^2/(2\hbar\delta t)}.
$$
Multiplying the short-time kernels gives the <time-sliced configuration-space path integral>,
$$
K=\lim_{M\to\infty}\left(\frac m{2\pi i\hbar\delta t}\right)^{M/2}
\int\prod_{j=1}^{M-1}dx_j\,
\exp\left\{\frac i\hbar\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}
\left[\frac{m(x_{j+1}-x_j)^2}{2\delta t}-\delta t V(x_j)\right]\right\},
$$
with $x_0=x_i$, $x_M=x_f$. Continuum notation abbreviates this limit as $\int_{x_i}^{x_f}\mathcal D x\,e^{iS[x]/\hbar}$, where $S=\int dt\,[m\dot x^2/2-V(x)]$. The measure includes the displayed normalization; it is not an unweighted count of paths. A small damping or analytic-continuation prescription defines the oscillatory integrals. The paths contribute complex amplitudes, so alternative histories interfere before <probabilities> are calculated. This is the central difference from a classical statistical mixture of trajectories.<Space-Time Approach to Non-Relativistic Quantum Mechanics by Richard Feynman>[pp.367–374, action phases and construction of the propagator]{ref}

When <actions> vary rapidly relative to $\hbar$, <stationary phase> selects <classical paths>. Put $x=x_{\rm cl}+\eta$, with $\eta$ zero at the endpoints. Vanishing first variation gives $m\ddot x_{\rm cl}+V'(x_{\rm cl})=0$. The quadratic term is
$$
S[x]=S[x_{\rm cl}]+\frac12\int dt\,
\eta\left(-m\frac{d^2}{dt^2}-V''(x_{\rm cl})\right)\eta+O(\eta^3).
$$
The resulting Gaussian fluctuation <determinant> is the <semiclassical propagator> prefactor. In one dimension, away from caustics,
$$
\boxed{K_{\rm sc}=\sum_{\rm cl}
\left(\frac{D_{\rm cl}}{2\pi i\hbar}\right)^{1/2}
\exp\left[\frac{iS_{\rm cl}}\hbar-\frac{i\pi\nu_{\rm cl}}2\right],\qquad
D_{\rm cl}=\left|-\frac{\partial^2S_{\rm cl}}{\partial x_f\partial x_i}\right|.}
$$
The branch convention makes $\nu=0$ for a direct free-particle path before any caustic. The <Maslov index> tracks the phase changes as fluctuations pass through a focal singularity. In several dimensions replace the endpoint <derivative> by the <Van Vleck determinant>. The approximation sums all relevant <classical paths>; real-time <action> need only be stationary, not a minimum. It fails in this simple form at a caustic or coalescing saddles, where a uniform approximation is needed. Quadratic <actions> have no higher fluctuation terms, making their Gaussian evaluation exact. For example $S_{\rm cl}=m(x_f-x_i)^2/(2T)$ gives the exact free-particle kernel $\sqrt{m/(2\pi i\hbar T)}e^{iS_{\rm cl}/\hbar}$. Tunnelling involves classically forbidden regions and can be described by imaginary-time saddles rather than real classical trajectories.

Quantum <statistical mechanics> follows by <Wick rotation> $t=-i\tau$. The imaginary-time evolution operator is $e^{-\tau H/\hbar}$, with <Euclidean action>
$$
S_E[x]=\int_0^{\beta\hbar}d\tau\left[\frac m2(\partial_\tau x)^2+V(x)\right].
$$
Taking a <thermal trace> identifies and integrates the endpoint <positions>:
$$
\boxed{Z=\operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta H}
=\int dx\int_{x(0)=x}^{x(\beta\hbar)=x}\mathcal D x\,e^{-S_E[x]/\hbar}.}
$$
This <thermal trace as a closed Euclidean path integral> explains the periodic imaginary-time direction. A density-matrix element instead retains open endpoints. For many identical particles the <trace> also sums over endpoint permutations, with a plus sign for <bosons> and permutation parity for <fermions>. In coherent-state language this becomes periodic bosonic fields and antiperiodic <Grassmann fields>, with corresponding <Matsubara frequencies>.

The Euclidean weight resembles a classical <Boltzmann weight>. A time-sliced local <quantum field> in $d$ spatial dimensions becomes a classical-looking field model in $d+1$ dimensions, with anisotropic couplings and extra extent $\beta\hbar$. Thus zero-temperature quantum criticality can be treated using an infinitely extended imaginary-time direction, while nonzero <temperature> imposes a finite thickness. The dynamical scaling of <time> need not equal the spatial scaling: the <dynamical critical exponent> specifies their relationship. This <quantum-to-classical imaginary-time mapping> requires qualification when weights have signs or imaginary terms; such a <functional integral> is not automatically an ordinary positive classical <probability> <distribution>. Even with a positive weight, the mapping concerns equilibrium statistics, not literal dissipative real-time trajectories. For a free particle the Euclidean kernel is a <diffusion> kernel with <diffusion> coefficient $\hbar/(2m)$, illustrating the mathematical correspondence.

The classical high-temperature limit is a further approximation, rather than simply another name for imaginary <time>. If quantum level spacings and exchange effects are negligible, a one-particle <partition function> in $d$ dimensions becomes
$$
Z_{\rm cl}\simeq\int\frac{d^dx\,d^dp}{(2\pi\hbar)^d}
 e^{-\beta[p^2/(2m)+V(x)]}.
$$
For <indistinguishable particles> there is a factor $1/N!$ in the dilute classical gas, and exchange is negligible when the <number density> <times> the <thermal de Broglie wavelength> to power $d$ is small. For a <harmonic oscillator>, the exact quantum result $Z=[2\sinh(\beta\hbar\omega/2)]^{-1}$ tends to $1/(\beta\hbar\omega)$ as $\beta\hbar\omega\to0$, recovering the classical phase-space integral. **<stationary phase> explains <classical mechanics>; the short imaginary-time, weak-exchange limit explains classical <statistical mechanics>.** These are related but distinct limits of the same quantum formulation.

Consider a conventional spin-singlet, weak-coupling, three-dimensional <superconductor>. Set $\hbar=k_B=1$ in the microscopic <action> and restore electromagnetic units below. An attraction $\lambda>0$ in an <energy> shell about the <Fermi surface> gives a Grassmann <action>
$$
S=\int d\tau\,d^3x\left[\sum_{\sigma=\uparrow,\downarrow}
\bar\psi_\sigma(\partial_\tau+\xi(-i\nabla))\psi_\sigma
-\lambda\bar\psi_\uparrow\bar\psi_\downarrow\psi_\downarrow\psi_\uparrow\right],
\qquad\xi_{\mathbf k}=\epsilon_{\mathbf k}-\mu.
$$
The attractive sign permits pairing even when the single-particle <Fermi gas> is otherwise stable. A complex <Hubbard–Stratonovich transformation> introduces a pair field $\Delta$ proportional at the saddle to $\lambda\langle\psi_\downarrow\psi_\uparrow\rangle$. The <action> is then quadratic in <fermions>. Integrating them out gives the <BCS pairing-field effective action>
$$
S_{\rm eff}[\Delta]=\int d\tau\,d^3x\,\frac{|\Delta|^2}{\lambda}
-\operatorname{Tr}\ln\mathcal G^{-1}[\Delta]+\text{constant}.
$$
Here the <trace> uses one independent <Nambu spinor> pairing block; a fully doubled particle-hole <representation> instead carries its corresponding one-half factor. At a uniform saddle the <Bogoliubov--de Gennes Hamiltonian> can be written as
$$
\mathcal H_{\rm BdG}(\mathbf k)=\begin{pmatrix}\xi_{\mathbf k}&\Delta\\
\Delta^*&-\xi_{\mathbf k}\end{pmatrix},\qquad
E_{\mathbf k}=\sqrt{\xi_{\mathbf k}^2+|\Delta|^2}.
$$
The negative branch describes the paired vacuum; excitations are <Bogoliubov quasiparticles> mixing particles and holes. Extremizing the <effective action> gives
$$
\boxed{\frac1\lambda=\int_{\rm shell}\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}
\frac{\tanh(E_{\mathbf k}/2T)}{2E_{\mathbf k}}.}
$$
The physical cutoff or a renormalized interaction is part of the microscopic model; an unregulated contact integral must not be used indiscriminately. With constant <density of states> $N(0)$ per <spin> and shell half-width $\Omega_c$, the zero-temperature weak-coupling solution is $\Delta_0\simeq2\Omega_c e^{-1/[N(0)\lambda]}$. The exponentially small scale follows from the logarithmic pairing susceptibility of states near the <Fermi surface>. For this ideal weak-coupling model, $2\Delta_0/(k_BT_c)\simeq3.53$. The microscopic theory relates the gap, pairing and thermodynamic transition.<Theory of Superconductivity by John Bardeen and others>[pp.1175–1177 and the energy-gap and finite-temperature developments]{ref}

Close to a continuous transition, for fields varying slowly compared with microscopic scales, expand the <effective action> in powers and <gradients> of $\Delta$. Its static part is the <Ginzburg-Landau theory of superconductivity>:
$$
F=\int d^3x\left[a(T)|\Delta|^2+\frac b2|\Delta|^4+
K\left|\left(-i\nabla-\frac q\hbar\mathbf A\right)\Delta\right|^2
+\frac{|\mathbf B|^2}{2\mu_0}\right],
\qquad q=-2e,\quad b,K>0.
$$
The normalization of $\Delta$ fixes the coefficients; here $a(T)$ changes sign at $T_c$. The uniform minimum has $|\Delta_0|^2=-a/b$ for $a<0$, condensation <free-energy density> $-a^2/(2b)$, and characteristic coherence-length scale $\xi=\sqrt{K/|a|}$. Above the transition the minimum is at zero. The quartic stabilization and <gradient> cost are precisely the ingredients of <Landau theory> for a complex <order parameter>. This controlled near-transition expansion supplies a bridge between microscopic <BCS theory> and macroscopic magnetic response; its coefficients and validity depend on the material and fluctuation regime.<Microscopic Derivation of the Ginzburg-Landau Equations in the Theory of Superconductivity by Lev Gor'kov>[pp.1364–1367, microscopic expansion near the transition]{ref}

Write $\Delta=(|\Delta_0|+\eta)e^{i\phi}$. The radial fluctuation has positive local restoring curvature, whereas the phase has no local potential in a neutral theory with global $U(1)$ <symmetry>. Freezing the amplitude gives
$$
F_\phi=\frac{\rho_s}{2}\int d^3x
\left(\nabla\phi-\frac q\hbar\mathbf A\right)^2,
\qquad\rho_s=2K|\Delta_0|^2.
$$
The neutral case supports a gapless collective phase mode. In the charged case the <electromagnetic field> couples to this phase rigidity. Under $\mathbf A\mapsto\mathbf A+\nabla\chi$ and $\phi\mapsto\phi+q\chi/\hbar$, the displayed combination is invariant. Locally choosing a gauge with constant phase leaves a quadratic term in $\mathbf A$. Varying the <free energy> gives the transverse London current and magnetic screening scale,
$$
\mathbf j=-\frac{q^2\rho_s}{\hbar^2}\mathbf A,
\qquad\boxed{\lambda_L^{-2}=\frac{\mu_0q^2\rho_s}{\hbar^2}.}
$$
Combining this relation with static <Maxwell equations> gives exponential field penetration, the <Meissner effect>. Single-valuedness of the pair field around a loop with negligible current gives flux in units $\Phi_0=2\pi\hbar/|q|=\pi\hbar/e$. These are gauge-invariant physical consequences of the condensate stiffness.

For the dynamics, the neutral phase and density mode is reorganized by the Coulomb interaction and the dynamical <electromagnetic field>. In a bulk three-dimensional system the long-wave longitudinal mode has a plasma gap, rather than remaining the neutral sound mode. This is the <Anderson-Higgs mechanism in a superconductor>: a phase coordinate becomes part of the electromagnetic collective response instead of surviving as an independent massless physical particle. Local <gauge transformations> are redundancies of the description, so a literal breaking of a local redundancy is not an observable. Phase coherence, current response and magnetic screening provide the physical statements.<Plasmons, Gauge Invariance, and Mass by Philip Warren Anderson>[pp.439–442, plasma oscillations and the charged versus neutral matter spectrum]{ref}

A static Landau functional alone cannot determine that full dynamical spectrum. A two-dimensional film, for example, need not have the same long-wave plasma dispersion as a bulk charged system. Nor does a positive radial curvature automatically guarantee a sharp Higgs amplitude <resonance>: coupling to quasiparticles and other modes can damp it. The chosen mean-field phase is also a thermodynamic symmetry-breaking description; an isolated finite system can be formulated with definite particle number. **The microscopic pairing saddle, the near-transition Landau expansion and the electromagnetic Anderson-Higgs response describe successive scales of the same superconducting state, with distinct assumptions at each step.**

For a normal-ordered bosonic <Hamiltonian>, insert coherent-state resolutions between short imaginary-time evolution factors. The overlaps generate $\bar\psi\partial_\tau\psi$ and the <Hamiltonian> symbol is obtained by replacing creation and <annihilation operators> by $\bar\psi$ and $\psi$. Including the <chemical potential> gives, in the stated $\hbar=1$ convention,
$$
\boxed{S=\int_0^\beta d\tau\int_Vd^dr\left[
\bar\psi\left(\partial_\tau-\frac{\nabla^2}{2m}-\mu\right)\psi
+\frac g2(\bar\psi\psi)^2\right].}
$$
The <trace> imposes **periodic imaginary-time <boundary conditions>**,
$$
\psi(\mathbf r,\beta)=\psi(\mathbf r,0),\qquad
\bar\psi(\mathbf r,\beta)=\bar\psi(\mathbf r,0),
$$
so bosonic <Matsubara frequencies> are $\omega_n=2\pi n/\beta$. The two complex coherent-state integration variables are treated independently before imposing the physical saddle contour. There is no extra zero-point ordering term: the supplied interaction is already normal ordered.

The spatial confinement is not specified beyond the volume. The uniform bulk results below use a homogeneous box with periodic spatial boundaries, or the interior of a large vessel where boundary effects can be neglected. A hard wall instead requires a vanishing field at that wall and an inhomogeneous saddle near it. Temporal periodicity follows from the <thermal trace> and does not by itself impose periodic spatial boundaries.

For a uniform time-independent saddle, extremizing the <action> gives $(-\mu+g|\psi_0|^2)\psi_0=0$. Its grand-potential density is $f(\rho)=-\mu\rho+g\rho^2/2$, with $\rho=|\psi_0|^2\ge0$. Since $g>0$, the minimum at positive density for $\mu>0$ is
$$
\boxed{\rho_0=\mu/g,\qquad
\psi_0=\sqrt{\rho_0}\,e^{i\phi_0},\qquad
f_{\min}=-\mu^2/(2g).}
$$
For $\mu\le0$ the uniform mean-field minimum is the vacuum, rather than a positive-density condensate. At fixed nonzero density in the weakly interacting condensed regime, the saddle relation sets $\mu\simeq g\rho_0$. The mean-field one-body <density matrix> factorizes as $\langle a^\dagger(\mathbf r)a(\mathbf r')\rangle\simeq\rho_0$, giving macroscopic occupation of the uniform mode. Depletion corrections are omitted at this stage.

The <action> has the global particle-number <symmetry> $\psi\mapsto e^{i\alpha}\psi$, $\bar\psi\mapsto e^{-i\alpha}\bar\psi$. Choosing one saddle phase breaks this $U(1)$ <symmetry>; the degenerate mean-field minima form the circle **$U(1)\simeq S^1$**. There is no energetic preference for a uniform value of $\phi_0$, but spatially varying phase costs <gradient energy>. Consequently a neutral condensate has a gapless <Goldstone mode> and a nonzero <phase stiffness>. The mode becomes sound once density dynamics is included. With $\hbar=1$ the <superfluid velocity> is $\nabla\phi/m$, so a single-valued field allows circulation in units $2\pi/m$ around a closed loop that avoids zeros of the amplitude. This phase coherence and rigidity are the physical content of the broken-symmetry description.

The claim is a mean-field saddle statement, not an assertion that every $d$ and <temperature> in the question supports true <long-range order>. In a finite number-conserving system $\langle a\rangle=0$; the symmetry-breaking <order parameter> is defined with the <thermodynamic limit> taken before an infinitesimal source is removed. In a homogeneous infinite short-range gas at nonzero <temperature>, infrared phase fluctuations prohibit true condensation in $d\le2$. In one dimension they also destroy true condensate <long-range order> at zero <temperature>. A two-dimensional gas can nevertheless have <superfluidity> and algebraically decaying correlations below a <Berezinskii–Kosterlitz–Thouless transition>. A finite trap can have substantial coherence without contradicting these thermodynamic statements.<Existence of Long-Range Order in One and Two Dimensions by Pierre Hohenberg>[abstract and pp.383–386; finite-temperature restriction]{ref}

On the physical density-phase contour put $\bar\psi=\sqrt\rho\,e^{-i\phi}$. Direct differentiation gives
$$
\bar\psi\partial_\tau\psi=\frac12\partial_\tau\rho+i\rho\partial_\tau\phi,
\qquad
|\nabla\psi|^2=\frac{|\nabla\rho|^2}{4\rho}+\rho|\nabla\phi|^2.
$$
Integrating the <Laplacian> by parts with the stated bulk boundary convention, and dropping the periodic total <derivative> of $\rho$, produces the <density-phase action of a Bose gas>,
$$
S=\int d\tau\,d^dr\left[i\rho\partial_\tau\phi
+\frac{|\nabla\rho|^2}{8m\rho}+\frac\rho{2m}|\nabla\phi|^2
+\frac g2(\rho-\rho_0)^2-\frac{\mu^2}{2g}\right].
$$
Write $\rho=\rho_0+\delta\rho$ and $\phi=\phi_0+\varphi$, where $\phi_0$ is constant. To quadratic order,
$$
\boxed{S_2=\int d\tau\,d^dr\left[
\frac g2(\delta\rho)^2+i\delta\rho\partial_\tau\varphi
+\frac{|\nabla\delta\rho|^2}{8m\rho_0}
+\frac{\rho_0}{2m}|\nabla\varphi|^2\right].}
$$
The uniform static density fluctuation has positive curvature $g$. Its inverse static susceptibility at <wavevector> $q$ is $A_q=g+q^2/(4m\rho_0)$, which remains nonzero at $q=0$. This is the sense in which the amplitude or density fluctuation is massive. The phase has no term proportional to $\varphi^2$ without <derivatives>; a uniform phase <rotation> costs no <action>. It is the massless direction required by global $U(1)$ <symmetry>.

The term $i\delta\rho\partial_\tau\varphi$ shows <density-phase conjugacy>. It is important to distinguish <static density stiffness versus an excitation gap>: the density and phase do not form two independent normal-mode branches with one gapped and one gapless particle. In this nonrelativistic first-order theory they supply the conjugate variables of one positive Bogoliubov branch, derived below. The omitted cubic terms include $\delta\rho|\nabla\varphi|^2/(2m)$ and corrections from the density-gradient denominator.

There is also a background term $i\rho_0\int d\tau\,d^dr\,\partial_\tau\varphi$. It vanishes for smooth fluctuations with zero temporal winding. Periodicity of $\psi$, however, permits $\varphi(\beta)-\varphi(0)=2\pi w$. The <compact phase winding term> must be retained when such sectors are included; it is not generally the same constant in all sectors. The local Gaussian <phonon> calculation concerns the smooth zero-winding sector and does not describe vortices or points where the density vanishes.

At wavelengths with $q^2/(4m\rho_0)\ll g$, neglect the density-gradient term in the <quadratic density-phase action>. Complete the square in its remaining density terms:
$$
\frac g2(\delta\rho)^2+i\delta\rho\partial_\tau\varphi
=\frac g2\left(\delta\rho+\frac i g\partial_\tau\varphi\right)^2
+\frac1{2g}(\partial_\tau\varphi)^2.
$$
The convergent <Gaussian integral> can be performed by a contour shift or by its Fourier-transform identity. Its <determinant> is independent of $\varphi$ at this order. Combining that normalization with the saddle <action> into a constant $S_0$ gives
$$
\boxed{Z=e^{-S_0}\int\mathcal D\varphi\,e^{-S_{\rm eff}[\varphi]},\qquad
S_{\rm eff}=\frac12\int_0^\beta d\tau\int_Vd^dr
\left[\frac{(\partial_\tau\varphi)^2}{g}+\frac{\rho_0}{m}|\nabla\varphi|^2\right].}
$$
The saddle contribution to $S_0$ is $-\beta V\mu^2/(2g)$; the additional measure-dependent constant need not be assigned an invented numerical value. The density Gaussian extends the fluctuation integration beyond its exact positivity domain, which is justified only while fluctuations around $\rho_0>0$ are small. The smooth zero-winding assumption is also required as discussed above. A constant <global phase> zero mode gives a finite compact-group volume, handled separately from nonzero <phonon> modes.

Fourier transforming the <Bose-gas phase-only action> gives a kernel $\omega_n^2/g+(\rho_0/m)q^2$. Analytically continuing $\omega_n\mapsto-i(\omega+i0^+)$ gives its real-frequency poles,
$$
\boxed{\omega(q)=c|q|,\qquad c=\sqrt{g\rho_0/m}.}
$$
In the present units the excitation <energy> equals $\omega$. Each real Fourier coordinate is a <harmonic oscillator> with inertia coefficient $1/g$ and spring coefficient $\rho_0q^2/m$, hence <frequency> equal to the square root of their ratio. This is the direct relation to a quantum <harmonic chain>. The mode is a gapless sound <phonon>; its finite speed reflects both the <compressibility> $\partial\rho/\partial\mu=1/g$ and the <phase stiffness> $\rho_0/m$.

Retaining the density-gradient term provides a useful check. Its integration instead gives a temporal coefficient $1/A_q$, so
$$
\omega^2(q)=\frac{\rho_0 q^2}{m}\left(g+\frac{q^2}{4m\rho_0}\right)
=c^2q^2+\frac{q^4}{4m^2},
\qquad\omega(q)=\sqrt{\epsilon_q(\epsilon_q+2g\rho_0)},\quad\epsilon_q=\frac{q^2}{2m}.
$$
This is the <Bogoliubov spectrum>. Its small-$q$ limit is the derived sound branch; at large $q$ within the contact model it crosses towards particle-like behaviour. The crossover in the squared dispersion is $q\sim2\sqrt{mg\rho_0}$, precisely the scale beyond which the discarded density <gradient> is not small.

The same <action> checks the dimensional restriction in the saddle analysis. At nonzero <temperature> its zero Matsubara mode gives phase fluctuations proportional to $\int d^dq/q^2$; at zero <temperature> integrating the <frequency> gives $\int d^dq/|q|$. Their <infrared divergences> invalidate uniform true <long-range order> for $d\le2$ at nonzero <temperature> and for $d=1$ at zero <temperature>. The long-wave collective description can remain useful for correlations even where a global condensate saddle is not a literal symmetry-broken state. The contact coupling here is an effective low-energy parameter; this calculation does not determine ultraviolet <renormalization> or trap-specific surface modes.
