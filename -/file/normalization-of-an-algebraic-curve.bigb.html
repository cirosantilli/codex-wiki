= Normalization of an algebraic curve

This is the one-dimensional case of <normalization of an algebraic variety>, constructed from integral closures of coordinate rings.

The normalization of an irreducible algebraic curve is a normal, hence regular, curve with a finite birational morphism to the original curve. Over a perfect field this regular curve is smooth.

= Finite normalization model of a one-variable function field
{parent=Normalization of an algebraic curve}
{title2=$K/k,\quad\operatorname{trdeg}_kK=1$}

For an <algebraically closed field> $k$ and a finitely generated <field extension> $K/k$ of <transcendence degree> one, choose $t$ with $K/k(t)$ finite. The <integral closures> of the two standard coordinate rings of the <projective line> in $K$ are finite modules and glue to a finite <normalization of an algebraic curve> of the projective line. A one-dimensional normal local ring is a <discrete valuation ring>; over the perfect field $k$ the resulting projective curve is smooth. Extension of rational maps gives uniqueness of this smooth projective model.

= Flat normalization is an isomorphism
{parent=Normalization of an algebraic curve}

If the normalization of an <integral scheme> is flat, then it is an <isomorphism>. On an affine chart, let $A\subseteq B\subseteq\operatorname{Frac}(A)$ be the integral closure. Integrality gives surjectivity on spectra, so flatness makes $B$ faithfully flat. For $b=u/v\in B$ with $u,v\in A$ and $v\ne0$, faithful flatness gives $vB\cap A=vA$. Thus $u=va$ for some $a\in A$, and $b=a$. Consequently $B=A$. This proof does not need finiteness of the normalization.

= Elliptic curve

An elliptic curve over a field $K$ is a smooth projective curve of genus one equipped with a specified $K$-rational point $O$. The chord-and-tangent law makes its rational points an abelian group with identity $O$.

= Galois cohomology of an elliptic curve
{parent=Elliptic curve}
{c}
{title2=$H^i(K,E)$}

For an <elliptic curve> $E/K$, regard $E(K^{\rm sep})$ as a discrete <Galois module> for the <absolute Galois group> $G_K$. Its continuous <Galois cohomology> has $H^0(K,E)=E(K)$; $H^1(K,E)$ classifies torsors under $E$. For an integer $n$ invertible in $K$, the <Kummer exact sequence of an elliptic curve> gives $0\to E(K)/nE(K)\to H^1(K,E[n])\to H^1(K,E)[n]\to0$. If $[n]Q=P$, the first map sends $P$ to $\sigma\mapsto\sigma Q-Q$. Changing $Q$ changes the cocycle by a coboundary; this class vanishes exactly when $P$ has a division point over $K$.

= Diagonal cubic elliptic curve
{parent=Elliptic curve}
{title2=$X^3+Y^3+dZ^3=0$}

For $d\in\mathbb Q^\times$, the <smooth projective curve> $X^3+Y^3+dZ^3=0$, with identity $O=(1:-1:0)$, is a genus-one <elliptic curve>. Its partial derivatives have no common projective zero. The invertible linear <projective transformation>
$$
(\mathcal X:\mathcal Y:\mathcal Z)=(-12dZ:-36d(X-Y):X+Y)
$$
sends it to $\mathcal Y^2\mathcal Z=\mathcal X^3-432d^2\mathcal Z^3$, taking $O$ to $(0:1:0)$. Indeed $(X+Y)^3+3(X+Y)(X-Y)^2=4(X^3+Y^3)=-4dZ^3$. In affine coordinates $u=X/Z,v=Y/Z$, this gives $x=-12d/(u+v)$ and $y=-36d(u-v)/(u+v)$, with inverse $u=(y-36d)/(6x)$, $v=(-y-36d)/(6x)$. Thus diagonal sums of two cubes are naturally studied through <elliptic-curve addition formula>.

= Rational pairs of cubes summing to seven
{parent=Diagonal cubic elliptic curve}
{title2=$u^3+v^3=7$}

There are infinitely many rational pairs $(u,v)$ with $u^3+v^3=7$. The corresponding <Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve> is $y^2=x^3-21168$, and $(u,v)=(2,-1)$ maps to $P=(84,756)$. The primes $5$ and $11$ are good, with point counts $6$ and $12$ because cubing is bijective in their residue fields. <Reduction of torsion points on an elliptic curve> bounds rational torsion by order dividing $6$. The <flexes of a diagonal cubic> exclude three-torsion for $d=-7$, while $d/2=-7/2$ is not a cube and hence excludes two-torsion. Thus $P$ has infinite order.

All nonzero multiples of $P$ map to distinct rational affine pairs, since the only rational point at infinity of the diagonal cubic is its identity. The inverse map is $u=(y+252)/(6x)$ and $v=(252-y)/(6x)$.

= Rational torsion of a diagonal cubic
{parent=Diagonal cubic elliptic curve}
{title2=$E_d(\mathbb Q)_{\mathrm{tors}}$}

For $d\in\mathbb Q^\times$ the rational <torsion subgroup> of $X^3+Y^3+dZ^3=0$ is cyclic of order $3$ if $d$ is a cube, cyclic of order $2$ if $d/2$ is a cube, and trivial otherwise.

To bound torsion, use the <Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve> $y^2=x^3-432d^2$. At every good <prime> $\ell\equiv2\pmod3$, cubing is a bijection on $\mathbb F_\ell$, so the number of points is $\ell+1$. Reduction injects the prime-to-$\ell$ torsion, since multiplication by an integer prime to $\ell$ is invertible in the <formal group of an elliptic curve>. For any prime $q>3$, the <Dirichlet theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions> supplies a good $\ell\equiv2\pmod3$, $\ell\equiv1\pmod q$, excluding $q$-torsion. Good primes $\ell\equiv5\pmod{12}$ exclude order $4$, and good primes $\ell\equiv11\pmod{18}$ exclude order $9$. Thus every rational torsion point is killed by $6$.

The <flexes of a diagonal cubic> give rational three-torsion exactly when $d$ is a cube, and then it has order $3$. Rational two-torsion requires $x^3=432d^2$, whose sole possible rational root exists exactly when $d/2$ is a cube. This equivalence follows by comparing prime valuations modulo $3$, including the valuation at $2$. The two conditions cannot hold together because $2$ is not a rational cube. The asserted classification follows.

= Flexes of a diagonal cubic
{parent=Diagonal cubic elliptic curve}
{title2=$E_d[3]$}

Let $r^3=d\ne0$ and let $\zeta$ be a primitive cube root of unity. The nine <inflection points of a plane cubic> on $X^3+Y^3+dZ^3=0$ are
$$
(1:-\zeta^j:0),\qquad(0:-r\zeta^j:1),\qquad(-r\zeta^j:0:1),\qquad j=0,1,2.
$$
The <Hessian criterion for flexes of a plane cubic> gives the Hessian determinant $216dXYZ$, so every flex lies on one of the three coordinate lines. Their intersections give precisely the displayed points. With $(1:-1:0)$ as identity, these are exactly the <three-torsion points are flexes of a plane cubic> configurations: the tangent divisor at a flex is $3P$, giving $3(P-O)=0$.

Over $\mathbb Q$ only the first point with $j=0$ is rational on $Z=0$. The other two coordinate lines each give one rational point precisely when $d$ is a rational cube. Consequently the rational three-torsion subgroup is $\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z$ when $d$ is a cube and is trivial otherwise.

= Elliptic curve group law from Riemann-Roch
{parent=Elliptic curve}
{title2=$E(k)\cong\operatorname{Pic}^0(E)$}

= Elliptic curve group law
{synonym}

Fix $O\in E(k)$. On a genus-one smooth projective curve, every degree-one divisor class has a unique effective point representative by the <Riemann-Roch theorem>: its section space has dimension one, since the complementary canonical-divisor degree is negative. Thus $P\mapsto[P-O]$ is a bijection onto the <degree-zero Picard group of a curve>. Transporting divisor-class addition proves the group axioms, with identity $O$, and characterizes $P+Q$ by $P+Q\sim R+O$.

= Three-torsion points are flexes of a plane cubic
{parent=Elliptic curve}
{title2=$3(P-O)=0$}

Let $E$ be a smooth plane cubic with an inflection point $O$ as identity. A point $P$ with $3(P-O)=0$ in <Picard group> has a line whose intersection divisor with $E$ is $3P$. Indeed, $\mathcal O_E(3P)\cong\mathcal O_E(3O)\cong\mathcal O_E(1)$. On this genus-one curve, the <Riemann-Roch theorem> gives $h^0(\mathcal O_E(1))=3$: the dual term has negative degree. Restriction of ambient linear forms is injective and its source also has dimension three, so it is an isomorphism. The section vanishing at $3P$ therefore comes from a line. Conversely, such a line gives $3P\sim3O$, so $P$ is a three-torsion point. This includes $O$; points of exact order three are the other such points.

= Complex uniformization of an elliptic curve
{parent=Elliptic curve}
{title2=$E(\mathbb C)\simeq\mathbb C/L$}

Every complex <elliptic curve>, as a complex Lie group, is the quotient of $\mathbb C$ by a rank-two lattice. The coordinate functions are built from the <Weierstrass elliptic function> and its derivative. Holomorphic group homomorphisms lift to scalar multiplication on $\mathbb C$, and their endomorphism condition is $aL\subseteq L$. This turns <complex multiplication> into a statement about lattice stabilizers.

= Weierstrass uniformization by half-period values
{parent=Elliptic curve}
{c}
{title2=$\mathbb C/\Lambda\cong\{y^2=4x^3-g_2x-g_3\}\cup\{O\}$}

The <Weierstrass elliptic function> and its derivative give an isomorphism $z\mapsto(\wp(z),\wp'(z))$ from a one-dimensional <complex torus> to a <smooth plane cubic>, with $0$ sent to its identity at infinity. The three nonzero <two-torsion points of a complex torus> are zeros of $\wp'$. Its triple pole implies that these three zeros are simple. If two <half-period values of the Weierstrass elliptic function> coincided, $\wp-e$ would have at least four zeros counted with <multiplicity>, contradicting its double pole. The three cubic roots are therefore distinct, proving <smoothness of an algebraic variety>. The degree-two map $\wp$ has fibers $\{z,-z\}$, distinguished by $\wp'$ away from the half-periods, proving bijectivity. A line pulls back to an <elliptic function> with a triple pole, and the <zero-pole sum of an elliptic function> says that its three intersections sum to zero on the torus. This gives exactly the <chord-and-tangent group law>, including repeated intersections interpreted by <intersection multiplicity>.

= Nonparametrizability of an elliptic curve
{parent=Elliptic curve}

An <elliptic curve> in characteristic zero admits no nonconstant <rational map of projective varieties> from the <projective line>. Over $\mathbb C$, write its equation as $y^2=\prod_{j=1}^3(x-e_j)$ with distinct $e_j$. Substituting $x=u/v$ with <coprime polynomials> gives $(yv^2)^2=v\prod_j(u-e_jv)$. These four pairwise coprime factors must all be squares. The <polynomial pencil with four square members> forces $u,v$ constant, ruling out a nonconstant <rational parametrization of an algebraic curve>.

= j-invariant of an elliptic curve
{parent=Elliptic curve}
{c}
{title2=$j(E)=c_4^3/\Delta$}

The j-invariant is unchanged by admissible changes of <Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve>. With $b_2=a_1^2+4a_2$ and $b_4=a_1a_3+2a_4$, set $c_4=b_2^2-24b_4$ and $j=c_4^3/\Delta$, where $\Delta$ is the <elliptic-curve discriminant>. For <good reduction> over a <local field>, an integral equation with unit discriminant shows that $j$ lies in the <valuation ring>. Thus a negative <valuation> of $j$ proves bad reduction, even if the displayed equation has not been proved minimal.

= Abel-Jacobi map of a genus-one curve
{title2=$P\mapsto[P-O]$}
{parent=Elliptic curve}

Let $E$ be a <genus one curve> over an algebraically closed field and choose $O\in E$. The map
$$
E\longrightarrow\operatorname{Cl}^0(E),
\qquad P\longmapsto[P-O]
$$
is an isomorphism. For a degree-zero divisor $D$, <Riemann-Roch theorem>[Riemann--Roch] gives a nonzero section of $D+O$, so $D\sim P-O$ for some point $P$. If $P-O\sim P'-O$ with $P\ne P'$, a rational function with divisor $P-P'$ would give a degree-one map to the <projective line>, contradicting that $E$ has genus one. The Abel--Jacobi map transports addition in the divisor class group to the elliptic-curve group law.

= Involution of a degree-two map from a genus one curve
{parent=Abel-Jacobi map of a genus-one curve}
{title2=$\iota(p)=s-p$}

Over an algebraically closed field of characteristic different from two, a degree-two map from a smooth <genus one curve> to the <projective line> has a deck involution. After choosing an origin, all its fiber <divisors on an algebraic curve> are linearly equivalent and have the same group sum $s$, by the <Abel-Jacobi map of a genus-one curve>. The involution is consequently $p\mapsto s-p$, including its fixed ramification points. Composing two such involutions gives a <translation on an elliptic curve>.

= Chord-and-tangent group law
{parent=Elliptic curve}

On a smooth plane cubic with identity point $O$, a line through points $P$ and $Q$, interpreted as the tangent when $P=Q$, meets the cubic in a third point $R$. The chord-and-tangent group law defines $P+Q$ as the third intersection of the line through $R$ and $O$ with the cubic.

= Torsion multiples in a two-parameter Weierstrass family
{parent=Chord-and-tangent group law}
{title2=$y^2+axy+by=x^3+bx^2,\quad P=(0,0)$}

Assume the <elliptic-curve discriminant> is nonzero. Then $b\ne0$, and tangency at $P$ gives $2P=(-b,b(a-1))$ and $-2P=(-b,0)$. The line through $P,2P$ has slope $1-a$ and gives $3P=(1-a,a-b-1)$, $-3P=(1-a,(1-a)^2)$. Consequently $P$ has exact order four when $a=1$, exact order five when $a=b+1$, and exact order six when $b=(a-1)(2-a)$. The nonsingularity condition excludes the zero values of $b$ that would otherwise invalidate these torsion conclusions.

= Associativity of the chord-and-tangent law via divisor classes
{parent=Chord-and-tangent group law}

For a smooth <genus one curve> with origin $O$, the map $P\mapsto[(P)-(O)]$ identifies geometric points with the <degree-zero Picard group of a curve>. <Riemann-Roch theorem> gives a unique effective representative for each divisor class of degree one: its Riemann-Roch space has dimension one. A line cuts a plane cubic in a divisor linearly equivalent to $3(O)$ when $O$ is the Weierstrass flex. The two successive line intersections in the <chord-and-tangent group law> therefore give addition of the corresponding divisor classes. Associativity follows by injectivity of the identification and associativity of divisor-class addition, including tangent intersection multiplicities.

= Translation on an elliptic curve
{parent=Chord-and-tangent group law}
{title2=$\tau_Q(P)=P+Q$}

For a fixed point $Q$ on an <elliptic curve>, translation is the automorphism $P\mapsto P+Q$. Its inverse is translation by $-Q$. Translations let local computations of an <invariant differential on an elliptic curve> control every point of the curve.

= Smooth-locus group of a singular Weierstrass cubic
{parent=Chord-and-tangent group law}
{title2=$C_{\mathrm{sm}}(K)$}

For a singular projective Weierstrass cubic, its <smooth locus of a variety> contains the point at infinity and carries the <chord-and-tangent group law>. The singular point is excluded. Over an <algebraic closure>, a node gives the <multiplicative algebraic group> and a cusp gives the <additive group>. A node whose branches are not defined over the base <field> gives a nonsplit one-dimensional <algebraic torus>. This group is distinct from an <elliptic curve>, whose projective model is smooth.

= Elliptic-curve addition formula
{parent=Chord-and-tangent group law}

On $y^2=x^3+a_2x^2+a_4x+a_6$, let $\lambda$ be the secant slope through distinct points $P,Q$, or the tangent slope when $P=Q$. Then
$$
x(P+Q)=\lambda^2-a_2-x(P)-x(Q),
\qquad
y(P+Q)=-y(P)+\lambda\bigl(x(P)-x(P+Q)\bigr).
$$

= Symmetric-coordinate identity for elliptic-curve addition
{parent=Elliptic-curve addition formula}
{title2=$(s_2-a)^2=4s_1(s_3+b)$}

On $y^2=x^3+ax+b$ in <characteristic of a field> other than two, take the <elementary symmetric polynomials> $s_i$ of $x(P),x(Q),x(P+Q)$. If the line through $P,Q$ is $y=\lambda x+\mu$, its third intersection is $-(P+Q)$, so coefficient comparison gives $s_1=\lambda^2$, $s_2=a-2\lambda\mu$, $s_3=\mu^2-b$. Hence $(s_2-a)^2=4s_1(s_3+b)$. This is an identity of <rational functions>; tangent cases follow by specialization, and coordinates at infinity are handled through the projective addition map.

= Weierstrass equations of an elliptic curve

A Weierstrass equation presents an elliptic curve as

y^2+a_1xy+a_3y=x^3+a_2x^2+a_4x+a_6
$$
with nonzero discriminant, together with the point at infinity $O$.

= Legendre form of an elliptic curve
{parent=Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve}
{c}
{title2=$Y^2Z=X(X-Z)(X-\lambda Z),\quad\lambda\ne0,1$}

Over an <algebraically closed field> of characteristic different from two, a cubic <Weierstrass model> with distinct roots becomes this form by sending two roots to zero and one, then scaling $Y$ by a square root of the resulting nonzero coefficient. The third root becomes $\lambda\notin\{0,1\}$. The point $[0:1:0]$ is a <flex> and the curve is a <genus one curve>.

= Pole orders on a smooth cubic in Weierstrass form
{parent=Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve}
{title2=$\operatorname{ord}_\infty x=-2,\quad\operatorname{ord}_\infty y=-3$}

For $y^2=\prod_{j=1}^3(x-\lambda_j)$ with distinct roots in characteristic different from two, the unique point at infinity has local coordinates $u=X/Y$, $v=Z/Y$ satisfying $v=u^3+O(u^5)$. Thus $x=u/v$ and $y=1/v$ have poles two and three. Regular functions off infinity have the unique form $A(x)+yB(x)$; the even and odd leading pole orders cannot cancel. A basis of $L(nP_\infty)$ consists of $x^j$ with $2j\le n$ and $yx^j$ with $3+2j\le n$. This gives dimension one at $n=0$ and dimension $n$ for $n\ge1$ without <Riemann-Roch theorem>.

= Weierstrass construction from Riemann-Roch in genus one
{parent=Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve}

Let $E$ be a <genus one curve> and choose $O\in E$. Since a canonical divisor is linearly equivalent to zero, <Riemann-Roch theorem>[Riemann--Roch] gives $\ell(nO)=n$ for $n\geq1$. Choose $x\in L(2O)\setminus L(O)$ and $y\in L(3O)\setminus L(2O)$. Their pole orders at $O$ are two and three. The seven functions
$$
1,x,y,x^2,xy,x^3,y^2\in L(6O)
$$
are linearly dependent because $\ell(6O)=6$, and comparison of pole orders puts the relation in <Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve>[Weierstrass form]. The complete linear system $|3O|$ is very ample and realizes this relation as a smooth plane cubic. In characteristic other than two and three, completing the square and translating $x$ produce a <short Weierstrass form>.

= Elliptic-curve discriminant
{title2=$\Delta$}
{parent=Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve}

The discriminant of a Weierstrass equation is a polynomial $\Delta$ in its coefficients whose nonvanishing is equivalent to the projective cubic being smooth. For $y^2=x^3+Ax+B$,
$$
\Delta=-16(4A^3+27B^2).
$$

= Admissible change of Weierstrass coordinates
{c}
{parent=Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve}

An admissible change of Weierstrass coordinates has the form
$$
x=u^2x'+r,
\qquad
y=u^3y'+u^2sx'+t,
$$
where $u$ is nonzero. These are exactly the coordinate changes preserving Weierstrass form and the point at infinity.

= Short Weierstrass form
{title2=$y^2=x^3+Ax+B$}
{c}
{parent=Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve}

Over a field of characteristic other than two or three, every elliptic curve has a short Weierstrass equation $y^2=x^3+Ax+B$ with $4A^3+27B^2\ne0$.

= Invariant differential on an elliptic curve
{parent=Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve}

= Elliptic invariant differential
{synonym}

On $y^2=x^3+ax+b$ in characteristic other than two, the differential
$$
\omega=\frac{dx}{2y}
$$
extends regularly and without zeros across the point at infinity. Translation by every point preserves it.

= Translation invariance of a Weierstrass differential
{parent=Invariant differential on an elliptic curve}
{title2=$\tau_Q^*\omega=\omega$}

On a nonsingular equation $y^2=x^3+a_2x^2+a_4x+a_6$ in characteristic different from two, $\omega=dx/(2y)$ is regular and nowhere zero. For a chord of slope $h$ giving $P+Q=(X,Y)$, differentiating the line-intersection identity yields $dX/dx=Y/y$, so $dX/(2Y)=dx/(2y)$. Extension across the exceptional cases proves invariance under every <translation on an elliptic curve>. The addition map consequently satisfies $\mu^*\omega=\operatorname{pr}_1^*\omega+\operatorname{pr}_2^*\omega$, and $[n]^*\omega=n\omega$.

= Reduction of an elliptic curve

Reducing a minimal integral Weierstrass equation modulo the maximal ideal gives a projective cubic over the residue field. Reducing primitive projective coordinates defines the reduction map on rational points.

= Good reduction of an elliptic curve
{parent=Reduction of an elliptic curve}

= Good reduction
{synonym}

An elliptic curve has good reduction at a finite place when its reduced minimal Weierstrass equation is nonsingular, equivalently when its minimal discriminant has valuation zero.

= Good-reduction map respects elliptic addition
{parent=Good reduction of an elliptic curve}
{title2=$\widetilde{P+Q}=\widetilde P+\widetilde Q$}

An integral <Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve> with unit <elliptic-curve discriminant> defines a smooth proper elliptic curve over the <valuation ring>. Properness identifies points over the <local field> with integral sections. The group law, constructed by adding degree-zero divisor classes, is a morphism over the valuation ring, and base change to the <residue field> preserves it. Hence reducing primitive projective coordinates is a <group homomorphism>, including exceptional affine addition cases.

= Supersingular reduction of an elliptic curve
{parent=Good reduction of an elliptic curve}

= Supersingular reduction
{synonym}

A good reduction in characteristic $p$ is supersingular when its geometric $p$-torsion is trivial, equivalently its <formal group of an elliptic curve> has height two. Good inert primes of a <CM elliptic curve> are supersingular. The ordinary bounded-measure and torsion-Selmer formulation must then be modified, for instance using signed local conditions in the cyclotomic tower.

= Ordinary reduction of an elliptic curve
{parent=Good reduction of an elliptic curve}

= Ordinary reduction
{synonym}

A good reduction in characteristic $p$ is ordinary when the geometric $p$-torsion has $p$ points, equivalently its <formal group of an elliptic curve> has height one. For a <CM elliptic curve>, a good prime split in its CM field is ordinary. One of the two roots of $X^2-a_pX+p$ is a $p$-adic unit, enabling the bounded ordinary <p-adic L-function>.

= Surjectivity of good reduction over a local field
{parent=Good reduction of an elliptic curve}

If an <elliptic curve> has <good reduction> over a <local field> $K$, its integral projective model is smooth. Every point over the <residue field> lifts by the <Hensel lemma>, applied in a chart with a unit <partial derivative>. Thus reduction gives an exact sequence $0\to E_1(K)\to E(K)\to\widetilde E(k)\to0$. The <kernel of reduction of an elliptic curve> is the <maximal ideal> of the <valuation ring>, with the <formal group of an elliptic curve> as its <group operation>.

= Kernel of reduction of an elliptic curve
{title2=$E_1(K)$}
{parent=Good reduction of an elliptic curve}

For an elliptic curve with good reduction over a local field, the kernel of reduction consists of the points reducing to the identity. The parameter $t=-x/y$ identifies it with the maximal ideal equipped with the <formal group of an elliptic curve>.

= Reduction certificate for a nonintegral elliptic point
{parent=Kernel of reduction of an elliptic curve}

On an integral <Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve> with <good reduction> at a <prime> $p$, a nonidentity <rational point> reducing to the identity cannot have both coordinates integral at $p$. Indeed, an affine integral pair reduces to an affine point, distinct from the point at infinity. This can prove nonintegrality of a large <linear combination> without computing its coordinates. The nonidentity condition is essential and can often be checked using the known <torsion subgroup>.

= Reduction exponent obstruction to integral multiples
{parent=Kernel of reduction of an elliptic curve}
{title2=$N\widetilde E(k)=0\ \Longrightarrow\ NP\in E_1(K)$}

At a prime of <good reduction of an elliptic curve>, the reduction map is a group homomorphism on all local points. If $N$ annihilates the reduced finite group, every $NP$ reduces to the identity at infinity. A finite point with integral affine coordinates reduces to an affine point, so a nonidentity $NP$ cannot have integral affine coordinates. The identity has no affine coordinates and must be treated separately.

= Bad reduction of an elliptic curve
{parent=Reduction of an elliptic curve}

= Bad reduction
{synonym}

An elliptic curve has bad reduction at a finite place when its reduced minimal Weierstrass equation is singular, equivalently when its minimal discriminant has positive valuation.

= Discriminant valuation obstruction to good reduction
{parent=Bad reduction of an elliptic curve}
{title2=$v_p(\Delta)\not\equiv0\pmod{12}\implies\text{bad reduction at }p$}

Discriminants of isomorphic <Weierstrass equations of an elliptic curve> differ by a twelfth power of the scale parameter, so their <valuations> differ by a multiple of twelve. An integral model with <good reduction> has discriminant valuation zero. Thus a displayed integral model with discriminant valuation not divisible by twelve proves intrinsic <bad reduction>. More generally, a displayed discriminant valuation between zero and eleven is already minimal, since any smaller integral-model valuation would be nonnegative and differ by twelve. Positive valuations divisible by twelve alone do not establish bad reduction; rescaling may remove them.

= Bad primes of a congruent number curve with odd parameter
{parent=Bad reduction of an elliptic curve}
{title2=$\{2\}\cup\{p\text{ odd}:v_p(D)\text{ odd}\}$}

For odd nonzero $D$, the intrinsic bad primes of $y^2=x^3-D^2x$ are exactly the displayed set. If $v_p(D)=2k$, the change $x=p^{2k}X$, $y=p^{3k}Y$ removes that even valuation from the parameter and gives unit discriminant at odd $p$. If the valuation is odd, after the same removal the discriminant valuation is six. Discriminant valuations of isomorphic Weierstrass models differ by multiples of twelve, so no integral model has unit discriminant. At two the original discriminant valuation is also six. Thus primes dividing a nonminimal displayed discriminant need not be intrinsic bad primes: $E_9$ is isomorphic to $E_1$ and has good reduction at three.

= Reduction of torsion points on an elliptic curve
{parent=Reduction of an elliptic curve}

At a prime of good reduction with residue characteristic $p$, reduction is injective on the prime-to-$p$ torsion of an elliptic curve. Comparing reductions at two good primes therefore bounds rational torsion by finite point counts.

= Torsion exclusion from good reduction counts
{parent=Reduction of torsion points on an elliptic curve}

For an <elliptic curve> over $\mathbb Q$, reduction at a good prime $p$ is injective on the $\ell$-primary <torsion subgroup> when $\ell\ne p$: its kernel is a <formal group>, where multiplication by $\ell$ is bijective. Hence a rational point of order $\ell$ forces $\ell\mid\#\widetilde E(\mathbb F_p)$. If for every possible prime $\ell$ some good $p\ne\ell$ has a point count not divisible by $\ell$, rational torsion is trivial. In particular two distinct good primes $p,r$ with coprime counts, $p\nmid\#\widetilde E(\mathbb F_r)$ and $r\nmid\#\widetilde E(\mathbb F_p)$ suffice: the coprime counts exclude other primes, and the two cross conditions exclude $p,r$. This argument does not incorrectly apply prime-to-$p$ injectivity to $p$-power torsion.

= Two-prime bound on rational elliptic torsion
{parent=Reduction of torsion points on an elliptic curve}

Let an <elliptic curve> over $\mathbb Q$ have <good reduction> at distinct primes $p,r$, with point counts $N_p,N_r$. For a torsion point, its $\ell$-primary part injects into every reduction whose residue characteristic differs from $\ell$. Consequently its $\ell$-power order has exponent at most $\min(v_\ell(N_p),v_\ell(N_r))$ when $\ell\ne p,r$, at most $v_p(N_r)$ when $\ell=p$, and at most $v_r(N_p)$ when $\ell=r$. This precise form avoids assuming injectivity on residue-characteristic torsion. Counts five at two and seven at three force trivial rational torsion.

= Prime-to-p torsion lifts uniquely at good reduction
{parent=Reduction of torsion points on an elliptic curve}
{title2=$E(K)[q^\infty]\cong\widetilde E(k)[q^\infty],\quad q\ne p$}

For <good reduction of an elliptic curve> over a complete <local field> of residue characteristic $p$, smoothness and the <Hensel lemma> make reduction surjective. Its kernel is a <formal group> on the maximal ideal, where multiplication by $q^m$, $q\ne p$, is bijective by <prime-to-residue-characteristic multiplication on a formal group>. Thus there is no nonzero $q$-power torsion in the kernel. Given a lift $P$ of a reduced point killed by $q^m$, choose the unique kernel point $R$ with $q^mR=q^mP$. Then $P-R$ is a torsion lift. This proves both surjectivity and uniqueness of torsion lifting. For finite residue field these groups are finite.

= Prime-two torsion bound for good reduction
{parent=Reduction of torsion points on an elliptic curve}
{title2=$\#(E_1\cap E(\mathbb Q_2)_{\mathrm{tors}})\leq2$}

For an <elliptic curve> with good reduction over $\mathbb Q_2$, let $E_1$ be the reduction kernel and $E_2$ its formal subgroup with parameter in $4\mathbb Z_2$. The <deep logarithm subgroup of a formal group> makes $E_2$ torsion-free, while $[E_1:E_2]=2$. Thus the torsion of the reduction kernel has order at most $2$. Combined with the <Hasse theorem for elliptic curves>, rational torsion of order $16$ forces bad reduction at $2$.

= Formal group
{synonym}

A one-dimensional commutative formal group law over a ring $R$ is a series $F(X,Y)\in R[[X,Y]]$ satisfying the identity, commutativity, and associativity axioms. A homomorphism from $F$ to $G$ is a series $\theta(T)\in TR[[T]]$ satisfying $\theta(F(X,Y))=G(\theta(X),\theta(Y))$.

= Invariant differential of a formal group law
{parent=Formal group law}

For a one-dimensional <formal group law>, write $f(T)=\partial_YF(T,0)$. Its invariant differential is $dT/f(T)$. Differentiating associativity gives $\partial_YF(X,Y)f(Y)=f(F(X,Y))$, which expresses invariance under translation. In characteristic zero, integrating this differential constructs the <formal logarithm>.

= Isomorphism of formal group laws
{parent=Formal group law}
{title2=$h(F(X,Y))=G(h(X),h(Y))$}

An isomorphism from <formal group law> $F$ to $G$ over $R$ is a formal series $h(T)=a_1T+a_2T^2+\cdots$ with $a_1\in R^*$ and $h(F(X,Y))=G(h(X),h(Y))$. Coefficient recursion gives a compositional inverse over $R$. An isomorphism of formal series need not converge on an arbitrary prescribed analytic domain; convergence must be checked separately.

= Formal inverse

Every one-dimensional <formal group law> has a unique formal inverse $i_F(X)=-X+O(X^2)$ satisfying $F(X,i_F(X))=0$. Comparing coefficients recursively determines it degree by degree.

Over an <algebra over a field> whose scalar field is $\mathbb Q$, the series $\exp(X)-1$ defines an isomorphism from the <formal additive group> to the <formal multiplicative group>, with inverse $\log(1+X)$.

= Invertible morphism criterion for formal group laws
{parent=Formal group law}

A morphism $h(T)=uT+O(T^2)$ of one-dimensional formal group laws over a ring is an isomorphism when $u$ is a unit. Recursive coefficient comparison constructs its compositional inverse, and the morphism identity shows that this inverse is also a formal-group morphism.

= Multiplication isomorphism of a formal group law
{title2=$[n]_F$}

For a one-dimensional <formal group law> $F$ over a ring $R$, the multiplication series satisfies $[n]_F(T)=nT+O(T^2)$. It is an isomorphism exactly when $n$ is a unit of $R$.

= Prime-to-residue-characteristic multiplication on a formal group
{parent=Multiplication isomorphism of a formal group law}
{title2=$[n]_F:F(\mathfrak m)\xrightarrow{\sim}F(\mathfrak m),\quad p\nmid n$}

Let $R$ be a complete <discrete valuation ring> of residue <characteristic of a field> $p$, with <maximal ideal> $\mathfrak m$, and let $F$ be a one-dimensional <formal group law> over $R$. Its multiplication series is $[n]_F(T)=nT+O(T^2)$. If $p\nmid n$, the <invertible morphism criterion for formal group laws> constructs its inverse with coefficients in $R$. Both <formal power series> converge on $\mathfrak m$, since their coefficients are integral and the powers of an element of $\mathfrak m$ tend to zero. Their compositions are the identity there. Thus multiplication by $n$ is bijective, including when $p=2$ and $n$ is odd. Every finite-order element of $F(\mathfrak m)$ consequently has <order of a group element> equal to a <prime power> with prime $p$.

= Formal logarithm

Over a characteristic-zero field, a one-dimensional commutative formal group law has a unique strict isomorphism to the additive formal group, called its formal logarithm. It satisfies $\log_F(F(X,Y))=\log_F(X)+\log_F(Y)$.

= Valuation bound for coefficients of an integral formal logarithm
{parent=Formal logarithm}
{title2=$v_K(c_n)\geq-e\,v_p(n)$}

For an integral one-dimensional <formal group law> over a finite extension of the p-adic field, the inverse of $\partial_YF(T,0)$ has integral coefficients because its constant term is one. Integrating this invariant differential gives $\log_F(T)=T+\sum_{n\geq2}c_nT^n$ with $nc_n$ integral. For $v_K(\pi)=1$ and $e=v_K(p)$ the displayed bound follows. The estimate $v_p(n)\leq(n-1)/(p-1)$ makes the nonlinear part strictly contracting on $\pi^s\mathcal O_K$ for $s>e/(p-1)$, yielding the <deep logarithm subgroup of a formal group>.

= Deep logarithm subgroup of a formal group
{parent=Formal logarithm}
{title2=$m>\frac{e}{p-1},\quad F(\pi^m\mathcal O_K)\cong(\mathcal O_K,+)$}

Let $F$ be a <formal group law> over the <valuation ring> of a finite extension $K/\mathbb Q_p$, with $v_K(\pi)=1$ and $e=v_K(p)$. If $m>e/(p-1)$, its <formal logarithm> is an isomorphism $F(\pi^m\mathcal O_K)\to(\pi^m\mathcal O_K,+)$. = Torsion test using a deep formal logarithm subgroup
{parent=Deep logarithm subgroup of a formal group}
{title2=$\log_F([N]P)\in Np^m\mathbb Z_p$}

Let $E/\mathbb Q_p$ have <good reduction>, and choose an integer $m>1/(p-1)$. Its <filtration of elliptic-curve points over a local field> has a <torsion-free group> $E_m$ identified by the <formal logarithm> with $p^m\mathbb Z_p$. The <quotient group> $G=E(\mathbb Q_p)/E_m$ is finite. For $g\in G$ of <order of a group element> $N$, choose a lift $P$. There is a <torsion point of an elliptic curve> in this coset exactly when the displayed condition holds. Indeed, it is equivalent to the existence of $Q\in E_m$ with $[N]Q=[N]P$. Then $P-Q$ is killed by $N$; conversely every torsion representative has order $N$, since $E_m$ is torsion-free. The logarithmic division and the <formal group exponential> give the unique such $Q$. Changing the lift changes the logarithm by an element of $Np^m\mathbb Z_p$, so the criterion is well-defined. This finite-coset test includes residue-characteristic torsion; only the <prime-to-p torsion lifts uniquely at good reduction> are automatic.

= Topological structure of a formal group on twice the 2-adic integers
{parent=Deep logarithm subgroup of a formal group}
{title2=$F(2\mathbb Z_2)\cong\mathbb Z_2\ \text{or}\ \mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z_2$}

For a one-dimensional commutative integral <formal group law>, the <formal logarithm> identifies $F(4\mathbb Z_2)$ with $4\mathbb Z_2$. This is an index-two subgroup of $F(2\mathbb Z_2)$. Choose a topological generator $h$ of the subgroup and an element $v$ outside it, and write $2v=ch$. If $c$ is odd, $v$ generates the whole group. If $c$ is even, $v-(c/2)h$ has order two and splits off a cyclic factor. The <formal additive group> and <formal multiplicative group> realize the two cases.

= Torsion-freeness of the formal group over Qp for odd p
{parent=Formal logarithm}
{title2=$\widehat E(p\mathbb Z_p)\text{ is torsion-free}$}

For odd $p$, a one-dimensional <formal group law> over $\mathbb Z_p$ has a <formal logarithm> $\log_F(T)=T+\sum_{j\geq2}c_jT^j/j$ with $c_j\in\mathbb Z_p$. This follows by integrating its integral invariant differential. For $t\in p\mathbb Z_p\setminus\{0\}$, $v_p(c_jt^j/j)>v_p(t)$ for $j\geq2$. Hence the series converges and $v_p(\log_F(t))=v_p(t)$, so its kernel is zero. As it is a homomorphism into a characteristic-zero additive group, there is no nonzero torsion. In particular, at an odd <prime> of <good reduction> over $\mathbb Q$, reduction injects the whole rational <torsion subgroup>, including its $p$-primary part.

= Two-prime formal-kernel test for nontorsion

= Positive definiteness of degree on elliptic-curve homomorphisms
{parent=Degree of an isogeny}
{title2=$q(\phi)=\deg\phi$}

Let $q(\phi)=\deg\phi$ for nonzero elements of the <homomorphism group of elliptic curves>, and $q(0)=0$. For an <elliptic curve> $E$, the degree-two coordinate $x$ on a <Weierstrass model> has a double pole at its identity and identifies a generic point only with its inverse. Thus on $E\times E$,
$$
\operatorname{div}(x(P)-x(Q))=\Delta+\Delta_- -2(\{O\}\times E)-2(E\times\{O\}),
$$
where the two diagonals are $P=Q$ and $P=-Q$. The resulting <line bundle> isomorphism is $a^*\mathcal O_E(O)\otimes s^*\mathcal O_E(O)\cong\operatorname{pr}_1^*\mathcal O_E(O)^{\otimes2}\otimes\operatorname{pr}_2^*\mathcal O_E(O)^{\otimes2}$, for the sum and difference maps $a,s$. Pull it back along $(\phi,\psi)$ and take degrees. This gives the <degree parallelogram law>, even when one homomorphism equals plus or minus the other: bundle restriction remains valid in these cases.

It implies $q(n\phi)=n^2q(\phi)$ by the second-difference recursion, and polarization gives the symmetric bilinear form $B(\phi,\psi)=(q(\phi+\psi)-q(\phi-\psi))/4$. To check additivity, the parallelogram law gives $B(x+z,y)+B(x-z,y)=2B(x,y)$; interchange $x,z$ and use oddness to get $B(x+z,y)-B(x-z,y)=2B(z,y)$. Adding proves bilinearity. Since a nonzero isogeny has positive degree, $q$ is positive on every nonzero element. On every finite-dimensional rational span the matrix of $B$ is rational. Positivity on rational vectors implies nonnegativity on real vectors by density; a nontrivial kernel of a rational matrix would contain a nonzero rational vector. Hence the real extension is positive definite. This proves the <positive-definite quadratic form> property, rather than only a parallelogram identity.

= Bilinear degree pairing for elliptic-curve homomorphisms

The integral polarization $2B(\phi,\psi)$ is integer-valued, while $B$ can be half-integral. The exceptional cases follow from $\deg[2]=4$. The same argument uses the quotient by negation in characteristic two. Thus degree, with degree zero assigned to the zero map, is a positive <quadratic form> on $\operatorname{Hom}(E_1,E_2)$.

= Vanishing trace criterion for a congruent number curve
{parent=Elliptic-curve point count over a finite field}
{title2=$a_p=0\iff p\equiv3\pmod4$}

= Two generators for elliptic curves over finite fields
{parent=Elliptic-curve point count over a finite field}
{title2=$E(\mathbb F_q)\cong\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z$}

For an <elliptic curve> over a finite field of characteristic $p$, its rational-point group is finite and abelian. <Prime-to-characteristic geometric torsion> bounds every other prime-torsion dimension by two. $$
The <binomial coefficient> is nonzero because every factorial involved is shorter than $p$. Thus the <trace of Frobenius> is not zero in the second case.

= Rational torsion in the family x times x plus one times x plus m squared
{parent=Torsion point of an elliptic curve}
{title2=$E(\mathbb Q)_{\rm tors}\cong\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$}

For integral $m\geq2$, suppose $E:y^2=x(x+1)(x+m^2)$ has <good reduction> at five or seven. At five, $m^2\equiv4$; at seven, $m^2\equiv2$ or $4$. These generate the stated group of order eight.

= Rational torsion on y squared equals x cubed plus positive k x
{parent=Torsion point of an elliptic curve}
{title2=$E(\mathbb Q)_{\rm tors}\cong\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\text{ or }\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$}

For positive integral $k$, the rational <torsion subgroup> of $y^2=x^3+kx$ has order dividing four. Indeed, <torsion-freeness of the formal group over Qp for odd p>, <reduction of torsion points on an elliptic curve>, and the <point-count criterion for y squared equals x cubed plus k x> bound its order by $p+1$ at all good <primes> $p\equiv3\pmod4$. The <Dirichlet theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions> excludes every odd torsion <prime> and bounds the two-primary part by four, by choosing $p\equiv3\pmod8$. There is exactly one nonzero rational <2-torsion> point, namely $(0,0)$. A rational half of it must have $x^2=k$ by the <elliptic-curve addition formula>. Writing $k=s^2$ with $s>0$, its equation is $y^2=2s^3$, so $s=2t^2$ and $k=4t^4$. Conversely $(2t^2,4t^3)$ has order four. Thus the cyclic group of order four occurs exactly for $k=4t^4$, $t\geq1$. The order bound itself holds for every nonzero integral $k$ of either sign: positivity was used only to classify the rational points of orders two and four.

{title2=$\widehat h(P+Q)+\widehat h(P-Q)=2\widehat h(P)+2\widehat h(Q)$}

= Symmetric-square proof of the naive height parallelogram estimate
{parent=Height parallelogram identity}
{title2=$h_x(P+Q)+h_x(P-Q)=2h_x(P)+2h_x(Q)+O(1)$}

The <biquadratic morphism for elliptic sums and differences> has no common zero, so the local coefficient norms give
$$
h([A:B:C])=2h(x(P))+2h(x(Q))+O(1),
$$
uniformly in $P,Q$. Upper bounds use the fixed coefficients. Lower bounds follow from the absence of common zeros over every <local field>; the constants are $1$ outside finitely many places, where the integral morphism still has no base point after reduction.

For the binary quadratic with roots $u,v\in\mathbb P^1(K)$, the <projective height> of its coefficient vector is $h(u)+h(v)+O(1)$. At nonarchimedean places the coefficient maximum norm of a product of linear <polynomials> is the product of their maximum norms by the <multiplicativity of the nonarchimedean polynomial norm>. At archimedean places the same norms are comparable up to a fixed factor for degree two. This also includes roots at infinity. Combining the comparisons yields $h_x(P+Q)+h_x(P-Q)=2h_x(P)+2h_x(Q)+O(1)$. After dividing the identity for $2^nP,2^nQ$ by $4^n$ and taking limits, it gives the exact <height parallelogram identity> for the <canonical height of an elliptic curve>.

= Biquadratic morphism for elliptic sums and differences
{parent=Height parallelogram identity}
{title2=$\Phi:\mathbb P^1\times\mathbb P^1\to\mathbb P^2$}

For a nonsingular <short Weierstrass form> $y^2=x^3+ax+b$ over a <number field>, the unordered pair of coordinates $x(P+Q),x(P-Q)$ depends only on $x(P),x(Q)$. As a binary quadratic's roots, that pair is represented in $\mathbb P^2$ by the coefficients
$$
A=x_1^2x_2^2-2ax_1x_2-4b(x_1+x_2)+a^2,\quad B=2(x_1x_2+a)(x_1+x_2)+4b,\quad C=(x_1-x_2)^2,
$$
with <polynomial> $CT^2-BT+A$. Homogenizing gives three forms of bidegree $(2,2)$ on $\mathbb P^1\times\mathbb P^1$. They have no common zero. Off the diagonal $C\ne0$; on the finite diagonal $x_1=x_2=t$, $B=4f(t)$ and $A=f'(t)^2-8tf(t)$ for $f(t)=t^3+at+b$, so simultaneous vanishing would make a multiple root of $f$. At $(\infty,\infty)$, the homogeneous $A$ is nonzero. Therefore they define a global morphism, also when a sum or difference is the identity.

= Kummer exact sequence of an elliptic curve
{c}
{parent=Kummer-theoretic proof of the weak Mordell-Weil theorem}

The exact sequence of Galois modules
$$
0\to E[n]\to E(\overline K)\xrightarrow{[n]}E(\overline K)\to0
$$
induces the <Kummer map of an elliptic curve> from $E(K)/nE(K)$ into $H^1(K,E[n])$.

= Unramified division points at good reduction
{synonym}

At a prime of <good reduction of an elliptic curve> with residue characteristic not dividing $m$, multiplication by $m$ extends to a finite étale map of the smooth proper elliptic group scheme over the valuation ring. A local rational point extends to a section by properness. Its division fibre is therefore a finite étale torsor, so the <Kummer map of an elliptic curve> gives an unramified cohomology class. This bounds ramification in the <weak Mordell-Weil theorem>.

A direct proof describes the coordinates, not only the cohomology class. Reduce $P$ by properness and choose a point $\overline Q$ with $[m]\overline Q=\widetilde P$ over a finite extension of the <residue field>. Over the corresponding <unramified extension> of the complete <local field>, lift $\overline Q$ by the <Hensel lemma>. The error $[m]Q_0-P$ is in the <kernel of reduction of an elliptic curve>; <prime-to-residue-characteristic multiplication on a formal group> corrects it uniquely. There is no $m$-torsion in that kernel, even after a ramified field extension, so every division point with that reduction equals this unramified lift. Consequently all <division points of an elliptic curve> above $P$ have unramified coordinate fields. The torsion case is discussed in \a[https://www.jmilne.org/math/Books/EC2.pdf][J. S. Milne's Elliptic Curves, Chapter V, Proposition 8.1].

= Selmer group of an elliptic curve
{c}
{synonym}

The $n$-Selmer group consists of the classes in $H^1(K,E[n])$ whose restriction at every place lies in the image of the local <Kummer map of an elliptic curve>. It is finite and contains $E(K)/nE(K)$.

= Selmer exact sequence and rank bound
{parent=n-Selmer group}
{c}
{title2=$\operatorname{Sel}^{(n)}(E/K)$}

For an <elliptic curve> over a number field $K$, global and local <Kummer maps of an elliptic curve> give $0\to E(K)/nE(K)\to\operatorname{Sel}^{(n)}(E/K)\to\operatorname{Sha}(E/K)[n]\to0$. Indeed a Selmer class maps to a locally trivial torsor; conversely any locally trivial torsor killed by $n$ has a global Kummer-sequence lift whose local restrictions lie in the local Kummer images. For a prime $\ell$, the <Mordell-Weil theorem> therefore gives $\dim_{\mathbb F_\ell}\operatorname{Sel}^{(\ell)}=\operatorname{rank}E(K)+\dim_{\mathbb F_\ell}E(K)[\ell]+\dim_{\mathbb F_\ell}\operatorname{Sha}(E/K)[\ell]$. Thus a Selmer computation supplies a rigorous upper rank bound, while independent rational points supply a lower bound; the gap need not represent undiscovered rational points.

= Prime-primary Selmer group of a CM elliptic curve
{parent=n-Selmer group}
{title2=$\operatorname{Sel}_{\mathfrak p^\infty}(E/F)$}

For a split CM prime $\mathfrak p$, use $E[\mathfrak p^\infty]$ in the <Galois cohomology> definition of the <Selmer group of an elliptic curve>, with local conditions given by its Kummer maps. The decomposition of the CM $p$-power torsion splits the full $p$-primary Selmer group into the two prime-primary pieces. Class-field descriptions must retain the CM-character twist and distinguish relaxed local conditions from the actual Selmer conditions.

= Tate–Shafarevich group
{title2=$\operatorname{Sha}(E/K)$}
{c}
{parent=n-Selmer group}
{wiki}

The Tate–Shafarevich group consists of the principal homogeneous spaces for $E$ over $K$ that have a point over every completion of $K$. Its $n$-torsion is the quotient of the $n$-Selmer group by $E(K)/nE(K)$.

= Height descent lemma
{parent=Mordell-Weil group}

= Height descent
{synonym}

Let an abelian group $A$ carry a nonnegative quadratic height with finite bounded subsets. If $A/nA$ is finite for some $n\geq2$, repeatedly choosing representatives modulo $nA$ reduces the height by a fixed factor until a bounded set is reached. The representatives and that finite bounded set generate $A$.

= Naive height contraction in elliptic halving descent
{parent=Height descent lemma}
{title2=$P=2Q+R\Longrightarrow h_x(Q)\leq\tfrac12h_x(P)+C_R$}

For a fixed rational point $R$ on an <elliptic curve> over a <number field>, the addition formula gives $h_x(P-R)\leq2h_x(P)+O_R(1)$. The duplication map has degree four, so $h_x(2Q)=4h_x(Q)+O(1)$. Combining them gives the displayed contraction. A finite set of representatives modulo twice the point group makes the constants uniform. Points above a fixed height threshold descend by a definite amount; <Northcott theorem> makes the terminal bounded-height set finite. This converts the <weak Mordell-Weil theorem> into the full finite-generation theorem.

= Canonical-height proof of Mordell-Weil finite generation
{parent=Height descent lemma}

For an <elliptic curve> over a <number field>, its nonnegative <canonical height of an elliptic curve> has finite bounded subsets by the <Northcott theorem> and its bounded difference from half the naive logarithmic height. By the <weak Mordell-Weil theorem>, choose representatives $R_i$ modulo $2E(K)$ and put $H=\max_i\widehat h(R_i)$. If $P=2Q+R_i$, the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> for the height pairing gives $\widehat h(Q)\leq(\widehat h(P)+H)/2$. Iterating brings every point into the finite set of height at most $H+1$. This set and the $R_i$ generate $E(K)$, proving the <Mordell-Weil theorem> without assuming finite generation when constructing the height.

{c}

The map is a connecting homomorphism from the multiplication exact sequence. Its image is constrained by the local conditions defining the <Selmer group of an elliptic curve>.

The multiplication-by-$n$ sequence gives an injective connecting map

= Isogeny Selmer group
{parent=Kummer map of an elliptic curve}
{title2=$\operatorname{Sel}^{\phi}(E/K)$}

For an <isogeny of elliptic curves> $\phi:E\to E'$ over a <number field> $K$, this finite subgroup of $H^1(K,E[\phi])$ consists of <Galois cohomology> classes whose restriction at every place comes from $E'(K_v)/\phi E(K_v)$. It fits into
$$
0\to E'(K)/\phi E(K)\to\operatorname{Sel}^{\phi}(E/K)\to\operatorname{Sha}(E/K)[\phi]\to0,
$$
where the last group is the kernel of the induced map on <Tate–Shafarevich groups>. Thus local computations bound the rational-point quotient without necessarily determining it. For multiplication by $n$, this recovers the <Selmer group of an elliptic curve>.

= Local-to-global gap in isogeny descent
{parent=Isogeny Selmer group}

Coverings surviving all <local solubility> tests need not have <rational points>. The quotient of an <isogeny Selmer group> by the rational-point image is a kernel in the <Tate–Shafarevich group>. A nonzero class therefore passes every local test but contributes no rational point to a search. This explains why <two-isogeny descent> can leave a gap between a certified upper bound and found lower bound for the <rank of an elliptic curve>. Higher descents address finer obstructions; see \a[https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.03234][Tom Fisher's paper on higher descents with rational two-torsion].

= Two-torsion square-class homomorphism
{parent=Kummer map of an elliptic curve}
{title2=$\alpha_T(P)=[x(P)-x(T)]$}

Let $E:y^2=f(x)$ with $f$ monic and separable, and let $T=(\theta,0)$. Over $K$ containing $\theta$, the map $\alpha_T(P)=[x(P)-\theta]$ for $P\ne O,T$, completed by $\alpha_T(O)=1$ and $\alpha_T(T)=[f^{\prime}(\theta)]$, is a <group homomorphism> to the <square-class group of a field>. A nonvertical chord has the product of its three $x-\theta$ values a square; a chord through $T$ instead has the product of the other two values equal to $f^{\prime}(\theta)$. These identities include tangencies.

= Two-descent on an elliptic curve
{parent=Kummer map of an elliptic curve}

When an elliptic curve has full rational <2-torsion>, its Kummer map embeds $E(\mathbb Q)/2E(\mathbb Q)$ into a product of square-class groups. Valuation and local-solubility conditions reduce the image to finitely many classes and thereby bound the rank of the <Mordell-Weil group>.

= Kummer pairing
{parent=Kummer map of an elliptic curve}

When $\mu_n\subset K$, the Kummer pairing sends $\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$ and $[x]\in K^*/(K^*)^n$ to $\sigma(\sqrt[n]{x})/\sqrt[n]{x}\in\mu_n$. Its elliptic analogue sends $\sigma$ and $[P]\in E(K)/nE(K)$ to $\sigma(Q)-Q\in E[n]$ for $nQ=P$.

= Halving cocycle with rational two-torsion
{parent=Kummer pairing}
{title2=$c_P(\sigma)=\sigma(Q)-Q,\quad 2Q=P$}

Suppose an <elliptic curve> $E/K$ has all four geometric <2-torsion> points rational. For $P\in E(K)$ choose $Q\in E(\overline K)$ with $2Q=P$. The function $c_P(\sigma)=\sigma(Q)-Q$ takes values in $E[2]$ and is a <group homomorphism> from the <absolute Galois group> to $E[2]$: its usual cocycle law becomes additivity because the action on $E[2]$ is trivial. Replacing $Q$ by another half, or $P$ by $P+2R$ with $R\in E(K)$, leaves it unchanged. Adding chosen halves proves additivity in $P$. Its kernel in $E(K)/2E(K)$ is zero, since $c_P=0$ precisely when the chosen half is rational. This constructs the injective map directly, without assuming any theorem of <two-descent on an elliptic curve> or finite generation of the <Mordell-Weil group>.

= Unramified halving fields for a split cubic
{parent=Halving cocycle with rational two-torsion}
{title2=$S=\{v:v(2(\alpha-\beta)(\alpha-\gamma)(\beta-\gamma))>0\}$}

Let $E:y^2=(x-\alpha)(x-\beta)(x-\gamma)$ with distinct <algebraic integers> in a <number field> $K$. For a rational point $P$, its halving field is unramified outside the finite set $S$ of primes dividing $2(\alpha-\beta)(\alpha-\gamma)(\beta-\gamma)$. At $v\notin S$, the <elliptic-curve discriminant> is a unit, so there is <good reduction>. A half of the reduction of $P$ is defined over a finite extension of the <residue field>. Pass to the corresponding <unramified extension> of the completion and lift this half using the <Hensel lemma>. The doubling error lies in the <formal kernel of a minimal Weierstrass equation>. Since two is a unit, <prime-to-residue-characteristic multiplication on a formal group> corrects this error uniquely. Every half differs by rational <2-torsion>, so all halves are unramified at $v$. The <halving cocycle with rational two-torsion> has image of order at most four, making the halving field a <Galois extension> of degree at most four. The <bounded-degree extensions with restricted ramification> are finite in number; there are only finitely many homomorphisms from their finite <Galois groups> to $E[2]$. Thus $E(K)/2E(K)$ is finite, without assuming any form of the <Mordell-Weil theorem>.

= Rational halving quotient bound from root size
{parent=Unramified halving fields for a split cubic}
{title2=$\#(E(\mathbb Q)/2E(\mathbb Q))\leq 2^{2m+2}$}

For distinct integral roots $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ with $m=\max(|\alpha|,|\beta|,|\gamma|)$, the finite set in <unramified halving fields for a split cubic> contains only <primes> at most $2m$. A quadratic <Galois character> unramified away from $s$ primes is represented by a signed <square-free integer> supported on those primes, giving at most $2^{s+1}$ characters. The <halving cocycle with rational two-torsion> has two independent coordinates in $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$, so the quotient has at most $2^{2s+2}$ elements. Since $m\geq1$ and there are at most $m$ primes at most $2m$, this gives $2^{2m+2}$. The bound is deliberately crude but depends only on the root size.

= Finite-extension kernel of a Kummer map
{parent=Kummer pairing}

For a finite Galois extension $L/K$, the kernel of
$$
K^*/(K^*)^n\longrightarrow L^*/(L^*)^n
$$
injects into the finite group $H^1(\operatorname{Gal}(L/K),\mu_n(L))$. Likewise, the kernel of $E(K)/nE(K)\to E(L)/nE(L)$ injects into $H^1(\operatorname{Gal}(L/K),E[n](L))$ and is finite.

= S-unramified power class group
{title2=$K(S,n)$}
{c}
{parent=Kummer pairing}

For a number field $K$ and a finite set $S$ of finite primes,
$$
K(S,n)=\{[x]\in K^*/(K^*)^n:v_\mathfrak p(x)\equiv0\pmod n\text{ for every }\mathfrak p\notin S\}.
$$

= Finiteness of S-unramified Kummer classes
{parent=S-unramified power class group}
{title2=$\#K(S,n)<\infty$}

The <S-unramified power class group> of a <number field> is finite. For $[a]\in K(S,n)$, the fractional ideal of $a$ away from $S$ is an $n$th power $\mathfrak a^n$. Sending $[a]$ to $[\mathfrak a]$ gives an exact sequence
$$
0\longrightarrow\mathcal O_{K,S}^{\times}/(\mathcal O_{K,S}^{\times})^n\longrightarrow K(S,n)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal O_{K,S})[n]\longrightarrow0.
$$
The <S-unit group> is finitely generated, and the localized <ideal class group> is a quotient of the finite ordinary ideal class group. Both end groups are finite. This is the arithmetic finiteness needed in the <Kummer-theoretic proof of the weak Mordell-Weil theorem>.

= Two-isogeny descent
{parent=Kummer map of an elliptic curve}

Here descent is an arithmetic calculation with square classes and an <isogeny of elliptic curves>, rather than the gluing of objects along a covering.

For $E:y^2=x^3+ax^2+bx$ and its two-isogenous curve $E':y^2=x^3-2ax^2+(a^2-4b)x$, square-class maps on $E(\mathbb Q)$ and $E'(\mathbb Q)$ determine the Mordell-Weil rank.

= Rank-zero elliptic curve with roots zero three and four
{parent=Two-isogeny descent}
{title2=$\operatorname{rank}E(\mathbb Q)=0,\quad E:y^2=x(x-3)(x-4)$}

For this <elliptic curve>, the <two-isogeny formula> gives the partner $E':Y^2=X^3+14X^2+X$. The <prime-support bound in two-isogeny descent> and positivity of nonzero rational $x$ restrict the first <square-class> image to $1,2,3,6$. The identity and the <rational points> $(2,2),(0,0),(6,6)$ realize these classes. The partner image is contained in $\{1,-1\}$, and the <negative unit obstruction for the fourteen-coefficient isogeny cover> excludes $-1$. The <two-isogeny rank formula> therefore gives $2^r=4\cdot1/4=1$.

= Rank-two elliptic curve with cubic x cubed minus seventeen x
{parent=Two-isogeny descent}
{title2=$E:y^2=x^3-17x,\quad r=2$}

The <two-torsion square-class homomorphism> on $E$ has candidate classes $\{1,-1,17,-17\}$ by the <prime-support bound in two-isogeny descent>. The identity, $(0,0)$ and $(-1,4)$ realize $1,-17,-1$, so multiplicativity gives all four. The <two-isogeny formula> companion $E':y^2=x^3+68x$ has only positive candidate classes $\{1,2,17,34\}$; $(0,0)$ realizes $[68]=17$ and $(2,12)$ realizes $2$. Thus both images have order four, and the <two-isogeny rank formula> gives $2^r=4\cdot4/4=4$. The <rank of an elliptic curve> is therefore two, without any unresolved covering class.

= Rank-one elliptic curve with cubic x cubed minus twice x
{parent=Two-isogeny descent}
{title2=$y^2=x^3-2x,\quad r=1$}

For $E:y^2=x^3-2x$, prime-support bounds allow image classes $\{1,-1,2,-2\}$. The rational points $(0,0)$ and $(2,2)$ realize $-2$ and $2$, and the <two-torsion square-class homomorphism> is multiplicative, so all four occur. Its companion $E':y^2=x^3+8x$ has only positive candidate classes $1,2$, both realized by the identity and $(0,0)$. The <two-isogeny rank formula> gives $2^r=4\cdot2/4=2$, so the rank is one. The point $(2,2)$ has infinite order: its double is $(9/4,-21/8)$, contradicting the integrality of torsion coordinates in the <Nagell–Lutz theorem> if it were torsion.

= Rank-zero elliptic curve with cubic x cubed plus x
{parent=Two-isogeny descent}
{title2=$y^2=x^3+x,\quad r=0$}

For $E:y^2=x^3+x$, the <two-torsion square-class homomorphism> has image $\{1\}$: prime-support bounds allow only $\pm1$, and every nonzero affine $x$ is positive. Its <two-isogeny formula> companion $E':y^2=x^3-4x$ has possible image classes $\{1,-1,2,-2\}$, all realized by its rational two-torsion. The <two-isogeny rank formula> gives $2^r=1\cdot4/4=1$, so the rank is zero.

= Rank-one elliptic curve with coefficients eight and minus seven
{parent=Two-isogeny descent}
{title2=$E:y^2=x^3+8x^2-7x$}

The <two-isogeny formula> gives the partner $E':y^2=x^3-16x^2+92x$. The <two-torsion square-class homomorphism> image for $E$ is $\{1,-7\}$: <prime> support leaves only $\{1,-1,7,-7\}$, the <modulo-sixteen obstruction for the negative unit descent class> excludes $-1$, and the subgroup property excludes $7$. For $E'$, $(x-8)^2+28>0$ forces nonnegative $x$, so the image is contained in $\{1,2,23,46\}$. The two-torsion point realizes $23$ and the <rational point> $(18,48)$ realizes $2$, giving all four classes. The <two-isogeny rank formula> yields $2^r=2\cdot4/4=2$, hence rank one. The <dual isogeny> sends $(18,48)$ to $(16/9,116/27)$; this statement does not assert that the resulting point is a generator of the full free part.

= Rank-one elliptic curve with roots zero two and ten
{parent=Two-isogeny descent}
{title2=$E:y^2=x(x-2)(x-10),\quad\operatorname{rank}E(\mathbb Q)=1$}

This <elliptic curve> has <torsion subgroup> $\{O,(0,0),(2,0),(10,0)\}\cong(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^2$ and the infinite-order point $(1,3)$. Good-reduction point counts at three and eleven are four and sixteen, which bound the torsion order by four using <reduction of torsion points on an elliptic curve>. Doubling $(1,3)$ gives $(361/36,-323/216)$; after the shift $X=x-4$ to $y^2=X^3-28X-48$, its nonintegral coordinate proves nontorsion by the <Nagell–Lutz theorem>. The <two-isogeny formula> gives the partner $Y^2=X^3+24X^2+64X$. The first <two-torsion square-class homomorphism> has image $\{1,2,5,10\}$, while the second has image $\{1,-1\}$ by the <signed divisor-two obstruction for an isogeny covering>. The <two-isogeny rank formula> therefore gives $2^r=4\cdot2/4=2$, so the rank is one. The argument does not determine the index of the subgroup generated by $(1,3)$.

= Unit square-class bound for two-isogeny descent
{parent=Two-isogeny descent}

Suppose the <ring of integers of a number field> is a <principal ideal domain>, $a,b$ are integral, and $b$ is a <unit>. On $y^2=x(x^2+ax+b)$ every nonzero $x$-coordinate has even <valuation> at every finite <prime>: positive valuation gives $2v(y)=v(x)$, and negative valuation gives $2v(y)=3v(x)$. Thus the <two-torsion square-class homomorphism> takes values among unit <square classes>. For the <Gaussian integers>, their <unit group> modulo squares has two elements, represented by $1,i$. This can reduce a <two-isogeny descent> to a very small calculation even though the field contains no ordering.

= Two-isogeny index formula over a number field
{parent=Two-isogeny descent}
{title2=$[E(K):2E(K)]=\#\alpha(E(K))\#\alpha'(E'(K))/\delta$}

For the <two-isogeny formula> $\phi:E\to E'$ over a <number field>, the <two-torsion square-class homomorphisms> have kernels $\widehat\phi E'(K)$ and $\phi E(K)$. Consequently
$$
[E(K):2E(K)]=\frac{\#\alpha(E(K))\#\alpha'(E'(K))}{\delta},\qquad
\delta=[\ker\widehat\phi:\ker\widehat\phi\cap\phi E(K)].
$$
Indeed $2E(K)=\widehat\phi\phi E(K)$, and the surjection from $E'(K)/\phi E(K)$ to $\widehat\phi E'(K)/2E(K)$ has kernel $\ker\widehat\phi/(\ker\widehat\phi\cap\phi E(K))$. Combined with the <Mordell-Weil theorem>, the index is $2^r\#E(K)[2]$, where $r$ is the <rank of an abelian group>. This formula prevents an erroneous extra factor of two in an isogeny descent.

= Two-isogeny formula
{parent=Two-isogeny descent}
{title2=$\phi:E\longrightarrow E^{\prime},\quad\deg\phi=2$}

For $E:y^2=x(x^2+ax+b)$, set $b^{\prime}=a^2-4b$ and $E^{\prime}:Y^2=X(X^2-2aX+b^{\prime})$. The <isogeny of elliptic curves> with kernel $\{O,(0,0)\}$ is $\phi(x,y)=(x+a+b/x,\ y(1-b/x^2))$. Its dual is $\widehat\phi(X,Y)=((X-2a+b^{\prime}/X)/4,\ Y(1-b^{\prime}/X^2)/8)$. The maps extend over their exceptional affine points and satisfy $\widehat\phi\phi=[2]$.

= Prime-support bound in two-isogeny descent
{parent=Two-isogeny descent}
{title2=$r\leq\nu(b)+\nu(a^2-4b)$}

For integral $a,b$ with $b(a^2-4b)\ne0$, every square class in the image of the <two-isogeny descent> map on $y^2=x(x^2+ax+b)$ has a signed square-free representative supported on <primes> dividing $b$. At a <prime> not dividing $b$, a positive <valuation> of $x$ must be even from the curve equation; a negative <valuation> is even because the cubic leading term determines $2v(y)=3v(x)$. Thus its image has at most $2^{\nu(b)+1}$ elements. Apply this also to the two-isogenous curve and use the <square-class index formula for two-isogeny descent> to obtain $r\leq\nu(b)+\nu(a^2-4b)$, where $\nu$ counts distinct <prime> divisors of the nonzero absolute value.

= Square-class index formula for two-isogeny descent
{parent=Two-isogeny descent}
{title2=$2^r=\#\alpha(E)\,\#\alpha'(E')/4$}

= Two-isogeny rank formula
{synonym}

For $E:y^2=x(x^2+ax+b)$ and $E':y^2=x(x^2-2ax+a^2-4b)$ over $\mathbb Q$, define the square-class homomorphisms by $\alpha(x,y)=[x]$, $\alpha(O)=1$, $\alpha((0,0))=[b]$, and similarly $\alpha'$ with $b'=a^2-4b$. Their kernels are the images of the dual degree-two <isogenies of elliptic curves>. If $r$ is the <rank of an abelian group> of $E(\mathbb Q)$, then $2^r=\#\alpha(E)\#\alpha'(E')/4$. To check the factor four, put $\delta=[\ker\widehat\phi:\ker\widehat\phi\cap\phi E]$. The index of $2E$ in $E$ is $\#\alpha(E)\#\alpha'(E')/\delta$. If $b'$ is a square, $\delta=1$ and $\#E(\mathbb Q)[2]=4$; otherwise $\delta=2$ and $\#E(\mathbb Q)[2]=2$. In either case $\delta\#E(\mathbb Q)[2]=4$.

For a square class $d$ dividing $b$, the associated quartic covering is
$$
C_d:\qquad w^2=du^4+au^2v^2+\frac bdv^4.

= Negative unit obstruction for the fourteen-coefficient isogeny cover
{parent=Quartic covering in a two-isogeny descent}
{title2=$N^2=-U^4+14U^2V^2-V^4$}

This <quartic covering in a two-isogeny descent> has no solution with coprime integers $U,V$ not both zero. If precisely one is odd, the right side is $3$ modulo four. If both are odd, their fourth powers are one modulo sixteen and $14U^2V^2=14$ modulo sixteen, so the right side is twelve modulo sixteen. Neither residue is a square. Coprimality excludes the remaining parity case. Consequently the negative unit <square class> is absent from the <two-torsion square-class homomorphism> for $Y^2=X^3+14X^2+X$.

= Modulo-sixteen obstruction for the negative unit descent class
{parent=Quartic covering in a two-isogeny descent}

For $y^2=x^3+ax^2+bx$ with $a\equiv8$ and $b\equiv9\pmod{16}$, the <square class> $-1$ in a <two-isogeny descent> would require <coprime integers> $u,v$ and an <integer> $N$ with $N^2=-u^4+au^2v^2-bv^4$. If $u$ is odd and $v$ even, the right side is $15$ modulo $16$; if $u$ is even and $v$ odd, it is $7$; if both are odd, it is $14$. None is a square modulo $16$. Therefore $-1$ does not occur in the <two-torsion square-class homomorphism> image.

= Five-adic obstructions for the congruent-number isogeny covers
{parent=Quartic covering in a two-isogeny descent}
{title2=$\alpha'(E'_5)=\{1,5\}$}

For $E'_5:y^2=x^3+100x$, the <two-isogeny descent> classes $2$ and $10$ have no rational covering points. Their primitive integral quartics are respectively $N^2=2U^4+50V^4$ and $N^2=10(U^4+V^4)$, with $\gcd(U,V)=1$.

For the first equation, reduction modulo $5$ forces $5\mid U$ and $5\mid N$, since $2$ is not a square modulo $5$ and a nonzero fourth power is $1$. Put $U=5U_1,N=5N_1$. Dividing by $25$ gives $N_1^2=50U_1^4+2V^4$, again a nonsquare modulo $5$ because $5\nmid V$. For the second equation, a primitive pair has $U^4+V^4$ nonzero modulo $5$: it is $1$ or $2$. The right side has five-adic <valuation> exactly $1$, impossible for a square. Since $(5,25)$ lies on $E'_5$, its square-class image is exactly $\{1,5\}$. Combined with the <rank bound for a prime congruent-number elliptic curve> computation, the <two-isogeny rank formula> yields rank exactly $1$ for $E_5:y^2=x^3-25x$.

= Five-adic obstruction to square class two on the curve with coefficient one hundred
{parent=Quartic covering in a two-isogeny descent}
{title2=$W^2=2U^4+50V^4$}

This quartic has no primitive integral solution. If $5\nmid U$, reduction modulo five makes $W^2\equiv2$, impossible. If $5\mid U$, coprimality gives $5\nmid V$ and $5\mid W$; dividing by twenty-five then gives $(W/5)^2\equiv2V^4\equiv2\pmod5$, again impossible. It excludes the square class two in <two-isogeny descent> on $Y^2=X^3+100X$. The point $(5,25)$ supplies class five, so the subgroup property excludes class ten as well.

= Signed divisor-two obstruction for an isogeny covering
{parent=Quartic covering in a two-isogeny descent}
{title2=$W^2=\pm2U^4+24U^2V^2\pm32V^4$}

The <two-isogeny descent quartics> associated with square classes $2$ and $-2$ on $Y^2=X^3+24X^2+64X$ have no rational points. A rational solution can be scaled to integral $W,U,V$ with <coprime integers> $U,V$. If $U$ is odd, reduction modulo sixteen gives $2$ or $10$ for the positive class, and $14$ or $6$ for the negative class; none is a <quadratic residue>. If $U=2u$ is even, then $V$ is odd and the right side is $32(\pm u^4+3u^2V^2\pm V^4)$, with the same sign on the two signed terms. The bracket is odd, so its <2-adic valuation> is exactly five, impossible for a nonzero square. These two parity cases exhaust primitive solutions.

= Modulo-thirty-two obstruction for a congruent-number descent class
{parent=Quartic covering in a two-isogeny descent}
{title2=$N^2=2(U^4+p^2V^4)$}

If $p\equiv3\pmod8$, this <quartic covering in a two-isogeny descent> has no primitive integral solution. Opposite parities of $U,V$ give $N^2\equiv2\pmod8$. If both are odd, their fourth powers are one modulo sixteen and $p^2\equiv9\pmod{16}$, giving $N^2\equiv20\pmod{32}$. Neither residue is a square. This excludes the class $2$ on $Y^2=X^3+4p^2X$.

= Odd-prime obstructions for the congruent-number isogeny coverings
{parent=Quartic covering in a two-isogeny descent}
{title2=$p\equiv3\pmod4$}

For the curve $Y^2=X^3+4p^2X$ with $p\equiv3\pmod4$, the classes $p$ and $2p$ in <two-isogeny descent> lead to $N^2=p(U^4+4V^4)$ and $N^2=2p(U^4+V^4)$. A primitive solution forces $p\mid N$ and $p\nmid V$. The resulting congruences make $-1$ a square modulo $p$, a contradiction. Thus these coverings have no rational points.

= Modulo-eight obstruction to a two-isogeny descent class
{parent=Quartic covering in a two-isogeny descent}
{title2=$N^2=3U^4+5U^2V^2-2V^4\quad\text{has no primitive solution}$}

A proposed square class in a <two-isogeny descent> can be excluded by testing its quartic modulo $8$. For $N^2=3U^4+5U^2V^2-2V^4$ with $\gcd(U,V)=1$, both variables odd give residue $6$; odd-even gives $3$ or $7$; even-odd gives $6$ or $2$. None is a square modulo $8$, excluding class $3$ and, by the subgroup property of the descent image, its entire coset.

For a degree-three isogeny whose kernel becomes $\mu_3$ under the <Weil pairing>, the Kummer connecting map embeds the quotient by the isogeny into $K^\times/(K^\times)^3$. Valuations outside the primes of bad reduction restrict its image to an <S-unramified power class group>.

= Congruent number elliptic curve
{title2=$E_D:Dy^2=x^3-x$}
{parent=Elliptic curve}

These <elliptic curves> encode the <congruent number> problem through rational right triangles.

For a positive square-free integer $D$, rational points on $E_D:Dy^2=x^3-x$ with $2P\ne O$ correspond to rational right triangles of area $D$. The point $(x,y)$ gives side lengths
$$
\left|\frac{x^2-1}{y}\right|,
\qquad
\left|\frac{2x}{y}\right|,
\qquad
\left|\frac{x^2+1}{y}\right|.
$$

= Rank bound for a prime congruent-number elliptic curve
{parent=Congruent number elliptic curve}
{title2=$\operatorname{rank}E_p(\mathbb Q)\le2$}

For an odd <prime> $p$, let $E_p:y^2=x^3-p^2x$. Its <two-isogeny descent> partner is $E'_p:y^2=x^3+4p^2x$. The first <two-torsion square-class homomorphism> has image exactly $\{1,-1,p,-p\}$: prime-support bounds allow only these classes, and the four rational two-torsion points realize all of them. Every nonidentity point on $E'_p$ has positive $x$, so its square-class image is contained in $\{1,2,p,2p\}$. The <two-isogeny rank formula> gives $2^{\operatorname{rank}E_p}=\#\alpha'(E'_p)\le4$, hence the rank is at most $2$.

= Rational torsion of a congruent number curve
{parent=Congruent number elliptic curve}
{title2=$E_D(\mathbb Q)_{\mathrm{tors}}\cong(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^2$}

For nonzero integral $D$, the <elliptic curve> $E_D:y^2=x^3-D^2x$ has rational torsion precisely $O,(0,0),(D,0),(-D,0)$. At every good <prime> $p\equiv3\pmod4$, cancellation of the <Legendre symbols> at $x$ and $-x$ gives $\#E_D(\mathbb F_p)=p+1$. The <torsion-freeness of the formal group over Qp for odd p> makes the rational <torsion subgroup> inject into this group. The <Dirichlet theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions> excludes odd factors of its order and bounds its two-primary part by four. The four displayed rational <2-torsion> points then give equality.

= Congruent number
{parent=Congruent number elliptic curve}
{wiki}

A positive integer is a congruent number when it is the area of a right triangle with rational side lengths. For square-free $D$, this is equivalent to the <congruent number elliptic curve> having a <Mordell-Weil group> of positive rank.

= Two is not a congruent number
{parent=Congruent number}
{title2=$2\text{ is not a congruent number}$}

A primitive <Pythagorean triple> of area twice a square would satisfy $mn(m-n)(m+n)=2s^2$. Pairwise coprimality makes the odd factors squares and the even factor twice a square. The possibility $m$ even is excluded modulo eight. Thus $m=a^2,n=2b^2,m+n=c^2,m-n=d^2$. The coprime integers $u=(c+d)/2,v=(c-d)/2$ have $uv=b^2$, hence are themselves squares, and $a^2=u^2+v^2$ contradicts <fourth-power sum cannot be a square>. Clearing denominators gives the assertion for rational triangles.

= Fermat right triangle theorem
{parent=Congruent number}
{c}

There is no positive integer <right triangle> whose area is a square. For a hypothetical primitive triangle of minimal hypotenuse, its <primitive Pythagorean parametrization> makes $m,n,m-n,m+n$ pairwise coprime squares. Write $m=r^2$, $n=s^2$, $m+n=u^2$, $m-n=v^2$. Modulo eight forces $m$ odd and $n$ even. The new integer legs $(u+v)/2,(u-v)/2$ have hypotenuse $r$ and area $(s/2)^2$, a smaller positive hypotenuse, contradicting <infinite descent>. Clearing denominators extends the conclusion to rational right triangles of area one.

= Infinitely many rational right triangles from a nontorsion point
{parent=Congruent number}

For $D>0$, a nontorsion <rational point> on the <congruent number elliptic curve> $y^2=x^3-D^2x$ gives infinitely many positive rational <right triangles> of area $D$. Apply the formulas $a=|(x^2-D^2)/y|$, $b=|2Dx/y|$, $c=|(x^2+D^2)/y|$ to its multiples. The map is finite-to-one: if $r=c/b$ is fixed, then $|x|$ satisfies $X^2-2DrX+D^2=0$, leaving only finitely many coordinate signs. Thus infinitely many points give infinitely many distinct triangles.

= Noncongruent primes congruent to three modulo eight
{parent=Congruent number}
{title2=$p\equiv3\pmod8\Longrightarrow p\text{ is not congruent}$}

For a <prime> $p\equiv3\pmod8$, apply <two-isogeny descent> to the <congruent number elliptic curve> $y^2=x^3-p^2x$ and its companion $y^2=x^3+4p^2x$. The first square-class image is exactly $\{1,-1,p,-p\}$, already supplied by <2-torsion>. The second has only positive candidates $1,2,p,2p$. The <quartic covering in a two-isogeny descent> equations for $p$ and $2p$ are impossible modulo $p$, because $-1$ is not a <quadratic residue>. For $2$ the equation is $W^2=2U^4+2p^2V^4$. Coprimality forces $U,V$ odd and $W=2Z$; modulo sixteen this gives $2Z^2=1+9=10$, which is impossible. Thus the second image is trivial and the <square-class index formula for two-isogeny descent> gives rank zero. The same prime-reduction argument bounds its rational torsion by four, and it already has four rational <2-torsion> points, so it has no <rational point> corresponding to a <right triangle> of area $p$.

= Mordell-Weil group of the congruent number curve for five
{title2=$E_5(\mathbb Q)$}
{parent=Congruent number elliptic curve}

For $E_5:5y^2=x^3-x$,
$$
E_5(\mathbb Q)\cong\mathbb Z\oplus(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^2.
$$
The torsion points are $O,(0,0),(1,0),(-1,0)$, and one generator of the free part is $(-4/5,6/25)$.

= Mordell-Weil group of y squared equals x times x plus one times x plus four
{title2=$E(\mathbb Q)$ for $E:y^2=x(x+1)(x+4)$}
{parent=Elliptic curve}

For $E:y^2=x(x+1)(x+4)$,
$$
E(\mathbb Q)\cong\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z\oplus\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z.
$$
The points $(-2,2)$ and $(-1,0)$ generate the two factors, and a <two-descent on an elliptic curve> proves that the <Mordell-Weil group> has rank zero.

= Euler theorem on four squares in arithmetic progression
{c}
{parent=Mordell-Weil group of y squared equals x times x plus one times x plus four}

There is no nonconstant four-term <arithmetic progression> of rational square numbers. If $r^2,s^2,1,t^2$ formed one, then $(-2s^2,2rst)$ would lie on $y^2=x(x+1)(x+4)$; its explicitly known <Mordell-Weil group> forces $s^2=1$ and the common difference to vanish.

= Normalization of y squared equals x times x minus one squared
{title2=$y^2=x(x-1)^2$}
{parent=Normalization of a nodal curve}

For the nodal affine cubic $y^2=x(x-1)^2$, the rational parameter
$$
t=\frac y{x-1}
$$
gives $x=t^2$ and $y=t(t^2-1)$. Its function field is $k(t)$, so its smooth projective normalization is $\mathbb P^1$ and has genus zero.
