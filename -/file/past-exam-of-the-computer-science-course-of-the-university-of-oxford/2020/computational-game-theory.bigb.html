= Computational game theory
{scope}
{title2=A15684W1}

Source paper: https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/ba_computer_science_past_papers/response/2577745/attach/2/202402%20183%20CS%20Papers.zip?cookie_passthrough=1[released examination archive], member `2019/T/a15684w1.pdf`. The dated cover identifies the undergraduate Second Public Examination, Trinity Term 2020, Friday 5 June, opening at 09:30 BST. The archive directory is misleading. The cover allows four hours for completion and upload; all printed parts are solved below.

== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use a simultaneous two-player <normal-form game>, with X choosing the row and Y the column. Each player's <pure strategies> are $b$ and $nb$; a <payoff> pair lists X first. Measure every dataset size in millions and use the stated normalization $M=1$. The source's reference to a shared half as “points” is interpreted in the same million-point units as the corpus, so the shared addition is $n/2$ to each initial dataset.

The <bimatrix game> has <payoffs>
$$
\begin{array}{c|cc}
 &Y:b&Y:nb\\ \hline
X:b&
\left(\dfrac{n+2}{2(n+3)}-\dfrac P2,\ 
\dfrac{n+4}{2(n+3)}-\dfrac P2\right)&
\left(\dfrac{n+1}{n+3}-P,\ \dfrac2{n+3}\right)\\[6pt]
X:nb&
\left(\dfrac1{n+3},\ \dfrac{n+2}{n+3}-P\right)&
\left(\dfrac13,\ \dfrac23\right).
\end{array}
$$
For example, joint purchase gives dataset sizes $1+n/2$ and $2+n/2$, total $3+n$, and each buyer pays $P/2$. A sole buyer gets the full corpus and pays $P$. The total <payoff> is $1-P$ whenever a purchase occurs and $1$ when neither buys: the <game> is not a <zero-sum game>, even though gross market shares always sum to one.

For the following calculations, set
$$
t=\frac n{n+3},\qquad 0<t<1.
$$
The <payoff> differences “buy minus do not buy,” with the other player's action held fixed, are
$$
\begin{array}{c|cc}
 &\text{opponent does not buy}&\text{opponent buys}\\ \hline
\Delta_X&\dfrac{2t}{3}-P&\dfrac{t-P}{2}\\[4pt]
\Delta_Y&\dfrac t3-P&\dfrac{t-P}{2}.
\end{array}
$$
These four differences give all the <best response> comparisons without mixing up row and column <payoffs>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

A <weakly dominant strategy> must be a <best response> to either opponent action. For X this requires
$$
\frac{2t}{3}-P\ge0,\qquad\frac{t-P}{2}\ge0.
$$
The first is stronger because $t>0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{X: }b\text{ is weakly dominant iff }0<P\le\frac{2n}{3(n+3)}.}
$$
For Y the comparisons are $t/3-P\ge0$ and $(t-P)/2\ge0$, giving
$$
\boxed{\text{Y: }b\text{ is weakly dominant iff }0<P\le\frac{n}{3(n+3)}.}
$$
Below each upper endpoint the corresponding <strategy> is strictly better against both opponent actions; at the endpoint one comparison ties and the other is strict. Thus the answers also satisfy the convention that weak dominance requires a strict advantage somewhere. Since <expected payoffs> are linear in the opponent's mixing <probability>, checking both opponent <pure strategies> suffices for every opponent <mixed strategy>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

At joint purchase, each player's gain from continuing to buy rather than deviating to $nb$ is $(t-P)/2$. Both actions are <best responses> exactly when this is nonnegative. Therefore
$$
\boxed{(b,b)\text{ is a Nash equilibrium iff }0<P\le\frac n{n+3}.}
$$
At equality both players are indifferent to their individual <unilateral deviations>, which is permitted in a <Nash equilibrium>. The condition is less restrictive than requiring either player's action to be a <weakly dominant strategy>: only the opponent's actual action needs to be considered.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

When neither buys, X's <unilateral deviation> gains $2t/3-P$, while Y's gains $t/3-P$. For both to be nonpositive, $P$ must exceed the larger threshold. Hence
$$
\boxed{(nb,nb)\text{ is a Nash equilibrium iff }P\ge\frac{2n}{3(n+3)}.}
$$
Equality is included because a zero-gain deviation does not violate a <Nash equilibrium>. Consequently both joint-purchase and joint-nonpurchase <Nash equilibria> exist throughout $2t/3\le P\le t$, a coordination region.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

**Neither asymmetric purchase outcome is a <Nash equilibrium> for any permitted $P>0,n>0$.** At $(b,nb)$, X's chosen purchase requires $P\le2t/3$, but Y's chosen nonpurchase, against X buying, requires $P\ge t$. These requirements are incompatible because $2t/3<t$.

At $(nb,b)$, Y's chosen purchase requires $P\le t/3$, whereas X's nonpurchase against a buying Y requires $P\ge t$. Again the inequalities are incompatible. Thus every <Nash equilibrium> in <pure strategies> has both players taking the same action; the conclusion is stronger than merely observing symmetry in their available actions, since their original market shares differ.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $P=999/1000$. If
$$
t>P\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\frac n{n+3}>\frac{999}{1000}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\boxed{n>2997},
$$
then both players prefer buying against a buyer, since $(t-P)/2>0$. Both prefer not buying against a nonbuyer: $2t/3<2/3<P$ and $t/3<1/3<P$. These strict opposite <best responses> give the interior crossing of a <coordination game>.

An explicit <fully mixed Nash equilibrium> follows from indifference. Let $p$ be X's <probability> of buying and $q$ Y's. X is indifferent when
$$
(1-q)\left(\frac{2t}{3}-P\right)+q\frac{t-P}{2}=0,
$$
and Y is indifferent when
$$
(1-p)\left(\frac t3-P\right)+p\frac{t-P}{2}=0.
$$
Solving gives
$$
\boxed{
p=\frac{2(P-t/3)}{P+t/3},\qquad
q=\frac{2(P-2t/3)}{P-t/3},\qquad t=\frac n{n+3},\ n>2997.
}
$$
Both numerators and denominators are positive. For $p<1$ the required inequality is $2P-2t/3<P+t/3$, equivalent to $P<t$; the same comparison for $q<1$ again reduces to $P<t$. Thus $p,q\in(0,1)$, and the equations make both actions <best responses> for each player. By linearity of <expected payoff>, neither player can profit by any other <mixed strategy>. At $n=2997$ these formulas reach $p=q=1$ and are not fully mixed; the strict threshold matters.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

**The assignment is <Pareto optimal>.** Write $a_i$ for the room selected by the $i$th student. Suppose another assignment $a'$ makes every student at least as well off. The first student already selected their uniquely most valuable room, so $a'_1=a_1$. Once the rooms of students $1,\ldots,i-1$ are fixed, the rooms available to student $i$ in the original <serial dictatorship> are exactly the rooms that remain after those fixed assignments. Student $i$ chose the unique maximum among them, so weak improvement requires $a'_i=a_i$.

<Mathematical induction> fixes every student's room. Thus $a'=a$ and nobody can be strictly better off, contradicting the definition of a <Pareto improvement>. Strict room rankings and the absence of <allocation externalities> are the essential assumptions: a student's <utility> is determined solely by their own room.<One-sided matchings: house allocation problems in the Cornell networks and markets text>[§13.2, Definition 13.1 and Theorem 13.2: Pareto efficiency for strict preferences]{ref}

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

**No: arbitrary ties can produce an assignment that is not <Pareto optimal>.** A two-student <counterexample> suffices. Give the first student value $1$ for both rooms; give the second student value $1$ for $r_1$ and $0$ for $r_2$. If the first student breaks the tie by taking $r_1$, the second must take $r_2$, and the <utility> <vector> is $(1,0)$.

Swapping the rooms gives <utility> <vector> $(1,1)$, so it is a <Pareto improvement>. Both choices in the original run maximized the corresponding student's available room value. The strict-ranking argument from (a) fails because the first student's <utility> need not decrease when that student's room changes.

Specifying these optimal continuations at every history, including the unplayed ones, supplies a <subgame perfect equilibrium> rather than merely a proposed path.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

**No. Strict room values do not remove the <allocation externalities> generated by friends.** Consider the following new instance, with the same friendship pairs $\{S_1,S_4\}$ and $\{S_2,S_3\}$ and friendship bonus $0.4$ for each student:
$$
\begin{array}{c|rrrr}
 &r_1&r_2&r_3&r_4\\ \hline
S_1&0.50&0.30&0.02&0.01\\
S_2&0.01&0.50&0.30&0.02\\
S_3&0.01&0.70&0.02&0.50\\
S_4&0.01&0.50&0.02&0.03
\end{array}
$$
Each row is strictly ranked and every room value is positive. In picking order $S_1,S_2,S_3,S_4$, <backward induction> gives the following continuations. Room numbers in a tuple are listed in student order.
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
S_1\text{ choice}&\text{resulting assignment}&U_1\\ \hline
r_1&(r_1,r_3,r_2,r_4)&0.50\\
r_2&(r_2,r_3,r_4,r_1)&0.70\\
r_3&(r_3,r_2,r_4,r_1)&0.02\\
r_4&(r_4,r_2,r_3,r_1)&0.01
\end{array}
$$
To check $S_2$'s incentives behind these continuations, the resulting <utilities> for their available choices, in ascending room order, are respectively $(0.50,0.70,0.02)$, $(0.01,0.70,0.42)$, $(0.41,0.50,0.02)$ and $(0.41,0.90,0.70)$. Student $S_3$'s own <best responses> determine those figures, and $S_4$ again takes the remaining room. All comparisons selecting these continuations are strict.

Hence $S_1$ chooses $r_2$ and the <subgame perfect equilibrium> outcome is
$$
a=(r_2,r_3,r_4,r_1),\qquad U(a)=(0.70,0.70,0.90,0.41).
$$
But the feasible assignment
$$
a'=(r_1,r_3,r_4,r_2),\qquad U(a')=(0.90,0.70,0.90,0.90)
$$
strictly improves $S_1$ and $S_4$ and leaves $S_2,S_3$ unchanged. Thus $a$ is not <Pareto optimal>. This is compatible with equilibrium: a <Pareto improvement> can require several players' actions to change. If $S_1$ alone takes $r_1$, the later <best responses> lead to $(r_1,r_3,r_2,r_4)$, not to $a'$; anticipating that reaction gives $S_1$ only $0.50$.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

**Every <subgame perfect equilibrium> outcome is <Pareto optimal> under the stated consecutive-friend and bonus conditions.** Partition the choosing order into blocks: a single student without a friend, or two friends who choose consecutively. Their slots in the choosing order are adjacent; the proof must separately establish when their allocated rooms will be adjacent.

Let $R$ be the rooms remaining before a friend block, with leader $i$ and follower $j$. Because $0\le v_{i,r},v_{j,r}\le1$ and $f_i,f_j>1$, each friend strictly prefers every adjacent-to-friend allocation to every nonadjacent one. After leader $i$ takes room $r$ with an available adjacent room, follower $j$ therefore takes their uniquely highest-valued room among the available neighbors of $r$. The leader consequently knows they will receive the adjacency bonus.

If $R$ contains at least one pair of adjacent rooms, let $E(R)$ be its nonisolated rooms: the rooms having an available neighbor. Leader $i$ must choose from $E(R)$, because choosing there guarantees <utility> greater than $1$, whereas an isolated choice gives at most $1$. Since the bonus is then fixed and room values are distinct, the leader chooses their unique maximum-value room in $E(R)$. The follower chooses their unique best neighbor. Later students cannot change either allocated room, so later play cannot alter these two students' <utilities>.

If $R$ contains no adjacent pair, neither friend can obtain the bonus under any allocation of rooms from $R$. Both simply choose their uniquely most valuable remaining room in turn. Removing rooms cannot create a new physical adjacency, so no hidden later move can change that conclusion. A one-student block likewise chooses their unique best available room.

Now compare the equilibrium assignment $a$ with any proposed <Pareto improvement> $a'$. Induct over these blocks, assuming earlier blocks' rooms are already fixed. Thus $a$ and $a'$ have the same remaining <set> $R$ at the present block. A lone student's room must stay fixed by strict preferences.

For a friend block obtaining adjacency in $a$, both students must retain adjacency in $a'$: losing a bonus larger than $1$ cannot be compensated by any room-value increase. The leader's room in $a'$ must therefore belong to $E(R)$. It cannot differ from the unique highest-valued room the leader selected there without reducing that leader's <utility>. Once the leader's room is fixed, the follower likewise must retain the uniquely best available neighbor. If $R$ has no adjacent pair, both rooms stay fixed by the ordinary strict-ranking argument.

Thus every block's rooms are unchanged. The only assignment weakly improving all students is $a$ itself, so no strict improvement is possible. This proves <Pareto efficiency of serial dictatorship with consecutive friends>. The proof applies to every <subgame perfect equilibrium>, since the required choices at each block are strict <best responses> determined by the available rooms.

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <simple cooperative game> is a <transferable utility game> $(N,v)$ with
$$
\boxed{v:2^N\to\{0,1\},\quad v(\varnothing)=0,\quad v(N)=1,\quad
S\subseteq T\Longrightarrow v(S)\le v(T).}
$$
A <coalition> of value $1$ is a <winning coalition>; a value-$0$ <coalition> loses. The displayed definition uses the usual monotone, nontrivial convention: the empty <coalition> loses and the grand <coalition> wins.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

An <induced subgraph game> is a <transferable utility game> represented by a weighted <undirected graph> with one <vertex> per player; a <coalition>'s value is the sum of the <edge weights> in its <induced subgraph>. With no <self-loops>,
$$
\boxed{v(S)=\sum_{\{i,j\}\subseteq S,\ i\ne j}w_{ij},}
$$
where each unordered pair is counted once and an absent <edge> has weight zero. Weights may have either sign. In an <induced subgraph game with self-loops>, write the loop weight at $i$ as $\ell_i$ and add $\sum_{i\in S}\ell_i$, counting each loop once. In particular, $v(\{i\})=\ell_i$ when loops are allowed and is zero otherwise.<On the Complexity of Cooperative Solution Concepts by Xiaotie Deng and Christos Papadimitriou>[§1, printed p. 258: weighted induced-subgraph representation; the self-loop extension is stated explicitly here]{ref}

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

**No representation exists, even allowing arbitrary signed weights and <self-loops>.** Every singleton <coalition> loses, so each loop weight must be $\ell_i=v(\{i\})=0$. Every two-player <coalition> wins, which then forces
$$
w_{12}=w_{13}=w_{23}=1.
$$
The represented grand-<coalition> value would consequently be
$$
v(\{1,2,3\})=\ell_1+\ell_2+\ell_3+w_{12}+w_{13}+w_{23}=3,
$$
contradicting its required value $1$. The obstruction is the additive <edge> representation, not a restriction that weights be nonnegative: the singleton and pair values already determine every relevant weight.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

**Yes; without <self-loops>, such a nontrivial <simple cooperative game> has exactly one positive <edge>, of weight $1$.** For each pair,
$$
w_{ij}=v(\{i,j\})\in\{0,1\},
$$
because singleton and loop contributions are zero. Summing all <edge weights> gives $v(N)=1$, so precisely one unordered pair $\{a,b\}$ has weight $1$, and all other pairs have weight zero.

Thus the <winning coalitions> are exactly those containing both $a$ and $b$. For any $0\le\lambda\le1$, set
$$
\boxed{x_a=\lambda,\quad x_b=1-\lambda,\quad x_i=0\quad(i\notin\{a,b\}).}
$$
This <payoff> <vector> is efficient, since $\sum_i x_i=v(N)=1$. Every <winning coalition> receives $1$ and every losing <coalition> receives a nonnegative total, so all inequalities $x(S)\ge v(S)$ hold. Hence it belongs to the <core of a cooperative game>.

In fact these are all <core> allocations. Singleton conditions require $x_i\ge0$, and the winning pair requires $x_a+x_b\ge1$; efficiency then forces equality and zero <payoffs> outside the pair. The endpoints are both <veto players>, consistent with the general characterization of the <core of a simple cooperative game>.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

**No. Two positive <self-loops> and one negative <edge> give a <simple cooperative game> with an empty <core of a cooperative game>.** Give players $1,2$ loop weights $\ell_1=\ell_2=1$, put $w_{12}=-1$, and give every other loop and <edge> weight zero. For a <coalition> $S$,
$$
v(S)=\mathbf1_{\{1\in S\}}+\mathbf1_{\{2\in S\}}
-\mathbf1_{\{\{1,2\}\subseteq S\}}
=\mathbf1_{\{S\cap\{1,2\}\ne\varnothing\}}.
$$
Thus a <coalition> wins exactly when it contains player $1$ or player $2$. This is monotone, has values in $\{0,1\}$, and satisfies $v(\varnothing)=0,v(N)=1$. Extra players for any $n\ge3$ do not alter any <coalition> value.

The <core> singleton inequalities would require $x_1\ge1$, $x_2\ge1$ and $x_i\ge0$ for every other player, while efficiency requires $\sum_i x_i=1$. These requirements are inconsistent. Equivalently, the two disjoint winning singleton <coalitions> leave no <veto player>. Signed weights are essential to this particular representation and are permitted in the <induced subgraph game> model.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

**A loop-free <induced subgraph game> is a <convex cooperative game> if and only if every <edge weight> is nonnegative.** Necessity follows by applying the <supermodular> inequality to $S=\{i\}$ and $T=\{j\}$. Since both singleton values and the empty value are zero,
$$
v(\{i,j\})+v(\varnothing)\ge v(\{i\})+v(\{j\})
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad w_{ij}\ge0.
$$

Conversely, direct cancellation of common <edge> contributions gives, for any <coalitions> $S,T$,
$$
v(S\cup T)+v(S\cap T)-v(S)-v(T)
=\sum_{\substack{i\in S\setminus T\\j\in T\setminus S}}w_{ij}.
$$
Every summand is nonnegative, proving that $v$ is a <supermodular set function> and hence convexity. Equivalently, a player's <marginal contribution> to $S$ is $\Delta_i(S)=\sum_{j\in S}w_{ij}$, which cannot decrease as $S$ grows. This establishes the <convexity criterion for an induced subgraph game> directly.<On the Complexity of Cooperative Solution Concepts by Xiaotie Deng and Christos Papadimitriou>[§1, printed p. 258, nonnegative-weight convexity; §2.2, printed p. 260, discussion preceding Lemma 4]{ref}

=== g
{scope}

==== Solution

**There is exactly one such nontrivial game for every $n\ge1$: the <unanimity game> requiring every player.** Put
$$
\boxed{u_N(S)=
\begin{cases}
1,&S=N,\\
0,&S\ne N.
\end{cases}
\qquad\text{Number of games}=1.}
$$
It is a <simple cooperative game>, and permuting players preserves every <coalition> value. Thus it has the required <symmetry of players in a cooperative game>. It is also a <convex cooperative game>: if both $S,T$ are winning, all four terms in the <supermodular> inequality have value $1$; if exactly one is winning, their union is $N$ and their intersection loses, giving equality $1=1$; if neither wins, the right-hand side is zero and the inequality follows from nonnegativity. For $n=1$ the same definition gives the sole player value $1$.

To prove uniqueness, first establish that <convex simple games are unanimity games>. If $S,T$ are <winning coalitions>, the <supermodular> inequality gives
$$
2=v(S)+v(T)\le v(S\cup T)+v(S\cap T)\le2,
$$
so their intersection is also winning. The family of <winning coalitions> is finite and nonempty. Its intersection $K$ is therefore winning and nonempty, since the empty <coalition> loses. Every winning <coalition> contains $K$, and monotonicity makes every superset of $K$ winning. Hence $v=u_K$.

If $K$ were a proper nonempty <subset> of $N$, take $i\in K$ and $j\notin K$, and let $T=N\setminus\{i,j\}$. Then $T\cup\{i\}=N\setminus\{j\}$ contains $K$ and wins, whereas $T\cup\{j\}=N\setminus\{i\}$ loses. This violates <symmetry of players in a cooperative game>, which requires those two <coalition> values to agree. Thus $K=N$, proving the claimed count. The “nontrivial” convention excludes the identically zero game; if that degenerate game were also called simple, it would supply one additional symmetric convex example.
