==== Solution

**With the conventional strictly positive step costs, the five answers in printed order are false, false, true, true, true.** The third answer needs that positivity convention: it is false in general if zero-cost <edges> and unrestricted ties are admitted. Take the <goal test> on selection from the <search frontier>, and assume a finite reachable problem with a reachable <goal state>; finite <branching factor> and a positive lower bound on step costs give the corresponding <tree search> guarantees on an infinite problem. These assumptions make the usual <A-star search> guarantees precise.<Informed search and exploration by Stuart Russell and Peter Norvig>[§4.1, printed pp. 94–100: best-first priorities, consistency and optimality conditions]{ref}

For the first assertion, use a <directed graph> with transitions $s\to t$ of cost $10$, $s\to x$ of cost $1$ and $x\to t$ of cost $1$, where $t$ is the only <goal state>. The <perfect heuristic> is $h^*(s)=2$, $h^*(x)=1$, $h^*(t)=0$. <Greedy best-first search> selects the direct goal of cost $10$ because its priority is zero. The least <path cost> is $2$. Knowing the exact remaining distance does not account for an expensive transition already taken.

The same <counterexample> settles the second assertion. <Greedy best-first search> selects $s,t$, while <A-star search> selects $s,x,t$: after expanding $s$, the <A-star search> priorities are $2$ for $x$ and $10$ for the direct copy of $t$. Thus even the expanded state <sets> differ.

For the third assertion, let the current nonterminal <search node> have finite remaining distance $h^*(s)$. The first transition of a least-cost continuation has some successor $s'$ with
$$
h^*(s)=c(s,s')+h^*(s')>h^*(s').
$$
Every older entry in the <search frontier> has estimate at least $h^*(s)$, since the current entry was selected with minimum estimate. A newly generated successor with strictly smaller estimate therefore wins over every older entry. Whichever new successor is selected has smaller remaining distance than the current <search node>, so the argument repeats. Consequently the selected <search nodes> form one parent–child chain; include the selected goal, or append it if “expanded” excludes goal processing. This <greedy perfect-heuristic descent> does not imply least <path cost>, as the first <counterexample> demonstrates.

The zero-cost qualification is substantive. Give $s$ two successors $a,b$ of cost $1$, add $a\to x$ of cost $0$, and add $x\to t$ and $b\to t$ of cost $1$. The <perfect heuristic> has values $2$ at $s$, $1$ at $a,b,x$ and $0$ at $t$. Alphabetical priority ties let <greedy best-first search> select $s,a,b,t$, which is not one <graph path>. Thus the unqualified third assertion requires a cost or tie convention absent from the printed text.

For the fourth assertion, a least remaining distance satisfies
$$
h^*(s)\le c(s,s')+h^*(s')
$$
on every <edge>, by taking that <edge> and then a least-cost continuation. Thus $h^*$ is a <consistent heuristic>. The usual <A-star search> <graph search> finalizes each expanded state's least <path cost> and returns a least-cost goal.

For the fifth assertion, write $C^*=h^*(s_0)$. Before a goal is selected, an unexpanded prefix of an optimal <graph path> remains on the <search frontier>, with $f=g+h^*=C^*$. <A-star search> therefore selects only <search nodes> with $f\le C^*$. Since $g\ge0$,
$$
\boxed{h^*(n)\le g(n)+h^*(n)\le C^*=h^*(s_0).}
$$
This compares remaining cost, not <search depth> or accumulated <path cost>. The statement can fail with negative costs; those are outside the convention used here.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Let the input words be $s,t$, let $L$ be their common length, let $\Sigma$ be the finite <alphabet>, and let $D_L$ contain the dictionary words of length $L$. Unequal input lengths immediately make the puzzle impossible because a mutation preserves length. A <word ladder> <search problem> can use the <state space> $S=D_L\cup\{s\}$, with initial state $s$; including $s$ handles an input word not itself in the dictionary.

At word $w$, an action is a position–letter pair $(i,\ell)$ with $1\le i\le L$, $\ell\in\Sigma$ and $\ell\ne w_i$, permitted precisely when replacing $w_i$ by $\ell$ gives $w'\in D_L$. The <search transition model> returns that $w'$, and the <goal test> is $w=t$. If $s=t$, the empty sequence is accepted; if $t\notin D_L$ and $s\ne t$, no transition can reach $t$.

Assign each permitted transition cost $1$. A <search node> at <search depth> $k$ has
$$
\boxed{g(n)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}1=k.}
$$
Thus minimizing <path cost> is exactly minimizing the number of mutations. <Uniform-cost search>, with repeated states handled by <graph search> and the <goal test> on selection, finds a shortest <word ladder> if one exists. Because all costs are equal, <breadth-first search> gives the same optimal-length guarantee.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

**The proposed <Hamming distance> is both an <admissible heuristic> and a <consistent heuristic>.** Write
$$
h(w)=|\{i:w_i\ne t_i\}|.
$$
Every position differing from the target must change at least once along any successful <word ladder>. One mutation changes only one position, so every solution from $w$ has at least $h(w)$ steps. This proves $h(w)\le h^*(w)$, including an unreachable target with $h^*(w)=\infty$. Dictionary restrictions may force extra changes, but cannot remove that lower bound.

Adjacent words differ in one position, so their <Hamming distances> to $t$ differ by at most one. Since every transition costs one,
$$
h(w)\le1+h(w'),\qquad h(t)=0.
$$
These are exactly the <consistent heuristic> conditions. Equivalently, allowing all length-$L$ words gives a <relaxation heuristic> whose exact distance is the <Hamming distance>; restoring the dictionary can only increase the minimum required number of steps.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

**The difficulty is representing dictionary membership and the one-letter relationship in positive conjunctive <STRIPS> <preconditions>; a finite dictionary nevertheless admits an exact encoding.** Pure <STRIPS> does not provide string indexing, a “differs in one position” operator, arbitrary disjunctive tests or a procedure that calls a dictionary lookup. Those relationships must be represented by ground facts or compiled into <ground actions>.

One exact whole-word encoding uses a <planning fluent> $\operatorname{At}(w)$ and a <static predicate> $\operatorname{Next}(w,w')$ containing exactly the permitted dictionary mutations. For the <action schema> $\operatorname{Change}(w,w')$, use
$$
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Pre}&=\{\operatorname{At}(w),\operatorname{Next}(w,w')\},\\
\operatorname{Add}&=\{\operatorname{At}(w')\},\\
\operatorname{Del}&=\{\operatorname{At}(w)\}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Initialize $\operatorname{At}(s)$ and the static transition facts, and use goal $\operatorname{At}(t)$. Only one $\operatorname{At}$ fact is true initially. Each applicable <ground action> deletes it and adds exactly its permitted successor, so <mathematical induction> proves that exactly one current-word fact remains true and that every <plan> is a legal <word ladder>. Conversely, every legal <word ladder> directly supplies a <plan>. An isolated non-dictionary start is handled as in (c). A shortest <plan> has the fewest mutations.

This encoding moves the hard lexical relationship into preprocessing. For $N$ dictionary words, checking all ordered pairs takes $O(N^2L)$ character comparisons, although indexing can do better; the number of stored transitions is at most $N L(|\Sigma|-1)$ when all states are dictionary words. The <STRIPS> representation is finite, but its size and preprocessing cost matter.

A position-based representation instead uses <planning fluents> $\operatorname{Letter}(i,\ell)$. Changing position $i$ must delete its old letter and add its new one; all other positions persist through the <STRIPS> update rule. Merely requiring each position to contain a letter admits non-dictionary strings. A disjunction over all dictionary words is not a positive conjunctive <STRIPS> <precondition>. It can be compiled into separate <ground actions>, each requiring every letter of a valid source word and changing one position to reach a valid destination. This again enumerates lexical cases and can be large. Arbitrary letter changes followed by an unconstrained “validate” action are unsound: several illegal intermediate strings could otherwise lead to a legal goal. Legality must hold after every mutation, not only at the endpoint.

==== Solution

For a finite <constraint satisfaction problem>, remove values violating unary restrictions before starting. The following <backtracking> <algorithm> makes independent copies of <constraint domains> at each branch, so failed <forward checking> cannot contaminate a sibling branch.

```
SOLVE(P):
    D := domains of P after unary filtering
    if some D[X] is empty: return failure
    return SEARCH({}, D)

SEARCH(assignment, D):
    if every variable is assigned: return assignment
    X := SELECT-UNASSIGNED-VARIABLE(assignment, D, P)
    for v in ORDER-DOMAIN-VALUES(X, assignment, D, P):
        if X=v is compatible with the current assignment:
            next_assignment := assignment extended by X=v
            next_domains := a deep copy of D
            next_domains[X] := {v}
            if INFERENCES(X, v, next_assignment, next_domains, P) succeeds:
                result := SEARCH(next_assignment, next_domains)
                if result is not failure: return result
    return failure
```

SELECT-UNASSIGNED-VARIABLE implements <minimum remaining values>: choose an unassigned <variable> with the smallest current <constraint domain>. Break ties by the <degree heuristic>, choosing the <variable> constrained to the greatest number of other unassigned <variables>; a fixed final tie rule makes execution reproducible. ORDER-DOMAIN-VALUES may use any complete ordering; ascending values suffice here.

INFERENCES implements <forward checking>: for each unassigned neighbor $Y$ of $X$, remove every value incompatible with $X=v$, and fail if any <constraint domain> becomes empty. For higher-arity constraints, reject any completely instantiated violation and use a sound chosen filtering rule; the stated neighbor test is the usual binary case. <Forward checking> never removes a value used by a possible extension of the current assignment. Exhausting every surviving value therefore makes this finite <backtracking> procedure both sound and complete.<CS 188 constraint filtering notes>[§2.3, opening paragraphs: filtering neighbors after assignment]{ref}<CS 188 constraint ordering and structure notes>[§2.4, Minimum Remaining Values]{ref}

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

**<Forward checking> makes <minimum remaining values> respond to current restrictions rather than the original <constraint domain> sizes.** After $X=v$, its neighbors may have fewer choices, become forced or lose all choices. <Minimum remaining values> then selects the newly constrained <variable> early, while an empty <constraint domain> triggers immediate failure. The two mechanisms expose failure sooner and reduce wasted <backtracking>.

<Forward checking> only tests an unassigned neighbor against the newly assigned value. It need not check whether two still-unassigned <variables> have mutually supporting values. <Arc consistency> requires every value at either end of each <binary constraint> to have support in the other <constraint domain>, and <AC-3> propagates deletions until this condition stabilizes or a <constraint domain> becomes empty.<CS 188 constraint filtering notes>[§2.3, forward checking and the directed support test]{ref}

For example, let $X,Y,Z$ initially have <constraint domains> $\{1,2\}$ with constraints $X\ne Y$ and $Y\ne Z$. Assigning $X=1$ makes <forward checking> reduce $D_Y$ to $\{2\}$, but leaves $D_Z=\{1,2\}$. <Arc consistency> also deletes $2$ from $D_Z$, leaving $\{1\}$. Neither procedure alone decides every general <constraint satisfaction problem>: a triangle of two-valued inequalities satisfies <arc consistency> but is unsatisfiable.

==== Solution

**One solution is $(a,b,c,d,e)=(1,2,3,2,1)$.** Use ascending value order; after <minimum remaining values> and the <degree heuristic>, break any remaining <variable> tie alphabetically. Apply the unary restrictions first:
$$
D_a=\{1,2\},\quad D_b=\{1,2,3\},\quad D_c=D_d=D_e=\{1,2,3,4\}.
$$

<Minimum remaining values> selects $a$, even though $c$ has greater initial <vertex degree>. Set $a=1$. <Forward checking> removes $1$ from the three neighbors $b,c,d$:
$$
D_b=\{2,3\},\quad D_c=D_d=\{2,3,4\},\quad D_e=\{1,2,3,4\}.
$$
Now $b$ is the unique <minimum remaining values> choice. Set $b=2$. <Forward checking> removes $2$ from $c,e$, giving
$$
D_c=\{3,4\},\quad D_d=\{2,3,4\},\quad D_e=\{1,3,4\}.
$$
Select $c$ and set $c=3$. Its remaining neighbors are $d,e$, so <forward checking> gives
$$
D_d=\{2,4\},\qquad D_e=\{1,4\}.
$$
The two remaining <variables> tie on <constraint domain> size and each has one unassigned neighbor, so the <degree heuristic> also ties. Alphabetical order selects $d$; take $d=2$. Since $2$ is already absent from $D_e$, <forward checking> makes no further deletion. Finally choose $e=1$. No empty <constraint domain> or violation occurs, so this branch needs no <backtracking>.

All eight <binary constraints> are checked explicitly by the unequal value pairs
$$
(a,b)=(1,2),\ (a,d)=(1,2),\ (a,c)=(1,3),\ (b,e)=(2,1),\ (b,c)=(2,3),\ (c,e)=(3,1),\ (c,d)=(3,2),\ (d,e)=(2,1).
$$
The unary restrictions also hold. Equalities $a=e$ and $b=d$ are permitted because those pairs have no <edges> in the <constraint graph>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

<Cutset conditioning> selects a <cycle cutset> $C$: deleting its <vertices> makes the binary <constraint graph> a <forest>. Enumerate assignments to $C$, reject those violating internal constraints, and filter each remaining <constraint domain> against the chosen cutset values. Solve the residual components by <tree-structured constraint satisfaction>. Stop at a successful assignment, or report failure after all cutset assignments fail. Completeness follows by considering the restriction of any full solution to $C$; soundness follows by checking internal, crossing and residual constraints. With maximum <constraint domain> size $q$ and $k$ cutset <variables>, a typical bound is $O(q^k(n-k)q^2)$ for the residual <forest> work, with cutset and crossing checks added explicitly.<CS 188 constraint ordering and structure notes>[§2.4.1, tree passes and cutset conditioning]{ref}

Here take **$C=\{c,e\}$**. The residual <constraint graph> has <vertices> $a,b,d$ and only <edges> $ab,ad$, a <tree> rooted at $a$. Choose the consistent cutset values $c=3,e=1$. Filtering against these assignments gives
$$
D_a=\{1,2\},\quad D_b=\{2\},\quad D_d=\{2,4\}.
$$
The upward support pass from $b$ deletes $a=2$, because $b$ can only equal $2$. The pass from $d$ retains $a=1$, which has support $2$ or $4$. Assign $a=1$ at the root and then supported child values $b=2,d=2$. Together with $c=3,e=1$, this gives the solution in (c).

No singleton <cycle cutset> exists: deleting $a,b,d$ or $e$ leaves, respectively, a triangle on $bce,acd,abc$ or $abc$; deleting $c$ leaves the <cycle in a graph> $a-b-e-d-a$. Thus the two-element choice is also minimum, although the <algorithm> only requires a valid cutset.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

**Use an <exactly-one constraint> for each finite-domain <variable> and a <Boolean clause> forbidding each disallowed constraint tuple.** Let the <variables> be $X_1,\ldots,X_n$, with explicitly listed finite <constraint domains> $D_i$, and let constraint $C$ have distinct-variable scope $S_C$ and allowed relation $R_C\subseteq\prod_{i\in S_C}D_i$. Introduce a <Boolean variable> $p_{i,v}$ for every $v\in D_i$, interpreted as $X_i=v$. The <direct SAT encoding of finite-domain constraints> is
$$
\varphi_P=
\bigwedge_i\left[
\left(\bigvee_{v\in D_i}p_{i,v}\right)
\land
\bigwedge_{\{u,v\}\subseteq D_i,\ u\ne v}
(\neg p_{i,u}\lor\neg p_{i,v})
\right]
\land
\bigwedge_C\ \bigwedge_{\mathbf t\in F_C}
\left(\bigvee_{i\in S_C}\neg p_{i,t_i}\right),
\qquad F_C=\left(\prod_{i\in S_C}D_i\right)\setminus R_C.
$$
In the middle <logical conjunction>, each unordered distinct pair is included once. The first <Boolean clause> requires at least one value, the pairwise <Boolean clauses> permit at most one, and a final <Boolean clause> is false exactly when all coordinates of one forbidden tuple are selected. The formula is in <conjunctive normal form>. An empty <constraint domain> produces an empty <Boolean clause>, correctly making the instance unsatisfiable.

For soundness of the reduction, a solution $x$ of the <constraint satisfaction problem> gives the <Boolean valuation> $p_{i,v}=1$ exactly when $v=x_i$. Every <exactly-one constraint> holds; a forbidden tuple differs from $x$ on at least one scoped coordinate, making its negative <Boolean clause> true. Thus $x$ supplies a <satisfying assignment> for $\varphi_P$.

Conversely, a <satisfying assignment> selects a unique value $x_i$ for every $X_i$. If a constraint failed, the selected scoped tuple would belong to $F_C$, and its entire negative <Boolean clause> would be false. That contradicts satisfiability. Hence $x$ is a solution. Decoding takes $O(\sum_i|D_i|)$ by scanning the value indicators. This proves **$P$ has a solution if and only if $\varphi_P$ is satisfiable**.

For explicit size accounting, put $q_i=|D_i|$, $r_C=|S_C|$ and $f_C=|F_C|$. There are $\sum_iq_i$ <Boolean variables>,
$$
K=n+\sum_i\binom{q_i}{2}+\sum_Cf_C
$$
<Boolean clauses>, and
$$
L=\sum_iq_i+2\sum_i\binom{q_i}{2}+\sum_Cr_Cf_C
$$
<literal> occurrences. These formulas count nonempty scopes; a false zero-arity constraint contributes an empty <Boolean clause>, while a true one contributes none. Formula storage is $O(K+L)$ <literal>-sized records; a bit-level encoding also stores indicator indices. With maximum <constraint domain> size $q$, $m$ constraints and maximum arity $r$, this is bounded by $O(nq^2+mrq^r)$ for nontrivial domains and scopes. In the common binary case it is **$O((n+m)q^2)$**.

The input representation matters: listing all forbidden tuples costs up to $\prod_{i\in S_C}q_i$ tests per constraint. This is polynomial for bounded arity and explicitly represented domains, but can be exponential in the description length of a succinct high-arity constraint. The translation is valid in that case without constituting a polynomial-size reduction. If a compact constraint is supplied as a polynomial-size <Boolean circuit>, one may instead encode that circuit using auxiliary <Boolean variables> and local <conjunctive normal form> equivalences, with size proportional to the circuit. No compact representation is silently assumed.

For the instance in (c), after unary filtering there are $17$ indicators. The five at-least-one <Boolean clauses>, $22$ pairwise at-most-one <Boolean clauses> and $24$ forbidden-equality <Boolean clauses> give **$51$ <Boolean clauses>**. Setting only $p_{a,1},p_{b,2},p_{c,3},p_{d,2},p_{e,1}$ true encodes the displayed solution.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

**The translation gives a sound and complete route through an optimized <SAT solver>; it trades access to general-purpose <SAT> machinery against encoding cost and the possible loss of constraint-specific structure.** Modern <SAT solvers> use efficient <unit propagation>, adaptive branching and <clause learning>. Learned <Boolean clauses> can summarize conflicts involving many original <variables>, allowing later branches to avoid repeating the same failed combination. The reduction also offers a standard, reusable interface: improvements to a <SAT solver> can benefit many finite-domain <constraint satisfaction problems>.

The <Boolean formula> may be much larger than the original <constraint satisfaction problem>, especially for a succinct high-arity relation. One original <variable> becomes many indicators, and general branching may lose the interpretation that the <minimum remaining values> and <degree heuristic> exploit directly. Native <constraint propagation> can exploit a relation without enumerating all its forbidden tuples. Even in the binary case, stronger propagation does not follow just from switching to <SAT>.

For example, take $D_X=\{1,2\}$, $D_Y=\{a,b,c\}$, and let the only allowed pairs be $(1,c),(2,a),(2,b)$. The forbidden-pair encoding contains $\neg p_{X,1}\lor\neg p_{Y,a}$ and $\neg p_{X,1}\lor\neg p_{Y,b}$. If $p_{Y,c}$ becomes false, <arc consistency> deletes $X=1$ because it has no support. Yet the direct encoding has no unit <Boolean clause> forcing $\neg p_{X,1}$: both $a,b$ remain possible individually, so plain <unit propagation> does not make that deletion. A <SAT solver> can eventually derive it through additional reasoning or search. Conversely, <clause learning> can combine information beyond the local consistency enforced by <AC-3>. **Neither approach uniformly dominates:** performance depends on representation, propagation strength and the instance's structure; both still face exponential worst-case search on general inputs.

=== a

==== Solution

**<Depth-first search> computes <minimax search> values with much less live storage and naturally supports <alpha-beta pruning>.** A parent's value needs only its current best child value and the next child to visit. Once a child subtree has been evaluated, its internal <search nodes> can be discarded; a final value cannot usually be computed from a nonterminal frontier alone.

With <branching factor> $b$ and horizon $d$, <breadth-first search> stores a frontier of order $b^d$. A <depth-first search> implementation storing sibling lists uses $O(bd)$ <space complexity>; lazy successor generation can reduce this to $O(d)$ frames plus game-state storage. Both unpruned traversals have $O(b^d)$ <time complexity> for $b>1$. <Depth-first search> also supplies a completed sibling outcome early, allowing <alpha-beta pruning> to reject later subtrees before generating them.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Use <utilities> from A's viewpoint, with A maximizing and B minimizing. The following <alpha-beta pruning> code assumes a finite horizon or terminating <game tree>; leaf evaluation uses terminal <utility> when available and a specified horizon estimate otherwise.

```
ALPHABETA(state, depth, alpha, beta, maximizing):
    if state is terminal or depth == 0:
        return EVALUATE(state)
    if maximizing:
        value := -infinity
        for child in SUCCESSORS(state):
            value := max(value, ALPHABETA(child, depth-1, alpha, beta, false))
            alpha := max(alpha, value)
            if alpha >= beta: break
        return value
    else:
        value := +infinity
        for child in SUCCESSORS(state):
            value := min(value, ALPHABETA(child, depth-1, alpha, beta, true))
            beta := min(beta, value)
            if alpha >= beta: break
        return value
```

Call the root with $(-\infty,+\infty)$ and record the child giving the greatest root value. In a nonterminal game state, SUCCESSORS must supply the game's legal moves, including any mandatory pass; a player with no moves must be handled according to the rules rather than left as an accidental empty loop.

The bound $\alpha$ records an outcome already available to MAX on the active ancestor path; $\beta$ records one already available to MIN. At a MIN node, once a child gives value at most $\alpha$, further children can only decrease the node's minimum, so MAX need not choose that branch. The dual argument applies at MAX when its current value is at least $\beta$. Returned values from cut-off subtrees can be bounds rather than exact local <minimax search> values, while the unrestricted root value is exact. Equal-value pruning preserves that value; retaining the first best root action gives a fixed tie rule.<CS 188 minimax notes>[§3.2.1, Alpha-Beta Pruning: ancestor alternatives and cutoffs]{ref}

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

**With excellent <move ordering>, the new horizon is approximately $2d$; without a <move ordering> assumption, no doubling is guaranteed.** For $b>1$, a full <minimax search> to depth $d$ visits $\Theta(b^d)$ <search nodes>, so the budget $n$ has $d\approx\log_b n$. Perfectly ordered <alpha-beta pruning> evaluates only about $\Theta(b^{d'/2})$ leaves at depth $d'$. More precisely, for a complete uniform <game tree>, the best leaf count is $b^{\lceil d'/2\rceil}+b^{\lfloor d'/2\rfloor}-1$. Equating the dominant costs gives
$$
b^{d'/2}\approx n\approx b^d,\qquad \boxed{d'\approx2\log_b n\approx2d.}
$$
Constant factors and rounding affect the exact horizon. Poor <move ordering> can leave the cost at $\Theta(b^{d'})$, giving no asymptotic depth improvement. For $b=1$, there is no branching to prune and the doubling argument does not apply.<CS 188 minimax notes>[§3.2.1, dependence of pruning efficiency on child order]{ref}

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

<Expectiminimax> adds <chance nodes> to the MAX and MIN decisions. A <chance node> uses
$$
V(s)=\sum_j p_jV(s_j),
$$
so exact evaluation generally needs all outcomes with positive <probability>. An unfavorable child does not determine the <expected value>: a later, sufficiently favorable child can offset it. Ordinary <alpha-beta pruning> therefore does not directly discard the remaining outcomes.

Random outcomes also enlarge the effective <branching factor>. If each player decision has $b$ choices and each action has $c$ chance outcomes, exploring $d$ player decisions can require order $(bc)^d$ leaves rather than $b^d$. Under the same budget, this gives roughly
$$
d_{\mathrm{stochastic}}\approx\frac{\log n}{\log b+\log c}
$$
before additional pruning benefits available in the deterministic game are considered. Exact <probability> calculations add overhead as well. These effects explain the typically shallower horizon; randomness alone is not a theorem of inevitable slowdown. Known <utility> bounds permit some <expected value> bounds and specialized pruning, and chance sampling can trade exactness for speed.<CS 188 stochastic and general game notes>[§3.3, chance-node expected-value backup; §3.3.1, mixed layer types]{ref}

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

**Propagate an entire <utility> pair from a child chosen to maximize the component of the player whose turn it is.** This is the two-player case of <max-n search>. At a terminal node let $V(s)=(U_A(s),U_B(s))$. At an A-node choose
$$
s^*\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{s'\in\operatorname{Children}(s)}V_A(s'),
\qquad V(s)=V(s^*),
$$
and at a B-node replace $V_A$ by $V_B$. Do not maximize the two coordinates independently: their separate maxima may belong to incompatible outcomes. Fix a tie rule, such as the first child in the specified order. The rule recursively gives a <pure strategy> <subgame perfect equilibrium> in a finite <perfect-information game>: <backward induction> makes the moving player's selected continuation a best response at every node.<CS 188 stochastic and general game notes>[§3.4, entire utility tuples selected by the moving player's component]{ref}

In the original Figure 2, each of the three children of the root belongs to B. The left B-node prefers its first leaf because $1>0$, the middle B-node prefers its first leaf because $2>-2$, and the right B-node prefers its second leaf because $3>1$. Their propagated <utility> pairs, from left to right, are
$$
(1,1),\qquad(-1,2),\qquad(-1,3).
$$
At the root A compares $1,-1,-1$ and chooses the left child. Thus **the root pair is $(1,1)$**, with play following the root's left branch and then that B-node's first branch. No tie affects this outcome.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

**The usual <alpha-beta pruning> inference fails because increasing B's <utility> need not decrease A's <utility>.** A partially explored B-node may have found a child unfavorable to A, but B may prefer an unseen child favorable to A as well. An observed low A-coordinate therefore supplies no upper bound on the A-coordinate ultimately propagated by B.

For a concrete <counterexample>, let a root A-node already have an alternative outcome $(1,0)$. In another B-node, the first explored child yields $(0,1)$. A zero-sum-style cutoff would discard its remaining children because $0<1$. But an unseen child with outcome $(100,2)$ is preferred by B, and makes the complete B-node return $(100,2)$, which A strictly prefers to its incumbent. Such a cutoff changes the answer.

The problem persists even after observing a large B-coordinate: without a known relation between coordinates or useful global <utility> bounds, an unseen pair may have still larger values for both players. Hence there is no generally valid ordinary scalar <alpha-beta pruning> rule for unrestricted <general-sum games>. This does not rule out all <search pruning>: additional bounds, dominance information or a relationship between players' <utilities> can justify specific cutoffs, as in (g).

=== g
{scope}

==== Solution

Consider a B-node whose parent is an A-node with an already completed incumbent outcome having A-coordinate $\alpha$. At the B-node, let $(x,y)$ be the best completed child pair found so far according to B's coordinate. Every remaining child can be skipped, and this B-option discarded by its parent, if
$$
\boxed{\epsilon-y\le\alpha,\quad\text{equivalently }y\ge\epsilon-\alpha.}
$$
Use a strict inequality if ties must remain open; equality is safe under the fixed rule that the parent retains its already selected child on an A-coordinate tie. This is a <nearly-zero-sum pruning> rule.

To prove it, note first that a completed <max-n search> value is some terminal <utility> pair: each backup copies one entire child pair. Thus the nearly-zero-sum inequality holds at every completed subtree, not just at leaves. Since B maximizes its own coordinate, the ultimately selected pair $(u,v)$ at this B-node has $v\ge y$. Therefore
$$
u+v\le\epsilon
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
u\le\epsilon-v\le\epsilon-y\le\alpha.
$$
The already available A-option is at least as good, so remaining children of this B-node cannot alter the parent's selected continuation under the stated tie rule. The second half of the two-sided nearly-zero-sum condition also gives $x+y\ge-\epsilon$, hence
$$
u\le\epsilon-y\le x+2\epsilon.
$$
Consequently **$x+2\epsilon\le\alpha$ is another sufficient cutoff**, useful if one phrases the test using the observed A-coordinate. The pair-based test is sharper: the value of $y$ can certify a cutoff even when the conservative $x+2\epsilon$ test cannot.

When $\epsilon=0$, every pair satisfies $y=-x$, and the rule reduces to $x\le\alpha$, the ordinary MIN-node <alpha-beta pruning> cutoff. The extra $2\epsilon$ in the A-coordinate-only condition is necessary in general: an observed pair $(-1,0)$ satisfies the bound for $\epsilon=1$, but B prefers an unseen $(1-\delta,\delta)$ for $0<\delta<1$. A increases by almost $2\epsilon$; using just $x+\epsilon$ as the upper bound would be invalid.

Implementation must discard the ruled-out B-option at its parent rather than return $(x,y)$ as an exact completed <utility> pair. The proof uses an actual completed alternative at that parent; it does not authorize passing arbitrary distant ancestor bounds through <general-sum game> backups as if every scalar <alpha-beta pruning> invariant still held. With these local certified cutoffs, an induction over completed subtrees preserves the selected pair and hence the root outcome.
