= Probability and computing
{scope}
{title2=A14681W1}

Solutions to the paper dated 1 June 2015.<Probability and Computing examination paper by the University of Oxford (2015)>[Cover, code A14681W1; printed pp. 2–3, Questions 1–3]{ref} Every numbered subpart is treated. **The original assertions in 2(b) and 2(c) are false:** the two-bin <repeated singleton-removal allocation> gives exact <counterexamples>. Those answers explain the defect and the intended conditional calculation; 2(d) also supplies an independent, valid bound for the actual process.

== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <expectation> of a real <random variable> $X$ on a <probability space> is its <integral> against the <probability measure>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E X=\int_\Omega X(\omega)\,d\mathbb P(\omega).}
$$
For a discrete <random variable>, this becomes $\mathbb E X=\sum_x x\mathbb P(X=x)$; for a <random variable> with <probability density function> $f$, it becomes $\mathbb E X=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}xf(x)\,dx$. A finite <expectation> requires $\mathbb E|X|<\infty$, making $X$ an <integrable random variable>. An extended <expectation> can also be defined when just one of the positive and negative parts has finite <expectation>; the indeterminate difference $\infty-\infty$ is not allowed.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For a real <random variable> $X$ with finite <expectation> $\mu=\mathbb E X$, its <variance> is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E[(X-\mu)^2].}
$$
When the <second moment> is finite, expansion and <linearity of expectation> give $\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E[X^2]-\mu^2$. The definition also permits infinite <variance> when the <expectation> is finite but the <second moment> is infinite.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

<Chebyshev's inequality> assumes a real <random variable> $X$ with finite <expectation> $\mu$ and finite <variance> $\sigma^2$. For every real $a>0$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(|X-\mu|\geq a)\leq\frac{\sigma^2}{a^2}.}
$$
No <independence> assumption is needed. Applying <Markov's inequality> to the nonnegative <random variable> $(X-\mu)^2$ proves the formula, including the zero-<variance> case.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $D_i$ be the result of roll $i$, so $X=\sum_{i=1}^{100}D_i$. Each $D_i$ has the <uniform distribution on a finite set> $\{1,\ldots,6\}$, giving
$$
\mathbb E D_i=\frac{1+2+3+4+5+6}{6}=\frac72,
\qquad
\mathbb E D_i^2=\frac{1+4+9+16+25+36}{6}=\frac{91}{6},
$$
and hence <variance> $\operatorname{Var}(D_i)=91/6-(7/2)^2=35/12$. The rolls are <independent>, so <linearity of expectation> and <variance additivity for independent random variables> give
$$
\mathbb E X=350,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(X)=100\frac{35}{12}=\frac{875}{3}.
$$
All the required <moments> are finite. Taking $a=50$ in <Chebyshev's inequality> now yields
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(|X-350|\geq50)\leq\frac{875/3}{2500}=\frac7{60}\approx0.1167.}
$$
This is an upper bound, rather than an exact tail <probability>.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Choose a positive integer-valued <random variable> with <probability mass function>
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(X=k)=\frac4{k(k+1)(k+2)},\qquad k=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
These masses are positive. They sum to one because the <partial fraction decomposition>
$$
\frac4{k(k+1)(k+2)}=2\left(\frac1{k(k+1)}-\frac1{(k+1)(k+2)}\right)
$$
makes their sum a <telescoping series> with value $2/(1\cdot2)=1$. Its <expectation> is another <telescoping series>:
$$
\mathbb E X=4\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{(k+1)(k+2)}
=4\sum_{k=1}^\infty\left(\frac1{k+1}-\frac1{k+2}\right)=2.
$$
By contrast, since $k+1\leq2k$ and $k+2\leq3k$ for $k\geq1$, its <second moment> satisfies
$$
\mathbb E X^2=4\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{k}{(k+1)(k+2)}
\geq\frac23\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1k=\infty.
$$
The comparison uses the divergent <harmonic series>. To check the <variance> directly, for $k\geq4$ we have $(k-2)^2\geq k^2/4$, so the corresponding tail of $\mathbb E[(X-2)^2]$ also diverges. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E X=2<\infty,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(X)=\infty.}
$$
Here “unbounded” means infinite <variance> for this single <probability distribution>.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

For $X$ with <binomial distribution> $B(n,p)$, where $n$ is a nonnegative <integer> and $0\leq p\leq1$, use its <probability mass function> and the <binomial theorem>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
M_X(t)&=\mathbb E[e^{tX}]
=\sum_{k=0}^n e^{tk}\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk (pe^t)^k(1-p)^{n-k}
=\boxed{(1-p+pe^t)^n},\qquad t\in\mathbb R.
\end{aligned}
$$
This <moment-generating function of a binomial distribution> is finite for every real $t$, since the <random variable> has finite support. The formula also includes the degenerate cases $p=0$, $p=1$ and $n=0$.

=== g
{scope}

==== Solution

The <independence> of $X$ and $Y$ permits factoring the <expectation> of the product $e^{tX}e^{tY}$. The <moment-generating function of a binomial distribution> from the previous part therefore gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{X+Y}(t)&=\mathbb E[e^{t(X+Y)}]
=\mathbb E[e^{tX}]\mathbb E[e^{tY}]\\
&=(1-p+pe^t)^n(1-p+pe^t)^m
=\boxed{(1-p+pe^t)^{n+m}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
This is the <moment-generating function> of $B(n+m,p)$. Both <moment-generating functions> are finite on a neighbourhood of zero, so the <uniqueness theorem for moment-generating functions> identifies the <probability distribution>:
$$
\boxed{X+Y\sim B(n+m,p).}
$$
The common success <probability> $p$ and <independence> are both used in this argument.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For each of the $b$ balls, let $I_i$ be the <indicator random variable> that ball $i$ survives the round of <uniform balls-in-bins allocation>. Condition on its chosen bin. Each of the other $b-1$ balls avoids that bin with <probability> $1-1/n$, independently, so the <probability> that ball $i$ is served is $(1-1/n)^{b-1}$. Hence, for $b\geq2$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[\text{balls next round}\mid b]
=b\left[1-\left(1-\frac1n\right)^{b-1}\right].}
$$
We used <linearity of expectation> to sum the <indicator random variables>; their mutual <independence> is unnecessary. For $b=0$ or $b=1$, the next count is zero. This states the boundary cases without an ambiguous $0^0$ when $n=1$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

**There is no proof of the printed assertion: it is false for the stated <expectations>.** The original explicitly defines $x_j$ as the expected remaining count, so replacing it by a deterministic upper envelope changes the problem.<Probability and Computing examination paper by the University of Oxford (2015)>[Printed p. 2, definition preceding Question 2(b), and Question 2(b)]{ref}

Let $B_j$ be the actual count after $j$ rounds and $x_j=\mathbb E B_j$. The <union bound> gives the valid conditional estimate
$$
\mathbb E[B_{j+1}\mid B_j=b]
\leq\frac{b(b-1)}n.
$$
By the <law of total expectation>, the resulting unconditional bound is
$$
\boxed{x_{j+1}\leq\frac{\mathbb E[B_j(B_j-1)]}{n}
=\frac{x_j^2-x_j+\operatorname{Var}(B_j)}n.}
$$
Discarding the <variance> term is an <expectation closure error for a nonlinear recurrence>. In particular, <Jensen's inequality> says $\mathbb E B_j^2\geq x_j^2$, which cannot justify the requested upper bound.

For an exact <counterexample>, start with two balls and two bins. A round serves both balls if they land separately and serves neither if they collide. Each outcome has <probability> $1/2$, independently in successive rounds while both balls remain. Consequently
$$
\mathbb P(B_j=2)=2^{-j},\qquad
\boxed{x_j=2^{1-j}.}
$$
At $j=2$, the requested inequality would say $x_3\leq x_2^2/2$, but
$$
\boxed{x_3=\frac14>\frac{(1/2)^2}{2}=\frac18.}
$$

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

**The printed bound also fails for the actual <expectations>.** In the two-bin <counterexample> from part (b), at $j=5$ the true value and proposed bound are
$$
\boxed{x_5=\frac1{16}>2\,2^{-2^{5-2}}=\frac1{128}.}
$$
The supplied premise $x_2\leq n/2$ does hold in this example: $x_2=1/2\leq1$. Thus the problem is the invalid recurrence in part (b), rather than failure of the supplied starting estimate.<Probability and Computing examination paper by the University of Oxford (2015)>[Printed p. 2, Question 2(c), including the supplied bound on x_2]{ref}

The intended <mathematical induction> is valid **conditionally on the recurrence**. For a deterministic nonnegative sequence $y_j$ satisfying $y_2\leq n/2$ and $y_{j+1}\leq y_j^2/n$, the base case is $y_2\leq n2^{-1}$. If $y_j\leq n2^{-2^{j-2}}$, then
$$
y_{j+1}\leq\frac{(n2^{-2^{j-2}})^2}{n}
=n2^{-2^{j-1}}.
$$
This proves the proposed form for $y_j$ but cannot identify $y_j$ with $\mathbb E B_j$.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

If the bound in part (c) were available, the intended answer would be
$$
j_{\mathrm{intended}}(n)=2+\left\lceil\log_2\!\left(\max\{1,\log_2 n\}\right)\right\rceil,
\qquad n\geq1.
$$
Indeed $2^{j_{\mathrm{intended}}(n)-2}\geq\log_2 n$, so that bound would give $x_{j(n)}\leq n2^{-\log_2 n}=1$. Its growth is $O(\log\log n)$ in <Big O notation>. Because (c) is false, this substitution alone does not prove the result for the actual <repeated singleton-removal allocation>.

The requested order can nevertheless be established for the actual process. Here is a concrete, deliberately generous replacement, with $j(0)=0$ for the empty system:
$$
\boxed{j(n)=\begin{cases}
\left\lceil\log_{3/2}(2n)\right\rceil,&1\leq n<2^{120},\\
11+\left\lceil\log_2\log_2 n\right\rceil,&n\geq2^{120}.
\end{cases}}
$$
The large cutoff makes the numerical estimates below simple. A finite initial range does not affect the $O(\log\log n)$ <asymptotic analysis>.

First, whenever $2\leq b\leq n$, a ball is served with <probability> at least
$$
\left(1-\frac1n\right)^{n-1}\geq e^{-1}>\frac13.
$$
Thus part (a) gives $\mathbb E[B_{t+1}\mid B_t]\leq2B_t/3$, also for $B_t\leq1$. The <law of total expectation> yields $x_t\leq n(2/3)^t$. This proves $x_{j(n)}\leq1/2$ for the small-$n$ branch, including $n=1$, whose single ball is served immediately.

For the large-$n$ branch, we need control of the random count, rather than an unjustified recurrence for its <expectation>. If $b$ balls are allocated in a round, changing one chosen bin changes the number of singleton bins by at most two. The surviving count $F_b$ therefore has the <bounded differences property> with constants $2$. <McDiarmid's inequality> gives
$$
\mathbb P(F_b>\mathbb EF_b+u)\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{u^2}{2b}\right),\qquad u>0.
$$
This can be applied conditionally after any history, since the next allocations are fresh <independent random variables>.

Use this <concentration inequality> in three stages. Write $L=\lceil\log_2\log_2 n\rceil$.

* For the first eight rounds, whenever $b>n^{3/4}$, the <conditional expectation> is at most $2b/3$. Taking $u=b/12$ shows that the next count is at most $3b/4$, except with <probability> at most $e^{-b/288}$. If the count has already fallen below $n^{3/4}$, it stays there. Since $(3/4)^8<1/8$ and $n^{3/4}\leq n/8$, this gives $B_8\leq n/8$ outside the stated exceptional events.
* Starting with $a_0=n/8$, set $a_{r+1}=2a_r^2/n$. If $B\leq a_r$ and $a_r\geq n^{3/4}$, part (a) and the <union bound> give $\mathbb EF_B\leq a_r^2/n$. Taking $u=a_r^2/n$ gives a next count at most $a_{r+1}$, except with <probability> at most $e^{-a_r^3/(2n^2)}\leq e^{-n^{1/4}/2}$. The deterministic solution is $a_r=(n/2)4^{-2^r}$. It falls below $n^{3/4}$ within $L$ steps. After reaching that threshold the count cannot increase, so $B_{8+L}\leq n^{3/4}$ outside the exceptional events.
* From $b\leq n^{3/4}$, the next <conditional expectation> is at most $n^{1/2}$. A deviation $u=n^{1/2}$ leaves at most $2n^{1/2}$ balls except with <probability> $e^{-n^{1/4}/2}$. From at most $2n^{1/2}$ balls, the next <conditional expectation> is at most $4$. Taking $u=n^{1/3}-4\geq n^{1/3}/2$ leaves at most $n^{1/3}$ balls except with <probability> $e^{-n^{1/6}/16}$. Conditional on this final count bound, one more round has <expectation> at most $n^{-1/3}$.

Every exceptional <probability> above is at most $e^{-n^{1/6}/300}$, and there are at most $L+10$ opportunities for an exception. For $n\geq2^{120}$, the elementary bounds $n^{1/6}/300\geq4\ln n$ and $L+10\leq n$ give, by the <union bound>, total exceptional <probability> at most $n^{-3}$. The first numerical inequality holds at the cutoff and remains true because $n^{1/6}/\ln n$ is increasing there. On an exceptional history there are at most $n$ balls. Taking the last round's <conditional expectation> and then averaging gives
$$
\boxed{x_{11+L}\leq n^{-1/3}+n\,n^{-3}=n^{-1/3}+n^{-2}<1.}
$$
This proves the displayed replacement $j(n)$ for the literal process without relying on either false assertion.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Consider any <edge> $\{u,v\}$ of the <simple graph>. One endpoint comes first in the <permutation> $\sigma$; suppose it is $u$. Then $v$ has a <graph neighbour> preceding it, namely $u$, so $v\notin S(\sigma)$. Therefore no <edge> has both endpoints in $S(\sigma)$:
$$
\boxed{S(\sigma)\text{ is an independent set for every }\sigma.}
$$
This argument uses only the order of the endpoints, with no random choice required.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

Choose a <uniform random permutation> of the $n$ <vertices>, giving every one of the $n!$ orders the same <probability>. For a fixed <vertex> $i$, consider $i$ together with its $d_i$ <graph neighbours>. The relative order of these $d_i+1$ <vertices> is uniform, so each is equally likely to be first. The membership rule for $S(\sigma)$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(i\in S(\sigma))=\frac1{d_i+1}.}
$$
Let $I_i$ be the <indicator random variable> of this event. The <cardinality> is $|S(\sigma)|=\sum_{i=1}^n I_i$, and <linearity of expectation> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E|S(\sigma)|=\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb E I_i=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac1{d_i+1}.}
$$
The <indicator random variables> are generally dependent; only the <uniform random permutation> and <linearity of expectation> were needed. An isolated <vertex> has $d_i=0$ and is always included, as the formula correctly predicts.

=== g
{scope}

==== Solution

The <expectation> from part (f) is the average of $|S(\sigma)|$ over the finite collection of <permutations>. At least one value is as large as its average. By part (e), the corresponding $S(\sigma)$ is an <independent set>, so
$$
\boxed{\alpha(G)\geq\sum_{i=1}^n\frac1{d_i+1}.}
$$
Here $\alpha(G)$ is the <independence number>. Since its value is an <integer>, one can equivalently round the right side up with the <ceiling function>. This application of the <probabilistic method> is the <Caro-Wei bound>.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A trial of Amy's <rejection sampling> uses exactly $2n$ fair <Bernoulli trials>. Exactly $\binom{2n}{n}$ of the $2^{2n}$ equally likely <binary sequences> have $n$ heads, so the acceptance <probability> is
$$
p_n=\frac{\binom{2n}{n}}{2^{2n}}.
$$
Independent retries make the number $T$ of trials a <geometric random variable> on $\{1,2,\ldots\}$ with <expectation> $1/p_n$. The total number $C$ of coin tosses is $2nT$, so
$$
\boxed{\mathbb EC=\frac{2n\,2^{2n}}{\binom{2n}{n}}.}
$$
Using <Stirling's approximation>, the <central binomial coefficient> satisfies
$$
\binom{2n}{n}
\sim\frac{\sqrt{4\pi n}(2n/e)^{2n}}{2\pi n(n/e)^{2n}}
=\frac{4^n}{\sqrt{\pi n}}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\mathbb EC\sim2\sqrt\pi\,n^{3/2}.}
$$
The <rejection sampling> output is uniform among the balanced <binary sequences>: they have equal <probability> on every trial, and conditioning on acceptance preserves that equality. For $n=0$, the empty output needs zero tosses; the formula above concerns $n\geq1$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

**Betty's scheme is biased for every $n\geq2$.** The output $H^nT^n$ occurs precisely when the first $n$ tosses are heads: the algorithm stops there and appends $n$ tails. Thus
$$
\mathbb P(H^nT^n)=2^{-n}.
$$
For comparison, take the output $(HT)^n$. Its first $2n-2$ symbols contain $n-1$ heads and $n-1$ tails, so neither stopping threshold has been reached. The next symbol is the $n$th head, at which point the last tail is appended. Its unique generating prefix has length $2n-1$, giving
$$
\mathbb P((HT)^n)=2^{-(2n-1)}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{\mathbb P(H^nT^n)}{\mathbb P((HT)^n)}=2^{n-1}>1\quad(n\geq2).}
$$
The faster stopping rule does not produce a <uniform distribution on a finite set> of balanced <binary sequences>. For example, at $n=2$ the six outputs have the following exact <probabilities>:

|| Output
|| Betty
|| Uniform target

| `HHTT`
| $1/4$
| $1/6$

| `HTHT`
| $1/8$
| $1/6$

| `HTTH`
| $1/8$
| $1/6$

| `THHT`
| $1/8$
| $1/6$

| `THTH`
| $1/8$
| $1/6$

| `TTHH`
| $1/4$
| $1/6$

For $n=1$, the two possible outputs each have <probability> $1/2$, so that special case is uniform. For $n=0$, simply return the empty output.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Use <uniform balanced binary sequence sampling> by choosing the positions of the heads. Number the $2n=2^m$ positions from zero to $2n-1$. Start with no selected positions. Repeatedly toss the fair coin $m$ times, interpret the resulting <bits> as a position, and select that position if it has not already been selected; otherwise discard this block of tosses and retry. Stop when $n$ distinct positions have been selected. Put heads at those positions and tails everywhere else.

After $s$ positions have been selected, every one of the remaining positions has the same <probability> of being the next new position. This follows either by summing a <geometric series> over rejected blocks or by conditioning a <uniform distribution on a finite set> on avoiding the selected positions. Every ordered list of $n$ distinct selected positions consequently has <probability>
$$
\frac1{(2n)(2n-1)\cdots(n+1)}.
$$
Each fixed $n$-element <subset> has $n!$ such orders, so its <probability> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{n!}{(2n)(2n-1)\cdots(n+1)}=\frac1{\binom{2n}{n}}.}
$$
Thus every balanced <binary sequence> is equally likely.

When $s$ positions are selected, a block succeeds with <probability> $(2n-s)/(2n)$, so the <geometric distribution> gives <expectation> $2n/(2n-s)$ blocks until the next new position. By <linearity of expectation>, the total expected toss count is
$$
\boxed{m\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}\frac{2n}{2n-s}<2nm=O(n\log n),\qquad n\geq1.}
$$
The strict inequality holds since $s<n$, making each term less than two. A Boolean <array> records membership of each position; initializing it and constructing the output require $O(n)$ additional work. The scheme terminates with <probability> one, since every stage has positive success <probability>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Colour each <edge> of the <complete graph> $K_n$ independently red or blue, with <probability> $1/2$ for each colour. Every four-element <subset> $A$ of <vertices> determines exactly one copy of $K_4$, containing six <edges>. These six <edges> are all red with <probability> $2^{-6}$ and all blue with <probability> $2^{-6}$. The events are <disjoint events>, so
$$
\mathbb P(A\text{ is monochromatic})=2\cdot2^{-6}=2^{-5}.
$$
Let $I_A$ be the <indicator random variable> for this event and let $Z=\sum_A I_A$ count the <monochromatic> copies. <Linearity of expectation> gives
$$
\mathbb EZ=\sum_A\mathbb E I_A=\binom n4\,2^{-5}.
$$
The <indicator random variables> for overlapping copies need not be <independent>. Averaging over all <edge colorings> ensures that at least one has $Z\leq\mathbb EZ$. Thus the <probabilistic method> proves
$$
\boxed{\text{Some two-colour edge coloring has }Z\leq\frac1{32}\binom n4.}
$$
For $0\leq n<4$, both sides are zero and any <edge coloring> works. Copies are counted by their four-<vertex> <subsets>, rather than by their $4!$ possible labellings.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Set $N=\binom n4$. Independently sample an <edge coloring> exactly as in part (d), count its <monochromatic> copies of $K_4$ by examining all four-<vertex> <subsets>, and accept precisely when $32Z\leq N$. Otherwise start over with a fresh <edge coloring>.

To prove an expected <polynomial time> bound, the existence argument alone is insufficient: we need a lower bound on the acceptance <probability> at this exact threshold. Since $32Z$ is a nonnegative <integer> with <expectation> $N$, rejection implies $32Z\geq N+1$. <Markov's inequality> therefore gives the <integer-valued expectation threshold restart bound>
$$
\mathbb P(\text{reject})\leq\frac{N}{N+1},
\qquad
\boxed{\mathbb P(\text{accept})\geq\frac1{N+1}.}
$$
The number of attempts has a <geometric distribution>, with <expectation> at most $N+1$. Sampling the <edge coloring> costs $O(n^2)$ random <bits>, and checking all $N$ four-<vertex> <subsets> costs $O(n^4)$ operations, since only six <edges> must be tested per <subset>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[\text{running time}]=O((N+1)n^4)=O(n^8).}
$$
This is a <randomized algorithm> that always returns an <edge coloring> meeting the original threshold and terminates with <probability> one. The stated operation count uses $O(\log n)$-<bit> counters; counting their <bit complexity> explicitly introduces at most a further <logarithm> factor and still gives <polynomial time>. For $n<4$, return any <edge coloring> immediately.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

Order the <edges> arbitrarily and fix their colours one at a time. After a partial assignment $P$, regard all unassigned <edges> as independent fair red/blue choices, and compute
$$
Q(P)=\mathbb E[Z\mid P].
$$
The <conditional expectation of a monochromatic clique count> is easy to compute. For each four-<vertex> <subset> $A$, let $r$ be the number of its six <edges> still unassigned. Its contribution $q_A(P)$ to $Q(P)$ is
$$
q_A(P)=\begin{cases}
0,&\text{both colours already occur among its fixed edges},\\
2^{-r},&\text{at least one edge is fixed, all in the same colour},\\
2^{1-r}=1/32,&\text{none of its six edges is fixed}.
\end{cases}
$$
In the second case, every remaining <edge> must match the already fixed colour; this includes a completely fixed <monochromatic> copy, for which $r=0$ and the contribution is one. In the last case, the six unassigned <edges> may become either all red or all blue. By <linearity of expectation>,
$$
Q(P)=\sum_{|A|=4}q_A(P).
$$
For the next unassigned <edge> $e$, compute $Q(P,e=\text{red})$ and $Q(P,e=\text{blue})$. The <law of total expectation> gives
$$
Q(P)=\frac12Q(P,e=\text{red})+\frac12Q(P,e=\text{blue}).
$$
Choose the colour with the smaller value, resolving a tie by a fixed rule. At least one is at most their average, so the <loop invariant> is that $Q$ never increases. Initially $Q=\binom n4/32$; once every <edge> is fixed, there is no randomness left and $Q=Z$. Therefore the <method of conditional expectations> guarantees
$$
\boxed{Z\leq\frac1{32}\binom n4.}
$$
A direct implementation recomputes both sums over all $O(n^4)$ four-<vertex> <subsets> for each of the $O(n^2)$ <edges>, requiring $O(n^6)$ arithmetic operations. The comparisons can be exact: every contribution is a multiple of $1/32$, so store $32Q$ as an <integer> with $O(\log n)$ <bits>. This gives $O(n^6\log n)$ <bit complexity> with elementary arithmetic, and hence a deterministic <polynomial time> construction. No random sampling is performed by this <derandomization>.
