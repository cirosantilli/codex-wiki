= Logic and proof
{scope}
{title2=A10468W1}

Solutions to the three questions of the <University of Oxford> <computer science> Finals examination held on 10 June 2015. Question 3(d) has a false assertion as printed; its solution gives a <countermodel> and explains the additional hypotheses under which the usual <least Herbrand model> argument works.<Logic and Proof examination paper of the University of Oxford (2015)>[Cover and printed pp. 2–4, Questions 1–3; the unrestricted quantifier-free matrix in Question 3(d) is visible on printed p. 4]{ref}

== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Apply the distributive law of <Boolean algebra> to the two <logical conjunctions>. With $A=P$, $B=\neg Q$, $C=\neg P$, and $D=Q$, the identity $(A\land B)\lor(C\land D)\equiv(A\lor C)\land(A\lor D)\land(B\lor C)\land(B\lor D)$ gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
(P\land\neg Q)\lor(\neg P\land Q)
&\equiv(P\lor\neg P)\land(P\lor Q)\land(\neg Q\lor\neg P)\land(\neg Q\lor Q)\\
&\equiv\top\land(P\lor Q)\land(\neg P\lor\neg Q)\land\top\\
&\equiv\boxed{(P\lor Q)\land(\neg P\lor\neg Q).}
\end{aligned}
$$
The second step uses complementation and commutativity in <Boolean algebra>; the last uses the identity law for <logical conjunction>. The result is in <conjunctive normal form>: its first <Boolean clause> requires at least one input to be true, and its second <Boolean clause> prohibits both inputs being true. Together they express <exclusive or>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Replace each implication by a <logical disjunction> and use the <conjunctive normal form> for <exclusive or> from part (a). The resulting <Boolean clauses> are
$$
\begin{array}{c|l}
F_1&C_1=\neg H\lor Y\\
F_2&C_2=\neg T\lor\neg I\\
F_3&C_3=H\lor T,\quad C_4=\neg H\lor\neg T\\
F_4&C_5=Y\lor I,\quad C_6=\neg Y\lor\neg I.
\end{array}
$$
Here the displayed array is a list of the six <Boolean clauses>, whose <logical conjunction> is equivalent to all four constraints.

Use <resolution> first on $I$, then on $T$, and finally on $H$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
C_7&=\operatorname{Res}_{I}(C_2,C_5)=\neg T\lor Y,\\
C_8&=\operatorname{Res}_{T}(C_3,C_7)=H\lor Y,\\
C_9&=\operatorname{Res}_{H}(C_1,C_8)=Y\lor Y=\boxed{Y.}
\end{aligned}
$$
Removing the repeated <Boolean literal> in the final <Boolean clause> uses idempotence. Neither $C_4$ nor $C_6$ is needed: even the weaker assumptions that the coin has at least one outcome and that at least one person wins already force $Y$, once the two implications hold.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Take the single <Boolean clause>
$$
\boxed{F=P\lor Q\lor R.}
$$
This is a <3-CNF formula>. Suppose a <2-CNF formula> $K$ were <logically equivalent> to $F$. Every <Boolean clause> $C$ of $K$ would then be a consequence of $F$.

Consider any nontautological <Boolean clause> $C$ containing at most two <Boolean literals>. Choose a <Boolean valuation> falsifying its <Boolean literals>. At least one of $P,Q,R$ does not occur in $C$; assign that <Boolean variable> true. The resulting <Boolean valuation> satisfies $F$ while falsifying $C$. This contradicts $F\models C$. An empty <Boolean clause> also cannot be a consequence of the satisfiable $F$.

Consequently every <Boolean clause> of $K$ would have to be a tautology. Their <logical conjunction> would be true under every <Boolean valuation>, whereas $F$ is false when $P=Q=R=0$. This proves that no such $K$ exists.

The argument even allows additional ordinary <Boolean variables> in $K$: at most two <Boolean literals> still leave one of $P,Q,R$ free to be made true. Introducing extra variables together with <existential quantification> would change the requested notion of <logical equivalence>, so it is not a way around the argument.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Enumerate the <vertices> as $V=\{1,\ldots,n\}$ and introduce a <Boolean variable> $X_{u,v}$ for each ordered pair of <vertices>. Its intended meaning is that a proposed <graph automorphism> sends $u$ to $v$. Build four groups of <Boolean clauses>.

First require exactly one image for each <vertex>:
$$
A=\bigwedge_{u\in V}\left[
\left(\bigvee_{v\in V}X_{u,v}\right)
\land\bigwedge_{v<w}(\neg X_{u,v}\lor\neg X_{u,w})\right].
$$
Next require that distinct <vertices> have distinct images:
$$
B=\bigwedge_{v\in V}\bigwedge_{u<w}(\neg X_{u,v}\lor\neg X_{w,v}).
$$
Together $A$ and $B$ specify an injective <function> $f:V\to V$. Since $V$ is finite, this <function> is a <bijection>; a separate surjectivity condition is unnecessary.

Write $e(u,w)=1$ when $(u,w)\in E$ and $e(u,w)=0$ otherwise. Prohibit every pair of images that would change adjacency:
$$
C=\bigwedge_{\substack{u,w,v,z\in V\\ e(u,w)\ne e(v,z)}}
(\neg X_{u,v}\lor\neg X_{w,z}).
$$
Checking both possible mismatches preserves both edges and nonedges. Finally require a moved <vertex>:
$$
D=\bigvee_{u\in V}\neg X_{u,u}.
$$
The desired <propositional formula> is
$$
\boxed{\varphi_G=A\land B\land C\land D.}
$$

If a <Boolean valuation> satisfies $\varphi_G$, $A$ selects a unique image for every <vertex>, $B$ makes the resulting <function> a <bijection>, $C$ preserves adjacency in both directions, and $D$ makes this <graph automorphism> different from the <identity function>. Conversely, given such a <graph automorphism> $f$, set $X_{u,v}$ true exactly when $v=f(u)$. Each of $A,B,C,D$ is then satisfied.

There are $n^2$ <Boolean variables>, $O(n^3)$ image and injectivity <Boolean clauses>, and at most $n^4$ adjacency <Boolean clauses>. With the <graph> stored by its <adjacency matrix>, testing a mismatch takes constant time. Thus the construction has <polynomial time> and output length $O(n^4\log(n+1))$ with binary-indexed variable names. For $n=0$, interpret the empty <logical disjunction> $D$ as false; for $n=1$, $A$ and $D$ conflict. These cases correctly have no nonidentity <graph automorphism>.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

The <propositional compactness theorem> states that an arbitrary set $S$ of <propositional formulas> is satisfiable if and only if every finite <subset> of $S$ is satisfiable:
$$
\boxed{S\text{ has a model}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{every finite }S_0\subseteq S\text{ has a model}.}
$$
The satisfying <Boolean valuation> may depend on $S_0$ on the right; the conclusion on the left provides one <Boolean valuation> satisfying all of $S$ simultaneously.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $S=\{F_i:i\ge1\}$. Every finite <subset> of $S$ is satisfiable: if $F_N$ has the largest index occurring, the successive <semantic entailments> imply $F_N\models F_i$ for every earlier index, so any <Boolean valuation> satisfying $F_N$ satisfies that finite <subset>. The <propositional compactness theorem> therefore also shows that $S$ itself is satisfiable.

Suppose that $G$ had exactly the common models of $S$. Then $S\models G$, and hence $S\cup\{\neg G\}$ is unsatisfiable. By the <propositional compactness theorem>, finitely many $F_i$ together with $\neg G$ are already unsatisfiable. Enlarge the finite list if necessary so it is nonempty, and let $N$ be its greatest index. We obtain
$$
F_N\models G.
$$
On the other hand, the asserted equality of model classes gives $G\models F_{N+1}$. Transitivity of <semantic entailment> would yield
$$
F_N\models F_{N+1},
$$
contradicting the strictness assumption at index $N$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{No single propositional formula defines the common models of all }F_i.}
$$
The obstruction is the finite character of <propositional formulas>: a single consequence of an infinite set already follows from finitely many premises, whereas this chain continues to impose strictly stronger requirements forever.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <first-order theory> in a fixed <first-order signature> is a set of <first-order sentences>. Under the closed-theory convention used in the question, it contains all its <semantic entailments>: $T\models\theta$ implies $\theta\in T$. One can equivalently begin with a set of axioms and take its closure under consequence.

A <complete theory> is a consistent <first-order theory> that decides every <first-order sentence> $\theta$ in its <first-order signature>:
$$
\boxed{T\models\theta\quad\text{or}\quad T\models\neg\theta.}
$$
Consistency ensures that exactly one of these alternatives holds.

A <decidable theory> is one for which an <algorithm> always terminates and determines whether a given <first-order sentence> is a consequence:
$$
\boxed{\{\theta:T\models\theta\}\text{ is decidable}.}
$$
If the <first-order theory> is already closed under consequence, this is its membership problem. It is a condition on the consequences, not merely on whether the chosen set of axioms has a decidable membership test.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

A <first-order theory> $T$ admits <quantifier elimination> when every <first-order formula> $\varphi(\bar x)$ has a <quantifier-free formula> $\psi(\bar x)$, in the same <first-order signature> and with no additional <free variables>, such that
$$
\boxed{T\models\forall\bar x\,[\varphi(\bar x)\leftrightarrow\psi(\bar x)].}
$$
Thus all <first-order models> of $T$ agree that the two <first-order formulas> define the same tuples. This definition concerns <first-order formulas> with <free variables>, as well as <first-order sentences>; merely deciding closed assertions would be weaker. The existence of an effective procedure is an additional issue, supplied by the explicit construction in part (d).

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the <Rado graph> on the <natural numbers>. Interpret the binary relation by
$$
\boxed{E(i,j)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
[i<j\land\mathit{Bit}(i,j)]\lor[j<i\land\mathit{Bit}(j,i)].}
$$
The relation is symmetric by its definition, and $E(i,i)$ is always false. Thus this <first-order structure> satisfies $F_1$ and $F_2$.

To verify an extension axiom, take distinct $x_1,\ldots,x_n$. Choose a <natural number> $k$ larger than every $x_i$ and put
$$
y=2^k+\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}2^{x_i}.
$$
The powers are distinct, so there are no carries in this <binary representation>. In particular $y>x_i$ for every $i$, and the <bit> in position $x_i$ is one exactly when $i<m$. Therefore $y$ is different from all the given <vertices>, is adjacent to $x_1,\ldots,x_{m-1}$, and is not adjacent to the others. If the $x_i$ are not distinct, the antecedent of the extension axiom is false, so it places no restriction. The case $n=0$, if included in the convention for <natural numbers>, simply asks for a nonempty domain, which this <first-order structure> has.

For example, prescribing adjacency to $1$ and $3$ and nonadjacency to $2$ permits $k=4$ and $y=16+2+8=26$, whose <binary representation> is $11010$. This explicitly realizes the required pattern. Hence
$$
\boxed{(\mathbb N,E)\models T_0.}
$$
Reordering a tuple makes the extension axioms applicable to any two disjoint finite sets of <vertices>, with any desired adjacency and nonadjacency pattern. This is the extension property defining the <theory of the random graph>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

It suffices to eliminate one <existential quantifier> from a <quantifier-free formula> $\theta(y,\bar x)$. Indeed, <logical connectives> preserve equivalence, and a <universal quantifier> can be rewritten using $\forall y\,\alpha\equiv\neg\exists y\,\neg\alpha$. An induction on the construction of a <first-order formula> then eliminates all its <quantifiers> from the inside out.

Convert $\theta$ to <disjunctive normal form>. Since <existential quantification> distributes over a finite <logical disjunction>, consider a single <logical conjunction> of atomic <first-order formulas> and their negations. There are no function or constant symbols in this <first-order signature>, so its atoms are equalities between variables and instances of $E$ between variables.

For a tuple $\bar x=(x_1,\ldots,x_r)$, enumerate its possible complete atomic diagrams $\delta(\bar x)$. Such a <quantifier-free formula> specifies which variables are equal and, between distinct equality classes, exactly which pairs are adjacent. Keep only the diagrams in which <first-order equality> is an <equivalence relation> and $E$ is symmetric and irreflexive. There are finitely many diagrams for each $r$, and every tuple in a <first-order model> of $T$ satisfies exactly one of them. Every retained diagram is realizable: insert one <vertex> for each equality class successively, using the extension axioms to prescribe its adjacency to earlier <vertices>.

Fix one diagram $\delta$. A potential <existential witness> for $y$ has one of two forms.

* **An existing <vertex>.** If $y=x_i$, substitute $x_i$ for $y$ in the <logical conjunction>. The diagram $\delta$ decides the <truth value> of every resulting atom, and so decides whether this choice works.
* **A new <vertex>.** Require $y\ne x_i$ for every $i$. Simplify $y=y$ to true and $E(y,y)$ to false. Use symmetry to rewrite every remaining edge requirement involving $y$ in the form $E(x_i,y)$ or $\neg E(x_i,y)$. An equality $y=x_i$, a positive loop requirement, or opposite edge requirements on the same equality class makes this case impossible. Otherwise the requirements give a consistent adjacency pattern on finitely many distinct <vertices>. Any equality classes without a specified edge requirement can be assigned either pattern. Reorder representatives with the desired neighbours first; the appropriate extension axiom supplies a fresh <vertex> with precisely this pattern.

All <first-order formulas> involving only $\bar x$ are already decided by $\delta$. Therefore, for each diagram, the existence of a suitable $y$ has a definite answer, determined by a finite syntactic test. Let $\mathcal D$ be the set of diagrams for which at least one disjunct and at least one of the two witness cases succeeds. Then
$$
\boxed{T\models\forall\bar x\left[
\exists y\,\theta(y,\bar x)\leftrightarrow
\bigvee_{\delta\in\mathcal D}\delta(\bar x)\right].}
$$
The right side is a <quantifier-free formula>. An empty <logical disjunction> means false, and for an empty tuple the empty atomic diagram means true. These logical truth constants also handle closed <first-order sentences> in this constant-free <first-order signature>.

For a concrete illustration of the fresh-witness case,
$$
T\models\forall x_1\forall x_2\forall x_3\left[
\exists y\,(E(y,x_1)\land\neg E(y,x_2)\land y\ne x_3)
\leftrightarrow x_1\ne x_2\right].
$$
If $x_1=x_2$, the edge requirements conflict. Otherwise the extension property supplies a <vertex> outside the finite set of parameters with the desired pattern; the extra avoidance of $x_3$ causes no obstruction. The general diagram construction handles all such equality coincidences systematically and proves <quantifier elimination>.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

By part (c), $T$ has a <first-order model>, so it is consistent. By part (d), every <first-order sentence> is equivalent modulo $T$ to a <quantifier-free sentence>. In the <first-order signature> consisting only of $E$ and <first-order equality>, there are no constant symbols, no ground <first-order terms>, and no nullary predicates. Thus a closed <quantifier-free formula> reduces to true or false, using the logical truth constants. All <first-order models> of $T$ therefore agree on every <first-order sentence>:
$$
\boxed{T\text{ is complete}.}
$$

The construction in part (d) is effective. Conversion to <disjunctive normal form>, enumeration of finite equality partitions and adjacency patterns, and testing the finitely many witness cases are all terminating operations. Apply them recursively to a given <first-order sentence> and evaluate the final truth constant. This decides whether the <first-order sentence> belongs to $T$, giving
$$
\boxed{T\text{ is decidable}.}
$$
No useful bound on the running time is needed for decidability; the potentially large enumeration still always terminates.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the <back-and-forth method>. Enumerate the two domains without repetitions as $a_0,a_1,\ldots$ and $b_0,b_1,\ldots$. We construct a sequence of finite maps, each a <partial isomorphism of structures>,
$$
f_0\subseteq f_1\subseteq f_2\subseteq\cdots,
$$
starting with the empty <function>. Each $f_s$ is a <bijection> between finite subsets of the domains and preserves both adjacency and nonadjacency on its domain.

At a **forth** step, choose the first $a_i$ outside the current domain. List the finitely many domain <vertices> as $u_1,\ldots,u_r$. In $\mathcal B$, prescribe that the new image $b$ be adjacent to $f_s(u_j)$ exactly when $a_i$ is adjacent to $u_j$ in $\mathcal A$. Since the image <vertices> are distinct, an extension axiom in $\mathcal B$ supplies such a $b$ outside the entire current range. Extend $f_s$ by $a_i\mapsto b$. This preserves injectivity, adjacency and nonadjacency, including the absence of loops.

At a **back** step, choose the first $b_i$ outside the current range. Apply the same argument to the inverse <partial isomorphism of structures> in $\mathcal A$, finding a fresh preimage with the required adjacency pattern to the current domain. Extend $f_s$ by this new pair. Alternate forth and back steps indefinitely.

Define
$$
\boxed{f=\bigcup_{s\ge0}f_s.}
$$
Because each finite <function> extends the preceding one, this is a well-defined injective <function>. The forth steps eventually include every $a_i$ in its domain, and the back steps eventually include every $b_i$ in its range, so $f$ is a <bijection>. Given any $u,v$, both lie in the domain of some finite $f_s$, where the edge equivalence already holds. It therefore also holds for $f$:
$$
\boxed{(u,v)\in E_{\mathcal A}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
(f(u),f(v))\in E_{\mathcal B}.}
$$
Thus the two <first-order structures> are isomorphic. Alternating the steps is essential for surjectivity: repeatedly extending only the domain would merely construct an embedding into $\mathcal B$.

=== a
{scope}

Use unary predicates $H$ and $M$ for happiness and merriness, a binary predicate $L$ with $L(x,y)$ meaning that $x$ loves $y$, and a constant symbol $\ell$ for Lucy. The domain consists of people. The following four <first-order sentences> use this same <first-order signature>.

==== A
{scope}
{c}

===== Solution

The <existential quantifier> supplies the person loved, with a choice that may depend on $x$:
$$
\boxed{\forall x\,[H(x)\lor\exists y\,L(x,y)].}
$$
The outer <universal quantifier> ranges over everyone. The <logical disjunction> is inclusive; it permits a person both to be happy and to love somebody.

==== B
{scope}
{c}

===== Solution

The property of loving at least one person is the antecedent:
$$
\boxed{\forall x\,[(\exists y\,L(x,y))\to L(x,\ell)].}
$$
An equivalent <first-order sentence>, useful for <Skolemization>, is $\forall x\forall y\,[L(x,y)\to L(x,\ell)]$. The person loved in the antecedent need not be Lucy; the conclusion concerns Lucy specifically.

==== C
{scope}
{c}

===== Solution

The same person $y$ must both love $x$ and be loved by $x$:
$$
\boxed{\forall x\,[(\exists y\,(L(y,x)\land L(x,y)))\to H(x)].}
$$
Equivalently, use $\forall x\forall y\,[(L(y,x)\land L(x,y))\to H(x)]$. There is no restriction that $x$ and $y$ be distinct; this matters when the later <ground resolution> proof considers Lucy loving herself.

==== D
{scope}
{c}

===== Solution

Lucy's happiness is a single antecedent, while the conclusion applies to everyone:
$$
\boxed{H(\ell)\to\forall x\,M(x).}
$$
Since $x$ does not occur in the antecedent, this is equivalent to $\forall x\,[H(\ell)\to M(x)]$. The <universal quantifier> does not assert that everyone is happy; it is the merriness conclusion that is universal.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

First convert the four <first-order sentences> to <clausal form>. In the first, pull the <existential quantifier> across the <logical disjunction>, then replace its witness by a fresh unary <Skolem function> $f(x)$. The usual nonempty-domain convention for <first-order logic> makes this transformation valid even when $H(x)$ already holds. The four <Skolem normal forms> are
$$
\begin{aligned}
A'&=\forall x\,[H(x)\lor L(x,f(x))],\\
B'&=\forall x\forall y\,[\neg L(x,y)\lor L(x,\ell)],\\
C'&=\forall x\forall y\,[\neg L(y,x)\lor\neg L(x,y)\lor H(x)],\\
D'&=\forall x\,[\neg H(\ell)\lor M(x)].
\end{aligned}
$$
The <Skolemization> of $A$ preserves satisfiability in an expanded <first-order signature>; it does not assert <logical equivalence> for every arbitrary interpretation of the new <Skolem function>. The other three <first-order sentences> only require elimination of implications and movement of <universal quantifiers>.

For a <ground resolution> refutation, negate the desired conclusion and introduce a fresh constant $c$ for a person who is not merry. This adds the <ground clause> $\neg M(c)$. Use these ground instances of the four universal <first-order sentences>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
K_1&=H(\ell)\lor L(\ell,f(\ell)),\\
K_2&=\neg L(\ell,f(\ell))\lor L(\ell,\ell),\\
K_3&=\neg L(\ell,\ell)\lor H(\ell),\\
K_4&=\neg H(\ell)\lor M(c),\\
K_5&=\neg M(c).
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $K_3$ comes from $C'$ with $x=y=\ell$, after removing a duplicate negative literal. All <first-order terms> in these <ground clauses> are ground. Resolving gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
K_6&=\operatorname{Res}_{L(\ell,f(\ell))}(K_1,K_2)
      =H(\ell)\lor L(\ell,\ell),\\
K_7&=\operatorname{Res}_{L(\ell,\ell)}(K_6,K_3)=H(\ell),\\
K_8&=\operatorname{Res}_{H(\ell)}(K_7,K_4)=M(c),\\
K_9&=\operatorname{Res}_{M(c)}(K_8,K_5)=\square.
\end{aligned}
$$
Repeated positive literals in $K_7$ have again been removed. The empty <ground clause> proves unsatisfiability of the premises together with the negated conclusion. Therefore
$$
\boxed{A,B,C,D\models\forall x\,M(x).}
$$
The key is to apply the first three premises to Lucy herself: either she is already happy, or she loves somebody, hence loves herself, which forces her happiness and activates the final premise.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For a <first-order signature> with at least one constant symbol, its <Herbrand universe> is the set of all <ground terms> built from its constant and function symbols. If necessary add a fresh constant to make this set nonempty. A <Herbrand structure> has exactly this domain; each constant denotes itself and each function symbol forms the corresponding syntactic <first-order term>. Its remaining choices are the <truth values> of the ground predicate atoms. For example, with constant $a$ and unary function $f$, the <Herbrand universe> is $\{a,f(a),f(f(a)),\ldots\}$.

One useful clausal form of <Herbrand's theorem> is the following. For a set $S$ of universally quantified, equality-free <first-order clauses>,
$$
\boxed{S\text{ is unsatisfiable}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{some finite set of ground instances of }S\text{ is propositionally unsatisfiable}.}
$$
Ground instances substitute <ground terms> for all variables. On the right, treat the ground predicate atoms as independent <Boolean variables>. Equivalently, $S$ is satisfiable if and only if it has a <Herbrand model>. A general <first-order sentence> can first be put into <Skolem normal form> and <clausal form>, with the new symbols included in the <Herbrand universe>.

An equivalent existential version says that a valid equality-free <first-order sentence> $\exists\bar x\,\theta(\bar x)$ with <quantifier-free formula> $\theta$ has finitely many ground tuples $\bar t_1,\ldots,\bar t_r$ such that $\bigvee_{i=1}^r\theta(\bar t_i)$ is propositionally valid, adding a constant if needed. This explains why a finite <ground resolution> refutation can establish a consequence involving infinitely many possible <ground terms>.

The equality-free qualification matters. If <first-order equality> is present, one must handle its axioms and use a term model modulo a suitable congruence, or use the corresponding theorem with equality. Literal syntactic term interpretation alone cannot model a consistent requirement such as $a=b$ for distinct constant symbols.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

**The assertion is false as printed, even for a satisfiable equality-free <Horn clause>.** The original paper allows an arbitrary <quantifier-free formula> $G^*$, so in particular permits negative atoms.<Logic and Proof examination paper of the University of Oxford (2015)>[Printed p. 4, Question 3(d), final sentence: the matrix is specified as quantifier-free, without a positivity restriction]{ref}

Take a <first-order signature> with a constant $a$ and unary predicates $P,Q$, and let
$$
F=\forall x\,[\neg P(x)\lor Q(x)],
\qquad G=\exists x\,\neg P(x).
$$
The matrix of $F$ is a <Horn clause>, and the <Herbrand universe> is the singleton $\{a\}$. The possible <Herbrand models> of $F$ assign $(P(a),Q(a))$ the values $(0,0)$, $(0,1)$, or $(1,1)$. Neither ground atom is true in every such <Herbrand model>, so the paper's intersection construction gives
$$
P^{\mathcal H_F}=Q^{\mathcal H_F}=\varnothing.
$$
Consequently $\mathcal H_F\models G$, with witness $a$. However, the one-element <first-order structure> in which both predicates hold everywhere satisfies $F$ and falsifies $G$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathcal H_F\models G\quad\text{but}\quad F\not\models G.}
$$
This directly refutes the requested implication; no proof of the stated equivalence is possible. It also isolates the issue: an atom being absent from the intersection means that it is false in *some* <Herbrand model>, not that it is false in every <first-order model>.

The standard correct version assumes that $F$ is satisfiable, uses an equality-free <first-order signature> with a nonempty <Herbrand universe>, and requires $G^*$ to be a positive <quantifier-free formula>: one built from atoms and truth constants using only <logical conjunction> and <logical disjunction>. Under those assumptions the expected argument is as follows.

First, the intersection of the ground atoms true in all <Herbrand models> is itself a <Herbrand model> of $F$. To see this, read each ground <Horn clause> as $A_1\land\cdots\land A_k\to B$, where $B$ is an atom or false. If every $A_i$ is true in the intersection, every $A_i$ is true in every <Herbrand model>. When $B$ is an atom, all these <Herbrand models> must make $B$ true, so the intersection does too. When $B$ is false, the antecedent cannot hold in any <Herbrand model>; since there is at least one such model, it cannot hold in the intersection either. Thus the intersection is the <least Herbrand model>.

Now let $\mathcal H_F\models G$ for positive $G^*$. There is a tuple of <ground terms> $\bar t$ with $\mathcal H_F\models G^*(\bar t)$. Expand the positive <quantifier-free formula> into <disjunctive normal form>; one of its <logical conjunctions> of positive atoms is true at $\bar t$. Each of those ground atoms $A$ holds in every <Herbrand model> of $F$. It also follows that $F\models A$: otherwise a <first-order model> of $F\land\neg A$ would, by <Herbrand's theorem> for universal equality-free <first-order clauses>, yield a <Herbrand model> of $F\land\neg A$, a contradiction. Every <first-order model> of $F$ therefore satisfies the same positive <logical conjunction> at the interpretations of $\bar t$, and hence satisfies $G$.

Conversely, if $F\models G$, the fact that the <least Herbrand model> is a <first-order model> of $F$ immediately gives $\mathcal H_F\models G$. Thus the repaired conclusion is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal H_F\models G\ \Longleftrightarrow\ F\models G
\quad\text{for satisfiable universal Horn }F\text{ and positive existential }G.}
$$
The satisfiability qualification is separately necessary when general <Horn clauses> may have no positive conclusion. For instance, $F=\forall x(P(x)\land\neg P(x))$ is a <logical conjunction> of <Horn clauses> with no models. The intersection convention then makes every ground atom true by vacuity, yet $F$ entails even $\exists x\neg P(x)$, which that <Herbrand structure> falsifies. The earlier satisfiable example already refutes the printed claim without relying on this further defect.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Use a <first-order signature> with a constant $a$ and unary predicates $P,Q$, and set
$$
F=\forall x\,[P(x)\lor Q(x)],
\qquad G=\exists x\,[P(x)\lor Q(x)].
$$
The displayed <first-order clause> has two positive atoms, so it is not a <Horn clause>. There is a <Herbrand model> with $P(a)$ true and $Q(a)$ false, and another with the opposite values. Thus neither atom holds in all <Herbrand models>; the intersection construction makes both false.

Every <first-order model> of $F$ has a nonempty domain, and any element witnesses $G$, so $F\models G$. Nevertheless $\mathcal H_F\not\models G$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{F\models G\quad\text{but}\quad\mathcal H_F\not\models G.}
$$
This example uses a positive <existential sentence>, so it also shows the necessity of the <Horn clause> restriction in the repaired result from part (d). Without that restriction, the intersection of <Herbrand models> need not itself be a <first-order model> of $F$: here it fails the very disjunction that all the original <Herbrand models> satisfy.
