= Computer aided formal verification
{scope}
{title2=A12072W1}

Solutions to the paper whose cover dates the examination to 6 June 2015, 2:30–4:00 pm. Source: https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/computer_science_past_exam_paper/response/956037/attach/3/Lawrence%20Attachment.zip?cookie_passthrough=1[released examination archive], member `2015-cafv.pdf`. All three questions are covered, although candidates were asked to attempt at most two. In the <binary decision diagrams>, dashed arrows mean zero and solid arrows mean one. In the <linear temporal logic> calculations, $F=\Diamond$, $G=\square$ and $X=\bigcirc$.

==== Solution

Follow the <binary decision diagram> from its top <decision node> to a <terminal node>, choosing the outgoing arrow specified by each tested <Boolean variable>. If $x=0$, the left $y$ <decision node> returns $y$, independently of $z$. If $x=1$, the right $y$ <decision node> returns one when $y=1$ and otherwise returns $z$. === b
{scope}

==== Solution

Splitting the <Boolean function> according to the first tested <Boolean variable> gives
$$
f=(\neg x\land y)\lor\bigl(x\land((\neg y\land z)\lor y)\bigr).
$$
The identity $y\lor(\neg y\land z)=y\lor z$ follows by considering $y=0$ and $y=1$. Using it and distributing <Boolean conjunction> over <Boolean disjunction>,
$$
f=(\neg x\land y)\lor(x\land y)\lor(x\land z)
 =\boxed{y\lor(x\land z)}.
$$
The first two terms combine to $y$ because $x\lor\neg x=1$. This also explains the <truth table>: $y=1$ always makes the output one, while with $y=0$ the output is one exactly when both $x$ and $z$ are one.

The eight <terminal nodes>, read from left to right in the figure, are $\boxed{0,0,1,1,0,1,1,1}$. The repeated tests are intentional: this is the full <binary decision tree> before any reduction.

For example, the branch $z=1,y=0,x=1$ ends at one, whereas $z=0,y=0,x=1$ ends at zero. This checks the dependence on $z$ that must survive the later reduction.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

A <reduced ordered binary decision diagram> is obtained by sharing equal <terminal nodes>, removing a <decision node> whose two children are equal, and sharing <decision nodes> with the same tested <Boolean variable> and the same ordered pair of children. Apply these rules to the <binary decision tree> in part (c) as follows.

* Merge the three zero <terminal nodes> into one zero <terminal node>, and merge the five one <terminal nodes> into one one <terminal node>.
* At $(z,y)=(0,0)$ the $x$ <decision node> has children $(0,0)$. Remove it and redirect its incoming arrow to zero.
* At $(z,y)=(0,1)$ and $(z,y)=(1,1)$ the two $x$ <decision nodes> each have children $(1,1)$. Remove both and redirect their incoming arrows to one.
* The remaining $x$ <decision node>, at $(z,y)=(1,0)$, has children $(0,1)$, so retain it.
* The $y$ <decision node> under $z=0$ now has children $(0,1)$, while the one under $z=1$ has children $(x,1)$. These are different, so neither can be merged or removed. The top $z$ <decision node> also has different children.

The left panel below is therefore the resulting <reduced ordered binary decision diagram>. Its full description is

|| <Decision node>
|| Test
|| Zero child
|| One child

| $r$
| $z$
| $u$
| $v$

| $u$
| $y$
| 0
| 1

| $v$
| $y$
| $w$
| 1

| $w$
| $x$
| 0
| 1

There are $\boxed{4+2=6}$ <vertices>: four <decision nodes> and two <terminal nodes>.

\Image[formal-verification-2015-decision-diagrams.png]
{title=Reduced ordered decision diagrams: reversing the given order preserves its size, while testing y first saves one decision node}

The original <binary decision diagram> also has four <decision nodes> and two <terminal nodes>, and is already reduced for $x<y<z$: the two $y$ <decision nodes> have different children and no <decision node> has two equal children. Thus **reversing the order has not made this example smaller**. Indeed $y\lor(x\land z)$ is unchanged by interchanging $x$ and $z$, and the new <binary decision diagram> is the original one with those two labels exchanged. The uniqueness of a <reduced ordered binary decision diagram> applies to a fixed <variable ordering of a binary decision diagram>; it does not assert that every ordering has the same size.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Choose the <variable ordering of a binary decision diagram> $y<x<z$. The <Boolean cofactor> with $y=1$ is the constant one, while the <Boolean cofactor> with $y=0$ is $x\land z$. Consequently the right panel in part (d) has the following arrows:
$$
\boxed{y:\ (0\to x,\ 1\to1),\qquad
x:\ (0\to0,\ 1\to z),\qquad
z:\ (0\to0,\ 1\to1).}
$$
Here an entry such as $0\to x$ denotes the zero arrow to the $x$ <decision node>. There are $\boxed{3+2=5}$ <vertices>: three <decision nodes> and two <terminal nodes>, saving one <decision node> relative to either of the previous orderings. Testing $y$ first is effective because its one branch settles the entire <Boolean function> immediately. The ordering $y<z<x$ works equally well.

This is also minimal among ordinary <binary decision diagrams> for this <Boolean function>. Each <Boolean variable> is essential: setting $y=0,z=1$ leaves $f=x$; setting $y=0,x=1$ leaves $f=z$; and setting $x=z=0$ leaves $f=y$. If a <Boolean variable> were never tested, the represented <Boolean function> could not depend on it. Thus at least three <decision nodes> are necessary, and the construction attains that bound.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

A <transition relation> requires an explicit interpretation of the <Boolean variables> as current and next <state> bits. Take the four <states> $q_{00},q_{01},q_{10},q_{11}$. Encode the current <state> by $(u,x)$ and the next <state> by $(y,z)$, and define
$$
\boxed{q_{ux}\to q_{yz}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad R(u,x,y,z)=y\lor(x\land z)=1.}
$$
The extra current bit $u$ is deliberately unused by the <Boolean formula>. A four-<state> <transition system> normally needs four bits to describe a pair of <states>; a <Boolean formula> using only three of them simply describes a <transition relation> independent of the fourth bit.

When $x=0$, the condition is $y=1$, so the permitted next <states> are $q_{10}$ and $q_{11}$. When $x=1$, the condition is $y\lor z$, so the permitted next <states> are $q_{01},q_{10},q_{11}$. The resulting <adjacency matrix>, with rows and columns both ordered $q_{00},q_{01},q_{10},q_{11}$, is
$$
A_R=\begin{pmatrix}
0&0&1&1\\
0&1&1&1\\
0&0&1&1\\
0&1&1&1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The <directed graph> below plots all ten <directed edges>, including the three <self-loops>. No arrow enters $q_{00}$, but it is a valid <state> with two outgoing arrows. Every <state> has a successor. As permitted, no initial <states> are distinguished.

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the two incoming initial arrows in the source figure: $I=\{s_0,s_3\}$. An <atomic proposition> is true precisely at the <states> whose displayed labels contain it. In particular, $c$ is false at every <state>. Hence $GF\neg c$ is true on every infinite <path in a transition system>, and the first implication in the <fairness assumption> imposes no restriction.

Write $p=a\land b$. The remaining implication is $FGp\to GF\neg b$. By the <linear temporal logic> duality $\neg FGp=GF\neg p$, it is equivalent to
$$
GF\neg p\lor GF\neg b=GF\neg p,
$$
since $\neg b$ implies $\neg p$. Thus **a path is fair exactly when it visits states not labelled with both $a$ and $b$ infinitely often**. This condition also follows directly: if $p$ eventually holds forever, then $b$ eventually holds forever and the required $GF\neg b$ is impossible; if $p$ does not eventually hold forever, the implication has a false antecedent.

Every initial <path in a transition system> in the upper component either alternates forever between $s_0$ and $s_1$, or makes finitely many alternations and then stays at $s_2$. These are all fair: the first visits $s_1$ infinitely often, and the second visits $s_2$ infinitely often; neither <state> satisfies $a\land b$.

In the lower component, a <path in a transition system> can stay at $s_3$ forever, or leave it after finitely many visits and pass through $s_4$ to $s_5$. The constant <path in a transition system> $s_3^\omega$ is unfair because $a\land b$ holds forever while $\neg b$ never holds. Every departing <path in a transition system> is fair because $s_5$ satisfies neither $a$ nor $b$. Here $v^\omega$ means infinite repetition of the finite word $v$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{FairPaths}(TS)={}&\{(s_0s_1)^\omega\}\\
&\cup\{(s_0s_1)^n s_2^\omega:n\geq1\}\\
&\cup\{s_3^m s_4s_5^\omega:m\geq1\}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
This notation lists initial <paths in a transition system>. If one instead allows arbitrary starting <states>, their suffixes starting at $s_1,s_2,s_4,s_5$ are fair as well; the fair <paths in a transition system> from each initial <state>, and hence all verdicts below, are unchanged.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Denote the three families found in part (a) by $A=(s_0s_1)^\omega$, $B_n=(s_0s_1)^n s_2^\omega$ and $C_m=s_3^m s_4s_5^\omega$, where $n,m\geq1$. All are fair <paths in a transition system>. The verdicts are
$$
\boxed{(TS\models_{\mathrm{fair}}\varphi_i)_{i=1}^6
=(\text{false},\text{false},\text{false},\text{false},\text{true},\text{false}).}
$$
Each verdict follows from the infinite <path in a transition system> semantics, rather than from a finite prefix alone.

For $\varphi_1=FGa$, take $C_1=s_3s_4s_5^\omega$. From $s_4$ onwards, $a$ is always false, so there is no time after which $a$ is always true. Therefore **the first specification fails**, with fair <counterexample> $C_1$. The <path in a transition system> $A$ is another fair <counterexample>, because it encounters $s_1$, where $a$ is false, infinitely often. Each $B_n$ does satisfy $FGa$, since $s_2$ satisfies $a$ forever.

For $\varphi_2=(X\neg a)\to FGa$, take $A$. Its next <state> is $s_1$, so $X\neg a$ is true, but $FGa$ is false as just shown. Thus **the second specification fails**, with fair <counterexample> $A$. Alternatively, $C_1$ has next <state> $s_4$, making its antecedent true and its consequent false. For $C_m$ with $m\geq2$, the next <state> is still $s_3$, so this implication is true there because its antecedent is false; this does not rescue a universally quantified specification.

For $\varphi_3=Ga$, the fair <path in a transition system> $C_1$ reaches $s_4$, where $a$ is false. Thus **the third specification fails**, with fair <counterexample> $C_1$. In fact every fair initial <path in a transition system> reaches $s_1$ or $s_4$, so none satisfies $Ga$.

For $\varphi_4=b\mathbin{\mathrm U}G\neg b$, <strong until> requires an eventual position satisfying $G\neg b$, together with $b$ at every earlier position. On $A$, $b$ is true forever, so $G\neg b$ never holds. Thus **the fourth specification fails**, with fair <counterexample> $A$. On each $B_n$, $b$ holds until entry into $s_2$, after which $\neg b$ holds forever. On each $C_m$, $b$ holds until entry into $s_5$, after which $\neg b$ holds forever. These latter <paths in a transition system> satisfy the <strong until> requirement.

For $\varphi_5=b\mathbin{\mathrm W}G\neg b$, <weak until> permits either the preceding <strong until> behavior or $Gb$. Every $B_n$ and $C_m$ satisfies the <strong until> behavior, and $A$ satisfies $Gb$. These exhaust the fair initial <paths in a transition system>. Therefore **the fifth specification holds on every fair initial path**. Allowing the left operand to continue forever is exactly what distinguishes this answer from the previous one.

For $\varphi_6=(XXb)\mathbin{\mathrm U}G\neg b$, the two <next-time operators> belong to the left operand of <strong until>. The fair <path in a transition system> $C_1$ has $b$ true at positions zero and one, then false at every position from two onwards. Therefore $G\neg b$ first holds at position two. But $XXb$ is false already at position zero, since $b$ is false at position two. A witness for <strong until> cannot occur at zero or one, where its right operand is false, or at two or later, where its left operand has already failed. Thus **the sixth specification fails**, with fair <counterexample> $C_1$.

Indeed, every fair initial <path in a transition system> fails the sixth specification. On $A$, the right operand never holds. On $B_n$ and $C_m$, let $j\geq2$ be the first position of the permanent $\neg b$ tail. The left operand $XXb$ is false at $j-2$, strictly before any possible right-operand witness. This also checks that the two <next-time operators> cannot be ignored or shifted onto the whole <strong until> formula.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

To apply <bounded model checking>, seek an initial <path in a transition system> satisfying
$$
\mathrm{fair}\land\neg\varphi_1
\equiv GF\neg(a\land b)\land GF\neg a.
$$
A <satisfying assignment> for its bounded encoding is enough to disprove the specification. Use a <lasso path>: positions $q_0,\ldots,q_k$ follow the <transition relation>, and a final arrow returns from $q_k$ to some $q_\ell$, $0\leq\ell\leq k$. The repeated part is $q_\ell\cdots q_k$. On such a <lasso path>, $GF r$ is equivalent to having $r$ true at some position of the repeated part.

For this example an explicit <Boolean formula> for the bounded search is
$$
\begin{aligned}
E_k={}&I(q_0)\land\bigwedge_{i=0}^{k-1}T(q_i,q_{i+1})\\
&\land\bigvee_{\ell=0}^{k}\left(
T(q_k,q_\ell)
\land\bigvee_{i=\ell}^{k}\neg(a(q_i)\land b(q_i))
\land\bigvee_{i=\ell}^{k}\neg a(q_i)
\right).
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $I(q)$ means $q\in\{s_0,s_3\}$ and $T(q,r)$ means that the source diagram has the arrow $q\to r$; each can be encoded by finite <Boolean variables> for the <states>. The loop requirements are the <fairness assumption> and the negated specification respectively. Unrolling the <transition relation> and adding a loop closure is the usual <bounded model checking> construction.<Symbolic Model Checking without BDDs by Armin Biere and others>[§2, worked example with an unrolled transition relation and a returning loop; §3, infinite-path semantics]{ref}

At bound $k=0$, an initial <self-loop> would be needed. The only such choice is $q_0=s_3$, whose repeated label satisfies $a\land b$ and $a$. It violates both loop requirements. The other initial <state>, $s_0$, has no <self-loop>. Thus $E_0$ has no <satisfying assignment>.

At bound $k=1$, choose
$$
\boxed{q_0=s_0,\qquad q_1=s_1,\qquad\ell=0.}
$$
Both required arrows $s_0\to s_1$ and $s_1\to s_0$ exist. At $q_1$, $a$ is false and hence $a\land b$ is false, so both repeated-part requirements are true. Equivalently, the label values are $a(q_0)=b(q_0)=b(q_1)=1$ and $a(q_1)=c(q_0)=c(q_1)=0$. These values give a <satisfying assignment> of $E_1$, representing the fair <counterexample> $(s_0s_1)^\omega$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{TS\not\models_{\mathrm{fair}}FGa.}
$$
Here the bound counts forward arrows before the returning arrow, so $k=1$ stores two positions and describes a two-<state> repeated part. There is no need for a completeness bound: the exhibited fair <counterexample> settles the negative answer. These <satisfying assignments> are checked directly; no claim of executing an external <SAT solver> is needed.

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $A=\operatorname{Sat}(\Phi)\subseteq S$. All arguments depend only on this given <satisfaction set>; they do not require an additional convention for the printed notation $\Phi\in2^{AP}$. For a <state> $s$, let $\operatorname{Post}(s)=\{t:s\to t\}$. The <power set> $2^S$ is ordered by <set inclusion>.

Take $X\subseteq Y\subseteq S$ and any $s\in H(X)$. By the definition of $H$, we have both $s\in A$ and $\operatorname{Post}(s)\subseteq X$. By transitivity of <set inclusion>, $\operatorname{Post}(s)\subseteq Y$, so $s\in H(Y)$. Since this applies to every member of $H(X)$,
$$
\boxed{X\subseteq Y\ \Longrightarrow\ H(X)\subseteq H(Y).}
$$
Thus $H$ is an <order-preserving function>, which is the meaning of monotonicity here. This proof does not need either finiteness or the absence of <terminal states>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $G_\Phi=\operatorname{Sat}(\forall\square\Phi)$. By <computation tree logic> semantics, a <state> belongs to $G_\Phi$ exactly when every infinite <path in a transition system> starting there stays in $A=\operatorname{Sat}(\Phi)$ at every position, including position zero. There are no <terminal states>, so every <state> lies at the start of an infinite <path in a transition system>.

If $s\in G_\Phi$, then $s\in A$. For any successor $t$ of $s$, every infinite <path in a transition system> from $t$ can be prefixed with $s$. Hence every such continuation stays in $A$, so $t\in G_\Phi$. Thus $\operatorname{Post}(s)\subseteq G_\Phi$ and $s\in H(G_\Phi)$.

Conversely, if $s\in H(G_\Phi)$, then $s\in A$ and every successor belongs to $G_\Phi$. Every infinite <path in a transition system> from $s$ therefore has its first <state> in $A$ and its entire remaining tail in $A$. Consequently $s\in G_\Phi$. Both inclusions give
$$
\boxed{H(G_\Phi)=G_\Phi,}
$$
so the desired <satisfaction set> is a <fixed point> of $H$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Every <state> has at least one successor, so $\operatorname{Post}(s)\nsubseteq\varnothing$ for every $s\in S$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{H(\varnothing)=A\cap\varnothing=\varnothing.}
$$
The <empty set> is always a <fixed point>. Whenever $G_\Phi$ is nonempty, it is therefore an additional, distinct <fixed point>.

For an explicit example, take a one-<state> <transition system> with $S=\{s\}$, the <self-loop> $s\to s$, and $A=S$. Then $G_\Phi=S$ and $H$ has the two distinct <fixed points> $\varnothing$ and $\{s\}$. This example meets the requirement of no <terminal states>.

The wording does not guarantee distinct additional <fixed points> for every instance. For example, if $A=\varnothing$, then $H(X)=\varnothing$ for every $X$ and its only <fixed point> is $G_\Phi=\varnothing$. Thus **additional fixed points can occur, but need not occur**. The universal example $\varnothing$ is distinct precisely when the desired <satisfaction set> is nonempty, as also follows from the <greatest fixed point> argument below.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $X$ be any <fixed point> of $H$. Then
$$
X=H(X)=A\cap\{s:\operatorname{Post}(s)\subseteq X\},
$$
so $X\subseteq A$ and every successor of every member of $X$ belongs to $X$. Take $s_0\in X$ and an arbitrary infinite <path in a transition system> $s_0s_1s_2\cdots$ from $s_0$. By <mathematical induction>, every $s_i$ belongs to $X$: the base case is the choice of $s_0$, and $s_i\in X$ implies $s_{i+1}\in\operatorname{Post}(s_i)\subseteq X$.

Since $X\subseteq A$, the whole <path in a transition system> stays in $A$. The <path in a transition system> was arbitrary, so $s_0\models\forall\square\Phi$. Thus $s_0\in G_\Phi$, and since $s_0$ was arbitrary in $X$,
$$
X\subseteq G_\Phi.
$$
Part (b) proves that $G_\Phi$ itself is a <fixed point>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Sat}(\forall\square\Phi)=\nu X.\,H(X),}
$$
the <greatest fixed point> with respect to <set inclusion>. In fact the same induction works for every $X\subseteq H(X)$, showing why a successor-closed <set> of good <states> certifies this universal property.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Start with every <state>, and repeatedly discard a <state> that is outside $A=\operatorname{Sat}(\Phi)$ or has any successor outside the current candidate <set>. Use a fresh <set> on each iteration so that the displayed procedure computes exactly $H(X)$:
``
function Sat_AG(S, Post, A):
    X = copy(S)
    repeat:
        Y = empty_set()
        for s in S:
            if s in A and every t in Post(s) belongs to X:
                insert s into Y
        if Y == X:
            return X
        X = Y
``
This is <fixed-point iteration> $X_0=S$, $X_{i+1}=H(X_i)$. The first step gives $X_1=A$, because every successor is a <state> of $S$. Thus $X_1\subseteq X_0$. If $X_i\subseteq X_{i-1}$, the <order-preserving function> property gives
$$
X_{i+1}=H(X_i)\subseteq H(X_{i-1})=X_i.
$$
The candidate <sets> therefore form a descending chain under <set inclusion>.

Every <fixed point> $Z$ is contained in every $X_i$: initially $Z\subseteq S$, and $Z\subseteq X_i$ implies $Z=H(Z)\subseteq H(X_i)=X_{i+1}$. At return, $X=H(X)$, and $X$ contains every <fixed point>. The returned <set> is consequently the <greatest fixed point>, so $\boxed{X=\operatorname{Sat}(\forall\square\Phi)}$ by part (d).

The iteration also has a direct interpretation. $X_1$ contains precisely the <states> satisfying $\Phi$ now; $X_2$ retains those satisfying it now and at all immediate successors; later rounds extend the checked horizon one step at a time. The stable <set> enforces the condition at every future position.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

**Yes: the procedure always terminates after finitely many steps.** If an iteration does not return, then $X_{i+1}\subsetneq X_i$. Thus at least one <state> is permanently removed. A finite <set> of $|S|$ <states> can suffer at most $|S|$ such strict decreases, after which the next equality test must return. Therefore the procedure makes at most
$$
\boxed{|S|+1\text{ evaluations of }H}
$$
when started from $X_0=S$, counting the final evaluation that detects equality. The bound also covers $S=\varnothing$, where the first evaluation already returns. Each evaluation inspects finitely many <states> and successors, so every individual step terminates as well. Finiteness, rather than monotonicity alone, supplies this finite termination bound.
