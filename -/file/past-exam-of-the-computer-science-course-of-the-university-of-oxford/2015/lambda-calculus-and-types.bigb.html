= Lambda calculus and types
{scope}
{title2=A12077W1}

Solutions to all twelve numbered subparts of the <University of Oxford> <computer science> Finals paper held on 4 June 2015. Question 2(d) has a wording issue: its literal impossibility claim is false, and the solution gives the exact condition for a valid <typing judgment>.<Lambda Calculus and Types examination paper of the University of Oxford (2015)>[Cover, p. 1; Questions 1–2, p. 2; Question 3, p. 3]{ref}

== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Choose all auxiliary bound variables fresh for the <free variables> of $p,q,r$; <alpha equivalence> permits this. The operation $\bullet$ is <composition of lambda terms>, so expand its definition and use <beta reduction>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
(p\bullet q)\bullet r
&\equiv\lambda x.(\lambda y.p(qy))(rx)
\longrightarrow_\beta\lambda x.p(q(rx)),\\
p\bullet(q\bullet r)
&\equiv\lambda x.p((\lambda y.q(ry))x)
\longrightarrow_\beta\lambda x.p(q(rx)).
\end{aligned}
$$
The two <lambda terms> have the same reduct, up to <alpha equivalence>, hence they are related by <beta equivalence>:
$$
\boxed{(p\bullet q)\bullet r=_\beta p\bullet(q\bullet r).}
$$
No <eta conversion> is needed for this associativity calculation.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $I\equiv\lambda u.u$ for the <I combinator>, and choose $x$ outside the <free variables> of $p$. Both orders of <composition of lambda terms> first give the same <lambda abstraction> by <beta reduction>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
p\bullet I&\equiv\lambda x.p((\lambda u.u)x)
\longrightarrow_\beta\lambda x.px,\\
I\bullet p&\equiv\lambda x.(\lambda u.u)(px)
\longrightarrow_\beta\lambda x.px.
\end{aligned}
$$
The freshness condition is exactly the side condition for <eta conversion>, which contracts $\lambda x.px$ to $p$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{p\bullet I=_{\beta\eta}p=_{\beta\eta}I\bullet p.}
$$
Here <eta conversion> matters: a <free variable> $p$ is already in <beta-normal form>, as is $\lambda x.px$, and <beta equivalence> alone does not equate them.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The argument order in $p$ is a <cyclic permutation>. Undo it by rotating in the other direction. For $n\geq1$, choose the <lambda term>
$$
\boxed{q\equiv\lambda z y_1\cdots y_n.\,z\,y_n\,y_1\cdots y_{n-1}.}
$$
Use fresh bound variables throughout. Substituting $qz$ for the first argument of $p$ and then performing <beta reduction> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
(p\bullet q)z
&\longrightarrow_\beta^*p(qz)\\
&\longrightarrow_\beta^*\lambda x_1\cdots x_n.\,(qz)x_2\cdots x_nx_1\\
&\longrightarrow_\beta^*\lambda x_1\cdots x_n.\,zx_1\cdots x_n.
\end{aligned}
$$
Indeed, the arguments received by $qz$ are $y_1=x_2,\ldots,y_{n-1}=x_n,y_n=x_1$, so its last argument moves back to the front. Apply <eta conversion> successively, starting with the innermost <lambda abstraction>. At each step the bound variable being removed is absent from the preceding expression. Thus the last expression is related to $z$ by <beta-eta equivalence>, and consequently
$$
\boxed{p\bullet q=_{\beta\eta}\lambda z.z=I.}
$$
For the induction needed in the next part, also compute the reverse <composition of lambda terms>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
(q\bullet p)z
&\longrightarrow_\beta^*\lambda y_1\cdots y_n.\,(pz)y_ny_1\cdots y_{n-1}\\
&\longrightarrow_\beta^*\lambda y_1\cdots y_n.\,zy_1\cdots y_n
=_\eta z.
\end{aligned}
$$
So this $q$ is a two-sided inverse. The empty argument list at $n=0$ means $p=\lambda z.z$; choose $q=I$. For $n=1$, both displayed rotations give $\lambda zy_1.zy_1$, which is also related to $I$ by <eta conversion>. These boundary cases require no exceptional inverse construction.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Use <structural induction> on the construction of $p\in\mathcal R$, with the stronger induction claim that $p$ is an <invertible lambda term>: there is a <lambda term> $q$ satisfying both
$$
p\bullet q=_{\beta\eta}I,
\qquad q\bullet p=_{\beta\eta}I.
$$
The base constructor has exactly the two-sided inverse proved in part (c). Strengthening the claim is useful because the inductive construction needs inverses of its constituent <lambda terms> in both orders.

For the inductive constructor, let $t_i$ be the two-sided inverse supplied for $s_i$. Thus, for a fresh variable $u$, <beta-eta equivalence> gives
$$
s_i(t_i u)=_{\beta\eta}u,
\qquad t_i(s_i u)=_{\beta\eta}u
\qquad(1\leq i\leq n).
$$
For $n\geq1$, define
$$
\boxed{q\equiv\lambda z y_1\cdots y_n.\,
z(t_1y_n)(t_2y_1)\cdots(t_ny_{n-1}).}
$$
This <lambda term> reverses the <cyclic permutation> and applies the appropriate inverse to each argument. First use <beta reduction> to expand one order of <composition of lambda terms>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
(p\bullet q)z
&\longrightarrow_\beta^*\lambda x_1\cdots x_n.\,
(qz)(s_2x_2)\cdots(s_nx_n)(s_1x_1)\\
&\longrightarrow_\beta^*\lambda x_1\cdots x_n.\,
z(t_1(s_1x_1))(t_2(s_2x_2))\cdots(t_n(s_nx_n))\\
&=_{\beta\eta}\lambda x_1\cdots x_n.\,zx_1\cdots x_n
=_\eta z.
\end{aligned}
$$
For the other order, <beta reduction> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
(q\bullet p)z
&\longrightarrow_\beta^*\lambda y_1\cdots y_n.\,
(pz)(t_1y_n)(t_2y_1)\cdots(t_ny_{n-1})\\
&\longrightarrow_\beta^*\lambda y_1\cdots y_n.\,
z(s_2(t_2y_1))\cdots(s_n(t_ny_{n-1}))(s_1(t_1y_n))\\
&=_{\beta\eta}\lambda y_1\cdots y_n.\,zy_1\cdots y_n
=_\eta z.
\end{aligned}
$$
These computations close the stronger induction. For $n=1$, each formula has the single argument involving $s_1$ and $t_1$; for $n=0$, the constructor is $I$ and its inverse is $I$. Every construction is finite, so the inverse is a finite <lambda term>. There is no requirement that this inverse itself belong to $\mathcal R$.
$$
\boxed{\forall p\in\mathcal R\;\exists q:\quad
p\bullet q=_{\beta\eta}I=q\bullet p.}
$$

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <universal typing axiom> supplies $\varnothing\mapsto q:\star$ for every <lambda term> $q$. Use this as the argument premise of the <lambda application> rule. The empty <typing context> is consistent with $\Gamma$, and its <union> with $\Gamma$ is $\Gamma$:
$$
\frac{\Gamma\mapsto p:\star\Rightarrow A
\qquad \varnothing\mapsto q:\star}
{\Gamma\cup\varnothing\mapsto pq:A}.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\Gamma\mapsto pq:A\quad\text{for every }q.}
$$
The <free variables> of $q$ need not appear in the resulting <typing context>: the <universal typing axiom> deliberately assigns $\star$ without recording them.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Choose $\star$ as the domain of the new <function type>. Since $x\notin\operatorname{Subjects}(\Gamma)$, the assumption $x:\star$ is consistent with $\Gamma$ and deleting $x$ leaves $\Gamma$ unchanged. The <lambda abstraction> rule gives
$$
\frac{\Gamma\mapsto p:A}
{\Gamma\mapsto\lambda x.p:\star\Rightarrow A}.
$$
Use the <universal typing axiom> for $q$, then the <lambda application> rule:
$$
\frac{\Gamma\mapsto\lambda x.p:\star\Rightarrow A
\qquad\varnothing\mapsto q:\star}
{\Gamma\mapsto(\lambda x.p)q:A}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Gamma\mapsto(\lambda x.p)q:A\quad\text{for every }q.}
$$
The condition is about the <typing context>, not the <free variables> of $p$. Some occurrences of $x$ in $p$ might have been hidden by a use of the <universal typing axiom>; the derivation above still follows the printed rules.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Begin with the variable <typing judgment> $\{x:A\}\mapsto x:A$. Because $z$ is absent from this <typing context>, the <lambda abstraction> rule permits $z$ to have type $\star$:
$$
\frac{\{x:A\}\mapsto x:A}
{\{x:A\}\mapsto\lambda z.x:\star\Rightarrow A}.
$$
Assign the entire <lambda application> $yx$ type $\star$ using the <universal typing axiom>, without attempting to assign a <function type> to $y$. Then
$$
\frac{\{x:A\}\mapsto\lambda z.x:\star\Rightarrow A
\qquad\varnothing\mapsto yx:\star}
{\{x:A\}\mapsto(\lambda z.x)(yx):A}.
$$
Although $y$ is a <free variable> of the body, it is absent from its recorded <typing context>. Consequently the <lambda abstraction> rule allows any <simple type> $B$ for $y$. Abstract over $y$ and then $x$:
$$
\frac{\displaystyle
\frac{\{x:A\}\mapsto(\lambda z.x)(yx):A}
{\{x:A\}\mapsto\lambda y.(\lambda z.x)(yx):B\Rightarrow A}}
{\varnothing\mapsto\lambda xy.(\lambda z.x)(yx):A\Rightarrow(B\Rightarrow A)}.
$$
The side condition for $y$ holds because $y$ is absent; the side condition for $x$ holds because its recorded <simple type> is $A$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\varnothing\mapsto\lambda xy.(\lambda z.x)(yx):A\Rightarrow(B\Rightarrow A)
\quad\text{for all }A,B\text{ in }\mathrm{TA}_\lambda^\star.}
$$

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The exact restriction in the ordinary <simply typed lambda calculus> is
$$
\boxed{\varnothing\mapsto\lambda xy.(\lambda z.x)(yx):A\Rightarrow(B\Rightarrow A)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
B=A\Rightarrow C\text{ for some simple type }C.}
$$
Thus the unrestricted choice of $A,B$ available in part (c) fails. A reading of the printed sentence as asserting impossibility for every choice of $A,B$ is false. This issue occurs in the original PDF as well as its transcription.<Lambda Calculus and Types examination paper of the University of Oxford (2015)>[p. 2, Question 2(d)]{ref}

To prove necessity, first observe why the ordinary rules cannot hide assumptions. <Structural induction> on a derivation proves
$$
\operatorname{Subjects}(\Gamma)=FV(t)
\quad\text{whenever }\Gamma\mapsto t:T.
$$
For a variable, both sides are the singleton containing that variable. For <lambda application>, both sides are the <union> of the two premise supports. For <lambda abstraction>, both sides delete the bound variable. There is no <universal typing axiom> to interrupt this equality.

Now invert the proposed derivation. Each outer <lambda abstraction> must have been introduced by the abstraction rule, since the other ordinary rules conclude a variable or a <lambda application>. The body must therefore have the <typing judgment>
$$
\{x:A,y:B\}\mapsto(\lambda z.x)(yx):A.
$$
The support equality just proved ensures that both displayed assumptions occur; consistency fixes their <simple types> to those specified by the outer <function type>. Inverting the <lambda application> gives a <simple type> $C$ for its argument and the following premises:
$$
\{x:A\}\mapsto\lambda z.x:C\Rightarrow A,
\qquad
\{x:A,y:B\}\mapsto yx:C.
$$
There is no flexibility in the supports: $\lambda z.x$ has the single <free variable> $x$, whereas $yx$ has both <free variables> $y,x$. Invert the second <lambda application>. Its two premises must be
$$
\{y:B\}\mapsto y:D\Rightarrow C,
\qquad
\{x:A\}\mapsto x:D
$$
for some <simple type> $D$. The variable rule is the only possible final rule for each premise. It forces $D=A$ and $B=D\Rightarrow C$, yielding $B=A\Rightarrow C$.

Conversely, suppose $B=A\Rightarrow C$. The ordinary rules give
$$
\frac{\{y:A\Rightarrow C\}\mapsto y:A\Rightarrow C
\qquad\{x:A\}\mapsto x:A}
{\{x:A,y:A\Rightarrow C\}\mapsto yx:C},
\qquad
\frac{\{x:A\}\mapsto x:A}
{\{x:A\}\mapsto\lambda z.x:C\Rightarrow A}.
$$
The <typing contexts> are consistent, so another <lambda application> gives the body type $A$. Two <lambda abstractions> discharge $y$ and $x$, establishing
$$
\varnothing\mapsto\lambda xy.(\lambda z.x)(yx):
A\Rightarrow((A\Rightarrow C)\Rightarrow A).
$$
This proves sufficiency and supplies a family of counterexamples to the literal impossibility claim. For a concrete failure of the unrestricted claim, choose $A=\alpha$ and $B=\beta$ as atomic <type variables>. The <type variable> $\beta$ is not a <function type> $\alpha\Rightarrow C$, so no derivation exists for that fixed pair. Here the atomic <type variables> are fixed type expressions, rather than unknowns that one may change during <type inference>.

The reason for the restriction is the otherwise discarded <lambda application> $yx$: the ordinary rules still type-check it. Its subsequent disappearance under <beta reduction> does not license reversing that <beta reduction> while preserving every <simple type> of the result. The <beta-normal form> $\lambda xy.x$ admits arbitrary $A\Rightarrow(B\Rightarrow A)$, whereas the unreduced <lambda term> has the additional constraint just derived.
$$
\boxed{\text{The all-}A,B\text{ claim fails; the valid instances have }B=A\Rightarrow C.}
$$

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Take an infinite supply of variables and the two distinguished constants of <combinatory logic>, the <K combinator> and the <S combinator>. The <combinatory logic terms> are exactly the finite expressions generated by
$$
\boxed{T::=x\mid\mathbf K\mid\mathbf S\mid(TT).}
$$
Thus every variable and each distinguished constant is a <combinatory logic term>, and the application of two <combinatory logic terms> is a <combinatory logic term>. Nothing else is a <combinatory logic term>. Application associates to the left, so $ABC$ means $(AB)C$; it is not assumed to be associative. The <I combinator> can be used as the abbreviation $\mathbf I\equiv\mathbf{SKK}$, without adding a new primitive constant.

The basic equality is the least relation containing the two axiom schemes
$$
\boxed{\mathbf KAB=A,\qquad\mathbf SABC=AC(BC)}
$$
for all <combinatory logic terms> $A,B,C$, and closed under the rules of <equational reasoning>:
$$
\frac{}{A=A},\qquad
\frac{A=B}{B=A},\qquad
\frac{A=B\quad B=C}{A=C},
\qquad
\frac{A=B\quad C=D}{AC=BD}.
$$
The final rule permits replacing equal <combinatory logic terms> inside either side of an application; repeated use permits replacement at any depth. Uniform <substitution> of <combinatory logic terms> for variables preserves equality, either included as a rule or proved by induction on these derivations, since the displayed axioms are schemes. No rule equating two <combinatory logic terms> merely because they agree after an extra argument belongs to this basic theory. Its equality is the symmetric, reflexive and transitive closure of <weak combinatory reduction>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $[x]A$ for the paper's <bracket abstraction> and $I\equiv\mathbf{SKK}$ for the <I combinator>. Prove the result by <structural induction> following the three clauses of <bracket abstraction>.

For $A\equiv x$, the rules for the <S combinator> and the <K combinator> give
$$
([x]x)B\equiv\mathbf{SKK}B
=\mathbf KB(\mathbf KB)=B
\equiv x[B/x].
$$
If $x$ does not occur in $A$, then <substitution> for $x$ leaves $A$ unchanged and
$$
([x]A)B\equiv\mathbf KAB=A\equiv A[B/x].
$$
These two cases cover all variables and constants as well as applications not containing $x$.

In the remaining case, $A\equiv CD$ and $x$ occurs in at least one of $C,D$. The <bracket abstraction> clause, the rule for the <S combinator>, the induction hypotheses and compatibility of equality with application give
$$
\begin{aligned}
([x](CD))B
&\equiv\mathbf S([x]C)([x]D)B\\
&=(([x]C)B)(([x]D)B)\\
&=C[B/x]D[B/x]\\
&\equiv(CD)[B/x].
\end{aligned}
$$
The last line is the defining application clause of <substitution>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{([x]A)B=A[B/x]\quad\text{for all }A,B.}
$$
The conclusion is equality in <combinatory logic>; it need not be literal identity of strings. There are no binders in a <combinatory logic term>, so this <substitution> introduces no <variable capture>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Again write $[x]A$ for <bracket abstraction>. In <combinatory logic>, all variable occurrences are <free variables>. Under $x\neq y$ and $x\notin FV(B)$, <substitution> for $y$ preserves the presence or absence of $x$:
$$
x\in FV(C[B/y])\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x\in FV(C).
$$
An existing $x$ is not replaced because $x\neq y$; replacing $y$ cannot introduce an $x$ because $B$ contains none. This ensures that the same clause of <bracket abstraction> applies before and after <substitution>.

Use <structural induction> on $A$, respecting those clauses. If $A\equiv x$, then
$$
([x]x)[B/y]\equiv\mathbf{SKK}
\equiv[x]x
\equiv[x](x[B/y]),
$$
because <substitution> leaves the <S combinator>, the <K combinator> and $x$ unchanged.

If $x$ is absent from $A$, it is also absent from $A[B/y]$. Therefore
$$
([x]A)[B/y]
\equiv(\mathbf KA)[B/y]
\equiv\mathbf K(A[B/y])
\equiv[x](A[B/y]).
$$
This includes $A\equiv y$: even when $B$ is a large application, the absence of $x$ means <bracket abstraction> still produces $\mathbf KB$.

Finally, let $A\equiv CD$ with $x$ occurring in $C$ or $D$. By the occurrence observation, $x$ also occurs in $C[B/y]$ or $D[B/y]$, so the application clause applies on both sides. Apply the induction hypotheses:
$$
\begin{aligned}
([x](CD))[B/y]
&\equiv\bigl(\mathbf S([x]C)([x]D)\bigr)[B/y]\\
&\equiv\mathbf S\bigl(([x]C)[B/y]\bigr)\bigl(([x]D)[B/y]\bigr)\\
&\equiv\mathbf S\bigl([x](C[B/y])\bigr)\bigl([x](D[B/y])\bigr)\\
&\equiv[x]\bigl(C[B/y]D[B/y]\bigr)\\
&\equiv[x]\bigl((CD)[B/y]\bigr).
\end{aligned}
$$
Every step uses the definitions of <substitution> and <bracket abstraction>, without applying the equality axioms for the <S combinator> or the <K combinator>. Thus the result is the stronger syntactic identity
$$
\boxed{([x]A)[B/y]\equiv[x](A[B/y]).}
$$
The side condition on $B$ is essential for this identity. For example, substituting $x$ for $y$ in $[x]y\equiv\mathbf Ky$ gives $\mathbf Kx$, whereas $[x](y[x/y])\equiv[x]x\equiv\mathbf{SKK}$, a different string.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Successive <bracket abstractions> are processed from the inside out: $[x][y]((\mathbf Kx)y)$. Retain $I\equiv(\mathbf S\mathbf K)\mathbf K$ only as a temporary abbreviation for the <I combinator>. Since $y$ is absent from $\mathbf Kx$, the first <bracket abstraction> is
$$
[y]((\mathbf Kx)y)
\equiv\mathbf S\bigl(\mathbf K(\mathbf Kx)\bigr)I.
$$
For the outer <bracket abstraction>, $x$ is absent from $I$ and the <S combinator>, while it occurs in $\mathbf K(\mathbf Kx)$. Hence
$$
\begin{aligned}
[x]\bigl(\mathbf S(\mathbf K(\mathbf Kx))I\bigr)
&\equiv\mathbf S\bigl([x](\mathbf S(\mathbf K(\mathbf Kx)))\bigr)(\mathbf KI),\\
[x](\mathbf S(\mathbf K(\mathbf Kx)))
&\equiv\mathbf S(\mathbf K\mathbf S)\bigl([x](\mathbf K(\mathbf Kx))\bigr),\\
[x](\mathbf K(\mathbf Kx))
&\equiv\mathbf S(\mathbf K\mathbf K)\bigl([x](\mathbf Kx)\bigr),\\
[x](\mathbf Kx)
&\equiv\mathbf S(\mathbf K\mathbf K)I.
\end{aligned}
$$
Substituting upward and then expanding $I$ gives the following fully bracketed string, consisting solely of the <S combinator>, the <K combinator> and parentheses:
$$
\boxed{
\left(
\left(\mathbf S
\left(
\left(\mathbf S(\mathbf K\mathbf S)\right)
\left(
\left(\mathbf S(\mathbf K\mathbf K)\right)
\left(\left(\mathbf S(\mathbf K\mathbf K)\right)\left((\mathbf S\mathbf K)\mathbf K\right)\right)
\right)
\right)
\right)
\left(\mathbf K\left((\mathbf S\mathbf K)\mathbf K\right)\right)
\right).
}
$$
For readability, the same result with the usual left association convention and the temporary abbreviation is
$$
\mathbf S\bigl(\mathbf S(\mathbf K\mathbf S)
(\mathbf S(\mathbf K\mathbf K)(\mathbf S(\mathbf K\mathbf K)I))\bigr)(\mathbf KI).
$$
The syntactic result comes from the specified <bracket abstraction> algorithm. Replacing it by the shorter <K combinator>, despite agreement after two arguments, would not answer the requested computation. As a check using the result of part (b), applying the computed <combinatory logic term> to $u,v$ gives $\mathbf Kuv=u$.
