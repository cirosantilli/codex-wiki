== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use nonnegative amounts on the original <directed edges> of the <flow network>. A <feasible flow> is a <function> $f:E\to\mathbb R_{\geq0}$ satisfying the <capacity constraints> $f(u,v)\leq c(u,v)$ and <flow conservation> at every internal <vertex>:
$$
\sum_{u:(u,v)\in E}f(u,v)=\sum_{w:(v,w)\in E}f(v,w)\qquad(v\notin\{s,t\}).
$$
Its <flow value> is net outflow at the source,
$$
\boxed{|f|=\sum_{v:(s,v)\in E}f(s,v)-\sum_{u:(u,s)\in E}f(u,s).}
$$
Summing <flow conservation> shows that this is also net inflow at the sink. Subtracting incoming <flow> matters when the source has incoming <edges>.

An $s$–$t$ <cut of a flow network> is a <partition of a set> $(S,V\setminus S)$ with $s\in S$ and $t\notin S$. Its <cut capacity> is **the total outward <flow network edge capacity>**,
$$
c(S,V\setminus S)=\sum_{(u,v)\in E:\,u\in S,\,v\notin S}c(u,v).
$$
Incoming <edges> do not contribute to this <sum>. Equivalently, one may use the antisymmetric net-<flow> convention, but the nonnegative arc convention above will also be used in the subsequent reductions.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Sum net outflow over all <vertices> in the source side $S$. An internal <directed edge> occurs once positively and once negatively, so it cancels. All internal <vertices> have zero net outflow by <flow conservation>, and the sink is outside $S$. Therefore
$$
|f|=\sum_{\substack{(u,v)\in E\\u\in S,\,v\notin S}}f(u,v)-\sum_{\substack{(u,v)\in E\\u\notin S,\,v\in S}}f(u,v).
$$
The second <sum> is nonnegative, while every term in the first satisfies its <capacity constraint>. Consequently,
$$
\boxed{|f|\leq\sum_{\substack{(u,v)\in E\\u\in S,\,v\notin S}}c(u,v)=c(S,V\setminus S).}
$$
This is direct accounting from <flow conservation>; it does not invoke the <max-flow min-cut theorem>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

A <path-cycle matching> is exactly an <edge> <subset> whose resulting <subgraph> has maximum <degree> at most two: every nontrivial <connected component of a graph> of this form is a <graph path> or a <cycle in a graph>. Thus the task is a <bipartite b-matching> problem with bound two at every <vertex>.

Find a <bipartition> $L,R$, orient every original <edge> from $L$ to $R$, and add a source $s$ and sink $t$. Give each arc $s\to u$, for $u\in L$, <flow network edge capacity> two; each original arc $u\to v$ <flow network edge capacity> one; and each arc $v\to t$, for $v\in R$, <flow network edge capacity> two. Compute an integral <maximum flow> and select the original <edges> whose <flow> is one. The <integral max-flow theorem> guarantees such an integral optimum.

By <flow conservation>, at most two selected <edges> touch each <vertex>, so they decompose into the required <vertex-disjoint paths> and <cycles in a graph>. Conversely, any permissible <path-cycle matching> gives a <feasible flow>: send one unit through its middle arc for each selected <edge>, with the outside arc amounts equal to the endpoint <degrees>. In both directions, **the <flow value> equals the number of selected <edges>**. Maximizing either quantity therefore maximizes the other.

\Image[algorithms-path-cycle-flow.png]
{title=An original <path-cycle matching> example and its integral <maximum flow> representation}

The blue <edges> in this example form a four-<cycle in a graph> and a one-<edge> <graph path>; the dashed <edge> is unused. Their five units of <flow> are optimal: the two left <vertices> $a,b$ can each contribute at most two selected <edges>, and $c$ has only one incident <edge>.

The constructed <flow network> has $|V|+2$ <vertices> and $|E|+|V|$ arcs. A <polynomial-time algorithm> for <maximum flow>, such as the <Edmonds–Karp algorithm>, followed by a traversal of the selected <subgraph>, gives an efficient solution.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Separate the forced <integer> portion from the choice of rounding direction. Put $p_{ij}=\lfloor a_{ij}\rfloor$ and $q_{ij}=a_{ij}-p_{ij}$. Define residual demands
$$
r_i=\sum_jq_{ij}=\sum_ja_{ij}-\sum_jp_{ij},\qquad c_j=\sum_iq_{ij}=\sum_ia_{ij}-\sum_ip_{ij}.
$$
These are <nonnegative integers>, since the original row and column <sums> are <integers>. Also $\sum_ir_i=\sum_jc_j=:T$. Each <fractional part> satisfies $0\leq q_{ij}<1$.

Construct a <flow network> with a <vertex> for each row and column, source-to-row arcs of <flow network edge capacity> $r_i$, row-to-column arcs of <flow network edge capacity> one exactly where $q_{ij}>0$, and column-to-sink arcs of <flow network edge capacity> $c_j$. The amounts $q_{ij}$ themselves define a fractional <feasible flow> of <flow value> $T$. No <flow> can exceed $T$, the total source <flow network edge capacity>. Because all constructed <flow network edge capacities> are <integers>, an integral <maximum flow> also has <flow value> $T$ and saturates every source and sink arc.

Let $h_{ij}$ be its middle-arc <flow>, taking $h_{ij}=0$ when that arc is absent. Then $h_{ij}\in\{0,1\}$, and the required answer is
$$
\boxed{b_{ij}=\lfloor a_{ij}\rfloor+h_{ij}.}
$$
At an already integral entry no middle arc exists, so that entry stays fixed. At a nonintegral entry the two possibilities are precisely its <floor> and <ceiling>. <Flow conservation> gives $\sum_jh_{ij}=r_i$ and $\sum_ih_{ij}=c_j$, preserving every original row and column <sum>.

This is <matrix rounding with fixed margins>. There are $m+n+2$ <vertices> and at most $mn+m+n$ arcs. Exact <rational arithmetic> computes the <floors> and residual demands in <polynomial time> in the encoded input length; the resulting <flow network edge capacities> are small <integers>, so there is no need to scale by a potentially large common denominator.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

First give $w$ the vote of every voter whose allowed <set> contains $w$. Let their number be $K$. This loses no possible strict victory: starting with any winning assignment, changing such a ballot to $w$ increases $w$'s score and weakly decreases an opponent's score. Thus a winning assignment exists exactly when one exists with these $K$ ballots fixed.

Every remaining voter must now be assigned to an allowed opponent, and **each opponent may receive at most $K-1$ votes**. If any allowed <set> is empty, there is no valid election assignment. With at least two candidates, $K=0$ immediately makes a unique victory impossible. With only the candidate $w$, every nonempty ballot <set> forces that candidate, so the answer is yes under the question's strict-comparison definition.

For the remaining case $K\geq1$, make a <flow network> with a <vertex> for each remaining voter and each opponent. Add source-to-voter arcs of <flow network edge capacity> one, voter-to-opponent arcs of <flow network edge capacity> one for the allowed choices, and opponent-to-sink arcs of <flow network edge capacity> $K-1$. Compute an integral <maximum flow>. Then
$$
\boxed{w\text{ can be the unique winner}\iff |f_{\max}|=n-K.}
$$
A <flow> saturating all voter arcs assigns each remaining voter to exactly one allowed opponent, by integrality and <flow conservation>. The sink-arc <capacity constraints> give every opponent strictly fewer than $K$ votes. Conversely, any winning assignment with the fixed $K$ votes defines just such a saturating <feasible flow>. The <flow network> has at most $n+m+2$ <vertices> and $O(n+m+\sum_i|C_i|)$ arcs, hence the decision takes <polynomial time>.

=== a

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Take $u,v\in P$ and $0\leq\lambda\leq1$. Their <convex combination> satisfies
$$
A\bigl(\lambda u+(1-\lambda)v\bigr)=\lambda Au+(1-\lambda)Av\leq\lambda b+(1-\lambda)b=b.
$$
Thus every such <convex combination> remains in $P$, so **$P$ is a <convex set>**. The assertion also holds when $P$ is empty.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

An <extreme point> $p\in P$ cannot be written as a nontrivial <convex combination> of two distinct <points> of $P$: if $p=\lambda u+(1-\lambda)v$ with $u,v\in P$ and $0<\lambda<1$, then $u=v=p$.

A <vertex of a linear polyhedron> is a <feasible point> at which the <active constraints> include $n$ <linearly independent> row normals. Equivalently, some $n$ independent bounding <hyperplanes> intersect uniquely at that <point>. This definition does not require $P$ to be bounded or full-dimensional. For a <linear polyhedron>, it is also equivalent to the <point> uniquely maximizing a suitable <linear function>: sum the selected active inequalities to construct one such <linear function>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $p$ be a <vertex of a linear polyhedron> and choose $n$ independent <active constraints>, written $Bp=d$, with $B$ invertible. Suppose $p=\lambda u+(1-\lambda)v$, where $u,v\in P$ and $0<\lambda<1$. Each selected inequality has $B_iu\leq d_i$ and $B_iv\leq d_i$, while
$$
d_i=B_ip=\lambda B_iu+(1-\lambda)B_iv.
$$
Since both weights are positive, equality in this weighted upper bound forces $B_iu=B_iv=d_i$ for every selected row. Hence $Bu=Bv=d$, and invertibility gives $u=v=p$. Therefore **every <vertex of a linear polyhedron> is an <extreme point>**.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

**No.** For the <linear polyhedron> $P=[0,1]^2$, take $c=(1,0)^T$ and $x^*=(1,1/2)^T$. The maximum of $c^Tx$ is one, attained at $x^*$. Yet
$$
x^*=\tfrac12(1,0)^T+\tfrac12(1,1)^T,
$$
so $x^*$ is not an <extreme point>, hence not a <vertex of a linear polyhedron>. An <optimal face> can contain many <points>; an arbitrary maximizer need not be one of its <vertices of a linear polyhedron>.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Only $z,w$ have printed nonnegativity restrictions; $x,y$ are unrestricted. In four-dimensional space, a <vertex of a linear polyhedron> needs four independent <active constraints>. There are exactly four available constraints, so all must be active. Solving them gives
$$
z=w=0,\qquad y=3,\qquad 2x-y=1,
$$
and therefore the only possible <vertex of a linear polyhedron> is **$(x,y,z,w)=(2,3,0,0)$**. It is feasible, and its four active normals are independent, so it is indeed a <vertex of a linear polyhedron>.

One can also see uniqueness geometrically from the <slack variables> $s_1=1-2x+y-5w$ and $s_2=3-y-z-w$. The <feasible region> is in affine bijection with the <nonnegative orthant> in $(s_1,s_2,z,w)$:
$$
y=3-s_2-z-w,\qquad x=2-\tfrac12s_1-\tfrac12s_2-\tfrac12z-3w.
$$
That <orthant> has only the origin as an <extreme point>, giving the same unique <vertex of a linear polyhedron>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the <standard maximum form of a linear program> convention $\max c^Tu$ subject to $Mu\leq b$ and $u\geq0$. Split the unrestricted variables as $x=x^+-x^-$ and $y=y^+-y^-$, with all four new variables nonnegative. The resulting <linear program> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\max\quad&2x^+-2x^-+3y^+-3y^-+4z+7w\\
\text{subject to}\quad&2x^+-2x^--y^++y^-+5w\leq1,\\
&y^+-y^-+z+w\leq3,\\
&x^+,x^-,y^+,y^-,z,w\geq0.
\end{aligned}
$$
If equality-constraint standard form is preferred, add a nonnegative <slack variable> to each inequality.

Give the two inequalities nonnegative <dual variables> $\alpha,\beta$. The <dual linear program> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\min\quad&\alpha+3\beta\\
\text{subject to}\quad&2\alpha\geq2,\qquad -2\alpha\geq-2,\\
&-\alpha+\beta\geq3,\qquad \alpha-\beta\geq-3,\\
&\beta\geq4,\qquad5\alpha+\beta\geq7,\\
&\alpha,\beta\geq0.
\end{aligned}
$$
The paired inequalities encode equalities for the two original unrestricted variables. Equivalently, the <dual linear program> has $2\alpha=2$, $-\alpha+\beta=3$, $\beta\geq4$, $5\alpha+\beta\geq7$ and $\alpha,\beta\geq0$. Thus **its only <feasible point> is $(\alpha,\beta)=(1,4)$, of <objective function> value $13$**.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <point> $(1,4)$ satisfies <dual feasibility> and supplies an upper bound of $13$ by <weak duality>. Directly, take one copy of the first constraint and four copies of the second:
$$
2x+3y+4z+9w\leq13.
$$
Since $w\geq0$, the original <objective function> is at most $13-2w\leq13$. The <feasible point> $(2,3,0,0)$ attains $13$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{OPT}=13,\qquad (x,y,z,w)=(2,3,0,0)\text{ is optimal}.}
$$
There are also nonvertex optima. In terms of the <slack variables>, the exact <objective function> identity is
$$
2x+3y+4z+7w=13-s_1-4s_2-2w.
$$
Equality requires $s_1=s_2=w=0$, leaving the entire <optimal face>
$$
(x,y,z,w)=(2-t/2,\,3-t,\,t,\,0),\qquad t\geq0.
$$
Negative $x$ or $y$ on this ray are allowed. This illustrates why the single <vertex of a linear polyhedron> does not imply a unique optimum.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

First suppose the stated nonnegative <vectors> $y,z$ exist. If $x\in P\cap Q$, multiply $Ax\leq b$ by $y^T$ and $Cx\leq d$ by $z^T$. Since $x\geq0$ and $y^TA+z^TC\geq0$, this gives the contradiction
$$
0\leq(y^TA+z^TC)x\leq y^Tb+z^Td<0.
$$
Thus the certificate implies $P\cap Q=\varnothing$.

For the converse, put
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}A\\C\end{pmatrix},\qquad h=\begin{pmatrix}b\\d\end{pmatrix}.
$$
A common <feasible point> exists exactly when $Mx\leq h$, $x\geq0$. Introducing a nonnegative <vector> of <slack variables> $s$ makes this equivalent to
$$
\begin{pmatrix}M&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\s\end{pmatrix}=h,\qquad x,s\geq0.
$$
If this system is infeasible, the nonnegative-equations version of <Farkas' lemma> supplies an unrestricted <vector> $u$ such that
$$
M^Tu\geq0,\qquad I^Tu=u\geq0,\qquad h^Tu<0.
$$
Split $u=(y,z)^T$ according to the original two blocks of inequalities. The three conditions become **$y,z\geq0$, $y^TA+z^TC\geq0$ and $y^Tb+z^Td<0$**, exactly the desired certificate. Both directions are proved. The role of $x\geq0$ is essential: it is why the combined normal need only be nonnegative rather than zero.<Convex Optimization by Stephen Boyd and Lieven Vandenberghe>[§5.8.3, printed pp. 261–262, linear-inequality alternatives and Farkas' lemma]{ref}

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $B=A^T$ and choose any column <probability vector> $q$, for example the first coordinate <unit vector>. Because $A$ has nonnegative entries and each row sums to one, $B$ maps the <probability simplex>
$$
\Delta=\{v\in\mathbb R^n:v\geq0,\ \mathbf1^Tv=1\}
$$
into itself: $Bv\geq0$ and $\mathbf1^TBv=(A\mathbf1)^Tv=\mathbf1^Tv=1$.

The iterates $B^jq$ need not converge, so take their arithmetic averages,
$$
x_N=\frac1N\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}B^jq\in\Delta.
$$
A telescoping <sum> gives
$$
Bx_N-x_N=\frac{B^Nq-q}{N}.
$$
Both <vectors> on the right before subtraction have <L1 norm> one, so $\|Bx_N-x_N\|_1\leq2/N\to0$.

The <probability simplex> is closed and bounded in finite-dimensional <Euclidean space>, hence compact by the <Heine-Borel theorem>. It follows that a <subsequence> $x_{N_j}$ converges to some $x\in\Delta$. Passing to the <limit> in the displayed identity, using <continuity> of the <linear map> $B$, gives **$A^Tx=Bx=x$, with $x\geq0$ and $\sum_i x_i=1$**.

This proves <stationary distribution existence for a finite Markov chain> without any <Markov chain irreducibility> or <aperiodicity> assumption. Those stronger hypotheses concern uniqueness and convergence, not the existence required here.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A parameterized <decision problem> is <fixed-parameter tractable> if there is an <algorithm> deciding inputs of encoded length $N$ and parameter $k$ in <running time> at most **$f(k)N^c$**, where $f$ is a computable <function> and $c$ is a constant independent of $k$. Arbitrarily expensive dependence on the parameter is permitted; putting $k$ in the exponent of $N$ does not by itself establish <fixed-parameter tractability>.

For a minimization problem with nonnegative costs, a factor-two <approximation algorithm> runs in <polynomial time>, always returns a <feasible solution>, and satisfies **$\operatorname{cost}(\operatorname{ALG})\leq2\operatorname{OPT}$** on every feasible input. A <feasible solution> also satisfies $\operatorname{OPT}\leq\operatorname{cost}(\operatorname{ALG})$. If the optimum is zero, the guarantee requires an answer of zero cost.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <vertex cover decision problem> takes a finite <simple graph> $G$ and a <nonnegative integer> $k$, and asks whether there is a <vertex cover> of <cardinality> at most $k$. The optimization problem asks for a <minimum vertex cover>, with cost equal to its <cardinality>.

For the decision problem, if there are no <edges>, accept; if there is an <edge> but $k=0$, reject. Otherwise choose an <edge> $uv$ and branch: either include $u$ and recurse on $G-u$ with budget $k-1$, or include $v$ and recurse on $G-v$ with budget $k-1$. Accept if either branch accepts. Every <vertex cover> contains at least one endpoint of $uv$, so every possible solution is retained by at least one branch. Conversely, adding the selected endpoint to a <vertex cover> returned by the smaller instance covers all original <edges>. This proves correctness by <induction> on the remaining budget.

The <search tree> has depth at most $k$ and at most $2^k$ leaves. Processing each node in $O(|V|+|E|)$ time gives **$O(2^k(|V|+|E|))$ <running time>**, establishing <fixed-parameter tractability>.

For optimization, greedily build a <maximal matching> $M$: repeatedly select an <edge> and discard every <edge> touching either endpoint. Return the <set> $C$ of all endpoints of $M$. Any <edge> with neither endpoint in $C$ could be added to $M$, contradicting maximality; thus $C$ is a <vertex cover>. Since the <edges> of a <matching in a graph> have pairwise <disjoint> endpoint <sets>, every <vertex cover> must contain at least one different <vertex> for each of them. Therefore
$$
\boxed{|C|=2|M|\leq2\tau(G)=2\operatorname{OPT}.}
$$
The greedy construction takes <polynomial time>, and can be implemented in $O(|V|+|E|)$ with <adjacency lists>. It needs only a <maximal matching>, not a <maximum matching>.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

If the basis has $r\leq k$ members, it has $2^r$ <subsets>, each specifying a possible <union>. Different <subsets> can produce the same <union>, so the number of distinct generated <sets> is at most $2^r$. Consequently,
$$
\boxed{|\mathcal F|\leq2^k.}
$$
Here $\mathcal F$ is a <set family> of distinct <sets>. If a representation lists duplicate input <sets>, discard those duplicates before applying this bound. The empty <union> is included.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $U'=U\setminus\{y\}$ and $\mathcal F'=\{B\setminus\{y\}:B\in\mathcal F\}$. If $\mathcal A$ is a basis for the original instance, replace each member $A$ by $A\setminus\{y\}$. Deletion commutes with <unions>, so the resulting family generates every member of $\mathcal F'$ and has at most $|\mathcal A|$ members.

Conversely, take a basis $\mathcal A'$ for the reduced instance. Lift each of its members by
$$
L(A')=\begin{cases}A'\cup\{y\},&x\in A',\\A',&x\notin A'.\end{cases}
$$
If $B\setminus\{y\}$ is represented as a <union> of reduced basis <sets>, the same <union> of their lifts agrees with $B$ on $U'$. It contains $y$ exactly when one of the selected reduced basis <sets> contains $x$, namely exactly when $x\in B$. By the assumed <incidence equivalence in a set family>, this occurs exactly when $y\in B$. Thus the lifted <union> is precisely $B$, and the lifted basis has no more members.

Therefore **deleting $y$ preserves the yes/no answer with the same parameter $k$**. The reverse construction does not require a reduced basis <set> to be an input <set>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

First discard duplicate input <sets>, and let $m=|\mathcal F|$. If $m>2^k$, reject by part (i); this rejection can be encoded as the fixed no-instance $U=\{u\}$, $\mathcal F=\{\{u\}\}$, $k=0$ of the <set basis problem>. Otherwise, represent each ground element $x$ by its incidence column
$$
\sigma(x)=\bigl(\mathbf1_{x\in B}\bigr)_{B\in\mathcal F}\in\{0,1\}^m.
$$
Keep one element from each class of equal columns. By repeatedly applying part (ii), this preserves the answer. The reduced number of elements $q$ satisfies
$$
\boxed{m\leq2^k,\qquad q\leq2^m\leq2^{2^k}.}
$$
Relabel these representatives and store their reduced incidence <matrix>. Its $mq$ membership bits, the dimensions and $k$ have encoded length bounded solely by a computable <function> of $k$; the leading incidence bound is $2^{k+2^k}$. Thus this is a <kernelization>, even though it is not a polynomial kernel. Forming columns and grouping identical ones takes <polynomial time> in the original explicit input length. To test $m>2^k$, compare bit lengths or truncate exponentiation at $m+1$; never construct an enormous $2^k$ merely to perform this preprocessing comparison.

For completeness, the bounded instance can be solved explicitly. Enumerate all ordered lists of $k$ <subsets> of the $q$-element ground <set>, allowing empty members to pad shorter bases. There are at most $2^{kq}$ such lists. For a candidate basis and each reduced input <set> $B$, form the <union> of every candidate basis <set> contained in $B$. If this <union> equals $B$, a representation exists; if it does not, no <union> of that candidate's members can represent $B$, because any member not contained in $B$ would add a forbidden element. Testing every $B$ therefore verifies a candidate in time polynomial in $m,q,k$.

The preprocessing is polynomial in the original input length, and the remaining search depends only on $k$. Hence **the <set basis problem> is <fixed-parameter tractable> with respect to $k$**. Empty input families and $k=0$ are covered by the same empty-<union> convention.

=== d
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Assume the standard nonempty instance convention $1\leq k\leq n$, and set $d(v,v)=0$. Start with no centers. Pick any city still farther than $2r^*$ from every selected center, add it as a center, and mark every city within distance $2r^*$ of it as covered. Stop when all cities are covered.

Distinct chosen centers have pairwise distance greater than $2r^*$. Fix an optimal <set> of $k$ centers and assign each city to one optimal center within distance $r^*$. Two cities assigned to the same center have distance at most $2r^*$ by the <triangle inequality>. Thus no two chosen centers can be assigned to that same optimal center. The <pigeonhole principle> shows that **the greedy procedure selects at most $k$ centers and has radius at most $2r^*$**. If fewer than $k$ are selected, add arbitrary distinct cities; this cannot worsen the radius.

With the supplied radius, a straightforward implementation updates coverage in $O(n)$ time per selected center, hence takes $O(nk)$ distance tests. The argument also handles $r^*=0$. It is the packing bound underlying the <metric k-center problem>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The optimum radius is a distance from some city to a selected city, so it belongs to
$$
D=\{0\}\cup\{d(u,v):u,v\in V,\ u\ne v\}.
$$
Sort the distinct values of $D$, try them in increasing order, and run part (i)'s greedy procedure with each trial value $r$. Declare a trial successful when all cities are covered using at most $k$ centers; stop a failed trial as soon as a $(k+1)$st center would be selected. The trial $r=r^*$ must succeed by part (i), so the first successful value $\widehat r$ satisfies $\widehat r\leq r^*$. Its output has
$$
\boxed{R\leq2\widehat r\leq2r^*.}
$$
There are $O(n^2)$ candidate distances and $O(n^2)$ work per trial, giving a <polynomial-time algorithm> with <running time> $O(n^4)$, plus sorting. Success at $r$ need not mean that radius $r$ itself is feasible; only the radius-$2r$ guarantee is used.

A faster alternative is the <farthest-first traversal>. Choose any first center, then repeatedly choose a city farthest from its nearest current center until $k$ have been chosen. Let $R$ be the resulting radius. If $R>0$, adjoin a city attaining that distance. At every earlier selection, the maximum distance to the then-current center <set> was at least $R$, so all pairs among these $k+1$ cities are at distance at least $R$. Two belong to the same optimal radius-$r^*$ <closed ball>, giving $R\leq2r^*$ by the <triangle inequality>. If $R=0$, the guarantee is immediate. Maintaining nearest-center distances gives **$O(nk)$ distance evaluations** without knowing $r^*$.
