= Computational game theory
{scope}
{title2=A15684W1}

Source paper: https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/ba_computer_science_past_papers/response/2577745/attach/2/202402%20183%20CS%20Papers.zip?cookie_passthrough=1[released Oxford examination archive], member `2018/T/a15684w1.pdf`. The actual cover identifies the Second Public Examination, Trinity Term 2019, Monday 3 June. Its printed time is **9:30 am–12:30 am**, including the apparent end-time typo; this is not silently changed to noon. All three questions are solved. Part 2(c) includes the necessary qualification of its literal premise.

== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The players are the two chains, each with the <set> of <pure strategies> $\{1,2,3,4,5\}$. A <strategy profile> $(x,y)$ gives the sites of $S$ and $C$. For later use, write the first player's <payoff> for a general street size as
$$
u(x,y)=\begin{cases}x+y-1,&x<y,\\k,&x=y,\\2k-x-y+1,&x>y.\end{cases}
$$
When $x<y$, count customers to the left of $(x+y)/2$, assigning value one rather than two to any midpoint customer. This gives $x+y-1$ whether the midpoint is an occupied building or lies between buildings. The other player receives $2k-u(x,y)$. At co-location, every customer's value is split, giving $k$ each.

For $k=5$, the <payoff matrix> for $S$ is
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}
5&2&3&4&5\\
8&5&4&5&6\\
7&6&5&6&7\\
6&5&4&5&8\\
5&4&3&2&5
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Rows and columns are indexed by sites $1,\ldots,5$; the full ordered <payoff> pair in cell $(x,y)$ is **$(M_{xy},10-M_{xy})$**. This <discrete location game> is a <constant-sum game>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

**Neither player has a <weakly dominant strategy> among its <pure strategies>.** Against an opponent at site one, the unique <best response> is site two, yielding $2k-2$. Co-location yields only $k$, and moving further right reduces one's <payoff>. Against an opponent at site $k$, the unique <best response> is $k-1$, by reflection. For $k\geq4$, these two sites differ. No single <pure strategy> can therefore be a <best response> to both opposing actions, as a <weakly dominant strategy> would require.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

**Yes to both questions, for both players.** Site one is <strictly dominated> by site two. Against opposing site $y$, the <payoff> difference is
$$
u(2,y)-u(1,y)=\begin{cases}k-2,&y=1\text{ or }2,\\1,&3\leq y\leq k.\end{cases}
$$
Every difference is positive for $k\geq4$. By reflection, site $k$ is <strictly dominated> by site $k-1$. These endpoint actions are therefore also <weakly dominated strategies>, since <strict dominance> is stronger than the requirement of being no worse everywhere and better somewhere.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

If a player chooses site $x$, the opponent can co-locate and hold its <payoff> to $k$. Among opposing sites to its right, the lowest <payoff> is obtained against $x+1$, giving $2x$. Among opposing sites to its left, the lowest is against $x-1$, giving $2(k-x+1)$. Thus the pure-action <security level payoff> is
$$
g(x)=\min\{k,\,2x,\,2(k-x+1)\}.
$$
The expression remains valid at an endpoint, where the missing-side term is redundant. It reaches its largest possible value $k$ exactly when $k/2\leq x\leq k/2+1$. Hence the pure maxmin <strategies> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{cases}x=(k+1)/2,&k\text{ odd},\\x\in\{k/2,k/2+1\},&k\text{ even},\end{cases}\qquad\text{with guaranteed payoff }k.}
$$
Reflection gives the same answer for the other player.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Both players can guarantee $k$ by part (d), so at any <Nash equilibrium> each must receive at least $k$. Their <payoffs> sum to $2k$, forcing both equilibrium <payoffs> to equal $k$.

Moreover, an equilibrium action must itself have the maxmin guarantee $k$. If a player's chosen action allowed an opposing response that reduced its <payoff> below $k$, that response would give the opponent more than $k$, a profitable <unilateral deviation>. Thus both equilibrium sites belong to the central <set> identified in part (d).

Conversely, if both choose from that central <set>, each chosen action guarantees $k$. Neither can improve above $k$, because the other player's fixed central action still guarantees the other player $k$. Consequently, **all pure <Nash equilibria> are**
$$
\boxed{\begin{cases}((k+1)/2,(k+1)/2),&k\text{ odd},\\(x,y)\in\{k/2,k/2+1\}^2,&k\text{ even}.\end{cases}}
$$
For even $k$, this includes both co-located central profiles and both orders of the two distinct central sites.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

**There is no pure <Nash equilibrium>.** If the firms choose different sites, $S$ serves at least its own building, so $C$ receives less than $2k$. By moving onto $S$'s site, $C$ makes every customer equidistant from the two shops and wins every tie, obtaining $2k$. Thus any separated profile has a profitable <unilateral deviation> by $C$.

At any co-located profile, $S$ receives zero. It can move to a different site and capture at least that site's customers, obtaining at least two. Thus every co-located profile also has a profitable <unilateral deviation>. These cases exhaust all pure <strategy profiles>.

=== g
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $k=2m+1$, where $m\geq2$. For three distinct sites $a<b<c$, the left, middle and right firms receive respectively
$$
a+b-1,\qquad c-a,\qquad2k-b-c+1.
$$
These follow by counting the two midpoint boundaries, including split ties. Therefore, after two firms occupy $\ell<r$, the final entrant compares **$2(\ell-1)$ at $\ell-1$, $2(k-r)$ at $r+1$, and $r-\ell$ at every available interior site**. Omit an unavailable region and retain every maximizing tie. This is the <final entrant payoff in a discrete location game>.

First suppose $C$ opens at the central site $m+1$. If $S$ chooses $s\leq m$, the final entrant's best possible <payoffs> on the left, right and interior are at most $2m-2$, exactly $2m$, and at most $m$, respectively. Hence $N$'s unique <best response> is $m+2$. The resulting <payoff> to $S$ is $m+s$, uniquely maximized on that side at $s=m$. Reflection gives the other optimal choice $s=m+2$, followed by $N=m$. Under either choice, **$C$ receives only $2$**. Any mixture of the tied optimal second-mover choices has the same consequence.

Now let $C$ deviate to $c=m$. If $S$ chooses $s<m$, the unique final <best response> is $m+1$, yielding $S$ at most $2m-2$. If $S$ chooses $m+1$, the unique final <best response> is $m+2$, yielding $S$ only two. Both classes will be strictly inferior to an available alternative, independently of how later ties are resolved:

* If $m\geq3$, $S$ can choose $m+3$. The final entrant compares $2m-2$, $2m-4$ and $3$, so uniquely chooses $m-1$. This gives $S$ exactly $2m$.
* If $m=2$, $S$ can choose site four after $C$ chooses two. All three remaining sites are final-mover <best responses>; they give $S$ <payoffs> five, four and three. Thus $S$ guarantees at least three, exceeding the excluded choices' <payoff> two.

Accordingly, in every <subgame perfect equilibrium> continuation after $C=m$, the second mover must choose a site $s\geq m+2$. This remains true with <mixed strategies>: every excluded choice pays strictly less than the displayed guaranteed alternative, under every possible continuation tie policy.

For any such $s$, every legal final site gives $C$ at least three. If $N$ lies left of $m$, then $C$ is the middle firm and receives $s-N\geq3$. If $N$ lies right of $m$, then $C$ is the left firm and receives $m+\min(s,N)-1\geq2m\geq4$. Thus **the deviation gives $C$ at least $3$, against every subgame-perfect continuation**, whereas the central opening gives it $2$.

\Image[computational-game-theory-location-continuations.png]
{title=Final-mover ties and the second mover's guarantee after the first firm deviates on a nine-building street}

In this original example, a final-mover tie produces the <line segment> for $S=6$: its points are <expected payoff> pairs from a <mixed strategy> over two final <best responses>. The second mover can independently secure eight by choosing $S=7$, so only the segment to the right of that guarantee can support its optimal choice. Mixing over a tied final response cannot make the first mover's <payoff> fall below three.

A <subgame perfect equilibrium> must prescribe optimal continuation play even after the first mover's off-path deviation. Since the deviation is strictly profitable under every such prescription, **no <subgame perfect equilibrium> can have $C$ open centrally**. The argument does not select favourable ties or assume that off-path players cooperate with the deviator.

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $p$ be the row player's <probability> of $T$. Its <expected payoffs> against $L$ and $R$ are $6-5p$ and $2+p$. The <security level payoff> is therefore
$$
\max_{0\leq p\leq1}\min\{6-5p,2+p\}.
$$
The first line decreases and the second increases, so their intersection is optimal: $6-5p=2+p$ gives $p=2/3$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{Security level }=8/3,\qquad\Pr(T)=2/3,\quad\Pr(B)=1/3.}
$$
The two column actions then give the same <expected payoff>, so an opposing <mixed strategy> cannot reduce it further.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The row player's unique <best response> to $L$ is $B$ and to $R$ is $T$. The column player's unique <best response> to $T$ is $R$ and to $B$ is $L$. Thus **the pure <Nash equilibria> are $(T,R)$ and $(B,L)$**.

For a fully mixed equilibrium, let $p=\Pr(T)$ and $q=\Pr(L)$. Row-player indifference requires
$$
q+3(1-q)=6q+2(1-q),\qquad q=1/6.
$$
Column-player indifference requires
$$
3(1-p)=6p+2(1-p),\qquad p=1/7.
$$
Both <probabilities> lie strictly between zero and one, so these indifference conditions make both supported actions <best responses>. Hence the remaining equilibrium is
$$
\boxed{(\Pr(T),\Pr(B))=(1/7,6/7),\qquad(\Pr(L),\Pr(R))=(1/6,5/6).}
$$
Its <expected payoffs> are $(8/3,18/7)$. There are no other partly mixed equilibria: if either player is pure, the other's <best response> is unique, forcing both to be pure. If both mix, the displayed linear indifference equations uniquely determine their <probabilities>. The row player's equilibrium mixture differs from its maxmin mixture in part (a); these are different optimization criteria in this non-zero-sum game.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

**As literally stated, the claim is false.** Give player one two pure actions, with <payoffs> one and zero against every opposing action. A <mixed strategy> assigning probability one to the first action has exactly that action's <payoffs>, so very weakly dominates it under the printed non-strict inequality. The other pure action nevertheless fails to dominate it.

The intended statement becomes true if the mixture gives positive <probability> to some distinct action, equivalently $p_i<1$. Fix any opposing pure <strategy profile> at which $s_i$ pays one. Since all <payoffs> are in $\{0,1\}$,
$$
1\leq\sum_jp_ju_1(s_j,s_{-1})\leq\sum_jp_j=1.
$$
Equality forces every positive-probability supported action to pay one there. At a profile where $s_i$ pays zero, every other action already pays at least zero. Therefore every action in the <support of a mixed strategy> is no worse than $s_i$ against every opposing profile. Choosing a distinct supported action proves the corrected conclusion: **nontrivial mixed <very weak dominance> implies very weak domination by a distinct pure action**. This proof does not insert the missing support condition silently.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

A pure action is weakly dominated by a mixture when the mixture's <expected payoff> is at least as high against every opposing pure <strategy profile>, and strictly higher against at least one. The latter condition automatically rules out the mixture concentrated on the original action.

**Yes: the pure-domination conclusion remains true for this weak version.** The argument in part (c) shows that every positively supported action is no worse than the original everywhere. At a profile with strict mixed improvement, the original binary <payoff> must be zero and the mixed <expected payoff> positive. Some positively supported action therefore pays one there. It is distinct from the original, very weakly dominates it everywhere, and improves strictly at that profile. Thus it **weakly dominates the original pure action**. Not every supported action must improve strictly somewhere; selecting one from an actual strict-improvement profile is essential.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Write column $j$ as $((a_j,c_j),(b_j,d_j))^T$, where the row player's binary <payoff> is first. Suppose, for a contradiction, there is no pure <Nash equilibrium>.

The column player's maximum <payoff> in the top row must be one. Otherwise every column would be its <best response> there. To avoid a top-row equilibrium, every column would then need $a_j=0,b_j=1$. But a column maximizing $d_j$ would immediately give a bottom-row equilibrium. The same reasoning with rows exchanged shows that the maximum bottom-row column <payoff> is also one.

Choose a column with $c_j=1$. To avoid a top-row equilibrium, it must have $a_j=0,b_j=1$. It cannot also have $d_j=1$, because its bottom cell would then be an equilibrium. Hence this column is $((0,1),(1,0))^T$, the specified second column type. Exchanging rows forces a column $((1,0),(0,1))^T$, the specified first type.

Thus **absence of a pure equilibrium forces both forbidden column types to occur**. If the given game omits them, this is a contradiction, proving existence of a pure <Nash equilibrium>. The argument actually proves the stronger statement that omitting either one of the two types suffices.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

Compute $C=\max_jc_j$ and $D=\max_jd_j$ in one pass. Scan the columns again: return the top cell of column $j$ if $c_j=C$ and $a_j\geq b_j$, or its bottom cell if $d_j=D$ and $b_j\geq a_j$. Each test is exactly mutual <best response>, so any returned cell is a pure <Nash equilibrium>.

If none is found, part (e) supplies columns
$$
\begin{pmatrix}(1,0)\\(0,1)\end{pmatrix},\qquad\begin{pmatrix}(0,1)\\(1,0)\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Locate one of each and mix with <probability> $1/2$ on each of those two columns, while the row player mixes $1/2$ on each row. The two row actions then both have <expected payoff> $1/2$.

To check all opposing deviations, observe that no column can have $(c_j,d_j)=(1,1)$. Such a column would maximize the column player's <payoff> in either row; choosing a row-player <best response> within it would give a pure equilibrium, contrary to the failed scan. Therefore every column has $c_j+d_j\leq1$, and against the equal row mixture its <expected payoff> is at most $1/2$. The two supported columns attain that bound.

Hence **the returned half-half profile is a <Nash equilibrium> against every column, not only the two supported ones**. A constant number of scans takes **$O(n)$ <running time>**, with constant auxiliary storage if the output mixture is represented by its two supported column indices and probabilities. This is the construction in <equilibrium in a two-row binary game>.

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For a fixed finite player <set> $N$ and characteristic <function> $v$ with $v(\varnothing)=0$, let $\psi(v)$ be the allocated <payoff> <vector>. A standard four-axiom characterization is:

**<Cooperative-game efficiency>:** $\sum_{i\in N}\psi_i(v)=v(N)$.

**<Cooperative-game symmetry>:** if $v(S\cup\{i\})=v(S\cup\{j\})$ for every $S\subseteq N\setminus\{i,j\}$, then $\psi_i(v)=\psi_j(v)$.

**The <null player> axiom:** if $v(S\cup\{i\})=v(S)$ for every $S\subseteq N\setminus\{i\}$, then $\psi_i(v)=0$.

**<Cooperative-game additivity>:** for games $v,w$ on the same $N$, $\psi_i(v+w)=\psi_i(v)+\psi_i(w)$, where $(v+w)(S)=v(S)+w(S)$.

These axioms uniquely specify the <Shapley value>. This is the zero-marginal null-player convention. The original work combines some requirements into an equivalent formulation.<A Value for N-Person Games by Lloyd Shapley (RAND paper)>[RAND P-295, 1952, abstract identifies an axiomatic construction; the four-part formulation and proof are given in Shapley value axioms]{ref}

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the inclusive quota: a <coalition> wins at weight **at least $25$**. For each player, count losing predecessor <coalitions> that become winning when that player joins. Group these swing counts by predecessor <cardinality> $t=0,1,2,3$:

|| Player and weight
|| Swing counts $(n_{i,0},n_{i,1},n_{i,2},n_{i,3})$
|| <Shapley value>
|| <Banzhaf index>

| $1:\ 15$
| $(0,1,3,1)$
| $7/12$
| $5/8$

| $2:\ 10$
| $(0,1,2,0)$
| $1/4$
| $3/8$

| $3:\ 5$
| $(0,0,1,0)$
| $1/12$
| $1/8$

| $4:\ 5$
| $(0,0,1,0)$
| $1/12$
| $1/8$

For example, player one is pivotal for predecessor <coalitions> $\{2\}$, $\{2,3\}$, $\{2,4\}$, $\{3,4\}$ and $\{2,3,4\}$. Player three is pivotal only for $\{1,4\}$; player four has the symmetric counterpart.

Each swing contributes $1/8$ to the <probabilistic Banzhaf index>. For the <Shapley value>, a swing with $t$ predecessors contributes $t!(3-t)!/4!$, which is $1/4,1/12,1/12,1/4$ for the four possible sizes. Applying these weights to the table gives
$$
\boxed{\phi=(7/12,\,1/4,\,1/12,\,1/12),\qquad\beta=(5/8,\,3/8,\,1/8,\,1/8).}
$$
The <Shapley values> sum to one. The unnormalized <Banzhaf indices> sum to $5/4$; they must not be replaced by normalized shares in this part.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Fix distinct players $i,j$ and write $Ti=T\cup\{i\}$, with analogous notation for $Tj$ and $Tij$. Splitting sampled <coalitions> according to whether they contain the other player gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
2^{n-1}\beta_i(v)&=\sum_{T\subseteq N\setminus\{i,j\}}\bigl[v(Ti)-v(T)+v(Tij)-v(Tj)\bigr],\\
2^{n-1}\beta_j(v)&=\sum_{T\subseteq N\setminus\{i,j\}}\bigl[v(Tj)-v(T)+v(Tij)-v(Ti)\bigr].
\end{aligned}
$$
Subtracting yields the <Banzhaf difference identity>,
$$
\beta_j(v)-\beta_i(v)=\frac1{2^{n-2}}\sum_{T\subseteq N\setminus\{i,j\}}\bigl[v(Tj)-v(Ti)\bigr].
$$
In a <weighted voting game>, $w_j>w_i$ means that $Tj$ has greater total weight than $Ti$. Thus $v(Tj)\geq v(Ti)$ for every $T$: if the lighter <coalition> reaches the quota, the heavier one does too. Every summand is nonnegative, proving **$\beta_i(v)\leq\beta_j(v)$**. Equality remains possible even when the weights differ.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The <probabilistic Banzhaf index> satisfies **<cooperative-game symmetry>, the <null player> axiom and <cooperative-game additivity>, but not <cooperative-game efficiency>**.

For <cooperative-game symmetry>, interchangeable players have $v(Ti)=v(Tj)$ for every $T$ omitting them. The <Banzhaf difference identity> therefore gives equal indices. For a <null player>, every <marginal contribution> in the defining average is zero, so its index is zero.

For <cooperative-game additivity>, each <marginal contribution> is additive:
$$
(v+w)(S\cup\{i\})-(v+w)(S)=[v(S\cup\{i\})-v(S)]+[w(S\cup\{i\})-w(S)].
$$
Averaging both sides over the same $2^{n-1}$ <coalitions> gives $\beta_i(v+w)=\beta_i(v)+\beta_i(w)$.

For failure of <cooperative-game efficiency>, the indices computed in part (b) sum to $5/4$, whereas the grand-<coalition> value is one. This is already a counterexample. Independently, a three-player majority game gives each player index $1/2$, also exceeding the total value when summed. Pivotality averaged over independently chosen <coalitions> does not telescope in the way the ordering-average <Shapley value> does.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Where its denominator is nonzero, normalization enforces **<cooperative-game efficiency>** and preserves **<cooperative-game symmetry> and the <null player> axiom**. The violated axiom is **<cooperative-game additivity>**.

For an exact counterexample on three players, let $v$ be the majority game, and let $w$ be the dictatorship in which a <coalition> wins precisely when it contains player one. Their <probabilistic Banzhaf indices> are
$$
\beta(v)=(1/2,1/2,1/2),\qquad\beta(w)=(1,0,0),
$$
with $v(N)=w(N)=1$. Hence
$$
\beta'(v)=(1/3,1/3,1/3),\qquad\beta'(w)=(1,0,0).
$$
Unnormalized indices add, so $\beta(v+w)=(3/2,1/2,1/2)$. Their <sum> is $5/2$, while $(v+w)(N)=2$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\beta'(v+w)=(6/5,2/5,2/5)\ne(4/3,1/3,1/3)=\beta'(v)+\beta'(w).}
$$
The sum game is a valid <transferable utility game>, although it is not binary; that is the natural domain of the additivity axiom. The failure comes from a game-dependent scaling factor. A zero denominator, as for the zero game, makes the displayed normalization undefined unless a separate convention is supplied; none of the counterexample's denominators vanish.
