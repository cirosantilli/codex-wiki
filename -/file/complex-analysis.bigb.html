= Complex analysis
{wiki}

Complex analysis studies <functions> of a <complex number>, especially <holomorphic functions>. Its distinctive feature is that a single complex <derivative> controls variation in every direction of the <complex plane>. The map $f(z)=z^2$ gives a first picture: straight coordinate lines become curved, yet small perpendicular arrows remain perpendicular wherever $f'(z)\ne0$.

\Image[complex-square-map.png]
{title=The complex square bends a grid globally and acts near 1 + i as a rotation through 45 degrees and a scaling by twice the square root of two}

The bottom panels show displacements rather than absolute positions. Multiplication by $f'(1+i)=2+2i$ predicts the image of each small arrow; the exact image differs by $h^2$. The origin is exceptional because $f'(0)=0$. This distinction between local <conformal maps> and a <critical point> is central: <complex differentiability> imposes strong geometric structure, without making every <holomorphic function> globally one-to-one.

The remarkable next step is that <holomorphic functions> are determined inside a circle by their values on its boundary. Precisely, let $R>0$, let $D=\{z:|z-a|<R\}$, and suppose $f$ is a <holomorphic function> on an <open set> containing the closed disc $\overline D$. Orient its boundary counterclockwise. The <Cauchy integral formula> states that, for every $w\in D$,
$$
f(w)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|z-a|=R}\frac{f(z)}{z-w}\,dz.
$$
The <contour integral> combines boundary values with a kernel depending on the requested interior point. It is a reconstruction formula, not merely a trick for evaluating an <integral>. The hypothesis about the closed disc ensures that all boundary values and the <contour integral> are well defined. \a[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-04-complex-variables-with-applications-spring-2018/b1a90df19f643b974555bbbb93138a48_MIT18_04S18_topic4.pdf][Orloff, MIT Topic 4, Theorem 4.1]{ref}

At the centre, substituting $z=a+Re^{i\theta}$ cancels the denominator against $dz=iRe^{i\theta}d\theta$:
$$
f(a)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(a+Re^{i\theta})\,d\theta.
$$
Thus the centre value is the angular average of the boundary values. For the <polynomial> $f(z)=z^2$, $a=1+i$ and $R=1/2$, expand the boundary data as
$$
f(a+Re^{i\theta})=2i+(1+i)e^{i\theta}+\tfrac14e^{2i\theta}.
$$
Both oscillating terms have zero average, leaving $f(a)=2i$. Away from the centre the weights change: on the <unit circle>,
$$
f(w)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{f(e^{i\theta})}{1-we^{-i\theta}}\,d\theta,\qquad |w|<1.
$$
With $f(z)=z^2$ and $w=1/2$, expanding the denominator as a <geometric series> leaves only its second-power term after integration, giving $f(1/2)=1/4$. This also explains how the <Cauchy integral formula> recovers an entire <Taylor series> from boundary data.

When a <holomorphic function> has <isolated singularities>, a different boundary calculation detects their <residues>. Suppose $\Gamma$ is a counterclockwise simple closed <contour> and $f$ is <holomorphic> on a neighbourhood of it and its interior except at finitely many interior <isolated singularities> $a_j$. The <residue theorem> gives
$$
\oint_\Gamma f(z)\,dz=2\pi i\sum_j\operatorname{Res}(f,a_j).
$$
A <residue> is the coefficient of $(z-a_j)^{-1}$ in the <Laurent series>. A short proof idea is to deform the <contour> to small circles around the <isolated singularities> using the <Cauchy integral theorem>. Integrating the <Laurent series> on each circle kills every power except $-1$. \a[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-04-complex-variables-with-applications-spring-2018/3c58ce71ec6f7573804221d76158a0bd_MIT18_04S18_topic8.pdf][Orloff, MIT Topic 8, residue theorem]{ref}

Here is how a complex <pole> determines a real <improper integral>. Set $g(z)=1/(1+z^2)$ and use the <contour> consisting of the segment from $-R$ to $R$, followed by the upper semicircle $A_R$, with $R>1$. Only the <simple pole> $i$ lies inside, and
$$
\operatorname{Res}(g,i)=\lim_{z\to i}\frac{z-i}{(z-i)(z+i)}=\frac{1}{2i},\qquad \int_{-R}^R\frac{dx}{1+x^2}+\int_{A_R}g(z)\,dz=\pi.
$$
The arc must actually disappear. On $A_R$, the <reverse triangle inequality> gives $|1+z^2|\ge R^2-1$, and its length is $\pi R$, so
$$
\left|\int_{A_R}g(z)\,dz\right|\le\frac{\pi R}{R^2-1}\longrightarrow0.
$$
The real integrand is bounded by $x^{-2}$ for $|x|\ge1$, so both tails converge separately. Taking the limit therefore proves the ordinary <improper integral>
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{1+x^2}=\pi.
$$
For example, at $R=10$ the real segment contributes $2\arctan(10)\approx2.942255$, and the arc contributes $0.199337$, below the bound $10\pi/99\approx0.317333$. The usefulness of the <residue theorem> is that the same strategy works for many <rational functions> whose real <antiderivatives> are cumbersome: find the enclosed <poles>, compute their <residues>, and control the connecting arc. \a[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-04-complex-variables-with-applications-spring-2018/f69bc227ae476839819fb18e5b283072_MIT18_04S18_topic9.pdf][Orloff, MIT Topic 9, contour decay estimates]{ref}

Historically, Cauchy's 1825 memoir on <integrals> with imaginary limits develops complex <contour integration> and its use in transforming and evaluating definite <integrals>. The opening sections define complex path <integrals>, discuss changing the path, and examine what happens near singular points. These are recognizable ancestors of the modern link between <holomorphic functions>, <contours> and <residues>. \a[https://www.numdam.org/item/BSMA_1874__7__265_0.pdf][Cauchy, Mémoire sur les intégrales définies, prises entre des limites imaginaires, 1825; reprinted 1874, sections 1–6]{ref}

= Polynomial hull
{parent=Complex analysis}
{title2=$\widehat K=\{z:|p(z)|\leq\sup_K|p|\text{ for every polynomial }p\}$}

For a compact set $K\subset\mathbb C^n$, its polynomial hull is the set displayed, where $p$ ranges over all complex <multivariate polynomials>. It contains $K$. In one complex dimension, bounded complementary components are filled in: the <maximum modulus principle> bounds every <polynomial> inside those components by its boundary values. Polynomial approximation and separation distinguish points outside the hull.

= Polynomial hull in one complex variable
{parent=Polynomial hull}
{title2=$\widehat K$}

For a <compact set> $K\subset\mathbb C$, its polynomial hull is $\widehat K=\{z:|P(z)|\le\sup_K|P|\text{ for every polynomial }P\}$. In one complex variable this is $K$ together with all bounded components of its complement. The <maximum modulus principle> proves inclusion of each filled hole. Conversely, polynomial approximation of an exterior pole by the <Runge approximation theorem> separates an exterior point from the filled compact set. The filled set has connected complement, which makes it the natural set for polynomial approximation of functions whose poles lie in the unbounded complementary component.

= Holomorphic square root
{parent=Complex analysis}

A holomorphic square root of a nonvanishing holomorphic function $f$ is a holomorphic function $g$ satisfying $g^2=f$. It exists locally, and globally exactly when the winding obstruction around every closed path vanishes.

= Holomorphic square root outside all polynomial roots

= Formal monodromy
{parent=Monodromy}
{title2=$M_f=e^{2\pi i\Theta}$}

An unramified formal <fundamental matrix> $\widehat Y=\widehat G(\lambda)\lambda^\Theta e^{Q(\lambda)}$ acquires the right factor $e^{2\pi i\Theta}$ on a positive turn of the <logarithm> branch. This is <formal monodromy>. It can be nontrivial even when the true equation has an entire <fundamental matrix> and trivial actual <monodromy>, because <Stokes matrices> compensate for it.

= Formal monodromy correction to an entire-system Stokes product
{parent=Formal monodromy}
{title2=$S_N\cdots S_1=M_f^{-1}$}

When Stokes factors act on solution coefficient vectors, a full positive turn gives their ordered product equal to the inverse of <formal monodromy> for a <polynomial> system with no finite singularities. The true <fundamental matrix> is entire, so its actual <monodromy> is trivial, but its normalized formal branch changes by $M_f$. Indeed, write $Y_{j+1}=Y_jS_j^{-1}$; then $Y_{N+1}=Y_1(S_N\cdots S_1)^{-1}$, whereas normalized continuation after the positive turn gives $Y_{N+1}=Y_1M_f$. Cancelling the invertible <fundamental matrix> proves the identity. The uncorrected product is identity only when $M_f=I$. For a cubic-exponential two-by-two system with $U=1,V=-1,P=1,R=2,d=3/2$, the formal exponent is $\theta=1/2$, so $M_f=-I$ and the Stokes product is $-I$.

= Holomorphy

= Holomorphically
{synonym}

A complex-valued <function> $f:U\to\mathbb C$ is holomorphic on an <open set> $U$ when it has a complex <derivative> at every point of $U$. Requiring a <derivative> throughout a neighbourhood is much stronger than <complex differentiability at a point>. It forces locally convergent <Taylor series> and <derivatives> of every order, as follows from the <Cauchy integral formula> and <Cauchy derivative formula>.

To see the restriction in real coordinates, write $z=x+iy$ and $f(z)=u(x,y)+iv(x,y)$. The real and imaginary increment quotients must agree, giving the <Cauchy-Riemann equations>
$$
u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x,\qquad f'=u_x+iv_x.
$$
Conversely, if $u,v$ have continuous <partial derivatives> on $U$ and satisfy the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> there, then $f$ is a <holomorphic function>. The equations at a lone point, without a differentiability hypothesis for the real coordinate map, are insufficient. Their role is to force the <Jacobian matrix> to have the special form
$$
Df=\begin{pmatrix}u_x&u_y\\v_x&v_y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}p&-q\\q&p\end{pmatrix},\qquad f'=p+iq.
$$
Such a <matrix> represents multiplication by a <complex number>; it has only two free real entries. \a[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-04-complex-variables-with-applications-spring-2018/330e301bd727c7bdaa679cf44cb75fe3_MIT18_04S18_topic2.pdf][Orloff, MIT Topic 2, sections 2.7.2–2.7.4]{ref}

For $f(z)=z^2$, $u=x^2-y^2$ and $v=2xy$, so $u_x=v_y=2x$ and $u_y=-v_x=-2y$. Directly,
$$
f(z_0+h)-f(z_0)=2z_0h+h^2,\qquad f'(z_0)=2z_0.
$$
At $z_0=1+i$, multiplication by $2+2i$ scales lengths by its <modulus> $2\sqrt2$ and rotates by its <argument> $\pi/4$. A horizontal increment $h=0.01$ gives the exact displacement $0.0201+0.02i$; a vertical increment $h=0.01i$ gives $-0.0201+0.02i$. The first-order predictions $0.02+0.02i$ and $-0.02+0.02i$ are perpendicular, and the errors have <modulus> $10^{-4}$.

At every nonzero $z_0$, this local rotation and scaling makes $z^2$ a local <conformal map>. At zero, the first-order map vanishes: $re^{i\theta}$ becomes $r^2e^{2i\theta}$, so the <argument> doubles and the origin is a <critical point>. Globally $z$ and $-z$ have the same square. Local preservation of <angles> therefore coexists with global folding, a useful warning when using <holomorphic functions> as coordinate changes.

= Complex differentiability
{parent=Holomorphic function}
{title2=$f'(z)=\lim_{h\to0}(f(z+h)-f(z))/h$}

A complex-valued <function> is complex differentiable at $z_0$ if
$$
f'(z_0)=\lim_{\substack{h\to0\\h\in\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}}}\frac{f(z_0+h)-f(z_0)}{h}
$$
exists. The same limit must hold for every approach, including curved paths. Equivalently,
$$
f(z_0+h)=f(z_0)+f'(z_0)h+o(|h|).
$$
The error divided by $|h|$ tends to zero. Thus the <derivative> is a single complex multiplier, not an arbitrary real <linear map> of the plane. If the real coordinate map is differentiable, <complex differentiability> is equivalent to its satisfying the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> at that point.

The simplest failure is <complex conjugation>, $f(z)=\overline z$. At every $z_0$ its increment quotient is $\overline h/h$. For real $h=t\ne0$ it is $1$, while for $h=it$ it is $-1$. More generally, $h=te^{i\theta}$ gives $e^{-2i\theta}$. Although <complex conjugation> is a smooth real reflection and has every real <directional derivative>, these incompatible quotients rule out a complex <derivative> everywhere.

Agreement on two axes is necessary, but does not by itself establish a limit over the whole <complex plane>. The <Cauchy-Riemann equations> need the real differentiability condition above, or a sufficient regularity condition such as continuous <partial derivatives> near the point. There is a second distinction: $f(z)=|z|^2$ has complex <derivative> zero at zero because its quotient there is $\overline h\to0$, but its <Cauchy-Riemann equations> fail at every nonzero point. It is complex differentiable at zero without being a <holomorphic function> on any neighbourhood of zero.

= Nevanlinna class
{c}
{parent=Holomorphic function}
{title2=$N(\mathbb D)=\{f:\sup_{r<1}\int\log^+|f(r\xi)|\,dm(\xi)<\infty\}$}

The analytic Nevanlinna class consists of <holomorphic functions> on the <unit disc> with uniformly bounded means of their positive logarithmic modulus. Here $m$ is normalized angular measure. A bounded <holomorphic function> belongs to this class, but the class also permits unbounded functions. It is the analytic version of <bounded characteristic>.

= Bounded characteristic
{parent=Nevanlinna class}

A <holomorphic function> on the <unit disc> has bounded characteristic when $\sup_{r<1}\int\log^+|f(r\xi)|\,dm(\xi)<\infty$, where $\log^+t=\max(0,\log t)$. Thus it belongs to the <Nevanlinna class>. A general <meromorphic function> requires the additional pole-counting term in its characteristic; the displayed analytic definition has no such term.

= Quotient characterization of bounded characteristic
{parent=Bounded characteristic}
{title2=$f=u/v,\qquad u,v\in H^\infty(\mathbb D),\quad v\not\equiv0$}

A <meromorphic function> on the <unit disc> has <bounded characteristic> if and only if it has this representation. Its pole <Blaschke product> $B$ makes $g=Bf$ analytic with bounded positive logarithmic means. The <least harmonic majorant by expanding disk lifts> gives $H\ge\log^+|g|$. A <harmonic conjugate> gives an analytic $A$ with $\operatorname{Re}A=H$, so $u=ge^{-A}$ and $v=Be^{-A}$ are bounded. Conversely the <Nevanlinna first main theorem> bounds the characteristic of $u/v$ by that of $u$ and $1/v$, hence by a constant.

= Zero multiplicity
{synonym}

A <holomorphic function> that is not identically zero has a zero of multiplicity $m$ at $z_0$ if $f(z)=(z-z_0)^m h(z)$ for a <holomorphic function> $h$ with $h(z_0)\ne0$. Its <Taylor series> identifies $m$ as the first nonzero coefficient index. Logarithmic differentiation gives $f'/f=m/(z-z_0)+h'/h$, so the <residue> of $f'/f$ is $m$. Counting zeros with <zero multiplicity> repeats that zero $m$ times.

= Periodic holomorphic descent through the exponential map
{parent=Holomorphic function}

If a <holomorphic function> of $w$ is periodic with period one, then $F((\log z)/(2\pi i))$ is independent of the choice of a local logarithm: two choices differ by $2\pi i k$, hence their arguments differ by an integer. Consequently it defines a holomorphic function on the image of the original domain under $w\mapsto e^{2\pi iw}$, provided that domain is invariant under integer shifts. Local logarithms prove holomorphy across an arbitrary global branch cut. Singularities on an integer lattice all map to $z=1$.

= Constant-modulus holomorphic function
{parent=Holomorphic function}

A <holomorphic function> with constant modulus on a connected <domain> is constant. If $h=u+iv$ and $u^2+v^2=c^2>0$, differentiation and the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> give $uu_x+vv_x=0$ and $-uv_x+vu_x=0$. Their coefficient <determinant> is $-c^2$, so $u_x=v_x=0$, and all first <derivatives> vanish. When $c=0$, the conclusion is immediate. Consequently two nonvanishing <holomorphic functions> with equal moduli differ by a single constant phase on a connected <domain>.

= Banach-space-valued holomorphic function
{parent=Holomorphic function}
{c}
{title2=$F'(z)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{F(z+h)-F(z)}h$}

A map from an open subset of $\mathbb C$ to a complex <Banach space> is holomorphic when the displayed limit exists in the Banach-space norm at every point. A norm-convergent power series is holomorphic inside its radius of convergence: on every smaller disk the series of derivatives converges uniformly by the geometric coefficient bound, allowing differentiation term by term. Applying a <continuous linear functional> produces an ordinary scalar <holomorphic function>. Scalar Cauchy identities therefore imply the corresponding vector-integral identities when bounded linear functionals separate points, as they do by the <Hahn-Banach theorem>.

= Spectral-radius maximum principle
{parent=Banach-space-valued holomorphic function}
{title2=$r(f(z))\le\sup_{w\in\partial K}r(f(w))$}

For a <Banach algebra>-valued <holomorphic function>, set $p_n(w)=\|f(w)^{2^n}\|^{1/2^n}$. These <continuous> functions decrease to the <spectral radius> by the <spectral radius formula>. If $M=\sup_{\partial K}r(f(w))$, the <continuous> functions $\max\{p_n,M\}$ on $\partial K$ decrease to the <continuous> constant $M$. The <Dini theorem> gives <uniform convergence>. Apply the <norm maximum principle for a Banach-space-valued holomorphic function> to each power, and then let $n\to\infty$. This proof does not assume <continuity> of <spectral radius>.

= Norm maximum principle for a Banach-space-valued holomorphic function
{parent=Banach-space-valued holomorphic function}
{title2=$\|f(z)\|\le\sup_{w\in\partial K}\|f(w)\|$}

For a <compact> set $K$ in the domain, a norming <bounded linear functional> at $f(z)$ reduces the assertion to the scalar <maximum modulus principle>. If $z$ lies inside $K$, use its <connected> component of the <interior>; that component's <boundary> lies in $\partial K$. If $z\in\partial K$, the inequality is immediate. No smoothness or connectedness assumption on $K$ is required.

= Prescribed zeros in a plane domain via Runge approximation
{parent=Holomorphic function}

Every <closed discrete subset> of a plane <domain> is the zero set of a <holomorphic function>, with assigned finite positive multiplicities if desired. Exhaust the domain by relatively filled compact sets. For a prescribed point outside an earlier compact, choose a rational factor with that zero and its possible pole outside the domain in the same complementary component. A slit joining zero and pole allows a <holomorphic logarithm> near the earlier compact. Approximate that logarithm by <Runge theorem> and multiply the rational factor by the negative exponential of the approximant. The resulting factors tend to one with summable errors on each compact. Their <infinite product> has the prescribed zeros and no additional zeros.

= Antiholomorphic map
{synonym}

A complex-valued function is antiholomorphic when its complex conjugate is a <holomorphic function>. In one complex dimension it locally depends on $\overline z$ and satisfies $\partial_zf=0$. A nonconstant antiholomorphic map reverses the orientation induced by the complex coordinates. Thus complex conjugating a holomorphic sphere map reverses its <topological degree> while preserving its Dirichlet energy.

= Holomorphic convex hull
{parent=Holomorphic function}
{title2=$\widehat K_\Omega$}

For <compact> $K$ in a plane domain $\Omega$, its <holomorphic convex hull> is
$$
\widehat K_{\Omega}=\{w\in\Omega:|f(w)|\le\sup_K|f|\text{ for every }f\in\mathcal O(\Omega)\}.
$$
It is $K$ together with the components of $\widehat{\mathbb C}\setminus K$ lying entirely in $\Omega$. Inclusion of those components follows from the <maximum modulus principle>. For exclusion, suppose $w$ lies in a complementary component meeting $\widehat{\mathbb C}\setminus\Omega$. Choose an allowed <pole> there. The open-and-closed resolvent argument in the proof of <Runge theorem> approximates $(z-w)^{-1}$ uniformly on $K$ by functions $h$ <holomorphic> on $\Omega$. Then $1-(z-w)h(z)$ has value one at $w$ and arbitrarily small modulus on $K$, separating $w$ from the hull. For $\Omega=\mathbb C$, this is the <polynomial hull in one complex variable>.

= Blaschke product
{parent=Holomorphic function}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$B(z)=\lambda z^m\prod_n b_{a_n}(z)$}

For zeros $a_n$ in the <unit disc> satisfying $\sum_n(1-|a_n|)<\infty$, with $m$ occurrences at zero, a <Blaschke product> is
$$
B(z)=\lambda z^m\prod_{a_n\ne0}\frac{|a_n|}{a_n}\frac{a_n-z}{1-\overline{a_n}z},\qquad |\lambda|=1.
$$
For $|z|\le R<1$ and $a\ne0$, the normalized factor $b_a$ satisfies $|1-b_a(z)|\le(1-|a|)(1+R)/(1-R)$. The product therefore converges locally uniformly; outside its prescribed zeros the logarithms of its tail factors converge absolutely, so it has no additional zeros. Every finite product has modulus at most one in the disc, and the limit does too. Since $|b_a(w)|$ is <pseudohyperbolic distance> from $w$ to $a$, its modulus can be estimated geometrically.

= Interior zero count for a finite Blaschke product minus a constant
{parent=Blaschke product}
{title2=$N(B-a)=\deg B\quad(|a|<1)$}

A finite <Blaschke product> is holomorphic across the unit circle and has modulus one there. For a constant $a$ with $|a|<1$, <Rouché's theorem> makes $B-a$ and $B$ have the same number of interior zeros, counting <multiplicity>. Each factor contributes its prescribed zero; the poles are outside the closed disk.

= Hyperbolic zero estimate for a Blaschke product
{parent=Blaschke product}
{title2=$|B(w)|\le\exp[-2\sum_n e^{-\rho(w,a_n)}]$}

Use curvature-minus-one <hyperbolic distance in the Poincare disc>. Each <Blaschke factor> has modulus $\tanh(\rho(w,a)/2)=(1-t)/(1+t)$, with $t=e^{-\rho(w,a)}$. For $0\le t<1$, differentiating shows $\log[(1+t)/(1-t)]\ge2t$. Sum this logarithmic inequality over the zero factors and pass to the <Blaschke product> by <locally uniform convergence>. At a prescribed zero, its modulus vanishes and the bound is immediate. All sums count <multiplicities>.

= Vanishing absolute logarithmic mean characterization of Blaschke products
{parent=Blaschke product}
{title2=$\int|\log|f(r\xi)||\,dm\to0\iff f\text{ is a Blaschke product}$}

For a <holomorphic function> on the <unit disc>, this boundary condition characterizes <Blaschke products>, including unimodular constants. The nondecreasing radial means of the nonnegative <subharmonic function> $\log^+|f|$ must vanish, so $|f|\le1$. In the <Blaschke factorization of a bounded holomorphic function> $f=Bg$, both logarithmic means tend to zero. The <mean value property for harmonic functions> applied to $\log|g|$ gives $|g(0)|=1$, and the <maximum modulus principle> makes $g$ unimodular constant. The converse follows from the <radial logarithmic mean of a Blaschke product>. Omitting the outer absolute value changes the condition: $e^z$ has zero mean logarithmic modulus, but is not a <Blaschke product>.

= Blaschke factorization of a bounded holomorphic function
{c}
{parent=Blaschke product}
{title2=$f=Bg,\quad \|g\|_\infty\le\|f\|_\infty$}

Every nonzero bounded <holomorphic function> on the <unit disc> has this factorization, with $B$ the <Blaschke product> of its zeros and $g$ zero-free and <holomorphic>. The <Blaschke condition> follows from <Jensen's formula>. Dividing by any finite partial zero product gives a <holomorphic function>; the <maximum modulus principle> and the finite product's boundary modulus one bound that quotient by $\|f\|_\infty$. <Locally uniform convergence> of the partial products gives the same bound for $g$, with removable extensions at their zeros. This factorization alone does not make $g$ constant or a full inner-outer factorization.

= Radial logarithmic mean of a Blaschke product
{parent=Blaschke product}
{title2=$\int\log|B(r\xi)|\,dm=m\log r+\sum_n\log\max(r,|a_n|)$}

For $B(z)=\lambda z^m\prod_n b_{a_n}(z)$ with nonzero zeros $a_n$ counted with <multiplicity>, <Jensen's formula> gives the displayed identity. The negative logarithms of partial products increase, so the <monotone convergence theorem> permits termwise integration. The <Blaschke condition> implies $\sum_n-\log|a_n|<\infty$, which dominates the absolute summands. The <dominated convergence theorem> therefore gives a limit of zero as $r\uparrow1$. Since $|B|\le1$, the mean of $|\log|B||$ tends to zero as well.

= Automorphy character of an orbit Blaschke product
{parent=Blaschke product}
{title2=$B(Tz)=\chi(T)B(z)$}

Let a <group> $G$ of <Möbius transformations> of the <unit disk> have an orbit satisfying the <Blaschke condition>, counted once per distinct orbit point. For its <Blaschke product> $B$, invariance of the <pseudohyperbolic distance> and permutation of the orbit give $|B(Tz)|=|B(z)|$. The ratio $B(Tz)/B(z)$ extends across its zeros and has modulus one, so the <open mapping theorem (complex analysis)> makes it a constant $\chi(T)$. Composition gives a <group homomorphism> $\chi:G\to\{\lambda:|\lambda|=1\}$. It is essential to compare moduli of the products: two arbitrary bounded <holomorphic functions> with identical zeros need not have constant quotient.

= Blaschke condition
{parent=Blaschke product}
{c}
{title2=$\sum_n(1-|a_n|)<\infty$}

The <Blaschke condition> for a sequence in the <unit disc> is $\sum_n(1-|a_n|)<\infty$. It characterizes the zero sequences, counted with <multiplicity>, of nonzero bounded <holomorphic functions>. Sufficiency follows by constructing the <Blaschke product>. For necessity, <Jensen's formula> gives $\sum_{|a_n|<r}\log(r/|a_n|)\le\log\|f\|_\infty-\log|f(0)|$ when $f(0)\ne0$. Letting $r\uparrow1$ and using $-\log|a|\ge1-|a|$ proves the condition. A finite zero at zero is factored out first. Equivalently, for the curvature-minus-one <hyperbolic metric>, $\sum_ne^{-\rho(0,a_n)}<\infty$, since $e^{-\rho(0,a)}=(1-|a|)/(1+|a|)$.

= Blaschke sequence
{parent=Blaschke condition}
{c}
{title2=$\sum_n(1-|a_n|)<\infty$}

A sequence in the <unit disc>, counted with <multiplicity>, is a Blaschke sequence when it satisfies the <Blaschke condition>. The zeros of every nonzero bounded <holomorphic function> form such a sequence: <Jensen's formula> bounds $\sum_{|a_n|<r}\log(r/|a_n|)$ uniformly as $r\uparrow1$, and $1-|a|\le-\log|a|$ gives the assertion. There are only finitely many zeros at the origin, which can be factored out first.

= Jensen's formula
{c}
{parent=Holomorphic function}
{wiki}
{title2=$\log|f(0)|+\sum_{|a_j|<R}\log(R/|a_j|)=\tfrac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\log|f(Re^{i\theta})|\,d\theta$}

For a <holomorphic function> nonzero at zero and without boundary zeros, the formula relates its interior zeros, counted by multiplicity, to the circle mean of its logarithmic modulus. Divide out the finite interior zero factors; the remaining logarithmic modulus is <harmonic>, and each zero contributes $\log(R/|a_j|)$ by the <harmonic> mean-value formula. Boundary radii follow by limits where appropriate.

= Poisson-Jensen formula
{parent=Jensen's formula}
{c}
{title2=$\log|f(z)|=\operatorname{Poisson}(\log|f|)(z)-\sum m_aG_R(z,a)+\sum m_pG_R(z,p)$}

For a <meromorphic function> with no zero or <pole> on $|w|=R$, set $G_R(z,a)=\log|(R^2-\overline az)/(R(z-a))|$. At source points that are neither zeros nor <poles>, the displayed identity expresses its logarithmic modulus as the Poisson integral of its boundary values, minus its zero Green potentials plus its <pole> Green potentials. The Poisson kernel is $(R^2-|z|^2)/|Re^{i\theta}-z|^2$ with normalized angular measure. Subtracting the corresponding logarithmic singularities leaves a harmonic function, so the harmonic Poisson formula proves the identity. At zero it reduces to <Jensen's formula>.

= Annular spherical Jensen formula
{parent=Poisson-Jensen formula}
{c}
{title2=$T(r)=I(a)\log(1/r)+N(r;a)+\overline m(r;a)$}

For a <meromorphic function> near $0<|z|\le1$, and a target $a$ off its image of the unit circle, let $u_a=-\log k(f,a)$ for the <chordal metric>, $M_a(s)$ its normalized angular mean, and $I(a)=M_a'(1)$. The weighted spherical area $T(r)=(4\pi)^{-1}\int_{r<|z|<1}\log(|z|/r)(f^{\#}_{\mathrm{round}})^2\,dA$ satisfies the displayed formula, where $N$ counts $a$-points with <multiplicity> and weight $\log(|z|/r)$ and $\overline m=M_a(r)-M_a(1)$. Indeed $\Delta u_a=(f^{\#}_{\mathrm{round}})^2/2-2\pi\sum\deg_z(f)\delta_z$, and <Green's second identity> with the radial logarithmic weight proves the formula. The angular mean must include $1/(2\pi)$.

= Jensen zero-count bound
{c}
{parent=Jensen's formula}
{title2=$n_f(r)\log(R/r)\le\log M_f(R)-\log|f(0)|$}

Every zero in the smaller disk contributes at least $\log(R/r)$ in <Jensen's formula>. Bounding the outer circle average by its maximum proves the estimate. With $R=2r$, an exponential $O(r\log r)$ maximum bound gives an $O(r\log r)$ zero count.

= Lattice zeros force quadratic exponential growth
{parent=Jensen zero-count bound}
{title2=$\limsup_{R\to\infty}R^{-2}\log M_F(R)>0$}

A nonzero <entire function> vanishing at every nonzero point of the integer lattice has at least a constant times $R^2$ zeros in the disc of radius $R/2$. Each contributes at least $\log2$ to <Jensen's formula> at radius $R$. Therefore the circle mean of $\log|F|$, and hence the logarithm of its maximum modulus, is bounded below by $cR^2$ for all sufficiently large boundary-zero-free radii. Choosing maximizing points yields $|F(z_j)|>e^{c'|z_j|^2}$ for some $c'>0$ and $|z_j|\to\infty$. If zero is also a zero, first factor out its finite multiplicity; the additional $m\log R$ term does not weaken the quadratic lower bound.

= Weighted holomorphic norm on a shrinking time domain
{parent=Space of holomorphic functions}
{title2=$\|u\|_\alpha=\sup\limits_{0<s<1,\;0<|t|<\alpha(1-s),\;|x|\leq sR}|u(x,t)|\frac{\alpha(1-s)-|t|}{|t|}$}

Let $R,\alpha>0$ and $\mathcal C_\alpha=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb C^2:|x|<R,\;|t|<\alpha(1-|x|/R)\}$. The displayed <norm> makes the <holomorphic functions> with finite norm a <Banach space>. Finiteness forces $u(x,0)=0$: for fixed $x$, the weight grows like $1/|t|$ near zero. A Cauchy sequence in this norm converges uniformly on every compact subset of $\mathcal C_\alpha$, including at $t=0$. Its limit is holomorphic by <locally uniform convergence of holomorphic functions>, and the pointwise norm bounds show convergence in the original norm. The shrinking domain compensates for loss of an $x$-<derivative> in a <Cauchy-Kovalevskaya theorem> argument.

= Contraction estimate on a shrinking holomorphic domain
{parent=Weighted holomorphic norm on a shrinking time domain}
{title2=$\left\|\int_0^t\partial_xu(x,z)\,dz\right\|_\alpha\leq\frac{4\alpha}{R}\|u\|_\alpha$}

Write $N=\|u\|_\alpha$, $r=|t|$ and $A=\alpha(1-s)>r$. Along the straight integration segment, choose $\sigma(\tau)=s+(A-\tau)/(2\alpha)$. A <Cauchy estimate> on a disc of radius $R(\sigma-s)$ bounds the integrand by $4\alpha N\tau/[R(A-\tau)^2]$. Therefore
$$
\frac{A-r}{r}\left|\int_0^t\partial_xu(x,z)\,dz\right|
\leq\frac{4\alpha N}{R}\left[1+\frac{A-r}{r}\log(1-r/A)\right]
\leq\frac{4\alpha N}{R}.
$$
The integral is taken at fixed $x$, and the disc and segment lie inside the shrinking domain. Consequently $u\mapsto\int_0^t(iu_x+f)\,dz$ is a <contraction mapping> when $0<\alpha<R/4$ and the holomorphic source $f$ is bounded on the relevant closed polydisc. The source term has weighted norm at most $\alpha\sup|f|$, so the <Banach fixed-point theorem> gives a holomorphic solution with zero initial data.

= Zero sets in the unit disc
{parent=Order of a zero of a holomorphic function}

A nonzero <holomorphic function> on the <unit disc> can have exactly a prescribed sequence of zeros, counted with <multiplicity>, if and only if that sequence is locally finite in the disc. Necessity is the <identity theorem for holomorphic functions>. For sufficiency, remove a finite number $m$ of zeros at zero and enumerate the others as $a_n$, so $|a_n|\to1$. Use the <Weierstrass elementary factors> $E_n(t)=(1-t)\exp(\sum_{j=1}^nt^j/j)$. On every <compact> subdisc, eventually $|z/a_n|\le q<1$ and $|\log E_n(z/a_n)|\le q^{n+1}/((n+1)(1-q))$. Hence $z^m\prod_nE_n(z/a_n)$ converges locally uniformly and has exactly the prescribed zeros. Finite sequences simply give finite products.

= Natural boundary of a holomorphic function

= Natural boundary
{synonym}

A boundary of a domain of a <holomorphic function> is natural if the function has no analytic continuation through any of its points. For $f(z)=\sum_{n\ge1}z^{2^n}$, each root of unity of order a power of two is a singular boundary point: along $z=r\zeta$ all sufficiently late terms equal $r^{2^n}$ and their sum tends to infinity as $r\uparrow1$. = Zeta function regularization
{parent=Analytic continuation}

= Zeta regularization
{synonym}

Zeta function regularization assigns a finite value to a divergent spectral sum by first forming a convergent complex-power series and then using its <analytic continuation>. For positive frequencies $\omega_n$, continue $\sum_n\omega_n^{-s}$ to $s=-1$ to define a regulated frequency sum, when that continuation is regular there. This does not turn the divergent positive-term sum into an ordinary convergent sum. It is a useful subtraction convention for the <normal-ordering constant of a string> and for vacuum energies.

= Half-integer zeta-regularized mode sum
{parent=Zeta function regularization}
{title2=$\sum_{n\geq0}^{\mathrm{reg}}(n+\tfrac12)=\tfrac1{24}$}

For $r=n+1/2$, $n\geq0$, the convergent frequency zeta function, expressed through the <Riemann zeta function>, is $\sum_r r^{-s}=(2^s-1)\zeta_R(s)$ when $\operatorname{Re}s>1$. Its <analytic continuation> at $s=-1$ gives $(1/2-1)(-1/12)=1/24$. A common exponential frequency cutoff gives $\sum_r r e^{-\varepsilon r}=\varepsilon^{-2}+1/24+O(\varepsilon^2)$, so the same finite part follows by subtracting the leading divergence. In contrast, integer frequencies have regulated sum $-1/12$.

\qquad z\mapsto z^{-1}

= Injective entire functions are affine
{parent=Entire function}
{title2=$g(z)=az+b,\quad a\ne0$}

An injective <holomorphic function> cannot have an <essential singularity>: <Casorati-Weierstrass theorem> would give values from a small punctured neighbourhood inside the open image of a disjoint neighbourhood, contradicting injectivity. Apply this at infinity to an injective <entire function>. A removable singularity there would make the function constant by <Liouville's theorem>. A pole makes it a polynomial by the <Cauchy coefficient formula>; its derivative has no zero, so the <fundamental theorem of algebra> forces degree one.

= Exponential polynomial
{parent=Entire function}
{title2=$F(z)=\sum_jP_j(z)e^{\lambda_jz}$}

An <exponential polynomial> is a finite sum of <polynomial> multiples of exponentials. For distinct exponents, its terms are linearly independent over the <polynomial> ring: to isolate one term, apply the product of operators $(D-\lambda_j)^{\deg P_j+1}$ for all the other terms. On the remaining <polynomial> each operator becomes $D+\lambda_0-\lambda_j$, which is injective because its nonzero constant preserves the leading coefficient. A nonzero <exponential polynomial> is an <entire function> of exponential type. Its maximum-modulus <logarithm> is $O(R+\log R)$, and <Jensen's formula> gives at most $O(R)$ zeros, counted with multiplicity, in a disk of radius $R$.

= Order of an entire function
{parent=Entire function}
{title2=$\rho_f=\limsup_{R\to\infty}\log\log M_f(R)/\log R$}

For a nonconstant <entire function>, put $M_f(R)=\max_{|s|\leq R}|f(s)|$. Its order measures the exponent in its exponential growth. Bounds $\log M_f(R)=O(R\log R)$ imply order at most one; a matching lower bound along the positive real axis proves order exactly one. This distinction is stronger than mere entire analyticity and controls the genus in <Hadamard factorization>.

= Weierstrass factorization theorem
{c}
{parent=Entire function}
{title2=$F(z)=z^m e^{h(z)}\prod_j E_{p_j}(z/a_j)^{n_j}$}

A nonzero <entire function> can be described by its discrete zero locations and multiplicities, a product of <Weierstrass elementary factors> and a zero-free exponential factor. = Canonical product
{parent=Weierstrass factorization theorem}
{title2=$\prod_n E_{p_n}(z/a_n)$}

A canonical product builds a prescribed discrete zero set from <Weierstrass elementary factors>. Choose the orders $p_n$ so that the logarithms of tail factors converge uniformly on each compact subset. <Infinite product convergence from logarithmic tails> then gives an <entire function> with exactly those zeros, counted with the prescribed multiplicities. = Canonical product construction for zeros escaping to the disk boundary
{parent=Canonical product}
{title2=$F(z)=z^m\prod_{n\ge1}E_n(z/a_n)$}

For nonzero prescribed zeros $a_n$ with $|a_n|\to1$, the <Weierstrass elementary factors> of increasing order give the displayed <holomorphic function> on the <unit disc>; $m$ handles finitely many zeros at zero. On any compact disc, eventually $|z/a_n|\le q<1$, and $|\log E_n(z/a_n)|\le q^{n+1}/[(n+1)(1-q)]$. The <infinite product convergence from logarithmic tails> gives a nonvanishing tail. The finite initial factors supply exactly the prescribed zeros and <multiplicities>. No <Blaschke condition> is required unless boundedness is imposed.

= Boundary-adapted holomorphic zero factor
{parent=Canonical product}
{title2=$E_{a,w,K}(z)$}

For a proper plane <domain> $D$, choose $a\in D$ and $w\notin D$ attaining $\delta(a)=\operatorname{dist}(a,\mathbb C\setminus D)$. The <holomorphic function>
$$
E_{a,w,K}(z)=\frac{z-a}{z-w}\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^K\frac1k\left(\frac{a-w}{z-w}\right)^k\right)
$$
has exactly one zero in $D$, a simple zero at $a$. For $|z-w|>2\delta(a)$ its zero-free <holomorphic logarithm> satisfies
$$
\left|\log E_{a,w,K}(z)\right|\leq\sum_{k>K}\frac{2^{-k}}k\leq\frac{2^{-K}}{K+1}.
$$
Thus products of such factors can realize prescribed zeros approaching the boundary. If the zero has multiplicity $m$, choose the logarithmic error smaller by a factor $m$ before taking the $m$th power. On a compact subset of $D$, eventual uniform summability of these logarithms gives a nonvanishing product tail and a <holomorphic function> with precisely the requested zeros.

= Monodromy of the complex inverse sine
{parent=Branches of the complex inverse sine}

Let $w$ be a local value of the <complex inverse sine>. Since the limiting values at the two <branch points> are $\pi/2$ at $z=1$ and $-\pi/2$ at $z=-1$, <monodromy reflection at a square-root branch point> gives
$$
R_+(w)=\pi-w,
\qquad
R_-(w)=-\pi-w.
$$
Following a counterclockwise loop that encloses both branch points in the convention where $\sqrt{1-z^2}$ is positive just above $(-1,1)$ applies the two reflections in the order that gives
$$
R_-\circ R_+(w)=w-2\pi.
$$
The reverse orientation gives $w+2\pi$. Thus the translational part of the monodromy is generated by $2\pi$.

{parent=Exponential integral}
{title2=$J(\varepsilon)$}

= Gamma function has no zeros
{parent=Gamma function}
{c}

= Complex beta integral
{parent=Beta function}

= Meromorphic differential on a Riemann surface
{parent=Riemann surfaces}
{title2=$\omega=f(z)\,dz$}

A meromorphic differential is specified in each holomorphic chart by a meromorphic coefficient $f(z)$, with the coefficients transforming by the coordinate derivative so that the one-form agrees on overlaps. The derivative of a meromorphic function gives such a differential, and the ratio of two nonzero differentials is a meromorphic function. Orders of zeros and poles are independent of chart because coordinate derivatives are nonvanishing holomorphic factors. On a compact connected surface the sum of orders of a nonzero differential is $2g-2$, allowing its divisor to determine the genus.

= Hyperbolic Riemann surface in potential theory
{parent=Riemann surfaces}

A noncompact <Riemann surface> is hyperbolic in potential theory when its Green-function exhaustion has a finite limit, equivalently when it admits a positive <Green function on a Riemann surface>. This definition does not assume a disk uniformization. The <unit disk> is an example, since $-\log|z|$ is its positive Green function with pole zero. For simply connected surfaces this is equivalent to being conformally equivalent to the disk. For general surfaces, potential-theoretic hyperbolicity should not be conflated with the condition that the universal cover is the disk: some surfaces with disk universal cover have no positive Green function.

= Compact Riemann surface
{parent=Riemann surfaces}

A <Riemann surface> which is compact as a topological space. For a connected one of genus $g$, the <Riemann-Roch theorem> gives $g$ independent <holomorphic differential forms>. The <Riemann bilinear relations for a compact surface> make their normalized periods a full lattice in $\mathbb C^g$, and the <Abel-Jacobi map of a compact Riemann surface> turns this lattice into a complex torus encoding <divisor classes>.

= Abel-Jacobi map of a compact Riemann surface
{c}
{parent=Riemann surfaces}
{title2=$\operatorname{AJ}:C^{(d)}\to H^0(C,K_C)^\vee/H_1(C,\mathbb Z)$}

Integration of <holomorphic differential forms> along cycles embeds the first <homology group> as a full <Euclidean lattice> in $H^0(C,K_C)^\vee$. Fixing a base point, sum the path integrals to the points of an effective <complex analytic divisor>. Different path choices differ by that lattice. The <Abel theorem for divisors> identifies the fibres with <linear equivalence of divisors>.

= Abel theorem for divisors
{c}
{parent=Abel-Jacobi map of a compact Riemann surface}

= Abel's theorem
{c}
{synonym}

A degree-zero <complex analytic divisor> $E$ on a <compact Riemann surface> is principal exactly when its <Abel-Jacobi map of a compact Riemann surface> is zero. Normalize a <differential of the third kind> with residues equal to the coefficients of $E$. The <Riemann bilinear relations for a compact surface> express its $b$-periods as $2\pi i$ times the Abel integrals. If those integrals are in the period lattice, subtract a suitable integral multiple of the normalized <holomorphic differential forms>; all periods then lie in $2\pi i\mathbb Z$, so exponentiation gives the required <meromorphic function>. Conversely, the logarithmic differential of a <meromorphic function> has integer residues and periods in $2\pi i\mathbb Z$, giving a zero Abel class by the same calculation.

= Differential of the third kind
{parent=Riemann surfaces}

A meromorphic one-form on a <compact Riemann surface> with only simple poles is a differential of the third kind. Prescribed residues can be realized exactly when their sum is zero: in the residue exact sequence for $K_C(S)$, the obstruction in $H^1(C,K_C)\cong\mathbb C$ is the sum of residues. Subtracting a <holomorphic differential form> normalizes its $a$-periods. Integer residues and periods in $2\pi i\mathbb Z$ make the exponential of its integral a single-valued <meromorphic function> with those orders of zeros and poles.

= Riemann bilinear relations for a compact surface
{c}
{parent=Riemann surfaces}

= Riemann bilinear relations
{c}
{synonym}

For a <compact Riemann surface> with a <symplectic basis> $a_i,b_i$ of its first <homology group>, cutting along that basis and integrating a primitive gives
$$
\int_C\omega\wedge\eta=\sum_i\left(\int_{a_i}\omega\int_{b_i}\eta-\int_{b_i}\omega\int_{a_i}\eta\right)
$$
for closed smooth one-forms. For a <holomorphic differential form> $\omega$ and a <differential of the third kind> $\eta$, the corresponding boundary calculation gives
$$
\sum_i\left(\int_{a_i}\omega\int_{b_i}\eta-\int_{b_i}\omega\int_{a_i}\eta\right)=2\pi i\sum_P\operatorname{res}_P(\eta)\int_{P_0}^P\omega.
$$
The paths and representatives of the cycles are chosen in the same cut surface. Normalized <holomorphic differential forms> consequently have a symmetric period matrix with positive definite imaginary part.

= Three-branch-point regular surface cover
{parent=Riemann surfaces}
{title2=$\chi(N)=|T|(1/m+1/n+1/r-1)$}

For a finite group generated by $A,B$, glue sheets labeled by the group over the three-punctured <Riemann sphere> using left monodromy multiplication. Fill the punctures by the local power charts dictated by the orders $m,n,r$ of $A,B,AB$. Right multiplication by inverses defines the commuting deck action. Its nontrivial stabilizers are conjugates of the corresponding cyclic <subgroups>. The <Riemann-Hurwitz formula> gives the displayed <Euler characteristic>. A compatible metric can be averaged over the group; in genus at least two the invariant hyperbolic metric is canonical by the <uniformization theorem>.

= Orientation-reversing equivalence of branched-cover monodromy

= Teichmüller's uniqueness theorem

= Reich–Strebel inequality
{c}
{parent=Teichmüller map}
{title2=$K_0\leq\int_X\frac{|1+\mu_fq/|q||^2}{1-|\mu_f|^2}|q|$}

On closed <Riemann surfaces> of <genus> at least two, if a <Teichmüller map> $f_0:X\to Y$ has unit-area source differential $q$ and dilatation $K_0$, any <quasiconformal map> $f:X\to Y$ in the same <homotopy class> satisfies $K_0\leq\int_X|q|\,|1+\mu_fq/|q||^2/(1-|\mu_f|^2)$. The plus sign corresponds to a <Teichmüller map> with $\mu_{f_0}=k_0|q|/q$ and $K_0=(1+k_0)/(1-k_0)$. This fundamental inequality yields <Teichmüller's uniqueness theorem> by the pointwise <triangle inequality> and its equality case. See \a[https://userhome.brooklyn.cuny.edu/gardiner/A%20short%20course%20on%20Teichmuller%27s%20theorem.pdf][Gardiner and Hu, §5, equation (11)].

= Green-function exhaustion proof of disk uniformization
{c}
{parent=Uniformization theorem}

Exhaust a simply connected <hyperbolic Riemann surface in potential theory> by compact bordered Jordan domains $R_n$. Their <Green functions on a Riemann surface> $G_n(\cdot,p)$ have logarithmic poles and zero boundary values. The <Schwarz lemma> makes $F'(p)$ extremal among normalized disk-valued maps. If $F$ omitted $a\ne0$, taking a global <holomorphic square root> of a disk automorphism applied to $F$, then renormalizing at $p$, would multiply its derivative by $(1+|a|)/(2\sqrt{|a|})>1$. Thus $F$ is onto and gives the desired <biholomorphism>.

= Degree of the derivative of a rational function
{title2=$\deg f'$}
{parent=Degree of a rational map of the Riemann sphere}

Let a nonconstant rational function $f$ have degree $d$, let $r$ be its number of distinct finite poles, and let $m_\infty$ be its pole order at infinity, taken as zero when infinity is not a pole. \deg f'=
\begin{cases}
d+r-1,&m_\infty>0,\\
d+r,&m_\infty=0.
\end{cases}
$$
In particular, $d-1\leq\deg f'\leq2d$.

= Chordal metric
{parent=Riemann sphere}
{title2=$\chi(z,w)$}

The chordal metric on the <Riemann sphere> is $\chi(z,w)=|z-w|/\sqrt{(1+|z|^2)(1+|w|^2)}$ for finite points, with $\chi(z,\infty)=(1+|z|^2)^{-1/2}$. It is half the Euclidean chord distance between stereographic images on the unit sphere, so the triangle inequality follows from the Euclidean one. If $\rho$ is round spherical geodesic distance, then $\rho=2\arcsin\chi$ and $2\chi\leq\rho\leq\pi\chi$. These inequalities transfer uniform-continuity estimates between spherical and chordal distances, including at poles of meromorphic functions.

= Rational map is Lipschitz in the chordal metric
{parent=Chordal metric}

In finite coordinates the differential norm is $R^\#(z)=|R'(z)|(1+|z|^2)/(1+|R(z)|^2)$, extending continuously to the whole sphere. This uniform bound is useful for passing equicontinuity through compositions.

= Elliptic integral
{parent=Complex analysis}
{wiki}

An elliptic integral is an integral of a rational function of $x$ and $\sqrt{P(x)}$, where $P$ is a cubic or quartic <polynomial> with distinct roots. The standard first, second and third kinds describe the essential normal forms. Choosing the full endpoint yields complete versions such as the <complete elliptic integral of the first kind>.

= Elliptic integral of the first kind
{title2=$F(z,k)$}
{parent=Elliptic integral}

For a parameter $k$, one form of the incomplete elliptic integral of the first kind is
$$
F(z,k)=\int_0^z\frac{dt}{\sqrt{(1-t^2)(1-k^2t^2)}}.
$$
Its value depends on the choices of square-root branches and, under <analytic continuation> around the <branch points>, on the integration path.

= Lemniscatic integral
{parent=Elliptic integral of the first kind}
{title2=$F(z)=\int_0^z(1-w^4)^{-1/2}\,dw$}

Choose the <holomorphic square root> equal to one at zero on the <unit disc>. The resulting <elliptic integral> maps that disc conformally onto the <square> with vertices $\pm B,\pm iB$, where $B=\int_0^1dt/\sqrt{1-t^4}$. For the inverse <Cayley transform between the half-plane and disk> $h(z)=i(1+z)/(1-z)$, the square of the <derivative> of $F_1\circ h$, with $F_1'(w)=1/\sqrt{w(1-w^2)}$, is $2i/(1-z^4)$. Thus the two <square> maps differ by an affine change of coordinate.

= Complete elliptic integral of the first kind
{title2=$K(k)$}
{parent=Elliptic integral of the first kind}

For $0<k<1$, the complete elliptic integral of the first kind is
$$
K(k)=\int_0^1\frac{dt}{\sqrt{(1-t^2)(1-k^2t^2)}}.
$$

It is the complete first-kind member of the <elliptic integral> family.

= Logarithmic endpoint asymptotic of the complete elliptic integral
{parent=Complete elliptic integral of the first kind}
{title2=$K(m)=L+(1-m)(L-1)/4+O((1-m)^2L),\quad L=\log[4/\sqrt{1-m}]$}

Here $m$ is the parameter, equal to the squared modulus. Near $m=1$ the endpoint integrand is locally $(1-m+s^2)^{-1/2}$. Matching its integral to the outer secant integral gives $L$. The next correction is of order $(1-m)\log(1/(1-m))$. Keeping the parameter/modulus convention explicit prevents a factor-of-two error in the complementary small quantity.

= Complementary complete elliptic integral of the first kind
{title2=$K'(k)$}
{parent=Complete elliptic integral of the first kind}

For the complementary modulus $k'=\sqrt{1-k^2}$,
$$
K'(k)=K(k')
=\int_1^{1/k}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{(t^2-1)(1-k^2t^2)}}.
$$

= Elliptic function
{parent=Complex analysis}
{wiki}

An elliptic function is a <meromorphic function> with two real-linearly independent <periods> $\omega_1,\omega_2$. A <fundamental parallelogram of a period lattice>[fundamental cell] is a half-open parallelogram
$$
z_0+\{s\omega_1+t\omega_2:0\leq s,t<1\}.
$$
Opposite boundary integrals cancel. Consequently a nonconstant elliptic function has equally many zeros and poles in a cell, counted with multiplicity, and the sum of its pole residues in a cell is zero.

= Zero-pole sum of an elliptic function
{parent=Elliptic function}
{title2=$\sum_j a_j-\sum_j b_j\in\Lambda$}

For a nonzero <elliptic function> with <period lattice> $\Lambda$, its <zeros of a function> $a_j$ and <poles> $b_j$, repeated with their <multiplicities>, have equal total number and satisfy $\sum_j a_j-\sum_j b_j\in\Lambda$. Choose a fundamental parallelogram avoiding all zeros and poles on its boundary. The <argument principle> applied to $f'/f$ gives equality of the numbers. Applying the <residue theorem> to $zf'/f$ gives their difference of sums. Pairing opposite edges, the extra factors are the two lattice generators multiplied by integrals of $f'/f$ along an edge. These integrals are integer multiples of $2\pi i$, because the endpoints have the same nonzero function value. Thus the difference of sums is a lattice element. This links zeros of a pulled-back line to the <chord-and-tangent group law>.

= Elliptic divisor-sum identity
{title2=$\sum_j r_jz_j-\sum_l m_lp_l\in\Lambda$}
{parent=Elliptic function}

For a nonzero <elliptic function>, the weighted sum of its zero positions minus its pole positions belongs to the <period lattice>. Integrating $zf^{\prime}/f$ around a <fundamental parallelogram> proves this: translating opposite edges gives a lattice-linear combination of integrals of $f^{\prime}/f$, each an integer multiple of $2\pi i$. The unweighted integral proves that the zero and pole multiplicities have equal total.

= Period lattice
{title2=$\Lambda$}
{parent=Elliptic function}

Two real-linearly independent <periods> $\omega_1,\omega_2$ generate the period lattice
$$
\Lambda=\mathbb Z\omega_1+\mathbb Z\omega_2.
$$

= Fundamental parallelogram of a period lattice
{parent=Period lattice}

= Fundamental parallelogram
{synonym}

A fundamental parallelogram for $\Lambda=\mathbb Z\omega_1+\mathbb Z\omega_2$ is a translate of
$$
\{s\omega_1+t\omega_2:0\leq s,t<1\}.
$$
Its translates tile the <complex plane>.

= Nonconstant elliptic function has a pole
{parent=Elliptic function}

If an <elliptic function> had no <poles>, it would be <entire function>[entire]. It is bounded on the closure of a <fundamental parallelogram>, and periodicity then makes it bounded on the whole <complex plane>. The <Liouville theorem> would make it constant. Thus every nonconstant elliptic function has a pole.

= Jacobi elliptic functions

Their ratios give the other standard members. The <Jacobi elliptic sine> is the inverse of the <elliptic integral of the first kind>.

{parent=Jacobi elliptic functions}

The Jacobi elliptic sine is the local inverse of the <elliptic integral of the first kind>: if

u=\int_0^z\frac{dt}{\sqrt{(1-t^2)(1-k^2t^2)}},
$$
then $z=\operatorname{sn}(u,k)$. Its analytic continuation is a meromorphic doubly periodic function.

It is the sn member of the <Jacobi elliptic functions>.

For real $0<k<1$, the <period lattice> of $\operatorname{sn}(u,k)$ is generated by

\quad\hbox{and}\quad

$$
The periods arise by composing the <monodromy reflection at a square-root branch point>[monodromy reflections] of its inverse <elliptic integral of the first kind>.

= Value multiplicity of an elliptic function
{parent=Elliptic function}

For any finite value $a$, apply the argument principle to $f-a$ around a fundamental parallelogram whose boundary avoids zeros and poles. Periodicity cancels the opposite-edge integrals, so the number of solutions of $f(z)=a$ equals the fixed number of poles of $f$, counting multiplicities.

= Weierstrass functions
{c}
{parent=Elliptic function}
{wiki}

Associated with a <period lattice> are the Weierstrass sigma, zeta, eta and elliptic functions. They satisfy $\zeta=\sigma'/\sigma$ and $\wp=-\zeta'$; the eta constants record quasi-period increments of the <Weierstrass zeta function>.

= Weierstrass elliptic function
{title2=$\wp$}
{c}
{parent=Weierstrass functions}
{wiki}

For a <period lattice> $\Lambda\subset\mathbb C$, the Weierstrass elliptic function is the normally convergent lattice sum
$$
\wp_\Lambda(z)=\frac1{z^2}+\sum_{\omega\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\left(\frac1{(z-\omega)^2}-\frac1{\omega^2}\right).
$$
It is an even <elliptic function> with a double <pole> of principal part $(z-\omega)^{-2}$ and zero <residue> at every point $\omega$ of the <period lattice>.

= Weierstrass addition formula
{c}
{parent=Weierstrass elliptic function}
{title2=$\wp(z+w)=-\wp(z)-\wp(w)+\tfrac14[(\wp'(z)-\wp'(w))/(\wp(z)-\wp(w))]^2$}

For generic fixed $w$, regard the right side as an <elliptic function> of $z$. The apparent <pole> at zero cancels, giving limiting value $\wp(w)$. At $z=w$ the slope ratio is finite. At $z=-w$, its squared slope has the same double principal part as $\wp(z+w)$ and no <simple pole> term. These exhaust the possible <poles> because the <Weierstrass elliptic function> has degree two. The difference is therefore an entire <elliptic function>, hence constant by <Liouville theorem>, and its value at zero is zero. <Meromorphic> continuation extends the identity to the exceptional parameters.

= Weierstrass sigma function
{parent=Weierstrass elliptic function}
{c}
{title2=$\sigma_\Lambda(z)=z\prod_{\omega\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}(1-z/\omega)e^{z/\omega+z^2/(2\omega^2)}$}

For a <period lattice>, the displayed canonical product converges normally because its logarithmic tails are bounded by a constant times $|\omega|^{-3}$. It defines an odd entire function with simple zeros exactly at the lattice and derivative one at zero. Its logarithmic derivative is the <Weierstrass zeta function>, and the derivative of that logarithmic derivative is minus the <Weierstrass elliptic function>. Sigma is quasi-periodic rather than elliptic; balanced products of its translates construct meromorphic functions with prescribed <divisors> on a complex torus.

= Legendre relation for Weierstrass quasi-periods
{parent=Weierstrass sigma function}
{c}
{title2=$\eta_1\omega_2-\eta_2\omega_1=2\pi i$}

For an oriented lattice basis with $\operatorname{Im}(\omega_2/\omega_1)>0$, write $\eta_j=\zeta(z+\omega_j)-\zeta(z)$. These differences are constant because $\zeta'=-\wp$ is periodic. Integrating zeta around a fundamental cell gives the displayed relation by its one simple pole of residue one. Oddness and the logarithmic derivative then give $\sigma(z+\omega_j)=-e^{\eta_j(z+\omega_j/2)}\sigma(z)$. This fixes both the sign and the exponent convention in sigma-product constructions.

= Four totally ramified Weierstrass values
{parent=Weierstrass elliptic function}
{title2=$e_1,e_2,e_3,\infty$}

The <Weierstrass elliptic function> has degree two on its period torus. Its three distinct half-period values $e_j$ have double local preimages, and its <pole> is double, so these four values are totally ramified. This also follows from $\wp'^2=4\prod_j(\wp-e_j)$ and the principal part at the <pole>. A <Möbius transformation> with <pole> away from these values moves all four to finite values without changing local degrees.

= Termwise derivative proof of Weierstrass periodicity
{parent=Weierstrass elliptic function}
{title2=$\wp'(z)=-2\sum_{\omega\in\Lambda}(z-\omega)^{-3}$}

The <normal convergence of the Weierstrass elliptic-function series> allows its <derivative> to be written as an absolutely locally uniformly convergent sum of inverse cubes. For a <period lattice> $\Lambda$, this derivative sum is invariant under translation by any lattice vector, by reindexing. Consequently $\wp(z+\omega)-\wp(z)$ is constant; its apparent <poles> cancel. The <Weierstrass elliptic function> is even, so for a primitive lattice generator $\omega$, evaluation at $z=-\omega/2$ makes that constant zero. Applying this to two generators proves that this is a <periodic function>, namely $\wp$. This argument avoids separating the original convergent series into two divergent inverse-square sums.

= Weierstrass shift-difference identity by pole cancellation
{c}
{parent=Weierstrass elliptic function}
{title2=$[\wp(z-a)-\wp(z+a)][\wp(z)-\wp(a)]^2=\wp'(z)\wp'(a)$}

For $2a\notin\Lambda$, the apparent double poles at $z=\pm a$ on the left are canceled by its squared zero factor. Subtract the right side: the order-three principal parts at lattice points cancel by the even Laurent expansion of $\wp$, leaving at most a simple pole per period cell. An <elliptic function> has total residue zero in a period cell, so that possible pole is removable. The resulting entire periodic function is constant, and its oddness makes the constant zero. This proves the identity without presupposing the elliptic differential equation.

= Elliptic collinearity determinant
{parent=Weierstrass elliptic function}

The determinant of the columns $(1,\wp(z),\wp'(z))$, $(1,\wp(w),\wp'(w))$ and $(1,\wp(-z-w),\wp'(-z-w))$ vanishes identically. Its apparent cubic poles at $z=0,-w$ have order at most two because the coefficient multiplying the cubic-pole entry vanishes linearly. Column coincidences give six zeros, unless $3w$ is a lattice point, when they give four zeros with one multiple zero. In the half-period case, the two apparent zero locations at poles are removable zeros: $\wp'(w)=0$ and the two $1/z$ terms cancel, leaving $O(z)$. Thus the zero count exceeds possible pole order in every case. The <argument principle> proves the identity, which expresses collinearity of the three points on the <Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve>.

= Confluent elliptic collinearity determinant
{parent=Elliptic collinearity determinant}
{title2=$\det(c(u),c'(u),c(w)),\quad c(z)=(1,\wp(z),\wp'(z))^t$}

For distinct finite point classes on a complex <elliptic curve>, ordinary column determinants test whether a line meets the curve at those three points. Repeated columns instead vanish for a purely linear-algebraic reason. To prescribe a tangent intersection at $u$, replace the second equal column by its derivative; to prescribe a triple intersection, use $c(u),c'(u),c''(u)$. These confluent determinants vanish exactly when the corresponding degree-three intersection divisor has point sum zero, away from the affine chart's pole. A local homogeneous lift supplies the version at the origin.

= Elliptic function-field decomposition
{title2=$\mathbb C(\mathbb C/\Lambda)=\mathbb C(\wp)\oplus\wp^{\prime}\mathbb C(\wp)$}
{parent=Weierstrass elliptic function}

Every <elliptic function> has a unique expression $A(\wp)+B(\wp)\wp^{\prime}$ with <rational functions> $A,B$. Its even part is rational in the <Weierstrass elliptic function>; its odd part divided by $\wp^{\prime}$ is even and meromorphic, so is also rational in $\wp$. The local even Laurent series at a half-period and at the pole justify meromorphic descent through the double cover of the <Riemann sphere>.

= Even elliptic functions are rational in the Weierstrass function
{parent=Weierstrass elliptic function}
{title2=$f(-z)=f(z)\Longrightarrow f=Q(\wp)$}

The <Weierstrass elliptic function> is the quotient map from the complex torus by $z\mapsto-z$. An even <elliptic function> descends through this map; its even Laurent series at each branch point makes the descended function meromorphic there. A <meromorphic function> on the <Riemann sphere> is rational, so $f=Q(\wp)$.

= Degree-two even elliptic function
{parent=Even elliptic functions are rational in the Weierstrass function}
{title2=$f=(a\wp+b)/(c\wp+d)$}

An even <elliptic function> descends through the degree-two quotient map $\wp$ to a <rational function> $R$ on the <Riemann sphere>. If the original function also has degree two, composition of map degrees gives $2=2\deg R$, so $R$ has degree one. Consequently it is a <Möbius transformation> with $ad-bc\ne0$. The shift $\operatorname{sn}(z+K,k)$ is even and has degree two, providing an example for the common <period lattice> of the <Jacobi elliptic sine> and $\wp$.

= Normal convergence of the Weierstrass elliptic-function series

On every compact set disjoint from the lattice, the summand

= Half-period values of the Weierstrass elliptic function

At the three nonzero two-torsion points $z_i$ of $\mathbb C/\Lambda$, put $e_i=\wp(z_i)$. The values are distinct because $\wp(z)=\wp(w)$ exactly when $z\equiv\pm w\pmod\Lambda$.

= Weierstrass half-period translation formula
{c}
{parent=Half-period values of the Weierstrass elliptic function}
{title2=$\wp(z+h_i)=e_i+\frac{(e_i-e_j)(e_i-e_l)}{\wp(z)-e_i}$}

Let $h_i$ represent a nonzero <two-torsion point of a complex torus>, and write $e_i=\wp(h_i)$. The product $(\wp(z+h_i)-e_i)(\wp(z)-e_i)$ is an <elliptic function> whose apparent <poles> cancel against double <zeros>. It is therefore constant by <Liouville theorem>. Its value at $z=0$ is $\wp''(h_i)/2$. The <Weierstrass elliptic differential equation> gives $\wp''(h_i)/2=3e_i^2-g_2/4=(e_i-e_j)(e_i-e_l)$, proving the formula.

= Weierstrass quarter-period derivative identity
{c}
{parent=Weierstrass half-period translation formula}
{title2=$\frac{\wp'(z-h_i)}{\wp'(z)}=-\frac{(\wp(h_i/2)-e_i)^2}{(\wp(z)-e_i)^2}$}

At $z=h_i/2$, evenness and periodicity give $\wp(z+h_i)=\wp(z)$. The <Weierstrass half-period translation formula> therefore identifies $(\wp(h_i/2)-e_i)^2$ with $(e_i-e_j)(e_i-e_l)$. Differentiating the <Weierstrass half-period translation formula> proves the identity, interpreted as equality of <meromorphic functions>, including the apparent singularities of its quotients.

The Weierstrass zeta function satisfies $\zeta'=-\wp$ and is quasi-periodic: for every period $\omega$, the difference $\zeta(z+\omega)-\zeta(z)$ is constant. It has a simple pole of residue one at each lattice point. Consequently, if $\sum_jc_j=0$, then

= Laurent coefficients of the Weierstrass elliptic function
{parent=Weierstrass elliptic function}

Writing $G_{2r}=\sum_{\omega\ne0}\omega^{-2r}$ for the lattice Eisenstein sums, expansion about zero gives
$$
\wp(z)=\frac1{z^2}
+\sum_{r=2}^{\infty}(2r-1)G_{2r}z^{2r-2}.
$$
Thus the constant coefficient vanishes and, for $k\geq1$, the coefficient of $z^{2k}$ is $(2k+1)G_{2k+2}$.

= Positive polynomial recurrence for lattice Eisenstein sums
{parent=Laurent coefficients of the Weierstrass elliptic function}
{title2=$c_n=\frac{3}{(2n+3)(n-2)}\sum_{r=1}^{n-2}c_rc_{n-1-r}$}

Write the <Weierstrass elliptic function> as $\wp(z)=z^{-2}+\sum_{n\geq1}c_nz^{2n}$, with $c_1=3G_4$, $c_2=5G_6$ and $c_n=(2n+1)G_{2n+2}$. Differentiate the <Weierstrass elliptic differential equation> to obtain $\wp''=6\wp^2-g_2/2$. Comparing coefficients of $z^{2n-2}$ gives the displayed recurrence for $n\geq3$. Every denominator is positive, so induction expresses every higher <lattice Eisenstein series> as a polynomial in $G_4,G_6$ with positive rational coefficients on its occurring monomials. In particular $G_8=3G_4^2/7$ and $G_{10}=5G_4G_6/11$.

= Weierstrass elliptic differential equation
{c}
{parent=Weierstrass elliptic function}

The <Weierstrass elliptic function> satisfies
$$
\wp'(z)^2=4\wp(z)^3-g_2\wp(z)-g_3.
$$
Writing $G_{2r}=\sum_{\omega\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\omega^{-2r}$, the <Laurent coefficients of the Weierstrass elliptic function> give
$$
\wp(z)=z^{-2}+3G_4z^2+5G_6z^4+O(z^6),
\qquad
\wp'(z)=-2z^{-3}+6G_4z+20G_6z^3+O(z^5).
$$
Thus $g_2=60G_4$ and $g_3=140G_6$ cancel every nonremovable term in the <Laurent series> of $\wp'^2-4\wp^3+g_2\wp+g_3$ at each point of the <period lattice>. The resulting <elliptic function> is an <entire function> and is bounded, hence is zero by <Liouville theorem>.

= Nonvanishing discriminant of a complex lattice
{parent=Weierstrass elliptic differential equation}

The three nonzero half-period classes make the odd derivative of the <Weierstrass elliptic function> vanish. = Runge's theorem

{synonym}
{c}

A holomorphic function near a compact set can be uniformly approximated by rational functions with poles in prescribed complementary components; connected complement permits polynomials.

= Uniform rational approximation algebra with prescribed poles
{parent=Runge's theorem}

Let $K\subset\mathbb C$ be compact and permit polynomial terms and finite poles in $S\subset\mathbb C\setminus K$, meeting every bounded complementary component. In the uniform closure $A$ of these rational functions, the coordinate $u(z)=z$ has spectrum exactly $K$. Indeed the set of $\lambda\notin K$ with $(u-\lambda)^{-1}\in A$ is relatively open by invertibility and relatively closed by uniform continuity of the scalar resolvent; it meets each component by a prescribed pole, or by a Neumann expansion at infinity. It is therefore all of $\mathbb C\setminus K$. <Holomorphic functional calculus> then places every function holomorphic near $K$ in $A$, proving prescribed-pole <Runge approximation theorem>.

= Polynomial Runge theorem
{parent=Runge's theorem}

If $K\subset\mathbb C$ is compact with connected complement and $f$ is holomorphic near $K$, then for each $\varepsilon>0$ there is a polynomial $p$ such that $\sup_K|p-f|<\varepsilon$.

= Runge exhaustion of a slit disk
{c}
{parent=Polynomial Runge theorem}

For the unit disk with the nonpositive real radius removed, compact sets $K_m=\{re^{i\theta}:1/m\leq r\leq1-1/m,\ |\theta|\leq\pi-1/m\}$, $m\geq3$, exhaust the domain. Each has connected complement: its inner and outer complementary regions join through the missing angular sector. The <polynomial Runge theorem> gives a <polynomial> within $1/m$ of a given <holomorphic function> on $K_m$. Every compact subset lies in some $K_m$, so these polynomials converge uniformly on compact subsets.

= Pointwise polynomial approximation of a half-plane sign
{parent=Polynomial Runge theorem}

There is a sequence of complex <polynomials> converging pointwise on the whole plane to the sign of the imaginary part, with value zero on the real axis. For each $n$, take the <compact> union of the upper rectangle $[-n,n]+i[1/n,n]$, its lower reflection and the real segment $[-n,n]$. Its complement is <connected>. The function equal to one, minus one and zero on disjoint <neighborhoods> of the three components is <holomorphic>. The <polynomial Runge theorem> provides a <polynomial> within $1/n$ of it on the union. Every fixed point lies in its corresponding component for all large $n$, proving <pointwise convergence>. The limit is discontinuous, so the convergence cannot be locally uniform near the real axis. This illustrates how pointwise approximation by <holomorphic functions> differs from locally uniform approximation.

= Pole-moving polynomial approximation
{parent=Polynomial Runge theorem}

Let the uniform closure of polynomials on a compact set be an algebra. A reciprocal at a sufficiently distant pole belongs to it by a geometric series. A path of poles avoiding the compact set can be divided into short steps; expanding each reciprocal in powers of the previous one transfers polynomial approximability along the path.

= Polynomially approximable resolvent point
{parent=Polynomial Runge theorem}

For a closed set $K\subsetneq\mathbb C$, a point $\lambda\notin K$ is a polynomially approximable resolvent point when $z\mapsto(z-\lambda)^{-1}$ is a <uniform limit> on $K$ of complex polynomials.

= Resolvent propagation across a complementary component
{parent=Polynomially approximable resolvent point}

The polynomially approximable resolvent points form a subset that is both open and closed relative to $\mathbb C\setminus K$. Openness follows from the uniformly convergent expansion
$$
\frac1{z-\mu}
=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\mu-\lambda)^n}{(z-\lambda)^{n+1}}
$$
when $|\mu-\lambda|<\operatorname{dist}(\lambda,K)$; relative closedness follows from the uniform continuity of the resolvent as its pole varies away from $K$. Hence approximability at one point propagates throughout its connected component of $\mathbb C\setminus K$.

= Elementary polynomial approximation on a square
{parent=Polynomial Runge theorem}

Let $S$ be a closed square and let $f$ be holomorphic on a neighbourhood of a slightly larger square. The <Cauchy integral formula> on the larger boundary is uniformly approximated on $S$ by Riemann sums of resolvents whose poles lie outside $S$. Far-away resolvents have convergent geometric-series expansions in $z$, and <resolvent propagation across a complementary component> moves this polynomial approximability to every pole outside the square. Approximating the finitely many resolvents in each Riemann sum by polynomials proves polynomial approximation on $S$ without invoking the general <Runge theorem>.

= Polynomial approximation of the reciprocal on a proper circular arc
{parent=Polynomial Runge theorem}

A proper closed arc $S$ of a circle centered at zero has connected complement and stays away from zero. The polynomial Runge theorem therefore gives polynomials converging uniformly to $1/z$ on $S$.

= Explicit polynomial approximation of the reciprocal on the left semicircle
{parent=Polynomial approximation of the reciprocal on a proper circular arc}

On $K=\{z:|z|=1,\operatorname{Re}z\leq0\}$, the polynomials
$$
p_n(z)=-\frac14\sum_{j=0}^n\sum_{k=0}^{n^2}\binom{j+k}{k}\left(\frac z4\right)^k
$$
converge uniformly to $1/z$. Expand $1/z$ first in powers of $4/(z-4)$, whose modulus is at most $4/\sqrt{17}$ on $K$, and then expand each $(z-4)^{-j-1}$ in powers of $z/4$. The estimate $\binom{j+k}{k}\leq2^{j+k}$ controls the diagonal truncation.

= Polynomial approximation obstruction on a punctured circle
{parent=Polynomial Runge theorem}

Polynomials cannot converge uniformly to $1/z$ on $\{z:|z|=1,z\ne1\}$. Uniform convergence there makes the polynomials uniformly Cauchy on the full circle because deleting one point does not change the supremum of a continuous function. They would therefore converge uniformly to $1/z$ on the full circle, contradicting
$$
\oint p(z)\,dz=0,
\qquad
\oint\frac{dz}{z}=2\pi i.
$$

= Pointwise approximation by a Runge exhaustion
{parent=Polynomial Runge theorem}

To approximate a piecewise constant function pointwise, exhaust each of its separated regions by compact subsets whose finite union has connected complement. Apply polynomial Runge approximation to the locally constant holomorphic function on each stage, with errors tending to zero. Every fixed point eventually lies in all later stages, so the uniform stagewise estimates imply pointwise convergence.

= Winding number
{title2=$\operatorname{wind}(\gamma,w)$}
{parent=Complex analysis}
{wiki}

For a closed piecewise smooth curve $\gamma$ avoiding $w$,
$$
\operatorname{wind}(\gamma,w)
=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_\gamma\frac{dz}{z-w}.
$$
It is the net change of a continuous argument divided by $2\pi$ and is an integer.

= Winding number of a continuous closed path
{parent=Winding number}

If $\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$ is <continuous function>[continuous] and closed, choose a continuous lift $\theta$ such that
$$
\frac{\gamma(t)}{|\gamma(t)|}=e^{2\pi i\theta(t)}.
$$
Then $\operatorname{wind}(\gamma,0)=\theta(1)-\theta(0)\in\mathbb Z$. This agrees with the contour-integral definition when the path is piecewise smooth.

= Continuous logarithm lifting criterion
{parent=Winding number of a continuous closed path}
{title2=$f=e^g\iff\operatorname{wind}(f\circ\gamma,0)=0\text{ for every loop }\gamma$}

On a locally <path-connected> space, on each path component choose a value of a <complex logarithm> of $f$ at a base point. Continue it along paths using local logarithms. Independence of the path is exactly vanishing of the <winding number of a continuous closed path> for all loops. This constructs a <continuous function> $g$ with $f=e^g$. The implication from a logarithm to zero winding requires no local connectivity. For applications to arbitrary planar <compact> sets, apply the criterion on polygonal neighborhoods rather than assuming the <compact> set is locally <path-connected>.

= Homotopy invariance of winding number
{parent=Complex analysis}

A homotopy through closed paths avoiding the base point cannot change the integer winding number.

= Dominated perturbation preserves winding number
{parent=Homotopy invariance of winding number}

If closed continuous paths $\gamma,\phi$ satisfy $|\gamma(t)|>|\phi(t)|$ for every $t$, then $H(s,t)=\gamma(t)+s\phi(t)$ never vanishes. It is therefore a homotopy through closed paths in $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$, and
$$
\operatorname{wind}(\gamma+\phi,0)=\operatorname{wind}(\gamma,0).
$$

= Winding-number proof of the fundamental theorem of algebra
{parent=Homotopy invariance of winding number}

For a polynomial $P(z)=a_nz^n+\cdots+a_0$ of positive degree, on a sufficiently large circle its leading term dominates the remaining terms. The <dominated perturbation preserves winding number>[dominated-perturbation lemma] therefore gives winding number $n$ to the loop $P(Re^{2\pi it})$. If $P$ had no zero, radial contraction of the input circle would map under $P$ to a homotopy with a constant loop in $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$, which has winding number zero. This contradiction proves the <Fundamental theorem of algebra>.

= Winding-number proof of the no-retraction theorem
{parent=Homotopy invariance of winding number}

If a continuous retraction from a closed disc to its boundary existed, applying it to a contraction of the boundary circle inside the disc would give a homotopy in $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$ from a loop of winding number one to a constant loop of winding number zero. <Homotopy invariance of winding number> rules this out.

= Monodromy reflection at a square-root branch point
{parent=Branch point}

Let
$$

$$
where $P$ has a simple zero at $a$, and let $A$ be the limiting value of $W$ at $a$ on one sheet. <Analytic continuation> once around $a$ changes the sign of the square root and hence of $W'$. The continued primitive agrees at $a$ with the original one, so it is
$$

$$
Thus pairs of branch-point loops generate additive periods of an inverse function.

= Euler's formula
{parent=Complex number}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$}

For a real <angle> $\theta$ in <radians>, Euler's formula states
$$
e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta,
$$
where $i$ is the <imaginary unit>. It connects the <complex exponential> to the <sine> and <cosine> coordinates of the <unit circle>. In particular, $|e^{i\theta}|=1$, and multiplying by $e^{i\theta}$ rotates a <complex number> through angle $\theta$ without changing its <modulus>. At $\theta=\pi/3$ it gives $e^{i\pi/3}=1/2+i\sqrt3/2$; at $\theta=\pi$, it gives $e^{i\pi}=-1$.

The identity makes <angle> addition algebraic: $e^{i\alpha}e^{i\beta}=e^{i(\alpha+\beta)}$. Expanding the product and comparing <real parts> and <imaginary parts> recovers the <sine addition formula> and <cosine addition formula>. Differentiating $e^{i\omega t}$ gives $i\omega e^{i\omega t}$, and differentiating again gives $-\omega^2e^{i\omega t}$. Its real and imaginary coordinates therefore solve the <harmonic oscillator equation>. This is why <complex exponentials> are useful building blocks in <Fourier analysis>: differentiation acts on each frequency by simple multiplication.

\Image[circle-exponential-oscillator.png]
{title=Euler's formula turns circular motion into sine and cosine traces}

A short justification separates the even and odd powers in the absolutely convergent <power series> for $e^{i\theta}$. The even powers give $\sum_{n\geq0}(-1)^n\theta^{2n}/(2n)!=\cos\theta$, and the odd powers give $i\sum_{n\geq0}(-1)^n\theta^{2n+1}/(2n+1)!=i\sin\theta$. The <absolute convergence> permits this regrouping. The formula thus links an analytic definition of the <complex exponential> to a geometric motion rather than merely introducing a convenient notation.

<Leonhard Euler> presents the relation between imaginary exponentials and <sines> and <cosines> in his 1748 Introductio in analysin infinitorum, volume 1, chapter VIII, section 138. His derivation uses limiting powers after connecting trigonometric addition to algebra. The original Latin and Ian Bruce's English translation can be read together in \a[https://www.17centurymaths.com/contents/euler/introductiontoanalysisvolone/ch8vol1.pdf][Euler, Introductio, chapter VIII, section 138]{ref}.

= Cancellation of positive-integer gamma singularities on a Hankel contour
{parent=Residue extraction by a Hankel contour}

Keep the circular portion of a <Hankel contour> at fixed nonzero radius. = Odd square-root transform
{synonym}

Because $f(z)/z$ is holomorphic and nowhere zero on the <unit disc>, its <square root> with value one at zero exists. Defining $h(z)=z\sqrt{f(z^2)/z^2}$ gives an odd normalized <univalent function>. If $h(z)=h(w)$, squaring gives $z^2=w^2$; the possibility $z=-w$ and oddness force both to be zero. Applying the <area theorem for univalent functions> to $1/h(1/\zeta)$ yields $|a_2|\le2$.

= A univalent function has nonzero derivative
{parent=Univalent function}

For an <injective> <holomorphic function> $f$ on an <open subset> of $\mathbb C$, $f'(z)$ is nowhere zero. If $f(z)-f(z_0)=(z-z_0)^m h(z)$ with $h(z_0)\ne0$, choose a small <circle> where both $h$ and $mh+(z-z_0)h'$ have no zeros. The <argument principle> counts $m$ zeros of $f(z)-f(z_0)-w$ inside that <circle> for sufficiently small $w\ne0$: deforming $w$ to zero cannot change the count. All these zeros are simple, since $f'$ only vanishes at $z_0$ inside the <circle> and $z_0$ is not a zero when $w\ne0$. Injectivity forces $m=1$, and hence $f'(z_0)\ne0$.

= Locally uniform limit of univalent functions
{parent=Univalent function}

A <locally uniform convergence> limit of <univalent functions> on a domain is either constant or univalent. For a nonconstant limit $f$, suppose distinct $x,y$ satisfy $f(x)=f(y)$. Choose a small closed disk around $x$ excluding $y$ with $f-f(y)$ nonzero on its boundary. The <Rouche theorem> then forces $f_j-f_j(y)$ to have a zero in that disk for large $j$, contradicting injectivity of $f_j$. This supplies the injectivity step in an extremal proof of the <Riemann mapping theorem> without assuming it as a special lemma.

= Koebe function
{parent=Univalent function}
{c}
{title2=$k(z)=z/(1-z)^2$}

The Koebe function $k(z)=z/(1-z)^2$ is a <univalent function> from the <unit disc> onto $\mathbb C\setminus(-\infty,-1/4]$. To see the image, $(1+z)/(1-z)$ maps the disc onto the right half-plane and $k(z)=(((1+z)/(1-z))^2-1)/4$. Its normalization $k(0)=0$, $k'(0)=1$ proves sharpness of the <Koebe quarter theorem>.

= Boundary logarithmic mean of a univalent function
{parent=Univalent function}

If $\phi$ maps the unit disc conformally to a proper simply connected domain, the nonvanishing holomorphic quotient $F(w)=(\phi(w)-\phi(0))/w$ has harmonic log modulus. Its radial mean equals $\log|\phi'(0)|$. Boundary values are understood as radial limits: the standard integral-mean bound $\sup_{r<1}\int|\phi(re^{i\theta})|^p\,d\theta<\infty$ for $0<p<1/2$, together with the <Koebe distortion theorem> lower bound $|F(w)|\geq|\phi'(0)|/4$, gives <uniform integrability> of these logarithms. Hence
$$
\log|\phi'(0)|=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\log|\phi(e^{i\theta})-\phi(0)|\,d\theta.
$$

= Liouville theorem

= Liouville's theorem
{c}
{synonym}

Every bounded entire function is constant.

= Entire function confined to a half-plane is constant
{parent=Liouville theorem}

If an <entire function> $g$ satisfies $\operatorname{Re}(cg)\ge b$ for constants $c\ne0$ and real $b$, then $e^{-cg}$ is entire and has modulus at most $e^{-b}$. The <Liouville theorem> makes it constant, and differentiating gives $g'=0$ since the exponential never vanishes. The conclusion also applies after rotating and translating any containing half-plane; separate bounds on the real and imaginary parts are unnecessary.

= One-sided product bound for an entire function
{parent=Liouville theorem}

If an <entire function> $f=u+iv$ has $uv$ bounded above, then $f$ is constant. Indeed $F=\exp(-if^2)$ is entire and $|F|=\exp(2uv)$ is bounded, so the <Liouville theorem> makes $F$ constant. Differentiating gives $ff'=0$. The derivative of $f^2$ is therefore zero, and continuity on the connected complex plane makes $f$ itself constant. No separate boundedness of $u$ or $v$ is required.

= Dense image of a nonconstant entire function
{parent=Liouville theorem}

The image of every nonconstant entire function is dense in $\mathbb C$. If a disc about $w$ were omitted, then $1/(f-w)$ would be bounded and entire, so Liouville's theorem would make $f$ constant.

= Entire function under a horizontal inverse-square-root bound
{parent=Liouville theorem}

If an entire function satisfies $|h(z)|\leq|\operatorname{Re}z|^{-1/2}$ away from the imaginary axis, then $h=0$. Cauchy's formula on $|z|=R$ bounds every Taylor coefficient by a constant times
$$
R^{-n-1/2}\int_0^{2\pi}|\cos\theta|^{-1/2}\,d\theta,
$$
which tends to zero as $R\to\infty$.

= Polynomial growth theorem for entire functions
{parent=Liouville theorem}
{wiki}

An entire function bounded by a polynomial in the radius is itself a polynomial, by Cauchy estimates.

= Cauchy derivative formula
{parent=Complex analysis}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$f^{(n)}(w)=\frac{n!}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f(z)}{(z-w)^{n+1}}\,dz$}

Suppose $f$ is a <holomorphic function> on an <open set> containing the closed disc $|z-a|\le R$, with $R>0$. For any nonnegative integer $n$ and $|w-a|<R$, the counterclockwise boundary <contour> satisfies
$$
f^{(n)}(w)=\frac{n!}{2\pi i}\oint_{|z-a|=R}\frac{f(z)}{(z-w)^{n+1}}\,dz.
$$
The case $n=0$ is the <Cauchy integral formula>. On a smaller closed disc, the denominator stays uniformly away from zero, allowing repeated differentiation under the <contour integral>. This proves that a <holomorphic function> has complex <derivatives> of every order. \a[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-04-complex-variables-with-applications-spring-2018/b1a90df19f643b974555bbbb93138a48_MIT18_04S18_topic4.pdf][Orloff, MIT Topic 4, derivative formula]{ref}

At the centre, if $M=\max_{|z-a|=R}|f(z)|$, taking <absolute values> gives the <Cauchy estimate> $|f^{(n)}(a)|\le n!M/R^n$. Boundary size therefore limits interior variation. For $f(z)=z^2$ on the <unit disc>, $M=1$, and the $n=2$ estimate is exact: $|f''(0)|=2$. This is one quantitative reason that <holomorphic functions> are so rigid.

= Recovering an analytic derivative from the boundary real part
{parent=Cauchy derivative formula}
{title2=$f'(0)=(\pi r)^{-1}\int_0^{2\pi}\operatorname{Re}f(re^{i\theta})e^{-i\theta}\,d\theta$}

For a <holomorphic function> on a neighbourhood of a closed disc, parametrize the <Cauchy derivative formula> on its boundary. The conjugate <Cauchy integral theorem> gives $\int\overline{f(re^{i\theta})}e^{-i\theta}\,d\theta=0$. Replacing $f$ in the derivative integral by $f+\bar f=2\operatorname{Re}f$ therefore gives the displayed identity. The boundary real part determines every nonconstant analytic coefficient; only an imaginary additive constant is lost.

= Lipschitz bound inside a bounded analytic half-plane
{parent=Cauchy derivative formula}

If $f$ is analytic and bounded by $K$ on $\operatorname{Re}z>0$, then Cauchy's derivative estimate gives
$$
|f'(z)|\leq K/c
$$
on $\operatorname{Re}z>c$. Integrating along line segments gives a Lipschitz bound with the same constant in that smaller half-plane.

= Radial crossing test for polynomial root counts
{parent=Argument principle}
{title2=$\frac d{dt}\log|z(t)|=\operatorname{Re}\frac1{z\,p'(z)}$}

If $p(z(t))=t$ and $p'(z)\ne0$, implicit differentiation gives $z'(t)=1/p'(z)$. At a crossing of the <unit circle>, the displayed radial derivative determines whether the root enters or leaves the <disk> as the real parameter increases. Root counts are constant between such boundary crossings, including multiplicities. At the crossing parameter itself, boundary roots are excluded from an open-disk count. This complements the <winding number> form of the <argument principle> and handles a self-intersecting <image> <contour> without guessing its enclosed regions.

= Quarter-sector winding test for a real quartic
{parent=Argument principle}

Suppose a monic real <quartic polynomial> has no nonnegative real zero and its real part on the imaginary axis is everywhere positive. For sufficiently large $R$, its image of the quarter-circle arc from $R$ to $iR$, closed by the straight chord from $p(iR)$ to $p(R)$, has <winding number> one: compare with $z^4$ on the arc and its constant image on the chord. The rest of the actual sector boundary maps into the right half-plane, so its replacement by that chord preserves the winding number. The <argument principle> then counts exactly one root in the open first quadrant, including multiplicity.

= Rouche theorem
{synonym}
{c}

If $f,g$ are holomorphic near the closure of a bounded domain and $|g|<|f|$ on its boundary, then $f$ and $f+g$ have the same number of interior zeros, counted with multiplicity. Apply the argument principle to the zero-free boundary homotopy $f+tg$.

= Rouché localization of polynomial roots
{parent=Rouché's theorem}
{c}
{title2=$\sup_{|z-r_j|=\delta}|H(z)|<\delta\prod_{\ell\ne j}(|r_j-r_\ell|-\delta)$}

Let $P(z)=\prod_\ell(z-r_\ell)$ have distinct roots. Choose a disc around $r_j$ with radius $\delta$ smaller than every separation from $r_j$. On its boundary, $|P(z)|\geq\delta\prod_{\ell\ne j}(|r_j-r_\ell|-\delta)$. If a <holomorphic> perturbation $H$ satisfies the displayed inequality, <Rouché's theorem> shows that $P+H$ has exactly one root in that disc, counted with multiplicity. Discs confined to specified half-planes or quadrants give direct geometric root-location proofs without needing a global root-continuation argument.

= Hadamard three-circle theorem
{parent=Maximum modulus principle}
{c}
{title2=$M(r)\leq M(r_0)^{1-\theta}M(r_1)^\theta,\quad\theta=\log(r/r_0)/\log(r_1/r_0)$}

For a holomorphic scalar function on a neighborhood of a closed annulus, $M(r)$ is the maximum modulus on its radius-$r$ circle. The logarithm of its modulus is <subharmonic>; compare it with the harmonic function affine in $\log|z|$ which takes the logarithms of the two boundary maxima. = Logarithmically divergent derivative at a regular singular endpoint

= Logarithmically divergent endpoint derivative
{synonym}

For $xy''+a(x)y=0$ with analytic coefficient and a continuous solution having $a(0)y(0)\ne0$, the equation gives $y''\sim-a(0)y(0)/x$. Integrating towards the endpoint gives the displayed logarithmic divergence of the <derivative>. A bounded solution value therefore does not imply a finite <derivative> at a <regular singular point>.

= Frobenius method
{c}
{parent=Regular singular point}
{wiki}

Near a regular singular point $x_0$, the Frobenius method seeks
$$
y=(x-x_0)^r\sum_{n\geq0}a_n(x-x_0)^n.
$$
The lowest power gives the indicial equation for $r$, and the remaining powers give a coefficient recurrence.

= Undetermined coefficient at Frobenius resonance
{parent=Frobenius method}

When two <indicial roots> differ by a positive integer, the recurrence for the smaller-root <Frobenius solution> can have a zero coefficient at the index of the larger root. If the corresponding right-hand side also vanishes, that coefficient is free and adds the larger-root solution; if it does not vanish, a pure Frobenius series is obstructed and a logarithmic term may be required. For $x^2y''-2xy'-x^2y=0$, the recurrence $n(n-3)a_n=a_{n-2}$ has $a_1=0$ and leaves $a_3$ free. Choosing $a_3=0$ selects the even solution, not the only solution with exponent zero.

= Square-root reduction of a regular-singular differential equation
{parent=Frobenius method}
{title2=$4zy''+2y'+y=0$}

With $s=\sqrt z$ and $Y(s)=y(s^2)$, the displayed <linear differential equation> becomes $Y_{ss}+Y=0$ away from zero. Its <Frobenius solutions> have exponents $0$ and $1/2$: $\cos\sqrt z=\sum_{n\geq0}(-1)^nz^n/(2n)!$ and $\sin\sqrt z=z^{1/2}\sum_{n\geq0}(-1)^nz^n/(2n+1)!$. The first is an entire function of $z$; the second needs a local square-root branch. This illustrates how a <regular singular point> can arise from a degenerate change of independent variable.

= Frobenius solution
{c}
{parent=Frobenius method}
{title2=$y(z)=z^r\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_nz^n$}

A local series solution at a regular singular point, with exponent $r$ satisfying the indicial equation. Resonant exponents can require a logarithmic companion solution.

= Hyperbolic reduction of a regular-singular differential equation
{parent=Frobenius method}
{title2=$y=xu\ \Longrightarrow\ u''-u=0$}

The equation $x^2y''-2xy'+(2-x^2)y=0$ has a <regular singular point> at zero. Factoring $y=xu$ cancels the Euler derivative terms and leaves $u''-u=0$ away from zero. Its analytic solutions $x\cosh x$ and $x\sinh x$ extend across zero. Their coefficient recurrence $(n-1)(n-2)c_n=c_{n-2}$ has independent free coefficients $c_1,c_2$, giving two entire solutions without a logarithmic branch despite integer-separated indicial roots.

= Terminating Frobenius series
{parent=Frobenius method}

A terminating Frobenius series is a <Frobenius method> solution whose analytic <power series> factor has only finitely many nonzero coefficients. Its coefficient <linear recurrence relation> must allow the first omitted coefficient and all following coefficients to be zero. At an integer separation of <characteristic exponents at a regular singular point>, a resonant coefficient equation can become $0=0$, permitting such a choice rather than forcing a logarithmic term. With exponent zero, termination produces a <polynomial> solution.

{c}

= Hypergeometric connection formula at one
{parent=Gauss hypergeometric equation}

{synonym}

{}_2F_1(a,b;c;z)=\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(d)}{\Gamma(c-a)\Gamma(c-b)}{}_2F_1(a,b;1-d;1-z)+\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(-d)}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}(1-z)^d{}_2F_1(c-a,c-b;1+d;1-z).

= Hypergeometric function

= Gauss hypergeometric function
{c}
{synonym}

The Gauss hypergeometric function is the normalized analytic solution at zero,
$$
{}_2F_1(a,b;c;z)
=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a)_n(b)_n}{(c)_n}\frac{z^n}{n!},
$$
initially for $|z|<1$ and $c\notin\{0,-1,-2,\ldots\}$, followed by <analytic continuation> where possible.

= Euler integral for the hypergeometric function
{parent=Hypergeometric function}

= Second local hypergeometric solution
{parent=Gauss hypergeometric equation}

When $C$ is not an integer, a second local solution at zero is $u^{1-C}F(A-C+1,B-C+1;2-C;u)$.

= Hypergeometric connection formula at infinity
{parent=Gauss hypergeometric equation}

Away from resonant parameter cases, two independent local solutions at infinity are
$$
z^{-a}F(a,1+a-c;1+a-b;z^{-1})
$$
and the expression obtained by exchanging $a$ and $b$. Every analytic continuation of a hypergeometric solution into their common domain is a constant linear combination of this basis.

= Hypergeometric cancellation identity
{parent=Gauss hypergeometric equation}

The binomial series gives $F(A,C;C;u)=(1-u)^{-A}$ wherever the defining series converges, followed by analytic continuation.

= Elementary specialization of a hypergeometric solution
{parent=Hypergeometric cancellation identity}

Special parameter choices can identify a hypergeometric solution with an elementary solution of the same second-order differential equation by matching its local normalization.

= Hypergeometric cosine identity
{parent=Elementary specialization of a hypergeometric solution}

For compatible local branches near $x=0$,
$$
F\left(\frac{k}{2},-\frac{k}{2};\frac12;\sin^2x\right)=\cos(kx).
$$
It follows by transforming the <harmonic oscillator equation> $y''+k^2y=0$ into the <Gauss hypergeometric equation> and selecting the solution with value one and derivative zero at the origin.

= Hypergeometric sine identity
{parent=Elementary specialization of a hypergeometric solution}

For $k\ne0$ and compatible local branches near $x=0$,
$$
F\left(\frac{1+k}{2},\frac{1-k}{2};\frac32;\sin^2x\right)
=\frac{\sin(kx)}{k\sin x}.
$$
This is obtained from the <second local hypergeometric solution> at zero and its normalization.
