= Solution
{parent=2008/A/1/a}

For a <matrix Lie group>, $T_gG$ consists of derivatives $\gamma'(0)$ of differentiable curves in $G$ with $\gamma(0)=g$, viewed inside $M_n(\mathbb C)$ as a real vector space. At the identity, multiplying two such curves adds their derivatives, and reparametrizing a curve multiplies its derivative by any real scalar. Thus $L(G)=T_IG$ is a real vector subspace.

If $Y\in L(G)$ and $g\in G$, conjugating a curve with derivative $Y$ proves $gYg^{-1}\in L(G)$. Let $g(s)$ have $g(0)=I$ and $g'(0)=X\in L(G)$. Differentiating the curve $g(s)Yg(s)^{-1}$ inside the finite-dimensional, hence closed, vector subspace $L(G)$ gives
$$
XY-YX\in L(G).
$$
The matrix <commutator> is real bilinear, antisymmetric, and satisfies the <Jacobi identity> by associativity of matrix multiplication. This proves that **$L(G)$ is a real <Lie algebra>**.

The exponential-map theorem for a linear <Lie group> says $\exp X\in G$ whenever $X\in L(G)$. Applying it to every real multiple of $X$ gives $\exp(tX)\in G$ for every real $t$. Conversely that curve has derivative $X$ at zero, so $X\in L(G)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{X\in L(G)\iff\exp(tX)\in G\ \text{for every }t\in\mathbb R}.
$$

Here the <orthogonal group> is the real <group> $O_n(\mathbb R)$. Differentiating $g(t)^Tg(t)=I$ at zero gives $X^T+X=0$. Conversely a real <skew-symmetric matrix> satisfies $(e^{tX})^Te^{tX}=e^{-tX}e^{tX}=I$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{L(O_n)=\{X\in M_n(\mathbb R):X^T=-X\},\qquad\dim_{\mathbb R}L(O_n)=n(n-1)/2}.
$$
For $n\ge2$ this is not a complex Lie subalgebra of $M_n(\mathbb C)$: multiplying a nonzero real member by $i$ leaves this real subspace. For $n=1$ the zero algebra is the trivial complex <Lie algebra> as well. The complex <orthogonal group> would be a different <group> from the real <orthogonal group> used here.

Differentiating the affine block form gives
$$
\boxed{L(\operatorname{Aff}_n(\mathbb C))=
\left\{\begin{pmatrix}B&u\\0&0\end{pmatrix}:B\in M_n(\mathbb C),\ u\in\mathbb C^n\right\}}.
$$
Conversely the exponential of such a block matrix has bottom row $(0,1)$ and invertible upper-left block $e^{tB}$, so it remains in the <affine group>. Its <commutator> corresponds to
$$
[(B,u),(C,v)]=([B,C],Bv-Cu).
$$
The space is closed under multiplication by $i$ and its bracket is complex bilinear. Thus it is a **complex <Lie algebra> of dimension $n^2+n$**, and its real dimension is $2n^2+2n$.

For fixed $X\in L(G)$, set $b(t)=\phi(e^{tX})$. The <homomorphism> property gives $b(s+t)=b(s)b(t)$ and $b(0)=I$. Its initial derivative is $L(\phi)(X)=D\phi_I(X)$. The <one-parameter subgroup> uniqueness theorem gives
$$
b(t)=e^{tL(\phi)(X)},\qquad\boxed{\phi(e^X)=e^{L(\phi)(X)}}.
$$
For the <adjoint representation>, $\operatorname{Ad}_gY=gYg^{-1}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ad}_XY=\left.\frac d{dt}(e^{tX}Ye^{-tX})\right|_{t=0}=XY-YX=[X,Y]}.
$$
The differential $L(\phi)$ is a real linear map of Lie algebras; the bracket identity follows by differentiating the compatibility of conjugation with $\phi$.

Write $\mathfrak g_{\mathbb C}=L(G_{\mathbb C})$ and $\mathfrak h=L(H)$. A root is a nonzero weight $\alpha\in\mathfrak h^*$ of the adjoint action. Its <root space> is
$$
\mathfrak g_\alpha=\{Y\in\mathfrak g_{\mathbb C}:[Z,Y]=\alpha(Z)Y\text{ for all }Z\in\mathfrak h\}.
$$
At the <group> level these weights come from nontrivial characters of the Cartan <subgroup> acting by conjugation. The Cartan <root-space decomposition> is
$$
\mathfrak g_{\mathbb C}=\mathfrak h\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Phi}\mathfrak g_\alpha.
$$
The zero-<weight space> is $\mathfrak h$; for the complexification of a <compact> <group> the algebra is reductive, with any centre contained in this zero-<weight space>.

For the product in question,
$$
G_{\mathbb C}=SL_2(\mathbb C)\times SL_2(\mathbb C),\qquad
\mathfrak g_{\mathbb C}=\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb C)\oplus\mathfrak{sl}_2(\mathbb C).
$$
Take the diagonal <Cartan subalgebra> generated by $H_1=(\operatorname{diag}(1,-1),0)$ and $H_2=(0,\operatorname{diag}(1,-1))$. On $Z=xH_1+yH_2$, define $\alpha_1(Z)=2x$ and $\alpha_2(Z)=2y$. The roots are exactly
$$
\boxed{\Phi=\{\alpha_1,-\alpha_1,\alpha_2,-\alpha_2\}}.
$$
If $E=E_{12}$ and $F=E_{21}$, their four root spaces are respectively $\mathbb C(E,0)$, $\mathbb C(F,0)$, $\mathbb C(0,E)$, and $\mathbb C(0,F)$. These four lines and the two-dimensional diagonal subalgebra form the full six-dimensional decomposition. There are no mixed roots: the two direct summands commute. This is the <root system> $A_1\times A_1$.

With the convention applicable also to disconnected <compact> groups, the <Weyl group> is $W=N_G(T)/T$, where the <normalizer> consists of elements taking $T$ to itself by conjugation. Put $\mathfrak t=L(T)$ and $\Lambda=\ker(\exp:\mathfrak t\to T)$. This is a full <lattice> with $T\cong\mathfrak t/\Lambda$.

If $\psi$ is a <torus> automorphism, exponential compatibility gives $\psi(\exp X)=\exp(D\psi_I X)$. Therefore $D\psi_I$ sends $\Lambda$ into itself; applying the inverse proves it sends the <lattice> onto itself. In a <lattice> <basis> this derivative is an element of $GL_r(\mathbb Z)$.

Conjugation by a <connected> <subgroup> $Q\subset N_G(T)$ induces a <continuous> family of these <integer> matrices. Since $GL_r(\mathbb Z)$ is discrete, this family is constant and equals its value at the identity. Its action on $\mathfrak t$ is therefore trivial, and surjectivity of the <torus> exponential shows that **$Q$ centralizes $T$**.

Apply this to the <identity component> $N_G(T)^0$. It lies in $G^0$ and centralizes $T$. The allowed maximal-abelian property of $T$ in $G^0$ gives $N_G(T)^0\subseteq T$, while the <connected> <group> $T$ lies in that <identity component>. Thus $N_G(T)^0=T$. The <normalizer> is a closed <compact> Lie <subgroup>, so its component quotient is discrete and <compact>, hence finite. Consequently **$W$ is finite and discrete**. For disconnected $G$, its action on the <lattice> need not be faithful; the component argument is essential.

The distinction is recorded in <Weyl group of a disconnected compact Lie group>.

= Solution

Use the given <torus> of independent rotations in $n$ mutually <orthogonal> two-planes and a fixed final axis. A <normalizer> element permutes the two-plane rotation characters, and on each plane either preserves or reverses its <orientation>. Its induced action therefore permutes the $n$ angle coordinates and changes their signs independently.

Every such signed permutation is realized. Permutations of two-dimensional blocks have <determinant> one. A reflection in any chosen plane reverses that angle; reflecting the remaining one-dimensional axis as necessary restores total <determinant> one. The kernel consists precisely of independent plane rotations: commuting with a generic <torus> element forces preservation of its distinct rotation planes, and the <determinant> condition then fixes the final axis. Thus the kernel is $T$ and
$$
\boxed{W\cong(C_2)^n\rtimes S_n,\qquad |W|=2^nn!}.
$$
This is the signed permutation <group>, the <Weyl group> of type $B_n$.

As usual here, representations are finite-dimensional and <continuous>. Start with any positive definite <Hermitian form> $h_0$ and normalized <Haar measure> $\mu$ on the <compact> <group>. Define
$$
h(v,w)=\int_Gh_0(gv,gw)\,d\mu(g).
$$
This is Hermitian, sesquilinear, and positive definite: for $v\ne0$ its <continuous> integrand $h_0(gv,gv)$ is strictly positive. Bi-invariance of <Haar measure> shows $h(av,aw)=h(v,w)$ for every $a\in G$.

If $U\subset V$ is invariant, then so is its <orthogonal complement>: for $v\perp U$ and $u\in U$, $h(av,u)=h(v,a^{-1}u)=0$. Hence $V=U\oplus U^\perp$ as a representation. Induct on dimension, splitting whenever a nontrivial proper <invariant subspace> exists. This proves **every such representation is a direct sum of irreducible representations**.

= Solution
{parent=2008/A/4/b}

Schur <orthogonality> for irreducible complex characters of a <compact> <group>, with normalized <Haar measure>, is
$$
\langle\chi_\alpha,\chi_\beta\rangle:=\int_G\chi_\alpha(g)\overline{\chi_\beta(g)}\,d\mu(g)=\delta_{\alpha\beta}.
$$
Equivalent representations clearly have equal characters, since trace is invariant under conjugation. Conversely complete reducibility gives $\chi_V=\sum_\alpha m_\alpha\chi_\alpha$, where the nonnegative <integer> multiplicity is recovered by $m_\alpha=\langle\chi_V,\chi_\alpha\rangle$. Equal characters thus give equal multiplicities of every irreducible constituent, hence **equivalent representations**.

= Solution
{parent=2008/A/4/c}

The usual <Weyl character formula> requires **a <compact> <connected> <Lie group>**. Choose a <maximal torus> $T$, a positive <root system> $\Phi^+$, <Weyl group> $W$, and $\rho=\frac12\sum_{\alpha\in\Phi^+}\alpha$. Irreducible representations are indexed by dominant integral highest weights $\lambda$ belonging to the <character> <lattice> of the actual <group> $T$. For $X\in L(T)$ such that $\exp X$ is regular,
$$
\boxed{\chi_\lambda(\exp X)=
\frac{\sum_{w\in W}\det(w)e^{w(\lambda+\rho)(X)}}
{\sum_{w\in W}\det(w)e^{w\rho(X)}}}.
$$
Weights are written as linear functionals on the complexified <torus> algebra; their values on the <compact> real <torus> algebra are imaginary. The denominator is $e^{\rho(X)}\prod_{\alpha>0}(1-e^{-\alpha(X)})$. If $\rho$ is not itself a <character> of $T$, the individual expressions are interpreted on its covering algebra; their quotient is single-valued and descends to $T$. The apparent singularities at nonregular elements are removable, and conjugacy into a <maximal torus> determines the <character> on the whole <connected> <group>.

The formula explicitly determines all irreducible characters from their highest weights, and its limit at the identity gives the dimension formula
$$
\dim V_\lambda=\prod_{\alpha>0}\frac{\langle\lambda+\rho,\alpha^\vee\rangle}{\langle\rho,\alpha^\vee\rangle}.
$$
<Character> multiplication and <orthogonality> then compute tensor-product multiplicities. For $SU_2$, highest weights are integers $m\ge0$, and at $\operatorname{diag}(z,z^{-1})$,
$$
\chi_m=\frac{z^{m+1}-z^{-m-1}}{z-z^{-1}}=z^m+z^{m-2}+\cdots+z^{-m},\qquad\dim V_m=m+1.
$$
<Connectedness> cannot simply be omitted from this version. For a nontrivial finite <group> the <maximal torus> is trivial, yet characters can differ away from the identity. The root data on that <torus> alone cannot supply the characters of all disconnected <compact> groups.

One definition of $T_xM$ is the space of derivations at $x$: linear maps $v:C^\infty(M)\to\mathbb R$ satisfying $v(fh)=f(x)v(h)+h(x)v(f)$. In local coordinates $u^1,\ldots,u^n$, each has the unique form $v=\sum_iv^i\partial/\partial u^i|_x$, identifying $T_xM$ with $\mathbb R^n$. Equivalently it is the space of velocities of curves through $x$, identifying curves with the same coordinate derivative.

For a chart $\phi:U\to\mathbb R^n$, give $TM|_U$ coordinates $(x,v)\mapsto(\phi(x),D\phi_xv)$. If $F=\psi\circ\phi^{-1}$ is a base transition, the induced transition is
$$
(u,v)\longmapsto(F(u),DF_u v).
$$
This is a smooth bijection with smooth inverse coming from the reverse chart. The resulting bundle atlas makes $TM$ a <smooth manifold> of dimension $2n$. These local product charts also give the usual <Hausdorff> and second-countable <topology>, inherited from a countable base atlas and its fibre coordinates.

For $n\ge1$, identify nonzero vectors in each fibre up to nonzero real scalar multiplication. Locally this gives $P(TM)|_U\cong U\times\mathbb{RP}^{n-1}$. In a projective chart where $v^j\ne0$, use the ratios $v^i/v^j$ for $i\ne j$. Under a base change, the fibre line maps by $[v]\mapsto[DF_uv]$; its projective coordinates are ratios of smooth linear expressions with nonzero denominator on the overlap. This defines a <smooth manifold> of dimension $n+(n-1)=2n-1$.

For the sphere, a point is a pair $(x,L)$ with $|x|=1$ and an unoriented line $L\subset x^\perp$. Choose a unit $v\in L$. The triple $(x,v,x\times v)$ is a positively oriented orthonormal frame. Given a second pair, the unique <orientation>-preserving linear map between chosen frames lies in $SO_3$ and carries the first pair to the second. Thus the action is transitive.

A rotation stabilizing $(x,L)$ fixes $x$, so rotates its tangent plane. To preserve the unoriented line it must act by $v\mapsto v$ or $v\mapsto-v$, giving exactly the identity and a half-turn about $x$. Hence **the <stabilizer> has order two**, and $P(TS^2)\cong SO_3/C_2$.

This is an example of the <projectivized tangent bundle>.

In a <coordinate chart>, define the <exterior derivative> by
$$
d\left(\sum_Ia_I\,dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_r}\right)
=\sum_Ida_I\wedge dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_r}.
$$
The chain rule makes this independent of coordinates. It is real linear, increases form degree by one, agrees with the differential on functions, satisfies $d^2=0$, and obeys the <graded Leibniz rule>
$$
d(\alpha\wedge\beta)=d\alpha\wedge\beta+(-1)^r\alpha\wedge d\beta.
$$
It also commutes with <pullback> by a <smooth map>. These properties characterize it locally and hence globally.

<De Rham cohomology> is the quotient of closed forms by exact forms. If $d\alpha=d\beta=0$, Leibniz gives $d(\alpha\wedge\beta)=0$. Changing $\alpha$ to $\alpha+d\eta$ changes the product by $d(\eta\wedge\beta)$; changing $\beta$ to $\beta+d\xi$ changes it by $(-1)^rd(\alpha\wedge\xi)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{[\alpha][\beta]=[\alpha\wedge\beta]}
$$
is independent of representatives. This gives the graded-commutative <de Rham cohomology> ring.

Use the standard convention that the <compact> <manifold> is nonempty and has no boundary. Orient it by $\omega^m$. Since $d\omega=0$, every power $\omega^k$ is closed. If $\omega^k=d\eta$ for some $1\le k\le m$, then

\omega^m=d(\eta\wedge\omega^{m-k}),
$$
again by Leibniz and closedness. <Stokes theorem> would give $\int_M\omega^m=0$. But this integral is strictly positive in the symplectic <orientation>, a contradiction. Therefore
$$
\boxed{[\omega^k]\ne0\in H^{2k}_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)\quad(1\le k\le m)}.
$$
The boundaryless hypothesis matters: a <compact> disk has a symplectic area form but no nonzero ordinary second cohomology class.

Let the two oriented surfaces carry positive area forms $\alpha$ and $\beta$, obtainable from oriented Riemannian metrics. A top-degree form on a surface is automatically closed. On their product set

It is closed, and

For <compact> <connected> oriented $n$-manifolds without boundary, the degree is characterized by
$$
\int_MF^*\eta=\deg(F)\int_N\eta
$$
for every smooth top-degree form $\eta$. Equivalently, at any <regular value> $y$,
$$
\deg(F)=\sum_{x\in F^{-1}(y)}\operatorname{sign}\det D F_x.
$$
The <preimage> is finite by <compactness> and the <inverse function theorem>. Taking a form supported in a small neighbourhood of a <regular value> and applying change of variables proves the signed-count formula. The top <de Rham cohomology> of a <compact> <connected> oriented <manifold> is one-dimensional, with integration giving its coordinate; <pullback> and <Stokes theorem> make the integral characterization independent of the chosen normalized form. Thus this signed count is independent of the <regular value>. In particular a nonzero degree forces surjectivity, and the number of points in any regular fibre is at least the absolute degree. Degree is invariant under <homotopy>, as follows by applying <Stokes theorem> to the <pullback> on $M\times[0,1]$.

For the <polynomial> assertion take $k\ge1$. The map $P:\mathbb C\to\mathbb C$ extends smoothly to a map of the <Riemann sphere> fixing infinity: in reciprocal coordinates its target coordinate is
$$
\frac1{P(1/\zeta)}=\frac{\zeta^k}{1+a_1\zeta+\cdots+a_k\zeta^k}.
$$
The <homotopy> $P_t(z)=z^k+t(a_1z^{k-1}+\cdots+a_k)$ extends smoothly in the same way. The map $z\mapsto z^k$ has $k$ distinct inverse images of the <regular value> one, each <orientation>-preserving, since the real Jacobian of multiplication by a nonzero complex derivative is its squared absolute value. Its degree is $k$, so $\deg P=k\ne0$. It is onto the sphere and must attain zero at a finite point. This proves **every nonconstant complex <polynomial> has a root**, without assuming the result to count roots of a general <polynomial>.

For a proper smooth $F:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^n$, choose a compactly supported $n$-form $\eta$ with $\int\eta=1$ and define
$$
\deg F=\int_{\mathbb R^n}F^*\eta.
$$
Properness makes its <pullback> compactly supported. If $\eta'$ is another choice, the given result gives $\eta-\eta'=d\beta$ with <compact> support. The difference of the defining integrals is $\int d(F^*\beta)=0$, proving <independence>. Sard's theorem supplies a <regular value>. A <proper map> has finitely many preimages of it, and a sufficiently small neighbourhood has no additional inverse sheets outside the chosen inverse-<function> neighbourhoods: otherwise a sequence of extra preimages would have a convergent subsequence by properness and limit in that fibre. A normalized form there therefore gives the same finite signed-count formula, proving that the degree is an <integer>.

Finally $x\mapsto x^k$ is proper for $k\ge1$. For odd $k$, a positive <regular value> has one inverse image with positive derivative. For positive even $k$, it has two inverse images with opposite derivative signs. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\deg(x\mapsto x^k)=\begin{cases}1,&k\ge1\text{ odd},\\0,&k\ge2\text{ even}.\end{cases}}
$$
For $k=0$ it is the constant map one, whose inverse image of the <compact> singleton $\{1\}$ is all of $\mathbb R$. It is **not proper**, so its degree is not defined by the proper-map construction requested here.

= Solution
{parent=2008/B/8}

A <Riemannian metric> is a smooth positive definite symmetric bilinear form $g_q$ on each <tangent space>. The inverse form on the cotangent space defines the kinetic <Hamiltonian>
$$
H(q,p)=\tfrac12g_q^{-1}(p,p)=\tfrac12g^{ij}(q)p_ip_j.
$$
It is smooth and satisfies $H(q,ap)=a^2H(q,p)$. With the canonical <symplectic form> $\Omega=\sum_i dq^i\wedge dp_i$, define $\iota_{X_H}\Omega=dH$. This gives Hamilton's equations
$$
\dot q^i=\partial_{p_i}H=g^{ij}p_j,\qquad\dot p_i=-\partial_{q^i}H.
$$
Thus $p_i=g_{ij}\dot q^j$. Differentiating the inverse metric yields $\dot p_i=\tfrac12\partial_i g_{jk}\dot q^j\dot q^k$. Differentiating $p_i=g_{ij}\dot q^j$ and substituting gives
$$
\ddot q^i+\Gamma^i_{jk}\dot q^j\dot q^k=0.
$$
Therefore the projections of the <Hamiltonian> integral curves are affinely parametrized geodesics, and lifting a <geodesic> by its metric covector gives a <Hamiltonian> integral curve.

For the <Killing vector field> define the fibrewise linear <function> $f(q,p)=p(Y_q)=p_iY^i(q)$. Along the lifted <geodesic> it equals $g(\dot q,Y)$. Metric compatibility and the <geodesic equation> give
$$
\frac d{dt}g(\dot q,Y)=g(\dot q,\nabla_{\dot q}Y)=\tfrac12(\mathcal L_Yg)(\dot q,\dot q)=0.
$$
Thus **$f$ is conserved**. Equivalently the cotangent lift of the <isometry> flow preserves $H$, and its generator has <Hamiltonian> $f$, so their <Poisson bracket> vanishes.

For the final two metrics write $s=\ell(x)+m(y)$, restricting to an <open set> where the printed tensors are smooth nondegenerate metrics. Their inverse forms give
$$
H_1=\frac{p_x^2+p_y^2}{2s},\qquad
H_2=\frac{m(y)p_x^2-\ell(x)p_y^2}{2s}=m(y)H_1-\frac12p_y^2.
$$
Along the $H_1$ flow, $H_1$ is constant, while
$$
\dot y=p_y/s,\qquad \dot p_y=\frac{m'(y)}{2s^2}(p_x^2+p_y^2)=\frac{m'(y)H_1}{s}.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\frac{dH_2}{dt}=m'(y)\frac{p_y}{s}H_1-p_y\frac{m'(y)H_1}{s}=0}.
$$
This is the <Liouville metric geodesic integral>, a quadratic conserved quantity. It does not require a <Killing vector field>; it arises from the separated metric coefficients. When both printed metrics are Riemannian, their positivity in particular requires $s>0$, $m>0$ and $\ell<0$; the calculation also applies wherever the corresponding inverse tensors are defined.
