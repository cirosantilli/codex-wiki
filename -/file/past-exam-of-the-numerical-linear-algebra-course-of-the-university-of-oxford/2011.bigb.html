= a

= Solution
{parent=a}

A real <singular value decomposition> has the form $A=U\Sigma V^T$, where $U\in\mathbb R^{m\times m}$ and $V\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}$ are <orthogonal matrices>, and $\Sigma$ is a rectangular <diagonal matrix> with entries $\sigma_1\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{\min(m,n)}\geq0$. These entries are the <singular values>. Their squares are the <eigenvalues> of $A^TA$, omitting the additional zero <eigenvalues> when $n>m$. The positive <singular values> number exactly the <matrix rank>.

= b
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Use the <singular value decomposition> and the <orthogonal invariance of the Euclidean norm>. Put $z=V^Tx$ and $c=U^Tb$. Since precisely $\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_r$ are positive,
$$
\|Ax-b\|_2^2=\sum_{j=1}^r(\sigma_jz_j-c_j)^2+\sum_{j=r+1}^m c_j^2.
$$
The second sum is independent of $x$ and cannot be removed. The first sum vanishes exactly when $z_j=c_j/\sigma_j$ for $1\leq j\leq r$, while $z_{r+1},\ldots,z_n$ are arbitrary. Thus every <least-squares solution> is
$$
\boxed{x=\sum_{j=1}^r\frac{u_j^Tb}{\sigma_j}v_j+\sum_{j=r+1}^n\alpha_jv_j,\qquad\alpha_j\in\mathbb R.}
$$
The unavoidable <linear-system residual> is the component of $b$ <orthogonal> to the <column space> of $A$:
$$
\boxed{\min_x\|Ax-b\|_2=\left(\sum_{j=r+1}^m|u_j^Tb|^2\right)^{1/2}.}
$$
The <minimum-norm least-squares solution> sets all $\alpha_j=0$ and equals $A^+b$, where $A^+$ is the <Moore-Penrose inverse>. The squared minimum, rather than the minimum itself, is the second sum in the first display.

{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Let $u_j$ and $v_j$ be the columns of the <orthogonal matrices> $U$ and $V$ in the <singular value decomposition>. Multiplying by the rectangular <diagonal matrix> retains its $r$ nonzero diagonal entries:
$$
A=U\Sigma V^T=\boxed{\sum_{j=1}^r\sigma_j u_jv_j^T.}
$$
The families $(u_j)$ and $(v_j)$ are <orthonormal>, hence in particular <orthogonal>. Each summand is a rank-one <outer product>, and the remaining terms vanish because their <singular values> are zero.

= d
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

For the matrix's Euclidean <operator norm>, <orthogonal invariance of the Euclidean norm> gives
$$
\|Ax\|_2=\|\Sigma V^Tx\|_2\leq\sigma_1\|x\|_2.
$$
Taking $x=v_1$ gives equality, so $\|A\|_2=\sigma_1$. For the <Frobenius norm>, the <trace> identity and the <singular value decomposition> give
$$
\|A\|_F^2=\operatorname{tr}(A^TA)=\operatorname{tr}(V\Sigma^T\Sigma V^T)=\sum_j\sigma_j^2.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\|A\|_2=\sigma_1,\qquad\|A\|_F=\left(\sum_{j=1}^r\sigma_j^2\right)^{1/2}.}
$$
Here the Euclidean matrix norm means the <operator norm> induced by the vector <Euclidean norm>, rather than the entrywise Euclidean length, which is the <Frobenius norm>.

= e
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=e}

The two columns are <orthogonal>, with lengths one and two; equivalently $A^TA=\operatorname{diag}(1,4)$. Ordering the <singular values> decreasingly requires swapping the right coordinate directions. One full <singular value decomposition> is
$$
\boxed{U=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&0&1\\0&-1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
V=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Both $U$ and $V$ are <orthogonal matrices>, and direct <matrix multiplication> gives $U\Sigma V^T=A$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\|A\|_2=2,\qquad\|A\|_F=\sqrt5.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=a}

The <spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices> gives $A=Q\Lambda Q^T$ with an <orthogonal matrix> $Q$. <Positive definiteness> makes every <eigenvalue> $\lambda_i$ positive. Define the <matrix square root>
$$
\boxed{A^{1/2}=Q\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_1},\ldots,\sqrt{\lambda_n})Q^T.}
$$
It is <symmetric>, its square is $A$, and for any nonzero <vector> $x$,
$$
x^TA^{1/2}x=\sum_i\sqrt{\lambda_i}\,(Q^Tx)_i^2>0.
$$
Thus it is also <positive definite>, and its <matrix inverse> is $A^{-1/2}=Q\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_i^{-1/2})Q^T$.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <induced matrix norm> associated with the given <energy norm> is a supremum of length ratios. The invertible <change of variables> $y=A^{1/2}x$ gives
$$
\|B\|_A=\sup_{x\ne0}\frac{\|Bx\|_A}{\|x\|_A}
=\sup_{y\ne0}\frac{\|A^{1/2}BA^{-1/2}y\|_2}{\|y\|_2}
=\boxed{\|A^{1/2}BA^{-1/2}\|_2}.
$$
The last expression is the Euclidean <operator norm>. This is the <matrix norm induced by an energy norm>: transforming to coordinates with Euclidean lengths transforms the operator by the corresponding <matrix similarity>.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Write $A=D+L+R$, where $D$ is the <diagonal matrix> of diagonal entries of $A$, and $L,R$ are its strictly lower- and upper-triangular parts. Since $A$ is <symmetric>, $R=L^T$. Subtract the two factors in the <SOR> iteration:
$$
(D+\omega L)-[(1-\omega)D-\omega R]=\omega A.
$$
Therefore, for $\omega\ne0$,
$$
C(\omega)=I-\omega(D+\omega L)^{-1}A
=\boxed{I-B(\omega)^{-1}A,\qquad B(\omega)=\omega^{-1}D+L.}
$$
<Positive definiteness> makes each $D_{ii}>0$, so the <triangular matrix> $B(\omega)$ is an <invertible matrix> for every $\omega\ne0$. The original formula gives $C(0)=I$; the expression for $B(\omega)$ itself is undefined there. The affine term ensuring that the desired solution is a <fixed point> is $c=B(\omega)^{-1}b$.

= d
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

Apply the <matrix norm induced by an energy norm> and set $T=A^{1/2}C(\omega)A^{-1/2}=I-A^{1/2}B^{-1}A^{1/2}$. Multiplication gives
$$
T^TT=I-A^{1/2}\bigl[B^{-1}+B^{-T}-B^{-T}AB^{-1}\bigr]A^{1/2}.
$$
The bracket factors as $B^{-T}(B+B^T-A)B^{-1}$. Because $B=D/\omega+L$ and $A=D+L+L^T$,
$$
B+B^T-A=\left(\frac2\omega-1\right)D.
$$
Thus $T^TT=M(\omega)$ for the stated <matrix>. In particular $M(\omega)$ is a <positive semidefinite matrix>. The Euclidean <operator norm> squared is the largest <eigenvalue> of $T^TT$, and all those <eigenvalues> are nonnegative. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\|C(\omega)\|_A=\|T\|_2=\rho(M(\omega))^{1/2}.}
$$
This identity is the algebraic step in the <energy-norm proof of convergence of SOR>; <spectral radius> equals the largest <eigenvalue> here because $M(\omega)=T^TT$.

= Solution
{parent=e}

For $0<\omega<2$, the coefficient $\gamma=2/\omega-1$ is positive. Moreover
$$
G=A^{1/2}B^{-T}DB^{-1}A^{1/2}
$$
is a <positive definite matrix>: for a nonzero <vector> $z$, put $y=B^{-1}A^{1/2}z\ne0$; then $z^TGz=y^TDy>0$. Hence every <eigenvalue> of $M=I-\gamma G$ is strictly below one. Part (d) also gives $M=T^TT$, so these <eigenvalues> are nonnegative. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\|C(\omega)\|_A=\sqrt{\rho(M(\omega))}<1,\qquad0<\omega<2.}
$$
The <energy-norm proof of convergence of SOR> thus makes the iteration a strict <contraction mapping> in this norm. Its <forward error> satisfies $e^{(j+1)}=C(\omega)e^{(j)}$, so it converges geometrically to zero.

= Solution
{parent=a}

For the <quadratic function>, write $r_j=b-Ax_j$ and restrict to the full line $x_j+\alpha p_j$. <Symmetry> of $A$ gives
$$
f(x_j+\alpha p_j)=f(x_j)-\alpha p_j^Tr_j+\frac{\alpha^2}{2}p_j^TAp_j.
$$
The coefficient $p_j^TAp_j$ is positive by <positive definiteness> and $p_j\ne0$. The unique <minimizer> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\alpha_j=\frac{p_j^Tr_j}{p_j^TAp_j},\qquad x_{j+1}=x_j+\alpha_jp_j.}
$$
<Quadratic minimization along a line> also gives the exact change
$$
f(x_{j+1})-f(x_j)=-\frac{(p_j^Tr_j)^2}{2p_j^TAp_j}.
$$
Thus **strict decrease holds exactly when $p_j^Tr_j\ne0$**. The search is over the whole line, so $\alpha_j$ can be negative; a positive <step size> would require the additional <descent direction> condition $p_j^Tr_j>0$.

= b
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The exact minimization gives $r_{j+1}=r_j-\alpha_jAp_j$, with $\alpha_j=p_j^Tr_j/(p_j^TAp_j)$. For <steepest descent method>, the next <search direction> is $p_{j+1}=r_{j+1}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{p_j^Tp_{j+1}=p_j^Tr_j-\alpha_jp_j^TAp_j=0.}
$$
Thus consecutive nonzero <search directions> are <orthogonal>. This calculation even covers an arbitrary initial direction $p_1$, as in the generic algorithm: it only needs the exact preceding line minimization and the choice $p_{j+1}=r_{j+1}$. If the new <linear-system residual> is zero, the <linear system> is already solved. No pairwise orthogonality of all nonconsecutive directions is asserted.

{parent=3}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Nonzero <vectors> $p_1,\ldots,p_k$ are <conjugate directions> with respect to $A$ when $p_i^TAp_j=0$ for $i\ne j$. They are <linearly independent>: if $\sum_i c_ip_i=0$, multiplication by $p_j^TA$ gives $c_jp_j^TAp_j=0$, hence $c_j=0$ by <positive definiteness>. There are consequently at most $n$ such directions.

The <finite termination of conjugate directions> follows from the residual orthogonality maintained by the exact line minimizations. At step $j$,
$$
r_{j+1}=r_j-\alpha_jAp_j,\qquad p_j^Tr_{j+1}=0.
$$
For $i<j$, conjugacy gives $p_i^Tr_{j+1}=p_i^Tr_j$. By <mathematical induction>, $r_{j+1}$ is <orthogonal> to every $p_1,\ldots,p_j$. After $n$ nonzero conjugate directions they form a <basis> of $\mathbb R^n$, forcing $r_{n+1}=0$. Thus **the method reaches $Ax=b$ in at most $n$ steps**, and <positive definiteness> makes that solution unique. The claim is in exact arithmetic; rounding errors need not preserve exact conjugacy.

Since $Ap_1=(5,2)^T$, an <A-conjugate direction> $p_2=(a,b)^T$ must satisfy $5a+2b=0$. One nonzero choice is
$$
\boxed{p_2=(2,-5)^T.}
$$
Direct <matrix multiplication> gives $Ap_2=(3,-3)^T$, and $p_1^TAp_2=0$. All nonzero scalar multiples of this <vector> also qualify.
