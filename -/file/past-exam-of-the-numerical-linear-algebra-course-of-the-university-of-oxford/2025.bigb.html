= Solution
{parent=i}

The <orthonormal> columns give $A^TA=I_n$. Thus $P=AA^T$ is the <orthogonal projection> onto the <column space> of $A$, and
$$
Ax-b=A(x-A^Tb)-(I_m-P)b.
$$
The two terms are <orthogonal>, so the <Pythagorean identity> gives
$$
\|Ax-b\|_2^2=\|x-A^Tb\|_2^2+\|(I_m-AA^T)b\|_2^2.
$$
Only the first term depends on $x$. Consequently the <linear least-squares problem> has the unique answer
$$
\boxed{x_*=A^Tb},\qquad \min_x\|Ax-b\|_2=\|(I_m-AA^T)b\|_2.
$$

For every <vector> $z\in\mathbb R^n$, splitting the rows gives
$$
\|A_1z\|_2^2+\|A_2z\|_2^2=\|Az\|_2^2=z^TA^TAz=\|z\|_2^2.
$$
Both summands are nonnegative. Taking the supremum over <unit vectors> in the definition of the <spectral norm> proves
$$
\boxed{\|A_1\|_2\leq1,\qquad \|A_2\|_2\leq1}.
$$

= Solution

Put $B=A_1^TA_1$. It is a <symmetric matrix> and a <positive semidefinite matrix>, while $A_2^TA_2=I_n-B$ is also a <positive semidefinite matrix>. By the <spectral theorem>, choose an <orthogonal matrix> $V=[v_1,\ldots,v_n]$ such that
$$
V^TBV=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n),\qquad 0\leq\lambda_j\leq1.
$$
The bounds follow directly from $z^TBz=\|A_1z\|_2^2\in[0,\|z\|_2^2]$. Define the <diagonal matrices>
$$
\Sigma_1=\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_1},\ldots,\sqrt{\lambda_n}),\qquad
\Sigma_2=\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{1-\lambda_1},\ldots,\sqrt{1-\lambda_n}).
$$
The columns of $A_1V$ are mutually <orthogonal>, since $(A_1v_i)^T(A_1v_j)=\lambda_j\delta_{ij}$. For each $\lambda_j>0$, set $u_{1j}=A_1v_j/\sqrt{\lambda_j}$. These columns are <orthonormal>. At a zero $\lambda_j$, one has $A_1v_j=0$; choose $u_{1j}$ by completing the already prescribed columns to an <orthonormal set> of $n$ columns in $\mathbb R^k$. Such a completion exists because $k\geq n$. This gives $A_1V=U_1\Sigma_1$, including the zero columns.

Likewise, the <Gram matrix> of $A_2V$ is $\operatorname{diag}(1-\lambda_j)$. Normalize its nonzero columns and complete at its zero columns in $\mathbb R^{m-k}$, using $m-k\geq n$. The resulting $U_2$ has <orthonormal> columns and $A_2V=U_2\Sigma_2$. Combining the identities gives
$$
\boxed{A=\begin{pmatrix}U_1&0\\0&U_2\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_1\\\Sigma_2\end{pmatrix}V^T}.
$$
This is a form of the <cosine-sine decomposition>. Completing the zero columns is essential: neither block was assumed to have <full column rank>.

Choose the nonnegative square roots of the <eigenvalues> of $A^TA$ for the diagonal entries of $\Sigma$, in nonincreasing order. Its assumed <matrix inverse> exists, so these entries are positive. The <eigendecomposition> gives
$$
U^TU=\Sigma^{-1}V^TA^TAV\Sigma^{-1}
=\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma^2\Sigma^{-1}=I_n.
$$
Hence $U$ has <orthonormal> columns. Also
$$
\boxed{U\Sigma V^T=AV\Sigma^{-1}\Sigma V^T=A}.
$$
Together with the <orthogonal matrix> $V$ and the nonnegative <diagonal matrix> $\Sigma$, these are precisely the properties of a thin <singular value decomposition>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Let $\widetilde A=\widehat U\widehat\Sigma\widehat V^T$. Interpret the diagonal of $\widehat\Sigma$ as nonnegative computed <singular values> and sort them consistently. If signed diagonal entries are used instead, take their absolute values and absorb signs into the <orthonormal> columns. Because the two outer factors have the stated <orthonormality>, these sorted numbers are exactly $\sigma_j(\widetilde A)$.

For completeness, the <singular value perturbation bound> follows from the <Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem>. If $B_{j-1}$ is the truncated <singular value decomposition> of a <matrix> $B$, the <triangle inequality> gives
$$
\sigma_j(A)=\min_{\operatorname{rank}D\leq j-1}\|A-D\|_2
\leq\|A-B_{j-1}\|_2
\leq\|A-B\|_2+\sigma_j(B).
$$
Exchange $A$ and $B$ to get $|\sigma_j(A)-\sigma_j(B)|\leq\|A-B\|_2$. Applying this to $B=\widetilde A$ and the given <backward error> proves
$$
\boxed{|\sigma_j(A)-\widehat\sigma_j|\leq\epsilon\quad(1\leq j\leq n)}.
$$
Thus <backward stability> of the <singular value decomposition> gives an absolute <forward error> bound for every <singular value>, even a very small one.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

An error of order $\epsilon$ in a squared <singular value> can become an error of order $\sqrt\epsilon$ after taking its square root. To keep $A$ nonsingular as required, take $0<\epsilon<1/2$ and
$$
A=\operatorname{diag}(1,\epsilon),\qquad
\widehat V=I_2,\qquad
\widehat\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(1,\sqrt{\epsilon^2+\epsilon}).
$$
Then $\|A\|_2=1$, and the computed <eigendecomposition> has exactly the permitted <backward error>:
$$
\|A^TA-\widehat V\widehat\Sigma^2\widehat V^T\|_2
=\|\operatorname{diag}(0,-\epsilon)\|_2=\epsilon.
$$
Nevertheless the second computed <singular value> has error
$$
\sqrt{\epsilon^2+\epsilon}-\epsilon
=\sqrt\epsilon\bigl(\sqrt{1+\epsilon}-\sqrt\epsilon\bigr)
\sim\sqrt\epsilon.
$$
By the <singular value perturbation bound>, every product with these computed <singular values> and exactly <orthonormal> outer factors has <spectral norm> error at least this large. In particular, even the favorable choice $\widehat U=I_2$ gives
$$
\boxed{\|A-\widehat U\widehat\Sigma\widehat V^T\|_2
=\sqrt{\epsilon^2+\epsilon}-\epsilon\sim\sqrt\epsilon},
$$
which has no uniform $O(\epsilon)$ bound in <big O notation>. Therefore <backward stability> for the <eigendecomposition> of the <Gram matrix> does not imply <backward stability> for the <singular value decomposition>.

There is a related loss of <orthonormality> in the raw formula: evaluating $A\widehat V\widehat\Sigma^{-1}$ exactly for these perturbed factors gives $\operatorname{diag}(1,\epsilon/\sqrt{\epsilon^2+\epsilon})$, whose second column has length tending to zero. Its product with $\widehat\Sigma\widehat V^T$ equals $A$, but its diagonal factors are consequently not a <singular value decomposition>. The paper's idealized <orthonormality> assumption lets us isolate the unavoidable <singular value> error even if this additional problem is repaired.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

**No uniform absolute error bound of order $\epsilon$ follows.** Take the <Jordan block>
$$
B=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
E=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\\epsilon&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Here $\|B\|_2=1$ and $\|E\|_2=\epsilon$. The exact <eigenvalues> of $B$ are both zero, whereas
$$
\det(\lambda I_2-B-E)=\lambda^2-\epsilon,
\qquad \boxed{\lambda_\pm(B+E)=\pm\sqrt\epsilon}.
$$
These are admissible outputs under a <backward error> guarantee of size $\epsilon$, yet their <forward errors> are $\sqrt\epsilon$. This is the <square-root splitting of a defective double eigenvalue>. More generally, <eigenvalue sensitivity> depends on the <matrix>; <backward stability> alone does not control it. The <singular value perturbation bound> used in part (ii) has no equally uniform counterpart for the <eigenvalues> of arbitrary nonsymmetric <matrices>.

$$
\boxed{A_r=\sum_{j=1}^r\sigma_j u_jv_j^T},\qquad
\boxed{\|A-A_r\|_2=\sigma_{r+1}},\qquad

The same $A_r$ minimizes the <spectral norm> and <Frobenius norm> errors over <matrices> of <matrix rank> at most $r$. This conventional meaning of rank-$r$ <low-rank approximation> includes the case $\operatorname{rank}A<r$, when $A_r=A$.

Put $P=QQ^T$. Since $Q$ has $r$ columns, the <rank bound for a matrix product> gives $\operatorname{rank}(PA)\leq r$. The <Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem> and part (a) then give
$$
\boxed{\|PA-A\|_2\geq\sigma_{r+1}=\|Q_*Q_*^TA-A\|_2}.

One can also see the lower bound directly. The restriction of $PA$ to the $(r+1)$-dimensional <linear span> of $v_1,\ldots,v_{r+1}$ has a nonzero <null space>, because its <matrix rank> is at most $r$. For a <unit vector> $z$ in that <null space>, $\|(A-PA)z\|_2=\|Az\|_2\geq\sigma_{r+1}$ by the <singular value decomposition>.

= Solution

The <orthogonal projection> $P=QQ^T$ gives
$$
QM-A=Q(M-Q^TA)-(I_m-P)A.
$$
The first summand has every column in the <column space> of $Q$, and the second has every column in its <orthogonal complement>. They therefore have zero <Frobenius inner product>. The <Pythagorean identity>, together with $Q^TQ=I_r$, gives the stronger exact formula
$$
\|QM-A\|_F^2=\|M-Q^TA\|_F^2+\|(I_m-P)A\|_F^2.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\|QQ^TA-A\|_F\leq\|QM-A\|_F},
$$
with equality exactly when $M=Q^TA$. Thus solving the <linear least-squares problem> for each column chooses the best coefficients in the prescribed <column space>.

= Solution

Because $r<n$, choose a <unit vector> $v$ in the <null space> of $X^T$, so that $v^TX=0$. Let $J\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$ have <orthonormal> columns, for example the first $n$ columns of $I_m$, and choose a real number $\alpha>1$. Set
$$
\boxed{A=J\bigl(I_n+(\alpha-1)vv^T\bigr)}.
$$
Its <singular value> along $v$ is $\alpha$, and its other <singular values> are $1$. Thus $\sigma_1(A)=\alpha>1=\sigma_2(A)$. Meanwhile $AX=JX$. If $X$ has <full column rank>, the <column space> of the thin <QR decomposition> is exactly $\operatorname{range}(JX)$, which is <orthogonal> to $Jv$. Hence $PJv=0$ for $P=QQ^T$, and
$$
\|(I_m-P)A\|_2\geq\|(I_m-P)Av\|_2=\|\alpha Jv\|_2=\alpha.
$$
Conversely, an <orthogonal projection> is a contraction in the <Euclidean norm>, so $\|(I_m-P)A\|_2\leq\|A\|_2=\alpha$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\|A-\widehat A\|_2=\sigma_1(A)=\alpha>\sigma_2(A)=1}.
$$
If $X$ is rank deficient, complete the <QR decomposition> within $J(v^\perp)$: this space has dimension $n-1\geq r$ and contains all columns of $JX$, so the required <orthonormal> completion exists there. The same proof applies. For rank-deficient data the choice of unused columns of $Q$ matters; the construction specifies a valid choice. If $Q$ instead contains only a <basis> of $\operatorname{range}(AX)$, the conclusion holds without any completion. A fixed sampling <matrix> can thus miss the unique largest <singular value> completely.

= Solution
{parent=v}

Write $\ell=r/2$; the requested expression requires the positive even integer $r\geq2$. Split the thin <singular value decomposition> after $\ell$ columns:
$$
A=U_aD_aV_a^T+U_bD_bV_b^T,\qquad
\Omega_a=V_a^TX\in\mathbb R^{\ell\times r},\qquad
\Omega_b=V_b^TX\in\mathbb R^{(n-\ell)\times r}.
$$
Here $\|D_b\|_2=\sigma_{\ell+1}=\|A-A_\ell\|_2$. Each column of a <Gaussian random matrix> is an <orthogonally invariant random vector>: its <probability density function> is proportional to $\exp(-\|z\|_2^2/2)$, and an <orthogonal matrix> preserves both $\|z\|_2$ and volume. Thus $V^TX$, where $V=[V_a,V_b]$, again has <independent and identically distributed> <standard normal> entries. In particular both displayed blocks have the Gaussian distributions needed below.

First prove a <deterministic error bound for a randomized range finder>. Put $P=QQ^T$ and suppose $\Omega_a$ has <full row rank>. Its <Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse> is $\Omega_a^\dagger=\Omega_a^T(\Omega_a\Omega_a^T)^{-1}$, so $\Omega_a\Omega_a^\dagger=I_\ell$. The <QR decomposition> implies $(I_m-P)AX=0$, hence
$$
(I_m-P)U_aD_a\Omega_a=-(I_m-P)U_bD_b\Omega_b.
$$
Multiplication by $\Omega_a^\dagger$ expresses the missed leading columns in terms of the trailing columns. Substitution into $(I_m-P)A$ gives the exact identity
$$
(I_m-P)A=(I_m-P)U_bD_b
\begin{pmatrix}-\Omega_b\Omega_a^\dagger&I_{n-\ell}\end{pmatrix}V^T.
$$
For any <matrix> $F$, the <spectral norm> of $[-F\ I]$ is $\sqrt{1+\|F\|_2^2}$, because its product with its <transpose> is $FF^T+I$. Using <submultiplicativity of the operator norm>, the <orthonormality> of $U_b$, and the contraction property of the <orthogonal projection>, we obtain
$$
\boxed{\|A-PA\|_2\leq\sigma_{\ell+1}
\sqrt{1+\|\Omega_b\Omega_a^\dagger\|_2^2}}.
$$
This estimate is deterministic and also covers rank-deficient $A$; if $\sigma_{\ell+1}=0$, its right-hand side vanishes.

Now use the paper's permitted <singular value> estimates. Apply the lower estimate to the tall <matrix> $\Omega_a^T$ of size $r\times\ell$, rather than to its wide <transpose>. It gives
$$
\sigma_{\min}(\Omega_a)\geq\sqrt r-\sqrt\ell
=\sqrt r(1-1/\sqrt2)>0,\qquad
\|\Omega_a^\dagger\|_2\leq\frac1{\sqrt r-\sqrt\ell}.
$$
The upper estimate for $\Omega_b$, transposing if necessary, gives
$$
\|\Omega_b\|_2\leq\sqrt{n-\ell}+\sqrt r\leq2\sqrt n.
$$
Thus, with $K=2/(1-1/\sqrt2)$,
$$
\|\Omega_b\Omega_a^\dagger\|_2\leq K\sqrt{n/r}.
$$
Since $n/r>1$, the deterministic estimate yields
$$
\boxed{\|A-\widehat A\|_2
\leq C\sqrt{\frac nr}\,\|A-A_{r/2}\|_2},\qquad C=\sqrt{1+K^2}.
$$
This proves the requested conclusion under the explicitly authorized estimates.

**The probabilistic qualification is necessary.** A <Gaussian random matrix> does not always have all its <singular values> between the two endpoints printed in the hint. Already a $1\times1$ example would require $|Z|\leq2$ for every <standard normal random variable> $Z$, which fails with positive <probability>. The proof above holds on the event where the two block estimates hold. In a probabilistic formulation one supplies estimates for that event, usually allowing a positive margin in both endpoints; no failure probability is specified by the paper.

Nor can the requested inequality with one universal $C$ hold almost surely for every $A$. For example, take $n=m=3$, $r=2$, $A=\operatorname{diag}(L,1,1)$ with $L>1$, and a sampling <matrix>
$$
X_0=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
At $X_0$, the sampled <column space> misses the first coordinate, so $\|A-\widehat A\|_2=L$, whereas $\|A-A_1\|_2=1$. Choose $L>C\sqrt{3/2}$. Because $AX_0$ has <full column rank>, its <orthogonal projection> depends continuously on $X$ near $X_0$ through $AX(X^TA^TAX)^{-1}X^TA^T$. The strict violation persists in an open neighborhood of $X_0$. The joint Gaussian <probability density function> is positive there, so that neighborhood has positive <probability>. This identifies the missing qualification without weakening the conditional calculation.

Write $x=Q_ky$ and $\beta=\|b\|_2$. The <Arnoldi decomposition> and $b=\beta Q_{k+1}e_1$ give
$$
Ax-b=Q_{k+1}(\widetilde H_ky-\beta e_1).
$$
Because $Q_{k+1}$ has <orthonormal> columns, it preserves the <Euclidean norm>. The <generalized minimal residual method> therefore reduces to the small <linear least-squares problem>
$$
\boxed{y_k=\underset{y\in\mathbb R^k}{\operatorname{argmin}}
\|\widetilde H_ky-\beta e_1\|_2,\qquad x_k=Q_ky_k}.
$$
Use <Givens rotations> on the <upper Hessenberg matrix> to obtain a <QR decomposition>
$$
Z^T\widetilde H_k=\begin{pmatrix}R_k\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
g=Z^T(\beta e_1),
$$
where $Z$ is an <orthogonal matrix> and $R_k$ is an <upper triangular matrix>. Then solve $R_ky_k=g_{1:k}$ by <backward substitution in a triangular system> and form $Q_ky_k$. The resulting minimum <linear-system residual> has <Euclidean norm> $|g_{k+1}|$.

The small problem has a unique answer: if $\widetilde H_ky=0$, the <Arnoldi decomposition> gives $AQ_ky=0$. The <invertible matrix> $A$ implies $Q_ky=0$, and the <orthonormality> of $Q_k$ then gives $y=0$. Thus $\widetilde H_k$ has <full column rank> and $R_k$ is nonsingular.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For the small <QR decomposition>, each of the $k$ <Givens rotations> eliminates one subdiagonal entry of the <upper Hessenberg matrix>. At column $j$, apply the rotation to two rows in columns $j,\ldots,k$, costing $O(k-j+1)$ arithmetic operations. Summing gives $O(k^2)$, and applying the rotations to the right-hand side costs $O(k)$. <Backward substitution in a triangular system> costs $O(k^2)$.

Computing $\|b\|_2$ costs $O(n)$ and the final dense <matrix multiplication> $Q_ky_k$ costs $O(nk)$. Thus the total, with the <Arnoldi decomposition> already supplied, is
$$
O(k^2+nk+n)=\boxed{O(nk)},\qquad \boxed{s=1,\quad t=1}.
$$
Here $1\leq k<n$, since $Q_{k+1}$ has <orthonormal> columns in $\mathbb R^n$. In the standard dense arithmetic model, forming the general <matrix>–<vector> product gives the sharp $\Theta(nk)$ cost. The pair above records that two-parameter bound; looser bounds such as $O(n^2)$ are also possible because $k<n$. Treating $\widetilde H_k$ as an unstructured dense <matrix> would unnecessarily replace its $O(k^2)$ factorization by an $O(k^3)$ computation.

**The claim is true**, with

\boxed{M=A^TA}.
$$
It is a <symmetric positive-definite matrix>: for every nonzero <vector> $z$, the fact that $A$ is an <invertible matrix> gives $z^TMz=\|Az\|_2^2>0$. Since $Ax_*=b$, every candidate $x$ satisfies

Multiplying the <Arnoldi decomposition> by $Q_k^T$ shows that the leading square block is
$$
H_k=Q_k^TAQ_k.

It is an <upper Hessenberg matrix> by the <Arnoldi iteration>. Because $A=A^T$, it is also a <symmetric matrix>. Entries below the first subdiagonal vanish by the Hessenberg structure, and their reflected entries above the first superdiagonal vanish by symmetry. Consequently
$$
\boxed{T_k=Q_k^TAQ_k\text{ is symmetric tridiagonal}},\qquad
\boxed{AQ_k=Q_{k+1}\widetilde T_k}.

The extra last row has only its final entry possibly nonzero. With the usual choice of positive normalization factors, the individual column relations are the three-term <Lanczos algorithm> recurrence
$$
Aq_j=\beta_{j-1}q_{j-1}+\alpha_jq_j+\beta_jq_{j+1},
\qquad \alpha_j=q_j^TAq_j,\quad \beta_j>0,
$$
where the first term is omitted for $j=1$. This reduction is an exact-arithmetic statement; <round-off error> can affect the <orthogonality> in an implementation.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The <least-squares normal equations> are
$$
\boxed{Hx=c,\qquad H=G^TG,\quad c=G^Tb}.
$$
The <full column rank> of $G$ gives $z^THz=\|Gz\|_2^2>0$ for nonzero $z$, so $H$ is a <symmetric positive-definite matrix>. Apply the <Conjugate gradient method> to this <linear system>. To verify that its solution is the desired one, let $x_*$ solve the displayed equations. Then $G^T(Gx_*-b)=0$, and for any $x$ the <Pythagorean identity> gives
$$
\|Gx-b\|_2^2=\|Gx_*-b\|_2^2+\|G(x-x_*)\|_2^2.
$$
Thus $x_*$ is the unique minimizer of the <linear least-squares problem>.

One can apply $H$ without forming the <Gram matrix>: compute $Hp=G^T(Gp)$. More explicitly, initialize $x_0$, $r_0=c-Hx_0$, $p_0=r_0$; until the <linear-system residual> vanishes, use the <Conjugate gradient method> recurrence
$$
\alpha_j=\frac{r_j^Tr_j}{p_j^THp_j},\quad
x_{j+1}=x_j+\alpha_jp_j,\quad
r_{j+1}=r_j-\alpha_jHp_j,
$$
$$
\beta_j=\frac{r_{j+1}^Tr_{j+1}}{r_j^Tr_j},\qquad
p_{j+1}=r_{j+1}+\beta_jp_j.
$$
Each dense application of $H$ this way costs $O(mn)$ operations. The <condition number> satisfies $\kappa_2(H)=\kappa_2(G)^2$, where $\kappa_2(G)=\sigma_{\max}(G)/\sigma_{\min}(G)$; this is the conditioning cost of the <least-squares normal equations>.

\sqrt n\leq\sigma_{\min}(G)\leq\sigma_{\max}(G)\leq3\sqrt n.
$$
For the <symmetric positive-definite matrix> $H=G^TG$ in part (iii), its <eigenvalues> are the squared <singular values> of $G$, so
$$
n\leq\lambda_{\min}(H)\leq\lambda_{\max}(H)\leq9n,
\qquad \boxed{\kappa_2(H)\leq9}.
$$
Apply the supplied <conjugate gradient convergence bound> to $H$. For $e_k=x_k-x_*$ and nonzero $e_0$, the function $(\sqrt\kappa-1)/(\sqrt\kappa+1)$ increases with $\kappa\geq1$, giving
$$
\boxed{\frac{\|e_k\|_H}{\|e_0\|_H}\leq2\left(\frac{3-1}{3+1}\right)^k
=2^{1-k}}.
$$
Thus the error in this <weighted norm> contracts at a guaranteed exponential factor of at most $1/2$ per iteration, up to the prefactor $2$. Here $\|e\|_H=\|Ge\|_2$. The <Pythagorean identity> in part (iii) also identifies $\|e_k\|_H^2$ with the excess squared <linear least-squares problem> objective, which consequently decreases by a bound proportional to $4^{-k}$. For the <Euclidean norm>, the same <eigenvalue> bounds give $\|e_k\|_2/\|e_0\|_2\leq6\,2^{-k}$.

For relative accuracy $0<\epsilon<1$ in the $H$-<weighted norm>, it is sufficient to take
$$
k\geq\left\lceil\log_2\frac2\epsilon\right\rceil.
$$
Compute $c=G^Tb$ once in $O(mn)$ operations. Each iteration applies $p\mapsto G^T(Gp)$ with two dense <matrix multiplications> and uses $O(n)$ further arithmetic operations; when $m=4n$ this costs $O(n^2)$. Hence the total cost is
$$
\boxed{O\!\left(n^2\log\frac2\epsilon\right)}.
$$
Using <finite termination of the conjugate gradient method> in exact arithmetic refines this to $O(n^2\min\{n,\lceil\log_2(2/\epsilon)\rceil\})$. Explicitly constructing $G^TG$ first would add $O(mn^2)=O(n^3)$ setup work and is unnecessary. These accuracy estimates concern relative error; an absolute tolerance replaces $2/\epsilon$ by $2\|e_0\|_H/\epsilon$ when $\|e_0\|_H>\epsilon$. If $e_0=0$, the initial guess already solves the problem.

As in part 2(b)(v), the Gaussian assumption alone does not make the printed <singular value> interval a certainty. For example, the event that every entry of $G$ has absolute value below a sufficiently small positive number has positive <probability> and forces $\|G\|_F<\sqrt n$, contradicting the asserted lower endpoint. The displayed rate is therefore **conditional on the singular-value bounds authorized by the paper**. The cost and convergence derivation are exact under that condition; an unconditional probabilistic statement would need an additional failure-probability estimate.
