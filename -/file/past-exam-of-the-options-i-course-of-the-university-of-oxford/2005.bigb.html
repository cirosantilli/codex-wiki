Use the right-action convention printed in the paper: $\omega1=\omega$ and $(\omega g)h=\omega(gh)$. The <orbit of a group action> and <stabilizer> are
$$
\operatorname{Orb}(\omega)=\{\omega g:g\in G\},\qquad H=\operatorname{Stab}(\omega)=\{g\in G:\omega g=\omega\}.
$$
The identity belongs to $H$, products of elements of $H$ fix $\omega$, and $\omega g=\omega$ implies $\omega g^{-1}=\omega$. Thus $H$ is a <subgroup>. For $k\in G$,
$$
k\in\operatorname{Stab}(\omega g)\iff\omega gkg^{-1}=\omega\iff gkg^{-1}\in H,
$$
proving **$\operatorname{Stab}(\omega g)=g^{-1}Hg$**.

The rotational action on the eight vertices of a <cube> is **<transitive>**. Model the vertices as $(\pm1,\pm1,\pm1)$. For any desired sign triple, choose a permutation whose parity makes the corresponding <signed permutation matrix> have <determinant> one; it maps $(1,1,1)$ to that vertex and preserves the <cube>. A <rotation> fixing a vertex fixes the axis through that vertex, the centre and the opposite vertex. It cyclically permutes the three incident edges, giving precisely <rotations> by $0,2\pi/3,4\pi/3$. Thus **the <stabilizer> is cyclic of order three**.

An element is algebraic over $F$ when some nonzero <polynomial> in $F[x]$ vanishes at it. Its <minimal polynomial> is the <monic polynomial> of least positive <degree> with this property; scaling a least-<degree> nonzero vanishing <polynomial> makes it monic.

If $m_1,m_2$ are two such <polynomials>, they have the same least <degree>. Their difference has smaller <degree> because both are monic, and it also vanishes at $a$. Minimality forces the difference to be zero. Hence **the monic <minimal polynomial> is unique**.

A set $E\subseteq[0,1]$ is null when for every $\varepsilon>0$ it can be covered by countably many open intervals whose lengths sum to less than $\varepsilon$. Equivalently its <Lebesgue outer measure> is zero. This definition does not presuppose measurability of $E$.

Let $E_n$ be null. Given $\varepsilon>0$, cover $E_n$ by intervals of total length less than $\varepsilon2^{-n}$, for $n\geq1$. Take the middle-thirds <Cantor set> $C$. Its $n$th stage is the union of $2^n$ closed intervals of length $3^{-n}$, and contains $C$. Their total length is $(2/3)^n\to0$; slightly enlarging them to open intervals gives covers of arbitrarily small total length. Therefore **$C$ is null**.

For uncountability map every <binary sequence> $(\varepsilon_j)_{j\geq1}$ to $\sum_{j\geq1}2\varepsilon_j3^{-j}\in C$. If two sequences first differ at index $k$, their image difference in absolute value is at least $2\cdot3^{-k}-\sum_{j>k}2\cdot3^{-j}=3^{-k}>0$. Thus the map is injective. The set of <binary sequences> is <uncountable> by <Cantor's diagonal argument>, so **$C$ is <uncountable>**.

A useful differentiation theorem says: if $f(t,x)$ is integrable in $x$, differentiable in $t$ for almost every $x$, and on a <neighborhood> of each parameter value the derivative is <bounded> in absolute value by one <integrable function> of $x$, then the integral is differentiable and its derivative is the integral of $\partial_tf$. The proof applies <dominated convergence> to difference quotients, <bounded> by that same majorant by the <mean value theorem>.

Fix $t_0>0$ and choose $0<a<t_0<b$. For $t\in[a,b]$,
$$
|e^{-x}x^{t-1}\log x|\leq\begin{cases}x^{a-1}|\log x|,&0<x\leq1,\\ e^{-x}x^{b-1}\log x,&x\geq1.\end{cases}
$$
The first part is integrable by part (i). The second is integrable by the permitted hypothesis for $h_b$; alternatively <exponential decay> dominates its <polynomial> and logarithmic factors. The theorem therefore applies locally at every $t_0$, giving
$$
\boxed{\Gamma'(t)=\int_0^\infty e^{-x}x^{t-1}\log x\,dx\qquad(t>0).}
$$

A <topology> on $X$ contains $\varnothing,X$, is closed under arbitrary unions and under finite intersections. The cofinite family has these properties: a nonempty union contains one cofinite member and therefore has finite complement; the complement of a finite intersection is a finite union of finite sets. The empty union and the sets $X,\varnothing$ are included explicitly.

Any two nonempty <open sets> intersect, because their intersection has finite complement in the infinite set $X$. Thus no two disjoint nonempty <open sets> can separate $X$, proving **<connectedness>**. For <compactness>, take any <open cover> and choose a nonempty member $U$. Only finitely many points lie outside $U$. Choose one covering member for each of those points. Together with $U$ these give a <finite subcover>. Thus **$X$ is compact**.

<Continuity> means that the <inverse image> of every open subset of $Y$ is open in $X$. If $x\in\overline A$ and $V$ is an <open neighborhood> of $f(x)$, then $f^{-1}(V)$ is an <open neighborhood> of $x$, so it meets $A$. Thus $V$ meets $f(A)$. The <closure> characterization gives
$$
\boxed{f(\overline A)\subseteq\overline{f(A)}.}
$$
If $B$ is empty the assertion is immediate. Otherwise, if $B$ is <bounded>, choose a centre $p$ and $R<\infty$ with $d(p,b)\leq R$ for all $b\in B$. The <closed ball> $\{y:d(p,y)\leq R\}$ is closed because $|d(p,y)-d(p,z)|\leq d(y,z)$. It therefore contains $\overline B$. Hence **the <closure> of a <bounded set> is <bounded>**. Empty sets cause no exception. If $f(A)$ is <bounded>, its <closure> is <bounded> by this result, and the displayed inclusion makes **$f(\overline A)$ <bounded>** too.

The <total derivative> is a <linear map> $Df(a):\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^n$ such that
$$
\boxed{f(a+h)=f(a)+Df(a)h+r(h),\qquad\frac{\|r(h)\|}{\|h\|}\longrightarrow0\quad(h\to0).}

It is unique: the difference of two such <linear maps>, evaluated on $h=tv$, must vanish on every fixed <vector> $v$ after division by $t$ and passage to the limit.

For fixed endpoint values, the <Euler-Lagrange equation> is
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dx}F_{y'}(x,y,y')-F_y(x,y,y')=0.}
$$
It follows by differentiating the functional along $y+\varepsilon\eta$ with $\eta(0)=\eta(\pi)=0$, integrating the $F_{y'}\eta'$ term by parts and applying the <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations>.

For this quadratic integrand it becomes $y''+ky=0$. If $k>0$, write $\nu=\sqrt k$; the condition at zero gives $y=B\sin(\nu x)$, and that at $\pi$ requires $B\sin(\nu\pi)=0$. Consequently **$y=0$ is the only <extremal> when $\sqrt k$ is not an integer**. If $\sqrt k=n$ is a positive integer, all <extremals> are
$$
\boxed{y(x)=B\sin(nx),\qquad B\in\mathbb R.}
$$
The integer case $n=0$ must be handled separately: $y''=0$ with both endpoints zero has only $y=0$. If negative real $k$ is allowed, the hyperbolic-<function> solutions also yield only zero under these endpoints. These statements concern stationary <functions>; no minimum is assumed.

<Polar coordinates> give speed squared $\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2$, so unit <mass> has <kinetic energy> $T=\frac12(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2)$. The radial <force> is $-1/r^2=-V'(r)$, giving $V=-1/r$ after choosing the additive constant zero. Therefore $L=T-V$ is the stated expression.

The two <Euler-Lagrange equations> are
$$
\boxed{\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2=-\frac1{r^2},\qquad\frac d{dt}(r^2\dot\theta)=0.}
$$
The second gives <conservation of angular momentum> $\ell=r^2\dot\theta$. The <mechanical energy> is
$$
\boxed{E=\frac12(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2)-\frac1r.}
$$
Direct differentiation expresses its derivative as
$$
\dot E=\dot r\left(\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2+\frac1{r^2}\right)+\dot\theta\frac d{dt}(r^2\dot\theta)=0.
$$
Thus <energy> is conserved for the motion wherever $r>0$.

The <time-independent Schrödinger equation> is
$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi''(x)+\frac12m\omega^2x^2\psi(x)=E\psi(x).
$$
Put $\alpha=m\omega/\hbar$. For $\psi=e^{-\alpha x^2/2}$, differentiation gives $\psi''=(\alpha^2x^2-\alpha)\psi$. Substitution cancels the terms in $x^2$ and leaves $E_0=\hbar^2\alpha/(2m)=\hbar\omega/2$.

The <Gaussian integral> gives $\int e^{-\alpha x^2}\,dx=\sqrt{\pi/\alpha}$. Hence a normalized <wave function> is
$$
\boxed{\psi_0(x)=\left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4}\exp\left(-\frac{m\omega x^2}{2\hbar}\right).}
$$
Multiplication by any constant of modulus one gives the same <quantum state>. The <position> <probability density> is $|\psi_0|^2=\sqrt{\alpha/\pi}e^{-\alpha x^2}$. Its first <moment> vanishes by oddness. <Integration by parts> gives $\int x^2e^{-\alpha x^2}\,dx=(2\alpha)^{-1}\int e^{-\alpha x^2}\,dx$, with the boundary term zero. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\langle x\rangle=0,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(x)=\langle x^2\rangle=\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}.}
$$

For a material fluid volume, <mass conservation> yields $\rho_t+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u)=0$. Constant <mass density> reduces this to $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$. For <inviscid flow>, the <Cauchy stress tensor> is $-pI$; its net surface <force> is the volume integral of $-\nabla p$ by the <divergence theorem>. <Newton's second law> for each material volume therefore gives $\rho D\mathbf u/Dt=-\nabla p$. Since $D/Dt=\partial_t+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla$, these are exactly the two stated equations.

In two dimensions they read
$$
u_t+uu_x+vu_y=-p_x/\rho,\qquad v_t+uv_x+vv_y=-p_y/\rho,\qquad u_x+v_y=0.
$$
Differentiate the second momentum equation in $x$ and subtract the $y$ derivative of the first. The <pressure> terms cancel. For $\omega=v_x-u_y$, collecting terms gives
$$
\omega_t+u\omega_x+v\omega_y+(u_x+v_y)\omega=0.
$$
<Incompressibility> removes the last term, giving
$$
\boxed{\frac{D\omega}{Dt}=0.}
$$
Thus **each fluid parcel retains its scalar <vorticity>** as it moves. In particular an initially <irrotational flow> remains irrotational in this smooth two-dimensional inviscid evolution. There is no <vortex stretching> in this two-dimensional setting.

Write the <complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow> as $w=\phi+i\psi$. Its <complex velocity> is $u-iv=dw/dz$. Here
$$
\frac{dw}{dz}=U\left(1-\frac{a^2}{z^2}\right)-\frac{i\Gamma}{2\pi z}\longrightarrow U\quad(|z|\to\infty),
$$
so the far-field motion has velocity $(U,0)$ and speed $U$. In <polar coordinates> the <stream function>, shifted by a harmless constant, is
$$
\psi(r,\theta)=U\left(r-\frac{a^2}r\right)\sin\theta-\frac\Gamma{2\pi}\log(r/a).
$$
It is zero on $r=a$, proving the cylinder is a <streamline>.

<Stagnation points> are zeros of $dw/dz$, hence roots of $Uz^2-i\Gamma z/(2\pi)-Ua^2=0$. Put $\gamma=\Gamma/(4\pi Ua)\in(0,1)$. The two roots are
$$
\boxed{z_\pm=\pm a\sqrt{1-\gamma^2}+ia\gamma.}
$$
Both have modulus $a$ and are distinct. Their angles satisfy $\sin\theta=\gamma$, namely $\arcsin\gamma$ and $\pi-\arcsin\gamma$. The tangential velocity on the cylinder is $-2U\sin\theta+\Gamma/(2\pi a)$, confirming the same zeros. <Streamlines> are level curves of $\psi$; the positive <circulation> places both <stagnation points> on the upper side and increases the flow speed below the cylinder.

The <moment generating function> is $M_X(t)=\mathbb E[e^{tX}]$ where finite. For <independent> $X,Y$, <independence> factors the expectation, giving $M_{X+Y}(t)=M_X(t)M_Y(t)$ wherever both factors are finite.

For the given <probability density function> and $t<1$, put $s=(1-t)x$ to obtain
$$
M_{X_n}(t)=\frac1{(n-1)!}\int_0^\infty x^{n-1}e^{-(1-t)x}\,dx=\frac{\Gamma(n)}{(n-1)!(1-t)^n}=\boxed{(1-t)^{-n}}.
$$
Here $\Gamma(n)=(n-1)!$, obtained by repeated <integration by parts>. For $t\geq1$ the defining integral diverges.

Each $Y_i$ has <moment generating function> $(1-t)^{-1}$, so <independence> gives
$$
M_{\sum_{i=1}^nY_i}(t)=(1-t)^{-n}\quad(t<1).
$$
The <uniqueness theorem for moment-generating functions> applies when the functions are finite on a <neighborhood> of zero. Hence **the sum has the same distribution as $X_n$**, a <Gamma distribution> of shape $n$ and rate one, with the displayed <probability density function> in the source.

States $i,j$ communicate when each is accessible from the other: $(P^m)_{ij}>0$ and $(P^n)_{ji}>0$ for some nonnegative integers $m,n$. The communicating classes are
$$
\boxed{\{1,4\}\text{ closed},\qquad\{2,3,5\}\text{ open},\qquad\{6\}\text{ closed}.}

Indeed $1$ and $4$ move only between themselves; $2\to5\to3\to2$ links the second class, but it has exits to $1,4,6$ and no return from the closed classes; state 6 is absorbing.

Let $h_i$ be the <probability> of ever reaching 6 from state $i$. Then $h_1=h_4=0$, $h_6=1$, and first-step conditioning gives
$$
h_2=\frac34h_5,\qquad h_3=\frac12h_2+\frac12h_3,\qquad h_5=\frac14h_3+\frac14h_4+\frac14h_5+\frac14.
$$
Thus $h_3=h_2=3h_5/4$ and $3h_5=h_3+1$, giving $h_5=4/9$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P_3(\text{eventual absorption in }6)=\frac13.}
$$

A $100(1-\alpha)\%$ <confidence interval> procedure produces random endpoints $L,U$ such that $\mathbb P_\mu(L\leq\mu\leq U)=1-\alpha$, under the model. In everyday terms, repeating the same sampling and interval construction many times would give intervals containing the true mean in about $100(1-\alpha)\%$ of repetitions.

For $n\geq2$, define the <sample mean> $\bar X=n^{-1}\sum_iX_i$ and the <sample variance> $S^2=(n-1)^{-1}\sum_i(X_i-\bar X)^2$. For <independent> observations from a <normal distribution> with $\sigma^2>0$, the standard sampling results are
$$
\frac{\sqrt n(\bar X-\mu)}S\sim t_{n-1},\qquad\frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1}.
$$
The first follows from the <independent> <normal sample> mean and <sample variance>. Let $q=t_{n-1,1-\alpha/2}$ be the positive Student <quantile>. Symmetry gives the central <confidence interval>
$$
\boxed{\left[\bar X-q\frac S{\sqrt n},\ \bar X+q\frac S{\sqrt n}\right].}
$$
Its width is $W=2qS/\sqrt n$, so
$$
\boxed{c=\frac{n(n-1)}{4t_{n-1,1-\alpha/2}^{\,2}},\qquad \frac{cW^2}{\sigma^2}=\frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1}.}
$$

To prove it, choose an <eigenvector> $v$ for an <eigenvalue> $\lambda$ and an index $i$ maximizing $|v_i|>0$. Its $i$th equation gives
$$
|\lambda-c_{ii}|\,|v_i|\leq\sum_{j\ne i}|c_{ij}|\,|v_j|\leq R_i|v_i|.
$$
Division proves $\lambda\in D_i$.

The usual counting refinement says that a connected component made of $k$ discs contains exactly $k$ <eigenvalues>, with <algebraic multiplicity>. For completeness, deform the <matrix> from its diagonal part to the original by multiplying all off-diagonal entries by $t\in[0,1]$. Its discs have radii $tR_i$ and stay inside the original discs. Continuous variation of the roots of the <characteristic polynomial> keeps the number inside any contour separating one original component from the others constant. At $t=0$, the diagonal entries give exactly $k$ roots there, proving the refinement.

For the application put $s=-\mu>0$. The <matrix> is
$$
A+sB=\begin{pmatrix}2+2s&-2&0\\-3&5+2s&s\\1&2+s&3+2s\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its three row discs have positive real centres and leftmost real coordinates
$$
(2+2s)-2=2s,\qquad(5+2s)-(3+s)=2+s,\qquad(3+2s)-(3+s)=s.
$$
All are strictly positive, so zero lies in none of the discs. It cannot be an <eigenvalue>. Thus **$A-\mu B$ is nonsingular for every real $\mu<0$**.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Suppose an approximation has an error expansion $A(h)=I+ch^p+O(h^q)$ with $q>p$. With refinement factor $r>1$, combining the two approximations eliminates the leading term:
$$
\boxed{R(h)=\frac{r^pA(h/r)-A(h)}{r^p-1}=I+O(h^q).}
$$
If further expansion terms are known, repeat the elimination at their successive powers. For an even-power trapezium expansion and repeated halving, this gives the Romberg recurrence $R_{k,j}=(4^jR_{k,j-1}-R_{k-1,j-1})/(4^j-1)$, starting from $R_{k,0}=T(2^{-k})$.

For the stated cubic the one-panel trapezium value is $T(1)=[0^3+1^3]/2=1/2$. The two-panel value is
$$
T(1/2)=\frac14\left[0+2\left(\frac12\right)^3+1\right]=\frac5{16}.
$$
The leading power is two, so one extrapolation step gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{4T(1/2)-T(1)}3=\frac{4(5/16)-1/2}3=\frac14.}
$$
This equals the exact integral $[x^4/4]_0^1=1/4$; for a cubic the trapezium error has only the leading $h^2$ term, which this combination removes exactly.
