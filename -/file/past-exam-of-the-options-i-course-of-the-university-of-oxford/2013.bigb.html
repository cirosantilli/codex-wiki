The <degree of a field extension> is the <dimension> of the larger <field> as a <vector space> over the smaller one:
$$
\boxed{|K:F|=\dim_FK.}
$$
The <field extension> is a <finite field extension> precisely when this <dimension> is finite, equivalently when finitely many elements of $K$ span $K$ over $F$. Finiteness here concerns the <dimension>, not the number of elements of the <fields>; for example, $\mathbb C$ has degree two over $\mathbb R$ although both <fields> are infinite.

Let $[L:F]=n<\infty$. The intermediate <field> $K$ is an $F$-linear <linear subspace> of $L$, so the finite-dimensional <linear subspace> theorem gives $[K:F]\leq n$. To handle the other <field extension>, take an $F$-<basis> $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ of $L$. Every $\ell\in L$ is a sum $\sum f_iv_i$ with $f_i\in F\subseteq K$, so the same list spans $L$ as a $K$-<vector space>. It need not remain linearly independent, but spanning suffices to give $[L:K]\leq n$. Hence **both intermediate extensions are finite**.

The <tower law for field extensions> states that, for $F\subseteq K\subseteq L$ with both successive degrees finite,
$$
\boxed{[L:F]=[L:K][K:F].}
$$
Indeed, if $u_i$ form an $F$-<basis> of $K$ and $v_j$ form a $K$-basis of $L$, their products $u_iv_j$ form an $F$-basis of $L$: expansion gives spanning, and independence follows first over $K$ and then over $F$.

Starting from a unit segment, every <straightedge and compass construction> introduces coordinates by solving linear or quadratic equations over the coordinates already available. Thus a <constructible number> belongs to a tower of <field extensions> whose successive degrees are one or two; the total degree is a power of two. Doubling the volume of a unit cube would require the edge length $\sqrt[3]2$. Its <minimal polynomial> over $\mathbb Q$ is $x^3-2$, irreducible by <Eisenstein's criterion> at the <prime number> two. Thus $[\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]2):\mathbb Q]=3$. If that number lay in a construction <field> of degree $2^r$, the <tower law> would make three divide $2^r$, a contradiction. Therefore **doubling the cube by straightedge and compass is impossible**.

An <isometry> of the <Euclidean plane> is a map $T:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^2$ preserving all <Euclidean distances>, $\|T(x)-T(y)\|=\|x-y\|$. Such a map of the whole plane is automatically bijective, and has the form $T(x)=Rx+b$ with $R$ an <orthogonal matrix>.

The classification is **identity, <translations>, <rotations> about a point, <reflections> in a line, and glide <reflections>**. Here a <glide reflection> is a <reflection> followed by a nonzero <translation> parallel to its mirror. The cases preserving <orientation> have $\det R=1$ and are <translations> or <rotations>; the orientation-reversing cases have $\det R=-1$ and are <reflections> or glide <reflections>. The identity is included by allowing a zero <translation> or <rotation> angle.

= Solution

<Euler's totient function> is
$$
\varphi(n)=\#\{1\leq r\leq n:\gcd(r,n)=1\},\qquad n\geq1.
$$
<Euler's theorem> states that $\gcd(a,n)=1$ implies $\boxed{a^{\varphi(n)}\equiv1\pmod n}$. For $n>1$, let $r_1,\ldots,r_{\varphi(n)}$ be the invertible residue classes modulo $n$. Multiplication by $a$ permutes these classes, since it has an inverse modulo $n$. Multiplying the resulting congruences gives
$$
a^{\varphi(n)}r_1\cdots r_{\varphi(n)}\equiv r_1\cdots r_{\varphi(n)}\pmod n.
$$
Every factor is a <unit>, so the product can be cancelled, proving the theorem. The case $n=1$ is immediate. For distinct <prime numbers> $p,q$, removing multiples of either prime from $1,\ldots,pq$ gives
$$
\boxed{\varphi(pq)=pq-q-p+1=(p-1)(q-1).}
$$

For the <RSA cryptosystem>, choose distinct large primes $p,q$, put $N=pq$, and choose $e$ coprime to $\varphi(N)$. Compute $d$ with $ed\equiv1\pmod{\varphi(N)}$. The public key is $(N,e)$; the private exponent $d$ is retained secretly. Represent a message by $0\leq m<N$, encrypt by $c\equiv m^e\pmod N$, and decrypt by $m'\equiv c^d\pmod N$.

To prove correct decryption even for messages not coprime to $N$, write $ed=1+k(p-1)(q-1)$. Modulo $p$, if $p\mid m$ then $m^{ed}\equiv m\equiv0$; otherwise <Fermat's little theorem> gives $m^{ed}\equiv m$. The same reasoning holds modulo $q$. The <Chinese remainder theorem> therefore gives $m^{ed}\equiv m\pmod N$, and the chosen representative recovers $m$. Thus **decryption works for every message residue**, not merely the units.

For a nonnegative <measurable function>, the <Lebesgue integral> is defined through nonnegative <simple functions>:
$$
\boxed{\int_{\mathbb R}f\,dx=\sup\left\{\int_{\mathbb R}s\,dx:s\text{ simple},\ 0\leq s\leq f\right\}.}
$$
The value may be infinite. A real measurable $g$ is <Lebesgue integrable> when $\int|g|dx<\infty$; then $\int g=\int g^+-\int g^-$, where $g^+=\max(g,0)$ and $g^-=\max(-g,0)$.

If $|g|\leq h$, every simple function below $|g|$ is also below $h$. Taking the defining suprema proves <monotonicity of the Lebesgue integral>:
$$
0\leq\int|g|\,dx\leq\int h\,dx<\infty.
$$
Hence **$g$ is integrable**, and both its positive and negative parts have finite <integrals>.

The oscillatory function $k$ is measurable because it is continuous off the singleton $\{0\}$ and has a specified value there. The elementary bounds $|\sin u|\leq\min(1,|u|)$ give the integrable majorant
$$
|k(x)|\leq H(x)=\begin{cases}1,&|x|\leq1,\\|x|^{-3},&|x|>1.\end{cases}
$$
Indeed, $\int H=2+2\int_1^\infty x^{-3}dx=3$. The comparison just proved gives $\int|k|\leq3$, so **$k$ is Lebesgue integrable over the whole real line**, despite its oscillations near zero. Its <odd> symmetry also gives $\int k=0$, now legitimately because absolute integrability has been established.

<Fubini's theorem> states that an absolutely integrable measurable function on a product of two $\sigma$-finite <measure spaces> has integrable sections almost everywhere, the corresponding iterated <integrals> exist, and both orders equal its product <integral>. In particular, for $f\in L^1(\mathbb R^2)$,
$$
\iint f(x,y)\,dx\,dy=\int\left(\int f(x,y)\,dx\right)dy=\int\left(\int f(x,y)\,dy\right)dx.
$$
To establish integrability of the nonnegative function here, first use <Tonelli's theorem>: for a nonnegative measurable function the product <integral> equals either iterated <integral> with infinity allowed. This avoids assuming the conclusion needed for <Fubini's theorem>.

If $\alpha\geq-1$, $\int_0^\infty(1+x+y)^\alpha dx=\infty$ for every fixed $y$. If $\alpha<-1$, it is
$$
\int_0^\infty(1+x+y)^\alpha dx=\frac{(1+y)^{\alpha+1}}{-\alpha-1}.
$$
The outer <integral> is finite exactly when $\alpha+1<-1$, that is, $\alpha<-2$. In that case
$$
\boxed{\iint_{(0,\infty)^2}(1+x+y)^\alpha dx\,dy=\frac1{(-\alpha-1)(-\alpha-2)},\qquad f\in L^1\iff\alpha<-2.}
$$
For $-2\leq\alpha<-1$ the outer <integral> diverges, including logarithmic divergence at $\alpha=-2$. Nonnegativity means finiteness of this <integral> is exactly absolute integrability; after proving it, <Fubini's theorem> allows either order.

A <basis of a topology> $\mathcal T$ is a collection $\mathcal B\subseteq\mathcal T$ such that every <open set> is a union of members of $\mathcal B$. Equivalently, for each open $U$ and every $x\in U$, there exists $B\in\mathcal B$ with $x\in B\subseteq U$. The empty union represents the empty set. In particular, the basis covers $X$, and any intersection of two basis elements is itself a union of basis elements.

Use the <equivalence relation> of belonging to the same fibre:
$$
\boxed{x\mathcal R x'\iff f(x)=f(x').}

The map $\bar f:X/\mathcal R\to Y$, $[x]\mapsto f(x)$, is well defined, injective by the definition of the relation, and surjective because $f$ is surjective. It is continuous by the <quotient topology>: for open $V\subseteq Y$, $q^{-1}(\bar f^{-1}(V))=f^{-1}(V)$ is open.

The <total derivative> of $f$ at $x$ is a <linear map> $Df(x):\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R$ satisfying
$$
\boxed{\lim_{h\to0}\frac{|f(x+h)-f(x)-Df(x)h|}{\|h\|}=0.}
$$
The <gradient> is the <vector> of <partial derivatives>, $\nabla f(x)=(\partial_1f(x),\ldots,\partial_nf(x))$. When $f$ is totally differentiable, these <partial derivatives> exist and
$$
\boxed{Df(x)h=\nabla f(x)\cdot h=\sum_{j=1}^n\partial_jf(x)h_j.}
$$
Thus the derivative is a linear functional represented by the <gradient> under the <Euclidean inner product>. Existence of <partial derivatives> alone does not imply total differentiability.

Take independent smooth <variations> $y+\varepsilon\eta$ and $z+\varepsilon\zeta$. Differentiating the <functional> and using <integration by parts> gives its <first variation>
$$
\delta J=[F_{y'}\eta+F_{z'}\zeta]_a^b+\int_a^b\left[(F_y-\tfrac d{dx}F_{y'})\eta+(F_z-\tfrac d{dx}F_{z'})\zeta\right]dx.
$$
First choose <variations> vanishing at the endpoints. To use the stated form of the <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations>, take $\eta=(x-a)(b-x)\chi$ with arbitrary smooth $\chi$, and similarly for $\zeta$. The lemma makes $(x-a)(b-x)(F_y-dF_{y'}/dx)$ vanish, and hence makes the unweighted coefficient vanish in the interior; continuity extends this to the endpoints. Applying the same argument to $z$ gives the <Euler-Lagrange equations>
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dx}F_{y'}=F_y,\qquad\frac d{dx}F_{z'}=F_z.}
$$
If either endpoint value of $y$ or $z$ is free, the corresponding variation there is arbitrary. The remaining boundary term gives the <natural boundary conditions for a free endpoint>: $F_{y'}=0$ for a free $y$ value and $F_{z'}=0$ for a free $z$ value at that endpoint. With both coordinates free at both endpoints, all four conditions apply.

For the specified integrand these equations reduce to
$$
y''+5y-2z=0,\qquad z''+2y=0,
$$
and the natural conditions are $y'=z'=0$ at each free endpoint. Eliminating $z$ gives
$$
y^{(4)}+5y''+4y=0,\qquad (D^2+1)(D^2+4)y=0.
$$
Its four independent modes give the full general solution
$$
\begin{aligned}
y&=A\cos x+C\sin x+B\cos2x+D\sin2x,\\
z&=2A\cos x+2C\sin x+\tfrac12B\cos2x+\tfrac12D\sin2x.
\end{aligned}
$$
At zero, $y'(0)=C+2D$ and $z'(0)=2C+D$. Vanishing forces $C=D=0$, giving
$$
\boxed{y=A\cos x+B\cos2x,\qquad z=2A\cos x+\frac B2\cos2x.}
$$
At $x=\pi/6$ the two derivative conditions become $A/2+\sqrt3B=0$ and $A+\sqrt3B/2=0$. Their coefficient determinant is nonzero, so $A=B=0$. Thus **the unique stationary extremal with all four natural boundary conditions is $y=z=0$**. The word extremal here means a stationary point of the functional; the indefinite integrand does not require this point to be a minimum.

Where the right side is positive, either sign of its square root is possible, according to the direction of motion. Its zeros are turning points. For $h\ne0$, the centrifugal term excludes the poles; if $h=0$, the polar-coordinate formula is interpreted through the original rod equations.

The polar <Euler-Lagrange equation> is
$$
\ddot\theta=\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2-\frac{3g}{2a}\sin\theta.
$$
For a constant angle with $0<\theta_0<\pi$, conservation of $h$ makes $\dot\phi$ constant, say $\Omega$. The remaining equation is
$$
\cos\theta_0\,\Omega^2=\frac{3g}{2a}.
$$
If $g>0$, every nonvertical constant-angle motion is a <conical pendulum> motion:
$$
\boxed{0<\theta_0<\frac\pi2,\qquad\phi(t)=\phi_0\pm\sqrt{\frac{3g}{2a\cos\theta_0}}\,t.}
$$
The rod sweeps out a cone about the downward vertical, in either azimuthal direction. Equivalently, $h^2=(3g/(2a))\sin^4\theta_0/\cos\theta_0$. The exactly downward and exactly upward vertical rods are also static solutions; azimuth has no physical meaning at either pole. No static tilted rod is possible under nonzero gravity.

If $g=0$, the condition is $\sin\theta_0\cos\theta_0\Omega^2=0$. Thus **a rod may remain at rest in any orientation, or rotate uniformly in the horizontal plane** with $\theta_0=\pi/2$ and arbitrary constant $\Omega$. The vertical cases are included among static orientations; changing only their azimuth does not move the rod.

Branch choices change additive constants, not the local <velocity> <field>. A vortex is transported by the nonsingular <velocity> due to the other vortex and the sink; its own singular term is omitted. At $z=c$ this gives

\dot{\bar c}=\frac{\Gamma}{4\pi i c}-\frac Q{2\pi c},\qquad \dot c=\frac{-Q/(2\pi)+i\Gamma/(4\pi)}{\bar c}.

\boxed{r^2=a^2-\frac Q\pi t,\qquad\theta=-\frac{\Gamma}{4Q}\log\left(1-\frac{Qt}{\pi a^2}\right).}

Equivalently, with $s=1-Qt/(\pi a^2)$,
$$
\boxed{c(t)=a\sqrt s\,e^{-i\Gamma\log s/(4Q)},\qquad z_1(t)=c(t),\quad z_2(t)=-c(t),\quad0\leq t<\frac{\pi a^2}{Q}.}
$$
The curves obey $\theta=(\Gamma/(2Q))\log(a/r)$, so they are <logarithmic spirals>. At $t=\pi a^2/Q$, both vortices reach the sink at the origin. For nonzero circulation they make infinitely many turns in that finite time; for zero circulation they approach radially. **The point-vortex evolution becomes singular at capture**, so the formula does not define a regular continuation through the event.

Put $\tau=T/\rho>0$ and take the positive-frequency branch for $k>0$. The <phase velocity> and <group velocity> are
$$
\boxed{c_p=\frac\omega k=\sqrt{\frac gk+\tau k},\qquad c_g=\frac{d\omega}{dk}=\frac{g+3\tau k^2}{2\sqrt{gk+\tau k^3}}.}
$$
Since the square root is increasing, minimizing $c_p$ is equivalent to minimizing $g/k+\tau k$. Its derivative is $-g/k^2+\tau$, which changes sign from negative to positive at
$$
\boxed{k_c=\sqrt{\frac g\tau}=\sqrt{\frac{\rho g}T}.}
$$
The phase speed diverges at both positive endpoints $k\downarrow0$ and $k\to\infty$, so this stationary point is its global minimum. At $k_c$, $\tau k_c^2=g$, giving
$$
\boxed{c_g(k_c)=c_p(k_c)=\left(\frac{4gT}{\rho}\right)^{1/4}.}
$$
The relative speed is especially transparent:
$$
c_g-c_p=\frac{\tau k^2-g}{2\omega}.
$$
A narrow <wave packet> moves with $c_g$, while its crests move with $c_p$. For $k<k_c$, $c_p>c_g$: **crests move forward through the packet**, from its rear towards its front. For $k>k_c$, $c_p<c_g$: **crests drift backwards relative to the packet**, although both absolute velocities are positive. At $k_c$ the leading-order relative drift vanishes. This is the <minimum phase speed of a gravity-capillary wave>.

$$
\boxed{\widehat\theta=\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^n|X_j|.}
$$
The exceptional data set $S=0$ has <probability> zero; there the likelihood has its supremum at the excluded boundary $\theta=0$. For the <Fisher information>, $|X_j|$ has exponential density $\theta^{-1}e^{-y/\theta}$ for $y>0$, hence mean $\theta$. Since $\ell''(\theta)=n/\theta^2-2S/\theta^3$,
$$
\boxed{I_n(\theta)=-\mathbb E_\theta\ell''(\theta)=\frac n{\theta^2}.}
$$
This also follows from the score <variance>, because $\operatorname{Var}(S)=n\theta^2$.
