= Solution
{parent=2010/A/A1/1}

In characteristic $p$, the binomial theorem gives $(\alpha+1)^p=\alpha^p+1$. Hence
$$
(\alpha+1)^p-(\alpha+1)-a=\alpha^p-\alpha-a=0.
$$
Iterating shows that all $\alpha+j$, with $j\in\mathbb F_p$, are roots. They are distinct, since the prime-<field> elements $j$ are distinct. There are $p$ of them, equal to the <polynomial>'s degree. Thus if $\alpha\in F$,
$$
\boxed{X^p-X-a=\prod_{j\in\mathbb F_p}(X-\alpha-j)}
$$
splits into distinct linear factors in $F[X]$. Equivalently, its derivative is $-1$, so no repeated roots are possible.

= 2
{parent=2010/A/A1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2010/A/A1/2}

Interpret the displayed factorization as a proper factorization, with $1\leq r\leq p-1$; that restriction is necessary for the conclusion. In a <splitting field>, the monic degree-$r$ factor has $r$ of the distinct roots of the original <polynomial>. Comparing its coefficient of $X^{r-1}$ gives
$$
-a_1=\sum_{j\in J}(\alpha+j)=r\alpha+\sum_{j\in J}j,
$$
for an $r$-element subset $J\subseteq\mathbb F_p$. Both $a_1$ and the final sum lie in $F$. Since $0<r<p$, the element $r$ is invertible in $F$, so
$$
\boxed{\alpha=r^{-1}\left(-a_1-\sum_{j\in J}j\right)\in F.}
$$
Part (1) then gives complete splitting into distinct linear factors. Thus an <Artin–Schreier polynomial> of this form either is irreducible or splits completely; a trivial factorization with $r=0$ or $r=p$ would not imply a <root> in $F$.

= Solution
{parent=2010/A/A1/3}

Reduce the monic <integer> <polynomial> modulo the prime $p$. For every $x\in\mathbb F_p$, $x^p=x$, so
$$
x^p-x-1=-1\neq0.
$$
If the reduction were reducible, a proper monic factorization would, by part (2), force a <root> in $\mathbb F_p$, a contradiction. Its reduction is therefore irreducible of degree $p$. The monic reduction criterion, obtained from <Gauss lemma for polynomials> by reducing a hypothetical <integer> factorization, now proves
$$
\boxed{X^p-X-1\text{ is irreducible in }\mathbb Q[X].}
$$

= Solution

<Burnside's lemma> gives the number of orbits as
$$
|\Omega/G|=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}|\operatorname{Fix}(g)|.
$$
For $m=2n-1\geq3$, the polygon symmetry <group> contains the $m$ rotations through angles $2\pi j/m$, $0\leq j<m$, and $m$ reflections. Each <reflection> axis passes through a vertex and the midpoint of the opposite edge. These are all the symmetries, so $|G|=2m=\boxed{4n-2}$.

For the pentagon, apply the same <group> element simultaneously to vertex colours and edge colours. The identity fixes $v^5e^5$ colourings. Each of the four nonidentity rotations is a 5-cycle on vertices and also on edges, fixing $ve$ colourings. Each of the five reflections has one fixed vertex and two swapped vertex pairs, and one fixed edge and two swapped edge pairs. It therefore fixes $v^3e^3$ colourings. Averaging yields
$$
\boxed{\frac{v^5e^5+4ve+5v^3e^3}{10}.}
$$
The two colour palettes are chosen independently, but rotations and reflections act on both parts of the same pentagon.

\boxed{\left(\frac ap\right)=\begin{cases}
0,&p\mid a,\\

= Solution
{parent=2010/C/C1/ii}

The <Euler criterion> states that for $p\nmid a$,
$$
\boxed{a^{(p-1)/2}\equiv\left(\frac ap\right)\pmod p.}
$$
The same <modular congruence> holds for $p\mid a$, with both sides zero.

= iii
{parent=2010/C/C1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2010/C/C1/iii}

Write $\chi(a)=(a/p)$. If $p\mid a$, the claimed identity has both sides zero. Otherwise let $\epsilon=\chi(a)\in\{1,-1\}$. Applying the <Euler criterion> gives
$$
\chi(\epsilon a)\equiv(\epsilon a)^{(p-1)/2}
\equiv\epsilon^{(p-1)/2}\chi(a)=\epsilon^{(p+1)/2}\pmod p.
$$
Both sides are in $\{1,-1\}$, which are distinct <residue> classes because $p$ is odd. Therefore the <modular congruence> is an equality:
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\left(\frac ap\right)a}p\right)
=\left(\frac ap\right)^{(p+1)/2}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=2010/C/C1/iv}

Again set $\epsilon=(a/p)\in\{1,-1\}$. The <Euler criterion> applied to $\epsilon$ gives $\chi(\epsilon)=\epsilon^{(p-1)/2}$, as an equality of signs. If $p\equiv1\pmod4$, the exponent is even and the result is $1$; if $p\equiv3\pmod4$, it is odd and the result is $\epsilon$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{\left(\frac ap\right)}p\right)
=\begin{cases}1,&p\equiv1\pmod4,\\(a/p),&p\equiv3\pmod4.\end{cases}}
$$

For every real $a$,
$$
\{x\in E:-g(x)\geq a\}
=E\setminus\{x\in E:g(x)>-a\}.

Part (1), applied to measurable $g$, makes the set being complemented measurable. Its relative complement in measurable $E$ is measurable too. The permitted half-line criterion therefore proves **$-g$ is measurable**.

= Solution
{parent=2010/D/D1/3}

If $f$ is measurable, then for a threshold $a\leq0$, $\{f^+\geq a\}=E$, while for $a>0$, $\{f^+\geq a\}=\{f\geq a\}$. The half-line criterion makes $f^+$ measurable. By part (2), $-f$ is measurable, and $f^-=(-f)^+$ is therefore measurable as well.

Conversely, if $f^+$ and $f^-$ are measurable, part (2) and the permitted <closure> under sums show that $f=f^+-f^-$ is measurable. Thus **measurability is equivalent to measurability of both parts**.

Since $|f|=f^++f^-$, part (3) and <closure> under sums prove that $|f|$ is measurable whenever $f$ is.

**The converse is false.** Let $A\subseteq[0,1]$ be a non-Lebesgue-measurable set and define $f=1$ on $A$ and $f=-1$ on its complement in $[0,1]$. Then $|f|=1$ is measurable, but $f^{-1}([0,\infty))=A$ is not. Taking absolute values can conceal a nonmeasurable sign choice.

A <topology> on $X$ contains $\varnothing$ and $X$, is closed under arbitrary unions, and is closed under finite intersections. Its members are the open sets. A subset is closed precisely when its complement in $X$ is open.

= ii
{parent=2010/E/E1/a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2010/E/E1/a/ii}

The <subspace topology> on $Y$ is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal T_Y=\{Y\cap U:U\in\mathcal T\}.}
$$
If $Y$ is open in $X$, every such intersection is open in $X$. Conversely, every ambient <open set> contained in $Y$ equals its intersection with $Y$. Thus the relatively open sets are exactly the ambient open subsets contained in $Y$.

= iii
{parent=2010/E/E1/a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2010/E/E1/a/iii}

The <closure> of $A$ is the smallest closed subset of $X$ containing it:
$$
\boxed{\overline A=\bigcap\{F\subseteq X:F\text{ closed and }A\subseteq F\}.}
$$
Equivalently, a point belongs to $\overline A$ exactly when every open neighborhood of it meets $A$.

= b
{parent=2010/E/E1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2010/E/E1/b}

For $(i)\Rightarrow(ii)$, suppose $B=U\cap F$ with $U$ open and $F$ closed. Since $B\subseteq F$, also $\overline B\subseteq F$. Therefore $U\cap\overline B=B$, showing that $B$ is relatively open in its <closure>.

For $(ii)\Rightarrow(iii)$, write $B=U\cap\overline B$ with $U$ open in $X$. Then $\overline B\setminus B=\overline B\cap(X\setminus U)$ is an intersection of closed sets, hence closed in $X$.

For $(iii)\Rightarrow(i)$, set $F=\overline B$ and $U=X\setminus(\overline B\setminus B)$. The assumed condition makes $U$ open; $F$ is closed and $U\cap F=B$. Thus **the three conditions are equivalent**. They characterize a <locally closed subset>.

A space is <Hausdorff space>[Hausdorff] if any two distinct points have disjoint open neighborhoods: for $x\neq y$, there are open $U\ni x$ and $V\ni y$ with $U\cap V=\varnothing$.

{parent=2010/E/E2/a}

= Solution
{parent=2010/E/E2/a/ii}

The map $f:X\to Y$ is continuous if
$$
\boxed{U\text{ open in }Y\ \Longrightarrow\ f^{-1}(U)\text{ open in }X.}
$$
This condition concerns inverse images of open sets; images of open sets need not be open.

Let $p:X\to X/R$ send a point to its <equivalence class>. The <quotient topology> is
$$
\boxed{U\subseteq X/R\text{ is open exactly when }p^{-1}(U)\text{ is open in }X.}
$$
It is the finest <topology> on the quotient making $p$ continuous.

= Solution
{parent=2010/E/E2/b/i}

For each <continuous map> to a Hausdorff target, equality of its values is reflexive, symmetric and transitive. The relation $R$ requires all these equalities simultaneously. Explicitly, $f(x)=f(x)$ gives reflexivity; $f(x)=f(y)$ implies $f(y)=f(x)$; and $f(x)=f(y)=f(z)$ gives $f(x)=f(z)$. These arguments apply to every map in the defining family, proving **$R$ is an <equivalence relation>**.

= ii
{parent=2010/E/E2/b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2010/E/E2/b/ii}

If $x\in A=g^{-1}(B)$ and $yRx$, then $g(y)=g(x)\in B$, so $y\in A$. Thus $A$ contains every <equivalence class> it meets: it is a <saturation of a subset>[saturated subset]. Always $A\subseteq p^{-1}(p(A))$; conversely, a point of the latter set is equivalent to some $x\in A$ and hence belongs to $A$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{p^{-1}(p(A))=A.}
$$

= iii
{parent=2010/E/E2/b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2010/E/E2/b/iii}

Take distinct classes $p(x)\neq p(y)$. The points are not related by $R$, so by the defining negation there is a <Hausdorff space> $Z$ and a <continuous map> $f:X\to Z$ with $f(x)\neq f(y)$. Choose disjoint open neighborhoods $U$ and $V$ of those two values. Their inverse images $A=f^{-1}(U)$ and $C=f^{-1}(V)$ are disjoint open subsets of $X$ and are saturated by part (ii).

Hence $p^{-1}(p(A))=A$ and $p^{-1}(p(C))=C$, making $p(A)$ and $p(C)$ open in the <quotient topology>. They contain the chosen classes and are disjoint: an <equivalence class> meeting both would contradict saturation and $A\cap C=\varnothing$. Thus **$X/R$ is Hausdorff**. This quotient is the <Hausdorff reflection by continuous maps>; every <continuous map> from $X$ to a <Hausdorff space> factors uniquely through it.

= Solution
{parent=2010/F/F1/a}

The <inverse function theorem> states that a $C^1$ map on an open subset of $\mathbb R^2$ has a $C^1$ local inverse near a point where its derivative is invertible; the inverse derivative at the image point is the inverse <matrix> of the original derivative.

Put $s=1+x+y$. Quotient differentiation gives
$$
\boxed{Df(x,y)=\frac1{s^2}\begin{pmatrix}1+y&-x\\-y&1+x\end{pmatrix},
\qquad\det Df(x,y)=\frac1{s^3}.}
$$
The entries are smooth and the determinant is nonzero everywhere on the stated domain, so the theorem supplies the required local inverse at every point. The point $(1/3,1/3)$ is the image of $(1,1)$, where
$$
Df(1,1)=\frac19\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{Df^{-1}(1/3,1/3)=\begin{pmatrix}6&3\\3&6\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Directly solving the two rational equations also gives $f^{-1}(u,v)=(u/(1-u-v),v/(1-u-v))$ on $u+v\neq1$, consistent with the derivative calculation.

= Solution
{parent=2010/F/F1/b}

For each fixed $(x,y)$, the function $z\mapsto z^3+z+xy-1$ is continuous, strictly increasing because its derivative is $3z^2+1>0$, and tends to opposite infinities at the two ends of the real line. The intermediate value theorem therefore gives exactly one zero.

For $F(x,y,z)=z^3+z+xy-1$, its partial derivative $F_z=3z^2+1$ never vanishes. The <implicit function theorem> gives a local $C^1$ solution near every point of the solution set. Uniqueness makes these local solutions agree, producing a global $g\in C^1(\mathbb R^2)$. Implicit differentiation gives
$$
Dg(x,y)=\frac{-1}{3g(x,y)^2+1}\begin{pmatrix}y&x\end{pmatrix}.
$$
At $(1,1)$ the <root> is $g(1,1)=0$, so
$$
\boxed{Dg(1,1)=\begin{pmatrix}-1&-1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=2010/G/G1}

For fixed endpoint values, independent variations of $u$ and $v$ give the <Euler-Lagrange equation>[Euler-Lagrange equations]
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dt}F_{\dot u}-F_u=0,\qquad
\frac d{dt}F_{\dot v}-F_v=0.}
$$
The <sphere> parametrization has metric $ds^2=a^2(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)$, obtained by differentiating its position vector and taking its Euclidean squared length. The displayed functional is therefore one half the integral of squared speed, the <geodesic energy>. Its fixed-endpoint extremals are affinely parametrized geodesics; energy stationarity chooses a constant-speed parametrization.

Its explicit equations, after dividing by $a^2$, are
$$
\boxed{\ddot\theta-\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2=0,
\qquad\frac d{dt}(\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi)=0.}
$$
The second gives $\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi=h$. Since the Lagrangian has no explicit time dependence, its energy $\dot q\cdot L_{\dot q}-L=L$ is conserved. Thus
$$
\boxed{\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi=h,\qquad
\dot\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2=E.}
$$
The same second identity follows by differentiating it and substituting the two equations. These polar-coordinate formulas hold on a nonsingular coordinate patch; the <geodesic> curve itself can pass through a pole in another chart.

= Solution
{parent=2010/H/H1}

Expand about the equilibrium and keep only terms quadratic in displacement and velocity. Writing $M_{ij}=T_{ij}(0)$ and $K_{ij}=\partial_i\partial_jV(0)$, the linearized Lagrangian is $L_2=\dot x^TM\dot x/2-x^TKx/2$, up to a constant. Its equations are $M\ddot x+Kx=0$. With positive-definite <mass matrix> $M$, the normal frequencies and mode vectors solve
$$
\boxed{Kv=\omega^2Mv,\qquad\det(K-\omega^2M)=0.}
$$
For a nondegenerate <stable equilibrium>, $K$ is positive definite and all these frequencies are positive. A zero <eigenvalue> indicates a zero-frequency linearized mode requiring higher-order analysis if nonlinear stability is in question.

The three spring extensions are $x_1$, $x_2-x_1$ and $-x_2$. Hence
$$
\boxed{T=\frac m2(\dot x_1^2+\dot x_2^2),\qquad
V=\frac k2\big[x_1^2+(x_2-x_1)^2+x_2^2\big].}
$$
The matrices are $M=mI$ and $K=k\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}$. The two independent modes are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
v_1&=(1,1)^T,&\omega_1&=\sqrt{k/m},\\
v_2&=(1,-1)^T,&\omega_2&=\sqrt{3k/m}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
In the first mode both masses move together with equal displacement, leaving the middle spring unchanged. In the second they move in opposite directions with equal magnitude; the midpoint stays fixed and the middle spring changes length by twice either displacement. Arbitrary small motion is a superposition of sinusoidal motions in these two modes.

\Image[2010-two-mass-spring-modes.png]
{title=The two normal modes of equal masses joined to fixed walls by three equal springs}
{description=The in-phase mode leaves the middle spring unchanged; the opposite-phase mode stretches or compresses it by twice either mass displacement.}

These are the <normal modes of two equal masses between three springs>.

= Solution
{parent=2010/J/J1}

For a real potential, the time-dependent <Schrödinger equation> is
$$
\boxed{i\hbar\partial_t\psi=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\Delta\psi+V\psi.}
$$
Multiply it by $\psi^*$, combine with its conjugate multiplied by $\psi$, and note that the real-potential terms cancel. This gives
$$
\partial_t|\psi|^2=\frac{i\hbar}{2m}(\psi^*\Delta\psi-\psi\Delta\psi^*)
=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf j,
$$
where the <probability current> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf j=\frac{\hbar}{2mi}(\psi^*\nabla\psi-\psi\nabla\psi^*)
=\frac\hbar m\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\nabla\psi).}
$$
The product rule gives the divergence identity because the gradient cross terms cancel.

For $F(r)=\sin(kr)/r$, the supplied radial identity gives $\Delta F=-k^2F$ for $r>0$. The time factor gives $i\hbar\psi_t=E\psi$, so the free equation holds for
$$
\boxed{k=\frac{\sqrt{2mE}}\hbar.}
$$
The apparent origin singularity is removable: $F(0)=k$ and $F(r)=k-k^3r^2/6+\cdots$, so the equation extends smoothly there as well. Since the spatial factor is real and its time dependence is a common phase, $\psi^*\nabla\psi=F\nabla F$ is real. Thus $\mathbf j=0$, while $\rho=F^2$ is independent of time. Both terms in the <continuity equation> vanish separately. This is a standing <spherical wave>; the real spatial factor carries no net radial <probability current>.

= K1

= Solution
{parent=2010/K/K1}

For a <material volume> $V(t)$, Newton's second law equates the rate of change of momentum to <pressure> and body forces:
$$
\frac d{dt}\int_{V(t)}\rho\mathbf u\,dV
=-\int_{\partial V(t)}p\mathbf n\,dS+\int_{V(t)}\rho\mathbf g\,dV.
$$
Apply the supplied <Reynolds transport theorem> to each component of $\mathbf u$, and apply the <divergence theorem> to the <pressure> force. The result is
$$
\int_{V(t)}\left[\rho\left(\partial_t\mathbf u+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\right)
+\nabla p-\rho\mathbf g\right]dV=0.
$$
Since the <material volume> at the chosen time is arbitrary, the integrand vanishes pointwise for smooth fields. Dividing by positive <density> gives the <Euler equations>
$$
\boxed{\partial_t\mathbf u+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u
=-\rho^{-1}\nabla p+\mathbf g.}
$$
At a fixed time $t$, a <streamline> is a curve tangent everywhere to the instantaneous velocity. Parametrize it by $d\mathbf x/ds=\mathbf u(\mathbf x(s),t)$. Then
$$
\frac d{ds}f(\mathbf x(s),t)=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u=0,
$$
so $f$ is constant along it when the displayed directional derivative vanishes. Here time is held fixed: streamlines need not coincide with particle paths in an unsteady flow.

For $k>0$, try
$$
\eta=A\cos(kx-\omega t),\qquad
\phi=B\cosh(k(y+h))\sin(kx-\omega t).
$$
The potential satisfies Laplace's equation, and its derivative with respect to $y$ vanishes at $y=-h$. The surface kinematic condition gives
$$
Bk\sinh(kh)=A\omega.
$$
The dynamic condition gives $-B\omega\cosh(kh)+gA=0$. Eliminating the nonzero amplitudes yields
$$
\boxed{\omega^2=gk\tanh(kh).}
$$
Thus choosing either frequency sign satisfying this relation and $B=A\omega/(k\sinh(kh))$ produces the required harmonic wave.

Each before/after pair concerns the same individual, so subject-specific baseline blood <pressure> can affect both measurements. Analyzing the within-person difference retains this pairing and removes a common baseline contribution. Under the stated independent normal-difference model,
$$
\overline D\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n),\qquad
S_D^2=\frac1{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(D_i-\overline D)^2.
$$
The normal-<sample variance> identity gives
$$
\boxed{c=\frac{n-1}{\sigma^2},\qquad cS_D^2\sim\chi^2_{n-1}.}
$$
Moreover $\overline D$ and $S_D^2$ are independent, because the sample-mean projection and centered residual projection of a Gaussian vector are orthogonal and hence independent. Thus the standardized normal variable $N=\sqrt n(\overline D-\mu)/\sigma$ is independent of $U=(n-1)S_D^2/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-1}$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\frac{\overline D-\mu}{S_D/\sqrt n}
=\frac N{\sqrt{U/(n-1)}}\sim t_{n-1}.}
$$
This distribution is free of both unknown parameters.

= Solution
{parent=2010/P/P2}

A <QR decomposition> is a factorization $A=QR$ with $Q^TQ=I$ and $R$ upper triangular. An orthogonal transformation preserves the Euclidean <norm> because
$$
\boxed{\|Qx\|^2=x^TQ^TQx=x^Tx=\|x\|^2.}
$$
For the basic <QR algorithm>, start with $A_0=A$ and repeatedly form
$$
A_k=Q_kR_k,\qquad A_{k+1}=R_kQ_k.
$$
In a convergent diagonal form, the diagonal entries give the eigenvalues; practical symmetric-<matrix> implementations use shifts and deflation to obtain and isolate such convergence. The basic unshifted iteration alone need not converge diagonally for every symmetric input.

At every step, however,
$$
\boxed{A_{k+1}=R_kQ_k=Q_k^TA_kQ_k.}
$$
Thus each iterate is orthogonally similar to its predecessor. Similar matrices have the same <characteristic polynomial> and eigenvalues, including multiplicities, so induction proves that every iterate has exactly the original eigenvalues. <Orthogonal similarity> also preserves symmetry.
