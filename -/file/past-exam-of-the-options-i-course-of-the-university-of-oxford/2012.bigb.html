The <degree of a field extension> is the dimension of the larger <field>, regarded as a <vector space> over the smaller field:
$$
\boxed{[K:F]=\dim_F K.}
$$
The <scalar multiplication> is the multiplication of $K$ restricted to scalars in $F$. A finite extension has finite dimension; otherwise the degree is infinite.

= b
{parent=A1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The notation $K=F(\delta)$ means that $K$ is the smallest <subfield> containing both $F$ and $\delta$, namely the <simple field extension> generated by $\delta$. Its elements can be expressed as rational functions in $\delta$ with coefficients in $F$ and nonzero denominator.

Write $d=\delta^2\in F$. The element $\delta$ is a root of $X^2-d$, so its <minimal polynomial> over $F$ has degree at most two. Since $\delta\notin F$, that degree cannot be one. The degree of the <simple field extension> equals the degree of the <minimal polynomial>, giving
$$
\boxed{[F(\delta):F]=2.}
$$
In particular $1,\delta$ form an $F$-basis, so every element is uniquely $a+b\delta$. This argument also works in characteristic two; separability is not needed for this part.

= Solution

Choose $\alpha\in K\setminus F$. Since $[K:F]=2$, the linearly independent elements $1,\alpha$ form a <basis> of $K$ over $F$. Thus there are $a,b\in F$ with $\alpha^2=a+b\alpha$. The assumption on the <characteristic of a field> allows division by two. Set
$$
\delta=\alpha-\frac b2.
$$
Completing the square gives
$$
\boxed{\delta^2=a+\frac{b^2}{4}\in F.}
$$
Also $\delta\notin F$, since otherwise $\alpha=\delta+b/2$ would lie in $F$. Because $\alpha\in F(\delta)$ and $\delta\in F(\alpha)$, these generated <subfields> agree. The basis representation shows $F(\alpha)=K$, hence
$$
\boxed{K=F(\delta),\qquad\delta^2\in F.}
$$
The square-root generator is obtained by translating an arbitrary generator to remove the linear term of its quadratic relation.

A left <group action> of $G$ on a <set> $S$ is a map $G\times S\to S$, written $(g,s)\mapsto g\cdot s$, satisfying
$$
\boxed{e\cdot s=s,\qquad (gh)\cdot s=g\cdot(h\cdot s)}
$$
for every $s\in S$ and $g,h\in G$, where $e$ is the <identity element> of $G$. In particular each $g$ acts by a bijection, whose inverse is the action of $g^{-1}$.

= b
{parent=B1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Given a <group action>, define $\rho(g)(j)=g\cdot j$. Each $\rho(g)$ is a <permutation>, since $\rho(g^{-1})$ is its inverse. The action law gives, for every $j$,
$$
\rho(gh)(j)=(gh)\cdot j=g\cdot(h\cdot j)=(\rho(g)\rho(h))(j).
$$
Thus $\boxed{\rho:G\to S_n\text{ is a group homomorphism}}$.

Conversely, given a <group homomorphism> $\rho:G\to S_n$, put $g\cdot j=\rho(g)(j)$. Since a homomorphism sends the identity to the identity permutation, $e\cdot j=j$. Also
$$
(gh)\cdot j=\rho(gh)(j)=\rho(g)(\rho(h)(j))=g\cdot(h\cdot j).
$$
These are exactly the two <group action> axioms, so the construction defines the required action.

The three nonzero <vectors> in $\mathbb F_2^2$ are $e_1$, $e_2$ and $e_1+e_2$. An invertible <linear map> permutes this three-element set, so part (b) gives a <group homomorphism>
$$
\rho:GL_2(\mathbb F_2)\longrightarrow S_3.
$$
If a matrix acts trivially on all three vectors, it fixes both basis vectors $e_1,e_2$, so it is the identity matrix. Thus the <kernel of a group homomorphism> is trivial and $\rho$ is injective.

To count the domain, its first column may be any of the three nonzero vectors. The second column must be outside the two-element span of the first, leaving two choices. Hence
$$
|GL_2(\mathbb F_2)|=3\cdot2=6=|S_3|.
$$
An injection between these finite sets of equal size is surjective, proving
$$
\boxed{GL_2(\mathbb F_2)\cong S_3.}
$$

For a positive <integer> $n$, <Euler's totient function> counts the integers in a complete residue system that are <coprime> to $n$:
$$
\boxed{\varphi(n)=\#\{k:1\leq k\leq n,\ \gcd(k,n)=1\}.}
$$
For $n>1$ this is equally the count with $0<k<n$. The convention gives $\varphi(1)=1$.

= b
{parent=C1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let $R_n=\{k\in\mathbb Z:0<k<n,\ \gcd(k,n)=1\}$. The map $k\mapsto n-k$ sends $R_n$ to itself because
$$
\gcd(n,n-k)=\gcd(n,k)=1.
$$
Applying it twice returns $k$, so it is a bijection. Reindexing the finite sum by this bijection gives
$$
\boxed{\sum_{k\in R_n}k=\sum_{k\in R_n}(n-k).}
$$
There is no need to assume that every residue belongs to a pair of distinct elements; the reindexing also handles the fixed point when $n=2$.

Write $S=\sum_{k\in R_n}k$. Adding the two equal sums from part (b), and using $|R_n|=\varphi(n)$, gives
$$
2S=\sum_{k\in R_n}\{k+(n-k)\}=\sum_{k\in R_n}n=n\varphi(n).
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{S=\frac12n\varphi(n).}
$$
Equivalently, the average of the positive reduced residues is $n/2$, a consequence of their symmetry under reflection about that midpoint.

A <sigma-algebra> $\mathcal F$ on $\Omega$ contains $\Omega$, is closed under complements relative to $\Omega$, and is closed under countable unions. Consequently it contains the empty set and is closed under countable intersections. A <measure space> is a triple $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mu)$ with a map $\mu:\mathcal F\to[0,\infty]$ satisfying $\mu(\varnothing)=0$ and <countable additivity> on pairwise disjoint measurable sets.

For the increasing sequence, set $C_1=A_1$ and $C_n=A_n\setminus A_{n-1}$ for $n\geq2$. These measurable sets are disjoint, their union is $A$, and $A_N=\bigcup_{n=1}^NC_n$. Thus <countable additivity> and finite additivity give
$$
\boxed{\mu(A)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(C_n)
=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=1}^N\mu(C_n)
=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mu(A_N).}
$$
The identity permits the value infinity; it uses no subtraction of infinite measures. This is <continuity from below of a measure>.

For a decreasing sequence, the analogous assertion is **not always true**. On $\Omega=\mathbb N$ with the <counting measure>, take $B_n=\{n,n+1,\ldots\}$. Then $\bigcap_nB_n=\varnothing$, so $\mu(B)=0$, whereas $\mu(B_n)=\infty$ for every $n$ and their limit is infinity.

A sufficient extra condition for <continuity from above of a measure> is $\mu(B_1)<\infty$. Indeed $B_1\setminus B_n$ increases to $B_1\setminus B$, and the increasing-sequence result gives
$$
\mu(B_n)=\mu(B_1)-\mu(B_1\setminus B_n)\longrightarrow\mu(B).
$$
More generally it suffices that one of the $B_n$ has finite measure. The missing finiteness assumption is exactly what the counterexample violates.

On any bounded parameter interval $|t|\leq T$, the <derivative> of the integrand has the integrable majorant
$$
\left|\frac\partial{\partial t}\bigl[e^{-x^2}\cos(2xt^2)\bigr]\right|
=|4xt e^{-x^2}\sin(2xt^2)|\leq4Tx e^{-x^2}.
$$
The <dominated convergence theorem> therefore justifies <differentiation under the integral sign> at every real $t$, giving
$$
f'(t)=-4t\int_0^\infty xe^{-x^2}\sin(2xt^2)\,dx.
$$
Use <integration by parts> in this absolutely convergent integral, with $xe^{-x^2}\,dx=-\tfrac12d(e^{-x^2})$. Both boundary terms vanish, since the sine is zero at $x=0$ and the Gaussian tends to zero at infinity. Hence
$$
\int_0^\infty xe^{-x^2}\sin(2xt^2)\,dx
=t^2\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}\cos(2xt^2)\,dx=t^2f(t).
$$
It follows that $f'=-4t^3f$, or equivalently $(e^{t^4}f(t))'=0$. This identity holds on all of $\mathbb R$, including zero. With the supplied Gaussian integral value,
$$
\boxed{f(t)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}e^{-t^4}\qquad(t\in\mathbb R).}
$$
The fourth power comes from the printed phase $2xt^2$; the parameter has been differentiated with that phase throughout.

The map is $K$-<Lipschitz continuous> when
$$
\boxed{d_Y(f(x),f(x'))\leq Kd_X(x,x')\quad\text{for every }x,x'\in X.}
$$
The same finite <Lipschitz constant> $K$ must work for all pairs of points.

The map is <uniformly continuous> when, for every $\epsilon>0$, there is a $\delta>0$ such that
$$
\boxed{d_X(x,x')<\delta\ \Longrightarrow\ d_Y(f(x),f(x'))<\epsilon
\quad\text{for all }x,x'\in X.}
$$
The choice of $\delta$ may depend on $\epsilon$ but must be independent of the locations of $x$ and $x'$.

Given $\epsilon>0$, choose $\delta=\epsilon/K>0$. If $d_X(x,x')<\delta$, the <Lipschitz condition> yields

d_Y(f(x),f(x'))\leq Kd_X(x,x')<K\delta=\epsilon.
$$
This single $\delta$ works for every pair, proving the map is <uniformly continuous>:
$$
\boxed{\delta(\epsilon)=\epsilon/K.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=c}

The <distance to a set> is finite and nonnegative because $A$ is nonempty. For $x,x'\in X$ and every $a\in A$, the <triangle inequality> gives
$$
d(x,a)\leq d(x,x')+d(x',a).
$$
Taking the infimum over $a$ yields $D_A(x)\leq d(x,x')+D_A(x')$. Interchanging $x$ and $x'$ gives the opposite inequality. Together these imply
$$
\boxed{|D_A(x)-D_A(x')|\leq d(x,x').}
$$
Thus the <distance to a set> is $1$-<Lipschitz continuous>. No closedness, compactness or attainment of the infimum was used.

= Solution
{parent=d}

For fixed $x\in X$, the function $a\mapsto d(x,a)$ on $A$ is continuous, indeed $1$-<Lipschitz continuous> by the <reverse triangle inequality>. A continuous real-valued function on a nonempty <compact space> attains its minimum by the <extreme value theorem>. Hence some $a_0\in A$ satisfies
$$
\boxed{d(x,a_0)=\min_{a\in A}d(x,a)=D_A(x).}
$$
The metric is symmetric, so this is also $d(a_0,x)$. Compactness supplies attainment of the infimum.

A <basis of a topology> $\mathcal B$ is a family of open subsets such that every open set is a union of members of $\mathcal B$. Equivalently, for every $U\in\mathcal T$ and $x\in U$, some $B\in\mathcal B$ satisfies
$$
\boxed{x\in B\subseteq U.}
$$
The empty open set is represented by the empty union.

= b
{parent=E2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Define $\mathcal T$ to be all unions of members of $\mathcal B$, including the empty union. Then $\varnothing\in\mathcal T$ and the covering assumption gives $X\in\mathcal T$. Arbitrary unions of such sets are again unions of basis members.

For two members $U=\bigcup_iB_i$ and $V=\bigcup_jB'_j$, distribute intersection over union:
$$
U\cap V=\bigcup_{i,j}(B_i\cap B'_j).
$$
Each intersection is a union of members of $\mathcal B$ by the second assumption, so $U\cap V\in\mathcal T$. Repeating gives closure under all finite intersections. These are precisely the <topology> axioms. Every $B\in\mathcal B$ belongs to $\mathcal T$, and every set in $\mathcal T$ is a union of such members by construction. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathcal T=\left\{\bigcup\mathcal C:\mathcal C\subseteq\mathcal B\right\},\qquad
\mathcal B\text{ is a basis for }\mathcal T.}
$$

{parent=E2}

= Solution

The <product topology> on $X\times Y$ has as a basis all open rectangles
$$
\boxed{U\times V,\qquad U\in\mathcal T_X,\quad V\in\mathcal T_Y.}
$$
Thus a subset $W\subseteq X\times Y$ is open precisely when, around every $(x,y)\in W$, there are open $U\ni x$ and $V\ni y$ with $U\times V\subseteq W$. These rectangles cover the product, and their pairwise intersections are rectangles because $(U\times V)\cap(U'\times V')=(U\cap U')\times(V\cap V')$. Hence they define a topology by part (b).

This is equivalently the coarsest <topology> making the two coordinate <projection maps> continuous: the preimages of factor-open sets are the open strips $U\times Y$ and $X\times V$, whose finite intersections are the basis rectangles.

Let $\{B_i:i\in I\}$ and $\{C_j:j\in J\}$ be countable bases of the two factors. The family
$$
\mathcal D=\{B_i\times C_j:(i,j)\in I\times J\}
$$
is countable, since the Cartesian product of two countable sets is countable. It is a <basis of a topology> for the <product topology>: given a product-open neighbourhood of $(x,y)$, choose a contained open rectangle $U\times V$, then basis members with $x\in B_i\subseteq U$ and $y\in C_j\subseteq V$. Their product contains $(x,y)$ and is contained in the original neighbourhood. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{The product has a countable basis.}}
$$
In standard terminology, a finite product of <second-countable spaces> is second-countable.

The function is <continuously differentiable> when all its first <partial derivatives> exist on $U$ and are continuous there. Equivalently, $h$ has a <Fréchet derivative> at every point and the map $x\mapsto Dh(x)$ is continuous. For a scalar-valued function,
$$
Dh(x)v=\sum_{j=1}^n\partial_jh(x)v_j.
$$
On the open domain, continuous first <partial derivatives> ensure that this linear expression is the total <derivative>.

For differentiable maps with compatible domains, the multivariable <chain rule> is
$$
\boxed{D(h_2\circ h_1)(x)=Dh_2(h_1(x))\circ Dh_1(x).}
$$
In <Jacobian matrix> notation this is matrix multiplication. In particular, if both maps are <continuously differentiable>, the composition is continuously differentiable. Componentwise,
$$
\partial_j(h_2\circ h_1)_\ell(x)
=\sum_{r=1}^m\partial_r(h_2)_\ell(h_1(x))\,\partial_j(h_1)_r(x).
$$

Let $L(x,y,z)=(x-y,y-z,z-x)$. This is a <linear map>, hence smooth; composing it with the given $C^1$ function proves that $g=f\circ L$ is <continuously differentiable> by the <chain rule>. Write $f_1,f_2,f_3$ for the first <partial derivatives>, evaluated at $L(p)$. Then

An embedded $k$-dimensional <submanifold> $M\subseteq\mathbb R^n$ is locally a coordinate $k$-plane: for every $p\in M$, there are open neighbourhoods $U\ni p$, $V\subseteq\mathbb R^n$ and a <diffeomorphism> $\Phi:U\to V$ with
$$
\Phi(M\cap U)=V\cap(\mathbb R^k\times\{0\}^{n-k}).
$$
For the present $C^1$ setting, $\Phi$ and its inverse are continuously differentiable; in the smooth convention both are smooth.

A <tangent vector> to $M$ at $p$ is a <velocity> $\gamma'(0)$ of a continuously differentiable curve $\gamma:(-\epsilon,\epsilon)\to M$ with $\gamma(0)=p$. The <tangent space> is the collection of all these vectors:
$$
\boxed{T_pM=\{\gamma'(0):\gamma(0)=p,\ \gamma\text{ is a }C^1\text{ curve in }M\}.}
$$
In the local coordinates above, it is $D\Phi(p)^{-1}(\mathbb R^k\times\{0\}^{n-k})$, so it is a $k$-dimensional linear subspace of $\mathbb R^n$.

The <regular level set theorem> says that for a $C^1$ map $g:U\subseteq\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^r$, if $Dg$ has rank $r$ on $g^{-1}(c)$, then $g^{-1}(c)$ is an $(n-r)$-dimensional <submanifold> and
$$
T_p(g^{-1}(c))=\ker Dg(p).
$$
Here $r=1$ and the given rank condition makes the zero set a two-dimensional <submanifold>. It is nonempty because $g(0)=f(0)=0$. Part (c) gives
$$
Dg(p)(1,1,1)=g_x(p)+g_y(p)+g_z(p)=0.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{(1,1,1)\in\ker Dg(p)=T_pM\quad\text{for every }p\in M.}
$$
There is also a direct curve proof: $g(p+t(1,1,1))=g(p)=0$, so the entire line through $p$ in this direction lies in $M$. Its <velocity> at zero is exactly the required <tangent vector>.

For variations vanishing at the endpoints, the <Euler-Lagrange equations> are
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dt}F_{\dot r}-F_r=0,\qquad
\frac d{dt}F_{\dot\theta}-F_\theta=0.}
$$
Differentiate the stated energy expression using the <chain rule>. The acceleration terms cancel, leaving
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{dH}{dt}
&=\dot r\left(\frac d{dt}F_{\dot r}-F_r\right)
+\dot\theta\left(\frac d{dt}F_{\dot\theta}-F_\theta\right)-F_t.
\end{aligned}
$$
Along a stationary trajectory the two brackets vanish. Thus $dH/dt=-F_t$, and in the absence of explicit time dependence $\boxed{H\text{ is constant}}$.

In plane <polar coordinates>, the squared speed is $\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2$, so the <kinetic energy> is $T=\tfrac12m(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2)$. An inverse-square central <gravitational force> has <potential energy> proportional to $1/r$. With the sign convention printed in this paper, write $V=km/r$, giving
$$
F=T-V=\frac m2(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2)-\frac{km}{r}.
$$
For attraction to a star of mass $M_\star$, this convention has $k=-GM_\star<0$. Keeping $k$ real and signed is essential; no sign is changed in differentiating the printed functional.

Since $F_r=mr\dot\theta^2+km/r^2$, $F_{\dot r}=m\dot r$, $F_\theta=0$ and $F_{\dot\theta}=mr^2\dot\theta$, the equations of motion are
$$
\boxed{\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2=\frac{k}{r^2},\qquad
\frac d{dt}(mr^2\dot\theta)=0.}
$$
The second equation gives the conserved <angular momentum> $J=mr^2\dot\theta$; equivalently $r\ddot\theta+2\dot r\dot\theta=0$. The autonomous-functional identity gives a second conserved quantity,
$$
\boxed{H=\dot rF_{\dot r}+\dot\theta F_{\dot\theta}-F
=\frac m2(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2)+\frac{km}{r}=T+V.}
$$
This is the total <mechanical energy>; for attractive gravity the potential contribution is negative because $k<0$.

= Solution
{parent=H1}

Away from a collision, let $r=q_1-q_2$ and $D=|r|>0$. The <Lagrangian> is
$$
\boxed{L=\frac12m_1|\dot q_1|^2+\frac12m_2|\dot q_2|^2-V(D).}
$$
Differentiating $D^2=r\cdot r$ with respect to $x_1$ and $x_2$ gives $2D\partial_{x_1}D=2(x_1-x_2)$ and $2D\partial_{x_2}D=-2(x_1-x_2)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\partial_{x_1}D=-\partial_{x_2}D=\frac{x_1-x_2}{D}.}
$$
The other coordinates give $\nabla_{q_1}D=r/D$ and $\nabla_{q_2}D=-r/D$. Substituting these into the <Euler-Lagrange equations> yields equal and opposite central forces:
$$
\boxed{m_1\ddot q_1=-\frac{V'(D)}D r,\qquad
m_2\ddot q_2=\frac{V'(D)}D r.}
$$
Adding the equations shows that the <derivative> of the total <linear momentum> is zero, so $P=m_1\dot q_1+m_2\dot q_2$ is constant. For the total <angular momentum>, the terms $\dot q_i\times\dot q_i$ vanish, giving
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac d{dt}(m_1q_1\times\dot q_1+m_2q_2\times\dot q_2)
&=q_1\times\left(-\frac{V'}D r\right)+q_2\times\left(\frac{V'}D r\right)\\
&=-\frac{V'}D(q_1-q_2)\times r=0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus both stated vectors are <conserved quantities>.

**The total energy is also constant.** Define $E=\tfrac12m_1|\dot q_1|^2+\tfrac12m_2|\dot q_2|^2+V(D)$. Since $\dot D=r\cdot(\dot q_1-\dot q_2)/D$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot E&=m_1\dot q_1\cdot\ddot q_1+m_2\dot q_2\cdot\ddot q_2+V'\dot D\\
&=-\frac{V'}D r\cdot(\dot q_1-\dot q_2)+V'\frac{r\cdot(\dot q_1-\dot q_2)}D=0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence $\boxed{E=T+V(D)\text{ is conserved}}$. The explicit cancellation establishes energy conservation for an arbitrary smooth separation-dependent potential, throughout any collision-free trajectory.

The interior <time-independent Schrödinger equation> for a free particle is
$$
\boxed{-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(\psi_{xx}+\psi_{yy})=E\psi.}
$$
Confinement by the rectangular infinite well imposes <Dirichlet boundary conditions>: $\psi=0$ at $x=0,b$ and $y=0,\sqrt5b$. The wave function is <square-integrable> and may be normalized to unit <probability> on the rectangle.

Separation of variables gives products of the one-dimensional well states,
$$
\psi_{n,\ell}(x,y)=\frac{2}{5^{1/4}b}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{b}\sin\frac{\ell\pi y}{\sqrt5b},
\qquad n,\ell=1,2,\ldots.
$$
The Hamiltonian is the sum of the independent $x$ and $y$ kinetic-energy operators, so the one-dimensional energies add. Put $C=\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mb^2)$. Then
$$
E_{n,\ell}=C\left(n^2+\frac{\ell^2}{5}\right).
$$
For $n=1$, increasing $\ell$ gives $6C/5,9C/5,14C/5,21C/5$. The smallest energy with $n\geq2$ is also $21C/5$, attained at $(2,1)$; all $n\geq3$ give larger energies. Thus the ground and first three distinct excited energy levels are
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{level}&E& (n,\ell)\\\hline
\text{ground}&6C/5&(1,1)\\
\text{first excited}&9C/5&(1,2)\\
\text{second excited}&14C/5&(1,3)\\
\text{third excited}&21C/5&(1,4),(2,1)
\end{array}}
$$
The largest listed energy therefore has <energy-level degeneracy> $\boxed{2}$, since the two product eigenfunctions are linearly independent and no other pair solves $5n^2+\ell^2=21$.

\boxed{p+\frac12\rho|u|^2+\rho gz=\text{constant}.}
$$
This is <Bernoulli's theorem> for a steady irrotational flow: irrotationality makes the same constant apply throughout a connected region, rather than only along an individual streamline.

For one interval, the <trapezoidal rule> gives
$$
\boxed{I_1=\frac12\{0^4+1^4\}=\frac12.}
$$
For two intervals of width $h=1/2$, the composite <trapezoidal rule> gives half weights at the endpoints and full weight at the internal node:
$$
\boxed{I_{1/2}=\frac12\left\{\frac12\,0^4+\left(\frac12\right)^4+\frac12\,1^4\right\}
=\frac9{32}.}
$$

Apply the first <Richardson extrapolation> step to the two computed approximations:
$$
\boxed{R_1(1)=\frac{4(9/32)-1/2}{3}=\frac5{24}.}
$$
The exact integral is $I=\int_0^1x^4\,dx=1/5$, so the actual absolute error is
$$
\boxed{\left|\frac5{24}-\frac15\right|=\frac1{120}\approx0.00833<10^{-2}.}
$$
Thus the requested strict error bound is verified directly. This first extrapolation of trapezoidal approximations is also the one-panel <Simpson's rule> value on the three nodes $0,1/2,1$.
