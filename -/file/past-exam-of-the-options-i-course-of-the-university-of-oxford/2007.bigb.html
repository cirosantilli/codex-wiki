= Solution
{parent=2007/A/A1}

<Burnside's lemma> counts the orbits of a finite <group action>:
$$
|\Omega/G|=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}|\operatorname{Fix}(g)|.
$$
Identify each face with its opposite vertex. Every symmetry induces the same <permutation> on these two four-element sets, so a <permutation> with $c$ cycles fixes $3^c2^c=6^c$ colorings. The rotational symmetry <group> is $A_4$, with one identity, eight three-cycles, and three products of two disjoint transpositions. Their cycle counts are four, two and two. Thus the <rotation> count is
$$
\boxed{\frac{6^4+8\cdot6^2+3\cdot6^2}{12}=141}.
$$
Allowing reflections gives the full tetrahedral <group> $S_4$. In addition there are six transpositions with three cycles and six four-cycles with one cycle. The count becomes
$$
\boxed{\frac{6^4+6\cdot6^3+3\cdot6^2+8\cdot6^2+6\cdot6}{24}=126}.
$$
Equivalently, 30 of the 141 rotational classes form 15 mirror pairs, while 111 rotational classes are their own mirror class.

The product of fixed-coloring counts is an instance of the <orbit count for simultaneous colorings>.

= Solution
{parent=2007/B/B1}

The degree $[K:F]$ is the dimension of $K$ as a vector space over $F$. The tower theorem states that for intermediate fields $F\subseteq E\subseteq K$ in a finite extension,
$$
[K:F]=[K:E][E:F].
$$
If the degree is prime, one factor must be one, forcing $E=K$ or $E=F$; no strictly intermediate field exists.

Let $K$ have 32 elements. Its prime subfield is $\mathbb F_2$ and $[K:\mathbb F_2]=5$. Every element outside that subfield has <minimal polynomial> of degree five, by the tower theorem. Also every member of $K$ satisfies $x^{32}=x$: nonzero elements form a <group> of order 31. Hence $x^{32}-x$ splits over $K$, with distinct roots because its derivative is $-1=1$ in characteristic two.

Every irreducible degree-five polynomial over $\mathbb F_2$ divides $x^{32}-x$. Indeed its quotient field has 32 elements, and its <residue> class of $x$ satisfies that equation. Thus every such polynomial splits into five distinct roots in $K$. Conversely each of the 30 elements outside $\mathbb F_2$ has one such <minimal polynomial>, and different minimal polynomials have disjoint root sets. Consequently the number is
$$
\boxed{30/5=6}.
$$
There is no extra leading-coefficient factor, since every nonzero leading coefficient over $\mathbb F_2$ is one.

Euler's theorem states that $a^{\phi(m)}\equiv1\pmod m$ whenever $\gcd(a,m)=1$, where $\phi(m)$ counts the invertible <residue> classes. For each prime-power divisor, the same theorem gives $a^{\phi(p_i^{e_i})}\equiv1\pmod{p_i^{e_i}}$. Since the exponent defined in the source is a multiple of every one of these exponents, raising that congruence to the corresponding integer power gives
$$
a^{\lambda(m)}\equiv1\pmod{p_i^{e_i}}.
$$
The prime powers are pairwise <coprime>, so their product divides $a^{\lambda(m)}-1$, proving the congruence modulo $m$.

For the displayed composite integer,
$$
\lambda(561)=\operatorname{lcm}(2,10,16)=80,\qquad560=7\cdot80.
$$
Thus every integer <coprime> to 561 satisfies $\boxed{a^{560}\equiv1\pmod{561}}$. This is a <Carmichael number>. The source's prime-power least common multiple need not be the minimal universal exponent for all moduli; for powers of two its definition can exceed the usual <Carmichael function>. No minimality claim is needed here.

The <monotone convergence theorem> states that if measurable nonnegative functions satisfy $f_n\uparrow f$ almost everywhere on a measure space, then
$$
\int f=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int f_n,
$$
allowing either side to be $+\infty$.

{parent=2007/D/D1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2007/D/D1/ii}

Use the nonnegative increasing truncations $f_n=x^{-2}\mathbf1_{[1/n,1]}$. They increase almost everywhere to the specified function. On each <compact> interval the <continuous function> has equal Riemann and Lebesgue integrals, so the <fundamental theorem of calculus> gives
$$
\int f_n=\int_{1/n}^1x^{-2}\,dx=n-1.
$$
Monotone convergence yields $\int f=+\infty$. Since a nonnegative function is <Lebesgue integrable> exactly when its integral is finite, **this function is not <Lebesgue integrable>**.

The truncations $g_n=x^{1/2}\mathbf1_{[1/n,1]}$ are measurable, nonnegative, and increase almost everywhere to $g$. As before their integrals agree with the corresponding Riemann integrals, giving

Monotone convergence therefore gives the finite value $\boxed{\int g=2/3}$. Nonnegativity makes this the integral of the absolute value as well, proving <Lebesgue integrability>.

A dominated differentiation theorem is the following. Suppose $\phi(x,t)$ is measurable in $x$ and differentiable in $t$ on an open interval, is integrable for at least one parameter value, and its parameter derivative is dominated in absolute value by an <integrable function> independent of $t$. Then its integral is differentiable and
$$
\frac d{dt}\int\phi(x,t)\,dx=\int\partial_t\phi(x,t)\,dx.
$$
Local versions require the domination only on a neighbourhood of each parameter. The proof bounds the difference quotients by the dominating function, using the <fundamental theorem of calculus> or the mean-value theorem, then applies dominated convergence. Apply the argument to real and imaginary parts for a complex integrand.

For fixed $t$, $f(x)e^{itx}$ is measurable and has absolute value $|f(x)|$, so is integrable. Its derivative with respect to $t$ is $ixf(x)e^{itx}$, whose absolute value is the assumed <integrable function> $|xf(x)|$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\widehat f'(t)=\int_{\mathbb R}ixf(x)e^{itx}\,dx}.
$$
Dominated convergence also shows this derivative is continuous, though only differentiability is required.

A sequence converges if there is $x\in X$ such that, for every $\epsilon>0$, some $N$ satisfies $d(x_n,x)<\epsilon$ whenever $n\ge N$. The limit must belong to $X$, not merely to a larger ambient space.

A <metric space> is <sequentially compact> if every sequence in it has a <subsequence> converging to a point of the same space.

= Solution
{parent=2007/E/E1}

The sequence $x_n=1/(n+1)$ lies in the open interval and converges in the ambient real line to zero. Every <subsequence> also converges to zero, and uniqueness of limits prevents it converging to any point of the interval. Thus **the interval is not <sequentially compact>**.

For a sequence $(x_n,y_n)$ in the product, <sequential compactness> of $X$ supplies indices $n_j$ with $x_{n_j}\to x\in X$. Apply <sequential compactness> of $Y$ to the corresponding $y_{n_j}$, extracting a further <subsequence> $y_{n_{j_\ell}}\to y\in Y$. The first coordinate still tends to $x$. For any $\epsilon>0$, both coordinate distances are eventually less than $\epsilon/2$, so their sum is less than $\epsilon$. Hence the same <subsequence> converges in the given product metric to $(x,y)$. This proves **the product is <sequentially compact>**.

The <subspace topology> is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal T_A=\{A\cap U:U\in\mathcal T\}}.
$$
These sets contain the empty set and $A$, are closed under arbitrary unions, and are closed under finite intersections because intersections distribute over the common factor $A$. Thus they form a topology on $A$.

The <implicit function theorem> applies when $D_2F(a,b)\ne0$. After shrinking the neighbourhood, this partial derivative stays nonzero along the implicit <graph>. Differentiating its defining identity gives
$$
f'(x)=-\frac{D_1F(x,f(x))}{D_2F(x,f(x))}.
$$
For the specified smooth function, $F_x=y+\cos(x+y)$ and $F_y=x+\cos(x+y)$, so both equal one at zero. In particular the theorem applies and $f'(0)=-1$. Differentiate the identity a second time:
$$
F_{xx}+2F_{xy}f'+F_{yy}(f')^2+F_yf''=0.
$$
At zero, $F_{xx}=F_{yy}=0$ and $F_{xy}=1$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{f'(0)=-1,\qquad f''(0)=2}.
$$

= Solution
{parent=2007/G/G1}

Let $y_\epsilon=y+\epsilon\eta$, with $\eta=\eta'=0$ at both endpoints. At an extremal the <first variation> is zero:
$$
0=\int_{x_1}^{x_2}(F_y\eta+F_{y'}\eta'+F_{y''}\eta'')\,dx.
$$
Integrate the second term by parts once and the third term twice. The boundary term is
$$
\left[F_{y'}\eta+F_{y''}\eta'-\frac d{dx}F_{y''}\eta\right]_{x_1}^{x_2}=0.
$$
The given fundamental lemma therefore gives
$$
\boxed{F_y-\frac d{dx}F_{y'}+\frac{d^2}{dx^2}F_{y''}=0}.
$$
This is the classical form under the usual smoothness hypotheses ensuring the displayed derivatives exist; without that extra regularity the variation first yields its distributional form.

For the concrete functional the equation is $y^{(4)}-y=0$. Write $L=\pi/2$, $C=\cosh L$ and $S=\sinh L$. A general solution is $A\cos x+B\sin x+D\cosh x+E\sinh x$. The two initial conditions give $D=-A$ and $B+E=1$. The final derivative condition gives $A=EC/(1+S)$, and the final value condition then gives $E=(1+S)/2$. Thus an extremal is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac C2(\cos x-\cosh x)+\frac{1-S}{2}\sin x+\frac{1+S}{2}\sinh x}.
$$
It solves the differential equation, has initial value zero and initial derivative one, and at $L$ both its value and derivative vanish, using $C^2-S^2=1$. These checks verify all four prescribed endpoint conditions.

= Solution
{parent=2007/H/H1}

Time <independence> conserves the <energy> $E=T+mga\cos\theta$. The two cyclic coordinates give
$$
p_\psi=C(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta)=Cn,\qquad
p_\phi=A\dot\phi\sin^2\theta+Cn\cos\theta=h.
$$
These are respectively <angular momentum> along the symmetry axis and <angular momentum> along the fixed upward vertical; $n$ is the axial spin rate. For $u=\cos\theta$ and $-1<u<1$, eliminate $\dot\phi$ using $\dot\phi=(h-Cnu)/[A(1-u^2)]$. The <energy> becomes
$$
E=\frac A2\dot\theta^2+\frac C2n^2+mga u+\frac{(h-Cnu)^2}{2A(1-u^2)}.
$$
Multiplying and rearranging gives
$$
\boxed{A^2\dot\theta^2=\frac{A(2E-Cn^2-2mga u)(1-u^2)-(h-Cnu)^2}{1-u^2}}.
$$
To justify the double-root condition, use the <effective potential>
$$
U(u)=\frac C2n^2+mga u+\frac{(h-Cnu)^2}{2A(1-u^2)}.
$$
Eliminating the cyclic coordinates by Routh reduction gives the remaining equation $A\ddot\theta=-dU(\cos\theta)/d\theta$. A constant nonvertical angle therefore has $U'(u_0)=0$ and $E=U(u_0)$. Since $P(u)=2A(E-U(u))(1-u^2)$, both $P(u_0)$ and $P'(u_0)$ vanish. This argument uses the second-order equation; merely differentiating the <energy> identity at a stationary angle would not establish the derivative condition.

Thus **steady nonvertical <precession> gives a common root in $(-1,1)$**. The nonvertical qualification is implicit in the denominator used by the source. A top spinning exactly upright or inverted is also possible but corresponds to an endpoint $u=\pm1$, not an interior root; the unrestricted literal claim would need that exception.

The distinction is captured by the <double-root criterion for steady precession of a symmetric top>.

<Ampère's law> in magnetostatics is
$$
\oint_\Gamma B\cdot d\ell=\mu_0 I_S,\qquad I_S=\int_SJ\cdot n\,dS,
$$
with boundary and surface orientations related by the right-hand rule. Stokes' theorem gives the left side as $\int_S(\nabla\times B)\cdot n\,dS$, proving the integral law from the stated curl equation.

For a circular contour of radius $r$ about the axis, the left side is $2\pi rB_\theta$. For $r\le a$ the enclosed current is $Ir^2/a^2$; for $r\ge a$ it is $I$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{B(r)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\mu_0 I r}{2\pi a^2}\,e_\theta,&0<r\le a,\\[2mm]\dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\,e_\theta,&r\ge a.\end{cases}}
$$
Both expressions agree at the boundary. At the axis the field is zero, interpreted by <continuity> even though the angular unit vector is undefined there.

For steady flow the given equation gives $\nabla H=-\rho\,\omega\times u$. Dotting with $u$ yields $u\cdot\nabla H=0$, so **$H$ is constant along each <streamline>**. It can have different values on different streamlines. Dotting with $\omega$ also makes it constant along vortex lines where they are defined.

If the flow is additionally irrotational, $\omega=0$ and hence $\nabla H=0$. Thus **$H$ is one constant on each <connected> fluid region**, the stronger global Bernoulli conclusion.

= Solution
{parent=2007/K/K1/ii}

Take the curl. A <gradient> has zero curl, and the supplied identity, with $\nabla\cdot u=0$ and $\nabla\cdot\omega=\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times u)=0$, gives
$$
\partial_t\omega+(u\cdot\nabla)\omega-(\omega\cdot\nabla)u=0.
$$
Therefore $D\omega/Dt=(\omega\cdot\nabla)u$. In planar flow $\omega=(0,0,\partial_xv-\partial_yu)$ and $\partial_z u=0$, so the stretching term vanishes. Consequently
$$
\boxed{D\omega/Dt=0}.
$$
<Vorticity> is carried unchanged with each fluid particle. It need not be spatially uniform or independent of time at a fixed spatial point.

The <Milne-Thomson circle theorem> replaces a potential $f$ analytic inside the circle of radius $a$ by $f(z)+\overline{f(a^2/\bar z)}$ outside it, when its original singularities are outside. The circle becomes a <streamline>, the imposed flow at infinity is preserved, and no extra <circulation> is introduced.

Use the convention that positive <circulation> is counterclockwise, so a vortex potential is $\Gamma\log(z-z_1)/(2\pi i)$ and its derivative is $u-iv$. With $b=a^2/\bar z_1$, the circle construction gives
$$
\boxed{w(z)=\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi i}\{\log(z-z_1)-\log(z-b)+\log z\}}
$$
up to an additive constant and local logarithm branches. There is an image vortex of strength $-\Gamma$ at $b$ and a central vortex of strength $\Gamma$. Their zero net interior <circulation> is needed for the specified motion equation.

The original vortex moves with the regular velocity of the image flow, excluding its own singular velocity. Thus
$$
\frac{d\bar z_1}{dt}=\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi i}\left\{-\frac1{z_1-a^2/\bar z_1}+\frac1{z_1}\right\}
=\boxed{\frac{i\Gamma a^2}{2\pi z_1(|z_1|^2-a^2)}}.
$$
The conjugation in this equation matches the printed source. Write $z_1=re^{i\theta}$ and cancel $e^{-i\theta}$ to obtain
$$
\dot r-ir\dot\theta=\frac{i\Gamma a^2}{2\pi r(r^2-a^2)}.
$$
Real and imaginary parts give
$$
\boxed{\dot r=0,\qquad\dot\theta=-\frac{\Gamma a^2}{2\pi r^2(r^2-a^2)}}.
$$
The vortex moves uniformly on the circle determined by its initial radius, clockwise for positive $\Gamma$ in this convention.

The unit disk has area $\pi$, so its joint density is $f_{X,Y}(x,y)=\pi^{-1}\mathbf1_{\{x^2+y^2\le1\}}$. The polar-coordinate Jacobian has absolute <determinant> $r$. Therefore
$$
f_{R,\Theta}(r,\theta)=\frac r\pi,\qquad 0<r<1,\quad0\le\theta<2\pi,
$$
and is zero outside this rectangle, up to irrelevant boundary choices. Integrating gives
$$
\boxed{f_R(r)=2r\mathbf1_{(0,1)}(r),\qquad f_\Theta(\theta)=\frac1{2\pi}\mathbf1_{[0,2\pi)}(\theta),\qquad\mathbb ER=2/3}.
$$
The joint density equals the product of the two marginal densities, so **$R$ and $\Theta$ are independent**. The angle at the centre can be chosen arbitrarily since that point has probability zero.

A state $i$ is absorbing if $p_{ii}=1$, so once visited it is never left. Condition on the first step and use the <Markov property>: for $i\ne1$, the subsequent probability of ever reaching state one is $h_j$ given $X_1=j$. Hence $h_i=\sum_jp_{ij}h_j$, with $h_1=1$.

= Solution

A frequentist <confidence interval> is a pair of data-dependent endpoints whose random interval covers the fixed parameter with probability at least $1-\alpha$ under every allowed parameter value. In repeated uses of the same sampling procedure, at least the advertised fraction of intervals cover the true value. It is not a posterior probability assigned to the parameter after observing the data.

For a sample of size $n$ from a Poisson law with mean $\mu$, the sum $S$ is Poisson with mean $n\mu$. At observed $s>0$, define the lower and upper limits by
$$
\mathbb P_{nL}(S\ge s)=\alpha/2,\qquad
\mathbb P_{nU}(S\le s)=\alpha/2.
$$
At $s=0$ set $L=0$ and use the same upper equation. This inverts the two exact one-sided tail tests. Discreteness means the resulting central interval is generally conservative: its coverage is at least the nominal value, not identically equal to it.

Here one observes a four-year total of 39, so the lower endpoint satisfies $\phi_L(38)=0.975$ and the upper satisfies $\phi_U(39)=0.025$. Selecting the corresponding entries supplied in the source gives
$$
\boxed{[27.7,\ 53.3]}.
$$
This interval concerns the expected four-year total, not an annual rate. The other two supplied numbers correspond to the wrong inclusive tail for one of these endpoints.

= Solution
{parent=2007/O/O2}

The power of a test at a parameter value is its probability of rejecting the null there. Its size is the supremum of this probability over the null parameter set. The <Neyman-Pearson lemma> states that for simple null and alternative densities $f_0,f_1$, a test rejecting where $f_1>cf_0$, accepting where $f_1<cf_0$, and randomizing as needed on equality to attain size $\alpha$, has greatest power among tests of size at most $\alpha$.

The specified function is nonnegative. Its integral is
$$
\frac{\theta^2}{\theta+1}\left\{\int_0^\infty xe^{-\theta x}\,dx+\int_0^\infty e^{-\theta x}\,dx\right\}
=\frac{\theta^2}{\theta+1}\left(\theta^{-2}+\theta^{-1}\right)=1.
$$
For independent observations write $S=\sum_iX_i$. The <likelihood ratio> of the smaller alternative parameter to the null is
$$
\frac{L(\theta_1)}{L(\theta_0)}=
\left\{\frac{\theta_1^2(\theta_0+1)}{\theta_0^2(\theta_1+1)}\right\}^n
\exp\{(\theta_0-\theta_1)S\}.
$$
It is strictly increasing in $S$. Thus the most powerful level-$\alpha$ test rejects for
$$
\boxed{S>c_\alpha,\qquad\mathbb P_{\theta_0}(S>c_\alpha)=\alpha}.
$$
The continuous distribution permits this size without randomization for $0<\alpha<1$. Its threshold depends only on the null and the desired size, not on which $\theta_1<\theta_0$ was chosen. The same test is therefore **uniformly most powerful against the entire one-sided alternative $\theta<\theta_0$**.

A <Givens rotation> equals the identity except in coordinates $i,j$, where its block can be taken as
$$
\begin{pmatrix}c&s\\-s&c\end{pmatrix},\qquad c=\cos\theta,\ s=\sin\theta.
$$
The two block columns are orthonormal because $c^2+s^2=1$; other coordinates are unchanged, so the full matrix is <orthogonal>. If $(v_i,v_j)\ne(0,0)$, take $r=\sqrt{v_i^2+v_j^2}$, $c=v_i/r$ and $s=v_j/r$. The transformed pair is $(r,0)$. If both entries are zero, the identity already suffices.

For the specified vector one possible matrix is
$$
\boxed{Q=\begin{pmatrix}4/5&0&-3/5\\3/5&0&4/5\\0&-1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad Qv=\begin{pmatrix}0\\5\\0\end{pmatrix}}.
$$
Its rows are orthonormal. It is **not unique**: premultiply by any <orthogonal> transformation fixing the second coordinate axis pointwise. This rotates or reflects the other two output coordinates without changing the required image. Equivalently its second row must be $v^T/\|v\|$, but any orthonormal completion of that row works.

= Solution
{parent=2007/P/P2}

<Richardson extrapolation> cancels a leading discretization error by combining two mesh sizes. If $A(h)=I+ch^p+O(h^q)$ with $q>p$, then
$$
\frac{2^pA(h/2)-A(h)}{2^p-1}=I+O(h^q).
$$
Use a uniform grid $x_i=a+ih$, $0\le i\le n$, with even $n$. The composite trapezium rule is
$$
T_h=h\left[\frac{f_0+f_n}{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}f_i\right],\qquad f_i=f(x_i).
$$
The composite Simpson rule, formed by integrating a quadratic interpolant on each pair of successive panels, is
$$
S_h=\frac h3\left[f_0+f_n+4\sum_{i\text{ odd}}f_i+2\sum_{\substack{0<i<n\\i\text{ even}}}f_i\right].
$$
Assume an even-power trapezium error expansion $T_h=I+c_2h^2+O(h^4)$; continuous fourth derivative is a standard sufficient smoothness hypothesis. The coarse grid has step $2h$, so one extrapolation step is $(4T_h-T_{2h})/3$. Its endpoint coefficients are $h/3$, its odd-index coefficients are $4h/3$, and its interior even-index coefficients are $2h/3$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{S_h=\frac{4T_h-T_{2h}}3=I+O(h^4)}.
$$
The algebraic identity between the two rules holds for any sampled values; the smoothness and error assumptions are used to infer the improved accuracy.
