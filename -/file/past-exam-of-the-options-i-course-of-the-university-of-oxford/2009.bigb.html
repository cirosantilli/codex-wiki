The <splitting field> is the smallest <subfield> of $\mathbb C$ containing $\mathbb Q$ and all the <roots of a polynomial> under consideration. Equivalently it is generated over $\mathbb Q$ by those roots, and the <polynomial> splits there into linear factors.

Put $\zeta_6=e^{\pi i/3}$. All sixth <roots of unity> are powers of $\zeta_6$, so the required <splitting field> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb Q(\zeta_6)=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3})}.
$$
Indeed $\zeta_6=(1+\sqrt{-3})/2$, and conversely $\sqrt{-3}=2\zeta_6-1$. Its <field extension degree> is two.

The <polynomial identity>

x^9-1=(x^3-1)(x^6+x^3+1)

shows that the roots are the ninth <roots of unity> which are not third roots. These are precisely the primitive ninth <roots of unity>, all powers of $\zeta_9=e^{2\pi i/9}$. Thus the <splitting field> is $\mathbb Q(\zeta_9)$. The supplied <irreducible polynomial>[irreducibility] statement makes the displayed degree-six <polynomial> the <minimal polynomial> of $\zeta_9$, giving
$$
\boxed{[\mathbb Q(\zeta_9):\mathbb Q]=6}.
$$

Write $g(x)=x^6+x^3+1$. The second <polynomial> is $g(-x)$. A <solvable group> has a finite <subnormal series> from the identity to the <group> with <abelian group> factors. An equivalent definition uses the <derived series>: set $G^{(0)}=G$ and $G^{(j+1)}=[G^{(j)},G^{(j)}]$; the series must reach the identity. For <completeness>, an abelian-factor series forces successive derived subgroups into its successive terms, while a terminating <derived series> itself supplies such a series.

If $H\leq G$, induction gives $H^{(j)}\leq G^{(j)}$: every <commutator> of elements of $H^{(j)}$ is also a <commutator> in $G^{(j)}$. Thus termination for $G$ implies termination for $H$, proving that every <subgroup> of a <solvable group> is solvable.

In $S_4$, use
$$
\{1\}\triangleleft V_4\triangleleft A_4\triangleleft S_4,
\qquad V_4=\{1,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}.
$$
The <Klein four-group> is invariant under <conjugation> in $S_4$; the other normality assertions follow from the <alternating group> being the kernel of the sign map. The <quotient group> factors are $V_4,C_3,C_2$, all abelian. Hence **$S_4$ is solvable**.

For the last example take $A_5\times C_2$. Its <subgroup> $\{1\}\times C_2$ is nontrivial, proper and normal, so the product is not a <simple group>. Projection onto $A_5$ is surjective, and every quotient of a <solvable group> is solvable, because a homomorphism carries each <derived subgroup> onto the <derived subgroup> of its image. But the nonabelian <simple group> $A_5$ has $A_5'=A_5$: its <derived subgroup> is nontrivial and normal. Thus the product is **not solvable either**. One standard justification of the simplicity used here is that the nonidentity <conjugacy class> sizes of $A_5$ are $15,20,12,12$; no proper nontrivial <union> with the identity has size dividing $60$, as a <normal subgroup> would require by <Lagrange's theorem for finite groups>.

<Fermat's little theorem> says that for a <prime number> $p$ and any <integer> $a$,
$$
\boxed{a^p\equiv a\pmod p}.
$$
If $p\nmid a$, cancellation gives the equivalent statement $a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p$.

= Solution
{parent=2009/C/C1/ii}

<Wilson's theorem> states that an <integer> $p>1$ is a <prime number> if and only if
$$
\boxed{(p-1)!\equiv-1\pmod p}.
$$
In particular this holds for every prime, including $p=2$.

= Solution
{parent=2009/C/C1/iii}

Apply <Fermat's little theorem> and <Wilson's theorem> in the <modular arithmetic>[ring of integers modulo n] with modulus $p$. They give
$$
a^p+(p-1)!a\equiv a-a=0,\qquad
(p-1)!a^p+a\equiv-a+a=0\pmod p.
$$
A vanishing <modular congruence> modulo $p$ is precisely divisibility by $p$, so **both divisibilities hold**, without requiring $p\nmid a$.

Because the primes are distinct, <Fermat's little theorem> gives $q^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p$ and $p^{q-1}\equiv1\pmod q$. The other term in each modulus vanishes. Therefore
$$
p^{q-1}+q^{p-1}-1\equiv0\pmod p,\qquad
p^{q-1}+q^{p-1}-1\equiv0\pmod q.
$$
The distinct primes are <coprime>, so their product divides this <integer>. Hence
$$
\boxed{p^{q-1}+q^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod{pq}}.
$$

The <Lebesgue outer measure> can be defined by
$$
m^*(E)=\inf\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|I_j|:E\subseteq\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}I_j,\ I_j\text{ open intervals}\right\}.
$$
A <null set> has $m^*(E)=0$, equivalently it admits such a cover of total length below every positive $\varepsilon$.

The usual <Cantor set> is the <intersection> of the sets obtained from $[0,1]$ by repeatedly removing every open middle third. At stage $n$ there are $2^n$ closed intervals of length $3^{-n}$. Enlarge them slightly to open intervals whose total added length is below $\varepsilon$. Thus $m^*(C)\leq(2/3)^n+\varepsilon$ for every $n$ and $\varepsilon>0$, proving $m^*(C)=0$.

For the different deletion rule in this problem, let $L_n$ be the total length of the stage-$n$ intervals. Removing the stated fraction from each component gives
$$
L_{n+1}=L_n\frac{2^n+1}{2^n+2},\qquad L_0=1.
$$
For $n\geq1$ the <telescoping product> simplifies to
$$
L_n=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{2^j+1}{2^j+2}
=\frac23\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{2^j+1}{2(2^{j-1}+1)}
=\frac{2^{n-1}+1}{3\,2^{n-1}}.
$$
Because $K\subseteq K_n$, the same finite <interval>-enlargement argument gives $m^*(K)\leq L_n$. Taking the limit proves
$$
\boxed{m^*(K)\leq\frac13}.
$$
Only <interval> covers and their lengths were used; no <continuity> theorem for integrals is needed.

One version of <dominated differentiation under the integral sign> is the following. Let the parameter range be open. Suppose $f(\cdot,y)$ is integrable, $\partial_yf(x,y)$ exists for almost every $x$, and near each parameter value its absolute value is bounded by an integrable <function> of $x$ independent of $y$. Then
$$
F'(y)=\int_I\partial_yf(x,y)\,dx.
$$
This follows by applying the <dominated convergence theorem> to difference quotients, bounded by that same integrable <function> through the <mean value theorem>.

On any <compact> parameter <interval> $0<y\leq B$, the original integrand is bounded by $B/(1+x^4)$, using $|\sin(x^2y)|\leq Bx^2$. Its first and second parameter derivatives are
$$
\frac{\cos(x^2y)}{1+x^4},\qquad
-\frac{x^2\sin(x^2y)}{1+x^4}.
$$
Their absolute values are bounded respectively by $(1+x^4)^{-1}$ and $x^2/(1+x^4)$, both integrable on $(0,\infty)$. Applying the theorem twice proves that $F$ is twice differentiable and gives
$$
F''(y)-F(y)
=-\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin(x^2y)}{x^2}\,dx.
$$
This last integral is absolutely convergent: its integrand is bounded near zero and bounded by $x^{-2}$ at infinity. Substituting $t=x\sqrt y$ yields
$$
\boxed{F''(y)-F(y)=c\sqrt y},\qquad
c=-\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin(t^2)}{t^2}\,dt.
$$
The expression for $c$ is finite and independent of $y$, which is all that is required.

The family $\mathcal F$ of <closed sets> contains $\varnothing$ and $X$, is closed under arbitrary <intersection>, and is closed under finite <union>. Conversely these axioms define a <topology> by declaring the complements of the members of $\mathcal F$ to be the <open sets>.

The map is <continuous> when the <preimage> $f^{-1}(U)$ is <open> in $X$ for every <open set> $U$ in $Y$. Equivalently the <preimage> of every <closed set> in $Y$ is closed in $X$, since preimages commute with complementation.

<Convergence of a sequence in a topological space>[Convergence of a sequence] to $\ell$ means that every <open neighbourhood> $U$ of $\ell$ contains all sufficiently late terms: there is $N$ such that $x_n\in U$ for all $n\geq N$. The threshold may depend on $U$.

= Solution
{parent=2009/E/E1/b/i}

Both $\varnothing$ and $X$ satisfy the condition. For an arbitrary <intersection> of members, either every member contains $a$, in which case the <intersection> contains $a$, or one does not, in which case that member is countable and so is the <intersection>. The empty <intersection> is $X$.

For a finite <union>, either a member contains $a$, so the <union> does too, or all members omit $a$ and are countable, so their finite <union> is countable. Thus the <closed set> axioms hold and define a <topology>. This is a <Fortissimo space>: every point other than $a$ is isolated, while the <open neighbourhoods> of $a$ have countable complement.

Let $S=\{x:f(x)\ne f(a)\}$, which is countable. For any <closed set> $C\subseteq\mathbb R$, consider its <preimage>. If $f(a)\in C$, then $a\in f^{-1}(C)$ and that <preimage> is closed by definition. If $f(a)\notin C$, then $f^{-1}(C)\subseteq S$, so it is countable and again closed. Therefore every <closed set> has a closed <preimage>, proving **<continuity>**.

Set $S=\{x_n:n\in\mathbb N,\ x_n\ne\ell\}$. This is a countable <set>, hence a <closed set> in the stated <topology>, and $\ell\notin S$. Consequently $X\setminus S$ is an <open neighbourhood> of $\ell$. By <convergence of a sequence in a topological space>[convergence of a sequence], all sufficiently late terms lie outside $S$. A sequence term outside $S$ must equal $\ell$. Thus the sequence is **eventually constant**, and only finitely many indices have $x_n\ne\ell$.

A subset $A$ is <compact> when every <open cover> of $A$ has a finite <subcover>. This may be stated using sets open in the ambient space or using the <subspace topology> on $A$; the definitions agree because every subspace-<open set> is the <intersection> of $A$ with an ambient-<open set>.

Let $(U_i)_{i\in I}$ be an <open cover> of $f(X)$, with the sets open in $Y$. The <preimages> $f^{-1}(U_i)$ form an <open cover> of $X$, because $f$ is <continuous>. By <compactness>, finitely many of these preimages cover $X$. Their corresponding sets $U_i$ cover $f(X)$, providing a finite <subcover>. Hence **the <continuous> image is <compact>**.

= Solution
{parent=2009/E/E2/b/i}

The family contains the empty set and the entire <real line>. The <intersection> of two proper nonempty members is
$$
(a,\infty)\cap(b,\infty)=(\max(a,b),\infty),
$$
so it is closed under finite <intersection>. For an arbitrary nonempty family of proper rays, put $c=\inf_i a_i$. If the thresholds are unbounded below, the <union> is $\mathbb R$; otherwise the <union> is $(c,\infty)$. In the latter case $c$ itself is in no ray, and every point greater than $c$ lies above some threshold, proving the equality. Empty unions and unions involving $\mathbb R$ cause no difficulty. Thus arbitrary <unions> also belong to the family, establishing the <topology> axioms.

{parent=2009/E/E2/b}

= Solution
{parent=2009/E/E2/b/ii}

If $m=\min A$, any <open cover> contains a member containing $m$. Such a member is either $\mathbb R$ or a ray $(a,\infty)$ with $a<m$. It therefore contains every element of $A$, so that single member is already a <subcover>.

Conversely suppose $A$ is nonempty and has no smallest element. Then
$$
\{(a,\infty):a\in A\}
$$
is an <open cover> of $A$: for any $x\in A$, the lack of a minimum gives an $a\in A$ below $x$. A nonempty finite subcollection has <union> $(a_0,\infty)$, where $a_0$ is the smallest of its thresholds. It misses $a_0\in A$. The empty subcollection cannot cover nonempty $A$ either. Hence there is no finite <subcover>, and $A$ is not <compact>. Thus **<compactness> is equivalent to having a smallest element**.

= Solution

Use constraints $h_1=x^2/4+y^2+z^2-1$ and $h_2=x+y-z$. Both constraints and the objective are <continuously differentiable>. The feasible set is a nonempty <closed subset> of a bounded ellipsoid, hence <compact>, so the <extreme value theorem> guarantees both extrema.

The gradients $(x/2,2y,2z)$ and $(1,1,-1)$ are independent everywhere feasible. If the first were $t$ times the second, then $x=2t$, $y=t/2$, $z=-t/2$. The plane constraint would give $3t=0$, forcing the origin, which is not on the ellipsoid. Thus the <Lagrange multiplier> theorem applies at every feasible point.

Its equations are
$$
1=\lambda x/2+\mu,\qquad
2=2\lambda y+\mu,\qquad
0=2\lambda z-\mu.
$$
Here $\lambda\ne0$, since otherwise the first two equations require $\mu=1=2$. Subtract twice the first equation from the second, and use $\mu=2\lambda z$ and $z=x+y$; the result is $0=-3\lambda x$, so $x=0$. Then $z=y$ and the ellipsoid gives $y=\pm1/\sqrt2$. The only candidates are therefore $(0,\pm1/\sqrt2,\pm1/\sqrt2)$, with matching signs. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\min g=-\sqrt2,\qquad\max g=\sqrt2}.
$$

= Solution
{parent=2009/G/G1}

Take a smooth <variation> $u+\varepsilon\eta$ with $\eta$ vanishing on the boundary; compactly supported test functions suffice for the interior equation. At an extremum, the <first variation> is zero:
$$
0=\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}I[u+\varepsilon\eta]\right|_{\varepsilon=0}
=\int_V\left(F_u\eta+F_{u_x}\eta_x+F_{u_y}\eta_y+F_{u_z}\eta_z\right)\,dV.
$$
<Integration by parts> removes the derivatives from $\eta$. The boundary terms vanish, giving
$$
\int_V\left(F_u-\partial_xF_{u_x}-\partial_yF_{u_y}-\partial_zF_{u_z}\right)\eta\,dV=0.
$$
The <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations> gives the <Euler-Lagrange equation>
$$
\boxed{\partial_xF_{u_x}+\partial_yF_{u_y}+\partial_zF_{u_z}-F_u=0}.
$$
This is the classical equation for sufficiently smooth admissible extrema; for weaker regularity the first-<variation> identity supplies its weak form.

For the squared-<gradient> integrand, the equation is $2(u_{xx}+u_{yy}+u_{zz})=0$, namely the <Laplace equation>. Put $u_0=ax+by+cz$ and write any admissible <function> as $u=u_0+v$, with $v=0$ on the boundary. The <energy> identity is
$$
I[u]=(a^2+b^2+c^2)\operatorname{vol}V
+2\int_V(a,b,c)\cdot\nabla v\,dV+\int_V|\nabla v|^2\,dV.
$$
By the <divergence theorem>, the middle integral is $\int_{\partial V}v(a,b,c)\cdot n\,dS=0$; equivalently it vanishes by weak <integration by parts> for zero-trace functions. The final term is nonnegative and vanishes at $v=0$. Thus this is a global minimum, not merely a stationary value:
$$
\boxed{\min I=(a^2+b^2+c^2)\operatorname{vol}V}.
$$

{parent=2009/H/H1}

With $\mathbf d=\mathbf q_1-\mathbf q_2$ and $r=|\mathbf d|$, the <Lagrangian> is
$$
L=\frac12\sum_{j=1}^3m_j|\dot{\mathbf q}_j|^2-f(r).
$$
At configurations with $r>0$, the <Euler-Lagrange equations> give
$$
m_1\ddot{\mathbf q}_1=-\frac{f'(r)}r\mathbf d,\qquad
m_2\ddot{\mathbf q}_2=\frac{f'(r)}r\mathbf d,\qquad
m_3\ddot{\mathbf q}_3=0.
$$
These formulas apply on the differentiable part of the potential; collision configurations require any additional smooth extension of $f(|\mathbf d|)$ to be specified. Summing the equations gives
$$
\frac d{dt}\sum_jm_j\dot{\mathbf q}_j=0,
$$
so the total <linear momentum> is conserved. The total <energy> is
$$
E=\frac12\sum_jm_j|\dot{\mathbf q}_j|^2+f(r).
$$
Differentiating, the two interacting forces do work $-f'(r)\dot r$, while the potential derivative is $f'(r)\dot r$ and particle three contributes no force term. Hence $\dot E=0$. Both **total momentum and total <energy> are conserved**.

For the first particle, the derivative of its <angular momentum> about the origin is its <torque>:
$$
\frac d{dt}\left(m_1\mathbf q_1\times\dot{\mathbf q}_1\right)
=\mathbf q_1\times\left(-\frac{f'(r)}r(\mathbf q_1-\mathbf q_2)\right)
=\frac{f'(r)}r\mathbf q_1\times\mathbf q_2.
$$
This need not vanish. For example take $f(r)=r^2/2$ and instantaneous positions $\mathbf q_1=(1,0,0)$, $\mathbf q_2=(0,1,0)$; the <torque> is $(0,0,1)$. Therefore **it is not conserved in general**, although it is in special configurations or for a constant potential.

The third particle is force-free, so its <torque> about the origin vanishes:
$$
\frac d{dt}\left(m_3\mathbf q_3\times\dot{\mathbf q}_3\right)
=\mathbf q_3\times m_3\ddot{\mathbf q}_3=0.

Thus **this <angular momentum> is conserved**.

= Solution
{parent=2009/H/H1/iii}

Let $\mathbf F$ be the force on particle one, so the force on particle two is $-\mathbf F$, with $\mathbf F$ parallel to $\mathbf q_1-\mathbf q_2$. The combined <torque> is
$$
\mathbf q_1\times\mathbf F+\mathbf q_2\times(-\mathbf F)
=(\mathbf q_1-\mathbf q_2)\times\mathbf F=0.
$$
Hence **the combined <angular momentum> of particles one and two is conserved**. Centrality of their interaction, in addition to equal and opposite forces, is what makes this <torque> sum zero.

For a <field> point in the plane of the loop, both the directed line element $d\mathbf s$ and the displacement $\mathbf r-\mathbf s$ lie in that plane. Their <cross product> is normal to it, so every term of the <Biot-Savart law> is normal, and their integral is normal as well. This argument also allows the <field> to vanish at particular points.

Choose the loop in the $xy$-plane with centre at the origin and let positive current be counterclockwise as seen from $+z$. On its lower side, write $\mathbf s=(t,-a,0)$, $-a\leq t\leq a$, so
$$
d\mathbf s\times(-\mathbf s)=a\,dt\,\mathbf e_z.
$$
That side contributes
$$
\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\int_{-a}^a\frac{a\,dt}{(t^2+a^2)^{3/2}}\,\mathbf e_z
=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\left[\frac{t}{a\sqrt{t^2+a^2}}\right]_{-a}^a\mathbf e_z
=\frac{\sqrt2\mu_0I}{4\pi a}\mathbf e_z.
$$
All four contributions have the same direction and size by <rotation>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B(0)=\frac{\sqrt2\mu_0I}{\pi a}\,\mathbf n},
$$
where $\mathbf n$ follows the <right-hand rule> for the current.

= Solution
{parent=2009/K/K1}

For a planar <incompressible flow>, a <stream function> satisfies $u=\psi_y$, $v=-\psi_x$. A <velocity potential> satisfies $u=\phi_x$, $v=\phi_y$ where the flow is irrotational; such a potential exists locally, and globally on a simply connected nonsingular region. The <irrotational flow> condition is $v_x-u_y=0$. Where both exist, the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> make the <complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow>[complex potential] $w=\phi+i\psi$ analytic with $w'=u-iv$.

For the logarithmic potential, write $z-z_0=re^{i\theta}$. Then
$$
\phi=\frac m{2\pi}\log r,\qquad \psi=\frac m{2\pi}\theta,
\qquad u_r=\frac m{2\pi r},\quad u_\theta=0.
$$
The outward <volume flux> per unit span across a circle is $2\pi r u_r=m$. Hence it is a <line source> of strength $m$. The potential may use local logarithm branches; its velocity is single-valued away from the source.

By <superposition>, the full flow has
$$
w(z)=Uz+\frac m{2\pi}\log z-\frac m{2\pi}\log(z-1),\qquad
w'(z)=U-\frac m{2\pi z(z-1)}.
$$
For positive $m$ and $U$, the <stagnation points> are
$$
\boxed{z_\pm=\frac{1\pm\sqrt{1+2m/(\pi U)}}2}.
$$
They are real, one upstream of the source and one downstream of the sink. Away from the singularities, <streamlines> are level curves of
$$
\psi(x,y)=Uy+\frac m{2\pi}\bigl(\arg z-\arg(z-1)\bigr).
$$
The zero level through the two stagnation points contains an upper and a lower separating curve, symmetric about the real axis and about $x=1/2$. Together they bound a <Rankine oval>. Flow inside goes from source to sink; the external stream divides around this oval and tends to horizontal flow far away. This also specifies the sketch for every positive parameter ratio; the figure illustrates one ratio.

= Solution
{parent=2009/K/K2}

For the isolated <line vortex>, use <polar coordinates> $z=re^{i\theta}$ to obtain
$$
\phi=\frac\Gamma{2\pi}\theta,\qquad
\psi=-\frac\Gamma{2\pi}\log r,\qquad
u_r=0,\quad u_\theta=\frac\Gamma{2\pi r}.
$$
Thus for nonzero $\Gamma$ the <streamlines> are circles and their counterclockwise <circulation> is $2\pi r u_\theta=\Gamma$. If $\Gamma=0$ the fluid is stationary and the <streamline> description degenerates.

Place an opposite image <vortex> at $z=-1$. The <method of images> gives
$$
\boxed{w(z)=-\frac{i\Gamma}{2\pi}\bigl(\log(z-1)-\log(z+1)\bigr)}.
$$
On $z=iy$, the distances to the two <vortex> positions agree, so $\psi=-(\Gamma/2\pi)\log(|z-1|/|z+1|)=0$. Hence the wall is a <streamline>. Directly the wall velocity has $u=0$ and
$$
v(0,y)=-\frac\Gamma{\pi(1+y^2)}.
$$
For the stipulated steady flow, <Bernoulli's equation> in the irrotational fluid outside the <vortex> core gives
$$
p(0,y)-p_\infty=-\frac12\rho v(0,y)^2
=-\frac{\rho\Gamma^2}{2\pi^2(1+y^2)^2}.
$$
The force exerted by the fluid on this wall points in the $-x$ direction for positive <pressure>. Relative to uniform <pressure> $p_\infty$, however, its change is in the $+x$ direction. Using the supplied integral, the force change per unit span is
$$
\boxed{\Delta\mathbf F_{\rm wall}=\frac{\rho\Gamma^2}{4\pi}\,\mathbf e_x}.
$$
Thus the wall is attracted **towards the <vortex>**, with this magnitude if the other side remains at $p_\infty$. Subtracting the uniform <pressure> is essential for an infinite wall. A freely moving <vortex> would translate parallel to the wall under its image-induced velocity; the calculation here is for the stated steady <vortex> configuration, with its position held fixed.

The inverse transformation is $x=uv$, $y=u(1-v)$, with support $u>0$, $0<v<1$. Its absolute <Jacobian determinant> is

The original joint <probability density function>, by <independence>, is $\lambda^2e^{-\lambda(x+y)}$ on the positive quadrant. The <change of variables formula> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{f_{U,V}(u,v)=\lambda^2u e^{-\lambda u}\mathbf1_{\{u>0,\ 0<v<1\}}}.
$$
Integrating over $v$ gives
$$
\boxed{f_U(u)=\lambda^2u e^{-\lambda u}\mathbf1_{\{u>0\}}},
$$
the <gamma distribution> of shape two and rate $\lambda$. Integrating over $u$ gives $f_V(v)=1$ on $(0,1)$. The factorization also shows that $U$ and $V$ are independent.

For $n=0$, the conditional transition probabilities are $\delta_{ij}$, the entries of $P^0=I$; for $n=1$ the assertion is the definition of $P$. For the induction step, condition on the state at time $n$ and use the <Markov property> and <time-homogeneous Markov property>[time-homogeneity]:
$$
\mathbb P(X_{n+1}=j\mid X_0=i)
=\sum_k\mathbb P(X_n=k\mid X_0=i)p_{kj}
=\sum_k(P^n)_{ik}p_{kj}=(P^{n+1})_{ij}.
$$
The countable sum is justified by the <law of total probability>; all terms are nonnegative. Thus the formula follows for every $n$ by induction. It is the <Chapman-Kolmogorov equation> for repeated one-step transitions.

Let $u_n=\mathbb P(X_n=1\mid X_0=1)$. Conditioning on $X_n$ gives

u_{n+1}=(1-a)u_n+b(1-u_n)=b+(1-a-b)u_n,\qquad u_0=1.

The formula includes $n=0$. If $a+b=1$, its value at $n=0$ is interpreted directly as $u_0=1$, while $u_n=b$ for all $n\geq1$. Since $|1-a-b|<1$, the limiting probability is $b/(a+b)$.

By <continuity> of the <cumulative distribution function> and nonnegativity, $F(0)=0$; strict increase and $F(x)\to1$ allow inversion for every $0<t<1$. The <probability integral transform> follows from
$$
\mathbb P(F(X)\leq t)=\mathbb P(X\leq F^{-1}(t))=t.
$$
Thus $F(X)$ is <uniformly distributed> on $(0,1)$.

The <order statistics> arrange the sample increasingly: $X_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq X_{(n)}$. Strict monotonicity means $F(X_{(i)})$ is the $i$th <order statistic> of $n$ independent uniform variables. Its <density> is
$$
\frac{n!}{(i-1)!(n-i)!}\,t^{i-1}(1-t)^{n-i},\qquad0<t<1,
$$
obtained by choosing the $i-1$ observations below $t$ and the $n-i$ above it. This is the <beta distribution> with parameters $i,n+1-i$, so
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E F(X_{(i)})=\frac i{n+1}}.
$$
For the given <log-logistic distribution>, rearrangement gives
$$
\log\frac{F(x)}{1-F(x)}=(\beta-1)\log x-(\beta-1)\log\alpha.
$$
Consequently plot $y_i=\log(i/(n+1-i))$ vertically against $x_i^*=\log X_{(i)}$ horizontally. Under a good fit, replacing $F(X_{(i)})$ by its plotting position $i/(n+1)$ gives an approximate straight line with
$$
\boxed{\text{slope}=\beta-1,\qquad\text{intercept}=-(\beta-1)\log\alpha}.
$$
This is a diagnostic <probability plot>, rather than an assertion that the <expectation> of the nonlinear transformed <order statistic> equals that transformed plotting position. Positive observations occur almost surely under the model.

With immediate release and ideal independent uniform sampling of fish, each catch is a <Bernoulli trial> with tagged-success probability $p=k/N$. Stopping at the $r$th success makes the count of untagged catches a <negative binomial distribution>[negative binomial random variable]. A total of $x$ failures requires $r-1$ successes among the first $x+r-1$ trials and a success on the last trial, so the probability is $\binom{x+r-1}{r-1}p^r(1-p)^x$. Separate days use disjoint independent sequences of trials, hence their counts form an <independent and identically distributed> sample. Equal catch probabilities alone do not mathematically imply <independence>; this is the usual repeated independent sampling assumption in the stated model.

Let $S=\sum_i x_i$ and $\bar x=S/n$. Terms independent of $p$ can be dropped from the <log-likelihood>, leaving
$$
\ell(p)=nr\log p+S\log(1-p)+C,
\quad\ell'(p)=\frac{nr}p-\frac S{1-p},
\quad\ell''(p)=-\frac{nr}{p^2}-\frac S{(1-p)^2}.
$$
For $S>0$, strict concavity gives the unique interior <maximum-likelihood estimator>[maximum likelihood estimator]
$$
\boxed{\widehat p=\frac{nr}{nr+S}=\frac r{r+\bar x}}.
$$
If $S=0$, the likelihood increases up to $p=1$: this is the boundary estimate on the closed parameter range, or a supremum if the model insists on $p<1$.

Using the given mean, the sample <Fisher information> is
$$
I_n(p)=-\mathbb E\ell''(p)
=\frac{nr}{p^2}+\frac{nr(1-p)/p}{(1-p)^2}
=\boxed{\frac{nr}{p^2(1-p)}}.
$$
The information per observation is $I_1(p)=r/[p^2(1-p)]$.

= Solution
{parent=2009/O/O2/ii}

For a fixed true parameter $0<p<1$, the usual <asymptotic normality of a maximum likelihood estimator>[asymptotic normality of maximum likelihood estimators] gives
$$
\sqrt n(\widehat p-p)\ \xrightarrow{d}\ N\!\left(0,\frac{p^2(1-p)}r\right).
$$
Equivalently, for a large sample, $\widehat p$ is approximately <normally distributed> with mean $p$ and <variance> $p^2(1-p)/(nr)$. Replacing the unknown <variance> by its consistent plug-in estimate gives the <Wald confidence interval>
$$
\boxed{\widehat p\ \pm\ z_{1-\alpha/2}\widehat p\sqrt{\frac{1-\widehat p}{nr}}}.
$$
Here $z_{1-\alpha/2}$ is a standard-normal <quantile>. Intersect with the physical parameter range $(0,1)$ if needed. This is an interior, large-sample approximation; the degenerate estimate $\widehat p=1$ from $S=0$ does not justify a zero-width finite-sample <interval>.

The transformation $N=k/p$ is strictly decreasing for $p>0$. Therefore an <interval> $[L,U]$ with $0<L<U\leq1$ transforms to
$$
\boxed{\left[\frac kU,\frac kL\right]}.
$$
The event $L\leq p\leq U$ is exactly the event $k/U\leq N\leq k/L$, so the approximate coverage is inherited. If the lower endpoint for $p$ reaches zero, the transformed upper endpoint for $N$ is $+\infty$; if the upper endpoint reaches one, the lower endpoint is $k$. Finally intersect with <integer> values $N>k$, rounding the lower bound upward and the upper bound downward. This respects the original discrete population constraint.

= Solution
{parent=2009/P/P1}

The <Gershgorin circle theorem> states that every complex <eigenvalue> $\lambda$ of $A=(a_{ij})$ belongs to at least one closed disc
$$
D_i=\left\{z\in\mathbb C:|z-a_{ii}|\leq\sum_{j\ne i}|a_{ij}|\right\}.
$$
To prove it, take an <eigenvector> $v\ne0$ and choose $i$ with $|v_i|=\max_j|v_j|>0$. The $i$th row of $Av=\lambda v$ gives
$$
|\lambda-a_{ii}|\,|v_i|
=\left|\sum_{j\ne i}a_{ij}v_j\right|
\leq\sum_{j\ne i}|a_{ij}|\,|v_j|
\leq|v_i|\sum_{j\ne i}|a_{ij}|.
$$
Division by $|v_i|$ proves the assertion. The proof works for real matrices even when their eigenvalues or eigenvectors are complex.

For $B+\mu I$, the three discs have centres $2+\mu,3+\mu,4+\mu$ and radii $2,3,4$. Every point in their <union> has real part at least $\mu$. Thus for $\mu>0$ no disc contains zero, and zero cannot be an <eigenvalue>. Hence **$B+\mu C$ is nonsingular**.

= Solution
{parent=2009/P/P2}

For any $q\in\Pi_n$, write $f-q=(f-p)+(p-q)$. The stated <orthogonality> makes the two summands perpendicular in the given <inner product>, because $p-q\in\Pi_n$. The <Pythagorean theorem for inner product spaces> therefore gives
$$
\|f-q\|^2=\|f-p\|^2+\|p-q\|^2\geq\|f-p\|^2.
$$
Equality is attained at $q=p$, and positive definiteness makes it unique. Thus $p$ is the <orthogonal projection> of $f$ onto the <polynomial> subspace.

For the weighted example set $p(x)=A+Bx$. <Orthogonality> against the <basis> $1,x$ gives
$$
0=\int_{-1}^1x^2(x^2-A-Bx)\,dx=\frac25-\frac{2A}3,
$$
$$
0=\int_{-1}^1x^3(x^2-A-Bx)\,dx=-\frac{2B}5.
$$
Odd terms vanish on the symmetric <interval>, so $A=3/5$ and $B=0$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{p(x)=\frac35}.
$$
The weight $x^2$ vanishes only at one point; its integral against $g^2$ is positive for every nonzero <continuous> $g$, so it really does define an <inner product>. For an optional error check, the squared minimum error is $2/7-12/25+6/25=8/175$.
