= i

= Solution
{parent=i}

Write the <lower central series of a Lie algebra> as $\mathfrak g^1=\mathfrak g$ and $\mathfrak g^{j+1}=[\mathfrak g,\mathfrak g^j]$. Since the <nilpotent Lie algebra> is nonzero, there is a last nonzero term $\mathfrak g^c$, with $\mathfrak g^{c+1}=0$. Thus $[\mathfrak g,\mathfrak g^c]=0$, so
$$
0\ne\mathfrak g^c\subseteq Z(\mathfrak g).
$$
Consequently **the centre is nonzero**. This argument works over every <field> and includes an abelian algebra, for which $c=1$.

= ii
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Fix $x$ with $x^N=0$ as a <matrix>. On the full <matrix> algebra define $L_x(y)=xy$ and $R_x(y)=yx$. These endomorphisms commute and each has $N$th power zero. The <adjoint representation of a Lie algebra> is $\operatorname{ad}(x)=L_x-R_x$. The binomial theorem gives
$$
(\operatorname{ad}x)^m(y)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^j\binom mjx^{m-j}yx^j.
$$
For $m=2N-1$, each summand has either $j\ge N$ or $m-j\ge N$, and hence vanishes. Therefore $\operatorname{ad}(x)$ is nilpotent on the full <matrix> algebra and on its <invariant subspace> $\mathfrak g$.

<Engel's theorem>, in its adjoint form, states that a finite-dimensional <Lie algebra> is nilpotent if every $\operatorname{ad}(x)$ is a <nilpotent endomorphism>. Applying it to all $x\in\mathfrak g$ proves
$$
\boxed{\mathfrak g\text{ is nilpotent}.}
$$
This is distinct from assuming that an arbitrary <representation> of a nilpotent algebra must act by nilpotent matrices, which is false.

= iii
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

If $\mathfrak g$ is nilpotent, every <Lie subalgebra> $\mathfrak a$ is nilpotent, since $\mathfrak a^j\subseteq\mathfrak g^j$ in the respective <lower central series of a Lie algebra>. A two-dimensional nilpotent algebra has nonzero centre by part (i). If $0\ne z$ is central, complete it to a <basis> $(z,u)$; every bracket of these <basis> elements is zero. Thus $\mathfrak a$ is abelian.

Conversely, suppose $\mathfrak g$ is not nilpotent. By <Engel's theorem>, some $\operatorname{ad}(x)$ is not nilpotent. Since the <field> is algebraically closed, it has an <eigenvalue> $\lambda\ne0$ with <eigenvector> $y\ne0$. Thus
$$
[x,y]=\lambda y.
$$
The vectors $x,y$ are independent: otherwise their bracket would be zero. Their span is therefore a two-dimensional <Lie subalgebra> and it is nonabelian. This contradicts the hypothesis. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathfrak g\text{ is nilpotent}\iff
\text{every two-dimensional subalgebra is abelian}.}
$$

= iv
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Suppose $\operatorname{ad}(x)$ has a nonzero <eigenvalue> $\lambda$ and corresponding vector $y$. Then $[x,y]=\lambda y$, so
$$
\operatorname{ad}(y)x=-\lambda y\ne0,\qquad
(\operatorname{ad}y)^2x=0.
$$
For a <diagonalizable> <endomorphism> $T$, its <eigenspace> decomposition shows $\ker T^2=\ker T$. The displayed equations therefore contradict diagonalizability of $\operatorname{ad}(y)$.

Thus no $\operatorname{ad}(x)$ has a nonzero <eigenvalue>. Each is <diagonalizable> over the <algebraically closed field>, so each is the zero <endomorphism>. Hence $[x,z]=0$ for every $x,z\in\mathfrak g$, proving
$$
\boxed{\mathfrak g\text{ is abelian}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=i}

By the <classification of finite-dimensional sl2 representations> over $\mathbb C$, every finite-dimensional <module> is a <direct sum> of simple modules $L_m$, indexed by integers $m\ge0$. The <module> $L_m$ has <dimension> $m+1$ and a <basis> $v_0,\ldots,v_m$ with
$$
\begin{aligned}
hv_j&=(m-2j)v_j,\\
ev_j&=jv_{j-1},\\
fv_j&=(m-j)v_{j+1},
\end{aligned}
$$
with out-of-range vectors interpreted as zero. In particular $v_0$ is a <highest weight> vector of weight $m$. The weights are $m,m-2,\ldots,-m$, each with multiplicity one. The multiplicities of the $L_m$ in a finite <module> determine that <module> up to isomorphism.

= ii
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

In the <simple module> $L_m$, weight $0$ occurs once if $m$ is even and never if $m$ is odd. Weight $1$ occurs once if $m$ is odd and never if $m$ is even. Thus each simple summand contributes exactly one to $\dim V_0+\dim V_1$.

<Complete reducibility of semisimple Lie algebra representations> writes $V=\bigoplus_mL_m^{\oplus a_m}$. Taking the two <weight spaces> commutes with this finite <direct sum>, so
$$
\boxed{\dim V_0+\dim V_1=\sum_ma_m.}
$$
The right side counts irreducible summands **with multiplicity**, independently of a chosen decomposition.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Every $2\times2$ <matrix> has the unique decomposition
$$
A=\frac{\operatorname{tr}A}{2}I+\left(A-\frac{\operatorname{tr}A}{2}I\right).
$$
Both summands define invariant subspaces for the <adjoint representation of a Lie algebra>. Scalar matrices commute with everything, so $\mathbb CI$ is $L_0$. The traceless matrices form $\mathfrak{sl}_2$ with <basis> $e,h,f$ of weights $2,0,-2$. Their brackets satisfy $[e,f]=h$, $[h,e]=2e$, $[h,f]=-2f$; the raising and lowering operations connect all three weight lines. This is the <simple module> $L_2$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathfrak{gl}_2(\mathbb C)\cong L_0\oplus L_2.}
$$
As a check, its zero-<weight space> has <dimension> two and its one-<weight space> is zero, agreeing with part (ii).

= iv
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

On a monomial $X^aY^b$, direct <differentiation> gives
$$
E(X^aY^b)=bX^{a+1}Y^{b-1},\quad
F(X^aY^b)=aX^{a-1}Y^{b+1},\quad
H(X^aY^b)=(a-b)X^aY^b.
$$
For example $(EF-FE)(X^aY^b)=[a(b+1)-b(a+1)]X^aY^b=(a-b)X^aY^b$. The same calculation with $H$ gives
$$
[H,E]=2E,\qquad[H,F]=-2F,\qquad[E,F]=H.
$$
These are exactly the brackets of $h,e,f$, so the <linear map> $\rho$ respects every bracket and is a <Lie algebra representation>.

For $m\ge0$, let $W_m$ be the space of <homogeneous polynomials> of <total degree> $m$. Each operator preserves <total degree>. Every <polynomial> is a finite sum of homogeneous polynomials, so
$$
W=\bigoplus_{m=0}^{\infty}W_m.
$$
In $W_m$, the monomials $v_j=X^{m-j}Y^j$ have distinct weights $m-2j$ and exactly the actions described in part (i). To see simplicity directly, a nonzero <invariant subspace> is invariant under $H$ and hence contains one of its weight components: <polynomial interpolation> in $H$ projects onto each of its distinct eigenspaces. Starting with a nonzero multiple of $v_j$, repeated application of $E$ reaches $v_0$, and repeated application of $F$ then reaches all $v_j$. All coefficients used are nonzero in characteristic zero. Therefore $W_m$ is simple and isomorphic to $L_m$.

Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbb C[X,Y]=\bigoplus_{m\ge0}\operatorname{span}\{X^m,X^{m-1}Y,\ldots,Y^m\}
\cong\bigoplus_{m\ge0}L_m.}
$$
This is an algebraic <direct sum>, not an infinite product or completion. Its simple submodules are precisely the $W_m$: they are pairwise nonisomorphic and each occurs once, so projection onto homogeneous degrees and <Schur's lemma> exclude diagonal copies between distinct degrees. Complete reducibility here follows from the explicit decomposition, rather than applying a finite-dimensional theorem directly to the infinite-dimensional $W$.

= Solution
{parent=3}

Equip the finite-dimensional real vector space $V$ with a positive-definite <inner product>. A reduced crystallographic <root system> is a finite set $R\subset V\setminus\{0\}$ spanning $V$ such that, for every $\alpha\in R$,
$$
R\cap\mathbb R\alpha=\{\alpha,-\alpha\},\qquad
s_\alpha(R)=R,\qquad
\frac{2(\beta,\alpha)}{(\alpha,\alpha)}\in\mathbb Z\quad(\beta\in R),
$$
where $s_\alpha(v)=v-2(v,\alpha)\alpha/(\alpha,\alpha)$ is the <orthogonal reflection> in $\alpha^\perp$. The <Weyl group> is $W(R)=\langle s_\alpha:\alpha\in R\rangle$. It permutes the finite set $R$. Its action is faithful because $R$ spans $V$: an element fixing every <root> fixes every vector. Hence it embeds in the finite <symmetric group> of $R$ and **is finite**.

A <Cartan subalgebra> is a nilpotent self-normalizing <Lie subalgebra>. For a finite-dimensional complex <semisimple Lie algebra> it is equivalently a maximal toral subalgebra: a maximal abelian subalgebra whose elements have <diagonalizable> adjoint actions. The commuting semisimple operators $\operatorname{ad}(h)$ give the <root-space decomposition>
$$
\mathfrak g=\mathfrak h\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in R}\mathfrak g_\alpha,\qquad
\mathfrak g_\alpha=\{x:[h,x]=\alpha(h)x\text{ for every }h\in\mathfrak h\}.
$$
The nonzero weights $\alpha$ are the roots. Their real span inside the complex dual $\mathfrak h^*$, with the positive-definite real <inner product> induced by the <Killing form>, is the Euclidean space in which they form a reduced crystallographic <root system>. In particular one uses this real span, rather than treating all of $\mathfrak h^*$ as a real vector space of twice the <rank>.

For the specified <special orthogonal Lie algebra>, put $\bar i=7-i$. The <matrix> relation is $x_{ij}=-x_{\bar j,\bar i}$. Thus its diagonal <Cartan subalgebra> consists of
$$
H(t)=\operatorname{diag}(t_1,t_2,t_3,-t_3,-t_2,-t_1).
$$
Let $\varepsilon_i(H(t))=t_i$. The <matrix> units satisfy $[H,E_{ij}]=(d_i-d_j)E_{ij}$, where $d=(t_1,t_2,t_3,-t_3,-t_2,-t_1)$. A <basis> of <root> vectors is supplied, for $1\le i<j\le3$, by
$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{root}&\text{root vector}\\\hline
\varepsilon_i-\varepsilon_j&E_{ij}-E_{\bar j,\bar i}\\
-\varepsilon_i+\varepsilon_j&E_{ji}-E_{\bar i,\bar j}\\
\varepsilon_i+\varepsilon_j&E_{i,\bar j}-E_{j,\bar i}\\
-\varepsilon_i-\varepsilon_j&E_{\bar j,i}-E_{\bar i,j}
\end{array}
$$
These are twelve independent off-diagonal matrices. Together with the three diagonal generators, they account for the full <dimension> $6\cdot5/2=15$, so no further roots occur. Consequently
$$
\boxed{R=\{\pm\varepsilon_i\pm\varepsilon_j:1\le i<j\le3\},\qquad R\text{ has type }D_3.}
$$
Choose the simple roots
$$
\alpha_1=\varepsilon_1-\varepsilon_2,\qquad
\alpha_2=\varepsilon_2-\varepsilon_3,\qquad
\alpha_3=\varepsilon_2+\varepsilon_3.
$$
The six positive roots are $\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3,\alpha_1+\alpha_2,\alpha_1+\alpha_3,\alpha_1+\alpha_2+\alpha_3$, and every other <root> is their negative. In the standard orthonormal coordinates all roots have squared length $2$, and their <Cartan matrix> is
$$
\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&-1\\-1&2&0\\-1&0&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its <Dynkin diagram> is the connected chain $\alpha_2-\alpha_1-\alpha_3$. A decomposition into mutually orthogonal nonempty <root> systems would separate this connected set of simple roots, which is impossible. Thus the <root system> is irreducible. The chain is also the diagram $A_3$, which is the <root system> of $\mathfrak{sl}_4(\mathbb C)$. The classification of complex semisimple Lie algebras by their <root> systems yields the <Lie algebra isomorphism>
$$
\boxed{\mathfrak{so}_6(\mathbb C)\cong\mathfrak{sl}_4(\mathbb C).}
$$
An explicit explanation of this coincidence uses the <exterior-power Lie algebra representation> of $\mathfrak{sl}_4$ on $\bigwedge^2\mathbb C^4$, a six-dimensional space. After choosing a volume form, $u\wedge v$ defines a nondegenerate <symmetric bilinear form> on that <exterior square>. The induced action preserves it because a traceless <endomorphism> acts trivially on the top exterior power. This gives a nonzero homomorphism $\mathfrak{sl}_4\to\mathfrak{so}_6$. Its <kernel> is an ideal in the simple algebra $\mathfrak{sl}_4$, so it is zero; equal dimensions $15$ then make it an isomorphism. Over $\mathbb C$ all nondegenerate symmetric forms in <dimension> six are equivalent, including the form with <matrix> $J$.
