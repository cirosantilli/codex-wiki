= Compressible flow
{wiki}

Compressible flow allows density to change materially and is required for finite-amplitude pressure waves and shocks.

= Transonic flow
{parent=Compressible flow}

A <compressible flow> that passes between subsonic and supersonic regions. A smooth steady crossing of a <sonic point> requires the corresponding regularity condition; a discontinuous transition can instead involve a <shock wave>.

= Stagnation temperature
{parent=Compressible flow}
{title2=$T_{\rm stag}$}

The <temperature> reached by bringing a flow to rest adiabatically while converting its <kinetic energy> to enthalpy. For a calorically <perfect gas>, $T_{\rm stag}=T+u^2/(2c_p)=T[1+(\gamma-1)M^2/2]$. It is conserved across an adiabatic normal shock even though <entropy> increases and <stagnation pressure> decreases.

= Choked flow
{parent=Compressible flow}

Flow whose <mass> flux is limited by a sonic condition in a controlling section, for fixed upstream thermodynamic conditions. In a smooth isentropic converging-diverging <nozzle>, the throat reaches <Mach number> one. Lowering downstream <pressure> further cannot indefinitely increase that fixed-upstream throat <mass> flux; shocks and downstream structure can still change.

= Quasi-one-dimensional flow
{parent=Compressible flow}

A model representing a passage by its varying cross-sectional area and approximately uniform section-averaged flow variables. It neglects detailed transverse <velocity> and thermodynamic structure. In a smooth lossless <nozzle>, conservation laws then connect area to <Mach number>; this model does not by itself determine the curved sonic surface of an arbitrary multidimensional passage.

= Isothermal flow through molecular hydrogen formation
{parent=Compressible flow}
{title2=$\rho_1u_1=\rho_2u_2,\quad p_1+\rho_1u_1^2=p_2+\rho_2u_2^2$}

Across a thin steady transition with negligible external impulse, <mass conservation> and <momentum conservation> give the displayed relations. If the gas stays isothermal while neutral atomic <hydrogen> becomes <molecular hydrogen>, the mean particle <mass> doubles and $a_2^2=a_1^2/2$ for its <isothermal sound speed>. Let $J=u_1+a_1^2/u_1$. The downstream subsonic branch has $u_2=[J-\sqrt{J^2-2a_1^2}]/2$, $\rho_2/\rho_1=u_1/u_2$, and $p_2-p_1=\rho_1u_1(u_1-u_2)>0$.

For $u_1\ll a_1$, the leading behavior is $u_2\simeq u_1/2$, $\rho_2\simeq2\rho_1$ and $p_2\simeq p_1$, at unchanged <temperature>. Radiation of the molecular binding energy prevents that released energy from necessarily heating the gas; it does not, by itself, establish an isothermal thermal closure. If temperature evolution is allowed, an energy balance and a cooling model are required to determine all four changes uniquely.

= Self-similar blast wave
{parent=Compressible flow}

A <self-similar blast wave> is an expanding <shock wave> whose post-shock profiles retain their shape in a rescaled radius or distance. A fixed explosion energy, ambient <mass density> and geometry set the front scaling through conservation of total energy. The dimensionless coefficient requires the profiles and an energy-normalization convention.

= Spherical blast-wave similarity energy integrals
{parent=Self-similar blast wave}
{title2=$E_{\rm kin}=2\pi E\int_0^{\xi_0}\xi^4f_\rho f_u^2d\xi$}

Use the fields of <pressure similarity in a spherical blast wave>, with front radius $R=\xi_0\ell$. Since $\rho_0\ell^5/t^2=E$, changing the radial integration variable to $\xi$ gives
$$
E_{\rm kin}=2\pi E\int_0^{\xi_0}\xi^4f_\rho f_u^2\,d\xi,\qquad
E_{\rm int}=\frac{4\pi E}{\gamma-1}\int_0^{\xi_0}\xi^2f_p\,d\xi.
$$
Both are time independent for fixed integrable <similarity profiles> and constant front coordinate $\xi_0$. With explosion energy $E$ and negligible external energy input or losses, their sum equals $E$. This normalization, together with shock matching and regularity, fixes the dimensionless blast coefficient rather than <dimensional analysis> alone.

= Pressure similarity in a spherical blast wave
{parent=Self-similar blast wave}
{title2=$p=E^{2/5}\rho_0^{3/5}t^{-6/5}f_p(\xi)$}

For a spherical <self-similar blast wave>, put $\ell=(Et^2/\rho_0)^{1/5}$, $\xi=r/\ell$, $\rho=\rho_0f_\rho(\xi)$ and $u=(r/t)f_u(\xi)$. The radial <Euler equation> then requires $p=\rho_0(\ell/t)^2f_p(\xi)$, where
$$
f_p'=-\xi f_\rho\left[f_u^2-f_u+\xi\left(f_u-\frac25\right)f_u'\right].
$$
This follows from $\partial_t\xi=-2\xi/(5t)$ and $\partial_r\xi=1/\ell$. The momentum equation alone leaves a spatially uniform additive <pressure> undetermined. The scale-free strong-shock boundary data fix that freedom consistently with the displayed similarity scaling; an independently prescribed ambient pressure introduces another scale.

= Thin-shell approximation for a spherical blast wave
{parent=Self-similar blast wave}
{title2=$\alpha=1/2$}

Let a strong <shock wave> of radius $R$ and speed $V$ sweep a uniform <mass density> $\rho_0$ into a shell of mass $4\pi\rho_0R^3/3$ and speed $2V/(\gamma+1)$. If the cavity <pressure> is $\alpha$ times the immediate post-shock <pressure>, shell <momentum> balance gives $R\dot V=3(\alpha-1)V^2$. Constant total <energy> gives $R^3V^2=\mathrm{constant}$ and hence $\alpha=1/2$. Including shell internal and kinetic <energy>, and treating the cavity volume as $4\pi R^3/3$ to thin-shell accuracy, yields $R=\xi(E/\rho_0)^{1/5}t^{2/5}$ with $\xi^5=75(\gamma-1)(\gamma+1)^2/[16\pi(5\gamma-3)]$. This coefficient is approximate; resolving the shell's finite volume changes it.

= Spherical blast wave in a power-law ambient density
{parent=Self-similar blast wave}
{title2=$R\propto(E/C)^{1/(5-\beta)}t^{2/(5-\beta)}$}

For an <energy>-conserving strong spherical <shock wave> expanding into <mass density> $Cr^{-\beta}$, $0\leq\beta<3$, <dimensional analysis> gives the displayed radius law. Neglect ambient <pressure>, radiation losses and gravity in this blast-dominated approximation. Write $u=\dot R U(\xi)$, $\rho=CR^{-\beta}D(\xi)$ and $p=CR^{-\beta}\dot R^2P(\xi)$, with $\xi=r/R$. The radial <continuity equation>, momentum equation and adiabatic <pressure> equation reduce to
$$
(U-\xi)D'+D(U'+2U/\xi-\beta)=0,\qquad
(U-\xi)U'+\frac{\beta-3}{2}U=-P'/D,
$$
$$
(U-\xi)P'+[\gamma(U'+2U/\xi)-3]P=0.
$$
The <strong-shock Rankine-Hugoniot conditions> give $U(1)=P(1)=2/(\gamma+1)$ and $D(1)=(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$. The explosion <energy> fixes the radius coefficient through $E=4\pi CR^{3-\beta}\dot R^2\int_0^1[DU^2/2+P/(\gamma-1)]\xi^2d\xi$.

= Linear-profile spherical blast wave
{parent=Spherical blast wave in a power-law ambient density}
{title2=$\beta=(7-\gamma)/(\gamma+1)$}

The <similarity profiles> $U=2\xi/(\gamma+1)$, $D=(\gamma+1)\xi/(\gamma-1)$ and $P=2\xi^3/(\gamma+1)$ solve the spherical blast equations at the displayed ambient exponent. For $\gamma=5/3$, $\beta=2$ and $R^3=3Et^2/(2\pi C)$. The dimensional fields are $u=r/(2t)$, $\rho=8\pi C^2r/(3Et^2)$ and $p=2\pi C^2r^3/(9Et^4)$ for $0<r<R$. Their kinetic and internal <energies> are equal. Substituting these profiles into all three equations and the shock conditions verifies the solution.

= Planar blast-wave energy scaling
{parent=Self-similar blast wave}
{title2=$Z\propto t^{2/3}$}

For a planar adiabatic explosion with energy per unit area $E$ in a uniform medium of <mass density> $\rho_0$, the conserved energy has form $E=I\rho_0Z\dot Z^2$, with a finite positive integral $I$ of the <similarity profiles>. Hence $Z=C(E/\rho_0)^{1/3}t^{2/3}$. If $E$ is shared by two fronts, its fraction assigned to either side is absorbed into $C$.

= Polytropic flow
{parent=Compressible flow}
{title2=$p=K\rho^\gamma$}

A polytropic flow uses a <polytropic equation of state>. When $K$ and the <adiabatic exponent> are constant, a smooth streamline has <specific enthalpy> $h=\gamma K\rho^{\gamma-1}/(\gamma-1)$ and <adiabatic sound speed> $c_s^2=\gamma K\rho^{\gamma-1}$. Spatially varying entropy can make $K$ depend on streamline; the constant-$K$ spherical wind is a special case.

= Spherically symmetric adiabatic flow
{parent=Compressible flow}

A smooth radial <compressible flow> with a fixed <adiabatic exponent> conserves $r^2\rho u$ and $p/\rho^\gamma$. In a prescribed <Newtonian gravitational potential>, its <Bernoulli function> is $u^2/2+c^2/(\gamma-1)+\Phi$, with $c$ the <adiabatic sound speed>. Eliminating the density derivative yields $(u-c^2/u)u'=2c^2/r-\Phi'$.

= Transonic spherical flow in a power-law potential
{parent=Spherically symmetric adiabatic flow}

A smooth <transonic branch> in a <power-law gravitational potential> has $u_*^2=c_*^2=A\beta/(2r_*^\beta)$ at its <sonic point>. Substitution into the <Bernoulli function> gives $\mathcal B=c_*^2[1/(\gamma-1)+1/2-2/\beta]$. The local <sonic-point slope discriminant> distinguishes a genuine crossing from a degenerate everywhere-sonic scaling solution.

= Transonic spherical accretion rate in a power-law potential
{parent=Transonic spherical flow in a power-law potential}
{title2=$\dot M$}

With reservoir density $\rho_\infty$ and <adiabatic sound speed> $c_\infty$, put $q=2/\beta-1/2$ and $h=1-q(\gamma-1)>0$. The selected <mass accretion rate> is $4\pi\rho_\infty(A\beta/2)^{2/\beta}c_\infty^{1-4/\beta}h^{q-1/(\gamma-1)}$. It follows by evaluating the conserved spherical flux at the <sonic point>.

= Endpoint limits of power-law spherical accretion
{parent=Transonic spherical accretion rate in a power-law potential}

The dimensionless accretion factor $(1-q\delta)^{q-1/\delta}$ tends to $e^q$ as $\delta\to0$. For $q>0$, it tends to $1$ as $\delta\uparrow1/q$, although the <sonic point> moves to the origin. Writing the latter logarithm as $-qh\ln h/(1-h)$ makes the finite limit transparent. These limits connect <transonic spherical accretion rate in a power-law potential> to the <isothermal equation of state> and the limiting critical <adiabatic exponent>.

= Critical adiabatic exponent for spherical power-law flow
{parent=Transonic spherical flow in a power-law potential}
{title2=$\gamma_{\rm crit}=(4+\beta)/(4-\beta)\quad(0<\beta<4)$}

For $0<\beta<4$, a nondegenerate <transonic spherical flow in a power-law potential> connected to infinity requires $\gamma<(4+\beta)/(4-\beta)$. For $\beta\geq4$, every finite $\gamma>1$ satisfies the local crossing condition; there is no finite upper bound. The uniformly valid inequality is $2\beta-(4-\beta)(\gamma-1)>0$.

= Isothermal shock
{parent=Compressible flow}
{title2=$\rho_2/\rho_1=\mathcal M_1^2$}

An isothermal shock joins two states at the same <temperature> and <isothermal sound speed>. Mass and <momentum> conservation imply $u_1u_2=c_s^2$ for a nontrivial planar jump, so the compression ratio is the upstream isothermal Mach number squared. <Heat> exchange replaces adiabatic energy conservation across the gas alone.

= Dissociating isothermal shock
{parent=Isothermal shock}
{title2=$2\chi^2-(\mathcal M_1^2+1)\chi+\mathcal M_1^2=0$}

Suppose molecular <hydrogen> is fully dissociated into atoms across a steady planar <shock>, with the same far-upstream and far-downstream <temperature> and negligible binding-energy mass correction. The upstream <isothermal sound speed> obeys $a_1^2=k_BT/(2m_H)$, while $a_2^2=2a_1^2$. Conservation of mass gives $\chi=\rho_2/\rho_1=u_1/u_2$. Momentum conservation then gives $u_1^2+a_1^2=u_1^2/\chi+2a_1^2\chi$, producing the displayed quadratic with $\mathcal M_1=u_1/a_1$.

The high root approaches $\mathcal M_1^2/2$ at large upstream Mach number. Its real compressive domain is $\mathcal M_1\ge1+\sqrt2$. At the endpoint the downstream flow is sonic; above it the high root is subsonic downstream. Completion of dissociation and maintenance of equal temperatures additionally require a consistent chemical and radiative energy budget. The two flux equations alone do not establish that physical realizability.

= Cooling across an isothermal shock
{parent=Isothermal shock}
{title2=$\mathcal L_A=j(u_1^2-u_2^2)/2$}

The <specific enthalpy> is equal on both sides of an isothermal perfect-gas shock. The energy removed per unit area per unit time is $j(u_1^2-u_2^2)/2$, with $j>0$ the normal mass flux. Net cooling therefore selects compression shocks; an expansion jump requires heating.

= Fluid total-energy equation
{parent=Compressible flow}
{title2=$\partial_tE+\nabla\cdot[(E+p)\mathbf u]=-\rho\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\Phi+Q$}

For an inviscid fluid with specific <internal energy> $e$, let $E=\rho(e+|\mathbf u|^2/2)$. In a prescribed potential $\Phi$, $\partial_tE+\nabla\cdot[(E+p)\mathbf u]=-\rho\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\Phi+Q$, where $Q$ is external <heat> supply. A static gravitational potential can instead be included in the conserved energy.

= Spherical polytropic flow with adiabatic exponent three halves
{parent=Compressible flow}

For steady spherical <isentropic flow> with $p=K\rho^{3/2}$ around a point mass, put $j=r^2\rho v>0$ and $\mathcal E=v^2/2+2c^2-GM/r>0$. Eliminating the <mass density> gives $c^2=[j(3K/2)^2]^{2/5}r^{-4/5}\mathcal M^{-2/5}$. With $r_s=GM/(4\mathcal E)$, $x=(r/r_s)^{1/5}$, $y=\mathcal M^{2/5}$ and $\lambda=[j(3K/2)^2]^{2/5}/(2\mathcal E r_s^{4/5})$, the <Bernoulli equation> reduces to $\lambda F(y)=F(x)$, where $F(t)=t^4+4/t$. Since $F$ has its unique minimum at $t=1$, a regular <sonic point> requires $\lambda=1$ and $r=r_s$. One <transonic branch> is $y=x$, which has $v=\sqrt{2\mathcal E}$, $\rho\propto r^{-2}$ and $p\propto r^{-3}$. The decreasing branch has inner <free fall> and an outer nearly static reservoir, giving <Bondi accretion> for inward <fluid flow>. A nonradiative stationary <shock wave> keeps $j,\mathcal E,r_s$ fixed but raises $K$, so $\lambda$ increases by the factor $(K_2/K_1)^{4/5}$. The inward transonic solution is the spherical $n=2$, $\gamma=3/2$ specialization of <transonic accretion in a power-law tube>.

= Isothermal sound speed
{title2=$a_i$}
{parent=Speed of sound}

The isothermal sound speed is $a_i^2=(\partial p/\partial\rho)_T$. For an ideal gas it is $a_i^2=p/\rho=\mathcal R T/\mu$.

= Adiabatic sound speed
{title2=$v_s$}
{parent=Speed of sound}

The adiabatic sound speed is $v_s^2=(\partial p/\partial\rho)_s$. For a perfect gas it is $v_s^2=\gamma p/\rho$.

= Isentropic flow
{parent=Compressible flow}
{wiki}

An isentropic flow has constant entropy along the fluid and contains no entropy-producing shocks or dissipation. For a perfect gas, $p/\rho^\gamma$ is constant and $c_s^2=\gamma p/\rho\propto\rho^{\gamma-1}$.

= Isentropic Euler equations
{parent=Isentropic flow}
{c}

In one spatial dimension, inviscid isentropic mass and momentum conservation are
$$
\rho_t+(\rho u)_x=0,
\qquad
u_t+uu_x+\rho^{-1}p_x=0,
\qquad p=p(\rho).
$$

= Homentropic flow
{parent=Isentropic flow}

A homentropic flow has one spatially uniform entropy value throughout the fluid. It is therefore isentropic along every fluid trajectory, and its pressure can be treated as a single-valued function of density.

= Globally isothermal equation of state
{title2=$p=c_s^2\rho$}
{parent=Compressible flow}

= Isothermal equation of state
{synonym}

A globally isothermal fluid has spatially and temporally constant sound speed $c_s$ and pressure $p=c_s^2\rho$. Maintaining this relation generally requires heat exchange rather than adiabatic evolution.

= Sonic point
{parent=Compressible flow}
{wiki}

A sonic point is a point at which the flow speed equals the local <sound speed>, so the <Mach number> is one.

= Plane-parallel isothermal sonic transition at a potential maximum
{parent=Sonic point}
{title2=$C=\tfrac12+\Phi_{\max}/c_s^2$}

In a nonzero steady one-dimensional flow with an <isothermal equation of state> with $p=c_s^2\rho$ and constant cross section, the <continuity equation> gives $\rho u=\text{constant}$. Eliminating density from momentum gives $(u-c_s^2/u)u'=-\Phi'$. With <Mach number> $\mathcal M=|u|/c_s$, integration yields $\mathcal M^2/2-\ln\mathcal M+\Phi/c_s^2=C$. The function $f(\mathcal M)=\mathcal M^2/2-\ln\mathcal M$ has minimum $1/2$ at $\mathcal M=1$, so a smooth transonic crossing at $x_*$ requires a local maximum of $\Phi$ and $C=1/2+\Phi(x_*)/c_s^2$. At a nondegenerate maximum, $f=1/2+(\mathcal M-1)^2+\cdots$ gives $\mathcal M'(x_*)=\pm\sqrt{-\Phi''(x_*)/(2c_s^2)}$.

= Sonic-point slope discriminant
{parent=Sonic point}

For <spherically symmetric adiabatic flow> in a <power-law gravitational potential>, let $X=(r_*/u_*)u_*'$ and $\delta=\gamma-1$. Differentiation at a <sonic point> gives $(2+\delta)X^2+4\delta X+4\delta-2\beta=0$. Its discriminant is $8[2\beta-(4-\beta)\delta]$; a positive value gives the two local crossing directions.

= Critical speed of a polytropic flow
{title2=$c_{\rm cr}$}
{parent=Sonic point}

For a steady polytropic streamline with Bernoulli function $C_B$, the critical speed is the sound speed at which the scalar mass flux $\rho u$ is maximal. It satisfies
$$
c_{\rm cr}^2=\frac{2(\gamma-1)}{\gamma+1}C_B.
$$

= Transonic branch
{parent=Sonic point}

A transonic branch is a solution that passes continuously between subsonic and supersonic flow through a regular <sonic point>.

= Transonic accretion in a power-law tube
{parent=Transonic branch}

Consider steady <isentropic flow> toward a <Newtonian gravitational potential> $-GM/r$ through a tube of cross-sectional area $A(r)=Cr^n$. Write $v>0$ for the inward speed and use a <polytropic equation of state> with <specific-heat ratio> $\gamma>1$. <Mass conservation> and the <Euler equations for an inviscid fluid> give
$$
\rho v A=\dot M,
\qquad
\left(v-\frac{c_s^2}{v}\right)v'=\frac{nc_s^2}{r}-\frac{GM}{r^2},
$$
where $c_s$ is the <adiabatic sound speed>. A regular <sonic point> therefore has $v_s=c_s$ and $r_s=GM/(nc_s^2)$. The <Bernoulli equation>, matched to a nearly stationary reservoir with <sound speed> $c_0$, gives
$$
\frac{v^2}{2}+\frac{c^2}{\gamma-1}-\frac{GM}{r}=\frac{c_0^2}{\gamma-1},
\qquad
c_s^2=\frac{2c_0^2}{(2n+1)-(2n-1)\gamma}.
$$
For $n>1/2$, a finite positive <sonic point> requires $1<\gamma<(2n+1)/(2n-1)$. Differentiating the flow equation at that point, with $x=r_sv'_s/c_s$, gives
$$
(\gamma+1)x^2+2n(\gamma-1)x+n^2(\gamma-1)-n=0.
$$
Its <discriminant> is $4n[(2n+1)-(2n-1)\gamma]$, so the same bound permits real regular slopes. The branch on which the <Mach number> rises inward selects the negative sign in
$$
x=\frac{-n(\gamma-1)\pm\sqrt{n[(2n+1)-(2n-1)\gamma]}}{\gamma+1}.
$$
For a spherical tube $n=2$, this recovers the $5/3$ threshold of <Bondi accretion>; for a <dipolar flux-tube area>, $n=3$ gives $7/5$. The tube approximation must remain valid between the reservoir matching region and the accretor.

= Mach number
{title2=$\mathcal M$}
{c}
{parent=Compressible flow}
{wiki}

The <Mach number> is the ratio of <fluid flow> speed to local <sound speed>.

More precisely, the <Mach number> is the <dimensionless quantity>
$$
M=\frac{|\mathbf v_{\mathrm{body}}-\mathbf u_{\mathrm{gas}}|}{c}=\frac{U}{c},
$$
where $U$ is the magnitude of the body's <relative velocity> through the surrounding <gas>, and $c>0$ is the local <sound speed> measured in the <gas> rest <reference frame>. For a <fluid flow> past a stationary body, the same <Mach number> uses the local <fluid> speed in the body's rest <reference frame>. A <Mach number> below, equal to, or above one means <subsonic flow>, <sonic flow>, or <supersonic flow>, respectively. The denominator is a property of the local <gas>: its <sound speed> depends on <temperature> and composition.<Mach Number (NASA Glenn Research Center)>[Mach Number, paragraphs defining the speed ratio and dependence on gas type and temperature; flight-regime list]{ref}

The <relative velocity> matters even when the body is at rest in a <wind tunnel>. A uniform change of <reference frame> subtracts the same <velocity> from body and <gas>, so their <relative velocity> and the <Mach number> stay unchanged under a <Galilean transformation>. Ground speed alone cannot supply $U$ when there is wind. For an original collinear example, a body moving at $680\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ over the ground through <gas> moving in the same direction at $170\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ has $U=510\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. With a stipulated <sound speed> $c=340\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, its <Mach number> is $1.5$, rather than $2$. A moving observer changes apparent <acoustic wave> arrival times, as in the <radial Mach number>, without changing the body's <Mach number> relative to the <gas>.

Why is the threshold one so useful? <Acoustic waves> carry small <pressure> disturbances at the <sound speed>. In <subsonic flow>, such information can travel upstream relative to the body; in a uniform <supersonic flow>, it is swept downstream. The <Mach cone> turns this difference in communication into visible geometry. Matching <Mach number> between a <wind tunnel> and flight is therefore essential to matching <compressible flow> effects.<Mach Number (NASA Glenn Research Center)>[Mach Number, paragraphs on downstream propagation and matching wind-tunnel and flight Mach numbers]{ref}

An actual <supersonic wind tunnel> experiment makes the distinction between <wavefronts> and <shock waves> concrete. In NASA Glenn's 1-by-1-foot <wind tunnel>, interchangeable converging-diverging <nozzle> blocks set the <Mach number>. A strut-mounted exhaust-<nozzle> model had a nosecone, an air plenum and transparent sidewalls for <schlieren photography>. Before installing the model, the researchers photographed the empty test section: two <shock wave> patterns originated at a wall joint after the <nozzle> block. This control guided model placement to keep reflected <shock waves> away from the plume-study region. The rig included internal <total pressure> and <static pressure> sensing and a traversing external <static pressure> probe. A baseline <nozzle> and previous <pressure> data supplied a planned validation comparison; the report distinguishes this plan from the observed empty-tunnel control. This establishes an apparatus origin for those visible <shock waves>, not a measurement of the <Mach cone> from an infinitesimal source. The reported finite <shock wave> angle cannot simply be inserted into the <Mach angle> formula to calibrate $M$: <shock wave> strength and geometry also matter.<Wind Tunnel Model Design for the Study of Plume Effects on Sonic Boom for Isolated Exhaust Nozzles by Raymond S. Castner>[Wind Tunnel Model Design for the Study of Plume Effects on Sonic Boom for Isolated Exhaust Nozzles, NASA/TM-2010-216259, §II printed p. 2; §§IV–V printed pp. 8–9; Figs. 7–10 and concluding remarks printed p. 10]{ref}

In conventional <schlieren photography>, a bright slit source and two concave mirrors send parallel <light rays> through the <wind tunnel> and focus them at a knife edge before an <optical camera>. Spatial changes in <mass density> cause <refraction>; the knife edge converts the deflection into image contrast, making <shock waves> visible. This is the general optical principle, rather than a specification of every component in the preceding rig. A photograph projects a three-dimensional <fluid flow> into two dimensions; contrast alone does not provide a calibrated <pressure> jump or <acoustic wave> amplitude.<Schlieren Flow Visualization (NASA Glenn Research Center)>[Schlieren Flow Visualization, paragraphs describing the two-mirror slit-source and knife-edge system, density-gradient contrast, and two-dimensional recording of three-dimensional shocks]{ref}

= Moving-point acoustic wavefronts
{parent=Mach number}
{title2=$(x-U\tau)^2+y^2=c^2\tau^2,\quad\tau<0$}

Take a steady moving point source in uniform resting <gas>, with constant <sound speed> $c$ and source <velocity> $U\mathbf e_x$. Place the source at the origin at the current <time> $t=0$. In the <linear acoustics> model, an emission at an earlier <time> $\tau<0$ has a spherical <wavefront> centered at $(U\tau,0,0)$ with radius $-c\tau$. An axial cross-section is the <circle> in the displayed equation. It is centered at the old release position, not the source's present position. The circles describe the geometry of weak <acoustic waves>, not a measured <shock wave> or its <pressure> jump.

For $M=0.5$, every old <circle> contains the present source because its center is $U|\tau|$ from the origin while its radius is the larger distance $c|\tau|$. It reaches ahead of the source. For $M=2$, the rightmost point of every old <circle> is $(U-c)\tau<0$: the source outruns all of its old <wavefronts>. The continuously emitted <wavefronts> have a common <geometric envelope>, the <Mach cone>. The plotted discrete release times sample this continuous family; rotating the axial section about the motion axis recovers the three-dimensional construction.

= Radial Mach number
{parent=Mach number}
{title2=$M_r=\widehat r\cdot v/c_0$}

The radial <Mach number> is the source <velocity> projected toward the observer, divided by the <sound speed>. It enters the <moving-surface retarded Jacobian> through $d|x-y(\tau)|/d\tau=-\widehat r\cdot v$. The sign is directional: a source moving toward the observer has positive $M_r$ and compressed arrival times.

= Subsonic flow
{parent=Mach number}
{wiki}

A subsonic flow has <Mach number> less than one, so its speed is below the local <sound speed>.

For uniform <gas> moving at $U\mathbf e_x$ past a resting body, an upstream-directed <acoustic wave> has body-frame <velocity> $(U-c)\mathbf e_x$. When $0\leq M<1$, this <velocity> points upstream. This is why the body can influence the upstream <fluid flow> through small <pressure> disturbances.

= Sonic flow
{parent=Mach number}
{title2=$M=1$}

A <sonic flow> has <Mach number> exactly one: the local <fluid> speed relative to the body equals the local <sound speed>. In uniform <gas> flowing at $U=c$ past a resting body, an upstream-directed <acoustic wave> has zero body-frame <velocity>. A <sonic point> is a location where this equality holds. In the <moving-point acoustic wavefronts> construction, old <spheres> touch the source at the origin; the <Mach angle> tends to $90^\circ$ as the <supersonic flow> approaches this threshold.

= Supersonic flow
{parent=Mach number}
{wiki}

= Supersonic
{synonym}

A supersonic flow has <Mach number> greater than one, so the flow speed exceeds the local <sound speed>.

For uniform <gas> moving at $U\mathbf e_x$ past a resting body, even an <acoustic wave> directed against the <fluid flow> has body-frame <velocity> $(U-c)\mathbf e_x$ downstream. More generally, an <acoustic wave> travelling along a <unit vector> $\mathbf n$ has body-frame <velocity> $U\mathbf e_x+c\mathbf n$; its $x$ component is at least $U-c>0$. All such weak signals move downstream and their possible directions fit inside a <Mach cone>.

= Riemann invariant
{parent=Compressible flow}
{c}
{wiki}

For one-dimensional homentropic ideal-gas flow,
$$
R_\pm=u\pm\frac{2(c-c_0)}{\gamma-1}
$$
is constant along the characteristic $dx/dt=u\pm c$.

= Loss of differentiability from square-root hyperbolic source data
{parent=Riemann invariant}
{title2=$R_x+RR_y=0,\quad S_x+SS_y=2$}

For $U_x+A(U)U_y=(1,\sqrt v)^T$ with $A=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}u&2\\2v&u\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ and $v>0$, the variables $R=u-2\sqrt v$ and $S=u+2\sqrt v$ decouple into an unforced and a uniformly forced <inviscid Burgers equation>. Smooth data for $u,v$ need not be smooth data for these variables when $v$ vanishes. For example $u(0,y)=2y$, $v(0,y)=y^2$ gives $R_0=4\min(y,0)$ and $S_0=4\max(y,0)$. Their <derivative> corners travel along different <characteristic> families. In the positive quadrant, $R=0$ and $S=2x$ below $y=x^2$, whereas $S=2(x+2x^2+2y)/(1+4x)$ above. Reconstructing $u=(R+S)/2$, $v=(S-R)^2/16$ gives a continuous piecewise classical continuation whose $y$ <derivative> jumps at the parabola. Treating $\sqrt{y^2}$ as $y$ for all real $y$ would miss this obstruction. The degeneracy at zero density and the regularity class are essential parts of the <Cauchy problem>.

= Source-balanced Riemann invariant
{parent=Riemann invariant}
{title2=$\nabla R\cdot S=0$}

For a smooth <hyperbolic system> $U_t+A(U)U_x=S(U)$, suppose $\nabla R$ is a left eigenvector of $A$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$. The chain rule gives $(\partial_t+\lambda\partial_x)R=\nabla R\cdot S$. Hence the homogeneous invariant remains an exact <Riemann invariant> when its gradient annihilates the source. For $U=(u,v)$, $A=\begin{pmatrix}v&u\\u^{-3}&v\end{pmatrix}$, $S=(-u^3,u)$ and $u>0$, the function $R=v-1/u$ has $\nabla R=(u^{-2},1)$, eigenvalue $v+1/u$, and $\nabla R\cdot S=0$. On its zero level, the other characteristic speed vanishes and $v_t=1/v$ along constant spatial positions. Source terms need not destroy every invariant.

= Riemann invariants for one-dimensional isentropic flow
{c}
{parent=Riemann invariant}

For a barotropic pressure law with $c^2=dp/d\rho$, the invariants
$$
R_\pm=u\pm\int^\rho\frac{c(s)}s\,ds
$$
obey $[\partial_t+(u\pm c)\partial_x]R_\pm=0$.

= Vacuum formation at an accelerating withdrawing piston
{parent=Riemann invariants for one-dimensional isentropic flow}
{title2=$t_v=2c_0/[f(\gamma-1)]$}

An initially resting perfect gas with adiabatic index $\gamma>1$ next to a piston $X(t)=-ft^2/2$ develops a smooth rarefaction. The incoming invariant gives $c_p=c_0-(\gamma-1)ft/2$. Pressure falls as $p_p=p_0(1-t/t_v)^{2\gamma/(\gamma-1)}$ until $t_v$, when the piston speed is $2c_0/(\gamma-1)$. Thereafter the gas edge moves at that limiting constant speed while the piston accelerates away, opening a vacuum gap.

= Simple wave
{parent=Riemann invariant}
{wiki}

A simple wave is a region of a hyperbolic flow in which one <Riemann invariant> is constant throughout, leaving the other invariant to determine all fluid variables.

= Area transformation of a nonlinear acoustic simple wave
{parent=Simple wave}
{title2=$q=gQ,\quad g=\sqrt{A(Z_0)/A(Z)},\quad \zeta=\int_{Z_0}^Zg(s)\,ds$}

For positive smooth tube area $A$, the equation $q_Z-qq_\theta+q(\log A)'/2=0$ becomes $Q_\zeta-QQ_\theta=0$ under the displayed transformation. Indeed, $g'/g=-A'/(2A)$ cancels geometrical spreading and $\zeta'=g$ makes the remaining nonlinear and transport coefficients equal. Starting with $q(\theta,Z_0)=f(\theta)$ gives $Q=f(\xi)$ and $\theta=\xi-f(\xi)\zeta$; <characteristic crossing> first occurs when $\zeta\sup f'=1$, if that supremum is positive.

= Shock distance for a spherical simple wave launched at finite radius
{parent=Area transformation of a nonlinear acoustic simple wave}
{title2=$R_s=R_0\exp[1/(R_0\sup f')]$}

For area $A(R)=R^2$ and smooth data $q(\theta,R_0)=f(\theta)$ at $R_0>0$, the transformed distance is $\zeta=R_0\log(R/R_0)$. If $M=\sup f'>0$, the first <shock wave> occurs at the displayed radius; if $M\leq0$, there is no forward characteristic crossing. Nontrivial bounded data cannot instead be imposed at $R=0$ in this outgoing simple-wave model: along every characteristic, $Rq$ is constant, so a bounded limit at the origin forces that constant to vanish. With distance measured from a finite emitting surface, use $A(Z)=(R_0+Z)^2$ and subtract $R_0$ from the shock radius.

= Linearly degenerate characteristic field
{parent=Simple wave}
{title2=$\nabla_{\mathbf U}v\cdot r=0$}

A characteristic field is linearly degenerate if the directional derivative of its speed along its right <eigenvector> vanishes. Its simple waves have constant propagation speed along the state curve, so they do not steepen by amplitude-dependent transport.

= Simple wave in magnetohydrodynamics
{parent=Simple wave}
{title2=$A_x\mathbf U_q=v(q)\mathbf U_q$}

An ideal-MHD simple wave traces a one-parameter state-space curve whose tangent is a right <eigenvector> of the characteristic matrix. Its parameter obeys $q_t+v(q)q_x=0$ and its characteristic speed obeys the <inviscid Burgers equation>. <Nonlinear Alfvén waves> are a linearly degenerate example.

= Perpendicular fast magnetosonic simple wave
{parent=Simple wave in magnetohydrodynamics}
{title2=$p=K\rho^\gamma,\ B=b\rho,\ du/d\rho=\pm c_f/\rho$}

For a one-dimensional <velocity> perpendicular to a transverse <magnetic field>, $c_f^2=\gamma p/\rho+B^2/(\mu_0\rho)$. The primitive-variable characteristic matrix has two advective <eigenvalues> and the two speeds $u\pm c_f$. Integrating the fast <eigenvectors> keeps $p/\rho^\gamma$ and $B/\rho$ constant. The state parameter obeys $\rho_t+(u\pm c_f)\rho_x=0$, so the <characteristic speed> satisfies the <inviscid Burgers equation>.

= Isothermal perpendicular magnetosonic simple wave
{parent=Perpendicular fast magnetosonic simple wave}
{title2=$B_y=K\rho,\ c_f^2=c_s^2+K^2\rho/\mu_0$}

With $B_x=0$, a fast <simple wave in magnetohydrodynamics> has constant $u_y$ and $B_y/\rho=K$. Its longitudinal velocity satisfies $du_x/d\rho=\pm c_f/\rho$, and $\rho_t+(u_x\pm c_f)\rho_x=0$. Along this state curve the speed derivative is $\pm[c_f/\rho+K^2/(2\mu_0c_f)]$, which is nonzero. Compressive profiles therefore exhibit <shock formation by characteristic intersection>, unlike a <linearly degenerate characteristic field>.

= Damping threshold for a simple wave
{parent=Simple wave}
{title2=$\alpha_c=b\max(-u_0')$}

For $u_t+(c_0+bu)u_x=-\alpha u$, $b,\alpha>0$, the <characteristic curves> are $u=e^{-\alpha t}u_0(\xi)$ and $x=c_0t+\xi+(b/\alpha)(1-e^{-\alpha t})u_0(\xi)$. If $m=\max[-u_0']>0$, finite-time <gradient blow-up> occurs precisely for $\alpha<bm$, at $t_s=-\alpha^{-1}\log(1-\alpha/(bm))$. Equality $\alpha=bm$ still gives a positive Jacobian at each finite time; only the limiting map degenerates.

= Receding-piston rarefaction wave
{parent=Simple wave}

For a piston receding from initially stationary homentropic gas, the right-moving <simple wave> has $R_-=0$, so $c=c_0+(\gamma-1)u/2$. A $C_+$ characteristic emitted by the piston at time $s$ carries $u=\dot X_p(s)$ and is the straight line
$$
x=X_p(s)+\left[c_0+\frac{\gamma+1}{2}\dot X_p(s)\right](t-s).
$$

= Vacuum formation behind a receding piston
{parent=Receding-piston rarefaction wave}

The gas state at a receding piston reaches vacuum when $\dot X_p=-2c_0/(\gamma-1)$, because then the <sound speed> and <mass density> both vanish. If the piston subsequently recedes faster, a vacuum gap opens between it and the gas boundary, which continues with velocity $-2c_0/(\gamma-1)$.

= Singular small-power piston emission time
{parent=Receding-piston rarefaction wave}

For $X_p(t)=-t^{1+\alpha}/(1+\alpha)$ with $0<\alpha\ll1$, a point on the ray $x=(c_0-a)t$, $0<a<(\gamma+1)/2$, receives the characteristic emitted at the exponentially small time
$$
s\sim\left(\frac{2a}{\gamma+1}\right)^{1/\alpha}.
$$
Although $s\to0$, the piston speed $-s^\alpha$ has the finite limit $-2a/(\gamma+1)$, producing the centered-rarefaction profile of the singular limit $\alpha=0$.

= Pressure in a perfect-gas simple wave
{parent=Riemann invariant}

In a right-moving simple wave entering gas initially at rest with sound speed $c_0$, the other Riemann invariant is constant, so
$$
c=c_0+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}u.
$$
The isentropic relation
$$
\frac p{p_0}=\left(\frac c{c_0}\right)^{2\gamma/(\gamma-1)}
$$
converts the velocity disturbance into its nonlinear pressure disturbance.

= Shock formation by characteristic intersection
{parent=Riemann invariant}

A smooth compressive simple wave forms a shock when characteristics first intersect. If characteristics are parametrized by their emission time $\tau$, the first shock occurs at the minimum positive time for which $\partial x(\tau,t)/\partial\tau=0$.

= Gradient catastrophe
{parent=Shock formation by characteristic intersection}

A smooth solution undergoes a <gradient catastrophe> when a spatial <derivative> becomes unbounded while the solution itself remains bounded. For $u_t+q'(u)u_x=0$, <characteristics> have $x=s+tq'(u_0(s))$ and $u=u_0(s)$; thus $u_x=u_0'(s)/[1+tq''(u_0(s))u_0'(s)]$. Vanishing of the <characteristic> projection <derivative> gives the first such time. A <weak solution> with an <entropy condition> can continue after this breakdown of the smooth description.

= Normal shock wave
{parent=Compressible flow}

A normal shock is a discontinuity perpendicular to the flow direction across which mass, momentum, and total energy fluxes are conserved.

Property ratios for this discontinuity are tabulated in <normal shock tables>.

= Shock tube
{parent=Normal shock wave}

A tube in which releasing an initial <pressure> difference produces a shock, often accompanied by an expansion wave and a contact surface. Spatially separated <pressure> probes can measure shock arrival speed and jump size. The shock and the contact surface must be identified separately when interpreting the downstream state.

= Normal shock reflection at a rigid wall
{parent=Normal shock wave}
{title2=$\frac{p_1}{p_s}=\frac{3\gamma-1-(\gamma-1)p_0/p_s}{\gamma-1+(\gamma+1)p_0/p_s}$}

For a perfect-gas <normal shock wave> reflected from a stationary rigid wall, apply the <Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas> using signed <velocity> in each <shock frame>. The once-shocked state is the common left state for both applications. Its two relative speeds are roots of $v^2-(\gamma+1)u_sv/2-\gamma p_s/\rho_s=0$. Their product and the <pressure> jump eliminate the <velocity>, giving the displayed relation. A strong incident <shock wave> gives $p_1/p_s\to(3\gamma-1)/(\gamma-1)$.

= Moving-interface conservation jump identity
{parent=Normal shock wave}
{title2=$\partial_ta+\nabla\cdot b=([b]\cdot n-v_n[a])\delta_s$}

For a moving level surface, $n=\nabla S/|\nabla S|$, $v_n=-S_t/|\nabla S|$, and the <surface delta distribution> is $\delta_s=|\nabla S|\delta(S)$. Piecewise classical conservation laws acquire a distributional flux defect $[b]\cdot n-v_n[a]$. Vanishing of this defect is the <Rankine-Hugoniot condition>.

= Normal shock tables
{parent=Normal shock wave}
{wiki}

Normal shock tables tabulate upstream-to-downstream property ratios for a <normal shock wave>, typically as functions of upstream <Mach number> and <heat capacity ratio>. They encode the <Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas> rather than defining the discontinuity itself.

= Shock frame
{parent=Normal shock wave}

The shock frame is an inertial reference frame in which the shock is stationary. A travelling shock then becomes a steady flow through a fixed discontinuity.

= Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas
{parent=Normal shock wave}
{c}

= Perfect-gas shock jump conditions
{synonym}

In the rest frame of a one-dimensional shock, states with velocities $w_0,w_1$ satisfy
$$
\rho_0w_0=\rho_1w_1,
$$
$$
p_0+\rho_0w_0^2=p_1+\rho_1w_1^2,
$$
and
$$
\frac{\gamma p_0}{(\gamma-1)\rho_0}+\frac12w_0^2
=\frac{\gamma p_1}{(\gamma-1)\rho_1}+\frac12w_1^2.
$$

These are the <Rankine-Hugoniot conditions> specialized to the mass, momentum and energy fluxes of a <perfect gas>.

= Prandtl shock relation
{title2=$u_1u_2=c_{\rm cr}^2$}
{c}
{parent=Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas}

For a stationary normal shock in a polytropic perfect gas, the upstream and downstream normal speeds obey $u_1u_2=c_{\rm cr}^2$, where $c_{\rm cr}$ is the sonic speed on their common Bernoulli streamline.

= Strong-shock Rankine-Hugoniot conditions
{c}
{parent=Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas}

When the upstream pressure is negligible, a perfect-gas shock has density ratio $(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$. For $\gamma=5/3$, the downstream density is four times the upstream density.

= Strong-shock accretion temperature
{parent=Strong-shock Rankine-Hugoniot conditions}
{title2=$k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm d}=3\mu v_{\mathrm u}^2/16$}

A strong, nonradiative <shock> in a monatomic ideal gas gives $p_{\mathrm d}=3\rho_{\mathrm u}v_{\mathrm u}^2/4$ and $\rho_{\mathrm d}=4\rho_{\mathrm u}$. The <ideal gas law> therefore gives the <temperature> in the title, with $\mu$ the mean particle mass. If the upstream speed is a Newtonian free-fall speed, $k_{\mathrm B}T_{\mathrm d}=3\mu GM/(8R)$. Mass alone does not determine this <temperature> without a radius and composition; electron-ion disequilibrium and radiation can also make the emergent spectrum differ from the ideal gas <temperature>.

= Energy fluxes of a strong adiabatic shock
{parent=Strong-shock Rankine-Hugoniot conditions}
{title2=$F_{\rm int}=2\rho_1u_1^3/(\gamma+1)^2,\quad\Delta F_{\rm kin}=2\gamma\rho_1u_1^3/(\gamma+1)^2$}

In the rest frame of a strong planar <shock wave>, a perfect gas has $\rho_2/\rho_1=(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$, $u_2/u_1=(\gamma-1)/(\gamma+1)$ and $p_2=2\rho_1u_1^2/(\gamma+1)$. Its downstream <specific internal energy> is $\epsilon_2=p_2/[(\gamma-1)\rho_2]=2u_1^2/(\gamma+1)^2$. Multiplying by mass flux $j=\rho_1u_1$ gives the internal-energy flux in the title. The lost bulk <kinetic energy> flux instead equals $j(u_1^2-u_2^2)/2=\gamma F_{\rm int}$: this includes downstream pressure work and is the increase of <specific enthalpy> flux in the cold-upstream limit. Neither quantity is a temperature change per unit time of a fixed target mass without further information about that target.

= Shock compression ratio
{title2=$\chi$}
{parent=Strong-shock Rankine-Hugoniot conditions}

The shock compression ratio is the downstream-to-upstream density ratio. For a strong perfect-gas shock,
$$
\chi=\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_0}=\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}.
$$
In the upstream rest frame, the downstream velocity is $2V_s/(\gamma+1)$ and its pressure is $2\rho_0V_s^2/(\gamma+1)$.

= Planar shock compression of a finite slab
{parent=Shock compression ratio}
{title2=$L_2=L/\chi,\quad v_2=V_s(1-\chi^{-1})$}

A plane shock moving at speed $V_s$ through initially stationary gas has upstream speed $u_1=V_s$ and downstream speed $u_2=V_s/\chi$ in the <shock frame>, where $\chi$ is the <shock compression ratio>. Transforming back to the initial slab frame gives $v_2=V_s-u_2$. At the breakout time $L/V_s$, a uniform downstream slab has thickness $L-v_2L/V_s=L/\chi$. The strong monatomic limit has $\chi=4$, hence $L_2=L/4$ and $v_2=3V_s/4$. This uses a maintained planar post-shock state; boundary rarefactions and variations of shock speed can change the actual thickness of a freely evolving finite cloud.

= Mach-number convention in shock unbinding estimates
{c}
{parent=Planar shock compression of a finite slab}
{title2=$\mathcal M_{\rm ad}^2\gtrsim(16/5)W/E_{\rm th},\quad \mathcal M_T^2\gtrsim(16/3)W/E_{\rm th}$}

For a strong monatomic shock, the <planar shock compression of a finite slab> gives bulk speed $3V_s/4$ and kinetic energy $E_{\rm bulk}=9M_cV_s^2/32$. The initial <internal energy> is $E_{\rm th}=3M_cp/(2\rho)$. A bulk-ballistic escape estimate $E_{\rm bulk}\gtrsim W$ uses positive binding-energy magnitude $W$, not negative gravitational potential energy. With <adiabatic sound speed> $c_{\rm ad}^2=5p/(3\rho)$ it yields the first displayed coefficient. Using the <isothermal sound speed> $c_T^2=p/\rho$ instead yields $16/3\simeq5.3$. The definitions obey $\mathcal M_T^2=(5/3)\mathcal M_{\rm ad}^2$. Heating, subsequent expansion and energy loss can alter the physical escape threshold, so the coefficient is not independent of the energy budget and Mach convention.

= Piston-driven normal shock
{parent=Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas}

If a piston moves at speed $V$ into stationary perfect gas and drives a shock at speed $U$, then the shock-frame upstream and downstream speeds are $U$ and $U-V$. If $p_2-p_1=\beta p_1$, the jump conditions give
$$
V^2=
\frac{2\beta^2}{2\gamma+(\gamma+1)\beta}
\frac{p_1}{\rho_1},
\qquad
\frac{U^2}{c_1^2}
=1+\frac{\gamma+1}{2\gamma}\beta.
$$

= Piston-driven shock with specific-heat ratio three
{parent=Piston-driven normal shock}

If a shock travels into stationary gas at speed $V$ while the downstream piston and gas travel at $V/3$, then for $\gamma=3$,
$$
\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_0}=\frac32,
\qquad
\frac{p_1}{p_0}=4,
\qquad
V=3\sqrt{\frac{p_0}{\rho_0}}.
$$

= Pressure-density Hugoniot relation for a perfect gas
{parent=Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas}

Let $P=p_2/p_1$ and $D=\rho_2/\rho_1$ be the <pressure> and <mass density> ratios across a <normal shock wave> in a <perfect gas> with <specific-heat ratio> $\gamma$. Eliminating the velocities from the <Rankine-Hugoniot conditions for a perfect gas> gives
$$
\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}(p_2/\rho_2-p_1/\rho_1)
=\frac12(p_2-p_1)(1/\rho_1+1/\rho_2),
\qquad
D=\frac{(\gamma+1)P+\gamma-1}{(\gamma-1)P+\gamma+1}.
$$
As $P\to\infty$, the ratio tends to the <shock compression ratio> $(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$.

= Entropy production in a perfect-gas shock
{parent=Pressure-density Hugoniot relation for a perfect gas}

For the <pressure-density Hugoniot relation for a perfect gas>, the change in <specific entropy> is $[s]/c_v=\log P-\gamma\log D(P)$, with $c_v$ the <specific heat capacity> at constant volume. Its derivative is
$$
\frac{d([s]/c_v)}{dP}
=\frac{(\gamma^2-1)(P-1)^2}{P[(\gamma+1)P+\gamma-1][(\gamma-1)P+\gamma+1]}.
$$
It is positive for a compressive <normal shock wave> with $P>1$. For a <weak shock> with $P=1+\delta$, integrating the leading term gives
$$
\frac{[s]}{c_v}=\frac{\gamma^2-1}{12\gamma^2}\delta^3+O(\delta^4).
$$
Thus the <entropy production> is cubic in the small <pressure> jump, even though the <mass density> and <temperature> changes already appear at first order.

= Entropy production in successive weak shocks
{parent=Entropy production in a perfect-gas shock}

For $N$ equal <weak shocks> with individual <pressure> ratio $1+\delta$ and total ratio $P_s=(1+\delta)^N$, <entropy production in a perfect-gas shock> gives
$$
\frac{\Delta s}{c_v}
=\frac{\gamma^2-1}{12\gamma^2}\delta^2\log P_s\,[1+O(\delta)].
$$
At fixed $P_s>1$, this tends to zero as the compression is divided into increasingly many smaller <weak shocks>. A single finite <normal shock wave> instead gives the positive value $\log P_s-\gamma\log D(P_s)$. Gradual compression can consequently approach reversible <isentropic flow> while an abrupt compression generates finite <entropy>.

= Weak shock
{parent=Normal shock wave}

A weak shock has a small relative pressure jump. Its density jump agrees with the reversible adiabatic prediction through second order in the pressure jump, while entropy production first appears at third order.

= Oblique shock
{parent=Normal shock wave}
{wiki}

An oblique shock is inclined relative to the upstream velocity. In an inviscid perfect gas, the normal velocity satisfies the normal-shock jump conditions while the tangential velocity is continuous.

= Strong oblique-shock angle branches
{parent=Oblique shock}
{title2=$\beta_{\rm weak,strong}=[\theta+\arcsin(\gamma\sin\theta),\ \theta+\pi-\arcsin(\gamma\sin\theta)]/2$}

Measure shock angle $\beta$ from the incident flow to the shock front, and deflection $\theta$ towards it. The strong normal-compression ratio and continuity of tangential <velocity> give $\tan\beta=[(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)]\tan(\beta-\theta)$. Angle-addition identities reduce this to $\sin(2\beta-\theta)=\gamma\sin\theta$. The two title branches exist for $0<\theta<\arcsin(1/\gamma)$ and merge at $\beta=\pi/4+\theta/2$. As $\theta\to0$, their formal limits are $(\gamma+1)\theta/2$ and $\pi/2-(\gamma-1)\theta/2$. The weak-branch expansion requires $M\sin\beta\gg1$; at finite <Mach number> its true zero-deflection limit is the <Mach angle>.

= Shock polar
{parent=Oblique shock}
{wiki}

A <shock polar> is the locus of possible downstream <velocity> vectors, or an equivalent <pressure>/deflection relation, as the orientation of an <oblique shock> varies for fixed upstream conditions. <Entropy> admissibility selects physical compressive shocks. The strong-shock perfect-gas <velocity> polar is a circle, while finite-Mach polars have a different shape.

= Strong-shock polar for a perfect gas
{parent=Shock polar}
{title2=$u_Y^2=(U-u_X)(u_X-qU),\quad q=(\gamma-1)/(\gamma+1)$}

The normal <velocity> is reduced by $q$ in the strong-shock limit while tangential <velocity> is unchanged. If the upstream speed is $U$ and the shock normal makes angle $\beta$ with its direction, then $u_X=U[1-(1-q)\cos^2\beta]$ and $u_Y=\pm U(1-q)\cos\beta\sin\beta$. Eliminating the angle gives the displayed circle, centered at $\gamma U/(\gamma+1)$ with radius $U/(\gamma+1)$. It is the limiting <shock polar> in downstream <velocity> space.

= Maximum deflection through a strong perfect-gas shock
{parent=Strong-shock polar for a perfect gas}
{title2=$\delta_{\max}=\arcsin(1/\gamma)$}

For the circle with center distance $a=\gamma U/(\gamma+1)$ and radius $R=U/(\gamma+1)$, the most deflected ray from the origin is tangent. The right triangle formed by the origin, center and tangency point gives $\sin\delta_{\max}=R/a=1/\gamma$. This is a velocity-vector deflection angle, not the angle of the shock normal.

= Weak-oblique-shock deflection
{parent=Oblique shock}

If $p_2/p_1=1+\epsilon$ with $0<\epsilon\ll1$ and the upstream Mach number is $M_1>1$, the shock angle is the Mach angle to leading order and the flow deflection is
$$
\theta\simeq\frac{\epsilon}{\gamma}
\frac{\sqrt{M_1^2-1}}{M_1^2}.
$$
The normal speed decreases, so the downstream flow turns toward the shock front.

= Mach angle
{title2=$\mu=\arcsin(M^{-1})$}
{c}
{parent=Oblique shock}
{wiki}

The <Mach angle> of a <supersonic flow> with <Mach number> $M>1$ is $\mu=\arcsin(M^{-1})$. Infinitesimal <pressure> disturbances lie on the corresponding <Mach cone>, and a weak <oblique shock> approaches this angle as its strength tends to zero.

The <Mach angle> is the half-angle between a <Mach cone> generator and its backward motion axis for a steady point source in a uniform three-dimensional medium with <isotropy> and constant <sound speed>. It obeys
$$
\sin\mu=\frac{c}{U}=\frac{1}{M},\qquad 0<\mu<\frac{\pi}{2},\quad M>1.
$$
The relation concerns weak <acoustic waves> in <linear acoustics>. It is not the angle of a finite <oblique shock> produced by finite turning of the <fluid flow>; that angle also depends on the deflection and the <gas> properties.<Mach Angle (NASA Glenn Research Center)>[Mach Angle, geometric construction and displayed sin(mu)=1/M and mu=asin(1/M) equations]{ref}

For a short proof, choose an old emission in the <moving-point acoustic wavefronts> construction and set $s=-\tau>0$. Its <circle> has center $(-Us,0)$ and radius $cs$. A <tangent line> from the present source at the origin meets the <circle> at a <right angle> to its radius. The resulting <right triangle> has hypotenuse $Us$ and opposite side $cs$, giving the displayed <sine> ratio. The emission age cancels: every <wavefront> has the same tangent directions, so a continuum of expanding <spheres> produces one simple <Mach cone>.

In an original example, stipulate uniform <gas> with <sound speed> $c=340\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and a source moving through it at $U=680\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Then $M=2$ and $\mu=\arcsin(1/2)=30^\circ$. An emission one second earlier is now a <sphere> centered $680\,\mathrm m$ behind the source with radius $340\,\mathrm m$. The selected <sound speed> defines this example; it is not a constant for air at every <temperature>. The <Mach angle> decreases as $M$ increases: its <derivative> is $-1/(M\sqrt{M^2-1})$ in <radians>, and its <limits> are $90^\circ$ as $M\downarrow1$ and $0$ as $M\to\infty$.

The <Mach cone> forms the <geometric envelope> of <acoustic wave> <wavefronts> from a source moving at a <supersonic> <Mach number> $M>1$. In the source <reference frame> the ray-<velocity> <sphere> is centered at $-Mc_0\mathbf e_x$ and has radius $c_0$. Its tangent rays make <angle> $\sin^{-1}(1/M)$ to the backward flight direction; all other emitted acoustic rays lie inside that <Mach cone>.

The <Mach cone> is the <geometric envelope> of the <spheres> in <moving-point acoustic wavefronts>. To obtain its axial boundary without a diagram, put $s=-\tau>0$ and impose the <geometric envelope> conditions $F=0$ and $\partial_sF=0$ on
$$
F(x,y,s)=(x+Us)^2+y^2-c^2s^2.
$$
The <partial derivative> condition gives $x=-(U^2-c^2)s/U$. Substituting into $F=0$ gives $y^2=c^2(U^2-c^2)s^2/U^2$, hence
$$
y=\pm\frac{-x}{\sqrt{M^2-1}},\quad x<0,
\qquad
\sqrt{y^2+z^2}=\frac{-x}{\sqrt{M^2-1}}quad\text{in three dimensions}.
$$
For $M=2$, the <Mach cone> section has slope magnitude $1/\sqrt3$, consistent with a <Mach angle> of $30^\circ$. If emission begins at a finite <time>, the reached region is limited by the oldest <wavefront>; the infinite cone describes the steady geometric boundary obtained with an indefinitely long source history. In a uniformly moving <gas>, <advection> shifts each emission's <sphere> by the gas <velocity> times its age; working with the source's <relative velocity> through the <gas> restores the same construction.
