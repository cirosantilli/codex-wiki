= Fermi liquid
{c}
{wiki}

Why can a strongly interacting liquid resemble a gas? Its low-energy excitations can be long-lived <Landau quasiparticles> near a <Fermi surface>. Normal liquid <helium-3> motivates this <Fermi liquid> description: interactions survive in effective excitation <energies> and interactions.<The Theory of a Fermi Liquid by Lev Davidovich Landau>[p.920 and section 1]{ref}

Interacting one-dimensional <fermion> systems often form <Luttinger liquids>; <non-Fermi liquids> also occur in higher dimensions. A <Cooper instability> can produce a paired phase.<Renormalization Group Approach to Interacting Fermions by Ramamurti Shankar>[Sections IV E, VI D and XI]{ref}

The <Fermi-surface renormalization-group scaling> below organizes the low-energy interactions; <zero sound> tests whether their collective dynamics survives even when collisions are rare.

= Fermi surface
{c}
{parent=Fermi liquid}
{wiki}

A Fermi surface is the codimension-one surface in momentum space separating occupied and unoccupied zero-temperature states of a fermionic system.

= Free-electron Fermi circle in a square lattice
{parent=Fermi surface}
{title2=$k_Fa=\sqrt{2\pi z}$}

For a two-dimensional <free electron gas> with two spin states and $z$ <electrons> per square cell of side $a$, counting occupied <wavevectors> gives $z/a^2=2\pi k_F^2/(2\pi)^2$. The square <first Brillouin zone> has half-width $\pi/a$. A monovalent circle lies inside its faces; a divalent circle crosses its faces but not its corners. A real <periodic potential> can deform and reconnect the crossings, as in <Fermi-surface reconstruction in a weak square potential>; valence alone is insufficient to prove insulation.

= Fermi-surface reconstruction in a weak square potential
{parent=Fermi surface}

For a free circle of radius $R$ and square reciprocal period $Q$ with $\sqrt2R<Q<2R$, the circle cuts the faces but not the corners of the first zone. Weak Bragg gaps reconnect the folded branches into two inequivalent edge-centred <electron pockets> and one corner-centred <hole pocket>. The zero-potential limit retains band crossings instead of avoided crossings.

= Circular-cap carrier count in a square Brillouin zone
{parent=Fermi-surface reconstruction in a weak square potential}

For $s=Q/(2R)$, each face cap has area $C=R^2[\arccos s-s\sqrt{1-s^2}]$. The total second-band electron area is $4C$; the first-band <hole> area is $Q^2-\pi R^2+4C$. Multiplying either area by the <spin degeneracy> two and dividing by $(2\pi)^2$ gives the carrier densities. This is an infinitesimal-potential count, not an exact finite-gap formula.

= Hole pocket
{parent=Fermi surface}

A <hole pocket> surrounds unoccupied states near a band maximum. Its <hole> <carrier density> counts missing states in the nearly filled band, not all occupied electrons. In two dimensions the count is $gA_h/(2\pi)^2$. Four corner pieces of a square-zone plot can be one pocket on the periodically identified zone.

= Electron pocket
{parent=Fermi surface}

An <electron pocket> surrounds occupied states near a conduction-band minimum. In two dimensions its <carrier density> is $gA/(2\pi)^2$, where $A$ is its total reciprocal-space area and $g$ the unresolved degeneracy. Portions drawn at opposite zone faces can belong to the same pocket after periodic identification.

= Fermi sphere
{c}
{parent=Fermi surface}
{title2=$|\mathbf k|\le k_F$}

For an isotropic three-dimensional <free electron gas> at zero temperature, occupied wavevectors fill a sphere of radius $k_F$. Including two spin states, $n=k_F^3/(3\pi^2)$. The volume in reciprocal space counts occupied states; folding an extended-zone sphere into a first <Brillouin zone> does not remove states outside that first zone.

= Fermi point
{c}
{parent=Fermi surface}
{title2=$\pm k_F$}

A <Fermi point> is a point of the zero-temperature <Fermi surface> in one-dimensional momentum space. For a spin-degenerate band with one occupied interval, its two boundaries are usually $-k_F$ and $+k_F$. Low-energy excitations near them form left- and right-moving branches. A perturbation at wavevector $2k_F$ can mix those branches, as in a <Peierls distortion>.

= Fermi contour
{parent=Fermi surface}
{c}
{title2=$E_n(\mathbf k)=E_F$}

For a two-dimensional <energy band>, a Fermi contour is the set of <wavevectors> at the <Fermi energy>. It is the two-dimensional counterpart of a <Fermi surface>. At a regular value it consists locally of curves; at a band extremum or a crossing it can acquire isolated points or intersecting branches. At zero <temperature>, each band's occupied region satisfies $E_n(\mathbf k)<E_F$, so the contours must be labelled by band when determining occupations. The <Fermi contours of an isotropic Rashba band> illustrate why two contours need not belong to two different bands.

= Fermi circle
{parent=Fermi contour}
{title2=$k_x^2+k_y^2=k_F^2$}

The circular zero-temperature occupation boundary of an isotropic two-dimensional <electron> gas. The occupied disk has area $\pi k_F^2$; converting its area to particle number requires the reciprocal-space state density and a separately specified <spin degeneracy>.

= Particle-conserving displacement of a Fermi circle
{parent=Fermi circle}
{title2=$\nu(\theta)=p_0\cos\theta+\sqrt{p_F^2-p_0^2\sin^2\theta}-p_F$}

Translating a <Fermi circle> of radius $p_F$ by $p_0\hat{\mathbf x}$ preserves its area. For $p_0<p_F$, its radial displacement from the original centre is the displayed expression. Its <Taylor expansion> is $\nu=p_0\cos\theta-p_0^2\sin^2\theta/(2p_F)+O(p_0^4/p_F^3)$. The second-order term cancels the apparent area increase from the first-order shape: $\int_0^{2\pi}(p_F\nu+\nu^2/2)\,d\theta=0$. This distinction matters when converting a grand-potential variation into a fixed-<particle number> <energy> change.

= Fermi-surface renormalization-group scaling
{c}
{parent=Fermi liquid}

Fermi-surface renormalization-group scaling sends frequency and normal displacement from the Fermi surface to zero while leaving tangential momentum fixed. Generic interactions are irrelevant, while forward-scattering and opposite-momentum Cooper channels can remain marginal.

= BCS theory
{c}
{parent=Fermi liquid}
{wiki}

BCS theory describes a superconducting instability caused by attractive interactions between opposite-momentum fermions near a <Fermi surface>.

<John Bardeen>, <Leon Neil Cooper> and <John Robert Schrieffer> developed <BCS theory> in 1957, using attraction mediated by <phonons>.<Theory of Superconductivity by John Bardeen and others>[pp.1175–1177, introduction and microscopic pairing mechanism]{ref}

Weak-coupling <Cooper pairs> overlap. The <Cooper instability> reorganizes an unpaired <Fermi surface>; it is not <Bose-Einstein condensation> of compact, pre-existing <molecules>. The original paper explicitly distinguishes these pictures.<Theory of Superconductivity by John Bardeen and others>[p.1177, footnote 18 and coherence-distance discussion]{ref}

Two complementary clues made the microscopic problem concrete. The <superconducting isotope effect> connected the transition to <phonons>.<Electron-Phonon Interactions and Superconductivity by John Bardeen>[p.55, isotope effect as a clue to the microscopic mechanism]{ref}

A 1957 transmission experiment used thin <lead> and <tin> films as samples in far-<infrared> and millimeter-<microwave> measurements. The measured transmission was converted to complex <electrical conductivity>, normalized to the normal-state value. Comparing film thickness, resistance, annealing and the two metals tested the response's reproducibility. Weak low-<photon>-<energy> absorption followed by an onset supported a <superconducting energy gap>; its extraction required a thin-film electrodynamic model.<Conductivity of Superconducting Films for Photon Energies between 0.3 and 40kTc by R. E. Glover III and M. Tinkham>[Abstract: sample metals, transmission method, normal-state normalization and comparisons; inferred excitation gap]{ref}

The later <Giaever superconducting tunnelling experiment> provided another way to resolve the <superconducting energy gap> through <quantum tunnelling>.<Electron-Phonon Interactions and Superconductivity by John Bardeen>[p.61, section 4, tunnelling conductance and the superconducting density of states]{ref} The derivations below explain what the model predicts, including the distinction between a single <quasiparticle>'s minimum <energy> and the threshold for making two of them.

= Eliashberg theory
{parent=BCS theory}
{c}

Eliashberg theory retains the frequency dependence of the electron–phonon interaction and the normal and anomalous electronic self-energies in a strong-coupling treatment of conventional <superconductivity>. The phonon spectral function $\alpha^2F(\omega)$ and a screened Coulomb pseudopotential enter the pairing equations. High phonon frequencies alone do not specify a transition temperature without the coupling strength and competing repulsion.

= Fermionic Bogoliubov quasiparticle
{parent=BCS theory}
{title2=$E_{\mathbf k}=\sqrt{\xi_{\mathbf k}^2+|\Delta|^2}$}

A fermionic excitation obtained by a canonical particle–hole <Bogoliubov transformation> of a paired mean-field Hamiltonian. The uniform spin-singlet problem has the displayed positive energy and weights $|u|^2=(1+\xi/E)/2$, $|v|^2=(1-\xi/E)/2$. It interpolates between particle-like and hole-like excitations across the Fermi surface. These single-particle excitations differ from collective phase and amplitude modes of the <order parameter>.

= BCS-BEC crossover
{c}
{parent=BCS theory}

The continuous evolution, in suitable attractive Fermi systems, from weakly paired overlapping <Cooper pairs> to tightly bound composite <bosons> undergoing <Bose-Einstein condensation>. Pair formation, phase coherence and the <chemical potential> must be determined consistently across the crossover. A weak-coupling gap-temperature formula with fixed <density of states> is not uniformly valid through the molecular limit.

= Asymmetric-cutoff BCS transition temperature
{parent=BCS theory}
{title2=$k_BT_c\simeq1.13\sqrt{\theta\sigma}\,e^{-1/\lambda}$}

For a constant effective <density of states> and attractive coupling $\lambda$, let the linearized <BCS theory> gap equation integrate $\tanh(\xi/(2k_BT_c))/(2\xi)$ over $-\sigma<\xi<\theta$, with positive cutoffs. Its even integrand makes the result the mean of two positive-cutoff logarithms. When $k_BT_c\ll\sigma,\theta$, $1/\lambda\simeq\frac12\ln(1.13\theta/(k_BT_c))+\frac12\ln(1.13\sigma/(k_BT_c))$, giving the displayed geometric mean of cutoffs.

If a band bottom truncates the lower side, $\sigma=\min(\mu,\theta)$ for positive <chemical potential> $\mu$. The weak-coupling limit at fixed $\mu/\theta>0$ must not be interchanged with an uncontrolled $\mu\to0$ extrapolation: the lower logarithm requires $\mu\gg k_BT_c$ when $\mu<\theta$.

= Density crossover of a fixed-coupling BCS cutoff model
{parent=Asymmetric-cutoff BCS transition temperature}
{title2=$n_\theta=(2m\theta/\hbar^2)^{3/2}/(3\pi^2)$}

Set the <chemical potential> equal to the degenerate three-dimensional spin-degenerate <Fermi energy>, proportional to $n^{2/3}$, and keep the effective coupling and upper cutoff fixed. The <asymmetric-cutoff BCS transition temperature> scales as $n^{1/3}$ below $n_\theta$ and saturates above it. At low density the predicted $k_BT_c/\mu$ instead grows as $n^{-1/3}$, eventually invalidating the degenerate and logarithmic approximations. This is a prescribed cutoff model, not a self-consistent treatment of the <BCS-BEC crossover>.

= BCS pairing-field effective action
{parent=BCS theory}
{c}
{title2=$S_{\rm eff}[\Delta]=\int|\Delta|^2/\lambda-\operatorname{Tr}\ln\mathcal G^{-1}[\Delta]$}

An attractive local pairing interaction can be decoupled with a complex <Hubbard–Stratonovich transformation>. Integrating the fermionic <Grassmann variables> gives an <effective action> for the pair field. Its uniform saddle is the <BCS gap equation>, and its slow-field expansion near the transition gives the <Ginzburg-Landau theory of superconductivity>. A <trace> over a fully doubled particle-hole basis requires the corresponding factor of one half; the displayed formula uses one independent pairing block.

= Subtracted grand potential of the reduced BCS model
{parent=BCS pairing-field effective action}
{title2=$\delta\Omega=V|\Delta|^2/\lambda-\beta^{-1}\sum_{kn}\ln[(\omega_n^2+E_k^2)/(\omega_n^2+\xi_k^2)]$}

Subtracting the normal-state <grand potential> fixes the reference constant in the uniform <BCS pairing-field effective action>. Here $\omega_n=(2n+1)\pi/\beta$ has energy units and $E_k^2=\xi_k^2+|\Delta|^2$. The ratio cancels a field-independent determinant phase and produces a convergent high-frequency sum under the same momentum regulator. Differentiation gives the <BCS gap equation>. For a fixed attractive pairing shell, this is a finite regulated model; an unrestricted continuum contact interaction additionally needs ultraviolet renormalization. Its weak-coupling condensation density $-\nu_F\Delta^2/2$ uses a single-spin <density of states> per volume.

= Fermionic pairing-block determinant normalization
{parent=BCS pairing-field effective action}

A two-component <Nambu spinor> $(\psi_{k\uparrow},\bar\psi_{-k\downarrow})$ for every k accounts for both spin species once after relabelling. Its <Grassmann integral> contributes one <functional determinant>. A fully doubled spin-particle-hole basis instead has redundancy and requires one half in the <trace> logarithm or a corresponding Pfaffian convention. A reliable check is the zero-gap limit: the <grand potential> must become $-2\beta^{-1}\sum_k\ln(1+e^{-\beta\xi_k})$, with exactly two spin states and no invented vacuum offset.

= Weak-coupling BCS thermodynamic ratios
{parent=BCS theory}
{title2=$2\Delta(0)/(k_BT_c)=2\pi e^{-\gamma_E},\quad\Delta C/(\gamma_nT_c)=12/[7\zeta(3)]$}

The isotropic weak-coupling <BCS theory> predicts the gap ratio approximately $3.528$ and the electronic heat-capacity jump approximately $1.426$ in units of the normal-state <heat capacity> at the transition. These compare distinct measurements of the same paired state. They are conditional predictions of the weak-coupling fully gapped model, rather than universal numbers for all <superconductors>.

= Superconducting gap
{parent=BCS theory}

The gap feature in a superconducting single-particle excitation spectrum. In a simple BCS model, excitations have $E_{\mathbf k}=\sqrt{\xi_{\mathbf k}^2+|\Delta(\mathbf k)|^2}$. The <superconducting gap function> specifies the pairing amplitude and its phase; its magnitude need not be constant, and nodes can leave gapless excitations.

= Superconducting gap function
{parent=Superconducting gap}
{title2=$\Delta(\mathbf k)$}

A complex momentum-dependent pairing amplitude. Its crystal-symmetry transformation, nodes and relative signs distinguish candidate superconducting states. Magnitude-sensitive spectra alone generally do not establish all relative signs; <phase-sensitive superconducting gap experiments> test them through interference.

= Phase-sensitive superconducting gap experiment
{parent=Superconducting gap function}

An experiment converting the relative phase of a superconducting pairing amplitude into a measurable interference or flux signal. Orthogonal-face Josephson junctions and tricrystal rings are examples. Junction asymmetry, trapped flux, loop inductance and comparison geometries are important controls.

= Phase-sensitive test of d-wave pairing
{parent=Phase-sensitive superconducting gap experiment}

Compare superconducting phase information from different crystal directions rather than only measuring the gap magnitude. Orthogonal-face junctions and a <tricrystal half-flux experiment> can test sign changes of a candidate d-wave gap. Junction properties, trapped flux and loop inductance must be controlled; a measured half-flux signal needs its actual device model.

= Tricrystal half-flux experiment
{parent=Phase-sensitive superconducting gap experiment}

A ring crossing three appropriately oriented grain-boundary junctions tests a superconducting pairing sign pattern. An odd total intrinsic $\pi$ offset frustrates integer phase matching and can favor a half-flux state. Actual enclosed flux also depends on loop inductance and junction currents; the measurement requires controls against ordinary trapped flux.<Pairing symmetry and flux quantization in a tricrystal superconducting ring by C. C. Tsuei and others>[1994 primary tricrystal-ring pairing-symmetry experiment]{ref}

= Cooper pair
{parent=BCS theory}
{c}
{wiki}

A Cooper pair is a correlated pair of <fermions> associated with <superconductivity>. The conventional <BCS ground state> uses opposite-momentum, opposite-spin pair creation operators $c_{k\uparrow}^\dagger c_{-k\downarrow}^\dagger$. Their total momentum is zero for every relative momentum $k$; zero total momentum does not restrict $k$ to zero.

= Cooper problem
{parent=Cooper pair}
{c}

Two added <electrons> move above a frozen filled <Fermi sea> and attract within a thin <energy> shell. The Pauli-blocked continuum has a logarithmic pair susceptibility, so an arbitrarily weak net attractive constant interaction produces a state below the two-particle threshold at zero <temperature>. This two-added-particle result is not by itself a full many-electron superconducting state.

= Cooper pair binding energy
{parent=Cooper problem}
{c}
{title2=$B=-E$}

For shell width $D$, attraction $g>0$ and constant single-spin <density of states> $\rho$, $1=(g\rho/2)\log[(2D+B)/B]$ gives $B=2D/[\exp(2/(g\rho))-1]$. The pair <energy> relative to the free threshold is $-B$. At weak coupling this scale is nonanalytic in $g$.

= Cooper shell projection
{parent=Cooper problem}
{c}

The pairing Hamiltonian is projected onto empty states in a specified shell above the inert <Fermi sea>. A state restricted to that shell is an eigenstate of this projected model, not necessarily of a contact interaction that remains unrestricted at all momenta.

= Pairing gap
{parent=Cooper pair}
{title2=$\Delta$}

A <pairing gap> is an <energy> scale for fermionic excitations above a paired <superfluid> or <superconductor> <ground state>. In a simple isotropic paired system the <quasiparticle> dispersion has the form $E_{\mathbf k}=\sqrt{\xi_{\mathbf k}^2+|\Delta|^2}$, where $\xi_{\mathbf k}$ measures the unpaired single-particle <energy> relative to the <chemical potential>. A nonzero minimum gap gives exponentially small fermionic thermal occupations when $k_BT\ll|\Delta|$. If the gap varies with direction and has nodes, gapless directions can instead produce power-law thermodynamics. Collective excitations must be counted separately from the gapped fermionic <quasiparticles>.

= BCS ground state
{parent=BCS theory}
{c}
{title2=$\prod_k(u_k+v_kc_{k\uparrow}^\dagger c_{-k\downarrow}^\dagger)|0\rangle$}

The BCS ground state is the normalized product of empty and occupied pair states $\prod_k(u_k+v_kb_k^\dagger)|0\rangle$. For $u_k^2+v_k^2=1$, each pair factor is normalized. The inverse <Bogoliubov transformation> annihilates it, so it is the vacuum of positive-energy <quasiparticles>. Although individual factors use opposite momenta, $k$ runs over the relative pair momenta; it is not restricted to $k=0$.

= Cooper-pair pseudospin coherent state
{parent=BCS ground state}
{c}

The empty and doubly occupied states of a paired <momentum> block form a two-level space. Define $S_k^+=a_{k\uparrow}^\dagger a_{-k\downarrow}^\dagger$, $S_k^-=a_{-k\downarrow}a_{k\uparrow}$ and $S_k^z=(n_{k\uparrow}+n_{-k\downarrow}-1)/2$. They obey the spin algebra on that space. The normalized factor $u_k|0\rangle+v_k|\mathrm{pair}\rangle$ is a spin-one-half coherent state; the product over blocks is the <BCS ground state>. Pauli exclusion prevents interpreting a block as an unrestricted harmonic-oscillator pair mode.

= BCS coherence factors
{parent=BCS theory}
{c}
{title2=$u_k^2+v_k^2=1$}

For real gap and the particle-hole block $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\xi_k&-\Delta_k\\-\Delta_k&-\xi_k\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, the BCS coherence factors obey $u_k^2=(1+\xi_k/E_k)/2$, $v_k^2=(1-\xi_k/E_k)/2$ and $u_kv_k=\Delta_k/(2E_k)$, where $E_k=\sqrt{\xi_k^2+\Delta_k^2}$. They diagonalize the <Bogoliubov--de Gennes Hamiltonian> and give the empty/pair amplitudes of the <BCS ground state>. Their relative sign depends on the pairing convention.

For an original check, write $x=\xi_k/\Delta$ with constant real $\Delta>0$. At $x=0$, the <BCS coherence factors> give $u_k^2=v_k^2=1/2$. At $x=1$, they give $(u_k^2,v_k^2)=(0.853553,0.146447)$; at $x=-1$ these values exchange places. Thus the zero-<temperature> occupation $v_k^2$ rounds the normal <Fermi sea>'s step over an <energy> interval of order $\Delta$. This mixing is strongest at the <Fermi surface>, where the <BCS quasiparticle spectrum> has its minimum.

= BCS population above the normal-state Fermi surface
{parent=BCS coherence factors}
{c}
{title2=$\Delta N/N\simeq3\Delta/(4\mu)$}

For a uniform isotropic weak-gap BCS state, the zero-temperature <electron> occupation is $n(\xi)=(1-\xi/\sqrt{\xi^2+\Delta^2})/2$. The number above the normal-state <Fermi surface> is approximately $D(\mu)\int_0^\infty n(\xi)d\xi$. The integral equals $\Delta/2$, while a three-dimensional parabolic band including both spins has $D(\mu)=3N/(2\mu)$. This gives the displayed leading fraction.

The result measures redistribution relative to a normal <Fermi sea>, not bosonic single-mode condensate depletion or <quasiparticle> occupation. A different band dimension or anisotropic gap requires its actual density-of-states or <momentum> integral. Material estimates need specified gap and chemical-potential scales.

= Hebel-Slichter coherence peak
{c}
{parent=BCS coherence factors}

In a conventional isotropic spin-singlet <superconductor>, the nuclear spin-lattice relaxation rate can rise just below the transition before falling at lower <temperature>. The <BCS coherence factors> and enhanced near-gap <density of states> produce the coherence enhancement. Nuclear relaxation and ultrasonic attenuation have different coherence factors, so their contrasting <temperature> dependences test pairing structure rather than only the existence of a gap.<Nuclear Spin Relaxation in Normal and Superconducting Aluminum by L. C. Hebel and Charles Pence Slichter>[Abstract, physical system and comparison with <BCS theory>]{ref}

= BCS anomalous average
{parent=BCS theory}
{c}
{title2=$\langle c_{-k\downarrow}c_{k\uparrow}\rangle$}

The BCS anomalous average is the pair expectation $\langle b_k\rangle=\langle c_{-k\downarrow}c_{k\uparrow}\rangle$. In the <BCS ground state> it is $u_kv_k=\Delta_k/(2E_k)$ for real gap at zero temperature. At temperature $T$ it becomes $\Delta_k\tanh(E_k/(2k_BT))/(2E_k)$. Substitution into the pairing-field definition gives the <BCS gap equation>.

= Reduced BCS pairing Hamiltonian
{parent=BCS theory}

The <reduced BCS pairing Hamiltonian> retains scattering of opposite-momentum, opposite-spin <Cooper pairs>:
$$
H=\sum_{k,\sigma}\epsilon_kc_{k\sigma}^\dagger c_{k\sigma}+\sum_{k,k'}V_{k,k'}b_k^\dagger b_{k'},\qquad b_k^\dagger=c_{k\uparrow}^\dagger c_{-k\downarrow}^\dagger.
$$
A <mean-field approximation> introduces the <BCS anomalous average> and pairing field $\Delta_k=-\sum_{k'}V_{k,k'}\langle b_{k'}\rangle$. The subtraction $-\sum V_{k,k'}\langle b_k^\dagger\rangle\langle b_{k'}\rangle$ corrects the double counting of the mean-field interaction energy.

= Cooper instability
{c}
{parent=BCS theory}
{wiki}

The Cooper instability is the logarithmic growth of an attractive BCS coupling at low energy. If $\nu_F$ is the density of states and $V<0$, perturbation theory breaks down at a scale proportional to $\Lambda e^{-1/(\nu_F|V|)}$.

= BCS gap equation
{c}
{parent=BCS theory}
{wiki}

At zero temperature and weak coupling, the BCS gap equation gives $\Delta\simeq2\Lambda e^{-1/(\nu_F|V|)}$. Its exponential scale agrees with the renormalization-group scale of the <Cooper instability>.

= Matsubara derivation of the BCS saddle
{parent=BCS gap equation}
{c}
{title2=$\beta^{-1}\sum_n(\omega_n^2+E^2)^{-1}=\tanh(\beta E/2)/(2E)$}

Differentiate the <subtracted grand potential of the reduced BCS model> with respect to the squared pairing amplitude. The displayed fermionic <Matsubara sum> converts the derivative of the trace logarithm into $\sum_k\tanh(\beta E_k/2)/(2E_k)$. Setting it equal to $V/\lambda$ gives the uniform nonzero-gap saddle. The limit E→0 is finite at positive temperature, $\beta/4$. The sum relies on antiperiodic temporal boundary conditions from the <fermionic coherent-state trace>.

= Mass-anisotropy enhancement of a weak-coupling BCS gap
{parent=BCS gap equation}
{title2=$\Delta\simeq2\varepsilon_c e^{-1/(\lambda_0\gamma^{1/3})}$}

For a fixed-density balanced two-spin Fermi gas with masses $(m,m,\gamma m)$, the Fermi energy scales as $\gamma^{-1/3}$ while the single-spin Fermi-level <density of states> scales as $\gamma^{1/3}$. A constant attractive interaction $g$ within a narrow symmetric energy window $\varepsilon_c\ll\varepsilon_F$ therefore gives $1=\lambda_0\gamma^{1/3}\operatorname{arsinh}(\varepsilon_c/\Delta)$. Solving gives $\Delta=\varepsilon_c/\sinh[1/(\lambda_0\gamma^{1/3})]$, whose weak-coupling limit is displayed. Extrapolating to arbitrarily large anisotropy violates the assumptions that the Fermi energy dominates the window and that the pairing coupling is small.

= Constant-shell BCS gap equation
{parent=BCS gap equation}
{title2=$\Delta=\Omega_D/\sinh(1/(\nu_sV))$}

An attractive interaction $V_{k,k'}=-V/\mathcal V$ within $|\xi_k|,|\xi_{k'}|<\Omega_D$ gives a constant gap inside that energy shell and zero outside. With approximately constant <single-spin density of states> $\nu_s$ per volume,
$$
1=\nu_sV\int_0^{\Omega_D}\frac{d\xi}{\sqrt{\xi^2+\Delta^2}}=\nu_sV\operatorname{arsinh}(\Omega_D/\Delta),\qquad \Delta=\frac{\Omega_D}{\sinh(1/(\nu_sV))}.
$$
The <weak coupling> limit is $\Delta\simeq2\Omega_De^{-1/(\nu_sV)}$. A density counting both spin species is twice $\nu_s$ and must be divided by two. The shell cutoff is a relative-energy condition, even when every <Cooper pair> has zero total momentum.

= BCS dimensionless coupling
{c}
{parent=Constant-shell BCS gap equation}
{title2=$\lambda=N_0V$}

Use $N_0=\nu_s$ for the normal-state <single-spin density of states> per unit <energy> and <volume> at the <Fermi surface>. Write the attraction as $-V/\mathcal V$, with $V>0$, and define $\lambda=N_0V$.<Theory of Superconductivity by John Bardeen and others>[p.1182, equations (2.34)–(2.40), one-spin density of states; volume normalization stated here]{ref}

Since $V/\mathcal V$ has units of <energy>, $V$ has units of <energy> times <volume>, making $\lambda$ dimensionless. Set $\Omega_D=\hbar\omega_D$, an <energy> cutoff using the <Debye frequency> $\omega_D$, rather than a frequency itself. These conventions also fix the meaning of the earlier <constant-shell BCS gap equation>.

Here is the reduction of the <BCS gap equation> in these conventions. Substituting the zero-<temperature> <BCS anomalous average> gives
$$
\Delta=\frac{V}{\mathcal V}\sum_{|\xi_k|<\Omega_D}\frac{\Delta}{2\sqrt{\xi_k^2+\Delta^2}}.
$$
The $k$ sum counts one <spin> species. For $\Delta>0$, cancelling $\Delta$ and replacing this sum by an <integral> with constant $N_0$ gives
$$
1=\frac{N_0V}{2}\int_{-\Omega_D}^{\Omega_D}\frac{d\xi}{\sqrt{\xi^2+\Delta^2}}
=\lambda\int_0^{\Omega_D}\frac{d\xi}{\sqrt{\xi^2+\Delta^2}}.
$$
The factor two from the symmetric <integral> cancels the $1/2$ in the <BCS anomalous average>; it is not an extra <spin> degeneracy. Substituting $\xi=\Delta\sinh t$ evaluates the <integral> as $\operatorname{arsinh}(\Omega_D/\Delta)$. Solving gives
$$
\Delta(0)=\frac{\hbar\omega_D}{\sinh(1/\lambda)}
=\frac{2\hbar\omega_De^{-1/\lambda}}{1-e^{-2/\lambda}}
\simeq2\hbar\omega_D\exp\left[-\frac{1}{N_0V}\right],\qquad \lambda\ll1.
$$
The first equality is exact within the constant-<density of states>, sharp-cutoff <mean-field approximation>; the last is the <weak coupling> asymptote. Extending the first equality to large $\lambda$ does not make this simplified model an accurate description of a strongly coupled material.

For an original numerical example, choose $\hbar\omega_D=20\ \mathrm{meV}$ and $\lambda=0.25$. The exact <constant-shell BCS gap equation> gives $\Delta(0)=0.732871\ \mathrm{meV}$; the <weak coupling> formula gives $0.732626\ \mathrm{meV}$. Its fractional underestimate, measured against the exact result, is exactly $e^{-2/\lambda}=e^{-8}\simeq0.03355\%$. At $\lambda=1$ that error is $e^{-2}\simeq13.53\%$, so the two formulas are no longer interchangeable. No material parameters have been fitted here.

At nonzero <temperature>, substitute the finite-<temperature> <BCS anomalous average> instead. The same cancellation yields
$$
1=\lambda\int_0^{\Omega_D}\frac{\tanh\!\left(\sqrt{\xi^2+\Delta(T)^2}/(2k_BT)\right)}{\sqrt{\xi^2+\Delta(T)^2}}\,d\xi.
$$
At the <superconducting transition temperature> take the continuous $\Delta\to0$ limit. The integrand at $\xi=0$ then has the finite limiting value $1/(2k_BT_c)$, so no divergent endpoint needs to be inserted into a numerical quadrature. For $k_BT_c\ll\Omega_D$, the resulting <integral> gives $k_BT_c\simeq1.134\Omega_De^{-1/\lambda}$ and $2\Delta(0)/(k_BT_c)\simeq3.528$. These ratios concern the isotropic <weak coupling> model.<Microscopic Quantum Interference Effects in the Theory of Superconductivity by Leon Neil Cooper>[1972 lecture, thermodynamic properties of the ideal superconductor, pp.80–82]{ref}

= BCS quasiparticle spectrum
{c}
{parent=BCS theory}
{title2=$E_k=\sqrt{\xi_k^2+|\Delta|^2}$}

The <BCS quasiparticle spectrum> is the positive excitation <energy> obtained by diagonalizing the paired particle-hole block. Here the <weak coupling> shell model has $\Delta\ll\Omega_D$: the minimum lies inside the pairing shell, and states outside it cost more. To derive it for a uniform isotropic real $\Delta>0$, measure the normal-state <energy> from the <chemical potential>, $\xi_k=\epsilon_k-\mu$, and use the same sign convention as the <BCS coherence factors>:
$$
\mathcal H_k=\begin{pmatrix}\xi_k&-\Delta\\-\Delta&-\xi_k\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\det(\mathcal H_k-\varepsilon I)=\varepsilon^2-\xi_k^2-\Delta^2=0.
$$
Its <eigenvalues> are $\varepsilon_\pm=\pm E_k$, where $E_k=\sqrt{\xi_k^2+\Delta^2}$. The positive $E_k$ is the cost of creating a <quasiparticle> above the <BCS ground state>. The lower branch $-E_k$ is its particle-hole partner in this doubled matrix description, not an excitation that lowers the <ground state> <energy> without bound.

The location and value of the minimum follow directly:
$$
\frac{dE}{d\xi}=\frac{\xi}{\sqrt{\xi^2+\Delta^2}},\qquad
\frac{d^2E}{d\xi^2}=\frac{\Delta^2}{(\xi^2+\Delta^2)^{3/2}}>0.
$$
The unique minimum as a function of $\xi$ is $E(0)=\Delta$, at the normal-state <Fermi surface>. On an isotropic three-dimensional <Fermi surface> it occurs for every direction of $k$, rather than at a unique point of <momentum> space. Near that minimum,
$$
E(\xi)=\Delta+\frac{\xi^2}{2\Delta}+O(\xi^4/\Delta^3).
$$
Farther away, $E(\xi)\simeq|\xi|$ when $\Delta\ll|\xi|\ll\Omega_D$. For the illustrative $\lambda=0.25$ example, a state at $\xi=\Delta$ costs $\sqrt2\Delta\simeq1.03644\ \mathrm{meV}$ rather than the minimum $0.732871\ \mathrm{meV}$.

= BCS quasiparticle
{parent=BCS quasiparticle spectrum}
{c}

An elementary excitation of the paired mean-field state whose <electron> operator has mixed creation and annihilation components. Its positive excitation <energy> is $E_k=\sqrt{\xi_k^2+|\Delta_k|^2}$. At zero <temperature> the <BCS ground state> has zero occupation of these positive-energy modes, even though physical <electrons> have a smooth nontrivial <momentum> occupation.

= BCS electronic density of states
{c}
{parent=BCS quasiparticle spectrum}
{title2=$N_s(E)=g_F|E|/\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2}$}

For a particle-hole-symmetric normal <density of states> $g_F$ and isotropic <superconducting energy gap> $\Delta>0$, the electron spectral density is zero for $|E|<\Delta$ and has the displayed form outside the gap. The <BCS coherence factors> weight the two roots $\xi=\pm\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2}$, where $\xi$ is normal-state energy relative to the <Fermi energy>. At positive $E$, their electron weights $(1+\xi/E)/2$ sum to one. Multiplying their sum by the Jacobian $|d\xi/dE|=E/\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2}$ proves the formula. This electron spectral density differs from the unweighted count of all positive-energy <quasiparticles>, which receives both roots. Its gap-edge divergence is integrable and approaches the normal electronic density at large $|E|$.

= Superconducting energy gap
{parent=BCS quasiparticle spectrum}
{title2=$\Delta,2\Delta$}

In this fully gapped isotropic <BCS quasiparticle spectrum>, $\Delta$ is the minimum single-<quasiparticle> excitation <energy>. The interval between $-\Delta$ and $+\Delta$ has width $2\Delta$. A process that creates two <quasiparticles> from the <BCS ground state> has an energetic threshold $2\Delta$, subject also to its transition-selection rules.<Microscopic Quantum Interference Effects in the Theory of Superconductivity by Leon Neil Cooper>[1972 lecture, excitation spectrum and correlated background, pp.78–80]{ref}

For example, two independently created excitations with $\xi_1=\xi_2=0$ cost $E_1+E_2=2\Delta$. With the chosen cutoff example this is $1.46574\ \mathrm{meV}$, while adding one excitation costs at least $0.732871\ \mathrm{meV}$. This factor of two matters when comparing <photon> absorption with the <Giaever superconducting tunnelling experiment>. <Quantum tunnelling> from a normal metal can add one excitation to a sample exhibiting <superconductivity>; with two gapped electrodes, their excitation costs add. A minimum excitation cost is an energetic condition; the measured onset can additionally depend on <temperature> and on the available transitions.

= BCS coherence length
{c}
{parent=BCS theory}
{title2=$\xi_0=\hbar v_F/(\pi\Delta(0))$}

The clean isotropic weak-coupling <BCS coherence length> is conventionally $\xi_0=\hbar v_F/(\pi\Delta(0))$, with <Fermi velocity> $v_F$. It characterizes the spatial extent of pairing correlations.<Theory of Superconductivity by John Bardeen and others>[pp.1195–1196, equations (5.43) and (5.50)]{ref}

The scale follows from the same spectral calculation. Linearizing the normal <energy> near the <Fermi surface> gives $\xi_k\simeq\hbar v_F(k-k_F)$. The region $|\xi_k|\lesssim\Delta$ therefore has radial width $\delta k\sim\Delta/(\hbar v_F)$. <Fourier analysis> associates this width with a distance $\ell\sim1/\delta k\sim\hbar v_F/\Delta$. This argument establishes the scale, not the conventional factor $1/\pi$. A small <superconducting energy gap> consequently permits pairing correlations over many interparticle spacings.

= Superconducting isotope effect
{parent=BCS theory}
{title2=$\alpha=-d\log T_c/d\log M$}

The <superconducting isotope effect> is the dependence of the <superconducting transition temperature> on isotopic <mass> $M$ at fixed chemical composition. Its discovery in 1950 was a crucial clue that <phonons> participate in conventional <superconductivity>.<Electron-Phonon Interactions and Superconductivity by John Bardeen>[p.55, isotope-effect discovery and its significance for the electron-phonon mechanism]{ref}

The model prediction can be derived from two assumptions. For harmonic motion with a fixed restoring-force constant $K$, a <phonon> frequency scales as $\omega\propto\sqrt{K/M}$. If the <BCS dimensionless coupling> is independent of $M$, the <weak coupling> result $k_BT_c\simeq1.134\hbar\omega_De^{-1/\lambda}$ then gives $T_c\propto M^{-1/2}$ and $\alpha=1/2$. Changes in the effective interaction can change this exponent; it is not a universal law for every material exhibiting <superconductivity>. For a stipulated mass ratio $M_2/M_1=1.04$, the ideal model predicts $T_{c,2}/T_{c,1}=1/\sqrt{1.04}\simeq0.98058$. This is an original scaling example, not a pair of measured transition temperatures.

= Landau quasiparticle
{c}
{parent=Fermi liquid}
{title2=$\xi_p$}

A <Landau quasiparticle> is a <fermionic> excitation connected continuously to a <Fermi gas> particle when interactions preserve the phase. Near an isotropic <Fermi surface>, its energy relative to the <chemical potential> is $\xi_p=\epsilon_p-\mu\simeq v_F(p-p_F)$, with <Fermi momentum> $p_F$ and <Fermi velocity> $v_F$.<The Theory of a Fermi Liquid by Lev Davidovich Landau>[Section 1, pp.921–922]{ref}

The <Pauli exclusion principle> restricts decay. For a three-dimensional normal <Fermi liquid> with regular short-range interactions, a representative low-energy lifetime model is
$$
\frac{\hbar}{\tau_p}=A\frac{\xi_p^2+(\pi k_BT)^2}{E_*},\qquad A>0,
$$
Here $E_*$ is an <energy> scale and $A$ is dimensionless; sharpness requires $\hbar/\tau_p\ll|\xi_p|$ at zero <temperature>.<Renormalization Group Approach to Interacting Fermions by Ramamurti Shankar>[Section VIII, manuscript p.114, quadratic lifetime corrections]{ref}

For an original example, stipulate $A=1$ and $T=0$. At $|\xi_p|/E_*=0.02$, the decay energy is $0.0004E_*$, just $2\%$ of the excitation <energy>; halving $|\xi_p|$ reduces that ratio to $1\%$. Approaching the <Fermi surface> improves the particle description even though the microscopic interaction has not weakened. This quadratic illustration is three-dimensional; low-dimensional corrections require separate analysis.

= Low-energy Fermi-liquid quasiparticle lifetime
{parent=Landau quasiparticle}
{title2=$\tau^{-1}\propto(\epsilon-\mu)^2+(\pi k_BT)^2$}

The <Pauli exclusion principle> restricts final states available to a low-energy <Landau quasiparticle> and the particle–<hole> pair it excites. In a regular three-dimensional <Fermi liquid>, this phase-space restriction makes the decay rate quadratic in excitation <energy> or <temperature>. The excitation's width divided by its <energy> therefore vanishes at the <Fermi surface>, allowing an asymptotically sharp quasiparticle despite interactions.

= Particle out-scattering versus quasiparticle spectral width
{parent=Low-energy Fermi-liquid quasiparticle lifetime}

A particle loss rate can include the factor $1-f(\xi)$, whereas a spectral decay width also contains the complementary inverse collision process. Their sum has the regular quadratic low-energy scale $\xi^2+(\pi k_BT)^2$. Particle and hole decay must not be confused with an occupied-state loss probability.

= Landau quasiparticle energy functional
{c}
{parent=Landau quasiparticle}
{title2=$\delta(E-\mu N)$}

Changing one <Landau quasiparticle> occupation changes the energies of others. To second order around equilibrium,
$$
\delta(E-\mu N)=\sum_{p\sigma}\xi_p\delta n_{p\sigma}+\frac{1}{2\mathcal V}\sum_{p\sigma,p'\sigma'}f_{\sigma\sigma'}(p,p')\delta n_{p\sigma}\delta n_{p'\sigma'}.
$$
Here $\mathcal V$ is <volume>, occupations are per <momentum> and <spin> state, and $f$ has units of <energy> times <volume>.<On the Theory of the Fermi Liquid by Lev Davidovich Landau>[p.70, equation (1); integrate the energy shift to obtain the quadratic functional]{ref}

For a toy two-state change at fixed <volume>, take $\delta n_a=\delta n_b=\eta$, $f_{aa}=f_{bb}=0$, $f_{ab}=f_{ba}=g\mathcal V$, and unperturbed energies $\xi_a=\xi_b=e$. The expression gives $2e\eta+g\eta^2$. The factor $1/2$ counts the interaction of the same pair once; it does not halve each excitation's energy. This finite-state illustration only explains the bookkeeping of the <Landau quasiparticle energy functional>.

= Thermal Landau quasiparticle energy shift
{parent=Landau quasiparticle energy functional}

For a symmetric interaction kernel, $\delta E/\delta n_a=\epsilon_a+V^{-1}\sum_bf_{ab}\delta n_b$. Smooth Fermi-surface kernels and fixed-density chemical-potential shifts give an $O(T^2)$ thermal correction by the <Sommerfeld expansion>. The reference dispersion already includes zero-temperature interactions.

= Landau parameter
{c}
{parent=Landau quasiparticle energy functional}
{title2=$F_\ell^s,F_\ell^a$}

For a three-dimensional isotropic unpolarized spin-one-half <Fermi liquid>, define $f^s=(f_{\uparrow\uparrow}+f_{\uparrow\downarrow})/2$ and $f^a=(f_{\uparrow\uparrow}-f_{\uparrow\downarrow})/2$. Dimensionless <Landau parameters> use the <spin-summed density of states> per <volume>:
$$
N^*(0)=\frac{m^*p_F}{\pi^2\hbar^3},\qquad N^*(0)f^{s,a}(\cos\theta)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}F_\ell^{s,a}P_\ell(\cos\theta).
$$
$P_\ell$ is a <Legendre polynomial>; $s,a$ label spin symmetry. No extra $(2\ell+1)$ multiplies this expansion.<Renormalization Group Approach to Interacting Fermions by Ramamurti Shankar>[Section VIII, angular interaction function; normalization stated explicitly here]{ref} The superscript $s$ on $F_0^s$ is unrelated to the dimensionless wave speed $s$ below.

= Fermi-liquid effective mass
{c}
{parent=Landau quasiparticle}
{title2=$m^*=p_F/v_F$}

In a three-dimensional uniform isotropic continuum obeying the <principle of Galilean relativity>, with one bare particle <mass> $m$, the <Fermi-liquid effective mass> satisfies
$$
\frac{m^*}{m}=1+\frac{F_1^s}{3}.
$$
A <band effective mass> need not satisfy this relation.<The Theory of a Fermi Liquid by Lev Davidovich Landau>[Section 2, equations (8)–(11), metallic-quasimomentum footnote]{ref}

For the stipulated $m^*=2m$ in the occupation figure, this relation requires $F_1^s=3$ if those assumptions apply. At fixed $p_F$, $v_F$ is half its free-particle value, so moving by $0.04p_F$ from the <Fermi surface> costs approximately $0.02p_F^2/m$ instead of $0.04p_F^2/m$. The factor of three belongs to the three-dimensional angular average, not to a universal definition of <effective mass>.

= Two-dimensional Landau effective-mass relation
{parent=Fermi-liquid effective mass}
{title2=$m^*/m=1+F_1/2$}

For an isotropic spinless continuum <Fermi liquid> with the <principle of Galilean relativity>, let $D^*=Am^*/(2\pi\hbar^2)$ be the total <density of states> and use a <Landau quasiparticle energy functional> whose double sum has no explicit $1/A$. Then
$$
\frac{m^*}{m}=1+D^*\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\phi}{2\pi}\,f(\phi)\cos\phi.
$$
Indeed, a small boost has radial displacement $p_0\cos\theta$. The one-<quasiparticle> term costs $Np_0^2/(2m^*)$, and the quadratic interaction multiplies that by $D^*\int f\cos\phi\,d\phi/(2\pi)$, since $\int\cos(\theta+\phi)\cos\theta\,d\theta=\pi\cos\phi$. Matching the <Galilean boost energy of an interacting gas> proves the formula. With <Fourier series> $D^*f=F_0+\sum_{\ell\geq1}F_\ell\cos(\ell\phi)$, the angular average is $F_1/2$. If the functional instead contains $1/A$, use its intensive kernel $Af$ and the <density of states> per area.

= Quasiparticle residue
{parent=Landau quasiparticle}
{title2=$Z$}

The <quasiparticle residue> is the coherent pole weight of the single-particle <Green's function>. Its coherent spectral term $Z\delta(E-\xi_p)$ contributes $Z\Theta(-\xi_p)$ to the zero-<temperature> <momentum> occupation. A smooth remainder leaves a jump $Z$ at the <Fermi surface>; <Landau quasiparticle> occupations have a unit step.<On the Theory of the Fermi Liquid by Lev Davidovich Landau>[p.70, equations (4)–(6); integrate the pole spectral weight]{ref}

For the local illustrative distribution in the figure, put $x=(p-p_F)/p_F$ and
$$
n_{\rm particle}(x)=0.65\Theta(-x)+0.35\left[\frac12-0.2\tanh(2x)\right].
$$
$\Theta$ is the <Heaviside step function>. Its one-sided limits are $0.825$ and $0.175$, giving $Z=0.65$. The smooth term is stipulated; this local curve does not specify a normalized distribution over all momenta. In particular, $Z$ is neither the fraction of atoms above the <Fermi surface> nor $m/m^*$.

= Zero sound
{parent=Fermi liquid}
{wiki}
{title2=$c_0$}

<Zero sound> is a collisionless collective <Fermi surface> deformation sustained by <Landau quasiparticle> interactions.<Oscillations in a Fermi Liquid by Lev Davidovich Landau>[Opening and section 1, pp.101–103]{ref}

A useful analogy is a crowd whose members respond to their neighbors' density without repeatedly bumping into them: the restoring interaction can remain effective when collisions cannot relax the distribution within one cycle. <Zero sound> is collective; it is not a single <Landau quasiparticle> outrunning every other excitation.

= Isotropic zero-sound model
{parent=Zero sound}
{title2=$1+F_0^s[1-\frac{s}{2}\log\frac{s+1}{s-1}]=0$}

Consider a neutral, isotropic, unpolarized three-dimensional spin-one-half <Fermi liquid>, with only $F_0^s>0$ nonzero. A longitudinal density wave with <angular frequency> $\omega$ and <wavevector> magnitude $q$ has $s=\omega/(qv_F)=c_0/v_F$. The collisionless angular equation for the energy displacement $\nu(u)$ of the <Fermi surface> is
$$
(s-u)\nu(u)=uF_0^s\langle\nu\rangle,\qquad \langle\nu\rangle=\frac12\int_{-1}^1\nu(u)\,du,\qquad u=\cos\theta.
$$
Averaging gives
$$
1=\frac{F_0^s}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{u}{s-u}\,du=F_0^s\left[\frac{s}{2}\log\frac{s+1}{s-1}-1\right].
$$
The bracket decreases from infinity to zero on $1<s<\infty$: each $F_0^s>0$ gives one undamped root above the small-$q$ <particle-hole continuum>.<Oscillations in a Fermi Liquid by Lev Davidovich Landau>[Section 1, equations (11)–(15), pp.103–104]{ref}

For an original example, $F_0^s=2$ gives $s=1.16474145$. Since $F_1^s=0$ here, the <principle of Galilean relativity> gives $m^*=m$ and taking the hydrodynamic limit before $T\to0$ gives $c_1/v_F=\sqrt{(1+F_0^s)/3}=1$. Stipulating $v_F=50\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ gives $c_0\simeq58.24\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and $c_1=50\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. These are model choices, not <helium-3> measurements. At large $F_0^s$, expanding the logarithm gives $s\sim\sqrt{F_0^s/3}$; for small positive $F_0^s$, the root approaches $1$ exponentially closely, so numerical resolution matters. A charged <electron> liquid needs its long-range interaction included; this neutral model is not its long-wavelength density mode.

= First-to-zero-sound crossover
{parent=Zero sound}
{title2=$\omega\tau$}

Angular-relaxation collision time $\tau$ distinguishes <first sound> ($\omega\tau\ll1$) from <zero sound> ($\omega\tau\gg1$). Collisions establish local <thermodynamic equilibrium> in the former.<Oscillations in a Fermi Liquid by Lev Davidovich Landau>[Sections 2–3, finite-temperature kinetic equation and absorption]{ref}

For the figure's stipulated law $\omega\tau=(T/T_\times)^{-2}$, cooling from $T=5T_\times$ to $T=T_\times/5$ changes $\omega\tau$ from $0.04$ to $25$, without changing the driving <angular frequency>. The crossover temperature is defined by $\omega\tau(T_\times)=1$. Calling <zero sound> a high-frequency mode therefore means high relative to the collision rate $1/\tau$, while its excitation <energy> must still remain within the low-energy domain of the <Fermi liquid>.

= Abel-Anderson-Wheatley zero-sound experiment
{c}
{parent=Zero sound}

In 1966, <William Russell Abel>, <Ansel Cochran Anderson> and <John Charles Wheatley> sent <acoustic waves> through bulk liquid <helium-3> at $0.32\ \mathrm{atm}$ and frequencies $15.4$ and $45.5\ \mathrm{MHz}$. A <quartz> delay line and receiving <acoustic transducer> bounded a $0.138\ \mathrm{mm}$ liquid gap; shielding reduced electrical pickup. Powdered <cerium magnesium nitrate> provided magnetic thermometry during a slow warm-up. Pulse amplitudes measured <acoustic attenuation>; <phase difference> comparison measured velocity. Calibration pulses checked gain; early received cycles avoided multiple reflections. On cooling through a maximum, <acoustic attenuation> changed from $\alpha\propto\omega^2/T^{*2}$ to $\alpha\propto T^{*2}$ with a velocity shift supporting <zero sound>; $T^*$ is their magnetic <temperature> scale. Weak <acoustic attenuation> was harder to resolve.<Propagation of Zero Sound in Liquid He3 at Low Temperatures by William Russell Abel and others>[pp.74–75, Fig.1 and readout/control paragraphs]{ref}

This 1966 test followed <Lev Landau>'s 1962 <Nobel Prize in Physics>.<The Nobel Prize in Physics 1962 by Nobel Prize Outreach>[1962 award to Landau for condensed-matter theory]{ref}

The experiment concerned normal liquid <helium-3>, distinct from the later discovery of <superfluid> <helium-3>.<John C. Wheatley by Robert E. Ecke and others>[pp.3–4, normal-liquid sound and later superfluid work]{ref} Landau's original papers are theoretical calculations, not reports of an apparatus.
