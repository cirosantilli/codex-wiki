= Electric field
{wiki}

The electric field is the <force> per unit charge on a stationary test particle.

= Sudden vector-potential pulse
{parent=Electric field}

In <temporal gauge>, a spatially uniform step $\mathbf A(t)=\mathbf A_0\Theta(t)$ gives $\mathbf E=-\mathbf A_0\delta(t)$. A charged scalar Fourier mode retains its canonical <wavevector> while its <kinetic momentum> changes from $\hbar\mathbf k$ to $\hbar\mathbf k-e\mathbf A_0$. The resulting <frequency> jump mixes positive- and negative-frequency modes.

= Electric field line
{parent=Electric field}
{title2=$d\mathbf r/ds\parallel\mathbf E$}

An oriented curve whose tangent points along the local <electric field>. Where the field is nonzero, choosing arc length $s$ gives $d\mathbf r/ds=\mathbf E/|\mathbf E|$. These curves visualize the direction of the <force> on a positive test charge; they are not generally particle trajectories, since a particle can have inertia and initial velocity. In <electrostatics>, they are normal to <equipotential surfaces> and cannot form a closed loop in a region with a single-valued potential that strictly decreases along the line. A field null can separate families of lines.

= Electric force
{parent=Electric field}
{title2=$\mathbf F_E=q\mathbf E$}

The <electric force> on an <electric charge> $q$ is $\mathbf F_E=q\mathbf E$, using the <electric field> of the other sources and excluding the charge's own self-field. Positive <electric charge> gives <force> along the <electric field>; negative <electric charge> reverses it. A field $\mathbf E=400\,\mathrm{N/C}\,\widehat{\mathbf x}$ therefore gives $\mathbf F_E=-8\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm N\,\widehat{\mathbf x}$ on $q=-2\,\mathrm{nC}$. This is the electric part of the <Lorentz force>; the magnetic contribution is $q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/5-4-electric-field[OpenStax, University Physics II, §5.4: electric field and force]{ref}.

= Electrostatic force
{parent=Electric force}
{title2=$\mathbf F=q\mathbf E$}

The <electric force> exerted by a time-independent <electric field>. For a <point charge>, the external field excludes its own singular self-field. The <method of images> evaluates conductor-induced forces by the image field at the physical charge's position; the fictitious images need not be physical charges in the conductor interior.

= Electric flux
{parent=Electric field}
{title2=$\Phi_E=\int_S E\cdot n\,dS$}
{wiki}

<Electric flux> measures how much <electric field> crosses a chosen <oriented surface>, with a sign distinguishing the two crossing directions. A strong <electric field> running along a surface contributes nothing to its <electric flux>: only the <normal component> counts. This lets <Gauss's law> turn a potentially complicated <electric field> into one number recording the net enclosed <electric charge>.

For a surface $S$ with chosen <unit normal> $\mathbf n$, the <electric flux> is the <surface integral>
$$
\Phi_E(S)=\int_S\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf n\,dA=\int_S\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf S,\qquad d\mathbf S=\mathbf n\,dA.
$$
Here $dA$ is positive <surface area>, while $d\mathbf S$ is the <vector area element>. The <dot product> makes <electric flux> a signed <scalar>, rather than a <vector> like the <electric field>. Its <SI> unit is $\mathrm{N\,m^2/C}$: <electric field> measured using the <newton (unit)> per <coulomb>, multiplied by <surface area> in square metres. Reversing the <orientation of a surface> replaces $\mathbf n$ by $-\mathbf n$ and reverses the <electric flux>. Calling it <electric flux> does not imply that <electric charge> is moving through the surface. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_03.html#Ch3-S2[Feynman lectures II.3, §3–2: flux of a vector field]{ref}.

For an open patch in a <plane>, with <surface area> $A$ and a constant <electric field>, the <unit normal> is constant and the <surface integral> simplifies to
$$
\Phi_E=\mathbf E\cdot(A\mathbf n)=EA\cos\theta,\qquad E=|\mathbf E|.
$$
The <angle> $\theta$ is between the <electric field> and the chosen <unit normal>, not between the <electric field> and the <plane>. The <cosine> describes the signed projected <surface area> seen by the <electric field>. Thus <electric flux> is positive for $0\leq\theta<90^\circ$, zero at $90^\circ$, and negative for $90^\circ<\theta\leq180^\circ$. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/6-1-electric-flux[Ling, Sanny and Moebs, University Physics II, §6.1: electric flux]{ref}.

For example, take $E=100\,\mathrm{N/C}$ and $A=0.02\,\mathrm{m^2}$, so $EA=2\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C}$. Three choices of <angle> give
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Phi_E(0^\circ)&=2\cos0^\circ=2\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C},\\
\Phi_E(60^\circ)&=2\cos60^\circ=1\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C},\\
\Phi_E(120^\circ)&=2\cos120^\circ=-1\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Keeping the $60^\circ$ patch fixed and reversing its <unit normal> gives $120^\circ$ and changes $+1$ to $-1\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C}$. The <electric field> and physical patch have not changed. If the <electric field> is instead tangent to the patch, its <dot product> with the <unit normal> vanishes: <electric flux> is zero even though $E=100\,\mathrm{N/C}$.

\Image[open-surface-electric-flux.png]
{title=Three open patches in a <plane> in a constant <electric field>: the <angle> to the chosen <unit normal> gives <electric fluxes> of 2, 1 and minus 1 newton square metres per <coulomb>}

For a <closed surface> enclosing a volume $V$, choose the outward <unit normal>. <Gauss's law> gives
$$
\Phi_E(\partial V)=\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf n\,dA=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{enclosed}}}{\varepsilon_0}.
$$
Here $\varepsilon_0$ is the <vacuum permittivity>, and $Q_{\mathrm{enclosed}}$ is the signed total <electric charge> inside. The <electric field> in the <surface integral> includes all sources, including external <electric charges>. Assume no ideal <point charge> lies on the integration surface. Zero enclosed <electric charge> implies zero total <electric flux>; it does **not** imply zero <electric field> everywhere on the <closed surface> or inside it. Positive and negative contributions to the <surface integral> can cancel. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/6-2-explaining-gausss-law[Ling, Sanny and Moebs, University Physics II, §6.2: outward flux and enclosed charge]{ref}.

To see the cancellation with sources inside, place <point charges> $+q$ at $(-a,0,0)$ and $-q$ at $(a,0,0)$, where $q>0$, $a=0.30\,\mathrm m$, and both lie inside the <sphere> of radius $R=1\,\mathrm m$ centered at the origin. This separated pair has the <electric dipole> geometry. Its enclosed <electric charge> is $q-q=0$, so its total outward <electric flux> is zero. At the point $P=(0,0,R)$ on the <sphere>, <Coulomb's law> and the <superposition principle> give the individual <electric fields>
$$
\mathbf E_+(P)=\frac{kq(a,0,R)}{(R^2+a^2)^{3/2}},\qquad
\mathbf E_-(P)=\frac{kq(a,0,-R)}{(R^2+a^2)^{3/2}},\qquad k=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}.
$$
The $z$ components cancel and the positive $x$ components add:
$$
\mathbf E(P)=\frac{2kqa}{(R^2+a^2)^{3/2}}\widehat{\mathbf x}\approx0.527244\frac{kq}{R^2}\widehat{\mathbf x}\ne\mathbf0.
$$
For the stipulated approximation $k=8.99\times10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}$ and $q=1\,\mathrm{nC}$, this is about $4.740\,\mathrm{N/C}$ along positive $x$. At $P$, the outward <unit normal> is $\widehat{\mathbf z}$, so this nonzero <electric field> is tangent to the <sphere>. At other points of the <sphere>, its <normal component> can be positive or negative. The source-field formula applies away from the two <point charges>, where the <electric field> has zero <divergence>; it is singular at the sources. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_04.html#Ch4-S2[Feynman lectures II.4, §§4–1 and 4–2: electrostatic sources and superposition]{ref}.

{title=Cross-section of the three-dimensional <electric field> of two opposite <point charges> inside a unit-radius <sphere>: zero total <electric flux> coexists with a nonzero tangential <electric field> at the top}

The diagram shows <streamlines> of the three-dimensional <electric field> in the section $y=0$. The circle is a cross-section of the <sphere>, not the integration surface: <Gauss's law> uses the entire three-dimensional <surface integral>. A two-dimensional <line integral> of outward field around that circle would be a different quantity, with units $\mathrm{N\,m/C}$ rather than the <electric flux> units $\mathrm{N\,m^2/C}$.

{wiki}

= Electrostatic potential
{synonym}

In <electrostatics>, the <electric potential> $\Phi$ tells us the interaction <potential energy> per unit test <electric charge> in the <electric field> of prescribed sources. Its most directly useful quantity is an <electric potential difference>: how much <work> per unit <electric charge> an external agent must supply to move that test <electric charge> slowly between two points. This replaces the bookkeeping of a <vector> <force> along a path with the difference of two <scalar> values. The test <electric charge> must be small enough not to rearrange the sources appreciably.

For motion from $A$ to $B$, write $\Delta\Phi=\Phi(B)-\Phi(A)$. With fixed sources and only the <electric force> doing <work>,
$$
\Delta\Phi=-\int_A^B\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l,\qquad
\Delta U=q\Delta\Phi,\qquad
W_{\mathrm{field}}=-q\Delta\Phi,\qquad
\Delta K=-\Delta U.
$$
Here $U$ is interaction <potential energy>, $K$ is <kinetic energy>, and the <line integral> uses the <electric field> produced by the other <electric charges>. For slow transport with unchanged <kinetic energy>, the external <work> is $W_{\mathrm{ext}}=\Delta U$, so $\Delta\Phi=W_{\mathrm{ext}}/q$ for nonzero $q$. This external <work> has the opposite sign to the <work> done by the <electric field>. The <SI> unit is the <volt>,
$$
1\,\mathrm V=1\,\mathrm{J/C}.
$$
A <volt> measures <energy> per unit <electric charge>, not <energy> itself. For example, crossing an <electric potential difference> of $+5\,\mathrm V$ changes the <potential energy> of $q=+2\,\mathrm{nC}$ by $+10\,\mathrm{nJ}$, but changes that of $q=-2\,\mathrm{nC}$ by $-10\,\mathrm{nJ}$. Both test <electric charges> encounter the same <electric potential difference>; their opposite signs reverse the <energy> change.<University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/7.2 Electric Potential and Potential Difference>[§7.2, equations 7.4–7.7: energy per charge, work and potential difference]{ref}

The <electrostatic potential> is a <scalar> <function> of position. The <electric field> is a <conservative vector field>, and all three components follow from the negative <gradient> of the <electric potential>:
$$
\mathbf E=-\nabla\Phi,\qquad E_x=-\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x},\quad E_y=-\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial y},\quad E_z=-\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial z}.
$$
The <electric field> points toward decreasing <electric potential>. A positive test <electric charge> feels <electric force> in that direction; a negative test <electric charge> feels <electric force> in the opposite direction, since $\mathbf F=q\mathbf E$. A freely accelerated negative test <electric charge> therefore moves toward higher <electric potential> while lowering its <potential energy> $U=q\Phi$.<University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/7.4 Determining Field from Potential>[§7.4, equations 7.13–7.15: components and negative gradient]{ref}

Only differences fix physical <electrostatic potentials>: adding the same constant $C_0$ everywhere, $\Phi'=\Phi+C_0$, changes neither the <gradient> nor any <electric potential difference>. Choosing an <electrical reference potential> is this elementary <gauge transformation>. Setting zero at infinity is convenient for finitely many localized <point charges>; it is not available for an ideal uniform <electric field> extending to infinity. There we can choose a finite reference point. Assigning zero at ground is a convention, whereas physically grounding an <electrical conductor> also allows exchange of <electric charge> to impose a boundary value. Zero <electric potential> at a point never by itself implies zero <electric field>.<University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/7.3 Calculations of Electric Potential>[§7.3, equation 7.8 and Figure 7.18: infinity and ground references]{ref}

For a fixed <point charge> $Q$ at the origin in <vacuum>, choose zero at infinity. <Coulomb's law> gives
$$
\Phi(r)=\frac{kQ}{r},\qquad
\mathbf E(r)=\frac{kQ}{r^2}\widehat{\mathbf r},\qquad
k=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0},\qquad r>0,
$$
where $\varepsilon_0$ is the <vacuum permittivity>. Use the stipulated approximation $k=8.99\times10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}$ and $Q=+2\,\mathrm{nC}$. At $r=0.20\,\mathrm m$,
$$
\Phi=89.9\,\mathrm V,\qquad E_r=449.5\,\mathrm{N/C}.
$$
A test <electric charge> $q=-3\,\mathrm{nC}$ has $U=-2.697\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm J$ and radial <electric force> $F_r=-1.3485\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm N$, directed inward. Moving it inward from $r_A=0.20\,\mathrm m$ to $r_B=0.10\,\mathrm m$ raises the <electric potential> from $89.9$ to $179.8\,\mathrm V$ but lowers its <potential energy>:
$$
\Delta\Phi=+89.9\,\mathrm V,\qquad
\Delta U=-2.697\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm J,\qquad
W_{\mathrm{field}}=+2.697\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm J.
$$
This is the <Coulomb potential energy> calculation using <electric potentials>. The source's singular self-field at $r=0$ is excluded; when finding the <force> on a source <point charge>, use only the <electric field> of the other sources.<University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/7.3 Calculations of Electric Potential>[§7.3: point-charge potential and scalar superposition]{ref}

For a uniform <electric field> $\mathbf E=300\,\mathrm{V/m}\,\widehat{\mathbf x}$, choose $\Phi(0)=12\,\mathrm V$. Then
$$
\Phi(x)=12\,\mathrm V-(300\,\mathrm{V/m})x,\qquad
\Delta\Phi=-\mathbf E\cdot(\mathbf r_B-\mathbf r_A).
$$
Moving from $x_A=0$ to $x_B=0.04\,\mathrm m$ gives $\Delta\Phi=-12\,\mathrm V$. For $q=+2\,\mathrm{nC}$ the change in <potential energy> is $-24\,\mathrm{nJ}$ and the <electric field> does $+24\,\mathrm{nJ}$ of <work>. For $q=-2\,\mathrm{nC}$, the same prescribed displacement gives $\Delta U=+24\,\mathrm{nJ}$: the external agent must supply that <work> for slow transport. This uniform <electric field> approximates the interior of a <parallel-plate capacitor> sufficiently far from the edges; the <fringing field of a capacitor> limits the approximation.<University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/7.4 Determining Field from Potential>[§7.4: voltage gradient in a uniform field]{ref}

An <equipotential> is a <level set> $\Phi=\text{constant}$. On a smooth <equipotential> with nonzero <gradient>, every tangent displacement has $d\Phi=\nabla\Phi\cdot d\mathbf l=0$, so the <electric field> is normal to the <equipotential>. Thus moving a test <electric charge> along an <equipotential> requires no net electrical <work>. Around a <point charge>, the <equipotentials> are <spheres>; the circles in the diagram are their sections, not an assumption of two-dimensional <Coulomb's law>. A uniform <electric field> has parallel <plane> <equipotentials>. For contours separated by the same small <electric potential difference>, smaller normal separation means a stronger <electric field>, approximately $|\mathbf E|\approx|\Delta\Phi|/\Delta n$. At a point where $\nabla\Phi=0$, the <electric field> is zero and has no direction to call normal.<University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/7.5 Equipotential Surfaces and Conductors>[§7.5, Figures 7.30–7.32: equipotentials, perpendicular field and zero work]{ref}

The <superposition principle> adds <electric potentials> as signed <scalars>. For finitely many stationary <point charges> $q_j$ at $\mathbf r_j$ in <vacuum>,
$$
\Phi(\mathbf r)=k\sum_j\frac{q_j}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_j|},\qquad
\mathbf E(\mathbf r)=k\sum_jq_j\frac{\mathbf r-\mathbf r_j}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_j|^3}.
$$
For example, the <electric dipole> with $+q$ at $(a,0,0)$ and $-q$ at $(-a,0,0)$ has zero <electric potential> everywhere in the midplane $x=0$, yet its <electric field> at the midpoint is $\mathbf E=-(2kq/a^2)\widehat{\mathbf x}$. With $q=2\,\mathrm{nC}$ and $a=0.10\,\mathrm m$, this is $-3596\,\mathrm{N/C}\,\widehat{\mathbf x}$. At $(2a,0,0)$, the same model gives $\Phi=2kq/(3a)\approx119.867\,\mathrm V$ and $E_x=8kq/(9a^2)\approx1598.222\,\mathrm{N/C}$. Cancellation of <electric potentials> is quite different from cancellation of <vector> <electric fields>.

A motivating measurement is <conductive-paper electric potential mapping>. A documented teaching experiment uses slightly conductive paper on an insulating board, two circular <electrodes> in highly conducting ink, a steady supply, and a digital <voltmeter>. Connect the supply to the two <electrodes>, keep the <voltmeter> common lead on the negative <electrode>, and use its other lead as a movable probe. Adjust the supply using the measured <electric potential difference> between <electrodes>; the published procedure uses $10\,\mathrm V$. The observable is the <voltmeter> reading $\Phi(P)-\Phi(\mathrm{COM})$ at the probe position $P$. Locate several points reading $2\,\mathrm V$, record their positions on a separate map, and join them into an <equipotential>; repeat at other readings. Lift the probe between contacts so it does not damage the paper. This procedure makes the <electrical reference potential> concrete: every reading compares two contacts.<Physics 2212 laboratory manual by Eric Murray/Physics 2212, Lab 5: Electric Potential and Field>{ref}

From neighboring readings infer an <electric field> component by a <finite difference>, $E_x\approx-[\Phi(x+h)-\Phi(x)]/h$. Reconstruct two in-plane components with displacements along two independent directions. Alternatively, draw the <electric field> normal to mapped <equipotentials>, toward lower <electric potential>. The second diagram illustrates the same measurement principle with bar <electrodes> spanning a uniform sheet, insulating top and bottom edges, and a synthetic model $\Phi(x)=(50\,\mathrm{V/m})x$. The model readings $4.0\,\mathrm V$ at $x=0.08\,\mathrm m$ and $5.0\,\mathrm V$ at $x=0.10\,\mathrm m$ imply $E_x=-50\,\mathrm{V/m}$. These values are calculated illustrations, not reported experimental data. The documented circular-<electrode> experiment similarly estimates the <electric field> from readings at successive centimetre marks.<Physics 2212 laboratory manual by Eric Murray/Physics 2212, Lab 5: Electric Potential and Field>{ref}

Useful controls include checking the <voltmeter> zero, checking that the <electric potential difference> along each painted <electrode> is negligible, and repeating reference readings to detect supply drift. Reversing the supply should reverse the signs relative to the same reference contact. Contact resistance, leakage <electric currents>, and touching the sheet can distort the map. A <voltmeter> has finite input <electrical resistance> and draws a small <electric current>; <voltmeter loading> changes the quantity being measured if that <electrical resistance> is insufficiently large relative to the sheet's effective source <electrical resistance>.<Electricity and Magnetism laboratory manual/EC-2: Electric Fields>{ref}<University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/10.4 Electrical Measuring Instruments>[§10.4, Measuring Voltage with a Voltmeter: parallel connection and large input resistance]{ref}

The paper carries a steady <electric current>, so this is a planar analogue rather than a direct measurement of isolated <point charges> in three-dimensional <vacuum>. For constant <electrical conductivity>, <Ohm's law> and steady <charge conservation> give $\mathbf j=-\sigma\nabla\Phi$ and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf j=0$, hence the two-dimensional <Laplace equation> away from <electrodes>. Insulating sheet edges impose zero normal <electric current>, while finite <electrodes> fix boundary values. This derivation explains both the analogy and its limits: inhomogeneous paper, finite boundaries, and finite <electrodes> can change the contours. One should not fit the three-dimensional <point charge> formula $kQ/r$ to a planar map without testing that model. Small probe spacing resolves spatial variation better, but amplifies uncertainty in the inferred <finite difference>. The published laboratory instructions explicitly ask students to assess whether their proposed field fit is appropriate.<Electricity and Magnetism laboratory manual/EC-2: Electric Fields>{ref}<Physics 2212 laboratory manual by Eric Murray/Physics 2212, Lab 5: Electric Potential and Field>{ref}

The scalar description has a crucial limit. <Faraday's law of induction> for a stationary closed path $\Gamma$ says
$$
\oint_\Gamma\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=-\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt},\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},
$$
where $\Phi_B$ is <magnetic flux>. Nonzero changing <magnetic flux> gives nonzero circulation, incompatible with a single-valued <electric potential> alone. More generally, a scalar <electric potential> and a <magnetic vector potential> together give
$$
\mathbf E=-\nabla\Phi-\frac{\partial\mathbf A}{\partial t},\qquad
\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A.
$$
Consequently, in the presence of induction, two <voltmeter> lead routes between the same contacts can produce different readings if the enclosed <magnetic flux> changes. This follows by subtracting the two path <line integrals>; it is a deduction from <Faraday's law of induction>, not a claimed outcome of the paper experiment. The steady setup and negligible induction justify interpreting its readings as <electric potential differences>. Merely introducing a scalar <electric potential> that depends on <time> cannot supply a nonzero <curl> of the <electric field>.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume II by Richard Feynman and others/Chapter 21: Solutions of Maxwell’s Equations with Currents and Charges>[§21–3]{ref}

A short mathematical justification connects the statements above. Along a smooth path, the <chain rule> gives $d\Phi=\nabla\Phi\cdot d\mathbf l$. Integrating and using $\mathbf E=-\nabla\Phi$ yields the endpoint formula by the <fundamental theorem for line integrals>, proving <path independence>. Conversely, vanishing circulation around every closed path lets us define $\Phi(P)=-\int_{P_0}^{P}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l$ independently of the route, with $\Phi(P_0)=0$. Zero <curl> is a local test; for a continuously differentiable <curl-free vector field>, a <simply connected> open region suffices for a global potential. The explicit finite-source formula already gives a global <electric potential> away from its source points.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume II by Richard Feynman and others/Chapter 4: Electrostatics>{ref}

= Grounded-plate potential of a central charged sheet
{parent=Electric potential}
{title2=$V_{\rm sheet}=\sigma d/(4\epsilon_0)$}

An ideal infinite sheet midway between parallel grounded plates has equal fields of magnitude $\sigma/(2\epsilon_0)$ on its two sides, directed from positive sheet charge toward the plates. Integrating over the half-separation d/2 gives the displayed <electric potential>. Each plate carries induced inner-face <surface charge density> $-\sigma/2$. The potential is triangular between the plates, and the field jumps by $\sigma/\epsilon_0$ across the sheet. Edge fields are excluded.

= Potential of a charged cylindrical shell with angular Fourier data
{parent=Electric potential}
{title2=$\Phi_n=\frac{Rs_n}{2n}(r_</r_>)^n$}

For an infinite circular cylinder of radius $R$, assume the <electrostatic potential> is independent of the axial coordinate. An angular <Fourier mode> $s_n\cos n\theta$, or $s_n\sin n\theta$, in the prescribed jump $\partial_r\Phi|_{R^+}-\partial_r\Phi|_{R^-}=-\sigma(\theta)$ produces the same angular mode in the potential, multiplied by
$$
\frac{Rs_n}{2n}\begin{cases}(r/R)^n,&r<R,\\(R/r)^n,&r>R.\end{cases}
$$
Here $r_<=\min(r,R)$ and $r_>=\max(r,R)$ in the abbreviated header formula. <Separation of variables> selects $r^n$ inside by regularity and $r^{-n}$ outside by decay. Continuity equates their boundary values, and the derivative jump is minus $2n/R$ times that value. A nonzero angular mean of the <surface charge density> introduces a logarithmic term, so zero mean is necessary for a potential decaying at infinity in this two-dimensional reduction.

= Voltmeter

{wiki}

A <voltmeter> measures an <electric potential difference> between two contacts; its finite input <electrical resistance> can cause <voltmeter loading>.

= Voltmeter loading
{parent=Voltmeter}

<Voltmeter loading> is the change in an <electric potential difference> caused by the <electric current> drawn by the measuring <voltmeter>.

For a <loaded voltage divider> in steady <direct current>, a <voltmeter> connected across the lower <resistor> places its finite input <electrical resistance> $R_m$ in parallel with the lower arm's <electrical resistance> $R_2$ and any intended <electrical load> of <electrical resistance> $R_L$. With positive, fixed <electrical resistances>, an upper <resistor> of <electrical resistance> $R_1$, and an ideal <voltage source> supplying <electric potential difference> $V_{\mathrm{in}}$, <Kirchhoff's current law> and <Ohm's law> give
$$
\frac{V_{\mathrm{in}}-V_m}{R_1}=\frac{V_m}{R_2}+\frac{V_m}{R_L}+\frac{V_m}{R_m},\qquad
R_p=\left(\frac1{R_2}+\frac1{R_L}+\frac1{R_m}\right)^{-1},\qquad
V_m=V_{\mathrm{in}}\frac{R_p}{R_1+R_p}.
$$
Here $V_m$ is the connected circuit's <electric potential difference> across the <voltmeter>; omit $1/R_L$ if there is no intended <electrical load>. The parallel connection and need for large input <electrical resistance> are standard <voltmeter> operating principles.<University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/10.4 Electrical Measuring Instruments>[§10.4, Measuring Voltage with a Voltmeter: parallel connection and large input resistance]{ref}

<Voltmeter loading> is a bias relative to the target <electrical circuit> with the <voltmeter> absent and any intended <electrical load> still present. <Voltage divider loading error> instead compares the attached-load output with the wholly unloaded output. An accurate readout of $V_m$, supported by <measurement calibration>, can still differ from either target: calibration accuracy concerns the indication of the connected <electric potential difference>, whereas <voltmeter loading> changes that <electric potential difference> itself.

= Electrical reference potential
{parent=Electric potential}
{title2=$\Phi_{\mathrm{ref}}=0$}

An <electrical reference potential> fixes the arbitrary additive constant of an <electric potential>. Assigning zero to ground makes other potentials differences from that baseline; it does not assert that the <electric field> vanishes there. If a source calculation gives $\Phi(a)=89.9\,\mathrm V$ and $\Phi(b)=179.8\,\mathrm V$ relative to infinity, choosing $a$ as the zero instead gives $\Phi'(a)=0$ and $\Phi'(b)=89.9\,\mathrm V$, with the same <electric potential difference>. Grounding an <electrical conductor> additionally connects it to a reservoir that can exchange <electric charge> to maintain the prescribed <electric potential>; that physical boundary condition differs from merely relabeling an unchanged solution.<University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/7.3 Calculations of Electric Potential>[§7.3: potential references, including ground in Figure 7.18]{ref}; https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_06.html#Ch6-S8[Feynman lectures II.6, §6–8: a grounded conducting plane]{ref}.

= Constrained electrostatic equilibrium
{parent=Electric potential}

A particle constrained to a surface can have a stable minimum of <electrostatic potential> restricted to that surface even when the unrestricted equilibrium is unstable. Away from source charges, <Laplace's equation> makes the full potential <Hessian matrix> trace zero, which rules out a positive-definite three-dimensional Hessian but does not rule out a positive-definite restriction to two dimensions. Equal positive charges at the corners of a square give a planar minimum at its centre and an unstable perpendicular direction.

= Electrostatic reciprocity identity
{parent=Electric potential}
{title2=$\int_V(\phi_1\rho_2-\phi_2\rho_1)\,dV=\varepsilon_0\int_{\partial V}(\phi_2\nabla\phi_1-\phi_1\nabla\phi_2)\cdot d\mathbf S$}

For two <electrostatic potentials> satisfying the <Poisson equation> $\Delta\phi_i=-\rho_i/\varepsilon_0$, <Green's second identity> yields the displayed reciprocity formula. The <product rule for divergence> applied to $\phi_1\nabla\phi_2-\phi_2\nabla\phi_1$ cancels the cross-gradient terms. The normal points outward from the integration region; for an exterior region this means inward toward the excluded object on its inner boundary. Boundary terms may be removed only under suitable decay or boundary conditions.

= Image charge
{parent=Electric potential}

An image charge is a fictitious point charge placed outside the physical solution region so that its potential, combined with the real sources, enforces the required conductor boundary values. The <uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem> then identifies the constructed potential with the physical one. The image need not describe the actual induced surface-charge distribution.

= Image charges in a right-angle conducting wedge
{parent=Image charge}
{title2=$F_y=-q^2(2\sqrt2-1)/(16\pi\epsilon_0y^2)$}

For conducting sheets $y=\pm x$ at common zero potential and a charge in $y>|x|$, successive reflections give two negative images and one positive double-reflection image. On the bisector, the negative images lie at $(\pm y,0)$ and the positive image at $(0,-y)$. Summing their fields gives the displayed downward <force>. Image <forces> exclude the real charge's self-field; replacing the induced charge by images does not make the images physical particles.

= Lateral stability of a spring-supported charge in a wedge
{parent=Image charges in a right-angle conducting wedge}
{title2=$T/\ell>q^2(\sqrt2+1/4)/(4\pi\epsilon_0y_e^3)$}

The electrostatic bisector <force> is laterally anti-restoring, with the positive stiffness coefficient on the right. A <spring> of tension $T$ and <length> $\ell$ adds transverse restoring stiffness $T/\ell$ when its fixed support is directly above the charge. The displayed inequality therefore distinguishes actual transverse stability from the saddle of a model with only a vertical <spring> <force>. Axial <spring> stiffness alone does not specify the transverse verdict. At fixed finite support geometry and positive gravitational load, sufficiently small <electric charge> preserves lateral stability: the destabilizing coefficient tends to zero as $q^2$, while the tension term approaches $mg/\ell>0$. The vertical <equilibrium> must separately satisfy its own positive-stiffness condition.

= Electrostatic softening above a conducting wedge
{parent=Image charges in a right-angle conducting wedge}
{title2=$\omega_y^2=(k-2\beta/y_e^3)/m$}

For an attractive <force> $-\beta/y^2$ and an otherwise linear vertical <spring>, differentiation about the charged <equilibrium> $y_e$ reduces the <spring> stiffness by $2\beta/y_e^3$. If the uncharged height is $y_0$ and the shift is small, the fractional <frequency> change is $-\beta/(ky_0^3)$. Using only the <force> value, without its <derivative>, would miss this <frequency> shift.

= Image charge at a dielectric interface
{parent=Image charge}
{title2=$q'=q(\epsilon_1-\epsilon_2)/(\epsilon_1+\epsilon_2)$}

For a point <electric charge> $q$ in a homogeneous medium of <permittivity> $\epsilon_1$ beside a plane interface with <permittivity> $\epsilon_2$, its potential on the source side is reproduced by a mirrored <image charge> $q'=q(\epsilon_1-\epsilon_2)/(\epsilon_1+\epsilon_2)$. The interface has no free surface charge; continuity of tangential <electric field> and normal <electric displacement field> fixes this coefficient.

= Permittivity convention for transmitted image charges
{parent=Image charge at a dielectric interface}

For a real charge $Q$ in medium 1, the potential in medium 2 can be represented by the source $Q_t=2\epsilon_2Q/(\epsilon_1+\epsilon_2)$ using medium 2's Coulomb prefactor $1/(4\pi\epsilon_2)$. The same potential uses $2\epsilon_1Q/(\epsilon_1+\epsilon_2)$ if medium 1's prefactor is retained instead. These different effective source labels do not change the physical field. A decomposition $Q_t=Q+Q'$ must state which prefactor it uses.

= Fall time of a charge toward a grounded plane
{parent=Image charge}
{title2=$t_{\rm hit}=\pi\sqrt{2\pi\varepsilon_0 m d^3}/|q|$}

The image of a charge $q$ at height $z$ is $-q$ at height $-z$. Their separation is $2z$, giving force $-q^2/(16\pi\varepsilon_0z^2)$. Starting at rest at height $d$, energy conservation gives $\dot z^2=q^2(1/z-1/d)/(8\pi\varepsilon_0m)$. Integrating the reciprocal speed to the plane yields the stated finite time in the electrostatic Newtonian model. The induced-conductor potential energy is $-q^2/(16\pi\varepsilon_0z)$; using the full image-pair energy would double the force incorrectly.

= Falling charge above a grounded conducting plane
{parent=Image charge}
{title2=$T=\pi\sqrt{2\pi\varepsilon_0md^3}/|q|$}

A real charge of height $z$ has a reflected <image charge> at $-z$. Its acceleration is $\ddot z=-\kappa/z^2$, with $\kappa=q^2/(16\pi\varepsilon_0m)$. Released from rest at height $d$, its ideal-model arrival time is
$$
T=\frac{\pi d^{3/2}}{2\sqrt{2\kappa}}.
$$
The image follows the instantaneous reflected position. The induced-charge energy carries one-half the full real-image pair interaction factor, consistent with this force.

= Image charge for a grounded conducting sphere
{parent=Image charge}
{title2=$q'=-aq/R,\quad\mathbf p'=a^2\mathbf p/R^2$}

For a charge $q$ at $\mathbf p$, $R=|\mathbf p|>a$, outside a grounded sphere of radius $a$, the displayed image gives zero potential on the sphere and decay at infinity. On $|\mathbf x|=a$, one has $|\mathbf x-\mathbf p'|=(a/R)|\mathbf x-\mathbf p|$, proving boundary cancellation. The induced force is inward, of magnitude $q^2aR/[4\pi\varepsilon_0(R^2-a^2)^2]$, from <Coulomb's law> applied to the image field. The self-field is excluded.

= Equal-charge balance around a grounded sphere
{parent=Image charge for a grounded conducting sphere}
{title2=$1/(4x^2)=x[(x^2-1)^{-2}+(x^2+1)^{-2}]$}

Place equal positive <point charges> at distances $d$ on opposite sides of a grounded conducting sphere of radius $a$, and set $x=d/a>1$. Each physical charge has an image $-qa/d$ at distance $a^2/d$ from the centre on its own side. The repulsion from the other physical charge balances attraction to both images precisely when the displayed equation holds. At large $x$, the two sides behave as $1/(4x^2)$ and $2/x^3$, explaining the approximate root $x\simeq8$. A constant nonzero sphere potential adds a central image and changes the force balance.

= Image charges for a neutral conducting sphere
{parent=Image charge for a grounded conducting sphere}
{title2=$q'=-aq/d,\quad x'=a^2/d,\quad q_0=aq/d$}

A real charge $q$ at $d>a$ outside a spherical conductor of radius $a$ has the usual grounded-sphere <image charge> $q'=-aq/d$ at $x'=a^2/d$. Adding a central image $q_0=-q'$ makes the total image charge zero, representing an isolated neutral conductor. The off-centre pair has zero potential on $r=a$; the central image therefore sets the constant conductor potential to $q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0d)$. Its induced <electric dipole moment> is $p_x=q'x'=-qa^3/d^2$. Keeping the applied central field $\mathbf E_0=-q\widehat{\mathbf x}/(4\pi\varepsilon_0d^2)$ fixed gives $\mathbf p=4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3\mathbf E_0$. As $d\to\infty$, the external field becomes uniform near the sphere; its divergent constant potential can be removed without affecting the <electric field>.

= Electric potential difference
{parent=Electric potential}
{title2=$V_{AB}=\phi(A)-\phi(B)$}

An <electric potential difference> compares <electric potentials> at two points. For a move from $a$ to $b$, define $\Delta\Phi=\Phi(b)-\Phi(a)$; the terminal convention $V_{AB}=\Phi(A)-\Phi(B)$ instead means the potential at $A$ relative to $B$, so the order must always be stated. In <electrostatics>, the <line integral> of the <electric field> gives
$$
\Delta\Phi=-\int_a^b\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l,\qquad
\Delta U=q\Delta\Phi,\qquad
W_{\mathrm{field}}=q\int_a^b\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=q\bigl(\Phi(a)-\Phi(b)\bigr)=-\Delta U.
$$
Here $\Delta U$ is the change in interaction <potential energy> of the test <electric charge> $q$ with fixed sources, while $W_{\mathrm{field}}$ is <work> done by the <electric field>. For an external agent moving the charge slowly without a change in <kinetic energy>, its <work> is $\Delta U$. A positive <electric charge> gains <kinetic energy> when accelerated toward lower <electric potential>; a negative <electric charge> gains <kinetic energy> when accelerated toward higher <electric potential>. Both motions lower $q\Phi$.<University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/7.2 Electric Potential and Potential Difference>[§7.2: potential difference, work, and energy]{ref}

For example, fix $Q=+2\,\mathrm{nC}$ at the origin and compare distances $r_a=0.20\,\mathrm m$ and $r_b=0.10\,\mathrm m$. With the stipulated approximation $k=8.99\times10^9\,\mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}$ and zero at infinity,
$$
\Phi(a)=\frac{kQ}{r_a}=89.9\,\mathrm V,\qquad
\Phi(b)=\frac{kQ}{r_b}=179.8\,\mathrm V,\qquad
\Delta\Phi=89.9\,\mathrm V.
$$
Moving $q=-3\,\mathrm{nC}$ inward therefore gives
$$
U_a=-2.697\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm J,\qquad
U_b=-5.394\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm J,\qquad
\Delta U=-2.697\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm J,\qquad
W_{\mathrm{field}}=+2.697\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm J.
$$
If only the <electric force> does <work>, <conservation of energy> makes the gain in <kinetic energy> $2.697\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm J$. Direct integration confirms the signs: $\int_{r_a}^{r_b}kQ\,dr/r^2=kQ(1/r_a-1/r_b)=-89.9\,\mathrm V$, which multiplied by the negative test <electric charge> gives positive <work>. The <electric potential difference> is unchanged by choosing a different <electrical reference potential>.

In an <electrical circuit>, an <electric potential difference> is the voltage across a component's terminals. In an <electrical network>, a vertex <voltage> is specified relative to a reference vertex; its differences across edges drive <current flow>. For a uniform <electric field>, the integral reduces to $\Delta\Phi=-\mathbf E\cdot(\mathbf r_b-\mathbf r_a)$. A field $\mathbf E=300\,\mathrm{V/m}\,\widehat{\mathbf x}$ across a displacement $0.04\,\mathrm m\,\widehat{\mathbf x}$ therefore gives $\Delta\Phi=-12\,\mathrm V$, as in the interior approximation for a <parallel-plate capacitor>. The <volt>, measured in <joules> per <coulomb>, expresses <energy> transfer per unit <electric charge>, which makes it useful for comparing components without specifying the amount of charge transported.

= Bias voltage
{parent=Electric potential difference}

An applied potential difference that sets a device operating condition or shifts reservoir electrochemical potentials. In a multi-terminal device the grounded lead, biased lead and gate reference must be stated; they determine the relation between measured slopes and <capacitances>.

= Point charge
{parent=Electric potential}
{wiki}

A point charge idealizes electric charge concentrated at one point. In vacuum, a charge $Q$ at the origin has potential $Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r)$.

= Equipotential
{parent=Electric potential}
{wiki}

An equipotential is a level set of the electric potential. The electric field is normal to every smooth equipotential.
