= Tangent vector
{wiki}

A <tangent vector> at a point $p$ of a <smooth manifold> without boundary records an instantaneous <velocity> through $p$. Its base point is part of the data; measuring its length additionally requires a <Riemannian metric>. Intrinsically, a <smooth curve> $\gamma$ with $\gamma(0)=p$ defines its <tangent vector> by how every local <smooth function> changes along it:
$$
v(f)=\left.\frac{d}{dt}f(\gamma(t))\right|_{t=0}.
$$
This is a <derivation at a point>. Two <smooth curves> through $p$ represent the same <tangent vector> if these <derivatives> agree for every such <smooth function>; equivalently, their coordinate <velocity vectors> agree in one <manifold chart>. The <chain rule> makes that agreement independent of the chosen <manifold chart>. https://www.math.toronto.edu/~jeffrey/matd67/tu.pdf[Loring W. Tu, An Introduction to Manifolds, §8.6: curves and intrinsic velocity]{ref}

For an <embedded submanifold> of <Euclidean space>, the intrinsic <tangent vector> is represented by the ordinary <derivative> $\gamma'(0)$, with its base point $p$ retained. A drawn arrow starting at $p$ represents that <tangent vector>; its endpoint $p+\gamma'(0)$ need not lie on the <embedded submanifold>. Nor is a <tangent vector> required to be a <unit tangent vector>: a stationary <smooth curve> gives the <zero vector>, which belongs to the <tangent space> but has no direction to normalize.

Here are explicit calculations. In $\mathbb R^2$, $\gamma(t)=(t,t^2)$ and $\eta(t)=(t,2t^2)$ have different routes but the same <tangent vector> $(1,0)$ at $(0,0)$. For $f(x,y)=3x+y$, both give $v(f)=3$. For a <circle> of <radius> $R$, $c(t)=(R\cos(\omega t),R\sin(\omega t))$ has <tangent vector> $(0,R\omega)$ at $(R,0)$: changing $\omega$ changes the <speed> or reverses travel without changing the <circle>.

A <reparametrization> $\beta(s)=\gamma(h(s))$ with $h(0)=0$ gives, by the <chain rule>, $\beta'(0)=h'(0)\gamma'(0)$. A regular change of <curve parameter> has $h'(0)\ne0$. The singular choice $h(s)=s^3$ instead gives
$$
\gamma(t)=(t,t^2),\qquad \beta(s)=(s^3,s^6),\qquad \gamma'(0)=(1,0),\qquad \beta'(0)=(0,0).
$$
Both describe the same <parabola>, whose <tangent line> at the origin is still the horizontal axis. The zero <velocity> diagnoses this singular change of <curve parameter>, whose inverse is not differentiable at zero; it does not show that the geometric <tangent line> is absent. The <regular curve> $\gamma$ supplies its nonzero direction.

= Derivation at a point
{parent=Tangent vector}
{title2=$v:C_p^\infty(M)\to\mathbb R$}

= Derivations at a point
{synonym}

At $p$ on a <smooth manifold>, a derivation at that point is a real-linear map $v:C_p^\infty(M)\to\mathbb R$ on <germs> of smooth functions with $v(fh)=f(p)v(h)+h(p)v(f)$. It defines a <tangent vector>. The local factorization $f-f(p)=\sum_i(x^i-x^i(p))f_i$ gives $v(f)=\sum_i v(x^i)\partial_i f(p)$, so the coordinate derivations form a basis of the <tangent space>.

= Tangent space
{title2=$T_pM$}
{parent=Tangent vector}
{wiki}

At a fixed point $p$ of an $n$-dimensional <smooth manifold> $M$ without boundary, the <tangent space> $T_pM$ is the real <vector space> of all <tangent vectors> at $p$. In <manifold charts> $x=(x^1,\ldots,x^n)$ and $y=(y^1,\ldots,y^n)$, a <tangent vector> has components
$$
v=\sum_{i=1}^n v_x^i\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\right|_p,\qquad
v_y^a=\sum_{i=1}^n\left.\frac{\partial y^a}{\partial x^i}\right|_p v_x^i.
$$
The <coordinate basis> transforms inversely, so the <tangent vector> is unchanged. This invertible <Jacobian matrix> also preserves <vector addition> and <scalar multiplication>, giving a <vector space> of <dimension> $n$ independently of coordinates. https://www.math.toronto.edu/mein/teaching/LectureNotes/Man.pdf[Eckhard Meinrenken, Differentiable Manifolds, §1.5: tangent spaces and coordinate bases]{ref}

For example, at $(x,y)=(1,0)$ in $\mathbb R^2$, use the alternative <manifold chart> $u=x$, $w=y+x^2$. The <Jacobian matrix> sends $(v_x,v_y)=(2,3)$ to $(v_u,v_w)=(2,7)$. Indeed, the <smooth curve> $(x(t),y(t))=(1+2t,3t)$ gives $w(t)=1+7t+4t^2$. Conversely, $\partial_u|_p=\partial_x|_p-2\partial_y|_p$ and $\partial_w|_p=\partial_y|_p$, so $2\partial_u|_p+7\partial_w|_p=2\partial_x|_p+3\partial_y|_p$. The numbers change; their action on every <smooth function> does not.

For an <embedded submanifold> $M\subseteq\mathbb R^N$, the <tangent space> can be identified with the <vector subspace>
$$
T_pM=\{\gamma'(0):\gamma:(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to M\text{ is smooth},\ \gamma(0)=p\}.
$$
A proof outline explains why every coordinate vector is possible: in a <manifold chart> $x$, lift the short straight path $x(p)+ta$ using $x^{-1}$. Its <tangent vector> has components $a$. For a regular local parametrization $X:U\subseteq\mathbb R^n\to M$, the same <chain rule> gives $T_pM=\operatorname{im}DX_q$, where $X(q)=p$. This is the <tangent space from a local parametrization>.

For the <sphere> $S_R^2=\{p\in\mathbb R^3:p\cdot p=R^2\}$, differentiating that equation gives $p\cdot v=0$. Conversely, for any nonzero $v$ with $p\cdot v=0$, the <smooth curve>
$$
\gamma_v(t)=p\cos\left(\frac{\|v\|t}{R}\right)+R\frac{v}{\|v\|}\sin\left(\frac{\|v\|t}{R}\right)
$$
lies on the <sphere> and has $\gamma_v'(0)=v$; use a constant <smooth curve> for $v=0$. Thus $T_pS_R^2=\{v:p\cdot v=0\}$, not merely a subset of that plane. At $p=(0,0,R)$ this <vector subspace> is $v_z=0$, while the affine <tangent plane> drawn through $p$ is $z=R$.

This also explains a <mechanical constraint> without requiring a force calculation. In the ideal mathematical model of a particle confined to a stationary <sphere>, its allowed <velocities> at $p$ are exactly $T_pS_R^2$: the radial component must vanish, but either independent tangential direction is available. For a general stationary <holonomic constraint> $F(q)=0$ with independent equations, the <regular level set theorem> gives $T_qM=\ker DF_q$. These are instantaneous kinematic possibilities; they do not determine the actual subsequent motion. On a general <smooth manifold>, <tangent spaces> at different base points cannot be identified canonically; an <affine connection> supplies <parallel transport> when such comparisons are needed.

= Singular defining maps can overestimate tangent spaces
{parent=Tangent space}
{title2=$T_a(g^{-1}(0))\subseteq\ker Dg_a\text{ need not be equality}$}

If the zero set of a differentiable <function> $g$ is a <submanifold>, the <chain rule> always places its <tangent space> inside $\ker Dg_a$. Equality follows from the full-rank hypotheses of the <regular level set theorem>, but may fail for a singular defining function even when the zero set itself is smooth. For $g:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$, $g(x)=x^2$, the zero set is the single point $\{0\}$. Its <tangent space> is zero, while $Dg_0=0$ has kernel $\mathbb R$. One should therefore check the <constraint qualification> before using the kernel formula.

= Tangent plane
{parent=Tangent space}

For an embedded <regular surface> $M\subseteq\mathbb R^3$ and $p\in M$, the affine <tangent plane> is
$$
p+T_pM=\{p+v:v\in T_pM\}.
$$
It is a set of points through $p$, whereas the <tangent space> $T_pM$ is a <vector space> of displacements with <zero vector>. Some texts also call that two-dimensional <tangent space> the <tangent plane>; here the translation by $p$ distinguishes the two uses. For a regular <parametric surface> $X(u,v)$ with $p=X(u_0,v_0)$,
$$
T_pM=\operatorname{span}\{X_u,X_v\},\qquad
p+T_pM=\{p+aX_u+bX_v:a,b\in\mathbb R\},
$$
where the <partial derivatives> are evaluated at $(u_0,v_0)$. Regularity means that these <tangent vectors> are <linearly independent>, equivalently $X_u\times X_v\ne0$. Their <cross product> is a <normal vector>. https://www.math.uga.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/ShifrinDiffGeo.pdf[Theodore Shifrin, Differential Geometry: A First Course in Curves and Surfaces, Chapter 2, §1: regular parametrizations and tangent planes]{ref}

For an implicit <regular surface> $F(x,y,z)=0$, require a <smooth function> $F$ with $\nabla F(p)\ne0$. Then
$$
T_pM=\ker DF_p=\{v:\nabla F(p)\cdot v=0\},\qquad
p+T_pM=\{r:\nabla F(p)\cdot(r-p)=0\}.
$$
Differentiating $F(\gamma(t))=0$ proves that every surface <velocity> lies in the displayed <kernel of a linear map>; the <implicit function theorem> gives a local graph with two independent coordinates, proving equality. The <gradient> is the <normal vector> with respect to the ambient <dot product>. https://www.math.toronto.edu/mein/teaching/LectureNotes/Man.pdf[Eckhard Meinrenken, Differentiable Manifolds, Theorem 1.15: regular level sets]{ref}

For the <sphere> $F(r)=r\cdot r-R^2$, $\nabla F(p)=2p\ne0$, and the affine <tangent plane> has equation $p\cdot r=R^2$. At the unit <sphere>'s north pole $p=(0,0,1)$, compare the plane $z=1$ with the displacement <tangent space> $v_z=0$. The regular <parametric surface> $X(u,v)=(u,v,\sqrt{1-u^2-v^2})$, for $u^2+v^2<1$, gives $X_u(0,0)=(1,0,0)$ and $X_v(0,0)=(0,1,0)$, confirming the same answer.

\Image[tangent-vectors-spaces-planes-sphere-plane.png]
{title=The sphere's tangent plane passes through the base point; its tangent space contains displacements}

This analytical illustration shows a unit <sphere> and a vertical cross-section. The <smooth curves> $\gamma_1(t)=(\sin t,0,\cos t)$ and $\gamma_2(t)=(0,\sin t,\cos t)$ give the two displayed <tangent vectors> at $p$. The straight point $p+tv$ for any nonzero $v\in T_pS^2$ is outside the <sphere> when $t\ne0$, since $\|p+tv\|^2=1+t^2\|v\|^2$. A <tangent plane> therefore captures first-order motion, not an exact patch of the <regular surface>.

The same idea makes local approximation useful. For the <graph of a function> $z=f(x,y)$ with $f$ a <smooth function>, the affine <tangent plane> is $z=f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b)$. https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-3/pages/4-4-tangent-planes-and-linear-approximations[Gilbert Strang and Edwin Herman, OpenStax Calculus Volume 3, §4.4: tangent planes and linear approximations]{ref} With continuous second <partial derivatives>, the vertical error is $O((x-a)^2+(y-b)^2)$ in <Big O notation>. On the unit <sphere> near its north pole, $\sqrt{1-u^2-v^2}=1-\tfrac12(u^2+v^2)+O((u^2+v^2)^2)$: the <tangent plane> loses quadratic bending while preserving all first-order <velocities>.

A failed regularity test needs interpretation. Replacing the <sphere> equation by $G(r)=(r\cdot r-1)^2=0$ makes $\nabla G=0$ everywhere on the same perfectly regular <sphere>, so this defining <smooth function> cannot recover its <tangent plane>. Similarly, the usual spherical <parametric surface> loses <matrix rank> at the poles, while the graph parametrization above remains regular there. A vanishing <gradient> or degenerate parametrization invalidates that calculation; it alone does not prove that the underlying geometry has no <tangent plane>.

= Tangent plane requires the point to lie on the surface
{parent=Tangent plane}
{title2=$G(p)=0,\quad\nabla G(p)\ne0$}

For a <level set> $G=0$, the <tangent plane> formula $\nabla G(p)\cdot(x-p)=0$ requires both that $p$ belongs to the <surface> and that its <gradient> is nonzero. Evaluating the gradient at an off-surface point instead gives a plane tangent to the level set through that different point. For example $G=x^2y-4ze^{x+y}+11$ has $G(2,-2,9)=-33$, so the gradient plane there cannot be a tangent plane to $G=0$. Holding the first two coordinates fixed gives the on-surface point $(2,-2,3/4)$ and its plane $11x-y+4z=27$.

= Tangent space by point derivations
{parent=Tangent space}
{title2=$T_xM=\operatorname{Der}_x(C^\infty_x(M),\mathbb R)$}

A tangent vector is a <linear map> on <germs> of smooth functions satisfying $D(ab)=a(x)D(b)+b(x)D(a)$. In a <manifold chart>, first-order expansion shows $Dh=\sum_iD(u^i)\partial_i h(x)$. Thus the coordinate <derivations> form a <basis>, and the <dimension> equals the manifold <dimension>. Composition of <germs> with a <smooth map> defines its <differential of a smooth map> without choosing charts.

= Cotangent space
{parent=Tangent space}
{title2=$T_p^*M$}

The cotangent space at $p$ is the <dual vector space> $T_p^*M=(T_pM)^*$. Its elements are <covectors> on the <tangent space>. In a <manifold chart> $x$, the basis $dx^i|_p$ is dual to $\partial_i|_p$. Under a coordinate change to $y$, the coefficients of $\alpha=a_i dx^i=b_jdy^j$ obey $b_j=\sum_i a_i\partial x^i/\partial y^j$. The disjoint union of these spaces is the <cotangent bundle>.

= Inner product on exterior powers of a cotangent space
{parent=Cotangent space}
{title2=$\langle\xi_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\xi_p,\zeta_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\zeta_p\rangle=\det(\langle\xi_i,\zeta_j\rangle)$}

A <Riemannian metric> induces a dual <inner product> on each <cotangent space>. On decomposable covectors define $\langle\xi_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\xi_p,\zeta_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\zeta_p\rangle=\det(\langle\xi_i,\zeta_j\rangle)$ and extend bilinearly. Increasing wedges of an orthonormal coframe form an orthonormal basis. This positive-definite inner product is the one used to define the <Hodge star operator>.

= Tangent space from a local parametrization
{parent=Tangent space}

If $\phi:U\subseteq\mathbb R^d\to M\subseteq\mathbb R^n$ is a local parametrization with $\phi(u)=p$, then
$$
T_pM=\operatorname{im}D\phi_u.
$$
The derivative has rank $d$, so this is a $d$-dimensional vector subspace. A change of parametrization composes $D\phi_u$ with an invertible coordinate-change derivative, leaving its image unchanged.

= Closedness of tangent spaces under convergent base points
{parent=Tangent space}

For an embedded smooth manifold $M\subseteq\mathbb R^n$, if $p_i\to p$, $w_i\in T_{p_i}M$, and $w_i\to w$, then $w\in T_pM$. In a local graph representation $M=\{(u,g(u))\}$, write $w_i=(v_i,Dg(u_i)v_i)$ and use continuity of $Dg$.

= Coordinate basis
{title2=$\partial/\partial x^\mu$}
{parent=Tangent space}

= Coordinate frame
{synonym}

A <manifold chart> with coordinates $x^\mu$ induces a smooth <local frame> of the <tangent bundle>, with <tangent vectors> $\partial_\mu=\partial/\partial x^\mu$ forming a <basis> of each <tangent space>. Their <Lie brackets of vector fields> vanish because mixed <partial derivatives> of smooth functions commute. Under a change of coordinates they transform by the <chain rule>: $\partial/\partial y^\nu=(\partial x^\mu/\partial y^\nu)\partial/\partial x^\mu$, with coefficients given by the <Jacobian matrix>.

= Unit tangent vector
{title2=$T$}
{parent=Tangent vector}

For a <regular curve> $\mathbf r(t)$ with nonzero <velocity vector>, its unit tangent vector is
$$
T(t)=\frac{\mathbf r'(t)}{\|\mathbf r'(t)\|}.
$$
The <Euclidean norm> in the denominator removes the traversal <speed>, leaving a <unit vector> that records direction. A positive change of traversal <speed> leaves this direction unchanged; reversing the direction of travel reverses $T$. In <arc length> coordinates, $T=d\mathbf r/ds$.

For the <parabola> $\mathbf r(t)=(t,t^2)$, the <velocity vector> is $(1,2t)$ and the <speed> is $\sqrt{1+4t^2}$. At $t=1$, $T=(1,2)^T/\sqrt5$. The <tangent vector> $(1,2)^T$ and the <unit tangent vector> point in the same direction, but only the latter has <Euclidean norm> one. The <principal normal vector> is a further choice based on how this direction changes, where the <curvature> is nonzero. https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-3/pages/3-3-arc-length-and-curvature[OpenStax Calculus Volume 3, §3.3: unit tangent and principal normal]{ref}

= Velocity vector
{parent=Tangent vector}

The velocity vector of a parametrized curve $\mathbf r(t)$ is its tangent vector $\mathbf r'(t)$.
