Put $z_i=(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf v_i)$ and $dz_i=d^3x_i\,d^3v_i$. Normalize the symmetric $N$-particle <phase-space distribution function> $D_N$ to one. Use factorial normalization for its one-particle marginal and <two-particle distribution function>:
$$
f(z_1)=N\int D_N\,dz_2\cdots dz_N,\qquad f_2(z_1,z_2)=N(N-1)\int D_N\,dz_3\cdots dz_N.
$$
Thus $\int f\,dz=N$ and $\int f_2(z_1,z_2)\,dz_2=(N-1)f(z_1)$. For unit particle <mass>, $f$ also carries <mass density>. These conventions explain why no extra factor $N-1$ occurs in the result.

Separate the <Hamiltonian> as $H=H_1+H_{\mathrm{rest}}+H_{\mathrm{int}}$, where
$$
H_1=\frac12|\mathbf v_1|^2,\qquad H_{\mathrm{int}}=\sum_{j=2}^N\phi(|\mathbf x_1-\mathbf x_j|),
$$
and $H_{\mathrm{rest}}$ contains all terms involving only particles $2,\ldots,N$. The stated <Poisson bracket> gives <Hamilton's equations>
$$
\dot{\mathbf x}_i=\mathbf v_i,\qquad \dot{\mathbf v}_i=-\sum_{j\ne i}\nabla_{\mathbf x_i}\phi(|\mathbf x_i-\mathbf x_j|).
$$
Conservation of probability along the <Hamiltonian flow> gives the <Hamiltonian Liouville equation>
$$
\partial_tD_N+\sum_{i=1}^N\left[\mathbf v_i\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf x_i}D_N-\sum_{j\ne i}\nabla_{\mathbf x_i}\phi_{ij}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf v_i}D_N\right]=0.
$$
Assume decay of the required <velocity moments> and either decay or periodic <boundary conditions> in position. Integrating over $z_2,\ldots,z_N$ eliminates the $H_{\mathrm{rest}}$ terms by <integration by parts>. The terms in $H_{\mathrm{int}}$ differentiating an integrated <velocity> also vanish. Indistinguishability makes the remaining $N-1$ terms equal, so
$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_tf+\mathbf v_1\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf x_1}f
&=N\sum_{j=2}^N\int\nabla_{\mathbf x_1}\phi_{1j}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf v_1}D_N\,dz_2\cdots dz_N\\
&=\int dz_2\,\nabla_{\mathbf x_1}\phi_{12}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf v_1}f_2.
\end{aligned}
$$
Relabeling the two particles gives the first <BBGKY hierarchy> equation:
$$
\boxed{\partial_tf+\mathbf v\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf x}f=\int d^3x_\star\,d^3v_\star\,\nabla_{\mathbf v}f_2\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf x}\phi(|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_\star|).}
$$
This is exact for a differentiable <pair potential>; no independent-particle closure has been made.

Take the zeroth <velocity moment> of <1/a>. The right side is a <velocity-space> divergence with vanishing boundary contribution, giving the <continuity equation>
$$
\boxed{\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u)=0,\qquad \rho=\int f\,d^3v,\qquad \rho\mathbf u=\int\mathbf v f\,d^3v.}
$$
For the first <velocity moment>, use <integration by parts> componentwise:
$$
\int d^3v\,v_i\partial_{v_j}f_2=-\delta_{ij}\int d^3v\,f_2.
$$
Write $\mathbf c=\mathbf v-\mathbf u$; then $\int\mathbf c f\,d^3v=0$, and the <momentum flux> splits into bulk transport and the <pressure tensor>:
$$
\int v_iv_jf\,d^3v=\rho u_iu_j+P_{ij},\qquad \boxed{P_{ij}=\int (v_i-u_i)(v_j-u_j)f\,d^3v.}
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\partial_t(\rho u_i)+\partial_{x_j}(\rho u_iu_j+P_{ij})=-\int d^3x_\star\,n_2(\mathbf x,\mathbf x_\star,t)\partial_{x_i}\phi(|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_\star|),}
$$
where repeated Cartesian indices are summed and $n_2=\int f_2\,d^3v\,d^3v_\star$. The negative sign is the attractive or repulsive <force> $-\nabla\phi$, rather than an additional sign convention for the <pressure tensor>.

For fixed $\mathbf R$ and $t$, define $h(\mathbf x,s)=n_2(\mathbf a,\mathbf b,t)$ with $\mathbf a=\mathbf x+(s-1)\mathbf R$ and $\mathbf b=\mathbf x+s\mathbf R$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
\partial_sh=\mathbf R\cdot(\nabla_{\mathbf a}+\nabla_{\mathbf b})n_2,\qquad \nabla_{\mathbf x}h=(\nabla_{\mathbf a}+\nabla_{\mathbf b})n_2,
$$
so $\boxed{\partial_sh=\mathbf R\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf x}h}$. The derivative with respect to $\mathbf x$ here translates *both* arguments of the <two-particle number density>; it is not a derivative of its first argument alone.

Take the spatial divergence of the proposed <interaction pressure tensor>. Its <pair potential> factor is independent of $\mathbf x$, so the preceding identity gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_{x_j}P^\phi_{ij}
&=-\frac12\int d^3R\,\frac{R_i\phi'(R)}R\int_0^1\partial_sh\,ds\\
&=-\frac12\int d^3R\,\frac{R_i\phi'(R)}R\left[n_2(\mathbf x,\mathbf x+\mathbf R)-n_2(\mathbf x-\mathbf R,\mathbf x)\right].
\end{aligned}
$$
Exchange symmetry of the <two-particle distribution function> implies $n_2(\mathbf x-\mathbf R,\mathbf x)=n_2(\mathbf x,\mathbf x-\mathbf R)$. In the second term, substitute $\mathbf R\mapsto-\mathbf R$. Since $R_i\phi'(R)/R$ is odd, both terms become equal:
$$
\partial_{x_j}P^\phi_{ij}=-\int d^3R\,\frac{R_i\phi'(R)}R\,n_2(\mathbf x,\mathbf x+\mathbf R).
$$
On the other hand, at fixed $\mathbf x_\star=\mathbf x+\mathbf R$, the first-argument derivative of the <pair potential> is $\nabla_{\mathbf x}\phi=-\mathbf R\phi'(R)/R$. Thus the interaction <force density> in <1/b> is $-\partial_{x_j}P^\phi_{ij}$. Moving it to the left yields local <conservation of momentum>:
$$
\boxed{\partial_t(\rho\mathbf u)+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u\mathbf u+\mathbf P+\mathbf P^\phi)=0.}
$$
The integral over $s$ distributes each pair's <momentum flux> along the straight segment joining the particles. The factor $1/2$ removes the double counting of ordered pairs. Central <forces> also make $P^\phi_{ij}=P^\phi_{ji}$.

<Spatial homogeneity> makes $\rho$ independent of position and makes the <two-particle number density> depend only on the separation. <Isotropy> removes its directional dependence. Consequently
$$
n_2(\mathbf x,\mathbf x_\star,t)=\rho^2g_2(|\mathbf x_\star-\mathbf x|,t).
$$
Here $g_2$ is a <radial distribution function>, with the finite-$N$ normalization inherited from <1/a>; statistical independence is unnecessary. In particular, the translated pair in <1/c> has separation $\mathbf R$ for every $s$, so its $s$ integral is simply $\rho^2g_2(R,t)$.

<Isotropy> of the random <velocity> $\mathbf c=\mathbf v-\mathbf u$ makes its second <velocity moment> proportional to the identity. Taking its trace fixes the proportionality constant:
$$
\boxed{\theta=\frac1{3\rho}\int|\mathbf v-\mathbf u|^2f\,d^3v,\qquad P_{ij}=\rho\theta\delta_{ij}.}
$$
Thus $\theta$ is the one-component random <velocity> variance; for unit <mass> in <thermal equilibrium>, $\theta=k_BT$. A <Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution> is not required for the <pressure tensor> to be isotropic.

For the interaction contribution, the angular integral is $\int d\Omega\,\widehat R_i\widehat R_j=(4\pi/3)\delta_{ij}$, by <isotropy> and its trace. With $d^3R=R^2\,dR\,d\Omega$, <1/c> becomes
$$
P^\phi_{ij}=-\frac{2\pi}{3}\rho^2\delta_{ij}\int_0^\infty R^3\phi'(R)g_2(R,t)\,dR.
$$
Combining the two <pressure tensors> gives the <virial pressure from pair correlations>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf P+\mathbf P^\phi=p\mathbf I,\qquad p=\rho\theta-\frac{2\pi}{3}\rho^2\int_0^\infty R^3\phi'(R)g_2(R,t)\,dR.}
$$
For a repulsive <pair potential>, the outward radial <force> is $-\phi'(R)\geq0$. Since $g_2\geq0$, the second term in $p$ is **nonnegative**, and positive when occupied separations feel nonzero repulsion. It expresses additional <momentum transfer> through interactions. The displayed integral assumes a differentiable <pair potential> and convergence; a hard core requires a contact formula or a limiting argument.

Use the electron <electric charge> $-e$, so $E=-\partial_x\varphi$ gives acceleration $+(e/m_e)\partial_x\varphi$. The <Vlasov equation>, its average, and its linearized fluctuating part are
$$
\begin{gathered}
\partial_tf+v\partial_xf+\frac e{m_e}\partial_x\varphi\,\partial_vf=0,\\
\partial_tf_0=-\frac e{m_e}\partial_v\langle\partial_x\varphi\,\delta f\rangle,\qquad
(\partial_t+ikv)\delta f_k=-\frac{ike}{m_e}\varphi_k f_0'.
\end{gathered}
$$
The <quasilinear approximation> retains the averaged quadratic term but drops products of fluctuations from the fluctuating equation. It freezes the slowly evolving $f_0$ during the fast response calculation. A real <electrostatic potential> has $\varphi_{-k}=\varphi_k^*$, and the sum includes both signs of every nonzero <wavenumber>. Spatial averaging selects the opposite <Fourier series> harmonics; statistical homogeneity supplies the corresponding diagonal noise covariance.

Let $\widehat g(p)=\int_0^\infty e^{-pt}g(t)\,dt$. Zero initial $\delta f_k$ gives
$$
\widehat{\delta f}_k(p,v)=-\frac{ike}{m_e}\frac{f_0'(v)}{p+ikv}\widehat\varphi_k(p).
$$
Insert this into the electrostatic self-consistency relation. Using $\omega_{pe}^2=4\pi e^2n_e/m_e$ and $p+ikv=ik(v-ip/k)$ gives
$$
\epsilon(p,k)\widehat\varphi_k=\widehat\chi_k,\qquad
\epsilon(p,k)=1-\frac{\omega_{pe}^2}{k^2n_e}\int_{C_L}\frac{f_0'(v')}{v'-ip/k}\,dv'.
$$
This derives the stated <dielectric function> with the <Landau contour>. A noise-free growing collective response is a zero of $\epsilon$.

The <Laplace transform> of the <white noise> covariance is particularly simple. Writing $q=p'^*$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle\widehat\chi_k(p)\widehat\chi_k(p')^*\rangle
&=2A_k\int_0^\infty ds\int_0^\infty ds'\,e^{-ps-qs'}\delta(s-s')\\
&=\frac{2A_k}{p+q}.
\end{aligned}
$$
For clarity about contour signs, introduce the inverse-transform measures
$$
\int_{\mathcal B}\frac{dp}{2\pi i},\qquad
\int_{\mathcal B^*}\frac{dq}{-2\pi i}.
$$
The first <Bromwich inversion formula> contour runs upward on $\operatorname{Re}p=\sigma$; its conjugate contour runs downward on $\operatorname{Re}q=\sigma$. Both measures therefore have the same positive orientation in their real <frequency> parameters. Combining the averaged equation with $\widehat{\delta f}_k$ yields
$$
\partial_tf_0=\partial_v\left[D(v,t)\partial_vf_0\right],\qquad
D=-\frac e{m_e f_0'}\sum_k(-ik)\langle\delta f_k(t,v)\varphi_k(t)^*\rangle.
$$
At a zero of $f_0'$, the last expression means its continuous coefficient obtained before cancelling $f_0'$. Substitution of the two inverse <Laplace transforms> and the transformed covariance gives
$$
\boxed{D(v,t)=\frac{e^2}{m_e^2}\sum_k\frac{k^2A_k}{2\pi^2}\int_{\mathcal B}dp\int_{\mathcal B^*}dp'^*\,\frac{e^{(p+p'^*)t}}{(p+ikv)(p+p'^*)\epsilon(p,k)\epsilon(p',k)^*}.}
$$
This is the paper's expression, with the orientation of $dp'^*$ made explicit. Reality follows by pairing $k$ and $-k$. The double integral retains switch-on transients; replacing it by a time-independent <diffusion coefficient> requires the additional long-time limit in <2/b>.

A linearly stable $f_0$ has no zeros of the causal <dielectric function> in $\operatorname{Re}p>0$. Hence the inverse <Laplace transform> contours can approach $\sigma=0^+$, retaining the causal boundary value at $p=-i\omega+0^+$. To obtain a stationary forced response, further assume that the excited collective perturbations actually damp. This is the decay condition in the question's long-time instruction.

A transparent evaluation of the double integral uses the stationary <power spectral density>. The <white noise> covariance $2A_k\delta(t-t')$ has temporal spectrum $2A_k$. Linear filtering by $1/\epsilon$ therefore gives
$$
S_{\varphi,k}(\omega)=\frac{2A_k}{|\epsilon(-i\omega+0^+,k)|^2},\qquad
\langle\varphi_k(t-\tau)\varphi_k(t)^*\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,S_{\varphi,k}(\omega)e^{i\omega\tau}.
$$
The causal solution for $\delta f_k$ in <2/a> gives, after the initial transients have decayed,
$$
D(v)=\frac{e^2}{m_e^2}\sum_k k^2\operatorname{Re}\int_0^\infty d\tau\,e^{-ikv\tau}\langle\varphi_k(t-\tau)\varphi_k(t)^*\rangle.
$$
The real part can be taken because the full $k$ sum pairs conjugates. Using the <distribution theory> identity $\operatorname{Re}\int_0^\infty e^{ia\tau}\,d\tau=\pi\delta(a)$, with a positive vanishing convergence factor, samples the temporal spectrum at $\omega=kv$:
$$
\boxed{D(v)=\frac{e^2}{m_e^2}\sum_k\frac{k^2A_k}{|\epsilon(-ikv+0^+,k)|^2}.}
$$
The factor $1/2$ from the one-sided time integral cancels the $2$ in the noise covariance. The result is nonnegative, as a stationary <velocity-space diffusion> coefficient should be.

**Qualification of stability.** Absence of growing modes alone does not guarantee this stationary limit. For example, the electron <waterbag distribution> $f_0(v)=n_e\mathbf1_{|v|<V}/(2V)$ has
$$
\epsilon(-i\omega,k)=1-\frac{\omega_{pe}^2}{\omega^2-k^2V^2},\qquad \omega^2=k^2V^2+\omega_{pe}^2
$$
as undamped collective roots. <White noise> drives their field variance to grow instead of settling. Thus the formula requires decay of excited modes, not merely absence of exponentially growing ones. For weak <Landau damping>, the waiting time can be much longer than $\omega_{pe}^{-1}$, and must still be short compared with evolution of $f_0$ for the frozen-background <quasilinear approximation> to apply.

For a regular thermal $f_0$ and noise at $k\lambda_{\mathrm{De}}\gg1$, the susceptibility $\epsilon-1$ is small at thermal <phase velocities>: its scale is $\omega_{pe}^2/(k^2v_{\mathrm{the}}^2)$. To leading order the electrons cannot appreciably screen such short-scale fields, and <2/b> becomes independent of <velocity>:
$$
D(v)\simeq D_0=\frac{e^2}{m_e^2}\sum_k k^2A_k.
$$
The electron <kinetic energy> per unit volume is $\mathcal K=(m_e/2)\int v^2f_0\,dv$. Assume $v^2D f_0'$ and $vf_0$ vanish at infinity. One <integration by parts> gives the general heating formula
$$
\frac{d\mathcal K}{dt}=-m_e\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}vD(v)f_0'(v)\,dv.
$$
For constant $D_0$, a second <integration by parts> and $\int f_0\,dv=n_e$ give
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\mathcal K}{dt}=m_en_eD_0=\frac{e^2n_e}{m_e}\sum_k k^2A_k.}
$$
This is the leading short-<wavelength> result. With vanishing boundary flux, the <diffusion equation> also conserves particle number; constant $D_0$ conserves the mean <momentum> while increasing the random <kinetic energy>.

Set $\kappa=|k|$ and first work with a representative positive $k=\kappa$. For $\kappa\lambda_{\mathrm{De}}\ll1$, the thermal core is strongly screened. However, the causal <dielectric function> has weakly damped <Langmuir wave> roots $\omega_s+i\gamma_s$, with $s=\pm1$, $\omega_s\simeq s\omega_{pe}$ and $\gamma_s<0$. Their <phase velocities> $v_s=\omega_s/\kappa$ are in the tail. Both roots must be retained for each spatial <Fourier series> harmonic.

Write $\epsilon(-i\omega+0^+,\kappa)=\epsilon_R(\omega)+i\epsilon_I(\omega)$. The supplied approximation gives
$$
\epsilon_R\simeq1-\frac{\omega_{pe}^2}{\omega^2},\qquad \epsilon_I\simeq-\frac{\pi\omega_{pe}^2}{\kappa^2n_e}f_0'(\omega/\kappa),\qquad \epsilon_R'(\omega_s)\simeq\frac2{\omega_s}.
$$
Expanding $\epsilon(\omega_s+i\gamma_s)=0$ to first order gives the <Landau damping> rate
$$
\gamma_s=-\frac{\epsilon_I(\omega_s)}{\epsilon_R'(\omega_s)}\simeq\frac{\pi\omega_s\omega_{pe}^2}{2\kappa^2n_e}f_0'(v_s).
$$
In particular, stable damping means $\omega_sf_0'(v_s)<0$. Near either root,
$$
|\epsilon(-i\kappa v+0^+,\kappa)|^2\simeq\frac4{\omega_{pe}^2}\left[(\kappa v-\omega_s)^2+\gamma_s^2\right].
$$
The narrow <Lorentzian resonance approximation> then gives the leading resonant contribution
$$
\frac1{|\epsilon(-i\kappa v+0^+,\kappa)|^2}\simeq\frac{\pi\omega_{pe}^2}{4}\sum_{s=\pm1}\frac{\delta(\kappa v-\omega_s)}{|\gamma_s|}.
$$
This is a formula under the <integral> over <velocity>; it is not a pointwise approximation away from the <resonances>. Insert it into the heating formula in <2/c>. For one spatial harmonic, the contribution from one root is
$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_{k,s}
&=-\frac{e^2}{m_e}k^2A_k\frac{\pi\omega_{pe}^2}{4\kappa}\frac{v_sf_0'(v_s)}{|\gamma_s|}\\
&=\frac{e^2n_e}{2m_e}k^2A_k,
\end{aligned}
$$
because
$$
\frac{v_sf_0'(v_s)}{|\gamma_s|}=-\frac{2\kappa n_e}{\pi\omega_{pe}^2}.
$$
Thus the tiny tail slope cancels the tiny <Landau damping> rate: weak absorption is compensated by a large noise-driven wave intensity. The two temporal roots supply two equal halves of the answer. Negative $k$ gives the same result by the conjugate response of a real <electric field>, without altering the original convention that the spatial sum includes both signs. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\mathcal K}{dt}=\sum_k\sum_{s=\pm1}Q_{k,s}\simeq\frac{e^2n_e}{m_e}\sum_k k^2A_k.}
$$
As in <2/c>, equality means the leading result in the stated asymptotic limit. This calculation assumes nonzero damping, observation times $t\gg\max_s|\gamma_s|^{-1}$, and forcing weak enough that $f_0$ changes negligibly during that relaxation. Taking zero damping before the long-time limit invalidates the stationary calculation.

The long-<wavelength> limit in <2/d> is **<resonant heating>**. Most electrons screen the slowly varying spatial field. The noise nevertheless excites collective <Langmuir waves>, whose sharp temporal spectrum lies near $\omega=\pm\omega_{pe}$. Electrons with $v\simeq\omega/k$ remain in phase with a wave and exchange <energy> with it. A stable tail absorbs net wave <energy> through <Landau damping>. The heating is initially deposited in narrow tail regions of <velocity space>, even though the volume-integrated rate equals that in <2/c>.

The short-<wavelength> limit in <2/c> is **<stochastic heating>** in the terminology intended here. With $\epsilon\simeq1$, the imposed <electric field> is essentially unscreened. Its temporally uncorrelated kicks cause a random walk of electron <velocity>, so $\langle(\Delta v)^2\rangle=2D_0\Delta t$. Electrons throughout the thermal population contribute to the resulting <kinetic energy> increase; no narrow collective wave peak selects a small tail population.

Both mechanisms are driven by <white noise>, and the formal condition $\omega=kv$ enters the stationary <quasilinear diffusion> calculation in both cases. The physical distinction is the sharp collective <resonance> in <2/d> versus the broad direct random forcing in <2/c>, rather than the mere presence of randomness. In collisionless theory this heating is an increase of the averaged <kinetic energy>; subsequent <collisions> can establish <thermal equilibrium>.

For an integrable or approximately integrable background <Hamiltonian>, <action-angle variables> turn an otherwise complicated <stellar orbit> into $\dot{\mathbf J}=0$ and $\dot{\boldsymbol\theta}=\boldsymbol\Omega(\mathbf J)$. This has two useful consequences. First, a phase-mixed equilibrium can be represented directly as $f_0(\mathbf J)$: the <action variables> label the unperturbed <orbits>, while <angle variables> describe position along them. Second, the angles are periodic, so <Fourier series> reduce unperturbed transport to multiplication by $i\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega$ and make <orbital resonances> explicit. The <action variables> also separate rapid orbital motion from slow secular changes. These advantages apply on regular orbital regions; a globally chaotic background need not admit global <action-angle variables>.

The transformation is canonical, preserving the <phase-space> measure and the stated total <mass> $M=\int f\,d^3\theta\,d^3J$. The collisionless <Vlasov equation> is
$$
\boxed{\partial_tf+\{f,H\}_{\theta,J}=\partial_tf+\frac{\partial H}{\partial\mathbf J}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta}-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf J}=0.}
$$
Here $H$ is the single-star specific <Hamiltonian>; the normalization of $f$ by <mass> changes neither the <Poisson bracket> nor the transport equation.

Since $H_0$ and $f_0$ depend only on the <action variables>, $\{f_0,H_0\}=0$. Drop the quadratic term $\{\delta f,\delta\Phi^{\mathrm{tot}}\}$ from the <Vlasov equation> in <3/a>. Defining the orbital <frequency> vector by $\boldsymbol\Omega(\mathbf J)=\partial H_0/\partial\mathbf J$, the linearized equation is

The sign of the driving term follows from the second term in the canonical <Poisson bracket>, because the background has no angle dependence.

Use the stated <Fourier series> normalization, so $g(\boldsymbol\theta,\mathbf J,t)=\sum_{\mathbf n}g_{\mathbf n}(\mathbf J,t)e^{i\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\theta}$. Then <3/b> gives
$$
\partial_t\delta f_{\mathbf n}+i\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega\,\delta f_{\mathbf n}-i\mathbf n\cdot\frac{\partial f_0}{\partial\mathbf J}\,\delta\Phi_{\mathbf n}^{\mathrm{tot}}=0.
$$
For the <Laplace transform> with kernel $e^{i\omega t}$, <integration by parts> yields
$$
\int_0^\infty e^{i\omega t}\partial_t\delta f_{\mathbf n}\,dt=-\delta f_{\mathbf n}(0)-i\omega\widetilde{\delta f}_{\mathbf n}.
$$
The upper boundary vanishes for sufficiently large $\operatorname{Im}\omega>0$, and the initial term is zero by hypothesis. Thus
$$
i(\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega-\omega)\widetilde{\delta f}_{\mathbf n}=i\mathbf n\cdot\frac{\partial f_0}{\partial\mathbf J}\,\widetilde{\delta\Phi}_{\mathbf n}^{\mathrm{tot}},
$$
or
$$
\boxed{\widetilde{\delta f}_{\mathbf n}(\mathbf J,\omega)=-\mathbf n\cdot\frac{\partial f_0}{\partial\mathbf J}\frac{\widetilde{\delta\Phi}_{\mathbf n}^{\mathrm{tot}}(\mathbf J,\omega)}{\omega-\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega(\mathbf J)},\qquad \boldsymbol\Omega=\frac{\partial H_0}{\partial\mathbf J}.}
$$
The denominator identifies the <orbital resonance> between the perturbation and unperturbed orbital <frequencies>. The zero harmonic has no driving term and remains zero with these initial data.

= Solution

In the periodic cube, $H_0=|\mathbf v|^2/2=(2\pi/L)^2|\mathbf J|^2/2$, so $\boldsymbol\Omega=(2\pi/L)\mathbf v$. The supplied <action-angle variables> are canonical: $d^3\theta\,d^3J=d^3x\,d^3v$. For $\mathbf k=2\pi\mathbf n/L$,
$$
\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega=\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf v,\qquad \mathbf n\cdot\partial_{\mathbf J}f_0=\mathbf k\cdot\partial_{\mathbf v}f_0.
$$
Consequently <3/c> becomes
$$
\widetilde{\delta f}_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf v,\omega)=\frac{\mathbf k\cdot\partial_{\mathbf v}f_0}{\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf v-\omega}\widetilde{\delta\Phi}_{\mathbf k}^{\mathrm{tot}}(\omega).
$$
For each nonzero <wavenumber>, the <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity> gives
$$
-k^2\widetilde{\delta\Phi}_{\mathbf k}=4\pi G\int\widetilde{\delta f}_{\mathbf k}\,d^3v.
$$
Substitute the preceding response and add the externally imposed <Newtonian gravitational potential>:
$$
\widetilde{\delta\Phi}_{\mathbf k}^{\mathrm{tot}}=\widetilde{\delta\Phi}_{\mathbf k}^{\mathrm{ext}}-\frac{4\pi G}{k^2}\widetilde{\delta\Phi}_{\mathbf k}^{\mathrm{tot}}\int d^3v\,\frac{\mathbf k\cdot\partial_{\mathbf v}f_0}{\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf v-\omega}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\widetilde{\delta\Phi}_{\mathbf k}^{\mathrm{tot}}=\frac{\widetilde{\delta\Phi}_{\mathbf k}^{\mathrm{ext}}}{\epsilon_{\mathbf k}(\omega)},\qquad \epsilon_{\mathbf k}(\omega)=1+\frac{4\pi G}{k^2}\int d^3v\,\frac{\mathbf k\cdot\partial_{\mathbf v}f_0}{\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf v-\omega}.}
$$
This <gravitational dielectric function> has the opposite susceptibility sign to the electron <dielectric function>, reflecting attractive rather than repulsive collective interactions. The homogeneous mean field is explicitly subtracted in this fictitious periodic model: the <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity> acts on density fluctuations, and the $\mathbf k=0$ mode is omitted. Otherwise a nonzero uniform positive <mass density> would be incompatible with a periodic potential. This is the convention often called the <Jeans swindle>.

For <analytic continuation>, resolve <velocity> parallel to $\mathbf k$: let $u=\widehat{\mathbf k}\cdot\mathbf v$ and $F(u)=\int f_0(u,\mathbf v_\perp)\,d^2v_\perp$. Then
$$
\epsilon_{\mathbf k}(\omega)=1+\frac{4\pi G}{k^2}\int\frac{F'(u)}{u-\omega/k}\,du,\qquad k=|\mathbf k|.
$$
Start at $\operatorname{Im}\omega>0$ and deform the <Landau contour> continuously below the moving pole. At real $\omega$, the integral is
$$
\operatorname{PV}\int\frac{F'(u)}{u-\omega/k}\,du+i\pi F'(\omega/k).
$$
For $\operatorname{Im}\omega<0$, this causal continuation equals the undeformed real-axis integral plus $2\pi iF'(\omega/k)$ wherever $F'$ has the necessary analytic extension. It must not be replaced by the undeformed integral alone.

Zeros of $\epsilon_{\mathbf k}$ are self-consistent collective <Landau modes>: with no external forcing, a nonzero <Newtonian gravitational potential> is possible at such a root. For time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$, upper-half-plane roots grow, lower-half-plane roots describe <Landau damping>, and real roots are neutral. Damped <Landau modes> are poles of the continued response; they need not be regular discrete eigenfunctions of the original collisionless operator.

**Regularity qualification.** Continuation throughout the complex plane presupposes suitable analytic $F$. Arbitrary smooth $F$ need not allow it. For example, $F(u)=C\exp[-1/(1-u^2)]$ for $|u|<1$ and zero elsewhere is smooth but not analytic at $u=\pm1$; its Cauchy integral cannot be holomorphically continued across those endpoints. The <Landau contour> gives the standard causal continuation on the domains where the equilibrium permits it, with possible singularities retained.

Represent the perturber by the periodic <Dirac delta function> at $\mathbf x_p+\mathbf v_pt$. With spatial <Fourier series> coefficient normalized by $L^{-3}$, its nonzero density coefficients are
$$
\rho_{p,\mathbf k}(t)=\frac{m_p}{L^3}e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x_p}e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf v_pt},\qquad t\geq0.
$$
The mean density is subtracted as in <3/d>. The <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity> gives
$$
\delta\Phi_{\mathbf k}^{\mathrm{ext}}(t)=C_{\mathbf k}e^{-iat},\qquad C_{\mathbf k}=-\frac{4\pi Gm_p}{L^3k^2}e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x_p},\qquad a=\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf v_p.
$$
The perturbation is zero before switch-on. For $\operatorname{Im}\omega>0$, its <Laplace transform> is
$$
\int_0^\infty e^{i(\omega-a)t}\,dt=-\frac1{i(\omega-a)}=\frac1{i(a-\omega)}.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\widetilde{\delta\Phi}_{\mathbf k}^{\mathrm{ext}}(\omega)=-\frac{4\pi Gm_p}{L^3k^2}e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x_p}\frac1{i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf v_p-\omega)}.}
$$
The switch-on creates a forcing pole at the real <frequency> $a$, approached causally from the original upper inversion contour.

With $C_{\mathbf k}$ and $a$ from <3/e>, inversion gives
$$
\delta\Phi_{\mathbf k}^{\mathrm{tot}}(t)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\operatorname{Im}\omega=\eta>0}d\omega\,e^{-i\omega t}\frac{iC_{\mathbf k}}{(\omega-a)\epsilon_{\mathbf k}(\omega)}.
$$
For $t>0$, deform the contour downwards. Closing below is clockwise, so the <residue theorem> multiplies the sum of enclosed residues by $-i$. The forcing pole has residue $iC_{\mathbf k}e^{-iat}/\epsilon_{\mathbf k}(a+i0^+)$ and therefore contributes
$$
\frac{C_{\mathbf k}e^{-iat}}{\epsilon_{\mathbf k}(a+i0^+)}.
$$
A simple collective root $\omega_j$ of the continued <gravitational dielectric function> contributes
$$
\frac{C_{\mathbf k}e^{-i\omega_jt}}{(\omega_j-a)\epsilon_{\mathbf k}'(\omega_j)}.
$$
These are the <Landau mode> transients excited by introducing the mass abruptly. For a strictly damped background they decay, together with any remaining phase-mixing contour contribution under the usual regularity and decay assumptions. Thus
$$
\boxed{\delta\Phi_{\mathbf k}^{\mathrm{tot}}(t)\sim\frac{\delta\Phi_{\mathbf k}^{\mathrm{ext}}(t)}{\epsilon_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf v_p+i0^+)},\qquad t\to\infty.}
$$
The causal boundary value is what the paper abbreviates by $\epsilon_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf v_p)$. Physically, the moving mass polarizes the stellar population and carries a <gravitational wake>. Each spatial harmonic is changed in amplitude and phase by $1/\epsilon_{\mathbf k}$ at the forcing <frequency>. <Self-gravity> can amplify the wake. The induced part alone is $(1/\epsilon_{\mathbf k}-1)\delta\Phi_{\mathbf k}^{\mathrm{ext}}$; the wake's back-reaction on the perturber produces <dynamical friction>.

The waiting time is controlled by the **slowest excited collective damping**, not just an orbital or crossing time. If $\omega_j=\omega_{rj}-i\Gamma_j$ with $\Gamma_j>0$, then a modal amplitude is suppressed by $e^{-\Gamma_jt}$, and one needs $t\Gamma_j\gg1$ for every appreciably excited mode, together with decay of the phase-mixing remainder. Large transient prefactors can require additional time. Near marginal stability, a relevant $\Gamma_j$ tends to zero and the required waiting time diverges; the stationary wake approximation is then not uniform in the stability parameter.<The linear response of stellar systems does not diverge at marginal stability by Chris Hamilton and Tobias Heinemann>[Sections 2.1–2.2: forcing-pole and Landau-mode contributions, and the nonuniform late-time limit]{ref}

**Qualification of stability.** If stable means only non-growing, a persistent neutral mode invalidates the asserted asymptote. A concrete example is a homogeneous <waterbag distribution> with parallel marginal $F(u)=\rho\mathbf1_{|u|<V}/(2V)$. Its <gravitational dielectric function> is
$$
\epsilon_k(\omega)=1-\frac{4\pi G\rho}{k^2V^2-\omega^2}.
$$
For $k^2V^2>4\pi G\rho$, the roots $\omega=\pm\sqrt{k^2V^2-4\pi G\rho}$ are real, so the switch-on transients persist although that harmonic has no growing mode. Taking the smallest box <wavenumber> to satisfy the same inequality makes the whole waterbag model free of growing harmonics. The claimed late-time formula therefore requires damping of all excited modes and $\epsilon_k(a)\ne0$; exact forcing at an undamped root instead produces a secular response.
