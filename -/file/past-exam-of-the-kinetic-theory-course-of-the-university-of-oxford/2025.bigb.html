= Solution
{parent=a}

Normalize the symmetric $N$-particle <phase-space distribution function> $D_N$ to one and write $z_i=(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf v_i)$, $dz_i=d^3x_i\,d^3v_i$. The number-normalized one- and <two-particle distribution functions> are
$$
f(z_1,t)=N\int D_N\,dz_2\cdots dz_N,\qquad f_2(z_1,z_2,t)=N(N-1)\int D_N\,dz_3\cdots dz_N.
$$
Thus $\int f\,dz=N$ and $\int f_2(z,z_\star)\,dz_\star=(N-1)f(z)$. These conventions absorb the otherwise explicit factor $N-1$ in the first <BBGKY hierarchy> equation.

Separate the <Hamiltonian> into <kinetic energy>, external <potential energy>, and <pair potential> terms. Their <Hamilton's equations> give
$$
\dot{\mathbf x}_i=\mathbf v_i,\qquad \dot{\mathbf v}_i=-\nabla U(\mathbf x_i)-\sum_{j\ne i}\nabla_{\mathbf x_i}\phi(|\mathbf x_i-\mathbf x_j|).
$$
The sign follows directly from the stated <Poisson bracket>: $\dot v_{i\alpha}=\{v_{i\alpha},H\}=-\partial_{x_{i\alpha}}H$. The <Hamiltonian Liouville equation> is consequently
$$
\partial_tD_N+\sum_i\left[\mathbf v_i\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf x_i}D_N-\left(\nabla U_i+\sum_{j\ne i}\nabla_{\mathbf x_i}\phi_{ij}\right)\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf v_i}D_N\right]=0.
$$
Integrate over $z_2,\ldots,z_N$ and multiply by $N$. Assume sufficient decay in <velocity space> and either decay or periodic <boundary conditions> in position, so that <integration by parts> removes all transport terms with $i\geq2$. The surviving interaction term is
$$
N\sum_{j=2}^N\int \nabla_{\mathbf x_1}\phi_{1j}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf v_1}D_N\,dz_2\cdots dz_N
=\int\nabla_{\mathbf x_1}\phi_{12}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf v_1}f_2\,dz_2,
$$
where indistinguishability makes all $N-1$ terms equal. Relabeling $(z_1,z_2)$ as $(z,z_\star)$ yields the exact result
$$
\boxed{\partial_tf+\mathbf v\cdot\nabla f-\nabla U\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf v}f=\int d^3x_\star\,d^3v_\star\,\nabla_{\mathbf x}\phi(|\mathbf x-\mathbf x_\star|)\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf v}f_2.}
$$
No assumption of <molecular chaos for the Boltzmann equation> has entered: the dependence on the <two-particle distribution function> is still exact.

To obtain the <Boltzmann equation>, take a dilute <gas> with a short-range <pair potential>. Its interaction range is small compared with the <mean free path> and the spatial scale on which the <phase-space distribution function> changes. The <binary collision approximation> treats encounters as isolated <elastic collisions>; simultaneous encounters involving three or more particles can be neglected. The encounter duration is short compared with the <collision relaxation time> and macroscopic evolution time, and the external <force> changes negligibly during an encounter. This permits local, effectively instantaneous scattering at one position $\mathbf x$.

The essential statistical closure is <molecular chaos for the Boltzmann equation>: the distribution of *incoming* pairs factorizes as $ff_\star$. Outgoing pairs need not be independent. Follow each incoming pair through its two-body scattering, and use conservation of <momentum> and <kinetic energy>, together with time reversal and the invariant scattering measure, to express both gain and loss in the same variables. This gives
$$
C_B[f](\mathbf v)=\int d^3v_\star\int_{\mathrm{hemisphere}}d\Omega\,B(g,\theta)\left(f'f'_\star-ff_\star\right),\qquad g=|\mathbf v-\mathbf v_\star|.
$$
Here $d\Omega=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi$; when the measure is written as $d\theta\,d\varphi$, its Jacobian is included in the <Boltzmann scattering kernel>. In a scattering-angle convention, that kernel contains relative <speed> times differential <scattering cross-section>. The hemisphere prevents double counting equivalent outcomes for indistinguishable particles; the precise factor is included in $B$. The primed <velocities> are connected to the unprimed ones by the reversible <elastic collision> map. This closure is appropriate for a dilute short-range <gas>, rather than a generic long-range interacting system with an important collective <mean-field theory> contribution.

The further replacement by the <BGK model> is a phenomenological relaxation closure. Replace the full <nonlinear Boltzmann collision operator> by relaxation towards a <local Maxwellian>, with a single, velocity-independent <collision relaxation time> $\tau>0$:
$$
C_{\mathrm{BGK}}[f]=-\frac{f-f^{(0)}}{\tau},\qquad f^{(0)}=\frac{\rho}{(2\pi\Theta)^{3/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{|\mathbf v-\mathbf u|^2}{2\Theta}\right).
$$
It models relaxation towards a <local Maxwellian> without resolving the detailed scattering. Treating $\tau$ as constant is an additional simplification; it is of the order of a mean time between effective collisions. Near a <local Maxwellian>, departures in the nonconserved <velocity moments> decay on this timescale.

Choose $f^{(0)}$ to match the <collision invariants> $1$, $\mathbf v$ and $|\mathbf v|^2$ of $f$. For unit particle <mass>, this means
$$
\boxed{\rho=\int f\,d^3v,\qquad \mathbf u=\rho^{-1}\int\mathbf v f\,d^3v,\qquad \Theta=\frac{1}{3\rho}\int|\mathbf v-\mathbf u|^2f\,d^3v.}
$$
Here $\rho$ is the <mass density>, $\mathbf u$ is the <mean velocity>, and $\Theta$ is the <temperature> in squared-<velocity> units, $\Theta=k_BT/m$ before setting $m=1$. In particular,
$$
\int (1,\mathbf v,|\mathbf v|^2)\,C_{\mathrm{BGK}}[f],d^3v=0.
$$
Thus relaxation preserves local <mass>, <momentum> and <kinetic energy> rather than relaxing them to externally prescribed values.

Use the <BGK model> from <1/b>. Multiply it by $v_i$ and integrate over <velocity space>. Matching the <collision invariants> makes the collision contribution vanish. For the external <force> term, <integration by parts> gives
$$
-\partial_jU\int v_i\partial_{v_j}f\,d^3v=\rho\,\partial_iU.
$$
Define the <momentum flux> and <force density> by
$$
\Pi_{ij}=\int v_iv_jf\,d^3v,\qquad F_i=-\rho\,\partial_iU.
$$
The resulting <momentum conservation> equation is
$$
\boxed{\partial_t(\rho u_i)+\partial_j\Pi_{ij}=F_i,\qquad \boldsymbol\Pi=\rho\mathbf u\mathbf u+\mathbf P,\qquad \mathbf F=-\rho\nabla U.}
$$
The <pressure tensor> is the second central <velocity moment>,
$$
P_{ij}=\int(v_i-u_i)(v_j-u_j)f\,d^3v.
$$
The zeroth <velocity moment> also supplies the <continuity equation> $\partial_t\rho+\partial_j(\rho u_j)=0$, which will be needed in <1/d>.

= Solution

Write $c_i=v_i-u_i$ and introduce the raw third <velocity moment> $M_{ijk}$ and the <third central velocity moment> $Q_{ijk}$:
$$
M_{ijk}=\int v_iv_jv_kf\,d^3v,\qquad \boxed{Q_{ijk}=\int c_ic_jc_kf\,d^3v.}
$$
The expansion of their product gives
$$
M_{ijk}=\rho u_iu_ju_k+u_iP_{jk}+u_jP_{ik}+u_kP_{ij}+Q_{ijk}.
$$
The <heat flux> is $q_i=Q_{ijj}/2$ for unit <mass>.

Multiply the <BGK model> by $v_iv_j$. The external <force> term is
$$
-\partial_kU\int v_iv_j\partial_{v_k}f\,d^3v=\rho(u_j\partial_iU+u_i\partial_jU).
$$
Therefore the raw second <velocity moment> satisfies
$$
\partial_t\Pi_{ij}+\partial_kM_{ijk}=-\rho(u_i\partial_jU+u_j\partial_iU)-\frac{\Pi_{ij}-\Pi_{ij}^{(0)}}{\tau}.
$$
The matching <local Maxwellian> has $\Pi_{ij}^{(0)}=\rho u_iu_j+\rho\Theta\delta_{ij}$, so
$$
\Pi_{ij}-\Pi_{ij}^{(0)}=P_{ij}-P_{ij}^{(0)},\qquad P_{ij}^{(0)}=\rho\Theta\delta_{ij}.
$$
To remove the bulk motion, let $D_t=\partial_t+u_k\partial_k$ be the <material derivative>. The <continuity equation> and the <momentum conservation> equation imply
$$
\rho D_tu_i=-\partial_kP_{ik}-\rho\partial_iU,
$$
and hence
$$
\partial_t(\rho u_iu_j)+\partial_k(\rho u_iu_ju_k)=-u_j\partial_kP_{ik}-u_i\partial_kP_{jk}-\rho(u_i\partial_jU+u_j\partial_iU).
$$
Subtract this identity from the raw second <velocity moment> equation and insert the expansion of $M_{ijk}$. The terms $u_i\partial_kP_{jk}$ and $u_j\partial_kP_{ik}$ cancel by the <product rule>. The external <force> terms also cancel, since a common acceleration changes the local <mean velocity> without directly changing the relative <velocities>. What remains is
$$
\boxed{\partial_tP_{ij}+\partial_k(u_kP_{ij}+Q_{ijk})+P_{ik}\partial_ku_j+P_{jk}\partial_ku_i=-\frac{P_{ij}-\rho\Theta\delta_{ij}}{\tau}.}
$$
The trace of the relaxation term is zero because $P_{ii}=3\rho\Theta$. Thus the <BGK model> relaxes anisotropy of the <pressure tensor> while preserving the local random <kinetic energy>. The evolution is still a <velocity-moment hierarchy>, since $Q_{ijk}$ requires further information about $f$.

Take $k>0$ initially; real solutions for $k<0$ follow by $(k,\omega)\mapsto(-k,-\omega)$. Put $V=v_0+v_2-v_1$. Approaching the real <phase velocity> from the upper half-plane, the <Landau contour> and the <Sokhotski–Plemelj formula> give the following <Cauchy principal value> decomposition
$$
\int_{C_L}\frac{f_0'(v)}{v-u}\,dv=\operatorname{PV}\int\frac{f_0'(v)}{v-u}\,dv+i\pi f_0'(u).
$$
The real <frequency> condition $\epsilon=0$ therefore requires $f_0'(u)=0$. This is possible in the interior of the <velocity plateau> and outside the occupied <velocity> range:
$$
\boxed{u<-v_0,\qquad v_1<u<v_2,\qquad u>V.}
$$
These are candidate intervals, not yet solutions of the real <dispersion relation>. The endpoints have jumps in $f_0'$ and logarithmic singularities in the response; they are not finite-$k$ undamped modes. At the cusp $u=0$, the slope is also discontinuous and the real response diverges, so it supplies no additional mode.

The physical condition is absence of net resonant energy exchange with particles moving at the wave's <phase velocity>. Outside the support there are no such particles. Within a <velocity plateau>, the equal populations on either side of the resonance cancel <Landau damping>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let $A_0=f_0(0)$, $\delta=v_2-v_1$, $V=v_0+\delta$ and $K=k^2\lambda_{\mathrm{De}}^2$. Use the <Debye length> convention of the paper, $\lambda_{\mathrm{De}}=v_0/\omega_{\mathrm{pe}}$. On the candidate intervals in <2/a>, the <Landau contour> contributes no imaginary part. The piecewise constant slopes give
$$
\begin{aligned}
I(u)&=\int\frac{f_0'(v)}{v-u}\,dv\\
&=\frac{A_0}{v_0}\left[\int_{-v_0}^{0}\frac{dv}{v-u}-\int_0^{v_1}\frac{dv}{v-u}-\int_{v_2}^{V}\frac{dv}{v-u}\right]\\
&=\frac{A_0}{v_0}\left[2\ln|u|-\ln|v_0+u|-\ln|v_1-u|-\ln|V-u|+\ln|v_2-u|\right].
\end{aligned}
$$
Since $n_0=v_0A_0$, setting the <dielectric function> to zero yields
$$
\boxed{\ln\frac{u^2|v_2-u|}{|v_0+u|\,|v_1-u|\,|V-u|}=K.}
$$
The absolute values express the real response on the allowed intervals. Solving this real equation inside a sloping portion of $f_0$ would not satisfy the imaginary part of the <dielectric function>.

An exact algebraic form will also make the number of undamped branches clear. Define
$$
A=v_0^2+\delta(v_0-v_1),\qquad B=v_0v_1V,\qquad q=1-e^{-K}.
$$
On each allowed interval, the quantity inside the logarithm equals $u^2(u-v_2)/[(u+v_0)(u-v_1)(u-V)]$. Exponentiating and using
$$
(u+v_0)(u-v_1)(u-V)=u^2(u-v_2)-Au+B
$$
gives the <cubic equation>
$$
q\,u^2(u-v_2)-Au+B=0.
$$
For every $k>0$, the logarithm runs from $0$ to $+\infty$ on $(-\infty,-v_0)$, from $+\infty$ to $-\infty$ on $(v_1,v_2)$, and from $+\infty$ to $0$ on $(V,\infty)$. The <intermediate value theorem> supplies a root in each interval, and the <cubic equation> permits at most three. Thus there is exactly one undamped branch in each interval.

For $K=k^2\lambda_{\mathrm{De}}^2\gg1$, the logarithm in <2/b> must be large and positive. In each allowed interval this happens near a denominator zero, approached from the undamped side:
$$
u_-=-v_0-O(e^{-K}),\qquad u_{\mathrm{a}}=v_1+O(e^{-K}),\qquad u_+=V+O(e^{-K}).
$$
The constants hidden by $O(e^{-K})$ have <velocity> units. Consequently,
$$
\boxed{\omega_-\sim-kv_0,\qquad \omega_{\mathrm{a}}\sim kv_1,\qquad \omega_+\sim k(v_0+v_2-v_1).}
$$
The two outer modes have <phase velocities> just outside the occupied range and therefore have no resonant particles. The middle <electron acoustic wave> lies just inside the lower edge of the <velocity plateau>, where $f_0'=0$. All three escape <Landau damping> in this collisionless model. The stated neglect of ion response remains necessary for the middle mode as well.

= Solution

For the two outer branches, $|u|\gg v_0$ at small $k$. An <integration by parts> and a large-$u$ expansion give
$$
I(u)=\int\frac{f_0(v)}{(v-u)^2}\,dv=\frac{n_*}{u^2}+O(u^{-3}),\qquad n_*=\int f_0(v)\,dv.
$$
There is no boundary contribution because the <phase-space distribution function> vanishes at both endpoints. Direct area integration gives
$$
n_*=A_0\left[v_0+\delta\left(1-\frac{v_1}{v_0}\right)\right],\qquad \frac{n_*}{n_0}=1+\frac{\delta(v_0-v_1)}{v_0^2}.
$$
Thus the exact small-$k$ cutoff, retaining the paper's definition $n_0=v_0A_0$, is $\omega\to\pm\omega_{\mathrm{pe}}\sqrt{n_*/n_0}$. To leading order in the narrow-<velocity plateau> approximation this is
$$
\boxed{\omega\approx\pm\omega_{\mathrm{pe}}.}
$$
These are <Langmuir waves>. The difference between $n_*$ and the reference $n_0$ is only first order in $\delta/v_0$, so it does not alter the requested leading-order result.

For the finite-<phase velocity> branch put $u=v_1+\delta a$, with $0<a<1$. To leading order in $\delta/v_0$ and $\delta/v_1$, the real <dispersion relation> becomes
$$
\ln\left[\frac{v_1^2}{v_0^2-v_1^2}\frac{1-a}{a}\right]=K.
$$
At long wavelength set $K=0$ to leading order. Then $a=v_1^2/v_0^2$, and
$$
\boxed{\omega_{\mathrm a}\approx k\left[v_1+\delta\frac{v_1^2}{v_0^2}\right].}
$$
For comparison, the exact $k\to0$ <phase velocity> from the <cubic equation> in <2/b> is $B/A$; expanding it gives precisely $v_1+\delta v_1^2/v_0^2+O(\delta^2/v_0)$ at fixed $v_1/v_0$.

A single <Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution> has a nonzero slope at every finite nonzero $u$ and has no <velocity plateau>. The would-be <electron acoustic wave> is therefore subject to strong <Landau damping>, so there is no corresponding completely undamped acoustic branch in a single <Maxwellian> electron population with immobile ions. At its only stationary point $u=0$, a <Maxwellian> has $\int f_0'(v)/v\,dv<0$, making the static <dielectric function> positive rather than zero. This statement does not exclude acoustic modes of suitably distinct electron populations or an ion-supported mode when ion dynamics is retained.

Retain $K=k^2\lambda_{\mathrm{De}}^2$ while making the same narrow-<velocity plateau> expansion as in <2/d>. With $u=v_1+\delta a$, the factor that varies rapidly across the plateau is $(v_2-u)/(u-v_1)=(1-a)/a$. The other factors vary slowly and may be evaluated at $u=v_1$, giving
$$
\frac{v_1^2}{v_0^2-v_1^2}\frac{1-a}{a}=e^K.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\omega_{\mathrm a}\approx k\left[v_1+\frac{v_2-v_1}{1+e^{k^2\lambda_{\mathrm{De}}^2}(v_0^2-v_1^2)/v_1^2}\right].}
$$
At $K\ll1$, the denominator tends to $v_0^2/v_1^2$, recovering <2/d>. At $K\gg1$,
$$
u_{\mathrm a}-v_1\approx\delta\frac{v_1^2}{v_0^2-v_1^2}e^{-K},
$$
recovering the exponentially close approach to the plateau edge in <2/c>. The <phase velocity> remains strictly inside the <velocity plateau> for every finite $k$.

As an asymptotic expansion, the formula takes $\delta\to0$ at fixed $0<v_1/v_0<1$. If the plateau also approaches the outer edge, so that $v_0-v_1$ is comparable to $\delta$, the factors treated as slowly varying need not be slowly varying. In that additional limiting regime, use the exact <dispersion relation> in <2/b> instead.

For $k>0$, the upper <Langmuir wave> starts at $\omega\approx+\omega_{\mathrm{pe}}$ and approaches the line $\omega=kV$ from above. The lower <Langmuir wave> starts at $\omega\approx-\omega_{\mathrm{pe}}$ and approaches $\omega=-kv_0$ from below. The <electron acoustic wave> starts at the origin with slope $v_1+\delta v_1^2/v_0^2$ and approaches $\omega=kv_1$ from above; it stays inside $kv_1<\omega<kv_2$. These are the only completely undamped branches, as shown by the <cubic equation> in <2/b>.

For a regular <integrable Hamiltonian system>, <action-angle variables> offer two related simplifications.

* Unperturbed <Hamilton's equations> are $\dot{\mathbf J}=0$ and $\dot{\boldsymbol\theta}=\boldsymbol\Omega(\mathbf J)$. Complicated <stellar orbits> become uniform motion on an <invariant torus>, and the unperturbed transport operator becomes $\boldsymbol\Omega\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol\theta}$. A <Fourier series> in the angles then makes <orbital resonances> explicit through $\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega$.
* A phase-mixed equilibrium <galactic distribution function> depends on the <action variables> rather than the fast angles, consistently with <Jeans theorem>. Its slow evolution, including <quasilinear diffusion in action space>, can therefore be described by an angle average in two <action variables> rather than by resolving every <stellar orbit> in position and <velocity>.

These advantages assume regular or approximately integrable mean motion; globally defined <action-angle variables> need not exist in chaotic regions.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Use the <collisionless Boltzmann equation> in <action-angle variables>,
$$
\partial_tf+\{f,H\}=0,\qquad \{f,H\}=\partial_{\boldsymbol\theta}f\cdot\partial_{\mathbf J}H-\partial_{\mathbf J}f\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol\theta}H.
$$
Define the orbital <frequency> vector by
$$
\boxed{\boldsymbol\Omega(\mathbf J)=\partial_{\mathbf J}H_0=(\Omega_\varphi,\Omega_R).}
$$
The slow evolution of the angle average $f_0$ starts at second order in the perturbation. At first order, the <Poisson bracket> therefore gives
$$
(\partial_t+\boldsymbol\Omega\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol\theta})\delta f=\partial_{\mathbf J}f_0\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol\theta}\delta\Phi.
$$
For the $\mathbf k$th term in the <Fourier series>, this reads
$$
(\partial_t+i\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega)\delta f_{\mathbf k}=i\mathbf k\cdot\partial_{\mathbf J}f_0\,\delta\phi_{\mathbf k}.
$$
The lowercase $\delta\phi_{\mathbf k}$ here denotes the <Fourier coefficient> of the uppercase $\delta\Phi$ in the <Hamiltonian>; these are the same perturbing potential. Multiply by the <integrating factor> $e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega t}$ and set the initial homogeneous response to zero. One obtains
$$
\boxed{\delta f_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf J,t)=i\int_0^t dt'\,\mathbf k\cdot\frac{\partial f_0(\mathbf J,t')}{\partial\mathbf J}\,e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega(t-t')}\delta\phi_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf J,t').}
$$
Retaining the slow time dependence of $f_0$ inside this <integral> is compatible with the subsequent second-order calculation; changes in $\boldsymbol\Omega$ caused by that slow evolution affect only higher orders in this approximation.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Let $\langle\cdot\rangle$ denote $(2\pi)^{-2}\int d^2\theta$. Average the <collisionless Boltzmann equation> to second order. All first-order angle derivatives average to zero, leaving
$$
\partial_tf_0=-\langle\{\delta f,\delta\Phi\}\rangle.
$$
An angle <integration by parts> shows that
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle\{\delta f,\delta\Phi\}\rangle
&=\left\langle\partial_{\theta_i}\delta f\,\partial_{J_i}\delta\Phi-\partial_{J_i}\delta f\,\partial_{\theta_i}\delta\Phi\right\rangle\\
&=-\partial_{J_i}\langle\delta f\,\partial_{\theta_i}\delta\Phi\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the angle-averaged <galactic distribution function> obeys an action-space <continuity equation>,
$$
\partial_tf_0=-\partial_{J_i}q_i,\qquad q_i=-\langle\delta f\,\partial_{\theta_i}\delta\Phi\rangle.
$$
This sign also follows from <Hamilton's equations>, $\dot J_i=-\partial_{\theta_i}\delta\Phi$: the flux is $\langle\delta f\,\dot J_i\rangle$.

Reality gives $\delta\phi_{-\mathbf k}=\delta\phi_{\mathbf k}^*$ and $\delta f_{-\mathbf k}=\delta f_{\mathbf k}^*$. Orthogonality of the <Fourier series> implies
$$
\langle\delta f\,\partial_{\theta_i}\delta\Phi\rangle=-i\sum_{\mathbf k}k_i\delta f_{\mathbf k}\delta\phi_{\mathbf k}^*.
$$
Insert <3/b> to obtain
$$
q_i=-\sum_{\mathbf k}k_i\int_0^t dt'\,\mathbf k\cdot\partial_{\mathbf J}f_0(\mathbf J,t')\,e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega(t-t')}\delta\phi_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf J,t')\delta\phi_{\mathbf k}^*(\mathbf J,t).
$$
Although individual terms are complex, pairing $\mathbf k$ with $-\mathbf k$ makes the flux real.

Now take the <marginalization> specified by $F_0=2\pi\int_0^\infty f_0\,dJ_R$. Assume vanishing radial-action flux at $J_R=0$ and $J_R\to\infty$: the first boundary does not permit negative <radial action>, and the second has no escaping flux in this calculation. Then
$$
\partial_tF_0=-2\pi\partial_{J_\varphi}\int_0^\infty q_\varphi\,dJ_R-2\pi[q_R]_0^\infty=-\partial_{J_\varphi}Q_0,
$$
with
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
Q_0={}&-2\pi\sum_{\mathbf k}k_\varphi\int_0^\infty dJ_R\int_0^t dt'\,\mathbf k\cdot\frac{\partial f_0(\mathbf J,t')}{\partial\mathbf J}\\
&\quad\times e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega(t-t')}\delta\phi_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf J,t')\delta\phi_{\mathbf k}^*(\mathbf J,t).
\end{aligned}}
$$
If radial-action boundary flux were present, its explicit boundary term would have to be retained.

= Solution

The restriction $k_\varphi=\pm m$ describes an $m$-fold rotating perturbation of a <galactic disk>, for example a transient <spiral density wave> or, for $m=2$, a <galactic bar>. Its <pattern speed> is $\Omega_{\mathrm p}$, its amplitude peaks at $t_{\mathrm{peak}}$, and its <Gaussian function> envelope has duration of order $\tau$. The coefficients $u_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf J)$ describe its strength along different <stellar orbits>. A real perturbing potential requires $u_{-\mathbf k}=u_{\mathbf k}^*$.

Write $g(t)=\exp[-(t-t_{\mathrm{peak}})^2/(2\tau^2)]$. The product entering <3/c> is
$$
\delta\phi_{\mathbf k}(t')\delta\phi_{\mathbf k}^*(t)=|u_{\mathbf k}|^2e^{ik_\varphi\Omega_{\mathrm p}(t-t')}g(t')g(t).
$$
The phase therefore measures detuning from the rotating pattern:
$$
\boxed{\omega_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf J)=\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega-k_\varphi\Omega_{\mathrm p}=k_\varphi(\Omega_\varphi-\Omega_{\mathrm p})+k_R\Omega_R.}
$$
Substitution gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
Q_0={}&-2\pi\sum_{k_\varphi=\pm m}\sum_{k_R}k_\varphi\int_0^\infty dJ_R\,|u_{\mathbf k}|^2\\
&\times\int_0^t dt'\,\mathbf k\cdot\partial_{\mathbf J}f_0(\mathbf J,t')\,e^{-i\omega_{\mathbf k}(t-t')}\\
&\times\exp\left[-\frac{(t-t_{\mathrm{peak}})^2}{2\tau^2}-\frac{(t'-t_{\mathrm{peak}})^2}{2\tau^2}\right].
\end{aligned}}
$$
The strongest long-lived response occurs near an orbital <resonance>, $\omega_{\mathbf k}=0$, where successive <force> contributions do not rapidly cancel in phase.

The required time separation is $\tau\ll t_{\mathrm{sec}}$, where $t_{\mathrm{sec}}$ is the evolution timescale of $f_0$, together with $t_{\mathrm{peak}}\gg\tau$ and an observation time well after the pulse. The first condition permits freezing $f_0(\mathbf J,t')$ at its pre-pulse value; its neglected change would enter beyond the retained second order. The second makes the original initial time lie in a negligible tail of the <Gaussian function>, permitting replacement of the lower time limits by $-\infty$. Short duration alone would not justify that replacement for a pulse centered at the initial time.

Integrate the <continuity equation> in <3/c> over the whole pulse. For fixed $\mathbf J$, the time factor is the <ordered Gaussian response integral>
$$
\mathcal I(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt'\,e^{-i\omega(t-t')}g(t)g(t').
$$
A common translation by $t_{\mathrm{peak}}$ removes the peak time. Pair the positive and negative azimuthal harmonics: $(-m,-k_R)$ is paired with $(m,k_R)$. Both $k_\varphi$ and $\mathbf k\cdot\partial_{\mathbf J}f_0$ change sign, so their product does not; $\omega_{-\mathbf k}=-\omega_{\mathbf k}$ makes the two time factors complex conjugates. Hence the pair contributes $2\operatorname{Re}\mathcal I$.

For completeness, the real part of the integrand is symmetric under $t\leftrightarrow t'$. The triangular time domain therefore gives half of the full plane, and the <Fourier transform> of a <Gaussian function> gives
$$
\operatorname{Re}\mathcal I(\omega)=\frac12\left|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(t)e^{-i\omega t}\,dt\right|^2=\pi\tau^2e^{-\omega^2\tau^2}.
$$
This derives the supplied identity rather than requiring a separate time-ordering calculation.

Define
$$
D_{mk_R}=m\frac{\partial f_0}{\partial J_\varphi}+k_R\frac{\partial f_0}{\partial J_R}.
$$
Using the paired harmonics and $\Delta F_0=-\partial_{J_\varphi}\int Q_0\,dt$ now yields
$$
\Delta F_0=4\pi^2m\tau^2\frac{\partial}{\partial J_\varphi}\sum_{k_R}\int_0^\infty dJ_R\,|u_{mk_R}|^2D_{mk_R}e^{-\omega_{mk_R}^2\tau^2}.
$$
By the stated assumption, $\omega_{mk_R}$ is independent of $J_R$, so its exponential can be taken outside the radial <integral>. Put $\Gamma=(\sqrt2\tau)^{-1}$ and use
$$
\mathcal R_\Gamma(\omega)=\frac{e^{-\omega^2/(2\Gamma^2)}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\Gamma},\qquad 4\pi^2\tau^2e^{-\omega^2\tau^2}=\frac{\sqrt{8\pi^5}}{\Gamma}\mathcal R_\Gamma(\omega).
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Delta F_0=\frac{\sqrt{8\pi^5}\,m}{\Gamma}\frac{\partial}{\partial J_\varphi}\sum_{k_R}\mathcal R_\Gamma(\omega_{mk_R})\int_0^\infty dJ_R\,|u_{mk_R}|^2\left(m\frac{\partial f_0}{\partial J_\varphi}+k_R\frac{\partial f_0}{\partial J_R}\right).}
$$
The outer derivative acts on the <Gaussian resonance broadening> factor as well as on the radial <integral>. The result describes <quasilinear diffusion in action space> weighted by the squared pulse amplitude, with a detuning width $\Gamma$. A shorter pulse has broader frequency selectivity, while a longer pulse selects narrower <orbital resonances>. If the action-space boundary flux vanishes, integrating $\Delta F_0$ over $J_\varphi$ gives zero, as required by <conservation of mass>.

For $k_R=0$, the detuning is $\omega_{m0}=m(\Omega_\varphi-\Omega_{\mathrm p})$. The <Gaussian resonance broadening> factor selects the <corotation resonance>, where the star's azimuthal <frequency> matches the <pattern speed>. The derivative driving the flux is just $m\partial f_0/\partial J_\varphi$. In fact this contribution alone has the form
$$
\Delta F_0\big|_{k_R=0}=\partial_{J_\varphi}\left[\frac{\sqrt{8\pi^5}\,m^2}{\Gamma}\mathcal R_\Gamma(\omega_{m0})\int_0^\infty|u_{m0}|^2\partial_{J_\varphi}f_0\,dJ_R\right].
$$
A single <Fourier series> harmonic changes the <action variables> in the direction $(m,k_R)$, because $\dot{\mathbf J}=-i\mathbf k\delta\phi_{\mathbf k}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\theta}$ plus its conjugate. For this harmonic, $\Delta J_R=0$: stars change <angular momentum> and shift their <epicyclic guiding center> without a leading-order increase of random radial motion. This is <stellar radial migration> driven by the <corotation resonance>. The resonance condition explains the absence of leading heating: $\Delta E\simeq\Omega_\varphi\Delta J_\varphi=\Omega_{\mathrm p}\Delta J_\varphi$ at the <corotation resonance>.

For $k_R=\pm1$, the selected <orbital resonances> satisfy
$$
m(\Omega_\varphi-\Omega_{\mathrm p})\pm\Omega_R=0.
$$
For near-circular <stellar orbits>, $\Omega_R$ is the <radial epicyclic frequency>. Thus $k_R=-1$ is the <inner Lindblad resonance>, $m(\Omega_\varphi-\Omega_{\mathrm p})=\Omega_R$, and $k_R=+1$ is the <outer Lindblad resonance>, $m(\Omega_\varphi-\Omega_{\mathrm p})=-\Omega_R$.

The flux is driven by $m\partial_{J_\varphi}f_0\pm\partial_{J_R}f_0$, and each resonant harmonic exchanges both <angular momentum> and <radial action> in the ratio
$$
\boxed{\Delta J_R=\frac{k_R}{m}\Delta J_\varphi.}
$$
These terms can therefore produce <galactic disk heating> as well as <angular momentum transport>. In the usual absorbing response, stars losing <angular momentum> at the <inner Lindblad resonance> gain <radial action>, and stars gaining <angular momentum> at the <outer Lindblad resonance> also gain <radial action>. The actual direction of flux follows from the displayed derivatives of $f_0$; it is not fixed by the resonance condition alone. A finite pulse replaces exact <Lindblad resonance> selection by a band of detunings of width of order $1/\tau$.
