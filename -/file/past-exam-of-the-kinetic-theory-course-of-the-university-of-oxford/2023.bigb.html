= Solution
{parent=a}

Write $K_{ij}=K(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf x_j)$ and $\mathbf P=\sum_i\mathbf v_i$. The unit particle <masses> make this sum the total <momentum>. Exchange the two indices and use the <symmetry> $K_{ij}=K_{ji}$:
$$
\dot{\mathbf P}=\sum_{i,j}K_{ij}(\mathbf v_j-\mathbf v_i)
=\frac12\sum_{i,j}K_{ij}\bigl[(\mathbf v_j-\mathbf v_i)+(\mathbf v_i-\mathbf v_j)\bigr]=\boxed{\mathbf0}.
$$
Thus every interacting pair transfers equal and opposite <momentum>, even though the <velocity alignment> dissipates <kinetic energy>.

For the total <kinetic energy> $E_N=\tfrac12\sum_i|\mathbf v_i|^2$, apply the same exchange of indices:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot E_N
&=\sum_{i,j}K_{ij}\mathbf v_i\cdot(\mathbf v_j-\mathbf v_i)\\
&=\frac12\sum_{i,j}K_{ij}\bigl[\mathbf v_i\cdot(\mathbf v_j-\mathbf v_i)+\mathbf v_j\cdot(\mathbf v_i-\mathbf v_j)\bigr]\\
&=\boxed{-\frac12\sum_{i,j}K_{ij}|\mathbf v_j-\mathbf v_i|^2\leq0}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The dissipated <kinetic energy> is that of relative motion. Since the total <momentum> is constant, $E_N-|\mathbf P|^2/(2N)=\tfrac12\sum_i|\mathbf v_i-\mathbf P/N|^2$ also decreases. Equality holds when every pair with $K_{ij}>0$ has the same <velocity>; the hypotheses permit disconnected interacting groups, so they alone do not guarantee eventual global <velocity alignment>.

Let $\mathbf A_i=\sum_{j\ne i}K_{ij}(\mathbf v_j-\mathbf v_i)$. Conservation of <probability> in the full <phase space> gives the <dissipative Liouville equation>
$$
\boxed{\partial_t\varrho+\sum_{i=1}^N\nabla_{\mathbf x_i}\cdot(\mathbf v_i\varrho)+\sum_{i=1}^N\nabla_{\mathbf v_i}\cdot(\mathbf A_i\varrho)=0.}
$$
Normalize $\int\varrho\,\prod_i d^3x_i\,d^3v_i=1$. Decay at infinity makes all boundary fluxes vanish, so the equation preserves this <normalization of a probability distribution>.

Unlike the <Hamiltonian Liouville equation>, this transport has nonzero <phase-space divergence>. In three <dimensions>,
$$
\nabla_{\mathbf v_i}\cdot\mathbf A_i=-3\sum_{j\ne i}K_{ij},\qquad
\partial_t\varrho+\sum_i\left(\mathbf v_i\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf x_i}\varrho+\mathbf A_i\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf v_i}\varrho\right)=3\varrho\sum_i\sum_{j\ne i}K_{ij}.
$$
The <probability density> increases along a contracting <characteristic flow map>; setting its material derivative to zero would lose conservation of <probability>. The $j=i$ force is identically zero, including its derivative, which is why the last sums exclude it.

Put $z_i=(\mathbf x_i,\mathbf v_i)$ and $dz_i=d^3x_i\,d^3v_i$. Indistinguishability gives an <exchangeable> ensemble. Define unit-normalized <marginal distributions>
$$
p_1(z_1,t)=\int\varrho\,dz_2\cdots dz_N,\qquad
p_2(z_1,z_2,t)=\int\varrho\,dz_3\cdots dz_N.
$$
Integrating the <dissipative Liouville equation> over $z_2,\ldots,z_N$ removes their boundary fluxes. The $N-1$ identical pair terms remain:
$$
\partial_tp_1+\mathbf v\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf x}p_1
+(N-1)\nabla_{\mathbf v}\cdot\int K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x_\star)(\mathbf v_\star-\mathbf v)p_2(z,z_\star,t)\,dz_\star=0.
$$
This is the first equation of a <BBGKY hierarchy>, with no independence assumed yet.

The additional physical assumption is a <mean-field closure for velocity alignment>: neglect pair correlations, $p_2(z,z_\star,t)\simeq p_1(z,t)p_1(z_\star,t)$. Initial independence alone does not make this exact at later times, because the particles interact. In the usual number convention, $f=Np_1$ and the <two-particle distribution function> is $f_2=N(N-1)p_2$. Consequently the closed equation at finite $N$ is
$$
\partial_tf+\mathbf v\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf x}f
+\left(1-\frac1N\right)\nabla_{\mathbf v}\cdot\bigl(f\mathbf L[f]\bigr)\simeq0,
\qquad
\mathbf L[f](z)=\int K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x_\star)(\mathbf v_\star-\mathbf v)f(z_\star,t)\,dz_\star.
$$
Neglecting the relative $1/N$ correction gives the intended <mean-field alignment equation>:
$$
\boxed{\partial_tf+\mathbf v\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf x}f+\nabla_{\mathbf v}\cdot\bigl(f\mathbf L[f]\bigr)=0.}
$$
Equivalently, the closure $f_2\simeq ff_\star$ incorporates this large-$N$ approximation. It cannot be an exact finite-$N$ identity with $\int f=N$, since $\int f_2\,dz_\star=(N-1)f$ whereas $\int ff_\star\,dz_\star=Nf$.

There is also a consistent finite-$N$ convention yielding the boxed equation directly under the independence closure: define $\widetilde f=(N-1)p_1$ instead. Its total <normalization of a probability distribution> is $N-1$, rather than the physical particle number $N$. Thus the displayed source equation needs a stated <normalization of a probability distribution> convention, or the large-$N$ approximation, in addition to neglect of correlations. In the remaining parts we use the displayed <mean-field alignment equation> itself; its moment identities do not depend on the total <normalization of a probability distribution>.

Use $dz=d^3x\,d^3v$ and set
$$
\rho(\mathbf x,t)=\boxed{\int f(\mathbf x,\mathbf v,t)\,d^3v},\quad
\mathcal E=\frac12\int|\mathbf v|^2f\,dz,\quad
\mathcal H=\int f\log f\,dz.
$$
For unit particle <mass>, $\rho$ is both the <number density> and the <mass density> in the number convention of <1/c>. A fixed reference scale inside the <logarithm> changes $\mathcal H$ by a constant multiple of the conserved total <mass>, leaving its derivative unchanged.

**Both prefactors in the source's part (d) need correction.** Multiplication of the <mean-field alignment equation> by $|\mathbf v|^2/2$ and <integration by parts> gives
$$
\dot{\mathcal E}=\int f\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf L[f]\,dz
=\iint K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x_\star)\mathbf v\cdot(\mathbf v_\star-\mathbf v)ff_\star\,dz\,dz_\star.
$$
Exchange $z$ and $z_\star$, use the <symmetry> of $K$, and average the two expressions:
$$
\boxed{\dot{\mathcal E}=-\frac12\iint K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x_\star)|\mathbf v_\star-\mathbf v|^2ff_\star\,dz\,dz_\star\leq0.}
$$
The printed right-hand side omits $1/2$. This corrected <velocity-alignment energy dissipation> agrees with the pair-counting calculation in <1/a>.

For <velocity-alignment entropy production>, let $A(\mathbf x,t)=\int K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x_\star)\rho(\mathbf x_\star,t)\,d^3x_\star$. The <velocity-space divergence> is $\nabla_{\mathbf v}\cdot\mathbf L[f]=-3A$. Assuming $f>0$ and sufficient decay, <integration by parts> yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot{\mathcal H}
&=-\int(1+\log f)\left[\mathbf v\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf x}f+\nabla_{\mathbf v}\cdot(f\mathbf L[f])\right]dz\\
&=\int\mathbf L[f]\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf v}f\,dz
=-\int f\nabla_{\mathbf v}\cdot\mathbf L[f]\,dz\\
&=\boxed{3\iint K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x_\star)\rho(\mathbf x,t)\rho(\mathbf x_\star,t)\,d^3x\,d^3x_\star\geq0.}
\end{aligned}
$$
The printed formula omits the factor $3$ from the three <velocity> components. In $d$ spatial <dimensions>, that factor would be $d$. Zeros of $f$ can be treated by a regularized <entropy> calculation when the required integrability holds.

A concrete smooth counterexample to both printed coefficients is $K\equiv1$ and $f(z,0)=g(\mathbf x)(2\pi)^{-3/2}e^{-|\mathbf v|^2/2}$, where $g$ is a positive normalized <Gaussian distribution>. The total <mass> is one, the total <momentum> is zero, and $\mathbf L[f]=-\mathbf v$ initially. The <kinetic energy> derivative is $-\int |\mathbf v|^2f\,dz=-3$, whereas the printed formula gives $-6$. The <entropy functional> derivative is $3$, whereas the printed formula gives $1$.

The signs describe cooling of relative <velocities> and contraction in <phase space>: $\mathcal H$ increases, so the fine-grained <Gibbs entropy> $-\mathcal H$ decreases. These dissipative particle dynamics are not an isolated <Hamiltonian system>; there is no contradiction with <Liouville's theorem> or the <Boltzmann equation>'s usual <entropy> increase. The microscopic mechanism removing <kinetic energy> is outside this alignment model.

Define the local <mean velocity> and <momentum flux> by
$$
\boxed{\rho\mathbf u=\int\mathbf v f\,d^3v,\qquad
\Pi_{ij}=\int v_iv_j f\,d^3v.}
$$
Here $\rho=\int f\,d^3v$ and $\mathbf u$ is defined where $\rho>0$. The <pressure tensor> separates random motion from the bulk <velocity>:
$$
\Pi_{ij}=\rho u_i u_j+P_{ij},\qquad
P_{ij}=\int(v_i-u_i)(v_j-u_j)f\,d^3v.
$$
Integrating the <mean-field alignment equation> over <velocity space> removes the force <divergence> and gives the <continuity equation>
$$
\boxed{\partial_t\rho+\nabla_{\mathbf x}\cdot(\rho\mathbf u)=0.}
$$
Its first <velocity moment> follows by multiplying by $v_i$ and using $\int v_i\partial_{v_j}(fL_j)\,d^3v=-\int fL_i\,d^3v$:
$$
\partial_t(\rho u_i)+\partial_{x_j}\Pi_{ij}=\int fL_i[f]\,d^3v.
$$
Inserting the <mean-field alignment equation>'s force and integrating each particle <velocity> separately gives
$$
\boxed{\partial_t(\rho\mathbf u)+\nabla_{\mathbf x}\cdot\boldsymbol\Pi
=\int K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x_\star)\bigl[\mathbf u(\mathbf x_\star,t)-\mathbf u(\mathbf x,t)\bigr]\rho(\mathbf x_\star,t)\rho(\mathbf x,t)\,d^3x_\star.}
$$
The right-hand side exchanges <momentum> between positions and aligns their bulk <velocities>. Its spatial <integral> vanishes by exchange <symmetry>, recovering total <momentum conservation>. No closure for the <pressure tensor> is needed for these exact <velocity moment> identities.

**The step in the source must be at zero.** We use the <Heaviside step function> $H(x)=1$ for $x>0$, zero for $x<0$, with its value at zero immaterial here. The printed threshold $1$ is inconsistent with both $H'=\delta(x)$ and the stated <number density>. For example, measuring <velocities> in fixed units and taking $v_F=2$, the literal printed cutoff fills only $v<1$ and gives one eighth of the stated <number density>. Its <distributional derivative> is $\delta(x-1)$, not $\delta(x)$. The zero-threshold convention restores the stated <Fermi sphere> and its <normalization of a probability distribution>.

Let $k=|\mathbf k|>0$, choose $u=\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf k/k$, and abbreviate $V_\alpha=v_{\mathrm F\alpha}$. The three-dimensional <phase-space distribution function> is $f_{0\alpha}=C_\alpha\mathbf1_{v<V_\alpha}$, where
$$
C_\alpha=\frac{m_\alpha^3}{4\pi^3\hbar^3}=\frac{3n_\alpha}{4\pi V_\alpha^3}.
$$
Integrate over the transverse <velocity> disk to obtain the parallel <marginal distribution>
$$
F_\alpha(u)=\int f_{0\alpha}\,d^2v_\perp
=\frac{3n_\alpha}{4V_\alpha^3}(V_\alpha^2-u^2)\mathbf1_{|u|<V_\alpha},\qquad
F_\alpha'(u)=-\frac{3n_\alpha u}{2V_\alpha^3}\mathbf1_{|u|<V_\alpha}.
$$
There are no edge <Dirac delta functions> in $F_\alpha'$ because $F_\alpha$ itself vanishes continuously at the endpoints. In particular, the three-dimensional constant occupation does not imply a constant parallel <marginal distribution>.

Use time dependence $e^{pt+i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}$, with $p=\gamma-i\omega$ and $\gamma<0$ for <damping>. The given <dielectric function> becomes
$$
\epsilon=1-\sum_\alpha\frac{\omega_{\mathrm p\alpha}^2}{n_\alpha k^2}
\int_{C_L}\frac{F_\alpha'(u)}{u-\omega/k-i\gamma/k}\,du.
$$
The <Landau contour> supplies causal continuation. For a real <phase velocity> $a=\omega/k>V_\alpha$, there is no resonant pole in the populated interval, and
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int\frac{F_\alpha'(u)}{u-a}\,du
&=-\frac{3n_\alpha}{2V_\alpha^3}\int_{-V_\alpha}^{V_\alpha}\frac{u}{u-a}\,du\\
&=-\frac{3n_\alpha}{2V_\alpha^3}\left[2V_\alpha+a\log\frac{a-V_\alpha}{a+V_\alpha}\right].
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence this species contributes
$$
\chi_\alpha=\frac{3\omega_{\mathrm p\alpha}^2}{k^2V_\alpha^2}
\left[1+\frac{s_\alpha}{2}\log\frac{s_\alpha-1}{s_\alpha+1}\right],\qquad
s_\alpha=\frac{\omega}{kV_\alpha}>1.
$$

For comparable species <number densities> and fixed charge ratios, $m_e\ll m_i$ implies $V_i/V_e=O(m_e/m_i)$ and $\omega_{\mathrm pi}^2/\omega_{\mathrm pe}^2=O(m_e/m_i)$. Since $\omega>kV_e$, the ions have $s_i\gg1$ and $\chi_i=-\omega_{\mathrm pi}^2/\omega^2+O(k^2V_i^2\omega_{\mathrm pi}^2/\omega^4)$. Their response is negligible to leading order in the <mass> ratio. The real <dispersion relation> is therefore
$$
\boxed{1+\frac{3\omega_{\mathrm pe}^2}{k^2V_e^2}\left[1+\frac{\omega}{2kV_e}\log\frac{\omega-kV_e}{\omega+kV_e}\right]=0.}
$$
This <degenerate-plasma dielectric response> treats a completely degenerate, nonrelativistic <Fermi gas> with classical particle trajectories. It does not include quantum recoil or a collision term. A finite ion response shifts the root slightly; the boxed equation is the stated leading electron approximation.

There is **no <Landau damping>** of this branch within the completely degenerate collisionless model. Its <phase velocity> obeys $\omega/k>V_e>V_i$, while each parallel <marginal distribution> vanishes outside $|u|\leq V_\alpha$. There are no particles at the resonant <velocity>, and $F_\alpha'(\omega/k)=0$. The <Landau contour> consequently contributes no imaginary part to the <dielectric function>; a real root is allowed with $\boxed{\gamma=0}$.

If a small nonzero <temperature> supplied a tail beyond the <Fermi surface>, the conclusion would change: the resonant derivative would generally be nonzero. For the convention $p=\gamma-i\omega$ and a simple weakly damped root, expanding the continued <dielectric function> would give $\gamma=-\epsilon_I(\omega,k)/\partial_\omega\epsilon_R(\omega,k)$. This conditional formula is not needed to manufacture <damping> for the exact cutoff. Neither collisional attenuation nor nonlinear evolution is included in the given <dispersion relation>.

Let $\kappa=kV_e/\omega_{\mathrm pe}$ and $s=\omega/(kV_e)$. The long-<wavelength> electron branch has $|s|\gg1$. The convergent <power series>
$$
\log\frac{s-1}{s+1}=-2\left(\frac1s+\frac1{3s^3}+\frac1{5s^5}+\cdots\right)
$$
turns the <degenerate-plasma dielectric response> into
$$
0=1-\frac{\omega_{\mathrm pe}^2}{\omega^2}
-\frac{3\omega_{\mathrm pe}^2 k^2V_e^2}{5\omega^4}
+O\left(\frac{\omega_{\mathrm pe}^2 k^4V_e^4}{\omega^6}\right).
$$
Substitute $\omega^2=\omega_{\mathrm pe}^2+a k^2V_e^2+O(k^4V_e^4/\omega_{\mathrm pe}^2)$. The coefficient of $k^2$ is $a-3/5$, so the <degenerate-plasma Langmuir dispersion> is
$$
\boxed{\omega^2=\omega_{\mathrm pe}^2+\frac35 k^2V_e^2+O\left(\frac{k^4V_e^4}{\omega_{\mathrm pe}^2}\right),\qquad
\omega_\pm=\pm\omega_{\mathrm pe}\left[1+\frac3{10}\kappa^2+O(\kappa^4)\right].}
$$
The <Langmuir wave> therefore has a finite <frequency> at zero <wavenumber>, with the first dispersive correction set by the <Fermi velocity>. Indeed the occupied <Fermi sphere> has $\langle v_\parallel^2\rangle=V_e^2/5$, so the coefficient $3\langle v_\parallel^2\rangle=3V_e^2/5$ agrees with the high-<phase velocity> <velocity moment> expansion.

**Screening-length convention.** The source defines $\lambda_{\mathrm De}^{\rm exam}=\sqrt3 V_e/\omega_{\mathrm pe}$, so its long-<wavelength> condition is equivalent to $\kappa\ll1$. We retain that definition when expressing answers in its symbols. The actual electron <Thomas-Fermi screening length> obtained from the static response is instead
$$
\epsilon_e(0,k)=1+\frac{3\omega_{\mathrm pe}^2}{k^2V_e^2}
=1+\frac1{k^2\lambda_{\rm TF}^2},\qquad
\boxed{\lambda_{\rm TF}=\frac{V_e}{\sqrt3\omega_{\mathrm pe}}=\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm De}^{\rm exam}}3.}
$$
For a concrete comparison, at $k=\omega_{\mathrm pe}/V_e$, the static <dielectric function> is $4$; using the printed length as the physical <Thomas-Fermi screening length> would give $1+1/3=4/3$. Calling the printed length the <Debye length> thus differs from the physical degenerate-electron screening convention by a factor of three. It does not affect the boxed dimensional <dispersion relation>.

For the positive-<frequency> branch write $s=1+\delta$, $\delta>0$, and $Q=k^2V_e^2/(3\omega_{\mathrm pe}^2)=\kappa^2/3$. The <degenerate-plasma dielectric response> is equivalent to
$$
1+\frac{1+\delta}{2}\log\frac{\delta}{2+\delta}=-Q.
$$
At large $Q$, a logarithmic divergence is needed to balance the right-hand side; hence $\delta\ll1$. Keeping the constant as well as the large term gives
$$
\log\frac\delta2=-2(1+Q)+O(\delta|\log\delta|),\qquad
\boxed{\delta\sim2\exp\left[-2-\frac{2k^2V_e^2}{3\omega_{\mathrm pe}^2}\right].}
$$
More precisely the relative correction is $O((1+Q)e^{-2(1+Q)})$. The additive constant $-2$ in the exponent fixes the leading prefactor $2e^{-2}$ and should not be discarded.

The negative-<frequency> branch follows from the even real <dielectric function> outside the occupied interval. Thus
$$
\boxed{\omega_\pm=\pm kV_e(1+\delta),\qquad
\delta\sim2\exp\left[-2-\frac29(k\lambda_{\mathrm De}^{\rm exam})^2\right].}
$$
Using the physical <Thomas-Fermi screening length> instead gives $\delta\sim2e^{-2-2(k\lambda_{\rm TF})^2}$. This is <degenerate-plasma zero-point sound>: the <phase velocity> lies exponentially close to, but strictly beyond, the edge of the <Fermi sphere> for every finite $k$ in this approximation. The limit is a property of the supplied classical-trajectory <kinetic theory>; applying it at microscopic <wavelengths> would require checking quantum recoil, which that equation omits.

The two real branches start at $\omega=\pm\omega_{\mathrm pe}$, have zero initial slope, and approach the lines $\omega=\pm kV_e$ from outside the populated parallel-<velocity> region. The original plot solves the <degenerate-plasma dielectric response> numerically; the shaded wedge shows where particles with resonant parallel <velocities> exist.

The distinction from a <nondegenerate gas> with a <Maxwellian> <Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution> is its tail. A <Maxwellian> has resonant particles at every finite <phase velocity>. Its long-<wavelength> <Langmuir waves> have exponentially weak <Landau damping>, but at shorter <wavelengths> that <damping> becomes strong. The ideal degenerate cutoff instead permits this undamped branch to persist continuously into <degenerate-plasma zero-point sound>. The coefficient $3V_e^2/5$ in <2/c> is also different from the classical thermal dispersive coefficient.

A natural additional branch is a <degenerate ion-acoustic wave>. For singly charged ions with equal electron and ion <number densities>, degenerate electron <pressure> supplies a restoring force while ion <mass> supplies inertia. The electron equation of state has $dP_e/dn_e=m_eV_e^2/3$, suggesting, at long <wavelengths>,
$$
\boxed{\omega\simeq\pm kc_s,\qquad c_s^2\simeq\frac{m_e}{3m_i}V_e^2.}
$$
Both species are degenerate here, so $V_i=(m_e/m_i)V_e$. The hierarchy $V_i\ll c_s\ll V_e$ explains the <damping>: there are no resonant ions, but there are resonant electrons inside their parallel <marginal distribution>. That electron <marginal distribution> has a small nonzero slope at $u=c_s$, even though the full three-dimensional occupation is constant inside the <Fermi sphere>. One therefore expects weak electron <Landau damping>, of relative order $\sqrt{m_e/m_i}$, rather than exact absence of <damping>. Ion degeneracy <pressure> adds a relatively small correction to $c_s^2$. This reasoning concerns an electrostatic <ion-acoustic wave>; transverse or magnetized branches require additional field dynamics or a background <magnetic field>.

The <action-angle variables> $(\boldsymbol\theta,\mathbf J)$ are <canonical coordinates>, with $[\theta_a,J_b]=\delta_{ab}$. In the convention $\dot g=\partial_tg+[g,H]$, their <Poisson bracket> is
$$
\boxed{[g,h]=\sum_{a=1}^3\left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial\theta_a}\frac{\partial h}{\partial J_a}-\frac{\partial g}{\partial J_a}\frac{\partial h}{\partial\theta_a}\right)
=\partial_{\boldsymbol\theta}g\cdot\partial_{\mathbf J}h-\partial_{\mathbf J}g\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol\theta}h.}
$$
In particular, <Hamilton's equations> read $\dot{\boldsymbol\theta}=\partial_{\mathbf J}H$ and $\dot{\mathbf J}=-\partial_{\boldsymbol\theta}H$, fixing the sign needed below.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Define the unperturbed orbital <angular frequency> vector by
$$
\boxed{\boldsymbol\Omega(\mathbf J)=\frac{\partial H_0}{\partial\mathbf J}.}
$$
It is the rate of advance of the <angle variables> along the unperturbed <Hamiltonian flow>; the <action variables> are constant. Expand the <collisionless Boltzmann equation> to first order in the perturbations. Since $f_0$ and $H_0$ depend only on the <action variables>, $[f_0,H_0]=0$, and
$$
\partial_t\delta f+[\delta f,H_0]+[f_0,\delta\Phi]=0,
\qquad
\partial_t\delta f+\boldsymbol\Omega\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol\theta}\delta f
-\partial_{\mathbf J}f_0\cdot\partial_{\boldsymbol\theta}\delta\Phi=0.
$$
The omitted <Poisson bracket> $[\delta f,\delta\Phi]$ is quadratic in the perturbations. Use the <Fourier series> convention
$$
\delta f=\sum_{\mathbf k\in\mathbb Z^3}\delta f_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf J,t)e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\theta},\qquad
\delta\Phi=\sum_{\mathbf k\in\mathbb Z^3}\delta\Phi_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf J,t)e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\theta}.
$$
For each harmonic the linear <ordinary differential equation> is
$$
\partial_t\delta f_{\mathbf k}+i\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega\,\delta f_{\mathbf k}
=i\mathbf k\cdot\partial_{\mathbf J}f_0\,\delta\Phi_{\mathbf k}.
$$
Multiplication by the <integrating factor> $e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega t}$ and use of $\delta f_{\mathbf k}(0)=0$ give the causal <linear response in action-angle variables>:
$$
\boxed{\delta f_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf J,t)
=i\mathbf k\cdot\partial_{\mathbf J}f_0\int_0^t\delta\Phi_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf J,t')e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega(t-t')}\,dt'.}
$$
The zero harmonic stays zero at this order. This formula retains the entire forcing history; replacing it immediately by a resonant <Dirac delta function> would discard the transient response.

Since $L_z=J_\phi$, <Hamilton's equations> give
$$
\boxed{\dot L_z=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial\theta_\phi}=-\frac{\partial\delta\Phi}{\partial\theta_\phi}.}
$$
For the spherical unperturbed <Hamiltonian>, $H_0=H_0(J_r,L)$; in these particular <action-angle variables> the third component $\Omega_\phi=0$ describes the fixed orientation of the orbital plane. It should not be confused with the physical azimuthal <angular velocity> of a particle around its orbit.

The conserved <normalization of a probability distribution> $\int f\,d^3\theta\,d^3J=1$ makes the <expected value> of specific <angular momentum> equal to $\int L_zf\,d^3\theta\,d^3J$. Its time derivative is the <torque> per unit halo <mass>:
$$
\boxed{\mathcal T(t)=-\int f(\boldsymbol\theta,\mathbf J,t)\frac{\partial\delta\Phi}{\partial\theta_\phi}\,d^3\theta\,d^3J.}
$$
This follows either by averaging the single-particle equation or by integrating the <collisionless Boltzmann equation> against $L_z$, with vanishing boundary fluxes.

The term $f_0\partial_{\theta_\phi}\delta\Phi$ integrates to zero over the periodic <angle variable>. Thus the first nonzero <torque> is quadratic in the perturbation. Use the <Fourier series> expansions of <3/b>; the source's reference here to part (a) is a numbering slip. Integrating the product over all three <angle variables> yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal T(t)
&=-(2\pi)^3\sum_{\mathbf k,\boldsymbol\ell}i\ell_\phi
\int\delta f_{\mathbf k}\delta\Phi_{\boldsymbol\ell}\,
\delta_{\mathbf k+\boldsymbol\ell,\mathbf0}\,d^3J\\
&=\boxed{i(2\pi)^3\sum_{\mathbf k}k_\phi\int\delta f_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf J,t)\delta\Phi_{\mathbf k}^*(\mathbf J,t)\,d^3J.}
\end{aligned}
$$
Reality of the <potential energy> per unit <mass> gives $\delta\Phi_{-\mathbf k}=\delta\Phi_{\mathbf k}^*$; likewise $\delta f_{-\mathbf k}=\delta f_{\mathbf k}^*$. Conjugate harmonics make the total <torque> real. The sum is over all integer vectors, so no extra factor of two should be inserted.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Write $m=k_\phi$, $B_{\mathbf k}=\mathbf k\cdot\partial_{\mathbf J}f_0$, and let the detuning from the rotating perturbation be
$$
\Delta_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf J)=\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega(\mathbf J)-m\Omega_{\mathrm p}.
$$
Substitute $\delta\Phi_{\mathbf k}=\Psi_{\mathbf k}e^{-im\Omega_{\mathrm p}t}$ into the <linear response in action-angle variables>, then set $\tau=t-t'$:
$$
\delta f_{\mathbf k}=iB_{\mathbf k}\Psi_{\mathbf k}e^{-im\Omega_{\mathrm p}t}\int_0^te^{-i\Delta_{\mathbf k}\tau}\,d\tau
=B_{\mathbf k}\Psi_{\mathbf k}e^{-im\Omega_{\mathrm p}t}\frac{1-e^{-i\Delta_{\mathbf k}t}}{\Delta_{\mathbf k}}.
$$
At exact <resonance> the last quotient is interpreted by its continuous limit $it$. Inserting this response in <3/c> gives
$$
\mathcal T(t)=i(2\pi)^3\sum_{\mathbf k}m\int B_{\mathbf k}|\Psi_{\mathbf k}|^2\frac{1-e^{-i\Delta_{\mathbf k}t}}{\Delta_{\mathbf k}}\,d^3J.
$$
Pair $\mathbf k$ with $-\mathbf k$. Because $B_{-\mathbf k}=-B_{\mathbf k}$, $\Delta_{-\mathbf k}=-\Delta_{\mathbf k}$, and $|\Psi_{-\mathbf k}|^2=|\Psi_{\mathbf k}|^2$, the imaginary cosine terms cancel. The finite-time <torque> is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal T(t)=-(2\pi)^3\sum_{\mathbf k}m\int B_{\mathbf k}|\Psi_{\mathbf k}|^2\frac{\sin(\Delta_{\mathbf k}t)}{\Delta_{\mathbf k}}\,d^3J.}
$$
This is a leading, second-order response in the perturbation amplitude.

For the long-time limit, assume a continuum of occupied <action variables>, sufficiently smooth integrable weights, convergent harmonic sums, and regular <resonance> surfaces $\nabla_{\mathbf J}\Delta_{\mathbf k}\ne0$ wherever a contributing surface intersects the distribution. Assume also that the perturbation is weak enough to neglect nonlinear trapping and secular changes in $f_0$, and that the prescribed <stellar bar> pattern and its coefficients stay fixed. Then <phase mixing> removes nonresonant transients in the integrated response. The relevant <long-time resonance kernel> is the <distributional convergence>
$$
\frac{\sin(tx)}x\longrightarrow\pi\delta(x)\qquad(t\to+\infty).
$$
It is not a pointwise limit: at $x=0$ the kernel equals $t$. To justify its use under the <integral> over <action variables>, resolve the integral into detuning surfaces; the <coarea formula> gives
$$
\int W(\mathbf J)\delta(\Delta_{\mathbf k}(\mathbf J))\,d^3J
=\int_{\Delta_{\mathbf k}=0}\frac{W(\mathbf J)}{|\nabla_{\mathbf J}\Delta_{\mathbf k}|}\,dS.
$$
Smoothness and decay of this surface weight reduce the limit to the one-dimensional <long-time resonance kernel>. Equivalently, a causal convergence factor $e^{-\eta\tau}$ gives $\operatorname{Re}\int_0^\infty e^{-\eta\tau-i\Delta\tau}\,d\tau=\eta/(\eta^2+\Delta^2)\to\pi\delta(\Delta)$ as $\eta\downarrow0$.

Therefore the <resonant torque from a rotating perturbation> is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal T=-\pi(2\pi)^3\sum_{\mathbf k}k_\phi\int|\Psi_{\mathbf k}|^2\,
\mathbf k\cdot\frac{\partial f_0}{\partial\mathbf J}\,
\delta\bigl(\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega-k_\phi\Omega_{\mathrm p}\bigr)\,d^3J.}
$$
Only orbital <resonances> contribute to this steady integrated <torque>. The limit is a formal weak-perturbation limit, or an intermediate-time approximation after <phase mixing> but before substantial secular redistribution. It does not prove an unchanged halo can absorb <angular momentum> at this rate forever. A discrete set of orbital <frequencies>, singular <resonance> surfaces, or strong trapping can invalidate the stated steady limit.

Apply the <chain rule> to $f_0=f_0(E)$ with $E=H_0(\mathbf J)$:
$$
\mathbf k\cdot\partial_{\mathbf J}f_0=f_0'(E)\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega.
$$
On the <resonance> selected by the <Dirac delta function>, $\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega=k_\phi\Omega_{\mathrm p}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathcal T=\pi(2\pi)^3\Omega_{\mathrm p}\sum_{\mathbf k}k_\phi^2\int|\Psi_{\mathbf k}|^2\bigl[-f_0'(E)\bigr]
\delta\bigl(\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\Omega-k_\phi\Omega_{\mathrm p}\bigr)\,d^3J\geq0.}
$$
Every weight is nonnegative, and $-f_0'(E)>0$. Strict positivity additionally requires a populated nonaxisymmetric <resonance> with nonzero coupling on a set of positive surface measure. **The unconditional statement is nonnegativity, rather than positive definiteness.** As a concrete counterexample to strict positivity of this formula, retain one nonzero conjugate harmonic pair with $k_\phi\ne0$ and choose its smooth coefficient $\Psi_{\mathbf k}$ supported in an open region $|\Delta_{\mathbf k}|>d>0$. The perturbation is nonzero but has zero resonant coupling, so $\mathcal T=0$ even when $f_0'(E)<0$. The even simpler zero-perturbation case also gives zero. Whether a particular physical <stellar bar> has nonzero resonant overlap is additional information.

For a coupled <stellar bar> and halo, the halo gains positive <angular momentum> and exerts the opposite <torque> on the <stellar bar>. If the halo <mass> is $M_h$ and the rigid <stellar bar> has constant positive <moment of inertia> $I_b$, then
$$
\boxed{I_b\frac{d\Omega_{\mathrm p}}{dt}=-M_h\mathcal T\leq0.}
$$
This <bar-halo angular-momentum transfer> is a form of <dynamical friction>: a resonantly coupled <stellar bar> tends to slow down while the <dark matter halo> spins up. Actual long-term evolution changes both the <stellar bar> pattern and the halo <distribution function>, so the fixed-pattern response must then be updated. If the <stellar bar> changes its <moment of inertia>, the general relation is $d(I_b\Omega_{\mathrm p})/dt=-M_h\mathcal T$; positive <moment of inertia> alone would not fix the sign of $d\Omega_{\mathrm p}/dt$ without that rigidity assumption.
