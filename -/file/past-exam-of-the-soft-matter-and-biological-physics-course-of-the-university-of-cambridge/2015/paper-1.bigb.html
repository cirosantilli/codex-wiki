= Solution
{parent=1}

Write $\varepsilon=\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r$ and take $n_0$ to be the reservoir <density> of **each** monovalent ion species. Equal electrochemical potentials in equilibrium give the <Boltzmann ion densities in a symmetric electrolyte>,$$
n_+=n_0e^{-e\psi/(k_BT)},\qquad n_-=n_0e^{e\psi/(k_BT)},\qquad\rho_e=e(n_+-n_-)=-2en_0\sinh\frac{e\psi}{k_BT}.
$$
Combining this with the electrostatic <Poisson equation> gives the planar <Poisson-Boltzmann equation>,$$
\psi''=\frac{2en_0}{\varepsilon}\sinh\frac{e\psi}{k_BT},\qquad\boxed{\kappa^2=\frac{2e^2n_0}{\varepsilon k_BT}.}
$$
The <Debye length> is $\kappa^{-1}$. If the symbol $n_0$ instead denotes the combined <density> of both species, the factor two is absent; the per-species convention above makes the normalization explicit. At $|e\psi|\ll k_BT$, the <linearized planar screening solution> obeys $\psi''=\kappa^2\psi$. With $\psi(0)=\zeta<0$ and $\psi(\infty)=0$,$$
\boxed{\psi(x)=\zeta e^{-\kappa x}.}
$$

The positive <ions> are <counterions> and the negative <ions> are <co-ions>. At weak potential,$$
n_+\simeq n_0\left(1-\frac{e\zeta}{k_BT}e^{-\kappa x}\right),\qquad n_-\simeq n_0\left(1+\frac{e\zeta}{k_BT}e^{-\kappa x}\right).
$$
They approach $n_0$ from above and below respectively, with equal first-order excess and deficit. For stronger negative potential, interpret the source's strong-potential comparison as a statement about **$|e\psi|$**, since $\psi$ itself is negative. <Counterions> become strongly concentrated near the wall, while <co-ions> are exponentially depleted and remain nonnegative. Linearization misses this asymmetry and would eventually predict unphysical negative <co-ion> <density>. Point-ion <mean-field theory> itself eventually needs finite-size and <correlation> corrections; it provides no steric cap at arbitrarily large <counterion> concentration.

For steady tangential <electro-osmosis>, with no imposed <pressure> gradient and negligible inertia, the <Stokes equation> is $\eta v''+\rho_eE=0$. Since $\rho_e=-\varepsilon\psi''$,$$
\eta v''=\varepsilon E\psi''.
$$
The conditions $v(0)=0$ and $v'(\infty)=0$ give$$
v(x)=\frac{\varepsilon E}{\eta}[\psi(x)-\zeta]=U(1-e^{-\kappa x}),\qquad\boxed{U=-\frac{\varepsilon\zeta E}{\eta}.}
$$
**The printed constant speed is the bulk <velocity> outside the double layer, not the <velocity> at the no-slip wall.** With negative $\zeta$ the positive <counterion> excess drives fluid along the applied field. The far-field value is the <Helmholtz-Smoluchowski electro-osmotic velocity>.<Induced-Charge Electro-osmosis and Electrophoresis by Martin Z. Bazant>[Section1,page1: diffuse charge, slip <velocity> and plug-flow limit]{ref}

For the finite channel, first model the added wall as an ideally uncharged insulating boundary with no normal field on its outer side: $\psi'(d)=0$, while $\psi(0)=\zeta$. The <one-charged-wall electro-osmotic channel> then has$$
\psi(x)=\zeta\frac{\cosh[\kappa(d-x)]}{\cosh(\kappa d)}.
$$
Integrating the same <force> balance and enforcing $v(0)=v(d)=0$ gives$$
\boxed{v(x)=U\left[1-\left(1-\operatorname{sech}(\kappa d)\right)\frac xd-\frac{\cosh[\kappa(d-x)]}{\cosh(\kappa d)}\right].}
$$
For $U>0$, the maximum occurs at$$
\boxed{x_{\max}=d-\frac1\kappa\operatorname{arsinh}\left[\frac{\cosh(\kappa d)-1}{\kappa d}\right]\simeq\frac{\log(\kappa d)}\kappa\quad(\kappa d\gg1).}
$$
In that limit $v/U\simeq1-x/d-e^{-\kappa x}$: the <velocity> rises rapidly from zero at the charged wall, reaches a maximum a few screening lengths away, then falls approximately linearly to zero at the neutral wall. **One charged wall does not give a symmetric plug across a channel with no-slip at the other wall.**

An alternative electrode condition $\psi(d)=0$ means a wall held at reservoir potential; it is not the same as zero surface charge. Under that convention,$$
v(x)=U\left[1-\frac xd-\frac{\sinh[\kappa(d-x)]}{\sinh(\kappa d)}\right],
$$
with $\cosh[\kappa(d-x_{\max})]=\sinh(\kappa d)/(\kappa d)$. Both boundary conventions have the same leading thin-double-layer profile above. An externally imposed <pressure> gradient would add a <pressure>-driven profile; none is specified here.

For dilute nonadsorbing uncharged <polymers> in equilibrium or sufficiently weak flow, the <entropic polymer depletion near nonadsorbing walls> suppresses chain configurations near each wall. A depletion layer of order the coil <radius of gyration> is expected at both walls, with a bulk-like central region when $d$ is much larger than the coil size. Strong confinement rounds this into a central enrichment. Neutrality removes a direct electrophoretic <force>, not excluded-volume <entropy>. Adsorption, concentration and strong-flow migration were not specified, so this is a conditional equilibrium sketch rather than a universal flowing-<polymer> profile.

The generic instrument uses a magnetically responsive bead, a controllable external field and optical tracking of bead position or orientation. A field gradient supplies translational <force>; rotating an appropriate anisotropic field or bead moment can supply <torque>. For a permanent dipole,$$
U=-\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf B,\qquad\mathbf F=\nabla(\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf B),\qquad\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf m\times\mathbf B.
$$
For an isotropic unsaturated induced moment with effective susceptibility contrast $\Delta\chi$ and bead volume $V$, the <induced magnetic bead force> instead follows from$$
U=-\frac{V\Delta\chi}{2\mu_0}B^2,\qquad\boxed{\mathbf F=\frac{V\Delta\chi}{2\mu_0}\nabla B^2.}
$$
The factor one-half accounts for inducing the moment. A spatially uniform field alone supplies no translational <force>; an isotropic induced moment parallel to the field supplies no orienting <torque> either. Saturation or magnetic anisotropy requires the corresponding <magnetization> model.

Position tracking needs a length calibration, and <force> calibration needs measured field gradients, a known reference load, or calibrated <thermal fluctuations>. For a generic tensioned tether of extension $\ell$, small transverse displacement $x$ costs energy $Fx^2/(2\ell)$, so $F=k_BT\ell/\langle x^2\rangle$ under equilibrium, small-angle and single-mode assumptions. Attachment geometry, bead rotation, wall drag and finite acquisition bandwidth can affect this calibration. These equations describe generic <magnetic tweezers> mechanics, without assigning a biological response.

**<Fluctuation-induced forces> arise because separation changes the fluctuations available to a system and therefore changes its <free energy>.** The <force> is $F=-\partial\mathcal F/\partial d$ at fixed relevant control variables, not necessarily a <force> from a permanent charge or dipole.

For two neutral polarizable particles, correlated electromagnetic fluctuations give a short-distance dispersion attraction of the form $-C_6/r^6$. Retardation changes the asymptotic distance dependence. Boundaries produce a related <Casimir force>; for parallel ideal conducting plates at zero <temperature>,$$
\frac{\mathcal E}{A}=-\frac{\pi^2\hbar c}{720d^3},\qquad\boxed{P=-\frac{\pi^2\hbar c}{240d^4}.}
$$
The negative <pressure> denotes attraction. Material response, nonplanar geometry and finite <temperature> alter the ideal result. The effect can be described through the separation-dependent electromagnetic mode spectrum rather than by treating a divergent absolute vacuum energy as observable.<Quantum Field Theory by David Tong>[Section2.3.2: Casimir effect and boundary-dependent fluctuation energy]{ref}

In a classical <depletion force>, smaller particles or <polymer> coils are excluded from a region near larger surfaces. Overlap of excluded regions increases their available volume. For ideal dilute depletants of osmotic <pressure> $\Pi$, $U(d)=-\Pi V_{\rm overlap}(d)$; the resulting attraction is entropic and scales with <temperature>. Confined interface fluctuations can instead generate repulsion when close approach removes <entropy>. Near a continuous phase transition, a large <correlation length> makes boundary-conditioned order-parameter fluctuations produce a <critical Casimir force>. Its sign depends on the boundary preferences, so fluctuation forces are not universally attractive.

The useful common idea is a free-energy difference with explicit boundary conditions; quantum vacuum, thermal field and excluded-volume mechanisms have different physical fluctuations and different ranges.

A generic chemical <reaction-diffusion system> couples local kinetics to spatial transport:$$
\partial_tu=f(u,v)+D_u\nabla^2u,\qquad\partial_tv=g(u,v)+D_v\nabla^2v,\qquad D_u,D_v>0.
$$
A homogeneous stationary state satisfies $f=g=0$. Let its reaction Jacobian be $J=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$. Local well-mixed stability requires $a+d<0$ and $ad-bc>0$. A Fourier perturbation of wavevector magnitude $q$ evolves under $J-q^2\operatorname{diag}(D_u,D_v)$, whose determinant is$$
D_uD_vq^4-(D_va+D_ud)q^2+\det J.
$$
Since its trace remains negative, a <diffusion-driven stationary instability> appears if the determinant becomes negative for some allowed wavevector. In a spatially large domain the conditions are$$
\boxed{D_va+D_ud>0,\qquad(D_va+D_ud)^2>4D_uD_v\det J.}
$$
Together with reaction-only stability, these give a finite unstable wavelength range. Equal <diffusion> coefficients cannot generate this instability: they simply shift each reaction eigenvalue by $-Dq^2$. On a finite domain, an admissible spatial eigenmode must actually lie in the unstable band. Nonlinearities then determine saturation and whether a predicted pattern is stable, so a linear unstable band alone does not calculate a final pattern.<Notes on the Turing Instability and Chemical Instabilities by Michael Cross>[Linear stability of two-component chemical reaction-<diffusion> equations]{ref}

Other kinetics support travelling fronts or oscillatory waves rather than a stationary pattern. For instance, the scalar abstract equation $\partial_tu=D\partial_x^2u+ru(1-u/u_s)$ has a pulled-front speed $2\sqrt{Dr}$ for a sufficiently steep invading initial profile. Here it is used as an abstract kinetics-and-<diffusion> example, without a biological interpretation. The ratio of <diffusion> time $L^2/D$ to reaction time characterizes whether spatial mixing is rapid enough to justify a well-mixed model.

Only the separable instrument and mechanical tasks are treated here; biological operation and stepping energetics remain deferred.

For generic <optical tweezers>, a tightly focused laser and objective with high <numerical aperture> create an intensity gradient that attracts a suitable dielectric bead toward the focus. <Momentum transfer> also creates a scattering <force>; stable three-dimensional trapping requires sufficient restoring gradient <force>. A condenser and photodetector or camera track displacement. Near a stable focus, $F_x=-kx$ defines the stiffness and requires both a position calibration and a <force> calibration.<Observation of a single-beam gradient force optical trap for dielectric particles by Arthur Ashkin and others>[Single-beam gradient trapping of dielectric particles]{ref}

At thermal equilibrium in a harmonic well, the <equipartition trap calibration> is $k\langle(x-\langle x\rangle)^2\rangle=k_BT$. Alternatively, fitting the <overdamped harmonic-trap power spectrum> gives $f_c=k/(2\pi\gamma)$, hence $k=2\pi\gamma f_c$. Detector noise, finite sampling and exposure time, wall corrections to drag, and drift must be controlled rather than attributed to stiffness.

For an isolated sphere in a Newtonian fluid, define $\mathrm{Re}=\rho_fv(2a)/\eta$ using its diameter. The supplied <viscosity> converts to $10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}$. The generic <Stokes drag> calculation gives$$
\boxed{F_D=6\pi\eta av=1.88\times10^{-14}\,\mathrm N=0.0188\,\mathrm{pN},\qquad\gamma=6\pi\eta a=1.88\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N\,s\,m^{-1}}.}
$$
Here $\mathrm{Re}=2\times10^{-6}[\rho_f/(10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}})]$; this retains the unspecified fluid <density> as a parameter rather than treating a <density> as supplied data. <Stokes drag> assumes an isolated no-slip sphere; nearby surfaces change the coefficient. Ignoring biological coupling, the mechanical thermal model is the <Langevin equation> for a harmonic trap,$$
m\ddot x+\gamma\dot x+kx=\xi(t),\qquad\langle\xi(t)\rangle=0,\qquad\langle\xi(t)\xi(t')\rangle=2\gamma k_BT\delta(t-t').
$$
For independent repeats with the specified initial conditions, the ensemble <mean> $X=\langle x\rangle$ obeys the homogeneous damped-oscillator equation. An average over $N$ independent zero-<mean> <force> samples reduces its <variance> by $N$, with a finite detector bandwidth needed to define samples of white noise. **High bandwidth alone does not remove random forces; averaging independent records or sufficiently separated samples is the additional operation.** A single trajectory retains <thermal fluctuations>. For stationary overdamped position fluctuations, a long time average over duration $\mathcal T\gg\tau_k=\gamma/k$ has approximately $\operatorname{Var}(\bar x)\simeq2(k_BT/k)\tau_k/\mathcal T$.

The <initial-value solution of a damped harmonic trap> has characteristic roots $r_\pm=[-\gamma\pm\sqrt{\gamma^2-4mk}]/(2m)$. For distinct roots,$$
\boxed{X(t)=\frac{v_0-r_-x_0}{r_+-r_-}e^{r_+t}+\frac{r_+x_0-v_0}{r_+-r_-}e^{r_-t}.}
$$
For critical damping, $\gamma^2=4mk$,$$
X(t)=e^{-\gamma t/(2m)}\left[x_0+\left(v_0+\frac{\gamma x_0}{2m}\right)t\right].
$$
For the underdamped case the same real solution can be written $e^{-\gamma t/(2m)}[x_0\cos\omega_dt+(v_0+\gamma x_0/(2m))\sin\omega_dt/\omega_d]$, with $\omega_d^2=k/m-\gamma^2/(4m^2)$. These describe the deterministic response or conditional <mean>, not the entire noisy trajectory.

For <overdamped trap relaxation after an inertial boundary layer>, let $\epsilon=km/\gamma^2\ll1$. The slow and fast roots are$$
r_s=-\frac k\gamma[1+O(\epsilon)],\qquad r_f=-\frac\gamma m[1+O(\epsilon)],\qquad\tau_m=\frac m\gamma\ll\tau_k=\frac\gamma k.
$$
After $t\gg\tau_m$, the fast exponential is negligible and the surviving amplitude is $x_0+(m/\gamma)v_0+O(\epsilon x_0,\epsilon mv_0/\gamma)$. If also $|(m/\gamma)v_0|\ll|x_0|$, then to leading overdamped order$$
\boxed{X(t)\simeq x_0e^{-kt/\gamma}.}
$$
Small <Reynolds number> justifies the linear viscous <force>; it does not alone prove the separate small inertial-trap parameter $\epsilon$. Neglecting $v_0$ requires both rapid <momentum> relaxation and a negligible slow-mode amplitude shift. If $x_0=0$, a nonzero initial <velocity> can leave a small slow displacement even after its own fast transient decays. The result concerns generic bead mechanics only.
