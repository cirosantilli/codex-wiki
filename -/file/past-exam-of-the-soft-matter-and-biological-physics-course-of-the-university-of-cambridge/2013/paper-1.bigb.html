For the generic instrument, <magnetic tweezers> combine a magnetically responsive bead, a controllable <magnetic field gradient>, a tether attached to a fixed support, and optical tracking of bead coordinates. For a fixed <magnetic dipole moment>, the <force> is $\nabla(\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf B)$; an induced linear isotropic moment instead gives $\mathbf F=V\Delta\chi\nabla B^2/(2\mu_0)$. A uniform <magnetic field> can orient an anisotropic bead, but a gradient is needed for translation. Magnet height sets the load, while field rotation can impose <torque> if bead orientation is coupled to the field. Tracking uses lateral images and a calibrated axial image/defocus response; a fixed reference removes stage drift. A sufficiently slowly varying gradient approximates a <force clamp> over the bead's displacement. For <transverse-fluctuation calibration of a tensioned tether>, use an ideal tether of vertical extension $h$ whose endpoint is displaced by small $x,y$. Keeping its <length> fixed to quadratic order lowers its vertical coordinate by $(x^2+y^2)/(2h)$, increasing load potential by $F_z(x^2+y^2)/(2h)$. The <equipartition theorem> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{F_z=\frac{k_BTh}{\langle\delta x^2\rangle}
=\frac{k_BTh}{\langle\delta y^2\rangle}
=\frac{2k_BTh}{\langle\delta x^2+\delta y^2\rangle}.}
$$
Here fluctuations are about the mean. The height is the effective pendulum <length> of the measured coordinate, not automatically the <polymer contour length>. The displayed $h$ formula uses the point-endpoint or fixed-bead-orientation idealization; freely rotating large beads can require a bead-radius correction to that lever arm. The <finite-exposure correction to fluctuation force calibration> restores variance lost through exposure averaging; ignoring it would overestimate <force>; localization noise increases it and would underestimate <force>. Drift, wall coupling, finite observation time and magnetic lateral stiffness must be checked. Vertical fluctuations measure longitudinal stiffness and do not obey the same geometrical formula.

For the <stretch and twist coefficients of an elastic filament>, take $s$ as reference contour <length> and $\epsilon$ as local axial <strain>. The three positive quadratic costs represent bending of the tangent, stretching of the material and excess twist about its relaxed orientation. With the paper's coefficient convention,
$$
K_b=k_BTA,\qquad S=k_BTB,\qquad K_t=k_BTC.
$$
$K_b,K_t$ are bending and torsional rigidities with units of <energy> times <length>; $S$ is an axial <force> modulus. Thus $A,C$ have units of <length> and $B$ inverse <length>. Positive quadratic forms describe a stable relaxed filament; odd terms vanish when expansion is about its zero-stress, zero-torque reference configuration.

At weak and intermediate <tension>, changing thermal bending conformations can extend the filament without appreciably stretching the backbone. Axial <strain> is $\epsilon\simeq F_z/S$, so material stretching must be included when this is no longer negligible. For a long strongly aligned but nearly inextensible three-dimensional <worm-like chain>, the thermal extension deficit is approximately $\tfrac12\sqrt{k_BT/(AF_z)}$; stretch adds approximately $F_z/S$ to fractional extension. Twist matters whenever <torque> or imposed end rotation is present, at any <tension>. A freely swivelling, <torque-free> tether need not store excess twist. There is no universal force-only threshold for switching on torsional <energy>.

The <circular bending energy of an elastic filament> follows because curvature is $1/R$ over contour <length> $2\pi R$. Integrating the bending term gives
$$
\boxed{E_{\rm loop}=\frac{k_BTA}{2R^2}(2\pi R)=\frac{\pi k_BTA}{R}.}
$$
This is bending <energy> alone: imposed <tension>, stretching and twist would add costs. The dimensionless bending action is $(A/2)\int|\partial_s\mathbf t|^2ds$. Its tangent propagator in three dimensions obeys <rotational diffusion> on the unit sphere, $\partial_sP=(2A)^{-1}\nabla_{S^2}^2P$. The vector <spherical harmonic> has angular <eigenvalue> $-2$, giving
$$
\boxed{\langle\mathbf t(s)\cdot\mathbf t(0)\rangle=e^{-s/A}.}
$$
Hence $A$ is the <persistence length> in this three-dimensional convention. A strictly planar filament instead has tangent-correlation <length> $2A$; one cannot infer the factor merely by setting a loop's <energy> equal to $k_BT$.

Use a <force-tilted activation barrier> for a generic one-coordinate escape model. A tensile <force> tilts a <free-energy landscape> to $G_F(x)=G_0(x)-Fx$. If $x_b$ is the initial <minimum> and $x_s$ the saddle, define $\Delta x=x_s-x_b>0$ and the zero-force barrier $\Delta G_0^\ddagger=G_0(x_s)-G_0(x_b)$. The relevant activation barrier is measured relative to the initial <minimum>, not an arbitrary absolute transition-state <energy>. To first order in <force>,
$$
\Delta G^\ddagger(F)=\Delta G_0^\ddagger-F\Delta x,
\qquad
\boxed{k(F)=k_0e^{F/F_0},\quad F_0=\frac{k_BT}{\Delta x},\quad
k_0=\nu e^{-\Delta G_0^\ddagger/(k_BT)}.}
$$
The attempt prefactor $\nu$ is assumed <force> independent, and the saddle displacement is approximated as constant. This single-barrier approximation need not survive large landscape deformations or a change of escape pathway. For a generic force-ramp measurement, a calibrated <atomic force microscope> <cantilever> pulls an attachment while a stage changes separation. <cantilever> deflection $d$ gives <force> $k_cd$, and the <cantilever compliance correction> subtracts $d$ from imposed separation to give tether extension, with further corrections for other apparatus compliance. A prescribed stage speed does not automatically produce constant <force> loading rate when tether stiffness varies; the assumed $F(t)=ft$ must be measured or controlled. The original schematic below illustrates a generic nonbiological bond/tether geometry. Protein-specific construction is deferred.

The no-rebinding approximation is a physical escape assumption: after the link breaks, separation removes the partners from the capture region before appreciable reassociation. It requires the integrated return/association probability during that separation to be small. If the separated state stays nearby or the <force> is held without separation, setting $k_+=0$ can fail. Under the stipulated approximation, with the link initially intact,
$$
\frac{dp}{dF}=-\frac{k_0}{f}e^{F/F_0}p,
\qquad
\boxed{p(F)=\exp\left[-\frac{k_0F_0}{f}(e^{F/F_0}-1)\right],\quad F\geq0.}
$$
This <survival under a linear force ramp> differs from the <probability density> for rupture. It starts at one, decreases to zero and shifts to larger <force> as the loading rate increases. For small $F/F_0$, $p\simeq e^{-k_0F/f}$; a slow ramp allows much escape before substantial <force> is reached.

The <median rupture force under a linear ramp> satisfies $p=1/2$, giving
$$
\boxed{F_{1/2}=F_0\ln\left(1+\frac{f\ln2}{k_0F_0}\right).}
$$
At high loading rate, the slope of $F_{1/2}$ against $\ln f$ tends to $F_0$; it is not exactly $F_0$ for a slow ramp. Fit the full expression across loading rates to recover $F_0$ and $k_0$. The rupture-force <probability density> is $\rho(F)=k(F)p(F)/f$, whose <most probable rupture force under a linear ramp> is $F_*=F_0\ln[f/(k_0F_0)]$ if $f>k_0F_0$, and zero otherwise. <median> and <mode> are different observables and must not be interchanged when fitting.

At known <temperature>, $F_0$ yields the activation distance $\Delta x=k_BT/F_0$; $1/k_0$ gives the zero-force mean lifetime in this exponential model. Absolute barrier height additionally requires the attempt prefactor, and neither equilibrium binding <free energy> nor an association rate follows from dissociation-only data. A loading-rate-dependent rupture <force> is a kinetic readout, not an intrinsic equilibrium strength. TODO: **user-deferred biological conclusions**. In <gel sieving of synthetic polymers>, <gel electrophoresis> combines drift under an <electric field> with size- and conformation-dependent obstruction by a porous matrix. In weak-field free solution, <force> balance gives $v=\mu E$ and, in a simple effective-charge/effective-friction model, $\mu=q_{\rm eff}/\zeta$. The <mobility> is motion relative to the medium, so fluid motion from <electro-osmosis> must be accounted for separately. This is the operational <mobility> convention, not a claim that the bare structural charge is always the effective driving charge.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology, fifth edition (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry>[entry E02024, electrophoretic mobility: migration speed divided by electric field strength]{ref}

For a chain with <free-draining polymer friction> with approximately constant effective <electric charge> per segment, both charge and friction scale with $N$. The free-solution <mobility> can therefore become nearly independent of <length>, making size separation poor. A gel adds steric constraints. Coils smaller than the mesh can pass relatively easily; larger coils require deformation and pore-to-pore rearrangement. A schematic weak-sieving law is $\mu(c_g)\simeq\mu(0)e^{-K_rc_g}$, where matrix <concentration> $c_g$ and retardation coefficient $K_r$ are defined by a calibration; it is not a universal size law.

When a long flexible charged <polymer> is confined to a tube of available paths, <reptation> supplies another regime. An additive-friction model gives contour <diffusion> $D_c\propto N^{-1}$ and renewal time $\tau\sim N^2/D_c\propto N^3$. For ideal large-scale chain statistics, $R^2\propto N$, so centre-of-mass <diffusion> scales as $N^{-2}$. With total effective driving charge proportional to $N$, weak-field <mobility> can then scale as $N^{-1}$. This assumes screened/ideal large-scale conformations and <linear response>; strong-field alignment, trapping and nonideal matrices can alter the scaling.

Operationally, load narrow zones of independently characterized synthetic standards and sample into a controlled gel, apply a known field and detect migration positions after a measured time. Compare with a free-solution measurement, vary matrix <concentration> and field, and control <temperature>, <ionic strength>, <pH> and matrix charge. Larger gel pores or lower matrix <concentration> usually reduce sieving within a given calibrated material system. Excessive field causes <Joule heating> and can change conformation and <mobility>. <adsorption> or electro-osmotic drift can mimic a size effect; standards should match charge chemistry and <topology> as well as nominal mass.

Band centres move as $\mu Et$ while ordinary <diffusion> gives $\sigma_x^2(t)=\sigma_x^2(0)+2Dt$. Resolution depends on separation of centres relative to band widths, not travel distance alone. A neutral tracer helps identify bulk flow, and multiple <concentrations> test <adsorption> or overloading. **A <mobility> or band position does not uniquely determine size without a regime-specific calibration.** TODO: biomolecular examples, sample preparation and biological applications are user deferred; the account here concerns synthetic charged <polymers> only.

Use SI units, uniform absolute <permittivity> $\varepsilon$ and a neutral bulk reservoir. Local equilibrium of ideal mobile <ions> gives $n_i=n_{i,\infty}e^{-z_ie\psi/(k_BT)}$. Combining their charge with the <Poisson equation> yields
$$
\boxed{\nabla^2\psi=-\frac1\varepsilon\left[\rho_f+\sum_i z_ie n_{i,\infty}e^{-z_ie\psi/(k_BT)}\right].}
$$
The reference is $\psi=0$ in the bulk. Boundary data specify fixed charge, fixed potential or a surface reaction/<charge regulation> law. These are physically different problems. The <mean-field theory> assumes point <ions>, <thermal equilibrium> and a continuum <solvent>; ion-size packing, dielectric structure and strong electrostatic correlations can invalidate it.

For a symmetric monovalent electrolyte let $n_\infty$ count each sign and define $u=e\psi/(k_BT)$, $\kappa_D^2=2e^2n_\infty/(\varepsilon k_BT)$. Outside fixed sources,
$$
\nabla^2u=\kappa_D^2\sinh u.
$$
For $|u|\ll1$, the Debye-Hückel approximation is $\nabla^2u=\kappa_D^2u$. A planar wall bounding electrolyte in $z>0$ has $\psi=\psi_0e^{-\kappa_Dz}$; assuming zero field on the other side, its charge is $\sigma=\varepsilon\kappa_D\psi_0$. A sphere of <radius> $a$ and total charge $Q$ instead gives
$$
\psi(r)=\frac{Qe^{-\kappa_D(r-a)}}{4\pi\varepsilon r(1+\kappa_Da)},\qquad r\geq a.
$$
The factors arise from geometry and <boundary conditions>; planar screening cannot be substituted unchanged for a small sphere. The weak-potential condition must hold where the potential is largest, particularly at the surface.

The <nonlinear planar Poisson-Boltzmann profile> is also exactly integrable. Multiply $u''=\kappa_D^2\sinh u$ by $u'$ and use $u,u'\to0$ at infinity:
$$
(u')^2=2\kappa_D^2(\cosh u-1),\qquad
u'=-2\kappa_D\sinh(u/2).
$$
The sign selects a profile decaying to the bulk for either sign of surface potential. Integration gives
$$
\boxed{u(z)=4\operatorname{artanh}\left[\gamma e^{-\kappa_Dz}\right],\qquad
\gamma=\tanh(u_0/4).}
$$
For weak $u_0$ this reduces to the linear solution. Strong surface potentials have a bounded far-field amplitude $4\gamma$, while the near-wall <ion> <density> still grows strongly. There is no need to permit the negative <ion> <densities> produced by an invalid extrapolation of a linearized <density> formula.

The same first integral yields the <Grahame equation> and its <diffuse-layer differential capacitance> per unit <area>:
$$
\boxed{\sigma=\frac{2\varepsilon\kappa_Dk_BT}{e}\sinh(u_0/2),\qquad
C_D=\frac{d\sigma}{d\psi_0}=\varepsilon\kappa_D\cosh(u_0/2).}
$$
These describe the <diffuse layer>. A compact interfacial layer adds a voltage drop and can place its <capacitance> in series with $C_D$; a measured electrode <capacitance> need not equal the ideal diffuse contribution.<The Electrical Double Layer and the Theory of Electrocapillarity by David C. Grahame>[section VI, printed pp. 470–474, especially equations (31)–(48); diffuse charge and differential versus integral capacitance]{ref}

For comparison, a <planar counterion-only double layer> next to a negative plane has positive <counterions> and no salt reservoir. Its profile is algebraic:
$$
\psi(z)-\psi(0)=\frac{2k_BT}{e}\ln(1+z/b),\qquad
n(z)=\frac1{2\pi\ell_B(z+b)^2},\qquad
b=\frac{e}{2\pi\ell_B|\sigma|},\quad
\ell_B=\frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon k_BT}.
$$
Integrating $en(z)$ over $z>0$ gives $|\sigma|$, checking charge neutrality. This problem cannot be obtained by putting a finite reservoir <density> into the symmetric-electrolyte answer without changing its <boundary conditions>.

Applications include screening around synthetic <colloids>, diffuse-layer electrode <capacitance> and <forces> between charged plates. In planar geometry, the bulk-subtracted normal <stress> is constant:
$$
\Pi=k_BT(n_++n_--2n_\infty)-\frac{\varepsilon E^2}{2}.
$$
At the midpoint of identical plates, symmetry sets $E=0$, so the remaining excess ionic <osmotic pressure> determines their repulsion. A comparison with <force> or <capacitance> measurements should vary salt and surface potential independently, test <charge regulation> and <adsorption>, and distinguish compact-layer effects from failures of diffuse screening. Nonlinear Poisson-Boltzmann theory retains finite-potential Boltzmann populations, but still omits correlations and finite-ion-volume saturation.
