<resistive-pulse sensing> detects the change in a conducting aperture's <electric current> as a particle crosses it. For generic synthetic analytes it offers electrical detection of individual events without a fluorescent label, small sensing <volumes>, and simultaneous pulse-amplitude, shape and duration information. Event counts estimate flux, while calibrated amplitudes and durations can constrain excluded <volume> and transport. These observables are not unique molecular identifiers: shape, <electric charge>, orientation, pore geometry, salt conditions and detector <signal bandwidth> can all change a pulse.

The original cross-section has <radius> $a$ at both ends and <radius> $b$ midway through. In the intended one-dimensional series-resistance approximation, put $s=(b-a)/l$ and
$$
r(z)=\begin{cases}a+sz,&0\leq z\leq l,\\a+s(2l-z),&l\leq z\leq2l.\end{cases}
$$
For constant ionic <electrical resistivity> $\rho$, a slice contributes $d\mathcal R=\rho\,dz/[\pi r(z)^2]$. The <ionic resistance of a conical pore> then gives
$$
\mathcal R=\frac{2\rho}{\pi}\int_0^l\frac{dz}{(a+sz)^2}
=\frac{2\rho}{\pi s}\left(\frac1a-\frac1b\right),\qquad
\boxed{\mathcal R=\frac{2\rho l}{\pi ab}.}
$$
This assumes locally Ohmic transport with negligible surface conduction and approximately uniform axial <current density> over each slice. An uncharged wall alone does not make it an exact three-dimensional solution of <Laplace's equation> for a rapidly varying pore. <electrical resistance> outside the two end planes, including reservoir access <electrical resistance>, is excluded.

Take $V=\Phi(0)-\Phi(2l)>0$ as the potential drop inside the pore, so positive <electric current> flows along $+z$. <current conservation> and <Ohm's law> give
$$
I_0=\frac{V}{\mathcal R}=\frac{\pi abV}{2\rho l},\qquad
\boxed{E(z)=\frac{\rho I_0}{\pi r(z)^2}=\frac{Vab}{2l\,r(z)^2}.}
$$
This is the leading cross-section-averaged axial <electric field>, and the corresponding on-axis estimate in the same approximation. It is largest at the two narrow ends and smallest at the wide centre:
$$
E(0)=E(2l)=\frac{Vb}{2la},\qquad E(l)=\frac{Va}{2lb},\qquad
\frac{E(0)}{E(l)}=\left(\frac ba\right)^2.
$$
The graph is symmetric, falls on the first half and rises on the second, with a minimum at $l$. The <electric potential difference> check is $\int_0^{2l}E(z)\,dz=V$. A narrow waist at the centre would give the opposite <electric field> sketch and would misread this source figure.

Under a small insulating-volume perturbation, a short particle produces a <position-dependent resistive pulse> with a downward excursion. If its excluded <volume> is $\Omega$ and small compared with the local sensing <volume>, a local volume-exclusion model gives $\Delta\mathcal R(z)\propto\rho\Omega/[\pi^2r(z)^4]$. Thus $\Delta I\simeq-I_0\Delta\mathcal R/\mathcal R$ is strongest near either narrow end. A sufficiently resolved event can show two deeper end features separated by a shallower middle depression, then return to baseline. Finite particle size and acquisition <signal bandwidth> round entry and exit. The original illustration below shows this conditional small-particle mechanism, not a fitted amplitude. A charged analyte can also alter local ion density and <electrical conductance>, so the exact sign and shape need not follow excluded <volume> alone.

For positive drift in this coordinate, require $\mu V>0$. The <electrophoretic transit time in a conical pore> is the integral of inverse local drift speed:
$$
\Delta t=\int_0^{2l}\frac{dz}{\mu E(z)}
=\frac{2l}{\mu Vab}\int_0^{2l}r(z)^2dz
=\frac{4l}{\mu Vab}\frac{b^3-a^3}{3s}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Delta t=\frac{4l^2}{3\mu Vab}\frac{b^3-a^3}{b-a}
=\frac{4l^2}{3\mu V}\left(\frac ba+1+\frac ab\right).}
$$
Reverse the polarity or use $|\mu V|$ when reporting a positive transit duration in the opposite direction. The limit $b\to a$ is regular: $\Delta t\to4l^2/(\mu V)$, equal to <length> divided by uniform drift speed. Also $b/a+a/b\geq2$, so nonuniform <electric field> makes this point-particle transit no faster than the uniform-field value at the same <length> and <electric potential difference>. The wide, weak-field interior dominates residence time.

The final length-dependent observations do not uniquely identify a microscopic mechanism. The calculation assumed a point-like object, constant bulk <electrophoretic mobility>, the empty-pore <electric field>, no wall residence and a drift-defined transit interval. An extended chain can sample entrance and reservoir fields simultaneously, alter its conformation and tension, and change the effective driving-to-friction ratio. These effects can make a bulk-mobility, local-field prediction underestimate the driving. For a small object, wall interactions, reversible trapping, or repeated entry excursions can add residence time that the deterministic formula omits. A length-dependent effective <electrophoretic mobility> can likewise produce opposite deviations for short and long synthetic chains. Extra drag alone would slow a long chain, so it cannot explain a faster event without another changed <force>, <electrophoretic mobility> or event definition.

Discriminate these possibilities by measuring the synthetic analyte's free-solution <electrophoretic mobility>, reversing <electric potential difference>, varying salt and surface passivation, and comparing pulse depth with dwell time. Resolved multiple occupancy or folds can alter amplitude as well as timing. Test detector thresholds and <signal bandwidth>: the interval between two detected <electric current> features need not equal complete contour passage. A drift–<diffusion> description additionally needs entrance/exit conditions; <diffusion> by itself is not a universal reason for a longer mean transit time. No unique anomalous-duration prediction follows from the provided parameters alone.

In <optical tweezers>, a tightly focused <laser> produces a restoring gradient <force> on a suitable transparent bead. Scattering also transfers <momentum> along propagation, so a stable three-dimensional trap needs axial as well as lateral restoration. An objective with high <numerical aperture>, correctly filled entrance pupil, controlled beam <polarization> and low-aberration sample chamber are practical design elements. Collect transmitted/scattered light with a condenser and measure bead <displacement> with calibrated imaging or back-focal-plane photodetection. A steerable beam or translation stage sets relative positions; the <force> signal comes from bead <displacement>, not commanded stage <displacement> alone.

Near a stable operating point, use a <harmonic optical trap> with $U(x)=kx^2/2$. For a slowly changing external <force>, $F_{\rm ext}=k\langle x\rangle$ in that coordinate; the optical <restoring force> is $-kx$. Calibrate each axis and the range over which its response is linear. Detector sensitivity must also be known: write $x=\beta(s-s_0)$, with measured detector voltage $s$, offset $s_0$ and position sensitivity $\beta$ in $\mathrm{m/V}$. A <spring constant> in arbitrary detector units is not a <force> calibration.

Three complementary physical calibrations are useful. At <thermal equilibrium> the <equipartition theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{k=\frac{k_BT}{\langle(x-\langle x\rangle)^2\rangle}.}
$$
This needs an independently calibrated position scale and a <variance> corrected for drift, detector noise and exposure averaging. In an overdamped Newtonian medium, the <optical trap Langevin calibration> obeys
$$
\zeta\dot x=-kx+\xi(t),\qquad
\langle\xi(t)\xi(t')\rangle=2k_BT\zeta\delta(t-t').
$$
Using a one-sided position <power spectral density> per hertz,
$$
\boxed{S_x(f)=\frac{k_BT}{\pi^2\zeta(f^2+f_c^2)},\qquad
f_c=\frac{k}{2\pi\zeta},\qquad\int_0^\infty S_x(f)df=\frac{k_BT}{k}.}
$$
The corner <frequency> and spectral amplitude test both relaxation and fluctuation size. If drag is known, $k=2\pi\zeta f_c$. The isolated-sphere value $\zeta=6\pi\eta a$ must be corrected near boundaries; otherwise measured height dependence can be mistaken for a changed trap stiffness. An independent steady <optical trap drag calibration> moves fluid past the trapped sphere: $k\langle x\rangle=\zeta u$. Use small positive and negative speeds to verify slope, offset and <force> range.

One can also determine position sensitivity without assuming the bulk drag. In a low-frequency <sinusoidal-stage optical trap calibration>, let the known local fluid <velocity> amplitude be $u_0$ at drive <angular frequency> $\Omega$. The coherent bead <displacement> amplitude is
$$
A_x=\frac{\zeta u_0}{\sqrt{k^2+\Omega^2\zeta^2}}
=\frac{u_0}{\sqrt{(2\pi f_c)^2+\Omega^2}}.
$$
If the corresponding detector amplitude is $A_s$, then
$$
\boxed{\beta=\frac{u_0}{A_s\sqrt{(2\pi f_c)^2+\Omega^2}}.}
$$
After removing the coherent drive line from the thermal record, combine this sensitivity with <equipartition theorem> to recover $k$, and then infer $\zeta$ from $f_c$. A published calibration combines thermal spectra and stage-driven motion without taking bead drag as an input.<Calibration of optical tweezers with positional detection in the back-focal-plane by Simon F. Tolić-Nørrelykke and others>[2006 author preprint, abstract: combined thermal/stage calibration and drag-independent sensitivity]{ref}

Controls determine whether these formulas measure the apparatus rather than its noise. Record blocked-light offsets and independent fixed-bead noise; calibrate <length> with known motion; keep sampling and electronics bandwidth adequate around $f_c$; and include exposure filtering and <aliasing> in a spectral fit. <motion blur> reduces measured <variance> and therefore makes naive <equipartition theorem> overestimate stiffness; additive position noise has the opposite bias. Changing exposure time provides a direct diagnostic.<The effect of integration time on fluctuation measurements: calibrating an optical trap in the presence of motion blur by Wesley P. Wong and others>[§II, equations (12)–(18): variance reduction, temporal filter and aliased spectrum]{ref} Check <temperature> and <laser> heating, wall separation, bead size and <refractive index>, alignment and power stability. Repeat after changing power or optical geometry and compare thermal, spectral and drag estimates. For a synthetic <force> handle, also measure the attachment's compliance and geometry so that bead motion is not automatically identified with its extension.

A <front solution> joins different asymptotic states in space. In a <reaction–diffusion system>, local nonlinear dynamics select possible states while <diffusion> couples neighboring points. A <travelling wave> with profile $u(x,t)=U(\xi)$, $\xi=x-ct$, converts a <partial differential equation> into a <boundary value problem> for the shape and speed. A pulse instead returns to the same resting state on both sides and can contain leading and trailing fronts.

A useful nondimensional <cubic fast-slow reaction-diffusion model>, in a convention of the <FitzHugh-Nagumo model>, is
$$
u_t=D u_{xx}+f(u)-w+I,\qquad
w_t=\varepsilon(u-\beta w),\qquad
f(u)=u(1-u)(u-\alpha),\quad0<\alpha<1,
$$
where $D>0$, $\varepsilon\ll1$, and $\beta>0$. Here $u$ is a fast activation variable, $w$ a slow negative-feedback variable and $I$ a generic drive; no physiological variable is assigned. Without spatial coupling, <nullclines> are $w=f(u)+I$ and $w=u/\beta$. At $I=0$, the resting state $(0,0)$ has <Jacobian matrix>
$$
\mathsf J=\begin{pmatrix}-\alpha&-1\\\varepsilon&-\varepsilon\beta\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\operatorname{tr}\mathsf J=-\alpha-\varepsilon\beta<0,\quad
\det\mathsf J=\varepsilon(1+\alpha\beta)>0.
$$
It has <linear stability>. With appropriate parameter choices a sufficiently large perturbation makes a fast excursion to the other outer branch of the cubic, followed by slow feedback and return. Thus a stable rest state can be excitable without being linearly unstable. Other parameters or applied drive can yield multiple equilibria or oscillations; these behaviours should not be asserted for every parameter set.

The fast-subsystem front can be obtained explicitly by freezing $w=I=0$. For the <bistable cubic reaction-diffusion equation>, $u=0,1$ are stable and $u=\alpha$ is unstable. The decreasing <logistic front of a bistable cubic equation> is
$$
\boxed{U(\xi)=\frac1{1+\exp[\xi/\sqrt{2D}]},\qquad
c=\sqrt{D/2}(1-2\alpha).}
$$
To check it, $U'=-U(1-U)/\sqrt{2D}$ and substitution into $DU''+cU'+f(U)=0$ cancels every term. For $\alpha<1/2$, the high state advances to the right; for $\alpha>1/2$, the direction reverses. At $\alpha=1/2$ the front is stationary. More generally, multiplying the travelling-wave equation by $U'$ and integrating yields
$$
c\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(U')^2d\xi=\int_0^1f(u)du=\frac{1-2\alpha}{12}.
$$
This links propagation direction to the bias between the two stable states and recovers <Maxwell balance for a scalar reaction-diffusion front> at zero speed. It is a bistable selected-speed problem, rather than a universal Fisher-type minimum-speed formula.

For the coupled fast-slow system, travelling profiles satisfy
$$
DU''+cU'+f(U)-W+I=0,\qquad
cW'+\varepsilon(U-\beta W)=0.
$$
A slow recovery wake can support a return front behind the advancing fast front, producing a travelling pulse for suitable parameters. Its width reflects the slow recovery time and propagation speed, while each sharp edge has the faster <diffusion>–reaction scale. The frozen-recovery speed above is a controlled subsystem result, not an exact speed for every coupled pulse. A definite pulse speed requires parameters and connecting <boundary condition>; none are specified by this brief-note question.
