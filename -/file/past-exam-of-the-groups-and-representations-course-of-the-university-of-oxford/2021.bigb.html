Let $T(v\otimes w)=w\otimes v$ on the <tensor product> $V\otimes V$. The <linear map> $R(g)\otimes R(g)$ commutes with $T$, so preserves its $+1$ and $-1$ <eigenspaces>, namely the <symmetric square> $S^2V$ and the <exterior square> $\wedge^2V$. Restricting the <tensor product representation> to these <invariant subspaces> defines $S^2R$ and $\wedge^2R$. The <representation> identity follows by restricting $(R(g)\otimes R(g))(R(h)\otimes R(h))=R(gh)\otimes R(gh)$.

For a <finite group>, $g$ has finite order $m$, so $R(g)^m=I$. Over the <complex numbers>, the <polynomial> $t^m-1$ has distinct roots; hence $R(g)$ is <diagonalizable>. Choose an <eigenbasis> $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ with <eigenvalues> $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$. A <basis> of the <symmetric square> consists of $v_i\otimes v_i$ and $v_i\otimes v_j+v_j\otimes v_i$ for $i<j$. A <basis> of the <exterior square> consists of $v_i\otimes v_j-v_j\otimes v_i$ for $i<j$. Their <eigenvalues> are respectively $\lambda_i^2,\lambda_i\lambda_j$ and $\lambda_i\lambda_j$. Taking <traces>,
$$
\chi_{S^2R}(g)=\sum_i\lambda_i^2+\sum_{i<j}\lambda_i\lambda_j,
\qquad \chi_{\wedge^2R}(g)=\sum_{i<j}\lambda_i\lambda_j.
$$
But $\chi(g)^2=\sum_i\lambda_i^2+2\sum_{i<j}\lambda_i\lambda_j$ and $\chi(g^2)=\sum_i\lambda_i^2$. Thus the <characters> are
$$
\boxed{\chi_{S^2R}(g)=\tfrac12[\chi(g)^2+\chi(g^2)],\qquad
\chi_{\wedge^2R}(g)=\tfrac12[\chi(g)^2-\chi(g^2)].}
$$
At $g=e$, these also give the <dimensions> $n(n+1)/2$ and $n(n-1)/2$.

Write the two <Pauli matrices> as
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
C=AB=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
They satisfy $A^2=B^2=I$, $BA=-AB$ and $C^2=-I$. Moving every $A$ to the left of every $B$ reduces each word to one of eight distinct <matrices>. Consequently
$$
\boxed{G=\{I,-I,A,-A,B,-B,C,-C\},\qquad |G|=8.}
$$
Taking $r=C$, $s=A$ gives $r^4=s^2=I$ and $srs=r^{-1}$, identifying the <group> with the <dihedral group> of a square.

The elements $\pm I$ are central. Conjugating $A$ by $B$ changes its sign, and similarly for $B$ and $C$. No conjugation mixes these pairs: modulo the central <subgroup> $\{\pm I\}$ the <quotient group> is the <Klein four-group> and hence <abelian>. Thus the <conjugacy classes> are precisely
$$
\boxed{\{I\},\quad\{-I\},\quad\{A,-A\},\quad\{B,-B\},\quad\{C,-C\}.}
$$
There are five complex <irreducible representations>, one for each <conjugacy class>. In a <one-dimensional representation>, the images of $A,B$ are signs $s,t\in\{1,-1\}$. Since they commute, $-I$ maps to $1$, and all four choices of $s,t$ satisfy the relations. These are four inequivalent <one-dimensional representations>. The <sum of squares of irreducible representation dimensions> leaves $8-4=4$ for the fifth, so the <dimensions> are
$$
\boxed{1,1,1,1,2.}
$$

Use the <conjugacy class> order of <1/b/solution>. The defining <representation> has <character>

\boxed{\chi_\rho=(2,-2,0,0,0).}

Its <character inner product> with itself is $(4+4)/8=1$, proving that it is an <irreducible representation>. There is also a direct <invariant subspace> argument: $B$ has two distinct coordinate <eigenspaces>, and $A$ exchanges them, so no line is invariant under both.

\boxed{\begin{array}{c|rrrrr}

\text{class size}&1&1&2&2&2\\\hline
L_{++}&1&1&1&1&1\\
L_{+-}&1&1&1&-1&-1\\
L_{-+}&1&1&-1&1&-1\\
L_{--}&1&1&-1&-1&1\\
\rho&2&-2&0&0&0
\end{array}}
$$
The four scalar rows obey <character orthogonality>, and the <dimensions> exhaust the <group order> as established in <1/b/solution>.

\boxed{S^2\rho=L_{++}\oplus L_{+-}\oplus L_{-+},\qquad

Write a homogeneous <quadratic polynomial> as $p(x,y)=ax^2+bxy+cy^2$. Invariance under $B:(x,y)\mapsto(x,-y)$ forces $b=0$. Invariance under $A:(x,y)\mapsto(y,x)$ then forces $a=c$. Since $A,B$ generate the <group>, this proves that all homogeneous quadratic <polynomial invariants> are
$$
\boxed{p(x,y)=a(x^2+y^2),\qquad a\in\mathbb C.}
$$
This agrees with the single <trivial representation> in the <symmetric square> in <1/d/solution>; more precisely, coordinate <polynomials> lie in the <symmetric square> of the <dual representation>, which here is equivalent to $\rho$. If “quadratic” permits terms of smaller degree, arbitrary constants can be added. There are no invariant linear terms because $-I\in G$ negates every linear <polynomial>.

The <symmetric group> $S_3$ has three <conjugacy classes>, determined by cycle type: the identity, three <transpositions>, and two three-cycles. Hence it has three complex <irreducible representations>. Two are the <trivial representation> $\mathbf1$ and the <sign representation> $\varepsilon$. Their <dimensions> are one, so the <sum of squares of irreducible representation dimensions> gives $1+1+d^2=6$, whence $d=2$ for the remaining <representation> $D$.

Write $\chi_D=(2,a,b)$ on these classes. Orthogonality to $\mathbf1$ and $\varepsilon$ gives $2+3a+2b=0$ and $2-3a+2b=0$. Thus $a=0,b=-1$, and the <character table> is
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|rrr}
&e&(12)&(123)\\\hline
\text{class size}&1&3&2\\\hline
\mathbf1&1&1&1\\
\varepsilon&1&-1&1\\
D&2&0&-1
\end{array}}
$$
The <character> of a <tensor product representation> is the product of the factor <characters>. Thus $\varepsilon^2=\mathbf1$, $\varepsilon\otimes D=D$, and $\chi_{D\otimes D}=(4,0,1)=\chi_{\mathbf1}+\chi_\varepsilon+\chi_D$. These determine all the <Clebsch-Gordan decomposition of group representations>:
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|ccc}
\otimes&\mathbf1&\varepsilon&D\\\hline
\mathbf1&\mathbf1&\varepsilon&D\\
\varepsilon&\varepsilon&\mathbf1&D\\
D&D&D&\mathbf1\oplus\varepsilon\oplus D
\end{array}}
$$

For <permutations> $\sigma,\tau$, the prescribed <linear maps> satisfy
$$
R(\sigma)R(\tau)e_k=R(\sigma)e_{\tau(k)}=e_{\sigma(\tau(k))}=R(\sigma\tau)e_k.
$$
Since this holds on a <basis>, $R(\sigma)R(\tau)=R(\sigma\tau)$. Also $R(e)=I$ and $R(\sigma)^{-1}=R(\sigma^{-1})$. Hence $R$ is a <representation>.

The <trace> of a <permutation matrix> counts its fixed coordinate <basis vectors>. There are respectively $3,1,0$ fixed vectors on the three <conjugacy classes>, so $\chi_R=(3,1,0)=\chi_{\mathbf1}+\chi_D$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{R=\mathbf1\oplus D.}
$$
In particular the <sign representation> is absent.

The two <invariant subspaces> are
$$
U_0=\mathbb C(1,1,1)^T,\qquad
U_2=\{(z_1,z_2,z_3)^T:z_1+z_2+z_3=0\}.
$$
Coordinate <permutations> preserve both equal coordinates and the coordinate sum. Their <dimensions> add to three and their intersection is zero, so $\mathbb C^3=U_0\oplus U_2$. On $U_0$, the <basis> $u_0=(1,1,1)^T$ gives $R_{U_0}(\tau)=(1)$ for every <transposition>.

On $U_2$ choose the <basis> $u=e_1-e_3$, $v=e_2-e_3$. Directly permuting their entries gives
$$
\begin{array}{c|cc}
\tau&R(\tau)u&R(\tau)v\\\hline
(12)&v&u\\
(13)&-u&v-u\\
(23)&u-v&-v
\end{array}
$$
Thus, taking images as columns, the <matrices> of the <matrix representation> are
$$
\boxed{D(12)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
D(13)=\begin{pmatrix}-1&-1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
D(23)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-1&-1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Each has <trace> zero and squares to $I$. Their three-cycle products have <trace> $-1$, so this is exactly the two-dimensional <irreducible representation> of <2/a/solution>. These are all nonzero <invariant subspaces> affording individual <irreducible representations>, since each occurs once in <2/b/solution>.

First choose the same orthonormal <basis> for the left and right copies of the two-dimensional <unitary representation> $D$. Then the family <representation> is $F=D\oplus\varepsilon$. Because the barred <fermion> transforms with $F(g)^\dagger$ and the <scalar field> $H$ is a <trivial representation>, invariance of the <Yukawa interaction> requires
$$
F(g)^\dagger\lambda F(g)=\lambda,
\quad\text{equivalently}\quad \lambda F(g)=F(g)\lambda
\qquad(g\in S_3).
$$
Write $\lambda$ in a $2+1$ block form. The off-diagonal blocks would be <intertwiners> between inequivalent <irreducible representations> $D$ and $\varepsilon$, so the <Schur lemma> makes them zero. The doublet block must be a scalar <matrix>, while the singlet block is arbitrary. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\lambda=\begin{pmatrix}a&0&0\\0&a&0\\0&0&b\end{pmatrix},\qquad a,b\in\mathbb C.}
$$
Equivalently, the allowed <Yukawa interaction> is $H[a(\bar\psi_L^1\psi_R^1+\bar\psi_L^2\psi_R^2)+b\bar\psi_L^3\psi_R^3]$, together with its <Hermitian conjugate> when constructing a real <Lagrangian>. The <tensor product> rules explain the two free parameters: both $D^*\otimes D$ and $\varepsilon^*\otimes\varepsilon$ contain one <trivial representation>, while the mixed products contain none.

There is a useful <basis> qualification. The <basis> used in <2/c/solution> is not orthonormal: its <Gram matrix> is $K=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$, and $D(g)^\dagger KD(g)=K$. If the fields are expressed in exactly those coordinates, with the barred components defined by ordinary conjugate transpose, the invariant doublet coefficient is $aK$, rather than $aI_2$. Canonically normalizing the <kinetic term> returns the boxed form. Independently chosen left and right orthonormal <bases> replace $aI_2$ by $aS$, where $S$ is their fixed unitary <intertwiner>; the physical degeneracy is unchanged.

After the <scalar field> obtains a <vacuum expectation value> $h$, the <fermion mass matrix> in a canonically normalized <basis> is $M=h\lambda$. Physical <masses> are its <singular values>, not arbitrary entries or signed <eigenvalues>. Thus
$$
\boxed{m_e=m_\mu=|ha|,\qquad m_\tau=|hb|.}
$$
The independent <Yukawa coupling> $b$ can fit the <tau lepton> <mass> relative to the doublet scale, but the <electron> and <muon> are predicted to have equal <masses>. The values supplied by the paper instead give $m_\mu/m_e\simeq105.7/0.511\simeq207$. Consequently exact unbroken $S_3$ with this <representation> assignment cannot reproduce all three <masses>. Complex phases of $a,b$, or changes of <basis>, cannot split the two equal <singular values>. Additional breaking of the <symmetry> or a different field content would be needed; the discrepancy cannot be removed merely by choosing different invariant <Yukawa couplings>.

Use <Dynkin labels> $w_i=\langle w,\alpha_i^\vee\rangle$, so the rows of the <Cartan matrix> are the <simple roots> expressed in <fundamental weight> coordinates. For the <A2 root system>,
$$
\boxed{A_{A_2}=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\alpha_1=(2,-1),\quad\alpha_2=(-1,2).}
$$
Starting at the <highest weight> $(1,0)$ and subtracting successive allowed <simple roots> gives $(1,0)\to(-1,1)\to(0,-1)$. Negating these <weights> gives the <dual representation>. Hence the two fundamental <weight systems> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\mathbf3=(1,0):&\quad (1,0),\ (-1,1),\ (0,-1),\\
\overline{\mathbf3}=(0,1):&\quad (0,1),\ (1,-1),\ (-1,0).
\end{aligned}}
$$
Each listed <weight> has <weight multiplicity> one. The <highest weight> $(1,1)=\alpha_1+\alpha_2$ is the <highest root>, so its <representation> is the <adjoint representation>. Its nonzero <weights> are the six <roots>, while the <Cartan subalgebra> supplies the zero <weight> twice:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf8=(1,1):\quad
\pm(2,-1),\quad\pm(-1,2),\quad\pm(1,1),\quad(0,0)^{[2]}.}
$$
The superscript $[2]$ indicates <weight multiplicity>. This also follows from $\mathbf3\otimes\overline{\mathbf3}=\mathbf8\oplus\mathbf1$: the nine <tensor product> <weights> comprise six differences of distinct fundamental <weights> and three zeros, one zero belonging to the <trivial representation>.

The <highest root> is $\theta=2\alpha_1+3\alpha_2$. In the <extended Dynkin diagram>, the extra node $-\theta$ is long and is joined by a single bond to the long node $\alpha_1$; it is orthogonal to the short node $\alpha_2$. Equivalently, the six long <roots> are a closed <root subsystem>, so their <root spaces> together with the <Cartan subalgebra> form an <A2 root system> <Lie subalgebra>. The corresponding compact <subgroup> is $SU(3)\subset G_2$.

For the particular projection in the paper, choose the <A2 root system> <simple roots> as $\beta_1=\alpha_1+3\alpha_2$ and $\beta_2=\alpha_1$. Since these are long, $\beta_1^\vee=\alpha_1^\vee+\alpha_2^\vee$ and $\beta_2^\vee=\alpha_1^\vee$. Consequently the <Dynkin labels> restrict as

\boxed{(a,b)^T\longmapsto P(a,b)^T=(a+b,a)^T,\qquad

(0,0,1)&\begin{array}{l}\boxed{1}\\[-3pt]\boxed{2}\\[-3pt]\boxed{3}\end{array}&4&B^{ijk}=B^{[ijk]}\ \leftrightarrow\ b_i\\[11pt]
(1,0,1)&\begin{array}{l}\boxed{1}\,\boxed{2}\\[-3pt]\boxed{3}\\[-3pt]\boxed{4}\end{array}&15&X^i{}_j,\quad X^i{}_i=0
\end{array}
$$
Here the <dimensions> are $4$, $\binom42=6$, $\binom43=4$, and $4^2-1=15$. The third is the <dual representation> $\overline{\mathbf4}$: the invariant volume <tensor> identifies $b_l=\epsilon_{lijk}B^{ijk}/3!$. The last is the <adjoint representation>, equivalent to the shape $(2,1,1)$ <Young symmetrizer> construction after converting its three-box column to one lower index and removing the <trace>.

For $U\in SU(4)$, the <tensor> transformation laws are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
v'^i&=U^i{}_jv^j,\\
A'^{ij}&=U^i{}_kU^j{}_lA^{kl},\\
B'^{ijk}&=U^i{}_lU^j{}_mU^k{}_nB^{lmn},\\
b'_i&=b_j(U^{-1})^j{}_i,\\
X'^i{}_j&=U^i{}_kX^k{}_l(U^{-1})^l{}_j.
\end{aligned}}
$$
These preserve antisymmetry and tracelessness. The <adjoint representation> can be treated as complex traceless <matrices>; a real adjoint <scalar field> is conventionally represented by Hermitian traceless <matrices> with the same conjugation action.

Adding one box to the one-box <Young diagram> gives row shapes $(2)$ and $(1,1)$. Thus the first <tensor product> splits into the <symmetric square> and <exterior square>:
$$
\boxed{(1,0,0)\otimes(1,0,0)=(2,0,0)\oplus(0,1,0),\qquad
\mathbf4\otimes\mathbf4=\mathbf{10}\oplus\mathbf6.}
$$
Their <tensors> are respectively $S^{ij}=S^{ji}$ and $A^{ij}=-A^{ji}$. Adding one box to the three-box column gives shapes $(2,1,1)$ and $(1,1,1,1)$. The full four-box column is the <determinant representation>, trivial because $\det U=1$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{(1,0,0)\otimes(0,0,1)=(1,0,1)\oplus(0,0,0),\qquad
\mathbf4\otimes\overline{\mathbf4}=\mathbf{15}\oplus\mathbf1.}
$$
For a mixed <tensor> $T^i{}_j$, the corresponding pieces are $T^i{}_j-\tfrac14\delta^i{}_jT^k{}_k$ and $\tfrac14\delta^i{}_jT^k{}_k$.

Under the two-block <subgroup> $H_2$, the defining <vector space> splits into its first and last two coordinates. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\mathbf4\downarrow_{H_2}&=(\mathbf2,\mathbf1)\oplus(\mathbf1,\mathbf2),\\
\mathbf6\downarrow_{H_2}&=(\mathbf1,\mathbf1)^{\oplus2}\oplus(\mathbf2,\mathbf2),\\
\mathbf{15}\downarrow_{H_2}&=(\mathbf3,\mathbf1)\oplus(\mathbf1,\mathbf3)
\oplus(\mathbf2,\mathbf2)^{\oplus2}\oplus(\mathbf1,\mathbf1).
\end{aligned}}
$$
For the <exterior square>, the <exterior square of a direct sum> identity gives two within-block <determinant representations>, each trivial for $SU(2)$, and the cross-block <tensor product> $(\mathbf2,\mathbf2)$. For the <adjoint representation>, use $\operatorname{End}(\mathbb C^4)-\mathbf1$: each diagonal two-by-two block contributes a scalar and a traceless triplet, while each off-diagonal block gives $(\mathbf2,\mathbf2)$ because the <SU(2)> doublet is self-dual. Removing the overall <trace> leaves one scalar. The <dimension> counts are $2+2=4$, $1+1+4=6$, and $3+3+4+4+1=15$.

Under $H_3$, the first three coordinates carry the defining <SU(3)> <representation> and the fourth is fixed. Hence
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\mathbf4\downarrow_{H_3}&=\mathbf3\oplus\mathbf1,\\
\mathbf6\downarrow_{H_3}&=\overline{\mathbf3}\oplus\mathbf3,\\
\mathbf{15}\downarrow_{H_3}&=\mathbf8\oplus\mathbf3\oplus\overline{\mathbf3}\oplus\mathbf1.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Indeed $\wedge^2(\mathbf3\oplus\mathbf1)=\wedge^2\mathbf3\oplus\mathbf3=\overline{\mathbf3}\oplus\mathbf3$. The upper three-by-three block of a traceless <matrix> supplies the <adjoint representation> $\mathbf8$ and one scalar, while the upper-right and lower-left blocks give $\mathbf3$ and $\overline{\mathbf3}$. This establishes the <branching of a representation> in each case without a dimension-only guess.

An unbroken <subgroup> $H$ must fix the <vacuum expectation value> itself. Thus a nonzero $H$-invariant <vacuum expectation value> can exist only if the <restriction of a representation> to $H$ contains a <trivial representation>. Applying the <singlet criterion for an unbroken subgroup> to <4/b/solution> gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|cc}
R&\text{can preserve }H_2&\text{can preserve }H_3\\\hline
\mathbf4&\text{no}&\text{yes}\\
\mathbf6&\text{yes}&\text{no}\\
\mathbf{15}&\text{yes}&\text{yes}
\end{array}}
$$
The two “no” entries are forbidden for every nonzero <vacuum expectation value>. Each “yes” means that the indicated <subgroup> can remain unbroken, subject to the <scalar potential> choosing such a vacuum. It does **not** by itself prove that the indicated <subgroup> is the entire <stabilizer>.

For exact <spontaneous symmetry breaking> to the stated <subgroup>, the distinction matters. A complex $\mathbf4$ admits an exact $H_3$ <stabilizer>; a complex $\mathbf6$ admits an exact $H_2$ <stabilizer>. A single $\mathbf{15}$ preserving $H_2$ or $H_3$ necessarily preserves an additional continuous $U(1)$, as computed in <4/d/solution>, so neither exact endpoint is possible with that single field. Also, if the $\mathbf6$ <scalar field> is constrained to the <real structure of the SU4 exterior square>, its nonzero <stabilizer> is larger than $H_2$. The paper does not specify whether this reality constraint is imposed, so both cases are distinguished below.

**The fundamental case.** Set $\langle\phi\rangle=v e_4$, where $v\ne0$. Fixing this <vector> forces the fourth column of a <unitary matrix> to be $e_4$, and hence also fixes its fourth row. The remaining three-by-three block has <determinant> one. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Stab}(ve_4)=\{\operatorname{diag}(W,1):W\in SU(3)\}=H_3.}
$$
Every nonzero complex <vector> can be brought to this form by an <SU(4)> transformation: complete its normalized direction to a unitary <basis> and adjust the phase of a complementary <basis vector> to obtain <determinant> one. Thus a generic nonzero fundamental <vacuum expectation value> has a conjugate of $H_3$ as its exact unbroken <subgroup>. The particular embedded $H_3$ of the question is fixed only when the <vacuum expectation value> lies along $e_4$.

**The antisymmetric case.** With $\epsilon=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$, take
$$
\langle A\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}a\epsilon&0\\0&b\epsilon\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Since $V\epsilon V^T=\epsilon$ for $V\in SU(2)$, this <antisymmetric tensor> is fixed by $H_2$ for every $a,b$. Its transformation is $A\mapsto UAU^T$, so any stabilizing $U$ also preserves
$$
AA^\dagger=\operatorname{diag}(|a|^2I_2,|b|^2I_2).
$$
If $|a|\ne|b|$, its two <eigenspaces> force $U=\operatorname{diag}(V,\widetilde V)$ with unitary two-by-two blocks. For each nonzero block, $V\epsilon V^T=(\det V)\epsilon$ forces the block <determinant> to be one. If one block is zero, the <SU(4)> <determinant> condition forces the other block <determinant> to be one as well. Hence
$$
\boxed{|a|\ne|b|\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\operatorname{Stab}(\langle A\rangle)=H_2.}
$$
The <unitary skew-diagonalization of an antisymmetric matrix> shows that a generic complex $\mathbf6$ has two distinct paired <singular values>; choosing an <SU(4)> rather than a $U(4)$ <basis> merely leaves an allowed overall phase in $a,b$. Thus its generic <stabilizer> is conjugate to $H_2$.

If instead $|a|=|b|\ne0$, the form is proportional, up to unitary coordinates and phases, to a nondegenerate standard symplectic form. Its <stabilizer> is the <compact symplectic group> $USp(4)$, of <dimension> ten, containing $H_2$ properly. The explicit choice $a=b=1$ is a concrete counterexample to inferring an exact $H_2$ <stabilizer> from the existence of two singlets alone. For a real $\mathbf6$, the <real structure of the SU4 exterior square> imposes $b=\bar a$ in this form, so these equal magnitudes are compulsory. Every nonzero real $\mathbf6$ therefore has the larger <compact symplectic group> <stabilizer>. The zero <vacuum expectation value> preserves all of $SU(4)$.

**The adjoint cases.** The $H_2$-fixed and $H_3$-fixed traceless <matrices> have respectively the forms
$$
X_2=a\operatorname{diag}(1,1,-1,-1),\qquad
X_3=a\operatorname{diag}(1,1,1,-3),\qquad a\ne0.
$$
For a Hermitian adjoint <scalar field>, choose $a$ real. More generally a complex $a$ gives the same <stabilizers>. The repeated <eigenvalues> imply
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Stab}(X_2)=S(U(2)\times U(2)),\qquad
\operatorname{Stab}(X_3)=S(U(3)\times U(1)).}
$$
Here $S$ means that the product of the block <determinants> is one. These <groups> contain respectively $H_2$ and $H_3$, together with an additional continuous $U(1)$. Explicit extra transformations are $\operatorname{diag}(e^{it}I_2,e^{-it}I_2)$ and $\operatorname{diag}(e^{it}I_3,e^{-3it})$. They commute with $X_2$ and $X_3$ for every $t$, proving that neither exact endpoint $H_2$ or $H_3$ occurs for a single adjoint <vacuum expectation value>.

Nor do these larger <subgroups> survive for a generic Hermitian traceless <matrix>: four distinct <eigenvalues> leave only the <maximal torus> $U(1)^3$. For a generic complex adjoint <matrix>, there need not even be a continuous <stabilizer>; generically its unitary <centralizer> is just the <centre of a group> $\mathbb Z_4$. Thus the displayed degenerate adjoint directions are special. All these statements concern the <stabilizer> of a possible <vacuum expectation value>; no unspecified <scalar potential> can be assumed to select a particular direction or a particular reality condition.
