= Solution
{parent=a}

The <matrices> obey $Q^5=P^2=I$ and $PQP=Q^{-1}$. Moving every $P$ to the right therefore reduces every product to $Q^k$ or $Q^kP$, with $0\leq k<5$. The five diagonal <matrices> $Q^k$ are distinct because $\alpha$ is a primitive fifth <root of unity>; the five $Q^kP$ are distinct and off-diagonal. Thus
$$
\boxed{G=\{Q^k,Q^kP:0\leq k<5\},\qquad |G|=10.}
$$
This is the <dihedral group> of the regular pentagon, with $Q$ a rotation and $P$ a reflection.

Conjugation by $P$ interchanges $Q^k$ and $Q^{-k}$, while conjugation by a power of $Q$ fixes rotations. Also
$$
Q^j(Q^kP)Q^{-j}=Q^{k+2j}P.
$$
Since multiplication by $2$ permutes the residues modulo $5$, all five reflections form one <conjugacy class>. The full list of <conjugacy classes> is
$$
\boxed{\{I\},\quad\{Q,Q^4\},\quad\{Q^2,Q^3\},\quad\{P,QP,Q^2P,Q^3P,Q^4P\}.}
$$
To prove the <representation> is <irreducible> over $\mathbb C$, any proper nonzero <invariant subspace> would be a line invariant under $Q$. Its two distinct <eigenvalues> force such a line to be one of its coordinate <eigenspaces>. But $P$ exchanges those two lines, so neither is $G$-invariant. Hence the given <complex representation> is <irreducible>.

In a <one-dimensional representation>, the scalar images of $Q$ and $P$ commute. The relation $PQP=Q^{-1}$ gives $R(Q)^2=1$, while $R(Q)^5=1$, so $R(Q)=1$ and $R(P)=\pm1$. These give exactly $R_0$ and $R_1$.

For $k=1,2$, define the two-dimensional <representations>
$$
D_k(Q)=\operatorname{diag}(\alpha^k,\alpha^{-k}),\qquad
D_k(P)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The same <invariant subspace> argument as in <1/a/solution> proves that each is <irreducible>. Set $R_2=D_1$ and $R_3=D_2$. Their <characters> differ at $Q$, so they are inequivalent. The <sum of squares of irreducible representation dimensions> is already $1^2+1^2+2^2+2^2=10$, proving completeness.

Put $s=2\cos(2\pi/5)=(\sqrt5-1)/2$ and $t=2\cos(4\pi/5)=-(\sqrt5+1)/2$. Taking <traces> yields the <character table>
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|rrrr}
 & I & Q,Q^4 & Q^2,Q^3 & Q^kP\\\hline
\text{class size}&1&2&2&5\\\hline
R_0&1&1&1&1\\
R_1&1&1&1&-1\\
R_2&2&s&t&0\\
R_3&2&t&s&0
\end{array}}
$$
The identities $s+t=-1$, $st=-1$, and $s^2+t^2=3$ also verify <character orthogonality> directly.

A <tensor product of group representations> has <character> equal to the product of its factor <characters>. Multiplying the rows of the <character table> in <1/b/solution>, using $s^2=2+t$, $t^2=2+s$ and $st=-1$, gives all the <Clebsch-Gordan decomposition of group representations>:
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|cccc}
\otimes&R_0&R_1&R_2&R_3\\\hline
R_0&R_0&R_1&R_2&R_3\\
R_1&R_1&R_0&R_2&R_3\\
R_2&R_2&R_2&R_0\oplus R_1\oplus R_3&R_2\oplus R_3\\
R_3&R_3&R_3&R_2\oplus R_3&R_0\oplus R_1\oplus R_2
\end{array}}
$$
For example, the <character> of $R_2\otimes R_2$ is $(4,s^2,t^2,0)=(4,2+t,2+s,0)$, the sum of the <characters> of $R_0$, $R_1$ and $R_3$. Likewise $R_2\otimes R_3$ has <character> $(4,-1,-1,0)=\chi_2+\chi_3$. The <trivial representation> and $R_1\otimes R_1=R_0$ account for the remaining entries.

Let $v_H=\langle H\rangle$ be the <vacuum expectation value> in the convention $M=v_H\lambda$. For canonically normalized <fermion> kinetic terms, physical <charged lepton> masses are the nonnegative values in the <singular value decomposition> of $M$, equivalently the square roots of the <eigenvalues> of $M^\dagger M$.

In case (i),
$$
M^\dagger M=|v_H|^2\operatorname{diag}(|y|^2,|y|^2,|y_3|^2),\qquad
\boxed{\{m_1,m_2,m_3\}=|v_H|\{|y|,|y|,|y_3|\}.}
$$
The opposite algebraic <eigenvalues> $y$ and $-y$ of the doublet block do not represent different physical masses. The unavoidable twofold degeneracy conflicts with the three distinct nonzero measured <charged lepton> masses, for any assignment of the three families.

In case (ii),
$$
\boxed{\{m_1,m_2,m_3\}=\{0,0,|v_Hy_3|\}.}
$$
One can fit the <tau lepton> mass by choosing $|v_Hy_3|$, but the <electron> and <muon> remain massless. Thus neither exact <dihedral group> assignment with this single singlet <complex scalar field> reproduces the observed spectrum. Additional symmetry-breaking <Yukawa interactions> or other sources of mass are required; a basis change cannot remove the degeneracy or change the <matrix rank>.

Write $a=a_1$, $b=a_2$ with $a,b\geq0$, and let $\lambda=a\omega_1+b\omega_2$ be the <highest weight>. The <positive roots> are $\alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$ and $\alpha_1+\alpha_2$, and the <Weyl vector> is $\rho=\omega_1+\omega_2$. The <Weyl dimension formula> gives
$$
d(a,b)=\prod_{\alpha>0}\frac{(\lambda+\rho,\alpha)}{(\rho,\alpha)}
=\frac{a+1}{1}\frac{b+1}{1}\frac{a+b+2}{2},\qquad
\boxed{d(a,b)=\tfrac12(a+1)(b+1)(a+b+2).}
$$
For the <quadratic Casimir operator>, use the root-length normalization $C(\lambda)=(\lambda,\lambda+2\rho)$. Substituting the <Gram matrix> in <2/a/solution> yields
$$
\boxed{C(a,b)=\frac23\bigl(a^2+ab+b^2+3a+3b\bigr).}
$$
Taking the <trace> of the defining <quadratic Casimir operator> identity gives $d(a,b)C(a,b)=\dim(\mathfrak{su}(3))c(a,b)=8c(a,b)$, where $c$ is the <Dynkin index> in the matching normalization. Therefore
$$
\boxed{c(a,b)=\frac{(a+1)(b+1)(a+b+2)}{24}
\bigl(a^2+ab+b^2+3a+3b\bigr).}
$$
In particular $c(\mathbf3)=1$ and $c(\mathbf8)=6$. The common particle-physics convention uses $C_2=C/2$ and $T=c/2$, giving $C_2(\mathbf3)=4/3$ and $T(\mathbf3)=1/2$. The subsequent discrete <gauge anomaly> congruences use the integer <Dynkin index> $c$, while the sign test for the supplied <beta function (physics)> is independent of a common rescaling of all indices.

The identity commutes with every $h\in H$, so the <centralizer> $C_G(H)$ is nonempty. If $x,y\in C_G(H)$, then for every $h\in H$,
$$
(xy)h=x(yh)=x(hy)=(xh)y=(hx)y=h(xy).
$$
Thus it is closed under multiplication. From $xh=hx$, multiplication by $x^{-1}$ on both sides gives $hx^{-1}=x^{-1}h$, so $x^{-1}\in C_G(H)$. These are exactly the <subgroup> conditions; hence
$$
\boxed{C_G(H)\leq G.}
$$
The proof works for the <centralizer of a subset> as well; it does not require that $H$ itself be a <subgroup>.

Write $X=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\C&D\end{pmatrix}$. Commuting with all $\operatorname{diag}(U_1,U_2)$ requires
$$
AU_1=U_1A,\quad DU_2=U_2D,\quad BU_2=U_1B,\quad CU_1=U_2C.
$$
For $n_1,n_2\geq2$, the defining <complex representations> of each factor are <irreducible>. <Schur lemma> gives $A=zI_{n_1}$ and $D=wI_{n_2}$. The two block <vector spaces> are inequivalent <representations> of the product <group>, even when $n_1=n_2$: the first factor acts nontrivially on just the first block. Thus their off-diagonal <intertwiners> vanish, $B=C=0$. <Unitarity> and the unit <determinant> give
$$
\boxed{C_G(H)=\{\operatorname{diag}(zI_{n_1},wI_{n_2}):|z|=|w|=1,\ z^{n_1}w^{n_2}=1\}.}
$$

**The assertion that this full group is always a circle needs correction.** Put $d=\gcd(n_1,n_2)$, $a=n_1/d$, $b=n_2/d$. The continuous <group homomorphism>
$$
(z,w)\longmapsto z^aw^b
$$
maps this <centralizer> onto the $d$th <roots of unity>. Its <kernel> is the <identity component of a Lie group>, parametrized injectively by $e^{i\theta}\mapsto(e^{ib\theta},e^{-ia\theta})$. Coprimality of $a,b$ proves injectivity; a <Bezout identity> proves that this parametrization exhausts the <kernel>. Extending the primitive integer row $(a,b)$ to a determinant-one integer matrix gives coordinates in which the constraint is simply $t^d=1$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{C_G(H)\cong U(1)\times\mathbb Z_d,\qquad C_G(H)^0\cong U(1).}
$$
For a concrete counterexample, take $n_1=n_2=2$. The constraint is $(zw)^2=1$, giving two separate circles $zw=1$ and $zw=-1$. The element $\operatorname{diag}(I_2,-I_2)$ lies in the second component, so this <centralizer> cannot be isomorphic as a <Lie group> to the connected <circle group>.

For the intended specialization of this <centralizer of two special unitary blocks>, $d=\gcd(2,3)=1$ and therefore the full <centralizer> really is a <circle group>:
$$
\boxed{U_c(1)=\{e^{i\theta X_c}:\theta\in\mathbb R/2\pi\mathbb Z\},\qquad
X_c=\operatorname{diag}(3,3,-2,-2,-2).}
$$
We use this primitive integer <U(1) charge> normalization below. If exactly one $n_i$ equals $1$ and the other is at least $2$, the same block-scalar formula still holds. If $n_1=n_2=1$, both factors are trivial and the <centralizer> is all of <SU(2)>, another exception to the printed claim.

The <complexification of a Lie algebra> in the <adjoint representation> of <SU(5)> consists of traceless endomorphisms of $V=A\oplus B$. \boxed{\mathbf{24}\downarrow=(\mathbf3,\mathbf1)_0\oplus(\mathbf1,\mathbf8)_0\oplus(\mathbf1,\mathbf1)_0
\oplus(\mathbf2,\overline{\mathbf3})_5\oplus(\mathbf2,\mathbf3)_{-5}.}

For the <special unitary group> $SU(N)$ define $\varepsilon_i=e_i-\frac1N\sum_{j=1}^Ne_j$. These are the <weights of a representation> in the defining space, with $\sum_i\varepsilon_i=0$. Their <Dynkin labels> are
$$
(w_i)_r=(\varepsilon_i,\alpha_r)=\delta_{ir}-\delta_{i,r+1},\qquad1\leq r<N.
$$
The <dual representation> negates each <weight of a representation>, and the <tensor product of group representations> adds them. Consequently the complete <weight systems> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\mathcal W(V)&=\{\varepsilon_i:1\leq i\leq N\},&
\mathcal W(V^*)&=\{-\varepsilon_i:1\leq i\leq N\},\\
\mathcal W(\Lambda^2V)&=\{\varepsilon_i+\varepsilon_j:1\leq i<j\leq N\},&
\mathcal W(\Lambda^2V^*)&=\{-\varepsilon_i-\varepsilon_j:1\leq i<j\leq N\}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Every listed <weight of a representation> has multiplicity one: the corresponding <weight basis> vectors are $v_i$, $v^i$, $v_i\wedge v_j$ and $v^i\wedge v^j$. Distinct pairs give distinct projected sums for $N=5,6$. For an explicit enumeration in <Dynkin labels>, the two defining <weight systems> are

For the embedding in <4/a/solution>, restriction retains the first four <Dynkin label> coordinates, since the <simple coroots> of $A_4$ are the first four of $A_5$. In a coordinate-free version of the same projection, let $\eta_i$ be the five defining <weights of a representation> of $A_4$. Restriction gives $\varepsilon_i\mapsto\eta_i$ for $1\leq i\leq5$, while $\varepsilon_6\mapsto0$.

For the <fundamental representation>, its first five <weights of a representation> therefore form a $\mathbf5$ and the sixth is the zero <weight of a representation> of a <trivial representation>. Negating the <weights of a representation> gives the <conjugate representation>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf6\downarrow_{SU(5)}=\mathbf5\oplus\mathbf1,\qquad
\overline{\mathbf6}\downarrow_{SU(5)}=\overline{\mathbf5}\oplus\mathbf1.}
$$
For the <two-index antisymmetric representation>, split the fifteen <weights of a representation> into the ten with $i<j\leq5$ and the five with $j=6$. Their restricted values are $\eta_i+\eta_j$ and $\eta_i$, respectively. These are precisely the <weight systems> of $\mathbf{10}$ and $\mathbf5$. Negating again gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf{15}\downarrow_{SU(5)}=\mathbf{10}\oplus\mathbf5,\qquad
\overline{\mathbf{15}}\downarrow_{SU(5)}=\overline{\mathbf{10}}\oplus\overline{\mathbf5}.}
$$
For instance the highest $A_5$ <weight of a representation> $(0,1,0,0,0)$ restricts to $(0,1,0,0)$ for the $\mathbf{10}$, while $(1,0,0,0,-1)$ restricts to $(1,0,0,0)$ for the $\mathbf5$. The restricted <weight systems> are disjoint unions of multiplicity-one sets in these cases, and their dimensions check as $6=5+1$ and $15=10+5$.

One left-handed <Standard Model> family requires the <SU(5)> <representation> content $\mathbf{10}\oplus\overline{\mathbf5}$. No single <irreducible representation> in <4/b/solution> contains both. At the level of containing the required fields, the smallest choice is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf{15}\oplus\overline{\mathbf6}\downarrow
=(\mathbf{10}\oplus\overline{\mathbf5})\oplus\mathbf5\oplus\mathbf1.}
$$
The additional <SU(5)> multiplets are a $\mathbf5$ and a <trivial representation>. Another minimal choice using only two of the listed <irreducible representations> is
$$
\mathbf{15}\oplus\overline{\mathbf{15}}\downarrow
=(\mathbf{10}\oplus\overline{\mathbf5})\oplus(\overline{\mathbf{10}}\oplus\mathbf5).
$$
Its additional multiplets form the <conjugate representation> of a family. More generally, for multiplicities $(m_6,m_{\bar6},m_{15},m_{\overline{15}})$, <branching of a representation> gives
$$
(m_6+m_{15})\mathbf5\oplus(m_{\bar6}+m_{\overline{15}})\overline{\mathbf5}
\oplus m_{15}\mathbf{10}\oplus m_{\overline{15}}\overline{\mathbf{10}}
\oplus(m_6+m_{\bar6})\mathbf1.
$$
Thus containing a family requires $m_{15}\geq1$ and $m_{\bar6}+m_{\overline{15}}\geq1$.

For a consistent chiral <grand unified theory>, containment alone is insufficient. Normalize the cubic <gauge anomaly> coefficient by $A(\mathbf6)=1$. For the <special unitary group> $SU(N)$, $A(\Lambda^2\mathbf N)=N-4$, so for <SU(6)> it is $2$. To verify this directly, take a traceless diagonal generator with entries $x_i$: its <exterior square> has entries $x_i+x_j$, and
$$
\sum_{i<j}(x_i+x_j)^3=(N-1)\sum_i x_i^3+3\sum_{i\ne j}x_i^2x_j
=(N-4)\sum_i x_i^3.
$$
The <conjugate representation> reverses this cubic <gauge anomaly>. The combination $\mathbf{15}\oplus\overline{\mathbf6}$ has coefficient $2-1=1$, so cannot alone be a quantum-consistent chiral <SU(6)> <gauge theory>. The minimal anomaly-free choice with one net <chiral gauge spectrum> of a family is instead
$$
\boxed{\mathbf{15}\oplus2\overline{\mathbf6}\downarrow
=(\mathbf{10}\oplus\overline{\mathbf5})\oplus(\mathbf5\oplus\overline{\mathbf5})\oplus2\mathbf1.}
$$
The additional nonsinglet fields form a <vectorlike gauge spectrum> that can acquire an <SU(5)>-invariant mass, and the two <gauge singlets> may serve as sterile <neutrinos>. More generally, precisely one net <chiral gauge spectrum> of a family with vectorlike extra nonsinglets requires $m_{15}-m_{\overline{15}}=1$ and $m_{\bar6}-m_6=2$; these conditions also cancel the cubic <SU(6)> <gauge anomaly>. The $\mathbf{15}\oplus\overline{\mathbf{15}}$ choice above is anomaly-free but has zero net <chirality>.
