A <subgroup> $N\leq G$ is a <normal subgroup> if $gNg^{-1}=N$ for every $g\in G$. A <group homomorphism> $f:G\to\widetilde G$ satisfies $f(gh)=f(g)f(h)$ for every $g,h\in G$. In particular $f(e)=\widetilde e$ and $f(g^{-1})=f(g)^{-1}$. A <group representation> on a <vector space> $V$ is a <group homomorphism> $R:G\to\operatorname{GL}(V)$ into the <general linear group> of invertible <linear maps> on $V$.

For the <kernel of a group homomorphism>, put $K=\ker f=\{g:f(g)=\widetilde e\}$. It contains $e$, and if $k,\ell\in K$ then $f(k\ell^{-1})=f(k)f(\ell)^{-1}=\widetilde e$, so $K$ is a <subgroup>. For $g\in G$ and $k\in K$,
$$
f(gkg^{-1})=f(g)f(k)f(g)^{-1}=\widetilde e.
$$
Thus $gKg^{-1}\subseteq K$. Applying this inclusion with $g^{-1}$ gives the reverse inclusion, proving that $\ker f$ is a <normal subgroup>.

For a <normal subgroup> $N$, define multiplication of left <cosets> by
$$
\boxed{(gN)(hN)=(gh)N.}
$$
To check independence of representatives, replace $g,h$ by $gn,hm$, with $n,m\in N$. Then
$$
(gn)(hm)=gh(h^{-1}nh)m\in ghN,
$$
because normality gives $h^{-1}nh\in N$. Hence the operation is well-defined. Associativity follows from $(gh)k=g(hk)$ in $G$; the identity is $N=eN$; and the inverse of $gN$ is $g^{-1}N$. These verify the <group axioms>, giving the <quotient group> $G/N$. The map $g\mapsto gN$ is a surjective <group homomorphism> with <kernel of a group homomorphism> equal to $N$.

Nonzero scalar <matrices> form a <subgroup> $N$ of the <general linear group>, since products and inverses remain nonzero scalar <matrices>. Moreover, for every invertible $g$,
$$
g(\lambda I_n)g^{-1}=\lambda I_n.
$$
Thus $N$ lies in the <center of a group> and is a <normal subgroup>. The <quotient group> is the complex <projective linear group> $\operatorname{PGL}_n(\mathbb C)$, whose elements identify invertible <matrices> differing by a nonzero scalar.

The canonical <group homomorphism> $\pi:g\mapsto[g]$ obeys $[g][h]=[gh]$. Composing it with the given <group representation> gives
$$
\widetilde R(gh)=[R(gh)]=[R(g)R(h)]=[R(g)][R(h)]=\widetilde R(g)\widetilde R(h).
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\widetilde R=\pi\circ R:G\longrightarrow\operatorname{PGL}_n(\mathbb C)\text{ is a group homomorphism}.}
$$
This is the <projective representation> associated with $R$: a nonzero scalar acts trivially on each line in the <projective space> of $V$. Its <kernel of a group homomorphism> consists of those $g$ for which $R(g)$ is scalar, which can be larger than the <kernel of a group homomorphism> of $R$ itself.

Write $A=g_1$, $B=g_2$ and $C=AB$. Direct multiplication gives
$$
A^2=B^2=I_2,\qquad BA=-AB,\qquad
C=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad C^2=-I_2.
$$
The relation $BA=-AB$ moves all occurrences of $A$ to the left of all occurrences of $B$. Removing even powers reduces every product of the generators to $\pm A^aB^b$ with $a,b\in\{0,1\}$. The resulting eight <matrices> are distinct, so
$$
\boxed{G=\{\pm I_2,\ \pm A,\ \pm B,\ \pm C\},\qquad |G|=8.}
$$
Explicitly, the four pairs are
$$
\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad
\pm\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
\pm\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Since $AB=-BA$ and $AB\ne0$, the <group> is noncommutative. More precisely, $C$ has order four, $A$ has order two and $ACA=C^{-1}$, identifying $G$ with the <dihedral group> of a square. This fixes the convention unambiguously: it is the <dihedral group> of <group order> eight, irrespective of whether it is denoted $D_4$ or $D_8$.

Use the <matrices> of <1/c/solution>. Their projective classes satisfy $[-M]=[M]$, since $-I_2\in N$. The <group homomorphism> is therefore explicitly
$$
\boxed{\widetilde R(\pm I_2)=[I_2],\quad
\widetilde R(\pm A)=[A],\quad
\widetilde R(\pm B)=[B],\quad
\widetilde R(\pm C)=[C].}
$$
These four classes are distinct: the two diagonal representatives are not scalar multiples of one another, the two off-diagonal representatives are not scalar multiples of one another, and a diagonal representative cannot be a nonzero scalar multiple of an off-diagonal one.

Although $A$ and $B$ anticommute as <matrices>, their projective classes commute:
$$
[A][B]=[AB]=[-BA]=[BA]=[B][A].
$$
Furthermore $[A]^2=[B]^2=[I_2]$ and $[C]^2=[-I_2]=[I_2]$. Thus every nonidentity element of the image has order two. An explicit <group isomorphism> to the <Klein four-group> is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Im}\widetilde R\longrightarrow\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_2,
\qquad [A^aB^b]\longmapsto(a,b),\qquad a,b\in\{0,1\}.}
$$
Indeed reordering products introduces only a sign, which disappears in the <projective linear group>, so this map turns multiplication into addition modulo two in each coordinate. Equivalently, $\ker\widetilde R=G\cap N=\{\pm I_2\}$ and the <first isomorphism theorem> gives
$$
G/\{\pm I_2\}\cong\operatorname{Im}\widetilde R\cong\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_2.
$$
This <projective image of the square symmetry group> illustrates how a noncommutative <group> can have an <abelian> projective image: its scalar commutator becomes the identity class.

\boxed{R_2^*\cong R_2.}

The <character> of a <tensor product representation> is the product of the factor <characters>. Hence $\chi_{R_2\otimes R_2}=(4,1,0)$. Its <character inner products> with the three rows of <2/c/solution> give multiplicities $(1,1,1)$, so

For $z\in U(1)$ and $U\in SU(n)$, the <matrix> $zU$ is unitary, since $(zU)^\dagger(zU)=|z|^2U^\dagger U=I_n$. Thus the map has its image in the <unitary group> $U(n)$. Scalar <matrices> commute with all <matrices>, so the <direct product of groups> multiplication gives
$$
f((z,U)(w,V))=f(zw,UV)=zwUV=(zU)(wV)=f(z,U)f(w,V).
$$
Also $f(1,I_n)=I_n$. Hence
$$
\boxed{f:U(1)\times SU(n)\longrightarrow U(n),\quad(z,U)\longmapsto zU\text{ is a group homomorphism}.}
$$
The <special unitary group> condition $\det U=1$ imposes no requirement that $zU$ itself have unit <determinant>; its <determinant> is $z^n$, as expected for a general element of the <unitary group>.

First the <group homomorphism> is surjective. For any $W\in U(n)$, choose $z\in U(1)$ with $z^n=\det W$ and set $U=z^{-1}W$. Then $U$ is unitary and $\det U=z^{-n}\det W=1$, so $W=f(z,U)$.

Next $f(z,U)=I_n$ is equivalent to $U=z^{-1}I_n$, and the <special unitary group> condition becomes $z^n=1$. With $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/n}$, the <kernel of a group homomorphism> is therefore
$$
\boxed{K=\{(\zeta^k,\zeta^{-k}I_n):k=0,1,\ldots,n-1\}\cong\mathbb Z_n.}
$$
It is the cyclic central <subgroup> generated by $(\zeta,\zeta^{-1}I_n)$; the scalar and <special unitary group> factors are paired with inverse phases. The <first isomorphism theorem> now gives
$$
\boxed{U(n)\cong\big(U(1)\times SU(n)\big)/K.}
$$
Explicitly the isomorphism sends the <coset> $[(z,U)]$ to $zU$. The $n$ possible choices of the root $z$ differ precisely by multiplication by elements of $K$, which explains why the product before quotienting is not a unique factorization.

For a differentiable curve $Q(t)\in SO(5)$ with $Q(0)=I_5$, differentiate $Q(t)^TQ(t)=I_5$. Its tangent <matrix> $X=Q'(0)$ is real and satisfies $X^T+X=0$. Conversely the <matrix exponential> of any real <antisymmetric matrix> is orthogonal, and $\det e^{tX}=e^{t\operatorname{tr}X}=1$ because $\operatorname{tr}X=0$. Hence the real <special orthogonal Lie algebra> is
$$
\boxed{\mathfrak{so}(5)=\{X\in M_5(\mathbb R):X^T=-X\},\qquad [X,Y]=XY-YX.}
$$
Let $E_{ab}$ denote a <matrix unit>. A simple real <basis> is
$$
\boxed{M_{ab}=E_{ab}-E_{ba},\qquad 1\leq a<b\leq5.}
$$
Each independent entry above the diagonal supplies one real parameter, giving $\binom52=10$ parameters. The <commutator> of two <basis> <matrices> is
$$
[M_{ab},M_{cd}]=\delta_{bc}M_{ad}-\delta_{ac}M_{bd}-\delta_{bd}M_{ac}+\delta_{ad}M_{bc},
$$
where $M_{ba}=-M_{ab}$ and $M_{aa}=0$.

A <Cartan subalgebra> is spanned by the commuting <matrices> $M_{12},M_{34}$, which generate independent rotations in two orthogonal planes. A generic $h=aM_{12}+bM_{34}$, with $ab(a^2-b^2)\ne0$, has distinct complex <eigenvalues> $ia,-ia,ib,-ib,0$. A real <antisymmetric matrix> commuting with it preserves those two planes and the remaining line; it is a multiple of the rotation in each plane and is zero on the line. Thus the commuting <Lie subalgebra> has <dimension> two and is maximal toral. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\dim_{\mathbb R}\mathfrak{so}(5)=10,\qquad\operatorname{rank}\mathfrak{so}(5)=2.}
$$

$$
which gives those same values. These are <Lie algebra> <representations>; the four-dimensional <spinor representation> integrates to the <Spin group> $\operatorname{Spin}(5)$, and does not descend to the <special orthogonal group> $SO(5)$. A concrete obstruction is a $2\pi$ rotation: it is the identity in $SO(5)$ but acts as $-I_4$ on the <spinor representation>, as verified in <4/d/solution>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Let $r=V(0,1)$ be the four-dimensional <spinor representation>. The <tensor product representation> splits into its <symmetric square> and <exterior square>, with <dimensions> ten and six. If $v$ is a <highest-weight vector> of $r$, then $v\otimes v$ is a <highest-weight vector> of <weight> $(0,2)$ in the <symmetric square>. The <irreducible representation> with that <highest weight> has <dimension> ten by <4/b/solution>, exhausting the <symmetric square>:
$$
S^2r\cong V(0,2)=\mathbf{10}.
$$
For the <exterior square>, add each pair of distinct <weights> from <4/b/solution>. The resulting <multiset> of <weights> is
$$
(1,0),\quad(-1,2),\quad(1,-2),\quad(-1,0),\quad(0,0)^{[2]}.
$$
Its maximal <weight> $(1,0)$ is the <highest weight> of a five-dimensional <irreducible representation>. It accounts for the four nonzero <weights> and one zero <weight>, leaving a single zero <weight>. By <complete reducibility of semisimple Lie algebra representations>, that remaining one-dimensional summand is the <trivial representation>. Thus $\Lambda^2r\cong\mathbf5\oplus\mathbf1$, and the <spinor tensor square for so5> is
$$
\boxed{r\otimes r\cong V(0,0)\oplus V(1,0)\oplus V(0,2)
=\mathbf1\oplus\mathbf5\oplus\mathbf{10}.}
$$
The <dimension> check is $16=1+5+10$. The <trivial representation> lies in the <exterior square>, reflecting the invariant nondegenerate alternating <bilinear form> of this <pseudoreal representation>. Although $r$ itself does not descend to $SO(5)$, its <tensor square> does: the central element $-1\in\operatorname{Spin}(5)$ acts as $(-I_4)\otimes(-I_4)=I_{16}$.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Use the <basis> <matrices> $M_{ab}=E_{ab}-E_{ba}$ from <4/a/solution>. Define the <Pauli matrices> and the two-by-two identity by
$$
\sigma_1=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\sigma_2=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\sigma_3=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad I=I_2.
$$
The following five <Hermitian> four-by-four <matrices>, written as <Kronecker products>, realize a Euclidean <Clifford algebra>:
$$
\gamma_1=\sigma_1\otimes I,\quad
\gamma_2=\sigma_2\otimes I,\quad
\gamma_3=\sigma_3\otimes\sigma_1,\quad
\gamma_4=\sigma_3\otimes\sigma_2,\quad
\gamma_5=\sigma_3\otimes\sigma_3,
\qquad \gamma_a\gamma_b+\gamma_b\gamma_a=2\delta_{ab}I_4.
$$
Define the <Lie algebra representation> on $\mathbb C^4$ by
$$
\boxed{\rho(M_{ab})=\frac14[\gamma_a,\gamma_b]=\frac12\gamma_a\gamma_b\qquad(a<b).}
$$
These are <anti-Hermitian matrices>, appropriate to the real compact <Lie algebra>. For complete explicitness, all ten <representation> <matrices> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c||c|c}
M_{12}&\tfrac i2\sigma_3\otimes I&M_{13}&-\tfrac i2\sigma_2\otimes\sigma_1\\
M_{14}&-\tfrac i2\sigma_2\otimes\sigma_2&M_{15}&-\tfrac i2\sigma_2\otimes\sigma_3\\
M_{23}&\tfrac i2\sigma_1\otimes\sigma_1&M_{24}&\tfrac i2\sigma_1\otimes\sigma_2\\
M_{25}&\tfrac i2\sigma_1\otimes\sigma_3&M_{34}&\tfrac i2 I\otimes\sigma_3\\
M_{35}&-\tfrac i2 I\otimes\sigma_2&M_{45}&\tfrac i2 I\otimes\sigma_1
\end{array}}
$$
Each table entry is the image under $\rho$ of the <basis> <matrix> immediately to its left. Since the two-by-two factors are specified above, these <Kronecker products> specify every entry of each four-by-four <matrix>.

To verify the <representation> law, the <Clifford algebra> relations imply
$$
[\rho(M_{ab}),\gamma_c]=\delta_{bc}\gamma_a-\delta_{ac}\gamma_b.
$$
Apply this identity to $\rho(M_{cd})=\tfrac14[\gamma_c,\gamma_d]$ and use the <commutator product rule>. The result is
$$
[\rho(M_{ab}),\rho(M_{cd})]
=\delta_{bc}\rho(M_{ad})-\delta_{ac}\rho(M_{bd})
-\delta_{bd}\rho(M_{ac})+\delta_{ad}\rho(M_{bc}),
$$
exactly the <Lie bracket> relation in <4/a/solution>. Thus $\rho$ is a <Lie algebra representation>.

It remains to identify its <highest weight>, rather than just its <dimension>. The commuting <Hermitian> <Cartan subalgebra> operators are
$$
-i\rho(M_{12})=\tfrac12\sigma_3\otimes I,\qquad
-i\rho(M_{34})=\tfrac12I\otimes\sigma_3.
$$
Their simultaneous <eigenvalues> are $(x,y)=(\pm\tfrac12,\pm\tfrac12)$. Converting with $(a,b)=(x-y,2y)$ gives exactly the four <weights> of $V(0,1)$ in <4/b/solution>. A state with $(x,y)=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ has <highest weight> $(0,1)$, so this <Clifford realization of the so5 spin representation> is the requested $r$.

Finally, $\rho(M_{12})^2=-I_4/4$, so
$$
\boxed{e^{2\pi\rho(M_{12})}=-I_4,\qquad e^{2\pi M_{12}}=I_5.}
$$
This explicitly exhibits the distinction between the <spinor representation> of $\mathfrak{so}(5)$, or of $\operatorname{Spin}(5)$, and a single-valued <group representation> of $SO(5)$.
