A <group representation> on a <vector space> $V$ over a <field> $K$ is a <group homomorphism> $R:G\to\operatorname{GL}(V)$. In particular, $R(gh)=R(g)R(h)$, $R(e)=I$ and $R(g^{-1})=R(g)^{-1}$. Choosing a <basis> identifies it with a <matrix representation>.

Two <group representations> $R$ on $V$ and $S$ on $W$ are <equivalent representations> when an invertible <linear map> $T:V\to W$ satisfies $TR(g)=S(g)T$ for every $g$. Thus $S(g)=TR(g)T^{-1}$; they differ only by a choice of <basis>.

A nonzero <group representation> is a <reducible representation> if there is a <vector subspace> $U$ with $0\ne U\ne V$ and $R(g)U\subseteq U$ for every $g$. It is an <irreducible representation> if it has no such <invariant subspace>. Reducibility alone does not assert the existence of an invariant complementary <vector subspace>; that stronger conclusion says that the <representation> is a <semisimple representation>.

The <Schur lemma> states that an <intertwiner> $T$ between <irreducible representations> is either zero or an <isomorphism>. Consequently an <intertwiner> between inequivalent <irreducible representations> is zero. For a finite-dimensional <irreducible representation> over the <complex numbers>, every commuting <endomorphism> is scalar:
$$
\boxed{TR(g)=R(g)T\quad(\forall g)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad T=\lambda I.}
$$
For the first assertion, the <kernel> and <image> of $T$ are <invariant subspaces>, so a nonzero $T$ is injective and surjective. For the scalar conclusion, choose an <eigenvalue> $\lambda$ of $T$. The <intertwiner> $T-\lambda I$ has a nonzero <kernel>, so it must vanish. The finite-dimensional complex hypothesis matters: the scalar conclusion need not hold over the <real numbers>, as a real planar rotation through $\pi/2$ is an <irreducible representation> of the cyclic <group> of order four, while the rotation itself is a commuting nonscalar <endomorphism>.

Write $P=(P_{ij})_{i,j=1}^3$, where $P_{ij}:\mathbb C^{m_j}\to\mathbb C^{m_i}$. Block multiplication makes the commuting condition equivalent to
$$
P_{ij}R_j(g)=R_i(g)P_{ij}\qquad(\forall g\in G).
$$
Thus every block is an <intertwiner>. For $i\ne j$, the <irreducible representations> are inequivalent, so the <Schur lemma> gives $P_{ij}=0$. On each diagonal block, the same <Schur lemma> gives $P_{ii}=\lambda_iI_{m_i}$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{P=\begin{pmatrix}
\lambda_1I_{m_1}&0&0\\
0&\lambda_2I_{m_2}&0\\
0&0&\lambda_3I_{m_3}
\end{pmatrix},\qquad \lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3\in\mathbb C.}
$$
Conversely, every displayed <matrix> commutes with $R(g)$, proving both necessity and sufficiency. This <commutant of a semisimple representation> has complex <dimension> three. The parameters may vanish: $P$ was not required to be invertible.

The generators are $g_1=rs$, $g_2=r^2s$ and $g_3=s$. Moving every occurrence of $s$ to the right using $sr=r^{-1}s$ reduces every word to $r^k$ or $r^ks$ with $k=0,1,2$. The three diagonal <matrices> are distinct, as are the three off-diagonal <matrices>, so
$$
\boxed{G=\{I,r,r^2,s,rs,r^2s\},\qquad |G|=6.}
$$
For an explicit <group isomorphism>, send $r$ to $(123)$ and $s$ to $(12)$ in the <symmetric group> $S_3$, composing <permutations> from right to left. \boxed{\{I\},\qquad \{r,r^2\},\qquad \{s,rs,r^2s\}=\{g_1,g_2,g_3\}.}
$$
Their sizes are $1,2,3$, respectively. They correspond to the identity, three-cycles and <transpositions> in $S_3$.

There are three complex <irreducible representations>, because there are three <conjugacy classes>. Two are the <trivial representation> $\mathbf1$ and the <sign representation> $\varepsilon$: both send $r$ to $1$, while they send $s$ to $1$ and $-1$, respectively. These exhaust the <one-dimensional representations>, since their scalar images must satisfy $r=r^{-1}$ and $r^3=1$, forcing $r=1$, and then $s^2=1$.

The defining two-dimensional <matrix representation> $D$ has <character>
$$
\chi_D(I)=2,\qquad \chi_D(r)=\chi_D(r^2)=\alpha+\alpha^2=-1,\qquad \chi_D(r^ks)=0.
$$
Its <character inner product> with itself is
$$
\langle\chi_D,\chi_D\rangle=\frac{1}{6}(1\cdot4+2\cdot1+3\cdot0)=1,
$$
so it is an <irreducible representation>. Directly, the two coordinate lines are the distinct <eigenspaces> of $r$, and $s$ exchanges them, leaving no common invariant line. The <sum of squares of irreducible representation dimensions> is $1^2+1^2+2^2=6$, proving completeness. In the <conjugacy class> order of <2/a/solution>, the <character table> is

\boxed{\begin{array}{c|rrr}

\text{class size}&1&2&3\\\hline
\mathbf1&1&1&1\\
\varepsilon&1&1&-1\\
D&2&-1&0
\end{array}}
$$
Representatives $r,s$ in this table stand for their full <conjugacy classes>. This lists all <irreducible representations> up to <equivalence of group representations>.

The variables commute, so $x_ix_j=x_jx_i$. Thus $V$ has <dimension> six, with <basis> $x_1^2,x_2^2,x_3^2,x_1x_2,x_1x_3,x_2x_3$, even though nine coefficients occur in the redundant expression in the source. Its <representation> is the <symmetric square> of the three-dimensional <permutation representation>.

The identity fixes all six <monomials>. A three-cycle fixes none. For a <transposition>, say $(12)$, exactly $x_3^2$ and $x_1x_2$ are fixed; the remaining <monomials> occur in exchanged pairs. Therefore $\chi_{\widetilde R}=(6,0,2)$ in the <conjugacy class> order of <2/b/solution>. The <character inner products> give multiplicities
$$
n_{\mathbf1}=\frac{6+3\cdot2}{6}=2,\qquad
n_\varepsilon=\frac{6-3\cdot2}{6}=0,\qquad
n_D=\frac{6\cdot2}{6}=2.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\widetilde R\cong2\mathbf1\oplus2D.}
$$
There is also a concrete decomposition: the span of the three squares is one copy of the <permutation representation>. The span of the three mixed <monomials> is another, since $x_2x_3,x_1x_3,x_1x_2$ are permuted exactly like their omitted indices $1,2,3$. Within each, the coefficient-sum-zero <vector subspace> gives a copy of $D$, and the sum of its three <basis> <monomials> gives a <trivial representation>.

A fixed <quadratic polynomial> must have equal coefficients on each of the two <orbits> of <monomials>. Hence its fixed <vector subspace> is
$$
\boxed{V^{S_3}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb C}\{x_1^2+x_2^2+x_3^2,\quad x_1x_2+x_1x_3+x_2x_3\}.}
$$
The two displayed <polynomial invariants> are linearly independent and account for both singlets. Equivalently, one may use $x_1^2+x_2^2+x_3^2$ and $(x_1+x_2+x_3)^2$ as a <basis>. No constant term belongs to this homogeneous degree-two <vector space>.

The componentwise product is closed in $G_1\times G_2$. Associativity follows componentwise from associativity in each <group>:
$$
((g_1,g_2)(h_1,h_2))(k_1,k_2)
=((g_1h_1)k_1,(g_2h_2)k_2)
=(g_1(h_1k_1),g_2(h_2k_2)).
$$
The identity is $(e_1,e_2)$, and $(g_1,g_2)^{-1}=(g_1^{-1},g_2^{-1})$. These verify all the <group axioms>, so this is the <direct product of groups>.

Write the paper's <Kronecker product> $\times$ as $\otimes$. Its mixed-product identity and the <representation> laws of $R_1,R_2$ give
$$
\begin{aligned}
R(g_1,g_2)R(h_1,h_2)
&=(R_1(g_1)\otimes R_2(g_2))(R_1(h_1)\otimes R_2(h_2))\\
&=R_1(g_1)R_1(h_1)\otimes R_2(g_2)R_2(h_2)\\
&=R_1(g_1h_1)\otimes R_2(g_2h_2)\\
&=R((g_1,g_2)(h_1,h_2)).
\end{aligned}
$$
Also $R(e_1,e_2)=I_{n_1}\otimes I_{n_2}=I_{n_1n_2}$, and
$$
R(g_1,g_2)^{-1}=R_1(g_1)^{-1}\otimes R_2(g_2)^{-1}.
$$
Thus the images are invertible and this is a <group representation> of <dimension> $n_1n_2$, called the <external tensor product of group representations>. No irreducibility assumption is needed for this construction.

Use upper indices for the defining <representation> of <SU(3)> and lower indices for its <dual representation>. \dim(p,q)=\frac{(p+1)(q+1)(p+q+2)}{2}.
$$
The six <irreducible representations> of smallest <dimensions> are $\mathbf1,\mathbf3,\overline{\mathbf3},\mathbf6,\overline{\mathbf6},\mathbf8$. The following box arrays draw their <Young diagrams>; filling boxes by tensor-slot labels gives representative <Young tableaux>:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c}
\text{representation}&\text{diagram}&(\ell_1,\ell_2)&(p,q)&\dim\\\hline
\mathbf1&\varnothing&(0,0)&(0,0)&1\\[2pt]

* $\mathbf3$: a <vector> $v^i$, with $v^i\mapsto U^i{}_jv^j$.

The pairs with $p+q\leq2$ are precisely the six listed pairs.

An element commuting with the full first <SU(3)> factor has its first component in that factor's <center of a group>, which is discrete. In the second factor, commutation with every $\operatorname{diag}(U,1)$ forces the doublet block to be scalar and the mixed blocks to vanish, by the <Schur lemma>. Unitarity and unit <determinant> then give the connected commuting <subgroup>
$$
\boxed{U(1)_X=\{(I_3,\operatorname{diag}(e^{i\theta},e^{i\theta},e^{-2i\theta})):\theta\in\mathbb R\},\qquad X=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,-2).}

This is the <centralizer of the standard SU2 subgroup in SU3> in the second factor; the full <centralizer> in $G$ also includes the first factor's discrete <center of a group> $\mathbb Z_3$. The <Lie algebra> generator is $iX$ in the <anti-Hermitian> convention. The opposite sign or a common nonzero rescaling changes the charge convention, not the commuting direction.

In the defining <representation>, the <U(1) charges> are $1,1,-2$. In the <dual representation> they have the opposite signs. \boxed{\begin{aligned}
(\mathbf3,\mathbf3)&\downarrow=(\mathbf3,\mathbf2)_{1}\oplus(\mathbf3,\mathbf1)_{-2},\\
(\overline{\mathbf3},\mathbf1)&\downarrow=(\overline{\mathbf3},\mathbf1)_{0},\\

Each subscript denotes the <eigenvalue> of $X$, so a state of charge $q$ acquires the phase $e^{iq\theta}$. The commuting direction in <3/d/solution> provides the correct candidate <Lie algebra> for a <gauge group>, $\mathfrak{su}(3)_c\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2)_L\oplus\mathfrak u(1)_Y$. This is a necessary structural condition for the <Standard Model>, but it does not by itself establish a viable <grand unified theory>.

The <representations> in <3/c/solution> do not give a <Standard Model fermion> generation with the correct <weak hypercharges>. Use $Q_{\mathrm{electric}}=T_3+Y$. Identifying $(\mathbf3,\mathbf2)_1$ with the left-handed <quark doublet>, whose <weak hypercharge> is $1/6$, fixes $Y=X/6$. But then the doublet in $(\mathbf1,\mathbf3)$ has $Y=1/6$, whereas the left-handed <lepton> doublet requires $Y=-1/2$. Its singlet has $Y=-1/3$, whereas a right-handed <electron> requires $Y=-1$. The $(\overline{\mathbf3},\mathbf1)_0$ is a further concrete mismatch: right-handed <quarks> written as left-handed <antiquarks> require $Y=-2/3$ or $1/3$, and a rescaling cannot turn zero into either value. These comparisons use the <electroweak representation and hypercharge table>.

The charge-$-3$ doublet in $(\mathbf1,\mathbf8)$ does have the required lepton <weak hypercharge> under this normalization, but the same <adjoint representation> also brings a doublet of the opposite charge and an extra triplet. Therefore merely adding that <representation> does not supply the missing chiral generation without additional states and a mechanism for their <masses>.

Furthermore $SU(3)\times SU(3)$ is a product of two <simple Lie groups>, permitting two independent <gauge couplings>; it does not enforce a common strong and electroweak <gauge coupling>. Thus **the embedding exists, but the displayed matter content does not furnish a viable minimal unification of the Standard Model**. A more elaborate model would require additional <representations>, consistent chiral matter, <anomaly cancellation>, and a specified <spontaneous symmetry breaking> mechanism. The calculations here do not prove that every possible extension using this product <group> is impossible.

Differentiate a curve $U(t)\in SU(4)$ through $U(0)=I$, writing $X=U'(0)$. Differentiating $U(t)^\dagger U(t)=I$ gives $X^\dagger+X=0$; differentiating $\det U(t)=1$ gives $\operatorname{tr}X=0$. Conversely, for every <anti-Hermitian matrix> $X$ with zero <trace>, the <matrix exponential> $e^{tX}$ is unitary and has <determinant> $e^{t\operatorname{tr}X}=1$. Thus the real <Lie algebra> is
$$
\boxed{\mathfrak{su}(4)=\{X\in M_4(\mathbb C):X^\dagger=-X,\ \operatorname{tr}X=0\},\qquad [X,Y]=XY-YX.}
$$
The <commutator> again has zero <trace> and is <anti-Hermitian>, so this set is closed under the <Lie bracket>. Let $E_{jk}$ be the <matrix units>. A convenient real <basis> is
$$
\boxed{A_{jk}=E_{jk}-E_{kj},\quad B_{jk}=i(E_{jk}+E_{kj})\quad(1\leq j<k\leq4),\qquad H_a=i(E_{aa}-E_{a+1,a+1})\quad(a=1,2,3).}
$$
There are six independent $A_{jk}$, six independent $B_{jk}$, and three independent $H_a$. Every permitted off-diagonal complex entry is supplied by one real combination of $A_{jk},B_{jk}$; the imaginary diagonal entries have one <trace> constraint and are spanned by the $H_a$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\dim_{\mathbb R}\mathfrak{su}(4)=15,\qquad\operatorname{rank}\mathfrak{su}(4)=3.}
$$
For the <rank of a semisimple Lie algebra>, the diagonal <Cartan subalgebra> consists of $i\operatorname{diag}(t_1,t_2,t_3,t_4)$ with real $t_i$ and $\sum_i t_i=0$, of <dimension> three. Commuting <anti-Hermitian matrices> are simultaneously unitarily diagonalizable, so no larger commuting toral <Lie subalgebra> exists. In the common physics convention one uses the <Hermitian> generators $-iA_{jk},-iB_{jk},-iH_a$ and writes <group> elements as $e^{itT}$; these are the same generators with the factor of $i$ moved into the exponential.

The <complexification of a Lie algebra> gives $\mathfrak{su}(4)_{\mathbb C}=\mathfrak{sl}_4(\mathbb C)$, of type <A3 root system>. Choose <simple roots> $\alpha_1=e_1-e_2$, $\alpha_2=e_2-e_3$, $\alpha_3=e_3-e_4$, with squared lengths two. The <Cartan matrix> and <Dynkin diagram> are
$$
\boxed{A=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&0\\-1&2&-1\\0&-1&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\underset{1}{\circ}\! -\! \underset{2}{\circ}\! -\! \underset{3}{\circ}.}
$$
Each line represents a single bond. In the <Dynkin label> convention $w_a=\langle w,\alpha_a^\vee\rangle$, the <simple roots> have coordinates equal to the rows of $A$.

In the defining <representation>, apply the <simple coroots> $E_{aa}-E_{a+1,a+1}$ to the four coordinate <basis vectors>. Their <weights> are
$$
\boxed{\mathbf4=(1,0,0):\quad
v_1=(1,0,0),\quad v_2=(-1,1,0),\quad v_3=(0,-1,1),\quad v_4=(0,0,-1).}
$$
Equivalently these are obtained by subtracting $\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3$ successively from the <highest weight>. The <dual representation> has the negative <weights>, hence
$$
\boxed{\overline{\mathbf4}=(0,0,1):\quad
(-1,0,0),\quad(1,-1,0),\quad(0,1,-1),\quad(0,0,1).}
$$
All listed <weight multiplicities> are one, so both <dimensions> are four.

The <highest weight> $(1,0,1)$ is $\alpha_1+\alpha_2+\alpha_3$, the <highest root>. Its <irreducible representation> is therefore the <adjoint representation>. Under the action on <traceless matrices> by conjugation, the off-diagonal <matrix unit> $E_{ij}$ has <weight> $v_i-v_j$, while the diagonal <Cartan subalgebra> has zero <weight>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{15}=(1,0,1):\quad
&\pm(2,-1,0),\quad\pm(-1,2,-1),\quad\pm(0,-1,2),\\
&\pm(1,1,-1),\quad\pm(-1,1,1),\quad\pm(1,0,1),\\
&(0,0,0)^{[3]}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The superscript $[3]$ indicates <weight multiplicity> three. The twelve nonzero <weights> each have <weight multiplicity> one, giving $12+3=15$ in total. This also follows from the <tensor product representation> $\mathbf4\otimes\overline{\mathbf4}=\mathbf{15}\oplus\mathbf1$: the sixteen differences $v_i-v_j$ contain four zero <weights>, and removing the identity <matrix> removes one of them.

The projection deletes the third <Dynkin label>, so the four <weights> $v_i$ of <4/b/solution> restrict to $(1,0),(-1,1),(0,-1),(0,0)$. The first three are the defining <SU(3)> <weight system>, and the fourth is a singlet. Negating them gives the <dual representation>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbf4\downarrow_{SU(3)}=\mathbf3\oplus\mathbf1,\qquad
\overline{\mathbf4}\downarrow_{SU(3)}=\overline{\mathbf3}\oplus\mathbf1.}
$$
For the <adjoint representation>, the projected nonzero <weights> consist of the six <A2 root system> <roots>, the three defining <weights> and their negatives. There are also three zero <weights>. Two zero <weights> belong to the <SU(3)> <adjoint representation>, leaving one singlet. Therefore the <SU4 adjoint branching to SU3> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf{15}\downarrow_{SU(3)}=\mathbf8\oplus\mathbf3\oplus\overline{\mathbf3}\oplus\mathbf1.}
$$
One can see all four <invariant subspaces> directly by writing a <traceless matrix> as
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}A&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix},\qquad \operatorname{tr}A+d=0.
$$
The traceless part of $A$ is $\mathbf8$, the column $b$ is $\mathbf3$, the row $c$ is $\overline{\mathbf3}$, and the remaining diagonal <matrix> is proportional to $\operatorname{diag}(1,1,1,-3)$ and is fixed by <SU(3)>. These have <dimensions> $8+3+3+1=15$. Equivalently restrict $\mathbf4\otimes\overline{\mathbf4}-\mathbf1$ and use $\mathbf3\otimes\overline{\mathbf3}=\mathbf8\oplus\mathbf1$.

Take the <quarks> $q=(u,d,s,c)^T$ in the defining $\mathbf4$ of flavour <SU(4)> and the <antiquarks> in $\overline{\mathbf4}$. The flavour part of a <meson> is a <quark>-<antiquark> pair $M^i{}_j=q^i\bar q_j$. It transforms as $M\mapsto UMU^{-1}$, so the complete <four-flavour meson multiplet> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf4\otimes\overline{\mathbf4}=\mathbf{15}\oplus\mathbf1.}
$$
Its traceless part $M-\tfrac14(\operatorname{tr}M)I_4$ is the <adjoint representation> $\mathbf{15}$, the direct four-flavour analogue of the three-flavour <meson octet>. The <trace> supplies an additional <trivial representation>. Thus “the mesons” can refer to the traceless multiplet or to the complete <quark model> <tensor product>; keeping both explicit avoids dropping a state.

Using <4/c/solution>, the <SU(3)> <representation> contents are
$$
\boxed{\mathbf{15}\downarrow_{SU(3)}=\mathbf8\oplus\mathbf3\oplus\overline{\mathbf3}\oplus\mathbf1,\qquad
(\mathbf{15}\oplus\mathbf1)\downarrow_{SU(3)}=\mathbf8\oplus\mathbf3\oplus\overline{\mathbf3}\oplus2\mathbf1.}
$$
Directly, the upper-left block $q^i\bar q_j$ with $i,j\leq3$ is the old $\mathbf3\otimes\overline{\mathbf3}=\mathbf8\oplus\mathbf1$; the three entries $q^i\bar c$ form $\mathbf3$, the three entries $c\bar q_j$ form $\overline{\mathbf3}$, and $c\bar c$ is another singlet. The <dimensions> are $9+3+3+1=16$. This is a classification by <flavour symmetry>, rather than colour or spin. The much larger <charm quark> <mass> breaks the flavour <SU(4)> approximation strongly; it does not change this <representation> counting.

Compared with the complete three-flavour <meson> <tensor product>, the additional flavour states are exactly
$$
\boxed{\underbrace{(u\bar c,\ d\bar c,\ s\bar c)}_{\mathbf3},\qquad
\underbrace{(c\bar u,\ c\bar d,\ c\bar s)}_{\overline{\mathbf3}},\qquad
\underbrace{c\bar c}_{\mathbf1}.}
$$
Thus there are seven new flavour states. For the <pseudoscalar mesons>, the six open-charm states are $\bar D^0,D^-,D_s^-$ and $D^0,D^+,D_s^+$, respectively, using the <D meson> and <Ds meson> conventions. A pure $c\bar c$ <pseudoscalar meson> is the idealized flavour state associated with the <eta c meson>. The <quark model> flavour calculation alone does not specify spin or spatial excitations, so the same flavour labels also organize other <meson> species.

If comparing the traceless <SU(4)> $\mathbf{15}$ with the traceless <SU(3)> <meson octet>, the extra seven states are instead the same two triplets and the normalized <SU(3)> singlet
$$
\boxed{\eta_{15}=\frac{u\bar u+d\bar d+s\bar s-3c\bar c}{\sqrt{12}}.}
$$
Its coefficient <matrix> is traceless and has squared norm one. In particular, $c\bar c$ by itself is not a vector in the traceless $\mathbf{15}$; its coefficient <matrix> has <trace> one.

To relate these two descriptions, let $S_4=(u\bar u+d\bar d+s\bar s+c\bar c)/2$ be the normalized <SU(4)> singlet and $S_3=(u\bar u+d\bar d+s\bar s)/\sqrt3$ the old <SU(3)> singlet. Then
$$
\boxed{S_3=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}S_4+\frac12\eta_{15},\qquad
c\bar c=\frac12S_4-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\eta_{15}.}
$$
This orthonormal change of <basis> shows explicitly why the complete <tensor product> contains two light-flavour singlets but adds only one state to the old light-flavour singlet sector.
