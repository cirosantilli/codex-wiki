= Solution

A <subgroup> $H$ of a <group> $G$ is a nonempty subset closed under the inherited multiplication and inverses; equivalently, $xy^{-1}\in H$ whenever $x,y\in H$. A <normal subgroup> satisfies $gHg^{-1}=H$ for every $g\in G$.

A <group representation> on a <vector space> $V$ is a <group homomorphism> $R:G\to\operatorname{GL}(V)$, so $R(gh)=R(g)R(h)$ and $R(e)=I_V$. An <irreducible representation> has $V\ne0$ and no <invariant subspaces> other than $0$ and $V$: there is no proper nonzero $W$ with $R(g)W\subseteq W$ for every $g\in G$. We use finite-dimensional <complex representations>, with continuity for <Lie groups>, throughout.

<Branching of a representation> describes the <irreducible representation> content of a <restriction of a representation>:
$$
R\big|_H\cong\bigoplus_\lambda m_\lambda S_\lambda.
$$
This direct decomposition applies when the <restriction of a representation> is a <semisimple representation>, as for <finite groups> or continuous finite-dimensional <representations> of <compact groups>. For an arbitrary <group> and <subgroup>, complete reducibility need not hold. A <vector subspace> preserved by every $R(h)$ for $h\in H$ need not be preserved by the additional $R(g)$ for $g\notin H$, so an <irreducible representation> can become reducible.

For a concrete example, take the defining <representation> of $G=SU(2)$ on $\mathbb C^2$ and $H=\{\operatorname{diag}(z,z^{-1}):|z|=1\}$, a <subgroup> isomorphic to the <circle group>. The defining <representation> is <irreducible>: $SU(2)$ acts transitively on unit vectors, so no complex line is preserved by all its elements. Under $H$, the two coordinate lines are <invariant subspaces>, and
$$
\boxed{\mathbf2\big|_{U(1)}=\mathbf1_{+1}\oplus\mathbf1_{-1}.}
$$
Here the two <one-dimensional representations> act by $z$ and $z^{-1}$.

Let $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/n}$. The <group homomorphism> $\mathbb Z_n\to U(1)$ given by $[k]\mapsto\zeta^k$ is injective, identifying the <finite cyclic group> with the <subgroup> of $n$th <roots of unity> in the <circle group>.

The continuous <irreducible representations> of the <circle group> are the <one-dimensional representations> $R_Q(e^{i\theta})=e^{iQ\theta}$ with $Q\in\mathbb Z$. One way to see this is to make the <representation> <unitary> by averaging, simultaneously diagonalize its commuting <unitary matrices>, and impose $R_Q(e^{i(\theta+2\pi)})=R_Q(e^{i\theta})$. Its <restriction of a representation> is
$$
R_Q(\zeta^k)=e^{2\pi iQk/n}=e^{2\pi iqk/n},\qquad
\boxed{q=Q\pmod n.}
$$
Thus each <irreducible representation> remains one-dimensional, and <representations> whose integer labels differ by a multiple of $n$ become equivalent. Every <irreducible representation> of the <finite cyclic group> occurs this way.

Choose a normalization in which the <circle group> has period $2\pi$ and the <U(1) charges> $\widehat Q_i$ of the <complex scalar fields> are integers. By <restriction of a representation>,
$$
\boxed{\widehat q_i=\widehat Q_i\pmod n.}
$$
Write $v_i=\langle\phi_i\rangle$. A <vacuum expectation value> $v_i\ne0$ is preserved by $e^{i\theta}$ precisely when $e^{i\widehat Q_i\theta}=1$. Therefore the specified <finite cyclic group> survives <spontaneous symmetry breaking> exactly when every condensing <complex scalar field> obeys
$$
\widehat Q_i\equiv0\pmod n.
$$
Only <U(1) charges> of fields with nonzero <vacuum expectation values> enter this test; in the paper all $N$ fields condense.

Suppose at least one condensing <U(1) charge> is nonzero and put $d=\gcd(|\widehat Q_i|:v_i\ne0)$. Since each such <U(1) charge> is a multiple of $d$, every $d$th <root of unity> preserves the <vacuum expectation values>. Conversely, the <Bézout identity> expresses $d$ as an integer combination of these <U(1) charges>, so a common stabilizer obeys $e^{id\theta}=1$. The <discrete remnant of a circle symmetry> is consequently
$$
\boxed{H_{\rm unbroken}=\mathbb Z_d,\qquad n_{\max}=d.}
$$
A nontrivial retained <finite cyclic group> exists exactly when $d>1$, and its possible orders are the divisors $n>1$ of $d$. For example, condensing <U(1) charges> $6$ and $10$ leave $\mathbb Z_2$. If $d=1$, the <stabilizer> is trivial. If all condensing <U(1) charges> vanish, the entire <circle group> is unbroken and there is no largest finite $n$; this case does not break the symmetry assumed in the question.

Normalize the <circle group> as in <1/d/solution> and count all <Weyl fermions> as left-handed; a right-handed <Weyl fermion> is replaced by its left-handed charge conjugate. The <restriction of a representation> gives
$$
\boxed{q_a=Q_a\pmod n.}
$$
The triangle coefficient of the <cubic U(1) gauge anomaly> is proportional to $\sum_aQ_a^3$. In a theory with this single <gauge group>, cancellation of its local pure <gauge anomaly> requires
$$
\boxed{\sum_{a=1}^M Q_a^3=0.}
$$
If absence of the <mixed gauge-gravitational anomaly> is also required, as when coupling to a general gravitational background, one additionally needs $\sum_aQ_a=0$. Additional <gauge group> factors would require their mixed <gauge anomalies> to cancel as well.

Choose integer representatives and write $Q_a=q_a+nk_a$. Expanding the cubic gives
$$
0=\sum_a Q_a^3
=\sum_aq_a^3+n\sum_a\bigl(3q_a^2k_a+3nq_ak_a^2+n^2k_a^3\bigr).
$$
Hence the inherited necessary congruence, and its gravitational counterpart when imposed, are
$$
\boxed{\sum_aq_a^3\equiv0\pmod n,\qquad
\sum_aq_a\equiv0\pmod n\quad\text{(if mixed gravitational cancellation is imposed)}.}
$$
These statements concern the same complete <fermion> spectrum used in the parent theory. They are consequences of an anomaly-free integer lift, rather than a full classification of intrinsic discrete <gauge anomalies> or a sufficient test for such a lift. They also do not imply an ordinary equality for the chosen residues: for $n=3$, the anomaly-free <U(1) charges> $(1,-1)$ have residues $(1,2)$, whose cubes sum to $9$, rather than zero. Integrating out heavy <fermions> can require extra contributions to the effective discrete <gauge anomaly> accounting.

<Conjugacy classes> in the <symmetric group> are specified by <cycle type>, equivalently a <partition of an integer>[partition] of $4$. If $m_j$ counts cycles of length $j$, the number of <permutations> of that <cycle type> is $4!/\prod_j(j^{m_j}m_j!)$: cyclic rotation of each cycle and interchange of equal-length cycles do not change the <permutation>. This gives
$$
\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
\text{partition}&1^4&2\,1^2&2^2&3\,1&4\\\hline
\text{representative}&e&(12)&(12)(34)&(123)&(1234)\\
\text{class size}&1&6&3&8&6
\end{array}
$$
The sizes sum to $24$. Since the number of complex <irreducible representations> of a <finite group> equals its number of <conjugacy classes>, $S_4$ has $\boxed{5}$ inequivalent complex <irreducible representations>.

The <trivial representation> and <sign of a permutation> give
$$
R_0(\sigma)=1,\qquad R_1(\sigma)=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma).
$$
They are <one-dimensional representations>, hence <irreducible representations>. Their <characters>, in the <conjugacy class> order of <2/a/solution>, are
$$
\chi_0=(1,1,1,1,1),\qquad
\chi_1=(1,-1,1,1,-1).
$$
These are the only <one-dimensional representations>. All transpositions are conjugate, so they have the same scalar image $t$ in any <one-dimensional representation>; since a transposition squares to the identity, $t=\pm1$. Transpositions generate the <symmetric group>, fixing the whole <representation> once $t$ is chosen.

By the <sum of squares of irreducible representation dimensions>, the three remaining dimensions satisfy $d_2^2+d_3^2+d_4^2=24-1-1=22$, with each $d_i\ge2$. No $d_i$ can be at least $4$, since $16+4+4>22$. Thus the only possible dimensions are
$$
\boxed{d_2=2,\qquad d_3=d_4=3.}
$$

Use the composition convention $(\sigma\tau)(i)=\sigma(\tau(i))$. Then $R_+(\sigma)R_+(\tau)e_i=e_{\sigma(\tau(i))}=R_+(\sigma\tau)e_i$. Since the <sign of a permutation> is multiplicative, the same calculation gives $R_-(\sigma)R_-(\tau)=R_-(\sigma\tau)$. Both send the identity to $I$ and have inverses given by $\sigma^{-1}$, so both are <group representations>.

The <trace> of a <permutation matrix> counts fixed points, and multiplying that <matrix> by its <sign of a permutation> multiplies its <trace> by that sign. Thus the two <characters> are
$$
\chi_+=(4,2,0,1,0),\qquad
\chi_-=(4,-2,0,1,0).
$$
Let $u=e_1+e_2+e_3+e_4$ and $W=\{x\in\mathbb C^4:\sum_ix_i=0\}$. Both are <invariant subspaces>, and $\mathbb C^4=\mathbb Cu\oplus W$. On $\mathbb Cu$, $R_+$ is the <trivial representation> and $R_-$ is the sign <representation>. Subtracting their <characters> yields
$$
\boxed{\chi_3=\chi_+-\chi_0=(3,1,-1,0,-1),\qquad
\chi_4=\chi_--\chi_1=(3,-1,-1,0,1).}
$$
The <character inner product> verifies that it is an <irreducible representation>:
$$
\langle\chi_3,\chi_3\rangle
=\frac{9+6+3+0+6}{24}=1.
$$
The squares of the entries of $\chi_4$ are identical, so its <character inner product> with itself is also one. Their values at a transposition differ, so these <irreducible representations> are inequivalent. Therefore
$$
\boxed{R_+=R_0\oplus R_3,\qquad R_-=R_1\oplus R_4,\qquad R_4=R_1\otimes R_3.}
$$

\chi_0(g)+\chi_1(g)+2\chi_2(g)+3\chi_3(g)+3\chi_4(g)=0.

$$
I_2=\sum_{i=1}^4x_i^2,
\qquad I_3=\sum_{i=1}^4x_i^3,
$$
because every <permutation> merely rearranges their summands. In these coordinates,
$$
\boxed{I_2=\phi_1^2+\phi_2^2+\phi_3^2+(\phi_1+\phi_2+\phi_3)^2,\qquad
I_3=-3(\phi_1+\phi_2)(\phi_2+\phi_3)(\phi_3+\phi_1).}
$$
Neither <polynomial> vanishes identically: at $(\phi_1,\phi_2,\phi_3)=(1,1,1)$ they equal $12$ and $-24$. Hence coefficients multiplying $I_2$ and $I_3$ provide nonzero invariant quadratic and cubic terms. A change of <basis> transports these <polynomial invariants> to any equivalent realization of $R_3$. For a real <scalar field>, both are real. For a <complex scalar field>, a real <scalar potential> may contain $\lambda_k I_k+\lambda_k^*I_k^*$; the quadratic <Hermitian form> $\sum_i|x_i|^2$ is another invariant.

As an independent <character> check, the <symmetric square of a group representation> has <character> $[\chi(g)^2+\chi(g^2)]/2$, and the <symmetric cube of a group representation> has <character> $[\chi(g)^3+3\chi(g)\chi(g^2)+2\chi(g^3)]/6$. With $\chi=\chi_3$ these are $(6,2,2,0,0)$ and $(10,2,-2,1,0)$. Their <character inner products> with $\chi_0$ are both one, so each degree has exactly one independent <polynomial invariant>.

<Schur's lemma> states that an <intertwiner> $T:V\to W$ between <irreducible representations> is either zero or an <isomorphism>. In particular, inequivalent <irreducible representations> have no nonzero <intertwiners>. For a finite-dimensional <irreducible representation> over the <complex numbers>, every commuting <endomorphism> is scalar:
$$
\boxed{TR(g)=R(g)T\ \text{for all }g\quad\Longrightarrow\quad T=\lambda I.}
$$
Indeed, the <kernel> and <image> of an <intertwiner> are <invariant subspaces>, proving the first statement. For the second, choose an <eigenvalue> $\lambda$ of $T$. The <intertwiner> $T-\lambda I$ has nonzero <kernel>, so it must vanish. The scalar conclusion uses an algebraically closed coefficient <field>: over the real numbers, planar rotation through $\pi/2$ gives an <irreducible> real <representation> of $\mathbb Z_4$ and commutes with itself without being a real scalar <matrix>.

Partition $M$ into the same five-by-five blocks as $R$. The equation for block $(i,j)$ is $M_{ij}R_j(g)=R_i(g)M_{ij}$, so each block is an <intertwiner>. By <Schur's lemma>, blocks between the two inequivalent <irreducible representations> vanish, whereas blocks between identical copies are arbitrary scalar multiples of the relevant identity <matrix>. Thus the <commutant of a semisimple representation> is
$$
\boxed{
M=\begin{pmatrix}
a_{11}I_{n_1}&a_{12}I_{n_1}&a_{13}I_{n_1}&0&0\\
a_{21}I_{n_1}&a_{22}I_{n_1}&a_{23}I_{n_1}&0&0\\
a_{31}I_{n_1}&a_{32}I_{n_1}&a_{33}I_{n_1}&0&0\\
0&0&0&b_{11}I_{n_2}&b_{12}I_{n_2}\\
0&0&0&b_{21}I_{n_2}&b_{22}I_{n_2}
\end{pmatrix},\quad a_{ij},b_{ij}\in\mathbb C.}
$$
Equivalently, $M=(A\otimes I_{n_1})\oplus(B\otimes I_{n_2})$, where $A\in M_3(\mathbb C)$ and $B\in M_2(\mathbb C)$. Substitution proves every such <matrix> commutes with every $R(g)$. There is no invertibility requirement on $M$, and its solution space has complex dimension $3^2+2^2=13$, even if $n_1=n_2$.

The identity belongs to the <centralizer> $C_G(H)$. If $x,y\in C_G(H)$, then for every $h\in H$,
$$
(xy)h=x(yh)=x(hy)=(xh)y=h(xy).
$$
Also $xh=hx$ implies $h x^{-1}=x^{-1}h$ by multiplying on both sides by $x^{-1}$. Hence $xy$ and $x^{-1}$ belong to $C_G(H)$, proving by the <subgroup test> that $\boxed{C_G(H)\le G}$. This argument works even when $H$ is any subset, giving the <centralizer of a subset>.

Write $D(z)=\operatorname{diag}(z^{-2}I_3,z^3I_2)$ and partition a <matrix> $X$ into $3+2$ blocks. The equation $XD(z)=D(z)X$ makes its off-diagonal blocks obey $(z^3-z^{-2})X_{12}=0$ and $(z^{-2}-z^3)X_{21}=0$ for every $z\in U(1)$. Choosing any $z$ with $z^5\ne1$ forces both blocks to vanish. Conversely, every block diagonal <matrix> commutes with every $D(z)$. Intersecting with the <special unitary group> gives the exact <centralizer>
$$
\boxed{C_{SU(5)}(U(1))=S(U(3)\times U(2))
=\{\operatorname{diag}(A,B):A\in U(3),\ B\in U(2),\ \det A\det B=1\}.}
$$
Its <Lie algebra> is $\mathfrak{su}(3)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2)\oplus\mathfrak u(1)$. At the <group> level there is a finite central identification. The surjective <group homomorphism>
$$
F:SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\longrightarrow S(U(3)\times U(2)),\qquad
F(A,B,z)=\operatorname{diag}(z^{-2}A,z^3B)
$$
has <kernel> $\{(z^2I_3,z^{-3}I_2,z):z^6=1\}\cong\mathbb Z_6$. Surjectivity follows by choosing $z$ with $z^{-6}=\det A'$ for any target pair $(A',B')$, and setting $A=z^2A'$, $B=z^{-3}B'$. Thus
$$
\boxed{C_{SU(5)}(U(1))\cong
\bigl(SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)\bigr)/\mathbb Z_6.}
$$
The six distinct <kernel> elements give a concrete reason the displayed map does not identify the <centralizer> with an unquotiented product.

Number the <simple roots> of the $A_4$ <root system> consecutively along its <Dynkin diagram>. For a <Young diagram> with row lengths $r_i$, its <highest weight> has <Dynkin labels> $a_i=r_i-r_{i+1}$. The three <irreducible representations> have the following <Young diagrams>, which can be filled increasingly down the column to give <Young tableaux>:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
&\text{column of boxes}&\text{tensor}&\text{highest-weight labels}\\\hline
\mathbf5&\boxed{1}&v^i&(1,0,0,0)\\
\overline{\mathbf5}&\begin{matrix}\boxed{1}\\\boxed{2}\\\boxed{3}\\\boxed{4}\end{matrix}
&w^{[i_1i_2i_3i_4]}\ \text{or }\widetilde v_i&(0,0,0,1)\\
\mathbf{10}&\begin{matrix}\boxed{1}\\\boxed{2}\end{matrix}
&T^{ij}=-T^{ji}&(0,1,0,0)
\end{array}
$$
Here $i=1,\ldots,5$, $v^i\mapsto U^i{}_jv^j$, and $T^{ij}\mapsto U^i{}_kU^j{}_lT^{kl}$. Contraction with the invariant alternating <tensor> identifies $\widetilde v_i=(1/4!)\epsilon_{ii_1i_2i_3i_4}w^{i_1i_2i_3i_4}$, giving the <dual representation> of $\mathbf5$. The dimensions follow from $\binom51=5$, $\binom54=5$, $\binom52=10$.

Use the <centralizer> and <circle group> normalization of <3/d/solution>. The first three components have <U(1) charge> $-2$ and the last two have <U(1) charge> $3$, so the <branching of a representation> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf5\longrightarrow(\mathbf3,\mathbf1)_{-2}\oplus(\mathbf1,\mathbf2)_3,\qquad
\overline{\mathbf5}\longrightarrow(\overline{\mathbf3},\mathbf1)_2\oplus(\mathbf1,\mathbf2)_{-3}.}
$$
For the <dual representation>, both <U(1) charges> reverse and the $SU(3)$ <representation> is conjugated; the $SU(2)$ doublet is equivalent to its conjugate. Taking the <exterior square> of the first decomposition gives three types of antisymmetric <tensor> components: $T^{ab}$, $T^{a\alpha}$, and $T^{\alpha\beta}$, where $a,b=1,2,3$ and $\alpha,\beta=4,5$. \boxed{\mathbf{10}\longrightarrow
(\overline{\mathbf3},\mathbf1)_{-4}\oplus(\mathbf3,\mathbf2)_1\oplus(\mathbf1,\mathbf1)_6.}
$$
The dimensions are $3+6+1=10$. These product labels describe genuine <representations> of the quotient <group> in <3/d/solution>: every element of its $\mathbb Z_6$ <kernel> acts trivially on each summand.

The <highest root> of the <D5 root system> in our numbering is $\theta=e_1+e_2=\alpha_1+2\alpha_2+2\alpha_3+\alpha_4+\alpha_5$. Adjoin $\alpha_0=-\theta$. The <extended Dynkin diagram> has an extra bond $0-2$. Deleting node $2$ leaves the two isolated nodes $0,1$ and the chain $4-3-5$, hence the <root subsystem> $A_1\oplus A_1\oplus A_3$.

This is a <maximal Lie subalgebra>, not merely a full-rank <Lie subalgebra>. In the orthogonal realization it is $\mathfrak{so}(4)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(6)\subset\mathfrak{so}(10)$, acting on a $4+6$ decomposition. The remaining off-diagonal part transforms as the irreducible <tensor product representation> $(\mathbf4,\mathbf6)$ of dimension $24$. Any intermediate <Lie subalgebra> containing one nonzero vector in that complement must contain the entire complement, because this <representation> is <irreducible>, and hence all $6+15+24=45$ dimensions. This proves maximality.

To apply the supplied projection, write a <D5 root system> <weight> as the column $w=(a,b,c,d,e)^T$ of <Dynkin labels>. Then
$$
Pw=(c+d+e,\ c,\ a+b+c+e,\ b+c+d,\ -a-b-c-d)^T.
$$
For example, the <highest weight> $w=(0,0,0,0,1)^T$ maps to $(1,0;1,0,0)^T$, and $w=(-1,0,1,-1,0)^T$ maps to $(0,1;0,0,1)^T$. Applying the same expression to all sixteen <weights> in <4/b/solution> gives exactly
$$
\{(\epsilon,0;f):\epsilon=\pm1,\ f\in F\}
\ \cup\ 
\{(0,\epsilon;\bar f):\epsilon=\pm1,\ \bar f\in\overline F\},
$$
where $F$ and $\overline F$ are the four-element <weight systems> in <4/a/solution>. Each set has eight distinct <weights>, each of multiplicity one. Since an $A_1$ doublet has <weights> $\pm1$ and an $A_1$ singlet has <weight> zero, the <branching of a representation> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf{16}\longrightarrow R=(\mathbf2,\mathbf1,\mathbf4)\oplus(\mathbf1,\mathbf2,\overline{\mathbf4}).}
$$
The <highest weights> of these two summands are $(1,0;1,0,0)$ and $(0,1;0,0,1)$, and $2\cdot1\cdot4+1\cdot2\cdot4=16$ checks the dimensions. Here $SU(2)\times SU(2)\times SU(4)$ is the convenient covering <group>; its image inside $\operatorname{Spin}(10)$ has a common $\mathbb Z_2$ central identification. Both summands are trivial on $(-I_2,-I_2,-I_4)$.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Choose the first $SU(2)$ as $SU_w(2)$ and embed $SU_c(3)$ in the upper-left three-by-three block of $SU(4)$. The <fundamental representations> then restrict as $\mathbf4\to\mathbf3\oplus\mathbf1$ and $\overline{\mathbf4}\to\overline{\mathbf3}\oplus\mathbf1$. Thus the <representation> in <4/c/solution>, forgetting <hypercharge>, restricts to
$$
(\mathbf2,\mathbf3)\oplus(\mathbf2,\mathbf1)
\oplus2(\mathbf1,\overline{\mathbf3})\oplus2(\mathbf1,\mathbf1).
$$
This contains every required $SU_w(2)\times SU_c(3)$ <representation>, with one extra singlet.

For the <hypercharge> embedding, let $X=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,1,-3)$ in the $SU(4)$ fundamental <representation>, and $T_{3R}=\operatorname{diag}(1/2,-1/2)$ in the second $SU(2)$. These commute with $SU_w(2)\times SU_c(3)$. The integer normalization in the paper is
$$
\boxed{Y=X+6T_{3R}.}
$$
Explicitly, this <circle group> embeds in the covering <group> by
$$
z\longmapsto\left(I_2,\begin{pmatrix}z^3&0\\0&z^{-3}\end{pmatrix},
\operatorname{diag}(z,z,z,z^{-3})\right).
$$
Both nontrivial blocks have determinant one, and the map is injective since the first three entries of the $SU(4)$ block recover $z$.

On $(\mathbf2,\mathbf1,\mathbf4)$, $T_{3R}=0$, so the <branching of a representation> gives $(\mathbf2,\mathbf3)_1\oplus(\mathbf2,\mathbf1)_{-3}$. On $(\mathbf1,\mathbf2,\overline{\mathbf4})$, the <U(1) charges> under $X$ are $-1$ on $\overline{\mathbf3}$ and $3$ on $\mathbf1$, while $6T_{3R}$ takes values $\pm3$. Hence
$$
\begin{aligned}
(\mathbf1,\mathbf2,\overline{\mathbf4})&\longrightarrow
(\mathbf1,\overline{\mathbf3})_{-1+3}\oplus
(\mathbf1,\overline{\mathbf3})_{-1-3}\\
&\qquad\oplus(\mathbf1,\mathbf1)_{3+3}\oplus(\mathbf1,\mathbf1)_{3-3}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Combining the two summands gives
$$
\boxed{R\longrightarrow R_F\oplus(\mathbf1,\mathbf1)_0.}
$$
This is the <Pati-Salam charge embedding>, with $X=3(B-L)$. In terms of the common <Standard Model> convention $Q_{\rm electric}=T_{3L}+Y_{\rm SM}$, the paper's integer <hypercharge> is $Y=6Y_{\rm SM}$.

The extra neutral singlet can be interpreted as the left-handed charge conjugate $\nu_R^c$ of a <right-handed neutrino>. It is a <sterile neutrino> under the <Standard Model> <gauge group>, and completes the sixteen <Weyl fermions> of one family in this <half-spin representation>. Its neutrality permits a <Majorana mass term> without violating the remaining <Standard Model> <gauge group>.
