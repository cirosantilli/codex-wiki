=== Solution

**Taken at face value, the printed two-sided <p-value> rejects equality of average <political efficacy> at the 5% level, with the non-employed group scoring higher. The effect magnitude and strength of evidence cannot be reported as one coherent result because the estimates, <interval>, <test statistic> and <p-value> contradict one another.**

Let $\mu_0$ and $\mu_1$ be the expected <political efficacy> scores in the two employment groups in the <target population>. The <null hypothesis> is $H_0:\mu_0-\mu_1=0$ and the two-sided <alternative hypothesis> is $H_1:\mu_0-\mu_1\ne0$. A <Welch's t-test> compares the difference of <sample means> with its <standard error>, estimating the two group <variances> separately:
$$
t=\frac{\bar y_0-\bar y_1}{\sqrt{s_0^2/n_0+s_1^2/n_1}}.
$$
The reference <Student t-distribution> has approximate, generally noninteger <statistical degrees of freedom>; it does not require equality of the two <variances>. Observations should be <independent>, with finite group <variances>; <normal distributions> give the usual small-sample rationale, while large samples permit an approximate normal <sampling distribution> under suitable conditions. A large sample cannot correct a biased sampling procedure or dependence between observations.<Two-Sample t-Test for Equal Means by National Institute of Standards and Technology>[§1.3.5.3, unequal-variance statistic and Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom]{ref}

A <p-value> is a <probability> calculated *under the <null hypothesis> and the sampling assumptions*: for this <two-sided test> it measures the <probability> of an absolute <test statistic> at least as large as the observed one. It is not the <probability> that the <null hypothesis> is true, the <probability> that chance caused the observed difference, or a measure of the <effect size>. If the <significance level> $\alpha=0.05$ was chosen in advance, the printed $p=0.049521$ falls just below it. This would reject $H_0$ at 5%, but not at 1%. The <significance level> describes the long-run <probability> of a <type I error>, rejecting a true <null hypothesis>; a <type II error> instead fails to reject a false <null hypothesis>. <Statistical power> is the rejection <probability> under a specified <alternative hypothesis>. None of these errors for this particular realised study can be read as a <posterior probability> from $p$.

Both the printed <sample means> and the positive <confidence interval> point toward greater <political efficacy> in group 0, the non-employed group. Their magnitudes disagree. Subtracting the printed <sample means> gives $8.656650-6.322812=2.333838$ scale units. Yet the printed 95% <confidence interval> has midpoint $(0.2822355+0.6743412)/2=0.47828835$, not $2.333838$. A two-sided Welch <interval> is centred on the difference of <sample means>. This violates the <consistency identities for a two-sided t-test>; the <interval> and estimates cannot describe the same reported comparison. If the <interval> alone were trusted, it would exclude zero and indicate an increase between about $0.28$ and $0.67$ units, but that is explicitly a conditional interpretation. A 95% <confidence interval> refers to repeated-sample coverage of a fixed <statistical parameter>, not a 95% <posterior probability> for that realised <interval>.<Student's t-Test in the R documentation>[Arguments and Value: two-sided default, separate group variances, estimate, statistic, p.value and conf.int]{ref}

There are additional arithmetic warnings. The printed $t=6.1616$ at $2022.4$ <statistical degrees of freedom> gives a two-sided <p-value> about $8.67\times10^{-10}$, not $0.049521$. Thus taking the <test statistic> as authoritative would imply much stronger evidence, not merely a marginal 5% result. The employment counts sum to $2311$, but their <percentages> are $905/2311\approx39.16\%$ and $1406/2311\approx60.84\%$, not $39.27\%$ and $60.73\%$. If those counts also apply to the two <political efficacy> <sample means>, their <weighted arithmetic mean> would be about $7.237$, not the reported overall $8.348$; separate complete-case group sizes are not supplied, so that last cross-output comparison is conditional on a common sample. No uniquely corrected difference, <standard error> or <interval> follows from these contradictory fields.

The <political efficacy> summary describes a bounded observed scale from $0$ to $16$, with <median> $9$ and middle <quartiles> $7$ and $12$. The <interquartile range> is $5$; the overall <sample mean> $8.348$ being below the <median> does not, by itself, establish a particular distributional shape. These <descriptive statistics> cannot supply the missing coherent group <variances> or a standardized <effect size>. Even a corrected small <p-value> would establish an association under a <statistical model>, rather than a <causal effect> of employment. Age, other background <covariates>, <selection bias> and the quality of the <political efficacy> measurement remain relevant. **A defensible report gives the conditional 5% decision and direction, identifies the contradictions, and withholds a single numerical effect-and-uncertainty claim until the output is checked.**

**Employment is observed for all 2053 listed cases. <Social media> use is observed for 1424 cases, with 629 missing. The age <percentages> describe the five visible rows, whose counts total 1730; they cannot be treated as a complete age distribution for the stated 2053 cases.**

A <frequency table> describes category counts and their <relative frequencies>. The full-sample <percentage> uses every listed case as denominator; a <valid percentage> excludes <missing data> for that variable. A <cumulative percentage> adds the <valid percentages> up to an ordered category. The denominator matters as much as the number: a statement about respondents with recorded use need not describe every respondent.

Employment is a <binary categorical variable>: 1216 cases are employed and 837 are not, or about $59.23\%$ and $40.77\%$. Neither employment category is missing in this output. Employment labels have no intrinsic numerical spacing; codes zero and one identify the two categories under a chosen ordering convention. <Cumulative percentages> for a <nominal categorical variable> depend on the arbitrary display order and add little to this two-category description.

Age groups are an <ordinal categorical variable> because their <intervals> have a natural ordering, although the grouped representation loses exact ages. Among the five *visible* age rows, ages 25–44 form the largest group, with $562/1730\approx32.49\%$, and ages 14–24 the smallest, with $182/1730\approx10.52\%$. The other visible groups account for $20.98\%$, $16.42\%$ and $19.60\%$ on that denominator. About $43.01\%$ of the visible five-row count is below 45. These <percentages> should not be silently applied to the stated total $2053$: for example, the same $562$ cases are only $27.37\%$ of $2053$.

The age variable key advertises six categories, whereas only five appear. Their counts sum to $1730$, leaving a difference of $323$ against the printed total; the table nevertheless prints zero missing values. This is an internal source discrepancy, not permission to label those $323$ cases as a particular unseen age group or as recorded <missing data>. The oldest row is labelled 65+ here but 65–74 in Figure 2. Even the age <percentage> total has a small display discrepancy: the five rounded <percentages> sum to $100.01\%$, while the printed valid total and last <cumulative percentage> say $99.99\%$. The much larger count inconsistency remains after allowing ordinary rounding.

<Social media> use is also an <ordinal categorical variable>. Its six ordered categories express increasing frequency, not equally spaced measurements: averaging category labels or claiming a one-unit change has a constant time meaning would require an additional scoring convention. Among the $1424$ observed responses, the <mode> is never, at $459/1424\approx32.23\%$, followed by daily, at $28.37\%$. Weekly use accounts for $18.19\%$, use more than once a day for $11.38\%$, less-than-monthly use for $6.39\%$, and monthly use for $3.44\%$. The first three categories together comprise about $42.06\%$ of valid responses. Consequently the middle ordered response lies in the weekly category: cumulative frequency first passes one half there.

Combining the two daily categories gives $566/1424\approx39.75\%$ of valid responses using <social media> at least daily. The same recorded responses are $566/2053\approx27.57\%$ of all listed cases. The second <percentage> is the fraction *recorded* as daily users, not an estimate that classifies all missing responses as nonusers. Missing <social media> responses make up $629/2053\approx30.64\%$. The directly calculated valid fraction is $69.36\%$; summing the individually rounded full-sample <percentages> produces the printed $69.37\%$, a minor rounding difference. With no account of the <missing-data mechanism>, the observed valid distribution should not automatically be generalized to those with missing use. Employment being fully observed does not make <social media> missingness ignorable. **The substantive distributional summary must retain both the valid-response denominator and the missing-data qualification.**

**The displayed age groups have fairly similar employment proportions, with no coherent <monotone> age trend. The printed test reports no evidence against age–employment <independence>; recomputing from the five visible rows reaches the same qualitative conclusion. Their totals and several auxiliary fields nevertheless do not represent one consistent table.**

Each row <percentage> estimates an employment proportion *within that age group*. For example, $105/182=57.69\%$ of the youngest displayed group are employed. The employed proportions in the next four rows are $61.57\%$, $62.26\%$, $56.69\%$ and $57.23\%$. The largest displayed proportion is in the 45–54 group and the smallest in the 55–64 group, a difference of about $5.57$ <percentage points>. These are conditional <percentages>, not the age distribution of employed people. The sequence rises and then falls; there is no simple increasing or decreasing pattern.

The <null hypothesis> for a <Pearson chi-squared test of independence> is that the joint category <probabilities> factor into their age and employment marginals. Its <expected frequencies> are $E_{ij}=n_{i+}n_{+j}/n$, and its <test statistic> adds the standardized squared count discrepancies, $X^2=\sum_{ij}(O_{ij}-E_{ij})^2/E_{ij}$. For a complete $r\times c$ <contingency table> with positive margins, the large-sample reference <chi-squared distribution> has $(r-1)(c-1)$ <statistical degrees of freedom>. <Independent> observations and an appropriate sampling scheme are essential; adequate <expected counts> make the approximation more plausible.<Pearson's Chi-squared Test for Count Data in the R documentation>[Details: independence null, asymptotic reference distribution and observed/expected counts]{ref}

Reading only the printed test block, $X^2=4.83463$, $5$ <statistical degrees of freedom> and $p\approx0.4364$ are mutually compatible. The printed <likelihood-ratio test> gives nearly the same <p-value>, about $0.435$. At a prechosen 5% <significance level> neither rejects <independence>. This is a failure to find evidence, not proof of equal employment <probabilities> at every age. The printed <Cramér's V> around $0.049$ suggests a small sample association; the <contingency coefficient> around $0.048$ has the same broad message. The <generalized phi coefficient> is an unsigned chi-squared magnitude here, not evidence for a positive age trend. A small association magnitude and a large <p-value> answer different questions.

But only a $5\times2$ table is actually visible. Those rows total $1730$, with employment column sums $698$ and $1032$, rather than the printed $2053$ with columns $837$ and $1216$. Computing solely from the five visible rows gives
$$
X^2\approx4.034032,\qquad \nu=4,\qquad p\approx0.40142,\qquad V\approx0.04829.
$$
Its <likelihood-ratio test statistic> is about $4.03243$, again with $4$ <statistical degrees of freedom>. The smallest count-based <expected frequency> is about $73.43$, comfortably large for this ordinary approximation, but different from the printed minimum $74.20068$. These figures are a labelled analysis of the *visible subset*, not a repaired full dataset. An unprinted sixth age row might explain some intended metadata, but neither its label nor a uniquely justified reconstruction is supplied. The oldest printed label also conflicts with Figures 1 and 8.

The ordinal measures look for an ordered tendency. <Goodman and Kruskal's gamma> compares <concordant pairs> with <discordant pairs> among pairs untied on both variables. The printed negative gamma, <Somers' D>, <Kendall's tau-b> and <Stuart's tau-c> therefore suggest, at most, a very weak tendency toward lower employment at higher age categories under the specified ordering. Their precise magnitudes are not reliable here. The ordinary <unordered-pair partition for ordinal data> fails for the printed pair counts, even when compared with the printed $2053$ cases. On the five visible rows the respective count-based values are about $-0.0356$, $-0.0172$ for employment conditional on age, $-0.0218$ and $-0.0266$. None gives a substantial <monotone> pattern; a near-zero ordinal measure would not exclude a nonmonotone relationship in general.

<Goodman and Kruskal's lambda> is zero in both predictive directions on the visible table: knowing age does not improve the number of correct predictions made by always choosing the most common employment category, and knowing employment does not improve modal age-category prediction. Zero modal-prediction improvement does not mean every category distribution is identical. **Both the reported test block and the separately recomputed visible-table test fail to reject <independence>; neither justifies asserting no association, a causal age effect, or a coherent full-sample calculation from the contradictory printout.**

=== Solution

**Within the 1424 complete records displayed here, employed respondents report more frequent use on average in the ordinal sense of positive pairwise association. The overall <independence> test is significant, but its sample association magnitude is modest and the result is observational. Some printed ordinal summaries are internally inconsistent.**

Compare row <percentages> because employment groups have different sizes. Never-use is reported by $219/599=36.56\%$ of the non-employed group and $240/825=29.09\%$ of the employed group. Daily use is $24.21\%$ versus $31.39\%$; more-than-daily use is $9.02\%$ versus $13.09\%$. Combining the last two categories gives $33.22\%$ versus $44.48\%$, an employed-minus-non-employed difference of about **11.26 <percentage points>**. That threshold comparison is a descriptive summary of the six-category table, not a replacement for the original omnibus test or a newly declared prespecified endpoint. It does not assign equal spacing to the six frequency categories.

Here the row counts, column counts and grand total are additive. Under the <null hypothesis> of employment–use <independence>, a $2\times6$ <contingency table> has $(2-1)(6-1)=5$ <statistical degrees of freedom>. The <Pearson chi-squared statistic> recomputed from the cells is $21.132241$, agreeing with the printed value, and its <p-value> is about $0.0007648$. The <likelihood-ratio test statistic> $G^2\approx21.25379$ gives $p\approx0.000725$. Both reject <independence> at the conventional 5% and 1% significance levels under the sampling assumptions. The minimum <expected frequency>, about $20.61$, gives no sparse-cell warning for this table. The tests compare whole category distributions; they do not by themselves identify one category as the source of the association or establish its causal direction.

<Cramér's V> is $\sqrt{21.132241/1424}\approx0.12182$. The <generalized phi coefficient> coincides with it for this two-row table, while the <contingency coefficient> is about $0.121$. These are modest magnitudes; a small <p-value> does not make the relationship large or useful for individual prediction. The employment–use association could change after conditioning on age or other <covariates>, and the sample excludes $629$ cases without recorded use. Relative to the employment margins in Figure 1, the omitted complete-case counts are $238$ non-employed and $391$ employed, or about $28.43\%$ and $32.15\%$ of those groups. Different missing fractions alone do not identify the <missing-data mechanism>, but they rule out treating complete records as automatically representative of everyone.

The count-based <Goodman and Kruskal's gamma> is
$$
\gamma=\frac{221614-157488}{221614+157488}\approx0.16915.
$$
With the employment categories ordered non-employed then employed and use ordered from never to more than daily, this positive value means <concordant pairs> outnumber <discordant pairs> among pairs untied on both variables. It is not a 16.9% increase in a person's use and is not a <causal effect>. Reversing one ordering reverses its sign. Excluding ties can make gamma appreciably larger than tie-adjusted measures.

For use as response and employment as predictor, the cells give <Somers' D> about $0.12976$, <Kendall's tau-b> about $0.10362$ and <Stuart's tau-c> about $0.12650$. The printed Somers value $0.141$ and tau-b $0.108$ should not be presented as verified calculations: the table prints $77297$ second-variable-only ties, whereas its cells give $115073$. The other four pair classes agree with the cells; the printed five classes sum to $975400$, not $\binom{1424}{2}=1013176$. The source does not establish an alternative tie convention that repairs those ordinary identities. The direction remains a weak positive association under either description, but the exact measures require coherent pair counts.

Modal prediction provides another perspective. Without employment information, predicting the globally most frequent use category, never, makes $1424-459=965$ errors. Predicting a separate modal use category in each employment group makes $1424-(219+259)=946$ errors. Thus <Goodman and Kruskal's lambda> for *use given employment* is $(965-946)/965\approx0.01969$: about a 1.97% reduction in modal-classification errors. For *employment given use* the corresponding reduction is $3/599\approx0.00501$. These identify the targets explicitly; the source's row-dependent/column-dependent labels should not be used to reverse the target interpretation. The two small numbers show why an <independence> test can be significant while coarse modal prediction improves very little. **The supported overall finding is a modest positive association among observed respondents, requiring conditional analysis and a missing-data qualification before broader claims.**

**Every displayed age stratum has a higher proportion of employed respondents reporting use at least daily, and each has positive ordinal association. The visible counts do not support the printed claim of a conventional significant omnibus result in any one stratum: several test fields are contradictory, and sparse cells further limit the chi-squared approximation. Age-specific significance labels do not establish <interaction> or causation.**

Conditioning on age means comparing the employment groups *within each displayed age category*. Each within-row <percentage> estimates a use distribution for that age–employment combination. This helps assess a marginal association that could reflect different age compositions, but broad age categories do not remove all residual <confounding>. The relevant conditional-independence <null hypothesis> is that employment and use are <independent> within every age stratum. It differs both from marginal <independence> and from a <null hypothesis> that the strength of association is identical across ages.

For the 14–24 stratum, $N=169$. The non-employed group reports use at least daily in $(27+15)/69=60.87\%$ of cases, versus $(48+24)/100=72.00\%$ of employed cases: an 11.13-percentage-point difference. Daily use alone is $39.13\%$ versus $48.00\%$. This is not a uniform upward shift at every threshold: never-use is also higher among employed respondents, $8.00\%$ versus $4.35\%$, whereas weekly use is lower, $11.00\%$ versus $24.64\%$. The count-based <Cramér's V> is about $0.199$ and <Goodman and Kruskal's gamma> about $0.100$. The cells give $X^2=6.721811$ on $5$ <statistical degrees of freedom>, with $p\approx0.24217$, agreeing qualitatively with the likelihood-ratio result $G^2=6.670009$, $p\approx0.246$. The printed Pearson $X^2=9.721811$ and $p=0.0491651$ disagree with the cells *and* with one another: that printed statistic at $5$ <statistical degrees of freedom> would give about $0.08351$, not $0.04917$. Four of twelve <expected frequencies> are below five, with minimum about $2.45$, so an exact conditional test or a <Monte Carlo test> is preferable for calibrated inference rather than trusting a marginal printed significance label.

For ages 25–44, $N=519$. At-least-daily use is $93/202=46.04\%$ in the non-employed group and $181/317=57.10\%$ in the employed group, a difference of 11.06 <percentage points>. Never-use is $17.82\%$ versus $15.46\%$, daily use $35.15\%$ versus $39.75\%$, and more-than-daily use $10.89\%$ versus $17.35\%$. The count-based <Cramér's V> is about $0.142$ and <Goodman and Kruskal's gamma> about $0.174$. The visible cells give $X^2=10.475487$, $p\approx0.06283$, and $G^2=10.451334$, $p\approx0.06341$, each with $5$ <statistical degrees of freedom>. These differ materially from the printed Pearson $12.47549$, $p=0.04283048$, and printed likelihood-ratio $12.451$, $p=0.043$. Even the printed Pearson statistic at its stated <statistical degrees of freedom> gives $p\approx0.02882$, not the stated <p-value>. The minimum <expected frequency> is about $5.06$, so the principal difficulty here is corrupted numerical output rather than a listed sparse-cell warning. The recomputed 5% test fails to reject <independence>, although its <p-value> is not evidence that the positive descriptive contrast is exactly zero.

For ages 45–54, $N=304$. At-least-daily use is $33/119=27.73\%$ versus $68/185=36.76\%$, a difference of 9.03 <percentage points>. Never-use is $37.82\%$ versus $31.89\%$, and daily use $19.33\%$ versus $27.03\%$. The count-based <Cramér's V> is about $0.105$ and <Goodman and Kruskal's gamma> about $0.133$. The cells give $X^2=3.350752$, $p\approx0.64608$, consistent with the likelihood-ratio $G^2=3.382693$, $p\approx0.641$. The printed Pearson $9.350752$ and $p=0.046081$ are incompatible with the cells and with one another: using that statistic at $5$ <statistical degrees of freedom> gives $p\approx0.09587$. The smallest <expected frequency> is about $6.26$. Thus this is another positive descriptive contrast without a count-supported significant omnibus result at 5%.

For ages 55–64, $N=203$. At-least-daily use is $16/86=18.60\%$ versus $31/117=26.50\%$, a difference of 7.89 <percentage points>. Never-use is $54.65\%$ versus $48.72\%$, and daily use $11.63\%$ versus $17.95\%$. <Cramér's V> is about $0.125$ and <Goodman and Kruskal's gamma> about $0.133$. The printed and count-based Pearson result agrees: $X^2=3.190901$, $5$ <statistical degrees of freedom>, $p\approx0.67058$. The likelihood-ratio <p-value> is about $0.668$. Two of twelve <expected frequencies> are below five, with minimum about $3.39$; the nominal result does not indicate rejection, but a simulation-based check would be prudent if formal calibration mattered. Neither a large <p-value> nor the smaller sample establishes an absence of association.

For the oldest stratum, labelled 65+ in Figure 8, $N=155$. At-least-daily use is $10/82=12.20\%$ versus $13/73=17.81\%$, a difference of 5.61 <percentage points>. Never-use is $70.73\%$ versus $56.16\%$. <Cramér's V> is about $0.217$ and <Goodman and Kruskal's gamma> about $0.269$, the largest of the five displayed strata. Yet $X^2=7.269606$ on $5$ <statistical degrees of freedom> gives $p\approx0.20135$, while the likelihood-ratio <p-value> is about $0.117$. There are four <expected frequencies> below five, with minimum about $0.471$. Sampling uncertainty and a sparse distribution therefore matter greatly; the larger descriptive ordinal magnitude is not evidence for a significant age <interaction>. The zero observed entries do not establish zero population <probabilities>. An exact conditional test or a <Monte Carlo test> can retain the six categories without relying on a doubtful asymptotic approximation. Collapsing categories should have a substantive justification; searching for a grouping that produces significance changes the inferential problem.<Pearson's Chi-squared Test for Count Data in the R documentation>[Details: asymptotic versus simulated fixed-margin tests and the role of positive margins]{ref}

Its overall reference is deliberately separated: the five age strata total $1350$, not the $1424$ in Figure 3, so their <percentages> cannot be pooled and called the reported overall result.

Across the visible strata, frequent use declines strongly with age in both employment groups, while the at-least-daily employment difference remains positive. This is compatible with age being important to the use distribution and with a positive employment association persisting after age stratification. It is not a demonstrated <Simpson's paradox>: none of these displayed threshold differences reverses direction. The evidence also does not establish how much age confounds the overall result, because the strata do not reproduce its sample and the oldest age labels disagree across figures. Among young respondents the positive ordinal association coexists with a reversed never-use contrast, so the threshold summary should not be described as <stochastic domination> of every <conditional distribution>.

Ordinal measures summarize a different aspect from the omnibus tests. Counting pairs from each visible table gives <Goodman and Kruskal's gamma> approximately $0.100,0.174,0.133,0.133,0.269$ in age order. For use as response, the corresponding <Somers' D> values are about $0.072,0.132,0.102,0.090,0.152$; <Kendall's tau-b> values are $0.059,0.105,0.081,0.076,0.144$; and <Stuart's tau-c> values are $0.070,0.125,0.097,0.088,0.152$. They are all positive and modest, but not interchangeable. Gamma discards pairs tied on either variable; <Somers' D> includes response ties separated by employment; <Kendall's tau-b> adjusts for ties in both variables; <Stuart's tau-c> adjusts for table dimensions. The printed second-variable tie counts violate the <unordered-pair partition for ordinal data> in all five strata, so the printed Somers and tau-b values should not be accepted as independently verified numbers.<Ordinal association measures in the DescTools documentation>[KendallTauB and StuartTauC: distinct tie/table-size adjustments]{ref}

<Goodman and Kruskal's lambda> for predicting the *use category from employment* is zero in all five visible strata: each employment row has the same modal use category as its stratum overall. This does not contradict their nonzero gamma or V, because the rest of each category distribution can change without its <mode> changing. Reversing the prediction target gives about $0.087,0.025,0,0.023,0.123$ for predicting *employment from use*. In the original display these are the second lambda values; identify the target directly rather than relying on ambiguous row/column-dependent labels. The <contingency coefficient>, another unsigned transformation of the Pearson statistic, adds no new direction and cannot repair a corrupted <test statistic>.

Even if all printed <p-values> were trusted, a significant result in one age group and a nonsignificant result in another would not prove that their effects differ. A <statistical interaction> needs a direct comparison of effects on a specified scale, not a comparison of significance labels. Five separate tests also create a multiple-testing issue if the aim is to claim at least one association; choosing categories or a threshold after inspection adds further selection. A <joint conditional independence test for stratified contingency tables> or an appropriate categorical-response model can target a clear combined hypothesis, with exact or simulation-based calibration where needed. Such a model would require coherent input tables before it could resolve the source discrepancies. **The sound conclusion is a consistent positive descriptive tendency within the five shown strata, with no count-supported nominal 5% omnibus rejection in an individual stratum, and insufficient coherent output for a claimed causal or precisely quantified age-adjusted effect.**

=== Solution

**Read conditionally, the fitted slope says that a one-point higher skill score is associated with about 0.209 more household devices in the fitted <conditional expectation>. The printed summary also claims a significant slope and about 28.93% explained sample variation. Those claims cannot be assembled into one valid fitted model: several regression identities fail, so a single verified coefficient-and-uncertainty report is unavailable.**

The response is a device count, while the predictor is a skill score. A <simple linear regression> models $\mathbb E(Y\mid x)=\beta_0+\beta_1x$, where $Y$ is the device count and $x$ the skill score. <Ordinary least squares> chooses the <intercept> and <regression slope> to minimize the <residual sum of squares>. The <regression slope> describes a conditional association, not the <causal effect> of training or acquiring a device.

The <descriptive statistics> put the device count between $0$ and $8$, with <sample mean> about $4.494$ and <sample standard deviation> about $2.128$ among $2053$ records. Skill scores range from $0$ to $24$, with <sample mean> about $13.546$ and <sample standard deviation> about $5.989$ among $2051$ records. Two missing skill values account for the stated two deleted records; because device counts have no missing values, the regression complete-case sample has $2051$ observations. Estimating two <regression coefficients> leaves $2051-2=2049$ residual <statistical degrees of freedom>, matching that part of the output. The units of the two <standard deviations> differ, so comparing $5.989$ with $2.128$ does not establish that skill is more variable in a substantive sense.

For conventional finite-sample t and F calibration, a <normal linear model> assumes <independent> mean-zero normal errors of common <variance> with an appropriate predictor specification. Mean-zero errors conditional on skill and adequate sampling remain essential even when large-sample approximations relax normality. A bounded count response can have nonconstant conditional <variance>, so residual diagnostics and a scientifically suitable response model matter; count data do not become normally distributed just because the sample is large. <Poisson regression> is one possible alternative to investigate, but its own conditional mean–<variance> assumptions and the bounded device range require assessment, not automatic substitution. Complete-case selection can also bias the fitted association if it depends on the response in a relevant way. **The only safe summary is conditional: the printed slope is positive, but the numerical effect, its <standard error>, t/F evidence and explanatory fit require a corrected, coherent output before a single definitive regression claim is made.**
