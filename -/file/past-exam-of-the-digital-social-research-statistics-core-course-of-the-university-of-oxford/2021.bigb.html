The cover identifies 9 December 2021; the reported survey was collected in October 2020. The five-category agreement item and numerical willingness scale are treated as separate reported measurements.

== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $\mu_0,\mu_1$ be the <population means> of the numerical <vaccination intention> score in the two recorded groups. The <two-sided hypothesis test> compares
$$
H_0:\mu_0-\mu_1=0,\qquad H_1:\mu_0-\mu_1\ne0.
$$
The <sample means> are $13.48553$ and $13.46565$, giving a <difference of sample means> of approximately **0.01988 score points**, in the order group zero minus group one. The reported group counts are 1,866 and 262; seven observations have a missing group indicator.

<Welch's t-test> divides that estimated difference by its estimated <standard error>. Its reported <test statistic> is $t=0.055032$, with approximately $344.43$ <statistical degrees of freedom>. This is an unequal-variance comparison; fractional <statistical degrees of freedom> are appropriate for the Welch approximation. The two groups need not have equal <sample sizes> or equal population <variances>.<Two-Sample t-Test for Equal Means by National Institute of Standards and Technology>[Section 1.3.5.3, unpaired test statistic, unequal-variance standard error, and Welch–Satterthwaite degrees of freedom]{ref}

The reported <p-value> is $0.9561$. Under the specified equal-mean <null hypothesis> and sampling model, a <p-value> measures the probability of a result at least as extreme as the observed one. It is not a posterior probability that the <null hypothesis> is true. **There is no rejection at a prespecified 5% significance level.** The observed <difference of sample means> is tiny relative to its estimated <standard error>.

The 95% <confidence interval> is approximately
$$
\boxed{\mu_0-\mu_1:\quad[-0.69071,\ 0.73047].}
$$
It includes zero and differences in either direction. The interval's coverage refers to repeated use of the procedure under its assumptions, rather than to a probability assigned to this fixed unknown parameter or to the spread of individual scores. Nonrejection does not establish that the two <population means> are identical.

<Simple random sampling> supports the sampling interpretation, but the score is bounded and its raw distribution need not be a <normal distribution>. The large group sizes can support an approximate normal sampling distribution of the <sample means> under regularity conditions. The supplied <observational study> does not identify a causal explanation of any difference, and the numeric coding of the group labels creates no biological or ordinal interpretation.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

**Under the question's stipulated nonzero population difference, failing to reject is a <type II error>.** A <type I error> would instead reject a true equal-mean <null hypothesis>. The premise concerns the underlying difference: another study's <statistical significance>, by itself, would not prove that its conclusion is correct.

For a specified alternative and sampling design, write
$$
\beta=\mathbb P_{\rm alternative}(\text{do not reject }H_0),\qquad
1-\beta=\mathbb P_{\rm alternative}(\text{reject }H_0).
$$
The second probability is <statistical power>. Across repeated <random samples> under that same alternative and design, the long-run frequency of this kind of error is $\beta$.

**No numerical value of $\beta$ follows from this output.** It depends on the true <effect size>, group <variances>, <sample sizes>, decision rule and chosen <significance level>. In particular, it is neither $0.9561$, nor $1-0.9561$, nor automatically 5%. The reusable result that <a p-value does not determine the type II error probability> demonstrates this directly in a simple normal testing model.

A small <effect size> or an imprecise estimator can produce low <statistical power>. The present <confidence interval> describes the precision of the estimated difference; it does not supply the missing true alternative needed to calculate the <power function>. A specified 5% <significance level> concerns the <type I error> rate under the null, rather than a universal error rate whenever a result is nonsignificant.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

Each <frequency table> describes a <marginal distribution>, rather than a relationship among variables. The recorded two-category gender variable has 1,042 male and 1,093 female responses: **48.81% and 51.19%**. It is a <nominal categorical variable>. Its ordering in the table supplies no quantitative ranking; a <cumulative percentage> over those arbitrarily ordered labels has no invariant substantive meaning.

Household income is an <ordinal categorical variable> with six bands. Their shares, from the lowest band upward, are $5.48\%$, $9.93\%$, $11.48\%$, $13.02\%$, $8.34\%$ and $51.76\%$. The highest band alone contains 1,105 respondents, **slightly more than half the sample**. The bands are not a numerical income measurement with equal steps: the top band is open-ended, so an exact <sample mean> income cannot be recovered from these category counts alone.

The agreement item is also an <ordinal categorical variable>. Combining the first two categories gives
$$
\boxed{\text{agree or strongly agree}=\frac{589+529}{2135}\approx52.37\%.}
$$
Neutral responses account for $447/2135\approx20.94\%$; the two disagreement categories together account for $570/2135\approx26.70\%$. These percentages describe the measured <vaccination intention> item, rather than actual <vaccination status>.

All three tables have 2,135 valid observations and no missing values for these variables, so their percentages and <valid percentages> coincide. This is different from the seven missing group-indicator values in question 1. The income and agreement columns sum to $100.01\%$ because the displayed percentages were rounded separately. That rounding is compatible with counts summing exactly to 2,135.

The tables alone cannot show whether the gender composition differs by income, or whether <vaccination intention> differs between groups. Those are questions about a joint <contingency table> and its <conditional probabilities>, not about separate <marginal distributions>.

== 3
{scope}

=== Solution

The displayed percentages use row denominators. The relevant comparison is therefore the <conditional probability> of each response within each recorded gender group, rather than the gender composition within a response category.

For the male group, $364/1042\approx34.93\%$ strongly agree and $286/1042\approx27.45\%$ agree. For the female group, the corresponding percentages are $225/1093\approx20.59\%$ and $243/1093\approx22.23\%$. Combining the two agreement categories gives
$$
\boxed{\widehat p_M=\frac{650}{1042}\approx62.38\%,\quad
\widehat p_F=\frac{468}{1093}\approx42.82\%,\quad
\widehat p_M-\widehat p_F\approx19.56\text{ percentage points}.}
$$
The female group has more neutral responses, $24.25\%$ versus $17.47\%$, and a higher combined disagreement percentage, $32.94\%$ versus $20.15\%$. Thus the observed distributions differ in more than a single cell.

The <Pearson chi-squared test> assesses the <null hypothesis> of <independence> in the complete $2\times5$ <contingency table>. Under that null, the <expected frequency> in a cell is its row total times its column total divided by 2,135. Recalculation gives
$$
X^2=\sum_{i,j}\frac{(O_{ij}-E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}}\approx90.56953,\qquad
\nu=(2-1)(5-1)=4.
$$
The reported <p-value>, approximately $9.97\times10^{-19}$, gives very strong evidence against <independence> under the test's sampling assumptions. The minimum <expected frequency> is about $106.88$, so sparse expected cells are not a problem here. The test concerns the full five-category distribution, not just the combined agreement comparison.

<Cramér's V> summarizes the magnitude without imposing an ordering on the gender labels:
$$
V=\sqrt{\frac{90.56953}{2135\min(2-1,5-1)}}\approx0.206.
$$
The small <p-value> does not imply a nearly deterministic relationship. For example, the column-dependent <Goodman and Kruskal's lambda> is only about $0.026$: choosing the modal response separately within the two groups reduces prediction errors from 1,546 to 1,506, a relative reduction of $40/1546\approx2.59\%$.

The positive <Goodman and Kruskal's gamma> must be interpreted using the table's chosen ordering. The agreement categories run toward less willingness, and gender has no intrinsic ordinal direction. A sign attached to an arbitrary group coding cannot replace the substantive comparison of <conditional probabilities>.

The <NaN> displayed for the likelihood-ratio calculation is not a valid <p-value> and is not evidence of no association. The <Pearson chi-squared statistic> can be independently recomputed from the positive cell counts. None of these calculations identifies a <causal effect>; the table has not controlled for income or other possible <confounders>.

In the lowest-income band, combined agreement differs by only about $4.39$ <percentage points>. The full distributions still differ descriptively: strong disagreement is $13.64\%$ in the male group and $27.40\%$ in the female group. The omnibus <Pearson chi-squared test> does not reject <independence> at 5%, but that decision does not prove equal distributions.

In the 10k–20k band, combined agreement is almost the same. Nevertheless, strong agreement is higher in the female group, $18.66\%$ versus $11.54\%$, while the less intense agreement category is higher in the male group, $24.36\%$ versus $16.42\%$. Collapsing the categories hides that distinction. The full-distribution <p-value> is approximately $0.2435$.

In the 20k–30k band, combined agreement is higher in the male group by about $12.96$ <percentage points>. Strong agreement alone is $27.17\%$ versus $18.30\%$. The observed difference is therefore larger than in the two lowest bands, despite its full-table <p-value> remaining above 5%.

In the 30k–40k band, combined agreement differs by about $17.40$ <percentage points>, with fewer male neutral responses, $15.69\%$ versus $26.14\%$. The full-table <p-value> of $0.03851$ is nominally significant at 5%. In the 40k–50k band, the combined agreement gap is a similar $16.18$ <percentage points>, but the full-table <p-value> is $0.1534$. These labels are not a direct comparison between the two effects: <significant and nonsignificant results need not differ significantly>.

In the highest-income band, strong agreement is $41.84\%$ in the male group and $22.15\%$ in the female group. Combined agreement differs by about $20.95$ <percentage points>. This band has both the largest <sample size> and the largest <Cramér's V> of the six tables, with a very small <p-value>. Its particularly strong evidence reflects both precision and an observed difference in the distribution.

All six minimum <expected frequencies> exceed five: approximately $7.15$, $9.57$, $12.02$, $15.04$, $7.93$ and $36.03$. Thus sparse expected counts do not provide a reason to reject the usual <chi-squared distribution> approximation here. If the six <hypothesis tests> form one exploratory family, a <Bonferroni correction> would use $0.05/6\approx0.00833$ for each test; only the highest-income result crosses that threshold. This qualifies the unadjusted 30k–40k result without turning its observed contrast into zero.

In the <elaboration model>, the larger agreement gaps in the middle and upper bands provide a descriptive pattern of specification: the conditional contrast varies with income. This is not a <Simpson's paradox> reversal, because the combined agreement difference has the same sign in every band. Nor is the larger high-income <effect size> established simply by comparing its <p-value> with the other <p-values>. A formal claim of <effect modification> requires a stated scale and an appropriate comparison of the effects.

The pooled comparison also mixes different income compositions. The highest-income band contains $631/1042\approx60.56\%$ of the male group but $474/1093\approx43.37\%$ of the female group. Average each group's six agreement proportions over the same pooled income weights using <standardization with common weights>. This gives
$$
\boxed{\widehat p_M^{(w)}\approx60.21\%,\qquad
\widehat p_F^{(w)}\approx43.94\%,\qquad
\widehat p_M^{(w)}-\widehat p_F^{(w)}\approx16.27\text{ percentage points}.}
$$
The crude difference was $19.56$ <percentage points>, so common weighting attenuates it by about $3.30$ <percentage points> but leaves a substantial descriptive contrast. With average-group rather than pooled-person weights, the <symmetric decomposition of a stratified mean difference> instead gives an exact split into approximately $16.29$ <percentage points> from conditional contrasts and $3.27$ <percentage points> from composition. These are different explicitly defined descriptive summaries, not estimates of a causally mediated share.

An antecedent income variable could be considered in a <confounding> explanation, whereas an income variable on an assumed causal pathway would enter an interpretation through <causal mediation>. The conditional tables do not distinguish these causal structures. They also do not identify whether any adjustment introduces <collider bias>. **The supported conclusion is income-dependent descriptive association plus a measurable composition difference, with causal interpretation left conditional on additional assumptions.**

== 5
{scope}

=== Solution

The fitted <simple linear regression> for the numerical <vaccination intention> scale is
$$
\widehat{\mathbb E}[Y\mid A=a]=12.645291+0.016489a.
$$
Here age is measured in years. **The fitted mean increases by about 0.01649 score points per year, or 0.16489 points per decade.** The <regression coefficient> is an association across respondents, not an estimate of how an individual would change on ageing or of a <causal effect> of age.

The <standard error> of the age <regression coefficient> is $0.006806$. Dividing gives $t\approx2.423$, with 2,133 residual <statistical degrees of freedom>, corresponding to $2135-2$ fitted residual degrees. The reported two-sided <p-value> is $0.0155$, so the zero-slope <null hypothesis> is rejected at a prespecified 5% level, but not at 1%. An approximate 95% <confidence interval> is
$$
\boxed{0.016489\pm t_{2133,0.975}(0.006806)
\approx[0.003142,\ 0.029836]\text{ score points per year}.}
$$
Its positive endpoints are consistent with the test decision; they do not make the slope large.

The <coefficient of determination> is $R^2=0.002744$, so the fitted age trend accounts for only **0.2744% of the observed score variation**, compared with an intercept-only prediction. Its adjusted value is $0.002276$. In an intercept-containing <simple linear regression>, the positive slope implies a positive <Pearson correlation coefficient> $r=\sqrt{R^2}\approx0.05238$. The reported overall $F$ statistic, $5.869$ on one and 2,133 degrees of freedom, agrees with the squared slope <test statistic> up to printed rounding.

The residual <standard deviation> estimate is $5.618$ score points, much larger than the fitted decade contrast. At age 50 the fitted value is about $13.4697$, and at 60 it is about $13.6346$. Across the observed ages 19 to 91, the fitted mean changes by only $1.1872$ points. Statistical detection with 2,135 observations therefore coexists with weak individual prediction.

The intercept predicts $12.645291$ at age zero, outside the observed age range; it should not be given a substantive infant interpretation. Centring age at its observed <sample mean>, $50.174$, gives
$$
\widehat{\mathbb E}[Y\mid A=a]\approx13.47261+0.016489(a-50.174),
$$
which agrees with the reported score <sample mean> of $13.473$ after rounding. The bounded scale and upper quartile at its maximum motivate attention to a possible <ceiling effect>; they do not establish <homoskedasticity>, a normal error distribution or a satisfactory linear conditional mean. Five residual quantiles cannot replace a <residual plot> or checks of the sampling assumptions.

There is also a source consistency problem. The output reports that all 2,135 records have age and score present, that the attained minimum score is one, and that ages lie between 19 and 91. The positive fitted slope therefore places every fitted value between $12.958582$ and $14.145790$. By <residual extrema when response extrema are attained>, the minimum <regression residual> must satisfy
$$
-13.145790\le e_{\min}\le-11.958582.
$$
At the observation with score one, its residual is already at most $1-12.958582=-11.958582$. The printed minimum residual, $-10.014$, lies outside this necessary interval. **Those summaries cannot all describe the same complete sample and fitted model.** The discrepancy is too large to be explained by the displayed rounding. It is present in the original PDF; the individual records would be needed to establish which printed item is wrong.
