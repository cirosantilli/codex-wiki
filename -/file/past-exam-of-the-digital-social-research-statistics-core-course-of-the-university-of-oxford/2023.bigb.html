== 1
{scope}

=== Solution

A <null hypothesis> specifies the population comparison to be tested, for example equality of the population <means> for women and their complement. The <alternative hypothesis> for a <two-sided test> is a nonzero difference. A <Student's t-test> measures an estimated difference in units of its estimated <standard error>:
$$
t=\frac{\widehat\Delta-0}{\widehat{\operatorname{SE}}(\widehat\Delta)}.
$$
On the reported calibration, the <sampling distribution> under the <null hypothesis> is <Student's t-distribution> with $1225$ <statistical degrees of freedom>. Consequently the <two-sided test> has <p-value>
$$
\boxed{p=\Pr\{|T_{1225}|\geq2.5\}\simeq0.01255.}
$$
With this many <statistical degrees of freedom>, the <Student t-distribution> is close to the <standard normal distribution>: $|t|=2.5$ exceeds the approximate $5\%$ two-sided critical value $1.96$, but falls short of the $1\%$ value $2.576$. **Using the supplied calibration, reject at the $5\%$ significance level, but not at $1\%$.** The large <statistical degrees of freedom> justify this comparison; a value $2.5$ need not be significant for an arbitrary very small number of <statistical degrees of freedom>.

The <p-value> is the <probability>, under the specified <null hypothesis> and sampling assumptions, of a <test statistic> at least as discrepant as the observed one. It is not the <probability> that the <null hypothesis> is true, nor the <probability> that the observed difference happened “by chance”. A <significance level> of $5\%$ controls the long-run <type I error> rate of the testing procedure when its assumptions hold. Failing to reject at $1\%$ does not prove equality. <Statistical significance> also does not measure the substantive size or importance of a difference.

The reported <sample means> place the women's <mean> $1.4$ scale units above the overall <mean>. The negative <test statistic> can reflect the order of subtraction; it does not reverse that observed ordering. An appropriate pre-specified directional <one-sided test> would have a tail <p-value> approximately $0.00627$ on the supplied calibration, but one cannot choose that direction after inspecting the results simply to halve a <p-value>.

There is a design issue behind the numerical calculation: the women are included in the overall sample. <Comparing a subgroup mean with its containing sample mean> requires accounting for shared observations. If $w$ is the women's sample fraction and $\overline x_O$ the <mean> for the remaining respondents, then
$$
\overline x=w\overline x_F+(1-w)\overline x_O,\qquad
\overline x_F-\overline x=(1-w)(\overline x_F-\overline x_O).
$$
These two estimates are not <independent>. An ordinary independent-two-sample <Student's t-test> of women against the entire respondent sample would therefore use an inappropriate <standard error>. The interpretation above accepts the supplied <test statistic> and its stated null calibration; a valid analysis of the underlying data should compare disjoint groups, or calculate the shared-sample <covariance> correctly. The summary alone cannot establish which calculation was used. <Simple random sampling> supports sampling inference under the stated design, but an observed gender association does not establish a <causal effect>.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

Each row of the <contingency table> is a <conditional distribution> of the safety category among respondents with that experience status. Thus the relevant comparison uses row <percentages>, rather than interpreting the larger number of respondents with some bad experiences as itself an effect on safety.

The <sample proportions> reporting feeling very safe are $39.59\%$ without bad experiences and $32.78\%$ with some bad experiences: the latter is lower by about $6.81$ <percentage points>. Feeling fairly safe rises from $28.49\%$ to $34.63\%$, a difference of about $6.14$ <percentage points>. Feeling unsafe changes only from $31.92\%$ to $32.59\%$, about $0.67$ <percentage points>. The main descriptive shift is therefore from **very safe toward fairly safe**, rather than a large increase in the unsafe category.

<Pearson's chi-squared test> tests the <null hypothesis> that experience status and safety category are <independent>. If that were true, the expected cell count would be its row total times column total divided by $n$. The <Pearson chi-squared statistic> sums squared observed-minus-expected count differences divided by their expected counts. Here
$$
\boxed{X^2=10.84662,\qquad \mathrm{df}=(2-1)(3-1)=2,\qquad p=0.004412523.}
$$
The expected counts are comfortably large, with minimum approximately $234.7$, so the ordinary large-sample <chi-squared distribution> approximation is appropriate under the sampling assumptions. There is evidence against <independence> even at the $1\%$ <significance level>. The reported likelihood-ratio result gives the same conclusion.

Magnitude is a separate question. <Cramér's V> gives
$$
V=\sqrt{\frac{10.84662}{1807}}\simeq0.07748,
$$
which describes a weak <statistical association>. It does not <mean> that $7.7\%$ of safety is explained. Nor does the <p-value> measure the size of that association.

For ordinal direction, order the experience categories as none, some, and the safety categories from very safe toward unsafe. <Goodman and Kruskal's gamma> compares <concordant pairs> with <discordant pairs> after excluding tied pairs:
$$
\gamma=\frac{282244-242582}{282244+242582}\simeq0.07557.
$$
Its small positive sign then corresponds to a weak tendency toward less safety among respondents with bad experiences. The sign would reverse if exactly one category ordering were reversed. Because this <contingency table> has many ties, the response-direction <Somers' D>, which retains response ties among pairs with different predictor values, is smaller:
$$
D_{Y\mid X}=\frac{39662}{524826+261384}\simeq0.05045.
$$
These coefficients describe ordered association; the omnibus <Pearson's chi-squared test> tests any departure from <independence> and does not supply a separate <p-value> for gamma.

The column-dependent <Goodman and Kruskal's lambda> makes the weak predictive improvement tangible. Predicting the overall most frequent safety category makes $1165$ errors in this table. Using a separate most frequent category within each experience row makes $1145$, giving $\lambda=20/1165\simeq0.0172$, only about a $1.7\%$ reduction in those classification errors.

**The sample supports a statistically detectable but weak association, chiefly a reduced <sample proportion> feeling very safe.** This <observational study> does not establish that bad experiences cause the change: reverse direction, reporting differences or <confounding> could also contribute. The numerical <p-value> also presumes the sampling and complete-case assumptions appropriate to the observations used.

The <p-values> test <independence> within the respective strata; the magnitudes and directions come from the conditional <percentages> and association measures. Differences in the table are calculated from the counts before rounding.

Among **single** respondents, the very-safe <sample proportion> changes from $33.95\%$ to $29.52\%$, while the unsafe <sample proportion> changes from $36.28\%$ to $38.10\%$. The direction resembles the weak pooled pattern, but $p=0.6114$ supplies little evidence against <independence> in this stratum. This is not evidence that the population association is exactly zero.

Among **married** respondents, the very-safe <sample proportion> changes from $41.18\%$ to $32.75\%$ and the fairly-safe <sample proportion> rises from $27.68\%$ to $35.39\%$. Unsafe remains almost unchanged, $31.14\%$ versus $31.87\%$. Thus the within-stratum association again chiefly shifts very safe toward fairly safe. Its $p=0.0257$ is below the $5\%$ <significance level>, while $V=0.092$ still indicates a small association.

For respondents **living with a partner**, very safe changes from $41.46\%$ to $28.32\%$, fairly safe from $30.08\%$ to $40.71\%$, and unsafe from $28.46\%$ to $30.97\%$. The very-safe difference is larger than in the married stratum. The printed <p-value> $0.04885$ would be just below the conventional $5\%$ <significance level>. However, this particular output is internally inconsistent: recalculating <Pearson's chi-squared test> from its six displayed counts gives $X^2=4.910389$, $p=0.08585$, and $V=0.14425$. The reported $V=0.144$ agrees with the counts, whereas the printed $X^2=6.900389$ would itself imply $p=0.03174$ for two <statistical degrees of freedom>, rather than the printed $0.04885$. **Using the displayed counts, this stratum does not reject independence at $5\%$.** The source PDF itself contains these incompatible figures; this is not resolved by rounding.

Among **divorced** respondents, very safe changes from $41.18\%$ to $37.17\%$, but unsafe also decreases, from $36.76\%$ to $30.97\%$, as fairly safe increases from $22.06\%$ to $31.86\%$. This is a redistribution toward the middle category, not a simple shift toward greater unsafety. The near-zero gamma, $-0.013$, reflects the lack of a consistent ordinal direction, while $V=0.106$ can still register distributional differences without a direction. The <p-value> $0.3583$ does not reject <independence>.

Among **widowed** respondents, very safe changes from $51.43\%$ to $42.47\%$, and unsafe from $14.29\%$ to $27.40\%$. The ordinal association is positive on the chosen ordering, with gamma $0.220$. Its lack of conventional <statistical significance>, $p=0.3158$, does not imply a smaller estimated association than in the married group: the widowed stratum has only $108$ complete observations, versus $857$ in the married stratum, and therefore less precision. All five tables have sufficiently large expected counts for the reported approximations; even the smallest expected count is about $8.10$.

The plot uses large-sample <Wald confidence intervals> for the <difference of independent sample proportions> within each stratum. They concern the individual category contrasts, whereas the tabulated <Pearson's chi-squared tests> compare the full three-category distributions. The intervals are unadjusted for <multiple hypothesis testing>; the pooled row reuses the stratum observations, so it is not a sixth independent sample.

In the <elaboration model>, the persistence of a generally weak positive ordinal pattern within four strata is closer to **partial replication** than to a complete explanation of the pooled association by marital status. The differences in estimated patterns suggest **possible specification**: the divorced group's shift toward the middle category differs from the widowed group's shift toward unsafe. They do not by themselves establish population <effect modification>. In particular, <significant and nonsignificant results need not differ significantly>; compare the effects using an <interaction contrast> or a fitted model with the appropriate <interaction terms>, rather than comparing their separate <p-values>.<The Difference Between Significant and Not Significant is not Itself Statistically Significant by Andrew Gelman and Hal Stern>[Printed p. 328, Introduction: comparing significance labels does not test the difference between estimates]{ref}

One additional omnibus check can be derived from the supplied tables without assuming a common effect. Recalculating the five statistics from the counts, including the corrected living-with-partner statistic, the <joint conditional independence test for stratified contingency tables> gives
$$
\sum_{k=1}^5 X_k^2=17.5776085,\qquad \mathrm{df}=5(2-1)(3-1)=10,\qquad p\simeq0.06252.
$$
Under independent-stratum sampling this tests <independence> within every stratum jointly; it does not test whether the association is homogeneous across strata. It does not reject at $5\%$, but this alone does not establish that stratification removed the population association: its ten-degree-of-freedom alternative is less focused than the pooled two-degree-of-freedom test, and nonrejection is not proof of equality.

The <elaboration model> also distinguishes an antecedent <confounder> from an intervening <causal mediator>. These cross-sectional tables do not establish the relevant time ordering or identify either role for marital status. Five separate stratum tests further require caution about <multiple hypothesis testing>: for example a <Bonferroni correction> at familywise $5\%$ would use $0.01$ per test, below all five individual <p-values>. That caution changes the interpretation of selected significance labels, not the observed conditional <percentages>.

**The estimated patterns vary across strata, but the tables neither establish a population interaction nor prove that marital status explains away the association.**

== 4
{scope}

=== Solution

The <ordinary least squares> fit estimates the conditional <mean> of the continuous safety response as a <linear function> of the income coding:
$$
\boxed{\widehat{\mathrm{feelsafe}}=6.5933+0.2369\,\mathrm{income}.}
$$
The <regression coefficient> for income is positive. Since larger response values <mean> greater safety, one additional coded income unit corresponds to an estimated increase of $0.2369$ safety-scale units in the fitted <mean>. Following the question's stated units, a unit is a £10,000 income step. The observed coding also uses income bands with open end categories, so this is a linear trend per coded band step; it is not evidence of a known constant effect for every extra pound, especially within the open top band. The output's abbreviated variable label does not override the stated units.

The <intercept> is the fitted <mean> at income code zero. It is estimated as $6.5933$, but zero is outside the observed income codes, so that value is an extrapolated model baseline, not an observed estimate for a particular income category. Its small <p-value> tests a zero intercept; it is not the main test of the income association.

For the income coefficient, the <standard error> is $0.0604$. The reported <test statistic> and <two-sided test> give
$$
t\simeq3.922,\qquad p=9.13\times10^{-5},
$$
providing strong evidence against the <null hypothesis> of zero linear slope under the stated model assumptions. A $95\%$ <Student t confidence interval>, using $1727$ residual <statistical degrees of freedom>, is
$$
\boxed{0.2369\pm t_{1727,0.975}(0.0604)\simeq[0.1184,\,0.3554].}
$$
This is an interval for the population linear coefficient under the model, not an interval containing $95\%$ of individual safety scores. The large-sample estimate is precise enough to detect a positive association, but that does not establish substantial individual predictive power.

The <coefficient of determination> is $R^2=0.008828$: the fitted line accounts for about $0.883\%$ of the in-sample variation about the response <sample mean>. The <adjusted coefficient of determination> is about $0.825\%$. Thus **income alone explains very little of the observed variation**, despite the small slope <p-value>. The <residual standard error> of $3.997$ response units describes substantial scatter about the fitted line. This is an estimated residual <standard deviation>, rather than the <standard error> of the slope or a complete individual prediction interval.

The overall <F-test> has $F=15.38$ on $1,1727$ <statistical degrees of freedom> and $p=9.129\times10^{-5}$. With one predictor and an <intercept>, it tests the same zero-slope hypothesis as the coefficient <Student's t-test>; $F=t^2$, up to rounding. It is not an additional independent confirmation. In this <simple linear regression>, the <Pearson correlation coefficient> has the slope's sign and magnitude $\sqrt{R^2}\simeq0.0940$, another description of the weak linear association.

The regression uses $1729$ complete observations and estimates two coefficients, giving $1729-2=1727$ residual <statistical degrees of freedom>. The deletion of $88$ observations is <complete-case analysis>; extending its conclusions to the full target population requires suitable assumptions about the <missing-data mechanism>. Classical reported <standard errors> also rely on an adequate linear conditional <mean>, independent errors and <homoscedasticity>; a <normal linear model> gives the finite-sample <Student t-distribution> and <F-test> calibration, while large-sample approximations can justify similar tests under weaker distributional assumptions. The printed summary alone does not establish these assumptions. <Regression diagnostics> and, where appropriate, <heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors> address different parts of that assessment.

Finally, this unadjusted <observational study> estimates an association. Other <covariates>, such as age or experience status, may be related to both income and reported safety. Without additional design or <causal identification> assumptions, the positive fitted slope does not establish that increasing income would cause an increase in safety.
