= Perturbative quantum field theory

= Hermitian matrix model
{c}
{parent=Perturbative quantum field theory}
{title2=$Z=\int dM\,e^{-S(M)}$}

A <Hermitian matrix model> replaces a spacetime field by a finite-dimensional <Hermitian matrix>. Its normalized integral supplies correlation functions and a zero-dimensional <perturbative quantum field theory> expansion. For an action built from <traces> of matrix powers, <unitary matrix> conjugation is a symmetry and the <eigenvalues> describe the invariant degrees of freedom. A quartic real integral needs a nonnegative highest-order coupling for convergence; a formal power series can be studied independently of this convergence issue.

= Matrix diagonalization ghost determinant
{parent=Hermitian matrix model}
{title2=$\det{}'\operatorname{ad}_{\operatorname{diag}\lambda}\propto\prod_{i<j}(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)^2$}

Imposing vanishing off-diagonal entries with auxiliary integrations and an off-diagonal <Grassmann Gaussian integral> gives a determinant of the commutator map on the off-diagonal matrix units. Its eigenvalues are $\lambda_i-\lambda_j$, $i\ne j$. Up to a constant sign, their product is the squared <Vandermonde determinant>. It contributes $-2\sum_{i<j}\log|\lambda_i-\lambda_j|$ to the eigenvalue action, encoding repulsion between distinct <eigenvalues>. Diagonal zero modes and constant normalization factors must be treated separately.

= Quartic Hermitian matrix two-point function
{parent=Hermitian matrix model}
{title2=$\langle M^i{}_jM^k{}_l\rangle_c$}

For $S=a\operatorname{tr}(M^2)/2+b\operatorname{tr}(M^4)/4$ with $a>0$, the <Wick theorem> gives
$$
\langle M^i{}_jM^k{}_l\rangle_c
=\frac1a\delta^i{}_l\delta^k{}_j-\frac b{a^3}\left(2N\delta^i{}_l\delta^k{}_j+\delta^i{}_j\delta^k{}_l\right)+O(b^2).
$$
There are eight adjacent external-attachment contractions with one free matrix-index sum and four opposite attachments with no free sum. The second tensor structure is needed at finite $N$; omitting it is an additional large-$N$ approximation, not merely restricting to <connected Feynman diagrams>.

= Radiative correction
{parent=Perturbative quantum field theory}

A radiative correction is a quantum correction beyond the classical or tree approximation, represented in <perturbative quantum field theory> by <Feynman diagrams> of positive <loop order>. Such corrections can alter a <scalar potential> and its derivatives; symmetries and <renormalization> constrain which changes are allowed.

= Perturbation series
{parent=Perturbative quantum field theory}

A perturbation series expands a correlation function or scattering amplitude in powers of interaction couplings. In quantum field theory it is commonly asymptotic because diagram counts grow factorially and nonperturbative effects are invisible to every finite order.

= Weak coupling
{parent=Perturbation series}

<Weak coupling> means a suitably dimensionless interaction strength is small. In <BCS theory>, $\nu_sV\ll1$ gives a gap exponentially small compared with the pairing-shell energy cutoff; the expansion parameter is not the dimensional quantity $V$ alone.

= Dyson series
{c}
{parent=Perturbative quantum field theory}
{wiki}

The Dyson series expands the interaction-picture time-evolution operator as
$$
S=T\exp\!\left(-i\int d^4x\,\mathcal H_{\mathrm{int}}(x)\right)
=\sum_{n\geq0}\frac{(-i)^n}{n!}\int d^4x_1\cdots d^4x_n\,T\!\left\{\prod_{j=1}^n\mathcal H_{\mathrm{int}}(x_j)\right\}.
$$

= Differentiation of the Dyson time-ordered exponential
{parent=Dyson series}
{title2=$i\partial_tU=H_I(t)U$}

For bounded norm-continuous <Hamiltonian operators> on $[t_0,t]$, the <Dyson series> has an $n$-th term bounded by $M^n|t-t_0|^n/n!$, so it converges in operator norm. In its nested-integral form, differentiating the outer endpoint puts $H_I(t)$ on the left of the $(n-1)$-fold term. Summing yields $\partial_tU=-iH_I(t)U$ and $U(t_0,t_0)=I$. If $H_I$ is Hermitian, differentiating $U^\dagger U$ proves unitarity. For unbounded field operators the same algebra is a formal perturbative identity unless domain and convergence assumptions or a regulator justify the steps. Evolution backward uses inverse, anti-time-ordered evolution.

= Wick theorem
{c}
{synonym}

Wick theorem expresses a time-ordered product of free fields as its normal-ordered product plus the sum over every possible contraction. It turns the <Dyson series> into <Feynman diagrams>.

= Two-field Wick contraction identity
{parent=Wick's theorem}
{title2=$T\phi(x)\phi(y)=:\phi(x)\phi(y):+D_F(x-y)$}

Split a free scalar field into its annihilation and creation parts. For $x^0>y^0$, changing the product into <normal ordering> adds $[\phi^{(+)}(x),\phi^{(-)}(y)]$, the positive-frequency two-point function. For the opposite ordering interchange the two points. Their combination is the <Feynman propagator>, equal to the vacuum expectation of the <time-ordered product>. Iterating this identity gives the scalar <Wick theorem>.

= Two-field scalar Wick identity
{parent=Wick's theorem}
{title2=$T\{\phi(x)\phi(y)\}=:\phi(x)\phi(y):+\Delta_F(x-y)\mathbf1$}

For a free real <scalar field>, split $\phi=\phi^{(+)}+\phi^{(-)}$ into annihilation and creation parts. Moving the creation operators left gives $\phi(x)\phi(y)=:\phi(x)\phi(y):+[\phi^{(+)}(x),\phi^{(-)}(y)]$. The <commutator> is the <Wightman function>. Choosing either time order gives the displayed identity with the <Feynman propagator>. It is an identity of <operator-valued distributions>; coincident-point products need a suitable prescription.

= Inductive proof of the scalar Wick theorem
{parent=Wick's theorem}

Split each free <real scalar field> as $\phi=\phi^{(-)}+\phi^{(+)}$, into creation and annihilation parts. For chronologically ordered arguments, the first field has the latest time. Multiplying it into a <normal-ordered product> of the remaining fields gives
$$
\phi_1:\!\prod_{r\in I}\phi_r\!:
=:\!\phi_1\prod_{r\in I}\phi_r\!:
+\sum_{j\in I}[\phi_1^{(+)},\phi_j^{(-)}]:\!\prod_{r\in I\setminus\{j\}}\phi_r\!:.
$$
This follows by commuting the annihilation part through each creation part; each <commutator> is a scalar, and all creation parts commute among themselves, as do all annihilation parts. The scalar commutator is the <Feynman propagator> for the chronological pair. Induction on the number of fields therefore lists every partial pairing exactly once: either field 1 remains unpaired or it pairs with one $j$. This proves the <Wick theorem> for free bosonic fields, including the empty pairing. Vacuum expectation retains only complete pairings. The identities are understood for regulated or smeared <operator-valued distributions>.

= Free scalar four-point function
{parent=Wick's theorem}
{title2=$\langle T\phi_1\phi_2\phi_3\phi_4\rangle$}

For a centered free <real scalar field>, its vacuum <time-ordered product> has expectation $\langle T\phi_1\phi_2\phi_3\phi_4\rangle=G_{12}G_{34}+G_{13}G_{24}+G_{14}G_{23}$, where $G_{ij}$ is the <Feynman propagator> between insertions $i,j$. These are the three complete pairings in the <Wick theorem>. All remaining <normal-ordered products> have zero vacuum expectation. No fermionic interchange signs occur for a bosonic field.

= Vanishing odd correlators of a free Gaussian field
{title2=$\langle\widehat f_1\cdots\widehat f_{2n+1}\rangle=0$}
{parent=Wick's theorem}

A centered free field in a Gaussian vacuum has only pair contractions, so every odd correlator vanishes by <Wick theorem>. Equivalently, number parity reverses the field while preserving the vacuum. This statement applies to smeared or regulated fields; it does not assert that unregulated coincident products exist. Interactions or a nonzero field mean can invalidate it.

= Time ordering
{parent=Wick's theorem}
{wiki}

Time ordering places operators with later time arguments to the left. Interchanging fermionic operators also contributes the sign of the required permutation.

= Time-ordered exponential
{parent=Time ordering}
{title2=$U(t,t_0)=\mathcal T\exp[-i\int_{t_0}^t H(s)ds/\hbar]$}

The time-ordered exponential solves $i\hbar\partial_tU=H(t)U$ with initial condition $U(t_0,t_0)=I$. Its expansion is the <Dyson series>. For bounded continuous <Hamiltonian operators> the series converges, as in <differentiation of the Dyson time-ordered exponential>. If the generators commute at all times, time ordering is unnecessary and an ordinary operator exponential suffices.

= Magnus expansion
{c}
{parent=Time-ordered exponential}
{title2=$U(T)=\exp(\Omega_1+\Omega_2+\cdots)$}
{wiki}

For $\dot U=A(t)U$ and $U(0)=I$, the Magnus expansion writes the logarithm of the propagator as iterated integrals of <commutators>. Its first terms are $\Omega_1=\int_0^TA(t)dt$ and $\Omega_2=\tfrac12\int_0^Tdt\int_0^tds\,[A(t),A(s)]$. For a <quantum Hamiltonian>, $A=-iH/\hbar$, so a noncommuting time-dependent control has corrections beyond the exponential of its integrated <quantum Hamiltonian>. This formula is a local convergent expansion under suitable smallness conditions, or a formal expansion; it must not be assumed globally convergent for arbitrary pulse durations.

= Constant pulse-axis condition for removing time ordering
{parent=Time-ordered exponential}
{title2=$[H(t),H(s)]=0\Longrightarrow U(T)=e^{-i\int_0^TH(t)dt/\hbar}$}

For $H(t)=\Omega_1(t)A+\Omega_2(t)B$ with fixed noncommuting <Hermitian operators>, $[H(t),H(s)]=[\Omega_1(t)\Omega_2(s)-\Omega_2(t)\Omega_1(s)][A,B]$. A constant ratio of the envelopes is sufficient to remove <time ordering>. Independently changing that ratio generally produces <commutator> corrections in the <Magnus expansion>, and a product of successive pulses need not equal the exponential of their summed areas.

= Time-ordered product
{parent=Time ordering}
{title2=$T\{\mathcal O_1\cdots\mathcal O_n\}$}

A <time-ordered product> sorts operator insertions from latest time on the left to earliest time on the right. Reordering odd <fermionic operators> contributes the sign of their permutation. For bosonic $A,B$, $T[A(t)B(t')]=\theta(t-t')A(t)B(t')+\theta(t'-t)B(t')A(t)$. Vacuum expectations of free <time-ordered products> are evaluated by the <Wick theorem>.

= Normal-ordered
{synonym}

Normal ordering places creation operators to the left of annihilation operators. For free fields it removes vacuum self-contractions from a composite operator.

= Normal and Weyl symbols of a harmonic oscillator
{parent=Normal ordering}

For the <quantum harmonic oscillator> $H=\hbar\omega(a^\dagger a+1/2)$, its normal symbol is $H_N=\hbar\omega(\bar\psi\psi+1/2)$. The <Weyl ordering> symbol of $a^\dagger a$ is $\bar\psi\psi-1/2$, so $H_W=\hbar\omega\bar\psi\psi=p^2/(2m)+m\omega^2q^2/2$. Switching from adjacent-label <coherent-state time slicing> to a midpoint <phase-space path integral> must retain this ordering correction. Both correctly normalized constructions give the <thermal partition function of a quantum harmonic oscillator> $Z=[2\sinh(\beta\hbar\omega/2)]^{-1}$; retaining the normal-symbol constant after switching prescriptions would count the zero-point shift twice.

= Normal-ordered product
{parent=Normal ordering}
{title2=$:\mathcal O_1\cdots\mathcal O_n:$}

A <normal-ordered product> of free-field oscillator operators places all <creation operators> to the left of all <annihilation operators>, including the <fermionic sign> required to reorder fermions. Its vacuum expectation vanishes if any nontrivial oscillator factors remain. The <Wick theorem> expresses a free-field <time-ordered product> through these products and all <Wick contractions>.

= Wick contraction
{c}
{parent=Wick's theorem}

A Wick contraction replaces a selected pair of free fields by their two-point function. Summing pairings computes Gaussian correlation functions and supplies the propagator lines and combinatorial factors of a <Feynman diagram>.

= Fermionic sign
{parent=Perturbative quantum field theory}

A fermionic sign is a factor $-1$ produced whenever two Grassmann-odd fields or fermionic creation and annihilation operators are interchanged. Relative signs between diagrams must be computed after putting every external fermion operator into one common order.

= Antifermion sign of a normal-ordered bilinear
{parent=Fermionic sign}
{title2=$:\bar\psi\Gamma\psi:\supset-d^\dagger d\,\bar v\Gamma v$}

In a <Dirac field> mode expansion, the antiparticle contribution to $\bar\psi\Gamma\psi$ first appears with operator order $dd^\dagger$. <Normal ordering> changes it to $-d^\dagger d$ using the fermionic anticommutation relations. Thus its scattering coefficient is $-\bar v_{\rm in}\Gamma v_{\rm out}$. Pair-annihilation and creation terms have the operator orders $db$ and $b^\dagger d^\dagger$. Keeping these orders and a fixed external-state convention determines interference signs, especially between exchange and annihilation graphs in a <Yukawa interaction>.

= Wick rotation
{c}
{parent=Perturbative quantum field theory}
{wiki}

A Wick rotation deforms a time or energy contour from real to imaginary values, converting suitable Minkowski integrals into Euclidean integrals while respecting pole prescriptions.

= Euclidean action
{c}
{parent=Wick rotation}
{title2=$S_E$}

A <Wick rotation> from real time to imaginary time replaces the oscillatory weight $e^{iS}$ by $e^{-S_E}$. For a real scalar with positive kinetic normalization, $S_E=\int[\tfrac12(\partial\phi)^2+V(\phi)]\,d^dx$. Positivity of its kinetic term does not ensure convergence if the potential is unbounded below. Fermionic integrals instead use independent <Grassmann fields> and need not define a positive measure.

= Generating functional
{title2=$Z[J]$}
{parent=Perturbative quantum field theory}
{wiki}

A generating functional is a <path integral> with a source $J$ coupled to a field. Its <functional derivatives> with respect to $J$ generate time-ordered correlation functions.

= Polynomial interactions as source differential operators
{parent=Generating functional}
{title2=$Z[J]=\exp\!\left(i\int\mathcal L_I\!\left[\tfrac1i\tfrac{\delta}{\delta J}\right]\right)Z_0[J]$}

In a finite-dimensional integral, $u_i e^{b\cdot u}=\partial_{b_i}e^{b\cdot u}$. Expand $e^{V(u)}$ as a formal perturbation series to obtain $\int du\,e^{-u^TAu/2+b\cdot u+V(u)}=e^{V(\partial_b)}I_0(b)$. With a finite-mode regulator, a Minkowski source insertion satisfies $\phi(x)e^{i\int J\phi}=(1/i)\delta e^{i\int J\phi}/\delta J(x)$; this gives the displayed interacting <generating functional>. The substitution is a perturbative differential-operator identity, not an assertion that the interaction exponential defines a convergent series. Divide the resulting functional by $Z[0]$ for normalized <correlation functions>.

= Source
{synonym}

An external <source> is a prescribed field coupled to a dynamical field or operator in the action, for example $S_J=S_0+\int J_i(x)\phi_i(x)\,d^dx$. Functional derivatives of the <generating functional> with respect to $J$ insert the corresponding fields into correlation functions. The source is varied to generate these insertions, rather than integrated over as a dynamical degree of freedom. Transforming it in the appropriate representation maintains an invariant source coupling when deriving a <Ward identity>.

= Source differentiation inserts time-ordered field operators
{parent=Generating functional}
{title2=$G_r=\left.i^{-r}\delta^r Z/\delta J(x_1)\cdots\delta J(x_r)\right|_{J=0}$}

A <functional derivative> of the source term in a Minkowski <scalar field path integral> inserts $i\phi(x)$. Time-slicing identifies the resulting product with the <time-ordered product> of field operators. Dividing by the zero-source vacuum functional removes disconnected vacuum diagrams. The normalized formula has one factor $1/i$ for every insertion.

= Normalized vacuum generating functional
{parent=Generating functional}
{title2=$\widehat Z[J]=Z[J]/Z[0]$}

For a vacuum <generating functional> $Z[J]$, normalization gives $\widehat Z[J]=Z[J]/Z[0]$ and $\widehat Z[0]=1$. <Functional derivatives> of $\widehat Z$ give normalized <vacuum expectation values>. The <linked-cluster theorem> shows that this ratio cancels <vacuum Feynman diagrams>, while $-i\log\widehat Z[J]$ gives connected correlators. Components attached to external insertions are not removed merely by the normalization.

= Uniform-source vacuum functional and spacetime volume
{parent=Normalized vacuum generating functional}
{title2=$F=\mathcal V_d\,\mu^d f(\lambda,m^2/\mu^2,h/\mu^3)$}

A translation-invariant connected <generating functional> with a constant <external source> is extensive in spacetime volume $\mathcal V_d$. Its total logarithm is dimensionless, while its density has <mass dimension> $d$. Consequently its dimensionless representation at fixed physical volume must retain $\mu^d\mathcal V_d$. For a free massive scalar with source coupling $\mu^{-\epsilon/2}h\phi$, <completing the square> gives $F=\mathcal V_d\mu^{-\epsilon}h^2/(2m^2)$. This already disproves a literal rewriting of the full uniform-source logarithm using only $\lambda,m^2/\mu^2,h/\mu^3$ at fixed volume. In infinite volume the total functional is generally infinite; use the density, a source of compact support, or an explicit volume regulator. After writing the density as $\mu^d f$, its <renormalization group> equation has the engineering term $+df$. Ultraviolet subtraction alone does not establish infrared finiteness for a massless constant source.

= Vacuum source functional of a quantum oscillator
{parent=Normalized vacuum generating functional}
{title2=$Z[J]/Z[0]=\exp(-iJGJ/2)$}

<Completing the square> in $S[q]=\tfrac12qKq+Jq$ with $K=-\partial_t^2-m^2+i\epsilon$ gives the displayed source factor, where $G=K^{-1}$. In the frequency convention $e^{i\omega t}$, $\widetilde G=(\omega^2-m^2+i\epsilon)^{-1}$ and $G(t)=-ie^{-im|t|}/(2m)$ for $m>0$. The physical time-ordered oscillator <quantum field theory propagator> is $iG$. Sources are first taken as <test functions> so the pairings are defined.

= Connected generating functional
{title2=$W[J]=-i\log Z[J]$}
{parent=Generating functional}

The logarithm of a <generating functional> generates connected correlation functions because its cumulant expansion removes products of disconnected vacuum components.

= Centered four-point cumulant decomposition
{parent=Connected generating functional}
{title2=$G_4=C_4+\sum C_2C_2$}

For a normalized Minkowski source functional $Z=e^{iW}$ with vanishing <one-point function>, $C_n=i^{1-n}\delta^nW/\delta J^n$ and
$$
G_{1234}=C_{1234}+C_{12}C_{34}+C_{13}C_{24}+C_{14}C_{23}.
$$
Differentiating $e^{iW}$ assigns the four labels to connected blocks. Blocks of size one vanish, leaving one block of four or one of the three pair partitions. Thus $G_4=iW_4-\sum W_2W_2$ in this source convention.

= Source renormalization of a scalar generating functional
{parent=Connected generating functional}
{title2=$h_0=\mu^{-\epsilon/2}Z^{-1/2}h$}

For $d=4-\epsilon$, write the bare field as $\phi_0=Z^{1/2}\phi$ and the renormalized source coupling as $\mu^{-\epsilon/2}h\phi$. In bare variables this is $h_0\phi_0$, with the displayed source. At fixed bare parameters, the source running is $\mu\,d\log h/d\mu=\epsilon/2+\tfrac12\widehat\beta\,\partial_\lambda\log Z$. Normalizing the <generating functional> by its zero-source value cancels source-independent vacuum diagrams and the constant measure factor from field rescaling. It does not remove source-dependent <ultraviolet divergences> or <infrared divergences>; the former require the ordinary field, mass and interaction <counterterms>.

= Feynman diagram

{wiki}

A Feynman diagram records a term in the perturbative expansion: edges represent propagators, vertices represent interactions, and loops represent unconstrained momentum integrals.

= Feynman integral
{c}
{parent=Feynman diagram}

A <Feynman integral> is the momentum-space integral associated with a <Feynman diagram>: each independent <loop momentum> is integrated with measure $d^dk/(2\pi)^d$, and <propagators>, vertex numerators and momentum conservation determine the integrand. Its definition also requires a pole prescription and, when divergent, a <regularization in quantum field theory>. <Feynman parameters> combine propagator denominators and permit shifts of loop momenta. A massless two-propagator example is $\int d^dk/[(2\pi)^d k^2(p-k)^2]$, with its causal prescriptions understood. Subtracting local <counterterms> turns a regulated diagram into a finite contribution to a renormalized amplitude.

= Fermion flow
{parent=Feynman diagram}

The arrow on a charged-fermion line in a <Feynman diagram> tracks fermion-number flow and fixes the order of the <gamma matrices> between external <Dirac spinors>. For an external particle it agrees with the particle direction; for an external antiparticle it is opposite to the antiparticle direction. It therefore need not point along the assigned four-momentum of every external leg.

= Box diagram
{parent=Feynman diagram}

A <box diagram> has a one-loop cycle of four propagator edges and four vertices connecting external legs. Its internal particle types depend on the theory. In a <charged weak box contribution to kaon mixing>, two charged weak-boson propagators connect two up-type <quark> lines, with flavor-changing charged-current vertices.

= Fermion loop
{parent=Feynman diagram}

A closed chain of <Dirac propagators> is a <fermion loop>. Its <closed fermion loop sign> distinguishes it from a bosonic loop. Tracing spinor indices produces <gamma matrix> traces, and the high-momentum powers of the propagators determine possible <ultraviolet divergences>.

= Yukawa fermion triangle
{c}
{parent=Fermion loop}
{title2=$\operatorname{tr}[(\not a+m)(\not b+m)(\not c+m)]=4m[m^2+a\cdot b+a\cdot c+b\cdot c]$}

Three scalar <Yukawa interactions> on a closed <Dirac field> loop give two cyclic orientations. Odd <gamma matrix> traces vanish, while $\operatorname{tr}(\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu)=4g^{\mu\nu}$ gives the displayed numerator. For external invariant mass squares $M^2,\mu^2,\mu^2$, write $D_a=a^2-m^2$ and similarly for the other two lines. The numerator becomes $4m[D_a+D_b+D_c+4m^2-\mu^2-M^2/2]$, reducing the <fermion loop> to three scalar bubbles and a <scalar triangle Feynman integral>. The <closed fermion loop sign> must be included. A massive <fermion> produces a logarithmic local divergence.

= Cubic counterterm from a Yukawa fermion triangle
{parent=Yukawa fermion triangle}
{title2=$\mathcal L_{\mathrm{ct}}=-\delta h\,\Phi\phi^2/2$}

For interactions $-G\bar\psi\psi\Phi-g\bar\psi\psi\phi$, the two <Yukawa fermion triangle> orientations generate a local divergent three-scalar vertex proportional to $mGg^2$. In $d=4-2\epsilon$, with $S-1=i\mathcal M$ convention, its loop pole is $\mathcal M_{\mathrm{div}}=-24mGg^2/(16\pi^2\bar\epsilon)$. A $\Phi\phi^2$ <counterterm> cancels it. The finite renormalized coefficient is additional data, fixed by a <renormalization condition>. Declaring this cubic absent from the displayed bare interaction is not by itself a prescription for its loop-generated finite value.

= Yukawa fermion box
{c}
{parent=Fermion loop}

Four scalar <Yukawa interactions> on a closed <fermion loop> generate a scalar four-point <one-particle-irreducible vertex>. At high momentum four <dirac propagators> contribute $k^{-4}$, leaving a logarithmic four-dimensional <ultraviolet divergence>. Its local scalar-fourth-power term requires a quartic scalar coupling; scalar mass and field <counterterms> cannot cancel it.

= Closed fermion loop sign
{parent=Fermion loop}
{title2=$-1$}

A closed <fermion loop> carries an additional minus sign from the odd <Grassmann parity> of the fields in the <Wick theorem> contraction expansion. Equivalently, integrating out <Grassmann fields> gives a <determinant>, whose logarithmic interaction expansion has the fermionic sign relative to a bosonic inverse <determinant>. This sign must be included in addition to propagator and vertex phases.

= Disconnected Feynman diagram
{parent=Feynman diagram}

A <Feynman diagram> with more than one connected component. Products of component contributions exponentiate; taking the logarithm of a Gaussian perturbation expansion removes disconnected products and keeps <connected Feynman diagrams>.

= Triangle Feynman diagram
{parent=Feynman diagram}

A triangle <Feynman diagram> has a loop of three propagators joining three vertices. In <six-dimensional cubic scalar field theory> it gives a one-loop three-point proper vertex. Attaching a cubic tree vertex makes a four-point connected diagram, but the connecting propagator is a bridge, so the latter is a <one-particle-reducible Feynman diagram>.

= Scalar triangle Feynman integral
{parent=Triangle Feynman diagram}
{title2=$C=\int\frac{d^4\ell}{(2\pi)^4}\frac1{D_0D_1D_2}$}

In mostly-minus signature, take $D_j=(\ell+r_j)^2-m^2+i0$. <Feynman parameters> and a momentum shift give
$$
C=-\frac{i}{16\pi^2}\int_{x_j\ge0}dx_0dx_1dx_2\,\delta(1-\textstyle\sum_jx_j)\left[m^2-\sum_{i<j}x_ix_j(r_i-r_j)^2-i0\right]^{-1}.
$$
For massive internal lines this is ultraviolet finite in four dimensions. Physical external kinematics can still create threshold cuts, encoded by the <Feynman i-epsilon prescription>.

= Supergraph
{parent=Feynman diagram}

A supergraph is a <Feynman diagram> formulated with <superfield> propagators and vertices in <superspace>. Performing its spinor-derivative algebra organizes perturbative corrections into <superspace integration> terms and provides a proof of the local <superpotential> <non-renormalization theorem>.

= Box Feynman diagram
{parent=Feynman diagram}

A one-loop <Feynman diagram> with four vertices joined into a box. Two <W bosons> and two internal <quarks> give a short-distance contribution to <neutral D-meson mixing>.

= Feynman vertex
{c}
{synonym}

An <interaction vertex> joins the field insertions from one interaction term in a <Feynman diagram>. Its number of incident lines is the number of fields in that term, and its momentum-space <Feynman rule> includes the interaction coefficient and <momentum conservation>.

= Squared-amplitude diagram
{parent=Feynman diagram}

A squared-amplitude diagram consists of an amplitude graph and its complex-conjugate graph, with matching external states. Summing an external fermion spin sews the associated spinor indices using a <fermion spin sum>. The resulting closed spinor chains become <gamma-matrix traces>. This notation represents probabilities and must be distinguished from adding another physical interaction loop to the amplitude.

= Feynman rule
{c}
{parent=Feynman diagram}

Feynman rules translate each diagram into momentum-space factors for propagators, vertices, external states, momentum conservation and loop integration.

= Scalar-field cubic and quartic Feynman vertices
{parent=Feynman rule}
{title2=$i\mu,\quad-i\lambda$}

With interaction density $\mu\phi^3/3!-\lambda\phi^4/4!$, the <Dyson series> contains $i\mathcal L_{\rm int}$ and hence cubic vertex $i\mu$ and quartic vertex $-i\lambda$. Wick contractions into the labelled slots cancel each factorial. Position integration gives the four-momentum delta function, and equivalent unlabelled contractions leave the <Feynman diagram symmetry factor>. The free line carries $i/(p^2-m^2+i0)$ in the mostly-minus convention.

= Four-leg vertex of a factorial-normalized scalar interaction
{parent=Feynman rule}
{title2=$V_4=i\lambda$}

An interaction $\mathcal L_{\rm int}=+\lambda\phi^4/4!$ produces a four-leg momentum-space vertex $i\lambda$. The $4!$ assignments of external legs cancel the coefficient factorial. With a negative interaction sign the vertex is $-i\lambda$. Graph <Feynman-diagram symmetry factors> are a separate combinatorial issue.

= Symmetry factor
{synonym}

The symmetry factor of a Feynman diagram divides out permutations of indistinguishable internal lines and vertices that leave the labeled diagram unchanged. It is the order of the corresponding automorphism group after external labels are fixed.

= Feynman parameter
{c}
{parent=Feynman rule}
{wiki}

A Feynman parameter combines propagator denominators; for example,
$$
\frac1{AB}=\int_0^1\frac{dx}{[xA+(1-x)B]^2}.
$$
After a momentum shift, this often converts a loop integral into a rotationally symmetric one.

= Cancellation of vacuum bubbles
{parent=Vacuum Feynman diagram}

A perturbative correlator factors into an exponential of connected <vacuum Feynman diagrams> times a sum of <Feynman diagrams> attached to external insertions. Dividing its <path integral> by the vacuum <generating functional> at zero source cancels that exponential, including all disconnected products of vacuum components. The corresponding <operator formalism> uses the same denominator in the vacuum expectation of the <Dyson series>. This leaves externally disconnected correlators intact; the <connected generating functional> removes their products.

= Vacuum energy density

The vacuum energy density is the bulk limit $\rho_{\mathrm{vac}}=\lim_{\mathcal V\to\infty}E_0(\mathcal V)/\mathcal V$, with boundary effects removed and a fixed convention for the Hamiltonian. = Vacuum energy sign of a fermionic oscillator
{parent=Vacuum energy}
{title2=$H=E(b^\dagger b-\tfrac12)$}

A fermionic mode with <canonical anticommutation relations> $\{b,b^\dagger\}=1$ has the symmetrically ordered <Hamiltonian operator> $H=\tfrac E2(b^\dagger b-bb^\dagger)=E(b^\dagger b-1/2)$. For a <Dirac field>, sum over both particle and <antiparticle> modes and over <spin>: there are four independent oscillators per spatial <momentum> in four spacetime dimensions. Comparing bosonic and fermionic <vacuum energies> requires a common <regularization in quantum field theory>, matching degrees of freedom and a stated vacuum-energy subtraction convention.

= Tadpole diagram

= Tadpole contribution to a cubic-theory two-point function
{parent=Tadpole diagram}

Use $-g\phi^3/3!$ and $D=6-2\epsilon$ with $m>0$. In an unshifted vacuum, the one-loop mean field has pole $v_{\rm div}=-gm^2/[4(4\pi)^3\epsilon]$. A disconnected product of two one-point functions is a separate two-loop term and is not a <propagator> insertion.

= Tadpole subtraction
{parent=Tadpole diagram}

A linear <counterterm> can be chosen so that the renormalized one-point function, or equivalently the chosen <vacuum expectation value>, is zero. This cancels attached <tadpole diagrams> in the perturbative expansion about that vacuum. In a massive <six-dimensional cubic scalar field theory>, the one-loop proper tadpole is $t=gI_1/2$; without subtraction the stationary background shifts by $v=-t/m^2+O(g^3)$ at one-loop order. One must specify this convention when comparing reducible exchange diagrams or mass corrections.

= Distinguishable scalar scattering by a shared mediator
{parent=Tree-level Feynman diagram}
{title2=$\mathcal M=-g_1g_2/(t-M^2+i0)$}

For three <real scalar fields> with <field interaction term> $\mathcal L_I=-\frac12(g_1\phi^2\Phi+g_2\psi^2\Phi)$ and mediator mass $M$, the connected <tree-level Feynman diagram> for $\phi\psi\to\phi\psi$ has one exchanged $\Phi$ and <Mandelstam invariant> $t=(p-p')^2$. = Loop order
{parent=Feynman diagram}

The loop order of a connected <Feynman diagram> is the number $L=I-V+1$ of independent momentum cycles, where $I$ is the number of internal lines and $V$ the number of vertices. Each loop introduces an unconstrained momentum integral.

= Vertex-count bounds for bounded scalar valence
{parent=Loop order}
{title2=$\tfrac12V\le L-1+\tfrac12n\le2V$}

For a connected <Feynman diagram> with external valence $n$ and cubic, quartic and sextic vertices, counting half-edges gives $2I+n=3V_3+4V_4+6V_6$. Using $L=I-(V_3+V_4+V_6)+1$ yields $L-1+n/2=V_3/2+V_4+2V_6$. Since each summand lies between half and twice its vertex count, the displayed bounds follow. More generally vertices of valence between $r_{\min}$ and $r_{\max}$ give $(r_{\min}/2-1)V\le L-1+n/2\le(r_{\max}/2-1)V$.

= Planck-constant loop counting with source normalization
{c}
{parent=Loop order}
{title2=$\hbar^{L-1}$}

In a <scalar field path integral> with weight $\exp[i(S+\int J\phi)/\hbar]$, each internal <propagator> contributes $\hbar$, each interaction vertex contributes $\hbar^{-1}$, and each source insertion contributes $\hbar^{-1}$. A connected graph with $I$ internal lines and $V$ interaction vertices has <loop order> $L=I-V+1$. Its amputated amplitude, or its coefficient in $\log Z[J]$ with the source insertions included, is proportional to $\hbar^{I-V}=\hbar^{L-1}$. An ordinary unamputated connected $n$-field <correlation function> instead has the additional $n$ external propagator factors and scales as $\hbar^{L+n-1}$. Multiplication by $-i\hbar$ to form the <connected generating functional> shifts the source-kernel counting to $\hbar^L$. These are compatible normalization conventions.

= Cubic-scalar exchange corrections at zero momentum
{parent=One-particle-reducible Feynman diagram}

In the <six-dimensional cubic scalar field theory>, use full external-propagator amputation and <tadpole subtraction>. The exchange part of the zero-momentum four-point amplitude is
$$
\mathcal A_{4,\mathrm{exchange}}=\frac{3g^2}{m^2}+\frac{6g^4}{m^2}I_3+\frac{3g^4}{2m^4}I_2+O(\text{two loops}).
$$
The triangle corrects either end of each of three exchanges, and the symmetry factor of an internal bubble is $1/2$. Equivalently insert $\Gamma^{(3)}=g+g^3I_3$ and $\Gamma^{(2)}=m^2-g^2I_2/2$ into $3[\Gamma^{(3)}]^2/\Gamma^{(2)}$. Without tadpole subtraction, the shift $v=-gI_1/(2m^2)$ adds $3g^4I_1/(2m^6)$. None of these is an additional local 1PI quartic coupling; zero-momentum bridges are also excluded from a strict high-mode Wilsonian shell. If bare rather than full external propagators are amputated, external-line decorations additionally contribute $6g^4I_2/m^4$, and $6g^4I_1/m^6$ without tadpole subtraction. Every displayed term has <mass dimension> minus two. In a renormalized expansion, the exchange diagrams also receive the <counterterm> insertions $6g\delta g/m^2-3g^2\delta m^2/m^4$. Without enforcing a zero one-point function, a linear <counterterm> $\delta h\phi$ contributes $3g^3\delta h/m^6$ through the background shift; choosing $\delta h=-gI_1/2$ cancels the attached tadpole. A kinetic <counterterm> is proportional to the exchanged momentum squared, so its insertion vanishes at this zero-momentum point.

= Asymptotic scalar field
{parent=LSZ reduction formula}
{title2=$\phi_{\rm in},\phi_{\rm out}$}

An <asymptotic scalar field> is a free field describing stable incoming or outgoing particles of an interacting <scalar field theory>. With a field that overlaps the stable one-particle state, the scattering asymptotic condition is $\phi\to\sqrt Z\phi_{\rm in}$ in the remote past and $\phi\to\sqrt Z\phi_{\rm out}$ in the remote future. These are scattering limits of suitably smeared matrix elements, not unrestricted strong pointwise limits of local fields. With covariant state normalization $\langle p'|p\rangle=(2\pi)^3 2E_p\delta^3(\mathbf p-\mathbf p')$, the free-field vacuum-to-one-particle matrix element is one. <Translation invariance> and the asymptotic condition then give $\langle p|\phi(0)|0\rangle=\sqrt Z$ after choosing its phase positive. The <wave-function renormalization> residue $Z$ measures that overlap.

= One-loop scalar effective potential

{title2=$V_1(\varphi)=\frac{[V''(\varphi)]^2}{64\pi^2}\left[\log\frac{V''(\varphi)}{\mu^2}-\frac32\right]$}

For one canonically normalized <real scalar field> and $V''(\varphi)>0$, the <modified minimal subtraction scheme> gives the displayed one-loop term. = Quartic closure of scalar effective-potential counterterms
{parent=One-loop scalar effective potential}
{title2=$V_{\rm div}=-[V''(\varphi)]^2/(32\pi^2\epsilon)$}

For $d=4-\epsilon$, the scalar one-loop determinant has the displayed local divergent potential. = Background gauge invariance of a scalar effective action
{parent=Effective action}
{title2=$\mathcal W_a\Gamma=0$}

For real scalar multiplets with antisymmetric generators $t_a$, transforming a source $J$ in the same representation leaves $J\cdot\phi$ invariant. With an invariant <functional measure>, local changes of integration variables imply the <Ward identity> for the <quantum effective action>. In the convention $Z=e^{iW}$, $\varphi=W_J$, $\Gamma=-W+\int J\varphi$, it is
$$
\left[(t_a\varphi)\cdot\frac{\delta}{\delta\varphi}+\partial_\mu\frac{\delta}{\delta A_{\mu a}}+f_{abc}A_{\mu b}\frac{\delta}{\delta A_{\mu c}}\right]\Gamma=0.
$$
The <Legendre transform> gives $\Gamma_\varphi=J$ and $\Gamma_A|_\varphi=-W_A|_J$. Its classical limit is $\Gamma=-S_0$, not $S_0$, for the source term $+J\phi$. Differentiating twice in $\varphi$ produces two contact terms and the momentum-space identity $p_{1\mu}\tau^\mu_{a,ij}=i(t_a)_{ik}\tau_{kj}-\tau_{ik}i(t_a)_{kj}$ with the appropriate shifted two-point momenta. Changing the overall convention for $\Gamma$ changes both two-point and mixed vertices together; changing just one violates the identity. A <gauge anomaly> would invalidate the assumed invariant-measure argument.

After shell integration and $x^\prime=bx$, restore the <kinetic term> by $\phi^\prime=b^{-(d-2)/2}\sqrt{1+\Delta Z}\,\phi$. An operator with $n$ scalar fields and $m$ derivatives scales by $b^{m+n(d-2)/2-d}$. In four dimensions this gives the displayed factor with $m+n-4$; the general exponent is $m+n(d-2)/2-d$. The coupling shift and <wavefunction renormalization> arise before the rescaling.

= Connected shell-contraction expansion
{parent=Wilsonian effective action}
{title2=$-\log\langle e^{-V}\rangle_>=\langle V\rangle_>-\tfrac12\langle V^2\rangle_{>,c}+\cdots$}

The <cumulant expansion> of the shell integral keeps precisely <connected Feynman diagrams> built from <shell-restricted scalar propagators>. The external low fields are backgrounds, not propagators. Connected graphs with bridges can contribute, so the expansion is not limited to <one-particle-irreducible Feynman diagrams>.

= Momentum-support exclusion for Wilsonian bridge diagrams
{parent=Connected shell-contraction expansion}
{title2=$C_>*\phi_<=0$}

If a bridge is the only line carrying momentum away from a low external field, tadpole pairs do not change its momentum and the <shell-restricted scalar propagator> has zero support there. A bridge attached to a product such as $\phi_<^3$ can survive because the sum of three low momenta can enter the shell. This matters before a local <derivative expansion>.

= Momentum-shell decomposition of a scalar field
{parent=Wilsonian effective action}
{title2=$\Phi=\phi_<+\phi_>$}

Split <Fourier transform> modes into a low-momentum ball and its disjoint shell. The quadratic scalar action has no cross term between these supports. Integrating the shell at fixed low field defines the lower-cutoff <Wilsonian effective action>.

= Renormalization
{parent=Perturbative quantum field theory}
{wiki}

= Renormalized
{synonym}

Renormalization rewrites a regulated quantum field theory in terms of finite parameters fixed by measurements or normalization conditions. Dependence on the regulator is absorbed into <counterterms>, while dependence on the chosen <renormalization scale> is governed by a <beta function (physics)>.

= Bare coupling
{parent=Renormalization}
{title2=$\lambda_0$}

A <bare coupling> is an interaction coefficient in a regulated action. It is related to a <renormalized coupling> through counterterms and any required powers of the <renormalization scale>. For quartic scalar theory in $4-\epsilon$ dimensions, its <mass dimension> is $\epsilon$, and $\lambda_0=\mu^\epsilon[\lambda_R+a\lambda_R^2/\epsilon+\cdots]$. At fixed regulator, changing $\mu$ while leaving the theory unchanged keeps $\lambda_0$ fixed; the corresponding change of $\lambda_R$ defines its <renormalization-group beta function>.

= Bare mass
{parent=Renormalization}
{title2=$m_0$}

The <bare mass> is the mass parameter in a regulated field-theory action before a <renormalization condition> relates it to a measured mass. For a scalar, loop <self-energy> corrections change the propagator denominator to $p^2-m_0^2-\Sigma(p^2)$. The <pole mass> solves $m^2-m_0^2-\Sigma(m^2)=0$ for a stable particle. The <bare mass> generally depends on the regulator when the <pole mass> is held fixed.

= Renormalization scheme
{parent=Renormalization}

A choice of <renormalization conditions> and finite <counterterms> defines a renormalization scheme. The <minimal subtraction scheme> removes only regulator poles; the <modified minimal subtraction scheme> also removes conventional universal constants. Different schemes can assign different values to renormalized couplings while describing the same physical observables after parameters are consistently matched.

= Running mass
{parent=Renormalization}
{title2=$m(t)=m(0)\exp(\int_0^t\delta(g(u))du)$}

A renormalized mass changes with the reference scale according to its mass <anomalous dimension> $\delta(g)$. In a dimensionless correlator at increasing physical energy, its effective argument includes the canonical factor $e^{-t}$. This distinguishes the <running mass> itself from its ratio to the growing energy scale.

= Renormalization-group mass suppression criterion
{parent=Running mass}
{title2=$\int_0^t(\delta(g(u))-1)du\to-\infty$}

The effective dimensionless mass is $e^{-t}m(t)/\mu$. It vanishes precisely when this integrated exponent tends to minus infinity, for positive initial mass. An eventual bound $\delta\le1-\eta$ is sufficient. Replacing the massive correlator by its <massless limit> additionally requires regularity uniform along the coupling trajectory.

= Zero-momentum renormalized mass
{parent=Renormalization}
{title2=$R=\widetilde\Gamma^{(2)}(0)$}

In a specified scalar-field normalisation, one can define a renormalized squared mass by $R=\widetilde\Gamma^{(2)}(0)$. Its value depends on the renormalization prescription and is not automatically the pole mass in a theory with momentum-dependent <self-energy>. A reference propagator $(p^2+R)^{-1}$ and <mass counterterm> then organise perturbation theory around that prescription.

= Renormalizable quantum field theory
{parent=Renormalization}

= Renormalizability
{synonym}

A renormalizable <quantum field theory> permits ultraviolet divergences to be absorbed into a fixed finite set of parameters and field normalizations. In ordinary four-dimensional perturbative <power counting in quantum field theory>, its interaction coefficients have nonnegative <mass dimension>.

= Counterterm
{parent=Renormalization}
{wiki}

A counterterm is a local term added to a regulated <Lagrangian density> to cancel ultraviolet divergences and impose chosen <renormalization conditions>. Field-strength, mass and coupling counterterms respectively adjust propagator normalization, pole position and interaction strength.

= Counterterm closure
{parent=Counterterm}

A family of actions is closed under <counterterms> when all required divergent local terms can be absorbed into that family. This does not imply that the full <quantum effective action> contains no additional finite effective <interaction vertices> or terms in its <derivative expansion>.

= Counterterm closure of a massive Yukawa theory
{parent=Counterterm closure}

Without a symmetry excluding odd scalar powers, a real-scalar <Yukawa interaction> with a massive <Dirac field> requires a general scalar potential through degree four. For a constant background, the fermion mass becomes $M+y\phi$, and its divergent local <determinant> contribution contains $(M+y\phi)^4$. Linear and cubic terms are therefore generated when $M\ne0$. A zero renormalized tadpole is a <renormalization condition>, not a prohibition of its <counterterm>. An exact discrete chiral symmetry in a massless fermion theory can instead enforce an even scalar potential.

= Mass counterterm
{title2=$\delta m$}
{parent=Counterterm}

A mass counterterm cancels the divergent part of a self-energy that has the tensor structure of a mass term. Its finite part depends on the renormalization scheme, while an on-shell scheme chooses it so that the renormalized mass equals the pole mass.

= Renormalization condition
{parent=Renormalization}

A renormalization condition defines a renormalized field or parameter by prescribing a correlation function at a chosen kinematic point. Changing that point changes the renormalized parameters while leaving physical predictions invariant.

= Renormalized coupling
{parent=Renormalization condition}
{title2=$\lambda_R(\mu)$}

A <renormalized coupling> is an interaction parameter defined by a specified finite <renormalization condition> or subtraction scheme at a scale $\mu$. Observables remain independent of that arbitrary scale when its running and the loop corrections are included consistently. The coupling itself, and higher-order coefficients in its <renormalization-group beta function>, can depend on the scheme; they should not be identified without qualification with a regulator-independent bare coefficient.

= Momentum-subtraction scheme
{parent=Renormalization condition}

A momentum-subtraction scheme defines renormalized masses, fields, and couplings by fixing correlation functions at specified external momenta.

This is a particular choice of <renormalization scheme>.

= On-shell renormalization scheme
{parent=Renormalization condition}
{wiki}

The on-shell renormalization scheme fixes masses at physical propagator poles, fixes field normalizations by pole residues, and fixes couplings through amplitudes evaluated with external particles on their mass shells.

= Callan-Symanzik equation
{c}
{parent=Renormalization}
{wiki}

The Callan-Symanzik equation states that a renormalized correlation function is independent of the arbitrary renormalization scale after the couplings and field normalization run. For an $n$-point function it has the form
$$
\left(\mu\partial_\mu+\beta(g)\partial_g+n\gamma_\phi\right)\Gamma_R^{(n)}=0
$$
up to sign conventions for the anomalous dimension.

= Massless renormalization-group characteristics with a source
{parent=Callan-Symanzik equation}
{title2=$d\lambda/d\log\mu=\widehat\beta,\quad d\log h/d\log\mu=\widehat\gamma_h$}

For a normalized connected functional obeying $(\mu\partial_\mu+\widehat\beta\partial_\lambda+\widehat\gamma_hh\partial_h)F=0$, solve the displayed ordinary differential equations and keep $F$ constant along their curves. Where $\widehat\beta\ne0$, $\log(\mu/\mu_*)=\int_{\lambda_*}^{\lambda(\mu)}dg/\widehat\beta(g)$ and $h(\mu)=h_*\exp\int_{\lambda_*}^{\lambda(\mu)}\widehat\gamma_h(g)dg/\widehat\beta(g)$. For the dimensionless density $f(\lambda,y)$ of a uniform source, with $y=h/\mu^3$, the equation is $[\widehat\beta\partial_\lambda+(\widehat\gamma_h-3)y\partial_y+d]f=0$. Its characteristic invariant is $y\exp[-\int(\widehat\gamma_h-3)dg/\widehat\beta]$, and its value acquires the factor $\exp[-\int d\,dg/\widehat\beta]$. A finite-volume total functional also retains the dimensionless volume argument.

= Characteristic solution of the massless Callan-Symanzik equation
{parent=Callan-Symanzik equation}
{title2=$G_n(\lambda(\mu),\mu)=e^{-n\int_{\mu_0}^{\mu}\gamma_\phi(\lambda(\nu))\,d\nu/\nu}G_n(\lambda_0,\mu_0)$}

For fixed external momenta, the massless <Callan-Symanzik equation> is $(\mu\partial_\mu+\beta(\lambda)\partial_\lambda+n\gamma_\phi)G_n=0$. Choose a <running coupling> satisfying $d\lambda/d\log\mu=\beta(\lambda)$ and $\lambda(\mu_0)=\lambda_0$. The <chain rule> reduces the equation along this curve to $dG_n/d\log\mu=-n\gamma_\phi G_n$, whose integrating factor gives the displayed solution. The reference <correlation function> at $\mu_0$ supplies boundary data; the <renormalization group> alone does not determine these data.

If $\beta=b\lambda^2$, then $\lambda(\mu)=\lambda_0/[1-b\lambda_0\log(\mu/\mu_0)]$. If in addition the <anomalous dimension> is $\gamma_\phi=c\lambda^2$, the integral in the exponent is $(c/b)(\lambda(\mu)-\lambda_0)$. These formulas use the stipulated truncated <renormalization group> functions and apply on a branch on which the running solution remains defined.

= Renormalization-group characteristic solution for a two-point function
{parent=Callan-Symanzik equation}
{title2=$d(e^{2t},g)=d(1,\bar g(t))e^{2\int_0^t\gamma_\phi(\bar g(s))ds}$}

For $t=\log(|p|/\mu)$ and $d\bar g/dt=\beta(\bar g)$, the displayed characteristic solution follows from $(-\partial_t+\beta\partial_g+2\gamma_\phi)d=0$. It separates the <running coupling> from the accumulated <field-renormalization anomalous dimension>. The exponent sign is determined by the precise <Callan-Symanzik equation> convention.

= Logarithmic two-point scaling at a cubic asymptotically free beta function
{parent=Renormalization-group characteristic solution for a two-point function}
{title2=$d(z,g)=d(1,\bar g)[1+bg^2\log z]^{c/b}$}

For $\beta=-bg^3$, $\gamma_\phi=cg^2$, and $b>0$, one has $\bar g^2=g^2/(1+bg^2\log z)$. Integrating the <field-renormalization anomalous dimension> gives the displayed logarithmic factor. At large $z$, a nonzero free matching factor yields $d\sim C(\log z)^{c/b}$ in the printed RG sign convention.

= Characteristic solution of the multiplicative Callan-Symanzik equation
{parent=Callan-Symanzik equation}
{title2=$C(e^{2t}r,g)=\exp(2\int_0^t\gamma(g(u))du)C(r,g(t))$}

For a dimensionless two-point factor obeying $(-2r\partial_r+\beta\partial_g+2\gamma)C=0$, evolve $g'=\beta(g)$ and accumulate the displayed anomalous-dimension multiplier. The flow property verifies the equation: its transport generator is $\beta\partial_g+2\gamma$. This fixes a frequent sign ambiguity between raising the physical momentum and changing the reference scale.

= Callan-Symanzik equation with additive renormalization
{c}
{parent=Callan-Symanzik equation}

= Inhomogeneous Callan-Symanzik equation

An additive subtraction can produce a source in the renormalization-group equation. If $G_R=G_B-a\log(\Lambda_0/\Lambda)$ and the bare correlator is invariant under $\mathcal D=\Lambda\partial_\Lambda+\beta(g)\partial_g$, then $\mathcal D G_R=a$. A vanishing operator <anomalous dimension> does not remove this source.

= Continuum limit of a quantum field theory

A continuum limit removes an ultraviolet cutoff while keeping specified physical observables finite. In renormalization-group language, the bare trajectory must approach an ultraviolet fixed point and the correlation length measured in cutoff units must diverge.

= Asymptotically safe quantum field theory

An asymptotically safe quantum field theory approaches a non-Gaussian ultraviolet fixed point with finitely many relevant directions. Those relevant coordinates are fixed by finitely many measurements.

Quantum triviality means that removal of the cutoff forces the renormalized interaction to vanish, leaving a free continuum theory. Keeping a nonzero interaction then requires treating the model as an effective theory with finite cutoff.

= Renormalization scale
{title2=$\mu$}
{parent=Renormalization}
{wiki}

The renormalization scale is the auxiliary momentum or energy scale at which <renormalization conditions> define the parameters of a quantum field theory.

= Principle of minimal sensitivity
{parent=Renormalization scale}
{title2=$dO_{\mathrm{trunc}}/d\ln\mu^2=0$}

This prescription selects an arbitrary scale or scheme parameter by making a truncated perturbative observable stationary under its variation. The complete physical observable is independent of the <renormalization scale>, but truncation leaves residual dependence. Stationarity is a prescription, not a guarantee of uniquely optimal accuracy or of a stationary point's existence.

= One-loop stationary scale for the hadronic annihilation correction
{parent=Principle of minimal sensitivity}
{title2=$\mu_*^2=q^2e^{-C/b}$}

Suppose $K=1+a+a^2[C-b\ln(q^2/\mu^2)]$, with $da/d\ln\mu^2=-ba^2$. Differentiation gives $dK/d\ln\mu^2=-2ba^3[C-b\ln(q^2/\mu^2)]$. For nonzero $a,b$, the <principle of minimal sensitivity> therefore gives $\boxed{\mu_*^2=q^2e^{-C/b}}$. The derivative vanishes identically through order $a^2$: this prescription retains the residual $a^3$ term generated by one-loop running of the truncated expression. Higher-order running and coefficients can shift the stationary scale. For $b=0$ or $a=0$, no unique scale is selected.

= Running coupling
{parent=Renormalization scale}
{wiki}

A running coupling is a renormalized coupling regarded as a function of the <renormalization scale>. Its scale derivative is its <beta function (physics)>.

= Two-loop asymptotic running coupling
{parent=Running coupling}
{title2=$\frac{4\pi}{\alpha_\lambda}=\beta_0L+\frac{\beta_1}{\beta_0}\log L+O(\log L/L)$}

For $\beta(\lambda)=-\beta_0\lambda^3/(4\pi)^2-\beta_1\lambda^5/(4\pi)^4+O(\lambda^7)$, define $y=(4\pi)^2/\lambda^2$ and $L=\log(\mu^2/\Lambda^2)$. Then $dy/dL=\beta_0+\beta_1/y+O(y^{-2})$. In the asymptotically free regime $\beta_0>0$, successive substitution gives the displayed expansion. If the <beta function (physics)> is exactly truncated to two loops, then $y-\frac{\beta_1}{\beta_0}\log[(y+\beta_1/\beta_0)/\beta_0]=\beta_0L$, after choosing the integration constant. Its next term is $\beta_1^2(\log L+1)/(\beta_0^3L)$. Thus the generic remainder after the first two terms is not $O(1/L)$ when $\beta_1\ne0$. Higher-loop terms change the constant coefficient of $1/L$, but not the forced $\log L/L$ term.

= One-loop inverse gauge coupling evolution
{parent=Running coupling}
{title2=$\alpha_i^{-1}(\mu)=\alpha_i^{-1}(\mu_0)-\frac{b_i}{2\pi}\log(\mu/\mu_0)$}

From $\mu\,dg_i/d\mu=b_i g_i^3/(16\pi^2)$ and $\alpha_i=g_i^2/(4\pi)$, the <chain rule> gives $d\alpha_i/d\log\mu=b_i\alpha_i^2/(2\pi)$. Taking its reciprocal makes the differential equation linear, proving the displayed relation. It assumes a fixed set of active fields and constant one-loop coefficients between the two scales; mass thresholds require piecewise matching. A negative coefficient gives <asymptotic freedom>.

= One-loop running of the strong coupling
{parent=Running coupling}
{title2=$\alpha_s(\mu)=4\pi/[\beta_0\ln(\mu^2/\Lambda^2)]$}

With $\beta_0>0$, differentiating $\alpha_s=g^2/(4\pi)$ under $\mu\,dg/d\mu=-\beta_0g^3/(16\pi^2)$ gives $d(1/\alpha_s)/d\ln\mu=\beta_0/(2\pi)$. Integration defines the <strong-coupling scale> $\Lambda$ and gives the displayed positive branch for $\mu>\Lambda$. The pole signals breakdown of <perturbative quantum field theory>. The equivalence between specifying $\alpha_s$ and specifying $\Lambda$ is <dimensional transmutation>.

= Infrared limitations of one-loop QCD running
{parent=One-loop running of the strong coupling}
{title2=$\alpha_s(\mu^2)=1/[\beta_0\ln(\mu^2/\Lambda^2)],\quad\mu>\Lambda$}

For positive $\beta_0$, the one-loop <running coupling> tends to zero at high energy and grows as energy decreases. Near the <strong-coupling scale>, omitted perturbative terms cease to be small; the apparent infrared pole is not a controlled prediction of a physical infinity. Continuing the algebraic formula below $\Lambda$ gives a negative coupling and is not a valid physical continuation. <Confinement> and hadron formation are nonperturbative phenomena, not a theorem proved by this one-loop pole. The integration constant obeys $\Lambda=\mu_0\exp[-1/(2\beta_0\alpha_0)]$, so an order-one coefficient alone cannot determine an absolute mass scale; a reference coupling or empirical input is also necessary.

= Matching the QCD scale across a quark threshold
{parent=One-loop running of the strong coupling}

At a heavy-quark mass $m$, leading-order continuity gives $\beta_- \ln(m/\Lambda_-)=\beta_+\ln(m/\Lambda_+)$. Therefore $\boxed{\Lambda_+=m(\Lambda_-/m)^{\beta_-/\beta_+}}$. This is a change of the <strong-coupling scale> parametrization across a change of active flavour number, not a discontinuity of the matched <QCD coupling>. For four to five active flavours in $SU(3)$, $\beta_-=25/3$ and $\beta_+=23/3$, hence $\Lambda_5=\Lambda_4(m_b/\Lambda_4)^{-2/23}$.

= Landau pole
{c}
{parent=Running coupling}
{wiki}

A Landau pole is a finite renormalization scale at which a perturbatively running coupling diverges. It signals that the perturbative description cannot be continued to that scale without new physics or a nonperturbative completion.

= Higgs self-coupling Landau-pole bound
{c}
{parent=Landau pole}
{title2=$\mu_L=\mu_0\exp[1/(2b\alpha_H(\mu_0))]$}

In the self-coupling-dominated approximation $d\alpha_H/d\ln\mu^2=b\alpha_H^2$, $b>0$, the <running coupling> reaches a perturbative <Landau pole> at the displayed scale. With the conventional <Higgs potential>, $m_H^2=2\lambda_Hv^2$ and one can normalize $\alpha_H=\lambda_H/(4\pi)$. A larger <Higgs boson> mass therefore decreases the logarithmic interval before the self-coupling becomes strong. Requiring perturbative validity up to a fixed high cutoff gives an upper Higgs-mass bound in this approximation. The full <Standard Model> beta function also contains gauge and <Yukawa couplings>; its sign is not universally positive. A perturbative pole is not a theorem excluding every nonperturbative ultraviolet completion.

= Beta function
{title2=$\beta(g)=\mu\,dg/d\mu$}
{disambiguate=physics}
{parent=Running coupling}
{wiki}

= Renormalization-group beta function
{synonym}

The beta function of a coupling $g$ is
$$
\beta(g)=\mu\frac{dg}{d\mu}=\frac{dg}{d\log\mu}.
$$
It describes how the <running coupling> changes when the <renormalization scale> changes.

= Spectator multiplicities in gauge beta functions
{parent=Beta function (physics)}
{title2=$T_i(R_i)\prod_{j\ne i}\dim R_j$}

For a field in a tensor-product <representation> of a product <gauge group>, its contribution to the <beta function (physics)> of factor $i$ includes the dimensions of all other factors. Those indices label independent copies for that factor. For an Abelian factor, replace its <Dynkin index> by the squared charge. Thus a quark weak doublet contributes three copies to the weak beta function and two copies to the colour beta function; <hypercharge> sums include all six components. <Chirality> conjugation changes a <representation> and the sign of an Abelian charge, but not its quadratic index.

= Gauge-coupling beta function in logarithmic squared scale
{parent=Beta function (physics)}
{title2=$\mu\,dg/d\mu=cg^3+O(g^5)\Longrightarrow d\alpha/d\ln\mu^2=4\pi c\alpha^2+O(\alpha^3)$}

For a four-dimensional dimensionless gauge coupling, the one-loop correction is cubic in $g$. With $\alpha=g^2/(4\pi)$, the chain rule and $d/d\ln\mu^2=(1/2)d/d\ln\mu$ give the displayed conversion. In <QCD>, $c=-(11N-2n_f)/(48\pi^2)$, hence $d\alpha_s/d\ln\mu^2=-(11N-2n_f)\alpha_s^2/(12\pi)+O(\alpha_s^3)$. Other types of coupling or several interacting couplings can have different leading expansions; the squared-scale convention must not be mixed with the unsquared one.

= Engineering term in a dimensionally continued beta function
{parent=Beta function (physics)}
{title2=$\lambda_0=\mu^\epsilon F(\lambda,\epsilon)\Longrightarrow\widehat\beta(\lambda)=-\epsilon\lambda+\beta(\lambda)$}

In $d=4-\epsilon$, the quartic scalar coupling has <engineering dimension> $\epsilon$. Factoring out $\mu^\epsilon$ makes the renormalized <running coupling> dimensionless. Differentiating its bare expression at fixed bare parameters generates the linear term $-\epsilon\lambda$, even when the quantum contribution to the <renormalization-group beta function> begins quadratically. The <simple-pole formulas for minimal-subtraction renormalization group functions> determine the remaining contribution from the simple pole of $F$.

For three <quark generations>, their corresponding <leptons>, and one complex <Higgs doublet>, use $Q=T_3+Y$ and $D_\mu\supset ig_Y YB_\mu$. In $\mu\,dg_i/d\mu=b_i g_i^3/(16\pi^2)$ the coefficients are the displayed values. = Nonzero ultraviolet fixed point of a cubic-quintic beta function
{parent=Beta function (physics)}
{title2=$\beta(g)=Bg^3-Ag^5,\quad g_*^2=B/A$}

For $A,B>0$, the nonzero zero of the <renormalization-group beta function> has slope $\beta^{\prime}(g_*)=-2B^2/A<0$ and is ultraviolet attractive. The origin is infrared attractive. A fixed-point <field-renormalization anomalous dimension> gives a power-law <quantum field theory propagator> factor $d(z)\sim Cz^{\gamma_\phi(g_*)}$. = Two-loop inverse-coupling logarithmic remainder
{parent=Beta function (physics)}
{title2=$a^{-1}=\beta_0L+(\beta_1/\beta_0)\log L+O(\log L/L)$}

For the <asymptotically free> two-loop <running coupling> $da/d\log\mu=-\beta_0a^2-\beta_1a^3$, with $\beta_0>0$, let $y=1/a$, $c=\beta_1/\beta_0$, and $L=\log(\mu/\Lambda)$. A choice of the scale $\Lambda$ gives the exact implicit equation $y-c\log((y+c)/\beta_0)=\beta_0L$ on the ultraviolet branch. Its expansion is
$$
y=\beta_0L+c\log L+\frac{c^2}{\beta_0}\frac{\log L+1}{L}+O\!\left(\frac{(\log L)^2}{L^2}\right).
$$
The remainder after the first two terms is therefore $O(\log L/L)$, and is not $O(1/L)$ when $\beta_1\ne0$. A fixed rescaling of $\Lambda$ cannot change the coefficient of $\log L/L$. This distinction matters when quoting errors for <asymptotic expansions>.

= Banks-Zaks fixed point

= Monopole core and mass scales in an SU(5) transition
{parent=SU(5) grand unified theory}

= Canonical field normalization

Canonical field normalization rescales a field so that its kinetic term has unit coefficient. For a <chiral superfield> with $K=Z\Phi^\dagger\Phi$, the change $\Phi_c=Z^{1/2}\Phi$ turns holomorphic <superpotential> parameters into canonical parameters $m_c=m/Z$ and $g_c=g/Z^{3/2}$.

= Charge renormalization

Charge renormalization relates a bare gauge coupling to a coupling fixed by a physical normalization condition. In QED, the <Ward identity> equates vertex and fermion wave-function renormalization, leaving photon wave-function renormalization to determine the charge counterterm.

= Pure Yang-Mills one-loop beta function
{parent=Charge renormalization}
{title2=$\beta(g)=-11C_Ag^3/[3(4\pi)^2]+O(g^5)$}

For pure <Yang-Mills theory> with a compact simple <gauge group> and canonically normalized <gauge field>, the one-loop <renormalization-group beta function> is $\mu\,dg/d\mu=-11C_Ag^3/[3(4\pi)^2]+O(g^5)$, where $C_A>0$ is the <Casimir operator> eigenvalue in the <adjoint representation>. This coefficient includes the gauge-field and <Faddeev-Popov ghost field> loops; it is the standard pure-gauge input when adding matter-loop contributions. Its negative sign gives <asymptotic freedom>. Integrating the leading equation gives $g(\mu)^{-2}=g(\mu_0)^{-2}+11C_A\log(\mu/\mu_0)/[24\pi^2]$, while perturbation theory is valid. Combining this term with the <adjoint Dirac contribution to the Yang-Mills beta function> gives the <integer obstruction to one-loop marginality with adjoint Dirac fermions>.

= Background gauge-field renormalization identity
{parent=Charge renormalization}
{title2=$Z_gZ_A^{1/2}=1$}

For a background <gauge field> with $A_0=Z_A^{1/2}A$ and $g_0=\mu^{\epsilon/2}Z_gg$, background gauge covariance preserves the combination $gA$ in the <gauge covariant derivative>. Its <Ward identity> therefore gives the displayed relation. This refers to background-field <renormalization>, not an unrestricted identity between the coupling and the ordinary quantum-gluon wave-function factor. Gauge-invariant one-loop matter determinants can be renormalized with this identity to extract their coupling running from vacuum polarization.

= Adjoint Dirac contribution to the Yang-Mills beta function
{parent=Charge renormalization}
{title2=$\beta_{\mathrm{Dirac,ad}}(g)=4C_Ag^3/[3(4\pi)^2]$}

One adjoint <Dirac spinor> contributes the displayed screening term to the four-dimensional <renormalization-group beta function> in the <modified minimal subtraction scheme>. The fermion contribution to the gluon <self-energy> insertion has pole $-8g^2C_A(k^2\delta_{\mu\nu}-k_\mu k_\nu)/(3\epsilon(4\pi)^2)$. The corresponding background gauge-field <counterterm> is $\delta Z_A=-8g^2C_A/(3\epsilon(4\pi)^2)$; the <background gauge-field renormalization identity> $Z_g=Z_A^{-1/2}$ gives $g_0=\mu^{\epsilon/2}(g+4C_Ag^3/[3\epsilon(4\pi)^2])$. Differentiation at fixed $g_0$ yields the screening coefficient. The mass-independent subtraction includes massive fields until effective-theory threshold matching is performed.

= Integer obstruction to one-loop marginality with adjoint Dirac fermions
{parent=Adjoint Dirac contribution to the Yang-Mills beta function}

For a non-Abelian simple gauge factor with $C_A>0$, pure <Yang-Mills theory> contributes $-11C_Ag^3/[3(4\pi)^2]$. Adding $N_D$ adjoint <Dirac spinors> changes this to $-C_A(11-4N_D)g^3/[3(4\pi)^2]$. Vanishing would require $N_D=11/4$, which is not a nonnegative integer. This excludes cancellation by these fermions alone, apart from the trivial value $g=0$; other matter representations or fields change the coefficient.

= Regularization in quantum field theory
{parent=Renormalization}

Regularization modifies divergent loop integrals by introducing an auxiliary parameter. The regulator is removed after its dependence has been absorbed into <counterterms>.

= Ultraviolet divergence
{parent=Regularization in quantum field theory}
{wiki}

An ultraviolet divergence comes from arbitrarily large loop momentum, equivalently from singular short-distance behavior. Locality makes its regulator dependence cancellable by local counterterms in a renormalizable theory.

= Ultraviolet subdivergence
{parent=Ultraviolet divergence}

= Subdivergence
{synonym}

An ultraviolet <subdivergence> arises when <loop momenta> internal to a proper subgraph tend to infinity while the momenta entering that subgraph are held fixed. It can occur even if simultaneous scaling of all momenta of the full <Feynman diagram> is convergent. Recursive local <counterterms> subtract these subgraph divergences before the remaining overall divergence is analyzed. A <tadpole diagram> inserted into an otherwise convergent six-point <phi-fourth theory> <Feynman diagram> is a simple massive example.

= Cutoff regularization
{parent=Regularization in quantum field theory}
{wiki}

Cutoff regularization restricts loop momenta to $|\ell|<\Lambda$. The ultraviolet cutoff $\Lambda$ makes individual integrals finite while displaying power and logarithmic ultraviolet divergences explicitly.

= Dimensional regularization
{parent=Regularization in quantum field theory}
{wiki}

Dimensional regularization analytically continues loop integrals from an integer spacetime dimension to $d=d_0-\epsilon$. Ultraviolet logarithms then appear as poles in $1/\epsilon$.

= Euclidean massive loop integral
{c}
{parent=Dimensional regularization}
{title2=$I_{d,n}(m)$}

For $m^2>0$ and $n>d/2$, <Schwinger parameterization> and a <Gaussian integral> give $I_{d,n}(m)=\int d^dk\,(2\pi)^{-d}(k^2+m^2)^{-n}=\Gamma(n-d/2)(m^2)^{d/2-n}/[(4\pi)^{d/2}\Gamma(n)]$. Its meromorphic continuation supplies <dimensional regularization> poles.

= Massive scalar bubble pole in four dimensions
{parent=Euclidean massive loop integral}
{title2=$\int\frac{d^{4-\epsilon}k}{(2\pi)^{4-\epsilon}}\frac1{(k^2+m^2)((p-k)^2+m^2)}=\frac1{8\pi^2\epsilon}+O(1)$}

For positive $m^2$, a <Feynman parameter> and a momentum shift turn the integral into $\int_0^1I_{d,2}(\sqrt{m^2+x(1-x)p^2})\,dx$. The <Euclidean massive loop integral> has factor $\Gamma(\epsilon/2)=2/\epsilon+O(1)$, while its other factors tend to $(4\pi)^{-2}$. The pole is independent of the external <momentum> and the <mass>, so it is removable by a local <counterterm>.

= Minimal subtraction scheme
{title2=$\mathrm{MS}$ scheme}
{parent=Dimensional regularization}
{wiki}

The minimal subtraction scheme chooses <counterterms> that remove only the poles in the dimensional regulator $\epsilon$, without additional finite terms.

= Simple-pole formulas for minimal-subtraction renormalization group functions
{parent=Minimal subtraction scheme}
{title2=$\beta(\lambda)=(\lambda\partial_\lambda-1)f_1(\lambda),\quad\gamma_{m^2}(\lambda)=\lambda b_1'(\lambda)$}

In the <minimal subtraction scheme>, let $\lambda_B=\mu^\epsilon F$, $F=\lambda+\sum_{k\geq1}f_k(\lambda)/\epsilon^k$, and $m_B^2=m^2H$, $H=1+\sum_{k\geq1}b_k(\lambda)/\epsilon^k$. Define the <renormalization-group beta function> at fixed bare parameters by $\widehat\beta=\mu d\lambda/d\mu=-\epsilon\lambda+\beta(\lambda)$, and the <running mass> function by $\gamma_{m^2}=\mu d\log m^2/d\mu$.

The bare coupling is scale independent, so $0=\epsilon F+\widehat\beta F'$. Its finite coefficient is $f_1+\beta-\lambda f_1'$, giving the first formula. Similarly $0=\gamma_{m^2}H+\widehat\beta H'$ has finite coefficient $\gamma_{m^2}-\lambda b_1'$. The remaining poles cancel recursively: $(\lambda\partial_\lambda-1)f_{k+1}=\beta f_k'$ and $\lambda b_{k+1}'=\gamma_{m^2}b_k+\beta b_k'$. Thus the simple poles determine the finite <renormalization group> functions, while higher poles obey consistency conditions.

{parent=Dimensional regularization}

= Modified minimal subtraction
{synonym}

The modified minimal subtraction scheme removes the dimensional pole together with the universal constants conventionally grouped as $-\gamma+\log4\pi$. This simplifies renormalization-group formulas while differing from minimal subtraction only by finite parameter redefinitions.

= Electron-positron annihilation into two photons
{parent=Quantum electrodynamics}
{title2=$e^-e^+\to\gamma\gamma$}

The leading <scattering amplitude> is the sum of two <tree-level Feynman diagrams>, with the outgoing photons attached in either order to the fermion line. The <Dirac propagator> carries momentum $p-k_1$ or $p-k_2$ for incoming electron momentum $p$. Their sum obeys the <two-photon fermion Ward identity>, although individual diagrams need not. <Crossing symmetry> relates this amplitude to <Compton scattering>.

= Muon-antimuon annihilation into two photons
{parent=Quantum electrodynamics}

At tree level this <QED> process has two internal <muon> exchange diagrams, differing by the order of the two outgoing <photon> vertices. Their propagator momenta are $p_1-k_1$ and $p_1-k_2$ for incoming <muon> momentum $p_1$. The amplitude begins at order $e^2$ and obeys the <two-photon fermion Ward identity>. Identical final <photons> require the usual factorial correction when integration over the full final-state <Lorentz-invariant phase space> would otherwise count them twice.

= Fine-structure constant

In rationalized <natural units>, the <fine-structure constant> is $\alpha=e^2/(4\pi)$. It measures the electromagnetic interaction strength. Its use in a specified perturbative calculation requires a consistent coupling and <renormalization scale> convention.

= Minimal electromagnetic coupling of a Dirac field
{parent=Quantum electrodynamics}
{title2=$\mathcal L_{\rm int}=-qA_\mu\bar\Psi\gamma^\mu\Psi$}

Replace the derivative in the <Dirac action> by the <gauge covariant derivative> $D_\mu=\partial_\mu+iqA_\mu$. The interaction is then $-qA_\mu\bar\Psi\gamma^\mu\Psi$. With $\Psi\mapsto e^{-iq\alpha}\Psi$ and $A_\mu\mapsto A_\mu+\partial_\mu\alpha$, the covariant derivative transforms in the same way as the spinor, proving local <gauge invariance>.

= Gauge covariance of the charged Dirac equation
{parent=Minimal electromagnetic coupling of a Dirac field}
{title2=$D'_\mu\Psi'=e^{-iq\alpha}D_\mu\Psi$}

The derivative of the local phase in $\Psi\mapsto e^{-iq\alpha}\Psi$ cancels the added gradient in the electromagnetic potential. Thus $D'_\mu\Psi'=e^{-iq\alpha}D_\mu\Psi$, and the charged <Dirac equation> maps solutions to solutions. The same cancellation makes its action gauge invariant.

= Electron-positron annihilation into a muon pair
{parent=Quantum electrodynamics}
{title2=$e^-e^+\to\mu^-\mu^+$}

At tree level, the <Feynman diagram> has an electron-positron current and a muon-antimuon current joined by one virtual <photon>. In the massless limit, the <spin average> and final-state <spin sum> give $\overline{|\mathcal M|^2}=e^4(1+\cos^2\theta)$, and the integrated <relativistic scattering cross-section> is $4\pi\alpha^2/(3s)$.

= QED Feynman rules
{c}
{parent=Quantum electrodynamics}

The QED Feynman rules combine the <Dirac propagator>, <photon propagator>, and vertex $-ie\gamma^\mu$ with external Dirac spinors and photon polarization vectors. In a covariant gauge they generate perturbative electron-photon scattering amplitudes.

= Vertex parity of a QED amplitude
{parent=QED Feynman rules}
{title2=$V=2I_\gamma+E_\gamma$}

Each elementary <QED> vertex has one photon leg, so counting these legs gives $V=2I_\gamma+E_\gamma$. An amplitude with two external photons has even $V$. In particular <Compton scattering> has no cubic-order elementary-vertex contribution. With four external legs and $V=4$, a connected diagram has four internal lines and one loop.

= Electron-positron annihilation into a quark pair
{parent=QED Feynman rules}
{title2=$\sigma_q=4\pi\alpha^2Q_q^2/s$}

For massless quarks at photon-exchange tree level, the initial-spin-averaged, final-color-summed amplitude is $\overline{|\mathcal M|^2}=8N_c e^4Q_q^2[(p_1\cdot q_1)(p_2\cdot q_2)+(p_1\cdot q_2)(p_2\cdot q_1)]/s^2$. In the centre-of-mass frame this is $N_c e^4Q_q^2(1+\cos^2\theta)$. With $N_c=3$ and the standard two-body flux, its integrated cross-section is the displayed result.

= Perturbative interactions in scalar electrodynamics

= Scalar particle-antiparticle tree scattering
{parent=Perturbative interactions in scalar electrodynamics}
{title2=$\mathcal M=e^2[(u-t)/(s+i0)-(s-u)/(t+i0)]$}

At order $e^2$, charged-scalar particle-antiparticle scattering has $s$ annihilation and $t$ photon exchange. For the convention $D=\partial+ieA$ and incoming momenta $p_1,p_2$, outgoing $p_3,p_4$, its amplitude is
$$
\mathcal M=e^2\left[\frac{(p_1-p_2)\cdot(p_3-p_4)}{s+i0}-\frac{(p_1+p_3)\cdot(p_2+p_4)}{t+i0}\right].
$$
Equal external masses turn the numerators into $u-t$ and $s-u$. The <scalar electrodynamics three-point vertex> gives conserved currents, allowing the <Coulomb-gauge propagator between conserved currents> identity to replace each propagator by the covariant one. The <seagull vertex> has only two scalar legs and does not give a four-scalar tree at this order.

= Scalar electrodynamics three-point vertex

With $D=\partial+ieA$, the vertex for an incoming charged particle of momentum $p$ and an outgoing one of momentum $p'$ is $-ie(p+p')^\mu$. The antiparticle has the opposite charge-flow sign. The quadratic term $e^2A^2\phi^*\phi$ gives the separate <seagull vertex> $2ie^2\eta^{\mu\nu}$. On equal-mass external shells, contracting the three-point momentum with the photon momentum gives zero, the tree <scalar quantum electrodynamics Ward identity>.

= Three-photon fermion Ward identity
{parent=Ward identity}
{title2=$\mathcal A(\epsilon_\ell\to q_\ell)=0$}

For tree <electron>-<positron> annihilation into three <photons>, all six orders along the <fermion> line must be summed. Write $D(r)=\not r-m$ and $S(r)=D(r)^{-1}=(\not r+m)/(r^2-m^2)$. Contracting an internal photon with its momentum gives $S(R)\not q S(r)=S(R)-S(r)$ when $q=r-R$. Contracting an end photon removes its adjacent <Dirac propagator> by the external <Dirac equation>, with a minus sign at the electron end and a plus sign at the positron end. For either fixed order of the two uncontracted photons, the three insertion positions therefore telescope to zero. Summing their two orders proves the <Ward identity>. The mass terms in both remaining <Dirac propagators> are essential when $m\ne0$. The result expresses the invariance of the <scattering amplitude> under $\epsilon_\ell\mapsto\epsilon_\ell+cq_\ell$.

= Proper-vertex Ward-Takahashi identity in QED
{parent=Ward identity}
{title2=$k_\mu\Gamma^\mu(p+k,p)=S^{-1}(p+k)-S^{-1}(p)$}

Define the proper electromagnetic vertex with the <electric charge> removed, and define $S^{-1}$ without the propagator's overall factor $i$. The displayed off-shell identity follows by localizing the vector symmetry in a time-ordered correlation function, including the contact terms at the charged-field insertions, and amputating. Its on-shell form gives the <Ward identity> for physical <photon> amplitudes. At zero <photon> momentum it identifies the vertex with the momentum derivative of the inverse <fermion> propagator.

= Ward identity for QED renormalization constants

= Two-photon fermion Ward identity

= Power counting in quantum field theory

Power counting assigns mass dimensions to fields and couplings. An interaction is relevant, marginal or irrelevant when its coupling has positive, zero or negative mass dimension, respectively.

= Truncation error in effective field theory
{parent=Power counting in quantum field theory}

The <truncation error in effective field theory> is the uncertainty from omitted orders of a stated energy, derivative or loop expansion. It is commonly estimated from the first omitted terms using an explicit assumption about <Wilson coefficients>. = Mass dimension
{title2=$[X]$}
{parent=Power counting in quantum field theory}

In units with $\hbar=c=1$, the mass dimension $[X]$ is the exponent with which a quantity scales under a common rescaling of energies and momenta. In $d$ spacetime dimensions a Lagrangian density has mass dimension $d$.

= Superficial degree of divergence
{title2=$D$}
{parent=Power counting in quantum field theory}
{wiki}

The superficial degree of divergence $D$ is the ultraviolet power predicted by rescaling all independent loop momenta together. For a scalar graph with $L$ loops and $I$ propagators behaving as $p^{-2}$ in $d$ dimensions, $D=dL-2I$ before cancellations and subdivergences are considered.

= Monomial scalar interaction power-counting identity
{parent=Superficial degree of divergence}
{title2=$\Delta=d-(d-2)E/2-[\lambda]V$}

For a connected scalar graph with nonderivative interaction $-\lambda\phi^n$, $E$ external legs and $V$ vertices, $nV=2I+E$ and $L=I-V+1$. Since $[\phi]=(d-2)/2$ and $[\lambda]=d-n(d-2)/2$, the <superficial degree of divergence> is
$$
\Delta=dL-2I=d-\frac{d-2}{2}E-[\lambda]V.
$$
The graph contributes at order $\lambda^V$. This is simultaneous large-loop-momentum counting for an amputated graph; it does not test subdivergences or guarantee that the predicted pole coefficient is nonzero.

= A superficially convergent graph can have a divergent subgraph
{parent=Superficial degree of divergence}

In massive four-dimensional <phi-fourth theory>, a six-point triangle is <one-particle irreducible> and has superficial degree $-2$. Insert a <tadpole diagram> self-energy into one of its internal lines. The resulting graph is still one-particle irreducible and still has degree $-2$, but its tadpole loop diverges. Recursive local <counterterms> remove the subgraph divergence before overall power counting is applied. Convergence requires attention to all ultraviolet subgraphs, not merely to simultaneous scaling of all loop momenta.

= Derivative-interaction scalar power counting
{parent=Superficial degree of divergence}
{title2=$D=d-(d-2)E/2-V[\lambda]$}

For interaction $\lambda\Phi^n(\partial\Phi)^r$ with a two-derivative kinetic term, each vertex has $n+r$ legs and $r$ momentum powers. Since $[\Phi]=(d-2)/2$, its coupling has dimension $[\lambda]=d-(n+r)(d-2)/2-r$. The graph identities $2I+E=(n+r)V$ and $L=I-V+1$, inserted into $D=dL-2I+rV$, prove the displayed formula. It is an upper bound on ultraviolet scaling; momentum contractions and symmetry can cause cancellations.

= Scalar graph power-counting identity
{parent=Superficial degree of divergence}
{title2=$D=d-(d/2-1)E-\sum_n[n-d(n/2-1)]V_n$}

For a connected <scalar field theory> graph without derivative vertices, each independent <loop momentum> supplies $d$ powers and each internal <scalar propagator> removes two. Thus $D=dL-2I$. The graph identities $L=I-\sum_nV_n+1$ and $2I+E=\sum_n nV_n$ give the displayed formula. The <mass dimension> of the field is $(d-2)/2$ and that of $g_n$ is $n-d(n/2-1)$. Negative-dimensional couplings allow the superficial degree to grow with vertex count. This is overall <power counting in quantum field theory>; subgraphs can diverge even when the whole graph has negative degree.
