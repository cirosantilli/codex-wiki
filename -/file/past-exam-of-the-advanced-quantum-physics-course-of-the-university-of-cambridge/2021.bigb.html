= Solution
{parent=1}

The initial <quantum state> is a <coherent state>. Since $a|\alpha\rangle=\alpha|\alpha\rangle$ and the state is normalized, its mean <occupation number> is $N=\langle a^\dagger a\rangle=|\alpha|^2$. Each <quantum harmonic oscillator> <energy eigenstate> acquires the phase $e^{-i\omega(n+1/2)t}$, so
$$
|\alpha(t)\rangle=e^{-i\omega t/2}|\alpha e^{-i\omega t}\rangle,
\qquad \langle a\rangle_t=\alpha e^{-i\omega t}.
$$
The prefactor is a <global phase> and does not change an <expectation value>. Inverting the definitions of the <annihilation operator> and <creation operator> gives $x=\sqrt{\hbar/(2m\omega)}(a+a^\dagger)$. If $\alpha=\sqrt N e^{i\varphi}$, then
$$
\boxed{\langle x\rangle_t=\sqrt{\frac{2\hbar N}{m\omega}}\cos(\omega t-\varphi).}
$$
For a classical <harmonic oscillator> with amplitude $A$, the conserved <energy> is $E=m\omega^2A^2/2$. Setting $E=\hbar\omega N$ gives exactly the same amplitude, $A=\sqrt{2\hbar N/(m\omega)}$. Thus these <coherent-state oscillator expectation values> follow the classical orbit.

The full quantum mean <energy> is nevertheless $\langle H\rangle=\hbar\omega(N+1/2)$: the extra <zero-point energy> belongs to the nonzero position and momentum uncertainties required by the <Heisenberg uncertainty relation>, rather than to the motion of the packet centre. In particular, $N=0$ gives zero mean displacement but a finite ground-state width. A <coherent state> follows a classical mean trajectory while retaining finite position and momentum uncertainties.

Use the uncoupled <angular momentum eigenstates> $|m_L,\uparrow\rangle$ and $|m_L,\downarrow\rangle$, with $m_L=-1,0,1$. For the <Hamiltonian operator> actually written in the paper, these are the <energy eigenstates>, with
$$
E=\mu_BB_z(m_L+2m_S),\qquad m_S=\pm\frac12.
$$
The initial $J=3/2$ states are not all <energy eigenstates> of this operator. To find their energy populations, first perform the <Clebsch-Gordan decomposition>. The extremal states are $|3/2,3/2\rangle=|1,\uparrow\rangle$ and $|3/2,-3/2\rangle=|-1,\downarrow\rangle$. Applying the total <angular momentum lowering operator> to the first gives
$$
\sqrt3\,|3/2,1/2\rangle=\sqrt2\,|0,\uparrow\rangle+|1,\downarrow\rangle.
$$
Similarly,
$$
|3/2,-1/2\rangle=\sqrt{\frac23}|0,\downarrow\rangle+\sqrt{\frac13}|-1,\uparrow\rangle.
$$
The initial preparation is an equal incoherent mixture of the four coupled states. Each extremal state contributes population $1/4$ to its one <energy eigenvalue>, while each nonextremal state contributes $2/3$ and $1/3$ of its own $1/4$ population to the two displayed components. The resulting <Zeeman splitting> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|ccccc}
E/(\mu_BB_z)&-2&-1&0&1&2\\ \hline
\text{fraction of atoms}&1/4&1/6&1/6&1/6&1/4\\
\text{energy multiplicity}&1&1&2&1&1
\end{array}}
$$
for nonzero $B_z$. The relative populations are $\boxed{3:2:2:2:3}$ in this order; the two zero-energy states each have population $1/12$. These <Zeeman populations from an unpolarized angular-momentum multiplet> remain constant under subsequent evolution by the stated static <Hamiltonian operator>, although off-diagonal <quantum coherence> can evolve.

There is a distinct weak-field model in which a retained <spin-orbit interaction> keeps $J=3/2$ isolated and one projects the magnetic perturbation into that quartet. The <Landé g-factor> is then $4/3$, giving four shifts $\mu_BB_z(\pm2,\pm2/3)$ with equal populations. That calculation requires the additional projection or dominant <spin-orbit interaction>; it is not diagonalization of the full magnetic <Hamiltonian operator> specified here, which also mixes the $J=1/2$ sector.

A confined <ground state> requires $k>0$; take $\alpha>0$ so the trial <wavefunction> is normalizable. Its normalized form is
$$
f_\alpha(x)=\left(\frac\alpha\pi\right)^{1/4}e^{-\alpha x^2/2}.
$$
The <Gaussian moment recurrence> gives $\langle x^2\rangle=1/(2\alpha)$ and $\langle x^4\rangle=3/(4\alpha^2)$. Also, <integration by parts> gives $\langle p^2\rangle=\hbar^2\int|f_\alpha'|^2dx=\hbar^2\alpha/2$. The <Rayleigh quotient> for the <quartic oscillator> is consequently
$$
\mathcal E(\alpha)=\frac{\hbar^2\alpha}{4m}+\frac{3k}{4\alpha^2}.
$$
Differentiation yields $\mathcal E'(\alpha)=\hbar^2/(4m)-3k/(2\alpha^3)$, so the unique minimum is at $\alpha_*=(6mk/\hbar^2)^{1/3}$. Since the quotient diverges at both endpoints $0$ and $\infty$, this is the global minimum in the trial family. The <Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle> gives
$$
\boxed{E_0\leq\mathcal E(\alpha_*)
=\frac{3\hbar^2}{8m}\left(\frac{6mk}{\hbar^2}\right)^{1/3}.}
$$
For the first excited <energy eigenstate>, choose the odd trial family
$$
\boxed{g_\beta(x)=\left(\frac{4\beta^3}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}x e^{-\beta x^2/2},\qquad\beta>0.}
$$
The even confining potential has an even, nodeless <ground state>, by the <nodeless theorem for a one-dimensional ground state>. By the <Sturm oscillation theorem>, its first excited state is odd with one node. The proposed family has the same <parity operator> eigenvalue and one node, and is orthogonal to the exact ground state, not merely to its Gaussian trial approximation. Minimization in the odd <linear subspace> therefore bounds the first excited energy from above. For completeness, its <Rayleigh quotient> is $3\hbar^2\beta/(4m)+15k/(4\beta^2)$, minimized at $\beta^3=10mk/\hbar^2$, giving $E_1\leq(9\hbar^2/8m)(10mk/\hbar^2)^{1/3}$. These are <Gaussian variational bounds for a quartic oscillator>.

If $k=0$, there is no normalizable <free particle> ground state on the whole line, though the spectral infimum is zero. If $k<0$, the <Hamiltonian operator> is not bounded below, so a ground-energy bound of the requested kind does not exist.

Start with the <Schrödinger equation> $i\hbar\partial_t|\psi\rangle=(H_0+V)|\psi\rangle$ and differentiate the definition of the <interaction picture> state:
$$
\begin{aligned}
i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}\left(e^{iH_0t/\hbar}|\psi\rangle\right)
&=-H_0e^{iH_0t/\hbar}|\psi\rangle
+e^{iH_0t/\hbar}(H_0+V)|\psi\rangle\\
&=e^{iH_0t/\hbar}Ve^{-iH_0t/\hbar}|\psi_I\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$
The free <Hamiltonian operator> terms cancel because $H_0$ commutes with its own <matrix exponential>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}|\psi_I(t)\rangle=V_I(t)|\psi_I(t)\rangle,
\qquad V_I(t)=e^{iH_0t/\hbar}V(t)e^{-iH_0t/\hbar}.}
$$
This <interaction picture> transformation is exact.

Write $\omega_z=\gamma B_z$ and use the ordered <spin> basis $(|\uparrow\rangle,|\downarrow\rangle)$. The free <Hamiltonian operator> is $H_0=-\hbar\omega_z\sigma_z/2$, so
$$
V_I(t)=-\frac{\gamma\hbar B_x}{2}\cos(\omega t)
\begin{pmatrix}0&e^{-i\omega_zt}\\e^{i\omega_zt}&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Resolving the cosine into its two complex exponentials gives the exact upper off-diagonal factor
$$
\cos(\omega t)e^{-i\omega_zt}
=\frac12\left[e^{i(\omega-\omega_z)t}+e^{-i(\omega+\omega_z)t}\right],
$$
with the complex conjugate in the lower entry. Choose the field orientation with $\omega_z>0$, as implicit in the printed near-resonance condition, and put $\delta=\omega-\omega_z$. The <rotating-wave approximation> discards the terms at $\omega+\omega_z$, retaining the slow terms at $\delta$. The resulting <interaction picture> equations are
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dt}\begin{pmatrix}c_1\\c_2\end{pmatrix}
=\frac{i\gamma B_x}{4}
\begin{pmatrix}0&e^{i\delta t}\\e^{-i\delta t}&0\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}c_1\\c_2\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The factor $1/4$ contains $1/2$ from $S_x=\hbar\sigma_x/2$ and $1/2$ from the cosine decomposition. This is a <driven two-level Rabi model>, with signed on-resonance coupling $\Omega=\gamma B_x/2$ and generalized <Rabi angular frequency> $\omega_R=\sqrt{\delta^2+\Omega^2}$. Here the <Rabi detuning> uses the paper's convention, drive minus free frequency.

Small detuning alone is insufficient to derive the printed equations. One also requires $|\gamma B_x|,|\delta|\ll\omega+\omega_z$, and an evolution time over which neglected terms stay small. At resonance this is the weak transverse-field regime $|B_x|\ll|B_z|$. The supplied solution is thus an approximate solution for the linearly oscillating field, not an exact one at arbitrary drive strength. If $\gamma B_z<0$, the other exponential is resonant and the physical positive resonance frequency is $|\gamma B_z|$.

With $\Omega=\gamma B_x/2$, the <Born rule> applied to the supplied down-spin <probability amplitude> gives
$$
P_\downarrow(t)=\frac{\Omega^2}{\delta^2+\Omega^2}
\sin^2\left(\frac{\sqrt{\delta^2+\Omega^2}\,t}{2}\right).
$$
For nonzero transverse field, the maxima occur at
$$
\boxed{t_j=\frac{(2j+1)\pi}{\omega_R},\quad j=0,1,\ldots,
\qquad P_{\max}(\omega)=\frac{\Omega^2}{(\omega-\omega_z)^2+\Omega^2}.}
$$
The first maximum is reached at $\pi/\omega_R$; on resonance this is a <Rabi pi pulse>. The <resonance envelope of a Rabi transition> is a <Lorentzian line shape>, centred on $\omega_{\mathrm{res}}=\omega_z$ under the orientation convention above. It has half its peak value at $|\omega-\omega_z|=|\Omega|$, so its <full width at half maximum> is
$$
\boxed{\Delta\omega=2|\Omega|=|\gamma B_x|,
\qquad\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{res}}}{\Delta\omega}=\frac{|B_z|}{|B_x|}.}
$$
If resonance width is instead defined as the half-width, the ratio differs by a factor of two but has the same requested proportionality. The <quality factor> is large precisely in the regime where the <rotating-wave approximation> is justified. The envelope is obtained by choosing the best time separately at each drive frequency; a measurement at one fixed pulse duration has a different line profile.

At resonance, retain the signed coupling $\Omega=\gamma B_x/2$. In the <interaction picture>, the given solution reduces to $c_1=\cos(\Omega t/2)$ and $c_2=i\sin(\Omega t/2)$. Returning to the <Schrödinger picture> gives
$$
|\psi(t)\rangle=e^{i\omega_zt/2}\cos(\Omega t/2)|\uparrow\rangle
+i e^{-i\omega_zt/2}\sin(\Omega t/2)|\downarrow\rangle.
$$
For a normalized spinor $(a,b)^T$, the <Pauli matrices> give $\langle\boldsymbol\sigma\rangle=(2\operatorname{Re}a^*b,2\operatorname{Im}a^*b,|a|^2-|b|^2)$. Therefore the <spin> <expectation value> within the <rotating-wave approximation> is
$$
\boxed{\langle\mathbf S\rangle_t=\frac\hbar2
\begin{pmatrix}
\sin(\Omega t)\sin(\omega_zt)\\
\sin(\Omega t)\cos(\omega_zt)\\
\cos(\Omega t)
\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Its magnitude is $\hbar/2$: the <Bloch vector> stays on the unit sphere. In a frame rotating about $z$ at angular velocity $-\omega_z$, it makes a uniform rotation in the $yz$ plane. For $|B_x|\ll|B_z|$, this gives slow <Rabi oscillations> between the poles, accompanied by rapid laboratory <Larmor precession> about $z$.

If the same formula is formally used for $|B_z|\ll|B_x|$, it predicts fast meridional rotations whose plane turns slowly about $z$. However, the original PDF really does ask for this opposite limit, and it lies outside the assumption used in part (b). That formal description is not a controlled prediction for the stated linearly driven <Hamiltonian operator>.

A valid strong-drive description starts from the exact equation for the <Bloch vector> $\mathbf s=2\langle\mathbf S\rangle/\hbar$:
$$
\frac{d\mathbf s}{dt}=\gamma\mathbf s\times\bigl(B_x\cos(\omega t),0,B_z\bigr).
$$
For times $|\gamma B_z|t\ll1$, neglecting the weak bias gives a controlled leading approximation, since the time integral of the omitted <Hamiltonian operator> has small norm. The remaining field has a fixed $x$ axis, so its generators commute at different times and
$$
\mathbf s(t)\simeq\bigl(0,\sin\Theta(t),\cos\Theta(t)\bigr),
\qquad\Theta(t)=\frac{\gamma B_x}{\omega}\sin(\omega t).
$$
At the nominal resonance $\omega=\gamma B_z$ and on the early spin-rotation scale $t\sim1/|\gamma B_x|$, this becomes $\Theta\simeq\gamma B_xt$. The initial rotation rate is consequently $\gamma B_x$, twice the rate predicted by extrapolating the <rotating-wave approximation>. For longer times the exact differential equation must be retained. The following original calculation illustrates both regimes rather than asserting an exact strong-drive Rabi formula.

The components of the <momentum operator> do not generally commute with a position-dependent <magnetic vector potential>. Expanding <minimal coupling> with the ordering intact gives
$$
H=\frac{p^2}{2m}+V+
\frac e{2m}\bigl(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf A+\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf p\bigr)
+\frac{e^2}{2m}\mathbf A^2.
$$
Acting on a <wavefunction> shows that $\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf A-\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf p=-i\hbar\nabla\cdot\mathbf A$. In the <Coulomb gauge> the divergence vanishes, so the two cross terms agree. Discarding the quadratic term in the weak radiation amplitude gives
$$
\boxed{H=H_0+H_1(t)+O(A^2),\qquad
H_0=\frac{p^2}{2m}+V,\qquad
H_1(t)=\frac em\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf A(\mathbf r,t).}
$$
The equality of the two operator orderings follows from the gauge condition, not from treating $\mathbf p$ and $\mathbf A$ as commuting numbers component by component.

= Solution

For the requested oscillator response, treat $y$ as the fixed transverse position of a one-dimensional <quantum harmonic oscillator> along $z$, and put $\theta=ky$. The supplied radiation is transverse and already satisfies the <Coulomb gauge>. Its perturbation is
$$
H_1(t)=\frac{eA_0}{m}p_z\left(e^{i\theta}e^{-i\omega t}+e^{-i\theta}e^{i\omega t}\right),\quad t>0.
$$
The <annihilation operator> and <creation operator> give
$$
p_z=i\sqrt{\frac{m\hbar\omega_0}{2}}(a^\dagger-a),\qquad
\langle n|p_z|0\rangle=i\sqrt{\frac{m\hbar\omega_0}{2}}\,\delta_{n1}.
$$
Thus the only first-order transition out of the <ground state> is to $|1\rangle$. More generally, the oscillator <electric-dipole selection rule> is $\Delta n=\pm1$. This is a rule for <matrix elements>; exact energy conservation is replaced by a finite-time resonance profile during a switched finite-duration interaction.

To display both the resonant and nonresonant terms without spurious singularities, define
$$
F_\nu(t)=\int_0^t e^{i\nu t'}dt'
=\begin{cases}(e^{i\nu t}-1)/(i\nu),&\nu\ne0,\\t,&\nu=0,\end{cases}
\qquad C= eA_0\sqrt{\frac{\omega_0}{2m\hbar}}.
$$
The <interaction picture> coefficient in the given expansion is then
$$
\boxed{b_1(t)=C\left[e^{i\theta}F_{\omega_0-\omega}(t)
+e^{-i\theta}F_{\omega_0+\omega}(t)\right],\qquad b_n(t)=0\ (n\geq2).}
$$
The full <Schrödinger picture> coefficient of $|1\rangle$ is $e^{-3i\omega_0t/2}b_1(t)$, while that of $|0\rangle$ is $e^{-i\omega_0t/2}$ at this order. The normalization changes only at order $A_0^2$. In particular, at $\omega=\omega_0$ the first term is $Ce^{i\theta}t$, finite for every finite time, and the perturbative result requires $|b_1|\ll1$.

The fixed-$y$ interpretation is needed for the displayed target formulae. If $p^2$ were instead a three-dimensional kinetic operator with no transverse confinement, that model would have no normalizable transverse <ground state>, and $e^{\pm iky}$ would transfer transverse <momentum> $\pm\hbar k$. A treatment of that different model needs specified transverse states and recoil energies; they are absent from the question. The following parts use the intended localized-oscillator response.

The only relevant position <matrix element> is $z_{01}=\sqrt{\hbar/(2m\omega_0)}$. To first order in the radiation amplitude, the two ground–excited-state cross terms give
$$
\langle z\rangle_t=2\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega_0}}
\operatorname{Re}\left[e^{-i\omega_0t}b_1(t)\right].
$$
Multiplying by the charge $-e$ gives the induced <electric dipole moment>. Since $2\sqrt{\hbar/(2m\omega_0)}C=eA_0/m$, the coefficient derived in part (b) yields
$$
\mathbf P(t)=-\frac{e^2A_0}{m}\hat{\mathbf z}\operatorname{Re}\left[
 e^{i\theta}\frac{e^{-i\omega t}-e^{-i\omega_0t}}{i(\omega_0-\omega)}
+e^{-i\theta}\frac{e^{i\omega t}-e^{-i\omega_0t}}{i(\omega_0+\omega)}\right]
$$
at linear order. Taking the complex conjugate inside the real part of the second term changes it to minus the same term with $e^{i\theta}$ and reversed frequency signs. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbf P(t)=-\frac{e^2A_0}{m}\hat{\mathbf z}\operatorname{Re}\left[e^{iky}\left(
\frac{e^{-i\omega t}-e^{-i\omega_0t}}{i(\omega_0-\omega)}
-\frac{e^{-i\omega t}-e^{i\omega_0t}}{i(\omega_0+\omega)}\right)\right].}
$$
The terms at $\omega_0$ are switch-on transients, while those at $\omega$ describe the driven response. Both are needed to make $\mathbf P(0)=0$. The expression is understood by continuity at $\omega=\omega_0$; the first quotient then tends to $t e^{-i\omega_0t}$, producing resonant secular growth rather than an infinite dipole at a finite time.

There is also an initial-velocity check on this <harmonic-oscillator dipole response to switched radiation>. The formula gives $\dot P_z(0^+)=-2e^2A_0\cos(ky)/m$. Because the <magnetic vector potential> jumps at zero time, its time derivative contains an impulsive <electric field>. Equivalently, the mechanical velocity is $(p_z+eA_z)/m$, whose initial mean is $2eA_0\cos(ky)/m$. The transient therefore implements the specified switch-on correctly.

For $\omega\ne\omega_0$, isolate the terms proportional to $e^{-i\omega t}$ in the previous answer. Their coefficient is
$$
\frac1{i(\omega_0-\omega)}-\frac1{i(\omega_0+\omega)}
=-\frac{2i\omega}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2}.
$$
Hence the driven <electric dipole moment> component is
$$
\mathbf P_\omega(t)=-\frac{2e^2A_0\omega}{m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)}
\sin(ky-\omega t)\hat{\mathbf z}.
$$
For $t>0$, away from the instantaneous switch, the radiation <electric field> is
$$
\mathbf E(t)=-\partial_t\mathbf A
=-2A_0\omega\sin(ky-\omega t)\hat{\mathbf z}.
$$
Thus the <dynamic electric polarizability> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf P_\omega=\alpha_{\mathrm{dyn}}(\omega)\mathbf E,
\qquad\alpha_{\mathrm{dyn}}(\omega)=\frac{e^2}{m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)}.}
$$
Below resonance the driven <electric dipole moment> is in phase with the <electric field>; above resonance its sign reverses. The same susceptibility follows from the classical <Lorentz oscillator model> without damping. At exact resonance the separation into bounded driven and transient terms fails: their apparent poles combine into the finite-time secular term identified in part (c). Without damping there is no bounded steady resonant response, and the first-order state expansion eventually ceases to apply.

Inside the <infinite square well>, the orbital and <spin> operators act on different factors of the <tensor product>. Write $\mathbf S=\mathbf S_1+\mathbf S_2$. The identity
$$
\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2=\frac12\left(S^2-S_1^2-S_2^2\right)
=\frac12\left(S^2-\frac32\hbar^2\right)
$$
shows that the <exchange interaction> commutes with $S^2$ and $S_z$. The sum of the one-particle kinetic energies also commutes with them and with particle exchange. Thus one may use orbital <quantum numbers> together with the <total-spin sector> $S=0,1$ and projection $M=-S,\ldots,S$, with physical $S_z=\hbar M$.

For identical <spin-one-half> particles the <Pauli exclusion principle> further restricts the states. The <spin-one-half singlet state> is antisymmetric in <spin> and must multiply a symmetric spatial <wavefunction>; a <spin-one-half triplet state> must multiply an antisymmetric spatial <wavefunction>. Thus $n_1,n_2$ label unordered orbital occupations in symmetry-adapted states, not arbitrary ordered product states. This gives the labels $|n_1,n_2,S,M\rangle$, with the paper's last label expressed in units of $\hbar$.

Let
$$
u_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac2a}\sin\left[\frac{n\pi(x+a/2)}a\right],\quad n=1,2,\ldots,
\qquad \varepsilon=\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2},\quad J=\lambda\hbar^2.
$$
The normalized orbital <energy eigenstates> vanish outside the well and have one-particle energies $n^2\varepsilon$. Define the normalized symmetric and antisymmetric combinations for $r\ne s$ by
$$
\Phi_{rs}^\pm=\frac{u_r(x_1)u_s(x_2)\pm u_s(x_1)u_r(x_2)}{\sqrt2},
\qquad\Phi_{rr}^+=u_r(x_1)u_r(x_2).
$$
The normalized spin functions are the <singlet state> $\chi_{00}=(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle-|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle)/\sqrt2$ and the <triplet states> $\chi_{11}=|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle$, $\chi_{10}=(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle+|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle)/\sqrt2$, $\chi_{1,-1}=|\downarrow\downarrow\rangle$. Their exchange energies are respectively $3J/4$ and $-J/4$.

For $0<J\ll\varepsilon$, the three lowest distinct <energy levels> of the <exchange-coupled spin-one-half particles in an infinite square well> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{energy}&\text{multiplicity}&\text{normalized eigenfunctions}\\ \hline
2\varepsilon+3J/4&1&\Phi_{11}^+\chi_{00}\\
5\varepsilon-J/4&3&\Phi_{12}^-\chi_{1M},\ M=-1,0,1\\
5\varepsilon+3J/4&1&\Phi_{12}^+\chi_{00}
\end{array}}
$$
The spatially doubly occupied orbital can only occur with the <singlet state>. The next orbital sum is $1^2+2^2=5$, where either exchange symmetry is possible. The next unused sum is $2^2+2^2=8$, well separated under the small-coupling assumption. The paper's inequality is equivalent, up to a numerical factor, to $|J|\ll\varepsilon$; the factor $\hbar^2$ in $J$ is essential because the <spin> operators carry units of angular momentum.

The sign of $\lambda$ is not explicitly specified. The table assumes the usual intended positive coupling in the displayed minus-sign convention. For $J<0$ with $|J|\ll\varepsilon$, the same three sets of <eigenfunctions> occur, but the two rows at $5\varepsilon$ exchange order. At $J=0$ those rows merge into a single <energy level> of multiplicity four; if three distinct energies are requested exactly at that point, the third is $8\varepsilon$ with the single state $\Phi_{22}^+\chi_{00}$.

Each one-particle <energy eigenstate> has definite parity about the centre of the well, so $\langle n|x|n\rangle=0$. Consequently, a doubly occupied orbital has $\langle x_1x_2\rangle=0$. For distinct orbitals, the two off-diagonal exchange terms instead survive:
$$
\langle\Phi_{rs}^\pm|x_1x_2|\Phi_{rs}^\pm\rangle
=\pm\langle r|x|s\rangle\langle s|x|r\rangle
=\pm|x_{rs}|^2.
$$
The <position matrix elements in a symmetric infinite square well>, with the present width $a$, give
$$
x_{12}=\int_{-a/2}^{a/2}u_1(x)x u_2(x)\,dx=-\frac{16a}{9\pi^2},
\qquad D=|x_{12}|^2=\frac{256a^2}{81\pi^4}.
$$
The sign of $x_{12}$ depends on the orbital phase convention, while $D$ does not. The perturbation acts only on the spatial part and cannot mix orthogonal <singlet states> and <triplet states>. Within a triplet it is proportional to the identity on the spin factor. Thus <first-order nondegenerate perturbation theory>, with this trivial diagonal treatment of spin degeneracy, gives the shifts corresponding to the three rows in part (b):
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{state or multiplet}&\delta E^{(1)}\\ \hline
\Phi_{11}^+\chi_{00}&0\\
\Phi_{12}^-\chi_{1M}&-\alpha D\\
\Phi_{12}^+\chi_{00}&+\alpha D
\end{array}\qquad D=\frac{256a^2}{81\pi^4}.}
$$
These formulae also apply for $J<0$, with the reordered levels described above. At $J=0$, they resolve the fourfold $5\varepsilon$ level into a shifted triplet and singlet. The next doubly occupied orbital $\Phi_{22}^+$ still has zero first-order shift. The perturbative requirement is $|\alpha|a^2\ll\varepsilon$ for mixing with remote orbital states; the spin-independent perturbation does not require division by the small singlet–triplet gap.

= Solution
{parent=d}

For distinguishable particles, the ordered spatial products are independent states, and neither <singlet states> nor <triplet states> are excluded from any orbital occupation. The unperturbed <energy eigenvalues> are
$$
E_{rs,0}=(r^2+s^2)\varepsilon+3J/4,\qquad
E_{rs,1}=(r^2+s^2)\varepsilon-J/4.
$$
For $0<J\ll\varepsilon$, the three lowest distinct <energy levels> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{energy}&\text{multiplicity}&\text{normalized eigenfunctions}\\ \hline
2\varepsilon-J/4&3&u_1(x_1)u_1(x_2)\chi_{1M}\\
2\varepsilon+3J/4&1&u_1(x_1)u_1(x_2)\chi_{00}\\
5\varepsilon-J/4&6&u_1(x_1)u_2(x_2)\chi_{1M},\ u_2(x_1)u_1(x_2)\chi_{1M}
\end{array}}
$$
where $M=-1,0,1$. The first two levels do have zero first-order correction, since their spatial <expectation value> factorizes as $\langle1|x|1\rangle^2=0$.

The last instruction in the original PDF is false for the third level. Although every diagonal product-state <matrix element> vanishes, the two orbital states have <degenerate energy levels>. <Degenerate perturbation theory> requires diagonalizing the perturbation within their span. For each $M$, its matrix in the ordered basis $(u_1u_2,u_2u_1)$ is
$$
W=\alpha\begin{pmatrix}0&D\\D&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
D=\langle1|x|2\rangle\langle2|x|1\rangle>0.
$$
The <eigenfunctions> are $\Phi_{12}^\pm\chi_{1M}$, with
$$
\boxed{\delta E_\pm^{(1)}=\pm\alpha D
=\pm\frac{256\alpha a^2}{81\pi^4},\qquad
\text{multiplicity three for each branch}.}
$$
Thus the sixfold unperturbed level splits into two triplets. Its average shift is zero, and the diagonal expectation in an individual ordered product state is zero, but neither fact makes all its <energy eigenvalue> corrections zero. This is the <degenerate orbital exchange splitting from a product perturbation>; distinguishability allows both combinations rather than removing their coupling.

For negative $J$, the lowest two rows exchange order and the third unperturbed level is instead the twofold singlet orbital pair at $5\varepsilon+3J/4$. It splits into $\Phi_{12}^\pm\chi_{00}$ with the same shifts $\pm\alpha D$, each nondegenerate. At $J=0$, the three lowest distinct unperturbed energies are $2\varepsilon,5\varepsilon,8\varepsilon$, with multiplicities $4,8,4$. The first and third have zero first-order shift; the middle splits by $\pm\alpha D$, each fourfold. The printed zero-shift assertion therefore fails also in the zero-exchange limit.
