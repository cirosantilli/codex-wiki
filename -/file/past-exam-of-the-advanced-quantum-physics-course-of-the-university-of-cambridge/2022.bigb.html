= Solution
{parent=1}

By <addition of angular momentum>, combining the first two <spin-one-half> particles gives $0\oplus1$. Adding the third gives the <three spin-one-half angular-momentum decomposition>
$$
\left(\frac12\right)^{\otimes3}
=\left(0\oplus1\right)\otimes\frac12
=\frac12\oplus\frac12\oplus\frac32.
$$
Thus there is one $S=3/2$ <total-spin sector> and two independent $S=1/2$ sectors. Since the particles are distinguishable, no <fermionic exchange symmetry> restriction removes either copy. In a simultaneous <angular momentum eigenstate>, $\hat S^2$ and $\hat S^{(z)}$ have <eigenvalues> $\hbar^2S(S+1)$ and $\hbar M$, so
$$
\boxed{E_{S,M}=\epsilon_t S(S+1)+\epsilon_z M.}
$$
The <energy levels> and their generic multiplicities are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
S&M&E_{S,M}&\text{multiplicity}\\ \hline
3/2&-3/2,-1/2,1/2,3/2&15\epsilon_t/4+\epsilon_zM&1\text{ for each }M\\
1/2&-1/2,1/2&3\epsilon_t/4+\epsilon_zM&2\text{ for each }M
\end{array}
$$
The count $4+2\times2=8$ agrees with the <dimension> of the three-particle <tensor product>. These multiplicities must be added whenever numerical <energy eigenvalues> coincide. In particular, for $\epsilon_z=0$ and $\epsilon_t\ne0$, there are two fourfold <degenerate energy levels>, $15\epsilon_t/4$ and $3\epsilon_t/4$. For $\epsilon_t=0$ and $\epsilon_z\ne0$, the multiplicities at $M=-3/2,-1/2,1/2,3/2$ are $1,3,3,1$. If both coefficients vanish, the single <energy level> has multiplicity eight. Other coincidences between the two <total-spin sectors> occur when $3\epsilon_t+(M-m)\epsilon_z=0$, with $M$ from the quartet and $m=\pm1/2$ from a doublet.

A <density matrix> has unit <trace>. The <Pauli matrices> are traceless, so $\operatorname{Tr}\hat\rho=C$ and hence $C=1$. Using $\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma_i\sigma_j)=2\delta_{ij}$ in the <expectation value> gives
$$
\langle\sigma_j\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho\sigma_j)=r_j.
$$
Consequently the required <Bloch vector> and <density matrix> are
$$
\boxed{C=1,\qquad \mathbf r=\left(\frac12,0,0\right),\qquad
\rho=\begin{pmatrix}1/2&1/4\\1/4&1/2\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The two <eigenvalues> are $3/4$ and $1/4$, both positive, so this is a valid <density operator>. Its <purity of a density operator> is
$$
\operatorname{Tr}\rho^2=\left(\frac34\right)^2+\left(\frac14\right)^2=\frac58<1.
$$
Therefore it is a <mixed state>. Equivalently, its <spectral decomposition> is $\rho=(3/4)|+x\rangle\langle+x|+(1/4)|-x\rangle\langle-x|$, an ensemble of the two distinct <spin-one-half along an axis> states, rather than a single <pure state>.

Write the trap <angular frequency> as $\omega$ and the single-particle <oscillator length> as $\ell=\sqrt{\hbar/(m\omega)}$. The <center of mass> coordinate $X=(x_1+x_2)/2$ and relative coordinate $r=x_1-x_2$ give independent <quantum harmonic oscillators>, both of frequency $\omega$, with masses $2m$ and <reduced mass> $m/2$. Label their excitation numbers by $N$ and $n$. The unperturbed <energy eigenvalue> is $\hbar\omega(N+n+1)$.

Particle exchange leaves $X$ unchanged and sends $r$ to $-r$. The <exchange symmetry of two identical spin-one-half fermions> therefore requires even $n$ to accompany the <spin-one-half singlet state>, and odd $n$ to accompany a <spin-one-half triplet state>. For the <contact shifts of two spin-one-half fermions in a harmonic trap>, the <delta potential> depends only on $r$. For odd $n$, the relative <wavefunction> vanishes at $r=0$, so its contact shift is zero. Within the first-excited manifold, both perturbations are diagonal in the labels $(N,n,S,M)$: this is a <perturbation diagonal within a degenerate eigenspace>. For $n=0$, the relative <oscillator length> is $\sqrt2\ell$, giving the <first-order nondegenerate perturbation theory> shift
$$
U=\lambda|\varphi_{\mathrm{rel},0}(0)|^2
=\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt{2\pi}\ell}.
$$
The <Zeeman splitting> is $2\mu_BB_zM$ for total <spin> projection $\hbar M$. Thus the five lowest states, counted with multiplicity, have
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
(N,n)&S&M&E\text{ to first order}\\ \hline
(0,0)&0&0&\hbar\omega+U\\
(0,1)&1&-1,0,1&2\hbar\omega+2\mu_BB_zM\\
(1,0)&0&0&2\hbar\omega+U
\end{array}}
$$
under the assumed conditions $|U|,2\mu_B|B_z|\ll\hbar\omega$. The three <triplet states> have slopes $-2\mu_B,0,2\mu_B$, while both <singlet states> are field independent at this order. The three <triplet state> branches coincide at zero <magnetic field>. Their crossing with the excited <singlet state> is allowed because the <total-spin sectors> do not mix. The odd relative <wavefunctions> are in fact unaffected by this <delta potential> at any coupling; the displayed <singlet state> shifts retain only first order.

Let the <electric charge> be $q=-e$, where $e>0$ is the <elementary charge>. <Minimal coupling> to a tangential <magnetic vector potential> gives
$$
H=\frac{(p-qA_x)^2}{2m}.
$$
A constant tangential <magnetic vector potential> is an available gauge on the ring, so $a=qA_x$. By <Stokes theorem>, its circulation is the oriented <magnetic flux>
$$
\Phi_C=\oint_{+x}\mathbf A\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=LA_x,
\qquad \boxed{aL=q\Phi_C=-e\Phi_C.}
$$
The orientation matters here. In the original diagram, increasing $x$ goes anticlockwise, whose associated positive normal points out of the page, whereas the indicated positive <magnetic flux> $\Phi$ points into the page. Hence $\Phi_C=-\Phi$ for that diagram, and its convention gives $\boxed{aL=e\Phi}$. If instead $\Phi$ is defined with the normal associated to $+x$, the relation is $aL=-e\Phi$.

There is no requirement that the <magnetic field> be nonzero on the ring: it can be confined to a <solenoid> inside it. The nonzero circulation of the <magnetic vector potential> still affects the <electron> through the <Aharonov-Bohm effect>.

Define a transformed <wavefunction> by
$$
\boxed{\widetilde\psi(x)=e^{-iax/\hbar}\psi(x).}
$$
Indeed, writing $\psi=e^{iax/\hbar}\widetilde\psi$ gives
$$
(p-a)\psi=e^{iax/\hbar}p\widetilde\psi,
\qquad H_a\psi=e^{iax/\hbar}\frac{p^2}{2m}\widetilde\psi.
$$
This is the electromagnetic <gauge transformation> generated by $\chi(x)=-A_xx$: $A_x'=A_x+\partial_x\chi=0$ and $\psi'=e^{iq\chi/\hbar}\psi$. The transformed <Hamiltonian operator> is consequently $p^2/(2m)$, but the original <periodic boundary condition> becomes the <twisted boundary condition>
$$
\boxed{\widetilde\psi(x+L)=e^{-iaL/\hbar}\widetilde\psi(x).}
$$
For the domain of the <self-adjoint> kinetic <Hamiltonian operator>, the derivative has the same twist, $\widetilde\psi'(x+L)=e^{-iaL/\hbar}\widetilde\psi'(x)$. Thus the <magnetic flux> has moved into the <boundary condition>. The transformation is a single-valued gauge on periodic representatives only when $aL/\hbar$ is an integer multiple of $2\pi$; otherwise it is understood on the cut interval with the displayed identification of its endpoints.

For the <magnetic flux spectrum of a quantum ring>, the <periodic boundary condition> permits the normalized <plane waves> $L^{-1/2}e^{2\pi inx/L}$, with $n\in\mathbb Z$. Their <energy eigenvalues> are
$$
E_n(a)=\frac1{2m}\left(\frac{2\pi\hbar n}{L}-a\right)^2
=E_L(n-\alpha)^2,\qquad
E_L=\frac{2\pi^2\hbar^2}{mL^2},\quad
\alpha=\frac{aL}{2\pi\hbar}.
$$
Shifting $\alpha$ by an integer relabels $n$, so the ordered <energy levels> have period one in $\alpha$, or $2\pi\hbar/L$ in $a$. For $-1/2\leq\alpha\leq1/2$, the lowest two are
$$
\boxed{\epsilon_0=E_L\alpha^2,\qquad
\epsilon_1=E_L(1-|\alpha|)^2,}
$$
extended periodically. The lowest branches cross at every half-integer $\alpha$, with energy $E_L/4$. At integer $\alpha$, the first excited <energy level> has multiplicity two. The corresponding <magnetic flux> period is the electron <magnetic flux quantum> $h/e$.

In the normalized <plane wave> basis, the only nonzero potential <matrix elements> are
$$
V_{mn}=-\frac\lambda2\bigl(\delta_{m,n+1}+\delta_{m,n-1}\bigr).
$$
Thus a useful small parameter is $|\lambda|/E_L\ll1$, which suppresses admixture of well-separated <energy levels>. <First-order nondegenerate perturbation theory> further requires $|\lambda|/2\ll|E_n-E_{n\pm1}|$ for the coupled states. This latter condition fails near a half-integer $\alpha$ even for very small $\lambda$; there one must use <degenerate perturbation theory> in the nearly degenerate pair. Smallness is measured against the gaps to the other states, not the vanishing gap inside this pair.

Near $\alpha=1/2$, keep $n=0,1$. The resulting <Hamiltonian operator> matrix and its <eigenvalues> are
$$
H_{\mathrm{pair}}=\begin{pmatrix}E_L\alpha^2&-\lambda/2\\-\lambda/2&E_L(1-\alpha)^2\end{pmatrix},
$$
$$
\widetilde\epsilon_{0,1}\simeq E_L\left[(\alpha-1/2)^2+\frac14\right]
\mp\sqrt{E_L^2(\alpha-1/2)^2+\frac{\lambda^2}{4}}.
$$
In particular,
$$
\boxed{\widetilde\epsilon_{0,1}(\pi\hbar/L)
=\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}\mp\frac{|\lambda|}{2}
+O(\lambda^2/E_L).}
$$
For positive $\lambda$, the lower <quantum state> at the crossing is the symmetric combination of $n=0,1$, and the upper state is the antisymmetric combination. A negative $\lambda$ exchanges these combinations but leaves the ordered <energy eigenvalues> unchanged.

This <cosine-potential gap in a quantum ring> appears in the middle panel above: each lowest-branch crossing opens into an <avoided crossing> of gap $|\lambda|$ to first order. Away from these crossings, the diagonal <matrix elements> vanish, so the leading shifts are second order. At integer $\alpha$, the two first-excited <plane waves> differ by two units of $n$ and therefore have no first-order coupling; their smaller splitting begins at second order. The numerical plot includes that splitting.

Put $\Lambda=|\lambda|\gg E_L$. Changing the sign of $\lambda$ translates the potential by $L/2$ without changing its <energy eigenvalues>, so take a minimum at $x=0$ and expand there. The <parabolic approximation> gives
$$
V(x)\simeq-\Lambda+\frac\Lambda2\left(\frac{2\pi x}{L}\right)^2
=-\Lambda+\frac12m\omega_{\mathrm{loc}}^2x^2,
\qquad \omega_{\mathrm{loc}}=\frac{2\pi}{L}\sqrt{\frac\Lambda m}.
$$
The low-lying <wavefunctions> are localized well inside one period. A local <gauge transformation> removes $a$ from their <quantum harmonic oscillator> equation, giving
$$
\widetilde\epsilon_n(a)\simeq-\Lambda+\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{loc}}\left(n+\frac12\right).
$$
Consequently,
$$
\boxed{\widetilde\epsilon_1(0)-\widetilde\epsilon_0(0)
\simeq\frac{2\pi\hbar}{L}\sqrt{\frac{|\lambda|}{m}}
=\sqrt{2|\lambda|E_L}.}
$$
The sketch consists of two nearly horizontal branches, separated by this gap, as in the right panel above. The <parabolic approximation> makes them completely independent of <magnetic flux>; in the actual ring they retain exponentially small periodic dependence through <quantum tunnelling> around the ring. Corrections from the nonquadratic part of the potential shift the mean energies and gap by relative order $\sqrt{E_L/\Lambda}$, whereas the tunnelling dependence is smaller than every power of that parameter.

The unperturbed <infinite square well> is invariant under the <parity operator>. Its one-dimensional <energy eigenvalues> are nondegenerate, so each <energy eigenstate> has definite parity. Its position <expectation value> therefore vanishes: $|\phi_n(x)|^2$ is even, while $x$ is odd. The <first-order nondegenerate perturbation theory> correction is consequently
$$
\delta\epsilon_n^{(1)}=eE\langle\phi_n|x|\phi_n\rangle=0.
$$
The <electric field> does mix opposite-parity <quantum states>, and <second-order nondegenerate perturbation theory> contains two factors of $eEx$. Its leading contribution is thus proportional to $E^2$: this is a <quadratic Stark effect>. More generally, applying the <parity operator> changes $H(E)$ into $H(-E)$, so their corresponding <energy eigenvalues> agree. An analytic expansion about zero <electric field> contains only even powers of $E$. We assume $e|E|a$ is small compared with the unperturbed excitation scale.

Use zero-based labels to agree with $\phi_0$ in the later parts. The normalized <infinite square well> <energy eigenstates> and <energy eigenvalues> are
$$
\phi_n(x)=\frac1{\sqrt a}\sin\left[\frac{(n+1)\pi(x+a)}{2a}\right],
\qquad \epsilon_n^{(0)}=K(n+1)^2,\quad
K=\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{8ma^2},\quad n=0,1,\ldots.
$$
The <position matrix elements in a symmetric infinite square well>, obtained by integrating the product of the two <wavefunctions> against $x$, are
$$
x_{kn}=\langle\phi_k|x|\phi_n\rangle
=\frac{8a(k+1)(n+1)[(-1)^{k+n}-1]}
{\pi^2[(k+1)^2-(n+1)^2]^2}\quad(k\ne n),\qquad x_{nn}=0.
$$
The integral in the question uses positive integer labels; replacing them by $k+1,n+1$ is essential here. Only opposite-parity states, $k+n$ odd, contribute, as also follows from the <parity operator> <selection rule>.

<Second-order nondegenerate perturbation theory> now gives the requested infinite sum:
$$
\boxed{\delta\epsilon_n^{(2)}=e^2E^2\sum_{k\ne n}
\frac{|x_{kn}|^2}{\epsilon_n^{(0)}-\epsilon_k^{(0)}}.}
$$
In fully explicit form this is
$$
\boxed{\delta\epsilon_n^{(2)}=
-\frac{2048me^2E^2a^4}{\pi^6\hbar^2}(n+1)^2
\sum_{\substack{k\geq0\\k+n\ \mathrm{odd}}}
\frac{(k+1)^2}{[(k+1)^2-(n+1)^2]^5}.}
$$
The symbol $m$ here denotes the electron mass, not a summation index. For the <ground state> $n=0$, every denominator in the first form is negative, so the ground <energy level> moves downward. For excited states, contributions from lower and higher <energy levels> have opposite signs.

The electron's <electric dipole moment> is $\mathbf d=-e\langle x\rangle\hat{\mathbf x}$. It vanishes in the unperturbed ground <energy eigenstate>. The first correction to the <wavefunction> is linear in the <electric field> and has the opposite parity; its interference with the unperturbed state therefore gives a nonzero position <expectation value> of first order.

In detail, the normalized ground <quantum state>, to the order needed, is
$$
|g\rangle=|\phi_0\rangle+
 eE\sum_{k\geq1}\frac{x_{k0}}{\epsilon_0^{(0)}-\epsilon_k^{(0)}}|\phi_k\rangle+O(E^2).
$$
The normalization correction starts at second order and does not affect the linear term below. The two cross terms give
$$
\langle x\rangle=2eE\sum_{k\geq1}
\frac{|x_{k0}|^2}{\epsilon_0^{(0)}-\epsilon_k^{(0)}}+O(E^3).
$$
The absence of an $E^2$ term follows from the <parity operator>: $\langle x\rangle$ changes sign under field reversal. Comparing with the <second-order nondegenerate perturbation theory> energy sum yields
$$
\boxed{\mathbf d=-\frac{2\delta\epsilon_0^{(2)}}{E^2}\mathbf E+O(E^3)
=\alpha_{\mathrm{pol}}\mathbf E+O(E^3),\qquad
\alpha_{\mathrm{pol}}=-\frac{2\delta\epsilon_0^{(2)}}{E^2}>0.}
$$
Thus the induced <electric dipole moment> points along the <electric field>, with positive <electric polarizability>. The <induced dipole moment and quadratic energy shift> obey $\delta\epsilon_0^{(2)}=-\alpha_{\mathrm{pol}}E^2/2$. This factor of one half accounts for the energy needed to induce the dipole. As a check, the <Hellmann–Feynman theorem> gives $d_x=-\partial\epsilon_0/\partial E$, with the same result.

Normalize the trial <quantum state> as $(|\phi_0\rangle+c|\phi_1\rangle)/\sqrt{1+c^2}$. With the phases used above, the off-diagonal position <matrix element> is $x_{01}=-32a/(9\pi^2)$. Define
$$
\Delta=\epsilon_1^{(0)}-\epsilon_0^{(0)}=3K,
\qquad W=eEx_{01}=-\frac{32eEa}{9\pi^2}.
$$
The diagonal perturbation <matrix elements> vanish by parity, so the <Rayleigh quotient> is
$$
\mathcal E(c)=\frac{\epsilon_0^{(0)}+2cW+c^2\epsilon_1^{(0)}}{1+c^2}.
$$
The stationarity equation is $W+\Delta c-Wc^2=0$. The minimum continuously connected to the unperturbed ground <energy eigenstate> has
$$
\boxed{c_{\min}=-\frac W\Delta+O(E^3)
=\frac{256meEa^3}{27\pi^4\hbar^2}+O(E^3).}
$$
Equivalently, diagonalize the <Hamiltonian operator> matrix in this two-dimensional trial space. Its lower <eigenvalue> is
$$
\mathcal E_{\min}=\frac{\epsilon_0^{(0)}+\epsilon_1^{(0)}}2
-\sqrt{\left(\frac\Delta2\right)^2+W^2}
=\epsilon_0^{(0)}-\frac{W^2}\Delta+O(E^4).
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E_{\min}=\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{8ma^2}
-\frac{8192me^2E^2a^4}{243\pi^6\hbar^2}+O(E^4).}
$$
This retains exactly the $k=1$ term of the full ground-state <second-order nondegenerate perturbation theory> sum. Every omitted term is negative, so the full quadratic correction is more negative than this restricted one. This is consistent with the <Rayleigh-Ritz variational principle>: minimizing over a two-state subspace gives an upper bound on the exact ground energy. The bound applies to the unexpanded two-state minimum; a truncated series is to be compared order by order.

For this <spin-boson model>, take $H_0=H_1+H_2$. Since $[H_0,\sigma_z]=0$, the <interaction picture> observable is
$$
\sigma_z^I(t)=e^{iH_0t/\hbar}\sigma_ze^{-iH_0t/\hbar}=\sigma_z.
$$
The normalized vectors $|\phi_\uparrow\rangle$ and $|\phi_\downarrow\rangle$ belong to different <eigenspaces> of the <Hermitian operator> $\sigma_z$, and are therefore orthogonal. This remains true even though each vector includes the oscillator degrees of freedom. Hence all cross terms in the <expectation value> vanish, while the diagonal terms have <eigenvalues> $+1$ and $-1$:
$$
\boxed{\langle\psi_\sigma^I(t)|\sigma_z^I(t)|\psi_\sigma^I(t)\rangle
=|c_{\uparrow\sigma}(t)|^2-|c_{\downarrow\sigma}(t)|^2.}
$$
Normalization also gives $|c_{\uparrow\sigma}|^2+|c_{\downarrow\sigma}|^2=1$, so these are the <probabilities> of the two <spin> outcomes.

The <orthogonal projection> onto the full spin-$\eta$ subspace is
$$
\Pi_\eta=|\eta\rangle\langle\eta|\otimes I_{\mathrm{osc}}
=\sum_{\{n'\}}|\eta,\{n'\}\rangle\langle\eta,\{n'\}|.
$$
Here the identity acts on all oscillator states. The finite-time <scattering operator> $S(t)$ in the question is the <interaction picture> <time-evolution operator>, so $|\psi_\sigma^I(t)\rangle=S(t)|\sigma,\{n\}\rangle$. Projecting its two orthogonal spin components yields
$$
\boxed{c_{\eta\sigma}(t)|\phi_\eta(t)\rangle
=\Pi_\eta S(t)|\sigma,\{n\}\rangle.}
$$
Expand the right-hand side in the <orthonormal basis> $|\eta,\{n'\}\rangle$. Taking its squared <norm>, and using the normalization of $|\phi_\eta\rangle$, gives
$$
\boxed{|c_{\eta\sigma}(t)|^2
=\sum_{\{n'\}}|\langle\eta,\{n'\}|S(t)|\sigma,\{n\}\rangle|^2
=\sum_{\{n'\}}P_{\sigma,\{n\}\to\eta,\{n'\}}.}
$$
Thus one sums <probabilities> over unobserved final oscillator states, not their <probability amplitudes>. <Unitarity> of $S(t)$ makes the sum over both $\eta$ equal to one.

= Solution
{parent=c}

The <Pauli matrix> $\sigma_x$ flips the <spin> with unit <matrix element>. A nonzero oscillator <matrix element> changes exactly one <occupation number>, through a <creation operator> or an <annihilation operator>:
$$
\langle n_j+1|a_j^\dagger|n_j\rangle=\sqrt{n_j+1},\qquad
\langle n_j-1|a_j|n_j\rangle=\sqrt{n_j}.
$$
For a <spin> flip, the spin energy change divided by $\hbar$ is $-s_\sigma\Omega$. Thus creation and annihilation in mode $j$ have total frequency changes $\omega_j-s_\sigma\Omega$ and $-\omega_j-s_\sigma\Omega$, respectively. Distinct modes or different occupation changes lead to orthogonal final <quantum states>, so their <probabilities> add. <Fermi's golden rule> consequently gives
$$
|c_{\eta\sigma}(t)|^2=\frac{2\pi\lambda^2t}{\hbar^2}
\sum_j\left[(n_j+1)\delta(\omega_j-s_\sigma\Omega)
+n_j\delta(\omega_j+s_\sigma\Omega)\right],\quad \eta\ne\sigma.
$$
The <Dirac delta function> is even, accounting for the sign in the annihilation term. Since all $\omega_j,\Omega>0$, an initially up spin can only emit an oscillator quantum, while an initially down spin can only absorb one. Writing the oscillator <density of states> per unit angular frequency as $g(\omega)=\sum_j\delta(\omega-\omega_j)$, the continuum limit gives
$$
\boxed{|c_{\eta\sigma}(t)|^2=\frac{2\pi\lambda^2t}{\hbar^2}
\begin{cases}g(\Omega)(n_\Omega+1),&\sigma=\uparrow,\\
g(\Omega)n_\Omega,&\sigma=\downarrow,
\end{cases}\quad\eta\ne\sigma.}
$$
The $+1$ permits emission even from the oscillator vacuum; absorption requires a quantum already present. The notation $n_\Omega$ assumes a common or smoothly specified occupation at the resonant frequency. If several resonant modes have different occupations, it denotes their <density of states> weighted mean, so that $g(\Omega)n_\Omega=\sum_j n_j\delta(\Omega-\omega_j)$ after continuum smoothing. This is the <golden-rule relaxation of a spin coupled to an oscillator bath>.

In first-order <time-dependent perturbation theory>, the resonant time factor before taking the <Fermi's golden rule> limit is
$$
\left|\int_0^t e^{i\nu t'}\,dt'\right|^2
=\frac{4\sin^2(\nu t/2)}{\nu^2}.
$$
Its central frequency width is of order $1/t$, and its total area is $2\pi t$. To replace it by $2\pi t\delta(\nu)$, the oscillator <density of states> and relevant occupations must be approximately constant over that width. Thus $t\Delta\omega\gg1$. At the same time, the perturbative flip <probability> must remain much smaller than one.

Define $\Gamma_0=2\pi\lambda^2g(\Omega)/\hbar^2$, so the two flip rates are $\Gamma_0(n_\Omega+1)$ and $\Gamma_0n_\Omega$. A sufficient common time window for both initial spins is
$$
\boxed{\frac1{\Delta\omega}\ll t\ll
\frac1{\Gamma_0(n_\Omega+1)}.}
$$
For a specified initial spin, the upper condition is simply its own flip rate times $t\ll1$. Such a window requires weak enough coupling. The continuum approximation also presumes times shorter than recurrences from a resolved finite oscillator spectrum, and long enough to resolve the positive-frequency resonance from its nonresonant counterpart.

The thermal oscillator occupation is the <Bose-Einstein distribution>. With the rate notation $\Gamma_{\eta\sigma}$ for a transition from $\sigma$ to $\eta$, the <master equation> for the up population is
$$
\frac{dp_\uparrow}{dt}=\Gamma_{\uparrow\downarrow}p_\downarrow
-\Gamma_{\downarrow\uparrow}p_\uparrow
=\Gamma_0\left[n_\Omega p_\downarrow-(n_\Omega+1)p_\uparrow\right].
$$
At <thermal equilibrium>, assuming $\Gamma_0>0$, vanishing time derivative is <detailed balance> between the two spin states. Hence
$$
\frac{p_\uparrow}{p_\downarrow}=\frac{n_\Omega}{n_\Omega+1}
=\frac{1/(e^{\beta\hbar\Omega}-1)}{e^{\beta\hbar\Omega}/(e^{\beta\hbar\Omega}-1)}
=e^{-\beta\hbar\Omega},
$$
or
$$
\boxed{p_\uparrow=e^{-\beta\hbar\Omega}p_\downarrow.}
$$
Together with normalization, this gives $p_\uparrow=1/(1+e^{\beta\hbar\Omega})$ and $p_\downarrow=e^{\beta\hbar\Omega}/(1+e^{\beta\hbar\Omega})$, the <Boltzmann distribution> for the <energy eigenvalues> $\pm\hbar\Omega/2$. At zero temperature, absorption vanishes and emission relaxes the spin into its down ground state.

The nonzero-rate condition is necessary: if $\lambda=0$ or $g(\Omega)=0$, both rates vanish, so every initial pair of populations is stationary within this approximation. In that case stationarity alone cannot imply a thermal distribution. The equilibrium conclusion therefore presumes coupling to resonant oscillator modes.
