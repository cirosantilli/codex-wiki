= Solution
{parent=a}

Use the isolated <nuclear spin> <Zeeman effect> implied by the supplied <nuclear magnetic moment>, with <hyperfine structure> neglected. The <proton> <gyromagnetic ratio> is
$$
\gamma_p=\frac{g_p\mu_N}{\hbar}=2.68\times10^8\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}\,T^{-1}}.
$$
The two <spin> levels are separated by $g_p\mu_NB_0$. Resonant <nuclear magnetic resonance> therefore requires the <Larmor frequency>
$$
\boxed{\omega=\gamma_p B_0=2.68\times10^8\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}},\qquad \frac{\omega}{2\pi}=42.7\ \mathrm{MHz}.
$$

The duration is a <Rabi pi pulse>: in a frame rotating with the resonant transverse <magnetic field>, the <spin> must rotate through $\pi$. If $B_1$ denotes the magnitude of a circularly rotating field with the correct sense of <rotation>, the <Rabi angular frequency> is $\Omega_R=\gamma_pB_1$. The shortest pulse is then
$$
\boxed{\tau=\frac{\pi}{\gamma_pB_1}=1.17\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{s}=117\ \mu\mathrm{s}}.
$$

There is an amplitude convention to specify. If the applied transverse <magnetic field> is instead the linearly oscillating $B_1\cos(\omega t)\hat{\mathbf x}$, its resonant circular component has magnitude $B_1/2$. The <rotating-wave approximation> gives $\Omega_R=\gamma_pB_1/2$ and hence
$$
\boxed{\tau_{\rm linear}=\frac{2\pi}{\gamma_pB_1}=234\ \mu\mathrm{s}}.
$$
The wording does not specify this polarization convention, so the two pulse durations should be distinguished. Odd multiples of either shortest <Rabi pi pulse> also invert the <spin> within the corresponding ideal model.

The operators associated with the two angular momenta commute with one another, and each squared <angular momentum operator> commutes with every component of that same <angular momentum>. Two useful identities are
$$
J_z=J_{1z}+J_{2z},\qquad
\mathbf J_1\cdot\mathbf J_2=\frac12(J^2-J_1^2-J_2^2).
$$
Consequently the two maximal commuting subsets of the displayed collection are
$$
\boxed{\{J_1^2,J_2^2,J_{1z},J_{2z},J_z\}},\qquad
\boxed{\{J_1^2,J_2^2,J^2,J_z,\mathbf J_1\cdot\mathbf J_2\}}.
$$

The first set gives the uncoupled <eigenbasis> $|j_1m_1\rangle\otimes|j_2m_2\rangle$. Its $J_z$ <eigenvalue> is already fixed by $m=m_1+m_2$. The second gives the coupled <eigenbasis> $|j_1j_2;jm\rangle$ for the <addition of angular momentum>. Its scalar-product <eigenvalue> is already fixed by $j,j_1,j_2$. Thus an independent <complete set of commuting observables> uses four operators in either case; literal maximality within the question's list includes the dependent fifth operator.

For generic nonzero angular momenta, no further listed operator can be added. In particular,
$$
[J_{1z},\mathbf J_1\cdot\mathbf J_2]
=i\hbar(J_{1y}J_{2x}-J_{1x}J_{2y})
$$
is generally nonzero, and $[J_{1z},J^2]=2[J_{1z},\mathbf J_1\cdot\mathbf J_2]$. The same obstruction applies to $J_{2z}$. Their sum $J_z$ commutes because the two <commutators> cancel.

Write $C=e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$. The <shell theorem> makes the <potential energy> of the <electron> constant inside the charged <spherical shell>. Relative to the point-proton <Coulomb potential>, the perturbation is
$$
\Delta V(r)=\begin{cases}
C\left(\dfrac1r-\dfrac1b\right),&0<r<b,\\
0,&r\geq b.
\end{cases}
$$
It is positive: the finite charge distribution weakens the attraction close to the origin.

The <first-order energy correction> is the <expectation value> of this perturbation in the unperturbed <hydrogen atom> <ground state>. Integrating its <probability density> over spherical volume gives
$$
\Delta E=\frac{4C}{a_0^3}\int_0^b e^{-2r/a_0}\left(r-\frac{r^2}{b}\right)\,dr.
$$
For a <proton> radius much smaller than the <Bohr radius>, expand the exponential uniformly over this short interval. The leading integral is
$$
\int_0^b\left(r-\frac{r^2}{b}\right)\,dr=\frac{b^2}{6},\qquad
\Delta E=\frac{2Cb^2}{3a_0^3}+O\left(\frac{Cb^3}{a_0^4}\right).
$$
Since $E_{1s}=-C/(2a_0)$, the signed fractional change is
$$
\boxed{\frac{\Delta E}{E_{1s}}=-\frac43\left(\frac b{a_0}\right)^2+O\left(\frac{b^3}{a_0^3}\right)}.
$$
Equivalently, $\Delta E/|E_{1s}|=+4b^2/(3a_0^2)$ to leading order: the <energy> increases and the binding-energy magnitude decreases. This is the <finite proton-shell correction to hydrogen ground energy>.

If no small-radius expansion is made, the same <time-independent perturbation theory> integral gives, with $\beta=b/a_0$,
$$
\frac{\Delta E}{|E_{1s}|}=2\left[1-\frac1\beta+\left(1+\frac1\beta\right)e^{-2\beta}\right].
$$
Its expansion starts with $4\beta^2/3$, in agreement with the leading result. The integral is exact for the first-order calculation; it is not an exact solution of the perturbed <Schrödinger equation>.

A <spherical tensor operator> of rank $k$ transforms under <rotation> like an <angular momentum> $k$. The <Wigner-Eckart theorem> writes its <matrix elements> as
$$
\langle\lambda'j'm'|T_q^{(k)}|\lambda jm\rangle
=\frac{\langle jm;kq|j'm'\rangle}{\sqrt{2j'+1}}\,
\langle\lambda'j'\Vert T^{(k)}\Vert\lambda j\rangle.
$$
The <reduced matrix element> contains the radial and other dynamical information; the <Clebsch-Gordan coefficients> determine the dependence on the projections. Ratios within a fixed pair of multiplets therefore follow without a radial integral. The angular <selection rules> include $m'=m+q$ and $|j-k|\leq j'\leq j+k$.

For the position vector, choose the rank-one components
$$
T_0=z,\qquad T_{\pm1}=\mp\frac{x\pm iy}{\sqrt2},\qquad
x=\frac{T_{-1}-T_{+1}}{\sqrt2}.
$$
Let $R=\langle\lambda',3/2\Vert T^{(1)}\Vert\lambda,1/2\rangle$. The stretched <Clebsch-Gordan coefficients> and one lowering step give
$$
\left\langle\frac12,\frac12;1,1\middle|\frac32,\frac32\right\rangle=1,\qquad
\left\langle\frac12,\frac12;1,0\middle|\frac32,\frac12\right\rangle=\sqrt{\frac23}.
$$
For example, apply the total <angular momentum ladder operator> to the stretched state. The result has components with coefficients $1/\sqrt3$ and $\sqrt{2/3}$, corresponding to lowering the initial <angular momentum> and the vector component, respectively.

Only the $q=+1$ component of $x$ contributes to $M_1$. The $q=0$ operator $z$ cannot connect the projections in $M_2$, so that <matrix element> vanishes. With the convention above,
$$
M_1=-\frac{R}{2\sqrt2},\qquad M_2=0,\qquad M_3=\frac{R}{\sqrt6}.
$$
Thus the requested relative magnitudes and their squared values are
$$
\boxed{|M_1|:|M_2|:|M_3|=\sqrt3:0:2},\qquad
\boxed{|M_1|^2:|M_2|^2:|M_3|^2=3:0:4}.
$$
These ratios presume a common nonzero <reduced matrix element>. The position operator has odd <parity>, so an <electric dipole transition> requires opposite-parity multiplets; for one <electron> the <electric-dipole selection rule> also gives $\Delta\ell=\pm1$. The displayed $j,m$ labels alone do not establish that condition. If the common <reduced matrix element> vanishes, all three <matrix elements> vanish and no nonzero ratio can be inferred.

A uniform <magnetic field> quantizes the transverse cyclotron motion of an <electron>. With <electron> charge $-e$ and the <Landau gauge for a uniform magnetic field> $\mathbf A=(0,Bx,0)$, <minimal coupling> gives
$$
H_\perp=\frac{p_x^2}{2m}+\frac{(p_y+eBx)^2}{2m}.
$$
For an <eigenstate> of $p_y$ with <eigenvalue> $\hbar k_y$, this is a <quantum harmonic oscillator> centered at $x_c=-\hbar k_y/(eB)$, with <cyclotron frequency> $\omega_c=eB/m$. Its <Landau levels> are
$$
\boxed{E_n=\hbar\omega_c\left(n+\frac12\right),\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots}.
$$
The oscillator width is set by the <magnetic length> $\ell_B=\sqrt{\hbar/(eB)}$. In three dimensions, the free motion parallel to the <magnetic field> adds $\hbar^2k_z^2/(2m)$. The <electron> <spin> adds a <Zeeman splitting> $\pm g_s\mu_BB/2$. In a crystal the appropriate <band effective mass> and effective <spin> factor replace the free-electron values.

Changing $k_y$ moves the orbit center without changing the <energy>. In a rectangle $L_xL_y$, adjacent allowed centers differ by $2\pi\ell_B^2/L_y$. Requiring the center to lie in the sample gives the <degeneracy of a Landau level>, per <spin>,
$$
N_\phi=\frac{L_xL_y}{2\pi\ell_B^2}=\frac{eBL_xL_y}{h},\qquad
\boxed{\frac{N_\phi}{L_xL_y}=\frac{eB}{h}}.
$$
This large <state degeneracy>, together with the increasing level spacing, rearranges the occupied states as the <magnetic field> changes. To resolve the <Landau levels>, thermal broadening $k_BT$ and disorder broadening should both be small compared with $\hbar\omega_c$.

\Image[2017-landau-levels.png]
{title=Landau levels rise with magnetic field and pass through a fixed reference Fermi energy}

The <de Haas-van Alphen effect> is an oscillation of <magnetization> as successive <Landau levels> pass the <Fermi energy>. The <Shubnikov-de Haas effect> is the corresponding oscillation in longitudinal <magnetoresistance>. For a simple orbit, successive crossings occur periodically in $1/B$; the general relation to the extremal orbit area is <Onsager quantization>. These measurements reveal the geometry and effective masses of a <Fermi surface>.

In a two-dimensional <electron> system, filling an integer number $\nu$ of resolved levels leads to the <integer quantum Hall effect>, with
$$
|\sigma_{xy}|=\nu\frac{e^2}{h},\qquad |\rho_{xy}|=\frac{h}{\nu e^2},
$$
and very small longitudinal resistivity on a plateau. Disorder localization helps produce finite plateau widths. <Cyclotron resonance> provides another direct signature: radiation is absorbed at the adjacent-level spacing $\hbar\omega_c$.

A <coherent state> is a normalized <eigenstate> of the <annihilation operator>, $a|\alpha\rangle=\alpha|\alpha\rangle$. Expanding in the <Fock state> <eigenbasis> gives a recurrence $c_{n+1}=\alpha c_n/\sqrt{n+1}$, and <wavefunction normalization> fixes
$$
|\alpha\rangle=e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{\alpha^n}{\sqrt{n!}}|n\rangle
=D(\alpha)|0\rangle,\qquad
D(\alpha)=e^{\alpha a^\dagger-\alpha^*a}.
$$
The <displacement operator> therefore shifts the oscillator <ground state> in <phase space>. Distinct <coherent states> are not orthogonal:
$$
\langle\alpha|\beta\rangle=
\exp\left[-\frac{|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2}{2}+\alpha^*\beta\right].
$$

The occupation probabilities form a <Poisson distribution>,
$$
P_n=e^{-N}\frac{N^n}{n!},\qquad N=|\alpha|^2,
\qquad \langle n\rangle=N,\quad (\Delta n)^2=N.
$$
For the <quantum harmonic oscillator>, the <expectation values> of position and momentum are
$$
\langle x\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{2\hbar}{m\omega}}\operatorname{Re}\alpha,\qquad
\langle p\rangle=\sqrt{2m\hbar\omega}\operatorname{Im}\alpha.
$$
The fluctuations equal those of the <ground state>,
$$
(\Delta x)^2=\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega},\qquad
(\Delta p)^2=\frac{m\hbar\omega}{2},\qquad
\boxed{\Delta x\,\Delta p=\frac{\hbar}{2}}.
$$
Thus the position <wavefunction> is a displaced <Gaussian wave packet> that saturates the <Heisenberg uncertainty principle>.

Time evolution under $H=\hbar\omega(a^\dagger a+1/2)$ preserves this form:
$$
\boxed{e^{-iHt/\hbar}|\alpha\rangle=e^{-i\omega t/2}|\alpha e^{-i\omega t}\rangle}.
$$
The center follows the classical oscillator trajectory while the packet width stays constant. Its mean <energy> is $\hbar\omega(N+1/2)$; it is generally a superposition of <energy eigenstates>, rather than a state of definite <energy>. For $N\gg1$, the relative occupation fluctuation is $1/\sqrt N$, explaining the classical appearance of a highly excited <coherent state>.

= Solution

The <Zeeman effect> comes from $-\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B$. For an <electron>, the orbital and <spin> <magnetic moments> are approximately $-\mu_B\mathbf L/\hbar$ and $-2\mu_B\mathbf S/\hbar$, respectively. This explains both the sign and the relative factor two in the term linear in $B$. The second term is the <spin-orbit interaction>: the <electron>'s motion through the atomic <electric field> produces a magnetic interaction in its rest frame. The relativistic correction, including <Thomas precession>, and the radial <expectation value> are incorporated into $K$. This effective <Hamiltonian> retains the linear <Zeeman splitting> and <spin-orbit coupling>, while omitting diamagnetic and <hyperfine structure> corrections.

Define the <angular momentum ladder operators> by $L_\pm=L_x\pm iL_y$ and similarly for $S_\pm$. Direct expansion gives
$$
\frac12(L_+S_-+L_-S_+)=L_xS_x+L_yS_y,
$$
so adding $L_zS_z$ gives the required scalar product. The off-diagonal terms change $m_\ell$ and $m_s$ in opposite directions. Consequently $J_z=L_z+S_z$ commutes with $H$, and $m_j$ remains a good <quantum number> at every <magnetic field>.

In the given product <eigenbasis>, the diagonal <matrix elements> are
$$
\langle m_\ell,m_s|H|m_\ell,m_s\rangle
=\mu_BB(m_\ell+2m_s)+2Km_\ell m_s.
$$
For $\ell=1$ and $s=1/2$, the only off-diagonal couplings are between $(1,-1/2)$ and $(0,1/2)$, and between $(0,-1/2)$ and $(-1,1/2)$. Each relevant orbital ladder factor is $\hbar\sqrt2$ and each <spin> ladder factor is $\hbar$. Multiplying by $K/\hbar^2$ gives $\sqrt2K$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{a=\mu_BB,\qquad b=K,\qquad c=\sqrt2K}.
$$
All other off-diagonal entries vanish either by the ladder endpoints or by conservation of $m_j$. This derives every entry of the displayed matrix.

The stretched states have no mixing and yield
$$
\boxed{E_{m_j=3/2}=K+2a,\qquad E_{m_j=-3/2}=K-2a}.
$$
The $m_j=1/2$ block is $\begin{pmatrix}-K&\sqrt2K\\\sqrt2K&a\end{pmatrix}$, while the $m_j=-1/2$ block is $\begin{pmatrix}-a&\sqrt2K\\\sqrt2K&-K\end{pmatrix}$. Taking the trace and determinant of each $2\times2$ block gives the remaining four <energy eigenvalues>:
$$
\boxed{E_{+}^{(\pm)}=\frac{a-K}{2}\pm\frac12\sqrt{(a+K)^2+8K^2}},
$$
$$
\boxed{E_{-}^{(\pm)}=\frac{-a-K}{2}\pm\frac12\sqrt{(a-K)^2+8K^2}}.
$$
The subscripts indicate $m_j=\pm1/2$ and the superscripts indicate the upper or lower root. These formulas are the <Zeeman spectrum for orbital angular momentum one> and remain valid for either sign of $K$.

For the usual ordering $K>0$, including sodium, the zero-field states follow from
$$
\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S=\frac12(J^2-L^2-S^2),\qquad
E_j=K\left[j(j+1)-\ell(\ell+1)-s(s+1)\right].
$$
The $j=3/2$ quartet lies at $K$ and the $j=1/2$ doublet at $-2K$, separated by $3K$. In a weak <magnetic field>, the <Landé g-factors> give
$$
E_{3/2,m_j}=K+\frac43am_j+O(a^2/K),\qquad
E_{1/2,m_j}=-2K+\frac23am_j+O(a^2/K).
$$
These label the curves near $B=0$. In a strong <magnetic field>, the <Paschen-Back effect> makes $(m_\ell,m_s)$ the appropriate approximate labels:
$$
E_{m_\ell,m_s}=a(m_\ell+2m_s)+2Km_\ell m_s+O(K^2/a)
$$
for the mixed states, with the stretched-state formulas already exact. The branch connections for $B>0$ are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{zero-field label }(j,m_j)&\text{strong-field label }(m_\ell,m_s)\\\hline
(3/2,3/2)&(1,1/2)\\
(3/2,1/2)&(0,1/2)\\
(1/2,1/2)&(1,-1/2)\\
(3/2,-1/2)&(-1,1/2)\\
(1/2,-1/2)&(0,-1/2)\\
(3/2,-3/2)&(-1,-1/2)
\end{array}
$$
Within a fixed $m_j$, the nonzero coupling prevents a crossing; this is an <avoided crossing>. Curves with different $m_j$ may cross because the <Hamiltonian> does not couple them.

\Image[2017-spin-orbit-zeeman-spectrum.png]
{title=Exact spin-orbit and Zeeman energies for orbital angular momentum one and positive spin-orbit constant}

For the sodium estimate, the zero-field frequency separation fixes
$$
3K=h\delta f,\qquad K=1.14\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm J.
$$
The lower $3s$ doublet has shifts $\pm a$. The <Sodium D-line Zeeman pattern> gives photon-frequency shifts $a/h$ times $\{\pm2/3,\pm4/3\}$ for D$_1$ and $\{\pm1/3,\pm1,\pm5/3\}$ for D$_2$. Thus the highest-frequency D$_1$ component and lowest-frequency D$_2$ component approach each other by $3a/h$. Extrapolating the <weak-field Zeeman splitting> gives
$$
\boxed{B_{\rm cross}\sim\frac{h\delta f}{3\mu_B}=12.3\ \mathrm T},
$$
so the crossover occurs at a field of order $10\ \mathrm T$, where $a\sim K$ and the weak-field approximation is becoming marginal.

The exact <eigenvalues> refine the estimate within this <Hamiltonian>. The relevant photon energies, with a common zero-field reference omitted, are $E_+^{(-)}+a$ and $E_-^{(+)}-a$. The corresponding <electric dipole transitions> have $\Delta m_j=+1$ and $-1$, respectively. Equating them gives
$$
6a=\sqrt{(a+K)^2+8K^2}+\sqrt{(a-K)^2+8K^2}.
$$
Its positive solution is $a/K=\sqrt{10}/3$, hence
$$
\boxed{B_{\rm cross}=\frac{\sqrt{10}\,h\delta f}{9\mu_B}\simeq13.0\ \mathrm T}
$$
in the retained model. The crossing is between optical transition frequencies; the participating upper states themselves need not be degenerate.

For elastic scattering by the <Yukawa potential>, take $\kappa>0$ and use the <first Born approximation> in the plane-wave normalization
$$
f_B(\mathbf q)=-\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\int e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}V(r)\,d^3r.
$$
Choose the polar axis along the <momentum transfer> $\hbar\mathbf q$. The angular integral is
$$
\int e^{-iqr\cos\vartheta}\,d\Omega
=2\pi\int_{-1}^{1}e^{-iqru}\,du
=4\pi\frac{\sin(qr)}{qr}.
$$
The radial volume element cancels the remaining powers of $r$, leaving
$$
f_B(q)=-\frac{2mA}{\hbar^2q}\int_0^\infty e^{-\kappa r}\sin(qr)\,dr
=-\frac{2mA}{\hbar^2(q^2+\kappa^2)}.
$$
For real $A$, the <differential scattering cross-section> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=|f_B(q)|^2
=\left[\frac{2mA}{\hbar^2(q^2+\kappa^2)}\right]^2}.
$$
This also has a finite forward limit when $\kappa>0$. The <Born amplitude for a Yukawa potential> provides a screened route to the <Coulomb potential> by taking $\kappa\to0^+$ at fixed $q>0$.

For an <alpha particle> of charge $2e$ scattering from a heavy nucleus of charge $Ze$, set $A=2Ze^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$. Neglect recoil, so that the reduced mass is approximately the projectile mass, $E=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)$, and elastic geometry gives
$$
q^2=|\mathbf k_{\rm out}-\mathbf k_{\rm in}|^2
=4k^2\sin^2\frac\theta2.
$$
The Coulomb-limit <scattering amplitude> becomes
$$
f_B(\theta)=-\frac{A}{4E\sin^2(\theta/2)}
=-\frac{Ze^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0E\sin^2(\theta/2)},
$$
which gives the <Rutherford differential cross-section>
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}
=\left[\frac{Ze^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0E\sin^2(\theta/2)}\right]^2}.
$$
The unscreened <Coulomb potential> has long range, so taking this limit does not justify the <Born approximation> for its full scattering state, particularly for a strongly charged target. The exact <Rutherford scattering> amplitude differs by a phase and has the same modulus. Thus the resulting <differential scattering cross-section> remains applicable to the ideal nonrelativistic Coulomb problem beyond the weak-potential regime.

To relate the detector counts to the <atomic number>, first compute the number of target atoms per illuminated area. With molar mass $M=0.197\ \mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}}$, the <number density> integrated through the foil is
$$
\mathcal N=\frac{\rho tN_A}{M}
=\frac{(19.3\times10^3)(2.1\times10^{-7})(6.02214\times10^{23})}{0.197}
=1.239\times10^{22}\ \mathrm{m^{-2}}.
$$
The detector's transverse area is already its area normal to the scattered ray. Its small <solid angle> is
$$
\Delta\Omega\simeq\frac{10^{-6}}{(10^{-2})^2}=10^{-2}\ \mathrm{sr}.
$$
There is no extra $\cos45^\circ$ factor: the foil is traversed normally and the detector area is transverse to its line of sight.

For a thin foil with negligible <multiple scattering> and <energy> loss, the detected fraction is $f=\mathcal N(d\sigma/d\Omega)\Delta\Omega$. Thus the measurements imply
$$
\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}
=\frac{3.7\times10^{-7}}{(1.239\times10^{22})(10^{-2})}
=2.986\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{m^2\,sr^{-1}}.
$$
Using $E=7.68\times10^6e\ \mathrm J=1.23047\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm J$ in the <Rutherford differential cross-section> gives
$$
Z=\frac{8\pi\epsilon_0 E\sin^2(22.5^\circ)}{e^2}
\sqrt{\frac{f}{\mathcal N\Delta\Omega}},\qquad
\boxed{Z\simeq85.4\approx85}.
$$

**The stated measurements do not reproduce gold's <atomic number> 79 in this model.** The values and units above agree with the original PDF. Substituting $Z=79$ instead would predict $f\simeq3.17\times10^{-7}$ with the other inputs unchanged, rather than $3.7\times10^{-7}$. No stated correction accounts for that discrepancy; forcing the answer to 79 would require changing the data or adding an experimental correction not specified in the problem.
