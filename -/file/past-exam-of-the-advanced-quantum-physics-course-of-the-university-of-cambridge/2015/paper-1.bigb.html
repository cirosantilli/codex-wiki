= Solution

The <Pauli exclusion principle> allows each one-particle magnetic substate only once, since the <fermions> share the other <quantum numbers>. To maximize the total <angular momentum> projection, occupy $m_j=5/2,3/2,1/2,-1/2$, giving
$$
M_{\max}=\frac52+\frac32+\frac12-\frac12=4.
$$
The corresponding <Slater determinant> is a <highest-weight vector>: every attempted raising operation either tries to exceed $j$ or duplicates an occupied substate and vanishes. Therefore it belongs to a multiplet with **$\boxed{J_{\max}=4}$**, not the distinguishable-particle value $4j=10$. Equivalently, four particles in a six-state shell have two holes whose maximum <angular momentum> projection is $5/2+3/2=4$. The general <maximum total angular momentum of equivalent fermions> is $J_{\max}=Nj-N(N-1)/2$ for $N\leq2j+1$.

At incident <wave number> $k$, low <kinetic energy> means $ka\ll1$: the <de Broglie wavelength> is much larger than the scattering region. A generic finite-range scatterer can then be described to leading order without resolving its angular structure. In <partial-wave scattering>, this is leading <S-wave scattering>. The $\ell=0$ <spherical harmonic> is constant, whereas the centrifugal term $\hbar^2\ell(\ell+1)/(2mr^2)$ impedes penetration to the potential for $\ell>0$.

For a <central potential> with regular threshold behaviour, the <finite-range partial-wave threshold law> gives $\delta_\ell=O(k^{2\ell+1})$. Thus
$$
f(\theta)=\frac1k\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+1)e^{i\delta_\ell}\sin\delta_\ell\,P_\ell(\cos\theta)
\simeq\frac{e^{i\delta_0}\sin\delta_0}{k},
$$
which is independent of scattering angle. For finite nonzero <scattering length> $a_s$, $f\to-a_s$ and **$d\sigma/d\Omega\to a_s^2$ is isotropic**. The potential range $a$ and <scattering length> $a_s$ are different quantities. The claim describes the generic leading <S-wave scattering> limit; tuned vanishing <S-wave scattering> amplitude or exceptional threshold resonances require separate analysis rather than a universal angular conclusion.

Closed $1s^2$ and $2s^2$ subshells have $L=S=0$. In the open $2p^4$ subshell, three <electrons> can have parallel <spins> in $m_\ell=-1,0,1$, and the fourth must have the opposite <spin>, giving maximum $S=1$. Treating the shell as two $p$ holes gives the same allowed terms as $p^2$; at this <spin>, antisymmetry permits $L=1$, rather than $L=2$. One can also see $M_L=1$ by placing the fourth <electron> in $m_\ell=1$.

<Hund's rules> therefore select the $ {}^3P$ term. Since four <electrons> exceed half filling of the six-state $p$ subshell, its lowest <atomic fine-structure level> has the largest $J=L+S=2$:
$$
\boxed{1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^4\;{}^3P_2,\qquad S=1,\ L=1,\ J=2.}
$$
Its zero-field magnetic degeneracy is $2J+1=5$. The experimentally tabulated neutral-oxygen ground level has precisely this term and $J$.<Energy Levels of Neutral Oxygen by National Institute of Standards and Technology>[first configuration block, 2s2 2p4, triplet P, J=2 at zero energy]{ref}

The <quantum variational principle> turns the ground-state problem into minimization of a <Rayleigh quotient>:
$$
E[\psi]=\frac{\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}\geq E_0.
$$
The <Hamiltonian> must be a <self-adjoint operator> bounded below, and the nonzero trial state must have finite form <energy>. Expanding a normalized trial state in exact <energy eigenstates> gives $E[\psi]=\sum_n|c_n|^2E_n$, a weighted average no smaller than $E_0$. Equality requires support only in the ground-state eigenspace. The spectral version also accommodates continuous <spectrum>; a lowest normalizable <eigenstate> is not required merely to bound the spectral infimum.

The <variational method> chooses a trial family with the correct <boundary conditions>, <fermionic exchange symmetry> and other required <quantum numbers>, evaluates its <energy> and minimizes over its parameters. It gives an **upper bound on <ground-state energy>**. Enlarging a nested trial family can only improve or preserve its optimal bound. A low <energy> does not by itself establish a highly accurate <wavefunction>, especially near degeneracy; <energy> errors are second order in small admixtures orthogonal to a nondegenerate exact <ground state>.

For example, a normalized Gaussian trial for a one-dimensional <quantum harmonic oscillator> is $\psi_\beta=(\beta/\pi)^{1/4}e^{-\beta x^2/2}$, with $\beta>0$. Its <energy> is
$$
E(\beta)=\frac{\hbar^2\beta}{4m}+\frac{m\omega^2}{4\beta},\qquad
\boxed{\beta_{\rm opt}=m\omega/\hbar,\quad E_{\min}=\hbar\omega/2.}
$$
The two terms balance kinetic localization cost against the potential cost of spreading out. This <Gaussian variational energy for the harmonic oscillator> happens to attain the exact answer because the family contains the exact <ground state>.

\Image[paper-1-variational.png]
{title=Gaussian variational energy and its exact harmonic-oscillator minimum}

A trial expansion $\psi=\sum_j c_j\chi_j$ in a finite, possibly nonorthogonal basis gives the <generalized eigenvalue problem> $H\mathbf c=ES\mathbf c$, with $H_{ij}=\langle\chi_i|H|\chi_j\rangle$ and $S_{ij}=\langle\chi_i|\chi_j\rangle$. Its lowest Ritz value minimizes the quotient in that subspace. For excited states, the <excited-state variational constraint> imposes exact <orthogonality> to the lower <eigenstates> to obtain a corresponding upper bound; the <Courant–Fischer min-max principle> yields upper bounds for ordered Ritz <eigenvalues> from a common subspace. <orthogonality> only to an independently approximate <ground state> does not automatically provide an excited-state bound: residual true-ground-state contamination can lower the trial <energy> below the desired excited <energy>. Symmetry can impose exact <orthogonality> when the relevant states have different symmetry sectors.

For a particle of charge $q$ and <mass> $m$ in a uniform <magnetic field> $\mathbf B=B\hat z$, <minimal coupling> replaces <momentum> by $\mathbf p-q\mathbf A$. Choose <Landau gauge> $\mathbf A=(0,Bx,0)$ and first restrict motion to the $xy$ plane. Since $p_y=\hbar k_y$ is conserved,
$$
H=\frac{p_x^2}{2m}+\frac{(\hbar k_y-qBx)^2}{2m}
=\frac{p_x^2}{2m}+\frac12m\omega_c^2(x-x_c)^2,
$$
$$
\omega_c=\frac{|qB|}{m},\qquad x_c=\frac{\hbar k_y}{qB},\qquad
\boxed{E_n=\hbar\omega_c(n+\tfrac12),\quad n=0,1,2,\ldots.}
$$
These <Landau levels> quantize the classical cyclotron motion. The <wavefunctions> are harmonic-oscillator functions centred at $x_c$, multiplied by $e^{ik_yy}$. Changing $k_y$ shifts the <guiding centre> without changing the <energy>. Gauge-dependent labels such as $k_y$ are not themselves observables; the level spacing and degeneracy are gauge independent. The <magnetic length> $\ell_B=\sqrt{\hbar/|qB|}$ is the transverse orbital scale.

For a large rectangle of sides $L_x,L_y$, periodicity along $y$ gives $\Delta k_y=2\pi/L_y$. Requiring centres inside the sample gives, for each orbital level and each specified <spin> state,
$$
\boxed{D=\frac{L_xL_y}{2\pi\ell_B^2}
=\frac{|qB|A}{h}=\frac{|\Phi|}{h/|q|}.}
$$
Thus each <magnetic flux quantum> supplies one guiding-centre state. The continuum bulk count neglects boundary corrections; magnetic periodic conditions on a torus require integer flux count. A finite confining edge lifts the simple flat guiding-centre <spectrum> near that edge.

\Image[paper-1-landau-levels.png]
{title=Translated oscillator centres and degenerate orbital Landau levels in a uniform field}

<spin> adds a Zeeman term. For <electrons> with gyromagnetic factor $g$, its contribution is $g\mu_B Bm_s$, with $m_s=\pm1/2$ and $\mu_B=e\hbar/(2m_e)$. If $g=2$ and the orbital <mass> is $m_e$, the branches become $\hbar\omega_c n$ and $\hbar\omega_c(n+1)$: the lowest combined level has one <spin> branch, while positive levels coincide in pairs. In a material, effective <mass> and effective $g$ need not give that coincidence.

In three dimensions, free motion parallel to $B$ adds $\hbar^2k_z^2/(2m)$, so each orbital index labels a one-dimensional continuum rather than an isolated three-dimensional <energy>. Resolved magnetic quantization requires the spacing to exceed substantial thermal and disorder broadening. The changing occupation of these levels underlies magnetic quantum oscillations; in two dimensions their degeneracy and filling are central to the <integer quantum Hall effect>. These statements assume nonrelativistic, approximately independent particles, rather than arbitrary interacting or disordered spectra.

A <helium atom> has two <electrons> bound to nuclear charge $+2e$. In the nonrelativistic fixed-nucleus approximation, using <atomic units>,
$$
H=-\frac12\nabla_1^2-\frac12\nabla_2^2-\frac2{r_1}-\frac2{r_2}+\frac1{r_{12}}.
$$
The last term couples the <electrons>, so a product of independent hydrogenic orbitals is not an exact solution. The dominant ground configuration is $1s^2$. Its spatial <wavefunction> is symmetric, so Fermi antisymmetry requires the <spin> singlet
$$
\chi_{00}=\frac{|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle-|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle}{\sqrt2},\qquad
\boxed{\text{helium ground level }1s^2\;{}^1S_0.}
$$
A triplet cannot put both <electrons> in the same $1s$ spatial orbital. Correlation changes the exact spatial function, including its dependence on $r_{12}$, while preserving this ground-state symmetry.

A useful <helium variational effective charge> takes normalized orbitals $\phi_\zeta(\mathbf r)=(\zeta^3/\pi)^{1/2}e^{-\zeta r}$ for both <electrons> and multiplies their product by $\chi_{00}$. The <energy> in Hartree units is
$$
E(\zeta)=\zeta^2-4\zeta+\frac58\zeta,\qquad
\boxed{\zeta_{\rm opt}=\frac{27}{16},\quad E_{\rm var}=-\frac{729}{256}E_h\simeq-77.5\,\mathrm{eV}.}
$$
The reduced effective charge expresses screening. Neglecting repulsion gives $-4E_h$, while its expectation in the unscreened $\zeta=2$ product gives $-2.75E_h$; optimizing the orbital improves that trial estimate. The exact ground <energy> is lower than the optimized product <energy>, since the product omits explicit <electron correlation>. This calculation keeps nuclear charge two in $H$; only the trial orbital parameter is varied.

For a <singly excited helium state> with distinct orthonormal orbitals $a=1s$ and $b=n\ell$, the spatial combinations are
$$
\Phi_\pm=\frac{a(1)b(2)\pm b(1)a(2)}{\sqrt2}.
$$
The symmetric $\Phi_+$ pairs with the antisymmetric <spin> singlet, while antisymmetric $\Phi_-$ pairs with the symmetric <spin> triplet. With the same orbitals, their leading energies are
$$
E_\pm=\epsilon_a+\epsilon_b+J_{ab}\pm K_{ab},\qquad
\boxed{E_{\rm singlet}-E_{\rm triplet}=2K_{ab}.}
$$
Here $J_{ab}$ is the direct <Coulomb integral> and $K_{ab}$ is the <exchange integral>. The latter is positive for the <Coulomb interaction>, so the triplet is lower for this fixed-orbital comparison. <singlet-triplet exchange splitting in helium> arises from antisymmetry and spatial correlation in the <Coulomb interaction>, not from inserting a separate classical spin-spin force. Spin-singlet <helium> states are <parahelium>; spin-triplet <helium> states are <orthohelium>. The two spectra approximately form separate families because the leading electric dipole operator does not change total <spin>.<Topics in Quantum Mechanics by David Tong>[§3.2.2, Helium and the Exchange Energy, printed pp. 91–96: spatial/spin symmetry and exchange splitting]{ref}

The lowest $1s2s$ terms are $ {}^3S_1$ and $ {}^1S_0$, with the triplet lower. The $1s2p$ configuration gives $ {}^3P_{0,1,2}^{\rm o}$ and $ {}^1P_1^{\rm o}$, where the odd <parity> allows singlet $P$ to connect to the even-parity ground singlet by an <electric dipole transition>. Both $1s2s$ levels lack an allowed direct electric dipole decay to $1s^2$: the triplet is <spin> forbidden, and singlet $S\to S$ is forbidden by <orbital angular momentum> and <parity>. This explains <helium metastability> without implying an infinite lifetime once weaker radiative processes or collisions are allowed.

\Image[paper-1-helium.png]
{title=Low neutral-helium singlet and triplet levels, plotted from independently cited tabulated energies}

The observed first $1s2s$ excitation energies are approximately $19.82$ and $20.62\,\mathrm{eV}$ for the triplet and singlet, respectively. Higher singly excited Rydberg series approach the He II ground-state ionization threshold; doubly excited states above that threshold can autoionize and are not all stable bound excited levels.<Energy Levels of Neutral Helium by National Institute of Standards and Technology>[1s2s and 1s2p configuration rows; He II ground-state limit at 198310.6691 inverse centimetres]{ref}

Introduce a bookkeeping parameter $\lambda$ in $H=H_0+\lambda H_1$ and expand $|\phi_n\rangle=|\psi_n\rangle+\lambda|\eta_n\rangle+O(\lambda^2)$ and $\mathcal E_n=E_n+\lambda\Delta E_n+O(\lambda^2)$. Fix <phase> and normalization at this order by $\langle\psi_n|\eta_n\rangle=0$. The first-order Schrödinger equation is
$$
(H_0-E_n)|\eta_n\rangle=-(H_1-\Delta E_n)|\psi_n\rangle.
$$
<angular momentum> projection onto $\langle\psi_n|$ gives $\Delta E_n=\langle\psi_n|H_1|\psi_n\rangle$. <angular momentum> projection onto a different <eigenstate> gives
$$
\langle\psi_j|\eta_n\rangle=\frac{\langle\psi_j|H_1|\psi_n\rangle}{E_n-E_j},\qquad j\ne n.
$$
Completeness therefore yields the <first-order nondegenerate perturbation theory> result
$$
\boxed{|\phi_n\rangle=|\psi_n\rangle+\sum_{j\ne n}
\frac{\langle\psi_j|H_1|\psi_n\rangle}{E_n-E_j}|\psi_j\rangle+O(H_1^2).}
$$
Nonzero <energy> denominators are essential. The displayed state has unit norm through first order; exact normalization would change terms of second order. A continuum, if present, contributes its corresponding <integral> as well as any discrete sum.

For the oscillator in a static <electric field>, electrostatic <potential energy> is $H_1=-q\epsilon x$ and $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}$. <parity> gives $\langle0|x|0\rangle=0$, and the supplied Gaussian <wavefunctions> give
$$
x_{\rm zp}\equiv\langle1|x|0\rangle
=\sqrt{2\alpha}\int x^2|\psi_0(x)|^2dx
=\frac1{\sqrt{2\alpha}}=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}.
$$
Since $E_0-E_1=-\hbar\omega$, the two-state first-order <ground state> is
$$
|\phi_0\rangle=|0\rangle+\eta|1\rangle+O(\epsilon^2),\qquad
\eta=\frac{q\epsilon x_{\rm zp}}{\hbar\omega}.
$$
Thus $\langle x\rangle=2\eta x_{\rm zp}+O(\epsilon^2)$, and the induced electric dipole is
$$
\boxed{d=q\langle x\rangle=\frac{q^2\epsilon}{m\omega^2}=\frac{q^2}{k}\epsilon
\quad\text{to first order}.}
$$
It is aligned with the field for either sign of $q$. The <harmonic-oscillator electric polarizability> is $q^2/k$. The <position operator> connects $|0\rangle$ only to $|1\rangle$, so the requested two-state calculation contains the complete first-order dipole response, rather than dropping other first-order terms.

Complete the square to find the exact <electric-field displacement of a quantum harmonic oscillator>:
$$
H=\frac{p^2}{2m}+\frac12k(x-x_0)^2-\frac{q^2\epsilon^2}{2k},\qquad x_0=\frac{q\epsilon}{k}.
$$
The exact normalized ground <wavefunction> is $\psi_0(x-x_0)$, with <energy> $\hbar\omega/2-q^2\epsilon^2/(2k)$. Its mean displacement is $x_0$, so
$$
\boxed{d_{\rm exact}=q x_0=\frac{q^2\epsilon}{k}.}
$$
**The first-order dipole equals the exact dipole**, because the restoring force is exactly linear and translating a harmonic well does not change its width or <frequency>. This agreement of one expectation value does not make the truncated <wavefunction> exact. The <Hellmann–Feynman theorem> provides an independent check: $d=-\partial E_0/\partial\epsilon$.

For <perturbation theory> to approximate the state, $|\eta|\ll1$, or $|x_0|\ll2x_{\rm zp}$. The exact translated <ground state> has the original-oscillator expansion
$$
|0;x_0\rangle=e^{-\eta^2/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\eta^n}{\sqrt{n!}}|n\rangle.
$$
It is a <coherent state> with mean occupation $\eta^2$. Large fields require many oscillator levels, whereas the first-order state retains only two. The <normalized two-state truncation of a displaced oscillator> instead gives
$$
|\phi_{01}\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle+\eta|1\rangle}{\sqrt{1+\eta^2}},\qquad
\langle x\rangle_{01}=\frac{x_0}{1+\eta^2},
$$
which incorrectly tends towards zero for large $|\eta|$ instead of following the displaced well. In <position> space the truncated polynomial times Gaussian also develops a node absent from the exact <ground state>. Taking a linearized formula outside its small-state-correction regime is therefore invalid even though its dipole coefficient happens to be exact.

Insert the interaction-picture expansion into the Schrödinger equation. The <phases> multiplying the basis states already solve the unperturbed evolution, leaving
$$
i\hbar\dot c_r=\sum_s H'_{rs}\cos(\omega t)
\,e^{i(\omega_r-\omega_s)t}c_s.
$$
Hermiticity requires $H'_{21}=\hbar(\omega')^*$, not $\hbar\omega'$ unless the coupling is real. Since the diagonal <matrix elements> vanish and $\omega_0=\omega_2-\omega_1$, expanding the cosine gives
$$
\boxed{i\dot c_2=\frac{(\omega')^*}{2}c_1
[e^{i(\omega+\omega_0)t}+e^{-i(\omega-\omega_0)t}],}
$$
$$
\boxed{i\dot c_1=\frac{\omega'}{2}c_2
[e^{i(\omega-\omega_0)t}+e^{-i(\omega+\omega_0)t}].}
$$
The conjugate pair of couplings conserves $|c_1|^2+|c_2|^2$. Near resonance, the difference <frequency> is slow while the sum <frequency> is approximately $2\omega_0$. Rapid terms alternate sign before an appreciable slow transfer accumulates. The <rotating-wave approximation> retains the slow terms when $|\omega'|,|\omega-\omega_0|\ll\omega+\omega_0$; resonance alone is insufficient for a strong drive. Counter-rotating corrections include small shifts of the resonance and need not remain negligible at arbitrary strength or arbitrarily long times.

For the <spin> preparation, use $H=-\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B=-\gamma\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf B$ and $\mathbf s=\hbar\boldsymbol\sigma/2$. Choose $B>0$ and $\gamma>0$ to fix helicity, with $|\uparrow\rangle=(1,0)^T$, $|\downarrow\rangle=(0,1)^T$ and $\omega_0=\gamma B$. The initial $|\uparrow\rangle$ is the lower state. For negative $\gamma$, reverse the RF rotation sense; <pulse areas> use $|\gamma|$, and signed <Larmor precession> determines the corresponding <phase> adjustment.

A <circularly rotating magnetic drive> uses two transverse fields in quadrature during $0<t<\tau$:
$$
\mathbf B(t)=(B_1\cos(\omega_0t+\varphi),\,-B_1\sin(\omega_0t+\varphi),\,B),
\qquad \Omega=\gamma B_1>0.
$$
With $U_0(t)=e^{+i\omega_0t\sigma_z/2}$, their interaction <Hamiltonian> is exactly constant,
$$
H_I=-\frac{\hbar\Omega}{2}(\cos\varphi\,\sigma_x-\sin\varphi\,\sigma_y).
$$
The minus sign in the second RF component selects co-rotation for the specified positive <gyromagnetic ratio>. Exponentiating this Pauli matrix gives
$$
|\psi_I(t)\rangle=\cos\frac{\Omega t}{2}|\uparrow\rangle
+i e^{-i\varphi}\sin\frac{\Omega t}{2}|\downarrow\rangle.
$$
The <laboratory phase of a resonant spin pulse> includes free precession. Returning to the laboratory frame and removing a common <phase>,
$$
\boxed{|\psi(t)\rangle\sim
\cos\frac{\Omega t}{2}|\uparrow\rangle+
 i e^{-i(\varphi+\omega_0t)}\sin\frac{\Omega t}{2}|\downarrow\rangle.}
$$
Unlike a linearly polarized RF field, this exactly co-rotating field has no counter-rotating component. A single linearly polarized field $B_{x,\rm peak}\cos(\omega_0t+\varphi)$ gives the same slow rotation in the weak-drive <rotating-wave approximation> with **$\Omega=|\gamma|B_{x,\rm peak}/2$**. For the circular field above, **$\Omega=|\gamma|B_1$**. Keeping these amplitude conventions distinct avoids a factor-of-two pulse-duration error.

\Image[paper-1-spin-pulses.png]
{title=Resonant rotations in rotating axes and the maximum transfer permitted by a fixed detuned pulse}

For a single harmonic pulse detuned by $\Delta=\omega_0-\omega$, the effective rotating-frame field tilts out of the transverse plane. Its rotation rate becomes $\sqrt{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}$, and its maximum population transfer is $\Omega^2/(\Omega^2+\Delta^2)$. **Exact inversion to $-z$ is impossible with one fixed nonzero-detuning pulse**. Half transfer, needed for a transverse <eigenstate>, gives the <equatorial reachability of a detuned spin pulse> criterion **$|\Delta|\leq\Omega$**. With that condition, changing the duration and RF <phase> can still prepare $+x$ or $+y$ at the chosen laboratory stopping time. The mere statement $\omega\approx\omega_0$ does not ensure this condition, because the drive can be much weaker than either <frequency>. If $|\Delta|>\Omega$, neither transverse target is reached by that single pulse. These restrictions concern fixed-frequency, fixed-amplitude single-pulse control; retuning, chirping or phase-controlled composite pulses change the available controls and can remove the restriction. No dissipative <spin relaxation> is included in this coherent two-state model.

The $+x$ <eigenstate> is $|+x\rangle=(|\uparrow\rangle+|\downarrow\rangle)/\sqrt2$, since $\sigma_x(1,1)^T=(1,1)^T$. Use a resonant quarter-turn pulse, $\tau_{\pi/2}=\pi/(2\Omega)$, and choose its <phase> so that
$$
\varphi+\omega_0\tau_{\pi/2}=\frac\pi2\pmod{2\pi}.
$$
The laboratory-frame relative amplitude then is $i e^{-i\pi/2}=1$, giving
$$
\boxed{|\psi(\tau_{\pi/2})\rangle\sim|+x\rangle,\qquad
\langle s_x\rangle=\hbar/2.}
$$
In rotating axes the equivalent target is obtained with $\varphi=\pi/2$; the extra laboratory <phase> above compensates <Larmor precession> during the pulse. After switching the RF field off, the <spin> continues to precess in the static field, so this statement specifies the preparation instant rather than a state fixed along laboratory $x$ forever.

The $+y$ <eigenstate> is $|+y\rangle=(|\uparrow\rangle+i|\downarrow\rangle)/\sqrt2$, with $\sigma_y(1,i)^T=(1,i)^T$. Use the same duration $\tau_{\pi/2}=\pi/(2\Omega)$ but <phase>
$$
\varphi+\omega_0\tau_{\pi/2}=0\pmod{2\pi}.
$$
The relative amplitude is now $i$, giving
$$
\boxed{|\psi(\tau_{\pi/2})\rangle\sim|+y\rangle,\qquad
\langle s_y\rangle=\hbar/2.}
$$
The two transverse preparations differ by a quarter-cycle of RF <phase>, not by changing the <pulse area>. Reversing the desired transverse direction adds $\pi$ to the chosen <phase>.

For $-z$, use a resonant half-turn pulse,
$$
\boxed{\tau_\pi=\pi/\Omega.}
$$
The up amplitude vanishes because $\cos(\Omega\tau_\pi/2)=0$, while the down amplitude has unit magnitude. Its <phase> is a common <phase> of the final state, so any RF <phase> gives
$$
\boxed{|\psi(\tau_\pi)\rangle\sim|\downarrow\rangle,
\qquad \langle s_z\rangle=-\hbar/2.}
$$
The final population inversion is insensitive to a free-precession <phase>, unlike the orientation of a transverse <superposition>. This is a <Rabi pi pulse> with the drive-amplitude convention specified above.
