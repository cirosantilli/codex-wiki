= Solution

Use the **number-density <virial expansion>**, following the <density versus molar second virial convention>, with $n=N/V$:
$$
\frac{p}{nk_BT}=1+B_2(T)n+O(n^2).
$$
Thus $B_2$ is the coefficient of the first <density> correction and has units of <volume>. With molar <density> instead, the molar coefficient is $N_AB_2$. For a classical pair potential $u(r)$,
$$
\boxed{B_2(T)=-\frac12\int\left(e^{-u(r)/(k_BT)}-1\right)d^3r=2\pi\int_0^\infty\left[1-e^{-u(r)/(k_BT)}\right]r^2dr.}
$$
Attractive separations contribute negatively and short-range repulsion contributes positively. For inert-gas interactions the attractive contribution dominates at sufficiently low <temperature>, $B_2$ crosses zero at the <Boyle temperature>, and repulsion dominates above it.

\Image[paper-1-second-virial.png]
{title=An illustrative Lennard-Jones second virial coefficient is negative at low temperature, crosses zero and has a broad positive maximum}
{source=paper-1-second-virial.py}

The <soft-core high-temperature second virial coefficient> illustrates why the far-high-temperature tail depends on the model. A hard-core estimate $B_2\simeq b-a/(k_BT)$ tends to positive excluded <volume> $b$. A soft repulsive potential, such as the <Lennard-Jones potential> in the original diagram, allows a positive maximum followed by decay toward zero. The sign change is the main attraction-versus-repulsion feature; neither sketch should be interpreted as measured data for a specified gas. This classical, dilute-gas sketch omits low-temperature quantum-exchange corrections.<Classical Gases by David Tong>[Section 2.5: interacting gas, virial expansion and Mayer pair function]{ref}

Take $\mu>0$ to be the magnitude of either <spin> projection's <magnetic moment>, so the independent magnetic levels are $E_\pm=\mp\mu B$. Their <partition function> and magnetic <energy> are
$$
Z_1=2\cosh x,\qquad Z=(2\cosh x)^N,\qquad U_{\rm mag}=-\partial_\beta\ln Z=-N\mu B\tanh x,
$$
where $\beta=1/(k_BT)$ and $x=\beta\mu B$. The <canonical entropy> is consequently
$$
\boxed{S=k_B\ln Z+\frac{U_{\rm mag}}T=Nk_B[\ln(2\cosh x)-x\tanh x].}
$$
This <independent-spin magnetic entropy> counts the two orientation states of each independent moment; translational <entropy> and interactions are excluded.

Along the <magnetic-entropy demagnetization adiabat>, constant magnetic <entropy> fixes $x$, hence **$B/T$ is constant**. Lowering the field therefore gives the ideal <adiabatic demagnetization> relation
$$
\boxed{T_f=T_i\frac{B_f}{B_i}.}
$$
A useful cycle first magnetizes isothermally while releasing heat to a reservoir, then thermally isolates the sample and lowers the field. Cooling the entire sample requires its other <heat capacities> and spin-lattice equilibration to be accounted for. Magnetic interactions, residual fields and heat leaks prevent extrapolating the independent-moment formula to an attainable zero <temperature>.<The Fundamentals of Statistical Mechanics by David Tong>[Canonical ensemble: two-state partition function, entropy and thermodynamic derivatives]{ref}

For a complete single-particle state, the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> is
$$
\boxed{\langle n(\epsilon)\rangle=\frac1{\exp[(\epsilon-\mu_{\rm ch})/(k_BT)]+1}.}
$$
Here $\mu_{\rm ch}$ denotes the <chemical potential>, distinct from the magnetic-moment symbol in (b). At zero <temperature> it approaches the <Fermi energy> $E_F$ and the occupation has a sharp edge. At $T\ll T_F=E_F/k_B$, thermal changes occur within an <energy> interval of order $k_BT$ about that edge.

The <Fermi-edge heat-capacity counting> argument uses the fact that only order $g(E_F)k_BT$ <electrons> can be rearranged because states well below the edge are blocked by the <Pauli exclusion principle>. Each active excitation carries <energy> of order $k_BT$, so $U(T)-U(0)\sim g(E_F)(k_BT)^2$ and **$C_{V,e}\propto T$**. For a smooth <density of states>, the <Sommerfeld expansion> gives
$$
\boxed{C_{V,e}=\frac{\pi^2}{3}g(E_F)k_B^2T.}
$$
Here $g$ is the total single-particle <density of states>, including <spin>. In a three-dimensional free-electron gas, $g(E_F)=3N/(2E_F)$, giving $C_{V,e}=(\pi^2/2)Nk_B(T/T_F)$. This is the low-temperature normal-state electronic contribution, not the lattice <heat capacity> or a description across a superconducting transition.<Quantum Gases by David Tong>[Sections 3.6.2–3.6.4: Fermi edge, low-temperature heat capacity and Sommerfeld expansion]{ref}

An **<statistical ensemble> specifies probabilities of microstates under particular macroscopic constraints**. It represents statistical information about a system; the <reservoir constraints of equilibrium ensembles> specify what it can exchange with its surroundings.

* The <microcanonical ensemble> has fixed $E,V,N$. Accessible states in a narrow <energy> shell are equally probable, with $S=k_B\ln\Omega$, where $\Omega$ counts the states in that shell. It describes an isolated system.
* The <canonical ensemble> has fixed $T,V,N$ and can exchange <energy> with a heat reservoir. A microstate has probability $p_i=e^{-\beta E_i}/Z$, with $Z=\sum_i e^{-\beta E_i}$ and $F=-k_BT\ln Z$. The <energy> fluctuates; its mean is not imposed independently.
* The <grand canonical ensemble> has fixed $T,V,\mu_{\rm ch}$ and exchanges both particles and <energy>. Its probabilities are proportional to $e^{-\beta(E_i-\mu_{\rm ch}N_i)}$, with $\Xi=\sum_N e^{\beta\mu_{\rm ch}N}Z_N$ and <grand potential> $\Omega_G=-k_BT\ln\Xi$. For a homogeneous extensive equilibrium system, $\Omega_G=-pV$.

The <thermodynamic potentials> generate mean quantities and response functions. For example, canonical <energy> variance is $\langle(\Delta E)^2\rangle=k_BT^2C_V$. With short-range interactions and stable bulk phases, relative fluctuations usually vanish for large systems and these ensembles give equivalent bulk predictions after matching their mean variables. Finite systems, constraints and long-range interactions require greater care; the ensembles retain different fluctuating variables.<The Fundamentals of Statistical Mechanics by David Tong>[Sections 1.2–1.4: microcanonical, canonical and grand canonical ensembles]{ref}

**<Brownian motion> is the irregular motion of a mesoscopic particle caused by thermal molecular impacts.** A colloidal sphere in a fluid provides a physical example. The random force competes with dissipative drag; averaging many trajectories produces <diffusion> even though each trajectory fluctuates.

A free-particle <Langevin equation> with <thermal Langevin force normalization> is
$$
m\dot{\mathbf v}=-\zeta\mathbf v+\boldsymbol\xi(t),\qquad\langle\xi_i(t)\xi_j(t')\rangle=2\zeta k_BT\,\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t').
$$
The noise has zero mean, and its strength is linked to drag by the <fluctuation-dissipation theorem>. The velocity relaxes on the time scale $\tau=m/\zeta$. At long times $t\gg\tau$, the <Einstein diffusion relation> is
$$
\boxed{D=\frac{k_BT}{\zeta},\qquad\langle|\mathbf r(t)-\mathbf r(0)|^2\rangle=6Dt\quad\hbox{in three dimensions}.}
$$
For a slowly moving no-slip sphere of radius $a$ in a continuum fluid of <dynamic viscosity> $\eta$, <Stokes drag law> gives $\zeta=6\pi\eta a$. The particle probability <density> obeys $\partial_tP=D\nabla^2P$; a localized initial position develops a Gaussian profile. The <ballistic-to-diffusive Brownian displacement> changes character: at $t\ll\tau$, equilibrium velocities instead give ballistic mean-square displacement $3(k_BT/m)t^2$.

\Image[paper-1-brownian-motion.png]
{title=An illustrative random trajectory and the inertial crossover from ballistic to diffusive mean-square displacement}
{source=paper-1-brownian-motion.py}

The drag-noise model assumes a classical bath with a sufficiently short correlation time; a mathematical white-noise trajectory does not resolve individual molecular collisions. Tracking nonliving colloidal particles measures <diffusion> and tests the connection between thermal fluctuations and dissipation.<Stochastic Processes by David Tong>[Sections 3.1–3.2: Brownian motion, Langevin equation, diffusion and the Einstein relation]{ref}

**<Landau theory> expands the <free energy> in a small <order parameter> and keeps terms allowed by the system's symmetries.** For a scalar <order parameter> $q$ with <sign-reversal symmetry in Landau theory> $q\mapsto-q$, a useful free-energy <density> is
$$
f(q,T,h)=f_0(T)+\frac a2(T-T_c)q^2+\frac b4q^4-hq,\qquad a,b>0.
$$
At zero conjugate field $h$, the <quartic Landau equilibrium branches> obtained by minimizing are
$$
\boxed{q_{\rm eq}=0\quad(T>T_c),\qquad q_{\rm eq}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{a(T_c-T)}b}\quad(T<T_c).}
$$
The single minimum becomes two symmetry-related minima: the <order parameter> turns on continuously and a chosen equilibrium phase spontaneously breaks the symmetry. At the <critical temperature> the quadratic restoring term vanishes. The mean-field exponent is $q\propto(T_c-T)^{1/2}$; the susceptibility above $T_c$ is $\partial q/\partial h=1/[a(T-T_c)]$.

\Image[paper-1-landau-free-energy.png]
{title=A symmetric quartic Landau free energy changes from one minimum to two as its quadratic coefficient changes sign}
{source=paper-1-landau-free-energy.py}

More general allowed terms describe other transitions. A negative quartic term, stabilized by a positive sixth-order term, can give a first-order transition with a discontinuous <order parameter> and metastable minima. Without the sign-reversal symmetry, a cubic term may also be allowed. The expansion is phenomenological: its coefficients depend on microscopic physics. Minimizing a uniform polynomial neglects important spatial fluctuations, particularly near a <critical point>, so its <critical exponents> need not be exact.<Phase Transitions by David Tong>[Section 5.4: Landau free energy, symmetry and mean-field transitions]{ref}

= Solution

Measure energies from the single-particle <ground state>, and assume the **three-dimensional ideal Bose gas in the large-particle-number limit**. The <harmonic-trap chemical-potential pinning> discussed here uses a <chemical potential> satisfying $\mu_{\rm ch}\leq0$. On cooling, it approaches zero; below the condensation <temperature> it remains at zero to leading thermodynamic order. Above that <temperature> it is negative. The detailed negative-chemical-potential branch shape is determined by the <density of states>; the figure uses the harmonic trap analysed below. The <finite-size condensate chemical potential> stays slightly below zero rather than being exactly pinned there.

A **<Bose-Einstein condensate> has macroscopic occupation of a single quantum state**. A dilute atomic gas needs low <temperature> and high phase-space <density> while remaining metastable against ordinary liquid or solid formation. <Laser cooling> followed by <evaporative cooling of atoms> in a weak trap can achieve the required regime. For the trapped ideal gas the quantitative threshold is derived below; a uniform ideal gas has $n\lambda_T^3=\zeta(3/2)$ at onset. An experimental signature is a narrow low-momentum component appearing within the broader thermal distribution after time-of-flight expansion, as observed in magnetically trapped rubidium-87.<Observation of Bose-Einstein Condensation in a Dilute Atomic Vapor by M. H. Anderson and others>[1995 rubidium experiment: low-velocity condensate component and nonthermal anisotropic expansion]{ref}

**<Spinless cubic-box wave-vector counting>.** Impose <periodic boundary conditions> on a cube of side $L$. The allowed vectors are $\mathbf k=(2\pi/L)(n_x,n_y,n_z)$ with integer components. Each state occupies $ (2\pi/L)^3$ in wave-vector space. For a spinless particle, counting the spherical shell gives
$$
d\mathcal N=\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3}4\pi k^2dk,\qquad\boxed{g_V(k)=\frac1V\frac{d\mathcal N}{dk}=\frac{k^2}{2\pi^2}.}
$$
There is no <spin> multiplicity. This is continuum shell counting; a finite box has discrete levels. Using $\epsilon=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)$ gives the free-particle <density of states> per unit <energy> and <volume>
$$
g_V(\epsilon)=\frac{(2m)^{3/2}}{4\pi^2\hbar^3}\sqrt\epsilon.
$$

**<Semiclassical density of states in a central potential>.** At position $\mathbf r$, the available <kinetic energy> is $\epsilon-U(r)$. The local-density approximation integrates the preceding free-particle result over the classically allowed region:
$$
g(\epsilon)=\int_{U<\epsilon}\frac{(2m)^{3/2}}{4\pi^2\hbar^3}\sqrt{\epsilon-U(r)}\,d^3r=\boxed{\frac{(2m)^{3/2}}{\pi\hbar^3}\int_0^{r_\epsilon}r^2\sqrt{\epsilon-U(r)}\,dr.}
$$
This form assumes the allowed radial region is the connected interval $0\leq r\leq r_\epsilon$. A nonmonotonic potential would require integrating all allowed shells.

For the isotropic harmonic trap, $r_\epsilon=\sqrt{2\epsilon/(m\omega^2)}$. Substituting $r=r_\epsilon\sin\vartheta$ gives
$$
\int_0^{r_\epsilon}r^2\sqrt{\epsilon-\tfrac12m\omega^2r^2}\,dr=r_\epsilon^3\sqrt\epsilon\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^2\vartheta\cos^2\vartheta\,d\vartheta=\frac\pi{16}r_\epsilon^3\sqrt\epsilon.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{g(\epsilon)=\frac{\epsilon^2}{2(\hbar\omega)^3}.}
$$
The exact oscillator ground <energy> is $3\hbar\omega/2$ above the potential minimum. The <harmonic-trap energy-zero convention> subtracts that constant, while the displayed <density of states> is the leading semiclassical continuum expression, neglecting the finite-level corrections produced by that shift. The discrete <ground state> must be counted separately.

**Condensate population.** Below the transition, use $\mu_{\rm ch}\simeq0$ for the excited states. The <saturation of harmonic-trap excited states> follows from the supplied integral:
$$
N_{\rm ex}=\int_0^\infty\frac{g(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon}{e^{\epsilon/(k_BT)}-1}=\frac12\left(\frac{k_BT}{\hbar\omega}\right)^3\int_0^\infty\frac{x^2dx}{e^x-1}=\zeta(3)\left(\frac{k_BT}{\hbar\omega}\right)^3,
$$
where $\zeta$ is the <Riemann zeta function> and $\zeta(3)\simeq1.202$. Setting $N_{\rm ex}(T_0)=N$ gives the <harmonic-trap critical-temperature prefactor>:
$$
\boxed{T_0=\frac{\hbar\omega}{k_B}\left(\frac N{\zeta(3)}\right)^{1/3}\simeq0.9405\frac{\hbar\omega}{k_B}N^{1/3},\qquad \frac{N_0(T)}N=1-\left(\frac T{T_0}\right)^3\quad(T<T_0).}
$$
Above $T_0$ there is no macroscopic ground-state fraction in this approximation. For completeness, above the transition the <quantum gas fugacity> $z=e^{\mu_{\rm ch}/(k_BT)}<1$ satisfies $N=(k_BT/\hbar\omega)^3\operatorname{Li}_3(z)$; the <polylogarithm> $\operatorname{Li}_3$ therefore defines the plotted negative branch. A finite trapped gas has rounded behaviour, weak interactions shift the threshold, and the continuum thermal population is inaccurate when $k_BT$ is comparable to or below $\hbar\omega$. The power law is intended for the large-$N$ condensed regime, not an exact discrete-spectrum formula at arbitrarily small <temperature>.<Quantum Gases by David Tong>[Section 3.5: Bose distribution, excited-state saturation and separate condensate population; trap density-of-states calculation derived here]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=4}

For the **ideal dilute Maxwell-Boltzmann gas** implicit in a single-particle <partition function>, indistinguishability gives $Z_N=Z_1^N/N!$. With <Stirling's approximation>,
$$
F=-k_BT\ln Z_N\simeq-Nk_BT\ln Z_1+k_BT(N\ln N-N).
$$
Differentiate at fixed $T,V$:
$$
\boxed{\mu_{\rm ch}=\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial N}\right)_{T,V}=k_BT\ln\frac N{Z_1}=-k_BT[\ln Z_1-\ln N].}
$$
This is a thermodynamic-limit ideal-gas result; arbitrary interacting classical gases do not have $Z_N=Z_1^N/N!$.

**Equilibrium constraints and the Gibbs minimum.** The <open-system equilibrium contacts> allow exchange of heat, <volume> and matter. For unconstrained contacts without external-field or interface corrections, equilibrium requires equal temperatures, pressures and each exchangeable species' <chemical potentials> across the contacts. These conditions follow by maximizing total <entropy> under conserved total <energy>, <volume> and species amounts.

The next minimization statement fixes the system's particle number. For a system in a reservoir at $T_0,p_0$, with $N$ fixed and only pressure-volume work, the reservoir <entropy> change gives
$$
\Delta S_{\rm tot}=\Delta S-\frac{\Delta U+p_0\Delta V}{T_0}=-\frac{\Delta(U+p_0V-T_0S)}{T_0}\geq0.
$$
For candidate states at the imposed $T=T_0$, $p=p_0$, the expression in parentheses is $G$. Thus **the <fixed-particle-number Gibbs minimum> selects stable equilibrium at fixed $T,p,N$**, with stationary first variation and nonnegative allowed second variation. If particle exchange remains unconstrained, $N$ is not fixed and this minimization statement must be replaced by one incorporating the particle reservoir; it is not a justification for minimizing $G$ over arbitrary $N$.

The simple-compressible-system first law gives
$$
dG=d(U-TS+pV)=\boxed{-S\,dT+V\,dp+\mu_{\rm ch}\,dN}.
$$
For a homogeneous extensive single-component phase, the <single-component Gibbs Euler relation> follows from $U=TS-pV+\mu_{\rm ch}N$, yielding **$G=\mu_{\rm ch}N$**. For a mixture, the corresponding identities are $G=\sum_i\mu_iN_i$ and $dG=-S\,dT+V\,dp+\sum_i\mu_i\,dN_i$. Surface effects or other nonextensive contributions require additional terms.<Classical Thermodynamics by David Tong>[Free energies, chemical potential and equilibrium with imposed reservoir variables]{ref}

**Reaction equilibrium.** Let $d\xi$ be an allowed reaction extent for the forward process, so $dN_A=-d\xi$ and $dN_B=2d\xi$. At fixed $T,p$,
$$
dG=(2\mu_B-\mu_A)d\xi.
$$
For an interior equilibrium allowing both reaction directions, the coefficient vanishes:
$$
\boxed{\mu_A=2\mu_B.}
$$
At fixed $T,V$, minimizing $F$ gives the same condition. The <reaction formula-unit conservation> gives $M=N_A+N_B/2$, not the total number $N_A+N_B$ of gas particles. This <conservation law> replaces fixed total particle number for the reacting mixture.

Using the stipulated single-particle functions,
$$
\mu_i=k_BT\ln\frac{N_i}{C_iVT^{3/2}},\qquad\frac{N_A}{C_AVT^{3/2}}=\left(\frac{N_B}{C_BVT^{3/2}}\right)^2.
$$
The <number-based dissociation equilibrium ratio> is therefore
$$
\boxed{K_N(V,T)=\frac{N_B^2}{N_A}=\frac{C_B^2}{C_A}VT^{3/2}.}
$$
This number-based quantity has a factor of <volume>; the corresponding concentration ratio $n_B^2/n_A=K_N/V$ depends on <temperature> alone in the stated ideal model. A standard activity-based equilibrium constant would additionally specify reference states. No bond-energy factor is added to the functions given in the paper.

Define the dissociated fraction of original A formula units by $\alpha=N_B/(2M)$. Then $N_A=M(1-\alpha)$ and $N_B=2M\alpha$, so
$$
\frac{\alpha^2}{1-\alpha}=\frac{K_N}{4M}\equiv q,\qquad\boxed{\alpha=\frac{\sqrt{q^2+4q}-q}{2}.}
$$
The <dissociation fraction versus conserved density> follows because the left side increases monotonically from zero to infinity on $0<\alpha<1$. At fixed $V,T$, increasing $M$ decreases $q$, hence **decreases dissociation**. Equivalently, at fixed $M,T$ compression raises $M/V$ and favours A. Association produces fewer independent translating particles; at high <density> the translational-entropy advantage of the two-particle side is smaller, so equilibrium shifts toward the one-particle side.
