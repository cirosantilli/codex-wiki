= Solution

Use <thermal de Broglie wavelength> $\lambda_T=h/\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}$ and spacing $a=n^{-1/3}$. <Degeneracy onset from thermal wavelength> occurs at
$$
\boxed{T_\lambda=\frac{h^2n^{2/3}}{2\pi mk_B}.}
$$
The numerical factor depends on the convention for a typical <momentum>, but the scale $\hbar^2n^{2/3}/(mk_B)$ does not. For <fermions> the <Fermi temperature> $T_F=\hbar^2(3\pi^2n)^{2/3}/(2mk_B)$ gives a comparable scale for spin degeneracy two.

Representative estimates, with the assumed <densities> stated explicitly, are:

|| System
|| Assumed <number density>
|| Assumed particle <mass>
|| $T_\lambda$

| <Conduction electrons>
| $10^{29}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$
| $m_e$
| $1.2\times10^5\,\mathrm K$

| Dilute heavy atomic cloud
| $10^{19}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$
| $87u$
| $1.6\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm K$

| <Neutrons> at nuclear-scale <density>
| $10^{44}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$
| $m_n$
| $6.5\times10^{11}\,\mathrm K$

Here $u$ is the atomic <mass> constant. Thus the characteristic orders are **$10^5\,\mathrm K$ for <electrons>, sub-microkelvin for a dilute heavy atomic gas, and $10^{12}\,\mathrm K$ for <neutrons>**. There is no density-independent quantum-degeneracy <temperature> for atomic gases: for example, taking $n=2.5\times10^{25}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$ and <mass> $4u$ instead gives about $0.065\,\mathrm K$. These are illustrative input estimates, not values supplied by the paper. Denser neutron-star cores require relativistic and interaction corrections; the estimate identifies the degeneracy scale rather than a precise stellar <temperature>.

= Solution

Assume three-dimensional isotropic gapless excitations, zero <chemical potential> and one independent branch. Momentum-state counting gives
$$
\frac{g(\epsilon)}V=\frac{1}{2\pi^2\hbar^3n}\alpha^{-3/n}\epsilon^{3/n-1}.
$$
Substituting $x=\epsilon/(k_BT)$ into the Bose <energy> integral shows $U/V\propto T^{1+3/n}$ and $C/V\propto T^{3/n}$. The observed exponent therefore gives **$n=2$**.

For the <quadratic-boson heat-capacity coefficient>, the required Bose integral is
$$
I_{3/2}=\int_0^\infty\frac{x^{3/2}\,dx}{e^x-1}
=\Gamma(5/2)\zeta(5/2)=1.78329.
$$
The equality follows by expanding $(e^x-1)^{-1}=\sum_{r\ge1}e^{-rx}$ and integrating term by term. Hence
$$
\frac UV=\frac{I_{3/2}(k_BT)^{5/2}}{4\pi^2\hbar^3\alpha^{3/2}},\qquad
\frac CV=\frac{5I_{3/2}k_B^{5/2}}{8\pi^2\hbar^3\alpha^{3/2}}T^{3/2}.
$$
The supplied units make its $C$ a <heat capacity> per <volume>. Equating its coefficient to $220\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-5/2}\,m^{-3}}$ gives
$$
\boxed{\alpha=4.58\times10^{27}\,\mathrm{kg^{-1}}.}
$$
Equivalently $\epsilon=p^2/(2m_*)$ has $m_*=1.09\times10^{-28}\,\mathrm{kg}$. For $q_b$ identical branches, the measured coefficient fixes $q_b/\alpha^{3/2}$, so instead $\alpha=(4.58\times10^{27}\,\mathrm{kg^{-1}})q_b^{2/3}$. The one-branch convention is necessary for the quoted numerical value. The supplied integral with $x^4$ is not the moment arising for the exponent determined here.

For either <phase>, the <entropy> differential at fixed composition is
$$
dS=\frac{C_P}{T}\,dT-\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P dP
=\frac{C_P}{T}\,dT-V\alpha_T\,dP.
$$
The pressure derivative uses the <Maxwell relation> from $dG=-S\,dT+V\,dP$. Differentiating the equality of <entropies> along coexistence gives the general relation
$$
\frac{\Delta C_P}{T}\,dT=\Delta(V\alpha_T)\,dP.
$$
For a differentiable coexistence curve with pressure varying, equality of <Gibbs free energies> also implies $-\Delta S\,dT+\Delta V\,dP=0$. Since $\Delta S=0$, the two <phases> then share $V$. Thus, with $\Delta C_P\ne0$,
$$
\boxed{\frac{dT}{dP}=\frac{TV\Delta\alpha_T}{\Delta C_P}.}
$$
This is the <Ehrenfest entropy-continuity relation>. <Entropy> equality alone would give $T\Delta(V\alpha_T)/\Delta C_P$ without the shared-volume condition; a constant-pressure boundary or vanishing discontinuities needs separate limiting analysis.

<Landau theory> expands a thermodynamic potential in an <order parameter>, constrained by <symmetry>. For a scalar with sign-reversal <symmetry>, a useful <free-energy density> is
$$
f=f_0+\frac{a(T-T_c)}2\eta^2+\frac b4\eta^4-h\eta,
\qquad a,b>0.
$$
Here $h$ is the <conjugate field>. At $h=0$, minimizing rather than merely finding stationary points gives
$$
\boxed{\eta_{\rm eq}=0\quad(T>T_c),\qquad
\eta_{\rm eq}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{a(T_c-T)}b}\quad(T<T_c).}
$$
The symmetry-related minima represent <spontaneous symmetry breaking>, as for a simple uniaxial <ferromagnet> with <magnetization> as <order parameter>. An infinitesimal field selects one sign. Above $T_c$, the susceptibility is $1/[a(T-T_c)]$; below it the stable-branch susceptibility is $1/[2a(T_c-T)]$. Minimization lowers $f$ by $-a^2(T-T_c)^2/(4b)$ below $T_c$, producing a heat-capacity <density> jump $T_ca^2/(2b)$ with continuous <entropy>.

\Image[paper-1-tsp2012-landau.png]
{title=Landau minima}
{description=Original normalized quartic free-energy curves above, at and below a continuous transition, with the two symmetry-related equilibrium order-parameter branches.}

A negative quartic coefficient requires a stabilizing $+c\eta^6/6$ with $c>0$. It can produce a first-order transition before the quadratic coefficient changes sign: coexistence occurs at $a(T-T_c)=3b^2/(16c)$ and $\eta^2=-3b/(4c)$. Odd terms are allowed when the assumed <symmetry> is absent. <Landau theory> predicts mean-field exponents; spatial fluctuations can change critical behaviour, especially close to a <critical point> in low dimensions. A <gradient> term extends the model to nonuniform <order parameters> but does not by itself supply dynamical equations.

= Solution

<Brownian motion> arises from many microscopic collisions. A one-dimensional Langevin description is
$$
m\dot v=-\zeta v-\kappa x+\xi(t),\qquad
\langle\xi(t)\rangle=0,\qquad
\langle\xi(t)\xi(t')\rangle=2\zeta k_BT\delta(t-t').
$$
The fluctuation-dissipation relation ties random forcing to drag, ensuring <thermal equilibrium>. For a free particle set $\kappa=0$. With initially thermal <velocities>, its mean-square displacement is
$$
\boxed{\langle[x(t)-x(0)]^2\rangle
=2D[t-\tau_v(1-e^{-t/\tau_v})],\qquad
D=\frac{k_BT}{\zeta},\quad \tau_v=\frac m\zeta.}
$$
It is ballistic, $(k_BT/m)t^2$, at short times and diffusive, $2Dt$, for $t\gg\tau_v$. In three dimensions the corresponding long-time result is $6Dt$. At diffusive times, the position propagator from a specified origin is <Gaussian> with <variance> $2Dt$ per coordinate. A small <sphere> in a viscous liquid supplies a physical example; in the slow unbounded-fluid limit its drag is $6\pi\eta_fa$.

For a harmonic trap, overdamped relaxation has time $\tau_\kappa=\zeta/\kappa$, equilibrium <variance> $k_BT/\kappa$ and stationary <correlation>
$$
\langle x(t)x(0)\rangle=\frac{k_BT}{\kappa}e^{-|t|/\tau_\kappa}.
$$
Consequently <stationary trapped Brownian displacement> obeys
$$
\boxed{\langle[x(t)-x(0)]^2\rangle=\frac{2k_BT}{\kappa}(1-e^{-t/\tau_\kappa}).}
$$
Confinement makes this displacement saturate, unlike free <diffusion>. A trajectory initialized exactly at the centre instead has <variance> $(k_BT/\kappa)(1-e^{-2t/\tau_\kappa})$; confusing that conditional <variance> with the stationary displacement gives an erroneous factor of two. The equilibrium position distribution is proportional to $\exp[-\kappa x^2/(2k_BT)]$. More general finite confinement has a geometry-dependent equilibrium distribution and bounded displacement, not necessarily a <Gaussian> one.

<Bose-Einstein condensation> is macroscopic occupation of a single lowest-energy one-particle state. For a homogeneous three-dimensional <ideal gas> of conserved massive <bosons>, set that <energy> to zero. The excited-state population is
$$
N_{\rm ex}=\frac{V}{\lambda_T^3}g_{3/2}(z),\qquad
z=e^{\mu/(k_BT)}\le1,\qquad
\lambda_T=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{mk_BT}}.
$$
Here $g_{3/2}(z)=\sum_{r\ge1}z^r/r^{3/2}$. As $\mu\to0^-$, the <excited states> can hold only $V\zeta(3/2)/\lambda_T^3$ particles. Additional particles occupy the <ground state>. Thus
$$
\boxed{T_c=\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{mk_B}\left[\frac{n}{\zeta(3/2)}\right]^{2/3},\qquad
\frac{N_0}{N}=1-\left(\frac T{T_c}\right)^{3/2}\quad(T<T_c).}
$$
The isolated <ground state> must be counted separately from the continuum <density of states>; otherwise one misses the condensate. A dilute atomic gas is a physical realization of the quantum-degeneracy regime, but trapping geometry and interactions modify the homogeneous ideal-gas formulas.<Statistical Physics by David Tong>[§3.5.3: saturation of excited states, separate ground-state occupation and ideal-gas condensation temperature]{ref}

\Image[paper-1-tsp2012-condensation.png]
{title=Ideal Bose condensate fraction}
{description=Original homogeneous three-dimensional ideal-gas condensate fraction and maximum thermal population, normalized to the critical temperature. No trapped-gas or experimental data are implied.}

In the homogeneous <ideal gas>, one and two dimensions do not have finite-temperature condensation in the <thermodynamic limit> because the low-energy population integral diverges. Finite size or a trap changes that conclusion's assumptions. Condensation and <superfluidity> are distinct: the ideal quadratic gas has zero <Landau critical velocity>, while interactions can support a sound mode and superfluid flow. Neither zero <viscosity> nor phase-rigid flow follows from the occupation formula alone.

= Solution

Let $\beta=1/(k_BT)$. Each independent <fermion> orbital is either empty or occupied once, so its grand partition factor is $1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon_j-\mu)}$. Multiplying factors and taking the logarithm gives
$$
\Xi=\prod_j[1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon_j-\mu)}],\qquad
\Phi=-k_BT\ln\Xi=-k_BT\int_0^\infty g(\epsilon)\ln[1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}]d\epsilon.
$$
Since $1-n_F=[1+e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}]^{-1}$, this is the requested form. Spin or other orbital degeneracy is already included in $g$.

For the <Fermi-window convolution of the grand potential>, define
$$
A(\epsilon)=\int_0^\epsilon g(t)dt,\qquad
\Phi_0(\epsilon)=-\int_0^\epsilon A(t)dt,\qquad
F(\epsilon)=k_BT\ln[1-n_F(\epsilon,\mu)].
$$
Direct differentiation gives **$F'=+n_F$**. The supplied PDF hint has the opposite sign; using that sign would contradict its grand-potential formula. Integrating twice with $A(0)=\Phi_0(0)=0$ and vanishing upper boundary terms,
$$
\Phi=\int_0^\infty A'F\,d\epsilon
=-\int_0^\infty A n_F\,d\epsilon
=\int_0^\infty\Phi'_0 n_F\,d\epsilon
=\boxed{\int_0^\infty\Phi_0(\epsilon)[-\partial_\epsilon n_F]d\epsilon.}
$$
At zero <temperature> $-\partial_\epsilon n_F\to\delta(\epsilon-\mu)$, confirming $\Phi_0(\mu)=\Phi(0,\mu)$.

Use the source's implicit unit normalization of the constant <density of states>; restoring a constant baseline $g_0$ multiplies the following potentials by $g_0$. For the sinusoidal <density of states>, direct integration gives
$$
\Phi_0(\epsilon)=-\frac{\epsilon^2}{2}
+\frac{\alpha\Delta^2}{4\pi^2}\left[\cos\left(\frac{2\pi\epsilon}{\Delta}\right)-1\right].
$$
Physical nonnegative <density of states> requires $|\alpha|\le1$. Write $t=k_BT$ and $y=\epsilon-\mu$. The <Fermi window>
$$
w(y)=\frac1{4t}\operatorname{sech}^2\left(\frac y{2t}\right)
$$
is even, with full-line moments $\int w\,dy=1$, $\int yw\,dy=0$ and $\int y^2w\,dy=\pi^2t^2/3$. The last integral follows, for example, by expanding $e^x/(1+e^x)^2$ on $x>0$ into its alternating exponential series. When $t\ll\mu$, extending the lower integration limit to $-\infty$ introduces only exponentially small band-edge corrections. The supplied <Fourier transform> gives
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}w(y)\cos\left[\frac{2\pi(\mu+y)}\Delta\right]dy
=\frac X{\sinh X}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi\mu}\Delta\right),\qquad
X=\frac{2\pi^2t}\Delta.
$$
The odd <sine> term vanishes. Substitution proves
$$
\boxed{\Phi(T,\mu)=\Phi_0(\mu)-\frac{\pi^2t^2}{6}
+\frac{\alpha\Delta^2}{4\pi^2}\left(\frac X{\sinh X}-1\right)
\cos\left(\frac{2\pi\mu}\Delta\right).}
$$
The <thermal damping of a sinusoidal density of states> is therefore $X/\sinh X$ in the full oscillatory potential. The requested ratio instead concerns the thermal change $\Phi(T,\mu)-\Phi_0(\mu)$. Its <oscillatory thermal-change amplitude ratio> is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal R(X)=\frac{6|\alpha|}{X^2}\left(1-\frac X{\sinh X}\right).}
$$
For positive $X$ it decreases smoothly from the limit $|\alpha|$ to zero:
$$
\mathcal R=|\alpha|\left[1-\frac{7X^2}{60}+O(X^4)\right]\quad(X\ll1),\qquad
\mathcal R\sim\frac{6|\alpha|}{X^2}\quad(X\gg1).
$$
At exactly $T=0$ both thermal changes vanish; the quoted value is their limiting ratio. The <amplitude> is the envelope in <chemical potential>. At a particular fixed $\mu$, the signed contribution ratio has $\alpha\cos(2\pi\mu/\Delta)$ in place of $|\alpha|$; at an <oscillation> <node> the actual oscillatory contribution is zero. The large-$X$ behaviour still requires $k_BT\ll\mu$, so the useful range is $1\ll X\ll2\pi^2\mu/\Delta$.

= Solution
{parent=4}

For two localized <energy> levels $0,\delta$ with degeneracies $d_0,d_1$, let $x=\delta/(k_BT)$. Canonical equilibrium gives
$$
z=d_0+d_1e^{-x},\qquad p_1=\frac{d_1e^{-x}}z,\qquad
u=\delta p_1,\qquad s=k_B(\ln z+xp_1),\qquad
c=k_Bx^2p_1(1-p_1).
$$
Here $p_1$ is the total upper-level occupation <probability> and $u,s,c$ are per unit. For nondegenerate levels the <entropy> rises from zero to $k_B\ln2$, while the <heat capacity> vanishes at both low and high <temperatures> and has a Schottky maximum near $k_BT=0.417\delta$. A <spin one-half> in a field, or a localized defect with two configurations, supplies an example. Higher levels must be thermally inaccessible; unequal degeneracies change the populations and peak. Distinguishable localized units have total <partition function> $z^N$, unlike a gas of indistinguishable particles sharing just two orbitals.

For the magnetic atom, choose level labels with $E_m=-mg\mu_BB$, $m=-J,\ldots,J$. Put $x=T_B/T=g\mu_BB/(k_BT)$. Its <partition function> and exact <entropy of a finite magnetic multiplet> are
$$
\boxed{Z_J(x)=\sum_{m=-J}^{J}e^{mx}
=\frac{\sinh[(2J+1)x/2]}{\sinh(x/2)},\qquad
\frac{s_J}{k_B}=\ln Z_J-x\frac{d\ln Z_J}{dx}.}
$$
The ratio form has removable limit $Z_J(0)=2J+1$. Since $s_J$ is a function of $x$ only, it depends on field and <temperature> solely through $B/T$. For fixed $J$ at small $x$, <symmetry> removes the odd <powers> and the supplied sum of $m^2$ gives
$$
Z_J=(2J+1)\left[1+\frac{J(J+1)}6x^2+O(x^4)\right],
\qquad
\boxed{s_J=k_B\left[\ln(2J+1)-\frac{J(J+1)}6\frac{T_B^2}{T^2}+O(x^4)\right].}
$$
For large $J$, the expansion requires $J|x|\ll1$, not merely $|x|\ll1$.

{title=Two-level heat and magnetic entropy}
{description=Original Schottky heat-capacity curve and exact finite-spin entropies show the entropy deficit removed by isothermal magnetization. Quadratic high-temperature expansions are shown only over a limited small-field interval.}

For <demagnetization with a linear-heat-capacity load>, integrate its specified $C_L/T$:
$$
S_L(T)=S_L(0)+\frac{Mk_B}{\theta}T.
$$
Isothermal <magnetization> at $T_i$ removes spin <entropy>; the bath receives the corresponding heat. During the following reversible, thermally isolated demagnetization, it is the combined <entropy> of spins and load that stays constant. At the minimum-field endpoint $B_f=0$, the ideal independent spins reach $s_{\max}=k_B\ln(2J+1)$. Therefore
$$
Ns_J(T_i,B_i)+S_L(T_i)=Nk_B\ln(2J+1)+S_L(T_{\min}),
$$
$$
\boxed{N\left[k_B\ln(2J+1)-s_J(T_i,B_i)\right]
=\frac{Mk_B}{\theta}(T_i-T_{\min}).}
$$
The bracket is the initial <magnetic entropy deficit>. The PDF's intermediate equality uses the initial <entropy> itself instead; with its preceding definition of $s$, that equality omits the maximum-entropy term. The corrected balance recovers its final <temperature> formula:
$$
\boxed{T_{\min}=T_i-\frac{N\theta J(J+1)g^2\mu_B^2B_i^2}{6Mk_B^2T_i^2}.}
$$
Without the small-field expansion, the exact independent-spin prediction is $T_i-(N\theta/M)[\ln(2J+1)-s_J(T_i,B_i)/k_B]$. As a check, zero initial field gives zero <entropy> deficit and no cooling. The incorrectly printed intermediate balance would fail this check.

The result requires the load law throughout the <temperature> interval, hence $T_i\ll\theta$; a small initial <Zeeman splitting>, quantitatively $JT_{B,i}/T_i\ll1$; positive final <temperature>; negligible additional <heat capacities> and heat leaks; thermal equilibration between subsystems; and a reversible sweep slow enough to maintain equilibrium. A negative extrapolated <temperature> is a breakdown of this approximation, not a cooling prediction. Spins alone would keep $B/T$ fixed, but the attached load changes that adiabat by transferring <entropy> to them as it cools.

Interactions invalidate the product of independent one-site <partition functions>. The <entropy> must be computed from the many-body <energy> spectrum or a justified interacting approximation. <Correlations> and possible magnetic ordering introduce an interaction scale and can remove the independent-spin <entropy> available for cooling near zero field. Internal fields and ordering commonly limit practical demagnetization. The detailed correction and any residual <entropy> depend on the interaction Hamiltonian, dimensionality and frustration; neither a universal ordering <temperature> nor a numerical final-temperature correction is specified here.
