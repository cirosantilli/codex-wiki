= Solution
{parent=a}

In the classical equilibrium circuit, the <capacitor> stores $CV^2/2$. <Equipartition> assigns this quadratic term mean <energy> $k_BT/2$, so
$$
\boxed{V_{\rm rms}=\sqrt{\frac{k_BT}{C}}\simeq6.4\,\mathrm{\mu V}\quad(T\simeq300\,\mathrm K).}
$$
The mean <voltage> is zero and its <variance> is $k_BT/C$. Resistance determines the equilibration time and noise spectrum, not this full-band equilibrium <variance>. A finite-bandwidth or appreciably loading readout can measure less than the ideal result.

= b
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let $\Delta=\hbar\Omega$ and choose the nondegenerate <energy> levels as 0 and $\Delta$. For N <independent> identical units, $Z=(1+e^{-\beta\Delta})^N$; set N=1 for a single <two-level system>. Differentiating its logarithm gives
$$
\boxed{U=\frac{N\Delta}{1+e^{\Delta/(k_BT)}},\qquad
C_V=Nk_B\frac{x^2e^x}{(1+e^x)^2},\quad x=\frac\Delta{k_BT}.}
$$
At low <temperature>, $U\sim N\Delta e^{-x}$ and $C_V\sim Nk_Bx^2e^{-x}$: the upper level is frozen out. At high <temperature>, the two levels approach equal populations, giving $U\to N\Delta/2$ while $C_V\sim Nk_Bx^2/4\to0$. The <energy> therefore rises monotonically to a plateau, while the <heat capacity> has a Schottky peak:
$$
\boxed{k_BT_{\rm peak}\simeq0.417\Delta,\qquad C_{V,\rm peak}\simeq0.439Nk_B.}
$$
Unequal degeneracies would change the populations and peak; the usual equal-degeneracy model is stated here. Shifting both <energy> levels changes the <energy> baseline but not <heat capacity>.

= c
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Use a three-dimensional <ideal gas> at the same <number density> n for both <isotopes>. An unpolarized <helium-3> gas has <spin degeneracy> two, hence
$$
k_BT_F=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_3}(3\pi^2n)^{2/3}.
$$
<Helium-4> has a single <spin> state, and the ideal Bose-gas saturation condition gives
$$
k_BT_B=\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{m_4}\left[\frac{n}{\zeta(3/2)}\right]^{2/3}.
$$
Eliminating n, using $m_3/m_4\simeq3/4$ and $\zeta(3/2)=2.612$, yields
$$
\boxed{\frac{T_B}{T_F}=\frac{4\pi}{(3\pi^2)^{2/3}\zeta(3/2)^{2/3}}\frac{m_3}{m_4}
\simeq0.519,\qquad T_B\simeq2.6\,\mathrm K.}
$$
The fermionic <spin degeneracy> must be included. This is an ideal-gas estimate; strong interactions in liquid helium prevent identifying it with an exact experimental transition <temperature>.

= Solution
{parent=a}

<Equipartition> gives mean <energy> $k_BT/2$ for each <independent> quadratic term in a classical canonical <Hamiltonian>. For example, $p^2/(2m)$ and $kx^2/2$ each contribute this amount in a <harmonic oscillator>. It is a thermal average, not an instantaneous equality; constraints, nonquadratic terms or quantum level spacings large compared with $k_BT$ require a different calculation.

The <fluctuation-dissipation theorem> connects equilibrium fluctuations with the dissipative part of <linear response>. If an applied <force> couples as $-f(t)x$, use response to $e^{-i\omega t}$ and define the two-sided angular-frequency spectrum $S_{xx}(\omega)=\int e^{i\omega t}\langle\delta x(t)\delta x(0)\rangle dt$. The classical relation is
$$
\boxed{S_{xx}(\omega)=\frac{2k_BT}{\omega}\operatorname{Im}\chi(\omega).}
$$
For a viscously damped particle, $m\dot v=-\zeta v+\xi(t)$, the corresponding white-noise <force> satisfies $\langle\xi(t)\xi(t')\rangle=2\zeta k_BT\delta(t-t')$. Noise replenishes <energy> lost by <friction>; their strengths cannot be varied independently at fixed equilibrium <temperature>.

Electrically, the one-sided voltage-noise spectrum per hertz of an equilibrium <resistor> is $4k_BTR$. A <capacitor> in parallel filters it to
$$
S_V^{(1)}(f)=\frac{4k_BTR}{1+(2\pi fRC)^2},\qquad
\int_0^\infty S_V^{(1)}(f)\,df=\frac{k_BT}{C}.
$$
The same equilibrium <voltage> <variance> follows from <equipartition>. Increasing R makes the spectrum taller and narrower without changing its integrated area. This thermodynamic connection between dissipative conductance and thermal electrical fluctuations is the content of the classical noise calculation.<Thermal Agitation of Electric Charge in Conductors by Harry Nyquist>[1928, abstract: equilibrium thermodynamic and statistical calculation of thermal electromotive force]{ref}

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

A black body absorbs incident electromagnetic radiation and, in equilibrium, emits a spectrum determined by <temperature> alone. A cavity with a small aperture approximates this ideal. <Photon> number is not conserved in <thermal equilibrium>, so its <chemical potential> is zero. Combining the Bose occupation with the two-polarization electromagnetic <mode> <density> gives the spectral <energy> <density>
$$
u_\omega=\frac{\hbar\omega^3}{\pi^2c^3}\frac1{e^{\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}-1}.
$$
Changing variable to $x=\hbar\omega/(k_BT)$ shows directly why the total <energy> <density> is proportional to $T^4$:
$$
u(T)=\frac{(k_BT)^4}{\pi^2\hbar^3c^3}\int_0^\infty\frac{x^3\,dx}{e^x-1}
=\frac{\pi^2k_B^4}{15\hbar^3c^3}T^4.
$$
Three <powers> of T arise from the number of thermally populated <modes> and one from their characteristic <energy>. Isotropic radiation incident on a surface has outward hemispherical <flux> $cu/4$, giving
$$
\boxed{j^\star=\sigma_{\rm SB}T^4,\qquad
\sigma_{\rm SB}=\frac{\pi^2k_B^4}{60\hbar^3c^2}.}
$$
The factor 1/4 is the hemispherical angular average; multiplying <energy> <density> by c alone would overcount. The spectrum's <frequency> scale grows with T. Classical <equipartition> over all <modes> would instead produce the Rayleigh-Jeans ultraviolet divergence; the Planck occupation suppresses high-frequency <modes>. Real materials can have wavelength-dependent <emissivity>, and a body's net radiative exchange also includes absorbed environmental radiation.

= Solution
{parent=c}

An <equation of state> relates equilibrium pressure, <temperature> and <density>. The ideal-gas form $p=nk_BT$ neglects intermolecular <forces> and excluded <volume>. A low-density real-gas expansion is
$$
\frac p{nk_BT}=1+B_2(T)n+B_3(T)n^2+\cdots,
$$
where the virial coefficients encode interaction effects and generally depend on <temperature>.

A simple phenomenological example is the molar <van der Waals equation>
$$
\boxed{p=\frac{RT}{v-b}-\frac a{v^2}.}
$$
Here v is molar <volume>, b represents excluded <volume> and the attractive interaction lowers pressure through a. Its dilute expansion gives $pv/(RT)=1+[b-a/(RT)]/v+\cdots$. It recovers the ideal-gas limit but is not a quantitatively universal equation for all <real gases>.

Below its <critical temperature>, the raw isotherm contains a region with $(\partial p/\partial v)_T>0$, corresponding to negative <isothermal compressibility> and mechanical instability. Equilibrium <phase> separation replaces that segment by a coexistence pressure satisfying equal <chemical potentials>, equivalently the <Maxwell equal-area construction>. The <critical point> follows from $p_v=p_{vv}=0$:
$$
\boxed{v_c=3b,\qquad T_c=\frac{8a}{27Rb},\qquad p_c=\frac{a}{27b^2}.}
$$
The distinction between an unstable homogeneous branch and a stable two-phase state is essential when interpreting a real-gas <equation of state>.

= Solution
{parent=3}

Write $z=e^{\beta(\mu-\epsilon)}$. For one nondegenerate fermionic <quantum state>, the allowed occupations 0 and 1 give $\Xi_F=1+z$. For a bosonic state, summing all nonnegative occupations gives $\Xi_B=(1-z)^{-1}$, requiring $z<1$ or $\mu<\epsilon$. Differentiating the logarithm,
$$
\bar n=z\frac{\partial\ln\Xi}{\partial z},\qquad
\boxed{\bar n_F=\frac1{e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}+1},\qquad
\bar n_B=\frac1{e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}-1}.}
$$
The <interacting point defect> has two distinct <spin> states, not one fermionic state. Its four <microstates> have grand-canonical weights $1,z,z,qz^2$, with $q=e^{-\beta U}$. The first two single-electron weights are separate up/down <spin> possibilities; the double state has one <electron> of each <spin>. Thus
$$
\Xi=1+2z+qz^2,\qquad
\boxed{\bar n=\frac{2z+2qz^2}{1+2z+qz^2}.}
$$
For $U=0$, $\Xi=(1+z)^2$ and $\bar n=2z/(1+z)$. The sketch is a single smooth step from 0 to 2, centred on $\mu=\epsilon$, where $\bar n=1$. As $T\to0$ its width tends to zero.

For positive U with $\beta U\gg1$, the lowest grand energies are successively $0$, $\epsilon-\mu$ and $2\epsilon+U-2\mu$. Away from the two crossings,
$$
\boxed{\bar n\simeq\begin{cases}
0,&\mu<\epsilon,\\
1,&\epsilon<\mu<\epsilon+U,\\
2,&\mu>\epsilon+U.
\end{cases}}
$$
Finite <temperature> smooths each step over an <energy> range of order $k_BT$. Near the lower step double occupation is exponentially suppressed, so $\bar n\simeq2z/(1+2z)$. Near the upper step put $y=e^{\beta(\mu-\epsilon-U)}$ and neglect the empty state, giving $\bar n\simeq1+y/(2+y)$. <Spin degeneracy> shifts the half-step positions to $\epsilon-k_BT\ln2$ and $\epsilon+U+k_BT\ln2$.

For a grand-canonical probability $p_s\propto e^{-\beta(E_s-\mu n_s)}$, differentiation gives $\partial_\mu p_s=\beta(n_s-\bar n)p_s$. Multiplying by $n_s$ and summing proves
$$
\boxed{\sigma^2=k_BT\left(\frac{\partial\bar n}{\partial\mu}\right)_T.}
$$
For this defect, direct state counting gives
$$
\langle n^2\rangle=\frac{2z+4qz^2}{\Xi},\qquad
\sigma^2=\frac{2z(1+2qz+qz^2)}{(1+2z+qz^2)^2}.
$$
At $\mu=\epsilon$, $z=1$ and therefore
$$
\boxed{\sigma^2=\frac{2+6e^{-\beta U}}{(3+e^{-\beta U})^2}\longrightarrow\frac29.}
$$
The same limit gives $\bar n=2/3$: empty, up and down states have equal weights, while the double state is suppressed. Replacing this degeneracy by a two-outcome equal-weight model would incorrectly give <variance> $1/4$ at this <chemical potential>.

Across the full chemical-potential range the <variance> has two well-separated narrow peaks and tends to zero on the 0, 1 and 2 plateaux. The limiting shapes are $2z/(1+2z)^2$ near the lower step and $2y/(2+y)^2$ near the upper one. Each peak has height $1/4$, at the shifted half-step position; the <variance> at the unshifted crossings is instead $2/9$.

For $H=0$ and $a>0$, $F(M)=aM^2/2+bM^4/4$ is a symmetric single well. Its only minimum is
$$
\boxed{\langle M\rangle=0,\qquad F_{\min}=0.}
$$
The zero is relative to the smooth background omitted from the supplied expansion.

{parent=4}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For $H=0$ and $a<0$, the <free energy> is a symmetric double well. The central stationary point is a maximum; the two stable minima are
$$
\boxed{M_0=\pm\sqrt{-\frac ab},\qquad F_{\min}=-\frac{a^2}{4b}.}
$$
These are the magnetisations of the two symmetry-broken <phases>, selected by an infinitesimal field or a <boundary condition>. An exactly symmetric finite-system ensemble can have $\langle M\rangle=0$ by averaging both wells; that does not make the central maximum a stable homogeneous <phase>.

{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For $a>0$ and $H>0$, the field tilts the single well toward positive M. Since $F''=a+3bM^2>0$ everywhere, there is exactly one minimum, at the positive solution $M_H$ of the <equation of state>. At that solution,
$$
\boxed{\langle M\rangle=M_H>0,\qquad
F_{\min}=-\frac a2M_H^2-\frac{3b}{4}M_H^4.}
$$
This follows by eliminating $\mu_0H=aM_H+bM_H^3$ from F. At weak field, $M_H\simeq\mu_0H/a$ and $F_{\min}\simeq-\mu_0^2H^2/(2a)$.

= Solution
{parent=4}

At zero applied field, <time-reversal symmetry> interchanges $M$ and $-M$ without changing the <free energy>, so the analytic expansion contains only even <powers>. The term $-\mu_0HM$ breaks this symmetry at fixed nonzero H. Stationarity and local stability require
$$
\boxed{\mu_0H=a(T)M+bM^3,\qquad a(T)+3bM^2>0.}
$$
For $T>T_c$ at small field, the cubic term is higher order and $M\simeq\mu_0H/a(T)$. Thus the susceptibility as defined in the question is
$$
\boxed{\chi=\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial H}\right)_T\xrightarrow[H\to0]{}\frac{\mu_0}{a(T)}.}
$$
The three requested free-energy sketches and their minima are given separately above.

For the plotted isotherms, divide the <equation of state> by nonzero M and rearrange:
$$
\boxed{M^2=\frac{\mu_0}{b}\frac HM-\frac{a(T)}b.}
$$
This is an <Arrott plot>: its slope determines b, and the isotherm through the origin identifies the mean-field <Curie temperature>.<Criterion for Ferromagnetism from Observations of Magnetic Isotherms by Anthony Arrott>[1957, abstract: molecular-field criterion from cubic magnetisation versus internal field at the Curie temperature]{ref} Under the paper's stated uniform-field Landau model, use H as supplied on its axis.

The ordinate unit is $(10^4\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}})^2=10^8\,\mathrm{A^2\,m^{-2}}$. Reading the original graph gives slopes about $0.076$–$0.080$ ordinate units per unit H/M, so the physical slope is approximately $7.8\times10^6\,\mathrm{A^2\,m^{-2}}$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{b\simeq1.6\times10^{-13}\,\mathrm{J\,m\,A^{-4}}.}
$$
Approximate horizontal intercepts and the corresponding $a=\mu_0(H/M)_{M^2=0}$ are:

* $4.2\,\mathrm K$: intercept $-62$, giving $a\simeq-7.8\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}}$.
* $13\,\mathrm K$: intercept $-40$, giving $a\simeq-5.1\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}}$.
* $19\,\mathrm K$: intercept $-15$, giving $a\simeq-1.9\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}}$.
* $25\,\mathrm K$: intercept $+17$, giving $a\simeq+2.2\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}}$.

The sign change brackets the transition between 19 and 25 K. A linear fit of $a(T)=\alpha(T-T_c)$ across the four read-offs gives approximately $\alpha=4.8\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ and
$$
\boxed{T_c\simeq22\,\mathrm K.}
$$
Interpolating only the two nearest isotherms also gives about 22 K. These are graph estimates, not exact recovered measurements.

Below $T_c$ at zero field, the stable branches have $M_0^2=-a/b$. Substitution gives the <free-energy density> relative to the omitted smooth background:
$$
F_{\min}=-\frac{a(T)^2}{4b}=-\frac{\alpha^2(T-T_c)^2}{4b}.
$$
The equilibrium excess heat-capacity <density> is $c_H=-T\partial_T^2F_{\min}=T\alpha^2/(2b)$ below $T_c$ and zero above it. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Delta c_H=\frac{T_c\alpha^2}{2b}>0.}
$$
The all-isotherm linear fit gives **about $1.5\times10^3\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}\,K^{-1}}$**. Estimating the slope only between the 19 K and 25 K curves instead gives $\alpha\simeq6.9\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ and a jump near $3\times10^3\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}\,K^{-1}}$. The lines are not exactly parallel and the intercepts are not perfectly linear in T, so **a jump of order a few $10^3\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}\,K^{-1}}$ is supported; finer precision is not**. This is per <volume> because F is a <density>; no sample <volume> is given to convert it to a total <heat capacity>.

If $b<0$, the quartic truncation is unbounded below. Add a positive sixth-order term $cM^6/6$ with $c>0$. At zero field the coexistence conditions give
$$
M_{\rm jump}^2=-\frac{3b}{4c},\qquad
 a_{\rm transition}=\frac{3b^2}{16c}>0.
$$
Thus the zero-field transition is generically first order, with a discontinuous spontaneous magnetisation and possible <metastability>. It occurs before the quadratic coefficient reaches zero. The case $b=0$ is the tricritical boundary of this mean-field model; no value of c is invented.
