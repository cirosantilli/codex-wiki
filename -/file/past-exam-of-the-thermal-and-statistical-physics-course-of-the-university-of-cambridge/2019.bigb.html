= Solution
{parent=1}

Use the <Gibbs-Duhem equation> for a homogeneous, extensive, single-component <thermodynamic phase>:
$$
S\,dT-V\,dp+N\,d\mu=0.
$$
At fixed <temperature>, this becomes $dp=n\,d\mu$, with <number density> $n=N/V$. Separately, at fixed <particle number>,
$$
\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial p}\right)_{T,N}
=-\frac{N}{V^2}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial p}\right)_{T,N}
=n\kappa_T.
$$
The <chain rule> along the same <isothermal process> in the <equation of state> now gives
$$
\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial\mu}\right)_T
=\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial p}\right)_T
\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial\mu}\right)_T
=n\kappa_T\,n.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\kappa_T=\frac1{n^2}\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial\mu}\right)_T.}
$$
This is <isothermal compressibility from chemical-potential response>. Since <chemical potential> has units of <energy> per particle, the right-hand side has units of <volume (thermodynamics)> per <energy>, or $\mathrm{Pa^{-1}}$, as required. The derivation assumes a smooth bulk <equation of state>; changing the fractions of coexisting <thermodynamic phases> at fixed <pressure> needs separate treatment.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Measure single-particle <energy> from the lowest state and write $\varepsilon_k=c k^\alpha$, with $c>0$. In a homogeneous system, the number of <wavevector> states in a thin spherical shell is proportional to $k^{d-1}dk$. Changing variables from $k$ to $\varepsilon$ gives the <density of states> per unit spatial <volume (thermodynamics)>,
$$
D(\varepsilon)=\frac{S_{d-1}}{(2\pi)^d\alpha}\,c^{-d/\alpha}\varepsilon^{d/\alpha-1},
$$
where $S_{d-1}$ is the <area> of the <unit sphere> in $d$ dimensions; an additional internal <state degeneracy> would multiply this expression by a constant.

The <Bose-Einstein distribution> has $\mu\leq0$. At saturation, $\mu\to0^-$, the maximum excited-state <number density> is
$$
n_{\mathrm{ex}}^{\max}(T)=\int_0^\infty\frac{D(\varepsilon)}{e^{\beta\varepsilon}-1}\,d\varepsilon,
\qquad\beta=\frac1{k_BT}.
$$
Only the lower endpoint can cause divergence. There the integrand is proportional to
$$
\frac{\varepsilon^{d/\alpha-1}}{\beta\varepsilon}
\propto\varepsilon^{d/\alpha-2}.
$$
The <improper integral> converges precisely when its power exceeds $-1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{d>\alpha\quad\text{is the condition for }T_c>0.}
$$
When $d=\alpha$, the divergence is logarithmic; when $d<\alpha$, it is a power divergence. In either case the <excited states> can accommodate any fixed finite density at every positive <temperature>, so there is no bulk <Bose-Einstein condensation> at nonzero <temperature>.

For completeness, putting $s=d/\alpha>1$ and using the <Gamma function> and <Riemann zeta function> gives
$$
n_{\mathrm{ex}}^{\max}(T)=\frac{S_{d-1}}{(2\pi)^d\alpha}c^{-s}\Gamma(s)\zeta(s)(k_BT)^s.
$$
At fixed density $n$, the right-hand side reaches $n$ at $T_c$; below it the excess particles occupy the lowest state, with <condensate fraction> $1-(T/T_c)^s$. This is <Bose condensation with power-law dispersion> in the homogeneous <thermodynamic limit>. A finite vessel or a trapping potential changes the low-energy state count and must not be silently substituted for that setting.

Use the classical dilute-gas convention
$$
\frac{p}{nk_BT}=1+B_2(T)n+O(n^2),
$$
so the <second virial coefficient> has units of <volume (thermodynamics)>. The <Mayer f-function> formula for an isotropic <pair potential> is
$$
B_2(T)=2\pi\int_0^\infty[1-e^{-\beta\phi(r)}]r^2\,dr.
$$
Only separations smaller than $a$ contribute. Define $x=\beta\varepsilon>0$ and substitute $t=r^3/a^3$, so $r^2dr=(a^3/3)dt$:
$$
B_2(T)=\frac{2\pi a^3}{3}\int_0^1[1-e^{-x(1-t)}]dt
=\boxed{\frac{2\pi a^3}{3}\left[1-\frac{1-e^{-x}}x\right],\qquad x=\frac{\varepsilon}{k_BT}.}
$$
This is the <second virial coefficient of a bounded cubic repulsion>. For every finite positive $T$, $1-e^{-x}<x$, so $B_2(T)>0$. There is no finite positive <Boyle temperature>. At high <temperature>, expanding the exponential gives
$$
B_2(T)=\frac{2\pi a^3}{3}\left(\frac x2-\frac{x^2}{6}+O(x^3)\right)
\sim\frac{\pi a^3\varepsilon}{3k_BT}\longrightarrow0^+.
$$
Thus the intended limiting statement is
$$
\boxed{T_B=+\infty\quad\text{in the sense that }B_2(T)\to0\text{ only as }T\to\infty.}
$$
At the other extreme the classical expression tends to $2\pi a^3/3$, the hard-exclusion value for separation $a$. This is a check on the <integral>, not a claim that a real molecular gas stays classical at arbitrarily low <temperature>. A molar <virial expansion> would instead use $B_{2,\mathrm m}=N_A B_2$.

= Solution

**Canonical <probabilities>.** A <canonical ensemble> describes a system of fixed <particle number> and fixed external parameters such as <volume (thermodynamics)>, weakly coupled to a much larger <thermal reservoir>. They exchange <energy> and reach <thermal equilibrium>, while the reservoir's <temperature> stays effectively constant. The interaction <energy> is negligible in the state counting. The combined isolated system is described by equal <probabilities> for its accessible <microstates>.

If its total <energy> is $E_{\mathrm{tot}}$, the <probability> of a particular system <microstate> $i$ is proportional to the reservoir multiplicity at $E_{\mathrm{tot}}-E_i$. Writing that multiplicity as $e^{S_R/k_B}$, expand its <entropy>:
$$
S_R(E_{\mathrm{tot}}-E_i)
=S_R(E_{\mathrm{tot}})-\left(\frac{\partial S_R}{\partial E_R}\right)E_i+\cdots
=S_R(E_{\mathrm{tot}})-\frac{E_i}{T}+\cdots.
$$
The large-reservoir limit suppresses the higher terms. Normalization therefore gives
$$
\boxed{P_i=\frac{e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z},\qquad
Z=\sum_j e^{-\beta E_j},\qquad\beta=\frac1{k_BT}.}
$$
The sum counts individual <microstates>; when grouping them by <energy>, their <state degeneracies> must be included. This $Z$ is the <canonical partition function>.

**Classical orientational <heat capacity>.** Hold the <magnetic field> fixed. Orientations of the <magnetic dipole moment> have the <solid angle> <measure> $d\Omega=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi$. With $x=\beta mB$, the angular <partition function> is
$$
Z_{\mathrm{ang}}=\int e^{x\cos\theta}d\Omega
=2\pi\int_{-1}^{1}e^{xu}du
=4\pi\frac{\sinh x}{x}.
$$
Choosing a normalized angular <measure> would remove $4\pi$, with no effect on the <energy> <derivatives>. Differentiating at fixed $B$,
$$
U=-\frac{\partial\ln Z_{\mathrm{ang}}}{\partial\beta}
=-mB\left(\coth x-\frac1x\right)
=-mB\coth x+k_BT.
$$
The angular average $\langle\cos\theta\rangle=\coth x-1/x$ is the <Langevin function>. Since $dx/dT=-x/T$,
$$
\boxed{C_B=\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_B
=k_B\left[1-\left(\frac{x}{\sinh x}\right)^2\right].}
$$
This is the <heat capacity of a classical magnetic dipole>, in $\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$ for one <magnetic moment>. The word specific here refers to that single moment; $N$ independent <magnetic moments> have $N$ times this <heat capacity>. At $B=0$ the <energy> is independent of orientation and $C_B=0$, obtained by the continuous $x\to0$ limit. At fixed nonzero $B$, the classical low-temperature limit instead gives $C_B\to k_B$.

**Equipartition and its proof.** The <equipartition theorem> states that an independent quadratic term $a q^2/2$ in a classical <Hamiltonian>, with $a>0$, contributes $k_BT/2$ to its mean <energy>. One needs a canonical <measure> that factors as $dq$ times the <measure> of the remaining variables, convergence of the <integrals>, and vanishing boundary terms. The coordinate must not be restricted by a thermally important hard boundary or a coupled <constraint>.

For $H=a q^2/2+H_0$, <integration by parts> gives
$$
0=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac\partial{\partial q}
\left(qe^{-\beta H}\right)dq
=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(1-\beta q\frac{\partial H}{\partial q}\right)e^{-\beta H}dq.
$$
Integrate over all other variables and divide by $Z$. It follows that
$$
\left\langle q\frac{\partial H}{\partial q}\right\rangle=k_BT,
\qquad
\boxed{\left\langle\frac{a q^2}{2}\right\rangle=\frac12k_BT.}
$$
The same reasoning applies to quadratic momenta among the <canonical variables>. It is the number of quadratic <energy> contributions that is counted, not an unrestricted count of coordinate labels.

**Two soft orientational directions.** When $x\gg1$, the moment is concentrated near alignment. Introduce local <Cartesian coordinates> in the <tangent plane> $q_1=\theta\cos\varphi$ and $q_2=\theta\sin\varphi$. To leading order,
$$
E=-mB+\frac{mB}{2}(q_1^2+q_2^2)+O(mB\theta^4),
\qquad d\Omega\simeq dq_1dq_2.
$$
The two quadratic terms each contribute $k_BT/2$, so
$$
\boxed{U=-mB+k_BT+o(T),\qquad C_B\longrightarrow k_B.}
$$
This agrees with the exact angular calculation. Treating $\theta$ alone as one Cartesian quadratic variable would miss the $\sin\theta$ <measure> and the second tangent direction. There are no extra rotational kinetic terms in the stipulated <Hamiltonian>; adding such terms would be a different model.

**Quantum <spin> one.** The <spin> projections are $m_s=-1,0,1$, with $S_z=\hbar m_s$. Put $\Delta=\hbar|\gamma|B>0$, where $\gamma$ is the <gyromagnetic ratio>. The three <energies> are $-\Delta,0,+\Delta$, irrespective of the sign of $\gamma$. Relative to the <ground state> they are $0,\Delta,2\Delta$. Thus, with $q=e^{-\beta\Delta}$,
$$
Z=e^{\beta\Delta}(1+q+q^2),\qquad
U=-\Delta+\Delta\frac{q+2q^2}{1+q+q^2}.
$$
Differentiation, or the <canonical energy fluctuation> identity $C_B=k_B\beta^2\operatorname{Var}(E)$, gives
$$
C_B=k_B(\beta\Delta)^2
\frac{q(1+4q+q^2)}{(1+q+q^2)^2}.
$$
In the requested limit,
$$
\boxed{C_B\sim k_B\left(\frac{\Delta}{k_BT}\right)^2e^{-\Delta/(k_BT)},
\qquad\Delta=\hbar|\gamma|B.}
$$
This <spin-1 paramagnet> has a nondegenerate <ground state> and a finite <spectral gap>. Excitations are exponentially rare, so its <heat capacity> tends to zero. Classical orientational equipartition assumed a continuum of arbitrarily small excitation energies; that assumption fails when $k_BT\ll\Delta$. This is a change in the accessible state spectrum, not a violation of the <equipartition theorem> within its classical domain.

**<Availability> and total <entropy>.** The reservoir changes by $dU_R=-dU$ and $dV_R=-dV$. Its <particle number> and the system's <particle number> are fixed. The reservoir's <first law of thermodynamics> therefore gives
$$
dS_R=\frac{dU_R+p_RdV_R}{T_R}
=-\frac{dU+p_RdV}{T_R}.
$$
Add the system's <entropy> change. Because $T_R,p_R$ are constant,
$$
dS_{\mathrm{tot}}=dS-\frac{dU+p_RdV}{T_R}
=-\frac1{T_R}d(U+p_RV-T_RS).
$$
Thus define the <availability>
$$
\boxed{A=U+p_RV-T_RS,\qquad\Delta S_{\mathrm{tot}}=-\frac{\Delta A}{T_R}.}
$$
An additive constant in $A$ is immaterial; its value above the equilibrium reference is the <exergy>. The <second law of thermodynamics> gives $\Delta S_{\mathrm{tot}}\geq0$, hence $\Delta A\leq0$. A stable equilibrium maximizes total <entropy> over the allowed variations and therefore minimizes $A$. The environmental $T_R,p_R$ enter this definition even when the system has not yet reached their values. Because both thermal and mechanical exchanges are allowed, equilibrium here includes mechanical balance; equality of temperatures alone does not ensure the minimum.

**<Probability> and curvature.** For a fixed value of a macroscopic coordinate $x$, let $S(x)$ count the compatible system <microstates>. The product of the system and reservoir multiplicities is
$$
\mathcal W(x)\propto
\exp\left[\frac{S(x)}{k_B}-\frac{U(x)+p_RV(x)}{k_BT_R}\right]
=\exp\left[-\frac{A(x)}{k_BT_R}\right].
$$
All factors independent of $x$ enter a normalization constant, giving
$$
\boxed{P(x)=\aleph e^{-A(x)/(k_BT_R)},\qquad
\aleph^{-1}=\int e^{-A(x)/(k_BT_R)}dx.}
$$
Here $A(x)$ is the constrained or effective <availability> for this coordinate, including the multiplicity of any eliminated variables. A nonuniform coordinate <measure> must likewise be retained or incorporated in that effective <availability>.

At a stable interior minimum, $A'(x_0)=0$ and $A''(x_0)>0$. Its <Taylor expansion> gives
$$
A(x)=A(x_0)+\frac12A''(x_0)(x-x_0)^2+\cdots.
$$
Keeping the quadratic term yields a <normal distribution>,
$$
P(x)\simeq\sqrt{\frac{A''(x_0)}{2\pi k_BT_R}}
\exp\left[-\frac{A''(x_0)(x-x_0)^2}{2k_BT_R}\right],
\qquad
\boxed{\left\langle(x-x_0)^2\right\rangle\simeq\frac{k_BT_R}{A''(x_0)}.}
$$
This is <availability curvature and equilibrium fluctuations>. The quoted fluctuation formula holds at quadratic order in the <Gaussian fluctuation approximation>; it is not an exact identity for an arbitrary nonlinear $A(x)$. Higher terms can shift the mean away from its most probable value $x_0$.

**Bubble equilibrium.** Keep the number of air <molecules> fixed and include the <surface energy> in the system's total <internal energy>. For constant <surface tension> $\Gamma$, this contribution is $\Gamma\Omega=\Gamma\alpha V^{2/3}$, with no surface <entropy> under the stated approximation. Its differential is
$$
dU=T\,dS-p_{\mathrm{air}}\,dV+\Gamma\,d\Omega.
$$
Hence
$$
dA=(T-T_R)dS+
\left[p_R-p_{\mathrm{air}}+\frac23\Gamma\alpha V^{-1/3}\right]dV.
$$
Independent thermal and <volume (thermodynamics)> variations give $T=T_R$ and
$$
\boxed{p_{\mathrm{air}}-p_R=\frac23\Gamma\alpha V_0^{-1/3},\qquad
V_0=\left[\frac{2\Gamma\alpha}{3(p_{\mathrm{air}}-p_R)}\right]^3.}
$$
The air <pressure> is evaluated at $V_0$; since the bubble is closed it generally depends on <volume (thermodynamics)>, so this equation is implicit until an air <equation of state> is supplied. For a <sphere>, $\alpha=(36\pi)^{1/3}$, $\Omega=4\pi R^2$, and the <pressure> jump becomes $2\Gamma/R$, the <Young–Laplace equation> for a single gas–liquid interface. There is no second surface as in a thin soap film.

**Isothermal <volume (thermodynamics)> fluctuations.** With the air maintained at $T_R$, eliminate its <entropy> in favor of its <Helmholtz free energy>:
$$
A_T(V)=F_{\mathrm{air}}(T_R,V,N)+p_RV+\Gamma\alpha V^{2/3}.
$$
The identity $(\partial F_{\mathrm{air}}/\partial V)_{T,N}=-p_{\mathrm{air}}$ gives
$$
A_T''(V_0)
=-\left(\frac{\partial p_{\mathrm{air}}}{\partial V}\right)_{T_R,N,V_0}
-\frac29\Gamma\alpha V_0^{-4/3}
=\frac1{V_0}\left[\kappa_T^{-1}-\frac29\Gamma\alpha V_0^{-1/3}\right].
$$
Using the curvature result,
$$
\boxed{\left\langle\Delta V^2\right\rangle\simeq
\frac{k_BT_RV_0}{\kappa_T^{-1}-\frac29\Gamma\alpha V_0^{-1/3}}.}
$$
These are the <volume fluctuations of a closed gas bubble>. Stability requires the denominator to be positive. The negative surface contribution reflects the concavity of $V^{2/3}$; gas compression supplies the positive restoring curvature. For ideal air, $\kappa_T^{-1}=p_{\mathrm{air}}$, and <mechanical equilibrium> reduces the denominator to
$$
\boxed{D=p_R+\frac49\Gamma\alpha V_0^{-1/3}>0.}
$$
The units are consistent: $D$ is <pressure>, so $k_BT_RV_0/D$ has units $\mathrm{m^6}$, the units of a <volume (thermodynamics)> <variance>. Holding $p_{\mathrm{air}}$ externally fixed while varying $V$ would remove the gas-compression term and change this stability problem; that is not the fixed-particle-number situation here.

**Numerical size of the fluctuations.** The <volume (thermodynamics)> conversion is important:
$$
V_0=10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mm^3}=10^{-12}\,\mathrm{m^3},\qquad
V_0^{1/3}=10^{-4}\,\mathrm m,\qquad T_R=298.15\,\mathrm K.
$$
Use the specified $\alpha=4.84$, $\Gamma=0.072\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$ and $p_R=10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}$. Then
$$
p_{\mathrm{air}}-p_R
=\frac23(0.072)(4.84)(10^4)\,\mathrm{Pa}
=2323.2\,\mathrm{Pa},
$$
$$
D=10^5+\frac49(0.072)(4.84)(10^4)
=101548.8\,\mathrm{Pa}.
$$
With the <Boltzmann constant> $k_B=1.380649\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$,
$$
k_BT_R=4.1164\times10^{-21}\,\mathrm J,
\qquad
\left\langle\Delta V^2\right\rangle\simeq4.05\times10^{-38}\,\mathrm{m^6}.
$$
Thus the fractional <root mean square> fluctuation and fractional <variance> are respectively
$$
\boxed{\frac{\sqrt{\langle\Delta V^2\rangle}}{V_0}\simeq2.01\times10^{-7},
\qquad\frac{\langle\Delta V^2\rangle}{V_0^2}\simeq4.05\times10^{-14}.}
$$
The absolute <root mean square> fluctuation is $2.01\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm{m^3}$. The very small fractional value supports the local <Gaussian fluctuation approximation>. This estimate neglects exchange of dissolved air, variations of <surface tension>, shape fluctuations and <temperature> fluctuations, consistently with the prescribed fixed-number, isothermal bubble model.

= Solution

**Occupation and the two-dimensional <Fermi energy>.** For an independent single-particle state, the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> is
$$
\boxed{n(\varepsilon)=\frac1{e^{\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}+1},\qquad\beta=\frac1{k_BT}.}
$$
The <chemical potential> enforces the chosen mean <particle number> through $N=\sum_j n(\varepsilon_j)$, including <spin> states in the sum. It is common to all states in particle-exchange equilibrium and must generally change with <temperature> or field when the total mean number is held fixed. At zero <temperature>, the distribution becomes a <Heaviside step function> at the <Fermi energy>.

Let the sample <area> be $\mathcal S$ and let $g_s$ be its internal <spin> <spin degeneracy>. In the continuum limit, filling the disk $k<k_F$ gives
$$
N=g_s\frac{\mathcal S}{(2\pi)^2}\pi k_F^2,
\qquad k_F^2=\frac{4\pi N}{g_s\mathcal S}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mu(T=0)=\varepsilon_F=\frac{\hbar^2k_F^2}{2m}
=\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{g_s m}\frac N{\mathcal S}.}
$$
For spin-half <fermions> with both <spin> states available, $g_s=2$ and $\varepsilon_F=\pi\hbar^2N/(m\mathcal S)$; for a spin-polarized gas $g_s=1$. The question does not specify the <area> or <spin degeneracy>, so these conventions must be stated rather than extracting an <energy> from $N$ alone. Although the original text calls $\mathbf k$ <momentum>, the supplied $\hbar^2k^2/(2m)$ dispersion makes it a <wave vector>; physical <momentum> is $\mathbf p=\hbar\mathbf k$. The result agrees with the constant <two-dimensional free-electron density of states>. Discrete level spacings in a finite sample would require a specified boundary spectrum.

**One level occupied on average.** Write $\delta=\mu_BB$ and $z=e^{\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}>0$. The two mean <spin> occupations are
$$
N_+=\frac1{1+ze^{\beta\delta}},\qquad
N_-=\frac1{1+ze^{-\beta\delta}}.
$$
Imposing $N_++N_-=1$ gives $2+z(e^{\beta\delta}+e^{-\beta\delta})=1+z(e^{\beta\delta}+e^{-\beta\delta})+z^2$, hence $z^2=1$. Thus, for every positive <temperature>,
$$
\boxed{\mu=\varepsilon,\qquad
N_+-N_-=-\tanh\left(\frac{\beta\mu_BB}{2}\right).}
$$
The total <magnetic moment> is $\mathcal M=-\mu_B(N_+-N_-)$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\chi=\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal M}{\partial B}\right)_{B=0}
=\frac{\mu_B^2}{2k_BT}.}
$$
This is the <grand-canonical spin susceptibility of a half-filled single level>. Empty, singly occupied and doubly occupied orbital states are allowed in the <grand canonical ensemble>. At zero field, the singly occupied configurations together have <probability> $1/2$, explaining the factor of two relative to one permanent spin-half moment. If exactly one <electron> were required at all times, a canonical two-state <partition function> would instead give $\mathcal M=\mu_B\tanh(\beta\mu_BB)$ and $\chi=\mu_B^2/(k_BT)$. That different answer does not implement the stated average-number <constraint>.

**Density-of-states formula for the susceptibility.** Here $g$ is the zero-field <single-spin density of states>. Regard its argument $E$ as the full spin-shifted <energy>. The shifted functions are $g_\pm(E)=g(E\mp\mu_BB)$, so
$$
N_\pm(B)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(E\mp\mu_BB)n(E;\mu(B),T)\,dE.
$$
The total number is even in $B$; thus $\mu(B)=\mu(0)+O(B^2)$ at fixed total number on a smooth branch. More directly, terms from $\mu'(0)$ cancel in the <spin> difference because the two densities coincide at zero field. To first order,
$$
N_+-N_-=-2\mu_BB\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g'(E)n(E;\mu(0),T)\,dE+O(B^3).
$$
It follows that
$$
\boxed{\chi=2\mu_B^2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g'(E)n(E)_{B=0}\,dE.}
$$
For a physical <energy band>, take $g$ to vanish below its lower edge. Provided the endpoint term vanishes, <integration by parts> also gives
$$
\boxed{\frac\chi{2\mu_B^2}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(E)[-n'(E)]\,dE.}
$$
This form displays the positive response and the narrow thermal window around $\mu$. A sharp band edge is included by the corresponding distributional <derivative> in the first expression. The susceptibility defined here is the <derivative> of total <magnetic moment> with respect to $B$, with units $\mathrm{J\,T^{-2}}$; it is not automatically a dimensionless SI <volume (thermodynamics)> susceptibility. A density-of-states convention per unit <volume (thermodynamics)> likewise gives a response per unit <volume (thermodynamics)>. These are the <field conventions for magnetic susceptibility>.

**<Chemical potential> at fixed number.** Define the integrated single-spin state count
$$
G(E)=\int_{-\infty}^E g(u)\,du,
\qquad K_T(E)=-n'(E).
$$
The <probability> kernel $K_T$ has unit <integral>, mean $\mu$, and <variance> $a(k_BT)^2$. Using <integration by parts>,
$$
\frac N2=\int g(E)n(E)\,dE=\int G(E)K_T(E)\,dE.
$$
Expand $G$ about $\mu$. The linear term integrates to zero, while $G''=g'$ gives
$$
\frac N2=G(\mu)+\frac a2(k_BT)^2g'(\mu)+O(T^4).
$$
This is the number form of the <Sommerfeld expansion>. Since $N/2=G(\varepsilon_F)$ at zero <temperature>, set $\mu=\varepsilon_F+\delta\mu$ with $\delta\mu=O(T^2)$:
$$
0=g(\varepsilon_F)\delta\mu+\frac a2(k_BT)^2g'(\varepsilon_F)+O(T^4).
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mu=\varepsilon_F-\frac a2\frac{g'(\varepsilon_F)}{g(\varepsilon_F)}(k_BT)^2+O(T^4).}
$$
The coefficient can be obtained directly. With $y=(E-\mu)/(k_BT)$,
$$
a=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{y^2e^y}{(1+e^y)^2}dy
=4\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{\ell+1}}{\ell^2}
=\boxed{\frac{\pi^2}{3}}.
$$
For example, the sum follows by expanding $e^{-y}/(1+e^{-y})^2$ on $y>0$, using the evenness of the kernel and integrating each exponential. Thus $a/2=\pi^2/6$. A <density of states> increasing with <energy> gives a downward quadratic chemical-potential shift. For the constant two-dimensional <density of states> that shift vanishes to this order; a finite lower band edge still allows exponentially small corrections.

**Low-temperature susceptibility at fixed number.** Apply the same kernel expansion to $g$ itself:
$$
\frac\chi{2\mu_B^2}
=g(\mu)+\frac a2(k_BT)^2g''(\mu)+O(T^4).
$$
The shift of $\mu$ must be retained in the first term. Writing $g_F=g(\varepsilon_F)$ and similarly for its <derivatives>,
$$
g(\mu)=g_F+g_F'\delta\mu+O(T^4)
=g_F-\frac a2\frac{g_F'^2}{g_F}(k_BT)^2+O(T^4).
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\frac\chi{2\mu_B^2}
=g_F-\frac a2\left(\frac{g_F'^2}{g_F}-g_F''\right)(k_BT)^2+O(T^4),
\qquad a=\frac{\pi^2}{3}.}
$$
This is the <fixed-number low-temperature Pauli susceptibility>. The $g_F''$ contribution is thermal broadening at fixed <chemical potential>; the $-g_F'^2/g_F$ contribution enforces fixed total number. Both are needed. If the <density of states> were instead defined with both <spin> directions included, the prefactor $2$ would be absorbed into that definition. Only the <spin> response has been calculated; no orbital magnetic response is included.

The expansion assumes $g_F>0$, sufficient smoothness near the <Fermi energy>, and a <temperature> small compared with the <energy> scale over which $g$ changes or encounters a band edge. It does not apply unmodified at a singular <density of states>. For the ideal two-dimensional parabolic band, $g_F'=g_F''=0$, so the leading Pauli response is <temperature> independent, consistently with the earlier state count.
