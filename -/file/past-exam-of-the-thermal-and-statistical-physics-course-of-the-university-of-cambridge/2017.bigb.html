= Solution

Put $\xi=\epsilon-\epsilon_F$ and $\Delta=|\Delta|>0$. The <BCS quasiparticle spectrum> has $E=\sqrt{\xi^2+\Delta^2}$, so on either inverse branch
$$
\xi_\pm=\pm\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2},\qquad
\left|\frac{d\xi_\pm}{dE}\right|=\frac E{\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2}}.
$$
Conservation of states on one branch gives $D_{\rm branch}(E)dE=g(\epsilon_F)|d\xi|$. Consequently the single-branch expression requested in the source is
$$
\boxed{D_{\rm branch}(E)=\frac{g(\epsilon_F)E}{\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2}},\qquad E>\Delta.}
$$
There are no such states for $0<E<\Delta$ in this ideal fully gapped model. Near the edge the <density of states> diverges as $(E-\Delta)^{-1/2}$, an integrable singularity; at large $E/\Delta$ it approaches its normal-state value.

**Counting qualification.** The <energy> map in the source is two-to-one. If $g(\epsilon)$ literally counts all normal-state levels both above and below $\epsilon_F$, the unweighted <positive-energy quasiparticle density from a folded spectrum> must sum both preimages, and is instead
$$
\boxed{D_{\rm qp}(E)=\sum_{\pm}g(\epsilon_F+\xi_\pm)
\left|\frac{d\xi_\pm}{dE}\right|
=\frac{2g(\epsilon_F)E}{\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2}}.}
$$
Already at $\Delta=0$, folding $\xi$ into $|\xi|$ doubles the positive-axis density. Thus the printed formula is not the unweighted count of all states under the literal mapping without an additional normalization convention.

There is also a standard physical quantity with exactly the printed normalization: the <BCS electronic density of states>, measured for example by <single-electron superconducting tunneling>. At positive $E$, the electron spectral weights of the two roots are the <BCS coherence factors> $u_\pm^2=(1+\xi_\pm/E)/2$. They sum to one, so the weighted sum is $g(\epsilon_F)E/\sqrt{E^2-\Delta^2}$. This supplies a valid electronic-DOS interpretation, while keeping it distinct from counting every positive-<energy> excitation. Spin degeneracy must be included consistently in $g$ and the transformed density; it does not remove the branch-counting issue.

**Repeated letters.** There are six positions, with two copies of each of two letters. Counting labeled arrangements and dividing by the <permutations> of each repeated pair gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{6!}{2!\,2!}=180.}
$$

**Single-occupancy cells.** For $N$ indistinguishable particles, specifying which $N$ of the $M$ distinct cells are occupied completely specifies a configuration. Thus
$$
\boxed{W_{\rm indist}=\binom MN=\frac{M!}{N!(M-N)!}.}
$$
For distinguishable particles, each chosen occupied set admits $N!$ assignments of particle labels:
$$
\boxed{W_{\rm dist}=\binom MN N!=\frac{M!}{(M-N)!}.}
$$
Equivalently, successively place labeled particles in $M,M-1,\ldots,M-N+1$ available cells. The single-occupancy constraint is essential: this is not unrestricted <stars and bars>. Both counts give one empty configuration at $N=0$; at $N=M$ they give one unlabeled occupation pattern and $M!$ labeled assignments.

In the <grand canonical ensemble>, the system exchanges both <energy> and particles with a reservoir at fixed <temperature> $T$ and <chemical potential> $\mu$. The probability of a particular many-body <microstate> $i$, with <energy> $E_i$ and <particle number> $N_i$, is its <Boltzmann weight> $e^{-\beta(E_i-\mu N_i)}$ divided by the <grand canonical partition function> $\Xi$, where $\beta=1/(k_BT)$. The denominator normalizes $\sum_i p_i=1$; a degeneracy is accounted for by counting distinct microstates, rather than changing an individual state's probability.

For noninteracting particles, specify a microstate by the <occupation numbers> $n_r$ of one-particle levels of <energy> $\epsilon_r$. Then $E-\mu N=\sum_r(\epsilon_r-\mu)n_r$, so the Gibbs probability factorizes between levels. Put $x_r=e^{-\beta(\epsilon_r-\mu)}$.

For <fermions>, the <Pauli exclusion principle> permits $n_r=0,1$ in each complete one-particle state, giving $\Xi_r=1+x_r$. Its mean occupancy is
$$
\boxed{\langle n_r\rangle=\frac{0+x_r}{1+x_r}
=\frac1{e^{\beta(\epsilon_r-\mu)}+1},}
$$
the <Fermi-Dirac distribution>.

For <bosons>, $n_r=0,1,2,\ldots$, so the geometric sum is $\Xi_r=1/(1-x_r)$ when $x_r<1$. Differentiating that sum gives
$$
\boxed{\langle n_r\rangle=
\frac{\sum_{n=0}^\infty n x_r^n}{\sum_{n=0}^\infty x_r^n}
=\frac{x_r}{1-x_r}
=\frac1{e^{\beta(\epsilon_r-\mu)}-1},}
$$
the <Bose-Einstein distribution>. Finite-system convergence requires $\mu<\epsilon_{\min}$; condensation is the limiting approach to the lowest <energy> with macroscopic ground-state occupation. Both quantum distributions reduce to $e^{-\beta(\epsilon_r-\mu)}$ when occupation is small. The quantities derived here are mean occupancies of states, rather than normalized probabilities over the set of one-particle energies.

**Structure through pair correlations.** For a homogeneous isotropic monatomic liquid of <number density> $n=N/V$, the <radial distribution function> $g(r)$ is defined by
$$
\boxed{dN_{\rm neighbours}=4\pi n r^2g(r)\,dr.}
$$
It compares the conditional density around a tagged particle with the bulk density. A repulsive core makes close neighbours unlikely; a first peak identifies a preferred coordination shell, and damped peaks at larger radii describe short-range order. Far away, $g(r)\to1$ in the bulk limit. Unlike a crystal, an ordinary liquid has no indefinitely repeated positional order. An ideal uncorrelated gas has $g(r)=1$ away from the tagged particle. The shell factor $4\pi r^2$ must not be confused with $g(r)$ itself.

\Image[2017-q2-liquid-correlations.png]
{title=Original schematic radial distribution function showing a core exclusion region and damped liquid-like coordination shells; the curve is illustrative rather than measured data}

The same pair structure enters the <static structure factor> measured through scattering. In the bulk isotropic convention,
$$
S(k)=1+4\pi n\int_0^\infty[g(r)-1]r^2\frac{\sin kr}{kr}\,dr.
$$
Thus real-space coordination and reciprocal-space scattering peaks describe the same correlations.

**Energy from the structure.** If interactions are sums of a central <pair potential> $v(r)$, the <equipartition theorem> gives the classical <kinetic energy> $3Nk_BT/2$. Count the interaction of a tagged particle with its neighbours and divide by two to avoid counting each pair twice. The <pair-correlation expression for internal energy> is
$$
\boxed{U=\frac32Nk_BT+2\pi Nn\int_0^\infty r^2v(r)g(r)\,dr.}
$$
This makes structure thermodynamically useful: short-distance repulsion and the attractive region contribute with different weights. Additional internal molecular modes or many-body interactions require extra terms. The formula is a classical bulk pair-potential result, not a universal <energy> formula for every liquid.

**Pressure and the dilute expansion.** The mechanical <virial theorem> relates the pair forces to <pressure>. The <virial pressure from pair correlations> is
$$
p=nk_BT-\frac{2\pi n^2}{3}\int_0^\infty r^3v'(r)g(r)\,dr.
$$
At low density, $g(r)\simeq e^{-\beta v(r)}$, giving the <virial expansion>
$$
\boxed{\frac p{nk_BT}=1+B_2(T)n+B_3(T)n^2+\cdots,\qquad
B_2(T)=-2\pi\int_0^\infty[e^{-\beta v(r)}-1]r^2\,dr.}
$$
The <second virial coefficient> has units of <volume> per particle; higher coefficients encode additional cluster correlations. A hard-sphere exclusion diameter $d$ gives $B_2=2\pi d^3/3>0$. Attractions contribute negative terms and can make $B_2$ negative. Although the pair-correlation formulas can describe a dense liquid when its actual $g(r)$ is known, truncating the virial series after a few terms is normally a dilute-fluid approximation. It should not be silently extrapolated through condensation or into a dense liquid.

**Excited-state saturation.** Consider first a uniform three-dimensional noninteracting gas of identical spinless <bosons> in a large box. Set the one-particle ground <energy> to zero. The <Bose-Einstein distribution> is $\bar n(\epsilon)=[z^{-1}e^{\beta\epsilon}-1]^{-1}$, with <quantum gas fugacity> $z=e^{\beta\mu}\leq1$. For dispersion $\epsilon=p^2/(2m)$, the excited <density of states> is
$$
g(\epsilon)=\frac V{4\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt\epsilon.
$$
Using the <thermal de Broglie wavelength> $\lambda_T=\sqrt{2\pi\hbar^2/(mk_BT)}$, integration gives
$$
N_{\rm ex}=\frac V{\lambda_T^3}\operatorname{Li}_{3/2}(z),\qquad
N_0=\frac z{1-z}.
$$
The <polylogarithm> is $\operatorname{Li}_s(z)=\sum_{\ell\geq1}z^\ell/\ell^s$. Its finite value at $z=1$ implies a maximum excited population $V\zeta(3/2)/\lambda_T^3$, with $\zeta(3/2)\simeq2.612$. Once this is less than $N$, the excess particles occupy the <ground state> macroscopically. This is <Bose-Einstein condensation>, rather than occupation of a state by merely one or a few atoms.

**Transition and condensate fraction.** Setting the saturation number equal to $N$ gives the <homogeneous ideal Bose gas condensation> <temperature>
$$
\boxed{T_c=\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{mk_B}
\left[\frac n{\zeta(3/2)}\right]^{2/3}.}
$$
Below this <temperature>, $\mu$ approaches zero from below in the <thermodynamic limit>, and particle conservation gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{N_0}{N}=1-\left(\frac T{T_c}\right)^{3/2}
\quad(0\leq T\leq T_c).}
$$
Above $T_c$ there is no macroscopic condensate and $\mu<0$ adjusts to keep $N$ fixed. At finite $N$, the <chemical potential> remains slightly below the ground <energy> and the transition is rounded.

\Image[2017-q2-bose-condensation.png]
{title=Original uniform ideal-gas condensate fraction and schematic accumulation in the lowest state below the critical temperature; the exponent is for a three-dimensional box}

**Energy and the role of geometry.** The excited-state <internal energy> is
$$
U=\frac32\frac{Vk_BT}{\lambda_T^3}\operatorname{Li}_{5/2}(z).
$$
The zero-<energy> condensate contributes no term to this <energy> in the ideal model. Below $T_c$,
$$
U=\frac32Nk_BT\frac{\zeta(5/2)}{\zeta(3/2)}
\left(\frac T{T_c}\right)^{3/2},\qquad
C_V=\frac{15}{4}Nk_B\frac{\zeta(5/2)}{\zeta(3/2)}
\left(\frac T{T_c}\right)^{3/2}.
$$
Condensation depends on low-<energy> state counting: for a uniform quadratic-dispersion gas, a finite-<temperature> bulk condensate requires spatial dimension greater than two. A trap changes the <density of states> and therefore the <temperature> law; a three-dimensional harmonic trap gives a cubic, rather than $3/2$, condensate-fraction exponent. Finally, an ideal-gas condensate should not automatically be called a <superfluid>: its free quadratic spectrum has zero critical speed by the <Landau criterion>. Interactions matter for the robust collective-flow properties of real dilute condensates.

**Quanta of collective motion.** An elementary excitation is a low-<energy> departure from the many-body <ground state> that can often be treated as a <quasiparticle>. Near an ordered equilibrium, small collective motions form <normal modes>. Quantization turns a harmonic mode of frequency $\omega_{\mathbf k}$ into bosonic excitations with <energy> $\hbar\omega_{\mathbf k}$. Their number is not an independently conserved thermodynamic variable in equilibrium, so their <chemical potential> is zero and
$$
\bar n_{\mathbf k}=\frac1{e^{\hbar\omega_{\mathbf k}/(k_BT)}-1},\qquad
\boxed{U-U_0=\sum_{\mathbf k,\nu}\hbar\omega_{\mathbf k,\nu}\bar n_{\mathbf k,\nu}.}
$$
Here $\nu$ labels branches, and $U_0$ contains the ground-state contribution, including <phonon> zero-point <energy> where appropriate. The approximation is useful while interactions and damping of the excitations are weak enough that the modes remain identifiable.

**Phonons.** A <phonon> is a quantum of an elastic lattice-vibration mode. Long-wavelength <acoustic phonon branches> have $\omega\simeq c_\nu k$, with one longitudinal and two transverse polarizations in a three-dimensional monatomic solid. A multi-atom primitive cell can also support <optical phonon branches>, generally with nonzero frequency at $k=0$. Linear acoustic dispersion gives $D(\epsilon)\propto\epsilon^2$, so the <energy> integral has $U-U_0\propto T^4$ and the <heat capacity> obeys the Debye $T^3$ law. In the <Debye model>,
$$
\boxed{C_V\simeq\frac{12\pi^4}{5}Nk_B
\left(\frac T{\Theta_D}\right)^3\quad(T\ll\Theta_D).}
$$
At temperatures high compared with all <phonon> energies but below structural changes, the classical harmonic limit gives approximately $C_V=3Nk_B$. Optical modes are frozen out at temperatures well below their gaps.

**Spin waves.** A <spin wave> is a spatially varying collective precession of ordered spins; its quantum is a <magnon>. In a simple isotropic <ferromagnet>, a long-wavelength gapless branch has <energy> $\epsilon_{\mathbf k}\simeq D_s k^2$, where the spin stiffness $D_s$ has units $\mathrm{J\,m^2}$. State counting gives $D(\epsilon)\propto\epsilon^{1/2}$. For one isotropic branch in three dimensions,
$$
\frac{U-U_0}{V}=\frac{\Gamma(5/2)\zeta(5/2)}{4\pi^2D_s^{3/2}}(k_BT)^{5/2},
\qquad\boxed{C_V^{\rm magnon}\propto T^{3/2}.}
$$
This exponent is specific to a gapless quadratic ferromagnetic branch. Long-wavelength <antiferromagnetic spin waves> can be linear and instead give a $T^3$ heat-capacity law in three dimensions. Magnetic anisotropy or an applied field can open a gap, causing activated behaviour at still lower temperatures. Near a magnetic ordering transition, a dilute noninteracting spin-wave description is no longer adequate.

\Image[2017-q2-excitation-dispersions.png]
{title=Original schematic linear acoustic-phonon and quadratic ferromagnetic-magnon dispersions with their three-dimensional low-temperature heat-capacity powers}

These examples illustrate the <low-temperature energy of gapless bosonic excitations>: for $\epsilon\propto k^s$ in $d$ dimensions, $D(\epsilon)\propto\epsilon^{d/s-1}$, hence $U-U_0\propto T^{1+d/s}$ and $C_V\propto T^{d/s}$. The exponent records both the spatial dimension and the dispersion, making <calorimetry> a probe of elementary excitations.

**Component chemical potentials.** Define the per-spin <quantum concentration> $n_Q=\lambda_T^{-3}=(mk_BT/(2\pi\hbar^2))^{3/2}$. For a classical component with one-particle <potential energy> $U_\sigma(\mathbf r)$, the local <Helmholtz free energy> density is $k_BTn_\sigma[\ln(n_\sigma/n_Q)-1]+n_\sigma U_\sigma$. Its derivative with respect to <number density> gives the full local <chemical potential>, including the magnetic <energy>. With the up state defined as the lower level,
$$
\boxed{\mu_\uparrow=k_BT\ln\frac{n_\uparrow}{n_Q}-\mu_BB,
\qquad
\mu_\downarrow=k_BT\ln\frac{n_\downarrow}{n_Q}+\mu_BB.}
$$
The <Bohr magneton> $\mu_B$ is a <magnetic moment>; $\mu_BB$ is an <energy>. The factor two for the two spin states is not already included in $n_Q$.

**Total equilibrium chemical potential.** If spin conversion is allowed, a particle can be added in either spin state at the same equilibrium free-<energy> cost. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mu=\mu_\uparrow=\mu_\downarrow,}
$$
not their sum or zero. The total <particle number>, or a reservoir, fixes this common value; the supplied information does not specify a numerical <chemical potential>. A useful local expression is $\mu=k_BT\ln[n/(2n_Q\cosh x)]$, with $x=\mu_BB/(k_BT)$, whose density dependence is found below.

**Why it is uniform and common.** Moving a small number $dN$ of one species from $\mathbf r_1$ to $\mathbf r_2$ changes the <free energy> by $[\mu_\sigma(\mathbf r_2)-\mu_\sigma(\mathbf r_1)]dN$. If this difference were nonzero, a transfer could lower the <free energy>. Equilibrium under diffusion therefore requires each full <chemical potential> to be spatially constant. A spin flip transferring $dN$ from down to up similarly changes <free energy> by $(\mu_\uparrow-\mu_\downarrow)dN$, proving equality when spin relaxation is permitted. This is <chemical equilibrium of a classical spin gas>. Mere thermal contact at fixed separate spin populations would not enforce the latter equality; full spin equilibrium is the necessary assumption. A spatially varying <magnetic field> is balanced by a spatially varying density, not by a varying full <chemical potential>.

**Magnetization.** Invert the component equations at the common $\mu$:
$$
n_\uparrow(\mathbf r)=n_Qe^{\beta\mu}e^{x(\mathbf r)},\qquad
n_\downarrow(\mathbf r)=n_Qe^{\beta\mu}e^{-x(\mathbf r)}.
$$
The two levels have moments $+\mu_B$ and $-\mu_B$ along the field. The <magnetization>, <magnetic moment> per unit <volume>, is therefore
$$
\boxed{M(\mathbf r)=\mu_B(n_\uparrow-n_\downarrow)
=2\mu_Bn_Qe^{\beta\mu}\sinh x(\mathbf r).}
$$
Its SI units are $\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}$. This expression neglects interactions and magnetic feedback of the gas on the specified external field.

**High-temperature response.** For $|x|\ll1$, $\sinh x\simeq x$ and $\cosh x\simeq1$, yielding
$$
\boxed{M(\mathbf r)\simeq
\frac{2\mu_B^2 n_Qe^{\beta\mu}}{k_BT}B(\mathbf r)
\simeq\frac{n(\mathbf r)\mu_B^2}{k_BT}B(\mathbf r).}
$$
At fixed <number density>, this is the <Curie law>: the susceptibility with respect to $B$ is $n\mu_B^2/(k_BT)$. The conventional dimensionless susceptibility with respect to $H$ is approximately $\chi_v=\mu_0n\mu_B^2/(k_BT)$ when $B\simeq\mu_0H$. At fixed $\mu$, however, $n_Qe^{\beta\mu}$ changes with <temperature>, so $M$ itself need not scale simply as $1/T$. The moment per particle has the Curie behaviour; the density constraint must be stated.

**Total density.** Adding the two populations gives
$$
\boxed{n(\mathbf r)=2n_Qe^{\beta\mu}\cosh x(\mathbf r),\qquad
\frac{M(\mathbf r)}{n(\mathbf r)}=\mu_B\tanh x(\mathbf r).}
$$
For a finite container and fixed $N$, normalization gives $e^{\beta\mu}=N/[2n_Q\int\cosh x(\mathbf r)\,d^3r]$. In a weak field the leading density is uniform, with changes only of order $x^2$. Particles preferentially occupy regions where the magnitude of the field lowers one of the spin energies, even though the common <chemical potential> remains uniform.

**Gibbs free energy and its minimum.** For fixed atom number $N$, there are $N+n$ sites and
$$
W(N,n)=\binom{N+n}{n}
$$
configurations with $n$ indistinguishable vacancies. Their <configurational entropy> is $k_B\ln W$. Relative to the vacancy-free <Gibbs free energy> $G_0$, creating $n$ vacancies costs <energy> $n\epsilon$ and pressure-<volume> work $npv$. Put $A=\epsilon+pv>0$. Then
$$
G(n)=G_0+nA-k_BT\ln\frac{(N+n)!}{N!\,n!}.
$$
At leading thermodynamic order, <Stirling's approximation> gives
$$
G(n)\simeq G_0+nA-k_BT\big[(N+n)\ln(N+n)-N\ln N-n\ln n\big].
$$
The minimum obtained from the stationary point below is
$$
\boxed{G_{\min}\simeq G_0+Nk_BT\ln\left(1-e^{-A/(k_BT)}\right).}
$$
If the perfect crystal's <internal energy> and other <entropy> are chosen as zero, its <pressure> term is $G_0=pNv$. More generally the information given fixes the vacancy contribution rather than the absolute bulk <free energy>. The pressure-<volume> term must be retained because the number of sites, and hence the <volume>, changes.

**Equilibrium vacancy population.** Differentiating the extensive <free energy> at fixed $N,T,p$ gives
$$
\frac{\partial G}{\partial n}=A+k_BT\ln\frac n{N+n},\qquad
\frac{\partial^2G}{\partial n^2}=\frac{k_BTN}{n(N+n)}>0.
$$
The stationary point is consequently the unique interior minimum. Write $q=e^{-A/(k_BT)}$; its condition is $n/(N+n)=q$, giving the requested thermodynamic result for <equilibrium vacancies with variable lattice size>,
$$
\boxed{n_*\simeq\frac{Nq}{1-q}
=\frac{N}{e^{(\epsilon+pv)/(k_BT)}-1}.}
$$
Substitution back into $G$ cancels the terms containing $n_*$ and yields the boxed minimum. In the dilute-defect limit $A\gg k_BT$, $n_*\simeq Ne^{-A/(k_BT)}$. The Bose-like denominator is a consequence of the changing number of lattice sites and combinatorial <entropy>; it does not assert that vacancies here are an ideal gas of bosonic particles.

There is a <finite-size correction to a variable-site vacancy model> if “average” is interpreted exactly. The microscopic count written above gives the relative isothermal-isobaric sum
$$
\mathcal Y=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\binom{N+n}{n}q^n=(1-q)^{-N-1},
$$
so
$$
\boxed{\langle n\rangle_{\rm exact}=q\partial_q\ln\mathcal Y
=\frac{(N+1)q}{1-q}.}
$$
Its relative equilibrium <free energy> is $-k_BT\ln\mathcal Y=(N+1)k_BT\ln(1-q)$. Thus the source's $N$ numerator is correct to leading extensive order, but is not the exact finite-$N$ mean for that stated configuration count. A free-<energy> minimum is likewise not generally identical to the mean of a finite distribution. These distinctions vanish relatively as $N\to\infty$.

**Thermal volume.** Each atom or vacancy occupies <volume> $v$, so at the same thermodynamic accuracy
$$
\boxed{\langle V(T)\rangle\simeq v(N+n_*)
=\frac{Nv}{1-e^{-(\epsilon+pv)/(k_BT)}}.}
$$
For the exact counting model, replace $n_*$ by $(N+1)q/(1-q)$ in $v(N+\langle n\rangle)$. The vacancy-free limit is $Nv$ as $T\to0$. This model isolates expansion from vacancies, taking $v$ and $\epsilon$ constant; it omits ordinary <phonon> thermal expansion. Its growth at high <temperature> should not be extrapolated to arbitrarily large vacancy fractions in a stable crystal, and $A>0$ is essential for a normalizable variable-site ensemble.

**One particle.** Use the classical phase-space normalization of one quantum state per <volume> $h^3$, with no unspecified internal degeneracy. For $a>0$ and $\beta=1/(k_BT)$, the <canonical partition function> factorizes into <momentum> and position integrals:
$$
Z_1=\frac1{h^3}\int e^{-\beta p^2/(2m)}d^3p
\int e^{-\beta ar}d^3r.
$$
The Gaussian <momentum> integral is $(2\pi mk_BT)^{3/2}$. In <spherical coordinates>, put $t=\beta ar$:
$$
\int e^{-\beta ar}d^3r
=4\pi\left(\frac{k_BT}{a}\right)^3
\int_0^\infty t^2e^{-t}\,dt
=8\pi\left(\frac{k_BT}{a}\right)^3.
$$
Therefore the <classical ideal gas in a spherical linear trap> has
$$
\boxed{Z_1(T,a)=\frac{8\pi}{\lambda_T^3}\left(\frac{k_BT}{a}\right)^3
=\frac{8\pi(2\pi m)^{3/2}(k_BT)^{9/2}}{h^3a^3}.}
$$
Here $a$ is a force, in $\mathrm{J\,m^{-1}}$, so $k_BT/a$ is a length and $Z_1$ is dimensionless. A nonconfining $a\leq0$ would not give this finite spatial integral. As required by the classical phase-space approximation, the <temperature> must exceed the trap's discrete level scale.

**Indistinguishable dilute particles.** Independent labeled particles would give $Z_1^N$. In the classical dilute regime, division by $N!$ removes the overcounting of <permutations> of identical particles:
$$
\boxed{Z_N=\frac{Z_1^N}{N!}.}
$$
This is the Maxwell-Boltzmann limit, not an exact quantum <canonical partition function> at high occupation. Exchange effects cannot be captured merely by continuing this formula beyond its dilute regime.

**Free energy, energy and entropy.** The <Helmholtz free energy> is
$$
F=-k_BT\ln Z_N=-Nk_BT\ln Z_1+k_BT\ln N!.
$$
With leading <Stirling's approximation>, $\ln N!\simeq N\ln N-N$, this becomes
$$
\boxed{F\simeq-Nk_BT\left[\ln\frac{Z_1}{N}+1\right].}
$$
At fixed trap strength, $\partial_T\ln Z_1=9/(2T)$. Differentiating $F$ gives the requested <entropy>,
$$
\boxed{S=-\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}\right)_{N,a}
\simeq Nk_B\left[\ln\frac{Z_1}{N}+\frac{11}{2}\right].}
$$
The $11/2$ is $1+9/2$: the first term comes from indistinguishability through Stirling's formula, and the second from the <temperature> dependence of $Z_1$. Without the large-$N$ approximation, $S=k_B[N\ln Z_1-\ln N!+(9/2)N]$. Independently,
$$
U=-\partial_\beta\ln Z_N=\frac92Nk_BT,\qquad
C_a=\frac92Nk_B.
$$
The kinetic contribution is $3Nk_BT/2$ and the linear-trap potential contribution is $3Nk_BT$, also obtained from generalized equipartition.

**Entropy curves and reversible cooling.** For a fixed $N$ and a reference pair $(T_*,a_*)$, the dependence can be written without logarithms of dimensional quantities:
$$
\frac S{Nk_B}=\frac{S_*}{Nk_B}
+\frac92\ln\frac T{T_*}-3\ln\frac a{a_*}.
$$
At each fixed $a$, the <entropy> increases logarithmically and is concave downward in $T$; decreasing $a$ shifts the curve upward. Its formal classical divergence as $T\to0$ marks failure of classical statistics, not a physical negative-infinite <entropy>.

\Image[2017-q4-linear-trap-cooling.png]
{title=Original classical entropy curves for two linear-trap strengths; a horizontal constant-entropy arrow shows cooling on reversible decompression, within the dilute regime}

For a reversible adiabatic change, $S$ is constant at fixed $N$, so
$$
0=\frac92\,d\ln T-3\,d\ln a,
\qquad
\boxed{T_f=T_i\left(\frac{a_f}{a_i}\right)^{2/3}.}
$$
Hence reducing $a$ lowers the <temperature>: this is <isentropic decompression of a spherical linear trap>. The gas does work on the control that weakens the trap. Equivalently $dU=\langle\partial H/\partial a\rangle da=N\langle r\rangle da$, with $\langle r\rangle=3k_BT/a$; inserting $U=9Nk_BT/2$ gives the same relation. Reversibility requires an equilibrium sequence without heat input or <entropy> production. Because $Z_1$ stays constant on this adiabat, $N/Z_1$ also stays constant: this mechanism cools the cloud but does not by itself increase <quantum degeneracy>.

**Chemical potential and quantum threshold.** From the extensive <free energy>,
$$
\boxed{\mu=\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial N}\right)_{T,a}
\simeq k_BT\ln\frac{N}{Z_1}.}
$$
Thus the <quantum gas fugacity> is $z=e^{\mu/(k_BT)}\simeq N/Z_1$. Classical statistics requires $z\ll1$; quantum effects become important as the <chemical potential> comes within order $k_BT$ of the lowest one-particle <energy>. With the semiclassical zero at the potential minimum, the <quantum degeneracy threshold in a spherical linear trap> is consequently
$$
\boxed{N_{\rm quantum}\sim Z_1(T,a)
=\frac{8\pi}{\lambda_T^3}\left(\frac{k_BT}{a}\right)^3.}
$$
There are no numerical values of $m,T,a$ in the paper, so this parameter-dependent estimate is the requested atom number. It also follows from the peak density: $n(0)=N/[8\pi(k_BT/a)^3]$, and degeneracy starts when $n(0)\lambda_T^3$ is order one. The criterion is a crossover estimate, not an assertion that the classical formula remains valid at $\mu=0$. For spinless <bosons> the continuum excited-state saturation is more specifically $N_c=\zeta(9/2)Z_1$, while for <fermions> the onset is occupation constrained by exclusion. At temperatures comparable with the quantum trap spacing, the actual ground <energy> must be included consistently rather than identified exactly with the classical minimum.
