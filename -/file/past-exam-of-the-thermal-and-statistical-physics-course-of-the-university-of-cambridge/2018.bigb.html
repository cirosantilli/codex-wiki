= Solution
{parent=a}

First solve the <equation of state> for the <volume (thermodynamics)>:
$$
V(T,p,N)=\frac{Nk_BT}{p}-NA(T).
$$
The <Gibbs free energy> has differential $dG=-S\,dT+V\,dp+\mu\,dN$. At fixed <temperature> and <particle number>, integrate $dG=V\,dp$ between the two pressures:
$$
\Delta G=\int_{p_0}^{p_1}\left[\frac{Nk_BT}{p}-NA(T)\right]dp.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\Delta G=Nk_BT\ln\frac{p_1}{p_0}-NA(T)(p_1-p_0).}
$$
Use $S=-(\partial G/\partial T)_{p,N}$, keeping both endpoint pressures fixed while differentiating the difference:
$$
\boxed{\Delta S=-Nk_B\ln\frac{p_1}{p_0}+NA'(T)(p_1-p_0).}
$$
Equivalently, the <Maxwell relation> $(\partial S/\partial p)_{T,N}=-(\partial V/\partial T)_{p,N}$ gives $-Nk_B/p+NA'(T)$, whose <pressure> <integral> is the same result. These are the <Gibbs free-energy change for a pressure-linear gas correction> identities. The physical path requires $p_0,p_1>0$ and positive <volume (thermodynamics)> throughout. The correction $A(T)$ has units of <volume (thermodynamics)> per particle, so each term in $\Delta G$ is an <energy> and each term in $\Delta S$ has units of <energy> per kelvin.

= Solution
{parent=b}

For a <phonon> mode of angular frequency $\omega$, the equilibrium occupation is
$$
n_\omega=\frac1{e^{\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}-1}.
$$
Well above the <Debye temperature>, the relevant vibrational <energies> are small compared with $k_BT$. Expanding the exponential gives
$$
n_\omega\simeq\frac{k_BT}{\hbar\omega},\qquad
N_{\mathrm{ph}}=\sum_{\mathbf q,s}n_{\mathbf q,s}
\simeq\frac{k_BT}{\hbar}\sum_{\mathbf q,s}\frac1{\omega_{\mathbf q,s}}.
$$
The spectrum and its cutoff are fixed in the model, so the remaining sum is <temperature> independent. Thus
$$
\boxed{N_{\mathrm{ph}}\propto T,\qquad\rho\propto T\quad(T\gg\Theta_D).}
$$
For example, the three-dimensional <Debye model> with $D(\omega)=9N\omega^2/\omega_D^3$ gives $N_{\mathrm{ph}}\simeq(9/2)NT/\Theta_D$. This is the <high-temperature phonon number in the Debye model>. The number of <phonon> quanta can exceed $3N$; $3N$ counts modes, not their total occupation. Under the stipulated proportionality to <phonon> number, only the <phonon> contribution to resistivity has been calculated. A temperature-independent <residual resistivity> would add an intercept in a real sample.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Particles within each species are indistinguishable, while the two species are physically distinguishable and their numbers are fixed. Independence makes the <canonical partition function> factor by species:
$$
\boxed{Z=\frac{Z_1^{N_A}}{N_A!}\frac{Z_1^{N_B}}{N_B!}
=\frac{Z_1^N}{N_A!N_B!},\qquad N=N_A+N_B.}
$$
Since $Z_1\propto T^{3/2}$ at fixed <volume (thermodynamics)>, $U=-\partial_\beta\ln Z=3Nk_BT/2$. The canonical <entropy> is therefore
$$
\boxed{S=k_B\left[N\ln Z_1-\ln(N_A!)-\ln(N_B!)+\frac32N\right].}
$$
For large species populations, <Stirling's approximation> gives the equivalent <Sackur-Tetrode equation> form
$$
\boxed{S\simeq k_B\sum_{i=A,B}N_i\left[\ln\frac{Z_1}{N_i}+\frac52\right].}
$$
For structureless particles with the same translational <partition function>, $Z_1=V/\lambda^3$, where $\lambda$ is their common <thermal de Broglie wavelength>. More generally the supplied common constant factor in $Z_1$ should be retained in the <entropy>.

Writing $x_i=N_i/N$ separates the total-number contribution from the composition term:
$$
S\simeq Nk_B\left[\ln\frac{Z_1}{N}+\frac52\right]
-Nk_B(x_A\ln x_A+x_B\ln x_B).
$$
The last term is the <entropy of mixing> relative to separated ideal components at the same <temperature> and <pressure>. This is the <entropy of a two-species classical ideal gas>. Artificially labeling otherwise identical <chemical atoms> would not justify dividing by separate factorials or adding a mixing <entropy>. The pure-species <limit> is obtained by $x\ln x\to0$.

= Solution

**<Fermion> occupation and ordinary metallic <heat capacity>.** The <Fermi-Dirac distribution> for a single-particle state is
$$
\boxed{\langle n(E)\rangle=\frac1{e^{(E-\mu)/(k_BT)}+1}.}
$$
At low <temperature> it changes appreciably only in an <energy> window of width of order $k_BT$ about $\mu\simeq E_F$. If the full <density of states> $D(E)$ is smooth and nonzero there, only about $D(E_F)k_BT$ states change population. Each excited particle or hole carries <energy> of order $k_BT$ relative to the <Fermi surface>, so $U(T)-U(0)$ is of order $D(E_F)(k_BT)^2$. Differentiation therefore gives $C_{\mathrm{el}}\propto T$.

More precisely, the fixed-number <Sommerfeld expansion> gives
$$
U(T)-U(0)=\frac{\pi^2}{6}D(E_F)(k_BT)^2+O(T^4),
\qquad
\boxed{C_{\mathrm{el}}=\frac{\pi^2}{3}D(E_F)k_B^2T+O(T^3).}
$$
Here $D$ includes all counted internal species. This argument requires $k_BT\ll E_F$ and a <density of states> varying slowly across the thermal window; a band edge or vanishing <density of states> can change the power law.

**Two-dimensional linear dispersion at zero <chemical potential>.** Let $g$ count identical positive-energy branches, keeping the state-counting convention explicit. The number of <wavevector> states in a ring of radius $k$ is $gA(2\pi k\,dk)/(2\pi)^2$. With $E=\hbar vk$, this gives
$$
D_+(E)=\frac{gAE}{2\pi\hbar^2v^2},\qquad E\geq0.
$$
At $\mu=0$, the thermal <energy> of this branch is
$$
U_+(T)=\int_0^\infty D_+(E)\frac{E\,dE}{e^{E/(k_BT)}+1}
=\frac{gA(k_BT)^3}{2\pi\hbar^2v^2}
\int_0^\infty\frac{x^2}{e^x+1}dx.
$$
The dimensionless <integral> is $3\zeta(3)/2$. Differentiating gives
$$
\boxed{C_+(T)=\frac{9g\zeta(3)}{4\pi}\frac{Ak_B^3T^2}{\hbar^2v^2}
\sim\frac{Ak_B^3T^2}{\hbar^2v^2}.}
$$
This is the <heat capacity for a two-dimensional linear fermion dispersion>. A physical neutral particle–hole-symmetric <Dirac cone> also has <electron holes> in its filled negative-energy band. Their excitation <energy> gives an equal contribution, doubling $C_+$ for the same cone count. The $T^2$ scaling is unchanged. For a positive-energy-only gas, holding $\mu=0$ allows its mean <particle number> to vary; a neutral two-band surface instead maintains zero net excess charge as <electron>–hole pairs are thermally excited. These conventions should not be conflated when specifying an exact coefficient.

**Nonzero <chemical potential>.** The ordinary metallic regime is
$$
\boxed{k_BT\ll\mu\quad\text{or}\quad T\ll\mu/k_B.}
$$
The thermal window then lies far from the cone tip. Since $D(E)\propto E$, its fractional change across that window is only of order $k_BT/\mu$. Treating it as locally constant restores the Sommerfeld result $C_{\mathrm{el}}\propto D(\mu)T\propto\mu T$. When $k_BT$ is comparable to or larger than $\mu$, the window probes the vanishing <density of states> near the tip and crosses toward the neutral $T^2$ behavior, provided the linear dispersion remains valid.

**Bulk <phonons> and the surface-to-bulk comparison.** For the <topological insulator> model, assume bulk electronic excitations are frozen out while the conducting surface remains active. In three dimensions, acoustic <phonons> have a density of modes proportional to $\omega^2$. Normalize the Debye spectrum to $3N$ modes:
$$
D_{\mathrm{ph}}(\omega)=\frac{9N\omega^2}{\omega_D^3},\qquad
\hbar\omega_D=k_B\Theta_D.
$$
The thermal part of the <energy> is
$$
U_{\mathrm{ph}}=\frac{9N\hbar}{\omega_D^3}
\int_0^{\omega_D}\frac{\omega^3}{e^{\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}-1}d\omega.
$$
At $T\ll\Theta_D$, extending the upper integration <limit> to infinity introduces an exponentially small error. With $x=\hbar\omega/(k_BT)$ and $\int_0^\infty x^3/(e^x-1)dx=\pi^4/15$,
$$
U_{\mathrm{ph}}\simeq\frac{3\pi^4}{5}Nk_B\frac{T^4}{\Theta_D^3},
\qquad
\boxed{C_{\mathrm{ph}}\simeq\frac{12\pi^4}{5}Nk_B\left(\frac T{\Theta_D}\right)^3.}
$$
Temperature-independent <zero-point energy> makes no contribution to this <heat capacity>. The requested ratio consequently scales as
$$
\boxed{\frac{C_{\mathrm{el}}}{C_{\mathrm{ph}}}
\sim\frac{Ak_B^2\Theta_D^3}{\hbar^2v^2NT}.}
$$
For a fixed shape and composition, $A\propto L^2$ and $N\propto L^3$, so the ratio grows as $1/(LT)$. Smaller samples and lower temperatures favor the surface contribution. It dominates if the dimensionless right-hand side, including its state-counting prefactor, exceeds unity. This is the <surface-electron and bulk-phonon heat-capacity crossover>.

The continuum electronic law also requires a thermal <energy> larger than the surface finite-size level spacing, of order $\hbar v/L$. The <phonon> continuum has its own finite-size cutoff. Therefore dominance obtained by formally taking $T\to0$ cannot always be inferred from the two continuum power laws alone.

There is a numerical-normalization qualification in the paper. Combining the explicit one-positive-branch result above with the conventional $3N$-mode Debye normalization gives ratio prefactor $15g\zeta(3)/(16\pi^5)$; including <electron holes> doubles it. For $g=1$, this is approximately $0.00368$ before that doubling, rather than the supplied $5/(12\pi^2)\simeq0.0422$. The problem specifies neither a cone/<spin> multiplicity that equates these nor another mode normalization. Its supplied prefactor is therefore used as the stipulated approximation for the requested numerical estimate, rather than claimed as an exact consequence of the single-branch count.

**Numerical estimate.** A cubic sample of <volume (thermodynamics)> $1\,\mathrm{mm^3}$ has side $L=10^{-3}\,\mathrm m$. Counting the conducting surface on all six faces gives
$$
A=6L^2=6\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}.
$$
For one atom per cubic cell of side $a=1.5\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm m$,
$$
N\simeq\frac{L^3}{a^3}=2.96\times10^{17}.
$$
Use $k_B=1.380649\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$ and $\hbar=1.05457\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$. Inserting the stated $v=5\times10^5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $\Theta_D=500\,\mathrm K$ and $T=1\,\mathrm K$ into the paper's supplied prefactor gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{C_{\mathrm{el}}}{C_{\mathrm{ph}}}\simeq7.33\times10^{-6}.}
$$
Thus bulk <phonons> dominate at this <temperature> in this estimate. If only one face contributes, the ratio is $1.22\times10^{-6}$; if there are $\nu$ <chemical atoms> per <unit cell>, divide the corresponding estimate by $\nu$. These geometrical and crystallographic factors are not supplied explicitly.

With the same prefactor and geometry, extrapolating $1/T$ would suggest equality near $7.3\,\mathrm{\mu K}$. However, the surface finite-size scale is already of order $\hbar v/(k_BL)\simeq3.8\,\mathrm{mK}$ for this specimen. That extrapolated crossover lies outside the continuum electronic regime, so it is not a reliable prediction of the sample's ultimate lowest-temperature <heat capacity>.

= Solution

**Classical translational statistics.** Write the <number density> as $n=N/V$ and the <thermal de Broglie wavelength> as $\lambda=\sqrt{2\pi\hbar^2/(mk_BT)}$. Quantum exchange is negligible when <wave packets> rarely overlap:
$$
\boxed{n\lambda^3\ll1\quad\text{or}\quad n^{-1/3}\gg\lambda.}
$$
The equivalent state-counting statement is that the mean occupation of each thermally relevant one-particle state is much smaller than one. The equations in this question count one internal species; with $g$ equally populated <spin> states the relevant density per species is $n/g$.

Let $z=e^{\beta\mu}$ be the dimensionless <quantum gas fugacity>, with the lowest one-particle <energy> chosen as zero. Both quantum distributions can be written
$$
n_\eta(E)=\frac1{z^{-1}e^{\beta E}-\eta}
=\frac{ze^{-\beta E}}{1-\eta ze^{-\beta E}},
\qquad\eta=\begin{cases}+1&\text{bosons},\\-1&\text{fermions}.\end{cases}
$$
If $z\ll1$, then $n_\eta(E)\simeq ze^{-\beta E}\ll1$ for all $E\geq0$, recovering <Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics>. Conversely, low occupation even at the spectrum's bottom requires $z/(1-z)\ll1$ for <bosons> or $z/(1+z)\ll1$ for <fermions>; either condition implies
$$
\boxed{z=e^{\beta\mu}\ll1.}
$$
A single high-energy state can be sparsely occupied even in a degenerate gas, so the condition must hold across the relevant spectrum, not just for one arbitrarily chosen <energy>. Also, classical translational statistics does not imply that all internal excitation <energies> or other quantum effects can be ignored.

**Grand <partition function>, density and <pressure>.** Noninteracting particles have independent occupations of each one-particle state. Put $x_{\mathbf k}=ze^{-\beta\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}}$. Summing the allowed occupations gives
$$
\Xi_{\mathbf k}=\begin{cases}
\sum_{r=0}^\infty x_{\mathbf k}^r=(1-x_{\mathbf k})^{-1}&\text{bosons},\\
1+x_{\mathbf k}&\text{fermions},
\end{cases}
\qquad
\Xi=\prod_{\mathbf k}(1-\eta x_{\mathbf k})^{-\eta}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\ln\Xi=-\eta\sum_{\mathbf k}\ln(1-\eta ze^{-\beta\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}}).
$$
The <grand potential> is $\Omega_G=-k_BT\ln\Xi=-pV$ for this homogeneous gas. Replacing the sum by $V\int d^3k/(2\pi)^3$ and using $\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)$ gives
$$
\boxed{\beta p=-\eta\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}
\ln\left[1-\eta z e^{-\beta\hbar^2k^2/(2m)}\right].}
$$
The mean number is $N=z\,\partial_z\ln\Xi$, giving
$$
\boxed{\frac NV=\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}
\frac1{z^{-1}e^{\beta\hbar^2k^2/(2m)}-\eta}.}
$$
For <bosons>, the grand sum requires $z<1$ when the ground <energy> is zero. A macroscopic condensate at the limiting $z\to1$ must be separated from the continuum <integral> in the condensed phase. The following small-$z$ calculation is in the normal, nondegenerate regime, so that complication does not enter.

**Density expansion and inversion.** Expand the occupation as a <geometric series>:
$$
n_\eta(E)=ze^{-\beta E}+\eta z^2e^{-2\beta E}+z^3e^{-3\beta E}+\cdots.
$$
The Gaussian <wavevector> <integral> for its $\ell$th term is
$$
\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}e^{-\ell\beta\hbar^2k^2/(2m)}
=\left(\frac{m}{2\pi\ell\beta\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}
=\frac1{\lambda^3\ell^{3/2}}.
$$
It follows that
$$
\boxed{n\lambda^3=z+\frac{\eta z^2}{2^{3/2}}+O(z^3).}
$$
For the inverse series, put $y=n\lambda^3$ and try $z=y+c y^2+O(y^3)$. Substitution gives $0=(c+\eta/2^{3/2})y^2+O(y^3)$, hence
$$
\boxed{z=n\lambda^3-\frac\eta{2^{3/2}}(n\lambda^3)^2+O((n\lambda^3)^3).}
$$
The leading equality $n\lambda^3\simeq z$ directly connects the density and occupancy criteria for classical behavior.

**<Pressure> and its first quantum correction.** Expanding the <logarithm> in the <pressure> expression gives one extra factor $1/\ell$ compared with the density series:
$$
\beta p\lambda^3=z+\frac{\eta z^2}{2^{5/2}}+O(z^3).
$$
Insert the inverse series. To second order in $y=n\lambda^3$,
$$
\beta p\lambda^3=y-\frac{\eta y^2}{2^{3/2}}+
\frac{\eta y^2}{2^{5/2}}+O(y^3)
=y-\frac{\eta y^2}{2^{5/2}}+O(y^3).
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{pV=Nk_BT\left[1-\frac\eta{2^{5/2}}\frac{N\lambda^3}{V}
+O\left((N\lambda^3/V)^2\right)\right].}
$$
The first quantum correction at fixed density and <temperature> is
$$
\boxed{\Delta p=-\frac{\eta}{2^{5/2}}k_BT\,n^2\lambda^3.}
$$
It is negative for <bosons> and positive for <fermions>, and vanishes in the classical density <limit>.

**Virial coefficient.** Define the number-density <virial expansion> by
$$
\frac p{nk_BT}=1+B_2(T)n+B_3(T)n^2+\cdots
=\sum_{j=1}^\infty B_j(T)n^{j-1},\qquad B_1=1.
$$
The coefficient $B_j$ has units $\mathrm{volume}^{j-1}$ in this convention. Comparing terms gives
$$
\boxed{B_2^{\mathrm{ex}}(T)=-\frac\eta{2^{5/2}}\lambda^3
=-\frac\eta{2^{5/2}}\left(\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{mk_BT}\right)^{3/2}.}
$$
This is the <exchange contribution to the second virial coefficient>. It arises without a pair-interaction potential; the underlying gas is noninteracting. A molar-density convention would multiply this $B_2$ by the Avogadro constant. For $g$ equally populated internal species, exchange occurs within each species and the coefficient in terms of total density is smaller by a factor $g$.

**Why the two signs differ.** <Bosonic exchange symmetry> enhances the <probability> of nearby identical <bosons>, whereas <fermionic exchange symmetry> suppresses nearby identical <fermions> in the same internal state. In the dilute regime, the normalized equal-time <pair detection correlation> is
$$
g^{(2)}(r)=1+\eta e^{-2\pi r^2/\lambda^2}.
$$
The exchange part follows by squaring the Gaussian one-particle coherence factor $e^{-\pi r^2/\lambda^2}$. At zero separation this gives $g^{(2)}(0)=2$ for <bosons> and $0$ for one-component <fermions>. Integrating the exchange pair weight provides a direct coefficient check:
$$
B_2^{\mathrm{ex}}=-\frac12\int[g^{(2)}(r)-1]d^3r
=-\frac\eta2\left(\frac{\lambda^2}{2}\right)^{3/2}
=-\frac{\eta\lambda^3}{2^{5/2}}.
$$
Bunching therefore acts like an effective statistical attraction in the dilute <equation of state>, lowering the <pressure> needed at fixed density. Exclusion acts like an effective statistical repulsion, raising it. Neither description asserts an actual interparticle force. Different internal <fermion> species do not exclude each other from a common spatial location in the same way, explaining the importance of the species convention.

= Solution

**<Order parameter> and the free-energy landscape.** In <Landau theory>, take a scalar <magnetization> component at fixed <volume (thermodynamics)> and composition. It is an <order parameter>: its nonzero spontaneous value distinguishes an ordered phase with <ferromagnetism> from the symmetric high-temperature phase. One first constructs a <constrained order-parameter free energy> at specified <magnetization> by summing over microscopic states compatible with it. An external <magnetic field> is an independent control, so it can also appear in that constrained function. For a total <magnetic moment> the conjugate term is $-BM$; if $M$ is a <magnetization> density, the total-energy term is $-VBM$, or $-BM$ in <free-energy density>. Minimizing over $M$ then yields the equilibrium thermodynamic potential as a function of the imposed field.

Thus treating both $M$ and $B$ as arguments of a Landau function does not treat them as independent natural variables of the already-minimized equilibrium <free energy>. The first is the fluctuating or constrained <order parameter>, and the second its <conjugate field>.

At zero field, reversal symmetry requires $F(M)=F(-M)$, excluding odd powers. Near a continuous transition, assume the homogeneous constrained <free energy> is analytic for small $M$, that the quadratic coefficient crosses zero linearly, and that the quartic coefficient is positive. Then
$$
F(T,M)=F_0(T)+b(T-T_c)M^2+dM^4,
\qquad b>0,\quad d>0.
$$
The <temperature> $T_c$ is where the zero-field quadratic coefficient vanishes and spontaneous ordering begins in this approximation. Higher powers are negligible sufficiently close to it; spatial fluctuations are neglected in this mean-field treatment.

Stationarity gives $2M[b(T-T_c)+2dM^2]=0$. Above $T_c$, the only minimum is at zero. Below it, zero becomes a maximum and two minima appear:
$$
\boxed{M_{\mathrm{eq}}=0\quad(T>T_c),\qquad
M_{\mathrm{eq}}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{b(T_c-T)}{2d}}\quad(T<T_c).}
$$
The two signs describe <spontaneous symmetry breaking>: they are the average magnetizations in the symmetry-broken pure phases. A strictly finite system at exactly zero field samples the two wells symmetrically and has <ensemble average> $\langle M\rangle=0$. A spontaneous pure-phase value is defined by selecting a phase, for example taking the <thermodynamic limit> before removing a small field. This qualification distinguishes a minimum's location from the average over a symmetric two-well distribution.

{title=Quartic Landau free energy above and below the transition, showing the disordered and symmetry-broken minima}

**Minimum <free energy> and the heat-capacity jump.** At a nonzero minimum, $M^2=-b(T-T_c)/(2d)$. Substituting this value gives
$$
b(T-T_c)M^2+dM^4
=-\frac{b^2(T-T_c)^2}{2d}+\frac{b^2(T-T_c)^2}{4d}.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{F_{\min}(T)=F_0(T)-\frac{b^2(T-T_c)^2}{4d}\quad(T<T_c),}
$$
while $F_{\min}=F_0$ above the transition. Take the <temperature> <derivatives> only after minimizing, so the equilibrium <order parameter> is allowed to change with <temperature>. With $C_0=-TF_0''(T)$,
$$
C_V=\begin{cases}
C_0+Tb^2/(2d),&T<T_c,\\
C_0,&T>T_c.
\end{cases}
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Delta C_V\equiv C_V(T_c^-)-C_V(T_c^+)=\frac{T_cb^2}{2d}.}
$$
This is the <heat-capacity jump in quartic Landau theory>. The <entropy> is continuous at $T_c$, so there is no latent <heat>. If $F$ denotes <free-energy density> rather than total <free energy>, these expressions denote <heat capacity> per unit <volume (thermodynamics)> with the same normalization.

**Stabilizing a tricritical expansion.** At a <tricritical point>, the quartic coefficient is tuned to zero at the same point where the quadratic coefficient changes sign. Reversal symmetry still excludes odd powers. The next available stabilizing term is therefore
$$
F=F_0+b(T-T_c)M^2+d'M^6,
\qquad\boxed{d'>0.}
$$
A negative or vanishing leading sextic coefficient would not stabilize this truncation for large $|M|$; a higher positive term would then be needed and the present tricritical form would be insufficient. The ordinary quartic theory has a continuous transition for $d>0$, while a stabilized theory with $d<0$ can have a first-order transition. Their meeting at zero field is what makes the simultaneous tuning tricritical.

**The tricritical minimum.** At zero field and $T<T_c$, stationarity gives
$$
\frac{\partial F}{\partial M}=2M[b(T-T_c)+3d'M^4]=0,
\qquad
\boxed{M_{\mathrm{eq}}^4=\frac{b(T_c-T)}{3d'}.}
$$
The nonzero solutions are minima: their curvature is $2b(T-T_c)+30d'M^4=8b(T_c-T)>0$. Put $t=T_c-T>0$. Since $d'M^6=(bt/3)M^2$ at stationarity,
$$
F_{\min}-F_0=-btM^2+\frac{bt}{3}M^2
=-\frac23bt\sqrt{\frac{bt}{3d'}}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{F_{\min}(T)=F_0(T)-\frac{2b^{3/2}(T_c-T)^{3/2}}{\sqrt{27d'}}.}
$$
The <order parameter> now vanishes as $(T_c-T)^{1/4}$ rather than the quartic theory's square root.

**Heat-capacity singularity.** Define $K=2b^{3/2}/\sqrt{27d'}>0$, so the ordered-side singular <free energy> is $F_{\mathrm{sing}}=-K(T_c-T)^{3/2}$. Its <derivatives> are
$$
\frac{dF_{\mathrm{sing}}}{dT}=\frac32K(T_c-T)^{1/2},\qquad
\frac{d^2F_{\mathrm{sing}}}{dT^2}=-\frac34K(T_c-T)^{-1/2}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{C_V^{\mathrm{sing}}=-T\frac{d^2F_{\mathrm{sing}}}{dT^2}
=\frac{Tb^{3/2}}{2\sqrt{3d'}}(T_c-T)^{-1/2},
\qquad\alpha=\frac12.}
$$
This is the <tricritical heat-capacity amplitude in a sextic Landau expansion>. It is positive on the ordered side and diverges as $T\to T_c^-$, rather than remaining a finite jump. This value of the <heat-capacity critical exponent> is the mean-field result along the path keeping the quartic term zero; a detuning to $d>0$ ultimately restores ordinary quartic behavior. Fluctuation corrections to the <mean-field theory> are not included in the stipulated <polynomial> minimization.
