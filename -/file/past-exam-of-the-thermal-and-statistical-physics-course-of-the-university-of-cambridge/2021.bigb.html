= Solution

The fixed common <temperature>, rigid external container and fixed <particle> numbers on either side make the total <Helmholtz free energy> the appropriate <thermodynamic potential>. Let the barrier have cross-sectional area $A$, and let increasing $x$ expand the left compartment. Then $dV_1=A\,dx$ and $dV_2=-A\,dx$. At fixed <temperature> and <particle> number, $dF_j=-p_j\,dV_j$, so
$$
F(x)=F_1(T,V_1(x),N_1)+F_2(T,V_2(x),N_2),\qquad
\frac{dF}{dx}=A(p_2-p_1).
$$
Thus <mechanical equilibrium>, $p_1=p_2$, makes $F$ stationary. The physical <force> on the barrier towards increasing $x$ is $f=A(p_1-p_2)=-F'(x)$. If the barrier is displaced slightly from $x_*$, strict <mechanical stability> requires a restoring <force>:
$$
f(x_*+\delta x)=A\left(\frac{\partial p_1}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial p_2}{\partial x}\right)_{x_*}\delta x+O(\delta x^2),
\qquad f'(x_*)<0.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{F''(x_*)=A\left(\frac{\partial p_2}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial p_1}{\partial x}\right)_{x_*}>0.}
$$
The <Taylor expansion> $F(x_*+\delta x)=F(x_*)+\tfrac12F''(x_*)\delta x^2+O(\delta x^3)$ proves that <thermodynamic equilibrium> is a strict local minimum of the <Helmholtz free energy>, rather than merely a <stationary point>.

For individually stable gases, the positive <isothermal compressibilities> $\kappa_{T,j}=-(1/V_j)(\partial V_j/\partial p_j)_{T,N_j}$ make the sign especially transparent:
$$
\frac{\partial p_1}{\partial x}=-\frac{A}{V_1\kappa_{T,1}},\qquad
\frac{\partial p_2}{\partial x}=\frac{A}{V_2\kappa_{T,2}},\qquad
F''=A^2\left(\frac1{V_1\kappa_{T,1}}+\frac1{V_2\kappa_{T,2}}\right)>0.
$$
If these inequalities hold throughout the accessible interval, $F$ is a <strictly convex function> and an interior <stationary point> is the unique global minimum of the <Helmholtz free energy>. Stability at one point alone proves only the local statement. A vanishing restoring <derivative> describes marginal stability and requires examining higher-order terms.

An additive-looking <energy> expression does not by itself establish two <noninteracting subsystems>. Their allowed <microstates> must also form a <Cartesian product> of independently available states. Here the second label's allowed values depend on the first. The four allowed pairs and their <energies> are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
J&m_J&E/\epsilon\\\hline
1&-1&0\\
1&+1&2\\
2&-2&0\\
2&+2&4
\end{array}
$$
The <canonical partition function>, with $\beta=(k_BT)^{-1}$, is therefore
$$
Z=\sum_{J=1,2}e^{-\beta\epsilon J}\left(e^{\beta\epsilon J}+e^{-\beta\epsilon J}\right)
=\boxed{2+e^{-2\beta\epsilon}+e^{-4\beta\epsilon}.}
$$
The conditional sum over $m_J$ is $2\cosh(\beta\epsilon J)$ and depends on $J$; it cannot be pulled out as the <partition function> of an independent second subsystem. Summing independently over $J\in\{1,2\}$ and all four values $m\in\{-2,-1,1,2\}$ would invent four forbidden pairs and give eight states instead of four.

One can make the state labels independent by writing $s=m_J/J=\pm1$, but then $E(J,s)=\epsilon J(1+s)$ contains a coupling between them. For genuinely additive independent <energies>, the difference between the $s=+1$ and $s=-1$ states would be independent of $J$; here it is $2\epsilon J$. Hence **the proposed $J$ and $m_J$ subsystems cannot be treated as noninteracting independent subsystems**. The literal two-value reading of the PDF's endpoint notation has been used. If that notation were instead intended as shorthand for full angular-momentum multiplets, including every integer between the endpoints, the eight allowed states would give
$$
Z_{\rm full}=2+2e^{-\beta\epsilon}+2e^{-2\beta\epsilon}+e^{-3\beta\epsilon}+e^{-4\beta\epsilon}.
$$
The conclusion about the failure of independent-subsystem factorization is unchanged. The two state-counting conventions should not be mixed.

A nonzero <superconducting energy gap> suppresses the population of thermally excited <quasiparticles>. When $k_BT\ll\Delta$, their <Boltzmann factor> produces an approximately activated electronic <heat capacity>,
$$
C_e(T)\simeq A(T)\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta}{k_BT}\right),
$$
where the prefactor $A(T)$ varies algebraically rather than exponentially. For the rough estimate requested, neglect its change between the two quoted <temperatures>. Taking a ratio eliminates its unknown magnitude:
$$
\frac{C_e(3\,\mathrm K)}{C_e(2\,\mathrm K)}\simeq14
=\exp\left[\frac{\Delta}{k_B}\left(\frac1{2\,\mathrm K}-\frac1{3\,\mathrm K}\right)\right].
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{\Delta}{k_B}\simeq6\ln14\,\mathrm K=15.8\,\mathrm K\approx16\,\mathrm K.}
$$
The corresponding excitation <energy> is $\Delta\simeq2.19\times10^{-22}\,\mathrm J\simeq1.36\,\mathrm{meV}$. The units $\mathrm{mJ\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ of the two heat-capacity readings cancel in their ratio.

This is a <heat-capacity activation estimate>, not an exact determination of a gap from two data points. More generally, if $C_e=AT^p e^{-\Delta/(k_BT)}$, the same measurements give
$$
\frac{\Delta}{k_B}=6\left[\ln14-p\ln\frac32\right]\mathrm K.
$$
For example, the low-temperature <BCS theory> form for a fully gapped isotropic <superconductor> has $p=-3/2$ and would give about $19.5\,\mathrm K$. The stated approximate data and constant-gap assumption support the simpler $16\,\mathrm K$ estimate; they do not specify a detailed <density of states> or heat-capacity prefactor. Here $\Delta$ means the minimum <quasiparticle> excitation <energy>, rather than automatically twice that value.

Put $\beta=1/(k_BT)$ and define the <thermal de Broglie wavelength> of species $\alpha$ by
$$
\lambda_\alpha=\frac{h}{\sqrt{2\pi m_\alpha k_BT}}=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\hbar^2\beta}{m_\alpha}}.
$$
Ignoring the internal <degrees of freedom> as stipulated, each <molecule>'s translational <canonical partition function> is $V/\lambda_\alpha^3$. The two species do not interact, so their <partition functions> multiply; the treatment of <indistinguishable particles> requires a separate factorial for each species, not a factorial of the total <particle> number. Thus
$$
\boxed{Z_{\mathrm{CO-O_2}}=\frac{(V/\lambda_{\rm CO}^3)^{N_{\rm CO}}}{N_{\rm CO}!}\frac{(V/\lambda_{\rm O_2}^3)^{N_{\rm O_2}}}{N_{\rm O_2}!}.}
$$
Using $F=-k_BT\ln Z$ and <Stirling's approximation> gives the extensive <Helmholtz free energy>
$$
\boxed{F_{\mathrm{CO-O_2}}=k_BT\sum_{\alpha=\mathrm{CO,O_2}}N_\alpha\left[\ln\left(\frac{N_\alpha\lambda_\alpha^3}{V}\right)-1\right].}
$$
These are <ideal gas> expressions, valid for $n_\alpha\lambda_\alpha^3\ll1$, with $n_\alpha=N_\alpha/V$. The <energy> zero has been chosen so that separated CO and <oxygen> <molecules> have zero internal reference <energy>.

The given binding <energy> $\epsilon$ belongs to the formation of two carbon-dioxide <molecules>. Relative to the reference used in (a), the internal <energy> offset of one carbon-dioxide <molecule> is therefore $-\epsilon/2$, not $-\epsilon$. Its mass is $m_c=m_{\rm CO}+m_{\rm O_2}/2$ and its <thermal de Broglie wavelength> is $\lambda_c=h/\sqrt{2\pi m_ck_BT}$, where the subscript $c$ denotes $\mathrm{CO_2}$.

The single-molecule <canonical partition function> is $(V/\lambda_c^3)e^{\beta\epsilon/2}$. For $N_c$ indistinguishable noninteracting <molecules>,
$$
\boxed{Z_{\rm CO_2}=\frac{(V/\lambda_c^3)^{N_c}}{N_c!}\exp\left(\frac{\beta\epsilon N_c}{2}\right).}
$$
The corresponding <Helmholtz free energy>, using <Stirling's approximation>, is
$$
\boxed{F_{\rm CO_2}=N_ck_BT\left[\ln\left(\frac{N_c\lambda_c^3}{V}\right)-1\right]-\frac{\epsilon N_c}{2}.}
$$
The negative sign in the last term expresses the stabilizing binding <energy> and ensures that low <temperature> favors association.

At fixed <temperature>, <volume> and the numbers of all other species, the <chemical potential> is $\mu_\alpha=(\partial F/\partial N_\alpha)_{T,V,N_{\alpha'\ne\alpha}}$. For the combined noninteracting mixture, add the free <energies> from (a) and (b). Since the <thermal de Broglie wavelengths> are independent of <particle> number at fixed <temperature>, differentiation gives
$$
\boxed{\mu_{\rm CO}=k_BT\ln(n_{\rm CO}\lambda_{\rm CO}^3),\qquad
\mu_{\rm O_2}=k_BT\ln(n_{\rm O_2}\lambda_{\rm O_2}^3),}
$$
$$
\boxed{\mu_{\rm CO_2}=k_BT\ln(n_c\lambda_c^3)-\frac\epsilon2.}
$$
Each logarithm has a dimensionless argument, and each <chemical potential> has units of <energy> per <molecule>. The binding offset must be kept consistently when comparing species.

Introduce an <extent of reaction> $\xi$ increasing in the dissociation direction. The allowed particle-number changes conserve both carbon and <oxygen> atoms:
$$
dN_c=-2\,d\xi,\qquad dN_{\rm CO}=2\,d\xi,\qquad dN_{\rm O_2}=d\xi.
$$
At fixed <temperature> and <volume>, the resulting <Helmholtz free energy> change is
$$
dF=\left(-2\mu_c+2\mu_{\rm CO}+\mu_{\rm O_2}\right)d\xi.
$$
At an interior <chemical equilibrium>, both forward and backward changes are allowed, so the coefficient must vanish:
$$
\boxed{2\mu_{\rm CO_2}=2\mu_{\rm CO}+\mu_{\rm O_2}.}
$$
Equality of the three individual <chemical potentials> would be wrong: a reaction exchanges species in its stoichiometric proportions rather than <molecule> for <molecule>. Stability is also explicit here, since
$$
\frac{d^2F}{d\xi^2}=k_BT\left(\frac4{N_c}+\frac4{N_{\rm CO}}+\frac1{N_{\rm O_2}}\right)>0
$$
when all three species are present. Thus the equilibrium composition minimizes the free <energy> along the conserved-composition constraint.

= Solution
{parent=e}

Substituting the <chemical potentials> into the equilibrium relation gives
$$
2\ln(n_c\lambda_c^3)-\beta\epsilon
=2\ln(n_{\rm CO}\lambda_{\rm CO}^3)+\ln(n_{\rm O_2}\lambda_{\rm O_2}^3).
$$
Exponentiation and the positive square root give the <law of mass action> in number-density form:
$$
\boxed{n_c=n_{\rm CO}\sqrt{n_{\rm O_2}}\,\frac{\lambda_{\rm CO}^3\lambda_{\rm O_2}^{3/2}}{\lambda_c^3}\,e^{\epsilon/(2k_BT)}.}
$$
All three <thermal de Broglie wavelengths> have the same $T^{-1/2}$ dependence. Define the temperature-independent mass factor
$$
B=\left(\frac{m_c}{m_{\rm CO}}\right)^{3/2}\left(\frac{m_c}{m_{\rm O_2}}\right)^{3/4},\qquad
\frac{\lambda_{\rm CO}^3\lambda_{\rm O_2}^{3/2}}{\lambda_c^3}=B\lambda_c^{3/2}.
$$
If $f_\alpha=n_\alpha/n$, this exact equilibrium relation can be written
$$
n_c=Bf_{\rm CO}\sqrt{f_{\rm O_2}}\,n^{3/2}\lambda_c^{3/2}e^{\epsilon/(2k_BT)}.
$$
It already has the requested dimensional structure: $n^{3/2}\lambda_c^{3/2}$ has units $\mathrm{m^{-3}}$. Its coefficient, however, depends on the conserved elemental composition through the species fractions.

For the intended case of a gas obtained by dissociating initially pure <carbon dioxide>, conservation of the elemental ratio gives $n_{\rm CO}=2n_{\rm O_2}$ throughout. Hence
$$
n_{\rm CO}=\frac23(n-n_c),\qquad n_{\rm O_2}=\frac13(n-n_c),
$$
and the exact relation within this ideal-mixture model is
$$
n_c=A_0(n-n_c)^{3/2}\lambda_c^{3/2}e^{\epsilon/(2k_BT)},\qquad A_0=\frac{2B}{3\sqrt3}.
$$
At high <temperature> with $k_BT\gg\epsilon$ and in the dilute <ideal gas> regime, $n\lambda_c^3\ll1$, this equation is self-consistently dissociation dominated:
$$
\frac{n_c}{n}\simeq A_0\sqrt{n\lambda_c^3}\,e^{\epsilon/(2k_BT)}\ll1.
$$
Thus $n-n_c\simeq n$, and to leading order
$$
\boxed{n_{\rm CO_2}\simeq A\,n^{3/2}\lambda_{\rm CO_2}^{3/2}e^{\epsilon/(2k_BT)},\quad
A=\frac{2}{3\sqrt3}\left(\frac{m_{\rm CO_2}}{m_{\rm CO}}\right)^{3/2}\left(\frac{m_{\rm CO_2}}{m_{\rm O_2}}\right)^{3/4}.}
$$
Using nominal molecular masses $28,32,44$ in common mass units gives $A\simeq0.963$, confirming that it is of order unity. The exponential has been retained as the leading binding correction, although it tends to one as $T\to\infty$.

The printed question does not explicitly fix the elemental composition. Without the initially-pure-$\mathrm{CO_2}$ assumption, the universal result is the boxed mass-action relation, and $A=Bf_{\rm CO}\sqrt{f_{\rm O_2}}$ approaches a composition-dependent constant when both dissociation products have nonzero high-temperature fractions. It need not be of order unity for an arbitrarily unbalanced mixture. Also, $k_BT\gg\epsilon$ alone does not justify replacing $n-n_c$ by $n$ at arbitrarily high density; the dilute classical regime is part of the model.

A <phase transition> is a qualitative change of <thermodynamic phase> as control parameters such as <temperature>, <pressure> or <chemical potential> are varied. A genuine equilibrium singularity is a nonanalyticity of the appropriate <thermodynamic potential> in the <thermodynamic limit>; finite systems generally show rounded crossovers instead.

An <order parameter> is a macroscopic quantity that distinguishes the phases. It often vanishes in a symmetric disordered phase and becomes nonzero after <spontaneous symmetry breaking>, although density-driven transitions need not have that simple symmetry interpretation. It can be a scalar, <vector> or <tensor>. At a <first-order phase transition> an appropriate first <derivative> of the free <energy> is discontinuous, and the <order parameter> commonly jumps; at a <continuous phase transition> the <order parameter> can approach zero continuously while a susceptibility or a higher <derivative> becomes singular.

Three examples are:

* A <ferromagnet> below its <Curie temperature> has a nonzero spontaneous <magnetization> $\mathbf M$ in zero applied <magnetic field>. Above the transition, the equilibrium <magnetization> vanishes in zero field. The direction of $\mathbf M$ selects a broken-symmetry state.
* Near a <liquid-gas critical point>, the density contrast $\rho_{\rm liquid}-\rho_{\rm gas}$ distinguishes the coexisting liquid and gas. It tends to zero at the critical point; below that point, changing the conjugate <chemical potential> through coexistence switches between low- and high-density phases.
* At an isotropic-to-nematic transition of a <liquid crystal>, the <nematic order parameter> is a symmetric traceless <tensor>, for example $Q_{ij}=\langle u_i u_j-\delta_{ij}/3\rangle$, where $\mathbf u$ is a molecular orientation. It vanishes in the <isotropic phase> and is nonzero when the <molecules> preferentially align in a <nematic liquid crystal>.

These examples also illustrate why an <order parameter> must reflect the physical distinction between phases rather than merely be any quantity that changes with <temperature>.

For $N$ indistinguishable classical <particles> the <canonical partition function> is an <integral> over <phase space>,
$$
Z_N=\frac{1}{N!h^{3N}}\int_{V^N}\prod_{i=1}^N d^3r_i\int\prod_{i=1}^Nd^3p_i\,
\exp\left[-\beta\sum_i\frac{p_i^2}{2m}-\frac\beta2\sum_{i\ne j}\phi(r_{ij})\right].
$$
The factor $1/N!$ removes overcounting under <particle> permutations, and $h^{3N}$ makes the classical phase-space measure dimensionless. The <pair potential> depends only on positions, so all momentum integrations separate. The three-dimensional <Gaussian integral> for each <particle> gives
$$
\frac1{h^3}\int_{\mathbb R^3}e^{-\beta p^2/(2m)}d^3p
=\frac1{h^3}\left(\frac{2\pi m}{\beta}\right)^{3/2}
=\lambda^{-3},\qquad
\lambda=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\hbar^2\beta}{m}}.
$$
The <grand canonical partition function> is $\Xi=\sum_{N=0}^\infty e^{\beta\mu N}Z_N$, where <chemical potential> $\mu$ controls the mean number of <particles>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Xi=\sum_{N=0}^\infty\frac1{N!}\left(\frac{e^{\beta\mu}}{\lambda^3}\right)^N
\int_{V^N}\prod_{i=1}^Nd^3r_i\,
\exp\left[-\frac\beta2\sum_{i\ne j}\phi(r_{ij})\right].}
$$
For $N=0$, the empty products and configurational <integral> equal one. The factor $1/2$ in the interaction prevents double counting of unordered <particle> pairs.

Write $M=\mathcal N=V/b$ for the number of cells; this is fixed even though the <particle> number $N$ fluctuates. Approximate each position <integral> by the cell sum $\int d^3r_i\simeq b\sum_{\alpha_i=1}^M$, evaluating the slowly varying intercell <pair potential> at cell centres. With the assumed single-occupancy restriction, the fixed-$N$ contribution becomes
$$
\frac1{N!}\left(\frac{e^{\beta\mu}b}{\lambda^3}\right)^N
\sum_{\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_N\ \mathrm{distinct}}
\exp\left[-\frac\beta2\sum_{i\ne j}\phi_{\alpha_i\alpha_j}\right].
$$
An occupation configuration $\{n_\alpha\}$ with $N=\sum_\alpha n_\alpha$ selects an unordered set of $N$ occupied cells. There are exactly $N!$ ways to assign the temporary <particle> labels to that set. They all have the same interaction <energy>, cancelling the prefactor $1/N!$.

Summing over all <particle> numbers is therefore equivalent to summing each cell occupation independently over $0,1$:
$$
\boxed{\Xi\simeq\sum_{n_1=0}^1\cdots\sum_{n_M=0}^1
\left(\frac{e^{\beta\mu}b}{\lambda^3}\right)^{\sum_\alpha n_\alpha}
\exp\left[-\frac\beta2\sum_{\alpha\ne\alpha'}n_\alpha n_{\alpha'}\phi_{\alpha\alpha'}\right].}
$$
The activity per cell, $e^{\beta\mu}b/\lambda^3$, is dimensionless. This is a <lattice gas>, with each cell either empty or occupied.

The exclusion of multiple occupation is an additional physical modeling assumption, explicitly imposed by the question. Making a cell small does not by itself forbid two point <particles> from entering it; a repulsive core or a dilute-occupancy approximation would be needed to justify that restriction starting from a general continuum <pair potential>. In the remaining parts it is part of the defined cell model.

Let $q$ be the <coordination number of a lattice>, the number of nearest neighbours of each cell away from boundaries. Define an effective <chemical potential> incorporating the cell's translational <entropy>:
$$
\widetilde\mu=\mu+k_BT\ln\frac b{\lambda^3},\qquad
\left(\frac{e^{\beta\mu}b}{\lambda^3}\right)^{\sum n_\alpha}=e^{\beta\widetilde\mu\sum n_\alpha}.
$$
Use $\langle\alpha\alpha'\rangle$ for an unordered nearest-neighbour bond, counted once. The occupation <Hamiltonian> including the chemical-potential term is
$$
\mathcal H_{\rm occ}=-4J\sum_{\langle\alpha\alpha'\rangle}n_\alpha n_{\alpha'}-\widetilde\mu\sum_\alpha n_\alpha.
$$
Under $n_\alpha=(1+\sigma_\alpha)/2$, one bond contributes
$$
-4Jn_\alpha n_{\alpha'}=-J\left(1+\sigma_\alpha+\sigma_{\alpha'}+\sigma_\alpha\sigma_{\alpha'}\right).
$$
In a regular lattice with periodic <boundary conditions>, the number of bonds is $Mq/2$, and each spin occurs in $q$ linear bond terms. Therefore
$$
\mathcal H_{\rm eff}=-J\sum_{\langle\alpha\alpha'\rangle}\sigma_\alpha\sigma_{\alpha'}
-\left(qJ+\frac{\widetilde\mu}{2}\right)\sum_\alpha\sigma_\alpha
-\frac{MqJ}{2}-\frac{M\widetilde\mu}{2}.
$$
The pair sum equals one half of the ordered sum used in the paper. Consequently the effective field is
$$
\boxed{h(T,\mu)=qJ+\frac\mu2+\frac{k_BT}{2}\ln\frac b{\lambda^3}.}
$$
Thus $\Xi=\sum_{\{\sigma\}}e^{-\beta\mathcal H_{\rm eff}}$ is the <Ising model> partition sum, including the displayed spin-independent constant if its absolute normalization is required. Dropping that constant changes $\Xi$ by a multiplicative factor but leaves spin <probabilities> and the phase-transition argument unchanged. For simple cubic cells in three dimensions, $q=6$ and the first term in $h$ is $6J$. Open boundaries replace $q$ by the local coordination near the surface; the stated uniform field is the bulk or periodic-lattice result.

= Solution

For attractive nearest-neighbour interactions, $J>0$, the transformed model is a ferromagnetic <Ising model>. In three dimensions and at $T<T_c$, its infinite-volume <spontaneous magnetization> has two branches. As the effective field crosses zero, the equilibrium mean spin changes discontinuously from a negative value to a positive value. Since $(\partial h/\partial\mu)_T=1/2$, varying the physical <chemical potential> crosses this same <first-order phase transition>.

The particle-hole-symmetric coexistence line is
$$
\boxed{h=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mu_{\rm coex}(T)=-2qJ-k_BT\ln\frac b{\lambda^3}.}
$$
The mean Ising spin is a rescaled occupation density. Writing $n=\langle N\rangle/V$ for the physical <number density>,
$$
\boxed{m_{\rm I}=\frac1M\sum_\alpha\langle\sigma_\alpha\rangle
=2\frac{\langle N\rangle}{M}-1=2bn-1.}
$$
The negative-spin phase is a dilute gas and the positive-spin phase is a dense, liquid-like phase. If the spontaneous spin magnitude at coexistence is $m_0(T)>0$, their number densities are
$$
n_{\rm gas}=\frac{1-m_0(T)}{2b},\qquad n_{\rm liquid}=\frac{1+m_0(T)}{2b},\qquad
n_{\rm liquid}-n_{\rm gas}=\frac{m_0(T)}b.
$$
Thus the physical <order parameter> is a density or occupation imbalance, not an actual magnetic <moment> of these <particles>. In a closed system with an intermediate mean density, the same transition appears as <phase coexistence> between the two densities. At $T=T_c$ the density contrast vanishes continuously; above $T_c$, sweeping the <chemical potential> gives no coexistence jump. A true discontinuity requires the <thermodynamic limit>; a finite sum over finitely many cells remains analytic.

Let each step have <length> $a$, and let $K$ be the number of right steps among $N$ independent steps. Then $K$ has a <binomial distribution> and the physical displacement is $x=a[K-(N-K)]=a(2K-N)$. Therefore $K=(N+x/a)/2$, and the position <probability mass> is
$$
\boxed{P_{\rm 1D}(x,N)=\binom{N}{(N+x/a)/2}
 w_+^{(N+x/a)/2}w_-^{(N-x/a)/2}.}
$$
This is zero unless $x/a$ is an integer, $|x/a|\leq N$, and $N+x/a$ is even. The parity condition matters because the walk alternates between the two sublattices. If distance is measured in lattice spacings, replace $x/a$ by $x$.

Normalization follows from the <binomial theorem>. Its first two <moments> provide useful checks:
$$
\langle x\rangle=Na(w_+-w_-),\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(x)=4Na^2w_+w_-=Na^2\left[1-(w_+-w_-)^2\right].
$$
These are exact discrete-time statements; a smooth continuum <probability density> requires coarse-graining over the lattice and its parity oscillation.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The one-step <master equation> for the <biased random walk> is
$$
P(x,t+\tau)=w_+P(x-a,t)+w_-P(x+a,t),\qquad t=N\tau.
$$
Expand in a <Taylor series> and retain the first <time> <derivative> and the first two space <derivatives>. Since $w_++w_-=1$ and $w_--w_+=2\epsilon$,
$$
\tau\partial_tP=2a\epsilon\,\partial_xP+\frac{a^2}{2}\partial_x^2P+\cdots.
$$
Thus the usual continuum truncation gives the requested <Fokker-Planck equation>,
$$
\partial_tP=D_0\partial_x^2P+C\partial_xP,\qquad
\boxed{D_0=\frac{a^2}{2\tau},\qquad C=\frac{2a\epsilon}{\tau}.}
$$
The <diffusion coefficient> has units $\mathrm{length^2/time}$, while $C$ has units of <velocity>. The physical <drift velocity> is $v=-C=a(w_+-w_-)/\tau$, because the standard advection term is $-v\partial_xP$. A rightward bias therefore means $\epsilon<0$, $C<0$, and $v>0$.

The approximation must be stated carefully. The truncation is a controlled <diffusion limit of a weakly biased random walk> when $a\to0$, $\tau\propto a^2$, and $\epsilon\propto a$, keeping $D_0$ and $C$ finite. It is also the spatial <diffusion> approximation for a continuous-time jump process with exponentially distributed waiting times of mean $\tau$.

If the original fixed-step-time walk has a finite bias, its exact <variance> from (a) instead gives the <diffusion coefficient> of the Gaussian envelope about the drifting mean:
$$
\boxed{D_{\rm fixed}=\frac{a^2}{2\tau}(1-4\epsilon^2)=\frac{2a^2w_+w_-}{\tau}.}
$$
One obtains the same correction by retaining $\tfrac12\tau^2\partial_t^2P$ in the <time> expansion and using the leading advection equation to replace $\partial_t^2P$ by $v^2\partial_x^2P$. The result is $D_{\rm fixed}=D_0-v^2\tau/2$. In particular, a deterministic one-way walk has zero <variance>, as this expression requires. Thus $D_0$ is the usual intended first-time-derivative approximation, not an exact variance-growth coefficient at arbitrary bias. The later continuum estimates use that convention, with the fixed-time alternative identified where relevant.

{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

For this <radial random walk on a regular tree>, the drawing is an infinite <regular tree> of degree $z$, not a planar lattice with closed loops. The radial coordinate is the number of <edges> in the unique path to the root. A non-root <vertex> has one neighbour inward and $z-1$ neighbours outward, so equal <probabilities> for its $z$ <edges> give
$$
w_-^{\rm rad}=\frac1z,\qquad w_+^{\rm rad}=\frac{z-1}{z}.
$$
Let $r$ be radial distance measured in <edge> units. For $r>1$, <probability> arriving in shell $r$ comes from shell $r-1$ by an outward step or from shell $r+1$ by an inward step:
$$
\boxed{P(r,N+1)=\frac{z-1}{z}P(r-1,N)+\frac1zP(r+1,N),\qquad r>1.}
$$
This has precisely the same bulk <master equation> as the one-dimensional <biased random walk>, with
$$
\boxed{\epsilon=\frac{w_-^{\rm rad}-w_+^{\rm rad}}2=\frac{2-z}{2z}.}
$$
The outward bias is geometric: beyond the first shell, the number of <vertices> is $M_r=z(z-1)^{r-1}$, so there are more outward than inward choices even though no <edge> direction is dynamically preferred. $P(r,N)$ is the <probability> summed over an entire shell, not the <probability> at one specified <vertex>; no additional shell-size factor belongs in the boxed recurrence.

The equality concerns the evolution equation away from the root, not equality to the unrestricted full-line <binomial distribution> at all positions. The root imposes a different boundary rule.

{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

At the root all $z$ neighbours are in shell one, so the <probability> of leaving the root for that shell is one. Conversely, a walker anywhere in shell one chooses its unique rootward <edge> with <probability> $1/z$. The two boundary recurrences are therefore
$$
\boxed{P(1,N+1)=P(0,N)+\frac1zP(2,N),}
$$
$$
\boxed{P(0,N+1)=\frac1zP(1,N).}
$$
Together with $P(0,0)=1$ and $P(r>0,0)=0$, these equations determine the distribution. In particular $P(1,1)=1$ and $P(0,2)=1/z$, which check the special root coefficient. For the original indexing, replace $N+1$ by $N$ and $N$ by $N-1$ on the right-hand sides. Summing these equations and the bulk recurrence conserves $\sum_{r\geq0}P(r,N)=1$.

= Solution
{parent=e}

Farther than a few <edge> lengths from the root, replace the radial coordinate $r$ by a physical coordinate $x=ar$ and smooth the <master equation>. It has the same constant-coefficient <Fokker-Planck equation> as in (b). Under the conventional first-time-derivative truncation,
$$
D_0=\frac{a^2}{2\tau},\qquad
\boxed{C=\frac{a(2-z)}{z\tau}<0\quad\text{for }z>2.}
$$
The radial drift $v=-C=a(z-2)/(z\tau)$ is outward. For $z=2$ the tree is the infinite line, its radial distance is an unbiased reflected walk, and $C=0$.

The root cannot absorb <probability> or allow negative radial distance. Write the equation as a <continuity equation>, using the <Fokker-Planck probability current>
$$
\partial_tP=-\partial_xj,\qquad j=-D\partial_xP-CP.
$$
The appropriate continuum <boundary condition> is reflecting, or zero flux:
$$
\boxed{j(0,t)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D\partial_xP(0,t)+CP(0,t)=0.}
$$
It is not generally $\partial_xP(0,t)=0$: that simpler condition is correct only when $C=0$. With the finite-bias fixed-step Gaussian convention from (b), use $D=D_{\rm fixed}=2a^2(z-1)/(z^2\tau)$ instead; the drift sign and zero-current <boundary condition> are unchanged. A continuum <boundary condition> represents the microscopic root region after coarse-graining, rather than reproducing each discrete root <probability> at individual step times.

= Solution
{parent=f}

For the infinite system with outward drift, total <probability> remains one, but <probability> at a fixed distance from the root eventually decreases as the walker moves away. The question concerns this local decay, not a loss of total <probability>.

The independent dimensions of the continuum coefficients are $[D]=L^2/t$ and $[C]=L/t$. The <drift-diffusion decay time> and its associated <length> are, up to numerical factors:
$$
\ell_*\sim\frac D{|C|},\qquad
\boxed{\tau^*\sim\frac D{C^2}.}
$$
Equivalently, equating the drift distance $|C|t$ to the diffusive width of order $\sqrt{Dt}$ gives this crossover scale. Rescaling $x=(D/|C|)y$ and $t=(D/C^2)s$ removes the dimensional coefficients from the equation.

With the conventional coefficients used in the requested continuum derivation,
$$
\frac{D_0}{C^2}=\frac{\tau z^2}{2(z-2)^2},\qquad
\boxed{\tau^*\sim\tau\frac{z^2}{(z-2)^2},\quad z>2,}
$$
where the last expression suppresses the numerical factor as requested. The elementary drifted <Gaussian distribution> on the full line has
$$
P(x,t)=\frac1{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}\exp\left[-\frac{(x+Ct)^2}{4Dt}\right].
$$
At fixed $x$, its exponential decay factor is $e^{-C^2t/(4D)}$, so an exponential e-folding <time> would conventionally be called $4D/C^2$, consistent with the same dimensional scale. The reflecting half-line changes the spatial form and algebraic prefactor. The substitution $P=e^{-Cx/(2D)}\psi$ gives $\partial_t\psi=D\partial_x^2\psi-(C^2/4D)\psi$, displaying the same exponential scale for outward drift.

For $z=2$, $C=0$ and no finite drift-induced exponential decay <time> exists: the <probability> near the root decays only algebraically. For the finite-bias, deterministic-step Gaussian envelope discussed in (b), the corresponding dimensional expression is instead $D_{\rm fixed}/C^2=2\tau(z-1)/(z-2)^2$. These continuum estimates are not exact return-probability decay rates for the lattice walk at arbitrary $z$; in particular, a <diffusion> approximation need not describe far-tail return events accurately at strong bias.
