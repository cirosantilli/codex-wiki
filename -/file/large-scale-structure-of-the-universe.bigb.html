= Large-scale structure of the universe
{wiki}

The large-scale structure of the universe is the cosmic distribution of matter on scales where galaxies and halos form a web of clusters, filaments, sheets, and voids.

= Correlation function
{disambiguate=astronomy}
{parent=Large-scale structure of the universe}
{title2=$\xi(r)$}
{wiki}

= Cosmological two-point correlation function
{synonym}

The <cosmological two-point correlation function> measures excess pairing or correlated density fluctuations at a separation. For a statistically homogeneous and isotropic mean-zero <density contrast>, $\xi(r)=\langle\delta(\mathbf x)\delta(\mathbf x+\mathbf r)\rangle$. For distinct points in a discrete galaxy population it instead specifies the factorial pair density, $\bar n^2[1+\xi(r)]$. The latter excludes self-pairs; an unsmoothed discrete-density covariance also contains a <Dirac delta> at zero separation. These definitions connect large-scale galaxy clustering with the <matter power spectrum> while keeping <galaxy power spectrum shot noise> separate.

= Isotropic cosmological correlation-power-spectrum relation
{parent=Correlation function (astronomy)}
{title2=$\xi(r)=\dfrac1{2\pi^2}\int_0^\infty k^2P(k)j_0(kr)dk$}

For the <Fourier transform> convention $\delta_{\mathbf k}=\int d^3x\,\delta(\mathbf x)e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}$ and covariance $\langle\delta_{\mathbf k}\delta_{\mathbf k'}\rangle=(2\pi)^3P(k)\delta_D(\mathbf k+\mathbf k')$, inversion gives $\xi(\mathbf r)=\int d^3k\,P(k)e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}/(2\pi)^3$. <Isotropy> reduces the angular integral to $4\pi j_0(kr)$, where $j_0(x)=\sin x/x$ is the order-zero <spherical Bessel function>. At zero separation this gives the <variance> if the spectrum integral converges. A nonnegative spectrum can produce negative correlations away from the origin.

= Galaxy two-point correlation function
{parent=Correlation function (astronomy)}
{title2=$dP_{12}=\bar n^2[1+\xi(r)]dV_1dV_2$}

For a homogeneous isotropic <point process> of <galaxies> with mean number density $\bar n$, the <galaxy two-point correlation function> gives the excess pair probability at distinct positions. Dividing the factorial pair density by the probability of a central <galaxy> gives conditional neighbouring-galaxy density $\bar n[1+\xi(r)]$. Choosing a central location uniformly in volume does not perform this conditioning.

= Galaxy-conditioned neighbour count
{parent=Galaxy two-point correlation function}
{title2=$N(<R)=4\pi\bar n\int_0^R[1+\xi(r)]r^2dr$}

The mean number of other <galaxies> within distance $R$ of a randomly chosen <galaxy> is the integral of its conditional number density. A random location instead has mean count $4\pi\bar nR^3/3$. For a <power law> $\xi(r)=(r_0/r)^\gamma$, the extra count is $4\pi\bar n r_0^\gamma R^{3-\gamma}/(3-\gamma)$ when $\gamma<3$. The central <galaxy> is excluded; including it adds one. This distinction matters even when the unconditional mean number density is spatially uniform.

= Galaxy cluster
{parent=Large-scale structure of the universe}
{wiki}

A <galaxy cluster> is a gravitationally bound collection of <galaxies>, diffuse gas and <dark matter>. Much of its ordinary matter can reside in hot intracluster gas, visible through <thermal bremsstrahlung> and the <Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect>. The two signals weight the electron density differently, making the cluster useful both as a structure-formation probe and, with a geometrical model, as a distance indicator.

= Cluster gravitational-lensing mass estimate
{parent=Galaxy cluster}
{title2=$\kappa=\Sigma/\Sigma_{\rm crit}$}

<Gravitational lensing> constrains a <galaxy cluster>'s projected mass independently of its light output or dynamical equilibrium. The <lensing convergence> is projected <surface mass density> divided by $\Sigma_{\rm crit}=c^2D_s/(4\pi GD_dD_{ds})$, using the appropriate <angular diameter distances>. <Strong gravitational lensing> constrains the central mass through multiple-image geometry, and <weak gravitational lensing> constrains larger-scale projected mass through coherent image distortions. Converting these observations into a three-dimensional mass needs geometrical modelling and the relevant lensing degeneracies.

= Cluster velocity-dispersion mass estimate
{parent=Galaxy cluster}
{title2=$M\sim R\sigma_v^2/G$}

The <virial theorem> balances a relaxed <galaxy cluster>'s orbital <kinetic energy>, of order $M\sigma_v^2$, against its <Newtonian gravitational potential energy>, of order $-GM^2/R$. Their ratio gives the displayed mass scale. The numerical coefficient depends on the density profile, radius definition and conversion from line-of-sight <velocity dispersion> to three-dimensional orbital motion. Membership contamination, anisotropy and nonequilibrium dynamics can bias the inference.

= Intracluster medium
{parent=Galaxy cluster}
{wiki}

= Intracluster gas
{synonym}

The <intracluster medium> is diffuse hot plasma between the <galaxies> of a <galaxy cluster>. Its X-ray radiation includes <thermal bremsstrahlung> and atomic line emission. Measured <temperature> and density profiles supply a <cluster hydrostatic mass estimator> when approximately thermal <hydrostatic equilibrium> holds. The medium supplies much of a cluster's baryonic mass, but this need not equal its total gravitating mass.

= Cluster hydrostatic mass estimator
{parent=Intracluster medium}
{title2=$M(<r)=-\dfrac{k_BTr}{\mu m_pG}\dfrac{d\log(\rho_gT)}{d\log r}$}

For spherical thermal <hydrostatic equilibrium>, $dP/dr=-\rho_gGM(<r)/r^2$. Inserting the <ideal gas law> $P=\rho_gk_BT/(\mu m_p)$ with constant mean molecular weight gives the displayed estimator. The density $\rho_g$ is gas density, while $M$ includes every gravitating component. A varying $\mu$ adds $-d\log\mu/d\log r$ inside the logarithmic bracket. Nonthermal pressure requires differentiating the total supporting pressure rather than thermal pressure alone.

= Galaxy survey
{parent=Large-scale structure of the universe}

A galaxy survey measures positions and other properties of a selected galaxy sample. A spectroscopic survey uses <cosmological redshift> to estimate radial distance, with corrections for <peculiar velocity>. Its volume, selection, <galaxy bias>, and sampling determine which <matter power spectrum> modes can be inferred.

= Redshift-space distortions
{parent=Galaxy survey}
{wiki}

Redshift-space distortions arise because observed galaxy redshifts include <peculiar velocity> as well as <cosmological redshift>. Distances inferred solely from expansion therefore distort clustering along the <line of sight>. Coherent large-scale motion and small-scale velocity dispersion produce different effects on the inferred <matter power spectrum>.

= Galaxy power spectrum shot noise
{parent=Galaxy survey}
{title2=$P_{\rm shot}=1/\bar n$}

Sampling with a homogeneous <Poisson point process> of mean <comoving number density> $\bar n$ adds a constant $1/\bar n$ to the dimensional galaxy <power spectrum>. Subtracting its expectation leaves sampling uncertainty. Correlated tracer populations and survey weights modify this simple formula; survey depth alone does not specify $\bar n$.

= Survey window function
{parent=Galaxy survey}
{title2=$W(\mathbf x)$}

A survey window function describes the finite spatial coverage and weights of a <galaxy survey>. Multiplication by this window in position space mixes <Fourier modes> by convolution in <Fourier space>. A dimension of extent $L$ cannot robustly measure wavelengths much larger than $L$; the fundamental <wavenumber> of a periodic box of that size is $2\pi/L$.

= Cosmological structure formation
{parent=Large-scale structure of the universe}
{wiki}

Cosmological structure formation is the growth of primordial density perturbations into sheets, filaments, halos, galaxies, and clusters under gravity.

= Hierarchical galaxy formation
{parent=Cosmological structure formation}
{title2=$\sigma(M)\text{ increases as }M\text{ decreases}$}

In <hierarchical galaxy formation>, low-mass dark-matter structures typically collapse before larger structures because the smoothed linear density variance is larger on smaller mass scales. Accretion and mergers assemble increasingly massive haloes. Cooling, feedback and angular momentum determine how baryons form galaxies within that halo hierarchy; the ordering is statistical rather than an absolute rule for every object.

= Galactic angular-momentum catastrophe
{parent=Hierarchical galaxy formation}

The galactic angular-momentum catastrophe is the excessive loss or redistribution of baryonic <specific angular momentum> in early simulated galaxy formation, producing overly compact disks or large central concentrations. Premature cooling, gravitational torques, interactions, and resolution contributed. Improved <stellar feedback> and gas accretion histories can mitigate it; it is a historical simulation problem rather than a theorem against <cold dark matter>.

= Downsizing in galaxy formation
{parent=Hierarchical galaxy formation}

Downsizing denotes the tendency for the characteristic mass of actively star-forming galaxies to decrease with time, together with related evidence that more massive quiescent systems formed their stars earlier or over shorter intervals. It does not assert that their <dark-matter haloes> assembled earlier: stellar formation and halo or stellar assembly are separate histories.

= Monolithic galaxy formation
{parent=Hierarchical galaxy formation}

Monolithic galaxy formation idealizes a <galaxy> as forming in one early rapid dissipative collapse, followed mainly by passive evolution. Rapid formation of the stars is distinct from early assembly of all those stars into their final object; <hierarchical galaxy formation> can assemble old stellar populations later.

= Halo baryon-to-star conversion efficiency
{parent=Hierarchical galaxy formation}
{title2=$\epsilon_*=M_*/(f_bM_h)$}

= Halo star-formation efficiency
{synonym}

The integrated <halo star-formation efficiency> is the fraction of a <dark-matter halo>'s reference baryonic mass converted into stars: $\epsilon_*=M_*/(f_bM_h)$, with <cosmic baryon fraction> $f_b$. It is distinct from <star-formation efficiency per free-fall time>. Cooling, gas supply and retention, <stellar feedback> and <active-galactic-nucleus feedback> make it strongly dependent on <dark-matter halo> mass. With stellar <mass-to-light ratio> $\Upsilon_*$, a simple central-galaxy mapping is $L=\epsilon_*f_bM_h/\Upsilon_*$. Changing this efficiency changes both the mapping and the logarithmic Jacobian between the <dark-matter halo> mass function and the <luminosity function>.

= Low-angular-momentum baryonic disk estimate
{parent=Hierarchical galaxy formation}
{title2=$r_d\simeq j_d^2/(GM_b)$}

Selecting a baryonic mass $M_b$ from a normalized cumulative distribution $M_b(<j)\propto j$ fixes a cutoff specific angular momentum $j_d=(M_b/M_{b,\rm tot})j_{\rm vir}$. With circular support and dominant enclosed baryonic gravity, the outer radius is $r_d\simeq j_d^2/(GM_b)$ and speed $v_d\simeq GM_b/j_d$. The mean specific angular momentum is $j_d/2$, which defines a different one-zone radius. The estimate assumes energy dissipation but negligible redistribution of <specific angular momentum>.

= Baryon conversion efficiency of a halo
{parent=Hierarchical galaxy formation}
{title2=$f_*=M_*/(f_bM_h)$}

The <baryon conversion efficiency of a halo> is the fraction of its baryonic mass incorporated into stars. If the halo retains the <cosmic baryon fraction> $f_b$, its stellar-to-total-mass ratio is $f_bf_*$. This efficiency depends on halo mass, epoch, gas supply and feedback and must not be confused with a star-formation efficiency per free-fall time.

= Halo-to-galaxy luminosity mapping
{parent=Baryon conversion efficiency of a halo}
{title2=$\Phi(L)=n_h(M_h)\left|dM_h/dL\right|$}

A monotonic luminosity assignment to a counted halo population converts its differential abundance per mass into a <luminosity function> by a change of variable. If $n_h\propto M_h^{-a}$ and $L\propto M_h^p$, then $\Phi\propto L^{-1-(a-1)/p}$. This simple mapping assumes one counted galaxy per halo, no scatter and a fixed selection. A mass-dependent <baryon conversion efficiency of a halo>, satellites and stellar-population differences modify it.

= Stellar-to-halo mass ratio
{parent=Baryon conversion efficiency of a halo}
{title2=$\epsilon_*=M_*/M_h=f_bf_*$}

The <stellar-to-halo mass ratio> compares a galaxy's <galactic stellar mass> with its host's total halo mass. In a simple baryon-conversion model it is $\epsilon_*=f_bf_*$. Observational comparisons must distinguish this total-mass ratio from the fraction of baryons converted, and must specify whether central, satellite or all stellar populations are included.

= Constant baryon conversion mapping of halo and stellar mass
{parent=Stellar-to-halo mass ratio}
{title2=$M_*=\epsilon_*M_h$}

If every counted halo hosts one counted galaxy and the baryon fraction and conversion efficiency are mass-independent without scatter, <galactic stellar mass> is a constant multiple of halo mass. The galaxy mass function is obtained by changing variable with $dM/dM_*=1/\epsilon_*$. Satellites, efficiency variation and scatter prevent this mapping from being generally exact.

= Cooling criterion for galaxy formation
{parent=Hierarchical galaxy formation}
{title2=$t_{\rm cool}\lesssim t_{\rm dyn}$}

Gas in a collapsing halo is heated toward a <virial temperature>. If its cooling time is short compared with the dynamical time, thermal support is removed and rapid condensation can form a galaxy. If cooling is slower, a hot atmosphere can persist or condense only gradually. This criterion links baryonic galaxy formation to the dark-matter assembly hierarchy without making every halo into one luminous object.

= Atomic and molecular cooling thresholds for galaxy formation
{parent=Cooling criterion for galaxy formation}
{title2=$T_{\rm atomic}\sim10^4\,\mathrm K,\quad T_{\rm molecular}\sim10^2\text{--}10^3\,\mathrm K$}

Primordial <atomic line cooling> becomes effective near the temperature where electronic excitations are accessible, of order ten thousand kelvin. <Molecular hydrogen> has lower-energy transitions and can cool colder gas if it forms and survives. The corresponding <virial temperature> thresholds translate into epoch-dependent halo mass thresholds. Radiation, molecular chemistry, <metal-line cooling> and gas density qualify a single universal minimum galaxy mass.

= Upper galaxy mass from gas cooling
{parent=Cooling criterion for galaxy formation}
{title2=$M_{\rm cool}\text{ of order a galactic mass}$}

In simple hierarchical models, increasing system mass raises the <virial temperature> and can carry primordial gas beyond its efficient atomic line-cooling interval. At assembly densities, the cooling-time constraint then distinguishes galaxy-sized condensations from larger hot groups or clusters. The resulting characteristic mass is approximate, depends on composition and structure, and does not prohibit subsequent mergers of already formed stellar systems.

= Zeldovich approximation
{c}
{parent=Cosmological structure formation}
{wiki}

The Zeldovich approximation maps a fluid element from its <Lagrangian coordinate> $\mathbf q$ to the comoving position $\mathbf x=\mathbf q+D(t)\mathbf s(\mathbf q)$, where $D$ is the <linear growth factor>. It follows the initial gravitational displacement along a straight comoving trajectory until trajectories cross.

= Planar exactness of the Zeldovich approximation
{parent=Zeldovich approximation}

For $\mathbf r=a[\mathbf q+b(t)(p(q_1),0,0)]$, put $J=1+bp'$. Before <shell crossing>, mass conservation gives $\rho=\bar\rho/J$. The dust background equation $3\ddot a/a=-4\pi G\bar\rho$ and the growing-mode equation $\ddot b+2H\dot b=4\pi G\bar\rho b$ imply $\nabla_r\cdot\ddot{\mathbf r}=-4\pi G\bar\rho+4\pi G\bar\rho bp'/J=-4\pi G\rho$. Thus planar <Zeldovich approximation> trajectories satisfy the full nonlinear dust force-divergence equation until the map loses invertibility. All three physical coordinates are included in the divergence; the two unperturbed directions still contribute background acceleration.

= Cosmological Lagrangian displacement
{title2=$\boldsymbol\psi(\mathbf q,t)$}
{parent=Zeldovich approximation}

The cosmological Lagrangian displacement sends an initially labelled matter element from its <Lagrangian coordinate> $\mathbf q$ to $\mathbf x(\mathbf q,t)=\mathbf q+\boldsymbol\psi(\mathbf q,t)$. Its <derivative>[time derivative] determines the <peculiar velocity> through $\mathbf v=a\dot{\boldsymbol\psi}$.

= Deformation tensor in the Zel'dovich approximation
{title2=$d_{ij}$}
{parent=Zeldovich approximation}

For an irrotational displacement $\mathbf s=-\nabla_q\Phi$, the deformation tensor is the symmetric Hessian $d_{ij}=-\partial_i\partial_j\Phi$. Its real eigenvalues determine the principal collapse directions.

= Shell crossing
{parent=Zeldovich approximation}
{wiki}

Shell crossing occurs when the Lagrangian-to-Eulerian map ceases to be one-to-one. Its Jacobian determinant vanishes, the pressureless single-stream density formally diverges, and several streams subsequently occupy the same position.

= Zeldovich pancake thickness after shell crossing
{c}
{parent=Shell crossing}
{title2=$1+\delta=\prod_{i=1}^3(1-b\lambda_i)^{-1}$}

For the <Zeldovich approximation> $\mathbf x=\mathbf q-b\nabla_q\psi$, let $\lambda_i$ be the eigenvalues of the potential Hessian, with this sign convention. Before <shell crossing>, <mass conservation> gives the displayed density. The first positive eigenvalue reaches $b\lambda_i=1$ and collapses one direction, creating a sheetlike <cosmological caustic>. Extrapolating straight trajectories after crossing lets particles pass through without self-consistent gravitational trapping, so the predicted multistream sheet spreads excessively. Collisionless gravitational evolution keeps matter more tightly bound; the linear displacement extrapolation cannot determine its late thickness reliably.

= Cosmological caustic
{parent=Shell crossing}

A cosmological caustic is the image of a singular point of the Lagrangian map. The ideal collisionless density diverges there, although velocity dispersion and other small-scale physics regularize the singularity.

= Tidal torque theory
{parent=Cosmological structure formation}
{wiki}

Tidal torque theory explains the early <angular momentum> of a protohalo through the misalignment of its <mass second-moment tensor> $I_{ij}$ and the <Hessian matrix> $T_{ij}=\partial_i\partial_j\Phi$ of the surrounding <peculiar gravitational potential>. At leading order,
$$
J_i\propto a^2\dot D\,\epsilon_{ijk}T_{jl}I_{lk}.
$$

= Cosmic web
{parent=Cosmological structure formation}
{wiki}

The cosmic web is the network of matter sheets, filaments, nodes, and intervening voids produced by anisotropic gravitational collapse.

= Collisionless dark-matter Vlasov equation
{c}
{parent=Cosmological structure formation}

For comoving position $\mathbf x$, canonical momentum $\mathbf p=am\mathbf x'$, and Newtonian potential $\phi$, the dark-matter phase-space density obeys
$$
\partial_\eta f+\frac{\mathbf p}{am}\mathbin\cdot\nabla_xf-am\nabla\phi\mathbin\cdot\nabla_pf=0.
$$

This is the gravitational, expanding-universe specialization of the <Vlasov equation>.

= Velocity-dispersion tensor of collisionless matter
{title2=$\sigma^{ij}$}
{parent=Collisionless dark-matter Vlasov equation}

= Velocity-dispersion tensor
{synonym}

The velocity-dispersion tensor is the second central moment of a phase-space distribution,
$$
\sigma^{ij}=\langle u^iu^j\rangle-v^iv^j.
$$
It contributes the stress-divergence term $\rho^{-1}\partial_j(\rho\sigma^{ij})$ to the Euler equation and vanishes in the ideal single-stream limit.

= Einstein-de Sitter universe
{c}
{parent=Large-scale structure of the universe}
{wiki=Einstein–de_Sitter_universe}

An Einstein-de Sitter universe is spatially flat and contains only pressureless matter. In conformal time, $a\propto\tau^2$ and $\mathcal H=a'/a=2/\tau$.

= Spherical secondary infall
{parent=Einstein-de Sitter universe}

Spherical secondary infall follows concentric collisionless shells around a primordial overdensity. Each shell initially expands with the Hubble flow, turns around, collapses, and subsequently crosses other shells.

= Turnaround radius
{title2=$r_*$}
{parent=Spherical secondary infall}
{wiki}

The turnaround radius of a shell is its maximum physical radius before collapse. At a given cosmic time, the currently turning shell defines a natural similarity length.

= Self-similar secondary infall
{parent=Spherical secondary infall}

If the initial fractional excess mass is a power law in enclosed mass, spherical secondary infall admits a similarity reduction in which radii scale by the current turnaround radius and masses by the mass of the currently turning shell.

= Standard perturbation theory in cosmology
{title2=SPT}
{parent=Large-scale structure of the universe}

Standard perturbation theory expands the density contrast and velocity divergence of a pressureless irrotational fluid in powers of the linear density field.

= Nonlinear wavenumber
{parent=Standard perturbation theory in cosmology}
{title2=$k_{\rm NL}$}

The nonlinear <wavenumber> is the inverse scale at which linear <density contrast> fluctuations become of order unity. For the one-dimensional convention $\langle\delta(k)\delta(k')\rangle=2\pi\delta^{(D)}(k+k')P_L(k)$, a usual estimate is $k_{\rm NL}P_L(k_{\rm NL})/\pi\sim1$. Loop momenta above this scale require the <effective field theory of large-scale structure> even when external modes are perturbative.

= Gaussian initial conditions
{c}
{parent=Standard perturbation theory in cosmology}

Gaussian initial conditions make every connected primordial correlation function above second order vanish. <Wick theorem> then reduces perturbative late-time correlation functions to sums of products of the linear power spectrum.

= Alpha mode-coupling kernel
{title2=$\alpha(\mathbf k_1,\mathbf k_2)$}
{parent=Standard perturbation theory in cosmology}

The continuity-equation kernel is
$$
\alpha(\mathbf k_1,\mathbf k_2)
=\frac{(\mathbf k_1+\mathbf k_2)\mathbin\cdot\mathbf k_1}{k_1^2}.
$$
Its symmetrization is $\alpha_s=1+(\mathbf k_1\mathbin\cdot\mathbf k_2)(k_1^{-2}+k_2^{-2})/2$.

= Beta mode-coupling kernel
{title2=$\beta(\mathbf k_1,\mathbf k_2)$}
{parent=Standard perturbation theory in cosmology}

The Euler-equation kernel is the symmetric function
$$
\beta(\mathbf k_1,\mathbf k_2)
=\frac{|\mathbf k_1+\mathbf k_2|^2
(\mathbf k_1\mathbin\cdot\mathbf k_2)}{2k_1^2k_2^2}.
$$

= Standard perturbation theory density kernel
{title2=$F_n$}
{parent=Standard perturbation theory in cosmology}

The symmetrized kernel $F_n$ convolves $n$ linear density fields to produce the $n$th-order density perturbation. In an Einstein-de Sitter universe,
$$
F_2=\frac57\alpha_s+\frac27\beta.
$$

= One-dimensional cosmological density kernel
{parent=Standard perturbation theory density kernel}
{title2=$F_{n,s}(q_1,\ldots,q_n)$}

For plane-parallel pressureless matter in an <Einstein-de Sitter universe>, before <shell crossing>, the symmetrized growing-mode <standard perturbation theory density kernel> is
$$
F_{n,s}(q_1,\ldots,q_n)=\frac{(q_1+\cdots+q_n)^n}{n!q_1\cdots q_n}.
$$
In particular $F_{2,s}=(q_1+q_2)^2/(2q_1q_2)$. An unsymmetrized representation of the one-dimensional <beta mode-coupling kernel> is $(q_1+q_2)/q_1$; its symmetric part is the expression above.

= Ultraviolet softness of the second-order density kernel
{parent=Standard perturbation theory density kernel}

At fixed external momentum $\mathbf k$, mass and momentum conservation imply
$$
F_2(\mathbf q,\mathbf k-\mathbf q)=O(k^2/q^2)
$$
as $q\to\infty$. In particular, the constant term in the hard-momentum expansion must cancel.

= Unsmoothed matter-density reduced skewness
{title2=$S_3=34/7$}
{parent=Standard perturbation theory density kernel}

For <Gaussian initial conditions> and the Einstein-de Sitter second-order <standard perturbation theory density kernel>, the unsmoothed <density contrast> obeys
$$
\langle\delta^3\rangle=\frac{34}{7}\sigma^4+O(\sigma^6),
\qquad \sigma^2=\langle\delta^2\rangle.
$$
There are three choices of the second-order field and two connected <Wick contractions>; the angular average of $F_2$ is $5/7+(2/7)/3=17/21$. Thus the reduced <skewness> is $6(17/21)=34/7$. Smoothing changes this result, and the unsmoothed calculation requires a finite <variance>.

= Tree-level connected matter five-point function
{parent=Standard perturbation theory density kernel}

For Gaussian initial conditions, the tree-level connected matter five-point function has perturbative-order partitions
$$
4+1+1+1+1,
\qquad 3+2+1+1+1,
\qquad 2+2+2+1+1.
$$
They correspond respectively to a four-valent star, an $F_3$--$F_2$ tree, and a chain of three $F_2$ vertices. Every topology contains four linear power spectra.

= One-loop matter power spectrum
{parent=Standard perturbation theory in cosmology}

For Gaussian linear perturbations, the one-loop matter power spectrum is
$$
P_{\rm 1-loop}=a^2P+a^4(P_{22}+2P_{13}),
$$
where $P_{22}$ correlates two second-order fields and $P_{13}$ correlates a linear field with a third-order field.

= P22 contribution to the one-loop matter power spectrum
{title2=$P_{22}(k)$}
{c}
{parent=One-loop matter power spectrum}

The $P_{22}$ contribution correlates two second-order density fields. For symmetrized kernels,
$$
P_{22}(k)=2\int\frac{d^3q}{(2\pi)^3}F_2(\mathbf q,\mathbf k-\mathbf q)^2P(q)P(|\mathbf k-\mathbf q|).
$$

= P13 contribution to the one-loop matter power spectrum
{title2=$P_{13}(k)$}
{c}
{parent=One-loop matter power spectrum}

The $P_{13}$ contribution correlates a linear density field with a third-order field:
$$
P_{13}(k)=3P(k)\int\frac{d^3q}{(2\pi)^3}F_3(\mathbf k,\mathbf q,-\mathbf q)P(q).
$$
Both orderings contribute $2P_{13}$ to the power spectrum.

= Infrared cancellation in large-scale structure
{parent=One-loop matter power spectrum}

At equal time, leading loop contributions from a very long-wavelength displacement cancel between perturbative diagrams. This is required by the <equivalence principle>, because a coherent long displacement merely translates short-scale structure.

= Scale-free matter power spectrum
{parent=One-loop matter power spectrum}

A scale-free linear matter spectrum has $P(k)=Ak^n$. Dimensional analysis then makes its one-loop $P_{22}$ contribution proportional to $A^2k^{2n+3}$ whenever the loop integral converges.

= Scale-free potential and bulk-flow variance
{parent=Scale-free matter power spectrum}
{title2=$\sigma_\Phi(R)\propto R^{(1-n)/2},\quad\sigma_v(R)\propto R^{-(n+1)/2}$}

For a <matter power spectrum> $P(k)=Ak^n$ at a fixed epoch, the <Poisson equation> gives potential Fourier amplitudes proportional to $\delta_k/k^2$, and the growing-mode <peculiar velocity> is proportional to $\delta_k\mathbf k/k^2$. With a <spherical top-hat window function>, their variances are proportional to $\int k^{n-2}W(kR)^2dk$ and $\int k^nW(kR)^2dk$, respectively. Rescaling $kR$ gives the displayed powers when the integrals converge: $1<n<5$ for the averaged potential and $-1<n<3$ for the bulk velocity. Absolute averaged potential has infrared sensitivity; band-limited fluctuations or potential differences can have different convergence requirements. The density variance converges only for $-3<n<1$, so these three absolute averages cannot all converge for one unbroken power law at every scale.

= Scale-free smoothed density variance
{title2=$\sigma_R\propto R^{-(n+3)/2}$}
{parent=Scale-free matter power spectrum}

For an unsmoothed density spectrum $P(k)=Ak^n$ and a scale-dependent smoothing window $\widetilde W(kR)$, substituting $q=kR$ in the variance integral gives $\sigma_R^2\propto R^{-(n+3)}$, provided $\int_0^\infty q^{n+2}|\widetilde W(q)|^2dq$ converges. For a window tending to one at zero, infrared convergence requires $n>-3$; the ultraviolet condition depends on the window. A real-space spherical top-hat also requires $n<1$.

= Standard perturbation theory velocity kernel
{title2=$G_n$}
{parent=Standard perturbation theory in cosmology}

The symmetrized kernel $G_n$ convolves $n$ linear density fields to produce the $n$th-order peculiar-velocity divergence in cosmological standard perturbation theory.

= One-loop matter bispectrum
{parent=Standard perturbation theory in cosmology}

For Gaussian initial conditions, the matter bispectrum through one loop is the sum of the tree diagram $B_{211}$ and the four one-loop topologies $B_{222}$, $B_{321}^{I}$, $B_{321}^{II}$, and $B_{411}$.

= B411 contribution to the one-loop matter bispectrum
{c}
{parent=One-loop matter bispectrum}
{title2=$B_{411}$}

The connected correlation of one fourth-order and two first-order <density contrasts> contracts a pair of inputs at the fourth-order vertex into a loop. For each choice of the fourth-order external field there are twelve <Wick contractions>. In one dimension,
$$
B_{411}=12P_L(k_1)P_L(k_2)\int\frac{dq}{2\pi}F_4(-k_1,-k_2,q,-q)P_L(q)+\text{two cyclic terms}.
$$
For the <one-dimensional cosmological density kernel>, $F_4=-k_3^4/(24k_1k_2q^2)$ exactly. Its leading cutoff dependence is therefore proportional to $k_3^2F_2(k_1,k_2)P_L(k_1)P_L(k_2)\int dq\,P_L(q)/q^2$.

= B222 contribution to the one-loop matter bispectrum
{c}
{parent=One-loop matter bispectrum}
{title2=$B_{222}$}

The connected correlation of three second-order <density contrasts> forms a triangle of three linear <power spectra>. It has eight <Wick contractions>. In one dimension, with $k_1+k_2+k_3=0$ and $P_L$ the power at the evaluation time,
$$
B_{222}=8\int\frac{dq}{2\pi}F_2(q,k_1-q)F_2(-q,k_2+q)F_2(q-k_1,-k_2-q)P_L(q)P_L(k_1-q)P_L(k_2+q).
$$
For the <one-dimensional cosmological density kernel>, its hard-loop integrand starts as $-k_1^2k_2^2k_3^2P_L(q)^3/q^6$.

= One-loop matter trispectrum
{title2=$T_{\rm 1-loop}$}
{parent=Standard perturbation theory in cosmology}

For Gaussian initial conditions, the one-loop connected matter trispectrum contains the perturbative-order classes $T_{5111}$, $T_{4211}$, $T_{3311}$, $T_{3221}$, and $T_{2222}$. Each class includes every inequivalent external-leg assignment and Wick contraction.

= Effective field theory of large-scale structure
{title2=EFTofLSS}
{parent=Large-scale structure of the universe}
{wiki}

The effective field theory of large-scale structure represents unresolved short-scale dynamics by an effective stress tensor. Its leading deterministic correction is proportional to $k^2\delta^{(1)}$ and renormalizes ultraviolet-sensitive loop contributions.

= Second-order density Laplacian counterterm
{parent=Effective field theory of large-scale structure}
{title2=$C(\Lambda)\partial_x^2\delta^{(2)}$}

For the one-dimensional <B411 contribution to the one-loop matter bispectrum>, contracting two hard inputs in the fourth-order field gives $-(I_\Lambda/2)k^2\delta^{(2)}$, where $I_\Lambda=\int_{q_h<|q|<\Lambda}dq\,P_L(q)/(2\pi q^2)$. A <counterterm> $C(\Lambda)\partial_x^2\delta^{(2)}$ cancels this cutoff-dependent part when $C(\Lambda)=-I_\Lambda/2+C_{\rm finite}$. This sign follows from $\partial_x^2\leftrightarrow-k^2$; the finite coefficient describes unresolved short-scale dynamics.

= Effective sound-speed counterterm in large-scale structure
{title2=$-2c_{\rm eff}^2k^2P(k)$}
{parent=Effective field theory of large-scale structure}

The leading deterministic power-spectrum counterterm in the effective field theory of large-scale structure is proportional to $-2c_{\rm eff}^2k^2P(k)$. Its coefficient depends on the coarse-graining convention and runs so that it cancels the cutoff dependence of the one-loop $P_{13}$ contribution.

= Ultraviolet sensitivity of the one-loop matter power spectrum
{parent=Effective field theory of large-scale structure}

For internal momentum $q\gg k$, the <P22 contribution to the one-loop matter power spectrum> starts at $k^4$, while twice the <P13 contribution to the one-loop matter power spectrum> starts at $k^2P(k)$. The latter has the same momentum dependence as the <effective sound-speed counterterm in large-scale structure>. Short-scale stochastic contributions start at $k^4$ by mass and <momentum conservation>.

= Bias expansion
{parent=Effective field theory of large-scale structure}

A bias expansion expresses a tracer density, such as a galaxy or halo density, as a renormalized sum of local and nonlocal operators built from matter density, tidal fields, derivatives, and stochastic fields. Its coefficients encode unresolved tracer formation physics.

= Galaxy bias
{title2=$b$}
{parent=Bias expansion}
{wiki}

Galaxy bias describes how fluctuations in galaxy number density differ from fluctuations in the underlying matter density. On sufficiently large scales, the leading deterministic relation is $\delta_g=b_1\delta_m$.

= Lagrangian-to-Eulerian linear bias relation
{parent=Bias expansion}

Number conservation under the displacement from Lagrangian coordinates to Eulerian coordinates gives
$$
\boxed{b_1^{(E)}=1+b_1^{(L)}}.
$$
The added one is the linear matter-density response of the coordinate Jacobian.

= Local quadratic galaxy bias
{parent=Bias expansion}

The simplest nonlinear local bias model is
$$
\delta_g=b_1\delta+\frac{b_2}{2}(\delta^2-\langle\delta^2\rangle).
$$
For Gaussian matter fluctuations, its leading galaxy three-point function is proportional to $b_1^2b_2$.

= Galaxy bispectrum
{title2=$B_g(k_1,k_2,k_3)$}
{parent=Local quadratic galaxy bias}

The galaxy bispectrum is the connected momentum-space three-point function of the galaxy density contrast after removing its momentum-conserving delta function.

= Dark matter halo
{parent=Large-scale structure of the universe}
{wiki}

= Dark-matter haloes
{synonym}

A dark-matter halo is a gravitationally bound concentration of dark matter that hosts galaxies and larger structures.

= Halo abundance mass-budget bound
{parent=Dark matter halo}
{title2=$\int M\,n_h(M)\,dM\leq\bar\rho_{m,0}$}

A population of distinct, nonoverlapping <dark-matter halos> cannot contain more total mass per <comoving volume> than the cosmic mean matter density. A narrow mass population therefore obeys $n_cM_h\leq\bar\rho_{m,0}$. Do not sum subhalo masses and their inclusive host masses in the same bound, because that double-counts material. This check can reveal incompatible inferred absorption incidence, object size and halo-mass assumptions.

= Virial radius of a dark-matter halo
{title2=$r_v$}
{parent=Dark matter halo}

The virial radius of a dark-matter halo is conventionally the radius within which the mean density is a chosen overdensity $v$ times a reference critical or matter density.

= Halo density estimate of formation redshift
{parent=Virial radius of a dark-matter halo}
{title2=$1+z_f\simeq[\rho_h/(\Delta_{\rm vir}\bar\rho_{m,0})]^{1/3}$}

If a halo retains the mean density acquired at collapse, $3M/(4\pi R^3)=\Delta_{\rm vir}\bar\rho_{m,0}(1+z_f)^3$ estimates its formation redshift. In the non-dissipative <spherical-collapse model> during matter domination, $\Delta_{\rm vir}=18\pi^2$. Subsequent mass growth, profile evolution and the reference-density convention limit this inference.

= Virial velocity of a spherical-overdensity halo
{parent=Virial radius of a dark-matter halo}
{title2=$v_{\rm vir}=H(z)R_h\sqrt{\Delta_v/2}$}

= Virial velocity
{synonym}

For halo mean density $\Delta_v$ times the <critical density> at its epoch, the circular virial velocity obeys $v_{\rm vir}^2=GM_h/R_h=(\Delta_v/2)H^2R_h^2$. Thus $v_{\rm vir}=HR_h\sqrt{\Delta_v/2}$. Specifying whether overdensity is relative to mean matter or <critical density> is necessary for numerical inference.

= Halo virial-velocity conversion between epochs
{parent=Virial velocity of a spherical-overdensity halo}
{title2=$v_2/v_1=[\Delta_{c,2}H_2^2/(\Delta_{c,1}H_1^2)]^{1/6}$}

For a fixed mass under a spherical-overdensity definition, $R_h\propto(\Delta_cH^2)^{-1/3}$ and $v_h^2=GM_h/R_h$. Thus equivalent virial mass scales at two epochs have the displayed velocity ratio. With fixed overdensity during matter domination, $v_h\propto(1+z)^{1/2}$. This converts an epoch-specific mass–velocity calibration; it does not require a previously collapsed bound halo to physically expand with the later background density.

= Virial mass of a dark-matter halo
{title2=$M_v$}
{parent=Virial radius of a dark-matter halo}

If the virial radius is defined relative to critical density $\rho_c$, the virial mass is
$$
M_v=\frac{4\pi}{3}v\rho_cr_v^3.
$$

= Concentration of a dark-matter halo
{title2=$c$}
{parent=Dark matter halo}

= Halo concentration
{synonym}

The concentration of a spherical dark-matter halo compares its outer radius with an inner scale radius. For a <Navarro--Frenk--White profile>, $c=r_v/r_s$.

= Mass-concentration relation of dark-matter haloes
{parent=Concentration of a dark-matter halo}

The mass-concentration relation describes the statistical dependence of halo concentration on virial mass and epoch. In hierarchical cold-dark-matter cosmologies, lower-mass haloes generally formed earlier against a denser background and are therefore more concentrated at fixed epoch.

= Navarro--Frenk--White profile
{title2=NFW profile}
{c}
{parent=Dark matter halo}
{wiki}

= NFW profile
{c}
{synonym}

The Navarro--Frenk--White profile is
$$
\rho(r)=\frac{\rho_s}{(r/r_s)(1+r/r_s)^2}.
$$
It has an inner $r^{-1}$ density cusp and an outer $r^{-3}$ tail.

= NFW mass and circular-speed profile
{parent=Navarro--Frenk--White profile}
{c}
{title2=$M(r)=4\pi\rho_s r_s^3[\log(1+x)-x/(1+x)]$}

For an <NFW profile>, $x=r/r_s$ and $v_c^2=GM(r)/r$. The <circular speed> rises as $r^{1/2}$ at small radius and eventually decreases as $[\log(r/r_s)/r]^{1/2}$. Its maximum occurs at $x\simeq2.16258$, with $v_{c,\max}^2\simeq2.71706G\rho_s r_s^2$.

= Scale radius of a Navarro--Frenk--White profile
{title2=$r_s$}
{parent=Navarro--Frenk--White profile}

The NFW scale radius is where the logarithmic density slope equals $-2$.

= Characteristic density of a Navarro--Frenk--White profile
{title2=$\rho_s$}
{parent=Navarro--Frenk--White profile}

The NFW characteristic density fixes the normalization of the halo profile at a given scale radius.

= Cusp--core problem
{parent=Navarro--Frenk--White profile}
{wiki}

The cusp--core problem is the tension between the central cusps of collisionless cold-dark-matter halo profiles and the approximately constant-density cores inferred for many dwarf and low-surface-brightness galaxies.

= Dark-matter halo merger
{parent=Dark matter halo}

A dark-matter halo merger joins previously distinct <dark-matter halos>. Orbital <angular momentum> can become internal halo spin, while escaping material carries away some mass and <angular momentum>. Halo mergers and accretion are important reasons that early <tidal torque theory> is only an approximate predictor of the spin of a later virialized remnant.

= Top-hat filter
{parent=Dark matter halo}
{wiki}

A top-hat filter averages a field with uniform weight inside a ball and zero weight outside it.

= Spherical-collapse model
{parent=Dark matter halo}

= Spherical collapse
{synonym}

The spherical-collapse model applies a spherical <top-hat filter> to an overdense region and predicts collapse when its linearly extrapolated smoothed overdensity exceeds the threshold $\delta_c\simeq1.686$ in an Einstein-de Sitter universe.

= Einstein-de Sitter virial overdensity
{c}
{parent=Spherical-collapse model}
{title2=$\rho_{\rm vir}/\bar\rho(t_{\rm coll})=18\pi^2$}

For ideal isolated <spherical collapse>, energy conservation and the <virial theorem> give $R_{\rm vir}=R_{\rm ta}/2$ and $\rho_{\rm vir}=8\rho_{\rm ta}$ if the density-profile shape is unchanged. The cycloid gives $t_{\rm coll}=2t_{\rm ta}$ and $\rho_{\rm ta}/\bar\rho(t_{\rm ta})=9\pi^2/16$. Since the <Einstein-de Sitter universe> has $\bar\rho\propto t^{-2}$, the overdensity at the nominal collapse time is $32(9\pi^2/16)=18\pi^2$. Hence $\rho_{\rm vir}=18\pi^2\rho_0(1+z_{\rm coll})^3$. The collapse time is that of the formal cold solution; actual virialization replaces its point collapse.

= Dimensionless spherical-collapse time normalization
{parent=Spherical-collapse model}
{title2=$\tau\sim(8/9\pi)y^{3/2}$}

For <spherical collapse> in an <Einstein-de Sitter universe>, take $y=R/R_{\rm ta}$ and $\tau=H_{\rm ta}t$, with $\Delta_{\rm ta}=(3\pi/4)^2$. The expanding branch has $\tau=\Delta_{\rm ta}^{-1/2}\int_0^y[u/(1-u)]^{1/2}\,du$. Its small-radius expansion is $\tau=(8/9\pi)y^{3/2}(1+3y/10+O(y^2))$, and its turnaround time is $2/3$. These constants are linked: using another leading coefficient without changing the time normalization no longer gives a <density contrast> approaching zero at early times. The collapsing branch is $4/3-\tau_{\rm expand}(y)$.

= Virial radius from turnaround energy
{parent=Spherical-collapse model}
{title2=$R_{\rm vir}=R_{\rm ta}/2$}

For a homologous, isolated collapsing sphere, write its <Newtonian gravitational potential energy> as $U=-aGM^2/R$. At <spherical-collapse turnaround> the coherent <kinetic energy> vanishes. Conserving total energy and applying the <virial theorem> to the final equilibrium gives $U_{\rm ta}=U_{\rm vir}/2$ and hence $R_{\rm vir}=R_{\rm ta}/2$. If the gravitational structure coefficient changes, the ratio is $a_{\rm vir}/(2a_{\rm ta})$ instead. Mass loss, surface pressure, external tides and energy exchange also change this estimate.

= Turnaround of spherical collapse
{parent=Spherical-collapse model}
{title2=$\theta_{\rm ta}=\pi$}

= Spherical-collapse turnaround
{synonym}

A pressureless bound spherical shell first expands, reaches $\dot R=0$ at its maximum radius, and recollapses. In the cycloidal <spherical-collapse model>, turnaround occurs at $\theta=\pi$, with $R_{\max}=2A$ and $t_{\max}=\pi B$. Its mean density is $9\pi^2/16$ times the background density in an <Einstein-de Sitter universe>.

= Uniform-sphere collapse with a cosmological constant
{parent=Spherical-collapse model}

For fixed mass $M$, a uniform sphere of physical radius $R$ has $W_G=-3GM^2/(5R)$ and $W_\Lambda=-\Lambda MR^2/10$, using the convention in which its outward acceleration is $\Lambda r/3$. The <virial theorem> reads $2T+W_G=2W_\Lambda$. Defining $x=R_f/R_{\rm ta}$ and $\eta=\Lambda/(4\pi G\rho_{\rm ta})$, <conservation of energy> gives $2\eta x^3-(2+\eta)x+1=0$. The collapsing branch has $x=1/2-\eta/8+O(\eta^2)$. For small positive <cosmological constant>, it is more compact at fixed turnaround data, even though the outward force inhibits turnaround and collapse.

= Turnaround bound with positive cosmological constant
{parent=Uniform-sphere collapse with a cosmological constant}
{title2=$\eta=\Lambda/(4\pi G\rho_m)<1$}

A fixed-mass spherical shell has acceleration $\ddot R=-GM/R^2+\Lambda R/3$, in units $c=1$. At a finite maximum radius $R_m$, inward acceleration requires $\eta=\Lambda R_m^3/(3GM)<1$. Equality is the unstable static radius, approached only in the limiting separatrix case rather than a finite expansion-to-collapse turnaround. The effective potential reaches its maximum there, so an initially expanding bound inner-branch shell also needs energy below that maximum.

= Minimum virial-radius ratio with positive cosmological constant
{parent=Turnaround bound with positive cosmological constant}
{title2=$\inf(R_v/R_m)=(\sqrt3-1)/2$}

For an isolated uniform sphere with fixed positive <cosmological constant>, energy conservation and the <virial theorem> give $2\eta x^3-(2+\eta)x+1=0$, $x=R_v/R_m$. The physical root starts at $x=1/2$ when $\eta=0$ and decreases for $0<\eta<1$. At the limiting value $\eta=1$, the cubic factors as $(x-1)(2x^2+2x-1)$; the collapsing branch approaches $(\sqrt3-1)/2$. Finite turnaround therefore gives a strict lower bound, whose infimum is approached rather than attained. Compared with zero vacuum energy at the same turnaround data, the negative vacuum potential and its virial contribution require stronger final gravitational binding.

= Linear spherical-collapse threshold
{title2=$\delta_c$}
{parent=Spherical-collapse model}

In an Einstein-de Sitter universe, extrapolating the linear growing mode to the time when the spherical top-hat solution collapses gives
$$
\delta_c=\frac3{20}(12\pi)^{2/3}\simeq1.686.
$$

= Present-extrapolated spherical-collapse barrier
{parent=Linear spherical-collapse threshold}
{title2=$\delta_c(z)/\sigma(M,0)=\delta_{\rm sc}/\sigma(M,z)$}

With linear growth normalized to unity today, a collapse threshold $\delta_{\rm sc}$ at epoch $z$ corresponds to a present-extrapolated initial barrier $\delta_c(z)=\delta_{\rm sc}/D(z)$. Comparing this barrier to present variance is equivalent to comparing $\delta_{\rm sc}$ to variance evolved to the collapse epoch. Mixing the two conventions counts growth twice.

= Halo peak height
{title2=$\nu$}
{parent=Spherical-collapse model}

The peak height of a halo of mass $M$ is $\nu=\delta_c/\sigma(M)$, where $\sigma^2(M)$ is the variance of the linear density field smoothed on the corresponding mass scale.

= Press-Schechter formalism
{c}
{parent=Spherical-collapse model}
{wiki=Press–Schechter_formalism}

The Press-Schechter formalism estimates the halo abundance from the Gaussian probability that a smoothed linear overdensity crosses the spherical-collapse threshold, with a factor of two enforcing total mass conservation.

= Excursion-set description of halo formation
{parent=Press-Schechter formalism}

A halo is associated with the first upcrossing of the <linear spherical-collapse threshold> by the smoothed <density contrast> as its variance increases. With a <sharp-k smoothing filter>, the <Brownian reflection principle> makes the crossing probability twice the one-scale Gaussian upper tail. This handles trajectories that crossed earlier but finish below the barrier and leads to the <Press-Schechter halo mass function>.

= Halo first crossing with a finite variance cutoff
{parent=Excursion-set description of halo formation}

A small-scale spectral cutoff bounds the smoothing variance by $S_{\max}$. A sharp-k excursion remains Brownian up to this terminal variance, with total crossed fraction $\operatorname{erfc}(\delta_c/\sqrt{2S_{\max}})<1$. The ordinary first-crossing density remains valid before the cutoff. Below the sharp-filter cutoff radius the variance is constant, so there are no new crossings and the idealized differential halo abundance vanishes. The missing fraction need not be artificially renormalized to one.

= Sharp-k smoothing filter
{title2=$\widetilde W(kR)=\mathbf1_{k<1/R}$}
{parent=Press-Schechter formalism}

= Sharp Fourier-space filter
{synonym}

A sharp-k filter includes every Fourier mode below a cutoff with unit weight and excludes all modes above it. For a <Gaussian random field>, increasing the cutoff adds independent Fourier shells, so the smoothed <density contrast> is a <Brownian motion> when parameterized by its variance. The real-space window is not a localized spherical top-hat, so its halo mass assignment needs a specified convention.

= Press-Schechter halo mass function
{c}
{parent=Press-Schechter formalism}

The Press-Schechter differential abundance is
$$
\frac{dn}{dM}
=-\sqrt{\frac2\pi}\frac{\bar\rho}{M^2}
\nu e^{-\nu^2/2}\frac{d\log\sigma}{d\log M}.
$$

= Cluster temperature function normalization
{parent=Press-Schechter halo mass function}
{title2=$k_BT\propto M^{2/3}[\Delta_v\rho_c(z)]^{1/3}$}

The <virial theorem> and <hydrostatic equilibrium> relate a cluster's gas temperature to $GM/R$, while $M=(4\pi/3)\Delta_v\rho_cR^3$. A calibrated mass-temperature relation converts the <Press-Schechter halo mass function> into $dn/dT=(dn/dM)|dM/dT|$. Its exponential rare-cluster tail is sensitive to the <density contrast> variance on cluster scales, hence to the power-spectrum amplitude, often parameterized by $\sigma_8$. Mass calibration, temperature scatter, nonthermal pressure, sample selection and the matter density must be included rather than treating temperature counts as an independent measurement of amplitude alone.

= Scale-free characteristic mass in the Press-Schechter formalism
{parent=Press-Schechter halo mass function}
{title2=$M_*\propto D^{1/s},\quad \sigma(M)\propto M^{-s}$}

If the linearly extrapolated rms amplitude scales as $\sigma(M)\propto M^{-s}$, a fixed peak-height cutoff in the <Press-Schechter halo mass function> has $M_*\propto D^{1/s}$, where $D$ is the <linear growth factor>. The differential prefactor scales as $M^{s-2}$ and the exponential argument as $M^{2s}$. The cutoff convention must be stated when fixing its absolute normalization: $\nu=1$ and $\nu^2/2=1$ select different masses, although their redshift scaling is the same.

= Halo spacing from a differential mass function
{parent=Press-Schechter halo mass function}
{title2=$\bar l=[Mn(M)]^{-1/3}$}

For comoving differential number density $n(M)=dn/dM$, the abundance in an order-unity logarithmic mass bin is approximately $Mn(M)$. A characteristic mean spacing is therefore $[Mn(M)]^{-1/3}$. A cumulative mass fraction cannot replace this number directly; cumulative halo counts require integrating $n(M)$ over mass.

= Press-Schechter abundance growth at fixed mass
{c}
{parent=Press-Schechter halo mass function}
{title2=$\partial_t\ln n\big|_M=(\nu^2-1)\dot D/D$}

For a fixed narrow mass bin with time-independent smoothing slope, the <Press-Schechter halo mass function> has time dependence $n\propto\nu e^{-\nu^2/2}$, with <halo peak height> $\nu\propto D^{-1}$. Differentiating gives the displayed rate. It is positive only for $\nu>1$; the bin can lose objects to higher masses later. Cumulative counts require integrating the differential abundance, and a moving mass boundary adds a selection term.

= Two abundance crossings of the Press-Schechter mass function
{parent=Press-Schechter abundance growth at fixed mass}
{title2=$\mathcal N_{\ln M}\propto\nu e^{-\nu^2/2}$}

At fixed mass, the <Press-Schechter halo mass function> has abundance per logarithmic interval proportional to $\nu e^{-\nu^2/2}$. This increases as $\nu$ falls from infinity to one and then decreases as $\nu$ falls toward zero, reflecting incorporation of small <dark-matter haloes> into larger ones. A target below the maximum therefore has two mathematical crossings if the <linear growth factor> can increase without limit. The large-$\nu$ crossing describes first appearance; the small-$\nu$ crossing is late depletion. Late vacuum domination limits growth, so a formal future crossing need not be reached physically.

= Peak-height calibration from an exponential mass-function cutoff
{parent=Press-Schechter halo mass function}
{title2=$\sigma(M,z)=\frac{\delta_{\rm sc}}{\sqrt2}(M/M_{h,\rm ch})^{-\beta}$}

Defining characteristic mass by an exponential factor $\exp[-(M/M_{h,\rm ch})^{2\beta}]$ fixes its peak height to $\nu=\sqrt2$, rather than one. Hence $\sigma(M_{h,\rm ch},z)=\delta_{\rm sc}/\sqrt2$ and the variance at other masses follows the same power-law scaling. The precise characteristic-mass convention determines this factor.

= Scale-free halo cutoff normalization
{parent=Peak-height calibration from an exponential mass-function cutoff}
{title2=$A=2q\bar\rho_{m,0}M_*^{-q}/\sqrt\pi$}

For $\sigma(M,0)=SM^{-q}$ with $q=(n+3)/6>0$, matching the <Press-Schechter halo mass function> to $AM^{q-2}\exp[-(M/M_*)^{2q}]$ requires $\sigma(M_*,0)=\delta_c(z)/\sqrt2$. The comoving normalization is $A=2q\bar\rho_{m,0}M_*^{-q}/\sqrt\pi$. Consequently both $A$ and $M_*$ evolve with the <linear growth factor>; $M_*\propto D^{1/q}$.

= Power-law Press-Schechter stellar mass function
{parent=Press-Schechter halo mass function}
{title2=$n_*(M_*)\propto M_*^{\beta-2}e^{-(M_*/M_{*,\rm ch})^{2\beta}}$}

For a power-law matter spectrum, smoothed variance scales as $\sigma\propto M^{-\beta}$ with $\beta=(n+3)/6$. Differentiating the Press-Schechter collapsed fraction gives a halo mass function proportional to $M^{\beta-2}\exp[-(M/M_{h,\rm ch})^{2\beta}]$. A constant <stellar-to-halo mass ratio> preserves both exponents and rescales characteristic mass and normalization.

= Peak-background split
{parent=Dark matter halo}
{wiki}

The peak-background split decomposes density fluctuations into long- and short-wavelength parts. A long overdensity lowers the effective local collapse threshold and thereby changes the abundance of halos.

= Scale-dependent halo bias from local non-Gaussianity
{parent=Peak-background split}
{title2=$\Delta b(k)=2f_{\rm NL}\delta_c b_L/\alpha(k)$}

For a universal abundance depending on <halo peak height>, the <peak-background split> gives a potential-response coefficient $b_\varphi=2f_{\rm NL}\delta_c b_L$, with $b_L$ the <linear Lagrangian halo bias>. If $\delta=\alpha(k)\varphi$, the corresponding deterministic <galaxy bias> correction is $\Delta b=b_\varphi/\alpha(k)$, proportional to $k^{-2}$ on sufficiently large scales. For a physical Eulerian tracer, $b_E=1+b_L$, so $b_\varphi=2f_{\rm NL}\delta_c(b_E-1)$. The deterministic tracer <power spectrum> is $(b_E+\Delta b)^2P_m$; it remains nonnegative even if a negative correction changes the sign of the tracer-matter correlation.

= Linear Lagrangian halo bias
{title2=$b_L(M)$}
{parent=Peak-background split}

Linear Lagrangian halo bias is the fractional response of halo abundance to a long-wavelength overdensity in the initial coordinates. For Press-Schechter halos, $b_L=(\nu^2-1)/\delta_c$.

= Linear Eulerian halo bias
{title2=$b(M)$}
{parent=Linear Lagrangian halo bias}

Mapping matter and halos from their initial positions adds the matter displacement contribution, giving $b=1+b_L$. Mass conservation requires the mass-weighted consistency relation $\int(dn/dM)(M/\bar\rho)b(M)dM=1$.

= Halo model
{parent=Dark matter halo}
{wiki}

The halo model represents the matter density as a sum of normalized halo profiles and decomposes its power spectrum into same-halo and distinct-halo contributions.

= One-halo term
{title2=$P_{1h}$}
{parent=Halo model}

The one-halo term comes from pairs of mass elements in the same halo:
$$
P_{1h}(k)=\int dM\,\frac{dn}{dM}
\left(\frac{M}{\bar\rho}\right)^2|\widetilde u(k|M)|^2.
$$

= Two-halo term
{title2=$P_{2h}$}
{parent=Halo model}

At linear order in halo correlations,
$$
P_{2h}(k)=
\left[\int dM\,\frac{dn}{dM}\frac{M}{\bar\rho}
b(M)\widetilde u(k|M)\right]^2P_{\rm lin}(k).
$$
