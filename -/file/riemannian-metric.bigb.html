= Riemannian metric
{title2=$g$}
{c}
{wiki}

A Riemannian metric is a smoothly varying <positive-definite> <inner product> $g_p$ on each <tangent space> $T_pM$ of a <smooth manifold>. It measures <tangent vectors> before measuring entire journeys:
$$
\|v\|_g=\sqrt{g_p(v,v)},\qquad
\cos\theta=\frac{g_p(u,v)}{\|u\|_g\|v\|_g}\quad(u,v\ne0).
$$
Integrating this speed along a <curve> gives <arc length>; taking the <infimum> over routes gives <Riemannian distance>. A <smooth manifold> together with this measuring rule is a <Riemannian manifold>.

In a <coordinate chart> $(x^1,\ldots,x^n)$, the rule is represented by a smooth <positive-definite symmetric matrix> $G=(g_{ij})$:
$$
ds^2=\sum_{i,j}g_{ij}(x)\,dx^i\,dx^j,\qquad
g(v,v)=\sum_{i,j}g_{ij}(x)v^iv^j.
$$
The notation $ds^2$ describes the squared infinitesimal line element, rather than an ordinary distance between distinct points. Changing coordinates changes the <matrix> entries; it preserves the measured <norm> of the same <tangent vector>.

For the plane with $ds^2=4dx^2+dy^2$, the <vector> $(1,0)$ has <norm> $2$ and $(0,1)$ has <norm> $1$. The coordinate change $X=2x,Y=y$ turns this into $ds^2=dX^2+dY^2$: coefficients that look different can describe the same geometry. On a <circular cylinder> the <induced metric> $R^2d\theta^2+dz^2$ similarly becomes Euclidean when unrolled. On a round <sphere>, polar coordinates give $R^2d\phi^2+R^2\sin^2\phi\,d\theta^2$, away from the poles; the apparent degeneracy at a pole belongs to this coordinate choice, not to the <Riemannian metric>.

The point of the construction is that measurements make sense intrinsically, even without a chosen surrounding <Euclidean space>. It gives a common starting point for <geodesics>, <curvature> encoded by the <Riemann curvature tensor>, and the <Laplace-Beltrami operator>. https://www.math.ucla.edu/~petersen/RG.pdf[Peter Petersen, Riemannian Geometry, §1.1: smoothly varying tangent-space inner products]{ref}.

= Rotationally symmetric surface metric
{parent=Riemannian metric}

An intrinsic two-dimensional metric locally written $ds^2=dr^2+f(r)^2d\phi^2$ with positive $f$. Rotation of the angular coordinate is an <isometry>. Such a metric need not have a global realization as a surface of revolution in three-dimensional Euclidean space.

= Cyclic angular momentum in a rotational metric
{parent=Rotationally symmetric surface metric}
{title2=$J=f(r)^2\dot\phi$}

For the affinely parametrized <geodesic Lagrangian> $\mathcal L=(\dot r^2+f(r)^2\dot\phi^2)/2$, the conserved cyclic momentum is $J=f(r)^2\dot\phi$. Its <Euler-Lagrange equations> are $\ddot r-ff^{\prime}\dot\phi^2=0$ and $\ddot\phi+2f^{\prime}\dot r\dot\phi/f=0$.

= Line element
{parent=Riemannian metric}
{title2=$ds^2=g_{ij}dx^idx^j$}

The line element of a <Riemannian metric> is its local expression for squared infinitesimal length. Along a parametrized <curve> $x(t)$, the <arc length> is $\int\sqrt{g_{ij}(x(t))\dot x^i\dot x^j}\,dt$. For a <circular cylinder> of radius $R$ parametrized by angle $\theta$ and axial position $z$, the <induced metric> gives $ds^2=R^2d\theta^2+dz^2$. Unrolling with $u=R\theta$ yields the Euclidean form $du^2+dz^2$, explaining the straight unrolled descriptions of <geodesics on a circular cylinder>.

= Line element on a sphere
{parent=Line element}
{title2=$ds^2=R^2(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)$}

In <spherical coordinates> on a <sphere> of radius $R$, the position is $X=R(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta)$. Its coordinate tangent <vectors> satisfy $X_\theta\cdot X_\phi=0$, $|X_\theta|^2=R^2$, and $|X_\phi|^2=R^2\sin^2\theta$. Expanding $|dX|^2$ proves the displayed <line element>, the <first fundamental form> of the sphere. Along a parametrized path it gives the <arc length> by integrating $R\sqrt{\dot\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2}$. The vanishing azimuthal coefficient at a pole is a <coordinate singularity>, not a singularity of the <sphere>.

= Riemannian orthonormal frame
{c}
{parent=Riemannian metric}
{title2=$g(X_i,X_j)=\delta_{ij}$}

A smooth local basis of <vector fields> whose <inner products> under a <Riemannian metric> are the identity matrix. Tracing the <Riemannian Hessian> in such a frame gives the <Laplace-Beltrami operator>, with the chosen sign.

= Normal orthonormal frame
{parent=Riemannian orthonormal frame}
{title2=$\nabla_{E_i}E_j(p)=0$}

At any point of a <Riemannian manifold>, choose <geodesic normal coordinates> and orthonormalize their coordinate fields smoothly. At the point, the metric is the identity and its first derivatives vanish, so the orthonormalization coefficients have zero first derivatives. The <Christoffel symbols> also vanish there. This gives a local <Riemannian orthonormal frame> with the displayed property, useful for invariant identities evaluated at one point.

= Nowhere locally homogeneous metric

= Bumpy metric in spectral geometry
{synonym}

A smooth <Riemannian metric> with no nonidentity <local isometry> between open subsets. Equivalently distinct nonempty open subsets never have isometric induced metrics. This strong local rigidity is also called bumpy in some spectral-geometry treatments; it is distinct from the closed-geodesic nondegeneracy convention for bumpy metrics.

= Sunada local isometry lemma
{c}
{parent=Nowhere locally homogeneous metric}

= Sunada's local isometry lemma
{c}
{synonym}

On a compact smooth manifold without boundary of dimension at least two, a <residual set> of smooth <Riemannian metrics> consists of <nowhere locally homogeneous metrics>. Thus such metrics are dense in the smooth topology by the <Baire category theorem>. In dimension one, arclength coordinates always provide nontrivial <local isometries>.

= Local flattening of a Riemannian metric
{parent=Riemannian metric}
{title2=$\widetilde g=(1-\chi)g+\chi\phi^*\delta$}

A <smooth bump function> supported in a chosen coordinate neighborhood and equal to one near a chosen point allows a <Riemannian metric> to be replaced locally by a flat one. The displayed convex combination stays positive definite and glues smoothly to the original metric outside the support. This is a local modification of the metric, not a claim that the original metric is flat.

= Riemannian gradient
{c}
{parent=Riemannian metric}
{title2=$\nabla_g f$}

For a smooth function $f$ on a <Riemannian manifold>, its Riemannian gradient is the unique <vector field> satisfying $g(\nabla_gf,V)=df(V)$ for every <vector field> $V$. It depends on the <Riemannian metric>, and its negative generates the downward <gradient flow>.

= Euclidean metric
{c}
{parent=Riemannian metric}
{title2=$\delta_{ij}dx^idx^j$}

The Euclidean metric is the constant <Riemannian metric> on $\mathbb R^n$ given in Cartesian coordinates by $g=\sum_i(dx^i)^2$. Its <Levi-Civita connection> has zero <Christoffel symbols> in those coordinates, so it is flat and its geodesics are straight lines. The <Euclidean norm> is the pointwise length determined by this metric.

= Product Riemannian metric
{parent=Riemannian metric}
{title2=$g_1\oplus g_2$}

The product of <Riemannian manifolds> has metric
$$
g=p_1^*g_1+p_2^*g_2,\qquad g((u_1,u_2),(v_1,v_2))=g_1(u_1,v_1)+g_2(u_2,v_2).
$$
The tangent summands are orthogonal, and positivity follows because a nonzero product tangent vector has a nonzero component. Its <Levi-Civita connection> is the <product affine connection> of the two factor <Levi-Civita connections>: mixed torsion is zero and metric compatibility follows from independence of the two coordinate sets.

= Metric duality between tangent and cotangent bundles
{synonym}

A <Riemannian metric> gives inverse <vector bundle isomorphisms>
$$
\flat_g:TM\to T^*M,\quad v\mapsto g(v,\cdot),\qquad
\sharp_g:T^*M\to TM.
$$
In coordinates they have <matrices> $g_{ij}$ and $g^{ij}$. Every <smooth manifold> admits a <Riemannian metric> by a <partition of unity>, so its tangent and <cotangent bundles> are isomorphic as real smooth <vector bundles>. This isomorphism depends on the metric, not merely on the manifold.

= Pullback of a Riemannian metric
{parent=Riemannian metric}
{title2=$f^*g$}

For a smooth map $f:M\to N$, the pullback of a <Riemannian metric> is $(f^*g)_p(u,v)=g_{f(p)}(df_pu,df_pv)$. It is positive definite precisely when $df_p$ is injective at every point, that is, when $f$ is an <immersion>. In coordinates, $(f^*g)_{ij}=g_{ab}(f(y))\partial_i f^a\partial_j f^b$.

= Conformal rescaling of a Riemannian metric
{title2=$\widetilde g=e^{2\omega}g$}
{parent=Riemannian metric}

A conformal rescaling multiplies a Riemannian metric by a positive scalar function. It preserves angles while changing lengths and volumes; in two dimensions, $\widetilde g=e^{2\omega}g$ gives $\Delta_{\widetilde g}=e^{-2\omega}\Delta_g$ on scalar functions.

= Conformal class of a Riemannian metric
{parent=Conformal rescaling of a Riemannian metric}
{title2=$[g]=\{e^{2\varphi}g:\varphi\in C^\infty(M,\mathbb R)\}$}

= Conformal class
{synonym}

Two <Riemannian metrics> lie in the same conformal class when one is a positive smooth scalar multiple of the other. They measure the same <angles>, while <lengths> may change from point to point. On an oriented <Riemannian surface>, rotation of each <tangent plane> through $\pi/2$ determines a <complex structure> which depends only on this conformal class.

= Levi-Civita connection under conformal rescaling
{c}
{parent=Conformal rescaling of a Riemannian metric}
{title2=$\widetilde\nabla_XY=\nabla_XY+d\sigma(X)Y+d\sigma(Y)X-g(X,Y)\operatorname{grad}_g\sigma$}

For $\widetilde g=e^{2\sigma}g$, the displayed correction is a symmetric tensor, so adding it to $\nabla$ gives a <torsion-free connection>. The two extra metric-compatibility pairings sum to $2d\sigma(X)g(Y,Z)$; this is exactly the derivative of the conformal factor. Uniqueness of the <Levi-Civita connection> proves the formula. It also yields the <critical-point criterion for equal conformal connections>.

= Critical-point criterion for equal conformal connections
{parent=Levi-Civita connection under conformal rescaling}
{title2=$\widetilde\nabla_p=\nabla_p\Longleftrightarrow(d\sigma)_p=0$}

On a positive-dimensional <Riemannian manifold>, equality means equality of the two covariant derivatives at $p$ for every pair of vector fields. A <critical point> of $\sigma$ annihilates the conformal correction. Conversely, writing that correction as $A$, the identity $g(A(v,v),v)=g(v,v)d\sigma(v)$ for all tangent vectors forces $d\sigma=0$ if $A=0$. A smooth function on a nonempty compact boundaryless manifold has a critical point, so conformally related connections coincide somewhere. Compact manifolds with boundary need not have one.

= Scalar curvature under conformal rescaling
{parent=Conformal rescaling of a Riemannian metric}
{title2=$\widetilde R=e^{-2\omega}[R-2(N-1)\Delta_g\omega-(N-1)(N-2)|\nabla\omega|_g^2]$}

In dimension $N$, the <conformal rescaling of a Riemannian metric> $\widetilde g=e^{2\omega}g$ changes the <Ricci scalar> by the displayed formula. In two dimensions the gradient-square term vanishes; for a flat base metric, $\widetilde R=-2e^{-2\omega}\Delta\omega$.

= Riemannian volume form
{title2=$d\operatorname{vol}_g$}
{c}
{parent=Riemannian metric}

On an oriented Riemannian $n$-manifold, the volume form is the unique positive $n$-form taking value one on every positively oriented orthonormal frame. In positive local coordinates,
$$
d\operatorname{vol}_g=\sqrt{\det(g_{ij})}\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n.
$$

= Riemannian volume
{c}
{parent=Riemannian volume form}
{title2=$\operatorname{Vol}_g(M)=\int_MdV_g$}

The total Riemannian volume is the integral of the <Riemannian volume form>, or its density on a nonorientable manifold. In local coordinates the density is $\sqrt{\det g}\,dx^1\cdots dx^d$. It is preserved by <Riemannian isometries>. On a closed manifold it is the leading coefficient among the integrated <heat invariants>, and on a surface it is the <surface area>.

= Coordinate invariance of the Riemannian volume form
{parent=Riemannian volume form}
{title2=$\sqrt{\det g_x}\,dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n=\sqrt{\det g_y}\,dy^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dy^n$}

For positive coordinate charts put $J=\partial y/\partial x$. The <metric tensor> transforms by $g_x=J^Tg_yJ$, giving $\det g_x=(\det J)^2\det g_y$. The top <wedge product of differential forms> transforms by $dy^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dy^n=(\det J)dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n$. Since $\det J>0$, the two factors match. Thus the local expressions define one global <Riemannian volume form>.

= Interior contraction of the Riemannian volume form
{parent=Riemannian volume form}
{title2=$\iota_X\omega_g=\sum_i(-1)^{i-1}f_i\theta_i$}

In a positive <Riemannian orthonormal frame> $X=\sum_i f_iE_i$ with dual coframe $\omega_i$, the <Riemannian volume form> is $\omega_1\wedge\cdots\wedge\omega_n$. Inserting $E_i$ in its first slot omits $\omega_i$ and produces sign $(-1)^{i-1}$, giving the displayed <interior product> formula.

= Exterior derivative of contracted volume form
{parent=Interior contraction of the Riemannian volume form}
{title2=$d(\iota_X\omega_g)=(\operatorname{div}X)\omega_g$}

At a point in a <normal orthonormal frame>, torsion-freeness makes the frame brackets vanish there, hence the dual one-forms have zero <exterior derivative> there. Differentiating the <interior product> formula then leaves only $\sum_iE_i(f_i)\omega_g$, the <divergence of a Riemannian vector field>. Since the point is arbitrary, the identity holds globally.

= Integration by parts for Riemannian divergence
{parent=Exterior derivative of contracted volume form}
{title2=$\int_M X(f)\omega_g=-\int_M f\operatorname{div}X\,\omega_g$}

On a compact oriented <Riemannian manifold> without boundary, the <generalized Stokes theorem> applied to $f\iota_X\omega_g$ gives the displayed identity. The product expansion is $d(f\iota_X\omega_g)=[X(f)+f\operatorname{div}X]\omega_g$. With boundary, the omitted boundary integral is $\int_{\partial M}f\iota_X\omega_g$.

= Parallelism of the Riemannian volume form
{parent=Riemannian volume form}
{title2=$\nabla\omega_g=0$}

The <Levi-Civita connection> of an oriented <Riemannian manifold> preserves its <Riemannian volume form>. In a positively oriented orthonormal frame its connection matrix is skew-symmetric. Differentiating the wedge of the dual coframe gives minus the trace of that matrix times the <volume form>, which is zero. Equivalently <parallel transport> preserves the metric and orientation, so it preserves the normalized <volume form>.

= Localized volume perturbation
{parent=Riemannian volume form}
{title2=$dV_{(1+t\eta)g}=(1+t\eta)^{m/2}dV_g$}

For a nonnegative smooth <bump function> supported in an open set, multiplying a <Riemannian metric> by $1+t\eta$ increases volume there for $t>0$ while leaving the metric elsewhere fixed. For distinct regular compact domains a perturbation supported in the interior of one outside the other breaks equal volume, and therefore breaks any <isometry> between the domains. The perturbations tend to the original metric in the smooth topology.

= Volume of a Euclidean unit sphere
{title2=$\operatorname{vol}(S^{n-1})=n\operatorname{vol}(D^n)$}
{parent=Riemannian volume form}

The $(n-1)$-dimensional <volume> of the Euclidean unit sphere equals $n$ times the $n$-dimensional volume of its unit ball. If $R=\sum_i x^i\partial_i$ is the radial vector field and $\omega=dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^n$, then $d(\iota_R\omega)=n\omega$, and the <generalized Stokes theorem> proves the formula.

= Parallel differential form
{title2=$\nabla\alpha=0$}
{parent=Riemannian volume form}

A differential form is parallel when its <covariant derivative> vanishes. Parallel transport then preserves it, and every parallel form is harmonic.

The form is preserved by <parallel transport>; this is the alternating-tensor case of vanishing <covariant derivative>.

= Dirichlet energy on a Riemannian manifold
{c}
{title2=$E(u)$}
{parent=Riemannian volume form}

For a function $u$ on a compact Riemannian manifold, its Dirichlet energy is $\frac12\int_M|\nabla u|_g^2d\operatorname{vol}_g$. Adding a linear source term $-\int_Mfu\,d\operatorname{vol}_g$ gives Euler-Lagrange equation $-\Delta_gu=f$.

For a scalar function $u$ on a <Riemannian manifold>, the <Dirichlet energy> is $E(u)=\tfrac12\int_M|\nabla u|_g^2\,d\mathrm{vol}_g$.

= Dirichlet energy of a map
{c}
{parent=Dirichlet energy on a Riemannian manifold}
{title2=$E(u)=\frac12\int|du|^2$}

For a smooth map between <Riemannian manifolds>, its Dirichlet energy is half the integral of the squared <norm> of its differential, using the source and target metrics. When the source is two-dimensional, a conformal change of its metric leaves this energy unchanged. For a <J-holomorphic curve> into a <symplectic manifold> with a <compatible almost complex structure>, the <energy identity for a J-holomorphic curve> identifies it with the pulled-back <symplectic area>. The norm of a differential here is the full tensor norm, summing its squared values on an orthonormal source frame.
