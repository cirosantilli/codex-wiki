= First law of thermodynamics
{title2=$dU=\delta Q-\delta W$}
{wiki}

The first law applies <conservation of energy> to a thermodynamic system. For a <closed system> with unchanged bulk <kinetic energy> and <potential energy>, write
$$
dU=\delta Q-\delta W,\qquad \Delta U=Q-W.
$$
Here $U$ is <internal energy>, $Q>0$ is <heat> entering the system and $W>0$ is <work> done by the system on its surroundings. Compression therefore gives negative $W$. Some references define <work> positive when done on the system; their plus sign represents the same balance with a different convention. If bulk <kinetic energy> or <potential energy> changes, include it in the total <energy> balance. <Thermodynamics and Propulsion>[MIT, First law and work sign convention]{ref}

Supplying $1000\,\mathrm J$ of <heat> while a gas does $300\,\mathrm J$ of <work> increases its <internal energy> by $700\,\mathrm J$. Compressing it with $200\,\mathrm J$ of input <work> in an <adiabatic process> instead gives $Q=0$, $W=-200\,\mathrm J$ and $\Delta U=200\,\mathrm J$.

The distinction between $d$ and $\delta$ records state versus path: <internal energy> is a <state function>, whereas <heat> and <work> depend on the process. For an <ideal gas> expanding reversibly at constant <temperature>, $W=Nk_BT\log(V_2/V_1)$ and $Q=W$, since <internal energy of an ideal gas> depends only on <temperature>. Choose $Nk_BT=1000\,\mathrm J$ and $V_2=2V_1$: then $Q=W=693.15\,\mathrm J$. An insulated <free expansion> into vacuum between the same equilibrium endpoints has $Q=W=0$. Both have $\Delta U=0$, yet their transfers differ. Their common <entropy> increase is $Nk_B\log2$. https://web.mit.edu/16.unified/www/FALL/thermodynamics/notes/node40.html[MIT, Ideal-gas entropy differences]{ref}

For a simple compressible equilibrium system of fixed composition, combining the laws gives
$$
dU=T\,dS-p\,dV.
$$
Here $p$ is <pressure> and $V$ is <volume>. This identity relates neighboring equilibrium states. Identifying $T\,dS$ with actual <heat> and $p\,dV$ with actual <work> requires a suitable <reversible thermodynamic process>; an irreversible piston calculation uses the external resisting <pressure> for boundary <work>. This distinction prevents applying a <reversible ideal-gas adiabat> to an <irreversible adiabatic piston compression>.

= Perpetual motion
{parent=First law of thermodynamics}
{wiki}

A proposed cyclic machine that continuously delivers useful <work> without the corresponding permitted <energy> input. A first-kind machine violates <conservation of energy>; a second-kind machine would turn heat from a single reservoir completely into work in a cycle, contrary to the <Kelvin-Planck statement>. Continuing ideal motion without withdrawing work is a different claim.

= Diabatic heating
{parent=First law of thermodynamics}
{title2=$\dot Q$}

Heat addition changes parcel entropy and departs from an <adiabatic process>. In an atmosphere, radiation, latent heating and turbulent heat transfer are examples. Spatially varying dust absorption can supply a thermal forcing for atmospheric waves; the heating distribution must project onto the wave's spatial structure to excite it efficiently.

= Heat-flow reversal along a thermodynamic path
{parent=First law of thermodynamics}
{title2=$\delta Q=dU+p\,dV$}

For a quasistatic simple compressible system with only pressure-volume <work>, the direction of <heat> transfer is determined by $dU+p\,dV$. A reversal occurs where this vanishes and changes sign. It need not coincide with a maximum or minimum of <temperature>, because the substance can still do <work> when its internal <energy> is stationary.

= Adiabatic process
{parent=First law of thermodynamics}
{wiki}

An <adiabatic process> has no <heat transfer> across the chosen boundary throughout the process: $\delta Q=0$, rather than merely zero net <heat> after incoming and outgoing transfers cancel. It is a condition on <energy> transfer, whereas an <isothermal process> holds <temperature> constant. A sealed cylinder can approach an <adiabatic process> through insulation or through motion short compared with the time needed for appreciable <heat transfer>. Its <temperature> can nevertheless change because <work> crosses the boundary.<Statistical Physics by David Tong/4 Classical Thermodynamics>[§§4.2–4.3: work, heat, and adiabatic legs of a cycle]{ref}

Take the trapped <gas> as a <closed system>, with fixed <amount of substance> and composition and no net change in bulk <kinetic energy> or <potential energy> between the endpoints. Define $Q_{\rm in}>0$ as <heat> entering and $W_{\rm out}>0$ as <work> delivered to the surroundings. The <first law of thermodynamics> gives
$$
\Delta U=Q_{\rm in}-W_{\rm out},\qquad
Q_{\rm in}=0\ \Longrightarrow\ \Delta U=-W_{\rm out}.
$$
Here $U$ is <internal energy>. An <adiabatic compression> normally supplies <work>, so $W_{\rm out}<0$; an <adiabatic expansion> against a resisting load delivers <work>, so $W_{\rm out}>0$. An <adiabatic process> need not preserve <internal energy> and need not make the <closed system> an <isolated system>. <Thermodynamics and Propulsion/2.5 Control volume form of the conservation laws>[MIT Unified Engineering, §2.5.2: heat inward and work outward in the first-law balance]{ref}

For a <reversible thermodynamic process>, $dS=\delta Q_{\rm rev}/T$ at positive absolute <temperature> $T$. A reversible <adiabatic process> therefore has $dS=0$: it is an <isentropic process>. Irreversible <adiabatic processes> can instead generate <entropy>; <free expansion> is the existing counterexample, with its calculation in <entropy change in free expansion>. Conversely, constant <entropy> alone does not certify an <adiabatic process> or reversibility: outward <heat> can export the <entropy> generated internally. For a <closed system> the macroscopic <entropy> balance is
$$
\Delta S=\int\frac{\delta Q_{\rm in}}{T_b}+S_{\rm gen},\qquad S_{\rm gen}\geq0,
$$
where $T_b$ is the absolute boundary <temperature> where each <heat> transfer occurs and $S_{\rm gen}$ is internal <entropy production>. With no <heat transfer>, this reduces to $\Delta S=S_{\rm gen}$; reversibility makes $S_{\rm gen}=0$. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/4-6-entropy[Ling, Moebs and Sanny, University Physics Volume 2, §4.6: reversible heat and irreversible entropy increase]{ref}

A motivating experiment measures the <gas> <temperature> directly during compression and expansion, instead of inferring it from how warm a cylinder feels. PASCO's authored TD-8565 manual describes a sealed piston cylinder, a fine nickel-wire <thermometer>, an absolute <pressure> sensor and a position readout for <volume (thermodynamics)>. A computer records all three channels together. The wire's <electrical resistance> changes with <temperature>; its small size permits a rapid response. https://cdn.pasco.com/product_document/Adiabatic-Gas-Law-Apparatus-TD-8565-Manual.pdf[PASCO scientific, Adiabatic Gas Law Apparatus, guide 012-05110D, pp. 1–2: apparatus and readouts]{ref}

Use ordinary trapped air and the apparatus's designed travel stops; mount the base securely, close the valve during a stroke, and record the actual <volume (thermodynamics)> limits. John Hart's university demonstration uses a large transparent cylinder with a low-<heat capacity> <thermocouple>, a <pressure> sensor, a travel gauge and a safety stop. It explicitly keeps the release valve closed during the demonstration. The basic observable is a rise in <temperature> on compression and a fall on loaded expansion, alongside the corresponding <pressure> and <volume (thermodynamics)> changes. Hart discusses wall <thermal conduction>, sensor response and leakage as reasons real records differ from the reversible model. https://atoc.colorado.edu/TeachingandLearning/Demonstrations/AdiabaticExpansion/Adiabatic_expansion.htm[John Hart, University of Colorado (2000), Adiabatic Expansion and Gas Law Physics: sensor demonstration and limitations]{ref}

For a proposed rapid-versus-slow comparison, let the air and walls return to the same initial <thermal equilibrium> before each run, then compare the same starting and finishing <volume (thermodynamics)> values with different stroke durations. Read <temperature>, absolute <pressure> and position continuously, including the hold after motion stops. A rapid compression gives a temporary <temperature> rise; holding the smaller <volume (thermodynamics)> lets <heat> leave and the <temperature> approach the surroundings again. Slow compression in good thermal contact allows that <heat transfer> during the stroke and approaches an <isothermal compression>. Loaded expansion reverses the signs: the rapid stroke cools, while <heat> entering during slow motion reduces the cooling. PASCO explicitly supports rapid nearly <adiabatic processes> and slow <isothermal processes>; the matched-run controls here are a proposed extension, not a reported set of measurements. https://www.pasco.com/products/lab-apparatus/thermodynamics/gas-laws/td-8565[PASCO scientific, TD-8565 product documentation: simultaneous readouts and adiabatic/isothermal comparisons]{ref}

Insulation suppresses <thermal conduction>; it does not prove that <heat transfer> vanishes. As proposed controls, repeat the matched strokes with and without a removable insulating jacket, record room and wall <temperatures>, measure fixed-position drift, and check valve closure and seal leakage. Calibrate the <thermometer> and position readout independently, including any connected dead <volume (thermodynamics)>; account for the <heat capacity> of the probe rather than treating it as a passive observer. Hart explicitly identifies the small air sample relative to the probe and walls, sensor inertia and seal leakage as limitations. A fast display cannot eliminate those physical effects. https://atoc.colorado.edu/TeachingandLearning/Demonstrations/AdiabaticExpansion/Adiabatic_expansion.htm[John Hart, Adiabatic Expansion and Gas Law Physics: wall, probe and seal limitations]{ref}

There are two distinct timing requirements. Let $\tau_{\rm eq}$ describe internal equilibration, $\tau_{\rm probe}$ the <thermometer> response, $t_{\rm move}$ the stroke duration and $\tau_{\rm heat}$ the effective <heat transfer> <time constant>. A useful sufficient window for a resolved, nearly reversible <adiabatic process> is
$$
\tau_{\rm eq},\tau_{\rm probe}\ll t_{\rm move}\ll\tau_{\rm heat},
$$
with negligible <friction>. The left inequality permits a <quasistatic process> and a usable readout; the right limits <heat> leakage. A violent stroke may satisfy only the right inequality. PASCO reports a roughly $30$–$50\,\mathrm{ms}$ lag of its <temperature> channel behind the other channels, so apparent simultaneous samples require care. If no such window exists, analyze equilibrium endpoints with the <first law of thermodynamics>, and regard the path as approximate. https://cdn.pasco.com/product_document/Adiabatic-Gas-Law-Apparatus-TD-8565-Manual.pdf[PASCO scientific, guide 012-05110D, p. 4: temperature-sensor lag]{ref}

The diagram's model is deliberately a hold after the stroke, when boundary <work> is zero. For constant whole-sample <heat capacity at constant volume> $C_V$ and effective thermal conductance $K>0$, it assumes
$$
C_V\frac{dT}{dt}=-K(T-T_b),\qquad
\tau_{\rm heat}=\frac{C_V}{K},\qquad
T(t)-T_b=[T(0)-T_b]e^{-t/\tau_{\rm heat}}.
$$
This is <Newton's law of cooling> with one uniform <gas> <temperature> and a fixed surrounding <temperature>; actual wall and probe dynamics can require more than one <time constant>. At $t/\tau_{\rm heat}=0.1$, the fraction of the initial <temperature> anomaly erased is $1-e^{-0.1}\simeq0.0952$; at one <time constant> it is about $0.6321$. These are analytical fractions, not experimental tolerances or evidence that the preceding stroke was reversible. Measuring the hold helps estimate <heat> leakage independently of the motion.

The usefulness of an <adiabatic process> is that it separates mechanical <work> from <heat transfer>. Its reversible limit connects <temperature> changes, mechanical stiffness and <entropy> in one model: <Rüchardt experiment> and <Clément-Desormes experiment> already use that connection to measure <heat capacity ratio>. The contrast with <free expansion> explains the deeper point: suppressing <heat> does not determine how much <work> is exchanged or how much <entropy> is produced.

= Adiabat with linear heat capacity
{parent=Adiabatic process}
{title2=$c_p(T)=A+BT$}

For a fixed-composition <ideal gas>, the reversible <adiabatic process> relation $c_p\,dT/T=R\,dp/p$ gives $A\ln(T/T_s)+B(T-T_s)=R\ln(p/p_s)$. Under <hydrostatic equilibrium>, $gz=A(T_s-T)+B(T_s^2-T^2)/2$. Increasing positive $B$ at fixed surface conditions increases temperature at a given smaller pressure and raises the model's formal zero-temperature height. That finite-height mathematical boundary is not a physical absolute-zero prediction.

= Dry adiabat
{parent=Adiabatic process}
{title2=$T/T_0=(p/p_0)^{R_a/c_p}$}

For an <ideal gas> of constant specific heat, an isentrope without phase changes satisfies $T=T_0(p/p_0)^{R_a/c_p}$. A dilute saturated atmosphere approximates this path only when both vapour sensible heat and latent-heating corrections are small.

= Moist adiabat
{parent=Adiabatic process}

A thermodynamic path for saturated adiabatic displacement including latent heating from condensation. A pseudoadiabatic model removes condensate, while a reversible model retains it; they need not have the same <heat capacity> or condensate loading.

= Adiabat
{parent=Adiabatic process}

An <adiabat> is a curve representing a quasistatic adiabatic change. In a reversible description it is also a curve of constant <entropy>. For an <ideal gas> with constant heat-capacity ratio $\gamma$, it obeys $pV^\gamma=\mathrm{constant}$. A general irreversible <adiabatic process> need not follow this equilibrium-state curve and can produce <entropy>.

= Adiabatic compression
{parent=Adiabatic process}

<Adiabatic compression> decreases the <volume (thermodynamics)> of a sample without <heat transfer> across its boundary. For the fixed-composition <ideal gas> and endpoint assumptions in <adiabatic process>, compression <work> increases <internal energy>; positive <heat capacity at constant volume> then means a higher final <temperature>. The effect comes from supplied <work>, even though no <heat> enters. The amount of <temperature> increase depends on the <work> actually delivered to the <gas>, not on the <adiabatic process> condition alone.

The <reversible ideal-gas adiabat> supplies a particularly useful quantitative benchmark. Assume a fixed <amount of substance> $n$, a <quasistatic process>, negligible <friction>, only <pressure>-<volume (thermodynamics)> <work>, and constant <heat capacity ratio> $\gamma=C_p/C_V>1$. Here $C_p$ and $C_V$ are the whole sample's <heat capacity at constant pressure> and <heat capacity at constant volume>. With $R$ the <molar gas constant>, <Mayer's relation> gives $C_V=nR/(\gamma-1)$. The proof outline is $C_V\,dT=-p\,dV$ from the <first law of thermodynamics>, followed by $p=nRT/V$ from the <ideal gas law> and integration. The result is
$$
pV^\gamma=\text{constant},\qquad TV^{\gamma-1}=\text{constant}.
$$
These finite power laws belong to this reversible, constant-$\gamma$ <ideal gas> model. If <heat capacity ratio> varies, use the integral relation in <heat capacity ratio>; if the compression is irreversible, use the actual <work> balance, as in <irreversible adiabatic piston compression>. A short stroke by itself justifies neither power law. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/3-6-adiabatic-processes-for-an-ideal-gas[Ling, Moebs and Sanny, University Physics Volume 2, §3.6: quasi-static ideal-gas adiabat]{ref}

Writing $r=V_1/V_2>1$ for the compression ratio, the reversible model predicts
$$
\frac{T_2}{T_1}=r^{\gamma-1},\qquad
\frac{p_2}{p_1}=r^\gamma,\qquad
W_{\rm in}=-W_{\rm out}=C_V(T_2-T_1)
=\frac{p_1V_1}{\gamma-1}\left(r^{\gamma-1}-1\right).
$$
All <temperatures> in these ratios are absolute. The larger <pressure> rise than in an <isothermal compression>, where $p_2/p_1=r$, explains the greater local stiffness described by <isentropic compressibility>.

For an original stipulated example, set $\gamma=7/5$, $p_1V_1=nRT_1=1\,\mathrm{kJ}$ and $r=2$. Then $T_2/T_1=2^{0.4}\simeq1.31951$ and $p_2/p_1=2^{1.4}\simeq2.63902$. The input <work> and <internal energy> increase are both $0.79877\,\mathrm{kJ}$, while $Q_{\rm in}=0$. No initial <temperature> or apparatus performance is implied by the chosen <energy> scale. Independent integration gives the signed outward <work>
$$
W_{\rm out}=\int_{V_1}^{V_2}p\,dV
=\frac{p_2V_2-p_1V_1}{1-\gamma},
$$
which is negative during compression and agrees with $-\Delta U$. This <integral> uses equilibrium gas <pressure> only because the path is reversible.

In the sensor experiment of <adiabatic process>, compare the <pressure>-<volume (thermodynamics)> <integral> with $C_V\Delta T$ as an <energy> check only when internal <pressure> is sufficiently uniform and piston dynamics permit that boundary-<work> estimate. Account for <friction> separately before comparing it with hand input <work>. = Adiabatic expansion
{parent=Adiabatic process}

<Adiabatic expansion> increases the accessible <volume (thermodynamics)> without <heat transfer>. In a loaded piston expansion of the fixed-composition <ideal gas> considered in <adiabatic process>, positive outward <work> comes from <internal energy>, so the final <temperature> decreases. This provides a cooling mechanism without removing <heat> during the stroke. In the <Carnot cycle>, reversible <adiabatic expansion> connects two different <temperatures> between its <isothermal processes>.<Statistical Physics by David Tong/4 Classical Thermodynamics>[§4.3.1: adiabatic expansion between the isothermal legs]{ref}

For the same constant-$\gamma$ <reversible ideal-gas adiabat>, put $b=V_2/V_1>1$. The scaling and <first law of thermodynamics> give
$$
\frac{T_2}{T_1}=b^{1-\gamma},\qquad
\frac{p_2}{p_1}=b^{-\gamma},\qquad
W_{\rm out}=C_V(T_1-T_2)
=\frac{p_1V_1}{\gamma-1}\left(1-b^{1-\gamma}\right)>0.
$$
With the figure's stipulated $\gamma=1.4$, $p_1V_1=1\,\mathrm{kJ}$ and $b=2$, the final ratios are $T_2/T_1\simeq0.75786$ and $p_2/p_1\simeq0.37893$. Outward <work> is $0.60535\,\mathrm{kJ}$ and $\Delta U=-0.60535\,\mathrm{kJ}$. Reversing this particular path from its cooled endpoint would require the same <work> input; the figure's separate compression starts at the original state, so its input is different. Endpoint <entropy> supplies another independent check:
$$
\Delta S=C_V\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1}+nR\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}=0.
$$
The two <logarithm> terms cancel using $C_V(\gamma-1)=nR$, confirming the reversible path is an <isentropic process>. https://web.mit.edu/16.unified/www/FALL/thermodynamics/notes/node40.html[MIT Unified Engineering, §5.4: ideal-gas endpoint entropy and constant-entropy scaling]{ref}

PASCO's expansion demonstration first compresses trapped air and waits for <thermal equilibrium> before the rapid expansion. Its manual warns that <friction>-warmed cylinder regions contact the <gas> during expansion, compromising quantitative comparison. https://cdn.pasco.com/product_document/Adiabatic-Gas-Law-Apparatus-TD-8565-Manual.pdf[PASCO scientific, guide 012-05110D, p. 3: expansion preparation and frictional wall-heating limitation]{ref} The hold separates cooling caused by expansion from simply undoing the earlier hot compression. As proposed controls, observe the <temperature> dip and subsequent warming, compare rapid and slow strokes from matched initial states, and assess wall <heat transfer> and probe lag before fitting a reversible model.

Cooling is not a universal consequence of <adiabatic expansion>: <free expansion> of an <ideal gas> has no outward <work> and returns to the same equilibrium <temperature>. Its <entropy> increases, so the reversible power laws do not describe it. Reuse <free expansion>, <Joule's free-expansion experiment> and <temperature sensitivity of a free-expansion experiment> for that apparatus, its <energy> budget and the real-gas qualifications. The useful distinction is the load: expansion cools this <ideal gas> when it must spend <internal energy> doing <work>.

= Isentropic process
{parent=Adiabatic process}
{wiki}

An isentropic process keeps the <entropy> constant. Every reversible <adiabatic process> is isentropic, while an irreversible adiabatic process can increase entropy.

= Reversible ideal-gas adiabat
{parent=Adiabatic process}

For a fixed amount of ideal gas with constant heat capacities, the first law and $\delta Q=0$ give
$$
TV^{\gamma-1}=\text{constant},
\qquad
pV^\gamma=\text{constant},
$$
where $\gamma=C_p/C_V$.
