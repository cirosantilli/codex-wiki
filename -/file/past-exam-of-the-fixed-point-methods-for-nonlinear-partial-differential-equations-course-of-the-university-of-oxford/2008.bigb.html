= Solution

Here “contractive” means <strictly distance-decreasing>: $\boxed{d(Tx,Ty)<d(x,y)\text{ whenever }x\ne y}$. “Strongly contractive” means a <contraction mapping> with one uniform constant $q\in[0,1)$ satisfying $\boxed{d(Tx,Ty)\leq qd(x,y)\text{ for all }x,y\in M}$.

If distinct $p,q$ were both <fixed points>, strict decrease would give $d(p,q)=d(Tp,Tq)<d(p,q)$, a contradiction. Thus there is **at most one fixed point**. This proves uniqueness if a fixed point exists; the pointwise strict inequality alone does not assert existence.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <contraction mapping theorem> states that a self-map $T$ of a nonempty <complete metric space> with a uniform contraction constant $q<1$ has a unique <fixed point> $p$. For every starting point, the iteration $x_{n+1}=Tx_n$ converges to $p$, with $d(x_n,p)\leq q^nd(x_1,x_0)/(1-q)$.

For the <cosine fixed-point iteration>, any real $x_0$ gives $x_1\in[-1,1]$. This <closed interval> is <complete>, and cosine maps it into $[\cos1,1]\subseteq[-1,1]$. The <mean value theorem> gives
$$
|\cos x-\cos y|\leq\sin(1)|x-y|\qquad(x,y\in[-1,1]),
$$
with $\sin(1)<1$. Apply the <contraction mapping theorem> to the tail starting at $x_1$. Therefore $\boxed{x_n\to p,\quad p=\cos p}$ for every real initial value. Numerically $p\approx0.739085$. The global real-line <derivative> bound is only one; entering the <invariant interval> supplies the strict uniform bound needed by the theorem.

= Solution
{parent=c}

The <Brouwer fixed-point theorem> says that every <continuous map> $T:\overline B_R(0)\to\overline B_R(0)$ has a <fixed point>. To use the supplied <outward vector field zero theorem>, first extend the construction to all of $\mathbb R^n$. Let
$$
P_R(x)=\begin{cases}x,&|x|\leq R,\\Rx/|x|,&|x|>R,\end{cases}\qquad g(x)=x-T(P_R(x)).
$$
The <radial retraction onto a closed ball> $P_R$ is continuous, so $g$ is a continuous vector field on the required whole space. On $|x|=R$, the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives
$$
g(x)\cdot x=R^2-T(x)\cdot x\geq R^2-|T(x)|R\geq0.
$$
The supplied theorem gives $g(x_0)=0$ with $|x_0|\leq R$. There $P_R(x_0)=x_0$, hence $\boxed{T(x_0)=x_0}$. This explicit extension avoids presuming that the original map $T$ was defined outside the ball.

= Solution
{parent=a}

A <nonlinear compact operator> is a continuous map that sends <bounded sets> of its domain to <relatively compact subsets> of the target space; linearity is not part of this general definition.

For the specified <diagonal operator on sequence space> on <l2 sequence space>,
$$
\|Tx\|_2^2=\sum_{i\geq1}\frac{|x_i|^2}{i^2}\leq\sum_{i\geq1}|x_i|^2,
$$
so it is well-defined, <linear> and <bounded>. Let $T_N$ retain only its first $N$ output coordinates. This is a <finite-rank operator>, and
$$
\boxed{\|T-T_N\|=\frac1{N+1}\longrightarrow0.}
$$
The upper bound follows by estimating each tail multiplier by $1/(N+1)$; equality follows on the unit coordinate vector $e_{N+1}$. Finite-rank operators between <Banach spaces> are compact, and a limit of compact linear operators in <operator norm> is compact, the <norm-closedness of compact operators>. Therefore $\boxed{T\text{ is compact}}$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <Schauder fixed-point theorem> states that a continuous compact self-map of a nonempty closed bounded <convex set> in a <Banach space> has a <fixed point>. Equivalently in this bounded-domain version, it suffices for the image of the map to be relatively compact. The theorem guarantees existence, without requiring a contraction estimate or promising uniqueness.

= c
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Work in the <Banach space> $C([0,1])$ with its <supremum norm>, and take the nonempty closed bounded convex set $M=\{u:100\leq u(t)\leq121\text{ for all }t\}$. Define
$$
(Tu)(t)=101+\int_0^t\bigl(\sqrt{|u(s)|}+1\bigr)\,ds.
$$
For $u\in M$ the absolute value is unnecessary, and $\boxed{101\leq Tu(t)\leq101+12=113\leq121}$. Thus $T:M\to M$. Also, for $a,b\geq100$,
$$
|\sqrt a-\sqrt b|=\frac{|a-b|}{\sqrt a+\sqrt b}\leq\frac1{20}|a-b|,
$$
so $\|Tu-Tv\|_\infty\leq\tfrac1{20}\|u-v\|_\infty$ and in particular $T$ is continuous.

The image is uniformly bounded, and $|Tu(t)-Tu(s)|\leq12|t-s|$, so it is <equicontinuous>. The <Arzelà-Ascoli theorem> says that a uniformly bounded equicontinuous family in $C([0,1])$ has <relatively compact> <closure> in the uniform norm. Hence $T$ is compact, and the <Schauder fixed-point theorem> supplies $\boxed{u=Tu}$, the required solution. In fact the displayed contraction estimate also lets the <contraction mapping theorem> give uniqueness within $M$ and convergence of the corresponding <Picard iteration>. The initial constant here is the printed integer $101$.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Using the <dual pairing>, a <monotone operator> satisfies $\langle A(u)-A(v),u-v\rangle\geq0$ for all $u,v\in M$. It is a <strongly monotone operator> if some $m>0$ gives
$$
\boxed{\langle A(u)-A(v),u-v\rangle\geq m\|u-v\|^2\quad(u,v\in M).}
$$
A <nonlinear coercive operator> with respect to $u_0\in M$ satisfies $\langle A(u),u-u_0\rangle/\|u-u_0\|\to+\infty$ as $\|u-u_0\|\to\infty$ in $M$.

Strong monotonicity, with $v=u_0$, gives
$$
\langle A(u),u-u_0\rangle\geq m\|u-u_0\|^2+\langle A(u_0),u-u_0\rangle
\geq m\|u-u_0\|^2-\|A(u_0)\|_{X^*}\|u-u_0\|.
$$
After division, the lower bound is $m\|u-u_0\|-\|A(u_0)\|_{X^*}\to+\infty$, proving coercivity. Reflexivity and separability are not needed for this particular estimate.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Take the <zero-boundary Sobolev space> $X=H_0^1(\Omega)$ with norm $\|u\|_X=\|\nabla u\|_2$. The <Poincare inequality> makes this equivalent to the usual <Sobolev norm>. Define
$$
\boxed{\langle A(u),v\rangle=\int_\Omega\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+\int_\Omega u^3v,\qquad\langle f,v\rangle=\int_\Omega v.}
$$
The <weak formulation> is $u\in X$ and $\langle A(u),v\rangle=\langle f,v\rangle$ for every $v\in X$.

In dimension three the <Sobolev embedding theorem> gives $\|u\|_6\leq C_6\|u\|_X$, so $u^3\in L^2$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> and <Poincare inequality> yield
$$
|\langle A(u),v\rangle|\leq\bigl(\|u\|_X+C_PC_6^3\|u\|_X^3\bigr)\|v\|_X,
\qquad|\langle f,v\rangle|\leq|\Omega|^{1/2}C_P\|v\|_X.
$$
Thus $A(u)\in X^*$ for every $u$, and $f\in X^*$. Finally
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle A(u)-A(w),u-w\rangle
&=\|u-w\|_X^2+\int_\Omega(u-w)^2(u^2+uw+w^2)\\
&\geq\boxed{\|u-w\|_X^2},
\end{aligned}
$$
since $u^2+uw+w^2\geq0$. Hence $A$ is strongly monotone with constant one in the chosen norm.

= Solution
{parent=a}

One-dimensional <Sobolev embedding> gives a continuous representative for each $v\in H_0^1(0,l)$, with zero endpoint values, so the pointwise obstacle condition is meaningful. The <positive part> $g_+=\max\{g,0\}$ is <Lipschitz> and vanishes near both endpoints because $g(0),g(l)<0$. It therefore belongs to $H_0^1$ and satisfies $g_+\geq g$, proving that $M$ is nonempty.

If $v,w\in M$ and $t\in[0,1]$, then $(1-t)v+tw\geq g$, so $M$ is a <convex set>. If $v_n\to v$ in $H_0^1$, the estimate $\|v_n-v\|_\infty\leq\sqrt l\,\|v_n'-v'\|_2$ gives <uniform convergence>. Passing to the limit in $v_n(x)\geq g(x)$ shows $v\in M$. Thus **the admissible set is nonempty, closed and convex**.

= b
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

For any $v\in M$, the segment $u+t(v-u)$ stays in $M$ for $0\leq t\leq1$. Since $u$ is a minimizer, the right derivative of its energy at $t=0$ is nonnegative. The <first variation> therefore gives the <variational inequality>
$$
\boxed{\int_0^l\bigl(2a(x)u'(x)(v'(x)-u'(x))+\gamma\cos(u(x))(v(x)-u(x))\bigr)\,dx\geq0
\quad(v\in M).}
$$
The factor two comes from the quadratic gradient term. Only a one-sided inequality follows because a variation pointing below the obstacle need not be admissible.

= c
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The <noncontact set> $D=\{x\in(0,l):u(x)>g(x)\}$ is open. For $\varphi\in C_0^\infty(D)$, the positive gap above the obstacle has a positive minimum on the support of $\varphi$. Thus both $u+t\varphi$ and $u-t\varphi$ are admissible for sufficiently small $t>0$. Applying the <variational inequality> to both signs yields
$$
\int_D\bigl(2au'\varphi'+\gamma\cos(u)\varphi\bigr)\,dx=0.
$$
Hence the <Euler-Lagrange equation> on the noncontact set is
$$
\boxed{-2(au')'+\gamma\cos u=0\quad\text{in }D.}
$$
It holds classically under the assumed smoothness. On the <contact set> the obstacle prevents the negative variations used to obtain this equality, so a variational inequality remains the appropriate global description.

{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

Let $u,w\in M$ both solve the <variational inequality>. Test the inequality for $u$ with $w$ and the one for $w$ with $u$, then add. Writing $d=u-w$ gives
$$
2\int_0^la|d'|^2\,dx\leq-\gamma\int_0^l(\cos u-\cos w)d\,dx\leq\gamma\|d\|_2^2,
$$
since <cosine> is <Lipschitz> with constant one. Since $d(0)=0$, the <fundamental theorem of calculus> and <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> give $|d(x)|\leq\sqrt l\,\|d'\|_2$; integrating gives $\|d\|_2^2\leq l^2\|d'\|_2^2$. Therefore
$$
(2c_0-\gamma l^2)\|d'\|_2^2\leq0.
$$
For $\boxed{0<\gamma<2c_0/l^2}$, the coefficient is positive, so $d'=0$ and the zero boundary value gives $d=0$. This proves uniqueness with an explicit sufficient smallness condition. More generally any <Poincare inequality> constant $C_P$ gives the condition $\gamma C_P^2<2c_0$; optimizing that constant is unnecessary for the requested conclusion. The underlying principle is <uniqueness for a strongly monotone variational inequality>.
