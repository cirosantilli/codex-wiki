= Solution
{parent=i}

The <Banach fixed-point theorem> states that a <contraction mapping> $T:X\to X$ of a nonempty <complete metric space> $(X,d)$ has a unique <fixed point>, and <iterations of a map> $x_{n+1}=T(x_n)$ converge to it from every $x_0\in X$. Precisely, assume a single constant $q\in[0,1)$ satisfies $d(Tx,Ty)\leq qd(x,y)$ for every $x,y\in X$.

Induction gives $d(x_{n+1},x_n)\leq q^n d(x_1,x_0)$. The <triangle inequality> and the <geometric series> therefore give, for $m>n$,
$$
d(x_m,x_n)\leq\sum_{j=n}^{m-1}q^j d(x_1,x_0)
\leq\frac{q^n}{1-q}d(x_1,x_0).
$$
Thus $(x_n)$ is a <Cauchy sequence>. By <completeness> it converges to some $x_*\in X$. The <contraction mapping> is a <continuous map>, so $Tx_*=\lim_n Tx_n=\lim_n x_{n+1}=x_*$. If $y_*$ is another <fixed point>, then $d(x_*,y_*)\leq qd(x_*,y_*)$, forcing $x_*=y_*$. Letting $m\to\infty$ in the estimate also gives the <contraction iteration error bound>:
$$
\boxed{Tx_*=x_*,\qquad d(x_n,x_*)\leq\frac{q^n}{1-q}d(x_1,x_0).}
$$
If $q=0$, $T$ is constant and the <sequence> reaches its unique <fixed point> after the first step; this also removes any ambiguity about $0^0$ in the displayed estimate at $n=0$.

= Solution

The native paper prints $n\geq1$ while specifying $x_0$ alone. This does not determine $x_1$. We use the intended convention $x_{n+1}=\sin x_n$ for $n\geq0$; even with an independently chosen real $x_1$, the same proof gives the requested convergence.

For the <sine fixed-point iteration>, after one step $x_1\in[-1,1]$. On this <interval>, the <sine> has the same sign as its argument, and
$$
0<\sin t<t\qquad(0<t\leq1).
$$
For example, $t-\sin t=\int_0^t(1-\cos s)\,ds>0$, since the <integrand> is positive for $0<s\leq1$. By oddness of the <sine>, $a_n=|x_n|$ consequently satisfies $a_{n+1}=\sin a_n\leq a_n$ for $n\geq1$. This <monotone sequence> is bounded below by zero, so it has a limit $a\in[0,1]$. The <continuity> of the <sine> gives $a=\sin a$, and the strict inequality above forces $a=0$.

Hence $x_n\to0$, and $0$ is a <fixed point> of the <sine>. It is the only real <fixed point>: every such point has absolute value at most one, where the preceding strict inequality applies. Thus
$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}x_n=0\quad\text{for every real initial value}.}
$$
The <Banach fixed-point theorem> does not directly apply to the <sine> on $[-1,1]$: the ratios $|\sin t-\sin0|/|t-0|$ tend to one as $t\to0$, so no uniform <contraction mapping> constant smaller than one exists there.

A <retraction> from a <topological space> $X$ onto a <subset> $Y$ is a <continuous map> $r:X\to Y$ satisfying $r(y)=y$ for every $y\in Y$. Equivalently, $r\circ\iota=\operatorname{id}_Y$, where $\iota:Y\hookrightarrow X$ is the inclusion.

The <no-retraction theorem> for the finite-dimensional <closed unit ball> says
$$
\boxed{\text{For }n\geq1,\text{ no retraction }\mathbb B^n\longrightarrow\partial\mathbb B^n\text{ exists}.}
$$
Indeed, if $r$ were such a <retraction>, $F(x)=-r(x)$ would be a <continuous map> from $\mathbb B^n$ into itself. The <Brouwer fixed-point theorem> would give $x=F(x)=-r(x)$. Since $|r(x)|=1$, $x$ lies on the <boundary>, where $r(x)=x$. Thus $x=-x$, contradicting $|x|=1$. Here the <Brouwer fixed-point theorem> asserts that every <continuous map> of a finite-dimensional <closed unit ball> into itself has a <fixed point>.

The <radial retraction of a punctured ball> gives a <retraction>: project away from the removed interior point until reaching the <unit sphere>. Write $a=x_0$, $v=x-a\ne0$, and set
$$
\tau(x)=\frac{-a\cdot v+\sqrt{(a\cdot v)^2+(1-|a|^2)|v|^2}}{|v|^2},
\qquad
\boxed{r(x)=a+\tau(x)(x-a).}
$$
The equation $|a+tv|^2=1$ is the <quadratic equation>
$$
|v|^2t^2+2(a\cdot v)t+|a|^2-1=0.
$$
Its constant term is negative, so it has exactly one positive root, namely $\tau(x)$. Since $|a+v|=|x|\leq1$, that positive root satisfies $\tau(x)\geq1$. Thus $r(x)\in\partial\mathbb B^n$ for every $x\ne a$ in the <closed unit ball>.

The displayed expression is a <continuous map> on the punctured <closed unit ball>: its denominator is nonzero there, and its <square root> has strictly positive argument. If $|x|=1$, $t=1$ is the unique positive root, so $r(x)=x$. These are exactly the requirements for a <retraction>. The missing interior point is essential: the formula need not extend continuously to $a$, in agreement with the <no-retraction theorem>.

The <retraction of a Hilbert ball onto its sphere> exists. In the real <Hilbert space> $\ell^2$, let $S(x_0,x_1,\ldots)=(0,x_0,x_1,\ldots)$ be the <unilateral shift operator>, and let $e_0=(1,0,\ldots)$. For $x$ in the <closed unit ball> $\mathbb B$, define
$$
F(x)=\sqrt{1-\|x\|^2}\,e_0+Sx.
$$
The <orthogonality> of $e_0$ and $Sx$ gives $\|F(x)\|^2=1$. Thus $F$ is a <continuous map> into the <unit sphere>. It has no <fixed point>: $F(x)=x$ would imply $x_{j+1}=x_j$ for every $j\geq0$. A constant <sequence> in $\ell^2$ must be zero, whereas $F(0)=e_0\ne0$.

Put $d=x-F(x)$, which is nonzero everywhere, and define
$$
\alpha(x)=\frac{2(1-\langle F(x),x\rangle)}{\|x-F(x)\|^2},
\qquad
\boxed{r(x)=F(x)+\alpha(x)(x-F(x)).}
$$
This is a <continuous map> on the whole <closed unit ball>, including zero. To check its range, observe that $\langle F(x),d\rangle=\langle F(x),x\rangle-1$, so
$$
\|F(x)+\alpha d\|^2
=1+2\alpha(\langle F(x),x\rangle-1)+\alpha^2\|d\|^2=1.
$$
If $\|x\|=1$, then $\|x-F(x)\|^2=2(1-\langle F(x),x\rangle)$, hence $\alpha(x)=1$ and $r(x)=x$. Therefore $r:\mathbb B\to\partial\mathbb B$ is a <retraction>.

Geometrically this is the second intersection of the line from $F(x)$ through $x$ with the <unit sphere>. In fact
$$
\alpha(x)-1=\frac{1-\|x\|^2}{\|x-F(x)\|^2}\geq0.
$$
The construction illustrates why the finite-dimensional hypothesis in the <no-retraction theorem> matters. The <closed unit ball> of $\ell^2$ is not a <compact set> in the <norm topology>.

Let $S_+^{n-1}$ be the nonnegative portion of the <unit sphere> specified in the paper. If $Ax=0$ for some $x\in S_+^{n-1}$, then this nonzero <vector> is an <eigenvector> with <eigenvalue> zero, which already proves the assertion.

Otherwise $Ax\ne0$ for every $x\in S_+^{n-1}$, and
$$
T(x)=\frac{Ax}{|Ax|}
$$
is a <continuous map> from $S_+^{n-1}$ to itself. Indeed, every entry of $Ax$ is nonnegative because $A$ is a <nonnegative matrix> and every entry of $x$ is nonnegative. By the supplied <homeomorphism> $h:\mathbb B^{n-1}\to S_+^{n-1}$, the <continuous map> $h^{-1}Th$ maps the finite-dimensional <closed unit ball> into itself. The <Brouwer fixed-point theorem> supplies a <fixed point>, hence $T(x_*)=x_*$ for some $x_*\in S_+^{n-1}$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{Ax_*=\lambda x_*,\qquad x_*\ne0,\qquad\lambda=|Ax_*|>0.}
$$
Together with the zero case this proves that $A$ has a nonnegative <eigenvalue>, with a nonnegative <eigenvector>. For $n=1$, the auxiliary <closed unit ball> is the one-point space $\mathbb B^0$ and the argument remains valid. No claim of strict positivity of every entry of the <eigenvector> is needed.

For <Banach spaces> $X,Y$, a <nonlinear compact operator> $T:X\to Y$ is a <continuous map> that sends every <bounded set> to a <relatively compact subset> of $Y$: the closure of its image is a <compact set>. For a <continuous map>, the image condition is equivalent to requiring that every bounded <sequence> $(x_j)$ has a <subsequence> along which $T(x_j)$ converges in the <norm topology> of $Y$. For a <linear operator>, this is the usual <compact operator> definition; the continuity requirement is included explicitly here because the subsequent applications are nonlinear.

The compact-domain version of the <Schauder fixed-point theorem> says
$$
\boxed{K\ne\varnothing\text{ compact and convex in a Banach space},\quad
T:K\to K\text{ continuous}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad Tu=u\text{ for some }u\in K.}
$$
A useful equivalent version replaces $K$ by a nonempty closed bounded <convex set> $C$ and assumes $T:C\to C$ is a <continuous map> with <relatively compact> image. This latter form will be used for the <partial differential equation>. Both forms assert existence of a <fixed point>, without asserting uniqueness.

Let $E=\operatorname{aff}K$ be the <affine hull> and put $k=\dim E\leq n$. If $k=0$, $K$ is a singleton and is <homeomorphic> to $\mathbb B^0$. Suppose $k>0$. Choose $k+1$ <affinely independent> points of $K$ spanning $E$. Their <simplex> is contained in $K$ because $K$ is a <convex set>, and its <barycentre> $a$ has a neighbourhood in $E$ contained in that <simplex>. Identifying $E-a$ with $\mathbb R^k$, put $D=K-a$. For some $0<r\leq R<\infty$,
$$
r\mathbb B^k\subset D\subset R\mathbb B^k.
$$
Here the inner inclusion follows from that neighbourhood, and the outer one follows from the <compactness> of $K$.

Use the <Minkowski functional>
$$
p(z)=\inf\{t>0:z\in tD\}.
$$
The inclusions imply $|z|/R\leq p(z)\leq|z|/r$. The fact that $D$ is a <convex set> implies $p(z+w)\leq p(z)+p(w)$: if $z\in sD$ and $w\in tD$, then $(z+w)/(s+t)$ is a <convex combination> of points of $D$. Also $p(tz)=tp(z)$ for $t\geq0$. Consequently
$$
p(z)-p(w)\leq p(z-w)\leq|z-w|/r,
\qquad |p(z)-p(w)|\leq|z-w|/r.
$$
Thus $p$ is a <continuous function>. Since $D$ is closed and contains zero, $D=\{p\leq1\}$: the forward inclusion is immediate, and for the reverse inclusion one takes a limit of $z/t_j\in D$ with $t_j\to p(z)\leq1$, using <positive homogeneity> and the fact that $D$ is a <convex set>. For $z\ne0$, $p(z)>0$, so this limiting argument has no division by zero.

Define a radial <continuous map> by
$$
h(0)=a,\qquad h(t\theta)=a+\frac{t}{p(\theta)}\theta
\quad(0<t\leq1,\ |\theta|=1).
$$
Each ray meets $D$ in exactly $\{s\theta:0\leq s\leq1/p(\theta)\}$. Hence $h$ is a <bijection> from $\mathbb B^k$ to $K$. Away from zero its <continuity> follows from that of $p$, and at zero $|h(t\theta)-a|\leq Rt$. A <continuous map> that is a <bijection> from a <compact space> to a <Hausdorff space> is a <homeomorphism>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{K\cong\mathbb B^k,\qquad k=\dim(\operatorname{aff}K).}
$$
Using the <affine hull> is essential: $K$ can have empty interior in the ambient $\mathbb R^n$.

We first prove the compact-domain <Schauder fixed-point theorem>. Let $K$ be a nonempty compact <convex set> in a <Banach space> $X$, and let $T:K\to K$ be a <continuous map>. The <Brouwer fixed-point theorem> states that every <continuous map> of a finite-dimensional <closed unit ball> into itself has a <fixed point>. By the <homeomorphism> proved in part (a)(ii), it applies equally to any nonempty finite-dimensional compact <convex set>, including a singleton.

For $\varepsilon>0$, use the supplied <Schauder approximation lemma> to obtain a finite-dimensional <linear subspace> $L_\varepsilon$ and a <continuous map> $T_\varepsilon:K\to K\cap L_\varepsilon$ with $\|Tx-T_\varepsilon x\|\leq\varepsilon$. The set $K_\varepsilon=K\cap L_\varepsilon$ is nonempty because it contains $T_\varepsilon(K)$. It is a compact <convex set>, since a finite-dimensional <linear subspace> is closed. Restricting $T_\varepsilon$ to $K_\varepsilon$ and applying the <Brouwer fixed-point theorem> gives $x_\varepsilon=T_\varepsilon x_\varepsilon$, and thus
$$
\|Tx_\varepsilon-x_\varepsilon\|\leq\varepsilon.
$$
Take $\varepsilon_j=1/j$. The <compactness> of $K$ yields a <convergent subsequence> $x_{\varepsilon_{j_m}}\to x_*\in K$. The <continuity> of $T$ and the preceding estimate give
$$
\boxed{Tx_*=x_*.}
$$
For completeness, this also proves the version on a nonempty closed bounded <convex set> $C$ when $T(C)$ is <relatively compact>. Let $K=\overline{\operatorname{conv}}T(C)\subset C$. The <closed convex hull of a compact set> in a <Banach space> is compact. To verify the fact needed here, cover $T(C)$ by finitely many <balls> of radius $\delta$. Replacing every term of a <convex combination> by a centre changes it by at most $\delta$ in <norm>. The <convex hull> of those finitely many centres is compact in a finite-dimensional <linear subspace>, so it has a <finite net> of radius $\delta$. Thus $\operatorname{conv}T(C)$ is <totally bounded>. Its closure is <complete> and <totally bounded>, hence compact. Now $T(K)\subset T(C)\subset K$, so the compact-domain result supplies a <fixed point> in $C$.

Use the real <zero-trace Sobolev space> $H_0^1(\Omega)$ with <norm> $\|\nabla u\|_2$. The <Poincaré inequality> on the bounded smooth <domain> gives $\|u\|_2\leq C_P\|\nabla u\|_2$, making this an equivalent <Sobolev norm>. The <weak formulation> is to find $u\in H_0^1(\Omega)$ with $u\geq0$ <almost everywhere> and
$$
\boxed{\int_\Omega\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx
=\int_\Omega(1+\sqrt u)v\,dx\qquad\text{for every }v\in H_0^1(\Omega).}
$$
The zero <Dirichlet boundary condition> is encoded in the <Sobolev trace>. Formally, the identity follows by multiplying the <partial differential equation> by a <test function> and using <integration by parts>; it then extends to all $v\in H_0^1(\Omega)$ by density.

To check all terms, boundedness of $\Omega$ gives finite <Lebesgue measure> $m=|\Omega|$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives $u\in L^1(\Omega)$ and
$$
\|\sqrt u\|_2^2=\int_\Omega u\,dx\leq m^{1/2}\|u\|_2,
\qquad
\|1+\sqrt u\|_2\leq m^{1/2}+m^{1/4}\|u\|_2^{1/2}.
$$
Thus the right side is a bounded <linear functional> of $v$, since its absolute value is at most $\|1+\sqrt u\|_2 C_P\|\nabla v\|_2$. The left side is finite by the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. These estimates work for every stated $n\geq2$.

Let $P=\{w\in L^2(\Omega):w\geq0\text{ almost everywhere}\}$. For $g\in L^2(\Omega)$, define $Gg=z\in H_0^1(\Omega)$ by
$$
\int_\Omega\nabla z\cdot\nabla v\,dx=\int_\Omega gv\,dx
\qquad(v\in H_0^1(\Omega)).
$$
The <Lax-Milgram theorem> guarantees a unique $z$: the <bilinear form> on the left is bounded and <coercive> in the <norm> $\|\nabla\cdot\|_2$, and the right side is a bounded <linear functional> by the <Poincaré inequality>. Testing with $z$ gives
$$
\|\nabla Gg\|_2\leq C_P\|g\|_2,
\qquad\|Gg\|_2\leq C_P^2\|g\|_2.
$$
Define $T:P\to L^2(\Omega)$ by $Tw=G(1+\sqrt w)$. This actually maps into $P$. Indeed the <weak maximum principle> says that a <weak solution> $z\in H_0^1(\Omega)$ of $-\Delta z=g\geq0$ is nonnegative <almost everywhere>. Here $1+\sqrt w\geq0$ and belongs to $L^2(\Omega)$. Directly, taking the <negative part> $z^-=\max\{-z,0\}$ as a <test function> gives $-\|\nabla z^-\|_2^2=\int_\Omega gz^-\,dx\geq0$, so $z^-=0$ by the <Poincaré inequality>.

The required <fixed point> formulation is
$$
\boxed{u\in P,\qquad u=Tu=G(1+\sqrt u).}
$$
Every such <fixed point> belongs automatically to $H_0^1(\Omega)$ and satisfies part (b)(i). Conversely, uniqueness in the <Lax-Milgram theorem> makes every nonnegative <weak solution> a <fixed point> of $T$.

We apply the closed-bounded-domain version of the <Schauder fixed-point theorem> in the <Banach space> $L^2(\Omega)$. Write $m=|\Omega|$ and use $G,T,C_P$ from part (b)(ii). Choose
$$
a=C_P^2m^{1/2},\qquad b=C_P^2m^{1/4},\qquad
R=\max\{1,2a,4b^2\},
\qquad C=\{w\in L^2(\Omega):w\geq0\text{ a.e.},\ \|w\|_2\leq R\}.
$$
The set $C$ is nonempty, bounded, closed and a <convex set>. For closedness of its positivity condition, if $w_j\geq0$ and $w_j\to w$ in $L^2$, then pointwise $w^-\leq|w-w_j|$, whence $\|w^-\|_2\to0$.

The <weak maximum principle> already gives $Tw\geq0$. Our estimates give, for $w\in C$,
$$
\|Tw\|_2\leq C_P^2\bigl(m^{1/2}+m^{1/4}\|w\|_2^{1/2}\bigr)
\leq a+b\sqrt R\leq R/2+R/2=R.
$$
Thus $T(C)\subset C$.

To prove <continuity>, use the pointwise <square root> estimate $|\sqrt s-\sqrt t|^2\leq|s-t|$ for $s,t\geq0$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> then gives
$$
\|\sqrt w-\sqrt z\|_2^2\leq\|w-z\|_1\leq m^{1/2}\|w-z\|_2,
\qquad
\|Tw-Tz\|_2\leq C_P^2m^{1/4}\|w-z\|_2^{1/2}.
$$
Hence $T$ is a <continuous map> on $C$, even though the <square root> need not have a bounded <derivative> near zero.

Finally,
$$
\|\nabla Tw\|_2\leq C_P\bigl(m^{1/2}+m^{1/4}\sqrt R\bigr)
\qquad(w\in C).
$$
The <Rellich-Kondrachov theorem> says that $H_0^1(\Omega)$ embeds compactly into $L^2(\Omega)$ on a bounded smooth <domain>. For $n>2$, $2<2n/(n-2)$, and for $n=2$ every finite target exponent is available, so the required <compact embedding> holds in every dimension in the question. Thus $T(C)$ is <relatively compact> in $L^2(\Omega)$.

All hypotheses of the <Schauder fixed-point theorem> are met. It gives $u=Tu\in C$, and part (b)(ii) converts this <fixed point> into the required <weak solution>:
$$
\boxed{\text{There exists }u\in H_0^1(\Omega),\ u\geq0\text{ a.e., with }
\int_\Omega\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx=\int_\Omega(1+\sqrt u)v\,dx
\quad\forall v\in H_0^1(\Omega).}
$$
Only existence is needed. The construction does not assume that $\Omega$ is small or that $T$ is a <contraction mapping>.

A useful form of <Minty's lemma> is the following. Let $X$ be a real <Banach space>, $K\subset X$ a nonempty <convex set>, and $A:K\to X^*$ a <monotone operator> that is <hemicontinuous>. Here <operator monotonicity> means $\langle Ax-Ay,x-y\rangle\geq0$ for $x,y\in K$, and <hemicontinuity> means that $t\mapsto\langle A(x+t(y-x)),z\rangle$ is continuous on $[0,1]$ for $x,y\in K$ and $z\in X$. For $u\in K$,
$$
\boxed{\langle Au,v-u\rangle\geq0\ \forall v\in K
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\langle Av,v-u\rangle\geq0\ \forall v\in K.}
$$
The forward implication follows from <operator monotonicity>:
$$
\langle Av,v-u\rangle
=\langle Au,v-u\rangle+\langle Av-Au,v-u\rangle\geq0.
$$
For the reverse implication, fix $w\in K$ and take $v_t=u+t(w-u)\in K$ for $0<t\leq1$. The assumed inequality gives
$$
0\leq\langle Av_t,v_t-u\rangle=t\langle A(u+t(w-u)),w-u\rangle.
$$
Divide by $t$ and send $t\downarrow0$. <Hemicontinuity> gives $\langle Au,w-u\rangle\geq0$, as required.

In the whole-space version, for a <monotone operator> $A:X\to X^*$ that is <hemicontinuous>,
$$
\boxed{Au=f\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\langle Av-f,v-u\rangle\geq0\quad\forall v\in X.}
$$
Apply the just-proved result to $A-f$ and $K=X$; the resulting inequality $\langle Au-f,v-u\rangle\geq0$ for all $v$ forces $Au=f$ by choosing $v=u\pm z$. This is the <variational inequality> form of <Minty's lemma>.

Choose the real <zero-trace Sobolev space>
$$
X=W_0^{1,4}(\Omega),\qquad\|u\|_X=\|\nabla u\|_4,
\qquad Y=L^{8/7}(\Omega).

The <Poincaré inequality> makes this an equivalent <Sobolev norm>. Since the dimension is five, the <Sobolev conjugate exponent> is $4^*=5\cdot4/(5-4)=20$. The <Sobolev embedding theorem> gives $X\hookrightarrow L^{20}(\Omega)$ continuously. Boundedness of $\Omega$ and <Lp inclusion on a finite measure space> then give $X\hookrightarrow L^8(\Omega)$ continuously, say $\|u\|_8\leq C_8\|u\|_X$.

For $f\in Y$, the <weak formulation> is
$$
\boxed{u\in X,\qquad
\int_\Omega |\nabla u|^2\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx
+\int_\Omega u^7v\,dx=\int_\Omega fv\,dx
\quad\forall v\in X.}
$$
The zero <Dirichlet boundary condition> is imposed through the <Sobolev trace>. <Hölder's inequality> verifies every term:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\left|\int_\Omega|\nabla u|^2\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx\right|
&\leq\|\nabla u\|_4^3\|\nabla v\|_4,\\
\left|\int_\Omega u^7v\,dx\right|
&\leq\|u\|_8^7\|v\|_8,\\
\left|\int_\Omega fv\,dx\right|
&\leq\|f\|_{8/7}\|v\|_8.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus $|\nabla u|^2\nabla u\in L^{4/3}(\Omega;\mathbb R^5)$, while $u^7\in L^{8/7}(\Omega)$, and all three pairings are finite bounded <linear functionals> in $v$. The choice $Y=L^{8/7}(\Omega)$ is sufficient; it is not intended to be the largest possible space of data.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For $u,w\in X$, the forcing terms cancel, giving
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle A(u)-A(w),u-w\rangle
={}&\int_\Omega\bigl(|\nabla u|^2\nabla u-|\nabla w|^2\nabla w\bigr)\cdot(\nabla u-\nabla w)\,dx\\
&+\int_\Omega(u^7-w^7)(u-w)\,dx.
\end{aligned}
$$
The scalar <function> $s\mapsto s^7$ is strictly increasing on all of $\mathbb R$, so the second <integrand> is nonnegative. For the first one, use the <quartic gradient monotonicity inequality>. Put $d=\xi-\eta$ and $m=(\xi+\eta)/2$ for real <vectors> $\xi,\eta$. Expanding gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
(|\xi|^2\xi-|\eta|^2\eta)\cdot(\xi-\eta)
&=|m|^2|d|^2+2(m\cdot d)^2+\frac14|d|^4\\
&\geq\frac14|\xi-\eta|^4.
\end{aligned}
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\langle A(u)-A(w),u-w\rangle
\geq\frac14\|\nabla(u-w)\|_4^4
=\frac14\|u-w\|_X^4>0\quad(u\ne w).}
$$
The last strict inequality uses the <Poincaré inequality>: if the <weak gradient> of $u-w\in W_0^{1,4}(\Omega)$ vanishes, then $u=w$. This proves that $A$ is a <strictly monotone operator>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

We prove existence by the <direct method in the calculus of variations>, making the step with <weakly compact sets> explicit. For $X=W_0^{1,4}(\Omega)$ and $f\in L^{8/7}(\Omega)$, introduce the <functional>
$$
E(u)=\frac14\|\nabla u\|_4^4+\frac18\|u\|_8^8-\int_\Omega fu\,dx.
$$
It is finite by part (b)(i). <Hölder's inequality> and the <Sobolev embedding theorem> give
$$
E(u)\geq\frac14\|u\|_X^4-C_8\|f\|_{8/7}\|u\|_X\longrightarrow+\infty
\quad\text{as }\|u\|_X\to\infty.
$$
Thus $E$ is a <coercive function>, bounded below, and every <minimizing sequence> is bounded in $X$.

The space $X$ is a <reflexive Banach space>: it is isomorphic to a closed <linear subspace> of the finite product of the <reflexive spaces> $L^4(\Omega)$ recording the <function> and its <weak derivatives>. A bounded <sequence> in a <reflexive Banach space> has a <weakly convergent> <subsequence>. Hence, after passing to a <subsequence>, a <minimizing sequence> satisfies $u_j\rightharpoonup u_*$ in $X$. The continuous <linear maps> $u\mapsto\nabla u$ into $L^4$ and $u\mapsto u$ into $L^8$ preserve <weak convergence>, so
$$
\nabla u_j\rightharpoonup\nabla u_*\text{ in }L^4,
\qquad u_j\rightharpoonup u_*\text{ in }L^8.
$$
The <norm> in any <Banach space> has <weak lower semicontinuity>, since it is the supremum of absolute values of bounded <linear functionals> of <dual norm> at most one, by the <Hahn-Banach theorem>. Therefore the two nonnegative <norm> powers in $E$ have <weak lower semicontinuity>. Also $u\mapsto\int_\Omega fu\,dx$ belongs to $X^*$ and is continuous for <weak convergence>. It follows that
$$
E(u_*)\leq\liminf_j E(u_j)=\inf_{u\in X}E(u).
$$
For any $v\in X$, the real <function> $t\mapsto E(u_*+tv)$ has a minimum at $t=0$. Its <derivative> there is
$$
0=\left.\frac{d}{dt}E(u_*+tv)\right|_{t=0}
=\int_\Omega|\nabla u_*|^2\nabla u_*\cdot\nabla v\,dx
+\int_\Omega u_*^7v\,dx-\int_\Omega fv\,dx.
$$
This differentiation is justified directly by finite polynomial expansions: the fourth power of the <Euclidean norm> of $\nabla u_*+t\nabla v$ and the eighth power of $u_*+tv$ have integrable coefficients by <Hölder's inequality>. Thus $A(u_*)=0$, giving existence of a <weak solution>.

If $u,w$ are two <weak solutions>, testing the difference by $u-w$ and using part (b)(iii) gives
$$
0=\langle A(u)-A(w),u-w\rangle\geq\tfrac14\|u-w\|_X^4.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{For every }f\in L^{8/7}(\Omega),\text{ there exists exactly one weak solution }u_*\in W_0^{1,4}(\Omega).}
$$
No sign assumption on $f$ or on the <weak solution> is required.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Take the <functional>
$$
\boxed{J(u)=\int_\Omega\left(\frac14|\nabla u|^4+\frac18|u|^8-fu\right)dx,
\qquad u\in W_0^{1,4}(\Omega).}
$$
The factor $1/8$ is essential: the <derivative> of $|s|^8/8=s^8/8$ is $s^7$, while the <gradient> with respect to $\xi$ of $|\xi|^4/4$ is $|\xi|^2\xi$. As in part (c), finite polynomial expansions and <Hölder's inequality> justify differentiation along every direction $v\in X$, giving $DJ(u)[v]=\langle A(u),v\rangle$.

We prove the global and strict minimum directly. The <strictly convex functions> $\Phi(\xi)=|\xi|^4/4$ and $\Psi(s)=|s|^8/8$ satisfy their tangent inequalities
$$
\Phi(\eta)\geq\Phi(\xi)+|\xi|^2\xi\cdot(\eta-\xi),
\qquad
\Psi(t)\geq\Psi(s)+s^7(t-s),
$$
with strict inequality when the respective arguments differ. For example, strict <convexity> of $\Phi$ follows from strict <operator monotonicity> of its <gradient>, proved by the midpoint identity in part (b)(iii); strict <convexity> of $\Psi$ follows because its <derivative> $s^7$ is strictly increasing.

Apply these inequalities pointwise to $\nabla u_*,\nabla v$ and $u_*,v$, then integrate. The <weak formulation> at $u_*$ with <test function> $v-u_*$ gives
$$
J(v)-J(u_*)\geq\langle A(u_*),v-u_*\rangle=0.
$$
If $v\ne u_*$ in $X$, the <Poincaré inequality> implies $\nabla v\ne\nabla u_*$ on a set of positive <Lebesgue measure>. The first tangent inequality is strict on that set and the second is nonnegative everywhere. Their integrable remainders therefore have strictly positive total <integral>. Hence
$$
\boxed{J(v)>J(u_*)\quad\text{for every }v\in X\setminus\{u_*\}.}
$$
Thus $u_*$ is the unique global <minimizer> of $J$. Conversely, every <minimizer> has zero directional <derivative> in every direction and so satisfies $A(u)=0$, which also identifies it with the unique <weak solution> from part (c).
