= Module theory

Module theory studies <abelian groups> with <scalar multiplication> by a <ring>. It extends <linear algebra>: a <module> over a <field> is a <vector space>, while <ring> scalars let the same language describe <integer> arithmetic and <linear operators>. The new phenomena are visible before any abstract classification: some nonzero scalars cannot be divided out, some <modules> have <torsion elements of a module>, and some have no <basis of a module>.

\Image[module-lattice-and-torsion.png]
{title=Free integer coordinates and a cyclic module with torsion}
{description=The <free module> $\mathbb Z^2$ has two independent <integer> directions. In the <cyclic module> $\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z$, repeatedly adding $\overline1$ returns to zero after six steps: $6\overline1=\overline0$. The circle records addition by the generator; its geometry is only a drawing convention.}

The distinction is useful because an <integer> relation such as $6m=0$ carries information which disappears if one assumes all nonzero scalars have <multiplicative inverses>. A <finitely generated module> over a <principal ideal domain> can be separated into a <finite free module> and a <torsion module>; the <structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain> unifies the classification of <finitely generated abelian groups> with the study of <linear operators> through <polynomial rings>. See https://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/linmultialg/modulesoverPID.pdf[Keith Conrad's Modules over a PID, Theorem 4.1 and the operator applications].

A historical anchor is Emmy Noether's 1921 paper https://gdz.sub.uni-goettingen.de/id/PPN235181684_0083[Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen, Mathematische Annalen 83, pages 24–66]{ref}. Its first section studies <rings> and <ideals> under the condition that every <ideal> is a <finitely generated module> over its <ring>, and connects this to the <ascending chain condition>; later sections develop <primary decomposition>. This is an early source for the structural viewpoint underlying <Noetherian rings>. https://arxiv.org/abs/1401.2577[Daniel Berlyne's translation of Noether's original paper]{ref} makes those statements accessible in English.

= Faithful module
{parent=Module theory}
{title2=$\operatorname{Ann}_R(M)=0$}

A <module> is faithful when its <annihilator of a module> is zero. Equivalently, the action <ring homomorphism> $R\to\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb Z}(M)$ to the <endomorphism ring> of its additive <abelian group> is <injective>: every nonzero scalar acts nontrivially somewhere on $M$. A <primitive ring> has a faithful <simple module>.

= Left regular module
{parent=Module theory}
{title2=${}_AA$}

The left regular <module> of a unital <associative algebra> $A$ is its underlying additive group with $A$ acting by left multiplication. Its <submodules> are exactly the <left ideals> of $A$. The <associative algebra> is a <semisimple algebra> precisely when this <module> is a <semisimple module>.

= Vanishing of infinitely divisible integer sequences
{parent=Module theory}
{title2=$\bigcap_{i\geq0}2^i\prod_{j\in J}\mathbb Z=\{0\}$}

If an element $w$ of the <module> $\prod_{j\in J}\mathbb Z$ is divisible by every power of two within that <module>, then every coordinate $w(j)$ is an <integer> divisible by every power of two. A nonzero <integer> $a$ cannot be divisible by $2^i>|a|$, so each coordinate is zero and $w=0$. This argument applies to any index set $J$ and to powers of any fixed integer $b$ with $|b|>1$.

= Module of integer sequences
{parent=Module theory}
{title2=$\prod_{j\in\mathbb N_0}\mathbb Z$}

The module of integer sequences consists of all <functions> $\mathbb N_0\to\mathbb Z$, with pointwise addition and integer scalar multiplication. Its <submodule> of finitely supported sequences is the <direct sum of modules> $\bigoplus_{j\geq0}\mathbb Z$, a <free module> with coordinate <basis of a module> $(e_j)$. The full product is not a <free module>.

To prove this, suppose it had a <basis of a module> $E$. The <countable basis support lemma> places every finitely supported sequence in a <submodule> $C$ generated by a <countable> subset of $E$. The <basis partition gives a module direct sum> lemma supplies a projection $\pi$ onto its complementary <direct summand>, with <kernel> $C$. By <countability of countably generated abelian groups>, $C$ is <countable>. The <Cantor diagonal argument> makes the family of all sequences $w(j)=\pm2^j$ <uncountable>, so choose one with $\pi(w)\ne0$. Truncating $w$ before coordinate $i$ writes $w=f_i+2^ig_i$, where $f_i$ is finitely supported and $g_i$ is an integer sequence. Thus $\pi(w)=2^i\pi(g_i)$ for every $i$. The <vanishing of infinitely divisible integer sequences> forces $\pi(w)=0$, a contradiction.

= Direct sum of modules
{parent=Module theory}
{title2=$\bigoplus_{j\in J}M_j$}

The direct sum of a family of <modules> over the same <ring> consists of all tuples $(m_j)$ with $m_j\in M_j$ and only finitely many nonzero coordinates, with coordinatewise addition and scalar multiplication. For two <submodules> $N,P\subseteq M$, an internal direct sum $M=N\oplus P$ means $M=N+P$ and $N\cap P=\{0\}$. Equivalently, every element of $M$ is uniquely $n+p$: equality of two such expressions puts their difference in $N\cap P$. The projection $n+p\mapsto p$ is a <module homomorphism> with <kernel> $N$, image $P$, and square equal to itself.

= Finite representation type
{parent=Module theory}

A finite-dimensional <algebra> has finite representation type when there are only finitely many isomorphism classes of finite-dimensional <indecomposable modules>. This counts module types, not the dimensions or the number of direct sums formed from them. For a <block of a group algebra>, finite representation type is equivalent to a cyclic <defect group of a block>.

= Auslander–Reiten quiver
{c}
{parent=Module theory}

For a finite-dimensional <algebra>, the Auslander–Reiten quiver has one vertex for each isomorphism class of finite-dimensional <indecomposable modules>, and its arrows record irreducible module homomorphisms. A homomorphism is irreducible when it is neither a split injection nor a split surjection and every factorization forces the first map to split injectively or the second to split surjectively.

= Stable Auslander–Reiten quiver
{parent=Auslander–Reiten quiver}
{title2=$\Gamma_s(A)$}

For a self-injective finite-dimensional <algebra>, remove the projective vertices from its <Auslander–Reiten quiver>. The resulting stable quiver has vertices the nonprojective indecomposable modules. Its translation sends the end term of an almost-split exact sequence to its start term. In particular a quiver of shape $\mathbb ZA_s/\langle\tau^e\rangle$ has $es$ vertices.

= Topological group module
{parent=Module theory}

A topological <abelian group> $A$ with a continuous action of a <topological group> $G$ by group <automorphisms>. Continuity means joint continuity of $G\times A\to A$. For a discrete coefficient group this is equivalent to every element of $A$ having an open stabilizer.

= Character module
{parent=Module theory}
{title2=$M^*=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb Z}(M,\mathbb Q/\mathbb Z)$}

= Character dual
{synonym}

For a <module> $M$ over a commutative <ring> $A$, its character module is the additive-group dual into $D=\mathbb Q/\mathbb Z$, with action $(a\phi)(m)=\phi(am)$. The group $D$ is an <injective cogenerator of abelian groups>. The natural adjunction $\operatorname{Hom}_A(N,M^*)\cong\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb Z}(N\otimes_AM,D)$ sends $h$ to the balanced pairing $(n,m)\mapsto h(n)(m)$.

= Representation of an associative algebra
{parent=Module theory}
{title2=$\rho:A\to\operatorname{End}_k(V)$}

= Algebra representation
{synonym}

An algebra representation is a unital <algebra homomorphism> into the <endomorphisms> of a <vector space>. It is equivalent to making that <vector space> a left <module> over the algebra, with $av=\rho(a)v$.

= Representation of a Banach algebra
{parent=Representation of an associative algebra}
{title2=$\pi:A\to\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb C}(X)$}

A representation of a complex <Banach algebra> is a complex-linear multiplicative action on a <vector space>. For a <unital> action require $\pi(1)=I$; a nonunital algebra can be handled by extending the action to its <unitization>. A nonzero action is algebraically irreducible when it has no nonzero proper invariant <vector> subspace, equivalently when it defines a <simple module>.

= Cyclic representation of a C-star algebra
{parent=Representation of a Banach algebra}
{title2=$\overline{\pi(A)\xi}=H$}

A representation of a <C-star algebra> on a <Hilbert space> is a star-homomorphism $\pi:A\to\mathcal B(H)$. It is cyclic if some <vector> $\xi$ has dense orbit span as displayed; the orbit $\pi(A)\xi$ is itself a <linear subspace>. For a unital representation, a unit cyclic <vector> defines a <state on a C-star algebra> by $a\mapsto\langle\pi(a)\xi,\xi\rangle$. Conversely, the <Gelfand–Naimark–Segal construction> realizes every state this way. Cyclicity means density under the action of the whole algebra, not necessarily under powers of a single chosen element.

= Algebraically irreducible representation of a Banach algebra
{parent=Representation of a Banach algebra}

= Strictly irreducible representation of a Banach algebra
{synonym}

Algebraic irreducibility excludes every proper nonzero invariant <vector subspace>, including those that are not <closed>. It is stronger than topological irreducibility, which excludes only <closed> <invariant subspaces>. For a nonzero irreducible action, every nonzero <vector> is cyclic in the algebraic sense: $A\xi=X$.

= Countable annihilation sequence in an irreducible Banach module
{parent=Algebraically irreducible representation of a Banach algebra}
{title2=$\pi(a_m\cdots a_1)\xi_n=0\ (m>n)$}

For countably many <linearly independent> <vectors> in an infinite-dimensional irreducible complex Banach module, there are elements $a_n$ satisfying the displayed annihilation conditions while the tail images $\{\pi(a_n\cdots a_1)\xi_j:j\ge n\}$ remain <linearly independent>. Use the <quotient norm on an irreducible Banach module> to make each finite annihilator a <closed> Banach subspace. The <Jacobson density theorem> makes each condition of independence of finitely many tail images <open> and dense there; the <Baire category theorem> intersects these countably many conditions. This is stronger than prescribing only finitely many images once.

= Quotient norm on an irreducible Banach module
{parent=Algebraically irreducible representation of a Banach algebra}
{title2=$\|\pi(a)\xi\|_0=\inf_{b\in L_\xi}\|a+b\|$}

For a <unital> irreducible action and $\xi\ne0$, the annihilator $L_\xi=\{a:\pi(a)\xi=0\}$ is a <maximal left ideal> and is <closed>. Thus $A/L_\xi$ supplies the displayed complete <norm> on the module, with contractive action. Every <module endomorphism> is bounded for this quotient <norm>: its value at the cyclic <vector> is $a\xi$, so it is induced by bounded right multiplication by $a$ on the quotient. The <Gelfand-Mazur theorem> then makes the module's commutant consist of scalar operators. This constructs one compatible <norm>; it does not by itself establish <continuity> in a different preassigned <norm>.

= Normed representation of a Banach algebra
{parent=Representation of a Banach algebra}
{title2=$\pi(A)\subseteq\mathcal B(X)$}

A normed representation acts by individually <bounded operators> on a <normed vector space>. This condition alone does not assume that $a\mapsto\pi(a)$ is <continuous> in the <operator norm>, nor that the representation space is complete. A <continuous> representation has a common bound $\|\pi(a)\|\le C\|a\|$.

= Johnson's continuity theorem for irreducible normed representations
{parent=Normed representation of a Banach algebra}
{c}
{title2=$\|\pi(a)\|\le C\|a\|$}

Every <algebraically irreducible representation of a Banach algebra> that is normed is <continuous>. The <continuous> orbit <vectors> form an <invariant subspace>. If all nonzero orbit maps were discontinuous, apply the <countable annihilation sequence in an irreducible Banach module> and the <gliding-hump continuity principle> to right multiplication on the algebra and evaluation of the operator-valued representation. Off-diagonal maps vanish, whereas diagonal maps are discontinuous, a contradiction. Once every orbit map is <continuous>, the <uniform boundedness principle> on the <Banach algebra> gives the common operator-norm bound. Completing the original normed representation space supplies the Banach operator space needed in the argument.

= Degeneration of a module
{parent=Module theory}
{title2=$X\rightsquigarrow Y$}

= Module degeneration
{synonym}

A finite-dimensional <module> $X$ degenerates to $Y$ when $Y$ has a representative in the closure of $X$'s change-of-basis orbit in the <representation variety of an associative algebra>. The full orbit of $Y$ then lies in that closure. The relation is transitive because orbit closures are closed and invariant under change of basis. A <split extension as a degeneration> shows that a module degenerates to the direct sum of its composition factors.

= Hereditary ring
{parent=Module theory}

A ring is left hereditary if every submodule of a left <projective module> is projective. Equivalently, every left module has a <projective resolution> of length at most one. The <standard projective resolution of a quiver representation>, used also for arbitrary infinite-dimensional modules, proves that <path algebras> are left hereditary.

= Basic hereditary algebra path-algebra theorem
{parent=Hereditary ring}
{title2=$A\cong kQ$}

Let $A$ be a finite-dimensional <basic algebra> over an <algebraically closed field> and a <hereditary ring>. The radicals of its indecomposable projectives are projective and satisfy $JP_i\cong\bigoplus_jP_j^{a_{ij}}$, where $a_{ij}$ are the <Ext quiver> arrow multiplicities. Hence $\dim P_i=1+\sum_ja_{ij}\dim P_j$, forcing strict dimension decrease along arrows and therefore acyclicity. Lifting an arrow basis in $J/J^2$ gives a surjective <path algebra> homomorphism $kQ\to A$. The number of paths starting at $i$ satisfies the same dimension recursion. Equality of total dimensions proves the homomorphism is an isomorphism. Sums of vertex <idempotents> over connected components are precisely the primitive <central idempotents>, so components give the blocks.

= Path algebras are hereditary
{parent=Hereditary ring}
{title2=$\operatorname{Ext}_{kQ}^{n}(V,W)=0\quad(n\ge2)$}

The <standard projective resolution of a quiver representation> has length one, even for quivers with oriented cycles. Thus every <path algebra> is a <hereditary ring>, and higher <extension groups> vanish. Applying a <long exact sequence of Ext groups> to $0\to I\to K\to C\to0$ gives a surjection $\operatorname{Ext}^1(K,W)\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{Ext}^1(I,W)$, since the next term $\operatorname{Ext}^2(C,W)$ is zero. This is the hereditary step in the <Ringel lemma on bricks>.

= Regular sequence on a module
{parent=Module theory}
{title2=$t_i:M/(t_1,\ldots,t_{i-1})M\hookrightarrow M/(t_1,\ldots,t_{i-1})M$}

For central elements, each multiplication map on the preceding quotient must be injective; a usual convention also requires the final quotient to be nonzero. The augmented <Koszul complex on central ring elements> tensored with $M$ is then a <quasi-isomorphism> to the final quotient in degree zero.

= Composition series of a module
{parent=Module theory}
{title2=$0=M_0\subset M_1\subset\cdots\subset M_r=M$}

A composition series of a <module> is a finite chain of <submodules> whose nonzero successive <quotient modules> are <simple modules>. Every finite-dimensional <Lie algebra representation> has such a chain, viewed as a <module> over the <universal enveloping algebra>.

= Jordan–Hölder theorem for modules
{c}
{parent=Composition series of a module}

Every two finite <composition series of a module> have the same multiset of simple <composition factors>, up to isomorphism and order. One proves this by induction on the length: if the first simple submodules differ, their intersection is zero, and the quotient by their sum identifies the remaining common comparison problem; if they coincide, pass directly to the quotient. The theorem makes the multiplicity of a simple module independent of a chosen composition series. It does not assert that the module is a direct sum of its composition factors.

= Composition factor
{parent=Composition series of a module}
{title2=$M_i/M_{i-1}$}

A composition factor is an irreducible successive quotient in a <composition series of a module>. For a central <Casimir operator>, its scalar on a composition factor is an <eigenvalue> of the operator on the original <Lie algebra representation>.

= Uniform module
{parent=Module theory}
{wiki}

A nonzero <module> is uniform if every pair of nonzero <submodules> has nonzero intersection. Equivalently, every nonzero <submodule> is an <essential submodule>. A <right Noetherian domain> is uniform as a right <module>: if $aA\cap bA=0$ for nonzero $a,b$, the <right ideals> $b^iaA$, $i\geq0$, form an infinite <direct sum>, contradicting the <ascending chain condition>.

= Uniform dimension
{parent=Uniform module}
{title2=$\operatorname{udim}M$}

The uniform dimension of a <module> is the supremum of the numbers of nonzero <submodules> that can occur in an internal <direct sum>. Finite uniform dimension is equivalent to having no infinite internal <direct sum> of nonzero <submodules>. A finite <direct sum> of <uniform modules> contained as an <essential submodule> gives that number as the dimension. The <finite uniform decomposition of a Noetherian module> therefore gives finite uniform dimension.

= Finite uniform decomposition of a Noetherian module
{parent=Uniform module}

Every nonzero <Noetherian module> contains a <uniform module>: otherwise repeatedly split a nonuniform nonzero submodule into two disjoint nonzero submodules, retaining one and collecting the other, to build an infinite direct sum. Repeatedly adding a uniform submodule disjoint from the current finite sum must likewise stop. The final finite sum is an <essential submodule>, since any disjoint nonzero submodule would allow another addition.

= Indecomposable module
{parent=Module theory}
{wiki}

A nonzero <module> is indecomposable if it cannot be expressed as a <direct sum> of two nonzero <submodules>. The zero <module> is excluded. A <module> of finite <composition length> is indecomposable exactly when its <endomorphism ring> has no <idempotents> other than zero and one: an <idempotent> splits the <module> into its image and <kernel>.

= Top of an indecomposable projective module
{parent=Indecomposable module}
{title2=$P/PJ(A)$}

For a nonzero finitely generated <indecomposable module> $P$ that is a <projective module> over a <right Artinian ring> $A$, its top $P/PJ(A)$ is a <simple module>. The <Jacobson radical> $J(A)$ is a <nilpotent ideal>, so the top is nonzero; the quotient is a <semisimple module> over the <semisimple ring> $A/J(A)$. If it decomposed, a nontrivial <idempotent> of its <endomorphism ring> would lift by projectivity to an <endomorphism> of $P$. The <Hopkins-Levitzki theorem> gives finite <composition length>, and the <Fitting lemma> makes the lift either invertible or nilpotent. Its induced map on the top would have the same property, impossible for that nontrivial <idempotent>.

= Fitting lemma
{c}
{parent=Module theory}
{wiki}

For an endomorphism $f$ of a finite-length module $M$, sufficiently large $n$ gives
$$
M=\ker(f^n)\oplus\operatorname{im}(f^n).
$$
If $M$ is indecomposable, every endomorphism is therefore either invertible or nilpotent, so its endomorphism ring is local.

= Finite-dimensional Fitting decomposition
{parent=Fitting lemma}
{title2=$V=\operatorname{im}h^n\oplus\ker h^n,\quad n=\dim V$}

Every <linear endomorphism> of a finite-dimensional <vector space> admits the displayed <direct sum of vector spaces>. It separates the part on which repeated application is invertible from the part eventually sent to zero.

**Proof.** <Stabilization of images of powers of a linear map> gives $\operatorname{im}h^n=\operatorname{im}h^{2n}$. The <rank-nullity theorem> and nested <null spaces> give $\ker h^n=\ker h^{2n}$. If $v=h^nu$ also satisfies $h^nv=0$, then $u\in\ker h^{2n}=\ker h^n$, so $v=0$. Thus the displayed summands have zero <intersection>. Their <dimensions> sum to $n$ by the <rank-nullity theorem>, proving the decomposition.

Both summands are preserved by $h$. On $\operatorname{im}h^n$, stabilization makes $h$ a <surjective linear map>, hence invertible in finite <dimension>. On $\ker h^n$, its $n$th power is zero, so the restriction is a <nilpotent linear map>.

= Krull–Schmidt theorem
{c}
{parent=Module theory}
{wiki}

The Krull–Schmidt theorem says that a finite-length module decomposes as a finite direct sum of indecomposable modules, uniquely up to permutation and isomorphism of the summands.

= Krull-Schmidt decomposition
{parent=Krull–Schmidt theorem}
{c}
{title2=$X\cong\bigoplus_aM_a^{\oplus m_a}$}

A finite-dimensional module is $\bigoplus_aM_a^{\oplus m_a}$ with pairwise nonisomorphic indecomposables and uniquely determined multiplicities. The <Fitting lemma> makes their endomorphism rings local. The multiplicity spaces provide the general linear factors in the <Levi decomposition of a quiver automorphism group>.

= Dual module

{parent=Module theory}
{wiki}

For an $R$-module $M$, its dual module is $M^*=\operatorname{Hom}_R(M,R)$, with pointwise addition and scalar multiplication.

= Double dual module
{title2=$M^{**}$}
{parent=Dual module}

The double dual module is $M^{**}=\operatorname{Hom}_R(M^*,R)$. The <evaluation homomorphism> sends $m\in M$ to the functional $f\mapsto f(m)$.

= Finitely generated module
{parent=Module theory}

An $R$-module is finitely generated when there are finitely many elements $m_1,\ldots,m_r$ such that every element is an $R$-linear combination of them.

= Finite generation is preserved by module extensions
{parent=Finitely generated module}
{title2=$N,\ M/N\text{ finitely generated}\implies M\text{ finitely generated}$}

If a <submodule> $N\subseteq M$ and the <quotient module> $M/N$ are <finitely generated modules>, then $M$ is a <finitely generated module>. Choose finitely many generators of $N$ and lift finitely many generators of $M/N$. For any $m\in M$, subtract an $R$-linear combination of the lifts to obtain an element of $N$, then express this using the generators of $N$. The union of the two finite lists generates $M$.

= Minimal number of generators of a module
{parent=Finitely generated module}
{title2=$\mu_A(M)=\dim_{A/\mathfrak m}(M/\mathfrak mM)\quad(M\text{ finite},\ A\text{ local})$}

For a <module> $M$, $\mu_A(M)$ is the least cardinality of a generating set, equivalently the least size of a <basis> of a <free module> surjecting onto $M$. If $(A,\mathfrak m)$ is a <local ring> and $M$ is finitely generated, then $\mu_A(M)=\dim_{A/\mathfrak m}(M/\mathfrak mM)$. Any generating set spans this quotient, proving the lower bound. Lift a <basis> of the quotient and let $J$ be the <submodule> generated by those lifts. Then $N=M/J$ is finitely generated and satisfies $N=\mathfrak mN$. For generators $n_i$ write $n_i=\sum_jc_{ij}n_j$ with $c_{ij}\in\mathfrak m$. The adjugate of $I-C$ shows that $\det(I-C)$ annihilates $N$; this determinant is one modulo $\mathfrak m$ and hence a <unit>. Thus $N=0$, giving the upper bound.

= Clearing denominators relative to an independent module subset
{parent=Finitely generated module}
{title2=$0\ne r\in R,\qquad rM\subseteq\langle T\rangle$}

Let a <finitely generated module> over an <integral domain> have a finite generating set $S$ and a maximal independent subset $T$. For each $s\notin T$, dependence of $T\cup\{s\}$ gives a nonzero coefficient $a_s$ with $a_ss\in\langle T\rangle$. The product of those coefficients is nonzero and carries the whole <module> into $\langle T\rangle$.

= Finite generation as a quotient of a finite free module
{parent=Finitely generated module}

An <R-module> is finitely generated exactly when it is the image of a <surjection> from a finite <free module> $R^n$. Given generators $m_i$, send $(r_i)$ to $\sum_i r_im_i$; conversely, the images of the standard free generators generate every such image. This criterion does not assert that the <module> is free: a quotient can have nontrivial relations.

= Artinian module
{c}
{parent=Module theory}
{wiki}

An $R$-module is Artinian when every descending chain of submodules stabilizes. Equivalently, every nonempty family of submodules has a minimal member.

= Artinian modules are co-Hopfian
{c}
{parent=Artinian module}
{title2=$f\text{ injective}\Longrightarrow f\text{ surjective}$}

For an injective <module endomorphism> $f:M\to M$ of an <Artinian module>, the chain $M\supseteq fM\supseteq f^2M\supseteq\cdots$ stabilizes. If $f^nM=f^{n+1}M$, then every $x$ satisfies $f^nx=f^{n+1}y$. Injectivity of $f^n$ gives $x=fy$, proving surjectivity. No finite-length or Noetherian hypothesis is needed.

= Artinian modules in a short exact sequence
{c}
{parent=Artinian module}

For a <short exact sequence> $0\to N\to M\to Q\to0$, the middle <module> is <Artinian> exactly when both outer <modules> are <Artinian>. Submodules and quotients inherit the <descending chain condition>. Conversely, for a descending chain $M_i$, both $M_i\cap N$ and its image in $Q$ eventually stabilize. If $x\in M_i$, choose $y\in M_{i+1}$ with the same image in $Q$; then $x-y\in M_i\cap N=M_{i+1}\cap N$, giving $M_i=M_{i+1}$. Induction shows that finite direct sums of <Artinian modules> are <Artinian>.

= Filtered colimit of modules

= Filtered colimit
{synonym}

A filtered colimit of modules is a direct limit indexed by a filtered category. Filtered colimits preserve exact sequences, and tensor products commute with them.

= Direct system of abelian groups
{parent=Filtered colimit of modules}

A direct system indexed by a <directed set> $A$ consists of <abelian groups> $G_a$ and homomorphisms $\phi_{ab}:G_a\to G_b$ for $a\leq b$, with $\phi_{aa}=1$ and $\phi_{bc}\phi_{ab}=\phi_{ac}$.

= Direct limit of abelian groups
{title2=$\varinjlim_aG_a$}
{parent=Direct system of abelian groups}

= Direct limit
{synonym}

The direct limit of a <direct system of abelian groups> is the quotient of $\bigoplus_aG_a$ by the relations identifying $g\in G_a$ with $\phi_{ab}(g)\in G_b$ whenever $a\leq b$.

= Sequential direct limit of multiplication maps on the integers
{title2=$\varinjlim(\mathbb Z\xrightarrow{a_0}\mathbb Z\xrightarrow{a_1}\cdots)$}
{parent=Direct limit of abelian groups}

For nonzero integers $a_i$ and $P_n=a_0\cdots a_{n-1}$, the compatible embeddings of stage $n$ into $\mathbb Q$ send $m$ to $m/P_n$. They identify the direct limit with
$$
\bigcup_{n\geq0}P_n^{-1}\mathbb Z\subseteq\mathbb Q.
$$

= Rational group with square-free denominators
{title2=$\mathbb Q_{\mathrm{sq}}$}
{parent=Sequential direct limit of multiplication maps on the integers}

The additive group $\mathbb Q_{\mathrm{sq}}$ consists of the <rational numbers> whose reduced denominators are <square-free integers>. If $p_0,p_1,\ldots$ lists the primes without repetition, then
$$
\mathbb Q_{\mathrm{sq}}\cong\varinjlim\left(\mathbb Z\xrightarrow{p_0}\mathbb Z\xrightarrow{p_1}\mathbb Z\xrightarrow{p_2}\cdots\right).
$$

= Bimodule

An $(R,S)$-bimodule is simultaneously a left $R$-module and a right $S$-module, with commuting scalar actions $(rm)s=r(ms)$.

= Invariant submodule

For a group $G$ acting on a module $M$, the invariant submodule is $M^G=\{m\in M:g\cdot m=m\text{ for all }g\in G\}$.

= Coinvariant module
{title2=$M_G$}
{parent=Module theory}

For a group $G$ acting on a module $M$, the coinvariant module is
$$
M_G=M/\langle g\cdot m-m:g\in G,\ m\in M\rangle.
$$

= Abelianization over a Zp-extension
{parent=Coinvariant module}
{title2=$\mathcal G^{\mathrm{ab}}:\quad X_\Gamma=X/(\gamma-1)X$}

Suppose $1\to X\to\mathcal G\to\Gamma\to1$ is an exact sequence of <pro-p groups>, with $X$ abelian and $\Gamma\cong\mathbb Z_p$. Conjugation gives $X$ a <compact Galois module> structure. The closed <commutator subgroup> is $(\gamma-1)X$: its image is closed by compactness, and after quotienting by it, a lift of $\gamma$ centralizes $X$ and topologically generates the remaining quotient. Hence $0\to X_\Gamma\to\mathcal G^{\mathrm{ab}}\to\Gamma\to0$ is exact.

= Support of a module
{parent=Module theory}

The support of an $R$-module $M$ is
$$
\operatorname{Supp}_R(M)=\{\mathfrak p\in\operatorname{Spec}R:M_{\mathfrak p}\ne0\}.
$$
For a nonzero finitely generated module, the support is nonempty and contains a maximal ideal.

= Tensor product of modules
{title2=$M\otimes_RN$}
{parent=Module theory}
{wiki}

The tensor product of an $R$-module $M$ and an $R$-module $N$ is an $R$-module $M\otimes_RN$ generated by pure tensors $m\otimes n$, subject to additivity in both variables and the balancing relation
$$
(rm)\otimes n=m\otimes(rn).
$$

= Right exactness of the tensor product
{parent=Tensor product of modules}
{title2=$U\otimes_AE\longrightarrow V\otimes_AE\longrightarrow W\otimes_AE\longrightarrow0$}

For a <short exact sequence> $0\to U\xrightarrow{i}V\xrightarrow{q}W\to0$ and any <module> $E$, the sequence $U\otimes_AE\to V\otimes_AE\to W\otimes_AE\to0$ is exact. The last map is surjective since every pure tensor $w\otimes e$ can be lifted using a preimage of $w$. Put $T=(V\otimes_AE)/\operatorname{im}(i\otimes1_E)$. There is a well-defined balanced map $W\times E\to T$, sending $(q(v),e)$ to the class of $v\otimes e$: replacing $v$ by $v+i(u)$ changes it by an element of the discarded image. This induces $W\otimes_AE\to T$, inverse to the map induced by $q\otimes1_E$. Thus the kernel of $q\otimes1_E$ is exactly the image of $i\otimes1_E$. Consequently a module is flat exactly when tensoring with it also preserves injections.

= Change-of-rings tensor quotient
{parent=Tensor product of modules}
{title2=$M\otimes_RN\twoheadrightarrow M\otimes_AN$}

For a homomorphism of <commutative rings> $R\to A$ and two $A$-<modules>, the <tensor product of modules> over $A$ is the quotient of the <tensor product of modules> over $R$ imposing all relations $am\otimes n=m\otimes an$. The quotient map is $A$-linear when $A$ acts through the first factor on the source. This expresses the extra balancing imposed by <change of rings>.

= Tensor product of commutative algebras
{parent=Tensor product of modules}
{title2=$A\otimes_RB$}

For commutative $R$-algebras, their module <tensor product> becomes a commutative algebra by $(a\otimes b)(a'\otimes b')=aa'\otimes bb'$. Its <universal property> is that pairs of compatible $R$-algebra maps from $A$ and $B$ into a commutative algebra $C$ correspond to maps $A\otimes_RB\to C$. This coproduct of algebras gives, contravariantly, the <fibre product of schemes> on affine charts.

= Tensor product of field extensions is nonzero
{parent=Tensor product of commutative algebras}
{title2=$L\otimes_KL^{\prime}\ne0$}

If $L,L^{\prime}$ are <field extensions> of $K$, choose a $K$-basis of $L$ containing $1$. After tensoring with $L^{\prime}$, that basis expresses $L\otimes_KL^{\prime}$ as a nonzero direct sum of copies of $L^{\prime}$, and $1\otimes1$ is nonzero. A <prime ideal> of this nonzero ring supplies a point used in the <point-lifting property of a scheme fibre product>.

= Pure tensor
{parent=Tensor product of modules}
{wiki}

A pure tensor is an element of a tensor product that can be written as $m\otimes n$. General tensors are finite sums of pure tensors and need not themselves be pure.

= Projectivity of factors of a nonzero finite free tensor product
{parent=Tensor product of modules}

If $M\otimes_RN\cong R^n$ for a positive integer $n$, then both $M$ and $N$ are <projective modules>. Choose a finite expression for the inverse image of one basis vector. It produces a split surjection $N^r\to R$; tensoring the splitting with $M$ exhibits $M$ as a direct summand of the finite free module $(M\otimes_RN)^r$. Symmetry gives the result for $N$.

= Tensor-nilpotent module
{parent=Tensor product of modules}

An $R$-module $M$ is tensor-nilpotent when $M^{\otimes k}=0$ for some positive integer $k$. A nonzero finitely generated module cannot be tensor-nilpotent: localize at a maximal ideal in its support and reduce modulo that maximal ideal, obtaining a nonzero tensor power of a nonzero vector space.

= Balanced map
{parent=Tensor product of modules}

A map $b:M\times N\to P$ is balanced when it is additive in each variable and satisfies $b(rm,n)=b(m,rn)$.

= Universal property of the tensor product of modules
{parent=Tensor product of modules}

Every balanced map $b:M\times N\to P$ factors uniquely through an $R$-module homomorphism $\widetilde b:M\otimes_RN\to P$ satisfying $\widetilde b(m\otimes n)=b(m,n)$.

= Tensor-hom adjunction
{parent=Tensor product of modules}
{wiki}

For modules over a commutative ring, there is a natural isomorphism
$$
\operatorname{Hom}_R(M\otimes_RN,P)
\cong
\operatorname{Hom}_R\bigl(M,\operatorname{Hom}_R(N,P)\bigr),
$$
given by $f\mapsto(m\mapsto(n\mapsto f(m\otimes n)))$.

= Tensor product and infinite direct product
{parent=Tensor product of modules}

Tensor product need not commute with an infinite direct product. For example,
$$
\mathbb Q\otimes_{\mathbb Z}\prod_{n\geq2}\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z\ne0,
\qquad
\prod_{n\geq2}\left(\mathbb Q\otimes_{\mathbb Z}\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z\right)=0.
$$
The element $(1\bmod n)_n$ has infinite order in the product, so it survives rational localization.

= Flat module
{parent=Module theory}
{wiki}

= Flat
{synonym}

An $R$-module $M$ is flat when tensoring with it preserves injections, equivalently when $-\otimes_RM$ is an exact functor.

= Flatness criterion over dual numbers
{parent=Flat module}
{title2=$M\text{ flat over }k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)\iff\ker\varepsilon=\varepsilon M$}

For $D=k[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2)$, a <module> $M$ is a <flat module> exactly when $\ker(\varepsilon:M\to M)=\varepsilon M$. Flatness implies this by tensoring the exact sequence $0\to(\varepsilon)\to D\to k\to0$. Conversely lift a $k$-basis of $M/\varepsilon M$ to $M$. Every element is a finite combination of the lifts plus $\varepsilon$ times another such combination, proving generation over $D$. A relation first reduces to zero coefficients modulo $\varepsilon$; the kernel condition then reduces its remaining coefficients to zero as well. The lifts form a free $D$-basis, proving flatness.

= Equational criterion for flatness
{parent=Flat module}
{title2=$\sum_i a_im_i=0\Longrightarrow m_i=\sum_jb_{ij}n_j,\quad\sum_i a_ib_{ij}=0$}

For a <flat module> $M$ over a <commutative ring>, every finite relation among elements of $M$ is a finite combination of relations among elements of the <ring> itself, in the displayed sense. To obtain this, tensor the kernel of the linear map $A^r\to A$, $(u_i)\mapsto\sum_i a_iu_i$, with $M$. Flatness says that this tensor maps onto the kernel in $M^r$. Writing an element of that tensor as a finite sum gives the columns $b_{ij}$ and the elements $n_j$. The converse follows by applying this condition to relations in a <tensor product> to prove that tensoring preserves injections.

= Free modules are flat
{parent=Flat module}
{title2=$U\otimes_AA^{(J)}\cong U^{(J)}$}

For a <free module> $F=\bigoplus_{i\in J}Ae_i$, the natural map $U\otimes_AF\to\bigoplus_{i\in J}U$ sends $u\otimes\sum_i a_ie_i$ to $(a_iu)_i$ and is an <isomorphism>, with inverse sending the tuple $(u_i)$ to $\sum_i u_i\otimes e_i$. For any <injection> $U\hookrightarrow V$, its <tensor product of modules> with $F$ is therefore the coordinatewise <injection> $\bigoplus_JU\hookrightarrow\bigoplus_JV$. Thus every <free module> is flat, including those of infinite rank.

= Direct summands of flat modules are flat
{parent=Flat module}
{title2=$F=P\oplus Q\text{ flat}\implies P,Q\text{ flat}$}

If $F=P\oplus Q$ is a <flat module>, then $P$ and $Q$ are <flat>. For an <injection> $u:U\hookrightarrow V$, distributivity of the <tensor product of modules> identifies $u\otimes F$ with $(u\otimes P)\oplus(u\otimes Q)$. This map is <injective> by flatness of $F$. Each component is therefore <injective>, proving flatness of both summands.

= Flatness criterion using ideals
{parent=Flat module}
{title2=$I\otimes_AM\xrightarrow{\sim}IM\quad\text{for every ideal }I$}

The arrow is the canonical multiplication map, not an arbitrary abstract module isomorphism. Its inclusion into $M$ is $\mu_I:I\otimes_AM\to M$. Dualizing identifies $\mu_I^*$ with the restriction $\operatorname{Hom}_A(A,M^*)\to\operatorname{Hom}_A(I,M^*)$. All these maps are surjective precisely when $M^*$ is injective, by the <Baer criterion>. The <injective cogenerator of abelian groups> detects injectivity of $\mu_I$, and the <Lambek theorem> gives flatness. No Noetherian or finite-generation hypothesis is needed.

= Lambek theorem
{c}
{parent=Flat module}
{title2=$M\text{ flat}\iff M^*\text{ injective}$}

A <module> over a commutative <ring> is <flat> exactly when its <character module> is <injective>. For a submodule inclusion $N'\hookrightarrow N$, the character adjunction identifies restriction $\operatorname{Hom}_A(N,M^*)\to\operatorname{Hom}_A(N',M^*)$ with the dual of $N'\otimes_AM\to N\otimes_AM$. The <injective cogenerator of abelian groups> detects whether the latter map is injective. Thus the two extension/exactness conditions are equivalent.

= Faithfully flat module
{parent=Flat module}
{wiki}

A flat module $M$ is faithfully flat when $N\otimes_RM=0$ implies $N=0$. Every field extension is faithfully flat over its base field.

= Flatness is local
{parent=Flat module}

An $R$-module $M$ is flat if and only if $M_{\mathfrak p}$ is flat over $R_{\mathfrak p}$ for every prime ideal $\mathfrak p$, equivalently for every maximal ideal.

= Torsion-free module over a principal ideal domain is flat
{parent=Flat module}

Over a principal ideal domain, a module is flat exactly when it is torsion-free. Hence the tensor product of two torsion-free modules over a principal ideal domain is torsion-free: both tensor functors are exact, so their composite is exact.

= Flat extension preserves finite ideal intersections
{parent=Flat module}

If an $R$-algebra $A$ is flat as an $R$-module, then for ideals $I,J\subseteq R$,
$$
IA\cap JA=(I\cap J)A.
$$
Tensor the exact sequence $0\to I\cap J\to I\oplus J\to I+J\to0$ with $A$.

= Local tensor nonvanishing criterion
{parent=Flat module}

A nonzero commutative ring $R$ has the property that $M\otimes_RN=0$ forces $M=0$ or $N=0$ exactly when $R$ is local with maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$ and $M=\mathfrak mM$ forces $M=0$ for every $R$-module. Reduction modulo $\mathfrak m$ turns a tensor product into a tensor product of vector spaces.

= Determinant trick
{parent=Module theory}
{wiki}

If a finitely generated faithful module $M$ over a commutative ring $R$ satisfies $xM\subseteq IM$, the determinant of a matrix presenting these relations gives a monic equation
$$
x^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0=0,
\qquad a_i\in I^{n-i}.
$$
In particular, an element of an extension field that preserves a nonzero finitely generated ideal is integral over $R$.

= Short exact sequence
{parent=Module theory}

A short exact sequence is an exact sequence
$$
0\longrightarrow L\xrightarrow{j}M\xrightarrow{q}N\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus $j$ is injective, $q$ is surjective, and $\operatorname{im}j=\ker q$.

= Module extension
{parent=Short exact sequence}
{title2=$0\to A\to B\to C\to0$}

= Extension of modules
{synonym}

A short exact sequence with specified kernel module $A$ and quotient module $C$. Its equivalence class is determined by the degree-one <Ext functor>; equivalence must preserve both end modules.

= Extension of trivial modules for an infinite cyclic group
{parent=Module extension}
{title2=$\operatorname{Ext}_{\mathbb Z[z,z^{-1}]}^1(\mathbb Z^s,\mathbb Z^r)\cong M_{r\times s}(\mathbb Z)$}

With trivial action on both end modules, an extension has underlying abelian group $\mathbb Z^r\oplus\mathbb Z^s$ and generator action $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}I&T\\0&I\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. The integer matrix $T$ is invariant under equivalences fixing the ends. The one-element <projective resolution> with differential $z-1$ computes the same classification over the <group ring>.

= Pushout of a module extension
{parent=Module extension}
{title2=$B_f=(A\prime\oplus B)/\{(f(a),-i(a))\}$}

Pushing an inclusion $i:A\to B$ along $f:A\to A\prime$ gives a new <module extension> with kernel $A\prime$ and the same quotient. This <pushout> realizes the covariance of the degree-one <Ext functor> in its second argument.

= Equivalence of module extensions
{parent=Module extension}
{title2=$(\mathrm{id}_A,\phi,\mathrm{id}_C)$}

A commuting isomorphism between two <module extensions> that is the identity on both end modules. Classifying middle modules up to arbitrary isomorphism is a different and generally coarser problem.

= Split short exact sequence
{parent=Short exact sequence}

A short exact sequence splits when its injection has a retraction, equivalently when its surjection has a section. In that case the middle module is isomorphic to the direct sum of the two outer modules.

= Short five lemma
{parent=Short exact sequence}

The <five lemma> has a short form: in a commutative diagram of short exact sequences, if the maps on both outer terms are isomorphisms, then the map on the middle terms is an isomorphism.

= Inverse system
{parent=Module theory}

An inverse system is a family $(N_i)$ with transition maps $g_{ij}:N_j\to N_i$ for $i\le j$, satisfying $g_{ii}=1$ and $g_{ik}=g_{ij}g_{jk}$.

= Inverse limit
{title2=$\varprojlim N_i$}
{parent=Inverse system}
{wiki}

The inverse limit is the module of compatible families
$$
\varprojlim_iN_i=\{(x_i)\in\prod_iN_i:g_{ij}(x_j)=x_i\}.
$$

= Universal property of an inverse limit
{parent=Inverse limit}

Maps from a module $X$ to $\varprojlim_iN_i$ correspond naturally to compatible families of maps $X\to N_i$.

= Nonemptiness theorem for inverse limits of finite sets
{parent=Inverse limit}

= Nonemptiness theorem for inverse limits of finite set
{synonym}

An inverse system of nonempty finite sets over a directed index set has nonempty inverse limit. Give each set the discrete topology: the compatibility conditions are closed subsets of the compact product, and directedness gives them the <finite intersection property>.

= Filtration of a module
{parent=Module theory}

= Filtrations of a module
{synonym}

A decreasing filtration of a module is a chain $M=M_0\supseteq M_1\supseteq\cdots$ of submodules.

= Quotient filtration
{parent=Filtration of a module}
{title2=$F_i(M/N)=(F_iM+N)/N$}

The quotient filtration records images of the filtered pieces in a <quotient module>. Together with the <subspace filtration>, it gives <exactness of associated graded modules for induced filtrations>.

= Subspace filtration
{parent=Filtration of a module}
{title2=$F_iN=N\cap F_iM$}

A <submodule> of a filtered <module> inherits the displayed filtration by intersection. It is also called the induced filtration.

= Ascending filtration
{parent=Filtration of a module}
{title2=$F_{-1}=0\subseteq F_0\subseteq F_1\subseteq\cdots$}

An ascending filtration is an increasing chain of additive subgroups, or vector subspaces when a scalar field is fixed. It is exhaustive if their union is the whole object. For a <filtered ring>, multiplicative compatibility means $F_iR\,F_jR\subseteq F_{i+j}R$. For an ideal $I$, intersecting each level with $I$ gives the induced filtration. Its <associated graded module> has components $(I\cap F_nR)/(I\cap F_{n-1}R)$.

= Good filtration of a module
{parent=Filtration of a module}

= Good filtrations of a module
{synonym}

= Good filtration
{synonym}

For a <filtered algebra> $A$ with finite-dimensional filtration pieces, a <good filtration of a module> on a <finitely generated module> is compatible with multiplication and has finitely generated <associated graded module> over the <associated graded ring>. Taking bounded-degree translates of a finite set of module generators supplies such a <filtration of a module> when the <associated graded ring> is <Noetherian>.

= Good filtration for the order filtration
{parent=Good filtration of a module}
{title2=$M_j=\sum_{\ell=1}^sF_{j-d_\ell}A_n\,m_\ell$}

= Good filtrations for the order filtration
{synonym}

For the <order filtration of a Weyl algebra>, a <filtration of a module> is good if it is increasing, exhaustive, zero in sufficiently negative degrees, compatible with multiplication, and has <associated graded module> finite over the <associated graded ring>. Equivalently it has the displayed finite shifted-generating description. Choose homogeneous generators of the <associated graded module>, lift them to $m_\ell$, and successively remove the top symbol of any element; the lower bound on the <filtration of a module> makes this process terminate. Conversely, the displayed description makes the symbols of the $m_\ell$ generate. Each $M_j$ is finite over the degree-zero <polynomial ring> $\mathbb C[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$; it need not be a <finite-dimensional vector space> over $\mathbb C$.

= Dimension of a filtered module
{parent=Good filtration of a module}
{title2=$d(M)=\deg\bigl(\dim_k F_jM\bigr)$}

For a <good filtration of a module> over a fixed finite-dimensional <filtration of a ring> whose associated graded algebra is commutative and finitely generated, the cumulative Hilbert function is eventually a <quasipolynomial> by the <Hilbert-Serre theorem>. Its leading asymptotic degree is the dimension of the nonzero filtered <module>. Monotonicity forces the leading coefficients in its residue classes to agree. If the associated graded algebra is a <standard graded algebra>, the Hilbert function is eventually polynomial. Two good module filtrations bound one another after fixed shifts, so they have the same growth degree. Set $d(0)=-\infty$. For the <Bernstein filtration> this is the <Bernstein growth dimension of a Weyl algebra module>.

= Multiplicity of a filtered module
{parent=Dimension of a filtered module}
{title2=$m(M)=d(M)!\,[j^{d(M)}]\dim_k F_jM$}

With the fixed algebra-filtration convention in <dimension of a filtered module>, the multiplicity is factorial times the common leading coefficient of the cumulative Hilbert function for $M\ne0$, and is zero for $M=0$. It is a positive integer with a <standard graded algebra> normalization, and can be a positive rational number for a weighted grading. Fixed shifts between <good filtrations of a module> preserve its leading Hilbert coefficient. Rescaling the algebra filtration itself can change multiplicity, so its normalization must be specified.

= Dimension and multiplicity in a filtered exact sequence
{parent=Multiplicity of a filtered module}
{title2=$d(M)=\max(d(N),d(M/N))$}

The <subspace filtration> and <quotient filtration> in a <short exact sequence> are good when the associated graded algebra is <Noetherian>. Their cumulative Hilbert functions add in every degree. Positive leading coefficients make the degree of the sum the larger degree; when both degrees agree, factorial times the leading coefficients add. Thus equal-dimensional nonzero factors consume positive <multiplicity of a filtered module>. With a <standard graded algebra> normalization these multiplicities are integral.

= Equivalent filtrations of a module
{parent=Filtration of a module}

Two decreasing filtrations $(M_n)$ and $(N_n)$ are equivalent when each contains a fixed shift of the other: $M_{n+a}\subseteq N_n$ and $N_{n+b}\subseteq M_n$ for fixed $a,b$ and every $n$.

The $I$-adic topology on a <module> $M$ has the powers $I^nM$ as a neighbourhood basis of zero. It is generated by the <I-adic filtration>; separatedness means $\bigcap_n I^nM=0$.

= Krull intersection theorem
{c}
{parent=I-adic filtration}
{wiki}

If $R$ is a <Noetherian local ring> with maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$ and $I\subseteq\mathfrak m$, then
$$
\bigcap_{n\geq1}I^n=0.
$$
More generally, the intersection of the powers acts trivially on every finitely generated module.

{c}

For an ideal $I\subseteq R$, the Rees ring is the graded subring
$$
\mathcal R(I)=\bigoplus_{n\geq0}I^nt^n\subseteq R[t].
$$
It packages all powers of $I$ into one algebra and turns stable filtrations into finitely generated graded modules.

= Rees module
{c}
{parent=Rees algebra}

The Rees module of an $R$-<module> $M$ with respect to an <ideal> $I$ is $\mathcal R_I(M)=\bigoplus_{j\ge0}I^jMt^j$, a <graded module> over the <Rees ring> $\mathcal R(I)$. Generators of $M$ in degree zero generate this module. For $N\subseteq M$, the graded <submodule> $\bigoplus_j(I^jM\cap N)t^j$ is finitely generated when $R$ is a <Noetherian ring>; a bound on its generators' degrees yields the <Artin-Rees lemma>.

= Artin-Rees lemma
{c}
{parent=I-adic filtration}

If $R$ is Noetherian, $I$ is an ideal, and $N\subseteq M$ are finitely generated modules, then some $k$ satisfies
$$
I^nM\cap N=I^{n-k}(I^kM\cap N)
$$
for every $n\ge k$.

= Filtered algebra
{parent=Filtration of a module}
{wiki}

A filtered algebra has a nested family of vector subspaces whose index is compatible with multiplication.

= Rees ring of a filtered algebra
{parent=Filtered algebra}
{c}
{title2=$\mathcal R_F(A)=\bigoplus_{m\ge0}F_mA\,h^m\subseteq A[h]$}

For an ascending multiplicative filtration with $1\in F_0A$, its Rees ring contains the central element $h$. The quotient by $h$ is the <associated graded ring>, and the quotient by $h-1$ is the original algebra. If $[F_iA,F_jA]\subseteq F_{i+j-1}A$, commutators of lifts are divisible by $h$. Their first-order term defines the <Poisson bracket from a central deformation parameter> on the associated graded ring.

= Almost commutative algebra
{parent=Filtered algebra}
{title2=$\operatorname{gr}A\text{ is commutative and finitely generated}$}

An almost commutative algebra over a <field> is an <algebra> with an exhaustive increasing nonnegative multiplicative <filtration> whose <associated graded ring> is commutative and finitely generated as an algebra over the field. A common presentation uses finitely many degree-one generators, scalar degree-zero part, and <commutators> that lower degree. The finite-generation requirement is essential: an infinitely generated commutative <polynomial ring> has commutative associated graded algebra but need not be <Noetherian>.

= Degree-one almost commutative algebra
{parent=Almost commutative algebra}
{title2=$F_0A=k,\quad A=\langle F_1A\rangle,\quad[F_1A,F_1A]\subseteq F_1A$}

This is the degree-one convention for an <almost commutative algebra>: the exhaustive filtration is generated by a finite-dimensional first step containing $1$, the degree-zero part is $k$, and its associated graded algebra is commutative. The first step is a finite-dimensional <Lie algebra> under commutators. Its inclusion into $A$ extends by the universal property to a surjection from its <universal enveloping algebra>. The element $1$ of the first step is retained as a central Lie generator and its image is the associative identity; quotienting the first step by $k1$ before taking the bracket can lose scalar commutators. This convention is stronger than merely requiring a finitely generated commutative associated graded algebra for an arbitrary weighted filtration.

= Filtered ring
{parent=Filtered algebra}

A filtered ring is a ring with subgroups $(R_n)$ satisfying $R_mR_n\subseteq R_{m+n}$.

= Filtration of a ring
{parent=Filtered ring}
{title2=$F_iR\,F_jR\subseteq F_{i+j}R$}

A filtration of a <ring> is an exhaustive increasing family of additive subgroups $(F_iR)_{i\in\mathbb Z}$, with $1\in F_0R$ and the displayed multiplicativity condition. Its <associated graded ring> is $\bigoplus_i F_iR/F_{i-1}R$. It differs from a probabilistic <filtration>, which is a nested family of sigma-algebras.

= Complete negative filtration
{parent=Filtration of a ring}
{title2=$F_iR=R\ (i\geq0),\quad R\cong\varprojlim_q R/F_{-q}R$}

A negative <filtration of a ring> has $F_iR=R$ for nonnegative indices. It is complete and separated when the displayed map to the <inverse limit> is an <isomorphism>. Products make $F_{-q}R$ two-sided <ideals>. Series whose terms eventually belong to every $F_{-q}R$ converge. In particular, for $x\in F_{-1}R$ and $r\in R$, the <geometric series> $\sum_{j\geq0}(xr)^j$ inverts $1-xr$, so the <unit criterion for the Jacobson radical> gives $F_{-1}R\subseteq J(R)$.

= Complete negative filtered-graded transfer of Noetherianity
{parent=Complete negative filtration}
{title2=$\operatorname{gr}R\text{ right Noetherian}\implies R\text{ right Noetherian}$}

Lift finite homogeneous generators of the <associated graded module> of a <right ideal> $I$ to $x_i\in I$ of degrees $d_i$. Successive leading-symbol cancellations write an element $x\in F_dI$ as finite partial sums $\sum_i x_i a_i^{(q)}$ with residual in $F_{d-q}R$. The coefficient increments lie in $F_{d-q-d_i}R$ and therefore converge in the <complete negative filtration>. Multiplication is continuous, so their limits express $x$ as a finite sum $\sum_i x_i a_i$. This generates the actual <right ideal> and does not presuppose that it is closed. The left-sided statement is analogous.

= Ascending filtered-graded transfer of Noetherianity
{parent=Filtered ring}
{title2=$\operatorname{gr}A\text{ left Noetherian}\implies A\text{ left Noetherian}$}

For an exhaustive nonnegative increasing multiplicative <filtration>, each <left ideal> $I$ induces a homogeneous <ideal> $\operatorname{gr}I$ in the <associated graded ring>. If that graded ring is a <left Noetherian ring>, lift a finite homogeneous generating set of $\operatorname{gr}I$ to elements of $I$. For any element of $I$, subtract a combination of these lifts with the same leading symbol; the remainder has strictly lower degree. Induction on the nonnegative degree proves the lifts generate $I$. There is no completion hypothesis because the cancellation terminates in finitely many steps. The analogous result holds for <right ideals>.

= Filtered-graded transfer for complete rings
{parent=Filtered ring}

For a complete separated multiplicatively filtered <ring> with nonnegative integer valuations, a domain <associated graded ring> makes the original ring a domain: the leading symbols of two nonzero elements have nonzero product. If the graded ring is left Noetherian, lift finitely many homogeneous generators of the graded ideal of a left ideal. Successively cancelling leading terms leaves remainders of increasing valuation. Completeness makes the coefficient series converge, expressing every ideal element as a finite sum of the chosen generators with completed coefficients. The right-handed argument is identical. Completeness is necessary for this infinite cancellation proof.

= Module length
{synonym}

The length of a module is the number of simple factors in a composition series. It is additive across short exact sequences.

= Primary decomposition theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain
{parent=Module theory}

A finitely generated module over a principal ideal domain decomposes as
$$
R^r\oplus\bigoplus_p\bigoplus_jR/(p^{e_{p,j}}),
$$
with finitely many irreducibles $p$. The free rank and primary cyclic factors are unique up to associates and ordering.

= Module over a ring
{synonym}

A left $R$-module consists of an <abelian group> $(M,+)$ and <scalar multiplication> $R\times M\to M$, written $(r,m)\mapsto rm$, where $R$ is a <ring> with <identity element> $1$. For every $r,s\in R$ and $m,n\in M$, require
$$
r(m+n)=rm+rn,\qquad (r+s)m=rm+sm,\qquad (rs)m=r(sm),\qquad 1m=m.
$$
The <module> elements can be added to one another and multiplied by scalars; a product of two <module> elements is not part of the definition. These conventions agree with https://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/linmultialg/moduleintro.pdf[Keith Conrad's Introductory Notes on Modules, Definition 1.2]{ref}. For a <noncommutative ring>, the side matters: a right <module> instead has $(mr)s=m(rs)$. Over a <commutative ring>, the two conventions agree.

Over a <field> $k$, these are exactly the <vector space axioms>. A nonzero $a\in k$ has a <multiplicative inverse>, so $am=0$ implies $m=a^{-1}(am)=0$. Over the <integers>, $2$ has no <multiplicative inverse>: $2x=1$ has no solution in $\mathbb Z$. In the <module> $\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z$, division can also lose uniqueness: $2\overline1=2\overline4=\overline2$. A <ring> element which is a <unit> still acts invertibly on every <module>; the difficulty lies with scalars which are not <units>.

= Central character of an associative-algebra module
{parent=Module (mathematics)}
{title2=$\theta:Z(A)\to\mathbb C$}

= Central character
{synonym}

A module over a complex associative algebra $A$ has central character $\theta$ if every element $z$ of the centre acts as the scalar $\theta(z)$, where $\theta$ is a unital algebra homomorphism. A <Verma module> has a central character: the centre preserves its one-dimensional highest-weight space, and centrality extends that scalar action from the cyclic generator to every vector. A nonzero homomorphism between two modules with scalar central actions forces their central characters to coincide. A generalized central character allows each $z-\theta(z)$ to act locally nilpotently instead of as zero.

= Right module
{parent=Module (mathematics)}
{title2=$M_R$}

A right <module> over a unital <ring> $R$ is an <abelian group> $M$ with an additive scalar action on the right satisfying $(mr)s=m(rs)$, $m(r+s)=mr+ms$ and $m1=m$. The order of multiplication matters for a noncommutative <ring>. Equivalently it is a left <module> over the <opposite ring>. Row vectors acted on by matrices on the right give a basic example.

= Rank of a module over an integral domain
{parent=Module (mathematics)}
{title2=$\operatorname{rank}_R M=\dim_{\operatorname{Frac}(R)}(\operatorname{Frac}(R)\otimes_R M)$}

= Rank of a module
{synonym}

For a <module> $M$ over an <integral domain> $R$ with <field of fractions> $K$, its rank is the <dimension> of $K\otimes_R M$. The <tensor product of modules> here extends scalars to a <field>, turning the question into a dimension calculation. For a <free module> $R^r$, the result is $r$.

For a <finitely generated module> over a <principal ideal domain>, the <structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain> gives $M\cong R^r\oplus\bigoplus_iR/(d_i)$, with $d_i$ nonzero nonunits. Each <torsion element of a module> vanishes after extension of scalars, because its annihilating nonzero scalar becomes invertible in $K$. Thus $K\otimes_R M\cong K^r$, proving that the rank is the number of free summands. The rank does not record the <torsion submodule>: for example, $R\oplus R/(d)$ and $R$ have the same rank.

= Generating set of a module

= Generating sets of a module
{synonym}

A subset $S$ of a <module> $M$ generates $M$ when every element of $M$ is a finite sum $\sum_{s\in S}r_ss$, with coefficients in the scalar <ring>. The existence of a finite such subset defines a <finitely generated module>.

= Abelian groups as integer modules
{parent=Module (mathematics)}
{title2=$\mathbb Z\text{-modules}\;\longleftrightarrow\;\text{abelian groups}$}

Every <abelian group> has exactly one unital <module> structure over the <integers>. For $n>0$, define $nm$ by adding $m$ to itself $n$ times, put $0m=0$, and put $(-n)m=-(nm)$. The <module> axioms force this action, so no additional choice is involved. Moreover, every <abelian group homomorphism> respects it and is a <module homomorphism>. See https://www.math.uwaterloo.ca/~dmckinno/pmath347/Modules.pdf[David McKinnon's Modules, the integer-scalar example].

For example, in the <free module> $\mathbb Z^2$,
$$
3(2,-1)+(-2)(1,4)=(4,-11).
$$
In the <cyclic group> $\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z$, the same repeated-addition rule gives $4\overline2=\overline8=\overline2$ and $3\overline2=\overline0$. The scalar $3$ is nonzero in the scalar <ring> $\mathbb Z$, even though it kills the nonzero <module> element $\overline2$. Thus the scalar <ring> and the <module> must be kept distinct.

= Polynomial module from a linear operator
{parent=Module (mathematics)}
{title2=$p(t)\cdot v=p(T)v$}

A <linear operator> $T:V\to V$ on a <vector space> over a <field> $k$ makes $V$ a <module> over the <polynomial ring> $k[t]$: let $t$ act as $T$, let constants act as the original scalars, and define $p(t)\cdot v=p(T)v$. The identity $(pq)(T)=p(T)q(T)$ for <polynomials> $p,q$ verifies the multiplication axiom. The <submodules> are precisely the <invariant subspaces> of $T$. This construction is described in https://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/linmultialg/moduleintro.pdf[Keith Conrad's Introductory Notes on Modules, section 6].

For a computed example, take $k=\mathbb Q$, $V=k^2$, and
$$
T=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad e_1=(1,0),\qquad e_2=(0,1).
$$
Then $Te_2=e_1$, $Te_1=0$, and $T^2=0$. Consequently
$$
(2+3t+t^2)\cdot e_2=2e_2+3e_1=(3,2).
$$
Every $(a,b)$ equals $(b+at)\cdot e_2$. The <module homomorphism> $k[t]\to V$, $p\mapsto p(T)e_2$, is therefore <surjective>, and its <kernel> is the <ideal> $(t^2)$. It induces a <module isomorphism> $V\cong k[t]/(t^2)$. This is a <cyclic module> and a <torsion module> over $k[t]$, since $t^2V=0$. Over $k$ it is a <free module> with <basis> $(e_1,e_2)$ and has no nonzero <torsion elements of a module>. Over $\mathbb Q$ its nonzero elements also have infinite additive <order of a group element>. Changing scalars changes the questions one asks about the same addition law.

For a <module> over a commutative ring presented by a <matrix> with n generators, its zeroth Fitting ideal is generated by all n-by-n minors of the relation <matrix>. = Locally free module
{parent=Module (mathematics)}
{title2=$P_{f_i}\cong A_{f_i}^r$}

A <locally free module> is one whose associated sheaf is locally a <free module>. In the finite constant-rank case, it becomes free of rank $r$ on a <principal open subset> cover of $\operatorname{Spec}A$. Equivalently it is a <finite projective module> of constant rank $r$. = Change of rings
{parent=Module (mathematics)}
{wiki}

Changing the coefficient <ring> of a <module> along a <ring homomorphism> can mean <restriction of scalars>, <extension of scalars>, or <coextension of scalars>. The underlying <module> may be retained with a smaller ring action, or replaced by a tensor or Hom construction with the new ring.

= Coextension of scalars
{parent=Change of rings}
{title2=$\operatorname{Hom}_R(A,M)$}

For a <ring homomorphism> $R\to A$ and a left $R$-<module> $M$, the left $A$-action on $\operatorname{Hom}_R(A,M)$ is $(a\cdot h)(b)=h(ba)$. This construction is right adjoint to <restriction of scalars>.

= Extension of scalars
{parent=Change of rings}
{title2=$A\otimes_RM$}

For a <ring homomorphism> $R\to A$, tensoring an $R$-<module> with the $(A,R)$-<bimodule> $A$ gives an $A$-<module>. This functor is left adjoint to <restriction of scalars>.

= Restriction of scalars
{parent=Change of rings}
{title2=$r\cdot m=\theta(r)m$}

For a <ring homomorphism> $\theta:R\to A$, an $A$-<module> becomes an $R$-<module> by the displayed action. Its underlying <abelian group> stays the same.

= Module isomorphism
{parent=Module (mathematics)}

A <module isomorphism> is a bijection of <modules> over the same <ring> preserving addition and scalar multiplication. Its inverse preserves these operations too. When a <linear operator> $T$ gives a <vector space> an $F[t]$-<module> structure by $t\cdot v=T(v)$, a <module isomorphism> is exactly an invertible <linear map> intertwining the two operators. This translates classification of <modules> into <matrix similarity> and <rational canonical form>.

= Torsion module
{parent=Module (mathematics)}
{title2=$T(M)=M$}
{wiki}

A torsion module over an <integral domain> $R$ is a <module> in which every element is a <torsion element of a module>. Each element may require a different nonzero annihilating scalar. The <module> $\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z$ is torsion over $\mathbb Z$, and the <polynomial module from a linear operator> $\mathbb Q[t]/(t^2)$ is torsion over $\mathbb Q[t]$.

If the <torsion module> is a <finitely generated module>, one common nonzero scalar kills the whole <module>: multiply annihilating scalars for a finite generating set. Without finite generation this can fail. The <quotient module> $\mathbb Q/\mathbb Z$ is torsion, since the class of $a/b$ is killed by $b$, but no nonzero <integer> $n$ kills it all: the class of $1/(2|n|)$ survives multiplication by $n$. See https://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/linmultialg/modulesoverPID.pdf[Keith Conrad's Modules over a PID, exercise 6.5(c)].

= Torsion submodule
{title2=$T(M)$}
{parent=Torsion module}

For a <module> $M$ over an <integral domain> $R$, its torsion submodule is
$$
T(M)=\{m\in M:rm=0\text{ for some }0\ne r\in R\}.
$$
This is a <submodule>. If $rm=0$ and $sn=0$ with $r,s\ne0$, then $rs(m+n)=0$ and $rs\ne0$; also $r(am)=a(rm)=0$ for every $a\in R$. The <integral domain> hypothesis supplies both the nonzero product and commutativity. These are the conventions of https://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/linmultialg/moduleintro.pdf[Keith Conrad's Introductory Notes on Modules, section 4]{ref}.

A mixed example is the <direct sum> $M=\mathbb Z^2\oplus\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z$. If a nonzero <integer> kills $(a,b,\overline c)$, then $a=b=0$. Conversely, $6$ kills every $(0,0,\overline c)$. Thus
$$
T(M)=\{(0,0,\overline c):\overline c\in\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z\},\qquad M/T(M)\cong\mathbb Z^2.
$$
The <torsion submodule> isolates the finite-order part while the <quotient module> keeps the independent <integer> directions. In general $M/T(M)$ is a <torsion-free module>: if $r(m+T(M))=0$ with $r\ne0$, some $s\ne0$ kills $rm$, so $sr\ne0$ kills $m$.

The hypothesis matters. In the <ring> $R=\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z$ viewed as a <module> over itself, the elements $\overline2$ and $\overline3$ are killed by nonzero scalars $\overline3$ and $\overline2$, but their sum $\overline5$ is a <unit> and is killed by no nonzero scalar. Thus using arbitrary nonzero scalars in a <ring> with <zero divisors> need not give a <submodule>. A common broader convention uses <regular elements of a ring> as annihilators; the <integral domain> convention here avoids that ambiguity.

= Torsion element of a module
{parent=Torsion submodule}
{title2=$\exists r\ne0:\ rm=0$}

= Torsion elements of a module
{synonym}

For a <module> $M$ over an <integral domain> $R$, a torsion element is an $m\in M$ with $rm=0$ for some $0\ne r\in R$. This includes zero. Equivalently, its <annihilator> $\operatorname{Ann}_R(m)=\{r\in R:rm=0\}$ is a nonzero <ideal>. The <torsion elements of a module> form the <torsion submodule>.

For $M=\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z$, computed <annihilators> are
$$
\operatorname{Ann}_{\mathbb Z}(\overline1)=6\mathbb Z,\qquad \operatorname{Ann}_{\mathbb Z}(\overline2)=3\mathbb Z,\qquad \operatorname{Ann}_{\mathbb Z}(\overline3)=2\mathbb Z.
$$
For these three elements, the additive <order of a group element> is respectively $6$, $3$, and $2$: these are the smallest positive <integers> killing them. Torsion over $\mathbb Z$ agrees exactly with finite additive <order of a group element>. Over a general <integral domain>, torsion concerns the chosen scalars instead; the <polynomial module from a linear operator> gives a torsion example whose nonzero elements have infinite additive <order of a group element>.

{c}

An $R$-module homomorphism is an additive map $f:M\to N$ satisfying $f(rm)=rf(m)$.

= Hom into the regular algebra as a right module
{c}
{parent=Module homomorphism}
{title2=$\operatorname{Hom}_A(M,A)_A$}

For a left <module> $M$ over a unital <associative algebra> $A$, right multiplication in $A$ gives a <right module> structure on $\operatorname{Hom}_A(M,A)$ by $(f\cdot a)(m)=f(m)a$. This remains a <module homomorphism> because $f(bm)a=bf(m)a$; associativity and the unit verify the action axioms. When $A=M_d(\mathbb C)$ and $M=\mathbb C^d$ is its <simple module> of columns, every such map is $m\mapsto mr$ for a unique row $r$, making the Hom space the simple row <right module>. This illustrates how left and right simple <modules> correspond for a <semisimple algebra>.

= Kernel of a module homomorphism
{parent=Module homomorphism}
{title2=$\ker f=\{m\in M:f(m)=0\}$}

The <kernel of a module homomorphism> $f:M\to N$ is the <submodule> of elements mapped to zero. Closure follows from $f(m+m')=f(m)+f(m')$ and $f(rm)=rf(m)$.

= First isomorphism theorem for modules
{parent=Module homomorphism}
{title2=$M/\ker f\cong\operatorname{Im}f$}

For a <module homomorphism> $f:M\to N$, the map $m+\ker f\mapsto f(m)$ is a <module isomorphism> $M/\ker f\to\operatorname{Im}f$. The formula is well-defined because two representatives differ by an element of the <kernel of a module homomorphism> exactly when their images agree; the same observation proves injectivity, and surjectivity onto the image follows by its definition.

= Module endomorphism
{parent=Module homomorphism}
{title2=$\operatorname{End}_R(M)$}

A module endomorphism is a <module homomorphism> from a <module> to itself. These maps form the <endomorphism ring> $\operatorname{End}_R(M)$ under pointwise addition and composition. An invertible module endomorphism is a <module automorphism>.

= Module automorphism
{parent=Module endomorphism}
{title2=$\operatorname{Aut}_R(M)$}

A module automorphism is an invertible <module endomorphism>. Equivalently, it is a bijective <module homomorphism> from a <module> to itself; its inverse is automatically a <module homomorphism>. They form a <group> under composition.

= Module retraction
{parent=Module homomorphism}
{title2=$ri=I$}

A <module retraction> onto a <submodule> $U\xrightarrow iX$ is a <module homomorphism> $r:X\to U$ with $ri=I_U$. It gives $X=i(U)\oplus\ker r$: write $x=i(r(x))+(x-i(r(x)))$. Thus a nonzero proper submodule admitting a retraction contradicts indecomposability. Extending a projection from a larger submodule can produce such a retraction through the <long exact sequence of Ext groups>.

= Endomorphism ring
{parent=Module homomorphism}
{wiki}

The endomorphism ring $\operatorname{End}_R(M)$ consists of the $R$-module homomorphisms $M\to M$, with pointwise addition and composition as multiplication.

= Local endomorphism ring
{parent=Endomorphism ring}

An <endomorphism ring> is local when its nonunits form a proper two-sided <ideal>; commutativity is not required. The <Fitting lemma> gives this property for an indecomposable finite-length <module>. If two nonunits had an invertible sum, multiplication by its inverse would give $I=f+g$ with noninvertible $f,g$. Fitting makes $f$ nilpotent, so $I-f=g$ would be invertible, a contradiction. Products with a nonunit are nonunits by finite-length injectivity/surjectivity. The quotient by the nonunit ideal is a <division ring>.

= Semisimple quotient of a module endomorphism algebra
{parent=Endomorphism ring}
{title2=$\operatorname{End}(X)/J\cong\prod_aM_{m_a}(k)$}

For a finite-dimensional module over an <algebraically closed field>, write a <Krull-Schmidt decomposition> $X=\bigoplus_aM_a^{\oplus m_a}$ with distinct indecomposable types. Each endomorphism ring of $M_a$ is a <local endomorphism ring> with residue division algebra $k$, by the <finite-dimensional division algebra over an algebraically closed field> result. Modulo the <Jacobson radical> of $\operatorname{End}(X)$, the blocks of one type become $M_{m_a}(k)$ and all maps between different types vanish. A composite through a different indecomposable type cannot be invertible, since that would make one type a direct summand of the other. This yields the displayed product and the <Levi decomposition of a quiver automorphism group>.

= Brick module
{parent=Endomorphism ring}

A nonzero module is a brick when every endomorphism is either zero or an isomorphism. Equivalently, its endomorphism ring is a division ring.

= Ringel lemma on bricks
{parent=Brick module}
{c}

An indecomposable finite-dimensional <quiver representation> that is not a <brick module> contains a brick with nonzero self-extensions. The <Ringel form> then gives $q_Q(\dim B)=1-\dim\operatorname{Ext}^1_Q(B,B)\leq0$, impossible for a positive definite <Tits form of a quiver>.

= Proof of Ringel lemma on bricks
{parent=Ringel lemma on bricks}

The <Fitting lemma> supplies a nonzero nilpotent endomorphism $f$ of a non-brick indecomposable $X$. Minimize its nonzero rank; nilpotence and minimality give $f^2=0$. Set $I=\operatorname{im}f\subset K=\ker f=\bigoplus_jK_j$. Choose a nonzero component $u:I\to K_j$. The square-zero endomorphism $X\xrightarrow fI\xrightarrow uK_j\hookrightarrow X$ has rank at least $\dim I$, so $u$ is injective.

If $\operatorname{Ext}^1(I,K_j)=0$, the projection $K\to K_j$ extends to $X\to K_j$ by the <long exact sequence of Ext groups>, giving a <module retraction> and contradicting indecomposability. Thus this extension group is nonzero. Since <path algebras are hereditary>, $u$ induces a surjection $\operatorname{Ext}^1(K_j,K_j)\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{Ext}^1(I,K_j)$. Hence $K_j$ is a proper indecomposable submodule with self-extensions. Iterate until a <brick module> is reached; dimensions strictly decrease.

This minimal-rank argument is given in section 2 of https://www.math.uni-bielefeld.de/~wcrawley/quivlecs.pdf[William Crawley-Boevey's quiver lectures].

= Submodule

A submodule $N$ of a <module> $M$ is a subset closed under addition, additive inverses, and scalar multiplication by every element of $R$.

= Essential submodule
{parent=Submodule}

A <submodule> $N\subseteq M$ is an <essential submodule> if it intersects every nonzero <submodule> of $M$ nontrivially. A nonzero <module> is a <uniform module> precisely when every nonzero <submodule> is an <essential submodule>.

= Essential right ideal
{parent=Essential submodule}

An essential right ideal is an <essential submodule> of the right regular <module> of a <ring>. Every <right ideal> containing an essential right ideal is essential.

= A regular principal right ideal in a right Noetherian ring is essential
{parent=Essential right ideal}
{title2=$c\text{ regular}\implies cR\text{ essential}$}

If $c$ is a <regular element of a ring> in a <right Noetherian ring> and a nonzero <right ideal> $B$ had $B\cap cR=0$, cancellation would make $B+cB+c^2B+\cdots$ an infinite <direct sum> of nonzero <right ideals>. Its finite partial sums contradict the <ascending chain condition>. Thus $cR$ is an <essential right ideal>.

= Essential extension
{parent=Essential submodule}

An extension $N\subseteq M$ is essential when $N$ is an <essential submodule> of $M$. Essentiality is transitive. It is an intersection condition, not a statement that the quotient is small in dimension or finitely generated.

= Quotient module

For a submodule $N\leq M$, the quotient module $M/N$ consists of additive cosets with scalar multiplication $r(m+N)=rm+N$.

= Universal property of a quotient module
{parent=Quotient module}

If an <R-module homomorphism> $f:M\to P$ vanishes on a <submodule> $N$, there is a unique homomorphism $\bar f:M/N\to P$ satisfying $f=\bar f\circ q$, where $q:M\to M/N$ is the quotient map. It is given by $\bar f(m+N)=f(m)$.

= Free module
{parent=Module theory}
{title2=$M\cong\bigoplus_{i\in I}R$}
{wiki}

A free module is a <module> with a <basis of a module> $(e_i)_{i\in I}$: every element has exactly one expression $m=\sum_{i\in I}r_ie_i$ with $r_i\in R$ and only finitely many $r_i$ nonzero. Equivalently, the coefficient map $\bigoplus_{i\in I}R\to M$ is a <module isomorphism>. Existence means generation; uniqueness means <linear independence in a module>. For finite $I$ of size $n$, this says $M\cong R^n$, a <finite free module>. See https://neilstrickland.github.io/modules/notes/modules.pdf[Neil Strickland's Rings, Modules and Linear Algebra, section 11].

The <integer lattice> $\mathbb Z^2$ in the opening diagram is a <free module> over $\mathbb Z$ with <basis of a module> $e_1=(1,0)$, $e_2=(0,1)$. For example, $(3,2)=3e_1+2e_2$, and comparing the two coordinates makes the coefficients unique. A different <basis of a module> is $u=(1,0)$, $v=(1,1)$, because $(a,b)=(a-b)u+bv$; in particular $(3,2)=u+2v$.

By contrast, $w_1=(2,0)$ and $w_2=(0,1)$ are <linearly independent in a module>, but generate only the <submodule> $2\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z$. They miss $(1,0)$ because $2a=1$ has no <integer> solution. They would form a <basis> over the <rational numbers>. Even generation need not let one extract a <basis of a module>: the elements $2,3$ generate $\mathbb Z$, since $1=3-2$, but neither singleton generates it, and $3\cdot2-2\cdot3=0$ is a nontrivial <linear relation> between them. These are concrete failures of the usual <vector space> argument that divides by any nonzero coefficient.

The <cyclic module> $\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z$ has no <basis of a module> over $\mathbb Z$: every element has the nontrivial relation $6m=0$, so no nonempty subset is <linearly independent in a module>, while the empty subset cannot generate this nonzero <module>. Specifying the scalar <ring> is essential: over $R=\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z$, the same additive object is a <free module> with the one-element <basis of a module> $\{\overline1\}$, since each coefficient is itself a unique class in $R$.

= Lifting property of a free module
{parent=Free module}
{title2=$\phi q=\psi$}

For a <free module> $F$, a <surjective> <module homomorphism> $\phi:P\to M$, and a <module homomorphism> $\psi:F\to M$, there is a <module homomorphism> $q:F\to P$ satisfying $\phi q=\psi$. Choose a preimage under $\phi$ of the image of each element of a <basis of a module> of $F$, and extend by linearity. Thus every <free module> is a <projective module>.

= Countable basis support lemma
{parent=Free module}
{title2=$N\subseteq\langle E'\rangle,\quad E'\subseteq E\text{ countable}$}

A <submodule> $N$ of a <free module> with <basis of a module> $E$, generated by a <countable set>, is contained in the <submodule> generated by some <countable> subset $E'\subseteq E$. Take $E'$ to be the union of the finite <coordinate supports in a free module> of a countable generating family for $N$. This union is <countable>, and every generator belongs to $\langle E'\rangle$, which proves the inclusion. Combining this with <basis partition gives a module direct sum> places $N$ inside a countably generated <direct summand>.

= Basis partition gives a module direct sum
{parent=Free module}
{title2=$E=E'\sqcup E''\Longrightarrow M=\langle E'\rangle\oplus\langle E''\rangle$}

Partition a <basis of a module> $E$ into disjoint subsets $E',E''$. Splitting the finite expression of each element gives $M=\langle E'\rangle+\langle E''\rangle$. An element in both generated <submodules> would give, by subtraction, a relation among the disjoint parts of $E$; <linear independence in a module> forces every coefficient to vanish. The intersection is zero, proving the <direct sum of modules> decomposition. Each part is itself a <basis of a module> for the <submodule> it generates.

= Vector-space freeness from maximal independence
{parent=Free module}
{title2=$V\cong\bigoplus_{b\in B}k$}

Order the <linearly independent> subsets of a <vector space> by inclusion. A chain's union is independent, since every finite relation is contained in one chain member. <Zorn's lemma> gives a maximal independent subset. A vector outside its span could be adjoined while preserving independence, so it spans. Sending finite-support coefficient families to their linear combinations gives an isomorphism from the <free module> on that subset to $V$. This supplies an arbitrary, possibly infinite-dimensional <basis> using the <axiom of choice>; no pre-existing <basis> theorem is invoked.

Elements of a <module> are linearly independent if every finite linear relation between them has all coefficients zero. An independent finite set freely generates its span, a <finite free module>. = Finite free module

A basis $S$ of an $R$-module $M$ is a subset such that every element of $M$ has a unique expression as a finite $R$-linear combination of elements of $S$.

= Universal property of a free module
{parent=Free module}

A module $F$ is free on a set $S$ exactly when every function from $S$ to any module $N$ extends uniquely to an $R$-module homomorphism $F\to N$.

= Rank of a free module
{title2=$\operatorname{rk}M$}
{parent=Free module}

For a nonzero commutative scalar ring, every two bases of a finite-rank free module have the same cardinality. This cardinality is the rank.

= Rank inequality for an injection of finite free modules
{parent=Rank of a free module}

Over a nonzero commutative ring, an injection $R^m\hookrightarrow R^n$ implies $m\leq n$. If $m>n$, appending zero coordinates gives an injective endomorphism of $R^m$ with zero determinant; the Cayley--Hamilton theorem and cancellation of that injection eventually force the identity to vanish.

= Invariant basis number
{title2=$R^m\cong R^n\Longrightarrow m=n$}
{parent=Free module}
{wiki}

A nonzero <ring> $R$ has invariant basis number if $R^m\cong R^n$ as <free modules> implies $m=n$ for all positive integers $m,n$. The property is not automatic for arbitrary rings; the commutative-ring case is proved below.

= Invariant basis number for a commutative ring
{parent=Invariant basis number}

If $R$ is a nonzero commutative ring and $R^m\cong R^n$, reduction modulo a maximal ideal produces isomorphic vector spaces of dimensions $m$ and $n$. Hence $m=n$.

= Torsion-free module
{parent=Module theory}
{title2=$T(M)=0$}
{wiki}

A <module> $M$ over an <integral domain> $R$ is torsion-free when $rm=0$ with $0\ne r\in R$ implies $m=0$, equivalently when its <torsion submodule> is zero. Every <free module> over an <integral domain> is torsion-free, as one can check in each coefficient. Every <vector space> is torsion-free over its scalar <field>.

The converse requires hypotheses. A <finitely generated module> over a <principal ideal domain> is free if it is torsion-free, but the <module> $\mathbb Q$ over $\mathbb Z$ is torsion-free and not free. Any two nonzero <rational numbers> have a nontrivial <integer> relation, so a possible <basis of a module> could have at most one element; no single $q$ generates all <rational numbers>, since $q/2\notin\mathbb Zq$ for $q\ne0$. The finite-generation hypothesis cannot be dropped. See https://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/linmultialg/modulesoverPID.pdf[Keith Conrad's Modules over a PID, Corollary 2.6].

= Embedding a finitely generated torsion-free module in a finite free module
{parent=Torsion-free module}
{title2=$M\hookrightarrow R^r$}

Apply <clearing denominators relative to an independent module subset> to find $0\ne a$ with $aM$ lying in a <finite free module>. Multiplication by $a$ is injective on a <torsion-free module>, giving the required embedding. A submodule need not itself be free over a general <integral domain>.

= Maximal torsion-free quotient
{parent=Torsion-free module}

For a module $M$ over an <integral domain>, the quotient $M/T(M)$ by its <torsion submodule> is torsion-free. Every homomorphism from $M$ to a torsion-free module factors uniquely through $M/T(M)$ by the <universal property of a quotient module>.

= Reflexive module
{parent=Torsion-free module}
{wiki}

A finitely generated module $M$ over a <commutative ring> $A$ is reflexive when its <evaluation homomorphism> $M\to M^{**}$ to the <double dual module> is an <isomorphism>.

= Reflexive-module second-syzygy criterion
{parent=Reflexive module}

Let $A$ be a <Noetherian ring> that is an <integral domain>. If
$$
0\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow N\longrightarrow P\longrightarrow0
$$
is <exact sequence>[exact], $N$ is a finitely generated <free module>, and $P$ is a <torsion-free module>, then $M$ is a <reflexive module>. In particular, the dual of every finitely generated $A$-module is reflexive.

= Projective module
{parent=Module theory}
{wiki}

A module $P$ is projective when every map from $P$ through the target of a surjection lifts through that surjection.

= Projective modules are flat
{parent=Projective module}
{title2=$P\text{ projective}\implies P\text{ flat}$}

A <projective module> is a <direct summand> of a <free module>. <Free modules> are flat because tensoring with a <free module> produces a <direct sum> of copies of the original module and map. If $F=P\oplus Q$ is flat, tensoring an <injection> with $F$ is the <direct sum> of its tensor maps with $P$ and $Q$, so each component is <injective>. Applying this to a <free module> containing $P$ proves that $P$ is <flat>.

= Projective modules are direct summands of free modules
{parent=Projective module}
{title2=$P\text{ projective}\iff P\text{ is a direct summand of a free module}$}

Every <module> $P$ is a quotient of a <free module> $F=\bigoplus_{p\in P}Ae_p$, via $e_p\mapsto p$. If $P$ is a <projective module>, lift $1_P$ through this <surjection> to obtain a section $s:P\to F$. The resulting <split short exact sequence> identifies $F$ with $\ker\pi\oplus P$. Conversely, if $P$ is a summand of a <free module>, extend a prescribed map out of $P$ to the <free module> using the summand projection, lift each <basis> image through the given <surjection>, and restrict the lift to $P$. This proves the equivalence with the lifting definition of a <projective module>, for arbitrary ranks.

= Principal indecomposable module
{parent=Projective module}
{title2=$P=Ae$}

= Projective indecomposable module
{synonym}

For a finite-dimensional <algebra> $A$, a principal indecomposable module is an indecomposable direct summand of the left regular <module>, equivalently $Ae$ for a <primitive idempotent> $e$. Its <head of a module> is simple, and it is the <projective cover> of that simple module.

= Finite projective module
{parent=Projective module}

= Finitely generated projective module
{synonym}

A finite projective module is a <finitely generated module> that is a <projective module>. Equivalently it is a direct summand of a finite <free module>: a finite free surjection splits by projectivity. Its <dual module> is finite projective and its evaluation map to its <double dual module> is an <isomorphism>, since both properties hold for finite free modules and pass to direct summands. Finite generation is needed for this elementary duality argument.

= Invertible module
{parent=Projective module}
{title2=$L\otimes_A L^*\cong A$}

= Algebraic line bundle
{synonym}

An invertible module over a <commutative ring> $A$ is a <finitely generated module> that is a <projective module> and whose <localization at a prime ideal> is free of rank one at every prime. Its <dual module> $L^*=\operatorname{Hom}_A(L,A)$ is also finite projective. Evaluation $L\otimes_A L^*\to A$ is an <isomorphism> after every prime localization, hence is an isomorphism globally by <localization detects zero elements>. The <Picard group of a ring> consists of the isomorphism classes of these modules under the <tensor product of modules>.

= Base change of invertible modules
{parent=Invertible module}
{title2=$L\mapsto B\otimes_A L$}

For a <ring homomorphism> $A\to B$, <extension of scalars> takes an <invertible module> to an invertible $B$-module. A direct-summand presentation in a finite <free module> remains a direct-summand presentation after tensoring. At a prime $\mathfrak q\subset B$ contracting to $\mathfrak p$, localization gives $B_{\mathfrak q}\otimes_{A_{\mathfrak p}}L_{\mathfrak p}\cong B_{\mathfrak q}$. Compatibility with tensor products, identities and composition makes the <Picard group of a ring> a covariant <functor>.

= Projective dimension
{parent=Projective module}
{title2=$\operatorname{pd}_RM$}

For a nonzero <module>, the projective dimension $\operatorname{pd}_RM$ is the shortest length of a <projective resolution> of the <module> $M$, or infinity if no finite <projective resolution> exists. Equivalently, it is the supremum of degrees with nonzero $\operatorname{Ext}_R^n(M,N)$ as $N$ ranges over all <modules>. This equivalence follows by dimension shifting and the characterization of <projective modules> by vanishing $\operatorname{Ext}^1$.

= Top Ext detects finite projective dimension
{parent=Projective dimension}
{title2=$\operatorname{pd}_A M=n<\infty\implies\operatorname{Ext}_A^n(M,A)\ne0$}

Let $M$ be a nonzero <finitely generated module> over a commutative <Noetherian ring>. Take a length-$n$ resolution with finite projective terms. If its top <Ext functor> with coefficients in $A$ vanished, the dual of its last injection would be surjective. The dual of a finite projective module is projective, so that surjection splits. Finite projective modules are isomorphic to their double duals, so dualizing gives a splitting of the original injection. The resolution then shortens, contradicting minimality of $n$. When $n=0$, a nonzero coordinate of a finite free module containing $M$ as a summand gives a nonzero map $M\to A$.

= Top Ext detects finite projective dimension over a left Noetherian ring
{parent=Top Ext detects finite projective dimension}
{title2=$\operatorname{pd}_RM=n<\infty\implies\operatorname{Ext}_R^n(M,R)\ne0$}

For a nonzero left <finitely generated module> over a <left Noetherian ring>, take a finite <projective resolution> with <finite projective modules>. Vanishing of its top <Ext functor> would make the dual of its final injection surjective. These duals are right <finite projective modules>, so the surjection splits. Dualizing back and using finite projective <double dual modules> splits the original injection and shortens the resolution, contradicting the definition of <projective dimension>. When the dimension is zero, a coordinate map of a finite free module containing the nonzero projective summand supplies a nonzero map to $R$. The left/right distinction in the duals is essential for a noncommutative <ring>.

= Vanishing top Ext without finite generation
{parent=Top Ext detects finite projective dimension}
{title2=$\operatorname{pd}_{k[[t]]}k((t))=1,\quad\operatorname{Ext}_{k[[t]]}^1(k((t)),k[[t]])=0$}

Put $A=k[[t]]$ and $K=A[1/t]$. A <free resolution> has countably many generators $e_i$ and relations $e_i-te_{i+1}$. Applying the <Hom functor> with coefficients in $A$ gives $(b_i)\mapsto(b_i-tb_{i+1})$ on sequence products. This is surjective: for prescribed $(c_i)$ use $b_i=\sum_{j\ge0}t^jc_{i+j}$, a well-defined <formal power series>. Therefore the degree-one <Ext functor> vanishes. But $\operatorname{Hom}_A(K,A)=0$, since every image is infinitely divisible by $t$. A nonzero projective module has a nonzero coordinate map into $A$, so $K$ is not projective. Its displayed resolution proves that its <projective dimension> is exactly one. The finite-generation hypothesis in <top Ext detects finite projective dimension> is essential.

= Global dimension
{parent=Projective dimension}
{wiki}
{title2=$\operatorname{gldim}R=\sup_M\operatorname{pd}_RM$}

= Global homological dimension
{synonym}

The global dimension of a <ring> is the supremum of the <projective dimensions> of all its <modules>, allowing infinity. It measures the longest <projective resolution> needed by any <module>. For a <field> it is zero, since every <module> is a <vector space> and hence free.

= Global dimension zero and split exact sequences
{parent=Global dimension}
{title2=$\operatorname{gldim}R=0\iff\text{every short exact sequence splits}$}

If <global dimension> is zero, every <module> is a <projective module>. Each <short exact sequence> splits because its quotient is projective. Conversely, splitting every <short exact sequence> makes each <module> a direct summand of a <free module> and therefore a <projective module>. Splitting also makes every <module> an <injective module>: for $A\hookrightarrow B$ choose a retraction $B\to A$ and compose it with any prescribed map out of $A$.

A projective cover of a module $M$ is an essential surjection $P\twoheadrightarrow M$ from a <projective module>. For a finite-dimensional algebra, every finite-dimensional module has one; the projective cover of a simple module is indecomposable and has that simple module as its head.

= Cyclic module
{parent=Module theory}
{title2=$M=Rm$}
{wiki}

A <module> is cyclic if one element $m$ generates it: every element is $rm$ for some scalar $r\in R$. The generating element need not give unique coefficients. The <module homomorphism> $R\to M$, $r\mapsto rm$, is <surjective>. Over a <commutative ring>, its <kernel> is the <annihilator> $\operatorname{Ann}_R(m)$, giving
$$
M\cong R/\operatorname{Ann}_R(m).
$$
Thus <cyclic modules> are exactly the <quotient modules> $R/I$ for <ideals> $I$ in $R$. For a left <module> over a <noncommutative ring>, use a <left ideal> and interpret the quotient as a <module>, with no claim that it is a <quotient ring>. See https://faculty.niu.edu/math_beachy/aaol/modules.shtml[John A. Beachy's Abstract Algebra Online, section 10.1].

The <module> $\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z$ is generated by $\overline1$, and also by $\overline5$, whose successive multiples are $\overline0,\overline5,\overline4,\overline3,\overline2,\overline1$. The element $\overline2$ generates only $\{\overline0,\overline2,\overline4\}$, a <submodule> with three elements. A cyclic <module> can be infinite and free, as $\mathbb Z=\mathbb Z\cdot1$ shows, or finite and torsion, as $\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z$ shows. One generator counts directions of generation, not the number of elements and not freedom from relations.

= Scalar action obstructs a cyclic module generator
{parent=Cyclic module}
{title2=$Av=Kv\text{ for all }v,\quad\dim_KV>1\implies V\text{ not cyclic}$}

If an <algebra> acts on a <vector space> only by scalar multiples of the identity, every vector generates at most a one-dimensional <submodule>. A higher-dimensional such <module> is therefore not cyclic. For example upper triangular $n$-by-$n$ matrices act by the first diagonal entry on their first-row <ideal>. For $n>1$ this <ideal> is a noncyclic <submodule> of the cyclic regular <module>. Thus a <submodule> of a <cyclic module> need not be cyclic, even over a finite-dimensional <algebra>.

= Uniserial module
{parent=Module theory}
{wiki}

A module is uniserial when its submodules are totally ordered by inclusion. A finite-length uniserial module has a unique composition series.

= Semisimple module
{parent=Module theory}
{wiki}

A module is semisimple, or completely reducible, when it is a <direct sum> of <irreducible module>[simple modules]. Equivalently, every submodule has a direct-sum complement.

= Maximal-submodule splitting criterion for semisimplicity
{parent=Semisimple module}
{title2=$\forall M\text{ maximal in }V,\quad V=M\oplus C\implies V\text{ semisimple}$}

For a finite-dimensional <module>, it suffices that every maximal proper <submodule> have a <submodule> complement in order that every <submodule> have one. Let $W$ be the sum of all <simple modules> contained in the given <module>. If $W$ is proper, choose a maximal <submodule> $M$ containing it. A complement $C$ is isomorphic to the simple quotient $V/M$ and hence is itself simple. This forces $C\subseteq W\subseteq M$, contradicting the complement. Thus $W=V$. Greedily adding simple summands not already in the sum gives a <direct sum> in finitely many steps. Finally, given any $U$, choose a maximal sum of these simple summands disjoint from $U$. A missing simple summand has zero intersection with their sum plus $U$, contradicting maximality unless their sum complements $U$. This proves the <semisimple module> criterion, including the zero <module>.

= Subdirect product of pairwise nonisomorphic simple modules
{parent=Semisimple module}
{title2=$W\leq\bigoplus_{i=1}^kS_i,\quad W\twoheadrightarrow S_i\implies W=\bigoplus_iS_i$}

Suppose the $S_i$ are pairwise nonisomorphic <simple modules>. Any <submodule> $W$ of their finite <direct sum> that projects onto every summand is the entire sum. Induct on $k$. Projection onto the first $k-1$ factors is onto by induction. Its <kernel> is a <submodule> of $S_k$, hence zero or all of $S_k$. If zero, $W$ is isomorphic to the first $k-1$ factors but also has quotient $S_k$, contradicting their distinct <composition factors> and the <Jordan-Hölder theorem>. Therefore the <kernel> is $S_k$, proving the assertion. Consequently, if $S_i=Ax_i$, the map $a\mapsto(ax_i)_i$ is onto and gives $A/(\bigcap_i\operatorname{Ann}_A(x_i))\cong\bigoplus_iS_i$ as left modules. The annihilators of these vectors need only be left ideals.

= Finite sum of simple submodules
{parent=Semisimple module}
{title2=$S_1+\cdots+S_n\text{ is semisimple}$}

A finite sum of <simple modules> inside one <module> is a <semisimple module>. Inductively, if $M$ is the sum already retained, then $M\cap S_j$ is a <submodule> of the <simple module> $S_j$, hence zero or $S_j$. In the first case append $S_j$ as a <direct summand>; in the second it adds nothing. This gives a <direct sum of modules> selected from the original family.

= Isotypic decomposition
{parent=Semisimple module}

A finite-length <semisimple module> has a canonical decomposition
$$
M=\bigoplus_i M_i
$$
in which $M_i$ is the sum of all simple submodules isomorphic to a fixed simple module $S_i$. Each $M_i$ is isomorphic to a finite direct sum $S_i^{m_i}$.

The decomposition is intrinsic to the <semisimple module>: the isomorphism types of its simple submodules determine the summands.

= Isotypic component
{parent=Isotypic decomposition}
{title2=$V_\theta=e_\theta V$}

In a complex representation of a <finite group> $N$, the $\theta$-isotypic component is the sum of all irreducible submodules with character $\theta$. The <character idempotent> $e_\theta=\theta(1)|N|^{-1}\sum_{n\in N}\theta(n^{-1})n$ projects onto it. Every $N$-submodule is preserved by these projections and decomposes into its intersections with the isotypic components. If $N\triangleleft G$, conjugation permutes them according to $\theta^g(n)=\theta(g^{-1}ng)$.

= Radical of a module
{title2=$J(M)$}
{parent=Module theory}

The radical $J(M)$ of a module is the intersection of its maximal submodules. For a finite-dimensional module over a finite-dimensional algebra $A$,
$$
J(M)=J(A)M,
$$
and it is the smallest submodule $N$ for which $M/N$ is a <semisimple module>.

= Radical series of a module
{title2=$J^i(M)$}
{parent=Radical of a module}

The radical series is the descending chain
$$
M\supseteq J(M)\supseteq J^2(M)\supseteq\cdots,
$$
where $J^{i+1}(M)=J(J^i(M))$. For finite-dimensional $A$ and $M$, one has $J^i(M)=J(A)^iM$.

= Socle series of a module

= Irreducible module
{parent=Module theory}

= Simple module
{synonym}

An irreducible, or simple, module is nonzero and has no submodules other than zero and itself.

= Nilpotent central elements annihilate simple modules
{parent=Irreducible module}
{title2=$z\in Z(A),\ z^r=0\Longrightarrow zM=0$}

For a <simple module> $M$ and a central element $z$ of an <algebra> $A$, the <image> $zM$ is a <submodule>. If it is nonzero, it equals $M$, and iterating gives $z^rM=M$ for every $r$. This is impossible when $z$ is nilpotent. Hence $zM=0$. It follows, for example, that the only simple <module> for $k[X]/(X^r)$ is the one-dimensional quotient on which $X$ acts by zero.

= Simple modules over an algebra finite over its center
{parent=Irreducible module}
{title2=$\operatorname{Ann}_{Z(A)}M\text{ is maximal}$}

If $A$ is a finite <module> over a commutative central subring $R$ and $M$ is a simple left $A$-module, then $M=Am$ is finite over $R$. A central element not annihilating $M$ acts bijectively, since its kernel and image are $A$-submodules. Put $B=R/\operatorname{Ann}_R(M)$. For $0\ne r\in B$, write generators as $m_i=r\sum_jb_{ij}m_j$. The <adjugate matrix> identity shows $\det(I-r(b_{ij}))$ annihilates $M$, hence is zero in $B$. Since this determinant has form $1-rq$, $r$ is invertible. Thus $B$ is a <field>. If $R$ is a finite-type algebra over an algebraically closed <field> $k$, the residue <field> is $k$, and every such <simple module> is finite-dimensional over $k$.

= Simple module over a commutative ring
{parent=Irreducible module}

Every simple module over a commutative ring $R$ is isomorphic to $R/\mathfrak m$ for a maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$; its annihilator is therefore maximal.

= Simple module of a finite-dimensional commutative complex algebra
{parent=Simple module over a commutative ring}
{title2=$\dim_{\mathbb C}S=1$}

For a finite-dimensional commutative complex <algebra>, a <simple module> is a cyclic quotient of the algebra and hence finite-dimensional. Every action map is a module endomorphism, since the algebra is commutative. The complex <Schur's lemma> makes each action scalar, so every vector subspace is a submodule; simplicity forces dimension one. The finite-dimensional hypothesis cannot simply be deleted: $\mathbb C(t)$ acting on itself is a simple module of infinite complex dimension.

= Associated prime of a module

An associated prime of an $R$-<module> $M$ is a <prime ideal> equal to the <annihilator> $\operatorname{Ann}_R(m)$ of some nonzero $m\in M$. The set of these primes is denoted $\operatorname{Ass}_R(M)$. = Localization of associated primes
{parent=Associated prime of a module}
{title2=$\operatorname{Ass}_{R_P}(M_P)=\{QR_P:Q\in\operatorname{Ass}_R(M),\ Q\subseteq P\}$}

For a commutative <Noetherian ring>, an <annihilator> $Q=\operatorname{Ann}_R(m)$ contained in $P$ localizes to the <annihilator> of the nonzero $m/1$. Conversely, contract an associated prime of $M_P$ represented by $m/1$ to an <ideal> $Q$. Choose finitely many generators of $Q$ and one denominator outside $P$ clearing all their annihilation equations. For this denominator $s$, the element $sm$ is nonzero and has <annihilator> exactly $Q$. This proves the formula; finite generation of the contracted <ideal> is the key use of <Noetherianity>.

{title2=$\operatorname{Min}_R(I)\subseteq\operatorname{Ass}_R(R/I)$}

For a <Noetherian ring> and a proper <ideal> $I$, every minimal prime $P$ over $I$ is an <associated prime of a module> $R/I$. The localized quotient has one prime, so its finitely generated <maximal ideal> is nilpotent and its <socle> is nonzero. An element with <annihilator> $PR_P$ can be lifted to the quotient. Clearing denominators for a finite generating set of $P$ gives a nonzero element with <annihilator> exactly $P$.

= Annihilator of a module
{parent=Annihilator (ring theory)}

The annihilator $\operatorname{Ann}_R(M)$ is the ideal of scalars that kill every element of $M$.

= Maximal annihilator of a module element is prime
{parent=Annihilator of a module}

Over a commutative unital <ring>, an ideal maximal among annihilators of nonzero elements of a <module> is a <prime ideal>. Each such ideal is the <annihilator of a module> given by the cyclic submodule generated by that element. If $P=\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ and $ab\in P$ with $b\notin P$, then $bm\ne0$ and $P\subseteq\operatorname{Ann}(bm)$. Maximality makes these annihilators equal, so $a\in P$. A <Noetherian ring> ensures that a nonzero module has a maximal element annihilator by the <ascending chain condition> on ideals.

= Structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain
{parent=Module theory}
{wiki}

Every finitely generated module over a PID is a direct sum of a free module and cyclic prime-power torsion modules.

= Invariant factors of a finitely generated module
{synonym}

For a <finitely generated module> over a <principal ideal domain>, its invariant factors are nonzero nonunits $d_i$ in a decomposition $M\cong R^r\oplus\bigoplus_iR/(d_i)$, ordered by $d_i\mid d_{i+1}$. They are unique up to multiplication by units and describe the <torsion submodule>. Over $F[t]$, making $t$ act as a <linear operator> recovers the <invariant factors of a linear operator> used in its <rational canonical form>.

= Finitely generated torsion-free module over a principal ideal domain
{parent=Structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain}

Every finitely generated torsion-free module over a principal ideal domain is free. In the structure theorem, torsion-freeness removes every cyclic torsion summand and leaves only the free summand.

= Classification of integral matrices satisfying the third cyclotomic polynomial
{parent=Finitely generated torsion-free module over a principal ideal domain}

An integral matrix satisfying $A^2+A+I=0$ makes $\mathbb Z^n$ a module over the <Eisenstein integers> by letting the primitive cube root act as $A$. This module is finitely generated and torsion-free, hence free. Since the Eisenstein integers have integer rank two, such matrices exist only for $n=2m$; for that dimension every one is integrally conjugate to
$$
\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}^{\oplus m}.
$$

= Elementary divisor
{parent=Structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain}
{wiki}

An elementary divisor is a prime power $p^e$ occurring in the cyclic decomposition of a finitely generated torsion module.

= Indecomposable finite abelian groups
{parent=Structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain}

A finite abelian group is indecomposable exactly when it is cyclic of prime-power order. The structure theorem proves necessity. Conversely, every nonzero subgroup of $C_{p^n}$ contains its unique subgroup of order $p$, so two nonzero subgroups cannot be complementary direct summands.
