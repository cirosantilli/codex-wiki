In <LS coupling>, first add the individual <orbital angular momentum>[orbital angular momenta] and <spins> separately, then add their totals:
$$
\mathbf L=\sum_i\mathbf l_i,\qquad \mathbf S=\sum_i\mathbf s_i,\qquad \mathbf J=\mathbf L+\mathbf S.
$$
The <Coulomb interaction> between <electrons> must separate different <atomic terms> more strongly than the <spin-orbit interaction> mixes them. Then $L,S,J,M_J$ are useful <quantum numbers>, with $J=|L-S|,\ldots,L+S$. This approximation is usually better for lighter <chemical atoms> and well-separated <atomic terms>; strong <configuration interaction> or strong <spin-orbit coupling> can spoil it. The <term symbol> is $^{2S+1}L_J$.

The general <electric-dipole selection rules>, for isolated <chemical atoms> with definite <parity> and total electronic <angular momentum>, are a change of <parity>, $\Delta J=0,\pm1$ excluding $J=0\leftrightarrow0$, and $\Delta M_J=0,\pm1$, with the accessible <magnetic sublevels> determined by the light's <polarization>. In pure <LS coupling>, the additional <selection rules> are $\Delta S=0$ and $\Delta L=0,\pm1$, excluding $L=0\leftrightarrow0$. In a one-<electron> description the <electric dipole operator> also requires $\Delta l=\pm1$ for the active <electron>. These latter statements can fail as exact labels when <atomic terms> mix; the general <parity> and total-<angular-momentum> restrictions still apply. Unresolved <hyperfine structure> is neglected here.

For <strontium>, the closed core contributes $L=S=0$. The <Pauli exclusion principle> makes the two equivalent $5s$ <electrons> a <singlet state>. Different radial <orbitals> permit both a <singlet state> and a <triplet state>. Thus the complete lists of <atomic fine-structure levels> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{configuration}&\text{terms}&\text{levels}\\\hline
5s^2&{}^1S&{}^1S_0\\
5s5p&{}^1P,{}^3P&{}^1P_1,{}^3P_0,{}^3P_1,{}^3P_2\\
5s6s&{}^1S,{}^3S&{}^1S_0,{}^3S_1
\end{array}}
$$
The $5s5p$ <electron configuration> has odd <parity>; the other two <electron configurations> have even <parity>.

Measure each <energy level> by the <wavenumber> $\widetilde E=(E-E_g)/(hc)$ in $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$. An upward <electronic transition> of vacuum <wavelength> $\lambda$ adds $1/\lambda$. Treat the quoted <wavelengths> as vacuum values to their limited accuracy; air corrections would be smaller than the precision justified here.

The strong ground-state <absorption> must be $5s^2\,{}^1S_0\to5s5p\,{}^1P_1$ at $461\,\mathrm{nm}$. The weak ground-state <electronic transition> at $689\,\mathrm{nm}$ must reach $5s5p\,{}^3P_1$: the <electric-dipole selection rule> on $J$ excludes the other two <triplet state> <energy levels>, while weak <spin-orbit coupling> admixes <singlet state> character and relaxes the approximate <spin selection rule>.

Assume the usual ordering $E({}^3P_0)<E({}^3P_1)<E({}^3P_2)$, as expected from a positive effective <spin-orbit coupling> constant. The three nearby strong <spectral lines> then join these <energy levels> to a common $5s6s\,{}^3S_1$ <energy level>. Larger lower-state <energy> means a smaller gap and longer <wavelength>, so $679,688,707\,\mathrm{nm}$ correspond to $J=0,1,2$ respectively. The remaining $1.12\,\mu\mathrm m$ <spectral line> joins $5s5p\,{}^1P_1$ and $5s6s\,{}^1S_0$. Assume the latter is the upper <energy level>. These assignments and orderings agree with independently tabulated <strontium> <energy levels>.<Energy Levels of Neutral Strontium by NIST>[Entries for 5s², 5s5p and 5s6s; the tabulated energies use cm⁻¹ rather than m⁻¹]{ref}

Starting with the two ground-state gaps, the construction is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde E({}^1P_1)&=(461\,\mathrm{nm})^{-1},\\
\widetilde E({}^3P_1)&=(689\,\mathrm{nm})^{-1},\\
\widetilde E({}^3S_1)&=\widetilde E({}^3P_1)+(688\,\mathrm{nm})^{-1},\\
\widetilde E({}^3P_0)&=\widetilde E({}^3S_1)-(679\,\mathrm{nm})^{-1},\\
\widetilde E({}^3P_2)&=\widetilde E({}^3S_1)-(707\,\mathrm{nm})^{-1},\\
\widetilde E(5s6s\,{}^1S_0)&=\widetilde E({}^1P_1)+(1.12\,\mu\mathrm m)^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The resulting <energy levels>, rounded to avoid suggesting spectroscopic precision, are

For $5s5d$, <addition of angular momentum> gives $L=2$ and $S=0,1$. The even-<parity> <atomic terms> and <atomic fine-structure levels> are
$$
\boxed{{}^1D_2,\qquad{}^3D_1,{}^3D_2,{}^3D_3.}
$$
The <electric-dipole selection rules> exclude all connections to the even-<parity> $5s^2$ and $5s6s$ <electron configurations>. To the odd-<parity> $5s5p$ <electron configuration>, all seven allowed connections in pure <LS coupling> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
{}^1D_2&\leftrightarrow{}^1P_1,\\
{}^3D_1&\leftrightarrow{}^3P_0,{}^3P_1,{}^3P_2,\\
{}^3D_2&\leftrightarrow{}^3P_1,{}^3P_2,\\
{}^3D_3&\leftrightarrow{}^3P_2.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Here a connection is listed independently of whether the $5s5d$ <energy level> is above or below its partner.

In a weak <magnetic field>, use the <Landé g-factor>
$$
g_J=1+\frac{J(J+1)+S(S+1)-L(L+1)}{2J(J+1)}.
$$
For the three ${}^3D_J$ <energy levels>, this gives $1/2,7/6,4/3$; for ${}^3P_1,{}^3P_2$ it gives $3/2$. The six-component <spectral line> is therefore
$$
\boxed{{}^3D_1\leftrightarrow{}^3P_1.}
$$
A component has <frequency> displacement $(\mu_BB/h)(m_D/2-3m_P/2)$. The <electric dipole transition> with $m_D=m_P=0$ has zero <matrix element>, since the relevant $J=1\leftrightarrow1$ rank-one <Clebsch-Gordan coefficients> vanish there. The remaining allowed pairs give
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{polarization}&m_D-m_P&h\Delta\nu/(\mu_BB)\\\hline
\pi&0&-1,+1\\
\sigma&+1&+1/2,+3/2\\
\sigma&-1&-3/2,-1/2
\end{array}
$$
Thus all six <frequency> positions $\pm1/2,\pm1,\pm3/2$ are distinct. Perpendicular to the <magnetic field>, both the $\pi$ and $\sigma$ components are visible, with mutually perpendicular linear <polarizations>. Along the <magnetic field> the $\pi$ components disappear. This is the weak-field <Zeeman effect>, before the <Paschen-Back effect> changes the coupling scheme.

The closest components above differ by $\Delta\nu_{\min}=\mu_BB/(2h)\simeq7.0\,\mathrm{GHz\,T^{-1}}B$. At $B=0.1\,\mathrm T$, their separation is about $0.70\,\mathrm{GHz}$. A visible <spectral line> near $500\,\mathrm{nm}$ would consequently require <spectral resolving power> of order $\nu/\Delta\nu_{\min}\simeq9\times10^5$. An ordinary low-resolution <spectrometer> would merge the components.

A suitable arrangement places an excited low-pressure <strontium> discharge or a transversely observed <atomic beam> between wide electromagnet pole pieces. Collect the emission perpendicular to the <magnetic field>, select the desired <spectral line> with a prefilter or <monochromator>, and send it through a high-finesse scanning <Fabry–Pérot interferometer> to a detector. A rotatable linear <polarizer> separates the two $\pi$ components from the four $\sigma$ components. Calibrate both the interferometer's <free spectral range> and the local <magnetic field>; take zero-field and several field-strength scans, and fit component positions against $B$.

The <magnetic field> should be uniform across the viewed volume, with its associated <Zeeman splitting> larger than the combined <natural linewidth>, <Doppler broadening>, <pressure broadening> and instrumental width, but much smaller than the separations of the relevant <atomic fine-structure levels>. A collimated transverse <atomic beam>, low collision rate and restricted collection angle reduce unwanted <spectral line broadening>. Avoid appreciable <power broadening>, resolve or model <isotope> and <hyperfine structure>, and check for self-<absorption>. A zero-field scan and the predicted linear scaling help distinguish actual <Zeeman splitting> components from neighbouring <spectral lines>.

Let the dimensionful <angular momentum operators> obey $\mathbf F=\mathbf I+\mathbf J$ and take the interaction <Hamiltonian> as $H'=A\mathbf I\cdot\mathbf J$. Since
$$
\mathbf I\cdot\mathbf J=\tfrac12(\mathbf F^2-\mathbf I^2-\mathbf J^2),
$$
the <energy eigenvalues> at fixed $I,J$ are
$$
E_F=\frac{a}{2}\{F(F+1)-I(I+1)-J(J+1)\},\qquad a=A\hbar^2,
$$
where $F=|I-J|,\ldots,I+J$. Subtraction gives the <Landé interval rule>
$$
\boxed{E_F-E_{F-1}=aF.}
$$
Many atomic conventions use dimensionless <angular momentum operators> and call the energy-valued constant itself $A$; then the right-hand side is $AF$. Each <energy level> has $2F+1$ <magnetic sublevels> with the same <energy> in zero field.

For <fine structure>, the effective interaction is $a_{LS}\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S$ with dimensionless <angular momentum operators>. It originates in the <spin-orbit interaction>, including the magnetic interaction of <electron spin> with orbital motion and its relativistic correction. Within an isolated <LS coupling> <atomic term>, the labels are the <electron configuration>, $L,S,J,M_J$, and the interval between adjacent $J$ <energy levels> is $a_{LS}J$. This is smaller than the electrostatic separation of <atomic terms>.

For magnetic-dipole <hyperfine structure>, the <nuclear magnetic dipole moment> interacts with the magnetic field generated by the <electrons>. Contributions include the <Fermi contact interaction> of penetrating $s$ <electrons>, orbital magnetic fields, and the dipolar field of <electron spin>. Projected onto one electronic $J$ <energy level>, their rotationally invariant magnetic-dipole part has the form $a_{IJ}\mathbf I\cdot\mathbf J$. The labels are $I,J,F,M_F$, together with the approximate electronic labels such as $L,S$. This splitting is generally smaller than <fine structure>, reflecting the much smaller nuclear <magnetic dipole moment>. An electric <nuclear quadrupole moment> can add a different interaction and violate the simple <Landé interval rule>.

At the larger electrostatic <energy> scale, an effective two-<spin> exchange interaction can also be written $a_s\mathbf s_1\cdot\mathbf s_2$ within fixed <orbitals>. Its origin is the <Coulomb interaction> combined with the <Pauli exclusion principle>, rather than a large direct magnetic interaction. It separates <singlet state> and <triplet state> <atomic terms>; before <spin-orbit coupling>, useful labels include the <electron configuration>, $L,M_L,S,M_S$. General electrostatic <atomic term> separations need not obey a single <Landé interval rule> as $L$ varies.

Choose the relative phases of the two <energy eigenstates> so that $d=\langle\psi_2|\widehat D_0|\psi_1\rangle$ is real and nonnegative. Define the resonant <Rabi angular frequency> $\Omega=d/\hbar$, the <Rabi detuning> $\delta=\omega_0-\omega$, and $\Sigma=\omega_0+\omega$. Substitution into the <Schrödinger equation>, followed by projection onto the two <eigenstates>, gives the exact <interaction-picture> equations
$$
i\dot c_1=\frac{\Omega}{2}(e^{-i\delta t}+e^{-i\Sigma t})c_2,\qquad
i\dot c_2=\frac{\Omega}{2}(e^{i\delta t}+e^{i\Sigma t})c_1.
$$
Keep both <probability amplitudes> dynamical: replacing $c_1$ by one would incorrectly impose weak excitation and miss complete population transfer.

For $|\delta|,\Omega\ll\omega_0$, apply the <rotating-wave approximation> to discard the rapidly oscillating terms at $\Sigma$. Differentiating the second remaining equation and using the first gives
$$
\ddot c_2-i\delta\dot c_2+\frac{\Omega^2}{4}c_2=0.
$$
The <initial conditions> are $c_2(0)=0$ and $\dot c_2(0)=-i\Omega/2$. With $\Omega_R=\sqrt{\Omega^2+\delta^2}$, their solution is
$$
c_2(t)=-i\frac{\Omega}{\Omega_R}e^{i\delta t/2}\sin\frac{\Omega_Rt}{2},\qquad
c_1(t)=e^{-i\delta t/2}\left(\cos\frac{\Omega_Rt}{2}+i\frac{\delta}{\Omega_R}\sin\frac{\Omega_Rt}{2}\right).
$$
Consequently,
$$
\boxed{|c_2(t)|^2=U\sin^2(\alpha t),\qquad
U=\frac{\Omega^2}{\Omega^2+\delta^2},\qquad
\alpha=\tfrac12\sqrt{\Omega^2+\delta^2}.}
$$
This result permits order-one excitation, including full population transfer on <resonance>, even though the drive is small compared with the optical <angular frequency>.

For an average from switch-on to time $\tau$, integrate the squared sine:
$$
\boxed{\overline P_2(\tau)=\frac1\tau\int_0^\tau|c_2(t)|^2dt
=\frac U2\left[1-\frac{\sin(\Omega_R\tau)}{\Omega_R\tau}\right],\qquad
\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\overline P_2=\frac U2.}
$$
On <resonance> this limiting average is $1/2$, although the instantaneous <probability> repeatedly reaches one. The zero-coupling, zero-<Rabi detuning> case is understood separately as $P_2=0$.

**Near <resonance> alone does not make this an exact result for arbitrarily strong driving.** The stated sinusoidal <Hamiltonian> also needs the <rotating-wave approximation>. A concrete counterexample is $\omega=\omega_0$ and $\Omega=\omega_0$: the exact equations give $P_2(t)=\Omega^2t^2+O(t^4)$ at switch-on, while the claimed rotating-wave sinusoid gives $\Omega^2t^2/4+O(t^4)$. Thus “without assuming weak radiation” can mean without assuming small excitation, but cannot remove the drive-to-carrier scale separation. The infinite-time average above belongs to the rotating-wave model; omitted corrections can accumulate over sufficiently long times.

<Rabi oscillations> require a coherent drive with a stable phase over several population cycles and an isolated, appreciably coupled pair of <quantum states>. For large-amplitude oscillations, the <Rabi detuning> must be comparable to or smaller than the <Rabi angular frequency>. The population lifetime, <optical coherence time>, transit time and drive coherence time should all exceed $2\pi/\Omega_R$ by enough to resolve the cycles. An ensemble also needs sufficiently uniform <Rabi frequencies> and <Rabi detunings> that phase averaging does not wash the oscillations out. The usual sinusoidal formula additionally assumes the <rotating-wave approximation>.

The <Einstein coefficients> describe incoherent transition *rates*, rather than the evolving optical <probability amplitudes>. Their simplest use is appropriate for broadband, randomly phased <radiation>, with field correlation time short relative to population evolution, or for rapidly dephased <chemical atoms> whose optical coherences can be eliminated. The <radiation energy density> per unit <frequency> must be smooth over the relevant <spectral line>. Stimulated upward and downward transitions and <spontaneous emission> then enter <rate equations>, without predicting coherent population return. A short observation time can give a small excitation <probability> even for a coherent field; that condition alone does not justify an <Einstein coefficients> rate description. Coherent <laser> irradiation can also admit steady <rate equations> after optical coherence has relaxed, with the appropriate <saturation> response.

Let the upper-state population lifetime be $T_1=1/\Gamma$, and take the lower <energy level> to be stable. <Spontaneous emission> gives survival <probability> $e^{-\Gamma t}$, hence a field or excited-state <probability amplitude> proportional to $e^{-\Gamma t/2}e^{-i\omega_0t}$ for $t\geq0$. Its <Fourier transform> at <angular frequency> $\omega$ is proportional to
$$
\int_0^\infty e^{-\Gamma t/2}e^{i(\omega-\omega_0)t}\,dt
=\frac1{\Gamma/2-i(\omega-\omega_0)}.
$$
The squared modulus therefore has the <Lorentzian line shape>
$$
\boxed{L(\omega)=\frac1\pi\frac{\Gamma/2}{(\omega-\omega_0)^2+(\Gamma/2)^2},
\qquad\Delta\omega_{\rm FWHM}=\Gamma=\frac1{T_1}.}
$$
Here $L$ is normalized over the full mathematical <frequency> axis. The <full width at half maximum> in ordinary <frequency> is $1/(2\pi T_1)$. If both <energy levels> decay, their population decay rates add in the <natural linewidth>; pure <dephasing> adds to the amplitude-decay rate instead.

Impact-regime <pressure broadening> provides another example. Independent phase-randomizing collisions with rate $\gamma_c$ give optical correlation proportional to $e^{-\gamma_c|t|}$, whose <Fourier transform> is a <Lorentzian line shape> of <full width at half maximum> $2\gamma_c$. This assumes short, uncorrelated impacts: a general static distribution of perturbing fields need not give a <Lorentzian line shape>.

Use first-order <time-dependent perturbation theory>, setting $c_1=1$ in the equation for $c_2$ and retaining the generally complex <matrix element> $d=\langle\psi_2|\widehat D_0|\psi_1\rangle$. With $\delta=\omega_0-\omega$ and $\Sigma=\omega_0+\omega$, the <probability amplitude> is
$$
c_2(t)=-\frac{id}{2\hbar}\int_{-\infty}^t e^{-b|s|}\{e^{i\delta s}+e^{i\Sigma s}\}\,ds.
$$
For $t>0$, split the <integral> at the pulse maximum. For either $x=\delta$ or $x=\Sigma$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
F(x,t)&=\int_{-\infty}^0e^{(b+ix)s}ds+\int_0^te^{(-b+ix)s}ds\\
&=\frac1{b+ix}+\frac{1-e^{(-b+ix)t}}{b-ix}
=\frac{2b}{b^2+x^2}-\frac{e^{(-b+ix)t}}{b-ix}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the full first-order answer, without discarding the counter-rotating contribution, is
$$
\boxed{c_2(t)=-\frac{id}{2\hbar}\{F(\delta,t)+F(\Sigma,t)\},\qquad t>0.}
$$
At long times it gives
$$
\boxed{P_2(\infty)=\frac{|d|^2b^2}{\hbar^2}
\left(\frac1{b^2+\delta^2}+\frac1{b^2+\Sigma^2}\right)^2.}
$$
This full expression is not exactly one squared <Lorentzian line shape>. To obtain the intended near-<resonance> <spectral line> profile, additionally assume $b,|\delta|\ll\omega_0$, so the pulse has a narrow positive-<frequency> lobe and $F(\Sigma,t)$ is negligible. Then
$$
c_2(t)\simeq-\frac{id}{2\hbar}\left[\frac{2b}{b^2+\delta^2}-\frac{e^{(-b+i\delta)t}}{b-i\delta}\right],
\qquad
\boxed{P_2(\infty)\simeq\frac{|d|^2}{\hbar^2b^2}\frac1{[1+(\delta/b)^2]^2}.}
$$
The weak-excitation condition near <resonance> is $|d|/(\hbar b)\ll1$. The squared <Lorentzian line shape> occurs because the symmetric exponential pulse has a <Lorentzian line shape> *amplitude* <Fourier transform>, which is then squared to obtain <probability>.

At half the maximum, $[1+(\delta/b)^2]^2=2$, so
$$
\boxed{\Delta\omega_{\rm FWHM}=2b\sqrt{\sqrt2-1}\simeq1.2872b,
\qquad\Delta\nu_{\rm FWHM}=\frac b\pi\sqrt{\sqrt2-1}.}
$$
The <full width at half maximum> refers to varying the drive <frequency> through the positive-<frequency> <resonance>. For a concrete failure of the unqualified squared-<Lorentzian line shape> claim, set $b=\omega=\omega_0$ while keeping $|d|/(\hbar b)\ll1$. The full first-order resonant <probability> is $36/25$ times the one-lobe prediction. Weak radiation alone therefore does not justify dropping the second <Fourier transform> lobe.

Define $r=g_2/g_1$ and let $\sigma=\sigma_{21}(\omega)$ be the stimulated-<emission cross section>, averaged over upper <magnetic sublevels>. The <Einstein coefficients> relation $g_1B_{12}=g_2B_{21}$ gives the <absorption cross section> $\sigma_{12}=r\sigma$. Assume equally populated <magnetic sublevels> within each <energy level>, a steady pump, and a monochromatic beam. The photon flux is $I/(\hbar\omega)$; define $W=\sigma I/(\hbar\omega)$ and the effective <population inversion> $D=N_2-rN_1$. Then the <rate equations> are
$$
\boxed{\dot N_2=R_2-A_{21}N_2-WD,\qquad
\dot N_1=A_{21}N_2+WD-\frac{N_1}{\tau_1}.}
$$
The $WD$ term includes both <stimulated emission> and <absorption>. In steady state, adding the two <rate equations> gives $N_1=R_2\tau_1$. The first equation then yields
$$
N_2=\frac{R_2+WrR_2\tau_1}{A_{21}+W},\qquad
D=\frac{R_2(1-rA_{21}\tau_1)}{A_{21}+W}.
$$
The directed beam gains <energy> at rate $\hbar\omega WD$ per volume, giving $dI/dz=\sigma DI$ and the <optical gain> coefficient $\alpha=\sigma D$. At line centre, write $\sigma_0=\sigma_{21}(\omega_{21})$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac{\alpha_0}{1+I/I_s},\qquad
\alpha_0=\sigma_0R_2\left(\frac1{A_{21}}-r\tau_1\right),\qquad
I_s=\frac{\hbar\omega_{21}A_{21}}{\sigma_0}.}
$$
Here $\alpha_0$ is the small-signal <optical gain> coefficient and $I_s$ is the <saturation intensity>, at which the local <optical gain> is half its small-signal value. Positive <optical gain> requires $rA_{21}\tau_1<1$: the lower <energy level> must empty sufficiently quickly.

The cancellation of $\tau_1$ from $I_s$ is specific to this model's steady fixed pump and the fact that **every upper <spontaneous emission> feeds the lower <energy level>**. Adding an extra lower-state accumulation factor to $I_s$ would contradict these <rate equations>. Alternative conventions using the <absorption cross section> must replace $\sigma_0$ by $\sigma_{12}/r$ throughout. <Homogeneous spectral broadening> permits the whole selected ensemble to share this local <saturation> response.

In a uniform steady amplifier, with no extra propagation losses, the local <optical gain> law gives
$$
\frac{dI}{dz}=\frac{\alpha_0I}{1+I/I_s},\qquad
\left(\frac1I+\frac1{I_s}\right)dI=\alpha_0\,dz.
$$
Integrate between the two faces of length $L$ to obtain
$$
\boxed{\ln\frac{I_{\rm out}}{I_{\rm in}}+\frac{I_{\rm out}-I_{\rm in}}{I_s}=\alpha_0L.}
$$
For $I\ll I_s$, this reduces to exponential amplification. In deep <saturation>, the <intensity> increment approaches $\alpha_0I_sL$: finite pumping limits the extracted power.

For a closed absorbing <two-level system>, write $N=N_1+N_2$, let $A=1/\tau$, and use $\sigma_a=\sigma_{12}$ for the <absorption cross section>. This differs from the stimulated-<emission cross section> convention in part (a). With $r=g_2/g_1$, the <rate equations> are
$$
\boxed{\dot N_2=\frac{\sigma_a I}{\hbar\omega}\left(N_1-\frac{N_2}{r}\right)-AN_2,\qquad
\dot N_1=-\dot N_2.}
$$
At steady state, $N_2=W_aN/[A+(1+1/r)W_a]$ for $W_a=\sigma_aI/(\hbar\omega)$. Hence the positive <absorption coefficient> is
$$
\kappa=\sigma_a\left(N_1-\frac{N_2}{r}\right)
=\frac{N\sigma_a}{1+I/I_{s,a}},\qquad
I_{s,a}=\frac{\hbar\omega}{(1+1/r)\sigma_a\tau}.
$$
For the given <sodium> fine-structure transition, the <statistical weights> are $g_1=2$, $g_2=4$, so $r=2$ and $1+1/r=3/2$. The small-signal transmission gives the <optical depth>
$$
D_0=N\sigma_aL=-\ln0.90=0.1053605.
$$
To account for the finite decrease of <intensity> through the sample, integrate $dI/dz=-\kappa I$ rather than treating the stronger beam as exactly uniform. If $T=I_{\rm out}/I_{\rm in}$, this gives
$$
\ln T+\frac{I_{\rm in}(T-1)}{I_{s,a}}=-D_0,\qquad
I_{s,a}=\frac{I_{\rm in}(1-T)}{D_0+\ln T}.
$$
Taking $270\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ as the incident <intensity> and $T=0.967$,
$$
I_{s,a}=\frac{270(0.033)}{0.1053605+\ln0.967}
\simeq124.09\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}.
$$
Using the <photon energy> $hc/\lambda$ then gives
$$
\boxed{\sigma_a=\frac{hc}{(3/2)\lambda\tau I_{s,a}}
\simeq1.13\times10^{-13}\,\mathrm{m^2}.}
$$
Ignoring the small variation of the stronger <intensity> would instead give about $1.11\times10^{-13}\,\mathrm{m^2}$; the integrated value uses the stated transmissions more consistently. The length is not needed to eliminate $N$, but gives the check $N\simeq9.3\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$.

The resonant <absorption cross section> is of optical-<wavelength>-squared size, much larger than a typical atomic geometric collision area. <Pressure-broadening> <cross sections> involve phase-changing interatomic encounters and need not equal hard-sphere collision areas; their numerical value depends on collision partners and speed. No precise ratio follows from the supplied data. A useful distinction is that resonant <absorption> involves coherent response on the optical scale, so its <cross section> need not resemble an <chemical atom>'s geometric area.

For <homogeneous spectral broadening>, all participating <chemical atoms> must share the same <resonance> and single-atom response width. In a dilute sample dominated by <natural linewidth>, the weak-beam requirement is
$$
\Delta\nu_D=\frac1\lambda\sqrt{\frac{8\ln2\,k_BT}{m_{\rm Na}}}
\ll\frac1{2\pi\tau}\simeq9.95\,\mathrm{MHz}.
$$
Using the <sodium> <atomic mass>, equality occurs near $T=0.017\,\mathrm K$, so $T\ll17\,\mathrm{mK}$ is a sufficient thermal condition. Spatial <Zeeman energy> shifts, light shifts and transit effects should also be small, the <laser> should address the same selected component, and the interaction should last long enough for steady populations. Uniform impact <pressure broadening> can also be homogeneous, but would change the width and effective <cross section>; it is not included in the lifetime-only numerical model.

Real <sodium> also has <hyperfine structure> and <optical pumping> among <magnetic sublevels>. The inferred $\sigma_a$ is the effective cross section of the stated $g_J=2J+1$ <two-level system> model, assuming statistical sublevel populations and no loss into unaddressed states. A measurement on a resolved, polarized closed <hyperfine structure> component requires its actual <transition dipole moment> and <selection rules>; a universal polarization-independent <sodium> cross section cannot be inferred from this simplified model.
