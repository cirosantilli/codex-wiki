= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

The printed <magnetic dipole moment> uses dimensionless <angular momentum operators>: their physical counterparts are $\hbar\mathbf L$ and $\hbar\mathbf S$. Choose the <magnetic field> along $z$. Then the <Zeeman energy> is $H_Z=\mu_BB(L_z+g_sS_z)$, whose characteristic size is $\mu_BB$. In <LS coupling>, treating this as a <time-independent perturbation theory> requires it to be much smaller than the relevant <fine-structure> separation, rather than merely smaller than the <Coulomb interaction> <energy>.

For a concrete <hydrogen atom> estimate take the $2p_{1/2},2p_{3/2}$ pair. The <hydrogen fine-structure correction> is
$$
E_{nj}=-\frac{\mathrm{Ry}}{n^2}-\frac{\mathrm{Ry}\,\alpha_{\rm fs}^2}{n^3}\left(\frac1{j+1/2}-\frac3{4n}\right),
$$
where $\alpha_{\rm fs}$ is the <fine-structure constant>, distinguished from the later potential coefficient. Thus
$$
\Delta E_{2p}=\frac{\mathrm{Ry}\,\alpha_{\rm fs}^2}{16}\simeq4.53\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{eV},\qquad
\frac{\Delta E_{2p}}{\mu_B}\simeq0.78\,\mathrm T.
$$
Consequently a suitable weak-<magnetic-field> condition for this pair is
$$
\boxed{B\ll0.8\,\mathrm T;\quad B\sim10^{-2}\,\mathrm T\text{ is comfortably perturbative.}}
$$
There is no single threshold for every <hydrogen> <energy level>: the relevant <fine-structure> separations decrease with $n$. The $1s$ <energy level> has no same-$n$ <spin-orbit interaction> partner, so it cannot itself define this comparison. This calculation neglects <hyperfine structure>; resolving that smaller scale requires an additional weak-<magnetic-field> condition.

Within a fixed $L,S,J$ <angular momentum> multiplet, the component of $\mathbf L$ along $\mathbf J$ follows from $2\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf J=\mathbf J^2+\mathbf L^2-\mathbf S^2$. The corresponding result for $\mathbf S$ follows by interchanging $L$ and $S$. For the <quantum state> $|LSJM_J\rangle$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle L_z\rangle&=M_J\frac{J(J+1)+L(L+1)-S(S+1)}{2J(J+1)},\\
\langle S_z\rangle&=M_J\frac{J(J+1)+S(S+1)-L(L+1)}{2J(J+1)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
These are dimensionless <expectation values> in the convention of part (a). Substituting into the first-order <time-independent perturbation theory> formula $\Delta E=\langle H_Z\rangle$ gives
$$
\boxed{\Delta E=g_J\mu_BBM_J,\qquad
g_J=1+(g_s-1)\frac{J(J+1)+S(S+1)-L(L+1)}{2J(J+1)}.}
$$
For $g_s=2$, the <Landé g-factor> becomes
$$
\boxed{g_J=1+\frac{J(J+1)+S(S+1)-L(L+1)}{2J(J+1)}.}
$$
The <magnetic sublevels> must first be diagonalized within a degenerate multiplet; choosing $M_J$ along the <magnetic field> already does this. For $J=0$ the displayed fraction is undefined, but all vector <expectation values> vanish and there is no first-order <Zeeman splitting>. A higher-order shift may remain.

Write $k=e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$ and retain the nuclear <Coulomb potential>. In <spherical coordinates>, the total <potential energy> is
$$
V=-\frac{k}{r}+r^2[\alpha+(\beta-\alpha)\cos^2\theta]
=-\frac{k}{r}+\frac{\alpha r^2}{2}[1-2P_2(\cos\theta)]
$$
when $\beta=-\alpha/2$; $P_2(u)=(3u^2-1)/2$ is a <Legendre polynomial>. Treat the added term using <quadratic anisotropic perturbation of hydrogen> and <degenerate perturbation theory> in the $n=2$ <hydrogen energy eigenbasis>. It preserves <parity> and $m$, so it cannot mix $2s$ with $2p$, or distinct $2p$ <magnetic sublevels>.

For a <hydrogen atom>, the <hydrogen radial second moment> gives
$$
\langle r^2\rangle_{nl}=\frac{a_0^2n^2}{2}[5n^2+1-3l(l+1)],
\qquad
\langle r^2\rangle_{1s}=3a_0^2,\quad
\langle r^2\rangle_{2s}=42a_0^2,\quad
\langle r^2\rangle_{2p}=30a_0^2.
$$
The angular <expectation values> of $\cos^2\theta$ are $1/3$ for an $s$ <orbital>, $3/5$ for $p_0$, and $1/5$ for $p_{\pm1}$, obtained by integrating the squared <spherical harmonics>. Therefore the five lowest spatial <quantum states>, counting <statistical weight>, have first-order <energy levels>
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{state}&E\text{ to first order}&\text{spatial multiplicity}\\\hline
1s&-\mathrm{Ry}+\tfrac32\alpha a_0^2&1\\
2p_0&-\mathrm{Ry}/4+3\alpha a_0^2&1\\
2s,\ 2p_{+1},\ 2p_{-1}&-\mathrm{Ry}/4+21\alpha a_0^2&3
\end{array}}
$$
Each spatial <quantum state> has two <electron spin> states when <spin-orbit coupling> and the <magnetic field> are omitted. Thus these are five spatial states but only three distinct first-order <energies>; five distinct <energies> would be an incorrect reading. The accidental $2s,2p_{\pm1}$ equality can be lifted at higher order. Quantitatively the added shifts must also be small compared with neighboring principal-shell gaps; $\alpha\ll k/a_0^3$ is an asymptotic condition, not a guarantee for arbitrary large $n$.

\Image[2021-quadratic-hydrogen-levels.png]
{title=First-order spatial levels of locally perturbed hydrogen; the gap between principal shells is compressed}

For general $\beta$, the $1s$ <expectation value> is
$$
\Delta E_{1s}^{(1)}=(2\alpha+\beta)a_0^2,\qquad
\boxed{\beta=-2\alpha\text{ cancels the first-order shift.}}
$$
It does **not** cancel every order. At $\beta=-2\alpha$, $V_{\rm ext}=-2\alpha r^2P_2(\cos\theta)$ couples $1s$ to $d$ <orbitals>. For example its angular <matrix element> with $Y_{20}$ is $1/\sqrt5$, and the $3d$ radial <matrix element> is nonzero. The <second-order nondegenerate perturbation theory> sum has negative denominators, so this is a concrete nonzero higher-order contribution.

There is also a global limitation: for either negative $\beta$ above, $V(0,0,z)=-k/|z|+\beta z^2\to-\infty$. A localized wavepacket translated far along $z$ has fixed <kinetic energy> but arbitrarily negative <potential energy>, proving there is no true lowest <energy level>. The results describe local perturbative <resonance poles>, or a potential completed to a confining form far from the <atomic nucleus>. No globally exact ground-state cancellation exists for the potential as printed.

Neglect <hyperfine structure>, <spin-orbit coupling> and the diamagnetic term, and set $b=\mu_BB$, $\varepsilon=\alpha a_0^2$. The <Zeeman energy> is $b(L_{\widehat B}+2S_{\widehat B})$. The <electron spin> is free, so every spatial result below is duplicated with an added $\pm b$.

For a <magnetic field> parallel to $z$, the old <magnetic quantum number> $m$ remains good and the first-order <energy levels> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
E_{1s,\pm}&=-\mathrm{Ry}+\tfrac32\varepsilon\pm b,\\
E_{2p_0,\pm}&=-\mathrm{Ry}/4+3\varepsilon\pm b,\\
E_{2s,\pm}&=-\mathrm{Ry}/4+21\varepsilon\pm b,\\
E_{2p_m,\pm}&=-\mathrm{Ry}/4+21\varepsilon+mb\pm b,\quad m=\pm1.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The two $s$ <orbitals> have zero <orbital angular momentum>, so only their <electron spin> contributes at this order.

For a <magnetic field> parallel to $x$, the expectation of $L_x$ in each individual $p_m$ <orbital> vanishes. One must still check the degenerate $m=\pm1$ subspace: its projected $L_x$ is zero as well. Thus **there is no first-order orbital splitting in a sufficiently weak transverse <magnetic field>**. Spin still contributes $\pm b$.

The <transverse orbital Zeeman mixing> can be seen without assuming its size. Put $E_0=-\mathrm{Ry}/4+3\varepsilon$, $E_t=-\mathrm{Ry}/4+21\varepsilon$, and $\Delta=E_t-E_0=18\varepsilon$. In the real $p_x,p_y,p_z$ <orbital> basis, $p_x$ is unaffected while $p_y,p_z$ have off-diagonal <matrix elements> of modulus $b$. Diagonalizing this two-dimensional <Hamiltonian> gives the spatial <energy eigenvalues>
$$
\boxed{E_t,\qquad \frac{E_t+E_0}{2}\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac\Delta2\right)^2+b^2}.}
$$
When $b\ll\Delta$, their shifts relative to the earlier levels are $0,+b^2/\Delta,-b^2/\Delta$, with $\pm b$ added for <electron spin>. The displayed square-root expression also handles $b$ comparable to $\Delta$, provided coupling to other principal shells stays negligible. The $2s$ <orbital> retains zero orbital correction.

These quadratic orbital estimates keep the enhanced contribution from mixing the split $2p$ levels. Minimal <magnetic minimal coupling> also produces $e^2B^2r_\perp^2/(8m_e)$; its <expectation value> is positive and must be added for a full order-$B^2$ calculation. For example it gives $e^2B^2a_0^2/(4m_e)$ for $1s$. It is parametrically smaller than the $2p$ mixing term when $\varepsilon\ll\mathrm{Ry}$.

Expand the <wavefunction> in unperturbed <energy eigenstates>,
$$
|\psi(t)\rangle=c_1(t)e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}|1\rangle+c_2(t)e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}|2\rangle.
$$
The $c_j$ are <interaction-picture> <probability amplitudes>, so $|c_j|^2$ are level populations and $|c_1|^2+|c_2|^2=1$ in this closed <two-level system>. A classical field $\mathbf E_0\cos\omega t$ supplies the <electric-dipole interaction> $-\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf E_0\cos\omega t$. Choosing the relative phase of the <energy eigenstates> makes $\Omega=-\langle1|\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf E_0|2\rangle/\hbar$ real. This is the resonant <Rabi angular frequency>.

Combining the field's two exponentials with the unperturbed <quantum phases> produces terms oscillating at $\omega-\omega_0=\delta$ and at $\omega+\omega_0$. The <rotating-wave approximation> drops the latter, leaving exactly the supplied equations with coupling $\Omega/2$. It requires $|\Omega|,|\delta|\ll\omega_0$ and a field envelope slow enough on the carrier timescale. <Spontaneous emission> and <dephasing> must be negligible during the sequence. For a ground-state <hyperfine-level> transition, the analogous direct drive is a <magnetic dipole transition>; the same equations hold with the magnetic <matrix element> replacing the electric one.

Assume an initially unexcited <chemical atom>, two nonoverlapping square pulses starting at $0,T$, and a common coherent field phase. In the weak-excitation limit $|\Omega|\tau_p\ll1$, substitute $c_1=1+O(\Omega^2)$ into the second <probability-amplitude> equation. After the second pulse,
$$
\begin{aligned}
c_2&=-\frac{i\Omega}{2}\left(\int_0^{\tau_p}e^{-i\delta t}\,dt+\int_T^{T+\tau_p}e^{-i\delta t}\,dt\right)+O(\Omega^3)\\
&=-i\Omega\tau_p\,e^{-i\delta(T+\tau_p)/2}\operatorname{sinc}(\delta\tau_p/2)\cos(\delta T/2)+O(\Omega^3),
\end{aligned}
$$
where the <sinc function> means $\operatorname{sinc}u=\sin u/u$, with value one at zero. Squaring gives
$$
\boxed{P_2=(\Omega\tau_p)^2\operatorname{sinc}^2(\delta\tau_p/2)\cos^2(\delta T/2)+O((\Omega\tau_p)^4).}
$$
The printed prefactor is a factor of four too small for the supplied equations and pulse durations. A direct counterexample is $\delta=0$: the exact resonant <Rabi oscillation> gives $P_2=\sin^2(\Omega\tau_p)$ for two pulses of duration $\tau_p$, because the intervening resonant rotating-frame evolution is trivial. Its small-area limit is $(\Omega\tau_p)^2$, rather than $(\Omega\tau_p/2)^2$. The latter is the single-pulse result before coherent addition.

The condition $\omega\tau_p\ll1$ printed in the source also does not justify the derivation from a physical carrier field. A consistent <rotating-wave approximation> pulse regime is
$$
\boxed{\omega_0^{-1}\ll\tau_p\ll|\Omega|^{-1},\qquad |\delta|\ll\omega_0.}
$$
If the supplied equations are treated as the mathematical model regardless, their weak-excitation integral remains valid for any $\tau_p$; subcycle pulses of the original real field require the discarded counter-rotating term and depend on carrier phase. $T$ here is the separation of pulse starts, as specified, rather than the dark interval $T-\tau_p$.

The ground <electron configuration> of <caesium> has a single $6s$ <electron>, with $J=1/2$. <Addition of angular momentum> giving $F=3,4$ fixes the <nuclear spin> to $I=7/2$. With dimensionless <angular momentum operators>, write the <Fermi contact interaction> as
$$
H_{\rm hf}=A\mathbf I\cdot\mathbf J,\qquad
E_F=\frac A2[F(F+1)-I(I+1)-J(J+1)].
$$
The two <hyperfine levels> therefore differ by $4A$. In the convention $\boldsymbol\mu_I=g_I\mu_B\mathbf I$ and $g_s\simeq2$,
$$
A=\frac{2\mu_0}{3}g_sg_I\mu_B^2|\psi_{6s}(0)|^2,\qquad
\boxed{\omega_0=\frac{4A}{\hbar}.}
$$
This exposes a missing ingredient in a prediction from the supplied data: $Z$ and $g_I$ alone do not specify the valence <electron> contact density. <Screening effect> and penetration of the closed core strongly affect it. If $g_I$ instead denotes the total nuclear moment in units of $\mu_B$, it must first be divided by $I$ in the displayed convention.

An explicitly crude unscreened <hydrogen-like ion> model uses $|\psi_{6s}(0)|^2=Z^3/(\pi6^3a_0^3)$ and gives $\nu_0\simeq5.76\times10^{11}\,\mathrm{Hz}$, or $\omega_0\simeq3.62\times10^{12}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. It describes neither the shielding nor the radial function of neutral <caesium>. Using a pure unit-charge $6s$ <hydrogen> <orbital> instead gives only $3.46\,\mathrm{MHz}$, illustrating why a unique useful estimate cannot follow from an unspecified effective charge.

For physical neutral $^{133}\mathrm{Cs}$, the independently known zero-<magnetic-field> <clock transition> is
$$
\boxed{\nu_0=9\,192\,631\,770\,\mathrm{Hz},\qquad
\omega_0=2\pi\nu_0\simeq5.776\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}.}
$$
This measured <frequency>, used to define the <second>, is supplementary physical information, not a consequence of $Z=55$ and the rounded $g_I$ alone.<SI base unit: second (s) by Bureau International des Poids et Mesures>[Definition: the unperturbed ground-state hyperfine transition frequency of caesium-133 is exactly 9 192 631 770 Hz]{ref} The direct <electronic transition> is in the microwave range and is a <magnetic dipole transition>, rather than a direct optical <electric dipole transition>. An optical implementation needs a two-field <Raman transition>.

Using the corrected <weak-pulse Ramsey spectrum> of part (b), the peak at $\delta=0$ has height $(\Omega\tau_p)^2$. The <quantum-interference> fringes have spacing $2\pi/T$ in <angular frequency>, with zeros at $(2k+1)\pi/T$, and the <sinc-function> envelope has its first zeros at $\delta=\pm2\pi/\tau_p$. For $T\gg\tau_p$, the central peak has approximately <full width at half maximum> $\pi/T$ in <angular frequency>. The <clock transition> between the two $M_F=0$ states has no first-order <Zeeman energy>, although a quadratic one remains.

The first-order excitation amplitude is the <Fourier transform> of the temporal pulse envelope evaluated at the <Rabi detuning>. Two rectangular time windows therefore give exactly the mathematical structure of <double-slit interference>: each window gives a <sinc-function> amplitude, and their separation gives a relative <quantum phase> $\delta T$. The <transition probability> is a <sinc-function> squared envelope multiplied by $\cos^2(\delta T/2)$.

Pulse duration corresponds to slit width, pulse-start separation to slit-center separation, and <Rabi detuning> to the transverse <wavevector> sampled on the screen. The narrow fringes measure the long separation, while the broad envelope measures the short interaction duration. As in spatial <interference>, adding <probabilities> instead of coherent <probability amplitudes> would lose the fringes. Randomizing the relative pulse phase averages the cosine squared to $1/2$.

Differentiating the specified rotating-frame amplitudes gives the time-independent <Schrödinger equation>
$$
i\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde c_1\\\widetilde c_2\end{pmatrix}
=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}\delta&\Omega\\\Omega&-\delta\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde c_1\\\widetilde c_2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The <characteristic polynomial> is $\lambda^2-(\delta^2+\Omega^2)/4=0$, hence
$$
\lambda_\pm=\pm\frac12\sqrt{\delta^2+\Omega^2}.
$$
For $\Omega\ne0$, corresponding normalized <eigenvectors> are proportional to $(\Omega,2\lambda_\pm-\delta)^T$. Their arbitrary complex coefficients evolve as $e^{-i\lambda_\pm t}$; this solves the amplitude equations for any initial <quantum state>.

Restoring the common <energy> origin $\overline E=(E_1+\hbar\omega+E_2)/2$, the two <dressed-state> <energy eigenvalues> are
$$
E_\pm=\overline E\pm\frac\hbar2\sqrt{\delta^2+\Omega^2}.
$$
Thus their positive <energy> separation increases by
$$
\boxed{\Delta(E_+-E_-)=\hbar[\sqrt{\delta^2+\Omega^2}-|\delta|]
=\frac{\hbar\Omega^2}{2|\delta|}+O(\Omega^4/|\delta|^3).}
$$
Equivalently, the branches connected to $E_1'=E_1+\hbar\omega$ and $E_2$ shift by $+\hbar\Omega^2/(4\delta)$ and $-\hbar\Omega^2/(4\delta)$ respectively. Their *signed* difference $E_1'-E_2$ changes by $\hbar\Omega^2/(2\delta)$, which is the printed expression with a signed convention. For negative <Rabi detuning>, an actual positive separation still increases; for example at $\delta=-10\Omega$ it cannot have the negative change implied by reading the printed expression as an unsigned gap. These opposite <AC Stark shifts> describe level repulsion.

Specify the radiation normalization first. Let $u_\nu$ be <radiation energy density> per ordinary <frequency>, with SI units $\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}\,Hz^{-1}}$. Then $A_{21}$ is the <spontaneous-emission> rate per upper-state <chemical atom>, while $B_{12}u_\nu$ and $B_{21}u_\nu$ are the induced <absorption> and <stimulated-emission> rates, evaluated at a sharp transition <frequency>. For a finite <spectral line profile>, replace $u_\nu$ by its profile-weighted average.

The <Einstein coefficients> obey
$$
\boxed{g_1B_{12}=g_2B_{21},\qquad
\frac{A_{21}}{B_{21}}=\frac{8\pi h\nu_0^3}{c^3}.}
$$
Here $g_i$ are the <statistical weights> of the two <energy levels>, and the field is unpolarized and isotropic. The units and <physical dimensions> are
$$
[A]=\mathrm{s^{-1}},\quad [A]=\mathsf T^{-1};\qquad
[B]=\mathrm{m^3\,J^{-1}\,s^{-2}},\quad [B]=\mathsf L\mathsf M^{-1}.
$$
The apparently time-independent dimension of $B$ follows because a per-<hertz> density includes an extra unit of time. If instead $B$ multiplies the <mean intensity> $J_\nu$, use $B^{(J)}=(4\pi/c)B$ and $A/B^{(J)}=2h\nu_0^3/c^2$. These distinct normalizations must not be mixed in the <radiative transfer equation>.

Use the energy-density convention of part (a), a normalized common <spectral line profile> $\int\phi(\nu)\,d\nu=1$, and isotropic <spontaneous emission>. For a narrow <spectral line> centered at $\nu_0$,
$$
j_\nu=\frac{h\nu_0}{4\pi}n_2A_{21}\phi(\nu),\qquad
\kappa_\nu=\frac{h\nu_0}{c}(n_1B_{12}-n_2B_{21})\phi(\nu).
$$
The factor $1/c$ follows from $u_\nu=4\pi J_\nu/c$: angle-integrated absorption removes $c\kappa_\nu u_\nu$ of <energy> per volume and time. The <emission coefficient> is per <solid angle>, whereas the <absorption coefficient> has units of inverse length.

The <Boltzmann distribution> in <thermal equilibrium> gives $n_2/n_1=(g_2/g_1)e^{-h\nu_0/(k_BT)}$. Dividing the two expressions and using the relations between <Einstein coefficients>,
$$
\begin{aligned}
S_\nu&=\frac{c}{4\pi}\frac{n_2A_{21}}{n_1B_{12}-n_2B_{21}}\\
&=\frac{2h\nu_0^3/c^2}{e^{h\nu_0/(k_BT)}-1}
=B_{\nu_0}(T).
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the <source function> equals the <Planck function> at the transition <frequency>, and in the usual narrow-line approximation
$$
\boxed{S_\nu=B_\nu(T).}
$$
With an exactly common finite-width $\phi$ and constant line-center <Einstein coefficients>, their ratio is constant across the profile; exact equality to a varying $B_\nu$ at every frequency is only a narrow-line approximation. Exact thermal <radiative transfer> requires frequency-resolved <detailed balance>, which supplies the corresponding small profile corrections. This distinction does not alter the intended equilibrium result.

For constant nonzero <absorption coefficient>, multiply the <radiative transfer equation> by the <integrating factor> $e^{\kappa_\nu z}$:
$$
\frac{d}{dz}(I_\nu e^{\kappa_\nu z})=j_\nu e^{\kappa_\nu z}.
$$
Integrating from the entrance to $z$ gives
$$
\boxed{I_\nu(z)=I_\nu(0)e^{-\kappa_\nu z}+S_\nu(1-e^{-\kappa_\nu z}),\qquad S_\nu=\frac{j_\nu}{\kappa_\nu}.}
$$
The boundary condition is immediate. Differentiation gives $I_\nu'=-\kappa_\nu[I_\nu(0)-S_\nu]e^{-\kappa_\nu z}=-\kappa_\nu I_\nu+j_\nu$, checking the equation. For positive <optical depth> $\kappa_\nu z\gg1$, the incident <specific intensity> is forgotten and $I_\nu\to S_\nu$. Negative <absorption coefficient> instead describes gain and does not have this relaxation limit. At $\kappa_\nu=0$ use the nonsingular form directly: $I_\nu(z)=I_\nu(0)+j_\nu z$.

In <local thermodynamic equilibrium>, write the one-ray <radiative transfer equation> as
$$
I_\nu+\kappa_\nu^{-1}\frac{dI_\nu}{dz}=B_\nu[T(z)].
$$
Large total <optical depth> removes the boundary contribution, but an additional local condition is essential: the <temperature> and <opacity> vary little over an absorption length $\kappa_\nu^{-1}$. Iterating once in that small gradient gives
$$
I_\nu=B_\nu-\frac1{\kappa_\nu}\frac{\partial B_\nu}{\partial T}\frac{dT}{dz}+\text{higher gradients}.
$$
The integrated <Planck function> and its derivative are
$$
\int_0^\infty B_\nu\,d\nu=\frac{\sigma T^4}{\pi},\qquad
\int_0^\infty\frac{\partial B_\nu}{\partial T}\,d\nu=\frac{4\sigma T^3}{\pi}.
$$
Using the specified <Rosseland mean opacity>, with $\kappa_R$ measured here in inverse length, the source's definition $F_{\rm ray}=\int I_\nu d\nu$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{F_{\rm ray}(z)=\frac{\sigma T^4}{\pi}-\frac{4\sigma T^3}{\pi\kappa_R}\frac{dT}{dz}+\text{higher gradients}.}
$$
The printed leading term $\sigma T^4$ lacks a factor $1/\pi$ under that definition. An isothermal optically thick medium is a concrete counterexample: $I_\nu=B_\nu$ gives $F_{\rm ray}=\sigma T^4/\pi$ exactly, while the printed formula gives $\sigma T^4$.

A physical <radiative flux> requires an angular integral. For direction cosine $\mu$, the same gradient expansion gives $I_\nu(\mu)=B_\nu-\mu\kappa_\nu^{-1}\partial_zB_\nu$. Integration over all directions gives the net <radiative-diffusion> flux
$$
\boxed{F_{\rm net}=2\pi\int_{-1}^1\mu\,d\mu\int_0^\infty I_\nu(\mu)\,d\nu
=-\frac{16\sigma T^3}{3\kappa_R}\frac{dT}{dz}.}
$$
The isotropic equilibrium contribution cancels between opposite directions. For comparison the outward-hemisphere flux alone is $\sigma T^4-8\sigma T^3(dT/dz)/(3\kappa_R)$ at this order. None of these consistent definitions yields the printed mixture of coefficients.

To derive <fermionic Einstein-coefficient relations>, first consider an idealized emitter that can exchange one ultrarelativistic <fermion> with the bath, with zero <chemical potential>. Let $\mathcal D_\nu=d\,4\pi\nu^2/c^3$ be its <density of states> per volume and ordinary <frequency>, with $d$ populated helicity states; for one <neutrino> helicity $d=1$, whereas the corresponding <photon> count is two. The <Fermi-Dirac distribution> gives
$$
f_\nu=\frac1{e^{h\nu/(k_BT)}+1},\qquad u_\nu=h\nu\mathcal D_\nu f_\nu.
$$
<Pauli blocking> changes the final-state factor in downward emission from the bosonic $1+f$ to $1-f$. Define positive coefficients $B_{12},C_{21}$ and $A_{21}=C_{21}h\nu\mathcal D_\nu$. The upward rate per lower emitter is $B_{12}u_\nu$, and the downward rate per upper emitter is
$$
A_{21}(1-f_\nu)=A_{21}-C_{21}u_\nu.
$$
Thermal <detailed balance>, $n_1B_{12}u_\nu=n_2(A_{21}-C_{21}u_\nu)$ with $n_2/n_1=(g_2/g_1)e^{-h\nu/(k_BT)}$, yields
$$
\boxed{g_1B_{12}=g_2C_{21},\qquad
\frac{A_{21}}{C_{21}}=d\,\frac{4\pi h\nu^3}{c^3}.}
$$
For example, substituting $e^{h\nu/(k_BT)}=x$ reduces the balance equation to $B_{12}=(g_2/g_1)C_{21}$ because $x f_\nu=1-f_\nu$.

If one insists on the original additive notation $A_{21}+B_{21}u_\nu$, its formal coefficient is negative:
$$
\boxed{B_{21}=-C_{21},\qquad g_1B_{12}=-g_2B_{21},\qquad
A_{21}/B_{21}=-d\,4\pi h\nu^3/c^3.}
$$
A negative correction means inhibited emission into occupied modes, not a negative total decay rate; $0\leq f_\nu\leq1$ ensures positivity. The dimensions are those of part (a), and the factor of two relative to the <photon> density is a helicity count, independent of the statistical sign.

There is a physical qualification: ordinary electronic <energy levels> of the same <chemical atom> have the same <fermion parity>, and cannot interconvert by emission or absorption of a single <neutrino>. A realizable single-<fermion> emitter must change the relevant particle-number and parity sector, with matching <conservation laws>. For ordinary atomic levels, neutrino-pair processes would require a different calculation. A bath with nonzero <chemical potential> would also require the corresponding chemical-equilibrium population ratio. The calculation above is the intended single-mode statistical analogy under explicit assumptions.

In the idealized isolated-<carbon> <chemical-atom> interpretation, both beams carry approximately $2.68\,\mathrm{eV}$ per incident particle, close to the ${}^1S_0$ threshold. However **equal incident <energies> do not imply equal excitation channels**.

A single optical <photon> cannot strongly excite any of the listed even-<parity> $2p^2$ <energy levels> from the even-<parity> ${}^3P_0$ ground state through an <electric dipole transition>. In particular ${}^3P_0\to{}^1S_0$ also violates $\Delta S=0$ and the $J=0\leftrightarrow0$ restriction. The lowest strong opposite-<parity> excitations lie well above $2.68\,\mathrm{eV}$. Thus one expects no strong resonant <absorption> or corresponding strong atomic fluorescence from these ground-state <chemical atoms> at this optical <wavelength>; weak higher-order processes and scattering are separate effects.

Incident <electrons> can undergo <inelastic scattering> and <collisional excitation>. Their <orbital angular momentum> and exchange with bound <electrons> allow transfers excluded for a single <electric-dipole> <photon>. The ${}^1D_2$ <energy level> at $1.264\,\mathrm{eV}$ and the low <fine-structure> excitations are energetically accessible. One therefore expects scattered <electrons> with discrete <energy> losses, possible weak forbidden <spectral lines>, and collisional or surface relaxation. For example ${}^1D_2\to{}^3P_2$ has <wavelength> approximately $10^7/(10192-43.4)=985\,\mathrm{nm}$; spin mixing supplies the weak spin-forbidden radiative channel.

There is a numerical threshold caveat. Literally,
$$
E({}^1S_0)=21648hc\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}\simeq2.68401\,\mathrm{eV},\quad
E_\gamma(462.6\,\mathrm{nm})\simeq2.68016\,\mathrm{eV},\quad E_e=2.68000\,\mathrm{eV}.
$$
Neither quoted beam reaches ${}^1S_0$ from the exact ground state if these are taken as exact monoenergetic values. The <electron> value rounded to two decimal places could represent an actual <energy> above threshold; if so, ${}^1S_0$ is collisionally accessible and its decay to ${}^1D_2$ produces the weak <electric quadrupole transition> at $10^7/11456\simeq873\,\mathrm{nm}$, followed by lower-level relaxation. These emissions require populations and sufficiently low <collisional quenching of a metastable line>.

An actual solid <carbon> foil has bands, collective excitations and surface losses, so it need not reproduce the isolated-<chemical atom> spectrum or selection rules. Its optical and electron-induced emission cannot be predicted quantitatively from the five atomic <energy levels> alone. This is the limitation of the foil wording in the source.

Take the initial ground-state <chemical atom> at rest in the laboratory. The incident <photon> has <energy> $h\nu$ and <momentum> $h\nu/c$. The excited <chemical atom> has <rest mass> $M+\Delta M$ and must carry that <momentum>. <Conservation of energy> and <conservation of momentum> give
$$
Mc^2+h\nu=\sqrt{(M+\Delta M)^2c^4+(h\nu)^2}.
$$
Squaring cancels the photon-energy square:
$$
M^2c^4+2Mc^2h\nu=(M+\Delta M)^2c^4.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\nu=\frac{\Delta Mc^2}{h}\left(1+\frac{\Delta M}{2M}\right)
=\nu_{\rm int}+\frac{h\nu_{\rm int}^2}{2Mc^2},\qquad
h\nu_{\rm int}=\Delta Mc^2.}
$$
The extra positive term is the <photon absorption recoil correction> and supplies atomic <recoil energy>. The formula is exact for the initially stationary isolated <chemical atom>; it does not assume $\Delta M/M\ll1$. Initial and final <chemical atoms> cannot both remain at rest during single-<photon> absorption without another body taking <momentum>.

Put $k_Z=Ze^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$. <Gauss's law> for a uniform spherical <nuclear charge distribution>, with the <electrostatic potential> matched at $R$, gives the electronic <potential energy>
$$
V_{\rm sph}(r)=\begin{cases}
-\dfrac{k_Z}{2R}(3-r^2/R^2),&r<R,\\
-k_Z/r,&r\geq R.
\end{cases}
$$
Subtracting the point-nucleus <Coulomb potential> leaves the nonnegative perturbation
$$
\delta V(r)=k_Z\left(\frac1r-\frac3{2R}+\frac{r^2}{2R^3}\right)\quad(r<R),\qquad
\delta V=0\quad(r\geq R).
$$
The normalized nonrelativistic <hydrogen-like-ion> $1s$ <wavefunction> has $|\psi(r)|^2=Z^3e^{-2Zr/a_0}/(\pi a_0^3)$. Since $ZR/a_0\ll1$, approximate it inside the <atomic nucleus> by $|\psi(0)|^2$. First-order <time-independent perturbation theory> then gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta E_{1s}&\simeq4\pi|\psi(0)|^2 k_Z\int_0^R\left(r-\frac{3r^2}{2R}+\frac{r^4}{2R^3}\right)dr\\
&=4\pi|\psi(0)|^2 k_Z\frac{R^2}{10}
=\frac25\frac{Z^4e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0a_0}\left(\frac R{a_0}\right)^2.
\end{aligned}
$$
Define $E_H=-Z^2\mathrm{Ry}$, as the paper specifies for the <hydrogen-like ion>, rather than the neutral-<hydrogen> value. Using $\mathrm{Ry}=e^2/(8\pi\epsilon_0a_0)$ gives
$$
\boxed{\Delta E_{1s}=\frac45Z^2|E_H|\left(\frac R{a_0}\right)^2
=\frac45 Z^4\mathrm{Ry}\left(\frac R{a_0}\right)^2}
$$
to leading order in $ZR/a_0$. For $A=208$, $R\simeq7.11\,\mathrm{fm}$, $ZR/a_0\simeq0.0110$, and
$$
\boxed{\Delta E_{1s}\simeq+8.88\,\mathrm{eV},\qquad
\Delta E_{1s}/h\simeq+2.15\times10^{15}\,\mathrm{Hz}.}
$$
The finite <nuclear charge radius> makes the <energy level> less bound. The quoted <frequency> is a level shift divided by $h$: a line frequency shifts by $(\Delta E_u-\Delta E_l)/h$, so a decay into this shifted $1s$ level alone has the opposite sign.

The formula is a nonrelativistic, small-<nuclear-charge-radius> estimate. Keeping the exponential in the first-order integral gives $\Delta E_{1s}=\Delta E_{\rm leading}[1-5ZR/(6a_0)+O((ZR/a_0)^2)]$, about a one-percent correction here. More seriously, $Z\alpha_{\rm fs}\simeq0.598$ for <lead>, so relativistic contraction is substantial. A precise physical prediction needs a finite-nucleus <Dirac equation> calculation; the boxed estimate is the model requested, not precision heavy-ion spectroscopy.

A nonrelativistic $2p$ <wavefunction> vanishes linearly at the origin. Its probability density is therefore proportional to $r^2$, so its first-order <finite nuclear size correction> shift scales as $R^4$, rather than the $R^2$ result for $1s$. It is positive but much smaller.

More explicitly, the normalized <hydrogen-like-ion> radial function obeys $R_{21}^2(r)\simeq Z^5r^2/(24a_0^5)$ near the origin. Since the <spherical harmonic> has unit angular norm,
$$
\Delta E_{2p}\simeq\frac{k_ZZ^5}{24a_0^5}\int_0^R\left(r^3-\frac{3r^4}{2R}+\frac{r^6}{2R^3}\right)dr
=\frac{k_ZZ^5R^4}{1120a_0^5}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\frac{\Delta E_{2p}}{\Delta E_{1s}}\simeq\frac1{448}\left(\frac{ZR}{a_0}\right)^2\simeq2.71\times10^{-7}.}
$$
This is about $2.4\,\mu\mathrm{eV}$ in the nonrelativistic model. At large $Z$, the relativistic $2p_{1/2}$ <Dirac spinor> has an $s$-type small component and a much stronger nuclear sensitivity than this nonrelativistic argument predicts; $2p_{3/2}$ remains much more suppressed. Neglecting <spin-orbit-interaction> corrections would miss that distinction.

The <finite nuclear size correction> perturbation is unchanged for the same <lead> <atomic nucleus>. Its leading effect on one occupied $1s$ <orbital> is still proportional to that orbital's contact density,
$$
\boxed{\Delta E_{1s}\simeq\frac{2\pi}{5}k_ZR^2|\psi_{1s}(0)|^2.}
$$
Other <electrons> screen the nuclear attraction and alter the self-consistent $1s$ <wavefunction>, usually reducing its contact density and hence its positive shift relative to the bare <hydrogen-like ion>. The reduction is modest for a deep $1s$ <orbital>: it penetrates well inside most other shells and still samples nearly the full $Z=82$ attraction. Replacing $Z$ by the neutral atom's asymptotic charge one would be inappropriate for this core <orbital>.

A neutral <lead> atom has two $1s$ <electrons>. Their contributions add in a frozen-orbital estimate of total atomic <energy>, roughly twice the per-<electron> shift, with relaxation and correlation corrections. An experimentally observed transition <frequency> again depends on the difference between the finite-size shifts of both participating many-<electron> <quantum states>. A quantitative neutral-<lead> result requires a screened relativistic many-<electron> calculation, not the one-<electron> formula with a single universally valid effective charge.
