= Cell membrane
{wiki}

= Plasma membrane
{synonym}

The <cell membrane>, also called the <plasma membrane>, is the selective boundary separating a <biological cell>'s <cytoplasm> from its surroundings. Both <prokaryotes> and <eukaryotes> have a <cell membrane>. Where a <cell wall> is present, it is a distinct structure outside the <cell membrane>; the two names are not synonyms. This separation lets a <biological cell> maintain internal conditions while exchanging material through <membrane transport>. See https://www.genome.gov/genetics-glossary/Cell-Membrane-Plasma-Membrane[NHGRI: cell membrane].

The familiar architecture is a <lipid bilayer> containing <membrane proteins>. In a <phospholipid>-based <lipid bilayer>, two <membrane leaflets> face opposite ways: <hydrophilic> heads face the <water> on each side, while <hydrophobic> tails face one another in the interior. The heads are therefore exposed both outside and inside the <biological cell>. This arrangement combines a water-facing surface with a barrier to many water-soluble <solutes>; the schematic represents organization rather than an atomistic structure. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26871/[Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: the lipid bilayer].

\Image[cell-membrane-selective-barrier.png]
{title=Two <membrane leaflets> and selective routes through a <cell membrane>}
{description=Original <cell membrane> schematic showing <phospholipids>, <hydrophilic> heads, <hydrophobic> tails, an <ion channel>, and a <carrier protein>. Arrows illustrate possible passage, not measured rates. Shapes, sizes, spacing and numbers of <molecules> are illustrative.}

<Selective permeability> means that different substances cross at different rates under comparable conditions. Small <nonpolar> <molecules> can pass through a <lipid bilayer> by <diffusion>, while <ions> encounter a strong barrier without a suitable route. An <ion channel> supplies a selective water-filled pathway when open; a <carrier protein> binds a <solute> and changes shape to expose it to the other side. These <membrane proteins> therefore add specific routes to the barrier. An open <ion channel> permits <passive transport>, whereas a <carrier protein> can mediate either <passive transport> or <active transport>. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26815/[Alberts et al.: principles of membrane transport].

The useful combination is retention plus controlled exchange: a completely impermeable boundary would prevent uptake, whereas unrestricted mixing would erase differences in <concentration> and <membrane potential>. The <fluid mosaic model> connects these functions to an organized but mobile <lipid bilayer> containing <proteins>.

= Lipid divide
{parent=Cell membrane}

The <lipid divide> is the contrast between characteristic archaeal membrane lipids and characteristic bacterial/eukaryotic membrane lipids, including glycerol stereochemistry and ether versus ester linkages. It constrains hypotheses for eukaryotic origins without uniquely determining the sequence of ancestral membrane changes.

= Membrane fission
{parent=Cell membrane}

<Membrane fission> separates a connected membrane into distinct components, for example by severing a vesicle neck. Bending a membrane into a bud without severing that neck is not yet fission.

= Membrane fusion
{parent=Cell membrane}
{wiki}

<Membrane fusion> joins initially separate lipid bilayers, changing connections between membrane compartments. It requires controlled rearrangement of both leaflets while avoiding uncontrolled leakage.

= Membrane curvature
{parent=Cell membrane}

Membrane shape affects the energetic cost and geometry of a bud, neck or protrusion. Proteins, lipid composition and mechanical forces can change curvature; curvature alone does not perform the topological reconnection required for <membrane fusion> or <membrane fission>.

= Homeoviscous adaptation
{parent=Cell membrane}

<homeoviscous adaptation> changes membrane composition in response to conditions such as growth <temperature>, helping preserve useful membrane dynamics. The original <Escherichia coli> study measured temperature-dependent fatty-acid changes and membrane-lipid viscosity by spin labeling.<Homeoviscous adaptation—a homeostatic process that regulates the viscosity of membrane lipids in Escherichia coli by Michael Sinensky>[pp. 522–525, temperature-dependent composition and viscosity measurements]{ref}

= Membrane nanodomain
{parent=Cell membrane}

A <membrane nanodomain> is a small region with locally differing composition or molecular organization. Its size, lifetime and physical origin require measurement; detergent resistance alone does not demonstrate a pre-existing domain in a living membrane.

= Selective permeability
{parent=Cell membrane}

<Selective permeability> means that a <cell membrane> permits different substances to pass at different rates under comparable conditions. Both the <lipid bilayer> and the available <membrane proteins> contribute to this selection.

Artificial boundaries can also have <selective permeability>. For <osmosis>, specify which <solutes> cross and over what observation interval: a boundary that retains one <solute> need not retain another. A <semipermeable membrane> is the ideal solvent-only boundary used in the <osmotic pressure> calculation.

= Membrane permeability
{parent=Selective permeability}

<membrane permeability> describes passage of a specified substance through a membrane under stated conditions. It is distinct from lateral <diffusion> within the membrane and from the mechanical force needed to rupture the membrane.

= Semipermeable membrane
{parent=Selective permeability}
{wiki}

A <semipermeable membrane> ideally passes the <solvent> while excluding the specified <solutes>. In the aqueous <osmosis> model below, <water> can cross but the counted dissolved particles cannot. Real <selective permeability> is often a matter of different rates, so this idealization needs checking over the measurement interval. Neither an impermeable sheet nor a freely mixing opening realizes this model.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology, fifth edition (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry/osmotic pressure>[Gold Book, osmotic pressure: definition of the solvent-only membrane]{ref}

= Membrane protein
{parent=Cell membrane}
{wiki}

A <membrane protein> is a <protein> associated with a biological membrane. Its association, orientation and mobility are separate properties: being embedded does not imply spanning, and spanning does not imply moving freely. <Membrane proteins> supply routes for <membrane transport> and recognition for <signal transduction>.<The Cell: A Molecular Approach, 2nd edition by Geoffrey M. Cooper/Cell Membranes>[Cooper, The Cell: A Molecular Approach, 2nd edition, Cell Membranes, opening paragraph and Membrane Proteins]{ref}

An <ion channel> supplies a pathway for <ions>; a <carrier protein> changes which side can reach a bound <solute>; a <cell-surface receptor> connects an external <ligand> to intracellular <signal transduction>. These examples distinguish passage from communication: a signal can cross the boundary as a change of <protein> activity without the <ligand> itself crossing.<Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others/Membrane Proteins>[Alberts and others, Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition, Membrane Proteins Can Be Associated with the Lipid Bilayer in Various Ways: association reflects function]{ref}

= Hydrophobic mismatch
{parent=Membrane protein}

<hydrophobic mismatch> is an energetic incompatibility between a membrane protein's hydrophobic span and the hydrophobic thickness of its surrounding <lipid bilayer>. Local deformation, altered conformation or changed association can partly accommodate it.

{parent=Membrane protein}

= Membrane topology
{parent=Membrane protein}
{wiki}

<Membrane topology> specifies the sidedness of a membrane-associated <polypeptide chain>: where its <N-terminus>, <C-terminus> and intervening <protein domains> lie relative to the <lipid bilayer>. Side-specific placement is essential to interpreting a <cell-surface receptor>.<Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others/Membrane Proteins>[Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others; Membrane Proteins Can Be Associated with the Lipid Bilayer in Various Ways: aqueous domains on either side and receptor orientation]{ref}

For an original worked example, stipulate a continuous <polypeptide chain> with its <N-terminus> outside a <biological cell>, exactly three complete crossings of the <cell membrane>, and no additional excursions into the <lipid bilayer>. Its segments lie, in order, outside, inside, outside, inside. A <protein domain> between the first and second crossings faces the <cytosol>; one between the second and third faces the exterior; the <C-terminus> faces the <cytosol>. Counting crossings determines these placements without specifying an atomic <protein structure>.

The proof is a side-switching argument. Assign exterior the sign $+1$ and interior $-1$. Each complete crossing multiplies the sign by $-1$, so after $m$ crossings,
$$
s_m=(-1)^m s_0.
$$
An odd number puts the termini on opposite sides, an even number on the same side. This assumes complete crossings of one boundary; a chain that enters and returns to the same side without crossing must not be counted as a complete crossing. Nor does the rule determine which side the <N-terminus> starts on.

As a constructed accessibility test, put an <epitope> on the outside segment between crossings two and three, and another on the inside segment between crossings one and two. Stipulate an intact <cell membrane> that excludes the test <antibody> and accessible <epitopes> that bind it. Applying the <antibody> outside can label only the first <epitope>. If both become accessible after opening the boundary, the difference tests sidedness under those assumptions. Failure to label an <epitope> alone is insufficient: it could also be inaccessible because of <protein folding> or poor <antibody affinity>. This is a proposed reasoning exercise, separate from the historical mixing experiment below.

= Lateral mobility of membrane proteins
{parent=Membrane protein}
{title2=$\langle r^2\rangle=4Dt$}

<Lateral mobility of membrane proteins> is movement along a membrane surface. It differs from <membrane transport> through the boundary and from reversing <membrane topology>. Attachment, crowding and barriers can restrict <diffusion>; the <fluid mosaic model> does not require every <membrane protein> to wander freely.<The Cell: A Molecular Approach, 2nd edition by Geoffrey M. Cooper/Structure of the Plasma Membrane>[Cooper, The Cell: A Molecular Approach, 2nd edition, Structure of the Plasma Membrane, Mobility of Membrane Proteins: lateral movement and restrictions]{ref}

An original local model treats a small, approximately flat patch as an unbounded plane with uniform <diffusion coefficient> $D$, no drift and no trapping. Represent the two displacement coordinates by independent <Brownian motions>, each with zero <expected value> and <variance> $2Dt$. Since $r^2=x^2+y^2$, addition of the two coordinate expectations gives
$$
\langle r^2\rangle=4Dt,\qquad r_{\mathrm{rms}}=\sqrt{4Dt}.
$$
This is a root-mean-square displacement, not distance traveled along a jagged trajectory or the displacement of every <protein>. For a stipulated $D=0.08\,\mu\mathrm{m}^2\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ and $t=5\,\mathrm{s}$, $\langle r^2\rangle=1.6\,\mu\mathrm{m}^2$ and $r_{\mathrm{rms}}\approx1.265\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. Reaching a stipulated root-mean-square scale $L=2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ gives $t=L^2/(4D)=12.5\,\mathrm{s}$. These model values are not measured <diffusion coefficients> or mixing times from the historical experiment.

Multiplying the target displacement scale by two multiplies the model time by four. This quadratic scaling is a useful way to see why local encounters and redistribution over an entire <biological cell> need not happen on the same timescale. A curved, bounded <cell membrane>, restricted paths and different mobile populations require more than this flat-patch model; disappearance of an initial pattern alone does not determine $D$.

= Mouse-human heterokaryon membrane-mixing experiment
{parent=Lateral mobility of membrane proteins}

In 1970 <Larry Frye> and <Michael Edidin> fused mouse C11D and human VA-2 <cell lines> with <Sendai virus>, forming <heterokaryons>. Suspensions were maintained in a shaking water bath at $37\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Removed aliquots were stained by indirect <immunofluorescence>: <antibodies> against parental surface <antigens>, then differently fluorescent secondary <antibodies>. A Leitz Ortholux <fluorescence microscope> used a mercury lamp and color-selecting filters. Green mouse and red human surface patterns became intermixed.<The rapid intermixing of cell surface antigens after formation of mouse-human heterokaryons by Larry Frye and Michael Edidin>[Frye and Edidin, The rapid intermixing of cell surface antigens after formation of mouse-human heterokaryons, pp. 319–325: Summary, Cell fusion, Fluorescent staining, Fluorescence microscopy, Table 1, time-course results]{ref}

Same-species fusions tested <antibody specificity>; stable hybrid lines supplied a two-color control. Inhibitors of <biological translation> did not prevent mixing; lowering <temperature> inhibited it. These observations supported lateral redistribution over rapid synthesis. The authors could not conclusively exclude passage through <cytoplasm> and return. The readout compared populations, not trajectories of a continuously observed individual <biological cell>, and did not establish unrestricted motion of every <membrane protein>.<The rapid intermixing of cell surface antigens after formation of mouse-human heterokaryons by Larry Frye and Michael Edidin>[pp. 324–331: specificity controls, inhibitor studies, temperature dependence and Discussion of mechanisms iii and iv]{ref}

The decisive design idea is to begin with spatially distinguishable material. A uniformly labeled surface can look unchanged while its components move. Bringing together differently identifiable parental surfaces creates an initial pattern whose loss reveals redistribution; <antibody specificity> makes that loss interpretable. <Membrane topology> asks which side a part faces, whereas this experiment asks where it can move along that side.

= Ion channel
{parent=Cell membrane}
{wiki}

An <ion channel> is a <membrane protein> that provides a selective pathway for <ions> across a <cell membrane>. An open pore permits <passive transport> down the relevant <electrochemical gradient>; it does not supply the <free energy> for uphill transport as a pump involved in <active transport> does. Two things are needed for a net <electric current>: an available pathway and an <electrochemical potential> difference. Opening a pore alone need not produce a net <electric current>. See <Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others/Ion Channels and the Electrical Properties of Membranes>.

The <selective permeability> of an <ion channel> depends on interactions between its pore and the passing <ions>, not simply on a hole's diameter. Some <ion channels> strongly favor <potassium> over <sodium>; others admit several kinds of positively charged <ions>. Thus selective does not necessarily mean exclusive to one <ion> species. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK11123/[Purves et al., Neuroscience, 2nd edition (2001): molecular structure of ion channels] and the mixed-channel example in https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK11013/[Purves et al.: principles derived from studies of the neuromuscular junction].

\Image[ion-channel-selective-passage.png]
{title=An open <ion channel> supplies a selective passive pathway}
{description=Original model schematic: selected positive <ions> can traverse a pore in a <membrane protein>, while the differently shaped particles are excluded from this modeled pore. The arrow shows one possible net direction when the corresponding <electrochemical gradient> favors entry; the pore does not enforce that direction. Shapes, particle counts and dimensions are illustrative, not physical microscopic data or a universal selectivity rule.}

Gating is the opening and closing of an <ion channel>'s pathway. A <voltage-gated ion channel> responds to <membrane potential>; an <ionotropic receptor> can respond to a bound <ligand>. Gating changes the available <electrical conductance>, while the <electrochemical gradient> supplies the drive for <passive transport>. For a fixed <reversal potential> in the simple model below, opening more identical <ion channels> increases the magnitude of the <electric current> at a fixed nonzero drive, without choosing its sign independently of <membrane potential>. The <two-state ion-channel gating> model describes how the open fraction can change. See the Alberts reference above.

For a worked approximation, hold an open population's <electrical conductance> at $g=10\,\mathrm{nS}$ and its <reversal potential> at $E_{\mathrm{rev}}=-80\,\mathrm{mV}$. Use $V=V_{\mathrm{inside}}-V_{\mathrm{outside}}$ for <membrane potential> and **inward-positive <conventional current>**:
$$
I_{\mathrm{in}}=g(E_{\mathrm{rev}}-V),\qquad g\geq0.
$$
At $V=-100,-80,-60\,\mathrm{mV}$, respectively, the <electric currents> are $+200,0,-200\,\mathrm{pA}$, since $1\,\mathrm{nS}\times1\,\mathrm{mV}=1\,\mathrm{pA}$. Positive <electric current> means net positive <electric charge> entering, or net negative <electric charge> leaving; it does not mean that every <ion> moves inward. For a pore passing only positive <ions>, the three results mean net inward passage, no net passage, and net outward passage. These stipulated values describe an illustrative Ohmic approximation, not measured properties of a particular <ion channel>; see <reversal potential> for the current plot and limitations.

The useful connection is between a molecular gate and a controllable <electric current>. A <ligand> can change <electrical conductance> through an <ionotropic receptor>, or <membrane potential> can change it through a <voltage-gated ion channel>, connecting recognition and electrical signaling. In a historical landmark, Erwin Neher and Bert Sakmann reported currents from individual <ion channels> in frog muscle membranes in 1976. See https://doi.org/10.1038/260799a0[Neher and Sakmann, Single-channel currents recorded from membrane of denervated frog muscle fibres, Nature 260, 799–802 (1976)].

= Membrane transport
{parent=Cell membrane}
{wiki}

<Membrane transport> is the passage of substances across a <cell membrane>. For the small <solutes> considered here, distinguish the available route from its driving force: a <lipid bilayer>, <ion channel>, or <carrier protein> determines how passage can occur, while differences in <chemical potential> and <membrane potential> determine which direction is energetically downhill. A large driving force cannot make an unavailable route conduct. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26815/[Alberts et al.: principles of membrane transport].

For an ideal dilute <chemical solution> at uniform <temperature> and <pressure>, with the same reference molar <chemical potential> on both sides, the molar <electrochemical potential> change for transferring a <solute> from compartment A to B is
$$
\Delta\widetilde\mu_{\mathrm{A}\to\mathrm{B}}=RT\ln\!\left(\frac{c_{\mathrm B}}{c_{\mathrm A}}\right)+zF(V_{\mathrm B}-V_{\mathrm A}).
$$
Here $c_{\mathrm A},c_{\mathrm B}$ are <concentrations>, $T$ is absolute <temperature>, $R$ is the <molar gas constant>, $z$ is signed <ion> charge number, $F$ is the <Faraday constant>, and $V_{\mathrm B}-V_{\mathrm A}$ is the <electric potential> difference. The <natural logarithm> contains a dimensionless ratio. The electrical term vanishes for an uncharged <solute>. More generally, use <thermodynamic activities> in place of the ideal <concentration> ratio. This combines the ideal-solution <chemical potential> difference with electrical <energy> per mole; see https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/E01945[IUPAC Gold Book: electrochemical potential]. For a <biological cell>, taking B inside and A outside makes the voltage term use the existing <membrane potential> convention.

= Passive transport
{parent=Membrane transport}
{wiki}

<Passive transport> is net movement down the transported substance's <electrochemical gradient> without coupling that movement to another source of <free energy>. <Facilitated diffusion> is <passive transport> through an <ion channel> or a passive <carrier protein>. Providing such a route can speed passage, but does not turn an uphill transfer into a downhill one. For an uncharged <solute> under the ideal conditions above, net <passive transport> runs from higher to lower <concentration>; for an <ion>, the <membrane potential> also matters. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK9847/[Cooper, The Cell: transport of small molecules].

In the figure, stipulate eight neutral <solute> tracers in A and two in B, with equal fixed <volumes>, identical surroundings, and no imposed <electric potential> difference. The <concentration> ratio is $c_{\mathrm A}/c_{\mathrm B}=8/2=4$, so A-to-B transfer has $\Delta\widetilde\mu=-RT\ln4\simeq-1.38629RT$, or $-3.458\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ at the assumed <temperature> $T=300\,\mathrm K$. If this <solute> can cross, <thermodynamic equilibrium> has equal expected <concentrations>: five tracers per side on average. Individual crossings occur in both directions; an observed ten-particle snapshot need not contain exactly five on each side. These stipulated counts illustrate a driving force, not a measured flow rate or a prediction of the time to equilibrate.

For a separate illustrative <ion> calculation, keep $c_{\mathrm A}/c_{\mathrm B}=4$, but assign $z=+1$ and $V_{\mathrm A}-V_{\mathrm B}=-60\,\mathrm{mV}$ at $300\,\mathrm K$. Using $R\simeq8.31446\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ and $F\simeq96485.33\,\mathrm{C\,mol^{-1}}$, B-to-A transfer gives $RT\ln4-0.060F\simeq3.458-5.789=-2.331\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. Thus an appropriate open <ion channel> can permit <passive transport> toward the higher <concentration>: the electrical contribution outweighs the opposing <chemical potential> contribution. This stipulated <membrane potential> is not inferred from the tracer counts.

= Facilitated diffusion
{parent=Passive transport}
{wiki}

<Facilitated diffusion> is <passive transport> through a <membrane transport protein>, such as an <ion channel> or <carrier protein>, down the relevant <electrochemical gradient>. The <protein> supplies a route; it does not by itself provide <free energy> for uphill transfer.

= Osmosis
{parent=Passive transport}
{wiki}

<Osmosis> is net movement of <water> across a boundary whose permeability differs for <water> and relevant <solutes>. The direction depends on the <chemical potential> of <water>, including both dissolved material and <pressure>. A <cell wall> can let <water> reach the <cell membrane>, yet oppose expansion through <turgor pressure>. Zero net movement does not mean individual <water> <molecules> have stopped crossing.

Imagine pure <water> on side A and a <chemical solution> on side B of a <semipermeable membrane>. At equal <temperature> and <pressure>, dissolved particles lower the <chemical potential> of <water> in B, and net <water> transfer initially favors A to B. Retaining the <solutes> lets <water> redistribute without simply mixing everything. Increasing B's <pressure> opposes entry; enough <pressure> gives <thermodynamic equilibrium> with unequal <solute> <concentrations>. This is why “<water> moves toward more <solute>” requires a pressure condition.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others/5.2 Passive Transport>[§5.2, Osmosis and Mechanism: selective passage and pressure opposing entry]{ref}

\Image[osmosis-membrane-pressure-balance.png]
{title=<Selective permeability> and the <pressure> that balances <osmosis>}
{description=Original schematic of a boundary passing <water> and retaining the illustrated <solute>. Left: equal <pressures> allow net transfer toward the <chemical solution>. Right: excess <pressure> on the solution side balances the osmotic drive while <water> exchanges in both directions. Equal opposing arrows denote zero net transfer, not a measured molecular rate. Particle counts and dimensions are illustrative.}

At <thermodynamic equilibrium>, the <chemical potentials> of exchangeable <water> are equal. Individual <water> <molecules> still move and cross in both directions, with equal mean opposing transfer rates and zero net <flux>. A stationary liquid level alone does not prove this: a blocked route can also give no visible transfer. <Selective permeability> provides a route; <water potential> supplies the direction, and neither alone determines the speed. The <cell wall>, <turgor pressure> and <plasmolysis> articles show why a living boundary's mechanical support and its <selective permeability> must be distinguished.

= Water potential
{parent=Osmosis}
{wiki}
{title2=$\Psi$}

<Water potential> expresses the <chemical potential> of <water> relative to a stated pure-<water> reference in units of <pressure>. For a nearly incompressible liquid at fixed <temperature>, use a constant reference <molar volume> $\bar V_w$ and write
$$
\Psi=\frac{\mu_w-\mu_w^{\mathrm{ref}}}{\bar V_w}.
$$
The reference is pure <water> at the same <temperature> and a chosen <pressure> $P_0$. Both sides must use the same reference. Neglecting height differences and interactions with solid surfaces, the approximation is
$$
\mu_w\simeq\mu_w^{\mathrm{ref}}+RT\ln a_w+\bar V_w(P-P_0),\qquad
\Psi\simeq(P-P_0)+\frac{RT}{\bar V_w}\ln a_w=\Psi_p+\Psi_s.
$$
Here $a_w$ is the dimensionless <thermodynamic activity> of <water>, $R$ the <molar gas constant>, and $T$ the absolute <temperature>. The <natural logarithm> is therefore applied to a dimensionless quantity. The <pressure> contribution $\Psi_p=P-P_0$ increases with <pressure>; the <solute potential> $\Psi_s$ is negative when $a_w<1$. Across an available passive water route, net <water> transfer favors higher to lower <water potential>. The familiar <plant> description adds effects of height and interactions with solid surfaces when they matter; they are omitted in the two liquid compartments here.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others/30.5 Transport of Water and Solutes in Plants>[Biology 2e, §30.5, Water Potential, Solute Potential, Pressure Potential and Figure 30.32: component balance and direction of transfer]{ref}

Adding <solute> and raising <pressure> can thus cancel in <water potential> while each contribution remains nonzero. Equality of <water potential> does not imply equality of <pressure> or of every <solute> <concentration>. In a supported <plant> cell, the <cell wall> permits a positive <turgor pressure> to balance a negative <solute potential>; <plasmolysis> illustrates the opposite loss of support after <water> leaves.

= Solute potential
{parent=Water potential}
{title2=$\Psi_s$}

= Osmotic potential
{synonym}

<Solute potential> is the contribution to <water potential> from dissolved material, with pure <water> assigned zero. In the nearly incompressible description, $\Psi_s=(RT/\bar V_w)\ln a_w$. In a dilute ideal <chemical solution>, $\Psi_s\simeq-RTc_{\mathrm{osm}}$, where $c_{\mathrm{osm}}$ is the <osmotic concentration> of dissolved particles. Thus <solute potential> is negative while the corresponding <osmotic pressure> is positive: $\Psi_s\simeq-\Pi$. This sign difference distinguishes a contribution to <water potential> from the compensating mechanical <pressure>.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others/30.5 Transport of Water and Solutes in Plants>[Biology 2e, §30.5, Solute Potential: negative concentration contribution]{ref}

= Osmotic pressure
{parent=Osmosis}
{wiki}
{title2=$\Pi$}

<Osmotic pressure> is the excess <pressure> needed on a <chemical solution> to maintain osmotic <thermodynamic equilibrium> with pure <solvent> across a <semipermeable membrane>. For a nearly incompressible <solvent>, equality of <chemical potential> gives $\Pi\simeq-(RT/\bar V_w)\ln a_w$. It is an equilibrium property; an observed initial swelling rate is a different measurement.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology, fifth edition (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry/osmotic pressure>[Gold Book, osmotic pressure: equilibrium definition, solvent activity formula and dilute ideal limit]{ref}

For a dilute ideal <chemical solution> of retained particles,
$$
\Pi=\frac{nRT}{V}=RTc_{\mathrm{osm}}.
$$
Here $n$ is the <amount of substance> of independently dissolved <solute> particles, measured in moles; $V$ is the final <volume> of <chemical solution>, not the starting <volume> of pure <solvent>. Count the resulting <ions> separately if a <solute> dissociates; do not multiply twice if $n$ already counts them. With $c_{\mathrm{osm}}$ in $\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}}$ and $R$ in $\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$, $RTc_{\mathrm{osm}}$ has units $\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}=\mathrm{Pa}$. This resembles the <ideal gas law>, although the particles here are dissolved in a liquid.

The assumptions are uniform <temperature>, a dilute ideal <chemical solution>, a nearly incompressible <solvent>, and a <semipermeable membrane> that retains the counted <solutes> while passing <solvent>. Association, incomplete dissociation and interactions change the relation between added material and independent particles; nonideal <thermodynamic activities> invalidate simple particle counting. The formula does not describe a concentrated brine merely because the apparatus contains a membrane.

A short derivation explains why particles are counted. In an ideal mixture, $a_w=x_w=n_w/(n_w+n)$, with $n_w$ the <amount of substance> of <water>. If $n\ll n_w$, then $-\ln x_w=\ln(1+n/n_w)\simeq n/n_w$ and $V\simeq n_w\bar V_w$. Substitution gives $\Pi\simeq RTn/V$. This is a dilute-limit approximation, not a claim that <water> <molecules> stop moving or that dissolved particles are a gas.

= Osmometry
{parent=Osmotic pressure}

Measure the <pressure> difference across a solvent-permeable barrier that retains the solute, at known <temperature>. For dilute <polymers>, the zero-concentration intercept of reduced <pressure> determines the <number-average molar mass>. Leakage, aggregation and ionic contributions can change the inferred molecular population.

= Reduced osmotic-pressure intercept
{parent=Osmometry}
{title2=$\lim_{c\to0}\Pi/c=RT/M_n$}

For <polymer> <mass> <concentration> $c$, the leading ideal chain <pressure> is $RTc/M_n$. Plotting $\Pi/c$ against $c$ and extrapolating removes the leading virial correction. The intercept has units of energy per <mass>; without the measurement <temperature> it determines $M_n/T$, not an absolute molar <mass>.

= Osmosis concentration-pressure example
{parent=Osmotic pressure}

Take two reservoirs separated by a <semipermeable membrane> that passes <water> and retains one neutral <solute>. Stipulate $T=300\,\mathrm K$, $c_A=10\,\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}}$ and $c_B=30\,\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}}$, equivalent to $10$ and $30\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$. Treat both <chemical solutions> as dilute and ideal. These are invented model conditions, not measurements or recommended biological conditions.

For <pressures> measured at the same height beside the membrane, define $\Delta P=P_B-P_A$. The model predicts
$$
\Psi_A-\Psi_B=RT(c_B-c_A)-\Delta P,\qquad
RT(c_B-c_A)\simeq49.89\,\mathrm{kPa}.
$$
At $\Delta P=0$, net <water> transfer favors A to B. At $\Delta P\simeq49.89\,\mathrm{kPa}$, the opposing <pressure> balances the <osmotic pressure> difference, giving zero net transfer with unequal <concentrations>. At $\Delta P=75\,\mathrm{kPa}$, the sign reverses and net <water> transfer favors B to A, toward the more dilute reservoir. Raising <pressure> changes the total <water potential>, rather than changing the membrane into a one-way gate.

{title=Stipulated <concentration> and <pressure> conditions determine net <water> transfer}
{description=Original computed comparison for ideal retained <solute> at $300\,\mathrm K$, with A at $10\,\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}}$ and B at $30\,\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}}$. The horizontal axis is $P_B-P_A$; the vertical axis is $\Psi_A-\Psi_B$. Three marked cases show forward transfer, balance and reversed transfer. These are instantaneous reservoir conditions, not a time course or measured biological data; slope does not specify permeability or speed.}

Fixed <concentrations> require large maintained reservoirs or an instantaneous comparison. In a closed <membrane osmometer>, entering <water> causes <dilution> of the receiving <chemical solution> and changes its <volume>; the final balance must use final <concentrations>. A supported <cell wall> provides another way to develop the opposing <pressure>, as explained under <turgor pressure>.

= Membrane osmometer
{parent=Osmosis}
{wiki}

A <membrane osmometer> connects <osmosis> to a visible liquid level or a measured balancing <pressure>. In a teaching version, a <solute>-containing pouch is joined to a narrow vertical tube and submerged in external <water>. Entry increases the enclosed <volume> and raises the meniscus. Both the column and any stretched pouch can contribute an opposing <pressure>.

The University of Toronto's <Osmosis (Brine) by University of Toronto Department of Physics> demonstration (activity Me-012b, Description, steps 1–14) specifies soaking a dialysis membrane tube, tying one end closed and fastening the other around a thistle tube's narrow end. A stand holds the tube. Colored brine is cooled to room <temperature> and injected using a syringe to reduce bubbles; the pouch is completely immersed in a $1$ or $2\,\mathrm L$ beaker of distilled <water>. A taped marker records the initial level; subsequent meniscus height is the readout. The protocol proposes another pouch containing distilled <water> as a comparison, and its older instructions explicitly check seals for leaks.<Osmosis (Brine) by University of Toronto Department of Physics>[Osmosis (Brine), Description steps 1–14, water-filled comparison and Old Description A.2: apparatus, readout and leak check]{ref}

A rise is consistent with net <water> entry. To infer <osmotic pressure> quantitatively, use a retained <solute> and establish a stable equilibrium after accounting for <dilution>. If both free surfaces are at the same atmospheric <pressure>, the liquids have approximately equal <mass densities> $\rho$, and the pouch adds negligible mechanical load, the <hydrostatic pressure> difference at membrane height is approximately $\Delta P=\rho gh$, where $h$ is the meniscus height above the external surface and $g$ is <gravitational acceleration>. This is the liquid-column inference, not a pressure calibration supplied by the Toronto demonstration. For unequal <mass densities>, compare the two column pressures separately.

Useful additional controls are matched initial levels and geometry, constant <temperature>, replicate runs, and monitoring external <solute> to test retention. These are proposed checks, not reported Toronto results. Leaks, trapped bubbles, <surface tension> in the narrow tube, <evaporation> and membrane stretching can alter the level. In particular, a dialysis membrane may pass dissolved salt: the brine setup illustrates transient movement but cannot be assumed to retain every dissolved <ion>. Color improves visibility without proving salt retention. A stationary level can reflect weakening <concentration> differences or leakage rather than osmotic <thermodynamic equilibrium>; a rising level alone supplies neither $n$ nor a verified value of $nRT/V$.

= Osmotic concentration
{parent=Osmosis}
{wiki}
{title2=$c_{\mathrm{osm}}$}

= Osmolarity
{synonym}

In the dilute ideal particle-count description, <osmolarity> is the total <amount of substance> of independently dissolved <solute> particles per <volume> of <chemical solution>: $c_{\mathrm{osm}}=\sum_j n_j/V$. It includes both membrane-permeating and retained <solutes>; specifying a particular membrane is unnecessary to count particles. A neutral undissociated <solute> contributes one particle per <molecule>, whereas separated <ions> are counted individually. Common units are $\mathrm{osmol\,L^{-1}}$ or $\mathrm{mOsm\,L^{-1}}$, with one osmole denoting one mole of the counted particles. <Osmolality> uses mass of <solvent> instead of <volume> of <chemical solution>; the two numerical values should not be identified without the appropriate mass-to-volume conversion.<Osmosis, Cell Membranes-Structure and Transport, BYU-Idaho>[Cell Membranes—Structure and Transport, Osmosis: paragraphs beginning Earlier this semester and Osmolarity is a useful term; total particle counting]{ref}

This ideal counting definition states its scope: interactions can require a <thermodynamic activity> description of <osmotic pressure>. <Tonicity> asks a different question, namely how that surrounding <chemical solution> changes a specified <biological cell>'s <volume> through its particular <cell membrane>.

= Tonicity
{parent=Osmosis}
{wiki}

<Tonicity> describes how a surrounding <chemical solution> changes cellular <volume> through <osmosis>. It depends on which <solutes> remain effectively excluded on the timescale of interest, not simply on the amount of dissolved material. The following comparisons take a functional <cell membrane>, an initially unpressurized <biological cell>, and effectively excluded <solutes> as the reference; rising <turgor pressure> can oppose entry in a walled <biological cell>.

Operationally, <hypotonic solutions> cause sustained swelling, <hypertonic solutions> sustained shrinking, and <isotonic solutions> no sustained <volume> change in the specified passive comparison. State the reference <biological cell>, membrane properties and observation interval. A <permeating solute> can transiently change <water potential> while crossing; after its <chemical potential> equilibrates, its initial unequal <concentrations> cannot be treated as a permanently retained osmotic load. Thus equal <osmolarities> need not imply <isotonic solutions>. Conversely, a stationary walled <biological cell> may have unequal <osmolarities> balanced by <turgor pressure>.<Osmosis, Cell Membranes-Structure and Transport, BYU-Idaho>[Cell Membranes—Structure and Transport, Osmosis: paragraphs beginning Here is another way and final Note; permeating solutes and transient effects]{ref}

= Permeating solute
{parent=Tonicity}

A <permeating solute> can cross the specified membrane appreciably during the observation interval. This is a property of a <solute>-membrane-time comparison, not an immutable label attached to a <molecule>. Passage can reduce a <concentration> difference through <passive transport> while <water> is also moving; it does not require <water> to wait.

For an original dilute ideal example, stipulate a flexible model <biological cell> with $20\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ retained particles and an external reservoir with $10\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ retained particles plus $10\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ freely permeating neutral <solute>. Initially the total <osmolarities> are equal. Suppose the permeating <solute> eventually has equal internal and external <concentrations>, there is negligible <pressure> difference, no <active transport>, and the internal retained particle amount is conserved. The permeating contributions cancel from the final osmotic balance; the internal retained <concentration> must fall from $20$ to $10\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$. The required <volume> is twice its initial value. This illustrates an initially equal-<osmolarity> but <hypotonic solution>; it assumes the boundary can survive that expansion and predicts no universal time course.

= Hypotonic solution
{parent=Tonicity}

A <hypotonic solution> favors initial net <water> entry under the reference conditions above. A susceptible <biological cell> may undergo <osmotic lysis>; a sufficiently strong <cell wall> can instead support <turgor pressure>.

= Hypertonic solution
{parent=Tonicity}

A <hypertonic solution> favors initial net <water> loss under the reference conditions above. In a walled <plant> cell, enough loss can produce <plasmolysis>.

= Isotonic solution
{parent=Tonicity}

An <isotonic solution> produces no sustained change in cellular <volume> under the stated comparison conditions. <Water> can still exchange in both directions.

= Osmotic lysis
{synonym}

<Osmotic lysis> is rupture after excessive <water> entry and swelling. It is an outcome for susceptible <biological cells>, not an inevitable response of every <biological cell> to a <hypotonic solution>. <Cell walls> can bear the resulting load, and cellular mechanisms can regulate <water> balance. = Active transport
{parent=Membrane transport}
{wiki}

<Active transport> moves a substance uphill in its <electrochemical gradient> by coupling the transfer to a downhill process. Some <carrier proteins> use <ATP> <hydrolysis>; others couple uphill transfer of one <solute> to downhill transfer of another <solute>, often an <ion>. The latter mechanism uses a stored <electrochemical gradient> and need not consume <ATP> directly at that <carrier protein>. Maintaining the driving <electrochemical gradient> still requires a source of <energy>. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26896/[Alberts et al.: carrier proteins and active membrane transport].

For the neutral 8:2 example, B-to-A transfer costs $RT\ln4\simeq1.38629RT$ per mole. As an explicitly assumed bookkeeping example, suppose a tightly coupled cycle supplies a downhill <free energy> change of $-2RT$ per mole of transferred <solute>. The total is then $\Delta G_{\mathrm{cycle}}=RT\ln4-2RT\simeq-0.61371RT$. This makes forward cycling energetically possible under those assumptions; it specifies neither a molecular mechanism nor a rate. Merely adding a <carrier protein> cannot supply the missing <free energy>. Conversely, as the transported <concentration> ratio rises, the required uphill cost rises, so a fixed driving process has a finite thermodynamic limit.

An ATP-driven proton pump that acidifies many eukaryotic endomembrane compartments.

{parent=Active transport}

Transport of a substrate against its electrochemical tendency using downhill movement of another solute, rather than direct ATP hydrolysis by that transporter.

A membrane pump coupling ATP hydrolysis to sodium extrusion and potassium uptake. Its gradients support electrical excitability and secondary transport; membrane location matters for epithelial directionality.

= Exocytosis
{parent=Membrane transport}
{wiki}

<Exocytosis> releases the contents of a membrane-bounded compartment to the extracellular space by fusion with the <cell membrane>. <Synaptic vesicle> fusion is an example. Its contents are released, while its membrane becomes connected to the <cell membrane>; see <synaptic vesicle> for recovery and reuse.

= Endocytosis
{parent=Membrane transport}
{wiki}

<Endocytosis> takes material into a <biological cell> by inward budding and separation of part of its <cell membrane>. At a <chemical synapse>, it helps recover membrane and <proteins> after <synaptic vesicle> <exocytosis>. This recovery is distinct from uptake of individual <neurotransmitter> <molecules> through <membrane transport proteins>.

= Synaptic vesicle recycling
{parent=Endocytosis}

<synaptic vesicle recycling> retrieves and reuses membrane and components after synaptic release. Uptake, uncoating, sorting and preparation for subsequent release are distinct measurable steps.

= Clathrin-mediated endocytosis
{parent=Endocytosis}

<clathrin-mediated endocytosis> internalizes selected surface material through assembly of adaptors and a <clathrin> coat, membrane deformation, scission and subsequent coat release. Counts of coated puncta alone do not distinguish initiation, successful internalization and stalled intermediates.

= Phagocytosis
{parent=Endocytosis}
{wiki}

<Phagocytosis> encloses a large extracellular particle by extending and closing the <cell membrane> around it. It is one possible conceptual route for internalizing a symbiotic partner, but a modern phagocytic pathway should not be assumed to have existed before eukaryotic organization evolved.

= Fluid mosaic model
{parent=Cell membrane}
{wiki}

The <fluid mosaic model> describes a <cell membrane> as a fluid <lipid bilayer> with embedded <membrane proteins>. It connects a continuous barrier to a mosaic of functional components: <diffusion> within the membrane can coexist with selective passage across it. S. J. Singer and G. L. Nicolson presented the <fluid mosaic model> in Science in 1972; see https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4333397/[Singer and Nicolson, The fluid mosaic model of the structure of cell membranes]. The useful picture does not require every <protein> to move freely: attachments and interactions can restrict movement, as discussed in https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK9898/[Cooper, The Cell: structure of the plasma membrane].
