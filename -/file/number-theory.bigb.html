= Number theory
{wiki}

Number theory studies <integers>, their <divisibility>, and the patterns created by <prime numbers>. Its questions often have short statements but rigid answers: which <integers> divide a given one, when does an <equation> have integer solutions, and when can arithmetic with <congruence classes> be reversed? The <Euclidean algorithm> and <modular multiplicative inverses> connect these questions through explicit calculations.

For example, $252=2^2\cdot3^2\cdot7$ and $198=2\cdot3^2\cdot11$. Their shared <prime factors> give <greatest common divisor> $18$. The <Euclidean algorithm> reaches that answer without finding either complete <prime factorization>. It also produces $18=4\cdot252-5\cdot198$, an instance of <Bézout's identity>. This relation certifies every common <divisor> at once and solves an associated <linear Diophantine equation>.

<Modular arithmetic> retains only remainders, but the lost information can be surprisingly controlled. Multiplication by $5$ modulo $17$ is a <permutation> of all residue classes, because $5$ has <modular inverse> $7$. Multiplication by $5$ modulo $15$ collapses fifteen residue classes to three. Thus an apparently small change in the modulus changes whether a calculation can be reversed. The <Chinese remainder theorem> explains another kind of compression: remainders modulo <coprime integers> can be combined into one remainder modulo their product.

Simple restrictions help reveal the structure without telling the whole story. Every <prime number> greater than three is congruent to $1$ or $5$ modulo $6$: the other four residue classes force <divisibility> by $2$ or $3$. The converse fails because $25$ and $35$ lie in those classes but are <composite numbers>. Necessary conditions are useful filters, not substitutes for <primality testing>.

The subject's appeal lies partly in this tension: <prime factorization> gives a clean description of every positive <integer>, while identifying factors and predicting their distribution lead to substantial further questions. <Analytic number theory> uses tools from <analysis> to study global patterns, and <algebraic number theory> extends arithmetic into <number fields>. Applications include <cryptography> and <error-correcting codes>; MIT's discrete applied mathematics course develops modular structures for both purposes. \a[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-200-principles-of-discrete-applied-mathematics-spring-2024/mit18_200_s24_lec15.pdf][Ankur Moitra, MIT 18.200, Modular Arithmetic and Elementary Algebra, 2024, introduction]{ref}

Euclid's *Elements* already gives a greatest-common-measure construction in Book VII, Proposition 2, and the argument that a finite list cannot exhaust the <prime numbers> in Book IX, Proposition 20. These are documented ancient treatments, rather than evidence that all of number theory began with one author. \a[https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/java/elements/bookVII/propVII2.html][Euclid, Elements VII.2, translated text hosted by David Joyce]{ref} \a[https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/java/elements/bookIX/propIX20.html][Euclid, Elements IX.20, translated text hosted by David Joyce]{ref}

= Integer factorization
{parent=Number theory}
{title2=$N=\prod_p p^{v_p(N)}$}
{wiki}

Finding the <prime factorization> of a positive integer. It is distinct from <primality testing>: deciding that an integer is composite does not itself supply its factors. For a semiprime, knowledge of a nontrivial square root of unity gives a factor through a <greatest common divisor> calculation.

= Factor base
{parent=Integer factorization}

A factor base is a finite set of small primes, often with the sign factor $-1$ included, over which selected integer residues are completely factored. A <smooth number> supported on this set gives an exponent vector. Linear dependence of such vectors modulo two produces a product that is a square, enabling congruence-of-squares <integer factorization>.

= Odd part of a positive integer
{parent=Integer factorization}
{title2=$n=2^{v_2(n)}d$}

The odd part of a positive <integer> $n$ is the unique odd positive <integer> $d$ obtained by repeatedly dividing $n$ by two. The process terminates because the positive <integers> decrease. If $2^r d=2^s e$ with $d,e$ odd, then $r>s$ would make $e$ even and $s>r$ would make $d$ even; hence $r=s$ and $d=e$. Two positive <integers> have the same odd part exactly when their ratio is an integral power of two. The odd positive <integers> therefore give representatives for the resulting <equivalence classes>.

= Congruence of squares
{parent=Integer factorization}
{title2=$a^2\equiv b^2\pmod N$}

A congruence of squares is useful for <integer factorization> because $N$ divides $(a-b)(a+b)$. If $a$ is not congruent to either $b$ or $-b$ modulo an odd semiprime, <greatest common divisor> computations with $a-b$ and $a+b$ recover proper factors. Smooth relations combined to make all prime exponents even produce such congruences in sieve-based factorization methods.

= Erdős-Turán inequality
{parent=Number theory}
{c}

The Erdős-Turán inequality bounds discrepancy of a finite sequence on the circle by a smoothing error and exponential sums of its low Fourier modes.

= Fermat number
{c}
{parent=Number theory}
{title2=$F_n=2^{2^n}+1$}
{wiki}

A Fermat number is $F_n=2^{2^n}+1$ for $n\geq0$. Distinct <Fermat numbers> are <coprime integers>. Their prime divisors are constrained by the <multiplicative order> of $2$ modulo that prime; this remains useful whether or not the Fermat number itself is prime. The initial value $F_0=3$ must be distinguished when a statement restricts to positive indices.

= Prime divisor of a Fermat number
{parent=Fermat number}
{title2=$\operatorname{ord}_p(2)=2^{n+1}$}

If a <prime number> $p$ divides the <Fermat number> $F_n$, it is odd and $2^{2^n}\equiv-1\pmod p$. The <multiplicative order> of $2$ divides $2^{n+1}$ but not $2^n$, so it is exactly $2^{n+1}$. <Fermat's little theorem> then gives $2^{n+1}\mid p-1$. For $n\geq1$, every prime divisor is $1$ modulo $4$. A prime need not divide any Fermat number merely because it is $1$ modulo $4$: order $12$ excludes the prime $13$ from the whole family.

= Bernoulli number
{c}
{parent=Number theory}
{title2=$B_n$}
{wiki}

The Bernoulli numbers in the convention $B_1=-1/2$ are defined by $t/(e^t-1)=\sum_{n\geq0}B_nt^n/n!$. The first values are $1,-1/2,1/6,0,-1/30$. The function $t/(e^t-1)+t/2$ is even, so all odd-index values beyond $B_1$ vanish.

= Hyperbolic cotangent generating function for Bernoulli numbers
{parent=Bernoulli number}
{title2=$\coth z=z^{-1}+\sum_{j\geq1}\frac{2^{2j}B_{2j}}{(2j)!}z^{2j-1}$}

The defining generating function of the <Bernoulli numbers>, with $B_1=-1/2$, gives this identity of formal <Laurent series> by $\coth z=1+2/(e^{2z}-1)$. Therefore $a\coth(az)-\coth z$ is regular and odd. Integrating it gives the even formal logarithm of $\sinh(az)/(a\sinh z)$. Its even derivatives of order $k$ at zero are $2^k(a^k-1)B_k/k$, while the odd derivatives vanish. This computes the <cyclotomic higher logarithmic derivatives> of symmetric <cyclotomic units> without introducing a spurious linear term.

= Euler evaluation of even zeta values
{c}
{parent=Bernoulli number}
{title2=$2\zeta(2r)=-\frac{B_{2r}(2\pi i)^{2r}}{(2r)!}$}

Expand the <cotangent partial-fraction expansion> at zero to obtain $\pi\cot(\pi z)=z^{-1}-2\sum_{r\geq1}\zeta(2r)z^{2r-1}$. Substituting $t=2\pi iz$ in the generating function of the <Bernoulli numbers> gives the same <Laurent series> as $z^{-1}+\sum_{r\geq1}B_{2r}(2\pi i)^{2r}z^{2r-1}/(2r)!$. Equality of <Laurent coefficients> proves the formula.

= Generalized Bernoulli number
{parent=Bernoulli number}
{title2=$B_{k,\chi}$}

For a <Dirichlet character> $\chi$ of conductor $f$, define the generalized Bernoulli numbers by
$$
\sum_{a=1}^{f}\chi(a)\frac{z e^{az}}{e^{fz}-1}
=\sum_{k\geq0}B_{k,\chi}\frac{z^k}{k!}.
$$
They express special values of <Dirichlet L-functions> at nonpositive integers and occur in the interpolation formula for <Kubota-Leopoldt p-adic L-functions>.

= Normal number
{parent=Number theory}
{wiki}

A real number is normal in base $K\geq2$ if every word of $r$ digits occurs in its base-$K$ expansion with limiting overlapping frequency $K^{-r}$, for every $r\geq1$.

= Normality and equidistribution under integer multiplication
{parent=Normal number}

A number $x$ is a <normal number> in base $K$ exactly when $(T_K^nx)$ is an <equidistributed sequence>. A word of length $r$ corresponds to a half-open interval of length $K^{-r}$; these intervals form arbitrarily fine grids, which approximate any interval from inside and outside.

= Absolutely normal number
{parent=Normal number}

An absolutely normal number is a <normal number> in every integer base $K\geq2$. The <Birkhoff ergodic theorem> and <ergodicity of integer multiplication on the circle>, followed by a countable intersection of full-measure sets, show that almost every real number is absolutely normal.

= Natural density
{parent=Number theory}
{title2=$d(S)$}
{wiki}

For $S\subseteq\mathbb Z_{\geq0}$, its natural density, when it exists, is $d(S)=\lim_{N\to\infty}|S\cap\{0,\ldots,N-1\}|/N$.

= Upper asymptotic density
{parent=Natural density}
{title2=$\overline d(A)$}

= Upper natural density
{synonym}

For a subset $A$ of the positive integers, its upper asymptotic density is $\overline d(A)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}|A\cap\{1,\ldots,N\}|/N$. Unlike <natural density>, it always exists. If <natural density> exists, the two agree. Positive upper asymptotic density suffices for the <Szemerédi theorem>.

= Full-density sequence
{parent=Natural density}

An increasing sequence of integers has full density when its range has <natural density> one. <Convergence in density of a sequence> means that, for each fixed tolerance, the exceptional indices have <natural density> zero.

= Lagrange four-square theorem

The Lagrange four-square theorem says that every nonnegative integer is a sum of four integer squares.

= Arithmetic function
{parent=Number theory}
{wiki}

An arithmetic function is a function whose domain is the <positive integer>[positive integers], usually taking values in the <complex number>[complex numbers].

= Radical of an integer
{parent=Arithmetic function}
{title2=$\operatorname{rad}(n)=\prod_{p\mid n}p$}
{wiki}

The radical of a positive <integer> is the product of its distinct <prime factors>, with $\operatorname{rad}(1)=1$. It is the squarefree integer recording which primes occur, independently of their exponents. Equality of radicals is equivalent to mutual divisibility by sufficiently high positive powers.

= Total number of prime factors
{parent=Arithmetic function}
{title2=$\Omega(n)$}

= Prime omega function with multiplicity
{synonym}

The <completely additive arithmetic function> $\Omega(n)=\sum_p v_p(n)$ counts <prime factors> with multiplicity, whereas the <prime omega function> $\omega(n)$ counts distinct <prime factors>.

= Excess prime-factor multiplicity
{parent=Total number of prime factors}
{title2=$\Omega(n)-\omega(n)$}

The nonnegative <arithmetic function> $\Omega(n)-\omega(n)$ counts the repeated occurrences beyond the first of each <prime factor>. Its <expectation> under uniform sampling from $[N]$ is bounded by $\sum_p1/(p(p-1))\le1$, independently of $N$.

= Additive function
{disambiguate=number theory}
{parent=Arithmetic function}
{wiki=Additive_function}

= Additive arithmetic function
{synonym}

An <arithmetic function> $f$ is additive when $f(mn)=f(m)+f(n)$ for <coprime integers> $m,n$. Equivalently, $f(n)=\sum_{p^k\parallel n}f(p^k)$. This concerns multiplication of integers, and differs from an <additive function> on a vector space.

= Mean of an additive arithmetic function
{parent=Additive function (number theory)}
{title2=$A_f(x)$}

For an <additive arithmetic function>, the model mean is $A_f(x)=\sum_{p^k\le x}f(p^k)(1-1/p)/p^k$. The exact uniform <expectation> differs by $O(x^{-1}\sum_{p^k\le x}|f(p^k)|)$.

= Strongly additive arithmetic function
{parent=Additive function (number theory)}

= Strongly additive function
{synonym}

A <strongly additive arithmetic function> satisfies $f(p^k)=f(p)$ for every <prime> $p$ and $k\ge1$, so $f(n)=\sum_{p\mid n}f(p)$. The <prime omega function> is an example.

= Completely additive arithmetic function
{parent=Additive function (number theory)}

= Completely additive function
{synonym}

A <completely additive arithmetic function> satisfies $f(mn)=f(m)+f(n)$ for all positive $m,n$, equivalently $f(p^k)=k f(p)$. The <total number of prime factors> is an example.

= Multiplicative function
{parent=Arithmetic function}
{wiki}

= Multiplicative arithmetic function
{synonym}

An arithmetic function $f$ is multiplicative when $f(mn)=f(m)f(n)$ whenever $m$ and $n$ are coprime.

= Multiplicativity of an arithmetic function
{parent=Multiplicative function}

= Multiplicativity
{synonym}

Multiplicativity means $f(mn)=f(m)f(n)$ whenever $m,n$ are <coprime integers>. It allows values of an <arithmetic function> to be assembled from its values on <prime powers>.

= Completely multiplicative function
{parent=Multiplicative function}
{wiki}

An arithmetic function $f$ is completely multiplicative when $f(mn)=f(m)f(n)$ for every pair of positive integers $m,n$, without a coprimality condition.

= Divisor sum
{parent=Multiplicative function}

The divisor sum of an arithmetic function $f$ is
$$
F(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}f(d).
$$
If $f$ is multiplicative, then $F$ is multiplicative as well.

= Sum-of-divisors function
{title2=$\sigma_1(n)$}
{parent=Divisor sum}

The sum-of-divisors function is
$$
\sigma_1(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}d.
$$
It is the <divisor sum> of the identity arithmetic function $n\mapsto n$.

= Pretentious distance
{title2=$\mathbb D(f,g;x)$}
{parent=Multiplicative function}

= Granville-Soundararajan distance
{synonym}
{c}

For multiplicative functions bounded by one, the pretentious distance up to $x$ is defined by
$$
\mathbb D(f,g;x)^2
=\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{1-\Re(f(p)\overline{g(p)})}{p}.
$$
It measures how similarly $f$ and $g$ behave on primes.

= Large Fourier coefficient criterion for pretentiousness
{parent=Pretentious distance}
{title2=$|\widehat\nu(t)|\leq C\log X\,e^{-D(f,n^{it};X)^2}$}

Let $X\geq3$, and let $f$ be a normalized <multiplicative arithmetic function> supported on <squarefree integers>, with $|f(p)|\leq1$. Form the <finite measure> $\nu=\sum_n f(n)n^{-1-1/\log X}\delta_{\log n}$ using <Dirac measures>. Under the transform convention $\widehat\nu(t)=\int e^{-itu}\,d\nu(u)$, its <Euler product> is $\prod_p(1+f(p)p^{-1-1/\log X-it})$. Expanding its logarithmic modulus, and replacing the damped <prime> weights by $1/p$ up to $X$ with <Mertens first theorem>, gives the displayed absolute bound, uniformly in real $t$. Hence $|\widehat\nu(t)|\geq\delta\log X$ forces $D(f,n^{it};X)^2\leq\log(C/\delta)$. The distance expression still defines a nonnegative pretentious score for <prime> values bounded by one; its actual <metric> interpretation uses unit-modulus values.

= Pretentious distance between one and the Möbius function
{parent=Pretentious distance}
{title2=$D(1,\mu;X)\sim\sqrt{2\log\log X}$}

Because $\mu(p)=-1$, the squared <pretentious distance> from one to the <Möbius function> is twice the reciprocal-prime sum. The <Mertens second theorem> therefore gives $D(1,\mu;X)^2=2\log\log X+2B_1+o(1)$. In particular the distance diverges, illustrating that a multiplicative function can be far from the constant function even though both have unit-modulus <prime> values.

= Prime-restriction metric for pretentious distance
{parent=Pretentious distance}
{title2=$D(f,g;X)^2=\tfrac12\sum_{p\leq X}|f(p)-g(p)|^2/p$}

For unit-modulus <prime> values, <pretentious distance> is the Euclidean <metric> on the finite vectors $(f(p)/\sqrt{2p})_{p\leq X}$. On whole <arithmetic functions> it is only a <pseudometric>: it sees neither values on <prime powers> nor <primes> above the cutoff. Even for every cutoff, the functions one and $\mu^2$ have distance zero but differ on nonsquarefree integers. The <norm> representation proves the <triangle inequality> and identifies exactly the quotient on which separation holds.

= Triangle inequality for pretentious distance
{parent=Pretentious distance}

The pretentious distance obeys
$$
\mathbb D(f_1f_2,g_1g_2;x)
\leq\mathbb D(f_1,g_1;x)+\mathbb D(f_2,g_2;x).
$$

= Archimedean character
{c}
{title2=$n^{it}$}
{parent=Pretentious distance}

An Archimedean character on the positive integers is the completely multiplicative function $n\mapsto n^{it}$ for a real parameter $t$.

= Halász theorem
{c}
{parent=Pretentious distance}

Halász's theorem bounds the mean of a bounded <multiplicative arithmetic function> in terms of its least <pretentious distance> from an <Archimedean character>. A large mean can occur only when this distance is small.

= Strong aperiodicity of the Möbius function
{parent=Pretentious distance}

The Möbius function stays far in <pretentious distance> from every <Archimedean character>. One useful uniform form is
$$
\inf_{|t|\leq2(\log x)^{1/10}}
\mathbb D(\mu,n^{it};x)^2\longrightarrow\infty.
$$
This follows by estimating the prime sum $\sum_{p\leq x}(1+\cos(t\log p))/p$.

= Dirichlet convolution
{c}
{title2=$*$}
{parent=Arithmetic function}
{wiki}

The Dirichlet convolution of arithmetic functions $f$ and $g$ is
$$
(f*g)(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}f(d)g(n/d).
$$
It is associative and commutative, its identity is the function supported at $1$, and the <Möbius function> is the convolution inverse of the constant-one function.

= Constant-one arithmetic function
{title2=$\mathbf 1$}
{parent=Dirichlet convolution}

The constant-one arithmetic function has $\mathbf 1(n)=1$ for every <positive integer> $n$. Its <Dirichlet convolution> with $f$ is the <divisor sum> $(\mathbf 1*f)(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}f(d)$.

= Identity arithmetic function
{title2=$\operatorname{id}$}
{parent=Dirichlet convolution}

The identity arithmetic function is $\operatorname{id}(n)=n$. The identity $\operatorname{id}=\mathbf 1*\varphi$ is equivalent to $\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d)=n$.

= Identity for Dirichlet convolution
{title2=$\varepsilon$}
{parent=Dirichlet convolution}

The identity for <Dirichlet convolution> is $\varepsilon(1)=1$ and $\varepsilon(n)=0$ for $n>1$. It satisfies $f*\varepsilon=f$ for every <arithmetic function> $f$.

= Dirichlet hyperbola method
{c}
{parent=Dirichlet convolution}
{wiki}

The Dirichlet hyperbola method evaluates a summatory <Dirichlet convolution> by splitting the lattice points $ab\leq x$ at $a,b\leq\sqrt x$. For the <divisor function>,
$$
\sum_{n\leq x}\tau(n)
=2\sum_{a\leq\sqrt x}\left\lfloor\frac xa\right\rfloor
-\lfloor\sqrt x\rfloor^2.
$$

= Divisor summatory asymptotic
{parent=Dirichlet hyperbola method}
{title2=$\sum_{n\le x}\tau(n)=x\log x+(2\gamma-1)x+O(\sqrt x)$}

The <Dirichlet hyperbola method> gives $D(x)=2\sum_{a\le r}\lfloor x/a\rfloor-r^2$, where $r=\lfloor\sqrt x\rfloor$. The <harmonic number> estimate $H_r=\log r+\gamma+O(1/r)$, with $\gamma$ the <Euler's constant>, yields $D(x)=2x(\log r+\gamma)-r^2+O(x/r+r)$. Substitution of $r=\sqrt x+O(1)$ gives the displayed <asymptotic expansion>. Comparison of $\sum_{n\le x}\log n$ with its integral consequently gives $\sum_{n\le N}(\tau(n)-\log n-2\gamma)=O(\sqrt N)$ for integer $N$.

= Harmonic divisor sum
{parent=Dirichlet hyperbola method}

Write $B(t)=\sum_{n\leq t}\tau(n)/n$ and $r=\lfloor\sqrt t\rfloor$. The weighted <Dirichlet hyperbola method> gives
$$
B(t)=2\sum_{a\leq r}\frac{H_{\lfloor t/a\rfloor}}a-H_r^2
=\tfrac12\log^2t+2\gamma\log t+c+O\left(\frac{\log(2t)}{\sqrt t}\right).
$$
Indeed, use $H_{\lfloor u\rfloor}=\log u+\gamma+O(1/u)$ and $\sum_{a\leq r}(\log a)/a=\tfrac12\log^2r+c\prime+O(\log(2r)/r)$. The latter follows by comparing each summand with the integral on its unit interval: the derivative of $(\log t)/t$ has an integrable tail. Since $r=\sqrt t+O(1)$, substitution yields the expansion. Here $H_n$ is the <harmonic number> and $\gamma$ the <Euler-Mascheroni constant>.

= Divisor function
{title2=$\tau(n)$}
{parent=Arithmetic function}
{wiki}

The divisor function $\tau(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}1$ counts the positive <integer divisor>[integer divisors] of $n$.

= Subpower bound for the divisor function
{parent=Divisor function}
{title2=$\tau(n)\ll_\eta n^\eta\quad(\eta>0)$}

For each $\eta>0$, the <divisor function> satisfies the displayed bound. Write $n=\prod p^{a_p}$, so $\tau(n)=\prod(a_p+1)$. For all sufficiently large <primes>, $a+1\le2^a\le p^{\eta a}$ for every $a\ge0$. For each of the finitely many smaller <primes>, $\sup_{a\ge0}(a+1)p^{-\eta a}$ is finite. Multiplying these finitely many constants proves the bound. Products of a fixed number of divisor functions obey the same conclusion after reducing $\eta$.

= Divisor-square summatory bound
{parent=Divisor function}
{title2=$\sum_{n\le X}d(n)^2\ll X\log^3(2X)$}

The <divisor function> satisfies $\sum_{n\le X}d(n)^2\ll X\log^3(2X)$. <Partial summation> consequently gives $\sum_{n\le X}d(n)^2/n\ll\log^4(2X)$.

= Von Mangoldt function
{c}
{title2=$\Lambda(n)$}
{parent=Arithmetic function}
{wiki}

The Von Mangoldt function is $\Lambda(n)=\log p$ when $n$ is a positive power of a <prime number> $p$, and is zero otherwise.

= Selberg symmetry formula
{parent=Von Mangoldt function}
{c}

The elementary Selberg symmetry formula can be written
$$
\psi(x)+\frac1{\log x}\sum_{n\leq x}\Lambda(n)\psi(x/n)=2x+O\left(\frac{x}{\log x}\right).
$$
Use the weights $\lambda_d=\mu(d)\log(x/d)/\log x$. Their <divisor sum> is $1_{n=1}+\Lambda(n)/\log x$. Apply them to the <integrated Chebyshev sum>, then use the <Möbius harmonic logarithmic moments>. The <Chebyshev estimate> shows this is equivalent, with an $O(x)$ error, to $\sum_{n\leq x}\Lambda(n)\log n+\sum_{mn\leq x}\Lambda(m)\Lambda(n)=2x\log x+O(x)$.

= Von Mangoldt divisor identity
{c}
{parent=Von Mangoldt function}

For every <positive integer> $n$,
$$
\sum_{d\mid n}\Lambda(d)=\log n.
$$
Equivalently, the constant-one function convolved with the <Von Mangoldt function> is the natural logarithm.

The two Chebyshev functions are

\vartheta(x)=\sum_{p\leq x}\log p,
\qquad
\psi(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}\Lambda(n)

$$

= Second Chebyshev function
{title2=$\psi(x)$}
{c}
{parent=Chebyshev function}

The second Chebyshev function is the summatory <Von Mangoldt function>, $\psi(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}\Lambda(n)$.

= First integral of the Chebyshev function
{parent=Second Chebyshev function}
{title2=$\Psi_1(x)=\int_0^x\psi(u)\,du$}

For the <second Chebyshev function>, finite summation gives $\Psi_1(x)=\sum_{n\le x}(x-n)\Lambda(n)$. For $c>1$, Mellin inversion of the kernel $1/(s(s+1))$ gives
$$
\Psi_1(x)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}-\frac{\zeta'(s)}{\zeta(s)}\frac{x^{s+1}}{s(s+1)}\,ds.
$$
The extra denominator makes this smoothing useful: the resulting sum over <nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function> is absolutely convergent. This integral differs from the <integrated Chebyshev sum>, which denotes a sum of the values $\psi(x/n)$.

= Smoothed explicit formula for the Chebyshev function
{parent=First integral of the Chebyshev function}
{title2=$\Psi_1(x)=x^2/2-\sum_\rho x^{\rho+1}/(\rho(\rho+1))+O(x)$}

For $x>1$, moving the Mellin contour past the <pole> at one, the <nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function>, and the remaining poles gives
$$
\Psi_1(x)=\frac{x^2}{2}-\sum_\rho\frac{x^{\rho+1}}{\rho(\rho+1)}-x\log(2\pi)+\frac{\zeta'(-1)}{\zeta(-1)}-\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{x^{1-2k}}{2k(2k-1)}.
$$
Zeros are included with multiplicity. The <Riemann–von Mangoldt formula> makes the zero sum absolutely convergent. If every nontrivial zero has real part below one, division by $x^2$ and <dominated convergence> give $\Psi_1(x)\sim x^2/2$. Monotonicity of $\psi$ then recovers $\psi(x)\sim x$ by upper and lower difference quotients.

= Integrated Chebyshev sum
{parent=Second Chebyshev function}
{title2=$T(x)$}

For $\psi(t)=\sum_{m\leq t}\Lambda(m)$, finite rearrangement and the <Von Mangoldt divisor identity> give
$$
T(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}\psi(x/n)=\sum_{m\leq x}\log m=\log(\lfloor x\rfloor!).
$$
The <Stirling formula>, or integral comparison for the <logarithm>, gives $T(x)=x\log x-x+O(\log(x+1))$ for real $x\geq1$.

= Chebyshev estimate
{c}
{parent=Second Chebyshev function}

The Chebyshev estimate is
$$
\psi(x)\asymp x.
$$
It gives upper and lower bounds of the correct linear order without asserting the <prime number theorem> asymptotic $\psi(x)\sim x$.

= Chebyshev estimate from central binomial coefficients
{c}
{parent=Chebyshev estimate}

The central <binomial coefficient> satisfies $4^m/(2m+1)\leq\binom{2m}{m}\leq4^m$. Prime factors between $m$ and $2m$ give a dyadic upper bound for the <Chebyshev theta function>. The prime-power exponents in the factorial quotient are sums of zeros and ones, so the central coefficient gives a linear lower bound for the <second Chebyshev function>. Higher prime powers contribute only $O(\sqrt x\log x)$; hence the prime-counting function has upper and lower bounds of order $x/\log x$.

= Type I–type II inverse principle for Möbius correlation
{parent=Vaughan identity for the Möbius function}

For $|f|\leq1$, if $|\sum_{n\leq X}\mu(n)f(n)|\geq\delta X$ and $U+V\leq\delta X/2$, the <Vaughan identity for the Möbius function> gives a <type I sum> or a <type II sum> of modulus at least $\delta X/4$. = Vaughan identity proof
{parent=Vaughan's identity}
{c}

For <Dirichlet convolution>, let $\mathbf1(n)=1$ and $\epsilon(n)=1_{n=1}$. <Prime factorization> gives $\mu*\mathbf1=\epsilon$ and $\Lambda*\mathbf1=\log$. With subscripts denoting truncation at $U,V$,

\begin{aligned}
\mu*\log&=\Lambda,\\
\mu_{>U}*\mathbf1*\Lambda_{>V}

Rearranging proves the <Vaughan identity>.

= Möbius harmonic logarithmic moments
{parent=Möbius function}
{c}
{title2=$A_j(x)$}

= Prime number asymptotic from Mertens cancellation
{parent=Mertens function}
{title2=$M(x)=o(x)\Longrightarrow\psi(x)=x+o(x)$}

= Mertens bound from a log-integrable Chebyshev error

= Repdigit
{parent=Integer}
{title2=$R_n=d\frac{b^n-1}{b-1}$}
{wiki}

A <repdigit> is a positive integer whose base-$b$ expansion repeats one nonzero digit $d$. For $b\ge2$, $1\le d<b$ and $n\ge1$, the <finite geometric series> gives $R_n=d(1+b+\cdots+b^{n-1})=d(b^n-1)/(b-1)$. For example, $5(10^n-1)/9$ has $n$ decimal digits, all equal to five.

Every <prime number> not dividing $b$ divides infinitely many such <repdigits>. If it divides $d$, every length works. Otherwise, if it does not divide $b-1$, the <Fermat little theorem> gives divisibility for lengths that are multiples of $p-1$, and division by $b-1$ is justified by its <modular inverse>. If $p\mid b-1$, the sum is congruent to $dn$ modulo $p$, so lengths divisible by $p$ work instead. A prime dividing $b$ but not $d$ divides none, since $R_n\equiv d\pmod p$. This distinction prevents invalid cancellation of the geometric-sum denominator.

= Integer congruence
{parent=Modular arithmetic}
{title2=$a\equiv b\pmod m\iff m\mid(a-b)$}

For a positive modulus $m$, integers $a,b$ are congruent when their difference is divisible by $m$. This is an <equivalence relation>: zero is divisible by $m$, changing sign preserves <divisibility>, and adding two divisible differences proves transitivity. Addition and multiplication respect it, because $(a+c)-(b+d)=(a-b)+(c-d)$ and $ac-bd=c(a-b)+b(c-d)$. Its <equivalence classes> are the residue classes used in <modular arithmetic>.

= Wilson theorem
{c}
{synonym}

For a prime $p$,
$$
(p-1)!\equiv-1\pmod p.
$$
Every nonzero residue other than $1$ and $-1$ pairs with a distinct multiplicative inverse, leaving only $1(-1)$ in the product.

= Wilson factorial congruence modulo a triangular number
{c}
{parent=Wilson's theorem}
{title2=$(p-1)!\equiv p-1\pmod{p(p-1)/2}$}

For an odd <prime number> $p$, put $m=(p-1)/2$. Both $(p-1)!$ and $p-1$ are divisible by $m$. By <Wilson's theorem>, they are also congruent modulo $p$, because each is $-1$ modulo $p$. The <coprime integers> $m,p$ therefore both divide their difference, giving the displayed <integer congruence>. This combines the prime-modulus factorial identity with an elementary divisibility constraint.

= Odd-residue squared product modulo a prime
{parent=Wilson's theorem}
{title2=$\prod_{j=1}^{(p-3)/2}(2j+1)^2\equiv(-1)^{(p+1)/2}\pmod p$}

For an odd prime $p$, the even residues are the negatives of the odd residues. If $O=1\cdot3\cdots(p-2)$, then $(p-1)!\equiv(-1)^{(p-1)/2}O^2$. Apply <Wilson theorem> to obtain the displayed congruence. The empty product for $p=3$ is one.

= Complementary factorial congruence
{parent=Wilson's theorem}
{title2=$h!k!\equiv(-1)^{h+1}\pmod p$}

For a <prime number> $p$ and nonnegative <integers> $h+k=p-1$, <Wilson theorem> gives $(-1)^k h!k!\equiv-1\pmod p$ by replacing the last $k$ factors of $(p-1)!$ with $-1,-2,\ldots,-k$. For odd $p$, $h,k$ have the same parity; for $p=2$, both signs have the same <residue class>. Thus $h!k!+(-1)^h\equiv0\pmod p$, including the endpoint cases $0!=1$.

= Congruence class

The <congruence class> of $a$ modulo a positive integer $N$ is $\{a+Nk:k\in\mathbb Z\}$. Two integers have the same class exactly when their difference is divisible by $N$. Addition and multiplication of representatives descend to well-defined operations on $\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z$, because replacing a representative by a multiple-of-$N$ translate changes the result by a multiple of $N$.

= Prime-power contraction under pth powers

= Infinitely many primes congruent to one modulo six
{parent=Prime number}
{title2=$\#\{p\text{ prime}:p\equiv1\pmod6\}=\infty$}

Assume that $x^2\equiv-3\pmod p$ with $p>3$ implies $p\equiv1\pmod6$. For a finite list of primes in that residue class, let $P$ be their product. Every prime divisor of $4P^2+3$ is greater than three and satisfies that quadratic <modular congruence>, hence has residue one modulo six. None is listed because $4P^2+3\equiv3$ modulo a listed prime.

= Infinitely many primes congruent to minus one modulo six
{parent=Prime number}
{title2=$\#\{p\text{ prime}:p\equiv-1\pmod6\}=\infty$}

For any finite list of such <prime numbers>, let $P$ be their product. The integer $6P-1$ has no listed prime divisor, nor the divisors $2,3$. Its remaining prime factors have residues $1,-1$ modulo six, and at least one has residue $-1$ because their product has that residue. Thus no finite list is exhaustive.

For every integer $n>1$, there is a <prime number> strictly between $n$ and $2n$. This supplies cyclic prime moduli comparable to an integer interval's length in <additive combinatorics>.

= Twin prime
{parent=Prime number}
{wiki}

Two <primes> differing by two form a twin-prime pair, for example $3,5$ or $11,13$. The <twin-prime upper bound using Bombieri–Vinogradov> limits their number without proving there are infinitely many.

= Twin prime conjecture
{parent=Twin prime}

The conjecture asserts that infinitely many <prime numbers> $p$ have $p+2$ prime. The <Hardy–Littlewood twin-prime asymptotic conjecture> predicts their quantitative frequency and is stronger. The <Selberg sieve> supplies an upper bound of the predicted order but does not prove infinitude.

= Hardy–Littlewood twin-prime asymptotic conjecture
{c}
{parent=Twin prime conjecture}
{title2=$\#\{p\leq N:p+2\text{ prime}\}\sim2C_2N/(\log N)^2$}

The local-density prediction for twin primes is the displayed asymptotic, with the <twin-prime constant> $C_2$. It is stronger than infinitude alone. The <Selberg sieve> proves the correct order of magnitude as an upper bound, but the <parity problem in sieve theory> prevents its elementary majorant from proving this conjectured asymptotic.

= Twin-prime constant
{parent=Twin prime conjecture}
{title2=$C_2=\prod_{p>2}(1-(p-1)^{-2})$}

This positive convergent product corrects for the dependence of primality of $n$ and $n+2$ at each odd prime. The associated singular series is $2C_2$: the factor at two is two, and at an odd prime the local correction is $(1-2/p)/(1-1/p)^2$. It appears in the <Hardy–Littlewood twin-prime asymptotic conjecture> and in upper bounds from the <Selberg sieve>.

= Brun theorem
{c}
{synonym}

The sum of reciprocals of <twin primes> converges, whether or not there are infinitely many <twin primes>. The <twin-prime upper bound from a quadratic sieve> and <reciprocal-sum convergence from a counting bound> prove it. This contrasts with the <Euler proof that the sum of reciprocals of primes diverges>.

= Twin-prime upper bound using Bombieri–Vinogradov
{parent=Twin prime}

Apply the <Selberg upper-bound sieve> to the numbers $p+2$ with $p\leq x$ <prime>. For <odd> <squarefree integers> $d$, the local count is $\pi(x;d,-2)$ and the density is $1/\phi(d)$. With sieve level $D=x^{1/3}$, the normalizing sum is $\gg\log x$ by the <truncated Euler-product lower bound>. The <Selberg least-common-multiple weights> have <absolute values> at most $3^{\omega(d)}$. The <weighted arithmetic-progression error bound> and <Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem> make their total error $O(x/\log^3x)$. Consequently the number of <twin-prime> pairs with smaller member at most $x$ is $O(x/\log^2x)$.

= Euclid's theorem
{parent=Prime number}
{c}
{wiki}

There are infinitely many <prime numbers>. Indeed, for any finite list of <prime numbers> $p_1,\ldots,p_k$, the <integer> $N=p_1\cdots p_k+1>1$ has a <prime factor>, by the <fundamental theorem of arithmetic>. None of the listed <prime numbers> divides $N$, since each divides $N-1$ and a common <divisor> would divide $1$. Thus no finite list contains all <prime numbers>. The constructed $N$ need not itself be a <prime number>.

= Odd prime
{parent=Prime number}
{title2=$p>2$}

An odd prime is a <prime number> that is an <odd integer>. The only <even number> that is a <prime number> is $2$, since every other positive <even number> has $2$ as a proper <divisor>. Thus every <prime number> greater than $2$ is an odd prime. There are infinitely many odd primes, by <Euclid's theorem>.

= Fermat prime
{parent=Prime number}
{c}
{wiki}

A Fermat prime is a <prime number> of the form
$$
F_k=2^{2^k}+1.
$$
For $k\geq1$, it is congruent to $1$ modulo $4$ and to $2$ modulo $3$.

= Quadratic nonresidues modulo a Fermat prime
{parent=Fermat prime}
{c}

For a Fermat prime $p=2^{2^k}+1$, the multiplicative group modulo $p$ has order $2^{2^k}$. Its $\varphi(p-1)=(p-1)/2$ primitive roots are all quadratic nonresidues; since there are also $(p-1)/2$ nonresidues, the two sets coincide.

= Fundamental theorem of arithmetic
{parent=Prime number}
{wiki}
{title2=$n=\prod_{p\mid n}p^{v_p(n)}$}

= Prime factorization
{synonym}

= Unique prime factorization
{synonym}

Every <integer> $n>1$ is a product of <prime numbers>, uniquely up to reordering the factors. In the displayed form, $v_p(n)$ is the exponent of $p$ in its <prime factorization>. For example, $360=2^3\cdot3^2\cdot5$. Reordering or regrouping these factors does not give a different factorization.

This turns <divisibility> into coordinatewise comparison of nonnegative exponents. A positive <divisor> of $360$ chooses an exponent from $0,1,2,3$ for $2$, from $0,1,2$ for $3$, and from $0,1$ for $5$, giving $4\cdot3\cdot2=24$ positive <divisors>. The <greatest common divisor> takes the smaller exponent at each <prime number>; the <least common multiple> takes the larger. The theorem is a structural description, not an assertion that finding the factors of every large <integer> is computationally easy.

= Strong pseudoprime
{parent=Fermat pseudoprime}
{wiki}

Write the even number $N-1$ as $2^s m$ with $m$ odd. An odd composite number $N$ is a strong pseudoprime to a coprime base $b$ when
$$
b^m\equiv1\pmod N
$$
or $b^{2^jm}\equiv-1\pmod N$ for some $0\leq j<s$.

= Strong-pseudoprime bases for a product of two primes with common predecessor divisor ten
{parent=Strong pseudoprime}

Let $N=pq$ for distinct odd <prime numbers> $p,q$ with $\gcd(p-1,q-1)=10$. Exactly $100$ unit bases make $N$ a <Fermat pseudoprime>, and exactly $50$ of these make it a <strong pseudoprime>. The Fermat bases form $C_{10}\times C_{10}$ under the <Chinese remainder theorem for unit groups>. If $N-1=2^sm$, then $5\mid m$ and $m$ is odd, so the $m$th-power map sends five elements of each $C_{10}$ to $1$ and five to $-1$. The strong condition requires equal signs in both components, giving $5^2+5^2=50$.

= Euler pseudoprime
{parent=Number theory}
{c}
{wiki}

An odd composite number $N$ is an Euler pseudoprime to a coprime base $b$ when
$$
b^{(N-1)/2}\equiv\left(\frac bN\right)\pmod N,
$$
where the right side is the Jacobi symbol.

= Euler witness
{parent=Euler pseudoprime}

For an odd integer $N$ and a unit $b$ modulo $N$, the base $b$ is an Euler witness when
$$
b^{(N-1)/2}\not\equiv\left(\frac bN\right)\pmod N.
$$
It therefore certifies that $N$ is composite.

= Euler witnesses occupy at least half the unit bases
{parent=Euler witness}

If one Euler witness $b_0$ exists modulo $N$, multiplication by $b_0$ injects the set of nonwitnesses into the set of witnesses. Indeed, the product of $b_0$ with a nonwitness cannot be another nonwitness, because cancellation and multiplicativity of the <Jacobi symbol> would make $b_0$ a nonwitness. Thus at least half of the units modulo $N$ are Euler witnesses.

= Existence of an Euler witness for every odd composite integer
{parent=Euler witness}

Every odd composite integer has an <Euler witness>. For a nonsquarefree integer, a base congruent to $1+p$ modulo a repeated prime factor $p^2$ fails the Euler congruence by the <binomial theorem>. For a squarefree integer, use the <Chinese remainder theorem> to choose a base that is a <quadratic nonresidue> modulo one prime factor and $1$ modulo another. Its <Jacobi symbol> is $-1$, while its power is $1$ modulo the second factor.

= Solovay-Strassen primality test
{c}
{parent=Euler witness}
{wiki=Solovay–Strassen_primality_test}

The Solovay-Strassen primality test samples unit bases and rejects an odd candidate as composite when it finds an <Euler witness>. Every prime passes every base by <Euler's criterion>, while an odd composite passes a random base with probability at most one half.

= P-adic valuation
{parent=Number theory}
{c}
{wiki}

For a prime $p$ and a nonzero integer $N$, the $p$-adic valuation is
$$
v_p(N)=\max\{a\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}:p^a\mid N\}.
$$
It extends to nonzero rational numbers by $v_p(a/b)=v_p(a)-v_p(b)$.

= p-adic integer
{title2=$\mathbb Z_p$}
{c}
{parent=P-adic valuation}
{wiki}

For a <prime number> $p$, the ring of p-adic integers is the <inverse limit>
$$
\mathbb Z_p=\varprojlim_n\mathbb Z/p^n\mathbb Z.
$$
Every p-adic integer has a unique convergent expansion $\sum_{j\geq0}b_jp^j$ with $0\leq b_j<p$. It is a complete <discrete valuation ring> with <uniformizer> $p$ and <residue field> $\mathbb F_p$.

= Open subgroups of the p-adic integers
{parent=p-adic integer}
{title2=$H\text{ open}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ [\mathbb Z_p:H]<\infty$}

The cosets $a+p^n\mathbb Z_p$ form a neighborhood basis in the additive group of <p-adic integers>. An open subgroup contains $p^n\mathbb Z_p$ for some $n$, so has finite index. Conversely, if the index is $m$, the finite quotient is annihilated by $m$, giving $m\mathbb Z_p\subset H$. Multiplication by the unit part of $m$ is bijective, so $m\mathbb Z_p=p^{v_p(m)}\mathbb Z_p$ is open. This proof applies to abstract subgroups without assuming beforehand that they are closed.

= Legendre formula

For a <prime number> $p$ and a positive <integer> $n$, the <P-adic valuation> of a <factorial> is

= Base-p digit sum
{title2=$d_p(n)$}
{parent=P-adic valuation}

If
$$
n=\sum_{r\geq0}\alpha_rp^r,
\qquad 0\leq\alpha_r<p,
$$
then the base-$p$ digit sum is $d_p(n)=\sum_r\alpha_r$. The <Legendre formula> can equivalently be written
$$
v_p(n!)=\frac{n-d_p(n)}{p-1}.
$$

= Subadditivity of the base-p digit sum
{parent=Base-p digit sum}

For nonnegative integers $x$ and $y$,
$$
d_p(x+y)\leq d_p(x)+d_p(y).
$$
In column addition, every carry replaces $p$ units in one digit by one unit in the next digit and therefore decreases the digit sum by $p-1$.

= Valuation of a near-central binomial coefficient
{parent=P-adic valuation}

If $k\geq1$ and $k+2\leq p\leq2k+1$ is prime, then
$$
v_p\left(\binom{2k+1}{k+1}\right)=1.
$$
Indeed, neither $(k+1)!$ nor $k!$ contains a factor $p$, while $(2k+1)!$ contains $p$ exactly once because $2p>2k+1$.

= Primorial

{wiki}

For real $X\geq1$, the primorial up to $X$ is
$$
P(X)=\prod_{p\leq X}p,
$$
where the product ranges over primes.

= Chebyshev theta function
{title2=$\vartheta(x)$}
{parent=Primorial}
{c}

The Chebyshev theta function is one of the two <Chebyshev functions> and is the <natural logarithm> of the <primorial>:
$$
\vartheta(x)=\sum_{p\leq x}\log p
=\log\prod_{p\leq x}p,
$$
where both the sum and the product range over <prime numbers>.

= Elementary lower bound for the Chebyshev theta function
{parent=Chebyshev theta function}

The <central binomial coefficient> and <Legendre formula> imply
$$
\vartheta(x)\geq\frac{x}{12}
$$
for every sufficiently large real number $x$.

= Elementary primorial bound
{parent=Primorial}

For every real $X\geq1$,
$$
P(X)\leq4^X.
$$
For integer $n$, strong induction proves this by splitting according to parity. If $n=2k$, every prime in $(k,2k]$ divides $\binom{2k}{k}$, so
$$
P(2k)\leq P(k)\binom{2k}{k}\leq4^k4^k.
$$
If $n=2k+1$, the <valuation of a near-central binomial coefficient> gives
$$
P(2k+1)\leq P(k+1)\binom{2k+1}{k+1}\leq4^{k+1}4^k.
$$
The binomial estimates follow from the binomial theorem; in the odd case the two equal central coefficients together are at most $2^{2k+1}$. The result for real $X$ follows by replacing $X$ with $\lfloor X\rfloor$.

= Decisive number
{parent=Number theory}

A positive <integer> $n$ is decisive when every <integer> $a$ satisfying $1<a<n$ and $\gcd(a,n)=1$ is a <prime number>. There are only finitely many decisive numbers: every prime $p<\sqrt n$ must divide a decisive $n$, so the <Chebyshev theta function> gives $\log n\geq\vartheta(\sqrt n)$, which eventually contradicts the linear lower bound for $\vartheta(\sqrt n)$.

= Prime-counting function
{parent=Number theory}
{wiki}

The prime-counting function $\pi(X)$ is the number of primes at most $X$.

= Legendre prime-counting formula
{parent=Prime-counting function}
{c}
{title2=$\pi(x)=\phi(x,\pi(\sqrt x))+\pi(\sqrt x)-1$}

For $a=\pi(\sqrt x)$, let $p_1,\ldots,p_a$ be the <primes> at most $\sqrt x$. The <inclusion-exclusion principle> counts unsieved positive <integers> by $\phi(x,a)=\sum_S(-1)^{|S|}\lfloor x/\prod_{i\in S}p_i\rfloor$. These are $1$ and the remaining <primes>, since every <composite number> has a <prime factor> at most its square root. Hence $\pi(x)=\phi(x,a)+a-1$ for $x\geq2$. This is a prime-counting identity, distinct from the <Legendre formula> for factorial valuations.

= Prime-counting upper bound from a primorial estimate
{parent=Prime-counting function}

If $P(X)\leq A^X$ for every $X\geq1$, where $A>1$, then
$$
\pi(X)\leq(1+2\log A)\frac{X}{\log X}
$$
for $X\geq2$. Every prime in $(\sqrt X,X]$ contributes at least $\sqrt X$ to the <primorial>, so
$$
(\sqrt X)^{\pi(X)-\pi(\sqrt X)}\leq P(X)\leq A^X.
$$
Taking logarithms, using $\pi(\sqrt X)\leq\sqrt X$, and observing that $\log X\leq\sqrt X$ for $X\geq2$ proves the claim.

= Square root of minus one modulo a prime

= Large-sieve logarithmic exceptional-prime bound for least quadratic nonresidues
{parent=Least quadratic nonresidue}
{title2=$\#\{p\leq N:n(p)>p^{0.01}\}\ll\log N$}

On a dyadic interval $Q<p\leq2Q$, every such exceptional prime makes all $Q^{0.01}$-smooth integers quadratic residues. By <positive density of power-smooth integers>, there are $\gg Q^2$ of these integers up to $Q^2$. Apply the <variance form of the large sieve> or its forbidden-residue consequence with interval length $Q^2$ and prime cutoff $2Q$. Each exceptional prime excludes $(p-1)/2$ residue classes and contributes at least a fixed positive amount to the sieve denominator. Hence each dyadic interval contains $O(1)$ exceptional primes. Summing the intervals gives the logarithmic bound.

= Bounded exceptional primes for least quadratic nonresidues
{parent=Least quadratic nonresidue}

If, for every $\eta>0$, the <smooth numbers> up to $T$ with all <prime factors> at most $T^\eta$ have density at least $\kappa(\eta)>0$, then for fixed $\varepsilon>0$ there are only $O_\varepsilon(1)$ <primes> $p\leq N$ with $n(p)>N^\varepsilon$. Apply the <variance form of the large sieve> to the <indicator function> of the $N^\varepsilon$-smooth numbers up to $N^2$. For every exceptional <prime>, all these numbers are nonzero <quadratic residues>, so half its nonzero <residue classes> are empty. Their variance contribution is at least one third of the square of the total count. The large sieve bounds the sum of contributions by a constant times $N^2$ times that count. The density assumption with $\eta=\varepsilon/2$ finishes the argument.

= Quadratic Gauss sum
{title2=$G(\chi)$}
{parent=Quadratic residue}
{c}
{wiki}

For $\omega=e^{2\pi i/p}$ and the <Legendre symbol> $\chi$, the quadratic Gauss sum is
$$
G(\chi)=\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}\chi(a)\omega^a.
$$
It equals $\sqrt p$ for $p\equiv1\pmod4$ and $i\sqrt p$ for $p\equiv3\pmod4$ with the standard choice of square root.

= Quadratic periods modulo eleven
{parent=Quadratic Gauss sum}

If $Q$ and $N$ are the nonzero square and nonsquare classes modulo eleven and $\omega=e^{2\pi i/11}$, then
$$
\sum_{q\in Q}\omega^q=\frac{-1+i\sqrt{11}}2,
\qquad
\sum_{n\in N}\omega^n=\frac{-1-i\sqrt{11}}2.
$$
Their sum is $-1$, and their difference is the <Quadratic Gauss sum> $i\sqrt{11}$.

= Legendre symbol

\left(\frac ap\right)=
\begin{cases}

= Multiplicativity of the Legendre symbol
{parent=Legendre symbol}

For an odd prime $p$,
$$
\left(\frac{ab}{p}\right)=
\left(\frac ap\right)\left(\frac bp\right).
$$
In particular, if $p\nmid a$, then $(a^{-1}/p)=(a/p)$.

= Complete Legendre-symbol sum
{parent=Legendre symbol}

For every odd prime $p$,
$$
\sum_{x\in\mathbb F_p}\left(\frac xp\right)=0.
$$
Multiplication by a quadratic nonresidue permutes $\mathbb F_p$ and negates every term.

= Quadratic Legendre-symbol correlation
{parent=Legendre symbol}

For every odd prime $p$,
$$
\sum_{x\in\mathbb F_p}\left(\frac{x(x+1)}p\right)=-1.
$$
Equivalently, count the $p-1$ solutions of $t^2=y^2-1$ by factoring $(y-t)(y+t)=1$.

\frac{p-4-(-1)^{(p-1)/2}}4.
$$

If $q_1,\ldots,q_r$ listed all primes congruent to seven modulo eight, then for $n=8q_1\cdots q_r$ the odd number $(n^2-2)/2$ is seven modulo eight. Every prime factor is one or seven modulo eight, at least one is seven, and none is among the $q_i$, a contradiction.

= Elementary logarithmic lower bound for primes in the quadratic-residue classes of two
{parent=Second supplementary law for quadratic reciprocity}

Let $\pi_k(x)$ count primes at most $x$ congruent to $k$ modulo eight. Bounding the prime-power divisibility of
$$
\prod_{\sqrt x/2\leq n\leq\sqrt x}(n^2-2)
$$
above and its size below gives, for all sufficiently large $x$,
$$
\pi_1(x)+\pi_7(x)+1\geq\frac{\log x}{6\log3}.
$$

= Prime-power binomial congruence for a matrix
{parent=Euler criterion}

For an integer matrix $A$ and prime $p$, the commuting-matrix binomial theorem and divisibility of $\binom pj$ for $0<j<p$ give
$$
(I+A)^p\equiv I+A^p\pmod p.
$$

= Weighted complete Legendre-symbol sum
{parent=Legendre symbol}

For $T=\sum_{a=1}^{p-1}a(\frac ap)$, one has $T=0$ when $p\equiv1\pmod4$, and $T\equiv0\pmod p$ for every $p>3$.

= Lower-half quadratic-residue sum for primes congruent to seven modulo eight
{parent=Legendre symbol}

If $p\equiv7\pmod8$, the sum of the least positive quadratic residues below $p/2$ is
$$
\frac{p^2-1}{16}.
$$
The proof uses $(\frac2p)=1$ to compare the sum of all residues with its image under doubling.

= Discriminant criterion for a quadratic congruence modulo an odd prime
{parent=Legendre symbol}

For $p\nmid a$, the congruence $aX^2+bX+c=0\pmod p$ has a solution exactly when its discriminant $b^2-4ac$ is zero or a quadratic residue modulo $p$.

= Primitive-root lifting criterion
{parent=Number theory}

If $g$ is primitive modulo an odd prime $p$ and $g^{p-1}\not\equiv1\pmod{p^2}$, then $g$ is primitive modulo every $p^k$.

= Jacobi reciprocity law
{c}
{parent=Jacobi symbol}

= Jacobi reciprocity
{c}
{synonym}

For coprime positive odd integers $a,b$, the <Jacobi symbol> satisfies $(a/b)(b/a)=(-1)^{(a-1)(b-1)/4}$. It follows by factoring $a$ and $b$ into primes and multiplying the <quadratic reciprocity> for each prime pair; the parity of the sums of prime half-differences agrees with $(a-1)/2$ and $(b-1)/2$. A <Jacobi symbol> equal to one does not imply that its numerator is a <quadratic residue> for a composite denominator.

= Infinitely many prime quadratic nonresidues of a nonsquare integer
{parent=Jacobi reciprocity law}

For an odd positive nonsquare integer $a$, there are infinitely many <primes> $p$ with <Legendre symbol> $(a/p)=-1$. Choose a residue $n$ modulo $a$ with <Jacobi symbol> $(n/a)=-1$ by making it a <quadratic nonresidue> at one prime occurring to odd multiplicity and a square at every other prime. The <Chinese remainder theorem> also imposes $n\equiv1\pmod4$ and $n\equiv1$ at any finite list of previously found primes. Then <Jacobi reciprocity> gives $(a/n)=-1$, so a prime factor of $n$ has $(a/p)=-1$ and is outside that list.

= Multiplicativity of the Jacobi symbol
{parent=Jacobi symbol}

For odd positive $n$, the <Jacobi symbol> is multiplicative in its numerator:
$$
\left(\frac{ab}{n}\right)=\left(\frac an\right)\left(\frac bn\right).
$$
It is also multiplicative in an odd denominator: $(a/mn)=(a/m)(a/n)$.

= Quadratic reciprocity
{parent=Number theory}
{wiki}

For distinct odd primes, $(p/q)(q/p)=(-1)^{(p-1)(q-1)/4}$.

= Quadratic character of minus three
{parent=Quadratic reciprocity}

For an odd prime $p\ne3$, quadratic reciprocity gives
$$
\left(\frac{-3}{p}\right)=\left(\frac p3\right).
$$
Thus $-3$ is a square modulo $p$ exactly when $p\equiv1\pmod3$.

= Cube roots of minus one modulo an odd prime
{parent=Quadratic character of minus three}

The factorization
$$
X^3+1=(X+1)(X^2-X+1)
$$
shows that $X^3=-1$ always has $X=-1$ as a solution. For $p\ne3$, it has two further solutions exactly when $-3$ is a quadratic residue; for $p=3$, all three factors coalesce at the unique root $-1$.

= Proof of Korselt criterion
{parent=Korselt criterion}

If every integer satisfies $a^n\equiv a\pmod n$, taking $a=p$ shows that $p^2$ cannot divide $n$. Taking a <primitive root> modulo each prime divisor $p$ shows $p-1\mid n-1$. Conversely, these divisibilities and squarefreeness let <Fermat's little theorem> prove the congruence modulo each prime divisor, and coprimality of those divisors combines it modulo $n$.

= Smooth number

= Positive density of power-smooth integers
{parent=Smooth number}
{title2=$\#\{n\leq H:P^+(n)\leq H^\eta\}\gg_\eta H$}

Fix $\eta=1/k$ with $k\geq2$. Choose $k$ primes in $[H^{\eta-\eta^2},H^\eta]$, repetitions allowed, and an integer cofactor $m$ so that $m p_1\cdots p_k\leq H$. The product is between $H^{1-\eta}$ and $H$, so $m\leq H^\eta$ and all resulting integers are $H^\eta$-smooth. The <Mertens theorem for reciprocal primes> makes the reciprocal prime sum tend to $-\log(1-\eta)>0$. Since $\lfloor H/(p_1\cdots p_k)\rfloor\geq H/(2p_1\cdots p_k)$, there are $\gg_\eta H$ ordered representations. Each integer has only $O_\eta(1)$ prime factors in that interval, counted with multiplicity, so representation multiplicities are bounded by a constant depending on $k$. This proves the density bound. For arbitrary fixed positive $\eta$, choose a smaller reciprocal-integer exponent.

= Diophantine equation
{parent=Number theory}
{wiki}

A Diophantine equation is a polynomial equation for which integer solutions are sought. Congruences, factorization, and size bounds often prove that no solution exists.

= Schinzel-Tijdeman exponent theorem
{c}
{parent=Diophantine equation}
{title2=$y^m=f(x),\quad |y|>1\Longrightarrow m\le C(f)$}

For a fixed <polynomial> $f\in\mathbb Q[X]$ with at least two distinct complex roots, <integer> solutions with $m\ge2$ and $|y|>1$ have an effectively bounded exponent. Factorization over a <splitting field> and the fixed differences between distinct roots control common <ideal> <divisors>. Outside a fixed set of primes, each factor's valuation is divisible by $m/\gcd(m,e_i)$, where $e_i$ is its root <multiplicity>. The generalized power-equation estimates from <linear forms in logarithms of algebraic numbers>, including <unit> and exceptional-prime factors, give the exponent bound. A single-root <polynomial> and the values $y=0,\pm1$ cannot be included: $4^m=(2^m)^2$, and $1^m=f(0)$ for $f(X)=X(X-1)+1$, give infinite exponent families.

= Local solubility
{parent=Diophantine equation}

= Locally soluble
{synonym}

A <Diophantine equation> is soluble at a place of a <number field> if it has a solution over the corresponding <completion of a valued field>. It is locally soluble everywhere if this holds at all finite and infinite places. Over the <rational numbers>, these are the real field and all <p-adic fields>. Local solubility is necessary for a <rational point>, but the <local-to-global gap in isogeny descent> shows it need not suffice.

= Fermat last theorem
{c}
{parent=Diophantine equation}
{title2=$x^n+y^n=z^n,\qquad n>2$}

= Fermat's last theorem
{c}
{synonym}

There are no positive <integer> solutions to the displayed equation when the exponent is an <integer> greater than two. Homogeneity means that any hypothetical solution would give infinitely many common multiples; statements bounding solutions by arithmetic height must therefore first distinguish primitive solutions from their multiples.

= abc conjecture
{c}
{parent=Diophantine equation}
{title2=$C\leq K_\epsilon\operatorname{rad}(ABC)^{1+\epsilon}$}

For every $\epsilon>0$ there is a constant $K_\epsilon$ such that the displayed inequality holds for coprime positive integers $A+B=C$. The radical is the product of the distinct primes dividing the argument. Applying it to a primitive Fermat triple bounds the exponent and the triple simultaneously. For $ax^n-by^n=c\ne0$, division by the gcd, which divides $c$, gives a uniform bound on $\max(x,y)^{n-2(1+\epsilon)}$. Finiteness in the varying exponent requires excluding $x=y=1$ when $a-b=c$.

= Catalan power bound from abc
{c}
{parent=abc conjecture}
{title2=$x^p-y^q=1,\quad p,q\ge3\Longrightarrow x^p\le C_{1/6}^{9/2}$}

Put $M=x^p$ for positive bases $x,y\ge2$. The <coprime> triple $(y^q,1,x^p)$ has radical at most $xy<M^{2/3}$. The <abc conjecture> with $\epsilon=1/6$ gives $M\le C_{1/6}M^{7/9}$, proving the bound. Both bases and both exponents are then bounded, since $2^p\le M$ and $2^q\le M-1$. Unlike a homogeneous Fermat equation, no common-scaling ambiguity occurs.

= Primitive Fermat bound from abc
{parent=abc conjecture}
{title2=$z^{n-7/2}\le C_{1/6}$}

For a primitive positive solution of $x^n+y^n=z^n$ with $n\ge4$, the three powers are pairwise <coprime> and their radical is at most $xyz<z^3$. The <abc conjecture> with $\epsilon=1/6$ gives the displayed inequality, hence $z\le C_{1/6}^2$ uniformly in $n$. The inequality $z^n\le2\max(x,y)^n$ then bounds $n$. Thus there are finitely many primitive triples and exponents. Homogeneity would produce infinitely many unscaled solutions from any one nonzero solution, so a primitive <arithmetic height> bound alone must not be described as finite many arbitrary multiples.

= Thue theorem
{c}
{parent=Diophantine equation}
{title2=$F(x,y)=m$}

An irreducible integral binary form of degree at least three has only finitely many integral solutions at a fixed nonzero value. Factor the form in a splitting field. Each linear factor generates an ideal dividing a fixed ideal, so it is one of finitely many chosen factors times a unit. The linear relation among any three distinct factors gives a <unit equation over a number field>, hence finitely many ratios and finitely many rational directions. Homogeneity and the fixed nonzero value then bound the scale on each direction. The same argument works for a binary form with at least three distinct projective roots, even without irreducibility.

= Unit equation over a number field
{parent=Diophantine equation}
{title2=$\alpha u+\beta v=1,\qquad u,v\in\mathcal O_K^\times$}

For fixed nonzero $\alpha,\beta$ in a <number field>, this equation has finitely many unit solutions. By the <Dirichlet unit theorem>, a large unit-exponent vector makes one unit exponentially small at some archimedean embedding. The equation makes the other fixed multiple exponentially close to one. Its principal logarithm is a nonzero linear form in fixed algebraic logarithms, with coefficients bounded by the exponent size; a polynomial lower bound contradicts the exponential upper bound. Finite torsion and finitely many embeddings allow uniform constants.

= Infinite descent
{parent=Diophantine equation}
{wiki}

An infinite descent rules out a nonempty set of positive integer solutions by constructing from any solution another with a strictly smaller positive integer measure. Choosing a solution of minimal measure gives a contradiction. Common measures are a hypotenuse, a denominator, or the size of a primitive solution.

= Fourth-power sum cannot be a square
{parent=Infinite descent}
{title2=$x^4+y^4\ne z^2\quad(x,y,z\in\mathbb Z_{>0})$}

Choose a primitive counterexample with minimal $z$ and $y$ even. Its <Pythagorean triple> $(x^2,y^2,z)$ has $x^2=m^2-n^2$, $y^2=2mn$. Modulo four forces $m$ odd and $n$ even, so $m=u^2$ and $n=2v^2$. The primitive triangle $(x,2v^2,u^2)$ then has a parametrization with $v^2=rs$ and $u^2=r^2+s^2$. Coprimality makes $r=a^2,s=b^2$, giving $a^4+b^4=u^2$ with $u<z$. This contradicts <infinite descent>.

= Diophantine approximation
{parent=Number theory}
{wiki}

Diophantine approximation studies how closely numbers can be approximated by <rational number>[rational] or <algebraic number>[algebraic numbers] subject to bounds on their arithmetic complexity.

= Transcendence theory
{parent=Diophantine approximation}

Transcendence theory proves that particular numbers are not <algebraic numbers>, or that several numbers satisfy no nonzero polynomial relation with algebraic coefficients. A recurring method constructs an auxiliary expression which is simultaneously too small analytically and too large arithmetically if the proposed values were algebraic.

= Schneider-Lang theorem
{c}
{parent=Transcendence theory}
{title2=$|S|\le[K:\mathbb Q](\rho_1+\rho_2)$}

Let $f_1,\ldots,f_s$ be meromorphic functions with $f_j'\in K[f_1,\ldots,f_s]$ for a fixed <number field> $K$. Suppose $f_1,f_2$ are algebraically independent over $\mathbb C$ and have finite orders $\rho_1,\rho_2$. The set $S$ of points where every $f_j$ is finite and belongs to $K$ satisfies the displayed bound. The orders are growth orders for meromorphic functions. For $f_1=z$, $f_2=e^z$, the orders are zero and one and the functions are algebraically independent; an algebraic value of $e^\alpha$ at nonzero algebraic $\alpha$ would produce infinitely many such points $n\alpha$.

= Symmetric Hermite integral obstruction
{parent=Transcendence theory}
{title2=$k+\sum_jb_je^{\beta_j}\ne0$}

Suppose nonzero <algebraic numbers> $\beta_j$ form a Galois-stable set, with Galois-invariant <integer> weights $b_j$, and $k\ne0$ is an <integer>. Choose $P\in\mathbb Z[X]$ vanishing at all $\beta_j$ with $P(0)\ne0$, and a positive <integer> $c$ making all $c\beta_j$ <algebraic integers>. For a large prime $p$, put $D=(1+\deg P)p-1$ and $f_p(X)=c^DX^{p-1}P(X)^p/(p-1)!$, and let $F_p$ be the sum of its derivatives. The weighted sum $kF_p(0)+\sum b_jF_p(\beta_j)$ is an <integer>, nonzero modulo $p$ when $p\nmid kcP(0)$. If the displayed exponential expression vanished, integrating $(e^{-z}F_p(z))'=-e^{-z}f_p(z)$ along the finitely many segments to $\beta_j$ would bound that nonzero <integer> by $C^p/(p-1)!\to0$. This contradiction proves the obstruction and supplies direct proofs for $e$ and $\pi$.

= Gelfond–Schneider theorem
{c}
{parent=Transcendence theory}

For algebraic $a\ne0,1$ and algebraic irrational $b$, every value $\exp(b\lambda)$ with $e^\lambda=a$ is transcendental. Consequently any quotient of chosen nonzero logarithms of algebraic numbers is either rational or transcendental.

= Schneider lattice proof of the Gelfond–Schneider theorem
{c}
{parent=Gelfond–Schneider theorem}

Assume $a$ and $a^b$ algebraic with algebraic irrational $b$. Apply <Siegel lemma> to construct a small-coefficient polynomial $P$ vanishing at the algebraic pairs $(s+tb,a^s(a^b)^t)$ on a large square grid. The function $P(z,e^{\lambda z})$ is a nonzero exponential polynomial. Its many initial zeros make later grid values exponentially tiny, whereas an algebraic norm estimate forbids such tiny nonzero values. Iterating extends the zero grid indefinitely. Its quadratic zero count in disks contradicts the linear zero-count growth of an exponential polynomial.

= Mahler method
{c}
{parent=Transcendence theory}

Mahler's method uses functional equations relating a function at $z$ and at $z^l$ to transport an auxiliary polynomial to points rapidly approaching zero. High vanishing order gives an upper bound which conflicts with an algebraic norm or height lower bound under assumed algebraicity of the values. Functional transcendence ensures that the auxiliary expression is not identically zero.

= Fredholm lacunary series
{c}
{parent=Mahler method}
{title2=$f_l(z)=\sum_{n\geq0}z^{l^n},\qquad l\geq2$}

= Fredholm series
{c}
{synonym}

This lacunary series is analytic for $|z|<1$ and satisfies $f_l(z^l)=f_l(z)-z$. At roots of unity of $l$-power order, its radial tail diverges, giving a dense set of singularities on the unit circle and excluding algebraicity as a function. The <Mahler method> proves transcendence of its values at every algebraic $z$ with $0<|z|<1$. At $z=1/2$, reduced truncations have denominators $2^{l^m}$ and error less than twice their $-l$th power; for $l\geq3$, the <Roth theorem> gives a shorter proof.

= Roth criterion for rational Fredholm values
{c}
{parent=Fredholm lacunary series}
{title2=$l(1-\log p/\log q)>2$}

For $0<p<q$, truncating the <Fredholm series> at $n$ gives a rational with denominator at most $Q_n=q^{l^n}$ and positive error at most $2Q_n^{-l(1-\log p/\log q)}$ for large $n$. The displayed strict inequality first rules out a rational value by the rational separation bound, and then rules out an algebraic irrational value by <Roth theorem>. Reduced denominators are at most $Q_n$ and tend to infinity because the truncations are distinct. In particular $l\ge3$ and $p<q^\delta$, $\delta<1/3$, imply transcendence.

= Auxiliary-value height argument for the Fredholm series
{parent=Fredholm lacunary series}
{title2=$P_N(z,f_l(z))=O(z^{T_N}),\qquad T_N\ge(N+1)^2-1$}

A nonzero <integer> <polynomial> of bidegree at most $N$ can cancel the first $(N+1)^2-1$ Taylor coefficients of the <Fredholm series>. Functional transcendence makes the resulting <analytic function> nonzero. If $\alpha$ and $f_l(\alpha)$ were both algebraic, the relation $f_l(\alpha^{l^r})=f_l(\alpha)-\sum_{j<r}\alpha^{l^j}$ would keep the auxiliary values in one fixed <number field>. Their <absolute logarithmic Weil height> is at most $N l^r h(\alpha)(1+1/(l-1))+O_N(r+1)$, whereas their chosen absolute value is $O_N(|\alpha|^{T_Nl^r})$. The <Liouville height inequality> forces $T_N=O(N)$, contradicting its quadratic lower bound for sufficiently large $N$.

= E-function
{c}
{parent=Transcendence theory}
{title2=$f(z)=\sum_{n\geq0}a_nz^n/n!$}

In the modern strict sense an E-function satisfies a nonzero linear differential equation over $\overline{\mathbb Q}(z)$, has algebraic coefficients $a_n$ with all conjugates bounded by $C^{n+1}$, and has common positive denominators $d_n\leq C^{n+1}$ making $d_na_0,\ldots,d_na_n$ algebraic integers. Siegel's original arithmetic growth condition permits, for each $\epsilon>0$, bounds of size $O_\epsilon(n^{\epsilon n})$ instead. The strict and original growth formulations should be distinguished rather than asserted equivalent. Exponentials with algebraic exponent satisfy the stronger conditions and a first-order differential equation.

= Addition closure of Siegel E-functions
{parent=E-function}
{title2=$f,g\in\mathcal E\Longrightarrow f+g\in\mathcal E$}

Work in the <compositum> of the two coefficient fields. Conjugate coefficient bounds add, while multiplying the common denominators clears both coefficient lists. For Siegel's original bounds $O_\epsilon(n^{\epsilon n})$, use $\epsilon/2$ in each denominator estimate. A nonzero <linear differential equation> for the sum follows because its derivatives lie in the sum of the two finite-dimensional derivative spaces over the <rational function> <field>. The stricter exponential arithmetic bounds are preserved as well.

= Siegel–Shidlovsky theorem
{c}
{parent=E-function}

= Siegel–Shidlovskii theorem
{c}
{synonym}

Let a vector of <E-functions> satisfy $f'=A(z)f$ with $A\in\operatorname{Mat}_s(\overline{\mathbb Q}(z))$. At a nonzero algebraic point $\xi$ which is not a pole of $A$, the transcendence degree of the values over $\overline{\mathbb Q}$ equals the transcendence degree of the functions over $\overline{\mathbb Q}(z)$. In particular algebraically independent functions give algebraically independent values. The exponential system $f_j=e^{\gamma_jz}$, for rationally independent algebraic $\gamma_j$, recovers the <Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem> by evaluating at one.

= Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem
{c}
{parent=Transcendence theory}

For distinct <algebraic numbers> $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_s$, the numbers $e^{\alpha_1},\ldots,e^{\alpha_s}$ are linearly independent over $\overline{\mathbb Q}$. Equivalently, exponentials of numbers linearly independent over $\mathbb Q$ are algebraically independent over $\overline{\mathbb Q}$. The special case of a nonzero algebraic exponent gives a transcendental exponential. In particular $e^{i\pi}=-1$ forces $\pi$ to be transcendental.

= Hermite auxiliary-polynomial proof for pi
{c}
{parent=Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem}

Under an assumed algebraicity of $i\pi$, form the product $\prod_j(1+e^{\alpha_j})$ over its conjugates; its one known vanishing factor makes the whole product zero and regroup the subset sums as $k+\sum b_je^{\beta_j}=0$, with nonzero exponents and Galois-invariant integral weights. After clearing their denominators, use $f_p(z)=(cz)^{p-1}\prod_j(cz-c\beta_j)^p/(p-1)!$. The sum of its derivatives at each $\beta_j$ is divisible by $p$ in the algebraic integers, whereas at zero its first nonzero derivative is a fixed nonzero residue for all but finitely many primes. Summing with the weights gives a nonzero rational integer. Integrating the derivative of $e^{-z}\sum f_p^{(j)}(z)$ expresses that integer as a quantity bounded by $C^p/(p-1)!$, which tends to zero. This is the contradiction.

= Badly approximable number
{parent=Diophantine approximation}
{title2=$\inf_{q\geq1}q\|q\alpha\|>0$}

An irrational real number is badly approximable when $\|q\alpha\|\geq c/q$ for some $c>0$ and every positive integer $q$, where the double bars mean distance to the nearest integer. Equivalently every rational approximation obeys $|\alpha-a/q|\geq c/q^2$. The <Diophantine bound for the square root of two> gives a concrete example and supplies separated rotations for <bilinear cancellation for badly approximable phases>.

= Diophantine bound for the square root of two
{c}
{parent=Badly approximable number}
{title2=$\|q\sqrt2\|\geq1/(4q)$}

For the nearest integer $a$ to $q\sqrt2$, the nonzero integer $2q^2-a^2$ has absolute value at least one. Dividing by $q\sqrt2+a\leq4q$ proves the bound. Thus the points $0,\sqrt2,\ldots,W\sqrt2$ modulo one are separated by at least $1/(4W)$. Ordering their distances from zero gives $\sum_{h\leq W}\|h\sqrt2\|^{-1}\ll W\log(2W)$.

= Diophantine frequency vector
{parent=Diophantine approximation}
{c}
{title2=$|k\cdot\omega|\ge\gamma |k|_1^{-\tau}$}

= Diophantine condition
{synonym}
{c}

A vector $\omega\in\mathbb R^n$ satisfying $|k\cdot\omega|\ge\gamma |k|_1^{-\tau}$ for all nonzero integer vectors $k$, with $\gamma>0$. This excludes resonances and controls the <small divisors> in analytic <cohomological equations on a Diophantine torus>. Different choices of norm change the constant but not the nature of the condition.

= Small divisor
{parent=Diophantine frequency vector}
{title2=$k\cdot\omega$}

A denominator such as $k\cdot\omega$ which may become small for large integer vectors $k$. Inverting a directional derivative on a <torus> divides the nonzero <Fourier coefficients> by $i k\cdot\omega$. A <Diophantine condition> bounds this loss polynomially, so exponential Fourier decay still gives convergence on a smaller complex strip.

= Quantitative quadratic recurrence
{parent=Diophantine approximation}
{title2=$\|\alpha n^2\|<\varepsilon$}

For a universal constant $C>0$ and $0<\varepsilon\leq1$, every real $\alpha$ admits $1\leq n\leq\varepsilon^{-C}$ with <distance to the nearest integer> of $\alpha n^2$ less than $\varepsilon$. A <Fejér kernel> detects failure of recurrence as a large <quadratic exponential sum>. The <Van der Corput inequality for finite scalar sequences> then produces a short linear near-return, whose suitable multiple gives the quadratic return. To include $\varepsilon>1$, use the bound $\max\{1,\varepsilon^{-C}\}$.

= Distance to the nearest integer
{parent=Diophantine approximation}
{title2=$\|x\|_{\mathbb R/\mathbb Z}$}

= Distance modulo one
{synonym}

For a <real number> $x$, define $\|x\|_{\mathbb R/\mathbb Z}=\min_{k\in\mathbb Z}|x-k|$. It lies in $[0,1/2]$ and obeys $\|\ell x\|\leq|\ell|\|x\|$ for an <integer> $\ell$. It is a distance on the circle, rather than a <norm> on the real vector space.

= Separated reciprocal-distance sum
{parent=Distance to the nearest integer}
{title2=$\sum_j\min(Q,(2\|\theta_j\|)^{-1})\ll Q+\delta^{-1}\log(2Q)$}

If finitely many points on $\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$ are pairwise separated by <distance modulo one> at least $\delta>0$, then the displayed bound holds for $Q\ge2$. An arc of length $2r$ contains at most $1+2r/\delta$ points. First sum over $\|\theta_j\|\le Q^{-1}$, then over the dyadic annuli $2^j/Q<\|\theta_j\|\le2^{j+1}/Q$. Their contributions are $O(Q+\delta^{-1})$ and $O(Q2^{-j}+\delta^{-1})$, respectively. Summing proves the estimate. A point at zero contributes $Q$, using the convention $\min(Q,\infty)=Q$.

= General lower bound for a linear form in logarithms
{parent=Linear form in logarithms of algebraic numbers}

Choose logarithms of nonzero algebraic numbers $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n$ and algebraic numbers $\beta_0,\ldots,\beta_n$, and put
$$
\Lambda=\beta_0+\sum_{j=1}^n\beta_j\log\alpha_j.
$$
Let $A_j$ bound $10$, the <naive polynomial height> of the minimal polynomial of $\alpha_j$, and $\exp(|\log\alpha_j|)$. Let $B$ bound all the corresponding heights of the $\beta_j$ and all $\log A_j$. There is an effective constant $C$, depending only on $n$ and the degree of the number field generated by the data, such that
$$
\Lambda\ne0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
|\Lambda|>\exp\!\left(-C\prod_{j=1}^n\log A_j\,\log B\right).
$$

= Baker lower bound for a homogeneous linear form in logarithms
{c}
{parent=Linear form in logarithms of algebraic numbers}

This quantitative refinement belongs to the theory of <Baker's theorem>; its dependence on the scaled coefficient bound is more specific than that broad theorem.

Choose logarithms of nonzero algebraic numbers $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n$, put
$$
\Lambda=\sum_{i=1}^n b_i\log\alpha_i,
\qquad b_i\in\mathbb Z,
$$
and choose $A_i\geq10$ large enough to bound the <naive polynomial height> of the minimal polynomial of $\alpha_i$ and $\exp(|\log\alpha_i|)$. After relabelling the terms if useful, set
$$
B^*=\max\left(\frac{|b_1|}{\log A_n},\ldots,
\frac{|b_{n-1}|}{\log A_n},|b_n|,10\right).
$$
There is an effectively computable constant $C$, depending only on $n$ and the degree of the number field generated by the $\alpha_i$, such that
$$
\Lambda\ne0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
|\Lambda|>\exp\!\left(-C(\log A_1)\cdots(\log A_n)\log B^*\right).
$$

The division by $\log A_n$ is the useful refinement over the <general lower bound for a linear form in logarithms> when one algebraic number has a large height correlated with a coefficient.

= Exponent bound for a binomial power equation
{parent=Baker lower bound for a homogeneous linear form in logarithms}
{title2=$ax^n-by^n=c\ne0,\qquad\max(x,y)>1$}

For fixed positive $a,b$ and fixed nonzero $c$, with $x\ne y$, put $Y=\max(x,y)$ and take $\Lambda=\log(a/b)+n\log(x/y)$, swapping the variables and coefficients if necessary. The equation gives $\log|\Lambda|\leq-n\log Y+O(1)$ when the larger-variable power is sufficiently large. A two-logarithm lower bound gives $\log|\Lambda|\geq-C(a,b)\log Y\log(2n)$. Hence $n\leq C\log(2n)+O(1)$, so $n$ is bounded. The cases $x=y$ and a bounded larger-variable power are elementary. For each remaining exponent, the <Thue theorem> for a binomial form supplies finitely many pairs. The constant-power exception $x=y=1$, $a-b=c$, must be removed.

= Effective norm-form height estimate
{parent=Baker lower bound for a homogeneous linear form in logarithms}

Let $L$ be a fixed <number field> and let $V\subset L$ be a fixed rational linear subspace for which the associated <norm form> has no unit-family degeneracy. There are effective constants $A,C>0$ such that every $z\in V\cap\mathcal O_L$ with nonzero <field norm> $m$ satisfies
$$
H(z)\leq C|m|^A.
$$
To prove this, factor the principal ideal $(z)$, write its generators as a bounded factor times powers of fundamental units, use the linear relations defining $V$, and apply the <Baker lower bound for a homogeneous linear form in logarithms> to bound the unit exponents by $O(\log|m|)$. For $L=\mathbb Q(\alpha)K$ with $[\mathbb Q(\alpha):\mathbb Q]=3$, $[K:\mathbb Q]=2$, and $V=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb Q}\{1,\alpha,\omega\}$ for a basis $1,\omega$ of $K$, the coprime degrees rule out the degeneracy.

= Siegel's lemma
{c}
{parent=Diophantine approximation}
{wiki}

= Siegel lemma
{c}
{synonym}

Let $K$ be a <number field> of degree $D$, and let $L_1,\ldots,L_M$ be <linear form>[linear forms] in $N>DM$ variables with coefficients in $K$ and <projective height> at most $\mathcal H\geq1$. There is a nonzero $\mathbf x\in\mathbb Z^N$ satisfying every $L_j(\mathbf x)=0$ and
$$
\lVert\mathbf x\rVert_\infty
\leq(N\mathcal H)^{DM/(N-DM)}.
$$
For $K=\mathbb Q$, this follows by applying the <pigeonhole principle> to the images of the integer box $\{0,\ldots,Y\}^N$ under $(L_1,\ldots,L_M)$. Expanding coefficients in a rational basis of $K$ gives the factor $D$ in the general count.

= Pigeonhole proof of integer Siegel lemma
{parent=Siegel's lemma}
{title2=$0<\|z\|_\infty\leq\lfloor(2NA)^{M/(N-M)}\rfloor$}

For $M<N$ homogeneous equations with integer coefficients of absolute value at most $A\geq1$, map the box $\{0,\ldots,Q\}^N$ to its $M$ equation values. There are $(Q+1)^N$ inputs and at most $(2NAQ+1)^M$ outputs. Taking $Q=\lfloor(2NA)^{M/(N-M)}\rfloor$ makes the first count larger, since $2NAQ+1\leq2NA(Q+1)$. The difference of two inputs with the same output is the required nonzero bounded integer solution. Clearing denominators also treats rational equations; expanding a number-field basis replaces each field equation by finitely many rational equations.

= Normalized derivative of a polynomial
{title2=$D_jP$}
{parent=Siegel's lemma}

The $j$th normalized derivative is
$$
D_jP(X)=\frac1{j!}\frac{d^jP}{dX^j}(X).
$$
If $P(X)=\sum_ra_rX^r$ has integer coefficients, then $D_jP(X)=\sum_{r\geq j}\binom rj a_rX^{r-j}$ also has integer coefficients. For $\deg P\leq n$, its <naive polynomial height> is at most $2^nH(P)$.

= Thue-Siegel auxiliary polynomial
{c}
{parent=Siegel's lemma}

For an algebraic number $\alpha$ of degree $d$ and a small $\kappa>0$, <Siegel lemma> constructs integer polynomials $P,Q$ of degree at most $n$ and exponentially bounded coefficients for which $P(X)+\alpha Q(X)$ has a zero at $X=\alpha$ of order close to $2n/d$. A <Wronskian> then prevents every specialization $P(X)+yQ(X)$ from having a high-order zero at a rational point. This is the auxiliary-polynomial mechanism in the Thue-Siegel method.

= Rational multiplicity bound for a linear auxiliary polynomial
{parent=Thue-Siegel auxiliary polynomial}

Let $F(X,Y)=P(X)+YQ(X)$ with $P,Q\in\mathbb Z[X]$ linearly independent, $\deg_XF\leq n$, and $H(F)\leq C^n$. For every $\delta>0$, if a reduced rational $p/q$ has sufficiently large denominator in terms of $C$ and $\delta$, then $F(X,y)$ has multiplicity at most $\delta n+1$ at $p/q$, for every fixed real $y$.

Indeed, the nonzero <Wronskian>
$$
W=PQ'-P'Q
$$
has degree below $2n$ and height at most $C_0^n$. A zero of multiplicity $r$ of $F(X,y)$ forces a zero of multiplicity at least $r-1$ of $W$. By <Gauss lemma for polynomials>, $(qX-p)^{r-1}$ then divides $W$ in $\mathbb Z[X]$, so $q^{r-1}$ divides the leading coefficient of $W$ and is at most $C_0^n$.

= Thue-Siegel rational approximation bound
{c}
{parent=Thue-Siegel auxiliary polynomial}

If a real algebraic number $\alpha$ has degree $d\geq3$, then for every $\varepsilon>0$ there are only finitely many reduced fractions $p/q$ such that
$$
\left|\alpha-\frac pq\right|<q^{-d/2-1-\varepsilon}.
$$
The <Thue-Siegel auxiliary polynomial> proof chooses two such approximations, constructs an auxiliary polynomial nonzero at their pair, and compares its denominator lower bound with the upper bound supplied by its high-order zero at $(\alpha,\alpha)$.

= Negative continued fraction
{parent=Continued fraction}
{title2=$[a_1,\ldots,a_r]^-$}

The finite negative continued fraction is

[a_1,\ldots,a_r]^-=a_1-\frac1{a_2-\frac1{\cdots-1/a_r}}.

For $a_i\ge2$, it describes a negative-framed chain of unknots: the diagonal framings are $-a_i$ and adjacent linking numbers are one. = Natural extension of the continued-fraction Gauss map
{parent=Gauss continued-fraction map}
{title2=$F(x,y)=(\{1/x\},(\lfloor1/x\rfloor+y)^{-1})$}

On each branch with digit $a=\lfloor1/x\rfloor$, the displayed extension of the <Gauss continued-fraction map> is invertible almost everywhere: $x=(a+x')^{-1}$ and $y=(y')^{-1}-a$, with $a=\lfloor1/y'\rfloor$. = Bounded distortion of continued-fraction cylinders
{parent=Gauss continued-fraction map}
{title2=$h(y)=(p_n+p_{n-1}y)/(q_n+q_{n-1}y)$}

The inverse branch of the $n$th iterate of the <Gauss continued-fraction map> on a fixed digit cylinder has the displayed form, with <continued fraction convergents> $p_j/q_j$. = Small norm residues from square-root continued fractions
{parent=Continued fraction convergent}

For nonsquare $N>1$, write the complete quotients after the initial term as $(\sqrt N+m)/d$. They exceed one, while their algebraic conjugates lie between minus one and zero. = Reversal identity for a finite continued fraction

= Positive generalized continued fraction
{parent=Continued fraction}

For positive $a_j,b_j$, define $p_0=a_0$, $p_{-1}=1$, $q_0=1$, $q_{-1}=0$ and
$$
p_j=a_jp_{j-1}+b_{j-1}p_{j-2},
\qquad

and $p_n/q_n$ is the corresponding finite generalized continued fraction.

= Convergence of a positive simple continued fraction
{parent=Positive generalized continued fraction}

When $b_j=1$ and $a_j$ are positive integers, adjacent convergents satisfy
$$
p_nq_{n-1}-q_np_{n-1}=(-1)^{n+1}.
$$
Even and odd convergents are monotone from opposite sides, their separation is $1/(q_nq_{n+1})$, and $q_n\to\infty$. They therefore have a common limit $x$, with
$$
\left|\frac{p_n}{q_n}-x\right|
+\left|\frac{p_{n+1}}{q_{n+1}}-x\right|
=\frac1{q_nq_{n+1}}.
$$

= All-one continued fraction and Fibonacci ratios

If every $a_j=b_j=1$, then $p_n=F_{n+2}$ and $q_n=F_{n+1}$. Binet's formula

\qquad
\phi=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2},
\quad
\psi=-\phi^{-1},
$$
shows that the convergents tend to $\phi$ and gives sharp scaled errors through $F_{n+1}-\phi F_n=\psi^n$.

= Liouville approximation theorem
{c}
{parent=Diophantine approximation}

If an irrational algebraic number has degree $d$, then $|\alpha-p/q|>C(\alpha)q^{-d}$. Approximations of arbitrarily larger polynomial order therefore prove that a <Liouville number> is transcendental.

= Unbounded continued-fraction coefficients exclude quadratic irrationality
{parent=Liouville approximation theorem}

For an irrational $x$ with <continued fraction convergents> $p_n/q_n$, one has $|x-p_n/q_n|\leq1/(q_nq_{n+1})\leq1/(a_{n+1}q_n^2)$. The <Liouville approximation theorem> for a <quadratic irrational> gives a positive lower bound $C/q_n^2$ instead. Thus unbounded partial quotients preclude quadratic irrationality, and in particular imply $x^2$ is irrational.

= Proof of Liouville approximation theorem
{parent=Liouville approximation theorem}

For the degree-$d$ minimal polynomial $P\in\mathbb Z[X]$ of $\alpha$, the nonzero integer $q^dP(p/q)$ gives $|P(p/q)|\geq q^{-d}$. On a fixed neighbourhood of $\alpha$, the mean value theorem bounds
$$
|P(p/q)|\leq\max|P'|\,|p/q-\alpha|.
$$
Outside that neighbourhood the desired lower bound is immediate, giving $|\alpha-p/q|>c(\alpha)q^{-d}$ after decreasing the constant.

= Separation of distinct reduced fractions
{parent=Liouville approximation theorem}

For distinct reduced fractions $p/q$ and $u/v$,
$$
\left|\frac pq-\frac uv\right|
=\frac{|pv-qu|}{qv}\geq\frac1{qv}.
$$
If $q>v$, this is strictly greater than $q^{-2}$.

= Transcendental subseries of a positive rational convergent series
{parent=Liouville approximation theorem}

Every convergent series of positive rational terms has a subseries whose rational partial sums $p_j/q_j$ satisfy
$$
0<\alpha-\frac{p_j}{q_j}<2^{-j}q_j^{-j}.
$$
Choose each new term small enough to enforce all previous tail bounds. Separation of reduced fractions first makes $\alpha$ irrational, and the <Liouville approximation theorem> then rules out every finite algebraic degree.

= Primes represented by x squared plus three y squared

= Prime representation via an ideal class
{parent=Binary quadratic form}
{title2=$q_I(x,y)=N(xe_1+ye_2)/N(I)$}

Let $I$ be an <integral ideal> of an imaginary quadratic <number field> and $(e_1,e_2)$ an <integral basis> of $I$. Its normalized norm is a positive definite <binary quadratic form>. An unramified rational prime $p$ is represented by this form exactly when there is an integral <prime ideal> $P$ of norm $p$ with $[P]=[I]^{-1}$. Indeed, a representation gives an element $\alpha\in I$ with $N\alpha=pN(I)$, so $P=(\alpha)I^{-1}$ is integral of norm $p$. Conversely, $IP=(\alpha)$ with $\alpha\in I$ produces a representation by expressing $\alpha$ in the basis. <Artin reciprocity> then detects this <ideal class> by Frobenius in the <Hilbert class field>.

= Discriminant criterion for prime representation by a binary quadratic form
{parent=Binary quadratic form}
{title2=$p\text{ represented}\iff d\text{ is a square modulo }p$}

For odd prime $p$ and an integral-form discriminant $d$, representation of $p$ by some form of discriminant $d$ is equivalent to solvability of $b^2\equiv d\pmod p$. A primitive representing vector can be completed to a unimodular change giving leading coefficient $p$. Conversely choose $b$ with the required parity and use $[p,b,(b^2-d)/(4p)]$.

= Proper equivalence of binary quadratic forms
{parent=Binary quadratic form}

Integral forms $f$ and $g$ are properly equivalent when
$$g(x,y)=f(\alpha x+\beta y,\gamma x+\delta y)$$
for some $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\\gamma&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)$. Proper equivalence preserves both represented integers and orientation.

= Primitive binary quadratic form
{parent=Binary quadratic form}

An integral binary quadratic form $[a,b,c]$ is primitive when $\gcd(a,b,c)=1$. This property is invariant under proper equivalence because a unimodular change and its integral inverse express either coefficient triple as integer combinations of the other.

= Discriminant of a binary quadratic form
{parent=Binary quadratic form}

The discriminant of $[a,b,c]$ is $D=b^2-4ac$. It is invariant under proper equivalence, and a real form with $a>0$ is positive definite exactly when $D<0$.

= Reduced positive definite binary quadratic form
{parent=Binary quadratic form}

A positive definite integral form $[a,b,c]$ is reduced when $|b|\leq a\leq c$, with $b\geq0$ if $|b|=a$ or $a=c$. Every proper equivalence class has a unique reduced representative under this boundary convention.

= Reduction algorithm for a positive definite binary quadratic form
{parent=Reduced positive definite binary quadratic form}

For a positive definite integral form $[a,b,c]$, a unimodular shear can replace $b$ by its representative $b'$ with $|b'|\leq a$. If the resulting $c'=(b'^2-D)/(4a)$ is smaller than $a$, swapping the variables replaces the leading coefficient by the smaller positive integer $c'$. Repeating terminates and produces $|b|\leq a\leq c$; determinant-one sign changes enforce the boundary convention. Thus every positive definite integral binary quadratic form is properly equivalent to a reduced form.

= Enumeration of reduced binary quadratic forms
{parent=Reduced positive definite binary quadratic form}

For a reduced form of negative discriminant $D$, the inequalities imply $3a^2\leq|D|$. One can therefore enumerate all classes by checking the finite set
$$1\leq a\leq\sqrt{|D|/3},\qquad |b|\leq a,$$
and retaining integral $c=(b^2-D)/(4a)$ with $a\leq c$ and the boundary sign convention.

= Reduced positive definite binary quadratic forms of discriminant minus thirty-nine
{parent=Enumeration of reduced binary quadratic forms}
{title2=$h(-39)=4$}

The <reduced positive definite binary quadratic form> bounds $|b|\leq a\leq c$ and $b^2-4ac=-39$ imply $1\leq a\leq\sqrt{13}$. Enumerating $a=1,2,3$ and using the nonnegative boundary sign convention gives the four triples $(a,b,c)=(1,1,10),(2,1,5),(2,-1,5),(3,3,4)$. Thus the number of reduced forms is four.

= Reduced forms of discriminant minus thirty-two
{parent=Enumeration of reduced binary quadratic forms}
{title2=$[1,0,8],\ [2,0,4],\ [3,2,3]$}

The reduced-form bound $3a^2\le32$ leaves $a=1,2,3$. Checking $c=(b^2+32)/(4a)$ and the boundary sign convention gives precisely the displayed forms. The middle one is imprimitive and represents the prime two. Odd represented primes are $1$ or $3$ modulo eight by the <discriminant criterion for prime representation by a binary quadratic form>. The first form represents only $1$ modulo eight among odd integers, and the last only $3$, so a prime is represented by $3x^2+2xy+3y^2$ exactly when it is $3$ modulo eight.

= Class number one for discriminant minus sixty-seven
{parent=Enumeration of reduced binary quadratic forms}
{title2=$D=-67$}

For negative discriminant $-67$, a <reduced positive definite binary quadratic form> satisfies $1\le a\le\sqrt{67/3}<5$ and $b$ odd with $|b|\le a$. Checking $4a\mid b^2+67$ leaves only $(a,b,c)=(1,1,17)$ under the boundary convention. The <reduction algorithm for a positive definite binary quadratic form> therefore puts every form in this one <proper equivalence of binary quadratic forms> class. Its unimodular shears give infinitely many distinct forms $x^2+(2n+1)xy+(n^2+n+17)y^2$.

= Reduced forms of discriminant minus forty-four
{parent=Enumeration of reduced binary quadratic forms}
{title2=$D=-44$}

The reduced positive definite triples are $(1,0,11)$, $(2,2,6)$ and $(3,\pm2,4)$. The imprimitive middle form represents only even numbers; the two last forms have identical value sets after reversing one variable.

= Reduced positive definite binary quadratic forms of discriminant minus thirty-five
{title2=$D=-35$}
{parent=Enumeration of reduced binary quadratic forms}

The two reduced positive definite integral forms of discriminant $-35$ are
$$
[1,1,9]\quad\hbox{and}\quad[3,1,3].
$$
For a prime $p>7$, the first represents $p$ exactly when
$$
\left(\frac p5\right)=\left(\frac p7\right)=1,
$$
and the second represents $p$ exactly when both <Legendre symbols> are $-1$.

= Primitive representation criterion at discriminant minus thirty-five
{parent=Reduced positive definite binary quadratic forms of discriminant minus thirty-five}
{title2=$(-35/q)=1\text{ for every prime }q\mid n$}

For odd $n$ prime to 35, proper representation by a positive definite <binary quadratic form> of discriminant $-35$ is equivalent to $-35$ being a square modulo every prime divisor of $n$. A primitive representing vector extends to a determinant-one integer matrix, producing a form with leading coefficient $n$ and middle coefficient $b$ satisfying $b^2\equiv-35\pmod{4n}$. Conversely this congruence constructs such a form. Its local roots lift to prime powers and combine by the <Chinese remainder theorem>. Every class reduces to one of $x^2+xy+9y^2$ and $3x^2+xy+3y^2$. The condition is local at each prime divisor; a product <Jacobi symbol> equal to one alone is insufficient.

= Class number of a negative discriminant
{parent=Class number problem}

For a negative discriminant $D\equiv0$ or $1\pmod4$, the class number $h(D)$ is the number of proper equivalence classes of primitive positive definite integral binary quadratic forms of discriminant $D$. Equivalently, it is the number of reduced primitive forms of discriminant $D$.

= Two distinguished classes of discriminant minus eight q
{title2=$D=-8q$}
{parent=Class number of a negative discriminant}

For an odd <prime number> $q$, the primitive positive definite forms
$$
[1,0,2q]
\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad
[2,0,q]
$$
have discriminant $-8q$ and lie in distinct proper equivalence classes. The first represents one, whereas the second has minimum positive value two.

= Prime representation when h of minus eight q equals two
{title2=$h(-8q)=2$}
{parent=Two distinguished classes of discriminant minus eight q}

If $q\equiv3\pmod8$ is prime and $h(-8q)=2$, then for every prime $p>q$,
$$
p=x^2+2qy^2
$$
for some integers $x,y$ exactly when $p\equiv\pm1\pmod8$ and $p$ is a <quadratic residue> modulo $q$.

= Coprime-factorization lower bound for a quadratic-form class number
{parent=Class number of a negative discriminant}

Let $d$ be odd and have $k$ distinct prime divisors. Assigning each complete prime-power factor of $d$ to one of two factors gives $2^k$ ordered coprime factorizations $d=ac$. Interchanging $a$ and $c$ identifies pairs, and each unordered factorization gives a different reduced primitive form $[\min(a,c),0,\max(a,c)]$ of discriminant $-4d$. Hence
$$
h(-4d)\geq2^{k-1}.
$$

= Unimodular reduction of a discriminant-minus-four form to two squares

The identity

5x^2+16xy+13y^2=(x+2y)^2+(2x+3y)^2
$$
comes from an integer change-of-variable matrix of determinant $-1$. It therefore preserves the set of integers represented and reduces the form to $u^2+v^2$.

= Euler totient function
{title2=$\varphi(n)$}
{parent=Number theory}
{c}
{wiki}

= Euler's totient function
{c}
{synonym}

Euler’s totient function counts residue classes modulo n that are coprime to n.

= Sum of reduced residues
{parent=Euler totient function}
{title2=$\sum_{1\leq j<n,\ (j,n)=1}j=n\varphi(n)/2\quad(n>1)$}

The map $j\mapsto n-j$ is a <bijection> of the <integers> between one and $n-1$ that are <coprime> to $n$. If their sum is $S$, this <bijection> gives $S=n\varphi(n)-S$, so $S=n\varphi(n)/2$. This works even at $n=2$, where the single residue is fixed. The same identity follows from the <weighted inclusion-exclusion principle> by excluding multiples of the <prime factors> of $n$.

= Integers whose totient divides them
{parent=Euler totient function}
{title2=$\varphi(n)\mid n\iff n=1\text{ or }n=2^c3^d,\ c\ge1,\ d\ge0$}

If $n>1$ is odd, its <Euler totient function> is even and cannot divide $n$. Otherwise write $n=2^c\prod_{j=1}^r p_j^{a_j}$ with odd primes $p_j$ and $c\ge1$. Comparing powers of two in $\varphi(n)\mid n$ gives $\sum_j v_2(p_j-1)\le1$. Thus there is at most one odd prime $p$, with $v_2(p-1)=1$. Since $p-1$ divides $\varphi(n)$, it divides $n$, and is <coprime> to $p$; with only the prime factors $2,p$ available it must be a power of two. Hence $p-1=2$ and $p=3$. Conversely, for $c\ge1$ the prime-power totient formula verifies divisibility for $2^c3^d$, and $n=1$ is immediate.

= Totient divisibility along divisors
{parent=Euler totient function}
{title2=$d\mid n\Longrightarrow\varphi(d)\mid\varphi(n)$}

Write $n=\prod p^{a_p}$ and $d=\prod p^{b_p}$, with $0\le b_p\le a_p$. The prime-power formula for the <Euler totient function> gives an integer contribution to $\varphi(n)/\varphi(d)$ from each prime: it is $p^{a_p-b_p}$ when $b_p>0$, and $p^{a_p-1}(p-1)$ when $b_p=0<a_p$. Multiplying proves the <divisibility> assertion, including $d=1$ with $\varphi(1)=1$.

= Rosser–Schoenfeld totient bound
{c}
{parent=Euler totient function}
{title2=$\varphi(n)/n>1/[e^{\gamma_E}\log\log n+2.50637/\log\log n]$}

For $n\ge3$, this explicit lower bound on the <Euler totient function> implies $\varphi(n)/n=\Omega(1/\log\log n)$ as $n\to\infty$. Here $\gamma_E$ is the <Euler-Mascheroni constant>. The prime-product formula also gives $\varphi(r)/r\ge\varphi(N)/N$ whenever $r\mid N$, making the bound useful for certified <quantum period finding>. The relevant statement is a lower bound: the claimed universal upper estimate $\varphi(n)=O(n/\log\log n)$ would fail at primes. Equations (3.41)–(3.42) of Theorem 15 provide the primary bound: https://denisevellachemla.eu/Rosser-Schoenfeld-1962.pdf .

= Elementary totient-ratio lower bound
{parent=Euler totient function}
{title2=$\varphi(r)/r\geq1/(1+\log_2r)$}

List the $k$ distinct prime divisors of $r$ in increasing order. The $j$th is at least $j+1$, and $k\leq\log_2r$. The <Euler totient function> product formula gives $\varphi(r)/r\geq\prod_{j=1}^k j/(j+1)=1/(k+1)$. This elementary bound is sufficient to show inverse-polynomial success probability for coprime phase labels in <quantum order finding>.

= Euler theorem
{parent=Euler totient function}
{c}

If a is coprime to n, then a raised to the power phi(n) is congruent to one modulo n.

Multiplication by a unit $a$ permutes the reduced residue classes modulo $n$. Multiplying all classes before and after this permutation and cancelling their unit product proves $a^{\phi(n)}\equiv1\pmod n$.

= Modular multiplicative inverse
{title2=$a^{-1}\pmod n$}
{parent=Number theory}
{wiki}

= Modular inverse
{synonym}

For <integers> $a$ and $n>1$, a modular multiplicative inverse is a <congruence class> $b$ satisfying $ab\equiv1\pmod n$. It exists exactly when $\gcd(a,n)=1$. Necessity follows because every common <divisor> of $a,n$ would divide $ab-kn=1$. Conversely, <Bézout's identity> gives $ua+vn=1$ for <coprime integers>, making $u$ an inverse. This is the basic bridge from <greatest common divisor> calculations to reversible <modular arithmetic>.

For example, $5\cdot7-2\cdot17=1$, so $5^{-1}\equiv7\pmod{17}$. The <modular congruence> $5x\equiv3\pmod{17}$ therefore has the unique solution class $x\equiv21\equiv4\pmod{17}$. Multiplication by $5$ sends all seventeen residue classes to different classes; multiplying by $7$ reverses it. The inverse criterion and its algorithmic construction are presented in \a[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-200-principles-of-discrete-applied-mathematics-spring-2024/mit18_200_s24_lec15.pdf][Ankur Moitra, MIT 18.200, 2024, modular inverses and Euclid's algorithm]{ref}.

By contrast, $5$ has no <modular inverse> modulo $15$. Its products have only residues $0,5,10$. Consequently $5x\equiv3\pmod{15}$ has no solution, while $5x\equiv10\pmod{15}$ has five solution classes, $x\equiv2,5,8,11,14$. Dividing by a noninvertible residue would discard this distinction. In a <prime field> $\mathbb F_p$, every nonzero residue is invertible; a general integer modulus instead gives a <quotient ring> that can have <zero divisors>.

= Decimal period from multiplicative order
{parent=Multiplicative order}
{title2=$\operatorname{ord}_q(10)$}

For $q>1$ coprime to $10$, long division of $1/q$ has remainders $r_n\equiv10^n\pmod q$ in $1,\ldots,q-1$. Its digits satisfy $10r_{n-1}=qa_n+r_n$. A congruence $10^m\equiv1\pmod q$ therefore makes the remainders and digits periodic with period dividing $m$. The least period is the <multiplicative order> of $10$ modulo $q$.

= Cyclic decimal divisibility
{parent=Multiplicative order}

For an $L$-digit decimal integer $n$, moving its leading digit to the end gives a cyclic rotation $c_1(n)$ satisfying
$$
c_1(n)\equiv10n\pmod{10^L-1}.
$$
Thus, when a modulus $d$ divides $10^L-1$, every rotation has the same divisibility by $d$ as $n$, up to multiplication by a power of ten.

= Euclidean algorithm
{parent=Number theory}
{wiki}

For positive <integers> $a,b$, the Euclidean algorithm repeatedly performs <division with remainder>: write $a=qb+r$ with $0\leq r<b$, then replace $(a,b)$ by $(b,r)$ until the second entry is zero. The final nonzero entry is their <greatest common divisor>. A remainder preserves the set of common <divisors>: an <integer> divides $a$ and $b$ exactly when it divides $b$ and $a-qb$. The positive remainders strictly decrease, so the calculation terminates.

The complete calculation for $252$ and $198$ is
$$
\begin{aligned}
252&=1\cdot198+54,\\
198&=3\cdot54+36,\\
54&=1\cdot36+18,\\
36&=2\cdot18+0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence **$\gcd(252,198)=18$**. The <fraction> $252/198$ reduces to $14/11$ by dividing numerator and denominator by that common factor. The same remainder calculation has a geometric interpretation: remove squares from a $252$-by-$198$ <rectangle>, then repeat on the remaining strip. Each division step records how many equal squares fit and the width of the new strip.

{title=Successive square removals record the Euclidean algorithm for 252 and 198}

The procedure does more than test individual <divisors>. Each step replaces the whole common-divisor problem by a smaller equivalent one. Euclid's Book VII, Proposition 2 describes the corresponding repeated-subtraction construction; batching those subtractions into <division with remainder> gives the present formulation. \a[https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/java/elements/bookVII/propVII2.html][Euclid, Elements VII.2, greatest common measure]{ref}

= Extended Euclidean algorithm
{parent=Euclidean algorithm}
{wiki}

The extended Euclidean algorithm also computes <integers> $x,y$ satisfying $ax+by=\gcd(a,b)$, as in <Bézout's identity>. Back-substitution in the calculation under the <Euclidean algorithm> gives
$$
18=54-36=54-(198-3\cdot54)=4(252-198)-198=4\cdot252-5\cdot198.
$$
Thus $(x,y)=(4,-5)$ solves $252x+198y=18$. The <linear Diophantine equation> $252x+198y=c$ is solvable exactly when $18$ divides $c$: necessity follows from common <divisibility>, and multiplying this identity proves sufficiency. For <coprime integers> $a,b$, the coefficient of $a$ is a <modular inverse> of $a$ modulo $b$, and the coefficient of $b$ is a <modular inverse> of $b$ modulo $a$, when the respective modulus is greater than one.

= Linear Diophantine equation
{parent=Euclidean algorithm}

A linear Diophantine equation in two variables has the form $ax+by=c$ with integer coefficients and seeks integer solutions.

= All solutions of a linear Diophantine equation
{parent=Linear Diophantine equation}
{title2=$(x,y)=(x_0,y_0)+t(b/d,-a/d)$}

If $a,b$ are nonzero <integers>, $d=\gcd(a,b)$, and $(x_0,y_0)$ is one solution of the <linear Diophantine equation> $ax+by=c$, then all solutions are
$$
x=x_0+\frac bd\,t,\qquad y=y_0-\frac ad\,t,\qquad t\in\mathbb Z.
$$
Subtract two solutions and use the fact that $a/d$ and $b/d$ are <coprime integers>. The <linear Diophantine equation solvability criterion> says that a particular solution exists exactly when $d$ divides $c$; the <extended Euclidean algorithm> constructs one. This parametrization separates existence from the complete family of answers.

= Linear Diophantine equation solvability criterion
{parent=Linear Diophantine equation}

The equation $ax+by=c$ has an integer solution exactly when $\gcd(a,b)$ divides $c$. Bézout's identity proves sufficiency, while every integer linear combination of $a,b$ is divisible by their greatest common divisor.

= Sum of two squares theorem
{parent=Number theory}
{wiki}

A positive integer is a sum of two integer squares exactly when every prime factor congruent to $3$ modulo $4$ has even exponent in its prime factorization.

= Congruence lattice proof of the prime sum of two squares
{parent=Sum of two squares theorem}
{title2=$\Lambda=\{(a,b)\in\mathbb Z^2:b\equiv ua\pmod p\}$}

If $u^2\equiv-1\pmod p$, this lattice has basis $(1,u),(0,p)$ and covolume $p$. The <Minkowski convex body theorem> applied to a disk of squared radius strictly between $4p/\pi$ and $2p$ gives a nonzero lattice vector with $0<a^2+b^2<2p$. The congruence makes its squared length divisible by $p$, forcing $a^2+b^2=p$. The strict radius bounds avoid assuming the critical-volume form of Minkowski's theorem.
