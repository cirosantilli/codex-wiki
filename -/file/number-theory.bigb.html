= Number theory
{wiki}

Number theory studies <integers>, their <divisibility>, and the patterns created by <prime numbers>. Its questions often have short statements but rigid answers: which <integers> divide a given one, when does an <equation> have integer solutions, and when can arithmetic with <congruence classes> be reversed? The <Euclidean algorithm> and <modular multiplicative inverses> connect these questions through explicit calculations.

For example, $252=2^2\cdot3^2\cdot7$ and $198=2\cdot3^2\cdot11$. Their shared <prime factors> give <greatest common divisor> $18$. The <Euclidean algorithm> reaches that answer without finding either complete <prime factorization>. It also produces $18=4\cdot252-5\cdot198$, an instance of <Bézout's identity>. This relation certifies every common <divisor> at once and solves an associated <linear Diophantine equation>.

<Modular arithmetic> retains only remainders, but the lost information can be surprisingly controlled. Multiplication by $5$ modulo $17$ is a <permutation> of all residue classes, because $5$ has <modular inverse> $7$. Multiplication by $5$ modulo $15$ collapses fifteen residue classes to three. Thus an apparently small change in the modulus changes whether a calculation can be reversed. The <Chinese remainder theorem> explains another kind of compression: remainders modulo <coprime integers> can be combined into one remainder modulo their product.

Simple restrictions help reveal the structure without telling the whole story. Every <prime number> greater than three is congruent to $1$ or $5$ modulo $6$: the other four residue classes force <divisibility> by $2$ or $3$. The converse fails because $25$ and $35$ lie in those classes but are <composite numbers>. Necessary conditions are useful filters, not substitutes for <primality testing>.

The subject's appeal lies partly in this tension: <prime factorization> gives a clean description of every positive <integer>, while identifying factors and predicting their distribution lead to substantial further questions. <Analytic number theory> uses tools from <analysis> to study global patterns, and <algebraic number theory> extends arithmetic into <number fields>. Applications include <cryptography> and <error-correcting codes>; MIT's discrete applied mathematics course develops modular structures for both purposes. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-200-principles-of-discrete-applied-mathematics-spring-2024/mit18_200_s24_lec15.pdf[Ankur Moitra, MIT 18.200, Modular Arithmetic and Elementary Algebra, 2024, introduction]{ref}

Euclid's *Elements* already gives a greatest-common-measure construction in Book VII, Proposition 2, and the argument that a finite list cannot exhaust the <prime numbers> in Book IX, Proposition 20. These are documented ancient treatments, rather than evidence that all of number theory began with one author. https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/java/elements/bookVII/propVII2.html[Euclid, Elements VII.2, translated text hosted by David Joyce]{ref} https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/java/elements/bookIX/propIX20.html[Euclid, Elements IX.20, translated text hosted by David Joyce]{ref}

= Product-sum number
{parent=Number theory}

A <positive integer> $N$ that is both the <sum> and the <product> of one <multiset> of $k$ <positive integers>. For factors at least $2$ with product $P$, sum $S$, and count $t$, appending $P-S$ ones gives $k=t+P-S$ without changing the <product>. For $k\ge2$, the example $2,k$ with $k-2$ ones proves that the smallest product-sum value is at most $2k$.

= Integer factorisation
{synonym}

Integer factorization seeks the <prime factorization> of a positive <integer> $N$. For $N>1$, this means identifying its <prime factors> and their multiplicities; one has the empty <prime factorization>. Finding one proper <divisor> $1<d<N$ is useful progress, but need not finish the job: factor $d$ and $N/d$ further until all factors are <prime numbers>.

For example, $77=7\cdot11$ is a <semiprime>, whereas $231=3\cdot7\cdot11$ has three <prime factors>. <Primality testing> asks whether a proposed <integer> is prime; it need not return a factor when the answer is no. <Trial division> is a direct factoring method: try division by small <prime numbers> until a <divisor> is found. More elaborate methods obtain a proper <divisor> through a <greatest common divisor> of $N$ with a specially constructed <integer>.

The beautiful trick in <congruence of squares> is that two <integers> with equal squares modulo $N$ can reveal different factors through their sum and difference. In the worked $N=77$ example, $20^2\equiv13^2\pmod{77}$ gives $\gcd(20-13,77)=7$ and $\gcd(20+13,77)=11$. Computing those <greatest common divisors> uses the <Euclidean algorithm>; it does not require trying every potential factor. A <factor base> supplies a systematic way to construct useful equal-square relations. https://crypto.stanford.edu/pbc/notes/crypto/factoring.html[Ben Lynn, Factoring and Discrete Logarithms: congruent squares and the linear-algebra step]{ref}.

This connection is useful in <cryptography> because recovering the factors of an <RSA cryptosystem> modulus defeats the factoring assumption behind its construction. That application concerns large, carefully chosen inputs; the small examples here explain the arithmetic mechanism and are not benchmarks or estimates of present cryptographic security.

= Pollard's p-1 algorithm
{c}
{parent=Integer factorization}
{wiki=Pollard's_p_−_1_algorithm}

For an <integer> $n$ to be factored, <John Pollard>'s method chooses an <integer> $a$ <coprime> to $n$ and an <exponent> $M$ divisible by many small <prime powers>. Compute $d=\gcd(a^M-1,n)$ by <modular exponentiation> and the <Euclidean algorithm>. If $1<d<n$, it is a proper <factor>. If a <prime divisor> $p$ of $n$ has $p-1\mid M$, <Fermat's little theorem> ensures $p\mid d$; success requires some other <prime divisor> not to divide $a^M-1$. Common choices are $M=B!$ and $M=\operatorname{lcm}(1,\ldots,B)$. The method exploits a smooth $p-1$, rather than a small <factor> $p$ itself.

= General number field sieve
{parent=Integer factorization}

A general-purpose classical <integer factorization> algorithm combining polynomial relations, arithmetic in <number fields> and sparse linear algebra to construct congruent squares modulo the input. Its familiar general-input complexity estimate is heuristic and subexponential in the input's value, roughly $\exp(O(n^{1/3}(\log n)^{2/3}))$ for $n$ input bits. This is not polynomial in the bit length. Special input structure, small factors and preprocessing can make other algorithms preferable.

= Ordered factorization
{parent=Integer factorization}

An ordered factorization of a <positive integer> $n>1$ is an ordered list of factors greater than one whose product is $n$. For example, $6$, $2\cdot3$, and $3\cdot2$ are the three ordered factorizations of $6$. Taking successive partial products gives a <bijection> with strictly increasing <divisor> chains from $1$ to $n$.

= Factor base
{parent=Integer factorization}

A factor base is a finite <set> of small <prime numbers> over which selected auxiliary <integers> are completely factored. Some methods also record a sign coordinate for $-1$, which is not a <prime number>. A positive <integer> supported entirely on the chosen <factor base> gives an <exponent vector>; reducing that vector modulo two records which <prime exponents> are <odd integers>. The product is a <square of a number> precisely when every prime exponent is an <even integer>.

For the original toy $N=77$, use <factor base> $\{2,3,5\}$ and the two relations
$$
13^2\equiv15=3\cdot5\pmod{77},\qquad
40^2\equiv60=2^2\cdot3\cdot5\pmod{77}.
$$
The residues have exponent vectors $(0,1,1)$ and $(2,1,1)$ in the order $(2,3,5)$. Adding the two vectors modulo two gives $(0,0,0)$, so multiplying the residues gives $15\cdot60=900=30^2$. Consequently
$$
(13\cdot40)^2\equiv30^2\pmod{77},\qquad
X=13\cdot40\bmod77=58,\quad Y=30,
$$
and **$\gcd(X-Y,77)=7$ and $\gcd(X+Y,77)=11$**. Neither individual residue was a square; combining the relations made all exponents even.

\Image[factor-base-parity-to-square-congruence.png]
{title=A <factor base> turns two smooth relations into a <congruence of squares> through exponent parity}
{description=The original $N=77$ example uses the ordered <factor base> $(2,3,5)$. The rows $(0,1,1)$ and $(2,1,1)$ add to zero modulo two, giving residue product $900=30^2$. The product of the original inputs is $520\equiv58\pmod{77}$; <greatest common divisor> calculations then extract seven and eleven.}

In a larger collection, choose relations $x_i^2\equiv r_i\pmod N$ with positive residues $r_i=\prod_{j=1}^B p_j^{e_{ji}}$. If a nonempty selection $I$ has an even sum in every exponent coordinate, set
$$
X=\prod_{i\in I}x_i\bmod N,\qquad
Y=\prod_{j=1}^B p_j^{\frac12\sum_{i\in I}e_{ji}}\bmod N.
$$
Then $X^2\equiv Y^2\pmod N$. Finding such a selection is a <linear dependence> problem over the <finite field> $\mathbb F_2$: the indicator vector of the chosen relations belongs to the <kernel of a linear map> given by the parity matrix. This is why a problem about <prime factors> recruits <linear algebra>. The final <congruence of squares> may still be trivial, so another selection can be needed. https://crypto.stanford.edu/pbc/notes/crypto/factoring.html[Ben Lynn, Factoring and Discrete Logarithms, Dixon's Algorithm: exponent matrix and kernel]{ref}.

There is a tradeoff: a small <factor base> gives a smaller parity matrix, but fewer auxiliary <integers> factor completely over it. A larger <factor base> accepts more relations but makes the <linear algebra> larger. Negative auxiliary values need an additional sign coordinate, and a selected product needs even sign parity as well as even prime exponents. Pomerance's account explains this balance and the historical development of combining smooth relations; the toy example omits algorithm-specific sieving and running-time claims. https://math.dartmouth.edu/~carlp/PDF/paper109.pdf[Carl Pomerance, A Tale of Two Sieves (1996), pp. 1475–1476: exponent parity, factor bases and signs]{ref}.

= Odd part of an integer
{synonym}

The odd part of a positive <integer> $n$ is the unique odd positive <integer> $d$ obtained by repeatedly dividing $n$ by two. The process terminates because the positive <integers> decrease. If $2^r d=2^s e$ with $d,e$ odd, then $r>s$ would make $e$ even and $s>r$ would make $d$ even; hence $r=s$ and $d=e$. Two positive <integers> have the same odd part exactly when their ratio is an integral power of two. The odd positive <integers> therefore give representatives for the resulting <equivalence classes>.

{parent=Integer factorization}

= Congruences of squares
{synonym}

A congruence of squares says that two <integers> $a,b$ have the same square modulo a positive <integer> $N$. Factoring their difference of squares gives
$$
a^2\equiv b^2\pmod N\quad\Longrightarrow\quad N\mid(a-b)(a+b).
$$
A useful <integer factorization> results when $\gcd(a-b,N)$ or $\gcd(a+b,N)$ is a proper <divisor> of $N$. Divisibility of a product does not generally force divisibility of either factor by all of $N$: different <prime factors> of $N$ can divide different parts of that product.

For $N=77$, $20^2-13^2=231=3\cdot77$. Therefore
$$
\gcd(20-13,77)=\gcd(7,77)=7,\qquad
\gcd(20+13,77)=\gcd(33,77)=11.
$$
**Thus $77=7\cdot11$.** The prime seven divides the difference, while eleven divides the sum. These computations already give a complete <prime factorization> because seven and eleven are <prime numbers>.

The <Chinese remainder theorem> explains the hidden sign choices. The four <congruence classes> solving $z^2\equiv15\pmod{77}$ have representatives $13,20,57,64$. Modulo seven each is $1$ or $-1$; modulo eleven each is $2$ or $-2$. Two independent sign choices produce four roots. The inputs thirteen and twenty agree modulo seven and disagree by sign modulo eleven, allowing their sum and difference to separate the two <prime factors>.

\Image[square-congruence-chinese-remainder-signs.png]
{title=The <Chinese remainder theorem> exposes four sign choices behind one square residue modulo $77$}
{description=Each cell gives the unique residue modulo $77$ with its indicated residues modulo seven and eleven. Relative to input thirteen, input twenty agrees modulo seven and has the opposite sign modulo eleven. The resulting <greatest common divisors> are seven and eleven. Diagonal all-same or all-opposite choices give only the trivial factors one and $77$.}

For distinct odd <prime numbers> $p,q$, $N=pq$ and $\gcd(b,N)=1$, a solution $a^2\equiv b^2\pmod N$ with $a\not\equiv b$ and $a\not\equiv-b$ modulo $N$ yields both proper factors by the two <greatest common divisors>. For a general composite <integer>, a proper <divisor> need not itself be prime; further factoring can remain. If $a\equiv b$ or $a\equiv-b\pmod N$ in the coprime case, the two calculations return $N$ and one instead. A <factor base> helps generate relations, but does not ensure every resulting relation is useful.

For historical context, Pomerance describes Kraitchik's 1920s enhancement of the difference-of-squares approach associated with Fermat: seek a difference that is a multiple of $N$, rather than exactly $N$. His account also notes antecedents in Gauss and Seelhoff, so this is not an attribution of the whole idea to a single inventor. https://math.dartmouth.edu/~carlp/PDF/paper109.pdf[Carl Pomerance, A Tale of Two Sieves (1996), pp. 1474–1475: difference-of-squares and congruent squares]{ref}.

= Nontrivial square root of unity modulo an integer
{parent=Congruence of squares}
{title2=$z^2\equiv1\pmod N,\quad z\not\equiv\pm1\pmod N$}

For an odd <integer> $N>1$, a nontrivial square root of unity modulo $N$ is a residue $z$ with $z^2\equiv1\pmod N$ and $z\not\equiv\pm1\pmod N$. Then both $\gcd(z-1,N)$ and $\gcd(z+1,N)$ are proper <divisors>. For $N=77$, $z=34$ works: $34^2\equiv1\pmod{77}$, $\gcd(33,77)=11$, and $\gcd(35,77)=7$.

A <congruence of squares> with $\gcd(b,N)=1$ converts to this form by $z\equiv a b^{-1}\pmod N$, using a <modular multiplicative inverse>. In the $a=20,b=13$ example, $13^{-1}\equiv6\pmod{77}$, so $z\equiv20\cdot6\equiv43\pmod{77}$. It yields $\gcd(42,77)=7$ and $\gcd(44,77)=11$. Nontriviality requires mixed sign choices across prime factors, which an odd <prime number> modulus cannot provide.

= Fermat number
{c}
{parent=Number theory}
{title2=$F_n=2^{2^n}+1$}
{wiki}

A <Fermat number> is the <positive integer> $F_n=2^{2^n}+1$, indexed by a <nonnegative integer> $n$. The first five are $3,5,17,257,65537$, all <prime numbers>; the next is **the <composite number> $F_5=4294967297=641\cdot6700417$**. This family offers a compact lesson in <number theory>: a very persuasive finite pattern can fail, while an exact <divisibility> relation settles a gigantic case with little computation.

\Image[fermat-numbers-growth-and-first-factors.png]
{title=Exact Fermat numbers: five initial primes, a composite sixth term, and rapidly increasing decimal lengths}

Why does the exponent itself have the form $2^n$? Suppose $m=ab$ is a <positive integer> with an <odd integer> factor $b>1$. The <polynomial factorization> $x^b+1=(x+1)(x^{b-1}-x^{b-2}+\cdots-x+1)$ shows that $2^a+1$ is a proper <divisor> of $2^m+1$. Consequently, if $2^m+1$ is a <prime number>, $m$ must be a <power of two>. That is a necessary condition, rather than a promise that the surviving candidates are prime.

The <Fermat number product identity> makes distinct <Fermat numbers> <coprime integers>. Thus every term has a <prime factor> absent from all earlier terms, whether or not that term is itself a <prime number>. This gives an explicit route to infinitely many different <prime numbers>, without establishing that infinitely many terms of this particular <sequence> are prime. Meanwhile the <prime divisor of a Fermat number> criterion filters possible factors through a small <multiplicative order> calculation.

The family also links arithmetic to <Euclidean geometry>: a <constructible regular polygon> can have a number of sides equal to a <power of two> times a product of distinct <Fermat primes>. The <Gauss-Wantzel theorem> gives the precise criterion. For instance, $15=3\cdot5$ and $17$ qualify, but $9=3^2$ does not. The word "distinct" matters. <Carl Pomerance> describes this connection in <Primality testing: then and now by Carl Pomerance> (slide 18).

Historically, <Leonhard Euler> records <Pierre de Fermat>'s proposed primality pattern, then refutes it by finding the factor $641$ of $F_5$. His paper was presented in 1732 and published in 1738; those are different events, not competing dates for one publication.<Observations on a certain theorem of Fermat and on others concerning prime numbers by Leonhard Euler>[pp. 1–2 and the publication note]{ref}

A short modern check needs only two representations of $641$:
$$
641=5\cdot2^7+1=5^4+2^4.
$$
In <modular arithmetic> these give $5^4 2^{28}\equiv1$ and $5^4\equiv-2^4\pmod{641}$. Substitution yields $2^{32}\equiv-1\pmod{641}$, so $641\mid F_5$. This verifies the factor directly; it does not claim to reconstruct Euler's search process.

= Fermat number product identity
{c}
{parent=Fermat number}
{title2=$\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}F_j=F_n-2$}

For each <positive integer> $n$, the product of the preceding <Fermat numbers> is two less than the next:
$$
\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}F_j=2^{2^n}-1=F_n-2.
$$
For example, $3\cdot5\cdot17=255=257-2$. The identity telescopes by repeated <difference of squares>: $(2^{2^j}-1)(2^{2^j}+1)=2^{2^{j+1}}-1$. Its usefulness is that a huge <integer> product exposes a tiny difference, two, from the next term.

= Distinct Fermat numbers are coprime
{parent=Fermat number product identity}
{title2=$\gcd(F_m,F_n)=1\quad(m\ne n)$}

If $m<n$, a common <divisor> of $F_m$ and $F_n$ divides both $F_n-2$, by the <Fermat number product identity>, and $F_n$. It therefore divides two. Both <Fermat numbers> are <odd integers>, so their <greatest common divisor> is one. Each term's <prime factors> are consequently different from the <prime factors> of every other term.

= Prime divisor of a Fermat number
{parent=Fermat number}
{title2=$\operatorname{ord}_p(2)=2^{n+1}$}

For a <prime number> $p$, the exact criterion is
$$
p\mid F_n\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\operatorname{ord}_p(2)=2^{n+1},\qquad n\geq0,
$$
where $p$ must be different from two and $\operatorname{ord}_p(2)$ is the <multiplicative order> of two modulo $p$. If $p\mid F_n$, then $2^{2^n}\equiv-1\pmod p$: the order divides $2^{n+1}$ but not $2^n$, so it is exactly $2^{n+1}$. Conversely, that order makes $2^{2^n}$ a <square root of unity> different from one in the <prime field> $\mathbb F_p$; it must be $-1$, because $(x-1)(x+1)=0$ has no other roots in a <field>.

\Image[fermat-prime-divisors-modular-orders.png]
{title=Exact modular powers distinguish Fermat divisors from the prime thirteen, whose multiplicative order is twelve}

For example, modulo seventeen the powers of two are $2,4,8,16,15,13,9,1$. Their first return to one occurs after eight steps. The half-way value is $16=-1\pmod{17}$, proving that $17\mid F_2$. Likewise, $\operatorname{ord}_{641}(2)=64$ identifies $641$ as a <prime factor> of $F_5$. These are statements about <divisibility>, not evidence that the entire <Fermat number> is prime.

The <multiplicative order> always divides $p-1$, by <Fermat's little theorem>, so $p\equiv1\pmod{2^{n+1}}$. In particular, for $n\geq1$ every <prime divisor of a Fermat number> is one modulo four. That weaker <modular congruence> is insufficient: thirteen is one modulo four, but $\operatorname{ord}_{13}(2)=12$, which is not a <power of two>. It divides no <Fermat number> at any index. The exact order criterion explains the whole family, instead of testing finitely many terms.

= Stronger prime-divisor congruence for Fermat numbers
{parent=Prime divisor of a Fermat number}
{title2=$p\mid F_n,\ n\geq2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p\equiv1\pmod{2^{n+2}}$}

For $n\geq2$, the first order bound already gives $p\equiv1\pmod8$. The <second supplementary law for quadratic reciprocity> says that two is then a <quadratic residue> modulo $p$. <Euler's criterion> gives $2^{(p-1)/2}\equiv1$, so its <multiplicative order> $2^{n+1}$ divides $(p-1)/2$. Thus $2^{n+2}\mid p-1$, further reducing the candidate factors. At $n=5$, $641=5\cdot128+1$ meets this bound. The restriction matters: $F_1=5$ has factor five, which is not one modulo eight.

= Normal number
{parent=Number theory}
{title2=$\lim_{N\to\infty}F_N(w)=K^{-r}$}
{wiki}

A <real number> is normal in an <integer> base $K\geq2$ when every finite <word> of digits occurs in its <positional notation> with the same limiting <digit block frequency> as every other <word> of the same length. Write its <fractional part> as $x=0.d_1d_2\ldots$ in base $K$, using the expansion that is not eventually all $K-1$ digits when two expansions are possible. For every length $r\geq1$ and every <word> $w\in\{0,\ldots,K-1\}^r$, the requirement is
$$
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\left|\{1\leq j\leq N-r+1:(d_j,\ldots,d_{j+r-1})=w\}\right|}{N-r+1}=K^{-r}.
$$
Occurrences overlap: the <word> $11$ occurs twice in $111$. There are $K^r$ possible <words> of length $r$, so $K^{-r}$ is the equal share for each. In a <decimal representation>, this means frequency $1/10$ for each digit, $1/100$ for each pair, and $1/1000$ for each triple, continuing through every finite length. The definition agrees with that used by Copeland and Erdős in their work on explicit <normal numbers>. https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1946-01.pdf[Arthur H. Copeland and Paul Erdős, Note on Normal Numbers, 1946, opening definition]{ref}

The appeal is that a precise, deterministic <real number> can have the same limiting <digit block frequencies> as repeated draws from a <uniform distribution on a finite set>, with <independence> between draws. <Champernowne constant> is an explicit example. Normality is useful for studying digit distribution, but balanced digits alone establish only a <simply normal number>, and a finite sample cannot establish any of these infinite <limits>.

Émile Borel introduced <normal numbers> and established their prevalence in his 1909 paper applying <probability> to <number theory>; the stronger all-base statement is discussed under <absolutely normal number>. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF03019651[Émile Borel, Les probabilités dénombrables et leurs applications arithmétiques, Rendiconti del Circolo Matematico di Palermo 27, 247–271, 1909]{ref}

= Digit block frequency
{parent=Normal number}
{title2=$F_N(w)$}

For a finite prefix of $N\geq r$ digits in <positional notation>, the digit block frequency $F_N(w)$ of a length-$r$ <word> $w$ is its number of overlapping occurrences divided by $N-r+1$, the number of available starting positions. All such <digit block frequencies> sum to one. The infinite <limit> used for a <normal number> can instead divide counts by $N$: the factor $(N-r+1)/N$ tends to one for fixed $r$.

For the six <binary digits> $010101$, the single-digit counts are three zeros and three ones. Its five adjacent pairs are $01,10,01,10,01$, giving
$$
F_6(00)=F_6(11)=0,\qquad F_6(01)=\frac35,\qquad F_6(10)=\frac25.
$$
This example explains both the overlapping convention and the end correction in a finite sample. The corresponding infinite <binary expansion> has pair frequencies $0,1/2,1/2,0$, rather than the four values $1/4$ required of a <normal number> in base two.

= Simply normal number
{parent=Normal number}
{title2=$\lim_{N\to\infty}F_N(a)=1/K$}

= Simple normality
{synonym}

A <real number> is simply normal in base $K$ if each individual digit in its <positional notation> has limiting <digit block frequency> $1/K$. This is the length-one part of the definition of a <normal number>. It places no equal-frequency requirement on longer <words>.

For example, $x=0.010101\ldots_2=1/3$ is a <simply normal number> in base two: each <binary digit> has frequency $1/2$. But $00$ and $11$ never occur, and $01$ and $10$ each have frequency $1/2$. Thus $x$ is not a <normal number> in base two. Even a <sequence> that looks more complicated, such as the deterministic <Thue–Morse sequence>, must be tested against the actual <digit block frequency> definition; visual complexity is no substitute for it.

The <decimal representation> $y=0.01234567890123456789\ldots$ makes the same point in base ten. Every digit has frequency $1/10$, yet the only adjacent pairs are
$$
01,12,23,34,45,56,67,78,89,90.
$$
Each has limiting <digit block frequency> $1/10$; the other ninety pairs have frequency zero. A <normal number> in base ten requires $1/100$ for every one of the hundred pairs. This <rational number> is a <simply normal number> in base ten, but it is not a <normal number> in that base.

= Champernowne constant
{c}
{parent=Normal number}
{title2=$C_{10}=0.123456789101112\ldots$}
{wiki}

= Champernowne's constant
{synonym}
{c}

Form <Champernowne constant> by using <concatenation> to join the <decimal representations> of the <positive integers> $1,2,3,\ldots$ after the decimal point. Champernowne proved in 1933 that this particular <real number> is a <normal number> in base ten. The construction is striking because its rule is completely explicit, while all finite <words> have the prescribed limiting <digit block frequencies>. https://doi.org/10.1112/jlms/s1-8.4.254[D. G. Champernowne, The Construction of Decimals Normal in the Scale of Ten, Journal of the London Mathematical Society 8, 254–260, 1933]{ref} Copeland and Erdős explicitly recall this result at the start of their own paper. https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1946-01.pdf[Copeland and Erdős, Note on Normal Numbers, 1946, first paragraph]{ref}

The first $100000$ digits of <Champernowne constant> have the following exact counts, in digit order $0,1,\ldots,9$:
$$
(8642,19753,11111,8642,8642,8642,8642,8642,8642,8642).
$$
For the $99999$ adjacent pairs fully inside that prefix, the <words> $00,01,12,99$ occur $642,1753,2088,753$ times respectively. Thus, for example, $F_{100000}(12)=2088/99999$, which is about $0.02088$, rather than $0.01$. The prefix ends partway through the <decimal representation> of $22222$; the many leading ones in earlier <positive integers> help explain the visible imbalance.

= Normality and equidistribution under integer multiplication
{parent=Normal number}
{title2=$x\text{ normal in base }K\iff(\{K^n x\})_{n\geq0}\text{ equidistributed}$}

A <real number> $x$ is a <normal number> in base $K$ exactly when its iterates under the <integer multiplication map on the circle>, $T_K(x)=\{Kx\}$, form an <equidistributed sequence>. Braces denote the <fractional part>. Multiplication by $K$ shifts the <positional notation> one digit to the left:
$$
T_K^n(x)=\{K^nx\}=0.d_{n+1}d_{n+2}\ldots\quad\text{in base }K.
$$
A length-$r$ <word> with base-$K$ <integer> value $a$ specifies the half-open <interval> $[a/K^r,(a+1)/K^r)$, whose length is $K^{-r}$. Visiting that <interval> is exactly encountering that <word> at the next digit position. These digit <intervals> form arbitrarily fine grids, so their correct limiting visiting frequencies imply <equidistribution> on every <interval>. This converts a question about digits into one about a <dynamical system>. Bailey and Crandall develop this connection using <equidistribution> of the <fractional parts> of $K^nx$. https://www.davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/bcnormal.pdf[David H. Bailey and Richard E. Crandall, Random Generators and Normal Numbers, 2003 author manuscript, Section 2]{ref}

In base ten the <word> $12$ corresponds to $[0.12,0.13)$. For <Champernowne constant>, counting the visits $T_{10}^n(x)$ for $0\leq n\leq99998$ gives precisely the $2088$ occurrences of $12$ in its first $100000$ digits. For $x=1/3$, the base-two <integer multiplication map on the circle> instead alternates between $1/3$ and $2/3$; it never visits $[0,1/4)$, the <interval> for $00$. This two-point <orbit> is not an <equidistributed sequence>.

= Absolutely normal number
{parent=Normal number}

= Absolute normality
{synonym}

An absolutely normal number is a <normal number> in every <integer> base $K\geq2$. This requires every finite <word> in every such <positional notation> to have its appropriate limiting <digit block frequency>. Knowing base-ten normality alone does not establish <absolute normality>; the base is part of the statement.

Borel's 1909 result says that <absolute normality> holds <almost everywhere> for <Lebesgue measure>: within $[0,1)$ the exceptions form a <Lebesgue null set>. In particular, the <set> of <absolutely normal numbers> has <Lebesgue measure> one there. This is a statement about the size of an exceptional <set>, not a test for a specified constant. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF03019651[Émile Borel, Les probabilités dénombrables et leurs applications arithmétiques, 1909]{ref} https://www.davidhbailey.com/dhbpapers/normal-pseudo.pdf[Bailey and Borwein, Normal Numbers and Pseudorandom Generators, 2011, introduction: simultaneous normality in all integer bases]{ref}

A modern route uses the <Birkhoff ergodic theorem> and <ergodicity of integer multiplication on the circle>. For each fixed base and digit <interval>, the time proportion equals its <Lebesgue measure> <almost everywhere>. The bases and finite <words> form <countable sets>, so a <countable intersection> of these measure-one <sets> still has <Lebesgue measure> one. For an <absolutely normal number>, the <word> $11$ therefore has frequency $1/4$ in base two, $1/9$ in base three, and $1/100$ in base ten. The same symbols represent different <intervals> in different bases.

= Natural density
{parent=Number theory}
{title2=$d(S)$}
{wiki}

For a <set> $S$ of <nonnegative integers>, its natural density is the limiting proportion of the first $N$ <nonnegative integers> that belong to $S$:
$$
d(S)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{|S\cap\{0,\ldots,N-1\}|}{N},
$$
provided this <limit> exists. Write $C_S(N)=|S\cap\{0,\ldots,N-1\}|$ for the counting <function>. The numerator is a finite <cardinality>; the denominator is exactly $N$, including zero. Every existing <natural density> lies between zero and one.

For the even <nonnegative integers> $E=\{0,2,4,\ldots\}$, there are $m$ elements below $N=2m$ and $m+1$ below $N=2m+1$. Thus $C_E(N)=\lceil N/2\rceil$ using the <ceiling function>, and $d(E)=1/2$. For the <square numbers> $Q=\{0,1,4,9,\ldots\}$, the exact count for $N\geq1$ is
$$
C_Q(N)=\lfloor\sqrt{N-1}\rfloor+1,\qquad d(Q)=0.
$$
The <floor function> counts the allowed indices $1,\ldots,\lfloor\sqrt{N-1}\rfloor$, and the extra one counts zero. At $N=10$, the <square numbers> below ten are $0,1,4,9$, so the proportion is $4/10$; at $N=100$, it is $10/100$. The count grows like the <square root> of $N$, while the denominator grows like $N$.

The <prime numbers> also have <natural density> zero. If $P$ is their <set>, the <prime number theorem> gives $C_P(N)\sim N/\log N$, hence $C_P(N)/N\to0$. The <prime number theorem> is a substantial result about the count, rather than a consequence of the fact that there are infinitely many <prime numbers>. https://math.mit.edu/classes/18.785/2025/LectureNotes16.pdf[Andrew V. Sutherland, MIT 18.785 lecture 16, Riemann's zeta function and the prime number theorem, 2025]{ref}

Why use <natural density> when <cardinality> already measures size? The even <nonnegative integers>, the <square numbers>, and the <prime numbers> all have the same countably infinite <cardinality>, yet their proportions among large initial <integer intervals> differ. <Natural density> distinguishes frequent membership from sparse membership. Equivalently, under a <uniform distribution on a finite set> of $N$ possible <nonnegative integers>, $C_S(N)/N$ is the <probability> of landing in $S$. A <set without natural density> shows why this limiting proportion need not exist.

= Set without natural density
{parent=Natural density}
{title2=$A=\bigcup_{k\geq0}([4^k,2\cdot4^k)\cap\mathbb Z)$}

Include the positive <integers> in $[1,2),[4,8),[16,32),[64,128),\ldots$, and exclude the intervening <integer intervals> and zero. Equivalently, include $[2^{2k},2^{2k+1})\cap\mathbb Z$ for $k\geq0$. Each included block is large enough to raise the running proportion; the following excluded block lowers it again.

With $C_A(N)=|A\cap\{0,\ldots,N-1\}|$, the exact boundary counts follow from a <geometric series>:
$$
C_A(4^k)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}4^j=\frac{4^k-1}{3},\qquad C_A(2\cdot4^k)=\sum_{j=0}^k4^j=\frac{4^{k+1}-1}{3}.
$$
The first sum is empty at $k=0$. Dividing by the corresponding boundary, rather than using one denominator for both, gives
$$
\frac{C_A(4^k)}{4^k}=\frac13-\frac{1}{3\cdot4^k},\qquad\frac{C_A(2\cdot4^k)}{2\cdot4^k}=\frac23-\frac{1}{6\cdot4^k}.
$$
For example, $C_A(16)=1+4=5$, whereas $C_A(32)=1+4+16=21$, yielding proportions $5/16$ and $21/32$. For $4^k\leq N\leq2\cdot4^k$, the exact count is $(4^k-1)/3+N-4^k$; for $2\cdot4^k\leq N\leq4^{k+1}$ it stays $(4^{k+1}-1)/3$. Hence the proportion increases through each included block and decreases through each excluded block. These boundary <subsequences> give the actual <limit inferior> and <limit superior>:
$$
\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{C_A(N)}N=\frac13,\qquad\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{C_A(N)}N=\frac23.
$$
Since these differ, $A$ has no <natural density>. Its <upper asymptotic density> still records its largest limiting proportion.

= Upper asymptotic density
{parent=Natural density}
{title2=$\overline d(A)$}

= Upper natural density
{synonym}

For a <subset> $A$ of the <positive integers>, its upper asymptotic density is
$$
\overline d(A)=\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{|A\cap\{1,\ldots,N\}|}{N}.
$$
The <limit superior> keeps the largest limiting proportion even when the running proportions oscillate. Because they lie in $[0,1]$, this <limit superior> always exists in $[0,1]$. If <natural density> exists, <upper asymptotic density> equals it.

The convention here uses $1,\ldots,N$, while <natural density> above uses $0,\ldots,N-1$. Replacing one interval by the other changes a count by at most one, so the proportions differ by at most $1/N$ and have the same <limit superior>. For the <set without natural density> above, the count in $\{1,\ldots,16\}$ is six rather than five, because $16$ is included and zero is absent. Nevertheless its <upper asymptotic density> is exactly $2/3$. The even <positive integers> have <upper asymptotic density> $1/2$ and the <prime numbers> have <upper asymptotic density> zero.

The useful feature is that a large proportion along arbitrarily large initial <integer intervals> already supplies structure. The <Szemerédi theorem> says that positive <upper asymptotic density> forces <arithmetic progressions> of every finite length, with positive common difference, even without an existing <natural density>. Szemerédi's 1975 paper resolved this general finite-length problem; it makes density a bridge from counting to additive structure. https://doi.org/10.4064/aa-27-1-199-245[Endre Szemerédi, On sets of integers containing k elements in arithmetic progression, Acta Arithmetica 27, 199–245, 1975]{ref}

= Full-density sequence
{parent=Natural density}

An increasing sequence of integers has full density when its range has <natural density> one. <Convergence in density of a sequence> means that, for each fixed tolerance, the exceptional indices have <natural density> zero.

= Lagrange's four-square theorem
{c}
{parent=Number theory}
{title2=$n=a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2$}
{wiki}

= Lagrange four-square theorem
{c}
{synonym}

**Every <nonnegative integer> is a <sum of squares> with four terms, with zero terms allowed.** Precisely, for every <integer> $n\geq0$, there exist <nonnegative integers> $a,b,c,d$ such that
$$
n=a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2.
$$
Allowing arbitrary <integer> roots gives the same statement: changing a root to its <absolute value> leaves the resulting <square number> unchanged. Four is a uniform upper bound on the number of nonzero terms, rather than a demand for four positive terms. Repeated <square numbers> are allowed. Conrad states the positive case and explicitly explains the role of zero terms; $n=0$ follows by taking all four roots zero. https://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/ugradnumthy/descent.pdf[Keith Conrad, Proofs by Descent, Section 6, opening paragraph and Theorem 6.6]{ref}

The small cases make the convention visible:
$$
\begin{aligned}
0&=0^2+0^2+0^2+0^2,\\
1&=1^2+0^2+0^2+0^2,\\
2&=1^2+1^2+0^2+0^2,\\
3&=1^2+1^2+1^2+0^2,\\
7&=2^2+1^2+1^2+1^2.
\end{aligned}
$$
In particular, $1$ could not be a <sum of squares> with four strictly positive roots, since each term would be at least one. Geometrically, a <square> of <integer> side length $a$ has <area> $a^2$: the theorem says that any nonnegative whole-number total area can be split into at most four such square areas. It does not assert that those <squares> tile one larger <square> or a prescribed <rectangle>.

Why does the fourth term matter? Two <square numbers> do not suffice for $3$: in <modular arithmetic>, a <square number> has <remainder> zero or one modulo four, so their sum cannot be three modulo four. The <sum of two squares theorem> gives a complete criterion for the positive <integers> representable with two terms. Even three terms leave gaps. A <square number> has <remainder> $0,1,$ or $4$ modulo eight; adding three of these, with repetition allowed, never gives seven modulo eight. Thus $7,15,23,31,\ldots$ cannot be sums of three <square numbers>, although <Lagrange's four-square theorem> represents all of them with four. This also proves that the bound four cannot be reduced to three.

The modulo-eight obstruction is not the only way that three terms can fail. For example, $28$ needs four: if three <square numbers> added to $28$, their <remainders> modulo four would force all three roots even. Dividing by four would then express $7$ as a sum of three <square numbers>, contradicting the preceding calculation. These short checks explain the examples without requiring a full classification of sums of three <square numbers>.

The practical benefit of <Lagrange's four-square theorem> is an existence guarantee for a family of <Diophantine equations>: for every <nonnegative integer> $n$, the displayed <equation> has an <integer> solution. It does not specify a unique solution. For instance, $65=8^2+1^2+0^2+0^2=7^2+4^2+0^2+0^2=6^2+4^2+3^2+2^2$. To find a representation by a finite search, enumerate $0\leq d\leq c\leq b\leq a\leq\lfloor\sqrt n\rfloor$ and test the sum. The <floor function> supplies a finite bound because no single term can exceed $n$, and sorting roots removes duplicate orderings without discarding any possible sum. The theorem guarantees that the search succeeds.

A <greedy algorithm> that repeatedly subtracts the largest available <square number> need not find four terms: for $23$ it chooses $16+4+1+1+1$, while $23=9+9+4+1$ uses four. The appeal of the theorem is this gap between local choices and a global promise: individual <square numbers> become farther apart, and two or three terms leave arithmetic obstructions, yet four terms cover every <nonnegative integer>.

For historical orientation, Conrad dates <Lagrange's four-square theorem> to $1770$ and identifies the memoir as *Démonstration d'un théorème d'arithmétique*: it appeared in the Berlin Academy's volume for the year $1770$, published in $1772$, pages $123$–$133$. Keeping the volume year and publication year distinct avoids an apparent conflict between dates. https://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/ugradnumthy/descent.pdf[Keith Conrad, Proofs by Descent, Theorem 6.6 and reference 11]{ref}

= Exponentiation by squaring
{parent=Number theory}
{wiki}
{title2=$a^n$}

= Repeated squaring
{synonym}

= Fast exponentiation
{synonym}

= Binary exponentiation
{synonym}

Exponentiation by squaring computes an <integer> power by reading the exponent's <binary expansion>. Appending a <binary digit> $b$ to a prefix of value $p$ replaces $p$ by $2p+b$, so the corresponding power changes by
$$
a^{2p+b}=(a^p)^2a^b,\qquad b\in\{0,1\}.
$$
Each new digit requires one squaring and, only for digit one, one further <multiplication>. The number of digits grows with the <logarithm> of the exponent, making this useful for <modular exponentiation>, <primality testing>, and powers of <matrices> using <matrix multiplication>.<A Computational Introduction to Number Theory and Algebra by Victor Shoup>[Section 3.4, pp. 65–66: binary-prefix recurrence and repeated-squaring algorithm; p. 67: primality-testing application]{ref}

For a concrete calculation, $13=(1101)_2=8+4+1$, so $3^{13}=3^8\cdot3^4\cdot3$. The **optimized left-to-right <algorithm> used here** instead keeps one accumulated power: initialize $r=3$ from the leading digit one, then process only the remaining digits $1,0,1$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
1&:\quad r=3,\\
11&:\quad r=3^2\cdot3=27,\\
110&:\quad r=27^2=729,\\
1101&:\quad r=729^2\cdot3=531441\cdot3=1594323.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus **$3^{13}=1594323$**, using three squarings and two further <multiplications>, instead of twelve successive <multiplications> starting at $3$.

\Image[binary-powering-prefix-and-modular-trace.png]
{title=Reading the same <binary expansion> computes $3^{13}$ and $2^{13}\bmod17$}
{description=Each appended <binary digit> changes the prefix value by $p\mapsto2p+b$. The ordinary-power row grows to $1594323$; the <modular exponentiation> row stores only remainders $2,8,13,15$. Both traces use three squarings and two further <multiplications>.}

For a general positive <integer> $n=(b_{\ell-1}\cdots b_0)_2$ with leading digit $b_{\ell-1}=1$, set $r=a$ and, for $i=\ell-2,\ldots,0$, first replace $r$ by $r^2$, then multiply it by $a$ if $b_i=1$. Return $r$. This explicitly skips the leading digit's otherwise unnecessary operations on one. <A Computational Introduction to Number Theory and Algebra by Victor Shoup> (Section 3.4, pp. 65–66) presents the version initialized at one and processing every digit; the optimization here makes the counting convention unambiguous.

The <loop invariant> is **$r=a^p$, where $p$ is the value of the digits already read**. Initially $p=1$ and $r=a$. If the next digit is $b$, then squaring and optionally multiplying give $r'=a^{2p+b}$, exactly the next prefix. At termination $p=n$. This proof uses <associativity> of <multiplication>; it works in any <monoid>, including <matrices> with the <identity matrix> as <identity element>, and needs no <commutativity> between different bases.

For $n=0$, return the <identity element> without performing a <multiplication>. This is the empty-product convention $a^0=1$; if $a=0$, the <algorithm> therefore returns one for $0^0$ by convention, rather than resolving its meaning in every mathematical setting. Negative <integer> exponents require an <inverse element>: compute $(a^{-1})^{-n}$, counting the inversion separately. Over the <integers>, such an inverse generally leaves the <integers>, and a zero base has no inverse.

= Coupled binary exponentiation
{parent=Exponentiation by squaring}
{title2=$p x^k=A^n,\quad q y^l=B^m$}

To compute two nonnegative <integer> powers in one <while loop>, maintain a separate accumulator, current base and remaining exponent for each <exponentiation by squaring>. The paired <loop invariant> is $p x^k=A^n$ and $q y^l=B^m$. For each positive remaining exponent, transfer its odd factor to its accumulator and halve the exponent while squaring its base. A zero remaining exponent is left unchanged. The mathematical <integer> $k+l$ is a <loop variant>: at least one positive term strictly decreases in every iteration. Both powers are therefore complete when the loop stops. The iteration count is $O(\log(\max(n,m)+1))$, with <constant space> and a constant number of <multiplications> per iteration. This is an exact-arithmetic identity; <floating-point rounding> introduces a separate error in a numerical implementation.

= Simultaneous exponentiation by squaring
{parent=Exponentiation by squaring}
{title2=$p_j u_j^k=a_j^n$}

Several powers with one nonnegative exponent can share a single exponent-halving loop. For bases $a_j$, maintain accumulators $p_j$, current bases $u_j$ and a remaining exponent $k$, with <loop invariants> $p_j u_j^k=a_j^n$. For an odd $k$, multiply every accumulator by its current base; square every current base and halve $k$. The equalities are preserved in parallel, and the nonnegative <loop variant> $k$ strictly decreases. For a fixed number of bases this uses $O(\log(n+1))$ arithmetic operations and one loop, while keeping each base in the intended numeric type.

= Binary powering multiplication count
{parent=Exponentiation by squaring}
{title2=$C(n)=\ell+\operatorname{wt}(n)-2$}

For the optimized left-to-right <exponentiation by squaring> <algorithm>, let $\ell=\lfloor\log_2 n\rfloor+1$ be the length of the <binary expansion> of a positive <integer> $n$, and let $w=\operatorname{wt}(n)$ be its <Hamming weight>, the number of digits equal to one. There are exactly $\ell-1$ digits after the leading one, and exactly $w-1$ remaining ones. Therefore the count is
$$
S(n)=\ell-1,\qquad T(n)=w-1,\qquad C(n)=S(n)+T(n)=\ell+w-2.
$$
Here $S$ counts squarings and $T$ counts further <multiplications> by the base; **every squaring counts as one <multiplication> in $C$**. Assignment, digit reading, and any inversion are outside this count. The formula includes $n=1$, which requires zero <multiplications>; define $C(0)=0$ separately.

For a fixed length $\ell$, the bounds are $\ell-1\leq C(n)\leq2\ell-2$. A <power of two> reaches the lower bound because its <Hamming weight> is one; $n=2^\ell-1$ reaches the upper bound because all digits are one. For $n=13$, $\ell=4$ and $w=3$, giving $C(13)=5$. Thus the <arithmetic operation> count is $O(\log(n+1))$ in <Big O notation>, but inputs of similar magnitude can have different counts.

This is a count for the specified <algorithm>, not a claim that it always minimizes <multiplications> among all methods. For instance, at $n=15$ the count is six, but the powers with exponents $1,2,3,6,12,15$ can be built in five <multiplications>: square, multiply by $a$, square, square, then multiply by the already stored $a^3$. Saving operations this way uses a different schedule and stored intermediate powers.

= Modular exponentiation
{parent=Exponentiation by squaring}
{title2=$a^b\bmod m$}

= Modular power
{synonym}

Modular exponentiation computes the <remainder> $a^n\bmod m$ for an <integer> base $a$, a <nonnegative integer> exponent $n$, and, here, an <integer> modulus $m\geq2$. It applies <exponentiation by squaring> while reducing after **each** <multiplication>, including each squaring. The result is the unique representative in $\{0,\ldots,m-1\}$. There is no requirement that the base be <coprime> to the modulus when the exponent is nonnegative.<A Computational Introduction to Number Theory and Algebra by Victor Shoup>[Section 3.4, pp. 64–66: canonical representatives, modular products, and nonnegative-exponent repeated squaring]{ref}

Take $a=2$, $n=13=(1101)_2$, and $m=17$. Initialize $r=2$; the remaining digits give
$$
\begin{aligned}
11&:\quad 2^2\bmod17=4,\quad4\cdot2\bmod17=8,\\
110&:\quad 8^2\bmod17=64\bmod17=13,\\
1101&:\quad13^2\bmod17=169\bmod17=16,\quad16\cdot2\bmod17=15.
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence **$2^{13}\bmod17=15$**. The final operation multiplies $16$ by $2$, since the preceding square has already been reduced. The computation never needs to construct $2^{13}=8192$.

In general reduce the base first, $x=a\bmod m$. For $n>0$, initialize $r=x$ and process the remaining <binary digits> with $r\leftarrow r^2\bmod m$, followed, for a digit one, by $r\leftarrow rx\bmod m$. The <loop invariant> is
$$
0\leq r<m,\qquad r\equiv a^p\pmod m,
$$
where $p$ is the processed prefix value. The congruence is preserved because <modular arithmetic> respects <multiplication>: if $u\equiv v\pmod m$, then $u^2\equiv v^2$ and $ux\equiv va\pmod m$. Thus changing representatives never changes the final <congruence class>.

Both factors in every product are less than $m$, so the unreduced product is less than $m^2$, and the stored result is again less than $m$. The same bounds hold for a gigantic exponent, such as $n=2^{1000}$: there are $1000$ squarings, each on bounded-size residues. For base two, the corresponding ordinary power would have $2^{1000}+1$ <binary digits>. The benefit is avoiding the huge ordinary power altogether, rather than constructing it and taking one final <remainder>. Initial reduction is essential when the supplied base is much larger than the modulus.

For $n=0$, return $1$ under the same empty-product convention as <exponentiation by squaring>. For $n=-k<0$, require $\gcd(a,m)=1$, find the <modular multiplicative inverse> $a^{-1}\bmod m$, and compute $(a^{-1})^k\bmod m$; the <extended Euclidean algorithm> can find that inverse. Its cost is separate from the positive-exponent <binary powering multiplication count>. For example, $2^{-1}\equiv9\pmod{17}$, while $2^{-1}\bmod4$ is undefined because $2$ and $4$ are not <coprime>.

A <reversible computation> gives the coherent <quantum modular exponentiation> used in <quantum order finding> and <discrete-logarithm Fourier sampling>.

= Bit cost of modular exponentiation
{parent=Modular exponentiation}
{title2=$O(\ell L^2)$}

The <binary powering multiplication count> counts <arithmetic operations>. The <time complexity> measured in operations on individual <bits> also depends on operand lengths. Put $L=\lfloor\log_2m\rfloor+1$, the binary length of the modulus, and let $\ell$ be the positive exponent's binary length. Stored residues use at most $L$ <bits>, and an unreduced product uses at most $2L$ <bits>.

With ordinary long <multiplication> and <division with remainder>, one modular product costs $O(L^2)$ operations on <bits>. Combining that cost with $O(\ell)$ modular products gives
$$
\text{bit cost of the powering loop}=O(\ell L^2).
$$
This bound includes reductions in the loop, and assumes the base has already been reduced and the exponent is available as a <binary expansion>. Reading the exponent takes $O(\ell)$ additional work; initial reduction of a very long base depends on its input length, and an inverse for a negative exponent adds further work. More generally, if a modular product costs $R(L)$ operations on <bits>, the powering loop costs $O(\ell R(L))$. Faster <multiplication> and reduction methods can improve the quadratic bound.<A Computational Introduction to Number Theory and Algebra by Victor Shoup>[Section 3.3.5, pp. 62–63, Theorem 3.3: operand-length costs; Section 3.4, p. 65: modular multiplication and powering bounds; Section 3.5, pp. 69–71: faster arithmetic]{ref}

For ordinary <integer> powers with $|a|\geq2$, the output itself has $\lfloor n\log_2|a|\rfloor+1$ <binary digits>. Even writing it takes work proportional to that length. Consequently $O(\log(n+1))$ <multiplications> does not imply $O(\log(n+1))$ operations on <bits>: the operands grow during ordinary <exponentiation by squaring>, whereas <modular exponentiation> keeps them bounded by the modulus. A squaring or multiplication by a small fixed base can also cost less than a general product, so equal counts need not mean equal running times.

= Composite number

= Composite
{synonym}

A <composite number> is an <integer> greater than one that is not a <prime number>. Equivalently, it has a factorization $n=ab$ with <integers> $a,b>1$. Thus $4=2\cdot2$, $9=3\cdot3$, and $12=3\cdot4$ are <composite numbers>; one is neither a <composite number> nor a <prime number>.

Every <composite number> has a <prime factor> at most its <square root>. In $91=7\cdot13$, seven is the smaller factor and lies below $\sqrt{91}$; in $49=7^2$, equality occurs. This square-root bound makes <trial division> finite and lets the <sieve of Eratosthenes> stop marking with <prime numbers> beyond $\sqrt N$ when listing <prime numbers> up to $N$.

= Semiprime
{parent=Composite number}
{title2=$N=pq$}
{wiki}

A <semiprime> is a <composite number> that is a product of exactly two <prime numbers>, counted with multiplicity. For example, $6=2\cdot3$ and $9=3^2$ are semiprimes. Distinct factors give $\varphi(pq)=(p-1)(q-1)$ for the <Euler totient function>, whereas equal factors give $\varphi(p^2)=p(p-1)$. This distinction matters when constructing a <uniform coprime state for a semiprime>.

= Euler's theorem
{parent=Number theory}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$a^{\varphi(n)}\equiv1\pmod n$}

= Fermat-Euler theorem
{c}
{synonym}

For an <integer modulus> $n\geq2$ and an <integer> $a$ with <greatest common divisor> $\gcd(a,n)=1$, Euler's theorem, also called the <Fermat-Euler theorem>, states
$$
a^{\varphi(n)}\equiv1\pmod n.
$$
Here <Euler's totient function> $\varphi(n)$ counts the <units> in the <unit group> $U(n)=(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^\times$. These <congruence classes> have <modular multiplicative inverses>, equivalently their representatives are <coprime> to $n$. The guaranteed return to one permits <exponent reduction for units>.<Euler’s Theorem by Keith Conrad>[p. 1, Theorem 1.2 and equation (1.1): modulus at least two, invertible classes and totient]{ref}

For a <prime number> $p$, $\varphi(p)=p-1$, recovering <Fermat's little theorem> in the form $a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p$ when $p\nmid a$. Multiplying by $a$ and separately handling $p\mid a$ gives $a^p\equiv a\pmod p$ for every <integer> $a$. For a <composite number> used as the <integer modulus>, a nonzero <congruence class> can be a <nonunit>.

Modulo twelve, the <units> have representatives $1,5,7,11$, so <Euler's totient function> gives $\varphi(12)=4$. <Multiplication> by five produces
$$
1\longmapsto5\longmapsto1,\qquad
7\longmapsto11\longmapsto7\pmod{12}.
$$
Thus this <permutation> has two <permutation cycles>, each of length two. In particular $5^2\equiv1\pmod{12}$, and the guaranteed return $5^4\equiv1$ follows. **The number of <units> need not be the length of one <permutation cycle>.** Modulo fifteen, <multiplication> by seven similarly gives the two <permutation cycles> $(1,7,4,13)$ and $(2,14,8,11)$, covering all eight <units>. The powers of seven follow the <permutation cycle> containing one; the other <permutation cycle> shows that these powers do not visit every <unit>.

There is a stronger obstruction for a <nonunit> than just an occasional failed calculation. If a <positive integer> $d>1$ divides both $a$ and $n$, then $a^k\equiv0\pmod d$ for every $k\geq1$, so $a^k$ cannot be congruent to one modulo $n$. For example, $\gcd(3,15)=3$ and $3^{\varphi(15)}=3^8\equiv6\pmod{15}$, rather than one. Such a base has no <multiplicative order> modulo that <integer modulus>.<Orders of Units in Modular Arithmetic by Keith Conrad>[p. 2, explanation following Example 1.8: a positive-exponent return to one forces coprimality]{ref}

= Multiplication by a unit permutes reduced residues
{parent=Euler's theorem}
{title2=$u\mapsto au\quad\text{on }U(n)$}

Choose a <reduced residue system> $u_1,\ldots,u_{\varphi(n)}$. For a <unit modulo n> $a$, the <bijection> $u\mapsto au$ on the <unit group> has inverse $u\mapsto a^{-1}u$. Comparing products before and after this <permutation> gives
$$
P=\prod_{i=1}^{\varphi(n)}u_i
\equiv\prod_{i=1}^{\varphi(n)}(au_i)
=a^{\varphi(n)}P\pmod n.
$$
The product $P$ is a <unit>; its <modular inverse> cancels it, proving <Euler's theorem>. Merely being nonzero would not justify cancellation.<Euler’s Theorem by Keith Conrad>[pp. 2–3, Section 2: permutation of the units, comparison of products, cancellation]{ref}

In the modulo-twelve example, $P=1\cdot5\cdot7\cdot11=385\equiv1\pmod{12}$. The permuted representatives $5,1,11,7$ have the same product, while the unreduced products $(5\cdot1)(5\cdot5)(5\cdot7)(5\cdot11)$ equal $5^4P$. Equating these products modulo twelve exposes the fourth power without calculating it directly. Using all nonzero <congruence classes> instead would include <nonunits>, whose product need not permit cancellation.

= Exponent reduction for units
{parent=Euler's theorem}
{title2=$a^k\equiv a^{k\bmod\varphi(n)}\pmod n$}

For a <unit modulo n> $a$ and a <nonnegative integer> $k$, <division with remainder> writes $k=q\varphi(n)+r$ with $0\leq r<\varphi(n)$. <Euler's theorem> then gives
$$
a^k=(a^{\varphi(n)})^q a^r\equiv a^r\pmod n.
$$
This replaces a potentially enormous <exponent> by a small <remainder>. The <exponent> itself is reduced modulo <Euler's totient function>, while the powers are reduced modulo $n$: these are different <integer moduli>.

For a worked modulo-fifteen calculation, seven is <coprime> to fifteen and $\varphi(15)=8$. Therefore
$$
2029=253\cdot8+5,\qquad 7^{2029}\equiv7^5\pmod{15}.
$$
Use <exponentiation by squaring> on the remaining <exponent> $5=(101)_2$, reducing after each <multiplication>:
$$
7^2\equiv49\equiv4,\qquad
7^4\equiv4^2\equiv1,\qquad
7^5\equiv1\cdot7\equiv7\pmod{15}.
$$
Thus **$7^{2029}\bmod15=7$**. <Modular exponentiation> supplies the computation; <Euler's theorem> supplies the optional shortening of its <exponent>. <Exponentiation by squaring> also works for <nonunit> bases, without using this shortening.

The guaranteed <exponent> $\varphi(n)$ is a common return <exponent> and an upper bound on the shortest period; it need not be that period. The <multiplicative order> $d=\operatorname{ord}_n(a)$ is the least positive return <exponent> for the chosen <unit>. The <Carmichael function> $\lambda(n)$ is the least positive return <exponent> that works for every <unit>. Their precise relation is
$$
\operatorname{ord}_n(a)\mid\lambda(n)\mid\varphi(n).
$$
Either smaller return <exponent> can replace $\varphi(n)$ in <exponent reduction for units>. Modulo fifteen, $\operatorname{ord}_{15}(7)=\lambda(15)=4<8=\varphi(15)$, so $2029=507\cdot4+1$ gives $7^{2029}\equiv7$ immediately. For base four the <multiplicative order> is only two, smaller even than $\lambda(15)$. A return at <exponent> eight alone does not prove that the <multiplicative order> is eight.<Orders of Units in Modular Arithmetic by Keith Conrad>[pp. 1–3, Definition 1.3, the warning on p. 2, Theorem 2.1, Corollary 2.2 and Theorem 2.4]{ref}

\frac{a^{2p}-1}{a^2-1}

= Fermat pseudoprime
{parent=Number theory}
{c}
{wiki}

An odd composite number $N$ is a Fermat pseudoprime to a coprime base $b$ when
$$
b^{N-1}\equiv1\pmod N.
$$

A <Fermat pseudoprime> is relative to its base. For example, $341=11\cdot31$ passes base $2$, but base $3$ yields $3^{340}\equiv56\pmod{341}$, a <Fermat witness>. A <Carmichael number> has the stronger all-coprime-base property. This explains why finding one deceptive base and proving universal deception require different arguments.

= Strong pseudoprime
{parent=Fermat pseudoprime}
{wiki}

Write the even number $N-1$ as $2^s m$ with $m$ odd. An odd composite number $N$ is a strong pseudoprime to a coprime base $b$ when
$$
b^m\equiv1\pmod N
$$
or $b^{2^jm}\equiv-1\pmod N$ for some $0\leq j<s$.

= Strong-pseudoprime bases for a product of two primes with common predecessor divisor ten
{parent=Strong pseudoprime}

Let $N=pq$ for distinct odd <prime numbers> $p,q$ with $\gcd(p-1,q-1)=10$. Exactly $100$ unit bases make $N$ a <Fermat pseudoprime>, and exactly $50$ of these make it a <strong pseudoprime>. The Fermat bases form $C_{10}\times C_{10}$ under the <Chinese remainder theorem for unit groups>. If $N-1=2^sm$, then $5\mid m$ and $m$ is odd, so the $m$th-power map sends five elements of each $C_{10}$ to $1$ and five to $-1$. The strong condition requires equal signs in both components, giving $5^2+5^2=50$.

= Euler pseudoprime
{parent=Number theory}
{c}
{wiki}

An odd composite number $N$ is an Euler pseudoprime to a coprime base $b$ when
$$
b^{(N-1)/2}\equiv\left(\frac bN\right)\pmod N,
$$
where the right side is the Jacobi symbol.

= Euler witness
{parent=Euler pseudoprime}

For an odd integer $N$ and a unit $b$ modulo $N$, the base $b$ is an Euler witness when
$$
b^{(N-1)/2}\not\equiv\left(\frac bN\right)\pmod N.
$$
It therefore certifies that $N$ is composite.

= Euler witnesses occupy at least half the unit bases
{parent=Euler witness}

If one Euler witness $b_0$ exists modulo $N$, multiplication by $b_0$ injects the set of nonwitnesses into the set of witnesses. Indeed, the product of $b_0$ with a nonwitness cannot be another nonwitness, because cancellation and multiplicativity of the <Jacobi symbol> would make $b_0$ a nonwitness. Thus at least half of the units modulo $N$ are Euler witnesses.

= Existence of an Euler witness for every odd composite integer
{parent=Euler witness}

Every odd composite integer has an <Euler witness>. For a nonsquarefree integer, a base congruent to $1+p$ modulo a repeated prime factor $p^2$ fails the Euler congruence by the <binomial theorem>. For a squarefree integer, use the <Chinese remainder theorem> to choose a base that is a <quadratic nonresidue> modulo one prime factor and $1$ modulo another. Its <Jacobi symbol> is $-1$, while its power is $1$ modulo the second factor.

= Solovay-Strassen primality test
{c}
{parent=Euler witness}
{wiki=Solovay–Strassen_primality_test}

The Solovay-Strassen primality test samples unit bases and rejects an odd candidate as composite when it finds an <Euler witness>. Every prime passes every base by <Euler's criterion>, while an odd composite passes a random base with probability at most one half.

= P-adic valuation
{parent=Number theory}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$v_p$}

= Prime exponent
{synonym}

For a <prime number> $p$ and a nonzero <integer> $N$, the <P-adic valuation> counts the exponent of $p$ in its <prime factorization>:
$$
v_p(N)=\max\{a\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}:p^a\mid N\}.
$$
For $72=2^3\cdot3^2$, it gives $v_2(72)=3$, $v_3(72)=2$, and $v_5(72)=0$. The sign of a nonzero <integer> does not change these <prime exponents>. Relative to an ordered <factor base>, the valuations supply the coordinates of its <exponent vector>.

The <P-adic valuation> extends to nonzero <rational numbers> by $v_p(a/b)=v_p(a)-v_p(b)$. For example, $v_5(72/25)=-2$ records a factor in the denominator instead of the numerator. <Multiplication> adds valuations, $v_p(xy)=v_p(x)+v_p(y)$; this is the arithmetic reason why adding exponent vectors can make a product a square. In particular, a positive <integer> is a <square of a number> precisely when all its <prime exponents> are <even integers>, the criterion used by a <factor base>.

= p-adic integer
{title2=$\mathbb Z_p$}
{c}
{parent=P-adic valuation}

The <p-adic integers> retain arithmetic information modulo arbitrarily high powers of one <prime number> $p$. They form the <subring>
$$
\boxed{\mathbb Z_p=\{x\in\mathbb Q_p:|x|_p\leq1\}}
$$
of the <P-adic numbers>. Their <p-adic absolute value> bounds the allowed denominators: **$1/3$ belongs to $\mathbb Z_2$, while $1/2$ does not**. The name therefore describes a local arithmetic <ring>, not just the ordinary <integers>.<P-adic numbers by Jan-Hendrik Evertse>[§3, printed pp. 6–7: definition of Zp, rational denominators and units]{ref}

A concrete description gives an element by a compatible <sequence> of <congruence classes>:
$$
\mathbb Z_p=\varprojlim_{n\geq1}\mathbb Z/p^n\mathbb Z,\qquad
r_{n+1}\equiv r_n\pmod{p^n}.
$$
The <inverse limit> condition says that gaining more precision must preserve the digits already determined. Each $r_n$ is a residue modulo $p^n$; choosing its least nonnegative <integer> representative gives $0\leq r_n<p^n$. The ordinary integer $13$ gives residues $1,1,5,13$ modulo $2,4,8,16$.

The useful feature is that one exact object organizes an unlimited family of finite <modular arithmetic> calculations. <Addition> and <multiplication> are performed modulo each $p^n$, and the results remain compatible. A <p-adic digit> expansion makes this concrete:
$$
x=\sum_{j\geq0}b_jp^j,\qquad b_j\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\},\qquad
r_n=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}b_jp^j.
$$
The expansion is unique and converges in the <p-adic metric>; its higher powers have smaller <p-adic absolute value>. The <digit agreement and p-adic distance> criterion tells exactly which finite digits a precision bound preserves.<P-adic numbers by Jan-Hendrik Evertse>[§3, Theorem 3.4 and digit/congruence discussion, printed pp. 7–9: unique expansion and agreement of low-order digits]{ref}

= p-adic digit
{c}
{parent=p-adic integer}
{title2=$b_j\in\{0,\ldots,p-1\}$}

The <p-adic digits> are the unique coefficients in $x=\sum_{j\geq0}b_jp^j$ for a <p-adic integer> $x$. Taking least nonnegative representatives of its <congruence classes>,
$$
r_{n+1}=r_n+b_np^n,
$$
so each new residue determines one more <p-adic digit>. For $13$ in $\mathbb Z_2$, the low-to-high digits are $1,0,1,1,0,\ldots$, giving $13=1+0\cdot2+1\cdot4+1\cdot8$. A finite prefix describes a whole <coset>, not a unique infinite expansion.

= Digit agreement and p-adic distance
{parent=p-adic digit}
{title2=$x\equiv y\pmod{p^n}\iff d_p(x,y)\leq p^{-n}$}

Two <p-adic integers> agree in their first $n$ low-order <p-adic digits> precisely when their difference is divisible by $p^n$. Equivalently,
$$
x\equiv y\pmod{p^n}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
v_p(x-y)\geq n\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
|x-y|_p\leq p^{-n}.
$$
If the first differing digit has index $j$, the remaining leading coefficient is nonzero modulo $p$, giving exact <P-adic valuation> $j$ and <p-adic metric> $p^{-j}$. If no digit differs, the two elements are equal and their distance is zero. Truncating at $r_n$ therefore gives $|x-r_n|_p\leq p^{-n}$, with a stricter bound possible when further digits are zero.

= Modular inversion converges to a p-adic inverse
{parent=p-adic integer}
{title2=$r_n\equiv a^{-1}\pmod{p^n}\ \Longrightarrow\ r_n\to a^{-1}$}

Let $a$ be an <integer> not divisible by $p$. Its <modular inverse> modulo $p^n$ has a unique least nonnegative representative $r_n$. Reducing the inverse equation modulo $p^n$ shows that the representatives are compatible. Since $a$ is a <p-adic unit>,
$$
|r_n-a^{-1}|_p=|ar_n-1|_p\leq p^{-n},
$$
so the sequence converges to the inverse $a^{-1}$ in $\mathbb Z_p$. This computes a fraction through finite <integer> operations.

For $a=3$ and $p=2$, the first eight representatives are **$1,3,3,11,11,43,43,171$**. Each satisfies $3r_n\equiv1\pmod{2^n}$. The resulting expansion is
$$
\boxed{\frac13=1+2+8+32+\cdots
=1+\sum_{k\geq0}2^{2k+1}\quad\text{in }\mathbb Z_2.}
$$
The <geometric series> has ratio $4$, whose <2-adic absolute value> is $1/4$, and its sum is $1+2/(1-4)=1/3$. In the ordinary <real number> <absolute value>, the representatives grow. Different <metrics> impose different meanings of a good approximation. The periodic digits here are a specific instance of the rational expansions studied in Conrad's authored note.<The p-adic expansion of rational numbers by Keith Conrad>[§1, printed p. 1: p-adic rational expansions and explicit low-order-first convention; §3, Theorem 3.1, printed pp. 3–5: eventual periodicity]{ref}

= Ring structure of the p-adic integers
{parent=p-adic integer}

The <p-adic integers> form a <complete metric space> and a <discrete valuation ring>. Every nonzero $x$ is uniquely $p^k u$ with $k=v_p(x)\geq0$ and $u$ a <p-adic unit>. The prime $p$ is a <uniformizer>, and reduction modulo $p$ gives the <residue field> $\mathbb F_p$. The element $p$ has no inverse inside $\mathbb Z_p$, so the ring is not a <field>.

For a short ideal-classification outline, take a nonzero <ideal> and choose an element of smallest <P-adic valuation> $k$, which exists by the <well-ordering principle>. Its unit factor is invertible, so the ideal contains $p^k$. Every other element is divisible by $p^k$, proving that the ideal is $p^k\mathbb Z_p$. Completeness follows because $\mathbb Z_p$ is a <closed subset> of the <complete metric space> $\mathbb Q_p$. The zero ideal is separate from this nonzero-ideal classification.

= Open subgroups of the p-adic integers
{parent=p-adic integer}
{title2=$H\text{ open}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ [\mathbb Z_p:H]<\infty$}

The cosets $a+p^n\mathbb Z_p$ form a neighborhood basis in the additive group of <p-adic integers>. An open subgroup contains $p^n\mathbb Z_p$ for some $n$, so has finite index. Conversely, if the index is $m$, the finite quotient is annihilated by $m$, giving $m\mathbb Z_p\subset H$. Multiplication by the unit part of $m$ is bijective, so $m\mathbb Z_p=p^{v_p(m)}\mathbb Z_p$ is open. This proof applies to abstract subgroups without assuming beforehand that they are closed.

= 7-adic integer
{parent=p-adic integer}
{title2=$\mathbb Z_7$}

A 7-adic integer is an element of the <p-adic integers> for $p=7$, equivalently a compatible <sequence> of residues modulo $7^r$. Its <p-adic digits> lie in $\{0,1,\ldots,6\}$. For example, $10=3+1\cdot7$ has low-to-high digits $3,1,0,\ldots$.

= 2-adic integer
{parent=p-adic integer}

= Legendre formula
{c}
{synonym}

For a <nonnegative integer> $n$ and a <prime number> $p$, the <Legendre formula> computes the <prime exponent> in a <factorial> without forming that huge product:
$$
v_p(n!)=\sum_{j\geq1}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{p^j}\right\rfloor.
$$
Here $\lfloor x\rfloor$ is the <floor function>. The sum is finite: every term with $p^j>n$ is zero. When $n=0$, all terms vanish, agreeing with $0!=1$ and $v_p(1)=0$. The <prime number> hypothesis matters: a <composite number> does not give the same <P-adic valuation> or additive <prime exponent>. For example, the floor sum with $n=6,p=4$ is one, whereas $4^2$ divides $6!=720$.

Why count multiples repeatedly? Each multiple of $p$ in $1,\ldots,n$ contributes one factor of $p$; each multiple of $p^2$ contributes another; and so on. A factor $m$ with <P-adic valuation> $a$ occurs in exactly the first $a$ counts. Adding those contributions proves the formula.<Number Theory for Mathematical Contests by David Anthony Santos>[David A. Santos, Number Theory for Mathematical Contests, revision 31 October 2007, §8.3, Theorem 245, printed pp. 89–90: factorial valuation and digit form]{ref}

For the original example $n=50,p=3$,
$$
v_3(50!)=\left\lfloor\frac{50}{3}\right\rfloor+
\left\lfloor\frac{50}{9}\right\rfloor+
\left\lfloor\frac{50}{27}\right\rfloor
=16+5+1=22.
$$
The five multiples $9,18,27,36,45$ each contribute a second factor of three. The factor $27$ contributes a third, so it appears in all three rows. Equivalently, among $1,\ldots,50$ there are eleven factors of <P-adic valuation> one, four of valuation two, and one of valuation three: $11+2\cdot4+3\cdot1=22$. Thus $3^{22}$ divides $50!$, while $3^{23}$ does not.

Repeated <Euclidean division> provides a practical evaluation: replace $q$ by $\lfloor q/p\rfloor$, starting from $q=n$, and add each new quotient until it is zero. This uses only the successive quotients $16,5,1,0$ for the example. The same calculation handles <divisibility> of <binomial coefficients>, where the <P-adic valuations> of the denominator <factorials> are subtracted.

= Base-p digit sum
{title2=$d_p(n)$}
{parent=P-adic valuation}

For a <nonnegative integer> $n$ and <integer> base $p\geq2$, write its <base-p expansion> as
$$
n=\sum_{r\geq0}\alpha_rp^r,
\qquad 0\leq\alpha_r<p,
$$
The base-$p$ digit sum is $d_p(n)=\sum_r\alpha_r$, with $d_p(0)=0$. When $p$ is a <prime number>, the <Legendre formula> becomes
$$
v_p(n!)=\frac{n-d_p(n)}{p-1}.
$$
To see the connection, divide the <base-p expansion> by $p^j$ and discard the fractional part. Swapping the resulting finite sums and using a <finite geometric series> gives
$$
\sum_{j\geq1}\left\lfloor\frac n{p^j}\right\rfloor
=\sum_{r\geq1}\alpha_r\sum_{j=1}^r p^{r-j}
=\sum_{r\geq0}\alpha_r\frac{p^r-1}{p-1}
=\frac{n-d_p(n)}{p-1}.
$$
In particular, $50=1\cdot27+2\cdot9+1\cdot3+2=(1212)_3$, so $d_3(50)=6$ and $v_3(50!)=(50-6)/2=22$. The leading <numeral digit> contributes thirteen, the next contributes eight, the next one, and the units contribute zero. Thus the <base-p digit sum> compresses the same contribution ledger into information already present in <positional notation>.

= Subadditivity of the base-p digit sum
{parent=Base-p digit sum}

For <nonnegative integers> $x,y$ and an <integer> base $p\geq2$,
$$
d_p(x+y)\leq d_p(x)+d_p(y).
$$
In column <addition>, every <arithmetic carry> replaces $p$ units in one <numeral digit> by one unit in the next position and therefore decreases the <base-p digit sum> by $p-1$. Count each outgoing carry separately, including each step of a chain. There is equality precisely when there are no carries. For <prime number> bases, <Kummer's theorem> turns this digit-sum deficit into a <prime exponent>.

= Kummer's theorem
{parent=P-adic valuation}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$v_p\binom nk=(d_p(k)+d_p(n-k)-d_p(n))/(p-1)$}

For a <prime number> $p$ and <integers> $0\leq k\leq n$, the <P-adic valuation> of the <binomial coefficient> $\binom nk$ equals the number of <arithmetic carries> in the base-$p$ <addition> of $k$ and $n-k$.<Number Theory for Mathematical Contests by David Anthony Santos>[David A. Santos, Number Theory for Mathematical Contests, revision 31 October 2007, §8.3, Theorem 246, printed p. 90: carry count for a binomial coefficient]{ref} Indeed, subtract the two denominator <factorial> valuations in the <Legendre formula>:
$$
v_p\binom nk=v_p(n!)-v_p(k!)-v_p((n-k)!)
=\frac{d_p(k)+d_p(n-k)-d_p(n)}{p-1}.
$$
Each <arithmetic carry> accounts for one unit of this quotient by <subadditivity of the base-p digit sum>. In particular, the <binomial coefficient> is divisible by $p$ exactly when at least one carry occurs.

For $n=50,k=17,p=3$, the <factorial> counts give
$$
v_3\binom{50}{17}=22-(5+1)-(11+3+1)=1.
$$
The matching <base-p expansions> are $17=(0122)_3$ and $33=(1020)_3$. Their units add to two; the next column gives $2+2=4=1+3$, sending one carry into the nines column. That column then gives $1+0+1=2$, and no further carry occurs. The result is $(1212)_3=50$. The <base-p digit sums> check the same result: $(5+3-6)/(3-1)=1$. Consequently $\binom{50}{17}$ is divisible by three but not by nine.

= Elementary primorial bound
{parent=Primorial}

For every real $X\geq1$,
$$
P(X)\leq4^X.
$$
For integer $n$, strong induction proves this by splitting according to parity. If $n=2k$, every prime in $(k,2k]$ divides $\binom{2k}{k}$, so
$$
P(2k)\leq P(k)\binom{2k}{k}\leq4^k4^k.
$$
If $n=2k+1$, the <valuation of a near-central binomial coefficient> gives
$$
P(2k+1)\leq P(k+1)\binom{2k+1}{k+1}\leq4^{k+1}4^k.
$$
The binomial estimates follow from the binomial theorem; in the odd case the two equal central coefficients together are at most $2^{2k+1}$. The result for real $X$ follows by replacing $X$ with $\lfloor X\rfloor$.

= Decisive number
{parent=Number theory}

A positive <integer> $n$ is decisive when every <integer> $a$ satisfying $1<a<n$ and $\gcd(a,n)=1$ is a <prime number>. There are only finitely many decisive numbers: every prime $p<\sqrt n$ must divide a decisive $n$, so the <Chebyshev theta function> gives $\log n\geq\vartheta(\sqrt n)$, which eventually contradicts the linear lower bound for $\vartheta(\sqrt n)$.

For $a=\pi(\sqrt x)$, let $p_1,\ldots,p_a$ be the <primes> at most $\sqrt x$. = Prime-counting upper bound from a primorial estimate
{parent=Prime-counting function}

If $P(X)\leq A^X$ for every $X\geq1$, where $A>1$, then
$$
\pi(X)\leq(1+2\log A)\frac{X}{\log X}
$$
for $X\geq2$. Every prime in $(\sqrt X,X]$ contributes at least $\sqrt X$ to the <primorial>, so
$$
(\sqrt X)^{\pi(X)-\pi(\sqrt X)}\leq P(X)\leq A^X.
$$
Taking logarithms, using $\pi(\sqrt X)\leq\sqrt X$, and observing that $\log X\leq\sqrt X$ for $X\geq2$ proves the claim.

= Jacobi reciprocity
{c}
{synonym}

For coprime positive odd integers $a,b$, the <Jacobi symbol> satisfies $(a/b)(b/a)=(-1)^{(a-1)(b-1)/4}$. It follows by factoring $a$ and $b$ into primes and multiplying the <quadratic reciprocity> for each prime pair; the parity of the sums of prime half-differences agrees with $(a-1)/2$ and $(b-1)/2$. A <Jacobi symbol> equal to one does not imply that its numerator is a <quadratic residue> for a composite denominator.

= Infinitely many prime quadratic nonresidues of a nonsquare integer
{parent=Jacobi reciprocity law}

For an odd positive nonsquare integer $a$, there are infinitely many <primes> $p$ with <Legendre symbol> $(a/p)=-1$. Choose a residue $n$ modulo $a$ with <Jacobi symbol> $(n/a)=-1$ by making it a <quadratic nonresidue> at one prime occurring to odd multiplicity and a square at every other prime. The <Chinese remainder theorem> also imposes $n\equiv1\pmod4$ and $n\equiv1$ at any finite list of previously found primes. Then <Jacobi reciprocity> gives $(a/n)=-1$, so a prime factor of $n$ has $(a/p)=-1$ and is outside that list.

\left(\frac{ab}{n}\right)=\left(\frac an\right)\left(\frac bn\right).
$$
It is also multiplicative in an odd denominator: $(a/mn)=(a/m)(a/n)$.

= Quadratic reciprocity
{parent=Number theory}
{wiki}

Quadratic reciprocity compares two questions: is the <prime number> $p$ a <quadratic residue> modulo $q$, and is $q$ a <quadratic residue> modulo $p$? Let $p,q$ be **distinct <odd primes>**. In terms of the <Legendre symbol>,
$$
\left(\frac pq\right)\left(\frac qp\right)
=(-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2}\frac{q-1}{2}}.
$$
**The two <Legendre symbols> have opposite signs exactly when both <primes> are $3$ modulo $4$; otherwise they are equal.** Their values are nonzero because the <primes> are distinct. Equal signs can mean two <quadratic residues> or two <quadratic nonresidues>: reciprocity compares the answers rather than declaring either answer positive.<NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions>[T. M. Apostol, Chapter 27, §27.9, equation 27.9.3: distinct odd prime hypotheses and reciprocity law]{ref}

For a direct computation, the nonzero <quadratic residues> modulo $3,5,7$ are respectively $\{1\}$, $\{1,4\}$, and $\{1,2,4\}$. Squaring just $1,\ldots,(p-1)/2$ suffices because $x$ and $p-x$ have the same square modulo an <odd prime> $p$.
$$
\begin{aligned}
\left(\frac37\right)&=-1,&\qquad \left(\frac73\right)&=\left(\frac13\right)=1,\\
\left(\frac57\right)&=-1,&\qquad \left(\frac75\right)&=\left(\frac25\right)=-1.
\end{aligned}
$$
The pair $3,7$ has a sign flip, whereas $5,7$ has two negative signs. These small <quadratic residue> tables make the distinction tangible.

{description=Each row uses half the nonzero inputs; the missing entries are unnecessary. The comparisons $3,7$ and $5,7$ are exact finite examples of <quadratic reciprocity>, not evidence by themselves for every <prime> pair.}

The <first supplementary law for quadratic reciprocity> handles numerator $-1$, and the <second supplementary law for quadratic reciprocity> handles numerator $2$:
$$
\left(\frac{-1}{p}\right)=(-1)^{(p-1)/2},\qquad
\left(\frac2p\right)=(-1)^{(p^2-1)/8}.
$$
For an <odd prime> $p$, $-1$ is a <quadratic residue> exactly when $p\equiv1\pmod4$, while $2$ is a <quadratic residue> exactly when $p\equiv1,7\pmod8$. The remaining odd <congruence classes> modulo $8$, namely $3,5$, make $2$ a <quadratic nonresidue>. These laws fill the cases excluded from swapping distinct <odd primes>.<NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions>[T. M. Apostol, Chapter 27, §27.9, equations 27.9.1–27.9.2: supplementary values]{ref}

= Fixed odd numerator Legendre-symbol periodicity
{parent=Quadratic reciprocity}
{title2=$p\equiv q\pmod{4n}\Longrightarrow(n/p)=(n/q)$}
{c}

For a fixed positive odd integer $n$, the <Legendre symbol> $(n/p)$ for odd prime denominators depends only on $p$ modulo $4n$. For prime numerator $r$, <quadratic reciprocity> expresses it as $(p/r)$ times a sign determined by $p$ modulo four. Factorization of $n$ and multiplicativity prove the general statement. If a denominator divides $n$, the congruence forces the other prime denominator to be identical, so the zero cases agree too.

= Computing Legendre symbols by quadratic reciprocity
{parent=Quadratic reciprocity}

The practical reward of <quadratic reciprocity> is a smaller modulus: swap a pair of distinct <odd primes>, record the required sign, then reduce the numerator by <modular arithmetic>. Use <multiplicativity of the Legendre symbol> and the <second supplementary law for quadratic reciprocity> to handle a factor of two. For example,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\left(\frac{11}{43}\right)
&=-\left(\frac{43}{11}\right)
=-\left(\frac{10}{11}\right)
=-\left(\frac2{11}\right)\left(\frac5{11}\right)\\
&=\left(\frac5{11}\right)
=\left(\frac{11}{5}\right)
=\left(\frac15\right)=1.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $11,43$ are both $3$ modulo $4$, so the first swap changes sign; $11\equiv3\pmod8$ gives $(2/11)=-1$; the swap involving five has no sign change. An independent square check is $21^2=441\equiv11\pmod{43}$. The method determines existence of a <square root> without itself finding that root.

A composite numerator is allowed in a <Legendre symbol>; a composite denominator instead calls for a <Jacobi symbol>. Its <Jacobi reciprocity law> supports repeated swaps and remainder reductions, in the spirit of the <Euclidean algorithm>.<Quadratic Reciprocity by Ben Lynn>[Ben Lynn, Quadratic Reciprocity, “The Jacobi Symbol”: product definition and reciprocity for computing symbols without factoring]{ref}

A final <Jacobi symbol> of one is not a square-existence certificate for a <composite number> modulus. For example,
$$
\left(\frac2{15}\right)=\left(\frac23\right)\left(\frac25\right)=(-1)(-1)=1,
$$
yet $x^2\equiv2\pmod{15}$ is impossible: reducing it modulo three would require a square congruent to two, and squares modulo three are only zero or one. The product has hidden two local failures. By contrast, for a <prime> denominator, a <Legendre symbol> of one does certify a <quadratic residue>.

= Lattice-point count for quadratic reciprocity
{parent=Quadratic reciprocity}
{title2=$M+N=(p-1)(q-1)/4$}

A short proof outline connects <Gauss's lemma (number theory)> to counting points with <integer> coordinates. For distinct <odd primes> $p,q$, define the two <floor function> sums
$$
M=\sum_{i=1}^{(p-1)/2}\left\lfloor\frac{qi}{p}\right\rfloor,\qquad
N=\sum_{j=1}^{(q-1)/2}\left\lfloor\frac{pj}{q}\right\rfloor.
$$
The parity form of <Gauss's lemma (number theory)> gives $(q/p)=(-1)^M$ and $(p/q)=(-1)^N$. In the open <rectangle> $0<x<p/2$, $0<y<q/2$, the <line> $y=qx/p$ separates $M$ points below it from $N$ points above it. None lies on the <line>, because $py=qx$ and <coprimality> would force $p\mid x$. The total is therefore $(p-1)(q-1)/4$. Combining that count with the parity identities gives <quadratic reciprocity>.<Quadratic Reciprocity by Ben Lynn>[Ben Lynn, Quadratic Reciprocity, first theorem and proof of the Law of Quadratic Reciprocity: floor-sum parity and the separating-line count]{ref}

= Carmichael number
{c}
{parent=Number theory}
{wiki}
{title2=$a^{N-1}\equiv1\pmod N$}

A Carmichael number is a <composite number> $N>1$ for which
$$
a^{N-1}\equiv1\pmod N\qquad\text{whenever }\gcd(a,N)=1.
$$
Every <unit> modulo $N$ satisfies the same <modular congruence> supplied by <Fermat's little theorem> for a <prime number>. The name refers to <Robert Daniel Carmichael>. The <coprime> qualification is essential: a <nonunit> can expose a <prime factor> through a <greatest common divisor> calculation. A <Carmichael number> is therefore a <Fermat pseudoprime> to every coprime base, rather than to every nonzero <residue class>.<Carmichael Numbers and Korselt's Criterion by Keith Conrad>[Definition 1, printed p. 1: composite input and coprime bases]{ref}

For example,
$$
\boxed{561=3\cdot11\cdot17\text{ is a Carmichael number}.}
$$
The factors are distinct <prime numbers>. Their predecessor numbers $2,10,16$ all divide $560$, so <Korselt criterion> applies. More explicitly, for any <integer> $a$ <coprime> to $561$, <Fermat's little theorem> gives
$$
a^{560}\equiv1\pmod3,\qquad a^{560}\equiv1\pmod{11},\qquad a^{560}\equiv1\pmod{17}.
$$
The <Chinese remainder theorem> combines these into $a^{560}\equiv1\pmod{561}$. This proves all coprime bases pass from three short divisibility checks, rather than from a long table of sampled bases.

\Image[carmichael-prime-factor-exponent-certificate.png]
{title=Three <prime factors> certify that every <unit> passes the Fermat congruence modulo 561}
{description=Original arithmetic certificate for $561=3\cdot11\cdot17$. Exponents $2,10,16$ divide $560$ with quotients $280,56,35$; the <Chinese remainder theorem> combines the three prime-modulus conclusions.}

Equivalently, a composite $N$ is a <Carmichael number> exactly when $a^N\equiv a\pmod N$ for every <integer> $a$, including nonunits. The equivalence follows from <Korselt criterion>: modulo each <prime factor>, either $a$ is zero or its nonzero power returns to one, and squarefreeness combines those congruences. This does not say a nonunit satisfies $a^{N-1}\equiv1$. For instance $a=3$ gives $3^{560}\equiv375\pmod{561}$, while $3^{561}\equiv3\pmod{561}$; the <greatest common divisor> already supplies the factor three.<Carmichael Numbers and Korselt's Criterion by Keith Conrad>[Corollary 4 and proof, printed p. 3]{ref}

The <Carmichael function> supplies another compact description: a composite $N$ is Carmichael exactly when $\lambda(N)\mid N-1$. For $561$, $\lambda(561)=\operatorname{lcm}(2,10,16)=80$ and $560=7\cdot80$. In contrast, <Euler's totient function> gives $\varphi(561)=320$, which does not divide $560$. The least common return exponent, rather than the number of <units>, controls this test.

The obstruction is useful to know when interpreting a <Fermat primality test>: **even checking every <coprime> base cannot certify primality**. The <passing-base fraction for the Fermat test> makes this failure quantitative. Stronger <primality tests> require additional structure, such as the squaring conditions in a <Miller-Rabin primality test>.

The historical record predates the name. <Václav Šimerka>'s 1885 paper lists $561,1105,1729,2465,2821,6601,8911$ on printed page 224, together with factorizations. This is an early published list of examples, independently of their later name.<Zbytky z arithmetické posloupnosti by Václav Šimerka>[Zbytky z arithmetické posloupnosti, printed p. 224, §9: list beginning with 561 and 1105; archive title sheet identifies Václav Šimerka and 1885]{ref}

{parent=Carmichael number}
{title2=$N\text{ squarefree},\ p-1\mid N-1\ (p\mid N)$}

A composite <integer> $N>1$ is a <Carmichael number> if and only if it is a <squarefree integer> and
$$
p-1\mid N-1\qquad\text{for every prime }p\mid N.
$$
The criterion is named for <Alwin Korselt>. The two requirements are different: the first excludes repeated <prime factors>, while the second makes the exponent $N-1$ a common multiple of the prime-field return exponents.<Carmichael Numbers and Korselt's Criterion by Keith Conrad>[Theorem 2 (Korselt), printed p. 2]{ref}

For $1105=5\cdot13\cdot17$, the numbers $4,12,16$ divide $1104$ with quotients $276,92,69$. Thus **1105 is another <Carmichael number>**. By contrast $341=11\cdot31$ is squarefree, but $30\nmid340$; a single failed divisor condition excludes it. This illustrates the appeal of the criterion: a statement about every <unit> becomes a small calculation using a known <prime factorization>.

The composite condition must be retained. A <prime number> is squarefree and satisfies the divisibility condition, but is excluded from the definition of a <Carmichael number>. The resulting certificate also depends on the supplied factors really being <prime numbers>; merely finding a product decomposition is insufficient.

= Proof of Korselt criterion
{parent=Korselt criterion}

For sufficiency, let $N$ be composite and squarefree, with $p-1\mid N-1$ for every <prime factor> $p$. For any <integer> $a$ <coprime> to $N$, <Fermat's little theorem> gives $a^{N-1}\equiv1\pmod p$ for every such $p$. Their product is $N$, so the <Chinese remainder theorem> gives the required congruence modulo $N$.

For necessity, first rule out a repeated factor. If $N=p^km$ with $k\ge2$ and $\gcd(p,m)=1$, the <Chinese remainder theorem> supplies $a\equiv1+p\pmod{p^k}$ and $a\equiv1\pmod m$. This $a$ is <coprime> to $N$. But the <binomial theorem> gives
$$
a^{N-1}\equiv(1+p)^{N-1}\equiv1+(N-1)p\equiv1-p\not\equiv1\pmod{p^2},
$$
contradicting the <Carmichael number> condition. Hence $N$ is squarefree.

Now choose a <primitive root> $g$ modulo a <prime factor> $p$. Lift it to a <unit> modulo $N$ by prescribing $a\equiv g\pmod p$ and $a\equiv1\pmod{N/p}$. The <Chinese remainder theorem> applies because $N$ is squarefree. The Carmichael congruence implies $g^{N-1}\equiv1\pmod p$. Since $g$ has <multiplicative order> $p-1$, that order divides $N-1$. This proves the other condition.<Carmichael Numbers and Korselt's Criterion by Keith Conrad>[Theorem 2 proof, printed p. 2: repeated prime factors, primitive roots and Chinese remainder lifting]{ref}

= Passing-base fraction for the Fermat test
{parent=Carmichael number}
{title2=$\alpha_N=|\{a\in U(N):a^{N-1}=1\}|/\varphi(N)$}

Among the <units> $U(N)$ modulo $N$, the passing <residue classes> form the <kernel of a group homomorphism> $a\mapsto a^{N-1}$. If each trial samples independently and uniformly from those <units>, with replacement, the probability that $r$ trials all pass is $\alpha_N^r$. This statement assumes that sampling rule; it does not describe arbitrary or dependent choices of bases.

For $341=11\cdot31$, the <Chinese remainder theorem> identifies the <unit group> with cyclic groups of orders $10$ and $30$. The number of solutions of $x^{340}=1$ is $\gcd(340,10)\gcd(340,30)=100$, among $\varphi(341)=300$ <units>. Thus $\alpha_{341}=1/3$. The <Carmichael numbers> $561$ and $1105$ have passing fractions $320/320$ and $768/768$, so their conditional false-pass probability remains one regardless of the number of independent trials.

= Integral solutions of x squared minus two equals y cubed
{parent=Diophantine equation}
{title2=$x^2-2=y^3\ \Longrightarrow\ (x,y)=(\pm1,-1)$}

In the <ring of integers> $\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-6}]$, which has class number two and units $\pm1$, write $\alpha=x+(y+1)\sqrt{-6}$. Its norm is $(y+2)^3$. Parity modulo eight and cubic residues modulo nine show that $y$ is odd and $3$ does not divide $y+2$, so $\gcd(y+2,6)=1$. The ideals of $\alpha$ and its conjugate are coprime: a common prime cannot lie above two or three by their norms; at any other prime their difference forces divisibility of $y+1$, contradicting divisibility of $y+2$.

Unique factorization of ideals gives $(\alpha)=A^3$. The class group of order two forces $A$ principal, and its unit factor can be absorbed into a cube. Hence $\alpha=(u+v\sqrt{-6})^3$ with integers $u,v$, and $u^2+6v^2=y+2$. Comparing coefficients and eliminating $y$ yields
$$
9u^2=18v^2+24v+8-\frac1{3v-1}.
$$
Integrality requires $3v-1=\pm1$, so $v=0$ is the only integral possibility. Then $u=\pm1$, $y=-1$, and $x=\pm1$. Direct substitution verifies both solutions. The class-group step is what permits extracting a cube of an element from a cube of an ideal despite failure of unique factorization of elements.

= No integer solutions to y cubed equals x squared plus thirty
{parent=Diophantine equation}
{title2=$y^3=x^2+30$}

The equation has no <integer> solutions. Work in the <ring of integers> $\mathbb Z[\theta]$ for $\theta=\sqrt{-30}$. If a rational prime divides both $y$ and thirty, it divides $x$, and the valuation of $x^2+30$ is one because thirty is squarefree, contradicting the valuation of a cube. Thus $\gcd(y,30)=1$. The two <principal ideals> $(x+\theta),(x-\theta)$ are coprime: any common <prime ideal> lies above a prime dividing both $N(2\theta)=120$ and $y$. Their product is $(y)^3$, so unique <prime ideal> factorization gives $(x+\theta)=A^3$. The <ideal class group of Q of square root minus thirty> has exponent two, forcing $A$ to be principal. The only units are $\pm1$ and both are cubes, so $x+\theta=(a+b\theta)^3$ for <integers> $a,b$. Comparison of the coefficient of $\theta$ gives $1=3b(a^2-10b^2)$, impossible modulo three.

= Schinzel-Tijdeman exponent theorem
{c}
{parent=Diophantine equation}
{title2=$y^m=f(x),\quad |y|>1\Longrightarrow m\le C(f)$}

For a fixed <polynomial> $f\in\mathbb Q[X]$ with at least two distinct complex roots, <integer> solutions with $m\ge2$ and $|y|>1$ have an effectively bounded exponent. Factorization over a <splitting field> and the fixed differences between distinct roots control common <ideal> <divisors>. Outside a fixed set of primes, each factor's valuation is divisible by $m/\gcd(m,e_i)$, where $e_i$ is its root <multiplicity>. The generalized power-equation estimates from <linear forms in logarithms of algebraic numbers>, including <unit> and exceptional-prime factors, give the exponent bound. A single-root <polynomial> and the values $y=0,\pm1$ cannot be included: $4^m=(2^m)^2$, and $1^m=f(0)$ for $f(X)=X(X-1)+1$, give infinite exponent families.

= abc conjecture
{c}
{parent=Diophantine equation}
{title2=$C\leq K_\epsilon\operatorname{rad}(ABC)^{1+\epsilon}$}

For every $\epsilon>0$ there is a constant $K_\epsilon$ such that the displayed inequality holds for coprime positive integers $A+B=C$. The radical is the product of the distinct primes dividing the argument. Applying it to a primitive Fermat triple bounds the exponent and the triple simultaneously. For $ax^n-by^n=c\ne0$, division by the gcd, which divides $c$, gives a uniform bound on $\max(x,y)^{n-2(1+\epsilon)}$. Finiteness in the varying exponent requires excluding $x=y=1$ when $a-b=c$.

= Abc-hit
{parent=abc conjecture}

A triple of <positive integers> $(a,b,c)$ with $a<b$, $a+b=c$, $\gcd(a,b)=1$, and $\operatorname{rad}(abc)<c$, where $\operatorname{rad}$ is the <radical of an integer>. The example $(1,8,9)$ has $\operatorname{rad}(72)=6<9$.

= Catalan power bound from abc
{c}
{parent=abc conjecture}
{title2=$x^p-y^q=1,\quad p,q\ge3\Longrightarrow x^p\le C_{1/6}^{9/2}$}

Put $M=x^p$ for positive bases $x,y\ge2$. The <coprime> triple $(y^q,1,x^p)$ has radical at most $xy<M^{2/3}$. The <abc conjecture> with $\epsilon=1/6$ gives $M\le C_{1/6}M^{7/9}$, proving the bound. Both bases and both exponents are then bounded, since $2^p\le M$ and $2^q\le M-1$. Unlike a homogeneous Fermat equation, no common-scaling ambiguity occurs.

= Primitive Fermat bound from abc
{parent=abc conjecture}
{title2=$z^{n-7/2}\le C_{1/6}$}

For a primitive positive solution of $x^n+y^n=z^n$ with $n\ge4$, the three powers are pairwise <coprime> and their radical is at most $xyz<z^3$. The <abc conjecture> with $\epsilon=1/6$ gives the displayed inequality, hence $z\le C_{1/6}^2$ uniformly in $n$. The inequality $z^n\le2\max(x,y)^n$ then bounds $n$. Thus there are finitely many primitive triples and exponents. Homogeneity would produce infinitely many unscaled solutions from any one nonzero solution, so a primitive <arithmetic height> bound alone must not be described as finite many arbitrary multiples.

= Thue theorem
{c}
{parent=Diophantine equation}
{title2=$F(x,y)=m$}

An irreducible integral binary form of degree at least three has only finitely many integral solutions at a fixed nonzero value. Factor the form in a splitting field. Each linear factor generates an ideal dividing a fixed ideal, so it is one of finitely many chosen factors times a unit. The linear relation among any three distinct factors gives a <unit equation over a number field>, hence finitely many ratios and finitely many rational directions. Homogeneity and the fixed nonzero value then bound the scale on each direction. The same argument works for a binary form with at least three distinct projective roots, even without irreducibility.

= Unit equation over a number field
{parent=Diophantine equation}
{title2=$\alpha u+\beta v=1,\qquad u,v\in\mathcal O_K^\times$}

For fixed nonzero $\alpha,\beta$ in a <number field>, this equation has finitely many unit solutions. By the <Dirichlet unit theorem>, a large unit-exponent vector makes one unit exponentially small at some archimedean embedding. The equation makes the other fixed multiple exponentially close to one. Its principal logarithm is a nonzero linear form in fixed algebraic logarithms, with coefficients bounded by the exponent size; a polynomial lower bound contradicts the exponential upper bound. Finite torsion and finitely many embeddings allow uniform constants.

= Infinite descent
{parent=Diophantine equation}
{wiki}

An infinite descent rules out a nonempty set of positive integer solutions by constructing from any solution another with a strictly smaller positive integer measure. Choosing a solution of minimal measure gives a contradiction. Common measures are a hypotenuse, a denominator, or the size of a primitive solution.

= Fourth-power sum cannot be a square
{parent=Infinite descent}
{title2=$x^4+y^4\ne z^2\quad(x,y,z\in\mathbb Z_{>0})$}

Choose a primitive counterexample with minimal $z$ and $y$ even. Its <Pythagorean triple> $(x^2,y^2,z)$ has $x^2=m^2-n^2$, $y^2=2mn$. Modulo four forces $m$ odd and $n$ even, so $m=u^2$ and $n=2v^2$. The primitive triangle $(x,2v^2,u^2)$ then has a parametrization with $v^2=rs$ and $u^2=r^2+s^2$. Coprimality makes $r=a^2,s=b^2$, giving $a^4+b^4=u^2$ with $u<z$. This contradicts <infinite descent>.

= Prime representation via an ideal class
{parent=Binary quadratic form}
{title2=$q_I(x,y)=N(xe_1+ye_2)/N(I)$}

Let $I$ be an <integral ideal> of an imaginary quadratic <number field> and $(e_1,e_2)$ an <integral basis> of $I$. Its normalized norm is a positive definite <binary quadratic form>. An unramified rational prime $p$ is represented by this form exactly when there is an integral <prime ideal> $P$ of norm $p$ with $[P]=[I]^{-1}$. Conversely, $IP=(\alpha)$ with $\alpha\in I$ produces a representation by expressing $\alpha$ in the basis. <Artin reciprocity> then detects this <ideal class> by Frobenius in the <Hilbert class field>.

= Discriminant criterion for prime representation by a binary quadratic form
{parent=Binary quadratic form}

For odd prime $p$ and an integral-form discriminant $d$, representation of $p$ by some form of discriminant $d$ is equivalent to solvability of $b^2\equiv d\pmod p$. A primitive representing vector can be completed to a unimodular change giving leading coefficient $p$. Conversely choose $b$ with the required parity and use $[p,b,(b^2-d)/(4p)]$.

= Proper equivalence of binary quadratic forms
{parent=Binary quadratic form}

Integral forms $f$ and $g$ are properly equivalent when
$$g(x,y)=f(\alpha x+\beta y,\gamma x+\delta y)$$
for some $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\\gamma&\delta\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)$. Proper equivalence preserves both represented integers and orientation.

= Primitive binary quadratic form
{parent=Binary quadratic form}

An integral binary quadratic form $[a,b,c]$ is primitive when $\gcd(a,b,c)=1$. This property is invariant under proper equivalence because a unimodular change and its integral inverse express either coefficient triple as integer combinations of the other.

= Discriminant of a binary quadratic form
{parent=Binary quadratic form}

The discriminant of $[a,b,c]$ is $D=b^2-4ac$. It is invariant under proper equivalence, and a real form with $a>0$ is positive definite exactly when $D<0$.

= Reduced positive definite binary quadratic form
{parent=Binary quadratic form}

A positive definite integral form $[a,b,c]$ is reduced when $|b|\leq a\leq c$, with $b\geq0$ if $|b|=a$ or $a=c$. Every proper equivalence class has a unique reduced representative under this boundary convention.

= Reduction algorithm for a positive definite binary quadratic form
{parent=Reduced positive definite binary quadratic form}

For a positive definite integral form $[a,b,c]$, a unimodular shear can replace $b$ by its representative $b'$ with $|b'|\leq a$. If the resulting $c'=(b'^2-D)/(4a)$ is smaller than $a$, swapping the variables replaces the leading coefficient by the smaller positive integer $c'$. Repeating terminates and produces $|b|\leq a\leq c$; determinant-one sign changes enforce the boundary convention. Thus every positive definite integral binary quadratic form is properly equivalent to a reduced form.

= Enumeration of reduced binary quadratic forms

For a reduced form of negative discriminant $D$, the inequalities imply $3a^2\leq|D|$. One can therefore enumerate all classes by checking the finite set
$$1\leq a\leq\sqrt{|D|/3},\qquad |b|\leq a,$$
and retaining integral $c=(b^2-D)/(4a)$ with $a\leq c$ and the boundary sign convention.

= Euclid lemma
{c}
{synonym}

If a <prime number> $p$ divides a <product> $ab$ of <integers> $a,b$, then $p$ divides $a$ or $b$ (possibly both):
$$
p\mid ab\quad\Longrightarrow\quad p\mid a\text{ or }p\mid b.
$$
For example, $5\mid6\cdot10$ and $5\mid10$. The hypothesis that $p$ is a <prime number> matters: $6\mid2\cdot3$, although six divides neither factor. When $p$ does not divide $a$, the <greatest common divisor> of $p$ and $a$ is one; multiply <Bézout's identity> $up+va=1$ by $b$ to see that $p\mid b$. Repeated application makes this the uniqueness step in the <fundamental theorem of arithmetic>. https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookVII/propVII30.html[Euclid, Elements, Book VII, Proposition 30]{ref}.

For a completely computed example, $7\mid20\cdot21$ because $20\cdot21=420=7\cdot60$, while $20=7\cdot2+6$ shows that seven does not divide twenty. <Bézout's identity> $3\cdot7-20=1$ becomes, after <multiplication> by $21$,
$$
21=3\cdot7\cdot21-20\cdot21=7\cdot63-7\cdot60=7\cdot3.
$$
The two terms on the right are divisible by seven, which explicitly recovers the forced <divisibility> of the second factor. Both alternatives can hold: $7\mid14\cdot21$, and seven divides both $14$ and $21$.

In fact the property characterizes <prime numbers> among the <integers> greater than one. If $c$ is a <composite number>, choose $c=ab$ with $1<a,b<c$; then $c\mid ab$, but $c$ divides neither $a$ nor $b$. For several factors, repeated application of <Euclid's lemma> says that a <prime number> dividing their <product> divides at least one factor. In a <product> of <prime numbers>, that factor must equal it, because a <prime number> has no other <prime factor>. This is what prevents a different collection of <prime factors> from representing the same <positive integer> in the <fundamental theorem of arithmetic>.

Book VII, Proposition 30 of <Euclid's Elements> states the result in terms of a <prime number> measuring a <product>; its proof uses <coprimality> and <ratios>. The calculation using <Bézout's identity> above is a modern way to explain the same <divisibility> statement. https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/java/elements/bookVII/propVII30.html[Euclid, Elements VII.30, translated text hosted by David Joyce]{ref}

= Prime valuation
{parent=Number theory}
{wiki}
{title2=$v_p(n)$}

For a <prime number> $p$ and a <positive integer> $n$, the prime valuation is the exponent of $p$ in the <prime factorization> of $n$, equivalently
$$
v_p(n)=\max\{e\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}:p^e\mid n\}.
$$
Thus $v_2(360)=3$ because $8$ divides $360$ but $16$ does not; $v_3(360)=2$, $v_5(360)=1$ and $v_7(360)=0$. In particular $v_p(1)=0$. <Multiplication> adds <prime valuations>, $v_p(ab)=v_p(a)+v_p(b)$, and the <fundamental theorem of arithmetic> makes comparison of <prime valuations> an exact test for <divisibility> between <positive integers>. This definition extends to a nonzero <integer> by taking its <absolute value>; for zero one commonly sets $v_p(0)=+\infty$.

<Euler's totient function> is the <arithmetic function>
$$
\varphi(n)=\#\{j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}:\gcd(j,n)=1\},\qquad n>0.
$$
It counts the <congruence classes> modulo $n$ represented by <integers> <coprime> to $n$. In particular, $\varphi(1)=1$, since $\gcd(1,1)=1$. For $12$, the <coprime integers> among $1,\ldots,12$ are $1,5,7,11$, so $\varphi(12)=4$; they form a <reduced residue system>. https://darijgrinberg.gitlab.io/floor.pdf[Darij Grinberg, MIT 18.781 lecture notes, Definition 2.1.2]{ref}

Counting these <congruence classes> is useful because they are exactly the classes with <modular multiplicative inverses>. Their number supplies the exponent in <Euler theorem>. <Möbius inversion> gives the product formula
$$
\varphi(n)=n\prod_{p\mid n}\left(1-\frac1p\right),
$$
where $p$ runs over distinct <prime factors> of $n$. Thus $\varphi(p^a)=p^{a-1}(p-1)$ for <prime powers> with $a\geq1$, and $\varphi(12)=12(1-1/2)(1-1/3)=4$. The product for $n=1$ is an <empty product>, so it gives the same convention $\varphi(1)=1$.

= Totient identity for a product and a greatest common divisor

The prime-power formula and <multiplicativity> of <Euler's totient function> imply $\phi(mn)\phi(\gcd(m,n))=\phi(m)\phi(n)\gcd(m,n)$. For a <prime> with positive exponents $a,b$ in the inputs, both local products equal $p^{a+b+\min(a,b)-2}(p-1)^2$. If one exponent is zero, the equality is immediate from $\phi(1)=1$.

= Totient Dirichlet series and its absolute convergence boundary
{parent=Euler totient function}
{title2=$\sum_{n\geq1}\phi(n)n^{-s}=\zeta(s-1)/\zeta(s),\quad\sigma_a=2$}

The <totient divisor-sum identity> and multiplication of absolutely convergent <Dirichlet series> give the quotient for $\operatorname{Re}s>2$. The bound $\phi(n)\leq n$ proves convergence there. The <simple pole> of the <Riemann zeta function> at one makes the quotient unbounded as real $s\downarrow2$. Since the coefficients are nonnegative, convergence at two would bound all these larger-real-part values, a contradiction. Thus the <abscissa of absolute convergence of a Dirichlet series> is two.

For every <prime divisor> $p$ of a solution, $p-1$ divides $m$, so the possible <primes> lie in the finite set $\{d+1:d\mid m,\ d+1\text{ is prime}\}$. The summatory totient function is $\Phi(x)=\sum_{n\le x}\varphi(n)$. It counts the <coprime> <ordered pairs> $(a,b)$ with $1\le a\le b\le x$. The identity $\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d)=n$ yields $\sum_{j=1}^{x}\Phi(\lfloor x/j\rfloor)=x(x+1)/2$ for a positive <integer> $x$.

= Asymptotic formula for the summatory totient function
{parent=Summatory totient function}
{title2=$\sum_{n\le x}\varphi(n)\sim3x^2/\pi^2$}

A smoothed form of the <summatory totient function> satisfies
$$
\sum_{n\le x}\varphi(n)(x-n)=\frac{x^3}{\pi^2}+O(x^{5/2}).
$$
Use the <Dirichlet series> $\zeta(s-1)/\zeta(s)$ and the kernel $x^s/[s(s+1)]$ to write the left side as a vertical <contour integral>. Move the line from real part greater than two to $3/2$. The only crossed pole is at two, with residue $x^3/(6\zeta(2))=x^3/\pi^2$; the remaining vertical integral is $O(x^{5/2})$ by the standard zeta bounds in the half-strip. If $A(x)=\sum_{n\le x}\varphi(n)$ and $S(x)=\int_0^xA(t)\,dt$, positivity of the summands makes $A$ nondecreasing. The backward and forward difference quotients of $S$ bound $A(x)$. Insert $S(x)\sim x^3/\pi^2$, then let the relative difference-quotient step tend to zero, proving the displayed asymptotic. This <monotone integral Tauberian lemma> justifies the passage from a smoothed estimate to an unsmoothed one.

= Totient chain
{parent=Euler totient function}

The sequence $n,\varphi(n),\varphi(\varphi(n)),\ldots,1$ obtained by iterating <Euler's totient function> on a <positive integer>. Since $1\le\varphi(n)<n$ for $n>1$, the sequence reaches 1. Counting both endpoints gives the length <recurrence relation> $L(1)=1$ and $L(n)=1+L(\varphi(n))$; increasing order of $n$ therefore suffices for <dynamic programming>.

= Totient divisor-sum identity
{parent=Euler totient function}
{title2=$\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d)=n$}

The <divisor sum> of <Euler's totient function> is the <identity arithmetic function>:
$$
\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d)=n,\qquad n>0.
$$
Equivalently, $\varphi*\mathbf1=\operatorname{id}$ under <Dirichlet convolution>, with $\mathbf1$ the <constant-one arithmetic function>. https://darijgrinberg.gitlab.io/floor.pdf[Darij Grinberg, MIT 18.781 lecture notes, Propositions 2.4.4 and 2.4.5]{ref}

To see why, group the <integers> $j=1,\ldots,n$ by $h=\gcd(j,n)$. Each $h$ is a positive <integer divisor> of $n$. Within its group, $j=ha$ with $1\leq a\leq n/h$ and $\gcd(a,n/h)=1$, by the <scaling identity for greatest common divisors>. Dividing by $h$ gives a <bijection> with the <integers> counted by $\varphi(n/h)$. The groups form a <partition of a set>, so
$$
n=\sum_{h\mid n}\varphi(n/h)=\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d).
$$
For $n=12$, the groups with <greatest common divisor> $h=1,2,3,4,6,12$ are respectively $\{1,5,7,11\},\{2,10\},\{3,9\},\{4,8\},\{6\},\{12\}$. Their sizes are $4,2,2,2,1,1$, totaling $12$. <Möbius inversion> reverses this <divisor sum> to recover <Euler's totient function> from $g(n)=n$.

= Sum of reduced residues
{parent=Euler totient function}
{title2=$\sum_{1\leq j<n,\ (j,n)=1}j=n\varphi(n)/2\quad(n>1)$}

The map $j\mapsto n-j$ is a <bijection> of the <integers> between one and $n-1$ that are <coprime> to $n$. If their sum is $S$, this <bijection> gives $S=n\varphi(n)-S$, so $S=n\varphi(n)/2$. This works even at $n=2$, where the single residue is fixed. The same identity follows from the <weighted inclusion-exclusion principle> by excluding multiples of the <prime factors> of $n$.

= Integers whose totient divides them
{parent=Euler totient function}
{title2=$\varphi(n)\mid n\iff n=1\text{ or }n=2^c3^d,\ c\ge1,\ d\ge0$}

If $n>1$ is odd, its <Euler totient function> is even and cannot divide $n$. Otherwise write $n=2^c\prod_{j=1}^r p_j^{a_j}$ with odd primes $p_j$ and $c\ge1$. Comparing powers of two in $\varphi(n)\mid n$ gives $\sum_j v_2(p_j-1)\le1$. Thus there is at most one odd prime $p$, with $v_2(p-1)=1$. Since $p-1$ divides $\varphi(n)$, it divides $n$, and is <coprime> to $p$; with only the prime factors $2,p$ available it must be a power of two. Hence $p-1=2$ and $p=3$. Conversely, for $c\ge1$ the prime-power totient formula verifies divisibility for $2^c3^d$, and $n=1$ is immediate.

= Totient divisibility along divisors
{parent=Euler totient function}
{title2=$d\mid n\Longrightarrow\varphi(d)\mid\varphi(n)$}

Write $n=\prod p^{a_p}$ and $d=\prod p^{b_p}$, with $0\le b_p\le a_p$. The prime-power formula for the <Euler totient function> gives an integer contribution to $\varphi(n)/\varphi(d)$ from each prime: it is $p^{a_p-b_p}$ when $b_p>0$, and $p^{a_p-1}(p-1)$ when $b_p=0<a_p$. Multiplying proves the <divisibility> assertion, including $d=1$ with $\varphi(1)=1$.

= Fermat's little theorem
{synonym}
{c}

For a <prime number> $p$ and an <integer> $a$ not divisible by $p$, <Fermat's little theorem> states the <modular congruence>
$$
a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p\qquad(p\nmid a).
$$
Equivalently, for every <integer> $a$, including $a<0$ and multiples of $p$,
$$
a^p\equiv a\pmod p.
$$
The first formulation follows from the second by multiplying by a <modular multiplicative inverse> of $a$. Conversely, multiply the first by $a$ when $p\nmid a$; when $p\mid a$, both sides of the second are zero modulo $p$. The <divisibility> condition in the first formulation is essential.<Euler’s Theorem by Keith Conrad>[p. 1, Theorem 1.1 and the equivalence of nonzero and coprime classes for a prime modulus]{ref}

For $p=7$, $3^6=729=7\cdot104+1$, so $3^6\equiv1\pmod7$ and $3^7\equiv3\pmod7$. Also $(-2)^6=64\equiv1\pmod7$, while $7^6\equiv0\pmod7$: a zero <residue class> cannot satisfy the first formulation. It does satisfy $7^7\equiv7\equiv0\pmod7$. A failure at a zero <residue class> therefore says nothing against a candidate's being a <prime number>.

The <prime number> $p=2$ is included. Its only nonzero <residue class> is one: every odd <integer> $a$ satisfies $a^{2-1}=a\equiv1\pmod2$. Every <integer> also satisfies $a^2\equiv a\pmod2$, because $a(a-1)$ is even. Testing the first formulation with an even <integer> would violate its hypothesis.

The useful surprise is that every nonzero <residue class> returns to one at the same <exponent> $p-1$, although its shortest return can be earlier. This gives <exponent reduction for units>: for a <nonnegative integer> $k$, $a^k\equiv a^{k\bmod(p-1)}\pmod p$ when $p\nmid a$. It also computes a <modular multiplicative inverse> as $a^{p-2}$ modulo $p$, including $p=2$. <Repeated squaring> makes these <modular exponentiations> practical.

In <group theory>, the nonzero <residue classes> form the <multiplicative group of integers modulo n> with $n=p$, of <group order> $p-1$. <Lagrange's theorem> says that the <multiplicative order> of $a$ divides $p-1$, giving the guaranteed return. <Euler's theorem> extends this to a general <integer modulus> $n$, using the <units> and <Euler's totient function> $\varphi(n)$; the <Carmichael function> gives the least common return <exponent> for all <units>. A nonzero <residue class> modulo a <composite number> need not be a <unit>.

<Leonhard Euler>'s paper presented in 1732 and published in 1738 states a historical formulation: Theorem 1 says that powers with <exponent> $n-1$ leave zero or one upon division by a <prime number> $n$. The preceding discussion states the difference-of-powers version and explicitly says that he does not have its proof.<Observations on a certain theorem of Fermat and on others concerning prime numbers by Leonhard Euler>[English translation, p. 1 publication note; p. 2 paragraph before footnote 3; p. 3, Theorem 1]{ref}

= Nonzero residue permutation
{parent=Fermat little theorem}
{title2=$x\mapsto ax\pmod p$}

For a <prime number> $p$ and $p\nmid a$, <multiplication> by $a$ gives a <permutation> of the $p-1$ nonzero <residue classes>. For $a=3$ modulo seven, the representatives $1,2,3,4,5,6$ map to $3,6,2,5,1,4$: the same collection in a different order. Repeating this <permutation> from one visits all six representatives before returning.

For $a=2$ modulo seven, the <permutation cycles> are instead $(1,2,4)$ and $(3,6,5)$. The powers of two visit only the <permutation cycle> containing one, so their <multiplicative order> is three. Both <permutation cycles> return after three steps; applying the <permutation> six times still fixes every nonzero <residue class>. <Fermat's little theorem> guarantees a return at six, not that every base visits every nonzero <residue class>.

More generally, every <permutation cycle> of this map has length equal to the <multiplicative order> of $a$: starting at a nonzero <residue class> $x$, a return means $a^kx\equiv x\pmod p$, and its <modular multiplicative inverse> allows cancellation of $x$. Thus the $p-1$ nonzero <residue classes> split into equal-length <permutation cycles>, which also explains the <divisibility> of the <multiplicative order> into $p-1$.

The product argument gives a short proof outline. Since <multiplication> by $a$ is a <permutation> of the nonzero <residue classes>,
$$
\prod_{r=1}^{p-1}r\equiv\prod_{r=1}^{p-1}(ar)
=a^{p-1}\prod_{r=1}^{p-1}r\pmod p.
$$
Every factor is a <unit>, so their product has a <modular multiplicative inverse> and can be cancelled. This leaves $a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p$. The whole argument works for $p=2$, where the product is just one.<Euler’s Theorem by Keith Conrad>[p. 2, proof of Theorem 1.1: the sets S and aS, product comparison and cancellation]{ref}

= Fermat witness
{parent=Fermat little theorem}
{c}
{title2=$a^{n-1}\not\equiv1\pmod n$}

A <Fermat witness> for an <integer> $n>1$ is a nonzero <residue class> represented by $1\leq a<n$ for which $a^{n-1}\not\equiv1\pmod n$. It proves that $n$ is a <composite number> by <Fermat's little theorem>. Here the definition includes <nonunits>; some conventions restrict <Fermat witnesses> to bases <coprime> to $n$. The <Fermat primality test> can first compute the <greatest common divisor>: if $1<\gcd(a,n)<n$, it already has a proper factor. A zero <residue class> is excluded. <Fermat's Test by Keith Conrad>[Keith Conrad, Fermat's Test, p. 2, Definition 2.4 and footnote 3 on the two witness conventions]{ref}

= Fermat test example
{parent=Fermat little theorem}
{c}

For the <Fermat primality test>, a passing base satisfies $a^{n-1}\equiv1\pmod n$. **Passing proves only this <modular congruence>, not that $n$ is a <prime number>.** An odd <composite number> that passes a <coprime> base is a <Fermat pseudoprime> to that base. A <Carmichael number> passes every <coprime> base, so even testing all the <units> would not certify that $n$ is a <prime number>. <Fermat's Test by Keith Conrad>[Keith Conrad, Fermat's Test, pp. 5–6, Definition 4.1 and the list beginning with 561]{ref}

A "factor" entry records a proper <greatest common divisor> and stops before <modular exponentiation>. The prime/composite classification is independently established by <trial division> or the displayed <prime factorization>, not by the passing entries. These four bases are illustrative, with no asserted universal coverage or error probability.

For $341=11\cdot31$, $\gcd(2,341)=1$ and $2^{10}=1024=3\cdot341+1$, so $2^{340}=(2^{10})^{34}\equiv1\pmod{341}$. Yet $\gcd(3,341)=1$ and $3^{340}\equiv56\pmod{341}$, so three is a <Fermat witness>. One <coprime> base passes and another fails: being a <Fermat pseudoprime> depends on the base. Choosing eleven instead reveals the proper factor through $\gcd(11,341)=11$.

For $561=3\cdot11\cdot17$, every <coprime> base passes: each of $2,10,16$ divides $560$, so <Fermat's little theorem> gives $a^{560}\equiv1$ modulo each <prime factor>; the <Chinese remainder theorem> combines the three <modular congruences>. Equivalently, the <Carmichael function> gives $\lambda(561)=80\mid560$. Bases two and five have <greatest common divisor> one and pass, but $\gcd(3,561)=3$, $\gcd(11,561)=11$ and $\gcd(17,561)=17$ expose factors. The qualification "<coprime>" is therefore crucial even for a <Carmichael number>.

The <Fermat primality test> is useful because a <Fermat witness> can prove compositeness using <repeated squaring> without finding a factor. Its failure to certify a passing candidate motivates stronger <primality testing> methods such as the <Miller-Rabin primality test>.

= Rational
{synonym}

A rational number is a quotient of two integers with nonzero denominator.

= Stern-Brocot tree
{parent=Rational number}
{c}

= Stern-Brocot
{synonym}
{c}

A <binary tree> enumerating every positive <reduced fraction> exactly once. Begin with boundary fractions $0/1$ and $1/0$; between neighboring fractions $a/b<c/d$, insert their mediant $(a+c)/(b+d)$ and recurse into the two new intervals. The <determinant> relation $bc-ad=1$ is preserved, and the new <denominator> is $b+d$.

= Fraction
{parent=Rational number}
{wiki}
{title2=$\frac ab$}

A fraction $a/b$ represents the quotient of its <numerator> $a$ by its nonzero <denominator> $b$. For <integers> $a,b$ this is a <rational number>. Different fractions can represent the same number: $252/198=14/11$. Dividing both entries by their <greatest common divisor> gives a <reduced fraction>, with the sign convention that the <denominator> is positive.

= Proper fraction
{parent=Fraction}
{title2=$0\leq m<n$}

A nonnegative <fraction> with positive <denominator> and <numerator> smaller than its denominator, so its value lies in $[0,1)$. The zero value has the <reduced fraction> representation $0/1$. The term is sometimes extended to negative fractions by comparing absolute values; the nonnegative convention is the one used here.

= Unit fraction
{parent=Fraction}

A <fraction> of the form $1/n$ for a <positive integer> $n$. Its <numerator> is 1 and its <denominator> determines it uniquely.

= Egyptian fraction
{parent=Unit fraction}
{c}
{wiki}

An <Egyptian fraction> expresses a nonnegative <rational number> as a finite <sum> of positive <unit fractions>. A computational representation may permit repeated denominators, while the classical distinct-denominator convention adds a further requirement. The empty sum represents zero. For example, $3/7=1/3+1/11+1/231$; each denominator is a positive <integer>. A representation that permits repetition also has the simple expansion $n/d=\sum_{j=1}^{n}1/d$ for nonnegative <integers> $n$ and positive $d$.

= Greedy Egyptian fraction expansion
{parent=Egyptian fraction}

For a positive proper residual <fraction> $n/d$, with $0<n<d$, choose $q=\lceil d/n\rceil$, append the <unit fraction> $1/q$, and replace the residual by $(nq-d)/(dq)$ in lowest terms. This is a <greedy algorithm>: the chosen <unit fraction> is the largest one not exceeding the residual.

The inequalities $q-1<d/n\leq q$ imply $0\leq nq-d<n$. Thus the numerator of the reduced residual strictly decreases while positive, proving <algorithm termination> by a nonnegative-<integer> <loop variant>. The <loop invariant> is that the accumulated <sum> plus the residual is the original <rational number>. Since $q\geq2$ and $n/d<1/(q-1)$, the new residual is less than $1/(q(q-1))\leq1/q$. If it remains positive, its next chosen denominator is therefore strictly greater than $q$; this expansion also satisfies the distinct-denominator convention. The proof does not bound the denominators by the input's fixed-width range. <Arbitrary-precision arithmetic>, checked conversion, or a different representable expansion can therefore be necessary in code.

= Rational arithmetic
{parent=Fraction}

Exact arithmetic on <rational numbers> represented by <integer> numerators and nonzero <integer> denominators. Common factors can be removed with the <greatest common divisor>. Exact <rational arithmetic> avoids rounding error when deciding equality or integrality.

= Cross multiplication
{parent=Fraction}

For nonzero denominators, $a/b=c/d$ exactly when $ad=bc$. For positive denominators, $a/b<c/d$ exactly when $ad<bc$. This compares <fractions> with exact <integer arithmetic>, avoiding rounding from <floating-point arithmetic>.

= Farey fraction
{c}
{parent=Farey sequence}

A member of a <Farey sequence>. Two distinct level-$Q$ fractions have ordinary distance at least $Q^{-2}$. On the quotient circle identify zero and one before forming a set; the shorter wrapped distance of two remaining distinct fractions obeys the same bound.

= Reduced fraction
{parent=Rational number}

A reduced fraction $a/b$ has <coprime integers> $a,b$ and positive denominator $b$. Distinct reduced fractions with denominators at most $Q$ differ by at least $Q^{-2}$, since their difference has a nonzero <integer> numerator over a denominator at most $Q^2$.

= Decimal preperiod
{parent=Repeating decimal}
{title2=$k_0$}

The shortest prefix before the repeating tail of a <decimal representation>. For a <reduced fraction> with <denominator> $2^u5^v m$, where $m>1$ is <coprime> to ten, its length is $\max(u,v)$; the tail length is given by <decimal period from multiplicative order>.

= Eventually periodic positional expansion of a rational number
{parent=Rational number}

A positional expansion in an integer base represents a rational number exactly when its digit sequence is eventually periodic. Long division proves the forward implication because only finitely many remainders occur; summing a periodic tail as a <geometric series> proves the converse.

= Modular multiplicative inverse
{title2=$a^{-1}\pmod n$}
{parent=Number theory}
{wiki}

= Modular inverse
{synonym}

For <integers> $a$ and $n>1$, a modular multiplicative inverse is a <congruence class> $b$ satisfying $ab\equiv1\pmod n$. It exists exactly when $\gcd(a,n)=1$. Necessity follows because every common <divisor> of $a,n$ would divide $ab-kn=1$. Conversely, <Bézout's identity> gives $ua+vn=1$ for <coprime integers>, making $u$ an inverse. This is the basic bridge from <greatest common divisor> calculations to reversible <modular arithmetic>.

For example, $5\cdot7-2\cdot17=1$, so $5^{-1}\equiv7\pmod{17}$. The <modular congruence> $5x\equiv3\pmod{17}$ therefore has the unique solution class $x\equiv21\equiv4\pmod{17}$. Multiplication by $5$ sends all seventeen residue classes to different classes; multiplying by $7$ reverses it. The inverse criterion and its algorithmic construction are presented in https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-200-principles-of-discrete-applied-mathematics-spring-2024/mit18_200_s24_lec15.pdf[Ankur Moitra, MIT 18.200, 2024, modular inverses and Euclid's algorithm].

By contrast, $5$ has no <modular inverse> modulo $15$. Its products have only residues $0,5,10$. Consequently $5x\equiv3\pmod{15}$ has no solution, while $5x\equiv10\pmod{15}$ has five solution classes, $x\equiv2,5,8,11,14$. Dividing by a noninvertible residue would discard this distinction. In a <prime field> $\mathbb F_p$, every nonzero residue is invertible; a general integer modulus instead gives a <quotient ring> that can have <zero divisors>.

= Newton iteration for modular inversion
{parent=Modular multiplicative inverse}
{c}
{title2=$x\gets x(2-ax)$}

If $ax\equiv1\pmod m$, the update $x'=x(2-ax)$ satisfies $ax'\equiv1\pmod{m^2}$ because $1-ax'=(1-ax)^2$. For odd $a$, starting with $x=1$ <modulo> $2$ and repeatedly doubling the number of correct bits computes its inverse <modulo> a <power of two>.

= Modular inversion
{parent=Modular multiplicative inverse}

The computation of an <integer> $x$ satisfying $ax\equiv1\pmod m$. An inverse exists exactly when $\gcd(a,m)=1$. The <extended Euclidean algorithm> produces such an $x$ from a <Bézout identity> $ax+my=1$.

= Batch modular inversion
{parent=Modular multiplicative inverse}

= Single inversion trick
{synonym}

= Batch inversion
{synonym}

For invertible <residue classes> $a_1,\ldots,a_n$, all their <modular inverses> can be recovered with one inversion and a linear number of multiplications. Store prefix products $P_i=a_1\cdots a_i$, invert $P_n$ once, and sweep backward: if the current inverse is $P_i^{-1}$, then $a_i^{-1}=P_i^{-1}P_{i-1}$ and $P_{i-1}^{-1}=P_i^{-1}a_i$. The same construction works in any commutative <group>.

= Carmichael's function
{synonym}
{c}

For an <integer> $n>1$, the <Carmichael function> $\lambda(n)$ is the <exponent of a finite group> for the <unit group> $U(n)=(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^\times$: the least <positive integer> $m$ for which
$$
a^m\equiv1\pmod n\qquad\text{for every }a\text{ with }\gcd(a,n)=1.
$$
Here the <greatest common divisor> condition selects the <units>. <Euler's totient function> $\varphi(n)$ counts them; the <Carmichael function> gives their least common return exponent.<Notes on primitive lambda-roots by Peter J. Cameron and others>[pp. 7–8, definition]{ref}

Modulo eight, the <units> are $1,3,5,7$, and their squares are all congruent to one. Their <multiplicative orders> are $1,2,2,2$, so $\lambda(8)=2$ although $\varphi(8)=4$. Modulo fifteen, the <units> $1,2,4,7,8,11,13,14$ have <multiplicative orders> $1,4,2,4,4,2,4,2$. For example, the powers of two return through $1,2,4,8,1$. Thus $\lambda(15)=4$ although $\varphi(15)=8$; the <multiplicative order> of four is only two.

\Image[carmichael-unit-orders-common-exponent.png]
{title=Individual unit orders divide the least common return exponent modulo eight and fifteen}

The three quantities are related by
$$
\lambda(n)=\mathop{\operatorname{lcm}}_{\substack{1\leq a<n\\\gcd(a,n)=1}}\operatorname{ord}_n(a),\qquad
\operatorname{ord}_n(a)\mid\lambda(n)\mid\varphi(n).
$$
A common return exponent must be a multiple of every <multiplicative order>, hence of their <least common multiple>. Conversely, that <least common multiple> makes every <unit> return to one. <Lagrange's theorem> makes each <multiplicative order> divide the <group order> $\varphi(n)$, proving the final <divisibility>. The <Carmichael function> applies to all <units> at once, while the <multiplicative order> concerns a chosen <unit>.

For the <prime factorization> $n=\prod_{i=1}^r p_i^{e_i}$ with distinct <prime numbers> $p_i$ and $e_i\geq1$, the formulas are
$$
\lambda(n)=\operatorname{lcm}\bigl(\lambda(p_1^{e_1}),\ldots,\lambda(p_r^{e_r})\bigr),\qquad
\lambda(p^e)=p^{e-1}(p-1)\quad(p\text{ an odd prime}),
$$
$$
\lambda(2^e)=
\begin{cases}
1,&e=1,\\
2,&e=2,\\
2^{e-2},&e\geq3.
\end{cases}
$$
These are <least common multiples>, rather than products: $\lambda(15)=\operatorname{lcm}(2,4)=4$. The convention $\lambda(1)=1$ handles the trivial case separately.<Notes on primitive lambda-roots by Peter J. Cameron and others>[p. 8, Proposition 5.1(a–c)]{ref}

The <Chinese remainder theorem for unit groups> explains the <least common multiple>: a power returns to one in every <prime power> component exactly when it is a multiple of each component's <exponent of a finite group>. For an odd <prime power>, the <unit group> is a <cyclic group> of <group order> $p^{e-1}(p-1)$. For $e\geq3$, the <unit group> modulo $2^e$ is a <direct product of groups> $C_2\times C_{2^{e-2}}$, generated by the classes of $-1$ and five, so its <exponent of a finite group> is $2^{e-2}$.<A Computational Introduction to Number Theory and Algebra by Victor Shoup>[Section 7.5, pp. 203–205, Theorem 7.28 and its proof]{ref}

This gives a uniform reduction for <modular exponentiation>: for a <unit> $a$ and a nonnegative <integer> $k$, reduce $k$ modulo $\lambda(n)$. For example, $2^{1001}\equiv2\pmod{15}$ because $1001\equiv1\pmod4$. The <unit> hypothesis matters: $3^2\equiv1\pmod8$, but $2^2\not\equiv1\pmod8$. Computing the formula assumes the <prime factorization> is available. The same common-exponent viewpoint explains <Carmichael numbers>: a <composite number> $n$ passes the <Fermat primality test> for every <unit> base exactly when $\lambda(n)\mid n-1$. For example, $\lambda(561)=\operatorname{lcm}(2,10,16)=80\mid560$.

= Multiplicative order
{parent=Number theory}
{wiki}
{title2=$\operatorname{ord}_n(a)$}

For <coprime integers> $a,n$ with $n>1$, the <multiplicative order> is the least <positive integer> $d$ such that
$$
d=\operatorname{ord}_n(a),\qquad a^d\equiv1\pmod n.
$$
It counts the distinct powers in the <cyclic subgroup> generated by the <congruence class> of $a$. Thus it is an <order of a group element> in the <unit group> $(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^\times$.<A Computational Introduction to Number Theory and Algebra by Victor Shoup>[pp. 153 and 156–157, Theorem 6.29]{ref}

For example, the <modular exponentiation> $2^k\bmod7$, starting at $k=0$, produces $1,2,4,1,2,4,\ldots$. The first return to one takes three steps, so $\operatorname{ord}_7(2)=3$. Starting at the <congruence class> three and repeatedly multiplying by two instead gives $3,6,5,3,\ldots$: these are the other three <units> modulo seven, outside the <cyclic subgroup> generated by two.

\Image[modular-power-orbits-modulo-seven.png]
{title=Multiplication by two modulo seven has two cycles; its powers follow the cycle through one}

<Euler's theorem> guarantees a return to one at exponent $\varphi(n)$, where $\varphi$ is <Euler's totient function>. Thus the <multiplicative order> exists for every <unit>.<Euler’s Theorem by Keith Conrad>[p. 1, Theorem 1.2; pp. 2–3, permutation of the units and cancellation]{ref} In fact,
$$
\operatorname{ord}_n(a)\mid\varphi(n).
$$
By <Lagrange's theorem>, the size of the <cyclic subgroup> divides the size of the <unit group>, which is <Euler's totient function>. For two modulo seven, this is $3\mid6$; the <multiplicative order> need not equal <Euler's totient function>. If $a\equiv1\pmod n$, its <multiplicative order> is one.

The <coprime integers> hypothesis is necessary: if $a^d=1+kn$, any common <divisor> of $a$ and $n$ divides one. For instance, powers of two modulo eight give $1,2,4,0,0,\ldots$, with no positive-exponent return to one. This <sequence> eventually settles, but two has no <multiplicative order> modulo eight.

= Decimal period from multiplicative order
{parent=Multiplicative order}
{title2=$\operatorname{ord}_q(10)$}

= Decimal period
{synonym}

For a <reduced fraction> $p/q$ with $0<p<q$, $q>1$, and $q,10$ <coprime integers>, its <decimal representation> repeats immediately after the decimal point. The shortest repeating block has length
$$
L=\operatorname{ord}_q(10),\qquad L\mid\varphi(q).
$$
Here $\operatorname{ord}$ denotes <multiplicative order>, and $\varphi$ denotes <Euler's totient function>. Changing the <numerator> preserves the <decimal period> as long as the <fraction> remains reduced.<Orders of Units in Modular Arithmetic by Keith Conrad>[pp. 7–8, Theorem 4.5 and Remark 4.9]{ref}

For example, <long division> of $1/7$ gives the <remainders> $1,3,2,6,4,5,1$, with emitted digits $1,4,2,8,5,7$. Consequently
$$
\frac17=0.\overline{142857},\qquad \operatorname{ord}_7(10)=6.
$$
Each <remainder> determines both the next digit and the next <remainder>; returning to one restarts the same six steps.

\Image[long-division-remainder-cycles.png]
{title=Long-division remainders explain the six-digit repeat of one seventh and the two-digit prefix of one twenty-eighth}

More generally, initialize <long division> with $r_0=p$. At each step, <division with remainder> gives
$$
10r_{j-1}=qd_j+r_j,\qquad 0\leq d_j\leq9,\qquad 0\leq r_j<q,
$$
so $r_j\equiv p10^j\pmod q$. Since $p,q$ are <coprime integers>, the <modular inverse> of $p$ shows that $r_L=r_0$ exactly when $10^L\equiv1\pmod q$. To see why the digit block cannot repeat sooner than the <remainders>, a purely <repeating decimal> with a block of $L$ digits represents $D/(10^L-1)$ for an <integer> $D$. A <reduced fraction> $p/q$ with that value forces $q\mid10^L-1$. Hence the shortest digit repeat and the <multiplicative order> coincide.

Knowing the <decimal period> predicts how many digits must be computed and stored: one block determines the whole <repeating decimal>. It also constructs the block directly. With $L=\operatorname{ord}_q(10)$, the <integer>
$$
D=\frac{p(10^L-1)}q
$$
is the block, padded on the left with zeros to $L$ digits. For $1/11$, $L=2$ and $D=9$, so the block is $09$ and $1/11=0.\overline{09}$. Reduction matters: $7/21=1/3=0.\overline3$ has <decimal period> one, whereas $\operatorname{ord}_{21}(10)=6$.

If the reduced <denominator> contains <prime factors> two or five, ten has no <multiplicative order> modulo that whole <denominator>. Write
$$
q=2^u5^v m,\qquad \gcd(m,10)=1,\qquad k_0=\max(u,v).
$$
The <decimal preperiod> has exactly $k_0$ digits. If $m>1$, the remaining <repeating decimal> has <decimal period> $\operatorname{ord}_m(10)$. For instance, $28=2^2\cdot7$ gives $1/28=0.03\overline{571428}$: two prefix digits, then six repeating digits. If $m=1$, the <decimal representation> is a <terminating decimal> with exactly $k_0$ fractional places. We use the representation ending in zeros, rather than its alternative ending in nines; for example $1/40=0.025$ terminates after three places.

A short derivation explains both cases. After $k$ digits, multiplying the <reduced fraction> by $10^k$ and discarding its <integer> part leaves a <reduced fraction> whose <denominator> is $q/\gcd(q,10^k)$. This <denominator> first becomes <coprime> to ten at $k=k_0$. It then equals $m$, giving an immediate repeat when $m>1$, or an <integer> when $m=1$. The same <long division> argument works for <positional notation> in any base $b\geq2$: for a reduced <denominator> $q>1$ <coprime> to $b$, the shortest repeating block has length $\operatorname{ord}_q(b)$.

= Cyclic decimal divisibility
{parent=Multiplicative order}

For an $L$-digit decimal integer $n$, moving its leading digit to the end gives a cyclic rotation $c_1(n)$ satisfying
$$
c_1(n)\equiv10n\pmod{10^L-1}.
$$
Thus, when a modulus $d$ divides $10^L-1$, every rotation has the same divisibility by $d$ as $n$, up to multiplication by a power of ten.

= Periodic modular exponentiation
{parent=Multiplicative order}

For a unit $a$ modulo $N$, the function $x\mapsto a^x\bmod N$ is periodic on the integers.

= Order as the exact period of modular exponentiation
{parent=Periodic modular exponentiation}
{title2=$a^x\equiv a^y\pmod n\iff\operatorname{ord}_n(a)\mid x-y$}

Let $a,n$ be <coprime integers> with $n>1$, and let $d=\operatorname{ord}_n(a)$ be their <multiplicative order>. For <integers> $x,y$,
$$
a^x\equiv a^y\pmod n\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad d\mid(x-y).
$$
Negative exponents mean powers of the <modular inverse> of $a$. Thus <periodic modular exponentiation> has smallest positive <period> $d$, and $1,a,\ldots,a^{d-1}$ are distinct <congruence classes>.<A Computational Introduction to Number Theory and Algebra by Victor Shoup>[pp. 156–157, Theorem 6.29; multiplicative notation on p. 153]{ref}

This lets a huge <modular exponentiation> reuse a short list: reduce its exponent modulo the <multiplicative order>, then look up the corresponding power. For example, $1000\equiv1\pmod3$ gives $2^{1000}\equiv2\pmod7$, while $1001\equiv2\pmod3$ gives $2^{1001}\equiv4\pmod7$. When the <multiplicative order> is unknown, <Euler's theorem> permits reduction modulo <Euler's totient function>, though that <period> may be longer. <Repeated squaring> remains useful when computing either the unreduced or the reduced power.

For a proof outline, multiply $a^x\equiv a^y$ by the <modular inverse> of $a^y$, leaving $a^{x-y}\equiv1$. Apply <Euclidean division> to write $x-y=td+r$ with $0\leq r<d$. Then $a^{x-y}\equiv a^r$; minimality of the <multiplicative order> forces $r=0$. The reverse implication follows from $a^d\equiv1$. The <coprime integers> hypothesis enables cancellation: modulo eight, $2^3\equiv2^4\equiv0$, but this repetition supplies no <multiplicative order> for two.

= Euclidean algorithm
{parent=Number theory}
{wiki}

= Euclid’s algorithm
{synonym}
{c}

For positive <integers> $a,b$, the Euclidean algorithm repeatedly performs <division with remainder>: write $a=qb+r$ with $0\leq r<b$, then replace $(a,b)$ by $(b,r)$ until the second entry is zero. The final nonzero entry is their <greatest common divisor>. A remainder preserves the set of common <divisors>: an <integer> divides $a$ and $b$ exactly when it divides $b$ and $a-qb$. The positive remainders strictly decrease, so the calculation terminates.

The complete calculation for $252$ and $198$ is
$$
\begin{aligned}
252&=1\cdot198+54,\\
198&=3\cdot54+36,\\
54&=1\cdot36+18,\\
36&=2\cdot18+0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence **$\gcd(252,198)=18$**. The <fraction> $252/198$ reduces to $14/11$ by dividing numerator and denominator by that common factor. The same remainder calculation has a geometric interpretation: remove squares from a $252$-by-$198$ <rectangle>, then repeat on the remaining strip. Each division step records how many equal squares fit and the width of the new strip.

\Image[euclidean-algorithm-rectangles.png]
{title=Successive square removals record the Euclidean algorithm for 252 and 198}

The procedure does more than test individual <divisors>. Each step replaces the whole common-divisor problem by a smaller equivalent one. Euclid's Book VII, Proposition 2 describes the corresponding repeated-subtraction construction; batching those subtractions into <division with remainder> gives the present formulation. https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/java/elements/bookVII/propVII2.html[Euclid, Elements VII.2, greatest common measure]{ref}

= Extended Euclidean algorithm
{parent=Euclidean algorithm}
{wiki}

= Extended-GCD
{synonym}

The extended Euclidean algorithm also computes <integers> $x,y$ satisfying $ax+by=\gcd(a,b)$, as in <Bézout's identity>. Back-substitution in the calculation under the <Euclidean algorithm> gives
$$
18=54-36=54-(198-3\cdot54)=4(252-198)-198=4\cdot252-5\cdot198.
$$
Thus $(x,y)=(4,-5)$ solves $252x+198y=18$. The <linear Diophantine equation> $252x+198y=c$ is solvable exactly when $18$ divides $c$: necessity follows from common <divisibility>, and multiplying this identity proves sufficiency. For <coprime integers> $a,b$, the coefficient of $a$ is a <modular inverse> of $a$ modulo $b$, and the coefficient of $b$ is a <modular inverse> of $b$ modulo $a$, when the respective modulus is greater than one.

= Linear Diophantine equation
{parent=Euclidean algorithm}

A linear Diophantine equation in two variables has the form $ax+by=c$ with integer coefficients and seeks integer solutions.

= All solutions of a linear Diophantine equation
{parent=Linear Diophantine equation}
{title2=$(x,y)=(x_0,y_0)+t(b/d,-a/d)$}

If $a,b$ are nonzero <integers>, $d=\gcd(a,b)$, and $(x_0,y_0)$ is one solution of the <linear Diophantine equation> $ax+by=c$, then all solutions are
$$
x=x_0+\frac bd\,t,\qquad y=y_0-\frac ad\,t,\qquad t\in\mathbb Z.
$$
Subtract two solutions and use the fact that $a/d$ and $b/d$ are <coprime integers>. The <linear Diophantine equation solvability criterion> says that a particular solution exists exactly when $d$ divides $c$; the <extended Euclidean algorithm> constructs one. This parametrization separates existence from the complete family of answers.

= Linear Diophantine equation solvability criterion
{parent=Linear Diophantine equation}

The equation $ax+by=c$ has an integer solution exactly when $\gcd(a,b)$ divides $c$. Bézout's identity proves sufficiency, while every integer linear combination of $a,b$ is divisible by their greatest common divisor.

= Brahmagupta-Fibonacci identity
{c}

= Brahmagupta-Fibonacci two-square identity
{synonym}
{c}

The identity $(a^2+b^2)(c^2+d^2)=(ac-bd)^2+(ad+bc)^2$ shows that a product of two sums of two squares is another sum of two squares. It follows by multiplying <Gaussian integers> and taking their squared absolute values.

= Congruence lattice proof of the prime sum of two squares
{parent=Sum of two squares theorem}
{title2=$\Lambda=\{(a,b)\in\mathbb Z^2:b\equiv ua\pmod p\}$}

If $u^2\equiv-1\pmod p$, this lattice has basis $(1,u),(0,p)$ and covolume $p$. The <Minkowski convex body theorem> applied to a disk of squared radius strictly between $4p/\pi$ and $2p$ gives a nonzero lattice vector with $0<a^2+b^2<2p$. The congruence makes its squared length divisible by $p$, forcing $a^2+b^2=p$. The strict radius bounds avoid assuming the critical-volume form of Minkowski's theorem.
