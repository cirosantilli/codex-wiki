= Cosmic microwave background
{title2=CMB}
{wiki}

= CMB
{c}
{synonym}

The <cosmic microwave background> is nearly isotropic thermal <electromagnetic radiation>. Its spectrum connects <radio receiver> measurements with the universe's thermal history: <redshift preservation of a thermal photon spectrum> cools a <blackbody> spectrum as space expands. The 1965 Princeton analysis described this relation.<Cosmic Black-Body Radiation by R. H. Dicke and others>[pp.415–416: expansion cools radiation while preserving a thermal spectrum]{ref}

A <radiometer> cannot label a received <photon> as cosmic, atmospheric or instrumental. Its measured <power> combines the incoming radiation with the receiver response. The useful question is which comparisons and controls can isolate a sky contribution. This makes the <Penzias-Wilson microwave background experiment> an example of both <observational cosmology> and <measurement calibration>.

= WMAP
{synonym}
{c}

A space mission that mapped the temperature and polarization of the <cosmic microwave background>. Its nine-year analysis separates foreground emission from the microwave sky signal and fits cosmological models to the resulting maps. Combined-data fits additionally use other observations; they are not measurements from microwave maps alone.<Nine-Year Wilkinson Microwave Anisotropy Probe (WMAP) Observations: Final Maps and Results by C. L. Bennett and others>[Version 1 abstract, maps, foreground separation and combined-data fits]{ref}

= Penzias-Wilson microwave background experiment
{c}
{parent=Cosmic microwave background}

In 1965, <Arno Penzias> and <Robert Woodrow Wilson> reported measurements with a 20-foot horn-reflector <antenna> at $4.080\,\mathrm{GHz}$. They manually switched a traveling-wave <maser> receiver between the horn and a helium-cooled reference. From $6.7\,\mathrm K$ zenith <antenna temperature>, atmospheric and antenna/ground contributions left an excess $3.5\pm1.0\,\mathrm K$.<A Measurement of Excess Antenna Temperature at 4080 Mc per Second by Arno Allan Penzias and Robert Woodrow Wilson>[pp.419–420: apparatus, temperature contributions and residual]{ref}

Elevation scans estimated the atmospheric term. Taping horn seams produced no measurable change; a nearby transmitter tested ground response. These controls addressed distinct possible contaminations.<A Measurement of Excess Antenna Temperature at 4080 Mc per Second by Arno Allan Penzias and Robert Woodrow Wilson>[p.420: elevation, seam and back-lobe tests]{ref}

\Image[cmb-horn-radiometer-and-reference-load.png]
{title=Comparing a sky horn with a cold reference separates receiver response from incident radiation}
{description=Original functional diagram inspired by the reported comparison, not a reconstruction or photograph of the historical apparatus. The added warm reference illustrates a generic <two-load radiometer calibration>; it is not claimed to have been switched in this form in the original experiment. Radiation from the sky, atmosphere, ground and antenna can contribute at the receiver input.}

The comparison does not make all nuisance contributions disappear. A reference can establish a response scale, but atmospheric emission and pickup through unwanted <antenna> directions are also real incident radiation. In his later account, <Robert Woodrow Wilson> describes these competing contributions as central to a <radio astronomy> measurement.<The Cosmic Microwave Background Radiation by Robert Woodrow Wilson>[1978 lecture, introduction and sectionII: sky, ground, atmosphere and instrument contributions]{ref}

The Princeton group proposed primordial thermal radiation as an interpretation and called for more <wavelength> measurements. Their separate $3\,\mathrm{cm}$ <radiometer> had not yet produced results.<Cosmic Black-Body Radiation by R. H. Dicke and others>[pp.415–416: interpretation, Princeton apparatus and need for further wavelengths]{ref} A single-frequency excess therefore does not by itself establish a complete <Planck spectrum>. Reproducibility across independent instruments, observing directions and frequencies tests the interpretation further.

= Microwave radiometer calibration example
{parent=Penzias-Wilson microwave background experiment}
{title2=$S=g(T+T_r)$}

Consider an original idealized narrow-band model for one matched spatial mode and one <polarization of waves>. In the classical <Rayleigh-Jeans law> regime, an available thermal <power> can be written $P=k_BT\Delta\nu$, where $\Delta\nu$ is the effective <bandwidth> and $T$ the equivalent input <noise temperature>. Assume a linear detector with stable positive gain and absorb its <bandwidth>, <Boltzmann constant> and response factor into $g$. Then the output is $S=g(T+T_r)$, with input-referred <receiver noise temperature> $T_r$. These are model assumptions, not a reconstruction of the historical electronics.

Two references with known <noise temperatures> $T_h\ne T_c$ give
$$
S_h=g(T_h+T_r),\qquad S_c=g(T_c+T_r),
$$
so the <two-load radiometer calibration> yields
$$
g=\frac{S_h-S_c}{T_h-T_c},\qquad
T_r=\frac{T_hS_c-T_cS_h}{S_h-S_c},\qquad
T_s=T_c+(T_h-T_c)\frac{S_s-S_c}{S_h-S_c}.
$$
Subtracting the reference equations eliminates the unknown receiver term; substituting back determines it. An arbitrary stable output offset can likewise be eliminated from the interpolation for $T_s$, although separating that offset from $T_r$ then requires additional information.

For deliberately chosen inputs, set $T_h=300\,\mathrm K$, $T_c=4.2\,\mathrm K$, $T_r=40\,\mathrm K$ and $g=2\,\mathrm{mV/K}$. These are equivalent <noise temperatures>; physical load thermometry must be converted consistently at the observing <frequency>. The reference outputs are $S_h=680\,\mathrm{mV}$ and $S_c=88.4\,\mathrm{mV}$. A sky-input output $S_s=90\,\mathrm{mV}$ gives $T_s=5.0\,\mathrm K$. If independent estimates assign $1.4\,\mathrm K$ to atmosphere, $0.5\,\mathrm K$ to antenna loss and $0.1\,\mathrm K$ to ground pickup in this lumped additive model, the residual is $3.0\,\mathrm K$.

\Image[cmb-radiometer-calibration-and-planck-correction.png]
{title=Original calibration arithmetic and the distinction between an antenna temperature and a thermodynamic temperature}
{description=Chosen reference temperatures, receiver response and additive contributions; none are fitted historical measurements. The final panel calculates the <Rayleigh-Jeans law> equivalent <antenna temperature> of a stipulated $3\,\mathrm K$ <blackbody>. A change in <frequency> changes that equivalent temperature even though the underlying thermodynamic temperature is fixed.}

The subtraction exposes what a noise budget must achieve. A $1\,\mathrm K$ error in the assigned atmospheric term becomes a $1\,\mathrm K$ error in the residual, even with exact detector calibration. Multiplicative attenuation requires a different correction: if an isothermal atmosphere has transmission $t$, its ideal contribution is $tT_{\rm cosmic}+(1-t)T_{\rm atm}$ in the same classical equivalent-temperature convention. Recovering $T_{\rm cosmic}$ then requires both subtracting atmospheric emission and dividing by $t$. The additive toy budget applies when its terms already refer to the same receiver input.

There is also a conditioning issue. If $S_h-S_c$ is small, a fixed output error strongly changes the inferred <temperature>. Widely separated reference temperatures improve the scale determination only if the receiver remains linear over that range; measuring the reference repeatedly tests stability rather than proving linearity. The experiment therefore motivates a model, a calibration and a set of independent checks, each with its own assumptions.

= Thermodynamic and Rayleigh-Jeans antenna temperatures
{parent=Microwave radiometer calibration example}
{title2=$T_A=\frac{h\nu/k_B}{e^{h\nu/(k_BT)}-1}$}

For a matched receiver accepting one spatial mode and one <polarization of waves>, the thermal contribution to the available spectral <power> of a <blackbody> is
$$
\frac{dP}{d\nu}=\frac{h\nu}{e^{h\nu/(k_BT)}-1}.
$$
This expression excludes a zero-point term; it is the thermally emitted contribution. Defining the <Rayleigh-Jeans law> equivalent <antenna temperature> by $dP/d\nu=k_BT_A$ gives the displayed relation. With $x=h\nu/(k_BT)$,
$$
\frac{T_A}{T}=\frac{x}{e^x-1}=1-\frac x2+\frac{x^2}{12}+O(x^4).
$$
Thus $T_A\simeq T$ requires $x\ll1$. It is an approximation with a frequency-dependent error, rather than a redefinition of thermodynamic <temperature>.

For the stipulated $T=3\,\mathrm K$ and $\nu=4.080\,\mathrm{GHz}$, $h\nu/k_B\simeq0.19581\,\mathrm K$ and $T_A\simeq2.90316\,\mathrm K$. Replacing $T_A$ by $T$ here would differ by about $3.23\%$. At much higher <frequencies>, the approximation fails more strongly. A real instrument averages over its measured <frequency response> and <antenna beam pattern>, so using a single central <frequency> adds a narrow-band approximation.

= COBE
{c}

The <Cosmic Background Explorer> connected two questions: does the <cosmic microwave background> follow a <Planck spectrum>, and how does its <temperature> vary across the sky? <John C. Mather> and <George F. Smoot> shared the 2006 <Nobel Prize in Physics> for these discoveries.<George F. Smoot – Facts by Nobel Prize Outreach>[2006 award and discovery summary]{ref} The <FIRAS> and <COBE DMR> comparisons below explain why measuring spectral shape and measuring angular structure require different information.

= COBE FIRAS blackbody spectrum experiment
{c}
{parent=Far Infrared Absolute Spectrophotometer}

<FIRAS> compared sky radiation with an adjustable internal <blackbody> using an <interferometer> and cold <bolometers>. An external <blackbody> could cover the sky entrance for <measurement calibration>. The team adjusted the internal <temperature> to minimize the difference signal.<Nobel Lecture: From the Big Bang to the Nobel Prize and beyond by John C. Mather>[§III.D, pp.1337–1338; §V.A, p.1341]{ref}

Comparisons across detectors and mirror strokes tested instrumental errors; sky mapping separated <Milky Way> emission. <John C. Mather>'s lecture reports a final root-mean-square spectral departure of 50 parts per million of peak brightness. This is neither the preliminary 1990 precision nor a relative error in every spectral bin. Calibration drift and nonideal reference emission required correction.<Nobel Lecture: From the Big Bang to the Nobel Prize and beyond by John C. Mather>[§V.A, p.1342: calibration checks and final spectrum]{ref}

\Image[cobe-firas-comparator-and-temperature-residual.png]
{title=FIRAS comparison principle and a chosen blackbody temperature residual}
{description=Functional comparison, not an optical layout. Calculated curves use stipulated <temperatures>, not measured COBE data.}

The advantage of a <null measurement> is that a small difference can be resolved without assigning the same absolute precision to a large detector output. Nevertheless, matching an internal reference is meaningful only after its relation to an independently calibrated <blackbody> has been tested. A bound on departures from a <Planck spectrum> constrains energy redistribution after <thermal equilibrium>; it does not require the <cosmic microwave background> to have exactly the same <temperature> in every direction.

= Temperature perturbation of a Planck spectrum
{parent=COBE FIRAS blackbody spectrum experiment}
{title2=$\delta B_\nu/B_\nu=[x/(1-e^{-x})]\,\delta T/T$}

For a <Planck function> expressed as <spectral radiance> per <frequency> interval,
$$
B_\nu(T)=\frac{2h\nu^3}{c^2}\frac{1}{e^x-1},\qquad x=\frac{h\nu}{k_BT},
$$
$h$ is the <Planck constant>, $c$ the <speed of light> and $k_B$ the <Boltzmann constant>. Differentiating at fixed <frequency> gives
$$
\frac{\partial B_\nu}{\partial T}=\frac{B_\nu}{T}\frac{x}{1-e^{-x}},\qquad
B_\nu(T+\delta T)-B_\nu(T)=\frac{\partial B_\nu}{\partial T}\delta T+O(\delta T^2).
$$
The proof uses $\partial_Tx=-x/T$ and the <chain rule>. At low <frequency>, the multiplier tends to one, recovering the <Rayleigh-Jeans law>. At high <frequency> it grows approximately as $x$: the same fractional <temperature> change has a larger fractional brightness effect on the declining spectral tail.

For an original example choose $T=2.725\,\mathrm K$ and $\delta T=1\,\mathrm{mK}$. At $100\,\mathrm{GHz}$, $x=1.76119$, so the first-order fractional residual is $7.80415\times10^{-4}$. At $300\,\mathrm{GHz}$, $x=5.28357$, it is $1.94881\times10^{-3}$. Both spectra are exact <blackbodies>: this residual is a <temperature> mismatch, not evidence of a nonthermal spectrum. Fitting <temperature> before assessing remaining spectral residuals therefore removes a physically distinct degree of freedom. The figure divides by a fixed peak brightness rather than by the rapidly vanishing high-<frequency> tail.

= Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect
{c}
{parent=Cosmic microwave background}
{wiki}

Hot <electrons> in <galaxy> clusters scatter <photons> of the <cosmic microwave background>, producing a characteristic spectral distortion. The thermal contribution measures a line-of-sight integral of <electron> <pressure>, whereas X-ray emission depends approximately on the integral of <electron> density squared times a temperature-dependent emissivity. Combining the two with a geometrical model can constrain a cluster's physical size and hence its <angular diameter distance>. <Temperature> structure and departures from the assumed geometry introduce systematic uncertainty.

= Cluster distance from Sunyaev-Zeldovich and X-ray signals
{parent=Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect}
{title2=$d_A\propto(\Delta T)^2\theta/[S_XT_e^{3/2}(1+z)^4]$}

For a uniform spherical cluster of depth $\ell\propto\theta d_A$, <thermal bremsstrahlung> gives received bolometric flux $S_X\propto n_e^2T_e^{1/2}\ell^3/d_L^2$, while the <Rayleigh-Jeans law> limit of the <thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect> gives $|\Delta T|\propto n_eT_e\ell$. Eliminating $n_e$ and using $d_L=(1+z)^2d_A$ yields the displayed <angular diameter distance>. A model for the density profile fixes its numerical coefficient. Clumping, nonsphericity and <temperature> structure can bias this distance because the two observables weight the gas differently.

= Kinetic Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect
{parent=Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect}
{title2=$\Delta T/T=-\tau v_{\parallel}/c$}

Bulk motion of a scattering electron cloud relative to the <cosmic microwave background> gives a first-order Doppler <temperature> shift. With positive $v_{\parallel}$ directed away from the observer, the displayed sign applies, and $\tau$ is the <Thomson scattering> <optical depth>. In the nonrelativistic, single-scattering limit it has the spectrum of a small <blackbody> temperature change, unlike the <thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect>.

= Thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect
{parent=Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect}
{title2=$\Delta T/T=y[x\coth(x/2)-4]$}

For a <Planck photon distribution>, the hot-electron <Kompaneets equation> gives $\Delta n=yx e^x(e^x-1)^{-2}[x\coth(x/2)-4]$, where $x=h\nu/(k_BT_\gamma)$. The equivalent thermodynamic <temperature> perturbation has the displayed form, while the <Rayleigh-Jeans law> gives $\Delta T/T=-2y$. The distortion changes sign at $x\simeq3.830$. This first-order expression assumes a small <Compton y parameter> and nonrelativistic thermal <electrons>.

= Cosmic microwave background temperature in energy units
{parent=Cosmic microwave background}
{title2=$k_BT_0\simeq2.35\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{eV}=0.235\,\mathrm{meV}$}

The <cosmic microwave background> <temperature> is about $2.725\,\mathrm K$, corresponding to the displayed thermal <energy>. The conversion uses $k_B\simeq8.61733\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{eV/K}$. Thus $2.35\,\mathrm{meV}$ would correspond to about $27.3\,\mathrm K$, rather than the present <CMB>. Primary references are https://asd.gsfc.nasa.gov/archive/arcade/cmb_temperature.html[NASA's CMB temperature measurements] and https://physics.nist.gov/cgi-bin/cuu/Value?kev[NIST's Boltzmann constant in electronvolts per kelvin].

= Reionization
{parent=Cosmic microwave background}
{wiki}

Reionization returns some neutral cosmic gas to an ionized state after <cosmological recombination>. The resulting free electrons increase the <cosmological optical depth> through <Thomson scattering> and create a second contribution to the <cosmological visibility function>.

= Reionization optical depth in an open matter universe
{parent=Reionization}
{title2=$\kappa(z_{\rm re})$}

For full <hydrogen> <ionization> after instantaneous <reionization>, $n_{e0}=3\Omega_bH_0^2/(8\pi Gm_p)$ and $H(z)=H_0(1+z)\sqrt{1+\Omega_mz}$. Integrating <Thomson scattering> gives
$$
\kappa=\frac{\Omega_b C_H}{\Omega_m^2}\{u^3-1-3(1-\Omega_m)(u-1)\},\quad u=\sqrt{1+\Omega_mz_{\rm re}},\quad C_H=\frac{\sigma_TcH_0}{4\pi Gm_p}.
$$
For $\Omega_m=1$, $\kappa=\Omega_bC_H[(1+z_{\rm re})^{3/2}-1]$. At fixed baryon density and redshift, lowering the matter density reduces the expansion rate and increases the <optical depth>.

= Photon-budget reionization threshold
{parent=Reionization}
{title2=$f_{\rm coll}=N_{\rm req}/N_\gamma$}

If each collapsed baryon supplies $N_\gamma$ effective ionizing photons and $N_{\rm req}$ photons per cosmic baryon are required, the threshold collapsed fraction is $f_{\rm coll}=N_{\rm req}/N_\gamma$. The <Press-Schechter formalism> gives $\operatorname{erfc}[\delta_{\rm sc}/(\sqrt2\sigma(M_{\min},z))]=f_{\rm coll}$. Escape losses and recombinations must either be incorporated in the effective yield/requirement or counted separately, not twice.

= Photoevaporation of a dark-matter minihalo
{parent=Reionization}
{title2=$t_{\rm evap}\sim R_h/c_{s,\rm ion}$}

<Photoionization> can heat a shallow halo's gas well above its <virial temperature>. If the heated <sound speed> exceeds the escape-speed scale, the previous <hydrostatic equilibrium> is lost and a pressure-driven outflow removes gas. A crossing time gives a useful estimate, but photon supply, the gas profile, shielding and recombinations can delay the process. This particularly affects haloes whose <virial temperatures> are below the usual atomic cooling threshold and reduces their subsequent <star formation>.

= Reionization damping of cosmic microwave background temperature anisotropy
{parent=Reionization}
{title2=$e^{-\tau_{\rm re}}$}

Ignoring velocities and evolving potentials, a thin <reionization> screen transmits the primary <cosmic microwave background anisotropy> with amplitude $e^{-\tau_{\rm re}}$ and adds a direction-averaged source. A plane wave propagating a distance $\Delta\chi$ from recombination to the screen has monopole factor $j_0(k\Delta\chi)=\sin(k\Delta\chi)/(k\Delta\chi)$. This suppresses the rescattered source at $k\Delta\chi\gg1$, while coherent large-scale modes are unchanged to leading order.

= Primordial-amplitude–optical-depth degeneracy
{parent=Reionization damping of cosmic microwave background temperature anisotropy}
{title2=$A_s e^{-2\tau_{\rm re}}$}

The primary small-scale <cosmic microwave background power spectrum> measures the amplitude $A_s e^{-2\tau_{\rm re}}$, because <reionization> damps each temperature amplitude once. The unaffected large-scale temperature modes are few and limited by <cosmic variance>. Polarization and gravitational lensing provide additional information that partly breaks this degeneracy.
