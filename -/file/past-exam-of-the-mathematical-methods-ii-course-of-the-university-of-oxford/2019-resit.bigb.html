**The printed point is not on the printed surface, so the requested tangent plane does not exist.** At $(2,-2,9)$, the left side of the defining equation is $x^2y=-8$, whereas the right side is $4ze^{x+y}-11=25$. This is a direct counterexample to the premise that the stated point belongs to the <surface>.

To make the obstruction precise, set $G(x,y,z)=x^2y-4ze^{x+y}+11$. The specified <surface> is the <level set> $G=0$, but $G(2,-2,9)=-33$. Although $\nabla G(2,-2,9)=(-44,-32,-4)$, the resulting plane $11x+8y+z=15$ is tangent to the different <level set> $G=-33$ through that point. It is not an answer for the printed <surface>. A <tangent plane requires the point to lie on the surface>.

If the intended correction preserves $x=2,y=-2$, the surface equation instead forces $z=3/4$. At the corrected point $p_*=(2,-2,3/4)$,
$$
\nabla G(p_*)=(-11,1,-4).
$$
The corresponding conditional <tangent plane> is
$$
\boxed{11x-y+4z=27\qquad\text{at the corrected point }(2,-2,3/4).}
$$
This conditional calculation identifies a possible repair; it does not silently replace the source's coordinate $9$.

Assume $\lambda>0$, as required for normalization of the <exponential distribution>. Its <cumulative distribution function> is the integral of its <probability density function> from $-\infty$ to $x$:
$$
\boxed{F_X(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x<0,\\1-e^{-\lambda x},&x\geq0.\end{cases}}
$$
For the <expected value>, <integration by parts> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[X]
&=\int_0^\infty x\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\,dx\\
&=\left[-xe^{-\lambda x}\right]_0^\infty+\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda x}\,dx
=\frac1\lambda.
\end{aligned}
$$
Similarly,
$$
\mathbb E[X^2]=\left[-x^2e^{-\lambda x}\right]_0^\infty
+2\int_0^\infty xe^{-\lambda x}\,dx=\frac2{\lambda^2}.
$$
The endpoint terms vanish because exponential decay dominates each fixed power of $x$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[X]=\frac1\lambda,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E[X^2]-\mathbb E[X]^2=\frac1{\lambda^2}.}
$$
The mean has the same units as $X$, while the <variance> has their square; $\lambda$ has reciprocal units.

A <conservative vector field> can be written as $F=\nabla\Phi$ for a single-valued <scalar potential> $\Phi$. Its <line integral> between two points depends only on the endpoints, and its integral around every closed curve is zero. A necessary local test for a smooth planar field $F=(P,Q)$ is $P_y=Q_x$. On a <simply connected> domain such as the whole plane, this is also sufficient.

Here
$$
P_y=(3x^2+x^3y)e^{xy}=Q_x.
$$
An explicit <scalar potential> is even more decisive:
$$
\boxed{\Phi(x,y)=x^2e^{xy}+y^2,\qquad F=\nabla\Phi.}
$$
Indeed $\Phi_x=2xe^{xy}+x^2ye^{xy}$ and $\Phi_y=x^3e^{xy}+2y$. Thus the field is conservative on $\mathbb R^2$.

An <exact differential> is a <differential one-form> equal to $d\Phi$ on its domain; an <inexact differential> is not the differential of a single-valued potential there. For example, $d(xy)=y\,dx+x\,dy$ is exact. For the supplied form $\eta=42y\,dx+x\,dy$, the cross derivatives are
$$
\frac{\partial(42y)}{\partial y}=42,\qquad
\frac{\partial x}{\partial x}=1.
$$
They disagree, so $\eta$ is not even closed and hence cannot be exact. Equivalently, its counterclockwise <line integral> around the unit square is $1-42=-41\ne0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{42y\,dx+x\,dy\text{ is inexact}.}
$$
The connection is $F\cdot d r=d\Phi$: a <vector field> is conservative precisely when its work <differential one-form> is exact. Locally vanishing <curl> alone need not guarantee global exactness on a domain with holes.

For an oriented piecewise smooth <surface> $S$, a continuously differentiable <vector field> $F$ on a neighborhood of it, and its compatibly oriented boundary $C=\partial S$, <Stokes theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\oint_CF\cdot d r=\iint_S(\nabla\times F)\cdot n\,dS.}
$$
Here $n$ is the chosen unit <normal vector>, $dS$ is the unsigned <surface area element>, and $d r$ is the tangent displacement along $C$. The <right-hand rule> relates the boundary direction to $n$.

The supplied field has <curl> $\nabla\times F=(2z,2x,2y)$. Parametrize the plane by $r(x,y)=(x,y,1-x-y)$ over the triangle $D=\{x,y\geq0:x+y\leq1\}$. Its upward oriented <surface area element> is $(1,1,1)\,dx\,dy$, giving
$$
\iint_S(\nabla\times F)\cdot d\boldsymbol S
=\int_D2(x+y+z)\,dx\,dy
=2\operatorname{area}(D)=\boxed{1}.
$$
For the boundary <line integral>, take the positively oriented vertices $A=(1,0,0)$, $B=(0,1,0)$, $C=(0,0,1)$. Their projections run counterclockwise in the horizontal plane. With $0\leq t\leq1$, the three edges are
$$
r_{AB}=(1-t,t,0),\qquad r_{BC}=(0,1-t,t),\qquad r_{CA}=(t,0,1-t).
$$
On each edge, direct substitution gives $F(r(t))\cdot r'(t)=t^2+2t-1$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\oint_CF\cdot d r
=3\int_0^1(t^2+2t-1)\,dt=3\left(\frac13\right)=1.}
$$
The two independent computations agree, including the orientation sign.

Assume small planar oscillations about the vertical equilibrium, with the connecting <spring> relaxed in that equilibrium. Let $x_j=l\theta_j$ be the first-order horizontal <displacements> of the two <simple pendulums>. The gravitational restoring <force> on bob $j$ is $-(mg/l)x_j$; the spring extension to first order is $x_2-x_1$. Thus the linearized <equations of motion> are
$$
\begin{aligned}
m\ddot x_1&=-\frac{mg}{l}x_1+k(x_2-x_1),\\
m\ddot x_2&=-\frac{mg}{l}x_2+k(x_1-x_2).
\end{aligned}
$$
Adding and subtracting them diagonalizes the system. For $q_+=x_1+x_2$ and $q_-=x_1-x_2$,
$$
\ddot q_++\frac glq_+=0,\qquad
\ddot q_-+\left(\frac gl+\frac{2k}{m}\right)q_-=0.
$$
The <normal modes> and <angular frequencies> are therefore
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{mode shape}&\text{angular frequency}\\\hline
(1,1)^T&\omega_1=\sqrt{g/l}\\
(1,-1)^T&\omega_2=\sqrt{g/l+2k/m}
\end{array}}
$$
The in-phase mode does not stretch the spring, while the opposite-phase mode adds a restoring force of magnitude $2k$ times each bob's displacement. These are the small-oscillation frequencies, not exact amplitude-independent frequencies for arbitrary pendulum angles.

For a bounded <volume> $V$ with outward-oriented piecewise smooth boundary $S$, the <divergence theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\iint_SG\cdot n\,dS=\iiint_V\nabla\cdot G\,dV.}
$$
Here $G$ is a continuously differentiable <vector field> on a neighborhood of $V$, $n$ the outward unit <normal vector>, and $dV$ the <volume element>. The left side is its outward <flux>.

For a twice continuously differentiable field $F$, equality of mixed <partial derivatives> gives
$$
\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times F)
=(F_{z,yx}-F_{y,zx})+(F_{x,zy}-F_{z,xy})+(F_{y,xz}-F_{x,yz})=0.
$$
Apply the <divergence theorem> to $G=\nabla\times F$ to obtain
$$
\boxed{\iint_S(\nabla\times F)\cdot d\boldsymbol S=0.}
$$
The regularity hypotheses justify differentiating and integrating the <curl>; a field singularity cannot simply be ignored in a volume argument.

Away from the origin, the <electric field> is $E=A r/r^3$, with $r$ here denoting the position vector in the numerator and its <Euclidean norm> in the denominator. The spherical-coordinate formula for <divergence> gives
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot E=\frac1{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac A{r^2}\right)=0,\qquad r>0.}
$$
At the origin the <electric field> is singular, so the ordinary <divergence> is undefined there. The equation above must not be extended to the whole space as an ordinary smooth-field identity. Its correct <distributional divergence>, derived in <8/b/ii/solution>, includes the point source.

On an outward-oriented sphere of radius $R>0$, the <electric field> is normal with constant magnitude $A/R^2$. Thus its <flux> is
$$
\boxed{\iint_{S_R}E\cdot d\boldsymbol S=\frac A{R^2}(4\pi R^2)=4\pi A=\frac Q{\epsilon_0}.}
$$
It is independent of $R$. There is no contradiction with <8/b/i/solution>: the classical <divergence theorem> cannot be applied to a full ball while pretending that $E$ is smooth at its center. On a punctured ball the outer flux and the inward-normal inner flux cancel, in agreement with zero ordinary <divergence> there.

The three-dimensional <Dirac delta distribution> $\delta^{(3)}(r)$ is supported at the origin and satisfies the sampling identity
$$
\int_{\mathbb R^3}\delta^{(3)}(r)\varphi(r)\,dV=\varphi(0)
$$
for smooth compactly supported test functions $\varphi$. It is not an ordinary function with a finite value at zero; its total integral is one, and translating it moves the point at which the test function is sampled.

The globally valid source equation is
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot E=4\pi A\delta^{(3)}(r)=\frac Q{\epsilon_0}\delta^{(3)}(r).}
$$
For a direct weak verification, excise a ball of radius $\varepsilon$ and integrate $-E\cdot\nabla\varphi$ outside it. Since the ordinary <divergence> vanishes there, the result is $A\int_{S^2}\varphi(\varepsilon\hat r)\,d\Omega\to4\pi A\varphi(0)$. This is precisely the displayed <distributional divergence> and restores the <divergence theorem> with the <point charge> included.

\Image[2019-resit-spherical-quadrant.png]
{title=The spherical patch of radius four with y and z nonpositive, shown with the two planar caps used to close the volume.}
{description=The colored curved part is the requested surface. The planar half-discs are auxiliary surfaces for the divergence theorem; their outward normals point in positive y and positive z.}

The <divergence> of the polynomial <vector field> is $\nabla\cdot F=4xy$. Its <volume integral> over the enclosed quarter-ball vanishes by oddness in $x$. Thus the <divergence theorem> gives $\Phi_S+\Phi_{D_y}+\Phi_{D_z}=0$.

On $D_y$, the outward normal is $+\hat y$ and $F_y=-3z^4$. On $D_z$, it is $+\hat z$ and $F_z=x^3+y^2$; the odd term integrates to zero. The needed half-disc moments for radius $R$ are
$$
\int_{D_y}z^4\,dx\,dz
=\left(\int_0^Rr^5\,dr\right)\left(\int_\pi^{2\pi}\sin^4\phi\,d\phi\right)

\int_{D_z}y^2\,dx\,dy
=\left(\int_0^Rr^3\,dr\right)\left(\int_\pi^{2\pi}\sin^2\phi\,d\phi\right)
=\frac{\pi R^4}{8}.

$$
\boxed{\Phi_S=768\pi-32\pi=736\pi.}

A <stationary point> is a point where the function is differentiable and its first <derivative> vanishes. Interpret the cube root as the real <cube root>, and put $q(x)=x^2-8x+7$. Then $f(x)=15+(x-3)\sqrt[3]{q(x)}$. For $x\ne1,7$, the <product rule> and <chain rule> give
$$
f'(x)=\frac{3q(x)+(x-3)q'(x)}{3\big(\sqrt[3]{q(x)}\big)^2}
=\frac{5x^2-38x+45}{3\big(\sqrt[3]{q(x)}\big)^2}.
$$
The denominator is positive wherever it is defined. Consequently the two stationary abscissae are
$$
\boxed{x_\pm=\frac{19\pm2\sqrt{34}}5.}
$$
Using the stationary equation to simplify the value of $q$ gives $q(x_\pm)=-2(x_\pm+5)/5$. Thus the complete stationary points are
$$
\boxed{\left(x_\pm,\ 15-(x_\pm-3)\sqrt[3]{\frac{2(x_\pm+5)}5}\right).}
$$
Numerically they are approximately $(1.46762,17.10362)$ and $(6.13238,9.84664)$. The numerator of $f'$ is positive outside $[x_-,x_+]$ and negative inside, so the first is a local maximum and the second a local minimum.

At $x=1,7$, the real function is continuous but has a vertical tangent; the factor $x-3$ does not cancel either cube-root singularity. Those points are not stationary points. This is the distinction required when finding <stationary points with real cube-root factors>.

For $|x|<1$, the <geometric series> gives $(1+x)^{-1}=\sum_{j=0}^\infty(-x)^j$. Integrating from zero to $x$ yields the <Maclaurin series>
$$
\boxed{\ln(1+x)=\sum_{j=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{j+1}x^j}{j}
=x-\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac{x^3}{3}-\cdots.}
$$
On the real axis the series also converges at $x=1$ to $\ln2$, but diverges at $x=-1$.

For fixed real $a$ and sufficiently large positive $x$, the base in the requested limit is positive. Apply the series with argument $-a^2/x^2$:
$$
x^2\ln\left(1-\frac{a^2}{x^2}\right)
=-a^2-\frac{a^4}{2x^2}+O(x^{-4})\longrightarrow-a^2.
$$
Continuity of the <exponential function> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\lim_{x\to\infty}\left(1-\frac{a^2}{x^2}\right)^{x^2}=e^{-a^2}.}
$$
This includes $a=0$, when the expression is identically one.

For general coordinates $q=(a,b,c)$ expressed as functions of $u=(u,v,w)$, the <chain rule> gives $dq=J\,du$, where
$$
J=\frac{\partial(a,b,c)}{\partial(u,v,w)}
=\begin{pmatrix}a_u&a_v&a_w\\b_u&b_v&b_w\\c_u&c_v&c_w\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The <Jacobian determinant> is the oriented volume scaling of this linearized map. Thus the unsigned <volume element> transforms as $da\,db\,dc=|\det J|\,du\,dv\,dw$. Where the coordinate change is invertible, the inverse <Jacobian determinant> is $1/\det J$, by the <Jacobian chain rule>.

For <cylindrical coordinates>, use $x=r\cos\phi$, $y=r\sin\phi$, and unchanged height $z$. Direct differentiation gives
$$
\frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(r,\phi,z)}
=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\phi&-r\sin\phi&0\\\sin\phi&r\cos\phi&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},
$$
whose <determinant> is $r$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{dV=r\,dr\,d\phi\,dz,\qquad
\det\frac{\partial(r,\phi,z)}{\partial(x,y,z)}=\frac1r\quad(r>0).}
$$
Both directions are displayed because “from Cartesian to cylindrical” can refer either to the derivative of the new coordinates or to the factor used in transforming a Cartesian volume integral. They are reciprocals; the cylindrical axis is a coordinate degeneracy.

Take <spherical polar coordinates> with polar <angle> $\theta$ measured from positive $z$ and <azimuthal angle> $\phi$:

x=r\sin\theta\cos\phi,\qquad y=r\sin\theta\sin\phi,\qquad z=r\cos\theta.

Assume a positive common density scale $\rho_0$ and use the unit-radius coordinates of the source. The cylinder's density depends on distance from its axis. Its <mass> is
$$
M_{\mathrm{cyl}}=\int_0^4\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1\rho_0r^{-1}\,r\,dr\,d\phi\,dz
=8\pi\rho_0.
$$
The radial factor in the <cylindrical volume element> cancels the apparent density singularity.

For the sphere, the <spherical volume element> gives
$$
M_{\mathrm{sph}}=4\pi\rho_0\int_0^1r^{n+2}\,dr
=\frac{4\pi\rho_0}{n+3},\qquad n>-3.
$$
The condition is necessary for finite <mass>; the integral diverges for $n\leq-3$. Equating the two finite masses yields $1/(n+3)=2$, and hence
$$
\boxed{n=-\frac52.}
$$
This satisfies $n>-3$. A divergent pointwise density can therefore have finite total <mass>: the <integrable power-law density at an axis or center> must be tested together with its volume factor. Dimensionally, the density laws can be understood as $\rho_0(r/R)^{-1}$ and $\rho_0(r/R)^n$ with unit radius $R=1$ in the chosen length units.

A scalar <line integral> is $\int_Cf\,ds=\int f(r(t))\|r'(t)\|\,dt$: it weights arc length by a <scalar field> and is unchanged by reversing orientation. A vector-work <line integral> is $\int_CF\cdot d r=\int F(r(t))\cdot r'(t)\,dt$: it measures the tangent component of a <vector field> and changes sign under reversal. Both are invariant under a regular orientation-preserving reparametrization.

For the prescribed counterclockwise curve, traverse the upper semicircle from $(1,0)$ to $(-1,0)$, then the lower parabola from $(-1,0)$ back to $(1,0)$.

\Image[2019-resit-semicircle-parabola-contour.png]
{title=Counterclockwise contour formed by the upper unit semicircle and the lower parabolic arc.}
{description=The upper arc runs from right to left; the lower arc runs from left to right. The enclosed area is the sum of a semicircle and a parabolic segment.}

On the upper arc, use $x=\cos t$, $y=\sin t$, $0\leq t\leq\pi$. Then
$$
F\cdot d r=\big[-3\sin^2t+\cos^3t-\sin t\cos t\big]dt,
$$
so its integral is $-3\pi/2$: the last two terms integrate to zero by symmetry or direct integration. On the parabolic arc, use $x=t$, $y=t^2-1$ for $-1\leq t\leq1$. Here $x^2-y=1$ and
$$
F\cdot d r=(3t^2-3+2t)dt,
\qquad\int_{-1}^1(3t^2-3+2t)\,dt=-4.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\oint_CF\cdot d r=-\frac{3\pi}{2}-4.}
$$
As an independent check, <Green's theorem> gives the integrand $\partial_x(x^2-y)-\partial_y(3y)=2x-3$. The region is symmetric in $x$, so its odd part vanishes. Its area is $\pi/2+4/3$, giving $-3(\pi/2+4/3)$, exactly the same value.

For opposite propagation directions on a uniform string, write the equal-frequency <transverse waves> as
$$
y_1=A\cos(kx-\omega t+\alpha),\qquad
y_2=A\cos(kx+\omega t+\beta).
$$
The cosine addition identity gives
$$
\boxed{y_1+y_2=2A\cos\left(kx+\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\right)
\cos\left(\omega t+\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2}\right).}
$$
This is a <standing wave>: the spatial pattern is fixed while its amplitude oscillates in time. Nodes occur at the zeros of the first cosine and are separated by $\pi/k$, half a <wavelength>; antinodes have displacement amplitude $2A$. The two equal opposite travelling waves carry equal opposite average <energy flux>, so the net average transported <power> is zero.

For the same propagation direction, the same identity instead gives
$$
\boxed{A\cos(kx-\omega t+\alpha)+A\cos(kx-\omega t+\beta)
=2A\cos\frac{\alpha-\beta}{2}\,
\cos\left(kx-\omega t+\frac{\alpha+\beta}{2}\right).}
$$
For a nonzero resultant this is another travelling wave, with fixed <amplitude> and moving constant-phase points, not a nontrivial <standing wave>. If the prefactor vanishes, the waves cancel identically; the zero disturbance is a trivial exception rather than a standing oscillation. The argument assumes propagating waves with $k,\omega>0$ on the same uniform string.

= Solution

The <wave equation on a string> gives $c_j=\sqrt{T/\rho_j}$ and $\omega=c_jk_j$, hence
$$
\boxed{k_j=\omega\sqrt{\frac{\rho_j}{T}},\qquad T k_j^2=\rho_j\omega^2.}
$$
The tension is common across the join, and the frequency is unchanged by reflection and transmission at the stationary boundary.

Use the complex time convention $e^{i\omega t}$. The physical displacement is the real part of
$$
y_L=(Ae^{-ik_1x}+Be^{ik_1x})e^{i\omega t},\qquad
y_R=Ce^{-ik_2x}e^{i\omega t}.
$$
The first term is incident from the left, the second reflected to the left, and the right-side term transmitted to the right. Continuity of <displacement> gives $A+B=C$. At a massless join, continuity of the transverse <force> $T y_x$ gives $k_1(A-B)=k_2C$. Solving these two equations yields
$$
\boxed{r=\frac BA=\frac{k_1-k_2}{k_1+k_2},\qquad
 t=\frac CA=\frac{2k_1}{k_1+k_2}=1+r.}
$$
These are displacement-amplitude coefficients for <reflection at a density step on a string>, not power fractions. Since the mean incident <power> is $T\omega k_1|A|^2/2$, the reflected and transmitted power fractions are $|r|^2$ and $(k_2/k_1)|t|^2$. They sum to one, as expected for the lossless join.

Keep the $e^{i\omega t}$ convention of <11/b/solution>. The point mass has the common displacement $y(0,t)=Ce^{i\omega t}$, so $A+B=C$ still holds. Its net transverse <force> is the right-side string force minus the left-side one:
$$
-M\omega^2C=T\big[-ik_2C-(-ik_1A+ik_1B)\big].
$$
After rearranging,
$$
k_1T(A-B)=(k_2T+iM\omega^2)C.
$$
Eliminate $C=A+B$ to obtain the <reflection at a string junction carrying a point mass>:
$$
\boxed{r=\frac{(k_1-k_2)T-iM\omega^2}{(k_1+k_2)T+iM\omega^2},\qquad
 t=\frac{2k_1T}{(k_1+k_2)T+iM\omega^2}.}
$$
Continuity checks the identity $t=1+r$. Setting $M=0$ recovers <11/b/solution>, while $M\to\infty$ at fixed nonzero frequency gives $r\to-1$ and $t\to0$, the fixed-boundary limit.

The signs of the imaginary terms depend on the time convention. With $e^{-i\omega t}$ and conjugate spatial conventions, both coefficients are complex conjugated; the source's displayed signs agree with the convention used here.

There is no damping in the point mass, so its cycle-averaged absorbed <power> is zero. Writing $D=T^2(k_1+k_2)^2+M^2\omega^4$, the power fractions are
$$
\mathcal R=\frac{T^2(k_1-k_2)^2+M^2\omega^4}{D},\qquad
\mathcal T=\frac{4T^2k_1k_2}{D},\qquad\mathcal R+\mathcal T=1.
$$
This provides an independent conservation check on both the numerator signs and the transmission prefactor.
