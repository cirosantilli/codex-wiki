== 1
{scope}

=== Solution

For a continuously differentiable <vector field> on a volume $V$ with piecewise smooth boundary $S$, the <divergence theorem> states
$$
\oiint_S\mathbf A\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,dS=\iiint_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf A\,dV,
$$
where $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is the **outward** unit normal. For the polynomial field here,
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=\partial_x(4xz)+\partial_y(-y^2)+\partial_z(yz)=4z-y.
$$
Therefore the outward <flux> through the unit cube is
$$
I=\int_0^1\int_0^1\int_0^1(4z-y)\,dx\,dy\,dz=2-\frac12=\boxed{\frac32}.
$$
As a direct boundary check, the $x=1$ face contributes $2$, the $y=1$ face contributes $-1$, and the $z=1$ face contributes $1/2$. All three opposite faces contribute zero, reproducing the result and fixing the orientation sign.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Maclaurin series> $\sin x=x-x^3/6+O(x^5)$ cancels the numerator's linear term, leaving
$$
\frac{\sin x-x}{x^3}=-\frac16+O(x^2).
$$
Thus $\boxed{\lim_{x\to0}(\sin x-x)/x^3=-1/6}$. The same expansion applies for positive and negative $x$, so this is a two-sided <limit>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Combine the terms before taking their <limit>:
$$
\frac1x-\frac1{e^x-1}=\frac{e^x-1-x}{x(e^x-1)}.
$$
Using the <exponential function> <Maclaurin series>, the numerator is $x^2/2+O(x^3)$ and the denominator is $x^2+O(x^3)$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\lim_{x\to0}\left(\frac1x-\frac1{e^x-1}\right)=\frac12}.
$$
The cancellation removes the two individual singularities. Subtracting their separate divergent limits would not determine the finite answer.

== 3
{scope}

=== Solution

Write $\tan x=a_1x+a_3x^3+a_5x^5+O(x^7)$. Multiplying by the <cosine> <Maclaurin series> and comparing with the <sine> series gives
$$
(a_1x+a_3x^3+a_5x^5)\left(1-\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac{x^4}{24}\right)=x-\frac{x^3}{6}+\frac{x^5}{120}+O(x^7).
$$
Coefficient comparison yields $a_1=1$, $a_3-a_1/2=-1/6$ and $a_5-a_3/2+a_1/24=1/120$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\tan x=x+\frac{x^3}{3}+\frac{2x^5}{15}+O(x^7)}.
$$
Substitute $u=\tan x$ into $e^u=1+u+u^2/2+u^3/6+O(u^4)$. Keeping one extra order to make the term-counting explicit,
$$
e^{\tan x}=1+x+\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac{x^3}{2}+O(x^4).
$$
Counting the constant term, the first three nonzero terms are $\boxed{1+x+x^2/2}$. If the phrase is taken to mean the first three nonconstant terms, they are $x+x^2/2+x^3/2$; the displayed expansion supplies both conventions. The cubic coefficient is $1/3+1/6=1/2$, combining the cubic term in $\tan x$ with the cubic exponential term.

== 4
{scope}

=== Solution

For $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}>0$, the <radial vector field> has components $F_i=x_if(r)$. The <product rule> gives
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=\sum_i\left[f(r)+x_if'(r)\frac{x_i}{r}\right]=3f(r)+rf'(r).
$$
Thus for $f(r)=r^{n-1}$,
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=(n+2)r^{n-1},\qquad r>0}.
$$
When $n=-2$, $\mathbf F=\hat{\mathbf r}/r^2$ and the ordinary <divergence> is zero at every nonzero radius. However, the field is singular at the origin. Its outward <flux> through a radius-$R$ sphere is $(1/R^2)(4\pi R^2)=4\pi$, so one cannot apply the smooth-field <divergence theorem> across the origin and conclude that this flux vanishes.

In the language of <distributions>, the complete <distributional divergence> identity is $\boxed{\nabla\cdot(\hat{\mathbf r}/r^2)=4\pi\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf r)}$. Here $\delta^{(3)}$ is the three-dimensional <Dirac delta function>. Without that generalized interpretation, the correct statement is simply zero divergence away from the origin and undefined ordinary divergence at the singular point. Removing a small ball around the origin restores the ordinary theorem: the inner boundary has inward radial orientation and cancels the outer flux.

== 5
{scope}

=== Solution

For <phase> $kx-\omega t$, a constant-phase point moves at the <phase velocity> $\boxed{v_P=\omega/k}$. A narrow-band wave packet has envelope speed given by the local <group velocity> $\boxed{v_G=d\omega/dk}$ on its <dispersion relation>.

Since $\omega=kv_P(k)$,
$$
v_G=v_P+k\frac{dv_P}{dk}.
$$
Use the in-medium <wavelength> $\lambda=2\pi/k$, so $d\lambda/dk=-\lambda/k$. The velocity relation becomes
$$
\boxed{v_G=v_P-\lambda\frac{dv_P}{d\lambda}}.
$$
The stated increase of <phase velocity> with <wavelength> means $dv_P/d\lambda>0$. Therefore $\boxed{v_G<v_P}$. The inequality alone does not provide their numerical values or guarantee a positive group speed for an arbitrary dispersion law; it fixes their ordering. For ordinary transparent glass in its normal-dispersion band, both are positive and the group speed is the smaller one.

== 6
{scope}

=== Solution

The boundaries intersect where $x^2=2x+8$, giving $x=-2$ and $x=4$. Between these values, the straight line lies above the <parabola>. Therefore
$$
I=\int_{-2}^{4}\int_{x^2}^{2x+8}x\,dy\,dx=\int_{-2}^{4}(-x^3+2x^2+8x)\,dx.
$$
Evaluation gives
$$
\boxed{I=\left[-\frac{x^4}{4}+\frac{2x^3}{3}+4x^2\right]_{-2}^{4}=36}.
$$
A useful independent check uses the region's symmetry about $x=1$: its vertical width is $9-(x-1)^2$. Its area is $\int_{-3}^{3}(9-u^2)\,du=36$, and its <centroid> has $x$ coordinate one, so its first moment $\iint x\,dA$ is again $36$.

== 7
{scope}

=== Solution

The integration limits describe the first-quadrant quarter of the unit disk: $x\ge0$, $y\ge0$, $x^2+y^2\le1$. Its circular boundary and the axes are shown in the right-hand panel below; the left panel gives the bounded region used in the preceding calculation.

\Image[2015-planar-integration-regions.png]
{title=The parabolic strip and quarter-disk integration regions}

Use <polar coordinates> $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$ with $0\le r\le1$ and $0\le\theta\le\pi/2$. The <Jacobian determinant> gives $dx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\theta$, so
$$
I=\int_0^{\pi/2}\int_0^1 e^{-r^2}r\,dr\,d\theta=\frac\pi2\left[-\frac12e^{-r^2}\right]_0^1.
$$
Thus $\boxed{I=\tfrac\pi4(1-e^{-1})}$. Including the radial Jacobian is essential; the exponential becomes radial, but the area element does not remain $dr\,d\theta$.

== 8
{scope}

=== Solution

Take the constant speed parameter $v\ne0$, so $r=x+vt$ and $s=x-vt$ are independent <characteristic coordinates>. The <chain rule> gives
$$
\partial_x=\partial_r+\partial_s,\qquad\partial_t=v(\partial_r-\partial_s).
$$
Applying these operators twice to a twice differentiable <function> yields
$$
F_{xx}=F_{rr}+2F_{rs}+F_{ss},\qquad F_{tt}=v^2(F_{rr}-2F_{rs}+F_{ss}).
$$
Consequently the <wave equation> reduces to $4F_{rs}=0$. Integrate first with respect to $s$, obtaining $F_r=A(r)$, and then with respect to $r$, allowing a separate integration <function> of $s$. The general local solution is
$$
\boxed{F(x,t)=G(x+vt)+H(x-vt)},
$$
with arbitrary sufficiently differentiable <functions> $G$ and $H$. For $v>0$, the first profile travels towards negative $x$ and the second towards positive $x$. Substitution directly checks the equation. Initial or boundary conditions select the two profiles; none are specified here. This is <D'Alembert's formula> in characteristic form.

== 9
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The first <partial derivatives> are
$$
z_x=e^{-x^2-y^2}(-\sin x-2x\cos x),\qquad z_y=-2y\cos x\,e^{-x^2-y^2}.
$$
If $\cos x=0$, then $z_x=-\sin x\,e^{-x^2-y^2}\ne0$, so no <stationary point> is lost by excluding those values. The remaining condition $z_y=0$ forces $y=0$, and $z_x=0$ then gives
$$
\boxed{\tan x_s=-2x_s,\qquad k(x)=-2x}.
$$
There are infinitely many such points. On every tangent branch $(n\pi-\pi/2,n\pi+\pi/2)$, the <function> $\tan x+2x$ has <derivative> $\sec^2x+2>0$ and goes from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$. The <intermediate value theorem> and strict monotonicity therefore give exactly one root on every branch. The central root is $x_s=0$, and oddness pairs the other roots at opposite signs.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For increments $h=x-x_s$ and $k=y-y_s$, the second-order multivariable <Taylor expansion> is
$$
z=z_s+(z_x)_s h+(z_y)_s k+\frac12\left[(z_{xx})_s h^2+2(z_{xy})_s hk+(z_{yy})_s k^2\right]+o(h^2+k^2).
$$
At a <stationary point> $(x_s,0)$, $\sin x_s=-2x_s\cos x_s$ and $z_s=\cos x_s e^{-x_s^2}$. Direct differentiation gives
$$
(z_{xx})_s=-(4x_s^2+3)z_s,\qquad(z_{xy})_s=0,\qquad(z_{yy})_s=-2z_s.
$$
Thus, since this particular <function> is analytic, the expansion can be written
$$
\boxed{z(x,y)=z_s\left[1-\frac{4x_s^2+3}{2}(x-x_s)^2-y^2\right]+O\!\left(((x-x_s)^2+y^2)^{3/2}\right)}.
$$
There are no linear terms because the <gradient> vanishes, and no mixed quadratic term because the mixed <derivative> vanishes on the axis.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

At a <stationary point> of a twice continuously differentiable <function>, write the <Hessian matrix> as $H=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\B&C\end{pmatrix}$ and its <determinant> as $D=AC-B^2$. The quadratic term in the <Taylor expansion> determines the local nature whenever $H$ is nonsingular.

If $D>0$, its two <eigenvalues> have the same sign. With $A>0$ the <quadratic form> is positive definite, giving a strict local minimum; with $A<0$ it is negative definite, giving a strict local maximum. If $D<0$, the <eigenvalues> have opposite signs and the point is a saddle, with nearby increasing and decreasing directions. Thus
$$
\boxed{D>0,\ A>0:\ \min;\qquad D>0,\ A<0:\ \max;\qquad D<0:\ \text{saddle}}.
$$
If $D=0$, the test is inconclusive. For example, $x^4+y^4$, $-x^4-y^4$ and $x^4-y^4$ all have zero <gradient> and zero <Hessian matrix> at the origin, yet give a minimum, a maximum and a saddle respectively. One must inspect higher-order terms or use another argument in such a case.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For every <stationary point> found above, $\cos x_s\ne0$ and hence $z_s\ne0$. The <Hessian matrix> is diagonal, with entries $-(4x_s^2+3)z_s$ and $-2z_s$, so
$$
D=2(4x_s^2+3)z_s^2>0.
$$
The entries always have the same sign. Therefore
$$
\boxed{z_s>0:\ \text{strict local maximum};\qquad z_s<0:\ \text{strict local minimum}}.
$$
The root on tangent branch $(n\pi-\pi/2,n\pi+\pi/2)$ has cosine sign $(-1)^n$. Thus even-numbered branches supply maxima and odd-numbered branches minima, symmetrically paired about the origin. There are no saddle points among these nondegenerate <stationary points>. The origin is a maximum with $z_s=1$, and is in fact the global maximum since $\cos x\le1$ and $e^{-x^2-y^2}\le1$.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

For $w=x^3e^{-x^2-y^2}$, the first <derivatives> are $w_x=x^2(3-2x^2)e^{-x^2-y^2}$ and $w_y=-2yx^3e^{-x^2-y^2}$. Both vanish at the origin. The local expansion is
$$
w(x,y)=x^3-x^5-x^3y^2+\cdots=x^3+O\!\left((x^2+y^2)^{5/2}\right).
$$
There are no constant, linear or quadratic terms, so every second <derivative> vanishes at the origin and the <Hessian determinant> is zero. The second-order test therefore cannot classify the point.

The cubic term does determine the behaviour: along $y=0$, $w(x,0)$ has the sign of $x$. Every neighbourhood contains values above and below $w(0,0)=0$, so it is neither a maximum nor a minimum. It is a <nonisolated stationary point> with nearby values of both signs. Indeed the whole line $x=0$ is stationary, so this is not an isolated nondegenerate quadratic saddle.

=== a

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $x_1,x_2$ be <displacements> from equilibrium, with positive directions chosen alike. <Hooke's law> and <Newton's second law> give
$$
m_1\ddot x_1=-(k_1+k_2)x_1+k_2x_2,\qquad m_2\ddot x_2=k_2x_1-(k_2+k_3)x_2.
$$
For a <normal mode> $x_j=a_j\cos\Omega t$, the <determinant> condition is
$$
\bigl(k_1+k_2-m_1\Omega^2\bigr)\bigl(k_2+k_3-m_2\Omega^2\bigr)-k_2^2=0.
$$
Solving the quadratic for $\Omega^2$ gives
$$
\boxed{\Omega_\pm^2=\frac12\left[\frac{k_1+k_2}{m_1}+\frac{k_2+k_3}{m_2}\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{k_1+k_2}{m_1}-\frac{k_2+k_3}{m_2}\right)^2+\frac{4k_2^2}{m_1m_2}}\right]}.
$$
The <angular frequencies> are the positive square roots. Positive masses and <spring constants> make the stiffness <quadratic form> $k_1x_1^2+k_2(x_2-x_1)^2+k_3x_2^2$ positive definite, ensuring both squared <frequencies> are positive. Distinct <spring constants> do not require equal masses or any symmetry approximation.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Put $k_1=k_3=k$ and $m_1=m_2=m$. The mass-normalized stiffness <matrix> is
$$
\frac1m\begin{pmatrix}k+k_2&-k_2\\-k_2&k+k_2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its symmetric <eigenvector> $(1,1)$ has <eigenvalue> $k/m$, while its antisymmetric <eigenvector> $(1,-1)$ has <eigenvalue> $(k+2k_2)/m$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\Omega_{\rm sym}=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}},\qquad\Omega_{\rm anti}=\sqrt{\frac{k+2k_2}{m}}}.
$$
The symmetric <frequency> does not depend on the middle <spring constant> because that <spring> has no extension in an in-phase <normal mode>. The antisymmetric motion stretches the middle <spring> and has the higher <frequency> for $k_2>0$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

With the same positive <displacement> convention for both masses, the first <eigenvector> equation is $(k+k_2-m\Omega^2)a_1-k_2a_2=0$. At the symmetric <frequency> it reduces to $k_2(a_1-a_2)=0$, while at the antisymmetric <frequency> it becomes $-k_2(a_1+a_2)=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\frac{a_1}{a_2}=+1\quad\text{for the symmetric mode},\qquad\frac{a_1}{a_2}=-1\quad\text{for the antisymmetric mode}}.
$$
In both <normal modes> the <displacement> magnitudes are equal. For the first, the masses move in <phase> and their separation stays unchanged; for the second they move in opposite <phase>, alternately approaching and separating. A negative signed <amplitude> ratio records a <phase> difference of $\pi$, not a negative <displacement> magnitude. The left panel of the later figure plots these signed <normal mode> shapes with the fixed wall values included.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Both masses in the source's absorber diagram are free: there is no additional <spring> attaching $M$ to a wall. Let their <displacements> be $X$ and $x$. The equations are
$$
M\ddot X=F_0\cos\omega t+k(x-X),\qquad m\ddot x=-k(x-X).
$$
For a periodic particular response $X=A\cos\omega t$, $x=B\cos\omega t$, the <amplitude> equations are $(k-M\omega^2)A-kB=F_0$ and $-kA+(k-m\omega^2)B=0$. Their solution is
$$
\boxed{A=\frac{F_0(k-m\omega^2)}{\omega^2[Mm\omega^2-k(M+m)]},\qquad B=\frac{F_0k}{\omega^2[Mm\omega^2-k(M+m)]}}.
$$
At $\boxed{k=m\omega^2}$, $A=0$ and $B=-F_0/k$: the <spring> <force> on the larger mass cancels the applied <force> at every instant. This is <antiresonance>, a frequency-selective cancellation, rather than dissipation of <energy>.

There is a nearby undamped resonance at $k_{\rm res}=Mm\omega^2/(M+m)$. For $m\ll M$ it lies close to the cancellation setting. More quantitatively, write $k=m\omega^2(1+\epsilon)$. Then
$$
A=-\frac{F_0\epsilon}{\omega^2[m+\epsilon(M+m)]}.
$$
For $|\epsilon|\ll m/(M+m)$, the <amplitude> relative to the unconnected mass's <amplitude> $F_0/(M\omega^2)$ is approximately $(M/m)|\epsilon|$. A light absorber therefore needs very accurate tuning and loses suppression when the driving <frequency> changes. Its own travel $|B|=F_0/(m\omega^2)$ can also become large, limiting linearity and available stroke.

\Image[2015-coupled-spring-modes-and-absorber.png]
{title=Symmetric spring modes and narrow tuning of a free-pair dynamic absorber}

Without damping, resonance growth and persistent free oscillations remain; special initial conditions select the displayed periodic particular response. The free pair also has rigid-translation motion. These limitations prevent interpreting the cancellation as broadband damping or as a unique attracting late-time state.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For an equilibrium string segment of length $dx$, the mass is $\rho\,dx$. Under the small-slope approximation, the transverse <tension> component at an endpoint is $T y_x$. The difference between its two endpoint contributions is $T[y_x(x+dx,t)-y_x(x,t)]\simeq T y_{xx}dx$.

<Newton's second law> in the transverse direction gives $\rho y_{tt}dx=T y_{xx}dx$. Cancelling the segment length produces
$$
\boxed{y_{tt}-c^2y_{xx}=0,\qquad c=\sqrt{T/\rho}}.
$$
This <wave equation> assumes a uniform string, small slopes, negligible gravity and negligible bending stiffness. Longitudinal changes in <tension> and the difference between actual and equilibrium segment length contribute only beyond the linear approximation used here.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The instantaneous <energy> flux towards positive $x$ is the rate at which the string on the left does work on the string on the right. To first order its transverse <force> is $-T y_x$, so
$$
P(x,t)=-T y_x y_t.
$$
For a right-travelling sinusoidal wave $y=A\cos(kx-\omega t)$, $y_x=-Ak\sin(kx-\omega t)$ and $y_t=A\omega\sin(kx-\omega t)$. Thus
$$
P(x,t)=T\omega kA^2\sin^2(kx-\omega t),\qquad\boxed{\langle P\rangle=\frac12T\omega kA^2}.
$$
The printed constant formula is therefore the **time-average <power>**, not the instantaneous <power>. The latter oscillates between zero and twice its average. A left-travelling wave carries negative signed flux through the same positively oriented section; its transported <power> magnitude has the same average formula with positive $k$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Place the massless junction at $x=0$, with common <tension> $T$ on both sides. Using a common time factor $e^{-i\omega t}$, write the left-hand wave as $A_i e^{ik_1x}+A_r e^{-ik_1x}$ and the right-hand wave as $A_t e^{ik_2x}$. A stationary junction does not change the <frequency>, while $k_j=\omega\sqrt{\rho_j/T}$.

<displacement> continuity requires $A_i+A_r=A_t$. Transverse <force> balance on a massless junction requires equal tension-weighted slopes, giving $k_1(A_i-A_r)=k_2A_t$. Hence the signed <displacement> coefficients are
$$
\boxed{r=\frac{A_r}{A_i}=\frac{k_1-k_2}{k_1+k_2},\qquad t=\frac{A_t}{A_i}=\frac{2k_1}{k_1+k_2}}.
$$
Here $\rho_2=4\rho_1$, so $k_2=2k_1$ and $\boxed{A_r=-A_i/3,\quad A_t=2A_i/3}$. The negative reflected coefficient signifies inversion. If “reflection and transmission coefficients” instead denotes <power> fractions, their values are $\mathcal R=1/9$ and $\mathcal T=8/9$, as derived separately below; <power> and <displacement> coefficients must not be conflated.

=== d
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The transmitted and incident waves have the same <angular frequency> and <tension>, but the transmitted <wavenumber> is twice as large. The time-average string-power formula therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{P_t}{P_i}=\frac{k_2|A_t|^2}{k_1|A_i|^2}=2\left(\frac23\right)^2=\frac89}.
$$
The extra factor $k_2/k_1$ is essential. Squaring the <displacement> transmission coefficient alone would give $4/9$, which is not the transmitted <power> fraction in strings with different <linear mass densities>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The reflected wave remains on the incident string, so its <wavenumber> magnitude is still $k_1$. Its outward <power> magnitude is
$$
\boxed{\frac{|P_r|}{P_i}=\frac{|A_r|^2}{|A_i|^2}=\frac19}.
$$
Thus $|P_r|+P_t=(1/9+8/9)P_i=P_i$, verifying <energy conservation>. With signed positive-$x$ fluxes, $P_r=-P_i/9$ and the same statement is $P_i+P_r=P_t$. The minus sign of the reflected <displacement> is a <phase> reversal, whereas the minus sign of its flux records travel in the opposite direction; these are different uses of a sign.

=== e
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For equal <linear mass densities> and common <tension>, $k_1=k_2$. The junction relations then give
$$
\boxed{r=0,\qquad t=1,\qquad\mathcal R=0,\qquad\mathcal T=1}.
$$
There is no mechanical-impedance discontinuity, so no reflected wave is needed to satisfy <displacement> and <force> continuity. The transmitted <displacement> is in <phase> with the incident wave and has the same <amplitude>; all incident <power> crosses the junction.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

If $\rho_1\ll\rho_2$, then $k_1\ll k_2$ and the wave meets a much larger string impedance. The <displacement> and <power> coefficients approach
$$
\boxed{r\to-1,\qquad t\to0^+,\qquad\mathcal R\to1,\qquad\mathcal T\to0}.
$$
The junction behaves approximately as a fixed end. The reflected <displacement> reverses sign, corresponding to a <phase> shift of $\pi$; the small transmitted <displacement> is not inverted. For a large but finite density ratio, $t\simeq2k_1/k_2$ and the transmitted <power> fraction is about $4k_1/k_2$, tending to zero rather than vanishing identically at every finite ratio.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

If $\rho_1\gg\rho_2$, then $k_1\gg k_2$ and the junction behaves approximately as a free end for the incident string. The limits are
$$
\boxed{r\to+1,\qquad t\to2,\qquad\mathcal R\to1,\qquad\mathcal T\to0}.
$$
The reflected <displacement> is not inverted, and at the junction it adds in <phase> to the incident <displacement>, producing nearly twice its <amplitude>. The transmitted <displacement> is also in <phase>. A transmission <amplitude> near two does not create <power>: the lighter string has much smaller impedance, and $\mathcal T=(k_2/k_1)t^2\simeq4k_2/k_1\to0$. These are limiting statements for positive densities, not an ordinary propagating-wave model for an exactly massless second string.

== 12
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Compute all three components of the Cartesian <curl> explicitly:
$$
\begin{aligned}
(\nabla\times\mathbf F)_x&=\partial_y(4xyz)-\partial_z(2xz^2)=4xz-4xz=0,\\
(\nabla\times\mathbf F)_y&=\partial_z(2yz^2)-\partial_x(4xyz)=4yz-4yz=0,\\
(\nabla\times\mathbf F)_z&=\partial_x(2xz^2)-\partial_y(2yz^2)=2z^2-2z^2=0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus $\boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf F=\mathbf0}$ throughout $\mathbb R^3$. There are no excluded singular points in this polynomial field.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The field is continuously differentiable on all of $\mathbb R^3$, a <simply connected> domain. Its vanishing <curl> therefore implies a global <potential of a conservative vector field>, so $\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=d\phi$ is an <exact differential>. This global domain condition matters: curl-free fields on domains with holes need not have single-valued potentials.

Integrating $\phi_x=2yz^2$ gives $\phi=2xyz^2+g(y,z)$. Matching $\phi_y=2xz^2$ forces $g_y=0$, so $g=h(z)$; matching $\phi_z=4xyz$ then forces $h'=0$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\phi(x,y,z)=2xyz^2+C},\qquad d\phi=2yz^2dx+2xz^2dy+4xyz\,dz.
$$
The additive constant does not affect the field or any endpoint difference.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

On the specified parametrized <curve>, $\mathbf r(t)=(t^2,2t,t^3)$ and $\mathbf r'(t)=(2t,2,3t^2)$. Substitution into the field gives $\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))=(4t^7,2t^8,8t^6)$. Hence
$$
\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf r'=8t^8+4t^8+24t^8=36t^8.
$$
The <line integral> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\int_0^1 36t^8\,dt=4}.
$$
Both the field and the tangent vector must be evaluated along the <curve>; integrating one field component alone would miss two contributions.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Interpret the axis-directed route as successive coordinate-axis-parallel segments
$$
(0,0,0)\longrightarrow(1,0,0)\longrightarrow(1,2,0)\longrightarrow(1,2,1).
$$
On the first segment, $y=z=0$, so the field vanishes. On the second, $z=0$, so it again vanishes. On the last, $x=1$, $y=2$ and only $dz$ is nonzero, giving $\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=8z\,dz$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=0+0+\int_0^1 8z\,dz=4}.
$$
The second and third segments are parallel to the corresponding global axes; they do not return to the global $y$- or $z$-axis before reaching the endpoint.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Both paths start at $(0,0,0)$ and end at $(1,2,1)$. The potential values are $\phi(0,0,0)=C$ and $\phi(1,2,1)=4+C$, so
$$
\boxed{\Delta\phi=4=\int_{C_1}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\int_{C_2}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r}.
$$
This is the <fundamental theorem for line integrals>: an exact one-form integrates to the difference of its single-valued potential at the endpoints. The agreement is guaranteed for every piecewise smooth connecting path, rather than being an accident of these two examples. In particular the integral around a closed path assembled from one route and the reverse of the other is zero.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

<Stokes' theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\oint_C\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf r=\iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf A)\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,dS},
$$
with boundary orientation induced by the chosen <surface> normal and with sufficient smoothness of the field and <surface>. Choose the outward normal on the upper unit hemisphere; its equatorial boundary runs counterclockwise as viewed from $+z$.

For the stated field, the <curl> is
$$
\nabla\times\mathbf A=\bigl(-2yz-(-2yz),\ 0-0,\ 0-(-1)\bigr)=\hat{\mathbf z}.
$$
On the unit sphere, the outward normal is $\hat{\mathbf n}=(x,y,z)$. In <spherical coordinates>, the <surface> integral over the upper hemisphere is
$$
\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi=\pi.
$$
For the boundary calculation, parametrize $\mathbf r(\varphi)=(\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi,0)$, with $0\le\varphi\le2\pi$. Then $\mathbf A=(2\cos\varphi-\sin\varphi,0,0)$ and
$$
\oint_C\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf r=\int_0^{2\pi}\left[-2\cos\varphi\sin\varphi+\sin^2\varphi\right]d\varphi=\pi.
$$
Thus both sides equal $\boxed{\pi}$. Reversing the hemisphere orientation reverses both signs. The hemisphere's flux calculation can also be read as the projected area of its equatorial unit disk.
