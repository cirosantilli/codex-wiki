= Solution

The <Maclaurin series> of the <exponential function> and <sine> give
$$
e^{x^2}=1+x^2+\frac{x^4}{2}+\frac{x^6}{6}+O(x^8),\qquad
x\sin x=x^2-\frac{x^4}{6}+\frac{x^6}{120}+O(x^8).
$$
Both the numerator and denominator first become nonzero at order $x^4$. Dividing by that common power gives the <limit>
$$
\boxed{\lim_{x\to0}\frac{1+x^2-e^{x^2}}{x\sin x-x^2}
=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{-1/2+O(x^2)}{-1/6+O(x^2)}=3.}
$$

The <fundamental theorem of calculus> differentiates the variable upper endpoint:
$$
I'(x)=\frac2{\sqrt\pi}e^{-x^2},\qquad
I''(x)=-\frac{4x}{\sqrt\pi}e^{-x^2},\qquad
I'''(x)=\frac{-4+8x^2}{\sqrt\pi}e^{-x^2}.
$$
At zero, $I(0)=0$, $I'(0)=2/\sqrt\pi$, $I''(0)=0$ and $I'''(0)=-4/\sqrt\pi$. The integrand is even, so its <integral> from zero is an <odd function>; all even coefficients in the <Maclaurin series> vanish. Hence
$$
\boxed{I(x)=\frac2{\sqrt\pi}\left(x-\frac{x^3}{3}\right)+O(x^5).}
$$
This is the local expansion of the <error function>.

**First method: substitute the trajectory.** Along the <parametric curve>, the <temperature> is $T(t)=20e^{-\cos^2t}-(1+\sin t)^3$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{dT}{dt}
=40\sin t\cos t\,e^{-\cos^2t}-3(1+\sin t)^2\cos t.}
$$
**Second method: use the spatial derivatives.** The <partial derivatives> of the <scalar field> are $T_x=-40xe^{-x^2}$ and $T_z=-3(z+1)^2$, while the <velocity> components are $\dot x=-\sin t$ and $\dot z=\cos t$. The <multivariable chain rule> therefore gives
$$
\frac{dT}{dt}=T_x\dot x+T_z\dot z
=(-40\cos t\,e^{-\cos^2t})(-\sin t)-3(1+\sin t)^2\cos t,
$$
which agrees with the first calculation. This is the <gradient> dotted with the <velocity>, rather than a <partial derivative> at a fixed position.

Apply the <product-to-sum formula> $2\cos u\cos v=\cos(u+v)+\cos(u-v)$ to the <amplitude modulation>:
$$
\boxed{A=a\cos(\omega t-kx)
+\frac b2\cos\bigl((\omega+\omega_m)t-kx\bigr)
+\frac b2\cos\bigl((\omega-\omega_m)t-kx\bigr).}
$$
The <amplitude modulation sidebands> have <angular frequencies> $\omega+\omega_m$ and $\omega-\omega_m$, each with <amplitude> $b/2$ in this real <cosine> convention. The <carrier wave> has <angular frequency> $\omega$ and <amplitude> $a$.

This identity is exact for the supplied space-time expression. A physical qualification is needed if every term is meant to be a freely propagating <electromagnetic wave> with <speed of light> $c$: the <dispersion relation> requires $k_\pm=(\omega\pm\omega_m)/c$, whereas the prescribed modulation at every position leaves all three <wavenumbers> equal to $k=\omega/c$. A freely propagating modulated <wave> instead has envelope $a+b\cos(\omega_m(t-x/c))$; its analogous expansion has $k_\pm$. For example, at $x=t=0$ the prescribed expression has $A_{tt}-c^2A_{xx}=-b\omega_m^2$, which is nonzero for nontrivial modulation. The given formula can describe modulation imposed throughout space or the temporal signal at one position.

\dot x=at\cos t,\qquad \dot y=at\sin t.

The <arc length> element is $ds=\sqrt{\dot x^2+\dot y^2}\,dt=|a|t\,dt$ on $0\le t\le2$. Thus

For the <scalar surface integral over an obliquely cut cylinder>, split the <scalar surface integral> into the curved <cylinder> and its two planar caps. Write $s=z-x$, so the solid has $x^2+y^2\le4$ and $-3\le s\le2$. On the curved <cylinder>, use the <parametrization>
$$
\mathbf R(\theta,s)=(2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta,2\cos\theta+s),
\qquad 0\le\theta\le2\pi,\quad -3\le s\le2.
$$
The <surface area element> follows from
$$
\mathbf R_\theta\times\mathbf R_s=(2\cos\theta,2\sin\theta,0),\qquad
dS=2\,d\theta\,ds,\qquad x-z=-s.
$$
Consequently the curved contribution is
$$
I_{\rm side}=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_{-3}^{2}(-s)\,2\,ds\,d\theta=10\pi.
$$
On either cap $z=x+s$, the <surface area element> is $\sqrt{1+z_x^2+z_y^2}\,dx\,dy=\sqrt2\,dx\,dy$ over a radius-two <disk>. The lower cap has $x-z=3$ and the upper cap has $x-z=-2$. Thus
$$
I_{\rm lower}=3\sqrt2(4\pi)=12\sqrt2\pi,\qquad
I_{\rm upper}=-2\sqrt2(4\pi)=-8\sqrt2\pi,
$$
and the complete <scalar surface integral> is
$$
\boxed{\iint_S(x-z)\,dS=\pi(10+4\sqrt2).}
$$
The <surface area element> is unsigned: opposite outward normals on the caps do not introduce extra minus signs.

For a continuously differentiable <vector field> $\mathbf F$ on a neighborhood of an oriented, piecewise smooth <surface> $S$, <Stokes theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
=\iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,dS.}
$$
Here $\partial S$ is the boundary <curve>, $d\mathbf r$ is its directed tangent element, $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is the chosen <unit normal vector>, $dS$ is the <surface area element>, and $\nabla\times\mathbf F$ is the <curl>. The <right-hand rule> relates the boundary direction to $\hat{\mathbf n}$. Thus <circulation> around the edge equals the <flux> of <curl> through the spanning <surface>. Reversing both orientations changes both signs. One can replace a difficult <line integral> by an easier <surface integral>, or replace one spanning <surface> by another within the region of regularity.

For a bounded <volume> $V$ with piecewise smooth closed boundary and a continuously differentiable <vector field> on a neighborhood of its closure, the <divergence theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\iint_{\partial V}\mathbf F\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}_{\rm out}\,dS
=\iiint_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV.}
$$
Here $\hat{\mathbf n}_{\rm out}$ points out of $V$, including into any holes, $dV$ is the <volume element>, and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F$ is the <divergence>. It equates total outward <flux> to integrated source strength. It converts a closed <surface integral> into a <volume integral>, and explains why internal <fluxes> cancel in a subdivided region. Singularities cannot be included without checking the hypotheses.

For a <conservative force>, the <work> around every closed path is zero. <Stokes theorem> gives zero <flux> of <curl> through every sufficiently small patch. Dividing by its area and shrinking the patch shows that every component of the continuous <curl> vanishes:
$$
\boxed{\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=0,\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf F=0,\qquad \mathbf F=-\nabla U.}
$$
Here $U$ is <potential energy>; conversely, zero <curl> implies a <conservative vector field> on a <simply connected domain>. The <divergence theorem> then gives the compatible relation $\iint_{\partial V}\mathbf F\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,dS=-\iiint_V\nabla^2U\,dV$. There is no general zero-<divergence> condition on a <conservative force>: $\mathbf F=-\mathbf r=-\nabla(r^2/2)$ is a concrete counterexample, with $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=-3$.

Take $0<r_1<r_2$, so the shell excludes the possible singularity at the origin. The <divergence and curl of a radial vector field> formula in <spherical coordinates> gives
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F
=\frac1{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2r^{-\beta}\right)
=(2-\beta)r^{-\beta-1}.
$$
With <volume element> $dV=r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi$, direct integration gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\iiint_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV
&=\int_{r_1}^{r_2}\int_0^\pi\int_0^{2\pi}
(2-\beta)r^{1-\beta}\sin\theta\,d\phi\,d\theta\,dr\\
&=\boxed{4\pi\left(r_2^{2-\beta}-r_1^{2-\beta}\right).}
\end{aligned}
$$
For the independent boundary calculation, the outer <sphere> has outward <unit normal vector> $+\hat{\mathbf r}$ and the inner <sphere> has outward <unit normal vector> $-\hat{\mathbf r}$. Their <fluxes> are
$$
\Phi_{\rm outer}=4\pi r_2^2r_2^{-\beta},\qquad
\Phi_{\rm inner}=-4\pi r_1^2r_1^{-\beta}.
$$
Their sum equals the direct <volume integral>, verifying the <divergence theorem>. The restriction $\beta\ne2$ permits the displayed power-antiderivative step. At $\beta=2$, treated separately, both the shell <volume integral> and net boundary <flux> are zero; the excluded origin must still not be filled in.

Away from the origin, write $\mathbf F=r^{-\beta-1}\mathbf r$. The <curl product rule for a scalar field> and the <gradient> of a radial function give
$$
\nabla\times\mathbf F
=\nabla(r^{-\beta-1})\times\mathbf r
+r^{-\beta-1}\nabla\times\mathbf r
=-(\beta+1)r^{-\beta-3}\mathbf r\times\mathbf r=0.
$$
Consequently the <surface integral> of <curl> over the upper hemisphere is zero. Its outward <unit normal vector> induces a counterclockwise equator when viewed from above. Parametrize that boundary by
$$
\mathbf r(\phi)=r_2(\cos\phi,\sin\phi,0),\qquad
d\mathbf r=r_2(-\sin\phi,\cos\phi,0)\,d\phi.
$$
The radial <vector field> is perpendicular to this tangent, so
$$
\boxed{\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=0
=\iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,dS.}
$$
This verifies <Stokes theorem> directly without passing through the singular origin.

Regard the planar <vector field> as $(A(x,y),B(x,y),0)$, with no dependence on $z$. Its <curl> is
$$
\nabla\times\mathbf V=
\begin{vmatrix}
\mathbf i&\mathbf j&\mathbf k\\
\partial_x&\partial_y&\partial_z\\
A&B&0
\end{vmatrix}
=\left(\frac{\partial B}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial A}{\partial y}\right)\mathbf k.

Thus, for continuously differentiable $A$ and $B$, the necessary and sufficient pointwise condition is
$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial B}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial A}{\partial y}.}
$$
To deduce global <path independence> from this local condition also requires a suitable domain, for example a <simply connected domain>.

An <exact differential> is an expression $P\,dx+Q\,dy$ that equals $df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy$ for a single-valued differentiable <function> $f$ on the domain. Its <line integral> is an endpoint difference by the <fundamental theorem for line integrals>.

Assume a simple, piecewise smooth closed <curve> oriented counterclockwise around its enclosed region $D$. Apply <Green's theorem> with $P=-y$ and $Q=x$:
$$
\oint_C(x\,dy-y\,dx)
=\iint_D(Q_x-P_y)\,dx\,dy
=\iint_D2\,dx\,dy
=\boxed{2\,\operatorname{area}(D).}
$$
The orientation assumption is essential. A clockwise unit <circle>, $x=\cos\theta$, $y=-\sin\theta$ for $0\le\theta\le2\pi$, gives $-2\pi$ although its enclosed <area> is $\pi$. For a self-intersecting <curve> the same <line integral> measures twice the <signed area>, with multiplicity, rather than necessarily twice an ordinary enclosed <area>.

For positive semiaxes $a,b$, the <ellipse> has the counterclockwise <parametrization> $x=a\cos\theta$, $y=b\sin\theta$. Then
$$
x\,dy-y\,dx
=ab(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta)\,d\theta=ab\,d\theta.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{area}(\text{ellipse})=\frac12\int_0^{2\pi}ab\,d\theta=\pi ab.}
$$
If nonzero signed constants are allowed in place of positive semiaxes, the geometric <area> is $\pi|ab|$.

= Solution

A reference string segment $dx$ has <mass> $\mu\,dx$ and transverse <velocity> $y_t$. Its <kinetic energy> is $dK=\mu y_t^2\,dx/2$. In the small-slope approximation its extra <arc length> is
$$
d\ell-dx=\bigl(\sqrt{1+y_x^2}-1\bigr)dx
=\frac12y_x^2\,dx+O(y_x^4)\,dx.
$$
The <work> against constant <tension> $T$ supplies the corresponding <elastic potential energy> $dU=T(d\ell-dx)$. To quadratic order, the <energy density> per unit reference length is therefore
$$
\boxed{u=\frac{d(K+U)}{dx}
=\frac12\left(\mu y_t^2+Ty_x^2\right).}
$$
For the <travelling wave> $y=A\cos(kx-\omega t+\phi)$, the <wave equation> implies $\omega^2=(T/\mu)k^2$. Its <kinetic energy density> and elastic <energy density> are equal, so
$$
u(x,t)=\mu A^2\omega^2\sin^2(kx-\omega t+\phi).
$$
The <energy per wavelength of a transverse string wave> comes from integration over any full <wavelength> $\lambda=2\pi/|k|$:
$$
\boxed{E_\lambda=\int_{x_*}^{x_*+\lambda}u(x,t)\,dx
=\frac12\mu A^2\omega^2\lambda,\qquad
\langle u\rangle=\frac12\mu A^2\omega^2.}
$$
**The printed expression for energy per wavelength is missing a factor $\lambda$.** Its right-hand side is the mean <energy density>, with units of <energy> per length. For a concrete counterexample within the small-slope regime, take $\mu=1\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}}$, $T=1\,\mathrm N$, $A=0.01\,\mathrm m$ and $\omega=\pi\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. Then $\lambda=2\,\mathrm m$ and direct integration gives $E_\lambda=10^{-4}\pi^2\,\mathrm J$, whereas the printed right-hand side is $5\times10^{-5}\pi^2\,\mathrm{J\,m^{-1}}$. It cannot equal an <energy>.

For the <reflection of a string wave from a freely moving endpoint mass>, let $c=\sqrt{T/\mu}$, $k=\omega/c>0$ and use the complex <time-harmonic wave> convention
$$
y(x,t)=\operatorname{Re}\left\{A_i\left(e^{ikx}+R e^{-ikx}\right)e^{-i\omega t}\right\},
\qquad x\le0.
$$
The string pulls its endpoint toward negative $x$, so its transverse <force> on the attached <mass> is $-Ty_x(0,t)$. <Newton's second law> gives the <boundary condition> $my_{tt}(0,t)=-Ty_x(0,t)$. Substitution yields
$$
m\omega^2(1+R)=iTk(1-R),\qquad
\boxed{R=\frac{iTk-m\omega^2}{iTk+m\omega^2}
=\frac{Z+i m\omega}{Z-i m\omega},\quad Z=\frac Tc=\sqrt{T\mu}.}
$$
Here $Z$ is the <characteristic impedance of a stretched string>. Since the incident and reflected <waves> share the same string, their mean <power> ratio is the squared modulus of this displacement <reflection coefficient>:
$$
\boxed{\mathcal R=|R|^2=1.}
$$
There is **no remaining fraction of the mean incident energy**: the freely moving <mass> provides no <dissipation> and no onward transmission channel. It exchanges <kinetic energy> with the string during each cycle but has zero mean absorbed <power>. Indeed the <energy flux of a transverse string wave> satisfies
$$
-Ty_xy_t\big|_{x=0}
=my_{tt}y_t
=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12my_t(0,t)^2\right),
$$
whose cycle average is zero. As checks, $m\to0$ gives $R=+1$, the <free-end reflection of a transverse string wave>, while $m\to\infty$ at fixed $\omega>0$ gives $R=-1$, the fixed-end limit.

Use signed <arc length> displacements $x_1,x_2$ in the common clockwise sense shown by both arrows in the source diagram. Near the diametrically opposite equilibrium, one <spring> changes length by $x_2-x_1$ and the other by $x_1-x_2$. Their combined <elastic potential energy> is
$$
U=\frac k2(x_2-x_1)^2+\frac k2(x_1-x_2)^2=k(x_1-x_2)^2.
$$
The <kinetic energy> is $K=(m_1\dot x_1^2+qm_1\dot x_2^2)/2$. The tangential components of <Newton's second law>, or the derivatives $-\partial U/\partial x_i$, give
$$
\boxed{m_1\ddot x_1=-2k(x_1-x_2),\qquad
qm_1\ddot x_2=2k(x_1-x_2),\quad q>0.}
$$
Both <springs> contribute to each restoring <force>. The hoop radius drops out because the coordinates are <arc lengths>; angular coordinates $x_i/r$ give the same equations after cancelling the common factor $r^2$ in the energies.

To find the <normal modes of two arc-spring-coupled beads on a hoop>, take $x_i=a_i e^{i\Omega t}$ for a <normal mode>. The resulting <generalized eigenvalue problem> is
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
2k-m_1\Omega^2&-2k\\
-2k&2k-qm_1\Omega^2
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}a_1\\a_2\end{pmatrix}=0.
$$
Its <determinant> is
$$
(2k-m_1\Omega^2)(2k-qm_1\Omega^2)-4k^2
=m_1\Omega^2\left(qm_1\Omega^2-2k(1+q)\right).
$$
Thus the <angular frequencies> are
$$
\boxed{\Omega_0=0,\qquad
\omega=\sqrt{\frac{2k(1+q)}{qm_1}}.}
$$
The ordinary nonzero <frequency> is $\omega/(2\pi)$. The <zero mode> has <mode shape> $(1,1)$ and represents a common <rotation> with no change in <spring> length. The oscillatory <mode shape> is $(1,-1/q)$.

Define a common displacement coordinate $X=(x_1+qx_2)/(1+q)$ and a relative displacement $\delta=x_1-x_2$. Adding and subtracting the equations of motion gives
$$
\ddot X=0,\qquad
\ddot\delta+\omega^2\delta=0.
$$
The general real motions of the two <normal modes> are therefore
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{zero mode:}\quad &(x_1,x_2)=(C+Dt)(1,1),\\
\text{oscillatory mode:}\quad &(x_1,x_2)=
\bigl(A\cos\omega t+B\sin\omega t\bigr)(1,-1/q).
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $C,D,A,B$ are independent real constants. The <zero mode> includes uniform drift as well as a static common displacement; writing only a constant times $\cos0t$ would miss the independent linear-in-time solution. Their <superposition> is
$$
\boxed{x_1=C+Dt+A\cos\omega t+B\sin\omega t,\qquad
x_2=C+Dt-\frac1q(A\cos\omega t+B\sin\omega t).}
$$
In the oscillatory <normal mode>, $m_1x_1+m_2x_2=0$ and the two <amplitudes> have ratio $1/q$, with opposite <phases>. Equivalently the oscillatory motion has zero total <angular momentum> about the hoop center.

As $q\to\infty$ with $m_1,k$ fixed, $\omega\to\sqrt{2k/m_1}$ and the heavier particle's oscillatory <amplitude> tends to zero for fixed lighter-particle <amplitude>. The lighter particle then oscillates against two effectively anchored <springs> with combined <spring constant> $2k$. A common uniform <rotation> remains an allowed <zero mode>; the heavy particle is stationary in the limit only if the initial common drift is zero or tends to zero, as it does for bounded total <angular momentum>.

= Solution
{parent=d}

For $q=2$, the common coordinate has $X(0)=(x_1(0)+2x_2(0))/3=0$ and $\dot X(0)=\pi\omega r/20$. The relative coordinate has $\delta(0)=3\pi r/20$ and $\dot\delta(0)=0$. Consequently
$$
X(t)=\frac{\pi\omega r}{20}t,\qquad
\delta(t)=\frac{3\pi r}{20}\cos\omega t.
$$
Since $x_1=X+2\delta/3$ and $x_2=X-\delta/3$, the full <superposition> is
$$
x_1(t)=\frac{\pi\omega r}{20}t+\frac{\pi r}{10}\cos\omega t,\qquad
x_2(t)=\frac{\pi\omega r}{20}t-\frac{\pi r}{20}\cos\omega t.
$$
The heavier particle's <velocity> is
$$
\dot x_2(t)=\frac{\pi\omega r}{20}(1+\sin\omega t).
$$
It first vanishes when $\sin\omega t=-1$, so
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm first}=\frac{3\pi}{2\omega}.}
$$
This <velocity> is nonnegative and only touches zero: the heavier particle does not reverse its direction at that instant. Both the uniform <zero mode> and the oscillatory <normal mode> are needed to obtain this result.
