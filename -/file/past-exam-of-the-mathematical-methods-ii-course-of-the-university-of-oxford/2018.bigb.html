= Solution
{parent=1}

The <Stokes theorem> states that for an oriented smooth <surface> $S$ with compatible oriented boundary $C$ and a continuously differentiable <vector field>,
$$
\oint_C F\cdot d\ell=\int_S(\nabla\times F)\cdot n\,dS.
$$
Here $\nabla\times F=(-2,-2,-2)$, whereas a <normal vector> to the specified <plane> is $(1,0,-1)$. Their <dot product> is zero. Thus **every closed curve in that <plane> has zero circulation** by the <Stokes theorem>.

The source says “any curve” and displays an ordinary <line integral>, without specifying that it is closed or giving endpoints. An open <curve> need not give zero. On the <plane>, put $z=x-1$ and $dz=dx$; the pulled-back <differential one-form> becomes
$$
F\cdot d\ell=(y-x+1)dx-dy+(x-y)dx=dx-dy=d(x-y).
$$
Therefore the complete endpoint-dependent answer is
$$
\boxed{\int_{P}^{Q}F\cdot d\ell=(x_Q-y_Q)-(x_P-y_P),}
$$
which specializes to zero for a closed <curve>. For example, the path from $(1,0,0)$ to $(2,0,1)$ lies in the <plane> and has integral $1$. This illustrates a <conservative restriction of a vector field to a plane> despite nonzero ambient <curl>.

The <velocity vector> of the <helix> is $r'(t)=(-\sin t,\cos t,1)$, whose <Euclidean norm> is $\sqrt2$. Thus its <arc length> over the stated parameter interval is
$$
\boxed{L=\int_0^{2\pi}\|r'(t)\|\,dt=2\pi\sqrt2.}
$$
Omitting the two endpoints does not change this finite <arc length>.

Rationalize the difference of <square roots> before taking the <limit>. For positive $x$, divide the resulting numerator and denominator by $x^2$:
$$
\frac{8x^2+2x}{\bigl(\sqrt{x^3+10x^2}+\sqrt{x^3+2x^2-2x}\bigr)\sqrt{6x+3}}
=\frac{8+2/x}{\bigl(\sqrt{1+10/x}+\sqrt{1+2/x-2/x^2}\bigr)\sqrt{6+3/x}}.
$$
Each factor now has a finite nonzero denominator <limit>, giving
$$
\boxed{\frac4{\sqrt6}=\frac{2\sqrt6}{3}.}
$$

= Solution

For $x>0$, $\sqrt{1+x^2}$ increases from $1$, while $2/\sqrt{1+x^2}$ decreases from $2$. They meet at $(1,\sqrt2)$, after which their order reverses. In particular, their positive separation on $0<x<1$ is $(1-x^2)/\sqrt{1+x^2}$.

\Image[2018-hyperbolic-area.png]
{title=The two curves and their enclosed positive area on $0<x<1$, with crossing at $(1,\sqrt2)$}

Use the <hyperbolic substitution> $x=\sinh t$, $dx=\cosh t\,dt$. With $T=\operatorname{arsinh}1$, the <definite integral> for the area becomes
$$
A=\int_0^T(2-\cosh^2t)\,dt
=\left[\frac32t-\frac14\sinh2t\right]_0^T.
$$
Since $\sinh T=1$, $\cosh T=\sqrt2$ and $T=\log(1+\sqrt2)$, the answer is
$$
\boxed{A=\frac{3\log(1+\sqrt2)-\sqrt2}{2}.}
$$

= Solution

Let $D=y(3y-2)$. <Implicit differentiation> of the two constraints gives the <linear system>
$$
D y'-2zz'=-2x,\qquad aD y'+2zz'=-2.
$$
Adding them, then substituting back, gives at regular points
$$
\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{2(x+1)}{(1+a)y(3y-2)},\qquad
\frac{dz}{dx}=\frac{ax-1}{(1+a)z}.}
$$
The relevant <Jacobian determinant> is $2(1+a)yz(3y-2)$. Thus the special parameter value is $\boxed{a=-1}$, assuming the other factors are nonzero. At that parameter, adding the original constraints gives $(x+1)^2=0$: the <intersection curve> lies wholly in the <plane> $x=-1$ and cannot be parametrized by $x$ on an interval. Calling its derivatives “infinite” describes a vertical <projection>, not a finite-valued differentiable pair $y(x),z(x)$ at $a=-1$; the displayed ratios then have simultaneous zero numerators on the intersection and are undefined.

There are also point-dependent failures when $y=0$, $y=2/3$ or $z=0$. These can produce vertical <tangents> or require a different local parameter, and simultaneous numerator zeros need separate analysis. The <implicit function theorem> guarantees finite $y',z'$ only where the stated <Jacobian determinant> is nonzero. This distinction is the geometry of a <singular projection of an implicit curve>.

Multiply the <secant function> integrand by $(\sec\theta+\tan\theta)/(\sec\theta+\tan\theta)$:
$$
\int\sec\theta\,d\theta
=\int\frac{\sec^2\theta+\sec\theta\tan\theta}{\sec\theta+\tan\theta}\,d\theta
=\boxed{\log|\sec\theta+\tan\theta|+C.}
$$
The numerator is the <derivative> of the denominator. The formula holds on any real interval avoiding zeros of $\cos\theta$; on such an interval $\sec\theta+\tan\theta$ cannot vanish because $(\sec\theta+\tan\theta)(\sec\theta-\tan\theta)=1$.

= Solution

Write the initial <triangular pulse> as the function $h$, extended by zero to negative arguments:
$$
h(s)=\begin{cases}as/L,&0\leq s\leq L,\\a(2L-s)/L,&L\leq s\leq2L,\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
$$
At $t=0$, the initial <displacement> and zero <velocity> give $f+g=h$ and $-cf'+cg'=0$ on the positive half-line. Fix the harmless opposite additive constants by taking both <travelling waves> to vanish at positive infinity. Then
$$
\boxed{f(s)=g(s)=\tfrac12h(s)\quad(s>0),\qquad f(s)=-\tfrac12h(-s)\quad(s<0).}
$$
The second formula follows from the fixed-end <boundary condition> $f(-ct)+g(ct)=0$. Thus $f$ is one-half of the <odd extension> $H$ of the initial <displacement>, and
$$
y(x,t)=\tfrac12[H(x-ct)+H(x+ct)],\qquad x\geq0.
$$
This is the <odd-extension solution for a semi-infinite fixed string>: the reflected <displacement> reverses sign. Evaluating the travelling profiles gives
$$
\boxed{y(x,L/c)=\tfrac12h(x-L),\qquad
y(x,2L/c)=\tfrac12[h(x-2L)-h(x)].}
$$
The first profile is a positive triangle supported on $[L,3L]$, with peak $a/2$ at $2L$. The second has a negative triangle on $[0,2L]$ with minimum $-a/2$ at $L$, and a positive triangle on $[2L,4L]$ with maximum $a/2$ at $3L$.

= Solution
{parent=8}

The <gradient> is $\nabla f=(2x-4x^3,-2y-4y^3)$, so the real <stationary points> are $(0,0)$ and $(\pm1/\sqrt2,0)$. The <Hessian matrix> is diagonal, with entries $2-12x^2$ and $-2-12y^2$. At the origin it has opposite-sign <eigenvalues>, giving a <saddle point>. At each of the other two <stationary points> it is negative definite, giving **strict maxima of value $1/4$**. These are also the global maxima, since
$$
f(x,y)=\frac14-\left(x^2-\frac12\right)^2-y^2-y^4\leq\frac14.
$$
There are **no local minima**, and the <function> is unbounded below. The zero <contour> has two lobes touching at the saddle; positive <contours> are closed curves around the maxima.

Under the stated <change of variables>,
$$
f=\frac12-X^2-Y^2,\qquad x>0\Longleftrightarrow X>-\frac12,\qquad y>0\Longleftrightarrow Y>\frac12.
$$
Inside the resulting circular cap the constraint $X>-1/2$ is automatic. Comparing its <circle> and horizontal boundary gives
$$
\boxed{r=\frac1{\sqrt2},\qquad a=\frac12,\qquad b=\frac12.}
$$
Here $a$ is the positive half-width used in the source figure, independent of the pulse-height notation in the preceding question. The <Jacobian determinant> is $\partial(X,Y)/\partial(x,y)=4xy>0$ in the chosen quadrant, so the integral is one-quarter of the <circular segment> area:
$$
\int_Rxy\,dx\,dy=\frac14\left[r^2\arccos\frac br-b\sqrt{r^2-b^2}\right]
=\frac14\left(\frac\pi8-\frac14\right)
=\boxed{\frac{\pi-2}{32}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=a}

Apply the <divergence theorem> to $A(r)=(C\cdot r)(C'\cdot r)J(r)$. Since $C,C'$ are constant and $J$ is a <solenoidal vector field>, the <product rule> gives
$$
\nabla\cdot A=(C\cdot J)(C'\cdot r)+(C'\cdot J)(C\cdot r).
$$
Its boundary flux vanishes when $J$ has <compact support>, or when its normal component vanishes on the boundary of the integration volume. Integrating therefore proves
$$
\boxed{\int_V(C\cdot r)(C'\cdot J)\,dV=-\int_V(C'\cdot r)(C\cdot J)\,dV.}
$$
For a discontinuous field, the divergence-free hypothesis must include its boundary jump, in the sense of a <weak derivative>. A field that merely has zero <divergence> at interior points can still have a nonzero surface source; that weaker hypothesis would not justify discarding the flux. The result is the <antisymmetric first moment of a localized solenoidal field>.

= Solution

Use the <vector triple product> identity:
$$
C\times(J\times r)=J(C\cdot r)-r(C\cdot J).
$$
For the $i$th component of the second integral, the <antisymmetric first moment of a localized solenoidal field> with $C'=e_i$ gives $\int_Vr_i(C\cdot J)\,dV=-\int_V(C\cdot r)J_i\,dV$. Thus the two terms on the right have opposite integrals. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\int_V(C\cdot r)J\,dV=\frac12C\times\int_V(J\times r)\,dV.}
$$

{parent=9}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Inside the unit <ball>, $\nabla\cdot J=0$. On its <sphere>, $J\cdot r=xy-xy=0$, so the jump to zero outside adds no surface <divergence>. The field therefore satisfies the required <weak derivative> condition.

By reflection <symmetry>, integrals of $xy,xz,yz$ over the <ball> vanish. Rotational <symmetry> and <spherical coordinates> give
$$
\int_{B_1}x^2\,dV=\frac13\int_{B_1}r^2\,dV=\frac{4\pi}{3}\int_0^1r^4\,dr=\frac{4\pi}{15}.
$$
Hence the left side is $\int x(y,-x,0)\,dV=(0,-4\pi/15,0)$. Meanwhile $J\times r=(-xz,-yz,x^2+y^2)$, so its integral is $(0,0,8\pi/15)$. Therefore
$$
\frac12(1,0,0)\times(0,0,8\pi/15)=\boxed{(0,-4\pi/15,0),}
$$
exactly the same <vector>.

= Solution

Let $\delta=x_2-x_1$. The inextensible-string constraint gives the upward displacement $y_2=l-\sqrt{l^2-\delta^2}=\delta^2/(2l)+O(\delta^4/l^3)$. Thus the quadratic <potential energy> is $U=kx_1^2/2+mg(x_2-x_1)^2/(2l)$. The vertical <velocity> of the second <mass> is of second order in the small motion, so the quadratic <kinetic energy> is $m(\dot x_1^2+\dot x_2^2)/2$.

The <Euler-Lagrange equations> for this quadratic <Lagrangian> give
$$
\boxed{\ddot x_1=-\left(\frac km+\frac gl\right)x_1+\frac glx_2,\qquad
\ddot x_2=\frac glx_1-\frac glx_2.}
$$
Small <displacements> are used both in the quadratic approximation to the <gravitational potential energy> and in neglecting the higher-order vertical <kinetic energy>. These are the <spring-supported pendulum normal modes> at linear order. The corresponding force picture sets the string <tension> to $mg$ at leading order and its horizontal angle to $\delta/l$.

= Solution

Put $\kappa=k/m$, $q=g/l$. The positive stiffness-per-mass <matrix> is
$$
K=\begin{pmatrix}\kappa+q&-q\\-q&q\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Substitution of a <normal mode> $x=v\cos\omega t$ requires $Kv=\omega^2v$. Its <characteristic equation> is $\lambda^2-(\kappa+2q)\lambda+\kappa q=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\omega_\pm^2=\frac\kappa2+q\pm\sqrt{\frac{\kappa^2}{4}+q^2}.}
$$
For $k,m,g,l>0$, their sum and product are positive, so both squared <angular frequencies> are positive and $\omega_+>\omega_->0$.

= Solution

Define $D=\sqrt{\kappa^2/4+q^2}$. Normalizing the first <displacement> <amplitude> to one, either row of the <eigenvector> equation gives
$$
\boxed{v_\pm=\begin{pmatrix}1\\r_\pm\end{pmatrix},\qquad r_\pm=\frac{\kappa/2\mp D}{q}.}
$$
The two <normal modes> are $x(t)=A_\pm v_\pm\cos(\omega_\pm t+\phi_\pm)$, and arbitrary small motion is their <superposition>. The lower-frequency mode has $r_->0$, so the <masses> move in phase; the upper-frequency mode has $r_+<0$, so they move in opposite phase. The identity $r_+r_-=-1$ verifies <orthogonality> of these <eigenvectors> for equal <masses>.

= Solution
{parent=d}

The product-to-sum identity gives $\sin\Omega t\sin\omega t=[\cos(\Omega-\omega)t-\cos(\Omega+\omega)t]/2$. Thus the first <mass> must have equal and opposite cosine-mode amplitudes and no sine-mode amplitudes. Equivalently, release the second <mass> from a <displacement> $X$ while the first is at equilibrium, both initially at rest:
$$
\boxed{x_1(0)=0,\quad x_2(0)=X,\quad\dot x_1(0)=\dot x_2(0)=0.}
$$
The two <normal mode> coefficients in $x_1$ are $qX/(2D)$ and $-qX/(2D)$. Therefore, with
$$
\boxed{\Omega=\frac{\omega_++\omega_-}{2},\qquad\omega=\frac{\omega_+-\omega_-}{2},}
$$
the two <displacements> are
$$
\boxed{x_1(t)=\frac{qX}{D}\sin\Omega t\sin\omega t,\qquad
x_2(t)=X\left[\cos\Omega t\cos\omega t+\frac{\kappa}{2D}\sin\Omega t\sin\omega t\right].}
$$
These initial conditions are necessary for a nontrivial product with exactly the stated time origin, as well as sufficient: $x_1(0)=\dot x_1(0)=0$ follows from the product, and its zero third derivative at zero, together with the first coupled equation, forces $\dot x_2(0)=0$. Since both <normal frequencies> are positive, $\Omega>\omega>0$. This is the product form used to describe <beating>, although a well-separated slow envelope requires the two <normal frequencies> to be close. No such extra near-degeneracy is assumed in the exact formula.

= Solution
{parent=11}

For a <plane wave>, $\partial_t$ acts as $-i\omega$ and $\partial_x$ as $ik$. Substitution gives
$$
\omega^2(\omega-\omega_n)-c^2k^2(\omega-\omega_d)=0,
$$
so, for $\omega\ne\omega_d$, the <dispersion relation> is
$$
\boxed{k^2=\frac{\omega^2(\omega-\omega_n)}{c^2(\omega-\omega_d)}.}
$$
Assume $\omega_n>\omega_d>0$ and real <angular frequency>. The ratio is negative exactly on $\omega_d<\omega<\omega_n$, giving imaginary <wavenumbers> and an <evanescent wave>. Thus **the propagating frequency gap is $(\omega_d,\omega_n)$**. At $\omega_d$ there is no finite-$k$ solution, while $\omega_n$ has $k=0$; neither endpoint is an ordinary finite-wavelength propagating mode.

Choose the branch $k=(\omega/c)\sqrt{(\omega-\omega_n)/(\omega-\omega_d)}$, so the <phase velocity> is positive in the allowed bands. Direct differentiation gives the <phase velocity> and <group velocity>
$$
v_p=c\sqrt{\frac{\omega-\omega_d}{\omega-\omega_n}},\qquad
v_g=\frac{v_p}{1+\dfrac{\omega(\omega_n-\omega_d)}{2(\omega-\omega_n)(\omega-\omega_d)}}.
$$
The opposite spatial branch reverses both signs. On the chosen branch, the requested <limits> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|cc}
\text{frequency limit}&v_p&v_g\\\hline
\omega\to0&c\sqrt{\omega_d/\omega_n}&c\sqrt{\omega_d/\omega_n}\\
|\omega|\to\infty&c&c\\
\omega\to\omega_n^+&+\infty&0^+\\
\omega\to\omega_d^-&0^+&0^+
\end{array}}
$$
The band-edge limits are taken from their propagating sides. More precisely, with $\epsilon=\omega-\omega_n\downarrow0$ and $\delta=\omega_d-\omega\downarrow0$,
$$
v_p\sim c\sqrt{\frac{\omega_n-\omega_d}{\epsilon}},\quad
v_g\sim\frac{2c}{\omega_n}\sqrt{(\omega_n-\omega_d)\epsilon};\qquad
v_p\sim c\sqrt{\frac\delta{\omega_n-\omega_d}},\quad
v_g\sim\frac{2c\delta^{3/2}}{\omega_d\sqrt{\omega_n-\omega_d}}.
$$

For the final case, set $K=k_0c>0$. Substitution of $T(t)e^{ik_0x}$ at $\omega_d=0$ gives $T^{(3)}+(3iK/2)T''+K^2T'=0$. Its <characteristic polynomial> factors as $r(r+2iK)(r-iK/2)$, hence
$$
T=A+Be^{-2iKt}+Ce^{iKt/2}.
$$
The initial data give $A+B+C=1$, $-2B+C/2=0$ and $-4B-C/4=-5$. Thus $B=1$, $C=4$, $A=-4$, and
$$
\boxed{\phi(x,t)=e^{ik_0x}\left[-4+e^{-2ik_0ct}+4e^{ik_0ct/2}\right].}
$$
The time-independent contribution is essential: <static modes lost by cancelling frequency> would be missed if one divided the $\omega_d=0$ <dispersion relation> by $\omega$ and retained only its two nonzero-frequency roots.
