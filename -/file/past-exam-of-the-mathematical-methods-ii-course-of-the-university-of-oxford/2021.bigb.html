The <arc length> of the <helix> follows from its parametrization:
$$
\mathbf r'(t)=\bigl(-10\sin(t-\pi/2),\ 10\cos(t-\pi/2),\ 5\bigr),\qquad
|\mathbf r'(t)|=\sqrt{100+25}=5\sqrt5.

Thus $\ell_h=15\pi\sqrt5\,\mathrm m$. Its endpoint is $(0,20,15\pi)$, at height $h=15\pi\,\mathrm m$.

Under the usual intended interpretation that the descending quarter of a <circle> drops through one <radius>, its <radius> is $R=h$. Its <arc length> is $\ell_c=\pi R/2=15\pi^2/2\,\mathrm m$, giving
$$
\boxed{\ell_h+\ell_c=15\pi\sqrt5+\frac{15\pi^2}{2}=179.39425397\ldots\,\mathrm m\simeq179\,\mathrm m.}
$$
**The printed smoothness condition is inconsistent.** The terminal <tangent vector> of the <helix> is $\mathbf r'(3\pi)=(-10,0,5)$. Every <tangent vector> to a <curve> confined to the $yz$ <plane> has zero $x$ component, so no such <regular curve> can join this <helix> with a common <tangent line>. Moreover, the <helix> is still rising at that endpoint. Thus no smooth ride satisfying all the literal conditions exists.

Even after allowing a corner, a quarter of a <circle> reaching the ground does not alone determine its <radius>: the orientation of its endpoint radii must also be specified. For example, in the $yz$ <plane>, the circular arc
$$
y=20+R\bigl(\cos\theta-\cos\theta_0\bigr),\quad
z=h+R\bigl(\sin\theta-\sin\theta_0\bigr),\quad
\theta_0-\pi/2\le\theta\le\theta_0,

Choosing $\theta_0=\pi/4$ gives $R=h/\sqrt2$, whereas $\theta_0=\pi/2$ gives $R=h$. The numerical answer above is conditional on the intended $R=h$ geometry; it does not repair the impossible smooth join.

The negative sign is essential. Absolute convergence follows by the same calculation with the sign removed, so the singular edge at $y=0$ causes no ambiguity.

Take an ideal flexible <mechanical string> with constant <tension> $T>0$ and uniform <linear mass density> $\mu>0$. Let $x$ measure distance along its straight equilibrium position and let $y(x,t)$ be its small transverse <displacement>. Neglect <damping>, bending stiffness and external distributed <forces>, and assume $|y_x|\ll1$.

A segment of reference length $\Delta x$ has mass $\mu\Delta x$. The endpoint <tension> forces have transverse resultant
$$
T\sin\theta(x+\Delta x)-T\sin\theta(x)
\simeq T\bigl[y_x(x+\Delta x,t)-y_x(x,t)\bigr],
$$
since $\sin\theta\simeq\tan\theta=y_x$ for small slopes. <Newton's second law>, divided by $\Delta x$ and taken to the limit, gives
$$
\mu y_{tt}=Ty_{xx},\qquad
\boxed{y_{xx}=\frac1{c^2}y_{tt},\quad c=\sqrt{T/\mu}.}
$$
Here $t$ is time, the subscripts denote <partial derivatives>, and $c$ is the <wave speed>. The transverse <acceleration> is $y_{tt}$; $y_{xx}$ describes the small-slope curvature responsible for the net transverse <force>.

A <normal mode> of a stable undamped linear <coupled oscillator> has a single <angular frequency> and a fixed ratio of component <displacements>. All components share a sinusoidal time factor, with relative <oscillation phases> of zero or $\pi$ for real <mode shapes>. A general free motion is a <superposition> of the <normal modes>. For this system the symmetric stiffness <matrix> gives real <eigenvalues> and orthogonal <eigenvectors>; positive <spring constants> make the squared <angular frequencies> positive.

Write $m=m_1=m_2>0$ and $\kappa=k_{12}$. Assume ideal massless linear springs, negligible <damping> and positive $k_1,k_2,\kappa$. By <Hooke's law> and <Newton's second law>,
$$
\boxed{m\ddot x_1=-(k_1+\kappa)x_1+\kappa x_2,\qquad
m\ddot x_2=\kappa x_1-(k_2+\kappa)x_2.}
$$
The coupling spring extends by $x_2-x_1$, giving equal and opposite coupling <forces>.

For a <normal mode>, take $\mathbf x=\operatorname{Re}(\mathbf a e^{i\omega t})$. Nonzero <eigenvectors> require
$$
\det\begin{pmatrix}k_1+\kappa-m\omega^2&-\kappa\\-\kappa&k_2+\kappa-m\omega^2\end{pmatrix}=0.
$$
Solving this <quadratic equation> in $m\omega^2$ gives
$$
\boxed{\omega_\pm^2=\frac{k_1+k_2+2\kappa\ \pm\sqrt{(k_1-k_2)^2+4\kappa^2}}{2m}.}
$$
A corresponding <mode shape> has ratio
$$
\frac{a_2}{a_1}=\frac{k_1+\kappa-m\omega_\pm^2}{\kappa},
$$
and the general real solution is $\mathbf x=C_-\mathbf a_-\cos(\omega_-t+\phi_-)+C_+\mathbf a_+\cos(\omega_+t+\phi_+)$. The arbitrary <amplitudes> and <oscillation phases> account for four independent initial data.

**Weak coupling alone does not guarantee large beats.** A useful representative sketch assumes nearly equal uncoupled <angular frequencies>. In the resonant case $k_1=k_2=k$ with $\kappa\ll k$, the <mode shapes> are $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$, and
$$
\omega_-^2=k/m,\qquad \omega_+^2=(k+2\kappa)/m.
$$
For $x_1(0)=A$, $x_2(0)=\dot x_1(0)=\dot x_2(0)=0$, both <normal modes> have equal coefficients. With $\bar\omega=(\omega_++\omega_-)/2$ and $\Delta\omega=\omega_+-\omega_-$,
$$
\boxed{x_1=A\cos(\bar\omega t)\cos(\Delta\omega t/2),\qquad
x_2=A\sin(\bar\omega t)\sin(\Delta\omega t/2).}
$$
These rapid <oscillations> have slowly varying complementary <wave envelopes>. The initial <displacement> shifts almost entirely to the other mass at $t=\pi/\Delta\omega$, and returns after $2\pi/\Delta\omega$. Arbitrary initial data give a different <superposition>; if $|k_1-k_2|\gg\kappa$, the <normal modes> are localized mainly on individual masses, and strong exchange is suppressed.

\Image[2021-coupled-modes.png]
{title=Representative weakly coupled resonant motion: rapid displacements inside slowly exchanging envelopes}

Holding one mass fixed leaves the coupling spring attached and stretched by the other mass. Therefore the two constrained <angular frequencies> are
$$
\boxed{\omega_1=\sqrt{(k_1+\kappa)/m},\qquad \omega_2=\sqrt{(k_2+\kappa)/m}.}
$$
These differ from the <angular frequencies> obtained by physically removing the coupling spring. Ordinary <frequencies> in hertz are each corresponding $\omega/(2\pi)$.

Finally, for $k_1>k_2$ and $\kappa>0$, put $D=\sqrt{(k_1-k_2)^2+4\kappa^2}>k_1-k_2$. Then
$$
\omega_-^2-\omega_2^2=\frac{k_1-k_2-D}{2m}<0,\qquad
\omega_+^2-\omega_1^2=\frac{D-(k_1-k_2)}{2m}>0.
$$
Since $\omega_1>\omega_2$, taking positive square roots proves
$$
\boxed{\omega_-<\omega_2<\omega_1<\omega_+.}
$$
At $\kappa=0$, the outer inequalities become equalities.

Let $S$ be a compact piecewise smooth <oriented surface>, $\mathbf n$ its chosen unit <normal vector>, and $\partial S$ its entire oriented boundary. Let $\mathbf A$ be a continuously differentiable <vector field> on a neighborhood of $S$. <Stokes theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf A)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf r.}
$$
Here $\nabla\times\mathbf A$ is the <curl>, $dS$ is the <surface area element>, and $d\mathbf r$ is the directed <line integral> element. The boundary follows the <right-hand rule> relative to $\mathbf n$; equivalently the surface lies to the left when walking along its boundary with one's head toward $\mathbf n$.

The topological requirement is <orientability>, together with the regularity and boundary assumptions above. **Simple connectivity is unnecessary.** An annulus or cylindrical side is allowed, provided every boundary component is included with its induced direction. For a concrete counterexample to a disk-only restriction, take the planar annulus $1\le x^2+y^2\le4$ and $\mathbf A=(-y/2,x/2,0)$. The <curl> is $\mathbf e_z$, the <surface integral> is $3\pi$, and the outer counterclockwise and inner clockwise <line integrals> sum to $4\pi-\pi=3\pi$.

For $S$ in the $xy$ <plane> with upward <normal vector> and $\mathbf A=(P,Q,0)$, its <curl> has vertical component $Q_x-P_y$. Thus <Stokes theorem> becomes <Green's theorem>:
$$
\boxed{\oint_{\partial D}(P\,dx+Q\,dy)=\iint_D\left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)dx\,dy.}
$$
The outer boundary is counterclockwise and any inner boundaries are clockwise.

The <position vector> field is a <gradient field>:
$$
\mathbf r=(x,y,z)=\nabla\left(\frac{x^2+y^2+z^2}{2}\right).
$$
Along any piecewise smooth <curve>, $\mathbf r\cdot d\mathbf r=d(|\mathbf r|^2/2)$. Therefore a closed <line integral> has identical endpoint values and
$$
\boxed{\oint_C\mathbf r\cdot d\mathbf r=\left[\frac{|\mathbf r|^2}{2}\right]_{\mathrm{start}}^{\mathrm{end}}=0.}
$$
For a loop bounding an admissible <oriented surface>, <Stokes theorem> gives the same result because $\nabla\times\mathbf r=\mathbf0$. The <gradient field> proof also applies directly to arbitrary piecewise smooth closed loops without choosing a spanning surface.

To apply <Stokes theorem> to a <flux> rather than a <curl> integral, first find a <vector potential>:

\mathbf A=(0,0,xyz^2),\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf A=(xz^2,-yz^2,0)=\mathbf F.

This <vector potential> is smooth everywhere. On either horizontal rim, $d\mathbf r$ is horizontal whereas $\mathbf A$ is vertical, giving $\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf r=0$. Let $\mathbf F=(F_x,F_y,F_z)$ be continuously differentiable near $P$. Take a small axis-aligned rectangular volume around $P$, with side lengths $\Delta x,\Delta y,\Delta z$ shrinking with bounded aspect ratios. The outward <flux> through its two $x$ faces is, to leading order,
$$
\bigl[F_x(x+\Delta x/2,y,z)-F_x(x-\Delta x/2,y,z)\bigr]\Delta y\Delta z
=\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x}(P)\,\Delta x\Delta y\Delta z+o(\Delta V),
$$
where $\Delta V=\Delta x\Delta y\Delta z$. The analogous face pairs give $\partial F_y/\partial y$ and $\partial F_z/\partial z$. Dividing total <flux> by $\Delta V$ and taking the limit gives
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial F_z}{\partial z}.}
$$
The <divergence> is thus the local outward <flux> per unit volume; the symbols on the right are Cartesian <partial derivatives>.

For a bounded volume $V$ with piecewise smooth closed boundary $S$, subdivide into small cells. Each internal face occurs twice with opposite <normal vectors>, so its <flux> cancels. The remaining sum is the exterior <surface integral>; the cell sums of $(\nabla\cdot\mathbf F)\Delta V$ converge to the <volume integral>. Assuming continuous first <partial derivatives> on a neighborhood of the closure of $V$, this proves the <divergence theorem>:
$$
\boxed{\oiint_S\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=\iiint_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV.}
$$
The unit <normal vector> $\mathbf n$ points out of $V$. Multiple boundary components are allowed; at a cavity wall it points into the cavity.

The two calculations agree exactly with the <divergence theorem>. The expression requested is a scalar <flux> of a <vector field>, namely the <dot product> with the directed <surface area element>.

<Wave dispersion> means that different <frequencies> propagate with different <phase velocities>, so a <wave packet> generally changes shape. Equivalently the <dispersion relation> is not proportional to $k$ over the relevant band. Three natural examples are the colour separation in a <rainbow>, where the refractive index of water depends on <wavelength>; deep-water <surface gravity waves>, for which $\omega^2=gk$ and long waves have larger <phase velocity>; and <capillary waves>, whose short-wave limit is $\omega^2=(\gamma/\rho)k^3$ and whose shorter waves have larger <phase velocity>. Here $g$ is <gravitational acceleration>, $\gamma$ is <surface tension>, and $\rho$ is fluid <mass density>, with positive $k$ and negligible viscosity.

A distinct application is an <optical prism> <spectrometer>: the wavelength-dependent <refractive index> produces different <refraction> angles, separating spectral components for measurement. This exploits <wave dispersion> deliberately.

Take constant complex <amplitude> $A$ and real <wavenumber> $k$ and <angular frequency> $\omega$. Substitution of the <complex exponential> gives
$$
y_t=-i\omega y,\qquad y_{xx}=-k^2y,\qquad
im(-i\omega)y=k^2y.

Thus every nonzero <plane wave> of the given form solves the equation precisely when
$$
\boxed{m\omega=k^2.}
$$
The <wavenumber> specifies spatial <wave phase> change per unit length, with <wavelength> $\lambda=2\pi/|k|$. The <angular frequency> specifies temporal <wave phase> change per unit time, with period $2\pi/|\omega|$ and ordinary <frequency> $|\omega|/(2\pi)$. The constant $m$ in this equation has dimensions time divided by length squared; without an additional dimensional coefficient, it is not an ordinary physical <mass>.

For propagating oscillatory solutions assume real $m\ne0$. If $m=0$, the equation becomes $y_{xx}=0$ and only $k=0$ among these spatial <plane waves> is possible; the usual <dispersion relation> branch and velocity discussion would degenerate.

= iii
{parent=11}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

A fixed <wave phase> satisfies $kx-\omega t=\text{constant}$, so the signed <phase velocity> is $v_p=\omega/k$. The <group velocity> $v_g=d\omega/dk$ gives the leading translation of the <wave envelope> of a narrow-band <wave packet> on a differentiable <dispersion relation> branch.

Here $v_p=k/m$. For the conventional positive branch $m>0$, $\omega>0$ and $k>0$,
$$
\boxed{v_p=\sqrt{\omega/m}.}
$$
On the opposite propagation branch $k<0$ it is $-\sqrt{\omega/m}$, so $\sqrt{\omega/m}$ is the <phase speed> for either sign of $k$. More generally, for real $m\ne0$ use $v_p=k/m$ with $k^2=m\omega$, allowing the sign of $\omega$ to follow that of $m$. The $k=0$ constant solution has no distinguished <phase velocity>.

= iv
{parent=11}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

For equal <complex amplitudes>, the <superposition principle> and the <complex exponential> formula for the cosine give the exact identity
$$
\begin{aligned}
y&=Ae^{i[(k_0+\delta k)x-(\omega_0+\delta\omega)t]}
+Ae^{i[(k_0-\delta k)x-(\omega_0-\delta\omega)t]}\\
&=\boxed{2A\cos(\delta k\,x-\delta\omega\,t)\,e^{i(k_0x-\omega_0t)}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The cosine is a real periodic modulation of the rapidly varying carrier. It varies slowly when $|\delta k|\ll|k_0|$ and $|\delta\omega|\ll|\omega_0|$. Its signed <wave envelope> travels with velocity $\delta\omega/\delta k$; its magnitude gives the visible <amplitude> envelope.

Equal magnitudes need not mean equal initial <wave phases>. If the two <complex amplitudes> are $ae^{i\phi_+}$ and $ae^{i\phi_-}$, write $\bar\phi=(\phi_++\phi_-)/2$ and $\delta\phi=(\phi_+-\phi_-)/2$. The same result becomes $2ae^{i\bar\phi}\cos(\delta kx-\delta\omega t+\delta\phi)e^{i(k_0x-\omega_0t)}$, still with a real slowly varying modulation.

= v
{parent=11}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=v}

Apply the <dispersion relation> separately to the two components and subtract:
$$
m(\omega_0+\delta\omega)=(k_0+\delta k)^2,\quad
m(\omega_0-\delta\omega)=(k_0-\delta k)^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{\delta\omega}{\delta k}=\frac{2k_0}{m}.
$$
In the narrow-band limit this is the <group velocity>, since $\omega(k)=k^2/m$ gives
$$
\boxed{v_g=\frac{d\omega}{dk}=\frac{2k}{m}=2v_p.}
$$
Equivalently on the positive branch $k=\sqrt{m\omega}$, $dk/d\omega=m/(2k)$ and its reciprocal is $2k/m$. The factor of two applies to signed velocities on either nonzero propagation branch.

There is a small but useful finite-band qualification. Adding the two component relations gives $m\omega_0=k_0^2+(\delta k)^2$. Thus the carrier formed by their exact midpoints has <phase velocity> $\omega_0/k_0=k_0/m+(\delta k)^2/(mk_0)$, and its finite-band envelope-to-carrier velocity ratio is
$$
\frac{\delta\omega/\delta k}{\omega_0/k_0}
=\frac{2k_0^2}{k_0^2+(\delta k)^2}.
$$
The exact derivative relation $v_g=2v_p$ refers to a single central point on the <dispersion relation>; the two-tone midpoint ratio approaches two as $\delta k/k_0\to0$.

{parent=11}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=vi}

The result depends on the component <amplitudes> and <wave phases>; adding many <frequencies> does not by itself guarantee one localized <wave packet>. For a definite sketch, take many equally spaced <wavenumbers> $k_j=k_0+j\Delta k$ in the same fixed band, with equal, aligned <complex amplitudes> $B/N$. Linearize the <dispersion relation> as $\omega(k_j)\simeq\omega_c+v_g(k_j-k_0)$, where $\omega_c=k_0^2/m$. Then with $\xi=x-v_gt$ the <wave envelope> is
$$
\frac BN\sum_{j=-(N-1)/2}^{(N-1)/2}e^{ij\Delta k\xi}
=\frac BN\frac{\sin(N\Delta k\xi/2)}{\sin(\Delta k\xi/2)}
$$
for odd $N$. This is a normalized <Dirichlet kernel>: <constructive interference> occurs near $\xi=0$, while most other positions undergo partial cancellation. At finite $N$ there are repeated groups separated by $2\pi/\Delta k$.

As $N$ grows with the half-bandwidth $K$ fixed, $\Delta k$ decreases and the repetition spacing grows. The continuous flat-band limit is
$$
\boxed{y(x,t)\simeq Be^{i(k_0x-\omega_ct)}\frac{\sin(K\xi)}{K\xi},\qquad \xi=x-v_gt.}
$$
Thus a <sinc function> envelope has a central group between its first zeros at $\xi=\pm\pi/K$, smaller side lobes and rapidly oscillating crests. At fixed bandwidth the limiting central width is $2\pi/K$; it does not shrink indefinitely just because more components are added. Smoother spectral weights can reduce the side lobes, and random <wave phases> would produce a different shape.

For the exact quadratic <dispersion relation>,
$$
y(x,t)=\frac{B}{2K}e^{i(k_0x-\omega_ct)}\int_{-K}^K e^{i[q(x-v_gt)-q^2t/m]}\,dq.
$$
The additional quadratic <wave phase> makes the <wave packet> spread and change shape. The translating <sinc function> approximation is valid while $|t|K^2/|m|\ll1$; the preceding figure is the exact $t=0$ comparison, where that approximation introduces no error.
