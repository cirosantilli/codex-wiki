= Solution
{parent=i}

The real <logarithm> restricts the approach to $x\to0^+$. The <Taylor expansion> $\sin x-x=-x^3/6+O(x^5)$ gives
$$
\frac1x+\frac{\ln x}{\sin x-x}
=\frac{-6\ln x}{x^3}\bigl(1+O(x^2)\bigr)+\frac1x.
$$
The positive first term dominates, since $x^2/|\ln x|\to0$. Thus the requested extended <limit> is $\boxed{+\infty}$. There is no two-sided real <limit> across zero because the real <logarithm> is undefined for $x<0$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For $x>2$, write
$$
\frac1x+\frac{\ln x}{\sin x-x}
=\frac{1-\ln x/(1-\sin x/x)}x.
$$
Since $|\sin x|\le1$, the denominator factor tends to one, and $\ln x/x\to0$. Therefore the <limit> is $\boxed{0}$. The function is eventually negative, so it approaches zero from below. Oscillation of $\sin x$ does not obstruct this <limit>, because it is bounded while $x$ grows.

Use <logarithmic differentiation> on the positive function: $\ln f=x^{-1}\ln(x^2+2)$. The <product rule> and <chain rule> give
$$
\boxed{f'(x)=(x^2+2)^{1/x}\left[\frac{2}{x^2+2}-\frac{\ln(x^2+2)}{x^2}\right],\qquad x\ne0.}
$$
The positive base permits this real definition on both components of the indicated domain. There is no need to treat a variable exponent as a constant.

= Solution
{parent=3}

For an <exact differential> $P\,dx+Q\,dy$, equality of mixed <partial derivatives> requires $P_y=Q_x$. The matching pair is $P=f_1$, $Q=f_3$, since
$$
\partial_yf_1=\frac{\cos y}{x}-e^x\sin2y=\partial_xf_3.
$$
The other pairings do not give the same <partial derivatives> on an open region. Integrating $f_1$ with respect to $x$ gives $\ln x\sin y+e^x\cos^2y+C(y)$. Its $y$ derivative agrees with $f_3$ precisely when $C'(y)=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{f(x,y)=\ln x\sin y+e^x\cos^2y+C,\qquad x>0.}
$$
This also proves existence, rather than relying only on the necessary mixed-<partial derivative> test.

= Solution

Put $h=x-1/\sqrt2$ and $k=y-1/(2\sqrt2)$. At the expansion point the exponent is one and the <gradient> is $(\sqrt2e,\sqrt2e)$. The first-order <multivariable Taylor expansion> is
$$
\boxed{f=e+\sqrt2e(h+k)+O(h^2+k^2).}
$$
The <directional error of a first-order Taylor approximation> is determined by the omitted terms, not just the equal first <partial derivatives>. The <Hessian matrix> at the expansion point is $e\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\2&2\end{pmatrix}$, so
$$
f-e-\sqrt2e(h+k)=e(2h^2+2hk+k^2)+O\bigl((h^2+k^2)^{3/2}\bigr).
$$
For equal small steps along the coordinate axes, the leading error is $2eh^2$ along $x$ and $ek^2$ along $y$. **The error grows faster along the $x$ axis**, although the first-order change in the function is equal along the two axes.

= Solution
{parent=5}

Choose the normal pointing away from the origin, equivalently the outward normal of the first-octant tetrahedron. Parametrize the face by $\mathbf r=(x,y,2-x/2-y/2)$ on $D=\{x,y\ge0:x+y\le4\}$. Its oriented area element is
$$
\mathbf r_x\times\mathbf r_y\,dx\,dy=(1/2,1/2,1)\,dx\,dy.
$$
Thus the <surface integral> is
$$
\iint_D\left(-\frac x2+\frac y2+2-\frac x2-\frac y2\right)dx\,dy
=\int_0^4(2-x)(4-x)\,dx
=\boxed{\frac{16}{3}}.
$$
The <divergence theorem> checks this result: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=1$, the tetrahedron's <volume> is $4\cdot4\cdot2/6=16/3$, and each coordinate-plane face has zero <flux>. The opposite orientation gives $-16/3$; a signed <flux> requires an orientation.

= Solution
{parent=6}

The triangular support can be written $0\le x\le\pi/2$, $\pi/2-x\le y\le\pi/2$. The density is nonnegative there, and the <normalization of a probability distribution> requires
$$
1=A\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin x(1-\cos x)\,dx=\frac A2.
$$
Hence $\boxed{A=2}$. Multiplying the <joint probability density> by the required function cancels $\sin x$ on the interior. Its <expectation> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E\left[\frac1{\sin X}\right]=2\int_0^{\pi/2}(1-\cos x)\,dx=\pi-2.}
$$
The singularity at $x=0$ lies at a support vertex of zero probability; the displayed <integral> establishes integrability. Assigning any value to the random function at that single vertex does not change the <expectation>.

= Solution
{parent=7}

The two planes bound $z-1\le y\le1-z$, and the elliptical cross-section has $-1\le z\le1$. Under $x=2r\cos\theta$, $z=r\sin\theta$, the absolute <Jacobian determinant> for $(x,z)$ is $2r$. Therefore
$$
V=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1\int_{r\sin\theta-1}^{1-r\sin\theta}2r\,dy\,dr\,d\theta
=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 4r(1-r\sin\theta)\,dr\,d\theta
=\boxed{4\pi}.
$$
As a check, the cross-sectional <ellipse> has area $2\pi$, while the part of the thickness proportional to $z$ integrates to zero by reflection. The mean thickness is therefore two.

= Solution

For positive <wavenumber>, the condition gives $\omega=Ck^{-n/2}$ on the positive-<frequency> branch. The <group velocity> and <phase velocity> are consequently related by
$$
v_g=\frac{d\omega}{dk}=-\frac n2\frac\omega k=-\frac n2v_p.
$$
The required positive ratio $v_g/v_p=2$ gives $\boxed{n=-4}$. Isotropy makes these <velocities> parallel to the wave vector when this coefficient is positive. Identifying <group velocity> with <energy> transport assumes the usual lossless narrowband <wave packet> regime.

= Solution

Since $\mathbf a$ is constant, direct differentiation gives $(\mathbf r\cdot\nabla)\mathbf a=0$, $(\mathbf a\cdot\nabla)\mathbf r=\mathbf a$, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf r=3$ and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf a=0$. The stated right-hand side is therefore $2\mathbf a$. Independently, <index notation> gives
$$
[\nabla\times(\mathbf a\times\mathbf r)]_i
=\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{klm}\partial_j(a_lx_m)=2a_i.
$$
Thus $\boxed{\nabla\times(\mathbf a\times\mathbf r)=2\mathbf a}$. A <conservative vector field> has zero <curl>, so the field is **not conservative unless $\mathbf a=0$**. For a closed planar circuit, its <circulation> is $2\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf A$ by <Stokes theorem>; the zero-vector exception is conservative with a constant potential.

= Solution
{parent=i}

For a sufficiently smooth vector field on an oriented surface $S$ with compatible boundary orientation, <Stokes theorem> states
$$
\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS.
$$
Apply it to $\mathbf F=(P,Q,0)$ in the plane. The closed <line integral> is the area <integral> of $Q_x-P_y$. Thus $P_y=Q_x$ makes it vanish whenever a spanning surface lies within the smooth domain. In a simply connected domain this implies <path independence>: join one path from $A$ to $B$ to the reverse of another, and their difference is a zero closed <line integral>.

The domain qualification is essential. On the punctured plane, $P=-y/(x^2+y^2)$, $Q=x/(x^2+y^2)$ satisfy $P_y=Q_x$, but the <line integral> around the unit circle is $2\pi$. A disk spanning that circle passes through the singularity. Hence the printed implication requires an appropriate domain assumption; zero <curl> alone is not a global <path independence> theorem.

{parent=b}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Let $F'=f$ and $G'=g$ on the relevant intervals. The <chain rule> gives
$$
d\{F(xy)+G(y/x)\}
=\left[yf(xy)-\frac y{x^2}g(y/x)\right]dx
+\left[xf(xy)+\frac1xg(y/x)\right]dy.
$$
Thus the one-form is explicitly an <exact differential>. Fixing its potential to vanish at $(1,1)$ determines it uniquely on the connected domain:
$$
\boxed{\phi(x,y)=\int_1^{xy}f(s)\,ds+\int_1^{y/x}g(t)\,dt.}
$$
Continuity of $f,g$ on the integration ranges suffices. For arbitrary smooth data defined throughout $x>0$, that half-plane also supplies the simply connected domain in the previous argument.

For the specialization $f(s)=s^{-2}$, the potential based at $(1,1)$ is defined in the positive quadrant; it cannot be continued through $xy=0$ using this formula. Taking $a,b>0$ keeps both endpoints in that component. Their ratios $y/x$ are both $b/a$, so the $G$ term cancels and
$$
\boxed{\phi(ab,b^2)-\phi(a,b)=\int_{ab}^{ab^3}s^{-2}\,ds
=\frac{b^2-1}{ab^3}.}
$$
No condition on $g$ beyond existence on the traversed ratios enters this difference.

Write $A(s)=s(s^2-4)$, so $z=A(x)A(y)$ and
$$
z_x=(3x^2-4)y(y^2-4),\qquad z_y=(3y^2-4)x(x^2-4).
$$
Solving these simultaneously gives **thirteen stationary points**: the nine grid points with $x,y\in\{-2,0,2\}$ and the four points $x,y\in\{-2/\sqrt3,2/\sqrt3\}$. There are no mixed extra cases, because $A$ and $A'$ have no common root.

At a grid point the <Hessian matrix> has zero diagonal and off-diagonal entry $A'(x)A'(y)\ne0$, so its <determinant> is negative: all nine are <saddle points>. At the other four points the <Hessian matrix> is $-16xy\,I$. Thus the equal-sign points are local maxima with value $256/27$, and the opposite-sign points are local minima with value $-256/27$.

In the requested nonnegative quadrant, the stationary points are the four boundary <saddle points> $(0,0),(0,2),(2,0),(2,2)$ and the interior local maximum $(2/\sqrt3,2/\sqrt3)$. The zero contours are $x=0,2$ and $y=0,2$. The <gradient> is perpendicular to regular contours and points toward increasing $z$. A boundary point's classification above refers to the function on the whole plane; its behavior restricted to the quadrant can be one-sided. The interior local maximum is not a global maximum on the unbounded quadrant, since $z\to+\infty$ along $x=y\to\infty$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <divergence> of $\mathbf F=\nabla z$ is the <Laplacian>
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=6xy(x^2+y^2-8).
$$
Its area <integral> over the square is
$$
\int_0^2\int_0^2 6xy(x^2+y^2-8)\,dx\,dy
=6(8+8-32)=\boxed{-96}.
$$
For the boundary <flux>, $\int_0^2A(s)\,ds=-4$. On $x=0$, $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n=4A(y)$, giving $-16$; on $x=2$ it is $8A(y)$, giving $-32$. The corresponding horizontal sides give $-16$ and $-32$. The total is again $-96$, verifying the <divergence theorem> with outward normals. With a counterclockwise boundary, this is the same orientation as $M\,dy-N\,dx$.

= Solution
{parent=a}

First solve the supplied equations as a linear system, with $\omega_0^2=g/l$ and $\alpha>0$. A <normal mode> $\mathbf x=\mathbf a e^{i\omega t}$ obeys
$$
\frac{\omega^2}{\omega_0^2}\mathbf a=D\mathbf a,\qquad
D=\begin{pmatrix}1+2\alpha&-\alpha\\-\alpha&\alpha\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its <characteristic polynomial> is $\lambda^2-(1+3\alpha)\lambda+\alpha(1+\alpha)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\omega_\pm^2=\frac g{2l}\left[1+3\alpha\pm\sqrt{1+2\alpha+5\alpha^2}\right].}
$$
Both are positive, since the <trace> and <determinant> of $D$ are positive.

There is a physical discrepancy in the source. The <horizontal-coordinate mass matrix of an unequal-mass double pendulum> follows from the stated horizontal <displacements>, which give
$$
T_2=\frac m2\dot x_1^2+\frac{\alpha m}{2}\dot x_2^2,\qquad
V_2=\frac{mg}{2l}\left[x_1^2+\alpha x_1^2+\alpha(x_2-x_1)^2\right].
$$
The <Euler-Lagrange equations> reproduce the supplied upper equation, but give $\ddot x_2=-(g/l)(x_2-x_1)$, without its extra $\alpha$. The true <normal modes of a double pendulum> consequently have $\omega_\pm^2=(g/l)[1+\alpha\pm\sqrt{\alpha(1+\alpha)}]$. The boxed answer and the remaining parts below concern the explicitly supplied linear system. The two models agree at $\alpha=1$, but not for a general mass ratio.

{parent=11}

= Solution
{parent=b}

At $\alpha=1$, the supplied and physical systems agree. Their squared <angular frequencies> are $(g/l)(2\pm\sqrt2)$. The upper row gives $a_2/a_1=3-\lambda$, so
$$
\boxed{\frac{a_2}{a_1}=1+\sqrt2\ \text{for the slower mode},\qquad
\frac{a_2}{a_1}=1-\sqrt2\ \text{for the faster mode}.}
$$
In the slower <normal mode>, both masses move horizontally in phase and the lower has the larger <amplitude>. In the faster <normal mode>, the lower mass moves in the opposite direction, with magnitude $(\sqrt2-1)|a_1|$. These are horizontal <displacement> ratios. The rod angles instead satisfy $\theta_2/\theta_1=\pm\sqrt2$, because $x_1=l\theta_1$, $x_2=l(\theta_1+\theta_2)$; confusing these ratios changes the sketch.

For the supplied system, the small-$\alpha$ <Taylor expansion> gives $\sqrt{1+2\alpha+5\alpha^2}=1+\alpha+O(\alpha^2)$, hence $\omega_-^2=\alpha g/l+O(\alpha^2g/l)$. Setting $\omega_-=(1/4)\sqrt{g/l}$ at this order yields

The physical unequal-mass <double pendulum> has a different small-mass limit, so the source's requested tuning does not follow for that model.

For the supplied equations, $\lambda_+\to1$ and $a_2/a_1=\alpha/(\alpha-\lambda_+)\sim-\alpha$. Thus the higher-<frequency> <normal mode> becomes an upper-mass <oscillation> at $\sqrt{g/l}$ with a vanishing, opposite-sign lower horizontal <displacement>. = Solution
{parent=a}

Use <separation of variables>, $y=X(x)T(t)$. A nontrivial separated solution satisfies $X''/X=T''/(c^2T)=-k^2$. Fixed endpoints force $X(0)=X(L)=0$, so $X=\sin(n\pi x/L)$, $n=1,2,\ldots$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\omega_n=\frac{n\pi c}{L},\qquad f_n=\frac{nc}{2L}.}
$$
Here $c>0$ is the wave <speed>. Zero or negative $k^2$ gives no nonzero fixed-end spatial solution. Each time coefficient is a <simple harmonic oscillator>.

= b
{parent=12}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Expand the initial <velocity> in the <Fourier sine basis>, $V(x)=\sum_{n\ge1}V_n\sin(n\pi x/L)$, where $V_n=(2/L)\int_0^LV(x)\sin(n\pi x/L)\,dx$. Zero initial <displacement> removes the cosine-in-time coefficients. Thus
$$
\boxed{y(x,t)=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{V_n}{\omega_n}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\sin(\omega_nt).}
$$
The <trigonometric identity> $2\sin a\sin b=\cos(a-b)-\cos(a+b)$ rewrites each summand as
$$
\frac{V_n}{2\omega_n}\left[\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}-\omega_nt\right)-\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}+\omega_nt\right)\right].
$$
It is therefore a superposition of <travelling waves> of <speeds> $+c$ and $-c$, whose interference enforces the fixed endpoints. For general square-integrable initial <velocity>, the series is interpreted in the standard finite-energy sense.

= Solution
{parent=c}

The product-to-sum identity gives $V_1=V_0$, $V_2=V_0/2$ and $V_3=2V_0/3$, with all other modal coefficients zero. If $\mu$ is the string's <linear mass density>, the initial <potential energy> vanishes and <orthogonality> gives the <modal energy of a string>
$$
E_n=\frac\mu2\int_0^LV_n^2\sin^2\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx=\frac{\mu L}{4}V_n^2.
$$
Consequently $\boxed{E_1:E_2:E_3=36:9:16}$ for $V_0\ne0$. **The fundamental mode carries the most energy**, with fraction $36/61$. Comparing displacement coefficients without their <frequency> factors would give the wrong energy test.

Substitute the three nonzero initial-<velocity> coefficients into the general <Fourier sine series>, with $\omega_1=\pi c/L$:
$$
\boxed{y(x,t)=\frac{V_0}{\omega_1}\left[
\sin\frac{\pi x}{L}\sin\omega_1t+
\frac14\sin\frac{2\pi x}{L}\sin2\omega_1t+
\frac29\sin\frac{3\pi x}{L}\sin3\omega_1t\right].}
$$
Every term satisfies the <wave equation> and the endpoint conditions. Differentiating in time at zero recovers the three coefficients $V_0,V_0/2,2V_0/3$, providing a direct initial-data check.

{parent=12}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=e}

Assume $V_0\ne0$. <Linear independence> of the <Fourier sine basis> makes a flat string equivalent to vanishing of all three temporal coefficients. The fundamental requires $\sin(\omega_1t)=0$, which automatically zeros the other two. Thus the earliest positive return is
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm flat}=\frac{\pi}{\omega_1}=\frac Lc.}
$$
The <velocity> need not have returned to its initial value at that time; full recurrence of the displacement-and-velocity data occurs at $2L/c$.

A nonzero pure fundamental profile would require $\sin2\omega_1t=\sin3\omega_1t=0$. The common zeros have $\omega_1t$ an integer multiple of $\pi$, so the fundamental vanishes too. A pure second or third harmonic would already require the fundamental to vanish, again making the whole string flat. **The string never has a nonzero pure sine profile.** If $V_0=0$, it remains identically zero and there is no earliest positive isolated return.
