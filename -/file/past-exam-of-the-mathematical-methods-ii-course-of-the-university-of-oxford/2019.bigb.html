The successive <derivatives> are $f^{(n)}(x)=(-6)^ne^{-6x}$, so $f^{(n)}(-4)=(-6)^ne^{24}$. Substitution in the <Taylor series> formula about $a=-4$ gives
$$
\boxed{e^{-6x}=e^{24}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-6)^n(x+4)^n}{n!}
=e^{24}\left[1-6(x+4)+18(x+4)^2-36(x+4)^3+\cdots\right].}
$$
The <ratio test> shows convergence for every finite real or complex $x$: the absolute ratio of successive terms is $6|x+4|/(n+1)\to0$. This is also the usual <exponential function> series applied to $e^{24}e^{-6(x+4)}$, proving that the series equals the stated function everywhere.

The <solid angle> is the intercepted <surface area> on a sphere of radius $R$, divided by $R^2$. Take the cone axis as the polar axis of <spherical coordinates>, and call its half-opening <angle> $\theta$. With polar integration variable $\vartheta$,
$$
\Omega=\frac1{R^2}\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\theta R^2\sin\vartheta\,d\vartheta\,d\phi
=\boxed{2\pi(1-\cos\theta).}
$$
For small $\theta$ measured in radians, the <Taylor series> of the <cosine> gives
$$
\boxed{\Omega=\pi\theta^2-\frac{\pi\theta^4}{12}+O(\theta^6)\simeq\pi\theta^2.}
$$
This is the <solid angle of a circular cone>, measured in <steradians>.

The supplied angular diameter is twice the half-opening <angle>, so
$$
\theta=\frac{0.533}{2}\frac\pi{180}=0.00465130246\ldots\ \mathrm{rad}.
$$
Hence either apparent disc subtends
$$
\boxed{\Omega\simeq6.797\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{sr},\qquad
\frac{\Omega}{4\pi}\simeq5.409\times10^{-6}.}
$$
The fraction is about $0.0005409\%$ of the entire celestial sphere. Using the angular diameter directly in $\pi\theta^2$ would give four times the correct small-angle answer.

The real <logarithm> restricts the domain to $x>0$. Differentiating gives
$$
f'(x)=2x\ln(3x)+x=x\big[2\ln(3x)+1\big].
$$
Since $x$ cannot vanish in the domain, a <stationary point> requires $\ln(3x)=-1/2$. The <logarithm> is strictly increasing, so this equation has exactly one solution:
$$
\boxed{x_* =\frac{e^{-1/2}}3=\frac1{3\sqrt e},\qquad
f(x_*)=-1-\frac1{18e}.}
$$
The <second derivative> is $f''(x)=2\ln(3x)+3$, giving $f''(x_*)=2>0$. In fact $f'$ is negative below $x_*$ and positive above it, so the point is the unique global <minimum> on $x>0$. The boundary value $x=0$ is not an additional <stationary point>, since the original function is not defined there.

For a continuous <random variable> with <probability density function> $f_X$, its <cumulative distribution function> is
$$
F_X(x)=\Pr(X\leq x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}f_X(u)\,du.
$$
Consequently $F_X'=f_X$ wherever the density is continuous, and almost everywhere for an absolutely continuous distribution.

Here $Y=X^2\geq0$. Thus $F_Y(y)=0$ for $y<0$. For $y\geq0$, the event $X^2\leq y$ is $-\sqrt y\leq X\leq\sqrt y$. Symmetry of the <Laplace distribution> gives
$$
F_Y(y)=\int_{-\sqrt y}^{\sqrt y}\frac12e^{-|x|}\,dx
=\int_0^{\sqrt y}e^{-x}\,dx=1-e^{-\sqrt y}.
$$
Therefore the <square of a standard Laplace variable> has <cumulative distribution function>
$$
\boxed{F_Y(y)=\begin{cases}0,&y<0,\\1-e^{-\sqrt y},&y\geq0.\end{cases}}
$$
It is continuous at zero, so there is no point mass there. As a check, its <probability density function> for $y>0$ is $e^{-\sqrt y}/(2\sqrt y)$, whose integral becomes $\int_0^\infty e^{-u}\,du=1$ after setting $u=\sqrt y$.

Work in the small-slope model of a <transverse wave> on a taut string. Let $T$ be the constant <tension>, $\rho$ the <linear mass density>, and take a right-moving sinusoidal <displacement> $y=A\cos(\kappa x-\omega t)$, with <wave number> $\kappa=\omega/v$. The <energy flux of a transverse string wave> through a cut is the transverse <force> times transverse <velocity>:
$$
P(x,t)=-T\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}
=T\kappa\omega A^2\sin^2(\kappa x-\omega t).
$$
A cycle average gives $\langle\sin^2\rangle=1/2$. Using $T=\rho v^2$, obtained from the string <wave equation>, yields
$$
\boxed{\overline P=\frac12T\kappa\omega A^2=\frac12\rho v\omega^2A^2.}
$$
The sign in the flux formula makes the <power> positive for propagation in the positive $x$ direction.

The <energy> per unit equilibrium length is the sum of the kinetic and elastic terms:
$$
\mathcal E=\frac12\rho y_t^2+\frac12T y_x^2,
\qquad \langle\mathcal E\rangle=\frac12\rho\omega^2A^2.
$$
For a segment of <mass> $M=\rho\ell$ containing many <wavelengths>, the spatial average agrees with the cycle average up to a small endpoint correction. Thus $E\simeq\tfrac12M\omega^2A^2$. Convert the given quantities to SI units: $M=0.080\ \mathrm{kg}$, $A=1.6\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm m$, and $\omega=2\pi(120)\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}$. Then
$$
\boxed{E\simeq\frac12(0.080)(240\pi)^2(1.6\times10^{-4})^2
=5.82\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm J.}
$$
The many-wavelength condition explains why no phase of the finite segment is needed. The result is exact as a cycle average, or for a segment containing an integer number of <wavelengths>.

Let the <angular velocity> vector be the spatially constant $\boldsymbol\omega$. Choose an origin on the fixed rotation axis. The <velocity field> of a <rigid body> is $v=\boldsymbol\omega\times r$. In components,
$$
v=(\omega_yz-\omega_zy,\ \omega_zx-\omega_xz,\ \omega_xy-\omega_yx).
$$
Taking the <curl> gives
$$
\boxed{\nabla\times v=(2\omega_x,2\omega_y,2\omega_z)=2\boldsymbol\omega.}
$$
An added spatially uniform translational <velocity> has zero <curl>, so it does not affect this relation. Equivalently, the <vorticity> of rigid rotation is twice the <angular velocity>; the local rotational part of a general smooth flow has <angular velocity> $\tfrac12\nabla\times v$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

For an oriented piecewise smooth <surface> $S$ with boundary $\partial S$, and a <vector field> $F$ continuously differentiable on a neighborhood of $S$, <Stokes theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\int_S(\nabla\times F)\cdot n\,dS=\oint_{\partial S}F\cdot dr.}
$$
Here $n$ is the chosen unit <normal vector>, $dS$ is the unsigned <surface area element>, and $dr$ is the tangent displacement along the boundary. The <right-hand rule> pairs the boundary orientation with $n$. If there are several boundary components, the <line integral> includes all of them with their induced orientations.

A <simply connected> <surface> is connected and every closed loop on it can be continuously contracted to a point while remaining on the <surface>. A disc is an example. An annulus is not <simply connected>: a loop winding once around its hole cannot contract within it.

**Stokes' theorem is valid for both types of surface**, under the smoothness and orientation hypotheses above. Simple connectivity is not a requirement of the theorem. For a concrete annular example, take $F=(-y/2,x/2,0)$ and $a\leq r\leq R$ in the horizontal plane. Its <curl> is $(0,0,1)$, so the left side is $\pi(R^2-a^2)$. The right side is the counterclockwise outer-circle integral $\pi R^2$ minus the counterclockwise inner-circle integral $\pi a^2$, giving exactly the same answer. Omitting the inner boundary would be an incorrect application of the theorem.

Write $r^2=x^2+y^2$ and work away from the $z$ axis, where $r>0$. The <vector field> is independent of $z$ and has zero vertical component, so only the vertical <curl> can be nonzero:
$$
\begin{aligned}
(\nabla\times F)_z
&=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big[x(x^2+y^2)^{-n}\big]
-\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\big[-y(x^2+y^2)^{-n}\big]\\
&=2r^{-2n}-2n(x^2+y^2)r^{-2n-2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\nabla\times F=2(1-n)r^{-2n}\,\hat z,\qquad r>0.}
$$
This <local spin in an azimuthal power-law flow> depends on the radial variation of the tangential speed, not merely on the sign of that speed.

In <cylindrical coordinates>, $F=r^{1-2n}\hat\theta$. The tangential coefficient is positive for every real $n$ and $r>0$, so the large-scale circulation is always counterclockwise when viewed from positive $z$.

By <8/c/i/solution>, the infinitesimal local <angular velocity> is $(1-n)r^{-2n}\hat z$. Its sign gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c}
n<1&\text{local spin in the same sense as circulation}\\
n=1&\text{zero local spin}\\
n>1&\text{local spin opposite to circulation}
\end{array}}
$$
“Zero local spin” does not mean zero <velocity>: at $n=1$ a marked small element is still carried around the axis while its infinitesimal rotational component is zero. Orbital motion around the axis and local <vorticity> are different measurements.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For $n=1$, the <azimuthal unit vector> is $\hat\theta=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,0)$, so
$$
\boxed{F=\frac1r\hat\theta.}
$$
On a counterclockwise circle $C_r$ at fixed height, $dr=r\hat\theta\,d\theta$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\oint_{C_r}F\cdot dr=\int_0^{2\pi}\frac1r\,r\,d\theta=2\pi,}
$$
independent of the positive radius. Clockwise orientation reverses the sign.

There is no contradiction with the zero <curl> found in <8/c/i/solution>. The <vector field> is undefined on the axis, so it is not continuously differentiable on a disc spanning $C_r$; the hypotheses of <Stokes theorem> fail for that disc. It is smooth on a planar annulus excluding the axis, where <Stokes theorem> correctly gives zero: the two boundary <line integrals> cancel, $2\pi-2\pi=0$.

The same check works for general $n$. A counterclockwise circle has circulation $\Gamma(r)=2\pi r^{2-2n}$. Integrating the <curl> over an annulus $a\leq r\leq R$ gives, for $n\ne1$,
$$
\int_a^R2(1-n)r^{-2n}\,2\pi r\,dr
=2\pi\big(R^{2-2n}-a^{2-2n}\big)=\Gamma(R)-\Gamma(a).
$$
For $n=1$, both sides are zero. Thus all three preceding results are consistent with <Stokes theorem>; the necessary distinctions are the regularity of the <vector field> and inclusion of every oriented boundary component.

= Solution

Assume small transverse slopes, a flexible string, constant <tension> $T$ and uniform <linear mass density> $\rho$. For an element of equilibrium length $dx$, the vertical <force> is the difference of the vertical tension components at its ends. To first order in slope,
$$
F_y=T\big[y_x(x+dx,t)-y_x(x,t)\big]=T y_{xx}\,dx.
$$
Its <mass> is $\rho\,dx$ and its transverse <acceleration> is $y_{tt}$. <Newton's second law> therefore gives $\rho\,dx\,y_{tt}=T\,dx\,y_{xx}$, or
$$
\boxed{y_{xx}=\frac1{c^2}y_{tt},\qquad c=\sqrt{\frac{T}{\rho}}.}
$$
This is the one-dimensional <wave equation> for small-amplitude <transverse waves>. Changes in string length and horizontal <acceleration> are second order in slope and are neglected in this linear approximation.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

At distance $x$ below the support, the cable must carry the weight of the remaining length $L-x$. Thus its equilibrium <tension> is $T(x)=\mu g(L-x)$, and the local <transverse wave> speed is
$$
\boxed{c(x)=\sqrt{\frac{T(x)}\mu}=\sqrt{g(L-x)},\qquad0\leq x<L.}
$$
The <linear mass density> cancels. More precisely the small-displacement equation with variable <tension> is $\mu y_{tt}=\partial_x[T(x)y_x]$; its local characteristic speed is the expression above, even though a first-spatial-derivative term is also present.

The <wave travel time on a hanging uniform cable> is obtained from $dt=dx/c(x)$:
$$
t_{\mathrm{down}}=\int_0^L\frac{dx}{\sqrt{g(L-x)}}
=\frac1{\sqrt g}\int_0^Lu^{-1/2}\,du
=\boxed{2\sqrt{\frac Lg}.}
$$
Although the local speed tends to zero at the free end, the endpoint singularity in the travel-time integral is integrable. This finite result belongs to the ideal flexible-cable model, with bending stiffness neglected.

= Solution
{parent=b}

A <dispersive> medium has frequency-dependent <phase velocity>, so different Fourier components generally propagate with different phases. A <dispersion relation> specifies $\omega$ as a function of the <wave number> $k$, or an equivalent relation between them. For a monochromatic factor $e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$, a point of fixed <phase> moves at
$$
\boxed{v_p=\frac\omega k.}
$$
For a narrow <wave packet> centered at $k_*$, expand $\omega(k)\simeq\omega(k_*)+(k-k_*)\omega'(k_*)$. The envelope then translates at the <group velocity>
$$
\boxed{v_g=\frac{d\omega}{dk}.}
$$
The <phase velocity> tracks crests of a monochromatic wave. The <group velocity> tracks a slowly varying envelope when the narrow-band approximation is valid; in the ideal lossless guide here it also equals the longitudinal energy-transport speed. It is not a universal formula for signal-front speed in every possible dispersive medium.

For the propagating branch of the supplied <electromagnetic waveguide> relation, take $\omega_0>0$, $k>0$ and $\omega=\sqrt{\omega_0^2+c^2k^2}$. Differentiation gives
$$
\boxed{v_p=\frac{\sqrt{\omega_0^2+c^2k^2}}k>c,\qquad
v_g=\frac{c^2k}{\sqrt{\omega_0^2+c^2k^2}}<c,\qquad v_pv_g=c^2.}
$$
The product alone would also allow $v_p=v_g=c$; the strictly positive cutoff is what makes the inequalities strict. At $k=0$ the phase-speed expression is undefined, and these formulas are interpreted through the propagating limit.

A moving monochromatic crest does not carry a new message. An information-bearing modulation is made from a range of frequencies; here its packet and energy move below $c$, and the high-frequency limit approaches $c$. Causal propagation of a newly switched-on disturbance is governed by the electromagnetic equations, so the superluminal <phase velocity> does not permit faster-than-light signalling. These are the <waveguide phase and group velocities> for a mode with nonzero cutoff, rather than a zero-cutoff mode with both velocities equal to $c$.

Use $s$ for <cylindrical radius>, to distinguish it from spherical radius $r$. In <cylindrical coordinates> $(s,\phi,z)$, the <azimuthal angle> $\phi$ is measured from positive $x$ in the horizontal plane:
$$
\boxed{x=s\cos\phi,\qquad y=s\sin\phi,\qquad z=z,\qquad s\geq0.}
$$
Here $0\leq\phi<2\pi$ and $z\in\mathbb R$.

In <spherical polar coordinates> $(r,\theta,\phi)$, take $\theta$ as the polar <angle> from positive $z$ and use the same <azimuthal angle> $\phi$:
$$
\boxed{x=r\sin\theta\cos\phi,\qquad y=r\sin\theta\sin\phi,\qquad z=r\cos\theta.}
$$
The ranges are $r\geq0$, $0\leq\theta\leq\pi$ and $0\leq\phi<2\pi$. The radial direction has horizontal projection of length $r\sin\theta$, which explains the extra factor in $x,y$. The azimuth is undefined on the polar axis, and both angles are nonunique at the origin; these are coordinate degeneracies rather than geometric singularities.

This is a <scalar surface integral>, not an oriented <flux>. Split the whole closed <surface> into the bottom disc, curved side and tilted top; shared edge curves have zero <surface area>.

On the bottom, $z=0$ and the integrand is $y$. Its integral over the symmetric disc $x^2+y^2\leq3$ vanishes by oddness:
$$
I_{\mathrm{bottom}}=0.
$$
On the top, $z=4-y$, so $y+z=4$. The graph has $z_x=0$, $z_y=-1$, giving $dS=\sqrt{1+z_x^2+z_y^2}\,dx\,dy=\sqrt2\,dx\,dy$. Hence
$$
I_{\mathrm{top}}=4\sqrt2\,(3\pi)=12\pi\sqrt2.
$$
For the side, let $R=\sqrt3$ and parametrize by $(R\cos\phi,R\sin\phi,z)$, with $0\leq z\leq4-R\sin\phi$. The <surface area element> is $R\,dz\,d\phi$. Thus
$$
\begin{aligned}
I_{\mathrm{side}}
&=R\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{4-R\sin\phi}(R\sin\phi+z)\,dz\,d\phi\\
&=R\int_0^{2\pi}\left[8-\frac{R^2}2\sin^2\phi\right]d\phi\\
&=16\pi R-\frac{\pi R^3}{2}=\frac{29\pi\sqrt3}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Adding all three pieces gives
$$
\boxed{\int_S(y+z)\,dS=\pi\left(12\sqrt2+\frac{29\sqrt3}{2}\right).}
$$
The tilted top remains above the bottom everywhere because $4-y\geq4-\sqrt3>0$. The calculation is an instance of a <scalar surface integral over a cylinder with one tilted cap>; reversing a surface orientation would not change any of its terms.

A <normal mode> is a motion of a linear oscillatory system in which every coordinate has the same <angular frequency> and a fixed relative amplitude and phase. For a conservative small-displacement system it has the form
$$
\boldsymbol x(t)=q\cos(\omega t+\phi),\qquad Kq=\omega^2Mq,
$$
where $q$ is the <mode shape>, $K$ the <stiffness matrix>, and $M$ the <mass matrix>. Superposing <normal modes> gives the general small-oscillation motion when their <eigenvectors> form a complete <basis>.

Two common examples are standing transverse oscillations of a string fixed at both ends, and oscillations of coupled masses and springs. The string's mode shapes are sinusoidal along its length; the coupled system's mode shapes are fixed patterns of particle displacements. The following parts determine those patterns for the three-mass system in the source figure.

= a
{parent=11}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $x_1,x_2,x_3$ be longitudinal <displacements> from equilibrium, with positive direction to the right. The fixed walls have zero displacement. The first mass feels forces $-kx_1$ and $k(x_2-x_1)$; the central mass feels $k(x_1-x_2)$ and $k(x_3-x_2)$; the last mass feels $k(x_2-x_3)$ and $-kx_3$. <Hooke's law> and <Newton's second law> therefore give
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
m\ddot x_1&=-2kx_1+kx_2,\\
m\ddot x_2&=kx_1-2kx_2+kx_3,\\
m\ddot x_3&=kx_2-2kx_3.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Equivalently,
$$
\boxed{m\ddot{\boldsymbol x}+kA\boldsymbol x=0,\qquad
A=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1&0\\-1&2&-1\\0&-1&2\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
All four springs contribute: the wall springs supply one of the two diagonal terms in the first and third rows. This is a fixed-end chain, not a freely ended chain with a zero-frequency translation mode.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Put $\boldsymbol x=q e^{i\omega t}$ in <11/a/solution>. The <normal mode> equation is $Aq=\lambda q$, with $\lambda=m\omega^2/k$. Its <characteristic polynomial> is
$$
\det(A-\lambda I)=(2-\lambda)\big[(2-\lambda)^2-2\big].
$$
The three <eigenvalues> are $2-\sqrt2$, $2$, and $2+\sqrt2$. Solving the corresponding linear equations yields
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{mode}&\omega&\text{mode shape }q\\\hline
1&\sqrt{(2-\sqrt2)k/m}&(1,\sqrt2,1)^T\\
2&\sqrt{2k/m}&(1,0,-1)^T\\
3&\sqrt{(2+\sqrt2)k/m}&(1,-\sqrt2,1)^T
\end{array}}
$$
Each <mode shape> may be multiplied by any nonzero scalar. Their squared <Euclidean norms> are $4,2,4$, respectively, so unit-normalized <eigenvectors> are $q_1/2,q_2/\sqrt2,q_3/2$. They are mutually orthogonal, as expected for the real symmetric <stiffness matrix> with equal <masses>.

The entries in the table are <angular frequencies>; the corresponding ordinary <frequencies> in cycles per unit time are $f_j=\omega_j/(2\pi)$. All three are positive because the fixed walls remove uniform translation as a free motion.

= Solution

To specify “initial motion” unambiguously, choose each mode to pass through equilibrium at $t=0$ with $\dot{\boldsymbol x}(0)$ proportional to its listed <mode shape>. The arrows in the figure then describe relative initial <velocities>:

* In mode 1, all three masses move in the same direction; the central mass has $\sqrt2$ times either outer <velocity>.
* In mode 2, the two outer masses move with equal and opposite <velocities>, while the central mass remains stationary.
* In mode 3, the outer masses move together while the central mass moves oppositely with $\sqrt2$ times their speed.

Reversing every arrow changes only the common <phase>. A quarter-cycle later these same ratios describe the extremal <displacements>, with zero instantaneous <velocity>.

\Image[2019-fixed-chain-mode-motions.png]
{title=Relative initial velocities of the three fixed-end normal modes, starting as the masses pass through equilibrium.}
{description=Arrow lengths follow the mode shapes. The antisymmetric mode has equal and opposite outer velocities and zero central velocity; its angular frequency is unchanged by any positive change in central mass.}

Now replace the central mass by $M$, leaving the outer <masses> equal to $m$. The antisymmetric <mode shape> $q=(1,0,-1)^T$ gives zero net force on the central mass, since $k x_1+k x_3=0$. Each outer mass still obeys $m\ddot x=-2kx$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\omega_2=\sqrt{\frac{2k}{m}}\text{ is unchanged for every }M>0.}
$$
This <middle-mass-independent mode of a fixed three-mass spring chain> is exact, not merely a large-$M$ approximation. The other two modes move the central mass and generally change. For example, their symmetric-sector frequencies satisfy
$$
(2k-m\omega^2)(2k-M\omega^2)-2k^2=0,
$$
which explicitly depends on $M$.
