== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The successive <derivatives> of cosine cycle through $\cos a,-\sin a,-\cos a,\sin a$. Its <Taylor series> about $a$ therefore separates into even and odd powers:
$$
\cos(a+x)=\cos a\left(1-\frac{x^2}{2!}+\frac{x^4}{4!}-\cdots\right)
-\sin a\left(x-\frac{x^3}{3!}+\frac{x^5}{5!}-\cdots\right).
$$
The two brackets are the <Maclaurin series> of cosine and sine, giving
$$
\boxed{\cos(a+x)=\cos a\cos x-\sin a\sin x.}
$$
Both series converge absolutely for every real $x$, so regrouping the even and odd terms is valid everywhere on the real axis.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <hyperbolic cotangent> is
$$
\coth x=\frac{e^x+e^{-x}}{e^x-e^{-x}}=\frac{e^{2x}+1}{e^{2x}-1},\qquad x\ne0.
$$
Expand the numerator and denominator using the exponential <Maclaurin series>:
$$
\coth x=\frac1x\frac{1+x^2/2+x^4/24+O(x^6)}{1+x^2/6+x^4/120+O(x^6)}
=\boxed{\frac1x+\frac x3-\frac{x^3}{45}+O(x^5).}
$$
This is a <Laurent series>, rather than an ordinary <Maclaurin series>: $\coth x$ has a simple <pole> at zero. The printed request should be understood as the expansion obtained from the exponential series, including this unavoidable $x^{-1}$ term.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

Invert the change of variables: $x=(v-u)/2$, $y=(v+u)/2$. The absolute <Jacobian determinant> is $1/2$, and the transformed triangle is $0\le v\le3$, $-v\le u\le v$. Hence
$$
I=\frac12\int_0^3\int_{-v}^{v}e^{u/v}\,du\,dv
=\frac12\int_0^3v(e-e^{-1})\,dv
=\boxed{\frac94(e-e^{-1})=\frac92\sinh1.}
$$
At the single vertex $v=0$, the printed integrand is undefined. This causes no integral divergence: throughout the remaining triangle $-1\le u/v\le1$, so the integrand is bounded between $e^{-1}$ and $e$. Giving the vertex any finite value, or taking a limiting integral with $v>0$, gives the same answer.

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The arrows point in the positive $x$ direction, with largest speed $v_0$ on $y=0$ and rapidly decreasing length away from that line. Since only $v_x$ is nonzero,
$$
\boxed{\nabla\times v=-\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial y}\,e_z
=2v_0y e^{-y^2}e_z.}
$$
The <vorticity> points towards positive $z$ above the centre line and negative $z$ below it. A small fluid element therefore has local <angular velocity> $(\nabla\times v)/2$: anticlockwise above and clockwise below when viewed from positive $z$. At $y=0$, its speed is maximal but its local <vorticity> is zero. Translation speed and local rotation are different properties.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Using $r=(x,y,z)$ and constant $a$, the <divergence> is
$$
\partial_i(\epsilon_{ijk}r_ja_k)=\epsilon_{ijk}\delta_{ij}a_k=0.
$$
For the <curl>, the <curl of a cross product> identity gives
$$
\nabla\times(r\times a)=r(\nabla\cdot a)-a(\nabla\cdot r)+(a\cdot\nabla)r-(r\cdot\nabla)a
=0-3a+a-0.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot(r\times a)=0,\qquad\nabla\times(r\times a)=-2a.}
$$
For a quick sign check, taking $a=e_z$ gives $r\times a=(y,-x,0)$, whose <curl> is $(0,0,-2)$.

== 4
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Take any differentiable curve $r(s)$ on the <level surface>. The <chain rule> gives
$$
0=\frac{d}{ds}f(r(s))=\nabla f\cdot r'(s).
$$
Its tangent vector $r'(s)$ can be any tangent direction at a regular point. Thus a nonzero <gradient> is orthogonal to the entire tangent plane, giving the <unit normal> $n=\pm\nabla f/|\nabla f|$. Nonvanishing is essential: at a <critical point>, the zero gradient alone does not determine a normal or guarantee a smooth <level surface>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The relevant projection is the disk $x^2+y^2\le9$. The <surface area of a graph> is $\iint\sqrt{1+z_x^2+z_y^2}\,dx\,dy$. Here $z_x=-2x$ and $z_y=-2y$, so <polar coordinates> give
$$
A=2\pi\int_0^3r\sqrt{1+4r^2}\,dr
=\frac\pi6\left[(1+4r^2)^{3/2}\right]_0^3
=\boxed{\frac\pi6\bigl(37\sqrt{37}-1\bigr).}
$$
The boundary circle has zero surface area, so including the points at height zero does not change the result.

== 5
{scope}

=== Solution

A vector <line integral> along an oriented parametrized curve $r(t)$ is $\int a(r(t))\cdot r'(t)\,dt$; for a force field it is the work along that path. On the upper branch, set $y=t$, $x=t^2$, $0\le t\le1$. Then
$$
a\cdot dr=(t^2-t)(2t\,dt)+(t^2+t)dt
=(2t^3-t^2+t)dt,
$$
so
$$
\boxed{\int_Ca\cdot dr=\left[\frac{t^4}{2}-\frac{t^3}{3}+\frac{t^2}{2}\right]_0^1=\frac23.}
$$
The stated $x$ endpoints do not distinguish the two branches of $y^2=x$. If the lower branch is intended, the same parametrization runs from $t=0$ to $t=-1$, giving instead $\boxed{4/3}$. The two values correspond to different terminal points, $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$; choosing a branch is necessary to make the question unambiguous.

== 6
{scope}

=== Solution

The <gradient> equations are $f_x=2x(1-y)=0$ and $f_y=4y-x^2=0$. The <critical points> are $(0,0)$ and $(\pm2,1)$. Their <Hessian matrix> is
$$
H=\begin{pmatrix}2-2y&-2x\\-2x&4\end{pmatrix}.
$$
At the origin $H=\operatorname{diag}(2,4)$ is positive definite. At either other point its <determinant> is $-16$, so its <eigenvalues> have opposite signs. Thus
$$
\boxed{(0,0)\text{ is a strict local minimum},\qquad (2,1),(-2,1)\text{ are saddle points}.}
$$
The minimum value is zero and both saddle values are two. The origin is not a global minimum: at any fixed $y>1$, the term $(1-y)x^2$ makes $f$ arbitrarily negative as $|x|$ increases.

For the <contour plot>, the local contours around the origin are approximately $x^2+2y^2=c$. The exact saddle contour factors as
$$
f-2=(y-1)(2y+2-x^2),
$$
so it is the union of $y=1$ and $y=x^2/2-1$, crossing at the two saddles. Other levels obey $x^2=(c-2y^2)/(1-y)$ where $y\ne1$; the right panel of the original figure in part 3(a) shows how these branches join around the minimum and pass through the saddle contour.

== 7
{scope}

=== Solution

The field is the <gradient> of $\Phi=x^2-3y^2+4z^2$ on all of $\mathbb R^3$. It is therefore a <conservative vector field>, and the <fundamental theorem for line integrals> gives $\int_C F\cdot dr=\Phi(r_{\rm end})-\Phi(r_{\rm start})$. The endpoints are $(3,0,0)$ and $(0,2,-1)$, so
$$
\boxed{\int_C F\cdot dr=(-12+4)-9=-17.}
$$
The complicated intermediate path does not affect the work. Directly, $\nabla\times F=0$, consistent with the globally defined <potential function>.

== 8
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For differentiable <vector fields>, expand the <divergence> using the <Levi-Civita symbol> and the product rule:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla\cdot(F\times G)
&=\partial_i(\epsilon_{ijk}F_jG_k)\\
&=\epsilon_{ijk}(\partial_iF_j)G_k+\epsilon_{ijk}F_j\partial_iG_k\\
&=G\cdot(\nabla\times F)-F\cdot(\nabla\times G).
\end{aligned}
$$
The first contraction uses the cyclic identity $\epsilon_{ijk}=\epsilon_{kij}$; the second uses $\epsilon_{ijk}=-\epsilon_{jik}$. This fixes the relative minus sign without guessing the orientation of the <cross products>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

To obtain the <volume integral of a curl>, test its components with an arbitrary constant vector $c$. By part (a), $\nabla\cdot(G\times c)=c\cdot(\nabla\times G)$. Applying the <divergence theorem> to the closed boundary, with outward <unit normal> $n$, gives
$$
c\cdot\iiint_V\nabla\times G\,dV
=\iint_S(G\times c)\cdot n\,dS
=c\cdot\iint_S n\times G\,dS.
$$
The last step is cyclic symmetry of the <scalar triple product>. Since this holds for every $c$, all vector components agree:
$$
\boxed{\iiint_V\nabla\times G\,dV=\iint_Sn\times G\,dS.}
$$
The same regularity conditions as the <divergence theorem> apply: for example a continuously differentiable field on a neighbourhood of a bounded volume with piecewise smooth boundary.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For constant $b$, the <curl product rule for a scalar field> gives
$$
\nabla\times[(b\cdot r)b]=\nabla(b\cdot r)\times b=b\times b=0.
$$
Using part (b) and the order of the <cross product>,
$$
\iint_SF\times dS=-\iint_Sn\times F\,dS
=-\iiint_V\nabla\times F\,dV
=\boxed{0.}
$$
Here $dS$ inside a vector product denotes the outward vector area element $n\,dS$. Closedness is the condition that permits replacing the entire surface by the boundary integral of a volume.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The closed boundary includes the curved unit hemisphere and the disk at $y=0$. On the disk, $F=(0,y,0)=0$, so that contribution vanishes. On the curved part the outward normal is $n=(x,y,z)$, giving $F\times n=(yz,0,-xy)$.

Use <spherical coordinates> with the polar axis along positive $y$:
$$
x=\sin\theta\cos\phi,\quad y=\cos\theta,\quad z=\sin\theta\sin\phi,
\quad 0\le\theta\le\frac\pi2,\quad0\le\phi\le2\pi.
$$
The area element is $\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi$. Consequently
$$
\iint_SF\times dS
=\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\sin^2\theta\,d\theta
\int_0^{2\pi}(\sin\phi,0,-\cos\phi)\,d\phi
=\boxed{(0,0,0).}
$$
This directly performs the surface integral and includes the planar cap, rather than applying the closed-surface theorem to only an open hemisphere.

== 9
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The central spring extension is $x_2-x_1$. Applying <Hooke's law> and <Newton's second law> to each mass gives
$$
\boxed{m\ddot x_1=-(k+k')x_1+k'x_2,\qquad
m\ddot x_2=k'x_1-(k+k')x_2.}
$$
In matrix form $\ddot x+(k/m)\begin{pmatrix}1+\epsilon&-\epsilon\\-\epsilon&1+\epsilon\end{pmatrix}x=0$. The positive off-diagonal coupling in the force equations means that displacing one mass pulls the other in the same direction.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <normal modes> are the <eigenvectors> of the stiffness matrix. The in-phase mode has normalized vector $(1,1)/\sqrt2$; its central spring is unstretched. The opposite-phase mode has normalized vector $(1,-1)/\sqrt2$ and stretches the central spring. Their frequencies are
$$
\boxed{\omega_-^2=\frac km,\qquad\omega_+^2=\frac{k+2k'}m=\frac km(1+2\epsilon).}
$$
Each mode has arbitrary amplitude and phase. Thus $x=A_-\cos(\omega_-t+\phi_-)(1,1)/\sqrt2+A_+\cos(\omega_+t+\phi_+)(1,-1)/\sqrt2$ is the general real motion. Equal coordinates belong to $\omega_-$ and opposite coordinates to $\omega_+$; the amplitudes are not fixed until initial data are supplied.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The initial vector $(d,0)$ has coefficient $d/\sqrt2$ in each normalized <normal mode>. Zero initial velocities remove their sine terms. Therefore
$$
\boxed{x_1(t)=\frac d2(\cos\omega_-t+\cos\omega_+t),\qquad
x_2(t)=\frac d2(\cos\omega_-t-\cos\omega_+t).}
$$
At zero these give $(d,0)$ and both derivatives vanish; substitution into the coupled equations confirms the opposite signs of the two higher-frequency components.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $\bar\omega=(\omega_++\omega_-)/2$ and $\delta=(\omega_+-\omega_-)/2$. The sum and difference identities give
$$
x_1=d\cos\bar\omega t\cos\delta t,\qquad
x_2=d\sin\bar\omega t\sin\delta t.
$$
For weak coupling, $\omega_+-\omega_-=\omega_-\epsilon+O(\epsilon^2)$, so the slow sine and cosine envelopes produce <beats>. The first maximum of the second envelope is at $t=\pi/(\omega_+-\omega_-)$, when the first envelope vanishes.

A literal <first turning point of an initially stationary coupled oscillator> is different from that slow envelope maximum. The first nonzero time at which the second mass is instantaneously at rest satisfies
$$
\boxed{\bar\omega\cos(\bar\omega t)\sin(\delta t)
+\delta\sin(\bar\omega t)\cos(\delta t)=0.}
$$
Set $z=\bar\omega t$ and $\eta=\delta/\bar\omega\ll1$. Dividing the derivative by $d\delta$ and expanding at $z$ of order one gives $z\cos z+\sin z+O(\eta^2)=0$. The first positive root lies in $(\pi/2,\pi)$, where $\tan z=-z$, so
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm first}=\frac{2.028757838\ldots}{\bar\omega}
+O\left(\frac{\eta^2}{\bar\omega}\right).}
$$
There is no earlier root: for $0<z\le\pi/2$, both terms in the exact derivative are positive when $\eta$ is sufficiently small. At the root the second displacement reaches its first local maximum. If the question instead intends the first *envelope* maximum, its time is $\pi/(\omega_+-\omega_-)\simeq\pi/(\epsilon\omega_-)$. That is a slow amplitude-transfer time, and is not generally a time of zero instantaneous velocity. The distinction matters because both masses keep oscillating while the envelopes change.

== 10
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

With $x=e^t$, the <chain rule> gives $xy'=\dot y$ and $x^2y''=\ddot y-\dot y$. The <Euler differential equation> becomes
$$
\boxed{\ddot y+2\dot y-7y=0.}
$$
Its <characteristic polynomial> has roots $-1\pm2\sqrt2$, so, in addition to the requested transformation, its general solution is $y(x)=C_1x^{-1+2\sqrt2}+C_2x^{-1-2\sqrt2}$ on $x>0$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Assume $v\ne0$ so that the change of variables is invertible. The <chain rule> gives $\partial_x=\partial_r+\partial_s$ and $\partial_t=v(\partial_r-\partial_s)$. Commuting the <mixed partial derivatives> for a twice continuously differentiable solution gives
$$
\partial_x^2-\frac1{v^2}\partial_t^2
=(\partial_r+\partial_s)^2-(\partial_r-\partial_s)^2=4\partial_r\partial_s.
$$
Thus the <wave equation> is $F_{rs}=0$. Integrating once says that $F_r$ depends only on $r$; integrating again gives
$$
\boxed{F(x,t)=f(x+vt)+g(x-vt).}
$$
The two arbitrary twice differentiable functions are waves moving in opposite directions at speed $|v|$. This is <D'Alembert's formula>, on a product domain in $(r,s)$ or globally on the usual full line.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Integrating the <mixed partial derivative> successively gives $z=x^3y^2/6+A(x)+B(y)$. The first boundary value gives $A(x)=x^2-1-B(0)$. Substitution at $x=1$ then gives $B(y)-B(0)=\sin y-y^2/6$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{z(x,y)=x^2-1+\sin y+\frac{(x^3-1)y^2}{6}.}
$$
Its mixed derivative is $x^2y$, and direct evaluation checks both boundary values. Their common corner is compatible: both prescribe $z(1,0)=0$.

== 11
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For two equal-amplitude waves with the same frequency and <wavenumber>, choose their phases so that
$$
A\cos(kx-\omega t)+A\cos(kx+\omega t)=\boxed{2A\cos kx\cos\omega t.}
$$
The spatial amplitude and temporal oscillation separate. Positions with $\cos kx=0$ are fixed <nodes of a standing wave>, while positions with $|\cos kx|=1$ are <antinodes>. Different initial phases merely shift this pattern in space and time.

A <travelling wave> has a phase profile that moves at its <phase velocity>; a <standing wave> has stationary nodes and each point oscillates with its fixed local amplitude. The two counterpropagating waves carry equal opposite time-averaged energy flux, so their superposition has zero net time-averaged energy transport. Instantaneous energy can still move within the standing pattern.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For a uniform string, let $c=\sqrt{T/\mu}$. A separated mode obeys $Y''+k^2Y=0$, $\omega=ck$. Fixed ends require $Y(0)=Y(L)=0$, so
$$
\boxed{k_n=\frac{n\pi}L,\qquad
y_n(x,t)=A_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}L\cos(ck_nt+\phi_n),\qquad n=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
The first three <normal modes> have one, two and three half-waves respectively. Their interior <nodes of a standing wave> are at $x=jL/n$ for $j=1,\ldots,n-1$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

A free end has zero transverse force, hence $TY'(L)=0$, rather than zero displacement. With $Y(0)=0$, write $Y=A\sin kx$. The remaining condition is $\cos kL=0$, giving
$$
\boxed{k_n=\frac{(n+1/2)\pi}L,\qquad
y_n=A_n\sin(k_nx)\cos(ck_nt+\phi_n),\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots.}
$$
The first three shapes have one, three and five quarter-waves. Each has a <node of a standing wave> at the fixed end and an <antinode> at the free end; the mode frequencies are in the ratio $1:3:5$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Free ends give $Y'(0)=Y'(L)=0$, so the oscillatory <normal modes> are
$$
\boxed{k_n=\frac{n\pi}L,\qquad
y_n=A_n\cos\frac{n\pi x}L\cos(ck_nt+\phi_n),\qquad n=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
There is also the <zero mode> $k_0=0$, with uniform displacement $y_0(x,t)=a+bt$: a free string can translate without a restoring force. It is not a positive-frequency oscillation. The first three oscillatory shapes are $\cos(\pi x/L)$, $\cos(2\pi x/L)$ and $\cos(3\pi x/L)$, with an <antinode> at each end. The figure includes the uniform mode too, so that the zero-frequency limit is explicit.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

At common tension, the lighter segment has three times the <wave speed>, so its <wavenumber> at the same frequency is $k_2=k_1/3$. The fixed left end and free right end select
$$
Y_1(x)=A\sin k_1x,\qquad
Y_2(x)=B\cos[k_2(4L-x)].
$$
Let $q=k_1L$. Since $k_2(4L-L)=q$, continuity of displacement and of transverse force at the junction gives
$$
A\sin q=B\cos q,\qquad
Ak_1\cos q=Bk_2\sin q.
$$
Equal tension makes the second condition continuity of slope. A nontrivial pair of amplitudes requires the coefficient <determinant> to vanish:
$$
k_1\cos^2q-k_2\sin^2q=0.
$$
For positive frequency, $k_2/k_1=1/3$, so
$$
\boxed{\sin^2(k_1L)=\frac34,\qquad\sin(k_1L)=\pm\frac{\sqrt3}{2}.}
$$
Neither $\sin q=0$ nor $\cos q=0$ gives a nonzero mode, as direct substitution into the two matching equations confirms. The positive roots are $q=\pi/3+n\pi$ or $q=2\pi/3+n\pi$ for $n\ge0$. The first modes in the figure show the changing wavelength across the junction while retaining continuous displacement and slope. The zero-frequency limit must be solved separately; the fixed and free boundary conditions allow only the trivial static solution.
