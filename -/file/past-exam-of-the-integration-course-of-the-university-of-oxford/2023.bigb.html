Writing $\mathcal L$ for the collection of <Lebesgue measurable sets>, saying that $\mathcal L$ is a <sigma-algebra> means that $\mathbb R\in\mathcal L$, that $\mathbb R\setminus A\in\mathcal L$ whenever $A\in\mathcal L$, and that $\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n\in\mathcal L$ whenever every $A_n\in\mathcal L$. These properties also imply closure under countable intersections and differences.

A real-valued <function> $f$ is a Lebesgue <measurable function> precisely when the <inverse image> of every <Borel set> is in $\mathcal L$. Equivalently, it suffices to test the half-lines:
$$
\boxed{\{x:f(x)>a\}\in\mathcal L\quad\text{for every }a\in\mathbb R.}
$$
The equivalence follows because these half-lines generate the <Borel sigma-algebra>, and <inverse images> preserve complements and countable unions.

The <density of the rational numbers> gives a countable way to separate two unequal values:
$$
\boxed{\{f<g\}=\bigcup_{q\in\mathbb Q}\bigl(\{f<q\}\cap\{g>q\}\bigr).}
$$
Indeed, $f(x)<g(x)$ holds exactly when some rational number lies strictly between them. Every set inside the union is a <Lebesgue measurable set>, by the definition of a <measurable function> and the closure properties of a <sigma-algebra>. Since $\mathbb Q$ is countable, the union is measurable.

First suppose $m(E)<\infty$. The interval-cover definition of <Lebesgue outer measure>, together with $m^*(E)=m(E)$, provides bounded open intervals $I_j$ covering $E$ such that $\sum_j|I_j|<m(E)+\varepsilon$. If the definition uses intervals that are not open, enlarge each by an amount whose total is smaller than the available error. For the <open set> $U=\bigcup_j I_j$, <countable subadditivity> and additivity of <Lebesgue measure> give
$$
m(U\setminus E)=m(U)-m(E)\leq\sum_j|I_j|-m(E)<\varepsilon.
$$
This subtraction is valid because both measures are finite.

For arbitrary $E$, including $m(E)=\infty$, set $E_n=E\cap[n,n+1)$ for $n\in\mathbb Z$, and choose positive weights $w_n=2^{-|n|}/3$, whose sum is one. Each $E_n$ has finite <Lebesgue measure>, so the preceding argument gives an <open set> $U_n\supseteq E_n$ with $m(U_n\setminus E_n)<\varepsilon w_n$. Then $U=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb Z}U_n$ is open, contains $E$, and
$$
\boxed{m(U\setminus E)\leq\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}m(U_n\setminus E_n)<\varepsilon.}
$$
Thus the required form of <regularity of Lebesgue measure> does not require $E$ itself to have finite measure.

The exact criterion is
$$
\boxed{m(E)<\infty.}
$$
Necessity follows already from one approximation with error less than one: a <compact set> $K\subseteq\mathbb R$ is bounded, hence has finite <Lebesgue measure>, so $m(E)=m(K)+m(E\setminus K)<\infty$.

Conversely, suppose $m(E)<\infty$. By <continuity from above of a measure>, the decreasing sets $E\setminus[-N,N]$ have measures tending to zero. Choose $N\geq1$ so that $m(E\setminus[-N,N])<\varepsilon/2$. Put $B=[-N,N]\setminus E$. By part (b)(i), there is an <open set> $V\supseteq B$ with $m(V\setminus B)<\varepsilon/2$. Define
$$
K=[-N,N]\setminus V.
$$
It is closed and bounded, hence a <compact set> by the <Heine-Borel theorem>, and $K\subseteq E$. The discarded portion satisfies
$$
m(E\setminus K)\leq m(E\setminus[-N,N])+m(E\cap[-N,N]\cap V)<\varepsilon.
$$
The second term is at most $m(V\setminus B)$. This proves the claimed <inner regularity of Lebesgue measure> with an arbitrarily small absolute error.

**False.** Take $E=\{0\}$ and any <non-measurable set> $A\subseteq\mathbb R$. The vertical line $\{0\}\times\mathbb R$ is a planar <Lebesgue null set>: each segment $\{0\}\times[-N,N]$ can be enclosed in rectangles of arbitrarily small area, and the line is a countable union of these segments. **True.** Suppose instead that $B=A_1\times A_2$ were a <Lebesgue measurable set> in the plane. The section consequence of <Fubini's theorem> says that there is a <Lebesgue null set> $N\subseteq\mathbb R$ such that
$$
B_x=\{y:(x,y)\in B\}
$$
is a <Lebesgue measurable set> for every $x\notin N$. This version applies to completed <Lebesgue measure>; it can be obtained on bounded rectangles and then extended by a countable exhaustion, so no finite-measure assumption on $B$ is needed.

For every $x\in A_1$ the section is exactly $A_2$, which is not measurable. Thus $A_1\subseteq N$. By <completeness of Lebesgue measure>, every subset of $N$ is measurable, contradicting the hypothesis on $A_1$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{A_1\times A_2\text{ is not Lebesgue measurable}.}
$$

This requires an argument using <Lebesgue outer measure>, since $E$ need not be a <Lebesgue measurable set>. Some bounded portion $F=E\cap[-N,N]$ has $a=m^*(F)>0$: otherwise <countable subadditivity> would give $m^*(E)=0$. Also $a\leq2N<\infty$.

Suppose, for a contradiction, that every nonempty bounded open <interval> $I$ satisfies $m^*(E\cap I)\leq\lambda|I|$. Because $a/\lambda>a$, the definition of <Lebesgue outer measure> gives a countable cover $F\subseteq\bigcup_j I_j$ by nonempty bounded open intervals with
$$
\sum_j|I_j|<\frac a\lambda.
$$
Using <countable subadditivity> again,
$$
a=m^*(F)\leq\sum_jm^*(F\cap I_j)\leq\sum_jm^*(E\cap I_j)\leq\lambda\sum_j|I_j|<a,
$$
a contradiction. Hence at least one interval in such a cover satisfies
$$
\boxed{m^*(E\cap I)>\lambda|I|.}
$$
This <high-density interval for outer measure> applies even to nonmeasurable sets; the argument does not require the <Lebesgue density theorem>.

The <function> is continuous on $(0,\infty)$, hence a <measurable function>. On $(0,1)$ its absolute value is at most one, while on $[1,\infty)$ the elementary estimate $|\sin u|\leq|u|$ gives $|\sin(1/x^3)|\leq x^{-3}$. By comparison for the <Lebesgue integral>,
$$

$$
It is therefore a <Lebesgue integrable function>. The oscillations near zero do not obstruct absolute integrability because their amplitude stays bounded on an interval of finite measure.

Again the <function> is continuous on its domain and therefore a <measurable function>. For $0<x<1$, $|(\log x)e^{-x}|\leq-\log x$, and
$$
\int_0^1-\log x\,dx=1.
$$
This standard <improper integral> follows from the <antiderivative> $x-x\log x$ and $x\log x\to0$ at zero. For $x\geq1$, $0\leq\log x\leq x$, and <integration by parts> gives $\int_1^\infty xe^{-x}\,dx=2/e$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty|(\log x)e^{-x}|\,dx\leq1+\frac2e<\infty.}
$$
The logarithmic singularity is integrable and the exponential factor controls the tail, so this too is a <Lebesgue integrable function>.

Let $J=\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}\,dx$. It is positive and finite: on $(0,1)$ the integrand is at most one, and on $[1,\infty)$ it is at most $e^{-x}$. Since the planar integrand is nonnegative and measurable, <Tonelli's theorem> gives
$$
J^2=\int_{(0,\infty)^2}e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dx\,dy.
$$
The <change of variables formula> in <polar coordinates>, with $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$ and absolute <Jacobian determinant> $r$, yields
$$
J^2=\int_0^{\pi/2}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}r\,dr\,d\theta
=\frac\pi2\cdot\frac12=\frac\pi4.
$$
One can apply the formula first on $\delta<r<R$, $0<\theta<\pi/2$, where the coordinate map is a <diffeomorphism>, and then exhaust the quadrant using the <monotone convergence theorem>. Taking the positive square root proves the half-line <Gaussian integral>:
$$
\boxed{J=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}.}
$$
Here $dx$ is understood in the one-dimensional integral; it is missing from the printed question.

Put $h(t,x)=e^{-x^2-t^2/x^2}$. For every $t\geq0$, $0<h(t,x)\leq e^{-x^2}$, and the upper bound is a <Lebesgue integrable function> by part (b)(i). Since $h(t,x)\to e^{-x^2}$ for each $x>0$ as $t\downarrow0$, the <dominated convergence theorem> gives right <continuity> at zero and
$$
F(0)=\lim_{t\downarrow0}F(t)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}.
$$
To apply <dominated differentiation under the integral sign>, fix $t_0>0$ and restrict $t$ to $[t_0/2,3t_0/2]$. Then
$$
|\partial_t h(t,x)|=\frac{2t}{x^2}e^{-x^2-t^2/x^2}
\leq D(x):=3t_0x^{-2}e^{-x^2-t_0^2/(4x^2)}.
$$
For $x\geq1$, $D(x)\leq3t_0e^{-x^2}$. For $0<x<1$, writing $a=t_0^2/4>0$, the <substitution rule> $u=1/x$ gives
$$
\int_0^1x^{-2}e^{-a/x^2}\,dx=\int_1^\infty e^{-au^2}\,du<\infty.
$$
Thus $D$ is integrable. The <mean value theorem> bounds nearby difference quotients by $D$, so the <dominated convergence theorem> justifies the derivative at $t_0$. As $t_0$ was arbitrary,
$$
F'(t)=-2t\int_0^\infty x^{-2}e^{-x^2-t^2/x^2}\,dx,\qquad t>0.
$$
Now make the <change of variables> $u=t/x$. The reversal of limits cancels the negative differential and gives
$$
\int_0^\infty x^{-2}e^{-x^2-t^2/x^2}\,dx
=\frac1t\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2-t^2/u^2}\,du=\frac{F(t)}t.
$$
Consequently $F'(t)=-2F(t)$. This also follows in sign from the fact that the positive integrand decreases strictly as $t$ increases: the printed equation with a positive sign cannot hold. Solving the <linear differential equation>, or differentiating $e^{2t}F(t)$, and using right <continuity> at zero gives
$$
\boxed{F'(t)=-2F(t)\ (t>0),\qquad F(t)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}e^{-2t}\ (t\geq0).}
$$
This is a half-line instance of the <Gaussian integral with an inverse-square term>.

<Minkowski inequality> states that on a <measure space> $(X,\Sigma,\mu)$, for $1\leq p\leq\infty$ and measurable real or complex $f,g$ of finite <Lp norm>,
$$
\boxed{\|f+g\|_p\leq\|f\|_p+\|g\|_p.}
$$
For $p=1$, integrate the scalar <triangle inequality> $|f+g|\leq|f|+|g|$. For $p=\infty$, the <almost everywhere> bounds $|f|\leq\|f\|_\infty$ and $|g|\leq\|g\|_\infty$ give the same result by taking the <essential supremum>.

For $1<p<\infty$, first ensure that the left side is finite. The <convex function> $s\mapsto s^p$ on $[0,\infty)$ gives
$$
|f+g|^p\leq(|f|+|g|)^p\leq2^{p-1}(|f|^p+|g|^p),
$$
so $f+g$ has finite <Lp norm>. Set $q=p/(p-1)$ and $H=\|f+g\|_p$. Since $(p-1)q=p$, <Holder inequality> gives
$$
H^p\leq\int_X(|f|+|g|)|f+g|^{p-1}\,d\mu
\leq(\|f\|_p+\|g\|_p)\left(\int_X|f+g|^p\,d\mu\right)^{1/q}
=(\|f\|_p+\|g\|_p)H^{p-1}.
$$
If $H=0$ the result is immediate; otherwise divide by $H^{p-1}$. This proves the <triangle inequality> for every claimed exponent.

Use <Lebesgue measure> restricted to the <Lebesgue measurable set> $E$. For $1\leq p<\infty$, start with the measurable real-valued <functions> $f$ on $E$ satisfying $\int_E|f|^p\,dm<\infty$. Define an <equivalence relation> by $f\sim g$ when $f=g$ <almost everywhere>, and let $L^p(E)$ consist of their <equivalence classes>. Addition and scalar multiplication are defined using representatives. The <Lp norm> is
$$
\boxed{\|[f]\|_p=\left(\int_E|f|^p\,dm\right)^{1/p},\qquad1\leq p<\infty.}
$$
For $p=\infty$, use measurable <functions> bounded <almost everywhere>, apply the same identification, and define the <L-infinity norm> by
$$
\boxed{\|[f]\|_\infty=\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{x\in E}|f(x)|
=\inf\{C\geq0:m(\{x\in E:|f(x)|>C\})=0\}.}
$$
These expressions are independent of the representative. For finite $p$, zero <norm> is equivalent to $f=0$ <almost everywhere>: each set $\{|f|>1/n\}$ must have measure zero, and their union is $\{f\ne0\}$. The same implication follows from the <essential supremum> for $p=\infty$. Absolute homogeneity is immediate; <Minkowski inequality> proves the <triangle inequality> and closure under addition. Thus these are <normed vector spaces>. Using complex-valued representatives gives their complex versions by the same construction.

Let $(f_n)$ be a <Cauchy sequence> in the <L2 space> $L^2(\mathbb R)$, choosing measurable representatives. There is a subsequence $(f_{n_k})$ with
$$
\|f_{n_{k+1}}-f_{n_k}\|_2\leq2^{-k}\qquad(k\geq1).
$$
Write $d_k=f_{n_{k+1}}-f_{n_k}$ and $S_N=\sum_{k=1}^N|d_k|$. Repeated <Minkowski inequality> gives $\|S_N\|_2\leq\sum_{k=1}^N2^{-k}\leq1$. As $S_N^2$ increases pointwise, the <monotone convergence theorem> gives
$$
\int_{\mathbb R}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|d_k|\right)^2dm
=\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb R}S_N^2dm\leq1.
$$
Hence $S=\sum_k|d_k|$ is finite <almost everywhere> and represents an <L2 function>. Define
$$
f=f_{n_1}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}d_k
$$
where the series converges, and set $f=0$ on the exceptional <Lebesgue null set>. It is a <measurable function>, and $|f|\leq|f_{n_1}|+S$ <almost everywhere>, so <Minkowski inequality> gives $f\in L^2(\mathbb R)$.

For fixed $k$, <Fatou's lemma> applied to squares of finite tails and then <Minkowski inequality> give
$$
\|f-f_{n_k}\|_2^2
\leq\liminf_{M\to\infty}\left\|\sum_{j=k}^{M}d_j\right\|_2^2
\leq\left(\sum_{j=k}^{\infty}2^{-j}\right)^2=2^{2-2k}.
$$
Thus the subsequence converges to $f$ in <norm>. Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose a sufficiently late $n_k$ with $\|f-f_{n_k}\|_2<\varepsilon/2$ and with $\|f_n-f_{n_k}\|_2<\varepsilon/2$ for every sufficiently large $n$, using the original <Cauchy sequence> property. The <triangle inequality> then gives $\|f_n-f\|_2<\varepsilon$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{L^2(\mathbb R)\text{ is complete, hence a Banach space}.}
$$
This proves the needed instance of <completeness of Lp spaces> without assuming the result being proved.

Thus there is no <pointwise convergence> at any $x\in(0,1)$, let alone convergence <almost everywhere>.

Let $M=\|f\|_\infty$, the <essential supremum> of $|f|$. If $M=0$, $f=0$ <almost everywhere> and every <Lp norm> is zero. Suppose $M>0$. Since $|f|\leq M$ <almost everywhere> and $m((0,1))=1$,
$$
\|f\|_p\leq M\qquad(1\leq p<\infty).
$$
For any $a$ with $0<a<M$, the <measurable set> $A_a=\{x\in(0,1):|f(x)|>a\}$ has positive <Lebesgue measure>; otherwise $a$ would be an essential upper bound. Hence
$$
\|f\|_p^p\geq\int_{A_a}|f|^p\,dx\geq a^pm(A_a),\qquad
\|f\|_p\geq a\,m(A_a)^{1/p}.
$$
For fixed $a$, $m(A_a)^{1/p}\to1$. Thus $\liminf_{p\to\infty}\|f\|_p\geq a$. Letting $a\uparrow M$ and combining with the upper bound proves
$$
\boxed{\lim_{p\to\infty}\|f\|_p=\|f\|_\infty.}
$$
The positive-measure superlevel sets explain why the <Lp norms converge to the supremum norm> even when the essential maximum is never attained.
