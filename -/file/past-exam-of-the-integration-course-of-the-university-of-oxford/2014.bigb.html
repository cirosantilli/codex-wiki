= Solution

The <monotone convergence theorem> states: on a <measure space> $(X,\mathcal F,\mu)$, let $u_n:X\to[0,\infty]$ be <measurable functions> with
$$
0\leq u_1(x)\leq u_2(x)\leq\cdots,\qquad u(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}u_n(x).
$$
Then $u$ is measurable and
$$
\boxed{\int_Xu\,d\mu=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_Xu_n\,d\mu.}
$$
The <integrals> and their <limit> may be infinite. No integrable dominator, <finite measure> hypothesis, or <sigma-finiteness> is required. The nonnegativity and increasing order are the essential hypotheses in this form of the theorem.

= Solution

Let $S_N(x)=\sum_{n=1}^N|f_n(x)|$. These are nonnegative <measurable functions> increasing to the extended-valued <function> $S(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|f_n(x)|$. The <monotone convergence theorem> gives
$$
\int_ES(x)\,dx=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=1}^N\int_E|f_n(x)|\,dx
=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|f_n\|_1<\infty.
$$
Therefore $S$ is finite <almost everywhere>: if $S=\infty$ on a <measurable set> of positive <Lebesgue measure>, its <integral> would exceed every finite bound. Hence the <series> $\sum_nf_n(x)$ converges absolutely outside the <null set> $N=\{S=\infty\}$. This is <summable L1 norms imply almost-everywhere absolute convergence>.

Define $s(x)=\sum_nf_n(x)$ off $N$ and set $s=0$ on $N$. The <function> is <Lebesgue measurable>, being the pointwise <limit> of measurable partial sums off a measurable <null set>. Since $|s|\leq S$ <almost everywhere>,
$$
\|s\|_1\leq\int_ES<\infty,
$$
so **the sum is <Lebesgue integrable>**.

For $s_N=\sum_{n=1}^Nf_n$, the absolute tail satisfies
$$
|s-s_N|\leq\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}|f_n|\qquad\text{almost everywhere}.
$$
Apply the <monotone convergence theorem> to the nonnegative tail partial sums. We obtain
$$
\|s-s_N\|_1\leq\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\|f_n\|_1\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus the partial sums converge in <L1 norm>. The <triangle inequality for an integral> yields
$$
\left|\int_Es-\sum_{n=1}^N\int_Ef_n\right|\leq\|s-s_N\|_1\longrightarrow0.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\int_E\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f_n(x)\,dx=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_Ef_n(x)\,dx.}
$$
The scalar <series> on the right also converges absolutely, since $\sum_n|\int_Ef_n|\leq\sum_n\|f_n\|_1<\infty$. This proves <integration of a series with summable L1 norms> without needing pointwise convergence on the exceptional <null set>.

The <function> is continuous for $x>0$, hence <Lebesgue measurable>. On $(0,1)$, $|\sin x|\leq x$ and $|\cos x|\leq1$ give
$$
|f(x)|\leq\frac{x}{x\sqrt x}+\frac1{\sqrt x}=2x^{-1/2}.
$$
Since $\int_0^12x^{-1/2}\,dx=4$, **$f$ is <Lebesgue integrable> on $(0,1)$** by <power singularity integrability>.

On $(1,\infty)$, the first summand is <Lebesgue integrable>:
$$
\int_1^\infty\frac{|\sin x|}{x^{3/2}}\,dx\leq\int_1^\infty x^{-3/2}\,dx=2.
$$
The second summand is not. To prove it, take the pairwise <disjoint> <intervals>
$$
I_k=\left[k\pi-\frac\pi3,\ k\pi+\frac\pi3\right],\qquad k\geq1.
$$
On $I_k$, $|\cos x|\geq1/2$, the length is $2\pi/3$, and $x\leq(k+1)\pi$. Hence
$$
\int_{I_k}\frac{|\cos x|}{\sqrt x}\,dx
\geq\frac{\pi}{3\sqrt{(k+1)\pi}}
=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{3\sqrt{k+1}}.
$$
The <p-series> with exponent $1/2$ diverges, so $\int_1^\infty|\cos x|/\sqrt x\,dx=\infty$.

If $f$ were <Lebesgue integrable> on this tail, subtracting its integrable first summand would make $\cos x/\sqrt x$ integrable as well, a contradiction. Therefore
$$
\boxed{f\in L^1((0,1)),\qquad f\notin L^1((1,\infty)).}
$$
This uses the general fact that <an integrable perturbation cannot repair nonintegrability>. The conclusion concerns <absolute integrability>; oscillatory cancellation in an <improper integral> is a separate issue.

The <dominated convergence theorem> states: on a <measure space> $(X,\mathcal F,\mu)$, let $u_n$ be <measurable functions> converging <almost everywhere> to a measurable $u$. Suppose there is a nonnegative <measurable function> $G$ with $\int_XG\,d\mu<\infty$ and $|u_n|\leq G$ <almost everywhere> for every $n$. Then $u$ is integrable and
$$
\boxed{\int_X|u_n-u|\,d\mu\longrightarrow0,\qquad
\int_Xu_n\,d\mu\longrightarrow\int_Xu\,d\mu.}
$$
Here integrability means integrability with respect to the given <measure>; for <Lebesgue measure> it is <Lebesgue integrability>. Neither a <finite measure> space nor monotonicity of the sequence is required. The same single integrable dominator must control the whole sequence.

= Solution

Fix $t_0\geq0$. On a compact parameter <interval> containing all nonnegative $t$ sufficiently close to $t_0$, the corresponding rectangle with $x\in[0,1]$ is <compact>. The <continuous function> $f$ is therefore bounded there by some $M<\infty$, by the <extreme value theorem>.

For any sequence $t_n\geq0$ tending to $t_0$, all sufficiently late $t_n$ lie in this rectangle's parameter range. For each $x\in[0,1]$, <continuity> gives
$$
f(t_n,x)\longrightarrow f(t_0,x),\qquad |f(t_n,x)|\leq M.
$$
The constant <function> $M$ is integrable on $[0,1]$. The <dominated convergence theorem> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{F(t_n)=\int_0^1f(t_n,x)\,dx\longrightarrow\int_0^1f(t_0,x)\,dx=F(t_0).}
$$
The sequential criterion for <continuity> on a real <interval> proves **$F$ is continuous on $[0,\infty)$**. At zero this is right-continuity. This is <continuity of parameter integrals on compact rectangles>; the bound is local in the parameter, so $f$ need not be bounded on the entire unbounded strip.

= Solution

We prove the required <differentiation under the integral sign> directly. Fix $t_0>0$, and take a compact parameter <interval> $J\subseteq[0,\infty)$ containing $t_0$ in its interior. The jointly continuous <partial derivative> $\partial_t f$ is bounded on the compact rectangle $J\times[0,1]$ by some $M_1$.

For nonzero $h$ sufficiently close to zero, define
$$
D_h(x)=\frac{f(t_0+h,x)-f(t_0,x)}h.
$$
For each $x$, the <mean value theorem> in the parameter gives $|D_h(x)|\leq M_1$. The definition of the <partial derivative> also gives $D_h(x)\to\partial_t f(t_0,x)$. Since $M_1$ is integrable on $[0,1]$, <dominated convergence> applied along any sequence $h_n\to0$ yields
$$
\lim_{h\to0}\frac{F(t_0+h)-F(t_0)}h
=\lim_{h\to0}\int_0^1D_h(x)\,dx
=\int_0^1\partial_t f(t_0,x)\,dx.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{F'(t)=\int_0^1\frac{\partial f(t,x)}{\partial t}\,dx\qquad(t>0).}
$$
At $t=0$, repeat the argument on $J=[0,\delta]$ with $h>0$. It proves that the <right derivative> exists and satisfies the same formula at zero. This is the appropriate meaning of differentiability on the closed half-line. In fact $F'$ is continuous there: apply part (i) to the continuous <function> $\partial_t f$.

The proof establishes <differentiation of parameter integrals on compact rectangles> under the exact regularity assumptions used here. In particular, it supplies the theorem's proof instead of assuming an interchange of a <limit> and an <integral>.

Remove the apparent singularity at $x=0$ by defining
$$
\phi(t,x)=\begin{cases}\sin(tx)/x,&x>0,\\t,&x=0.\end{cases}
$$
This is jointly continuous: write $\phi(t,x)=t\,\operatorname{sinc}(tx)$ using the <unnormalized sinc function>, whose value at zero is one. Its <partial derivative> in $t$ is
$$
\partial_t\phi(t,x)=\cos(tx)
$$
also at $x=0$, since there $\phi(t,0)=t$. This <partial derivative> is jointly continuous on the whole strip.

The value at the single endpoint $x=0$ does not change a <Lebesgue integral>. Part (b), whose <dominated convergence> proof applies to $\phi$, therefore makes $g$ continuously differentiable on $[0,\infty)$, with a <right derivative> at zero, and
$$
g'(t)=\int_0^1\cos(tx)\,dx.
$$
For $t>0$, direct <integration> gives
$$
\boxed{g'(t)=\frac{\sin t}{t},\qquad tg'(t)=\sin t.}
$$
At zero the same integral gives $\boxed{g'_+(0)=1}$, so no division by zero is required. Also $g(0)=0$. The <substitution rule> $u=tx$ identifies $g(t)=\int_0^t\sin u/u\,du$ for $t>0$, the <sine integral>.

Let $(X,\mathcal A,\mu)$ and $(Y,\mathcal B,\nu)$ be <measure spaces> with <sigma-finite measures>, and use their <product measure>.

**<Fubini's theorem>:** if $u$ is a measurable absolutely integrable <function> on $X\times Y$, then its sections are integrable for <almost every> fixed value of the other coordinate, the resulting section-integral <functions> are integrable, and
$$
\boxed{\int_{X\times Y}u\,d(\mu\times\nu)
=\int_X\left(\int_Yu(x,y)\,d\nu(y)\right)d\mu(x)
=\int_Y\left(\int_Xu(x,y)\,d\mu(x)\right)d\nu(y).}
$$
All three <integrals> are finite. Values of an inner <integral> on an exceptional <null set> can be assigned arbitrarily.

**<Tonelli's theorem>:** if $u:X\times Y\to[0,\infty]$ is measurable and nonnegative, the same equality holds for its nonnegative product and iterated <integrals>, allowing the common value $+\infty$. No advance <absolute integrability> assumption is needed. On a completed <Lebesgue measure> product, the section and measurability assertions are interpreted <almost everywhere>.

Thus <Tonelli's theorem> can first establish that $\int|u|<\infty$; after this, <Fubini's theorem> permits signed integration in either order.

= Solution

Assign any value, for example zero, at $(0,0)$, where the displayed quotient is undefined. A single point is a <Lebesgue null set>, so this choice does not affect <Lebesgue integrability>. Elsewhere the <function> is continuous, hence it is <Lebesgue measurable> on the plane after this extension.

In <polar coordinates>, $x^2+y^2=r^2$ and the area element is $r\,dr\,d\theta$. Applying the nonnegative <change of variables formula> to the <absolute value> gives
$$
\int_{\mathbb R^2}|k(x,y)|\,dx\,dy
=2\pi\int_0^\infty|\sin(r^2)|r^{1-2\alpha}\,dr.
$$
For $0<r\leq1$, $|\sin(r^2)|\leq r^2$, so the radial <integrand> is bounded by $r^{3-2\alpha}$. This is integrable at zero when $\alpha<2$. For $r\geq1$, $|\sin(r^2)|\leq1$, giving the integrable tail bound $r^{1-2\alpha}$ when $\alpha>1$. In particular,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\|k\|_1
&\leq2\pi\left(\int_0^1r^{3-2\alpha}\,dr+\int_1^\infty r^{1-2\alpha}\,dr\right)\\
&=\pi\left(\frac1{2-\alpha}+\frac1{\alpha-1}\right)<\infty.
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{k\in L^1(\mathbb R^2)\qquad(1<\alpha<2).}
$$
Equivalently, the <substitution rule> $u=r^2$ reduces its exact <L1 norm> to
$$
\|k\|_1=\pi\int_0^\infty\frac{|\sin u|}{u^\alpha}\,du.
$$
This is <radial sine-power kernel integrability>, reducing a two-dimensional question to the <sine power kernel integrability> thresholds at zero and infinity.

Fix $0<x<1$. On the open <interval> $(0,x)$, the <function> $y\mapsto(x-y)^{\beta-1}$ is finite, positive and continuous, and $f$ is nonnegative and <Lebesgue measurable>. Their product is therefore a nonnegative <measurable function>. Its <Lebesgue integral> is always defined as an element of $[0,\infty]$, with no possibility of cancellation of two infinities. Thus **$g(x)$ is well defined as a nonnegative extended integral**.

The endpoint $y=x$ need not receive its formal power value: for $\beta<1$ that value would be singular. The integral is over $(0,x)$, and any chosen value at the endpoints has no effect because endpoints form a <null set>.

Finiteness is not implied by the present assumptions. For example, $f(y)=1/y$ is an admissible nonnegative <measurable function>. For any fixed $x$, the factor $(x-y)^{\beta-1}$ is bounded below by a positive constant on $0<y<x/2$, so $g(x)\geq C_x\int_0^{x/2}dy/y=\infty$. The later <L1 norm> bound will establish finiteness <almost everywhere> when $f$ is integrable. The construction is a <Volterra power-kernel integral>.

= Solution

Define the joint <function> by an unambiguous piecewise prescription:
$$
H(x,y)=\begin{cases}(x-y)^{\beta-1}f(y),&0<y<x<1,\\0,&\text{otherwise in }(0,1)^2.\end{cases}
$$
It is nonnegative and <Lebesgue measurable>: on the open triangle the power factor is continuous and $f(y)$ is measurable for the <product sigma-algebra>, and the zero extension is measurable. This also resolves the diagonal singularity and avoids evaluating a noninteger power of a negative number outside the triangle. The source's inclusion of $y=x$ in its indicator <set> changes only a <null set>; set the value there to zero.

The <function> $g(x)=\int_0^1H(x,y)\,dy$ is measurable by <Tonelli's theorem>. Apply that theorem to exchange the nonnegative <integrals>, with infinity allowed until the estimate proves otherwise:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1g(x)\,dx
&=\int_0^1f(y)\left(\int_y^1(x-y)^{\beta-1}\,dx\right)dy\\
&=\frac1\beta\int_0^1(1-y)^\beta f(y)\,dy\\
&\leq\frac1\beta\int_0^1f(y)\,dy<\infty.
\end{aligned}
$$
The inner <integral> is $(1-y)^\beta/\beta$ because $\beta>0$. The weight is at most one because $0<y<1$. Since $g\geq0$, this proves
$$
\boxed{\|g\|_1=\frac1\beta\int_0^1(1-y)^\beta f(y)\,dy
\leq\frac1\beta\|f\|_1,\qquad g\in L^1((0,1)).}
$$
In particular $g$ is finite <almost everywhere>; any exceptional infinite values may be replaced by zero to obtain an everywhere finite representative. This is the <L1 bound for a Volterra power-kernel integral>.

An everywhere-finite conclusion would be stronger and can fail when $0<\beta<1$, even for integrable $f$. For example, put $x_0=1/2$ and $\gamma=(1+\beta)/2$, and take $f(y)=(x_0-y)^{-\gamma}$ for $0<y<x_0$ and zero otherwise. Since $\gamma<1$, $f$ is integrable. But
$$
g(x_0)=\int_0^{x_0}(x_0-y)^{\beta-1-\gamma}\,dy=\infty,
$$
because $\beta-1-\gamma=(\beta-3)/2<-1$. This does not contradict <Lebesgue integrability>, which requires finiteness only <almost everywhere>.
