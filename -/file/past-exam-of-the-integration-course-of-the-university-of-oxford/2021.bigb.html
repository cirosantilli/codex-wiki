Solutions to all three questions of the examination sat on 16 June 2021, including all optional parts. Integrability means <Lebesgue integrability>; a conditionally convergent <improper integral> does not suffice. The Roman items in Question 3(a) are the three required properties of one construction; the construction and its limiting properties are explained first, and each Roman item is checked separately.

= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

Extend the integrand by zero to all of $(0,\infty)$:
$$
h_n(x)=\mathbf1_{(0,2n\pi)}(x)\frac{(n+x)\sin(x/n)}{x(1+x^2)}.
$$
For each fixed $x>0$, eventually the <indicator function> is one and $\sin(x/n)/(x/n)\to1$. Hence $h_n(x)\to(1+x^2)^{-1}$ <pointwise>. On its support, $x/n\leq2\pi$, so $|\sin t|\leq t$ for $t\geq0$ gives
$$
|h_n(x)|\leq\frac{1+x/n}{1+x^2}\leq\frac{1+2\pi}{1+x^2}.
$$
The final bound is <Lebesgue integrable> on $(0,\infty)$ and independent of $n$. The <dominated convergence theorem> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^{2n\pi}\frac{(n+x)\sin(x/n)}{x(1+x^2)}\,dx
=\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x^2}=\frac\pi2.}
$$
The last integral follows from the <antiderivative> $\arctan x$.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

On $(0,1)$, the integrand is bounded by one. For $x\to\infty$, $\alpha x^\beta-2\log x\to\infty$, since every positive power grows faster than the <natural logarithm>. Thus $e^{-\alpha x^\beta}\leq x^{-2}$ eventually. The <comparison test for integrals> proves <Lebesgue integrability>.

To prove joint <continuity>, fix $(\alpha_0,\beta_0)$ with both coordinates positive. In a sufficiently small parameter rectangle, $\alpha\geq a:=\alpha_0/2>0$ and $\beta\geq b:=\beta_0/2>0$. For every parameter pair in this rectangle,
$$
0\leq e^{-\alpha x^\beta}\leq H(x):=\begin{cases}1,&0<x<1,\\e^{-a x^b},&x\geq1.\end{cases}
$$
The first argument shows that $H$ is <integrable>. Along any parameter <sequence> tending to $(\alpha_0,\beta_0)$, the integrands converge <pointwise>, and the same $H$ dominates their eventual tail. The <dominated convergence theorem> gives $g(\alpha,\beta)\to g(\alpha_0,\beta_0)$. The <sequential criterion for continuity> proves the required joint <continuity> throughout the positive quadrant.

For comparison, the <change of variables> $u=\alpha x^\beta$ identifies the value with a <gamma function>:
$$
\boxed{g(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{\Gamma(1/\beta)}{\beta\alpha^{1/\beta}}.}
$$
The domination argument establishes <continuity> directly, without needing a separate theorem about parameter dependence of the <gamma function>.

For each nonnegative <integer> $k$, the <change of variables> $x=e^{-t}$ gives
$$
\int_0^1|(x\log x)^k|\,dx=\int_0^\infty t^ke^{-(k+1)t}\,dt
=\frac{\Gamma(k+1)}{(k+1)^{k+1}}<\infty.
$$
The <gamma function> identity $\Gamma(k+1)=k!$ follows by repeated <integration by parts>. Since $\log x<0$ on $(0,1)$, the sign of the original integrand is $(-1)^k$, so
$$
\boxed{\int_0^1(x\log x)^k\,dx=\frac{(-1)^kk!}{(k+1)^{k+1}}.}
$$
For $k=0$, the integral is one, consistent with the formula.

= ii
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

The <exponential series> gives $x^x=e^{x\log x}=\sum_{k=0}^\infty(x\log x)^k/k!$. Since $|x\log x|\leq1/e$ on $(0,1)$, the <Weierstrass M-test> proves <uniform absolute convergence>, using the summable bounds $e^{-k}/k!$. Therefore one can integrate the <series> term by term over this finite interval. Using part <1/c/i>,
$$
\int_0^1x^x\,dx=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac1{k!}\int_0^1(x\log x)^k\,dx
=\boxed{\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k}{(k+1)^{k+1}}.}
$$
This is the alternating identity in <sophomore's dream>. The same estimate also proves <absolute convergence> of the integrated <series>; no cancellation is needed to justify the interchange.

= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

Near zero, $\sin x/x\to1$, so $|\sin x|/x^\alpha$ is comparable to $x^{1-\alpha}$. The <power integral test> shows that the integral near zero is finite exactly when $\alpha<2$.

For the tail, $|\sin x|\leq1$ proves integrability when $\alpha>1$. To prove necessity, take the disjoint <intervals>
$$
I_k=[2k\pi+\pi/6,\ 2k\pi+5\pi/6],\qquad k\geq1.
$$
On each, $\sin x\geq1/2$, and $x\leq2\pi(k+1)$. Therefore $\int_{I_k}|\sin x|x^{-\alpha}\,dx\geq c_\alpha(k+1)^{-\alpha}$ with $c_\alpha>0$. The <p-series> diverges when $\alpha\leq1$, proving that the absolute tail integral diverges in that range.

Combining the two conditions,
$$
\boxed{f_\alpha\text{ is Lebesgue integrable on }(0,\infty)\iff1<\alpha<2.}
$$
For $0<\alpha\leq1$, the tail <improper integral> converges conditionally by the <Dirichlet test for improper integrals>, but that does not give <Lebesgue integrability>.

The absolute value is bounded by $e^{-ax}$, whose integral is $1/a$. Thus the <function> is <Lebesgue integrable>. The complex integral of an <exponential function> is
$$
\int_0^\infty e^{(-a+ib)x}\,dx=\left[\frac{e^{(-a+ib)x}}{-a+ib}\right]_0^\infty=\frac1{a-ib}=\frac{a+ib}{a^2+b^2}.
$$
Taking the <real part>, justified by <absolute integrability>, gives
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty e^{-ax}\cos(bx)\,dx=\frac a{a^2+b^2}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=i}

**The <function> is not <Lebesgue integrable>.** Fix $0<\eta<\pi/2$ and restrict to $x\geq1$, $0<y<\eta$. There $\cos y\geq\cos\eta>0$. <Tonelli's theorem> for the nonnegative absolute value gives the lower bound
$$
\begin{aligned}
\iint|g(x,y)|\,dx\,dy
&\geq\cos\eta\int_1^\infty|\cos2x|\int_0^\eta e^{-xy}\,dy\,dx\\
&=\cos\eta\int_1^\infty|\cos2x|\frac{1-e^{-\eta x}}x\,dx\\
&\geq\cos\eta(1-e^{-\eta})\int_1^\infty\frac{|\cos2x|}x\,dx.
\end{aligned}
$$
The last integral diverges. Indeed, on $[k\pi,k\pi+\pi/6]$, $|\cos2x|\geq1/2$; each such interval contributes at least a positive constant times $1/(k+1)$. The <harmonic series> diverges. Hence the integral of $|g|$ is infinite, regardless of possible signed cancellations. One cannot infer <Lebesgue integrability> by formally applying <Fubini's theorem> to the oscillatory integrand.

= ii
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

**The <function> is <Lebesgue integrable>.** The inequalities $|\sin u|\leq\min\{u,1\}$ for $u>0$ give bounds that handle the two ends of the $x$-axis differently. For $0<x<1$,
$$
\int_0^\infty e^{-2xy}|\sin x\sin y|\,dy
\leq x\int_0^\infty e^{-2xy}\,dy=\frac12.
$$
For $x\geq1$,
$$
\int_0^\infty e^{-2xy}|\sin x\sin y|\,dy
\leq\int_0^\infty y e^{-2xy}\,dy=\frac1{4x^2}.
$$
<Tonelli's theorem> therefore proves
$$
\boxed{\iint_{(0,\infty)^2}e^{-2xy}|\sin x\sin y|\,dx\,dy
\leq\frac12+\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{4x^2}=\frac34<\infty.}
$$
The factors of <sine> suppress the near-axis contributions that caused divergence in part <2/c/i>.

Set $C_0=[0,1]$. Inductively, replace each component interval of $C_n$ by its two closed end intervals, each of length $\ell_{n+1}:=q_{n+1}/2^{n+1}$. The current component length is $\ell_n=q_n/2^n$. Because $q_{n+1}<q_n$, the removed central gap has positive length
$$
\ell_n-2\ell_{n+1}=\frac{q_n-q_{n+1}}{2^n}.
$$
This is a <generalized Cantor set> construction. The three required properties are checked in parts <3/a/i>, <3/a/ii> and <3/a/iii>.

The <sets> are <closed>, <measurable>, decreasing, and contained in $[0,1]$, which has finite <Lebesgue measure>. By <continuity from above of a measure>,
$$
\boxed{m(C)=m\!\left(\bigcap_{n=0}^\infty C_n\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}q_n.}
$$
This limit exists because $(q_n)$ is a bounded decreasing <monotone sequence>. To exclude a nondegenerate <interval> $J\subseteq C$, note that its <connectedness> forces it into a single component interval of every $C_n$: crossing two components would cross a removed gap. Its length would therefore be at most $2^{-n}$ for every $n$, which is impossible for a positive-length <interval>. Thus **$C$ contains no nondegenerate <interval>**, even when its <Lebesgue measure> is positive.

At stage zero there is one <closed interval>. At every later stage each component is replaced by two closed end intervals separated by a positive open gap. Induction therefore gives exactly $2^n$ pairwise disjoint <closed intervals> at stage $n$. Their common length is
$$
\boxed{\ell_n=\frac{q_n}{2^n}\leq2^{-n},}
$$
since the decreasing <sequence> starts at $q_0=1$.

The $2^n$ component intervals are pairwise disjoint and each has length $q_n/2^n$. <Finite additivity> of <Lebesgue measure> gives
$$
\boxed{m(C_n)=2^n\frac{q_n}{2^n}=q_n.}
$$
Their endpoints do not cause overlaps, because the central gaps have strictly positive length.

The points belonging to infinitely many $E_n$ form their <limit superior of sets>:
$$
G=\bigcap_{N\geq1}\bigcup_{n\geq N}E_n.
$$
For every $N$, <countable subadditivity> gives
$$
0\leq m(G)\leq\sum_{n\geq N}m(E_n)\leq\sum_{n\geq N}2^{-n}=2^{1-N}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus $\boxed{m(G)=0}$. Outside this <null set>, each point belongs to only finitely many $E_n$, so its <indicator functions> are eventually zero. Therefore $\boxed{\chi_{E_n}(x)\to0\text{ almost everywhere}}$. This is the summable-measure argument behind the first <Borel-Cantelli lemma>; no <independence> assumption is needed.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

By part <3/b/i>, $\chi_{E_n}|g|\to0$ <almost everywhere>, while $0\leq\chi_{E_n}|g|\leq|g|$ and $g$ is <Lebesgue integrable>. The <dominated convergence theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\int_{E_n}|g(x)|\,dx\longrightarrow0.}
$$
To deduce <absolute continuity of the Lebesgue integral>, suppose the stated uniform conclusion failed for some $\varepsilon_0>0$. For each $n$ one could choose a <measurable set> $A_n$ with $m(A_n)<2^{-n}$ but $\int_{A_n}|g|\geq\varepsilon_0$. Applying the result just proved to this <sequence> contradicts the lower bound. Hence
$$
\boxed{\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0:\quad m(A)<\delta\Longrightarrow\int_A|g|<\varepsilon}
$$
for every <measurable set> $A$.

= Solution
{parent=c}
{c}

The a.e. limit $f$ is nonnegative. For a fixed <measurable set> $A$, <Fatou's lemma> gives $\int_Af\leq\liminf_n\int_Af_n$. Apply the same lemma to $A^c$ and use convergence of the total integrals:
$$
\limsup_n\int_Af_n
=\int_{\mathbb R}f-\liminf_n\int_{A^c}f_n
\leq\int_{\mathbb R}f-\int_{A^c}f=\int_Af.
$$
The lower and upper bounds prove $\boxed{\int_Af_n\to\int_Af}$.

For the asserted <L1 convergence>, choose an <integer> $k$ so large that $\int_{|x|>k}f<\varepsilon$, possible by <Lebesgue integrability>. The first result applied to this fixed tail <set> makes $\int_{|x|>k}f_n<2\varepsilon$ eventually. The additional local hypothesis makes $\int_{-k}^k|f_n-f|<\varepsilon$ eventually. Therefore
$$
\int_{\mathbb R}|f_n-f|
\leq\int_{-k}^k|f_n-f|+\int_{|x|>k}f_n+\int_{|x|>k}f<4\varepsilon.
$$
Thus $\boxed{\|f_n-f\|_{L^1(\mathbb R)}\to0}$.

In fact the extra local hypothesis is unnecessary here. <Scheffé's lemma> follows directly from $\min\{f_n,f\}\to f$ <almost everywhere> and domination by $f$:
$$
\|f_n-f\|_1=\int f_n+\int f-2\int\min\{f_n,f\}\longrightarrow0.
$$
This stronger argument uses precisely the already assumed nonnegativity, a.e. convergence, and convergence of total integrals.
