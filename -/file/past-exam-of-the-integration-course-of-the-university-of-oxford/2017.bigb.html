=== a

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

If $\sum_nm^*(A_n)=\infty$, the required inequality is automatic. Otherwise each $A_n$ has finite <Lebesgue outer measure>. Fix $\varepsilon>0$. By the defining <infimum>, for each $n\geq1$ choose a countable <interval> cover $\{J_{n,k}:k\geq1\}$ with
$$
\sum_{k\geq1}|J_{n,k}|<m^*(A_n)+\varepsilon2^{-n}.
$$
The family indexed by $(n,k)\in\mathbb N^2$ is countable and covers $\bigcup_nA_n$. Since all lengths are nonnegative, its total length is at most $\sum_nm^*(A_n)+\varepsilon$. Taking the infimum over covers of the union and then letting $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ gives
$$
\boxed{m^*\!\left(\bigcup_{n\geq1}A_n\right)\leq\sum_{n\geq1}m^*(A_n).}
$$
This proves <countable subadditivity of Lebesgue outer measure> directly from interval covers, without assuming that any $A_n$ is measurable.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

If the <intervals> $J_k$ cover $A$, their translates $J_k+a$ cover $A+a$ and have the same lengths. Every candidate total length in the definition of $m^*(A)$ is therefore also a candidate for $m^*(A+a)$. Taking <infima> gives $m^*(A+a)\leq m^*(A)$. Apply the same argument to $A+a$ and the translation $-a$ to obtain the reverse inequality. Hence
$$
\boxed{m^*(A+a)=m^*(A).}
$$
This <translation invariance of Lebesgue outer measure> holds for every <subset> $A$, including non-measurable sets and sets of infinite outer measure.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

A <subset> $E\subseteq\mathbb R$ is a <Lebesgue measurable set> when it satisfies <Carathéodory's criterion>: for every $T\subseteq\mathbb R$,
$$
m^*(T)=m^*(T\cap E)+m^*(T\setminus E).
$$
Suppose $m^*(A)=0$ and let $B\subseteq A$, allowing $B=A$. The <monotonicity of Lebesgue outer measure> follows directly from covers: every cover of a larger set covers a smaller set. Thus $m^*(B)=m^*(T\cap B)=0$, while $m^*(T\setminus B)\leq m^*(T)$. Consequently
$$
m^*(T\cap B)+m^*(T\setminus B)\leq m^*(T).
$$
The reverse inequality is <countable subadditivity of Lebesgue outer measure> applied to $T=(T\cap B)\cup(T\setminus B)$. Equality follows, even if $m^*(T)=\infty$. Therefore **$A$ and every <subset> $B\subseteq A$ are <Lebesgue measurable sets> of measure zero**. This is the <completeness of Lebesgue measure>, obtained without presupposing measurability of the outer-null set.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For a <Lebesgue measurable function> $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$, the <preimage> $f^{-1}(U)$ of every open <set> $U$ is a <Lebesgue measurable set>. For a <Borel measurable function>, each such preimage must instead be a <Borel set>. Equivalently, both definitions can test all Borel target sets: the collection
$$
\mathcal C_f=\{B\subseteq\mathbb R:f^{-1}(B)\text{ belongs to the source sigma-algebra}\}
$$
is a <sigma-algebra>, since preimages preserve complements and countable unions. Containing all open sets therefore makes it contain the <Borel sigma-algebra>.

Now let $U$ be open. Since $h$ is a <Borel measurable function>, $h^{-1}(U)$ is Borel. Since $g$ is a <Lebesgue measurable function>, $g^{-1}(h^{-1}(U))$ is a Lebesgue measurable set. But
$$
(h\circ g)^{-1}(U)=g^{-1}(h^{-1}(U)).
$$
Thus **$h\circ g$ is a <Lebesgue measurable function>**. The intermediate sigma-algebra is Borel; this is why <Borel postcomposition preserves Lebesgue measurability>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <dominated convergence theorem> says that if measurable $f_n$ converge <almost everywhere> to $f$ and $|f_n|\leq H$ almost everywhere for a single <Lebesgue integrable function> $H\geq0$, then $f$ is integrable and
$$
\int|f_n-f|\longrightarrow0,\qquad \int f_n\longrightarrow\int f.
$$
Here, for $x>0$, put $H_0(x)=x^{-1/2}e^{-x}$ and $f_n(x)=H_0(x)(1-(-1)^ne^{-nx})$. The <exponential function> gives $e^{-nx}\to0$ at every such $x$, so $f_n\to H_0$. Also $|f_n(x)|\leq2H_0(x)$. This dominator is integrable: on $(0,1)$, $H_0(x)\leq x^{-1/2}$, whose integral is two; on $[1,\infty)$, $H_0(x)\leq e^{-x}$, whose integral is finite. DCT therefore proves the required limit.

The limiting value can also be evaluated. Substitute $x=t^2$ and use the <Gaussian integral>:
$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^\infty f_n(x)\,dx
=\int_0^\infty x^{-1/2}e^{-x}\,dx=\sqrt\pi.}
$$
In fact, scaling the second exponential term gives the exact integral $\sqrt\pi\,[1-(-1)^n/\sqrt{n+1}]$. The integral error decays as $n^{-1/2}$ despite exponential convergence at each fixed positive $x$; the slow part lies near the endpoint zero.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $\phi:I\to J$ be a $C^1$ <diffeomorphism> between <intervals>, which may be unbounded. For a nonnegative <Lebesgue measurable function> $f$ on $J$, <Lebesgue substitution on an interval> gives
$$
\boxed{\int_J f(y)\,dy=\int_I f(\phi(x))|\phi'(x)|\,dx,}
$$
allowing the value infinity. The same identity applied to $|f|$ shows that $f$ is <Lebesgue integrable> exactly when the transformed integrand is integrable; in that case the displayed formula holds for signed or complex $f$ as well. The absolute value on the <derivative> makes the formula valid for both increasing and decreasing substitutions. Finite endpoints can be omitted without changing a <Lebesgue integral>, because individual points are <null sets>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Both functions are continuous on the positive domains in question, so they are measurable. For the first function, the decreasing substitution $t=1/x$ and $|dx/dt|=t^{-2}$ give
$$
\int_0^1\left|\frac{\sin(1/x)}x\right|\,dx
=\int_1^\infty\frac{|\sin t|}{t}\,dt.
$$
For every integer $k\geq1$,
$$
\int_{k\pi}^{(k+1)\pi}\frac{|\sin t|}{t}\,dt
\geq\frac1{(k+1)\pi}\int_{k\pi}^{(k+1)\pi}|\sin t|\,dt
=\frac{2}{(k+1)\pi}.
$$
Summing this lower bound gives a divergent <harmonic series>. Thus **$f$ is not <Lebesgue integrable> on $(0,1)$**.

For the second function, $|\sin x|\leq x$ on $(0,1)$ and $|\sin x|\leq1$ everywhere give
$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{|\sin x|}{x^{5/4}}\,dx
\leq\int_0^1x^{-1/4}\,dx+\int_1^\infty x^{-5/4}\,dx
=\frac43+4<\infty.
$$
Therefore **$g$ is <Lebesgue integrable> on $(0,\infty)$**. The <sine power kernel integrability> threshold is $1<p<2$: one endpoint requires $p<2$, and the other requires $p>1$. The signed improper integral for $f$ does converge by the <Dirichlet test for improper integrals>, but conditional improper convergence does not give a finite integral of $|f|$.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <monotone convergence theorem> states that, for nonnegative measurable $f_n$ on a <Lebesgue measurable set> $E$ with $f_n\uparrow f$ <almost everywhere>,
$$
\int_Ef_n\uparrow\int_Ef,
$$
allowing infinity. <Fatou's lemma> states that, for nonnegative measurable $f_n$,
$$
\int_E\liminf_nf_n\leq\liminf_n\int_Ef_n.
$$
Now write $C=\sup_n\int_E|g_n|<\infty$. A pointwise <limit superior> of measurable functions is measurable, and it agrees with $g$ off the exceptional <null set>. By <completeness of Lebesgue measure>, assigning any values on that null set preserves measurability. Therefore $g$ is measurable. Since $|g_n|\to|g|$ almost everywhere, Fatou's lemma gives
$$
\boxed{\int_E|g|\leq\liminf_n\int_E|g_n|\leq C<\infty.}
$$
Thus $g$ is finite almost everywhere and is a <Lebesgue integrable function>. This is an <almost-everywhere limit under a uniform L1 bound>. The argument proves integrability of the limit, not convergence in <L1 space>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== A
{c}
{scope}

====== Solution

Let $S_N=\sum_{n=1}^N|a_n|$. These are nonnegative measurable functions with $S_N\uparrow S=\sum_{n\geq1}|a_n|$. The <monotone convergence theorem> and linearity of the finite sum give
$$
\int_ES=\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_ES_N
=\sum_{n\geq1}\int_E|a_n|<\infty.
$$
Hence $S<\infty$ <almost everywhere>: if $S=\infty$ on a set of positive measure, the integral would exceed every finite constant. Also each $a_n$ is finite almost everywhere, since its absolute integral is finite. Outside the union of these countably many <null sets>, all terms are real and
$$
\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^\infty|a_n(x)|<\infty.}
$$
Thus the signed series converges absolutely almost everywhere, as asserted by <summable L1 norms imply almost-everywhere absolute convergence>. Removing the common null set avoids any undefined $+\infty-\infty$ in signed partial sums.

===== B
{c}
{scope}

====== Solution

Let $A(x)=\sum_{n\geq1}a_n(x)$ on the full-measure set where part (A) gives absolute convergence, and set $A=0$ on its null complement. After similarly replacing the individual terms by zero on that common <null set>, the partial sums are finite measurable functions and converge to $A$. Hence $A$ is a <Lebesgue measurable function>. The <triangle inequality> gives
$$
|A(x)|\leq\sum_{n\geq1}|a_n(x)|=S(x)
$$
almost everywhere. Part (A) proved $S$ integrable, so
$$
\boxed{\int_E|A|\leq\sum_{n\geq1}\int_E|a_n|<\infty.}
$$
Therefore the summed function is <Lebesgue integrable>. Its values on the exceptional null set do not affect the integral. This is the integrability step in <integration of a series with summable L1 norms>.

===== C
{c}
{scope}

====== Solution

Let $A_N=\sum_{n=1}^Na_n$, using the finite representatives from part (B). Then $A_N\to A$ <almost everywhere>, and $|A_N|\leq S=\sum_n|a_n|$, where $S$ is integrable by part (A). The <dominated convergence theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\int_E\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n(x)\,dx
=\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_EA_N
=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\int_Ea_n(x)\,dx.}
$$
The numerical series on the right converges absolutely because $|\int_Ea_n|\leq\int_E|a_n|$ and the sum of these bounds is finite. Moreover,
$$
\|A-A_N\|_1\leq\sum_{n>N}\int_E|a_n|\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus <integration of a series with summable L1 norms> also yields convergence of the partial sums in <L1 norm>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the <power series> of the <exponential function>:
$$
\frac{e^{-x^2}}{\sqrt x}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n(x),\qquad
a_n(x)=\frac{(-1)^n x^{2n-1/2}}{n!},\quad0<x<1.
$$
Each term is a <Lebesgue integrable function>, and
$$
\int_0^1|a_n(x)|\,dx
=\frac{1}{n!(2n+1/2)}=\frac{2}{(4n+1)n!}.
$$
The sum of these nonnegative integrals is at most $2\sum_{n\geq0}1/n!=2e<\infty$. Applying <integration of a series with summable L1 norms>, just proved in part (ii), therefore justifies exchanging the series and the integral:
$$
\boxed{\int_0^1\frac{e^{-x^2}}{\sqrt x}\,dx
=2\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n}{(4n+1)n!}.}
$$
The absolute-integral bound is the justification; pointwise convergence of the exponential series alone would not suffice.

==== i

===== Solution

A sufficient theorem is <dominated differentiation under the integral sign>. Let $t_0$ be interior to a parameter interval, suppose $u(\cdot,t)$ is measurable for each $t$, and suppose $u(\cdot,t_0)$ is integrable. Assume $u(x,\cdot)$ is differentiable near $t_0$ for $x$ outside one fixed <null set>, and that throughout this neighborhood
$$
|\partial_tu(x,t)|\leq H(x)
$$
for one nonnegative <Lebesgue integrable function> $H$. Then $F(t)=\int_Eu(x,t)\,dx$ is defined near $t_0$, is differentiable at $t_0$, and
$$
\boxed{F'(t_0)=\int_E\partial_tu(x,t_0)\,dx.}
$$
Indeed the <mean value theorem> bounds both $|u(x,t)-u(x,t_0)|$ by $|t-t_0|H(x)$ and the difference quotient by $H(x)$. The first bound proves local integrability; the second lets the <dominated convergence theorem> pass the limit of difference quotients through the integral.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Fix $t\in\mathbb R$. The <mean value theorem> for the exponential gives
$$
|e^{tx}-1|\leq |t|x e^{|t|},\qquad0<x<1.
$$
Consequently
$$
|u(x,t)|\leq\frac{|t|e^{|t|}}{\sqrt{1-x}},\qquad
\int_0^1\frac{|t|e^{|t|}}{\sqrt{1-x}}\,dx=2|t|e^{|t|}<\infty.
$$
The function is continuous on $(0,1)$ and hence measurable, so **$u(\cdot,t)$ is <Lebesgue integrable> for every real $t$**. In particular, the numerator cancels the apparent $1/x$ singularity at zero: $u(x,t)\to t$ as $x\downarrow0$. Only the integrable square-root singularity at one remains. At $t=0$ the function is identically zero.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Fix any real $t_0$ and restrict $t$ to $|t-t_0|<1$. The parameter derivative is
$$
\partial_tu(x,t)=\frac{e^{tx}}{\sqrt{1-x}},\qquad
|\partial_tu(x,t)|\leq\frac{e^{|t_0|+1}}{\sqrt{1-x}}.
$$
This bound is integrable on $(0,1)$ and is uniform throughout the parameter neighborhood. Part (ii) supplied integrability at $t_0$, so <dominated differentiation under the integral sign> proves differentiability there. As $t_0$ was arbitrary,
$$
\boxed{F'(t)=\int_0^1\frac{e^{tx}}{\sqrt{1-x}}\,dx\quad(t\in\mathbb R).}
$$
For $t>0$, $e^{tx}\geq1$; for $t<0$, $e^{tx}\leq1$. Since $\int_0^1(1-x)^{-1/2}\,dx=2$, these comparisons give
$$
\boxed{F'(t)\geq2\ (t>0),\qquad F'(t)\leq2\ (t<0),\qquad F'(0)=2.}
$$
The first two inequalities are actually strict, because the exponential differs strictly from one throughout $(0,1)$. Differentiating once more under the same type of bound gives $F''(t)=\int_0^1xe^{tx}/\sqrt{1-x}\,dx>0$. Hence $F$ is <strictly convex> and lies above its tangent $2t$ at zero. These properties are collected in <exponential integral with a square-root endpoint singularity>.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

On <sigma-finite measure> spaces, including the real line with <Lebesgue measure>, <Tonelli's theorem> says that a nonnegative measurable function $H$ has equal product and iterated integrals:
$$
\int_{X\times Y}H\,d(\mu\times\nu)
=\int_X\left(\int_YH(x,y)\,d\nu(y)\right)d\mu(x)
=\int_Y\left(\int_XH(x,y)\,d\mu(x)\right)d\nu(y),
$$
with infinity allowed. Finiteness is not a prerequisite.

<Fubini's theorem> says that if a measurable signed or complex $H$ is absolutely integrable on the product, then its sections are integrable for <almost every> value of the other variable, the iterated integral functions are integrable, and the same equalities hold with finite values. For completed Lebesgue measure, exceptional sections need not themselves be measurable; the integrals use the almost-everywhere defined sections. Thus Tonelli can first establish absolute integrability using $|H|$, after which Fubini permits changing the order for $H$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Because $f$ is <continuous>, $g(x,y)=e^{-x^2}f(e^{-x^2/2}y)$ is continuous on $\mathbb R^2$, so $|g|$ is a nonnegative measurable function. Apply <Tonelli's theorem> before assuming integrability. For each fixed $x$, set $z=e^{-x^2/2}y$, so $dy=e^{x^2/2}\,dz$. <Lebesgue substitution on an interval> gives
$$
\int_{\mathbb R}|g(x,y)|\,dy
=e^{-x^2/2}\int_{\mathbb R}|f(z)|\,dz.
$$
Therefore the <Gaussian integral> yields
$$
\boxed{\iint_{\mathbb R^2}|g(x,y)|\,dx\,dy
=\sqrt{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}|f(z)|\,dz<\infty.}
$$
This proves that $g$ is <Lebesgue integrable> on $\mathbb R^2$. <Fubini's theorem> now permits changing the order for $g$ itself. The same substitution without absolute values gives the stronger exact identity
$$
\int_{\mathbb R}\int_{\mathbb R}g(x,y)\,dx\,dy
=\sqrt{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}f(z)\,dz.
$$
Taking absolute values and using the <triangle inequality for an integral> proves
$$
\boxed{\left|\int_{\mathbb R}\int_{\mathbb R}g(x,y)\,dx\,dy\right|
\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}|f(z)|\,dz.}
$$
The constant is sharp: any nonzero nonnegative continuous integrable $f$ gives equality. This is a <weighted dilation kernel> with $a(x)=e^{-x^2}$ and $b(x)=e^{-x^2/2}$. The inner $x$ integral in the requested order is guaranteed to be integrable for almost every $y$, which is all the iterated integral requires.
