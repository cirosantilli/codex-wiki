= Solution

A <measurable space> is a pair $(\Omega,\mathcal F)$ where $\mathcal F$ is a <sigma-algebra> of <subsets> of $\Omega$: it contains $\Omega$, is closed under <complements> relative to $\Omega$, and is closed under <countable unions>. A <measure> on it is a <function> $\mu:\mathcal F\to[0,\infty]$ with $\mu(\varnothing)=0$ and <countable additivity>:
$$
\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(A_n)
$$
whenever the <measurable sets> $A_n$ are pairwise <disjoint>. Infinite values are allowed. The triple $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mu)$ is a <measure space>.

Because $E\in\mathcal F$, the family $\mathcal F_E$ contains $E$. If $A\in\mathcal F_E$, then $E\setminus A=E\cap(\Omega\setminus A)$ belongs to $\mathcal F$ and is a <subset> of $E$, so it belongs to $\mathcal F_E$. If each $A_n\in\mathcal F_E$, their <countable union> belongs to $\mathcal F$ and remains a <subset> of $E$. Thus **$\mathcal F_E$ is a <sigma-algebra> on $E$**, with relative <complements> taken in $E$, not in $\Omega$.

Equivalently,
$$
\mathcal F_E=\{E\cap B:B\in\mathcal F\},
$$
the <trace sigma-algebra> on the <measurable set> $E$. The measurability of $E$ is what makes this equal to the family in the question.

The <function> $\nu$ takes values in $[0,\infty]$ and has $\nu(\varnothing)=0$. For any pairwise <disjoint> $A_n\in\mathcal F_E$, <countable additivity> of $\mu$ gives
$$
\nu\left(\bigcup_nA_n\right)=\mu\left(\bigcup_nA_n\right)
=\sum_n\mu(A_n)=\sum_n\nu(A_n).
$$
Therefore **$\nu$ is a <measure> on $(E,\mathcal F_E)$**: it is the <restriction of a measure to a measurable subset>.

For the increasing family, define the <measurable sets>
$$
D_1=A_1,\qquad D_n=A_n\setminus A_{n-1}\quad(n\geq2).
$$
They are pairwise <disjoint>, with $A_N=\bigcup_{n=1}^ND_n$ and $\bigcup_nA_n=\bigcup_nD_n$. By <countable additivity>,
$$
\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_n\right)
=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mu(D_n)
=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=1}^N\mu(D_n)
=\lim_{N\to\infty}\mu(A_N).
$$
The nonnegative <series> is defined by its increasing partial-sum <limit>, so this proof also covers an infinite value. This is <continuity from below of a measure>.

For the decreasing family, put $C_n=B_1\setminus B_n$. These <measurable sets> increase, and
$$
\bigcup_nC_n=B_1\setminus\bigcap_nB_n.
$$
Since $\mu(B_1)<\infty$, <finite additivity> gives legitimate finite subtractions:
$$
\mu(C_n)=\mu(B_1)-\mu(B_n),\qquad
\mu\left(B_1\setminus\bigcap_nB_n\right)=\mu(B_1)-\mu\left(\bigcap_nB_n\right).
$$
Apply the first result to $C_n$ and subtract from $\mu(B_1)$ to obtain
$$
\boxed{\mu\left(\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}B_n\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu(B_n).}
$$
This is <continuity from above of a measure>. Its finiteness hypothesis matters: for <Lebesgue measure> and $B_n=[n,\infty)$, every $B_n$ has infinite <measure> but their <intersection> is empty. The proof must not subtract $\infty-\infty$.

For $x<y$, <finite additivity> on the <disjoint> pieces of the half-line gives
$$
h(y)-h(x)=m\bigl(E\cap(x,y]\bigr).
$$
By <monotonicity of a measure> and the <Lebesgue measure> of an <interval>,
$$
0\leq h(y)-h(x)\leq m((x,y])=y-x.
$$
Exchanging the two points if necessary proves
$$
\boxed{|h(y)-h(x)|\leq|y-x|\qquad(x,y\in\mathbb R).}
$$
Thus the <cumulative measure of a finite-measure set> is a <Lipschitz function> with <Lipschitz constant> at most one, hence is **<uniformly continuous>**: given $\varepsilon>0$, the choice $\delta=\varepsilon$ works simultaneously at every point.

To locate its endpoint <limits>, the <measurable sets> $E\cap(-\infty,N]$ increase to $E$, so <continuity from below of a measure> gives $h(N)\to m(E)$. The <measurable sets> $E\cap(-\infty,-N]$ decrease to the <empty set> and all have finite <measure>, so <continuity from above of a measure> gives $h(-N)\to0$. Since $h$ is nondecreasing,
$$
\lim_{x\to-\infty}h(x)=0,\qquad
\lim_{x\to\infty}h(x)=m(E).
$$
For $0<c<m(E)$, choose $R>0$ with $h(-R)<c<h(R)$. The <intermediate value theorem> on $[-R,R]$ gives $t$ with $h(t)=c$. Then
$$
\boxed{A=E\cap(-\infty,t]\text{ is Lebesgue measurable and }m(A)=c.}
$$
The endpoint values $c=0,m(E)$ can also be achieved by $A=\varnothing,E$. This proves <divisibility of Lebesgue measure> for arbitrary finite-measure <Lebesgue measurable sets> on the <real line>, including unbounded ones. The <non-atomic measure> structure permits prescribed fractions of <measure>; a <measure> concentrated at one point would not have this property.

The <function> is continuous for $x>0$, hence <Lebesgue measurable>. Near zero, the bound $|\sin x|\leq x$ gives
$$
\int_0^1\left|\frac{\sin x}{x^q}\right|\,dx
\leq\int_0^1x^{1-q}\,dx=\frac{1}{2-q}<\infty\qquad(0<q<2).
$$
Thus **$f$ is <Lebesgue integrable> on $(0,1]$ for $q<2$**. The convergence is an instance of <power singularity integrability>.

For the tail, take the pairwise <disjoint> <intervals>
$$
I_k=\left[k\pi+\frac\pi6,\ k\pi+\frac{5\pi}6\right],\qquad k\geq1.
$$
On each $I_k$, $|\sin x|\geq1/2$, $x\leq(k+1)\pi$, and the interval length is $2\pi/3$. Therefore
$$
\int_{I_k}\frac{|\sin x|}{x^q}\,dx
\geq\frac{\pi}{3}\bigl((k+1)\pi\bigr)^{-q}
=\frac{\pi^{1-q}}{3(k+1)^q}.
$$
Summing the nonnegative <integrals> over these <disjoint> <intervals> yields
$$
\int_1^\infty\frac{|\sin x|}{x^q}\,dx
\geq\frac{\pi^{1-q}}3\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac1{(k+1)^q}=\infty
\qquad(0<q\leq1),
$$
by divergence of the <p-series>. Hence **$f$ is not <Lebesgue integrable> on $[1,\infty)$ for $0<q\leq1$**.

The test is <absolute integrability>: oscillatory sign cancellation does not make the absolute-value <integral> finite. A conditionally convergent <improper integral> is a different notion. The <sine power kernel integrability> criterion separates these endpoint thresholds.

= Solution

Define
$$
H(x)=\frac{e^{-x}}{\sqrt x}\qquad(x>0).
$$
This is a <Lebesgue integrable function>: on $(0,1]$ it is bounded by $x^{-1/2}$, whose <integral> is two, while on $[1,\infty)$ it is bounded by $e^{-x}$, whose <integral> is $e^{-1}$. Since $|\sin(xy)|\leq x|y|$,
$$
|h_y(x)|\leq|y|H(x).
$$
Thus **$h_y$ is <Lebesgue integrable> for every $y\geq0$**, including $h_0=0$; the same argument actually works for all real $y$.

The <mean value theorem> for the <sine> gives $|\sin u-\sin v|\leq|u-v|$. Hence
$$
|h_y(x)-h_z(x)|\leq|y-z|H(x)
$$
and the <triangle inequality for an integral> gives
$$
\boxed{|F(y)-F(z)|\leq\left(\int_0^\infty H(x)\,dx\right)|y-z|.}
$$
Thus $F$ is a <globally Lipschitz function>, in particular **continuous on $[0,\infty)$**, including the endpoint zero.

For the <derivative>, fix $y>0$ and take nonzero $t$ sufficiently small that $y+t>0$. The difference quotient has the <integrand>
$$
D_t(x)=\frac{e^{-x}}{x\sqrt x}\frac{\sin(x(y+t))-\sin(xy)}{t}.
$$
For every $x>0$, $D_t(x)\to H(x)\cos(xy)$ as $t\to0$. The same <mean value theorem> gives the uniform-in-$t$ bound $|D_t(x)|\leq H(x)$.

The <dominated convergence theorem> states that if <measurable functions> $u_n$ converge <almost everywhere> to $u$ and $|u_n|\leq G$ for a single <Lebesgue integrable function> $G$, then $u$ is <Lebesgue integrable> and $\int|u_n-u|\to0$, hence $\int u_n\to\int u$. Apply it to the difference quotients along every sequence $t_n\to0$, using the common dominator $H$. The resulting <limit> is independent of the sequence, so
$$
\boxed{F'(y)=\int_0^\infty e^{-x}\frac{\cos(xy)}{\sqrt x}\,dx\qquad(y>0).}
$$
The right-hand <integral> is absolutely convergent because its <integrand> has absolute value at most $H$. This is <dominated differentiation under the integral sign>, justified by an explicit integrable bound rather than a formal interchange.

For every fixed $x>0$, $\log(x+n)/n\to0$, so $f_n(x)\to0$. To exchange the <limit> with the <Lebesgue integral>, we need a common integrable bound over the whole half-line.

For $n\geq1$, $x+n\leq n(1+x)$, so
$$
0\leq\frac{\log(x+n)}n
\leq\frac{\log n}{n}+\frac{\log(1+x)}n
\leq1+\log(1+x).
$$
Together with $|\sin x|\leq\min(x,1)$, this gives
$$
|f_n(x)|\leq G(x):=\bigl(1+\log(1+x)\bigr)\frac{\min(x,1)}{x^q}.
$$
For $0<x\leq1$, $\log(1+x)\leq x\leq1$, hence $G(x)\leq2x^{1-q}$, which is integrable because $q<2$. For $x\geq1$,
$$
G(x)\leq\frac{1+\log2+\log x}{x^q}.
$$
Both terms are integrable because $q>1$:
$$
\int_1^\infty x^{-q}\,dx=\frac1{q-1},\qquad
\int_1^\infty\frac{\log x}{x^q}\,dx=\frac1{(q-1)^2}.
$$
The second equality follows from <integration by parts>, or the <substitution> $x=e^t$. Consequently $G$ is a <Lebesgue integrable function>, and every $f_n$ is <Lebesgue integrable>.

The <dominated convergence theorem> states that <almost everywhere> convergence of <measurable functions> under one integrable absolute-value bound implies <L1 convergence>, and in particular convergence of their <integrals>. Here the pointwise <limit> is zero and the dominator is $G$, so
$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_0^\infty f_n(x)\,dx=0.}
$$
In fact we obtain the stronger conclusion $\|f_n\|_1\to0$. A quantitative version follows by retaining the factors $1/n$ in the bound:
$$
\|f_n\|_1\leq\frac{A\log n+B}{n},
\quad
A=\int_0^\infty\frac{|\sin x|}{x^q}\,dx,
\quad
B=\int_0^\infty\frac{\log(1+x)|\sin x|}{x^q}\,dx.
$$
The preceding endpoint estimates show $A,B<\infty$, so the <L1 norm> is $O(\log n/n)$ as $n\to\infty$, with $O$ denoting <Big O notation>. This is <logarithmic reweighting under dominated convergence>.

Fix $y\ne0$. The <function> $x\mapsto f(x,y)$ is continuous on $[-1,1]$, because its denominator is bounded away from zero, and it is an <odd function> of $x$. Its <integral> over this symmetric <interval> is therefore zero. At $y=0$ the entire section is zero, including the specified value at the origin. Thus the inner $x$ <integral> exists for every $y$ and is identically zero. The same reasoning with $x$ and $y$ exchanged gives
$$
\boxed{\int_{-1}^1\left(\int_{-1}^1f(x,y)\,dx\right)dy
=\int_{-1}^1\left(\int_{-1}^1f(x,y)\,dy\right)dx=0.}
$$
To test <Lebesgue integrability> in two dimensions, examine the <absolute value> near the origin. For $0<\varepsilon<1$, the annulus $\varepsilon<\sqrt{x^2+y^2}<1$ lies inside the square. In <polar coordinates>,
$$
|f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)|=\frac{|\cos\theta\sin\theta|}{r^4},\qquad dx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\theta.
$$
The <change of variables formula> on this annulus, where the <integrand> has no singularity, gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{[-1,1]^2}|f(x,y)|\,dx\,dy
&\geq\int_\varepsilon^1r^{-3}\,dr\int_0^{2\pi}|\cos\theta\sin\theta|\,d\theta\\
&=\frac{\varepsilon^{-2}-1}{2}\cdot2
=\varepsilon^{-2}-1.
\end{aligned}
$$
The lower bound tends to infinity as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\int_{[-1,1]^2}|f|=\infty,\qquad f\notin L^1([-1,1]^2).}
$$
Moreover reflection in either coordinate axis exchanges the <positive part> and <negative part> while preserving <Lebesgue measure>. Both parts therefore have infinite <integral>, so a signed double <Lebesgue integral> would involve the undefined expression $\infty-\infty$.

There is no contradiction with <Fubini's theorem>: its <absolute integrability> hypothesis is missing. This is an example of <finite iterated integrals without joint integrability>, with every individual one-dimensional section integrable despite the two-dimensional singularity.

= Solution

Define the one-dimensional <convolution kernel>
$$
k(t)=\begin{cases}\sin t/|t|^{3/2},&t\ne0,\\0,&t=0.\end{cases}
$$
It is a <Borel measurable function>, and $f(x,y)=k(x-y)g(y)$. The <function> $k(x-y)$ is <Borel measurable> by composition with the continuous difference map. Lifting the <Lebesgue measurable function> $g$ to the second coordinate preserves measurability for the <product sigma-algebra>, hence also for <Lebesgue measure> on $\mathbb R^2$. Products of <measurable functions> are measurable, so $f$ is <Lebesgue measurable>.

Near zero, $|\sin t|\leq|t|$; away from zero, $|\sin t|\leq1$. Consequently
$$
|k(t)|\leq\begin{cases}|t|^{-1/2},&0<|t|\leq1,\\|t|^{-3/2},&|t|>1.\end{cases}
$$
Both bounds are integrable on their respective regions by <power singularity integrability>. In particular,
$$
\boxed{\|k\|_1\leq2\int_0^1t^{-1/2}\,dt+2\int_1^\infty t^{-3/2}\,dt=4+4=8.}
$$

We use the following forms of the two product-integration theorems. On the product of <measure spaces> equipped with <sigma-finite measures>, <Tonelli's theorem> says that a nonnegative measurable <function> has equal product and iterated <integrals>, with infinity allowed. <Fubini's theorem> says that for an absolutely integrable measurable <function>, its sections are integrable for <almost every> fixed coordinate, and both orders of iterated <integration> equal the finite product <integral>. The <Lebesgue measure> on each factor is a <sigma-finite measure>, because the <real line> is a <countable union> of finite-length <intervals>.

Apply <Tonelli's theorem> first to $|f|$, before presuming finiteness. By <translation invariance of Lebesgue measure>,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{\mathbb R^2}|f(x,y)|\,dx\,dy
&=\int_{\mathbb R}|g(y)|\left(\int_{\mathbb R}|k(x-y)|\,dx\right)dy\\
&=\|k\|_1\int_{\mathbb R}|g(y)|\,dy
\leq8\int_{\mathbb R}|g(y)|\,dy<\infty.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus **$f$ is <Lebesgue integrable> on $\mathbb R^2$**. The <triangle inequality for an integral> now gives the requested estimate:
$$
\boxed{\left|\int_{\mathbb R^2}f(x,y)\,dx\,dy\right|
\leq\int_{\mathbb R^2}|f(x,y)|\,dx\,dy
\leq8\int_{\mathbb R}|g(y)|\,dy.}
$$
This is the <L1 norm identity for a translated kernel product> followed by a simple bound on the <convolution kernel>.

There is also a stronger signed conclusion. The integrable <convolution kernel> $k$ is an <odd function>, so $\int_{\mathbb R}k(t)\,dt=0$. Having established <absolute integrability>, we may apply <Fubini's theorem> to $f$ itself:
$$
\boxed{\int_{\mathbb R^2}f(x,y)\,dx\,dy
=\left(\int_{\mathbb R}k(t)\,dt\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb R}g(y)\,dy\right)=0.}
$$
Unlike part (a), the signed cancellation is justified by a finite <integral> of the <absolute value>. The constant eight is useful as an <L1 norm> bound even though this particular signed total vanishes.
