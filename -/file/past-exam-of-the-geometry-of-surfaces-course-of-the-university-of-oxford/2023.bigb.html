= Solution

The <classification of compact surfaces> says that every <compact>, <connected> <topological surface> without <boundary> is <homeomorphic> to exactly one of the following: the <sphere> $S^2$; a <connected sum of surfaces> consisting of $g\geq1$ <tori>; or a <connected sum of surfaces> consisting of $k\geq1$ <real projective planes>. The first two cases are <orientable>; the last case is a <nonorientable surface>. Their <Euler characteristics> are
$$
\boxed{\chi(S^2)=2,\qquad\chi(\Sigma_g)=2-2g,\qquad\chi(N_k)=2-k.}
$$
Here $\Sigma_g$ has <genus> $g$, and $N_k$ has $k$ crosscaps; $N_1$ is the <real projective plane> and $N_2$ the <Klein bottle>. Thus <orientability> together with the <Euler characteristic> determines the closed <surface> up to <homeomorphism>.

If the convention permits a <surface with boundary>, also remove the interiors of $b\geq0$ disjoint <closed discs> from one of these closed <surfaces>. The <Euler characteristic> decreases by $b$, giving $2-2g-b$ in the <orientable> case and $2-k-b$ in the <nonorientable surface> case. The number of <boundary> components is then an additional invariant.

Number the <polygon vertices> $v_0,\ldots,v_9$ in the order of the given <polygonal schema>, with its $i$th <polygon edge> going from $v_i$ to $v_{i+1}$, and subscripts interpreted modulo ten. Equal labels identify equal arrow endpoints. The five pairings give
$$
\begin{array}{c|cc}
\text{label}&\text{first endpoints}&\text{second endpoints}\\
a_1&v_0\sim v_4&v_1\sim v_3\\
a_2&v_2\sim v_7&v_3\sim v_6\\
a_3&v_5\sim v_9&v_6\sim v_8\\
a_4&v_2\sim v_9&v_1\sim v_0\\
a_5&v_5\sim v_7&v_4\sim v_8
\end{array}
$$
Consequently there are two vertex <equivalence classes>,
$$
\{v_0,v_1,v_3,v_4,v_6,v_8\},\qquad\{v_2,v_5,v_7,v_9\}.
$$
The <quotient space> has one face, five edges and two vertices, so the <polygonal-schema Euler count> gives
$$
\boxed{\chi(X)=2-5+1=-2.}
$$
Each label occurs once with each sign. Hence every pairing reverses the direction induced by traversing the <boundary> of the <polygon>, so an <orientation of a surface> on its interior extends consistently across every glued edge. Thus $X$ is <orientable>. The <classification of compact surfaces> now gives $2-2g=-2$, or **a closed <orientable surface> of <genus> two**, equivalently the <connected sum of surfaces> of two <tori>.

Write $\theta_i$ for the internal <angle> at $v_i$, using the numbering in part (b), and use the <hyperbolic plane> with <Gaussian curvature> $-1$. The necessary and sufficient conditions are **equal <lengths> for each pair of equally labelled <polygon sides>, and total <angle> $2\pi$ at each vertex <equivalence class>**. Explicitly, the two <angle> conditions are
$$
\boxed{\theta_0+\theta_1+\theta_3+\theta_4+\theta_6+\theta_8=2\pi,\qquad
\theta_2+\theta_5+\theta_7+\theta_9=2\pi.}
$$
Necessity follows because the identification must preserve <arc length> along each seam, and a smooth interior point of a <Riemannian surface> has total <angle> $2\pi$. For sufficiency, equally long <hyperbolic geodesic> segments can be identified by <isometries>. Across their interiors, two half-disc <neighbourhoods> join to a hyperbolic disc. At an identified vertex, the incident sectors form a cyclic disc with total <angle> $2\pi$, so no <cone singularity of a surface metric> remains. These <local isometries> therefore extend the <Riemannian metric> smoothly everywhere. This is the <smooth hyperbolic polygon gluing criterion>.

The ten internal <angles> sum to $4\pi$. The <area of a hyperbolic geodesic polygon> consequently gives
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Area}(X)=(10-2)\pi-4\pi=4\pi.}
$$
Equivalently, the <Gauss-Bonnet theorem> gives $-\operatorname{Area}(X)=2\pi\chi(X)=-4\pi$.

The <uniformization theorem> says that every <simply connected> <Riemann surface> is <biholomorphic> to the <Riemann sphere>, the <complex plane>, or the <unit disc>. Its consequence for a <compact Riemann surface> of <genus> at least two is that the <universal cover> is the <unit disc>, equivalently the <hyperbolic plane>.

To explain the restriction, a spherical <universal cover> would make the <fundamental group> finite. Since a finite <covering map> multiplies <Euler characteristic>, $2=m(2-2g)$ is impossible for $g\geq2$. If the <universal cover> were the <complex plane>, its <deck transformations> would be <biholomorphisms> $z\mapsto az+b$. A nonidentity such map with $a\ne1$ has a fixed point, impossible for a <deck transformation>. Thus the <deck transformation group> would consist of translations. A <discrete subgroup> of translations with <compact> <quotient space> is a rank-two <Euclidean lattice>, whose <quotient space> is a <torus>, again incompatible with $g\geq2$.

On the <unit disc>, the <Poincare metric on a Riemann surface>
$$
ds^2=\frac{4|dz|^2}{(1-|z|^2)^2}
$$
has <Gaussian curvature> $-1$ and is invariant under every <biholomorphism> of the <unit disc>. It therefore descends through the <deck transformation group> to a <hyperbolic metric> on $X$. Its local expression is a positive multiple of $|dz|^2$ in every holomorphic <coordinate chart>, so it is compatible with the given <complex structure>. In fact, this <hyperbolic metric> is unique within that <conformal class>.

Take a hexagonal <polygonal schema> with <boundary> word
$$
\boxed{a\,a\,b\,b\,c\,c.}
$$
Identify equally labelled <polygon edges> in the indicated directions. The two consecutive occurrences of $a$ identify the first three vertices; the two $b$ edges extend this class to the next two vertices, and the $c$ edges include the remaining vertex. Thus all six <polygon vertices> become one, while the six edges become three and the interior is one face. Its <Euler characteristic> is
$$
\boxed{\chi(Y)=1-3+1=-1.}
$$
This is the usual <polygonal schema> for the <connected sum of surfaces> of three <real projective planes>. The same-sign edge pairs force a <nonorientable surface>, consistently with $\chi(N_3)=2-3=-1$. A closed <orientable surface> cannot have odd <Euler characteristic>.

Let $x\in X$ and choose holomorphic <coordinate charts> centred at $x$ and $f(x)$. A nonconstant <holomorphic map> has a local expansion $w\circ f(z)=c z^m+O(z^{m+1})$, where $c\ne0$ and $m\geq1$. Taking a local holomorphic $m$th root of the nonvanishing factor and changing the source coordinate gives the exact local form $w=z^m$. The integer
$$
\boxed{\nu_f(x)=m}
$$
is the <ramification index of a holomorphic map>. A <ramification point of a holomorphic map> is a source point with $\nu_f(x)>1$, equivalently where its <derivative> vanishes; a <branch value of a holomorphic map>, also called a branch point here, is the image of such a point in $Y$. These are different sets, one in the source and one in the target.

Every <fiber of a function> is finite: locally it is the zero set of a nonzero <holomorphic function>, so its points are isolated, and <compactness> prevents infinitely many such zeros without an accumulation point. The same argument applied to the local <derivatives> makes the set of <ramification points of a holomorphic map> finite. The <open mapping theorem> and <compactness> show that $f(X)$ is both open and closed in the <connected> target $Y$, hence $f$ is <surjective>.

For any $y\in Y$, the <degree of a holomorphic map> is
$$
\boxed{d=\deg f=\sum_{x\in f^{-1}(y)}\nu_f(x).}
$$
To see independence of $y$, choose disjoint small <coordinate charts> about its finitely many preimages. The local form $z\mapsto z^{\nu_f(x)}$ shows that nearby values have $\nu_f(x)$ preimages counted with multiplicity in the corresponding chart. <Compactness> excludes additional preimages outside these charts for sufficiently nearby values: a sequence of such preimages would have a limit in $f^{-1}(y)$. The displayed sum is therefore locally constant, hence constant on <connected> $Y$. Away from <branch values of a holomorphic map> it is the ordinary number of preimages, and $f$ is a $d$-sheeted <covering map> there.

The <Riemann-Hurwitz formula> is
$$
\boxed{\chi(X)=d\chi(Y)-\sum_{x\in X}(\nu_f(x)-1),}
$$
or, in terms of the two <genera>,
$$
\boxed{2g_X-2=d(2g_Y-2)+\sum_{x\in X}(\nu_f(x)-1).}
$$
For its proof, choose a <triangulation of a surface> on $Y$ containing every <branch value of a holomorphic map> among its vertices. Every open edge and open face has $d$ lifts, since its interior avoids <branch values of a holomorphic map>. The lifted cells give a finite <CW complex> structure on $X$. If $V_Y,E_Y,F_Y$ are the target counts and $R=\sum_x(\nu_f(x)-1)$, the source counts are
$$
E_X=dE_Y,\qquad F_X=dF_Y,\qquad
V_X=\sum_{y\text{ vertex}}\#f^{-1}(y)=dV_Y-R.
$$
Indeed, at a target vertex the deficit from $d$ distinct preimages is precisely $\sum_{x\mapsto y}(\nu_f(x)-1)$, and all nonzero contributions occur over vertices. Taking the alternating sum gives $\chi(X)=d(V_Y-E_Y+F_Y)-R$, as required. This is the <triangulation proof of the Riemann-Hurwitz formula>.

Every nonidentity <biholomorphism> in the finite <group> $G$ has only finitely many fixed points, by the <identity theorem on a Riemann surface> and <compactness>. Because the <group action> is a <faithful group action>, the union of these finite sets does not exhaust $X$. A point outside this union has trivial <stabilizer subgroup>, so its <group orbit> has $|G|$ points by the <orbit-stabilizer theorem>. The generic <fiber of a function> of the projection is such an <group orbit>, giving
$$
\boxed{\deg f=|G|.}
$$
More precisely, put $H=G_x$ and $m=|H|$. Differentiation at $x$ gives a <group homomorphism> $H\to\mathbb C^\times$. It is <injective>: if a nonidentity finite-order germ had the form $h(z)=z+a_kz^k+O(z^{k+1})$ with $k\geq2$ and $a_k\ne0$, then its $q$th iterate would begin $z+qa_kz^k$, so no positive iterate could be the identity. A germ equal to the identity gives the identity on $X$ by the <identity theorem on a Riemann surface>. Thus $H$ is a <cyclic group>, with derivative image the $m$th <roots of unity>.

For completeness, an explicit local coordinate linearizing $H$ is
$$
w(p)=\frac1m\sum_{h\in H}h'(x)^{-1}z(hp).
$$
It has $w'(x)=1$, and reindexing the sum gives $w(hp)=h'(x)w(p)$. Therefore a coordinate on the local <quotient space> is $w^m$. Other translates of a sufficiently small <neighbourhood> of $x$ are disjoint unless their translating element lies in $H$, so this local <quotient space> is also a chart for $X/G$. The <local cyclic quotient of a Riemann surface> consequently gives
$$
\boxed{\nu_f(x)=|G_x|.}
$$

In a <cyclic group> of <prime> order $p$, a <stabilizer subgroup> has order either one or $p$. The latter case consists exactly of points fixed by all of $G$. Thus the $n$ fixed points each have <ramification index of a holomorphic map> $p$, and every other point has index one. Applying the <Riemann-Hurwitz formula> with <degree of a holomorphic map> $p$ gives
$$
\boxed{2g-2=p(2h-2)+n(p-1).}
$$
Equivalently,
$$
\boxed{g=ph+\frac{(p-1)(n-2)}2.}
$$
The count $n$ concerns fixed points upstairs. Each is its own <group orbit>, so their images are also $n$ distinct <branch values of a holomorphic map>.

For a <free action of a group>, every <stabilizer subgroup> is trivial. Hence every <ramification index of a holomorphic map> is one, and the projection is an unramified <covering map> of <degree of a holomorphic map> $|G|$. The <Riemann-Hurwitz formula> reduces to
$$
\boxed{2g-2=|G|(2h-2),\qquad g-1=|G|(h-1).}
$$
This is also the multiplicativity of <Euler characteristic under a finite covering>.

The <dihedral group> here has order $2n$. Substituting $h=2$ into part (d) gives
$$
\boxed{g=2n+1.}
$$
In particular, $n=3$ requires <genus> seven. A geometric construction is to start with a round <solid torus> about the vertical axis, then drill $2n$ thin radial tunnels through its tube in the horizontal midplane, equally spaced at azimuths $k\pi/n$, $0\leq k<2n$. The tunnels run from the central opening to the exterior; their directions are horizontal and radial. Round all their rims in the same way, preserving the <dihedral symmetry>, and let $X$ be the <boundary> of the resulting solid.

\Image[2023-dihedral-free-action.png]
{title=A genus-seven surface with a free dihedral action: six radial bores contain the horizontal rotation axes}

Here is why the construction has exactly the required properties. Every nonidentity power of $\beta$ is a <rotation in three dimensions> fixing the vertical axis, which lies in the central opening and is disjoint from $X$. Each element $\alpha\beta^j$ is a half-turn about a horizontal line at azimuth $j\pi/n$, modulo $\pi$. The $n$ such lines meet the original <solid torus> in $2n$ short radial segments. Each segment lies strictly inside one drilled tunnel, so these axes are also disjoint from $X$. A nonidentity <rotation in three dimensions> fixes only its axis. Thus no nonidentity element fixes a point of $X$, proving a <free action of a group>.

Rotation by $2\pi/n$ permutes the tunnels, while $\alpha:(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,-y,-z)$ preserves their arrangement and the original <solid torus>. Hence $X$ is preserved by the two generators and therefore by $G$. The tunnels can be chosen sufficiently thin to be pairwise disjoint, and the rounding can be made <group>-invariant in small disjoint <neighbourhoods> of their rims. It neither changes the <genus> nor introduces an intersection with any axis.

Each tunnel removes two discs from the existing <boundary> and inserts the cylindrical wall of the bore. Its contribution to <Euler characteristic> is $-2$: the two removed discs contribute $-2$, and the inserted <annulus> and its boundary <circles> have zero <Euler characteristic>. The <boundary> remains <connected> and <orientable>, so each tunnel increases <genus> by one. Starting from a <torus> and drilling $2n$ tunnels therefore gives $g=1+2n$. The construction also supplies a <Riemann surface>: use the <complex structure> associated with the induced oriented <Riemannian metric>. The ambient generators are <isometries> preserving the solid and ambient <orientation of a vector space>, so they preserve the induced <boundary orientation> and act by <biholomorphisms>. Part (d) then confirms that the <quotient space> has <genus> two. This is a <free dihedral action on a drilled torus>.

For a regular <parametric surface> $\mathbf r(u,v)$, its <tangent vectors> $\mathbf r_u,\mathbf r_v$ are linearly independent. The <first fundamental form> is the induced <inner product> on the <tangent plane>, written
$$
I=E\,du^2+2F\,du\,dv+G\,dv^2,\qquad
E=\mathbf r_u\cdot\mathbf r_u,\quad F=\mathbf r_u\cdot\mathbf r_v,\quad G=\mathbf r_v\cdot\mathbf r_v.
$$
It measures squared <lengths> and <angles>; its <determinant> satisfies $EG-F^2=|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v|^2>0$. Choose the <unit normal>
$$
N=\frac{\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v}{\sqrt{EG-F^2}}.
$$
The <second fundamental form>, with <shape operator> $S=-dN$, is
$$
II=e\,du^2+2f\,du\,dv+g_2\,dv^2,\qquad
e=\mathbf r_{uu}\cdot N,\quad f=\mathbf r_{uv}\cdot N,\quad g_2=\mathbf r_{vv}\cdot N.
$$
The subscript distinguishes the coefficient $g_2$ from a <genus> or a <Riemannian metric>. In these coordinates the <shape operator> has matrix $I^{-1}II$, so the <Gaussian curvature>, the product of its two <principal curvatures>, is
$$
\boxed{\kappa=\det S=\frac{eg_2-f^2}{EG-F^2}.}
$$
Reversing $N$ reverses $II$ and both <principal curvatures>, leaving the <Gaussian curvature> unchanged.

Let $R_t$ be the horizontal <rotation matrix> through <angle> $t$. Define the <screw motion>
$$
\boxed{\Phi_t(x,y,z)=(x\cos t-y\sin t,\ x\sin t+y\cos t,\ z+t).}
$$
Its linear part is an orthogonal <matrix> with <determinant> one, and its remaining part is a translation. Thus it is a <proper Euclidean motion of Euclidean three-space>, with $\Phi_s\Phi_t=\Phi_{s+t}$.

In horizontal-vector notation the given <parametric surface> satisfies $\mathbf r(u,v)=(R_v(1+\cos u,\sin u),v)$. Therefore
$$
\Phi_t(\mathbf r(u,v))=\mathbf r(u,v+t).
$$
The defining circle in each horizontal plane is entirely parametrized by $u$, so $\mathbf r$ covers $X$. The displayed equality and $\Phi_{-t}=\Phi_t^{-1}$ prove $\Phi_t(X)=X$. This <one-parameter subgroup> preserves the <dot products> and <cross products> defining $I$, $II$ and the chosen <unit normal>. Consequently their coordinate coefficients, and the <Gaussian curvature>, depend only on $u$.

Set $C=\cos u$, $S=\sin u$, and $D=1+S^2=2-C^2$. At $v=0$, the first <partial derivatives> are
$$
\mathbf r_u=(-S,C,0),\qquad\mathbf r_v=(-S,1+C,1).
$$
Their <dot products> give $E=1$, $F=1+C$, $G=3+2C$. Hence
$$
\boxed{I=du^2+2(1+\cos u)\,du\,dv+(3+2\cos u)\,dv^2.}
$$
Since $EG-F^2=3+2C-(1+C)^2=D>0$, the <parametric surface> is regular everywhere. Its <cross product> and <unit normal> at $v=0$ are
$$
\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v=(C,S,-S),\qquad N=\frac{(C,S,-S)}{\sqrt D}.
$$
The second <partial derivatives> at $v=0$ are
$$
\mathbf r_{uu}=(-C,-S,0),\qquad
\mathbf r_{uv}=(-C,-S,0),\qquad
\mathbf r_{vv}=(-1-C,-S,0).
$$
Their <dot products> with $N$ give $e=f=-1/\sqrt D$ and $g_2=-(1+C)/\sqrt D$. Thus
$$
\boxed{II=-\frac{du^2+2\,du\,dv+(1+\cos u)\,dv^2}{\sqrt{1+\sin^2u}}.}
$$
Part (b) transports these <first fundamental form> and <second fundamental form> coefficients to every $v$. Finally,
$$
\boxed{\kappa(u,v)=\frac{(1+C)/D-1/D}{D}=\frac{\cos u}{(1+\sin^2u)^2},\qquad dA=\sqrt{1+\sin^2u}\,du\,dv.}
$$
The last expression is the <surface area> element from $\sqrt{EG-F^2}$. In particular the <Gaussian curvature> changes sign where $\cos u$ does; it vanishes at $u=\pi/2$ modulo $\pi$.

Use the <unit normal> from part (c), and orient $\gamma_a$ by increasing $v$. Write $T=\gamma_a'/|\gamma_a'|$ for its <unit tangent vector>. The signed <geodesic curvature> convention used below is
$$
\kappa_g=\frac{dT}{ds}\cdot(N\times T)
=\frac{N\cdot(\gamma_a'\times\gamma_a'')}{|\gamma_a'|^3},
$$
where $s$ is <arc length>. The second equality follows by differentiating $T$: the term parallel to $\gamma_a'$ disappears in the <scalar triple product>. It makes the correction for a parameter which is not <arc-length parametrization> explicit.

Put $C=\cos a$, $S=\sin a$, $D=1+S^2$, and $G=3+2C$. The <speed> is the constant $\sqrt G$. At $v=0$,
$$
\gamma_a'=(-S,1+C,1),\qquad \gamma_a''=(-1-C,-S,0),
$$
so
$$
\gamma_a'\times\gamma_a''=(S,-1-C,(1+C)^2+S^2)
=(S,-1-C,2+2C).
$$
Taking its <dot product> with $N=(C,S,-S)/\sqrt D$ yields $-S(3+2C)/\sqrt D=-SG/\sqrt D$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\kappa_g(\gamma_a)=-\frac{\sin a}{\sqrt{1+\sin^2a}\sqrt{3+2\cos a}},\qquad ds=\sqrt{3+2\cos a}\,dv.}
$$
The <screw motion> of part (b) makes this independent of $v$. Thus $\gamma_a$ is a <geodesic> precisely when $a$ is an integer multiple of $\pi$. Reversing the direction of traversal or the <unit normal> changes the sign of signed <geodesic curvature>; its magnitude is the absolute value of the displayed expression. The signed convention is needed in part (e).

= Solution

First interpret the <surface integral> through the given <parametric surface>, counting multiplicity. Set
$$
J(u)=\frac{\sin u}{\sqrt{1+\sin^2u}}.
$$
Its <derivative> is
$$
J'(u)=\frac{\cos u}{\sqrt{1+\sin^2u}}-\frac{\sin^2u\cos u}{(1+\sin^2u)^{3/2}}
=\frac{\cos u}{(1+\sin^2u)^{3/2}}.
$$
Using part (c), the <fundamental theorem of calculus> gives
$$
\int_c^d\int_a^b\kappa(\mathbf r(u,v))\sqrt{EG-F^2}\,du\,dv
=(d-c)\bigl(J(b)-J(a)\bigr).
$$
On the other hand part (d), including its <arc length> factor, gives
$$
\int_{\gamma_a([c,d])}\kappa_g\,ds
=-\int_c^d\frac{\sin a}{\sqrt{1+\sin^2a}}\,dv=-(d-c)J(a),
$$
and the corresponding expression for $b$ is $-(d-c)J(b)$. Subtraction proves
$$
\boxed{\int_c^d\int_a^b\kappa(\mathbf r(u,v))\sqrt{EG-F^2}\,du\,dv
=\int_{\gamma_a([c,d])}\kappa_g\,ds-\int_{\gamma_b([c,d])}\kappa_g\,ds.}
$$
If $0<b-a<2\pi$, the <parametric surface> is <injective> on the rectangle, so this is exactly the stated <surface integral> over its image. It also remains the ordinary image integral at $b-a=2\pi$, since the repeated seam has zero <surface area>.

**The assertion for arbitrary $a<b$ needs a multiplicity convention or the restriction $b-a\leq2\pi$.** This omission is present in the native PDF. For example, take $a=0$, $b=5\pi/2$, $c=0$, $d=1$. The image is the entire cylindrical band $0\leq z\leq1$, since $u$ already covers a full turn. Its ordinary <surface integral> of <Gaussian curvature> is
$$
\int_0^1\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\cos u}{(1+\sin^2u)^{3/2}}\,du\,dv=J(2\pi)-J(0)=0.
$$
But the difference of the two signed <geodesic curvature> integrals is $J(5\pi/2)-J(0)=1/\sqrt2$. Thus ordinary integration over the image set makes the unrestricted assertion false. The boxed parameter integral is valid for all $a<b$ and $c<d$ and is the appropriate corrected statement. This distinction is the <multiplicity of a parametrized surface integral>.

= Solution
{parent=f}

For $0<b-a<2\pi$, let $D=\mathbf r([a,b]\times[c,d])$ and use the <orientation of a surface> determined by $N=\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v/|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v|$. Its positive <boundary orientation> follows the bottom side in increasing $u$, the right side in increasing $v$, the top side in decreasing $u$, and the left side in decreasing $v$. The <Gauss-Bonnet theorem> for this disc with four corners reads
$$
\int_D\kappa\,dA+\int_{\partial D}\kappa_g\,ds+\sum_{j=1}^4\varepsilon_j=2\pi,
$$
where the $\varepsilon_j$ are the signed exterior <angles>.

The bottom and top sides have cancelling <geodesic curvature> integrals: a <screw motion> takes one to the other with opposite <boundary orientation>. Explicitly, along increasing $u$ at fixed $v$, the <speed> is one and the signed <geodesic curvature> is $-\sin u/\sqrt{1+\sin^2u}$ on both sides. The right and left sides contribute, respectively, the integral along $\gamma_b$ and minus the integral along $\gamma_a$.

Although the coordinate sides are generally not perpendicular, the corner contributions still sum to $2\pi$. Indeed, let $\theta(u)$ be the <angle> between $\mathbf r_u$ and $\mathbf r_v$, so
$$
\cos\theta(u)=\frac{1+\cos u}{\sqrt{3+2\cos u}}.
$$
Starting at the bottom-left corner, the exterior <angles> are $\pi-\theta(a)$, $\theta(b)$, $\pi-\theta(b)$ and $\theta(a)$. Their sum is $2\pi$. Cancelling this from the <Gauss-Bonnet theorem> leaves
$$
\int_D\kappa\,dA=\int_{\gamma_a}\kappa_g\,ds-\int_{\gamma_b}\kappa_g\,ds,
$$
