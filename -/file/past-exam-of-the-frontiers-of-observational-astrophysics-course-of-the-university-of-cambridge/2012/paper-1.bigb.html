= Solution
{parent=1}

The geometric path difference for a direction at zenith angle $\theta+\alpha$ is $b\sin(\theta+\alpha)$. Equal cable lengths leave that difference unchanged; the inserted electronic delay cancels $b\sin\theta$ for the tracking center. The residual delay is therefore
$$
\tau=\frac bc[\sin(\theta+\alpha)-\sin\theta]\simeq\frac{b\alpha\cos\theta}{c}.
$$
The <complex visibility> of the unresolved source has phase $2\pi\nu\tau$, giving $\exp(ikb\alpha\cos\theta)$ in the chosen baseline ordering. Reversing the signal order complex-conjugates it without changing the measured visibility magnitude. Tracking the <antennas> geometrically does not itself provide this <fringe stopping>.

\Image[paper-1-interferometer-delay.png]
{title=Tracking delay in a two-antenna interferometer}
{description=Original functional schematic. The nearer antenna receives the tracked wave earlier and its electronic delay compensates that geometric lead. Angles are exaggerated and cable paths are schematic.}

For a top-hat band centered at $\nu_0$, let $f=\nu-\nu_0$. Averaging the residual fringe gives
$$
\frac1{\Delta\nu}\int_{-\Delta\nu/2}^{\Delta\nu/2}e^{2\pi i(\nu_0+f)\tau}\,df
=e^{2\pi i\nu_0\tau}\operatorname{sinc}(\pi\Delta\nu\tau),
\qquad\operatorname{sinc}x=\frac{\sin x}{x}.
$$
After removing the central fringe phase, its envelope is exactly
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{sinc}\left(\frac{\pi\Delta\nu b\alpha\cos\theta}{c}\right).}
$$
The full complex average retains the displayed central-frequency phase; the bare sinc in the question is the phase-referenced envelope or an average written in frequency-offset coordinates. This <bandwidth decorrelation of a point source> is cancellation between different frequency-dependent phases, not absorption of source radiation.

The center-to-center extent is $18\,\mathrm m$ on each side and $18\sqrt2=25.46\,\mathrm m$ diagonally, while the shortest nonzero baseline is $6\,\mathrm m$. At $\lambda=0.02\,\mathrm m$, characteristic scales are
$$
\boxed{\theta_{\rm syn}\sim\frac\lambda{b_{\max}}=2.70',\qquad
\theta_{\rm large}\sim\frac\lambda{b_{\min}}=11.46',\qquad
\theta_{\rm primary}\sim\frac\lambda D=17.19'.}
$$
These describe the finest synthesized scale, a largest recoverable scale estimate, and the individual-dish field of view, respectively. Along an array side the longest baseline is only $18\,\mathrm m$, giving $3.82'$ rather than the diagonal estimate. Projection, weighting and sidelobes determine an actual <synthesized beam>.

There is no sharp universal largest-scale cutoff: the commonly used $0.6\lambda/b_{\min}$ convention would give $6.88'$. For an ideal uniformly illuminated circular dish, the primary-beam full width at half maximum is approximately $1.02\lambda/D=17.5'$, while the first-null angular radius is $1.22\lambda/D=21.0'$. These definitions should not be mixed. Uniform lattice spacing also produces grating sidelobes; the <primary beam> restricts the useful field but does not supply the missing zero-baseline <flux>.

Use the projected baseline along the source offset, $b_p$, and $\alpha=10'=2.909\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{rad}$. The first <bandwidth> null occurs at $\Delta\nu=c/(b_p\alpha)$. A worst-case $b_p=25.46\,\mathrm m$ gives $4.05\,\mathrm{GHz}$. **That is a zero of the response, not a good usable <bandwidth>.** A quantitative maximum needs an allowed attenuation.

For example, requiring a response of at least $0.9$ gives $\operatorname{sinc}x=0.9$ at $x\simeq0.787$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\Delta\nu_{10\%}\simeq0.250\frac c{b_p\alpha}\simeq1.01\,\mathrm{GHz}}
$$
for that worst projection. A strictly east-west offset in a sides-aligned square instead samples at most $18\,\mathrm m$, giving a $5.73\,\mathrm{GHz}$ first null and a $1.43\,\mathrm{GHz}$ ten-percent-loss <bandwidth>. The original two-antenna geometry supplies an additional $\cos\theta$ projection; these examples use its maximum magnitude. Thus **a <bandwidth> of order $1\,\mathrm{GHz}$** is a reasonable conservative estimate with a stated small-loss criterion, rather than a unique geometry-free limit.

\Image[paper-1-array-and-bandwidth.png]
{title=Array baselines and bandwidth decorrelation away from the tracking center}
{description=Original array geometry and rectangular-band sinc calculations for a ten-arcminute offset. The marked ten-percent-loss bandwidth is an illustrative response criterion; the first null is not a usable-bandwidth recommendation.}

To use a much wider total band, channelize the signals before correlation or before irreversible <frequency> averaging. Correlate sufficiently narrow channels, track the reference delay and phase at each <frequency>, and combine frequency-aware visibilities or images. This preserves sensitivity while avoiding <field-dependent averaging losses in radio interferometry>. Merely adding a phase correction after a broad channel has decorrelated cannot recover the lost signal; nor can one tracking delay cancel every sky direction simultaneously.<Field of View by National Radio Astronomy Observatory>[VLA Observational Status Summary 2018A, bandwidth and time-averaging limitations]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The <wavelength> corresponds to about $15\,\mathrm{GHz}$, in the low-frequency decrement part of the <thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect>. Hot cluster <electrons> inverse-Compton scatter CMB <photons>. In the Rayleigh-Jeans limit,
$$
\frac{\Delta T}{T_{\rm CMB}}\simeq-2y,\qquad
y=\int n_e\sigma_T\frac{k_BT_e}{m_ec^2}\,dl.
$$
The signal probes integrated <electron> <pressure>. Short baselines give sensitivity to extended arcminute-scale cluster structure, while the primary field covers the cluster and surrounding sky. The array's several-arcminute resolution suits many such structures; it is not equally well matched to every cluster size.

Simultaneous cross-correlations reject uncorrelated receiver <noise> and provide spatial information; compact radio sources can be constrained with the relatively longer baselines and separated from extended structure. Broad <frequency> coverage helps sensitivity provided channel smearing is controlled. Correlated atmospheric emission, <calibration> error, primordial CMB fluctuations and radio-source contamination remain relevant. The lack of zero and very short spacings also <means> that total integrated cluster <flux> requires a model or complementary data. An interferometer is therefore useful for the spatially resolved decrement, rather than automatically measuring every cluster's full <pressure> integral.

= Solution

An observed catalogue is the result of a population, an instrument and a selection rule. Its <astronomical selection function> can depend on <flux>, color, size, <surface brightness>, sky location, cadence and follow-up success. The relevant cut is often on noisy measured quantities. A model that selects on noiseless true <flux> instead can predict a different sample even when it uses the same nominal threshold.

<Malmquist bias> occurs because brighter members of a population are visible through a larger <volume> in a flux-limited survey. For a uniform Euclidean population, $r_{\max}\propto\sqrt L$ and $V_{\max}\propto L^{3/2}$. The selected <luminosity> distribution is therefore weighted toward large $L$. For an intrinsic <Gaussian> absolute-magnitude distribution with <mean> $M_0$ and <variance> $\sigma_M^2$, multiplying by $10^{-0.6M}$ and completing the square gives the <Gaussian absolute-magnitude Malmquist shift>
$$
\boxed{\langle M\rangle_{\rm selected}=M_0-0.6\ln(10)\,\sigma_M^2
\simeq M_0-1.382\sigma_M^2.}
$$
This assumes the stated simple population, a complete <flux> cut and no additional boundaries. Exactly identical noiseless <standard candles> have a distance truncation, not a <luminosity> spread to reweight. Scatter, <extinction>, cosmological <volume> and source evolution need their own model.

<Lutz-Kelker bias> concerns parallax errors, spatial population weighting and selected <calibration> samples. Uniform spatial <density> gives $p(r)\propto r^2$ locally, hence $p(\varpi)\propto\varpi^{-4}$ on the physically valid range. More distant objects can scatter into a measured-parallax cut. In addition, inversion $r=1/\widehat\varpi$ is nonlinear and gives misleading estimates at low signal-to-noise; a negative measured parallax is possible even for a positive true distance. A <posterior> instead combines the parallax <likelihood> with a justified population <prior>. An unbounded uniform-volume <prior> is improper here; a finite population model or a <proper distance prior for parallax inference> is necessary.<On the Use of Trigonometric Parallaxes for the Calibration of Luminosity Systems: Theory by T. E. Lutz and D. H. Kelker>[classical selected-sample parallax-calibration model]{ref}

<Bayes' theorem> makes the assumptions explicit. If $\eta$ denotes population parameters, $x$ latent properties and $S$ the catalogue inclusion event, a selected-sample <likelihood> has the form
$$
p(d\mid\eta,S)=\frac{\mathbf1_S(d)\int p(d\mid x)p(x\mid\eta)\,dx}
{\int_{d'\in S}\!dd'\int p(d'\mid x)p(x\mid\eta)\,dx}.
$$
This <selection-normalized astronomical likelihood> accounts for the model-dependent <probability> of entering the catalogue. For individual inference, selection information already determined by the fully conditioned measured data must not be multiplied in a second time. The sampling design, inferential target and actual conditioned information determine the appropriate expression.

Other effects include <Eddington bias>, where steep number counts and measurement scatter move more faint objects upward than bright objects downward, and surface-brightness selection that misses diffuse galaxies despite substantial total <flux>. Dust <extinction> affects both detectability and inferred <luminosity>. Variable-source detection depends on cadence and duration; spectroscopic follow-up can favor easy-to-measure redshifts or particular colors. Blends, <calibration> drift and redshift errors can mimic population changes.

Examples include magnitude-limited supernova samples used in distance-redshift fits, where <luminosity> scatter and follow-up must be modeled, and radial-velocity planet surveys, which favor large-amplitude, short-period systems and leave inclination degeneracies. Forward modeling through the survey pipeline, injection-and-recovery estimates of completeness, calibrated <noise> and properly modeled censored or nondetected observations address these effects. A universal correction or an unexamined inverse-volume weight does not replace an explicit generative and selection model.

<Thermal bremsstrahlung> arises from <electron> acceleration in <ion> Coulomb fields. For a thermal <plasma> its optically thin spectral emissivity has the dependence
$$
j_\nu\propto n_e\sum_iZ_i^2n_i\,T_e^{-1/2}e^{-h\nu/(k_BT_e)}g_{\rm ff}(\nu,T_e).
$$
The exponential high-energy slope helps constrain <temperature> in X-ray spectra, with composition, absorption and other emission components included. At radio frequencies with $h\nu\ll k_BT_e$, the thin spectrum is much flatter and does not alone determine a unique <temperature>; the <Gaunt factor> supplies a weak <frequency> dependence.

For an approximately isothermal layer, <radiative transfer> gives $I_\nu=B_\nu(T_e)(1-e^{-\tau_\nu})$. Radio free-free opacity scales approximately as $\tau_\nu\propto\mathrm{EM}\,T_e^{-3/2}\nu^{-2}g_{\rm ff}$, where the hydrogen-plasma <emission measure> is $\mathrm{EM}=\int n_e^2dl$. A resolved optically thick brightness <temperature> approaches $T_e$; an unresolved filling factor lowers the observed value. The turnover between thin and thick regimes helps constrain <emission measure>.

An angular extent and distance give a physical size, and an assumed geometry supplies a line-of-sight depth. Combining that depth with <emission measure> estimates an rms <density>, $n_{e,\rm rms}\sim\sqrt{\mathrm{EM}/\ell}$. Clumping, filling factor, composition and unknown depth preserve a <density-depth degeneracy of free-free radiation>. These measurements constrain different combinations of <temperature>, <density> and size rather than independently fixing all three without assumptions.

= Solution

The <radial-velocity method> measures repeated Doppler shifts of stellar <spectral lines> as a star responds to an orbiting companion. A stable high-resolution spectrograph and <wavelength> reference distinguish stellar motion from instrumental drift; many lines or a spectral template improve precision. A Keplerian fit yields period, eccentricity, phase and velocity semi-amplitude. For a companion <mass> $m_p$, stellar <mass> $M_*$ and inclination $i$,
$$
\boxed{K=\left(\frac{2\pi G}{P}\right)^{1/3}
\frac{m_p\sin i}{(M_*+m_p)^{2/3}\sqrt{1-e^2}}.}
$$
With a stellar-mass estimate and $m_p\ll M_*$, this measures approximately $m_p\sin i$, a minimum <mass> unless the inclination is independently constrained. A nearly face-on orbit can hide a massive companion. The method preferentially detects larger masses, shorter periods and favorable inclinations.

Starspots, activity, pulsations, rotation, blended spectra and sampling aliases can imitate or distort a periodic signal. Line-shape diagnostics, activity indicators, repeated observations and independent constraints help test a planetary explanation. <Photon> <noise> and <spectral line> width constrain precision even for a perfectly calibrated spectrograph. Multiple planets or incomplete orbital coverage require an adequate model and longer monitoring; a periodic centroid shift alone is not a proof of a particular planetary system.

A <charge-coupled device> converts absorbed <photons> into stored charge in semiconductor potential wells. An exposure accumulates a pixelated image; clocked electrodes transfer rows toward a serial register and output amplifier. <Quantum efficiency>, pixel sampling and optical throughput connect collected <photons> to the expected signal. In ordinary optical imaging it primarily counts photoelectrons rather than independently measuring every <photon>'s <energy>.

The <CCD noise budget> combines source and sky <shot noise>, dark-current fluctuations and <read noise>. For an aperture containing $n$ pixels, known background expectation $N_b$, total dark expectation $N_d$, source expectation $N_s$ and per-pixel <read noise> $r$,
$$
\mathrm{SNR}=\frac{N_s}{\sqrt{N_s+N_b+N_d+nr^2}}.
$$
Estimating the background from a finite separate region adds its own uncertainty. Cooling reduces <dark current>; longer integration reduces the relative importance of one read but can encounter saturation, cosmic rays or changing conditions. <Full well capacity>, amplifier gain, <linearity>, blooming and charge-transfer efficiency limit useful <detector dynamic range>.

Bias frames establish the electronic offset; matched dark frames characterize time- and temperature-dependent dark signal. Flat fields calibrate pixel-to-pixel response and illumination, with appropriate attention to <wavelength>. Bad-pixel masks, repeated exposures and dithering help reject defects and cosmic rays. A calibrated <PSF> and astrometric solution are needed for reliable <photometry> and positions. High <quantum efficiency> and stable linear integration are advantages, but an uncalibrated count array is not yet an astronomical <flux> map.

= Solution

Atmospheric refractive-index fluctuations distort the incoming wavefront. In a Kolmogorov model, the long-exposure seeing width is approximately $0.98\lambda/r_0$, where the <Fried parameter> scales as $r_0\propto\lambda^{6/5}$. High-quality sites and longer <wavelengths> can improve the observing conditions, but do not eliminate the distorted phase. Short coherence times also require rapid correction or exposure.

<Adaptive optics> uses a guide source, <wavefront sensor>, <deformable mirror> and fast feedback loop. Tip-tilt correction removes image wander; higher-order actuators correct finer spatial distortions. Finite actuator spacing, sensor <noise>, delay and changing turbulence leave residual errors. A nearby natural <guide star> limits sky coverage, while a laser beacon supplies higher-order reference information over more directions. Conventional laser-guide-star systems still need independent image-motion information, commonly from a natural star.<Laser Guide Star for Adaptive Optics by European Southern Observatory>[ESO, tip-tilt information and sky-coverage limitation]{ref} <Anisoplanatism> limits the corrected field; laser finite-altitude sampling and variable <PSFs> need their own <calibration>.

<Lucky imaging> selects favorable short exposures and aligns them, trading exposure efficiency and <photon> statistics for reduced blur. <Speckle interferometry> uses short-exposure spatial-frequency information and suitable phase/statistical reconstruction instead of simply averaging away the speckles. Both need adequate time resolution, detector performance and <calibration>; selection cannot restore information never recorded. Interferometric methods can also recover fine structure with phase control or closure information. These approaches correct or infer wavefront effects; they do not make clouds, absorption or every atmospheric amplitude fluctuation disappear.<Adaptive Optics by European Southern Observatory>[wavefront sensing, deformable-mirror correction and atmospheric limitations]{ref}
