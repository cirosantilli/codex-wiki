Choose the baseline vector from the first <antenna> to the second. Apart from a sign convention for receiver delay, the <geometric delay> is $\tau_g=\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf s/c$, and the phase difference is $\phi=2\pi\nu\tau_g$. For equal received source powers $P_a$, adding the voltages and averaging over many wave periods gives
$$
P(t)=2P_a\{1+\cos[2\pi\nu\tau_g(t)+\phi_0]\}.
$$
Uncorrelated receiver noise adds a mean level but not the source's coherent cross term. For an east-west baseline and one choice of hour-angle sign, $\tau_g=(b/c)\cos\delta\sin H(t)$. Earth rotation therefore sweeps the phase; locally the fringe <frequency> is $(b/\lambda)\cos\delta\cos H\,\dot H$. Tracking the <antenna> pointing keeps the source in the <primary beam>. Electronic <fringe stopping> is a separate operation: it can remove this phase evolution, making a source at the <phase centre> constant in the corrected <complex visibility>. The oscillating power above is the geometrical fringe before that correction.

\Image[paper-1-interferometer-fringes.png]
{title=Projected interferometer baseline, geometric delay and illustrative fringes before electronic fringe stopping}

For a small angular displacement $d\theta$ along the projected baseline, $d\phi=2\pi b_p\,d\theta/\lambda$. One full fringe therefore spans **$\Delta\theta\sim\lambda/b_p$**. Here $b_p=|\mathbf b-(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf s)\mathbf s|$. This is the angular period of the measured Fourier component and the characteristic resolution scale when such long baselines are synthesized; precise beam width and image ambiguity also depend on baseline coverage and weighting. A single pair by itself does not give an unambiguous complete image.

Writing $x=h\nu/(k_BT)$, the expansion $e^x-1=x+O(x^2)$ immediately gives the <Rayleigh-Jeans law>,
$$
\boxed{B_\nu\simeq\frac{2k_BT\nu^2}{c^2}\quad(h\nu\ll k_BT).}
$$
The factor two counts the two independent modes of <polarization of waves>. A small brightness-temperature perturbation thus has $\Delta I_\nu=2k_B\nu^2\Delta T_{\rm RJ}/c^2$ in this limit.

Hot intracluster <electrons> inverse-Compton scatter the microwave-background <photons>. The scattering <optical depth> is $d\tau=n_e\sigma_T\,dl$ and the characteristic thermal fractional energy transfer is of order $k_BT_e/(m_ec^2)$. The integrated distortion is therefore controlled by
$$
y=\int\frac{k_BT_e}{m_ec^2}n_e\sigma_T\,dl.
$$
Scattering conserves <photon number> while shifting <photons> toward higher <frequencies>, giving a low-frequency decrement and a high-frequency increment. Thus the printed estimate correctly gives a negative change of order $yT_{\rm CMB}$. Its numerical coefficient, however, is not the standard Rayleigh-Jeans coefficient. For nonrelativistic <electrons> and small $y$, the <Kompaneets equation> gives $\Delta n=y x^{-2}\partial_x(x^4\partial_x n_0)$. At small $x$, $n_0\simeq1/x$, so $\Delta n\simeq-2y/x$ and
$$
\boxed{\frac{\Delta T_{\rm RJ}}{T_{\rm CMB}}\simeq-2y.}
$$
Equivalently the thermodynamic-temperature spectral factor is $x\coth(x/2)-4\to-2$. The original PDF really prints the estimate with $-y$, not $-2y$; both numerical conventions are retained below. Relativistic corrections at the supplied $T_e$ are small compared with this factor-of-two difference.<Relativistic corrections to the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect by A. D. Challinor and A. N. Lasenby>[abstract and Rayleigh-Jeans limit: the leading coefficient is minus twice the conventional Compton y parameter]{ref}

The uniform cube has a constant line-of-sight depth $L=6\times10^{22}\,\mathrm m$. Using the supplied cross-section rather than replacing it with a more precise value gives
$$
\tau=n_e\sigma_TL=8.04\times10^{-4},\qquad \frac{k_BT_e}{m_ec^2}=1.01\times10^{-2},\qquad y=8.14\times10^{-6}.
$$
Adopt $T_{\rm CMB}=2.72548\,\mathrm K$, explicitly an external measured constant, not a <temperature> supplied in the question. The values $k_B=1.380649\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$, $c=299792458\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and $m_e\simeq9.10938\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$ set the physical units. Hence **the printed relation gives $\Delta T\simeq-22\,\mathrm{\mu K}$; the standard Rayleigh-Jeans result gives $\Delta T\simeq-44\,\mathrm{\mu K}$**. Keeping the answer as $-8.14\times10^{-6}T_{\rm CMB}$, or twice that, avoids needing a numerical background <temperature>.<The Temperature of the Cosmic Microwave Background by Dale J. Fixsen>[abstract, literature-combined temperature $2.72548\pm0.00057$ K; adopted value rounded only at the end]{ref}

The face is a square, so its <solid angle> is $\Omega=(6\pi/10800)^2=3.046\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{sr}$, not the area of a circular six-arcminute aperture. At $15\,\mathrm{GHz}$,
$$
\Delta S_\nu=\int\Delta I_\nu\,d\Omega\simeq\frac{2k_B\nu^2}{c^2}\Delta T\,\Omega.
$$
Thus **$\Delta S_\nu\simeq-4.67\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{Jy}=-0.47\,\mathrm{mJy}$ using the printed estimate**, or **$-0.93\,\mathrm{mJy}$ using the standard factor two**. The sign denotes missing brightness relative to the surrounding microwave background. At this <frequency> $h\nu/(k_BT_{\rm CMB})\simeq0.264$; the exact nonrelativistic spectral intensity differs from the strict Rayleigh-Jeans approximation by about one percent. This precision does not affect the estimates.

For the array noise, the <system temperature> sets the receiver noise scale; let $A_e=\eta_A\pi d^2/4$ be the effective area of each dish, and let $\eta_c$ describe correlation losses. No efficiencies or <polarization of waves> count are supplied, so first take $\eta_A=\eta_c=1$ and a single independent <polarization of waves>. An unpolarized <point source> produces <antenna> <temperature> $T_a=S_\nu A_e/(2k_B)$, and the <system-equivalent flux density> is $\mathrm{SEFD}=2k_BT_{\rm sys}/A_e$. The <interferometric radiometer equation> gives one baseline an rms flux error $\mathrm{SEFD}/\sqrt{2\Delta\nu t}$. There are $N(N-1)/2=45$ cross-correlation baselines; averaging their independent <thermal noise> for a <point source> gives
$$
\sigma_S=\frac{2k_BT_{\rm sys}}{A_e\eta_c\sqrt{n_{\rm pol}N(N-1)\Delta\nu t}}.
$$
The ideal single-dish effective area is $4\pi\,\mathrm{m^2}$, giving $\mathrm{SEFD}=6.59\times10^3\,\mathrm{Jy}$ and
$$
\boxed{\sigma_S\simeq9.0\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{Jy}=9.0\,\mathrm{\mu Jy}\quad(n_{\rm pol}=1).}
$$
Combining two independent parallel-hand <polarization of waves> measurements gives $6.3\,\mathrm{\mu Jy}$. Nonunit efficiencies increase these estimates by $1/(\eta_A\eta_c)$. Here signal equal to noise means a weak-source <signal-to-noise ratio> of one after integration; it does not mean equating the source to the entire unaveraged receiver-noise power.<Sensitivity by Joan Wrobel>[2004 Synthesis Imaging Summer School, slides 6–12 and 20: SEFD, independent baseline noise and single-polarization synthesis-image sensitivity]{ref}

At the band centre, $\lambda=0.01999\,\mathrm m$. Taking a representative maximum projected baseline of $20\,\mathrm m$ gives **$\theta_{\rm res}\sim3.4$ arcminutes**. A corner-to-corner baseline of $20\sqrt2\,\mathrm m$ would give about $2.4$ arcminutes before projection and weighting. The exact <antenna> locations and source declination are not supplied, so neither a unique <synthesized beam> shape nor an exact FWHM is determined. The individual-dish primary-beam scale, roughly $\lambda/4\,\mathrm m\sim17$ arcminutes, is the field of view, not the synthesized resolution.

To extract the cluster signal, retain frequency-resolved <complex visibilities> and fit an extended SZ template together with contaminating compact sources. For the ideal uniform square centred at the <phase centre> and with a nearly constant <primary beam> across it, the <Fourier transform> is
$$
V(u,v)=\Delta S_\nu\,\operatorname{sinc}(\pi\Theta u)\operatorname{sinc}(\pi\Theta v),\qquad \operatorname{sinc}x=\frac{\sin x}{x},\qquad\Theta=6\ \text{arcminutes in radians}.
$$
This is why the integrated decrement must not be treated as a point-source flux on every baseline. The first axial visibility null occurs at $b=\lambda/\Theta\simeq11.5\,\mathrm m$. Short baselines carry most of the large-scale decrement; the longest baselines are useful for separating unresolved positive radio sources. <natural visibility weighting> or a suitable taper gives a clearer extended-source image than emphasizing the longest baselines, while a <visibility-domain matched filter> uses all samples with weights proportional to template amplitude divided by noise <variance>. Its amplitude estimate is $\widehat a=\operatorname{Re}(T^\dagger C^{-1}d)/(T^\dagger C^{-1}T)$ for a real template amplitude. A joint fit propagates radio-source uncertainty instead of subtracting exact, noise-free source models.

\Image[paper-1-square-cluster-visibility.png]
{title=Normalized visibilities of the six-arcminute square cluster and their suppression on long baselines}

Earth-rotation and multifrequency synthesis improve Fourier coverage, but each channel must use its own baseline in <wavelengths>, <primary beam> and SZ spectrum. Use the longer baselines and spectra to identify variable <point sources>, flag bad data, and test the decrement with time, <antenna> and <frequency> splits. Primary-beam and absolute-flux <calibration> must be included. Large-scale microwave-background fluctuations and residual ground or atmospheric signals can remain even below the thermal-noise estimate. The narrow low-frequency band alone gives limited separation of SZ and primary microwave-background <temperature> structure. There is no zero-spacing interferometric datum: integrated flux is model dependent unless sufficiently short baselines or additional total-power information are available. Exact cluster detection significance cannot be obtained from the point-source noise alone without the actual projected baseline distribution and weighting.

For data $D$, parameters $\theta$ and model $M$, <conditional probability> gives
$$
\boxed{p(\theta\mid D,M)=\frac{p(D\mid\theta,M)\,p(\theta\mid M)}{p(D\mid M)},\qquad p(D\mid M)=\int p(D\mid\theta,M)p(\theta\mid M)\,d\theta.}
$$
The <likelihood> describes the measurement process, the <prior> encodes parameter information before these data, and the <Bayesian evidence> normalizes the <posterior>. A <prior> is a <probability density> with respect to a specified measure; a uniform <prior> in distance is not uniform in parallax. <Bayes' theorem> combines <conditional probabilities> and does not itself select an appropriate <likelihood> or <prior>.

An astronomical application starts with a forward model. For measured fluxes $d_i$ with predictions $f_i(\theta)$ and known Gaussian covariance $C$, $L\propto|C|^{-1/2}\exp[-(d-f)^{\mathsf T}C^{-1}(d-f)/2]$. Correlated <calibration> errors belong in $C$ or in explicit <calibration> <nuisance parameters>; assuming every filter or epoch is independent can give falsely narrow uncertainties. For a faint photon-counting source, the more appropriate <likelihood> can instead be $p(N\mid s,b)=e^{-(s+b)}(s+b)^N/N!$, with nonnegative expected source and background counts. Background measurements constrain $b$, which is then marginalized rather than set to an exact subtracted number. The observed background-subtracted count can be negative even though the physical expected source count is nonnegative.

For a simple flux example with $d\mid F\sim\mathcal N(F,\sigma^2)$ and a Gaussian <calibration> <prior> $F\sim\mathcal N(F_0,\sigma_0^2)$, completing the square gives
$$
\sigma_p^{-2}=\sigma^{-2}+\sigma_0^{-2},\qquad F_p=\sigma_p^2\left(\frac d{\sigma^2}+\frac{F_0}{\sigma_0^2}\right).
$$
This explicitly shows how independent information combines. A physical nonnegative-flux <prior> would truncate or replace that illustrative Gaussian <prior>. Photometric-redshift fitting similarly compares predicted <galaxy> colours with measured fluxes, then integrates over <luminosity>, dust and other <nuisance parameters> to obtain $p(z\mid D)$. Multimodal <posteriors> should be retained when different spectral features produce similar colours; a best fit alone hides that ambiguity. The same framework applies to orbit parameters, stellar <temperatures>, cosmological parameters and cluster SZ amplitudes.

<credible intervals> state <posterior> <probability> conditional on the model and <prior>. <posterior predictive checks> compare replicated observations with the data and can reveal a deficient noise or source model. Sensitivity to plausible <priors> is especially important for weak signals and inverse problems. For competing models, <posterior> odds equal <prior> odds times the <Bayes factor> $Z_1/Z_2$; the evidence averages fit quality over the <prior> volume and therefore depends on proper, physically justified <prior> ranges. Detection thresholds and catalogue selection must enter the sampling model when inferring a population, otherwise a carefully calculated <posterior> can still describe a biased sample. **The practical gain is a joint uncertainty distribution with <nuisance parameters> propagated, not merely a single fitted number.**

Atmospheric refractive-index fluctuations impose a changing phase screen on the pupil. For a <telescope> much larger than the <Fried parameter> $r_0$, long-exposure resolution is approximately $0.98\lambda/r_0$, rather than the <diffraction> scale $\lambda/D$. The scaling $r_0\propto\lambda^{6/5}$ makes the <seeing> width proportional to $\lambda^{-1/5}$ for fixed turbulence. An <atmospheric coherence time> sets the exposure or correction cadence needed to freeze the phase, while the <isoplanatic angle> sets the useful angular separation from a reference source. These are atmospheric quantities, not improvements automatically supplied by a larger <mirror>.

First reduce avoidable turbulence: choose a good high site, control <mirror> and enclosure <temperatures>, ventilate the dome, and observe when the <line of sight> crosses less turbulent air. Guiding removes <telescope> drift, and a fast tip-tilt correction removes image motion. Slow <active optics> corrects <mirror> figure and alignment; it cannot by itself follow all atmospheric phase fluctuations. These measures improve the input wavefront but do not guarantee diffraction-limited performance.

In <adaptive optics>, a <wavefront sensor> measures the reference-star wavefront, a reconstructor estimates its aberration, and a fast control loop commands a <deformable mirror> and tip-tilt element. A Shack-Hartmann sensor obtains local slopes from the displacement of focused lenslet spots. The science beam receives the corresponding optical-path correction. Residual phase <variance> limits the <Strehl ratio> approximately as $S\simeq e^{-\sigma_\phi^2}$ when the residual is small. Enough corrected spatial modes, adequate reference <photons> and a response faster than atmospheric changes are all needed. <infrared> correction is easier because a given path error has smaller phase in radians and the atmospheric coherence length is larger. A faint or distant <guide star> leaves larger errors and a core-plus-halo <point spread function>. The NACO manual documents these reference-star and anisoplanatic limitations.<NACO User Manual by European Southern Observatory>[§§3.1–3.2, printed p.13; §4.3, printed p.19: atmospheric coherence, real-time correction and anisoplanatism]{ref}

A <laser guide star> supplies a wavefront reference in fields lacking a bright suitable star, but a finite-altitude beacon samples a cone rather than the full stellar cylinder. It also does not by itself supply the absolute celestial tip-tilt reference; a natural source is still required for that measurement. Multiple <guide stars> and conjugate correction planes can improve wider-field compensation, with greater hardware and reconstruction demands. The <point spread function> can vary across the field and with time, so reference stars and telemetry are needed to calibrate <astrometry> and <photometry>; a sharper image alone is not a guarantee of accurate science.<Adaptive Optics by European Southern Observatory>[Laser guide stars: artificial reference sources, limited sky coverage and retained natural-star requirements]{ref}

Short-exposure methods provide another route. Exposures shorter than the <atmospheric coherence time> freeze <speckle patterns>. <speckle interferometry> uses calibrated Fourier statistics of many frames to recover diffraction-scale information; phase information requires methods such as <bispectrum> reconstruction, not just averaging the blurred frames. <lucky imaging> selects the best short frames, registers them and combines them. It sacrifices <photons> through selection and can be read-noise limited for faint targets, so it is most effective with enough reference <photons>. Ordinary shift-and-add mainly corrects motion; it does not remove every high-order phase error. Deconvolution using a measured <point spread function> can recover supported structure but amplifies noise and cannot reliably invent <spatial frequencies> absent from the observations. **Good-site engineering, fast wavefront correction and short-exposure reconstruction address different limitations and can be combined.**

An <electron> accelerated in an <ion>'s Coulomb field emits <bremsstrahlung>. Both <electron> states are unbound, giving free-free radiation rather than a discrete bound-state line. Averaging over a Maxwellian <electron> distribution gives an optically thin spectral emissivity with dependence
$$
j_\nu=C_{\rm ff}Z^2n_en_iT^{-1/2}e^{-h\nu/(k_BT)}\bar g_{\rm ff}(\nu,T).
$$
Here $C_{\rm ff}$ depends on the emissivity convention, and the <Gaunt factor> supplies the quantum correction. No numerical emissivity normalization is needed for these diagnostics. For a mixture, sum $Z_i^2n_i$ over <ion> species. The bolometric emissivity is proportional to $n_e\sum Z_i^2n_i\,T^{1/2}$ with a frequency-averaged <Gaunt factor>. Thermal <electron> speeds control the spectrum while the encounter rate supplies its density-squared dependence.

<temperature> can be measured from the high-energy exponential cutoff at <photon> energy of order $k_BT$, provided the spectrum spans that turnover and foreground absorption and nonthermal contamination are modelled. At radio <frequencies> $h\nu\ll k_BT$, the exponential is nearly one, so the optically thin continuum is a poor thermometer by slope alone. Kirchhoff's law, $\alpha_\nu=j_\nu/B_\nu$, and the <Rayleigh-Jeans law> give $\alpha_\nu\propto n_en_iT^{-3/2}\nu^{-2}\bar g_{\rm ff}$ for an isothermal thermal <plasma>. The transfer solution is
$$
I_\nu=B_\nu(T)(1-e^{-\tau_\nu}),\qquad T_b\simeq T(1-e^{-\tau_\nu})
$$
in the radio limit. A resolved optically thick region therefore measures its <electron> <temperature> through <brightness temperature>, if it fills the beam. The turnover between thick and thin emission constrains the line-of-sight <emission measure> $\mathrm{EM}=\int n_e^2dl$ for a hydrogen-dominated fully ionized <plasma>. In the thin regime $T_b\simeq T\tau_\nu$, rather than $T_b=T$. The slowly varying <Gaunt factor> gives a nearly flat optically thin radio flux-density spectrum, in contrast to the $\nu^2$ thick limit at fixed projected area.<Essential Radio Astronomy by James J. Condon and Scott M. Ransom>[chapter 4, §4.3, especially equations (4.57)–(4.58): emission measure, absorption and optically thick/thin diagnostics]{ref}

To obtain <density>, one also needs geometry. An angular extent and independent distance give a transverse size; an assumed shape provides a depth or volume. For approximately uniform emitting gas with <astrophysical volume filling factor> $f_V$, $L_{\rm ff}\propto n_e^2 f_VVT^{1/2}$, so $n_e\propto[L_{\rm ff}/(f_VVT^{1/2})]^{1/2}$. Alternatively a measured column <emission measure> and assumed path length give an rms <density>. Clumping raises the mean squared <density> relative to the square of the mean: the inferred value is not automatically the mean gas <density>. **Free-free brightness alone does not uniquely determine <temperature>, <density> and physical size.** Resolved imaging, a distance, optical-depth information or an independent column-density diagnostic is needed to remove those degeneracies. Cluster SZ data are complementary because their leading signal depends on $\int n_eT_e\,dl$, whereas free-free emission depends on <density> squared. Metal lines, unresolved <temperature> structure, foreground absorption, synchrotron emission and <background subtraction> must be controlled before attributing the continuum to a single thermal component.

Type Ia supernovae are <standardizable candles>. Repeated multiband <photometry> measures peak <apparent magnitude>, light-curve width and colour; spectra identify the supernova class and determine <redshift>, often from the host <galaxy>. Nearby objects calibrate the relation between light-curve shape, colour and absolute <luminosity>. Applying those relations produces a corrected <apparent magnitude> $m_{\rm corr}$, not an assumption that every event has identical <luminosity>. The <distance modulus> then gives
$$
\mu=m_{\rm corr}-M=5\log_{10}\left(\frac{d_L}{\mathrm{Mpc}}\right)+25.
$$
A common uncertainty in $M$ is degenerate with the absolute distance scale, but does not erase the redshift-dependent shape of the <Hubble diagram>.

<luminosity distance> probes the expansion history. In a spatially flat model,
$$
d_L(z)=(1+z)c\int_0^z\frac{dz'}{H(z')},\qquad d_L(z)=\frac c{H_0}\left[z+\frac{1-q_0}{2}z^2+O(z^3)\right],\qquad q_0=-\frac{a\ddot a}{\dot a^2}\bigg|_0.
$$
For fixed low-redshift <calibration>, a negative $q_0$ increases the second-order distance relative to a decelerating model. Objects at a given <redshift> are therefore fainter. A <Hubble diagram> spanning enough <redshift> can distinguish this curvature from the common magnitude intercept. In matter-plus-cosmological-constant models, $q_0=\Omega_m/2-\Omega_\Lambda$; fitting the distances can test whether $q_0<0$. The two original supernova teams found distances supporting an accelerating expansion in that framework.<Observational Evidence from Supernovae for an Accelerating Universe and a Cosmological Constant by Adam Riess and others>[abstract and cosmological interpretation of standardized supernova luminosity distances]{ref}<Measurements of Omega and Lambda from 42 High-Redshift Supernovae by Saul Perlmutter and others>[abstract; cosmological likelihood constraints and tests of systematic uncertainty]{ref}

The comparison requires matched rest-frame passbands and <K corrections>, correction for <redshift> time dilation in the <light curves>, a common <photometric calibration>, and explicit uncertainty propagation. Dust <extinction> can imitate dimming; multicolour and <infrared> observations test reddening and the adopted <extinction> law. Evolution in the progenitor population or host-galaxy dependence can alter standardization, so one compares matched populations and fits host-dependent terms where warranted. Flux-limited discovery introduces <Malmquist bias> and other selection effects, which must be modelled through the survey detection efficiency. <peculiar velocities> matter at low <redshift>, while <gravitational lensing> and intrinsic scatter broaden the distant sample. Classification and outlier tests reduce contamination by other transient types.

Independent data reductions, nearby anchors, spectroscopic checks and a fit over the full <redshift> range distinguish an expansion-history signal from one instrumental offset. Complementary cosmological observations help separate matter <density>, curvature and dark-energy parameters. **The acceleration inference comes from the relative distance-redshift relation, conditional on standardization and an expansion model.** Distant supernovae integrate the past expansion history; they do not directly measure the instantaneous second time derivative of today's <scale factor> without such modelling.

In a <CCD>, absorbed optical <photons> create photoelectrons collected in electrostatic wells beneath gate electrodes. During readout, clock voltages move the charge packets row by row into a serial register; serial clocks move them one <pixel> at a time to an output node. The node converts charge to a voltage, approximately $\Delta V=Q/C$, which is amplified and digitized. <pixel> addresses are recovered from the row and serial-clock sequence. Resetting and sampling the output between packets establishes the baseline. Efficient transfer preserves charge; trapping or incomplete transfer produces loss and trailing. This is <CCD clocked charge transfer>, not independent wiring of an <amplifier> to every imaging <pixel>.<ASCA Technical Description: SIS by NASA HEASARC>[§7, SIS: charge generation, electrode-clock transfer and preamplifier readout]{ref}

<dark current> is thermally generated charge accumulated even without illumination. If its mean rate is $d$ <electrons> per <pixel> per second, a single exposure adds mean $dt$ and Poisson <variance> $dt$. Cooling the detector and using low-defect material suppresses this generation. A dark <calibration> removes the reproducible mean pattern, but cannot remove the dark <electrons>' random <shot noise> in the science exposure. <read noise> comes from measuring the output packet and resetting the sense node, <amplifiers> and electronics; write its rms as $r$ <electrons> per read. <correlated double sampling> reduces reset and correlated offsets by subtracting the baseline measurement from the signal measurement. Low-noise electronics and an appropriate slower readout reduce the remaining contribution. The <CCD noise budget> for $M$ separate exposures combined over total time $t$ contains independent dark and read contributions $dt+Mr^2$, not $(dt)^2+M^2r^2$. Long exposures reduce repeated <read noise>, but saturation, cosmic rays and tracking requirements constrain the exposure length. On-chip binning can reduce read-noise penalties for a fixed binned area at the cost of angular sampling.

For the source and sky calculation, define the count conversion factor $K=At\Delta\nu/(h\nu)$ and the source flux per <pixel> $s_\nu=F_\nu/N_{\rm pix}$. The ideal source and background <electron> counts are $S=Ks_\nu$ and $B=Kb_\nu\Omega_{\rm pix}$. These assume unit throughput and <quantum efficiency>, approximately constant quantities across the band and a well-determined mean sky level from many background <pixels>. Source and sky <photons> have independent Poisson fluctuations, so the <variance> after subtracting that mean sky is $S+B$. Subtracting a background removes its mean, not its <photon> noise. Therefore
$$
R=\frac S{\sqrt{S+B}}=\frac{F_\nu}{N_{\rm pix}}\left[\frac{At\Delta\nu}{h\nu(F_\nu/N_{\rm pix}+b_\nu\Omega_{\rm pix})}\right]^{1/2}.
$$
Thus **the required expression follows directly from adding photon-count <variances>**. Finite sky-estimation noise, <dark current> and <read noise> would add further <variance>. A nonunit common efficiency replaces $A$ by the effective collecting area; a nonflat spectrum requires the corresponding <frequency> integral. For example, estimating the sky from one equally exposed background <pixel> would add an extra $B$, whereas a large sky region makes that extra term small.

With $A=\pi(1\,\mathrm m)^2$ and the supplied <photon> <frequency>, the sky flux per <pixel> is $b_\nu\Omega_{\rm pix}=1.75\times10^{-32}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,Hz^{-1}}$. Multiplying arcsecond-squared <pixel> area by brightness per arcsecond squared already gives a flux: no additional steradian conversion belongs in this product. Rearranging gives
$$
\frac t{R^2}=\frac{h\nu}{A\Delta\nu}\frac{F_\nu/N_{\rm pix}+b_\nu\Omega_{\rm pix}}{(F_\nu/N_{\rm pix})^2}.
$$

The first case is mainly source-shot-noise limited, while the last is strongly sky limited. At high source brightness $t/R^2\propto F_\nu^{-1}$; at low source brightness it approaches $F_\nu^{-2}$. Reducing source flux by ten can therefore require nearly a hundred times the exposure once sky noise dominates. These are per-pixel values for the ideal model, not guaranteed integration times including <calibration>, cosmic rays or <read noise>.

Increasing the diameter increases both source and sky <photon> rates as $D^2$ for fixed angular <pixels>. In this ideal limit the required exposure decreases as $D^{-2}$, whereas the stated <telescope> cost increases as $D^{2.5}$: collecting area grows only as cost to the power $0.8$. A larger ground <telescope> does not remove atmospheric <photons> or recover arbitrary wavefront information lost in a long seeing-limited exposure. Space observations avoid atmospheric airglow, weather and <atmospheric turbulence>, and offer stable high-resolution imaging and access to very faint structure. They still have zodiacal light, instrumental backgrounds and their own <calibration> limits. <adaptive optics> can provide excellent ground-based resolution in suitable fields, but its guide-star, <wavelength> and field-dependent limitations do not remove the need for space-based continuum imaging. **The need is for complementary low-background, stable and high-resolution measurements; the cost exponent alone is not a proof that every imaging programme is cheaper in space.**

For X rays, one fully absorbed <photon> produces many <electron-hole pairs> in <silicon>, rather than one optical photoelectron count. At moderate <surface brightness>, the <probability> of two <photons> landing in the same event island during one <CCD> frame is small. The charge packet or sum over neighbouring split-event <pixels> then supplies a pulse height for each <photon>, while its <pixel> position supplies the image coordinate. Calibrating pulse height against energy produces an event list from which a spectrum can be accumulated for any spatial region. <photon pile-up> at larger occupancies merges <photon> energies and corrupts both spectra and event grades, so fast enough frames and event-pattern screening are essential.<ASCA Technical Description: SIS by NASA HEASARC>[§7, SIS: silicon electron-hole yield, charge collection and imaging spectroscopy]{ref}

A measured <silicon> pair-creation energy $w\simeq3.65\,\mathrm{eV}$ gives $N_e=E/w\simeq2.74\times10^3$ <electrons> for a $10\,\mathrm{keV}$ <photon>. Let $F$ be the <Fano factor> of pair production, $n_r$ the number of independently read <pixels> in the event sum, and $r$ the rms <read noise> per <pixel>. Then
$$
\sigma_E=w\sqrt{F\frac Ew+n_rr^2+\sigma_d^2+\cdots},\qquad \Delta E_{\rm FWHM}=2\sqrt{2\ln2}\,\sigma_E.
$$
The <Fano-limited X-ray CCD energy resolution> using the representative <silicon> value $F\simeq0.12$ gives an ideal Fano-limited **$\Delta E_{\rm FWHM}\simeq0.16\,\mathrm{keV}$ at $10\,\mathrm{keV}$**, or resolving power $E/\Delta E\simeq64$. Finite <read noise>, charge loss and event summing broaden this; an order-of-magnitude practical estimate is a few tenths of a keV, but the particular detector's value is not determined by the given optical-pixel parameters. These $w$ and $F$ values are external detector properties, not supplied question data. Treating every pair as independent Poisson production instead would give $0.19\,\mathrm{keV}$ rms, or $0.45\,\mathrm{keV}$ FWHM; stating which width is meant avoids conflating those estimates. Radiation-induced charge-transfer inefficiency requires additional gain and spectral-response <calibration>.<The ASCA Data Reduction Guide by NASA HEASARC>[§4.7, non-uniform CTI: silicon yield, Fano factor near 0.12 and FWHM resolution formula]{ref}
