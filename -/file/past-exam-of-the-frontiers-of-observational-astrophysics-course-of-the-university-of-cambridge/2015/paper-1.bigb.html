**Within-model inference.** The data $\mathbf d$ are the measurements, $H$ specifies a <statistical model> including its sampling assumptions, and $\theta$ is its unknown parameter or parameter vector. The posterior $p(\theta\mid\mathbf d,H)$ describes parameter uncertainty after the observation. The <likelihood> $\mathcal L(\theta)=p(\mathbf d\mid\theta,H)$ describes how that observed data set depends on a proposed parameter. The parameter prior $\pi(\theta\mid H)=p(\theta\mid H)$ encodes information before these data. The evidence $Z_H=p(\mathbf d\mid H)$ is the prior predictive <probability density> of the data and normalizes the posterior:
$$
\boxed{Z_H=\int\mathcal L(\theta)\pi(\theta\mid H)\,d\theta.}
$$
For continuous observations the <likelihood> and evidence are densities in data space, rather than <probabilities> of one exact observation. The evidence compares different models only when they use the same data and reference measure.

**Uniform parameter priors.** Normalize each model's top-hat prior, so $\pi_H=1/(\theta_2-\theta_1)$ inside its allowed interval and zero elsewhere. Then
$$
\boxed{Z_H=\frac1{\theta_2-\theta_1}\int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2}\mathcal L(\theta)\,d\theta.}
$$
Thus evidence is the average <likelihood> over the allowed parameter range, not its maximum and not the unnormalized area alone. Interpret the supplied measured distribution as proportional to a common <likelihood>, $\mathcal L(\theta)=C\exp[-|100u-\theta|/(50u)]$, where the common constant $C$ cancels in model comparisons. If that distribution were instead a posterior calculated using a nonuniform reference prior, it would first need to be divided by that prior. No such alternative prior is specified here.

**Evidence calculation.** Put $x=\theta/u$. The three integrals, divided by the same $C$, are
$$
\begin{aligned}
Z_A/C&=\frac1{10}\int_{50}^{60}e^{(x-100)/50}dx
=5(e^{-0.8}-e^{-1})=0.407247615,\\
Z_B/C&=\frac1{20}\left[\int_{90}^{100}e^{(x-100)/50}dx+
\int_{100}^{110}e^{-(x-100)/50}dx\right]
=5(1-e^{-0.2})=0.906346235,\\
Z_C/C&=\frac1{100}\left[\int_{50}^{100}e^{(x-100)/50}dx+
\int_{100}^{150}e^{-(x-100)/50}dx\right]
=1-e^{-1}=0.632120559.
\end{aligned}
$$
Consequently the three pairwise <Bayes factors> may be given as
$$
\boxed{\frac{Z_B}{Z_A}=2.22554,\qquad
\frac{Z_B}{Z_C}=1.43382,\qquad
\frac{Z_C}{Z_A}=1.55218.}
$$
**B has the largest evidence.** Model A is narrow but lies away from the <likelihood> peak. Model C contains that peak but spreads much of its normalized prior over less well-fitting values. Model B concentrates its parameter prior near the peak and achieves the best average. Width is therefore not a universal penalty by itself: changing an interval can also improve its location. At fixed centre near a localized <likelihood> peak, extending the interval into poorly fitting values dilutes the evidence.

{title=Original likelihood averages over the three top-hat prior intervals; bars show evidence and separately prior-weighted model probabilities}

**Between-model prior information.** The supplied $R_A:R_B:R_C=1:1:0.3$ are model prior odds, distinct from each model's uniform parameter prior. They multiply the evidences to give posterior model weights. The corresponding posterior odds are
$$
\boxed{\frac{p(B\mid\mathbf d)}{p(A\mid\mathbf d)}=2.22554,\qquad
\frac{p(B\mid\mathbf d)}{p(C\mid\mathbf d)}=4.77940,\qquad
\frac{p(A\mid\mathbf d)}{p(C\mid\mathbf d)}=2.14752.}
$$
Normalizing these weights gives $p(A\mid\mathbf d)=0.270915$, $p(B\mid\mathbf d)=0.602933$ and $p(C\mid\mathbf d)=0.126152$. **B is preferred with either equal model priors or the stated theoretical prior odds.** Multiplying by $R$ changes posterior odds; it does not redefine the <Bayes factors>.

**Euclidean <luminosity> selection.** In a flux-limited catalogue, a source with isotropic <luminosity> $L$ is detected out to
$$
r_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{L}{4\pi f_{\min}}},\qquad
V_{\max}\propto r_{\max}^3\propto L^{3/2}.
$$
A homogeneous Euclidean population with intrinsic <luminosity function> $\varphi(L)$ is sampled approximately as $dN_{\rm det}/dL\propto\varphi(L)L^{3/2}$. Brighter objects occupy a much larger accessible volume and are overrepresented; at a fixed large distance the faint tail is missing entirely. This is <Malmquist bias>. Using the selected population as if it were representative can bias <luminosity> <calibrations> or inferred correlations with $\theta$. A selection function, a volume-limited sample, or appropriate accessible-volume weighting can address the sampling effect, subject to completeness assumptions.

The Euclidean derivation concerns flux-limited luminous objects or selected tracers associated with the structures. A <CMB> temperature map is a diffuse surface-brightness field on a cosmological last-scattering surface: it is not automatically a Euclidean catalogue of identical inverse-square point sources. For a particular CMB-structure catalogue, its actual detection statistic and selection function must be specified before applying this luminosity-weighting law quantitatively.

**Delay-compensated geometry.** Choose an east-west baseline vector $\mathbf b=b\hat{\mathbf x}$ and let the upward unit vector be $\hat{\mathbf z}$. A ray direction at zenith angle $\beta$ in their plane has $\mathbf s(\beta)=(\sin\beta,0,\cos\beta)$. Its signed geometric path difference is $\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf s=b\sin\beta$. Removing the phase-centre delay $b\sin\theta/c$ leaves the residual path
$$
\delta\ell=b[\sin(\theta+\alpha)-\sin\theta]
\simeq b\alpha\cos\theta.
$$
With the receiver ordering chosen to give positive phase for this path, a complex correlator measures
$$
\boxed{V(\alpha)\propto e^{ik\delta\ell}
\simeq\exp(ikb\alpha\cos\theta).}
$$
The reverse ordering complex-conjugates the expression without changing its information. A single real multiplier supplies its cosine component; the stated exponential represents an in-phase/quadrature correlator. Equal cable lengths to the central correlator remove a fixed instrumental difference but do not remove the changing <geometric delay>. Pointing the <antennas> and electronically compensating the path are separate operations.

\Image[paper-1-interferometer-geometry.png]
{title=Original east-west interferometer geometry and normalized visibility of a centred one-dimensional uniform source}

**Resolved uniform source.** Contributions from mutually incoherent sky elements add as brightness-weighted <complex visibilities>. Put $I(\alpha)=I_0$ over $-\Delta\alpha/2<\alpha<\Delta\alpha/2$ and zero elsewhere, and define $q=kb\cos\theta$. Integrating gives
$$
V=I_0\int_{-\Delta\alpha/2}^{\Delta\alpha/2}e^{iq\alpha}d\alpha
=I_0\Delta\alpha\,\frac{\sin(q\Delta\alpha/2)}{q\Delta\alpha/2}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{V(b)}{V(0)}=\operatorname{sinc}\left(\frac{kb\Delta\alpha\cos\theta}{2}\right),
\qquad\operatorname{sinc}x=\frac{\sin x}{x}.}
$$
The zero-argument value is defined by continuity as one. Normalization by $V(0)=I_0\Delta\alpha$ isolates the visibility shape from total <flux>. The first null occurs when the source spans one fringe period, $\Delta\alpha=\lambda/(|b\cos\theta|)$. A negative real visibility beyond a null is a phase reversal, not negative <surface brightness>.

**Path-compensation precision.** A finite observing band sets a coherence envelope. For a rectangular band of width $\Delta\nu$, an uncorrected residual delay $\delta\tau=\delta\ell/c$ multiplies the correlation by
$$
e^{2\pi i\nu_0\delta\tau}\operatorname{sinc}(\pi\Delta\nu\delta\tau).
$$
Coarse delay steps must leave $\Delta\nu|\delta\tau|\ll1$ if decorrelation is to be small. Thus a coarse path-step scale must be well below the <coherence length> $c/\Delta\nu$, with the precise bound determined by the tolerated amplitude loss and maximum quantization residual. A digital fine-delay or phase-rotation system can separately correct the central-frequency phase.

If there is no such carrier-phase correction, the steps must also be a small fraction of $\lambda$ to avoid large untracked phase jumps or time-averaging loss. For a step $d_\ell$ rounded to the nearest setting, $|\delta\ell|\le d_\ell/2$ and the maximum carrier-phase error is $\pi d_\ell/\lambda$. **<Bandwidth> controls coarse group-delay decorrelation; <wavelength> controls the precision of untracked carrier phase.** No numerical delay step can be inferred from the central <frequency> alone without a <bandwidth>, loss tolerance and compensation architecture. Earth rotation additionally sets how rapidly the delay setting must be updated.

**Largest sampled angular scale.** For a near-zenith view take the shortest projected baseline as $b_{\min}\simeq0.5\ {\rm m}$. At $\nu=30\ {\rm GHz}$, $\lambda=c/\nu=9.9931\times10^{-3}\ {\rm m}$. The inverse shortest <spatial frequency> gives
$$
\boxed{\Theta_{\max}\sim\frac{\lambda}{b_{\min}}
=0.0199862\ {\rm rad}=68.7\ {\rm arcmin}\approx1.15\text{ degrees}.}
$$
This is an order-of-magnitude largest fringe scale. A convention defining maximum recoverable source size as $0.6\lambda/b_{\min}$ would instead give about $41$ arcminutes; source profile, weighting and projected baseline coverage determine the operational value. The <antenna> diameter and <primary beam> can further restrict the usable field but were explicitly neglected in the source's initial idealization. Ten <antennas> supply $10\times9/2=45$ pairwise baselines; their number improves sampling and sensitivity, not the inverse-shortest-baseline scale by a factor of ten. The longest baseline is not supplied, so the finest synthesized resolution is not numerically fixed.

**A suitable observing programme.** A compact centimetre-wave array is well suited to <CMB> temperature anisotropy on degree to sub-degree scales sampled by its baselines, and, with sufficiently long baselines and suitable foreground control, thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich structure in <galaxy clusters>. At $30\ {\rm GHz}$ the thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich signal is a decrement relative to the <CMB>. The compact baselines retain sensitivity to extended brightness features; the <spatial frequencies> measured by each baseline have a direct statistical interpretation in <CMB> analysis.

Compared with lower radio <frequencies>, synchrotron foregrounds are generally reduced, while thermal dust rises toward higher <frequencies>; observing near this centimetre-wave atmospheric window is therefore useful, although foregrounds are not absent. Correlation and phase switching reject mean uncorrelated receiver offsets and some instrumental drifts. Receiver <thermal noise> still contributes <variance>, and atmosphere, ground pickup or cross-talk can be correlated and require control. Additional long baselines can identify compact radio-source contaminants for subtraction, but the question does not specify enough array geometry to guarantee that capability. There is no zero baseline in ordinary pairwise cross-correlation, so the array does not directly measure the sky monopole or arbitrary larger-scale smooth emission.

**<Stellar reflex motion>.** A <planet> and its host orbit their common <barycentre>. The <star>'s line-of-sight velocity shifts its <absorption lines> by $\Delta\lambda/\lambda\simeq v_r/c$ at low speeds. A time series can be fitted with
$$
v_r(t)=\gamma+K[\cos(\nu(t)+\varpi)+e\cos\varpi],\qquad
K=\left(\frac{2\pi G}{P}\right)^{1/3}
\frac{M_p\sin i}{(M_\star+M_p)^{2/3}\sqrt{1-e^2}}.
$$
Here $\nu(t)$ is <true anomaly>, $\varpi$ the argument of periastron and $\gamma$ an arbitrary systemic velocity offset. At small companion mass and known stellar mass, the orbit supplies $M_p\sin i$, not the true <planet> mass without an inclination constraint. Long monitoring helps distinguish a reproducible Keplerian signal from stellar oscillations, magnetic activity, spots and instrumental trends.

**The Lick reference-cell implementation.** Starlight passes through a temperature-controlled cell of molecular <iodine> before entering the Hamilton high-resolution echelle <spectrograph>. The cell imprints a dense pattern of reference <absorption lines> on the stellar spectrum, predominantly in the approximately $500$–$630\ {\rm nm}$ interval. These lines share the same exposure and downstream optical path as the stellar light. A calibrated high-resolution Fourier-transform <iodine> spectrum and an iodine-free stellar template allow the <wavelength> solution and instrumental line-spread function to be fitted along with the stellar <Doppler shift>. The method corrects shifts and line-shape changes that a separate <calibration> exposure would not necessarily follow.<Precision Radial Velocities with an Iodine Absorption Cell by Geoffrey W. Marcy and R. Paul Butler>[Marcy and Butler (1992), sections 3–4, printed pages 271–274: Hamilton setup, iodine wavelength interval, templates and in-situ instrumental profile]{ref}

A useful forward model for one short spectral segment is
$$
\boxed{f_{\rm obs}(\lambda)=
\left\{S\!\left(\frac{\lambda}{1+v/c}\right)T_{\rm I_2}(\lambda)\right\}
*\mathcal P(\lambda),}
$$
followed by pixel integration, continuum scaling and a fitted <wavelength> <calibration>. Here $S$ is the intrinsic stellar spectrum, $T_{\rm I_2}$ the cell transmission and $\mathcal P$ the instrumental line-spread function. The spectra multiply before <convolution>; simply dividing a recorded blended spectrum by the laboratory <iodine> spectrum is not equivalent to this model. The <iodine> pattern is stationary in the observatory frame while stellar lines move relative to it. Separate spectral segments accommodate <wavelength> dependence of the instrumental profile.

\Image[paper-1-iodine-forward-model.png]
{title=Original synthetic iodine-cell forward model showing multiplication before instrumental convolution; all line positions and strengths are illustrative}

Earth's rotational and orbital velocities must then be removed with a barycentric correction using accurate timing, ideally weighted by <photon> arrival rate. Many stellar lines constrain a shift smaller than one instrumental resolution element; spectral resolution alone is not the velocity error. <Photon> statistics, template quality, instrumental-profile modelling and intrinsic stellar variability limit the precision. The method measures changes relative to a template and does not by itself provide a perfectly absolute centre-of-mass velocity. No particular discovery, modern precision floor or historical observing performance is assumed here.

<Atmospheric turbulence> produces time-dependent refractive-index fluctuations and phase errors across the telescope pupil. A large aperture's long-exposure <point spread function> is broadened approximately to $0.98\lambda/r_0$, where $r_0$ is the <Fried parameter>, rather than the <diffraction> scale $\lambda/D$. For fixed turbulence, $r_0\propto\lambda^{6/5}$, so the <seeing> angle improves weakly as $\lambda^{-1/5}$. The <atmospheric coherence time> scales with $r_0$ divided by an effective wind speed. This temporal scale concerns changing turbulence, not the optical <bandwidth>'s coherence time.

**Reduce the disturbance.** A good high-altitude site, favourable weather, observing near the zenith, thermal control and dome ventilation reduce atmospheric and local <seeing>. They improve the uncorrected image but do not remove all high-order wavefront distortion. <Active optics> slowly maintains the main mirror's figure and alignment; it is distinct from the rapid adaptive correction of atmospheric phase errors.

**Correct the wavefront.** <Adaptive optics> uses a nearby natural <guide star> or artificial <laser guide star>, a <wavefront sensor>, a real-time controller and a <deformable mirror>. A Shack-Hartmann sensor estimates local wavefront slopes; mirror actuators apply the opposite optical-path distortion fast enough to track the atmosphere. A separate fast steering element or the mirror can correct image motion. In an ideal well-corrected direction this approaches <diffraction-limited resolution> and increases the concentration of light in the central image.

Finite guide-star <flux>, actuator sampling, control latency and <anisoplanatism> leave residual errors. A laser beacon samples a finite-height cone rather than the full distant-source cylinder, and a conventional laser reference does not determine absolute tip-tilt, so a natural reference remains useful. Multiple <guide stars> and multiple conjugate mirrors can widen the corrected field, with extra sensing and reconstruction complexity. These are fundamental geometry and control limits, not claims of a universal corrected sky fraction.<Adaptive Optics by European Southern Observatory>[Single Conjugate Adaptive Optics and Multi-Conjugate Adaptive Optics sections: bandwidth, fitting, anisoplanatism, laser-beacon tilt and cone limitations]{ref}

**Freeze and reconstruct.** Short exposures can freeze the instantaneous <speckle pattern>. <Speckle interferometry> uses calibrated Fourier statistics to recover diffraction-scale structure; power-spectrum information alone loses phase, while <bispectrum> or other phase-sensitive measurements support image reconstruction. <Lucky imaging> selects sharp short frames, registers them and combines them, trading <photon> efficiency for improved image quality. Tip-tilt alignment alone removes image motion rather than all high-order aberration. These methods require sufficiently bright sources or references and can incur substantial read-noise penalties. Deconvolving a long-exposure image is useful only where a known <point spread function> and adequate signal preserve information; it cannot recover arbitrarily lost <spatial frequencies>. Moving to space avoids atmospheric <seeing>, although it changes the observing platform and its limitations rather than correcting a ground-based telescope.

**Weak transitions in dilute gas.** A <forbidden emission line> is a transition absent in the electric-dipole approximation because of its <selection rules>, but permitted through weaker channels such as magnetic-dipole or electric-quadrupole radiation, sometimes aided by state mixing. It is not an impossible transition. Its upper atomic level is relatively long lived, so collisions can deactivate it before a <photon> is emitted in dense gas. Low-density astrophysical plasmas allow radiative decay to compete successfully.

In a schematic two-level statistical equilibrium,
$$
n_l n_e q_{lu}=n_u(A_{ul}+n_eq_{ul}),\qquad
\epsilon_{ul}=n_uA_{ul}h\nu_{ul}.
$$
The critical density is $n_{\rm crit}=A_{ul}/q_{ul}$ for this simple level system; multiple radiative and collisional channels require summed rates. At $n_e\ll n_{\rm crit}$ each <collisional excitation> is likely to produce radiation, so $\epsilon_{ul}\propto n_l n_e q_{lu}h\nu$. At high density collisional de-excitation suppresses the line relative to this low-density expectation. The rate need not decrease absolutely with increasing density; the suppression is relative to the naive excitation scaling.

**A temperature-sensitive ratio.** For a Maxwell-Boltzmann electron distribution,
$$
q_{lu}(T)\propto T^{-1/2}\Upsilon_{lu}(T)
\exp[-\Delta E_{lu}/(k_BT)],
$$
where $\Upsilon$ is an effective collision strength. Lines from upper levels with different excitation energies therefore have a strongly temperature-dependent ratio. Using one ion reduces sensitivity to abundance and <ionization> fraction compared with comparing unrelated species.

A standard example is doubly ionized oxygen: the $4959$ and $5007$ angstrom lines descend from ${}^1D_2$ toward the ${}^3P$ ground term, while the $4363$ angstrom line connects ${}^1S_0$ to ${}^1D_2$. The higher excitation threshold makes the auroral $4363$ line relatively stronger in hotter gas. In the low-density Maxwellian approximation, with slowly varying collision strengths,
$$
\boxed{R_{\rm OIII}=\frac{I_{4959}+I_{5007}}{I_{4363}}
\simeq7.90\exp\left(\frac{32900\ {\rm K}}{T_e}\right),
\qquad T_e\simeq\frac{32900\ {\rm K}}{\ln(R_{\rm OIII}/7.90)}.}
$$
The numerical form is a useful approximation, not a universal exact atomic law; a multilevel atomic calculation with current collision strengths is needed for precise work.<Resolving the electron temperature discrepancies in H II regions and planetary nebulae: kappa-distributed electrons by David C. Nicholls and others>[Section 4, equation 18 and the following approximation warning; arXiv:1204.3880v2, PDF pages 5–6]{ref}

\Image[paper-1-oxygen-thermometer.png]
{title=Original oxygen forbidden-line level schematic and low-density Maxwellian temperature-ratio curve; level spacings are approximate}

In an <active galactic nucleus> these lines commonly diagnose the relatively dilute narrow-line gas; dense broad-line gas can collisionally quench them. Measure and deblend the <fluxes>, subtract continuum and stellar features, correct differential <extinction>, and check density independently. The $5007/4959$ ratio mainly tests radiative branching and <calibration> because both lines share one upper level; it is not the required temperature thermometer. Temperature gradients, multiple density components, <shocks>, <ionization> structure and departures from the Maxwellian electron distribution can bias a single inferred temperature. Without a measured ratio and density regime, no particular AGN temperature is numerically determined here.

Measure counts in electrons so that <photon> and detector <noise> can be combined directly. For source electrons $S$, $n$ aperture pixels, total time $t$, sky rate $b$ per pixel, dark-current rate $d$ per pixel, $M$ reads with rms <read noise> $r$ per pixel, and an independently estimated mean background per pixel of <variance> $\sigma_{\widehat B}^2$, an ideal aperture-sum budget is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(\widehat S)
\simeq S+n(b+d)t+nMr^2+n^2\sigma_{\widehat B}^2
+\sigma_{\rm cal}^2,\qquad
{\rm SNR}=\frac{S}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\widehat S)}}.}
$$
The last term represents the propagated <calibration> uncertainty if it can be treated as a random <variance>; systematic residuals can instead bias the measurement and need an explicit error model. For a background estimated from $n_b$ independent, equally exposed sky pixels, one approximation is $\sigma_{\widehat B}^2=[(b+d)t+Mr^2]/n_b$. Correlated resampling, detector cross-talk or a spatially structured sky invalidates the independent-pixel simplification.

**<Photon> counting and sky.** Source <photon shot noise> has <variance> $S$ and cannot be calibrated away. Longer integration or greater throughput improves its fractional error as $S^{-1/2}$. Sky <photons>, including moonlight, airglow and scattered light, add their own Poisson fluctuations. A dark site, appropriate observing time and filter, good baffling and a smaller or optimally weighted source aperture reduce sky contributions. Better <seeing> or <adaptive optics> can concentrate a point source into fewer noise-bearing pixels. Sky subtraction removes the mean background but not the random sky <photons> already recorded, and the sky estimate adds uncertainty of its own.

**Dark and <read noise>.** Thermal <dark current> contributes a mean and shot <noise>. Cooling reduces its rate; well-matched dark frames remove a reproducible mean and map hot pixels, but do not undo the science exposure's random dark-current fluctuations. Each read adds electronic <noise>. At fixed total integration, many short exposures add more read <variance> than fewer long exposures, so use exposures long enough to become sky limited when saturation, <cosmic rays> and temporal changes permit. Multiple non-destructive reads can help on detectors that support them. Subtracting a bias frame corrects an electronic offset, not the stochastic <read noise>.

**<Calibration> and non-Poisson effects.** Pixel sensitivity differences, illumination gradients, fringes, nonlinearity, imperfect charge transfer and electronic pattern <noise> leave <calibration> errors. High-count flat fields, stable operating conditions, linearity <calibration>, dithering and appropriate detector models help, while the finite signal-to-noise of <calibration> frames propagates into the result. Cosmic-ray hits, hot pixels, satellite trails and scattered-light artefacts are not ordinary stationary Gaussian <noise>: identify or mask them, often using several exposures. Overaggressive clipping can remove real transient or compact-source signal.

<Atmospheric scintillation> adds correlated intensity fluctuations, especially in short bright-star measurements; <seeing> changes and guiding errors move <flux> between pixels or apertures. Differential photometry using appropriate reference <stars> and stable apertures reduces common variations but does not guarantee removal of chromatic or position-dependent effects. For a constant flux-limited source in independent stationary <noise> the final signal-to-noise usually grows as $\sqrt t$ once background limited; a systematic floor or correlated sky can stop that improvement. Distinguish improvement of <calibration>, which removes reproducible bias, from elimination of intrinsically random <photon> arrivals, which is impossible.
