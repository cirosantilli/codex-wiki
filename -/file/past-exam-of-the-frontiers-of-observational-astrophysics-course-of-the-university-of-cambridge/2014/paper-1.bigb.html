= Solution
{parent=1}

The detector is intended for arcsecond-scale <astronomical seeing>, rather than the telescope's <diffraction limit>. A circular <aperture> of this area has diameter $1.13\,\mathrm m$, giving a <Rayleigh criterion> angle about $0.11$ arcsec at the observing <wavelength>. One-arcsecond pixels conventionally sample an <astronomical seeing> profile of roughly two arcseconds across with two samples; a one-arcsecond profile would be undersampled. **The pixel scale alone does not determine an exact <astronomical seeing> width.**

Use the supplied <surface brightness> as <radiative flux> integrated over the <photometric passband>: its units contain no inverse hertz. Treat $h\nu$ as the representative <photon energy>, and assume unit throughput and <quantum efficiency>. A genuinely frequency-specific brightness would require a <bandwidth>. With angles measured consistently in arcseconds, an annulus has area $2\pi r\,dr$ square arcseconds, so
$$
F(r_m)=\int_0^{r_m}2\pi r\,a(1-kr)\,dr
=\boxed{2\pi a\left(\frac{r_m^2}{2}-\frac{kr_m^3}{3}\right)}.
$$
This is flux per collecting area; collected <energy> is $AtF$. There is no extra conversion to <steradians> when both brightness and angular area use square arcseconds.

For <Poisson photon counting>, a pixel with expected count $N$ has <energy> $e=Nh\nu$ and <standard deviation> $\sigma_e=h\nu\sqrt N$. Thus the local four-sigma rule gives $N=4\sqrt N$, or **16 <photons> at the boundary**. With pixel area $\Delta\Omega=1\,\mathrm{arcsec}^2$,
$$
h\nu=3.9756\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J,\qquad
N(r)=\frac{At\Delta\Omega\,a}{h\nu}(1-kr)=32.6991(1-kr).
$$
Consequently $r_m=(1-16/32.6991)/k=10.2138$ arcsec and
$$
\boxed{E_m=AtF(r_m)=2.8100\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm J}.
$$
Integrating to the model's outer radius $R=1/k=20$ arcsec instead gives
$$
\boxed{F_{\rm total}=\frac{\pi a}{3k^2}=5.4454\times10^{-17}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}},
\qquad E_{\rm total}=5.4454\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm J.
$$
The pixel cut retains **51.6%** of this total. It is an imposed <isophotal photometry> rule, not a fundamental limit: averaging annuli or fitting a justified profile can recover signal below the per-pixel cut.

{title=Original photon-counting profile and fraction of total flux inside the imposed detection radius}

In practice, <sky background> <photons> add <shot noise> even after subtraction of the mean sky. <read noise>, <dark current>, imperfect <flat-field correction>, throughput <calibration> and <atmospheric extinction> also matter. At large radius the dominant uncertainty can be a tiny error in the sky level, scattered light or the wings of the <point spread function>, rather than source <photon> statistics. Mask foreground <stars> and background objects, compare blank-sky regions and dithered exposures, and check profile recovery by inserting artificial extended sources. <astronomical seeing> and finite pixels convolve the intrinsic profile; model that response when extrapolating the total.

The <luminosity convergence of an outer surface-brightness profile> follows directly: $I\propto r^{-p}$ contributes $dF\propto r^{1-p}dr$, hence $F(<R)\propto R^{2-p}$ at large radius for $p<2$. For either shallow index considered, ever larger annuli dominate: extending the law to infinity gives an unphysical divergent <luminosity>. Such a law must eventually steepen or truncate. Over a finite range, however, substantial light can lie below a <surface-brightness selection> threshold and be mistaken for sky. This biases <galaxy> <luminosities>, the <luminosity function> and the inferred diffuse light budget; distant diffuse <galaxies> become especially incomplete because bolometric <cosmological surface-brightness dimming> scales as $(1+z)^{-4}$. An assumed finite-aperture total is therefore a model-dependent quantity.

The observed population is the parent population filtered by an <astronomical selection function> and measurement errors. <Malmquist bias> arises because a <flux-limited galaxy selection> admits luminous objects from larger volumes: in Euclidean space $d_{\max}\propto L^{1/2}$ and $V_{\max}\propto L^{3/2}$. Thus observed <luminosity> counts are weighted by $V_{\max}$. Even a nominal <standard candle> with intrinsic scatter is preferentially observed on its bright side near the limit; ignoring this can underestimate distances. A volume-limited sample or a justified $1/V_{\max}$ weighting helps, subject to completeness, geometry and evolution assumptions. Separately, <Eddington bias> occurs when noise scatters a steeply rising population across a detection threshold: more faint objects enter than bright objects leave.

<Lutz-Kelker bias> concerns noisy <stellar parallaxes> and population/selection weighting. Locally uniform spatial <density> gives $p(r)\propto r^2$ and, by changing variables, $p(\varpi)\propto\varpi^{-4}$ over the model's valid finite range. More distant <stars> are available to scatter into a parallax-limited sample than nearby <stars> to scatter out. Inverting an uncertain parallax also introduces a distinct nonlinear-estimation problem. **There is no universal magnitude correction for every <star>**: spatial <density>, <luminosity>, <extinction> and how the sample was selected all matter. The classical treatment motivates this issue; it should not replace an explicit generative model.<On the Use of Trigonometric Parallaxes for the Calibration of Luminosity Systems: Theory by T. E. Lutz and D. H. Kelker>[PASP 85, 573–578; classical uniform-density calibration model]{ref}

<Bayes' theorem> combines the measurement <likelihood function> with a justified <prior distribution>. For example, with a <Gaussian> parallax error and consistent distance/parallax units,
$$
p(r\mid\widehat\varpi)\propto
\exp\!\left[-\frac{(\widehat\varpi-1/r)^2}{2\sigma_\varpi^2}\right]p(r),\qquad r>0.
$$
A <proper distance prior for parallax inference> might have $p(r)\propto r^2e^{-r/L}$ with a justified scale $L$; extending $r^2$ to infinity gives an improper <posterior> here. Negative measured parallaxes remain valid noisy data. For <selection conditioning in Bayesian inference>, distinguish individual inference from population inference: if selection is determined entirely by the exact data already conditioned on, it adds no further information about that individual's parameters. For population parameters $\Lambda$, the selected-data <likelihood> must instead include the detection normalization $\alpha(\Lambda)=\int S(d)p(d\mid\Lambda)\,dd$. Applying a cut twice is also an error.

Selection can depend on <surface brightness>, <angular resolution>, dust, sky coverage, colour and observing cadence as well as total flux. Crowding and unresolved binaries bias distance calibrators. Transit surveys favour large <planets> and short periods, with an additional geometric transit probability approximately $R_\star/a$ for small <planets> on circular orbits; injection/recovery tests quantify the remaining <transit survey completeness>. Cluster catalogues select noisy mass proxies from a steep abundance distribution. Planck's 2013 SZ catalogue explicitly characterized detection completeness and reliability rather than identifying every detection with an unbiased mass census.<Planck 2013 results. XXIX. Planck catalogue of Sunyaev-Zeldovich sources by Planck Collaboration>[abstract and selection-function/completeness analysis, 2013 preprint]{ref} Calibrators, artificial-source tests and a forward model of detection, measurement and follow-up are the practical checks on any correction.

The <thermal Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect> is <inverse Compton scattering> of <cosmic microwave background> <photons> by hot cluster <electrons>. For an <optically thin>, nonrelativistic thermal <plasma>,
$$
y=\frac{\sigma_T}{m_ec^2}\int n_ek_BT_e\,dl,\qquad
\Delta I_\nu=I_0y\frac{x^4e^x}{(e^x-1)^2}\left[x\coth(x/2)-4\right],
\quad x=\frac{h\nu}{k_BT_{\rm CMB}},\quad I_0=\frac{2(k_BT_{\rm CMB})^3}{h^2c^2}.
$$
The <Compton y parameter> measures the <electron> <pressure> column. The distortion is a decrement below roughly **217 GHz**, an increment above it, and has $\Delta T/T=-2y$ in the <Rayleigh-Jeans law> limit. Relativistic <electron> corrections modify the spectrum and null.<Relativistic corrections to the Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect by A. D. Challinor and A. N. Lasenby>[abstract and relativistic thermal-distortion calculation]{ref}

Multifrequency maps separate this spectrum from primary <Cosmic microwave background anisotropy>, radio sources and dust; atmospheric subtraction, beams and <calibration> require control. The <kinetic Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect> has $\Delta T/T\simeq-\tau v_{\rm los}/c$ and a CMB-temperature-like spectrum, so it is both a contaminant and a peculiar-velocity probe. Planck demonstrated blind multifrequency cluster detection with external confirmation and a measured selection function.<Planck 2013 results. XXIX. Planck catalogue of Sunyaev-Zeldovich sources by Planck Collaboration>[abstract; multifrequency detection and catalogue validation]{ref}

For a fixed physical <pressure> column, $y$ has **no ordinary <cosmological surface-brightness dimming>**: the distorted spectrum and CMB redshift together. The <integrated Compton parameter> $Y=\int y\,d\Omega$ still scales as $D_A^{-2}$ for fixed integrated physical <pressure>. SZ observations therefore find distant clusters and measure thermal <pressure>; integrated $Y$ is a useful mass proxy, requiring <calibration> and a selection model for cosmological counts. X-ray emission scales approximately as $\int n_e^2\Lambda(T_e)dl$, while SZ scales as $\int n_eT_e dl$. Combining them and a measured <temperature> constrains <density>, depth and, with angular geometry, distance. <gas clumping factor>, asphericity, <temperature> structure and nonthermal support limit that inference.

An <astronomical interferometer> combines light from collectors separated by projected <interferometric baseline> $\mathbf B$. For a distant, narrowband, spatially incoherent source, the <Van Cittert-Zernike theorem> gives normalized <complex visibility>
$$
\mathcal V(\mathbf B/\lambda)=\frac{\int I(\boldsymbol\theta)e^{-2\pi i\mathbf B\cdot\boldsymbol\theta/\lambda}\,d^2\theta}{\int I(\boldsymbol\theta)\,d^2\theta}.
$$
The resolving scale is **$\lambda/B$**, while the individual collector and beam combiner restrict the field of view. Delay lines compensate geometric path differences. To see broadband fringes, residual <optical path length> mismatch must lie within the <coherence length> of order $\lambda^2/\Delta\lambda$; <fringe tracking> follows rapidly changing <atmospheric piston in interferometry>. Baseline changes and <Earth-rotation aperture synthesis> sample additional <spatial frequencies>.

For an ideal uniform circular <star> of full <angular diameter> $\Theta$,
$$
\mathcal V=\frac{2J_1(x)}x,\qquad x=\frac{\pi B\Theta}{\lambda},\qquad
\boxed{\Theta=1.2197\,\lambda/B_{\rm first\ null}}.
$$
Real <limb darkening> changes the conversion. Several baselines constrain binaries, <stellar radii> and circumstellar structure; orbital angular separations combined with <radial velocities> can give physical orbital scales. <calibration> <stars> test contrast losses, alongside <wavelength>, photometric imbalance, <polarization> and <bandwidth> controls. For three collectors the <closure phase> cancels telescope-based phase errors: $\phi_{12}+\phi_{23}+\phi_{31}$ retains source asymmetry information. Sparse baseline coverage still leaves image/model ambiguities.

In <stellar intensity interferometry>, thermal-light correlations instead give $g^{(2)}(0)-1=|g^{(1)}|^2$ for an ideal single-polarization resolved temporal mode. The Sirius experiment correlated separate detector outputs rather than optically combining the beams.<A Test of a New Type of Stellar Interferometer on Sirius by Robert Hanbury Brown and Richard Quentin Twiss>[Nature 178, 1046–1048 (1956)]{ref} Pure atmospheric phase shifts do not alter local <intensities>, but finite detector <bandwidth> dilutes the correlation and <photon> statistics limit sensitivity; the two-detector observable also loses the complex phase.

<annual stellar parallax> uses the known Earth-orbit baseline, with **$d(\mathrm{pc})=1/\varpi(\mathrm{arcsec})$**. Repeated positions separate parallax from <proper motion>; <calibration> offsets, binarity and noisy-parallax selection require checks. Nearby <stars> calibrate absolute <luminosities>. Extinction-corrected <distance modulus> then gives
$$
\boxed{m_\lambda-M_\lambda=5\log_{10}(d/10\,\mathrm{pc})+A_\lambda}.
$$
<main-sequence fitting> compares a cluster sequence with nearby calibrated <stars>; age, <metallicity>, reddening and unresolved binaries limit the match. <Cepheid variables> extend the range to nearby <galaxies>: measure periods and mean multiband magnitudes, calibrate the <Cepheid period-luminosity relation>, and correct for <extinction>, <metallicity>, crowding and pulsation mode. <RR Lyrae stars> and the <tip of the red giant branch> provide complementary population-II routes with their own composition and <calibration> dependence.

Some anchors bypass intermediate rungs. An <eclipsing-binary distance> starts from a double-lined <eclipsing binary>: velocities give $a\sin i$; eclipses give inclination $i$ and relative radii $R/a$, hence physical radii. An independently calibrated surface-brightness/colour relation gives angular radii and distance; alternatively corrected bolometric flux satisfies $F_{\rm bol}=\sigma T_{\rm eff}^4(R/d)^2$ for a component. <temperature>, <extinction>, third light and surface-brightness <calibration> remain systematic errors. Eight late-type binaries gave a 2.2%-accuracy <LMC> anchor in 2013.<An eclipsing-binary distance to the Large Magellanic Cloud accurate to two per cent by G. Pietrzyński and others>[abstract, Nature 495, 76–79; photometric and orbital information]{ref}

A <maser-disk geometric distance> uses water <masers> orbiting in the nearly edge-on nuclear disk of <NGC 4258>. <very-long-baseline interferometry>, Doppler velocities, <proper motions> and accelerations constrain its orbit scale and distance. In an ideal circular, matched-radius example $R=v^2/a_{\rm orb}$ and $d=R/\theta$; real fits account for disk warp and different <maser> locations. This geometric method calibrated an extragalactic distance independently of stellar <luminosities>.<A 4% Geometric Distance to the Galaxy NGC4258 from Orbital Motions in a Nuclear Gas Disk by J. R. Herrnstein and others>[abstract; orbital proper motions and accelerations, 1999]{ref} These overlapping anchors cross-check the nearby-galaxy ladder; no Hubble-flow fit is needed here.

= Solution

<thermal bremsstrahlung> is continuum radiation from <electrons> accelerated in ion electric fields. For <Maxwellian> <electrons>,
$$
j_\nu\propto n_e\sum_i Z_i^2n_i\,T_e^{-1/2}e^{-h\nu/(k_BT_e)}g_{\rm ff}.
$$
At high <frequency> the exponential cutoff constrains $T_e$; X-ray fitting must also model foreground absorption, line emission, detector response and possible multitemperature or nonthermal components. At radio <frequencies>, $h\nu\ll k_BT_e$, an <optically thin> free-free spectrum is almost flat, with the weak <frequency> dependence set mainly by the <Gaunt factor>.

For pure ionized hydrogen, radio <free-free opacity> gives
$$
\tau_\nu\propto T_e^{-3/2}\nu^{-2}g_{\rm ff}\,\mathrm{EM},\qquad
\mathrm{EM}=\int n_e^2dl,\qquad
I_\nu=B_\nu(T_e)(1-e^{-\tau_\nu}).
$$
The turnover constrains <emission measure>; a resolved, <optically thick> source has <brightness temperature> approaching $T_e$. An unresolved source has an unknown beam <filling factor>, so its observed brightness need not equal its <electron> <temperature>. Angular size and an independent distance give a geometric depth $L$; a uniform model then yields **$n_e=\sqrt{\mathrm{EM}/L}$**. Clumping instead measures a density-squared average, not the mean <density>.<Essential Radio Astronomy by James J. Condon and Scott M. Ransom>[chapter 4, equations (4.57)–(4.60); emission measure and thick/thin spectra]{ref}

**A free-free spectrum alone does not uniquely determine <density> and size.** The <density-depth degeneracy of free-free radiation> is explicit: for a resolved uniform column, $n_e\mapsto qn_e$, $L\mapsto L/q^2$ leaves both emissivity-integrated brightness and <optical depth> unchanged at fixed <temperature>. With independent SZ information and known $T_e$, the <electron> column is $N_e=ym_ec^2/(\sigma_Tk_BT_e)$; then
$$
\boxed{n_e=\mathrm{EM}/N_e,\qquad L=N_e^2/\mathrm{EM}}.
$$
These equalities require consistent composition and geometry, and uniform <density> and <temperature>. Relating $L$ to angular extent can give distance; asphericity and the <gas clumping factor> are important limitations of this combined cluster method.
