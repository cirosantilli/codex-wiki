= Solution
{parent=i}

Use the <Fourier transform> convention $\widehat f(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb R}f(x)e^{-ix\xi}\,dx$, with complex-linear <distribution> pairings throughout. The <Riemann-Lebesgue lemma> says that, for an <integrable function> $f\in L^1(\mathbb R)$, its <Fourier transform> is continuous and tends to zero as $|\xi|\to\infty$. It also satisfies $\|\widehat f\|_\infty\leq\|f\|_1$. The product rule, or <convolution theorem>, is
$$
\boxed{\widehat{f*g}=\widehat f\,\widehat g\qquad(f,g\in L^1(\mathbb R)).}
$$
Here $(f*g)(x)=\int f(y)g(x-y)\,dy$ belongs to $L^1(\mathbb R)$ by <Tonelli's theorem>, and <Fubini's theorem> proves the displayed identity. The companion <Fourier product formula>, for <Schwartz functions>, is $\widehat{fg}=(2\pi)^{-1}\widehat f*\widehat g$.

A <Schwartz function> is a <smooth function> $\phi:\mathbb R\to\mathbb C$ for which every <Schwartz seminorm>
$$
p_{m,n}(\phi):=\sup_{x\in\mathbb R}|x^m\phi^{(n)}(x)|,\qquad m,n\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\},
$$
is finite. These <seminorms> define the topology of the <Schwartz space> $\mathcal S(\mathbb R)$. In particular,
$$
|\phi(x)|\leq\frac{p_{0,0}(\phi)+p_{2,0}(\phi)}{1+x^2},\qquad
\boxed{\|\phi\|_1\leq\pi\bigl(p_{0,0}(\phi)+p_{2,0}(\phi)\bigr)<\infty.}
$$
Thus every <Schwartz function> is an <integrable function>.

The <Fourier bounds for Schwartz functions> assert that each <Schwartz seminorm> of $\widehat\phi$ is bounded by finitely many <Schwartz seminorms> of $\phi$. Explicitly, <differentiation under the integral sign> and <integration by parts> give
$$
\xi^m\partial_\xi^n\widehat\phi(\xi)
=i^{-m}\mathcal F\!\left[\partial_x^m\bigl((-ix)^n\phi\bigr)\right](\xi),
$$
so the <generalized Leibniz rule> and the preceding integrability estimate give
$$
\boxed{p_{m,n}(\widehat\phi)
\leq\pi\sum_{j=0}^{\min(m,n)}\binom mj\frac{n!}{(n-j)!}
\bigl(p_{n-j,m-j}(\phi)+p_{n-j+2,m-j}(\phi)\bigr).}
$$
In particular the <Fourier transform> maps the <Schwartz space> continuously into itself.

For $\varepsilon>0$, insert a <Gaussian function> into the proposed <inverse Fourier transform>:
$$
I_\varepsilon(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}\widehat\phi(\xi)e^{ix\xi}e^{-\varepsilon\xi^2/2}\,d\xi.
$$
The <Fourier bounds for Schwartz functions> show that $\widehat\phi$ is an <integrable function>, so <dominated convergence> gives
$$
I_\varepsilon(x)\longrightarrow\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}\widehat\phi(\xi)e^{ix\xi}\,d\xi.
$$
On the other hand, <Fubini's theorem> applies because $\|\phi\|_1\int e^{-\varepsilon\xi^2/2}\,d\xi<\infty$. The given <Fourier transform of a Gaussian>, rescaled by $\sqrt\varepsilon$, yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
I_\varepsilon(x)
&=\int_{\mathbb R}\phi(y)\left(\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-\varepsilon\xi^2/2}e^{i(x-y)\xi}\,d\xi\right)dy\\
&=\int_{\mathbb R}\phi(y)\frac{e^{-(x-y)^2/(2\varepsilon)}}{\sqrt{2\pi\varepsilon}}\,dy
=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb R}\phi(x-\sqrt\varepsilon z)e^{-z^2/2}\,dz.
\end{aligned}
$$
The last <integrand> is bounded by $\|\phi\|_\infty e^{-z^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$. Since $\phi$ is continuous, another application of <dominated convergence> makes this tend to $\phi(x)$. Equating the limits proves the <Fourier inversion theorem>:
$$
\boxed{\phi(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}\widehat\phi(\xi)e^{ix\xi}\,d\xi\qquad(x\in\mathbb R).}
$$
The <Gaussian functions> above form an <approximate identity>; no <Fourier inversion> was used in evaluating their <integrals>.

A <tempered distribution> is a continuous <linear functional> $u:\mathcal S(\mathbb R)\to\mathbb C$. Equivalently, for some integer $N$ and constant $C$,
$$
|\langle u,\phi\rangle|\leq C\sum_{0\leq m,n\leq N}p_{m,n}(\phi)
\qquad(\phi\in\mathcal S(\mathbb R)).
$$
The <Fourier transform of a tempered distribution> is defined by
$$
\boxed{\langle\widehat u,\phi\rangle:=\langle u,\widehat\phi\rangle.}
$$
The <Fourier bounds for Schwartz functions> make the right side a continuous <linear functional> on the <Schwartz space>, so $\widehat u$ is again a <tempered distribution>.

For an <integrable function> $f$, the <regular distribution> $u_f$ acts by $\langle u_f,\phi\rangle=\int f\phi$. The estimate $|\langle u_f,\phi\rangle|\leq\|f\|_1p_{0,0}(\phi)$ proves it is a <tempered distribution>. Since $\iint|f(x)\phi(\xi)|\,dx\,d\xi<\infty$, <Fubini's theorem> gives
$$
\langle\widehat{u_f},\phi\rangle
=\int f(x)\int\phi(\xi)e^{-ix\xi}\,d\xi\,dx
=\int\widehat f(\xi)\phi(\xi)\,d\xi
=\langle u_{\widehat f},\phi\rangle.
$$
Thus the <distributional Fourier transform> agrees with the ordinary <Fourier transform>.

Since $0<f_\alpha\leq1$, its <regular distribution> satisfies
$$
|\langle f_\alpha,\phi\rangle|\leq\|\phi\|_1
\leq\pi\bigl(p_{0,0}(\phi)+p_{2,0}(\phi)\bigr),
$$
so $f_\alpha$ is a <tempered distribution> for every $\alpha>0$.

Put $a=\alpha/2$. The defining <integral> of the <gamma function>, with $r=(1+x^2)t$, gives
$$
f_\alpha(x)=\frac1{\Gamma(a)}\int_0^\infty t^{a-1}e^{-t}e^{-tx^2}\,dt.
$$
The <Fourier transform of a Gaussian> suggests the following representative for the <Fourier transform of an inverse power of one plus x squared>:
$$
\boxed{h_\alpha(\xi)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{\Gamma(a)}\int_0^\infty t^{a-3/2}e^{-t-\xi^2/(4t)}\,dt\quad(\xi\ne0).}
$$
All factors are positive. <Tonelli's theorem> evaluates its total <integral>, allowing an infinite value at the single point zero:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_{\mathbb R}h_\alpha(\xi)\,d\xi
&=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{\Gamma(a)}\int_0^\infty t^{a-3/2}e^{-t}
\left(\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-\xi^2/(4t)}\,d\xi\right)dt\\
&=\frac{2\pi}{\Gamma(a)}\int_0^\infty t^{a-1}e^{-t}\,dt=2\pi.
\end{aligned}
$$
Assign any finite value at zero if necessary. This gives an <integrable function> with $\|h_\alpha\|_1=2\pi$.

To verify equality as <tempered distributions>, take any <Schwartz function> $\phi$. Substituting the mixture into $\langle f_\alpha,\widehat\phi\rangle$ is justified by
$$
\frac1{\Gamma(a)}\int_0^\infty t^{a-1}e^{-t}\int e^{-tx^2}|\widehat\phi(x)|\,dx\,dt
\leq\|\widehat\phi\|_1.
$$
For each $t>0$, <Fubini's theorem> and the <Fourier transform of a Gaussian> identify the inner pairing with $\int\sqrt{\pi/t}\,e^{-\xi^2/(4t)}\phi(\xi)\,d\xi$. Interchanging the resulting <integrals> is valid because their absolute value is bounded by $2\pi\|\phi\|_\infty$ after integrating the positive kernel. Therefore
$$
\langle\widehat{f_\alpha},\phi\rangle=\int h_\alpha(\xi)\phi(\xi)\,d\xi.
$$
Finally, on a <compact set> with $|\xi|\geq\delta>0$, the mixture <integrand> is bounded by $t^{a-3/2}e^{-t-\delta^2/(4t)}$, which is integrable both at zero and at infinity. <Dominated convergence> proves continuity there. Hence $\boxed{\widehat{f_\alpha}=h_\alpha\in L^1(\mathbb R),\ h_\alpha(\xi)>0\text{ and continuous for }\xi\ne0}$.

Equivalently, in the mixture for $h_\alpha$ the majorant $t^{(\alpha-3)/2}e^{-t}$ is integrable at zero precisely when $\alpha>1$. In this case <dominated convergence> also gives
$$
h_\alpha(0)=\frac{\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma((\alpha-1)/2)}{\Gamma(\alpha/2)}.
$$
If $0<\alpha\leq1$, <Fatou's lemma> applied along any sequence of nonzero $\xi$ tending to zero gives
$$
\liminf_{\xi\to0,\,\xi\ne0}h_\alpha(\xi)
\geq\frac{\sqrt\pi}{\Gamma(\alpha/2)}\int_0^\infty t^{(\alpha-3)/2}e^{-t}\,dt=+\infty.
$$
The divergence comes from zero, where the exponent is at most $-1$. A continuous representative $H$ would agree with $h_\alpha$ <almost everywhere>. Since both are continuous on each open half-line, they must agree everywhere on those half-lines: a nonzero continuous difference would be nonzero on an interval of positive <Lebesgue measure>. The displayed blow-up then contradicts continuity of $H$ at zero. Changing a value at one point cannot remove this singularity. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\widehat{f_\alpha}\text{ has a continuous representative on }\mathbb R\iff\alpha>1.}

Let $R\phi(x)=\phi(-x)$, and define reflection of a <tempered distribution> by $\langle Ru,\phi\rangle=\langle u,R\phi\rangle$. The <Fourier inversion theorem> on the <Schwartz space> says $\widehat{\widehat\phi}=2\pi R\phi$. Thus
$$
\langle\widehat{\widehat u},\phi\rangle
=\langle u,\widehat{\widehat\phi}\rangle
=2\pi\langle u,R\phi\rangle
=2\pi\langle Ru,\phi\rangle.
$$
Reflection commutes with the <Fourier transform>, since $\widehat{R\phi}=R\widehat\phi$ and the same relation follows by duality for <tempered distributions>. Define the <inverse Fourier transform> on <tempered distributions> by
$$
\mathcal F^{-1}v=\frac1{2\pi}R\widehat v,
\qquad
\langle\mathcal F^{-1}v,\phi\rangle=\langle v,\mathcal F^{-1}\phi\rangle.
$$
It follows that
$$
\boxed{\mathcal F^{-1}\mathcal Fu=u=\mathcal F\mathcal F^{-1}u\quad(u\in\mathcal S'(\mathbb R)).}
$$
In particular $u=(2\pi)^{-1}R\widehat{\widehat u}$ is the general <Fourier inversion> formula. When $\widehat u$ is an <integrable function>, this is the <regular distribution> of $(2\pi)^{-1}\int\widehat u(\xi)e^{ix\xi}\,d\xi$; an arbitrary <tempered distribution> need not have an ordinary integral representation.

Set $\widetilde\phi(x)=\overline{\phi(-x)}$. This is a <Schwartz function> and its <Fourier transform> is $\widehat{\widetilde\phi}(\xi)=\overline{\widehat\phi(\xi)}$. The <convolution theorem> gives
$$
\widehat{\phi*\widetilde\phi}(\xi)=|\widehat\phi(\xi)|^2.
$$
The <convolution> of two <Schwartz functions> is again a <Schwartz function>: differentiating under the <integral> and expanding $x^m=(y+(x-y))^m$ bounds every weighted <derivative> by products of integrable and bounded weighted <derivatives>. Evaluate <Fourier inversion> for this <convolution> at zero:
$$
\boxed{\int_{\mathbb R}|\phi(x)|^2\,dx
=(\phi*\widetilde\phi)(0)
=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}|\widehat\phi(\xi)|^2\,d\xi.}
$$
The <Schwartz space> is dense in the <L2 space>. For $f\in L^2(\mathbb R)$ choose <Schwartz functions> $\phi_j\to f$ in the <L2 norm>. The identity makes $\widehat\phi_j$ a <Cauchy sequence> in the <L2 space>, and its limit defines $\mathcal Ff$, independently of the approximation. It agrees with the <distributional Fourier transform>, because <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> makes convergence in the <L2 norm> imply convergence when paired with every <Schwartz function>. For $f\in L^1\cap L^2$ it therefore agrees <almost everywhere> with the ordinary <Fourier transform>.

The extension is onto: its image is closed by the norm identity and contains the <Schwartz space>, since <Fourier inversion> maps that space to itself. This proves <Plancherel theorem>:
$$
\boxed{\|\mathcal Ff\|_2^2=2\pi\|f\|_2^2,\qquad
(2\pi)^{-1/2}\mathcal F:L^2(\mathbb R)\longrightarrow L^2(\mathbb R)\text{ is unitary}.}
$$
Its inverse is the extension of the <inverse Fourier transform>, with limits understood in the <L2 norm>.

For real $s$, the <Sobolev space> $H^s(\mathbb R)$ consists of those <tempered distributions> $u$ whose <Fourier transform> is represented by a <locally square-integrable function> and for which
$$
\boxed{\|u\|_{H^s}^2:=\int_{\mathbb R}(1+\xi^2)^s|\widehat u(\xi)|^2\,d\xi<\infty.}
$$
This is the <Sobolev norm> used here; inserting the fixed factor $(2\pi)^{-1}$ gives an equivalent convention.

For the <constant function> $c$, <Fourier inversion> at zero gives
$$
\langle\widehat c,\phi\rangle=c\int\widehat\phi(x)\,dx=2\pi c\phi(0),
\qquad \widehat c=2\pi c\delta_0.
$$
A nonzero <Dirac delta distribution> cannot be represented by a <locally square-integrable function>. Indeed, if $G$ represented $2\pi c\delta_0$, choose a <test function> $\rho$ with $\rho(0)=1$, and put $\rho_\varepsilon(\xi)=\rho(\xi/\varepsilon)$. On a fixed <compact interval> containing their supports, <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives
$$
|2\pi c|=\left|\int G\rho_\varepsilon\right|
\leq\|G\|_{L^2(K)}\sqrt\varepsilon\,\|\rho\|_2\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus $c=0$. Every <Sobolev space> requires local square integrability of the <Fourier transform>, regardless of how negative $s$ is. Therefore
$$
\boxed{c\in H^s(\mathbb R)\text{ for some }s\in\mathbb R\iff c=0.}
$$
The zero <constant function> belongs to every <Sobolev space>.

The <distributional derivative> is defined by $\langle u',\phi\rangle=-\langle u,\phi'\rangle$. Since <differentiation under the integral sign> gives $(\widehat\phi)'=\widehat{-i\xi\phi}$, we obtain
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle\widehat{u'},\phi\rangle

=-\langle u,(\widehat\phi)'\rangle

&=\langle\widehat u,i\xi\phi\rangle
=\langle i\xi\widehat u,\phi\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$
This proves the <Fourier transform of a derivative> identity $\boxed{\widehat{u'}=i\xi\widehat u}$ for all <tempered distributions>.

If $u,u'\in H^s(\mathbb R)$, their <Fourier transforms> are represented by <locally square-integrable functions>, and the identity identifies $\widehat{u'}$ with $i\xi\widehat u$ <almost everywhere>. Hence the <Sobolev norms> satisfy
$$
\begin{aligned}
\|u\|_{H^{s+1}}^2
&=\int(1+\xi^2)^s(1+\xi^2)|\widehat u|^2\,d\xi\\
&=\int(1+\xi^2)^s|\widehat u|^2\,d\xi
+\int(1+\xi^2)^s|\widehat{u'}|^2\,d\xi,
\end{aligned}
$$
so $\boxed{u\in H^{s+1}(\mathbb R),\quad\|u\|_{H^{s+1}}^2=\|u\|_{H^s}^2+\|u'\|_{H^s}^2}$ for every real $s$.

Write $U=\widehat u$, $V=\widehat v$, and $\langle\xi\rangle=(1+\xi^2)^{1/2}$. By the <Sobolev space> hypotheses, $A=\langle\xi\rangle^sU$ and $B=\langle\xi\rangle^tV$ belong to the <L2 space>. Their product is an <integrable function> by <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. Thus $UV=\langle\xi\rangle^{-s-t}AB$ is a <locally integrable function> defining a <tempered distribution>, with estimate
$$
\left|\int UV\phi\,d\xi\right|
\leq\|u\|_{H^s}\|v\|_{H^t}
\sup_\xi\bigl(\langle\xi\rangle^{-s-t}|\phi(\xi)|\bigr).
$$
The supremum is controlled by finitely many <Schwartz seminorms>. Define the <convolution of Sobolev distributions> by
$$
\boxed{u*v:=\mathcal F^{-1}(UV)\in\mathcal S'(\mathbb R).}
$$
It is independent of the representatives chosen for $U,V$, and the <convolution theorem> shows that it agrees with ordinary <convolution> for <Schwartz functions>. More generally, approximate $u,v$ by <Schwartz functions> in their respective <Sobolev norms>; the displayed estimate shows convergence of the products in <tempered distributions>, so their ordinary <convolutions> converge to this definition. For $u,v\in L^1$ that also satisfy the stated <Sobolev space> hypotheses, the ordinary <convolution theorem> gives the same result.

There need not be any real $r$ with $u*v\in H^r(\mathbb R)$. Choose
$$
U(\xi)=V(\xi)=\xi^{-1/3}\mathbf1_{(0,1)}(\xi),\qquad
u=\mathcal F^{-1}U,\quad v=\mathcal F^{-1}V.
$$
Here $\mathbf1_{(0,1)}$ is an <indicator function>. Since $\int_0^1\xi^{-2/3}\,d\xi=3$ and every weight $(1+\xi^2)^q$ is bounded above and below by positive constants on $[0,1]$, both $u$ and $v$ belong to every <Sobolev space>. But their <convolution> has <Fourier transform> $UV=\xi^{-2/3}\mathbf1_{(0,1)}$, and for every real $r$,
$$
\int(1+\xi^2)^r|UV|^2\,d\xi
\geq\min(1,2^r)\int_0^1\xi^{-4/3}\,d\xi=\infty.
$$
Thus $\boxed{u,v\in\bigcap_{q\in\mathbb R}H^q(\mathbb R)\quad\text{but}\quad u*v\notin\bigcup_{r\in\mathbb R}H^r(\mathbb R)}$. The obstruction is failure of local square integrability at zero; a weight controlling large <frequencies> cannot fix it.

The $2\pi$ <periodization of a Schwartz function> is
$$
\boxed{P\phi(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}\phi(x+2\pi k).}
$$
For every nonnegative integer $j$, the <Schwartz seminorms> give $|\phi^{(j)}(x+2\pi k)|\leq C_j(1+|k|)^{-2}$ uniformly for $x\in[0,2\pi]$. The <Weierstrass M-test> gives absolute and <uniform convergence> there of the defining <series> and all its differentiated <series>. Termwise differentiation proves that $P\phi$ is a <smooth function>, with $(P\phi)^{(j)}=P(\phi^{(j)})$. Reindexing the absolutely convergent <series> proves $P\phi(x+2\pi)=P\phi(x)$, so it is a <periodic function>.

For an explicit <smooth periodic partition of unity>, choose the <bump function>
$$
\eta(x)=\begin{cases}
\exp\!\bigl(-1/(1-(x/(2\pi))^2)\bigr),&|x|<2\pi,\\
0,&|x|\geq2\pi.
\end{cases}
$$
It is a <test function>, positive on $[-\pi,\pi]$. The <smooth function> $S(x)=P\eta(x)$ is $2\pi$ periodic and strictly positive, since every $x$ has a translate in $[-\pi,\pi]$. Set
$$
\boxed{\Psi(x)=\frac{\eta(x)}{S(x)}\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb R),\qquad
P\Psi(x)=\frac{\sum_k\eta(x+2\pi k)}{S(x)}=1.}
$$
Division preserves smoothness because $S>0$, and the <support> remains contained in $[-2\pi,2\pi]$.

A <distribution> $u$ is a $2\pi$ <periodic distribution> when
$$
\boxed{\langle u,\theta(\cdot+2\pi)\rangle=\langle u,\theta\rangle\qquad(\theta\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb R)).}
$$
Iteration gives invariance under all integer multiples of $2\pi$. For a <tempered distribution> the identity also holds for every <Schwartz function>, since <test functions> are dense in the <Schwartz space>: multiplying by <cutoff functions> $\chi(x/R)$ equal to one near zero gives such an approximation in every <Schwartz seminorm>.

For $\phi\in\mathcal S(\mathbb R)$ define $a_k(x)=\Psi(x+2\pi k)\phi(x)$. The <smooth periodic partition of unity> gives $\phi=\sum_ka_k$ with convergence in the <Schwartz space>. Indeed, each $a_k$ is supported where $|x+2\pi k|\leq2\pi$, and the <generalized Leibniz rule> and arbitrarily rapid decay of the <derivatives> of $\phi$ give, for every $m,n,M$,
$$
p_{m,n}(a_k)\leq C_{m,n,M,\phi}(1+|k|)^{-M}.
$$
Consequently continuity and periodicity of the <tempered distribution> give
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle u,\phi\rangle
&=\sum_k\langle u,\Psi(\cdot+2\pi k)\phi\rangle\\
&=\sum_k\langle u,\Psi(\cdot)\phi(\cdot-2\pi k)\rangle\\
&=\left\langle u,\Psi(\cdot)\sum_k\phi(\cdot-2\pi k)\right\rangle
=\boxed{\langle u,\Psi P\phi\rangle}.
\end{aligned}
$$
For the last interchange, all summands have the fixed <compact support> of $\Psi$ and their <derivatives> decrease faster than any power of $|k|$. Hence the series converges in the <space of test functions>, as well as in the <Schwartz space>. Notice that $P\phi$ itself need not be a <Schwartz function>; the factor $\Psi$ makes the pairing legitimate.

Let $D=\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}\delta_{2\pi k}$ be the <Dirac comb>. Pair the given identity $D=(2\pi)^{-1}\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}e^{in\cdot}$ with the <Schwartz function> $y\mapsto\phi(x-y)$. On the left this gives $\sum_k\phi(x-2\pi k)=P\phi(x)$. On the right,

\int_{\mathbb R}e^{iny}\phi(x-y)\,dy
=e^{inx}\int_{\mathbb R}\phi(z)e^{-inz}\,dz
=e^{inx}\widehat\phi(n).
$$
Therefore the <Fourier series> is
$$
\boxed{P\phi(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}\widehat\phi(n)e^{inx}.}

The <Fourier bounds for Schwartz functions> imply $|\widehat\phi(n)|\leq C_N(1+|n|)^{-N}$ for every $N$. For each nonnegative integer $j$, choose $N>j+1$ to obtain $\sum_n|n|^j|\widehat\phi(n)|<\infty$. Thus the <Fourier series> and every differentiated <Fourier series> converge absolutely and uniformly on all of $\mathbb R$. In particular convergence holds in the topology of <smooth> $2\pi$ <periodic functions>, defined by uniform convergence of every <derivative> on one period, and also in <tempered distributions>.

Use the <smooth periodic partition of unity> $\Psi$ above and define the <Fourier coefficients> of the <periodic distribution> by
$$
c_n=\frac1{2\pi}\langle u,\Psi(x)e^{-inx}\rangle,\qquad n\in\mathbb Z.
$$
These are the <smooth-window Fourier coefficients of a periodic distribution> for period $2\pi$. The finite <Schwartz seminorm> bound for $u$, restricted to the fixed <compact support> of $\Psi$, gives $|c_n|\leq C(1+|n|)^N$ for some $C,N$. Indeed, only finitely many <derivatives> of $\Psi e^{-inx}$ occur, and each contributes at most a fixed power of $|n|$.

For a <Schwartz function> $\phi$, the preceding <Fourier series> formula and $\widehat{\widehat\phi}(n)=2\pi\phi(-n)$ yield
$$
P\widehat\phi(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}\phi(-n)e^{inx}.
$$
All <derivatives> of this series converge uniformly. The localization formula in the previous answer therefore gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle\widehat u,\phi\rangle
&=\langle u,\widehat\phi\rangle
=\langle u,\Psi P\widehat\phi\rangle\\
&=\sum_n\phi(-n)\langle u,\Psi e^{inx}\rangle
=2\pi\sum_n c_n\phi(n).
\end{aligned}
$$
Consequently the <Fourier transform of a periodic distribution> is
$$
\boxed{\widehat u=2\pi\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}c_n\delta_n\quad\text{in }\mathcal S'(\mathbb R).}
$$
The polynomial bound on $c_n$ and rapid decay of $\phi(n)$ make every displayed pairing absolutely convergent. In particular there are no <derivatives> of <Dirac delta distributions> in this formula.

The <inverse Fourier transform> of $\delta_n$ is $(2\pi)^{-1}e^{inx}$. Applying continuous <Fourier inversion> on <tempered distributions> to the symmetric partial sums gives
$$
\boxed{u=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}c_ne^{inx}\quad\text{in }\mathcal S'(\mathbb R).}
$$
Explicitly, for every <Schwartz function> $\theta$, the pairing of the partial sum with $\theta$ is $\sum_{|n|\leq M}c_n\widehat\theta(-n)$, which converges absolutely to $\langle u,\theta\rangle$. The <Fourier transform> formula also proves that $c_n$ is independent of the chosen <smooth periodic partition of unity>. For a locally integrable $2\pi$ <periodic function> it reduces, by splitting the integral into periods, to $c_n=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_0^{2\pi}u(x)e^{-inx}\,dx$.

Splitting the <Fourier transform> integral at zero gives two absolutely convergent <integrals>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\widehat{g_t}(\xi)
&=\int_0^\infty e^{-(t+i\xi)x}\,dx+\int_0^\infty e^{-(t-i\xi)x}\,dx\\

=\boxed{\frac{2t}{t^2+\xi^2}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Both $g_t$ and $\widehat{g_t}$ are <integrable functions>. Let $h_t(x)=(t^2+x^2)^{-1}$, so $\widehat{g_t}=2t h_t$. The <duality property of the Fourier transform>, or <Fourier inversion> in <tempered distributions>, gives
$$
2t\widehat{h_t}(\xi)=\widehat{\widehat{g_t}}(\xi)=2\pi g_t(-\xi).
$$
These <Fourier transforms> have continuous representatives by the <Riemann-Lebesgue lemma>, so the identity holds at every $\xi$. Since $g_t$ is an <even function>,
$$
\boxed{\mathcal F_{x\to\xi}\!\left(\frac1{t^2+x^2}\right)=\frac\pi t e^{-t|\xi|}.}
$$

{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Put $h(x)=(\alpha^2+x^2)^{-1}$ and form its period-one <periodization of an integrable function>
$$
H(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}h(x+k).
$$
The <function> $h$ is not a <Schwartz function>, so justify the needed <Poisson summation formula> directly. For every $j\geq0$, its <derivative> satisfies $h^{(j)}(x)=O(|x|^{-2-j})$ as $|x|\to\infty$. Thus the <Weierstrass M-test> gives <uniform convergence> on $[0,1]$ of this <series> and each differentiated <series>. It follows that $H$ is a <smooth> period-one <periodic function>.

Absolute integrability and <Fubini's theorem> allow its <Fourier coefficients> to be computed by splitting the real line into unit intervals:
$$
\begin{aligned}
b_n&=\int_0^1H(x)e^{-2\pi inx}\,dx
=\sum_k\int_k^{k+1}h(y)e^{-2\pi iny}\,dy\\
&=\widehat h(2\pi n)=\frac\pi\alpha e^{-2\pi\alpha|n|}.
\end{aligned}
$$
These <Fourier coefficients> are absolutely summable. Their <Fourier series> defines a continuous <periodic function> with the same <Fourier coefficients> as $H$; <uniqueness of Fourier coefficients in L1> identifies the two <almost everywhere>, and continuity then identifies them everywhere. Evaluating at zero gives the rigorously justified <Poisson summation formula>
$$
\frac1{\alpha^2}+2\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{\alpha^2+n^2}
=H(0)=\frac\pi\alpha\left(1+2\sum_{n=1}^\infty e^{-2\pi\alpha n}\right).
$$
Rearranging proves
$$
\boxed{\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{\alpha^2+n^2}
=\frac\pi{2\alpha}-\frac1{2\alpha^2}+\frac\pi\alpha\sum_{n=1}^\infty e^{-2\pi\alpha n}\qquad(\alpha>0).}
$$
The last <series> is a <geometric series> with sum $(e^{2\pi\alpha}-1)^{-1}$. Equivalently, the left side is $\pi\coth(\pi\alpha)/(2\alpha)-1/(2\alpha^2)$, where $\coth$ is the <hyperbolic cotangent>.
