= Design of experiments
{wiki}

= Experimental design
{synonym}

<Design of experiments> chooses <experimental units>, <treatments>, <replication>, <blocks in experimental design> and <randomization> so that scientifically useful <treatment contrasts> can be estimated with meaningful <standard errors>. Allocation determines which measurements supply independent treatment information.

= Crossed disconnection experiment
{parent=Design of experiments}

A design comparing disruption of two connected regions in opposite hemispheres with disruption on the same side. If the relevant interactions are mainly within a hemisphere, opposite-side disruption can remove an intact regional pair while same-side disruption leaves one. Interpretation requires tests of anatomical laterality, unilateral effects, damage extent and alternative routes; a deficit does not automatically identify a monosynaptic pathway.

= Demand characteristic
{parent=Design of experiments}

A feature of an experimental setting that suggests how a participant is expected to respond. It can change a <self-report> without the intended change in underlying experience. Concealing a hypothesis helps, but does not prove that all expectancy or reporting effects have been removed.

= Dismantling study
{parent=Design of experiments}

An experimental comparison that varies the presence of a component within otherwise comparable treatment packages. It tests the incremental contribution of that component under the chosen context; it does not automatically establish its psychological mediator.

= Waiting-list control
{parent=Design of experiments}

A comparison group whose intervention is delayed. It can estimate improvement relative to waiting under the study conditions, but does not match all expectations, contact or active treatment ingredients.

= Manipulation check
{parent=Design of experiments}

A measure assessing whether the intended experimental change occurred. Failure to alter the proposed process limits interpretation of a null outcome, while a successful check does not guarantee that no other process changed.

= Yoked control
{parent=Design of experiments}

A participant or animal receives outcomes matched to another subject while the dependence of those outcomes on its own responses differs. This isolates controllability more effectively than merely comparing exposure with no exposure, while leaving motor effort and predictability as possible additional considerations.

= Active control
{parent=Design of experiments}

A comparison condition receiving an activity intended to match nonspecific influences such as contact, expectations or practice, while differing in the component of interest. Whether it actually matches those influences must be assessed, not assumed from its label.

= Befriending intervention
{parent=Active control}

Supportive interpersonal contact used as an intervention or comparison. Its effects are not assumed inert: whether it matches credibility, <attention> and relevant mechanisms must be assessed.

= Pilot study
{parent=Design of experiments}

A preliminary investigation used to examine feasibility, procedures or measurement behavior before a larger study. A pilot can expose ambiguous <questionnaire> items without providing a stable final estimate of the underlying measurement structure.

= Randomized experiment
{parent=Design of experiments}

An experiment using <randomization> to allocate the comparison conditions. With valid implementation and analysis it can support <causal inference> within the studied setting; it does not by itself establish <external validity>.

= Between-subject design
{parent=Design of experiments}

= Between-subjects
{synonym}

A design in which distinct participants supply the observations for different levels of a factor. A study may combine <between-subjects> factors with <within-subjects> factors. The number of recruited participants alone does not determine which design was used.

= Within-subject design
{parent=Design of experiments}

= Within-subjects
{synonym}

A design in which the same participant supplies measurements under several levels of a factor. The observations are dependent, so the analysis must preserve the pairing or repeated-measure structure. Order, practice and <carryover effects> can require design controls.

= Independent variable
{parent=Design of experiments}

A variable whose levels define a comparison or predictor of interest. In an experiment it can be assigned or manipulated; calling an observed predictor an independent variable does not establish a causal effect. The measured outcome is the <response variable>.

= Positive control
{parent=Design of experiments}

= Negative control
{parent=Design of experiments}

= Vehicle control
{parent=Negative control}

A comparison receiving the solvent or delivery formulation without the active compound. It separates compound-associated effects from effects of the formulation.

= Pair feeding
{parent=Design of experiments}

A dietary control in which a comparison group receives an amount of food matched to the intake of another group. The timing, energy density and method of matching determine which intake-related explanations it addresses.

= Blinding
{parent=Design of experiments}

Withholding treatment or hypothesis information from participants, handlers or scorers where appropriate to reduce expectation-dependent differences. What was blinded and which cues remained available should be reported.

= Sham treatment
{parent=Design of experiments}

A control reproducing handling or other incidental aspects of an intervention while omitting its intended active feature. A sham mark should match tactile and olfactory properties while differing in visibility.

= Construct validity
{parent=Design of experiments}

Whether an operational measurement supports the intended theoretical interpretation. Successful hidden-mark localization supports a narrower claim than a general claim about consciousness.

= Predictive validity
{parent=Construct validity}

Evidence that a measure predicts a relevant later outcome. Prediction does not itself establish that the measured characteristic causes that outcome or is a sufficient intervention target.

= Task impurity
{parent=Construct validity}

A task intended to measure one cognitive process also requires other processes, such as <perception>, <language comprehension> or producing a response. Impaired performance therefore does not identify the intended process uniquely. Matched control conditions and converging tasks with different incidental demands help constrain the explanation.

= Criterion validity
{parent=Construct validity}

Evidence relating a measure to a relevant external outcome or criterion. Criterion choice, measurement error and shared method can affect the relationship; an internal factor solution does not supply this evidence automatically.

= Incremental validity
{parent=Criterion validity}

Predictive contribution beyond specified existing measures. A social-<intelligence> measure should be tested against relevant general-ability or <personality> measures rather than credited for any unadjusted <correlation> with social outcomes.

= Discriminant validity
{parent=Construct validity}

Evidence that measures distinguish the intended construct from relevant competing constructs. It requires substantive expectations about relationships, not a universal rule that every <correlation> with another construct must be zero.

= Convergent validity
{parent=Construct validity}

Agreement among defensible measures intended to assess the same construct. A lack of association can reflect different constructs, measurement error or restricted variation; a positive association can also arise from shared incidental demands.

= Content validity
{parent=Construct validity}

The adequacy with which a measure's items cover the intended domain of content. Expert review, target-user input and <cognitive interviewing> can identify missing or irrelevant material. Good numerical fit cannot compensate for a questionnaire that omits essential parts of its intended construct.

= Ecological validity
{parent=Design of experiments}

Relevance of a test’s cues, actions and setting to the processes of interest outside that test. A task can be controlled yet poorly suited to a <species>’ sensory or motivational capacities.

= Before-after control-impact design
{parent=Design of experiments}

Comparison of changes at affected and control sites to separate an intervention from background temporal change.

= Batch effect
{parent=Design of experiments}

A systematic difference associated with sample processing groups rather than the biological contrast of interest. Balanced allocation and recorded metadata help separate these effects; statistical adjustment cannot fully recover a contrast perfectly confounded with batch.

= Cross-fostering
{parent=Design of experiments}

Transfer of offspring between providers to separate offspring origin from rearing environment. Egg composition, prenatal influences and handling require additional controls.

= Optimal experimental design
{parent=Design of experiments}
{wiki}

= Optimal design
{synonym}

<Optimal experimental design> chooses observation locations and allocations to optimize a specified criterion for a specified <statistical model>. Criteria can control the <determinant> of an estimator's <covariance matrix> or its worst prediction <variance>. Optimality depends on the model and the permitted design class, including whether allocations are integer or approximate.

= General equivalence theorem for optimal design
{parent=Optimal experimental design}

= Kiefer-Wolfowitz equivalence theorem
{c}
{synonym}

For continuous regressors spanning a $p$-dimensional space on a compact design region, and approximate designs with nonsingular <information matrix of an experimental design>, the following are equivalent: <D-optimal design>, <G-optimal design>, and $\sup_x d(x,\xi)=p$. A D-optimal design has nonpositive directional derivatives $d(x,\xi)-p$. Conversely, if every such derivative is nonpositive, <concavity> of $\log\det$ bounds every competing information <matrix> by the tangent at $M(\xi)$, proving D-optimality. The sensitivity average is $p$, and a D-optimal design exists by compactness of the information-matrix set and the spanning assumption; hence the optimal G-value is $p$. Sensitivity equals $p$ at every support point of an optimal design. The equivalence concerns approximate allocations; integer restrictions can change exact-design optima.

= G-optimal design
{c}
{parent=Optimal experimental design}
{title2=$\min_\xi\sup_x d(x,\xi)$}

A G-optimal design minimizes the maximum <design sensitivity function> over the full design region. Thus it minimizes the worst <variance of a fitted regression mean>, excluding the separate noise <variance> of a new observation. The sensitivity average is $p$, so its supremum is at least $p$.

= D-optimal design
{c}
{parent=Optimal experimental design}
{title2=$\max_\xi\det M(\xi)$}

A D-optimal design maximizes the <determinant> of the <information matrix of an experimental design>, equivalently minimizing the <determinant> of the <covariance matrix> of a fixed-size <least-squares estimator>. It minimizes the volume of a fixed-level confidence ellipsoid for the model coefficients under the usual <Gaussian> model.

= Approximate experimental design
{parent=Optimal experimental design}
{title2=$\xi=\sum_iw_i\delta_{x_i}$}

An approximate design is a <probability measure> on the design region, with weight $w_i$ specifying a proportion of observations at $x_i$. For $N$ independent observations and common error <variance> $\sigma^2$, a <linear regression> with vector $f(x)$ has normalized <information matrix of an experimental design> $M(\xi)=\int f(x)f(x)^T\,d\xi(x)$; its <least-squares estimator> has <covariance> $\sigma^2M(\xi)^{-1}/N$ when the allocations realize these proportions. Exact designs restrict $Nw_i$ to integers.

= Information matrix of an experimental design
{parent=Approximate experimental design}
{title2=$M(\xi)=\int f(x)f(x)^T\,d\xi(x)$}

The normalized information <matrix> for a homoscedastic <linear regression> model is the average outer product of its regressor vector. It is <positive definite> precisely when the supported regressors span the parameter space. The full <Fisher information matrix> under <normal> errors is $NM(\xi)/\sigma^2$.

= Design sensitivity function
{parent=Information matrix of an experimental design}
{title2=$d(x,\xi)=f(x)^TM(\xi)^{-1}f(x)$}

The sensitivity function is the normalized <variance of a fitted regression mean>. Its average under the design is the number $p$ of model coefficients, since $\int d\,d\xi=\operatorname{tr}(M^{-1}M)=p$. The directional derivative of $\log\det M$ towards a point mass at $x$ is $d(x,\xi)-p$.

= Latin square
{c}
{parent=Design of experiments}
{wiki}

A Latin square of order $q$ is a $q\times q$ array of $q$ symbols, each occurring once in each row and column. Used as a <row-column design>, its symbols label <treatments>. Centered <treatment> indicators are <orthogonal> to both row and column indicators, because every row-treatment and column-treatment pair occurs once. This balance separates additive effects from two nuisance directions.

= Latin square enumeration
{parent=Latin square}
{title2=$n^{n^2}\quad\text{or}\quad(n!)^n$}

Enumerating every <Latin square> of a specified order, without identifying row, column or symbol <permutations> as the same output. Arbitrary square generation visits $n^{n^2}$ candidates. Restricting every row to a <permutation> reduces this to $(n!)^n$ candidates. Both counts follow by independent choices for positions or rows; neither count is the number of valid <Latin squares>.

= Pruned Latin square search
{parent=Latin square enumeration}

Build a <Latin square> row by row, maintaining the symbols already used in each column. While choosing a row, remove each selected symbol from the row's available pool and reject any symbol already used in that column. The <invariant> proves every complete output valid. Conversely every valid target row survives the checks, proving completeness by <induction>. A partial branch may still have no completion; avoiding complete invalid outputs does not mean every explored <list prefix> is extendable.

= Analysis of variance for a Latin square
{parent=Latin square}

For one response per cell in an order-$q$ <Latin square>, fit an <intercept> and additive row, column and <treatment> effects with sum-to-zero constraints. Each factor contributes $q-1$ <statistical degrees of freedom>; the residual has $q^2-1-3(q-1)=(q-1)(q-2)$. The centered factor spaces are mutually <orthogonal>, so sequential sums of squares do not depend on fitting order. With an additional <orthogonal> symbol factor from a <Graeco-Latin square>, residual <statistical degrees of freedom> become $(q-1)(q-3)$. Exact <F-tests> require independent equal-variance <normal> errors; unmodeled <interactions> can invalidate the error term.

= Mutually orthogonal Latin squares
{parent=Latin square}
{wiki}

<Latin squares> on the same array are mutually <orthogonal> when every ordered pair of symbols from any two squares occurs exactly once on superposition. Pairwise balance makes the centered symbol-indicator spaces <orthogonal> in an additive <linear regression> model.

= Graeco-Latin square
{c}
{parent=Mutually orthogonal Latin squares}

A Graeco-Latin square is the superposition of two <orthogonal> <Latin squares> of the same order. Each symbol of either square occurs once per row and column, and every cross-square pair occurs once. It permits four mutually <orthogonal> additive factor spaces: rows, columns, and the two symbol factors.

= Contrast
{disambiguate=statistics}
{parent=Design of experiments}

= Contrast
{synonym}

A contrast is a <linear combination> $\sum_i c_i\mu_i$ of group means with $\sum_i c_i=0$. It compares responses without depending on a common additive level. <Treatment contrasts> and <factorial contrasts> specify comparisons in a <design of experiments>.

= Response surface methodology
{parent=Design of experiments}
{wiki}

<Response surface methodology> fits local <regression> approximations to a quantitative response as experimental settings change. A first-order model estimates a local gradient; augmenting the design permits <quadratic regression>, curvature assessment and local optimization. Extrapolating a fitted surface outside the experimentally supported region requires caution.

= Center-point curvature contrast
{parent=Response surface methodology}
{title2=$\widehat\gamma=\bar Y_F-\bar Y_0$}

In a two-factor design with four corner observations and $n_0$ independent center replicates, the difference between the corner mean and center mean has <variance> $\sigma^2(1/4+1/n_0)$. Under a full <quadratic regression> surface it estimates $\beta_{11}+\beta_{22}$; the linear and interaction terms average to zero at the corners. With $n_0\geq2$, the center <pure error> estimate makes its squared standardized contrast an $F_{1,n_0-1}$ test under a first-order mean model. Zero contrast does not exclude curvature, since the two squared-term coefficients may cancel.

= Steepest ascent in response surface methodology
{parent=Response surface methodology}
{title2=$d=\widehat b/\|\widehat b\|$}

For a fitted local first-order response $\widehat m(x)=\widehat\beta_0+\widehat b^Tx$, the unit direction maximizing its directional derivative in coded Euclidean distance is $d=\widehat b/\|\widehat b\|$, by the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. Run sequential feasible settings $x_0+hd$, increase $h$ while measured response improves, and refit near promising settings. Physical increments are $h_jhd_j$, so coded and unscaled physical gradients need not give the same experimental path. A near-zero gradient calls for further modeling rather than division by zero.

= Coded experimental variable
{parent=Response surface methodology}
{title2=$x_j=(\xi_j-c_j)/h_j$}

A coded experimental variable rescales a physical setting $\xi_j$ around its center $c_j$, dividing by a chosen positive half-range $h_j$. The endpoints $c_j\pm h_j$ become $\pm1$, giving dimensionless <factorial design> coordinates. The choice of scale affects the metric used for <steepest ascent in response surface methodology>.

= Rotatable design
{parent=Response surface methodology}

A design for a specified polynomial model is rotatable when the <variance of a fitted regression mean> depends on a setting only through its distance from the centre. For a full $2^d$ factorial portion plus symmetric axial points in a <central composite design>, fourth-order rotational symmetry requires $\sum x_i^4=3\sum x_i^2x_j^2$. These sums are $2^d+2\alpha^4$ and $2^d$, giving $\alpha=(2^d)^{1/4}$. Sign symmetry removes odd moments and equal second moments complete the required moment symmetry for a full quadratic model.

= Central composite design
{parent=Response surface methodology}
{wiki}

A central composite design augments a factorial portion with axial points $\pm\alpha e_j$ and replicated centre points. It supports a full quadratic response model: intercept, linear terms, cross-products and squared coordinates. Centre-point <replication> estimates <pure error>. Choosing the axial distance appropriately gives a <rotatable design>.

= Split-plot design
{parent=Design of experiments}

A <split-plot design> randomizes one factor to whole plots and another to subplots within each whole plot. Whole-plot treatment effects and subplot treatment effects therefore use different error <ANOVA strata>. Whole plots provide <replication> for the first factor; their subplots do not supply extra independent whole-plot replication. Within-whole-plot comparisons can remove a shared additive whole-plot <variance component>.

= Factorial design
{parent=Design of experiments}

A <factorial design> includes every combination of specified factor levels. Balanced <replication> permits separate estimation of marginal effects and <interaction terms>. The factor allocation, not the number of subsamples, determines the appropriate error term for each effect.

= Main effect
{parent=Factorial design}
{wiki}

A main effect compares the mean response at different levels of one factor after averaging over the other factors according to the design. With sign coding, its two-level difference is twice the singleton <factorial contrast> coefficient. In a <fractional factorial design>, that coefficient can include aliased higher-order effects.

= Factorial contrast
{parent=Factorial design}
{title2=$\chi_A(x)=\prod_{j\in A}x_j$}

In a two-level <factorial design>, a factor subset defines a sign column. The coefficient of this column is its response <contrast> divided by the number of runs when the columns are <orthogonal>. A singleton describes a <main effect> and a pair describes a <two-factor interaction>. On the full cube, distinct columns have zero <inner product>: summing over any coordinate in their <symmetric difference> cancels the terms.

= Fractional factorial design
{parent=Factorial design}
{wiki}

A fractional <factorial design> uses a selected subset of the full factor-level combinations. In a regular two-level fraction, encode levels by signs and impose $k$ independent equations $\prod_{j\in G_i}x_j=s_i$. Independence of the generator incidence vectors over $\mathbb F_2$ leaves $2^{m-k}$ runs. Reduced run count creates <aliasing in a fractional factorial design>, which must be evaluated against the effects scientifically required.

= Resolution of a fractional factorial design
{parent=Fractional factorial design}
{title2=$R=\min_{G\in\mathcal H\setminus\{I\}}|G|$}

The resolution of a regular fraction is the shortest nonidentity word in its <defining contrast subgroup>. Two effects alias only when their word product belongs to that subgroup. Thus resolution VI separates all <main effects> and <two-factor interactions> from one another and from three-factor interactions, though some three-factor interactions alias in complementary pairs.

= Defining contrast subgroup
{parent=Fractional factorial design}
{title2=$\mathcal H=\langle G_1,\ldots,G_k\rangle$}

Factor subsets multiply by <symmetric difference>; their sign columns multiply pointwise. The subgroup generated by independent defining words has $2^k$ elements. On a selected <fractional factorial design>, every word in this subgroup is constant, with sign determined by the chosen generator signs. The cosets classify identical or opposite contrast columns.

= Aliasing in a fractional factorial design
{parent=Defining contrast subgroup}
{title2=$\chi_{AG}=s_G\chi_A$}

Two <factorial contrasts> are aliased if their columns are proportional on the selected <fractional factorial design>. A word $G$ that has fixed sign $s_G$ gives $\chi_{AG}=s_G\chi_A$. Conversely, averaging a character over the fraction vanishes unless its word belongs to the <defining contrast subgroup>, so every alias class is exactly a subgroup coset. In a half fraction each class has two distinct words; their separate coefficients cannot be identified without assumptions or additional runs.

= Crossover design
{parent=Design of experiments}

A <crossover design> assigns each subject a sequence of <treatments> in successive periods. Subject blocking can reduce between-subject variation; balanced sequences can separate treatment from period effects. A <carryover effect> or irreversible change can invalidate a simple within-subject comparison.

= Carryover effect
{parent=Crossover design}

A <carryover effect> is an effect of a previous <treatment> on a later response. Balanced transitions help prevent a treatment from being preceded disproportionately by one particular alternative, but do not ensure that <carryover effects> vanish or are separately identifiable in every model.

= Completely randomized design
{parent=Design of experiments}
{wiki}

A <completely randomized design> assigns <treatments> directly to available <experimental units> using <complete randomization>, without blocking. Fixed group sizes can be enforced. The residual then includes uncontrolled between-unit variation.

= Blocks in experimental design
{parent=Design of experiments}

= Blocking in experimental design
{synonym}

= Experimental block
{synonym}

A block groups <experimental units> expected to have similar background responses. Comparing <treatments> within blocks can reduce the <variance of an estimator>. Block allocation must be specified before responses are used to evaluate treatment effects.

= Estimability from within-block differences
{parent=Blocks in experimental design}

For a <linear regression> model $Y_{bj}=\alpha_b+f(x_{bj})^T\beta+\epsilon_{bj}$ with one unrestricted <intercept> per two-run <experimental block>, differencing the responses removes all <experimental block> effects. The parameter vector $\beta$ is identifiable precisely when the <matrix> whose rows are $f(x_{b1})-f(x_{b2})$ has full column <rank>. This criterion applies to arbitrary pairings, including nonregular <factorial designs>; requiring regular <orthogonal> confounding is a stronger constraint.

= Block confounding in a factorial design
{parent=Blocks in experimental design}

Partition a regular <factorial design> by fixed signs of independent <factorial contrasts>. Products of those block-generating columns span the block effects. A treatment contrast that is constant on each block is therefore confounded with block differences. Select generators so that only scientifically dispensable treatment effects enter this space; randomize the assignment of blocks and the run order within each block.

= Block design
{parent=Blocks in experimental design}

A <block design> assigns <treatments> to <experimental units> partitioned into blocks. Its incidence counts $n_{ij}$ record how often <treatment> $i$ occurs in block $j$. Balance and connectedness determine which <treatment contrasts> are estimable and how much adjustment for blocks costs.

= Balanced incomplete block design
{parent=Block design}
{title2=$(t,b,r,k,\lambda)$}
{wiki}

= BIBD
{c}
{synonym}

A binary <block design> has $t$ <treatments>, $b$ blocks of size $k<t$, every treatment in $r$ blocks, and every distinct pair in $\lambda$ blocks. Counting occurrences and pairs gives $tr=bk$ and $r(k-1)=\lambda(t-1)$. Its <incidence matrix of a set system> $N$ satisfies $NN^T=(r-\lambda)I+\lambda\mathbf1\mathbf1^T$. For statistical comparison of all treatment effects one also needs connectedness; $\lambda>0$ guarantees it, whereas singleton blocks with $\lambda=0$ do not.

= Symmetric balanced incomplete block design
{parent=Balanced incomplete block design}
{title2=$b=t,\quad r=k$}

A <balanced incomplete block design> is symmetric when its number of blocks equals its number of treatments. The identity $bk=tr$ then gives $r=k$. Its square <incidence matrix of a set system> $N$ obeys $NN^T=(r-\lambda)I+\lambda J$, with eigenvalues $r-\lambda$ of multiplicity $t-1$ and $r^2$ of multiplicity one.

= Even-order symmetric design square obstruction
{parent=Symmetric balanced incomplete block design}
{title2=$t\text{ even}\ \Longrightarrow\ r-\lambda\text{ is a square}$}

In a nontrivial <symmetric balanced incomplete block design>, $\det(N)^2=r^2(r-\lambda)^{t-1}$. If $t$ is even, $t-1$ is odd. For every prime, the exponent in the left side is even and that in $r^2$ is even, so its exponent in $r-\lambda$ must be even. Thus $r-\lambda$ is a perfect square. This supplies an elementary necessary existence condition without claiming it is sufficient.

= Fisher's inequality for block designs
{c}
{parent=Balanced incomplete block design}
{title2=$b\geq t$}

For a <balanced incomplete block design> with $r>\lambda$, $NN^T$ has <eigenvalues> $r-\lambda>0$ on the zero-sum subspace and $r+(t-1)\lambda>0$ on the constant vector. Thus its <rank> is $t$. As $N$ is $t\times b$, $t=\operatorname{rank}(NN^T)\leq\operatorname{rank}(N)\leq b$.

= Row-column design
{parent=Block design}

A <row-column design> has two crossed partitions of <experimental units> into rows and columns. Equal treatment proportions in every row and every column make treatment contrasts <orthogonal> to both centered block spaces. A repeated three-treatment arrangement in a six-by-six array can give a balanced example.

= Randomized complete block design
{parent=Block design}

A <randomized complete block design> places every <treatment> in every block, with a common replication pattern, and uses independent within-block <randomization>. The usual basic version has one occurrence of each <treatment> per block; equal repeated occurrences give a replicated complete-block version. Such equal-proportion designs are <orthogonal block designs>.

= Orthogonal block design
{parent=Block design}
{title2=$n_{ij}=r_i k_j/n$}

An <orthogonal block design> has centered treatment-indicator vectors <orthogonal> to centered block-indicator vectors on the <experimental units>. With treatment replication $r_i$, block size $k_j$ and total $n$, this is equivalent to $n_{ij}=r_i k_j/n$. It permits additive block adjustment without changing treatment estimates.

= Replication in experimental design
{parent=Design of experiments}

= Replication
{synonym}

<Replication in experimental design> repeats a <treatment> on multiple independent <experimental units>, so that variation between those units can inform a <treatment contrast> and its <standard error>. Repeated measurements on one <experimental unit> can improve measurement precision without providing additional independent <treatment> <replication>. The <experimental unit> is identified by where the <treatment> is applied, rather than by how many responses are recorded.<Experimental unit (NC3Rs Experimental Design Assistant)>[Experimental unit, “Why is the experimental unit important?” and “Know your experimental unit”, revised 31 October 2023]{ref}

For a constructed example, grow one <plant> in each of eight separate pots. Apply <treatment> A to four pots and <treatment> B to four pots, without sharing drainage water between pots. Measure the <length> of three <leaves> per pot. The pot is the <experimental unit>; the <leaf> is the <observational unit>. There are four <treatment> <replicates> per group, eight <experimental units> overall, and $8\cdot3=24$ measurements. The three <leaves> in one pot are <nested measurements>, because they share the same <plant> and <treatment> application. The setup assumes that pots do not affect one another; simply putting them in separate containers does not establish statistical <independence>.

\Image[experimental-replication-pots-and-leaves.png]
{title=Constructed two-<treatment> design: eight pot <experimental units> contain twenty-four <leaf> <observational units>}
{description=Each outlined pot receives one <treatment> application and supplies three <nested measurements>. A pot label marks an <experimental unit>, not a measured response value.}

<Randomization> chooses which <experimental units> receive which <treatments>; <replication> supplies multiple <experimental units> receiving the same <treatment>. These solve different problems. In the balanced allocation above, choose the four A pots uniformly among the $\binom84=70$ possible <subsets>. A particular pot has <probability> $1/2$ of receiving A. Given that one pot receives A, a second receives A with <conditional probability> $3/7$, rather than $1/2$. Thus the assignments are not <independent random variables>, even though the <experimental units> can supply independent responses conditional on those assignments in a suitable <statistical model>.

An actual <ecology> experiment illustrates why the distinction matters. In <Stuart H. Hurlbert>'s account, twelve experimental ponds initially received zero, fifty or 450 <fish>, with five, four and three ponds respectively. Preparation included assessing variation between ponds before introducing <fish>; allocation considered both location and existing differences between ponds. The zero-<fish> ponds supplied the <scientific control>. These were repeated <treatment> applications to ponds, not twelve samples from one pond.<Pseudoreplication and the Design of Ecological Field Experiments by Stuart H. Hurlbert>[Pseudoreplication and the Design of Ecological Field Experiments, pp. 194–195, combined or hybrid allocation approach and the Hurlbert–Mulla experiment]{ref}

The original article followed the southern California ponds for ten months. Its readouts included <zooplankton> populations and total <phytoplankton>; it reported decreases in some <zooplankton> populations and increased total <phytoplankton>.<Impacts of mosquitofish (Gambusia affinis) predation on plankton communities by Stuart H. Hurlbert and Mir Subhan Mulla>[Impacts of mosquitofish (Gambusia affinis) predation on plankton communities, abstract, Hydrobiologia 83, pp. 125–151, August 1981]{ref} Repeated sampling within a pond helps describe its response through time, while ponds supply <treatment> <replication>. Unequal group sizes and initial pond variation matter for the <statistical model>; the <treatment> counts alone do not establish equal <variances> or independent responses.

= Replicate
{parent=Replication in experimental design}

A <replicate> is an instance of a <treatment> on a distinct <experimental unit>. Independent <replicates> permit estimation of response variation between <experimental units>; subsamples within one <experimental unit> do not supply additional independent <treatment> <replication>. In the pot example, another pot receiving A supplies another A <replicate>, while another <leaf> from an existing A pot supplies another <observational unit>.

= Nested measurements
{parent=Replication in experimental design}

<Nested measurements> are responses grouped inside larger units, with each measured object belonging to just one such unit. Three <leaves> within each pot give <nested measurements>; repeatedly reading one <leaf> gives another level of repetition. Either can yield <clustered data>. A common <random effect> can describe the shared influence of the containing <experimental unit>.

For the constructed pot example, label <treatments> by $g\in\{A,B\}$, pots within each group by $i=1,\ldots,n$, and <leaves> within each pot by $j=1,\ldots,m$. Consider the <statistical model>
$$
Y_{gij}=\mu+\alpha_g+U_{gi}+\varepsilon_{gij},
\qquad
\mathbb E[U_{gi}]=\mathbb E[\varepsilon_{gij}]=0,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(U_{gi})=\tau^2,
\quad
\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_{gij})=\sigma^2.
$$
Here $Y_{gij}$ is a <leaf> <length>, $\mu+\alpha_g$ is its group <expected value>, $U_{gi}$ is a pot <random effect>, and $\varepsilon_{gij}$ describes remaining variation among <leaves> and <measurement error>. Assume that all pot <random effects> are mutually <independent>, all remaining errors are mutually <independent>, and the two collections are <independent> of one another and of the <randomization>. These are explicit modelling assumptions, conditional on the assigned <treatments>; no <normal distribution> assumption is needed for the following <variance> calculation.

Two distinct <leaf> measurements in one pot share $U_{gi}$. Their <covariance> is $\tau^2$, their individual <variance> is $\tau^2+\sigma^2$, and their <intraclass correlation coefficient> is
$$
\rho=\frac{\tau^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}
\qquad (\tau^2+\sigma^2>0).
$$
Measurements from different pots have zero <covariance> and are <independent> under the stated <statistical model>. Statistical <independence> therefore concerns the whole response structure, not just the fact that the measured <leaves> are different objects.

= Variance of nested measurements
{parent=Nested measurements}
{title2=$\operatorname{Var}(\overline Y_g)=\tau^2/n+\sigma^2/(nm)$}

For $n$ pots per <treatment> and $m$ <leaves> per pot, the group <sample mean> decomposes into two averages:
$$
\overline Y_g=\frac1{nm}\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^mY_{gij}
=\mu+\alpha_g+\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nU_{gi}
+\frac1{nm}\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^m\varepsilon_{gij}.
$$
The <variance of a sum> adds the <variances> of the <independent> terms. Every pot <random effect> has weight $1/n$, whereas each remaining error has weight $1/(nm)$, giving the short proof
$$
\operatorname{Var}(\overline Y_g)
=n\frac{\tau^2}{n^2}+nm\frac{\sigma^2}{n^2m^2}
=\frac{\tau^2}{n}+\frac{\sigma^2}{nm}.
$$
This is also the <variance> of the <arithmetic mean> of the $n$ pot <sample means>, each with <variance> $\tau^2+\sigma^2/m$. Equal numbers of <leaves> per pot make the two averaging procedures identical.

If all $nm$ <leaf> measurements were falsely treated as <independent>, the predicted <variance> would instead be $(\tau^2+\sigma^2)/(nm)$. The ratio of correct to false <variance> is
$$
\frac{m\tau^2+\sigma^2}{\tau^2+\sigma^2}
=1+(m-1)\rho.
$$
Thus <pseudoreplication> can underestimate a <standard error> by the square root of this ratio. Equality holds when $m=1$ or $\tau^2=0$; sharing a pot creates an error here precisely when the shared influence has positive <variance>.

Choose $\tau^2=4$, $\sigma^2=1$, $n=4$ and $m=3$ as constructed model parameters in squared <length> units. These are assumptions rather than fitted experimental values. For each group,
$$
\operatorname{Var}(\overline Y_g)=\frac44+\frac1{12}=\frac{13}{12},
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}_{\mathrm{false}}(\overline Y_g)=\frac5{12},
\qquad \rho=\frac45.
$$
The <treatment contrast> $\widehat\Delta=\overline Y_B-\overline Y_A$ has <expected value> $\alpha_B-\alpha_A$. Since the group errors are <independent> conditional on the allocation, its <variance> is twice the group <variance>, namely $13/6$, whereas the false calculation gives $5/6$. Its correct <standard error> is $\sqrt{13/6}\approx1.472$ <length> units; the false value is $\sqrt{5/6}\approx0.913$. The <variance> ratio is $13/5=2.6$.

With four pots fixed, increasing $m$ drives the error contribution $\sigma^2/(4m)$ towards zero, but the <limit> of the group <variance> remains $\tau^2/4=1$. Measuring twelve <leaves> on each of four pots gives $49/48$. Using the same forty-eight measurements on sixteen pots, with three <leaves> per pot, gives $13/48$. More independent <experimental units> reduce both contributions. Extra <nested measurements> are useful for characterizing each <experimental unit>, but cannot average away its shared <random effect>. = Biological replicate
{parent=Replication in experimental design}

A <biological replicate> repeats a procedure using a distinct biological preparation, such as another <plant> or a separately established <cell culture>. The relevant level must be specified: several <cell cultures> derived from one donor can still form <nested measurements> for a donor-level question. Calling a sample a <biological replicate> does not by itself establish an independent <experimental unit>.

= Between-replicate variation
{parent=Biological replicate}

Variation among independent biological samples. Pooling reads can conceal this variation; treating within-sample technical observations as independent biological replicates causes <pseudoreplication>.

= Technical replicate
{parent=Replication in experimental design}

A <technical replicate> repeats a measurement or analytical procedure on the same biological preparation. For example, read the <length> of the same <leaf> three times to assess <measurement error>. Measuring three different <leaves> within one pot instead samples variation within the <plant>; it should be described explicitly rather than silently equated with repeated readings of one <leaf>. Neither procedure supplies another pot-level <treatment> <replicate>.

= Experimental unit
{parent=Design of experiments}

An <experimental unit> is a unit to which a <treatment> is assigned under the experimental allocation. A whole orchard can be an <experimental unit> even when responses are recorded separately on its trees. Different factors in a <split-plot design> can have different <experimental units>.

= Observational unit
{parent=Experimental unit}

An <observational unit> is the object or occasion supplying a recorded response. Several <observational units> can belong to one <experimental unit>; counting them as independent treatment <replicates> creates <pseudoreplication>.
