= Solution
{parent=A1}

Take $u>0$ to mean that the mirror recedes along the incident <plane electromagnetic wave>. Put $\beta=u/c$, with $|\beta|<1$. The <moving-mirror Doppler shift> can be obtained directly by matching <wave phase> on the moving surface. Write the incident <wave phase> as $k_i z-\omega_i t$ and the reflected <wave phase> as $-k_r z-\omega_r t$, where $k_i=\omega_i/c$ and $k_r=\omega_r/c$ are positive <wavenumbers>. At the mirror, $z=z_0+ut$, the two phases must have the same time dependence; their constant offset incorporates the reflection <phase shift>. Thus
$$
k_i u-\omega_i=-k_r u-\omega_r,\qquad
\omega_i(1-\beta)=\omega_r(1+\beta).
$$
Consequently the observed reflected <angular frequency> and ordinary <frequency> are
$$
\boxed{\omega_r=\omega_i\frac{1-u/c}{1+u/c},\qquad
f_r=f_i\frac{1-u/c}{1+u/c}.}
$$
This is a redshift for a receding mirror and a blueshift for an approaching mirror. At small <velocity>, $\omega_r\simeq\omega_i(1-2u/c)$.

Equivalently, the <relativistic Doppler effect> gives $\omega_i'=\gamma(1-\beta)\omega_i$ in the mirror rest <inertial frame>. Reflection there reverses propagation and preserves <frequency>. Transforming the backward-going reflected wave to the laboratory gives another factor $\gamma(1-\beta)$, so $\omega_r=\gamma^2(1-\beta)^2\omega_i=\omega_i(1-\beta)/(1+\beta)$. A single Doppler factor would miss the effect of the return path.

= Solution
{parent=A2}

Use a transparent reciprocal <wave plate> at its design <wavelength>. For the return calculation, assume a mirror at <normal incidence> with the same reflection coefficient for both transverse components; this is the usual ideal <double-pass quarter-wave plate> arrangement.

= Retardation mechanism
{parent=Solution}

<Birefringence> gives two orthogonal linear eigenpolarizations with different <refractive indices>, $n_f$ and $n_s$. They accumulate different <wave phases> through the same physical thickness $d$. Their relative <phase retardation> is
$$
\delta=\frac{\omega d}{c}(n_s-n_f)=\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda_0}(n_s-n_f),\qquad
\boxed{\delta=\pm\frac\pi2\pmod{2\pi}}.
$$
Here $\lambda_0$ is the <vacuum> <wavelength>. For the thinnest positive-retardance plate, $d=\lambda_0/[4(n_s-n_f)]$. The plate uses a difference in <phase velocity>, rather than differential <wave absorption>. Equal <wave amplitudes> along the two axes, obtained from input <linear polarization> at $45^\circ$ to them, acquire <phase quadrature> and become <circular polarization>. Reversing that conversion gives <linear polarization> from the corresponding circular input. A different input angle generally gives <elliptical polarization> on one pass.

= Returned polarization
{parent=Solution}

Use the same fixed laboratory $x,y$ axes for the incident and returning <Jones vectors>, with $x$ along the fast axis. Remove a common <phase factor> so that the one-pass <Jones matrix> is $J=\operatorname{diag}(1,i)$. The reverse pass through this reciprocal plate accumulates the same relative <phase retardation>, not its inverse. The mirror has scalar reflection coefficient $r_m$ in these fixed axes. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbf e_{\rm return}=J(r_mI)J\mathbf e_{\rm in}
=r_m\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\mathbf e_{\rm in}.}
$$
The effective retardance is $\pi$: the complete return acts as a <half-wave plate>. For initial <linear polarization> at angle $\alpha$ to the fast axis,
$$
\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha\\\sin\alpha\end{pmatrix}
\longmapsto
\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha\\-\sin\alpha\end{pmatrix},
\qquad \boxed{\alpha_{\rm return}=-\alpha\pmod\pi.}
$$
In particular, the $45^\circ$ input used for linear-to-circular conversion returns in <linear polarization> perpendicular to its original line: $(1,1)/\sqrt2\mapsto(1,-1)/\sqrt2$. An input along either plate axis returns along that same axis.

The wording does not specify the initial <polarization of waves>. Thus “orthogonal linear return” requires the usual $45^\circ$ linearly polarized input; it is not a universal answer for arbitrary polarized input. The <Jones matrix> above supplies the general answer. A non-normal metallic reflection may introduce different complex reflection coefficients for the two components and would require additional geometry and optical data. Descriptions of the intermediate <circular polarization> must also account for the reversed propagation direction when assigning handedness.

= Solution
{parent=A3}

The transverse and longitudinal quantities characterize different correlations: <spatial optical coherence> across a wavefront and <temporal optical coherence> between delayed fields. Their numerical values require a convention for how much correlation counts as coherent.

= Transverse coherence estimate
{parent=Solution}

Convert the distances to SI units: $r=1.5\times10^{11}\,\mathrm m$ and $D=1.4\times10^9\,\mathrm m$. The angular solar diameter is
$$
\Theta\simeq\frac Dr=9.33\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{rad}.
$$
Two receivers separated by a transverse baseline $b$ see a spread of relative <wave phases> of order $2\pi b\Theta/\lambda$. Averaging over incoherent source directions therefore reduces <interferometric visibility> when $b$ reaches the <transverse optical coherence length> scale $\lambda/\Theta$. At $\lambda=500\,\mathrm{nm}$,
$$
\boxed{\ell_\perp\sim\frac{\lambda r}{D}
=\frac{(5.00\times10^{-7})(1.5\times10^{11})}{1.4\times10^9}\,\mathrm m
=5.36\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm m\simeq54\,\mu\mathrm m.}
$$
If “coherence length” specifically means the first zero of <uniform-disk stellar visibility>, a uniformly bright circular solar disk gives $g^{(1)}(b)=2J_1(x)/x$, with $x=\pi b\Theta/\lambda$. The first zero then lies at $\boxed{b_0=1.21967\lambda/\Theta\simeq65\,\mu\mathrm m}$. This more specific number assumes a uniform disk and a first-zero criterion; the characteristic estimate is approximately $50$–$60\,\mu\mathrm m$. Solar brightness variation across the disk changes the exact contrast curve.

= Longitudinal coherence estimate
{parent=Solution}

The <coherence length from a finite spectral band> is most safely estimated by converting its endpoints to <frequency>, because the quoted band is broad:
$$
\Delta\nu=c\left(\frac1{275\,\mathrm{nm}}-\frac1{875\,\mathrm{nm}}\right)
\simeq7.48\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}.
$$
The reciprocal-bandwidth <optical coherence time> and corresponding longitudinal <optical coherence length> are
$$
\tau_c\sim\frac1{\Delta\nu}\simeq1.34\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm s,\qquad
\boxed{\ell_\parallel\sim c\tau_c=
\frac{(275)(875)}{875-275}\,\mathrm{nm}
\simeq401\,\mathrm{nm}=0.40\,\mu\mathrm m.}
$$
The given <temperature> is consistent with the spectral peak: <Wien's displacement law> gives $\lambda_{\rm peak}=2.898\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m\,K}/5778\,\mathrm K\simeq502\,\mathrm{nm}$. Using the common narrow-band estimate $\lambda_{\rm peak}^2/\Delta\lambda$, with $\Delta\lambda=600\,\mathrm{nm}$, gives about $0.42\,\mu\mathrm m$. Its closeness here does not justify the narrow-band approximation for arbitrary broad spectra.

The $0.40\,\mu\mathrm m$ result is a bandwidth-scale estimate. A flat spectral density per unit <frequency> would make it the first-zero <optical coherence length>; a truncated <Planck law> spectrum is not flat and would have a different correlation function. Neither the stated endpoints nor the word “coherence” specifies an exact visibility threshold. Propagation to Earth sets the angular size and transverse scale, but does not by itself narrow the solar <optical spectrum> or lengthen this longitudinal scale.

= Solution
{parent=B4}

Use <retarded time> $t_r$ and reserve $r$ for the large observation distance. The source velocity is nonrelativistic, $|\mathbf u|/c\ll1$. There are two errors in the original PDF that must be corrected before the <radiation zone> calculation: the vector-potential prefactor contains an extraneous $1/r$, and the displayed magnetic radiation field has the wrong sign for the stated outward unit vector $\mathbf n$. The extra distance factor is dimensionally wrong; the magnetic sign would make the <Poynting vector> point inward. The following derivation uses dimensionally consistent retarded potentials and outgoing radiation.

= Radiation fields
{parent=Solution}

The consistent <Liénard–Wiechert potential> obeys $\mathbf A=\mathbf u\varphi/c^2$. To first order in $\boldsymbol\beta=\mathbf u/c$, its large-distance form is
$$
\varphi\simeq\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0r}[1+\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\beta(t_r)],\qquad
\mathbf A\simeq\frac{\mu_0q}{4\pi r}\mathbf u(t_r),\qquad
t_r\simeq t-\frac rc.
$$
Terms varying as $1/r^2$ give near fields and are negligible for the radiated <power> at large $r$. Keep the first-order velocity term in $\varphi$: although small, its retarded-time derivative contributes a leading <acceleration> field. To the required order, $\partial t_r/\partial t\simeq1$ and $\nabla t_r\simeq-\mathbf n/c$. Holding the observation direction fixed at leading order gives
$$
-\nabla\varphi\big|_{1/r}=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0cr}\mathbf n(\mathbf n\cdot\dot{\boldsymbol\beta}),\qquad
-\frac{\partial\mathbf A}{\partial t}\bigg|_{1/r}=-\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0cr}\dot{\boldsymbol\beta}.
$$
Thus the outgoing <electric field> is transverse:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E_{\rm rad}(\mathbf r,t)=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0c r}
\left[\mathbf n\times(\mathbf n\times\dot{\boldsymbol\beta})\right]_{t_r}
=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0c^2r}
\left[\mathbf n\times(\mathbf n\times\mathbf a)\right]_{t_r}.}
$$
Here $\mathbf a=d\mathbf u/dt_r$. The <magnetic field> follows from $\nabla\times\mathbf A$, or from the outgoing <plane electromagnetic wave> relation:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B_{\rm rad}=-\frac{\mu_0q}{4\pi r}
[\mathbf n\times\dot{\boldsymbol\beta}]_{t_r}
=\frac1c\mathbf n\times\mathbf E_{\rm rad}.}
$$
The minus sign is essential. Both fields fall as $1/r$, are mutually orthogonal and obey $|\mathbf E|=c|\mathbf B|$. The corresponding <Liénard-Wiechert field> also contains nonradiative terms, omitted in this leading far-field calculation.

= Mean angular power
{parent=Solution}

Since $\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf n=0$, the <Poynting vector> is
$$
\mathbf S=\frac1{\mu_0}\mathbf E\times\mathbf B
=\epsilon_0c|\mathbf E|^2\mathbf n.
$$
The area on a distant sphere corresponding to <solid angle> $d\Omega$ is $r^2d\Omega$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\langle P\rangle}{d\Omega}
=\frac{q^2}{16\pi^2\epsilon_0c^3}
\left\langle |\mathbf a|^2-(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf a)^2\right\rangle
=\frac{q^2}{16\pi^2\epsilon_0c}
\left\langle|\mathbf n\times\dot{\boldsymbol\beta}|^2\right\rangle.}
$$
The average is over the source motion at <retarded time>. At leading nonrelativistic order, differences between source-time and reception-time averaging are beyond the approximation. The units are <power> per <solid angle>, $\mathrm{W\,sr^{-1}}$.

The <acceleration covariance in nonrelativistic radiation> is another way to organize this result: with $C_{ij}=\langle a_i a_j\rangle$, the angular factor is $\operatorname{tr}C-\mathbf n^TC\mathbf n$. Integrating over all observation directions uses $\int[|\mathbf a|^2-(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf a)^2]d\Omega=(8\pi/3)|\mathbf a|^2$ and gives the averaged <Larmor formula>, $\langle P\rangle=q^2\langle a^2\rangle/(6\pi\epsilon_0c^3)$.

= Linear oscillation
{parent=Solution}

For the prescribed displacement along $z$, the source <acceleration> is $\mathbf a(t_r)=-a\omega^2\cos(\omega t_r)\widehat{\mathbf z}$. If $\theta$ is the angle from the $z$ axis, $|\mathbf a|^2-(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf a)^2=a^2\omega^4\cos^2(\omega t_r)\sin^2\theta$. Averaging $\cos^2$ over one <wave period> gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\langle P\rangle_{\rm linear}}{d\Omega}
=\frac{q^2a^2\omega^4}{32\pi^2\epsilon_0c^3}\sin^2\theta.}
$$
The <angular distribution of electric dipole radiation> has zero intensity on the oscillation axis and maximum intensity in the equatorial plane, with no azimuthal dependence. Its total <power> is
$$
\boxed{\langle P\rangle_{\rm linear}=\frac{q^2a^2\omega^4}{12\pi\epsilon_0c^3}.}
$$
The condition $a\omega\ll c$ is the required nonrelativistic condition. The plotted radial coordinate below is angular <power>, rather than field <wave amplitude>.

= Circular orbit
{parent=Solution}

Denote the orbit radius by $b$ here to distinguish it from observation distance; it is the quantity called $R$ in this part of the paper. Write
$$
\mathbf x(t_r)=b(\cos\omega t_r,\sin\omega t_r,0),\qquad
\mathbf a(t_r)=-b\omega^2(\cos\omega t_r,\sin\omega t_r,0).
$$
For observation at <spherical coordinates> $(\theta,\phi)$, measured from the orbit normal,
$$
\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf a=-b\omega^2\sin\theta\cos(\omega t_r-\phi),\qquad
\langle(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf a)^2\rangle=\tfrac12b^2\omega^4\sin^2\theta.
$$
The acceleration magnitude is constant, $|\mathbf a|=b\omega^2$. Therefore the <radiation pattern of a circularly moving nonrelativistic charge> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\langle P\rangle_{\rm circular}}{d\Omega}
=\frac{q^2b^2\omega^4}{32\pi^2\epsilon_0c^3}(1+\cos^2\theta),\qquad
\langle P\rangle_{\rm circular}=\frac{q^2b^2\omega^4}{6\pi\epsilon_0c^3}.}
$$
Time averaging removes the azimuthal dependence. The axial intensity is twice the equatorial intensity, and no direction has zero mean intensity: the instantaneous dipole axis continually rotates. This is also the sum of the two orthogonal linear-oscillation patterns with their relative <phase quadrature>. At equal radius and linear-oscillation amplitude, the total mean <power> is twice the linear result. The calculation requires $b\omega\ll c$; relativistic <synchrotron radiation> is outside this approximation.

= Solution
{parent=B5}

Treat a single weakly driven <electron> or an independent bound charge, so that the incident <electric field> is approximately uniform across its displacement. The classical calculation uses <electric dipole radiation> and excludes both a resonant divergence and substantial quantum recoil.

= Rayleigh limit
{parent=Solution}

In <Rayleigh scattering>, a small bound system develops an induced <electric dipole moment> $\mathbf p(t)=\operatorname{Re}[\alpha(\omega)\mathbf E_0e^{-i\omega t}]$. Well below its binding resonances, the <electric polarizability> is approximately its static value $\alpha(0)$, independent of <frequency>. The mean radiated <power> and incident <light intensity> are
$$
\langle P\rangle=\frac{\omega^4|\alpha E_0|^2}{12\pi\epsilon_0c^3},\qquad
\langle S\rangle=\frac12\epsilon_0c|E_0|^2.
$$
Dividing gives the total scattering <cross section>
$$
\boxed{\sigma_R=\frac{\omega^4|\alpha(0)|^2}{6\pi\epsilon_0^2c^4}
\propto\omega^4\propto\lambda^{-4}.}
$$
This <Rayleigh scattering wavelength dependence> comes from the two time derivatives of an oscillating <electric dipole moment>, whose radiated field is proportional to $\omega^2p_0$. It requires the same scatterer with approximately constant low-frequency <electric polarizability>; resonances and strong <optical dispersion> invalidate the simple fourth-power law.

= Thomson limit
{parent=Solution}

<Thomson scattering> describes the low-recoil scattering of <electromagnetic radiation> by a free charge. For charge $q$ and <mass> $m$, the weak incident field gives peak <acceleration> $|q|E_0/m$. The mean <Larmor formula> <power> is therefore
$$
\langle P\rangle=\frac{q^4|E_0|^2}{12\pi\epsilon_0m^2c^3}.
$$
There is no binding force to introduce a characteristic <frequency>. Dividing by the same incident <light intensity> gives the <Thomson cross-section>:
$$
\boxed{\sigma_T=\frac{q^4}{6\pi\epsilon_0^2m^2c^4}
=\frac{8\pi}{3}\left(\frac{q^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0mc^2}\right)^2.}
$$
For an <electron>, the length inside parentheses is the <classical electron radius>. The <cross section> has units $\mathrm m^2$ and is independent of <wavelength> in this classical limit. The driven <velocity> must remain small compared with $c$, and $\hbar\omega\ll mc^2$ must make <Compton recoil kinematics> negligible.

= Bound-charge response
{parent=Solution}

Put $\omega_0^2=k/m$, where $k$ is the binding spring constant rather than an optical <wavenumber>. The steady <complex amplitude> solution of the forced <Lorentz oscillator model> is
$$
\mathbf x_0=\frac{q\mathbf E_0}{k-m\omega^2}
=\frac{q\mathbf E_0}{m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)},\qquad
\alpha(\omega)=\frac{q^2}{m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)}.
$$
The acceleration <complex amplitude> is $-\omega^2\mathbf x_0$. Hence
$$
\langle P\rangle=\frac{q^2\omega^4|\mathbf x_0|^2}{12\pi\epsilon_0c^3},\qquad
\boxed{\sigma(\omega)=\frac{q^4\omega^4}{6\pi\epsilon_0^2c^4(k-m\omega^2)^2}
=\sigma_T\frac{\omega^4}{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2}.}
$$
This is the <scattering cross-section of a harmonically bound charge>. The displacement and induced <electric dipole moment> are parallel to the incident <linear polarization>, and integrating their dipole radiation over all directions gives the total <cross section> above.

For $\omega\ll\omega_0$, binding dominates and
$$
\boxed{\sigma\simeq\sigma_T(\omega/\omega_0)^4,}
$$
recovering <Rayleigh scattering> with static <electric polarizability> $q^2/k$. For $\omega\gg\omega_0$, inertia dominates: $\mathbf x_0\simeq-q\mathbf E_0/(m\omega^2)$, so its second time derivative becomes frequency independent and
$$
\boxed{\sigma\simeq\sigma_T.}
$$
This is a classical <Thomson scattering> regime only while $\hbar\omega\ll mc^2$. Exactly at $\omega=\omega_0$, an undamped driven oscillator has no finite steady-state amplitude; the divergent formula must not be used. Finite <damping> and <radiation reaction> regulate the physical resonant response. Coherent contributions from many charges in an extended medium can also require a structure calculation rather than multiplying this single-charge result by a particle count.

= Binding scale and recoil
{parent=Solution}

A distinct classical <Thomson scattering> window requires two separated scales:
$$
\omega_0\ll\omega\ll\omega_C,\qquad \omega_C=\frac{mc^2}{\hbar}.
$$
To remove that window, the binding frequency must be comparable with or greater than the <Compton scattering> recoil scale. Thus the formal condition for <bound-electron scattering without a Thomson frequency window> is
$$
\sqrt{\frac km}\gtrsim\frac{mc^2}{\hbar},\qquad
\boxed{k\gtrsim\frac{m^3c^4}{\hbar^2}.}
$$
Using the stipulated approximate <electron mass> $m=10^{-30}\,\mathrm{kg}$, $c=2.998\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and $\hbar=1.055\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$ gives
$$
\boxed{k_{\rm scale}\simeq7.3\times10^{11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}},\qquad
\omega_C\simeq8.5\times10^{20}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}.}
$$
The dimensions are $[m^3c^4/\hbar^2]=\mathrm{kg\,s^{-2}}=\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$. The inequality denotes a scale comparison, not a uniquely specified spring constant or a sharp transition.

There is also a physical limitation to the question's premise. <Compton scattering> does not begin discontinuously at $\hbar\omega=mc^2$; recoil corrections grow as photon <energy> approaches the rest <energy>. If $\hbar\omega_0\gtrsim mc^2$, the assumed nonrelativistic binding model is itself no longer reliable. The calculation therefore locates where no well-separated classical <Thomson scattering> range exists; it cannot establish a literal direct transition of a real strongly bound system using this oscillator equation alone.

= Solution
{parent=B6}

First prove the two <electromagnetic field invariants> from the supplied three-dimensional transformations. Then distinguish the correct <covariant transversality of a vacuum plane wave> from the index and equivalence issues in the original tensor statement.

= Field invariants
{parent=Solution}

Let $\mathbf u$ be the boost <velocity>, and decompose the fields into components parallel and perpendicular to it. The parallel components are unchanged. The perpendicular components obey
$$
\mathbf E'_\perp=\gamma(\mathbf E_\perp+\mathbf u\times\mathbf B_\perp),\qquad
\mathbf B'_\perp=\gamma(\mathbf B_\perp-\mathbf u\times\mathbf E_\perp/c^2),\qquad
\gamma=(1-u^2/c^2)^{-1/2}.
$$
All vectors inside these expressions are perpendicular to $\mathbf u$. Thus $|\mathbf u\times\mathbf B_\perp|^2=u^2B_\perp^2$, and similarly for $\mathbf E_\perp$. In the difference of squares, the mixed terms cancel because
$$
\mathbf E_\perp\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B_\perp)
=-\mathbf B_\perp\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf E_\perp).
$$
It follows that
$$
\begin{aligned}
E_\perp'^2-c^2B_\perp'^2
&=\gamma^2\left[(1-u^2/c^2)E_\perp^2-(c^2-u^2)B_\perp^2\right]\\
&=E_\perp^2-c^2B_\perp^2.
\end{aligned}
$$
Adding the unchanged parallel components proves
$$
\boxed{E'^2-c^2B'^2=E^2-c^2B^2.}
$$
For the mixed <dot product>, expand directly. The terms $\mathbf E_\perp\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf E_\perp)$ and $(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B_\perp)\cdot\mathbf B_\perp$ vanish. The remaining cross-product identity is
$$
(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B_\perp)\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf E_\perp)
=u^2\mathbf E_\perp\cdot\mathbf B_\perp.
$$
Hence
$$
\mathbf E'_\perp\cdot\mathbf B'_\perp
=\gamma^2(1-u^2/c^2)\mathbf E_\perp\cdot\mathbf B_\perp
=\mathbf E_\perp\cdot\mathbf B_\perp.
$$
Parallel-perpendicular mixed <dot products> are zero in both frames, so
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E'\cdot\mathbf B'=\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B.}
$$
Rotations also preserve these two expressions, completing the proof for proper <Lorentz transformations>. The second expression is a pseudoscalar under spatial parity, but is invariant under the boosts at issue here.

These results also follow from <tensor contractions>. In the convention used below, $F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}=2(B^2-E^2/c^2)$ and $F_{\mu\nu}\widetilde F^{\mu\nu}=-4\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B/c$. The numerical sign of the latter depends on the chosen orientation of the <dual electromagnetic field tensor>, while its invariance under proper <Lorentz transformations> does not.

= Transversality and tensor conventions
{parent=Solution}

Write a nonzero <vacuum> <plane electromagnetic wave> with <wavevector> $\mathbf k=(\omega/c)\mathbf s$, where $\mathbf s$ is a unit propagation direction. Its propagation <Lorentz four-vector> is $K^\mu=(\omega/c,\mathbf k)$, or, up to a scalar normalization in any one frame, $n^\mu=(1,\mathbf s)$. It is null because $K_\mu K^\mu=0$.

Choose coordinates $x^\mu=(ct,\mathbf x)$ and <Minkowski metric> $\eta_{\mu\nu}=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$. With the supplied three-dimensional boost formulas, a consistent convention assigns the printed array to upper-index components,
$$
F^{0i}=-E_i/c,\qquad F^{ij}=-\epsilon_{ijk}B_k.
$$
The <index conventions for the electromagnetic field tensor> then require $F_{0i}=+E_i/c$ and $F_{ij}=F^{ij}$ when both indices are lowered. The printed lower-index label therefore cannot be kept together with its electric signs and a forward propagation vector in this convention.

This is not merely an arbitrary overall sign. For a valid forward wave with $\mathbf s=\widehat{\mathbf z}$, $\mathbf E=E_0\widehat{\mathbf x}$ and $\mathbf B=(E_0/c)\widehat{\mathbf y}$, multiplying the printed array, unchanged, by $n^\mu=(1,0,0,1)$ gives an $x$ component $-2E_0/c$, rather than zero. One consistent repair is to retain the printed array as $F^{\mu\nu}$ and contract with $n_\mu=(1,-\mathbf s)$. Equivalently, lower both tensor indices before forming the contraction asked for in the question.

Using that corrected lower-index tensor gives
$$
n^\mu F_{\mu\nu}
=\left(-\frac{\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf E}{c},\frac{\mathbf E}{c}+\mathbf s\times\mathbf B\right).
$$
To establish both electric and magnetic transversality, include the <dual electromagnetic field tensor>. Choose $\widetilde F^{0i}=-B_i$ and $\widetilde F^{ij}=\epsilon_{ijk}E_k/c$; lowering both indices then gives
$$
n^\mu\widetilde F_{\mu\nu}
=\left(-\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf B,\mathbf B-\frac{\mathbf s\times\mathbf E}{c}\right).
$$
The source-free plane-wave <Maxwell equations> are precisely the pair of covariant conditions
$$
\boxed{n^\mu F_{\mu\nu}=0,\qquad n^\mu\widetilde F_{\mu\nu}=0.}
$$
Their temporal components give $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf E=0$ and $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf B=0$. Their spatial components give the associated amplitude and orientation relations, $\mathbf E/c=-\mathbf s\times\mathbf B$ and $\mathbf B=\mathbf s\times\mathbf E/c$.

To see the <Lorentz covariance> explicitly, let $V_\nu=K^\mu F_{\mu\nu}$. It is a <covector>, so if $V_\nu=0$ in one <inertial frame>, its <Lorentz transformation> is also zero in every other <inertial frame>. The same is true of $K^\mu\widetilde F_{\mu\nu}$. Since the transformed wave still has $K'^0>0$ and $|\mathbf k'|=\omega'/c$, their temporal components imply
$$
\boxed{\mathbf k'\cdot\mathbf E'=\mathbf k'\cdot\mathbf B'=0.}
$$
Here $K^\mu$, rather than a vector renormalized to have temporal component one in every frame, is the actual <Lorentz four-vector>; multiplying a zero contraction by a nonzero normalization does not affect the argument.

Finally, the two scalar perpendicularity statements alone are not equivalent to one tensor contraction. They do not enforce the amplitude relation between $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$. Conversely $n^\mu F_{\mu\nu}=0$ alone cannot exclude a longitudinal <magnetic field>: adding $B_\parallel\mathbf s$ leaves its cross product unchanged. For a genuine source-free <plane electromagnetic wave>, the remaining <Maxwell equations> supply that missing constraint, and both tensor contractions vanish. This qualification and the corrected index placement are necessary for the intended invariance argument.
