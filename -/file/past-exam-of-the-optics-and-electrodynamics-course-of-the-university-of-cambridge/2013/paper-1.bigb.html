= Solution
{parent=a}

The <wavelength> is $\lambda=c/f\simeq0.50\,\mathrm m$, and the aperture diameter is $D=1.0\,\mathrm m$. A uniformly illuminated circular aperture has normalized <power> pattern $[2J_1(x)/x]^2$, where $x=\pi D\sin\theta/\lambda$. Its first null is at $\sin\theta\simeq1.22\lambda/D$, while its half-power point has $\sin\theta\simeq0.514\lambda/D$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{Point to within roughly }0.25\,\mathrm{rad}\ (15^\circ)\text{ for modest loss}.}
$$
The first-null small-angle estimate is $0.61\,\mathrm{rad}$, or roughly $35^\circ$; using the sine gives about $38^\circ$. This is a broad beam because the diameter is only about two <wavelengths>. Real illumination and aperture efficiency alter the exact beam shape; a required allowable gain loss would specify the pointing tolerance more precisely.

For a matched, correctly polarized half-wave dipole, $A_{e,d}=G_d\lambda^2/(4\pi)$ with $G_d\simeq1.64$. For an efficient dish, $A_{e,s}\simeq\eta\pi(D/2)^2$. Received available <powers> are proportional to these areas. With both <voltages> referred to the same matched receiver resistance,
$$
\boxed{\frac{V_{\rm dipole}}{V_{\rm dish}}\simeq
\sqrt{\frac{G_d\lambda^2}{\eta\pi^2D^2}}
=\frac{0.204}{\sqrt\eta}.}
$$
For ideal aperture efficiency this is **about one fifth**, or five times greater <voltage> from the dish. Terminal <voltages> without a common impedance or matching convention are not fixed by aperture alone: $V_{\rm rms}^2=P_{\rm available}R_L$ for the respective <matched loads>. A short-dipole rather than half-wave convention changes the order-one gain factor, not the main estimate.

= Solution
{parent=b}

<Spatial coherence> compares the <electric fields> at different positions at a common time; <temporal coherence> compares the field with itself at a delay. Experimentally, they control whether separated apertures or unequal optical paths can form stable <interference> fringes. They are correlation properties, not a requirement that every emitted wave have a deterministic <phase>.

The source diameter is $\alpha=40/206265=1.94\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{rad}$. At a representative visible <wavelength> $\lambda=550\,\mathrm{nm}$,
$$
\boxed{\ell_\perp\sim\frac{\lambda}{\alpha}=2.8\,\mathrm{mm}.}
$$
For a uniformly bright circular disc, the first visibility zero is at $1.22\lambda/\alpha=3.5\,\mathrm{mm}$; the coefficient depends on the chosen coherence criterion and brightness distribution. Atmospheric <phase> fluctuations can further spoil measured coherence.

For an explicitly assumed broad visible band from $400$ to $700\,\mathrm{nm}$, the <frequency> span is $\Delta\nu=c(1/\lambda_{\min}-1/\lambda_{\max})=3.21\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\ell_\parallel\sim\frac c{\Delta\nu}\simeq0.93\,\mathrm{\mu m},\qquad
\tau_c\sim\frac1{\Delta\nu}\simeq3.1\,\mathrm{fs}.}
$$
Thus broadband planetary <light> has millimetre-scale transverse and micrometre-scale longitudinal coherence. The receiver band is not supplied: a narrow filter would increase the <temporal coherence> length, so this is a stated broadband estimate, not a unique source-only constant.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Use ideal large plates and neglect edge effects. A Lorentz boost normal to the plates does not contract either transverse plate dimension. Charge is invariant, so charge per plate area remains $\sigma'=\sigma$. <Gauss's law> then gives
$$
\boxed{E_\parallel'=\frac{\sigma'}{\epsilon_0}=\frac\sigma{\epsilon_0}=E_\parallel.}
$$
The plate separation contracts, but the infinite-plate field is <independent> of separation. It is therefore incorrect to infer a stronger field merely from the shorter gap. The ideal field is parallel to the boost, so there is no induced transverse <magnetic field> from this boost.

{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For a long <solenoid> at rest, $B=\mu_0nI$, where n is turns per unit axial length and I is the current around a turn. Under a boost along its axis, longitudinal contraction gives $n'=\gamma n$. The circumference of a turn is transverse to the boost and does not contract; <time dilation> reduces its charge-circulation rate, measured relative to the moving material of that turn, to $I'=I/\gamma$. Hence
$$
\boxed{B_\parallel'=\mu_0n'I'=\mu_0(\gamma n)(I/\gamma)=B_\parallel.}
$$
Equivalently, the transverse volume-current <density> of an ideal continuous current sheet is unchanged by an axial boost. The increase in turn <density> must not be included while leaving the per-turn current unchanged. End effects and the microscopic winding geometry are outside this ideal-solenoid argument.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $\mathbf R$ run from the charge's present position to the observation point, and let $\boldsymbol\beta=\mathbf v/c$ be constant. Lorentz-transforming the Coulomb field gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(\mathbf R,t)=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{(1-\beta^2)\mathbf R}{[R^2-|\boldsymbol\beta\times\mathbf R|^2]^{3/2}},\qquad
\mathbf B=\frac{\mathbf v\times\mathbf E}{c^2}.}
$$
At fixed R, the radial magnitude is proportional to $(1-\beta^2)/(1-\beta^2\sin^2\theta)^{3/2}$. It is reduced by $\gamma^{-2}$ along the motion and enhanced by $\gamma$ perpendicular to it. The field is therefore concentrated around the transverse plane at high <speed>. For negative charge all electric-field arrows reverse.

\Image[paper-1-oe2013-moving-charge.png]
{title=Original angular field-strength comparison for a uniformly moving charge, measured from its instantaneous position, with the direction of motion marked}

Although the fields depend causally on retarded motion, for constant <velocity> the electric-field direction points along the separation from the present position. This does not imply instantaneous action: the velocity-dependent part of the retarded field removes a naive directional lag. Both fields fall as $R^{-2}$; **uniform straight-line motion produces no acceleration-radiation term proportional to $R^{-1}$**.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Sunlight is approximately unpolarized before its interaction with the environment. For <Rayleigh scattering> by small isotropic scatterers, the induced <electric dipoles> radiate only their components transverse to the viewing direction. For unpolarized incident <light> scattered through angle $\chi$, the perpendicular and parallel <intensities> are proportional to 1 and $\cos^2\chi$. The degree of <linear polarization> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathcal P=\frac{\sin^2\chi}{1+\cos^2\chi}.}
$$
At $90^\circ$ single scattering is fully linearly polarized perpendicular to the <scattering plane>. This explains the polarized sky away from the <Sun>; multiple scattering and aerosols reduce the ideal contrast.

<Reflection> at a <dielectric> surface treats the two <linear polarizations> differently. At Brewster incidence, $\tan\theta_B=n_2/n_1$, the reflected p component vanishes for ideal transparent nonmagnetic media. The remaining reflected glare is s polarized, perpendicular to the incidence plane, approximately horizontal for a horizontal water surface. Away from this angle the <reflection> is generally partially polarized. The transmitted beam also has unequal s/p transmission.

\Image[paper-1-oe2013-environmental-polarization.png]
{title=Original diagrams of polarization perpendicular to a single-scattering plane and of s-polarized reflection near Brewster incidence at a dielectric surface}

Refraction and internal <reflection> in water droplets likewise have polarization-dependent Fresnel coefficients, producing strong <linear polarization> in rainbow <light>. These are generic scattering and boundary-condition mechanisms; no biological detection mechanism is required.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

An accelerated charge radiates. For ultrarelativistic circular motion, the emitted radiation is concentrated into a cone of angular width of order $\gamma^{-1}$ about the instantaneous <velocity>. As the cone sweeps past a distant observer, propagation-time compression produces a short pulse of width of order $\rho/(\gamma^3c)$, where $\rho$ is orbit curvature radius. Its Fourier spectrum extends to high harmonics, with conventional critical <angular frequency>
$$
\boxed{\omega_c=\frac{3\gamma^3c}{2\rho}.}
$$
This is a spectral scale, not a sharp upper cutoff. For transverse <acceleration> and $v\simeq c$, the classical radiated <power> is
$$
P=\frac{q^2\gamma^4a^2}{6\pi\epsilon_0c^3}
\simeq\frac{q^2c\gamma^4}{6\pi\epsilon_0\rho^2}.
$$
The radiation is linearly polarized for an observer in the orbit plane, with <elliptical polarization> away from it. The two sides of the orbit plane have opposite handedness. The strong forward preference and high-frequency spectrum follow from the relativistic radiation calculation.<On the Classical Radiation of Accelerated Electrons by Julian Schwinger>[1949, abstract: angular distribution, forward preference and high-frequency radiation]{ref}

\Image[paper-1-oe2013-synchrotron-cone.png]
{title=Original tangent-beaming geometry and normalized illustrative pulse showing the origin of a broad synchrotron spectrum; the pulse curve is schematic rather than measured}

Magnetic bending fields steer the charge but do no work, so maintaining a storage-ring beam requires <energy> replacement by accelerating fields. Bright <electron> beams provide useful radiation from <infrared> to <X-rays>; stronger curvature or higher <electron> <energy> shifts the spectral scale. The classical formulas assume negligible quantum recoil.

= Solution
{parent=3}

For propagation along z, write $\mathbf E=\operatorname{Re}\{(E_x\widehat{\mathbf x}+E_y\widehat{\mathbf y})e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\}$. A <Jones vector> contains the complex transverse components. Equal <phase> modulo $\pi$ gives <linear polarization>; equal magnitudes with <phase difference> $\pm\pi/2$ give <circular polarization>. A common overall <phase> has no effect on the <polarization ellipse>. A deterministic optical element acts by a <Jones matrix>, with successive matrices ordered from right to left along the <light> path.

A <circular polarizer> uses a linear <polarizer> transmitting x and a <quarter-wave plate> whose principal axes are at $\pm45^\circ$. Up to an overall <phase>, convenient matrices in fixed physical x/y axes are
$$
P=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
Q=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\ i&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The plate is lossless, $QQ^\dagger=I_2$, and $Q(1,0)^T=(1,i)^T/\sqrt2$ is circular in the supplied convention. **Put P on side A and Q on side B**: forward passage is $J_{A\to B}=QP$.

<Unpolarized light> has no single <Jones vector>. Represent its <intensity> by the <coherency matrix> $C_{\rm in}=(I_0/2)I_2$ and propagate it as $C_{\rm out}=JC_{\rm in}J^\dagger$. Forward transmission gives
$$
C_{A\to B}=\frac{I_0}{2}\,\mathbf e_+\mathbf e_+^\dagger,
\qquad\mathbf e_+=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\i\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Thus **the transmitted <light> is circular with <intensity> $I_0/2$**. The initial <polarizer> removes one incoherent linear component; the plate changes <phase> without restoring the lost <intensity>.

Reverse passage encounters Q before P and has $J_{B\to A}=PQ$ in the same fixed transverse axes. It gives
$$
\boxed{C_{B\to A}=\frac{I_0}{2}P.}
$$
The output is linearly polarized along x and again has <intensity> $I_0/2$. A retarder alone leaves an unpolarized <coherency matrix> unchanged. Consequently an unpolarized scene appears darker from either side, although the transmitted polarization differs. No polarization-dependent response of the observer is needed to explain this reduction.

For the mirror calculations below, use an ideal near-normal isotropic reflector, $M=-I_2$ up to a common <phase>. In fixed physical transverse axes <reflection> leaves the <polarization ellipse> unchanged; it reverses circular <helicity> because the propagation direction reverses. A reciprocal plate has the same relative <phase> delay on the return traversal in this fixed-axis convention. In particular $Q^2=i\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$: two passes exchange x and y linear states.

{title=Original outward and return polarization-state diagrams for the two orientations of a polarizer-plus-quarter-wave-plate filter in front of a mirror}

= Solution
{parent=a}

With B nearest the observer, <light> from the reflecting target near the observer first crosses Q and then P on its way to the mirror. <Unpolarized light> remains unpolarized through Q, then becomes x linear after P. Mirror <reflection> preserves that linear orientation. On return it passes the same P without further ideal rejection and then Q, becoming circular toward the observer. Thus **the reflected target remains visible**.

Algebraically, with input and output at the observer side,
$$
J_{\rm return}=QPM P Q=-QPQ,\qquad
\operatorname{tr}(J_{\rm return}C_{\rm in}J_{\rm return}^\dagger)=\frac{I_0}{2}.
$$
The target here is used only as a source/reflector of ordinary <unpolarized light>; no physiological model is involved.

= b
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

With A nearest the observer, the outward order is P then Q. The initially <unpolarized light> becomes x linear, then circular, before reaching the mirror. <Reflection> reverses its <helicity>. Its return through Q gives y linear, which is blocked by P:
$$
\boxed{J_{\rm return}=PQM QP=-PQ^2P=0.}
$$
Hence **the ideal reflected filter is dark and the target cannot be seen through it**. This is a double-pass polarization rejection effect, not unequal one-pass transmission from its two sides. Real mirrors at oblique incidence, imperfect retardance, finite <bandwidth> and depolarization can leave leakage.

= Solution
{parent=4}

A <Hertzian dipole> is an ideal localized oscillating source much smaller than its radiation <wavelength>. Its electric version is an infinitesimal current element; its magnetic version is a small current loop with moment $\mathbf m=IA\mathbf n$. Such elements model the far-field radiation and <radiation resistance> of electrically small antennas. The approximation requires source dimensions much smaller than $c/\omega$.

The supplied <magnetic field> has a quasistatic $[m]/r^3$ term, an <induction> $[\dot m]/(cr^2)$ term and a transverse radiation $[\ddot m]/(c^2r)$ term. The <electric field> has <induction> and radiation terms. Brackets mean evaluation at the <retarded time> $t_r=t-r/c$, not a time average. Near terms mainly exchange stored <energy>; only the $r^{-1}$ fields give a nonvanishing outgoing <power> integral as $r\to\infty$.

For a fixed dipole axis the radiation fields satisfy $E_\phi=-cB_\theta$. The radial Poynting <flux> is therefore
$$
S_r=\frac{cB_\theta^2}{\mu_0}
=\frac{\mu_0[\ddot m]^2}{16\pi^2c^3r^2}\sin^2\theta.
$$
Using $\int\sin^2\theta\,d\Omega=8\pi/3$ yields
$$
\boxed{P(t)=\frac{\mu_0[\ddot m]^2}{6\pi c^3}
=\frac{[\ddot m]^2}{6\pi\epsilon_0c^5}.}
$$
For a general changing dipole direction, replace the squared scalar <acceleration> by $|\ddot{\mathbf m}|^2$.

<Radiation resistance> is the equivalent input resistance whose mean <power> equals radiated <power>, not ohmic heating. For a fixed small loop carrying $I(t)=I_0\cos\omega t$, $m=AI$, so
$$
\overline P=\frac{A^2I_0^2\omega^4}{12\pi\epsilon_0c^5}
=\frac12I_0^2R_{\rm rad},\qquad
\boxed{R_{\rm rad}(\omega)=\frac{A^2\omega^4}{6\pi\epsilon_0c^5}.}
$$
This uses peak current $I_0$; with rms current the <power> is $I_{\rm rms}^2R_{\rm rad}$.

The satellite loop needs its changing orientation as well as its induced current. Put the spin axis along z, the external field along x, and let $\mathbf n(t)=(\cos\omega t,\sin\omega t,0)$. With $A=\pi b^2$ and resistance $R=10^{-4}\,\Omega$, the resistance-only approximation gives
$$
\Phi=BA\cos\omega t,\quad\mathcal E=BA\omega\sin\omega t,\quad
I(t)=\frac{BA\omega}{R}\sin\omega t.
$$
Write $m_0=BA^2\omega/R$. The full magnetic moment is
$$
\mathbf m(t)=m_0\sin\omega t\,\mathbf n(t)
=\frac{m_0}{2}\left[\sin(2\omega t)\widehat{\mathbf x}
+(1-\cos(2\omega t))\widehat{\mathbf y}\right].
$$
It has a static component and a rotating component at $2\omega$. Consequently $|\ddot{\mathbf m}|^2=4m_0^2\omega^4$, and
$$
\boxed{\overline P_{\rm loop}
=\frac{2B^2A^4\omega^6}{3\pi\epsilon_0c^5R^2}
=\frac{2\pi^3B^2b^8\omega^6}{3\epsilon_0c^5R^2}.}
$$
Holding the dipole axis fixed while inserting the sinusoidal current would instead give $B^2A^4\omega^6/(12\pi\epsilon_0c^5R^2)$, eight times smaller. That is the fixed-axis-loop result; it does not describe the stated rotating loop. This is <frequency doubling of an induction-driven rotating loop>; the changing vector direction is essential.

\Image[paper-1-oe2013-rotating-moment.png]
{title=Original calculated rotation-induced current and vector magnetic-moment components, illustrating the static plus twice-spin-frequency decomposition}

The resistance-only model is conditional: loop <inductance> is not supplied. If <self-inductance> L matters, solve $L\dot I+RI=BA\omega\sin\omega t$. In the <steady state> $I_0=BA\omega/\sqrt{R^2+\omega^2L^2}$. Its <phase> changes the static moment direction but not the twice-frequency component's magnitude, so the <power> becomes
$$
\boxed{\overline P_{\rm loop}(L)=
\frac{2B^2A^4\omega^6}{3\pi\epsilon_0c^5(R^2+\omega^2L^2)}.}
$$
The first expression is the requested resistive idealization and an upper estimate within this passive RL correction. Radiation reaction is negligible compared with the stipulated ohmic resistance. The radiated <energy> comes from <rotational kinetic energy>: the induced magnetic <torque> brakes the loop. Most lost rotational <energy> is <Joule heating>; a small part escapes as radiation. Maintaining a constant spin would require external mechanical <power>.

For scale only, take $B=50\,\mathrm{\mu T}$, a representative near-Earth field rather than a supplied orbital measurement. The terrestrial surface range reported by NOAA is roughly $25$–$65\,\mathrm{\mu T}$; the actual orbital value depends on position.<Geomagnetism Frequently Asked Questions by National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration>[What are the magnetic elements? Total field intensity range]{ref} With $b=1\,\mathrm m$ and $\omega=2\pi(500\,\mathrm{s^{-1}})$, the resistance-only model gives
$$
\boxed{\overline P\simeq2.3\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm W
\left(\frac{B}{50\,\mathrm{\mu T}}\right)^2,
\qquad f_{\rm rad}=1000\,\mathrm{Hz},\qquad\lambda_{\rm rad}\simeq300\,\mathrm{km}.}
$$
<Inductance> would reduce this <power>. The kilohertz signal is exceptionally weak and a metre-scale receiving antenna is electrically tiny. Ionospheric absorption/<reflection> and strong natural or instrumental low-frequency backgrounds make ordinary ground reception impractical. This is not a theorem that the <ionosphere> blocks every low-frequency <mode>: field-guided propagation and condition-dependent leakage are real, as the VIPER mission's measurements were designed to test.<NASA Rocket Mission Studying Escaping Radio Waves by National Aeronautics and Space Administration>[VIPER description: absorption/reflection layers and condition-dependent trans-ionospheric propagation]{ref}

At a separation $r=100\,\mathrm{km}$, $k_{\rm rad}r\simeq2.1$, so the observer is not securely in the far field. The radiation term alone has at most the characteristic magnetic <amplitude>
$$
B_{\rm rad}\sim\frac{\mu_0(2m_0\omega^2)}{4\pi c^2r}
\simeq3.4\times10^{-18}\,\mathrm T
\left(\frac{B}{50\,\mathrm{\mu T}}\right)
$$
in this optimistic resistance-only vacuum model. Near and <induction> fields must be retained for a full prediction; a simple far-field inverse-square <power> estimate is not an exact received signal here. Another satellite avoids the whole ground ionospheric path, but <plasma> propagation, receiving sensitivity, <bandwidth>, orientation, integration time and spacecraft <noise> remain unspecified. **Detection is not forbidden in principle, but routine practical detection is implausible at this scale; a definitive instrument-level yes/no does not follow from the supplied data.**
