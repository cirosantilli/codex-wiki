= Solution

The <electric field> of the reflected beam is perpendicular to the vertical <plane of incidence>, hence parallel to the water surface. At <Brewster's angle> the reflected <p-polarization> <amplitude> vanishes; only the <s-polarization> component remains. For incidence from air, approximated by <refractive index> one,
$$
\tan\theta_B=n_w=1.33,\qquad \theta_B=53.1^\circ.
$$
Incidence is measured from the surface normal, whereas solar elevation is measured from the horizontal. The <Brewster solar elevation> is therefore
$$
\boxed{h=90^\circ-\theta_B=\tan^{-1}(1/1.33)=36.9^\circ.}
$$
This assumes an ideally flat, transparent, nonmagnetic interface; ripples, absorption and diffuse light would spoil complete <polarization>.

For a <Hertzian dipole> of <length> $\ell$ driven at peak <electric current> $I_0$, the <electric dipole moment> <amplitude> is $p_0=I_0\ell/\omega$. Consequently
$$
\overline P=\frac{\omega^4p_0^2}{12\pi\epsilon_0c^3}
=\frac{I_0^2\ell^2\omega^2}{12\pi\epsilon_0c^3}.
$$
The <Hertzian dipole fixed-current power scaling> is thus $\overline P\propto I_0^2\ell^2/\lambda^2$, giving
$$
\boxed{\frac{P_1}{P_2}=\left(\frac{1}{0.5}\right)^2\left(\frac{25}{20}\right)^2=6.25.}
$$
The inverse-fourth-power <wavelength> scaling would hold at fixed <electric dipole moment>, not at the fixed <electric current> specified here.

During the time light crosses a <telescope> of <length> $L$, <Earth> moves sideways by approximately $vL/c$. The <telescope> must tilt towards <Earth>'s motion so that both ends intercept the ray. Thus the <annual stellar aberration amplitude> at a transverse sightline is $\delta\simeq v/c$.

Taking one orbital year as $365.25$ days and treating the orbit as circular,
$$
v=\frac{2\pi(1.5\times10^{11}\,\mathrm m)}{365.25(86400\,\mathrm s)}=2.99\times10^4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},
\qquad
\boxed{\delta\simeq9.96\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{rad}=20.5\text{ arcseconds}.}
$$
This is the instantaneous shift relative to a stationary heliocentric observer, not the doubled peak-to-peak annual excursion. The exact <Lorentz transformation> for a ray transverse in that frame gives $\sin\delta=v/c$, with the same first-order result.

An accelerating <point charge> has a transverse <radiation field> in addition to its near field. At nonrelativistic <speed> and large distance $R$,
$$
\mathbf E_{\rm rad}(\mathbf r,t)=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0c^2R}
\widehat{\mathbf n}\times[\widehat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf a(t-R/c)],\qquad
\mathbf B_{\rm rad}=\frac1c\widehat{\mathbf n}\times\mathbf E_{\rm rad}.
$$
The <acceleration> is evaluated at <retarded time>. <Radiation fields> decay as $1/R$, so their outward <energy> <flux> integrated over a <sphere> is independent of <radius>; the near <electric field> instead decays as $1/R^2$. The radiation vanishes along the <acceleration> and is strongest perpendicular to it.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume I by Richard Feynman and others>[§28–2, Eqs. (28.5)–(28.6): transverse acceleration field and inverse-distance radiation]{ref}

Using the <Poynting vector> gives
$$
\frac{dP}{d\Omega}=\frac{q^2a^2\sin^2\vartheta}{16\pi^2\epsilon_0c^3},\qquad
\boxed{P=\frac{q^2a^2}{6\pi\epsilon_0c^3}.}
$$
The integral uses $\int\sin^2\vartheta\,d\Omega=8\pi/3$. This is the <Larmor formula>. A harmonically accelerated charge with peak <acceleration> $a_0$ has mean <power> $q^2a_0^2/(12\pi\epsilon_0c^3)$; external work supplies this radiated <energy> in steady operation. Uniform inertial motion does not radiate.

\Image[paper-1-oe2012-charge-radiation.png]
{title=Original nonrelativistic radiation pattern, with zero power along the acceleration and maximum power transverse to it}

At relativistic <speed> the angular pattern becomes directionally concentrated and retarded-source factors matter. The total instantaneous radiated <power> generalizes to $q^2\gamma^6[a^2-|\boldsymbol\beta\times\mathbf a|^2]/(6\pi\epsilon_0c^3)$, with $\boldsymbol\beta=\mathbf v/c$. The displayed simple $\sin^2\vartheta$ pattern is restricted to the <nonrelativistic limit>.

= Solution

<Temporal coherence> measures <correlation> between an optical field and a delayed copy. For a stationary scalar complex field with carrier convention $e^{-i\omega t}$,
$$
\Gamma(\tau)=\langle E^*(t)E(t+\tau)\rangle,\qquad
g^{(1)}(\tau)=\frac{\Gamma(\tau)}{\Gamma(0)}.
$$
The <power spectrum> $S(\omega)\ge0$ and the field <autocorrelation> form a <Fourier transform> pair, the <Wiener-Khinchin theorem>:
$$
g^{(1)}(\tau)=\frac{\int S(\omega)e^{-i\omega\tau}\,d\omega}{\int S(\omega)\,d\omega}.
$$
A <Michelson interferometer> supplies delay $\tau=\Delta L/c$. With equal arm <intensities> and matched <polarization>, its <interferometric visibility> is $|g^{(1)}(\tau)|$; unequal <intensities> or mode mismatch lower visibility independently of <temporal coherence>. The relevant spectrum is the optical field spectrum, not the low-frequency spectrum of detector <intensity> fluctuations.

For a single Lorentzian optical line of angular-frequency <full width at half maximum> $\Delta\omega$, use the narrow-line detuning model
$$
\frac{S(\omega)}{\int S\,d\omega}=\frac{\Delta\omega/(2\pi)}{(\omega-\omega_0)^2+(\Delta\omega/2)^2},\qquad
\boxed{g^{(1)}(\tau)=e^{-i\omega_0\tau}e^{-\Delta\omega|\tau|/2}.}
$$
Such a model describes a phase-diffusing monochromatic source. An $e^{-1}$ <correlation> time is $2/\Delta\omega$, and its <coherence length> is $2c/\Delta\omega$. The Lorentzian extension over all detunings approximates a narrow optical line with $\Delta\omega\ll\omega_0$.

For two equal, mutually incoherent ideal monochromatic spectral lines,
$$
S\propto\delta(\omega-\omega_1)+\delta(\omega-\omega_2),\qquad
\boxed{g^{(1)}(\tau)=e^{-i\bar\omega\tau}\cos(\Delta\omega_{12}\tau/2).}
$$
Here $\bar\omega=(\omega_1+\omega_2)/2$ and $\Delta\omega_{12}=\omega_2-\omega_1$. Visibility has zeros and revivals, so the overall spectral span alone does not determine a unique decay time. Giving each line a common finite Lorentzian linewidth multiplies this beating <correlation> by the corresponding exponential envelope.

\Image[paper-1-oe2012-coherence-spectra.png]
{title=Original spectrum–coherence examples: a Lorentzian line and a doublet with visibility revivals}

These two examples distinguish irreversible envelope decay from cancellation and revival of discrete spectral components. The line profiles are explicit models, not claimed measurements of a particular source.

= Solution
{parent=c}

The <electric potential> and <magnetic vector potential> represent the fields through $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$ and $\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi-\partial_t\mathbf A$. They are not unique: a differentiable function $\chi$ gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf A\mapsto\mathbf A+\nabla\chi,\qquad
\phi\mapsto\phi-\partial_t\chi,}
$$
without changing either field. <Gauge fixing> restricts this freedom to suit the sources, boundaries or symmetry; it does not alter observables.

The <Lorenz gauge> $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A+c^{-2}\partial_t\phi=0$ is <Lorentz covariant>. <Maxwell's equations> then reduce to sourced <wave equations>,
$$
(\nabla^2-c^{-2}\partial_t^2)\phi=-\rho/\epsilon_0,\qquad
(\nabla^2-c^{-2}\partial_t^2)\mathbf A=-\mu_0\mathbf J.
$$
Retarded solutions implement causal radiation conditions. This gauge is useful for radiation and relativistic calculations; residual transformations obey the homogeneous <wave equation>.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume II by Richard Feynman and others>[§18–6, Eqs. (18.23)–(18.25): gauge choice and potential wave equations]{ref}

The <Coulomb gauge> $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$ instead gives $\nabla^2\phi=-\rho/\epsilon_0$. Its <electric potential> is an instantaneous Poisson solution, but the accompanying <magnetic vector potential> makes the full physical <electric field> causal. It is useful in separating longitudinal <electric fields> from transverse radiation. In a source-free <vacuum> region, suitable radiation <boundary conditions> permit <radiation gauge> $\phi=0$, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$, leaving two transverse plane-wave <polarizations>. These extra conditions are not a gauge choice applicable unchanged to arbitrary nonzero charge distributions.

**Different gauges redistribute information between potentials; the same boundary-value problem gives the same <electric fields> and <magnetic fields>.**

Use $\mathbf E(z,t)=\operatorname{Re}[\mathbf J e^{i(kz-\omega t)}]$. A <Jones vector> $\mathbf J=(J_x,J_y)^{\mathsf T}$ records the two transverse <complex amplitudes>. The <amplitude> ratio and relative <phase> determine the <polarization ellipse>; a common nonzero scalar does not change the <polarization>. A fixed <Jones vector> describes fully polarized monochromatic light, not an unpolarized statistical mixture.

<Linear birefringence> gives <orthogonal> principal <polarizations> different <refractive indices>. Put $n_y-n_x=\Delta n>0$, so $x$ is the fast axis. A plate of thickness $d$ gives slow-minus-fast <phase retardation>
$$
\delta(\omega)=\frac{\omega d\Delta n(\omega)}c.
$$
The thinnest <quarter-wave plate> has
$$
\boxed{d=\frac{\pi c}{2\omega_0\Delta n(\omega_0)}=\frac{\lambda_0}{4\Delta n(\omega_0)}.}
$$
Here $\lambda_0=2\pi c/\omega_0$ is the <vacuum> <wavelength>. For linear input at angle $\theta$ to the fast axis, the output at design <frequency> is $(\cos\theta,i\sin\theta)^{\mathsf T}$ up to common <phase>. Axis-aligned input remains linear; equal nonzero components at $45^\circ$ modulo $90^\circ$ give <circular polarization>; other input angles give <elliptical polarization>. There is no need to spatially separate the two components.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume I by Richard Feynman and others>[§33–3: phase accumulation between orthogonal principal polarizations and quarter-wave conversion]{ref}

For <waveplate retardance detuning>, define
$$
\epsilon=\frac{\omega d\Delta n(\omega)}c-\frac\pi2
\simeq\frac dc[\Delta n_0+\omega_0\Delta n'_0](\omega-\omega_0).
$$
Neglecting material dispersion reduces this to $\epsilon=(\pi/2)(\omega-\omega_0)/\omega_0$. Remove the common fast-axis <phase>. Then $Q=\operatorname{diag}(1,ie^{i\epsilon})$. A similar <half-wave plate> of twice the thickness has twice the retardation, giving $H=\operatorname{diag}(1,-e^{2i\epsilon})$. The shared error parameter presumes the same material and matched thickness ratio.

For the <half-wave and quarter-wave achromatic circularizer>, the first fast-axis angle is $\alpha=15^\circ$ and the second is $\gamma=\alpha+\beta=75^\circ$. With the stated passive rotation convention,
$$
\mathbf J_{\rm out}=R(-\gamma)Q R(\gamma)R(-\alpha)H R(\alpha)
\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
An explicit expression valid for the given shared error is obtained by putting $z=e^{i\epsilon}$ and defining
$$
v_x=\cos^2\alpha-z^2\sin^2\alpha,\qquad
v_y=\sin\alpha\cos\alpha(1+z^2),
$$
$$
\mathbf J_{\rm out}=\begin{pmatrix}
(\cos^2\gamma+iz\sin^2\gamma)v_x+(1-iz)\sin\gamma\cos\gamma\,v_y\\
(1-iz)\sin\gamma\cos\gamma\,v_x+(\sin^2\gamma+iz\cos^2\gamma)v_y
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
At $\epsilon=0$, the <half-wave plate> rotates the linear input to $30^\circ$, which is $-45^\circ$ relative to the second fast axis. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbf J_0=\frac{e^{i75^\circ}}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-i\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
This is <circular polarization>. With our time convention its fixed-point electric vector is proportional, after a time-origin shift, to $(\cos\omega t,-\sin\omega t)$; this specifies the rotation without relying on conflicting handedness conventions.

Expanding the exact product to first order gives
$$
\mathbf J_{\rm out}=\begin{pmatrix}0.183+0.683i\\0.683-0.183i\end{pmatrix}
+\epsilon\begin{pmatrix}-0.433+0.116i\\0.116+0.433i\end{pmatrix}+O(\epsilon^2).
$$
These coefficients are rounded to three significant figures. Before rounding, the derivative is exactly $\mathbf J'_0=i\kappa\mathbf J_0$, with $\kappa=(3-\sqrt3)/2$. The <Jones-component ratio sensitivity> therefore vanishes:
$$
\boxed{\frac{J_y}{J_x}=\frac{J_{0y}(1+i\kappa\epsilon)}{J_{0x}(1+i\kappa\epsilon)}+O(\epsilon^2)=-i+O(\epsilon^2).}
$$
Only common <phase> changes at first order. This proves compensation for the prescribed input near the design <frequency>, not exact achromatic performance throughout the visible spectrum for arbitrary input <polarization>.

A transverse static <electric field> can induce <linear birefringence> through the <electro-optic Kerr effect>. Define its coefficient by $\Delta n=\lambda_0 K E_{\rm dc}^2$, with the <principal axes> parallel and perpendicular to the applied field. A <length> $d_K$ then gives $\delta_K=2\pi Kd_KE_{\rm dc}^2$.

For input polarized at $45^\circ$ to those axes, switching from zero retardation to $|\delta_K|=\pi/2$ switches linear to <circular polarization>. Other input angles generally become elliptical, and axis-aligned input stays linear. The required field magnitude for the lowest-order setting is
$$
\boxed{|E_{\rm dc}|=\frac1{\sqrt{4|K|d_K}}.}
$$
**A Kerr cell is suitable as a switchable <quarter-wave plate>.** The field can be changed electronically without mechanically rotating the axes; actual switching time depends on the material response and driving circuit. This is induced <linear birefringence>, not polarization-selective absorption.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume I by Richard Feynman and others>[§33–3, final paragraphs: an applied electric field produces birefringence and electrical light switching in a Kerr cell]{ref}

The longitudinal <Faraday effect> produces <circular birefringence>: right- and left-circular components have different <phase velocities>. Linear input contains equal <amplitudes> of both circular eigenpolarizations. A relative <phase> $\delta_F$ rotates its <linear polarization> through $\delta_F/2$, preserving linearity in an ideal transparent sample. Conventionally the signed rotation is $\vartheta_F=VB_\parallel d_F$, with <Verdet constant> $V$.

**A pure Faraday rotator cannot serve as the required switchable <quarter-wave plate>.** It rotates a line into another line rather than producing the required relative <phase> between fixed <orthogonal> linear components. Applying a field that gives a nominal quarter-cycle circular-eigenmode retardation simply rotates <linear polarization> by $45^\circ$. Circular dichroism or a separate linear retarder would change the device, and is not the stated pure Faraday mechanism.

= Solution

Take $x^\mu=(ct,x,y,z)$ and <metric signature> $(+,-,-,-)$. The <four-current>, <four-potential> and <four-gradient> are
$$
J^\mu=(c\rho,\mathbf j),\qquad
A^\mu=(\phi/c,\mathbf A),\qquad
\partial_\mu=(c^{-1}\partial_t,\nabla),\qquad
\partial^\mu=(c^{-1}\partial_t,-\nabla).
$$
The first two transform as contravariant <four-vectors> under an inertial-frame <Lorentz transformation>, within a consistently transformed electromagnetic gauge. The lowered-index <gradient> is a <covector>. Their contraction is a <Lorentz scalar>:
$$
\partial_\mu J^\mu=\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\mathbf j=0.
$$
Indeed, $J'^\mu=\Lambda^\mu{}_{\nu}J^\nu$ and $\partial'_\mu=(\Lambda^{-1})^\alpha{}_{\mu}\partial_\alpha$, so the two matrices cancel in the contraction. **Local <charge conservation> in one <inertial frame> implies local <charge conservation> in every <inertial frame>.** Integrating over a region gives the charge change equal to minus its outward current <flux>; global conserved charge requires no <flux> through the system boundary.

= Solution

Let $\widehat{\mathbf r}$ point transversely away from the line and define $\widehat{\boldsymbol\varphi}=\widehat{\mathbf x}\times\widehat{\mathbf r}$. Cylindrical <Gauss's law> in the line's <rest frame> gives
$$
\mathbf E'=\frac{\lambda'}{2\pi\epsilon_0r'}\widehat{\mathbf r},\qquad \mathbf B'=0.
$$
The transverse distance is unchanged by the axial boost: $r=r'$. Applying the inverse of the given field transformation to a <boosted uniform line charge>,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E=\frac{\gamma\lambda'}{2\pi\epsilon_0r}\widehat{\mathbf r},\qquad
\mathbf B=\frac{\mathbf u\times\mathbf E}{c^2}
=\frac{\mu_0\gamma\lambda'u}{2\pi r}\widehat{\boldsymbol\varphi}.}
$$
Here $\gamma=(1-u^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$ and all longitudinal field components vanish. Substituting back verifies $\mathbf B'=\gamma(\mathbf B-\mathbf u\times\mathbf E/c^2)=0$ and $\mathbf E'=\gamma(1-u^2/c^2)\mathbf E=\mathbf E/\gamma$. The sign of $\lambda'$ determines the radial and circulation directions.

= Solution

A material segment of the line with rest <length> $dx'$ has laboratory <length> $dx=dx'/\gamma$, while its charge $dQ$ is unchanged. Thus
$$
\boxed{\lambda=\gamma\lambda',\qquad I=\lambda u=\gamma\lambda'u.}
$$
Equivalently the line-integrated <four-current> transforms from $(c\lambda',0)$ to $(\gamma c\lambda',\gamma u\lambda')$. Gauss's and <Ampère's laws> in $S$ now give $E_r=\lambda/(2\pi\epsilon_0r)$ and $B_\varphi=\mu_0I/(2\pi r)$, exactly the fields found in part (a). <Charge density> is frame-dependent even though the charge of the same set of particles is invariant.

= Solution
{parent=c}

In $S$, adding the stationary opposite line cancels <charge density> and <electric field> but contributes no current. Hence
$$
\lambda_{\rm tot}=0,\qquad I_{\rm tot}=\lambda u,\qquad
\mathbf E_{\rm tot}=0,\qquad
\mathbf B_{\rm tot}=\frac{\mu_0\lambda u}{2\pi r}\widehat{\boldsymbol\varphi}.
$$
The <neutral current filament in a boosted frame> is not neutral. Transform its line-integrated <four-current>:
$$
\boxed{\lambda'_{\rm tot}=-\frac{\gamma u I_{\rm tot}}{c^2}=-\gamma\lambda\frac{u^2}{c^2},\qquad
I'_{\rm tot}=\gamma I_{\rm tot}=\gamma\lambda u.}
$$
The same result follows by adding the original resting line $(\lambda',0)$ to the new negative line, whose primed <density> and current are $(-\gamma\lambda,+\gamma\lambda u)$. In particular $\lambda'-\gamma\lambda=-\gamma\lambda u^2/c^2$ because $\lambda=\gamma\lambda'$.

Transforming the total fields gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E'_{\rm tot}=-\frac{\gamma\lambda u^2}{2\pi\epsilon_0c^2r}\widehat{\mathbf r},\qquad
\mathbf B'_{\rm tot}=\frac{\mu_0\gamma\lambda u}{2\pi r}\widehat{\boldsymbol\varphi}.}
$$
For positive $\lambda$, $\mathbf u\times\mathbf B$ points inward, explaining the electric-field sign. The primed Gauss and Ampère fields calculated from $\lambda'_{\rm tot}$ and $I'_{\rm tot}$ reproduce these expressions. Both a moving charge distribution and a moving current must be transformed; boosting just one constituent would give an inconsistent total.

= Solution
{parent=d}

An ideal current-carrying wire consists of opposite charge populations, one associated with the stationary lattice and one with drifting carriers. They may cancel in the lattice frame while carrying a net current, as in part (c), up to exchanging the charge signs and current direction. In a carrier-rest description their <densities> no longer cancel because the two populations undergo different <length contractions>. The wire then has both <electric fields> and <magnetic fields>.

**A neutral steady current in one frame generally becomes a charged steady current in another.** This illustrates how <electric fields> and <magnetic fields> are parts of one relativistic field rather than separately invariant <forces>. Real resistive wires also have a driving longitudinal <electric field> and geometry-dependent surface charges; the infinite uniform filament model isolates the transverse fields of a neutral current and does not describe every field of a complete circuit.
