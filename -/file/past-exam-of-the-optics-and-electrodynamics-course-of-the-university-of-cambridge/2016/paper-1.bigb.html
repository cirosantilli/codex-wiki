Interpreting the cone as **<Cherenkov radiation>**, its angle gives the speed, not directly the <mass>:
$$
\cos\theta=\frac{c}{nv}=\frac1{n\beta},\qquad\gamma=\left(1-\frac1{n^2\cos^2\theta}\right)^{-1/2}.
$$
Combining this with the relativistic <kinetic energy> $T=(\gamma-1)mc^2$ gives the <Cherenkov mass inference>
$$
\boxed{m=\frac{T}{c^2\left[\left(1-\frac1{n^2\cos^2\theta}\right)^{-1/2}-1\right]}.}
$$
The material must be transparent at the observed <frequency>, $n$ is its <phase refractive index> there, and $n\cos\theta>1$. For $T>0$ this requires $0<\theta<\arccos(1/n)$ for a massive particle above threshold. Radiation of an unspecified other origin would not determine the <mass> from these data alone.

**The transverse <spatial coherence> width is much larger than the longitudinal <coherence length>.** The longitudinal scale is controlled by spectral <bandwidth>:
$$
\ell_c\sim\frac{c}{\Delta\nu}\sim\frac{\lambda^2}{\Delta\lambda}.
$$
For broad, unfiltered visible sunlight it is of order a <wavelength> to a few wavelengths, roughly $10^{-6}\,\mathrm m$. This is an order-of-magnitude scale, not a uniquely defined solar constant; the precise value depends on spectral response and the coherence-width convention.

The <Sun> subtends angular diameter $\delta\simeq1919''\simeq9.3\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{rad}$ at one astronomical unit.<Sun Fact Sheet by National Aeronautics and Space Administration>[Sun Observational Parameters: apparent diameter at 1 A.U.]{ref} Consequently the <sunlight coherence comparison> gives a transverse scale
$$
\boxed{w_c\sim\frac{\lambda}{\delta}\sim 6\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm m\gg\ell_c,}
$$
using $\lambda=550\,\mathrm{nm}$ only as an illustrative visible <wavelength>. The <uniform-disk spatial coherence> model has first coherence zero at $1.22\lambda/\delta$, about $72\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ at that <wavelength>. The small angular size permits coherence across tens of micrometres, while the broad spectrum destroys coherence over much shorter path differences. Spectral filtering increases $\ell_c$; spatial filtering changes the transverse scale.<Interference and Coherence by Sander Konijnenberg, Aurèle J.L. Adam and H. Paul Urbach>[Coherence of Light Sources; More on Spatial Coherence]{ref}

Write $C=e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)=mc^2r_0$. For a slowly evolving circular <Coulomb orbit>, force balance and the mechanical energy give
$$
v^2=\frac{C}{mr}=\frac{c^2r_0}{r},\qquad |\boldsymbol\alpha|=\frac{C}{mr^2},\qquad E=-\frac{C}{2r}.
$$
The supplied <Larmor formula> therefore yields
$$
P=\frac{e^2|\boldsymbol\alpha|^2}{6\pi\epsilon_0c^3}=\frac{2mc^3r_0^3}{3r^4}.
$$
Set $dE/dt=-P$. Since $dE/dt=C\dot r/(2r^2)$,
$$
\dot r=-\frac{4cr_0^2}{3r^2},\qquad\frac{d(r^3)}{dt}=-4cr_0^2.
$$
Thus the <classical radiation collapse of a Coulomb orbit> obeys
$$
\boxed{r(t)^3=a_0^3-4r_0^2ct.}
$$
The decreasing mechanical energy means a more tightly bound orbit, not a smaller speed. The formal collapse time is $a_0^3/(4r_0^2c)$. The nonrelativistic, small-loss-per-orbit approximation eventually fails; the result demonstrates the classical instability rather than describing an actual <hydrogen atom>.

**<Birefringence> is polarization-dependent propagation in an optically anisotropic medium.** Its <permittivity tensor> relates $\mathbf D$ to $\mathbf E$. In a lossless uniaxial <dielectric>, take the <optic axis> along $z$ and the principal relative permittivities as $(n_o^2,n_o^2,n_e^2)$. For a <wave vector> making angle $\vartheta$ with that axis, the ordinary mode has index $n_o$, while the <extraordinary phase-index angular dependence> is
$$
\boxed{\frac1{n_{\rm ex}(\vartheta)^2}=\frac{\cos^2\vartheta}{n_o^2}+\frac{\sin^2\vartheta}{n_e^2}.}
$$
The ordinary <electric field> is perpendicular to the plane containing the <wave vector> and <optic axis>. The extraordinary <electric displacement field> lies in that plane and is perpendicular to the <wave vector>, but its <electric field> need not be. Its energy-flow direction can therefore differ from the wave-normal direction: <birefringent walk-off>. Along the <optic axis> the two indices coincide. <Refraction> generally splits an incident beam into two differently polarized rays, producing double images, as in calcite.

\Image[paper-1-birefringent-indices.png]
{title=Illustrative positive-uniaxial ordinary and extraordinary phase indices coincide along the optic axis}
{source=paper-1-birefringent-indices.py}

A <waveplate> instead uses the relative phase of two eigenpolarizations. With the axes chosen for the propagation direction, the <retardance> is $\Delta=2\pi L(n_2-n_1)/\lambda_0$. A <quarter-wave plate> gives $|\Delta|=\pi/2$ modulo $2\pi$ and converts equal-amplitude orthogonal components between linear and <circular polarization>. A <half-wave plate> gives $|\Delta|=\pi$ and rotates a <linear polarization>. This is a phase effect; unequal absorption is a separate property.<Polarisation by Sander Konijnenberg, Aurèle J.L. Adam and H. Paul Urbach>[Creating Different Polarisation States; wave plates and relative phase]{ref}

**<Synchrotron radiation> is radiation from relativistic charges undergoing transverse <acceleration>**, for example electrons bent by a <magnetic field>. <Relativistic aberration> concentrates the radiation within an angle of order $1/\gamma$ about the instantaneous velocity. An observer sees short pulses as this cone sweeps past; <synchrotron pulse compression> produces a broad spectrum extending far above the orbital <frequency>.

For an ultrarelativistic circular trajectory of curvature radius $\rho$, the characteristic scales are
$$
\boxed{\omega_c\simeq\frac{3\gamma^3c}{2\rho},\qquad\Delta t_{\rm pulse}\sim\frac{\rho}{\gamma^3c}.}
$$
<Synchrotron Radiation in the Geant4 Physics Reference Manual by Geant4 Collaboration>[Critical energy and critical mode number]{ref} The <synchrotron critical frequency> sets the spectral turnover; it is not a sharp upper cutoff. The transverse-acceleration <Liénard formula> gives
$$
P=\frac{q^2\gamma^4v^4}{6\pi\epsilon_0c^3\rho^2}\simeq\frac{q^2c\gamma^4}{6\pi\epsilon_0\rho^2}.
$$
The strong energy dependence is a major loss mechanism in electron storage rings. Radiation observed in the orbital plane is linearly polarized in that plane; away from it the two components generally produce <elliptical polarization>, with opposite handedness on opposite sides. Accelerator light sources exploit its brightness, broad tunable spectrum and pulsed timing. At low speeds the relativistic beaming and high-frequency extension disappear, approaching ordinary cyclotron radiation.<Synchrotron Radiation by Richard Fitzpatrick>[Relativistic radiation cone, pulse compression and synchrotron spectrum]{ref}

**A <photonic crystal> has a spatially periodic <dielectric> response on the scale of the optical <wavelength>.** For a nonmagnetic, lossless, nondispersive <dielectric>, the source-free <Maxwell equations> can be written as the eigenproblem
$$
\nabla\times\left[\epsilon_r(\mathbf r)^{-1}\nabla\times\mathbf H\right]=\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\mathbf H.
$$
The <Bloch theorem> gives $\mathbf H_{n\mathbf k}=e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}\mathbf u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)$ with periodic $\mathbf u$. The allowed frequencies form <photonic band structures>. Coupling of counterpropagating waves near a <Brillouin zone> boundary opens a stop band. A one-dimensional <dielectric> multilayer is a simple example; two-dimensional hole arrays and three-dimensional <dielectric> networks allow more extensive control.

\Image[paper-1-photonic-bands.png]
{title=An exactly calculated one-dimensional dielectric bilayer has allowed bands separated by a stop band}
{source=paper-1-photonic-bands.py}

A **complete <photonic band gap>** excludes bulk propagating modes for every direction and <polarization> in a <frequency> interval. Periodicity alone does not guarantee such a gap: dimensionality, symmetry and refractive-index contrast matter. A missing or altered element can support a localized defect state in the gap, making a cavity; a line defect can guide light. Finite crystals have leakage and wavelength-dependent transmission rather than an infinitely sharp exclusion. Applications include selective mirrors, optical filters, small resonators and integrated waveguides.<Mathematical Methods in Nanophotonics, MIT 18.369, Spring 2016 by Steven G. Johnson>[Lecture 2: Maxwell eigenproblem; lectures 9–14: Bloch states, one-dimensional gaps and defect localization]{ref}

= Solution

The <magnetic vector potential> is defined by $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$. The <gauge transformation>
$$
\boxed{\mathbf A\mapsto\mathbf A+\nabla\chi,\qquad\phi\mapsto\phi-\partial_t\chi}
$$
leaves both $\mathbf B$ and $\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi-\partial_t\mathbf A$ unchanged. In <magnetostatics> choose the <Coulomb gauge> $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$. Then <Ampère's law> and the curl-of-curl identity give
$$
\mu_0\mathbf J=\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf A)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A)-\nabla^2\mathbf A,\qquad\boxed{\nabla^2\mathbf A=-\mu_0\mathbf J.}
$$
The free-space <Green function> identity $\nabla^2|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|^{-1}=-4\pi\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf A(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\mathbf J(\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}\,d^3r'.}
$$
The <boundary condition> selects the homogeneous solution; the usual decay condition assumes suitably localized sources. An infinitely long static wire needs a reference for its logarithmically divergent potential, although its <magnetic field> is finite away from the wire.

For time-dependent sources use the **<Lorenz gauge>**, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A+c^{-2}\partial_t\phi=0$, and the outgoing <retarded potential>
$$
\boxed{\mathbf A(\mathbf r,t)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\mathbf J(\mathbf r',t-|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|/c)}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}\,d^3r'.}
$$
The <scalar potential> has the analogous retarded charge-density integral. The two gauges should not be conflated for a general time-dependent source.<Retarded Potentials by Richard Fitzpatrick>[Equations (73)–(76): Lorenz-gauge retarded scalar and vector potentials]{ref}

For the prescribed uncharged wire, $\rho=0$ and $\phi=0$ is consistent. Translation invariance makes $\mathbf A=A_z(r,t)\hat{\mathbf z}$ with $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$, so this particular solution satisfies both gauge conditions. With $s=z'-z$,
$$
A_z=\frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\Theta(t-\sqrt{r^2+s^2}/c)}{\sqrt{r^2+s^2}}\,ds.
$$
It is zero for $t<r/c$. For $t>r/c$, set $L=\sqrt{c^2t^2-r^2}$. The causal interval is $-L<s<L$, giving the <switched infinite-wire retarded potential>
$$
\boxed{A_z=\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi}\operatorname{arsinh}\frac Lr=\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi}\operatorname{arcosh}\frac{ct}{r}=\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi}\ln\!\left(\frac{ct+\sqrt{c^2t^2-r^2}}r\right).}
$$
Differentiation gives the fields and <Poynting vector>, for $r>0$, $t>r/c$:
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\mathbf E&=-\frac{\mu_0Ic}{2\pi\sqrt{c^2t^2-r^2}}\hat{\mathbf z},\\
\mathbf H&=\frac{Ict}{2\pi r\sqrt{c^2t^2-r^2}}\hat{\boldsymbol\phi},\\
\mathbf N=\mathbf E\times\mathbf H&=\frac{\mu_0I^2c^2t}{4\pi^2r(c^2t^2-r^2)}\hat{\mathbf r}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Here $\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf H$ and $B_\phi=-\partial_rA_z$. All three fields vanish before arrival. For positive $I$, $\mathbf E$ opposes the imposed current and the <radial energy flow after switching a line current> points **radially outward**: $(-\hat{\mathbf z})\times\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}=\hat{\mathbf r}$.

\Image[paper-1-switched-wire.png]
{title=At fixed radius the causal fields start at t equals r over c and relax toward the magnetostatic limit}
{source=paper-1-switched-wire.py}

For the graph, let $u=ct/r$. Normalized to $E_*=\mu_0Ic/(2\pi r)$, $H_*=I/(2\pi r)$ and $N_*=\mu_0I^2c/(4\pi^2r^2)$, the curves are
$$
E_z/E_*=-\frac1{\sqrt{u^2-1}},\qquad H_\phi/H_*=\frac u{\sqrt{u^2-1}},\qquad N_r/N_*=\frac u{u^2-1}\quad(u>1).
$$
At the <wavefront>, $|E_z|$ and $H_\phi$ diverge as $(u-1)^{-1/2}$ and $N_r$ as $(u-1)^{-1}$. This <abrupt line-current wavefront singularity> reflects the ideal instantaneous turn-on of a line current; a finite rise time smooths the front. At late times,
$$
E_z\sim-\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi t}\to0,\qquad H_\phi\to\frac I{2\pi r},\qquad N_r\sim\frac{\mu_0I^2}{4\pi^2rt}\to0.
$$
The vector potential grows logarithmically with time in this infinite-wire idealization even as the local <magnetic field> approaches its static limit. The divergent front should not be interpreted as a finite-energy pulse from a real instantaneous switching experiment.

**Spherical-particle <frequency> scaling.** For a <dielectric> sphere of radius $a\ll\lambda$, the quasistatic <electric polarizability> is
$$
\alpha_s=4\pi\epsilon_0a^3\frac{\epsilon_r-1}{\epsilon_r+2},\qquad\mathbf p(t)=\operatorname{Re}(\alpha_s\mathbf E_0e^{-i\omega t}).
$$
The <electric dipole radiation> formula and $I_0=\epsilon_0c|E_0|^2/2$ give
$$
\langle P\rangle=\frac{\omega^4|\alpha_s|^2|E_0|^2}{12\pi\epsilon_0c^3},\qquad\boxed{\sigma=\frac{\langle P\rangle}{I_0}=\frac{8\pi}{3}k^4a^6\left|\frac{\epsilon_r-1}{\epsilon_r+2}\right|^2.}
$$
Thus **<Rayleigh scattering> scales as $\omega^4$, or $\lambda^{-4}$**, when the polarizability varies slowly with <frequency>. Material resonances and absorption can change this simple scaling. For the incident $z$ <polarization>, the dipole is along $z$: intensity is proportional to $\sin^2\chi$, where $\chi$ is the angle from $z$. There is no radiation on the dipole axis and a maximum in the $xy$ plane. In a direction $\hat{\mathbf n}$ the scattered <electric field> is along $\hat{\mathbf z}-(\hat{\mathbf z}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n})\hat{\mathbf n}$, hence linearly polarized wherever nonzero.<Rayleigh Scattering by Richard Fitzpatrick>[Small-particle dipole response and inverse-fourth-power wavelength dependence]{ref}

\Image[paper-1-rayleigh-pattern.png]
{title=A z-directed electric dipole radiates most strongly perpendicular to its axis and has axial zeros}
{source=paper-1-rayleigh-pattern.py}

**Needle model used in parts (a)–(d).** Let its axis be $\mathbf u=(u_x,u_y,u_z)=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta)$. A very slender, high-permittivity needle has dominant longitudinal response, so use the <rank-one needle polarizability> $\boldsymbol\alpha\simeq\alpha_\parallel\mathbf u\mathbf u^{\mathsf T}$. Its transverse response is neglected at leading order. For observation along $y$, define $\mathbf v=u_x\hat{\mathbf x}+u_z\hat{\mathbf z}$, $s=|\mathbf v|^2=1-u_y^2$ and
$$
K=\frac{k^4|\alpha_\parallel|^2}{16\pi^2\epsilon_0^2R^2}.
$$
For a unit incident <Jones vector> $\mathbf e$, the common far-field relation is $\mathbf E_s\propto\mathbf v(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf e)$, and $I_s=KI_0s|\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf e|^2$. Here $I_s$ is irradiance at distance $R$; multiplying by $R^2$ gives power per solid angle. No absolute value of $\alpha_\parallel$ is given or needed for the requested relative intensities.

**Diffuse random cloud, with the incident <polarization> of (a).** The single-particle amplitudes through $x$ and $z$ analysers are proportional to $u_xu_z$ and $u_z^2$. The <isotropic orientation fourth moment> gives the <needle-scattering analyzer moments>
$$
\langle u_x^2u_z^2\rangle=\frac1{15},\qquad\langle u_z^4\rangle=\frac15.
$$
For example, $\langle u_z^4\rangle=\tfrac12\int_{-1}^{1}\mu^4d\mu=1/5$; symmetry and $u_x^2+u_y^2+u_z^2=1$ give $2\langle u_x^2u_z^2\rangle=1/3-1/5$. Since the particles scatter mutually incoherently, intensities add. For $N$ illuminated particles,
$$
\boxed{I_x=\frac{NKI_0}{15},\qquad I_z=\frac{NKI_0}{5},\qquad I_x:I_z=1:3.}
$$
The cross correlation vanishes by reflection symmetry. The degree of <linear polarization> is $(I_z-I_x)/(I_z+I_x)=\boxed{1/2}$: random anisotropic scatterers produce partially depolarized light, even though every individual needle produces a single <linear polarization>.

\Image[paper-1-needle-ensemble.png]
{title=Independent isotropically oriented needles give the analyser ratio one to three, while coherent mean amplitudes have no x component}
{source=paper-1-needle-ensemble.py}

**Continuous homogeneous <dielectric>.** One must sum amplitudes coherently and include their spatial phases, rather than retain the preceding sum of intensities. Random orientations give $\langle\mathbf u\mathbf u^{\mathsf T}\rangle=\mathbf I/3$, so the averaged response is isotropic and its induced <polarization> is along $z$. Its coherent radiation toward $y$, when allowed by the sample geometry, is therefore **purely $z$ polarized, with $I_x=0$**. The independent-needle prefactor is no longer a quantitative dense-medium model; local-field and collective effects determine the effective <permittivity>.

There is also <coherent continuum phase matching>: a uniform bulk driven by a <plane wave> has no arbitrary outgoing $90^\circ$ plane-wave component. In an ideal infinite homogeneous medium there is no diffuse side scattering at all; propagation, <refraction> and reflection are determined by the medium and its interfaces. A finite sample's coherent side signal depends on its shape and boundaries. Thus the old $1:3$ diffuse ratio is replaced by a coherent isotropic-medium response, not by a universal nonzero $N^2$ side-scattering intensity.

Using the needle definitions in the question-level solution, $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf e=u_z=\cos\theta$. The scattered field is parallel to $\mathbf v=u_x\hat{\mathbf x}+u_z\hat{\mathbf z}$, and
$$
\boxed{\frac{I_a}{KI_0}=u_z^2(1-u_y^2)=\cos^2\theta\left(1-\sin^2\theta\sin^2\phi\right).}
$$
The light is **linearly polarized** along the needle's projection into the $xz$ observation plane. Its <Jones vector> in the $(x,z)$ basis is $(u_x,u_z)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt{1-u_y^2}$ whenever the intensity is nonzero. A needle perpendicular to the incident <electric field>, or pointing directly at the observer, has zero leading-order signal.

Now $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf e=u_y=\sin\theta\sin\phi$, so
$$
\boxed{\frac{I_b}{KI_0}=u_y^2(1-u_y^2)=\sin^2\theta\sin^2\phi\left(1-\sin^2\theta\sin^2\phi\right).}

Whenever nonzero, the scattered light has **the same <linear polarization>** along $\mathbf v$ as in (a). Although the incident field points toward the observer, the tilted anisotropic needle can induce a dipole component perpendicular to that direction. An isotropic sphere would instead have an axial radiation zero for this incident <polarization>.

= Solution

Choose one circular convention as $\mathbf e_R=(\hat{\mathbf z}+i\hat{\mathbf y})/\sqrt2$ with time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$. Reversing the handedness convention changes the sign of $i$ but not the result here. The scattered complex amplitude is
$$
\mathbf E_{s,R}\propto\frac{u_z+iu_y}{\sqrt2}\mathbf v.
$$
The scalar factor contributes a common phase to both output components. Consequently the scattered wave is **linearly polarized along $\mathbf v$**, rather than circular or elliptical, with
$$
\boxed{I_R=\frac{KI_0}{2}(u_y^2+u_z^2)(1-u_y^2)=\frac{I_a+I_b}{2}.}
$$
The dominant longitudinal needle response has rank one, so both incident circular components emerge in the same real output direction.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Unpolarized incident light has equal, mutually incoherent $y$- and $z$-polarized intensities. Both components scatter into the same direction $\mathbf v$, so their <coherency matrix>[coherency matrices] add to
$$
\mathbf J_s=\frac{KI_0}{2}(u_y^2+u_z^2)\begin{pmatrix}u_x^2&u_xu_z\\u_xu_z&u_z^2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
This matrix has rank one when the signal is nonzero. The light is therefore **fully linearly polarized along $\mathbf v$**, with
$$
\boxed{I_{\rm unpol}=\frac{I_a+I_b}{2}=I_R.}
$$
A fixed highly anisotropic needle can act as a polarizing scatterer even for unpolarized input. The equality of total intensities with (c) does not mean the incident states were identical. These conclusions use the leading longitudinal-polarizability approximation; finite transverse response can change the <polarization> purity.
