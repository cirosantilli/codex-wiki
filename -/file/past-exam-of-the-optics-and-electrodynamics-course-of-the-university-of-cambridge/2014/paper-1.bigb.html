= Solution
{parent=a}

For <two-element polarization identification>, assume ideal elements and use an <intensity> detector. For each source, test both element orders while rotating their relative axes. **The circular source followed by the <quarter-wave plate> and then the <polarizer> is the combination that gives full <intensity> modulation.** The plate converts circular <light> to linear <light>, and the downstream <polarizer> then obeys <Malus's law>.

If the <polarizer> comes first, it transmits half the circular source's <intensity> and the downstream lossless plate cannot change that <intensity>. <Unpolarized light> also gives constant half-intensity in either order: a wave plate leaves it unpolarized, while a <polarizer> followed by a wave plate has no final analyzer. Thus the modulating source is circular, its first element is the <quarter-wave plate>, and its second is the <polarizer>; the remaining source is unpolarized. Testing both orders avoids needing to know either source's original brightness.

= Solution
{parent=b}

For the <oblique coupling of parallel Hertzian dipoles>, a lossless <Hertzian dipole> has directional gain $G(\theta)=\tfrac32\sin^2\theta$. The separation is $r=\sqrt2d$, and both transmitting and receiving patterns have $\sin^2\theta=1/2$, so $G_t=G_r=3/4$. <Reciprocity> gives matched receiving effective area $A_e=\lambda^2G_r/(4\pi)$. The emitted polarization matches the transverse projection of the receiving dipole axis, so there is no additional polarization mismatch. Consequently
$$
\boxed{P_L=\frac{PG_t}{4\pi r^2}\frac{\lambda^2G_r}{4\pi}
=\frac{9P\lambda^2}{512\pi^2d^2}.}
$$
Both the off-axis transmitting pattern and the off-axis receiving aperture matter. This assumes the stated far field, conjugate matching and negligible dissipative antenna losses.

= Solution

Interpret the motion as **uniform rectilinear motion**: the charge is stationary in an inertial <rest frame> and has no radiation field. Constant <speed> alone is insufficient; a curved trajectory has <acceleration> and can radiate even in vacuum.

In a transparent medium, a charge can move faster than the <light> <phase velocity> $c/n$ without exceeding $c$. The induced polarization disturbance then adds coherently on a Cherenkov cone, with $\cos\theta_C=1/(n\beta)$. Thus
$$
\beta_{\rm th}=\frac1n,\qquad
\boxed{K_{\rm th}=m_pc^2\left[\frac1{\sqrt{1-n^{-2}}}-1\right]
\simeq321\,\mathrm{MeV}\quad(n=1.5).}
$$
This is the kinematic threshold limit for the specified <refractive index>; appreciable emission requires exceeding it, and dispersion selects which <frequencies> satisfy the condition.

In the magnetic <Aharonov-Bohm effect>, coherent electron paths enclose inaccessible <magnetic flux> even though the field vanishes along the paths. The <magnetic vector potential> contributes relative <phase>
$$
\boxed{\Delta\phi=\frac q\hbar\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell
=\frac{q\Phi_B}{\hbar}\pmod{2\pi}.}
$$
Recombination turns this <phase> into a fringe displacement. For an electron $q=-e$, changing <flux> by $h/e$ gives one full fringe period. Reversing the loop orientation reverses the <phase> sign. A gauge change modifies each path's <phase> convention but leaves the closed-loop <phase> and observed <interference> unchanged. The accessible region surrounds excluded <flux>, so a globally trivial gauge cannot remove that <phase>.

\Image[paper-1-oe2014-aharonov-bohm.png]
{title=Original two-path Aharonov-Bohm schematic and the half-period fringe shift from half an electron flux period; paths avoid the flux region}

A shielded toroidal magnet provides a concrete test: <electron holography> compared waves passing through its hole and outside it. Superconducting and copper coatings confined the field and excluded electrons from the magnet; the measured relative <phase> tested enclosed <flux> rather than a classical Lorentz deflection along the paths.<Evidence for Aharonov-Bohm effect with magnetic field completely shielded from electron wave by Akira Tonomura and others>[Physical Review Letters 56, 792 (1986), primary abstract: shielded toroid and electron-holographic relative phase]{ref}

= Solution

<Photonic structures> control <light> through the spatial arrangement of <dielectric> material. <Waveguides> confine transverse <modes>; periodic <photonic crystals> combine <interference> and scattering to produce a <photonic band structure> $\omega_n(\mathbf k)$. A <photonic band gap> excludes propagating <modes> in specified directions and polarizations. **A one-dimensional stop band is not automatically a complete three-dimensional gap.**

A simple example is a <distributed Bragg reflector> with alternating high- and low-index layers. At normal incidence, the quarter-wave choice
$$
\boxed{n_Hd_H=n_Ld_L=\lambda_0/4}
$$
lets successive interface reflections reinforce near the design <wavelength>. A departure from periodicity can create a localized defect <mode> inside the stop band. An isolated defect forms a cavity; a line defect can guide <light> through otherwise forbidden bulk propagation. Finite structures leak and real materials absorb, so ideal band arguments do not ensure infinite confinement.

\Image[paper-1-oe2014-photonic-stack.png]
{title=Original transfer-matrix calculation for lossless quarter-wave dielectric stacks, showing a stop band and a transmission resonance from a central defect}

Changing the photonic <density> of states can suppress or enhance <spontaneous emission> and control how emitters couple to selected <modes>. This is distinct from electronic band-gap engineering: it is the electromagnetic <modes> that are being structured. A complete electromagnetic gap overlapping an emitter's transition is the ideal mechanism proposed for suppressing otherwise allowed <spontaneous emission>.<Inhibited Spontaneous Emission in Solid-State Physics and Electronics by Eli Yablonovitch>[Physical Review Letters 58, 2059 (1987), primary abstract: three-dimensional periodic dielectric and overlapping electromagnetic gap]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=c}

<Synchrotron radiation> is emitted by relativistic charges accelerated along curved paths. A <magnetic field> can supply the transverse <acceleration> while doing no work on the charge; the radiated <energy> must ultimately be replenished if the orbit <energy> is to remain constant. <Relativistic beaming> confines much of the radiation to angles of order $1/\gamma$ about the instantaneous <velocity>.

For circular motion of curvature radius $\rho$ and $\gamma\gg1$,
$$
\boxed{\omega_c\simeq\frac{3\gamma^3c}{2\rho},\qquad
P\simeq\frac{q^2c\gamma^4}{6\pi\epsilon_0\rho^2}.}
$$
The exact classical circular-orbit <power> contains an additional factor $\beta^4$. A distant observer sees short pulses as the beamed direction sweeps past, producing a broad high-frequency spectrum. Radiation is predominantly linearly polarized in the orbital plane and becomes elliptically polarized off it. The critical <frequency> is a spectral scale, not a monochromatic emission <frequency> or a hard cutoff.

\Image[paper-1-oe2014-synchrotron.png]
{title=Original geometric synchrotron-beaming schematic: radiation near the velocity tangent, cone angle of order inverse Lorentz factor and curved electron trajectory}

Storage rings provide intense radiation for <spectroscopy> and <diffraction>, while relativistic electrons in astrophysical <magnetic fields> also produce synchrotron emission. The classical theory relates total <power>, angular beaming and spectrum to the particle's trajectory; sufficiently strong quantum recoil requires corrections.<On the Classical Radiation of Accelerated Electrons by Julian Schwinger>[Physical Review 75, 1912 (1949), primary abstract: total radiation, angular preference and high-frequency spectrum of accelerated energetic electrons]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=3}

Use metric $\eta=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$ and common spacetime origins. A <four-vector> transforms as $A'=\Lambda A$ under a <Lorentz transformation>. A <Lorentz invariant> is a scalar unchanged by that transformation. Since $\Lambda^T\eta\Lambda=\eta$,
$$
\boxed{A'\cdot B'=A^T\Lambda^T\eta\Lambda B=A^T\eta B=A\cdot B.}
$$
The <wave phase> labels the same stage of a physical <oscillation> at a <spacetime event>, independently of the observer's coordinates. Equal-phase wavefronts and <interference> <phase differences> are physical; fixing a common <phase> origin makes the continuous <phase> a <Lorentz scalar>. For a plane wave,
$$
\phi=\omega t-\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r=K_\mu X^\mu,
\qquad K_\mu=\partial_\mu\phi.
$$
The <gradient> of a scalar is a <covector>; raising its index gives the <four-wavevector> $K^\mu=(\omega/c,\mathbf k)$. Under an $x$ boost,
$$
\boxed{\omega'=\gamma(\omega-vk_x),\quad
k'_x=\gamma(k_x-v\omega/c^2),\quad k'_y=k_y,\quad k'_z=k_z.}
$$
For vacuum <light>, $k_x=(\omega/c)\cos\theta$, giving
$$
\boxed{\omega'=\gamma\omega(1-\beta\cos\theta).}
$$
A <photon> has <four-momentum> $p^\mu=\hbar K^\mu$, with $K^2=0$. For two <photons> separated by angle $\alpha$,
$$
(p_1+p_2)^2=2\hbar^2K_1\cdot K_2
=\frac{2\hbar^2\omega_1\omega_2}{c^2}(1-\cos\alpha).
$$
In the <centre-of-momentum frame> the least possible pair <energy> is $2m_ec^2$, when both final particles are at rest. Thus the threshold has $(p_1+p_2)^2=4m_e^2c^2$; above it, excess <energy> supplies their <kinetic energy>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\omega_{2,\rm th}=
\frac{2m_e^2c^4}{\hbar^2\omega_1(1-\cos\alpha)}.}
$$
The kinematic equality is a limiting threshold; nonzero production <phase> space requires energies above it. For parallel <photons> no finite threshold is available, whereas head-on incidence minimizes the required <energy>:
$$
\boxed{E_{2,\min}=\frac{(m_ec^2)^2}{E_1}\quad(\alpha=\pi).}
$$
The <blackbody-peak photon pair-production estimate>, using the frequency-spectrum blackbody peak, $E_1=2.82144k_BT=7.29\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{eV}$ at $3\,\mathrm K$, gives **$E_{2,\min}\simeq3.58\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{eV}$**, or $358\,\mathrm{TeV}$. If “peak” means the wavelength-spectrum peak, $E_1=4.96511k_BT$ instead gives $2.03\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{eV}$. The spectral convention changes the prefactor, not the conclusion that <photons> of order a few $10^{14}\,\mathrm{eV}$ are needed for this idealized peak-photon threshold.

= Solution

<Temporal coherence> compares the field at different times at one location: a narrow spectrum maintains a predictable <phase> relation over a longer delay. <Spatial coherence> compares the field at different locations at the same time: different angular source components illuminate a baseline with different <phase differences>, which wash out <interference> when averaged. These are properties of correlations, not claims that all thermal source points have a common <phase>.

Assume a distant spatially incoherent, quasimonochromatic source, small angles, identical slit transmission, matched polarizations and equal mean <intensity> at the two slits. For an angular component $\theta$, the relative input <phase> is $-kd\theta$. Let $\varphi$ be the additional observation-plane <phase>. Incoherent angular components add <intensities>, giving
$$
I_{\rm obs}(\varphi)=2F(0)+2\operatorname{Re}[e^{i\varphi}F(d)],
\qquad F(d)=\int I(\theta)e^{-ikd\theta}\,d\theta.
$$
Its extrema are $2F(0)\pm2|F(d)|$, hence
$$
\boxed{V(d)=\frac{I_{\max}-I_{\min}}{I_{\max}+I_{\min}}
=\frac{|F(d)|}{F(0)}.}
$$
For symmetric $I(\theta)$, $F(d)$ is a real cosine transform. **Physical visibility is its normalized absolute value**; the printed proportionality omits that qualification. A negative signed coherence reverses fringe <phase> rather than giving negative contrast. This is the small-angle <Van Cittert-Zernike theorem>. <Temporal coherence> analogously depends on the <Fourier transform> of the spectrum.

A <Michelson stellar interferometer> uses separated collecting mirrors to obtain a large adjustable projected baseline without requiring a monolithic objective of that diameter. Each collector supplies <light> to a common combiner. Optical path compensation keeps the delay within the <temporal coherence> range; varying mirror separation samples <spatial coherence> and resolves stellar angular structure finer than a small collector alone.

Let $g_\star(Y)$ be the normalized signed coherence of one star, and take the baseline along the projected binary separation. The <equal-brightness binary-star visibility> for identical profiles centred at $\pm\beta/2$ give
$$
\boxed{g(Y)=g_\star(Y)\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi\beta Y}{\lambda}\right),\qquad
V(Y)=|g(Y)|.}
$$
For **uniform circular discs of <angular diameter> $\alpha$**,
$$
\boxed{g_\star(Y)=\frac{2J_1(x)}x,\qquad
x=\frac{\pi\alpha Y}{\lambda},\qquad g_\star(0)=1.}
$$
For <binary separation from visibility-zero spacing>, the rapid binary visibility zeros satisfy $Y_j=(j+\tfrac12)\lambda/\beta$, so their repeated spacing is $\lambda/\beta$. The slower single-star envelope first vanishes at $Y=1.21967\lambda/\alpha$. The <stellar angular diameter from a visibility envelope> and the binary separation follow by fitting both scales; multiple baselines distinguish envelope zeros from binary zeros.

\Image[paper-1-oe2014-binary-visibility.png]
{title=Original normalized binary-star visibility sketch for two uniform circular discs, with projected separation eight times their diameter; binary zeros and the stellar-diameter envelope are labelled}

Angular width alone does not specify a unique brightness profile. If the intended one-dimensional model is a uniform angular strip of full width $\alpha$, replace the disc factor by $\operatorname{sinc}(\pi\alpha Y/\lambda)$ with $\operatorname{sinc}u=\sin u/u$; its first envelope zero is $\lambda/\alpha$. <Limb darkening> changes the disc envelope too. Rotating the baseline is necessary to recover an unknown separation direction; a single orientation measures only its projection.
