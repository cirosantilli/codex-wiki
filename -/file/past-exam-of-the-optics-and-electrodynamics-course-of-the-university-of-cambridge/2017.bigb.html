= Solution

At an air–glass interface, resolve the incident <unpolarized light> into components perpendicular ($s$) and parallel ($p$) to the plane of incidence. The <Fresnel equations> give the parallel reflection coefficient
$$
r_p=\frac{n_2\cos\theta_i-n_1\cos\theta_t}{n_2\cos\theta_i+n_1\cos\theta_t},\qquad n_1\sin\theta_i=n_2\sin\theta_t.
$$
At <Brewster's angle>, $r_p=0$. Together with <Snell's law>, this gives $\theta_i+\theta_t=\pi/2$ and $\tan\theta_i=n_2/n_1$. Thus, taking the incident medium to be air,
$$
\boxed{\theta_i=\arctan(1.5)=56.31^\circ\text{ from the surface normal}.}
$$
The remaining reflected component has <linear polarization> **perpendicular to the plane of incidence**. This assumes a transparent, nonmagnetic glass with negligible absorption.

Let $I_0$ be the <intensity> from either fibre alone at the observation point, assuming equal output <intensities>, matching <polarizations> and negligible drift. The screen samples the relative <phase> $\Phi(X)$ between the two outputs. For an ideal monochromatic <laser>, <first-order optical coherence> is complete and
$$
\boxed{I_{\rm ideal}(X)=2I_0[1+\cos\Phi(X)]},\qquad I_{\min}=0,\quad I_{\max}=4I_0.
$$
The <interference fringes> are approximately equally spaced in the small-angle far field, with a slowly varying <diffraction> envelope. If the output separation is $a$ and the screen distance is $D$, the <interference fringe spacing> is approximately $\lambda_0D/a$. Equal phases at the two *input* ends do not ensure a central bright fringe: the extra fibre contributes carrier <phase> $2\pi n_{\rm eff}(l_2-l_1)/\lambda_0$ in addition to the free-space <optical path difference>.

For a finite linewidth, the time-averaged <interference> signal becomes
$$
I(X)=2I_0\{1+|g^{(1)}(\tau)|\cos[\Phi(X)+\arg g^{(1)}(\tau)]\},\qquad V=\frac{I_{\max}-I_{\min}}{I_{\max}+I_{\min}}=|g^{(1)}(\tau)|.
$$
Here $g^{(1)}$ may be defined for the slowly varying field envelope, with the carrier <phase> already included in $\Phi$. A realistic <laser> therefore gives reduced <interferometric visibility>; for $V=1/2$, its extrema are $I_0$ and $3I_0$.

\Image[2017-q1-fibre-interference.png]
{title=Ideal and finite-linewidth interference for equal output intensities}

For a <Lorentzian line shape> of <frequency> <full width at half maximum> $\Delta\nu$, <temporal optical coherence> gives
$$
|g^{(1)}(\tau)|=e^{-\pi\Delta\nu|\tau|},\qquad \tau=\frac{n_g(l_2-l_1)}c.
$$
The relevant index is the <group index>. Using the intended nondispersive approximation $n_g\simeq n_{\rm eff}=2$,
$$
\tau=\frac{20\,\mathrm m}{c}=66.71\,\mathrm{ns},\qquad \boxed{\Delta\nu=\frac{\log2}{\pi\tau}=3.31\,\mathrm{MHz}\quad\text{(FWHM)}.}
$$
This <delayed-fibre linewidth measurement> assumes the loss of contrast comes from linewidth. Unequal output <intensities>, polarization mismatch or mechanical <phase> fluctuations also lower <interferometric visibility>. If <dispersion> matters, the stated <phase> index alone does not specify the necessary <group index>.

Use axes adapted to the two orthogonal rotations in the diagram:
$$
\mathbf n_A=\hat{\mathbf z},\qquad \mathbf n_B=\frac{\hat{\mathbf x}+\hat{\mathbf z}}{\sqrt2},\qquad \mathbf n_C=\frac{\hat{\mathbf y}+\hat{\mathbf z}}{\sqrt2}.
$$
For a linearly oscillating <Hertzian dipole> with real unit axis $\mathbf d$ and total time-averaged radiated <power> $P$, the <angular distribution of electric dipole radiation> is
$$
I(\mathbf n)=\frac{3P}{8\pi R^2}\bigl[1-(\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf n)^2\bigr].
$$
The normalization follows from $\int\sin^2\vartheta\,d\Omega=8\pi/3$. Equal readings therefore imply
$$
(d_x+d_z)^2=2d_z^2,\qquad (d_y+d_z)^2=2d_z^2.
$$
Here $d_z\neq0$, since otherwise all components would vanish. Reversing the axis is immaterial, so choose $d_z>0$. The complete family is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf d\parallel(s_B\sqrt2-1,\ s_C\sqrt2-1,\ 1),\qquad s_B,s_C\in\{+1,-1\}.}
$$
Define $a=\sqrt2-1$, $b=-\sqrt2-1$, and $D=1+(s_B\sqrt2-1)^2+(s_C\sqrt2-1)^2$. Then $d_z^2=1/D$, and the absolute <intensity> fixes
$$
P=\frac{8\pi R^2I}{3}\frac{D}{D-1}.
$$
For the measured distance and <intensity>, the allowed axes and powers are:

* $\mathbf d\parallel(a,a,1)$: $D=7-4\sqrt2$, $\boxed{P=32.79\,\mathrm{kW}}$.
* $\mathbf d\parallel(a,b,1)$ or $(b,a,1)$: $D=7$, $\boxed{P=9.774\,\mathrm{kW}}$ for either axis.
* $\mathbf d\parallel(b,b,1)$: $D=7+4\sqrt2$, $\boxed{P=9.096\,\mathrm{kW}}$.

**The <intensity> data do not select a unique orientation or total <power>.** This is the <dipole-axis ambiguity from three intensity measurements>: the readings constrain squared projections and lose their signs. For example, the first branch makes an angle $30.36^\circ$ with $A$, but the other branches are equally consistent with the three measurements. A further suitable observation direction or polarization measurement is needed to remove the ambiguity. The calculation uses radiation-zone <intensities>, as appropriate to the <Hertzian dipole> model.

= Solution

Molecules in clean air are much smaller than visible wavelengths. Their induced <electric dipole moments> undergo <Rayleigh scattering>. Away from molecular resonances, the induced dipole amplitude is approximately $p=\alpha E$, with only weak <wavelength> variation in the <polarizability> $\alpha$. The radiation-zone <electric field> is proportional to $\ddot p$, so the scattered <intensity> satisfies
$$
\boxed{I_{\rm sc}\propto\omega^4|\alpha|^2 I_{\rm in}\ \simeq\ \lambda^{-4}I_{\rm in}.}
$$
This is the <Rayleigh scattering wavelength dependence>. For equal incident <intensities>, $450\,\mathrm{nm}$ light scatters about $(650/450)^4=4.35$ times as strongly as $650\,\mathrm{nm}$ light. Thus light scattered out of the direct solar beam and into our line of sight is enriched in blue wavelengths.

The dipole mechanism also determines the <Rayleigh scattering angular pattern>. For <unpolarized light>, its angular factor is $1+\cos^2\vartheta$; near $90^\circ$ scattering, the scattered light has strong <linear polarization>. The sky's perceived colour depends additionally on the solar spectrum, atmospheric transmission and the eye's spectral response, so the $\lambda^{-4}$ law alone does not predict a violet sky. Over a long near-horizontal path the direct beam loses more short-wavelength light, explaining red sunsets. Large aerosol particles instead produce <Mie scattering>, which is less strongly wavelength-selective and can make haze look whitish.

A <phased array> uses fixed <antennas> driven coherently, with adjustable electronic delays or <phases>. Changing those relative <phases> changes the direction of <constructive interference>.

For $N$ identical elements at $\mathbf r_j=jd\hat{\mathbf x}$, take time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$ and feed <phase> $j\psi$. In a far-field direction at angle $\theta$ from the array normal, the <array factor> is
$$
F(\theta)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{ij(\psi-kd\sin\theta)}=e^{i(N-1)\chi/2}\frac{\sin(N\chi/2)}{\sin(\chi/2)},\qquad \chi=\psi-kd\sin\theta.
$$
Multiply $|F|^2$ by the single-element <antenna beam pattern> to obtain the total angular <intensity>. All elements add in <phase> in the selected direction if
$$
\boxed{\psi=kd\sin\theta_0\pmod{2\pi}.}
$$
Thus electronic control of $\psi$ steers the beam to $\theta_0$ without moving an element. <Reciprocity> gives the corresponding directionality in reception.

\Image[2017-q2-phased-array.png]
{title=An electronically steered linear array and its normalized array factors}

The aperture $Nd$ controls angular resolution: the nearest nulls obey $|\sin\theta-\sin\theta_0|=\lambda/(Nd)$, giving a main-beam width of order $\lambda/(Nd\cos\theta_0)$ away from endfire. Additional equally strong maxima can occur when $\chi=2\pi m$; these are <grating lobes>. Choosing $d<\lambda/2$ avoids them for all steering directions, with $d=\lambda/2$ the usual limiting spacing. Amplitude tapering reduces side lobes at the cost of a wider main beam. For a broad <frequency> band, true time delays preserve steering more accurately than frequency-independent <phase> shifts.

A charge moving uniformly through a transparent dielectric can emit <Cherenkov radiation> if its speed exceeds the <phase velocity> of light in that medium:
$$
\boxed{v>\frac{c}{n(\omega)},\qquad \beta n(\omega)>1,\quad\beta=\frac vc.}
$$
It need not exceed the vacuum speed $c$. The passing charge polarizes the medium; the induced response produces radiation that adds coherently along selected directions. Equivalently, <phase> matching along the trajectory requires $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf v=\omega$. Since $|\mathbf k|=n(\omega)\omega/c$,
$$
\boxed{\cos\theta_C=\frac{1}{\beta n(\omega)}}.
$$
Here $\theta_C$ is the angle of the emitted light rays to the forward particle <velocity>: the rays form a cone around the track. With <dispersion>, both the threshold and cone angle depend on <frequency>; only transparent <frequency> bands satisfying the threshold contribute escaping radiation.

\Image[2017-q2-cherenkov-cone.png]
{title=Wavefront envelope and photon direction for Cherenkov radiation}

In the wavefront construction, a disturbance emitted a time $t$ earlier has radius $ct/n$, while the particle has moved $vt$. Tangents from the present particle position to these wavefronts make a backward-opening wavefront cone of half-angle $\alpha$, where $\sin\alpha=1/(\beta n)$. The light rays are normal to this cone. Consequently $\alpha+\theta_C=\pi/2$: the <Cherenkov photon-cone and wavefront-cone angles> are complementary, rather than identical.

= Solution

**General pure state.** Use time dependence $e^{i(kz-\omega t)}$. The most general nonzero <Jones vector> can be written
$$
\mathbf J=Ae^{i\chi}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha\\e^{i\delta}\sin\alpha\end{pmatrix},\qquad A>0,\quad0\leq\alpha\leq\frac\pi2,\quad0\leq\delta<2\pi.
$$
There are four real parameters in the full complex field. Removing overall <intensity> $A^2$ and the unobservable common <phase> $\chi$ leaves **two real degrees of freedom for <pure polarization>**, $\alpha$ and $\delta$. Their endpoint degeneracies simply reflect the fact that the <phase> of a vanishing component is irrelevant. A single <Jones vector> cannot represent partially polarized light.

**Three linear <polarizers>.** Define the transmission axis $\mathbf a_\theta=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)^T$. An ideal linear <polarizer> has <Jones matrix> $P_\theta=\mathbf a_\theta\mathbf a_\theta^T$. For definiteness label $(1,-i)^T/\sqrt2$ as right-circular in the chosen convention; reversing the handedness convention changes no <intensity> below. The first <polarizer> produces $(1,0)^T/\sqrt2$. The intermediate <polarizer> and final $y$ analyzer then give
$$
\mathbf J_{\rm out}=P_{\pi/2}P_\theta P_0\mathbf J_R=\frac{\sin\theta\cos\theta}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix},
$$
so <Malus's law> gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{I_{\rm out}}{I_{\rm in}}=\frac18\sin^2(2\theta).}
$$
The maximum is $1/8$ at $\theta=\pi/4+m\pi/2$, and the transmission vanishes at $\theta=m\pi/2$. The first <polarizer> accounts for an initial factor $1/2$; retaining that factor is essential when quoting transmission relative to the incident circularly polarized beam.

**<Waveplate> and quarter-wave matrix.** A lossless <waveplate> has <linear birefringence>: its orthogonal fast and slow axes have indices $n_f<n_s$. A thickness $L$ produces slow-minus-fast <phase retardation>
$$
\delta=\frac{2\pi L}{\lambda_0}(n_s-n_f).
$$
Resolving the incident <Jones vector> into the plate axes, advancing the two phases and transforming back gives, up to a common <phase>,
$$
J_\delta(\theta)=R(-\theta)\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&e^{i\delta}\end{pmatrix}R(\theta).
$$
A <quarter-wave plate> has $\delta=\pi/2$ modulo $2\pi$. With $c_\theta=\cos\theta$ and $s_\theta=\sin\theta$, its <Jones matrix> is therefore
$$
\boxed{Q(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}c_\theta^2+i s_\theta^2&(1-i)c_\theta s_\theta\\(1-i)c_\theta s_\theta&s_\theta^2+i c_\theta^2\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
In particular $Q(0)=\operatorname{diag}(1,i)$. Equal incident components along the two axes acquire a relative quarter-cycle <phase>, converting $45^\circ$ <linear polarization> to <circular polarization>; an incident component along either principal axis stays linearly polarized.

**Half-wave replacement.** A <half-wave plate> has $\delta=\pi$ and hence
$$
H(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos2\theta&\sin2\theta\\\sin2\theta&-\cos2\theta\end{pmatrix}.
$$
It converts the $x$-polarized output of the first <polarizer> to a linear state at angle $2\theta$. The final $y$ analyzer gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{I_{\rm out}}{I_{\rm in}}=\frac12\sin^2(2\theta).}
$$
The maximum is $1/2$, because the ideal <half-wave plate> loses no light after the first <polarizer>.

**Quarter-wave replacement.** The transmitted $y$ amplitude is $(1-i)c_\theta s_\theta/\sqrt2$, giving
$$
\boxed{\frac{I_{\rm out}}{I_{\rm in}}=\frac14\sin^2(2\theta).}
$$
More generally, the retarder gives $I_{\rm out}/I_{\rm in}=\tfrac12\sin^2(\delta/2)\sin^2(2\theta)$. All three requested curves have the same zeros and angular period, but different heights:

\Image[2017-q3-polarization-transmission.png]
{title=Transmission through crossed outer polarizers with three different middle elements}

**Preparing an arbitrary ellipse.** Yes: two rotatable <quarter-wave plates> already suffice. Let the input left-circular state be $\mathbf J_L=(1,i)^T/\sqrt2$. Up to an overall <phase>, any target <pure polarization> can be parameterized by its <polarization ellipse>:
$$
\mathbf J_{\rm target}=R(-\beta)\begin{pmatrix}\cos\eta\\i\sin\eta\end{pmatrix},\qquad-\frac\pi4\leq\eta\leq\frac\pi4.
$$
Here $\beta$ is the major-axis orientation, and $\eta$ sets the signed minor-to-major semiaxis ratio $\tan\eta$. First note directly from $Q(\theta)$ that
$$
Q(\theta)\mathbf J_L\ \propto\ \begin{pmatrix}\cos(\theta-\pi/4)\\\sin(\theta-\pi/4)\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Choose the two fast-axis angles as
$$
\boxed{\theta_1=\beta+\eta+\frac\pi4,\qquad\theta_2=\beta.}
$$
The first <quarter-wave plate> produces linear light at $\beta+\eta$. In the axes of the second, its components are $(\cos\eta,\sin\eta)$; the second gives precisely $\mathbf J_{\rm target}$. This proves <pure-polarization preparation with two quarter-wave plates>, including the linear and circular limiting cases. A <half-wave plate> can also rotate the axes, but is not required. The phrase about left-handed input is understood as left-circular light in this construction; the explicit vector fixes the convention.

**Switchable retardation.** The <electro-optic Kerr effect> induces <linear birefringence> quadratic in a static transverse <electric field>. Defining the coefficient $K$ by $\Delta n=\lambda_0 K E_{\rm dc}^2$, its <phase retardation> is
$$
\delta_K=\frac{2\pi L\Delta n}{\lambda_0}=2\pi KLE_{\rm dc}^2.
$$
The two eigenpolarizations are linear, parallel and perpendicular to the applied field. Thus an appropriate field produces a switchable <quarter-wave plate>:
$$
\boxed{E_{\rm dc}^2=\frac{1}{4|K|L}\quad\text{for the smallest }|\delta_K|=\pi/2.}
$$
The sign of $K$ determines which axis is fast. Switching the field off removes the induced retardation, assuming no residual birefringence.

The <Faraday effect> instead gives <circular birefringence> for a longitudinal <magnetic field>. The two circular eigenmodes acquire different phases, rotating a <linear polarization> through
$$
\theta_F=VBL=\frac{\pi L}{\lambda_0}(n_+-n_-),
$$
with sign set by the handedness convention and the <Verdet constant> $V$. Its <Jones matrix> in the linear basis is a real rotation, up to a common <phase>. It preserves the ellipticity of a pure state, so a linear input remains linear. A $\pi/2$ <phase> difference between circular eigenmodes rotates a linear state by $\pi/4$; it does not make it circular. **The Kerr device supplies the switchable <quarter-wave plate>; a Faraday rotator alone does not.**

**Gauge freedom.** The <magnetic vector potential> satisfies $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$. The transformation
$$
\boxed{\mathbf A\mapsto\mathbf A+\nabla\chi}
$$
leaves $\mathbf B$ unchanged because the <curl> of a <gradient> is zero. For time-dependent fields, transform the electric scalar potential as $\phi\mapsto\phi-\partial_t\chi$ as well, so that $\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi-\partial_t\mathbf A$ is unchanged. The scalar $\chi$ must have the regularity and single-valuedness appropriate to the physical region and its boundary conditions. Local values of $\mathbf A$ therefore depend on the <gauge transformation>.

**Gauge and nonzero component.** In the standard axisymmetric <Coulomb gauge> representative for the <long-solenoid approximation>,
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0,\qquad A_r=A_z=0,\qquad\mathbf A=A_\phi(r)\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}.}
$$
The divergence vanishes since $A_\phi$ is independent of the azimuth. Inside, this agrees with the usual <symmetric gauge> representative $\mathbf A=\tfrac12\mathbf B\times\mathbf r$. The <Coulomb gauge> condition alone does not force the other two components to vanish; the symmetry, regularity and customary boundary choice select this representative from the remaining gauge freedom.

**Radial dependence.** Write the interior <magnetic field> as $B_0\hat{\mathbf z}$, where $B_0=\mu_0nI$ for a vacuum <solenoid> of <solenoid turn density> $n$. Its exterior <magnetic field> vanishes in the infinitely long idealization. Applying <Stokes theorem> to a coaxial circle gives $2\pi rA_\phi(r)=\Phi(r)$. Therefore the <Coulomb-gauge vector potential of a long solenoid> is
$$
\boxed{A_\phi(r)=\begin{cases}B_0r/2,&r<R,\\B_0R^2/(2r),&r>R.\end{cases}}
$$
It grows linearly inside, is continuous at $R$, and decays as $r^{-1}$ outside. Although its exterior <curl> is zero, its circulation is the enclosed <magnetic flux> $\Phi=B_0\pi R^2$. No regular single-valued gauge function removes it throughout the exterior while preserving this circulation.

**<Electric field> of the rotating charged surface.** For clarity retain absolute <permittivity> $\epsilon$ and permeability $\mu$ in the cylinder formulae; setting them to unity gives the rationalized-unit convention in the question. If the stated unity means relative material constants in SI, use $\epsilon_0,\mu_0$ instead. Assume prescribed charge densities in the laboratory frame and the nonrelativistic, steady-rotation model.

The axisymmetric surface charge is stationary as a distribution even while the material rotates. A Gaussian cylinder of length $L$ has enclosed charge zero for $r<R$ and $2\pi RL\sigma$ for $r>R$. <Gauss's law> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(r)=\begin{cases}0,&r<R,\\\dfrac{\sigma R}{\epsilon r}\hat{\mathbf r},&r>R.\end{cases}}
$$
The radial <electric field> jumps from zero to $\sigma/\epsilon$ at the surface and then decays as $r^{-1}$. Its direction reverses for negative $\sigma$. The <angular velocity> does not enter this electric-field result for a specified surface charge.

**<Magnetic field> of the volume-charged cylinder.** A shell of radius $a$ and thickness $da$ has surface charge $\rho\,da$, azimuthal surface current $dK_\phi=\rho\omega a\,da$, and the field of an infinite <solenoid>: $dB_z=\mu\rho\omega a\,da$ inside that shell and zero outside it. At radius $r<R$, only shells with $a>r$ contribute. Thus the <magnetic field of a rotating charged solid cylinder> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B(r)=\mu\rho\omega\int_r^R a\,da\,\hat{\mathbf z}=\frac{\mu\rho\omega}{2}(R^2-r^2)\hat{\mathbf z},\qquad r<R.}
$$
With no applied background <magnetic field>, $\mathbf B=0$ outside. The profile is parabolic, largest on the axis and zero at the boundary. The sign follows $\rho\omega$. As a check, the <current density> is $J_\phi=\rho\omega r$, and <Ampère's circuital law> gives $-dB_z/dr=\mu J_\phi$, as required.

\Image[2017-q4-cylindrical-fields.png]
{title=Radial profiles of the solenoid vector potential and the two charged-cylinder fields}

**<Electron> impulse when the current is switched off.** Choose positive $I$ so the initial <magnetic flux> is $\Phi_i=\mu_0nI\pi R^2$ along $+z$. <Faraday's law of induction> gives, outside the <solenoid>,
$$
E_\phi(d,t)=-\frac{1}{2\pi d}\frac{d\Phi}{dt}.
$$
For an <electron> of <electric charge> $q=-e$, treat its position as fixed while the short pulse acts. The <inductive impulse outside a switched solenoid> is
$$
\Delta\mathbf p=-\frac{q}{2\pi d}(\Phi_f-\Phi_i)\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}=-\frac{e\Phi_i}{2\pi d}\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}.
$$
Consequently the nonrelativistic final <velocity> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf v_f=-\frac{e\mu_0nIR^2}{2m_ed}\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}.}
$$
The induced <electric field> circulates in the positive azimuthal direction while a positive flux decreases, so the <electron> accelerates in the negative direction. Once the pulse has passed, there is no exterior field and it travels along the tangent at the impulse point, rather than continuing in a circular orbit. If the resulting <momentum> is relativistic, use $\mathbf v_f=c\mathbf p_f/\sqrt{m_e^2c^2+|\mathbf p_f|^2}$ instead. The sudden-switch result assumes negligible displacement during the pulse and the usual quasistatic <electromagnetic induction> approximation; “instantaneous” switching does not mean instantaneous propagation of fields to arbitrary distance.

One can check the sign using conserved canonical angular <momentum>: axial symmetry conserves $p_\phi^{\rm can}=L_z+qrA_\phi$. Initially $L_z=0$, and finally $A_\phi=0$, so $L_z=q\Phi_i/(2\pi)=-e\Phi_i/(2\pi)$.

**Interferometric measurement at constant current.** Use a coherent <electron> beam split into two paths passing on opposite sides of a shielded long <solenoid>, then recombine them to form <interference fringes>. Keep the current constant during the passage and suppress leakage fields on the paths. In the <Aharonov-Bohm effect>, the <phase> difference due to the <magnetic vector potential> is the closed-loop integral
$$
\boxed{\Delta\varphi=\frac{q}{\hbar}\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf l=\frac{q\Phi}{\hbar}=-\frac{e\mu_0n\pi R^2}{\hbar}I\pmod{2\pi}.}
$$
The overall sign reverses if the path orientation is reversed. The displacement in fringe periods is $\Delta\varphi/(2\pi)$, so the measured fringe shift is linear in $I$ and repeats when
$$
\boxed{\Delta I=\frac{h}{e\mu_0n\pi R^2}.}
$$
A <gauge transformation> adds a gradient whose closed-loop integral vanishes for a single-valued $\chi$. Endpoint changes in <electron> wavefunction <phase> cancel in the interference measurement. The measured <Aharonov-Bohm phase> is therefore gauge invariant: it determines the flux, or equivalently the circulation of $\mathbf A$. In the chosen <Coulomb gauge> this implies $A_\phi(d)=\mu_0nIR^2/(2d)$, but **the experiment does not select or measure a unique local vector potential independently of gauge**.

**Far field of the rotating sphere.** Interpret uniform charge as uniform volume charge of total $Q$, and take $\boldsymbol\omega=\omega\hat{\mathbf z}$. The <magnetic dipole moment of a rigidly rotating charge distribution> is
$$
\mathbf m=\frac12\int\mathbf r\times\mathbf J\,d^3r,\qquad \mathbf J=\rho\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r.
$$
Its axial component is $m_z=(\rho\omega/2)\int r_\perp^2d^3r$. For a solid sphere, $\int r_\perp^2d^3r=8\pi R^5/15$ and $\rho=3Q/(4\pi R^3)$, so
$$
\boxed{\mathbf m=\frac{QR^2}{5}\boldsymbol\omega.}
$$
The <magnetic field of a rotating charged spherical body> is a static <magnetic dipole field> at large distances:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r^3}\bigl[3(\mathbf m\cdot\hat{\mathbf r})\hat{\mathbf r}-\mathbf m\bigr],\qquad r\gg R.}
$$
In particular, $|\mathbf B|=\mu_0|\mathbf m|\sqrt{1+3\cos^2\vartheta}/(4\pi r^3)$. Both the angular mean of the magnitude and its <root mean square> therefore scale as $\boxed{r^{-3}}$; the latter is $B_{\rm rms}=\sqrt2\mu_0|\mathbf m|/(4\pi r^3)$. The angular average of the *vector* is zero, which is different from average strength. Steady rotation leaves the charge and current distributions stationary, so this is <magnetostatics>, not a radiative $r^{-1}$ field. If uniform charge instead means a thin spherical surface, replace the dipole moment by $QR^2\boldsymbol\omega/3$; the angular pattern and $r^{-3}$ scaling are unchanged.
