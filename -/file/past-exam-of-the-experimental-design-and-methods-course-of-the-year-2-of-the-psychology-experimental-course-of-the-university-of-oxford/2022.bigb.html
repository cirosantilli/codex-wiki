==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

**TODO: the numerical results, association direction and statistical evidence are unavailable.** The required output includes the actual fitted model, estimates and uncertainty, test statistics, <statistical degrees of freedom>, <p-values> and an appropriate model-fit summary. An age-adjusted <regression coefficient>, if reported, would describe a conditional association under that model rather than the same estimand as an unadjusted <correlation>. ==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

**Several mechanisms could explain an observed association without a causal effect of power on friendliness.** No exposure was assigned, so this is an <observational study>. Age, organizational role, wealth, gender, industry, public prominence or other common causes could produce <confounding>. Reverse direction is also possible: interpersonal behavior might influence advancement and hence power. Adjusting for age alone would not remove every alternative explanation.

The measured outcome is *perceived friendliness from a photograph*. Facial expression, pose, lighting, clothing, attractiveness and familiarity could affect those ratings independently of everyday behavior. If raters recognize an executive or know their status, expectations could alter the outcome; financial experts' power ratings may themselves involve judgment and <measurement error>. These are <construct validity> issues, since a photographic impression need not measure friendliness toward colleagues.

The preselected thirty executives are not a probability sample of all workers or even necessarily of all executives. <Selection bias>, narrow representation and <restriction of range> could alter associations and limit <external validity>. Multiple ratings of the same executive are also not independent executive observations; ignoring that structure would be <pseudoreplication>. These possibilities apply whether the reported association is positive, negative or inconclusive; the unavailable numerical findings are not assumed.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

**Improve the sampling, measurement and dependence model before interpreting the association.** Define the <target population> and recruit a broader sample with a transparent sampling frame, ideally using probability sampling where feasible and adequate representation across the relevant ages and roles. Justify the <sample size> for the intended <effect size> and precision. Preselection on reputation or the outcomes should be avoided or explicitly modeled.

Operationalize workplace friendliness with several relevant measures, such as independently obtained colleague reports and standardized behavioral tasks, rather than relying only on photographs. Use standardized photographs if impressions are themselves the research target, several independent raters, and <blinding> to power and the specific hypothesis. Measure <inter-rater reliability> and retain the distinction between impression and behavior. Establish what the power measure actually assesses and record plausible <confounders> with a causal rationale.

For a <linear regression> analysis, use friendliness as an explicitly declared outcome and power as a continuous predictor. Retain actual age as a continuous <covariate> when available instead of treating three age groups as equally spaced measurements; consider justified nonlinearity. If only age categories are available, use explicit categorical contrasts and nonoverlapping category definitions. Check <regression diagnostics>, influential observations, <heteroscedasticity> and <multicollinearity>. Account for executive and rater dependence with an appropriate <mixed-effects model> when repeated or crossed ratings are analyzed; do not count each rating as a new executive. Report estimates with <confidence intervals> and disclose exclusions and missing observations.

<Preregistration> can distinguish planned analyses from exploratory ones. A longitudinal study with repeated measures can establish temporal ordering more clearly, though it remains vulnerable to <confounding>. A feasible, ethical <randomized experiment> manipulating assigned power or role in a controlled task could test a more narrowly defined causal effect; its <external validity> to senior executives must still be assessed. No observational adjustment alone guarantees removal of all unmeasured alternatives.

=== 3
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

Use **<one-way analysis of variance>** to compare a quantitative <response variable> across levels of one categorical factor; for example, compare task accuracy across three independently assigned teaching methods. The ordinary independent-groups procedure tests the <null hypothesis> that all group <population means> are equal. Its <F-test> compares variation associated with the group factor against within-group error variation; a significant result indicates that not all <population means> are equal, without identifying which groups differ.<One-Way ANOVA by National Institute of Standards and Technology>[Handbook Section 3.2.3.1, Description and Model]{ref}

The classical <one-way normal linear model> assumes independent observations and within-group errors approximately following a <normal distribution> with a common <variance>. Repeated observations on the same people require a different dependence structure; pronounced unequal <variances> may call for <Welch's one-way analysis of variance>. Planned <contrasts> or appropriately controlled follow-up comparisons identify scientifically relevant differences. Report the <means>, differences and <confidence intervals>, not just significance. Causal interpretation depends on the <design of experiments>, particularly <randomization>, rather than on the <F-test> alone.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Use a **bivariate <correlation>** to describe the direction and strength of association between two measured variables, such as study time and test score. The <Pearson correlation coefficient> describes linear association between two quantitative variables: its sign gives direction, and its magnitude reflects linear association on a standardized scale. A <confidence interval> describes estimation uncertainty; a corresponding <hypothesis test> can assess a specified null association under its sampling assumptions.

Inspect a <scatter plot> for curvature, clusters, influential observations and <restriction of range>. A coefficient near zero does not exclude a strong nonlinear relationship. For ordered or markedly nonnormal measurements, a <Spearman rank correlation> can describe monotone association, using ranks; it does not require a linear relation in the original units. Ordinary inferential procedures require independent observational units and appropriate assumptions for their chosen method. **<Correlation> alone does not identify a causal direction or exclude <confounding>.**

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Use a **<Student t-test>** to assess a mean or mean difference relative to its <standard error>. A one-sample version compares a <population mean> with a specified reference. An independent two-sample version compares two separate groups, such as groups assigned different instructions; <Welch's t-test> permits unequal group <variances>. A <paired t-test> uses within-pair differences, such as each participant's before/after scores, rather than pretending those measurements are independent.<Two-Sample t-Test for Equal Means by National Institute of Standards and Technology>[Handbook Section 1.3.5.3, paired/unpaired data and equal/unequal variance cases]{ref}

The relevant <normality assumption> concerns within-group errors for the independent-groups model or the distribution of within-pair differences for the paired model. Independent participants or pairs remain essential. Report the estimated difference and its <confidence interval> together with the test result. A small <p-value> indicates incompatibility with the specified <null hypothesis> under the model; it does not measure the size of the difference, the probability that the <null hypothesis> is true, or whether the difference matters practically.

=== 4
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

**<Qualitative research methods> investigate meanings, experiences and processes in context.** For example, interviews about why lecturers find scheduling difficult could reveal unanticipated constraints that a fixed-response <questionnaire> omits. Interviews, focus groups, observations and documents provide material; a clearly stated approach such as <thematic analysis> or <qualitative content analysis> organizes its interpretation. These methods can develop an explanation or refine a construct rather than estimate a population prevalence.

Recruit participants whose experiences address the research question, for example by <purposive sampling> across different teaching roles, and explain the basis for the sample's adequacy. Record and transcribe material where appropriate and agreed. Describe how the analysis moved from material to codes, interpretations and claims; support interpretations with contextualized, confidentiality-preserving extracts. Consider contrary cases, alternative interpretations and the researcher's role through <researcher reflexivity>. The criteria for quality should fit the analytic approach: agreement between coders is useful in some structured coding studies, but is not a universal validity criterion for every interpretive method. <Informed consent> and protection against identification are especially important when detailed accounts concern identifiable colleagues.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

The **<normality assumption> concerns the model's error distribution, not necessarily the pooled raw observations**. In an independent-groups <analysis of variance>, errors around each group <mean> are modeled as following a <normal distribution>; combining groups with genuinely different <means> can produce a nonnormal pooled distribution even when this assumption holds. In a <paired t-test>, the relevant distribution is that of within-pair differences. <Linear regression> conventionally assumes normal errors conditional on the predictors for exact small-sample <Student t-tests> and <F-tests>.<The one-way ANOVA model and assumptions by National Institute of Standards and Technology>[Handbook Section 7.4.3.2, fixed-effects errors]{ref}

Inspect suitable within-group or fitted-model <regression residuals> using a <quantile-quantile plot> and examine outliers and asymmetry. A formal normality test is not a decisive automatic gatekeeper: with small samples it may miss important departures, while large samples can detect minor departures. Approximate robustness depends on the <sample size>, balance, tail behavior and intended inference. The <central limit theorem> concerns sampling distributions and does not make every raw observation normal or repair dependence. Depending on the question, a justified transformation, a better-fitting probability model, robust inference or an appropriate <bootstrap> may be preferable. The <normality assumption>, independence and equal <variance> are distinct assumptions.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

**<Research ethics> governs how a worthwhile question is investigated while respecting those involved.** The <British Psychological Society>'s framework emphasizes respect for autonomy, privacy and dignity, scientific integrity, social responsibility, and maximizing benefit while minimizing harm. Seek appropriate independent ethical review before recruitment and assess risks in the actual study context.<Code of Human Research Ethics by British Psychological Society>[2021, Sections 2–3 and 13: principles, risk and ethics review]{ref}

Give understandable information, seek voluntary <informed consent>, explain the practical limits of withdrawal, and avoid pressure arising from a teacher–student or employer–employee relationship. Limit identifiable information, control access and distinguish confidential storage from genuine anonymity. Any justified withholding or <research deception> needs proportionate review and appropriate <research debriefing>; debriefing does not undo every possible harm. Explain relevant procedures and offer questions and support consistent with the study's risks.<Code of Human Research Ethics by British Psychological Society>[2021, Section 4: valid consent; informing participants and ongoing consent]{ref}

In the lecturer <questionnaire> study, make participation independent of employment evaluation and prevent identifiable responses reaching supervisors. Ethical reporting also requires honest analysis, disclosure of limitations and proportionate data sharing: <open science> does not require exposing confidential participant records.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

**TODO: the model's explained-variance value is absent.** For an ordinary least-squares <linear regression> with an <intercept>, the <coefficient of determination> is $R^2=1-SS_{\mathrm{residual}}/SS_{\mathrm{total}}$. Its value multiplied by one hundred is the corresponding percentage of sample variation explained by that fitted model. Read the actual reported $R^2$ and distinguish it from the <adjusted coefficient of determination>; neither is a verified prediction-performance estimate on new data. Without the missing summary or sums of squares, no numerical percentage can be recovered.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

**TODO: the coefficients and their numerical $t$ and $p$ entries cannot be recovered.** The coefficient estimate gives a modeled difference relative to the stated reference coding; its <standard error> quantifies uncertainty, and the usual coefficient test uses $t=\widehat\beta/\operatorname{SE}(\widehat\beta)$ for a zero-coefficient <null hypothesis>. The <p-value> requires the appropriate reference distribution and <statistical degrees of freedom>. In a conventional R coefficient table an `Estimate` is usually an unstandardized <regression coefficient>, not automatically a <standardized regression coefficient>; the table and model documentation must establish which meaning of $\beta$ is intended. These definitions supply no missing numerical values.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

**TODO: directions, magnitudes and inferential conclusions require the unavailable coefficient table, coding and model specification.** For illustration of interpretation only, let $D$ and $S$ be binary indicators whose reference levels are explicitly defined. If an ordinary linear model includes both factors and their <interaction>, its conditional mean has the form
$$
\operatorname E(Y\mid D,S)=\beta_0+\beta_DD+\beta_SS+\beta_{DS}DS.
$$
Then the distance difference is $\beta_D$ at the reference size and $\beta_D+\beta_{DS}$ at the other size; the <interaction> is the difference between those differences. If the <interaction> is omitted, the model assumes a common distance difference across sizes. These are conditional model meanings, not a claim that the missing analysis used this model or that either predictor had an observed effect. The uncertainty of a coefficient sum also involves their <covariance>, not merely their separate <p-values>.

{scope}

===== Solution

If the study aims to test whether the predictors combine additively but only an additive model was fitted, an adequately supported, prespecified <interaction> comparison may be relevant. Respect <multiple testing> when several comparisons are examined.

Check model fit, influential observations and <heteroscedasticity>; a response bounded between zero and one thousand may require particular care near its bounds, but that scale alone does not choose a replacement model. If observations are dependent, the analysis must account for that structure. Such checks are possible recommendations; the missing supplementary sheet prevents identifying which problems, if any, actually occurred.

=== 6
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

**Treat the forty-person convenience sample as a pilot, then develop and validate the measure in its intended population.** Start by defining whose organizational ability the <questionnaire> measures and who answers it: lecturer self-reports and student ratings are different measurements. Specify the relevant activities and context. Discuss the candidate items with lecturers and appropriate users, seek expert review, and use <cognitive interviewing> to find ambiguous wording, double-barreled items and irrelevant content. This establishes <content validity> rather than assuming that correlations alone identify the intended construct.

Recruit a larger, better-targeted sample covering relevant roles, disciplines and institutions. A <convenience sample> of adults who cannot meaningfully judge lecturers' organization is not made suitable simply by increasing its size. Plan the <sample size> using plausible <factor loadings>, <communalities>, number of dimensions and indicators, response distributions, missingness and required estimation stability, preferably with simulation. Forty observations for sixteen items offer limited grounds for a stable generalizable structure; **no universal participants-per-item cutoff makes this analysis automatically impossible or automatically adequate**. If students rate the same lecturers, recognize that the ratings are clustered rather than independent lecturer-level observations.

Check missing responses, item distributions, scoring direction and <correlations>, identifying redundant or poorly functioning items. If the underlying dimensions are not already well specified, use <exploratory factor analysis> to model shared variation among items. <Principal component analysis> instead forms combinations explaining total observed variation; it is not the same latent measurement model. Choose a suitable common-factor estimator, such as <principal axis factoring>, or <maximum likelihood estimation> when its assumptions are credible. For ordered response categories, consider an ordinal estimator and <polychoric correlations>, while checking whether category counts support their estimation. Do not treat those correlations as assumption-free.<Principal Components and Exploratory Factor Analysis with SPSS by University of California, Los Angeles>[Common Factor Analysis; comparison with principal components; extraction methods]{ref}

Choose the number of factors using <parallel analysis>, the <scree plot>, substantive interpretation and the quality of the resulting model, rather than an eigenvalue-one rule alone. <Parallel analysis> compares the observed <eigenvalues> with a reference generated from uncorrelated data of comparable dimensions; its implementation must match the chosen correlation and extraction approach.<A rationale and test for the number of factors in factor analysis by John L. Horn>[1965, abstract: random-variable procedure to assess sampling contributions to latent roots]{ref} Use <oblique factor rotation> if organizational dimensions can plausibly correlate. Inspect <factor loadings>, cross-loadings, <communalities>, factor correlations and model discrepancies, and retain enough substantively relevant indicators for each dimension. Do not repeatedly delete items solely to improve numerical fit at the expense of coverage.

After developing the structure, test it using <confirmatory factor analysis> in an independently recruited validation sample, with the proposed structure fixed beforehand. Splitting the existing forty participants would not solve the shortage of information. Evaluate model fit and uncertainty, <measurement reliability>, <test-retest reliability> where stability is intended, and evidence for <construct validity> using related, distinct and relevant behavioral measures. Check <measurement invariance> before comparing scores across groups. Report alternative plausible models, all item decisions, sampling limitations and the distinction between an initial exploratory instrument and an independently validated measure.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

**Make the development process inspectable and separate exploratory decisions from independent confirmation.** Create a <preregistration> for the validation stage before examining its outcome-relevant data, specifying recruitment, stopping rules, exclusions, missing-data treatment, item scoring, estimator, factor structure and criteria for evaluating it. The exploratory stage can also have a recorded protocol, including a planned <parallel analysis> rule; label data-driven item revisions and model changes as exploratory rather than disguising them as prior predictions. Document deviations and their reasons.<Preregistration by Center for Open Science>[What is Preregistration?; planned versus unplanned work; FAQ on detail and prior data knowledge]{ref}

Keep an immutable copy of the collected data, record cleaning steps, and provide a data dictionary, final item wording, scoring instructions and executable analysis code. Record software and package versions, random seeds for simulations or <bootstrap> calculations, and the steps needed to regenerate tables from the permitted data. These support <computational reproducibility>; an independently collected validation study or <scientific replication> addresses a different question.

Share appropriately de-identified data where consent and re-identification risk permit, with controlled access or a clear restriction statement where they do not. Protect lecturer and rater identities and disclose the pilot's <convenience sampling> and all item-selection steps. Report inconclusive and unfavorable findings as well as favorable ones. A <registered report> can obtain peer review of the validation plan before its results are known, reducing outcome-dependent publication incentives.<Registered Reports by Center for Open Science>[Details and Workflow: Stage 1 review, in-principle acceptance, Stage 2 reporting]{ref}

=== 7
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

Use **<cluster analysis>** to discover groups of observations with similar profiles when no correct group labels have been supplied. For example, it could group lecturers by patterns of reported organizational behavior. Define the analysis unit and features, handle missing data, and choose scaling and a meaningful similarity measure; otherwise a feature with large numerical units may dominate the groups.

<K-means clustering> partitions observations around centers, whereas <agglomerative hierarchical clustering> produces nested groupings represented by a <dendrogram>. Their results include group membership and interpretable profiles, conditional on these choices. Check stability across resampling and reasonable analysis choices and, when possible, in new data. **A clustering does not prove that natural psychological types exist**: an algorithm can partition a continuous distribution. Its units are cases; <factor analysis> instead primarily models shared variation among measured variables.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Use **<logistic regression>** when the <response variable> is binary, for example whether a lecturer missed a deadline. It relates the event's <log odds> to one or more predictors, producing fitted <probabilities> between zero and one. Exponentiating a coefficient gives an <odds ratio> for a specified predictor change, conditional on the other model terms. An <odds ratio> is not generally a ratio of probabilities; the corresponding probability change depends on the starting probability and other predictors.

Report the outcome coding, estimated associations with <confidence intervals>, and relevant model checks. Sparse events, <complete separation> and dependent observations can make ordinary estimates or inferential procedures unreliable; repeated observations may need a <generalized linear mixed model>. For prediction, evaluate calibration and discrimination on data not used to tune the model. An adjusted association in an <observational study> does not automatically identify a causal effect.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Use **<factor analysis>** when several observed measurements may reflect fewer unobserved dimensions, such as related <questionnaire> items about planning, prioritization and record keeping. A common-factor model separates shared variation represented by <latent factors> from item-specific variation. <Factor loadings> describe how measurements relate to those <latent factors>; <communalities> summarize their shared-variance contributions.

<Exploratory factor analysis> investigates a structure not yet fixed; <confirmatory factor analysis> evaluates prespecified measurement restrictions, preferably in independent data. Extraction, factor count and <factor rotation> affect interpretation, so report them and justify the sample and measurement assumptions. A statistically convenient factor solution does not alone establish <construct validity> or demonstrate the existence of a literal psychological mechanism. The distinction from <principal component analysis> is the common-versus-total-variation objective, and from <cluster analysis> the modeling of measurement relationships rather than the grouping of cases.

=== 8
{scope}

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

**<Bootstrapping> estimates sampling uncertainty by repeating an analysis on resampled data.** In the ordinary nonparametric <bootstrap>, draw observations with replacement from the empirical sample, retaining its size, and recalculate the statistic many times. The resulting distribution can estimate a <standard error>, assess bias or form a justified <bootstrap confidence interval>. It is useful when an analytic sampling distribution is difficult to obtain.

The resampling unit must match the design: keep paired measurements together, and resample independent clusters when observations are clustered. Resampling individual ratings as if they were independent lecturers would misrepresent uncertainty. The ordinary <bootstrap> assumes the sample and chosen resampling scheme adequately represent the relevant data-generating variation; it does not repair <selection bias>, guarantee accurate inference from a tiny sample, or succeed for every statistic. Specify the interval method and computational settings, since a simple percentile interval is not universally appropriate.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

**<Preregistration> creates a time-stamped record of research questions and planned methods before the relevant results are examined.** Specify the design, recruitment and stopping rules, outcome definitions, exclusions, principal tests, model choices and any conditional decision rules. For existing data, disclose prior access and what was already known. A sufficiently precise record helps readers distinguish prior predictions from choices prompted by the results.<Preregistration by Center for Open Science>[Definition; When Can You Preregister?; FAQ on existing data]{ref}

It does not prohibit useful changes or new discoveries: report departures, their timing and reasons, and distinguish <exploratory analysis> from <confirmatory analysis>. A vague or selectively followed registration does not solve selective reporting, and <preregistration> cannot repair a poor measure or inadequate <design of experiments>. A <registered report> adds pre-result peer review and conditional publication acceptance, which are not implied by registration alone.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

**<HARKing> means presenting a hypothesis generated after inspecting results as though it had been specified beforehand.** For example, after trying several possible factor structures in the lecturer data, a researcher might claim that the best-fitting structure was their original prediction. The problem is the misrepresented evidential history, not the act of discovering a pattern.

Such undisclosed selection makes apparent confirmation too persuasive because the reported test does not represent the whole search that produced the hypothesis. Label new ideas as exploratory, record prior plans through <preregistration>, disclose the search and evaluate the resulting prediction in independent data. <Registered reports> and transparent reporting can reduce incentives for that misrepresentation while preserving legitimate exploratory work.<Registered Reports by Center for Open Science>[Details: HARKing and outcome-dependent reporting; Scientific Creativity and Exploration]{ref}
