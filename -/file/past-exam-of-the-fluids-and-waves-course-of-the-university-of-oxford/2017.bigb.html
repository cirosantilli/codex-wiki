= Solution
{parent=i}

For a scalar fluid property $f(\mathbf x,t)$, the <Eulerian time derivative> $\partial_t f$ measures change at a fixed <Eulerian coordinate>. The <material derivative>, also called the Lagrangian time derivative, measures change while following one <fluid element>. If its <Lagrangian trajectory> is $\mathbf X(\mathbf a,t)$, where $\mathbf a$ is its fixed <Lagrangian coordinate>, then $\partial_t\mathbf X=\mathbf u(\mathbf X,t)$.

The <chain rule> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{Df}{Dt}=\frac d{dt}f(\mathbf X(\mathbf a,t),t)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla f.}
$$
The last term accounts for entering a region where the property differs. For example, a spatially varying but time-independent <velocity field> has zero <Eulerian time derivative>, while a moving <fluid element> can have nonzero <material acceleration>. The same formula applies componentwise to a <vector field>.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Let $V(t)$ be a smoothly moving material <volume>, carried by a smooth <velocity field> $\mathbf u$, and let $f$ be continuously differentiable. The material-volume form of the <Reynolds transport theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dt}\int_{V(t)}f\,dV=\int_{V(t)}\left(\frac{Df}{Dt}+f\nabla\cdot\mathbf u\right)dV.}
$$
Equivalently, the <divergence theorem> gives
$$
\frac d{dt}\int_{V(t)}f\,dV=\int_{V(t)}\partial_t f\,dV+\int_{\partial V(t)}f\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n\,dS,
$$
where $\mathbf n$ is the outward <unit normal vector>. The second integral measures the <flux> carried across the instantaneous boundary by its motion.

For the proof, use the particle map $\mathbf x=\mathbf X(\mathbf a,t)$ from $V(0)$, with positive <Jacobian determinant> $J=\det(\partial\mathbf X/\partial\mathbf a)$. <Euler's volume identity> is
$$
\partial_tJ=J\nabla\cdot\mathbf u.
$$
It is the <Jacobian evolution of a smooth flow>: if $F=\partial\mathbf X/\partial\mathbf a$, differentiation gives $F_t=(\nabla\mathbf u)F$, and differentiation of the <determinant> gives $J_t=J\operatorname{tr}(F^{-1}F_t)=J\nabla\cdot\mathbf u$. The <change of variables> formula now moves the integral to the fixed reference <volume>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac d{dt}\int_{V(t)}f\,dV
&=\frac d{dt}\int_{V(0)}f(\mathbf X(\mathbf a,t),t)J\,d\mathbf a\\
&=\int_{V(0)}\left(\frac{Df}{Dt}J+fJ_t\right)d\mathbf a\\
&=\int_{V(t)}\left(\frac{Df}{Dt}+f\nabla\cdot\mathbf u\right)dV.
\end{aligned}
$$
Finally, $Df/Dt+f\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=\partial_t f+\nabla\cdot(f\mathbf u)$, giving the equivalent boundary form.

= Solution

Write $\rho$ for the <mass density>. A material <volume> contains the same <fluid elements> at all times, so <conservation of mass> requires
$$
0=\frac d{dt}\int_{V(t)}\rho\,dV=\int_{V(t)}\left(\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u)\right)dV,
$$
by the <Reynolds transport theorem>. Since the initial material <volume> can be chosen arbitrarily, continuity of the integrand forces it to vanish pointwise. Thus the <continuity equation> is
$$
\boxed{\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u)=0,\qquad\frac{D\rho}{Dt}+\rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0.}
$$
For positive <mass density>, the <incompressibility condition> $D\rho/Dt=0$ reduces this to $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$. <Mass conservation> itself does not require <incompressible flow>.

Work away from the <line source>, so $r>0$. In <polar coordinates>, $u_r=Q/(2\pi r)$ and $u_\theta=0$. The supplied <divergence> and <curl> formulas give
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=\frac1r\partial_r\left(\frac Q{2\pi}\right)=0,
\qquad
(\nabla\times\mathbf u)_z=\frac1r\left[\partial_r(ru_\theta)-\partial_\theta u_r\right]=0.
$$
Hence **the field defines an <incompressible flow> and an <irrotational flow> for $r>0$**. The restriction matters: the outward <volume flux> through any enclosing <circle> is
$$
\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{Q}{2\pi r}r\,d\theta=Q.
$$
The origin is the excluded injection point. Applying the <divergence theorem> to a disk through that <singularity> would violate its smoothness hypothesis.

= Solution

A <velocity potential> satisfies $\mathbf u=\nabla\phi$. Integration of $\phi_r=Q/(2\pi r)$ and $r^{-1}\phi_\theta=0$ gives
$$
\boxed{\phi=\frac Q{2\pi}\log r.}
$$
Choose the <streamfunction> convention $u=\psi_y$, $v=-\psi_x$. Its <streamfunction in polar coordinates> satisfies $u_r=r^{-1}\psi_\theta$ and $u_\theta=-\psi_r$, so
$$
\boxed{\psi=\frac Q{2\pi}\theta,\qquad w(z)=\phi+i\psi=\frac Q{2\pi}\log z.}
$$
Additive constants are immaterial. This <complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow> is defined on a chosen <logarithm branch>. Its <complex velocity> $w'=Q/(2\pi z)=u-iv$ is single-valued away from the origin. Continuing once around the <line source> increases $\psi$ by $Q$, consistently with its nonzero <volume flux>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Set $f(z)=\cosh(\pi z/a)$. The <hyperbolic cosine> addition formula gives
$$
f(x+iy)=\cosh\frac{\pi x}a\cos\frac{\pi y}a+i\sinh\frac{\pi x}a\sin\frac{\pi y}a.
$$
For $x>0$ and $0<y<a$, both factors in the imaginary part are positive. Thus the image lies in the <upper half-plane>. The three walls map onto the entire <real axis>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
y=0&:\quad f\in[1,\infty),\\
x=0&:\quad f\in[-1,1],\\
y=a&:\quad f\in(-\infty,-1].
\end{aligned}
$$
Also $f'(z)=(\pi/a)\sinh(\pi z/a)\ne0$ in the interior.

To verify that this is a one-to-one <conformal map> onto the whole <upper half-plane>, factor it through $s=e^{\pi z/a}$. This first map is one-to-one onto $|s|>1$, $\operatorname{Im}s>0$, and $f(z)=(s+s^{-1})/2$. Given any $\operatorname{Im}\zeta>0$, the roots of $s^2-2\zeta s+1=0$ have product one. Neither lies on $|s|=1$, since that would make $\zeta$ real. Exactly one root therefore has $|s|>1$, and
$$
\operatorname{Im}\zeta=\tfrac12(1-|s|^{-2})\operatorname{Im}s
$$
forces that root into the upper half of the exterior disk. It has a unique preimage $z=(a/\pi)(\log|s|+i\arg s)$ with $0<\arg s<\pi$. This proves both surjectivity and injectivity. The construction is the <hyperbolic-cosine map of a half-strip>.

The source location $c$ is complex, with $\operatorname{Re}c>0$ and $0<\operatorname{Im}c<a$. Put $C=f(c)$, so $\operatorname{Im}C>0$. The <method of images> places an equal <line source> at $\bar C$ below the <real axis>:
$$
W(\zeta)=\frac Q{2\pi}\left[\log(\zeta-C)+\log(\zeta-\bar C)\right].
$$
For real $\zeta$, the product of the two logarithm arguments is $|\zeta-C|^2>0$. Hence the <streamfunction> is constant along the wall, on locally consistent branches of the <complex logarithm>, and there is no normal <velocity>. Pulling back gives the requested <complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow>:
$$
\boxed{w(z)=\frac Q{2\pi}\left[\log\!\left(\cosh\frac{\pi z}a-\cosh\frac{\pi c}a\right)+\log\!\left(\cosh\frac{\pi z}a-\cosh\frac{\pi\bar c}a\right)\right].}
$$
This is the <line source in a closed-end semi-infinite strip>, with no extra imposed flow. Since $f'(c)\ne0$, near the physical source $f(z)-f(c)=f'(c)(z-c)+O((z-c)^2)$. Thus $w=(Q/(2\pi))\log(z-c)+$ a regular term, verifying that the source strength remains $Q$ under the <conformal map>. The image source has no preimage inside the physical strip. Finally, as $x\to\infty$, $w(z)\sim Qz/a+$ a constant, so the far-field <velocity> is $(Q/a,0)$ and the channel carries exactly the injected <volume flux> $Q$.

Orient the planar <material curve> $C(t)$ counterclockwise, and let $\mathbf t$ and $\mathbf n$ be its unit tangent and outward <unit normal vector>. The <circulation> and outward <volume flux> are
$$
\boxed{\Gamma(t)=\oint_{C(t)}\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf t\,ds,\qquad M(t)=\oint_{C(t)}\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf n\,ds.}
$$
For $\mathbf u=(u,v)$ and this orientation, $\mathbf t\,ds=(dx,dy)$ and $\mathbf n\,ds=(dy,-dx)$, giving
$$
\Gamma=\oint_C(u\,dx+v\,dy),\qquad M=\oint_C(u\,dy-v\,dx).
$$
Here $M$ is the instantaneous <flux> of the <velocity field>, rather than transport relative to the moving <material curve>; that relative transport is zero. If the enclosed region contains no source and the <velocity field> is smooth there, <incompressibility> and the planar <divergence theorem> imply $M=0$.

{parent=a}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For smooth <inviscid flow> of constant <mass density> under a conservative <body force>, <Kelvin's circulation theorem> states that the <circulation> around any closed <material curve> is conserved:
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\Gamma}{dt}=0.}
$$
The constant-<mass density> assumption is the usual homogeneous <incompressible flow> setting here. Merely requiring $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$ in a fluid of varying <mass density> would not by itself make the pressure term below a <gradient>.

Parameterize the <material curve> by a periodic particle label $s\in[0,1]$, writing $\mathbf r_t(s,t)=\mathbf u(\mathbf r(s,t),t)$. Differentiate its <line integral>, keeping $s$ fixed:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d\Gamma}{dt}
&=\int_0^1\left[\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}\cdot\mathbf r_s+\mathbf u\cdot\partial_s\mathbf r_t\right]ds\\
&=\oint_{C(t)}\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}\cdot d\mathbf r+\int_0^1\partial_s\left(\frac12|\mathbf u|^2\right)ds\\
&=\oint_{C(t)}\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}\cdot d\mathbf r.
\end{aligned}
$$
The second term vanishes by periodicity. If the conservative <body force> per unit <mass> is $-\nabla\Omega$, the <Euler momentum equation> gives
$$
\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}=-\frac1\rho\nabla p-\nabla\Omega=-\nabla(p/\rho+\Omega).
$$
Its <line integral> around a <closed curve> is zero, proving the claim. The <velocity field> must remain smooth on the moving curve, which cannot pass through a <line vortex> singularity during this argument. No assumption of <irrotational flow> was needed.

= Solution

Define the <line vortex> strength $\kappa$ by $\mathbf u=\kappa\mathbf e_\theta/(2\pi r)$, with $\kappa>0$ meaning counterclockwise motion. On an enclosing <circle> of radius $R$,
$$
\Gamma=\int_0^{2\pi}\frac\kappa{2\pi R}R\,d\theta=\kappa,\qquad M=0,
$$
because the <velocity> is tangent to the <circle>. For any simple positively oriented enclosing curve, the same results follow by <Stokes theorem> and the <divergence theorem> on the region between that curve and a small inner <circle>. Both <curl> and <divergence> vanish throughout that intervening <annulus>. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\Gamma=\kappa,\qquad M=0.}
$$
Equivalently, $\Gamma=(\kappa/(2\pi))\oint d\theta$ and the total angle change is $2\pi$. If strength is instead denoted by $K$ in $u_\theta=K/r$, the answer is $\Gamma=2\pi K$. An enclosing disk through the singular origin cannot be used to infer zero <circulation> from zero local <vorticity>.

= Solution

For planar <incompressible flow> and <irrotational flow>, combine a <velocity potential> and a <streamfunction> into the <complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow>:
$$
\boxed{w(z)=\phi(x,y)+i\psi(x,y),\qquad z=x+iy.}
$$
The chosen conventions give
$$
u=\phi_x=\psi_y,\qquad v=\phi_y=-\psi_x.
$$
These are the <Cauchy-Riemann equations>, so $w$ is a <holomorphic function> wherever the flow is regular and a consistent local potential is chosen. Its <complex derivative> is the <complex velocity>
$$
\boxed{\frac{dw}{dz}=\phi_x+i\psi_x=u-iv.}
$$
A spatially <uniform flow> has $w=(U-iV)z+$ a constant. The following <contour integral> identities also apply to nonuniform <potential flow>, including the specified <line vortex>. A <complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow> may be multivalued around a source or vortex even though its derivative is single-valued.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Multiply the <complex velocity> by the complex displacement:
$$
\frac{dw}{dz}\,dz=(u-iv)(dx+i\,dy)=(u\,dx+v\,dy)+i(u\,dy-v\,dx).
$$
The real term is the <circulation> integrand. With a counterclockwise curve, $\mathbf n\,ds=(dy,-dx)$ is outward, so the imaginary term is the outward <volume flux> integrand. Integrating gives
$$
\boxed{\oint_{C(t)}\frac{dw}{dz}\,dz=\Gamma(t)+iM(t).}
$$
This also fixes the sign of $M$: a positive <line source> has $w'=Q/(2\pi z)$ and the integral is $iQ$, so its outward <volume flux> is $Q$.

{parent=b}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Assume $q(z)=w'(z)$ is single-valued and a <holomorphic function> between $C$ and a sufficiently large enclosing <circle> $|z|=R$. There are no fluid singularities in that region, so <Cauchy's integral theorem> permits <contour deformation>:
$$
\Gamma+iM=\oint_Cq(z)\,dz=\oint_{|z|=R}q(z)\,dz.
$$
Use the exterior <Laurent series> about zero, valid for $|z|$ larger than every finite singularity:
$$
q(z)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_nz^n,\qquad a_{-1}=b_1.
$$
It converges uniformly on the chosen <circle>, permitting termwise <contour integration>. For integer $n$, parameterization by $z=Re^{i\theta}$ gives
$$
\oint_{|z|=R}z^n\,dz=iR^{n+1}\int_0^{2\pi}e^{i(n+1)\theta}\,d\theta
=\begin{cases}2\pi i,&n=-1,\\0,&n\ne-1.\end{cases}
$$
Only the $z^{-1}$ coefficient survives, proving
$$
\boxed{\Gamma+iM=2\pi i b_1.}
$$
Thus if $b_1=\alpha+i\beta$, then $\Gamma=-2\pi\beta$ and $M=2\pi\alpha$. A <uniform flow> contributes a constant to $q$ and changes neither integral. The <Laurent series> here is an exterior expansion; it need not converge in a punctured neighborhood of zero if other singularities intervene.

For the same counterclockwise <line vortex> strength $\kappa$, its local <velocity potential> and <streamfunction> are $\phi=\kappa\theta/(2\pi)$ and $\psi=-\kappa\log r/(2\pi)$. Consequently
$$
w(z)=-\frac{i\kappa}{2\pi}\log z,\qquad w'(z)=-\frac{i\kappa}{2\pi z},\qquad b_1=-\frac{i\kappa}{2\pi}.

Substitution gives
$$
\boxed{2\pi i b_1=\kappa=\Gamma+iM,\qquad M=0,}

in agreement with the direct <circulation> and <volume flux> calculation. The sign of the imaginary coefficient is essential for a positive counterclockwise <circulation>.

Represent a <time-harmonic wave> by $A\cos(kx-\omega t+\delta)$, with $k,\omega>0$. The <angular frequency> $\omega$ is the rate at which the <wave phase> changes at a fixed position, measured in radians per unit time. The <wavenumber> $k$ is its spatial rate of change, measured in radians per unit length. One full spatial cycle defines the <wavelength> $\lambda$, and one temporal cycle defines the <wave period> $T$, so
$$
\boxed{k=\frac{2\pi}\lambda,\qquad\omega=\frac{2\pi}T.}
$$
The ordinary <frequency> is $f=1/T=\omega/(2\pi)$; the paper's symbol $\omega$ denotes <angular frequency>.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

A fixed <wave phase> satisfies $kx-\omega t=$ a constant. Differentiating gives the <phase velocity>, called the <wave speed> here:
$$
\boxed{c=\frac\omega k.}
$$
It is the speed of an individual crest or other fixed-phase point. The <group velocity> is
$$
\boxed{c_g=\frac{d\omega}{dk},}
$$
evaluated on the relevant <dispersion relation>. To see its meaning, superpose two equal-<amplitude> <waves> with nearby $(k,\omega)$:
$$
\cos(k_1x-\omega_1t)+\cos(k_2x-\omega_2t)
=2\cos\frac{\Delta k\,x-\Delta\omega\,t}{2}\cos(\bar kx-\bar\omega t).
$$
The slowly varying <wave envelope> moves with speed $\Delta\omega/\Delta k$, tending to $d\omega/dk$ as the two components approach one another. Thus <group velocity> describes a narrow <wave packet> and, for conservative linear <surface gravity waves> in still water, their <energy> transport. A single <time-harmonic wave> has no distinguished packet envelope. When $c=c(k)$, the equivalent relation is $c_g=c+k\,dc/dk$.

= Solution

Treat the <free surface> as the material level set $F(x,y,t)=y-\eta(x,t)=0$. The <kinematic boundary condition> $DF/Dt=0$, with $\mathbf u=\nabla\phi$, gives
$$
-\eta_t-\phi_x\eta_x+\phi_y=0,
\qquad
\boxed{\phi_y=\eta_t+\phi_x\eta_x\quad\text{on }y=\eta.}
$$
This says that fluid initially on the <free surface> remains on it.

For constant <mass density> $\rho$, the <Euler momentum equation> under <gravity> is $D\mathbf u/Dt=-\nabla p/\rho-g\mathbf e_y$. Since the flow is <irrotational>, $(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=\nabla(|\mathbf u|^2/2)$. Integration in space therefore gives the <unsteady Bernoulli equation>
$$
\phi_t+\frac12|\nabla\phi|^2+\frac p\rho+gy=B(t).
$$
At the <free surface>, $p=P_a$ and $y=\eta$. Adding a function $G(t)$ to the <velocity potential> leaves the <velocity field> unchanged; choose $G'=P_a/\rho-B(t)$. With this choice the surface <dynamic boundary condition for an inviscid interface> becomes
$$
\boxed{\phi_t+\frac12|\nabla\phi|^2+g\eta=0\quad\text{on }y=\eta.}
$$
The constant atmospheric <pressure> has been absorbed into this choice of potential.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Linearize about fluid at rest, with mean <free surface> $y=0$ and mean bottom $y=-d$. Regard $a$, $\eta$ and the perturbation <velocity potential> as first-order quantities. A <Taylor expansion> from $y=\eta$ to $y=0$ produces products of first-order quantities, as do $\phi_x\eta_x$ and $|\nabla\phi|^2$. Discarding these second-order terms gives the <linearized free-surface boundary conditions>
$$
\boxed{\phi_y=\eta_t,\qquad\phi_t+g\eta=0\quad(y=0).}
$$
Equivalently, differentiate the second condition in time and substitute the first to obtain $\phi_{tt}+g\phi_y=0$ at $y=0$.

The bottom is also impermeable and moving. Its exact <kinematic boundary condition> follows from $D(y-h)/Dt=0$:
$$
\phi_y=h_t+\phi_xh_x\quad(y=h).
$$
Here $h_t=-akc\cos(k(x-ct))$, while $\phi_xh_x$ and the change from evaluating $\phi_y$ at $h$ to evaluating it at $-d$ are second order. Hence
$$
\boxed{\phi_y(x,-d,t)=-akc\cos(k(x-ct)).}
$$
The interior <Laplace equation> remains $\phi_{xx}+\phi_{yy}=0$ on $-d<y<0$. This <linearization> requires small slopes and small resulting excursions as well as $a\ll d$; close to <resonance> a small prescribed bottom displacement can produce a response too large for it.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Seek the periodic response of a <surface gravity wave forced by a traveling bottom>, with the same <wavenumber> $k$ and <angular frequency> $kc$ as the bottom. The linear equations have constant coefficients in $x,t$, so they preserve each such harmonic. There is no dissipative phase lag in this model; away from <resonance>, the surface response is in phase or in opposition with the bottom. Thus write $\eta=b\sin\theta$, $\theta=k(x-ct)$, with a signed coefficient $b$. Its physical <amplitude> is $|b|$. Arbitrary additional free <surface gravity waves> depend on initial data; the paper's expression selects the forced traveling component.

For $k>0$, $c\ne0$, use $\phi=F(y)\cos\theta$. The <Laplace equation> gives $F''-k^2F=0$. At $y=0$, the two <linearized free-surface boundary conditions> imply
$$
kcF(0)+gb=0,\qquad F'(0)=-kcb.
$$
These determine both constants in the vertical solution:
$$
\boxed{\phi(x,y,t)=-\frac b{kc}\left[g\cosh(ky)+kc^2\sinh(ky)\right]\cos(k(x-ct)).}
$$
Its vertical derivative at the bottom is
$$
\phi_y(x,-d,t)=\left[\frac{gb}{c}\sinh(kd)-kcb\cosh(kd)\right]\cos\theta.
$$
Matching it to $-akc\cos\theta$ and multiplying by $c$ gives
$$
b\left[kc^2\cosh(kd)-g\sinh(kd)\right]=akc^2,
$$
and therefore
$$
\boxed{b=\frac{akc^2}{kc^2\cosh(kd)-g\sinh(kd)}.}
$$
An equivalent expression for the <velocity potential>, which makes the bottom condition immediate, is
$$
\phi=\left[D\cosh(k(y+d))-ac\sinh(k(y+d))\right]\cos\theta,\qquad
D=\frac c{\sinh(kd)}\left[a\cosh(kd)-b\right].
$$
Let $\omega_f=kc$ be the forcing <angular frequency>, and let $\omega_0^2=gk\tanh(kd)$ be the natural <dispersion relation> from <Airy wave theory>. Then
$$
\frac ba=\frac{\omega_f^2\operatorname{sech}(kd)}{\omega_f^2-\omega_0^2}.
$$
The denominator vanishes at $c^2=(g/k)\tanh(kd)$, when the forcing travels at the free-wave <phase velocity>. For nonzero forcing there is **no bounded periodic forced solution at <resonance>**; the undamped linear response develops secular growth. Below that speed $b<0$, giving a phase reversal, while above it $b>0$. At $c=0$ the bottom is stationary: the rest solution has $\phi=0$ and $\eta=0$, consistent with the zero limit of $b$, though the formula containing $1/c$ must be interpreted through that limit.

= Solution

Consider the nonresonant traveling forced solution, with $k>0$ and $c\ne0$. Put $x=\bar x+\xi$, $y=\bar y+\upsilon$, where $(\bar x,\bar y)$ is the mean position of a <fluid element>. At <linear order>, evaluate the <velocity field> at this mean position: substituting $\xi,\upsilon$ into its spatial arguments would change the already first-order <velocity> only at second order. With $\Theta=k(\bar x-ct)$, differentiation of the <velocity potential> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot\xi&=b\left[\frac gc\cosh(k\bar y)+kc\sinh(k\bar y)\right]\sin\Theta,\\
\dot\upsilon&=-b\left[\frac gc\sinh(k\bar y)+kc\cosh(k\bar y)\right]\cos\Theta.
\end{aligned}
$$
Since $\dot\Theta=-kc$, integration, with zero mean displacements, yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
\xi&=A(\bar y)\cos\Theta,&A(\bar y)&=b\left[\frac g{kc^2}\cosh(k\bar y)+\sinh(k\bar y)\right],\\
\upsilon&=B(\bar y)\sin\Theta,&B(\bar y)&=b\left[\frac g{kc^2}\sinh(k\bar y)+\cosh(k\bar y)\right].
\end{aligned}
$$
If both coefficients are nonzero, eliminating the <wave phase> proves that the <pathline> is an <ellipse>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{(x-\bar x)^2}{A(\bar y)^2}+\frac{(y-\bar y)^2}{B(\bar y)^2}=1.}
$$
Its <principal axes of an ellipse> are horizontal and vertical, with respective <semi-axes>
$$
\boxed{|A(\bar y)|,\qquad|B(\bar y)|.}
$$
Thus the full axis lengths are $2|A|$ and $2|B|$. The signed coefficients also determine the direction of traversal. The useful checks $B(0)=b$ and $B(-d)=a$ reproduce the vertical displacements of the <free surface> and bottom.

To identify the major axis without comparing the two expressions separately, use $\cosh^2s-\sinh^2s=1$:
$$
A^2-B^2=b^2\left[\left(\frac g{kc^2}\right)^2-1\right].
$$
Consequently the major axis is horizontal if $c^2<g/k$ and vertical if $c^2>g/k$; equality gives <circles>. If $A$ or $B$ vanishes at a particular depth, the first-order orbit degenerates to a line segment. These are <linearized particle orbits of a bottom-forced gravity wave>, not exact closed trajectories at all orders: a higher-order calculation may introduce a mean drift.
