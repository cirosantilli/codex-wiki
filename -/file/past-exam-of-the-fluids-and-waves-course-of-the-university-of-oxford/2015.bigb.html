= Solution
{parent=a}

Write the planar <velocity field> as $\mathbf u=(u,v,0)$, with $\mathbf k=(0,0,1)$. Expanding the <curl> gives
$$
\mathbf u=\nabla\times(\psi\mathbf k)=(\psi_y,-\psi_x,0),
\qquad \nabla\psi=\mathbf k\times\mathbf u=(-v,u,0).
$$
Consequently, for a piecewise smooth path $\gamma$ from the origin to $\mathbf x=(x,y)$,
$$
\boxed{\psi(\mathbf x)=\psi(\mathbf0)+\int_\gamma(\mathbf k\times\mathbf u)\cdot d\mathbf r
=\psi(\mathbf0)+\int_\gamma(u\,dy-v\,dx).}
$$
The arbitrary additive constant $\psi(\mathbf0)$ may be set to zero. The integrand is exactly $\nabla\psi\cdot d\mathbf r$, so the <fundamental theorem for line integrals> proves <path independence> immediately: the difference between two paths is the <line integral> of a <gradient> around a <closed curve>, which is zero.

One can also see the local integrability condition from <Green's theorem>:
$$
\oint(u\,dy-v\,dx)=\iint(u_x+v_y)\,dx\,dy=0,
$$
because the given representation enforces <incompressibility>. This second proof applies whenever the region between the paths lies in the regular fluid domain. The question already assumes a globally defined <streamfunction>; without that assumption, <incompressibility> alone would guarantee a global <streamfunction> on a <simply connected domain>, but a hole carrying nonzero <volume flux> could obstruct it.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Substitute the given <Stokes streamfunction> representation into the <divergence> in <spherical coordinates>. The mixed derivatives cancel:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla\cdot\mathbf u
&=\frac1{r^2}\partial_r\left(\frac{\Psi_\theta}{\sin\theta}\right)
+\frac1{r\sin\theta}\partial_\theta\left(-\frac{\Psi_r}{r}\right)\\
&=\frac{\Psi_{r\theta}-\Psi_{\theta r}}{r^2\sin\theta}=0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus <incompressibility> is automatic for a smooth <Stokes streamfunction>. Since this is an <axisymmetric flow> with no azimuthal <velocity>, the only possibly nonzero component of its <vorticity> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
(\nabla\times\mathbf u)_\varphi
&=\frac1r\left[\partial_r\left(-\frac{\Psi_r}{\sin\theta}\right)
-\partial_\theta\left(\frac{\Psi_\theta}{r^2\sin\theta}\right)\right]\\
&=-\frac1{r^3\sin\theta}\left[r^2\Psi_{rr}+\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta\left(\frac{\Psi_\theta}{\sin\theta}\right)\right].
\end{aligned}
$$
Define the differential operator in the <Stokes streamfunction equation for irrotational flow>
$$
\mathcal L\Psi=r^2\Psi_{rr}+\Psi_{\theta\theta}-\cot\theta\,\Psi_\theta.
$$
The stated equation is $\mathcal L\Psi=0$, and therefore
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0,\qquad\nabla\times\mathbf u=\mathbf0.}
$$
The <irrotational flow> has a <velocity potential> locally, and globally on a regular <simply connected domain>. Regularity of the physical <velocity field> supplies the limiting interpretation on the polar axis, where the coordinate expressions contain $\sin\theta$ in the denominator.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Put $s=a^2/r$ and define the image term $G(r,\theta)=(r/a)\Psi(s,\theta)$. The key step is to verify that inversion preserves the <Stokes streamfunction equation for irrotational flow>. Direct differentiation gives
$$
G_r=\frac1a(\Psi-s\Psi_s),\qquad
G_{rr}=\frac{s^2}{ar}\Psi_{ss},\qquad
G_{\theta\theta}-\cot\theta\,G_\theta
=\frac r a(\Psi_{\theta\theta}-\cot\theta\,\Psi_\theta),
$$
where the quantities on the right are evaluated at $(s,\theta)$. Hence
$$
\mathcal L_rG=\frac r a\left[s^2\Psi_{ss}+\Psi_{\theta\theta}-\cot\theta\,\Psi_\theta\right]
=\frac r a\mathcal L_s\Psi=0.
$$
By <linearity>, $\widetilde\Psi=\Psi-G$ obeys the same equation and describes an <incompressible flow> and an <irrotational flow> wherever the original exterior flow is regular.

On the <sphere>, $s=a$ and the two terms cancel identically:
$$
\widetilde\Psi(a,\theta)=0,\qquad
\boxed{\widetilde u_r(a,\theta)=\frac{\partial_\theta\widetilde\Psi(a,\theta)}{a^2\sin\theta}=0.}
$$
Thus the <sphere> is a stream surface and satisfies the <no-penetration boundary condition>. Tangential slip is allowed in this <potential flow> model. For every finite $r>a$, the image argument satisfies $0<s<a$, where $\Psi$ has no singularities. The prefactor $r/a$ is regular there. Therefore the construction creates **no new finite exterior singularities**; any original exterior singularities remain those of the prescribed incident flow. This is the <sphere theorem for a Stokes streamfunction>.

For the far-field claim, the stated bound gives
$$
|G(r,\theta)|\leq\frac r a c\frac{a^2}r=ca
\qquad(r\text{ sufficiently large}).
$$
One must compare <velocities>, rather than require the correction to the <Stokes streamfunction> to tend to zero. Smoothness at the original origin, together with $\Psi(0,\theta)=0$, gives $\Psi_s=O(1)$ and $\Psi_\theta=O(s)$ there. Thus $G_r=O(r^{-1})$ and $G_\theta=O(1)$, and the correction has
$$
\delta u_r=-\frac{G_\theta}{r^2\sin\theta}=O(r^{-2}),\qquad
\delta u_\theta=\frac{G_r}{r\sin\theta}=O(r^{-2}).
$$
These estimates hold away from the coordinate poles, with the regular-axis limits supplied by the original smooth physical flow. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\widetilde{\mathbf u}-\mathbf u\longrightarrow\mathbf0\quad(r\to\infty).}
$$
The far-field <velocity field> is unchanged. For a fully smooth incident <velocity field> at the origin, the usual regular expansion is stronger and makes the sphere disturbance decay as $r^{-3}$.

= Solution
{parent=d}

The incident <uniform flow> $U\mathbf e_z$ has spherical components $u_r=U\cos\theta$, $u_\theta=-U\sin\theta$. Integrating the <Stokes streamfunction> formulas, with the additive constant chosen to vanish on the axis, gives
$$
\Psi=\frac12Ur^2\sin^2\theta.
$$
It is regular at the origin. Applying the <sphere theorem for a Stokes streamfunction> yields
$$
\boxed{\widetilde\Psi(r,\theta)=\frac U2\left(r^2-\frac{a^3}r\right)\sin^2\theta.}
$$
The resulting <velocity> components are
$$
\widetilde u_r=U\left(1-\frac{a^3}{r^3}\right)\cos\theta,
\qquad
\widetilde u_\theta=-U\left(1+\frac{a^3}{2r^3}\right)\sin\theta.
$$
To find the <velocity potential>, integrate $\phi_r=\widetilde u_r$ and verify $r^{-1}\phi_\theta=\widetilde u_\theta$. This gives
$$
\boxed{\phi(r,\theta)=U\left(r+\frac{a^3}{2r^2}\right)\cos\theta+\text{constant}.}
$$
The first term is the incident <uniform flow>, and the second is a <potential dipole> located inside the excluded <sphere>. At $r=a$, the radial <velocity> vanishes, while the far-field <velocity> tends to $U\mathbf e_z$. The poles are <stagnation points>; the surface tangential speed reaches $3U/2$ at the equator. These formulas describe <uniform potential flow around a fixed sphere>.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Orient $C$ counterclockwise around the body. For an arc element $ds$, the body-outward <unit normal vector> obeys $\mathbf n\,ds=(dy,-dx)$. The <pressure> acts normally into the body, so its <force per unit length> in the suppressed third direction is
$$
d\mathbf F=-p\mathbf n\,ds=(-p\,dy,p\,dx).
$$
Integrating gives
$$
\boxed{F_x=-\oint_Cp\,dy,\qquad F_y=\oint_Cp\,dx.}
$$
Taking positive <torque> to mean counterclockwise about the origin, the infinitesimal moment is $dM=x\,dF_y-y\,dF_x=p(x\,dx+y\,dy)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{M=\oint_Cp(x\,dx+y\,dy).}
$$
All three expressions are per unit span. They are the normal-<pressure> contributions assumed by the inviscid model; a viscous <traction> would require extra terms. A constant <pressure> contributes zero net <force> and <torque> on a closed body, since $\oint dx=\oint dy=\oint d(x^2+y^2)=0$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Use the <complex velocity> $q(z)=w'(z)=u-iv$. For this steady, constant-<mass density>, <irrotational flow> with no <body force>, the applicable <Bernoulli equation> is
$$
p+\frac\rho2|q|^2=P_*,
$$
with a single constant $P_*$ throughout the connected regular fluid region. The body's impermeability makes the <velocity> tangent to $C$. Thus $q\,dz$ is real there, giving
$$
q\,dz=\bar q\,d\bar z,\qquad |q|^2d\bar z=q^2dz.
$$
The <complex force> from part (a) is
$$
\begin{aligned}
F_x-iF_y
&=-i\oint_Cp\,d\bar z\\
&=\frac{i\rho}{2}\oint_C|q|^2d\bar z
=\frac{i\rho}{2}\oint_Cq^2dz,
\end{aligned}
$$
where the constant-$P_*$ term vanishes. This proves the <Blasius force theorem>:
$$
\boxed{F_x-iF_y=\frac{i\rho}{2}\oint_C\left(w'(z)\right)^2dz.}
$$
For the moment, observe that
$$
z\,d\bar z=(x\,dx+y\,dy)+i(y\,dx-x\,dy).
$$
Therefore $M=\operatorname{Re}\oint_Cp\,z\,d\bar z$. The constant <pressure> contribution has zero real part, because $x\,dx+y\,dy=\tfrac12d(x^2+y^2)$. Substituting <Bernoulli's principle> and the same tangency identity gives the <Blasius moment theorem>:
$$
\boxed{M=-\frac\rho2\operatorname{Re}\oint_Cz\left(w'(z)\right)^2dz.}
$$
The real-part operation is essential: $\oint z\,d\bar z$ itself need not vanish, but its real part does.

= Solution
{parent=c}

The <method of images> places an equal <line source> at $-ia$, outside the physical <upper half-plane>. A convenient <complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow> is
$$
\boxed{w(z)=\frac Q{2\pi}\left[\log(z-ia)+\log(z+ia)\right],\qquad
w'(z)=\frac{Qz}{\pi(z^2+a^2)}.}
$$
The two logarithms can be combined locally as $(Q/(2\pi))\log(z^2+a^2)$; their branches of the <complex logarithm> affect only the potential convention, not the single-valued <complex velocity>. The <point source> in this two-dimensional problem has the <line source> normalization: its outward <volume flux> is $Q$ per unit span.

Writing $R_-^2=x^2+(y-a)^2$, $R_+^2=x^2+(y+a)^2$, and using $w'=u-iv$, the <velocity field> is
$$
\boxed{u=\frac Q{2\pi}\left(\frac{x}{R_-^2}+\frac{x}{R_+^2}\right),\qquad
v=\frac Q{2\pi}\left(\frac{y-a}{R_-^2}+\frac{y+a}{R_+^2}\right).}
$$
On the wall $y=0$, the vertical components cancel:
$$
\boxed{v(x,0)=0,\qquad u(x,0)=\frac{Qx}{\pi(x^2+a^2)}.}
$$
This is the required <no-penetration boundary condition>. Tangential slip is permitted. At large distance the <velocity> decays as $1/r$, and near $z=ia$ its singular part is exactly that of the specified source; the reflected source is regular throughout the fluid. Away from the injection point, this is an <incompressible flow> and an <irrotational flow>.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Since the <velocity> tends to zero at infinity, the steady <Bernoulli equation> gives $p+\rho|\mathbf u|^2/2=p_\infty$. On the wall, therefore,
$$
p(x,0)-p_\infty=-\frac{\rho Q^2}{2\pi^2}\frac{x^2}{(x^2+a^2)^2}.
$$
Compute the finite source-induced <force> relative to uniform ambient <pressure>, or equivalently use the <gauge pressure> $p-p_\infty$. The fluid lies above the wall, so its normal <force> on the wall points in $-\mathbf e_y$ for positive <pressure>. The negative excess <pressure> found here consequently produces an upward net force, toward the source.

For the symmetric section $-R\leq x\leq R$, there is no horizontal <force>, and the vertical excess <force> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
F_y(R)&=-\int_{-R}^R(p-p_\infty)\,dx\\
&=\frac{\rho Q^2}{2\pi^2}\left[\frac1a\arctan\frac Ra-\frac R{R^2+a^2}\right].
\end{aligned}
$$
Here an antiderivative of $x^2/(x^2+a^2)^2$ is $(1/(2a))\arctan(x/a)-x/[2(x^2+a^2)]$. Taking $R\to\infty$ gives the <hydrodynamic attraction of a wall to a line source>:
$$
\boxed{F_x=0,\qquad F_y=\frac{\rho Q^2}{4\pi a}.}
$$
Each component is a <force per unit length> of the wall in the out-of-plane direction. The result is an attraction even though the source sends fluid away from itself: faster tangential motion reduces the wall <pressure>.

The excess <torque> about the origin is
$$
M(R)=-\int_{-R}^R x(p-p_\infty)\,dx=0
$$
for every $R$, because the integrand is odd. Thus the symmetric infinite-wall limit is
$$
\boxed{M=0.}
$$
The symmetric prescription matters: the separate one-sided moment integrals grow logarithmically, whereas the <force> integral converges absolutely. This is the appropriate <Cauchy principal value> for the infinite wall. If one instead asks for the absolute fluid force with no balancing ambient <pressure> on the other side, its vertical component on a finite section is $-2Rp_\infty+F_y(R)$ and has no finite infinite-wall limit. The finite answer above is the flow-induced load.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $V(t)$ be any material <volume>, moving with the <velocity field> $\mathbf u$. The <Reynolds transport theorem> in the required form is
$$
\frac d{dt}\int_{V(t)}f\,dV=\int_{V(t)}\left[\partial_t f+\nabla\cdot(f\mathbf u)\right]dV,
$$
applied componentwise when $f$ is vector-valued. <Mass conservation> gives
$$
0=\frac d{dt}\int_{V(t)}\rho\,dV
=\int_{V(t)}\left[\rho_t+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u)\right]dV.
$$
Since the material <volume> is arbitrary, the local <continuity equation> follows:
$$
\boxed{\rho_t+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u)=0.}
$$
For an inviscid fluid with no <body force>, the <balance of linear momentum> equates the rate of change of total <momentum> to the boundary <pressure> force:
$$
\frac d{dt}\int_{V(t)}\rho\mathbf u\,dV
=-\int_{\partial V(t)}p\mathbf n\,dS=-\int_{V(t)}\nabla p\,dV,
$$
using the <divergence theorem>. The integrand on the left expands as
$$
\partial_t(\rho\mathbf u)+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u\otimes\mathbf u)
=\rho\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}+\mathbf u\left[\rho_t+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf u)\right].
$$
The bracket vanishes by <mass conservation>, leaving the <Euler momentum equation>
$$
\boxed{\rho\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}=-\nabla p,\qquad
\frac{D}{Dt}=\partial_t+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla.}
$$
Unlike the <incompressible flow> case, the <mass density> need not be constant along a <fluid element>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The rest state is $\rho=\rho_0$, $p=p_0$, $\mathbf u=\mathbf0$. At <linear order> in the perturbations, the <continuity equation>, <Euler momentum equation> and the given <equation of state> become
$$
\rho'_t+\rho_0\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0,\qquad
\rho_0\mathbf u_t=-\nabla p'=-c^2\nabla\rho'.
$$
For the acoustic component take $\mathbf u=\nabla\phi$. This is consistent with initially zero <vorticity>, since taking the <curl> of the linear momentum equation gives $\partial_t(\nabla\times\mathbf u)=0$. Any pre-existing linear vortical component is time independent and is separate from the potential oscillations being sought.

Spatial integration of $\rho_0\nabla\phi_t=-c^2\nabla\rho'$ gives $\rho_0\phi_t+c^2\rho'=K(t)$. Adding a function of time to the <velocity potential> removes $K(t)$ without altering the <velocity field>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\rho'=-\frac{\rho_0}{c^2}\phi_t,\qquad p'=-\rho_0\phi_t.}
$$
Substitute the first relation into <mass conservation>:
$$
-\frac{\rho_0}{c^2}\phi_{tt}+\rho_0\nabla^2\phi=0.
$$
Thus the <velocity potential> satisfies the <wave equation>
$$
\boxed{\phi_{tt}-c^2\nabla^2\phi=0.}
$$
Differentiating that equation in time and using the same density-potential relation gives
$$
\boxed{\rho'_{tt}-c^2\nabla^2\rho'=0.}
$$
Alternatively, differentiate the linear <continuity equation> in time and take the <divergence> of the linear momentum equation. The coefficient $c$ is the <sound speed>, since $dp/d\rho=c^2$.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The fixed upper and lower walls have zero normal <velocity>. Since $v=\phi_y$, their <no-penetration boundary conditions> are
$$
\boxed{\phi_y=0\quad\text{at }y=0,H.}
$$
For the moving end wall, use its level-set equation $F(x,y,t)=x-X(y,t)=0$. The <kinematic boundary condition> $DF/Dt=0$ gives the exact impermeability relation
$$
u=X_t+vX_y\quad\text{at }x=X(y,t).
$$
Both $v$ and $X_y$ are first order, so their product is second order. A <Taylor expansion> from $x=X$ to $x=0$ changes the first-order perturbation <velocity> only at second order as well. Thus
$$
\boxed{\phi_x(0,y,t)=X_t(y,t)=\epsilon\omega\cos(\omega t)\cos\frac{\pi y}H.}
$$
At infinity, select a bounded response with **no waves incident from $x=+\infty$**. A propagating <acoustic waveguide mode> must satisfy the outgoing <radiation condition for a waveguide mode>, while a nonpropagating mode must decay. For a time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$, a propagating axial factor has the form $e^{iqx}$, $q>0$, and obeys $\Phi_x-iq\Phi\to0$. An evanescent factor is $e^{-\alpha x}$, $\alpha>0$.

Requiring $\phi\to0$ for all frequencies would incorrectly exclude the radiated traveling waves. In this lossless channel they retain finite <amplitude> arbitrarily far downstream.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Let $\mu=\pi/H$. The end-wall forcing has transverse factor $\cos(\mu y)$, which already satisfies both fixed-wall <Neumann boundary conditions>. By <linearity> and <separation of variables>, seek its periodic response as
$$
\phi(x,y,t)=\operatorname{Re}\left\{\Phi(x)\cos(\mu y)e^{-i\omega t}\right\}.
$$
Substitution into the <wave equation> and the moving-wall condition gives
$$
\Phi''+\left(\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}-\mu^2\right)\Phi=0,\qquad
\Phi'(0)=\epsilon\omega.
$$
The <acoustic waveguide cutoff> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\omega_c=c\mu=\frac{\pi c}H.}
$$
It is the lowest nonzero transverse-mode frequency. The uniform transverse mode has no cutoff, but is not excited here because $\int_0^H\cos(\pi y/H)\,dy=0$.

For $0<\omega<\omega_c$, put $\alpha=\sqrt{\mu^2-\omega^2/c^2}>0$. The axial equation has solutions $e^{\pm\alpha x}$. Boundedness removes the growing exponential, and $\Phi'(0)=\epsilon\omega$ fixes the coefficient. Thus
$$
\boxed{\phi=-\frac{\epsilon\omega}{\alpha}e^{-\alpha x}\cos\frac{\pi y}H\cos(\omega t)
\qquad(0<\omega<\omega_c).}
$$
This <evanescent wave> oscillates in time but decays with penetration length $1/\alpha$. Its axial <velocity> is proportional to $\cos(\omega t)$, while its excess <pressure> $p'=-\rho_0\phi_t$ is proportional to $\sin(\omega t)$. They are in <phase quadrature>, so their mean product, the axial <acoustic energy flux>, is zero.

For $\omega>\omega_c$, put $q=\sqrt{\omega^2/c^2-\mu^2}>0$. The axial solutions are $e^{\pm iqx}$. The outgoing condition selects $e^{iqx}$ for the chosen time convention, and
$$
iq\Phi(0)=\epsilon\omega,\qquad
\Phi(x)=\frac{\epsilon\omega}{iq}e^{iqx}.
$$
Taking the real part gives
$$
\boxed{\phi=\frac{\epsilon\omega}{q}\cos\frac{\pi y}H\sin(qx-\omega t)
\qquad(\omega>\omega_c).}
$$
Its axial <velocity> is $\epsilon\omega\cos(\pi y/H)\cos(qx-\omega t)$, so the moving-wall condition is satisfied with the correct sign. This is a traveling <acoustic waveguide mode> carrying <energy> toward positive $x$. Its <dispersion relation> is
$$
\omega^2=c^2(q^2+\mu^2),\qquad
c_{\rm phase}=\frac\omega q>c,\qquad
c_{\rm group}=\frac{d\omega}{dq}=\frac{c^2q}\omega<c.
$$
The larger axial <phase velocity> does not imply faster energy transport: the <group velocity> controls that transport.

At $\omega=\omega_c$, the axial equation becomes $\Phi''=0$. The imposed nonzero derivative forces $\Phi=\epsilon\omega_c x+$ a constant, which is unbounded. Thus **there is no bounded periodic response at exact cutoff** for this infinite, undamped, velocity-driven model. The divergent coefficients as $q\to0$ or $\alpha\to0$ also show why the assumed small perturbations fail sufficiently close to cutoff at fixed forcing. Initial conditions can add free transients; the displayed formulas give the selected periodic forced response.
