Solutions to all three questions of the <University of Oxford> Part A examination of 17 June 2014, printed as “Waves and Fluids.” Source: https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/maths_papers_2/response/1850333/attach/3/FOI%2020210708%201%20Data.zip?cookie_passthrough=1[released examination archive], member `FOI-20210708-1 - Data/4329.pdf`, printed pages 1–3. Question statements are omitted. A bounded exact-paper check found no advertised full or partial solutions. The department's https://www.maths.ox.ac.uk/members/students/undergraduate-courses/examinations-assessments/overview-examinations[published policy] explicitly describes even-year coverage back to at least 2016, which does not by itself establish availability for this 2014 paper. Energies and effective masses below are per unit length along the cylinder axis.

= a
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=a}

Choose the planar <streamfunction> convention $u=\psi_y$, $v=-\psi_x$. For an <irrotational flow>, a <velocity potential> gives $\mathbf u=\nabla\phi$. Thus
$$
\boxed{u=\phi_x=\psi_y,\qquad v=\phi_y=-\psi_x.}
$$
The <streamfunction> enforces <incompressibility>, since $u_x+v_y=\psi_{yx}-\psi_{xy}=0$. Conversely, <incompressibility> permits a local <streamfunction>, while zero <curl> permits a local <velocity potential>. These are the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> for the <complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow> $w=\phi+i\psi$.

The <gradients> of the two functions satisfy
$$
\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\psi=(u,v)\cdot(-v,u)=0.
$$
Each <gradient> is normal to its regular level curves. Therefore **regular curves of constant <velocity potential> and constant <streamfunction> meet orthogonally**. At a <stagnation point>, both gradients vanish and this tangent-direction argument needs a limiting interpretation. The conclusion concerns regular portions of the two families of curves.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Use instantaneous <polar coordinates> centered on the moving <circular cylinder>, but measure the fluid <velocity> in the laboratory frame. Impermeability equates the normal components of body and fluid <velocity>:
$$
(\mathbf u-U\mathbf e_x)\cdot\mathbf e_r=0\quad(r=a).
$$
Since $\mathbf e_x\cdot\mathbf e_r=\cos\theta$ and $u_r=\phi_r$, the <no-penetration boundary condition> is
$$
\boxed{\phi_r(a,\theta)=U\cos\theta.}
$$
The <velocity potential> obeys the exterior <Laplace equation>, by <incompressibility>. The boundary data have only the first cosine harmonic. A decaying harmonic potential of this form is $\phi=C\cos\theta/r$. Matching its radial derivative gives $-C/a^2=U$, so $C=-Ua^2$. The zero <circulation> condition rules out an angular potential term, and the integrated boundary condition gives zero net <volume flux>, ruling out a radial logarithmic source term. The remaining decaying harmonics have zero coefficients by the boundary data. Hence
$$
\boxed{\phi=-\frac{Ua^2}{r}\cos\theta.}
$$
Using the <streamfunction in polar coordinates>, $u_r=r^{-1}\psi_\theta$, $u_\theta=-\psi_r$, gives
$$
\boxed{\psi=\frac{Ua^2}{r}\sin\theta,\qquad
u_r=\frac{Ua^2}{r^2}\cos\theta,\quad u_\theta=\frac{Ua^2}{r^2}\sin\theta.}
$$
One may also obtain both functions as the real and imaginary parts of $w(z)=-Ua^2/z$, a <two-dimensional potential doublet>. The <velocity> decays as $r^{-2}$ and the source of this singular representation lies inside the excluded body. These are the fields used to calculate the <added mass of a circular cylinder>.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The <product rule>, the <Laplace equation> and $\mathbf u=\nabla\phi$ give
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot(\phi\nabla\phi)=|\nabla\phi|^2+\phi\nabla^2\phi=|\mathbf u|^2.}
$$
First truncate the exterior fluid domain by a large <circle> $C_R$. Let $\mathbf n_B$ point outward from the body into the fluid. The outward normal of the exterior fluid domain at the body is $-\mathbf n_B$. The planar <divergence theorem>, equivalently <Green's theorem>, therefore gives
$$
\int_{D_R}|\mathbf u|^2\,dA
=\oint_{C_R}\phi\phi_r\,ds-\oint_{\partial B}\phi\,\partial_{n_B}\phi\,ds.
$$
Orient $\partial B$ counterclockwise around the body. Then $\mathbf n_B\,ds=(dy,-dx)$ and
$$
\partial_{n_B}\phi\,ds=\phi_x\,dy-\phi_y\,dx
=\psi_y\,dy+\psi_x\,dx=d\psi.
$$
For the finite-energy exterior rigid-body flow intended here, the outer boundary term vanishes as $R\to\infty$. In particular, zero net <volume flux> and zero <circulation> eliminate the $1/z$ term in the exterior <Laurent series> of the <complex velocity>. Thus $\mathbf u=O(R^{-2})$ and, after choosing the additive potential constant, $\phi=O(R^{-1})$. The outer integral is then $O(R^{-2})$.

Multiplying by half the constant <mass density> gives the <boundary integral for kinetic energy of planar potential flow>:
$$
\boxed{T=\frac\rho2\int_{\mathbb R^2\setminus B}|\mathbf u|^2\,dA
=-\frac\rho2\oint_{\partial B}\phi\,d\psi.}
$$
The minus sign comes from the fluid's inner-boundary orientation, rather than a negative physical <kinetic energy>.

There is a necessary qualification to the printed hypotheses. Decaying <velocity> and zero <circulation> alone do not ensure a finite <kinetic energy>: $\phi=A\log r$ gives $\mathbf u=A\mathbf e_r/r$, zero <circulation>, and logarithmically divergent exterior <kinetic energy>. It carries a nonzero <volume flux> from the body and is not the flow of an impermeable rigid body. The <finite-energy condition for planar exterior potential flow> makes explicit the source-free assumption needed to discard the outer boundary term.

= d
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

On the translating <circular cylinder> $r=a$, the preceding <velocity potential> and <streamfunction> give
$$
\phi=-Ua\cos\theta,\qquad d\psi=Ua\cos\theta\,d\theta.
$$
Using the <boundary integral for kinetic energy of planar potential flow>,
$$
T=-\frac\rho2\int_0^{2\pi}(-U^2a^2\cos^2\theta)\,d\theta
=\frac{\rho\pi a^2U^2}{2}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{T=\frac12MU^2,\qquad M=\rho\pi a^2.}
$$
As a direct check, $|\mathbf u|^2=U^2a^4/r^4$, and integration over $r>a$ gives the same result.

The fluid <kinetic energy> has the same dependence on body speed as the <kinetic energy> of an extra mass $M$ carried by the body. Thus $M$ is its <added mass>, here equal to the mass of displaced fluid per unit cylinder length. For a cylinder of physical mass per length $m$, the combined <kinetic energy> is $\tfrac12(m+M)U^2$. Accelerating the body requires accelerating the surrounding <fluid>, producing the inertial reaction $-M\dot U$ along the direction of motion. This is the <added mass of a circular cylinder>.

= Solution
{parent=a}

The <Milne-Thomson circle theorem> constructs a circular impermeable boundary in planar <potential flow>. Suppose $f(\zeta)$ is an incident <complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow> with no singularities on or inside $|\zeta|=R$. Then
$$
\boxed{W(\zeta)=f(\zeta)+\overline{f\!\left(\frac{R^2}{\bar\zeta}\right)}}
$$
is a <complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow> outside the <circle>, with the same exterior singularities and their strengths as the incident flow; the added image singularities lie inside the excluded disk. On $|\zeta|=R$, $R^2/\bar\zeta=\zeta$, so $W=f+\bar f$ is real. Its imaginary part, the <streamfunction>, is constant on the <circle>, proving the <no-penetration boundary condition>. The theorem gives the usual zero-<circulation> construction; a separately prescribed <circulation> can be added by its logarithmic vortex potential.

= b
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

A <uniform flow> with physical <velocity> $(U\cos\alpha,U\sin\alpha)$ has <complex velocity> $Ue^{-i\alpha}$ and incident potential $f(\zeta)=Ue^{-i\alpha}\zeta$. Apply the <Milne-Thomson circle theorem> with $R=1$:
$$
\boxed{W(\zeta)=U\left(e^{-i\alpha}\zeta+\frac{e^{i\alpha}}\zeta\right).}
$$
Indeed, on $\zeta=e^{i\theta}$ it is $W=2U\cos(\theta-\alpha)$, which is real, so the boundary is a <streamline>. At infinity, $W'(\zeta)\to Ue^{-i\alpha}$, verifying the specified incoming <uniform flow>.

= c
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Parameterize the <unit circle> by $\zeta=e^{i\theta}$. Under the <Joukowski map>,
$$
z=e^{i\theta}+a^2e^{-i\theta}
=(1+a^2)\cos\theta+i(1-a^2)\sin\theta.
$$
Consequently its image is the <ellipse>
$$
\boxed{\frac{x^2}{(1+a^2)^2}+\frac{y^2}{(1-a^2)^2}=1,}
$$
with horizontal and vertical <semi-axes>
$$
\boxed{A=1+a^2,\qquad B=1-a^2.}
$$
Both are positive because $0<a<1$. The horizontal axis is the major axis. The derivative $dz/d\zeta=1-a^2/\zeta^2$ is nonzero for $|\zeta|\geq1$. Also, equality of two images implies $(\zeta_1-\zeta_2)(1-a^2/(\zeta_1\zeta_2))=0$; two distinct exterior points cannot satisfy the second factor since $|\zeta_1\zeta_2|>1>a^2$. Thus the exterior map is one-to-one, mapping the exterior of the <unit circle> conformally onto the exterior of the <ellipse>.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

At large $|\zeta|$, $z=\zeta+O(\zeta^{-1})$, so the map tends to the identity. Its exterior inverse has the expansion $\zeta=z-a^2/z+O(z^{-3})$. Therefore the physical <complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow> is $w(z)=W(\zeta(z))$, with
$$
w(z)=Ue^{-i\alpha}z+\frac{U(e^{i\alpha}-a^2e^{-i\alpha})}{z}+O(z^{-3}).
$$
Its leading term is unchanged, so the far-field <velocity> still has magnitude $U$ and inclination $\alpha$.

The <chain rule> gives the <complex velocity>:
$$
\boxed{u-iv=\frac{dW/d\zeta}{dz/d\zeta}
=U\frac{e^{-i\alpha}-e^{i\alpha}/\zeta^2}{1-a^2/\zeta^2}
=U\frac{e^{-i\alpha}\zeta^2-e^{i\alpha}}{\zeta^2-a^2}.}
$$
The parameter $\zeta$ denotes the unique exterior preimage of $z$. If an expression solely in $z$ is desired, take
$$
\zeta(z)=\frac{z+\sqrt{z^2-4a^2}}2,
$$
where the exterior <holomorphic square root> is chosen so that $\sqrt{z^2-4a^2}\sim z$ at infinity. Its branch points lie inside the <ellipse>. This is <uniform potential flow around an ellipse>.

= Solution
{parent=e}

The <complex velocity> vanishes where $e^{-i\alpha}\zeta^2-e^{i\alpha}=0$, provided its denominator is nonzero. Thus $\zeta=\pm e^{i\alpha}$. Both lie on the <unit circle>, and $\zeta^2-a^2\ne0$ since $a<1$. Mapping these points into the physical plane gives
$$
\boxed{z_{\rm stag}=\pm\left[(1+a^2)\cos\alpha+i(1-a^2)\sin\alpha\right].}
$$
Equivalently, the two <stagnation points> are the opposite points $(A\cos\alpha,B\sin\alpha)$ and $(-A\cos\alpha,-B\sin\alpha)$ on the <ellipse>. The <conformal map> preserves the zeros of the <complex velocity> here because its derivative is finite and nonzero on the boundary.

= Solution
{parent=a}

The total <velocity fields> on the two sides are
$$
\mathbf u_+=(U,0)+\nabla\phi_+,\qquad \mathbf u_-=\nabla\phi_-.
$$
Each base flow is divergence-free. <Incompressibility> therefore makes both disturbance <velocity potentials> satisfy the <Laplace equation>:
$$
\boxed{\phi_{+,xx}+\phi_{+,yy}=0,\qquad
\phi_{-,xx}+\phi_{-,yy}=0.}
$$
At the fixed horizontal walls, impermeability requires zero vertical <velocity>:
$$
\boxed{\phi_{+,y}(x,h,t)=0,\qquad\phi_{-,y}(x,-h,t)=0.}
$$
The discontinuity in tangential base <velocity> at the interface is a <vortex sheet>. The assumption of <irrotational flow> is understood separately in each open fluid region; it cannot hold distributionally across that sheet when $U\ne0$.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The interface is material. Applying the <kinematic boundary condition> to $y-\eta(x,t)=0$ gives, exactly,
$$
\phi_{-,y}=\eta_t+\phi_{-,x}\eta_x,\qquad
\phi_{+,y}=\eta_t+(U+\phi_{+,x})\eta_x
\quad(y=\eta).
$$
The disturbance <velocities> and $\eta$ are small. Products of two disturbance quantities are second order, and a <Taylor expansion> moves their evaluation to $y=0$ at <linear order>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\phi_{-,y}=\eta_t,\qquad
\phi_{+,y}=\eta_t+U\eta_x\quad(y=0).}
$$
The extra upper-fluid term is advection of the displaced interface by the base <uniform flow>.

Use the <unsteady Bernoulli equation> separately on each side, with constant <mass density> $\rho$. There are no restoring terms specified at this internal interface, and its <pressure> is continuous. Choose the time-dependent potential gauges so that the undisturbed <pressure> is $p_0$ on both sides. Subtracting the base values from <Bernoulli's principle> gives, at <linear order>,
$$
p'_-=-\rho\phi_{-,t},\qquad
p'_+=-\rho(\phi_{+,t}+U\phi_{+,x}).
$$
The quadratic disturbance-<velocity> terms have been discarded, while the cross term with the base <velocity> is first order and must remain. Equating the perturbation <pressures> yields the third condition:
$$
\boxed{\phi_{-,t}=\phi_{+,t}+U\phi_{+,x}\quad(y=0).}
$$

= Solution
{parent=c}

Use complex <normal modes> and take their real parts at the end. Write $\eta=Ae^{i(kx-\omega t)}$, with $k>0$. The <Laplace equation> and the wall <Neumann boundary conditions> give the vertical forms
$$
\phi_+=C_+\cosh(k(y-h))e^{i(kx-\omega t)},\qquad
\phi_-=C_-\cosh(k(y+h))e^{i(kx-\omega t)}.
$$
At $y=0$, the two <kinematic boundary conditions> become
$$
-k\sinh(kh)C_+=-i(\omega-kU)A,\qquad
k\sinh(kh)C_-=-i\omega A.
$$
Thus
$$
C_+=\frac{i(\omega-kU)A}{k\sinh(kh)},\qquad
C_-=-\frac{i\omega A}{k\sinh(kh)}.
$$
The dynamic condition is
$$
\omega C_-\cosh(kh)=(\omega-kU)C_+\cosh(kh).
$$
Substituting the two amplitudes and cancelling the common nonzero depth factor gives the <dispersion relation> for a <vortex sheet in a symmetric rigid channel>:
$$
\omega^2+(\omega-kU)^2=0.
$$
Equivalently,
$$
\left(\omega-\frac{kU}{2}\right)^2=-\frac{k^2U^2}{4},
\qquad
\boxed{\omega=\frac{kU}{2}\pm i\frac{kU}{2}.}
$$
For the specified $U>0$, the plus sign gives positive <temporal growth rate> $\gamma=kU/2$ in the convention $e^{-i\omega t}$. A real growing interface displacement is therefore
$$
\boxed{\eta(x,t)=A_0e^{kUt/2}\cos\left(kx-\frac{kU}{2}t\right).}
$$
The other branch decays at the same rate. Consequently **the interface is linearly unstable**, exhibiting <Kelvin-Helmholtz instability>. There is no nonzero-amplitude mode with a purely real temporal <frequency> for $k>0$ and $U\ne0$; the paper's sinusoidal notation must allow a complex <frequency>, with the physical displacement obtained by a real part.

The wave pattern is advected at speed $U/2$, the mean of the two base <velocities>. The growth rate is independent of $h$ here because both layers have the same <mass density> and the same depth, making their factors $\coth(kh)$ cancel. In an asymmetric channel those inertia factors would differ. In the zero-shear limit $U=0$, the two roots merge at zero and there is no exponential growth.
