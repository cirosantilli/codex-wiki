Solutions to all three questions of the <University of Oxford> Part A fluids and waves paper, sat on 16 June 2023. Source: https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/bsc_mathematics_part_a_examinati/response/2820674/attach/3/FOI%20202410%20944%20Data.pdf#page=242[original released PDF, bundle pages 242–245; paper pages 1–4]. Question statements are omitted. Throughout, the liquid has constant positive <mass density> $\rho$; a nonzero doublet and positive <bending stiffness> are understood in the corresponding questions.

= a

= Solution
{parent=a}

An <irrotational flow> has $\nabla\times\mathbf u=0$. Hence a <velocity potential> $\phi$ exists locally, and globally on a <simply connected domain>, with $\mathbf u=\nabla\phi$. The exterior of a sphere in three dimensions has this property. Constant <mass density> reduces the <continuity equation> to $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0$, so
$$
\boxed{\nabla^2\phi=0}.
$$
Thus the <velocity potential> is a <harmonic function>. For the <Euler equations>, use the <vorticity cross-product identity>
$$
(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u
=\nabla\!\left(\frac12|\mathbf u|^2\right)
-\mathbf u\times(\nabla\times\mathbf u).
$$
The last term vanishes. Since $\partial_t\mathbf u=\nabla\phi_t$, the <Euler momentum equation> becomes
$$
\nabla\!\left(\phi_t+\frac12|\nabla\phi|^2+\frac p\rho\right)=0.
$$
On each connected fluid region, a function with zero <gradient> is spatially constant. Therefore the <unsteady Bernoulli equation> is
$$
\boxed{\frac p\rho+\phi_t+\frac12|\nabla\phi|^2=F(t)}.
$$
The freedom to add a function of time to the <velocity potential> changes $F(t)$ but leaves the <velocity field> unchanged. On a multiply connected region, zero <vorticity> alone guarantees only local potentials; a global single-valued <velocity potential> additionally requires zero <circulation> around every closed path.

= Solution

Use the <spherical symmetry> of the expansion, with fluid at rest at infinity and no superposed imposed flow. The radial <Laplace equation> gives
$$
\partial_r(r^2\phi_r)=0,
\qquad \phi_r=\frac{A(t)}{r^2},
\qquad \phi=G(t)-\frac{A(t)}r.
$$
The moving sphere is a material boundary: its <kinematic boundary condition> is $u_r(R,t)=\dot R$. Consequently $A(t)=R^2\dot R$, and the <spherically symmetric incompressible radial flow> has
$$
\boxed{\phi(r,t)=G(t)-\frac{R^2\dot R}{r},\qquad
\mathbf u=\frac{R^2\dot R}{r^2}\,\widehat{\mathbf r}}.
$$
Equivalently, its <volumetric flow rate> through any concentric sphere is $4\pi r^2u_r=4\pi R^2\dot R$, the rate of change of the enclosed <volume>.

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Put $A=R^2\dot R$. The <partial derivative> in the <unsteady Bernoulli equation> is taken at fixed spatial position, so
$$
\phi_t=\dot G-\frac{\dot A}{r},\qquad
|\nabla\phi|^2=\frac{A^2}{r^4}.
$$
At infinity the <velocity> vanishes and $p\to p_\infty$, giving $F(t)=\dot G+p_\infty/\rho$. Substitution cancels the arbitrary $G$ and yields the <pressure field of a pulsating sphere>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{p(r,t)}\rho
=\frac1r\frac d{dt}(R^2\dot R)
-\frac{R^4\dot R^2}{2r^4}+\frac{p_\infty}\rho}.
$$
The distinction between $\partial_t\phi$ at fixed $r$ and the <material derivative> along the moving boundary is essential here.

= Solution
{parent=i}

With zero internal <pressure> and negligible <surface tension>, <pressure continuity> sets the liquid-side boundary value to $p(R,t)=0$. Evaluating the <pressure field of a pulsating sphere> there gives
$$
0=\frac1R(R^2\ddot R+2R\dot R^2)-\frac12\dot R^2+\frac{p_\infty}\rho.
$$
Thus the <Rayleigh equation for an inviscid spherical bubble>, together with its <initial conditions>, is
$$
\boxed{R\ddot R+\frac32\dot R^2=-\frac{p_\infty}\rho,
\qquad R(0)=a,\quad\dot R(0)=0}.
$$
For $p_\infty>0$, initially $\ddot R(0)=-p_\infty/(\rho a)<0$, so the physically relevant branch describes <Rayleigh collapse of a spherical cavity>.

{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Write $P=p_\infty/\rho>0$. Multiplying the radius <ordinary differential equation> by $2R^2\dot R$ gives an exact derivative:
$$
\frac d{dt}(R^3\dot R^2)
=2R^2\dot R\left(R\ddot R+\frac32\dot R^2\right)
=-2PR^2\dot R=-\frac{2P}{3}\frac d{dt}(R^3).
$$
The <initial conditions> therefore give the <first integral>
$$
R^3\dot R^2=\frac{2P}{3}(a^3-R^3),\qquad
\boxed{\dot R=-\sqrt{\frac{2p_\infty}{3\rho}
\left(\frac{a^3}{R^3}-1\right)}}.
$$
Choose the negative square root because the cavity starts accelerating inward. It cannot turn around while $0<R<a$, since the squared speed is then strictly positive. Separating variables, with $s=R/a$, proves the <collapse time of a spherical cavity> is
$$
t_{\rm collapse}
=\sqrt{\frac{3\rho}{2p_\infty}}
\int_0^a\frac{R^{3/2}}{\sqrt{a^3-R^3}}\,dR
=a\sqrt{\frac\rho{p_\infty}}
\sqrt{\frac32}\int_0^1\frac{s^{3/2}}{\sqrt{1-s^3}}\,ds.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm collapse}=k\sqrt{\frac{\rho a^2}{p_\infty}},\qquad
k=\sqrt{\frac32}\int_0^1\frac{s^{3/2}}{\sqrt{1-s^3}}\,ds}.
$$
The <improper integral> is finite: its integrand behaves as $s^{3/2}$ near zero and as $[3(1-s)]^{-1/2}$ near one. The divergent inward speed as $R\to0$ signals a singular endpoint of this ideal <incompressible flow> model; the finite collapse time follows within that model.

= Solution

Use $W=\phi+i\psi$ for the <complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow>, so $W'(z)=u-iv$. The <Milne-Thomson circle theorem> says that if $f(z)$ is an incident <complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow> whose singularities lie outside $|z|=a$, then
$$
W(z)=f(z)+\overline{f\!\left(\frac{a^2}{\bar z}\right)}
$$
makes $|z|=a$ a <streamline>. Indeed, on that circle $a^2/\bar z=z$, so $W=f+\bar f$ is real and the <stream function> is constant. This imposes the <no-penetration boundary condition>.

For the <two-dimensional potential doublet>, put $M=me^{i\alpha}$ and $f=M/(z-b)$, where $b>a$ is real. The theorem gives
$$
\boxed{W(z)=\frac{me^{i\alpha}}{z-b}
+\frac{me^{-i\alpha}z}{a^2-bz}}.
$$
Up to an additive constant, this is equivalently
$$
W(z)=\frac{me^{i\alpha}}{z-b}
-\frac{ma^2e^{-i\alpha}}{b^2(z-a^2/b)}.
$$
The <method of images> has placed the image <two-dimensional potential doublet> at $a^2/b$, inside the obstacle. We use the solution with zero added <circulation> and no imposed far-field flow; the original doublet is the only singularity in the liquid.

= b
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow> gives
$$
q(z):=W'(z)
=-\frac{me^{i\alpha}}{(z-b)^2}
+\frac{ma^2e^{-i\alpha}}{(bz-a^2)^2}.
$$
At a <stagnation point>, $q=0$. For $m\ne0$, taking the two square-root alternatives gives
$$
bz-a^2=\sigma a e^{-i\alpha}(z-b),\qquad \sigma\in\{+1,-1\}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{z_\sigma
=a\,\frac{a-\sigma b e^{-i\alpha}}{b-\sigma a e^{-i\alpha}},
\qquad \sigma=\pm1}.
$$
The denominators are nonzero because $b>a$. Moreover
$$
|a-\sigma b e^{-i\alpha}|^2
=a^2+b^2-2\sigma ab\cos\alpha
=|b-\sigma a e^{-i\alpha}|^2,
$$
so both <stagnation points> satisfy $|z_\sigma|=a$. They are distinct: equality would require $2e^{-i\alpha}(a^2-b^2)=0$. Neither is a singularity of $q$. Conversely, the numerator of $q$ is a quadratic polynomial with nonzero leading coefficient, so these are all its finite zeros. Thus there are exactly two boundary <stagnation points> and none in the open fluid region. If $m=0$, the flow instead vanishes everywhere, which is the degenerate exception to the assertion.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

For $q=W'=u-iv$, the <Blasius force theorem> states that the <force> per unit length exerted by a steady <incompressible flow> and <irrotational flow> on an impermeable body is
$$
X-iY=\frac{i\rho}{2}\oint_C q(z)^2\,dz,
$$
where $C$ runs counterclockwise around the body and excludes external flow singularities. The sign corresponds to the <force> on the body.

Let $c=a^2/b$ and $A=ma^2e^{-i\alpha}/b^2$. The <complex velocity> may be written
$$
q(z)=\frac{A}{(z-c)^2}+H(z),\qquad
H(z)=-\frac{me^{i\alpha}}{(z-b)^2}.
$$
Only the image point $c$ lies inside the circular contour. The term $A^2/(z-c)^4$ has zero <residue>; $H^2$ is regular there. In the cross term, the linear term of the <Taylor expansion> of $H$ supplies
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=c}q^2
=2AH'(c)
=\frac{4m^2a^2}{b^2(c-b)^3}
=-\frac{4m^2a^2b}{(b^2-a^2)^3}.
$$
The orientation factors have canceled. The <residue theorem> now gives
$$
X-iY=-\pi\rho\operatorname{Res}_{z=c}q^2,
\qquad
\boxed{X=\frac{4\pi\rho m^2a^2b}{(b^2-a^2)^3},\qquad Y=0}.
$$
Since $b>a$, $X>0$: the obstacle is pulled toward the doublet, independently of $\alpha$. This is a <force> per unit length perpendicular to the plane of flow.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Keep the prescribed orientation $\alpha$ and strength $m$ fixed, and now let the doublet position $b(t)$ be complex. The <Milne-Thomson circle theorem> applies instantaneously with
$$
W(z,t)=\frac{M}{z-b}+\frac{\bar M z}{a^2-\bar b z},\qquad M=me^{i\alpha}.
$$
When taking its spatial derivative, $b(t)$ is a parameter. Removing the doublet's own singular term leaves the regular <complex velocity> at its position:
$$
q_{\rm reg}(b)
=\left.\frac{\bar M a^2}{(a^2-\bar b z)^2}\right|_{z=b}
=\frac{\bar M a^2}{(|b|^2-a^2)^2}.
$$
Because $q=u-iv$, the stipulated <image-induced motion of a planar doublet> obeys $\dot b=\overline{q_{\rm reg}(b)}$, not $q_{\rm reg}(b)$ itself. Hence
$$
\boxed{\dot b=\frac{ma^2e^{i\alpha}}{(|b|^2-a^2)^2}}.
$$
Its direction is constant. Indeed $\operatorname{Im}(be^{-i\alpha})$ is constant and, since $b(0)=b_0>a$ is real, equals $-b_0\sin\alpha$. Writing $b=re^{i\theta}$ in <polar coordinates> gives the path equation
$$
\boxed{r\sin(\theta-\alpha)=-b_0\sin\alpha},
\qquad
\boxed{r(\theta)=\frac{b_0\sin\alpha}{\sin(\alpha-\theta)}}
$$
where the quotient is used when $\sin\alpha\ne0$ and the branch through $(r,\theta)=(b_0,0)$ with $r>a$ is selected. It is the straight line through $b_0$ parallel to $e^{i\alpha}$. An unambiguous parametrization, also covering the degenerate polar cases, is
$$
b=b_0+s e^{i\alpha},\qquad
\dot s=\frac{ma^2}{(|b_0+s e^{i\alpha}|^2-a^2)^2},\qquad s(0)=0.
$$
If $\sin\alpha=0$, the path is radial along the positive real axis, with $\theta=0$ and changing $r$; it cannot be represented as a single-valued $r(\theta)$. The sign of $m$ selects which direction along the line is followed. These paths apply only while the singularity remains outside the obstacle; the model prescribes no orientation evolution.

= Solution
{parent=a}

For a <plane wave> with <amplitude> $\alpha$, <wavenumber> $k>0$ and <angular frequency> $\omega>0$, the <dispersion relation> $\omega=\omega(k)$ specifies which frequencies the governing equations permit. A point of fixed <wave phase> obeys $kx-\omega t=\text{constant}$, so the <phase speed> is
$$
\boxed{c_p=\frac\omega k}.
$$
It is the speed of individual crests. For a narrow <wave packet>, two nearby <plane waves> with differences $\Delta k$ and $\Delta\omega$ have a slowly varying <wave envelope> depending on $\Delta k\,x-\Delta\omega\,t$. Taking the limit gives the <group velocity>
$$
\boxed{c_g=\frac{d\omega}{dk}}.
$$
This is the envelope's propagation speed and, in the present linear lossless wave model, the <energy> transport speed for a narrow packet.

A <dispersive wave> has <phase speed> dependent on <wavenumber>, so different components can change their relative phases and alter the packet's shape. A <nondispersive wave> on the chosen propagation branch has constant <phase speed> over the relevant band:
$$
\boxed{\omega(k)=ck,\qquad c_p=c_g=c}.
$$
Indeed $dc_p/dk=(c_g-c_p)/k$, so equality throughout a band is equivalent to constant <phase speed> there. Equality at an isolated <wavenumber> does not make the entire wave family nondispersive.

= Solution

The <irrotational flow> has a <velocity potential> $\phi$ with $(u,w)=(\phi_x,\phi_z)$. The <incompressible flow> satisfies $u_x+w_z=0$, hence the interior <Laplace equation> is
$$
\boxed{\phi_{xx}+\phi_{zz}=0,\qquad -h<z<\eta(x,t)}.
$$
The <ice> supplies a boundary load through its <bending stiffness>; it does not alter this interior consequence of <mass conservation>.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

At the rigid bed, the <no-penetration boundary condition> gives
$$
\boxed{\phi_z=0\quad\text{at }z=-h}.
$$
The displaced surface is material, so taking the <material derivative> of $z-\eta(x,t)$ gives the exact <kinematic boundary condition for a free-surface graph>:
$$
\boxed{\eta_t+\phi_x\eta_x=\phi_z\quad\text{at }z=\eta(x,t)}.
$$
With upward vertical coordinate $z$, the gravitational potential per unit <mass> is $gz$. The <unsteady Bernoulli equation>, evaluated on the surface with the prescribed <pressure> $p=B\eta_{xxxx}$, gives
$$
\boxed{\phi_t+\frac12(\phi_x^2+\phi_z^2)
+g\eta+\frac B\rho\eta_{xxxx}=0
\quad\text{at }z=\eta(x,t)}.
$$
Here the spatially uniform time-dependent Bernoulli term has been absorbed into the <velocity potential>. The <pressure> is measured relative to the constant exterior pressure. This dynamic boundary condition uses the specified massless bending-cover model, with no ice inertia or tension.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Linearize about the resting state $\eta=0$, $\phi=0$. Introduce a dimensionless small parameter $\epsilon$ and write
$$
\eta=\epsilon\eta_1+O(\epsilon^2),\qquad
\phi=\epsilon\phi_1+O(\epsilon^2),
$$
holding the depth, <bending stiffness> and variation scales fixed. Thus both boundary displacement and induced <velocity> are small. For any first-order derivative $D\phi$, a <Taylor expansion> at the moving surface gives
$$
(D\phi)(x,\eta,t)
=\epsilon(D\phi_1)(x,0,t)+O(\epsilon^2).
$$
The displacement correction to the leading field is $\epsilon^2\eta_1\partial_z(D\phi_1)(x,0,t)$, so evaluating it on $z=0$ changes it only at second order. Also $\phi_x\eta_x=O(\epsilon^2)$ and $|\nabla\phi|^2=O(\epsilon^2)$. The first-order <kinematic boundary condition> and dynamic boundary condition are therefore
$$
(\eta_1)_t=(\phi_1)_z(x,0,t),\qquad
(\phi_1)_t(x,0,t)+g\eta_1+\frac B\rho(\eta_1)_{xxxx}=0.
$$
Dropping the subscript, the <linearized boundary condition> pair is
$$
\boxed{\eta_t=\phi_z,\qquad
\phi_t+g\eta+\frac B\rho\eta_{xxxx}=0\quad\text{at }z=0}.
$$
The fixed-bed condition remains $\phi_z(x,-h,t)=0$. The source's shorthand $|\eta|\ll1$ requires a length scale: for the subsequent <plane wave>, small surface slope $k|\alpha|\ll1$ and displacement small compared with depth are sufficient conditions. Small height alone, with arbitrarily rapid spatial variation or a finite background disturbance, does not justify discarding the products.

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Let $\chi=kx-\omega t$, with $k>0$. The <surface elevation> is $\eta=\alpha\cos\chi$, so $\eta_t=\alpha\omega\sin\chi$. Use <separation of variables> in the interior <Laplace equation>:
$$
\phi=A\cosh(k(z+h))\sin\chi.
$$
The $\sinh(k(z+h))$ term is excluded by the bed <no-penetration boundary condition>. The linear surface <kinematic boundary condition> determines
$$
Ak\sinh(kh)=\alpha\omega,
\qquad
\boxed{\phi(x,z,t)=\frac{\alpha\omega}{k\sinh(kh)}
\cosh(k(z+h))\sin(kx-\omega t)}.
$$
An irrelevant additive constant is omitted. At $z=0$,
$$
\phi_t=-\frac{\alpha\omega^2}{k}\coth(kh)\cos\chi,
\qquad \eta_{xxxx}=\alpha k^4\cos\chi.
$$
The linear dynamic boundary condition then reduces to
$$
-\frac{\omega^2}{k}\coth(kh)+g+\frac B\rho k^4=0.
$$
Consequently the <dispersion relation of a floating elastic plate> with zero plate inertia is
$$
\boxed{\omega^2=\left(gk+\frac B\rho k^5\right)\tanh(kh),\qquad
\omega(k)=\sqrt{\left(gk+\frac B\rho k^5\right)\tanh(kh)}}.
$$
The negative-frequency branch gives propagation in the opposite direction. For signed <wavenumber>, replace $k$ by $|k|$ in the squared-frequency formula. Setting $B=0$ recovers the finite-depth <surface gravity wave> relation, a useful sign check.

= Solution
{parent=i}

In the deep-water limit $kh\gg1$, $\tanh(kh)\sim1$. Put $\beta=B/\rho>0$. The squared <phase speed> of the <flexural-gravity wave> is then
$$
c_p^2\sim\frac gk+\beta k^3.
$$
It diverges as $k\to0^+$ and as $k\to\infty$ in this limiting model. Its unique stationary point satisfies
$$
-\frac g{k^2}+3\beta k^2=0,
\qquad \boxed{k_* =\left(\frac{\rho g}{3B}\right)^{1/4}}.
$$
The second derivative $2g/k^3+6\beta k$ is positive. Using $g/k_*=3\beta k_*^3$ gives the <minimum phase speed of a flexural-gravity wave>:
$$
c_{\min}^2=4\beta k_*^3
=\frac4{3^{3/4}}\beta^{1/4}g^{3/4},
\qquad
\boxed{c_{\min}=\frac2{3^{3/8}}
\left(\frac{Bg^3}\rho\right)^{1/8}}.
$$
This is the deep-water minimum: applying it to a finite depth requires $k_*h\gg1$. Otherwise the $\tanh(kh)$ factor must be retained in the minimization. Within the deep-water approximation, <phase speed> and <group velocity> coincide at $k_*$, although the wave family remains dispersive.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Define the dimensionless parameter
$$
\delta=\left(\frac{\rho gh^4}{B}\right)^{1/4}.
$$
The stipulated hierarchy $kh\ll\delta\ll1$ implies both $kh\ll1$ and
$$
\frac{Bk^4}{\rho g}=\left(\frac{kh}{\delta}\right)^4\ll1.
$$
Thus the finite-depth correction uses $\tanh(kh)\sim kh$, while the restoring effect of <bending stiffness> is small compared with <gravity>. The <dispersion relation> becomes
$$
\omega^2
=ghk^2\left[1+\left(\frac{kh}{\delta}\right)^4\right]
\frac{\tanh(kh)}{kh}
\sim ghk^2.
$$
Hence, to leading order, these <surface gravity waves> are <nondispersive waves> with
$$
\boxed{\omega\sim k\sqrt{gh},\qquad c_p\sim c_g\sim c=\sqrt{gh}}.
$$
For clarity, the first corrections to the <phase speed> are
$$
\frac{c_p}{\sqrt{gh}}
=1+\frac12\left(\frac{kh}{\delta}\right)^4
-\frac16(kh)^2
+O\!\left((kh/\delta)^8+(kh)^4+(kh/\delta)^4(kh)^2\right).
$$
They exhibit the weak residual <wave dispersion> at finite <wavenumber>. The claimed nondispersive behavior is the asymptotic leading-order result, not exact equality for the complete relation.
