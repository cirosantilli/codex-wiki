= Solution
{parent=i}
{c}

The <velocity> of a <fluid element> is the <time derivative> of its position with its <Lagrangian coordinates> $X,Y$ held fixed. Direct <differentiation> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot x&=-e^{-t}(X\cos\Omega t+Y\sin\Omega t)
+\Omega e^{-t}(Y\cos\Omega t-X\sin\Omega t)
=-x+\Omega e^{-2t}y,\\
\dot y&=e^t(Y\cos\Omega t-X\sin\Omega t)
-\Omega e^t(X\cos\Omega t+Y\sin\Omega t)
=y-\Omega e^{2t}x.
\end{aligned}
$$
Replacing the <Lagrangian coordinates> by the current <Eulerian coordinates> therefore gives the <velocity field>
$$
\boxed{\mathbf u(x,y,t)=(-x+\Omega e^{-2t}y,\ y-\Omega e^{2t}x)}.
$$
The distinction between following a <fluid element> and differentiating at fixed <Eulerian coordinates> is what connects the given <deformation map> to this <velocity field>.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

For a planar <incompressible flow>, the <divergence> of the <velocity field> must vanish. Here
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}=-1+1=0.
$$
Equivalently, the <Jacobian determinant> of the <deformation map> is
$$
\det\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(X,Y)}
=e^{-t}e^t(\cos^2\Omega t+\sin^2\Omega t)=1,
$$
so every material area is preserved. However, the out-of-plane <vorticity> is
$$
\boxed{\omega_z=\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}
=-\Omega(e^{2t}+e^{-2t})=-2\Omega\cosh(2t)}.
$$
Since $\Omega>0$, this <vorticity> never vanishes. The motion is an <incompressible flow> but is not an <irrotational flow>; its local spin is clockwise.

Adopt the <streamfunction> convention $u=\psi_y$, $v=-\psi_x$. Integrating the first component of the <velocity field> in $y$ gives $\psi=-xy+\tfrac12\Omega e^{-2t}y^2+f(x,t)$. The second component then requires $f_x=\Omega e^{2t}x$. Thus, omitting an arbitrary function of time,
$$
\boxed{\psi(x,y,t)=\frac\Omega2(e^{2t}x^2+e^{-2t}y^2)-xy}.
$$
At a fixed instant, the <streamlines> are the connected curves $\psi=K$. To classify this <quadratic form>, introduce the invertible scaled and rotated coordinates
$$
\xi=e^t x,\quad \upsilon=e^{-t}y,\qquad
s=\frac{\xi+\upsilon}{\sqrt2},\quad d=\frac{\xi-\upsilon}{\sqrt2}.
$$
Then
$$
\boxed{2\psi=(\Omega-1)s^2+(\Omega+1)d^2}.
$$
For $\Omega>1$, both coefficients are positive. Every $K>0$ therefore gives an <ellipse>, and $K=0$ is the <stagnation point> at the origin. For $0<\Omega<1$, the <quadratic form> is an <indefinite quadratic form>: each $K\ne0$ gives a <hyperbola>. The level $K=0$ consists of the straight <separatrices>
$$
d=\pm\sqrt{\frac{1-\Omega}{1+\Omega}}\,s,
$$
whose rays approach or leave the origin; the origin itself is a <stagnation point>. At the transition $\Omega=1$, $\psi=d^2$, so the nonstationary <streamlines> are parallel straight lines and $d=0$ is a line of <stagnation points>. These are instantaneous <streamlines>; the time dependence means that they need not coincide with <pathlines>.

Use $w=\phi+i\psi$ for the <complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow>, so its <complex velocity> is $q=w'=u-iv$. The incident <uniform flow> and <line source> have
$$
f(z)=Uz+\log(z-b).
$$
The coefficient of the <complex logarithm> is one because a <line source> of strength $Q$ has <complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow> $(Q/(2\pi))\log(z-b)$, and here $Q=2\pi$. Applying the <Milne-Thomson circle theorem> to the unit <circle> gives
$$
w(z)=f(z)+\overline{f(1/\bar z)}
=Uz+\log(z-b)+\frac Uz+\log(1/z-b).
$$
Since $1/z-b=-b(z-1/b)/z$, locally consistent branches of the <complex logarithm> give
$$
\boxed{w(z)=Uz+\frac Uz+\log(z-b)
+\log(z-1/b)-\log z+C}.
$$
Changes of branch only alter the constant in a local expression; $w'$ is single valued away from its singularities. The added terms represent a <two-dimensional potential doublet> at the origin, an image <line source> at $1/b$, and a compensating <line sink> at the origin. The image <line source> and <line sink> have equal and opposite strengths, so no net <volume flux> enters the <cylinder>.

On $|z|=1$, the <Milne-Thomson circle theorem> makes $w=f(z)+\overline{f(z)}$ up to a constant. Consequently the <streamfunction> is constant there, establishing the <no-penetration boundary condition>. All <line source> strengths are real, and the <two-dimensional potential doublet> has zero <circulation>, so the <circulation> around the <cylinder> is zero. There is no additional <line vortex> term.

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Let $F_x,F_y$ denote the <force per unit length> exerted by the fluid on the <cylinder>, and let $\rho$ be the constant <mass density>. For a counterclockwise contour $C$ around the unit <circle>, the <Blasius force theorem> gives
$$
F_x-iF_y=\frac{i\rho}{2}\oint_C q(z)^2\,dz,
\qquad
q(z)=U-\frac U{z^2}+\frac1{z-b}+\frac1{z-a}-\frac1z,
\quad a=\frac1b.
$$
An efficient use of the <residue theorem> finds the sum of the enclosed <residues> by subtracting the external <pole> at $b$ from the sum of all finite <residues>. At large $z$,
$$
q(z)=U+\frac1z+O(z^{-2}),\qquad
q(z)^2=U^2+\frac{2U}{z}+O(z^{-2}).
$$
The sum of all finite <residues> of $q^2$ is therefore $2U$. At $b$, write $q=(z-b)^{-1}+H(z)$, with $H$ a <holomorphic function> near $b$. Then
$$
\operatorname{Res}_{z=b}q^2=2H(b),\qquad
H(b)=U-\frac U{b^2}+\frac1{b-a}-\frac1b.
$$
Thus
$$
\oint_Cq^2\,dz=2\pi i\,[2U-2H(b)],
\qquad
F_x-iF_y=2\pi\rho[H(b)-U].
$$
Using $a=1/b$ and $1/(b-a)-1/b=1/[b(b^2-1)]$ gives
$$
\boxed{F_x=2\pi\rho\left(\frac1{b(b^2-1)}-\frac U{b^2}\right),
\qquad F_y=0}.
$$
The vanishing vertical <force> also follows from <reflection symmetry> about the real axis. This <force on a unit cylinder due to a uniform flow and an external source> uses the counterclockwise orientation in the <Blasius force theorem> to fix the sign of the horizontal <force>.

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

Since $b>1$, the denominator in
$$
F_x=\frac{2\pi\rho}{b^2(b^2-1)}\,[b-U(b^2-1)]
$$
is positive. The positive root of $Ub^2-b-U=0$ is
$$
\boxed{b_* =\frac{1+\sqrt{1+4U^2}}{2U}>1}.
$$
The other root is negative, so the horizontal <force> points in the positive $x$ direction for $1<b<b_*$, vanishes at $b=b_*$, and points in the negative $x$ direction for $b>b_*$. In particular, the nearby <line source> pulls the <cylinder> towards itself as $b\downarrow1$, while at large $b$ the horizontal <force> is $F_x\sim-2\pi\rho U/b^2$.

The <Joukowski map> is a <holomorphic function> for $z\ne0$, with
$$
g'(z)=1-\frac1{z^2}.
$$
A <holomorphic function> is a local <conformal map> wherever its <derivative> is nonzero. Hence the two finite nonzero critical points are
$$
\boxed{z=1,\qquad z=-1}.
$$
They lie on the boundary of the fluid region. At $z=0$ the displayed formula has a <pole> and is undefined as a map to the finite <complex plane>; zero is outside the fluid region. Thus $g$ is locally <conformal> everywhere in the fluid.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

For $z=x+iy$,
$$
\operatorname{Im}g(z)=y\left(1-\frac1{x^2+y^2}\right).
$$
Both factors are positive in the fluid region, so its image is contained in the <upper half-plane>. The boundary correspondence is also explicit: on the upper unit <semicircle>, $z=e^{i\theta}$ gives $g(z)=2\cos\theta\in[-2,2]$, while the real rays $x>1$ and $x<-1$ map onto the rays $\zeta>2$ and $\zeta<-2$.

To prove the image is the whole <upper half-plane>, fix any $\operatorname{Im}\zeta>0$. The equation $g(z)=\zeta$ is
$$
z^2-\zeta z+1=0.
$$
Its roots have product one. Neither root can have unit modulus, since that would make $\zeta=z+1/z$ real. Exactly one root therefore has modulus greater than one. For that root the formula for $\operatorname{Im}g$ forces $y>0$, so it belongs to the fluid region. Moreover, if $g(z_1)=g(z_2)$, then
$$
(z_1-z_2)\left(1-\frac1{z_1z_2}\right)=0.
$$
Two distinct roots in the fluid region would require $z_1z_2=1$, impossible when both moduli exceed one. Thus the <Joukowski map of the upper exterior of a unit disk> is a <bijection> from the fluid region onto the <upper half-plane>.

= Solution

The <quadratic formula> gives two algebraic possibilities. To select one consistent <holomorphic square root>, set
$$
S(\zeta)=\sqrt{\zeta-2}\,\sqrt{\zeta+2},\qquad \operatorname{Im}\zeta>0,
$$
where each factor is the <principal square root>. Both arguments lie in the <upper half-plane>, so neither crosses its <branch cut>. This choice satisfies $S(\zeta)^2=\zeta^2-4$ and $S(\zeta)\sim\zeta$ at large $\zeta$ in the <upper half-plane>. The required <inverse function> is
$$
\boxed{z=g^{-1}(\zeta)=\frac{\zeta+S(\zeta)}2
=\frac{\zeta+\sqrt{\zeta-2}\sqrt{\zeta+2}}2}.
$$
For $\zeta=iR$, $R>0$, this gives $z=i(R+\sqrt{R^2+4})/2$, whose modulus exceeds one. As $\zeta$ varies continuously in the <upper half-plane>, this root cannot cross $|z|=1$, since that would make $\zeta$ real. Hence the selected root always lies in the fluid region. The other root is its reciprocal and lies inside the unit <disk>. Simply writing the <principal square root> of $\zeta^2-4$ would not specify this branch correctly throughout the <upper half-plane>.

Take positive $\Gamma$ to mean counterclockwise <circulation>. A <line vortex> at $\zeta_0$ has <complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow> $\Gamma\log(\zeta-\zeta_0)/(2\pi i)$. The <method of images> imposes the <no-penetration boundary condition> on the real axis by placing an image <line vortex> of strength $-\Gamma$ at $\bar\zeta_0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{W(\zeta)=\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi i}
\log\!\left(\frac{\zeta-\zeta_0}{\zeta-\bar\zeta_0}\right)+C}.
$$
For real $\zeta$, the numerator and denominator have equal modulus, so the <streamfunction> is
$$
\operatorname{Im}W=-\frac\Gamma{2\pi}
\log\left|\frac{\zeta-\zeta_0}{\zeta-\bar\zeta_0}\right|
=\text{constant}.
$$
The real axis is therefore a <streamline>. The image <line vortex> lies outside the mapped fluid region, while the <complex velocity>
$$
W'(\zeta)=\frac\Gamma{2\pi i}
\left(\frac1{\zeta-\zeta_0}-\frac1{\zeta-\bar\zeta_0}\right)
$$
decays as $|\zeta|\to\infty$. This selects the flow due to the <line vortex> and the boundary, without superposing an imposed background <velocity field>.

The physical <complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow> is $w(z)=W(g(z))$. Applying the <chain rule> gives the <complex velocity>
$$
\boxed{u-iv=w'(z)=\frac\Gamma{2\pi i}
\left(1-\frac1{z^2}\right)
\left[\frac1{z+z^{-1}-c-c^{-1}}
-\frac1{z+z^{-1}-\bar c-\bar c^{-1}}\right]}.
$$
The physical <velocity> is $(\operatorname{Re}w',-\operatorname{Im}w')$. A useful equivalent expression follows from
$$
g(z)-g(c)=\frac{(z-c)(z-c^{-1})}{z}.
$$
Factoring the image expression in the same way makes the $-1/z$ terms cancel, leaving
$$
\boxed{u-iv=\frac\Gamma{2\pi i}
\left[\frac1{z-c}+\frac1{z-c^{-1}}
-\frac1{z-\bar c}-\frac1{z-\bar c^{-1}}\right]}.
$$
This represents the physical <line vortex> at $c$, an image <line vortex> of the same strength at $c^{-1}$, and image <point vortices> of opposite strength at $\bar c$ and $\bar c^{-1}$. All three image locations are outside the fluid region. Both boundary pieces inherit the constant <streamfunction> of the real axis in the mapped <complex plane>.

{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

A free <point vortex> is advected by the regular part of the <velocity field> at its position. Its own singular <complex velocity> $\Gamma/[2\pi i(z-c)]$ must first be subtracted. The image expression therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\dot x_c-i\dot y_c
=\lim_{z\to c}\left[w'(z)-\frac\Gamma{2\pi i(z-c)}\right]
=\frac\Gamma{2\pi i}
\left[\frac1{c-c^{-1}}-\frac1{c-\bar c}
-\frac1{c-\bar c^{-1}}\right]}.
$$
Equivalently, $\dot c$ is the <complex conjugate> of the boxed expression. This is the instantaneous <velocity>, evaluated with the current value of $c$.

To see why mapping the image <velocity> alone would miss a term, expand the mapped physical singularity:
$$
\frac{g'(z)}{g(z)-g(c)}
=\frac1{z-c}+\frac{g''(c)}{2g'(c)}+O(z-c).
$$
Thus the same <point-vortex drift under a conformal map> can be written
$$
\dot x_c-i\dot y_c
=\frac\Gamma{2\pi i}
\left[\frac{g''(c)}{2g'(c)}
-\frac{g'(c)}{g(c)-\overline{g(c)}}\right].
$$
The finite term involving $g''$ is part of the physical regular <velocity field>.

If $c=id$ with $d>1$, direct substitution into the image expression gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot x_c-i\dot y_c
&=\frac\Gamma{2\pi}
\left[-\frac d{d^2+1}+\frac1{2d}+\frac d{d^2-1}\right]\\
&=\boxed{\frac\Gamma{2\pi}
\left[\frac1{2d}+\frac{2d}{d^4-1}\right]}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The bracket is strictly positive, and the result is real. Hence a positive <line vortex> initially moves horizontally to the right, a negative <line vortex> moves to the left, and the initial vertical <velocity> is zero. As $d\to\infty$, the leading term $\Gamma/(4\pi d)$ is the familiar drift of a <point vortex> above a straight wall.

For an <irrotational flow>, write $\mathbf u=\nabla\phi$. The <incompressible flow> condition then gives the <Laplace equation>
$$
\boxed{\phi_{xx}+\phi_{yy}=0,\qquad -h<y<\eta(x,t)}.
$$
At leading order in a small disturbance this equation holds on $-h<y<0$. Including <gravity>, the <unsteady Bernoulli equation> is
$$
\phi_t+\frac12|\nabla\phi|^2+\frac p\rho+gy=B(t).
$$
Choose the time-dependent additive freedom in the <velocity potential> so that $B(t)=p_0/\rho$. Neglecting <surface tension>, <pressure continuity> at the <free surface> gives the exact dynamic condition
$$
\phi_t(x,\eta,t)+\frac12|\nabla\phi(x,\eta,t)|^2
+\frac{p_1}{\rho}\cos(x-ct)+g\eta=0.
$$
The <free surface> is a material boundary: applying the <material derivative> to $y-\eta(x,t)=0$ yields the <kinematic boundary condition for a free-surface graph>
$$
\eta_t+\phi_x(x,\eta,t)\eta_x=\phi_y(x,\eta,t).
$$
For a small disturbance, the <Taylor expansion> from $y=\eta$ to $y=0$ changes the first-order <velocity potential> and its <partial derivatives> only at second order. Both $|\nabla\phi|^2$ and $\phi_x\eta_x$ are also second-order terms. The <linearized free-surface boundary conditions> are therefore
$$
\boxed{\phi_t+\frac{p_1}{\rho}\cos(x-ct)+g\eta=0,
\qquad \phi_y=\eta_t\quad (y=0)}.
$$
The fixed bed has the <no-penetration boundary condition>
$$
\boxed{\phi_y=0\quad(y=-h)}.
$$
The required approximation is small <surface elevation>, small <wave steepness>, and small <velocity> perturbations. The stated $p_1\ll p_0$ alone does not ensure this approximation near <resonance>.

Seek the travelling forced component of the <surface elevation> in the form $\eta=A\cos\chi$, where $\chi=x-ct$ and $A$ is a signed coefficient. The forcing has <wavenumber> one. <Separation of variables> in the <Laplace equation>, followed by the bed <no-penetration boundary condition>, gives
$$
\phi=B\cosh(y+h)\sin\chi.
$$
Since $\eta_t=Ac\sin\chi$, the surface <kinematic boundary condition> requires $B\sinh h=Ac$, so
$$
\boxed{\phi=\frac{Ac}{\sinh h}\cosh(y+h)\sin(x-ct)}.
$$
At $y=0$, $\phi_t=-Ac^2\coth h\cos\chi$. The dynamic member of the <linearized free-surface boundary conditions> therefore becomes
$$
A(g-c^2\coth h)+\frac{p_1}{\rho}=0.
$$
Consequently the signed response and the nonnegative <amplitude> are
$$
\boxed{A=-\frac{p_1}{\rho(g-c^2\coth h)},\qquad
a(c)=|A|=\frac{p_1}{\rho\,|g-c^2\coth h|}}.
$$
The <travelling-pressure response of finite-depth surface gravity waves> is opposite in phase to the imposed <pressure> below the resonant speed and in phase above it. These expressions describe the travelling forced component; freely propagating <surface gravity waves> can be superposed to meet additional <initial conditions>.

= Solution

The denominator of the travelling response vanishes at
$$
\boxed{c^*=\sqrt{g\tanh h}}.
$$
For the positive speeds in the paper, a finite travelling response exists whenever $c\ne c^*$. At $c=c^*$, the dynamic condition would reduce to $p_1/\rho=0$, which is impossible. The finite-depth <dispersion relation> of a <surface gravity wave> with <wavenumber> $k$ is $\omega^2=gk\tanh(kh)$. At $k=1$, its <phase speed> $\omega/k$ equals $c^*$: the moving <pressure> pattern drives a natural <surface gravity wave> at its own speed, producing <resonance>.

The restriction concerns a bounded travelling response. To distinguish this from existence of a solution of an <initial value problem>, set $T=\tanh h$, $P=p_1/\rho$, and $\omega_*^2=gT$. If
$$
\eta(x,t)=\operatorname{Re}(H(t)e^{ix}),\qquad
\phi(x,y,t)=\operatorname{Re}\!\left(
\frac{\dot H(t)}{\sinh h}\cosh(y+h)e^{ix}\right),
$$
the <linearized free-surface boundary conditions> reduce to the <forced harmonic oscillator>
$$
\boxed{\ddot H+\omega_*^2H=-PT\,e^{-ict}}.
$$
For example, if the forcing is switched on at $t=0$ from a flat surface with zero initial <velocity>, then, for $c\ne\omega_*$,
$$
H(t)=\frac{PT}{\omega_*^2-c^2}
\left[\cos(\omega_*t)-i\frac c{\omega_*}\sin(\omega_*t)-e^{-ict}\right].
$$
This satisfies $H(0)=\dot H(0)=0$ and contains both free oscillations and the forced component. At $c=\omega_*$ a solution of the <initial value problem> still exists, but its <particular solution> contains
$$
H_{\rm sec}(t)=-\frac{iPT}{2\omega_*}\,t e^{-i\omega_*t},
$$
which grows linearly with time. Thus the ideal <inviscid flow> model predicts resonant growth rather than a finite constant <amplitude>. Additional free oscillations can set the <initial conditions> but cannot remove that secular growth.

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

For $0<c<c^*$, the nonnegative <amplitude> is
$$
a(c)=\frac{p_1\tanh h}{\rho[(c^*)^2-c^2]}
=\frac{p_1}{\rho g}\frac1{1-(c/c^*)^2}.
$$
Its first two <derivatives> are positive in this interval:
$$
\frac{da}{dc}=\frac{2p_1\tanh h\,c}
{\rho[(c^*)^2-c^2]^2}>0,\qquad
\frac{d^2a}{dc^2}=\frac{2p_1\tanh h\,[(c^*)^2+3c^2]}
{\rho[(c^*)^2-c^2]^3}>0.
$$
The graph is increasing and convex, starts with a horizontal tangent at the limiting value $a(0)=p_1/(\rho g)$, and has a vertical <asymptote> at $c=c^*$. Therefore the <hydrostatic lower bound for a travelling-pressure response> is
$$
\boxed{a_{\rm crit}=\frac{p_1}{\rho g},\qquad a(c)>a_{\rm crit}\quad(0<c<c^*)}.
$$
Because the paper assumes $c>0$, this is an <infimum>, approached as $c\downarrow0$, rather than an attained <minimum>. If the stationary limit $c=0$ is admitted, equality holds: the <hydrostatic equilibrium> condition is $\rho g\eta=-p_1\cos x$. Near resonance,
$$
a(c)\sim\frac{p_1}{\rho g}\frac{c^*}{2(c^*-c)}.
$$
The divergence describes the linear model; sufficiently close to $c^*$ the small-disturbance assumptions fail. The lower bound and sketch refer to the travelling forced component, since adding free oscillations can change the instantaneous <surface elevation>.
