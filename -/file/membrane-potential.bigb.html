= Membrane potential
{title2=$V=V_{\mathrm{inside}}-V_{\mathrm{outside}}$}
{wiki}

A <membrane potential> is an <electric potential difference> across a <cell membrane>. We use the convention
$$
V_m=\phi_{\mathrm{inside}}-\phi_{\mathrm{outside}}.
$$
Taking the external <electric potential> as zero makes $V_m<0$ mean that the inside is at lower <electric potential>. “Negative inside” describes a difference, not an absolute <electric potential>, and does not imply that the entire <cytosol> has a large net negative <electric charge>.

Consider a <cell membrane> permeable only to <potassium> <ions>, with higher <potassium> <concentration> inside. <Diffusion> initially favors outward transport. If balancing negative <ions> cannot follow, that loss of positive <electric charge> produces a small inner excess of negative <electric charge> and an outer positive excess. The resulting <electric field> favors inward <potassium> transport. At the <Nernst equilibrium potential>, chemical and electrical contributions to <electrochemical potential> balance; <ions> still cross both ways, with zero net transport.<Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others/Ion Channels and the Electrical Properties of Membranes>[Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others; The Resting Membrane Potential Is Determined Mainly by K+ Leak Channels in Most Animal Cells: selective potassium passage and opposing electrical influence]{ref}

\Image[membrane-potential-forces-and-electrodes.png]
{title=Opposing influences on <potassium> transport and the sign of an intracellular voltage recording}
{description=Original conceptual diagrams: a selective <cell membrane> with $V_m<0$, and an intracellular <electrode> compared with an external <reference electrode> in seawater. The recording diagram illustrates connections and sign, not a historical apparatus reconstruction or a measured trace. Arrows indicate transport tendencies; particle numbers and distances are schematic.}

In living <biological cells>, several <ions> can permeate and pumps maintain their <concentration> differences through <active transport>. Pumps transporting net <electric charge> also contribute <electric current>. Thus $V_m=E_{\mathrm K}$ is a special limit, not a universal rule; <membrane potential> need not always be negative.<Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others/Ion Channels and the Electrical Properties of Membranes>[Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others; K+ leak-channel discussion; The Resting Potential Decays Only Slowly When the Na+-K+ Pump Is Stopped; action-potential discussion]{ref} The existing <reversal potential> example shows how opposing <electric currents> can balance without each <ion> reaching <thermodynamic equilibrium>. This makes <selective permeability> a way to convert chemical differences into electrical signals: changing the available <ion channels> changes which <electrochemical gradients> influence the voltage.

= Repolarization
{parent=Membrane potential}
{wiki}

Return of a depolarized <membrane potential> toward a more negative value. During an <action potential>, reduced inward current and increased outward current commonly contribute. It is distinct from <hyperpolarization>, which takes the membrane beyond its reference resting value.

= Selective potassium equilibrium
{parent=Membrane potential}
{title2=$E_{\mathrm K}=\frac{RT}{F}\ln(c_{\mathrm{out}}/c_{\mathrm{in}})$}

For an ideal dilute <chemical solution>, moving one <mole> of a species with signed <ion> charge number $z$ from outside to inside changes its <electrochemical potential> by
$$
\Delta\widetilde\mu=RT\ln\!\left(\frac{c_{\mathrm{in}}}{c_{\mathrm{out}}}\right)+zFV_m.
$$
Here $R$ is the <molar gas constant>, $T$ the uniform absolute <temperature>, $F$ the <Faraday constant>, and $c$ the <concentration>. This assumes the same reference <chemical potential> on both sides. At <thermodynamic equilibrium>, $\Delta\widetilde\mu=0$, hence
$$
E_{\mathrm{ion}}=\frac{RT}{zF}\ln\!\left(\frac{c_{\mathrm{out}}}{c_{\mathrm{in}}}\right).
$$
The <Nernst equilibrium potential> uses <thermodynamic activities> in the general case; replacing them by <concentrations> is an ideal-solution approximation.

Choose an original passive model: only $\mathrm K^+$ permeates ($z=+1$), other <ions> cannot cross, there are no pumps or imposed <electric currents>, and there is no net <water> transfer. Stipulate reservoir <concentrations> $c_{\mathrm{in}}=140\,\mathrm{mM}$ and $c_{\mathrm{out}}=5\,\mathrm{mM}$, with $T=310\,\mathrm K$, $R=8.314\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ and $F=96485\,\mathrm{C\,mol^{-1}}$. Treat reservoir <concentrations> as effectively unchanged by charging. Then
$$
E_{\mathrm K}=\frac{8.314\times310}{96485}\ln\!\left(\frac5{140}\right)\,\mathrm V
\approx-0.08901\,\mathrm V=-89.01\,\mathrm{mV}.
$$
The <concentration> ratio is dimensionless; $\mathrm{J/C}=\mathrm V$ supplies the voltage unit. At $V_m=0$, $\Delta\widetilde\mu>0$ for inward transport, so outward transport is favored. At $V_m=E_{\mathrm K}$ it vanishes. A more negative $V_m$ favors inward transport. These sign checks explain the result physically.

Keeping the other chosen inputs fixed, doubling $c_{\mathrm{out}}$ to $10\,\mathrm{mM}$ raises $E_{\mathrm K}$ by $(RT/F)\ln2\approx18.52\,\mathrm{mV}$. Equal inside and outside <concentrations> give $E_{\mathrm K}=0$; reversing their ratio reverses its sign. In finite reservoirs, transport changes both <concentrations>; the exact final <thermodynamic equilibrium> must also satisfy material conservation. The fixed-reservoir calculation is useful when only a small fraction of the available <ions> moves.

= Membrane charge separation
{parent=Membrane potential}
{title2=$Q_{\mathrm{inside}}=CV_m$}

A <membrane potential> can coexist with near <electroneutrality> in the bulk <cytosol> and external <chemical solution>. The small imbalance producing the voltage lies near the <cell membrane>; most positive and negative <electric charges> balance in the bulk.<Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others/Ion Channels and the Electrical Properties of Membranes>[Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others; The Resting Potential Decays Only Slowly When the Na+-K+ Pump Is Stopped; Figure 11-22: minute charge redistribution and interfacial excess]{ref}

For a separate original <capacitor> model, choose a total <membrane capacitance> $C=5\,\mathrm{pF}$ and $V_m=-70\,\mathrm{mV}$. These are stipulated parameters, not measured properties of a particular <biological cell>, and this voltage is not asserted to equal the preceding $E_{\mathrm K}$. Model the separated <electric charge> relative to an uncharged reference by
$$
Q_{\mathrm{inside}}=CV_m=(5\times10^{-12})(-70\times10^{-3})\,\mathrm C=-3.5\times10^{-13}\,\mathrm C,\qquad Q_{\mathrm{outside}}=-Q_{\mathrm{inside}}.
$$
Using the <elementary charge> $e=1.602176634\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$, this represents
$$
N_{\mathrm{excess}}=\frac{|Q_{\mathrm{inside}}|}{e}\approx2.18\times10^6
$$
excess monovalent negative-charge equivalents inside. These can represent a deficit of positive <ions>, rather than the arrival of that many negative <ions>.

Independently choose an internal reservoir <volume (thermodynamics)> $\mathcal V=1\,\mathrm{pL}=10^{-12}\,\mathrm L$ containing $140\,\mathrm{mM}=0.140\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ <potassium>, with balancing negative <electric charge>. With the <Avogadro constant> $N_A=6.02214076\times10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}$, its <potassium> inventory is
$$
N_{\mathrm K}=c\mathcal V N_A\approx8.43\times10^{10},\qquad
\frac{N_{\mathrm{excess}}}{N_{\mathrm K}}\approx2.59\times10^{-5}=0.00259\%.
$$
Thus, if charging were entirely due to outward monovalent <potassium> transport, about one in $38\,600$ of these <potassium> <ions> would suffice. This is a comparison with the chosen <potassium> inventory, not a fraction of all charged particles or a measurement of spatial <charge density>. No shape, surface area or thickness has been inferred from the chosen <capacitance> and <volume (thermodynamics)>. The calculation illustrates why substantial voltage does not require a strongly charged bulk <cytosol>.

= Intracellular recording of membrane potential
{parent=Membrane potential}

In 1939, <Alan Hodgkin> and <Andrew Huxley> reported intracellular <action potential> recordings from squid giant <axons> at Plymouth.<Action Potentials Recorded from Inside a Nerve Fibre by Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley>[Action Potentials Recorded from Inside a Nerve Fibre, Nature 144 (1939), pp. 710–711: inspected publisher abstract, preparation and intracellular recording]{ref}

<Alan Hodgkin>'s later account describes advancing a capillary <electrode> from a cut end into intact <axon>, avoiding scraping the <cell membrane>. Its Figure 3 uses a seawater-filled capillary internally, with external seawater as zero: the readout is internal relative to external <electric potential>. This supports a voltage across the surface <cell membrane>. As a separate ionic control, the account reports that raising external <potassium> toward its internal <concentration> reversibly abolished resting voltage; this was later work, not a control asserted for the 1939 recording. Injury and electrode-solution junction potentials limit interpretation. Voltage recording alone does not identify every conducting <ion>. A <voltage clamp> additionally uses feedback to impose voltage and measures the required <electric current>.<The ionic basis of nervous conduction by Alan Hodgkin>[Alan Lloyd Hodgkin, The ionic basis of nervous conduction, Nobel Lecture (11 December 1963), pp. 34–36, Figures 2–3: placement, seawater reference and later potassium control; p. 42: voltage clamp]{ref}

In the schematic, connect the intracellular <electrode> to the positive input and the external <reference electrode> to the negative input of a voltage recorder. After electrode-offset correction, a negative reading means $\phi_{\mathrm{inside}}<\phi_{\mathrm{outside}}$. A useful baseline check is to compare both <electrodes> in the same external <chemical solution> before insertion; this is a measurement check, not an additional historical observation claimed here. Swapping inputs reverses the readout sign without changing the <biological cell>'s <membrane potential>.

= Depolarization
{parent=Membrane potential}
{wiki}

For a negative neuronal <membrane potential>, <depolarization> raises it toward zero or beyond, making the interior less negative relative to the exterior. This describes a voltage change, not necessarily increased <action potential> generation; see the depolarizing inhibition example under <synaptic potential>.

= Hyperpolarization
{parent=Membrane potential}

<Hyperpolarization> makes a neuronal <membrane potential> more negative relative to the exterior. It is a voltage description; inhibition need not produce <hyperpolarization>, as <shunting inhibition> illustrates.

= Reversal potential
{parent=Membrane potential}
{wiki}
{title2=$I=g(E_{\mathrm{rev}}-V)$}

A <reversal potential> $E_{\mathrm{rev}}$ is a <membrane potential> at which a specified open <ion channel>, or specified combination of <ion channels>, carries zero net <electric current>, with the surrounding conditions fixed; the <electric current> changes sign across that voltage. Specifying which current matters: an individual <ion> contribution and a sum of several contributions can reverse at different voltages. Zero net <electric current> does not mean that all <ions> have stopped moving. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK11013/[Purves et al., Neuroscience, 2nd edition (2001): reversal of a mixed-ion channel current].

Throughout these examples, <membrane potential> is $V=V_{\mathrm{inside}}-V_{\mathrm{outside}}$, and **<conventional current> is inward-positive**. Positive <electric current> corresponds to net positive <electric charge> entering or net negative <electric charge> leaving. Negative <electric current> corresponds to the reverse charge transfer. A convenient Ohmic approximation is
$$
I_{\mathrm{in}}=g(E_{\mathrm{rev}}-V),\qquad g\geq0,
$$
where $g$ is the available <electrical conductance>. For fixed $g>0$, $V<E_{\mathrm{rev}}$ gives positive <electric current>, $V=E_{\mathrm{rev}}$ gives zero, and $V>E_{\mathrm{rev}}$ gives negative <electric current>; the line has slope $-g$. If $g=0$, the modeled pathway carries zero <electric current> at every voltage: closing it does not locate its <reversal potential>. <Neuronal Dynamics: From Single Neurons to Networks and Models of Cognition by Wulfram Gerstner and others/3.1 Synapses> (§3.1: conductance-based synaptic current) uses outward-positive $g(V-E)$; the formula here reverses that sign to match this wiki's inward-positive convention.

For the stipulated single-pathway example from <ion channel>, $E_{\mathrm{rev}}=-80\,\mathrm{mV}$ and $g=10\,\mathrm{nS}$ give
$$
\begin{array}{c|rrr}
V\ (\mathrm{mV})&-100&-80&-60\\
E_{\mathrm{rev}}-V\ (\mathrm{mV})&20&0&-20\\
I_{\mathrm{in}}\ (\mathrm{pA})&200&0&-200
\end{array}
$$
The open <ion channel> carries zero net <electric current> at $-80\,\mathrm{mV}$ even though $g$ remains positive. Near that point a change of <membrane potential> changes the <electric current>, which is why zero current at one voltage does not make an open pathway electrically irrelevant; see <shunting inhibition>.

\Image[ion-channel-current-and-reversal.png]
{title=Inward-positive <electric current>: one reversal and two-channel cancellation}
{description=Original computed curves for stipulated constant <electrical conductances> and fixed <reversal potentials>. Left: $g=10\,\mathrm{nS}$, $E_{\mathrm{rev}}=-80\,\mathrm{mV}$. Right: a <potassium> pathway with $g_{\mathrm K}=10\,\mathrm{nS}$, $E_{\mathrm K}=-80\,\mathrm{mV}$ and a <sodium> pathway with $g_{\mathrm{Na}}=2\,\mathrm{nS}$, $E_{\mathrm{Na}}=+50\,\mathrm{mV}$. Their net <electric current> vanishes at $-58.3333\,\mathrm{mV}$ while both individual <electric currents> remain nonzero. These are model values, not empirical measurements.}

For an <ion channel> passing only one <ion> species by <passive transport>, its <reversal potential> can equal that species' <Nernst equilibrium potential>, where the chemical and electrical contributions to <electrochemical potential> balance. At that <thermodynamic equilibrium>, passage in both directions can continue with no net transport. For an <ion channel> admitting several <ion> species, opposing contributions can cancel while each species continues to have net transport. Such a <reversal potential> is not generally any one species' <Nernst equilibrium potential>. See the Purves reference above.

To work out that cancellation explicitly, put two Ohmic <ion channel> populations across the same <cell membrane>. Stipulate constant <electrical conductances> and fixed <reversal potentials>:
$$
g_{\mathrm K}=10\,\mathrm{nS},\quad E_{\mathrm K}=-80\,\mathrm{mV},\qquad g_{\mathrm{Na}}=2\,\mathrm{nS},\quad E_{\mathrm{Na}}=+50\,\mathrm{mV}.
$$
The <potassium> and <sodium> contributions add because both pathways experience the same <membrane potential>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
I_{\mathrm{total}}&=g_{\mathrm K}(E_{\mathrm K}-V)+g_{\mathrm{Na}}(E_{\mathrm{Na}}-V)\\
&=(g_{\mathrm K}+g_{\mathrm{Na}})(E_{\mathrm{eff}}-V),\\
E_{\mathrm{eff}}&=\frac{g_{\mathrm K}E_{\mathrm K}+g_{\mathrm{Na}}E_{\mathrm{Na}}}{g_{\mathrm K}+g_{\mathrm{Na}}}
=\frac{-700}{12}\,\mathrm{mV}\approx-58.3333\,\mathrm{mV}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus $I_{\mathrm{total}}=12\,\mathrm{nS}(E_{\mathrm{eff}}-V)$. This <weighted mean> follows simply by expanding and regrouping the two current terms; it assumes additive Ohmic contributions and a positive total <electrical conductance>. At $V=E_{\mathrm{eff}}$, the inward-positive individual <electric currents> are
$$
I_{\mathrm K}=-\frac{650}{3}\,\mathrm{pA}\approx-216.6667\,\mathrm{pA},\qquad
I_{\mathrm{Na}}=+\frac{650}{3}\,\mathrm{pA}\approx+216.6667\,\mathrm{pA}.
$$
The <potassium> contribution is outward and the <sodium> contribution inward. Equal and opposite <electric currents> give $I_{\mathrm{total}}=0$; neither pathway has stopped transporting its <ions>. The calculation treats two distinct pathways. Applying it to one mixed-species <ion channel> would additionally require a justified decomposition into independent Ohmic contributions; a general mixed-channel <reversal potential> cannot be obtained by assuming those hypotheses silently.

This is an instantaneous balance of the specified passive <electric currents>. It would be the stationary voltage of a passive <capacitance> model containing only these constant pathways and no applied <electric current>, but it is not a complete prediction of a living cell's resting <membrane potential>. Other <electric currents>, voltage-dependent gating, and pumps involved in <active transport> can matter. Pumps that transfer net <electric charge> are electrogenic and contribute their own <electric current>. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK10857/[Purves et al.: functional properties of the sodium/potassium pump].

The toy model holds <ion> <concentrations> and therefore the stipulated <reversal potentials> fixed. Continuing inward <sodium> and outward <potassium> transport would change finite reservoirs unless <active transport> or external control maintained them. Transport can be a small fraction of the reservoir's <ions> over a short interval, making fixed <concentrations> a useful approximation; it is not a claim that the reservoirs never change. See https://neuronaldynamics.epfl.ch/online/Ch2.S1.html[Gerstner et al., Neuronal Dynamics (2014), §2.1: equilibrium potentials, channel currents and maintained gradients].

A <reversal potential> makes the effect of opening an <ion channel> predictable from the present <membrane potential>. The same open pathway can carry inward or outward <electric current> as $V$ crosses its <reversal potential>. The two-pathway example reveals a particularly useful distinction: zero net charge transfer can coexist with continuing exchange of different substances. That distinction connects <membrane transport> to <electric current> measurements without mistaking electrical balance for <thermodynamic equilibrium>.

= Nernst equilibrium potential
{c}
{parent=Reversal potential}
{title2=$E_{\mathrm{ion}}=\frac{RT}{zF}\ln\!\left(\frac{a_{\mathrm{outside}}}{a_{\mathrm{inside}}}\right)$}

= Nernst potential
{c}
{synonym}

The <Nernst equilibrium potential> is the inside-minus-outside <membrane potential> at which one <ion> species has equal <electrochemical potential> on both sides of a <cell membrane>. At uniform <temperature> $T$, using the same reference <chemical potential> on both sides, $z\neq0$ is the signed <ion> charge number, $R$ the <molar gas constant>, $F$ the <Faraday constant>, and $a$ the dimensionless <thermodynamic activity> of that <ion>. For an ideal dilute <chemical solution>, the activity ratio becomes the <concentration> ratio. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK11102/[Purves et al.: the forces that create membrane potentials and the Nernst equation].

The short derivation is to set the outside-to-inside <electrochemical potential> change from <membrane transport> to zero: $RT\ln(a_{\mathrm{inside}}/a_{\mathrm{outside}})+zFV=0$, and solve for $V$. An open pathway carrying only this species by <passive transport> then has this <reversal potential>. A balance of several nonzero <ion> <electric currents> does not establish this equilibrium condition for each species.

= Voltage-gated ion channel
{parent=Membrane potential}
{wiki}

A membrane channel whose opening depends on <membrane potential>. Regenerative activation and subsequent inactivation or opposing currents help produce an <action potential>.

= Ion-channel deactivation
{parent=Voltage-gated ion channel}

Closure of an activation gate when the activating stimulus is removed. It is distinct from <ion-channel inactivation>, which reduces channel availability while the activating conditions can remain present.

= Ion-channel gating current
{parent=Voltage-gated ion channel}

A transient electrical current associated with displacement of voltage-sensor charge during <ion channel> gating, distinct from current carried through an open permeation pathway. Its voltage dependence and changes following sensor mutations constrain the charge motions that couple <membrane potential> to opening.

= Ion-channel gating charge
{parent=Ion-channel gating current}
{title2=$Q_g=\int I_g\,dt$}

The net displaced <electric charge> associated with a change in channel conformation, inferred by integrating the <ion-channel gating current> after subtracting unrelated currents. It is distinct from the <electric current> itself and from charge carried through the open pore.

= Voltage-gated sodium channel
{parent=Voltage-gated ion channel}

A <voltage-gated sodium channel> couples voltage-sensor movement to a sodium-permeable pore. Activation and inactivation are distinct transitions, and loss of current must be separated from altered expression or membrane delivery.

= Voltage-sensing domain
{parent=Voltage-gated ion channel}

A <voltage-sensing domain> changes conformation in response to membrane voltage. Charged residues, often in an S4 helix, redistribute in the electric field and couple to other functional parts of the protein.

= Voltage-gated potassium channel
{parent=Voltage-gated ion channel}

A <voltage-gated potassium channel> couples a voltage-sensing region to a potassium-selective pore. The sensor determines voltage-dependent opening, whereas filter coordination chemistry contributes to which ions permeate.

= Voltage-gated calcium channel
{parent=Voltage-gated ion channel}

A <voltage-gated calcium channel> couples membrane voltage to a calcium-permeable pore. In a pancreatic beta cell, depolarization-dependent calcium entry can connect metabolic sensing to insulin-granule exocytosis.

= Ion-channel inactivation
{parent=Voltage-gated ion channel}

<Ion-channel inactivation> is loss of availability for conduction, distinct from simply closing an otherwise activatable <ion channel>. Recovery depends on channel type and <membrane potential>; incomplete recovery of <sodium>-selective <voltage-gated ion channels> contributes to a <neuronal refractory period>.

= C-type inactivation
{parent=Ion-channel inactivation}
{c}

An availability change associated with the outer pore and selectivity filter of certain <potassium> <ion channels>. Its kinetics and structural origin differ from <ball-and-chain inactivation>, although the two processes can interact.

= Ball-and-chain inactivation

{wiki}

= N-type inactivation
{synonym}
{c}

A tethered intracellular particle blocks an open <ion channel> from the cytoplasmic side. In classical Shaker-type channels the particle is N-terminal: removing it suppresses fast inactivation, while cytoplasmic application of a corresponding peptide can restore block. The pore-blocking mechanism differs from ordinary activation-gate closure.

= Two-state ion-channel gating
{parent=Voltage-gated ion channel}
{title2=$\dot X=\alpha_X(V)(1-X)-\beta_X(V)X$}

<Two-state ion-channel gating> models an individual gate as a random switch between closed and open states. Write $p(t)$ for its open <probability>; this is the $X$ in the header equation. An individual gate's state is always closed or open, rather than partly open. A population of identical gates can have a fraction open near $p(t)$, and averaging repeated identically prepared trials estimates the same <probability>. A finite population's observed fraction fluctuates. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK10841/[Purves and colleagues, Neuroscience, 2nd edition (2001): Ion Channels Underlying Action Potentials] for single-channel steps and their population averages.

At fixed <membrane potential> $V$, let constant rates $\alpha,\beta>0$ govern closed-to-open and open-to-closed transitions respectively, in $\mathrm{s}^{-1}$. This is a two-state <continuous-time Markov chain>. Over a short interval $\Delta t$, an initially closed gate opens with <probability> $\alpha\Delta t+o(\Delta t)$, and an initially open gate closes with <probability> $\beta\Delta t+o(\Delta t)$. Gain minus loss gives the deterministic <ordinary differential equation> for the <probability>:
$$
\frac{dp}{dt}=\alpha(1-p)-\beta p=\frac{p_\infty-p}{\tau},\qquad
p_\infty=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta},\qquad \tau=\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}.
$$
Here $p_\infty$ is the fixed-voltage limiting open <probability>, and $\tau$ is a <time constant>. The ratio of the rates fixes the limit, while their sum fixes the speed. With initial <probability> $p(0)=p_0\in[0,1]$,
$$
p(t)=p_\infty+(p_0-p_\infty)e^{-t/\tau}.
$$
For a short derivation, subtract $p_\infty$: the deviation obeys $d(p-p_\infty)/dt=-(p-p_\infty)/\tau$, yielding the <exponential function>. After one <time constant>, $1/e$ of the initial deviation remains. The solution stays between $p_0$ and $p_\infty$, so it remains a valid <probability>. The rate and <time constant> formulations are described in https://neuronaldynamics.epfl.ch/online/Ch2.S2.html[Gerstner and colleagues, Neuronal Dynamics, §2.2.2: transition rates and channel kinetics].

For a worked toy condition A, choose $\alpha=\beta=50\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ and $p_0=0.1$. Then $p_\infty=0.5$, $\tau=0.01\,\mathrm{s}=10\,\mathrm{ms}$, and
$$
p(10\,\mathrm{ms})=0.5-0.4e^{-1}\approx0.352848.
$$
In a separately prepared condition B, choose $\alpha=200\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, $\beta=50\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ and the same $p_0=0.1$. Now $p_\infty=0.8$, $\tau=4\,\mathrm{ms}$, and $p(10\,\mathrm{ms})=0.8-0.7e^{-2.5}\approx0.742540$. Condition B approaches a higher limit more quickly. These are two stipulated constant-rate conditions with $V$ held fixed in each experiment, not an empirical function relating rates to voltage or a sequential switch between conditions. The initial $p_0=0.1$ need not be the limiting <probability> at the newly imposed voltage.

{title=<Two-state ion-channel gating>: two constant-rate relaxation conditions}
{description=Original computed <probability> curves, both starting at $p_0=0.1$. The dot marks condition A at $10\,\mathrm{ms}$, where $p\approx0.352848$. Smooth curves represent open <probability> or population averages, not an individual gate's random state trace or an ionic <electric current>.}

In the <Hodgkin-Huxley model>, the rates depend on <membrane potential>: $\alpha_X(V)$ and $\beta_X(V)$. Its $X_\infty(V)=\alpha_X(V)/(\alpha_X(V)+\beta_X(V))$ is the limit for voltage held at that $V$. During an <action potential>, changing $V$ changes the rates and gates feed back onto $V$ through ionic <electric currents>; substituting the present voltage into the constant-rate solution does not solve that coupled system. Gating describes availability of a conducting pathway. Even an open <ion channel> carries zero net ionic <electric current> at its <reversal potential>.

The useful bridge is from abrupt random molecular events to a smooth population prediction. A channel requiring several <independent permissive gates in an ion channel> has an open <probability> equal to their product; this can make channel availability much smaller than each gate's availability. The <Hodgkin-Huxley model> uses $\bar g_{\mathrm{Na}}m^3h$ and $\bar g_{\mathrm K}n^4$ for <sodium> and <potassium> <electrical conductances>, respectively.

= Equal open probability with unequal ion-channel relaxation times
{parent=Two-state ion-channel gating}
{title2=$p_\infty=a/(a+b),\quad\tau=(a+b)^{-1}$}

Multiplying opening and closing rates by the same factor preserves equilibrium open <probability> but rescales the relaxation and dwell times. At equal opening and closing rates, $p_\infty=1/2$ and relaxation is twice as fast as the reciprocal mean individual-state dwell. A two-state constant-voltage model cannot alone produce activation followed by inactivation during a maintained step.

= Independent permissive gates in an ion channel
{parent=Two-state ion-channel gating}
{title2=$P_{\mathrm{open}}=p^q$}

A permissive gate allows conduction when it is open. Suppose an <ion channel> conducts only when all $q$ gates are open, where $q$ is a positive <integer>. If the gate-open <events> are mutually <independent events> and each has the same open <probability> $p$ at the time considered, then
$$
P_{\mathrm{open}}=\prod_{j=1}^q p=p^q.
$$
This follows from the multiplication rule for <independent events>. Identical transition rates alone do not guarantee independence: the initial gate states must also be independently prepared, and the model must not couple their transitions. Gates sharing a fluctuating <membrane potential> can be correlated; the simple product requires an appropriate independence assumption, for example independent gates at an externally fixed voltage.

At $p=0.5$, channels requiring $q=1,2,4$ permissive gates have open <probabilities> $0.5$, $0.25$ and $0.0625$. For four independent gates there are $2^4=16$ possible open/closed configurations, equally probable at $p=0.5$; only the all-open configuration conducts. Thus $0.5$ is the gate-open <probability>, while $0.0625$ is the whole-channel open <probability>. Neither is a fractional state of one observed channel. Among $160$ independently prepared identical four-gate channels, the <expected value> of the open count is $160/16=10$, although an actual count fluctuates.

Only the highlighted all-open configuration permits conduction. This is an illustrative model, not a molecular structure or an ionic <electric current> measurement.}

For unequal independent gate-open <probabilities> $p_j$, use $P_{\mathrm{open}}=\prod_j p_j$ rather than $p^q$. This is the distinction behind the <Hodgkin-Huxley model>'s $m^3h$: $m$ and $h$ are different activation and inactivation-availability variables, rather than one common $p$. Its $n^4$ uses four identical activation factors. Independence and the all-open requirement are model assumptions; correlated gates or alternative conducting configurations require a different calculation. The simple product is useful because a modest gate-open <probability> can produce sharply suppressed whole-channel availability without any additional gate interaction.
