= Solution
{parent=a}

At an <equilibrium point>, $x(1-x-y)=0$ and $2-2x-y=0$. The first equation gives $x=0$ or $y=1-x$. Substituting in the second gives, respectively,
$$
\boxed{(x,y)=(0,2)\quad\text{or}\quad(1,0).}
$$
These are all the <equilibrium points>. The <Jacobian matrix> of the <vector field> is
$$
J(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}1-2x-y&-x\\-2&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
At $(0,2)$,
$$
J(0,2)=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\-2&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The <eigenvalue> $-1$ has algebraic multiplicity two and <eigenspace> $\operatorname{span}\{(0,1)^T\}$. For the linearized coordinates $u=x$, $v=y-2$,
$$
u(t)=Ae^{-t},\qquad v(t)=(B-2At)e^{-t}.
$$
Both components tend to zero. The repeated <eigenvalue> with one independent <eigenvector> makes this a **stable improper <degenerate node>**, a <sink equilibrium>. The term $t e^{-t}$ is the signature of the nontrivial <Jordan block>. Since it is a <hyperbolic equilibrium point>, the <linearization stability theorem> gives local <asymptotic stability> of the nonlinear system. Approaching trajectories are tangent to the vertical <eigendirection>.

At $(1,0)$,
$$
J(1,0)=\begin{pmatrix}-1&-1\\-2&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\lambda_+=-1+\sqrt2,\quad v_+=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-\sqrt2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\lambda_-=-1-\sqrt2,\quad v_-=\begin{pmatrix}1\\\sqrt2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
One <eigenvalue> is positive and one negative, so this point is a **<saddle equilibrium>**. Its <stable manifold> is tangent to $y=\sqrt2(x-1)$, and its <unstable manifold> is tangent to $y=-\sqrt2(x-1)$.

For the <phase portrait>, the <nullclines> are
$$
\dot x=0:\quad x=0\text{ or }y=1-x,\qquad
\dot y=0:\quad y=2-2x.
$$
Above the second line the vertical flow is downward, and below it upward. For $x>0$, horizontal flow is to the right below $y=1-x$ and to the left above it; for $x<0$ the directions reverse. The line $x=0$ is invariant, with $\dot y=2-y$, so it flows toward $(0,2)$. Together with the saddle's two invariant directions, these signs determine the sketch.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <Bendixson-Dulac theorem> says that a continuously differentiable planar <vector field> has no nonconstant <periodic orbit> in a <simply connected> region if a continuously differentiable <Dulac function> makes its rescaled <divergence> have one sign and not vanish identically on any open subset of that region. Isolated zeros of the <divergence> are allowed in this form.

Take the <Dulac function> to be one. For the exact <vector field> $(P,Q)$,
$$
\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial Q}{\partial y}
=3x^2+7y^2+12x^2y^2.
$$
This is positive except at the origin. The plane is <simply connected>, so **there are no nonconstant periodic solutions**. Equivalently, a hypothetical closed trajectory would have zero outward flux of its tangent <vector field>, while <Green's theorem> would make that flux the strictly positive area <integral> of the <divergence>.

The origin is the only <equilibrium point>: $Q=0$ forces $y=0$, and then $P=x^3$ forces $x=0$. Its <Jacobian matrix> is zero, so the linearized equations are $\dot x=\dot y=0$. They give no stability classification.

Keeping cubic terms gives the homogeneous approximation
$$
\dot x=x(x^2+y^2),\qquad\dot y=2y^3.
$$
In a sector where $xy\ne0$, use $y$ as the independent variable and put $w=x^{-2}$. Dividing the two equations gives
$$
\frac{dx}{dy}=\frac{x(x^2+y^2)}{2y^3},\qquad
\frac{dw}{dy}+\frac wy=-\frac1{y^3}.
$$
The <integrating factor> $y$ gives $(yw)'=-y^{-2}$, hence $yw=1/y+C_0$. Rearranging and renaming the arbitrary constant yields the implicit trajectories
$$
\boxed{\frac1y-\frac y{x^2}=C,\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad x^2-y^2=Cx^2y.}
$$
This is the <first integral> of the cubic approximation, not an exact first integral of the higher-degree original system. The invariant axes, excluded by the divisions, must be added: on $y=0$, $\dot x=x^3$; on $x=0$, $\dot y=2y^3$. The diagonal trajectories $y=\pm x$ correspond to $C=0$. For fixed finite $C$, non-axis trajectories approaching the origin satisfy $y^2/x^2=1-Cy\to1$, so their limiting tangent lines are the diagonals. This is the <cubic planar flow with diagonal approach directions>.

Every nonzero trajectory moves outward locally. For $V=x^2+y^2$, the exact system gives
$$
\dot V=2x^4+2x^2y^2+4y^4+2x^4y^2+6x^2y^4\geq V^2>0.
$$
This implies departure from any fixed small circle for every nonzero starting point inside it: while a trajectory stayed inside forever, $\frac{d}{dt}(1/V)\leq-1$ would eventually force $1/V$ negative. Thus the origin is **unstable and repelling** for both the cubic approximation and the full system. The argument establishes nonlinear instability despite the zero linearization.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Work first on $0<x<1$. Divide by $x-1$ to put the equation in leading-coefficient-one form. Its first-derivative coefficient is $-x/(x-1)$, so the <integrating factor> for the <self-adjoint differential operator> is
$$
p(x)=\exp\left(\int\frac{-x}{x-1}\,dx\right)=\frac{e^{-x}}{1-x}.
$$
The resulting coefficients are
$$
\boxed{p(x)=\frac{e^{-x}}{1-x},\qquad q(x)=-\frac{e^{-x}}{(1-x)^2},\qquad
F(x)=-\frac{e^{-x}}{(1-x)^2}.}
$$
Indeed $p'(x)=x e^{-x}/(1-x)^2$, and multiplying the original equation by $-e^{-x}/(1-x)^2$ gives $(py')'+qy=F$. These transformed coefficients are singular at the right endpoint; the boundary value there is understood as the endpoint limit.

Substituting $y=x$ and $y=e^x$ in the homogeneous original equation gives zero in both cases. Their <Wronskian> is
$$
W(x,e^x)=xe^x-e^x=(x-1)e^x,
$$
which is nonzero for $0<x<1$. Thus they are <linearly independent> on the solution interval.

To construct a <Dirichlet Green function>, choose homogeneous solutions adapted to the two endpoints:
$$
u(x)=x,\qquad v(x)=e^x-ex,\qquad u(0)=0,\quad v(1)=0.
$$
Their <Wronskian> is again $(x-1)e^x$, and $pW=-1$. Therefore the Green function for $L=(p\,d/dx)'+q$ with zero endpoint values is
$$
\boxed{G(x,s)=-u(\min(x,s))v(\max(x,s))
=\begin{cases}-x(e^s-es),&x\leq s,\\-s(e^x-ex),&s\leq x.\end{cases}}
$$
For fixed $s\in(0,1)$ it is continuous in $x$, vanishes at both endpoints, and satisfies the unit-flux jump condition
$$
p(s)\bigl[G_x(s^+,s)-G_x(s^-,s)\bigr]=1.
$$
Consequently $L_xG(x,s)=\delta(x-s)$ in the sense of <distributions>. This is a <singular-endpoint Green function with a double-zero right solution>.

The endpoint data are nonhomogeneous, so use the homogeneous lifting $2x$ and write
$$
y(x)=2x+\int_0^1G(x,s)F(s)\,ds.
$$
Although $F(s)$ is singular at one, the integral exists for each interior $x$: the right solution satisfies $v(s)\sim e(1-s)^2/2$, cancelling the $(1-s)^{-2}$ forcing when $s>x$.

Splitting the integral at $s=x$ gives
$$
y(x)=2x+v(x)I_1(x)+xI_2(x),
$$
where elementary antiderivatives yield
$$
I_1(x)=\int_0^x\frac{s e^{-s}}{(1-s)^2}\,ds=\frac{e^{-x}}{1-x}-1,
$$
$$
I_2(x)=\int_x^1\frac{(e^s-es)e^{-s}}{(1-s)^2}\,ds
=\frac{e^{1-x}-1}{1-x}-1.
$$
For the second equality, an antiderivative is $(1-e^{1-s})/(1-s)$, whose endpoint limit at one is $-1$. Simplifying the Green representation gives
$$
\boxed{y(x)=1+(1+e)x-e^x.}
$$
It has the prescribed endpoint values. Direct substitution gives $(x-1)y''-xy'+y=1$, and at the degenerate endpoint it also obeys $-y'(1)+y(1)=1$. The only homogeneous solution satisfying both zero endpoint conditions is zero, so this solution is unique in the corresponding classical class.

Let $X=(x,y)$ be the integration variable and $P=(\xi,\eta)$ the interior source point. <Green's second identity>, obtained from <Green's theorem> by applying the <divergence theorem> to $u\nabla G-G\nabla u$, is
$$
\int_D(u\Delta G-G\Delta u)\,dA
=\int_{\partial D}\left(u\frac{\partial G}{\partial n}-G\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\right)ds.
$$
The outward normal is used throughout. To obtain the prescribed representation with its positive volume term, choose
$$
\boxed{\Delta_XG(X,P)=\delta(X-P),\qquad
G=0\text{ on }\partial D_1,\qquad
\partial_nG=0\text{ on }\partial D_2.}
$$
Indeed, substituting $\Delta u=f$ makes the left side $u(P)-\int_DGf$. On the Dirichlet portion, $u=g$ and $G=0$ leave only $g\partial_nG$. On the Neumann portion, both normal derivatives vanish. Rearranging gives exactly the desired boundary-plus-volume representation. The distributional identity can equivalently be justified by excluding a small circle around $P$ and using the logarithmic singularity there.

For the quarter-plane, the <fundamental solution of the Laplace equation> with this sign convention is $\Phi(X,P)=(2\pi)^{-1}\log|X-P|$, since $\Delta\Phi=\delta_P$. The <method of images> uses an equal-sign reflection across the Neumann boundary $x=0$ and an opposite-sign reflection across the Dirichlet boundary $y=0$. Hence
$$
G(X,P)=\frac1{2\pi}\bigl[\log|X-(\xi,\eta)|+\log|X-(-\xi,\eta)|
-\log|X-(\xi,-\eta)|-\log|X-(-\xi,-\eta)|\bigr].
$$
Equivalently,
$$
\boxed{G(x,y,\xi,\eta)=\frac1{4\pi}\log
\frac{\bigl((x-\xi)^2+(y-\eta)^2\bigr)\bigl((x+\xi)^2+(y-\eta)^2\bigr)}
{\bigl((x-\xi)^2+(y+\eta)^2\bigr)\bigl((x+\xi)^2+(y+\eta)^2\bigr)}.}
$$
Only $(\xi,\eta)$ lies inside the domain, so the interior <Laplacian> has exactly the required unit point source. At $y=0$ numerator and denominator agree, giving $G=0$. The formula is even in $x$, giving $\partial_xG=0$ at $x=0$, and therefore zero outward normal derivative there.

On the Dirichlet boundary $y=0$, the outward normal is $-\partial_y$, and differentiation gives the useful check
$$
\frac{\partial G}{\partial n}(x,0,\xi,\eta)
=\frac\eta\pi\left[\frac1{(x-\xi)^2+\eta^2}+\frac1{(x+\xi)^2+\eta^2}\right]>0.
$$
This is the <mixed Dirichlet-Neumann Green function in a quadrant>. The image solution tends to zero at spatial infinity; on the unbounded domain the representation uses data and solutions for which the <boundary term at infinity in Green's second identity> vanishes.

= Solution

Write the equation as $\epsilon x^3-(x-1)^2=0$. For $\epsilon\to0^+$, two <roots of a polynomial> approach the double root one. Their splitting follows a fractional-power <dominant balance>, rather than an ordinary expansion in integer powers.

Put $\delta=\sqrt\epsilon$ and $x=1+a\delta+b\delta^2+\cdots$. Substitution gives
$$
0=(1-a^2)\delta^2+(3a-2ab)\delta^3+O(\delta^4).
$$
Thus $a=\pm1$ and $b=3/2$. The two bounded roots have the <Puiseux series> expansions
$$
\boxed{x_\pm=1\pm\sqrt\epsilon+\frac32\epsilon+O(\epsilon^{3/2}).}
$$
The first two nonzero terms are $1\pm\sqrt\epsilon$; the displayed correction also resolves their common order-$\epsilon$ shift.

The third root escapes to infinity because the leading <polynomial degree> drops when $\epsilon=0$. Balance $\epsilon x^3$ against $x^2$, giving $x=O(\epsilon^{-1})$. Set $X=\epsilon x$; the equation becomes
$$
X^3-X^2+2\epsilon X-\epsilon^2=0.
$$
For $X=1+A\epsilon+B\epsilon^2+\cdots$, the first two coefficient equations are $A+2=0$ and $B+2A^2+2A-1=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{x_\infty=\epsilon^{-1}-2-3\epsilon+O(\epsilon^2).}
$$
Its first two nonzero terms are $\epsilon^{-1}-2$. These three distinct branches account for all roots of the cubic for sufficiently small positive $\epsilon$. The <Vieta formulas> provide a consistency check: their sum is $\epsilon^{-1}$, with the constant and order-$\epsilon$ terms cancelling.

If $\epsilon$ approaches zero through negative values, the bounded pair is complex: $x_\pm=1\pm i\sqrt{|\epsilon|}+\tfrac32\epsilon+O(|\epsilon|^{3/2})$. The large-root expansion is unchanged as a real asymptotic formula. The positive-parameter regime is used for the boundary-layer problem below.

Seek an order-one <outer expansion> as $\epsilon\to0^+$:
$$
y_{\rm out}(x)=y_0(x)+\epsilon y_1(x)+O(\epsilon^2).
$$
At leading order, $0=y_0-x^2$, so $y_0=x^2$. At order $\epsilon$,
$$
y_0''=y_1+2+y_0^2.
$$
Since $y_0''=2$, this gives $y_1=-x^4$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{y_{\rm out}(x)=x^2-\epsilon x^4+O(\epsilon^2).}
$$
This is an approximation away from any <boundary layer>; the small coefficient of the highest <derivative> means the reduced equation cannot generally impose both endpoint conditions.

At the left endpoint, both computed <outer expansion> terms vanish, so $y_{\rm out}(0)=0$ agrees with the boundary data. At the right endpoint,
$$
y_{\rm out}(1)=1-\epsilon+O(\epsilon^2),
$$
which differs by order one from the required zero value. Hence **the boundary layer is at $x=1$**. The left boundary requires no order-one layer for this outer branch.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Suppose the right-end <boundary layer> has width $\ell(\epsilon)$, and let $X=(1-x)/\ell$. An order-one change of $y$ over this width gives $\epsilon y''=O(\epsilon/\ell^2)$. Near $x=1$, the leading terms $y-x^2$ are order one. The <dominant balance> is therefore
$$
\frac\epsilon{\ell^2}\sim1.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\ell=\sqrt\epsilon,\qquad X=\frac{1-x}{\sqrt\epsilon}.}
$$
The <inner variable> satisfies $X\geq0$ inside the domain and resolves the <reaction boundary layer with square-root thickness>.

= Solution

Write $y(x)=Y(X)$ with $X=(1-x)/\sqrt\epsilon$. The transformed equation is
$$
Y''=Y-(1-\sqrt\epsilon X)^2+\epsilon(2+Y^2).
$$
Since the rescaled forcing contains $\sqrt\epsilon$, an <inner expansion> naturally uses powers of $\sqrt\epsilon$. Its leading term satisfies
$$
Y_0''=Y_0-1,\qquad Y_0(0)=0.
$$
The <asymptotic matching condition> with the leading <outer solution> is
$$
Y_0(X)\longrightarrow y_0(1)=1\qquad(X\to\infty).
$$
The general solution is $Y_0=1+Ae^X+Be^{-X}$. Matching eliminates $Ae^X$, and the boundary condition gives $B=-1$. Hence
$$
\boxed{Y_0(X)=1-e^{-X},\qquad
y_{\rm inner}(x)\sim1-\exp\left(-\frac{1-x}{\sqrt\epsilon}\right).}
$$
This tends to the boundary value zero at $x=1$ and to the matching value one on the interior side of the layer. The negative sign in the exponential follows from measuring the <inner variable> inward from the right endpoint.

For an optional uniform leading approximation, combine the leading inner and outer terms and subtract their common overlap value one. The <additive composite expansion> is
$$
\boxed{y_{\rm comp}(x)=x^2-\exp\left(-\frac{1-x}{\sqrt\epsilon}\right).}
$$
It satisfies the right boundary condition exactly; its left-end discrepancy is the exponentially small value $-e^{-1/\sqrt\epsilon}$. The requested leading <inner expansion> is $Y_0$ above.
