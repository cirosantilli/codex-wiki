== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

On an <interval> with $x>0$, use the <power-law ansatz> $y=x^r$. Substitution gives $r(r-1)+2r+1=r^2+r+1=0$, whose roots are $r=-1/2\pm i\sqrt3/2$. Taking real and imaginary parts of $x^r$ gives
$$
\boxed{y(x)=x^{-1/2}\left[C_1\cos\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\log x\right)+C_2\sin\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\log x\right)\right].}
$$
These two real solutions are <linearly independent>: their <Wronskian> is $(\sqrt3/2)x^{-2}\ne0$. They therefore span the solution space of the second-order <linear ordinary differential equation> on that <interval>.

For $x<0$, replace $x^{-1/2}$ by $|x|^{-1/2}$ and $\log x$ by $\log|x|$, with independent real constants on the negative <interval>. This follows equally by the change of variable $t=\log|x|$, which turns the <Cauchy-Euler equation> into $d^2y/dt^2+dy/dt+y=0$. The <coefficient> of $y''$ vanishes at zero, so the general formula is stated separately on either side. Every nonzero mode has an unbounded oscillatory envelope as $x\to0$; a continuous extension through zero requires the solution to vanish identically on both sides.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the usual <inner product> $\langle u,v\rangle=\int_0^1\overline u v\,dx$. Moving four <derivatives> by <integration by parts> leaves $L^*=D^4+1$ and gives the fourth-order boundary identity
$$
\langle u,Lv\rangle-\langle Lu,v\rangle
=\left[\overline u v'''-\overline {u'}v''+\overline {u''}v'-\overline {u'''}v\right]_0^1.
$$
Thus the differential expression is formally <self-adjoint>. If both <functions> satisfy the <clamped boundary conditions>, every displayed endpoint product vanishes, proving symmetry on the specified domain.

To check full <self-adjointness>, also identify the adjoint domain. Allow $v$ to run through the clamped domain. At either endpoint, $v''$ and $v'''$ can be chosen independently, for instance using a <polynomial> near that endpoint multiplied by a <smooth cutoff function>. The remaining boundary form is $[\overline u v'''-\overline {u'}v'']_0^1$. It vanishes for every such $v$ precisely when $u=u'=0$ at both endpoints. These are exactly the original <boundary conditions>. On the regular finite <interval>, the adjoint domain consequently equals $\{u\in H^4(0,1):u(0)=u'(0)=u(1)=u'(1)=0\}$, rather than a larger domain. Here $H^4$ is the <Sobolev space> of <functions> whose <weak derivatives> through order four are <square-integrable>.

**The operator together with these <boundary conditions> is fully <self-adjoint>.** As a further consistency check, <integration by parts> gives $\langle y,Ly\rangle=\int_0^1(|y''|^2+|y|^2)\,dx>0$ for every nonzero admissible $y$. The reusable calculation is the <Lagrange identity for a fourth-order differential operator>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Start with a <fundamental solution> of the second <derivative> operator:
$$
\widetilde g(x,\xi)=(x-\xi)H(x-\xi),\qquad \partial_x\widetilde g=H(x-\xi),\qquad \partial_x^2\widetilde g=\delta(x-\xi).
$$
Here $H$ is the <Heaviside step function> and the last equality holds in the sense of <distributions>. The factor $x-\xi$ removes a jump in the <function> itself, while its first <derivative> has a unit jump. Adding any <homogeneous solution> preserves the <Dirac delta> source.

Choose $y_1(x)=x-2$ and $y_2(x)=x-3$. They are independent <homogeneous solutions>, with $B_1y_1=0$, $B_2y_2=0$, $B_1y_2=-1$, and $B_2y_1=1$. Since $1<\xi<2$, the <fundamental solution> has $B_1\widetilde g=1$ and $B_2\widetilde g=2-\xi$. Imposing the two <boundary conditions> on the proposed combination therefore gives $-A_2+1=0$ and $A_1+2-\xi=0$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{A_1(\xi)=\xi-2,\qquad A_2(\xi)=1,\qquad
 g(x,\xi)=(\xi-2)(x-2)+(x-3)+(x-\xi)H(x-\xi).}
$$
Equivalently,
$$
g(x,\xi)=\begin{cases}(\xi-1)x+1-2\xi,&1\leq x\leq\xi,\\ \xi x+1-3\xi,&\xi\leq x\leq2.\end{cases}
$$
At $x=\xi$ both expressions equal $\xi^2-3\xi+1$ and the right slope minus the left slope is $\xi-(\xi-1)=1$. At the endpoints, $g(1,\xi)=-\xi$ and $g_x(2,\xi)=\xi$, while $g_x(1,\xi)=\xi-1$ and $g(2,\xi)=1-\xi$. These explicitly verify both <coupled boundary conditions>. The homogeneous equation has only affine solutions; its two <boundary conditions> give $2a+b=3a+b=0$, hence $a=b=0$, proving <uniqueness> of the <Green function>.

The reciprocity defect is $g(x,\xi)-g(\xi,x)=2(x-\xi)$. It records how the <coupled boundary conditions> affect <self-adjointness>, even though the differential expression $D^2$ is formally <self-adjoint>. The construction is an instance of the <boundary-matrix construction of an ordinary differential Green function>.

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Divide by $2x$ away from zero to obtain $y''+P(x)y'+Q(x)y=0$, with $P(x)=x/2-1/(2x)$ and $Q(x)=\lambda/2$. At zero, the quantities $xP(x)=(x^2-1)/2$ and $x^2Q(x)=\lambda x^2/2$ are analytic. The <pole> in $P$ makes zero singular, so **$x=0$ is a <regular singular point>**. Its <indicial equation> is
$$
r(r-1)+\left(-\frac12\right)r+0=0,
\qquad\boxed{r_1=0,\quad r_2=\frac32.}
$$
At $x=1$, both normalized <coefficients> are analytic <functions>, so **$x=1$ is an <ordinary point>**. These are the standard normalized-<coefficient> and indicial tests.<NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions>[§2.7(i), equations 2.7.1–2.7.3]{ref}

For infinity, set $t=1/x$ and $Y(t)=y(1/t)$. The <chain rule> gives $y'=-t^2Y'$ and $y''=t^4Y''+2t^3Y'$, so the transformed normalized equation is
$$
Y''+\left(\frac5{2t}-\frac1{2t^3}\right)Y'+\frac\lambda{2t^4}Y=0.
$$
The product of the first-<derivative> <coefficient> with $t$ still has a second-order <pole> at zero. Hence the criterion for a <regular singular point> fails and **$x=\infty$ is an <irregular singular point>**, including when $\lambda=0$. The primes on $Y$ here refer to <differentiation> with respect to $t$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Seek a <Frobenius series> $y=x^r\sum_{n\geq0}a_nx^n$, with $a_0\ne0$, on the positive side of zero. The lowest <coefficient> gives the <indicial equation> from part (a); the next gives $(r+1)(2r-1)a_1=0$. For either permitted <indicial exponent>, $a_1=0$. Matching the subsequent powers gives
$$
a_{n+2}=-\frac{n+r+\lambda}{(n+r+2)(2n+2r+1)}a_n,\qquad n\geq0.
$$
The two <indicial exponents> differ by a noninteger, so there are two independent Frobenius solutions without a logarithmic companion. Normalizing each leading <coefficient> to one, their forms are
$$
f_0(x)=1-\frac\lambda2x^2+O(x^4),\qquad
f_{3/2}(x)=x^{3/2}\left[1-\frac{2\lambda+3}{28}x^2+O(x^4)\right].
$$
Thus the general local solution is $C_0f_0+C_1f_{3/2}$. The endpoint condition at zero forces $C_0=0$. For a nontrivial solution, $C_1\ne0$, so $y(x)\sim C_1x^{3/2}$ and **$\alpha=3/2$**, with the arbitrary nonzero amplitude understood.

The condition at one selects values of $\lambda$ for which this branch also vanishes there; it does not change the selected local exponent. The zero solution, available for every $\lambda$, has no nonzero leading <coefficient> and is excluded when asking for a leading exponent. This is a direct application of the <Frobenius method> and the noninteger-separated-root case.<NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions>[§2.7(i), equations 2.7.3–2.7.4]{ref}

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

First classify nonzero <polynomial> solutions of the <differential equation> itself. If $y$ has degree $d$ and leading <coefficient> $a_d\ne0$, the <coefficient> of $x^{d+1}$ is $(d+\lambda)a_d$. Hence $\lambda=-d$ is necessary.

An analytic solution at zero belongs to the exponent-zero Frobenius branch. Its odd-power <coefficients> vanish, because $a_1=0$ and the <recurrence relation> preserves <parity>. For the even-power <coefficients>,
$$
a_{2j+2}=-\frac{2j+\lambda}{(2j+2)(4j+1)}a_{2j}.
$$
All denominators are nonzero for $j\geq0$. With $\lambda=-2m$, the <coefficients> through $a_{2m}$ are nonzero when $a_0\ne0$, the next <coefficient> vanishes, and every subsequent <coefficient> vanishes. This proves sufficiency as well as the restriction to even <polynomial> degree:
$$
\boxed{\lambda=-2m,\qquad m=0,1,2,\ldots,\qquad \deg y=2m=-\lambda.}
$$
For example, setting $a_0=1$ gives $1$ when $\lambda=0$, $1+x^2$ when $\lambda=-2$, and $1+2x^2+x^4/5$ when $\lambda=-4$. A <polynomial> cannot instead begin with a <positive integer> power: its lowest power must have an <indicial exponent>, and the only <integer> <indicial exponent> is zero. This also proves that no odd-degree family was missed. The finite-series mechanism is a <terminating Frobenius series>.

**If the additional endpoint conditions from part (b) are imposed on this part too, there is no nonzero <polynomial> solution:** every nonzero <polynomial> just classified has $y(0)=a_0\ne0$. The displayed family and degree answer therefore concern the <differential equation> without those extra endpoint conditions.

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

By the definition of <little o notation>, the required quotient is
$$
\frac{\epsilon^2\log\epsilon}{\epsilon}=\epsilon\log\epsilon.
$$
As $\epsilon\to0^+$, write this as $\log\epsilon/(1/\epsilon)$. <L'Hôpital's rule> applies to this infinite-over-infinite ratio and gives
$$
\lim_{\epsilon\to0^+}\frac{\log\epsilon}{1/\epsilon}
=\lim_{\epsilon\to0^+}\frac{1/\epsilon}{-1/\epsilon^2}
=\lim_{\epsilon\to0^+}(-\epsilon)=0.
$$
Therefore **$\epsilon^2\log\epsilon=o(\epsilon)$**. The <logarithm> is negative on this approach, which is consistent with a quotient tending to zero from below; <little o notation> concerns the limiting ratio, not its sign.<NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions/2.1 Definitions and Elementary Properties>[§2.1(i), equation 2.1.2]{ref}

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Take $\epsilon\to0^+$. A root on the smaller scale has $x=\epsilon u$, giving $u+2+\epsilon u^3=0$. Put $u=u_0+\epsilon u_1+O(\epsilon^2)$. Successive <coefficients> give $u_0=-2$ and $u_1+u_0^3=0$, hence $u_1=8$. The real root is therefore
$$
\boxed{x_0=-2\epsilon+8\epsilon^2+O(\epsilon^3).}
$$
The other <dominant balance> is between the cubic and linear terms, requiring $x=\epsilon^{1/2}z$. Set $t=\epsilon^{1/2}$; the equation becomes $z^3+z+2t=0$. At $t=0$ the two nonzero roots are $z_0=\pm i$. With $z=z_0+t z_1+O(t^2)$, the first correction satisfies $(3z_0^2+1)z_1+2=0$, so $-2z_1+2=0$ and $z_1=1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{x_+=i\epsilon^{1/2}+\epsilon+O(\epsilon^{3/2}),\qquad
x_-=-i\epsilon^{1/2}+\epsilon+O(\epsilon^{3/2}).}
$$
The limiting roots in both rescalings are simple, so the <implicit function theorem> justifies these regular expansions in their respective small parameters. The fractional powers in the original variable are a <fractional-power root expansion>. The real <derivative> $3x^2+\epsilon$ is strictly positive; the cubic therefore has exactly one real root. Its other roots are the displayed <complex conjugate> pair, accounting for all three roots. Their leading real corrections sum to $-2\epsilon+\epsilon+\epsilon=0$, as required by the absence of a quadratic <coefficient>.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For a positive <eigenvalue> write $\mu=\sqrt\lambda>0$. The <general solution> of the limiting <constant-coefficient differential equation> is $y=A\cos(\mu x)+B\sin(\mu x)$. The endpoint at zero gives $A=0$. A nonzero <eigenfunction> then requires $B\ne0$, and the endpoint at $\pi$ gives $\sin(\mu\pi)=0$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\lambda_k=k^2,\qquad y_k(x)=C_k\sin(kx),\qquad k=1,2,\ldots,\quad C_k\ne0.}
$$
For real <eigenfunctions> the constants may be any nonzero real amplitudes. Requiring unit <L2 norm> would set $|C_k|=\sqrt{2/\pi}$, but that normalization is not required by the question. The lowest <eigenvalue> is $\lambda_1=1$ and its <eigenspace> is spanned by $\sin x$. For completeness, zero yields only an affine solution annihilated by both endpoints, and negative <eigenvalues> yield <hyperbolic functions> whose two zero endpoint values also force the zero solution.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Choose the real leading <eigenfunction> $y_1=C\sin x$, keeping its amplitude $C\ne0$ fixed. At first order in the <regular perturbation> expansion, the equation becomes
$$
y_{11}''+y_{11}=-\lambda_{11}y_1-y_1^2,\qquad y_{11}(0)=y_{11}(\pi)=0.
$$
The operator $L=D^2+1$ with these homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions> is <self-adjoint>, as allowed in the question, and its <null space> is spanned by $y_1$. The <Fredholm solvability condition> requires the forcing to be <orthogonal> to that <null space>:
$$
0=\int_0^\pi y_1\bigl(-\lambda_{11}y_1-y_1^2\bigr)\,dx.
$$
The same condition follows directly by integrating $y_1Ly_{11}$ by parts: the endpoint term $[y_1y_{11}'-y_1'y_{11}]_0^\pi$ vanishes, and $Ly_1=0$. Since a nonzero real $y_1$ has a positive squared <integral>,
$$
\boxed{\lambda_{11}=-\frac{\displaystyle\int_0^\pi y_1(x)^3\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_0^\pi y_1(x)^2\,dx}.}
$$
This is the requested expression and is an instance of the <leading nonlinear eigenvalue shift in a Sturm-Liouville problem>. In the amplitude convention above, $\int_0^\pi\sin^2x\,dx=\pi/2$ and $\int_0^\pi\sin^3x\,dx=4/3$, so $\lambda_{11}=-8C/(3\pi)$. For the particular choice $C=1$, it is $-8/(3\pi)$.

The <amplitude dependence of a nonlinear eigenvalue correction> follows here from the quadratic term: rescaling the <eigenfunction> changes the <eigenvalue> shift. There is no single numerical correction independent of the chosen leading amplitude. Once the <solvability condition> is met, $y_{11}$ is determined up to addition of a multiple of $y_1$; a normalization such as $\int_0^\pi y_1y_{11}\,dx=0$ can fix that remaining freedom.
