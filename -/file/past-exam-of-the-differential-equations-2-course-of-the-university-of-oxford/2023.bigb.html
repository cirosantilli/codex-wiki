The <characteristic equation> of the <linear ordinary differential equation> is $r^2+2r+2=0$, with roots $-1\pm i$. A real pair of <homogeneous solutions> is therefore
$$
\boxed{y_1(x)=e^{-x}\cos x,\qquad y_2(x)=e^{-x}\sin x}.
$$
Their <Wronskian>, with the convention $W=y_1y_2'-y_1'y_2$, is
$$
W(x)=e^{-2x}\{\cos x(\cos x-\sin x)+(\sin x+\cos x)\sin x\}
=\boxed{e^{-2x}\ne0}.
$$
Thus the two functions are <linearly independent> throughout the interval and span its <homogeneous solutions>.

Use the unweighted <inner product> $\langle w,y\rangle=\int_0^a\overline w y\,dx$. Two applications of <integration by parts> give
$$
\int_0^a\overline w(y''+2y'+2y)\,dx
=\int_0^a(\overline w''-2\overline w'+2\overline w)y\,dx
+[\overline w y'-\overline w'y+2\overline w y]_0^a.
$$
The given <boundary conditions> set $y=0$ at both ends, but leave $y'(0)$ and $y'(a)$ independently variable. The remaining boundary term vanishes for every admissible $y$ precisely when $w(0)=w(a)=0$. Consequently the <formal adjoint> and the boundary conditions specifying the <adjoint operator> are
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L^*w=w''-2w'+2w,\qquad w(0)=w(a)=0}.
$$

Set $a=\pi/2$. To construct the <Green's function> from a <Dirac delta function>, require $\mathcal L_xg(x,\xi)=\delta(x-\xi)$ and zero endpoint values. Away from $x=\xi$, the <homogeneous solutions> satisfying the left and right <boundary conditions> are respectively
$$
u(x)=e^{-x}\sin x,\qquad v(x)=e^{-x}\cos x.
$$
Write $g=A(\xi)u(x)$ for $x<\xi$ and $g=B(\xi)v(x)$ for $x>\xi$. A discontinuity in $g$ would generate a derivative of the <Dirac delta function>, which is absent from the desired equation. Thus $g$ is continuous. Integrating that equation through $\xi$ then gives the <jump condition>
$$
g_x(\xi^+,\xi)-g_x(\xi^-,\xi)=1.
$$
The resulting two equations are $Au(\xi)=Bv(\xi)$ and $Bv'(\xi)-Au'(\xi)=1$. Since the <Wronskian> $uv'-u'v=-e^{-2\xi}$, they give
$$
\boxed{g(x,\xi)=-e^{\xi-x}
\begin{cases}
\sin x\cos\xi,&0\leq x\leq\xi,\\
\sin\xi\cos x,&\xi\leq x\leq\pi/2.
\end{cases}}
$$
Both endpoint values vanish. At $x=\xi$, subtracting the two first <derivatives> gives $\sin^2\xi+\cos^2\xi=1$, verifying the sign and normalization of the <Dirac delta function>.

The <homogeneous solution> $h(x)=e^{-x}\cos x$ already has the prescribed endpoint values. Thus $z=y-h$ has zero <boundary conditions> and satisfies $\mathcal Lz=f$. The <Green's function> gives
$$
\boxed{y(x)=e^{-x}\cos x+\int_0^{\pi/2}g(x,\xi)f(\xi)\,d\xi}.
$$
Equivalently, splitting the <integral> at $\xi=x$ gives the explicit formula
$$
y(x)=e^{-x}\left[
\cos x\left(1-\int_0^xe^\xi\sin\xi\,f(\xi)\,d\xi\right)
-\sin x\int_x^{\pi/2}e^\xi\cos\xi\,f(\xi)\,d\xi
\right].
$$
Any difference between two solutions would be $e^{-x}(C\cos x+D\sin x)$ with zero endpoint values, forcing $C=D=0$. The displayed solution is therefore unique.

At $a=\pi$, the nonzero <homogeneous solution> $e^{-x}\sin x$ satisfies both zero <boundary conditions>. Thus the <boundary value problem> has a nontrivial <kernel> and cannot have an inverse for arbitrary forcing.

More directly, the <adjoint operator> has the zero-endpoint <homogeneous solution> $w(x)=e^x\sin x$. If an ordinary <Green's function> existed, the <integration by parts> identity would imply
$$
0=\int_0^\pi w(x)\mathcal L_xg(x,\xi)\,dx
=\int_0^\pi w(x)\delta(x-\xi)\,dx=w(\xi).
$$
But $w(\xi)\ne0$ for $0<\xi<\pi$. This contradiction rules out the <Green's function> for the unmodified operator. It is the <Fredholm alternative> obstruction.

= Solution

The nonzero endpoint data contribute to the <Fredholm alternative> condition. With $w=e^x\sin x$, the same <integration by parts> identity gives
$$
\int_0^\pi w\mathcal Ly\,dx
=[wy'-w'y+2wy]_0^\pi
=-w'(\pi)y(\pi)+w'(0)y(0)=1.
$$
For the specified forcing the left side is $A\int_0^\pi\sin^2x\,dx=A\pi/2$. Hence
$$
\boxed{A=\frac2\pi}.
$$
To verify sufficiency, set $y=e^{-x}u$. Direct <differentiation> gives $\mathcal Ly=e^{-x}(u''+u)$. At this value of $A$, a <particular solution> of $u''+u=(2/\pi)\sin x$ is $-(x/\pi)\cos x$. The complete family obeying the two <boundary conditions> is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=e^{-x}\left[\left(1-\frac{x}{\pi}\right)\cos x+C\sin x\right],\qquad C\in\mathbb R}.
$$
The arbitrary constant records the one-dimensional <kernel>; existence does not imply uniqueness here.

Put $N=n(n+1)$. After division by $1-x^2$, the <Legendre differential equation> has coefficients
$$
p(x)=\frac{-2x}{1-x^2},\qquad q(x)=\frac{N}{1-x^2}.
$$
Both are <analytic functions> at zero, so $\boxed{x=0\text{ is an ordinary point}}$. At either $x_0=\pm1$, $p$ has a genuine <simple pole>; hence these are singular points even when $n=0$ makes $q$ vanish. However,
$$
\lim_{x\to x_0}(x-x_0)p(x)=1,\qquad
\lim_{x\to x_0}(x-x_0)^2q(x)=0,
$$
and both products extend analytically across $x_0$. By the <regular singular point criterion for a second-order equation>,
$$
\boxed{x=-1\text{ and }x=1\text{ are regular singular points}}.
$$
None of the three specified points is an <irregular singular point>.

Use $t=x+1$. The <Legendre differential equation> becomes
$$
t(2-t)y_{tt}+2(1-t)y_t+Ny=0.
$$
Substituting a <Frobenius series> $y=t^r\sum_{k\geq0}a_kt^k$, $a_0\ne0$, gives the <indicial equation> $2r^2=0$: the <indicial root> zero is repeated. For $r=0$, the coefficient of $t^k$ yields
$$
2(k+1)^2a_{k+1}+[n(n+1)-k(k+1)]a_k=0,
$$
or
$$
\boxed{a_{k+1}=\frac{(k-n)(k+n+1)}{2(k+1)^2}a_k}.
$$
For $0\leq k<n$ the multiplying factor is nonzero. At $k=n$ it vanishes, so $a_{n+1}$ and all later coefficients are zero. Thus one solution is a <polynomial> of exact <polynomial degree> $n$, including a nonzero constant when $n=0$. Denote a chosen normalization by $P_n$.

The <linear recurrence relation> determines every analytic coefficient from $a_0$, so there cannot be a second independent analytic solution at $t=0$. To see the required logarithm explicitly, <reduction of order> gives the second solution locally as
$$
P_n(x)\int^x\frac{ds}{(1-s^2)P_n(s)^2}.
$$
Near $t=0$, the integrand is $1/(2a_0^2t)$ plus an <analytic function>. After rescaling the second solution, its form is therefore
$$
\boxed{y_2(x)=P_n(x)\log(x+1)+\sum_{k=0}^\infty b_k(x+1)^k}.
$$
The analytic remainder converges locally; its constant term may be set to zero by adding a multiple of $P_n$. The nonzero logarithmic coefficient makes $y_2$ <linearly independent> of $P_n$. This <Frobenius expansion of the Legendre equation at an endpoint> gives a <logarithmic Frobenius solution>; the same endpoint calculation at $x=1$ explains why the independent <Legendre function of the second kind> is excluded when boundedness at both ends is required.

Write the operator in divergence form:
$$
\mathcal Ly=\big((1-x^2)y'\big)'.
$$
The <Legendre differential equation> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal LP_n=-n(n+1)P_n,\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots}.
$$
These are the <eigenvalues> of $\mathcal L$. With the common positive-sign convention for a <Sturm-Liouville problem>, use $-\mathcal L$ instead:
$$
-\big(pP_n'\big)'=\lambda_n wP_n,\qquad
\boxed{p=1-x^2,\quad q=0,\quad w=1,\quad\lambda_n=n(n+1)}.
$$
This is a singular <Sturm-Liouville problem>, because $p$ vanishes at the endpoints. Bounded <eigenfunctions> select the nonlogarithmic branches and satisfy $pP_n'\to0$ there. The <Lagrange identity for a Sturm-Liouville operator> gives
$$
[m(m+1)-n(n+1)]\int_{-1}^1P_mP_n\,dx
=[(1-x^2)(P_mP_n'-P_nP_m')]_{-1}^1=0.
$$
The <eigenvalues> are distinct, proving the <orthogonality> condition
$$
\boxed{\int_{-1}^1P_m(x)P_n(x)\,dx=0\quad(m\ne n)}.
$$
The weight is one. No choice of normalization of the <Legendre polynomials> is needed for this conclusion.

= Solution

First take the usual endpoint-regular interpretation $f\in C([-1,1])$. Integrating the divergence form shows that $F=(1-x^2)y'$ has finite endpoint limits. A nonzero limit would make $y'$ proportional to $1/(1\mp x)$ near that endpoint, forcing a logarithmically unbounded $y$. Thus boundedness requires $F(-1)=F(1)=0$, and consequently
$$
\boxed{\int_{-1}^1f(x)\,dx=0}.
$$
This is <orthogonality> to the constant <eigenfunction> $P_0$, hence the <Fredholm alternative> condition. It is also sufficient under the stated endpoint regularity: put
$$
F(x)=\int_{-1}^xf(t)\,dt,\qquad
y(x)=C+\int_0^x\frac{F(s)}{1-s^2}\,ds.
$$
If $M=\max|f|$, the zero-mean condition gives $|F(s)|\leq M\min(1+s,1-s)$. Hence the last integrand is bounded, so $y$ extends continuously to both endpoints. Any two bounded solutions differ by a constant: their difference solves $((1-x^2)z')'=0$, whose other solution is $\tfrac12\log((1+x)/(1-x))$.

For the requested <eigenfunction expansion>, set
$$
N_k=\int_{-1}^1P_k(t)^2\,dt,\qquad
f_k=\frac{\int_{-1}^1f(t)P_k(t)\,dt}{N_k}.
$$
The <Legendre polynomials> span all <polynomials>, so their completeness follows from the <Weierstrass approximation theorem>. Projecting $\mathcal Ly=f$ onto each <eigenfunction> gives $-k(k+1)y_k=f_k$ for $k\geq1$. The zero mode is free, while $f_0=0$. Thus the full family is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=C-\sum_{k=1}^\infty
\frac{P_k(x)}{k(k+1)}
\frac{\int_{-1}^1f(t)P_k(t)\,dt}{\int_{-1}^1P_k(t)^2\,dt},\qquad C\in\mathbb R}.
$$
The formula is unchanged by rescaling any $P_k$. For the usual normalization $P_k(1)=1$, the facts $|P_k(x)|\leq1$ on $[-1,1]$ and $N_k=2/(2k+1)$, together with the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>, bound each summand by a constant times $k^{-3/2}$. The series therefore converges absolutely and uniformly. Its identification with the preceding integral solution avoids unjustified termwise second <differentiation>.

There is a source ambiguity: continuity only on $(-1,1)$ does not supply the endpoint control used above. Even for integrable forcing, zero mean alone is insufficient. For example, let
$$
H(x)=\log\frac{e}{1-x^2},\qquad
F(x)=\frac1{H(x)},\qquad
f(x)=F'(x)=\frac{-2x}{(1-x^2)H(x)^2}.
$$
This $f$ is continuous in the interior, absolutely integrable, and has zero mean because $F(\pm1)=0$. Yet every candidate with zero endpoint values of $(1-x^2)y'$ has
$$
y'(x)=\frac1{(1-x^2)H(x)},
$$
whose <integral> diverges logarithmically in $H$ at each endpoint. Thus no bounded solution exists. For a merely interior-continuous $f$, the exact criterion is that some constant $B$ makes
$$
\int_0^x\frac{B+\int_0^sf(t)\,dt}{1-s^2}\,ds
$$
bounded as $x$ approaches both endpoints. The <bounded solvability for the Legendre operator> criterion above covers the intended regular forcing and makes this endpoint limitation explicit.

When $n=0$, the forcing has nonzero integral $\int_{-1}^11\,dx=2$, violating the <Fredholm alternative> condition. There is no bounded solution. Directly, integration gives $(1-x^2)y'=x+B$; cancellation of its logarithmic divergence at $x=-1$ requires $B=1$, while cancellation at $x=1$ requires $B=-1$.

For $n\geq1$, the constant $1/[n(n+1)]$ is a <particular solution>. Subtract it from any solution. The remainder satisfies the <Legendre differential equation>, whose <general solution> is a combination of $P_n$ and the logarithmically unbounded <Legendre function of the second kind>. Boundedness removes the latter coefficient, giving
$$
\boxed{\begin{cases}
\text{no bounded solution},&n=0,\\
y(x)=\dfrac1{n(n+1)}+CP_n(x),\quad C\in\mathbb R,&n\geq1.
\end{cases}}
$$
The constant forcing is <orthogonal> to $P_n$ for $n\geq1$, consistently with <orthogonality of Legendre polynomials> and the <Fredholm alternative>.

Consider real roots. Every zero at $\epsilon=0$ is $k\pi$, $k\in\mathbb Z$, and its <derivative> $\cos(k\pi)=(-1)^k$ is nonzero. For a <regular perturbation>, write $x=k\pi+\delta$. The <Taylor series>
$$
\sin(k\pi+\delta)=(-1)^k\left(\delta-\frac{\delta^3}{6}+O(\delta^5)\right)
$$
gives $\delta=(-1)^k\epsilon+O(\epsilon^3)$. Thus, through the requested order,
$$
\boxed{x_k=k\pi+(-1)^k\epsilon+O(\epsilon^3),\qquad k\in\mathbb Z}.
$$
There is no quadratic term. The exact parametrization $x_k=k\pi+(-1)^k\arcsin\epsilon$ also verifies completeness and the remainder order for $|\epsilon|<1$.

Take $\epsilon\to0^+$. There are two real branches, on different scales. For a root tending to zero, the <Taylor series> gives
$$
-\frac{x^3}{6}+O(x^5)=\epsilon(1-x^2).
$$
The <dominant balance> is $x^3\sim-6\epsilon$, so a <fractional-power root expansion> yields
$$
\boxed{x_-\sim-(6\epsilon)^{1/3}}.
$$
More precisely $x_-=-(6\epsilon)^{1/3}+O(\epsilon)$. An ordinary <power series> in integer powers of $\epsilon$ would miss this scale.

There is also a root escaping to positive infinity. Boundedness of the <sine> term makes the leading <dominant balance> $x-\epsilon x^2=0$. The nonzero branch is
$$
\boxed{x_+\sim\epsilon^{-1}}.
$$
Indeed the equation rearranges to $\epsilon x_+^2-x_+=\epsilon-\sin x_+$, which yields $x_+=\epsilon^{-1}+O(1)$ on this branch. The bounded oscillatory correction need not have a constant limiting value.

To check that these are all the real solutions, solve for the parameter:
$$
\epsilon=E(x):=\frac{x-\sin x}{x^2-1}.
$$
Positive values occur only on $(-1,0)$ and $(1,\infty)$; $x=\pm1$ are not roots. A useful exact <derivative> is
$$
E'(x)=-\frac{2\{x\cos(x/2)-\sin(x/2)\}^2}{(x^2-1)^2}\leq0.
$$
It vanishes only at isolated points, so $E$ is strictly decreasing on each of these intervals. Its endpoint limits are $+\infty$ and zero on each interval. This <monotone parametrization of sine perturbation roots> gives exactly one root in each interval for every $\epsilon>0$, proving that both asymptotic branches, and no others, have been included. If negative small parameters are allowed, the transformation $(x,\epsilon)\mapsto(-x,-\epsilon)$ reflects these two branches and gives all real solutions for that sign as well.

For the positive coefficient of $y''$, setting $\epsilon=0$ gives the reduced equation $y_0'+xy_0=0$, with <general solution> $y_0=Ce^{-x^2/2}$. A fast decaying <homogeneous solution> of $\epsilon y''+y'=0$ is $e^{-x/\epsilon}$, placing the <boundary layer> at the left endpoint. Thus use the right <boundary condition> for the <outer solution>: $y_0(1)=0$ implies
$$
\boxed{y_{\mathrm{out},0}(x)=0}.
$$
The <dominant balance> $\epsilon/\delta^2\sim1/\delta$ gives width $\delta=\epsilon$. With the <inner variable> $X=x/\epsilon$ and $Y(X)=y(\epsilon X)$, the exact rescaled equation is
$$
Y_{XX}+Y_X+\epsilon^2XY=0.
$$
At leading order, $Y_{0,XX}+Y_{0,X}=0$, so $Y_0=A+Be^{-X}$. The <asymptotic matching condition> $Y_0(\infty)=y_{\mathrm{out},0}(0)=0$ sets $A=0$, and $Y_0(0)=1$ sets $B=1$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{Y_0(X)=e^{-X},\qquad y_{\mathrm{comp},0}(x)=e^{-x/\epsilon}}.
$$
The <additive composite expansion> has zero overlap to subtract. Its graph starts at one, decreases steeply over $x=O(\epsilon)$, and then stays close to zero; its initial slope is $-1/\epsilon$. The right endpoint discrepancy $e^{-1/\epsilon}$ is exponentially small. The left panel of the figure below sketches this profile. A right-end <inner expansion> would instead contain a growing $e^X$ term and could not repair the left endpoint mismatch.

With the negative coefficient of $y''$, the reduced equation is unchanged, but the fast mode now decays away from the right endpoint. Apply the left <boundary condition> to the <outer solution>, giving
$$
\boxed{y_{\mathrm{out},0}(x)=e^{-x^2/2}}.
$$
The <boundary layer> still has width $O(\epsilon)$. Put $X=(1-x)/\epsilon$ and $Y(X)=y(1-\epsilon X)$. The rescaled equation is
$$
-Y_{XX}-Y_X+\epsilon(1-\epsilon X)Y=0.
$$
Its leading <inner expansion> has $Y_0=A+Be^{-X}$. The <asymptotic matching condition> is $Y_0(\infty)=e^{-1/2}$, while $Y_0(0)=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{Y_0(X)=e^{-1/2}(1-e^{-X})}.
$$
Subtracting the overlap $e^{-1/2}$ in the <additive composite expansion> gives
$$
\boxed{y_{\mathrm{comp},0}(x)=e^{-x^2/2}-e^{-1/2}e^{-(1-x)/\epsilon}}.
$$
Its graph starts exponentially close to one, follows the gently decreasing <outer solution>, and drops from near $e^{-1/2}$ to zero in a right <boundary layer> of width $O(\epsilon)$. The right endpoint value is exactly zero; the left endpoint error is $-e^{-1/2-1/\epsilon}$. Its right endpoint slope is $-e^{-1/2}(1+1/\epsilon)$, showing the rapid final descent.
