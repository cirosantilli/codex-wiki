= Solution
{parent=i}
{c}

Take $l>0$ and use the <inner product> $\langle u,v\rangle=\int_0^l\overline u v\,dx$ on the <Hilbert space> $L^2(0,l)$. The <differential operator> has domain
$$
D(\mathcal L)=\{u\in H^2(0,l):u(0)=u(l)=0\},
$$
where $H^2$ is a <Sobolev space>. Twice applying <integration by parts> gives the <Lagrange identity for a Sturm-Liouville operator>
$$
\langle u,\mathcal Lv\rangle-\langle\mathcal Lu,v\rangle
=[\overline u v'-\overline{u'}v]_0^l.
$$
The <Dirichlet boundary conditions> make this expression zero for $u,v\in D(\mathcal L)$, and the <formal adjoint> is $\mathcal L^*=d^2/dx^2+1$. To obtain the domain of the <adjoint operator>, initially impose the <boundary conditions> only on $u$. The remaining expression is $-[\overline{u'}v]_0^l$. Since $u'(0)$ and $u'(l)$ vary independently, it vanishes for all such $u$ exactly when $v(0)=v(l)=0$. The adjoint domain is therefore the original domain: this is full <self-adjointness>, including the <boundary conditions>.

Consequently the <eigenvalues> are real, <eigenfunctions> for distinct <eigenvalues> are <orthogonal>, and regular <Sturm-Liouville theory> supplies a complete <orthonormal eigenbasis>. Here these objects can be exhibited explicitly:
$$
\boxed{\phi_n(x)=\sqrt{\frac2l}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{l},\qquad
\lambda_n=1-\left(\frac{n\pi}{l}\right)^2,\qquad n=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
Indeed $\mathcal L\phi_n=\lambda_n\phi_n$, and the <Fourier sine basis> is complete in $L^2(0,l)$. There are no additional <eigenvalues>: for a nonzero <eigenfunction> $u$, <integration by parts> gives
$$
\lambda=1-\frac{\int_0^l|u'|^2\,dx}{\int_0^l|u|^2\,dx}<1,
$$
so $u''+(1-\lambda)u=0$ and the endpoint values force $\sqrt{1-\lambda}\,l=n\pi$. All these <eigenvalues> are simple. The <eigenvectors> are precisely the nonzero multiples of these <eigenfunctions>, regarded as <vectors> in the <Hilbert space>.

The <homogeneous solution> is $A\cos x+B\sin x$. The left <boundary condition> removes $A$, and the right <boundary condition> gives $B\sin l=0$. Thus the <kernel of a linear map> associated with this <boundary value problem> is
$$
\boxed{\ker\mathcal L=
\begin{cases}
\{0\},&l\notin\pi\mathbb N_{>0},\\
\operatorname{span}\{\sin x\},&l=m\pi,\quad m\in\mathbb N_{>0}.
\end{cases}}
$$
The <Fredholm alternative> now gives the complete classification:
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c}
l&\text{condition on }f&\text{solutions}\\\hline
l\notin\pi\mathbb N_{>0}&\text{any prescribed }f&\text{exactly one}\\
l=m\pi&\displaystyle\int_0^{m\pi}f(x)\sin x\,dx=0&\text{infinitely many}\\
l=m\pi&\displaystyle\int_0^{m\pi}f(x)\sin x\,dx\ne0&\text{none}.
\end{array}}
$$
For necessity at $l=m\pi$, multiply by the <homogeneous solution> $\sin x$ and use the <Lagrange identity for a Sturm-Liouville operator> to obtain $\int_0^lf\sin x\,dx=0$. For sufficiency, the <particular solution>
$$
z(x)=\int_0^x\sin(x-t)f(t)\,dt
$$
satisfies $z(0)=0$ and $z''+z=f$. At the other endpoint,
$$
z(m\pi)=(-1)^{m+1}\int_0^{m\pi}\sin t\,f(t)\,dt,
$$
which is zero under the compatibility condition. All solutions are then $z+B\sin x$, with $B$ arbitrary. The assumed <Lipschitz continuity> of $f$ in particular makes $f$ continuous, so the displayed <integral> defines a classical solution. The length $l=0$ is excluded: it would not define a nonempty interval for this <boundary value problem>.

For $\sin l\ne0$, use <variation of parameters> with the <homogeneous solutions> $\cos x,\sin x$, whose <Wronskian> is one. Write $y=A(x)\cos x+B(x)\sin x$ and impose $A'\cos x+B'\sin x=0$. The <differential equation> gives $-A'\sin x+B'\cos x=f$, hence
$$
A'=-f\sin x,\qquad B'=f\cos x.
$$
After integrating from zero and using the left <boundary condition>,
$$
y(x)=C\sin x+\int_0^x\sin(x-t)f(t)\,dt.
$$
The right <boundary condition> determines
$$
C=-\frac1{\sin l}\int_0^l\sin(l-t)f(t)\,dt,
$$
so the unique solution is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\int_0^x\sin(x-t)f(t)\,dt
-\frac{\sin x}{\sin l}\int_0^l\sin(l-t)f(t)\,dt.}
$$
Combining the two <integrals> gives $y(x)=\int_0^lG(x,t)f(t)\,dt$, with <Green's function>
$$
\boxed{G(x,t)=-\frac1{\sin l}
\begin{cases}
\sin x\sin(l-t),&0\leq x\leq t,\\
\sin t\sin(l-x),&t\leq x\leq l.
\end{cases}}
$$
It is symmetric in $x,t$, as expected from <self-adjointness>, and has zero endpoint values. At $x=t$ it is continuous, while its first <derivative> obeys the <jump condition>
$$
G_x(t^+,t)-G_x(t^-,t)
=\frac{\sin t\cos(l-t)+\cos t\sin(l-t)}{\sin l}=1.
$$
Thus $(\partial_x^2+1)G=\delta(x-t)$, with the positive normalization of the <Dirac delta function> required for $\mathcal Ly=f$.

Expand in the <Fourier sine basis>, using unnormalized <eigenfunctions>:
$$
f(x)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty f_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{l},\qquad
f_n=\frac2l\int_0^lf(t)\sin\frac{n\pi t}{l}\,dt,
\qquad y(x)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty c_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{l}.
$$
Projection of $\mathcal Ly=f$ onto each <eigenfunction> gives
$$
\boxed{\left[1-\left(\frac{n\pi}{l}\right)^2\right]c_n=f_n.}
$$
When $l\notin\pi\mathbb N_{>0}$, every <eigenvalue> is nonzero and the <eigenfunction expansion> is uniquely determined:
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty
\frac{f_n}{1-(n\pi/l)^2}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{l}.}
$$
When $l=m\pi$, the exceptional index is $n=m$, for which the <eigenfunction> is $\sin x$. If $f_m\ne0$, its coefficient equation is $0=f_m$, an inconsistency: there is no solution. If $f_m=0$, the coefficient equation is $0=0$, and $c_m$ is arbitrary. The full family is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=C\sin x+
\sum_{\substack{n\geq1\\n\ne m}}
\frac{f_n}{1-(n/m)^2}\sin\frac{nx}{m},\qquad C\in\mathbb R.}
$$
This is the <Fredholm alternative> directly in the coefficient equations: nonzero <eigenvalues> fix their coefficients; a zero <eigenvalue> requires zero forcing in that direction and leaves the corresponding coefficient free. An inconsistent equation at a zero <eigenvalue> cannot be repaired by assigning an infinite coefficient.

For convergence, <Lipschitz continuity> implies <absolute continuity> of $f$, with bounded <derivative> almost everywhere. One <integration by parts> gives $f_n=O(n^{-1})$; since the denominators grow quadratically, $c_n=O(n^{-3})$ apart from the single free coefficient. The solution series and its first <derivative> therefore converge uniformly. The second <derivative> converges in $L^2$, and the resulting <Sobolev space> solution satisfies $y''=f-y$. As $f-y$ is a <continuous function>, this is a classical $C^2$ solution, with both <boundary conditions> satisfied. The forcing's <Fourier sine series> is interpreted on the open interval or in $L^2$; its endpoint values need not equal the endpoint values of $f$.

At $l=\pi$, the <Fredholm solvability condition> is
$$
0=\int_0^\pi(a\sin x+b\sin2x)\sin x\,dx=\frac{a\pi}{2},
$$
by <orthogonality> of the <Fourier sine basis>. Thus a non-unique solution exists exactly when $a=0$, with any real $b$. Since $(d^2/dx^2+1)\sin2x=-3\sin2x$, the complete family is
$$
\boxed{a=0:\quad y(x)=C\sin x-\frac b3\sin2x,\quad C\in\mathbb R.}
$$
If $a\ne0$ there is no solution, for any $b$. In particular, when $a=b=0$, the family reduces to the <kernel> $C\sin x$; it is still non-unique. There is no choice of $a,b$ producing a unique solution for this length.

Putting both data at zero makes this an <initial value problem>. The <uniqueness theorem for ordinary differential equations> applies, and one can also prove uniqueness directly: the difference of two solutions is a <homogeneous solution> $A\cos x+B\sin x$ whose value and first <derivative> at zero are both zero, forcing $A=B=0$. In fact, for every prescribed <continuous function> $f$,
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\int_0^x\sin(x-t)f(t)\,dt}
$$
satisfies both initial data and $y''+y=f$. Consequently non-uniqueness is impossible for every $l>0$, including $l=m\pi$. In this setting the <kernel> is trivial. The <boundary conditions> at a single endpoint do not give the same <self-adjoint operator> domain as the two separated <Dirichlet boundary conditions>, so the earlier exceptional lengths do not apply.

Work first on $x>0$. For $b=0$, the <power-law ansatz> $y=x^r$ gives the <indicial equation> $r(r-1)+r-a^2=r^2-a^2=0$. Therefore the <Cauchy-Euler equation> has <general solution>
$$
\boxed{y(x)=
\begin{cases}
C_1x^a+C_2x^{-a},&a>0,\\
C_1+C_2\log x,&a=0.
\end{cases}}
$$
For $a=0$ the repeated <indicial root> supplies the <logarithm> rather than two copies of the constant solution. Alternatively, the <change of variables> $t=\log x$ turns the <differential equation> into $Y''-a^2Y=0$. On $x<0$ the corresponding real formulas use $|x|$ and $\log|x|$, with independent constants on the two sides of zero.

For general $b$, normalize the <second-order linear differential equation> as
$$
y''+P(x)y'+Q(x)y=0,\qquad P(x)=\frac1x,\qquad Q(x)=-\frac{a^2}{x^2}+bx^{\beta-2}.
$$
The coefficient $P$ is singular at zero, but $xP(x)=1$ and $x^2Q(x)=-a^2+bx^\beta$ are <analytic functions> there because $\beta$ is a positive integer. The <regular singular point criterion for a second-order equation> therefore proves that zero is a <regular singular point> for every allowed $a,b,\beta$. The <indicial equation> remains $r^2-a^2=0$.

For the first <Frobenius solution>, take the larger <indicial root> $r=a$ and normalize the first coefficient to one:
$$
y_+(x)=x^a\sum_{n=0}^\infty c_nx^n,\qquad c_0=1,
\qquad c_j=0\quad(j<0).
$$
Substitution and comparison of the coefficient of $x^{n+a}$ give
$$
\big[(n+a)^2-a^2\big]c_n+bc_{n-\beta}=0,
\qquad\boxed{c_n=-\frac{b\,c_{n-\beta}}{n(n+2a)}\quad(n\geq1).}
$$
Every denominator is nonzero because $a\geq0$.

For $\beta=1$, the <recurrence relation> is $c_n=-bc_{n-1}/[n(n+2a)]$, so
$$
\boxed{y_+(x)=x^a\left[1-\frac{b}{1+2a}x+
\frac{b^2}{2(1+2a)(2+2a)}x^2+\cdots\right],\quad
c_n=\frac{(-b)^n}{\prod_{j=1}^n j(j+2a)}.}
$$
For $\beta=2$, $c_1=0$ and all odd coefficients vanish; the even coefficients satisfy $c_{2k}=-bc_{2k-2}/[2k(2k+2a)]$. Hence
$$
\boxed{y_+(x)=x^a\left[1-\frac{b}{4(1+a)}x^2+
\frac{b^2}{32(1+a)(2+a)}x^4+\cdots\right].}
$$
For any positive integer $\beta$, induction in the <recurrence relation> shows that only indices divisible by $\beta$ survive:
$$
\boxed{y_+(x)=x^a\sum_{k=0}^\infty
\frac{(-b)^k x^{k\beta}}{\prod_{j=1}^k j\beta(j\beta+2a)}.}
$$
The empty product for $k=0$ equals one. Successive terms in the <power series> factor have ratio of magnitudes
$$
\frac{|b|\,|x|^\beta}{(k+1)\beta((k+1)\beta+2a)}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus the <ratio test> gives convergence for every finite $x$; a nonintegral $a$ still requires a chosen side of zero or a complex branch for the factor $x^a$.

For a putative second <Frobenius solution> with the smaller <indicial root> $-a$, set $y_-=x^{-a}\sum_{n\geq0}d_nx^n$, with $d_0=1$ and $d_j=0$ for $j<0$. Its <recurrence relation> is
$$
\boxed{n(n-2a)d_n=-b\,d_{n-\beta}.}
$$
The <Frobenius case classification> concerns the gap $2a$ between the <indicial roots>. In the course convention, Case IIIa is the obstructed recurrence requiring a <logarithmic Frobenius solution>; Case IIIb permits two pure <Frobenius series>.

If $a>0$ and $2a\notin\mathbb Z$, this is **Case I**: no denominator vanishes. There are two <linearly independent> pure <Frobenius solutions>, with
$$
y_-(x)=x^{-a}\sum_{k=0}^\infty
\frac{(-b)^kx^{k\beta}}{\prod_{j=1}^k j\beta(j\beta-2a)}.
$$
Their different leading powers establish <linear independence>.

If $a=0$, this is **Case II**, with a repeated <indicial root>. There is only one pure <Frobenius series> with a nonzero leading coefficient. To see the second solution's form, <reduction of order> gives
$$
y_2(x)=y_+(x)\int^x\frac{dt}{t\,y_+(t)^2}.
$$
Here $y_+(t)=1+O(t^\beta)$, so the integrand is $t^{-1}+O(t^{\beta-1})$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{y_2(x)=y_+(x)\log x+\text{an analytic power series}.}
$$
The <logarithm> has a nonzero coefficient and cannot be removed by adding a multiple of $y_+$.

If $N=2a$ is a positive integer, the only potentially obstructed coefficient is $n=N$. Below that index, $d_n=0$ unless $\beta$ divides $n$. At the exceptional index the equation reads
$$
0=-b\,d_{N-\beta}.
$$
If $N=m\beta$ for some positive integer $m$, all coefficients $d_0,d_\beta,\ldots,d_{(m-1)\beta}$ are nonzero because $b\ne0$. The exceptional equation is inconsistent. This is **Case IIIa**, and the independent second solution has the form
$$
\boxed{y_2=x^{-a}\sum_{n=0}^\infty d_nx^n+K y_+\log x,\qquad K\ne0.}
$$
The <Frobenius resonance for an Euler equation with monomial potential> explains why divisibility, rather than the integer gap alone, decides this outcome. For example, with $d_0=1$, applying the original <differential operator> to $y_+\log x$ gives $2xy_+'$. Cancellation of the coefficient of $x^a$ requires $K=-bd_{N-\beta}/(2a)\ne0$.

If $\beta$ does not divide $N$, then $d_{N-\beta}=0$; this includes $N<\beta$ through the negative-index convention. The exceptional equation is $0=0$, so $d_N$ is free. This is **Case IIIb**: set $d_N=0$ to select an independent pure <Frobenius series> starting with $x^{-a}$. Subsequent coefficients again follow the <recurrence relation>. Changing $d_N$ simply adds a multiple of $y_+$, the <undetermined coefficient at Frobenius resonance>.

The complete answer is therefore
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{parameters}&\text{case}\\\hline
a=0&\mathrm{II}\\
a>0,\quad 2a\notin\mathbb Z&\mathrm{I}\\
2a\in\mathbb N_{>0},\quad 2a/\beta\in\mathbb N_{>0}&\mathrm{IIIa}\\
2a\in\mathbb N_{>0},\quad 2a/\beta\notin\mathbb N_{>0}&\mathrm{IIIb}.
\end{array}}
$$
In particular, for $\beta=1$ every positive integer gap gives Case IIIa. For $\beta=2$, positive integer $a$ gives Case IIIa, while $a=1/2,3/2,5/2,\ldots$ gives Case IIIb; the remaining positive $a$ give Case I. The condition $b\ne0$ matters: at $b=0$ the two <Cauchy-Euler equation> powers already give independent solutions whenever $a>0$.

Normalize both leading coefficients to one. For the <indicial root> $a=1/2$, the <recurrence relation> from part (b) gives
$$
c_{2k}=\frac{(-1)^k}{(2k+1)!},\qquad c_{2k+1}=0.
$$
Recognizing the <Taylor series> of the <sine> gives the first <Frobenius solution>
$$
\boxed{y_+(x)=x^{1/2}\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^kx^{2k}}{(2k+1)!}
=\frac{\sin x}{\sqrt x}.}
$$
For the smaller <indicial root> $-1/2$, $n(n-1)d_n=-d_{n-2}$. The equation at $n=1$ is $0=0$; choose the free coefficient $d_1=0$ to avoid adding the first <Frobenius solution>. The remaining coefficients are
$$
d_{2k}=\frac{(-1)^k}{(2k)!},\qquad d_{2k+1}=0.
$$
Thus the <Taylor series> of the <cosine> gives
$$
\boxed{y_-(x)=x^{-1/2}\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^kx^{2k}}{(2k)!}
=\frac{\cos x}{\sqrt x}.}
$$
Their leading terms are $x^{1/2}$ and $x^{-1/2}$, as required for Case IIIb. A direct check uses $y=x^{-1/2}u$: the <differential equation> becomes $x^{3/2}(u''+u)=0$, so these two functions solve it on $x>0$. Their <Wronskian> is $-1/x\ne0$, proving <linear independence>. The <general solution> is $(C_1\sin x+C_2\cos x)/\sqrt x$.

The <function> $F$ is an <odd function>, passes through $(0,0)$, and has the horizontal <asymptote> $F=0$ at both infinities. Its <derivative> is
$$
F'(x)=\frac{1-x^2}{(1+x^2)^2}.
$$
It decreases for $|x|>1$ and increases for $|x|<1$, with its lowest value $F(-1)=-1/2$ and highest value $F(1)=1/2$. These features determine the <graph of a function> shown below.

For $0<\epsilon<1/2$, the equation is the <quadratic equation> $\epsilon x^2-x+\epsilon=0$. The <quadratic formula> gives exactly two real <equation roots>:
$$
x_\pm=\frac{1\pm\sqrt{1-4\epsilon^2}}{2\epsilon}.
$$
The <binomial series> gives $\sqrt{1-4\epsilon^2}=1-2\epsilon^2-2\epsilon^4-4\epsilon^6+O(\epsilon^8)$, hence the two requested <asymptotic expansions> are
$$
\boxed{x_-=\epsilon+\epsilon^3+O(\epsilon^5),\qquad
x_+=\epsilon^{-1}-\epsilon+O(\epsilon^3).}
$$
Their product is exactly one. Both are positive, with $x_-<1<x_+$; there are no negative <equation roots> because $F(x)<0$ for $x<0$. At $\epsilon=1/2$ the two real <equation roots> coincide at one, and for $\epsilon>1/2$ there are none. In the stated limit $\epsilon\to0^+$, these two branches are therefore exhaustive.

The <power-law ansatz> $y=x^r$ gives
$$
\epsilon r(r-1)+r-1=(r-1)(\epsilon r+1)=0.
$$
Since $\epsilon>0$, the <equation roots> $1$ and $-1/\epsilon$ are distinct. The <Cauchy-Euler equation> therefore has <general solution>
$$
\boxed{y(x)=C_1x^{-1/\epsilon}+C_2x.}
$$
For $C_1=C_2=1$, the first <derivative> and second <derivative> are
$$
y'=1-\frac1\epsilon x^{-1/\epsilon-1},\qquad
y''=\frac{1+\epsilon}{\epsilon^2}x^{-1/\epsilon-2}>0.
$$
The <strictly convex function> diverges to $+\infty$ as $x\to0^+$, passes through $(1,2)$, and has a unique lowest point at
$$
\boxed{x_{\min}=\epsilon^{-\epsilon/(1+\epsilon)},\qquad
y_{\min}=(1+\epsilon)x_{\min}.}
$$
As $x\to\infty$, $y-x\to0$, so the line $y=x$ is an <asymptote>. When $0<\epsilon<1$, the lowest point is to the right of one; at $\epsilon=1$ it is at one, and for $\epsilon>1$ it is to the left of one.

The ratio of the two terms is
$$
\frac{x^{-1/\epsilon}}x=x^{-(1+1/\epsilon)}.
$$
Thus $x^{-1/\epsilon}$ is larger for $0<x<1$, the terms are equal at $x=1$, and $x$ is larger for $x>1$. In the stronger asymptotic sense as $\epsilon\to0^+$, $x^{-1/\epsilon}$ dominates at each fixed $x<1$, and $x$ dominates at each fixed $x>1$. Their crossover has $|\log x|=O(\epsilon)$, equivalently $x=1+O(\epsilon)$, as described by the <power-law crossover in a singular perturbation>. In that region $x=1+\epsilon X$ with bounded $X$ gives $y\sim1+e^{-X}$.

Substitute the proposed <regular perturbation> $y=y_0+\epsilon y_1+\cdots$ into the <differential equation>. The leading and subsequent equations are
$$
xy_0'-y_0=0,\qquad xy_j'-y_j=-x^2y_{j-1}''\quad(j\geq1).
$$
Consequently $y_0=Cx$, and in fact each successive coefficient has the form $y_j=C_jx$, because the preceding second <derivative> is zero. The leading approximation is therefore
$$
\boxed{y\sim Cx;\qquad y\sim x\text{ when matched to the specified solution at fixed }x>1.}
$$
It has only one adjustable constant and generally cannot impose two independent <boundary conditions>. Those assumed in the question require a nonzero $x^{-1/\epsilon}$ coefficient, which a <power series> in $\epsilon$ cannot recover: at fixed $x>1$ it is exponentially small, and at fixed $x<1$ it grows without bound as $\epsilon\to0^+$. Thus the <regular perturbation> describes the specified solution only in the region to the right of the crossover, away from $x=1$; it is not a uniform approximation on $(0,\infty)$.

Setting $\epsilon=0$ gives the reduced <first-order linear differential equation> $y'=y$. Its nonzero solutions cannot satisfy the zero value at $x=2$, so one endpoint needs a <boundary layer>. To determine which endpoint, freeze the coefficient at $x=x_b>0$ in the rapidly varying part: $\epsilon y''-x_by'=0$ has fast mode $e^{x_b(x-x_b)/\epsilon}$. It decays into the interval from the right endpoint but grows into the interval from the left endpoint. Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{the boundary layer is at }x=2,\text{ with thickness }O(\epsilon).}
$$
The <dominant balance> $\epsilon/\delta^2\sim x_b/\delta$ gives $\delta\sim\epsilon/x_b$, so a convenient local thickness is $\epsilon/2$. The left <boundary condition> must select the <outer solution>. Equivalently, a putative left <inner variable> $X=(x-1)/\epsilon$ would give $Y_{XX}-Y_X=0$, whose nonconstant solution grows like $e^X$ and cannot satisfy the <asymptotic matching condition> as $X\to\infty$.

The reduced equation and the left <boundary condition> give the leading <outer solution>
$$
y_{\mathrm{out},0}'=y_{\mathrm{out},0},\qquad y_{\mathrm{out},0}(1)=1,
\qquad\boxed{y_{\mathrm{out},0}(x)=e^{x-1}.}
$$
Introduce the right <inner variable> $X=(2-x)/\epsilon\geq0$ and write $Y(X)=y(2-\epsilon X)$. The <chain rule> gives $y'=-Y_X/\epsilon$ and $y''=Y_{XX}/\epsilon^2$. Multiplying the rescaled <differential equation> by $\epsilon$ gives the exact equation
$$
Y_{XX}+(2-\epsilon X)Y_X+\epsilon(2-\epsilon X)Y=0.
$$
At leading order, $Y_{0,XX}+2Y_{0,X}=0$, with <general solution> $Y_0=A+Be^{-2X}$. The right <boundary condition> gives $Y_0(0)=0$. The <asymptotic matching condition> in the <overlap region> $\epsilon\ll2-x\ll1$ gives
$$
\lim_{X\to\infty}Y_0(X)=\lim_{x\to2^-}y_{\mathrm{out},0}(x)=e.
$$
Thus $A=e$ and $B=-e$, yielding the leading <inner expansion>
$$
\boxed{Y_0(X)=e(1-e^{-2X}),\qquad
y_{\mathrm{in},0}(x)=e\left(1-e^{-2(2-x)/\epsilon}\right).}
$$
Equivalently, the <inner variable> $\xi=2(2-x)/\epsilon$ gives $Y_0=e(1-e^{-\xi})$. Both normalizations describe the same <boundary layer>.

= iii
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

The common value in the <overlap region> is $e$. The <additive composite expansion> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{y_{\mathrm{comp},0}(x)=y_{\mathrm{out},0}(x)+y_{\mathrm{in},0}(x)-e
=e^{x-1}-e\,e^{-2(2-x)/\epsilon}.}
$$
This leading approximation has $y_{\mathrm{comp},0}(2)=0$ and $y_{\mathrm{comp},0}(1)=1-e^{1-2/\epsilon}$; the left endpoint error is exponentially small. It rises from near one following the <outer solution> $e^{x-1}$, approaches a value close to $e$, then drops rapidly to zero in the right <boundary layer>. Its first <derivative> is
$$
y_{\mathrm{comp},0}'=e^{x-1}-\frac{2e}{\epsilon}e^{-2(2-x)/\epsilon},
\qquad y_{\mathrm{comp},0}'(2)=e\left(1-\frac2\epsilon\right).
$$
For small $\epsilon$ the largest value lies slightly inside the interval. For this <additive composite expansion>, the first <derivative> vanishes at
$$
2-x_{\max}=\frac{\epsilon\log(2/\epsilon)}{2-\epsilon}
\sim\frac\epsilon2\log\frac2\epsilon.
$$
This locates the rounded top of the sketch; the rapid final drop has the $O(\epsilon)$ scale found above. The <additive composite expansion> is a leading approximation, so its exponentially small endpoint error does not imply an exponentially small error throughout the interval: neglected algebraic corrections to the <outer expansion> and <inner expansion> remain.
