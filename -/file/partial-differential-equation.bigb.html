= PDE
{synonym}
{c}

A <partial differential equation> relates an unknown <function> of several independent variables to its <partial derivatives>. For $u(x,t)$, the notation $u_t=\partial u/\partial t$ means changing time while holding position fixed; $u_{xx}=\partial^2u/\partial x^2$ measures how its spatial slope changes. Unlike an <ordinary differential equation>, a <partial differential equation> can describe an entire evolving profile rather than a single trajectory.

The <heat equation> $u_t=D u_{xx}$ smooths spatial variations, the <transport equation> $u_t+c u_x=0$ moves them, and <Laplace's equation> $\Delta u=0$ describes a spatial equilibrium. Here $D>0$ is a <diffusivity>, $c$ is a <velocity>, and $\Delta$ is the <Laplacian>. The <periodic heat and transport comparison> makes the difference visible using identical <initial conditions>.

A <partial differential equation> usually supplies a local rule rather than a complete answer. For example, every $u(x,t)=A+Bx$ satisfies the <heat equation> with zero <PDE source term>; data select which profile is relevant. A complete problem specifies its <domain of a function>, coefficients, <initial conditions>, <boundary conditions>, and meaning of <solution of a partial differential equation>. A <well-posed problem> has existence, uniqueness, and <continuous dependence on initial data> in stated spaces; the equation alone guarantees none of these.https://www.math.toronto.edu/ivrii/PDE-textbook/Chapter1/S1.2.html[Victor Ivrii, Initial and Boundary Value Problems]{ref}

= Initial and boundary data for evolution equations
{parent=Partial differential equation}

On $0<x<L$, $t>0$, an <initial condition> $u(x,0)=g(x)$ gives the starting profile. For the <heat equation>, <Dirichlet boundary conditions> $u(0,t)=a(t)$, $u(L,t)=b(t)$ can model ends held at prescribed <temperatures>. Alternatively, zero <Neumann boundary conditions> describe insulated ends in the constant-conductivity model. These choices can produce different evolutions from the same <initial condition>.

For a continuous <classical solution> reaching the corners, the <Dirichlet boundary data> must satisfy $g(0)=a(0)$ and $g(L)=b(0)$; higher smoothness can require further compatibility. In dimensionless variables, a complete worked <heat equation> problem is
$$
u_t=u_{xx},\quad 0<x<1,\quad t>0,\qquad u(x,0)=x+\sin(\pi x),\quad u(0,t)=0,\quad u(1,t)=1.
$$
Its exact <classical solution> is $u(x,t)=x+e^{-\pi^2t}\sin(\pi x)$. The straight profile $x$ is the equilibrium; the extra bump decays. Substituting verifies the equation and every datum, and the <energy estimate> proves uniqueness among smooth solutions with these data.

For the <transport equation> with $c>0$ on the same <interval>, prescribe an <inflow transport boundary condition> only at $x=0$. Backtracking a <characteristic curve> gives $g(x-ct)$ if $x\geq ct$, and $a(t-x/c)$ otherwise. Values at $x=L$ are determined by this formula; independently prescribing them can make the problem inconsistent.https://www.math.utoronto.ca/ivrii/PDE-textbook/Chapter2/S2.1.html[Victor Ivrii, First order PDEs, initial-boundary value problems]{ref}

= PDE source term
{synonym}
{c}

A prescribed <PDE source term> changes the <heat equation> to $u_t=D u_{xx}+f(x,t)$, or the <transport equation> to $u_t+c u_x=f(x,t)$. If $u$ is a <temperature>, $f$ has units of temperature per time. The formulas below assume $f=0$; a nonzero <PDE source term> can change the spatial average even with <periodic boundary conditions>.

= Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation

{c}
{title2=$u_t+uu_x+u_{xx}+u_{xxxx}=0$}

= KS equation
{synonym}
{c}

The Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation $u_t+uu_x+u_{xx}+u_{xxxx}=0$ combines destabilizing long-wave growth, stabilizing fourth-order <diffusion>, and nonlinear transport. Its potential form is $\Phi_t=-\Phi_{xx}-\Phi_{xxxx}+(\Phi_x)^2$, with $u=-2\Phi_x$. It arises as a long-wave phase <amplitude equation> near loss of the <Benjamin-Feir stability condition>. On a domain of period $P$, the zero solution's <Fourier modes> grow at rate $k^2-k^4$, $k=2\pi n/P$.

= Primary periodic bifurcation of the Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation
{parent=Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation}

Fix zero mean and period $P$ in the <Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation>. At $P=2\pi$ the first <Fourier mode> becomes neutral and all higher modes are damped. For $k=2\pi/P$, expand $u=a\sin(kx)+b\sin(2kx)+\cdots$. The first equations are $\dot a=\lambda_1a+kab/2+\cdots$, $\dot b=\lambda_2b-ka^2/2+\cdots$, with $\lambda_j=(jk)^2-(jk)^4$. Slaving the second harmonic gives $\dot a=\lambda_1a+k^2a^3/(4\lambda_2)+\cdots$. Since $\lambda_2=-12$ at onset, this is a <supercritical bifurcation>. The small nonzero branch is stable in the sense of <orbital stability> against disturbances of the same fixed period, modulo <translation>; stability against disturbances of larger period does not follow from this local argument.

= Green's first theorem
{c}
{synonym}

For smooth functions $u,v$ on a region $V$,
$$
\int_V\left(u\Delta v+\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\right)dV
=\int_{\partial V}u\frac{\partial v}{\partial n}\,dS.
$$

= Gradient pairing with a boundary-constant function and a harmonic function
{parent=Green's first identity}
{title2=$\int_V\nabla f\cdot\nabla g\,dV=0$}

If $f$ is constant on the boundary and $g$ is a regular <harmonic function> throughout a volume, the <divergence theorem> applied to $f\nabla g$ gives $\int_V\nabla f\cdot\nabla g=\int_{\partial V}f\partial_ng$. The constant boundary value factors out; a second application of the divergence theorem makes the remaining flux $\int_V\Delta g=0$. This requires enough regularity to use <Green's first identity> over the entire volume.

= Boundary flux from a singular harmonic potential
{parent=Gradient pairing with a boundary-constant function and a harmonic function}
{title2=$\int_{|x|<a}\nabla(r/a)\cdot\nabla(1/r)\,dV=-4\pi$}

Although $1/r$ is harmonic away from the origin in three dimensions, it is not a regular <harmonic function> on a ball containing the origin. Its <gradient> is $-\widehat r/r^2$, so the displayed improper volume integral follows by canceling the $r^2$ in the spherical volume element. On the annulus $\varepsilon<r<a$, <Green's first identity> gives outer boundary contribution $-4\pi$ and inner contribution $4\pi\varepsilon/a$, recovering the same limit. Its global <Laplacian> contains a <Dirac delta distribution>, so the regular-harmonic gradient-pairing theorem is inapplicable.

= Maximum principle
{parent=Partial differential equation}
{wiki}

A maximum principle bounds a solution through its boundary or initial data, often forbidding nonconstant solutions from attaining an interior extremum. Weak and strong versions depend on the differential operator and hypotheses. The <maximum principle for harmonic functions> and its strong counterpart are examples.

= Strong maximum principle for harmonic functions
{parent=Maximum principle}

If a harmonic function on a connected domain attains an interior maximum or minimum, it is constant. The mean value property forces equality throughout every sufficiently small ball around an interior extremum, and connectedness propagates the equality.

This is the strong harmonic instance of the <maximum principle>.

= Maximum principle for harmonic functions
{parent=Maximum principle}

A continuous function harmonic on a bounded domain attains its maximum and minimum on the boundary. Apply the strictly subharmonic result to $u+\epsilon|x|^2$ and let $\epsilon\downarrow0$, then apply the same argument to $-u$.

= Burgers' equation
{parent=Partial differential equation}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$u_t+uu_x=\nu u_{xx}$}

<Burgers' equation> is the one-dimensional <partial differential equation>
$$
u_t+uu_x=\nu u_{xx},\qquad \nu\geq0.
$$
The term $uu_x$ supplies nonlinear transport in a <transport equation>: the transported value $u$ is also its own <characteristic speed> when $\nu=0$. Larger values catch up with smaller values ahead of them, steepening a decreasing profile. The <diffusion> term $\nu u_{xx}$ opposes this steepening. Positive $\nu$ gives the <viscous Burgers equation>; setting $\nu=0$ gives the <inviscid Burgers equation>.

This small equation makes three different behaviors accessible in one model: <Burgers characteristic breaking> from continuous decreasing data, an <entropy shock> for a downward step, and a <rarefaction wave> for an upward step. The <Burgers Riemann problem with positive flux> and the <viscous Burgers travelling shock> below give exact examples. These are mathematical calculations with chosen data; the figures contain no experimental measurements.

Unless stated otherwise, take $x\in\mathbb R$, $t\geq0$, constant $\nu$, no <PDE source term>, and an <initial condition> $u(x,0)=u_0(x)$. There are no finite endpoints requiring <boundary conditions>. Where a profile has different limiting states at $x=\pm\infty$, those states are specified explicitly. On a finite <interval>, <boundary conditions> become part of the problem: positive <diffusion> normally requires a condition at each endpoint, whereas the <inviscid Burgers equation> requires appropriate <inflow transport boundary conditions>. With dimensional variables, $u$ has units of <velocity> and $\nu$ of <kinematic viscosity>; all numerical examples below use <dimensionless quantities>.

= Viscous Burgers equation
{title2=$u_t+uu_x=\nu u_{xx}$}
{parent=Burgers' equation}

The <viscous Burgers equation> combines a nonlinear <transport equation> with <diffusion> with constant $\nu>0$. On the infinite line, a bounded <initial condition> gives a <smooth function> solving the equation for every positive time; a jump in the <initial condition> is smoothed rather than retained as a discontinuity. Small $\nu$ can nevertheless produce a steep layer, as the <viscous Burgers travelling shock> illustrates. The <Cole-Hopf transformation> $u=-2\nu\,\partial_x\log\phi$, with positive $\phi$, converts this equation to the <heat equation> $\phi_t=\nu\phi_{xx}$.<Asymptotic and Perturbation Methods by Victor Ivrii/Burgers equation. 1>[§9.1, Problem set-up, (9.1.1)–(9.1.2); Cole-Hopf transform, (9.1.11)–(9.1.13)]{ref}

Positive <diffusion> also explains the admissibility of the inviscid limit. For the <entropy flux for a scalar conservation law> associated with $\eta(u)=u^2/2$, direct multiplication by $u$ gives
$$
\partial_t\frac{u^2}{2}+\partial_x\frac{u^3}{3}=\nu\partial_{xx}\frac{u^2}{2}-\nu u_x^2.
$$
The last term is nonpositive. With convergence sufficient to pass to the limit, the <vanishing viscosity approximation> therefore retains an entropy inequality rather than just the <weak solution> equation. For general bounded data, the full <Kruzhkov entropy inequalities> specify the <entropy solution>; the quadratic identity is a useful illustration of dissipation.<Asymptotic and Perturbation Methods by Victor Ivrii/Burgers equation. 1>[§9.1, Case epsilon=0 as a limit, identity following (9.1.10)]{ref}

= Viscous Burgers travelling shock
{parent=Viscous Burgers equation}
{c}
{title2=$U(z)=s-\frac{u_L-u_R}{2}\tanh\frac{(u_L-u_R)z}{4\nu}$}

A <travelling wave> of the <viscous Burgers equation> can connect constant states $u_L>u_R$ through a smooth decreasing layer. Require $u(x,t)=U(z)$, $z=x-st$, $U(-\infty)=u_L$, $U(+\infty)=u_R$, and $U'\to0$ at both ends. Substitution and one <integration> give
$$
(U-s)U'=\nu U'',\qquad \nu U'=\frac12(U-u_L)(U-u_R),\qquad s=\frac{u_L+u_R}{2}.
$$
The speed follows by evaluating the integration constant at both end states. It is precisely the <Rankine-Hugoniot condition> for <conservation law flux> $f(u)=u^2/2$. Choosing the center $U(0)=s$ gives the displayed <hyperbolic tangent> profile. Its characteristic width scales as $\nu/(u_L-u_R)$; a smaller positive $\nu$ makes a thinner smooth layer.

For an original worked example in <dimensionless quantities>, take $u_L=2$, $u_R=0$ and $s=1$:
$$
u(x,t)=1-\tanh\frac{x-t}{2\nu}=\frac{2}{1+\exp((x-t)/\nu)}.
$$
This is an exact <classical solution> on $\mathbb R$ with its own smooth <initial condition> $u(x,0)=1-\tanh(x/(2\nu))$ and limiting states $2,0$. It is a family of moving profiles, not the finite-$\nu$ solution starting from an exact discontinuous step. For that different <initial condition>, the <Cole-Hopf transformation> supplies the evolution. At $\nu=1/2$, this front starts from the same $1-\tanh x$ profile as the <Burgers characteristic breaking> example: positive <diffusion> maintains a smooth moving layer while the inviscid profile breaks at $t=1$.

To check the <partial differential equation> explicitly, set $w=(x-t)/(2\nu)$ and $S=1-\tanh^2w$. Then
$$
u_t=\frac{S}{2\nu},\qquad u_x=-\frac{S}{2\nu},\qquad u_{xx}=\frac{S\tanh w}{2\nu^2},\qquad u_t+uu_x=\frac{S\tanh w}{2\nu}=\nu u_{xx}.
$$
The independent <Rankine-Hugoniot condition> check gives $s=(0-2)/(0-2)=1$, using $f(2)=2$ and $f(0)=0$. At fixed $x\ne t$, the <vanishing viscosity approximation> tends to the <entropy shock> $2$ for $x<t$ and $0$ for $x>t$. At the center the smooth profiles stay at $1$; changing the value on the discontinuity does not change the limiting <weak solution>.

For $u_L<u_R$, the same integrated <ordinary differential equation> has $U'<0$ between the two states when $\nu>0$, so it cannot supply an increasing connecting front. The inviscid admissible expansion is the <rarefaction wave> of the <Burgers Riemann problem with positive flux>.

= Modified Burgers equation with cubic flux
{parent=Viscous Burgers equation}
{title2=$q_Z-q^2q_\theta=\epsilon q_{\theta\theta}$}

The cubic-flux variant replaces the quadratic conservative flux of <Burgers' equation> by $-q^3/3$. Its inviscid <characteristic speed> is $-q^2$, while positive diffusivity regularizes <shock waves>. It is not linearized by the usual <Cole-Hopf transformation>.

= Traveling front of the cubic-flux Burgers equation
{parent=Modified Burgers equation with cubic flux}
{title2=$q=\beta[1+e^{-2(s-s_0)/(\epsilon c^2)}]^{-1/2},\quad c=3/\beta^2$}

For a front written in $s=Z+c\theta$ connecting $0$ at $s=-\infty$ to nonzero $\beta$ at $s=+\infty$, one integration gives $\epsilon c^2q'=q-cq^3/3$. The endpoint condition fixes $c=3/\beta^2$. Setting $w=q^2$ gives a logistic equation and the displayed profile, with the sign of $q$ selected by $\beta$. The propagation speed in the $\theta$ coordinate is $-\beta^2/3$, agreeing with the <Rankine-Hugoniot condition> for flux $-q^3/3$.

= Fay solution
{parent=Viscous Burgers equation}
{c}
{title2=$q=2\delta\sum_{n\ge1}\sin(n\theta)/\sinh(n\delta Z)$}

The Fay solution is a periodic solution of the negative-flux <viscous Burgers equation> for $Z>0$. Its <Fourier series> has exponentially decreasing coefficients at each positive $\delta Z$. Expanding $1/\sinh(ns)=2\sum_{j\ge0}e^{-(2j+1)ns}$ and summing the sine series gives
$$
q=2\delta\sin\theta\sum_{j\ge0}\frac1{\cosh((2j+1)s)-\cos\theta},\qquad s=\delta Z.
$$
For $s\gg1$, the first harmonic gives $q\sim4\delta e^{-s}\sin\theta$. The <Fay shock-layer asymptotics> describe its opposite, small-$s$ regime.

= Fay shock-layer asymptotics
{parent=Fay solution}
{c}
{title2=$q\sim(\pi/Z)\tanh[\pi\theta/(2\delta Z)]$}

For $|\theta|\ll1$ and $s=\delta Z\ll1$, the <Fay solution> becomes $q\sim4\delta\theta\sum_{j\ge0}[((2j+1)s)^2+\theta^2]^{-1}$. The partial-fraction identity for the <hyperbolic tangent> sums this to the displayed smooth shock layer. It resolves the odd transition from $-\pi/Z$ to $+\pi/Z$ on angular thickness $O(\delta Z)$; at $\theta=0$ both sides vanish and the comparison is read through their derivatives.

= Burgers N-wave
{parent=Viscous Burgers equation}
{c}

A localized negative-flux Burgers N-wave has initial profile $-U\theta$ on $|\theta|<L$ and zero outside, for $U,L>0$. The <Cole-Hopf solution for a Burgers N-wave> expresses its viscous evolution in Gaussian interval masses. In the <vanishing-viscosity limit>, the interior slope becomes $-U/(1+UZ)$ and its entropy-shock fronts move to $\pm L\sqrt{1+UZ}$.

= Cole-Hopf solution for a Burgers N-wave
{parent=Burgers N-wave}
{c}

For the negative-flux <viscous Burgers equation>, the <Cole-Hopf transformation> uses $f=2\alpha\partial_\theta\log\psi$. Initial N-wave data give $\psi_0=\exp[U(L^2-\theta^2)/(4\alpha)]$ inside $[-L,L]$ and one outside. Completing the square in the <heat kernel> convolution gives, with $a=1+UZ$,
$$
\psi=1-I_\alpha(\theta,L,Z)+I_\alpha(\theta,La,Za)a^{-1/2}e^{U(L^2-\theta^2/a)/(4\alpha)}.
$$
The <Gaussian interval masses> and their exponentially weighted tails must be treated together when taking the small-diffusion limit.

= Viscous Burgers step solution with negative flux
{parent=Viscous Burgers equation}
{title2=$f=U/(1+Je^{-U(2\theta+UZ)/(4\alpha)})$}

For positive diffusivity, the <Cole-Hopf transformation> with $f=2\alpha\partial_\theta\log\psi$ converts the negative-flux equation into the <heat equation>. Step data give a ratio of two Gaussian-tail integrals $J$. At $\theta=-UZ/2$, $J=1$ and $f=U/2$. Its <vanishing-viscosity limit> selects the shock or <rarefaction wave> in the <Burgers Riemann problem with negative flux>, depending on the sign of $U$.

= Midpoint symmetry of a viscous Burgers step
{parent=Viscous Burgers step solution with negative flux}
{title2=$f(z,-Uz-\theta)=U-f(z,\theta)$}

The <viscous Burgers step solution with negative flux> is symmetric around $\theta=-Uz/2$ after subtracting $U/2$. Its Gaussian-tail ratio equals one precisely at this midpoint, giving $f=U/2$ for either sign of $U$. For $U>0$ this point becomes the entropy-shock center in the <vanishing viscosity approximation>; for $U<0$ it is the center of a <rarefaction wave>. The same trajectory therefore does not by itself identify a <shock>.

= Cole-Hopf transformation
{title2=$u=-2\nu\,\partial_x\log\phi$}
{c}
{parent=Viscous Burgers equation}
{wiki}

The Cole-Hopf transformation $u=-2\nu\phi_x/\phi$ converts the <viscous Burgers equation> into the <heat equation> $\phi_t=\nu\phi_{xx}$. Initial data $u(x,0)=g(x)$ correspond to $\phi(x,0)=C\exp[-(2\nu)^{-1}\int^xg(s)\,ds]$.

= Time normalization in the Cole-Hopf transformation
{parent=Cole-Hopf transformation}
{title2=$\Psi_t-\nu\Psi_{xx}=C(t)\Psi$}

Substituting $u=-2\nu\partial_x\log\Psi$ into the <viscous Burgers equation> gives $\partial_x[(\Psi_t-\nu\Psi_{xx})/\Psi]=0$. Integration permits the arbitrary function $C(t)$ in the title. Multiplication of $\Psi$ by $e^{-\int C(t)dt}$ preserves $u$ and reduces its equation to the <heat equation>. Thus the heat-equation representation uses a permissible time normalization rather than a condition uniquely imposed on every representative $\Psi$.

= Sinusoidal Cole-Hopf solution for negative-flux Burgers flow
{parent=Cole-Hopf transformation}
{title2=$\psi=I_0(a)+2\sum_{n\ge1}(-1)^nI_n(a)e^{-n^2\delta Z}\cos n\theta,\quad a=1/(2\delta)$}

For $q_Z-qq_\theta=\delta q_{\theta\theta}$ and initial data $q=\sin\theta$, the <Cole-Hopf transformation> $q=2\delta\psi_\theta/\psi$ gives $\psi(\theta,0)=\exp[-\cos\theta/(2\delta)]$. The <Fourier series> of this exponential and the <heat equation> give the displayed exact positive diffusion solution. Its first harmonic gives $q\sim4\delta[I_1(a)/I_0(a)]e^{-\delta Z}\sin\theta$. The <large-argument asymptotic expansion of a modified Bessel function> makes the ratio tend to one as $\delta\to0$.

= Finite-exponential Cole-Hopf solution
{parent=Cole-Hopf transformation}
{title2=$q=\frac{\sum_jq_je^{A_j}}{\sum_je^{A_j}},\quad A_j=\frac{q_j(\theta-\theta_j)+q_j^2Z/2}{2\epsilon}$}

For the negative-flux <viscous Burgers equation> $q_Z-qq_\theta=\epsilon q_{\theta\theta}$, set $q=2\epsilon\partial_\theta\log\psi$. A sum of exponentials in the initial heat data evolves into $\psi=\sum_je^{A_j}$, giving the displayed <softmax function> average. Two terms produce a traveling tanh shock from the lower to the higher state, with speed minus their mean.

= Extremal-state asymptotics of exponential Burgers solutions
{parent=Finite-exponential Cole-Hopf solution}
{title2=$s=-(q_1+q_N)/2$}

For ordered states $q_1<\cdots<q_N$, in the moving frame $\theta=sZ+\xi$ the first and last exponents in the <finite-exponential Cole-Hopf solution> have the same leading coefficient. Each interior state's coefficient is smaller by $(q_j-q_1)(q_N-q_j)/(4\epsilon)>0$. Thus the large-$Z$ moving-frame profile is the two-state shock between the extremes. At fixed $\theta$, the endpoint of largest absolute value dominates; equal opposite extremes leave a stationary shock.

= Semilinear partial differential equation
{parent=Partial differential equation}

A semilinear partial differential equation is linear in its highest-order derivatives, with their coefficients depending only on the independent variables; lower-order terms may depend nonlinearly on the solution and its lower derivatives. A <semilinear heat equation> such as $u_t=\Delta u+f(u)$ is an example. This differs from a quasilinear equation, whose highest-order derivative coefficients can depend on the solution or its lower derivatives.

= Obstacle problem
{wiki}
{parent=Partial differential equation}

An obstacle constraint requires a solution to lie above a given function. In an <optimal stopping> problem, the value dominates the payoff, satisfies a generator equation where continuation is optimal, and a generator inequality where stopping is optimal. The complementary conditions can be expressed as a maximum equal to zero.

= Parabolic comparison principle
{parent=Partial differential equation}

A first-contact argument compares a smooth solution with a subsolution or supersolution. At a first spatial minimum of their difference, the Hessian is nonnegative; a strictly favorable time derivative then contradicts contact. For $u_t=u^2u_{xx}+u^3$, a positive decreasing constant-in-space barrier proves that positive periodic initial data stay positive for the lifetime of the smooth solution.

= Invariant interval for a cubic reaction-diffusion equation
{parent=Parabolic comparison principle}
{title2=$|u|\leq\max(\|u_0\|_\infty,\sqrt{\max(\lambda,0)})$}

For $u_t=\Delta u+\lambda u-u^3$ with zero <Dirichlet boundary condition>, choose $K\geq\max(\|u_0\|_\infty,\sqrt{\max(\lambda,0)})$. The constants $K$ and $-K$ are a supersolution and a subsolution. More explicitly, testing with $(u-K)_+$ gives $\frac12\frac d{dt}\|(u-K)_+\|_2^2\leq\lambda\|(u-K)_+\|_2^2$, since the reaction derivative is at most $\lambda$ and the reaction at $K$ is nonpositive. The <Gronwall inequality> and the corresponding argument for $-u$ prove that $[-K,K]$ is invariant. A local <variation-of-constants formula> in the supremum norm then has a lifespan depending only on $K$, which prevents finite-time loss of the <classical solution>.

= Domain of dependence
{parent=Partial differential equation}

The domain of dependence of a solution value consists of the initial and boundary locations whose data can influence it. For $u_t+a u_x=0$ on the whole line, $u(x,t)=u(x-at,0)$, so its initial-data domain of dependence is the foot of the characteristic. For a finite-difference scheme, repeated stencil dependencies determine a numerical domain of dependence. The <Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy condition> requires the numerical domain to cover the physical one for convergence; it is necessary under its usual hyperbolic hypotheses, but not in general sufficient for <stability>.

= Linear partial differential equation
{parent=Partial differential equation}

A linear partial differential equation is a <partial differential equation> in which the unknown function and its <partial derivatives> occur linearly, with coefficients independent of the unknown. Its homogeneous solutions obey the <superposition principle>.

For example, the <heat equation> permits adding independently decaying <Fourier modes>. For an equation $Lu=f$ with a fixed nonzero <PDE source term>, adding two solutions gives $L(u+v)=2f$, so the sum generally solves a different problem. Likewise, adding solutions adds their <initial conditions> and <boundary conditions>.

An ill-posed problem fails existence, uniqueness or continuous dependence in the specified function spaces. Backward <heat equation> evolution is unstable because its <Fourier modes> grow like $e^{t|\xi|^2}$, making arbitrarily small high-frequency errors grow without a uniform bound.

A radial function depends only on the distance from the origin: $f(x)=g(|x|)$. Differential operators then reduce to radial formulas such as $\Delta f=g''+(n-1)g'/r$.

= Periodic domain
{parent=Partial differential equation}

A <periodic domain> identifies opposite faces of a fundamental cell, producing a <flat torus>. On an <interval> $[0,L]$, smooth <periodic boundary conditions> require $u(0,t)=u(L,t)$ and matching spatial <derivatives>. These endpoints are one location, rather than two independently controlled walls. Periodic <integration by parts> has no boundary term, and zero-mean <functions> have no zero <Fourier mode>.

= Energy estimate
{parent=Partial differential equation}
{wiki}

An energy estimate tests a differential equation against the solution or one of its derivatives to control a norm by initial data and forcing. Coercive terms represent dissipation, while <Young inequality> and the <Gronwall inequality> control lower-order terms.

For the difference $w$ of two smooth <heat equation> solutions with $D>0$, identical <PDE source term>, <initial conditions>, and either <periodic boundary conditions> or <Dirichlet boundary data>, <integration by parts> gives
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int w^2\,dx=-2D\int w_x^2\,dx\leq0.
$$
The initially zero nonnegative <integral> stays zero, proving uniqueness. The same computation bounds changes in the <L2 norm> when the <initial conditions> differ. Specifying data and a solution class makes this argument meaningful.https://web.stanford.edu/class/math220b/handouts/heateqn.pdf[Stanford Math 220B heat-equation notes, section 2.8.3]{ref}

= Bootstrap argument
{parent=Energy estimate}

A bootstrap argument assumes an a priori bound on an initial time interval and derives a strict improvement using an <energy estimate> or another inequality. <Continuity> extends the improved bound to the entire interval under consideration. For example $A(t)\leq C\varepsilon+C\int_0^t(1+s)^{-1}A(s)^2\,ds$ closes $A\leq2C\varepsilon$ when $\varepsilon\log(1+t)$ is sufficiently small. The strict improvement is what prevents a first failure time.

= Wave energy estimate
{parent=Energy estimate}
{title2=$\|\partial u(t)\|_2\leq\|\partial u(0)\|_2+\int_0^t\|F(s)\|_2\,ds$}

If $\Box u=F$ with <d'Alembert operator> $\Box=-\partial_t^2+\Delta$, then $\|\partial u(t)\|_2\leq\|\partial u(0)\|_2+\int_0^t\|F(s)\|_2\,ds$, where $\partial u=(u_t,\nabla u)$. Test the <wave equation> against $u_t$, use <integration by parts>, and apply the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. The same <energy estimate> applies to a finite system of <wave equations>.

= Local wave energy estimate
{parent=Wave energy estimate}
{title2=$E_{B_r}(T)\leq E_{B_{r+T}}(0)$}

For the unit-speed <wave equation>, the integral of $(u_t^2+|\nabla u|^2)/2$ over $B_r(x_0)$ at time $T\geq0$ is at most its initial integral over $B_{r+T}(x_0)$. On a shrinking ball the outward energy flux minus the loss from its moving boundary is $-((u_t-\partial_nu)^2+|\nabla_{\mathrm{tan}}u|^2)/2$, hence nonpositive. This estimate establishes the <domain of dependence> and <finite propagation speed> without any assumptions at spatial infinity.

= Hole-filling argument
{parent=Energy estimate}
{title2=$E(R)\leq\frac{C}{1+C}E(2R)$}

An estimate $E(R)\leq C(E(2R)-E(R))$ bounds the energy in a ball by the energy in the surrounding <annulus>. Moving the smaller-ball energy to the left gives the strict contraction $E(R)\leq\theta E(2R)$ with $\theta=C/(1+C)<1$. Iteration turns this contraction into decay at small scales.

= Dyadic energy decay
{parent=Hole-filling argument}
{title2=$E(r)\leq C(r/R)^\mu E(R)$}

If $E(R/2)\leq\theta E(R)$ and $E$ is nondecreasing, then $E(2^{-k}R)\leq\theta^kE(R)$. Between dyadic radii, monotonicity gives $E(r)\leq2^\mu(r/R)^\mu E(R)$ for any $0<\mu\leq-\log_2\theta$. This transfers a discrete contraction into a uniform power bound.

= Signed power test for a Laplacian eigenfunction
{parent=Energy estimate}
{title2=$(\gamma-1)\int|u|^{\gamma-2}|Du|^2=\lambda\int|u|^\gamma$}

For a smooth <Dirichlet Laplacian eigenfunction>, testing $-\Delta u=\lambda u$ with $|u|^{\gamma-2}u$ for $\gamma\geq2$ gives the displayed <energy estimate>. The <Sobolev chain rule> for $v=|u|^{\gamma/2}$ then yields $\int|Dv|^2=\lambda\gamma^2/[4(\gamma-1)]\int|u|^\gamma$. The absolute value and the sign in the test function permit nodal changes. At $\gamma=2$, use the Lipschitz absolute-value chain rule and the fact that a <gradient of a Sobolev function vanishes on a level set>.

= Caccioppoli inequality
{c}
{parent=Energy estimate}
{wiki}

A Caccioppoli inequality is a local energy estimate obtained by testing an elliptic equation with a cutoff times the solution. It controls the gradient on a smaller ball by the function and forcing on a larger ball.

= Caccioppoli inequality with bounded lower-order terms
{c}
{parent=Caccioppoli inequality}
{title2=$\|u\|_{H^1(U)}\leq C(\|u\|_{L^2(V)}+\|f\|_{L^2(V)})$}

For $\operatorname{div}(A Du)+b\cdot Du+cu=f$ with bounded measurable coefficients and uniformly elliptic $A$, testing with $\eta^2u$ and using <Young inequality> gives $\int\eta^2|Du|^2\leq C\int(1+|D\eta|^2)u^2+C\int\eta^2f^2$. No sign condition on $c$ is needed for this interior estimate. A cutoff equal to one on $U$ and supported in $V$ yields the displayed bound; the constant depends only on dimension, ellipticity, coefficient bounds and the separation of $U$ from the boundary of $V$.

= Strong local Sobolev compactness for a fixed elliptic equation
{parent=Caccioppoli inequality with bounded lower-order terms}
{title2=$u_k\longrightarrow u\text{ in }H^1_{\mathrm{loc}}$}

Solutions of one fixed bounded-coefficient uniformly elliptic equation $Lu=f$ that are bounded in global $L^2$ have a subsequence converging strongly in $H^1$ on every relatively compact subdomain. Local energy bounds and the <Rellich-Kondrachov compactness theorem> first give local strong $L^2$ and weak $H^1$ convergence on a smooth exhaustion. Pass to the weak equation to identify the limit. Differences solve $L(u_k-u)=0$, so the interior <Caccioppoli inequality> upgrades the local strong $L^2$ convergence to strong gradient convergence. Global weak $L^2$ convergence preserves the original global bound.

= Annular Caccioppoli inequality
{parent=Caccioppoli inequality}
{title2=$\int_{B_R}|\nabla u|^2\leq CR^{-2}\int_{B_{2R}\setminus B_R}|u-c|^2$}

= Caccioppoli inequality with an annular right side
{synonym}
{c}

For a <weak solution> of a <uniformly elliptic> homogeneous <divergence-form elliptic operator>, testing with $\eta^2(u-c)$ and choosing the <gradient> of the <cutoff function> in $B_{2R}\setminus B_R$ gives $\int_{B_R}|\nabla u|^2\leq CR^{-2}\int_{B_{2R}\setminus B_R}|u-c|^2$. The arbitrary constant $c$ can be chosen as an annular average, which makes the estimate suitable for a <hole-filling argument>.

= Power Caccioppoli inequality
{parent=Caccioppoli inequality}
{title2=$\int |Du|^2u^{\beta-2}\eta^2\leq\frac{C}{(\beta-1)^2}\int u^\beta|D\eta|^2$}

For a nonnegative bounded <weak subsolution> and $\beta>1$, test with $\eta^2(u+\varepsilon)^{\beta-1}$. Uniform ellipticity and <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> give $\int |Du|^2u^{\beta-2}\eta^2\leq4\Lambda_*^2/[\lambda^2(\beta-1)^2]\int u^\beta|D\eta|^2$, with $\Lambda_*$ an <operator norm> bound for the coefficient <matrix>. The <Sobolev chain rule> and $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ justify fractional powers and zero values. An entrywise bound $\Lambda$ yields $\Lambda_*\leq n\Lambda$.

= Uniform power gain for elliptic solutions
{parent=Power Caccioppoli inequality}
{title2=$\|u\|_{q\alpha,r_1}\le[D_\ell R/(r_2-r_1)]^{2/q}\|u\|_{q,r_2}$}

For a nonnegative $H^1$ <weak solution> of a <homogeneous divergence-form elliptic equation> on $B_1\subset\mathbb R^\ell$, $\ell\ge2$, with symmetric measurable $\lambda I\le A\le\Lambda I$, set $R=\Lambda/\lambda$ and $\alpha=1+1/\ell$. There is $D_\ell\ge1$ independent of $q\ge2$ such that
$$
\|u\|_{L^{q\alpha}(B_{r_1})}\le\left(\frac{D_\ell R}{r_2-r_1}\right)^{2/q}\|u\|_{L^q(B_{r_2})},\qquad0<r_1<r_2\le1.
$$
Test the equation with a cutoff times $u^{q-1}$, first regularized or truncated, to get $\int\zeta^2|\nabla u^{q/2}|^2\le[q/(q-1)]^2R^2\int u^q|\nabla\zeta|^2$. Apply a fixed-domain <Sobolev embedding theorem> to $\zeta u^{q/2}$ and take the $2/q$ power. Keeping the constant inside that power is crucial for <Moser iteration>.

= Logarithmic Caccioppoli inequality
{c}
{parent=Caccioppoli inequality}

For a positive weak supersolution $u$ of a uniformly elliptic divergence-form equation, testing with $\eta^2/u$ gives
$$
\int\eta^2|D\log u|^2\leq C\int|D\eta|^2.
$$
The constant depends only on the ellipticity bounds.

= Second-order partial differential equation
{parent=Partial differential equation}

A second-order partial differential equation contains derivatives of the unknown function of order at most two and at least one second derivative. Its <principal symbol> determines its characteristic directions and elliptic, parabolic or hyperbolic type.

= Quasilinear partial differential equation
{parent=Partial differential equation}
{wiki}

A quasilinear partial differential equation is linear in its highest-order derivatives, while its coefficients may depend on the independent variables, the unknown function, and lower-order derivatives.

= Averaged linearization of a nonlinear divergence-form equation
{parent=Quasilinear partial differential equation}
{title2=$A_h=\int_0^1DF((1-t)p_-+tp_+)\,dt$}

For a $C^1$ flux $F$ and a <weak solution> of $\operatorname{div}F(Du)=0$, <discrete integration by parts> and the <fundamental theorem of calculus along a line segment> show that $w_h=\delta_{\ell,-h}u$ solves $\operatorname{div}(A_hDw_h)=0$, with $p_-=Du(x-he_\ell)$ and $p_+=Du(x)$. Coercive quadratic-form bounds and <operator norm> bounds for $DF$ on all segments between these <gradients> pass to $A_h$. This produces a <divergence-form elliptic operator> without initially assuming second <partial derivatives> of $u$.

= p-Laplacian

The p-Laplacian is the nonlinear divergence-form operator

\Delta_pu=\operatorname{div}(|Du|^{p-2}Du).

It is the <Euler-Lagrange equation>[Euler-Lagrange operator] of the <p-energy>. For $p>2$ it is <degenerate ellipticity>[degenerately elliptic] where $Du=0$.

= Principal symbol of a partial differential equation
{title2=$p(x,\xi)$}
{parent=Quasilinear partial differential equation}

= Principal symbol
{synonym}

For a scalar differential operator of order $k$, the principal symbol replaces each order-$k$ derivative $\partial^\alpha$ by $\xi^\alpha$ and discards lower-order terms. For a <quasilinear partial differential equation>, the coefficients are evaluated at the prescribed lower-order data.

= Characteristic hypersurface
{parent=Principal symbol of a partial differential equation}

A <hypersurface> $\Sigma=\{\phi=0\}$ is characteristic for a differential equation at $x\in\Sigma$ when its <principal symbol> vanishes on the conormal $d\phi(x)$:
$$
p(x,d\phi(x))=0.
$$
It is non-characteristic where this quantity is nonzero.

= Characteristic plane
{parent=Characteristic hypersurface}

A characteristic plane is a planar <characteristic hypersurface>. For $u_{zz}-m^2u_{xx}=0$, the planes $x\pm mz=\text{constant}$ carry the two travelling-wave families.

= Non-characteristic hypersurface
{parent=Characteristic hypersurface}

= Non-characteristic
{synonym}

A non-characteristic hypersurface satisfies $p(x,d\phi(x))\ne0$. This lets the equation solve for the highest derivative normal to the hypersurface.

= Characteristic coordinate
{parent=Characteristic hypersurface}

A characteristic coordinate is a function constant along one family of <characteristic hypersurface>[characteristics]. For a second-order equation in two variables, two independent characteristic coordinates reduce the principal part to a mixed derivative.

= Expanding-flow transformation of a wave equation
{parent=Characteristic coordinate}
{title2=$X=\rho e^{-t},\quad S=e^{-t}$}

The change of variables $X=\rho e^{-t}$, $S=e^{-t}>0$ turns
$$
u_{tt}-\partial_\rho((1-\rho^2)u_\rho)+\partial_\rho(\rho u_t)+\rho u_{\rho t}=0
$$
into $w_{SS}-w_{XX}=0$. Indeed, with $D=\partial_t+\rho\partial_\rho$, the original operator is $D^2+D-\partial_\rho^2$, while $D=-S\partial_S$ and $\partial_\rho=S\partial_X$. Its <characteristic curves> are $\rho=-1+Ce^t$ and $\rho=1+Ce^t$, including the characteristic lines $\rho=\pm1$. General smooth solutions have the form $F(e^{-t}(\rho+1))+G(e^{-t}(\rho-1))$. Zero <Cauchy data> on $-1<\rho<1$ at $t=0$ force vanishing throughout that strip for $t\geq0$, but not for all negative time: $u=(e^{-t}(\rho+1)-2)_+^3$ is a global $C^2$ counterexample. Thus the characteristic barriers describe a forward domain of dependence, not a barrier in both time directions.

= Hyperbolic partial differential equation
{parent=Principal symbol of a partial differential equation}
{wiki}

A scalar second-order equation
$$
Au_{tt}+2Bu_{tx}+Cu_{xx}+\text{lower-order terms}=0
$$
is hyperbolic where $B^2-AC>0$. Equivalently, its <principal symbol> has two distinct real characteristic directions.

= Hyperbolicity
{parent=Hyperbolic partial differential equation}

For a one-dimensional first-order system $v_t+A(v,x,t)v_x=s$, hyperbolicity means that the coefficient <matrix> has real <eigenvalues> and a complete set of <eigenvectors>. Distinct real eigenvalues give strict hyperbolicity and separate <characteristic curves>. Along a curve of speed $\lambda$, a left <eigenvector> $\ell A=\lambda\ell$ gives the compatibility equation $\ell\,dv/dt=\ell s$.

= Travel-time coordinate for a one-dimensional variable-speed wave equation
{parent=Hyperbolic partial differential equation}

For a one-dimensional <wave equation> whose <principal symbol> is $\xi_t^2-c(y)^2\xi_y^2$, a travel-time coordinate is
$$
s(y)=\int^y\frac{dr}{c(r)}.
$$
Its two families of <characteristic curves> are $s(y)-t=\text{constant}$ and $s(y)+t=\text{constant}$ wherever the <wave speed> $c$ is positive.

= Characteristic curves for speed one minus y squared
{title2=$dy/dx=\pm(1-y^2)$}
{parent=Travel-time coordinate for a one-dimensional variable-speed wave equation}

For $c(y)=1-y^2$ in the strip $|y|<1$, the travel-time coordinate is $s(y)=\operatorname{artanh}y$. The <characteristic curves> are
$$
\operatorname{artanh}y\pm x=\text{constant},
$$
equivalently $y=\tanh(C\mp x)$. Outside the strip the same separated <ordinary differential equation> gives <hyperbolic cotangent> branches, and the equilibrium curves $y=\pm1$ are also characteristic.

= Cauchy problem
{c}
{parent=Partial differential equation}
{wiki}

= Cauchy problem for a partial differential equation
{synonym}

A Cauchy problem prescribes a solution and enough of its derivatives on a hypersurface to determine local evolution away from that hypersurface.

= Noncharacteristic Cauchy data
{c}
{parent=Cauchy problem}
{title2=$a\eta'-b\xi'\ne0$}

For $au_{x_1}+bu_{x_2}=c$ with data along $(\xi(s),\eta(s))$, the initial curve is noncharacteristic when its tangent is linearly independent of the projected characteristic vector $(a,b)$ evaluated at the initial value of $u$. The determinant is $a\eta'-b\xi'$, up to sign. For $C^1$ coefficients and data this permits the <inverse function theorem> to invert the local characteristic parametrization and gives a unique local <classical solution>.

= Continuous dependence on initial data
{parent=Cauchy problem}

Continuous dependence means that convergence of the initial data in a specified topology implies convergence of the corresponding solutions in the specified solution topology. It is a stability requirement, not merely existence or uniqueness. <Smooth-data instability of the Cauchy-Riemann Cauchy problem> gives a counterexample even when the initial data converge uniformly with every fixed number of derivatives.

= Cauchy data
{parent=Cauchy problem}

For a scalar equation of order $k$, Cauchy data consist of the first $k$ normal jets $u,\partial_Nu,\ldots,\partial_N^{k-1}u$ on the initial hypersurface.

These prescribed values define the initial conditions of a <Cauchy problem>.

= Boundary jet
{parent=Cauchy data}
{title2=$j^{m-1}u|_{\partial\Omega}$}

The boundary jet through order $m-1$ is the collection of traces $\partial^\alpha u|_{\partial\Omega}$ with $|\alpha|\le m-1$. Vanishing of the whole jet is stronger than vanishing of one <derivative>. On a smooth <hypersurface>, zero normal Cauchy <derivatives> through order $m-1$, together with their tangential <derivatives>, give a zero full boundary jet.

= Cauchy-Kovalevskaya theorem
{c}
{parent=Cauchy problem}
{wiki=Cauchy–Kovalevskaya_theorem}

For an order-$k$ scalar <quasilinear partial differential equation> with real-analytic coefficients, real-analytic <Cauchy data> on a real-analytic <non-characteristic hypersurface> determine a unique real-analytic solution in a neighbourhood of each point of that hypersurface.

= Holmgren uniqueness theorem
{c}
{parent=Cauchy-Kovalevskaya theorem}

For a linear differential operator with <real analytic> coefficients, a classical solution with zero <Cauchy data> on a <real analytic> <non-characteristic hypersurface> vanishes locally near that <hypersurface>. The solution itself need not be <real analytic>. A proof solves the <real analytic> inhomogeneous <formal adjoint> problem against <polynomial> sources, annihilates all <polynomial> moments using boundary jets, and uses <polynomial> density to force the solution to vanish.

= Parabolic-cap proof of Holmgren uniqueness
{parent=Holmgren uniqueness theorem}

Near a non-characteristic plane $x=0$, use the small cap $\omega_\varepsilon=\{x>0,\ x+y^2<\varepsilon\}$. Its curved face is non-characteristic by continuity of the <principal symbol>. A <polynomial> coordinate change flattens that face. Uniform <real analytic> coefficient bounds and the <uniform Cauchy radius for polynomial forcing> solve $P^*v=g$ throughout a common cap, with zero <boundary jet> on the curved face. The <complete boundary-jet condition for formal adjoints> then gives $\int_{\omega_\varepsilon}ug=0$ for every <polynomial> $g$. Uniform approximation of $\bar u$ gives $\int|u|^2=0$. Repeating on the other side of the plane proves two-sided local uniqueness.

= Uniform Cauchy radius for polynomial forcing
{parent=Cauchy-Kovalevskaya theorem}

For a linear <real analytic> non-characteristic <Cauchy problem for a partial differential equation> with zero data, the local <real analytic> radius may be chosen uniformly over <polynomial> forcing terms. Fix an analyticity exponent $R^{-1}$. Each <polynomial>, including a <polynomial> obtained by a <polynomial> coordinate change, satisfies the corresponding factorial <derivative> bound with some finite prefactor, uniformly over a compact parameter range. Divide the forcing by that prefactor to put all such data in one fixed majorant class. The <Cauchy-Kovalevskaya theorem> supplies a common radius, and linearity rescales the solution back without shrinking it. This argument does not bound the amplitudes of the resulting solutions uniformly.

= General-order Cauchy-Kovalevskaya theorem
{parent=Cauchy-Kovalevskaya theorem}

A <real analytic> system solved for the highest <normal derivatives>, with <real analytic> <Cauchy data>, has a unique local <real analytic> solution. For common order $m$, its normal form is $\partial_t^mU=F(t,x,\{\partial_t^j\partial_x^\alpha U:j<m,\ j+|\alpha|\le m\})$. An invertible coefficient/Jacobian matrix for the highest <normal derivatives> is the system's <non-characteristic hypersurface> condition. A <real analytic> coordinate change flattens the initial <hypersurface>, and adjoining <derivatives> through order $m-1$ reduces the problem to a first-order <real analytic> system. Uniqueness here is in the <real analytic> class; it does not assert <Hadamard well-posedness> in smooth or Sobolev <norms>.

= Analytic Goursat problem for a Klein--Gordon equation
{c}
{parent=Cauchy-Kovalevskaya theorem}

In null coordinates, the characteristic boundary problem
$$
u_{\xi\eta}=-\frac14u,\qquad
u(\xi,0)=a(\xi),\qquad
u(0,\eta)=b(\eta)
$$
is a Volterra integral equation. Compatible analytic boundary data determine a unique analytic solution.

= Global continuation for the analytic Goursat problem
{c}
{parent=Analytic Goursat problem for a Klein--Gordon equation}

On a compact characteristic rectangle, the Volterra iterates for the analytic Goursat problem are bounded by $C^m/(m!)^2$. Their series and differentiated series converge throughout the rectangle, extending the local analytic solution globally across it.

= Solution of a partial differential equation
{parent=Partial differential equation}

A solution of a partial differential equation is a function satisfying the equation in a specified sense and obeying its initial or boundary data.

= Classical solution
{parent=Solution of a partial differential equation}

A <classical solution> has enough ordinary <derivatives> for the <differential equation> and its data to hold pointwise. For the <heat equation>, $u_t$ and $u_{xx}$ should be continuous in the interior, with the specified initial and boundary traces. A <weak solution> instead imposes an integrated identity, allowing less regular data; uniqueness must always be stated for the solution class used.

= Positive-time smoothing for a semilinear heat equation
{parent=Classical solution}

For a <semilinear partial differential equation> $u_t=\Delta u+F(u)$ with smooth $F$ and smooth fixed boundary data, local boundedness permits positive-time <elliptic regularity> and parabolic smoothing. If $u_t$ is continuous with values in a <Hölder space>, elliptic estimates first give continuous $C^{2,\beta}$ spatial regularity for $u$. Time difference quotients then solve a linear parabolic equation with coefficient converging to $F'(u)$; smoothing away from the initial time gives $u_t\in C(C^2)$, hence $u\in C^1(C^2)$. It is essential to gain spatial regularity for the time derivative too; $u\in C(C^2)\cap C^1(C^0)$ alone does not imply $C^1(C^2)$.

= Bounded parabolic differentiability class
{parent=Classical solution}
{title2=$C_b^{1,2}$}

The notation $C_b^{1,2}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb R)$ ordinarily denotes functions with continuous time derivative and two continuous space derivatives, with the function and indicated derivatives bounded globally. A different condition, often intended in finite-horizon <partial differential equations>, is membership in $C_b^{1,2}([0,T]\times\mathbb R)$ for each finite $T$, allowing bounds to grow with $T$. These conventions must be distinguished in a long-time growth problem: the second permits divergence with time; the first does not. There is no universal notation for the finite-horizon condition, so it should be stated explicitly.

= Weak solution
{parent=Solution of a partial differential equation}
{wiki}

= Distributional weak solution
{synonym}

A weak solution satisfies an integrated identity in which derivatives are transferred to test functions. It can therefore exist with fewer classical derivatives.

= Viscosity solution
{parent=Weak solution}
{wiki}

For a <partial differential equation> $F(x,u,Du,D^2u)=0$ whose operator is nonincreasing in the Hessian variable, a continuous viscosity subsolution satisfies $F(x_0,u(x_0),D\phi(x_0),D^2\phi(x_0))\leq0$ whenever a smooth <test function> $\phi$ touches it from above. A viscosity supersolution satisfies the reverse inequality for tests touching from below. A viscosity solution satisfies both conditions. For $F=-\operatorname{tr}D^2u$, these conditions are respectively $\Delta\phi\geq0$ and $\Delta\phi\leq0$. They make sense even when $u$ has no classical second derivatives.

= Continuous viscosity harmonic functions are classical
{parent=Viscosity solution}
{title2=$\Delta u=0$}

Let a continuous function satisfy both viscosity inequalities for the <Laplace operator>. On any relatively compact ball, take its <harmonic replacement> $h$ with matching boundary values. If $u-h$ has a positive maximum, the function $h+\varepsilon(R^2-|x-a|^2)$ still lies below $u$ somewhere for sufficiently small positive $\varepsilon$, while matching it on the boundary. At an interior maximum of their difference it is a valid upper test, but its Laplacian is $-2n\varepsilon<0$, a contradiction. The opposite perturbation excludes a negative minimum. Thus $u=h$ throughout the ball and is a smooth <harmonic function>. A locally defined test is extended near its touching point using a smooth <cutoff function> when global tests are required.

= Weak-strong uniqueness principle
{parent=Weak solution}

A weak-strong uniqueness principle says that a <weak solution> with the same data as a sufficiently regular <classical solution> agrees with that solution throughout its interval of regular existence, within the specified weak class. The conclusion is class-dependent: all bounded weak solutions of a prescribed $C^1$ linear transport field satisfy it, while nonlinear conservation laws require an admissibility condition such as an <entropy solution> condition.

= Weak energy solution of a variable-coefficient wave equation
{parent=Weak solution}

For $u_{tt}+L(t)u=f$ with homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions>, smooth uniformly elliptic principal coefficients, and initial data $(\psi_0,\psi_1)\in H_0^1(U)\times L^2(U)$, a space-time $H^1$ <weak solution> has zero lateral trace, trace $u(0)=\psi_0$, and satisfies
$$
\int_0^T\bigl[-(u_t,v_t)_{L^2}+B_t[u,v]\bigr]dt
=\int_0^T(f,v)_{L^2}dt+(\psi_1,v(0))_{L^2}
$$
for every space-time $H^1$ test function with zero lateral trace and $v(T)=0$. The velocity initial condition is encoded by the last term; an arbitrary space-time $H^1$ function need not have an $L^2$ trace of its time derivative. The equation itself gives $u_{tt}\in L^2(0,T;H^{-1}(U))$, so $u_t$ has a continuous $H^{-1}$ representative.

= Weak formulation
{parent=Weak solution}
{wiki}

A weak formulation multiplies a differential equation by a <test function>, integrates, and uses <integration by parts> to move derivatives away from the unknown function. Natural boundary conditions appear in the resulting boundary terms.

= Galerkin method
{c}
{parent=Weak solution}
{wiki}

The Galerkin method seeks an approximate solution in a finite-dimensional subspace and requires the equation's residual to be orthogonal to that subspace. Uniform energy estimates and compactness can then produce a <weak solution> as the subspaces become dense.

= Petrov-Galerkin method
{c}
{parent=Galerkin method}
{wiki=Petrov–Galerkin_method}
{title2=$a(u_h,v_h)=\ell(v_h)\quad(v_h\in W_h)$}

The <Petrov-Galerkin method> chooses an approximate solution $u_h$ in a finite-dimensional trial <vector space> $V_h$ and tests a <weak formulation> against a possibly different test space $W_h$. For a <bilinear form> $a$ and a <linear functional> $\ell$, it imposes $a(u_h,v_h)=\ell(v_h)$ for every $v_h\in W_h$. Taking $W_h=V_h$ recovers the usual <Galerkin method>. Different test functions can alter stability and accuracy; the <Galerkin orthogonality> is then against the test space and need not be an <orthogonal projection> in an <energy norm>.

= Fourier truncation
{parent=Galerkin method}
{c}
{title2=$u_N=\sum_{k\in K_N}a_k(t)\phi_k$}

A <Fourier truncation> retains a finite set of boundary-compatible <Fourier modes> and projects the PDE onto their span by the Galerkin method. Nonlinear products can generate unretained harmonics, so their coefficients are discarded by projection rather than declared zero pointwise. Agreement with a perturbation expansion through cubic order requires retention of every quadratically forced mode that feeds back on the critical mode at cubic order.

= Aubin-Lions lemma
{c}
{parent=Galerkin method}
{wiki}

If $X_0$ embeds compactly into $X$ and $X$ embeds continuously into $X_1$, then boundedness in $L^p(0,T;X_0)$ together with a suitable time-derivative bound in $L^q(0,T;X_1)$ makes a family relatively compact in $L^p(0,T;X)$. A standard case is
$$
L^2(0,T;H^1)\cap H^1(0,T;H^{-1})Subset L^2(0,T;L^2).
$$

= Weak continuity from evolution-space bounds
{parent=Aubin-Lions lemma}

If $u\in L^\infty(0,T;H)$ and $u_t\in L^q(0,T;V')$ for a dense continuous embedding $V\hookrightarrow H$ and some $q>1$, then $u$ has a representative in $C([0,T];H_{\rm weak})$. Indeed, its pairing with each element of a dense subset of $V$ is absolutely continuous, and the uniform $H$ bound extends continuity to every element of $H$.

= Strong continuity from weak continuity and an energy equality
{parent=Weak continuity from evolution-space bounds}

If $u:[0,T]\to H$ is weakly continuous and an energy equality makes $t\mapsto\|u(t)\|_H$ continuous, then $u$ is strongly continuous. This follows from the <Radon-Riesz theorem> applied whenever $t_n\to t$.

= Helmholtz equation
{title2=$\Delta u+k^2u=0$}
{parent=Partial differential equation}
{c}
{wiki}

The Helmholtz equation is the constant-coefficient elliptic equation
$$
\Delta u+k^2u=0.
$$
On a bounded domain, nonzero Dirichlet solutions occur when $k^2$ is an eigenvalue of the negative Laplacian.

= Radial Dirichlet modes of a spherical cavity
{parent=Helmholtz equation}
{title2=$\psi_n(r)\propto\sin(n\pi r/a)/r,\quad k_n^2=n^2\pi^2/a^2$}

A bounded spherically symmetric solution of the <Helmholtz equation> in a ball with zero value at radius $a$ satisfies $-(r^2\psi')'=k^2r^2\psi$. The substitution $u=r\psi$ gives $u''+k^2u=0$ with $u(0)=u(a)=0$, so the displayed modes have $n\geq1$; the apparent singularity at zero is removable. Their <Rayleigh quotient> is $\int_0^ar^2\psi'^2dr/\int_0^ar^2\psi^2dr$. The trial profile $1-r^2/a^2$ gives the upper bound $21/(2a^2)$, compared with the exact lowest value $\pi^2/a^2$. Boundedness at zero eliminates the $1/r$ solution and makes the endpoint term in the <Sturm-Liouville problem> vanish.

= Dirichlet and Neumann spectra of a square
{parent=Helmholtz equation}
{c}
{title2=$\lambda_{jk}=j^2+k^2\quad\text{on }[0,\pi]^2$}

For $-\Delta\Phi=\lambda\Phi$ on the square, <separation of variables> gives <Dirichlet eigenfunctions> $\sin(jx)\sin(ky)$ with $j,k\geq1$, and <Neumann eigenfunctions> $\cos(jx)\cos(ky)$ with $j,k\geq0$. The <eigenvalues> in both cases are $j^2+k^2$, with multiplicity equal to the number of ordered admissible pairs. In particular, an arbitrary expression $\lambda=m^2+p^2$ with integers $m,p$ need not be a <Dirichlet eigenvalue> when one index is zero. The Neumann zero eigenvalue has the constant eigenfunction, whereas the Dirichlet zero problem has only the zero solution by <Dirichlet uniqueness for the Poisson equation>.

= Helmholtz Green-function reciprocity
{c}
{parent=Helmholtz equation}
{title2=$G(\mathbf r_1,\mathbf r_2)=G(\mathbf r_2,\mathbf r_1)$}

Two <Green functions> of the same <Helmholtz equation> with the same homogeneous <Robin boundary condition> satisfy the displayed reciprocity by <Green's second identity>. It is a bilinear identity without complex conjugation, so it concerns the transpose rather than the Hermitian adjoint. In an exterior problem both kernels use the same radiation convention.

= Discrete Helmholtz resonance
{parent=Helmholtz equation}
{title2=$\lambda_{pq}=4h^{-2}[\sin^2(p\pi h/2)+\sin^2(q\pi h/2)]$}

On a square Dirichlet grid with spacing $h=1/J$ and interior indices $1,\ldots,J-1$, the <five-point Laplacian> is diagonalized by products of the sine modes used in the <discrete sine transform>. The discrete equation $(\Delta_h+\lambda I)U=F$ is nonsingular exactly when $\lambda$ is not one of the displayed positive <eigenvalues> of $-\Delta_h$, with $1\leq p,q\leq J-1$. Values below the first resonance are sufficient but not necessary for invertibility.

= Dirichlet-to-Neumann map for a Helmholtz half-space
{c}
{parent=Helmholtz equation}
{title2=$\partial_z\mathcal E g\big|_0=i\mathcal B g$}

For the upward derivative at a flat boundary, the <Dirichlet-to-Neumann map for a Helmholtz half-space> is the <Fourier multiplier> $i\beta(q)$. The outward normal of the upper half-space points downward and has the opposite sign. Its evanescent multiplier grows like $|q|$, explaining why fine roughness matters even when the height is small.

= Outgoing Green function for the three-dimensional Helmholtz equation
{title2=$G_k(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')=e^{ik|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}/(4\pi|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|)$}
{parent=Helmholtz equation}

The outgoing <Green function> satisfies $(\Delta+k^2)G_k=-\delta_{\mathbf r'}$ and the <Sommerfeld radiation condition>. For time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$, its radial phase travels outward. Away from its source it solves the homogeneous <Helmholtz equation>. At large $r$, $G_k=e^{ikr}e^{-ik\widehat{\mathbf r}\cdot\mathbf r'}/(4\pi r)+O(r^{-2})$.

= Neumann representation for a radiating Helmholtz half-space
{parent=Outgoing Green function for the three-dimensional Helmholtz equation}
{c}
{title2=$u(x)=-\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R^2}\partial_3u(y',0)\frac{e^{ik|x-(y',0)|}}{|x-(y',0)|}\,d^2y'$}

Let $(\Delta+k^2)u=0$ for $x_3>0$, with real $k>0$, $u=O(r^{-1})$ and $r(\partial_ru-iku)\to0$ uniformly in direction. With sufficient boundary regularity, <Green's second identity> on an expanding half-ball gives the title formula. The plane integral is interpreted as the limit over expanding disks when it is not absolutely convergent. Its sign uses the upward derivative $\partial_3u$; the outward <normal derivative> is $-\partial_3u$.

For $G=e^{ik|x-y|}/(4\pi|x-y|)$, $G=O(r^{-1})$ and $(\partial_r-ik)G=O(r^{-2})$ at large $|y|=r$. The hemispherical integrand can be written $u(\partial_r-ik)G-G(\partial_r-ik)u$, whose integral tends to zero by the <Sommerfeld radiation condition>. Add the boundary identities for a source point $x$ and its reflected point $x^*=(x_1,x_2,-x_3)$. On the plane $G(x,y)=G(x^*,y)$ and their normal derivatives are opposite. The terms involving boundary values of $u$ cancel, leaving twice $G\partial_3u$ and hence the title formula. In particular, zero <Neumann boundary data> imply $u=0$ in this radiating class.

= Radial Fourier representation of the Helmholtz Green function
{parent=Outgoing Green function for the three-dimensional Helmholtz equation}
{title2=$G_+(l)=\frac1{2\pi^2l}\int_0^\infty\frac{k\sin(kl)}{k^2-k_0^2-i0}\,dk$}

For $k_0>0$, $l=|r-r'|>0$ and the convention $(\Delta+k_0^2)G=-\delta$, the spatial <Fourier transform> gives the multiplier $(k^2-k_0^2)^{-1}$. Aligning the Fourier polar axis with $r-r'$ gives $\int_{S^2}e^{ikl\cos\theta}d\Omega=4\pi\sin(kl)/(kl)$ and hence the displayed radial integral.

The pole on the integration path requires a prescription. The <Cauchy principal value> gives $G_{\rm PV}(l)=\cos(k_0l)/(4\pi l)$. The outgoing prescription is $k^2-k_0^2-i0$, yielding $G_+(l)=e^{ik_0l}/(4\pi l)$; the opposite prescription gives the incoming exponential. The <Sokhotski–Plemelj formula> $1/(q-i0)=\operatorname{PV}(1/q)+i\pi\delta(q)$ shows that the additional radial contribution is $i\sin(k_0l)/(4\pi l)$.

For the inverse-kernel convention $(\Delta+k_0^2)H=+\delta$, use $H=-G$. Thus a source equation $(\Delta+k_0^2)\Phi=V$ has $\Phi=H*V=-G*V$, with the same boundary or radiation convention throughout. Without a pole prescription or an equivalent condition at infinity, the real-frequency source problem does not specify a unique Green function.

= Dirichlet image kernel for the three-dimensional Helmholtz equation
{parent=Outgoing Green function for the three-dimensional Helmholtz equation}
{c}
{title2=$G_D=-[e^{ik|r-r'|}/|r-r'|-e^{ik|r-r'^*|}/|r-r'^*|]/(4\pi)$}

For the upper half-space and the sign convention $(\Delta+k^2)G_D=\delta_{r'}$, reflect the source $r'=(x',y',z')$ to $r'^*=(x',y',-z')$ and use the displayed difference. This is the negative of the conventional outgoing kernel, whose source sign is $-\delta$. The <method of images> makes the <Dirichlet boundary condition> zero on the plane. With $R=|r'-r|$ and $\cos\theta=z'/R$, its outward normal derivative there is
$$
\boxed{\partial_nG_D=-\frac{ik}{2\pi}\frac{e^{ikR}}R\left(1+\frac{i}{kR}\right)\cos\theta.}
$$
The outward <unit normal> is $-e_z$. Differentiating $e^{ikR}/R$ gives $e^{ikR}(ik/R-1/R^2)$; the real and reflected source contributions add in the normal derivative. <Green's second identity> therefore expresses a solution of the <Helmholtz equation> as $\phi(r')=\int_{z=0}\phi(r)\partial_nG_D(r,r')\,dS$ when the boundary term at infinity vanishes. Suitable decay or compatible outgoing <Sommerfeld radiation conditions> are needed; vanishing at infinity without further control is not a general uniqueness condition for a real-frequency Helmholtz problem.

= Weyl plane-wave representation
{c}
{parent=Outgoing Green function for the three-dimensional Helmholtz equation}
{title2=$\frac{e^{ikR}}R=\frac{ik}{2\pi}\int\frac{e^{ik[p(x-x')+q(y-y')+m|z-z'|]}}m\,dp\,dq$}

Here $m=\sqrt{1-p^2-q^2}$ has nonnegative imaginary part. This representation of the outgoing <Helmholtz equation> kernel separates propagating <plane waves> from <evanescent waves>. The absolute vertical separation is required for fields on either side of a source; choosing its sign fixes the upward or downward angular spectrum.

= Wave scattering at a planar interface
{parent=Helmholtz equation}

Wave scattering at a planar interface separates an incident <plane wave> into reflected and transmitted plane waves. Translation invariance parallel to the interface conserves the tangential component of the wavevector.

= Vertical wavenumber
{title2=$q$}
{parent=Wave scattering at a planar interface}

Relative to a distinguished propagation coordinate $z$, the vertical wavenumber is the corresponding wavevector component $q$. For a plane wave of wavenumber $k$ making angle $\theta$ with the vertical, $q=k\cos\theta$.

= Snell's law
{c}
{parent=Wave scattering at a planar interface}
{wiki}

Snell's law follows from conservation of tangential wavenumber across a planar interface. If angles are measured from the interface normal, it reads $n_1\sin\theta_1=n_2\sin\theta_2$.

= Scalar-wave interface condition
{parent=Wave scattering at a planar interface}

= Interface condition
{synonym}

For a scalar field satisfying second-order wave equations with the same leading coefficient on either side of an interface, continuity of the field and its normal derivative supplies two interface conditions. Different constitutive laws can instead impose weighted normal-derivative continuity.

= Reflection and transmission coefficients at a scalar-wave interface
{title2=$R,T$}
{parent=Wave scattering at a planar interface}

For incident and transmitted normal wavenumbers $q_0$ and $q_1$, continuity of a scalar field and its normal derivative gives
$$
R=\frac{q_0-q_1}{q_0+q_1},
\qquad
T=\frac{2q_0}{q_0+q_1}.
$$

= Reflection coefficient
{title2=$R$}
{parent=Reflection and transmission coefficients at a scalar-wave interface}
{wiki}

The reflection coefficient is the complex ratio between reflected and incident wave amplitudes. Its modulus determines amplitude attenuation and its argument determines the phase shift on reflection.

= Transmission coefficient
{title2=$T$}
{parent=Reflection and transmission coefficients at a scalar-wave interface}
{wiki}

The transmission coefficient is the complex ratio between transmitted and incident wave amplitudes. Flux transmission may also contain an impedance or normal-wavenumber factor and therefore need not equal $|T|^2$.

= Phase screen
{parent=Helmholtz equation}
{wiki}

A phase screen is a thin inhomogeneous layer idealized as changing only a wave's phase. A random phase screen converts a coherent incident wave into coherent and diffuse components whose moments are determined by the phase covariance.

= Phase-to-intensity conversion near a deterministic phase screen
{parent=Phase screen}
{title2=$I=1-(x/k)\phi''+(x^2/k^2)[(\phi'')^2+\phi'\phi''']+O(x^3)$}

For $E(0,z)=e^{i\phi(z)}$ and sufficiently smooth real phase, expand free <paraxial propagation> as $E=E_0+ixE_0''/(2k)-x^2E_0''''/(8k^2)+O(x^3)$. Taking the squared modulus gives the displayed intensity. Negative phase curvature initially focuses the field. With $\phi=\cos z$, $I=1+(x/k)\cos z+(x/k)^2\cos2z+O(x^3)$, with transverse length scaled so the modulation wavenumber is one. Period-averaged intensity stays one.

= Random phase screen
{parent=Phase screen}

= Stationary phase-screen power spectrum
{parent=Random phase screen}
{title2=$S_E$}

For the <random phase screen> $E(z)=e^{i\beta W(z)}$, the <Gaussian phase-screen correlation> and <spectral measure of a stationary random field> give
$$
S_E(\nu)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-\beta^2(1-\rho(\zeta))}e^{-i\nu\zeta}\,d\zeta.
$$
If $\rho(\zeta)\to0$ and $e^{\beta^2\rho}-1$ is integrable, this is $e^{-\beta^2}\delta(\nu)$ plus the continuous density $(2\pi)^{-1}e^{-\beta^2}\int(e^{\beta^2\rho(\zeta)}-1)e^{-i\nu\zeta}d\zeta$. The total mass is one, split into coherent mass $e^{-\beta^2}$ and diffuse mass $1-e^{-\beta^2}$. Without the decay and integrability assumptions there may be additional spectral atoms or singular components.

= Gaussian phase-screen correlation
{c}
{parent=Random phase screen}
{title2=$C_E(\zeta)=e^{-\beta^2(1-\rho(\zeta))}$}

Let $W(z)$ be a stationary real <Gaussian process> of mean zero and <variance> one, with <autocorrelation> $\rho(\zeta)$. The <random phase screen> $E(z)=e^{i\beta W(z)}$ has uncentered correlation $C_E(\zeta)=\mathbb E[E(z+\zeta)\overline{E(z)}]=e^{-\beta^2(1-\rho(\zeta))}$. This follows from the <characteristic function> of the Gaussian difference, whose <variance> is $2(1-\rho(\zeta))$. Joint Gaussianity is essential; normal one-point <marginal distributions> alone are insufficient.

= Gaussian phase averaging
{c}
{parent=Random phase screen}
{title2=$\mathbb E e^{i\phi}=e^{-\operatorname{Var}(\phi)/2}$}

For a centered real <Gaussian random variable> $\phi$, its <characteristic function> gives $\mathbb E e^{i\phi}=\exp[-\operatorname{Var}(\phi)/2]$. Applied to a <random phase screen>, this controls the <coherent and diffuse wave fields>.

= Parabolic wave equation
{parent=Phase screen}
{wiki}

The parabolic wave equation is a one-way, slowly varying envelope approximation to the <Helmholtz equation>. For carrier wavenumber $k$ and transverse coordinate $z$, a common form is $2ikE_x+E_{zz}=0$.

= Stationary intensity under free paraxial propagation
{parent=Parabolic wave equation}
{title2=$\langle|E(x,z)|^2\rangle=\langle|E(0,z)|^2\rangle$}

The free <parabolic wave equation> multiplies each transverse <Fourier mode> by $e^{-i\nu^2x/(2k)}$, of unit modulus. A statistically stationary input therefore keeps its uncentered spectral measure and mean intensity. A pure phase screen of unit incident amplitude has total mean intensity one even when its coherent intensity is smaller. This statement does not require a Gaussian joint phase distribution; second-order stationarity and free paraxial propagation suffice.

= Mutual coherence in a white-noise parabolic medium
{parent=Parabolic wave equation}
{title2=$\Gamma_x=\frac{i}{2k}(\Delta_1-\Delta_2)\Gamma-k^2\mu^2[C(0)-C(\mathbf s_1-\mathbf s_2)]\Gamma$}

A <parabolic wave equation> driven by longitudinal <Gaussian white noise> with transverse <covariance kernel> $C$ has the displayed closed <mutual coherence> equation. The noise is the derivative of a <longitudinal Brownian field with transverse covariance>; the physical smooth-noise limit uses the <Stratonovich integral>. Conversion to the <Ito integral> supplies the attenuation drift and the cross variation supplies the $C(\mathbf s_1-\mathbf s_2)$ term. For unit plane-wave incidence and transverse <statistical homogeneity>, $\Gamma=\exp[-k^2\mu^2x(C(0)-C(\mathbf s_1-\mathbf s_2))]$. General Gaussian colored media do not have this local closure.

= Norm conservation for the scalar parabolic wave equation
{parent=Parabolic wave equation}
{title2=$\partial_x\int|U|^2\,dS=0$}

For $U_x=iL(x)U$ with a self-adjoint transverse <differential operator> $L(x)$, differentiating the squared <L2 norm> gives $i\langle U,LU\rangle-i\langle LU,U\rangle=0$. Reality of the refractive coefficient and vanishing transverse boundary flux are required. This is exact for the scalar envelope model. Its physical interpretation as leading paraxial power uses a fixed reference impedance; a varying local impedance or a longitudinal electromagnetic polarization requires a consistent Maxwell calculation and cannot be inferred from this invariant alone.

= Colored Gaussian paraxial mean propagator
{parent=Parabolic wave equation}
{title2=$m(x)=\langle U_W(x,0)\rangle E_0$}

For $E_x=i\Delta_\perp E/(2k)+ik\mu WE$, a jointly Gaussian colored medium has covariance $C(s,\mathbf r)$. Averaging the equation gives a term $\langle WE\rangle$; the <Furutsu–Novikov formula> expresses it through the random propagator and the functional derivative of $E$. It does not generally close in the mean field alone. A phase-screen product followed by Gaussian averaging gives a formal exact mean propagator, with factor $\exp[-k^2\mu^2\Delta x^2\sum_{j,l}C(x_j-x_l,\mathbf r_j-\mathbf r_l)/2]$ for each transverse path. In the longitudinal white-noise limit the covariance collapses to equal slices, and the mean closes as $m_x=i\Delta_\perp m/(2k)-k^2\mu^2A(0)m/2$.

= Paraxial approximation
{parent=Parabolic wave equation}
{wiki}

= Paraxial propagation
{synonym}

The paraxial approximation describes waves whose propagation directions make small angles with a preferred axis. Factoring out a carrier $e^{ikx}$ and neglecting the envelope's second longitudinal derivative turns the <Helmholtz equation> into a <parabolic wave equation> and suppresses backward propagation.

= Split-step Fourier method
{parent=Parabolic wave equation}
{wiki}

The split-step Fourier method alternates local phase accumulation with free diffraction. For $E_x=(D+S)E$ over a step $\Delta x$, <Lie-Trotter splitting> gives
$$
E(x+\Delta x)\simeq e^{\Delta xD}e^{\Delta xS}E(x),
$$
and a transverse <Fourier transform> applies the constant-coefficient diffraction exponential efficiently.

= Free-space diffraction
{parent=Parabolic wave equation}

In a homogeneous medium, a paraxial envelope satisfies $E_x=i\Delta_\perp E/(2k)$. This free-space diffraction spreads transverse Fourier modes by the phase factor $\exp(-ix|\mathbf p|^2/(2k))$.

= Sommerfeld half-plane diffraction
{c}
{parent=Free-space diffraction}

For a Neumann screen on the negative horizontal axis, the <Helmholtz equation> has an exact solution in <Fresnel integrals>. With time dependence $e^{i\omega t}$, a wave incident from angle $0<\theta_0<\pi$ has total field $e^{ikr\cos(\theta-\theta_0)}\mathcal F(s_-)+e^{ikr\cos(\theta+\theta_0)}\mathcal F(-s_+)$, where $s_\pm=\sqrt{2kr}\cos[(\theta\pm\theta_0)/2]$ and $\mathcal F(s)=\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}(-e^{i\pi/4}s)$. The angular derivatives cancel on both faces of the screen. Away from the two shadow boundaries, the <Fresnel tail expansion> splits this into incident/reflected <geometrical optics> fields and an outgoing edge wave proportional to $r^{-1/2}e^{-ikr}$.

= Wiener-Hopf solution of rigid half-plane diffraction
{parent=Sommerfeld half-plane diffraction}
{c}
{title2=$\widehat\phi^-=-i\sqrt{2k_0}\sin(\theta_0/2)/[(\kappa+k_0\cos\theta_0)\sqrt{\kappa+k_0}]$}

For a rigid screen $x<0$, use time dependence $e^{i\omega t}$ and inverse <Fourier transform> $e^{-i\kappa x}$. The scattered field is odd in $y$. Its upper-face trace is supported on $x<0$, while its normal derivative on $x>0$ is unknown. With the outgoing factor $\gamma=\sqrt{\kappa^2-k_0^2}$, these traces satisfy $\gamma\widehat\phi^-+G^+=b/(\kappa+a)$, where $a=k_0\cos\theta_0$ and $b=k_0\sin\theta_0$. Factor $\gamma=\sqrt{\kappa-k_0}\sqrt{\kappa+k_0}$ into upper/lower analytic factors. <Pole subtraction in a Wiener-Hopf equation> and the finite-energy edge condition give the displayed trace. The forcing-pole <residue> yields <geometrical optics>, while <stationary phase> yields the outgoing edge wave
$$
\phi_d\sim\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi k_0r}}\frac{\sin(\theta_0/2)\sin(\theta/2)}{\cos\theta+\cos\theta_0}e^{-ik_0r-i\pi/4}.
$$
The angular formula excludes the shadow-boundary transition regions; the exact integral supplies their uniform continuation.

= Fresnel propagator
{c}
{parent=Free-space diffraction}
{title2=$U_x=\exp(i x\Delta_\perp/(2k_0))$}

In two transverse dimensions, the <Fresnel propagator> has kernel $k_0(2\pi i x)^{-1}\exp(ik_0|\mathbf z-\mathbf z'|^2/(2x))$ for $x>0$. It evolves the homogeneous <parabolic wave equation>.

= One-dimensional transverse Fresnel propagation
{parent=Fresnel propagator}
{title2=$U_x=\exp(ix\partial_z^2/(2k))$}

For one transverse coordinate, the <parabolic wave equation> $2ikE_x+E_{zz}=0$ has the <Fresnel propagator> kernel
$$
(U_x f)(z)=\sqrt{\frac{k}{2\pi ix}}\int_{\mathbb R}
e^{ik(z-z')^2/(2x)}f(z')\,dz',\qquad x>0.
$$
The square root uses $\sqrt{1/i}=e^{-i\pi/4}$. With $\widehat f(\nu)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int f(z)e^{-i\nu z}dz$, its <Fourier transform> multiplier is $e^{-i\nu^2x/(2k)}$. This distinguishes the one-transverse-coordinate prefactor from the two-coordinate kernel. The operator is unitary on $L^2(\mathbb R)$, although its spatial integral is interpreted as an oscillatory integral.

= Free-space fourth-moment propagator
{parent=Free-space diffraction}

For $m_x=(i/k)m_{r_1r_2}$, the free-space fourth-moment propagator multiplies the two-dimensional Fourier transform by $e^{-ixp_1p_2/k}$. In physical coordinates its oscillatory kernel is
$$
K_x(r_1,r_2)=\frac{k}{2\pi x}e^{ikr_1r_2/x}.
$$

= Coherent and diffuse wave fields
{parent=Phase screen}

The coherent field is the ensemble mean $\langle E\rangle$ of a randomly scattered wave. The zero-mean remainder $E-\langle E\rangle$ is the diffuse or incoherent field.

= Coherent attenuation by a Gaussian phase screen
{parent=Coherent and diffuse wave fields}
{title2=$m=e^{-\beta^2/2}$}

For a unit-amplitude <random phase screen> $e^{i\beta W}$ with standard normal <marginal distribution>, the <characteristic function> gives coherent amplitude $m=\mathbb E[e^{i\beta W}]=e^{-\beta^2/2}$. The coherent power is $|m|^2=e^{-\beta^2}$, although the total power remains one. In homogeneous paraxial space the mean constant transverse field propagates without additional attenuation. Under decorrelation at large separation, the remaining power $1-e^{-\beta^2}$ is diffuse.

= Coherent attenuation in a white-noise random medium
{parent=Coherent and diffuse wave fields}
{title2=$\gamma=k_0^2\mu^2 B(0)/2$}

For a <parabolic wave equation> driven by longitudinal <Gaussian white noise> with transverse <covariance kernel> $B$, the mean envelope obeys $\partial_x m=i\Delta_\perp m/(2k_0)-\gamma m$. This closure requires <independent increments> in the longitudinal direction; <statistical homogeneity> and one-point <Gaussian distributions> alone are insufficient.

= Phase curvature
{title2=$\phi''$}
{parent=Phase screen}

Phase curvature is a transverse second derivative of wave phase. Under subsequent free-space propagation, positive and negative curvature cause local defocusing and focusing and thereby convert phase modulation into amplitude modulation.

= Modified Helmholtz equation
{title2=$(\Delta-m^2)u=0$}
{parent=Partial differential equation}

The modified Helmholtz equation is
$$
\Delta u-m^2u=0.
$$
It reduces to the <Laplace equation> when $m=0$.

= Zero Dirichlet solution of the modified Helmholtz equation
{parent=Modified Helmholtz equation}
{title2=$\Delta u=\mu^2u,\ u|_{\partial\Omega}=0\ \Longrightarrow\ u=0$}

Let $\mu>0$ and let $u$ be a real smooth solution on a bounded regular domain with zero <Dirichlet boundary conditions>. <Green's first identity> gives
$$
\mu^2\int_\Omega u^2\,dV=\int_\Omega u\Delta u\,dV=-\int_\Omega|\nabla u|^2\,dV.
$$
The two sides have opposite signs unless both vanish, so $u=0$. This <energy method> also proves uniqueness for prescribed boundary data by applying it to the difference of two solutions. For complex solutions use the complex conjugate in the integral. This argument does not apply with the same sign to the <Helmholtz equation>, which can have nonzero zero-boundary <eigenfunctions>.

= Yukawa kernel convolution
{c}
{parent=Modified Helmholtz equation}
{title2=$\frac1{4\pi}\int\frac{e^{-m|x-y|}}{|x-y|}\frac{e^{-\beta|y|}}{|y|}d^3y=\frac{e^{-m|x|}-e^{-\beta|x|}}{|x|(\beta^2-m^2)}$}

For $m,\beta>0$, set $K_m(r)=e^{-mr}/r$. Distributionally, $(\Delta-m^2)K_m=-4\pi\delta$, so the <modified Helmholtz equation> with unit delta source has <Green function> $-K_m/(4\pi)$. The difference $[K_\beta-K_m]/(\beta^2-m^2)$ solves the equation with source $K_\beta$: the two delta terms cancel. It has no added point source at zero and decays at infinity. The <Green function> representation proves the displayed convolution with its positive sign.

At coincident rates, the continuous limit is $e^{-mr}/(2m)$; at $r=0$, it is $1/(m+\beta)$. The latter also follows by directly integrating $e^{-(m+\beta)r'}dr'$ after the spherical factors cancel. These limits prevent the formula's apparent singular denominators from being mistaken for physical divergences.

= Quadrant modified Helmholtz Dirichlet Poisson formula
{parent=Modified Helmholtz equation}
{title2=$q(x,y)=\frac2\pi\int_0^\infty[\sin(px)e^{-B(p)y}S_2(p)+\sin(py)e^{-B(p)x}S_1(p)]dp$}

Here $B(p)=\sqrt{p^2+4\lambda}$ and $S_j$ are the <Fourier sine transforms> of the boundary values. The <modified Helmholtz closed spectral one-form> and its reflected <global relations> remove the unknown normal traces. Equivalently, four reflected <fundamental solutions> give a zero-boundary <Green function> in the quadrant. Its inward derivatives give the displayed formula. The two terms recover the bottom and left traces respectively; matching corner values ensure continuity at the corner.

= Modified Helmholtz closed spectral one-form
{parent=Modified Helmholtz equation}
{title2=$W=e^{-ikz-\lambda\bar z/(ik)}[(q_z+ikq)dz-(q_{\bar z}+\lambda q/(ik))d\bar z]$}

For $k\ne0$, differentiation gives $dW=-2e^{-ikz-\lambda\bar z/(ik)}(q_{z\bar z}-\lambda q)dz\wedge d\bar z$. Thus the <differential form> is closed precisely for the <modified Helmholtz equation> $\Delta q-4\lambda q=0$. Integrating around an oriented domain gives a <global relation>. The primitive $\mu e^{-ikz-\lambda\bar z/(ik)}$ also produces compatible first-order equations whose spectral jumps reconstruct $q$.

= Two-dimensional modified Helmholtz fundamental solution
{parent=Modified Helmholtz equation}
{title2=$\Gamma_m(r)=K_0(mr)/(2\pi)$}

For $m>0$, the decaying fundamental solution of $-\Delta+m^2$ in two dimensions is $K_0(mr)/(2\pi)$, with $K_0$ the <modified Bessel function of the second kind>. <Green second identity> reconstructs an interior solution from its <Dirichlet boundary condition> $f$ and outward <normal derivative> $h$ as $q(r)=\int_{\partial\Omega}(\Gamma_m h-f\partial_n\Gamma_m)ds$.

= Modified Helmholtz adjoint plane wave
{parent=Modified Helmholtz equation}
{title2=$v_\lambda$}

For $k>0$, a nonzero <spectral parameter for a linear boundary value problem> gives
$$
v_\lambda=\exp[ik(\lambda z-\overline z/\lambda)/2]=e^{Ax+By},\quad A=ik(\lambda-\lambda^{-1})/2,\quad B=-k(\lambda+\lambda^{-1})/2.
$$
The identity $A^2+B^2=k^2$ proves that this solves the <modified Helmholtz equation>. The reciprocal companion is $v_{1/\lambda}=\overline{v_{\overline\lambda}}$.

= Side localization of modified Helmholtz plane waves
{parent=Modified Helmholtz adjoint plane wave}
{title2=$e^{\eta(n\cdot r-1)+ip\,t\cdot r},\quad\eta^2-p^2=m^2$}

On a square, a normalized <modified Helmholtz adjoint plane wave> with positive normal growth toward one side has magnitude one there, magnitude $e^{-2\eta}$ on the opposite side, and an adjacent-side magnitude integral bounded by $1/\eta$. Thus large tangential spectral frequencies suppress remote-side normal-trace coefficients. Paired <sine collocation of square modified Helmholtz global relations> further cancels adjacent-side unknown traces exactly.

= Radial modified Helmholtz equation
{parent=Modified Helmholtz equation}

For a <radial function> in three dimensions, $(\Delta-1)y=g(x)$ becomes $y''+2y'/x-y=g(x)$. Setting $u=xy$ gives $u''-u=xg(x)$, reducing it to a constant-coefficient <linear ordinary differential equation>. If $g=e^{-3x}/x^2$, a decaying particular solution obtained by <variation of parameters> is
$$
y(x)=\alpha\frac{e^{-x}}x+\frac{e^{-x}E_1(2x)-e^xE_1(4x)}{2x}.
$$
The <small-argument expansion of the exponential integral> gives
$$
y(x)=\frac{\alpha+\tfrac12\log2}{x}+\log x-\alpha+\tfrac32\log2+\gamma-1+O(x\log x).
$$
The undetermined decaying homogeneous coefficient $\alpha$ is fixed by a boundary condition or by a <matched asymptotic expansion>.

= Poisson equation with a spherical harmonic source
{parent=Poisson's equation}
{c}
{title2=$\Delta(r^pY_\ell)=[p(p+1)-\ell(\ell+1)]r^{p-2}Y_\ell$}

Let $Y_\ell$ be a <spherical harmonic> of degree $\ell$ on the unit <sphere>. The <Laplacian> in <spherical coordinates> separates its angular part from a radial power, giving the displayed identity for $r>0$. Thus a <Poisson equation> $\Delta\Phi=r^qY_\ell$ has the particular solution $\Phi=r^{q+2}Y_\ell/[(q+2)(q+3)-\ell(\ell+1)]$ when the denominator is nonzero. Add $Ar^\ell Y_\ell+Br^{-\ell-1}Y_\ell$ to impose the radial <boundary conditions>. = Power-law angular mode for the planar Poisson equation
{parent=Poisson's equation}
{title2=$\Delta[r^s\cos(m\theta)]=(s^2-m^2)r^{s-2}\cos(m\theta)$}

For integer angular frequency $m\geq0$, the <Laplacian> in <polar coordinates> gives the displayed identity on $r>0$ by direct differentiation. Consequently a source $\beta r^q\cos(m\theta)$ has particular solution $\beta r^{q+2}\cos(m\theta)/[(q+2)^2-m^2]$ when the denominator is nonzero. The sine case is identical. If $s=q+2=\pm m\ne0$, differentiating the identity with respect to $s$ gives the resonant particular solution $\beta r^s\ln r\cos(m\theta)/(2s)$. For $s=m=0$, the radial source $\beta r^{-2}$ instead has particular solution $\beta(\ln r)^2/2$. These are local statements on the punctured plane; boundedness, smoothness at the origin and boundary data must be imposed separately by adding suitable <harmonic functions>.

= Poisson potential of an exponential-over-radius source
{parent=Poisson's equation}
{title2=$\rho(r)=Ae^{-\beta r}/r$}

For $\beta>0$, solve $\nabla^2\phi=-\rho$ in three dimensions with $\rho(r)=Ae^{-\beta r}/r$, finite potential at the origin and decay at infinity. The <Green function> is $1/(4\pi|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|)$ in this sign convention. The angular integral supplied by the <spherical shell theorem> gives
$$
\phi(r)=\frac1r\int_0^r s^2\rho(s)\,ds+\int_r^\infty s\rho(s)\,ds
=\boxed{\frac{A(1-e^{-\beta r})}{\beta^2r}}.
$$
The value at the origin is the limit $A/\beta$. A second derivation sets $u=r\phi$: the radial <Poisson equation> becomes $u''=-Ae^{-\beta r}$. Decay excludes the constant-potential term and boundedness at the origin cancels the remaining $1/r$ singularity. Although the source is locally <integrable>, it is singular at the origin; the resulting potential solves the equation there in the sense of <distributions>, without an extra <Dirac delta>.

= Poisson equation on a disk with angular forcing
{c}
{parent=Poisson's equation}
{title2=$R_n(r)=\frac{r^n-r^2}{n^2-4}\quad(n\ne2)$}

For the <Poisson equation> $\Delta u=\sum_{n\geq1}b_n\sin(n\theta)$ on the unit disk with zero <Dirichlet boundary condition>, <separation of variables> in <polar coordinates> gives $r^2R_n''+rR_n'-n^2R_n=r^2$. For $n\ne2$, boundedness at $r=0$ and $R_n(1)=0$ select $R_n=(r^n-r^2)/(n^2-4)$. The resonant mode is $R_2=r^2\log r/4$. For discontinuous angular data, the <Poisson equation> is interpreted in its <weak formulation>; its <Dirichlet energy> gives uniqueness. <Orthogonality> of the angular <Fourier modes> gives $\int u f\,dA=-\sum_n\pi b_n^2/[4(n+2)^2]$, with the resonant case obtained directly or by the limit $n\to2$.

= Neumann Poisson problem
{c}
{parent=Poisson's equation}
{title2=$-\Delta u=f,\quad\partial_nu=0$}

On a smooth bounded connected domain, the homogeneous Neumann problem seeks $u\in H^1(U)$ such that $\int\nabla u\cdot\nabla v=\int fv$ for every $v\in H^1(U)$. For $f\in L^2(U)$ it is solvable exactly when $\int f=0$, and the <weak solution> is unique up to constants. The <Poincare-Wirtinger inequality> and the <Riesz representation theorem> give existence on the <mean-zero Sobolev space>. The test function one gives necessity of the compatibility condition.

= Neumann point source on a half-space boundary
{parent=Neumann Poisson problem}
{c}
{title2=$\Phi(x,y,z)=1/[2\pi\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}]$}

In the upper half-space $z>0$, the displayed decaying <harmonic function> has outward normal derivative $\partial_n\Phi=-\partial_z\Phi=z/[2\pi(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}]$. This is the <Poisson kernel for the upper half-space>, whose integral on a horizontal plane is one and whose limit as $z\downarrow0$ is the two-dimensional <Dirac delta>. Thus $\partial_n\Phi=\delta(x)\delta(y)$ in the distributional boundary sense. The compensating flux through a large hemisphere is minus one. This illustrates why a nonzero total boundary source is possible on an unbounded <domain> even when the interior source is zero.

= Radial Poisson gradient estimate
{parent=Poisson's equation}
{title2=$\|\nabla u\|_\infty\le C\|f\|_2$}

For a radial Dirichlet solution on a fixed three-dimensional shell $a<r<b$, $(r^2U')'=r^2F$. Thus $r^2U'=C_0+\int_a^rs^2F(s)\,ds$, and $U(a)=U(b)=0$ bounds $|C_0|$ by the supremum of that integral. <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> in the radial volume measure bounds the integral by $C\|f\|_2$. Since $r\ge a>0$, the displayed <gradient> bound follows. The estimate exploits radiality and does not extend to arbitrary forcing in three dimensions.

= Failure of L2-to-Linfinity Poisson gradient bounds in three dimensions
{parent=Radial Poisson gradient estimate}

Inside any three-dimensional domain containing a ball, let $u_\varepsilon(x)=\varepsilon^{1/2}\phi((x-x_0)/\varepsilon)$ for a fixed smooth compactly supported nonconstant function. The right-hand side $f_\varepsilon=\Delta u_\varepsilon$ has constant $L^2$ <norm>, whereas $\|\nabla u_\varepsilon\|_\infty=\varepsilon^{-1/2}\|\nabla\phi\|_\infty$ diverges. The functions have zero boundary trace, giving a scaling counterexample to a uniform $L^2$-forcing <gradient> estimate.

= Poisson kernel
{parent=Poisson's equation}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$P_\Omega(x,\xi)=-\partial_{n_\xi}G_\Omega(x,\xi)$}

A Poisson kernel represents the <harmonic extension> of <Dirichlet boundary data> by an integral over the boundary. When the <Dirichlet Green function> is normalized by $-\Delta G_\Omega=\delta$, its negative outward normal <derivative> gives the displayed kernel. The <Poisson kernel for the upper half-space> and the <Poisson kernel for the upper half-plane> give explicit examples with different boundary dimensions. Boundedness or other stated growth conditions are needed when selecting a solution in an unbounded domain.

= Poisson kernel for the upper half-space
{parent=Poisson kernel}
{c}
{title2=$P_z(u,v)=z/[2\pi(u^2+v^2+z^2)^{3/2}]$}

In three dimensions the <Poisson kernel for the upper half-space> is
$$
P_z(u,v)=\frac{z}{2\pi(u^2+v^2+z^2)^{3/2}},\qquad z>0.
$$
It gives the <harmonic function> $\phi(x,y,z)=\int_{\mathbb R^2}P_z(x-\xi,y-\eta)f(\xi,\eta)\,d\xi\,d\eta$ for compatible boundary data. The <method of images> constructs the zero-boundary <Dirichlet Green function> by subtracting the reflected-source <Green function of the Laplacian> from the source kernel. On the boundary plane the outward <unit normal> is $-e_z$, and $-\partial_nG=P_z$, giving the integral formula through <Green's second identity>.

The kernel is positive and has integral one: in <polar coordinates> this is $z\int_0^\infty r(r^2+z^2)^{-3/2}dr=1$. Its mass outside any fixed disk tends to zero as $z\downarrow0$. It is therefore an <approximate identity>, recovering bounded boundary data at continuity points. At a jump across a smooth curve, the vertical limit gives the average of the two adjacent values; assigning a different value only on that curve does not change the harmonic extension.

= Axial Poisson extension of a circular boundary patch
{parent=Poisson kernel for the upper half-space}

Take boundary data one on a disk of radius $a>0$ and zero outside. The <Poisson kernel for the upper half-space> gives on the axis
$$
\boxed{\phi(0,0,z)=z\int_0^a\frac{r\,dr}{(r^2+z^2)^{3/2}}=1-\frac{z}{\sqrt{a^2+z^2}},\qquad z>0.}
$$
The axis value tends to one at the boundary and behaves as $a^2/(2z^2)$ far away. The boundary data at the circular rim are understood almost everywhere: changing a value on the rim, a set of planar measure zero, leaves the integral unchanged. This does not assert a continuous boundary extension across the jump.

= Poisson kernel for the upper half-plane

It is the density of <harmonic measure> on the real boundary as viewed from $x+iy$.

= Dirichlet Poisson integral in a quadrant
{c}
{parent=Poisson integral}

The <conformal map> $w=z^2$ sends $x,y>0$ to the <complex upper half-plane>. For bounded continuous boundary values $g_2$ on the horizontal axis and $g_1$ on the vertical axis with equal corner values, set $f(s)=g_2(\sqrt s)$ for $s>0$ and $f(s)=g_1(\sqrt{-s})$ for $s<0$. The bounded <harmonic function> with those <Dirichlet boundary conditions> is
$$
u(z)=\frac1\pi\int_{\mathbb R}\frac{\operatorname{Im}(z^2)f(s)}{|s-z^2|^2}\,ds.
$$
Equivalently, if $z^2=a+ic$ with $c>0$, this is $\frac{2c}{\pi}\int_0^\infty r\left[g_2(r)/((r^2-a)^2+c^2)+g_1(r)/((r^2+a)^2+c^2)\right]dr$. The <Poisson kernel> is a positive <approximate identity>, so the prescribed edge values are attained. Boundedness is essential for uniqueness on this unbounded domain.

= Bounded Dirichlet uniqueness in a quadrant
{parent=Dirichlet Poisson integral in a quadrant}

Two bounded <harmonic functions> continuous to the quadrant edges with the same <Dirichlet boundary conditions> are equal. Their difference is bounded and zero on both axes. <Odd reflection> across each axis gives a bounded harmonic extension away from the corner; the <removable singularity for a bounded harmonic function> fills in that corner. The <harmonic Liouville theorem> makes that extension constant, and oddness makes the constant zero. This does not exclude unbounded examples such as the <zero-boundary harmonic growth in a quadrant>.

= Zero-boundary harmonic growth in a quadrant
{parent=Dirichlet Poisson integral in a quadrant}

The <harmonic function> $v(x,y)=xy$ vanishes on both axes but is nonzero in the quadrant, and $v_z=-iz/2$. More generally $\operatorname{Im}z^{2m}$, for a positive integer $m$, vanishes on both axes and is harmonic. Adding these unbounded functions leaves all edge <Dirichlet boundary conditions> unchanged. Thus decay of the boundary data does not imply decay of every solution, and an edge-data-only formula cannot determine an arbitrary solution's <Wirtinger derivative>. A boundedness or suitable growth condition must select the solution.

= Holomorphic derivative of a quadrant Dirichlet solution
{parent=Dirichlet Poisson integral in a quadrant}
{title2=$u_z$}

For the bounded <Dirichlet Poisson integral in a quadrant>, the <Wirtinger derivative> is
$$
u_z(z)=\frac{2z}{\pi i}\int_0^\infty r\left[\frac{g_2(r)}{(r^2-z^2)^2}+\frac{g_1(r)}{(r^2+z^2)^2}\right]dr.
$$
This follows by differentiating the <Schwarz integral formula> composed with $z^2$, remembering that for real boundary data $u_z$ is half the derivative of a <holomorphic function> whose <real part> is $u$. For complex boundary data, apply the construction to the real and imaginary parts separately and use linearity. If the data and their <derivatives> decay sufficiently, <integration by parts> gives
$$
u_z(z)=\frac z{\pi i}\left[\int_0^\infty\frac{g_2'(r)}{r^2-z^2}\,dr+\int_0^\infty\frac{g_1'(r)}{r^2+z^2}\,dr\right].
$$
The endpoint terms cancel exactly when $g_1(0)=g_2(0)$. Interior denominators do not vanish because $\operatorname{Im}z^2>0$.

= Schwarz integral formula
{c}
{parent=Poisson integral}
{wiki}

The Schwarz integral formula reconstructs a <holomorphic function> from boundary values of its <real part>. For a real boundary function $f$ on the <complex upper half-plane> satisfying $\int_{\mathbb R}|f(s)|/(1+s^2)\,ds<\infty$, the regularized form is
$$
W(w)=\frac1{\pi i}\int_{\mathbb R}f(s)\left[\frac1{s-w}-\frac{s}{1+s^2}\right]ds.
$$
Its <real part> is the <Poisson integral> of $f$. The subtraction makes the integral converge and changes only the imaginary normalization. <Integration by parts>, when the boundary terms vanish, gives $W'(w)=(\pi i)^{-1}\int f'(s)/(s-w)\,ds$; jumps of $f$ are included as <Dirac delta function> terms in its <distributional derivative>.

= Harmonic quadrant solution from tangential boundary derivatives

For a <harmonic function> in the quadrant with boundary derivatives $u_y(0,y)=g_1(y)$ and $u_x(x,0)=g_2(x)$, integrating the data to equal corner values and applying the <Schwarz integral formula> after the <conformal map> $w=z^2$ gives

= Nonlinear Schrödinger equation
{parent=Nonlinear partial differential equation}
{wiki}

The nonlinear Schrödinger equation adds an amplitude-dependent interaction to Schrödinger-type wave evolution. A one-dimensional cubic specialization is the <focusing nonlinear Schrodinger equation>; members of this broader family need not be <integrable systems>.

= Complex Ginzburg–Landau equation
{parent=Nonlinear partial differential equation}

= cGLE
{c}
{synonym}

The <complex Ginzburg–Landau equation> is a nonlinear complex-amplitude evolution equation with linear and cubic terms and generally complex coefficients. The driven-condensate form $\Psi_t=(\alpha-\beta|\Psi|^2)\Psi+i(\nabla^2-g|\Psi|^2+s)\Psi$ has gain/loss coefficients $\alpha,\beta$ and conservative interaction/frequency coefficients $g,s$. A uniform nonzero state has <number density> $\alpha/\beta$ when $\alpha,\beta>0$.

A <complex Ginzburg-Landau equation> describes a <complex number> disturbance amplitude through linear amplification, <advection>, complex <diffusion equation> terms, and often <cubic amplitude saturation>. A constant-coefficient example is $\psi_t+U\psi_x=\mu\psi+(1+ic_d)\psi_{xx}-|\psi|^2\psi$. Here real $U,\mu,c_d$ describe transport, linear <growth rate> and dispersive <diffusion equation> terms. Omitting the cubic term gives the <linear complex Ginzburg-Landau equation>.

= Benjamin-Feir stability condition
{parent=Complex Ginzburg–Landau equation}
{c}

For $A_t=A+(1+ib)A_{xx}-(1+ic)|A|^2A$, the uniform oscillation $A=e^{-ict}$ has phase <diffusion> coefficient $D=1+bc$. Its amplitude-phase <dispersion relation> is $\lambda^2+(2+2k^2)\lambda+2Dk^2+(1+b^2)k^4=0$. Thus $D<0$ gives a long-wavelength <modulational instability>, whereas $D>0$ gives <linear stability> of all nonzero <Fourier modes>. On a fixed periodic domain, instability requires an allowed wavenumber with $k^2<-2D/(1+b^2)$.

= Long-wave phase reduction of the complex Ginzburg-Landau equation
{parent=Benjamin-Feir stability condition}

Write $A=e^{-ict}(1+r)e^{i\phi}$. The amplitude relaxes at rate two, while the phase is neutral under constant shifts. Eliminating the damped amplitude on long scales gives $\phi_t=(1+bc)\phi_{xx}+(c-b)(\phi_x)^2-E\phi_{xxxx}+\cdots$, where the linear phase branch has $E=b^2(1+c^2)/2$. Near $1+bc=0$, $E=(1+b^2)/2$ at leading order. Constant phase <symmetry> forbids undifferentiated phase terms; spatial parity of the leading amplitude equation forbids odd linear derivatives. Balancing negative phase diffusion against the fourth derivative produces the <Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation>.

= Real-coefficient cubic-quintic amplitude equation
{parent=Complex Ginzburg–Landau equation}
{title2=$A_t=\mu A+\alpha|A|^2A-|A|^4A+A_{xx}$}

For real $\mu$ and $\alpha>0$, this complex <amplitude equation> has a destabilizing cubic term and a saturating quintic term. Nonzero uniform intensities solve $\mu+\alpha q-q^2=0$. The upper branch is radially stable and the lower branch radially unstable; constant phase is neutral because multiplication by $e^{i\phi_0}$ preserves the equation. Stable conduction and the stable upper pattern coexist for $-\alpha^2/4<\mu<0$. At the fold, a zero radial eigenvalue conceals one-sided nonlinear instability, so strict stability cannot be inferred from eigenvalues alone.

= Stationary front of a cubic-quintic amplitude equation
{parent=Real-coefficient cubic-quintic amplitude equation}
{title2=$\mu_M=-3\alpha^2/16$}

A nontrivial stationary front of the <real-coefficient cubic-quintic amplitude equation> joining a nonzero pattern to zero must have constant phase. Writing $A=Re^{i\phi}$ gives $R^2\phi'=J$; the finite first integral contains $J^2/(2R^2)$, so approaching zero forces $J=0$. Equal endpoint potentials then give $R_-^2=3\alpha/4$ and the unique parameter $\mu_M=-3\alpha^2/16$. The explicit profile is $A=e^{i\phi_0}\sqrt{(3\alpha/4)/(1+e^{\sqrt3\alpha(x-x_0)/2})}$. This proves existence as well as the necessary equal-potential condition. The zero solution is excluded from the term front.

= Nonlinear Ginzburg-Landau equation
{parent=Complex Ginzburg–Landau equation}
{title2=$\psi_t+U\psi_x=\mu\psi+\psi_{xx}-|\psi|^2\psi$}

This nondispersive cubic <complex Ginzburg-Landau equation> balances linear growth against nonlinear damping. Its real-wavenumber <Ginzburg-Landau plane wave> has amplitude $\sqrt{\mu-k^2}$ when $\mu>k^2$. Relaxation within that single-mode amplitude family does not by itself establish stability against general perturbations.

= Ginzburg-Landau plane wave
{c}
{parent=Nonlinear Ginzburg-Landau equation}
{title2=$\psi=\sqrt{\mu-k^2}\,e^{ik(x-Ut)}$}

With real $k,U$ and $\mu>k^2$, substitution into the <nonlinear Ginzburg-Landau equation> cancels the <material derivative> and gives $Q^2=\mu-k^2$. A complex $k$ makes the modulus spatially varying, so it generally does not admit this constant-amplitude <nonlinear Ginzburg-Landau equation> ansatz.

= Cubic amplitude saturation
{parent=Ginzburg-Landau plane wave}
{title2=$R'=aR-R^3$}

For $a>0$, the positive amplitude <ordinary differential equation> $R'=aR-R^3$ has solution $R(t)=\sqrt a[1+(a/R_0^2-1)e^{-2at}]^{-1/2}$. This follows from the <logistic differential equation> for $R^2$. Every $R_0>0$ approaches $\sqrt a$ as a <monotone function>, and the positive distance to this limit decreases; an oscillating complex wave carrying this amplitude has no ordinary monotone ordering.

= Linear complex Ginzburg-Landau equation
{parent=Complex Ginzburg–Landau equation}
{title2=$\psi_t+U\psi_x=\mu\psi+(1+ic_d)\psi_{xx}$}

A <normal mode> $e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$ has <dispersion relation> $\omega=Uk+(c_d-i)k^2+i\mu$. The <absolute wavenumber> is the saddle where $d\omega/dk=0$. The <Green function of the linear complex Ginzburg-Landau equation> distinguishes <convective hydrodynamic instability> from <absolute hydrodynamic instability>.

= Airy global modes of a linearly confined Ginzburg-Landau equation
{c}
{parent=Linear complex Ginzburg-Landau equation}
{title2=$\omega_n=i[\mu_0-U^2/(4\gamma)+(\epsilon^2\gamma\lambda^2)^{1/3}z_n]$}

On $x>0$ with a homogeneous wall condition, positive <diffusion> $\gamma$ and growth $\mu(x)=\mu_0-\epsilon\lambda x$, remove constant drift by $A=e^{Ux/(2\gamma)-i\omega t}f$. The scale $\xi=(\epsilon\lambda/\gamma)^{1/3}x$ reduces the <eigenvalue problem> to the <Airy ordinary differential equation>. Decay selects $f=\operatorname{Ai}(\xi+z_n)$, where $\operatorname{Ai}(z_n)=0$ enforces the wall. The least negative zero $z_1$ sets the <global instability> threshold $\mu_0>U^2/(4\gamma)+|z_1|(\epsilon^2\gamma\lambda^2)^{1/3}$. A locally absolutely unstable pocket can therefore exist without an unstable <global mode>.

= Green function of the linear complex Ginzburg-Landau equation
{c}
{parent=Linear complex Ginzburg-Landau equation}
{title2=$G(x,t)=\frac{e^{\mu t-(x-Ut)^2/[4(1+ic_d)t]}}{\sqrt{4\pi(1+ic_d)t}}$}

For $t>0$ this <Green function> is the response to a point impulse on the infinite line, using the continuous <square root> of $1+ic_d$. The <Fourier transform> multiplies the initial transform by $e^{\mu t-ikUt-(1+ic_d)k^2t}$; its Gaussian inverse gives the displayed expression. At fixed $x$, its exponential rate is $\mu-U^2/[4(1+c_d^2)]$, whereas along $x=Ut$ it is $\mu$. Thus a temporally growing flow with $0<\mu<U^2/[4(1+c_d^2)]$ is convectively rather than absolutely unstable.

= Radial flow near a driven vortex core
{parent=Complex Ginzburg–Landau equation}
{title2=$u_r'(0)=-\operatorname{Im}\xi/2$}

For $[\nabla^2+\xi(1-|\psi|^2)]\psi=0$ and a regular unit-charge core $\psi=f(r)e^{i[\theta+\chi(r)]}$, write current <velocity> $u_r=2\chi'$ when the time-dependent kinetic operator is $-\nabla^2$. Separating the imaginary part gives
$$
(rf^2u_r)'=-2\operatorname{Im}\xi\;rf^2(1-f^2).
$$
With $f=ar+O(r^3)$ and no singular core flux, integration gives $u_r=-\operatorname{Im}\xi\,r/2+O(r^3)$. If $\xi=gn_\infty-i\alpha$, the slope is $\alpha/2$. The phase-gradient convention $k=\chi'$ has half this slope, $\alpha/4$. These are local regularity results, not a global vortex-existence assertion.

= Axisymmetric harmonic interpolation between concentric spheres

= Spherical-shell harmonic interpolation
{synonym}

Suppose the inner and outer boundary values on spheres of radii $0<a<b$ have convergent <Legendre polynomial> expansions with coefficients $u_n,v_n$. For each degree set $F_n(r)=r^n-a^{2n+1}r^{-n-1}$ and $H_n(r)=r^n-b^{2n+1}r^{-n-1}$. Then
$$
R_n(r)=u_n\frac{H_n(r)}{H_n(a)}+v_n\frac{F_n(r)}{F_n(b)}.
$$
Both radial terms solve $(r^2R_n')'=n(n+1)R_n$, so the displayed angular expansion is <harmonic> wherever the series and required derivatives converge. The two radial factors take endpoint values $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$, respectively, proving the boundary matching. For continuous data with the usual harmonic-extension convergence, the <maximum principle for harmonic functions> gives uniqueness. The construction separates the angular <orthogonality> from the two-boundary radial interpolation.

= Nondegenerate stationary points of a planar harmonic function

For a planar <harmonic function>, $u_{yy}=-u_{xx}$. Its <Hessian matrix> is therefore $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&-a\end{pmatrix}$, with <eigenvalues> $\pm\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$. = Harmonic odd reflection across a hyperplane

A <harmonic function> on one side of a hyperplane that is continuous up to an open portion of the hyperplane and zero there extends harmonically by odd reflection. = Harmonic functions of Brownian motion

For a $C^2$ <harmonic function> $u$ and <Brownian motion> stopped before leaving its domain, <Itô formula> gives $du(B_t)=\nabla u(B_t)\cdot dB_t+\frac12\Delta u(B_t)dt$. The drift vanishes. = Pluriharmonic function

A smooth real function on a <complex manifold> is pluriharmonic if $\partial\bar\partial f=0$. = Compact convergence of locally bounded harmonic functions
{parent=Harmonic function}

A locally uniformly bounded sequence of <harmonic functions> with a pointwise limit converges uniformly on compact subsets, and its limit is harmonic. A radial <mollifier> of fixed local radius reproduces each harmonic function by the <mean value property for harmonic functions>. Differentiating that convolution gives uniform interior gradient bounds, hence equicontinuity. A finite covering of a compact set converts pointwise convergence to uniform convergence. Passing to the limit in the radial convolution identity proves smoothness; passing to the limit against the Laplacian of each <test function> proves harmonicity.

= Poisson integral converges nontangentially at Lebesgue points
{c}
{parent=Poisson integral on the unit disk}

For $F\in L^1(\partial\mathbb D)$ and a <Lebesgue point> $\xi_0$, its <Poisson integral> tends to $F(\xi_0)$ through every fixed <Stolz region>. If $\delta=1-|z|$, the <Poisson kernel> there is at most $C_A\delta/(\delta^2+d(\xi,\xi_0)^2)$, where $d$ is shorter angular distance. Dyadic annuli about $\xi_0$ bound the near contribution by small local averages of $|F-F(\xi_0)|$ times a summable sequence $C_A2^{-k}$. The contribution outside a fixed small arc is $O(\delta)\|F-F(\xi_0)\|_1$ and vanishes. This is the local kernel argument underlying the <Fatou theorem for bounded holomorphic functions>.

= Poisson kernel on the circle
{parent=Poisson integral on the unit disk}
{c}
{title2=$P_r(t)=(1-r^2)/(1-2r\cos t+r^2)$}

For $0<r<1$, the <geometric series> gives $P_r(t)=1+2\sum_{n\ge1}r^n\cos nt$. Hence this positive even kernel has normalized integral one, and normalized <convolution> with it multiplies the $n$th <Fourier coefficient> by $r^{|n|}$. Its mass outside any fixed neighbourhood of zero tends to zero as $r\uparrow1$. If an integrable function has finite one-sided limits $A,B$ at zero, evenness gives half the mass to each side and the convolution tends to $(A+B)/2$.

= Uniform Poisson summability of continuous circle functions
{parent=Poisson kernel on the circle}
{title2=$\|f*P_r-f\|_\infty\to0$}

The <Poisson kernel on the circle> is nonnegative, has normalized integral one, and its mass outside any fixed arc about zero tends to zero as $r\uparrow1$. Split the convolution error into a short arc, controlled by the <modulus of continuity>, and its complement, controlled by the vanishing kernel mass. This proves <uniform convergence> for every continuous periodic function. Its Fourier multiplier is $r^{|n|}$, so the convolution is the Abel sum of the <Fourier series>.

= Conjugate Poisson kernel on the circle
{parent=Poisson kernel on the circle}
{title2=$Q_r(t)=2r\sin t/(1-2r\cos t+r^2)$}

The <geometric series> gives $Q_r(t)=2\sum_{n\ge1}r^n\sin nt$. Its <convolution> multiplier is $-i\operatorname{sgn}(n)r^{|n|}$ for nonzero frequencies and zero at frequency zero. Since the series is uniformly absolutely convergent for $r<1$, these identities hold for every <Lebesgue integrable function> by termwise integration. If all negative <Fourier coefficients> vanish, $Q_r*f=-i(P_r*f-\widehat f(0))$.

= One-sided Fourier spectrum excludes jumps
{parent=Conjugate Poisson kernel on the circle}
{title2=$\widehat f(n)=0\ (n<0)\Longrightarrow f(t^-)=f(t^+)$}

Let an integrable function have finite one-sided limits $A,B$ at zero. Oddness gives $(Q_r*f)(0)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_0^\pi Q_r(t)[f(-t)-f(t)]\,dt$. Also $\int_0^\delta Q_r(t)\,dt=\log[(1-2r\cos\delta+r^2)/(1-r)^2]=2\log(1/(1-r))+O_\delta(1)$. Thus $(Q_r*f)(0)/\log(1/(1-r))\to(A-B)/\pi$: make the near-zero error in $f(-t)-f(t)$ arbitrarily small and bound the remaining integral uniformly. When negative <Fourier coefficients> vanish, the conjugate-kernel identity makes this convolution bounded because $P_r*f$ tends to $(A+B)/2$. Therefore $A=B$. A <removable discontinuity> caused solely by altering a point value is not excluded.

Equivalently its negative is a <subharmonic function>. The canonical lower semicontinuous representative satisfies the reverse ball mean inequality. = Canonical representative of a subharmonic distribution
{parent=Subharmonic function}
{title2=$\widetilde u(x)=\lim_{r\downarrow0}\frac{1}{|B_r(x)|}\int_{B_r(x)}u(y)\,dy$}

For locally integrable real $u$ with nonnegative <distributional derivative> $\Delta u$, its ball means increase with radius. To see this, mollify: for a smooth subharmonic function the derivative of its spherical mean is the integral of $\Delta u$ over the ball divided by the sphere area. The ball means inherit monotonicity; local $L^1$ convergence passes this to $u$. Their decreasing-radius limit exists, may be minus infinity at exceptional points, and equals $u$ almost everywhere by the <Lebesgue differentiation theorem>. It is upper semicontinuous as an infimum of continuous ball means and satisfies the mean-value inequality. Arbitrary changes of a representative on a null set can destroy its pointwise maximum principle.

= Maximum principle for subharmonic functions
{parent=Subharmonic function}

A continuous <subharmonic function> on the closure of a bounded domain attains its maximum on the boundary. If it is twice differentiable, the strict condition $\Delta u>0$ rules out an interior maximum immediately because the <Hessian matrix> there would be negative semidefinite.

= Strong maximum principle for subharmonic functions
{parent=Maximum principle for subharmonic functions}

If the <canonical representative of a subharmonic distribution> on a connected domain attains a finite global maximum at an interior point, it is constant. The ball mean inequality forces equality almost everywhere on a small ball around the maximum. The canonical representative then equals the maximum everywhere on that ball. The maximum set is open by this argument and closed by upper semicontinuity; <connectedness> makes it the whole domain.

= Gradient maximum principle for harmonic functions

= Gaussian heat-kernel proof of the harmonic Liouville theorem
{c}
{parent=Harmonic Liouville theorem}

A bounded entire <harmonic function> satisfies $u(x)=\mathbb E_xu(B_t)$ because $u(B)$ is a bounded <local martingale>. = One-sided bounded harmonic functions on the punctured plane are constant
{parent=Harmonic Liouville theorem}

A <harmonic function> on $\mathbb R^2\setminus\{0\}$ bounded on one side is constant. Shift and change sign to make it nonnegative. Its circular mean has the form $a\log r+b$; positivity for every $r>0$ forces $a=0$. For $|x|=r$, the <mean value property for harmonic functions> bounds its value by the mean integral over $r/2<|z|<3r/2$, giving at most $8b$. A <removable singularity for a bounded harmonic function> fills the origin, and the <harmonic Liouville theorem> finishes the proof. The dimensional distinction matters: $|x|^{2-n}$ is a positive nonconstant <harmonic function> on punctured $\mathbb R^n$ for $n\geq3$.

= Brownian coupling proof of the harmonic Liouville theorem
{c}
{parent=Harmonic Liouville theorem}

For a bounded harmonic function $f$ on $\mathbb R^d$, the processes $f(B_t^x)$ and $f(B_t^y)$ are bounded martingales. Couple the Brownian motions from $x$ and $y$ so that they coalesce in finite time almost surely. Then
$$
|f(x)-f(y)|
\leq2\lVert f\rVert_\infty\mathbb P(T>t)
\longrightarrow0,
$$
which proves that $f$ is constant in every dimension.

= Orthogonal level curves of harmonic conjugates
{parent=Harmonic conjugate}

$$

The identity follows by taking the <real part> of the <geometric series> $1+2\sum_{n\ge1}(re^{i\theta})^n$. For integrable $2\pi$-periodic boundary data $f$, its Poisson extension is
$$

=\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^\infty r^n[a_n\cos(n\theta)+b_n\sin(n\theta)],

where $a_n,b_n$ are the usual <Fourier coefficients>. Each term is a <harmonic function> in the open <disk>. For continuous boundary data, the extension solves the <Dirichlet problem> with those boundary values.

For $0<\theta<\gamma$ and $0<r<R$, a bounded <harmonic function> that vanishes on the straight edges and has outer boundary data $\varphi$ has coefficients
$$
b_n=\frac2\gamma\int_0^\gamma\varphi(\theta)\sin\frac{n\pi\theta}\gamma\,d\theta.
$$
This follows by <separation of variables> and <Fourier orthogonality>. Negative radial powers are excluded by boundedness at the vertex. Reflection-symmetric data $\varphi(\theta)=\varphi(\gamma-\theta)$ have only odd <Fourier modes>. Knowing the resulting solution on the bisector determines all these odd coefficients by its radial <power series>. Without reflection symmetry, the even angular modes vanish on the bisector and cannot be recovered from that restriction alone.

= Laplace equation in cylindrical coordinates
{parent=Laplace equation}
{c}

In cylindrical coordinates $(r,\theta,z)$, <Laplace's equation> is
$$
\frac1r\frac{\partial}{\partial r}
\left(r\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial r}\right)
+\frac1{r^2}\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial\theta^2}
+\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial z^2}=0.
$$

= Side boundary data do not determine a bounded harmonic function in a half-cylinder
{parent=Laplace equation in cylindrical coordinates}
{title2=$e^{-j_{0,1}z}J_0(j_{0,1}r)$}

In the unit half-cylinder $z\ge0$, specifying only the curved-side boundary values does not determine a bounded <harmonic function>. The displayed nonzero function, where $j_{0,1}$ is a positive zero of the <Bessel function of the first kind> $J_0$, is regular at the axis, bounded, harmonic, and zero on $r=1$. It may be added to any particular solution without changing the side data, and even decays at infinity. Boundary information at the base $z=0$ is still required for uniqueness.

= Radial Laplacian
{parent=Laplace operator}
{wiki}

For radial $u(r)$ in $n$ dimensions, $\Delta u=u\prime\prime+(n-1)u\prime/r$.

= Radial reduction and growth at infinity
{parent=Radial Laplacian}
{title2=$u''+2u'/r-u=g\iff y''-y=rg,\quad y=ru$}

For $r>0$, the substitution $y=ru$ removes the first derivative in the three-dimensional <radial Laplacian>, since $u''+2u'/r=y''/r$. Bounded $u$ at zero implies $y(0)=0$. Bounded $u$ at infinity means $y=O(r)$; it does not require bounded $y$. For example, constant forcing $g=1$ has the bounded particular solution $u=-1$, whose reduced function is $y=-r$. A <Dirichlet half-line Green function for d2 minus 1> can still represent this solution when its integral converges, but the resulting reduced function has the growth demanded by the forcing.

= Axisymmetric harmonic function
{parent=Laplacian in spherical coordinates}
{wiki}

Axisymmetric harmonic functions separate into radial powers times Legendre polynomials.

= Axisymmetric harmonic extension from a sphere
{parent=Axisymmetric harmonic function}
{title2=$\Psi_{\rm in}=\sum_\ell f_\ell(r/a)^\ell P_\ell,\quad\Psi_{\rm out}=\sum_\ell f_\ell(a/r)^{\ell+1}P_\ell$}

For boundary data $f(u)$ on the sphere $r=a$, with $u=\cos\theta$, define its <Legendre polynomial> coefficients by $f_\ell=(2\ell+1)\int_{-1}^1f(u)P_\ell(u)\,du/2$. <Separation of variables> in the <Laplace equation> gives radial factors $r^\ell$ and $r^{-\ell-1}$. Regularity at the origin selects the first for the interior <Dirichlet problem>; decay at infinity selects the second for the exterior problem. Matching each boundary coefficient gives the displayed <series>. For continuous boundary data the resulting <harmonic functions> attain those data, with convergence interpreted through their harmonic extension rather than requiring uniform convergence of the unweighted boundary series. The <maximum principle for harmonic functions> proves uniqueness in the bounded interior and decaying exterior classes.

= Odd Legendre extension for an equatorial Dirichlet plane
{parent=Axisymmetric harmonic extension from a sphere}

Suppose the exterior of a sphere also has zero <Dirichlet boundary conditions> on the part of its equatorial plane outside the sphere. The plane separates the upper and lower domains. Extend the sphere data of each hemisphere oddly across the equator, and apply the <axisymmetric harmonic extension from a sphere> separately to these two odd extensions. Only odd-degree <Legendre polynomials> occur, and every such polynomial vanishes at $u=\cos\theta=0$. Thus the resulting <harmonic function> vanishes on the plane. For upper data $f(u)$ on $0\le u\le1$, the exterior coefficients are $(2\ell+1)\int_0^1f(u)P_\ell(u)\,du$ for odd $\ell$. If $f(0)=0$, the plane and sphere data agree at their shared edge. The same construction with the lower data gives the lower solution; their normal <derivatives> need not match through a conducting sheet.

= Laplacian eigenvalue
{c}
{parent=Laplacian eigenfunction}
{title2=$-\Delta u=\lambda u$}

A scalar $\lambda$ for which a nonzero <Laplacian eigenfunction> satisfies this equation, with specified boundary conditions or on a closed <manifold>, is an eigenvalue of the positive <Laplacian>. A <Dirichlet Laplacian eigenvalue> imposes zero boundary values; other boundary conditions generally change the <spectrum>.

= Dirichlet Laplacian eigenfunction
{parent=Laplacian eigenfunction}
{c}
{wiki}

A Dirichlet Laplacian eigenfunction vanishes on the boundary and satisfies $-\Delta u=\lambda u$.

= Dirichlet eigenfunction supremum estimate
{c}
{parent=Dirichlet Laplacian eigenfunction}
{title2=$\|u\|_\infty\leq C(n)\lambda^{n/4}\|u\|_2$}

For $n\geq3$ and a bounded smooth domain, a <Dirichlet Laplacian eigenfunction> with <eigenvalue> $\lambda$ satisfies the displayed estimate. A <signed power test for a Laplacian eigenfunction> and the zero-boundary <Sobolev inequality> give a <Moser iteration> with exponents $p_j=2[n/(n-2)]^j$. The <geometric series> $\sum_j1/p_j=n/4$ gives the exact power of $\lambda$, while $\sum_j(\log p_j)/p_j<\infty$ controls the remaining constant. The constant depends only on dimension, because the zero-boundary <Sobolev inequality> follows by extension by zero to the whole space.

= Dirichlet Laplacian eigenvalue
{title2=$\lambda_j$}
{c}
{parent=Dirichlet Laplacian eigenfunction}

A Dirichlet Laplacian eigenvalue is a number $\lambda$ for which $-\Delta u=\lambda u$ has a nonzero solution with zero boundary trace. At such a value, the shifted operator $\Delta+\lambda$ is not invertible.

= Green second identity

= Green's second identity
{c}
{synonym}

Green’s second identity is the divergence theorem applied to phi grad psi minus psi grad phi.

= Boundary term at infinity in Green's second identity
{parent=Green second identity}
{title2=$\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{|x|=R}(u\partial_nv-v\partial_nu)dS=0$}

The whole-space form of <Green's second identity> requires the displayed boundary limit, together with the existence of the relevant volume integrals. Pointwise decay of the fields does not by itself control their normal derivatives or the growing sphere area. In three dimensions, sufficient power bounds are $u=O(r^{-a})$, $\nabla u=O(r^{-a-1})$, $v=O(r^{-b})$ and $\nabla v=O(r^{-b-1})$, with $a+b>1$: the surface integral is then $O(R^{1-a-b})$.

For a smooth counterexample to pointwise decay alone, take $u=(1+r^2)^{-1/2}$ and $v=\cos r\,(1+r^2)^{-1/2}$. Both are smooth radial functions on all of $\mathbb R^3$ and tend to zero. Nevertheless $u v'-v u'=-\sin r/(1+r^2)$, giving surface integral $-4\pi R^2\sin R/(1+R^2)$, which has no zero limit. Screened <Green functions> with exponential decay avoid this particular problem.

= Lie point symmetry
{parent=Partial differential equation}
{c}
{wiki}

A Lie point symmetry is a continuous transformation of independent and dependent variables that maps solutions of a differential equation to solutions.

= Infinitesimal generator of a Lie point symmetry
{parent=Lie point symmetry}

A one-parameter point transformation has infinitesimal generator
$$
V=\sum_i\xi^i(x,u)\partial_{x_i}+\eta(x,u)\partial_u.
$$
Its flow recovers the finite transformations.

= Prolongation of a Lie point symmetry
{parent=Lie point symmetry}

The prolongation of a point-symmetry generator is its induced vector field on derivatives of the dependent variable. A generator is a symmetry of a differential equation $F=0$ when its required prolongation sends $F$ to zero on the solution manifold.

= Second prolongation of a Lie point symmetry
{parent=Prolongation of a Lie point symmetry}

For $V=\xi^i\partial_{x_i}+\eta\partial_u$, the second prolongation adds coefficients for $u_i$ and $u_{ij}$ obtained by total differentiation:
$$
\eta_i=D_i\eta-u_jD_i\xi^j,
\qquad
\eta_{ij}=D_j\eta_i-u_{ik}D_j\xi^k.
$$

= Scaling symmetry
{parent=Lie point symmetry}

A scaling symmetry acts by weighted dilations of the independent and dependent variables. Its infinitesimal generator is a weighted sum of Euler vector fields such as $x\partial_x+pu\partial_u$.

= Scaling symmetry of a partial differential equation
{parent=Scaling symmetry}

A scaling is a symmetry when every term of the equation transforms with the same weight.

= Simultaneous spacetime scaling symmetry of the wave equation
{parent=Scaling symmetry of a partial differential equation}

The flow of $V=t\partial_t+x\partial_x$ is $(t,x,u)\mapsto(e^\epsilon t,e^\epsilon x,u)$. Its second prolongation scales both $u_{tt}$ and $u_{xx}$ with weight minus two, so it preserves $u_{tt}-u_{xx}=0$.

= Symmetry reduction of a partial differential equation
{parent=Lie point symmetry}

An invariant of a one-parameter symmetry group becomes a similarity variable that reduces a PDE to an ODE.

= Group-invariant solution
{parent=Symmetry reduction of a partial differential equation}

A group-invariant solution is constant along the prolonged symmetry orbits. For a scaling of two independent variables with fixed dependent variable, it depends only on a ratio such as $x/t$.

= Projective Lie symmetry of the potential Burgers equation
{parent=Symmetry reduction of a partial differential equation}

The vector field
$$
V=4t^2\partial_t+4tx\partial_x-(x^2+2t)\partial_u
$$
generates the local transformations
$$
T=\frac{t}{1-4\epsilon t},
\quad
X=\frac{x}{1-4\epsilon t},
\quad
U=u-\frac{\epsilon x^2}{1-4\epsilon t}
+\frac12\log(1-4\epsilon t).
$$
Its second prolongation sends $u_t-u_{xx}-u_x^2$ to $-8t$ times that expression, so it is a <Lie point symmetry> of the <potential Burgers equation>.

= Similarity variable
{parent=Symmetry reduction of a partial differential equation}

A similarity variable is constant along symmetry-group orbits and parametrizes group-invariant solutions.
