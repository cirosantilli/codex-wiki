= Dual space
{wiki}
{title2=$V^*=\operatorname{Hom}_F(V,F)$}

= Dual vector space
{synonym}

= Algebraic dual
{synonym}

The <dual space> of a <vector space> $V$ over a <field> $F$ is the <vector space> of all <linear functionals> $\varphi:V\to F$. Think of a <vector> as an input and a <linear functional> as a scalar-valued measurement of that input. For $V=\mathbb R^2$, the rules $(x,y)\mapsto x$, $(x,y)\mapsto y$, and $(x,y)\mapsto2x-y$ are different elements of the <dual space>. None needs a notion of <length> or <angle>.

Its operations combine measurements pointwise:
$$
(\varphi+\psi)(v)=\varphi(v)+\psi(v),\qquad
(a\varphi)(v)=a\varphi(v).
$$
The <dual basis> explains the finite-dimensional structure: specifying a <linear functional>'s values on a <basis> determines its value on every <vector>. Thus an $n$-dimensional <vector space> has an $n$-dimensional <dual space>. The spaces have the same <dimension>, but their elements play different roles: an input and a rule that measures an input. <Linear Algebra Done Right by Sheldon Axler (4th edition)>[Axler, Linear Algebra Done Right, fourth edition, §3F, definitions 3.109–3.112]{ref}.

This separation is useful when changing coordinates. The <dual pairing> $\varphi(v)$ must stay the same while the component lists for $v$ and $\varphi$ change in opposite ways. <Covector transformation under a change of basis> makes the compensation explicit. An <inner product> on a finite-dimensional real <vector space> gives one way to represent each <linear functional> by a <vector>, but that representation uses the chosen <inner product>; the <dual space> itself does not require one.

Here <dual space> means the <algebraic dual>: all <linear functionals>. For a <topological vector space>, the <continuous dual space> keeps only the <continuous linear functionals>. In infinite <dimension>, this can be a proper <vector subspace>; <discontinuous functional detected by vanishing sequence tails> gives an example. The distinction matters when a scalar-valued measurement must respond continuously to small input changes.

= Dual invariance under dense inclusion
{parent=Dual space}
{title2=$X'\cong Z'\quad(\overline Z=X)$}

If a normed subspace $Z$ is <dense> in a <normed vector space> $X$ and carries the inherited <norm>, restriction gives an onto linear <isometry> from the <dual space> of $X$ to that of $Z$. A bounded functional on $Z$ extends uniquely by limits along sequences from $Z$; its <norm> is unchanged because its inequality passes to the limit. Thus completion does not change the <continuous> dual. For example, the subspace of sequences with $\sum j|a_j|<\infty$, equipped with the unweighted $\ell^1$ <norm>, has dual $\ell^\infty$, since it contains the <dense subspace> of finitely supported sequences. The inherited <norm> matters; the weighted <norm> would give a different dual <norm>.

= Double dual
{parent=Dual space}
{title2=$V^{**}=\operatorname{Hom}_F(V^*,F)$}

= Algebraic double dual
{synonym}

The algebraic double dual of a <vector space> is the <dual space> of its <algebraic dual>. An element assigns a <scalar> linearly to each <linear functional> on the original space. The <bidual evaluation map> sends $v$ to the rule $\ell\mapsto\ell(v)$. It is a canonical <linear isomorphism> in finite <dimension>, unlike an identification of $V$ with $V^*$, which generally requires an additional choice. For spaces with topology, using the <continuous dual space> instead defines the topological bidual; that is a distinct construction in infinite <dimension>.

= Dual pairing
{parent=Dual space}
{title2=$\langle\varphi,v\rangle=\varphi(v)$}

= Evaluation pairing
{synonym}

The <dual pairing> evaluates a <linear functional> on a <vector>:
$$
\langle\varphi,v\rangle=\varphi(v),\qquad (\varphi,v)\in V^*\times V.
$$
It is <bilinear>: it is linear in each argument separately. Even over the <complex numbers>, this algebraic evaluation has no <complex conjugation>. An <inner product> instead has two <vectors> as inputs and, over the <complex numbers>, is <sesquilinear>. For $\varphi(x,y)=2x-y$ and $v=(3,1)$, the <dual pairing> equals five. Its value is independent of the <basis> used to describe its two inputs.

= Biorthogonal system
{parent=Dual pairing}
{title2=$f_i(x_j)=\delta_{ij}$}

For a <normed vector space> $X$, a family $(x_i,f_i)_{i\in I}$ in $X\times X^*$ is biorthogonal when the displayed Kronecker-delta equations hold. Its <vectors> are <linearly independent>: applying $f_i$ to a finite linear dependence isolates its $i$th coefficient. No inner product is required, and the family need not span a dense <vector subspace>[subspace]. If its <linear span> is dense, each prescribed coordinate functional is unique by <continuity>. For a countable total family, the finite sums $P_nx=\sum_{i\leq n}f_i(x)x_i$ are bounded <projection operators>, since biorthogonality gives $P_n^2=P_n$.

= Algebraic dual of a direct sum
{parent=Dual space}
{title2=$(\bigoplus_iV_i)^*\cong\prod_iV_i^*$}

Restricting a <linear functional> to each summand gives a family of functionals, with no finite-support restriction. Conversely an arbitrary such family acts on a vector by the finite sum of its nonzero coordinates. These constructions are inverse and prove the displayed algebraic <dual space> identity. A group action on the summands induces the product action by their algebraic contragredients.

= Bidual of a normed space
{parent=Dual space}
{title2=$X^{**}$}

= Bidual space
{synonym}

The bidual of a <normed vector space> $X$ is the continuous dual $X^{**}=(X^*)^*$. Evaluation defines the <canonical embedding into the bidual> $J_X(x)(f)=f(x)$. The <norm> inequality gives $\|J_Xx\|\le\|x\|$, and a norming functional from the <Hahn-Banach theorem> gives equality. Thus $J_X$ is isometric, and its image is dense in its closure, a <completion of a normed space>.

= Perfect pairing
{parent=Dual space}
{title2=$B:V\times W\to F$}

For finite-dimensional <vector spaces> $V,W$ over a <field> $F$, a bilinear pairing $B:V\times W\to F$ is perfect when the induced <linear maps> $V\to W^*$ and $W\to V^*$ are <isomorphisms>. Equivalently, both dimensions agree and the matrix of the pairing in any two bases is invertible. The evaluation pairing between a finite-dimensional <vector space> and its <dual space> is perfect. This notion allows two different spaces; a <nondegenerate bilinear form> is the case $V=W$.

= Separating subspace of an algebraic dual
{parent=Dual space}

= Separating subspaces of an algebraic dual
{synonym}

A <linear subspace> $W\subseteq X^*$ is separating if every nonzero $x\in X$ has $T(x)\ne0$ for some $T\in W$. For finite-dimensional $X$, an evaluation map $X\to F^{\dim W}$ is <injective>, so the <rank-nullity theorem> forces $\dim W\geq\dim X=\dim X^*$ and $W=X^*$. For the infinite-dimensional <polynomial> space, the span of its coefficient functionals is a proper separating <linear subspace> of the <dual space>, which contains all sequences of coefficients, not just finitely supported ones.

= Bidual evaluation map
{parent=Dual space}
{title2=$J(x)(T)=T(x)$}

For an algebraic <dual space> $X^*=\operatorname{Hom}_F(X,F)$, the <linear map> $J:X\to X^{**}$ sends $x$ to evaluation at $x$. It is <injective> because <linear functionals> separate nonzero vectors: extend any nonzero vector to a <basis> and use its coordinate functional. If $X$ has a finite <basis> $e_i$ with <dual basis> $e_i^*$, every $B\in X^{**}$ equals $J(\sum_iB(e_i^*)e_i)$, so $J$ is an <isomorphism>. For infinite-dimensional spaces, <surjection> does not follow from this finite sum argument.

= Covector
{parent=Dual space}
{title2=$\varphi\in V^*$}

A <covector> is an element of the <dual space>, hence a <linear functional> on a <vector space>. In finite-dimensional coordinates, it acts as a row multiplied by a <coordinate vector> column. The row $\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\end{pmatrix}$ acts on $\begin{pmatrix}3\\1\end{pmatrix}$ to give five. The terminology emphasizes its role as a scalar-valued measurement and its behavior under a <change of basis>.

For example, the <total derivative> of $f(x,y)=x^2+y^2$ at $(1,2)$ sends a small displacement $h$ to the linear change $df(h)=2h_x+4h_y$. This <covector> describes the first-order response. The <gradient> $\nabla f=(2,4)$ represents the same rule through the chosen Euclidean <inner product>. In <differential geometry>, the <cotangent space> collects these scalar-valued measurements of <tangent vectors>.

= Covector transformation under a change of basis
{parent=Covector}
{title2=$x'=B^{-1}x,\quad\ell'=\ell B$}

Let the columns of an <invertible matrix> $B$ be a new <basis> expressed in the old <basis>. The same <vector> has old <coordinate vector> $x$ and new <coordinate vector> $x'=B^{-1}x$. If a <covector> has old row $\ell$, its new row is $\ell'=\ell B$, since
$$
\ell'x'=\ell B B^{-1}x=\ell x.
$$
Thus the scalar <dual pairing> stays unchanged. If <covector> coefficients are stored as a column $c=\ell^T$, their new column is $c'=B^Tc$; this is the inverse transpose of the transformation $x\mapsto B^{-1}x$ on <vector> coordinates. These conventions express the <covariance and contravariance of vectors>. https://www.math.brown.edu/streil/papers/LADW/HTML_2026_04-30/Ch8.html[Sergei Treil, Linear Algebra Done Wrong, §8.1.1: coordinate changes in the dual space]{ref}.

For the <basis> $b_1=(1,0)$, $b_2=(1,1)$,
$$
B=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
x=\begin{pmatrix}3\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
x'=\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The <linear functional> $\varphi(x,y)=2x-y$ has rows $\ell=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\ell'=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\end{pmatrix}$. Both calculations give five: $2\cdot3-1\cdot1=2\cdot2+1\cdot1$. Changing the <basis> changes the descriptions, not the measured value.

= Linear functional
{parent=Dual space}
{wiki}
{title2=$\varphi:V\to F$}

= Linear form
{synonym}

A <linear functional> is a <linear map> from a <vector space> $V$ to its scalar <field> $F$:
$$
\varphi(av+bw)=a\varphi(v)+b\varphi(w),\qquad a,b\in F,\quad v,w\in V.
$$
Its output is one <scalar>, rather than an arbitrary <vector> in another space. In $\mathbb R^2$, $\varphi(x,y)=2x-y$ gives $\varphi(3,1)=5$, $\varphi(1,2)=0$, and $\varphi(4,3)=5$: adding the two inputs adds their measured values.

The <level sets> $\varphi(v)=c$ visualize what the measurement retains and discards. For this example they are parallel <straight lines> $y=2x-c$. Moving parallel to $(1,2)$ changes the input but leaves $\varphi$ unchanged. The zero <level set> is its <kernel of a linear map>, while the other <level sets> are translates of that <vector subspace>.

\Image[linear-functional-level-sets.png]
{title=A <linear functional> groups <vectors> into parallel <level sets> with the same measured value}

Inputs need not be geometric arrows. On the <vector space> of <polynomials> of <polynomial degree> at most two, evaluating at zero is a <linear functional>, as is taking the <definite integral> from zero to one. For $p(t)=a+bt+ct^2$, they give $p(0)=a$ and $\int_0^1p(t)\,dt=a+b/2+c/3$. Thus <polynomial evaluation> and <integration> are two different measurements of the same <polynomial>. Coordinate extraction is another measurement, developed under <dual basis>.

A <linear functional> is defined by <linearity>, not by <continuity>. In finite-dimensional real or complex <normed vector spaces> it is automatically continuous; in infinite <dimension> it need not be. The <continuous dual space> adds this requirement when small changes in the input should give small changes in the output.

= Minimizing a linear functional on a sphere
{parent=Linear functional}
{title2=$\min_{\|z\|=r}z\cdot w=-r\|w\|$}

For a nonzero Euclidean vector $w$, the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives $z\cdot w\geq-\|w\|$ when $\|z\|=1$. Equality is attained uniquely at $z=-w/\|w\|$. On a sphere of radius $r>0$ the minimizer is $-rw/\|w\|$, and the minimum is $-r\|w\|$. This turns many constrained vector problems into a normalization of one fixed vector. If $w=0$, every point minimizes the functional.

= Discontinuous functional detected by vanishing sequence tails
{parent=Linear functional}
{title2=$f(v_N)=1,\quad\|v_N\|_2\to0$}

If a <linear functional> on a <normed vector space> has $f(v_N)=1$ for a sequence tending to zero in norm, it is discontinuous and unbounded. An explicit example is the algebraic span in $\ell^2$ of the coordinate vectors and $w=(2^{-n})_{n\geq1}$. Define $f(w)=1$ and $f(e_n)=0$. The tails $v_N=w-\sum_{n=1}^N2^{-n}e_n$ have norm $2^{-N}/\sqrt3$ but image one. Thus the functional vanishes on the dense finite-support subspace while being nonzero elsewhere.

= Dual basis
{parent=Dual space}
{wiki}
{title2=$e_i^*(e_j)=\delta_{ij}$}

= Dual bases
{synonym}

For a <basis> $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ of a finite-dimensional <vector space> $V$, its <dual basis> consists of <linear functionals> $e_1^*,\ldots,e_n^*$ satisfying
$$
e_i^*(e_j)=\delta_{ij}.
$$
The <Kronecker delta> means that each rule gives one on its own <basis vector> and zero on the others. These rules extract coordinates:
$$
v=\sum_{i=1}^n e_i^*(v)e_i.
$$
Applying $e_i^*$ to a <linear combination> removes every term except the $i$th. Consequently the <dual basis> is a <basis> of the <dual space>, and every <linear functional> has expansion $\varphi=\sum_i\varphi(e_i)e_i^*$. <Linear Algebra Done Right by Sheldon Axler (4th edition)>[Axler, Linear Algebra Done Right, fourth edition, §3F, definitions and results 3.112–3.116]{ref}.

Coordinate extraction works for a nonorthogonal <basis> too. With $b_1=(1,0)$ and $b_2=(1,1)$, the <dual basis> is
$$
b_1^*(x,y)=x-y,\qquad b_2^*(x,y)=y.
$$
The checks $b_1^*(b_1)=1$, $b_1^*(b_2)=0$, $b_2^*(b_1)=0$, and $b_2^*(b_2)=1$ fix the two rules. For $v=(3,1)$ they give **coordinates $(2,1)$**, so $v=2b_1+b_2$. The usual <dot products> with $b_1,b_2$ instead give $3,4$ and do not extract these coordinates: simple projection formulas depend on an <orthonormal basis>.

\Image[dual-basis-skew-coordinates.png]
{title=The <dual basis> extracts coefficients in a skew <basis> by two scalar-valued measurements}

The same idea explains <polynomial interpolation>. On <polynomials> of <polynomial degree> at most $n$, evaluation at distinct <interpolation nodes> $x_0,\ldots,x_n$ gives the <dual basis> to the <Lagrange cardinal polynomials>, because $\ell_i(x_j)=\delta_{ij}$. Samples are coordinates in this <basis>, so the reconstruction $p=\sum_i p(x_i)\ell_i$ is a dual-basis expansion. <Differentiation weights from nodal evaluations> and <factorial monomial basis for polynomial differentiation> apply the same principle to different measurements.

= Coefficient forms from three polynomial evaluations
{parent=Dual basis}
{title2=$a=p(0),\quad b=[p(1)-p(-1)]/2,\quad c=[p(1)+p(-1)]/2-p(0)$}

For $p(X)=a+bX+cX^2$, the three <point evaluation functionals> at $0,1,-1$ form a <basis> of the <dual space> of real <polynomials> of degree at most two. The displayed recovery formulas express the coordinate functionals, hence the <dual basis> to $1,X,X^2$, as <linear combinations> of these evaluations. More generally distinct-node <polynomial interpolation> identifies values with <coefficient> data through an invertible <Vandermonde matrix>.

= Dual basis of cumulative coordinate sums
{parent=Dual basis}
{title2=$v_j=e_1+\cdots+e_j\implies v_i'=e_i'-e_{i+1}'\ (i<n),\quad v_n'=e_n'$}

For the <basis> $v_j=e_1+\cdots+e_j$ obtained from a <basis> $e_1,\ldots,e_n$, the <dual basis> is obtained by successive differences of the original <dual basis>. Indeed, if $x=\sum_jc_jv_j$, its original <coordinates> are $x_i=\sum_{j\geq i}c_j$. Hence $c_i=x_i-x_{i+1}$ for $i<n$, and $c_n=x_n$. These are precisely the new coordinate <linear functionals>. This gives an elementary example of the inverse relation between <change of basis> and <covector transformation under a change of basis>.

= Coordinate functional
{parent=Dual basis}
{title2=$e_i^*(\sum_j a_je_j)=a_i$}

A <coordinate functional> extracts one coefficient in a specified finite <basis>. It is an element of that <basis>'s <dual basis>, rather than a coordinate rule independent of the <basis>. For the <standard basis> of $F^n$, the $i$th <coordinate functional> sends $(a_1,\ldots,a_n)$ to $a_i$.

= Factorial monomial basis for polynomial differentiation
{parent=Dual basis}
{title2=$p_j(x)=x^j/j!$}

On polynomials of degree at most $n$, the derivative-evaluation functionals $e_j(\phi)=\phi^{(j)}(0)$ are dual to $p_j=x^j/j!$, since $e_i(p_j)=\delta_{ij}$. Differentiation maps $p_j$ to $p_{j-1}$, with $p_0$ mapped to zero. Its matrix is a single nilpotent Jordan block with ones above the diagonal, and its dual has the transpose matrix.

= Annihilator of a vector subspace
{parent=Dual space}
{title2=$U^\circ=\{\varphi\in V^*: \varphi|_U=0\}$}

= Annihilators of vector subspaces
{synonym}

For a <vector subspace> $U\leq V$,
$$
U^\circ=\{f\in V^*:f|_U=0\}.
$$
In finite <dimension>, $\dim U^\circ=\dim V-\dim U$.

An <annihilator of a vector subspace> collects the <linear functionals> that ignore every input from that <vector subspace>. For $U=\operatorname{span}\{(1,2)\}\subseteq\mathbb R^2$, a <linear functional> $\varphi(x,y)=ax+by$ vanishes on $U$ exactly when $a+2b=0$. Hence $U^\circ$ is the one-dimensional <span> of $(x,y)\mapsto2x-y$. The zero <level set> in the <linear functional> diagram is precisely $U$.

= Finite-dimensional subspace is an intersection of kernels
{parent=Annihilator of a vector subspace}
{title2=$U=\bigcap_{f\in U^0}\ker f$}

For a <vector subspace> $U$ of a finite-dimensional <vector space> $V$, its <annihilator of a vector subspace> $U^0\subseteq V^*$ determines $U$ exactly: $U=\bigcap_{f\in U^0}\ker f$. Indeed, a <basis> of $U$ can be extended to one of $V$. If $v\notin U$, include $v$ among the added basis vectors and define a <linear functional> taking value one at $v$ and zero on all the other basis vectors. This functional vanishes on $U$ but not at $v$, excluding every outside vector from the intersection.

It suffices to intersect the <kernel of a linear map>[kernels] of any <basis> $g_1,\ldots,g_k$ of $U^0$: every other annihilating functional is their <linear combination>. The <dual basis> for a basis of $V$ extending a basis of $U$ shows $k=\dim V-\dim U$. If $U=V$, the empty intersection is $V$. This translates a subspace description by spanning vectors into an exact description by independent linear equations.

= Invariant subspaces and annihilators
{parent=Annihilator of a vector subspace}
{title2=$T(U)\subseteq U\Longrightarrow T'(U^0)\subseteq U^0$}

For a <linear map> $T:V\to V$, an <invariant subspace> $U$ has an <annihilator of a vector subspace> $U^0$ invariant under the <dual map> $T'$. Indeed, for $\varphi\in U^0$ and $u\in U$,
$$
(T'\varphi)(u)=\varphi(Tu)=0.
$$
For a <finite-dimensional vector space>, the converse also holds. If $T'(U^0)\subseteq U^0$, every $\varphi\in U^0$ vanishes on $Tu$ for each $u\in U$. The <dual basis> description of $U^0$ shows that the common zero set of its <linear functionals> is exactly $U$, so $Tu\in U$. Thus
$$
\boxed{U\text{ is invariant under }T\iff U^0\text{ is invariant under }T'}.
$$
The <dimension> identity $\dim U^0=\dim V-\dim U$ exchanges a one-dimensional <invariant subspace> with an invariant <hyperplane> in the <dual space>.

= Transpose of a linear map
{parent=Dual space}
{wiki}
{title2=$T^*:W^*\to V^*,\quad T^*(\varphi)=\varphi\circ T$}

= Dual map
{synonym}

For a <linear map> $T:V\to W$, the <dual map> $T^*:W^*\to V^*$ sends a <linear functional> $\varphi$ on the output space to the input measurement $T^*(\varphi)=\varphi\circ T$. It reverses direction because a measurement of the output can be evaluated on an input by first applying $T$:
$$
(T^*\varphi)(v)=\varphi(Tv).
$$
For $T(x,y)=(x+y,2y)$ and $\varphi(u,v)=2u-v$, the input measurement is $T^*\varphi(x,y)=2(x+y)-2y=2x$. The output measurement ignores the part of this transformation contributed by $y$.

If $T$ has <matrix> $A$ in specified <bases>, a row representing $\varphi$ changes to $\ell A$. Storing the coefficients of a <linear functional> as a column in the corresponding <dual basis> gives $A^T\ell^T$, explaining why the <transpose of a linear map> is represented by the <matrix transpose>. This algebraic operation uses no <inner product>; it agrees with a matrix transpose even over the <complex numbers>, rather than automatically introducing <complex conjugation>. The <adjoint operator> is defined using an <inner product> and is a different construction. https://www.math.brown.edu/streil/papers/LADW/HTML_2026_04-30/Ch8.html[Treil, Linear Algebra Done Wrong, §8.3: dual transformations and transpose]{ref}.

In finite <dimension>, the <kernel> and image of the <dual map> are described by <annihilators of vector subspaces>:
$$
\ker T^*=(\operatorname{im}T)^\circ,
\qquad
\operatorname{im}T^*=(\ker T)^\circ.
$$

= Dual image and kernel annihilator identity
{parent=Transpose of a linear map}
{title2=$\operatorname{im}(\alpha^\vee)=(\ker\alpha)^\circ$}

For a <linear map> between finite-dimensional <vector spaces>, the image of its <dual map> is the <annihilator of a vector subspace> given by its kernel. Inclusion follows by composition. Conversely a <linear functional> vanishing on the kernel descends to the image and extends to the target by <basis extension>. The identity and the <rank-nullity theorem> show that a linear map and its dual have equal rank.

= Equality of row rank and column rank
{parent=Transpose of a linear map}

For the map represented by a matrix $A$, column rank is the rank of the map and row rank is the rank of its dual. Since the dual map has kernel equal to the annihilator of the original image, rank-nullity gives equal ranks.

= Simultaneous basis exchange from a nonzero minor
{parent=Equality of row rank and column rank}

If the columns $v_1,\ldots,v_m$ of an $n$ by $m$ matrix are independent, some $m$ by $m$ row minor is nonzero. A nonzero term of that minor's determinant selects distinct nonzero coordinates $f(i)$, and replacing the corresponding standard basis vectors $e_{f(i)}$ by the $v_i$ leaves a basis.

= Duals of a subspace and its quotient
{parent=Transpose of a linear map}

For a finite-dimensional vector space and $U\leq V$, dualizing the quotient and inclusion maps gives
$$
(V/U)^*\cong U^\circ,
\qquad
U^*\cong V^*/U^\circ.
$$

= Surjectivity of independent linear functionals
{parent=Dual space}

If $q_1,\ldots,q_n\in V^*$ are linearly independent, then
$$
x\longmapsto(q_1(x),\ldots,q_n(x))
$$
maps $V$ onto $F^n$. Otherwise a nonzero functional annihilating its proper image would give a nontrivial linear relation among the $q_j$.

= Polynomial moment functional
{parent=Dual space}

A polynomial moment functional evaluates a polynomial through a weighted integral, such as $p\mapsto\int p(t)w(t)dt$; finitely many moments determine it on a bounded-degree polynomial space.
