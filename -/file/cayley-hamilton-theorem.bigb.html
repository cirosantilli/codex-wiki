= Cayley-Hamilton theorem
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$\chi_A(A)=0$}

Every <square matrix> satisfies its own <characteristic polynomial>. More explicitly, for an $n$-by-$n$ <matrix> $A$ over a <commutative ring> with identity, form
$$
\chi_A(t)=\det(tI-A)=t^n+c_{n-1}t^{n-1}+\cdots+c_1t+c_0.
$$
Then
$$
\boxed{A^n+c_{n-1}A^{n-1}+\cdots+c_1A+c_0I=0.}
$$
Here $0$ is the <zero matrix>, and the constant term multiplies the <identity matrix>. This is a <polynomial evaluated at a matrix>; it is stronger than saying that each <eigenvalue> is a <root of a polynomial>. Neither <diagonalizability> nor distinct <eigenvalues> is required.<Universal Identities by Keith Conrad>[§3, Theorem 3.4 and the universal-identity discussion, printed pp. 6–7: matrices over arbitrary commutative rings]{ref}

The practical payoff is that arbitrarily high <matrix powers> can be reduced to a combination of $I,A,\ldots,A^{n-1}$. These powers arise when repeatedly applying a <linear map>, solving a <linear recurrence relation>, or evolving a finite <state space> model. The <characteristic polynomial> turns that repeated action into a fixed-order scalar recurrence. The <minimal polynomial> may reduce the number of required powers further.

= Polynomial evaluated at a matrix
{parent=Cayley-Hamilton theorem}
{title2=$p(A)=\sum_{j=0}^d a_jA^j$}

For a <polynomial> $p(t)=\sum_{j=0}^d a_jt^j$, substitute a fixed <square matrix> $A$ for $t$, taking $A^0=I$. In particular, $p(t)=t^2-3t+1$ becomes $p(A)=A^2-3A+I$, not $A^2-3A$ with the scalar $1$ added separately to each entry. <Polynomials> in the same <matrix> commute with each other, even though arbitrary <matrix multiplication> is <noncommutative>.

= Two-coefficient reduction of matrix powers
{parent=Cayley-Hamilton theorem}
{title2=$A^n=a_nA+b_nI$}

Consider the real <matrix>
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\chi_A(t)=(t-2)(t-1)-1=t^2-3t+1.
$$
The <Cayley-Hamilton theorem> gives $A^2=3A-I$. Consequently
$$
A^3=3A^2-A=8A-3I,\qquad
A^4=21A-8I,\qquad A^5=55A-21I.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{A^5=\begin{pmatrix}89&55\\55&34\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
All four entries come from just two scalar coefficients. If $A^n=a_nA+b_nI$, multiplying once by $A$ and replacing $A^2$ gives the <linear recurrence relation>
$$
a_{n+1}=3a_n+b_n,\qquad b_{n+1}=-a_n,
\qquad(a_0,b_0)=(0,1).
$$
Equivalently $a_{n+2}=3a_{n+1}-a_n$, with $a_0=0,a_1=1$. The appearance of <Fibonacci numbers> is explained by $A=Q^2$, where $Q=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ advances the <Fibonacci recurrence>. No <eigenvectors> need to be computed.

\Image[cayley-hamilton-matrix-power-reduction.png]
{title=Cayley–Hamilton reduces powers of a chosen two-by-two matrix to two integer coefficients; the fifth power is 55A − 21I}

The same identity computes an inverse. Rearrange it as $A(3I-A)=I$, giving
$$
\boxed{A^{-1}=3I-A=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
This works because the constant term of this <characteristic polynomial> is nonzero and the coefficients lie in a <field>. Generally, when $c_0\ne0$ over a <field>, rearranging the <Cayley-Hamilton theorem> expresses $A^{-1}$ as a <polynomial evaluated at a matrix>. Over a general <commutative ring>, a nonzero constant need not be a <unit>; invertibility requires that extra condition.

= Polynomial reduction of matrix powers
{parent=Cayley-Hamilton theorem}
{title2=$f=q\chi_A+r\Longrightarrow f(A)=r(A)$}

By <polynomial division>, write $f(t)=q(t)\chi_A(t)+r(t)$ with $\deg r<n$. Because $\chi_A$ is a <monic polynomial>, this division works even over a <commutative ring>. Evaluating at $A$ makes the multiple of $\chi_A(A)$ vanish, leaving $f(A)=r(A)$. This explains the reduction in <two-coefficient reduction of matrix powers> without multiplying out every intermediate <matrix>.

Over a <field>, the <minimal polynomial> $m_A$ divides $\chi_A$ and gives the strongest such universal reduction: use the <polynomial remainder> modulo $m_A$ instead. For the <scalar matrix> $2I_2$, the <characteristic polynomial> is $(t-2)^2$, while the <minimal polynomial> is only $t-2$. Every <matrix power> is then a scalar multiple of $I_2$.

= Repeated eigenvalues in the Cayley-Hamilton theorem
{parent=Cayley-Hamilton theorem}
{title2=$B=2I+N,\quad N^2=0$}

Take a different real <matrix>,
$$
B=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}=2I+N,
\qquad N=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its only <eigenvalue> is $2$, with <algebraic multiplicity> two. However, the <polynomial> $q(t)=t-2$, which vanishes at that <eigenvalue>, gives $q(B)=N\ne0$. Merely checking values at <eigenvalues> cannot prove a matrix identity. The full <characteristic polynomial> does work:
$$
\chi_B(t)=(t-2)^2,\qquad \chi_B(B)=N^2=0.
$$
The <matrix> is a <Jordan block>, and its <minimal polynomial> is also $(t-2)^2$.

The <binomial theorem> gives, for positive <integers> $n$,
$$
\boxed{B^n=2^nI+n2^{n-1}N.}
$$
Terms containing $N^2$ or a higher <matrix power> vanish. After removing the common growth factor $2^n$, the remaining <linear map> is the <shear mapping> $I+(n/2)N$. It moves $(x,y)$ to $(x+ny/2,y)$. The single <eigenvalue> controls the exponential scale, while the <nilpotent matrix> adds a factor growing linearly in $n$.

\Image[cayley-hamilton-jordan-block-shear.png]
{title=Normalized powers of a Jordan block retain a growing shear despite having only one eigenvalue; equal axes scales show the top edge moving by n/2}

In terms of <polynomial interpolation>, reduction modulo $(t-2)^2$ retains both a value and a <derivative>:
$$
f(B)=f(2)I+f'(2)N.
$$
Indeed, <polynomial division> gives $f(t)=f(2)+f'(2)(t-2)+(t-2)^2h(t)$. This is a simple <Hermite interpolation> condition. For $f(t)=t^n$, it gives exactly the displayed <matrix power>. Repeated <eigenvalues> retain information about how a <polynomial> changes, not just where it vanishes.

= Cayley's matrix memoir
{c}
{parent=Cayley-Hamilton theorem}

<Arthur Cayley>'s 1858 paper A Memoir on the Theory of Matrices motivates <matrices> as an abbreviated way to write <systems of linear equations>. Its opening already connects the characteristic equation with reducing <polynomials> in a <matrix> to lower degree. In §21 he works out the two-by-two identity; in §23 he says he has verified the three-by-three case but does not give a formal proof for every size. Thus this source distinguishes the general statement from a general proof.<A Memoir on the Theory of Matrices by Arthur Cayley>[Printed pp. 17–18, opening motivation; pp. 23–24, §§21–25, characteristic equation and reduction of matrix functions; received 10 December 1857, read 14 January 1858, published 1858]{ref} The theorem's name also commemorates <William Rowan Hamilton>.

= Cayley-Hamilton theorem from cyclic subspaces
{parent=Cayley-Hamilton theorem}
{c}
{title2=$\chi_T(T)=0$}

Over a <field>, a <cyclic vector> gives a <companion matrix>, whose characteristic relation annihilates its generator and hence every iterate. If there is no <cyclic vector> spanning the whole space, a nonzero proper <cyclic subspace> is invariant. Induction annihilates the restriction and the quotient; the quotient polynomial first sends the whole space into the invariant subspace, and the restriction polynomial then kills it. The block-triangular <characteristic polynomial> is the product of these two polynomials.
