= Astrophysics
{wiki}

Astrophysics applies physical laws to stars, planets, galaxies, radiation, and the universe.

= Luminosity function
{disambiguate=astronomy}
{parent=Astrophysics}

= Luminosity function
{synonym}

An astronomical luminosity function gives the number density of objects per luminosity interval, often also per comoving volume. Integrating luminosity times the function gives their luminosity density.

= Astronomical magnitude
{parent=Astrophysics}
{wiki}

Astronomical magnitude is a logarithmic measure of the brightness of an astronomical source; a smaller magnitude denotes a brighter source.

= Apparent magnitude
{title2=$m$}
{parent=Astronomical magnitude}
{wiki}

Apparent magnitude measures how bright an astronomical source appears to an observer.

= Absolute magnitude
{title2=$M$}
{parent=Astronomical magnitude}
{wiki}

Absolute magnitude is the <apparent magnitude> that an astronomical source would have at a conventional reference distance. It separates intrinsic luminosity from distance-dependent dimming.

= Distance modulus
{title2=$\mu$}
{parent=Astronomical magnitude}
{wiki}

The distance modulus is the difference between <apparent magnitude> and <absolute magnitude>. For distance $d$ in parsecs,
$$
\mu=m-M=5\log_{10}(d/10\,\mathrm{pc}).
$$

= Exoplanet
{parent=Astrophysics}
{wiki}

An exoplanet is a planet orbiting a star other than the Sun.

= Exoplanet atmosphere
{parent=Exoplanet}
{wiki}

An exoplanet atmosphere controls the planet's spectrum, heat redistribution, chemistry, clouds, and atmospheric escape.

= Lapse rate
{title2=$\Gamma$}
{disambiguate=atmosphere}
{parent=Exoplanet atmosphere}
{wiki}

= Atmospheric lapse rate
{synonym}

The atmospheric lapse rate is the decrease of temperature with altitude, $\Gamma=-dT/dz$. In an ideal gas with constant gravity, a constant lapse rate produces a power-law relation between temperature and pressure.

= Irradiated planetary atmosphere
{parent=Exoplanet atmosphere}

An irradiated planetary atmosphere receives stellar energy from above while internal heat emerges from below. Their competition creates radiative, convective, and sometimes thermally inverted regions.

= Single-layer greenhouse model
{parent=Irradiated planetary atmosphere}

A single-layer greenhouse model represents an atmosphere by one uniform layer that absorbs and emits a fraction of thermal radiation. With infrared emissivity $\epsilon$, the layer emits $\epsilon\sigma T_a^4$ both upward and downward.

= Planetary equilibrium temperature
{title2=$T_{\rm eq}$}
{parent=Irradiated planetary atmosphere}

For a planet around a star of radius $R_*$ and temperature $T_*$, global blackbody reradiation gives
$$
T_{\rm eq}^4=T_{\rm int}^4
+(1-A_B)T_*^4\frac{R_*^2}{4a^2}.
$$
The denominator is $2a^2$ for uniform dayside-only reradiation, and a general heat-redistribution factor can interpolate between these assumptions.

= Semi-grey irradiated atmosphere
{parent=Irradiated planetary atmosphere}

A semi-grey atmosphere uses separate mean visible and infrared opacities. Their ratio $\gamma=\kappa_{\rm vis}/\kappa_{\rm IR}$ controls where stellar energy is deposited.

= Irradiation temperature
{title2=$T_{\rm irr}$}
{parent=Semi-grey irradiated atmosphere}

The irradiation temperature is defined from the incident stellar flux, commonly by $T_{\rm irr}=T_*\sqrt{R_*/a}$ before redistribution and albedo factors are applied.

= Internal effective temperature of a planet
{title2=$T_{\rm int}$}
{parent=Semi-grey irradiated atmosphere}

The internal effective temperature parametrizes the intrinsic cooling luminosity of a planet through $F_{\rm int}=\sigma T_{\rm int}^4$.

= Atmospheric thermal inversion
{parent=Semi-grey irradiated atmosphere}
{wiki=Inversion_(meteorology)}

= Temperature inversion
{synonym}

An atmospheric thermal inversion is a region where temperature increases outward. In a strongly irradiated atmosphere it can arise when shortwave opacity exceeds thermal infrared opacity and stellar energy is absorbed high in the atmosphere.

= Spectral-line emission from an atmospheric thermal inversion
{parent=Atmospheric thermal inversion}

A strong-opacity line forms higher than the nearby continuum. Under the Eddington-Barbier approximation, an outward-decreasing temperature therefore makes an absorption line, while an <atmospheric thermal inversion> makes the line-forming layer hotter and reverses it into emission.

= Radiative-convective boundary
{title2=RCB}
{parent=Irradiated planetary atmosphere}
{wiki}

The radiative-convective boundary is where radiative transport first requires a temperature gradient steeper than the adiabatic gradient, causing convection below.

= Radiative temperature gradient
{title2=$\nabla_{\rm rad}$}
{parent=Radiative-convective boundary}

The radiative temperature gradient is the logarithmic pressure gradient $\nabla_{\rm rad}=d\log T/d\log P$ required to carry the flux by radiation. Convection begins when it exceeds the relevant adiabatic gradient.

= Adiabatic temperature gradient
{title2=$\nabla_{\rm ad}$}
{parent=Radiative-convective boundary}

The adiabatic temperature gradient is $\nabla_{\rm ad}=(\partial\log T/\partial\log P)_s$. For a perfect gas with constant adiabatic index, $\nabla_{\rm ad}=(\gamma-1)/\gamma$.

= Exoplanet secondary eclipse
{parent=Exoplanet atmosphere}
{wiki=Methods_of_detecting_exoplanets#Secondary_eclipse}

During secondary eclipse the planet passes behind its star. The lost system light measures the planet-star flux ratio from reflected starlight and planetary thermal emission.

= Geometric albedo
{title2=$A_g$}
{parent=Exoplanet secondary eclipse}
{wiki}

Geometric albedo compares a body's full-phase brightness with that of a flat, perfectly diffusing reference disc of the same cross-section.

= Bond albedo
{title2=$A_B$}
{c}
{parent=Geometric albedo}
{wiki}

Bond albedo is the fraction of total incident power reflected in all directions and over all wavelengths.

= Lambertian surface
{c}
{parent=Geometric albedo}
{wiki=Lambert's_cosine_law}

A Lambertian surface has direction-independent radiance. A perfectly reflecting Lambertian patch receiving normal flux $F$ emits specific intensity $I=F/\pi$ into its outward hemisphere.

= Thermal eclipse depth
{parent=Exoplanet secondary eclipse}

For blackbody star and planet, the monochromatic thermal eclipse depth is
$$
\frac{F_p}{F_*}=\left(\frac{R_p}{R_*}\right)^2
\frac{B_\lambda(T_p)}{B_\lambda(T_*)}.
$$

= Exoplanet transmission spectrum
{parent=Exoplanet atmosphere}
{wiki=Transit_spectroscopy}

An exoplanet transmission spectrum measures the wavelength-dependent area blocked as starlight passes tangentially through the atmosphere during transit.

= Atmospheric scale height
{title2=$H$}
{parent=Exoplanet transmission spectrum}
{wiki=Scale_height}

For an isothermal ideal-gas atmosphere, $H=k_BT/(\mu m_Hg)$. A strong transmission feature spanning $N_H$ scale heights has approximate transit-depth amplitude $2R_pN_HH/R_*^2$.

= Isothermal pressure-level transit radius
{parent=Atmospheric scale height}

If an isothermal thin atmosphere has surface radius $R_p$, surface pressure $P_0$, and nearly constant gravity, the radius of a pressure level $P$ is
$$
R(P)=R_p+H\log\frac{P_0}{P},
\qquad
H=\frac{k_BT}{\mu m_Hg}.
$$

= Mass of a thin hydrostatic atmosphere
{parent=Atmospheric scale height}

The column mass between pressures $P_0$ and $P_{\rm top}$ is $(P_0-P_{\rm top})/g$. A thin atmosphere over a spherical planet therefore has
$$
M_{\rm atm}\simeq\frac{4\pi R_p^2(P_0-P_{\rm top})}{g}
\simeq\frac{4\pi R_p^4P_0}{GM_p}
$$
when its top pressure is negligible.

= Scattering slope of a transmission spectrum
{parent=Exoplanet transmission spectrum}

If extinction scales as $\sigma\propto\lambda^\gamma$, an isothermal hydrostatic atmosphere has $dR_p/d\log\lambda=\gamma H$. The measured continuum slope can therefore estimate atmospheric temperature when gravity and mean molecular mass are known.

= Day-night terminator
{parent=Exoplanet transmission spectrum}

The day-night terminator is the boundary between the illuminated and dark hemispheres of a planet. During transit, stellar rays probe morning and evening limbs that can have different temperatures, clouds, and compositions.

= Two-limb transmission spectrum
{parent=Day-night terminator}

If the morning and evening limbs have effective radii $R_m(\lambda)$ and $R_e(\lambda)$ and each occupies half the annulus, the transit area is $\pi[R_m^2(\lambda)+R_e^2(\lambda)]/2$.

= Exoplanet emission spectrum
{parent=Exoplanet atmosphere}
{wiki}

An exoplanet emission spectrum is the wavelength-dependent thermal radiation emerging from the planet. Vertical temperature gradients turn opacity differences into absorption or emission features.

= Brightness temperature
{title2=$T_b$}
{parent=Exoplanet emission spectrum}
{wiki}

The brightness temperature at a frequency or wavelength is the temperature for which a blackbody has the measured specific intensity there: $I_\nu=B_\nu(T_b)$. It need not equal a physical temperature shared by the entire emitting object.

= Exoplanet cloud
{parent=Exoplanet atmosphere}

An exoplanet cloud is a suspended condensate aerosol that scatters or absorbs radiation. Its particle sizes, composition, vertical extent, and horizontal coverage shape transmission, reflection, and thermal-emission spectra.

= Exoplanet cloud deck
{parent=Exoplanet cloud}

An exoplanet cloud deck is an optically thick cloud layer whose top pressure fixes the deepest level visible over a wavelength interval. A high deck can truncate a transmission spectrum and mute molecular features.

= Atmospheric haze
{parent=Exoplanet cloud}
{wiki=Haze}

Atmospheric haze consists of small suspended particles, often produced photochemically. A haze can flatten molecular features or produce a wavelength-dependent scattering slope.

= Exoplanet atmospheric dynamics
{parent=Exoplanet atmosphere}

Exoplanet atmospheric dynamics includes day-night circulation, zonal jets, waves, vortices, storms, and heat redistribution under irradiation, rotation, drag, and magnetic effects.

= Day-night heat redistribution
{parent=Exoplanet atmospheric dynamics}

Day-night heat redistribution transports absorbed stellar energy from an irradiated hemisphere to a dark hemisphere through atmospheric or oceanic motion. Its efficiency controls the thermal phase-curve amplitude and nightside temperature of a synchronously rotating planet.

= Nightside equilibrium temperature
{title2=$T_n$}
{parent=Day-night heat redistribution}

If a fraction $\epsilon$ of total absorbed stellar power reaches the night side, blackbody balance gives
$$
T_n=T_*\sqrt{\frac{R_*}{a}}
\left[\frac{\epsilon(1-A_B)}2\right]^{1/4}.
$$

= Atmospheric superrotation
{parent=Exoplanet atmospheric dynamics}
{wiki=Super-rotation}

Atmospheric superrotation is an eastward equatorial jet whose angular momentum per unit mass exceeds that of the underlying solid body at the equator. Hot-Jupiter waves can transport angular momentum into the equatorial region and maintain such a jet.

= Disequilibrium chemistry in an exoplanet atmosphere
{parent=Exoplanet atmosphere}

Atmospheric chemistry is out of equilibrium when reaction times exceed transport or photochemical times. Vertical quenching, ultraviolet photochemistry, and atmospheric escape can then maintain abundances far from local thermochemical equilibrium.

= Atmospheric photochemistry
{parent=Disequilibrium chemistry in an exoplanet atmosphere}

Atmospheric photochemistry begins when a molecule absorbs a photon and dissociates or ionizes. The products can drive reaction networks whose steady abundances differ strongly from thermochemical equilibrium.

= Photochemical steady state
{parent=Atmospheric photochemistry}

A photochemical steady state has production and loss rates balanced for each short-lived species even though individual photochemical reactions continue.

= Chapman ozone equilibrium
{c}
{parent=Atmospheric photochemistry}

The Chapman ozone mechanism combines oxygen photodissociation, three-body ozone formation, ozone photodissociation, and the reaction between atomic oxygen and ozone. Under local steady state and dominant photolytic ozone loss,
$$
[{\rm O}_3]\simeq[{\rm O}_2]
\left(\frac{K_2J_1[{\rm M}]}{K_4J_3}\right)^{1/2}.
$$

= Ozone layer
{parent=Chapman ozone equilibrium}
{wiki}

An ozone layer is an atmospheric altitude range with enhanced ozone abundance. Oxygen-dissociating ultraviolet radiation is strongest aloft while three-body formation is fastest at greater density, so their competition can concentrate ozone at intermediate altitude.

= Eddy mixing time
{title2=$\tau_{\rm mix}\simeq H^2/K_{zz}$}
{parent=Disequilibrium chemistry in an exoplanet atmosphere}

The eddy mixing time over one atmospheric scale height is $\tau_{\rm mix}\simeq H^2/K_{zz}$, where $K_{zz}$ is the vertical eddy diffusion coefficient.

= Chemical quench level
{parent=Disequilibrium chemistry in an exoplanet atmosphere}

A chemical quench level is where a reaction time equals the atmospheric transport time. Above it, transport is faster and carries an approximately fixed deeper abundance faster than local chemistry can restore equilibrium.

= Carbon monoxide–methane quenching
{title2=$\mathrm{CO}\rightleftharpoons\mathrm{CH}_4$ quenching}
{parent=Chemical quench level}

Carbon monoxide–methane quenching freezes the CO-to-CH4 abundance ratio near the level where their interconversion time first exceeds the vertical mixing time.

= Nitrogen–ammonia quenching
{title2=$\mathrm{N}_2\rightleftharpoons\mathrm{NH}_3$ quenching}
{parent=Chemical quench level}

Nitrogen–ammonia quenching similarly preserves a deeper N2-to-NH3 ratio above the level where chemical conversion becomes slower than atmospheric mixing.

= Atmospheric metallicity of a giant planet
{parent=Exoplanet atmosphere}

Atmospheric metallicity measures enrichment in elements heavier than hydrogen and helium relative to the host star. Solar-System giant planets suggest decreasing enrichment with increasing planetary mass, although clouds, condensation, and formation history complicate comparisons.

{parent=Exoplanet atmosphere}

An exoplanet atmospheric observatory characterizes planetary spectra through transit, eclipse, phase-curve, high-dispersion, or direct-imaging observations.

The Hubble Space Telescope provides ultraviolet, visible, and near-infrared transit and eclipse spectroscopy and established many early detections of atoms, aerosols, and atmospheric escape.

The James Webb Space Telescope combines a 6.5-metre aperture with stable near- and mid-infrared spectroscopy. Its instruments measure transit, eclipse, and phase-curve spectra across multiple molecular bands.

= Ariel space telescope

Ariel is an ESA mission designed to survey the visible and infrared atmospheres of about one thousand planets through transit and eclipse spectroscopy.

The Extremely Large Telescope combines a 39-metre-class aperture, adaptive optics, direct imaging, and high-dispersion spectroscopy to characterize giant and terrestrial exoplanets.

= Exoplanet direct imaging
{parent=Exoplanet atmospheric observatory}

= Directly imaged exoplanet
{synonym}

Exoplanet direct imaging spatially separates planetary light from host-star light. It is most sensitive to young, hot, self-luminous giant planets on wide orbits around nearby stars.

= Atmospheric escape
{parent=Exoplanet atmosphere}
{wiki}

Atmospheric escape removes gas from a planet through thermal motion, hydrodynamic outflow, photochemistry, and interactions with stellar radiation and plasma.

= Jeans escape parameter
{title2=$\lambda$}
{c}
{parent=Atmospheric escape}

For particles of mass $m$ at radius $r$ and temperature $T$, the Jeans escape parameter is
$$
\lambda=\frac{GM_pm}{k_BTr}.
$$
Large $\lambda$ gives weak tail escape, while order-unity $\lambda$ indicates hydrodynamic blow-off.

= Jeans escape flux
{c}
{parent=Jeans escape parameter}

For exobase number density $n_e$ and $v_{\rm th}=\sqrt{2k_BT/m}$, the collisionless Jeans number flux is
$$
\Phi_J=\frac{n_ev_{\rm th}}{2\sqrt\pi}(1+\lambda)e^{-\lambda}.
$$

= Hydrodynamic atmospheric escape
{parent=Atmospheric escape}
{wiki=Hydrodynamic_escape}

Hydrodynamic atmospheric escape is a collisional transonic outflow in which bulk gas carries multiple species away. It replaces the hydrostatic Jeans-tail description when gravity is weak compared with thermal energy.

= Energy-limited atmospheric escape
{title2=$\dot M=\eta\pi R_{\rm EUV}^2F_{\rm EUV}R_p/(GM_p)$}
{parent=Hydrodynamic atmospheric escape}

Energy-limited atmospheric escape equates a fraction $\eta$ of absorbed high-energy stellar power to the gravitational work needed to remove gas. Ignoring tidal and cooling corrections gives $\dot M=\eta\pi R_{\rm EUV}^2F_{\rm EUV}R_p/(GM_p)$.

= Exoplanet habitability
{parent=Exoplanet}
{wiki}

Exoplanet habitability concerns whether planetary conditions could sustain liquid water and life, while recognizing that observability and habitability are distinct questions.

= Circumstellar habitable zone
{title2=HZ}
{parent=Exoplanet habitability}
{wiki}

The circumstellar habitable zone is the orbital range in which a specified planet and atmosphere can maintain surface liquid water. Its boundaries depend on stellar spectrum, albedo, greenhouse physics, clouds, planetary mass, and atmospheric inventory.

= Runaway greenhouse
{parent=Circumstellar habitable zone}
{wiki}

A runaway greenhouse occurs when outgoing longwave radiation reaches a limiting value while absorbed stellar energy is larger, causing ocean evaporation and rapid surface warming.

= Maximum greenhouse outer habitable-zone limit
{parent=Circumstellar habitable zone}

At the maximum-greenhouse limit, adding carbon dioxide no longer warms efficiently because condensation and Rayleigh scattering offset its greenhouse effect. This defines a common conservative outer habitable-zone boundary.

= Hycean planet
{c}
{parent=Exoplanet habitability}
{wiki}

A Hycean planet is a proposed sub-Neptune with a hydrogen-rich atmosphere above a liquid-water ocean. The category is a model hypothesis rather than evidence of habitation.

= Exoplanet biosignature
{parent=Exoplanet habitability}
{wiki=Biosignature}

An exoplanet biosignature is an observable gas, surface feature, temporal variation, or chemical disequilibrium whose biological explanation survives plausible abiotic alternatives and environmental context.

= Atmospheric spectral-feature amplitude
{parent=Exoplanet habitability}

For transmission through $N_H$ scale heights, a useful estimate is
$$
\Delta D\simeq\frac{2R_pN_HH}{R_*^2}.
$$
Small host stars, low gravity, high temperature, and low mean molecular mass make atmospheric features larger.

= Exoplanet interior
{parent=Exoplanet}

An exoplanet interior is inferred from mass, radius, age, irradiation, and atmospheric composition. Distinct mixtures of metal, rock, water, and hydrogen-helium can share the same bulk mass and radius.

= Two-layer constant-density planet
{parent=Exoplanet interior}

A two-layer constant-density planet has a uniform core density $\rho_1$ and uniform mantle density $\rho_2$. Hydrostatic pressure follows by integrating $dP/dr=-G\rho(r)M(r)/r^2$ separately in each layer and matching pressure at their boundary.

= Exoplanet interior-composition degeneracy
{parent=Exoplanet interior}

The interior-composition degeneracy is the failure of mass and radius alone to determine unique layer mass fractions. Atmospheric mean molecular mass and composition, stellar abundances, tidal response, and thermal evolution add independent constraints.

= Mass-radius relation
{parent=Exoplanet interior}
{wiki}

A mass-radius relation compares the characteristic radius of a class of self-gravitating objects with mass. Its form records the equation of state and composition.

= Planetary mass-radius relation
{parent=Mass-radius relation}

= Planetary mass-radius curve
{synonym}

A planetary mass-radius relation compares the sizes of bodies at a given mass. Rock compression, volatile layers, gaseous envelopes, thermal entropy, irradiation, and electron degeneracy produce distinct branches and substantial composition degeneracy.

= Hot-Jupiter radius inflation
{c}
{parent=Planetary mass-radius relation}

Hot-Jupiter radius inflation is the anomalously large radius of many strongly irradiated gas giants relative to isolated cooling models. Delayed cooling and energy deposition by tides, atmospheric circulation, or electrical currents are leading contributions.

= Exoplanet bulk-composition ternary diagram
{parent=Exoplanet}

A bulk-composition ternary diagram represents the mass fractions of three planetary components at a point whose barycentric coordinates sum to one.

= Radiative transfer
{parent=Astrophysics}
{wiki}

Radiative transfer describes emission, absorption, and scattering of radiation through matter.

= Constant flux in a plane-parallel radiative-equilibrium atmosphere
{parent=Radiative transfer}

Taking the first angular moment of the radiative-transfer equation gives $dF_\nu/dz=4\pi\alpha_\nu(S_\nu-J_\nu)$. Radiative equilibrium sets the frequency integral of the right-hand side to zero, so the bolometric flux is constant with depth.

= Planck law
{c}
{parent=Radiative transfer}
{wiki=Planck's_law}

The Planck spectral radiance of a blackbody is
$$
B_\lambda(T)=\frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}
\frac1{e^{hc/(\lambda k_BT)}-1}.
$$

= Planck function
{title2=$B_\nu(T)$}
{c}
{parent=Planck law}

The Planck function is the equilibrium spectral specific intensity of a blackbody. It can be written per unit frequency or per unit wavelength, with $B_\nu\,d\nu=-B_\lambda\,d\lambda$ under the corresponding change of variable.

= Radiation constant
{title2=$a_{\rm rad}$}
{parent=Planck law}
{wiki}

The radiation constant relates equilibrium radiation energy density to temperature by $u=a_{\rm rad}T^4$. It satisfies $a_{\rm rad}=4\sigma/c$ in terms of the Stefan-Boltzmann constant.

= Rayleigh-Jeans law
{c}
{parent=Planck law}
{wiki}

The Rayleigh-Jeans law is the long-wavelength, low-frequency limit of <Planck law>. When $hc/(\lambda k_BT)\ll1$,
$$
B_\lambda(T)\simeq\frac{2ck_BT}{\lambda^4}.
$$

= Wien approximation
{c}
{parent=Planck law}
{wiki=Wien_approximation}

= Wien limit
{c}
{synonym}

The Wien approximation is the short-wavelength limit of the Planck law:
$$
B_\lambda(T)\simeq\frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}
e^{-hc/(\lambda k_BT)}.
$$

= Wien's displacement law
{c}
{parent=Planck law}
{wiki}

= Wien displacement law
{c}
{synonym}

For a <blackbody>, the wavelength at which $B_\lambda(T)$ is largest obeys
$$
\lambda_{\max}T=b,
$$
where $b$ is <Wien displacement constant>.

= Wien displacement constant
{c}
{title2=$b$}
{parent=Wien's displacement law}
{wiki}

The <Wien displacement constant> is the <constant of proportionality> $b$ in $\lambda_{\max}T=b$.

= Blackbody
{parent=Radiative transfer}
{wiki}

A <blackbody> absorbs all incident <electromagnetic radiation>. In <thermal equilibrium> it emits the universal <Planck law> spectrum determined solely by its <temperature>.

= Luminosity of a spherical blackbody
{parent=Blackbody}

An isotropic blackbody surface has outward flux $F=\pi\int B_\nu\,d\nu=\sigma T^4$. A sphere of radius $R$ therefore has luminosity
$$
L=4\pi R^2\sigma T^4.
$$

= Thermal bremsstrahlung
{parent=Radiative transfer}

Thermal bremsstrahlung is free-free electromagnetic radiation from the acceleration of charged particles during Coulomb encounters in a thermal plasma. Its frequency-integrated emissivity in a fully ionized, nonrelativistic plasma scales approximately as $n_en_iT^{1/2}$.

= Specific intensity
{title2=$I_\nu$}
{parent=Radiative transfer}
{wiki}

Specific intensity is power per projected area, solid angle, and frequency interval. In vacuum it is conserved along a ray.

= Optical depth
{title2=$\tau_\nu$}
{parent=Radiative transfer}
{wiki}

Optical depth satisfies $d\tau_\nu=\alpha_\nu ds$ along a path and measures attenuation through $I_\nu\propto e^{-\tau_\nu}$ in the absence of emission.

= Optically thin medium
{parent=Optical depth}

= Optically thin
{synonym}

An optically thin medium has $\tau\ll1$, so attenuation and emission may be expanded to first order in optical depth.

= Absorption coefficient
{title2=$\alpha_\nu$}
{parent=Optical depth}
{wiki}

The absorption coefficient is the fractional reduction of beam intensity per unit path length: $dI_\nu/ds=-\alpha_\nu I_\nu$ when emission and scattering are absent.

= Dust optical depth
{title2=$\tau_d$}
{parent=Optical depth}

For dust-grain number density $n_d$ and absorption cross-section $\sigma_d$, the dust optical depth along a path is $\tau_d=\int n_d\sigma_d\,ds$.

= Mass absorption coefficient
{title2=$\kappa_\nu$}
{parent=Dust optical depth}
{wiki}

The mass absorption coefficient is absorbing cross-section per unit mass. For identical spherical dust grains of radius $a$, mass $m_d$, and absorption efficiency $Q_\nu$, it is $\kappa_\nu=\pi a^2Q_\nu/m_d$.

= Optically thin dust-mass estimator
{title2=$M_d=F_\nu D^2/[\kappa_\nu B_\nu(T)]$}
{parent=Mass absorption coefficient}

For isothermal <optically thin> dust at distance $D$, its measured spectral flux density determines its mass through $M_d=F_\nu D^2/[\kappa_\nu B_\nu(T)]$. Temperature and opacity uncertainty commonly dominate the inferred mass uncertainty.

= Radiative transfer equation
{parent=Radiative transfer}
{wiki}

In plane-parallel optical-depth coordinates without scattering,
$$
\mu\frac{dI_\nu}{d\tau_\nu}=I_\nu-S_\nu,
$$
with source function $S_\nu$.

= Radiative diffusion
{parent=Radiative transfer equation}

In an optically thick medium whose radiation field is nearly isotropic and thermal, the first angular correction to the Planck function produces the diffusive flux
$$
F=-\frac{16\sigma T^3}{3\kappa_R\rho}\nabla T,
$$
where $\kappa_R$ is the Rosseland mean opacity.

= Rosseland mean opacity
{title2=$\kappa_R$}
{c}
{parent=Radiative diffusion}
{wiki=Rosseland_mean_opacity}

The Rosseland mean opacity is the harmonic frequency average weighted by $\partial B_\nu/\partial T$. It controls radiative diffusion because flux preferentially travels through low-opacity frequencies.

= Local thermodynamic equilibrium
{title2=LTE}
{parent=Radiative transfer equation}
{wiki}

In local thermodynamic equilibrium, matter populations are thermal at the local temperature and the source function is the Planck function, $S_\nu=B_\nu(T)$.

= Formal solution of the radiative transfer equation
{parent=Radiative transfer equation}

For an outward ray through a nonscattering atmosphere,
$$
I_\nu(0,\mu)=I_\nu(\tau_b,\mu)e^{-\tau_b/\mu}
+\int_0^{\tau_b}B_\nu[T(t)]e^{-t/\mu}\frac{dt}{\mu}.
$$

= Eddington-Barbier relation
{c}
{parent=Formal solution of the radiative transfer equation}
{wiki=Eddington–Barbier_relation}

When the source function varies approximately linearly with optical depth, the emergent intensity obeys $I_\nu(0,\mu)\simeq S_\nu(\tau_\nu=\mu)$.

= Coherent isotropic scattering
{parent=Radiative transfer equation}

Coherent isotropic scattering preserves photon frequency and redistributes direction uniformly. Its source function is the mean intensity $J_\nu$, so conservative scattering removes light from a pencil beam while preserving luminosity integrated over all escaping directions.

= Single-scattering albedo
{title2=$\omega_\nu$}
{parent=Coherent isotropic scattering}
{wiki}

The single-scattering albedo is the fraction of total extinction due to scattering, $\omega_\nu=\alpha_{\nu,\rm sca}/\alpha_{\nu,\rm ext}$. Its complement is the absorptive fraction.

= Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation
{c}
{parent=Radiative transfer equation}
{wiki}

Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation states that a body in thermal equilibrium has equal absorptivity and emissivity at each frequency and direction. A layer with infrared absorptivity $\epsilon$ therefore emits $\epsilon B_\nu(T)$.

= Radiation-field moment
{parent=Radiative transfer}

For plane-parallel intensity, the standard moments are
$$
J_\nu=\frac12\int_{-1}^1I_\nu d\mu,
\quad
H_\nu=\frac12\int_{-1}^1\mu I_\nu d\mu,
\quad
K_\nu=\frac12\int_{-1}^1\mu^2I_\nu d\mu,
$$
and the radiative flux is $F_\nu=4\pi H_\nu$.

= Mean intensity
{title2=$J_\nu$}
{parent=Radiation-field moment}

The mean intensity is the angular average $J_\nu=(4\pi)^{-1}\int I_\nu d\Omega$ of the <specific intensity>.

= Radiative flux
{title2=$F_\nu$}
{parent=Radiation-field moment}

Radiative flux is the first angular moment $F_\nu=\int I_\nu\cos\theta,d\Omega$ and measures net radiant power crossing unit area.

= Eddington closure approximation
{c}
{parent=Radiation-field moment}

The Eddington closure approximation sets the second angular moment to one third of the zeroth, so the radiation pressure and mean intensity satisfy $cP_r=4\pi J/3$.

= Grey atmosphere
{parent=Radiative transfer}
{wiki}

A grey atmosphere has frequency-independent opacity. In plane-parallel radiative equilibrium under the Eddington closure, its temperature obeys
$$
T^4=\frac34T_{\rm eff}^4\left(\tau+\frac23\right).
$$

= Plane-parallel atmosphere
{parent=Grey atmosphere}

A plane-parallel atmosphere neglects curvature over its vertical thickness, so thermodynamic variables depend on one Cartesian height coordinate.

= Line of sight
{parent=Astrophysics}

The line of sight is the ray from an observer toward an astronomical source.

= Plane of the sky
{parent=Line of sight}

The plane of the sky is the plane through a source perpendicular to the observer's line of sight.

= Position angle
{title2=PA}
{parent=Plane of the sky}
{wiki}

Position angle specifies a projected direction on the sky, conventionally measured from North toward East.

= Projected separation
{title2=$R_{\rm sky}$}
{parent=Plane of the sky}

Projected separation is the distance between two objects after their relative displacement is projected onto the plane of the sky.

= Planetary science
{parent=Astrophysics}
{wiki}

Planetary science studies planets, moons, small bodies, planetary systems, and the processes that form and evolve them.

= Planetary system dynamics
{parent=Planetary science}

Planetary system dynamics studies the orbital evolution of planets, planetesimals, and dust under gravity, radiation, collisions, and dissipative forces.

= Astronomical conjunction
{parent=Planetary system dynamics}
{wiki=Conjunction_(astronomy)}

An astronomical conjunction occurs when two orbiting bodies have the same apparent longitude as viewed from their primary or from an observer.

= Keplerian shear
{c}
{parent=Planetary system dynamics}

Keplerian shear is differential orbital motion caused by $n\propto a^{-3/2}$: neighboring material at smaller semi-major axis advances in longitude relative to material farther out.

= Phase mixing
{parent=Planetary system dynamics}

Phase mixing disperses an initially localized ensemble because its members have slightly different orbital frequencies, causing their phases to spread with time.

= Planetesimal
{parent=Planetary system dynamics}
{wiki}

A planetesimal is a solid body in a young or evolved planetary system whose collisions and gravitational interactions help build or erode larger bodies.

= Planetesimal collision rate
{parent=Planetesimal}

For an impactor number density per size $n(D_{\rm im})$, relative speed $v_{\rm rel}$, and collision cross-section $\sigma(D,D_{\rm im})$, the differential collision rate of one target is $n(D_{\rm im})v_{\rm rel}\sigma(D,D_{\rm im})dD_{\rm im}$.

= Specific impact energy
{title2=$Q$}
{parent=Planetesimal}

The specific impact energy is an impactor's kinetic energy divided by the target mass. For equal-density spherical bodies,
$$
Q=\frac12\left(\frac{D_{\rm im}}D\right)^3v_{\rm rel}^2.
$$

= Tisserand parameter
{title2=$T_p$}
{c}
{parent=Planetary system dynamics}
{wiki}

For a small body with orbital elements $a,e,I$ relative to a planet on a circular orbit of semi-major axis $a_p$, the Tisserand parameter is approximately
$$
T_p=\frac{a_p}{a}+2\sqrt{\frac a{a_p}(1-e^2)}\cos I.
$$
It is derived from the Jacobi constant and is approximately conserved across separated weak encounters in the circular restricted three-body problem.

= Disturbing function
{title2=$\mathcal R$}
{parent=Planetary system dynamics}
{wiki}

The disturbing function is the perturbing part of the gravitational potential used to derive the evolution of orbital elements in celestial mechanics.

= Direct disturbing function
{parent=Disturbing function}

The direct disturbing function is the gravitational potential of the perturbing body evaluated at the disturbed body. In heliocentric coordinates it is proportional to the inverse separation of the two bodies.

= Indirect disturbing function
{parent=Disturbing function}

The indirect disturbing function accounts for the acceleration of a non-inertial origin, such as a star-centered coordinate frame accelerated by a perturbing planet.

= D'Alembert characteristic
{c}
{parent=Disturbing function}

The D'Alembert characteristic constrains each disturbing-function harmonic so that the integer coefficients of its angular arguments sum to zero, with corresponding parity and eccentricity-inclination order restrictions imposed by rotational symmetry.

= Laplace coefficient
{title2=$b_s^{(j)}(\alpha)$}
{c}
{parent=Disturbing function}

A Laplace coefficient is the Fourier coefficient
$$
b_s^{(j)}(\alpha)=\frac1\pi\int_0^{2\pi}
\frac{\cos(j\psi)\,d\psi}{(1-2\alpha\cos\psi+\alpha^2)^s}.
$$
It packages the radial dependence of a disturbing-function expansion.

= Short-period orbital perturbation
{parent=Disturbing function}

A short-period orbital perturbation is driven by rapidly varying orbital-angle combinations and usually averages to zero over many conjunctions away from resonance.

= Resonant orbital perturbation
{parent=Disturbing function}

A resonant orbital perturbation has a slowly varying angular argument because a nearly integer relation between orbital frequencies prevents successive conjunction effects from averaging away.

= Secular perturbation
{parent=Disturbing function}
{wiki}

A secular perturbation survives averaging over fast orbital phases and drives slow precession and exchange of eccentricity or inclination.

= Laplace-Lagrange secular theory
{c}
{parent=Secular perturbation}

Laplace-Lagrange secular theory linearizes the orbit-averaged disturbing function to second order in eccentricities and inclinations. It represents planetary eccentricity evolution by a constant matrix.

= Laplace-Lagrange secular matrix
{title2=$\mathbf A$}
{c}
{parent=Laplace-Lagrange secular theory}

The eigenvalues of the Laplace-Lagrange secular matrix are apsidal precession frequencies, and its eigenvectors give the relative eccentricities and apsidal phases of the corresponding normal modes.

= Complex eccentricity
{title2=$z$}
{parent=Laplace-Lagrange secular theory}

The complex eccentricity packages eccentricity and longitude of periapsis as $z=e e^{i\varpi}$, turning linear secular dynamics into a complex linear system.

= Secular eigenmode
{parent=Laplace-Lagrange secular theory}

A secular eigenmode is a normal mode in which all complex eccentricities precess at one eigenfrequency with fixed amplitude ratios and relative apsidal phases.

= Forced eccentricity
{parent=Secular perturbation}

The forced eccentricity is the particular secular response of a small body to planetary eigenmodes, as distinct from its freely precessing homogeneous eccentricity.

= Secular resonance
{parent=Forced eccentricity}
{wiki}

A secular resonance occurs when a small body's free precession frequency equals a planetary secular eigenfrequency. Undamped linear theory then predicts resonant eccentricity growth.

= Eccentricity damping
{parent=Secular perturbation}

Linear eccentricity damping contributes $\dot z=-z/\tau$ to the complex-eccentricity equation, exponentially suppressing the free mode and regularizing a secular resonance.

= Secular torque
{parent=Secular perturbation}

A secular torque is the orbit-averaged gravitational torque that changes orbital orientation or shape after fast orbital phases have been averaged out.

= Quadrupole approximation
{parent=Secular perturbation}

In a hierarchical orbital system, the quadrupole approximation keeps the first nontrivial term beyond the monopole in the orbit-averaged expansion in the ratio of inner to outer separation.

= Nodal precession
{parent=Secular perturbation}
{wiki}

Nodal precession is the gradual rotation of an orbit's line of nodes, usually driven by a nonspherical or external gravitational torque.

= Differential nodal precession
{parent=Nodal precession}

Differential nodal precession occurs when neighboring orbits precess at different rates, winding a flat collection of rings into a warp or a phase-mixed thick structure.

= Laplace plane
{c}
{parent=Nodal precession}
{wiki}

The Laplace plane is the local equilibrium orbital plane about which a satellite or disk annulus precesses under competing secular torques.

= Apsidal precession
{parent=Secular perturbation}
{wiki}

Apsidal precession is the gradual rotation of an orbit's line of apsides, equivalently a change in its <longitude of periapsis>.

= Kozai–Lidov mechanism
{c}
{parent=Secular perturbation}
{wiki}

The Kozai–Lidov mechanism exchanges eccentricity and inclination in a hierarchical gravitational system while preserving a component of angular momentum in the quadrupole limit.

= Planetary scattering
{parent=Planetary system dynamics}

Planetary scattering is the alteration of a smaller body's orbital energy and angular momentum by close encounters with a planet. Repeated encounters can eject, accrete, or detach the body.

= Debris disk
{parent=Planetary science}
{wiki}

= Debris belt
{synonym}

A debris disk is an optically thin circumstellar population of dust and larger solid bodies maintained mainly by collisions.

= Fractional luminosity of a debris disk
{title2=$f$}
{parent=Debris disk}

The fractional luminosity is $f=L_{\rm disk}/L_*$; for blackbody grains in an optically thin belt at radius $r$, it equals their total geometric cross-section divided by $4\pi r^2$.

= Dust clump
{parent=Debris disk}

A dust clump is a localized concentration of collision fragments or trapped grains whose orbital phases have not yet become uniformly distributed around their parent belt.

= Collisional cascade
{parent=Debris disk}
{wiki}

A collisional cascade transfers mass from large bodies to progressively smaller fragments. A steady cascade has a size distribution whose mass flux through logarithmic size bins is independent of size.

= Power-law size distribution
{parent=Collisional cascade}

A differential power-law size distribution has $n(D)dD=kD^{-\alpha}dD$ objects with sizes between $D$ and $D+dD$.

= Dohnanyi collisional cascade
{c}
{parent=Collisional cascade}

The ideal steady Dohnanyi cascade assumes self-similar fragmentation, size-independent collision speeds, and a size-independent catastrophic disruption threshold. Its differential size distribution is $n(D)\propto D^{-7/2}$.

= Catastrophic disruption threshold
{title2=$Q_D^*$}
{parent=Collisional cascade}

The catastrophic disruption threshold is the impact energy per unit target mass required both to shatter a body and to disperse enough fragments against self-gravity.

= Rubblising collision
{parent=Catastrophic disruption threshold}

A rubblising collision shatters a body but does not supply enough energy to disperse its fragments against self-gravity, leaving a gravitationally bound rubble pile.

= Catastrophic planetesimal collision
{parent=Catastrophic disruption threshold}

A catastrophic planetesimal collision has $Q\geq Q_D^*$ and disperses a substantial fraction of the target mass.

= Catastrophic planetesimal collision rate
{title2=$R_{\rm cc}$}
{parent=Catastrophic planetesimal collision}

The catastrophic planetesimal collision rate is the collision rate integrated over impactors large enough to satisfy $Q\geq Q_D^*$.

= Catastrophic collision mass-loss timescale
{parent=Catastrophic planetesimal collision}

The catastrophic collision mass-loss timescale is the target mass divided by the rate at which catastrophic impacts remove mass from its largest remnant.

= Cratering collision
{parent=Collisional cascade}

A cratering collision has $Q<Q_D^*$ and removes part of a target without catastrophically dispersing it.

= Interplanetary dust
{parent=Planetary science}
{wiki}

Interplanetary dust consists of small solid particles orbiting within a planetary system and responding to gravity, radiation, plasma, and collisions.

= Radiation-pressure coefficient
{title2=$\beta$}
{parent=Interplanetary dust}

The radiation-pressure coefficient is the ratio of outward stellar radiation pressure to inward stellar gravity on a dust grain. The grain therefore feels effective gravitational parameter $(1-\beta)GM_*$.

= Radiation-pressure efficiency
{title2=$Q_{\rm pr}$}
{parent=Radiation-pressure coefficient}

The radiation-pressure efficiency is the radiation-force cross-section of a grain divided by its geometric cross-section. It is of order unity for grains larger than the illuminating wavelength and depends strongly on size and composition in the small-particle regime.

= Radiation-pressure blowout orbit
{parent=Radiation-pressure coefficient}

A grain released from a circular parent orbit without a relative velocity impulse is unbound when radiation pressure reduces the effective stellar attraction sufficiently. For $\beta>1$, the effective inverse-square force is repulsive.

= Finson–Probstein model
{c}
{parent=Interplanetary dust}

The Finson–Probstein model follows cometary dust after release with negligible ejection speed under stellar gravity reduced by <radiation pressure>. A grid of <synchrones> and <syndynes> organizes the resulting dust-tail morphology.

= Synchrone
{parent=Finson–Probstein model}

A synchrone is the locus, at one observation time, of grains released at the same time with different <radiation-pressure coefficients>.

= Syndyne
{parent=Finson–Probstein model}

A syndyne is the locus, at one observation time, of grains with the same <radiation-pressure coefficient> released at different times.

= Cometary dust tail
{parent=Finson–Probstein model}
{wiki=Comet_tail}

A cometary dust tail is the extended distribution of solid grains released by a comet. <Radiation pressure> sorts its grains by size: high-$\beta$ grains are displaced rapidly in the anti-stellar direction, while larger low-$\beta$ grains remain closer to the projected orbit and form a more curved trail.

= Steady radial dust outflow
{parent=Interplanetary dust}

For an axisymmetric steady dust outflow with surface density $\Sigma$ and radial speed $v_r$, number or mass conservation gives $2\pi r\Sigma v_r=\text{constant}$.

= Poynting–Robertson drag
{c}
{parent=Interplanetary dust}
{wiki}

Poynting–Robertson drag is the tangential component of stellar radiation force caused by aberration in an orbiting dust grain's frame. It removes orbital energy and angular momentum and drives the grain inward.

= Stellar-wind drag
{parent=Interplanetary dust}

Stellar-wind drag is the tangential force exerted by a star's outflow on an orbiting grain. Like Poynting–Robertson drag, it removes orbital energy and angular momentum.

= Resonant trapping of dust
{parent=Interplanetary dust}

During inward migration, dust can be captured into an exterior mean-motion resonance with a planet. A phase-shifted conjunction then lets planetary torque replace angular momentum lost to Poynting–Robertson drag.

= Stellar astrophysics
{parent=Astrophysics}
{wiki}

Stellar astrophysics studies the structure, formation, energy production, observable properties, and evolution of stars.

= Cepheid variable
{c}
{parent=Stellar astrophysics}
{wiki}

A Cepheid variable is a pulsating star whose period is tightly related to its luminosity, allowing its distance to be inferred from its measured period and <apparent magnitude>.

= Supernova
{parent=Stellar astrophysics}
{wiki}

A supernova is a luminous stellar explosion produced by thermonuclear disruption or the collapse of a stellar core.

= Type Ia supernova
{c}
{parent=Supernova}
{wiki}

A Type Ia supernova is a thermonuclear supernova whose standardized luminosity makes it a useful astronomical distance indicator.

= Brown dwarf
{parent=Stellar astrophysics}
{wiki}

A brown dwarf is a self-gravitating object too light to sustain ordinary hydrogen fusion. Electron degeneracy limits its contraction, leaving radii near that of Jupiter across a broad mass range.

= Deuterium-burning mass
{parent=Brown dwarf}

The conventional deuterium-burning boundary is near $13$ Jupiter masses, with composition-dependent variation. It is a useful classification scale rather than a sharp formation-based distinction between planets and brown dwarfs.

= Hydrogen-burning minimum mass
{parent=Brown dwarf}

The hydrogen-burning minimum mass is the lowest mass that can sustain core hydrogen fusion, about $0.075$--$0.08$ solar masses for near-solar composition. Less massive brown dwarfs cool continually after any deuterium burning ends.

= Star formation
{parent=Stellar astrophysics}
{wiki}

Star formation is the gravitational collapse and fragmentation of cold interstellar gas into protostars and eventually stars. Cooling, turbulence, magnetic fields, rotation, and feedback determine its efficiency and characteristic masses.

= Kennicutt–Schmidt law
{c}
{parent=Star formation}
{wiki}

The Kennicutt–Schmidt law relates star-formation-rate surface density to gas surface density, commonly as $\Sigma_{\rm SFR}=A\Sigma_g^N$. Disk-averaged total-gas measurements give $N$ near $1.4$, while resolved molecular-gas relations are often closer to linear.

= Gas-depletion time
{title2=$t_{\rm dep}$}
{parent=Kennicutt–Schmidt law}

The gas-depletion time is the time required to consume a gas reservoir at its current star-formation rate, $t_{\rm dep}=M_g/\dot M_*$. It ignores replenishment, returned stellar mass, and changes in the rate.

= Star-formation efficiency per free-fall time
{title2=$\epsilon_{\rm ff}$}
{parent=Gas-depletion time}

The star-formation efficiency per free-fall time is the fraction of gas converted into stars during one gravitational free-fall time, $\epsilon_{\rm ff}=t_{\rm ff}/t_{\rm dep}$. Molecular clouds commonly have values of order one per cent.

= Jeans mass
{title2=$M_J$}
{c}
{parent=Star formation}
{wiki}

The Jeans mass is the characteristic gas mass above which self-gravity overcomes pressure support. Up to the convention used to define the collapsing region,
$$
M_J\propto\frac{c_s^3}{G^{3/2}\rho^{1/2}}
\propto T^{3/2}\rho^{-1/2}
$$
for an ideal gas of fixed composition.

= Isothermal fragmentation
{parent=Jeans mass}

During isothermal collapse, $T$ is constant and the <Jeans mass> decreases as $\rho^{-1/2}$. Successively denser regions can therefore become independently unstable and fragment to smaller masses.

= Adiabatic suppression of fragmentation
{parent=Jeans mass}

For adiabatic monatomic gas, $T\propto\rho^{2/3}$ and the <Jeans mass> increases as $\rho^{1/2}$. Once cooling fails and collapse becomes adiabatic, further fragmentation is suppressed.

= Initial mass function
{title2=IMF}
{parent=Star formation}
{wiki}

The initial mass function is the distribution of stellar birth masses. It records the mass scales selected by cloud fragmentation, accretion, feedback, and stellar multiplicity.

= Population II star
{c}
{parent=Initial mass function}
{wiki=Stellar_population#Population_II_stars}

A Population II star is an old metal-poor star formed from gas enriched by earlier stellar generations. Metal and dust cooling let enriched gas fragment to lower masses than primordial gas under otherwise comparable conditions.

= Population III star
{c}
{parent=Initial mass function}
{wiki=Stellar_population#Population_III_stars}

A Population III star is a first-generation star formed from essentially metal-free primordial gas. Limited cooling generally raises its characteristic fragmentation mass and favors a more top-heavy <initial mass function> than in enriched star formation.

= Stellar structure
{parent=Stellar astrophysics}
{wiki}

Stellar structure follows from mass conservation, hydrostatic equilibrium, energy generation, and energy transport together with an equation of state and opacity law.

= Stellar structure equations
{parent=Stellar structure}

The stellar structure equations describe spherical hydrostatic stars through radial conservation of mass, force balance, energy generation, and energy transport. An equation of state, opacity, energy-generation law, and boundary conditions close the system.

= Stellar adiabatic exponent
{title2=$\Gamma_i$}
{parent=Stellar structure}

The stellar adiabatic exponents describe pressure and temperature responses to an isentropic density change:
$$
\Gamma_1=\left(\frac{\partial\log P}{\partial\log\rho}\right)_s,
\qquad
\Gamma_3-1=\left(\frac{\partial\log T}{\partial\log\rho}\right)_s,
\qquad
\frac{\Gamma_2}{\Gamma_2-1}
=\left(\frac{\partial\log P}{\partial\log T}\right)_s.
$$

= Enclosed mass
{title2=$m(r)$}
{parent=Stellar structure}

For a spherically symmetric density $\rho(r)$, the mass enclosed inside radius $r$ is
$$
m(r)=4\pi\int_0^r\rho(s)s^2\,ds,
\qquad
\frac{dm}{dr}=4\pi r^2\rho(r).
$$

= Hydrostatic pressure support equation
{parent=Stellar structure}

For a static spherically symmetric Newtonian star, balancing the inward gravitational force against the pressure gradient gives
$$
\frac{dP}{dr}=-\frac{Gm(r)\rho(r)}{r^2},
\qquad
\frac{dm}{dr}=4\pi r^2\rho(r).
$$

= Stellar polytrope
{parent=Stellar structure}

A stellar polytrope obeys $P=K\rho^{1+1/n}$ for constant $K$ and polytropic index $n$. Its dimensionless density profile satisfies the <Lane-Emden equation>.

= Lane-Emden variables for a stellar polytrope
{c}
{parent=Stellar polytrope}

Writing
$$
\rho=\rho_c\theta^n,\qquad r=\alpha\xi,\qquad
\alpha^2=\frac{(n+1)K}{4\pi G}\rho_c^{(1-n)/n}
$$
reduces Newtonian hydrostatic balance to
$$
\frac1{\xi^2}\frac d{d\xi}\left(\xi^2\theta'\right)=-\theta^n,
\qquad \theta(0)=1,\quad\theta'(0)=0.
$$
The stellar surface is the first zero $\xi_1$ of $\theta$, and $R=\alpha\xi_1$.

= Eddington standard model
{c}
{parent=Stellar polytrope}
{wiki}

The Eddington standard model is a chemically homogeneous $n=3$ stellar polytrope with constant gas-pressure fraction $\beta$. It approximates massive radiative stars in which gas and radiation both contribute appreciably to pressure.

= Polytropic index
{title2=$n$}
{parent=Stellar polytrope}

The polytropic index $n$ is defined by $P=K\rho^{1+1/n}$. A monatomic convective ideal gas has $n=3/2$, while a radiation-supported Eddington model has $n=3$.

= Adiabatic stellar polytrope
{parent=Stellar polytrope}

An adiabatic stellar polytrope has $P=K\rho^\gamma$ with constant specific entropy, so $n=1/(\gamma-1)$. For a monatomic perfect gas, $\gamma=5/3$ and $n=3/2$.

= Polytropic mass-radius relation
{parent=Stellar polytrope}

Eliminating central density from the Lane-Emden scalings gives
$$
R\propto K^{n/(3-n)}M^{(1-n)/(3-n)}
$$
for $n\ne3$. In particular, $R\propto KM^{-1/3}$ for $n=3/2$.

With $Y_n=-\xi_1^2\theta'(\xi_1)$, the exact rearranged relation is
$$
M=A_nK^{n/(n-1)}R^{(3-n)/(1-n)},
$$
where
$$
A_n=4\pi Y_n
\left(\frac{n+1}{4\pi G}\right)^{n/(n-1)}
\xi_1^{(3-n)/(n-1)}.
$$
For $n=3$, the radius dependence disappears and the mass is proportional to $K^{3/2}$.

= Lane-Emden mass formula
{c}
{parent=Stellar polytrope}

If $\rho=\rho_c\theta^n$, $r=\alpha\xi$, and $\xi_1$ is the first zero of the Lane-Emden function, then a spherical polytrope has
$$
M=4\pi\alpha^3\rho_c\left[-\xi_1^2\theta'(\xi_1)\right].
$$

= Lane-Emden surface mass constant
{title2=$Y_n$}
{c}
{parent=Lane-Emden mass formula}

For a Lane-Emden function whose first zero is $\xi_1$,
$$
Y_n=-\xi_1^2\theta'(\xi_1)
=\int_0^{\xi_1}\xi^2\theta(\xi)^n\,d\xi.
$$

= Eddington quartic relation
{c}
{parent=Stellar polytrope}

For a chemically uniform $n=3$ gas-plus-radiation polytrope with constant gas-pressure fraction $\beta$, the mass obeys
$$
\frac{1-\beta}{\beta^4}\propto\mu^4M^2.
$$
It follows from the $n=3$ mass scaling $M\propto K^{3/2}$ and the equation of state.

= Stellar homology
{parent=Stellar structure}

Homologous stellar models have the same dimensionless radial profiles after mass, radius, density, pressure, and temperature are scaled by characteristic values.

= Surface gravity of a star
{title2=$g$}
{parent=Stellar structure}

The surface gravity of a spherical star of mass $M$ and radius $R$ is $g=GM/R^2$ in Newtonian gravity.

= Stellar surface boundary condition
{parent=Stellar structure}

A stellar surface boundary condition matches the interior pressure and temperature to an atmospheric solution, commonly near optical depth $\tau=2/3$ where a grey atmosphere has $T=T_{\rm eff}$.

= Photosphere
{parent=Stellar surface boundary condition}
{wiki}

The photosphere is the layer from which most observed stellar radiation escapes; in a grey atmosphere it is represented approximately by optical depth $\tau=2/3$.

= Hydrostatic equilibrium in optical depth
{parent=Photosphere}

In a plane-parallel atmosphere with $d\tau=-\kappa\rho\,dr$ and $dP/dr=-\rho g$, one has $dP/d\tau=g/\kappa$. If gravity and opacity vary slowly, the pressure at optical depth $\tau$ is approximately $P\simeq g\tau/\kappa$.

= Stellar virial theorem
{parent=Stellar structure}

For a self-gravitating fluid star with surface pressure $P_s$, volume $V$, kinetic energy $T$, gravitational energy $\Omega$, and $I=\int r^2dm$,
$$
\frac12\ddot I=2T+3\int P\,dV-3P_sV+\Omega.
$$

= Stellar convective stability
{parent=Stellar structure}

Stellar convective stability compares the density of an adiabatically displaced fluid element with its new surroundings. Temperature and composition gradients determine whether buoyancy restores the element or amplifies its displacement.

= Schwarzschild criterion
{c}
{parent=Stellar convective stability}
{wiki}

For uniform composition, a stellar layer is convectively stable when $\nabla<\nabla_{\rm ad}$ and unstable when $\nabla>\nabla_{\rm ad}$, where $\nabla=d\log T/d\log P$.

= Ledoux criterion
{c}
{parent=Stellar convective stability}
{wiki}

With a composition gradient, stability requires
$$
\nabla<\nabla_{\rm ad}+\frac{\phi}{\delta}\nabla_\mu.
$$
A mean molecular weight increasing inward normally provides an additional stabilizing term.

= Convective core
{parent=Stellar convective stability}

A convective core is a central stellar region in which the radiative temperature gradient exceeds the adiabatic threshold. Efficient convection makes its entropy nearly uniform and mixes its composition.

= Radiative stellar structure
{parent=Stellar structure}

In a radiative stellar region, photons carry luminosity down the temperature gradient. Dimensional scaling of the radiative-diffusion equation gives $L\sim RT_c^4/(\kappa_c\rho_c)$.

= Opacity
{title2=$\kappa$}
{parent=Radiative stellar structure}
{wiki}

Opacity is an extinction cross-section per unit mass. It controls the photon mean free path and therefore the radiative temperature gradient in an optically thick star.

= Kramers opacity law
{c}
{parent=Opacity}
{wiki=Kramers'_opacity_law}

The Kramers opacity law has $\kappa=\kappa_0\rho T^{-7/2}$ for bound-free and free-free absorption under its idealized ionized-gas assumptions.

= Electron-scattering opacity
{title2=$\kappa_{\rm es}$}
{parent=Opacity}

Electron-scattering opacity is approximately independent of density and temperature in a fully ionized nonrelativistic plasma of fixed composition. For hydrogen mass fraction $X$, $\kappa_{\rm es}\simeq0.2(1+X)\,{\rm cm^2\,g^{-1}}$.

= Free-free opacity
{title2=$\kappa_{\rm ff}$}
{parent=Opacity}

Free-free opacity is absorption caused when a photon is absorbed during a Coulomb encounter between an electron and an ion. In the Kramers approximation its Rosseland mean scales approximately as $\kappa_{\rm ff}\propto\rho T^{-7/2}$.

= Radiative diffusion in a star
{parent=Radiative stellar structure}

In an optically thick stellar region, radiative diffusion carries luminosity according to
$$
\frac{dT}{dr}=-\frac{3\kappa\rho L_r}{16\pi a_{\rm rad}c r^2T^3}.
$$

= Effective temperature
{title2=$T_{\rm eff}$}
{parent=Stellar structure}
{wiki}

The effective temperature is the temperature of a blackbody with the same radiative flux as a star: $L=4\pi R^2\sigma T_{\rm eff}^4$.

= Mass-luminosity relation
{parent=Stellar structure}
{wiki}

The mass-luminosity relation connects a star's mass to its luminosity. Its slope depends on opacity, nuclear burning, pressure support, composition, and evolutionary state.

= Eddington luminosity
{c}
{title2=$L_{\rm Edd}$}
{parent=Mass-luminosity relation}
{wiki}

Balancing outward radiative acceleration against gravity gives
$$
L_{\rm Edd}=\frac{4\pi cGM}{\kappa}.
$$
It is an approximate upper luminosity for a hydrostatic star of opacity $\kappa$.

= Radiative force multiplier
{title2=$\mathcal M$}
{parent=Eddington luminosity}

A radiative force multiplier is the ratio of total radiative acceleration to a reference electron-scattering acceleration. Spectral lines, bound-free absorption, and dust can make $\mathcal M\gg1$, whereas highly ionized gas approaches $\mathcal M=1$.

= Stellar nuclear fusion
{parent=Stellar astrophysics}
{wiki}

Stellar nuclear fusion converts light nuclei into more tightly bound nuclei and supplies most main-sequence stellar luminosity.

= Stellar energy-generation rate
{title2=$\epsilon$}
{parent=Stellar nuclear fusion}

The stellar energy-generation rate $\epsilon$ is power produced per unit mass. Spherical energy conservation is $dL_r/dr=4\pi r^2\rho\epsilon$.

= Hydrogen mass fraction
{title2=$X$}
{parent=Stellar nuclear fusion}

The hydrogen mass fraction is the fraction $X$ of a stellar material's mass carried by hydrogen nuclei.

= Hydrogen burning
{parent=Stellar nuclear fusion}

Hydrogen burning converts hydrogen into helium through the <proton–proton chain> or <CNO cycle>.

= Triple-alpha process
{c}
{parent=Stellar nuclear fusion}
{wiki}

The triple-alpha process fuses three helium-4 nuclei into carbon-12 through an unstable beryllium-8 intermediate. Its steep temperature dependence can make helium-shell burning thermally unstable.

= CNO mass fraction
{title2=$X_{\rm CNO}$}
{c}
{parent=Stellar nuclear fusion}

The CNO mass fraction is the combined mass fraction of carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen nuclei available to catalyse the CNO cycle.

= Proton–proton chain
{parent=Stellar nuclear fusion}
{wiki}

The proton–proton chain converts hydrogen to helium and dominates hydrogen burning in stars near and below the Sun's mass. Near $2\times10^7\,\mathrm K$, its specific energy generation is roughly proportional to $\rho T^4$.

= CNO cycle
{c}
{parent=Stellar nuclear fusion}
{wiki}

The CNO cycle catalyses hydrogen fusion through carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen nuclei. Near $2\times10^7\,\mathrm K$, its specific energy generation is approximately proportional to $\rho T^{17}$ and is therefore strongly concentrated near a star's centre.

= Stellar thermostat
{parent=Stellar nuclear fusion}

The stellar thermostat is the negative feedback between temperature-sensitive nuclear burning and hydrostatic expansion: excess burning expands and cools a star, while deficient burning permits contraction and heating.

= Core helium burning
{parent=Stellar nuclear fusion}

Core helium burning begins when a hydrogen-exhausted stellar core becomes hot enough for the <triple-alpha process>. Subsequent alpha capture on carbon produces oxygen, leaving a carbon-oxygen core after central helium exhaustion.

= Carbon burning
{parent=Stellar nuclear fusion}
{wiki}

Carbon burning fuses carbon nuclei at temperatures of order $10^9\,\mathrm K$. In intermediate-mass stars it can ignite off-centre under partial electron degeneracy and propagate inward as a burning flame.

= Slow neutron-capture process
{title2=$s$-process}
{parent=Stellar nuclear fusion}
{wiki=S-process}

The slow neutron-capture process builds nuclei heavier than iron when neutron captures occur more slowly than typical beta decays. Thermal pulses in asymptotic-giant-branch stars produce characteristic enhancements of elements such as strontium, zirconium, and barium.

= Main sequence
{parent=Stellar astrophysics}
{wiki}

A main-sequence star is in a long-lived phase of core hydrogen burning.

= Lower main sequence
{parent=Main sequence}

The lower main sequence consists of relatively cool, low-mass hydrogen-burning stars whose luminosity is supplied mainly by the proton–proton chain.

= Red dwarf
{parent=Lower main sequence}
{wiki}

A red dwarf is a cool low-mass main-sequence star. The lowest-mass red dwarfs are fully convective.

= Zero-age main sequence
{title2=ZAMS}
{parent=Main sequence}
{wiki}

The zero-age main sequence is the locus where stars begin stable core hydrogen burning with nearly their initial composition.

= Hertzsprung-Russell diagram
{c}
{parent=Stellar astrophysics}
{wiki}

A Hertzsprung-Russell diagram plots stellar luminosity against effective temperature or spectral type. By convention, temperature usually decreases from left to right.

= Stellar evolution
{parent=Stellar astrophysics}
{wiki}

Stellar evolution is the change of a star's structure and composition as nuclear reactions, energy transport, mass loss, and interactions proceed.

= Kelvin-Helmholtz contraction
{c}
{parent=Stellar evolution}
{wiki=Kelvin–Helmholtz_mechanism}

Kelvin-Helmholtz contraction releases gravitational binding energy as a self-gravitating body cools and shrinks. It powers young stars and giant planets before nuclear burning or degeneracy dominates their thermal evolution.

= Pre-main-sequence star
{parent=Stellar evolution}
{wiki}

A pre-main-sequence star contracts toward the main sequence before sustained core hydrogen burning supplies its luminosity. Convection, accretion, and deuterium burning shape its track across the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram.

= Hayashi track
{c}
{parent=Pre-main-sequence star}
{wiki}

A Hayashi track is the nearly vertical path of a fully convective pre-main-sequence star on the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram. Photospheric opacity fixes a weak relation between luminosity and effective temperature.

= Accretion luminosity
{title2=$L_{\rm acc}$}
{parent=Pre-main-sequence star}

Matter accreting at rate $\dot M$ onto a star of mass $M$ and radius $R$ releases gravitational power of order
$$
L_{\rm acc}=\frac{GM\dot M}{R},
$$
up to an efficiency and an inner-boundary correction.

= Deuterium-burning energy-generation rate
{title2=$\epsilon_D$}
{parent=Pre-main-sequence star}

At the temperatures relevant to pre-main-sequence stars, a useful steep power-law approximation to the specific deuterium-burning rate is $\epsilon_D\propto\rho T^{15}$. Its sensitivity to central temperature makes deuterium burning act as a temporary thermostat.

= Asymptotic giant branch
{c}
{title2=AGB}
{parent=Stellar evolution}
{wiki}

= AGB star
{c}
{synonym}

An asymptotic-giant-branch star has an inert carbon-oxygen core surrounded by helium- and hydrogen-burning shells and a deep convective envelope. Alternating shell burning produces thermal pulses and dredge-up, while strong mass loss removes the envelope.

= Stellar dredge-up
{parent=Asymptotic giant branch}

Stellar dredge-up occurs when a convective envelope penetrates into layers altered by nuclear burning and transports their material to the surface.

= First dredge-up
{parent=Stellar dredge-up}
{wiki}

First dredge-up occurs as a star ascends the red-giant branch. Its deepening convective envelope exposes material processed by hydrogen burning, especially enhanced helium and nitrogen and depleted carbon.

= Second dredge-up
{parent=Stellar dredge-up}
{wiki}

Second dredge-up occurs after core helium exhaustion in sufficiently massive intermediate-mass stars. The convective envelope moves inward, mixes helium- and hydrogen-burning products to the surface, and reduces the hydrogen-exhausted core mass.

= Third dredge-up
{parent=Stellar dredge-up}
{wiki}

Third dredge-up follows a thermal pulse on the asymptotic giant branch, when the convective envelope reaches into the intershell and can carry freshly made carbon and slow-neutron-capture elements to the surface.

= Thermal pulse of an asymptotic-giant-branch star
{parent=Asymptotic giant branch}

= AGB thermal pulse
{c}
{synonym}

A thermal pulse is a helium-shell flash caused by thermally unstable burning in a thin shell. It briefly drives intershell convection, expands and cools the hydrogen-burning shell, and can be followed by third dredge-up.

= Härm–Schwarzschild instability
{c}
{parent=Thermal pulse of an asymptotic-giant-branch star}

The Härm–Schwarzschild instability is the thin-shell thermal instability of helium burning in an asymptotic-giant-branch star. Heating greatly raises the nuclear rate while the geometrically thin shell initially expands too little to provide stabilizing cooling.

= Super-AGB star
{c}
{parent=Asymptotic giant branch}
{wiki}

A super-AGB star is massive enough to ignite carbon and form a degenerate oxygen-neon core, but initially too light to proceed directly through all advanced burning stages. Its fate depends on competition between core growth and envelope mass loss.

= Oxygen-neon-magnesium core
{title2=$\mathrm{O/Ne/Mg}$ core}
{parent=Super-AGB star}

An oxygen-neon-magnesium core is the degenerate product of carbon burning, composed mainly of oxygen and neon with a smaller magnesium abundance. It can become an oxygen-neon white dwarf or collapse after electron captures if it approaches the <Chandrasekhar mass>.

= Electron-capture supernova
{parent=Super-AGB star}
{wiki}

An electron-capture supernova occurs when electron captures, especially on neon and magnesium nuclei in a degenerate oxygen-neon core, remove pressure support and trigger collapse before ordinary iron-core formation.

= Stellar wind
{parent=Stellar evolution}
{wiki}

A stellar wind is a continuous outflow of gas from a star. Radiation, gas pressure, waves, or magnetic fields can supply its acceleration, and the resulting mass loss can determine a star's final remnant.

= Neutron star
{parent=Stellar evolution}
{wiki}

A neutron star is a compact stellar remnant whose gravity compresses roughly a stellar mass into a radius of order ten kilometres. Degenerate matter supplies most of its pressure support, while rapid rotation and intense magnetic fields can power observable emission.

= Pulsar
{parent=Neutron star}
{wiki}

= Radio pulsar
{synonym}

A pulsar is a rotating magnetized neutron star whose directed emission is observed as periodic pulses. Rotation supplies the energy of an ordinary radio pulsar.

= Pulsar magnetosphere
{parent=Pulsar}

A pulsar magnetosphere is the plasma and electromagnetic field surrounding a pulsar. Close to the star, charges tend to screen electric fields parallel to the magnetic field and approximately corotate; near the light cylinder, relativistic motion and field distortion become essential.

= Corotating pulsar magnetosphere
{parent=Pulsar magnetosphere}

In an idealized corotating pulsar magnetosphere, plasma moves with $\mathbf u=\boldsymbol\Omega\mathbin\times\mathbf r$. In Gaussian units, ideal magnetohydrodynamics then gives $\mathbf E=-\mathbf u\mathbin\times\mathbf B/c$.

= Goldreich-Julian density
{c}
{parent=Corotating pulsar magnetosphere}
{wiki}

= Goldreich-Julian charge density
{c}
{synonym}

The Goldreich-Julian density is the charge density needed to support corotation in an idealized pulsar magnetosphere. Where exterior currents vanish,
$$
\rho_{\rm GJ}=-\frac{\boldsymbol\Omega\mathbin\cdot\mathbf B}{2\pi c}
$$
in Gaussian units.

= Light cylinder
{parent=Pulsar magnetosphere}
{wiki}

The light cylinder is the notional cylindrical surface on which rigid corotation with angular speed $\Omega$ would reach the speed of light.

= Light cylinder radius
{title2=$R_{\rm LC}$}
{parent=Light cylinder}

The light cylinder radius is $R_{\rm LC}=c/\Omega$. A nonrelativistic corotation model must fail as cylindrical radius approaches this scale.

= Pulsar polar cap
{parent=Pulsar magnetosphere}

A pulsar polar cap is the region on the stellar surface threaded by magnetic field lines that reach the outer magnetosphere rather than closing close to the star.

= Polar-cap opening angle
{title2=$\theta_*$}
{parent=Pulsar polar cap}

For an aligned dipole whose last relevant field line reaches equatorial radius $R_e$, the polar-cap opening angle obeys $\sin^2\theta_*=R_*/R_e$. It is approximately $\sqrt{R_*/R_e}$ for a small cap.

= White dwarf
{parent=Stellar evolution}
{wiki}

A white dwarf is a compact stellar remnant supported mainly by electron degeneracy pressure.

= Chandrasekhar mass
{c}
{parent=White dwarf}
{wiki=Chandrasekhar_limit}

The Chandrasekhar mass is the limiting mass of a cold nonrotating white dwarf supported by relativistic electron degeneracy pressure, about $1.4M_\odot$ for ordinary composition.

= Mestel's cooling law
{c}
{parent=White dwarf}

Mestel's cooling law matches a degenerate isothermal white-dwarf core to a nondegenerate radiative envelope. With Kramers opacity it gives $L/M\propto T_c^{7/2}$ in terms of core temperature.

= Hydrogen-burning white-dwarf envelope
{parent=White dwarf}

A hydrogen-burning white-dwarf envelope is a thin nondegenerate surface layer in which accreted or residual hydrogen can undergo nuclear fusion above a degenerate core.

= Thin-shell instability
{parent=Hydrogen-burning white-dwarf envelope}
{wiki=Thin_shell_instability}

Thin-shell instability is a thermal runaway of nuclear burning in a geometrically thin layer whose pressure is constrained by overlying weight, weakening expansion-driven cooling.

= Classical nova
{parent=Thin-shell instability}
{wiki}

A classical nova is a thermonuclear runaway in hydrogen-rich material accreted onto a white dwarf, producing a sudden luminous outburst and ejecting part of the envelope.

= Schönberg-Chandrasekhar limit
{c}
{parent=Stellar evolution}
{wiki}

The Schönberg-Chandrasekhar limit is the maximum fractional mass of an isothermal inert core that can support a surrounding hydrogen-burning envelope in hydrostatic and thermal equilibrium.

= Binary star
{parent=Stellar astrophysics}
{wiki}

A binary star is a gravitationally bound pair orbiting their common centre of mass.

= Binary mass function
{title2=$F$}
{parent=Binary star}
{wiki=Binary_mass_function}

For a single-lined circular spectroscopic binary whose visible component has radial-velocity amplitude $K_1$ and orbital period $P$, the binary mass function is
$$
F=\frac{PK_1^3}{2\pi G}
=\frac{M_2^3\sin^3i}{(M_1+M_2)^2}.
$$

= Isotropic binary inclination distribution
{title2=$p(i)=\sin i$}
{parent=Binary mass function}

Randomly oriented binary angular-momentum vectors are uniform in $\cos i$. After identifying $i$ with $\pi-i$, their normalized inclination density on $0\leq i\leq\pi/2$ is $p(i)=\sin i$.

= Recycled pulsar
{parent=Binary star}
{wiki}

A recycled pulsar is a neutron star spun up by accretion from a binary companion. Accreted angular momentum can reduce its spin period to milliseconds, while donor-envelope removal often leaves a white-dwarf companion.

= Binary millisecond pulsar
{parent=Recycled pulsar}

A binary millisecond pulsar is a rapidly rotating recycled neutron star that remains in a binary, commonly with the white-dwarf remnant of its donor.

= Wind mass transfer in a binary star
{parent=Binary star}

Wind mass transfer occurs when one star accretes part of a companion's stellar wind without the donor necessarily filling its Roche lobe.

= Wind Roche-lobe overflow
{c}
{parent=Wind mass transfer in a binary star}

Wind Roche-lobe overflow occurs when a slow dense wind nearly fills the donor's Roche lobe and is gravitationally focused through the inner Lagrange region. It can transfer a much larger fraction of the outflow than a uniform fast-wind Bondi--Hoyle estimate.

= Jeans-mode mass loss
{c}
{parent=Wind mass transfer in a binary star}

In Jeans-mode mass loss, an isotropic wind escapes from one binary component carrying that star's specific orbital angular momentum. If the companion accretes negligibly, a circular binary obeys $aM=\text{constant}$ and $PM^2=\text{constant}$.

= Barium star
{parent=Wind mass transfer in a binary star}
{wiki}

A barium star is a main-sequence or giant star enriched in carbon and slow-neutron-capture elements despite being too unevolved to have made them itself. It was polluted by an asymptotic-giant-branch companion that is now a white dwarf.

= S-type star
{c}
{parent=Wind mass transfer in a binary star}
{wiki}

An S-type star has strong zirconium-oxide bands and enhanced slow-neutron-capture elements. Intrinsic S-type stars are thermally pulsing asymptotic giants, while extrinsic S-type stars were polluted by a former asymptotic-giant-branch companion.

= Cataclysmic variable
{parent=Binary star}
{wiki}

A cataclysmic variable is a close binary in which a Roche-lobe-filling low-mass star transfers matter to a white dwarf. Angular-momentum loss drives long-term transfer, while unstable burning of accumulated hydrogen produces classical novae.

= Magnetic braking of a binary star
{parent=Cataclysmic variable}

A magnetized wind removes spin angular momentum from a cool donor star. Tidal synchronization replenishes that spin from the orbit, so the wind indirectly removes orbital angular momentum and drives the binary toward shorter separation.

= Isotropic re-emission from a binary star
{parent=Cataclysmic variable}

In isotropic re-emission, transferred mass is expelled from the vicinity of the accretor and carries the accretor's specific orbital angular momentum. The total binary mass and orbital angular momentum then decrease even though the accretor retains no net mass.

= Supernova kick in a binary star
{parent=Binary star}

Asymmetric ejecta or neutrino emission can impart a natal kick to a compact remnant. The impulsive change in relative velocity and total mass can alter the binary's semimajor axis and eccentricity or unbind the system.

= Gravitational-wave emission from a binary system
{parent=Binary star}

An accelerating binary mass quadrupole emits gravitational waves. The resulting energy and angular-momentum loss shrinks and circularizes a detached compact binary, with the evolution accelerating sharply as its separation decreases.

= Equipotential stellar-structure approximation
{parent=Binary star}

The equipotential stellar-structure approximation replaces distorted closed equipotentials in a synchronized binary by one-dimensional shells labeled by enclosed volume and mass.

= Volume-equivalent radius
{title2=$r'$}
{parent=Equipotential stellar-structure approximation}

The volume-equivalent radius of a closed surface enclosing volume $V$ is defined by $V=4\pi r'^3/3$.

= Roche lobe
{c}
{parent=Binary star}
{wiki}

A Roche lobe is the region around one component of a circular binary inside the critical effective-potential surface through the inner Lagrange point.

= Roche-lobe overflow
{c}
{parent=Roche lobe}
{wiki}

Roche-lobe overflow begins when a star fills its Roche lobe and matter flows through the inner Lagrange point toward its companion.

= Conservative binary mass transfer
{parent=Roche-lobe overflow}

Conservative binary mass transfer moves matter between the components while preserving total binary mass and orbital angular momentum.

= Dynamical stability of binary mass transfer
{parent=Conservative binary mass transfer}

Binary mass transfer is dynamically stable when the donor's radius decreases relative to its Roche-lobe radius after a small mass loss, reducing overflow instead of amplifying it.

= White-dwarf mass--orbital-period relation
{parent=Roche-lobe overflow}

Stable envelope stripping of a low-mass red giant leaves a helium white dwarf. Because the giant radius is determined mainly by its core mass and fixes its Roche-lobe radius at detachment, the final binary orbital period is tightly related to the white-dwarf mass.

= Common envelope
{parent=Roche-lobe overflow}
{wiki}

A common-envelope phase occurs when unstable binary interaction engulfs both stellar cores in one envelope. Orbital energy and angular momentum may eject the envelope, leaving a compact binary, or the cores may merge.

= Common-envelope energy formalism
{parent=Common envelope}

The common-envelope energy formalism equates a fraction $\alpha_{\rm CE}$ of the released orbital energy to the envelope binding energy, often written $E_{\rm bind}=GM_dM_{\rm env}/(\lambda R_d)$. It estimates the post-envelope separation but compresses uncertain hydrodynamics into $\alpha_{\rm CE}$ and $\lambda$.

= Double-degenerate Type Ia supernova scenario
{c}
{parent=Common envelope}

The double-degenerate scenario forms a close pair of carbon--oxygen white dwarfs, usually through mass transfer and one or more common-envelope phases. Gravitational radiation then shrinks the orbit until merger or unstable transfer triggers thermonuclear disruption.

= Algol binary
{c}
{parent=Roche-lobe overflow}
{wiki=Algol_variable}

An Algol binary is a semidetached system in which an evolved, less massive donor fills its Roche lobe while its more massive companion remains near the main sequence. The apparently reversed evolutionary state records an earlier phase of mass transfer that reversed the mass ratio.

= Astrophysical disk
{parent=Astrophysics}
{wiki}

= Astrophysical disc
{synonym}

An astrophysical disk is a flattened rotating distribution of gas, dust, stars, or other matter whose orbital motion strongly influences its structure and evolution.

= Keplerian disk
{c}
{parent=Astrophysical disk}

= Keplerian rotation
{c}
{synonym}

A Keplerian disk rotates with angular frequency $\Omega_K=\sqrt{GM/r^3}$ set mainly by a central mass, so $\Omega_K\propto r^{-3/2}$.

= Thin disk
{parent=Astrophysical disk}
{wiki}

A thin disk has vertical semi-thickness $H$ much smaller than cylindrical radius $r$. Vertical gravity is then approximately linear in height and orbital dynamics is nearly planar.

= Razor-thin disk approximation
{parent=Thin disk}

The razor-thin disk approximation replaces a disk's vertical density profile by $\rho(x,y,z)=\Sigma(x,y)\delta(z)$. It is useful for horizontal dynamics on scales much longer than the disk thickness.

= Razor-thin disk Poisson kernel
{parent=Razor-thin disk approximation}

For a horizontal Fourier mode of <wavenumber> $k>0$, the midplane gravitational potential of a razor-thin disk is
$$
\widehat\Phi_m(\mathbf k)=-\frac{2\pi G}{k}\widehat\Sigma(\mathbf k).
$$

= Surface density of a disk
{title2=$\Sigma$}
{parent=Thin disk}

The surface density of a disk is its volume density integrated vertically, $\Sigma=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\rho\,dz$.

= Vertical structure of an astrophysical disk
{parent=Thin disk}

The vertical structure of a thin disk is set by the balance among vertical gravity, pressure, self-gravity, magnetic forces, and motions of gas and solids.

= Disk scale height
{title2=$H$}
{parent=Vertical structure of an astrophysical disk}

The disk scale height is the characteristic vertical thickness over which density or pressure decreases. Vertical hydrostatic balance in a thin Keplerian gas disk gives $H\simeq c_s/\Omega_K$.

= Vertical epicyclic frequency
{title2=$\Omega_z$}
{parent=Vertical structure of an astrophysical disk}

The vertical epicyclic frequency is the frequency of a small vertical oscillation about a circular orbit. In an axisymmetric potential, $\Omega_z^2=\partial_z^2\Phi$ at the midplane; in a spherical potential it equals the circular orbital frequency.

= Dust settling in an astrophysical disk
{parent=Vertical structure of an astrophysical disk}

Dust settling is the vertical drift of solid particles toward a disk's midplane under gravity and <drag force>. In the strong-drag limit, a particle with <aerodynamic stopping time> $\tau$ settles through a linear vertical gravitational field at rate $\Omega_z^2\tau$.

= Homologous vertical motion of an astrophysical disk
{parent=Vertical structure of an astrophysical disk}

In homologous vertical motion, every height scales by one time-dependent thickness $H(t)$, so $z=H(t)\zeta$ and the vertical velocity is $u_z=\dot H\zeta$. A fixed dimensionless density profile then expands and contracts without changing shape.

= Vertical breathing mode of an astrophysical disk
{title2=$\omega_H$}
{parent=Homologous vertical motion of an astrophysical disk}

The vertical breathing mode is a coherent oscillation of a disk's thickness. For an adiabatic gas with pressure proportional to $H^{1-\gamma}$ in a harmonic vertical potential, $\omega_H=\sqrt{\gamma+1}\,\Omega_z$.

= Accretion disk
{parent=Astrophysical disk}
{wiki}

= Accretion disc
{synonym}

An accretion disk transports mass inward and angular momentum outward through stresses, while dissipating orbital energy as heat.

= Accretion flow
{parent=Accretion disk}

An accretion flow carries matter inward through a gravitationally bound system. In a disk, angular-momentum transport permits the inward drift while stresses carry angular momentum outward.

= Accretion rate
{title2=$\dot M$}
{parent=Accretion disk}

The accretion rate is the mass crossing an inward-oriented cylindrical surface per unit time. A common convention takes $\dot M>0$ for inward flow, so the outward radial mass flux is $-\dot M$.

= Hyperaccreting black-hole disk
{parent=Accretion disk}

A hyperaccreting black-hole disk has an accretion rate so large that photon cooling can be trapped and neutrino emission can dominate the cooling of its hot dense inner region. Such disks are models for the central engines of some gamma-ray bursts.

= Neutrino-dominated accretion flow
{title2=NDAF}
{parent=Hyperaccreting black-hole disk}

A neutrino-dominated accretion flow is a hyperaccreting disk whose inner plasma cools primarily through neutrino-producing weak interactions.

= Viscous evolution of an accretion disk
{parent=Accretion disk}

Viscous disk evolution combines mass conservation with radial transport of angular momentum by stress. In a steady Keplerian disk, the sum of advective and stress-carried angular-momentum fluxes is independent of radius.

= Viscous timescale
{title2=$t_\nu$}
{parent=Viscous evolution of an accretion disk}

The viscous timescale is the radial diffusion time $t_\nu\sim R^2/\nu$ associated with a kinematic viscosity $\nu$ on length scale $R$.

= Viscous torque in an accretion disk
{title2=$\mathcal G$}
{parent=Viscous evolution of an accretion disk}

For vertically integrated kinematic viscosity $\bar\nu$, the viscous torque across a cylinder of radius $r$ is
$$
\mathcal G=-2\pi\bar\nu\Sigma r^3\frac{d\Omega}{dr}.
$$

= Zero-torque inner boundary condition
{parent=Viscous torque in an accretion disk}

The zero-torque inner boundary condition sets the viscous torque to zero where nearly circular disk flow joins a plunging or stress-free region.

= Plunging region of a black-hole accretion disk
{parent=Zero-torque inner boundary condition}

The plunging region begins near the <innermost stable circular orbit>, where nearly circular motion becomes unstable and radial inflow becomes rapid. If the inflow time is shorter than the stress-transport time, the gas approximately conserves its <specific angular momentum> there.

= Surface magnetic torque on an accretion disk
{title2=$\mathcal T$}
{parent=Viscous evolution of an accretion disk}

= Surface magnetic torque
{synonym}

= Magnetic torque
{synonym}

A magnetic field crossing the two faces of an accretion disk exerts a surface torque through its azimuthal and vertical Maxwell stress. This torque can remove angular momentum without relying on radial internal stress.

= Keplerian accretion disk
{c}
{parent=Viscous evolution of an accretion disk}

In a steady Keplerian accretion disk with zero inner torque,
$$
\bar\nu\Sigma=\frac{\dot M}{3\pi}\left[1-\left(\frac{r_{\rm in}}r\right)^{1/2}\right].
$$

= Viscous instability of an accretion disk
{parent=Viscous evolution of an accretion disk}

A viscous instability occurs when the viscous mass flux acts as anti-diffusion. In a local Keplerian disk this happens at long wavelength when $d(\nu\Sigma)/d\Sigma<0$: a surface-density enhancement then transports angular momentum less effectively and grows.

= Viscous spreading of a narrow planetary ring
{parent=Viscous instability of an accretion disk}

In a local Keplerian sheet, slow viscous drift gives $\partial_t\Sigma=3\partial_x^2(\nu\Sigma)$. For $\nu=A\Sigma^2$, the compact profile $\Sigma=\sigma\sqrt{1-x^2/w^2}$ preserves its shape, conserves $\sigma w$, and spreads with $w\propto t^{1/4}$ at late times.

= Decretion disk
{parent=Viscous evolution of an accretion disk}
{wiki}

A decretion disk is fed near its inner edge and transports mass outward. Rapidly rotating Be stars can sustain gaseous decretion disks whose outer material is removed by a companion, radiation, or a wind.

= Be star
{c}
{parent=Decretion disk}
{wiki}

A Be star is a rapidly rotating B-type star showing Balmer emission from a circumstellar gaseous decretion disk.

= Alpha disk
{title2=$\alpha$-disk}
{parent=Accretion disk}
{wiki}

The alpha-disk prescription writes turbulent kinematic viscosity as $\nu=\alpha c_sH\simeq\alpha c_s^2/\Omega$, with dimensionless $\alpha\lesssim1$. It reflects eddies no larger than the disk thickness and no faster than the sound speed.

= Accretion-disk self-gravity
{parent=Alpha disk}

Accretion-disk self-gravity becomes important when the disk's own gravitational field is comparable with the central object's restoring field. The outer disk can then become gravitationally unstable, fragment, and form stars.

= Shakura--Sunyaev thin disk
{c}
{parent=Alpha disk}
{wiki}

The Shakura--Sunyaev thin disk is a geometrically thin, optically thick, radiatively efficient accretion-disk model whose turbulent stress is parametrized by the alpha prescription.

= Multitemperature blackbody disk
{parent=Shakura--Sunyaev thin disk}

A multitemperature blackbody disk is an optically thick disk approximated as concentric blackbody annuli of different effective temperatures. If $T\propto R^{-3/4}$, its intermediate-frequency spectrum satisfies $S_\nu\propto\nu^{1/3}$.

= Pressure-opacity zones of a Shakura--Sunyaev thin disk
{c}
{parent=Shakura--Sunyaev thin disk}

The standard thin-disk solution separates approximately into an inner radiation-pressure and electron-scattering zone, a middle gas-pressure and electron-scattering zone, and an outer gas-pressure and free-free-opacity zone.

= Subsonic radial drift in a thin disk
{parent=Shakura--Sunyaev thin disk}

In a steady thin alpha disk, $|u_R|/c_s$ is of order $\alpha H/R\ll1$, whereas $u_\phi/c_s$ is of order $R/H\gg1$. Matter therefore completes many nearly circular orbits during its slow viscous drift inward.

= Standard thin-disk dissipation flux
{parent=Shakura--Sunyaev thin disk}

For a steady Keplerian disk with a zero-torque inner edge $R_{\rm in}$, the flux summed over both faces is
$$
F_{\rm diss}(R)=\frac{3GM\dot M}{4\pi R^3}
\left[1-\left(\frac{R_{\rm in}}R\right)^{1/2}\right].
$$

= Standard thin-disk luminosity
{parent=Standard thin-disk dissipation flux}

Integrating the <standard thin-disk dissipation flux> gives
$$
L_{\rm thin}=\frac{GM\dot M}{2R_{\rm in}}
=\frac12\dot M R_{\rm in}^2\Omega_{K,\rm in}^2.
$$
Half of the Newtonian potential-energy decrease remains as orbital kinetic energy until the inner edge.

= Magnetorotational instability
{title2=MRI}
{parent=Alpha disk}
{wiki}

The magnetorotational instability destabilizes a magnetized differentially rotating flow when angular velocity decreases outward. Magnetic tension couples neighboring fluid elements, allowing the inner one to lose angular momentum and fall inward while the outer one gains angular momentum and moves outward.

= Critical wavelength of the magnetorotational instability
{title2=$\lambda_{\rm crit}$}
{parent=Magnetorotational instability}

For a vertical magnetic field in a Keplerian disk, axisymmetric vertical MRI modes are unstable when $k^2v_A^2<3\Omega^2$. The shortest unstable wavelength is $\lambda_{\rm crit}=2\pi v_A/(\sqrt3\Omega)$.

= Thermal stability of an accretion disk
{parent=Alpha disk}

A local thermal equilibrium is stable when a small temperature increase raises cooling faster than heating at fixed surface density. The standard total-pressure alpha prescription makes a radiation-pressure-dominated, Thomson-opacity disk thermally unstable.

= Thermal instability of a radiation-pressure-dominated alpha disk
{parent=Thermal stability of an accretion disk}

At fixed surface density, a total-pressure alpha disk supported by radiation pressure has viscous heating $Q^+\propto T_c^8$ but radiative cooling $Q^-\propto T_c^4$ when electron scattering supplies a nearly constant opacity. Heating therefore wins after a positive temperature perturbation.

= Gravitational instability of an astrophysical disk
{parent=Astrophysical disk}
{wiki}

Disk self-gravity can overcome pressure and rotational support, producing growing density disturbances, spiral structure, or fragmentation.

= Secular gravitational instability of an astrophysical disk
{parent=Gravitational instability of an astrophysical disk}

Secular gravitational instability uses dissipation to weaken the epicyclic support that stabilizes a disk dynamically. It can therefore grow more slowly than an orbital time in parameter ranges where the inviscid disk is stable.

= Toomre stability criterion
{title2=$Q$}
{c}
{parent=Gravitational instability of an astrophysical disk}
{wiki=Toomre's_stability_criterion}

For a razor-thin isothermal gas disk,
$$
Q=\frac{c_s\kappa}{\pi G\Sigma}.
$$
Axisymmetric disturbances are stable when $Q\geq1$: pressure stabilizes short wavelengths, epicyclic motion stabilizes long wavelengths, and self-gravity drives intermediate wavelengths.

= Gravito-turbulent self-regulation
{parent=Toomre stability criterion}

In a cooling self-gravitating disk, $Q<1$ excites gravitational turbulence whose dissipation raises the sound speed, while $Q>1$ suppresses that heating and lets cooling lower the sound speed. A sustained state can therefore regulate itself near $Q\simeq1$.

= Gravitoturbulent viscosity
{parent=Gravito-turbulent self-regulation}

A mixing-length estimate for gravitational turbulence takes the largest unstable wavelength $\ell$ and orbital growth rate $\Omega$, giving turbulent velocity $u_{\rm turb}\sim\Omega\ell$ and kinematic viscosity $\nu\sim u_{\rm turb}\ell\sim\Omega\ell^2$.

= Polytropic vertical structure of a self-gravitating disk
{parent=Toomre stability criterion}

For $P=K\rho^{1+1/n}$, dimensionless pseudo-enthalpy $w=n(\rho/\rho_0)^{1/n}$, and $\zeta=\Omega z/c_0$, vertical hydrostatic balance and the <Poisson equation> reduce to
$$
w''+\frac4{n^nQ_0}w^n=-1.
$$
The midplane conditions are $w(0)=n$ and $w'(0)=0$, and the first zero gives the disk surface.

= Self-gravitating radius of an accretion disk
{title2=$R_{\rm sg}$}
{parent=Toomre stability criterion}

The self-gravitating radius of an accretion disk is the radius at which its <Toomre stability criterion> reaches $Q=1$. Beyond it, a smooth thin disk is vulnerable to spiral structure, gravitational transport, or fragmentation.

= Shearing sheet
{parent=Gravitational instability of an astrophysical disk}
{wiki}

The shearing sheet is a local Cartesian approximation to a differentially rotating disk. It retains Coriolis force, linearized tidal gravity, and a background linear shear.

= Kida vortex
{c}
{parent=Shearing sheet}

A Kida vortex is an exact elliptical patch of uniform vorticity embedded in a linear shear flow. In a Keplerian shearing sheet its aspect ratio controls both the circulation period and the stability of three-dimensional perturbations.

= Stratified incompressible shearing sheet
{parent=Shearing sheet}

The stratified incompressible shearing sheet adds a buoyancy displacement $\theta$ with $D\theta/Dt=u_z$ and acceleration $-N^2\theta\mathbf e_z$. Axisymmetric plane waves obey
$$
\omega^2=\frac{k_z^2\Omega^2+k_x^2N^2}{k_x^2+k_z^2},
$$
interpolating between inertial and buoyancy oscillations.

= Keplerian shearing sheet
{c}
{parent=Shearing sheet}

A Keplerian shearing sheet has background velocity $\mathbf u_0=-(3/2)\Omega x\mathbf e_y$, radial shear rate $S=3\Omega/2$, and radial epicyclic frequency $\kappa_r=\Omega$.

= Vortensity
{title2=$\zeta$}
{parent=Shearing sheet}
{wiki}

Vortensity is <absolute vorticity> divided by surface density. In a two-dimensional inviscid barotropic flow, it is conserved along fluid trajectories.

= Linearized vortensity
{parent=Vortensity}

Linearized vortensity is the first-order perturbation of vortensity about a background flow. For a shearing wave in a uniform Keplerian sheet, its amplitude is constant.

= Linearized vortensity conservation
{parent=Linearized vortensity}

Linearized vortensity conservation is the perturbative form of material vortensity conservation. In an inviscid barotropic shearing sheet, the linearized vortensity obeys $Df'/Dt=0$.

= Shearing wave
{parent=Shearing sheet}

A shearing wave is a local disturbance whose wavevector evolves under background shear. In a Keplerian sheet, $k_x(t)=k_x(0)+(3/2)\Omega k_yt$.

= Shearing-wave ansatz
{parent=Shearing wave}

The shearing-wave ansatz writes a perturbation as a time-dependent amplitude times $\exp[i k_x(t)x+i k_yy]$, choosing $k_x(t)$ so that background advection does not leave an explicit factor of position in the amplitude equations.

= Shearing-wave oscillator
{parent=Shearing wave}

For an unforced inertial-acoustic disturbance, the shearing-wave amplitude obeys a time-dependent oscillator equation whose frequency depends on $k_x(t)=k_x(0)+Sk_yt$. Fourier transformation of the corresponding stationary forced equation turns radial wavenumber into the oscillator's time-like coordinate.

= Swing of a shearing wave
{parent=Shearing wave}

During the swing of a shearing wave, the radial wavenumber passes through zero and the disturbance changes from leading to trailing before winding ever more tightly.

= Inertial-acoustic wave
{parent=Shearing sheet}

An inertial-acoustic wave in a rotating compressible disk is restored jointly by pressure and epicyclic motion. For an axisymmetric radial wave in a Keplerian sheet, $\omega^2=\Omega^2+c_s^2k_x^2$.

= Satellite-forced density wave in an astrophysical disk
{parent=Shearing sheet}

A satellite-forced density wave is a compressive disk response to the satellite's periodic tidal potential. In a local corotating sheet, propagating zones lie where the relative orbital motion is supersonic.

= Thermal relaxation in a self-gravitating disk
{parent=Shearing sheet}

Thermal relaxation drives the local sound speed toward an equilibrium value on a cooling time $\tau$. Fast relaxation gives an isothermal response, while slow relaxation gives an adiabatic response.

= Shearing-sheet tidal potential
{title2=$\Phi_t$}
{parent=Shearing sheet}

For a frame rotating about a Keplerian circular orbit, radial gravity and centrifugal acceleration combine to give $\Phi_t=-3\Omega^2x^2/2$.

= Viscous-convective instability
{parent=Shearing sheet}

Viscous-convective instability is slow growth in a rotating fluid with an adverse buoyancy gradient when viscosity weakens epicyclic stabilization.

= Radial epicyclic frequency
{title2=$\kappa_r$}
{parent=Astrophysical disk}
{wiki}

The radial epicyclic frequency describes small radial oscillations about a circular orbit. For specific angular momentum $h(r)$, $\kappa_r^2=r^{-3}d(h^2)/dr$.

= Epicyclic motion
{parent=Radial epicyclic frequency}

Epicyclic motion is the small oscillation of a displaced fluid element or particle about a circular orbit. Its natural radial frequency is the <radial epicyclic frequency>.

= Plasma beta
{title2=$\beta$}
{parent=Astrophysical disk}
{wiki}

Plasma beta is the ratio of gas pressure to magnetic pressure, $\beta=P_g/P_m$.

= Sub-Keplerian rotation
{parent=Astrophysical disk}
{c}

= Sub-Keplerian
{c}
{synonym}

Sub-Keplerian rotation has angular speed below that of a circular <Keplerian orbit> at the same radius. In a gaseous disk, an outwardly decreasing pressure supplies some radial support and makes the gas sub-Keplerian.

= Streaming instability
{parent=Astrophysical disk}
{wiki}

The streaming instability is an instability of mutually coupled gas and solid particles in a disk. Relative drift and drag can concentrate the particles into dense structures.

= Streaming instability resonance
{parent=Streaming instability}

A streaming-instability resonance occurs when a gas wave has frequency $\omega=\mathbf k\mathbin\cdot\Delta\mathbf u$, matching the wave phase to the particle drift. The resonant forcing then produces algebraic particle-density growth in the test-particle limit.

= Galaxy
{parent=Astrophysics}
{wiki}

A galaxy is a gravitationally bound system of stars, interstellar gas and dust, stellar remnants, and dark matter.

= Galaxy stellar mass
{title2=$M_*$}
{parent=Galaxy}

Galaxy stellar mass is the total mass in a galaxy's stars and stellar remnants. It is usually inferred from luminosity and colour or a spectral-energy-distribution fit, so it depends on stellar-population models, dust treatment, and the adopted <initial mass function>.

= Schechter function
{title2=$\Phi(M)$}
{c}
{parent=Galaxy stellar mass}
{wiki}

= Stellar mass of a galaxy
{synonym}

The Schechter function models a galaxy luminosity or mass distribution as a low-value power law with an exponential high-value cutoff. In stellar-mass form,
$$
\Phi(M)\,dM=\frac{\Phi_*}{M_*}
\left(\frac M{M_*}\right)^\alpha e^{-M/M_*}\,dM.
$$

= Gas-to-stellar mass ratio
{title2=$r_g=M_g/M_*$}
{parent=Galaxy}

The gas-to-stellar mass ratio compares a galaxy's gas reservoir with its stellar mass. Different observational definitions may include only molecular gas, only atomic gas, or both with a helium correction.

= Galaxy evolution
{parent=Galaxy}
{wiki}

Galaxy evolution studies how galaxies acquire mass, form stars, enrich their gas, exchange material with their surroundings, change morphology, and merge over cosmic time.

= Dry galaxy merger
{parent=Galaxy evolution}
{wiki=Galaxy_merger}

A dry galaxy merger joins galaxies with little cold gas, so dissipation and merger-triggered star formation are weak. Conservation of energy followed by <virial theorem>[re-virialization] can make repeated dry minor mergers increase a galaxy's radius faster than its mass.

= Minor galaxy merger
{parent=Dry galaxy merger}

A minor galaxy merger has a small satellite-to-host mass ratio. Its stars are commonly deposited at large host radii after <tidal stripping> and <Chandrasekhar dynamical friction>.

= Stellar feedback
{parent=Galaxy evolution}
{wiki}

Stellar feedback is the transfer of energy, momentum, mass, and newly formed elements from stars to surrounding matter through radiation, winds, supernovae, and cosmic rays.

= Impulsive mass-loss expansion law
{parent=Stellar feedback}

If a virialized, well-mixed self-gravitating system instantaneously retains mass fraction $\epsilon>1/2$, homologous revirialization gives $R'/R=\epsilon/(2\epsilon-1)$. At or below one half, the idealized remnant is unbound.

= Adiabatic mass-loss expansion law
{parent=Stellar feedback}

If a self-gravitating system loses mass slowly compared with its orbital times, adiabatic invariance gives $MR=\text{constant}$ in the homologous approximation, so retaining fraction $\epsilon$ expands the system by $R'/R=1/\epsilon$.

= Galactic outflow
{parent=Galaxy evolution}

A galactic outflow carries gas, metals, energy, and momentum away from a galaxy under stellar or active-galactic-nucleus feedback. Whether the material escapes or later returns depends on its speed and the galactic potential.

= Mass-loading factor
{title2=$\lambda$}
{parent=Galactic outflow}

The mass-loading factor is the ratio of outflow rate to star-formation rate, $\lambda=\dot M_{\rm out}/\dot M_*$. Its value can depend on galaxy mass, radius, gas phase, and the surface through which the flow is measured.

= Galactic gas inflow
{parent=Galaxy evolution}

Galactic gas inflow supplies a galaxy from its circumgalactic medium, cooling halo, or intergalactic filaments. It can sustain <star formation> and dilute the metallicity of the existing interstellar gas.

= Galactic chemical evolution
{parent=Galaxy evolution}
{wiki}

Galactic chemical evolution follows the production, transport, dilution, locking, and loss of chemical elements as stars form and die and gas enters or leaves a galaxy.

= Galactic metallicity
{parent=Galactic chemical evolution}

Galactic metallicity measures the abundance of elements heavier than helium in a galaxy. Gas and stars retain different time-weighted records of enrichment.

= Gas-phase metallicity
{title2=$Z_g$}
{parent=Galactic metallicity}

Gas-phase metallicity is the heavy-element abundance of interstellar gas. In star-forming galaxies it is commonly inferred from nebular emission lines in <H II regions>, often through oxygen abundance $12+\log_{10}(\mathrm O/\mathrm H)$.

= Stellar metallicity
{title2=$Z_*$}
{parent=Galactic metallicity}

Stellar metallicity is the heavy-element abundance locked in stars. Integrated-light measurements are luminosity weighted unless a stellar-population reconstruction converts them to a mass-weighted quantity.

= Galaxy mass--metallicity relation
{parent=Galactic metallicity}

The galaxy mass--metallicity relation is the empirical tendency for galaxies with greater <galaxy stellar mass> to have higher <gas-phase metallicity> and <stellar metallicity>. Mass-dependent metal loss, gas inflow, star-formation efficiency, and recycling all contribute.

= Instantaneous recycling approximation
{title2=IRA}
{parent=Galactic chemical evolution}

The instantaneous recycling approximation treats short-lived stars as returning their ejecta immediately after formation while long-lived stars retain their mass indefinitely. It turns delayed chemical enrichment into local differential equations.

= Stellar yield
{title2=$y_z$}
{parent=Instantaneous recycling approximation}

The stellar yield is the newly synthesized metal mass returned to gas per unit mass locked into long-lived stars, under a specified recycling convention and <initial mass function>.

= Closed-box model of galactic chemical evolution
{parent=Galactic chemical evolution}

The closed-box model evolves a fixed total baryonic mass with no inflow or outflow. Under instantaneous recycling and constant yield it predicts $Z=y\log(1/\mu)$ for gas fraction $\mu$.

= Leaky-box model of galactic chemical evolution
{parent=Closed-box model of galactic chemical evolution}

The leaky-box model adds a <galactic outflow> to the closed box. If the outflow has the ambient gas metallicity and constant <mass-loading factor> $\lambda$, its effective yield is reduced to $y/(1+\lambda)$ when returned fractions are absorbed into the definitions.

= Effective yield
{title2=$y_{\rm eff}$}
{parent=Leaky-box model of galactic chemical evolution}

The effective yield is the yield that a simple closed-box relation would need to reproduce an observed gas fraction and metallicity. Outflow and pristine inflow generally lower it below the nucleosynthetic yield.

= G-dwarf problem
{c}
{parent=Closed-box model of galactic chemical evolution}
{wiki}

The G-dwarf problem is the deficit of low-metallicity long-lived G dwarfs near the Sun relative to the prediction of a constant-yield closed-box model. Prolonged accretion of metal-poor gas is a standard ingredient in its resolution.

= Metallicity distribution function
{title2=MDF}
{parent=G-dwarf problem}

A metallicity distribution function gives the number or mass of stars per metallicity interval. Its interpretation depends on sample selection, stellar lifetimes, spatial migration, and whether metallicity means total heavy-element abundance or a particular abundance ratio.

= Dwarf galaxy
{parent=Galaxy}
{wiki}

A dwarf galaxy has relatively low stellar mass and luminosity and is often strongly dark-matter dominated. Its shallow potential makes gas loss and stellar feedback dynamically important.

= Dark-matter core
{parent=Dwarf galaxy}

A dark-matter core is an inner halo region whose density approaches a finite value rather than continuing as a steep cusp. Repeated rapid potential fluctuations driven by bursty stellar feedback can transfer orbital energy to dark matter and create such a core.

= Elliptical galaxy
{parent=Galaxy}
{wiki}

An elliptical galaxy has a smooth spheroidal stellar distribution and little cold gas. Low-mass systems are commonly flattened fast rotators, while the most massive systems are commonly slowly rotating, anisotropic, and triaxial.

= Oblate isotropic rotator
{parent=Elliptical galaxy}

An oblate isotropic rotator is flattened primarily by ordered rotation while its random velocity dispersion is isotropic. Its location in a $v/\sigma$--ellipticity diagram provides a reference against which anisotropy is measured.

= Fast and slow rotator galaxy
{parent=Elliptical galaxy}

Fast rotators have substantial ordered stellar rotation and are often disky and nearly axisymmetric. Slow rotators have low specific angular momentum and are often massive, anisotropic, triaxial, and core-profiled.

= Local Group
{c}
{parent=Galaxy}
{wiki}

The Local Group is the gravitationally bound galaxy group containing the Milky Way, M31, and their satellite galaxies.

= Local Group timing argument
{c}
{parent=Local Group}
{wiki=Timing_argument}

The Local Group timing argument treats the Milky Way and M31 as a nearly radial two-body orbit whose present separation, approach speed, and cosmic age constrain their total mass.

= Milky Way
{c}
{parent=Galaxy}
{wiki}

The Milky Way is the barred spiral galaxy containing the Solar System.

= Interstellar medium
{title2=ISM}
{parent=Milky Way}
{wiki}

The interstellar medium is the gas, dust, magnetic field, and energetic-particle population between stars in a galaxy.

= Supernova remnant
{title2=SNR}
{parent=Interstellar medium}
{wiki}

A supernova remnant is the expanding ejecta and shocked ambient medium left by a supernova explosion.

= Adiabatic phase of a supernova remnant
{parent=Supernova remnant}

During its adiabatic phase, a supernova remnant loses little energy radiatively and expands as a hot, broad shocked flow rather than a thin cooled shell.

= Molecular cloud
{parent=Interstellar medium}
{wiki}

A molecular cloud is a cold dense region of the interstellar medium in which hydrogen is predominantly molecular and stars can form.

= Carbon-monoxide tracer of molecular hydrogen
{title2=CO tracer of $\mathrm H_2$}
{parent=Molecular cloud}

Rotational carbon-monoxide line emission traces cold molecular clouds because abundant molecular hydrogen is difficult to observe directly. A conversion factor $X_{\rm CO}$ relates integrated CO intensity to molecular-hydrogen column density.

= Interstellar dust
{parent=Interstellar medium}
{wiki}

Interstellar dust absorbs and scatters short-wavelength starlight and reradiates the absorbed energy thermally in the infrared.

= Interstellar extinction
{parent=Interstellar dust}
{wiki}

Interstellar extinction is the attenuation of light by absorption and scattering from dust between a source and observer. Its wavelength dependence commonly reddens a source as well as dimming it.

= H II region
{c}
{parent=Interstellar medium}
{wiki}

An H II region is a volume of ionized hydrogen surrounding one or more sources of ultraviolet photons. Photoionization heating, recombination, gas dynamics, and dust determine its structure and emission spectrum.

= Ionization front
{parent=H II region}
{wiki}

An ionization front is the transition between predominantly ionized and predominantly neutral gas. Its motion and thickness depend on photon supply, recombination, gas density, and hydrodynamic response.

= Photoionization equilibrium
{parent=H II region}

Photoionization equilibrium holds when the local or integrated photoionization rate equals the radiative recombination rate, so the ionized fraction is stationary.

= Recombination time
{title2=$t_{\rm rec}$}
{parent=Photoionization equilibrium}

The recombination time is the characteristic time for an ionized plasma to recombine after its ionizing source is removed. Uniform hydrogen with electron density $n_e$ and recombination coefficient $\alpha$ has $t_{\rm rec}=1/(\alpha n_e)$.

= Case B recombination
{c}
{title2=$\alpha_B$}
{parent=Photoionization equilibrium}

Case B recombination excludes direct recombinations to the ground state because their ionizing photons are assumed to be absorbed locally. The coefficient $\alpha_B$ therefore counts recombinations to excited states.

= Hydrogen-ionizing photon production rate
{title2=$Q_{\rm H}$}
{parent=H II region}

The hydrogen-ionizing photon production rate is the number of photons emitted per unit time above the hydrogen ionization threshold:
$$
Q_{\rm H}=\int_{\nu_0}^{\infty}\frac{L_\nu}{h\nu}\,d\nu.
$$

= Strömgren sphere
{c}
{parent=H II region}
{wiki}

A Strömgren sphere is the idealized ionized region around a steady source embedded in uniform hydrogen gas, bounded where integrated recombinations consume the source's ionizing-photon supply.

= Strömgren radius
{title2=$R_S$}
{c}
{parent=Strömgren sphere}

For ionizing-photon rate $Q_{\rm H}$, uniform pure-hydrogen density $n$, and <Case B recombination> coefficient $\alpha_B$, the Strömgren radius is
$$
R_S=\left(\frac{3Q_{\rm H}}{4\pi\alpha_Bn^2}\right)^{1/3}.
$$

= Ionization-front growth of a Strömgren sphere
{c}
{parent=Strömgren sphere}

Before hydrodynamic expansion matters, photon counting in a uniform initially neutral medium gives
$$
R(t)=R_S\left(1-e^{-t/t_{\rm rec}}\right)^{1/3}.
$$
The ionization front approaches the Strömgren radius on the recombination time.

= Dusty Strömgren sphere
{c}
{parent=Strömgren sphere}

A dusty Strömgren sphere includes ionizing photons absorbed by dust before they can ionize hydrogen. Dust therefore decreases the ionized radius below the dust-free <Strömgren radius> at fixed source luminosity and gas density.

= Neutral-hydrogen 21-centimeter line
{title2=H I 21-cm line}
{parent=Interstellar medium}
{wiki}

The neutral-hydrogen 21-centimeter line is the ground-state hyperfine transition of atomic hydrogen. Its brightness and Doppler shift map atomic-gas column density and line-of-sight velocity.

= Cosmic ray
{parent=Interstellar medium}
{wiki}

A cosmic ray is a high-energy charged particle propagating through space. Galactic cosmic-ray electrons emit synchrotron and inverse-Compton radiation, while cosmic-ray nuclei produce gamma rays through collisions with gas.

= Galactic multiwavelength astronomy
{parent=Milky Way}

Galactic multiwavelength astronomy combines spectral bands that respond differently to stars, gas, dust, magnetic fields, temperature, and energetic particles.

= Galactic synchrotron emission
{parent=Galactic multiwavelength astronomy}

Galactic synchrotron emission is nonthermal radio radiation from relativistic electrons spiralling in the Galactic magnetic field. Its broad latitude distribution reflects cosmic-ray transport and the magnetic halo.

= Galactic gamma-ray emission
{parent=Galactic multiwavelength astronomy}

Diffuse Galactic gamma rays arise mainly from neutral-pion decay after cosmic-ray nuclear collisions, electron bremsstrahlung in gas, and inverse-Compton scattering from radiation fields.

= Galactic scale height
{title2=$H$}
{parent=Galactic multiwavelength astronomy}

The Galactic scale height measures the vertical thickness of a tracer. Gravity, velocity dispersion, thermal pressure, magnetic and cosmic-ray support, particle transport, and source lifetime determine its value.

= Galactic bar
{parent=Milky Way}
{wiki}

The Galactic bar is the elongated central stellar structure of the Milky Way. Extinction-corrected near-infrared standard candles and gas position--velocity maps constrain its distance, orientation, and three-dimensional shape.

= Star formation rate
{title2=SFR}
{parent=Galaxy}
{wiki}

The star formation rate is the mass converted into stars per unit time. Infrared luminosity traces dust-reprocessed light from young stars, while molecular-gas tracers locate their cold fuel.

= Galaxy rotation curve
{parent=Galaxy}
{wiki}

A galaxy rotation curve gives circular speed as a function of radius. Its decomposition into stellar, gaseous, and dark-matter contributions is generally nonunique.

= Circular speed
{title2=$v_c$}
{parent=Galaxy rotation curve}

In a spherical potential, the circular speed satisfies
$$
v_c^2(r)=r\frac{d\Phi}{dr}=\frac{GM(<r)}r.
$$
It measures the gravitational field even when the system itself is not supported by circular motion.

= Flat galaxy rotation curve
{parent=Galaxy rotation curve}

A flat galaxy rotation curve has approximately constant circular speed $v_c$ over a radial interval. Under spherical symmetry it implies $M(\lt r)=v_c^2r/G$ and mass density $\rho(r)=v_c^2/(4\pi Gr^2)$.

= Exponential galactic disk
{parent=Galaxy rotation curve}

A thin exponential galactic disk has surface density $\Sigma(R)=\Sigma_0e^{-R/R_d}$ and total mass $2\pi\Sigma_0R_d^2$.

= Maximum disk
{parent=Exponential galactic disk}
{wiki}

The maximum disk hypothesis assigns the largest stellar mass-to-light ratio compatible with a measured rotation curve, conventionally making the disk supply about $85\%$ of the circular speed near $2.2R_d$.

= Disk--halo degeneracy
{parent=Galaxy rotation curve}

The disk--halo degeneracy is the ability of different stellar mass-to-light ratios and dark-halo profiles to reproduce nearly the same total galaxy rotation curve.

= Galaxy dynamics
{parent=Galaxy}
{wiki}

Galaxy dynamics studies the gravitational motions and evolution of stars, gas, dark matter, and satellite systems in galaxies.

= Chandrasekhar dynamical friction
{c}
{parent=Galaxy dynamics}
{wiki}

Chandrasekhar dynamical friction is the drag on a massive body produced by its gravitational wake in a background of lighter particles. Its magnitude is proportional to the body's mass and the background density.

= Tidal radius
{title2=$r_t$}
{parent=Galaxy dynamics}
{wiki}

The tidal radius bounds material that remains gravitationally attached to a satellite in the differential gravitational field of its host.

= Jacobi tidal radius
{c}
{parent=Tidal radius}

For a low-mass satellite of mass $M_s$ on a circular orbit of radius $r_o$ in a spherical host,
$$
r_t=r_o\left[
\frac{M_s}{(3-d\log M_h/d\log r_o)M_h(r_o)}
\right]^{1/3}.
$$
This local approximation requires $r_t\ll r_o$.

= Tidal stripping
{parent=Tidal radius}
{wiki}

Tidal stripping removes satellite material outside the tidal radius. The resulting mass loss weakens dynamical friction and can greatly delay or prevent a merger.

= Tidal tail
{parent=Tidal stripping}
{wiki}

A tidal tail is an elongated stream of material removed by a differential gravitational field. A satellite generally produces one leading and one trailing tail as stripped material enters nearby orbits with slightly different energy and angular momentum.

= Dynamical-friction stalling by tidal stripping
{parent=Tidal stripping}

Dynamical-friction stalling by tidal stripping occurs when inward motion shrinks a satellite's tidal radius so rapidly that its remaining mass and frictional drag tend to zero before it reaches the host center.

= Stellar dynamics
{parent=Galaxy dynamics}
{wiki}

Stellar dynamics studies the collective gravitational motion of stars in galaxies and star clusters.

= Star cluster
{parent=Stellar dynamics}
{wiki}

A star cluster is a gravitationally associated population of stars formed in a compact region.

= Globular cluster
{parent=Star cluster}
{wiki}

A globular cluster is an old, dense, approximately spheroidal star cluster whose long-term evolution is shaped by two-body relaxation and the tidal field of its host galaxy.

= Oblate spheroid
{parent=Stellar dynamics}
{wiki=Spheroid#Oblate_spheroids}

An oblate spheroid is obtained by rotating an ellipse about its shorter axis. If its equatorial semiaxis is one and its polar semiaxis is $q\leq1$, then $q$ is its intrinsic axis ratio.

= Projected axis ratio of an oblate spheroid
{title2=$Q$}
{parent=Oblate spheroid}

If the symmetry axis of an <oblate spheroid> makes angle $i$ with the line of sight, its projected axis ratio satisfies
$$
Q^2=\cos^2i+q^2\sin^2i.
$$
Thus a face-on spheroid appears round and an edge-on spheroid reveals its intrinsic axis ratio.

= Random-orientation distribution of an apparent oblate axis ratio
{parent=Projected axis ratio of an oblate spheroid}

For an unoriented symmetry axis chosen uniformly on the sphere, $\cos i$ is uniform on $[0,1]$. At fixed intrinsic ratio $q$, the <change-of-variables formula for a probability density> gives
$$
p(Q\mid q)=\frac{Q}{\sqrt{1-q^2}\sqrt{Q^2-q^2}},
\qquad q\leq Q\leq1.
$$

= Spherical isochrone model
{parent=Stellar dynamics}
{wiki=Isochrone_model}

The spherical isochrone model has potential
$$
\Phi(r)=-\frac{GM}{b+\sqrt{b^2+r^2}}.
$$
Its radial period depends only on orbital energy, while its <radial action> and frequencies are elementary functions of the integrals of motion.

= Radial action of the spherical isochrone model
{title2=$J_r$}
{parent=Spherical isochrone model}

For specific energy $E<0$ and specific angular momentum $L$,
$$
J_r=\frac{GM}{\sqrt{-2E}}
-\frac12\left(L+\sqrt{L^2+4GMb}\right).
$$
Consequently the Hamiltonian in actions is
$$
H(J_r,L)=-\frac{2(GM)^2}
{\left(2J_r+L+\sqrt{L^2+4GMb}\right)^2}.
$$

= Frequency ratio of the spherical isochrone model
{parent=Spherical isochrone model}

The radial and azimuthal frequencies obey
$$
\frac{\Omega_\phi}{\Omega_r}
=\frac12\left(1+\frac{L}{\sqrt{L^2+4GMb}}\right).
$$
The ratio tends to one half in the harmonic core and to one in the Keplerian exterior.

= Hypervirial model
{parent=Stellar dynamics}

The hypervirial family has relative potential
$$
\Psi(r)=\frac{GM}{(r^p+a^p)^{1/p}},
\qquad 0<p\leq2,
$$
and density
$$
\rho(r)=\frac{(p+1)Ma^p}{4\pi}
\frac{r^{p-2}}{(r^p+a^p)^{2+1/p}}.
$$
Its name comes from satisfying the scalar virial relation pointwise.

= Hypervirial distribution function
{parent=Hypervirial model}

The <hypervirial model> is generated self-consistently by a <constant-anisotropy distribution function> proportional to
$$
L^{p-2}\mathcal E^{(3p+1)/2}.
$$
Its <velocity-anisotropy parameter> is $\beta=1-p/2$.

= Local virial relation of the hypervirial model
{parent=Hypervirial model}

For the <hypervirial model>, $\langle v^2\rangle=\Psi/2=-\Phi/2$. The local kinetic- and potential-energy densities
$$
K=\frac12\rho\langle v^2\rangle,
\qquad
W=\frac12\rho\Phi
$$
therefore obey $2K+W=0$ at every radius, a stronger statement than the integrated <virial theorem>.

= Galactic distribution function
{title2=$f(\mathbf x,\mathbf v,t)$}
{parent=Stellar dynamics}

A galactic distribution function gives stellar mass or number per <phase space> volume. Integrating over velocity gives spatial density, while velocity moments and projection along the line of sight predict kinematic observables.

= Action-based galactic distribution function
{title2=$f(\mathbf J)$}
{parent=Galactic distribution function}

An action-based galactic distribution function is a nonnegative function of orbital actions. Actions are integrals of motion in an integrable potential, so $f(\mathbf J)$ automatically gives a steady collisionless model and can represent flattened or rotating systems.

= Schwarzschild orbit-superposition model
{c}
{parent=Galactic distribution function}

A Schwarzschild orbit-superposition model integrates a library of orbits in a trial gravitational potential and chooses nonnegative orbit weights to reproduce observed density and kinematics.

= Made-to-measure stellar-dynamical model
{parent=Galactic distribution function}

A made-to-measure stellar-dynamical model evolves particles in a trial potential while adjusting their weights so that projected observables approach measured constraints.

= Velocity dispersion
{title2=$\sigma$}
{parent=Galactic distribution function}
{wiki}

Velocity dispersion is the standard deviation of velocities about their mean. Its tensor form records anisotropy, while a line-of-sight measurement combines intrinsic kinematics with projection and observational weighting.

= Velocity-anisotropy parameter
{title2=$\beta$}
{parent=Velocity dispersion}

For spherical radial dispersion $\sigma_r^2$ and total two-component tangential dispersion $\sigma_t^2$, the velocity-anisotropy parameter is
$$
\beta=1-\frac{\sigma_t^2}{2\sigma_r^2}.
$$
Positive values are radially biased, zero is isotropic, and negative values are tangentially biased.

= Relative potential
{title2=$\Psi$}
{parent=Galactic distribution function}

In a bounded stellar system, a relative potential shifts the gravitational potential so that $\Psi$ vanishes at a chosen escape boundary. A common sign convention is $\Psi=\Phi_0-\Phi$.

= Relative energy
{title2=$\mathcal E$}
{parent=Relative potential}

Relative energy is the binding energy per unit mass measured from the chosen escape level, commonly $\mathcal E=\Psi-v^2/2$. Bound phase-space points have $\mathcal E>0$ in this convention.

= Constant-anisotropy distribution function
{parent=Galactic distribution function}

A spherical constant-anisotropy distribution function has
$$
f(\mathcal E,L)=L^{-2\beta}f_E(\mathcal E).
$$
Its velocity moments have the radius-independent <velocity-anisotropy parameter> $\beta$.

= Scale-free density-potential component
{parent=Constant-anisotropy distribution function}

For $f=f_0L^{-2\beta}\mathcal E^n$, velocity integration gives
$$
\rho=A r^{-2\beta}\Psi^{n+3/2-\beta}.
$$
Thus a scale-free monomial in radius and relative potential corresponds directly to a power-law constant-anisotropy distribution function.

= Jeans theorem
{c}
{parent=Galactic distribution function}
{wiki}

Jeans theorem states that a steady <collisionless Boltzmann equation> solution depends on phase-space coordinates only through <integrals of motion>, and that a nonnegative function of isolating integrals gives a steady collisionless distribution function on its domain.

= Two-body relaxation
{parent=Stellar dynamics}
{wiki}

Two-body relaxation is the cumulative randomization of stellar velocities by many weak gravitational encounters. Its timescale in a system of $N$ comparable stars is of order $N/\log N$ crossing times.

= Coulomb logarithm in stellar dynamics
{title2=$\log\Lambda$}
{parent=Two-body relaxation}

The Coulomb logarithm $\log\Lambda=\log(b_{\max}/b_{\min})$ collects the logarithmically equal contributions of gravitational encounters over a wide range of impact parameters. For a self-gravitating system of $N$ comparable stars, $\Lambda$ is of order $N$.

= Collisional stellar system
{parent=Two-body relaxation}

A stellar system is collisional on a timescale of interest when its two-body relaxation time is shorter than that timescale, so discrete encounters materially alter its distribution function.

= Collisionless Boltzmann equation
{c}
{parent=Stellar dynamics}
{wiki}

The collisionless Boltzmann equation states that the phase-space distribution function is constant along trajectories in a smooth gravitational potential: $dF/dt=0$.

= Jeans equation
{c}
{parent=Collisionless Boltzmann equation}
{wiki}

The Jeans equations are velocity moments of the <collisionless Boltzmann equation>. They relate density, mean motion, velocity dispersion, and gravitational acceleration without requiring a closed fluid equation of state.

= Spherical Jeans equation
{c}
{parent=Jeans equation}

For a steady nonrotating spherical system,
$$
\frac{d(\rho\sigma_r^2)}{dr}
+\frac{2\beta}{r}\rho\sigma_r^2
=-\rho\frac{d\Phi}{dr}.
$$
It relates radial pressure, velocity anisotropy, and the gravitational field.

= Scale-free spherical Jeans solution
{parent=Spherical Jeans equation}

For tracer density $\nu\propto r^{-\alpha}$, circular speed $V_c\propto r^\gamma$, and constant anisotropy $\beta$, the boundary-free scale-free solution is
$$
\sigma_r^2=\frac{V_c^2}{\alpha-2\beta-2\gamma},
$$
which requires a positive denominator.

= Mass--anisotropy--density degeneracy
{parent=Spherical Jeans equation}

The mass--anisotropy--density degeneracy is the ability of different gravitational mass profiles, tracer-density profiles, and orbital-anisotropy profiles to predict similar projected tracer kinematics through the Jeans equation.

= Tensor virial theorem
{parent=Jeans equation}
{wiki}

For an isolated collisionless system, the tensor virial theorem is $\frac12\ddot I_{jk}=2T_{jk}+\Pi_{jk}+W_{jk}$, separating ordered kinetic, random kinetic, and gravitational potential-energy tensors.

= Singular isothermal sphere
{title2=SIS}
{parent=Galaxy dynamics}
{wiki}

A singular isothermal sphere with one-dimensional velocity dispersion $\sigma$ has $\rho(r)=\sigma^2/(2\pi Gr^2)$, enclosed mass $M(<r)=2\sigma^2r/G$, and constant circular speed $\sqrt2\sigma$.

= Astrophysical black hole
{parent=Astrophysics}

An astrophysical black hole is a black hole studied through its formation, accretion, mergers, dynamical environment, and electromagnetic or gravitational-wave signatures.

= Black-hole spin
{title2=$a_*$}
{parent=Astrophysical black hole}

Black-hole spin is the angular momentum of a black hole, commonly expressed by the dimensionless Kerr parameter $a_*=cJ/(GM^2)$. It changes the <innermost stable circular orbit>, accretion efficiency, and relativistic frame dragging.

= Black-hole accretion
{parent=Astrophysical black hole}
{wiki}

Black-hole accretion converts gravitational binding energy of inflowing matter into heat, radiation, outflows, and growth of the black hole.

= Inflow time
{title2=$t_{\rm in}$}
{parent=Black-hole accretion}

The inflow time is the characteristic time for accreting matter to move radially by its current radius, $t_{\rm in}\sim R/|u_R|$.

= Paczyński-Wiita potential
{c}
{title2=$\Phi=-GM/(r-r_S)$}
{parent=Black-hole accretion}
{wiki}

The Paczyński-Wiita potential is a Newtonian pseudo-potential that reproduces the Schwarzschild innermost stable circular orbit at $r=3r_S$.

= Radiative efficiency of black-hole accretion
{title2=$\eta$}
{parent=Black-hole accretion}

The radiative efficiency is $\eta=L/(\dot M c^2)$. For a zero-torque thin disk it is $1-E_{\rm ISCO}$, the binding energy per unit rest mass released before matter crosses the innermost stable circular orbit.

= Maximal radiative efficiency of black-hole accretion
{title2=$\eta_{\max}$}
{parent=Radiative efficiency of black-hole accretion}

The maximal radiative efficiency is the available binding energy per unit accreted rest-mass energy when all energy released outside the capture boundary escapes as radiation. A relativistic zero-torque disk has $\eta_{\max}=1-E_{\rm ISCO}$.

= Innermost stable circular orbit
{title2=ISCO}
{parent=Radiative efficiency of black-hole accretion}
{wiki}

The innermost stable circular orbit is the smallest stable circular timelike orbit around a black hole. Its radius and orbital energy depend on black-hole spin and on whether the orbit is prograde or retrograde.

= Bondi accretion
{c}
{parent=Black-hole accretion}
{wiki}

Bondi accretion is steady spherical accretion from a uniform gas reservoir onto a compact gravitating object. In its adiabatic form, compression raises internal energy without providing the irreversible shear heating of a radiatively efficient disk.

= Bondi–Hoyle accretion
{c}
{parent=Bondi accretion}
{wiki}

= Bondi--Hoyle--Lyttleton accretion
{c}
{synonym}

Bondi--Hoyle--Lyttleton accretion is gravitational capture by a compact object moving through a gas. In the pressureless supersonic limit, ballistic streamlines focus into a downstream wake.

= Bondi--Hoyle--Lyttleton accretion rate
{title2=$\dot M_{\rm BHL}$}
{c}
{parent=Bondi–Hoyle accretion}

In the ballistic supersonic limit, streamline collisions bind gas with impact parameter $b<2GM/v_\infty^2$, giving
$$
\dot M_{\rm BHL}=\frac{4\pi G^2M^2\rho_\infty}{v_\infty^3}.
$$

= Isothermal Bondi accretion
{c}
{parent=Bondi accretion}

Isothermal Bondi accretion has $p=c_s^2\rho$ with constant sound speed. Its regular transonic solution crosses the <Bondi sonic point> and approaches free fall near the accretor.

= Isothermal Bondi equation
{c}
{parent=Isothermal Bondi accretion}

For inward speed $u>0$, steady spherical mass conservation and radial momentum balance give
$$
\left(u-\frac{c_s^2}{u}\right)\frac{du}{dr}
=\frac{2c_s^2}{r}-\frac{GM}{r^2}.
$$

= Bondi sonic point
{title2=$r_s$}
{c}
{parent=Isothermal Bondi equation}

The isothermal Bondi sonic point is $r_s=GM/(2c_s^2)$, where the regular transonic solution has $u=c_s$.

= Critical point of the isothermal Bondi equation
{parent=Bondi sonic point}

At the critical point, both the coefficient of $du/dr$ and the remaining right-hand side of the <isothermal Bondi equation> vanish, allowing a finite derivative through the sonic transition.

= Mass accretion rate
{title2=$\dot M$}
{parent=Bondi accretion}

The mass accretion rate is the mass entering an accretor per unit time. In a steady spherical flow, $\dot M=4\pi r^2\rho u$ is independent of radius.

= Transonic accretion in a steep dark-matter cusp
{title2=Transonic accretion in an $r^{-5/2}$ density cusp}
{parent=Mass accretion rate}

For a background gravitating density $\rho_{\rm dm}=\Psi_0r^{-5/2}$, the potential is $\Phi=-16\pi G\Psi_0r^{-1/2}$. A steady polytropic inflow has a regular sonic point only for $1<\gamma<9/7$, and its accretion rate scales as $\dot M\propto\rho_0G^4\Psi_0^4c_0^{-7}$.

= Free-fall speed
{title2=$v_{\rm ff}$}
{parent=Bondi accretion}

The Newtonian free-fall speed from rest at infinity toward a point mass is $v_{\rm ff}=\sqrt{2GM/r}$.

= Critical Bondi accretion rate for gamma equals five thirds
{parent=Bondi accretion}

For an adiabatic gas with $\gamma=5/3$, asymptotic density $\rho_0$, and sound speed $v_{s0}$ accreting onto a Newtonian point mass $M$, the largest globally subsonic steady rate is
$$
\dot M_{\rm crit}=\frac{\pi\rho_0(GM)^2}{v_{s0}^3}.
$$

= Radiatively inefficient accretion flow
{title2=RIAF}
{parent=Black-hole accretion}

A radiatively inefficient accretion flow carries a substantial fraction of locally generated energy with the gas instead of radiating it before accretion. It can occur at low density because emission is weak or at high optical depth because photons are trapped.

= Advection-dominated accretion flow
{title2=ADAF}
{parent=Radiatively inefficient accretion flow}
{wiki}

An advection-dominated accretion flow is a hot, geometrically thick, radiatively inefficient flow in which most dissipated energy remains in the gas and is carried inward rather than radiated locally.

= Two-temperature accretion flow
{parent=Advection-dominated accretion flow}

A two-temperature accretion flow has ions and electrons at substantially different temperatures because their Coulomb equilibration time exceeds the inflow time. Ions can retain most viscous heat while electrons, which radiate more effectively, remain cooler.

= Electron--proton thermal equilibration time
{title2=$t_{e-p}$}
{parent=Two-temperature accretion flow}

The electron--proton thermal equilibration time is the timescale on which Coulomb collisions exchange energy between electron and proton populations. Its inverse dependence on number density helps make dilute accretion flows two-temperature and radiatively inefficient.

= Slim accretion disk
{parent=Radiatively inefficient accretion flow}
{wiki=Slim_disc}

= Slim accretion disc
{synonym}

A slim accretion disk is an optically thick, moderately geometrically thick flow at a high accretion rate. Its inflow time can be shorter than its photon-diffusion time, so radial advection carries trapped radiation inward.

= Photon trapping in an accretion flow
{parent=Slim accretion disk}

Photon trapping occurs when the radiative diffusion time across an optically thick flow exceeds its inward advection time. The trapped radiation is carried inward, reducing the emergent radiative efficiency.

= Photon-trapping radius
{title2=$r_t$}
{parent=Photon trapping in an accretion flow}

The photon-trapping radius is where the outward <radiative diffusion> time equals the inward <inflow time>. Radiation generated inside it is predominantly advected toward the accretor.

= Luminous hot accretion flow
{title2=LHAF}
{parent=Radiatively inefficient accretion flow}

A luminous hot accretion flow has radiative cooling greater than local viscous heating, so compressive inward advection supplies the difference. In the convention $q_{\rm adv}=q^+-q^-$, it has $q_{\rm adv}<0$.

= Accretion-flow advection balance
{title2=$q_{\rm adv}=q^+-q^-$}
{parent=Radiatively inefficient accretion flow}

The accretion-flow advection balance compares local volumetric heating $q^+$, radiative cooling $q^-$, and energy transport with the gas. Positive $q_{\rm adv}$ means advection removes locally generated heat; zero gives local radiative balance; negative means advection supplies heat that is radiated locally.

= Eddington ratio
{title2=$f_{\rm Edd}$}
{parent=Black-hole accretion}
{wiki}

The Eddington ratio is the luminosity divided by the Eddington luminosity. At fixed radiative efficiency it also measures the accretion rate relative to the Eddington rate.

= Eddington accretion rate
{title2=$\dot M_{\rm Edd}$}
{c}
{parent=Eddington ratio}

An Eddington accretion rate converts the <Eddington luminosity> into a mass supply rate using an adopted reference efficiency $\eta_0$: $\dot M_{\rm Edd}=L_{\rm Edd}/(\eta_0c^2)$. The common choice $\eta_0=0.1$ gives $\dot M_{\rm Edd}=10L_{\rm Edd}/c^2$.

= Salpeter time
{title2=$t_{\rm Sal}$}
{c}
{parent=Eddington ratio}
{wiki}

The Salpeter time is the e-folding time for Eddington-limited black-hole growth. If $t_E=\sigma_Tc/(4\pi Gm_p)$, then $t_{\rm Sal}=\eta t_E/[(1-\eta)f_{\rm Edd}]$.

= Intermediate-mass black hole
{title2=IMBH}
{parent=Astrophysical black hole}
{wiki}

An intermediate-mass black hole has a mass between stellar remnants and supermassive black holes, approximately $10^2$--$10^5M_\odot$.

= Supermassive black hole
{title2=SMBH}
{parent=Astrophysical black hole}
{wiki}

A supermassive black hole has a mass of roughly $10^6M_\odot$ or more and commonly occupies the nucleus of a massive galaxy.

= Black-hole--bulge mass relation
{parent=Supermassive black hole}

The black-hole--bulge mass relation is the empirical correlation between a galaxy's central black-hole mass and the stellar mass of its spheroidal component. Locally, the black hole is typically of order $10^{-3}$--$10^{-2}$ of the bulge mass, with substantial scatter and possible redshift evolution.

= Active galactic nucleus
{title2=AGN}
{parent=Supermassive black hole}
{wiki}

An active galactic nucleus is a compact luminous galactic nucleus powered primarily by accretion onto a supermassive black hole.

= Quasar
{parent=Active galactic nucleus}
{wiki}

A quasar is an extremely luminous active galactic nucleus whose compact central emission can be observed across cosmological distances.

= Quasar light curve
{parent=Quasar}

A quasar light curve records its measured brightness as a function of time. Intrinsic accretion variability, measurement noise, and gravitational lensing can all contribute structure.

= Active-galactic-nucleus feedback
{title2=AGN feedback}
{parent=Active galactic nucleus}
{wiki}

Active-galactic-nucleus feedback transfers radiative or mechanical energy and momentum from black-hole accretion to surrounding galactic gas.

= Active-galactic-nucleus wind bubble
{title2=AGN wind bubble}
{parent=Active-galactic-nucleus feedback}

An active-galactic-nucleus wind bubble forms when a fast nuclear wind shocks against galactic gas. From inside outward it contains free wind, a reverse shock, shocked wind, a contact discontinuity, a swept-up shocked shell, a forward shock, and undisturbed interstellar gas.

= Momentum-driven outflow
{parent=Active-galactic-nucleus wind bubble}

An outflow is momentum driven when shocked driving material cools rapidly, so it cannot retain enough thermal energy to inflate a pressure-supported bubble. The swept shell then receives approximately the direct wind momentum flux, often of order $L/c$.

= Energy-driven outflow
{parent=Active-galactic-nucleus wind bubble}

An outflow is energy driven when cooling of the shocked wind is slow. Its hot bubble stores mechanical energy and expands by pressure work on the swept-up shell.

= Momentum boost
{parent=Energy-driven outflow}

A momentum boost is a shell momentum flux exceeding the direct input momentum flux. An energy-conserving wind of speed $v_w$ driving a shell of speed $v_s\ll v_w$ can give $\dot p_s/(L/c)$ of order $v_w/(2v_s)$.

= Dusty radiation-pressure-driven shell
{parent=Active-galactic-nucleus feedback}

A dusty radiation-pressure-driven shell is swept up when ultraviolet radiation from an active nucleus is absorbed by dust and reradiated in the infrared. Single scattering supplies up to roughly $L/c$ of momentum flux, while trapped infrared photons can enhance it by the infrared optical depth.

= Critical luminosity of a dusty shell
{title2=$L_{\rm crit}$}
{parent=Dusty radiation-pressure-driven shell}

The critical luminosity of a dusty shell is the luminosity at which radiation pressure balances gravity. For shell mass $M_s$, enclosed gravitating mass $M$, and ultraviolet and infrared optical depths,
$$
L_{\rm crit}=\frac{GcMM_s}{r^2(1+\tau_{\rm IR}-e^{-\tau_{\rm UV}})}.
$$

= Thin-shell momentum equation
{parent=Dusty radiation-pressure-driven shell}

For a spherical swept shell of mass $M_s(R)$, radial speed $\dot R$, driving force $F_d$, and enclosed gravitating mass $M(<R)$,
$$
\frac d{dt}[M_s(R)\dot R]=F_d-\frac{GM_s(R)M(<R)}{R^2}.
$$
The derivative includes momentum needed to accelerate newly swept ambient mass.

= Dust transparency radius
{title2=$R_\tau$}
{parent=Dusty radiation-pressure-driven shell}

The dust transparency radius is where a swept shell's ultraviolet optical depth equals one. For a gas fraction $f_g$ in a singular isothermal sphere, $R_\tau=\kappa_{\rm UV}f_g\sigma^2/(2\pi G)$.

= Soltan argument
{c}
{parent=Supermassive black hole}
{wiki}

The Soltan argument compares the cosmological energy density radiated by active galactic nuclei with the present mass density in supermassive black holes. Their ratio constrains the mass-weighted mean radiative efficiency of black-hole growth.

= Tidal disruption event
{title2=TDE}
{parent=Astrophysical black hole}
{wiki}

A tidal disruption event occurs when a star passes close enough to a black hole that differential gravity tears it apart outside the capture boundary, producing returning debris and often a luminous flare.

= Tidal disruption radius
{title2=$r_t$}
{parent=Tidal disruption event}

For a star of mass $M_*$ and radius $R_*$ near a black hole of mass $M_{\rm BH}$, the Newtonian tidal disruption radius is $r_t\simeq R_*(2M_{\rm BH}/M_*)^{1/3}$ under a peak-to-center tidal-acceleration convention.

= Hills mass
{c}
{parent=Tidal disruption event}
{wiki}

The Hills mass is the black-hole mass above which a given star crosses the capture boundary before being tidally disrupted. For a Schwarzschild capture radius $2GM_{\rm BH}/c^2$, it is $c^3R_*^{3/2}/(2G^{3/2}M_*^{1/2})$ with the corresponding tidal-radius convention.

= Black-hole seed
{parent=Astrophysical black hole}
{wiki}

A black-hole seed is an early black hole from which a later massive black hole can grow by accretion and mergers.

= Population III remnant black-hole seed
{c}
{parent=Black-hole seed}

A Population III remnant seed forms when a massive metal-free star collapses, typically leaving a light seed of order $10$--$10^2M_\odot$ depending on stellar mass loss and pair-instability effects.

= Runaway stellar-collision black-hole seed
{parent=Black-hole seed}

A runaway stellar-collision seed forms when mass segregation and repeated mergers in a dense young cluster create a very massive star that collapses to a black hole of roughly $10^2$--$10^4M_\odot$.

= Direct-collapse black-hole seed
{title2=DCBH seed}
{parent=Black-hole seed}

A direct-collapse black-hole seed forms when rapidly inflowing primordial gas avoids fragmentation, builds a supermassive star or quasistar, and collapses to a seed of roughly $10^4$--$10^6M_\odot$.
