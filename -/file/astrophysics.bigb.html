= Astrophysics
{wiki}

Astrophysics uses <physics> to explain <stars>, <planets>, <galaxies>, and their <electromagnetic radiation>; <cosmology> extends the questions to the universe as a whole. Much of its evidence arrives as <light>. The challenge is to infer the source's physical properties from what reaches an observer: a bright <star> might be nearby, unusually luminous, or both. <Stellar parallax> and <observed radiative flux> make this distinction quantitative.

This is an <inverse problem>. A <forward problem> predicts observations from a source model and the intervening <radiative transfer>; the <inverse problem> works backwards from observations to possible models. A <spectral line> can constrain <chemical elements>, <temperature>, and motion through the <Doppler effect>, but its strength also depends on the <stellar atmosphere>. Combining different measurements is therefore more informative than interpreting brightness alone. The attraction is that a tiny change in the direction of <light> can supply a distance, turning received <radiative flux> into intrinsic <luminosity>. https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Gaia/How_does_Gaia_study_the_Milky_Way[ESA: Parallax, spectra, and physical properties]{ref}.

= Point-like astronomical source
{parent=Astrophysics}

A point-like astronomical source has an angular extent negligible for the calculation or measurement being considered. The ideal mathematical limit concentrates its luminosity at one source-plane position. In <gravitational lensing>, this approximation permits divergent formal magnifications on a caustic; a finite source averages the magnification over its emitting area. An unresolved source need not have negligible size compared with the lens's caustic scale.

= Annual stellar parallax
{synonym}

Stellar parallax is the apparent change in a <star>'s viewing direction caused by the observer's changing position. Nearby <stars> shift more against a distant reference background. In an ideal circular <Earth> orbit, the conventional annual <stellar parallax> $p$ uses a baseline of one <astronomical unit>, approximately the <Earth>-<Sun> distance. Two opposite observing positions, about six months apart, are separated by approximately two <astronomical units>. Their maximum angular separation is $2p$, so $p$ is half that excursion, not the full January-to-July shift. The observed excursion depends on the projected baseline and observing dates. https://www.esa.int/ESA_Multimedia/Images/2013/06/Measuring_stellar_distances_by_parallax[ESA: Six-month parallax geometry]{ref}.

For the perpendicular baseline in the diagram, a <right triangle> gives
$$
\tan p=\frac{1\,\mathrm{au}}{d},\qquad d=\frac{1\,\mathrm{au}}{\tan p}\simeq\frac{1\,\mathrm{au}}{p},
$$
where $p$ in the final expression is in <radians>. Combining the <small-angle approximation> with the <parsec> convention gives
$$
\frac{d}{1\,\mathrm{pc}}\simeq\frac{1}{p/(1\,\mathrm{arcsec})}.
$$
Thus $p=0.05$ <arcseconds> implies $d\simeq20$ <parsecs>, while the ideal six-month excursion is $0.10$ <arcseconds>. Geometry supplies this distance without assuming a <luminosity> or a particular <stellar atmosphere>.

\Image[stellar-parallax-geometry.png]
{title=One-AU parallax angle and an ideal annual signal; geometry schematic, not to scale}

In practical <astrometry>, many observations separate the annual <stellar parallax> signal from <proper motion>, the <star>'s own changing sky position. The Gaia DR3 catalogue reports <stellar parallax> and its uncertainty in milliarcseconds: $50\,\mathrm{mas}=0.05$ <arcseconds>. A catalogue angle is a fitted measurement with <measurement error>. https://gea.esac.esa.int/archive/documentation/GDR3/Gaia_archive/chap_datamodel/sec_dm_main_source_catalogue/ssec_dm_gaia_source.html[ESA Gaia DR3: Parallax, errors, and proper motion columns]{ref}.

For a positive <stellar parallax> with small <standard deviation> $\sigma_p$, first-order propagation gives $\sigma_d/d\simeq\sigma_p/p$. Thus $p=50\,\mathrm{mas}$ with $\sigma_p=1\,\mathrm{mas}$ gives $d\simeq20$ <parsecs> with $\sigma_d\simeq0.4$ <parsecs>, assuming systematic offsets are negligible. This linear estimate requires $\sigma_p\ll p$; a reciprocal is nonlinear and does not generally preserve symmetric uncertainty intervals.

= Parsec
{parent=Stellar parallax}
{title2=$\mathrm{pc}$}
{wiki}

The parsec is a distance unit defined by the exact convention
$$
1\,\mathrm{pc}=\frac{648000}{\pi}\,\mathrm{au}\simeq3.085677581\times10^{16}\,\mathrm m.
$$
The <astronomical unit> is exactly $149597870700\,\mathrm m$; one <arcsecond> is exactly $\pi/648000$ <radians>. The IAU resolution explicitly records the <parsec> convention. A megaparsec is one million <parsecs>. https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.06262[IAU 2015 Resolution B2, note 4: Exact parsec convention]{ref}. https://www.esa.int/ESA_Multimedia/Images/2013/06/Measuring_stellar_distances_by_parallax[ESA: Astronomical-unit length]{ref}.

The familiar description "the distance giving one <arcsecond> of <stellar parallax>" uses the <small-angle approximation>. Exact <right triangle> geometry instead gives $\mathrm{au}/\tan(\pi/648000)$, slightly shorter than the conventional <parsec>. The relative difference is approximately $(\pi/648000)^2/3\simeq7.83\times10^{-12}$. This is negligible for the examples here, but distinguishing an exact unit convention from an approximate geometric interpretation prevents a misleading equality.

= Intergalactic medium
{parent=Astrophysics}
{title2=IGM}

The intergalactic medium is the gas between <galaxies>. Its diffuse, ionized component contains much of the ordinary matter outside stars. <Lyman-alpha absorption> measures its small neutral-hydrogen fraction, while hotter phases require other tracers.

= Temperature-density relation of photoionized intergalactic gas
{parent=Intergalactic medium}
{title2=$T=T_0(1+\delta)^{\gamma-1}$}

Diffuse gas in <photoionization equilibrium> is often approximately described by the displayed <temperature>-<density contrast> relation. Photoheating and expansion cooling produce its thermal history. It is an empirical model for low-density gas, not the adiabatic equation of state of every intergalactic phase. <Lyman-alpha forest> line widths constrain both the temperature at a reference density and the slope through their lower envelope.

= Ionizing background
{synonym}

The cosmic ionizing background is the diffuse radiation field capable of <photoionization> of intergalactic gas. Its <specific intensity> depends on source <luminosity functions>, <ionizing photon escape fractions>, cosmological redshifting, and <radiative transfer> through absorbers. <Quasars> and <star formation> supply major contributions with different spectral shapes and evolution.

= Lyman forest
{c}
{synonym}

The <Lyman-alpha forest> is the multitude of foreground <Lyman-alpha absorption> features in a distant <quasar>'s spectrum, at <wavelengths> shorter than its own redshifted <Lyman-alpha emission>. The <quasar> supplies a bright background; intervening neutral <hydrogen atoms> remove <photons> near their local <Lyman-alpha line> resonance. Many dips can therefore come from one atomic transition encountered at different <redshifts>, rather than from many different <chemical elements>. Some features are instead transitions of other ions, so a dip alone does not identify <Lyman-alpha absorption>. This makes faint foreground gas detectable through the <light> it removes, even when its own emission would be difficult to see. https://ned.ipac.caltech.edu/level5/Sept01/Rauch/Rauch1.html[Rauch (1998), The Lyman Alpha Forest in the Spectra of QSOs, section 1]{ref}.

Ignoring <peculiar velocity> for the moment, an absorber at <cosmological redshift> $z_a$ puts its <Lyman-alpha line> at
$$
\lambda_{\mathrm{obs}}=\lambda_\alpha(1+z_a),\qquad z_a=\frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{obs}}}{\lambda_\alpha}-1,\qquad \lambda_\alpha=1215.67\,\text{Å}.
$$
The rest <wavelength> is in the <ultraviolet>; at $z_a=2$ it appears at $3647.01\,\text{Å}$ in the <optical spectrum>. In a toy sightline to a <quasar> at $z_Q=3$, absorbers at $z_a=2,\ 2.35,\ 2.7$ appear at $3647.01,\ 4072.4945,\ 4497.979\,\text{Å}$, all shortward of the <quasar>'s <Lyman-alpha emission> position $4862.68\,\text{Å}$. The absorbing atoms see the selected <photons> at their own resonance; the observed dip's <redshift> belongs to the foreground gas. <Peculiar velocity> adds a <Doppler shift>, so the inferred $z_a$ is not a pure distance coordinate. The rest <wavelength> and resonance interpretation are given in https://ned.ipac.caltech.edu/level5/Sept01/Rauch/Rauch1.html[Rauch (1998), section 1].

The smooth comparison explains the Gunn-Peterson effect: neutral <hydrogen> distributed throughout an expanding <intergalactic medium> can absorb continuously over a <wavelength> interval, because different parts of the path resonate with different observed <wavelengths>. The resolved <Lyman-alpha forest> instead has strong transmission fluctuations and distinguishable <absorption lines>. These need not represent isolated compact clouds: diffuse sheets and filaments in the <cosmic web> can generate peaks in <optical depth> along one <line of sight>. A continuously varying gas distribution can look like a collection of lines. https://ned.ipac.caltech.edu/level5/Sept01/Rauch/Rauch1.html[Rauch (1998), section 1: smooth absorption]{ref}. https://ned.ipac.caltech.edu/level5/Sept01/Rauch/Rauch5_1.html[Rauch (1998), section 5.1: synthetic sightlines and extended structures]{ref}.

An early observation makes the identification concrete. Lynds's 1971 paper analyzed six spectrograms of the <quasar> 4C 05.34, with emission <redshift> $2.877$, taken in spring 1970 using an image-tube <spectrograph> on Kitt Peak's 84-inch telescope. Five covered $3200$–$7500\,\text{Å}$ with approximately $10\,\text{Å}$ resolution; the most informative covered about $3500$–$6000\,\text{Å}$ at $5\,\text{Å}$ resolution. Its reproduced spectrogram shows the strong <Lyman-alpha emission> flanked mostly on the short-<wavelength> side by numerous <absorption lines>. Lynds tabulated their <wavelengths> and estimated strengths, identified five absorption <redshift> systems, and proposed that many remaining unidentified lines were the same <Lyman-alpha line> at additional <redshifts>. He explicitly warned about blending, an incomplete census of weak lines, and <wavelength> errors around $0.5$–$1\,\text{Å}$. The result established a striking spectral pattern; the paper's proposed interpretation did not measure a separate physical cloud for every dip. https://adsabs.harvard.edu/pdf/1971ApJ...164L..73L[Lynds (1971), The Absorption-Line Spectrum of 4C 05.34, Figure 1, Table 1 and discussion]{ref}.

A contrasting observation demonstrates smooth, very strong absorption. Becker and collaborators (2001) used the Echelle Spectrograph and Imager on Keck II to observe the <quasar> SDSSp J103027.10+052455.0 at $z_Q=6.28$. Sky lines gave an instrumental full width at half maximum of about $1.8\,\text{Å}$ near $8500\,\text{Å}$, or <spectral resolving power> $R\simeq4700$. After sky subtraction and flux calibration, the mean continuum-normalized transmission over $8450$–$8710\,\text{Å}$ was $0.0038\pm0.0026$, consistent with zero; this corresponds to absorption <redshifts> approximately $5.95$–$6.16$. They identified a Gunn-Peterson trough. Crucially, resonant <Lyman-alpha absorption> is so sensitive that even a small neutral fraction can make transmission undetectable: the trough alone does not establish a mostly neutral <intergalactic medium> or uniquely date <reionization>. https://arxiv.org/pdf/astro-ph/0108097[Becker et al. (2001), sections 2–4: instrument response, transmitted flux and interpretation]{ref}.

What a spectrum constrains is a <radiative transfer> problem. For an ideal foreground screen with negligible emission into the observed beam, the intrinsic continuum-normalized transmission is
$$
\mathcal T(\lambda)=\frac{F(\lambda)}{F_{\mathrm{cont}}(\lambda)}=e^{-\tau(\lambda)},\qquad N_{\mathrm{HI}}=\int n_{\mathrm{HI}}\,d\ell.
$$
Here $F$ is spectral <radiative flux>, $F_{\mathrm{cont}}$ is its unabsorbed continuum, $\tau$ is <optical depth>, $n_{\mathrm{HI}}$ is neutral <hydrogen> <number density>, and $N_{\mathrm{HI}}$ is neutral <hydrogen> <column density> along proper path length $\ell$. A <Voigt profile> model constrains <redshift>, <column density> and a velocity width together. A nearly black line has lost much of its sensitivity to further increases in <optical depth>; blending and an uncertain continuum can also admit different fitted components. Finite <spectral resolving power> and noise limit which widths and weak features can be measured. https://ned.ipac.caltech.edu/level5/Sept01/Rauch/Rauch2.html[Rauch (1998), section 2: resolution, noise and profile analysis]{ref}. https://arxiv.org/pdf/astro-ph/0101005[Kim, Cristiani and D'Odorico (2001), section 3: fitted parameters and nonunique profile fits]{ref}.

For a purely thermal Doppler core, use the velocity convention $\tau(v)\propto\exp[-(v-v_0)^2/b^2]$. The <Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution> then gives
$$
b_{\mathrm{th}}=\sqrt{\frac{2k_BT}{m_{\mathrm H}}}\simeq12.85\sqrt{\frac{T}{10^4\,\mathrm K}}\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}},
$$
where $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant>, $T$ is <temperature> and $m_{\mathrm H}$ is a <hydrogen atom>'s mass. This $b$ is $\sqrt2$ times the <Gaussian distribution>'s standard deviation. Higher <temperature> spreads the same <column density> over more velocities. An instrument also broadens a line: locally, for a nearly flat continuum, the recorded transmission is approximately the <convolution> of $\mathcal T$ with the normalized instrumental response, not the exponential of a convolved <optical depth>. A Gaussian response with full width at half maximum $c/R$ has standard deviation $c/[R\sqrt{8\log2}]$, where $c$ is the <speed of light> and $R$ the <spectral resolving power>.

Better observations provide the inputs to that <inverse problem>. Kim, Cristiani and D'Odorico (2001) analyzed three <quasars> with the VLT's UVES <spectrograph>. For HE2217–2818, Table 1 records $z_Q=2.413$ and $16200\,\mathrm s$ of exposure in the $3050$–$3860\,\text{Å}$ setup in October 1999. Isolated thorium-argon lamp lines measured $R\simeq45000$; the data were resampled into $0.05\,\text{Å}$ bins, with signal-to-noise ratios about $45$–$50$ per pixel over $3550$–$4050\,\text{Å}$. They fitted a local polynomial continuum, fitted <Voigt profiles>, and compared statistics of the transmitted flux. Their analysis excluded the region near the <quasar> to avoid the <quasar proximity effect> and used the interval between its Lyman-beta and <Lyman-alpha emission> to reduce confusion with other Lyman-series absorption. Features below $N_{\mathrm{HI}}=10^{12.5}\,\mathrm{cm^{-2}}$ became confused with noise, setting their adopted lower <column density> limit. Figure 1 shows the measured spectrum, fitted profiles and residuals. This was a calibrated absorption measurement, with explicit selection limits. https://arxiv.org/pdf/astro-ph/0101005[Kim, Cristiani and D'Odorico (2001), UVES observations, sections 2–3, Table 1 and Figure 1]{ref}.

Even after instrumental correction, a width need not be purely <thermal Doppler broadening>. <Hubble flow> across extended gas, <peculiar velocity> structure and unresolved blends can contribute. If an isolated line's intrinsic width obeys $b^2=b_{\mathrm{th}}^2+b_{\mathrm{nt}}^2$ with an independent Gaussian nonthermal contribution $b_{\mathrm{nt}}^2\geq0$, then $T\leq m_{\mathrm H}b^2/(2k_B)$; $b=25\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ gives a computed upper bound about $3.79\times10^4\,\mathrm K$. A complex blend need not satisfy this single-component model. Noise can also bias the fitted widths of weak lines. This is why the <thermal cutoff of Lyman-alpha line widths>, calibrated against gas models, is more informative than assigning a <temperature> to every dip. https://ned.ipac.caltech.edu/level5/Sept01/Rauch/Rauch3_3.html[Rauch (1998), section 3.3: width measurements, noise bias and bulk motions]{ref}.

Finally, neutral <hydrogen> <column density> is not total gas <mass density>. In a pure <hydrogen> approximation, with gas optically thin to ionizing radiation and nearly fully ionized in <photoionization equilibrium>, the <Lyman-alpha forest photoionization scaling> gives
$$
n_{\mathrm{HI}}\simeq\frac{\alpha(T)n_{\mathrm H}^2}{\Gamma},\qquad x_{\mathrm{HI}}\simeq\frac{\alpha(T)n_{\mathrm H}}{\Gamma},
$$
where $n_{\mathrm H}$ counts all <hydrogen> nuclei, $\Gamma$ is the <photoionization rate>, $\alpha(T)$ is the radiative recombination coefficient and $x_{\mathrm{HI}}=n_{\mathrm{HI}}/n_{\mathrm H}$ is the neutral fraction. At fixed <temperature> and $\Gamma$, doubling $n_{\mathrm H}$ quadruples $n_{\mathrm{HI}}$; doubling $\Gamma$ halves it. These computed examples explain both the sensitivity to <density contrast> and the ambiguity: stronger absorption can mean denser gas, weaker ionizing radiation, a different <temperature>, or more path length. The <Jeans-length ionization correction for the Lyman-alpha forest> adds a size model rather than an independently measured depth. Optically thick <Lyman limit systems> and <damped Lyman-alpha systems> require different treatment. https://ned.ipac.caltech.edu/level5/Sept01/Rauch/Rauch5.html[Rauch (1998), section 5: neutral fraction and ionization correction]{ref}. https://arxiv.org/pdf/astro-ph/0104272[Schaye (2001), section 2 and Appendix equations A6–A7: ionization balance and the Jeans-length model]{ref}.

The beauty of the <Lyman-alpha forest> is that a spectrum converts otherwise faint foreground gas into a sequence of markers through cosmic time. With a model of <radiative transfer>, velocities, ionizing radiation and the <temperature-density relation of photoionized intergalactic gas>, its transmission statistics probe the <cosmic web> and gas thermal history. A single <line of sight> remains one narrow sample, not a unique three-dimensional density map. https://ned.ipac.caltech.edu/level5/Sept01/Rauch/Rauch5_2.html[Rauch (1998), section 5.2: the forest as a record of fluctuations]{ref}.

= Lyman-alpha forest photoionization scaling
{c}
{parent=Lyman-alpha forest}
{title2=$n_{\rm HI}\simeq\alpha(T)n_H^2/\Gamma$}

In optically thin, almost fully ionized <hydrogen> in <photoionization equilibrium>, $n_{\rm HI}\Gamma=n_en_p\alpha(T)$ and $n_e\simeq n_p\simeq n_H$. Thus the neutral fraction is approximately $\alpha(T)n_H/\Gamma$, while the neutral density is proportional to $n_H^2$. At fixed overdensity, cosmic dilution gives $n_H\propto(1+z)^3$, hence $n_{\rm HI}\propto\alpha(T)(1+z)^6/\Gamma$. Gas density, temperature, ionizing radiation and path length are all required to infer a baryon density from <Lyman-alpha absorption>.

= Jeans-length ionization correction for the Lyman-alpha forest
{c}
{parent=Lyman-alpha forest photoionization scaling}
{title2=$N_H\propto N_{\rm HI}^{1/3}\Gamma^{1/3}[f_gT/\alpha(T)]^{1/3}$}

Assume optically thin <photoionization equilibrium> and an absorber depth of order its local <Jeans length>, $L\propto(f_gT/n_H)^{1/2}$, where $f_g$ is the local gas fraction. Then $N_{\rm HI}\simeq\alpha(T)n_H^2L/\Gamma\propto[\alpha(T)/\Gamma](f_gT)^{1/2}n_H^{3/2}$. Solving for $n_H$ and substituting in $N_H=n_HL$ gives the displayed correction. Thus the ionized mass integral weights the <neutral-hydrogen column-density distribution> by $N_{\rm HI}^{1/3}$ rather than $N_{\rm HI}$. This model does not apply without modification to optically thick systems or arbitrary unconfined gas.

= Redshift evolution of the Lyman-alpha forest
{parent=Lyman-alpha forest}

The <Lyman-alpha forest> becomes denser and more opaque towards higher <cosmological redshift>, for a fixed absorption selection over the ordinary post-reionization range. The incidence is often parametrized as $d\mathcal N/dz=A(1+z)^\gamma$, with the index depending on the <column density> threshold and epoch. The evolution flattens at low redshift as the declining <cosmic ionizing background> partly offsets dilution of the <intergalactic medium>. For optically thin gas at fixed overdensity, <photoionization equilibrium> gives approximately $n_{\rm HI}\propto\alpha(T)(1+z)^6/\Gamma$, illustrating why density, temperature and ionizing radiation all matter.

= Proximity effect
{parent=Lyman-alpha forest}
{disambiguate=astrophysics}

= Quasar proximity effect
{synonym}

The quasar proximity effect is a reduction of nearby <Lyman-alpha forest> absorption caused by extra <photoionization> from the <quasar>. At fixed density and temperature in <photoionization equilibrium>, an added rate $\Gamma_Q$ changes the neutral fraction by the factor $\Gamma_{\rm bg}/(\Gamma_{\rm bg}+\Gamma_Q)$. A host environment with positive <density contrast> can mask this reduction, and a variable or anisotropic source complicates its interpretation.

= Proximity-effect estimator of the ionizing background
{parent=Proximity effect (astrophysics)}
{title2=$N_{\rm HI}=N_{\rm HI}^{(0)}/(1+\omega),\quad\omega=\Gamma_Q/\Gamma_{\rm bg}$}

In <photoionization equilibrium> at fixed gas density and temperature, the extra <quasar> <photoionization rate> reduces neutral-hydrogen <column density> by the displayed factor. Its local rate is calculated from the quasar flux, while the isotropic background rate is $\Gamma_{\rm bg}=4\pi\int J_\nu\sigma_{\rm HI}(\nu)/(h\nu)\,d\nu$. Fitting the absorption suppression versus proper distance constrains the <cosmic ionizing background>. For an unperturbed column-density distribution proportional to $N^{-p}$ with $p>1$, the line abundance above a fixed threshold is reduced by $(1+\omega)^{1-p}$. Density enhancement, quasar age, anisotropy and redshift errors must be distinguished from this ionization effect.

= Photoevaporation
{parent=Astrophysics}
{wiki}

Radiation heats gas sufficiently to drive an escaping thermal outflow. It can remove material from <protoplanetary disks>, planetary atmospheres and irradiated clouds. In a heated <Keplerian disk>, the <photoevaporative gravitational radius> compares the <sound speed> with the orbital speed. The mass-loss rate depends on the heating, density and wind geometry rather than radiation pressure alone.

= Photoevaporative gap opening
{parent=Photoevaporation}

When a <protoplanetary disk>'s inward viscous supply approaches its wind-loss rate, <photoevaporation> can prevent replenishment of the inner disk. That region drains on a <viscous timescale>, leaving a gap or hole. A negative density in a formal <steady state> calculation signals the breakdown of that approximation, not a physical continuation. An exposed outer edge can subsequently disperse rapidly.

= Steady photoevaporating-disk mass flux
{parent=Photoevaporation}
{title2=$F_{\mathrm{acc}}=\dot M_d-\dot M_w\sqrt{r_g/r}$}

For a sink $W=W_0(r/r_g)^{-5/2}$ outside $r_g$, <mass conservation> gives $dF_{\mathrm{acc}}/dr=2\pi rW$ in <steady state>. Remote feeding gives $F_{\mathrm{acc}}=\dot M_d-\dot M_w$ inside $r_g$, and $\dot M_d-\dot M_w\sqrt{r_g/r}$ outside, where $\dot M_w=4\pi W_0r_g^2$. The inward flux decreases towards the star because mass is diverted into the wind. A finite feeding radius or outer truncation supplies an additional boundary condition.

= Surface density of a steady photoevaporating disk
{parent=Steady photoevaporating-disk mass flux}
{title2=$3\pi\nu\Sigma=\dot M_d-\dot M_w\sqrt{r_g/r}[1+\tfrac12\log(r/r_g)]$}

With constant <kinematic viscosity> and zero inner <viscous torque in an accretion disk>, $\nu\Sigma\sqrt r=(6\pi)^{-1}\int_0^rF_{\mathrm{acc}}(r')/\sqrt{r'}\,dr'$. For the <steady photoevaporating-disk mass flux>, the density is $(\dot M_d-\dot M_w)/(3\pi\nu)$ inside $r_g$ and the displayed logarithmic profile outside. At equal feeding and wind rates the inner density vanishes, and the outer normalized profile rises from zero with zero slope and tends to one. Its near-edge expansion is $(r/r_g-1)^2/8$.

= Photoevaporative gravitational radius
{parent=Photoevaporation}
{title2=$r_g=GM/c_s^2$}

The thermal binding scale defined by equating heated gas <sound speed> to circular <Keplerian orbit> speed. It is $r_g=GM/c_s^2$. The circular-orbit binding energy is $GM/(2r)$ per unit mass, while the gas <specific enthalpy> is of order $c_s^2$. A heated disk surface at radii comparable to or larger than $r_g$ can therefore drive <photoevaporation>. The actual launching radius can differ by a factor of order unity; $c_s$ is not the ballistic escape speed.

= Gravitational collapse
{parent=Astrophysics}
{wiki}

<Gravitational collapse> is contraction driven by a system's own gravitational attraction or an enclosing potential. Pressure, angular momentum and other stresses can oppose it. A free-fall timescale measures the dynamical contraction rate when pressure support is removed; gas cooling determines whether thermal support can be removed fast enough.

= Pressureless homologous spherical collapse
{parent=Gravitational collapse}
{title2=$R^2\ddot R=-GM$}

A uniform spherical cloud with negligible <pressure> and <velocity> $\mathbf u=(\dot R/R)\mathbf r$ remains uniform: the <continuity equation> gives $\rho R^3=\rho_{00}R_0^3$. The <shell theorem> gives interior <gravitational acceleration> $-(4\pi G\rho/3)\mathbf r$, so its outer radius satisfies the displayed equation, with fixed mass $M=4\pi\rho_{00}R_0^3/3$. Collapse from rest has $\dot R^2=2GM(1/R-1/R_0)$. Writing $C^2=2GM/R_0$, its <cycloid> parametrization is $R=R_0(1+\cos\phi)/2$ and $Ct=R_0(\phi+\sin\phi)/2$, for $0\le\phi\le\pi$. Complete collapse occurs after $\sqrt{3\pi/(32G\rho_{00})}$. This Newtonian dust solution ceases to be a regular fluid configuration at zero radius.

= Comoving perturbations of a homologously collapsing cloud
{parent=Pressureless homologous spherical collapse}
{title2=$\mathbf k(t)=\mathbf q/R(t)$}

A <Fourier mode> advected with a <homologous spherical flow> has phase $\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r/R(t)$. Its <material derivative> in the background vanishes because $\dot{\mathbf k}=-(\dot R/R)\mathbf k$. Thus contraction shortens its physical wavelength while preserving its phase on each fluid element. Linearizing the <continuity equation> and pressureless <momentum> equation gives $\dot\delta=-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf u_1$ and $\dot{\mathbf u}_1+(\dot R/R)\mathbf u_1=-i\mathbf k\Phi_1$. Combining these with the <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity> yields $\ddot\delta+2(\dot R/R)\dot\delta-4\pi G\rho\delta=0$ for nonzero compressive <wavenumber>. It is a local bulk calculation, without the moving cloud-boundary conditions.

= Density modes of a collapsing pressureless sphere
{parent=Comoving perturbations of a homologously collapsing cloud}
{title2=$\delta_g\sim R^{-3/2},\quad\delta_d\sim R$}

For <pressureless homologous spherical collapse>, let $\phi$ be its <cycloid> parameter. The <density contrast> equation becomes $\delta_{\phi\phi}-\tan(\phi/2)\delta_\phi-3\delta/(1+\cos\phi)=0$. One solution is $\delta_g=\sin\phi/(1+\cos\phi)^2$. <Reduction of order> gives an independent solution that decays at collapse,
$$
\delta_d=\delta_g\int_\phi^\pi\frac{(1+\cos s)^3}{\sin^2s}\,ds
=\delta_g\left[4\cot(\phi/2)+\sin\phi-3(\pi-\phi)\right].
$$
For $\epsilon=\pi-\phi$, the integral is $\epsilon^5/40+O(\epsilon^7)$, while $\delta_g\sim4\epsilon^{-3}$. Therefore $\delta_d\sim\epsilon^2/10$. Since $R\sim R_0\epsilon^2/4$, the displayed powers follow. The growing branch is unique up to scale and addition of the decaying branch; many mixed solutions grow, so it is not literally the only function with growth. Finite perturbations eventually leave the linear regime.

= Horizontal coordinate system
{parent=Astrophysics}
{wiki}

A <horizontal coordinate system> describes a viewing direction using <altitude> $\gamma$ above the observer's <astronomical horizon> and <azimuth> $\eta$ around it. Here <azimuth> starts at true north and increases through east: north is $0^\circ$, east $90^\circ$, south $180^\circ$, and west $270^\circ$, with $0\leq\eta<360^\circ$. True north is the northward intersection of the <celestial meridian> with the <astronomical horizon>, rather than an uncorrected magnetic-compass reading. Other origins and directions are legitimate, but their formulae must be converted consistently. https://www.astro.uvic.ca/~tatum/celmechs/celm6.pdf[Jeremy Tatum: Celestial mechanics, section 6.2, altazimuth coordinates and azimuth conventions]{ref}.

Construct a local <orthonormal basis> of east $\mathbf e_E$, north $\mathbf e_N$, and upward $\mathbf e_U$, with $\mathbf e_E\times\mathbf e_N=\mathbf e_U$. The upward <unit vector> points to the <zenith>; the horizontal <plane> is perpendicular to it. For a ground instrument, a freely hanging weight establishes the local vertical through <gravity>. In an elementary spherical-<Earth> model, this vertical is radial; precision work distinguishes it from the normal to a reference surface. With the observer as origin, write a direction's <unit vector> as
$$
\mathbf n=\cos\gamma\sin\eta\,\mathbf e_E+\cos\gamma\cos\eta\,\mathbf e_N+\sin\gamma\,\mathbf e_U.
$$
Its local components $(E,N,U)$ obey $E^2+N^2+U^2=1$. Conversely, $\gamma=\arcsin U$ and $\eta=\operatorname{atan2}(E,N)$ modulo one turn, except where $E=N=0$. The quadrant-aware <arctangent> takes east as its first argument and north as its second. A synthetic direction with $\eta=60^\circ$ and $\gamma=30^\circ$ has $(E,N,U)=(3/4,\sqrt3/4,1/2)$. Thus its horizontal component has <norm> $\sqrt3/2$; its <altitude> is not the <angle> of the projected arrow in the horizontal <plane>.

\Image[horizontal-sky-coordinates-local-frame.png]
{title=Analytical illustration of north-through-east azimuth and altitude in a local orthogonal frame; synthetic direction}

A <horizontal coordinate system> depends on the observer's site and the observation <time>. The <Earth>'s <rotation> changes the local axes relative to a fixed distant direction, while changing the site changes the <zenith> and the <astronomical horizon>. This makes the <horizontal coordinate system> directly useful for pointing an <optical telescope> and checking whether terrain blocks a target. A pair of <angles> without a site, <time>, <azimuth> convention, and geometric or refracted interpretation is an incomplete pointing instruction.

For a check against an <equatorial coordinate system>, let $\theta$ be the observer's <geographical latitude>, $\delta$ the direction's <declination>, and $h$ its west-positive <hour angle>, obtained from local <sidereal time> minus <right ascension>. In the spherical-<Earth>, unrefracted fixed-direction model, projection onto the local <orthonormal basis> gives
$$
E=-\cos\delta\sin h,\qquad N=\cos\theta\sin\delta-\sin\theta\cos\delta\cos h,\qquad U=\sin\theta\sin\delta+\cos\theta\cos\delta\cos h.
$$
Taking the <dot product> with the upward <unit vector> gives the last formula; the other two follow by the same projection. Recover <altitude> and <azimuth> from $(E,N,U)$ as above, using <radians> inside software <trigonometric functions>. The minus sign in $E$ is essential: a negative <hour angle> puts this synthetic direction east of the <celestial meridian>. https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/alt_az[US Naval Observatory: Computing altitude and azimuth, local hour angle and quadrant-aware conversion]{ref}.

For illustrative <geographical latitude> $\theta=45^\circ$ and invented <declination> $\delta=15^\circ$, <hour angle> $h=-60^\circ$ gives $\gamma\simeq31.636^\circ$ and $\eta\simeq100.729^\circ$. At $h=+60^\circ$, the same <altitude> accompanies $\eta\simeq259.271^\circ$: the direction is now westward. At $h=0$, it is due south with <altitude> $60^\circ$, agreeing with $90^\circ-|\theta-\delta|$ at upper <meridian transit>. These are analytical checks, not present positions of any <star>. For nearby objects, the direction must also be referred to the observer; simply rotating a geocentric direction omits the change of viewing position. Catalogue-to-observation refinements are described under <equatorial coordinate system>.

= Altitude
{parent=Horizontal coordinate system}
{title2=$\gamma$}

= Elevation angle
{synonym}

<Altitude> $\gamma$, also called <elevation angle>, is the signed <angle> from the <astronomical horizon> to a viewing direction, measured in the vertical <plane> containing that direction and the <zenith>. Its range is $-90^\circ\leq\gamma\leq90^\circ$: positive <altitude> is above the horizontal <plane>, zero <altitude> lies on the <astronomical horizon>, and $90^\circ$ points to the <zenith>. <Altitude> measures direction, rather than a <Euclidean distance> or a height in metres. In the local <horizontal coordinate system>, $\sin\gamma=\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf e_U$; the <dot product> makes the sign unambiguous even for directions below the <astronomical horizon>.

A concrete motivating observation is the Exploratorium Teacher Institute's Height Sight activity. Its <inclinometer> uses a sighting tube fixed to a card and an angular scale, with a string and small weight hanging from the scale's pivot. A partner reads where the freely hanging string meets the scale. Sighting a level target should give a readout near zero; sighting a taller target gives an angular height. The activity asks the observer to move closer and farther: the closer position gives the larger <angle>. The interpretation is that the readout measures the tube's tilt relative to the <gravity> reference, while converting that <angle> to a physical height also requires distance and eye height. The activity also extends the <inclinometer> to sky observations. https://www.exploratorium.edu/snacks/height-sight[Exploratorium Teacher Institute: Height Sight, construction, level check and distance-dependent angular observations]{ref}.

The following calibration and sky sequence is a proposed extension, with no claimed observing dataset. First establish two reference marks at the same height with a water level, rather than assuming that a sloping floor or distant ridge is horizontal. Keep the <inclinometer> in a vertical <plane>, sight between the marks, wait for the weight to settle, and read the zero several times. Next sight a mark whose independently measured rise $q$ above the tube and horizontal separation $d$ are known; a <right triangle> predicts $\gamma_{\rm ref}=\arctan(q/d)$. A synthetic $q=3\,\mathrm m$, $d=5\,\mathrm m$ gives $\gamma_{\rm ref}=30.964^\circ$. Checking both zero and this nonzero <angle> can reveal a wrongly attached tube or a scale read from the wrong end. Use level separation, not distance along inclined ground.

If the scale's indicated <altitude> is $m=\gamma_{\rm app}+b$, estimate its additive zero $b$ from the level sight, then obtain apparent <altitude> $\gamma_{\rm app}=m-b$. For synthetic readings $b=1.0^\circ$ and $m=32.0^\circ$, the corrected <altitude> is $31.0^\circ$, close to the $30.964^\circ$ calibration prediction. Declaring illustrative readout bounds of $0.5^\circ$ for each reading gives a conservative combined bound of $1.0^\circ$ on $m-b$; these bounds are assumptions, not measured performance. Repeatability tests the variation between readings, whereas a persistent zero offset remains a systematic <measurement error>. Include uncertainty in the reference rise and separation before judging agreement.

At a fixed site, sight a recognizable <star>, record the observation <time>, corrected <altitude> and north-through-east <azimuth>, and repeat over a short interval. Record the terrain boundary at the same <azimuth> separately from the gravity-defined zero. The fixed-direction model in <horizontal coordinate system> predicts changing angular readouts as the <Earth> rotates; this is a prospective test, rather than a fabricated measurement curve. Keep the tube aligned, the string free of contact and shielded from wind, the eye at the same reference position, and repeat the level check afterward. Timestamp each sight so genuine sky motion is not mistaken for poor repeatability. An <optical telescope> with an angular readout can replace the sighting tube, but its mounting still needs an independently established level and angular zero.

The corrected instrument reads apparent <altitude>, including <atmospheric refraction>. Under ordinary conditions <atmospheric refraction> raises a low direction, so if $R$ is the upward angular correction, $\gamma_{\rm app}=\gamma_{\rm geom}+R$. Subtracting the instrument zero does not subtract $R$. Record site height, <temperature> and <pressure> if applying a <refraction> model: these are explicit observer inputs in the US Naval Observatory's NOVAS software. A coarse <inclinometer> may be unable to resolve the correction, and a local weather reading alone does not determine every atmospheric layer. https://aa.usno.navy.mil/downloads/Circular_180C_C.pdf[US Naval Observatory: NOVAS C3.0 user's guide, observer inputs and equ2hor refraction options]{ref}.

= Zenith
{parent=Horizontal coordinate system}
{wiki}

The <zenith> is the point on the <celestial sphere> in the direction of the observer's local upward vertical. In the <horizontal coordinate system>, its <unit vector> is $\mathbf e_U$ and its <altitude> is $90^\circ$. The <zenith> belongs to the observer's site; it is not generally the north <celestial pole>, whose <altitude> depends on <geographical latitude>. A direction's <zenith distance> is its nonnegative <angle> from the <zenith>, so $z=90^\circ-\gamma$. A direction of <altitude> $30^\circ$ therefore has <zenith distance> $60^\circ$. https://www.astro.uvic.ca/~tatum/celmechs/celm6.pdf[Jeremy Tatum: Celestial mechanics, section 6.2, zenith and complementary zenith distance]{ref}.

At the <zenith>, every value of <azimuth> produces $(E,N,U)=(0,0,1)$. There is no horizontal component to identify a bearing: $\operatorname{atan2}(0,0)$ cannot provide a geometrically meaningful <azimuth>, even if software returns a number. This <coordinate singularity> is a defect of the angular description; the viewing direction itself remains regular. The same ambiguity occurs at the opposite downward vertical. Near the <zenith>, two directions at fixed <altitude> separated by a small <azimuth> increment $d\eta$ have separation $\cos\gamma\,|d\eta|$ to first order, with <angles> in <radians>. At <altitude> $89^\circ$, a $1^\circ$ <azimuth> increment spans only about $0.01745^\circ$ on that small arc. An <optical telescope> can consequently require a large bearing change for a small change of pointing near the <zenith>; working with <unit vectors> avoids inventing a preferred bearing at the singular point.

= Zenith distance
{parent=Zenith}
{title2=$z$}

<Zenith distance> is the <angle> from the <zenith> to a viewing direction: $z=\arccos(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf e_U)=90^\circ-\gamma$, where $\gamma$ is <altitude> and $0\leq z\leq180^\circ$. The <astronomical horizon> has $z=90^\circ$, while the <zenith> has $z=0$. This unsigned separation supplies no <azimuth> and is distinct from the signed north/south angular readout used under <declination>.

= Astronomical horizon
{parent=Horizontal coordinate system}

The <astronomical horizon> is the <great circle> where the horizontal <plane> through the observer intersects the <celestial sphere>. It is perpendicular to the local vertical, so every direction on the <astronomical horizon> has <altitude> $0^\circ$ and <zenith distance> $90^\circ$. In the local <horizontal coordinate system>, it consists of the <unit vectors> satisfying $\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf e_U=0$. This geometric reference remains defined when buildings or mountains hide it; a visible skyline is not a calibration of zero <altitude>.

Three boundaries must be distinguished. The <astronomical horizon> fixes the angular reference. Terrain supplies a site-dependent boundary with apparent <altitude> $t(\eta)$ that changes with <azimuth>. <Atmospheric refraction> changes the apparent direction of incoming <light>, and can also change the apparent distant boundary. A point source clears an opaque terrain boundary only if $\gamma_{\rm app}>t(\eta)$ when both are expressed in the same apparent <horizontal coordinate system>; this clearance condition does not guarantee visibility through clouds or bright sky. In the synthetic diagram, a source with geometric <altitude> $3^\circ$ and an imposed illustrative upward correction $R=0.2^\circ$ remains behind a $4^\circ$ boundary. The chosen $R$ is an algebraic illustration, not a fitted atmospheric prediction.

{title=Synthetic calibration triangle and invented terrain boundary; angular zero, apparent altitude and geometric horizon are separate}

Even an unobstructed sea boundary need not coincide with the <astronomical horizon>. On a spherical <Earth> of radius $r$, an observer at height $q$ sees the unrefracted tangent to the surface at a downward <angle> $D$ satisfying $\cos D=r/(r+q)$. The right-angle tangent <triangle> supplies this relation; the visible boundary then has <altitude> $-D$. With illustrative $r=6371\,\mathrm{km}$ and $q=10\,\mathrm m$, $D\simeq0.1015^\circ$, before <atmospheric refraction>. Raising the eye therefore changes the visible boundary without redefining the horizontal <plane>.

The US Naval Observatory's rise/set definitions explicitly distinguish the geometric reference from apparent contact with a visible disk: the standard <Sun> calculation puts its centre $50'$ below the horizontal <plane>, combining an average $16'$ radius with $34'$ of <atmospheric refraction>. An angular minute is $1'=1/60^\circ$ in the <minute and second of arc> convention. This is an operational average under stated conditions, rather than a universal zero of <altitude>. The same source identifies weather, local topography and observer height as limits on predicted rise/set <time>. An observation log should therefore retain the level reference, site and eye height, terrain profile, observation <time>, and whether each reported <altitude> is geometric or apparent. https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/RST_defs[US Naval Observatory: Rise, set and twilight definitions, geometric horizon and observational limitations]{ref}.

= Equatorial coordinate system
{parent=Astrophysics}
{wiki}

An <equatorial coordinate system> labels a viewing direction on the <celestial sphere> by <right ascension> $\alpha$, increasing eastward around the reference <celestial equator>, and <declination> $\delta$, increasing northward from that <celestial equator>. These two <angles> locate a direction, without specifying the object's <Euclidean distance>. In the traditional convention, zero <right ascension> points toward the <reference equinox>, the northward crossing of the <Sun>'s annual path through the <celestial equator>. An <hour circle> through the direction supplies the north-south arc on which <declination> is measured. https://www.astro.uvic.ca/~tatum/celmechs/celm6.pdf[Jeremy Tatum: Celestial mechanics, chapter 6, the celestial sphere]{ref}.

For a concrete construction, choose <Cartesian coordinates> with $x$ pointing toward the <reference equinox>, $z$ toward the north reference <celestial pole>, and positive $y$ given by the <cross product> of the positive $z$ and $x$ directions. The direction's <unit vector> is
$$
\mathbf n=(\cos\delta\cos\alpha,\ \cos\delta\sin\alpha,\ \sin\delta).
$$
This is a <spherical coordinate system> with polar <angle> $\pi/2-\delta$ and no radial coordinate. The <trigonometric functions> require consistent angular units: convert <right ascension> in hours to <radians> before evaluating them. The synthetic direction $\alpha=4^{\rm h}=60^\circ$, $\delta=30^\circ$ gives $\mathbf n=(\sqrt3/4,3/4,1/2)$; its squared <norm> is $3/16+9/16+1/4=1$. A change of reference axes applies a <rotation> to this <unit vector> before recovering the two <angles>. Away from the <celestial poles>, recover <right ascension> using the quadrant-aware $\operatorname{atan2}(n_y,n_x)$ reduced modulo $2\pi$, and <declination> using $\arcsin n_z$; a plain <arctangent> of $n_y/n_x$ loses the quadrant. Adding a common offset to every <right ascension> describes only the special case of a <rotation> about the same polar axis.

\Image[equatorial-coordinates-reference-geometry.png]
{title=Analytical geometry of right ascension, declination and the shrinking east-west arc toward a celestial pole}

The reference specification matters. For an <equatorial coordinate system> tied to the <Earth>, <precession> changes the reference <celestial equator> and <reference equinox>, and <nutation> distinguishes their mean and true versions. The date specifying those axes is distinct from the <astronomical epoch> at which a catalogue's source directions apply: advancing a <star>'s <proper motion> and changing reference axes are separate operations. A modern catalogue may instead use fixed <reference frame> axes, aligned approximately with the traditional J2000.0 axes, without a moving <reference equinox>. Thus a pair of numbers labelled merely "J2000" does not settle every convention. State the <reference frame>, the <astronomical epoch>, and whether a direction is catalogued or apparent. https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/ICRS_doc[US Naval Observatory: Reference systems, catalogue epochs and fixed celestial axes]{ref}.

During one night's elementary fixed-direction approximation, a <star>'s <right ascension> and <declination> remain constant while its <altitude>, <azimuth> and <hour angle> change because the <Earth> turns. In angular units, with $\Theta$ the local <sidereal time>, west-positive <hour angle> $H$ obeys
$$
H=\Theta-\alpha\pmod{2\pi}.
$$
At upper <meridian transit>, $H=0$ and the local <sidereal time> equals the transiting direction's <right ascension>. Pair apparent <sidereal time> with apparent <right ascension> referred to the true <celestial equator> and <reference equinox> of date; mean <sidereal time> refers to the mean <reference equinox>. Raw fixed-frame catalogue coordinates require conversion before precision pointing. https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/GAST[US Naval Observatory: Mean and apparent sidereal time and the local longitude correction]{ref}.

This separation makes the <equatorial coordinate system> useful: a catalogue can identify a target independently of the observer's current <astronomical horizon>, while local <sidereal time> tells the observer which <hour circle> is on the <celestial meridian>. Precision observations also account for <proper motion>, <stellar parallax>, <aberration of light> and <atmospheric refraction>; the fixed-direction construction above omits those effects. A reference-axis <rotation> preserves <angles> between directions, whereas changing the observer or correcting <atmospheric refraction> is a different operation. https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/ICRS_doc[US Naval Observatory: Transforming catalogue directions into observable directions]{ref}.

= Declination
{parent=Equatorial coordinate system}
{title2=$\delta$}
{wiki}

<Declination> $\delta$ is the signed <angle> from the reference <celestial equator> to a direction, measured along its <hour circle>: $-90^\circ\leq\delta\leq+90^\circ$. Positive <declination> means celestial north, rather than necessarily north of the observer's <zenith>. A direction with $\delta=0$ lies on the <celestial equator>; $\delta=+90^\circ$ is the north reference <celestial pole>. <Declination> is an <angle>, not a height or a measure of <Euclidean distance>. https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/asa_glossary[US Naval Observatory: Astronomical Almanac glossary, declination and culmination]{ref}.

The connection to an observation follows from a <dot product>. In the elementary spherical-<Earth>, unrefracted model, an observer at <geographical latitude> $\phi$ has <zenith> direction $\mathbf z=(\cos\phi\cos\Theta,\cos\phi\sin\Theta,\sin\phi)$ in the same reference axes. Since $\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf z=\sin a$ for <altitude> $a$, the <cosine addition formula> gives
$$
\sin a=\sin\phi\sin\delta+\cos\phi\cos\delta\cos H.
$$
At upper <meridian transit>, $H=0$, so $a_{\rm upper}=90^\circ-|\phi-\delta|$. For the synthetic site $\phi=52^\circ$ and synthetic direction $\delta=20^\circ$, this yields $a_{\rm upper}=58^\circ$. A direction with $\delta=70^\circ$ instead reaches $72^\circ$, north of the <zenith>; positive <declination> does not imply a crossing on the southern half of the <celestial meridian>. A negative computed upper <altitude> means the direction never rises above the geometric <astronomical horizon> at that site.

For an angular readout, define signed zenith distance $z_s$ on the upper <celestial meridian>, positive toward the south and negative toward the north. Then $\delta=\phi-z_s$. A synthetic southward readout $z_s=32^\circ$ at $\phi=52^\circ$ recovers $\delta=20^\circ$. An unsigned distance $|z_s|$ alone would leave two possibilities, $\phi\pm|z_s|$, when both are in range. A calibrated <optical telescope> must therefore record which side of the <zenith> it points toward. In this linear relation, a $1'$ error in the assumed <geographical latitude> produces a $1'$ error in inferred <declination>; averaging repeated readings cannot remove that shared offset. Near the <astronomical horizon>, uncorrected <atmospheric refraction> also invalidates the geometric readout.

The <unit vector> construction explains a subtle feature of sky maps. At fixed <declination>, changing <right ascension> traces a parallel whose radius on the unit <sphere> is $\cos\delta$. Its arc <length> is $\cos\delta\,|d\alpha|$, with <angles> in <radians>; at $\delta=60^\circ$, a $15^\circ$ <right ascension> increment spans a $7.5^\circ$ arc along that parallel. This arc is not generally the shortest <great circle> separation. At either <celestial pole>, all <right ascension> values represent the same direction: the <coordinate singularity> belongs to the coordinate grid. The <unit vector> remains regular there.

= Right ascension
{parent=Equatorial coordinate system}
{title2=$\alpha$}
{wiki}

<Right ascension> $\alpha$ is the eastward <angle> along the reference <celestial equator> from the <reference equinox> to a direction's <hour circle>, conventionally reduced to $0\leq\alpha<24^{\rm h}$. Its hours label <angles>: $24^{\rm h}=360^\circ$, $1^{\rm h}=15^\circ$, $1^{\rm m}=15'$ and $1^{\rm s}=15''$. Thus $6^{\rm h}30^{\rm m}=97.5^\circ=13\pi/24$ <radians>. A minute of <right ascension> differs from a minute of arc, and the actual arc on a fixed-<declination> parallel also contains the <cosine> factor described under <declination>.

The hours become useful through <sidereal time>. At local <sidereal time> $8^{\rm h}$, a synthetic direction of <right ascension> $6^{\rm h}30^{\rm m}$ has west-positive <hour angle> $1^{\rm h}30^{\rm m}=22.5^\circ$ and has passed its upper <meridian transit>. Conversely, at $5^{\rm h}30^{\rm m}$ it has <hour angle> $-1^{\rm h}$ and has yet to cross. Interpret these intervals in sidereal hours; ordinary civil hours are not interchangeable with them. The <right ascension> of the upper <celestial meridian> advances as the <Earth> turns, while the synthetic fixed target's <right ascension> stays unchanged.

An instrument makes this geometry tangible. DePauw University's description of McKim Observatory documents a meridian <optical telescope> with an angular circle, astronomical clocks and a chronograph. The circle measures zenith distance, and the crossing <time> supplies <right ascension>. Clock pulses and the observer's electrical key mark a rotating paper record, so crossing <time> is read relative to the clock marks. One clock kept <sidereal time> and another solar <time>. This apparatus separates two measured quantities: when a <star> crosses the <celestial meridian>, and at what <angle> from the <zenith>. https://www.depauw.edu/about/campus/mckim-observatory/observatory-instruments/[DePauw University: McKim's meridian telescope, angular circle, clocks and chronograph]{ref}.

A teaching reconstruction would fix an <optical telescope> to sweep the <celestial meridian>, align its central sighting line, and observe a recognizable reference <star> followed by a target. Record each central-line crossing against a clock or timestamped camera, and read the angular circle at upper <meridian transit>. A short sequence shows the image moving across the stationary sighting line; the central crossing supplies a repeatable event. In the ideal model, successive upper crossings of a fixed direction recur once per sidereal day, and directions with increasing <right ascension> cross later in the same cycle. These are predictions for the reconstruction, rather than reported McKim measurements. Tatum derives the equality between local <sidereal time> and <right ascension> at upper <meridian transit>. https://www.astro.uvic.ca/~tatum/celmechs/celm6.pdf[Jeremy Tatum: Section 6.3, timing upper meridian transit]{ref}.

Calibrate the clock's zero by comparing its reference crossing readout $C_r$ with the reference <star>'s predicted apparent <right ascension> $\alpha_r$ in the same date convention: the additive correction is $b=\alpha_r-C_r$, modulo $24^{\rm h}$. Then infer target <right ascension> $\alpha_t=C_t+b$ if the clock rate is correct between crossings. In a purely synthetic example, $\alpha_r=6^{\rm h}00^{\rm m}00^{\rm s}$ and $C_r=6^{\rm h}00^{\rm m}20^{\rm s}$ imply $b=-20^{\rm s}$. A target readout $C_t=6^{\rm h}30^{\rm m}20^{\rm s}$ therefore gives $\alpha_t=6^{\rm h}30^{\rm m}00^{\rm s}=97.5^\circ$. The $20^{\rm s}$ clock offset is $300''$ in <right ascension>, while its local east-west angular effect at <declination> $60^\circ$ is approximately $150''$. The reference and target in this calculation are invented directions, with no claim about any current <star>'s coordinates.

Controls give the inference its meaning. Use independently established <geographical latitude> and an angular-circle zero for <declination>; check sighting-line alignment against several reference <stars>, and bracket the target in <time> with references to reveal clock drift. Record north or south of the <zenith>, distinguish upper from lower <meridian transit>, and account for <atmospheric refraction>. Convert the observing timestamp to local <sidereal time> using the site's east-positive longitude correction, rather than a time-zone offset; precise conversion uses the <Earth>'s measured rotation <time>. A reference <star> calibrates a zero only after its catalogue direction has been converted to the observation convention. Repeated crossings reduce random <measurement error>, while an incorrect clock rate, axis alignment or catalogue convention remains a systematic limitation. https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/GAST[US Naval Observatory: Local sidereal time, longitude convention and rotation-time input]{ref}. These controls are requirements inferred from the geometric and clock model, not a claimed historical observing log.

= Light curve
{parent=Astrophysics}

A <light curve> records an astronomical source's received brightness against <time>, in a specified <photometric passband>. Its measured form is a <time series> of timestamps, brightness estimates, and uncertainties; a smooth $F(t)$ is a model of that record. Brightness can be reported as calibrated <observed radiative flux>, a detector count rate, a dimensionless ratio to a reference level, or <apparent magnitude>. These quantities require different units and calibration conventions. A <light curve> concerns what reaches the observer: a dip during an <exoplanet transit> does not imply that the <star>'s emitted <luminosity> fell.

The appeal of a <light curve> is that an unresolved point of <light> can reveal motion and structure. In <exoplanet transit photometry>, repeated dips constrain the <orbital period>; under the uniform-disc, dark-<planet>, full-overlap assumptions, the <exoplanet transit depth> is the blocked <area> fraction $(R_p/R_\star)^2$. A toy one-percent dip therefore corresponds to a <radius> ratio of $0.10$. Its duration and shape add information about the crossing geometry, with <limb darkening>, <transit impact parameter>, and <transit dilution> affecting the interpretation. See <exoplanet transit photometry> for the geometry. The first figure instead follows the measurement: an instrumental trend and sky contribution can obscure a small physical dip.

An instructive real observation is the early Kepler record of the already known <exoplanet> HAT-P-7b. Welsh and colleagues analysed 33.5 days from 13 May to 15 June 2009, containing 15 complete <exoplanet transits> at an <orbital period> of about $2.20473$ days. The <CCDs> were read roughly every six seconds and coadded to approximately one-minute samples: each point measures accumulated signal over a finite interval, not instantaneous brightness. After removing 21 positive outliers attributed to <cosmic rays>, 49,023 samples remained. The authors estimated a scatter of about $150$ parts per million per one-minute sample. A gradual $0.13\%$ count decrease, suspected to arise from focus drift with a fixed extraction aperture, was removed with a cubic <polynomial> fitted while masking the <exoplanet transits>. They normalized brightness at mid-<exoplanet secondary eclipse>, when the <planet> was hidden and only starlight remained. https://www.ucolick.org/~jfortney/papers/Welsh10.pdf[Welsh et al. (2010), observations, section 2]{ref}.

What does the instrument actually measure? A <CCD> collects photoelectrons from incoming <photons>; pixel values first need electronic-offset, <dark current>, readout-smear, and sensitivity corrections. https://nexsci.caltech.edu/workshop/2012/keplergo/PipelineCAL.shtml[Kepler Guest Observer Program, pixel calibration; edited by Martin Still]{ref}. <Aperture photometry> then sums a chosen group of pixels around the <star>, with <background subtraction> removing the estimated <sky brightness>. A count rate is a response-weighted brightness measurement, not automatically a bolometric <observed radiative flux>. Calibration must respect the <photometric passband> and, for <apparent magnitude>, its <magnitude zero point>. Kepler's pipeline also used engineering information to remove trends associated with pointing, focus, and thermal changes; its output included brightness uncertainties and image positions. https://arxiv.org/pdf/1001.0258[Jenkins et al. (2010), pipeline overview, sections 2–5]{ref}.

Calibration and interpretation need separate checks. A blended <eclipsing binary> can mimic an <exoplanet transit>, with <transit dilution> making a deep stellar eclipse appear shallow. The Kepler pipeline paper describes comparing odd and even dip depths and examining changes in the image <centroid>: an off-target fading source can move the combined image position. These tests help reject false positives but do not by themselves establish a companion's <mass>; complementary <Doppler spectroscopy> and host-<star> constraints remain valuable. https://arxiv.org/pdf/1001.0258[Jenkins et al. (2010), data validation, section 6]{ref}.

Even a real <exoplanet>'s signal can have several contributors. Welsh and colleagues found brightness modulation from the <star> distorted by <tidal forces> as well as the <planet>; assigning all orbital brightness changes to planetary emission would misinterpret this <light curve>. https://www.ucolick.org/~jfortney/papers/Welsh10.pdf[Welsh et al. (2010), interpretation, section 4]{ref}. <Starspots>, <limb darkening>, and <transit dilution> likewise belong in the relevant physical model. A flexible trend fitted through a dip could absorb that dip; the masked fit in the real example illustrates why the fitting procedure is part of the measurement. <Exposure-averaged light curves> and <phase folding of a light curve> make two further limitations explicit.

= Aperture photometry
{parent=Light curve}
{title2=$\widehat r=(S-nb)/\Delta t$}

<Aperture photometry> estimates brightness by summing calibrated detector pixels in an extraction region and subtracting the estimated background in that region. For $n$ equally weighted pixels, let $S$ be their total accumulated signal in <electrons>, $b$ the estimated background <electrons> per pixel over the same exposure, and $\Delta t$ the exposure duration. Then $\widehat r=(S-nb)/\Delta t$ is a background-subtracted electron rate. Kepler applied <aperture photometry> to calibrated pixels with a fitted sky background. https://arxiv.org/pdf/1001.0258[Jenkins et al. (2010), photometric analysis, section 3]{ref}.

This measurement trades source collection against background: enlarging the extraction region can retain more starlight but includes more <sky brightness> and neighbouring sources. <Photon shot noise>, <read noise>, and uncertainty in <background subtraction> contribute to its uncertainty. Dividing $\widehat r$ by a consistently measured reference rate gives a dimensionless <light curve>; it does not supply an absolute <observed radiative flux>. If contaminating light remains, the reference includes it and the inferred <exoplanet transit depth> suffers <transit dilution>.

In the first toy figure, the source's reference rate is $100{,}000$ electrons per second, the summed sky contribution is $4{,}000$ electrons per second, and a known multiplicative response is $g(t)=1+0.0003t$ with $t$ in hours from mid-transit. The artificial <exoplanet transit depth> is $0.010$, its total duration four hours, and its flat-bottom duration $2.8$ hours; the straight ingress and egress are a toy approximation, not an exact <exoplanet transit photometry> model. Each synthetic sample averages a $0.5$-hour exposure and adds seeded <normal distribution> noise with relative <standard deviation> $0.0006$. Subtracting the known sky rate and dividing by the known response and reference rate recovers the dip. Real observations require estimating those corrections and carrying their uncertainty.

= Exposure-averaged light curve
{parent=Light curve}
{title2=$\overline F_i$}

An <exposure-averaged light curve> models what a finite exposure measures. For a constant response during an exposure of duration $\Delta t_i>0$, centred at $t_i$, the expected mean brightness is
$$
\overline F_i=\frac{1}{\Delta t_i}\int_{t_i-\Delta t_i/2}^{t_i+\Delta t_i/2}F(t)\,dt.
$$
The <integral> has units of brightness times <time>; division restores the units of $F$. Observed points fluctuate around this mean. A response that changes during the exposure must instead be included inside the <integral>, as in the first figure's known drift. Exposure duration and the interval between samples are distinct: there may be dead time or missing exposures.

A short dip can be blurred even with perfectly noiseless measurements. For a toy rectangular dip of fractional depth $\delta$ and duration $T<\Delta t$, an exposure containing the whole dip has fractional depth $\delta T/\Delta t$: averaging hides part of its depth. A $1\%$ dip lasting ten minutes therefore appears only $0.333\%$ deep in a thirty-minute exposure. No <interpolation> recovers its shape from that single number without additional assumptions. Forward modelling should average the predicted <light curve> over each exposure before comparing it with the measurements. Kipping's analysis of <exoplanet transit photometry> demonstrates how ignoring this averaging biases inferred system parameters. https://arxiv.org/abs/1004.3741[Kipping (2010), Binning is sinning]{ref}.

= Phase folding of a light curve
{parent=Light curve}
{title2=$\phi_i$}

<Phase folding of a light curve> maps timestamps onto a trial cycle of duration $P>0$. Given a reference time $t_0$, define the phase in cycles by
$$
\phi_i=\operatorname{frac}\left(\frac{t_i-t_0}{P}\right),\qquad 0\le\phi_i<1,
$$
where $\operatorname{frac}(x)=x-\lfloor x\rfloor$ is the <fractional part>, using the <floor function> also for negative $x$. Plot brightness against $\phi_i$ rather than $t_i$; measurements separated by an integer multiple of $P$ occupy the same phase. For a <periodic function> $F(t+P)=F(t)$, this reveals the shared shape of many cycles. Folding changes coordinates; it neither averages samples nor chooses the correct $P$ by itself. Welsh et al.'s folded Kepler plot separates the <exoplanet transit> and orbital brightness modulation. https://www.ucolick.org/~jfortney/papers/Welsh10.pdf[Welsh et al. (2010), figure 1]{ref}.

The second figure uses a completely synthetic trapezoidal dip: $P=3$ days, $t_0=1$ day, and the same $1\%$ depth and four-hour duration as the first figure. Different colours identify four successive cycles. Correct folding superimposes the dips; a wrong trial <orbital period> spreads them out. If $P'=P+\epsilon$, the centre of the $n$th repeated event shifts by approximately $-n\epsilon/P$ cycles when folded on $P'$, for $|\epsilon|\ll P$. With $P'=3.03$ days, the fourth plotted event is displaced by $-3(0.03)/3.03\simeq-0.0297$ cycles. <Transit-timing variations> can also smear a constant-period fold, so retain the original timestamps and inspect individual events.

\Image[light-curve-phase-folding-magnitudes.png]
{title=Toy phase folding: repeated synthetic dips align at the correct period; a flux dip becomes a positive magnitude change}

One may combine nearby phases with a <weighted mean>, but the uncertainty reduction requires assumptions. For $N$ independent measurements with common <standard deviation> $\sigma$ and effectively the same expected brightness within the bin, their <arithmetic mean> has <standard deviation> $\sigma/\sqrt N$. Thus sixteen independent $0.0006$-precision samples give $0.00015$ precision on that phase-bin mean. Shared calibration errors, changing <starspots>, and bins wide enough to mix ingress with out-of-transit brightness violate these assumptions. A folded plot can conceal changes between cycles. <Gaussian process> models can represent correlated variability and interpolate irregular samples, but their predictions and uncertainties depend on their assumed <covariance function>.

The bottom panels show the same model as relative brightness $f=F/F_0$ and as <magnitude difference> in one fixed <photometric passband> with a fixed <magnitude zero point>:
$$
\Delta m(t)=-2.5\log_{10}f(t),\qquad f(t)>0.
$$
A dip to $f=0.99$ gives $\Delta m\simeq+0.010912$ magnitudes: fainter means a larger <apparent magnitude>. The figure places larger <magnitude differences> lower on the page so the two dip shapes are easy to compare; reversing that plotting convention does not change the formula. For $f=1-\delta$ and small $\delta$, expansion of the <logarithm> gives $\Delta m\simeq(2.5/\ln10)\delta\simeq1.085736\delta$. The exact transform is nonlinear: transforming an exposure's mean brightness is generally different from averaging its instantaneous <apparent magnitudes>. The ordinary <logarithm> cannot be applied to a nonpositive background-subtracted measurement; keep such measurements and their uncertainties in brightness units for an appropriate statistical model.

= Luminosity
{title2=$L$}
{parent=Astrophysics}
{wiki}

= Bolometric luminosity
{synonym}

Luminosity is emitted radiative <power>: the <energy> carried away by <electromagnetic radiation> per unit time, measured in watts. <Bolometric luminosity> includes all <wavelengths>; a measurement through one <photometric passband> captures only part of it. <Spectral luminosity> records how that <power> is distributed over <frequency>. Received <observed radiative flux> has different units, watts per square metre, because it measures what crosses an observer's collecting area. https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.06262[IAU 2015 Resolution B2: Radiative luminosity and irradiance]{ref}.

For example, an ideal source with <luminosity> $10^{26}\,\mathrm W$ emits $10^{26}\,\mathrm J$ each second regardless of whether it is viewed from $20$ or $40$ <parsecs>. Its <observed radiative flux> changes with distance, while its <luminosity> remains fixed. Combining <stellar parallax> with <observed radiative flux> therefore lets us compare the actual outputs of <stars> rather than their apparent brightnesses.

= Luminosity density
{parent=Luminosity}
{title2=$\lambda$}

The <luminosity> per unit spatial <volume>. For a spherical source, its enclosed <luminosity> is $L(r)=4\pi\int_0^r\lambda(s)s^2\,ds$, and its projected <surface brightness> is $\mu(R)=2\int_R^\infty\lambda(r)r\,dr/\sqrt{r^2-R^2}$.

= Comoving spectral emissivity
{parent=Luminosity density}
{title2=$\epsilon_\nu=dL_\nu/dV_c$}

<Comoving spectral emissivity> is emitted <spectral luminosity> per <comoving volume>, measured using source-rest-frame time and <frequency>. For identical sources with <comoving number density> $n_c$, it is $\epsilon_\nu=n_cL_\nu$. For a population with a <luminosity function>, integrate its rest-frame <spectral luminosity> over that distribution. Multiplying a proper-volume emissivity by $a^3$ gives this comoving convention for dimensionless <scale factor> $a$.

= Spectral luminosity
{parent=Luminosity}
{title2=$L_\nu$}

Spectral luminosity is energy radiated per unit time per unit frequency. Its frequency integral is the total <luminosity>. For an isotropic source at small distance $r$, neglecting attenuation and cosmological effects, the <spectral flux density> is $L_\nu/(4\pi r^2)$.

= Luminosity function
{synonym}

An astronomical luminosity function gives the number density of objects per luminosity interval, often also per comoving volume. Integrating luminosity times the function gives their luminosity density.

= Astronomical magnitude
{parent=Astrophysics}
{wiki}

Astronomical magnitude expresses brightness on a <logarithmic scale>: equal steps in <astronomical magnitude> represent equal ratios of received <radiative flux>. The scale runs backwards, so a brighter source has a smaller <astronomical magnitude>. A difference of five magnitudes means exactly a factor of $100$ in flux; one magnitude means a factor of $100^{1/5}\simeq2.511886$, rather than exactly $2.5$. This compresses the enormous range of astronomical brightnesses into manageable numbers. https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/17-1-the-brightness-of-stars[Fraknoi, Morrison and Wolff, Astronomy 2e, 17.1: The brightness of stars]{ref}.

<Apparent magnitude> describes received brightness, while <absolute magnitude> compares sources at a shared reference distance. Both require a specified <photometric passband> and <magnitude zero point>. A <magnitude difference> turns a ratio of positive <radiative fluxes> into a difference of numbers; it does not measure an additive change in emitted <luminosity>.

= Magnitude difference
{parent=Astronomical magnitude}
{title2=$\Delta m$}

For two positive measurements $F_1,F_2$ in the same <photometric passband>, using the same <magnitude zero point>, units, and weighting convention, their <apparent magnitudes> obey
$$
\Delta m=m_2-m_1=-2.5\log_{10}\left(\frac{F_2}{F_1}\right),\qquad \frac{F_2}{F_1}=10^{-0.4\Delta m}.
$$
The <logarithm> takes a dimensionless ratio. An <energy>-weighted measurement must be compared with another <energy>-weighted measurement, and a <photon>-counting measurement with another consistently calibrated <photon>-counting measurement. Changing <photometric passband> or <magnitude zero point> requires the corresponding calibration, as in a <colour index>. https://arxiv.org/pdf/1510.06262[IAU 2015 Resolution B2: Energy-flux magnitude convention]{ref}. https://xmm-tools.cosmos.esa.int/external/xmm_calibration/calib/documentation/CALHB/node597.html[ESA XMM-Newton: Filter-specific count-rate calibration]{ref}.

For example, $F_2=100F_1$ gives $\Delta m=-5$: source 2 is brighter. Conversely, $\Delta m=+5$ gives $F_2/F_1=0.01$, and $\Delta m=+1$ gives $F_2/F_1\simeq0.398107$. Even fractional steps work: $\Delta m=+0.1$ gives $F_2/F_1\simeq0.912011$, an approximately $8.80\%$ decrease. These are illustrative ratios, independent of any particular <star> catalogue.

= Apparent magnitude
{title2=$m$}
{parent=Astronomical magnitude}
{wiki}

Apparent magnitude $m$ is a calibrated description of the <observed radiative flux> received from an astronomical source. It answers how bright the source appears in a specified <photometric passband>, rather than how much <luminosity> the source emits. Define $F_{\mathrm{ref}}>0$ as the measurement assigned zero <apparent magnitude> by the chosen <magnitude zero point>. Then
$$
m=-2.5\log_{10}\left(\frac{F}{F_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right).
$$
Here $F$ is the positive passband measurement, with the same units and response convention as $F_{\mathrm{ref}}$. For an <energy>-weighted detector this measures received <radiative flux> through its response; for a <photon>-counting detector use the corresponding calibrated count rate for both quantities. Actual <photometric passbands> include the instrument's response across <wavelength>, so naming the <photometric system> is part of reporting an <apparent magnitude>. https://www.cosmos.esa.int/web/gaia/edr3-passbands[ESA/Gaia/DPAC: Passbands and zero points]{ref}. https://xmm-tools.cosmos.esa.int/external/xmm_calibration/calib/documentation/CALHB/node597.html[ESA XMM-Newton calibration handbook: Count rate to magnitude]{ref}.

As toy examples in one <photometric passband>, $F=F_{\mathrm{ref}}$ gives $m=0$, $F=100F_{\mathrm{ref}}$ gives $m=-5$, and $F=0.01F_{\mathrm{ref}}$ gives $m=+5$. A negative <apparent magnitude> therefore means more received <radiative flux> than the reference; it is physically allowed. Zero <apparent magnitude> does not mean zero <radiative flux>. The ordinary <logarithm> formula requires $F>0$.

A small <apparent magnitude> alone cannot distinguish a nearby faint source from a distant source with large <luminosity>: this is the <flux-distance degeneracy>. <Stellar parallax> supplies independent distance information, while a calibrated <standard candle> supplies intrinsic brightness. <Interstellar extinction> also changes the received <radiative flux> and hence the measured <apparent magnitude>.

= Magnitude zero point
{parent=Apparent magnitude}

A magnitude zero point fixes the reference measurement $I_0$ in a specified <photometric passband>. Relative to a zero-magnitude reference, the <apparent magnitude> is $m=-2.5\log_{10}(I/I_0)$. Both measurements must use the same passband and calibration convention.

= Euclidean magnitude-limited source count
{parent=Apparent magnitude}

For identical <luminosities>, uniform spatial density, and no extinction, the <inverse-square law> gives limiting distance $r\propto F_{\min}^{-1/2}$. The number in the enclosed volume is therefore $N\propto F_{\min}^{-3/2}\propto10^{0.6m_{\lim}}$. Increasing the sample by a factor $b$ needs a magnitude-limit increase $(5/3)\log_{10}b$. This relation need not hold with evolving source populations, dust, or cosmological distance effects.

A photometric passband is the wavelength-dependent response used for a brightness measurement. = Infrared photometric band

The traditional near- and thermal-infrared photometric bands in the 1–5 micrometre region are $J,H,K,L,M$, with approximate centers $1.25,1.65,2.2,3.5,4.8$ micrometres. = Absolute magnitude
{title2=$M$}
{parent=Astronomical magnitude}
{wiki}

Absolute magnitude $M$, in the stellar convention used here, is the <apparent magnitude> a source would have at $10$ <parsecs>, with <interstellar extinction> removed. The hypothetical observation uses the specified <photometric passband> and <magnitude zero point>, preserving the source's emitted spectrum and <luminosity>. Moving every source to this common distance lets us compare intrinsic brightness without confusing it with distance. https://sites.astro.caltech.edu/~george/ay20/Ay20-Lec3x.pdf[Caltech Ay 20, Lecture 3: Apparent versus absolute magnitudes]{ref}.

For a fixed source with <isotropy> of emission, in transparent space with negligible relativistic and cosmological effects, the <inverse-square law> gives the hypothetical measurement $F_{10}$ and <absolute magnitude>
$$
F_{10}=F(d)\left(\frac{d}{10\,\mathrm{pc}}\right)^2,\qquad M=-2.5\log_{10}\left(\frac{F_{10}}{F_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right).
$$
The factor applies in the same <photometric passband>, with unchanged emitted spectrum. Thus <absolute magnitude> compares <luminosity> in that response, rather than automatically the total <bolometric luminosity> over all <wavelengths>.

Consider a toy source with $M=0$. Its <apparent magnitude> is $m=0$ at $10$ <parsecs>, $m=5$ at $100$ <parsecs>, and $m=10$ at $1000$ <parsecs>, neglecting <interstellar extinction>. Its <absolute magnitude> remains $M=0$ throughout: moving the observer changes received <radiative flux>, not emitted <luminosity>. Another toy source with $M=-5$ has $100$ times its passband output under the same <isotropy> and calibration assumptions. Negative <absolute magnitudes> are allowed too; "absolute" names the reference-distance convention, not an <absolute value>.

A calibrated <standard candle> reverses this reasoning: its known <absolute magnitude> and measured <apparent magnitude> give a <distance modulus>. This is why estimating intrinsic brightness is so useful for measuring astronomical distances. https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/19-3-variable-stars-one-key-to-cosmic-distances[Fraknoi, Morrison and Wolff, Astronomy 2e, 19.3: Intrinsic brightness and distance]{ref}.

= K correction
{c}
{parent=Absolute magnitude}
{title2=$m_R=M_R+5\log_{10}(d_L/10\,\mathrm{pc})+K_R$}
{wiki}

The K-correction converts an observed <photometric passband> to a specified emitted <photometric passband>, accounting for <cosmological redshift> and the source spectrum. For a narrow same-band measurement, $K_R=-2.5\log_{10}[(1+z)L_\nu((1+z)\nu_R)/L_\nu(\nu_R)]$. Thus its sign depends on the spectrum, not on <redshift> alone. For $L_\nu\propto\nu^\alpha$, it is $-2.5(1+\alpha)\log_{10}(1+z)$. Finite-band corrections use the actual detector response and <magnitude zero point>.

= Power-law spectral K correction
{parent=K correction}
{title2=$S_{\nu_o}=P_{\nu_o}(1+z)^{1-\alpha}/d_L^2$}

For emitted spectral power per steradian $P_\nu\propto\nu^{-\alpha}$, the <luminosity distance> gives $S_{\nu_o}=(1+z)P_{(1+z)\nu_o}/d_L^2$. Photon energy and arrival-rate redshifts supply two factors of $1+z$, while the observed frequency interval is reduced by one. Substitution of the power-law spectrum gives the displayed <K correction>. This convention uses spectral flux per unit frequency and power per steradian; using per unit wavelength or total spectral luminosity changes the corresponding factors.

= Standard candle
{parent=Absolute magnitude}

A standard candle is an astronomical source whose calibrated <absolute magnitude> or <luminosity> allows its <apparent magnitude> or flux to determine a distance. A population with unknown common absolute magnitude only determines relative distances without an external luminosity calibration. <Type Ia supernova cosmology> models this distinction explicitly.

= Distance modulus
{title2=$\mu$}
{parent=Astronomical magnitude}
{wiki}

The distance modulus compares received brightness with intrinsic brightness through $\mu=m-M$, the difference between <apparent magnitude> and <absolute magnitude> in the same <photometric passband> and <magnitude zero point>. With no <interstellar extinction>, fixed emitted spectrum and <luminosity>, <isotropy> of emission, and the local <inverse-square law>, it determines <Euclidean distance>:
$$
\mu=m-M=5\log_{10}\left(\frac{d}{10\,\mathrm{pc}}\right),\qquad d=10\,\mathrm{pc}\;10^{\mu/5}.
$$
The ratio inside the <logarithm> is dimensionless. To see the factor $5$, use the <inverse-square law> in the <magnitude difference> formula:
$$
\frac{F(d)}{F_{10}}=\left(\frac{10\,\mathrm{pc}}{d}\right)^2,\qquad m-M=-2.5\log_{10}\left[\left(\frac{10\,\mathrm{pc}}{d}\right)^2\right].
$$
For example, $m=5$ and $M=0$ give $\mu=5$, hence $d=10\,\mathrm{pc}\times10=100\,\mathrm{pc}$. A tenfold increase in distance adds five magnitudes; an increase of one magnitude multiplies distance by $10^{1/5}\simeq1.584893$. The <distance modulus> is a <logarithmic scale> for distance, not a distance in length units. https://lco.global/spacebook/distance/what-is-distance-modulus/[Las Cumbres Observatory: What is distance modulus?]{ref}.

At $10$ <parsecs>, $\mu=0$ and $m=M$. Within $10$ <parsecs>, the <distance modulus> is negative; for example, $\mu=-5$ gives the positive distance $d=1\,\mathrm{pc}$. A negative <distance modulus> does not imply negative distance. At cosmological distances, the inference needs <luminosity distance> and a <K correction>; the simple local formula must not silently absorb these effects.

<Interstellar extinction> increases <apparent magnitude>. The observed <distance modulus> then includes both geometric dimming and attenuation; <extinction bias in photometric distance> explains how to separate them.

= Extinction bias in photometric distance
{parent=Distance modulus}
{title2=$d_{\mathrm{inferred}}/d=10^{A/5}$}

= Distance overestimate from ignored extinction
{synonym}

Let $A\geq0$ be the <interstellar extinction> in magnitudes in the specified <photometric passband>. Compared with the same unobscured source at the same distance, its <radiative flux> is multiplied by $10^{-0.4A}$, so its <apparent magnitude> increases by $A$. Consequently,
$$
m-M=5\log_{10}\left(\frac{d}{10\,\mathrm{pc}}\right)+A,\qquad \mu_0=m-M-A.
$$
Here $\mu_0$ is the extinction-corrected <distance modulus>. The correction subtracts $A$ before using the distance formula. Caltech's NED distance-method documentation explicitly distinguishes an apparent <distance modulus> from an extinction-corrected <distance modulus>. https://ned.ipac.caltech.edu/Library/Distances/distintro.html[NASA/IPAC NED: Cepheid apparent and reddening-corrected distances]{ref}.

Ignoring positive <interstellar extinction> attributes too much dimming to distance, producing an overestimate:
$$
\frac{d_{\mathrm{inferred}}}{d}=10^{A/5}.
$$
As an illustrative toy, put the source with $M=0$ at $100$ <parsecs> behind $A=1$ magnitude of <interstellar extinction>. The measured <apparent magnitude> is $m=6$. Setting $A=0$ incorrectly gives $d_{\mathrm{inferred}}=10\,\mathrm{pc}\times10^{6/5}\simeq158.489\,\mathrm{pc}$, a factor $1.584893$ too large. Subtracting the known $A=1$ recovers $100$ <parsecs>. The diagram's fixed $A=1$ offset is a controlled illustration, not a model of how actual <interstellar extinction> grows with distance, and none of its values are <star> catalogue measurements.

= Radiative transfer
{parent=Astrophysics}
{wiki}

Radiative transfer describes emission, absorption, and scattering of radiation through matter.

= Constant flux in a plane-parallel radiative-equilibrium atmosphere
{parent=Radiative transfer}

Taking the first angular moment of the radiative-transfer equation gives $dF_\nu/dz=4\pi\alpha_\nu(S_\nu-J_\nu)$. Radiative equilibrium sets the frequency integral of the right-hand side to zero, so the bolometric flux is constant with depth.

= Planck function
{title2=$B_\nu(T)$}
{c}
{parent=Radiative transfer}

The <Planck function> is the equilibrium spectral specific intensity of a <blackbody>, given by <Planck's law>. Per unit wavelength it is
$$
B_\lambda(T)=\frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\frac1{e^{hc/(\lambda k_BT)}-1}.
$$
It can also be written per unit frequency, with $B_\nu\,d\nu=-B_\lambda\,d\lambda$ under the corresponding change of variable.

= Radiation constant
{title2=$a_{\rm rad}$}
{parent=Planck function}
{wiki}

The radiation constant relates equilibrium radiation energy density to temperature by $u=a_{\rm rad}T^4$. It satisfies $a_{\rm rad}=4\sigma/c$ in terms of the Stefan-Boltzmann constant.

= Rayleigh-Jeans law
{c}
{parent=Planck function}
{wiki}

The Rayleigh-Jeans law is the long-wavelength, low-frequency limit of <Planck law>. When $hc/(\lambda k_BT)\ll1$,
$$
B_\lambda(T)\simeq\frac{2ck_BT}{\lambda^4}.
$$

= Wien approximation
{c}
{parent=Planck function}
{wiki}

= Wien limit
{c}
{synonym}

The Wien approximation is the short-wavelength limit of the Planck law:
$$
B_\lambda(T)\simeq\frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}
e^{-hc/(\lambda k_BT)}.
$$

= Wien's displacement law
{c}
{parent=Planck function}
{wiki}

= Wien displacement law
{c}
{synonym}

For a <blackbody>, the wavelength at which $B_\lambda(T)$ is largest obeys
$$
\lambda_{\max}T=b,
$$
where $b$ is <Wien displacement constant>.

= Frequency form of Wien's displacement law
{parent=Wien's displacement law}
{title2=$\nu_{\max}/T=x_*k_B/h,\quad x_*=2.821439\ldots$}

For the frequency version of <Planck law>, differentiating $x^3/(e^x-1)$ gives $3(1-e^{-x})=x$ at its nonzero maximum. Here $x=h\nu/(k_BT)$, so the peak frequency is proportional to <temperature>. This peak differs from converting the peak of the wavelength version by $\nu=c/\lambda$, because spectral densities transform with a Jacobian.

= Wien displacement constant
{c}
{title2=$b$}
{parent=Wien's displacement law}
{wiki}

The <Wien displacement constant> is the <constant of proportionality> $b$ in $\lambda_{\max}T=b$.

= Blackbody
{parent=Radiative transfer}
{wiki}

A <blackbody> absorbs all incident <electromagnetic radiation>. In <thermal equilibrium> it emits the universal <Planck law> spectrum determined solely by its <temperature>.

= Luminosity of a spherical blackbody
{parent=Blackbody}

An isotropic blackbody surface has outward flux $F=\pi\int B_\nu\,d\nu=\sigma T^4$. A sphere of radius $R$ therefore has luminosity
$$
L=4\pi R^2\sigma T^4.
$$

= Thermal bremsstrahlung
{parent=Radiative transfer}

= Thermal free-free emission
{synonym}

Thermal bremsstrahlung is free-free electromagnetic radiation from the acceleration of charged particles during Coulomb encounters in a thermal plasma. Its frequency-integrated emissivity in a fully ionized, nonrelativistic plasma scales approximately as $n_en_iT^{1/2}$.

= Gaunt factor
{parent=Thermal bremsstrahlung}
{c}
{title2=$g_{\rm ff}$}
{wiki}

The <Gaunt factor> is the dimensionless quantum correction to a classical free-free radiation calculation. Its weak temperature and frequency dependence modifies the approximate <thermal bremsstrahlung> scaling; a frequency-averaged value appears in the bolometric cooling coefficient.

= Optical depth
{title2=$\tau_\nu$}
{parent=Radiative transfer}
{wiki}

Optical depth satisfies $d\tau_\nu=\alpha_\nu ds$ along a path and measures attenuation through $I_\nu\propto e^{-\tau_\nu}$ in the absence of emission.

= Optically thick medium
{parent=Optical depth}

= Optically thick
{synonym}

An optically thick medium has <optical depth> $\tau\gg1$ along the relevant path. Photons undergo many interactions before escaping. <Radiative diffusion> applies under suitable near-isotropy conditions, but a large scattering depth alone does not guarantee thermalization when true absorption is weak.

= Optically thin medium
{parent=Optical depth}

= Optically thin
{synonym}

An optically thin medium has $\tau\ll1$, so attenuation and emission may be expanded to first order in optical depth.

= Optically thin atomic line intensity
{parent=Optically thin medium}
{title2=$I_{ul}=\frac{h\nu_{ul}}{4\pi}\int n_uA_{ul}ds$}

For isotropic emission without significant absorption, integrating the spontaneous photon-production rate along the sightline gives the displayed energy intensity per solid angle. Photon intensity omits the factor $h\nu$. Summing unresolved transitions is necessary before comparing with an observed blended spectral feature.

= Absorption coefficient
{title2=$\alpha_\nu$}
{parent=Optical depth}
{wiki}

The absorption coefficient is the fractional reduction of beam intensity per unit path length: $dI_\nu/ds=-\alpha_\nu I_\nu$ when emission and scattering are absent.

= Collision-induced absorption and emission
{wiki}
{parent=Absorption coefficient}

= Collision-induced absorption
{synonym}

A collision or transient molecular pair can possess a dipole even when the isolated molecules do not. The resulting <opacity> can be important in a dense <molecular hydrogen> atmosphere. Pair absorption per unit length scales approximately as the product of the two partner number densities, so its <mass absorption coefficient> is generally density-dependent. It supplies continuum absorption between ordinary spectral lines.

= Dust optical depth
{title2=$\tau_d$}
{parent=Optical depth}

For dust-grain number density $n_d$ and absorption cross-section $\sigma_d$, the dust optical depth along a path is $\tau_d=\int n_d\sigma_d\,ds$.

= Mass absorption coefficient
{title2=$\kappa_\nu$}
{parent=Dust optical depth}
{wiki}

The mass absorption coefficient is absorbing cross-section per unit mass. For identical spherical dust grains of radius $a$, mass $m_d$, and absorption efficiency $Q_\nu$, it is $\kappa_\nu=\pi a^2Q_\nu/m_d$.

= Optically thin dust-mass estimator
{title2=$M_d=F_\nu D^2/[\kappa_\nu B_\nu(T)]$}
{parent=Mass absorption coefficient}

For isothermal <optically thin> dust at distance $D$, its measured spectral flux density determines its mass through $M_d=F_\nu D^2/[\kappa_\nu B_\nu(T)]$. Temperature and opacity uncertainty commonly dominate the inferred mass uncertainty.

= Radiative transfer equation
{parent=Radiative transfer}
{wiki}

In plane-parallel optical-depth coordinates without scattering,
$$
\mu\frac{dI_\nu}{d\tau_\nu}=I_\nu-S_\nu,
$$
with source function $S_\nu$.

= Radiative transfer source function
{parent=Radiative transfer equation}
{title2=$S_\nu$}

= Source function
{synonym}

The <radiative transfer> <source function> is emission per unit extinction. If $j_\nu$ is emission per unit path length and $\rho\kappa_\nu$ is extinction per unit path length, $S_\nu=j_\nu/(\rho\kappa_\nu)$ and the transfer equation is $\mu\partial_{\tau_\nu}I_\nu=I_\nu-S_\nu$. Under thermal absorption in <local thermodynamic equilibrium> it is the <Planck function>; for conservative <coherent isotropic scattering> it is the <mean intensity>.

= Radiative diffusion
{parent=Radiative transfer equation}

In an optically thick medium whose radiation field is nearly isotropic and thermal, the first angular correction to the Planck function produces the diffusive flux
$$
F=-\frac{16\sigma T^3}{3\kappa_R\rho}\nabla T,
$$
where $\kappa_R$ is the Rosseland mean opacity.

= Rosseland mean opacity
{title2=$\kappa_R$}
{c}
{parent=Radiative diffusion}

The Rosseland mean opacity is the harmonic frequency average weighted by $\partial B_\nu/\partial T$. It controls radiative diffusion because flux preferentially travels through low-opacity frequencies.

It is a frequency-averaged <opacity> appropriate to the radiative-diffusion limit, not the general opacity concept itself.

= Local thermodynamic equilibrium
{title2=LTE}
{parent=Radiative transfer equation}
{wiki}

= LTE
{c}
{synonym}

In local thermodynamic equilibrium, matter populations are thermal at the local temperature and the source function is the Planck function, $S_\nu=B_\nu(T)$.

= Formal solution of the radiative transfer equation
{parent=Radiative transfer equation}

For an outward ray through a nonscattering atmosphere,
$$
I_\nu(0,\mu)=I_\nu(\tau_b,\mu)e^{-\tau_b/\mu}
+\int_0^{\tau_b}B_\nu[T(t)]e^{-t/\mu}\frac{dt}{\mu}.
$$

= Constant source transfer through a thin layer
{parent=Formal solution of the radiative transfer equation}
{title2=$I_{\rm out}=I_{\rm in}e^{-\tau}+S(1-e^{-\tau})$}

Along a radial ray through a layer with constant <source function>, the incident <specific intensity> is attenuated by $e^{-\tau}$ and the layer adds $S(1-e^{-\tau})$. At small <optical depth>, $I_{\rm out}=I_{\rm in}+(S-I_{\rm in})\tau+O(\tau^2)$. Whether the source exceeds the incident <specific intensity> determines the first-order emission or absorption contrast.

= Polynomial source function moments for emergent intensity
{parent=Formal solution of the radiative transfer equation}
{title2=$A_j=j!a_j$}

= Emergent-intensity polynomial coefficients
{synonym}

For a semi-infinite plane-parallel atmosphere with polynomial <source function> $S(\tau)=\sum a_j\tau^j$, the outward <specific intensity> is $\int_0^\infty S(\tau)e^{-\tau/\mu}\,d\tau/\mu$. Substitution $u=\tau/\mu$ gives coefficients $A_j=j!a_j$ in the surface polynomial in $\mu$. The deep boundary term must vanish. A local truncated Taylor series instead gives this contribution plus a remainder, so its surface polynomial is not automatically exact.

= Blackbody limit of an isothermal atmosphere
{parent=Formal solution of the radiative transfer equation}
{title2=$I_\lambda\longrightarrow B_\lambda(T)$}

For a thermalized <radiative transfer source function> $S_\lambda=B_\lambda(T)$ constant along a ray, the <specific intensity> after optical-depth interval $\Delta\tau$ is $I_\lambda=B_\lambda+(I_{\rm in}-B_\lambda)e^{-\Delta\tau/\mu}$ for $\mu>0$. As $\Delta\tau\to\infty$, it tends to the <Planck function>, independently of a bounded boundary input. A temperature gradient is unnecessary for <blackbody> emission. In a scattering medium, <local thermodynamic equilibrium> alone does not guarantee $S_\lambda=B_\lambda$; thermalization must be justified.

= One-layer spectral contrast
{parent=Formal solution of the radiative transfer equation}

A non-scattering isothermal atmospheric layer in <local thermodynamic equilibrium>, of <temperature> $T_a$ and slant <optical depth> $t_\lambda$, lies above a <blackbody> background at $T_b$. The <specific intensity> is
$$
I_\lambda=B_\lambda(T_b)e^{-t_\lambda}+B_\lambda(T_a)(1-e^{-t_\lambda}).
$$
Thus an increase in <opacity> shifts the <specific intensity> toward the layer's <Planck function>. If a line is more opaque than its continuum, it appears in absorption for $T_a<T_b$, in emission for $T_a>T_b$, and has no contrast for $T_a=T_b$. This is a local explanation, not a claim that all features diagnose one uniform layer.

= Eddington-Barbier relation
{c}
{parent=Formal solution of the radiative transfer equation}

When the source function varies approximately linearly with optical depth, the emergent intensity obeys $I_\nu(0,\mu)\simeq S_\nu(\tau_\nu=\mu)$.

The relation is an approximation within <radiative transfer>, not the entire transfer theory.

= Coherent isotropic scattering
{parent=Radiative transfer equation}

Coherent isotropic scattering preserves photon frequency and redistributes direction uniformly. Its source function is the mean intensity $J_\nu$, so conservative scattering removes light from a pencil beam while preserving luminosity integrated over all escaping directions.

= Single-scattering albedo
{title2=$\omega_\nu$}
{parent=Coherent isotropic scattering}
{wiki}

The single-scattering albedo is the fraction of total extinction due to scattering, $\omega_\nu=\alpha_{\nu,\rm sca}/\alpha_{\nu,\rm ext}$. Its complement is the absorptive fraction.

= Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation
{c}
{parent=Radiative transfer equation}
{wiki}

Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation states that a body in thermal equilibrium has equal absorptivity and emissivity at each frequency and direction. A layer with infrared absorptivity $\epsilon$ therefore emits $\epsilon B_\nu(T)$.

= Radiation-field moment
{parent=Radiative transfer}

For plane-parallel intensity, the standard moments are
$$
J_\nu=\frac12\int_{-1}^1I_\nu d\mu,
\quad
H_\nu=\frac12\int_{-1}^1\mu I_\nu d\mu,
\quad
K_\nu=\frac12\int_{-1}^1\mu^2I_\nu d\mu,
$$
and the radiative flux is $F_\nu=4\pi H_\nu$.

= Mean intensity
{title2=$J_\nu$}
{parent=Radiation-field moment}

The mean intensity is the angular average $J_\nu=(4\pi)^{-1}\int I_\nu d\Omega$ of the <specific intensity>.

= Radiative flux
{title2=$F_\nu$}
{parent=Radiation-field moment}

Radiative flux is the first angular moment $F_\nu=\int I_\nu\cos\theta\,d\Omega$ of <specific intensity> and measures net radiant <power> crossing unit area. Integrating over <frequency> gives bolometric <radiative flux>. The receiving surface's orientation matters through $\cos\theta$; <observed radiative flux> from a compact distant source conventionally refers to an area perpendicular to its <line of sight>.

= Observed radiative flux
{parent=Radiative flux}
{title2=$F=L/(4\pi d^2)$}

For a steady <point-like astronomical source> with <isotropy> of emission, in transparent space with negligible relativistic or cosmological effects, <conservation of energy> spreads its <luminosity> over a <sphere> of <surface area> $4\pi d^2$. Its bolometric observed radiative flux is
$$
F=\frac{L}{4\pi d^2},\qquad L=4\pi d^2F.
$$
This <inverse-square law> reflects geometric dilution: the same emitted <power> crosses increasingly large <spheres>. It does not require <light> to lose <energy> on the journey. For a fixed <luminosity>, doubling <Euclidean distance> quarters the <observed radiative flux>. https://science.nasa.gov/learn/basics-of-space-flight/chapter6-1/[NASA: Inverse-square propagation]{ref}.

With $L_0=10^{26}\,\mathrm W$ and $d_0=20$ <parsecs>, the result is $F_0\simeq2.09\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. At $40$ <parsecs>, the same source gives $F_0/4\simeq5.22\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. An ideal collecting area of $1\,\mathrm{m^2}$ at $20$ <parsecs> intercepts approximately $2.09\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm W$, before detector losses.

= Flux-distance degeneracy
{parent=Observed radiative flux}
{title2=$(L,d)\mapsto(k^2L,kd)$}

One <observed radiative flux> cannot determine both <luminosity> and <Euclidean distance>: for every $k>0$, replacing $(L,d)$ by $(k^2L,kd)$ leaves $F$ unchanged. In particular, $4\times10^{26}\,\mathrm W$ at $40$ <parsecs> produces the same <observed radiative flux> as $10^{26}\,\mathrm W$ at $20$ <parsecs>. This is a concrete failure of <identifiability> in an <inverse problem>.

An independent <stellar parallax> fixes the distance and breaks this degeneracy; alternatively, a calibrated <standard candle> supplies the <luminosity>. <Interstellar extinction> can further reduce the <observed radiative flux>, and a <photometric passband> measures only a selected spectral range. At cosmological distances, use <luminosity distance> in the bolometric relation. Each inference must specify which <radiative flux> was measured and what propagation assumptions connect it to <luminosity>.

= Observed flux of an unresolved spherical emitter
{parent=Radiative flux}

For a uniformly emitting spherical body of <radius> $R$ at <Euclidean distance> $d\gg R$, integration of its <specific intensity> over the apparent <solid angle> gives $f_\lambda=\pi I_\lambda(R/d)^2$. For a <blackbody>, $I_\lambda=B_\lambda(T)$ and the bolometric result is $f=\sigma_{\rm SB}T^4(R/d)^2=L/(4\pi d^2)$. A grey <emissivity> factor multiplies this expression; isothermality alone does not imply blackbody emission.

= Spectral flux density
{parent=Radiative flux}
{title2=$F_\lambda,\ F_\nu$}

Spectral flux density is radiant power received per unit area and per unit wavelength or frequency. The two conventions satisfy $F_\lambda\,d\lambda=-F_\nu\,d\nu$ under $\nu=c/\lambda$, hence $F_\lambda=(c/\lambda^2)F_\nu$ when the arguments refer to the same photons. Integrating over a <photometric passband> gives its measured flux.

= Eddington closure approximation
{c}
{parent=Radiation-field moment}

The Eddington closure approximation sets the second angular moment to one third of the zeroth, so the radiation pressure and mean intensity satisfy $cP_r=4\pi J/3$.

= Positivity limitation of a linear Eddington intensity
{parent=Eddington closure approximation}
{title2=$I(0,-1)=-F/(4\pi)$}

For the affine angular ansatz $I=A+C\cos\theta$, the <radiative flux> is $F=4\pi C/3$. Setting the inward hemispheric flux to zero gives $A(0)=2C/3$, but then the formal intensity at $\cos\theta=-1$ is negative when $F>0$. Exact no-incoming radiation and nonnegative intensity cannot both be represented by a nonzero affine polynomial on all directions. The <Eddington surface boundary condition> therefore specifies approximate moments, rather than an exact emergent angular radiation field.

= Grey atmosphere
{parent=Radiative transfer}
{wiki}

A grey atmosphere has frequency-independent opacity. In plane-parallel radiative equilibrium under the Eddington closure, its temperature obeys
$$
T^4=\frac34T_{\rm eff}^4\left(\tau+\frac23\right).
$$

= Obstruction to a single smooth exponential mode in conservative grey transfer
{parent=Grey atmosphere}

Consider $I=\alpha[\beta+\tau+\mu+\gamma e^{-\delta\tau}/(1+\epsilon\mu)]$ with real $|\epsilon|<1$, $\alpha\gamma\ne0$, and $\delta>0$. Its angular mean includes $A_0=\operatorname{atanh}\epsilon/\epsilon$. Substitution into $\mu I_\tau=I-J$ requires $1-A_0+(\delta-A_0\epsilon)\mu=0$. Thus $A_0=1$ and $\delta=\epsilon$. For real $\epsilon$, $A_0=1$ only at zero, giving $\delta=0$, a contradiction. Independently, its flux contains a varying term proportional to $A_1=(1-A_0)/\epsilon$, which is nonzero for nonzero real $\epsilon$. Exact conservative transfer therefore needs a more general angular/depth structure than this one smooth exponential correction.

= Hopf function of a grey atmosphere
{parent=Grey atmosphere}
{c}
{title2=$q(\tau)$}

The Hopf function encodes the departure of a plane-parallel <grey atmosphere> from a purely linear source profile. With physical bolometric <radiative flux> $F$ and standard <mean intensity> $J$, it is defined by $J=3F[\tau+q(\tau)]/(4\pi)$. In thermal <radiative equilibrium>, $T^4=3T_{\rm eff}^4[\tau+q(\tau)]/4$. The <Eddington closure approximation> with its approximate surface condition gives constant $q=2/3$; exact angular transfer generally gives a depth-dependent function.

= Eddington surface boundary condition
{c}
{parent=Grey atmosphere}
{title2=$P_{\rm rad}(0)=2F/(3c)$}

For no incident radiation and outward intensity approximated as direction independent, the surface angular moments are $F=\pi I_0$ and $P_{\rm rad}(0)=2\pi I_0/(3c)$. Integrating the radiation momentum equation with the <Eddington closure approximation> yields the <grey atmosphere> law $T^4=3T_{\rm eff}^4(\tau+2/3)/4$. This is an approximate angular boundary condition, not isotropy across both hemispheres.

= Plane-parallel atmosphere
{parent=Grey atmosphere}

A plane-parallel atmosphere neglects curvature over its vertical thickness, so thermodynamic variables depend on one Cartesian height coordinate.

= Plane-parallel validity from pressure scale height
{parent=Plane-parallel atmosphere}
{title2=$H/R\ll1$}

A <plane-parallel atmosphere> is geometrically accurate only if the relevant line-forming region is thin relative to the stellar radius. An isothermal estimate gives $H=k_BT/(\mu m_ug_{\rm eff})$. Radiative acceleration reduces the effective gravity and increases extension. A small photospheric $H/R$ does not justify a plane-parallel description of wind lines formed far above that photosphere.

= Astrophysical disk
{parent=Astrophysics}
{wiki}

= Astrophysical disc
{synonym}

An astrophysical disk is a flattened rotating distribution of gas, dust, stars, or other matter whose orbital motion strongly influences its structure and evolution.

= Axisymmetric waves of a homogeneous incompressible disk
{parent=Astrophysical disk}

In a thin constant-density disk with <free surfaces> $z=\pm H$, neglect slow radial gradients and let <perturbations> vary as $e^{ikr-i\omega t}$. With $h=p'/\rho$, the local inviscid equations are $-i\omega u-2\Omega v=-ikh$, $-i\omega v+\kappa^2u/(2\Omega)=0$, $-i\omega w=-h_z$ and $iku+w_z=0$. Eliminating <velocity> gives $h_{zz}=k^2\omega^2h/(\omega^2-\kappa^2)$. This local <WKB approximation> assumes $H/r\ll1$ and $|kr|\gg1$ and has both surface and <inertial wave> branches.

// Target: astrophysics.bigb

= Energy identity for free-surface disk waves
{parent=Axisymmetric waves of a homogeneous incompressible disk}

For radial and vertical <Lagrangian fluid displacements> of a local axisymmetric mode, integrate the momentum equations and use <incompressible flow> plus the <free-surface pressure condition of an incompressible disk>. The result is
$$
\omega^2\int_{-H}^H(|\xi_r|^2+|\xi_z|^2)dz
=\kappa^2\int_{-H}^H|\xi_r|^2dz+\Omega_z^2H\bigl(|\xi_z(H)|^2+|\xi_z(-H)|^2\bigr).
$$
For nonnegative $\kappa^2$ and positive $\Omega_z^2$, every nontrivial oscillatory mode has real nonnegative $\omega^2$. The surface term is the gravitational restoring energy of the displaced vacuum interfaces.

= Inertial modes of an incompressible disk
{parent=Axisymmetric waves of a homogeneous incompressible disk}

For $0<\omega^2<\kappa^2$, set $S=kH\omega/\sqrt{\kappa^2-\omega^2}$. Even-pressure modes satisfy $\omega^2=-\Omega_z^2S\tan S$, while odd-pressure modes satisfy $\omega^2=\Omega_z^2S\cot S$. In the case $\Omega_z>\kappa>0$, the branches approach $\kappa$ as $kH\to0$. For each fixed vertical order $n\geq1$ and large $kH$, $S\to n\pi/2$ and $\omega\sim\kappa n\pi/(2kH)$. Even orders have even <pressure> and odd orders odd <pressure>. The limiting <boundary condition> is zero normal derivative of <pressure> at both interfaces.

// Target: astrophysics.bigb

= Surface modes of an incompressible disk
{parent=Axisymmetric waves of a homogeneous incompressible disk}

For $\omega^2>\kappa^2$, set $Q=kH\omega/\sqrt{\omega^2-\kappa^2}$. Even-pressure and odd-pressure modes have $p'\propto\cosh(Qz/H)$ and $\sinh(Qz/H)$ and obey $\omega^2=\Omega_z^2Q\tanh Q$ and $\omega^2=\Omega_z^2Q\coth Q$, respectively. At large $kH$, both become free-surface gravity waves with
$$
\omega^2\sim\tfrac12\left[\kappa^2+\sqrt{\kappa^4+4\Omega_z^4(kH)^2}\right]=\Omega_z^2kH+\tfrac12\kappa^2+O((kH)^{-1}).
$$
Their opposite-parity splitting is exponentially small as the two interfaces decouple.

= Kuzmin disc
{c}
{synonym}

A Kuzmin disk is an infinitesimally thin axisymmetric <astrophysical disk> whose <relative potential> is $\psi=Gm/\sqrt{R^2+(|z|+b)^2}$, with $m,b>0$. Away from the disk this is a point-mass vacuum field. The jump of its vertical derivative is $-2Gmb/(R^2+b^2)^{3/2}$, so the <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity>, written $\nabla^2\psi=-4\pi G\rho$, gives the displayed <surface density>. Integrating $2\pi R\Sigma$ gives total <mass> $m$.

= Kuzmin reflection method
{c}
{parent=Kuzmin disk}
{title2=$\Sigma(R)=-\partial_z\psi(R,0^+)/(2\pi G)$}

Reflect a vacuum <gravitational potential> in the upper half-space evenly across $z=0$. For the convention in which <gravitational acceleration> is $\nabla\psi$, the derivative jump is $2\partial_z\psi(R,0^+)=-4\pi G\Sigma(R)$. Thus a point mass $m$ below the plane produces a <Kuzmin disk>, and superposing point masses with weight $\mu(b)\,db$ produces $\Sigma(R)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_0^\infty b\mu(b)(R^2+b^2)^{-3/2}\,db$. Positive weights give a nonnegative <surface density> wherever the integral converges.

= Scale-free Kuzmin superposition
{parent=Kuzmin reflection method}
{title2=$\mu(b)=Bb^\beta,\quad0<\beta<1$}

For $B>0$ and $0<\beta<1$, the unreferenced <relative potential> of the <Kuzmin reflection method> diverges at large $b$. Referencing each component to the origin before integrating gives the finite potential difference
$$
\psi(R,z)=GB\int_0^\infty b^\beta\left[\frac1{\sqrt{R^2+(|z|+b)^2}}-\frac1b\right]db.
$$
At small $b$ the subtraction is integrable because $\beta>0$; at large $b$ the difference is $O(b^{-2})$, integrable because $\beta<1$. Its plane force is $\partial_R\psi=-CR^{\beta-1}$, where $C=(GB/2)B((1+\beta)/2,1-\beta/2)$. A <circular speed> $V=KR^{\beta/2}$ fixes $C=K^2$, giving
$$
\Sigma(R)=\frac{K^2R^{\beta-1}}{2\pi G}\frac{\Gamma(1+\beta/2)\Gamma((1-\beta)/2)}{\Gamma((1+\beta)/2)\Gamma(1-\beta/2)}.
$$
The <beta function> substitutions for the force and <surface density> have different exponents. Its central mass is locally finite, but its total mass diverges at large radius; a potential zero at infinity cannot be imposed. The force and density approach those of the <Mestel disk> as $\beta\downarrow0$.

= Disk warp
{parent=Astrophysical disk}
{title2=$W(R)=\beta(R)e^{i\gamma(R)}$}

A disk warp is a radial change of the orbital plane of successive disk annuli. In the small-tilt approximation, a complex tilt $W$ records its magnitude and azimuth. Pressure and internal torques communicate the tilt through <bending waves of a Keplerian disk> in the inviscid thin-disk regime. A flat tilted disk has constant $W$ even though its absolute orientation differs from the reference equatorial plane.

= Inviscid warp equations of a nearly Keplerian disk
{parent=Disk warp}
{title2=$\Sigma R^2\Omega(W_t-i\omega_1W)=R^{-1}G_R,\quad G_t-i\omega_2G=\tfrac14 PR^3\Omega W_R$}

Here $G$ is the complex internal horizontal torque, $P$ the vertically integrated pressure, and $\omega_1,\omega_2$ the approximate <nodal precession> and <apsidal precession> rates. In the exactly Keplerian case, local plane waves obey $\omega^2=k^2P/(4\Sigma)=k^2c_s^2/4$, so tilt disturbances propagate at $c_s/2$. The secular thin-disk approximation requires wavelengths large relative to the height while the local approximation requires them small relative to the orbital radius.

= Stationary bending warp of a Kerr disk
{parent=Inviscid warp equations of a nearly Keplerian disk}
{title2=$W=W_\infty\cos(x_{\mathrm{in}}-x)/\cos x_{\mathrm{in}},\quad x=4\sqrt{24a}\,r^{-5/4}/(5\epsilon)$}

For $a>0$, $\Sigma\propto R^{-3/4}$, constant <disk aspect ratio> $\epsilon=H/R$, and $r=Rc^2/(GM)$, the inviscid steady <disk warp> equation reduces to $W_{xx}+W=0$ in the displayed coordinate. The vanishing inner torque gives $W_x(x_{\mathrm{in}})=0$; the outer tilt gives $W(0)=W_\infty$. The displayed solution holds when $\cos x_{\mathrm{in}}\ne0$. If $x_{\mathrm{in}}=\pi$, it is $W=W_\infty\cos x$, with an aligned node at $r=2^{4/5}r_{\mathrm{in}}$ and opposite signed inner tilt. For negative spin the corresponding equation is hyperbolic, giving $W=W_\infty\cosh(x_{\mathrm{in}}-x)/\cosh x_{\mathrm{in}}$ with $x=4\sqrt{24|a|}\,r^{-5/4}/(5\epsilon)$.

= Hankel representation of thin-disk gravity
{c}
{parent=Astrophysical disk}
{title2=$\Phi=\int_0^\infty S(k)J_0(kR)e^{-k|z|}\,dk$}

For an axisymmetric reflection-symmetric <thin disk>, the <Laplace equation in cylindrical coordinates> separates into regular radial <Bessel functions> and vertical decaying exponentials. The <divergence theorem> imposes $\Phi_z^+-\Phi_z^-=4\pi G\Sigma$. <Hankel transform> inversion gives $S(k)=-2\pi G\int_0^\infty\Sigma(R)J_0(kR)R\,dR$. Its radial derivative gives the <galaxy rotation curve>. Infinite scale-free models may require subtracting an additive divergent constant.

= Angular-momentum-conserving self-gravitating disc radius
{parent=Astrophysical disk}
{title2=$R_h/R_d=C_df_d/q^2$}

Let a disc contain fraction $f_d$ of total halo mass, take its edge gravity to be $v_d^2=C_dGM_d/R_d$, and equate its edge specific angular momentum to $qR_hv_{\rm vir}$. With $v_{\rm vir}^2=GM_h/R_h$, these conditions give $R_h/R_d=C_df_d/q^2$. The structural coefficient $C_d$ is essential: disc mass alone does not uniquely determine its edge rotation curve.

A protoplanetary disk is a gas-and-solid <astrophysical disk> around a young star in which <planet formation> occurs. Its evolution combines <accretion>, radiation, gas dynamics and the growth of solid material. A <minimum-mass solar nebula> is one reconstruction of the material needed to form the Solar System.

= Mestel disc
{c}
{synonym}

A Mestel disk is a thin axisymmetric <astrophysical disk> with <surface density> inversely proportional to radius. Its <galaxy rotation curve> is exactly flat, $v_c=v_M$, and $v_c^2=GM(R)/R$ despite its nonspherical geometry. In the <Legendre expansion of thin-disk gravity>, all positive-degree interior and exterior terms cancel. Its total mass is infinite, so only potential differences, rather than a potential zero at infinity, are defined.

= Rotating Mestel disk distribution function
{parent=Mestel disk}
{title2=$f=A L_z^q e^{-E/s},\quad q=v_c^2/s-1$}

In the midplane <Mestel disk> potential $\Phi=v_c^2\log(R/R_0)$, take specific <energy> $E=(v_R^2+v_\phi^2)/2+\Phi$ and $L_z=Rv_\phi$. A prograde <galactic distribution function> $f=A L_z^qe^{-E/s}$ for $L_z>0$, with $s>0$, has <surface density> proportional to $R^{q-v_c^2/s}$. Matching $\Sigma=\Sigma_0R_0/R$ gives $q=v_c^2/s-1>-1$ and
$$
A=\frac{\Sigma_0R_0^{-q}}{\sqrt\pi\,2^{q/2}s^{(q+2)/2}\Gamma((q+1)/2)}.
$$
The denominator is the product of the full radial Gaussian integral and the positive-azimuthal half-line moment. Here $s$ is a squared-velocity scale, not a <velocity dispersion> itself. Changing the potential's additive constant changes $A$. With positive exponent $e^{\varepsilon/s}$, $\varepsilon$ must instead be the negative of the specific total energy.

= Velocity moments of an isotropic Mestel disk
{parent=Mestel disk}
{title2=$\sigma_R^2=\sigma_\phi^2=v_0^2$}

The planar <Mestel disk> has $F(E)\propto e^{-E/v_0^2}$, giving independent centered <Gaussian distributions> for $v_R,v_\phi$ with <velocity dispersions> $\sigma_R=\sigma_\phi=v_0$. Its <streaming velocity> is zero. A <maximally prograde stellar distribution> instead has mean $\sqrt{2/\pi}v_0$ and azimuthal <variance> $v_0^2(1-2/\pi)$, while all second raw moments remain unchanged.

= Mestel disk potential-density pair
{c}
{parent=Mestel disk}
{title2=$\Phi=v_0^2\log[(r+|z|)/R_*]$}

The reflection-symmetric <Newtonian gravitational potential> $\Phi=v_0^2\log[(\sqrt{R^2+z^2}+|z|)/R_*]$ is harmonic off the plane. Its normal-derivative jump is $2v_0^2/R$, so the <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity> gives <surface density of a disk> $S=v_0^2/(2\pi GR)$ and volume <mass density> $\rho=S\delta(z)$. The midplane <circular speed> is constant. The scale-free model has infinite total <mass> and does not use a finite-mass boundary condition at infinity.

= Boundary ambiguity of a flat disk rotation curve
{parent=Mestel disk potential-density pair}

The midplane <galaxy rotation curve> fixes the radial derivative of the <Newtonian gravitational potential>, not every exterior boundary condition. Adding $a|z|$ leaves that radial derivative unchanged but adds a uniform sheet of <surface density of a disk> $a/(2\pi G)$. The pure scale-free <Mestel disk> excludes such extra sources.

= Legendre expansion of thin-disk gravity
{parent=Astrophysical disk}
{title2=$\Phi=-2G\sum_{k\geq0}\alpha_{2k}(R^{-2k-1}I_{2k}+R^{2k}J_{2k})$}

For a thin axisymmetric <astrophysical disk>, angular integration of the Newtonian kernel eliminates odd <Legendre polynomials>. Define $I_n=\int_0^R\Sigma(s)s^{n+1}ds$ and $J_n=\int_R^\infty\Sigma(s)s^{-n}ds$. The even coefficient is $\alpha_n=\pi[n!/(2^n((n/2)!)^2)]^2$. In differentiating the paired inner and outer contributions, their moving-boundary terms cancel. The resulting <galaxy rotation curve> includes an inward inner-ring contribution and an outward exterior-ring contribution. For smooth <surface density>, the force at the disk is interpreted with a symmetric <Cauchy principal value> or a vanishing-thickness regularization.

= Radiation-supported vertical disk
{parent=Astrophysical disk}

A <radiation-supported vertical disk> has vertical <hydrostatic equilibrium> supplied mainly by <radiation pressure>. In a constant-<opacity>, optically thick layer in gravity $-\Omega^2z$, <radiative diffusion> gives $F=c\Omega^2z/\kappa$. This statement applies at a fixed radius and does not resolve the optically thin surface.

Combining $F=c\Omega^2z/\kappa$ with viscous heating $dF/dz=(9/4)\mu\Omega^2$ gives a height-independent <dynamic viscosity> $\mu=4c/(9\kappa)$ under constant <opacity>. This exact limiting relation differs from an approximate vertically averaged <alpha disk> closure.

= Thin disc

A thin disk has vertical semi-thickness $H$ much smaller than cylindrical radius $r$. Vertical gravity is then approximately linear in height and orbital dynamics is nearly planar.

= Passive disk

= Passive disc
{synonym}

A passive <astrophysical disk> receives most of its heat from external irradiation rather than internal <viscous dissipation>. For a stellar disk, a rough criterion is $GM\dot M/R_*\ll L_*$. The actual local requirement compares absorbed stellar flux with viscous flux; incidence angle, shadowing and absorption can invalidate a luminosity comparison alone.

= Irradiation grazing angle of a disk
{parent=Passive disk}
{title2=$\alpha\simeq z_s'-z_s/R=R\,d(z_s/R)/dR$}

For a thin illuminated surface $z=z_s(R)$ and a central point source, the radial light ray has slope $z_s/R$ whereas the surface tangent has slope $z_s'$. Their angle difference is the grazing angle. The incident flux is $L_*\alpha/(4\pi R^2)$ to leading order. This requires a positive grazing angle and an unobstructed ray; a negative formal flux instead signals failure of direct illumination, not negative heating.

= Mean projected area of a randomly oriented thin disc
{parent=Thin disk}
{title2=$\langle A_{\rm proj}\rangle=\pi R^2/2$}

A thin circular disc presents projected area $\pi R^2|\cos i|$ to a sightline. Isotropic normals have a uniform distribution of $\cos i$ on $[-1,1]$, giving mean absolute cosine one half. The resulting interception cross-section is therefore half the face-on area; no extra orientation weighting is needed after this average is used in an incidence calculation.

= Thin-disk expansion of the stellar gravitational potential

At fixed <cylindrical radius> $R$ with $|z|\ll R$, the central <gravitational potential> is harmonic in height. The <vertical epicyclic frequency> equals $\Omega_K$ in this spherical potential.

= Razor-thin disk
{synonym}

The razor-thin disk approximation replaces a disk's vertical density profile by $\rho(x,y,z)=\Sigma(x,y)\delta(z)$. It is useful for horizontal dynamics on scales much longer than the disk thickness.

= Sharp-edge force singularity of a thin disk
{parent=Razor-thin disk approximation}
{title2=$g_R\sim G\Sigma_{\rm edge}\log(\ell/\epsilon)$}

At an abruptly truncated <razor-thin disk> with nonzero edge <surface density of a disk>, the midplane radial force diverges logarithmically. Locally, mass occupies a half-plane and the inward force contains a nonzero angular factor multiplying $G\Sigma_{\rm edge}\int_\epsilon^\ell ds/s$. The opposite exterior half-plane is absent, so its cancellation does not occur. Finite thickness or a taper taking the edge density to zero regularizes the force. Therefore a finite-edge <galaxy rotation curve> requires more than the ideal scale-free <Mestel disk> normalization.

= Barotropic closure of a razor-thin disk
{parent=Razor-thin disk approximation}
{title2=$c_s^2=dP/d\Sigma$}

A two-dimensional disk equation with <pressure> acceleration $-\Sigma^{-1}\nabla P$ requires an equation of state for vertically integrated <pressure> $P$ in terms of <surface density of a disk> $\Sigma$. A <barotropic fluid> closure is $P=P(\Sigma)$, with linear enthalpy perturbation $h'=c_s^2\Sigma'/\Sigma_0$. Merely giving $P=P(\rho)$ for a volume density does not close the two-dimensional equations unless a vertical conversion $\rho(\Sigma)$ is specified. Positive $c_s^2$ gives ordinary <pressure> restoration of an <inertial-acoustic wave>.

= Razor-thin disk Poisson kernel
{parent=Razor-thin disk approximation}

For a horizontal Fourier mode of <wavenumber> $k>0$, the midplane gravitational potential of a razor-thin disk is
$$
\widehat\Phi_m(\mathbf k)=-\frac{2\pi G}{k}\widehat\Sigma(\mathbf k).
$$

= Thin-disk potential-density inversion
{parent=Razor-thin disk Poisson kernel}
{title2=$\widetilde\Sigma=-k\widetilde\Phi/(2\pi G)$}

For an isolated <razor-thin disk>, define the two-dimensional <Fourier transform> by $\widetilde g(\mathbf k)=\int e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}g(\mathbf x)d^2x$. At $k>0$, the <razor-thin disk Poisson kernel> gives the displayed inversion. Since the transform of $1/|\mathbf x|$ is $2\pi/k$, inversion also gives $\Sigma=(4\pi^2G)^{-1}(1/|\mathbf x|)*\Delta_2\Phi$. The forward relation is $\Phi=-G(1/|\mathbf x|)*\Sigma$. An additive potential constant is irrelevant; a uniform infinite sheet and scale-free divergent potentials require separate zero-mode or potential-difference treatment rather than an arbitrary decaying boundary condition.

= Off-plane razor-thin Poisson kernel
{parent=Razor-thin disk Poisson kernel}
{title2=$\widetilde\Phi(\mathbf k,z)=-(2\pi G/k)\widetilde\Sigma(\mathbf k)e^{-k|z|}$}

For $k>0$, horizontal <Fourier transform> gives $(\partial_z^2-k^2)\widetilde\Phi=4\pi G\widetilde\Sigma\delta(z)$. Decay and <continuity> give an exponential on each side; its derivative jump fixes the coefficient. The uniform mode needs a separate linear vertical solution and a potential reference.

= Positive-kernel comparison of thin-disk fields

The <triangle inequality> therefore bounds a signed-<surface density> field by the field of its absolute <surface density>. If that <surface density> is everywhere smaller than a nonnegative comparison <surface density>, their vertical fields satisfy the displayed strict comparison, provided the integrals exist and the strict bound holds on positive measure. This is useful when comparing <gravity-equivalent magnetic surface density> with actual disk mass.

= Vertical structure of an astrophysical disk
{parent=Thin disk}

The vertical structure of a thin disk is set by the balance among vertical gravity, pressure, self-gravity, magnetic forces, and motions of gas and solids.

= Vertical mass coordinate of a disk
{parent=Vertical structure of an astrophysical disk}
{title2=$m(z)=\int_0^z\rho(z')\,dz'$}

In a reflection-symmetric <astrophysical disk>, the vertical mass coordinate measures <mass> per unit area above the midplane. It satisfies $dm=\rho\,dz$, so $\partial_m=\rho^{-1}\partial_z$ inside the material. If the total <surface density> is $\Sigma$, the upper face is $m=\Sigma/2$; the normalized coordinate $\zeta=2m/\Sigma$ ranges from zero to one. Integrals weighted by <mass density> become ordinary integrals in $m$, which is especially useful for <radiative diffusion> and the <density-weighted mean kinematic viscosity>.

= Isothermal vertical structure in stellar gravity
{parent=Vertical structure of an astrophysical disk}
{title2=$\rho(z)=\rho_0e^{-z^2/(2H^2)},\quad H=c_s/\Omega_K$}

For a <thin disk> in the harmonic vertical <gravitational potential> $\Omega_K^2z^2/2$, <hydrostatic equilibrium> and $p=c_s^2\rho$ with height-independent <isothermal sound speed> give the displayed Gaussian density. For perturbations with <adiabatic index> $\gamma$, the <Brunt–Väisälä frequency> is $N^2=(1-1/\gamma)\Omega_K^2z^2/H^2$. An isothermal background can therefore support buoyancy for adiabatic perturbations; isothermal perturbations instead have $\gamma=1$ and zero buoyancy frequency.

= Vertically integrated pressure of an isothermal disk
{parent=Isothermal vertical structure in stellar gravity}
{title2=$P=c_s^2\Sigma=\Omega_z^2\Sigma H^2$}

A locally isothermal disk in vertically harmonic external gravity has <hydrostatic equilibrium> $c_s^2\rho_z=-\Omega_z^2z\rho$, hence $\rho=\rho_0e^{-z^2/(2H^2)}$ with $H=c_s/\Omega_z$. Because $c_s$ is independent of height, integrating $p=c_s^2\rho$ gives the displayed pressure. The harmonic-gravity approximation has the accuracy of the <thin disk> expansion.

= Hermite vertical mode of an isothermal disk
{c}
{parent=Isothermal vertical structure in stellar gravity}
{title2=$\xi_{xx}-x\xi_x+n\xi=0$}

= Hermite vertical modes of an isothermal disk
{c}
{synonym}

In an inviscid locally isothermal <Keplerian disk>, a radial <normal mode> of <wavenumber> $k$ has pressure variable $\xi$ proportional to a <probabilists' Hermite polynomial> in $x=z/H$. Gaussian-weighted finite energy excludes the exponentially growing vertical branch. Polynomial termination gives $n=0,1,\ldots$ and the local dispersion relation $(\widehat\omega^2-n\Omega_K^2)(\widehat\omega^2-\Omega_K^2)=k^2c_s^2\widehat\omega^2$, where $\widehat\omega=\omega-m\Omega_K$. At singular values introduced by eliminating velocity components, the original equations must also be checked. The $n=0$ propagating branch is an <inertial-acoustic wave>; $n=1$ has vertically uniform vertical velocity and odd pressure perturbation, permitting bending disturbances.

= Bending wave of a Keplerian disk
{parent=Hermite vertical mode of an isothermal disk}
{title2=$\omega\simeq\pm kc_s/2\quad(m=1,\ |k|H\ll1)$}

= Bending waves of a Keplerian disk
{synonym}

For the $n=1$, $m=1$ <Hermite vertical mode of an isothermal disk>, the local dispersion relation is $[(\omega-\Omega_K)^2-\Omega_K^2]^2=k^2c_s^2(\omega-\Omega_K)^2$. Its low inertial-frame frequency branches satisfy $\omega\simeq\pm kc_s/2$. These <waves> transport a changing tilt of successive annuli, or a warp, with <group velocity> $\pm c_s/2$. The vertical velocity is approximately independent of height. A rigid tilt is the zero-wavenumber, zero-pattern-frequency limit; the gas still executes vertical orbital motion at frequency $\Omega_K$ in its rotating frame.

= Constant gas-radiation ratio vertical disc structure
{parent=Vertical structure of an astrophysical disk}
{title2=$T\propto1-z^2/H^2,\quad\rho\propto T^3,\quad p\propto T^4$}

For constant positive $\lambda=p_r/p_g$ and molecular gas constant $\mathcal R=k/(\mu_m m_p)$, the gas–radiation <equation of state> gives $\rho=4\sigma T^3/(3c\lambda\mathcal R)$. Vertical <hydrostatic equilibrium> in harmonic central gravity therefore gives $T=T_0(1-z^2/H^2)$ with $T_0=\Omega^2H^2/[8(1+\lambda)\mathcal R]$. The <mass density> and <pressure> powers are three and four. Integrating both faces yields $\Sigma=(32/35)\rho_0H$ and $\int p\,dz=\Sigma\Omega^2H^2/9$. With constant <opacity>, <radiative diffusion> produces a linear flux and height-independent <dynamic viscosity> $\mu=4c\lambda/[9\kappa(1+\lambda)]$. This is an interior formal profile; its low-density surface requires a photospheric treatment beyond diffusion.

= Vertical dynamical timescale of a disk
{parent=Vertical structure of an astrophysical disk}
{title2=$t_{\rm dyn}\sim\Omega_z^{-1}$}

The time for a vertical <pressure> or gravity response. A <sound-crossing time> and a small vertical oscillation period have this scale in a <thin disk>. It need not equal a damping time.

= Polytropic vertical structure in stellar gravity
{parent=Vertical structure of an astrophysical disk}
{title2=$T=T_0(1-z^2/H^2),\quad\rho\propto T^n$}

In harmonic stellar gravity, an <ideal gas> with a <polytropic equation of state> of index $n>0$ has a quadratic <temperature> profile and finite semi-thickness $H^2=2(n+1)\mathcal R T_0/\Omega^2$. This differs from the <polytropic vertical structure of a self-gravitating disk>.

= Zero-radiative-flux obstruction for a polytropic ideal gas
{parent=Polytropic vertical structure in stellar gravity}

For positive finite <opacity> and nonvacuum <ideal gas> satisfying <radiative diffusion>, zero flux implies $dT/dz=0$. Write $P=\mathcal R\rho T$, where $\mathcal R=k_B/(\mu_m m_p)$ for constant <mean molecular weight> $\mu_m$ in proton-mass units. If a <polytropic equation of state> $P=K\rho^\gamma$ with constant $K>0$ is also imposed, then $T=(K/\mathcal R)\rho^{\gamma-1}$. For $\gamma\ne1$, constant <temperature> would force constant density, contradicting vertical <hydrostatic equilibrium> $P'=-\rho\Omega^2z$ on an open interval away from the midplane. The familiar finite polytropic slab for $\gamma>1$ is therefore a solution of the hydrostatic/adiabatic closure, not of this finite-opacity zero-radiative-flux system. For $\gamma=1$, the consistent isothermal profile is $\rho=\rho_m\exp[-\Omega^2z^2/(2\mathcal R T_m)]$, with no finite surface.

= Disk scale height
{title2=$H$}
{parent=Vertical structure of an astrophysical disk}

The disk scale height is the characteristic vertical thickness over which density or pressure decreases. Vertical hydrostatic balance in a thin Keplerian gas disk gives $H\simeq c_s/\Omega_K$.

= Disk aspect ratio
{parent=Disk scale height}
{title2=$H/r$}

The aspect ratio of an <astrophysical disk> is its vertical thickness scale divided by cylindrical radius. A <thin disk> has $H/r\ll1$. In gas-supported vertical <hydrostatic equilibrium> with harmonic external gravity $-\Omega_z^2z$, $H/r\sim c_s/(r\Omega_z)$, where $\Omega_z$ is the <vertical epicyclic frequency> and $c_s$ is the <sound speed>. In a centrally dominated <Keplerian disk>, $\Omega_z=\Omega$, so this compares <sound speed> with orbital speed. If disk self-gravity supplies the vertical restoring force, the relation $H\sim c_s/\Omega$ requires replacement. A polytropic free-surface height and an isothermal scale height differ by model-dependent constants even though their aspect ratios have the same basic role.

= Disk flaring
{parent=Disk aspect ratio}
{title2=$d(H/r)/dr>0$}

A disk flares when its <disk aspect ratio> increases outward. This is stronger than the statement that its height increases: a disk with $H\propto r$ has constant aspect ratio and is not flaring by this definition. The radial <temperature> and <surface density of a disk> profiles determine flaring in a specified vertical equilibrium model.

= Flaring passive disk
{parent=Disk flaring}
{title2=$z_s/R\propto R^{2/7},\quad T\propto R^{-3/7}$}

Assume a <passive disk> has local <isothermal sound speed> squared $c_s^2=aT$, <disk scale height> $H=c_s/\Omega_K$, and irradiated height $z_s=fH$ with constant $f$. Its <irradiation grazing angle of a disk> and <Stefan–Boltzmann law> give $q'=Cq^8/R^3$ for $q=z_s/R$, where $C=4\pi\sigma(GM/a)^4/(L_*f^8)$. Integrating gives $q^{-7}=R_0^2/R^2+B$, with $R_0^2=7C/2$. Where the first term dominates and the <thin disk> assumptions hold, $q\simeq(R/R_0)^{2/7}$, $H\propto R^{9/7}$ and $T\propto R^{-3/7}$. The law cannot be extrapolated into a geometrically thick region using the same grazing-angle approximation.

= Vertical epicyclic frequency
{title2=$\Omega_z$}
{parent=Vertical structure of an astrophysical disk}

The vertical epicyclic frequency is the frequency of a small vertical oscillation about a circular orbit. In an axisymmetric potential, $\Omega_z^2=\partial_z^2\Phi$ at the midplane; in a spherical potential it equals the circular orbital frequency.

= Radiation-to-gas pressure ratio
{title2=$\beta_{\rm rg}=p_{\rm rad}/p_{\rm gas}$}
{parent=Vertical structure of an astrophysical disk}

The radiation-to-gas pressure ratio compares <radiation pressure> with <gas pressure>. It is distinct from <plasma beta>, which compares gas pressure with <magnetic pressure>. Both are sometimes denoted by $\beta$, so the convention must be stated.

= Constant-pressure-ratio vertical disk model
{parent=Radiation-to-gas pressure ratio}

Assume a <thin disk> has height-independent <opacity> $\kappa_{\rm op}$ and <radiation-to-gas pressure ratio> $\beta$, with vertical gravity $-\Omega_z^2z$. <Hydrostatic equilibrium> and <radiative diffusion> imply
$$
F_z=\frac\beta{1+\beta}\frac{c\Omega_z^2}{\kappa_{\rm op}}z.
$$
Balancing its derivative against viscous heating $\rho\nu q^2\Omega^2$ fixes the <dynamic viscosity>
$$
\rho\nu=\frac\beta{1+\beta}\frac{c}{q^2\kappa_{\rm op}}\frac{\Omega_z^2}{\Omega^2}.
$$
It is independent of height under these assumptions. For a <spherically symmetric potential>, $\Omega_z=\Omega$. Integrating $\rho\nu$ through the full surface-to-surface thickness $2H$ gives $\bar\nu\Sigma=2H\rho\nu$, which determines the thickness from the radial <accretion rate> solution.

= Dust settling in an astrophysical disk
{parent=Vertical structure of an astrophysical disk}

Dust settling is the vertical drift of solid particles toward a disk's midplane under gravity and <drag force>. In the strong-drag limit, a particle with <aerodynamic stopping time> $\tau$ settles through a linear vertical gravitational field at rate $\Omega_z^2\tau$.

= Homologous vertical motion of an astrophysical disk
{parent=Vertical structure of an astrophysical disk}

In homologous vertical motion, every height scales by one time-dependent thickness $H(t)$, so $z=H(t)\zeta$ and the vertical velocity is $u_z=\dot H\zeta$. A fixed dimensionless density profile then expands and contracts without changing shape.

= Slow cooling of an astrophysical disk
{parent=Homologous vertical motion of an astrophysical disk}
{title2=$\eta=(1+Ct)^{-1/(1+2\beta)}$}

A laminar column can contract through nearly <hydrostatic equilibrium> when its cooling time greatly exceeds its <vertical dynamical timescale of a disk>. For cooling $AT^{1+\beta}$ and fixed column mass, a <similarity solution> has $\rho\propto\eta^{-1}$, $T\propto\eta^2$, $P\propto\eta$, and vertical velocity $u_z=\dot\eta z/\eta$. The coefficient $C$ also depends on the initial <mass density> normalization.

= Vertical breathing mode of an astrophysical disk
{title2=$\omega_H$}
{parent=Homologous vertical motion of an astrophysical disk}

The vertical breathing mode is a coherent oscillation of a disk's thickness. For an adiabatic gas with pressure proportional to $H^{1-\gamma}$ in a harmonic vertical potential, $\omega_H=\sqrt{\gamma+1}\,\Omega_z$.

An accretion disk transports mass inward and angular momentum outward through stresses, while dissipating orbital energy as heat.

= Quadratic-polytrope disk with constant aspect ratio
{parent=Accretion disk}
{title2=$\Sigma=\frac{GM}{K}[\operatorname{arsinh}\epsilon-\epsilon/\sqrt{1+\epsilon^2}]$}

For $p=K\rho^2$, a point-mass <gravitational potential> and a zero-density free surface at $z=\pm\epsilon r$, the <barotropic cylindrical rotation theorem> gives $\Omega^2=GM/(r^3\sqrt{1+\epsilon^2})$. = Thin-accretion-disk truncation error
{parent=Accretion disk}
{title2=$O((H/r)^2)$}

For smooth radial variation on scale $r$, $c_s\sim\Omega H$, $\nu\sim\alpha\Omega H^2$, $u_r\sim\nu/r$ and $u_z\sim Hu_r/r$. The exact point-mass vertical gravity differs from $\Omega_K^2z$ by relative order $(H/r)^2$. Radial <pressure> support changes the rotation and dominant shear heating by the same order. Meridional viscous forces, radial radiative-flux divergence, thermal <advection> and compressional work are at most order $(H/r)^2$ relative to their retained vertical terms for $\alpha\lesssim1$. These estimates require secular disk evolution and exclude sharp radial <boundary layers> or independently rapid vertical motion.

= Wind-powered mass loss from a steady accretion disk
{parent=Accretion disk}
{title2=$\dot M'=2\pi RS,\quad\nu\Sigma=R\dot M'/(9\pi f)$}

For a <Keplerian disk>, let $S$ be the total loss through both faces per unit projected area and $\dot M>0$ the inward <accretion rate>. Annular <mass conservation> gives $\dot M'=2\pi RS$. A circular orbit has <specific orbital energy> $-GM/(2R)$, so a marginally escaping wind requires additional energy $GM/(2R)$ per unit lost mass. Equating this sink to a fraction $f$ of the two-face <viscous dissipation> $(9/4)\nu\Sigma\Omega^2$ gives the displayed relation. If the wind carries the local <specific angular momentum> $h=\sqrt{GMR}$, <conservation of angular momentum> gives $(\dot Mh-\mathcal G)'=h\dot M'$, with <viscous torque in an accretion disk> $\mathcal G=3\pi\nu\Sigma h$. Eliminating the torque yields $R^2\dot M''+\tfrac32R\dot M'=\tfrac32f\dot M$.

= Accretion-disk boundary layer
{parent=Accretion disk}
{title2=$T_{\rm BL},\ L_{\rm BL}$}

An accretion-disk boundary layer connects nearly <Keplerian rotation> to a stellar surface rotating at a different angular speed. For a slowly rotating star, dissipation of the incoming orbital <kinetic energy> gives $L_{\rm BL}\simeq GM\dot M/(2r_*)$, comparable to the <standard thin-disk luminosity>. If the emitting area scales as $r_*H$, its <blackbody> <effective temperature> scales as $T_{\rm BL}\sim(r_*/H)^{1/4}T_{\rm in}$. The coefficient depends on geometry, and stellar rotation reduces the available energy.

= Accretion flow
{parent=Accretion disk}

An accretion flow carries matter inward through a gravitationally bound system. In a disk, angular-momentum transport permits the inward drift while stresses carry angular momentum outward.

= Magnetically channelled accretion
{parent=Accretion flow}

In magnetically channelled accretion, a strong <magnetic field> guides an <accretion flow> along <flux tubes>. The component of the <Lorentz force density> parallel to the <magnetic field> vanishes, so the longitudinal flow can be treated hydrodynamically while the field controls the transverse geometry. A <magnetic dipole field> near a <neutron star> channels matter toward small polar caps.

= Accretion column
{parent=Magnetically channelled accretion}

An <accretion column> carries an inward <accretion flow> onto a localized region of a star. In the strong-field slender approximation, the <magnetic field> controls the <flux-tube area> while longitudinal pressure and gravity determine acceleration. The signed outward-coordinate velocity is negative; the inward speed and <mass accretion rate> are usually defined positive.

= Magnetic spin equilibrium
{parent=Magnetically channelled accretion}
{title2=$\Omega_*\sim\Omega_K(r_m)$}

The spin at which the net material and <magnetic torque> on an accreting star vanishes. The <corotation radius> is typically comparable to the <magnetospheric truncation radius>, but the precise torque balance depends on the coupling and any outflow.

A rapidly spinning magnetosphere adds <angular momentum> to inflowing matter outside the <corotation radius>, inhibiting accretion and potentially ejecting gas. The centrifugal onset condition does not by itself ensure that every parcel escapes to infinity.

= Magnetospheric truncation radius

{title2=$r_m$}

The inner radius at which a stellar <magnetic dipole field> disrupts a surrounding <accretion disk>. In a <free fall> <pressure>-balance estimate with fixed aspect ratio $\epsilon=H/r$, $r_m\sim(\epsilon^2\mu^4/(GM\dot M^2))^{1/7}$. Geometry and inflow structure change its numerical coefficient.

= Transonic accretion in a dipolar flux tube
{parent=Magnetically channelled accretion}

The <dipolar flux-tube area> has $A(r)=A_\star(r/R_\star)^3$. Applying <transonic accretion in a power-law tube> with $n=3$ and a <polytropic equation of state> gives
$$
c_s^2=\frac{2c_0^2}{7-5\gamma},
\qquad
r_s=\frac{GM(7-5\gamma)}{6c_0^2},
\qquad
\rho_s=\rho_0\left(\frac2{7-5\gamma}\right)^{1/(\gamma-1)}.
$$
Here $c_0$ and $\rho_0$ are the reservoir <sound speed> and <mass density>, and $c_s$ is the <sound speed> at the <sonic point>. The <mass accretion rate> is
$$
\dot M=A_\star\left(\frac{r_s}{R_\star}\right)^3\rho_s c_s.
$$
For two equal polar caps of surface angular radius $\theta_\star\ll1$, $A_\star\simeq2\pi R_\star^2\theta_\star^2$. One loaded cap gives half the total rate. The finite-radius <transonic branch> exists for $1<\gamma<7/5$, must cross outside the star ($r_s>R_\star$), and requires $\theta_\star^2r_s/R_\star\ll1$ for the sonic region to remain a narrow <flux tube>.

= Isothermal dipolar accretion equation
{parent=Transonic accretion in a dipolar flux tube}
{title2=$(v-c_s^2/v)v\prime=3c_s^2/z-GM/z^2$}

A narrow <dipolar flux-tube area> $A(z)\propto z^3$ gives $A\rho v=\dot M$ for inward speed $v>0$. The <isothermal magnetic Bernoulli integral> in $\Phi=-GM/z$ then gives $(v-c_s^2/v)v\prime=3c_s^2/z-GM/z^2$. A smooth <transonic branch> crosses at $z_s=GM/(3c_s^2)$ with $v_s=c_s$, $\rho_s=e^{5/2}\rho_\infty$ and $\dot M=\rho_sc_sA(z_s)$. The crossing must lie in the domain; subsonic branches need not attain it.

= Critical adiabatic index for dipolar accretion
{title2=$\gamma_{\rm crit}=7/5$}
{parent=Transonic accretion in a dipolar flux tube}

For <transonic accretion in a dipolar flux tube>, the <Bernoulli equation> at the <sonic point> contains $c_s^2[1/(\gamma-1)-5/2]$. A positive reservoir energy requires $\gamma<7/5$. Compression in the $r^3$ <dipolar flux-tube area> raises the <specific enthalpy> more rapidly than in the $r^2$ geometry of spherical <Bondi accretion>, whose corresponding upper index is $5/3$.

The accretion rate is the mass crossing an inward-oriented cylindrical surface per unit time. A common convention takes $\dot M>0$ for inward flow, so the outward radial mass flux is $-\dot M$.

= Hyperaccreting black-hole disk
{parent=Accretion disk}

A hyperaccreting black-hole disk has an accretion rate so large that photon cooling can be trapped and neutrino emission can dominate the cooling of its hot dense inner region. Such disks are models for the central engines of some gamma-ray bursts.

= Viscous evolution of an accretion disk
{parent=Accretion disk}

Viscous disk evolution combines mass conservation with radial transport of angular momentum by stress. In a steady Keplerian disk, the sum of advective and stress-carried angular-momentum fluxes is independent of radius.

= Compact self-similar disk with density-linear viscosity
{parent=Viscous evolution of an accretion disk}
{title2=$\bar\nu=Ar^2\Sigma,\quad\Sigma=\sigma[(R/r)^a-1]_+$}

The <Keplerian viscous diffusion equation> with the displayed <kinematic viscosity> has two such nontrivial finite-support <self-similar solutions>: $a=1$, $R=Ct^{2/5}$, and $a=5/4$, $R=Ct^{1/2}$. Both have $\sigma=(15At)^{-1}$ for $A,C,t>0$. Matching powers of radius eliminates the third power only when $(5/2-2a)(2-2a)=0$. The first solution conserves disk <angular momentum>; the second conserves disk <mass> but requires an inner torque.

= Nonaccreting similarity disk supplied by an inner torque
{parent=Compact self-similar disk with density-linear viscosity}
{title2=$M=\mathrm{const},\quad J\propto t^{1/4},\quad\mathcal G(0)\propto t^{-3/4}$}

The $a=5/4$ <compact self-similar disk with density-linear viscosity> has outward drift $r/(2t)$ but zero limiting mass flux at the origin. Its <viscous torque in an accretion disk> has a positive finite inner limit for $t>0$, equal to $dJ/dt$. Thus disk mass conservation coexists with growth of <angular momentum>; an external central torque supplies that growth.

= Accretion trajectories in a compact viscous similarity disk

For the $a=1$ <compact self-similar disk with density-linear viscosity>, radial drift is $\dot r=r/(2t)-R/(10t)$. An integrating factor gives the displayed trajectories. From $0<r_i<R(t_i)$, the arrival time at the origin is $t_i[1-r_i/R(t_i)]^{-10}$, finite for every interior point. The outer-edge trajectory has zero <surface density of a disk> and carries no mass.

= Wind-loaded Keplerian disk evolution
{parent=Viscous evolution of an accretion disk}
{title2=$\Sigma_t=\frac3r\partial_r[\sqrt r\,\partial_r(\sqrt r\bar\nu\Sigma)]+\frac1r\partial_r[2(q-1)r^2S]-S$}

Subtract local <specific angular momentum> times <mass conservation> from <conservation of angular momentum> including wind losses S,T. With outward radial mass flux $\mathcal F$ and outward <viscous torque in an accretion disk> $\mathcal G=-2\pi\bar\nu\Sigma r^3\Omega'$, this gives $\mathcal Fh'+\mathcal G'=2\pi r(Sh-T)$. For <Keplerian rotation> and $T=qSh$, substitution in mass conservation yields the displayed equation. A wind with q=1 removes local angular momentum but supplies no additional drift term; q>1 can drive accretion even with zero viscosity.

= Inward transport by an angular-momentum-enhanced disk wind
{parent=Wind-loaded Keplerian disk evolution}
{title2=$x'=\int\frac{dr}{\beta rf},\quad g'=fr^{2-1/\beta}\Sigma,\quad g'_t=g'_{x'}$}

For zero viscosity, $S=f(r)\Sigma$ and constant $q>1$, set $\beta=2(q-1)$. On intervals where f>0, the <wind-loaded Keplerian disk evolution> equation becomes $\Sigma_t=\beta rf\Sigma_r+[\beta(2f+rf')-f]\Sigma$. Multiplication by $fr^{2-1/\beta}$ and the displayed travel-time coordinate reduce it to constant-speed <advection>. Its profile is $g'=F(x'+t)$ and its characteristics move inward with $dr/dt=-\beta rf$. The weight accounts for mass loss as well as radial compression.

= Tracer diffusion in a power-law accretion disk
{parent=Viscous evolution of an accretion disk}
{title2=$C_t-vC_Y=DC_{YY},\quad Y=\ln R,\ v=3\nu_0/2,\ D=\zeta\nu_0$}

In a steady <Keplerian accretion disk> with $\Sigma=\Sigma_0R^{-2}$, $\nu=\nu_0R^2$, and tracer diffusivity $k=\zeta\nu$, the trace <passive scalar> concentration obeys the displayed constant-coefficient <advection-diffusion equation> in logarithmic radius. Contaminant mass is proportional to $C\,dY$, because $2\pi R\sigma\,dR=2\pi\Sigma_0 C\,dY$. A radial impulse $C_0\delta(R-R_0)$ therefore has logarithmic amplitude $C_0/R_0$ and evolves by the <Gaussian heat kernel> centered at $\ln R_0-vt$. The full-line solution idealizes radii far from the disk boundaries.

= Compact self-similar disk with square-root density viscosity
{parent=Viscous evolution of an accretion disk}
{title2=$S=\tau^{-1}(1-x/(6\sqrt\tau))_+^2$}

For $\nu=\nu_0(\Sigma/\Sigma_0)^{1/2}(R/R_0)^{9/4}$, the variables $x=\sqrt{R/R_0}$, $S=(\Sigma/\Sigma_0)x^3$ and $\tau=3\nu_0t/(4R_0^2)$ reduce the source-free <Keplerian viscous diffusion equation> to $S_\tau=(xS^{3/2})_{xx}$. The displayed <self-similar solution> has an outer edge $R_e=36R_0\tau$ and a surface-density cusp proportional to $R^{-3/2}$ at the origin. The positive-part cutoff is essential: beyond the edge, $S^{3/2}$ is the cube of the absolute value, so continuing the squared polynomial without truncation does not solve the same equation.

The disk mass is $8\pi\Sigma_0R_0^2\tau^{-1/2}$ and its <angular momentum> is $12\pi\Sigma_0R_0^2\sqrt{GMR_0}$, independent of time. It therefore describes accretion with outward spreading and conserved disk angular momentum, under the formal zero-torque condition at the origin. The inward mass flux changes sign at $R=9R_0\tau/4$, so the outer portion moves outward while the inner portion accretes.

= Viscous stability of an accretion disk
{parent=Viscous evolution of an accretion disk}
{title2=$\partial_\Sigma(\bar\nu\Sigma)>0$}

Within an instantaneous vertically averaged transport closure, the local <Keplerian viscous diffusion equation> has effective diffusion coefficient $3\partial_\Sigma(\bar\nu\Sigma)$. A positive coefficient smooths short radial <Fourier modes> in the allowed thin-disc wavelength range; a negative coefficient gives the <negative-diffusion criterion for viscous disk instability>. A zero coefficient is linearly degenerate and does not establish strict decay. This local response criterion alone does not settle thermal, self-gravitating or boundary-driven stability.

= Vertically averaged viscous disk equations
{parent=Viscous evolution of an accretion disk}
{title2=$\partial_t\Sigma+r^{-1}\partial_r(r\Sigma v_r)=0$}

In an axisymmetric <thin disc> with no vertical mass or <torque> flux, integrate mass and <conservation of angular momentum> over height. With fixed height-independent $l=r^2\Omega$, the equations are $\partial_t\Sigma+r^{-1}\partial_r(r\Sigma v_r)=0$ and $\partial_t(\Sigma l)+r^{-1}\partial_r(r\Sigma v_rl-r^3\bar\nu\Sigma\Omega')=0$. Their difference gives $r\Sigma v_rl'=(r^3\bar\nu\Sigma\Omega')'$. The <density-weighted viscosity of a disk> supplies the integrated stress.

= General steady viscous flux in a Keplerian disk

= Two-boundary mass split of a ring-fed disk

{title2=$\dot M_{\rm in}/\dot M=(\sqrt{R_{\rm out}}-\sqrt{R_0})/(\sqrt{R_{\rm out}}-\sqrt{R_{\rm in}})$}

For steady injection into a <Keplerian disk> between two zero-<torque> boundaries, <conservation of mass> gives $\dot M=\dot M_{\rm in}+\dot M_{\rm out}$. <Conservation of angular momentum> gives $\dot M h_0=\dot M_{\rm in}h_{\rm in}+\dot M_{\rm out}h_{\rm out}$, where $h(R)=\sqrt{GMR}$. = Angular-momentum integrability of a Keplerian disk
{parent=Viscous evolution of an accretion disk}

A <Keplerian disk> with <surface density> $\Sigma\sim r^{-p}$ at infinity has finite outer <mass> for $p>2$ and finite outer <angular momentum> for $p>5/2$, since the relevant integrals are $\int\Sigma r\,dr$ and $\int\Sigma r^{3/2}dr$. = Inverse-radius viscous disk similarity
{parent=Viscous evolution of an accretion disk}
{title2=$g=C e^{-1/(\tau\sqrt x)}$}

For a <Keplerian accretion disk> with $\bar\nu\propto r^{5/2}$, dimensionless $g=\bar\nu\Sigma/(\bar\nu_0\Sigma_0)$ satisfies $g_\tau=4x^{5/2}g_{xx}+6x^{3/2}g_x$. Its nonzero profile has logarithmically infinite disk <angular momentum> on an infinite radial domain.

= Steady viscous disk supplied by a radial source
{parent=Viscous evolution of an accretion disk}
{title2=$\mathcal F(r)=2\pi\int_r^{r_2}r'S(r')\,dr'$}

For a <Keplerian disk> with evolution $\partial_t\Sigma=(2\pi r)^{-1}\partial_r\mathcal F+S$ and $\mathcal F=6\pi r^{1/2}\partial_r(r^{1/2}\bar\nu\Sigma)$, take zero viscous <torque> at $r_1>0$ and zero flux outside the source region at $r_2>r_1$. The <steady state> gives $\mathcal F'=-2\pi rS$, and its outer condition gives the displayed flux. Integrating once more from the <zero-torque inner boundary condition> yields
$$
\bar\nu(r)\Sigma(r)=\frac{1}{3\sqrt r}\int_{r_1}^{r}\frac{du}{\sqrt u}\int_u^{r_2}vS(v)\,dv.
$$
The equation determines the viscosity-density product, so a thermodynamic viscosity closure can be imposed afterward. For nonnegative integrable $S$, it is nonnegative and the inner <accretion rate> is exactly the total supplied mass per unit time.

= Outer-edge source of a viscous disk
{parent=Steady viscous disk supplied by a radial source}
{title2=$\bar\nu\Sigma=\frac{\dot M}{3\pi}(1-\sqrt{r_1/r})$}

A narrow <surface density of a disk> source approaching the outer edge from inside, with $S=S_0\delta(r-r_2)$ normalized to have full weight, gives $\dot M=2\pi r_2S_0$ and the displayed steady interior profile. This follows by replacing the inner source integral by $r_2S_0$ and integrating $u^{-1/2}$. The flux jumps from $\dot M$ just inside to zero outside the injecting edge. Thus a zero exterior flux is consistent, whereas a continuous zero interior boundary trace is not. A half-weight convention for a symmetric endpoint <Dirac delta function> represents a different total injected mass; it must not be silently mixed with the full-weight boundary source.

= Steady accretion disk with arbitrary rotation law
{parent=Viscous evolution of an accretion disk}

For inward <accretion rate> $\dot M>0$, the steady <conservation of angular momentum> equation is $\mathcal G=\dot M(h-h_{\rm in})$ when the inner torque vanishes. The <viscous torque in an accretion disk> is $\mathcal G=2\pi qh\bar\nu\Sigma$, where $h=r^2\Omega$ and $q$ is the <orbital shear parameter>. Thus
$$
\bar\nu\Sigma=\frac{\dot M}{2\pi q}\left(1-\frac{h_{\rm in}}h\right).
$$
The familiar <Keplerian accretion disk> follows when $q=3/2$ and $h\propto r^{1/2}$. The general form also applies to the non-Keplerian <Paczyński-Wiita circular orbit> rotation law.

= Viscous timescale
{title2=$t_\nu$}
{parent=Viscous evolution of an accretion disk}

The viscous timescale is the radial diffusion time $t_\nu\sim R^2/\nu$ associated with a kinematic viscosity $\nu$ on length scale $R$.

= Viscous torque in an accretion disk
{title2=$\mathcal G$}
{parent=Viscous evolution of an accretion disk}

For vertically integrated kinematic viscosity $\bar\nu$, the viscous torque across a cylinder of radius $r$ is
$$
\mathcal G=-2\pi\bar\nu\Sigma r^3\frac{d\Omega}{dr}.
$$

= Torque transport of energy in a steady accretion disk
{parent=Viscous torque in an accretion disk}
{title2=$D_{a,b}=B_{a,b}+\Omega(a)\mathcal G(a)-\Omega(b)\mathcal G(b)$}

For a steady <Keplerian accretion disk> with inward <accretion rate> $\dot M$ and vanishing inner <torque>, the outward angular-momentum torque is $\mathcal G=\dot M(\sqrt{GMr}-\sqrt{GMr_{\rm in}})$. It transports <energy> outward at rate $\Omega\mathcal G$. The binding-energy acquisition rate between radii $b$ and $a$ is $B_{a,b}=GM\dot M(1/a-1/b)/2$. Integrating the vertical <viscous dissipation> and then the annulus gives
$$
D_{a,b}=GM\dot M\left[\frac32\left(\frac1a-\frac1b\right)-\sqrt{r_{\rm in}}(a^{-3/2}-b^{-3/2})\right].
$$
Since $\Omega\mathcal G=GM\dot M[r^{-1}-\sqrt{r_{\rm in}}r^{-3/2}]$, subtraction proves the displayed energy balance. Far from the inner edge $D_{a,b}\simeq3B_{a,b}$, whereas the boundary torque powers vanish for the complete disk and $D=B=GM\dot M/(2r_{\rm in})$. Local dissipation is therefore not supplied solely by local changes in orbital binding energy.

= Nonaccreting constant-torque disk
{parent=Viscous torque in an accretion disk}
{title2=$\bar\nu\Sigma=B r^{-1/2},\quad\mathcal G=3\pi\sqrt{GM_*}B$}

A steady <Keplerian disk> can transport <angular momentum> by viscous stress with zero mass flux. The radial drift vanishes, but the <torque> is constant and nonzero. An inner source and outer sink must supply its boundary angular-momentum exchange. This is distinct from a decretion flow, which transports mass outward.

= Outer angular-momentum flux of a viscous disk
{parent=Viscous torque in an accretion disk}

Integrating the <Keplerian accretion disk> diffusion equation gives $dJ/dt=6\pi\sqrt{GM_\star}[r^{3/2}\partial_r(\bar\nu\Sigma)]_{\rm inner}^{\rm outer}$ when the angular-momentum integral and boundary limits are legitimate. An inner <zero-torque inner boundary condition> alone does not eliminate the outer <angular-momentum flux>. For divergent total $J$, use finite-radius cutoffs before making a conservation claim.

= Zero-torque inner boundary condition
{parent=Viscous torque in an accretion disk}

The zero-torque inner boundary condition sets the viscous torque to zero where nearly circular disk flow joins a plunging or stress-free region.

= Formal viscous inspiral time in Schwarzschild spacetime
{parent=Zero-torque inner boundary condition}
{title2=$\Delta t=(m^2/\nu)\mathcal T(r_1/(3m))$}

For constant <kinematic viscosity> $\nu$, use the phenomenological <viscous torque in an accretion disk>, <specific angular momentum> $h=rc\sqrt{m/(r-3m)}$, and $h_0=\sqrt{12}mc$ at the <innermost stable circular orbit>. Neglect radial kinetic motion in the clock relation and neglect differential light travel time. The <Schwarzschild time> to drift formally from $r_1\geq6m$ to $6m$ is the displayed expression, with
$$
\mathcal T(X)=3X^2+6X+6\log(X-1)-8(X-1)^{3/2}-24\sqrt{X-1}+8.
$$
Indeed $\mathcal T(2)=0$ and $\mathcal T'(X)=6X[X-2\sqrt{X-1}]/(X-1)\geq0$ for $X\geq2$. The <zero-torque inner boundary condition> makes the formal drift speed diverge as $r\downarrow6m$, so the nearly circular approximation must give way to the <plunging region of a black-hole accretion disk> sufficiently close to that orbit. This formula describes the stipulated approximation, not an exact relativistic flow solution.

= Plunging region of a black-hole accretion disk
{parent=Zero-torque inner boundary condition}

The plunging region begins near the <innermost stable circular orbit>, where nearly circular motion becomes unstable and radial inflow becomes rapid. If the inflow time is shorter than the stress-transport time, the gas approximately conserves its <specific angular momentum> there.

= Weak viscous torque in a thin-disk plunging region
{parent=Plunging region of a black-hole accretion disk}
{title2=$\mathcal G_{\rm plunge}/\mathcal G_{\rm circular}=O[\alpha(H/r)^2]$}

A <thin disk> with <alpha disk> viscosity $\nu\sim\alpha H^2\Omega$ has a <viscous timescale> much longer than its dynamical infall time in the <plunging region of a black-hole accretion disk>. At fixed nonzero <accretion rate>, rapid inflow reduces its <surface density of a disk>. The resulting <viscous torque in an accretion disk> is smaller by the displayed factor than in the slowly drifting circular disk. This estimate concerns radii outside the narrow matching layers.

= Steady Keplerian accretion disk with an inner torque

For a positive inner <magnetic torque> $T_m$, define $\lambda=T_m/(\dot M h_{\rm in})$. Integrating steady <conservation of angular momentum> gives $\mathcal G=T_m+\dot M(h-h_{\rm in})$. With <Keplerian rotation>, this determines the <surface density of a disk> in the displayed formula.

= Torque-dominated accretion disk

When the applied inner torque dominates the advective <angular-momentum flux>, the constant-<kinematic viscosity> inner profile resembles a <decretion disk>. This resemblance does not alone imply outward mass transport.

= Viscous instability of an accretion disk
{parent=Viscous evolution of an accretion disk}

The <negative-diffusion criterion for viscous disk instability> is $\partial_\Sigma(\bar\nu\Sigma)<0$. A <surface density> enhancement then transports angular momentum less effectively and grows. Freezing the <Keplerian viscous diffusion equation> locally gives growth proportional to the square of radial <wavenumber>, within the thin-disc transport range; wavelengths must still be sufficiently long compared with the <disk scale height> for that closure. The formal short-wave limit is a <backward heat equation>.

= Negative-diffusion criterion for viscous disk instability
{parent=Viscous instability of an accretion disk}
{title2=$\partial_\Sigma(\bar\nu\Sigma)<0$}

Freezing coefficients locally in the <Keplerian viscous diffusion equation> gives $\Sigma_{1,t}\simeq3\partial_\Sigma(\bar\nu\Sigma)\Sigma_{1,rr}$. A radial <Fourier mode> grows at rate $-3\partial_\Sigma(\bar\nu\Sigma)k_r^2$. Negative response reverses ordinary smoothing and gives a <backward heat equation>, within the <wavelength> range where the <thin disc> transport closure applies.

= Viscous transport response exponent
{parent=Viscous instability of an accretion disk}
{title2=$q=\partial\log(\bar\nu\Sigma)/\partial\log\Sigma$}

The response to a small <surface density> perturbation is $\delta(\bar\nu\Sigma)=q\bar\nu\delta\Sigma$. For an instantaneous law $\bar\nu\propto r^a\Sigma^b$, $q=b+1$. This exponent is not the orbital shear exponent or a satellite-to-star mass ratio, which also often use the letter $q$.

= Radiation-pressure viscous instability
{parent=Viscous instability of an accretion disk}

In a characteristic vertical model of a radiation-pressure-dominated <alpha disk> with constant <opacity>, $\mu\sim\alpha p/\Omega\sim c/\kappa$ and $p\sim\Sigma\Omega^2H$ imply $H\propto\Sigma^{-1}$ and $\bar\nu\Sigma\propto\Sigma^{-1}$. The resulting $d(\bar\nu\Sigma)/d\Sigma<0$ gives a <viscous instability of an accretion disk>, distinct from a <thermal instability of a radiation-pressure-dominated alpha disk>.

= Viscous spreading of a narrow planetary ring
{parent=Viscous instability of an accretion disk}

In a local Keplerian sheet, slow viscous drift gives $\partial_t\Sigma=3\partial_x^2(\nu\Sigma)$. For $\nu=A\Sigma^2$, the compact profile $\Sigma=\sigma\sqrt{1-x^2/w^2}$ preserves its shape, conserves $\sigma w$, and spreads with $w\propto t^{1/4}$ at late times.

= Alpha disk
{title2=$\alpha$-disk}
{parent=Accretion disk}
{wiki}

The alpha-disk prescription writes turbulent kinematic viscosity as $\nu=\alpha c_sH\simeq\alpha c_s^2/\Omega$, with dimensionless $\alpha\lesssim1$. It reflects eddies no larger than the disk thickness and no faster than the sound speed.

= Gas-pressure viscosity closure with mixed pressure support
{parent=Alpha disk}
{title2=$\bar\nu=\frac{3I_t}{4}(\alpha^4\kappa\mathcal R^4/\sigma)^{1/3}\Sigma^{2/3}\Omega^{-2/3}$}

Let $\mathcal R=k/(\mu_m m_H)$, take constant <opacity> $\kappa$, and prescribe the <dynamic viscosity> $\mu=\alpha p_g/\Omega=\alpha\mathcal R\rho T/\Omega$ in a <Keplerian disk>. The pressure supporting the disk may contain both <gas pressure> and <radiation pressure>. Combining <viscous dissipation> and <radiative diffusion> gives $\partial_m^2T^4+A\Omega T=0$ in the <vertical mass coordinate of a disk>, where $A=27\alpha\kappa\mathcal R/(16\sigma)$. Set $\zeta=2m/\Sigma$, $T=T_*t(\zeta)$ and $T_*^3=A\Omega\Sigma^2/4$. With a <radiative-zero disk surface>, $t$ satisfies $(t^4)''+t=0$, $t'(0)=t(1)=0$. Writing $I_t=\int_0^1t\,d\zeta$, the <density-weighted mean kinematic viscosity> is $\bar\nu=\alpha\mathcal R T_*I_t/\Omega$, giving the displayed formula. This calculation does not assume gas-pressure domination: the closure depends on gas pressure while hydrostatic support can be mixed.

= Homologous vertical structure of a constant-opacity alpha disk
{parent=Alpha disk}

Let $\mathcal R=k/(\mu_m m_p)$ and choose $h^6=3\alpha\kappa\Sigma^2\mathcal R^4/(16\sigma\Omega^5)$. Set $z=h\zeta$, $\rho=(\Sigma/h)D$, $p=\Sigma\Omega^2hP$, $T=(\Omega^2h^2/\mathcal R)\Theta$ and $F=\alpha\Sigma\Omega^3h^2Q$. Vertical <hydrostatic equilibrium>, <viscous dissipation>, <radiative diffusion> and the <ideal gas> law reduce to $P'=-D\zeta$, $Q'=9P/4$, $\Theta'=-DQ/\Theta^3$, $P=D\Theta$. Midplane <symmetry> gives $Q(0)=0$; a regular radiative-zero surface has $P=\Theta=0$ at a universal $\zeta_s$, with $2\int_0^{\zeta_s}D\,d\zeta=1$. All physical parameters have disappeared. Uniqueness of this normalized boundary-value problem makes every physical profile a rescaling of one dimensionless solution.

= Radiative-zero disk surface
{parent=Homologous vertical structure of a constant-opacity alpha disk}

For a constant-opacity, gas-pressure-supported radiative disk surface at $z=H$, imposing $p=T=0$ does not impose $F=0$. If the outgoing surface <flux> is finite and positive, dividing hydrostatic balance by the <temperature> gradient gives $dp/dT\sim16\sigma\Omega^2HT^3/(3\kappa F_s)$. Consequently $p\propto T^4$, $\rho\propto T^3$, and the diffusion equation gives $T\propto H-z$. Thus the <mass density> also vanishes regularly while the surface radiates a finite <flux>. This ideal boundary replaces the finite-temperature photospheric transition; its accuracy is not controlled solely by the disk aspect ratio.

= Gas-pressure alpha disk with inverse-density opacity
{parent=Alpha disk}
{title2=$\kappa\propto\rho^{-3/2}T^{5/4}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\nu\propto\Sigma^{1/2}R^{9/4}$}

For an optically thick <alpha disk> dominated by <gas pressure>, vertical <hydrostatic equilibrium> gives $H\propto T^{1/2}/\Omega$ and $\rho\propto\Sigma\Omega T^{-1/2}$. The specified <opacity> therefore scales as $\kappa\propto\Sigma^{-3/2}\Omega^{-3/2}T^2$. Viscous heating scales as $Q^+\propto\alpha\Sigma T\Omega$, while <radiative diffusion> gives $Q^-\propto T^4/(\kappa\Sigma)\propto\Sigma^{1/2}\Omega^{3/2}T^2$. Their balance gives $T\propto\alpha\Sigma^{1/2}\Omega^{-1/2}$, hence $\nu\propto\alpha^2\Sigma^{1/2}\Omega^{-3/2}$. Fixed $\alpha$ and <Keplerian rotation> yield the displayed law.

The local <thermal stability of an accretion disk> follows because, at fixed surface density and radius, cooling has logarithmic temperature slope two while heating has slope one. The <viscous stability of an accretion disk> follows from $\partial_\Sigma(\nu\Sigma)>0$ on the thermal-equilibrium branch. Both conclusions apply within this pressure, opacity and transport closure.

= Vertically averaged alpha closure for a mixed-pressure layer
{parent=Alpha disk}
{title2=$\bar\nu=\alpha\Omega H^2/9$}

For the <constant gas-radiation ratio vertical disc structure>, the integrated alpha prescription $\bar\nu\Sigma=\int\mu\,dz=(\alpha/\Omega)\int p\,dz$ gives the displayed mean <kinematic viscosity> and $\lambda/(1+\lambda)=\alpha H\Omega\kappa\Sigma/(8c)$. Uniform <dynamic viscosity> and nonuniform <pressure> are compatible because this prescription is an integrated equality. Imposing the corresponding pointwise equality would not preserve the exact vertical solution.

= Gas-pressure branch of a mixed-pressure alpha disk
{parent=Vertically averaged alpha closure for a mixed-pressure layer}
{title2=$\bar\nu\propto r\Sigma^{2/3}$}

For small positive radiation-to-gas ratio, constant <opacity>, fixed alpha and molecular constants, eliminate $\lambda$ between $\lambda\propto H\Omega\Sigma$ and $\lambda\Sigma\propto H^7\Omega^6$. This gives $H^6\propto\Sigma^2\Omega^{-5}$ and $\bar\nu\propto\Sigma^{2/3}\Omega^{-2/3}$. <Keplerian rotation> supplies the displayed radial power. Since $\bar\nu\Sigma\propto\Sigma^{5/3}$, its derivative with respect to <surface density> is positive and local viscous perturbations diffuse rather than amplify.

= Pointwise alpha-prescription obstruction in a radiation-supported layer
{parent=Alpha disk}

In the exact constant-<opacity>, pure-<radiation pressure> diffusion model, <dynamic viscosity> is height-independent. Imposing an additional exact pointwise relation $\mu\propto p(z)$ forces constant pressure, incompatible with nonzero vertical <hydrostatic equilibrium> away from the midplane. The characteristic-pressure <alpha disk> prescription can still be used for order-of-magnitude scalings; it is not an exact simultaneous vertical solution.

The Shakura--Sunyaev thin disk is a geometrically thin, optically thick, radiatively efficient accretion-disk model whose turbulent stress is parametrized by the alpha prescription.

= Multitemperature blackbody disk
{parent=Shakura--Sunyaev thin disk}

A multitemperature blackbody disk is an optically thick disk approximated as concentric blackbody annuli of different effective temperatures. If $T\propto R^{-3/4}$, its intermediate-frequency spectrum satisfies $S_\nu\propto\nu^{1/3}$.

= One-third spectrum of a multitemperature disk
{parent=Multitemperature blackbody disk}
{title2=$F_\nu\propto\nu^{1/3}$}

For a <blackbody> disk with $T\propto r^{-3/4}$, the substitution $x=h\nu/(k_BT)$ in $F_\nu\propto\nu^3\int r(e^x-1)^{-1}dr$ produces $F_\nu\propto\nu^{1/3}\int x^{5/3}(e^x-1)^{-1}dx$. When the inner and outer temperatures make the limits approximately zero and infinity, that convergent integral is independent of <frequency>. The law applies between the outer-edge turnover and the hot inner-disk cutoff.

= Rayleigh-Jeans spectrum of a finite blackbody disk
{parent=Multitemperature blackbody disk}
{c}
{title2=$F_\nu\propto\nu^2$}

For a finite <multitemperature blackbody disk>, the low-<frequency> <Rayleigh-Jeans law> gives $F_\nu\propto\nu^2\int rT(r)\,dr$. If $T\propto r^{-3/4}$ away from the inner edge, outer annuli dominate because the weight per logarithmic radial interval grows as $r^{5/4}$. A measure-zero zero-temperature inner edge does not invalidate the leading law when $rT(r)$ is integrable.

= Pressure-opacity zones of a Shakura--Sunyaev thin disk
{c}
{parent=Shakura--Sunyaev thin disk}

The standard thin-disk solution separates approximately into an inner radiation-pressure and electron-scattering zone, a middle gas-pressure and electron-scattering zone, and an outer gas-pressure and free-free-opacity zone.

= Radiation-supported inner-disk height
{title2=$H(R)$}
{parent=Pressure-opacity zones of a Shakura--Sunyaev thin disk}

The inner <Shakura--Sunyaev thin disk> is supported mainly by <radiation pressure> and has nearly constant <electron-scattering opacity>. Equating <radiative diffusion> with local dissipation and imposing vertical <hydrostatic equilibrium> gives the characteristic half-thickness
$$
H(R)\simeq\frac{3\kappa_{\rm es}\dot M}{8\pi c}\left(1-\sqrt{R_{\rm in}/R}\right).
$$
The radiation-supported height approaches a plateau away from the inner boundary. The formal zero at that boundary is outside the reliable radiation-dominated approximation. At an <Eddington accretion rate> defined using efficiency $0.1$, this height can make the inner flow only marginally thin.

= Subsonic radial drift in a thin disk
{parent=Shakura--Sunyaev thin disk}

In a steady thin alpha disk, $|u_R|/c_s$ is of order $\alpha H/R\ll1$, whereas $u_\phi/c_s$ is of order $R/H\gg1$. Matter therefore completes many nearly circular orbits during its slow viscous drift inward.

= Standard thin-disk dissipation flux
{parent=Shakura--Sunyaev thin disk}

For a steady Keplerian disk with a zero-torque inner edge $R_{\rm in}$, the flux summed over both faces is
$$
F_{\rm diss}(R)=\frac{3GM\dot M}{4\pi R^3}
\left[1-\left(\frac{R_{\rm in}}R\right)^{1/2}\right].

= Effective-temperature profile of a zero-torque disk
{parent=Standard thin-disk dissipation flux}
{title2=$T_{\rm eff}(r)$}

A <Keplerian accretion disk> with a <zero-torque inner boundary condition> and local <blackbody> cooling has $T_{\rm eff}^4=3GM\dot M(1-\sqrt{r_{\rm in}/r})/(8\pi\sigma r^3)$. The <Stefan–Boltzmann law> uses the flux through one face; the <standard thin-disk dissipation flux> includes both faces. The scale $T_{\rm in}=[3GM\dot M/(8\pi\sigma r_{\rm in}^3)]^{1/4}$ is a normalization, not the actual temperature at the stress-free inner edge.

= Maximum effective temperature of a zero-torque disk

For the <effective-temperature profile of a zero-torque disk>, differentiation of $y^{-3}(1-y^{-1/2})$ with $y=r/r_{\rm in}$ gives $y_{\max}=49/36$. L_{\rm thin}=\frac{GM\dot M}{2R_{\rm in}}

= Magnetorotational instability
{title2=MRI}
{parent=Alpha disk}
{wiki}

= MRI
{c}
{synonym}

The magnetorotational instability destabilizes a magnetized differentially rotating flow when angular velocity decreases outward. Magnetic tension couples neighboring fluid elements, allowing the inner one to lose angular momentum and fall inward while the outer one gains angular momentum and moves outward.

= Equal-diffusivity magnetorotational channel
{parent=Magnetorotational instability}
{title2=$\omega_A^2=A(2\Omega-A),\quad\gamma=A-\nu k_z^2$}

For $0<A<2\Omega$ and equal <kinematic viscosity> and <magnetic diffusivity>, a vertical wavevector admits $\mathbf v=(V,V,0)$ and $\mathbf b=(i\omega_AV/A,-i\omega_AV/A,0)$ in Alfvén-speed units. Substitution into the <magnetorotational instability> equations gives the displayed frequency and growth rate. In a <Keplerian disk>, growth of this polarization requires $B_z^2/(\mu_0\rho)>5\nu\Omega/4$. This is a bound for this selected channel and its wavelength, not a universal threshold for every MRI mode in an unbounded domain.

= Stress-maximizing magnetorotational channel polarization
{parent=Equal-diffusivity magnetorotational channel}
{title2=$\langle T_{xy}\rangle=\frac\rho2(|V|^2+|B|^2)>0$}

For complex velocity amplitudes $v_x=v_y=V$ and magnetic amplitudes $b_x=-b_y=B$ in Alfvén-speed units, phase averaging gives the displayed stress. For fixed horizontal velocity energy, equal radial and azimuthal components maximize their positive <Reynolds stress>. Opposite equal magnetic components maximize the outward tension contribution of the <Maxwell stress tensor>, since $-B_xB_y$ is then positive. These conclusions follow from $2|ab|\leq |a|^2+|b|^2$. The vertical channel transports angular momentum outward and extracts energy from decreasing angular velocity.

= Spring model of magnetorotational instability
{parent=Magnetorotational instability}

For an incompressible vertical-field mode, frozen-in <magnetic tension> supplies a spring of frequency $\omega_A$. Its displacement satisfies $\ddot\xi_x-2\Omega\dot\xi_y-4\Omega A\xi_x=-\omega_A^2\xi_x$ and $\ddot\xi_y+2\Omega\dot\xi_x=-\omega_A^2\xi_y$. Their <determinant> gives the <ideal magnetorotational dispersion relation>. A weak spring transfers <angular momentum> from the faster inner element to the slower outer element, causing inward and outward migration that increase their separation when angular velocity decreases outward. A sufficiently strong spring instead supplies a restoring force.

= Horizontally invariant magnetized shearing-sheet equations
{parent=Magnetorotational instability}

For <ideal magnetohydrodynamics> at constant <mass density> $\rho$, a <shearing sheet> with velocity $-q\Omega x\mathbf e_y+\mathbf v_h(z,t)$ and <magnetic field> $\mathbf B_h(z,t)+B_z\mathbf e_z$ obeys
$$
\partial_tv_x-2\Omega v_y=\frac{B_z}{\mu_0\rho}\partial_zB_x,\qquad
\partial_tv_y+(2-q)\Omega v_x=\frac{B_z}{\mu_0\rho}\partial_zB_y,
$$
$$
\partial_tB_x=B_z\partial_zv_x,\qquad
\partial_tB_y+q\Omega B_x=B_z\partial_zv_y.
$$
Horizontal invariance and vanishing vertical velocity eliminate nonlinear <advection>. These equations retain <Coriolis acceleration>, orbital shear and <magnetic tension>. The vertical pressure adjusts to balance gravity and <magnetic pressure>.

= Magnetic bending in an incompressible disk
{parent=Horizontally invariant magnetized shearing-sheet equations}

In the <steady state> of the <horizontally invariant magnetized shearing-sheet equations>, let $B_x(\pm H)=\pm B_+$ and $B_y(\pm H)=0$. For $q>0$ and $B_z\ne0$, the symmetric equilibrium has
$$
B_x=B_+\frac{\sin(Kz)}{\sin(KH)},\quad B_y=v_x=0,\quad
v_y=-\frac{B_z}{2\mu_0\rho\Omega}\frac{dB_x}{dz},\qquad
K^2=\frac{2q\mu_0\rho\Omega^2}{B_z^2}.
$$
This follows by combining azimuthal induction with radial <magnetic tension> balance to obtain $B_x''+K^2B_x=0$. Nonzero $B_+$ requires $\sin(KH)\ne0$; even homogeneous additions at $\cos(KH)=0$ are excluded by imposing midplane symmetry.

= Nonmonotonic magnetic bending
{parent=Magnetic bending in an incompressible disk}

For nonzero imposed surface inclination, <magnetic bending in an incompressible disk> is nonmonotonic when $KH>\pi/2$: the first maximum of $|B_x|$ occurs at $z=\pi/(2K)<H$, followed by a decrease. This is exactly the <finite-thickness magnetorotational instability criterion> for the same slab. The zero-inclination equilibrium can still be unstable, so nonmonotonic bending is a property of the forced profile, rather than a condition on every possible unstable equilibrium.

= Ideal magnetorotational dispersion relation
{parent=Magnetorotational instability}

For a vertical <magnetic field> in a homogeneous <shearing sheet>, a horizontal <normal mode> with growth rate $s$ and vertical <Alfvén frequency> $\omega_a=kv_{Az}$ satisfies
$$
(s^2+\omega_a^2)(s^2+\omega_a^2-2q\Omega^2)+4\Omega^2s^2=0.
$$
The two roots for $s^2$ are real. For $q>0$, a growing magnetic mode exists precisely when $0<\omega_a^2<2q\Omega^2$. <Magnetic tension> is strong enough to couple radially displaced elements, yet weak enough to allow their separation to increase. https://www.damtp.cam.ac.uk/user/gio10/dad14.pdf[Ogilvie's lecture on magnetorotational instability] derives this relation and its finite-thickness boundary conditions.

= Maximum growth rate of ideal magnetorotational instability
{parent=Ideal magnetorotational dispersion relation}
{title2=$s_{\max}=q\Omega/2,\quad\omega_{A,\max}^2=q(4-q)\Omega^2/4$}

For local cylindrical shear $q=-d\ln\Omega/d\ln r$ with $0<q<2$, maximizing the positive squared-growth root of the <ideal magnetorotational dispersion relation> gives the displayed formulas. For a Keplerian disk these are $s_{\max}=3\Omega/4$ and $\omega_A^2=15\Omega^2/16$. Field strength selects the fastest wavelength, while the maximum ideal rate is set by shear. Finite domains and magnetic diffusion can exclude that wavelength.

= Oblique axisymmetric magnetorotational dispersion relation
{parent=Ideal magnetorotational dispersion relation}
{title2=$\frac{k^2}{k_z^2}(s^2+\omega_A^2)^2+\kappa^2(s^2+\omega_A^2)-4\Omega^2\omega_A^2=0$}

In the local incompressible weak-field limit, take an axisymmetric <wavevector> $(k_r,0,k_z)$ and $\omega_A=\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf B/\sqrt{\mu_0\rho}$. Eliminating the pressure projects the radial force by $k_z^2/k^2$. This yields the displayed dispersion relation, where $\kappa$ is the <radial epicyclic frequency>. For a hydrodynamically stable rotation profile with outward-decreasing angular velocity, instability requires $0<\omega_A^2<-(k_z^2/k^2)d\Omega^2/d\ln r$. A purely toroidal field has no axisymmetric local field-line-bending frequency and therefore no ordinary weak-field MRI in this approximation.

= Radially stratified magnetorotational dispersion relation
{parent=Ideal magnetorotational dispersion relation}
{title2=$\sigma^4+(2A+N_r^2+\Omega^2)\sigma^2+A(A+N_r^2-3\Omega^2)=0$}

For an <axisymmetric vertical mode of a shearing sheet> with a vertical <magnetic field>, <radial buoyancy frequency> $N_r$, and $A=k^2v_A^2>0$, the nonstationary amplitudes obey $\sigma^4+(2A+N_r^2+\Omega^2)\sigma^2+A(A+N_r^2-3\Omega^2)=0$. The two roots for $\sigma^2$ are real. A growing mode exists exactly when $A+N_r^2<3\Omega^2$. If the advected <buoyancy displacement variable> is retained as an independent amplitude, the full characteristic polynomial also has a stationary factor $\sigma$.

= Maximum growth rate of radially stratified magnetorotational instability
{parent=Radially stratified magnetorotational dispersion relation}
{title2=$\sigma_{\max}=(3\Omega^2-N_r^2)/(4\Omega)$}

For $-5\Omega^2<N_r^2<3\Omega^2$ and a continuum of vertical <wavenumbers>, the growing branch of the <radially stratified magnetorotational dispersion relation> is maximized at $k^2v_A^2=\Omega^2-(N_r^2+\Omega^2)^2/(16\Omega^2)$, with $\sigma_{\max}=(3\Omega^2-N_r^2)/(4\Omega)$. For $N_r^2\leq-5\Omega^2$ the optimum moves to the $k\to0$ boundary and the limiting rate is $\sqrt{-N_r^2-\Omega^2}$. For $N_r^2\geq3\Omega^2$ there is no exponential instability. Boundaries that discretize the allowed <wavenumbers> can alter the actual maximum.

= Finite-thickness magnetorotational instability criterion
{parent=Ideal magnetorotational dispersion relation}

If the horizontal <magnetic field> perturbations vanish at $z=\pm H$, their allowed <vertical wavenumbers> are $k_m=m\pi/(2H)$, $m=1,2,\ldots$. The lowest <wavenumber> is unstable exactly when
$$
0<\frac{\pi^2B_z^2}{8q\mu_0\rho H^2\Omega^2}<1.
$$
If that magnetic mode is stable, all higher magnetic modes are stable as well. A weak field admits longer unstable wavelengths that fit inside the slab; sufficiently strong <magnetic tension> stabilizes every allowed wavelength.

= Bounded vertical modes of a sech-squared magnetized disk
{parent=Finite-thickness magnetorotational instability criterion}
{title2=$F_n=P_n(\tanh(z/H)),\quad k_n^2H^2=n(n+1)$}

For density proportional to $\operatorname{sech}^2(z/H)$, the magnetic vertical equation $F''+k^2\operatorname{sech}^2(z/H)F=0$ becomes the <Legendre differential equation> under $\xi=\tanh(z/H)$. Bounded solutions at both surfaces are <Legendre polynomials>, with $k_n=\sqrt{n(n+1)}/H$. The $n=0$ constant function has no magnetically coupled finite wavenumber. The smallest MRI-relevant eigenvalue is $k_1^2=2/H^2$, which makes sufficiently strong <magnetic tension> stabilize all allowed modes.

= Fastest discrete mode of a stratified magnetorotational instability
{parent=Bounded vertical modes of a sech-squared magnetized disk}
{title2=$s^2/\Omega^2=[-(1+2a)+\sqrt{1+16a}]/2$}

The growing root of the <ideal magnetorotational dispersion relation> is maximized continuously at $a=v_A^2k^2/\Omega^2=15/16$, with growth $3\Omega/4$. Discrete vertical modes instead have $a_n=2n(n+1)/\beta$. The fastest one is found by comparing admissible integers on either side of the continuous maximum, rather than rounding a vertical wavenumber blindly. At $\beta=24$, $n=3$ gives $s=\Omega\sqrt{(\sqrt{17}-3)/2}$.

= Critical wavelength of the magnetorotational instability
{title2=$\lambda_{\rm crit}$}
{parent=Magnetorotational instability}

For a vertical magnetic field in a Keplerian disk, axisymmetric vertical MRI modes are unstable when $k^2v_A^2<3\Omega^2$. The shortest unstable wavelength is $\lambda_{\rm crit}=2\pi v_A/(\sqrt3\Omega)$.

= Thermal stability of an accretion disk
{parent=Alpha disk}

A local thermal equilibrium is stable when a small temperature increase raises cooling faster than heating at fixed surface density. The standard total-pressure alpha prescription makes a radiation-pressure-dominated, Thomson-opacity disk thermally unstable.

= Hydrogen-ionization disk instability
{parent=Thermal stability of an accretion disk}

Partial <hydrogen> <ionization> changes disk cooling and permits cold and hot stable thermal branches separated by unstable equilibria. A <dwarf nova> can cycle between a cold matter-storing disk and a hot, rapidly accreting disk. The cycle is described by the evolution of local <surface density> and <temperature>, coupled to <viscous evolution of an accretion disk>.

= Accretion-disk thermal S-curve
{parent=Hydrogen-ionization disk instability}
{title2=$T_{\rm eff}(\Sigma)$}

At fixed disk radius, balancing local heating and cooling gives thermal equilibria parameterized by <surface density> and <temperature>. In the <hydrogen-ionization disk instability>, this locus has cold and hot stable branches joined by unstable equilibria. Crossing the turning points drives the state changes of a <dwarf nova>. <Thermal stability of an accretion disk> tests the local heating and cooling response, while <viscous evolution of an accretion disk> changes the surface density.

= Thermal instability from negative hydrogen ion opacity
{parent=Thermal stability of an accretion disk}
{title2=$Q^+\propto T,\quad Q^-\propto T^{-35/6}$}

For a gas-supported <alpha disk> with $\kappa\propto\rho^{1/3}T^{10}$, compare rates at fixed <surface density of a disk>, radius, $\alpha$ and composition on the <thermal timescale of an accretion disk>. Assume that the <vertical dynamical timescale of a disk> is shorter than that thermal time, and that the <viscous timescale> is longer; the separation is controlled for $\alpha\ll1$ and small <disk aspect ratio>. Vertical <hydrostatic equilibrium> gives $H\propto T^{1/2}$, $\rho\propto T^{-1/2}$, so $\kappa\propto T^{59/6}$. Viscous heating satisfies $Q^+\propto T$ and <radiative cooling> satisfies $Q^-\propto T^4/(\kappa\Sigma)\propto T^{-35/6}$. At equilibrium a positive <temperature> perturbation changes the net heating by $(41/6)(Q_0/T)\delta T>0$. With positive effective <heat capacity>, it grows rather than relaxes. This is a local thermal statement and uses the fixed-column, rather than fixed-volume-density, comparison.

= Thermal timescale of an accretion disk
{title2=$t_{\rm th}$}
{parent=Thermal stability of an accretion disk}

The thermal timescale compares the internal energy stored in a disc annulus with its local heating or cooling. In a gas-pressure-supported <alpha disk>, $t_{\rm th}\sim(\alpha\Omega_K)^{-1}$, whereas the <viscous timescale> is longer by approximately $(R/H)^2$. A local thermal perturbation is therefore studied at fixed <surface density of a disk>, rather than fixed steady-state <mass accretion rate>.

= Thermal instability of a radiation-pressure-dominated alpha disk
{parent=Thermal stability of an accretion disk}

At fixed surface density, a total-pressure alpha disk supported by radiation pressure has viscous heating $Q^+\propto T_c^8$ but radiative cooling $Q^-\propto T_c^4$ when electron scattering supplies a nearly constant opacity. Heating therefore wins after a positive temperature perturbation.

= Radial epicyclic frequency
{title2=$\kappa_r$}
{parent=Astrophysical disk}
{wiki}

The radial epicyclic frequency describes small radial oscillations about a circular orbit. For specific angular momentum $h(r)$, $\kappa_r^2=r^{-3}d(h^2)/dr$.

= Rayleigh centrifugal stability criterion
{parent=Radial epicyclic frequency}
{c}
{title2=$\kappa^2=r^{-3}(l^2)\prime$}

For inviscid cylindrical rotation without a <magnetic field>, an axisymmetric radial interchange conserves each parcel's <specific angular momentum> $l=r^2\Omega$. Its restoring coefficient is $\kappa^2=r^{-3}d(l^2)/dr$. Increasing $l^2$ gives centrifugal stability; decreasing $l^2$ gives an exponentially growing interchange mode when radial displacement is allowed by the boundaries. A vanishing derivative is marginal, not a strictly restoring condition.

= Epicyclic frequency bounds for monotone spherical density
{parent=Radial epicyclic frequency}
{title2=$\Omega\leq\kappa\leq2\Omega$}

For a nonnegative, radially nonincreasing spherical <mass density>, $\Omega^2=GM(r)/r^3$ and $\kappa^2=\Omega^2+4\pi G\rho(r)$. Since $\rho(r)$ does not exceed the mean enclosed density, the <radial epicyclic frequency> lies between $\Omega$ and $2\Omega$. General axisymmetric disks need not satisfy this bound.

= Kepler-harmonic epicyclic precession
{parent=Radial epicyclic frequency}
{c}
{title2=$\Omega^2/\kappa^2=\frac{GM/r^3+\alpha\omega^2}{GM/r^3+4\alpha\omega^2}$}

For $\Phi=-GM/r+\alpha\omega^2r^2/2$, nearly circular motion has $\Omega^2=GM/r^3+\alpha\omega^2$ and $\kappa^2=GM/r^3+4\alpha\omega^2$. Their generally nonrational ratio makes small-eccentricity orbits precess even if $\alpha$ was added slowly. This illustrates <adiabatic actions do not preserve orbital closure>.

= Epicyclic motion
{parent=Radial epicyclic frequency}

Epicyclic motion is the small oscillation of a displaced fluid element or particle about a circular orbit. Its natural radial frequency is the <radial epicyclic frequency>.

= Axisymmetric incompressible epicyclic wave
{parent=Epicyclic motion}
{title2=$\omega^2=4\Omega(\Omega-A)k_z^2/k^2$}

For $k_y=0$ and $k_z\ne0$, <pressure> elimination gives $\dot v_x=2\Omega\chi v_y-\nu k^2v_x$ and $\dot v_y=-2(\Omega-A)v_x-\nu k^2v_y$, where $\chi=k_z^2/k^2$. The inviscid frequency is the <radial epicyclic frequency> times $\sqrt\chi$. With $w=v_x/\sqrt\chi$, $(\Omega-A)|w|^2+\Omega|v_y|^2$ is conserved when <viscosity> vanishes. In a Keplerian flow it bounds kinetic-energy amplification by four, whereas with zero rotation the same system has the algebraic <lift-up effect>.

= Velocity-kick epicycle
{parent=Epicyclic motion}
{title2=$x/(\gamma a)=2\cos\theta(1-\cos nt)+\sin\theta\sin nt$}

A small planar <velocity> kick on a stellar <circular orbit> produces a local <epicyclic motion> in the source's <rotating reference frame>. With outward $x$, forward $y$, and initial displacements zero, <Hill equations> give $x/(\gamma a)=2\cos\theta(1-\cos nt)+\sin\theta\sin nt$ and $y/(\gamma a)=\cos\theta(4\sin nt-3nt)+2\sin\theta(\cos nt-1)$. Tangential kicks combine an epicycle with <Keplerian shear>; a radial kick gives a closed leading-order <epicyclic ellipse>.

= Epicyclic azimuthal advance
{parent=Epicyclic motion}
{title2=$\Delta\psi=2\pi\Omega/\kappa$}

The azimuthal advance over one complete small radial oscillation is $\Delta\psi=2\pi\Omega/\kappa$. The oscillatory azimuthal correction averages to zero at first order. This angle compares successive radial turning points of the same type and describes <apsidal precession> when it differs from a full revolution.

= Epicyclic ellipse
{parent=Epicyclic motion}
{title2=$X/Y=\kappa/(2\Omega)$}

In a Cartesian frame rotating with an <epicyclic guiding center>, a small planar oscillation obeys $x=X\cos(\kappa t+\chi)$ and $y=-(2\Omega X/\kappa)\sin(\kappa t+\chi)$. The ellipse is elongated azimuthally when the <radial epicyclic frequency> satisfies $\kappa<2\Omega$. <Conservation of angular momentum> makes the motion around the guiding centre retrograde.

= Epicyclic guiding center
{title2=$x_0$}
{parent=Epicyclic motion}

The epicyclic guiding center is the circular-orbit location about which a particle performs <epicyclic motion>. In a <Keplerian shearing sheet>, the conserved <canonical momentum> is $p_y=\dot y+2\Omega x=\Omega x_0/2$. The guiding center therefore labels the particle's first-order <specific angular momentum> offset, even while its instantaneous radial coordinate oscillates.

= Epicyclic energy
{title2=$E_{\rm epi}$}
{parent=Epicyclic motion}

For <epicyclic motion> in a <Keplerian shearing sheet>, write $x-x_0=\operatorname{Re}(Ae^{-i\Omega t})$. The energy of the radial <harmonic oscillator> per unit mass is $E_{\rm epi}=\Omega^2|A|^2/2$. The full horizontal <Jacobi energy in a shearing sheet> also includes the negative guiding-center term $-3\Omega^2x_0^2/8$.

The streaming instability is an instability of mutually coupled gas and solid particles in a disk. Relative drift and drag can concentrate the particles into dense structures.

= Astrophysical black hole
{parent=Astrophysics}

An astrophysical black hole is a black hole studied through its formation, accretion, mergers, dynamical environment, and electromagnetic or gravitational-wave signatures.

= Black-hole sphere of influence
{parent=Astrophysical black hole}
{title2=$r_{\rm inf}\simeq GM_\bullet/\sigma^2$}

The <black-hole sphere of influence> is a characteristic radius where the central <black hole> appreciably changes surrounding stellar dynamics. Equating its <Newtonian gravitational potential> scale $GM_\bullet/r$ to the ambient squared <velocity dispersion> gives $r_{\rm inf}\simeq GM_\bullet/\sigma^2$. Dynamical mass measurements depend on angular resolution $r_{\rm inf}/D$, tracer brightness, and degeneracies in the orbital distribution, not solely on <black-hole> mass.

= Black-hole spin
{title2=$a_*$}
{parent=Astrophysical black hole}

Black-hole spin is the angular momentum of a black hole, commonly expressed by the dimensionless Kerr parameter $a_*=cJ/(GM^2)$. It changes the <innermost stable circular orbit>, accretion efficiency, and relativistic frame dragging.

= Black-hole spin-up by coherent accretion
{parent=Black-hole spin}

Coherent prograde gas accretion increases the <angular momentum> of a <black hole>. For a zero-torque thin disc, the rest mass $dm_0$ entering from the <innermost stable circular orbit> changes the hole by $dM=E_{\rm ISCO}dm_0$ and $dJ=\ell_{\rm ISCO}dm_0$. Its dimensionless spin obeys $da_*/d\log M=c\ell_{\rm ISCO}/(GME_{\rm ISCO})-2a_*$. Starting from zero spin and neglecting captured radiation, the formal extremal endpoint has $M_f=\sqrt6M_i$ and requires rest-mass supply approximately $1.8464M_i$. Photon capture leads instead to the <Thorne spin limit>.

= Thorne spin limit
{c}
{title2=$a_*\simeq0.998$}
{parent=Black-hole spin-up by coherent accretion}

The Thorne spin limit is the equilibrium spin produced by competition between coherent thin-disc matter accretion and capture of disc photons by a <Kerr black hole>. Preferential capture of counterrotating photon angular momentum prevents the standard radiatively efficient model from reaching $a_*=1$. The commonly quoted limit is approximately $0.998$, with some dependence on emission and accretion assumptions. https://articles.adsabs.harvard.edu/pdf/1974ApJ...191..507T[Thorne's original calculation] derives this result.

= Black-hole accretion
{parent=Astrophysical black hole}
{wiki}

Black-hole accretion converts gravitational binding energy of inflowing matter into heat, radiation, outflows, and growth of the black hole.

= Collisionless black-hole capture
{parent=Black-hole accretion}

Collisionless matter approaching a <black hole> generally cannot dissipate enough orbital energy to remain bound, so direct capture requires sufficiently small <angular momentum>. For nonrelativistic incident speed $v$, a <Schwarzschild black hole> has direct test-particle capture cross-section approximately $16\pi G^2M^2/(c^2v^2)$. A Newtonian pericentre estimate using the <Schwarzschild radius> instead gives $4\pi G^2M^2/(c^2v^2)$. Both show that capture at a given mass density is suppressed by order $(v/c)^2$ relative to supersonic <Bondi--Hoyle--Lyttleton accretion>. <Tidal disruption events> provide a distinct way to accrete some stellar debris.

= Dynamical upper bound on black-hole fuelling
{parent=Black-hole accretion}

For a <singular isothermal sphere> of dispersion $\sigma$ and gas fraction $f_g$, placing all gas inside radius $R$ onto a hole in time $R/\sigma$ gives $\dot M_{\rm dyn}\sim2f_g\sigma^3/G$. This is an optimistic supply bound that omits <angular momentum> transport, <star formation>, and <active-galactic-nucleus feedback>. A numerical comparison with the <Eddington accretion rate> requires a gas fraction, reference radiative efficiency, opacity, and crossing-time convention.

= Inflow time
{title2=$t_{\rm in}$}
{parent=Black-hole accretion}

The inflow time is the characteristic time for accreting matter to move radially by its current radius, $t_{\rm in}\sim R/|u_R|$.

= Paczyński-Wiita potential
{c}
{title2=$\Phi=-GM/(r-r_S)$}
{parent=Black-hole accretion}
{wiki}

The Paczyński-Wiita potential is a Newtonian pseudo-potential that reproduces the Schwarzschild innermost stable circular orbit at $r=3r_S$.

= Isothermal transonic accretion in the Paczyński-Wiita potential
{parent=Paczyński-Wiita potential}
{title2=$r_c=\frac{GM}{4c_s^2}(1+\beta/2+\sqrt{1+\beta})$}

For $\beta=16c_s^2/c^2$, the exterior <sonic point> of the <barotropic spherical accretion equation> lies outside $r_G=2GM/c^2$. Its <mass density> is $\rho_c=\rho_\infty\exp[4/(1+\sqrt{1+\beta})-1/2]$. The selected smooth transonic accretion rate is $4\pi r_c^2\rho_cc_s$. Subsonic formal solutions are not assigned this rate without transonic selection.

= Paczyński-Wiita circular orbit
{c}
{parent=Paczyński-Wiita potential}

Let $r_S=2GM/c^2$. In the <Paczyński-Wiita potential>, <circular orbit> balance and the <radial epicyclic frequency> formula give
$$
\Omega^2=\frac{GM}{r(r-r_S)^2},\quad
h=\frac{\sqrt{GM}\,r^{3/2}}{r-r_S},\quad
\kappa_r^2=\frac{GM(r-3r_S)}{r(r-r_S)^3}.
$$
Thus the <innermost stable circular orbit> lies at $r=3r_S$. Its <specific orbital energy> is $-c^2/16$, giving a zero-torque <radiative efficiency of black-hole accretion> of $1/16$. The model reproduces the Schwarzschild inner orbit exactly but approximates its relativistic energy and frequency. https://arxiv.org/abs/0904.0913[Abramowicz's derivation] explains the potential's relation to the Schwarzschild effective potential.

= Paczyński-Wiita ballistic plunge
{c}
{parent=Paczyński-Wiita circular orbit}
{title2=$j^2=27GMa/4,\quad E=-GM/(8a)$}

The limiting inward departure from the marginal <Paczyński-Wiita circular orbit> at $r=3a$ conserves the displayed <specific angular momentum> and <specific orbital energy>. In the <Paczyński-Wiita potential>, its inward speed satisfies $v_r^2=GM(3a-r)^3/[4ar^2(r-a)]$. The exactly marginal circular orbit does not depart without a perturbation; the formula describes the separatrix limit of inward releases.

= Two-face dissipation of a Paczyński-Wiita disk
{parent=Paczyński-Wiita circular orbit}
{title2=$D=-\mathcal G\Omega'/(2\pi R)$}

For a <steady accretion disk with arbitrary rotation law> and a <zero-torque inner boundary condition> at $R=3R_S$, the outward <viscous torque in an accretion disk> is $\mathcal G=\dot M(j-j_{\mathrm{in}})$. The dissipation per unit planar area summed over both disk faces is $D=-\mathcal G\Omega'/(2\pi R)=\nu\Sigma(R\Omega')^2$. Integrating $2\pi R D$ gives $L=\dot M GM/(8R_S)=\dot M c^2/16$ in the <Paczyński-Wiita potential>. The radiative flux from one face is $D/2$, preventing a factor-of-two error in the luminosity.

= Radiative efficiency of black-hole accretion
{title2=$\eta$}
{parent=Black-hole accretion}

The radiative efficiency is $\eta=L/(\dot M c^2)$. For a zero-torque thin disk it is $1-E_{\rm ISCO}$, the binding energy per unit rest mass released before matter crosses the innermost stable circular orbit.

= Maximal radiative efficiency of black-hole accretion
{title2=$\eta_{\max}$}
{parent=Radiative efficiency of black-hole accretion}

The maximal radiative efficiency is the available binding energy per unit accreted rest-mass energy when all energy released outside the capture boundary escapes as radiation. A relativistic zero-torque disk has $\eta_{\max}=1-E_{\rm ISCO}$.

= Innermost stable circular orbit
{title2=ISCO}
{parent=Radiative efficiency of black-hole accretion}
{wiki}

The innermost stable circular orbit is the smallest stable circular timelike orbit around a black hole. Its radius and orbital energy depend on black-hole spin and on whether the orbit is prograde or retrograde.

= Bondi accretion
{c}
{parent=Black-hole accretion}
{wiki}

Bondi accretion is steady spherical accretion from a uniform gas reservoir onto a compact gravitating object. In its adiabatic form, compression raises internal energy without providing the irreversible shear heating of a radiatively efficient disk.

= Monatomic Bondi accretion
{parent=Bondi accretion}
{title2=$\gamma=5/3,\quad \dot M_{\max}=\pi G^2M^2\rho_\infty c_\infty^{-3}$}

For isentropic spherical accretion with this <adiabatic index>, define $\lambda=\dot M c_\infty^3/(4\pi G^2M^2\rho_\infty)$ and $x=rc_\infty^2/(GM)$. The steady-flow equation is $\sqrt\lambda[\mathcal M^{3/2}/2+3\mathcal M^{-1/2}/2]=1+3x/2$. The bracket has minimum two at Mach one. Reaching arbitrarily small positive radii therefore requires $\lambda\le1/4$. For smaller rates the outer-rest branch remains subsonic; at the maximal rate it approaches Mach one only as $r\to0$, not at a finite <sonic point>. Supersonic outer branches fail the specified resting finite-density condition, and larger rates terminate at a positive minimum radius. An additional inner boundary prescription is needed to select a unique rate among the globally defined subsonic solutions.

= Relativistic spherical accretion
{parent=Bondi accretion}

= Michel accretion
{c}

Relativistic spherical accretion is steady radial gas inflow in <Schwarzschild spacetime>, with conserved rest-mass flux and relativistic <Bernoulli function>. A regular solution passes a finite <sonic point> outside the <event horizon>. Relativistic corrections resolve the zero-radius critical point of the Newtonian $\gamma=5/3$ model without eliminating accretion.

= Bondi--Hoyle--Lyttleton accretion
{c}
{synonym}

Bondi--Hoyle--Lyttleton accretion is gravitational capture by a compact object moving through a gas. In the pressureless supersonic limit, ballistic streamlines focus into a downstream wake.

= Quadratic accretion in a central Keplerian potential
{parent=Bondi–Hoyle accretion}
{title2=$\dot M=\lambda M^2,\quad M(t)=M_0/(1-\lambda M_0t)$}

In a fixed central <Newtonian gravitational potential>, pressureless <free fall> has speed $u=aR^{-1/2}$, and randomly moving stars have typical gas-relative speed $V=bR^{-1/2}$. A steady, undepleted spherical <mass flux> $A$ gives $\rho=A R^{-3/2}/(4\pi a)$. The <Bondi--Hoyle--Lyttleton accretion rate> therefore becomes $\dot M=\lambda M^2$ with $\lambda=G^2A/(ab^3)$, independent of radius. Integrating $d(1/M)/dt=-\lambda$ gives the displayed solution before $\lambda M_0t=1$. Its finite-time divergence signals failure of the fixed potential, undepleted gas supply and negligible feedback assumptions; it is not a physically unlimited mass supply.

= Mass-spectrum transport under quadratic accretion
{parent=Quadratic accretion in a central Keplerian potential}
{title2=$F(M,t)=F_0(M/(1+\lambda tM))/(1+\lambda tM)^2$}

If stars are neither created nor lost and all follow <quadratic accretion in a central Keplerian potential>, conservation of number gives $F(M,t)dM=F_0(M_0)dM_0$. Inverting the trajectory gives $M_0=M/(1+\lambda tM)$ and its <Jacobian determinant> is $(1+\lambda tM)^{-2}$. This proves the displayed mass-density-in-mass-space transformation. In a broad high-mass range with $\lambda tM\gg1$, whose inverse images remain in a narrow interval where $F_0$ varies little, the <power law> is $F\propto M^{-2}$. A strictly monochromatic initial population remains monochromatic. Finite initial support imposes a finite upper cutoff before the earliest formal blow-up, so the power law is an intermediate asymptotic subject to the accretion model's limits.

= Ballistic streamline focusing by a moving star
{parent=Bondi–Hoyle accretion}
{title2=$b=d^2v^2/(2GM)$}

In a cold pressureless stream of asymptotic speed $v$, conservation of <specific angular momentum> and the <eccentricity vector> makes a streamline at impact parameter $d$ cross the downstream axis at $b=d^2v^2/(2GM)$. Its axial velocity there remains $v$, while opposing transverse velocities collide in a shock.

= Post-shock capture criterion in ballistic accretion
{parent=Ballistic streamline focusing by a moving star}
{title2=$d_{\rm cap}=2GM/v^2$}

If transverse <kinetic energy> is dissipated at the downstream axis, the remaining <specific orbital energy> is $v^2/2-GM/b$. Binding requires $b<2GM/v^2$, corresponding to incident impact parameter $d<2GM/v^2$. Integrating the captured <mass flux> gives the <Bondi--Hoyle--Lyttleton accretion rate>.

= Bondi--Hoyle--Lyttleton accretion rate
{title2=$\dot M_{\rm BHL}$}
{c}
{parent=Bondi–Hoyle accretion}

In the ballistic supersonic limit, streamline collisions bind gas with impact parameter $b<2GM/v_\infty^2$, giving
$$
\dot M_{\rm BHL}=\frac{4\pi G^2M^2\rho_\infty}{v_\infty^3}.
$$

= Isothermal Bondi accretion
{c}
{parent=Bondi accretion}

Isothermal Bondi accretion has $p=c_s^2\rho$ with constant sound speed. Its regular transonic solution crosses the <Bondi sonic point> and approaches free fall near the accretor.

= Isothermal Bondi accretion rate
{parent=Isothermal Bondi accretion}
{title2=$\dot M=\pi e^{3/2}G^2M^2\rho_\infty/c_s^3$}

The regular spherical isothermal inflow from <mass density> $\rho_\infty$ at rest at infinity has sonic <mass density> $e^{3/2}\rho_\infty$. Combining it with the <Bondi sonic point> and <mass conservation> gives $\dot M=\pi e^{3/2}G^2M^2\rho_\infty/c_s^3$.

= Isothermal Bondi equation
{c}
{parent=Isothermal Bondi accretion}

For inward speed $u>0$, steady spherical mass conservation and radial momentum balance give
$$
\left(u-\frac{c_s^2}{u}\right)\frac{du}{dr}
=\frac{2c_s^2}{r}-\frac{GM}{r^2}.
$$

= Integrated isothermal Bondi flow relation
{parent=Isothermal Bondi equation}
{title2=$\mathcal M^2/2-\ln\mathcal M=2/x+2\ln x-3/2$}

With $x=r/r_s$ and $\mathcal M=|u_r|/c_s$, the radial isothermal equation integrates to $\mathcal M^2/2-\ln\mathcal M=2/x+2\ln x+C$. A regular <transonic branch> has $C=-3/2$, and the reservoir-fed accretion branch crosses $(x,\mathcal M)=(1,1)$ with slope $-1$.

= Bondi sonic point
{title2=$r_s$}
{c}
{parent=Isothermal Bondi equation}

The isothermal Bondi sonic point is $r_s=GM/(2c_s^2)$, where the regular transonic solution has $u=c_s$.

= Critical point of the isothermal Bondi equation
{parent=Bondi sonic point}

At the critical point, both the coefficient of $du/dr$ and the remaining right-hand side of the <isothermal Bondi equation> vanish, allowing a finite derivative through the sonic transition.

= Mass accretion rate
{title2=$\dot M$}
{parent=Bondi accretion}

The mass accretion rate is the mass entering an accretor per unit time. In a steady spherical flow, $\dot M=4\pi r^2\rho u$ is independent of radius.

= Transonic accretion in a steep dark-matter cusp
{title2=Transonic accretion in an $r^{-5/2}$ density cusp}
{parent=Mass accretion rate}

For a background gravitating density $\rho_{\rm dm}=\Psi_0r^{-5/2}$, the potential is $\Phi=-16\pi G\Psi_0r^{-1/2}$. A steady polytropic inflow has a regular sonic point only for $1<\gamma<9/7$, and its accretion rate scales as $\dot M\propto\rho_0G^4\Psi_0^4c_0^{-7}$.

= Free-fall speed
{title2=$v_{\rm ff}$}
{parent=Bondi accretion}

The Newtonian free-fall speed from rest at infinity toward a point mass is $v_{\rm ff}=\sqrt{2GM/r}$.

= Critical Bondi accretion rate for gamma equals five thirds
{parent=Bondi accretion}

For an adiabatic gas with $\gamma=5/3$, asymptotic density $\rho_0$, and sound speed $v_{s0}$ accreting onto a Newtonian point mass $M$, the largest globally subsonic steady rate is
$$
\dot M_{\rm crit}=\frac{\pi\rho_0(GM)^2}{v_{s0}^3}.
$$

= Radiatively inefficient accretion flow
{title2=RIAF}
{parent=Black-hole accretion}

A radiatively inefficient accretion flow carries a substantial fraction of locally generated energy with the gas instead of radiating it before accretion. It can occur at low density because emission is weak or at high optical depth because photons are trapped.

= Advection-dominated accretion flow
{title2=ADAF}
{parent=Radiatively inefficient accretion flow}
{wiki}

An advection-dominated accretion flow is a hot, geometrically thick, radiatively inefficient flow in which most dissipated energy remains in the gas and is carried inward rather than radiated locally.

= Two-temperature accretion flow
{parent=Advection-dominated accretion flow}

A two-temperature accretion flow has ions and electrons at substantially different temperatures because their Coulomb equilibration time exceeds the inflow time. Ions can retain most viscous heat while electrons, which radiate more effectively, remain cooler.

= Electron--proton thermal equilibration time
{title2=$t_{e-p}$}
{parent=Two-temperature accretion flow}

The electron--proton thermal equilibration time is the timescale on which Coulomb collisions exchange energy between electron and proton populations. Its inverse dependence on number density helps make dilute accretion flows two-temperature and radiatively inefficient.

= Slim accretion disk
{parent=Radiatively inefficient accretion flow}

= Slim accretion disc
{synonym}

A slim accretion disk is an optically thick, moderately geometrically thick flow at a high accretion rate. Its inflow time can be shorter than its photon-diffusion time, so radial advection carries trapped radiation inward.

A slim <accretion disk> is optically thick and retains an important inward advective heat flux. It is distinct from the optically thin version of a <radiatively inefficient accretion flow>.

= Photon trapping in an accretion flow
{parent=Slim accretion disk}

Photon trapping occurs when the radiative diffusion time across an optically thick flow exceeds its inward advection time. The trapped radiation is carried inward, reducing the emergent radiative efficiency.

= Photon-trapping radius
{title2=$r_t$}
{parent=Photon trapping in an accretion flow}

The photon-trapping radius is where the outward <radiative diffusion> time equals the inward <inflow time>. Radiation generated inside it is predominantly advected toward the accretor.

= Luminous hot accretion flow
{title2=LHAF}
{parent=Radiatively inefficient accretion flow}

A luminous hot accretion flow has radiative cooling greater than local viscous heating, so compressive inward advection supplies the difference. In the convention $q_{\rm adv}=q^+-q^-$, it has $q_{\rm adv}<0$.

= Accretion-flow advection balance
{title2=$q_{\rm adv}=q^+-q^-$}
{parent=Radiatively inefficient accretion flow}

The accretion-flow advection balance compares local volumetric heating $q^+$, radiative cooling $q^-$, and energy transport with the gas. Positive $q_{\rm adv}$ means advection removes locally generated heat; zero gives local radiative balance; negative means advection supplies heat that is radiated locally.

= Eddington ratio
{title2=$f_{\rm Edd}$}
{parent=Black-hole accretion}
{wiki}

The Eddington ratio is the luminosity divided by the Eddington luminosity. At fixed radiative efficiency it also measures the accretion rate relative to the Eddington rate.

= Eddington accretion rate
{title2=$\dot M_{\rm Edd}$}
{c}
{parent=Eddington ratio}

An Eddington accretion rate converts the <Eddington luminosity> into a mass supply rate using an adopted reference efficiency $\eta_0$: $\dot M_{\rm Edd}=L_{\rm Edd}/(\eta_0c^2)$. The common choice $\eta_0=0.1$ gives $\dot M_{\rm Edd}=10L_{\rm Edd}/c^2$.

= Salpeter time
{title2=$t_{\rm Sal}$}
{c}
{parent=Eddington ratio}
{wiki}

The Salpeter time is the e-folding time for Eddington-limited black-hole growth. If $t_E=\sigma_Tc/(4\pi Gm_p)$, then $t_{\rm Sal}=\eta t_E/[(1-\eta)f_{\rm Edd}]$.

= Intermediate-mass black hole
{title2=IMBH}
{parent=Astrophysical black hole}
{wiki}

An intermediate-mass black hole has a mass between stellar remnants and supermassive black holes, approximately $10^2$--$10^5M_\odot$.

= Supermassive black hole
{title2=SMBH}
{parent=Astrophysical black hole}
{wiki}

A supermassive black hole has a mass of roughly $10^6M_\odot$ or more and commonly occupies the nucleus of a massive galaxy.

= M-sigma relation
{c}
{title2=$M_{\rm BH}\propto\sigma^\alpha$}
{parent=Supermassive black hole}

The M-sigma relation is an empirical correlation between <supermassive black hole> mass and the stellar <velocity dispersion> of its host's spheroidal component. A commonly used rough parametrization has exponent near four, but the normalization, exponent, scatter, and selection effects depend on the observed sample. It is distinct from the <black-hole--bulge mass relation>.

= Black-hole--bulge mass relation
{parent=Supermassive black hole}

The black-hole--bulge mass relation is the empirical correlation between a galaxy's central black-hole mass and the stellar mass of its spheroidal component. Locally, the black hole is typically of order $10^{-3}$--$10^{-2}$ of the bulge mass, with substantial scatter and possible redshift evolution.

= Active galactic nucleus
{title2=AGN}
{parent=Supermassive black hole}
{wiki}

= Active galactic nuclei
{synonym}

An active galactic nucleus is a compact luminous galactic nucleus powered primarily by accretion onto a supermassive black hole.

= Quasar
{parent=Active galactic nucleus}
{wiki}

= QSO
{synonym}
{c}

A quasar is an extremely luminous active galactic nucleus whose compact central emission can be observed across cosmological distances.

= Quasar light curve
{parent=Quasar}

A quasar light curve records its measured brightness as a function of time. Intrinsic accretion variability, measurement noise, and gravitational lensing can all contribute structure.

= Active-galactic-nucleus feedback
{title2=AGN feedback}
{parent=Active galactic nucleus}
{wiki}

Active-galactic-nucleus feedback transfers radiative or mechanical energy and momentum from black-hole accretion to surrounding galactic gas.

= Thermal suppression of black-hole accretion
{parent=Active-galactic-nucleus feedback}

Heating a gas reservoir reduces its <Bondi accretion> rate because $\dot M_B\propto\rho_\infty c_s^{-3}$. At fixed density, increasing the <sound speed> by ten reduces the rate by about a thousand and the gravitational capture radius by a hundred. Expansion and density loss can strengthen this negative feedback. Neither the density nor the cold pre-heating accretion rate is determined by the temperature change alone.

= Active-galactic-nucleus wind bubble
{title2=AGN wind bubble}
{parent=Active-galactic-nucleus feedback}

An active-galactic-nucleus wind bubble forms when a fast nuclear wind shocks against galactic gas. From inside outward it contains free wind, a reverse shock, shocked wind, a contact discontinuity, a swept-up shocked shell, a forward shock, and undisturbed interstellar gas.

= Momentum-driven outflow
{parent=Active-galactic-nucleus wind bubble}

An outflow is momentum driven when shocked driving material cools rapidly, so it cannot retain enough thermal energy to inflate a pressure-supported bubble. The swept shell then receives approximately the direct wind momentum flux, often of order $L/c$.

= Energy-driven outflow
{parent=Active-galactic-nucleus wind bubble}

An outflow is energy driven when cooling of the shocked wind is slow. Its hot bubble stores mechanical energy and expands by pressure work on the swept-up shell.

= Momentum boost
{parent=Energy-driven outflow}

A momentum boost is a shell momentum flux exceeding the direct input momentum flux. An energy-conserving wind of speed $v_w$ driving a shell of speed $v_s\ll v_w$ can give $\dot p_s/(L/c)$ of order $v_w/(2v_s)$.

= Dusty radiation-pressure-driven shell
{parent=Active-galactic-nucleus feedback}

A dusty radiation-pressure-driven shell is swept up when ultraviolet radiation from an active nucleus is absorbed by dust and reradiated in the infrared. Single scattering supplies up to roughly $L/c$ of momentum flux, while trapped infrared photons can enhance it by the infrared optical depth.

= Terminal speed of a constant-mass dusty shell
{title2=$v_\infty$}
{parent=Dusty radiation-pressure-driven shell}

A fixed-mass shell with ultraviolet opacity $\kappa_{\rm UV}$ has <dust transparency radius> $R_{\rm UV}=\sqrt{\kappa_{\rm UV}M_{\rm sh}/(4\pi)}$. For an initially expanding shell driven by constant ultraviolet luminosity, a sharp opaque-to-transparent approximation gives
$$
v_\infty^2=v_0^2+\frac{2GM}{R_0}\left[\Gamma_{\rm ss}(2X-1)+\Gamma_{\rm IR}(1-X^{-1})-1\right],
\qquad X=R_{\rm UV}/R_0.
$$
Here $\Gamma_{\rm ss}=LR_0^2/(GM M_{\rm sh}c)$ is the initial single-scattering force ratio and $\Gamma_{\rm IR}=\kappa_{\rm IR}L/(4\pi GMc)$. The trajectory must actually reach $R_{\rm UV}$. Large initial ultraviolet <optical depth> enhances the radiative work at fixed initial single-scattering ratio; the same conclusion does not follow unconditionally at fixed electron-scattering luminosity.

= Critical luminosity of a dusty shell
{title2=$L_{\rm crit}$}
{parent=Dusty radiation-pressure-driven shell}

The critical luminosity of a dusty shell is the luminosity at which radiation pressure balances gravity. For shell mass $M_s$, enclosed gravitating mass $M$, and ultraviolet and infrared optical depths,
$$
L_{\rm crit}=\frac{GcMM_s}{r^2(1+\tau_{\rm IR}-e^{-\tau_{\rm UV}})}.
$$

= Thin-shell momentum equation
{parent=Dusty radiation-pressure-driven shell}

For a spherical swept shell of mass $M_s(R)$, radial speed $\dot R$, driving force $F_d$, and enclosed gravitating mass $M(<R)$,
$$
\frac d{dt}[M_s(R)\dot R]=F_d-\frac{GM_s(R)M(<R)}{R^2}.
$$
The derivative includes momentum needed to accelerate newly swept ambient mass.

= Dust transparency radius
{title2=$R_\tau$}
{parent=Dusty radiation-pressure-driven shell}

The dust transparency radius is where a swept shell's ultraviolet optical depth equals one. For a gas fraction $f_g$ in a singular isothermal sphere, $R_\tau=\kappa_{\rm UV}f_g\sigma^2/(2\pi G)$.

= Soltan argument
{c}
{parent=Supermassive black hole}
{wiki}

The Soltan argument compares the cosmological energy density radiated by active galactic nuclei with the present mass density in supermassive black holes. Their ratio constrains the mass-weighted mean radiative efficiency of black-hole growth.

= Tidal disruption event
{title2=TDE}
{parent=Astrophysical black hole}
{wiki}

A tidal disruption event occurs when a star passes close enough to a black hole that differential gravity tears it apart outside the capture boundary, producing returning debris and often a luminous flare.

= Tidal disruption radius
{title2=$r_t$}
{parent=Tidal disruption event}

For a star of mass $M_*$ and radius $R_*$ near a black hole of mass $M_{\rm BH}$, the Newtonian tidal disruption radius is $r_t\simeq R_*(2M_{\rm BH}/M_*)^{1/3}$ under a peak-to-center tidal-acceleration convention.

= Hills mass
{c}
{parent=Tidal disruption event}
{wiki}

The Hills mass is the black-hole mass above which a given star crosses the capture boundary before being tidally disrupted. For a Schwarzschild capture radius $2GM_{\rm BH}/c^2$, it is $c^3R_*^{3/2}/(2G^{3/2}M_*^{1/2})$ with the corresponding tidal-radius convention.

= Black-hole seed
{parent=Astrophysical black hole}
{wiki}

A black-hole seed is an early black hole from which a later massive black hole can grow by accretion and mergers.

= Population III remnant black-hole seed
{c}
{parent=Black-hole seed}

A Population III remnant seed forms when a massive metal-free star collapses, typically leaving a light seed of order $10$--$10^2M_\odot$ depending on stellar mass loss and pair-instability effects.

= Runaway stellar-collision black-hole seed
{parent=Black-hole seed}

A runaway stellar-collision seed forms when mass segregation and repeated mergers in a dense young cluster create a very massive star that collapses to a black hole of roughly $10^2$--$10^4M_\odot$.

= Direct-collapse black-hole seed
{title2=DCBH seed}
{parent=Black-hole seed}

A direct-collapse black-hole seed forms when rapidly inflowing primordial gas avoids fragmentation, builds a supermassive star or quasistar, and collapses to a seed of roughly $10^4$--$10^6M_\odot$.

= Observational astronomy
{parent=Astrophysics}
{wiki}

= Flux-limited galaxy selection
{parent=Observational astronomy}
{title2=$V_{\max}\propto L^{3/2}$}

For a nearby Euclidean survey with limiting <flux> $f_{\min}$, a <galaxy> of <luminosity> $L$ is detectable out to $d_{\max}=\sqrt{L/(4\pi f_{\min})}$. Its accessible volume is therefore proportional to $L^{3/2}$. With a <Schechter function> $\phi(L)\propto L^\alpha e^{-L/L_*}$, counts per logarithmic interval are proportional to $L^{\alpha+5/2}e^{-L/L_*}$ and peak at $(\alpha+5/2)L_*$ when $\alpha>-5/2$. This selection suppresses faint objects relative to a volume-limited census; finite survey boundaries and cosmological evolution modify the simple law.

= Angular resolution
{parent=Observational astronomy}
{title2=$\theta_{\rm res}$}
{wiki}

<Angular resolution> describes the smallest angular separation or structure an observation can distinguish. A physical scale $r$ at distance $D$ subtends approximately $r/D$ radians when $r\ll D$. Resolving a <black-hole sphere of influence> therefore involves distance as well as its physical radius, and useful kinematics also require adequate <surface brightness>.

= Surface brightness
{parent=Observational astronomy}
{title2=$I$}
{wiki}

<Surface brightness> is received flux per unit solid angle on the sky. It describes the brightness of an extended source independently of a particular aperture's area. An <isophote> is a contour of constant <surface brightness>; central <surface brightness> sets the available photons for resolved galaxy spectroscopy.

= Sérsic profile
{c}
{parent=Surface brightness}
{wiki}
{title2=$I(R)=I_e e^{-b_n[(R/R_e)^{1/n}-1]}$}

A <Sérsic profile> models projected galactic light, with index $n$ controlling curvature and $b_n$ chosen so that $R_e$ encloses half the <luminosity>. Its logarithmic slope is $d\ln I/d\ln R=-(b_n/n)(R/R_e)^{1/n}$. The $n=1$ case is exponential; $n=4$ gives the traditional de Vaucouleurs form. Unlike a fixed power law, its slope steepens outward.

= Projection of a spherical power-law emissivity
{parent=Surface brightness}
{title2=$j\propto r^{-p}\Longrightarrow I\propto R^{1-p}$}

An optically thin spherical source with volume emissivity $j(r)=j_0r^{-p}$ has <surface brightness>
$$
I(R)=\frac{j_0}{4\pi}R^{1-p}\sqrt\pi\,\frac{\Gamma((p-1)/2)}{\Gamma(p/2)},\qquad p>1.
$$
Substitute $z=Ru$ in the line-of-sight <integral> and evaluate $\int_{-\infty}^\infty(1+u^2)^{-p/2}du$ using the <beta function>. The condition $p>1$ is convergence at large distance. A core or finite outer boundary changes the projection near that boundary; the stated scaling applies between those scales.

= Isophote
{parent=Observational astronomy}
{wiki}

An <isophote> is a contour of equal surface brightness in an astronomical image. Deviations from fitted ellipses encode additional information about a <galaxy>'s projected stellar structure.

= Boxy and disky isophotes
{parent=Isophote}
{title2=$a_4\cos(4\theta)$}

Writing an <isophote>'s radial residual about its fitted ellipse as $\delta r=a_4\cos4\theta+\cdots$, positive $a_4$ extends the major and minor axes and gives a pointed, disky shape; negative $a_4$ extends diagonal directions and gives a boxy shape. The sign stated here is for radial residuals, not an unconverted brightness residual. Massive slowly rotating <elliptical galaxies> commonly have boxy <isophotes>, while embedded rotating disks commonly make lower-mass <elliptical galaxies> disky.

= Sky brightness
{parent=Observational astronomy}
{wiki}

= Sky background
{synonym}

Sky brightness is the diffuse radiance received away from an astronomical target. Ground-based observations include atmospheric airglow, scattered light, and <thermal radiation>; extraterrestrial diffuse light also contributes. Its photon fluctuations contribute to the <signal-to-noise ratio in photon counting>.
