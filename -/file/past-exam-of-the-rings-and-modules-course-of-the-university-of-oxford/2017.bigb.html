== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For a <ring> $R$, its <ring characteristic> is the least positive <integer> $n$ with $n1_R=0$, or zero if no positive such integer exists. Equivalently it is the least positive integer satisfying $nr=0$ for every $r\in R$, because $nr=(n1_R)r$. This additive formulation also defines the <characteristic of a rng> when an identity is not assumed.

Let $R$ be an <integral domain>. Since $1_R\ne0$, its characteristic is not one. If its positive characteristic were a composite integer $n=ab$ with $1<a,b<n$, minimality would give $a1_R\ne0$ and $b1_R\ne0$, but
$$
(a1_R)(b1_R)=n1_R=0.
$$
This contradicts the absence of <zero divisors>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{char}R=0\quad\text{or a prime number}.}
$$
This is the <characteristic of an integral domain> theorem. If an integral domain is defined without requiring an identity, the same conclusion follows from the additive definition: for a hypothetical composite characteristic $n=ab$, choose $r$ with $ar\ne0$, which exists by minimality of $n$. Then $(ar)(bs)=n(rs)=0$ for every $s$, forcing $bs=0$ for every $s$ and contradicting minimality again.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Take **$R=\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$**, a <quotient ring> with four residue classes. The class $\overline2$ is nonzero but $\overline2\,\overline2=\overline0$. Thus $R$ has a <zero divisor> and is not an <integral domain>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

**No such ring exists.** Characteristic two gives $r+r=0$ for every $r$. Choose a nonzero element $a$, which exists in a five-element set. Translation $r\mapsto r+a$ is an <involution> without fixed points: applying it twice gives $r$, while $r+a=r$ would force $a=0$ by additive cancellation. Its disjoint pairs would partition all five elements, which is impossible because five is <odd>.

This argument applies even to a <nonunital ring>. With the unital convention, it is also the case $n=2$ of <finite ring characteristic divides cardinality>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

**No such ring exists:** every <finite integral domain is a field>. Here is the proof. For $a\ne0$, the map $L_a:R\to R$ defined by $L_a(x)=ax$ is <injective>, since $ax=ay$ gives $a(x-y)=0$ and therefore $x=y$. An <injective> self-map of a <finite set> is <surjective>, so some $x$ satisfies $ax=1$. Since an <integral domain> is commutative, $xa=1$ as well. Thus every nonzero element has a <multiplicative inverse>, making $R$ a <field>.

The conclusion still holds if an identity was not initially required. Surjectivity first gives an element $e$ with $ae=a$. Then $a(ex-x)=0$ implies $ex=x$ for every $x$, and commutativity makes $e$ a two-sided identity. Applying the preceding argument with $e$ supplies all inverses.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

A suitable <field with four elements> is
$$
\boxed{R=\mathbb F_2[t]/(t^2+t+1).}
$$
Write $\alpha$ for the class of $t$. <Monic polynomial division over a ring> gives a unique representative $a+b\alpha$, with $a,b\in\mathbb F_2$: existence follows by division, and uniqueness follows because a nonzero multiple of the degree-two modulus cannot have degree less than two. The four elements are therefore $0,1,\alpha,\alpha+1$.

The defining relation gives $\alpha^2=\alpha+1$, and hence $\alpha(\alpha+1)=1$. Thus the nonzero elements have inverses
$$
1^{-1}=1,\qquad\alpha^{-1}=\alpha+1,\qquad(\alpha+1)^{-1}=\alpha.
$$
The <quotient ring> is commutative and every nonzero element is a <unit>, so it is a <field>. This verifies the field property directly, without quoting a theorem about irreducible-polynomial quotients.

==== v
{scope}

===== Solution

**No field has six elements.** A <finite field> has positive <characteristic>: the sequence $0,1,2\cdot1,\ldots$ must repeat. By part (a), this characteristic is a <prime number> $p$. Translation by $1$ partitions the field into cycles of length $p$, since returning to a starting point after $k$ steps is equivalent to $k1=0$. Thus $p$ divides six and $p=2$ or $p=3$.

The multiples of $1$ form a copy of the <prime field> $\mathbb F_p$. For completeness, the inverse of a nonzero residue $a$ lies among these multiples: the integer <Bezout identity> $ab+cp=1$ gives $(a1)(b1)=1$. The entire field is a <vector space> over this subfield. A maximal <linearly independent set> is finite and spans, so it is a <basis> of some positive size $d$. Unique basis coordinates give exactly $p^d$ elements. This proves <finite field cardinality is a prime power> here. But six is neither a power of two nor a power of three, a contradiction.

==== vi
{scope}

===== Solution

Under the unital <ring> convention used elsewhere in this wiki, **no such pair exists**. In a two-element ring the elements are $0$ and $1$. Additive cancellation forces $1+1=0$, and the identity axiom forces $1\cdot1=1$. The <distributive property> forces products involving zero to vanish. Every operation is therefore determined, giving the <prime field> $\mathbb F_2$ as the unique possibility up to <ring isomorphism>.

If the word ring allows a <nonunital ring>, there are instead **two non-isomorphic examples: $\mathbb F_2$ and the <zero-multiplication rng> on the additive group $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$**. In the second example the unique nonzero element $u$ satisfies $u+u=0$ and $u^2=0$; every product is zero, so associativity and both distributive laws hold. An isomorphism would have to send $1$ to $u$ because it preserves zero and is bijective, but then it would send $1^2=1$ to both $u^2=0$ and $u$, which is impossible. These exhaust the <rngs with two elements>, since $u^2$ can only be $0$ or $u$. Specifying the identity convention is essential for this part.

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <Euclidean domain> is an <integral domain> $R$ with a <Euclidean function> $\nu:R\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb Z_{\geq0}$ such that, for $a,b\in R$ with $b\ne0$, there exist $q,r\in R$ satisfying
$$
a=qb+r,\qquad r=0\ \text{or}\ \nu(r)<\nu(b).
$$
The <polynomial ring> $\mathbb Q[x]$ has this property with $\nu(f)=\deg f$. To perform <polynomial division>, subtract an appropriate multiple of the divisor to cancel the leading term of the current remainder. Its <polynomial degree> decreases strictly at each step, so the process terminates below the divisor's degree. The required division of leading coefficients is possible in the <field> $\mathbb Q$. Thus a <polynomial ring over a field is Euclidean>, with the same argument applying to every field.

Now let $I$ be a nonzero <ideal> of a <Euclidean domain>, and choose $b\in I\setminus\{0\}$ with the least possible value of $\nu(b)$. For every $a\in I$, divide $a=qb+r$. The remainder belongs to $I$, since $r=a-qb$. If it were nonzero, its smaller Euclidean value would contradict the choice of $b$. Thus $r=0$ and every $a\in I$ lies in $(b)$. Conversely $(b)\subseteq I$ because $I$ is an ideal. Hence $I=(b)$. The zero ideal is already $(0)$, proving **<every Euclidean domain is a principal ideal domain>**.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The three answers, in the given order, are **no, yes, yes**.

For $\mathbb Z[x]$, consider the <ideal> $I=(2,x)$. Its members have even constant coefficient, so $1\notin I$ and the ideal is proper. If $I=(g)$, then $g$ divides the constant $2$, forcing $g$ to be constant by additivity of <polynomial degree> in this <integral domain>. Since $g$ also divides $x$, it must divide the coefficient $1$, giving $g=\pm1$. That would make $I$ the whole ring, a contradiction. Thus $\mathbb Z[x]$ is not a <principal ideal domain>, and part (a) shows it cannot be a <Euclidean domain>. This is the <nonprincipal ideal generated by a prime and an indeterminate> obstruction.

Evaluation at $i$ gives the <ring isomorphism>
$$
\boxed{\mathbb Z[x]/(x^2+1)\cong\mathbb Z[i].}
$$
Indeed, division by the <monic polynomial> $x^2+1$ leaves a unique remainder $a+bx$, and its value $a+bi$ is zero only when both integer coefficients vanish. Thus the quotient maps bijectively, with operations preserved, onto the <Gaussian integers>. These form a <Euclidean domain> for the <Gaussian integer norm> $N(a+bi)=a^2+b^2$, a standard Euclidean example allowed in this part. Geometrically, rounding the real and imaginary coordinates gives a quotient error with squared modulus at most $1/2<1$.

Finally,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb Z[x,y]/(2,x)\cong\mathbb F_2[y],}
$$
via $f(x,y)\mapsto f(0,y)\bmod2$. Every polynomial over $\mathbb F_2$ occurs. The kernel consists exactly of polynomials whose $x$-independent part has even coefficients: write $f(x,y)=xg(x,y)+h(y)$ to see that this is precisely $(2,x)$. Hence the induced quotient map is a bijective <ring homomorphism>. Its target is a <Euclidean domain> with Euclidean function $\deg$: a <polynomial ring over a field is Euclidean> by the division algorithm in part (a).

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Since $\omega^2=-1-\omega$, products of integer linear combinations of $1,\omega$ remain of the same form. Thus the <Eisenstein integers> $E=\mathbb Z[\omega]$ form a subring of $\mathbb C$, and hence an <integral domain>. Their <Eisenstein norm> is
$$
N(a+b\omega)=(a+b\omega)(a+b\overline\omega)=a^2-ab+b^2
=\left(a-\frac b2\right)^2+\frac34b^2.
$$
It is a positive <integer> on nonzero elements and is multiplicative, because the <complex modulus> is multiplicative.

For $\alpha,\beta\in E$ with $\beta\ne0$, write $\alpha/\beta=u+v\omega$ with real $u,v$. Choose integers $m,n$ with $|u-m|,|v-n|\leq1/2$, and let $q=m+n\omega$. With $s=u-m$ and $t=v-n$,
$$
\left|\frac\alpha\beta-q\right|^2=s^2-st+t^2
\leq s^2+|st|+t^2\leq\frac34<1.
$$
Consequently $r=\alpha-q\beta$ belongs to $E$ and satisfies $N(r)\leq\tfrac34N(\beta)<N(\beta)$ when $r\ne0$. This proves **$E$ is a <Euclidean domain> with Euclidean function $N$**, the <Euclidean norm on the Eisenstein integers>.

\Image[2017-eisenstein-division.png]
{title=Rounding the coordinates in the <Eisenstein integer> lattice gives a Euclidean remainder}
{description=After subtracting the chosen lattice point, the error lies in the shaded parallelogram. Its squared modulus is at most $3/4$, strictly smaller than one. The parallelogram is a rounding cell, not the hexagonal nearest-point cell.}

For the representation of the prime $p$, first note that **$3=N(1-\omega)$**. Now suppose $p>3$ and $p\mid n^2+3$. The factorization
$$
n^2+3=(n+1+2\omega)(n-1-2\omega)
$$
shows that the <Eisenstein integer> $p$ divides a product. It divides neither factor: an element $a+b\omega$ lies in $pE$ exactly when both integer coefficients are divisible by $p$, and the second coefficients here are $2$ and $-2$. Hence $p$ is not a <prime element> of $E$.

To justify the necessary factorization step, in any <principal ideal domain> an <irreducible element> $\pi$ is prime. If $\pi\nmid a$, write $(\pi,a)=(d)$. Since $d\mid\pi$, irreducibility makes $d$ a unit or an associate of $\pi$. The latter would give $\pi\mid a$, so $d$ is a unit and $u\pi+va=1$ for some $u,v$. If $\pi\mid ab$, multiplication of this identity by $b$ gives $\pi\mid b$. This proves <irreducible elements of a principal ideal domain are prime> without assuming unique factorization.

By part (a), $E$ is a <principal ideal domain>. The integer $p$ has norm $p^2>1$, so it is a nonzero nonunit. Since it is not prime, the result just proved implies that it is reducible: $p=uv$ with $u,v$ both nonunits. A nonzero element of $E$ is a <unit> exactly when its norm is one, since $N(z)=1$ gives inverse $\overline z$ and multiplicativity proves the converse. Thus
$$
1<N(u),N(v),\qquad N(u)N(v)=N(p)=p^2.
$$
The only possible positive integer factors exceeding one are $N(u)=N(v)=p$. Writing $u=a+b\omega$ gives
$$
\boxed{a^2-ab+b^2=p.}
$$
This is the <prime representation by the Eisenstein norm> argument.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $d=\dim V$. A <cyclic vector> for $S$ is a vector $v$ whose successive iterates span $V$; equivalently, when $d>0$,
$$
v,Sv,\ldots,S^{d-1}v
$$
are a <basis>. The equivalence follows because the first linear dependence in the iterates makes their preceding span invariant under $S$, so no later iterate can enlarge it.

In this basis there are unique coefficients $c_0,\ldots,c_{d-1}$ with $S^dv=-\sum_{j<d}c_jS^jv$. Put $f(t)=t^d+\sum_{j<d}c_jt^j$. Then $f(S)v=0$, and commutation with powers of $S$ gives $f(S)S^jv=S^jf(S)v=0$. Therefore $f(S)$ kills an entire basis and is the zero map. No nonzero polynomial of degree less than $d$ annihilates $v$, because that would be a linear dependence of the displayed basis. Thus $m_S=f$.

The matrix of $S$ in the cyclic basis is the <companion matrix> $C(f)$ with ones on its subdiagonal and last column $(-c_0,\ldots,-c_{d-1})^{\mathsf T}$. Expanding $\det(tI-C(f))$ along its last column gives
$$
c_0+c_1t+\cdots+c_{d-2}t^{d-2}+(t+c_{d-1})t^{d-1}=f(t):
$$
the cofactors are $1,t,\ldots,t^{d-1}$. This proves the <characteristic polynomial of a companion matrix> formula here. Hence
$$
\boxed{m_S(t)=c_S(t)=f(t).}
$$
This proves that a <cyclic operator has equal minimal and characteristic polynomials>. If $V=\{0\}$, both polynomials are one by convention, so the equality remains valid.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <rational canonical form> of $T$ is a block diagonal matrix
$$
C(f_1)\oplus\cdots\oplus C(f_r),\qquad
f_1\mid f_2\mid\cdots\mid f_r,
$$
similar to a matrix of $T$, where the $f_j\in F[t]$ are monic nonconstant polynomials and the sum of their degrees is $\dim V$. They are the <invariant factors of a linear operator>. The block $C(f_j)$ is the <companion matrix> for $f_j$, representing multiplication by $t$ on $F[t]/(f_j)$. The invariant factors are uniquely determined by $T$.

Every companion block has a <cyclic vector> and therefore has both <minimal polynomial> and <characteristic polynomial> equal to $f_j$, by part (a). The <determinant of a block diagonal matrix> is the product of its block determinants, giving
$$
\boxed{c_T(t)=\prod_{j=1}^r f_j(t).}
$$
A polynomial $h$ annihilates the whole matrix exactly when it annihilates every block. On block $j$, dividing $h$ by $f_j$ leaves a remainder of smaller degree; if that remainder annihilated the block without being zero, it would contradict minimality of $f_j$. Thus the annihilation condition is exactly $f_j\mid h$ for every $j$. The monic polynomial of least degree with this property is their <least common multiple>. The divisibility chain makes this $f_r$, so
$$
\boxed{m_T(t)=f_r(t).}
$$
These are the <minimal and characteristic polynomials from invariant factors> formulae. On the zero vector space the product is empty and both polynomials are defined to be one.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-2\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, so the displayed <rational canonical form> of $T$ is $J\oplus J$. Since $J^2=-2I_2$, we have $T^2=-2I_4$.

If $a=0$, then $U=bI_4$ is a <scalar matrix>. Its <invariant factors of a linear operator> are four copies of $t-b$, so its rational canonical form is
$$
\boxed{C(t-b)^{\oplus4}=bI_4\qquad(a=0).}
$$
Here $m_U=t-b$ and $c_U=(t-b)^4$, including when $b=0$.

Now let $a\ne0$ and put
$$
q(t)=(t-b)^2+2a^2=t^2-2bt+(b^2+2a^2).
$$
On each two-dimensional invariant block, $A=aJ+bI_2$ satisfies $q(A)=0$. The vectors $e_1,Ae_1$ form a <cyclic vector> basis, since their coordinate matrix is $P=\begin{pmatrix}1&b\\0&a\end{pmatrix}$ with <determinant> $a\ne0$. Directly,
$$
P^{-1}AP=
\begin{pmatrix}0&-(b^2+2a^2)\\1&2b\end{pmatrix}=C(q).
$$
Applying this basis change to both blocks gives
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{RCF}(U)=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&-(b^2+2a^2)&0&0\\
1&2b&0&0\\
0&0&0&-(b^2+2a^2)\\
0&0&1&2b
\end{pmatrix}\qquad(a\ne0).}
$$
Thus $m_U=q$ and $c_U=q^2$. In particular there are two invariant factors $q,q$, rather than a single block for $q^2$. Also $q(t)>0$ for every real $t$, so it is an <irreducible polynomial> over $\mathbb R$. This is an instance of <rational canonical form under an affine change>.

Finally $T^2=-2I_4$ implies $T^{-1}=-T/2$. Substituting $a=-1/2$, $b=0$ in the preceding result gives $q(t)=t^2+1/2$, and hence
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{RCF}(T^{-1})=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&-1/2&0&0\\
1&0&0&0\\
0&0&0&-1/2\\
0&0&1&0
\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Its minimal polynomial is $t^2+1/2$ and its characteristic polynomial is $(t^2+1/2)^2$. The normalized reciprocal of $t^2+2$ is also $t^2+1/2$, agreeing with <rational canonical form of an inverse>.
