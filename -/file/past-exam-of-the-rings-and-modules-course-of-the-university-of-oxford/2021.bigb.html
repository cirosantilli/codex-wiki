= Solution

{c}

We prove the stated <Eisenstein criterion> directly. Assume $n\geq1$. Suppose $f=gh$ in the <polynomial ring> $R[X]$. Since the <principal ideal domain> $R$ is an <integral domain>, <polynomial degree> is additive under <polynomial multiplication>, and the product of the <leading coefficients> of $g,h$ is the <leading coefficient> $1$ of $f$. These two <coefficients> are therefore <units>. Multiplying $g$ and $h$ by inverse <units>, without changing their product, makes both factors <monic polynomials>.

If either factor is a <constant polynomial>, that factor is now $1$, so the original factor was a <unit>. It remains to rule out $r=\deg g>0$ and $s=\deg h>0$.

The <prime element> $p$ generates a <maximal ideal> in the <principal ideal domain> $R$. Indeed, if $(p)\subseteq(d)$, then $p=de$, and the <irreducible element> property of $p$ forces either $(d)=R$ or $(d)=(p)$. Consequently $k=R/(p)$ is a <field>. Apply <reduction modulo an ideal> to every <coefficient>. The <monic polynomial> factors retain their <polynomial degrees>, and the hypotheses give
$$
\overline g\,\overline h=\overline f=X^n\quad\text{in }k[X].
$$
The <polynomial ring> over a <field> is a <unique factorization domain>, and $X$ is an <irreducible polynomial>. Thus its only <monic polynomial> divisors in $X^n$ are powers of $X$. Hence $\overline g=X^r$ and $\overline h=X^s$. Both have zero <constant term>, so $p\mid g(0)$ and $p\mid h(0)$. This gives $p^2\mid g(0)h(0)=a_0$, a contradiction.

The <monic polynomial> $f$ has positive <polynomial degree>, so it is a nonzero <nonunit>. Every factorization therefore has a <unit> factor, proving
$$
\boxed{f\text{ is irreducible in }R[X]}.
$$

Regard $\mathbb R[X,Y]$ as the <polynomial ring> $(\mathbb R[X])[Y]$. The <coefficient> <ring> $\mathbb R[X]$ is a <Euclidean domain>, hence a <principal ideal domain>. The element $X$ is a <prime element>: the <quotient ring> $\mathbb R[X]/(X)\cong\mathbb R$ is an <integral domain>.

The <monic polynomial> $Y^2+X$ has its other <coefficients> divisible by $X$, while $X^2\nmid X$. Part (a)(i), with $X$ playing the role of $p$ and $Y$ the <indeterminate>, proves that $Y^2+X$ is an <irreducible polynomial>.

Now use the <automorphism of a polynomial ring>
$$
\tau:\mathbb R[X,Y]\longrightarrow\mathbb R[X,Y],\qquad
\tau(X)=X,\quad\tau(Y)=Y+1.
$$
Its inverse fixes $X$ and sends $Y$ to $Y-1$. A <ring isomorphism> preserves <units> and factorizations, hence preserves <irreducible elements>. Since
$$
\tau(Y^2+X)=(Y+1)^2+X=Y^2+2Y+1+X,
$$
we obtain
$$
\boxed{Y^2+X\text{ and }Y^2+2Y+1+X\text{ are irreducible in }\mathbb R[X,Y]}.
$$
Applying the <Eisenstein criterion> directly to the second expression with the <prime element> $X$ would fail, since $X\nmid2$; the change of <indeterminate> supplies the required argument.

All <principal ideals> in this part are taken inside the <subring> $T$, rather than inside $\mathbb R[X]$. First, $T$ is a <subring>: sums and <additive inverses> retain zero <coefficient> of $X$, and the <coefficient> of $X$ in a product $uv$ is $u(0)[X]v+v(0)[X]u=0$. It contains $1$.

The <principal ideal> $I=(X^3)_T=X^3T$ is precisely
$$
I=\left\{a_3X^3+\sum_{j\geq5}a_jX^j:\text{only finitely many }a_j\ne0\right\}.
$$
Indeed, multiplying an element of $T$ by $X^3$ gives a possible term of <polynomial degree> $3$ and terms of <polynomial degrees> at least $5$, with no $X^4$ term. Conversely, dividing any displayed <polynomial> by $X^3$ gives a <polynomial> with no $X$ term, hence an element of $T$.

Given $p\in T$, let $a,b,c$ be its <coefficients> of $1,X^2,X^4$, respectively. Subtracting these terms leaves an element of $I$, so
$$
\boxed{p+I=a+bX^2+cX^4+I}.
$$
For uniqueness, if two such representatives differ by an element of $I$, their difference has possible <monomials> only $1,X^2,X^4$, whereas every element of $I$ has zero <coefficients> at all three. Comparing <coefficients> gives $a=a'$, $b=b'$, $c=c'$.

In particular, $X^4\notin I$. Treating $I$ as the <principal ideal> of $\mathbb R[X]$ instead would incorrectly discard this surviving term.

Set $I=(X^3)_T$ and $J=(Y^3)_{\mathbb R[Y]}$. Part (b)(i) gives a unique representative $a+bX^2+cX^4$ for each element of the <quotient ring> $T/I$. By <polynomial division>, each element of the <quotient ring> $\mathbb R[Y]/J$ likewise has a unique representative $a+bY+cY^2$. Thus the proposed map $\Phi$ is well-defined and a <bijection>.

It preserves <addition> and $1$. To verify <multiplication>, all terms of <polynomial degree> at least $5$ lie in $I$. Hence
$$
\begin{aligned}
&(a+bX^2+cX^4)(a'+b'X^2+c'X^4)+I\\
&\qquad=aa'+(ab'+ba')X^2+(ac'+bb'+ca')X^4+I.
\end{aligned}
$$
In the other <quotient ring>, $Y^3=0$, giving exactly the same <coefficient> formula:
$$
(a+bY+cY^2)(a'+b'Y+c'Y^2)+J
=aa'+(ab'+ba')Y+(ac'+bb'+ca')Y^2+J.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Phi:T/(X^3)_T\xrightarrow{\ \cong\ }\mathbb R[Y]/(Y^3),\qquad
a+bX^2+cX^4+I\longmapsto a+bY+cY^2+J}
$$
is a <ring isomorphism>. Both sides are examples of a <truncated polynomial ring>; the generator on the left is the class of $X^2$, whose square is nonzero and whose cube vanishes.

**No such property exists.** The <ring isomorphism> in part (b)(ii) identifies <quotient rings> by elements having different <irreducible element> behaviour.

The element $X^3$ is an <irreducible element> of $T$. It is nonzero and a <nonunit>. Every nonzero <nonunit> of $T$ has <polynomial degree> at least $2$: the only possible <polynomials> of smaller <polynomial degree> are <constant polynomials>, and every nonzero <constant polynomial> is a <unit>. If $X^3=uv$ with $u,v\in T$ both <nonunits>, additivity of <polynomial degree> in the <integral domain> $\mathbb R[X]$ would give
$$
3=\deg u+\deg v\geq2+2=4,
$$
which is impossible. Thus one factor must be a <unit>.

In $\mathbb R[Y]$, however, $Y^3=Y\cdot Y^2$ is a product of two <nonunits>, so $Y^3$ is not an <irreducible element>. If a <ring isomorphism invariant> $P$ characterized <irreducible elements> by the indicated <quotient ring>, the <irreducible element> $X^3\in T$ would imply that $T/(X^3)_T$ has $P$. Its isomorphic <quotient ring> $\mathbb R[Y]/(Y^3)$ would then have $P$, incorrectly making $Y^3\in\mathbb R[Y]$ an <irreducible element>.

Consequently,
$$
\boxed{\text{irreducibility cannot be determined from the isomorphism type of }R/(x)}.
$$

= Solution
{parent=iv}
{c}

The required property is
$$
\boxed{P:\quad\text{being an integral domain}}.
$$
For a nonzero element $x$ of an <integral domain> $R$, this follows from
$$
\begin{aligned}
x\text{ is a prime element}
&\iff (x)\text{ is a prime ideal}\\
&\iff R/(x)\text{ is an integral domain}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Indeed, $\overline a\,\overline b=0$ in the <quotient ring> exactly when $x\mid ab$. The <prime element> condition says this forces $x\mid a$ or $x\mid b$, exactly the absence of <zero divisors> in the <quotient ring>. The <nonunit> condition makes the <quotient ring> nonzero. Being an <integral domain> is preserved by every <ring isomorphism>.

With the usual definition of a <prime element>, $x\ne0$ is understood. The <prime ideal> formulation works for every <ideal> of any <commutative ring>, including the zero <ideal>; it avoids this convention about elements.

Choose a finite <generating set of a module> $n_1,\ldots,n_t$ for the <finitely generated module> $N$. For any $m\in M$, the <surjectivity> of the <module homomorphism> $\phi$ gives $n\in N$ with $\phi(n)=m$. Write $n=\sum_{j=1}^t r_jn_j$. Since $\phi$ is a <module homomorphism>,
$$
m=\phi\left(\sum_{j=1}^t r_jn_j\right)=\sum_{j=1}^t r_j\phi(n_j).
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{M=R\phi(n_1)+\cdots+R\phi(n_t)}
$$
is a <finitely generated module>. The argument includes $N=0$, in which case <surjectivity> forces $M=0$ and the empty <generating set of a module> suffices.

Let $n_1,\ldots,n_t$ generate the <submodule> $N$, and let $m_1+N,\ldots,m_s+N$ generate the <quotient module> $M/N$. Choose each $m_i\in M$ as a representative of its class. For any $m\in M$, there are $r_1,\ldots,r_s\in R$ such that
$$
m+N=\sum_{i=1}^s r_i(m_i+N).
$$
Therefore $m-\sum_i r_im_i\in N$. Expressing that difference using the <generating set of a module> $n_1,\ldots,n_t$ gives
$$
m=\sum_{i=1}^s r_im_i+\sum_{j=1}^t u_jn_j.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{M=Rm_1+\cdots+Rm_s+Rn_1+\cdots+Rn_t}
$$
is a <finitely generated module>. This proves that <finite generation is preserved by module extensions>.

Let $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ denote the standard <basis of a module> of the <free module> $R^n$. Since the <module homomorphism> $\phi:R^m\to M$ is <surjective>, choose $v_j\in R^m$ such that $\phi(v_j)=\psi(e_j)$ for each $j$. Define
$$
\boxed{q:R^n\longrightarrow R^m,\qquad q\left(\sum_{j=1}^n r_je_j\right)=\sum_{j=1}^n r_jv_j}.
$$
Uniqueness of coordinates in a <basis of a module> makes this well-defined, and the formula preserves <addition> and <scalar multiplication>, so $q$ is a <module homomorphism>. For every $x=\sum_jr_je_j$,
$$
\phi(q(x))=\sum_jr_j\phi(v_j)=\sum_jr_j\psi(e_j)=\psi(x).
$$
Thus $\boxed{\phi\circ q=\psi}$. This is the <lifting property of a free module>; only the <surjectivity> of $\phi$ is needed for this part.

Put $K=\ker\phi$, $H=\ker\psi$, and $Q=\operatorname{Im}q$. The <kernel of a module homomorphism> $H$ is a <submodule>, so its image $L=q(H)$ is a <submodule>. For $x\in H$, $\phi(q(x))=\psi(x)=0$, showing $L\subseteq K$. Thus the <quotient module> $K/L$ exists. Also $L\subseteq Q$.

Consider the restriction to $K$ of the natural <module homomorphism> $R^m\to R^m/Q$:
$$
\alpha:K\longrightarrow R^m/Q,\qquad y\longmapsto y+Q.
$$
Its <kernel of a module homomorphism> is $K\cap Q$. We have $L\subseteq K\cap Q$. Conversely, if $y\in K\cap Q$, choose $x\in R^n$ with $y=q(x)$. Then
$$
\psi(x)=\phi(q(x))=\phi(y)=0,
$$
so $x\in H$ and $y\in L$. Hence $\boxed{K\cap Q=L}$.

The map $\alpha$ is <surjective>. Given $v+Q\in R^m/Q$, use the <surjectivity> of $\psi$ to choose $x\in R^n$ with $\psi(x)=\phi(v)$. Set $y=v-q(x)$. Then
$$
\phi(y)=\phi(v)-\psi(x)=0,\qquad y+Q=v+Q.
$$
Thus $y\in K$ is a preimage of the arbitrary class $v+Q$.

By the <first isomorphism theorem for modules>, $\alpha$ induces the <module isomorphism>
$$
\boxed{\overline\alpha:\ker\phi/L\xrightarrow{\ \cong\ }R^m/\operatorname{Im}q,\qquad
y+L\longmapsto y+\operatorname{Im}q}.
$$
Explicitly, replacing $y$ by $y+\ell$ with $\ell\in L$ leaves its class modulo $Q$ unchanged, proving well-definedness. Its formula preserves <addition> and <scalar multiplication>. If its value is zero, $y\in K\cap Q=L$, so $y+L=0$, proving <injectivity>; <surjectivity> was proved above. These checks give all the requested properties of the induced map.

Assume $H=\ker\psi$ is a <finitely generated module>. The restriction $q:H\to L$ is a <surjective> <module homomorphism>, so part (a)(i) makes $L$ a <finitely generated module>.

The <free module> $R^m$ has its finite standard <basis of a module>. Its <quotient module> $R^m/\operatorname{Im}q$ is therefore a <finitely generated module> by part (a)(i). Part (c) transfers this property to the isomorphic <quotient module> $\ker\phi/L$. Finally apply part (a)(ii) to the <submodule> $L\subseteq\ker\phi$. It follows that
$$
\boxed{\ker\psi\text{ finitely generated}\ \Longrightarrow\ \ker\phi\text{ finitely generated}}.
$$
More concretely, take the images under $q$ of finitely many generators of $H$, and adjoin lifts in $\ker\phi$ of finitely many generators of $\ker\phi/L$. This finite list generates $\ker\phi$. The argument shows why <finite presentation of a module> is independent of the chosen finite <generating set of a module>.

**The exact orders printed in the question are inconsistent.** The source PDF says that the <orders of group elements> $a,b,c$ are $4,12,24$, respectively, and also imposes $3a+2b=0$. Multiplying this relation by $6$ and using $12b=0$ gives $18a=0$. Since $4a=0$ also holds, subtracting $4(4a)=0$ gives $2a=0$, contradicting the stated <order of a group element> $4$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{no group satisfies all the hypotheses literally as printed}}.
$$

The natural <abelian group presentation> interpretation is to impose the relations $4a=12b=24c=0$ and $3a+2b=0$, with no further relations, without requiring the orders of the images of the generators to stay $4,12,24$. Under this interpretation the calculation is as follows. Since $3a=-a$ follows from $4a=0$, the mixed relation gives $a=2b$. Consequently $8b=0$, and together with $12b=0$ this gives $4b=0$. Conversely, $a=2b$, $4b=0$, and $24c=0$ imply every original relation. Eliminating $a$ leaves two independent <cyclic groups>:
$$
\boxed{G_{\mathrm{pres}}\cong\mathbb Z_4\oplus\mathbb Z_{24},\qquad n=2,\quad d_1=4,\quad d_2=24}.
$$
To verify that these relations force no further identifications, map $a,b,c$ to $(2,0),(1,0),(0,1)$ in $\mathbb Z_4\oplus\mathbb Z_{24}$. All defining relations hold, and the inverse <group homomorphism> sends the two standard generators to $b,c$. These <group homomorphisms> are mutually inverse. The <divisibility> condition $4\mid24$ gives the requested <invariant factors of a finitely generated module> over $\mathbb Z$. In this presented <group>, $a,b,c$ actually have orders $2,4,24$.

Equivalently, the relation <matrix>
$$
\begin{pmatrix}4&0&0\\0&12&0\\0&0&24\\3&2&0\end{pmatrix}
$$
has nonzero <Smith normal form> entries $1,4,24$, with the trivial $\mathbb Z_1$ factor omitted. The displayed <direct sum of modules> is the answer to the presentation interpretation; the inconsistency of the literal exact-order formulation remains.

Every multiple of $dh$ is a multiple of both $d$ and $h$, so $dh\mathbb Z\subseteq d\mathbb Z\cap h\mathbb Z$.

For the reverse inclusion, let $z=du=hv$ lie in this <intersection of ideals>. Since $d,h$ are <coprime integers>, <Bézout's identity> supplies $r,s\in\mathbb Z$ with $rd+sh=1$. Multiplying by $u$ gives
$$
u=rdu+shu=rhv+shu=h(rv+su).
$$
Thus $h\mid u$, and $dh\mid du=z$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{d\mathbb Z\cap h\mathbb Z=dh\mathbb Z}.
$$
Here <Bézout's identity> follows from the allowed <principal ideal domain> property of $\mathbb Z$: the <ideal> $d\mathbb Z+h\mathbb Z$ is generated by their <greatest common divisor>, which is $1$.

Define the <group homomorphism>
$$
\theta:\mathbb Z\longrightarrow\mathbb Z_d\oplus\mathbb Z_h,\qquad
t\longmapsto(t+d\mathbb Z,t+h\mathbb Z).
$$
By part (b)(i), its <kernel of a group homomorphism> is
$$
\ker\theta=d\mathbb Z\cap h\mathbb Z=dh\mathbb Z.
$$
It is <surjective>. Indeed, choose $r,s\in\mathbb Z$ with $rd+sh=1$ by <Bézout's identity>. For any prescribed classes $u+d\mathbb Z$ and $v+h\mathbb Z$, the <integer>
$$
t=ush+vrd
$$
satisfies $t\equiv u\pmod d$ and $t\equiv v\pmod h$. The <first isomorphism theorem for groups> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb Z_{dh}\xrightarrow{\ \cong\ }\mathbb Z_d\oplus\mathbb Z_h,\qquad
t+dh\mathbb Z\longmapsto(t+d\mathbb Z,t+h\mathbb Z)}.
$$
This proves the required isomorphism and is the <Chinese remainder theorem> for <coprime integers>. It also shows that $(1+d\mathbb Z,1+h\mathbb Z)$ generates the <direct sum of modules> when these additive <groups> are viewed as $\mathbb Z$-<modules>.

Let $N=|G|$ be a <square-free integer>. If $N=1$, then $G$ is the trivial <cyclic group>. Otherwise, finiteness means that a <direct sum of modules> decomposition into <cyclic groups> has only finitely many nontrivial factors, each of finite <group order>. Omit trivial factors and write
$$
G\cong\mathbb Z_{n_1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb Z_{n_r},\qquad
n_j>1,\qquad N=\prod_{j=1}^r n_j.
$$
The <integers> $n_i,n_j$ are <coprime integers> whenever $i\ne j$. If a <prime number> $p$ divided both, then $p^2\mid N$, contradicting the <square-free integer> hypothesis.

Moreover, $n_1\cdots n_{j-1}$ and $n_j$ are <coprime integers>: any common <prime divisor> would divide one of the earlier factors as well as $n_j$. Thus part (b)(ii) can be applied repeatedly to combine factors, giving
$$
\boxed{G\cong\mathbb Z_{n_1\cdots n_r}=\mathbb Z_N}.
$$
In particular $G$ is a <cyclic group>. Under the displayed identifications, the element whose coordinate in every factor is the class of $1$ is a <generator of a group>.

Write $q=|\mathbb F|$. The <unit group> $U(\mathbb F)=\mathbb F\setminus\{0\}$ is a <finite abelian group> of <group order> $q-1$, since <multiplication> in a <field> satisfies <commutativity>. The <structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups>, applied to this finite <group>, gives <invariant factors of a finitely generated module>
$$
U(\mathbb F)\cong\mathbb Z_{d_1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb Z_{d_r},\qquad
1<d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_r,
$$
after omitting trivial factors. If there are no factors, the <unit group> is trivial and hence a <cyclic group>.

Otherwise put $D=d_r$. Every element $u\in U(\mathbb F)$ satisfies $u^D=1$, because the <order of a group element> in each coordinate divides $D$. Hence all $q-1$ distinct nonzero elements of $\mathbb F$ are <roots of a polynomial> $X^D-1$. The <root bound for a polynomial> over a <field> gives
$$
q-1\leq D.
$$
For completeness, that bound follows by induction from the <factor theorem>: if a nonzero <polynomial> $f$ vanishes at $a$, write $f=(X-a)g$; every other <root of a polynomial> is a root of $g$, because a <field> has no <zero divisors>. Each new distinct root therefore uses one unit of <polynomial degree>.

On the other hand, the <group order> in the decomposition is
$$
q-1=d_1\cdots d_r\geq d_r=D.
$$
Equality is forced, so $d_1\cdots d_{r-1}=1$. As every retained factor is greater than $1$, there can be only one. Consequently
$$
\boxed{U(\mathbb F)\cong\mathbb Z_{q-1}\text{ is cyclic}}.
$$
The same proof establishes the more general result <finite multiplicative subgroup of a field is cyclic>. The <square-free integer> result in part (c)(i) alone would not suffice, since $q-1$ need not be a <square-free integer>; the <root bound for a polynomial> rules out multiple <invariant factors of a finitely generated module> even then.
