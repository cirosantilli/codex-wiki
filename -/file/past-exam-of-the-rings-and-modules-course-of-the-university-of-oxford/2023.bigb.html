Multiplication in a <direct product of rings> is coordinatewise. Thus $(r_1,\ldots,r_n)$ has a two-sided inverse exactly when each $r_j$ has a two-sided inverse in $R_j$, and then
$$
(r_1,\ldots,r_n)^{-1}=(r_1^{-1},\ldots,r_n^{-1}).
$$
Consequently its <unit group> is the <direct product of groups>
$$
\boxed{U(R_1\times\cdots\times R_n)=U(R_1)\times\cdots\times U(R_n)}.
$$
The identification preserves multiplication because both sides use coordinatewise multiplication. This is the <unit group of a direct product of rings> formula.

Write $T=\prod_{j=1}^nR_j$, and let $\rho_j:T\to R_j$ be the coordinate <ring homomorphism>. Set $I_j=\rho_j(I)$. Each $I_j$ is an <ideal>: it is an additive subgroup, and multiplying a lift in $I$ on either side by a tuple with prescribed $j$th coordinate proves closure under left and right multiplication by $R_j$.

Certainly $I\subseteq\prod_jI_j$. Conversely, let $a=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)\in\prod_jI_j$. For each $j$, choose $b^{(j)}\in I$ with $j$th coordinate $a_j$, and let $e_j$ have $1_{R_j}$ in coordinate $j$ and zero elsewhere. Since $I$ is an <ideal>, $e_jb^{(j)}\in I$, and
$$
a=\sum_{j=1}^ne_jb^{(j)}\in I.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{I=I_1\times\cdots\times I_n}.
$$
The finite sum is essential to this proof of <ideals of a finite product of rings>.

The element $-1_R$ lies in the <unit group> and satisfies $(-1_R)^2=1_R$. Its <order of a group element> divides two and, by <Lagrange's theorem>, divides the odd number $|U(R)|$. Its order is therefore one, so $-1_R=1_R$ and $2\cdot1_R=0$.

It follows that
$$
\boxed{\mathbb F_2\longrightarrow R,\qquad\overline a\longmapsto a\cdot1_R}
$$
is a well-defined unital <ring homomorphism>. For a nonzero <ring>, this says its <characteristic of a ring> is two. If the <zero ring> is permitted, the same map still exists. This is the <odd unit group forces characteristic two> argument, with that harmless exception to the characteristic wording.

Since the <irreducible element> $x$ is not a <unit>, the <principal ideal> $(x)$ is proper. Suppose $(x)\subseteq J\subseteq R$. As $R$ is a <principal ideal domain>, write $J=(d)$. Then $x=dr$ for some $r\in R$. The definition of an <irreducible element> says that $d$ or $r$ is a <unit>.

If $d$ is a <unit>, then $J=R$. If $r$ is a <unit>, then $d$ and $x$ are <associate elements>, so $J=(x)$. Thus $(x)$ is a <maximal ideal>. By the permitted criterion that a <quotient ring> by a <maximal ideal> is a <field>,
$$
\boxed{R/(x)\text{ is a field}}.
$$

Every multiple of $x_1\cdots x_r$ is a multiple of each $x_j$, giving one inclusion. For the reverse inclusion, a <principal ideal domain> is a <unique factorization domain>, so each <irreducible element> $x_j$ is a <prime element>.

Let $a\in\bigcap_{j=1}^r(x_j)$. The case $a=0$ is immediate. Otherwise, write $a=x_1b$. Since $x_2\mid a$ and the <prime element> $x_2$ cannot divide the non-<associate> <irreducible element> $x_1$, it divides $b$. Write $a=x_1x_2b_2$, and repeat. At stage $j$, the <prime element> $x_j$ divides none of $x_1,\ldots,x_{j-1}$, so it divides the remaining factor. Hence $x_1\cdots x_r\mid a$, proving
$$
\boxed{(x_1\cdots x_r)=(x_1)\cap\cdots\cap(x_r)}.
$$

The <polynomial ring> $\mathbb F_2[X]$ is a <euclidean domain> under <polynomial degree>, hence a <principal ideal domain>. By part (b)(i), each
$$
K_j=\mathbb F_2[X]/(f_j)
$$
is a <field>. By <polynomial division>, every residue class has exactly one representative of <polynomial degree> less than $n_j$. Thus $1,\overline X,\ldots,\overline X^{n_j-1}$ form a <basis> over $\mathbb F_2$, and $|K_j|=2^{n_j}$.

For $j\ne k$, the <ideals> $(f_j)$ and $(f_k)$ are distinct <maximal ideals>, so they are <comaximal ideals>: their sum is an <ideal> properly containing $(f_j)$ and must be the whole <ring>. The <Chinese remainder theorem for ideals> and part (b)(ii) therefore give the <ring isomorphism>
$$
\boxed{\mathbb F_2[X]/(f_1\cdots f_r)\ \cong\ \prod_{j=1}^rK_j,\qquad\overline g\longmapsto(g+(f_j))_{j=1}^r}.
$$
In particular, this is a product of <finite fields> of the required sizes. The map preserves multiplication as well as addition and is surjective by the <Chinese remainder theorem for ideals>.

Choose a generator $u$ of the <cyclic group> $U(R)$, so $u^p=1$. Part (a)(iii) gives a unital <ring homomorphism> $\mathbb F_2\to R$. Its image lies in the centre of $R$, so even if $R$ is not a <commutative ring>, evaluating a <polynomial> at $u$ defines a <ring homomorphism>
$$
\phi:\mathbb F_2[X]\longrightarrow R,\qquad\sum_ja_jX^j\longmapsto\sum_ja_ju^j.
$$
Because $\phi(X^p-1)=0$, it factors through the <quotient ring> to give
$$
\boxed{\overline\phi:\mathbb F_2[X]/(X^p-1)\longrightarrow R,\qquad\overline X\longmapsto u}.
$$
Its image is the <subring> $S=\mathbb F_2[u]$. Every <unit> of $S$ is a <unit> of $R$, because an inverse in $S$ also lies in $R$. Conversely, every member of $U(R)$ is $u^j$ for some $j$, and both $u^j$ and its inverse $u^{p-j}$ lie in $S$. Hence
$$
\boxed{U(S)=U(R)}.
$$
The inverse argument matters: merely belonging to a <subring> would not by itself make an ambient <unit> invertible there.

Using the assumed <square-free polynomial> property, factor
$$
X^p-1=f_1\cdots f_r
$$
into distinct <monic polynomials> that are <irreducible polynomials>, with $n_j=\deg f_j$. Part (b)(iii) identifies $T=\mathbb F_2[X]/(X^p-1)$ with $\prod_jK_j$, where $|K_j|=2^{n_j}$.

By the <first isomorphism theorem for rings>, the image $S$ from part (c)(i) is $T/J$, where $J$ is the <kernel of a ring homomorphism>. By part (a)(ii), $J=\prod_jJ_j$ for <ideals> $J_j\subseteq K_j$. A <field> has only the zero <ideal> and the whole <field> as <ideals>, so each $K_j/J_j$ is either $K_j$ or the <zero ring>. Consequently, for a subset $D\subseteq\{1,\ldots,r\}$,
$$
S\cong\prod_{j\in D}K_j.
$$
This is the <quotient of a finite product of fields> principle. The subset is nonempty because $|U(S)|=p>2$, whereas the <zero ring> has only one <unit>. By part (a)(i),
$$
\boxed{p=|U(S)|=\prod_{j\in D}(2^{n_j}-1)}.
$$
Each factor is a positive <integer>. Since $p$ is a <prime number>, exactly one factor is $p$ and all remaining factors are one. Thus
$$
\boxed{p=2^n-1\text{ for some }n\geq2},
$$
so $p$ is a <Mersenne prime>. If $n=ab$ with $a,b>1$, then $2^a-1$ would be a proper divisor of $2^{ab}-1$; thus $n$ is itself a <prime number>, although only the displayed form is needed.

The assumed <square-free polynomial> property can also be checked directly: in <characteristic of a ring> two, the <formal derivative> of $X^p-1$ is $X^{p-1}$ because $p$ is odd. These two <polynomials> have <greatest common divisor> one, since $X$ does not divide $X^p-1$, so no repeated <irreducible polynomial> factor occurs.

A subset $E\subseteq M$ is a <basis of a module> if every $m\in M$ has a unique expression
$$
\boxed{m=\sum_{e\in E}r_ee,\qquad r_e\in R,\qquad r_e=0\text{ for all but finitely many }e}.
$$
Uniqueness concerns the entire family of coefficients, including the zero coefficients. Equivalently, $E$ generates $M$ and is <linearly independent in a module>: every finite relation $\sum_{e\in H}r_ee=0$, with $H\subseteq E$, has all $r_e=0$.

A <module> is a <free module> if it has a <basis of a module>. An infinite <basis of a module> still uses only finite sums in each expression; arbitrary infinite sums are not part of the <module> operations.

= Solution

**Yes.** An $\mathbb F$-<module> over a <field> is a <vector space>. Start with a finite generating list. If it is <linearly dependent>, one vector has a nonzero coefficient in a <linear relation>; dividing by that coefficient expresses the vector using the others, so it can be removed without changing the <span>. Repeating terminates with a <linearly independent> generating list, which is a <basis> and therefore a <basis of a module>.

Thus every <finitely generated module> over a <field> is a <free module>. For the zero <module>, the empty list is the required <basis of a module>.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

In the <direct sum of modules> $M=N\oplus P$, every $m\in M$ has a unique expression $m=n+p$, with $n\in N$ and $p\in P$. Define
$$
\boxed{\pi:M\to M,\qquad\pi(n+p)=p}.
$$
The uniqueness makes this well-defined. For $m=n+p$, $m'=n'+p'$ and $r\in R$,
$$
\pi(m+m')=p+p'=\pi(m)+\pi(m'),\qquad\pi(rm)=rp=r\pi(m),
$$
so $\pi$ is a <module homomorphism>. Finally, $\pi(n+p)=0$ exactly when $p=0$, equivalently $m\in N$. Therefore $\boxed{\ker\pi=N}$. Its image is $P$, and $\pi^2=\pi$, so it is the projection onto that <direct summand>.

= Solution

Put $N=\langle E'\rangle$ and $P=\langle E''\rangle$, the <submodules> generated by the two parts of the <basis of a module>. Every finite expression in the <basis of a module> splits into its $E'$ terms and its $E''$ terms. Thus $M=N+P$.

If $m\in N\cap P$, there are finite expressions
$$
m=\sum_{e\in H'}a_ee=\sum_{e\in H''}b_ee,
\qquad H'\subseteq E',\quad H''\subseteq E''.
$$
Subtracting gives a finite relation among distinct members of $E$, because $E'\cap E''=\varnothing$. Their <linear independence in a module> forces every coefficient to vanish, so $m=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{M=\langle E'\rangle\oplus\langle E''\rangle}.
$$
This proves the <basis partition gives a module direct sum> lemma, also when one part is empty.

Choose a countable generating family $(n_k)$ for $N$. For each $k$, the expression of $n_k$ in the <basis of a module> $E$ has a finite set $H_k$ of nonzero coefficients, its <coordinate support in a free module>. Let
$$
E'=\bigcup_kH_k.
$$
By the <countable union of countable sets> property, $E'$ is a <countable set>. Every $n_k$ belongs to $\langle E'\rangle$, so all finite $R$-linear combinations of the $n_k$ do too. Therefore
$$
\boxed{N\subseteq\langle E'\rangle\quad\text{for a countable }E'\subseteq E}.
$$
Here and below, <countable> includes finite. The same construction applies to a finite or empty generating family. This is the <countable basis support lemma>.

For $j\in\mathbb N_0$, let $e_j\in B$ have $e_j(j)=1$ and $e_j(k)=0$ for $k\ne j$. If $f,g\in F$ and $z\in\mathbb Z$, then $f+g$ can be nonzero only where $f$ or $g$ is nonzero, and $zf$ can be nonzero only where $f$ is nonzero. These are finite sets. Since $0\in F$, this proves that $F$ is a <submodule>.

Every $f\in F$ has the finite expression
$$
f=\sum_{j:\,f(j)\ne0}f(j)e_j.
$$
Evaluating at coordinate $k$ recovers the coefficient of $e_k$, so the expression is unique. Thus $\{e_j:j\in\mathbb N_0\}$ is a <basis of a module> for $F$, and
$$
\boxed{F\cong\bigoplus_{j\in\mathbb N_0}\mathbb Z}
$$
is a <free module>. By contrast, the ambient <module of integer sequences> $B=\prod_{j\in\mathbb N_0}\mathbb Z$ allows arbitrary integer sequences.

Under the temporary assumption that $E$ is a <basis of a module> for $B$, part (a)(v) applied to the countably generated <submodule> $F$ gives a <countable set> $E'\subseteq E$ such that $F\subseteq C=\langle E'\rangle$. By part (a)(iv),
$$
B=C\oplus\langle E\setminus E'\rangle.
$$
Use part (a)(iii) to project onto the second <direct summand>. This gives a <module homomorphism> $\pi:B\to B$ with
$$
\boxed{F\subseteq\ker\pi=C}.
$$
It remains to check the required size of this <kernel>. Enumerate $E'$ as $b_1,b_2,\ldots$, with the finite case treated the same way. Every element of $C$ is a finite sum of these elements with <integer> coefficients. For each $m$, the set $\langle b_1,\ldots,b_m\rangle$ is the image of the <countable set> $\mathbb Z^m$, so it is <countable>. Taking their <countable union> proves
$$
\boxed{\ker\pi\text{ is countable}}.
$$
This is the <countability of countably generated abelian groups> argument; it uses the countability of the scalar <ring> $\mathbb Z$.

For every $i\in\mathbb N_0$, truncate $w$ before coordinate $i$:
$$
f_i(j)=\begin{cases}w(j),&j<i,\\0,&j\geq i.\end{cases}
$$
This $f_i$ lies in $F$. For $j<i$, $(w-f_i)(j)=0$. For $j\geq i$, $(w-f_i)(j)=w(j)=\pm2^j$, which is divisible by $2^i$. In particular the integer sequence
$$
g_i(j)=\begin{cases}0,&j<i,\\w(j)/2^i,&j\geq i\end{cases}
$$
belongs to $B$ and satisfies $w=f_i+2^ig_i$.

Apply the <module homomorphism> $\pi$. Since $f_i\in F\subseteq\ker\pi$,
$$
\pi(w)=2^i\pi(g_i)\qquad\text{for every }i\in\mathbb N_0.
$$
Fixing any coordinate $j$, the <integer> $\pi(w)(j)$ is therefore divisible by $2^i$ for every $i$. The only such <integer> is zero: if $a\ne0$, choose $i$ with $2^i>|a|$, which rules out $a=2^ib$ for any nonzero <integer> $b$. Thus every coordinate of $\pi(w)$ vanishes, contradicting part (b)(iii).

It follows that
$$
\boxed{B=\prod_{j\in\mathbb N_0}\mathbb Z\text{ is not a free }\mathbb Z\text{-module}}.
$$
The decisive property is <vanishing of infinitely divisible integer sequences>, namely $\bigcap_{i\geq0}2^iB=\{0\}$.

Since $A^2+I=0$, its <minimal polynomial> $m_A$ divides $X^2+1$. The <polynomial> $X^2+1$ is an <irreducible polynomial> over the <rational numbers>: a reducible quadratic would have a rational root, whereas $r^2+1>0$ for every $r\in\mathbb Q$.

Because $n>0$, the constant <polynomial> one evaluates to the nonzero <identity matrix> $I$, so $m_A\ne1$. Both $m_A$ and $X^2+1$ are <monic polynomials>; hence the only remaining divisor is
$$
\boxed{m_A(X)=X^2+1}.
$$

The nonconstant <invariant factors of a linear operator> satisfy $d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_k=m_A$. By part (a)(i), $m_A=X^2+1$ is an <irreducible polynomial>, so every $d_j$ must equal $X^2+1$. Their <polynomial degrees> sum to $n$, giving $n=2k$.

The <companion matrix> of $X^2+1$, in the <basis> $(1,X)$ of $\mathbb Q[X]/(X^2+1)$, is
$$
C=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Therefore, for each fixed even $n$, every such $A$ has <rational canonical form>
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{C,\ldots,C}_{n/2\text{ blocks}})}.
$$
There are no such <matrices> when $n$ is odd. This is an instance of the <rational canonical form with irreducible minimal polynomial> principle; equality of <minimal polynomials> alone does not determine <rational canonical form> in general.

The <Gaussian integers> form an <integral domain>, since they are a <subring> of the <complex numbers>. Use the <Gaussian integer norm>
$$
N(a+bi)=a^2+b^2=|a+bi|^2,
$$
which is a positive <integer> for nonzero $a+bi$ and satisfies $N(\alpha\beta)=N(\alpha)N(\beta)$.

For $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb Z[i]$ with $\beta\ne0$, write $\alpha/\beta=x+iy$. Choose <integers> $m,n$ with $|x-m|\leq1/2$ and $|y-n|\leq1/2$, put $q=m+in$, and set $r=\alpha-\beta q$. Then
$$
\alpha=\beta q+r,\qquad
N(r)=N(\beta)\big((x-m)^2+(y-n)^2\big)\leq\tfrac12N(\beta)<N(\beta).
$$
Thus division with remainder has either $r=0$ or a strictly smaller <Gaussian integer norm>. This proves that $N$ is a <euclidean function> and
$$
\boxed{\mathbb Z[i]\text{ is a Euclidean domain}}.
$$

Over a <principal ideal domain> $D$, an $m$-by-$n$ <matrix> is in <Smith normal form> if all entries off the main diagonal vanish and its nonzero diagonal entries $d_1,\ldots,d_r$ occur first and satisfy
$$
\boxed{d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_r}.
$$
All remaining diagonal entries are zero. The zero <matrix> is allowed, with $r=0$. For an arbitrary <matrix> $M$, the <Smith normal form> theorem gives <invertible matrices> $P\in\operatorname{GL}_m(D)$ and $Q\in\operatorname{GL}_n(D)$ with
$$
PMQ=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_r,0,\ldots,0),
$$
where the right side is interpreted as a rectangular diagonal <matrix> when necessary. The $d_j$ are determined up to multiplication by <units>. Positivity can be required over $\mathbb Z$, but is not a condition over the <Gaussian integers>.

Regard $A$ as a <matrix> over the <rational numbers>. Part (a)(ii), with $n=2$, gives its <characteristic polynomial> $X^2+1$. Therefore, writing $i$ for the <imaginary unit>,
$$
\det(iI-A)=i^2+1=0.
$$
The <matrix> $M=iI-A$ is not zero: otherwise $A=iI$ would have noninteger diagonal entries. It consequently has <rank of a matrix> one over the <fraction field> $\mathbb Q(i)$ of the <Gaussian integers>.

By part (b)(i), $\mathbb Z[i]$ is a <euclidean domain>, hence a <principal ideal domain>, so apply <Smith normal form>. There exist $P,Q\in\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbb Z[i])$ such that
$$
P(iI-A)Q=\begin{pmatrix}d&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad d\ne0.
$$
Indeed, invertible multiplication preserves <rank of a matrix>, so exactly one diagonal entry is nonzero. For $e_2=(0,1)^{\mathsf T}$, multiplication on the right gives $P(iI-A)Qe_2=0$. Since $P$ is an <invertible matrix>,
$$
\boxed{(iI-A)Qe_2=0}.
$$
The <Smith normal form supplies a kernel basis> lemma ensures that the null vector $Qe_2$ is a column of an <invertible matrix> over $\mathbb Z[i]$; an arbitrary nonzero solution over $\mathbb Q(i)$ would not itself establish that requirement.

Each entry of the <matrix> $Q$ from part (b)(iii) has unique real and imaginary <integer> coefficients. Write
$$
Q=U+iV,\qquad U,V\in M_2(\mathbb Z).
$$
It already belongs to the <general linear group> $\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbb Z[i])$. Expanding the relation from part (b)(iii) gives
$$
0=(iI-A)(U+iV)e_2=(-AU-V)e_2+i(U-AV)e_2.

The real and imaginary parts must both vanish, so
$$
\boxed{AUe_2=-Ve_2,\qquad AVe_2=Ue_2,\qquad U+iV\in\operatorname{GL}_2(\mathbb Z[i])}.

These are the required identities, with $e_2=(0,1)^{\mathsf T}$.
