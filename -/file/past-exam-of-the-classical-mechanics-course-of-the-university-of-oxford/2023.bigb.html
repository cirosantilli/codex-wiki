= Solution
{parent=a}

Each <simple pendulum> moves in the vertical plane containing the pivots. Ignoring contact between the ideal rods and <point masses>, each rod can turn through a full <circle>. Thus the <configuration space> is the <torus>
$$
\boxed{\mathcal Q=S^1\times S^1.}
$$
Choose <generalized coordinates> $\theta_1,\theta_2$, measured from the downward vertical, positive towards the right and understood modulo $2\pi$. These <angles> are local coordinates on the <torus>, rather than a single globally one-to-one real <coordinate chart>. With upward Cartesian coordinate $y$, the bob <positions> are
$$
\mathbf r_1=(l\sin\theta_1,-l\cos\theta_1),\qquad
\mathbf r_2=(r+l\sin\theta_2,-l\cos\theta_2).
$$
Their <velocities> satisfy $|\dot{\mathbf r}_j|^2=l^2\dot\theta_j^2$, so <kinetic energy from Cartesian position vectors> gives
$$
T=\frac{ml^2}{2}(\dot\theta_1^2+\dot\theta_2^2).
$$
The <spring> length is the <Euclidean distance>
$$
d=|\mathbf r_2-\mathbf r_1|
=\sqrt{\bigl(r+l(\sin\theta_2-\sin\theta_1)\bigr)^2+l^2(\cos\theta_2-\cos\theta_1)^2}.
$$
Taking gravitational <potential energy> zero at pivot height gives $V_g=-mgl(\cos\theta_1+\cos\theta_2)$. The <spring> <potential energy> is $V_s=k(d-r)^2/2$, since $r$ is its natural length. Consequently the <Lagrangian> is
$$
\boxed{L=T-V_g-V_s
=\frac{ml^2}{2}(\dot\theta_1^2+\dot\theta_2^2)
+mgl(\cos\theta_1+\cos\theta_2)-\frac{k}{2}(d-r)^2.}
$$
In the paper's notation $t(\theta_1,\theta_2)=V_s$; this function is distinct from the time variable. This is the <exact Lagrangian of two spring-coupled pendulums>.

One alternative <Lagrangian> is
$$
\boxed{\widetilde L=L-2mgl.}
$$
It corresponds to setting gravitational <potential energy> zero at the downward position. The constant disappears from every <Euler-Lagrange equation>. More generally, <total-time-derivative invariance of a Lagrangian> allows $\widetilde L=L+dF(\theta_1,\theta_2,t)/dt$ for any smooth $F$.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Set $l=m=g=1$. At $(\theta_1,\theta_2)=(0,0)$ the <spring> has $d=r$, so its <potential energy> has zero <gradient>: $\partial_jV_s=k(d-r)\partial_jd=0$. The gravitational <potential energy> has $\partial_jV_g=\sin\theta_j=0$ there. Zero <velocities> therefore satisfy the <Euler-Lagrange equations>, proving this is an <equilibrium>.

For fixed $r>0$, let $\varepsilon=\max(|\theta_1|,|\theta_2|)$ and $\delta=\theta_2-\theta_1$. The <Taylor series> give
$$
\sin\theta_2-\sin\theta_1=\delta+O(\varepsilon^3),\qquad
\cos\theta_2-\cos\theta_1=O(\varepsilon^2).
$$
Thus, near the downward <equilibrium>, the horizontal separation is positive and
$$
d=\sqrt{\bigl(r+\delta+O(\varepsilon^3)\bigr)^2+O(\varepsilon^4)}
=r+\delta+O(\varepsilon^3).
$$
In particular, $V_s=k\delta^2/2+O(\varepsilon^4)$ and $V_g=-2+(\theta_1^2+\theta_2^2)/2+O(\varepsilon^4)$. Dropping the additive constant and terms beyond quadratic order gives the <harmonic approximation>
$$
\boxed{L_{\mathrm{quadratic}}=\frac12(\dot\theta_1^2+\dot\theta_2^2)
-\frac12\left(\theta_1^2+\theta_2^2+k(\theta_1-\theta_2)^2\right).}
$$
For physical <spring constant> $k\geq0$, the quadratic <potential energy> is a <positive-definite quadratic form>. The downward position is therefore a <stable equilibrium in Lagrangian mechanics>, and its nearby motion admits <small oscillations>.

The <Euler-Lagrange equations> of the quadratic <Lagrangian> are
$$
\ddot{\boldsymbol\theta}+\mathsf K\boldsymbol\theta=0,\qquad
\mathsf K=\begin{pmatrix}1+k&-k\\-k&1+k\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The <mechanical mass matrix> is the identity. A <normal mode> $\boldsymbol\theta=\mathbf a\cos(\omega t+\phi)$ therefore satisfies the <eigenvalue problem> $\mathsf K\mathbf a=\omega^2\mathbf a$. Direct multiplication gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c}
&\text{angular frequency}&\text{mode vector}\\\hline
\text{in phase}&\omega_+=1&(1,1)\\
\text{opposite phase}&\omega_-=\sqrt{1+2k}&(1,-1)
\end{array}}
$$
These are <normal modes of two spring-coupled equal pendulums>. The corresponding <normal mode coordinates> $u=\theta_1+\theta_2$ and $v=\theta_1-\theta_2$ obey $\ddot u+u=0$ and $\ddot v+(1+2k)v=0$.

For the in-phase <normal mode>, the bobs undergo identical <displacements>, leaving their separation exactly $r$. The <spring> stays at its natural length and exerts no <force>, so this <angular frequency> is independent of $k$. The value $1$ is the individual <simple pendulum> <angular frequency> $\sqrt{g/l}$ in the chosen units.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Write $w=\sqrt{1+2k}$. The <initial conditions> on the <normal mode coordinates> are $u(0)=v(0)=0$ and $\dot u(0)=\dot v(0)=k$. Solving the two <harmonic oscillator equations> and recovering the original <angles> gives the exact solution within the <harmonic approximation>:
$$
\boxed{\theta_1(t)=\frac{k}{2}\left(\sin t+\frac{\sin wt}{w}\right),\qquad
\theta_2(t)=\frac{k}{2}\left(\sin t-\frac{\sin wt}{w}\right).}
$$
Their <angular velocities> are $\dot\theta_1=k(\cos t+\cos wt)/2$ and $\dot\theta_2=k(\cos t-\cos wt)/2$, which verify all four <initial conditions>.

To expose the <beat>, define $\Delta=w-1$ and $\Omega=(w+1)/2$. Replacing $1/w$ by $1$ changes each <angle> by $O(k^2)$ uniformly in time. The sum-to-product identities then give
$$
\begin{aligned}
\theta_1&=k\sin(\Omega t)\cos(\Delta t/2)+O(k^2),&
\dot\theta_1&=k\cos(\Omega t)\cos(\Delta t/2),\\
\theta_2&=-k\cos(\Omega t)\sin(\Delta t/2)+O(k^2),&
\dot\theta_2&=k\sin(\Omega t)\sin(\Delta t/2).
\end{aligned}
$$
The displayed <angular velocities> are exact in the linear system. The <absolute and signed beat envelopes> have leading <amplitudes> $k|\cos(\Delta t/2)|$ and $k|\sin(\Delta t/2)|$. Because $\Delta=k+O(k^2)$, at $\tau=\pi/k$ we have $\Delta\tau/2=\pi/2+O(k)$ and $\Omega\tau=\tau+\pi/2+O(k)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\theta_1(\tau)&=O(k^2),&\dot\theta_1(\tau)&=O(k^2),\\
\theta_2(\tau)&=k\sin\tau+O(k^2),&\dot\theta_2(\tau)&=k\cos\tau+O(k^2).
\end{aligned}}
$$
The first <simple pendulum> has negligible <oscillation> <amplitude>. Its <kinetic energy> is $T_1(\tau)=O(k^4)$, while the second has $T_2(\tau)=k^2\cos^2\tau/2+O(k^3)$. Thus, to the retained order, all instantaneous <kinetic energy> belongs to the second <simple pendulum>. The fast phase can make that <kinetic energy> small: the whole initial <energy> $k^2/2$ is transferred as the second <simple pendulum>'s combined <kinetic energy> and gravitational <potential energy>, since
$$
\frac12\left(\dot\theta_2(\tau)^2+\theta_2(\tau)^2\right)=\frac{k^2}{2}+O(k^3),
\qquad V_s(\tau)=O(k^3).
$$
This is <energy transfer after an impulse in two weakly coupled oscillators>. It does not require $\cos\tau=1$.

At $2\tau$, $\Delta(2\tau)/2=\pi+O(k)$, so the <beat> has returned the <oscillation> to the first <simple pendulum>:
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\theta_1(2\tau)&=k\sin(2\tau)+O(k^2),&\dot\theta_1(2\tau)&=k\cos(2\tau)+O(k^2),\\
\theta_2(2\tau)&=O(k^2),&\dot\theta_2(2\tau)&=O(k^2).
\end{aligned}}
$$
The <energy> localization returns, although the fast <oscillation> phase need not equal its initial phase. The exact first minimum of the leading <beat> envelope is at $\pi/\Delta$, whose leading value is $\pi/k$. Keeping the slow phase $\Delta t$ is essential: expanding $\sin(wt)$ at fixed $t$ and then using $t=O(1/k)$ would lose the <energy> exchange. Although <angles> are retained only to order $k$, their leading <energies> are of order $k^2$.

Take the origin at the <centre of mass>, so $\int_B\rho\mathbf r\,d^3r=0$, and write $r_i=\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf e_i$ in the specified <orthonormal basis>. The <inertia tensor> is
$$
\boxed{I_{ij}=\int_B\rho(\mathbf r)\left(|\mathbf r|^2\delta_{ij}-r_ir_j\right)\,d^3r.}
$$
Here $\delta_{ij}$ is the <Kronecker delta>. For a <rigid body> with <angular velocity> $\boldsymbol\omega$, this <tensor> gives $\mathbf L=\mathbf I\boldsymbol\omega$ and <rotational kinetic energy> $T=\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf I\boldsymbol\omega/2$.

The <inertia tensor> is a real <symmetric matrix>, so it admits an <orthonormal basis> of <eigenvectors>. The lines in these <eigenvector> directions through the <centre of mass> are its <principal axes>; the corresponding <eigenvalues> $I_1,I_2,I_3$ are its <principal moments of inertia>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf I\mathbf e_i=I_i\mathbf e_i,\qquad
\mathbf I=\operatorname{diag}(I_1,I_2,I_3)\quad\text{in principal axes}.}
$$
If an <eigenvalue> repeats, the corresponding <principal axes> need not be unique.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Put $\mathbf r=(x,y,z)$ with $\mathbf e_3$ along $z$, and interpret the symmetry as <axisymmetry> of the whole <mass density>. A half-turn about $\mathbf e_3$ sends $(x,y,z)$ to $(-x,-y,z)$ and leaves the integration domain and <mass density> invariant. Therefore $\int\rho xz\,d^3r=\int\rho yz\,d^3r=0$. A quarter-turn sends $(x,y,z)$ to $(-y,x,z)$; it gives
$$
\int\rho xy\,d^3r=-\int\rho xy\,d^3r=0,\qquad
\int\rho x^2\,d^3r=\int\rho y^2\,d^3r.
$$
All off-diagonal entries of the <inertia tensor> consequently vanish. Writing $s^2=x^2+y^2$, the <principal moments of inertia> satisfy
$$
\boxed{I_1=I_2=\int_B\rho\left(\frac{s^2}{2}+z^2\right)\,d^3r,\qquad
I_3=\int_B\rho s^2\,d^3r.}
$$
Thus every <orthonormal basis> with third vector along the symmetry axis is a <principal body frame>. This is <axisymmetry of the inertia tensor>. In general there is no requirement that $I_3=I_1$.

For a vector $\mathbf A=\sum_i A_i\mathbf e_i$ in the <principal body frame>, the <derivative of a body-fixed basis vector> is $(d\mathbf e_i/dt)_{\widehat S}=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf e_i$. Differentiating the expansion gives the <rotating-frame derivative formula>, also called the Coriolis formula:
$$
\left(\frac{d\mathbf A}{dt}\right)_{\widehat S}
=\left(\frac{d\mathbf A}{dt}\right)_S+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf A.
$$
Apply this to <angular momentum> $\mathbf L=\sum_iI_i\omega_i\mathbf e_i$. The <principal moments of inertia> are constant in the <principal body frame>, so external <torque> balance becomes
$$
\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf I\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}+\boldsymbol\omega\times(\mathbf I\boldsymbol\omega).
$$
Resolving the <cross product> gives <Euler's equations (rigid body dynamics)>:
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
I_1\dot\omega_1+(I_3-I_2)\omega_2\omega_3&=\tau_1,\\
I_2\dot\omega_2+(I_1-I_3)\omega_3\omega_1&=\tau_2,\\
I_3\dot\omega_3+(I_2-I_1)\omega_1\omega_2&=\tau_3.
\end{aligned}}
$$
All components and dotted components in this display refer to the <principal body frame>.

For the <symmetric top>, set $I_1=I_2=J$. The third of <Euler's equations (rigid body dynamics)> reduces to $I_3\dot\omega_3=\tau_3=0$. Thus the axial <angular velocity> component is a <conserved quantity>:
$$
\boxed{\omega_3(t)=\omega_3(0).}
$$
The transverse <angular velocity> components are generally variable. A <torque> constant in the <principal body frame> generally rotates in the <inertial frame>; constancy in the two frames is different.

Let $J=I_1=I_2$, $\omega_3=c$ and
$$
z(t)=\omega_1(t)+i\omega_2(t),\qquad
\nu=\frac{(I_3-J)c}{J}.
$$
Here $c$ and $\nu$ are constant. The first two of <Euler's equations (rigid body dynamics)> give $\dot\omega_1=-\nu\omega_2+\tau_0/J$ and $\dot\omega_2=\nu\omega_1+\tau_0/J$. Combining them produces
$$
\boxed{\dot z=i\nu z+\frac{\tau_0}{J}(1+i).}
$$
Multiplication by the <integrating factor> $e^{-i\nu t}$ gives, for $\nu\ne0$,
$$
z(t)=z(0)e^{i\nu t}+\frac{\tau_0(1+i)}{i\nu J}\left(e^{i\nu t}-1\right).
$$
For completeness, $\nu=0$ instead gives $z(t)=z(0)+\tau_0(1+i)t/J$. These formulas describe a <symmetric top under constant transverse body torque>.

The stated <initial conditions> require $b\ne0$ and $I_3\ne J$. They give $c=b/(I_3-J)$, $\nu=b/J$ and $z(0)=i\tau_0/b$. Substitution and simplification yield
$$
z(t)=\frac{\tau_0}{b}\left(e^{ibt/J}-1+i\right).
$$
Taking the <real part> and <imaginary part>, the <angular velocity> in the <principal body frame> is
$$
\boxed{\boldsymbol\omega(t)=\left(
\frac{\tau_0}{b}\left[\cos\left(\frac{bt}{I_1}\right)-1\right],\quad
\frac{\tau_0}{b}\left[\sin\left(\frac{bt}{I_1}\right)+1\right],\quad
\frac{b}{I_3-I_1}\right).}
$$
Its value at $t=0$ is the required <initial condition>; differentiating the first two components verifies both forced <Euler's equations (rigid body dynamics)>.

Now take zero <torque> and $\boldsymbol\omega(0)=(1,0,1)$. The axial <angular velocity> is $\omega_3=1$ and the complex transverse component obeys $\dot z=i\nu z$ with $z(0)=1$ and $\nu=(I_3-I_1)/I_1$. Hence the <angular momentum> components in the <principal body frame> are
$$
\boxed{\mathbf L_S(t)=\left(I_1\cos\nu t,\ I_1\sin\nu t,\ I_3\right),\qquad
\nu=\frac{I_3-I_1}{I_1}.}
$$
The transverse projection therefore traces a <circle> with signed <angular frequency> $\nu$. Its magnitude is constant, $L=\sqrt{I_1^2+I_3^2}$.

In the <inertial frame>, zero <torque> means that $\mathbf L$ is fixed. Using
$$
\mathbf L=I_1\boldsymbol\omega+(I_3-I_1)\omega_3\mathbf e_3
$$
and the <derivative of a body-fixed basis vector>, the symmetry axis satisfies
$$
\dot{\mathbf e}_3=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf e_3
=\frac{\mathbf L}{I_1}\times\mathbf e_3.
$$
Thus there is uniform <torque-free precession of a symmetric top> about the fixed <angular momentum>, at a constant cone <angle> $\beta$ and space <angular frequency>
$$
\boxed{\cos\beta=\frac{I_3}{\sqrt{I_1^2+I_3^2}},\qquad
\Omega_{\mathrm{space}}=\frac{\sqrt{I_1^2+I_3^2}}{I_1}.}
$$
The cone <angle> stays constant, with no <nutation>. The <body-frame and space-frame frequencies of free symmetric-top precession> differ: $\nu$ describes rotating components, whereas $\Omega_{\mathrm{space}}$ describes the symmetry axis in space. The formulas also cover $I_3=I_1$, where the <angular momentum> components in the <principal body frame> are constant.

Define the <canonical momenta> by
$$
\boxed{p_i=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}(\mathbf q,\dot{\mathbf q},t),\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.}
$$
To obtain an ordinary unconstrained <Hamiltonian>, assume a <regular Lagrangian>: the velocity <Hessian matrix> $\bigl(\partial^2L/\partial\dot q_i\partial\dot q_j\bigr)$ is invertible. The <inverse function theorem> then lets us solve locally for $\dot{\mathbf q}=\mathbf v(\mathbf q,\mathbf p,t)$. The <phase space> is the <cotangent bundle> $T^*\mathcal Q$, with local <canonical coordinates> $(\mathbf q,\mathbf p)$ and dimension $2n$. The <Legendre transform in mechanics> defines
$$
\boxed{H(\mathbf q,\mathbf p,t)=\sum_{i=1}^np_iv_i(\mathbf q,\mathbf p,t)
-L\bigl(\mathbf q,\mathbf v(\mathbf q,\mathbf p,t),t\bigr).}
$$
The velocities in this formula have been eliminated in favour of <phase space> variables. A <hyperregular Lagrangian> makes this construction global; regularity alone guarantees it locally. <Hamilton's equations> are
$$
\boxed{\dot q_i=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i},\qquad
\dot p_i=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.}
$$

In <canonical coordinates> on <phase space>, the <Poisson bracket> is
$$
\boxed{\{f,g\}=\sum_{i=1}^n\left(
\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i}
-\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q_i}\right).}
$$
It treats $t$ as a parameter. Along a solution of <Hamilton's equations>, the <chain rule> gives
$$
\frac{df}{dt}=\sum_i\left(f_{q_i}\dot q_i+f_{p_i}\dot p_i\right)+f_t
=\sum_i\left(f_{q_i}H_{p_i}-f_{p_i}H_{q_i}\right)+f_t.
$$
Therefore the <Hamiltonian conservation law> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{df}{dt}=\{f,H\}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}.}
$$
The total <derivative> follows the trajectory; the explicit <partial derivative> holds $(\mathbf q,\mathbf p)$ fixed.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The <Poisson bracket> is antisymmetric, so $\{H,H\}=0$. The <Hamiltonian energy balance> consequently gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{dH}{dt}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial t}=0.}
$$
For a <regular Lagrangian> the <Legendre transform in mechanics> gives $H_t=-L_t$, evaluated at the corresponding velocity. In particular, a <Lagrangian> with no explicit time dependence has <time-translation symmetry>. <Noether theorem> associates this continuous <symmetry> of the <action> with the <conserved quantity> $\sum_i\dot q_i\partial L/\partial\dot q_i-L=H$. For an ordinary natural mechanical <Lagrangian> $L=T-V$, with $T$ quadratic in the velocities, this quantity is the total <energy> $T+V$.

= Solution

Set $D=\partial_t+\{\,\cdot\,,H\}$. The hypotheses mean $Df=Dg=0$ as identities on <phase space>. By the <Jacobi identity for the Poisson bracket>,
$$
\{\{f,g\},H\}=\{\{f,H\},g\}+\{f,\{g,H\}\}.
$$
Also, the <product rule> applied to the defining coordinate formula gives
$$
\partial_t\{f,g\}=\{f_t,g\}+\{f,g_t\}.
$$
Adding these identities proves
$$
D\{f,g\}=\{Df,g\}+\{f,Dg\}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\{f,g\}=0.}
$$
This proves the <Poisson bracket of time-dependent conserved quantities> theorem, including explicit time dependence of $f$ and $g$.

= Solution

Since $H$ is a <conserved quantity>, the <Hamiltonian energy balance> gives $H_t=0$. Since $f$ is a <conserved quantity>, $f_t+\{f,H\}=0$. Take an explicit <partial derivative> with respect to time, holding the <phase space> coordinates fixed:
$$
f_{tt}+\{f_t,H\}+\{f,H_t\}=0.
$$
The last <Poisson bracket> vanishes because $H_t=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\right)
=f_{tt}+\{f_t,H\}=0.}
$$
This is the <time derivative of a conserved quantity for an autonomous Hamiltonian>. The conservation hypotheses are understood to hold for every trajectory, as identities on <phase space>.

= Solution

Work on $I>0$, with a local choice of the periodic <angle> $\theta$. The inverse formulas are
$$
I=\frac12\left(\alpha q^2+\frac{p^2}{\alpha}\right),\qquad
\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(\alpha q,p)\pmod{2\pi}.
$$
In particular, $\theta_q=p/(2I)$, $\theta_p=-q/(2I)$, $I_q=\alpha q$ and $I_p=p/\alpha$. Their <Poisson bracket> is
$$
\boxed{\{\theta,I\}=\frac{p^2}{2I\alpha}+\frac{\alpha q^2}{2I}=1.}
$$
The other fundamental <Poisson brackets> vanish by antisymmetry. Thus this is a <canonical transformation> for every $\alpha>0$, on local charts away from the origin. At $I=0$ the <angle> is undefined.

In the new coordinates the <harmonic oscillator> <Hamiltonian> is
$$
H_0(\theta,I)=I\left(\frac{\alpha}{m}\cos^2\theta
+\frac{m\omega^2}{\alpha}\sin^2\theta\right).
$$
For $I$ to be a <conserved quantity> throughout <phase space>, <Hamilton's equations> require $\dot I=-\partial_\theta H_0=0$ identically. The two coefficients must coincide. For the usual $m>0$ and $\omega>0$, positivity of $\alpha$ selects
$$
\boxed{\alpha=m\omega,\qquad H_0=\omega I,\qquad
I(t)=I(0),\qquad\theta(t)=\theta(0)+\omega t\pmod{2\pi}.}
$$
These are <harmonic oscillator action-angle variables>; the <action variable> is $I=H_0/\omega$.

A <type-two generating function for a canonical transformation> uses the mixed variables $(q,P)$ and defines
$$
\boxed{p=\frac{\partial F_2}{\partial q},\qquad
Q=\frac{\partial F_2}{\partial P}.}
$$
For a local invertible <canonical transformation>, require $F_{2,qP}\ne0$ so that $p=F_{2,q}(q,P)$ can be solved for $P$. The differential identity $dF_2=p\,dq+Q\,dP$ gives $d(F_2-PQ)=p\,dq-P\,dQ$, expressing preservation of the canonical <symplectic form>.

= Solution

On the interior of the principal inverse-cosine branch, $0<q<\pi$, the given <canonical transformation> has
$$
Q=\cos q\cos P,\qquad
\sqrt{1-Q^2\sec^2P}=\sin q,\qquad
p=-\sin q\sin P.
$$
Integrating $Q=\partial_PF_2$ suggests $F_2=\cos q\sin P+C(q)$. Its other derivative is $\partial_qF_2=-\sin q\sin P+C'(q)$; comparison with $p$ requires $C'(q)=0$. Hence a suitable <type-two generating function for a canonical transformation> is
$$
\boxed{F_2(q,P)=\cos q\sin P.}
$$
Both derivative equations reproduce the specified transformation exactly. Its mixed derivative is $F_{2,qP}=-\sin q\cos P$, so it defines a local <canonical transformation> where $0<q<\pi$ and $\cos P\ne0$, equivalently where $|Q/\cos P|<1$. At $q=0,\pi$ or $\cos P=0$, the stated formulas fail to give a regular coordinate chart. These restrictions explain the square-root branch and the local meaning of canonicity. This is a <trigonometric type-two canonical transformation>.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For a time-dependent <type-two generating function for a canonical transformation>, the differential identity
$$
p\,dq-H\,dt=P\,dQ-K\,dt+d(F_2-PQ)
$$
gives $K=H+\partial_tF_2$, with the <partial derivative> taken at fixed $(q,P)$. Here $F_2=\cos q\sin P$ has no explicit time dependence, so the <Hamiltonian> correction is
$$
\boxed{\Lambda(Q,P,t)=0,\qquad
K(Q,P,t)=H\left(\arccos\frac{Q}{\cos P},
-\sin P\sqrt{1-Q^2\sec^2P},t\right).}
$$
As always, adding a function of time alone to the new <Hamiltonian> leaves <Hamilton's equations> unchanged. Choosing a time-independent <type-two generating function for a canonical transformation> fixes that irrelevant freedom and gives the displayed $\Lambda=0$.
