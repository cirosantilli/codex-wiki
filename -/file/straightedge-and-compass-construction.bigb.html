= Straightedge and compass construction
{wiki}

= Compass-and-straightedge construction
{synonym}

A <compass-and-straightedge construction> uses an unmarked straightedge to draw a <Euclidean line> through two already constructed points, and a compass to draw a <circle> with a constructed centre and radius. New points arise from intersections of these lines and circles. The allowed operations matter: an accurate numerical drawing need not supply such a construction.

= General angle quintisection is impossible with straightedge and compass
{parent=Straightedge and compass construction}
{title2=$96c^5-120c^3+30c-5=0$}

Consider the constructible angle whose cosine is $5/6$ and sine is $\sqrt{11}/6$. If one fifth of this angle were constructible, its cosine $c$ would satisfy the displayed polynomial, by the identity $\cos5\theta=16\cos^5\theta-20\cos^3\theta+5\cos\theta$. The identity follows by taking the real part of $(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^5$ and substituting $\sin^2\theta=1-\cos^2\theta$.

The polynomial is irreducible by <Eisenstein's criterion> at five, so $c$ has degree five over the rational numbers. The initial angle field $\mathbb Q(\sqrt{11})$ already has degree two, and adjoining any finite collection of <constructible numbers> gives total degree over the rational numbers a power of two. Such a field cannot contain the degree-five element $c$, by the <tower law for field extensions>. Hence this constructible angle cannot be divided into five equal angles by <straightedge and compass construction>. Some particular angles can be quintisected; the obstruction disproves a construction valid for all angles.

= Impossibility of doubling the cube by straightedge and compass
{parent=Straightedge and compass construction}
{title2=$[\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]2):\mathbb Q]=3$}

Doubling the volume of a unit cube requires constructing its new side length $\sqrt[3]2$. Its minimal polynomial $X^3-2$ is irreducible by <Eisenstein's criterion> at two, so this length has degree three. Every <constructible number> belongs to a finite tower of quadratic extensions, whose total degree is a power of two. The <tower law for field extensions> would force the degree three to divide that power of two, a contradiction. Thus no such <straightedge and compass construction> exists.

= Constructible polygon
{parent=Straightedge and compass construction}
{wiki}

A <constructible polygon> is a <polygon> whose vertices can be obtained by a <compass-and-straightedge construction> from the specified initial data. The classical question for a <regular polygon> asks which numbers of sides permit an exact construction from a given side or circumscribed <circle>.

= Constructible regular polygon
{parent=Constructible polygon}

A <constructible regular polygon> is a <regular polygon> admitting a <compass-and-straightedge construction>. The <Gauss-Wantzel theorem> classifies its possible numbers of sides.

= Gauss-Wantzel theorem
{c}
{parent=Constructible regular polygon}
{title2=$N=2^a p_1\cdots p_r$}

For $N\geq3$, a <regular polygon> with $N$ sides is a <constructible regular polygon> exactly when $N=2^a p_1\cdots p_r$, where $a$ is a <nonnegative integer> and the $p_j$ are distinct <Fermat primes>; the product may be empty. Thus seventeen and fifteen qualify, while nine does not. <Carl Friedrich Gauss> established sufficiency, and <Pierre Wantzel> proved necessity in 1837.<Primality testing: then and now by Carl Pomerance>[slide 18]{ref}
