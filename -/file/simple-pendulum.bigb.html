= Simple pendulum
{title2=$\ddot\theta+(g/\ell)\sin\theta=0$}

A point mass constrained to a fixed-length massless arm under uniform gravitational <acceleration> is a simple pendulum. With no drag its angular equation is $\ddot\theta+(g/\ell)\sin\theta=0$. The <small-angle approximation> yields <simple harmonic motion>, whereas the full restoring force is nonlinear. Adding drag gives a <damped pendulum>.

For an ideal point mass $m$ swinging in a vertical plane, the <torque> about the pivot is $-mg\ell\sin\theta$. Dividing by its <moment of inertia> $m\ell^2$ gives the displayed equation. Both the inertia and the gravitational <torque> scale with $m$, explaining why the point mass cancels. This derivation and the small-amplitude period are given in https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/15-4-pendulums[Ling, Sanny and Moebs, University Physics, pendulums].

Released from rest at a small angular <amplitude> $\theta_0$, the linearized solution is $\theta(t)\simeq\theta_0\cos(\sqrt{g/\ell}\,t)$, with <oscillation period> $T_0=2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}$. For $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, lengths one and four metres give $T_0\approx2.0061\,\mathrm s$ and $4.0121\,\mathrm s$. This same scaling follows from <dimensional analysis>; the dynamical calculation supplies the coefficient $2\pi$.

Finite angular <amplitude> changes the exact ideal period. <Conservation of energy> gives $\dot\theta^2=(2g/\ell)(\cos\theta-\cos\theta_0)$ for release from rest. Integrating a quarter oscillation and substituting $\sin(\theta/2)=\sin(\theta_0/2)\sin\phi$ gives
$$
T(\theta_0)=4\sqrt{\frac\ell g}\,K\!\left(\sin\frac{\theta_0}2\right),\qquad
\frac{T(\theta_0)}{T_0}=\frac2\pi\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\phi}{\sqrt{1-\sin^2(\theta_0/2)\sin^2\phi}},\quad 0<\theta_0<\pi.
$$
Here $K$ is the <complete elliptic integral of the first kind>. The <small-angle approximation> gives a ratio tending to one; at $10^\circ,60^\circ,90^\circ$, the ratios are approximately $1.00191,1.07318,1.18034$. Thus almost constant period at small <amplitude> has a quantitative limit. As $\theta_0\to\pi$, the period diverges, connecting to the <pendulum separatrix approach takes infinite time>.

\Image[pendulum-period-scaling.png]
{title=Length scaling and finite-amplitude period corrections for an ideal simple pendulum}

These predictions assume the specified ideal system. Drag changes the <mechanical energy>; a massive support changes the <moment of inertia>. The distinction is useful in experiments: a change in period can test an approximation rather than merely indicate an arithmetic mistake.

= Spherical pendulum
{parent=Simple pendulum}
{title2=$L=\frac12mr^2(\dot\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\dot\phi^2)+mgr\cos\theta$}
{wiki}

A <point mass> constrained to a fixed <distance> $r$ from a pivot and free to move in three dimensions under uniform <gravitational acceleration>. With the vertical axis downward and <polar angle> $\theta$ measured from it, the <Lagrangian> is the expression in the title. The <azimuth> $\phi$ is a <cyclic coordinate>, so $mr^2\sin^2\theta\dot\phi$ is conserved <angular momentum> about the vertical axis. A rigid massless rod enforces the constraint without the loss-of-tension limitation of a string.

= Small nutation about a conical pendulum
{parent=Spherical pendulum}
{title2=$\omega_\theta^2=\frac gl\frac{1+3\cos^2\theta_0}{\cos\theta_0}$}

For a <spherical pendulum> with nonzero conserved vertical <angular momentum> $h$, eliminate the azimuthal <angular velocity> using $h=ml^2\sin^2\theta\dot\phi$. The polar <effective potential> is $V_{\mathrm{eff}}=h^2/(2ml^2\sin^2\theta)-mgl\cos\theta$. A <conical pendulum> at $0<\theta_0<\pi/2$ is a minimum satisfying $h^2=m^2gl^3\sin^4\theta_0/\cos\theta_0$. <Linearization> at fixed $h$ yields polar <angular frequency> $\omega_\theta^2=V_{\mathrm{eff}}''(\theta_0)/(ml^2)=\Omega^2(1+3\cos^2\theta_0)$, where $\Omega^2=g/(l\cos\theta_0)$ is the conical revolution frequency squared. Holding $\dot\phi$ fixed during the perturbation would describe a different, externally driven constraint. The polar perturbation is a <nutation>; the exactly planar $h=0$ limit requires separate treatment.

= Exact Lagrangian of two spring-coupled pendulums
{parent=Simple pendulum}
{title2=$L=\tfrac12ml^2(\dot\theta_1^2+\dot\theta_2^2)+mgl(\cos\theta_1+\cos\theta_2)-\tfrac12k(d-r)^2$}

Two identical planar <simple pendulums> of length $l$ and bob <mass> $m$, with pivots separated horizontally by $r>0$, are coupled by a straight <spring> of natural length $r$ and <spring constant> $k$. In the ideal model without contact restrictions, their <configuration space> is the <torus> $S^1\times S^1$. Measure both <angles> from the downward vertical towards the same horizontal direction. Their bob separation is
$$
d=\sqrt{\bigl(r+l(\sin\theta_2-\sin\theta_1)\bigr)^2+l^2(\cos\theta_2-\cos\theta_1)^2}.
$$
The exact <Lagrangian> is the expression in the title. To derive it, use bob <positions> $(l\sin\theta_1,-l\cos\theta_1)$ and $(r+l\sin\theta_2,-l\cos\theta_2)$. <Kinetic energy from Cartesian position vectors> gives $T=ml^2(\dot\theta_1^2+\dot\theta_2^2)/2$. Gravitational <potential energy> is $-mgl(\cos\theta_1+\cos\theta_2)$ and <spring> <potential energy> is $k(d-r)^2/2$; subtracting these from $T$ proves the formula. Near the downward <equilibrium>, $d-r=l(\theta_2-\theta_1)+O(|\boldsymbol\theta|^3)$ for fixed $r$, giving the familiar <normal modes of two spring-coupled equal pendulums>.

= Pendulum with a prescribed translating support
{parent=Simple pendulum}
{title2=$l\ddot\theta+\ddot a_x\cos\theta+(g+\ddot a_y)\sin\theta=0$}

For support displacement $\mathbf a(t)$ and angle measured from the downward vertical, the mass position is $(a_x+l\sin\theta,a_y-l\cos\theta)$. Its <Euler-Lagrange equation> is $l\ddot\theta+\ddot a_x\cos\theta+(g+\ddot a_y)\sin\theta=0$. In the support frame the effective acceleration is $\mathbf g-\ddot{\mathbf a}$; taking its <torque> about the hinge gives $l^2\ddot\theta\hat{\mathbf z}=\mathbf l\times(\mathbf g-\ddot{\mathbf a})$. The prescribed support can do work, so pendulum energy need not be conserved.

= Harmonic forcing of a horizontally driven pendulum
{parent=Pendulum with a prescribed translating support}
{title2=$\ddot\theta+(g/l)\theta=(A\omega^2/l)\cos\omega t$}

For small <angles> about the downward vertical and prescribed pivot <position> $x=A\cos\omega t$, a <simple pendulum> obeys the displayed forced-oscillator equation. Off resonance, its harmonic particular solution is $[A\omega^2/(l(\omega_0^2-\omega^2))]\cos\omega t$, with $\omega_0^2=g/l$. At exact resonance, a pendulum initially vertical with zero angular <velocity> has $\theta=(A\omega_0/(2l))t\sin\omega_0t$ in the undamped linear model. The growing envelope eventually violates the small-angle assumption; <damping> and nonlinear frequency shifts matter in a real pendulum.

= Horizontal-vibration averaged pendulum potential
{parent=Pendulum with a prescribed translating support}
{title2=$V_{\rm eff}=-mgl\cos\Theta+ma^2\omega^2\cos^2\Theta/4$}

For a small, rapid horizontal displacement $a\cos\omega t$, the leading fast angular excursion is $\xi=-(a/l)\cos\Theta\cos\omega t$. Correlating that excursion with the driving <torque> gives the averaged force $\ddot\Theta=-(g/l)\sin\Theta+(a^2\omega^2/2l^2)\sin\Theta\cos\Theta$. Its <effective potential> is $-mgl\cos\Theta+ma^2\omega^2\cos^2\Theta/4$. The downward state loses <linear stability> above $a\omega=\sqrt{2gl}$ and two tilted minima appear. Small $a/l$ and slow dynamics compared with $\omega$ are required; horizontal and vertical support vibration produce different <effective potentials>.

= Pendulum clock below a uniform-density surface
{parent=Simple pendulum}
{title2=$t_{\mathrm{read}}/t=\sqrt{1-h/R}$}

Inside a spherical body of uniform density, the <shell theorem> gives gravitational acceleration $g(r)=g(R)r/R$. A <simple pendulum> at depth $h$ therefore has period $P_h=P_0/\sqrt{1-h/R}$. If its clock is calibrated at the surface, its indicated elapsed time is $t\sqrt{1-h/R}$ and it loses $t[1-\sqrt{1-h/R}]\simeq th/(2R)$. This is a mechanical clock-rate change caused by weaker local gravity, not gravitational time dilation. The uniform-density assumption is essential; Earth's actual density profile gives a different interior field.

= Conical pendulum
{parent=Simple pendulum}
{wiki}

A <conical pendulum> is a pendulum whose bob executes uniform horizontal <circular motion> beneath a fixed pivot, with its string at a constant <angle> $\theta$ to the downward vertical. The string sweeps a <right circular conical surface>; the bob follows its circular rim, without any physical cone supporting it. The ideal model has a <point mass> $m>0$, a taut, massless, inextensible string of length $\ell>0$, uniform <gravitational acceleration> of magnitude $g>0$, and negligible <drag force> and pivot <friction>. A nonzero circular motion requires $0<\theta<\pi/2$. This geometry and the corresponding <forces> are described in https://farside.ph.utexas.edu/teaching/301/lectures/node88.html[Richard Fitzpatrick, The conical pendulum, equations 262–267].

\Image[conical-pendulum-geometry.png]
{title=Conical pendulum geometry, horizontal orbit, and the two forces on the bob}

The <right triangle> formed by the pivot, the orbit centre, and the bob gives $r=\ell\sin\theta$ and $h=\ell\cos\theta$. Here $r$ is the orbit radius and $h$ is the vertical drop from the pivot to the bob's horizontal plane, not the bob's height above a table. Write $T$ for <tension>, $P$ for the <oscillation period> of one revolution, and $\omega=2\pi/P$ for the <angular speed>; then the bob's <speed> is $v=\omega r$. A side view compresses the horizontal circle to a line segment; the top view reveals the circle and its tangent <velocity>.

The two actual <forces> on the bob are <tension> toward the pivot and <gravitational weight> vertically downward. The vertical <acceleration> is zero, while the horizontal <centripetal acceleration> is $v^2/r=\omega^2r$. Thus <Newton's second law> gives
$$
T\cos\theta=mg,\qquad T\sin\theta=m\omega^2r=\frac{mv^2}{r}.
$$
The inward net <force> is the horizontal component of <tension>, rather than an extra interaction to add to these two <forces>. Constant <speed> still requires <acceleration> because the <velocity> changes direction. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/6-3-centripetal-force[Ling, Sanny and Moebs, University Physics 1, §6.3: centripetal acceleration and net force]{ref}

Dividing the two component equations and using $r=\ell\sin\theta$ gives the useful collection of predictions
$$
\omega^2\cos\theta=\frac g\ell,\qquad T=\frac{mg}{\cos\theta},\qquad v^2=g\ell\frac{\sin^2\theta}{\cos\theta},\qquad P=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\ell\cos\theta}{g}}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac hg}.
$$
If $\phi$ is the azimuthal <angle>, then $\dot\phi=\pm\omega$; either direction of rotation satisfies $\dot\phi^2\cos\theta=g/\ell$. The <mass> cancels from the <angular speed> and <oscillation period>, but not from the <tension>. The motion is a <relative equilibrium>: in a <rotating frame> following the bob at <angular speed> $\omega$, its <position> is fixed. This is a concrete way to see how rotational <symmetry> can turn an apparently moving solution into a stationary one.

= Magnetically charged conical pendulum
{parent=Conical pendulum}
{title2=$Q=CB\omega r^2/2$}

A conducting <conical pendulum> connected to a grounded pivot develops a motional <electric potential difference> $B\omega r^2/2$ between the rotation axis and a bob at radius $r$. If the bob dominates the <capacitance>, its <electric charge> is $CB\omega r^2/2$. An additional uniform vertical <electric field> changes both the force on that charge and, while the grounding connection remains, the background <electric potential> entering the charging relation. These effects must be treated consistently when calculating a first-order mechanical response.

= Rotating bob experiment for a conical pendulum
{parent=Conical pendulum}

The motivating observation is a bob going around at almost constant height while its string traces a cone. The University of Iowa's demonstration places a camera above a short pendulum to reveal this motion. https://instructional-resources.physics.uiowa.edu/1d5025-conical-pendulum[University of Iowa, demonstration 1D50.25: short pendulum and overhead camera]{ref} The following proposed measurement procedure develops that observation into a test of the <conical pendulum> model; it is not a report of collected data.

Suspend a compact bob from a rigid, fixed support using a light string, and measure $\ell$ from the pivot to the bob's centre. Put a stationary marker beside the orbit as a timing reference. Displace the bob sideways and give it a horizontal push tangent to the intended circle, then let go. For a chosen starting <angle> $\theta$, the predicted initial <speed> is $v=\sqrt{g\ell\sin^2\theta/\cos\theta}$. Adjust the launch between trials until a short observation interval has nearly constant orbit radius and height. Keep the pivot fixed while recording: circling the support by hand changes the problem, and a maintained drive would introduce another <force>.

Record from above with the camera looking approximately along the vertical axis. Check that the bob's centre follows a circle and that successive returns to the marker take nearly equal times. A separate side recording checks whether the bob's height stays constant. A markedly elliptical orbit or visibly rising and falling bob is not the uniform <circular motion> assumed by the <conical pendulum> equations. Measure the orbit diameter with a scale at the orbit's height; in a level side view, use the two extreme horizontal positions, not one arbitrary frame. Halve the diameter to obtain $r$, or measure the vertical drop $h$ directly in a calibrated side view. An oblique camera view and a scale at a different depth distort apparent lengths, so arrange the geometry before interpreting the images.

Time $N$ complete returns to the same marker in the same direction and estimate $P=\Delta t/N$. Counting ten revolutions, when the orbit stays sufficiently steady, spreads an endpoint timing error over ten periods. Repeat the timing and compare the first and last few revolutions to detect drift; using a longer interval helps only if the motion remains close to uniform. From independently measured geometry, compute $\sin\theta=r/\ell$ or $\cos\theta=h/\ell$, and check $r^2+h^2\simeq\ell^2$ if both were measured. Then compare the measured <oscillation period> with $P^2=4\pi^2h/g$. MIT's 8.01X experimental assignment explicitly asks students to compare the <conical pendulum>'s period, length, and orbit radius with theory. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01x-physics-i-classical-mechanics-with-an-experimental-focus-fall-2002/a056f6a3e071767d4f7620bca6a59d0c_expt_vs_problem.pdf[MIT 8.01X, Fall 2002, Vibrating Systems experimental problem, question 8]{ref}

Repeat at several launch radii with the same $\ell$. Larger $r$ means smaller $h=\sqrt{\ell^2-r^2}$, so the ideal <oscillation period> decreases. A plot of measured $P^2$ against independently measured $h$ should follow a straight line through the origin with slope $4\pi^2/g$; conversely, $g=4\pi^2h/P^2$ estimates <gravitational acceleration>. Do not infer $h$ from the timing equation and then use that same equation as a test. Compare discrepancies with the spread of repeated timings and the resolution of the length measurements. String stretch, a moving pivot, and appreciable bob size challenge the stated geometry; <drag force> and pivot <friction> challenge the assumption of undriven uniform motion. Observe a short interval and record any shrinking or drifting orbit rather than silently treating it as an exact <conical pendulum>.

For an illustrative calculation, assume $\ell=1.00\,\mathrm m$, $m=0.050\,\mathrm{kg}$, $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, and $\theta=40^\circ$. The model gives $r\approx0.643\,\mathrm m$, $h\approx0.766\,\mathrm m$, $P\approx1.756\,\mathrm s$, $v\approx2.300\,\mathrm{m/s}$, and $T\approx0.640\,\mathrm N$. Its vertical <tension> component is $0.4905\,\mathrm N$, equal to the <gravitational weight>; its inward component is approximately $0.412\,\mathrm N$. These are predictions from chosen parameters, not measured data or measurements of local $g$. The next figure uses the same stipulated $\ell$ and $g$, including illustrative <angles> $15^\circ$, $40^\circ$, and $60^\circ$.

= Period and small-angle limit of a conical pendulum
{parent=Conical pendulum}
{title2=$P/P_0=\sqrt{\cos\theta},\quad P_0=2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}$}

\Image[conical-pendulum-period.png]
{title=Ideal conical pendulum period decreases with cone angle and its square is proportional to the vertical drop}

For fixed $\ell$ and $g$, the exact <conical pendulum> <oscillation period> satisfies $P/P_0=\sqrt{\cos\theta}$. In the <small-angle approximation>, with $\theta$ in <radians>,
$$
\frac{P}{P_0}=1-\frac{\theta^2}{4}+O(\theta^4),\qquad P_0=2\pi\sqrt{\frac\ell g}.
$$
Thus the limiting revolution period equals the small-amplitude <oscillation period> of a planar <simple pendulum>. This equality has a geometric explanation: to first order, $x(t)=r\cos(\omega t)$ and $y(t)=r\sin(\omega t)$ are perpendicular <simple harmonic motions> of equal <amplitude>, a quarter period apart, with $\omega\to\sqrt{g/\ell}$. At finite <angle>, however, the <conical pendulum>'s <oscillation period> decreases with $\theta$, whereas a planar <simple pendulum>'s <oscillation period> increases with its angular <amplitude>.

The endpoint $\theta=0$ itself is a stationary bob with $r=0$ and no observable revolution; $P_0$ is a limit of nonzero circular motions, not a measured revolution period at rest. For fixed $\ell$, nonzero <conical pendulum> motion also requires $\omega>\sqrt{g/\ell}$, because $\cos\theta=g/(\ell\omega^2)<1$. At the other endpoint, $\theta\to\pi/2$ gives $h\to0$, $P\to0$, and unbounded <tension> and <speed>. These mathematical limits explain why the ideal string cannot sustain a horizontal orbit at finite <tension>; a real experiment ceases to satisfy the ideal assumptions before that limit.

= Damped pendulum
{parent=Simple pendulum}
{title2=$\ddot\theta+c\dot\theta+\sin\theta=0$}

A <damped pendulum> is a <simple pendulum> whose <mechanical energy> is removed by a resisting <torque>. Here the model is undriven, with a fixed pivot, a <point mass> $m>0$ at distance $\ell>0$, uniform <gravitational acceleration> $g>0$, and resisting <torque> $-b\,d\theta/dt$ with constant $b>0$. The <angle> $\theta$ is measured from the downward vertical in <radians>. An ideal viscous rotary <dashpot> realizes this torque law; tangential <linear drag> $-\alpha v$ on the bob gives the same equation with $b=\alpha\ell^2$. The <moment of inertia> is $m\ell^2$, so
$$
m\ell^2\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}+b\frac{d\theta}{dt}+mg\ell\sin\theta=0,\qquad
\omega_0=\sqrt{\frac g\ell},\quad \gamma=\frac{b}{m\ell^2},\quad \tau=\omega_0t,\quad c=\frac\gamma{\omega_0}>0.
$$
Here $t$ is physical <time>, $\omega_0$ is the undamped small-amplitude <angular frequency>, and $\gamma$ has units of inverse <time>. In the normalized equation $\ddot\theta+c\dot\theta+\sin\theta=0$, dots mean differentiation with respect to $\tau$, not $t$. The parameter $c$ is dimensionless. This separates the chosen damping law from the <small-angle approximation>: retaining $\sin\theta$ allows finite <angles> even when the resisting <torque> is linear in <angular velocity>.

\Image[damped-pendulum-decay-observation.png]
{title=Proposed viscous-damper experiment and illustrative small-angle decay: angle versus time and successive peak ratios, not measured data}

To motivate the model, release a pendulum without a push and record how its swing shrinks. A published example by Gintautas and Hubler uses a rotary sensor built from a computer mouse to measure the frequency and damping coefficient of a <physical pendulum>. https://arxiv.org/abs/0901.4319[Gintautas and Hubler, A simple, low-cost, data-logging pendulum built from a computer mouse, 2009]{ref} The following is a proposed experiment with a chosen viscous rotary <dashpot>; the plotted observations are predictions, not a reproduction of their measurements.

Mount a compact bob on a light rigid rod with a fixed pivot, couple the pivot to an adjustable viscous rotary <dashpot>, and measure $\ell$ from the pivot to the bob's centre. First record decay with the <dashpot> disconnected, then repeat with it connected at several settings; this establishes how much decay is already due to other losses. Keep the bob, rod, camera, and release <angle> unchanged between these trials. The simple model requires rod and damper-rotor <moments of inertia> to be negligible compared with $m\ell^2$. Otherwise measure the total <moment of inertia> $I$ and the distance $d$ to the assembly's centre of mass: the <physical pendulum> equation becomes $I\,d^2\theta/dt^2+b\,d\theta/dt+Mgd\sin\theta=0$, where $M$ is the total moving <mass>. A damper that also exerts an elastic restoring <torque> would require another term.

Place a camera perpendicular to the swing plane, include the pivot and bob centre in every frame, and determine their coordinates relative to the resting vertical. For horizontal and downward separations $x,y$, use $\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(x,y)$; record the frame timestamps rather than assuming a frame rate. Release from rest at about $10^\circ$, record through several complete swings, and repeat the release. For every same-side turning point, tabulate its <time> $t_n$ and angular <amplitude> $A_n=|\theta(t_n)|$. Measure the <oscillation period> from differences $t_{n+1}-t_n$; comparing several periods exposes drift. Interpolating around each observed extremum reduces frame discretization error. Check that late peaks still exceed the angular resolution; taking the <natural logarithm> of tiny, uncertain peaks amplifies their relative errors.

In the <small-angle approximation>, the <damped pendulum> becomes a <damped harmonic oscillator>. For $0<\gamma<2\omega_0$ and release from rest at $\theta_0$, its exact linear-model solution is
$$
\theta_{\mathrm{lin}}(t)=\theta_0e^{-\gamma t/2}\left(\cos\omega_dt+\frac{\gamma}{2\omega_d}\sin\omega_dt\right),\qquad
\omega_d=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\frac{\gamma^2}{4}},\qquad T_d=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_d}.
$$
The decaying sinusoidal envelope has coefficient $|\theta_0|\omega_0/\omega_d$; because release is from rest, its successive same-side extrema occur at $t_n=nT_d$ and have $A_n=|\theta_0|e^{-\gamma nT_d/2}$. Thus the envelope and the sequence of actual peak heights are different descriptions of the same trace. The linear damping regimes and this solution are derived in https://farside.ph.utexas.edu/teaching/336k/Newton/node19.html[Richard Fitzpatrick, Damped Oscillatory Motion, equations 82–93].

The experiment tests two predictions independently: nearly equal same-side peak spacing and a straight line when plotting $\ln(A_n/A_0)$ against $t_n-t_0$. Its predicted slope is $-\gamma/2$, giving
$$
\gamma=\frac{2}{t_{n+1}-t_n}\ln\frac{A_n}{A_{n+1}},\qquad b=m\ell^2\gamma,\qquad
\omega_d^2+\frac{\gamma^2}{4}=\frac g\ell.
$$
Use several peaks and repeated trials rather than a single ratio, and compare the frequency identity with independently measured $\ell$ and $g$. For the illustrative choices $\ell=1.00\,\mathrm m$, $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, $c=0.20$, and $\theta_0=10^\circ$, the linear model gives $\gamma\approx0.6264\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $T_d\approx2.0162\,\mathrm s$, and $A_{n+1}/A_n\approx0.5318$. The first two positive peaks after release are therefore approximately $5.318^\circ$ and $2.828^\circ$. The undamped small-amplitude <oscillation period> is about $2.0061\,\mathrm s$: substantial loss per swing need not imply a large change in period.

Repeat at a larger release <angle> to distinguish two limitations. The relative error in replacing $\sin\theta$ by $\theta$ is about $\theta^2/6$ for small <angles>: about $0.51\%$ at $10^\circ$, but about $17.3\%$ at $60^\circ$ when compared with $\theta$. Finite-amplitude swing spacing can therefore change as the swing shrinks even if $b$ is constant. Separately, <friction> at the pivot or <quadratic drag> can invalidate the assumed linear resisting <torque>; exponential peak decay should be checked, not imposed on all recorded motion. Peters discusses why real pendulum damping can require nonlinear terms. https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0306081[Randall D. Peters, Nonlinear Damping of the 'Linear' Pendulum, 2003]{ref} Repeatability, residual pivot motion, changes in damper <temperature>, and finite camera resolution are useful checks on this proposed measurement.

The normalized <equilibrium points> are $(\theta,\dot\theta)=(k\pi,0)$. Even multiples of $\pi$ are attracting: their <linearization> has <eigenvalues> $(-c\pm\sqrt{c^2-4})/2$, giving a <stable focus> for $0<c<2$, repeated negative <eigenvalues> at $c=2$, and a <stable node> for $c>2$. These are the underdamped, critically damped, and overdamped small-displacement regimes. Odd multiples of $\pi$ have <eigenvalues> $(-c\pm\sqrt{c^2+4})/2$ of opposite signs and are <saddle equilibria>. The <energy dissipation of a damped pendulum> explains why trajectories in the <phase portrait> spiral inward near a <stable focus> instead of closing into the recurring orbit of an undamped <simple pendulum>.

= Energy dissipation of a damped pendulum
{parent=Damped pendulum}
{title2=$\dot E=-c\dot\theta^2$}

For the normalized <damped pendulum>, the sum of <kinetic energy> and gravitational <potential energy>, divided by $mg\ell$, is
$$
E(\tau)=\frac12\dot\theta^2+1-\cos\theta,\qquad
\dot E=\dot\theta(\ddot\theta+\sin\theta)=-c\dot\theta^2\leq0.
$$
The <chain rule> and the full nonlinear equation give this identity exactly, without a <small-angle approximation>. Here $\dot\theta=d\theta/d\tau$ as in <damped pendulum>. In physical units the <mechanical energy> and the <power> of the resisting <torque> are
$$
\mathcal E(t)=\frac12m\ell^2\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)^2+mg\ell(1-\cos\theta),\qquad
\frac{d\mathcal E}{dt}=-b\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)^2.
$$
The pendulum's lost <mechanical energy> is transferred to the damper and surroundings; it does not violate <conservation of energy>. In an ideal viscous <dashpot> this loss is <frictional heating>. The exact cumulative balance is particularly useful for checking a numerical solution:
$$
Q(\tau)=c\int_0^\tau\dot\theta(s)^2\,ds,\qquad E(\tau)+Q(\tau)=E(0).
$$

\Image[damped-pendulum-nonlinear-energy.png]
{title=Illustrative nonlinear and linear damped pendulum solutions at 10 and 60 degrees, with exact mechanical energy transfer and loss; no measured data}

The figure uses $c=0.20$ and release from rest. Over $0\leq\tau\leq25$, the largest angular discrepancy between the nonlinear solution and the <damped harmonic oscillator> solution is approximately $0.000702\,\mathrm{rad}$ ($0.0402^\circ$) for a $10^\circ$ release, but $0.14995\,\mathrm{rad}$ ($8.592^\circ$) for a $60^\circ$ release. Their nonlinear initial energies are $1-\cos10^\circ\approx0.015192$ and $1-\cos60^\circ=0.5$, respectively. At $\tau=25$ the remaining fractions are approximately $0.0064080$ and $0.0063757$. These are illustrative calculations from specified initial conditions, not observations. The generator uses a <Runge-Kutta method> and integrates $Q$ alongside the motion; halving its step and independently integrating $c\dot\theta^2$ checks the result.

For $\ell=1\,\mathrm m$ and $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, the first three nonlinear same-side swing intervals after a $60^\circ$ release are approximately $2.1008$, $2.0378$, and $2.0221\,\mathrm s$. They approach the linear-model <oscillation period> $2.0162\,\mathrm s$ as the <amplitude> shrinks. After a $10^\circ$ release the corresponding intervals are already about $2.0185$, $2.0168$, and $2.0164\,\mathrm s$. This makes the experiment's period drift a quantitative test of the <small-angle approximation>, rather than an automatic reason to change the damping coefficient.

At a turning point, $\dot\theta=0$ and $\dot E=0$ even though the swing is losing <mechanical energy> over the cycle. Its <potential energy> there is $E_n=1-\cos A_n$, so measured turning <angles> give successive energies without estimating <angular velocity>. In the proposed decay experiment, multiply these values by $mg\ell$ to obtain energies in <joules>, then subtract adjacent same-side energies to estimate the loss over each full swing. Only for small <amplitudes> is $E_n\simeq A_n^2/2$. In the linear underdamped model, same-side peak energies consequently satisfy $E_{n+1}/E_n=e^{-\gamma T_d}$, the square of the angular <amplitude> ratio. For the illustrative parameters above this energy ratio is approximately $0.2828$. Between turning points, <kinetic energy> and <potential energy> continue to exchange while their sum decreases; the energy slope vanishes briefly at each extremum and is negative whenever the bob moves.

It is therefore inaccurate to replace the exact identity by $\dot E=-cE$. Even for the linear model, $E_{\mathrm{lin}}=(\dot\theta^2+\theta^2)/2$ has $\dot E_{\mathrm{lin}}=-c\dot\theta^2$. For weak damping and small <angles>, cycle averaging gives $\langle\dot\theta^2\rangle\simeq\langle E_{\mathrm{lin}}\rangle$, so the averaged energy approximately follows $e^{-c\tau}$ while angular <amplitude> follows $e^{-c\tau/2}$. This <exponential decay> is an approximation to the energy trace; the same-side peak ratio is exact within the linear model. The figure distinguishes the actual energy curves from this averaging approximation.

The energy identity also gives a global conclusion without solving the nonlinear equation. If $E$ were constant on a positive-length interval, then $\dot\theta$ would vanish throughout it; the equation would force $\sin\theta=0$, making the entire trajectory an <equilibrium>. Every nonstationary trajectory therefore loses <mechanical energy> over every positive-length interval, even though its instantaneous <derivative> can vanish at a turning point. A nonconstant periodic motion would have to return to its initial <mechanical energy>, so it is impossible for this undriven <damped pendulum>. Oscillations near a <stable focus> are successive, shrinking swings, not a repeating cycle. Moreover, if $E(0)<2$, the <angle> cannot reach an upright <equilibrium>, whose <potential energy> is $2$; such motion stays within its original potential well. This is the usefulness of the energy description: one scalar balance controls both the observed decay and which nonlinear motions remain accessible.

= Foucault pendulum
{parent=Simple pendulum}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$\dot\psi=-\Omega\sin\varphi$}

A <Foucault pendulum> is a long <simple pendulum> whose suspension allows the bob to swing in both independent horizontal directions. Its slowly changing swing direction relative to the building reveals <Earth's rotation>. A hinge restricting the bob to one vertical <plane> would prevent this measurement: the support would impose the very direction we want the pendulum to select freely. <Léon Foucault> demonstrated the effect in 1851.<The Foucault pendulum by Joe Wolfe>[The Foucault pendulum, Does the Earth turn?: 1851 demonstration]{ref}

The bob's back-and-forth <oscillation> and the swing direction's <precession> are different motions. The bob traverses its arc in seconds, while successive arcs change direction over hours. In a <conical pendulum> the bob itself circulates around the vertical; a nearly planar <Foucault pendulum> instead reverses along an almost straight horizontal trace, whose orientation changes slowly. The measured quantity is that orientation against a fixed floor or wall reference, rather than the bob's instantaneous <azimuth>.

= UNSW Foucault pendulum observation
{parent=Foucault pendulum}
{c}

The University of New South Wales documents an undriven, visitor-started <Foucault pendulum>. A fixed vertical wire and wall line define its initial north–south swing <plane>. The documented comparison one hour later shows reduced <amplitude> and about nine degrees of anticlockwise <precession>. The apparatus swings for several hours without an electromagnetic drive.<The Foucault pendulum by Joe Wolfe>[The Foucault pendulum, opening observation sequence and Our Foucault Pendulum: wire/wall reference, one-hour comparison, no electromagnetic drive]{ref}

\Image[foucault-pendulum-apparatus-and-latitude.png]
{title=Functional Foucault pendulum apparatus, fixed sighting reference, and predicted latitude dependence; schematic with no historical dimensions}

To turn that demonstration into a quantitative experiment, the following is a proposed measurement protocol. Establish a rigid support with no preferred horizontal swing direction, measure the wire length to the bob's centre, and mark two perpendicular horizontal axes fixed to the building. Start with a small displacement along the sighting <plane>, then release with as little transverse <velocity> as possible. Record the actual launch method; burning a fine restraining thread is one possible proposed release, not a claim about the documented UNSW launch. Keep people clear of the bob and avoid touching the suspension during the observation.

Record the horizontal <position> from above with a stationary, calibrated camera, or sight the bob against the wire and wall line at recorded times. Fit the long axis of each short group of swings to estimate its orientation $\psi$; use the extrema on both sides rather than following the bob's rapidly reversing <velocity>. A swing line has orientation modulo $\pi$, so unwrap successive small direction changes continuously. Rotate no camera or reference between measurements. Record the fast <oscillation period> separately, and compare the slope of $\psi(t)$ with the <latitude law of Foucault precession>. Repeat launches along different directions and monitor <ellipses>, pivot motion, and air currents before attributing the slow change to <Earth's rotation>.

= Latitude law of Foucault precession
{parent=Foucault pendulum}
{c}
{title2=$\dot\psi=-\Omega\sin\varphi,\quad T_{\mathrm p}=T_{\mathrm{sid}}/|\sin\varphi|$}

Use local east, north, and up <unit vectors>, with $x$ eastward, $y$ northward, and positive orientation $\psi$ anticlockwise as viewed from above. Let $\varphi$ be signed <geographical latitude>, positive north, and $\Omega>0$ the magnitude of <Earth>'s <angular velocity>. In the spherical-<Earth> approximation,
$$
\boldsymbol\Omega=\Omega\cos\varphi\,\mathbf e_N+\Omega\sin\varphi\,\mathbf e_U,\qquad
\alpha=\Omega\sin\varphi,\qquad \omega_0^2=\frac g\ell.
$$
Here $g$ is the local <effective gravity in a rotating frame>, including the steady terrestrial <centrifugal acceleration>. For small horizontal displacements compared with $\ell$, the horizontal restoring <acceleration> from <tension> is $-\omega_0^2(x,y)$. Vertical displacement is second order in the swing <amplitude>, so vertical <velocity> can be omitted at this order. The horizontal <Coriolis acceleration in local Earth coordinates> then gives the coupled <linear differential equations>
$$
\ddot x+\omega_0^2x=2\alpha\dot y,\qquad
\ddot y+\omega_0^2y=-2\alpha\dot x.
$$
These equations neglect <damping>, finite-amplitude corrections, support <anisotropy>, and spatial changes of the <centrifugal force> of order $\Omega^2$; they retain the leading terrestrial <Coriolis force>. A related derivation uses south/east axes instead.<Foucault's pendulum - the physics (and maths) explained by Joe Wolfe>[Foucault's pendulum - the physics (and maths) explained, equations (9.1*)–(11): horizontal small-oscillation equations]{ref}

Introduce the <complex number> $z=x+iy$. Multiplying the second equation by $i$ and adding gives
$$
\ddot z+2i\alpha\dot z+\omega_0^2z=0.
$$
Set $z=e^{-i\alpha t}u$. Direct <differentiation> cancels the first <derivative> of $u$ and yields
$$
\ddot u+(\omega_0^2+\alpha^2)u=0,\qquad
\beta=\sqrt{\omega_0^2+\alpha^2},\qquad
z=e^{-i\alpha t}(C\cos\beta t+D\sin\beta t).
$$
The $\alpha^2$ in $\beta$ comes from solving these reduced equations exactly; it is not a complete prediction of terrestrial corrections of order $\Omega^2$. Thus the horizontal <trajectory> is an <ellipse> in coordinates rotating at <angular velocity> $-\alpha$ relative to the building. When $|\alpha|\ll\omega_0$, each rapid swing samples an almost fixed long axis; its slow <precession> is
$$
\dot\psi=-\Omega\sin\varphi,\qquad
T_{\mathrm p}=\frac{2\pi}{\Omega|\sin\varphi|}=\frac{T_{\mathrm{sid}}}{|\sin\varphi|}.
$$
The <sidereal day> $T_{\mathrm{sid}}=2\pi/\Omega$ is a rotation relative to distant directions. $T_{\mathrm p}$ denotes a full $2\pi$ turn of a continuously followed orientation; an unlabelled swing line repeats after $T_{\mathrm p}/2$. At the equator there is no finite <precession period> in this model. The <Coriolis parameter> is $f=2\alpha$, so the plane's rate is $-f/2$, not $-f$.

The limiting cases check the sign and scale. At the north pole $\alpha=\Omega$: the swing direction turns clockwise once per <sidereal day>. At the equator $\alpha=0$: both horizontal equations reduce to independent <simple harmonic motions> and a line launch stays on its original line. At the south pole $\alpha=-\Omega$: the direction turns anticlockwise once per <sidereal day>. Vanishing horizontal <precession> at the equator does not mean that every component of <Coriolis acceleration> vanishes there.

For a stipulated $\Omega=7.292115\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, the <sidereal day> is approximately $23.9345\,\mathrm h$. At $45^\circ$ north the predicted rate is $-10.6356^\circ/\mathrm h$ and $T_{\mathrm p}\simeq33.8485\,\mathrm h$; at $45^\circ$ south the rate changes sign. At the illustrative <geographical latitude> $34^\circ$ south, the prediction is $+8.4109^\circ/\mathrm h$. These calculations are model predictions, not new observations of the UNSW apparatus.

A swing <plane> fixed among the stars is generally only a polar picture. A north–south swing at the equator turns with <Earth> in an <inertial frame>, while having no <precession> against the building.<The Foucault pendulum by Joe Wolfe>[The Foucault pendulum, The 'plane' of the pendulum's swing is not fixed in space: equatorial counterexample]{ref}

= Exact linear Foucault pendulum trajectory
{parent=Foucault pendulum}
{c}
{title2=$z=Ae^{-i\alpha t}(\cos\beta t+i\alpha\sin\beta t/\beta)$}

For release from rest relative to the building, take $x(0)=A>0$, $y(0)=\dot x(0)=\dot y(0)=0$. The exact solution of the stated coupled <linear differential equations> is
$$
z(t)=Ae^{-i\alpha t}\left(\cos\beta t+i\frac\alpha\beta\sin\beta t\right),\qquad
\beta=\sqrt{\omega_0^2+\alpha^2}.
$$
Indeed $u(0)=A$ and $\dot u(0)=i\alpha A$, so $\dot z(0)=0$. In the slowly rotating coordinates, the <ellipse> has semiaxes $A$ and $A|\alpha|/\beta$. A bob released from rest therefore does not describe a perfectly straight line even in this ideal linear model. Replacing the bracket by $\cos\omega_0t$ gives the familiar leading approximation but introduces a spurious initial transverse <velocity> $-i\alpha A$. The exact expression keeps the initial condition and separates that tiny orbit width from the slow rotation of its long axis.

\Image[foucault-pendulum-exact-slow-trajectories.png]
{title=Exact trajectories of the linear Foucault pendulum equations at the actual Earth rotation rate, showing one swing near zero, three, and six hours at northern, equatorial, and southern latitudes}

For the illustrative $\ell=20\,\mathrm m$ and $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, the fast period $2\pi/\beta$ is approximately $8.9714\,\mathrm s$. At $45^\circ$ north, $|\alpha|/\beta\simeq7.3624\times10^{-5}$: the transverse semiaxis is too small to distinguish at the figure's scale. Nevertheless the long-axis orientation changes by about $-63.8138^\circ$ in six hours. Changing the sign of <geographical latitude> conjugates the entire solution, reflecting the <trajectory> across the eastward axis; setting $\alpha=0$ gives $z=A\cos\omega_0t$ exactly. These are stronger checks than merely drawing a rotating line.

The coupled equations give an additional independent constraint. Multiplying them by $\dot x$ and $\dot y$ and adding cancels the <Coriolis force>'s contributions:
$$
\frac d{dt}\left[\frac12(\dot x^2+\dot y^2)+\frac12\omega_0^2(x^2+y^2)\right]=0.
$$
This is <mechanical energy> per unit <mass> in the reduced model; <precession> does not require a loss of <mechanical energy>. Also, direct <differentiation> gives
$$
\frac d{dt}\left[x\dot y-y\dot x+\alpha(x^2+y^2)\right]=0.
$$
For this release the constant is $\alpha A^2$. The exact <trajectory> satisfies both identities. They test the coupled dynamics separately from the visible slow rotation and express the fact that the <Coriolis force does no work>.

= Controls and limitations of a Foucault pendulum
{parent=Foucault pendulum}
{c}

An unwanted <ellipse> is an experimental warning: a finite-amplitude pendulum can develop additional orbit-axis <precession> even without <Earth's rotation>. A symmetrical, firm support and careful launch reduce this effect.<Foucault's pendulum - the physics (and maths) explained by Joe Wolfe>[Foucault's pendulum - the physics (and maths) explained, Complications: finite elliptical motion and asymmetries in support, launch, and drag]{ref} Distinguish that nonlinear effect from the tiny <ellipse> already present in the <exact linear Foucault pendulum trajectory>. The linear calculation establishes a controlled approximation, not the complete motion of an arbitrary large swing.

Support <anisotropy> creates a second competing mechanism. As an illustrative modification, let the two horizontal restoring <angular frequencies> be $\omega_x$ and $\omega_y$. Even with $\alpha=0$, a diagonal release from rest gives
$$
x=A_x\cos\omega_xt,\qquad y=A_y\cos\omega_yt.
$$
When $\omega_x\ne\omega_y$, the relative <phase> changes on the scale $1/|\omega_x-\omega_y|$: the trace changes shape without any terrestrial <Coriolis force>. It need not be uniform <precession> of a <Foucault pendulum>. To cleanly resolve the terrestrial effect over times of order $1/|\alpha|$, a useful design target is $|\omega_x-\omega_y|\ll|\alpha|$. Near the equator that target becomes particularly demanding. Compare fast periods along perpendicular launch directions to look for this frequency splitting; repeat at several launch orientations to detect preferred suspension axes.

Other controls address distinct alternatives: shelter the apparatus from air currents, track the support against the building, measure both orbit axes, and compare early and late fitted directions as the <amplitude> decays. A sustaining drive, if used in a different apparatus, must supply <mechanical energy> without imposing an azimuthal bias; an undriven run provides a useful comparison. A change in swing direction alone cannot exclude these effects. Agreement of the signed rate with independently known <geographical latitude> and <Earth's rotation>, together with stable repeat launches and small competing effects, is the inference the experiment is designed to support.
