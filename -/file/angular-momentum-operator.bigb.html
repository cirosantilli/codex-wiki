= Angular momentum operator
{wiki}

= Common eigenstate of two angular-momentum components
{parent=Angular momentum operator}

A common eigenstate of $J_x$ and $J_y$ must satisfy $J_z\psi=0$ by their <commutator>. Applying the other angular-momentum <commutators> then forces both remaining <eigenvalues> to vanish. Thus the state lies in a spin-zero sector. Noncommuting components do not possess a general common eigenbasis, but spin-zero states are an important exception to the claim that they can never share any eigenstate.

= Spin-independent axis-angle composition
{parent=Angular momentum operator}
{title2=$D^{(S)}(g_1g_2)=D^{(S)}(g_1)D^{(S)}(g_2)$}

The axis and angle of a product of <rotations> are properties of the group elements, independent of which <spin> <representation> acts on a state. Higher-spin generators do not obey the two-term Pauli exponential, but <representations> preserve the resulting <rotation> product. The central $2\pi$ <rotation> acts as $(-1)^{2S}I$.

= Half-ring sine-squared angular state
{parent=Angular momentum operator}

The normalized periodic state $\psi=\sqrt{8/(3\pi)}\sin^2\phi$ on $0<\phi<\pi$, zero on the remaining semicircle, has $\langle L_z\rangle=0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(L_z)=4\hbar^2/3$. Projection on $e^{i\phi}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ gives probability $64/(27\pi^2)$ for $L_z=\hbar$. Its first derivative is continuous at the joins, so integration by parts has no boundary contribution.

= Angular momentum expectation
{parent=Angular momentum operator}

For a normalized periodic angular <wavefunction>, $\langle L_z\rangle=-i\hbar\int_0^{2\pi}\psi^*\partial_\phi\psi\,d\phi$. A real periodic <wavefunction> with matching boundary values has zero mean, even though nonzero angular momenta can have positive measurement probabilities.

= Spherical tensor operator
{parent=Angular momentum operator}
{title2=$T_q^{(k)}$}

A rank-$k$ <spherical tensor operator> has components $T_q^{(k)}$, $-k\leq q\leq k$, that transform under <rotation> like an <angular momentum> $k$. Equivalently, their <commutators> with the <angular momentum operator> satisfy
$$
[J_z,T_q^{(k)}]=\hbar qT_q^{(k)},\qquad
[J_\pm,T_q^{(k)}]=\hbar\sqrt{k(k+1)-q(q\pm1)}\,T_{q\pm1}^{(k)}.
$$
For a vector operator, the rank is one and a conventional choice is $T_0=V_z$, $T_{\pm1}=\mp(V_x\pm iV_y)/\sqrt2$. The <Wigner-Eckart theorem> separates the <matrix elements> of a <spherical tensor operator> into <Clebsch-Gordan coefficients> and a <reduced matrix element>.

= Atomic orientation

A distribution of <quantum state populations> over atomic <magnetic sublevels> with a nonzero <expectation value> of the <angular momentum operator> <vector>.

= Axially symmetric quadratic orbital eigenfunction
{parent=Angular momentum operator}
{title2=$\psi=(x_3^2-r^2/3)f(r),\quad L^2\psi=6\hbar^2\psi,\quad L_3\psi=0$}

Each <orbital angular momentum> operator annihilates a radial factor and $r^2$. Direct differentiation gives $L^2x_3^2=\hbar^2(6x_3^2-2r^2)$ and $L_3x_3^2=0$. Hence the displayed trace-free quadratic polynomial, times any admissible radial factor, has orbital labels $l=2,m=0$. Its angular dependence is proportional to the <spherical harmonic> $Y_{20}$; subtracting $r^2/3$ removes the rotational scalar part.

= Angular momentum lowering operator
{parent=Angular momentum operator}
{title2=$J_-$}

The operator $J_-=J_x-iJ_y$ acts by $J_-|j,m\rangle=\hbar\sqrt{(j+m)(j-m+1)}|j,m-1\rangle$. It generates lower magnetic states and determines <Clebsch-Gordan coefficients>.

= Normalized highest-weight lowering formula
{parent=Angular momentum lowering operator}
{title2=$|I,m\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{(I+m)!}{(2I)!(I-m)!}}J_-^{I-m}|I,I\rangle$}

In an <irreducible spin representation>, the <angular momentum lowering operator> has squared <norm> factor $(I+m)(I-m+1)$. Multiplying these factors from the <highest-weight vector> gives the factorial normalization displayed above. All factorial arguments are integers even when $I$ is half-integral. This produces a consistent positive-coefficient phase convention for all weight states and fixes signs in subsequent <isospin rotations>.

= Angular momentum eigenstate
{parent=Angular momentum operator}
{title2=$|j,m\rangle$}

A joint eigenstate $|j,m\rangle$ of $J^2$ and $J_z$, with eigenvalues $\hbar^2j(j+1)$ and $\hbar m$. Its allowed labels satisfy $j\in\{0,1/2,1,\ldots\}$ and $m=-j,-j+1,\ldots,j$.

= Angular momentum commutation relations
{parent=Angular momentum operator}

Every angular momentum operator satisfies
$$
[J_i,J_j]=i\hbar\varepsilon_{ijk}J_k,
\qquad
[J^2,J_i]=0.
$$

= Addition of angular momentum
{parent=Angular momentum operator}
{wiki}

For two subsystems, the total operator is $\mathbf J=\mathbf J_1\otimes I+I\otimes\mathbf J_2$, and $J_\pm=J_{1\pm}+J_{2\pm}$. The coupled basis diagonalizes $\mathbf J^2$ and $J_z$.

= Magnetic-substate subtraction
{parent=Addition of angular momentum}
{title2=$n_J=d_J-d_{J+1}$}

Let $d_M$ count states of fixed <angular momentum> projection $M$ in a finite rotational representation, and $n_J$ its spin-$J$ multiplicity. Every spin-$J$ <irreducible representation> contributes one state at each $M=-J,\ldots,J$. Thus $d_M=\sum_{J\geq M}n_J$ for nonnegative $M$, and subtraction gives $n_J=d_J-d_{J+1}$. Counting occupation states first incorporates <Bose-Einstein statistics> before decomposing identical-boson angular states.

= Exchange parity of two coupled identical spins
{parent=Addition of angular momentum}
{title2=$P_{12}|(ss)jm\rangle=(-1)^{2s-j}|(ss)jm\rangle$}

Exchanging the factors in the <Clebsch-Gordan decomposition> of two equal <spins> gives the phase $(-1)^{2s-j}$. This follows from the exchange symmetry of the <Clebsch-Gordan coefficients>. The total exchange parity includes the spatial state and any other internal factors: the spin parity alone does not impose the full <symmetrization postulate>. For two half-integer <spins>, even $j$ is spin-antisymmetric and odd $j$ spin-symmetric.

= Maximum total angular momentum of equivalent fermions
{parent=Addition of angular momentum}
{title2=$J_{\max}=Nj-N(N-1)/2$}

For $N$ identical <fermions> restricted to one angular-momentum multiplet $j$, with all other one-particle labels fixed, the <Pauli exclusion principle> permits distinct <angular momentum> projections $j,j-1,\ldots,j-N+1$. Their sum is $Nj-N(N-1)/2$, provided $N\leq2j+1$. The resulting <Slater determinant> is killed by the total raising <angular momentum ladder operator> because raising any occupied state either exceeds $j$ or duplicates an occupied state. It is therefore a <highest-weight vector> of precisely this total <angular momentum>. The formula is invariant under replacing particles by holes in the full shell.

= Coupled angular-momentum state count
{parent=Addition of angular momentum}
{title2=$(2J_++1)+(2J_-+1)=4L+2$}

Coupling orbital <angular momentum> $L>0$ to <spin one-half> produces $J=L\pm1/2$. The <dimensions> add as $(2L+2)+2L=2(2L+1)$. A change between coupled and product <bases> changes labels, not the <dimension> of the <Hilbert space>.

= Three spin-one-half angular-momentum decomposition
{parent=Addition of angular momentum}
{title2=$(1/2)^{\otimes3}=3/2\oplus1/2\oplus1/2$}

For three distinguishable <spin-one-half> systems, the <Clebsch-Gordan decomposition> first combines two spins into $0\oplus1$, then couples the third: $0\otimes1/2=1/2$ and $1\otimes1/2=1/2\oplus3/2$. Thus the <tensor product> has one quartet and two doublets. A <Hamiltonian operator> depending only on the total $S^2$ and $S_z$ acts identically on the two doublet copies. In particular $H=\epsilon_tS^2/\hbar^2+\epsilon_zS_z/\hbar$ has <energy eigenvalues> $\epsilon_tS(S+1)+\epsilon_zM$, generically once for each quartet projection and twice for each doublet projection. Equal numerical energies have their multiplicities added.

= LS coupling
{c}
{parent=Addition of angular momentum}
{title2=$\mathbf J=\mathbf L+\mathbf S$}

An atomic <addition of angular momentum> scheme in which the individual <orbital angular momentum> vectors first combine into $\mathbf L$ and the individual <spins> into $\mathbf S$, then these combine into total <angular momentum> $\mathbf J$. When <spin-orbit coupling> is weak compared with the interactions between <electrons> described by <electrostatics>, $L$ and $S$ remain useful approximate <quantum numbers>. The <Landé g-factor> describes the resulting weak-field magnetic splitting.

= Nonequivalent p-s and p-p term counting
{parent=LS coupling}

For electrons in distinct radial orbitals, angular coupling permits all $L$ from $|l_1-l_2|$ to $l_1+l_2$ and both total <spins> $S=0,1$. A p-s pair therefore gives singlet and triplet P terms. A nonequivalent p-p pair gives singlet and triplet S, P and D terms. The equivalent-subshell exclusion restrictions do not remove these nonequivalent terms. Counting $(2S+1)(2L+1)$ gives 12 p-s and 36 p-p microstates, matching products of one-electron state counts.

= Allowed LS terms of a p-squared configuration
{parent=LS coupling}

Two equivalent $l=1$ <electrons> require an antisymmetric complete state. Orbital exchange has <parity> $(-1)^L$ and the <spin> singlet is antisymmetric, giving allowed $L+S$ even. The terms are ${}^3P_{0,1,2}$, ${}^1D_2$ and ${}^1S_0$, with $9+5+1=15$ states, equal to $\binom62$. Hund ordering puts ${}^3P_0$ lowest for the less-than-half-filled configuration.

= Total angular momentum operator
{title2=$\mathbf J=\sum_i\mathbf J_i$}
{parent=Addition of angular momentum}

For a composite quantum system, the total angular momentum operator is the sum of the constituent angular momenta, with each summand acting on its own tensor factor. Its simultaneous eigenstates with $J_z$ organize the state space into <total-spin sector>[total-spin sectors].

= Total-spin sector
{parent=Addition of angular momentum}

A total-spin-$J$ sector is an irreducible subspace on which $\mathbf J^2$ has eigenvalue $J(J+1)\hbar^2$. For two spin-one particles, <Clebsch-Gordan decomposition> gives sectors $J=0,1,2$ of dimensions one, three, and five.

= Three spin-one angular-momentum decomposition
{parent=Total-spin sector}

Coupling three distinguishable spin-one systems gives
$$
1\otimes1\otimes1
=0\oplus3(1)\oplus2(2)\oplus3,
$$
where the labels denote total spin and coefficients denote multiplicities. The dimensions are respectively $1$, $3\cdot3$, $2\cdot5$, and $7$, summing to $27$.

= Symmetric three-spin-one subspace
{parent=Three spin-one angular-momentum decomposition}

The completely symmetric subspace of three spin-one systems has dimension
$$
\binom{3+3-1}{3}=10
$$
and decomposes into one total-spin-$3$ multiplet and one total-spin-$1$ multiplet:
$$
\operatorname{Sym}^3(1)=3\oplus1.
$$
The highest-weight product state generates the seven-dimensional spin-$3$ multiplet; its three-dimensional invariant complement is spin $1$.

= Spin-one Cartesian basis
{parent=Total-spin sector}

The spin-one Cartesian basis $|x\rangle,|y\rangle,|z\rangle$ transforms as a three-dimensional vector under rotations. In its two-site tensor square, the scalar, antisymmetric, and symmetric traceless subspaces are respectively the total-spin $0$, $1$, and $2$ sectors.

= Spin-one reversal under quadratic anisotropy
{parent=Spin-one Cartesian basis}
{title2=$P_{-x}(t)=\sin^2[(A+B)\hbar t/2]$}

For a spin-one <Hamiltonian> $H=AS_z^2+B(S_x^2-S_y^2)$, Cartesian basis energies are $\hbar^2(A-B)$, $\hbar^2(A+B)$ and zero along the $x,y,z$ basis labels. The $S_x=\hbar$ state combines the latter two basis vectors equally. Their relative phase produces the displayed spin-reversal probability. Complete reversal occurs at odd multiples of $\pi/(|A+B|\hbar)$ if $A+B\ne0$; when it vanishes those two components remain degenerate. Individual matrix signs depend on basis conventions, while the <commutators> and physical probability must remain consistent.

= Spin-dot-product eigenvalue
{parent=Total-spin sector}

For two spins of magnitude $s$, $\mathbf S_1\mathbin\cdot\mathbf S_2=\tfrac12(\mathbf J^2-\mathbf S_1^2-\mathbf S_2^2)$. For two spin-one particles its eigenvalues in total-spin sectors $J=0,1,2$ are $-2,-1,1$ in units of $\hbar^2$.

= Spin-three-halves pair spectrum
{parent=Spin-dot-product eigenvalue}
{title2=$\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2/\hbar^2\in\{-15/4,-11/4,-3/4,9/4\}$}

For $s_1=s_2=3/2$, <angular momentum addition> gives one copy of $j=0,1,2,3$. The identity $2\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2=\mathbf J^2-\mathbf S_1^2-\mathbf S_2^2$ gives the four <eigenvalues> in the title, with <state degeneracy> $1,3,5,7$. These exhaust the $4\times4$ spin space. If the particles are identical <fermions>, an appropriate spatial exchange parity accommodates each sector; only a specified spatial restriction can remove sectors.

= Clebsch-Gordan decomposition
{c}
{parent=Addition of angular momentum}

The tensor product of spin-$j_1$ and spin-$j_2$ irreducible representations decomposes into one copy of each spin
$$
j=|j_1-j_2|,|j_1-j_2|+1,\ldots,j_1+j_2.
$$
The change-of-basis entries between uncoupled and coupled states are Clebsch--Gordan coefficients.

= Lowest-weight coupling of two angular momenta
{parent=Clebsch-Gordan decomposition}
{title2=$|s-1,-s+1\rangle=\sqrt{j_2/s}|a\rangle-\sqrt{j_1/s}|b\rangle$}

Put $s=j_1+j_2$, with both $j_i>0$. At magnetic quantum number $-s+1$, let $|a\rangle$ raise only the first factor of the lowest product state and $|b\rangle$ raise only the second. Applying total $J_+$ to the lowest product gives the normalized $J=s$ combination $\sqrt{j_1/s}|a\rangle+\sqrt{j_2/s}|b\rangle$. Its orthogonal combination is annihilated by total $J_-$ and therefore has $J=s-1$. This obtains a useful <Clebsch-Gordan coefficients> without a table.

= Highest-weight states in angular momentum addition
{parent=Addition of angular momentum}

With $J=j_1+j_2$, the maximal state is the product $|J,J\rangle=|j_1,j_1\rangle|j_2,j_2\rangle$. Lowering it once gives
$$
|J,J-1\rangle=
\sqrt{\frac{j_1}{J}}|j_1,j_1-1\rangle|j_2,j_2\rangle
+\sqrt{\frac{j_2}{J}}|j_1,j_1\rangle|j_2,j_2-1\rangle.
$$
The orthogonal combination is the highest-weight state $|J-1,J-1\rangle$.

= Singlet state
{parent=Addition of angular momentum}
{wiki}

= Angular momentum singlet state
{synonym}

= Rotation singlet
{synonym}

Two equal spins $j$ have the unique total-spin-zero state
$$
|0,0\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt{2j+1}}
\sum_{m=-j}^j(-1)^{j-m}|j,m\rangle|j,-m\rangle.
$$
It is invariant under joint rotations.

A <singlet state> is the one-dimensional rotation representation; this construction supplies the angular-momentum-zero case for two equal spins.

= Spin-one singlet state
{parent=Singlet state}
{title2=$|0,0\rangle=(|1,-1\rangle-|0,0\rangle+|-1,1\rangle)/\sqrt3$}

In the product basis $|m_1,m_2\rangle$ for two spin-one particles, total magnetic quantum number zero allows only $|1,-1\rangle$, $|0,0\rangle$ and $|-1,1\rangle$. Applying the total <ladder operators> to $\alpha|1,-1\rangle+\beta|0,0\rangle+\gamma|-1,1\rangle$ gives
$$
S_+|\psi\rangle=\sqrt2\hbar[(\alpha+\beta)|1,0\rangle+(\beta+\gamma)|0,1\rangle].
$$
A <singlet state> is annihilated by $S_+$ and $S_-$, so $\alpha=\gamma=-\beta$. <Wavefunction normalization> gives the displayed state, up to an overall phase. Here the ket on the left of the title formula has total-spin labels, while its right-hand kets have individual magnetic labels. The state is symmetric under exchanging the particles, although no exchange constraint is needed for distinguishable particles.

= Spin-one-half singlet state
{title2=$|0,0\rangle$}
{parent=Singlet state}

For two spin-one-half particles, the singlet is
$$
|0,0\rangle=\frac{|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle-|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle}{\sqrt2}.
$$
Every component of the total spin annihilates it, and $\mathbf S^2$ has eigenvalue zero.

= Spin-one-half triplet state

The symmetric two-particle states

|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle,
\qquad
\frac{|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle+|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle}{\sqrt2},

= Total spin
{parent=Spin}
{title2=$\mathbf S_{\rm tot}=\sum_n\mathbf S_n$}

The <total spin> is the <vector> sum of subsystem <spin operators> and generates their simultaneous <rotation>. For a rotationally invariant <Heisenberg model>, both its squared magnitude and any chosen component commute with the <Hamiltonian>. An anisotropic exchange or field can preserve only a component.

= Spin rotation
{parent=Spin}
{title2=$U(\boldsymbol\theta)=e^{-i\boldsymbol\theta\cdot\mathbf S}$}

A <spin rotation> acts on a <quantum state> through the <unitary representation> generated by its <angular momentum operators>. With $\hbar=1$, <rotation> by angle $\theta$ about axis $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is $e^{-i\theta\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf S}$. Simultaneously rotating every site is a global <symmetry> of an isotropic <Heisenberg model>.

= Spin operator
{parent=Spin}
{title2=$\hat{\mathbf S}$}

A <spin operator> describes intrinsic <angular momentum>. Its components obey angular-momentum <commutators>; for spin one half, $\hat S_j=\hbar\sigma_j/2$ in terms of the <Pauli matrices>.

= Uniform-field spin precession
{parent=Spin operator}
{title2=$U(t)=\exp[-i\alpha Bt\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2]$}

For a spin-half particle with $H=\alpha\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf S$ and $\mathbf S=\hbar\boldsymbol\sigma/2$, exponentiating the <Pauli matrices> gives a rotation about the field axis. A field along $y$ evolves initial $z$-up <spin> to $(\cos(\alpha Bt/2),\sin(\alpha Bt/2))^T$. The sign of $\alpha$ sets the precession direction. <spin> parallel to the field is conserved even while transverse components vary.

= Spin measurement in the yz plane
{parent=Uniform-field spin precession}
{title2=$P_\pm=[1\pm\cos\theta\cos(\alpha Bt)]/2$}

For a state initially $z$-up and precessing about $y$, measurement along $\mathbf n=(0,\sin\theta,\cos\theta)$ has the displayed <probabilities>. <eigenvectors> can be chosen as $(\cos(\theta/2),i\sin(\theta/2))^T$ and $(i\sin(\theta/2),\cos(\theta/2))^T$. Their <phases> must be retained when taking <inner products>. Full $2\pi$ rotation changes their signs but leaves isolated-state <probabilities> unchanged.

= Spin-one-half quadratic anisotropy is constant
{parent=Spin operator}
{title2=$S_x^2=S_y^2=S_z^2=\hbar^2I/4$}

For <spin> one-half, the Pauli representation makes every diagonal quadratic component a multiple of the identity. Thus $AS_z^2+B(S_x^2-S_y^2)=A\hbar^2I/4$ cannot split the doublet or cause <spin> reversal. This conclusion is special to this quadratic <Hamiltonian> and does not exclude linear Zeeman dynamics.

= Spin contamination
{parent=Spin}
{title2=$\langle\hat S^2\rangle-S(S+1)\hbar^2$}

A <wavefunction> intended for a specified total-spin state contains components of other total spins. For an unrestricted <determinant> with one alpha and one beta orbital, $\langle S^2\rangle=1-|\langle\phi_\alpha|\phi_\beta\rangle|^2$ in units of $\hbar^2$.

= Spin multiplicity
{parent=Spin}
{title2=$2S+1$}
{wiki}

A state with total <spin angular momentum> quantum number $S$ has $2S+1$ magnetic projections. A <singlet state> has $S=0$, a <triplet state> has $S=1$, and the $S=3$ <chromium> ground term is a septet.

= Two-spin XY Hamiltonian
{parent=Spin}
{title2=$H=-J(\sigma_1^x\sigma_2^x+\sigma_1^y\sigma_2^y)-h(\sigma_1^z+\sigma_2^z)$}

The two-site quantum version of an <XY model> couples the transverse components of two <spin one-half> degrees of freedom. In the ordered basis $|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle,|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle,|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle,|\downarrow\downarrow\rangle$, its only off-diagonal entries are $H_{23}=H_{32}=-2J$. The parallel states have <energies> $-2h$ and $2h$; the symmetric and antisymmetric opposite-spin combinations have <energies> $-2J$ and $2J$ respectively. The latter is the <singlet state>. The formulas remain valid at accidental degeneracies.

= Spin eigenstate
{parent=Spin}
{title2=$|s,m\rangle$}

A spin eigenstate along a specified axis is a simultaneous <eigenstate> of the squared <spin> operator $S^2$ and its component $S_{\hat n}$. Its <eigenvalues> are $\hbar^2s(s+1)$ and $\hbar m$, where $m=-s,-s+1,\ldots,s$. The axis must be specified because components along different axes generally do not commute. For <spin one-half>, the two field-axis eigenstates have projections $\pm\hbar/2$ and form the branches of a <Stern-Gerlach experiment>.

= Spin-spin interaction
{parent=Spin}
{title2=$H_{ij}\propto\mathbf S_i\cdot\mathbf S_j$}

A <spin-spin interaction> couples two <spin angular momenta>. In an isotropic model it is proportional to their <dot product>, whose expectation in a total-pair-spin state follows from $2\mathbf S_i\cdot\mathbf S_j=S_{ij}^2-S_i^2-S_j^2$. Magnetic interactions generate examples in atoms and effective colour-magnetic interactions in <hadron> models.

= Spin polarization
{parent=Spin}
{wiki}
{title2=$\mathbf P_s=2\langle\mathbf S\rangle/\hbar$}

For a spin-one-half ensemble, <spin polarization> measures unequal occupation or coherent alignment of <spin> states. A spin-dependent energy splitting does not alone determine the ensemble's response: occupation, <temperature> and <spin relaxation> matter. A <spin polarization> is distinct from a net change of <charge density> and from the optical <polarization of waves>.

= Beam spin polarization
{parent=Spin polarization}
{title2=$P=(N_+-N_-)/(N_++N_-)$}

A beam's unequal population of <spin> projections along a specified axis. For spin-one-half particles the displayed <longitudinal polarization> uses populations parallel and antiparallel to the <momentum>. An unpolarized beam has equal populations and requires an initial-spin average in a <cross section>; a polarized beam weights the corresponding <helicity> amplitudes differently. This differs from <photon> linear polarization.

= Spin texture
{parent=Spin polarization}
{title2=$\mathbf s(\mathbf r)\text{ or }\mathbf s(\mathbf k)$}

A spin texture describes how a <spin expectation value> or <spin polarization> varies with <position> or <wavevector>. It specifies a <vector field>, including its magnitude when that matters; a direction field alone is undefined wherever that magnitude vanishes. A <Rashba spin texture> is an example in which <spin expectation values> wind around circles in the plane of <wavevectors>.

= Spin-rotation coupling
{parent=Spin}
{title2=$H_{\mathrm{rot}}=-\boldsymbol\Omega\cdot\boldsymbol J$}

The <spin-rotation coupling> contribution to a rotating-frame <Hamiltonian> couples <angular velocity> to <angular momentum>. When discussing <spin> alone, identify which part of the total <angular momentum> enters the effective model.

= Spin relaxation

A transverse magnetic field rotates a nonequilibrium <spin polarization> while diffusion and <spin relaxation> redistribute it. The field dependence of a detector signal can constrain spin lifetime and transport parameters. = Spin-memory loss
{parent=Spin relaxation}

Loss of transported spin polarization at an interface, distinct from bulk spin relaxation in an adjacent layer.

= Spin diffusion length
{parent=Spin relaxation}
{title2=$\ell_s$}

The distance over which a nonequilibrium spin imbalance decays appreciably in a diffusive conductor. In a simple diffusion-relaxation model it is $\ell_s=\sqrt{D\tau_s}$; interfaces can contribute additional spin-memory loss.

= Spin-lattice relaxation
{parent=Spin relaxation}

Exchange of <energy> between magnetic <spin> degrees of freedom and the surrounding lattice, allowing their <temperatures> to approach <thermal equilibrium>. It permits <adiabatic demagnetization> to cool a solid and its attached load.

= Jordan–Wigner transformation
{c}
{parent=Spin}
{wiki}
{title2=$S_n^+=\prod_{m<n}(1-2c_m^\dagger c_m)c_n$}

The Jordan–Wigner transformation represents a one-dimensional <spin one-half> chain by fermions with a parity string preceding each site. With $P_n=\prod_{m<n}(1-2n_m)$, one has $S_n^+=P_nc_n$ and $S_n^z=1/2-n_n$. The string converts the off-site <canonical anticommutation relations> into commuting spin operators. Adjacent exchange terms become quadratic fermion expressions, while a periodic end bond depends on global <fermion parity>.

= Bondwise cancellation of a Jordan-Wigner string
{parent=Jordan–Wigner transformation}
{title2=$4S_n^xS_{n+1}^x=(c_n^\dagger-c_n)(c_{n+1}^\dagger+c_{n+1})$}

For $S_n^+=P_nc_n$ with $P_n=\prod_{m<n}(1-2c_m^\dagger c_m)$, neighbouring <Jordan–Wigner transformation> strings differ by only the local parity factor. The <canonical anticommutation relations> give $(c_n+c_n^\dagger)(1-2c_n^\dagger c_n)=c_n^\dagger-c_n$, proving the displayed local <spin> identity. The closing bond of a periodic chain instead retains total <fermion parity>; cancellation on bulk bonds does not justify discarding that global sector.

= Parity-dependent boundary condition of the Jordan-Wigner chain
{parent=Jordan–Wigner transformation}
{title2=$c_{N+1}=-(-1)^{N_f}c_1$}

For $S_n^+=\prod_{m<n}(1-2n_m)c_n$ and periodic <spins>, the end exchange term carries the global <fermion parity> $P=(-1)^{N_f}$. = Two-site Jordan-Wigner normalization and signs
{parent=Jordan–Wigner transformation}
{title2=$s_j^\pm=(\sigma_j^x\pm i\sigma_j^y)/2,\quad\sigma_j^z=2f_j^\dagger f_j-1$}

= Neutron Stern-Gerlach splitting

= Stern-Gerlach force and flight model
{parent=Stern-Gerlach experiment}
{c}
{title2=$F_z\simeq\mu_z\partial_zB_z,\quad z_s-z_0=\mu_zG\ell(D+\ell/2)/(Mv^2)$}

Let the <atomic beam> travel along $x$, with <velocity> $v>0$, and let $z$ measure displacement along the dominant <magnetic field> <gradient>. For a fixed effective <magnetic moment> component $\mu_z$, the <magnetic dipole potential energy> is $U\simeq-\mu_zB_z$. Taking its negative <partial derivative> gives
$$
F_z=-\frac{\partial U}{\partial z}\simeq\mu_z\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial z}=\mu_zG.
$$
A spatially uniform <magnetic field> can produce <torque> and <Larmor precession>, but cannot produce this translational separation. In a <quantum state> following a local internal <energy eigenvalue> $E_n(B)$, the more general expression is $F_z=-\partial_zE_n$, with effective <magnetic moment> $-\partial E_n/\partial B$. Treating that moment as constant is an approximation with a physical regime, not an extra measured fact. Assume the local <magnetic field> direction stays nearly fixed across the sampled beam, and that internal states follow it without transitions, as in the <adiabatic theorem>. The scalar <gradient> model describes the beam's neighborhood; it omits the full <magnetic field>'s transverse components and fringe regions.

Assume a constant $G$ over magnet length $\ell$, followed by a field-free drift $D$. Neglect transverse motion at entry and changes in longitudinal <velocity>. <Newton's second law> gives transverse <acceleration> $a_z=\mu_zG/M$ for whole-atom <mass> $M$. The magnet transit time is $t_m=\ell/v$, and the drift time is $t_d=D/v$. The magnet supplies both a displacement and an exit transverse <velocity>:
$$
z_m-z_0=\frac{a_zt_m^2}{2},\qquad
v_{z,m}=a_zt_m,\qquad
z_s-z_0=\frac{\mu_zG}{Mv^2}\left(\frac{\ell^2}{2}+\ell D\right).
$$
The $\ell D$ contribution is motion with the acquired transverse <velocity>, not continued <acceleration> outside the magnet. For the plate-at-exit geometry, set $D=0$. For a nonzero entrance transverse <velocity> $w$, add $w(\ell+D)/v$. These terms show why initial <collimation> and flight geometry matter when inferring a <magnetic moment> from a deposit.

Choose hypothetical parameters $M=1.79\times10^{-25}\,\mathrm{kg}$, $G=300\,\mathrm{T\,m^{-1}}$, $\ell=0.035\,\mathrm m$, $D=0.100\,\mathrm m$, and $v=500\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Approximate the two effective <magnetic moments> by $\mu_z=\pm\mu_B$, where the <Bohr magneton> is $\mu_B\simeq9.274\times10^{-24}\,\mathrm{J\,T^{-1}}$. Then $|a_z|\simeq1.55\times10^4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, $t_m=70\,\mu\mathrm s$ and $t_d=200\,\mu\mathrm s$. The positive displacement is about $0.0381\,\mathrm{mm}$ inside the magnet plus $0.2176\,\mathrm{mm}$ during drift: $d=0.2557\,\mathrm{mm}$, with full separation $2d=0.5114\,\mathrm{mm}$. These chosen values illustrate the model and are not reconstructed 1922 measurements.

The exit transverse <velocity> is only about $1.09\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, small compared with the chosen forward <velocity>; this checks the small-deflection assumption. Halving $G$ halves $d$, doubling $v$ divides $d$ by four, and setting $D=0$ removes most of this example's separation. A real pole has fringe <magnetic fields> and a position-dependent <gradient>; these require a more complete trajectory calculation.

= Stern-Gerlach discrete and classical profiles
{parent=Stern-Gerlach experiment}
{c}
{title2=$d(v)=\mu_BG\ell(D+\ell/2)/(Mv^2)$}

For an equally populated ideal pair of <angular momentum eigenstates>, the <magnetic moment> component takes two values, $\pm\mu_B$, and the displacements at fixed <velocity> are $\pm d(v)$. Compare a specified classical alternative: every <chemical atom> carries a permanent <magnetic moment> of fixed magnitude $\mu_B$, with independently isotropic initial orientations and no dissipative alignment. If $u=\cos\theta$, the sphere's area element is $2\pi\,du$, so $u$ has a <uniform distribution> on $[-1,1]$. With precession preserving this component in the ideal geometry, the displacement $d(v)u$ fills the interval $[-d(v),d(v)]$ continuously. A pair of bands therefore tests allowed components rather than merely proving that <silver> is magnetic. Other classical preparation assumptions would be different models and must be stated.

To include finite instrumental width, choose a normalized <Gaussian distribution>
$$
\phi_\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\exp\left(-\frac{z^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)
$$
with <standard deviation> $\sigma=0.035\,\mathrm{mm}$, representing a combined field-off spatial width. At fixed <velocity> the model <probability density functions> are
$$
p_{\rm two}(z\mid v)=\frac{\phi_\sigma(z-d(v))+\phi_\sigma(z+d(v))}{2},\qquad
p_{\rm classical}(z\mid v)=\frac12\int_{-1}^1\phi_\sigma(z-d(v)u)\,du.
$$
Each has unit <integral> and zero <mean>; the different shapes, rather than a different average displacement, distinguish them. The discrete model has <variance> $\sigma^2+d(v)^2$, whereas the classical model has <variance> $\sigma^2+d(v)^2/3$. Their common mean also explains why the raw average landing position is insufficient to identify the internal <quantum state>.

Now choose a <uniform distribution> of forward <velocities> from $400$ to $600\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, explicitly as an example rather than the oven's actual distribution. Average either conditional <probability density function> over that interval. The positive-channel displacement ranges from $0.1776$ to $0.3995\,\mathrm{mm}$, since slower <chemical atoms> spend longer accelerating and drifting. The two components broaden into bands even though their internal <magnetic moment> values remain discrete. The corresponding classical profile continues to populate the center. The displayed strip darkness is proportional to predicted deposited number density, with equal total arrivals; it is not a photograph or a calibrated optical response of a developed film.

= Stern-Gerlach historical and modern interpretations
{parent=Stern-Gerlach experiment}
{c}

The 1922 interpretation concerned <space quantization> of atomic <magnetic moments> in the old <quantum theory>. The modern electron <spin> proposal came in 1925; the original observers did not identify intrinsic electron <spin> as the cause of their splitting. An orbital interpretation then seemed plausible, but the later <spin> explanation gives a different account of the same two-component evidence.<The Stern-Gerlach Experiment and the Electron Spin by Sandip Pakvasa>[Pakvasa, pp. 1–3: original orbital interpretation, 1925 spin proposals and later reinterpretation]{ref}

Modern atomic data assign neutral <silver> the <ground state> <electron configuration> $[\mathrm{Kr}]4d^{10}5s$ and electronic term ${}^2S_{1/2}$. The filled <electron shells> contribute zero net electronic <angular momentum>; the outer <unpaired electron> has <orbital angular momentum> $L=0$, <spin> $S=1/2$, and electronic total <angular momentum> $J=1/2$. The <magnetic quantum number> has two values $m_J=\pm1/2$.<Handbook of Basic Atomic Spectroscopic Data by Jean E. Sansonetti and William C. Martin/Atomic Data for Silver (Ag)>[Handbook of Basic Atomic Spectroscopic Data by Jean E. Sansonetti and William C. Martin; Ag I ground-state configuration and term; electronic J, distinct from isotope nuclear-spin rows]{ref}

In the effective electronic description,
$$
\mu_z=-g_J\mu_B\frac{J_z}{\hbar},\qquad
J_z|J,m_J\rangle=\hbar m_J|J,m_J\rangle,\qquad
\mu_z\simeq\mp\mu_B\quad(g_J\simeq2).
$$
The minus sign matters: for positive $G$, the component with $m_J=+1/2$ has negative electronic <magnetic moment> and deflects toward negative $z$ in our coordinates. Calling the upper spot “spin up” without specifying the <magnetic field> and channel calibration can therefore reverse the assignment. The <total angular momentum operator> here sums electronic <orbital angular momentum> and electronic <spin>; $J$ is not a claim that the <atomic nucleus> has no <spin>. <Hyperfine structure> and nuclear <magnetic moments> can require additional internal levels, so a two-channel electronic approximation must be justified for the field and resolution being used.

This is a neutral <atomic beam>, not a beam of free <electrons>. The deflected <mass> in the flight equations is the entire <silver> <chemical atom>; its dominant electronic <magnetic moment> supplies the state-dependent <force>. Replacing that atom by a free <electron> would also introduce the charged particle's <Lorentz force> and a different preparation problem. The ideal two-state model is useful because it isolates the connection between discrete internal components and spatially resolved outputs; it does not retroactively turn the 1922 deposit into a direct contemporary identification of intrinsic electron <spin>.

= Holstein–Primakoff transformation

The Holstein–Primakoff transformation represents a spin-$S$ <operator algebra> with a bosonic <creation operator> and <annihilation operator>:
$$
S^+=\sqrt{2S}\sqrt{1-\frac{a^\dagger a}{2S}}\,a,\qquad S^-=\sqrt{2S}\,a^\dagger\sqrt{1-\frac{a^\dagger a}{2S}},\qquad S^z=S-a^\dagger a.
$$
The order of the square root and oscillator operator matters. On the physical <Fock states> $|n\rangle$, $0\le n\le2S$, the raising and lowering matrix elements are $\sqrt{n(2S-n+1)}$ and $\sqrt{(n+1)(2S-n)}$. Their squared difference gives $[S^+,S^-]|n\rangle=2(S-n)|n\rangle$, proving the <spin commutation relations>. Expanding the square root yields the <linear spin-wave approximation> and its interaction corrections.

For an even periodic classical chain with <spin> length $S$ and $J>0$, each bond attains the negative largest <singular value> of $\operatorname{diag}(1,1,\Delta)$. For $\Delta>1$ the two minimizing configurations are alternating $z$ <spins>. For $|\Delta|<1$ they are planar <Néel states> with arbitrary in-plane angle. For $\Delta<-1$ the two configurations have uniform $z$ <spins>. At $\Delta=1$ the Néel direction is arbitrary on a sphere; at $\Delta=-1$ a staggered transverse component and uniform longitudinal component parameterize another spherical degeneracy. These are classical configuration manifolds, not a claim about finite quantum-chain degeneracy.

= Classical Heisenberg model
{parent=Heisenberg model}
{title2=$E=-J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\mathbf s_i\cdot\mathbf s_j,\quad|\mathbf s_i|=1$}

The <classical Heisenberg model> assigns a three-component unit <vector> to each site and couples pairs by their <dot product>. Positive $J$ favors parallel alignment. With no applied <magnetic field> or anisotropy, simultaneous rotations of all vectors preserve the <energy>, so an ordered state chooses a direction and exhibits <spontaneous symmetry breaking>. The <vector order parameter> is the spatially averaged orientation. A quartic <Landau free energy> describes its mean-field onset near a continuous transition; fluctuations can change the critical exponents.<Statistical Field Theory by David Tong>[§4.2, $O(3)$ Heisenberg model]{ref}

= Classical Heisenberg mean-field partition function
{parent=Classical Heisenberg model}

For unit three-component classical <spins> and energy $-J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\mathbf S_i\cdot\mathbf S_j$ on a lattice of coordination $z_c$, mean-field decoupling gives $E_{\rm MF}=-z_cJS\sum_iS_{iz}+Nz_cJS^2/2$. Integrating each <spin> over <solid angle> yields
$$
Z_{\rm MF}(S)=e^{-\beta Nz_cJS^2/2}
[4\pi\sinh(\beta z_cJS)/(\beta z_cJS)]^N.
$$
The constant term corrects double counting. A normalized angular measure removes $(4\pi)^N$ but does not change the <self-consistency equation>. The lattice dimension and spin-space dimension are different data.

= Classical Heisenberg Langevin self-consistency
{parent=Classical Heisenberg mean-field partition function}
{title2=$S=\coth(\beta z_cJS)-1/(\beta z_cJS)$}

The single-site average in molecular field $z_cJS$ is $S=\coth x-1/x$, where $x=\beta z_cJS$. Thus $\tau x=\coth x-1/x$ with $\tau=k_BT/(z_cJ)$. The small-$x$ slope $1/3$ gives $k_BT_c^{\rm MF}=z_cJ/3$. On a <square lattice> $z_c=4$. This finite mean-field transition does not imply actual positive-temperature <magnetization> for the ideal two-dimensional short-range isotropic model, where the <Mermin-Wagner theorem> applies.

= Magnetization sectors of a spin chain
{parent=Heisenberg model}
{title2=$S=N-2k,\quad\dim\mathcal H_k=\binom Nk$}

With $S=\sum_{n=1}^NZ_n$, a computational basis vector containing $k$ excitations has eigenvalue $N-2k$. There are $N+1$ distinct eigenspaces, of dimensions $\binom Nk$. Any Hamiltonian commuting with $S$ preserves each sector and their population weights. In particular, a state supported in one sector cannot be transferred to a different sector by such controls.

= Excitation-number conservation in XXZ spin chains
{c}
{parent=Heisenberg model}
{title2=$[H,\sum_nZ_n]=0$}

For $H=\sum_{m<n}c_{mn}(X_mX_n+Y_mY_n)+\gamma_{mn}Z_mZ_n$, Pauli <commutators> give cancellation between the $XX$ and $YY$ terms in $[H,Z_m+Z_n]$, while each $ZZ$ term commutes individually. Hence total spin magnetization is conserved. Local $Z_k$ controls preserve the same law, so dynamics split into fixed-excitation sectors even when a particular sector is controllable.

= Single-excitation subspace
{parent=Heisenberg model}
{title2=$\mathcal H_1=\operatorname{span}\{|10\cdots0\rangle,\ldots,|0\cdots01\rangle\}$}

For a network of $N$ spin-one-half sites with $Z|0\rangle=|0\rangle$ and $Z|1\rangle=-|1\rangle$, the single-excitation subspace has exactly one spin in state $|1\rangle$ and dimension $N$. Excitation-conserving exchange interactions preserve it. Its restricted Hamiltonian is a hopping matrix whose entries connect the corresponding sites. Controllability within this subspace need not imply controllability on the full $2^N$-dimensional space.

= XY exchange interaction
{parent=Single-excitation subspace}
{c}
{title2=$E=(X\otimes X+Y\otimes Y)/2$}

The isotropic XY exchange interaction between two <qubits> is $E=(X\otimes X+Y\otimes Y)/2$. Its action is
$$
E|00\rangle=E|11\rangle=0,\qquad E|01\rangle=|10\rangle,\qquad E|10\rangle=|01\rangle.
$$
Indeed, $X|0\rangle=|1\rangle$, $X|1\rangle=|0\rangle$, $Y|0\rangle=i|1\rangle$, and $Y|1\rangle=-i|0\rangle$. The two terms cancel on equal bits and add on unequal bits. Thus a network <Hamiltonian operator> $H=\sum_{a,b}K_{ab}E_{ab}$ preserves excitation number; in the <single-excitation subspace>, it is the weighted adjacency <matrix>, moving an excitation from $a$ to $b$ with hopping coefficient $K_{ab}$.

= Symmetric-arm reduction of an exchange Hamiltonian
{parent=XY exchange interaction}
{title2=$HF=FH_T,\quad |s_k\rangle=q^{-1/2}\sum_a|a,k\rangle$}

Consider $q$ identical length-$n$ arms joined to one hub, with <XY exchange interactions> of strength $J_0/\sqrt q$ from the hub to each first site and strength $J_k$ between sites $k,k+1$ on each arm. Let $|s_0\rangle$ be the hub excitation and $|s_k\rangle=q^{-1/2}\sum_{a=1}^q|a,k\rangle$. This <orthonormal> symmetric <single-excitation subspace> is invariant, and its restricted <Hamiltonian operator> is
$$
H|s_0\rangle=J_0|s_1\rangle,\qquad H|s_k\rangle=J_{k-1}|s_{k-1}\rangle+J_k|s_{k+1}\rangle,
$$
with endpoint terms omitted. At the first link the $q$ hub contributions add to $J_0$; all other links act identically on the arms. Thus the <linear isometry> $F|k\rangle=|s_k\rangle$ intertwines $H$ with the path <Hamiltonian operator> $H_T=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}J_k(|k\rangle\langle k+1|+|k+1\rangle\langle k|)$: $HF=FH_T$. Expanding the <matrix exponential> gives $e^{-iHt}F=Fe^{-iH_Tt}$. <Perfect quantum state transfer> from zero to $n$ on the path therefore produces an equal excitation superposition at the $q$ arm tips. Since all other <qubits> are zero, the tip state is a pure <W state>, up to the transfer's common phase.

= Endpoint control of an XX spin chain
{c}
{parent=Single-excitation subspace}
{title2=$\mathfrak g|_{\mathcal H_1}=\mathfrak u(N)\ (N\ge3)$}

In the <single-excitation subspace>, take a connected real nearest-neighbor hopping matrix $A$ with every coupling nonzero and endpoint control $B=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,\ldots,1)$. Endpoint commutators isolate the first transition, then propagate along the chain to generate both off-diagonal skew-Hermitian directions and adjacent diagonal differences. These generate $\mathfrak{su}(N)$. For $N\ge3$, $\operatorname{Tr}B=N-2\ne0$ supplies the central direction, giving $\mathfrak u(N)$. For $N=2$ the algebra is $\mathfrak{su}(2)$, which still gives full state controllability but omits an arbitrary global phase. A broken coupling separates an unreachable chain component.

= Heisenberg antiferromagnet
{c}
{parent=Heisenberg model}

A Heisenberg antiferromagnet has positive exchange coupling in $H=J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\mathbf S_i\mathbin\cdot\mathbf S_j$, so neighboring spins energetically prefer antiparallel alignment.

= Bipartite spin rotation
{parent=Heisenberg antiferromagnet}

A <bipartite spin rotation> rotates the <spin> on one sublattice by $\pi$, converting an alternating classical <Néel state> to an all-up reference state. Rotation about $x$ sends $S^z\mapsto-S^z$ and $S^\pm\mapsto S^\mp$ on that sublattice. For an isotropic nearest-neighbor <Heisenberg antiferromagnet>, a bond becomes $-S_i^zS_j^z+(S_i^+S_j^++S_i^-S_j^-)/2$. The transformation is unitary and preserves the <spin commutation relations>; it changes the reference frame, not the model's spectrum.

= Heisenberg ferromagnet
{c}
{parent=Heisenberg model}

A Heisenberg ferromagnet has negative exchange coupling in $H=J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\mathbf S_i\mathbin\cdot\mathbf S_j$, so neighboring spins energetically prefer parallel alignment.

= Ferromagnetic ground-state multiplet
{parent=Heisenberg ferromagnet}
{title2=$S_{\rm tot}=NS,\quad\dim=2NS+1$}

A connected isotropic spin-$S$ ferromagnet with positive exchanges has a fully polarized <ground state> and a maximal-total-spin multiplet. For $N$ sites the total spin is $NS$, with $2NS+1$ magnetic states generated by total-spin lowering. Global rotations span the same degenerate space. Slowly varying reorientations produce gapless <ferromagnetic magnons>; finite-volume degeneracy must be distinguished from a thermodynamic statement about spontaneous order.

= Two-spin Heisenberg Hamiltonian in an opposing longitudinal field
{c}
{parent=Heisenberg model}

For two spin-one-half particles,
$$
H=\alpha\mathbf S^{(1)}\mathbin\cdot\mathbf S^{(2)}
+B(S_z^{(1)}-S_z^{(2)})
$$
has two eigenvalues $\alpha\hbar^2/4$ and two further eigenvalues
$$
-\frac{\alpha\hbar^2}{4}
\pm\sqrt{B^2\hbar^2+\frac{\alpha^2\hbar^4}{4}}.
$$
For positive $\alpha$, the zero-field limit has a unique <spin-one-half singlet state> ground state and a triply degenerate <spin-one-half triplet state> excited level.

= All-to-all Heisenberg model
{c}
{parent=Heisenberg model}

For $N$ spin-one-half systems and one term per unordered pair,
$$
\sum_{i<j}\boldsymbol\sigma_i\mathbin{\cdot}\boldsymbol\sigma_j
=2\mathbf S^2-\frac{3N}{2},
\qquad
\mathbf S=\frac12\sum_i\boldsymbol\sigma_i.
$$
Its minimum lies in the smallest available total-spin sector.

= Infinite-coordination Heisenberg antiferromagnet
{c}
{parent=Heisenberg model}

For a bipartite spin-one-half Heisenberg antiferromagnet with coordination $z\to\infty$, the two-sublattice mean-field state becomes exact. Antiparallel pure Bloch vectors give bond energy $-J/4$ for the convention $J\mathbf S_i\cdot\mathbf S_j$.
