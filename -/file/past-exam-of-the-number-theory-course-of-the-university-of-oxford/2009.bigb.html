= Solution
{parent=2009/a/1/a}

The <alternating polynomial> given by the Vandermonde product is alternating: interchanging two variables changes its sign. One proof writes it as a <Vandermonde determinant>, up to a fixed sign, and interchanging two variables interchanges the corresponding determinant rows. Since <transpositions> generate $S_n$,
$$
\boxed{\sigma(\delta)=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\delta,\qquad\sigma(\Delta)=\Delta.}
$$
Thus every even <permutation> fixes $\delta$, while a <transposition> negates the nonzero formal <polynomial>, so it is not invariant under all of $S_n$. This uses $n\ge2$ and <characteristic> different from two, as in the subsequent rational setting. For $n=1$ both <groups> are trivial and the last distinction does not arise.

= Solution
{parent=2009/a/1/b}

The <discriminant> is a <symmetric polynomial> in the four roots, so the <fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials> expresses it polynomially in the <elementary symmetric polynomial>. For this depressed quartic they are $e_1=e_2=0$, $e_3=-r$, $e_4=s$. Scaling each root by $t$ multiplies the <discriminant> by $t^{12}$, while $r,s$ scale by $t^3,t^4$. Thus only weighted-degree-twelve monomials can appear, giving $\Delta_f=Ar^4+Bs^3$.

For $r=0$, use $f'=4X^3$ and the monic <discriminant> identity $\Delta_f=(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}\prod_if'(\alpha_i)$. At $n=4$ this gives $4^4(\prod_i\alpha_i)^3=256s^3$, so $B=256$. For $s=0$, the roots are zero together with those of $g=X^3+r$. The cubic's <discriminant> is $-\prod g'(\beta_i)=-27r^2$. The extra squared differences from zero multiply this by $(\prod\beta_i)^2=r^2$, giving $-27r^4$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\Delta_f=256s^3-27r^4.}
$$
These computations are <polynomial> identities, so they extend to parameter values with repeated roots as well.

= Solution

For a <polynomial> with $n$ distinct roots, the criterion is
$$
\boxed{G_f\subseteq A_n\iff\Delta_f\text{ is a nonzero square in }\mathbb Q.}
$$
Write $\delta=\prod_{i<j}(\alpha_i-\alpha_j)\ne0$. Part (a) gives $\sigma\delta=\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\delta$. If every Galois <permutation> is even, $\delta$ is fixed by the entire <Galois group> and therefore belongs to its <fixed field> $\mathbb Q$. Thus $\Delta_f=\delta^2$ is a rational square. Conversely, if $\Delta_f=q^2$ with $q\in\mathbb Q^\times$, then $\delta^2=q^2$ in a <field> implies $\delta=\pm q$. Every <automorphism> fixes this nonzero element, so its <permutation> sign must be one.

The distinct-root hypothesis is necessary in the statement's intended degree-$n$ <permutation> action. The source says “arbitrary degree” without stating that hypothesis. If repeated roots were allowed, zero <discriminant> would carry no sign information: $(X-1)^2(X^2-2)$ has <discriminant> zero, while its nontrivial <automorphism> swaps $\sqrt2,-\sqrt2$ and fixes the two rational-root positions, an odd <permutation>. The criterion above applies to the <squarefree polynomial>, or to the squarefree part with its degree and root action adjusted accordingly.

= Solution
{parent=2009/a/2/a}

A <splitting field> is an extension in which the <polynomial> factors into linear factors and which is generated over the base <field> by its roots. Prove existence together with the factorial divisibility by strong induction on $d$, with degrees zero and one immediate for a nonzero <polynomial>.

If $f$ is reducible, write $f=gh$ with $1\le e=\deg g<d$, $\deg h=d-e$. By induction first split $g$ in $K_1/K$ with degree dividing $e!$, then split $h$ over $K_1$ with degree dividing $(d-e)!$. The resulting <field> is generated over $K$ by all roots of $f$, hence is a <splitting field>. Its degree divides $e!(d-e)!$, which divides $d!$ because the binomial coefficient $d!/[e!(d-e)!]$ is an <integer>.

If $f$ is irreducible, adjoin a root $\alpha$, obtaining degree $[K(\alpha):K]=d$. Over this <field> divide out $X-\alpha$ and split the degree-$(d-1)$ quotient by induction. That <splitting field> together with the base already contains every root and has degree over $K$ dividing $d(d-1)!=d!$. Thus
$$
\boxed{[L:K]\mid d!.}
$$
The reducible/irreducible distinction proves divisibility, not just an upper bound on the degree, and works without assuming separability.

= Solution
{parent=2009/a/2/b}

In the distinct-root convention, a <polynomial> is separable if it has no repeated roots in its <splitting field>, equivalently $\gcd(f,f')=1$. A common factorwise convention instead requires each distinct irreducible factor to have no repeated roots; repeated occurrences of an already separable factor do not affect the <splitting field>. Under either separability convention, the <splitting field> is a finite normal separable extension and
$$
\boxed{|\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)|=[L:K].}
$$
For failure in the inseparable case, take $K=\mathbb F_p(t)$ and $f=X^p-t$. It is irreducible, for example by Eisenstein at the prime $t$ in $\mathbb F_p[t]$. Its <splitting field> is $L=K(t^{1/p})$ of degree $p$, but the <polynomial> has just one distinct root. Every $K$-<automorphism> fixes that root, so the <Galois group> is trivial rather than having order $p$.

= Solution
{parent=2009/a/2/c/ii}

<Eisenstein's criterion> at two makes $X^5-4X+2$ irreducible: all nonleading coefficients are divisible by two and the constant is not divisible by four. Its <Galois group> is therefore transitive on five roots, and its order is divisible by five, so <Cauchy's theorem for finite groups> supplies a five-cycle.

The <derivative> is $5X^4-4$, with real critical points $\pm c$, $c=(4/5)^{1/4}$. The values are $f(-c)=2+16c/5>0$ and $f(c)=2-16c/5<0$. The <derivative> is positive outside these two points and negative between them, so there are exactly three real roots and one nonreal conjugate pair. <Complex conjugation> therefore acts as a single <transposition>.

A five-cycle together with any <transposition> generates $S_5$: conjugating that <transposition> by powers of the cycle gives edges joining labels with a fixed nonzero difference modulo five. These edges form a connected graph, and <transpositions on a connected graph generate the symmetric group>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb Q)=S_5,\qquad[L:\mathbb Q]=120.}
$$
The equality of degree and <group> order uses separability in <characteristic> zero.

The nonzero cubes modulo thirteen are $\{1,5,8,12\}$, which excludes three. Thus this cubic has no root in its base <field> and is irreducible. For a root $\alpha$, the extension $\mathbb F_{13}(\alpha)$ has degree three. The base <field> already contains the primitive cube <root of unity> $3$, since $3^3=1$ and $3\ne1$. Hence the other two roots are $3\alpha$ and $9\alpha$, already in this extension, making it the full <splitting field>.

The <Frobenius endomorphism> satisfies $\alpha^{13}=\alpha(\alpha^3)^4=3\alpha$, so it cycles all three roots. Therefore
$$
\boxed{[L:\mathbb F_{13}]=3,\qquad\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb F_{13})\cong C_3.}
$$

A <finite group> is solvable if its <derived series>, defined by $G^{(0)}=G$ and $G^{(j+1)}=[G^{(j)},G^{(j)}]$, reaches the identity after finitely many steps. Equivalently it has a <subnormal series> with abelian factor <groups>. The factors need not all have prime order for this definition.

Let $V_4=\{1,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}$. This is an <abelian subgroup>, and conjugation preserves its nonidentity cycle type, so it is normal in $S_4$ and hence in $A_4$. The series
$$
1\trianglelefteq V_4\trianglelefteq A_4\trianglelefteq S_4
$$
has factors $V_4\cong C_2\times C_2$, $A_4/V_4\cong C_3$, and $S_4/A_4\cong C_2$, all abelian. Thus $\boxed{S_4\text{ is solvable}}$.

= Solution
{parent=2009/a/3/d}

Let $\alpha=p^{1/p}$ and let $\zeta$ be a primitive $p$th <root of unity>. The roots are $\alpha\zeta^j$, so $L=\mathbb Q(\alpha,\zeta)$. Eisenstein gives $[\mathbb Q(\alpha):\mathbb Q]=p$, while the prime cyclotomic extension has degree $[\mathbb Q(\zeta):\mathbb Q]=p-1$. Both degrees divide $[L:\mathbb Q]$, and they are coprime. Conversely adjoining $\alpha$ to the <cyclotomic field> has degree at most $p$. These two bounds give
$$
\boxed{[L:\mathbb Q]=p(p-1).}
$$
Restriction to $E=\mathbb Q(\zeta)$ gives a <normal subgroup> $N=\operatorname{Gal}(L/E)$ of order $p$, hence cyclic, with quotient $\operatorname{Gal}(E/\mathbb Q)\cong C_{p-1}$. Restriction is onto because embeddings of the intermediate <field> extend in the finite Galois <splitting field>. Thus $1\trianglelefteq N\trianglelefteq G$ has abelian factors, proving $\boxed{G\text{ is solvable}}$. For $p=2$ the <cyclotomic field> is just $\mathbb Q$, and the same argument gives degree two and a <cyclic group>.

= Solution
{parent=2009/b/4}

The set $\mathcal O_K$ consists of the elements of $K$ integral over $\mathbb Z$, meaning roots of monic polynomials in $\mathbb Z[X]$.

First prove the <finite-module criterion for integrality> in a slightly stronger form, for any nonzero finitely generated $\mathbb Z$-submodule $M\subseteq K$ stable under multiplication by $\alpha$. Choose generators $m_1,\ldots,m_r$ and write $\alpha m_i=\sum_j a_{ij}m_j$ with <integer> coefficients. Multiply the vector equation $(\alpha I-A)m=0$ by its <adjugate matrix>. This gives $\det(\alpha I-A)m_i=0$ for every generator. Some generator is nonzero and $K$ is a <field>, so $\det(\alpha I-A)=0$. The <polynomial> $\det(XI-A)$ is monic with <integer> coefficients, proving integrality of $\alpha$. This implication needs no prior closure theorem for $\mathcal O_K$.

Conversely, if $\alpha$ satisfies a <monic polynomial> of degree $d$, then $M=\mathbb Z[\alpha]$ is generated by $1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha^{d-1}$ and is stable under $\alpha$. Every $\beta\in M$ also maps $M$ into itself because it is an <integer> <polynomial> in $\alpha$; the <finite-module criterion for integrality> just proved makes every such $\beta$ integral. Thus this $M$ really lies in $\mathcal O_K$, proving the stated equivalence without circularity.

For two integral elements $\alpha,\beta$, the module spanned by $\alpha^i\beta^j$ for $i<d_\alpha$, $j<d_\beta$ contains one, is finitely generated, and is stable under both elements by their monic equations. It is therefore stable under $\alpha+\beta$ and $\alpha\beta$. The <finite-module criterion for integrality> proves that both are integral. Negatives follow by replacing a multiplication matrix by its negative, and $0,1$ are integral. Hence $\boxed{\mathcal O_K\text{ is a ring}}$.

The <integral basis of the cubic field of discriminant minus 140> can be obtained as follows. For the specified <field>, Eisenstein at two makes $X^3+2X+2$ irreducible, and $\theta$ is integral because the <polynomial> is monic. The <discriminant> of a monic cubic $X^3+rX+s$ is $-4r^3-27s^2$, so
$$
\boxed{\Delta^2(1,\theta,\theta^2)=-4\cdot2^3-27\cdot2^2=-140.}
$$
Here $\Delta^2$ is the printed notation for the signed <discriminant>, namely the square of a Vandermonde product of the conjugates; that product need not be real.

Suppose $\eta=(a+b\theta+c\theta^2)/2$ is integral with the prescribed binary coefficients. The root traces give $\operatorname{Tr}(1)=3$, $\operatorname{Tr}(\theta)=0$, $\operatorname{Tr}(\theta^2)=-4$. Thus $\operatorname{Tr}(\eta)=(3a-4c)/2$ must be an <integer>, forcing $a=0$. If $b=0$, nonzeroness of the coefficient triple forces $c=1$, giving $\eta=\theta^2/2$. If $b=1$, multiply by $\theta$ and use $\theta^3=-2\theta-2$:
$$
\theta\eta=\theta^2/2-c\theta-c.
$$
Closure of the <ring> then again gives $\theta^2/2\in\mathcal O_K$.

But $N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\theta)=-2$, so the <field norm> of $\theta^{-1}$ is $-1/2$. The <field norm> of an <algebraic integer> is an <integer>, proving $\theta^{-1}\notin\mathcal O_K$. Dividing the defining cubic by $2\theta$ gives $\theta^2/2=-1-\theta^{-1}$. Thus $\theta^2/2$ cannot be integral either.

Finally the <discriminant-index formula for an integral lattice> says $-140=[\mathcal O_K:\mathbb Z[\theta]]^2\operatorname{disc}(K)$. The square of the <integer> index divides $140$, so its only possibilities are one and four, giving index one or two. If the index were two, a nonzero coset would supply an integral element $(a+b\theta+c\theta^2)/2$ after reducing coefficients modulo two, with a nonzero binary triple. The preceding argument would force the impossible integrality of $\theta^2/2$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathcal O_K=\mathbb Z[\theta],\qquad\{1,\theta,\theta^2\}\text{ is an integral basis}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=2009/b/5}

<ideals> are coprime when $A+B=\mathcal O_K$, equivalently $1=a+b$ for some $a\in A$, $b\in B$. Always $AB\subseteq A\cap B$. If $x\in A\cap B$, then $x=xa+xb\in AB$, giving $\boxed{AB=A\cap B}$ in the coprime case. For nonzero integral <ideals>, the <Chinese remainder theorem> gives $\mathcal O_K/(AB)\cong\mathcal O_K/A\times\mathcal O_K/B$, and taking finite cardinalities yields $\boxed{N(AB)=N(A)N(B)}$.

For a nonzero <prime ideal>, the chain of finite-index inclusions gives
$$
N(P^e)=[\mathcal O_K:P]\prod_{i=1}^{e-1}[P^i:P^{i+1}]=N(P)^e
$$
by the given hypothesis. Distinct nonzero <prime ideals> are maximal and coprime, as are their positive powers. <Unique factorization of ideals in a number field> therefore gives $N(A)=\prod_PN(P)^{v_P(A)}$. Exponents add under <ideal> multiplication, proving multiplicativity for arbitrary nonzero integral <ideals>. This is the standard domain of the finite <ideal norm> used here.

For the <Dedekind factorization theorem>, choose an integral primitive element $\theta$ of $K$ with monic <minimal polynomial of an algebraic element> $f\in\mathbb Z[X]$, and assume the rational prime $p$ does not divide the index $[\mathcal O_K:\mathbb Z[\theta]]$. Factor
$$
\bar f=\prod_{i=1}^g\bar f_i^{e_i}
$$
over $\mathbb F_p$, with distinct monic irreducible factors. For <integer> <polynomial> lifts $f_i$, the distinct <prime ideals> above $p$ are $P_i=(p,f_i(\theta))$, and
$$
\boxed{(p)=\prod_iP_i^{e_i},\quad\mathcal O_K/P_i\cong\mathbb F_p[X]/(\bar f_i),\quad N(P_i)=p^{\deg\bar f_i}.}
$$
Thus the <residue degree> is $\deg\bar f_i$, the <ramification index> is $e_i$, and $\sum_i e_i\deg\bar f_i=[K:\mathbb Q]$. The index restriction is essential to this direct <factorization> formula.

For $K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-2})$, the <ring of integers> is $\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-2}]$, so the index is one and the theorem applies to every prime. Modulo two, $X^2+2=X^2$, giving one <prime ideal> of <ideal norm> two. For an odd rational prime, a <prime ideal> has <ideal norm> $p$ if the <polynomial> splits, and <ideal norm> $p^2$ if it is irreducible. In the former case the supplied condition forces $p\equiv1$ or $3\pmod8$; in the latter case every odd square is one modulo eight. Consequently
$$
\boxed{N(P)=2\quad\text{or}\quad N(P)\equiv1,3\pmod8.}
$$
The odd part of any <ideal norm> is thus one or three modulo eight, because $3^2\equiv1$. Multiplication by a power of two leaves possible residues $1,3$ for exponent zero, $2,6$ for exponent one, $4$ for exponent two, and $0$ for exponent at least three. Hence $\boxed{N(I)\not\equiv5,7\pmod8}$ for every nonzero <integral ideal>.

= Solution
{parent=2009/b/6}

For nonzero <ideals>, <unique factorization of ideals in a number field> converts $AB=C^3$ to $v_P(A)+v_P(B)=3v_P(C)$. Coprimality says at most one of the first two exponents is positive, so each is divisible by three. Dividing its exponents by three constructs <ideals> whose cubes are $A,B$. If a zero <ideal> is allowed, coprimality forces the other <ideal> to be the whole <ring> and the conclusion is immediate with the zero <ideal> as its own cube.

To compute the <ideal class group of Q of square root minus thirty>, put $\theta=\sqrt{-30}$. The <ring of integers> is $\mathbb Z[\theta]$, with <discriminant> $-120$. The given <Minkowski bound for ideal classes> is $2\sqrt{120}/\pi=4\sqrt{30}/\pi<7$. Hence the <ideal class group> is generated by <prime ideals> with <ideal norm> below seven. The rational primes two, three and five all ramify, giving unique <prime ideals> $P_2=(2,\theta)$, $P_3=(3,\theta)$, $P_5=(5,\theta)$ with $P_i^2=(i)$ and <ideal norm> $i$. There is no additional <prime ideal> of <ideal norm> four because two ramifies with <residue degree> one; six is not a <prime power>. Thus these three classes generate and each has order at most two.

As $\theta^2=-30$, <unique factorization of ideals in a number field> gives $(\theta)=P_2P_3P_5$, so their three classes multiply to one. None is principal: a generator of one would be an integral element $a+b\theta$ of <field norm> $a^2+30b^2$ equal to two, three or five, and none of those <field norm> equations has <integer> solutions. The three nontrivial classes are therefore distinct, with the third the product of the first two. Hence
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-30}))\cong C_2\times C_2.}
$$

To rule out <integer> solutions, suppose $x^2+30=y^3$. Necessarily $y>0$. If a prime $p\mid30$ divided $y$, then reduction modulo $p$ would force $p\mid x$. Because thirty is squarefree, the valuation of $x^2+30$ at that prime would be exactly one, while that of $y^3$ is a positive multiple of three, a contradiction. Thus $\gcd(y,30)=1$.

If a <prime ideal> divided both $(x+\theta)$ and $(x-\theta)$, it would divide their difference $(2\theta)$ and their product $(y)^3$. Its underlying rational prime would divide $N(2\theta)=120$, hence divide thirty, and would also divide $y$. This contradicts the gcd just proved, so the two <ideals> are coprime. Their product is $(y)^3$, so the first part gives $(x+\theta)=A^3$ for an <ideal> $A$.

In a <ideal class group> of exponent two, $[A]^3=1$ forces $[A]=1$. Thus $A=(a+b\theta)$ with $a,b\in\mathbb Z$. Equality of principal <ideals> gives $x+\theta=\varepsilon(a+b\theta)^3$ for a <unit> $\varepsilon$. The only units are $\pm1$, since $a^2+30b^2=1$ forces $b=0$, $a=\pm1$; both signs are cubes and can be absorbed into the generator. Comparing the coefficient of $\theta$ after cubing now gives
$$
1=3a^2b-30b^3=3b(a^2-10b^2),
$$
which is impossible modulo three. Therefore
$$
\boxed{x^2+30=y^3\text{ has no solutions in }\mathbb Z^2.}
$$
