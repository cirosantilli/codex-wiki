= Solution
{parent=2008/a/1}

For an <irreducible polynomial> $f\in K[X]$, take $L=K[X]/(f)$ and let $\alpha$ be the class of $X$. The quotient is a <field>, is generated by $\alpha$ over $K$, and satisfies $f(\alpha)=0$. Its degree is $\boxed{[L:K]=\deg f}$, with <basis> $1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha^{\deg f-1}$.

A <splitting field> is a <field> generated over $K$ by all roots of the <polynomial> and in which the <polynomial> is a product of linear factors. Prove existence and the factorial bound together by induction on the degree. Constant nonzero <polynomials> and linear <polynomials> require no extension. For a reducible degree-$n$ <polynomial> write $g=hk$ with positive degrees $r,n-r$. Split $h$ by induction, then split $k$ over that <field>. The resulting <field> is generated by all roots of $g$, and its degree over $K$ is at most $r!(n-r)!\le n!$. If $g$ is irreducible, adjoin one root in degree $n$, divide out its linear factor, and split the degree-$(n-1)$ quotient over that extension. The tower formula gives degree at most $n(n-1)!=n!$. This also works in inseparable <characteristic> and proves the required bound without assuming Galoisness.

For the sextic with constant term three, <Eisenstein's criterion> at three gives degree six. Choose a root $\alpha$, so $\alpha^3$ is a square root of $-3$. Then
$$
\zeta=\frac{1+\alpha^3}{2}
$$
satisfies $\zeta^2-\zeta+1=0$, and is a primitive sixth <root of unity>: $\zeta^3=-1$ and $\zeta\ne-1$. The six distinct roots are $\alpha\zeta^j$ for $0\le j<6$, all in $\mathbb Q(\alpha)$. Hence the simple extension is itself the <splitting field> and $\boxed{[L:\mathbb Q]=6}$.

For the constant term two, Eisenstein at two gives $[\mathbb Q(\gamma):\mathbb Q]=6$ for a root $\gamma$. Its <splitting field> is $\mathbb Q(\gamma,\zeta_6)$. The supplied noncontainment of a root of $X^2+3$ means that adjoining $\zeta_6=(1+\sqrt{-3})/2$ has degree two. Thus
$$
\boxed{[M:\mathbb Q]=12.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=2008/a/2}

Every <automorphism> fixes zero and one, and preserves sums, products, negatives and multiplicative inverses of nonzero elements. Consequently elements fixed by every member of $G$ are closed under these operations and form a <field>. <Artin fixed-field theorem> gives $\boxed{[L:L^G]=|G|}$ for a finite <automorphism> <group>.

Here $\mathbb Q(t)$ is the fraction <field> of $\mathbb Q[t]$, with $t$ transcendental; its elements are fractions of rational-coefficient <polynomials>, with nonzero denominator and the usual equality by cross-multiplication. Substitution of $1-t$ or $1/t$ defines an <automorphism>, each its own inverse. With $\sigma\tau$ meaning first apply $\tau$ to a <field> element and then $\sigma$, its image on $t$ is $1/(1-t)$. Iterating gives $(t-1)/t$ and then $t$, so its order is three. The relations reduce every word to at most six possibilities, and the six resulting images are
$$
t,\quad1-t,\quad1/t,\quad1/(1-t),\quad(t-1)/t,\quad t/(t-1).
$$
They are distinct <rational functions>, so the <group> has six elements. Its faithful <permutation> action on $\{0,1,\infty\}$ identifies it with $\boxed{S_3}$.

For the first <fixed field> put $u=t(1-t)$. It is fixed by $\sigma$, and $t$ satisfies $X^2-X+u=0$ over $\mathbb Q(u)$. The degree is exactly two because the nontrivial <automorphism> $\sigma$ fixes that <field>. The fixed-<field> degree is also two, so
$$
\boxed{\mathbb Q(t)^{\langle\sigma\rangle}=\mathbb Q(t(1-t)).}
$$
For the inversion put $v=t+1/t$. It is fixed and $t$ satisfies $X^2-vX+1=0$. The same degree argument gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb Q(t)^{\langle\tau\rangle}=\mathbb Q(t+1/t).}
$$
The source requests <polynomial> generators in both cases. The second requirement is impossible. If a nonconstant <polynomial> $p(t)=a_dt^d+\cdots+a_0$ were fixed by inversion, $p(t)=p(1/t)$ would equate a <Laurent polynomial> with highest exponent $d>0$ to one with no positive exponent, a contradiction. Thus the only <polynomial> invariants of inversion are constants, which cannot generate its nonconstant <fixed field>. The <fixed field of inversion in a rational function field> therefore has the second displayed rational-function generator.

= Solution
{parent=2008/a/3}

A finite extension is Galois when it is both normal and separable; over $\mathbb Q$ separability is automatic, so it is equivalently a <splitting field> of a <polynomial> with distinct roots. Its <Galois group> consists of the <field> <automorphisms> fixing $\mathbb Q$. If $G=\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb Q)$, the <fundamental theorem of Galois theory> gives mutually inverse inclusion-reversing bijections
$$
H\longmapsto L^H,\qquad K\longmapsto\operatorname{Gal}(L/K).
$$
They satisfy $[L:L^H]=|H|$ and $[L^H:\mathbb Q]=[G:H]$. The intermediate extension $K/\mathbb Q$ is Galois exactly when $H=\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$ is normal, and then $\boxed{\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb Q)\cong G/H}$ via restriction.

Every <permutation> of the four roots permutes the three unordered partitions into two pairs and hence permutes the three $\beta_j$. Thus each coefficient of $g(X)=\prod_{j=1}^3(X-\beta_j)$ is a <symmetric polynomial> in the four roots. The <fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials> expresses it in the <elementary symmetric functions>, which are rational coefficients of $f$. Hence $g\in\mathbb Q[X]$. Each product $\alpha_i\alpha_j$, $i<j$, occurs exactly twice in $\beta_1+\beta_2+\beta_3$, so that sum is $2q$. Therefore the quadratic coefficient is $\boxed{-2q}$.

For the given cubic write $g(X)=(X-2)^3+12$. The translated cubic $Y^3+12$ is Eisenstein at three, hence irreducible. It has one real root and a nonreal conjugate pair. Its <Galois group> is transitive on three roots, so its order is divisible by three; <complex conjugation> is a <transposition>. A <subgroup> of $S_3$ with both a three-cycle and a <transposition> is all of $S_3$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb Q)\cong S_3,\qquad[K:\mathbb Q]=6.}
$$
Let $G=\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb Q)\le S_4$. Irreducibility of the quartic makes the root action transitive, so four divides $|G|$. The cubic <splitting field> $K$ lies inside $L$ by the definitions of the $\beta_j$, and restriction onto its <Galois group> gives a quotient of order six. Thus six also divides $|G|$. Since $|G|$ divides 24, it is 12 or 24. If it were 12, the supplied index-two result would give $G=A_4$. The restriction kernel would then be a <normal subgroup> of $A_4$ of index six, contrary to the other supplied result. Thus
$$
\boxed{G=S_4.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=2008/a/4}

A <straightedge and compass construction> uses finitely many steps drawing lines through known points and circles with known centres and constructible radii, and adjoining their intersection points. The coordinates obtained by line–line intersections require only <field> operations; line–circle and circle–circle intersections require at most a <quadratic extension> after eliminating a linear equation. Thus all coordinates lie in a tower of <quadratic extensions> of the initial coordinate <field>. The degree of any finite intermediate coordinate <field> containing that initial <field> divides a <power of two> and is therefore $\boxed{2^r}$ for some $r\ge0$.

For the source's notation this assumes $K\supseteq K_0$, as is customary when previously constructed points are retained. If only the coordinates of a final subset are called $K$, replace it by the compositum $K_0K$; otherwise the expression $[K:K_0]$ need not be defined. The power-of-two condition is necessary and is not being asserted as sufficient for an arbitrary algebraic number.

Starting from a unit cube, <impossibility of doubling the cube by straightedge and compass>[doubling its volume] would construct a length $\sqrt[3]2$. Its <polynomial> $X^3-2$ is Eisenstein at two, so its degree over $\mathbb Q$ is three, which cannot divide a <power of two>. Thus **doubling the cube is impossible by ruler and compass**.

Set $c=\cos\theta$, $s=\sin\theta$. The real part of $(c+is)^5$ is $c^5-10c^3s^2+5cs^4$. Replacing $s^2$ by $1-c^2$ gives
$$
\boxed{\cos5\theta=16c^5-20c^3+5c.}
$$
Choose the given angle to have cosine $5/6$. It is constructible, since its <unit circle> point has coordinates $(5/6,\sqrt{11}/6)$. If a fifth of that angle were constructible and had cosine $c$, the identity would imply
$$
96c^5-120c^3+30c-5=0.
$$
This <polynomial> is Eisenstein at five: all nonleading coefficients are divisible by five and the constant term is not divisible by 25, while 96 is not divisible by five. Hence $[\mathbb Q(c):\mathbb Q]=5$. But the initial angle <field> is $\mathbb Q(\sqrt{11})$, itself of degree two, so adjoining any finite collection of constructible coordinates to it would still give total degree over $\mathbb Q$ a <power of two>. It could not contain a degree-five element. Thus **<general angle quintisection is impossible with straightedge and compass>**.

= Solution
{parent=2008/b/5}

For the <basis> $w_1,\ldots,w_d$, its <discriminant> is
$$
\operatorname{disc}(B)=\det\big(\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb Q}(w_iw_j)\big)_{i,j}.
$$
Equivalently it is the square of the <determinant> of the <matrix> of the $d$ embeddings applied to the <basis> vectors. It is nonzero because <number fields> are separable. An <integral basis> is a <basis> of the <ring of integers> as a <free abelian group>; in particular it is a rational <basis> of the <field> and expresses every <algebraic integer> uniquely with <integer> coefficients.

To prove existence, start with any rational <basis> and multiply each vector by a suitable nonzero <integer> to make it integral. This is possible by clearing coefficients in its monic rational <minimal polynomial of an algebraic element> and rescaling the variable. Let $M$ be the <free abelian group>[lattice] spanned over $\mathbb Z$ by these integral vectors, and let $D\ne0$ be its <discriminant>. For any <algebraic integer> $\alpha=\sum_jc_jw_j$, each trace $\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha w_i)$ is an <integer>. The resulting <linear system> has integral coefficient <matrix> with <determinant> $D$. Its <adjugate matrix> shows $c_j\in D^{-1}\mathbb Z$. Thus
$$
M\subseteq\mathcal O_K\subseteq D^{-1}M.
$$
A <subgroup> of a finitely generated <free abelian group> is free and finitely generated. Here its rank is $d$, since it contains $M$ and is contained in a rank-$d$ <free abelian group>[lattice]. Its free generators are the required <integral basis>.

For squarefree $d\ne1$, the quadratic <integral basis> is
$$
\boxed{\{1,(1+\sqrt d)/2\}\quad(d\equiv1\pmod4),\qquad\{1,\sqrt d\}\quad(d\equiv2,3\pmod4).}
$$
The source allows squarefree nonzero <integers>, so the degenerate value $d=1$ gives $K=\mathbb Q$ and <basis> $\{1\}$ instead of a two-element <basis>.

In the biquadratic <field> define the three <automorphisms> by changing respectively the sign of $\sqrt r$, the sign of $\sqrt2$, and both signs. Their actions are
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_1(\mu)&=a+b\sqrt2-c\sqrt r-d\sqrt{2r},\\
\sigma_2(\mu)&=a-b\sqrt2+c\sqrt r-d\sqrt{2r},\\
\sigma_3(\mu)&=a-b\sqrt2-c\sqrt r+d\sqrt{2r}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence the three sums are $2a+2b\sqrt2$, $2a+2c\sqrt r$ and $2a+2d\sqrt{2r}$. If $\mu$ is integral, each sum is integral and lies in its displayed quadratic subfield. Their radicands are squarefree and respectively congruent to two, three and two modulo four. The quadratic <basis> formula therefore forces all their two coefficients to be <integers>, proving
$$
\boxed{2a,2b,2c,2d\in\mathbb Z.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=2008/b/6}

The <Dedekind factorization theorem> says: if $K=\mathbb Q(\theta)$ with $\theta$ integral, <minimal polynomial of an algebraic element> $f\in\mathbb Z[X]$, and the rational prime $p$ does not divide $[\mathcal O_K:\mathbb Z[\theta]]$, factor $\bar f=\prod_i\bar f_i^{e_i}$ into distinct monic irreducibles over $\mathbb F_p$. Then the distinct <prime ideals> above $p$ are $P_i=(p,f_i(\theta))$, with <integer> lifts $f_i$, and
$$
\boxed{(p)=\prod_iP_i^{e_i},\qquad N(P_i)=p^{\deg\bar f_i}.}
$$
The index condition is part of the theorem and must be checked before applying it at a ramified prime.

For this cubic, $X^3-6$ is Eisenstein and has <discriminant> $-972$. We first verify $\mathcal O_K=\mathbb Z[\theta]$. The square of the index divides 972, so only two and three can divide the index. If either prime $p$ divided it, the finite quotient <group> would contain an element of order $p$, giving an integral element $(a+b\theta+c\theta^2)/p$ with digits $0\le a,b,c<p$, not all zero. Its <field norm> is
$$
\frac{a^3+6b^3+36c^3-18abc}{p^3}.
$$
For $p=2$, the numerator has valuation zero if $a=1$, valuation one if $a=0,b=1$, and valuation two if $a=b=0,c=1$. For $p=3$ the same three cases, according to the first nonzero digit, give valuations zero, one and two. None gives an <integer> <field norm> after dividing by $p^3$, contradicting integrality. Thus the index is one.

Multiplication by $a+b\theta$ in the <power basis>, or the product over the three conjugates, gives $\boxed{N(a+b\theta)=a^3+6b^3}$. Define
$$
\begin{aligned}
P_2&=(2,\theta),&P_3&=(3,\theta),\\
P_5&=(5,\theta-1),&Q_5&=(5,\theta^2+\theta+1),\\
P_{7,r}&=(7,\theta-r)&& (r=3,5,6).
\end{aligned}
$$
Reduction of the cubic gives $X^3$ at two and three, $(X-1)(X^2+X+1)$ at five, and $(X-3)(X-5)(X-6)$ at seven. The quadratic factor at five is irreducible since its <discriminant> is two, a nonsquare modulo five. Hence
$$
\boxed{(2)=P_2^3,\quad(3)=P_3^3,\quad(5)=P_5Q_5,\quad(7)=P_{7,3}P_{7,5}P_{7,6}.}
$$
The <norms> are respectively $2,3,5,25,7,7,7$. <Norms> and reduction of the elements at those primes give
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
(\theta)&=P_2P_3,&(\theta-1)&=P_5,&(\theta-2)&=P_2,\\
(\theta-3)&=P_3P_{7,3},& (\theta+4)&=P_2P_5P_{7,3}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
For example their absolute element <field norms> are $6,5,2,21,70$; each indicated prime divides the element by substitution in its <residue field>, and the product of the indicated <ideal norms> already equals that absolute <field norm>. Thus no additional factor or exponent is possible.

In the <ideal> of the specified quotient every prime exponent cancels:
$$
(\mu)=\frac{P_2^2(P_3P_{7,3})P_5}{(P_2P_3)(P_2P_5P_{7,3})}=\mathcal O_K.
$$
Equality with the <unit ideal> implies that both $\mu$ and its inverse are integral, so $\boxed{\mu\text{ is a unit}}$. For the real root, $1<\theta<2$. The numerator is negative and has absolute value less than two, while the denominator is greater than five. Thus $-2/5<\mu<0$, proving $\boxed{\mu\ne\pm1}$. Its <field norm> is $-1$, consistent with the <ideal> calculation.

= Solution
{parent=2008/b/7}

Choose a nonzero <integral ideal> $B$ whose class is $[A]^{-1}$; one can multiply the fractional inverse $A^{-1}$ by a suitable nonzero <integer> to obtain such a $B$. By the given bound choose $0\ne\beta\in B$ with $|N(\beta)|\le c_KN(B)$. Put $I_A=(\beta)B^{-1}$. Since $(\beta)\subseteq B$, multiplication by $B^{-1}$ makes $I_A$ an <integral ideal>. Its class is $[B]^{-1}=[A]$, and multiplicativity of <ideal norms> gives
$$
\boxed{N(I_A)=\frac{|N(\beta)|}{N(B)}\le c_K.}
$$
This proves the class representative bound without accidentally constructing a representative of the inverse class instead.

To determine the <ideal class group of Q of square root minus thirty-four>, write $\eta=\sqrt{-34}$. The given <integral basis> has <discriminant> $-136$, so the imaginary-quadratic bound is $4\sqrt{34}/\pi<8$. Every class therefore has a representative of <ideal norm> at most seven, and factoring such representatives shows that the <ideal class group> is generated by <prime ideals> of <ideal norm> at most seven.

Two ramifies: let $P_2=(2,\eta)$, with $P_2^2=(2)$. Three is inert because $X^2+34\equiv X^2+1$ is irreducible modulo three, so its <prime ideal> <ideal norm> is nine and supplies no small generator. At five and seven the roots are $\pm1$, so the primes of <ideal norm> five and seven are conjugate pairs. Set $P_5=(5,\eta-1)$ and $P_7=(7,\eta+1)$. Their conjugates have inverse <ideal> classes. No new <prime ideal> has <ideal norm> four, since the only prime above two has <residue degree> one; six is not a <prime power>. Thus $[P_2],[P_5],[P_7]$ generate the <ideal class group>.

The element <field norms> $N(4+\eta)=50$ and $N(1+\eta)=35$, together with reduction at the split primes, give
$$
(4+\eta)=P_2P_5^2,\qquad(1+\eta)=\bar P_5P_7.
$$
The <ideal norm> products verify both exponents. Consequently $[P_5]^2=[P_2]$ and $[P_7]=[P_5]$, so the whole <group> is generated by $[P_5]$ and its order divides four. The class $[P_2]$ is not trivial: a generator would be an element of the <ring of integers> $a+b\eta$ of <field norm> two, but $a^2+34b^2=2$ has no <integer> solution. Since $[P_2]^2=1$, its order is exactly two. Hence $[P_5]$ has order four and
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-34}))\cong C_4.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=2008/b/8}

For <integral solutions of x squared minus two equals y cubed>, the equation implies $y\ge-1$, so $y+2>0$. If $y$ were even, then $x$ would be even and $x^2-2$ would be two or six modulo eight, whereas $y^3$ would be zero modulo eight. Thus $y$ and $x$ are odd. Also $3\nmid x$: otherwise $x^2-2\equiv7\pmod9$, which is not a cubic residue modulo nine. Reducing modulo three then gives $y\equiv2\pmod3$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\gcd(y+2,6)=1.}
$$
Put $\eta=\sqrt{-6}$ and $\alpha=x+(y+1)\eta$. Its <field norm> is $(y+2)^3$. The <principal ideals> $(\alpha)$ and $(\bar\alpha)$ are coprime. Indeed a common <prime ideal> has underlying rational prime $p$ dividing $y+2$. The displayed gcd excludes two and three, so both two and $\eta$ are invertible modulo that <prime ideal>. The difference $\alpha-\bar\alpha=2(y+1)\eta$ therefore implies $p\mid y+1$, contradicting $p\mid y+2$.

Their product is $(y+2)^3$, so <unique factorization of ideals in a number field> and coprimality imply $(\alpha)=A^3$ for an <integral ideal> $A$. In the given <ideal class group> of order two, $[A]^3=1$ implies $[A]=1$. Hence $A=(u+v\eta)$ with <integers> $u,v$. Equality of <principal ideals> gives $\alpha=\varepsilon(u+v\eta)^3$. The only <units> are $\pm1$, and both are cubes, so the sign can be absorbed into $u,v$. Taking <norms>, and using $y+2>0$, gives
$$
\boxed{x+(y+1)\eta=(u+v\eta)^3,\qquad u^2+6v^2=y+2.}
$$
The coefficient of $\eta$ gives $y+1=3u^2v-6v^3$. Eliminating $y$ with the <field norm> identity yields
$$
(3v-1)u^2=6v^3+6v^2-1,\qquad u^2=\frac{6v^3+6v^2-1}{3v-1}.
$$
The denominator is nonzero for integral $v$. <Polynomial> division makes the integrality obstruction especially clear:
$$
\boxed{9u^2=18v^2+24v+8-\frac1{3v-1}.}
$$
If $u$ is integral, the reciprocal must be an <integer>, forcing $3v-1=\pm1$. The only integral possibility is $v=0$. Then $u^2=1$, the <field norm> gives $y=-1$, and the real coefficient in the cube gives $x=u^3=\pm1$. Both pairs directly satisfy the original equation. Therefore all integral solutions are
$$
\boxed{(x,y)=(1,-1),\ (-1,-1).}
$$
