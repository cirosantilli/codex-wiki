= Solution
{parent=2007/a/1}

By <Vieta's formulas>, the elementary <symmetric polynomials> in the roots give
$$
c_1=-(\alpha_1+\alpha_2+\alpha_3),\qquad
c_2=\alpha_1\alpha_2+\alpha_2\alpha_3+\alpha_3\alpha_1,\qquad
c_3=-\alpha_1\alpha_2\alpha_3.
$$
For each root, $\alpha_j^3+c_1\alpha_j^2+c_2\alpha_j+c_3=0$. Multiplying by $\alpha_j^k$ and summing gives the <power sum> recurrence
$$
\boxed{s_{k+3}+c_1s_{k+2}+c_2s_{k+1}+c_3s_k=0\qquad(k\ge0).}
$$
Its initial values are $s_0=3$, $s_1=-c_1$ and $s_2=c_1^2-2c_2$. They are <polynomials> with <integer> coefficients in $c_1,c_2,c_3$. The recurrence uses only addition and multiplication by those coefficients, so <induction> proves the same for every $s_k$. This is the cubic case of <Newton's identities>.

For $c_1=0$, put $\alpha_1=u$, $\alpha_2=v$, $\alpha_3=-u-v$. Then $c_2=-(u^2+uv+v^2)$ and $c_3=uv(u+v)$. Expanding the product of root differences gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta&=(u-v)^2(u+2v)^2(2u+v)^2\\
&=4(u^2+uv+v^2)^3-27u^2v^2(u+v)^2
=\boxed{-4c_2^3-27c_3^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
This is the <discriminant of a depressed cubic> in depressed form, and the displayed identity also proves its expression in integer-coefficient <symmetric polynomials>.

Scaling every root scales its elementary <symmetric polynomials> by the corresponding powers of $d$. Therefore the <monic polynomial> is
$$
\boxed{X^3+dc_1X^2+d^2c_2X+d^3c_3.}
$$
This remains valid for $d=0$, when all three roots are zero; deriving it by symmetric coefficients avoids dividing by $d$.

Reciprocal roots require $c_3\ne0$, equivalently every original root is nonzero. Their <monic polynomial> is the normalized reciprocal <polynomial>
$$
\frac{X^3f(1/X)}{c_3}
=\boxed{X^3+\frac{c_2}{c_3}X^2+\frac{c_1}{c_3}X+\frac1{c_3}}.
$$
If $c_3=0$, at least one reciprocal in the source is undefined, so that part has no <polynomial> interpretation without the nonzero-root qualification.

The sum of squared roots is $c_1^2-2c_2$. The sum of their pairwise products is
$$
(\alpha_1\alpha_2+\alpha_2\alpha_3+\alpha_3\alpha_1)^2
-2\alpha_1\alpha_2\alpha_3(\alpha_1+\alpha_2+\alpha_3)
=c_2^2-2c_1c_3.
$$
Their product is $c_3^2$. Thus the required <monic polynomial> is
$$
\boxed{X^3+(2c_2-c_1^2)X^2+(c_2^2-2c_1c_3)X-c_3^2.}
$$

= Solution

An <algebraic element> is a <separable algebraic element> over $K$ if its <minimal polynomial of an algebraic element> has distinct roots in a <splitting field>. A <field extension> is a <separable extension> if all its elements are <separable algebraic elements>.

The <formal derivative> is additive and satisfies $D(X^m)=mX^{m-1}$. For monomials, $D(X^rX^s)=(r+s)X^{r+s-1}=X^rD(X^s)+X^sD(X^r)$. Distributing over the finitely many coefficients proves the <product rule> $D(fg)=fDg+gDf$ over any <field>. If $f=(X-\alpha)^2h$ in an extension, the rule gives $Df=2(X-\alpha)h+(X-\alpha)^2Dh$, so $Df(\alpha)=0$, including in <characteristic two>. If $f,Df$ were relatively prime in $K[X]$, the <Bézout's identity for polynomials> would give $uf+vDf=1$, whose evaluation at $\alpha$ is impossible. Hence they are not relatively prime.

In <characteristic zero>, the <formal derivative> of a positive-degree <irreducible polynomial> $m$ is nonzero and has smaller degree. Thus $\gcd(m,Dm)=1$, since an <irreducible polynomial> has no proper nonconstant factor. By the previous argument, $m$ has no repeated root. Every element of a <finite field extension> is algebraic, so the extension is a <separable extension>.

For $K=\mathbb F_p(t)$, let $\alpha^p=t$. The characteristic-$p$ identity gives $X^p-t=(X-\alpha)^p$, so $K(\alpha)$ is already a <splitting field>. It is a proper extension by the allowed no-root fact. More precisely, the <minimal polynomial of an algebraic element> $\alpha$ divides $X^p-t$ and, over $K(\alpha)$, equals $(X-\alpha)^r$ for some $1\le r\le p$. If $r<p$, its next-to-leading coefficient is $-r\alpha\in K$, forcing $\alpha\in K$ because $r\ne0$ in $\mathbb F_p$, a contradiction. Hence its <minimal polynomial of an algebraic element> is $X^p-t$, with its one root repeated $p$ times. The extension is therefore **inseparable**, of degree $p$.

By contrast, every <finite field extension> $L/\mathbb F_p$ is a <separable extension>. If $[L:\mathbb F_p]=n$, then $|L|=p^n$. <Lagrange's theorem> in its nonzero multiplicative <group> gives $a^{p^n}=a$ for all $a\in L$. Thus every element is a root of $X^{p^n}-X$, whose <formal derivative> is $-1$; it has no repeated roots. Every <minimal polynomial of an algebraic element> divides this separable <polynomial>, proving the assertion.

= Solution
{parent=2007/a/3}

Put $\theta=\sqrt{p+\sqrt p}$. Then $(\theta^2-p)^2=p$, so $\theta$ is a root of
$$
F(X)=X^4-2pX^2+p^2-p.
$$
This <polynomial> satisfies <Eisenstein's criterion> at $p$: every nonleading coefficient is divisible by $p$, while the constant coefficient $p(p-1)$ is not divisible by $p^2$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{m_\theta(X)=X^4-2pX^2+p^2-p,\qquad[\mathbb Q(\theta):\mathbb Q]=4.}
$$
Now suppose $p=n^2+1$, taking $n>0$ without loss of generality. Let $\beta=\sqrt{p-\sqrt p}$. Since $\theta\beta=n\sqrt p$ and $\sqrt p=\theta^2-p$,
$$
\beta=\frac{n(\theta^2-p)}\theta\in\mathbb Q(\theta).
$$
Consequently all four distinct roots $\pm\theta,\pm\beta$ lie in this <field>. This gives a <cyclic quartic field from a nested quadratic radical>. It is a <splitting field> in <characteristic zero>, hence a <normal extension> and a <Galois extension>.

There is a <Galois automorphism> $\sigma$ sending $\theta$ to $\beta$: a root image defines an embedding, and normality makes it an automorphism of the <splitting field>. It sends $\sqrt p=\theta^2-p$ to $-\sqrt p$, so
$$
\sigma(\beta)=\frac{-n\sqrt p}{\beta}=-\theta.
$$
Thus $\theta\mapsto\beta\mapsto-\theta\mapsto-\beta\mapsto\theta$ is a four-cycle. The <Galois group> has order four and contains an element of order four, giving
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb Q(\theta)/\mathbb Q)\cong C_4.}
$$
For the requested lower degrees, $(q,r)=(3,4)$ gives $\sqrt{q+\sqrt r}=\sqrt5$ and degree two; $(q,r)=(3,1)$ gives $2$ and degree one. Both pairs use positive <integers>.

= Solution

A finite <straightedge and compass construction> repeatedly draws the line through two available distinct points or the <circle> with an available center and an available distance as radius, and adjoins isolated intersections of two such lines or <circles>. A finite set is constructible when its points occur after finitely many such steps, starting with the specified points.

If the current coordinate <field> is $F$, intersections of two lines solve <linear equations> over $F$. A line and a <circle> lead to a <quadratic equation> over $F$. For two distinct <circles>, subtraction of their equations gives a line, reducing to the previous case. Thus each step adjoins coordinates in an extension of degree one or two. The <tower law> gives a finite tower of quadratic-or-trivial <field extensions> containing all output coordinates. If the final coordinate <field> contains $K_0$, then
$$
\boxed{[K:K_0]=2^r\quad\text{for some integer }r\ge0,}
$$
because its degree divides the power-of-two degree of that tower.

Two implicit qualifications are necessary for this degree statement. The output is obtained in finitely many steps; the aggregate of infinitely many individually constructible points need not generate a finite extension. Also, unless the output retains the original coordinates, $K$ need not contain $K_0$. In that case the degree statement applies to the compositum $K_0K$, rather than an undefined $[K:K_0]$. The heptagon application explicitly includes $S_0$, so neither issue obstructs it.

Here $K_0=\mathbb Q$, and a vertex adjacent to $(1,0)$ has first coordinate $c=\cos(2\pi/7)$, so $\mathbb Q(c)\subseteq K$. Let $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/7}$. The <cyclotomic polynomial> $1+X+\cdots+X^6$ is <irreducible>, giving $[\mathbb Q(\zeta):\mathbb Q]=6$. Since $\zeta+\zeta^{-1}=2c$, it satisfies $X^2-2cX+1$ over $\mathbb Q(c)$. The element $\zeta$ is nonreal while $\mathbb Q(c)\subset\mathbb R$, so this relative degree is exactly two. By the <tower law>,
$$
[\mathbb Q(c):\mathbb Q]=3.
$$
If the regular <regular heptagon> were constructible, this degree would divide $[K:\mathbb Q]=2^r$, impossible. Therefore **the <regular heptagon> is not constructible** from the given unit segment.

= Solution
{parent=2007/b/5}

For the $n$ distinct complex embeddings $\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_n$ of the <number field>, define the <discriminant of elements of a number field> by
$$
D(w_1,\ldots,w_n)=\det(\sigma_i(w_j))^2
=\det\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb Q}(w_iw_j)\bigr).
$$
The equality follows by multiplying the transpose of the embedding <matrix> by itself. Each <field trace> is rational: it is also the trace of the rational <matrix> of multiplication by $w_iw_j$ on $K$. Thus the <determinant> lies in $\mathbb Q$. If the $w_i$ are <algebraic integers>, the products and their conjugates are <algebraic integers>, and so are their sums. A rational <algebraic integer> is an <integer>, hence all trace entries and their <determinant> are <integers>.

Write $u_j=\sum_i a_{ij}v_i$. The inclusion of the generated abelian <groups> makes $A=(a_{ij})$ an <integer> <matrix>. Its embedding <matrix> is the embedding <matrix> of the $v_i$ multiplied by $A$. Taking squared <determinants> gives
$$
\boxed{D(u_1,\ldots,u_n)=(\det A)^2D(v_1,\ldots,v_n).}
$$
For independent <algebraic integers> $v_i$, choose an <integral basis> $\omega_i$ of $\mathcal O_K$. The same formula gives $D(v)=[\mathcal O_K:\sum_i\mathbb Zv_i]^2D(\omega)$. If $D(v)$ is nonzero and a <square-free integer>, the <integer> index must be one, proving that the $v_i$ form an <integral basis>.

Now put $\beta=3\alpha+4$. Since $\mathbb Q(\beta)=\mathbb Q(\alpha)$ has degree three, the <monic polynomial>
$$
27f\!\left(\frac{X-4}{3}\right)=X^3-6X^2+59
$$
is its <minimal polynomial of an algebraic element>. The <field norm> is the negative of its constant term:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Norm}_{K/\mathbb Q}(3\alpha+4)=-59.}
$$
For the discriminant calculation, $f'(\alpha)=3\alpha^2+4\alpha$. In the <basis> $1,\alpha,\alpha^2$, using $\alpha^3=-2\alpha^2-1$, multiplication by this element has <matrix>
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}0&-3&2\\4&0&-3\\3&-2&4\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det M=59.
$$
Thus the given derivative-norm formula gives $D(1,\alpha,\alpha^2)=-59$. Since $\alpha$ satisfies a <monic polynomial> with <integer> coefficients, $1,\alpha,\alpha^2$ are <algebraic integers>; the fact that a <squarefree discriminant certifies an integral basis> proves
$$
\boxed{\mathcal O_K=\mathbb Z[\alpha],\qquad D_K=-59.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=2007/b/6}

<Dedekind factorization theorem> states that if $K=\mathbb Q(\theta)$ with integral $\theta$ and $p$ does not divide $[\mathcal O_K:\mathbb Z[\theta]]$, then a factorization $\overline f=\prod_i\overline f_i^{e_i}$ of its <minimal polynomial of an algebraic element> into distinct monic <irreducible polynomials> over $\mathbb F_p$ gives
$$
p\mathcal O_K=\prod_iP_i^{e_i},\qquad
P_i=(p,f_i(\theta)),\qquad N(P_i)=p^{\deg f_i}.
$$
The factors are distinct <prime ideals>; the residue degrees and ramification indices satisfy $\sum_i e_i\deg f_i=[K:\mathbb Q]$. Unique <prime ideal factorization> holds in the <ring of integers>.

For a <square-free integer> $d\equiv1\pmod4$, $\mathcal O_K=\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-d}]$, so the index condition is automatic. If $p_i\mid d$, the <polynomial> $X^2+d$ reduces to $X^2$ modulo $p_i$. Hence
$$
p_i\mathcal O_K=P_i^2,\qquad P_i=(p_i,\sqrt{-d}),\qquad N(P_i)=p_i.
$$
Therefore $d\mathcal O_K=(\prod_iP_i)^2=(\sqrt{-d})^2$. By unique <prime ideal factorization>, $\prod_iP_i=(\sqrt{-d})$.

Let $S$ be a nonempty proper subset and put $m=\prod_{i\in S}p_i$. For $d>1$, $1<m<d$ and $m$ is a <square-free integer>. If $\prod_{i\in S}P_i=(u+v\sqrt{-d})$, its <ideal norm> gives
$$
m=u^2+dv^2.
$$
The inequality $m<d$ forces $v=0$, leaving $m=u^2$, impossible for a <square-free integer> greater than one. Thus every such subproduct is nonprincipal.

These are <ramified prime classes for discriminant minus four d>. The <ideal classes> $[P_i]$ have order dividing two. Their product is the identity, and the preceding proof rules out every other nonempty subset relation. The <homomorphism> $\mathbb F_2^k\to\operatorname{Cl}(K)$ defined by these classes consequently has kernel exactly $\{0,(1,\ldots,1)\}$. Its image is therefore
$$
\boxed{C_2^{\,k-1}.}
$$
The boundary case $d=1$ has $k=0$ and makes the printed exponent $k-1$ meaningless; its <field> is $\mathbb Q(i)$ with trivial <ideal class group>. The subgroup statement is understood for $d>1$, while the prime-factor and proper-subset statements are empty for $d=1$.

= Solution
{parent=2007/b/7}

<Minkowski convex body theorem> states that a centrally symmetric <convex> measurable subset of $\mathbb R^n$ with <volume> greater than $2^n$ times the <covolume> of a full <Euclidean lattice> contains a nonzero lattice point. The equivalent closed-boundary form follows by compactness for compact <convex> bodies.

The set $S_t$ is centrally symmetric. It is <convex> because for $0\le\lambda\le1$, the <triangle inequality> gives $\|\lambda u+(1-\lambda)v\|_1\le\lambda\|u\|_1+(1-\lambda)\|v\|_1\le t$. It is a diamond of area $2t^2$. For the embedded nonzero <integral ideal> $I$, the given <covolume> is $2N(I)\sqrt d$. Hence <Minkowski convex body theorem> applies when
$$
2t^2>8N(I)\sqrt d,\qquad t^2>4N(I)\sqrt d.
$$
For the resulting nonzero $\alpha\in I$, write $x=\sigma_1(\alpha)$, $y=\sigma_2(\alpha)$. Then
$$
|\operatorname{Norm}_{K/\mathbb Q}(\alpha)|=|xy|
\le\frac{(|x|+|y|)^2}{4}\le\frac{t^2}{4}.
$$
Decrease $t$ to $2\sqrt{N(I)\sqrt d}$. A fixed slightly larger diamond contains only finitely many lattice points, so one nonzero $\alpha$ recurs along a sequence of such $t$ values. Passing to the limit proves
$$
\boxed{|\operatorname{Norm}_{K/\mathbb Q}(\alpha)|\le N(I)\sqrt d.}
$$
Given a target <ideal class> $C$, choose an <integral ideal> $I$ in $C^{-1}$. Since $\alpha\in I$, the <ideal> $J=(\alpha)I^{-1}$ is integral, belongs to $C$, and has <ideal norm>
$$
N(J)=\frac{|\operatorname{Norm}(\alpha)|}{N(I)}\le\sqrt d.
$$
For $K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{14})$, every <ideal class> thus has an integral representative of norm at most $\sqrt{14}<4$. The only possibilities are norms one, two or three. The prime two ramifies, giving the unique <ideal> of norm two $P_2=(2,\sqrt{14})$. The element $4+\sqrt{14}$ has <field norm> $16-14=2$, so $(4+\sqrt{14})=P_2$, making it principal. The prime three is inert because $X^2-14\equiv X^2+1\pmod3$ is an <irreducible polynomial>; its <prime ideal> has norm nine, so there is no <ideal> of norm three. Every possible small representative is principal, proving <class number one for Q square root of fourteen>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{h(\mathbb Q(\sqrt{14}))=1.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=2007/b/8}

Work in $\mathcal O_K=\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-d}]$, since $d\equiv2\pmod4$. First $x$ must be odd: if it were even, coprimality would make $a$ odd, so $x^2+a^2d\equiv2\pmod4$, which cannot be an odd power $y^p$ for $p\ge3$. Thus $y$ is odd too. Also no odd prime $\ell$ can divide both $x$ and $d$. Such a prime does not divide $a$, and square-freeness of $d$ gives $v_\ell(x^2+a^2d)=1$, whereas $v_\ell(y^p)$ is divisible by $p$. Hence $\gcd(x,d)=1$.

Put $\alpha=x+a\sqrt{-d}$. Suppose a <prime ideal> $P$ divides both $(\alpha)$ and $(\overline\alpha)$, and let $\ell$ be the rational prime below it. It then contains $2x$ and $2a\sqrt{-d}$. The case $\ell=2$ is impossible because $N((\alpha))=y^p$ is odd. If $\ell$ is odd, the first inclusion gives $\ell\mid x$, and $\gcd(a,x)=1$ makes $a$ nonzero in the residue <field>. The second inclusion therefore gives $\sqrt{-d}\in P$, so $\ell\mid d$, contradicting the previous paragraph. Thus $(\alpha)$ and $(\overline\alpha)$ are coprime <ideals>. In particular they generate the <unit ideal>, the precise meaning of coprimality here; no unique factorization of elements is assumed.

Their product is $(y)^p$. Unique <prime ideal factorization> and disjoint prime support imply $(\alpha)=A^p$ for an <integral ideal> $A$. In the <ideal class group>, $[A]^p=1$. Since $p$ does not divide the <class number>, <Lagrange's theorem> forces $[A]=1$. Write $A=(u+v\sqrt{-d})$. Equality of the principal <ideals> gives $\alpha=\varepsilon(u+v\sqrt{-d})^p$. The only units are $\pm1$, since a unit satisfies $u^2+dv^2=1$ and $d\ge2$. Both signs are $p$th powers for odd $p$, so they can be absorbed into the generator:
$$
\boxed{x+a\sqrt{-d}=(u+v\sqrt{-d})^p\quad(u,v\in\mathbb Z).}
$$
Apply the <class-number obstruction from a ramified odd exponent>. For the equation with $a=1$ and $p\mid d$, compare the coefficients of $\sqrt{-d}$ in this identity. The coefficient on the right is
$$
v\sum_{\substack{1\le j\le p\\j\text{ odd}}}
\binom pj u^{p-j}v^{j-1}(-d)^{(j-1)/2}.
$$
Its $j=1$ term is divisible by $p$, and every $j\ge3$ term contains a factor $d$, so the whole coefficient is divisible by $p$. The coefficient on the left is one, a contradiction. Thus **there are no positive <integer> solutions under the stated class-number and divisibility assumptions**.

Finally,
$$
\boxed{13^2+174=7^3.}
$$
The <integer> $174=2\cdot3\cdot29$ is square-free, is two modulo four, and is divisible by three. If its imaginary quadratic <class number> were not divisible by three, the preceding result with $p=3$ would prohibit this solution. Consequently
$$
\boxed{3\mid h(\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-174})).}
$$
