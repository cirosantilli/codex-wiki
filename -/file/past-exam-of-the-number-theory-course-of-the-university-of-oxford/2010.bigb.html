= Solution

Any extending <field homomorphism> $j$ is determined by $j(\alpha)$, since every element of $K(\alpha)$ is represented by a <polynomial> in $\alpha$ with coefficients in $K$. Applying $j$ to the minimal-<polynomial> equation shows $i(m_\alpha)(j(\alpha))=0$. Thus there are at most $m$ choices.

For each listed root $\beta_r$, define $K[x]\to L$ by $\sum a_kx^k\mapsto\sum i(a_k)\beta_r^k$. Its kernel contains $(m_\alpha)$, so it induces a unital map from the <field> $K[x]/(m_\alpha)\cong K(\alpha)$ into $L$. A unital map from a <field> is injective. This gives an extending homomorphism $j_r$ with $j_r(\alpha)=\beta_r$. Distinct roots give distinct maps, proving **exactly $m$ extensions**.

An irreducible <polynomial> is separable if its roots in a <splitting field> are distinct, equivalently if it is coprime to its formal <derivative>. For a general nonzero <polynomial>, the factorwise separability convention means that every irreducible factor is separable. This is the convention under which the printed <splitting field> implication holds without an extra hypothesis.

If instead separability of an arbitrary <polynomial> is defined to require all its roots to be distinct, that implication needs the <polynomial> to be squarefree. For example, $(x-1)^2$ has separable splitting extension $K/K$, but a repeated root. These two conventions agree for irreducible polynomials; repeated factors are the entire distinction here.

An algebraic <field extension> $E/K$ is separable if every $\alpha\in E$ has a separable <minimal polynomial> over $K$.

For an inseparable example, take $K=\mathbb F_p(t)$ and $f(X)=X^p-t$. <Eisenstein's criterion> at the prime $t$ in $\mathbb F_p[t]$, followed by <Gauss lemma for polynomials>, proves irreducibility over its fraction <field>. Its <derivative> is zero, and in a <splitting field> it is $(X-t^{1/p})^p$, so it is inseparable.

Now let $M/K$ be a separable splitting extension of a nonzero <polynomial> $f$. Every irreducible factor $g$ of $f$ has a root $\alpha\in M$, and $g$ is a scalar multiple of the <minimal polynomial> of $\alpha$. Separability of $M/K$ makes this <polynomial> separable. Therefore every irreducible factor of $f$ is separable, which proves the requested conclusion in the factorwise convention. The squarefree qualification under the alternative convention is explained in part (a).

= Solution

First, the number of embeddings extending a prescribed base embedding is at most the extension degree. Prove this for any finite extension by choosing finitely many algebraic generators and applying part (i) successively. At each step the number of extensions is at most the degree of that simple step; multiplication and the <tower law for field extensions> give the total bound.

If $\widetilde K/K$ is not separable, choose $\alpha$ with inseparable <minimal polynomial> of degree $e=[K(\alpha):K]$. Its image under $i$ has strictly fewer than $e$ distinct roots in any target <field>: inseparability is preserved under the coefficient embedding. Thus there are $r<e$ embeddings of $K(\alpha)$ extending $i$. Each extends to $\widetilde K$ in at most $[\widetilde K:K(\alpha)]$ ways, giving at most
$$
r[\widetilde K:K(\alpha)]<e[\widetilde K:K(\alpha)]=d.
$$
If instead $i(m_\alpha)$ does not split in $L$, it again has fewer than $e$ distinct roots in $L$, and exactly the same estimate applies. Hence **either obstruction makes the embedding count strictly less than $d$**.

= Solution

Set $K_r=K(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_r)$, beginning with $K_0=K$. Suppose $j:K_{r-1}\to L$ extends $i$. The <minimal polynomial> of $\alpha_r$ over $K_{r-1}$ divides its <minimal polynomial> over $K$. Its image under $j$ therefore divides $i(m_{\alpha_r})$, which splits into distinct linear factors in $L$. It consequently has exactly $[K_r:K_{r-1}]$ distinct roots in $L$. Part (i) supplies exactly this many extensions of $j$ to $K_r$.

Starting with the unique prescribed map on $K$, multiply these branching counts:
$$
\boxed{\#\{\widetilde\jmath:\widetilde K\to L,\ \widetilde\jmath|_K=i\}
=\prod_r[K_r:K_{r-1}]=d.}
$$
For the final assertion, let $M$ be a <splitting field> of a <separable polynomial> and embed it in an algebraic closure $\Omega$. It is generated by roots whose minimal polynomials are separable and split in $\Omega$. Applying the just-proved count with $L=\Omega$ gives $[M:K]$ embeddings. Were $M/K$ inseparable, part (a) would give strictly fewer. Thus **the splitting extension is separable**. This works for the factorwise convention as well, since the generators' minimal polynomials divide the separable irreducible factors.

The <Galois group> $\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$ is the <group> of all <field automorphisms> of $L$ fixing every element of $K$, with composition as its operation. A finite extension is a <Galois extension> if it is both normal and separable; equivalently, it is a splitting extension of separable polynomials, or its <group> has order $[L:K]$. Normality means that every <irreducible polynomial> over $K$ with a root in $L$ splits there.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The <fundamental theorem of Galois theory> gives an inclusion-reversing bijection for a finite Galois extension $L/K$ with <group> $G$:
$$
H\longmapsto L^H,\qquad
M\longmapsto\operatorname{Gal}(L/M).
$$
It satisfies $[L:L^H]=|H|$ and $[L^H:K]=[G:H]$. An intermediate extension $M/K$ is Galois precisely when its corresponding <subgroup> $H$ is normal, in which case restriction induces $\operatorname{Gal}(M/K)\cong G/H$.

The <cyclotomic polynomial> is
$$
\Phi_n(X)=\prod_{\substack{1\le a\le n\\\gcd(a,n)=1}}(X-\xi^a).
$$
By the allowed irreducibility, this is the <minimal polynomial> of $\xi$ and its degree is $\varphi(n)$. The <field> $\mathbb Q(\xi)$ contains every $n$th <root of unity> and is the <splitting field> of $X^n-1$. This <polynomial> has no repeated roots in characteristic zero, so the extension is Galois.

An <automorphism> sends $\xi$ to another primitive root $\xi^a$, where $a$ is a unit modulo $n$. Conversely each such conjugate defines an embedding, and it is an <automorphism> of $\mathbb Q(\xi)$ because $a$ has an inverse modulo $n$. Composition corresponds to multiplication of exponents. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb Q(\xi)/\mathbb Q)\cong(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^\times.}
$$

= Solution

Let $a=\sqrt[n]2$ and $F=\mathbb Q(\xi)$. The roots of $X^n-2$ are $a\xi^j$, so $L=F(a)$. This splitting extension is separable in characteristic zero, hence Galois over $\mathbb Q$. The subextension $F/\mathbb Q$ is itself Galois by part (iii), so restriction gives a surjection
$$
G\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(F/\mathbb Q)
$$
with normal kernel $H=\operatorname{Gal}(L/F)$. Surjectivity follows from the extension theorem for embeddings and normality of $L/\mathbb Q$.

For $\sigma\in H$, write $\sigma(a)=a\xi^{j(\sigma)}$. This defines an injective homomorphism $H\to\langle\xi\rangle$: injectivity holds because $L=F(a)$, and multiplicativity holds because $H$ fixes all <roots of unity> in $F$. A <subgroup> of a <cyclic group> is cyclic. The first isomorphism theorem therefore gives
$$
\boxed{H\text{ cyclic and normal},\qquad G/H\cong(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^\times.}
$$
There is no need to assume that $|H|=n$; the intersection with the cyclotomic <field> can reduce its order.

Let the monic <minimal polynomial> of $\alpha$ over $L$ have coefficients $a_0,\ldots,a_{r-1}$, and put $F=K(a_0,\ldots,a_{r-1})$. Each coefficient is algebraic over $K$, so adjoining these finitely many coefficients successively gives $[F:K]<\infty$. The same <polynomial> makes $\alpha$ algebraic over $F$, giving $[F(\alpha):F]\le r$. The <tower law for field extensions> yields $[F(\alpha):K]<\infty$. Its subfield $K(\alpha)$ therefore also has finite degree over $K$. This holds for every $\alpha\in M$, proving **$M/K$ is algebraic**.

An <algebraically closed field> is a <field> in which every nonconstant <polynomial> has a root, equivalently every such <polynomial> splits. An <algebraic closure> of $K$ is an algebraic extension of $K$ which is algebraically closed.

For the displayed subset $\overline K\subseteq\mathbb C$, if $\alpha,\beta$ are algebraic over $K$, then $K(\alpha,\beta)/K$ is finite. Sums, differences, products and nonzero inverses of its elements are algebraic over $K$, so $\overline K$ is a subfield. If a nonconstant <polynomial> has coefficients in $\overline K$, those finitely many coefficients generate a finite extension $F/K$. It has a root $z\in\mathbb C$ by algebraic closure of $\mathbb C$, and $z$ is algebraic over $F$. Part (i) makes it algebraic over $K$, so $z\in\overline K$. Hence $\overline K$ is algebraically closed and is an algebraic closure of $K$.

Every $z\in\overline K$ lies in the finite subextension $K(z)$, and every element of a finite subextension is algebraic over $K$. Therefore $\overline K$ is exactly the union of all finite subextensions inside $\mathbb C$. The two examples follow.

Take $K=\mathbb Q$. For every positive <integer> $n$, the real number $\sqrt[n]2$ lies in $\overline{\mathbb Q}$ and has degree $n$, by <Eisenstein's criterion> at $2$. If $\overline{\mathbb Q}/\mathbb Q$ had finite degree, all its subextension degrees would be bounded by that degree, contradicting this unbounded family. Therefore **$[\overline{\mathbb Q}:\mathbb Q]$ is infinite**.

= Solution

Identify $\mathbb C$ with the Euclidean plane and start with the points $0,1$. A complex number is a <constructible number> if a finite sequence of drawing lines through known points and circles with known centres and known distances as radii, and selecting their intersections, produces that point.

Line-line intersections solve linear equations in the current coordinate <field>. Line-circle and circle-circle intersections require at most one quadratic equation after eliminating a coordinate. Hence all coordinates obtained lie in a finite tower of real quadratic extensions of $\mathbb Q$. If $\alpha=x+iy$ is constructible, choose a common such real <field> $F$ containing $x,y$. Adjoining $i$ if needed still gives an extension $F(i)/\mathbb Q$ of power-of-two degree. By the <tower law for field extensions>,
$$
\boxed{[\mathbb Q(\alpha):\mathbb Q]\text{ is a power of }2.}
$$
A regular heptagon centred at $0$ with one vertex $1$ would have $\xi=e^{2\pi i/7}$ as another constructible vertex. But its cyclotomic degree is $\varphi(7)=6$, which is not a power of two. Thus **the regular heptagon is not constructible**.

An <integral basis> is a $\mathbb Z$-<basis> of the <ring of integers> $\mathcal O_K$; its elements also form a $\mathbb Q$-<basis> of $K$.

To prove existence, first multiply the elements of a rational <basis> by suitable nonzero integers to obtain an integral rational <basis> $w_1,\ldots,w_n$. Put $M=\sum_j\mathbb Zw_j$. The trace pairing is nondegenerate in characteristic zero, and the matrix $T=(\operatorname{Tr}(w_iw_j))$ has <integer> entries and nonzero determinant $\Delta$. If $\alpha=\sum_jc_jw_j$ is integral, all $\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha w_i)$ are integers, so $Tc\in\mathbb Z^n$. The adjugate formula gives $c\in\Delta^{-1}\mathbb Z^n$. Consequently
$$
M\subseteq\mathcal O_K\subseteq\Delta^{-1}M.
$$
A <subgroup> of a free <abelian group> is free, and these inclusions force rank $n$. A $\mathbb Z$-<basis> of $\mathcal O_K$ is therefore an <integral basis>.

Now put $s=\theta^2=\sqrt d$ and $F=\mathbb Q(s)$. The <polynomial> $X^4-d$ is irreducible by Eisenstein at any prime dividing the squarefree $d$, except for $d=-1$, where $(X+1)^4+1$ is Eisenstein at $2$. Thus $[K:\mathbb Q]=4$ and $[K:F]=2$. Every $\gamma\in K$ has the unique form $\alpha+\beta\theta$ with $\alpha,\beta\in F$. The nontrivial $F$-<automorphism> sends $\theta$ to $-\theta$, so an integral $\gamma$ has integral conjugate $\alpha-\beta\theta$. Relative trace and <norm> of integral elements are integral in $F$. Applying them to $\gamma$ and taking the trace of $\theta\gamma$ gives
$$
\boxed{2\alpha,\quad\alpha^2-\beta^2s,\quad2\beta s\in\mathcal O_F=\mathbb Z[s].}
$$
The <power basis> has <discriminant>
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{disc}(1,\theta,\theta^2,\theta^3)=-256d^3.}
$$
Indeed, the resultant of $X^4-d$ and $4X^3$ is $4^4\prod\theta_j^3=-256d^3$; the <discriminant> sign factor is $(-1)^6=1$. By the <discriminant-index formula for an integral lattice>, a prime dividing $[\mathcal O_K:\mathbb Z[\theta]]$ must divide $2d$.

No odd prime $p\mid d$ can divide this index. The Eisenstein equation gives $\theta^4/p\in\mathcal O_K$, while $r\theta^i/p$ has nonintegral <field norm> for $p\nmid r$ and $0\le i<4$: its $p$-adic <norm> valuation is $i-4<0$. If $p^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^3r_i\theta^i$ were integral, multiplication by $\theta^3$ first forces $p\mid r_0$; subtracting $r_0/p$ and multiplying by $\theta^2$ forces $p\mid r_1$. Continuing forces all coefficients divisible by $p$. This excludes an index element of order $p$.

To exclude $2$, any index element of order two would be represented by an integral
$$
\gamma=\frac{a+b\theta+c\theta^2+e\theta^3}{2}
$$
with integers $a,b,c,e$ not all even. Here $\alpha=(a+cs)/2$ and $\beta=(b+es)/2$. The integrality of $\alpha^2-\beta^2s$ forces
$$
a^2+dc^2-2dbe\equiv0\pmod4,\qquad
2ac-b^2-de^2\equiv0\pmod4.
$$
Since $d\equiv3\pmod4$, the first congruence implies $a,c$ have the same parity. If both were odd, the second would require $b^2+3e^2\equiv2\pmod4$, impossible. Hence $a,c$ are even. The second congruence then forces $b,e$ to have the same parity, while the first forces $be$ even. Thus $b,e$ are even too, a contradiction. Therefore the index is one and
$$
\boxed{\mathcal O_K=\mathbb Z[\theta],\qquad(1,\theta,\theta^2,\theta^3)\text{ is an integral basis}.}
$$
For the last request, let $t=\sqrt[4]5$ and take $\delta=(t+t^3)/2$. It satisfies $\delta^2=(5+3\sqrt5)/2$, and hence
$$
\boxed{\delta^4-5\delta^2-5=0.}
$$
This monic <integer> <polynomial> proves integrality. Its coordinates in the rational power <basis> have halves, so $\delta\notin\mathbb Z[t]$. Also $\delta=t(1+\sqrt5)/2$ with a nonzero coefficient in $\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)$; if $\delta$ were in that <quadratic field>, then $t$ would be too, contradicting degree four. Thus it meets both exclusions.

The <product of ideals> $AB$ consists of finite sums $\sum_ja_jb_j$ with $a_j\in A$, $b_j\in B$. <Ideal> divisibility $P\mid A$ means $A=PC$ for an <integral ideal> $C$; in this ring it is equivalent to $A\subseteq P$. The <ideal norm> is $N(A)=|\mathcal O_K/A|$, equivalently its additive lattice index.

If the <prime ideal> $P$ divides $AB$ but does not divide $A$, choose $a\in A\setminus P$. For every $b\in B$, $ab\in AB\subseteq P$, so primality gives $b\in P$. Thus $B\subseteq P$, proving $P\mid B$.

Every nonzero proper <ideal> $A$ is contained in a maximal <ideal> $P$, which is prime. Then $P\mid A$, so $A=PB$. <Norm> multiplicativity gives $N(B)=N(A)/N(P)<N(A)$ because $N(P)\ge2$. Induction on the positive <integer> $N(A)$ gives a prime-<ideal> factorization, ending at <norm> one. For uniqueness, a prime in one factorization divides some prime in the other. Every nonzero <prime ideal> is maximal, since its quotient is a finite integral domain and hence a <field>. The two primes therefore coincide. Cancel that common factor using the permitted cancellation law, and continue. This proves uniqueness up to order.

The finite additive <group> $\mathcal O_K/A$ has order $N(A)$, so that <integer> annihilates the class of $1$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{N(A)\in A.}
$$
In particular an <ideal> of <norm> $m$ contains $(m)$. Such ideals correspond to certain additive subgroups of the finite <group> $\mathcal O_K/(m)$, so there are finitely many. Finiteness of these quotients follows from an <integral basis>; a nonzero <ideal> contains a nonzero <principal ideal>, whose index is the absolute <field norm> of its generator.

For the computation put $s=\sqrt{-6}$. The <Kummer-Dedekind theorem> says that when $p$ does not divide the power-<basis> index, a factorization $\bar f=\prod_i\bar g_i^{e_i}$ gives $(p)=\prod_i(p,g_i(s))^{e_i}$, with each factor having <norm> $p^{\deg g_i}$. The given full power <basis> has index one. Since $X^2+6\equiv X^2\pmod2$ and $X^2+6\equiv(X-1)(X+1)\pmod7$,
$$
\boxed{(2)=P^2,\quad P=(2,s);\qquad
(7)=QQ',\quad Q=(7,s-1),\ Q'=(7,s+1).}
$$
Here $N(P)=2$ and $N(Q)=N(Q')=7$. A <prime ideal> dividing an <ideal> of <norm> $98=2\cdot7^2$ lies above $2$ or $7$, and these are all the prime ideals above them. Unique factorization and <norm> multiplicativity therefore leave exactly
$$
\boxed{PQ^2,\qquad PQQ'=(7)P,\qquad P(Q')^2.}
$$
They are distinct by their prime-<ideal> valuations, and each has <norm> $98$.

= Solution

Equality $(\alpha)=(\beta)$ gives $\alpha=u\beta$ and $\beta=v\alpha$ for some $u,v\in\mathcal O_K$. Substitution and cancellation of the nonzero $\alpha$ yield $uv=1$. Thus $u$ is a unit and $\alpha=u\beta$.

For the <ideal>-power assertion, suppose first that all ideals are nonzero. A <prime ideal> divides at most one $Q_i$ because the $Q_i$ are pairwise coprime. Taking its valuation in the given equality gives
$$
n_i v_P(Q_i)=k v_P(A).
$$
Since $\gcd(n_i,k)=1$, every exponent $v_P(Q_i)$ is divisible by $k$. Dividing all its prime-factorization exponents by $k$ constructs an <integral ideal> $I_i$ with $Q_i=I_i^k$. If a $Q_i$ is zero, pairwise coprimality forces all other $Q_j$ to be the unit <ideal>; taking $I_i=0$ and the other $I_j=\mathcal O_K$ covers that degenerate case too.

For the first Diophantine equation, $x$ cannot be odd: then $x^2+17\equiv2\pmod8$, while a cube modulo eight is $0$ or odd. Hence $x$ is even and $y$ is odd. Also $17\nmid x$, since otherwise $v_{17}(x^2+17)=1$, impossible for a cube.

In $\mathcal O=\mathbb Z[s]$, $s=\sqrt{-17}$, factor
$$
(x+s)(x-s)=y^3.
$$
The two principal ideals are coprime. A common <prime ideal> would divide their sum $2x$ and difference $2s$, and hence lie above $2$ or $17$; odd <norm> excludes $2$, while $17\nmid x$ excludes $17$. Their coprime product is the cube $(y)^3$, so the <ideal>-power assertion makes $(x+s)=I^3$. The class <group> has order $4$, while $[I]^3=1$, so $[I]=1$. Write $I=(a+bs)$ for integers $a,b$.

The only units are $\pm1$, since their <norm> equation is $a^2+17b^2=1$. Equality of principal ideals therefore gives $x+s=\pm(a+bs)^3$. Absorb the sign into $a+bs$. Comparing the coefficient of $s$ yields
$$
b(3a^2-17b^2)=1.
$$
Thus $b=1$ or $-1$. The first gives $a^2=6$ and the second gives $3a^2=16$, both impossible for an <integer> $a$. Consequently **the first equation has no integral solutions**.

For the product equation, odd $x$ gives $(x^2+17)(9x^2+17)\equiv4\pmod8$, again not a cube. If $17\mid x$, each factor has $17$-adic valuation one, so the product has valuation two, also impossible. Thus any hypothetical solution has $x$ even and $17\nmid x$. The two positive factors are odd, and their greatest common divisor divides
$$
(9x^2+17)-9(x^2+17)=-136=-8\cdot17.
$$
It must therefore be one. A product of coprime positive integers is a cube only if each factor is a cube. This would make $x^2+17$ a cube, contradicting the result already proved. Hence **the product equation also has no integral solutions**.
