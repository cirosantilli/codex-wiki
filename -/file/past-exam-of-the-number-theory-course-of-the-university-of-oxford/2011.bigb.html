= Solution
{parent=a}

A <splitting field> of a nonconstant <polynomial> $f$ over a <field> $K$ is an <field extension> $L$ in which $f$ factors into linear factors and which is generated over $K$ by those roots. We prove existence and the divisibility simultaneously by induction on $n=\deg f$; constant nonzero polynomials have <splitting field> $K$.

If $f$ is reducible, write $f=gh$ with positive degrees $r,s<n$. By induction first split $g$, obtaining $E/K$ of degree dividing $r!$, then split $h$ over $E$, obtaining $L/E$ of degree dividing $s!$. The <tower law for field extensions> gives $[L:K]\mid r!s!\mid(r+s)!$. All adjoined elements are roots of $f$, so $L$ has the required minimality.

If $f$ is irreducible, take $E=K[x]/(f)$ and let $\alpha$ be the image of $x$. Then $[E:K]=n$ and $f=(x-\alpha)h$ in $E[x]$. Apply induction to $h$, of degree $n-1$, to obtain $[L:E]\mid(n-1)!$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{[L:K]=n[L:E]\mid n!.}
$$
This proof also covers inseparable polynomials; it does not incorrectly identify extension degree with <automorphism> count in that case.

For a <splitting field> $L$ of $f$ over $K$, define $\operatorname{Gal}(f)=\operatorname{Aut}_K(L)$. Each <automorphism> permutes the distinct roots, since it fixes the coefficients. An <automorphism> fixing every root fixes the <field> they generate, so this action is faithful. If there are $r\le n$ distinct roots, it gives an embedding into $S_r$, and hence into $S_n$ by fixing $n-r$ additional symbols.

Factorization over the <rational numbers> gives
$$
f(x)=(x-1)(x+1)(x^3-7).
$$
The rational roots add nothing to the <splitting field>. By <Eisenstein's criterion> at $7$, $x^3-7$ is irreducible. Put $a=\sqrt[3]7$ and $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/3}$. Its <splitting field> is $L=\mathbb Q(a,\zeta)$. The real <field> $\mathbb Q(a)$ has degree $3$; the nonreal element $\zeta$ has degree $2$ over it. Thus $[L:\mathbb Q]=6$, and its faithful action on the three nonrational roots identifies
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Gal}(f)\cong S_3.}
$$

The <polynomial> is irreducible by <Eisenstein's criterion> at $2$. Its <Galois group> therefore acts transitively on five roots, so its order is divisible by $5$ by the <orbit-stabilizer theorem>.

To count the real roots, differentiate:
$$
f'(x)=5x(x^3-4).
$$
It is positive on $(-\infty,0)$, negative on $(0,\sqrt[3]4)$, and positive thereafter. The local maximum is $f(0)=2>0$ and the local minimum is $2-6(\sqrt[3]4)^2<0$. The limits at infinity have opposite signs, so there are exactly three real roots and one conjugate pair of nonreal roots. <Complex conjugation> fixes the three real roots and interchanges the other two; its permutation is a <transposition>. The prime-degree <subgroup> result supplied in the paper now gives
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Gal}(f)\cong S_5.}
$$

The <fixed field> is
$$
K^G=\{a\in K:\sigma(a)=a\text{ for every }\sigma\in G\}.
$$
It is a subfield because every <field automorphism> preserves sums, products and inverses.

Set $F=K^G$. If $[K:F]$ is infinite the desired finite lower bound is immediate. Otherwise let $b_1,\ldots,b_d$ be an $F$-<basis> of $K$. If $|G|>d$, the homogeneous system over $K$
$$
\sum_{\sigma\in G}c_\sigma\sigma(b_j)=0\qquad(1\le j\le d)
$$
has a nonzero solution. For $a=\sum_j a_jb_j$, with $a_j\in F$, every $\sigma$ fixes $a_j$, so the same coefficients give $\sum_\sigma c_\sigma\sigma(a)=0$. This contradicts <linear independence of distinct field embeddings>. Consequently
$$
\boxed{[K:K^G]\ge |G|.}
$$

The <fundamental theorem of Galois theory> says that for a finite <Galois extension> $L/F$ with <group> $G$, the maps $H\mapsto L^H$ and $E\mapsto\operatorname{Gal}(L/E)$ are mutually inverse inclusion-reversing correspondences between subgroups and intermediate fields. They satisfy $[L:L^H]=|H|$ and $[L^H:F]=[G:H]$. An intermediate extension $E/F$ is Galois exactly when its <subgroup> is normal, in which case its <group> is the quotient by that <subgroup>.

Let $a=\sqrt2$, $b=\sqrt3$, $c=i$. The real <field> $\mathbb Q(a,b)$ has degree $4$: if $\sqrt3=u+v\sqrt2$ with $u,v\in\mathbb Q$, squaring forces $uv=0$ and either $u^2=3$ or $v^2=3/2$, both impossible. Adjoining the nonreal $i$ doubles the degree. All three square-root signs can be changed independently, giving
$$
\boxed{G\cong C_2^3,\qquad [L:\mathbb Q]=8.}
$$
Write $\sigma_{uvw}$ for the <automorphism> sending $(a,b,c)$ to $((-1)^ua,(-1)^vb,(-1)^wc)$, where $(u,v,w)\in\mathbb F_2^3$. Every nonzero vector generates an order-two <subgroup>, so there are $7$ such subgroups. Their fixed fields are:

|| Generator
|| Fixed field

| $\sigma_{100}$
| $\mathbb Q(\sqrt3,i)$

| $\sigma_{010}$
| $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,i)$

| $\sigma_{001}$
| $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3)$

| $\sigma_{110}$
| $\mathbb Q(\sqrt6,i)$

| $\sigma_{101}$
| $\mathbb Q(\sqrt3,\sqrt{-2})$

| $\sigma_{011}$
| $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt{-3})$

| $\sigma_{111}$
| $\mathbb Q(\sqrt6,\sqrt{-2})$

Each listed <field> has degree $4$ and is fixed by the indicated sign change, which proves equality with the fixed <field>.

There are also $7$ order-four subgroups: they are the kernels of the seven nonzero linear functionals on $\mathbb F_2^3$. For $e=(e_1,e_2,e_3)\ne0$, define $H_e=\{\sigma_{uvw}:e_1u+e_2v+e_3w=0\}$. Then
$$
L^{H_e}=\mathbb Q\left(\sqrt{2^{e_1}3^{e_2}(-1)^{e_3}}\right).
$$
Thus the seven quadratic fields have radicands $2,3,-1,6,-2,-3,-6$. Their square classes are distinct, so all are different. Finally the trivial <subgroup> fixes $L$, and all of $G$ fixes $\mathbb Q$. This accounts for every <subgroup> and intermediate <field>.

The <fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials> states that every symmetric <polynomial> in $n$ variables over a <field> is a <polynomial> in the elementary symmetric polynomials, uniquely so. For the three roots, <Vieta's formulas> give $e_1=0$, $e_2=p$, $e_3=-q$.

The squared product of root differences is symmetric. Expansion in elementary symmetric polynomials gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
(\alpha-\beta)^2(\beta-\gamma)^2(\gamma-\alpha)^2
&=e_1^2e_2^2-4e_2^3-4e_1^3e_3-27e_3^2+18e_1e_2e_3\\
&=\boxed{-4p^3-27q^2.}
\end{aligned}
$$
One can verify the displayed identity simply by substituting the definitions of $e_1,e_2,e_3$ and expanding; it is the cubic <polynomial discriminant>.

If the cubic is irreducible, its roots are distinct in characteristic zero, so $\delta\ne0$. Its <Galois group> is a transitive <subgroup> of $S_3$, hence either $A_3$ or $S_3$. A permutation of the roots multiplies $\delta$ by its sign. The fixed-<field> assertion of the <fundamental theorem of Galois theory> therefore gives $\delta\in\mathbb Q$ exactly when all <group> elements are even. Thus
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Gal}(f)=A_3\ \text{if }\delta\in\mathbb Q,\qquad
\operatorname{Gal}(f)=S_3\ \text{otherwise}.}
$$
For example, $f=x^3-3x-1$ has no rational root: neither $1$ nor $-1$ is a root, and these are the only possibilities by the <rational root theorem>. Its <discriminant> is $108-27=81$, so $\delta=\pm9\in\mathbb Q$ and its <group> is $A_3$.

= Solution

A finite <solvable group> is a <group> admitting a finite chain of normal subgroups with abelian successive quotients; equivalently its derived series eventually becomes trivial. The chain
$$
S_4\triangleright A_4\triangleright V_4\triangleright1
$$
has quotients $C_2,C_3,V_4$, all abelian. Thus $S_4$ is solvable. Every <subgroup> of a <solvable group> is solvable, because intersecting the chain with the <subgroup> gives factors isomorphic to subgroups of the original abelian factors. A <Galois group of a polynomial> of degree at most $4$ embeds in $S_4$ by its action on the roots, so it is solvable even if the <polynomial> is reducible or has repeated roots.

For the substitution, the characteristic-zero <solvability by radicals> theorem says that a <polynomial> has solvable <Galois group> if and only if its <splitting field> is contained in a finite tower formed by adjoining successive roots of elements of the preceding <field>. First place all roots $\alpha$ of $f$ in such a tower. Adjoin a primitive $m$th <root of unity> and, for every nonzero $\alpha$, an $m$th root $\beta_\alpha$. These are radical adjunctions, and all roots of $x^m-\alpha$ are $\beta_\alpha\zeta_m^j$. Zero roots require no adjunction. The resulting radical tower therefore contains the <splitting field> of $f(x^m)$. The theorem implies
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Gal}(f(x^m)/\mathbb Q)\text{ is solvable}.}
$$

An <integral basis> is a $\mathbb Z$-<basis> of the <ring of integers> $\mathcal O_K$; it consists of $n$ algebraic integers and is also a $\mathbb Q$-<basis> of $K$.

The lattice $M$ has finite index $h=[\mathcal O_K:M]>1$. Choose a prime $p\mid h$. The finite <abelian group> $\mathcal O_K/M$ has an element of order $p$, represented by $\alpha\in\mathcal O_K\setminus M$. Thus
$$
p\alpha=\sum_{j=1}^nc_jw_j\in M.
$$
If all $c_j$ were divisible by $p$, cancellation in the characteristic-zero <field> would put $\alpha$ in $M$, a contradiction. The <discriminant-index formula for an integral lattice> gives
$$
\Delta^2(M)=h^2\Delta^2(K),
$$
where $\Delta^2(K)$ is an <integer>. Hence $p^2\mid\Delta^2(M)$, and the required integral element is $\alpha=p^{-1}\sum_jc_jw_j$.

The <polynomial> is irreducible by <Eisenstein's criterion> at $q$, so $[K:\mathbb Q]=n$. Its constant coefficient gives the <field norm>
$$
\boxed{N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\theta)=(-1)^na_0.}
$$
The defining equation gives
$$
\frac{r\theta^n}{q}=-r\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{a_j}{q}\theta^j\in\mathbb Z[\theta]\subseteq\mathcal O_K.
$$
If $q\nmid r$ and $0\le i<n$, then
$$
N_{K/\mathbb Q}\left(\frac{r\theta^i}{q}\right)
=\frac{r^n((-1)^na_0)^i}{q^n}
$$
has $q$-adic valuation $i-n<0$. It is not an <integer>. The <norm> of an <algebraic integer> is an <integer>, so $r\theta^i/q$ is not integral.

Suppose now that $\gamma$ is integral. Multiply it by $\theta^{n-1}$. All terms except $r_0\theta^{n-1}/q$ are integral, since their powers of $\theta$ are at least $n$. Thus this remaining term is integral, and the <norm> obstruction forces $q\mid r_0$. Subtract the <integer> $r_0/q$ from $\gamma$. Multiply the remainder by $\theta^{n-2}$; the only term of exponent below $n$ is $r_1\theta^{n-1}/q$, so $q\mid r_1$. Continuing in this way proves $q\mid r_j$ for every $j$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\gamma\in\mathbb Z[\theta].}
$$
For $n=1$ the same conclusion follows directly from the <norm> obstruction with $i=0$.

The <Kummer-Dedekind theorem> states the following. If $K=\mathbb Q(\theta)$ with $\theta$ integral and $p\nmid[\mathcal O_K:\mathbb Z[\theta]]$, factor the <minimal polynomial> modulo $p$ as $\bar f=\prod_i\bar g_i^{e_i}$ into distinct monic irreducible factors. Then
$$
p\mathcal O_K=\prod_i\mathfrak p_i^{e_i},\qquad
\mathfrak p_i=(p,g_i(\theta)),\qquad
N(\mathfrak p_i)=p^{\deg g_i}.
$$
The index restriction is essential.

For the proposed <ideal> relation, reflexivity uses $\alpha=\beta=1$ and symmetry interchanges $\alpha,\beta$. If $(\alpha)I_1=(\beta)I_2$ and $(\gamma)I_2=(\delta)I_3$, multiplication gives $(\alpha\gamma)I_1=(\beta\delta)I_3$. All multipliers remain nonzero. Thus it is an <equivalence relation>, namely equality of <ideal classes>.

Put $s=\sqrt{-2}$. The <minimal polynomial> is $x^2+2$ and the given full power <basis> makes every prime eligible for the theorem. Modulo $3$ its roots are $\pm1$; modulo $5$ and $7$ it is irreducible; modulo $17$ its roots are $\pm7$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
(3)&=(3,s-1)(3,s+1),\\
(5)&\text{ is prime},\\
(7)&\text{ is prime},\\
(17)&=(17,s-7)(17,s+7).
\end{aligned}}
$$
The two inert ideals have norms $25$ and $49$; each split factor has <norm> equal to its rational prime.

For $p_1\equiv1\pmod8$, <quadratic reciprocity> and its supplementary laws give $(-2/p_1)=1$. Thus $x^2+2$ has two distinct roots modulo $p_1$, giving distinct prime ideals $P,P'$ with
$$
(p_1)=PP',\qquad N(P)=N(P')=p_1.
$$
The given <principal ideal domain> property makes $P=(x+ys)$ for some integers $x,y$. Taking norms, which are positive here, gives $p_1=N(P)=x^2+2y^2$.

Let $\omega=(1+\sqrt{-3})/2$, whose <minimal polynomial> is $x^2-x+1$. For $p_2\equiv1\pmod3$, the <cyclic group> $\mathbb F_{p_2}^{\times}$ has a nontrivial cube <root of unity> $t$. Then $-t$ and $-t^2$ are distinct roots of $x^2-x+1$. By the <Kummer-Dedekind theorem>,

(p_2)=QQ',\qquad \boxed{N(Q)=N(Q')=p_2.}

Thus $x=2a+b$ and $y=b$ supply the required integers.

Put $s=\sqrt{-5}$ and $P=(2,1+s)$. The <polynomial> $x^2+5$ reduces to $(x+1)^2$ modulo $2$, so the <Kummer-Dedekind theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{(2)=P^2,\qquad N(P)=2.}
$$
If $P=(a+bs)$, its <norm> would imply $a^2+5b^2=2$, impossible for integers $a,b$. Thus $P$ represents the nontrivial <ideal class>. Since the <class number> is $2$, every nonprincipal <integral ideal> $I$ has $[I]=[P]$, and $[IP]=1$. Write $IP=(\gamma)$ with $\gamma\in\mathcal O_M$; integrality follows from $IP\subseteq\mathcal O_M$. Multiplying by $P$ gives
$$
(2)I=(\gamma)P.
$$
Norms give $4N(I)=2N(\gamma)$, hence $N(\gamma)=2N(I)$. If $N(I)=p_3\equiv1\pmod{20}$, writing $\gamma=a+bs$ yields
$$
a^2+5b^2=2p_3\equiv2\pmod5,
$$
contradicting the list $0,1,4$ of squares modulo $5$. Therefore **no nonprincipal <ideal> has <norm> $p_3$**.

As $p_3\equiv1\pmod4$ and $p_3\equiv1\pmod5$, <quadratic reciprocity> gives $(-5/p_3)=1$. Thus $x^2+5$ has two distinct roots modulo $p_3$ and
$$
(p_3)=RR',\qquad N(R)=N(R')=p_3.
$$
The <norm> obstruction shows that $R$ must be principal, say $R=(x+ys)$. Taking its <norm> proves
$$
\boxed{p_3=x^2+5y^2.}

$$
I_A=(\beta)B^{-1}\subseteq\mathcal O_K.

It is an <integral ideal> in class $[B]^{-1}=[A]$. By multiplicativity of the <ideal norm>,
$$
\boxed{N(I_A)=\frac{|N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\beta)|}{N(B)}\le c_K.}

The same argument applies if the starting $A$ is a <fractional ideal>, since its class is unchanged by clearing denominators.

Write $s=\sqrt{-33}$. The <discriminant> is $-132$, and the bound of part (a) is
$$
c_K=\frac2\pi\sqrt{132}=\frac4\pi\sqrt{33}<8.
$$
Every <ideal class> therefore has an integral representative of <norm> at most $7$. The <Kummer-Dedekind theorem> gives
$$
(2)=P^2,\quad P=(2,1+s);\qquad
(3)=Q^2,\quad Q=(3,s).
$$
Modulo $5$, $x^2+33=x^2+3$ is irreducible, so there is no <ideal> of <norm> $5$. Modulo $7$ it has roots $\pm3$, giving $(7)=R\bar R$, where $R=(7,s+3)$. The <ideal> of $3+s$ has <norm> $42$, and reduction in the three residue fields shows that it lies in $P,Q,R$. Equality of norms therefore gives
$$
(3+s)=PQR.
$$
It follows that $[R]=[P][Q]$, because $[P]^2=[Q]^2=1$. The other prime over $7$ has the inverse class, which is the same class here.

By unique factorization of nonzero ideals, all norms at most $7$ now give only the classes $1,[P],[Q],[PQ]$: <norm> $4$ gives $P^2=(2)$, <norm> $6$ gives $PQ$, and <norm> $7$ was handled above. None of $P,Q,PQ$ is principal, since their norms are $2,3,6$ and
$$
a^2+33b^2\notin\{2,3,6\}\qquad(a,b\in\mathbb Z).
$$
Also $[P]\ne[Q]$, since equality would make $PQ$ principal. Thus there are exactly four classes, all of exponent two:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-33}))\cong C_2\times C_2.}
$$

= Solution

Part (a), applied to any <ideal class>, produces an <integral ideal> of positive <integer> <norm> at most $c_K$, so $c_K\ge1$. Writing $n=[K:\mathbb Q]$ and using $s\le n/2$ gives
$$
\sqrt{|\Delta^2(K)|}\ge\left(\frac\pi4\right)^s\frac{n^n}{n!}
\ge\left(\frac\pi4\right)^{n/2}2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}.
$$
For the last inequality, write $n^n/n!=\prod_{j=1}^n(n/j)$; the first $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ factors are at least $2$, and the remaining factors are at least $1$. Since $\lfloor n/2\rfloor\ge(n-1)/2$, squaring yields
$$
|\Delta^2(K)|\ge\frac12\left(\frac\pi2\right)^n.
$$
As $\pi/2>1$, the assumed upper bound $D$ forces
$$
\boxed{n\le\frac{\log(2D)}{\log(\pi/2)}.}
$$
For $D\ge1$, the ceiling of the right-hand side is a valid <integer> $M$. If $D=0$, no number <field> satisfies the premise, so any positive $M$ works.
