For a partition $0=t_0<\cdots<t_n=1$, approximate the integral by $X_\pi=\sum_jt_j(B_{t_{j+1}}-B_{t_j})$, using left endpoints. The defining independent <Brownian increment> of <Brownian motion> make this a centered Gaussian variable with variance $\sum_jt_j^2(t_{j+1}-t_j)$. As the mesh tends to zero, that variance tends to $\int_0^1t^2dt=1/3$.

The <stochastic integral> is the <mean-square convergence> of these sums. <mean-square convergence> implies <convergence in distribution>, while the <characteristic function> converge to $e^{-u^2/6}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{X\sim N(0,1/3),\qquad\mathbb P(X>0)=\frac12.}
$$
The <probability> is exactly one half because this centered nondegenerate <normal distribution> is symmetric and has no atom at zero.

Use a <filtration (probability theory)> with respect to which $B$ is Brownian. The process is adapted and continuous because it is a continuous function of $B_t$ and $t$. The <moment-generating function> gives
$$
\mathbb E M_t=1,\qquad
\mathbb E M_t^2
=e^{-\alpha^2t}\mathbb E e^{2\alpha B_t}
=e^{\alpha^2t}<\infty.
$$
For $0\leq s<t$, write
$$
M_t=M_s\exp\{\alpha(B_t-B_s)-\alpha^2(t-s)/2\}.
$$
The increment is independent of $\mathcal F_s$ and normal with variance $t-s$, so the <conditional expectation> of the second factor is one. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E(M_t\mid\mathcal F_s)=M_s.}
$$
This verifies the <martingale> property from the definition and proves square integrability at each finite time, as well as <continuity>. It does not assert uniform square integrability over an infinite time horizon.

<Lévy's characterization of Brownian motion> states that a real continuous <local martingale> $M$ with $M_0=0$ and <quadratic variation> $\langle M\rangle_t=t$ is a standard <Brownian motion> with respect to its <filtration (probability theory)>. Equivalently, its increments are independent of the past and have centered <normal distribution> with variances equal to elapsed times.

Let $H_s=\operatorname{sgn}(\sup_{r\leq s}B_r-1)$. The running maximum is continuous and adapted, so $H$ is bounded and <progressively measurable process>. It has a predictable version for integration; changing its value at the single switching time does not change its <stochastic integral>. Thus $X=\int H\,dB$ is a continuous <local martingale> starting at zero. Since $H_s^2=1$,
$$
\langle X\rangle_t=\int_0^tH_s^2ds=t.
$$
<Lévy's characterization of Brownian motion> yields **$X$ is standard <Brownian motion>**. The sign depends on the entire past through the running maximum, but the unit quadratic-variation rate is exactly what the characterization needs.

The <drift coefficient> $b(z)=rz$ and <diffusion coefficient> $a(z)=\sigma z$ are <Lipschitz continuity> and have linear growth. The standard existence and <pathwise uniqueness> theorem for <stochastic differential equations> therefore supplies a unique global <strong solution of a stochastic differential equation>, adapted to the driving <Brownian filtration>.

Apply <Itô's formula> to $x\exp((r-\sigma^2/2)t+\sigma B_t)$. Its time derivative contributes $(r-\sigma^2/2)X_t$ and its Itô correction contributes $\sigma^2X_t/2$, giving the required equation and initial value. Hence
$$
\boxed{X_t=x e^{(r-\sigma^2/2)t+\sigma B_t}.}
$$
In particular the solution stays positive. For $t>s$, factor out $X_s$ and use <independence> and the normal law of $B_t-B_s$:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E(X_t\mid\mathcal F_s^0)
=e^{r(t-s)}X_s.}
$$
The exponential moment exists, so this <conditional expectation> is well-defined for every finite $s,t$.

<Itô's formula> identifies the <infinitesimal generator> on twice continuously differentiable functions:
$$
\boxed{(Lf)(z)=rzf'(z)+\tfrac12\sigma^2z^2f''(z).}
$$
Subtract the <drift coefficient> integral from that formula to obtain
$$
M_t^f=\int_0^t\sigma X_s f'(X_s)\,dB_s.
$$
Stopping when $|X|$ leaves a bounded interval makes the integrand bounded on each finite time interval. Therefore $M^f$ is a continuous <local martingale>. In fact $f'\in L^\infty$ and the <lognormal distribution> solution has finite second moments on finite intervals, so here it is square integrable on each such interval as well.

The <quadratic covariation> of two <stochastic integrals> against the same <Brownian motion> is the integral of the product of their integrands. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\langle M^f,M^g\rangle_t
=\sigma^2\int_0^tX_s^2 f'(X_s)g'(X_s)\,ds.}
$$
This is the <quadratic covariation>, which agrees with the pathwise <quadratic covariation> for these continuous <local martingales>.

Fix a finite horizon $T$ and use the completed <natural Brownian filtration>. <martingale representation theorem> states that every $F\in L^2(\mathcal F_T)$ can be written
$$
F=\mathbb EF+\int_0^T H_s\,dB_s,
\qquad \mathbb E\int_0^T H_s^2ds<\infty,
$$
where $H$ is predictable and is unique up to equality for $ds\,d\mathbb P$ almost every $(s,\omega)$.

The <martingale representation theorem> states that every <square-integrable martingale> $M$ in this <filtration (probability theory)> has a continuous version of the form
$$
\boxed{M_t=M_0+\int_0^tH_s\,dB_s,\quad0\leq t\leq T,}
$$
with the same integrability and uniqueness condition. The completed natural <filtration (probability theory)> has only null-set randomness at time zero, so $M_0$ is almost surely constant. The first statement gives the second by representing $M_T$ and taking <conditional expectations>; the second gives the first by taking $M_t=\mathbb E(F\mid\mathcal F_t)$.

There is also the local version: every <local martingale> in the <Brownian filtration> has a continuous version representable by a predictable, locally square-integrable integrand. The <filtration (probability theory)> restriction matters; an enlarged <filtration (probability theory)> with independent additional <martingales> need not have representation using $B$ alone.

Condition on the Brownian path up to $t\leq T$. The independent normal increment $B_T-B_t$ has $\mathbb E\cos(B_T-B_t)=e^{-(T-t)/2}$ and zero expected sine. Hence

M_t:=\mathbb E(\sin B_T\mid\mathcal F_t)

$$
dM_t=e^{-(T-t)/2}\cos B_t\,dB_t,\qquad M_0=0.

Therefore the required representation is
$$
\boxed{\sin B_T=\int_0^T e^{-(T-s)/2}\cos B_s\,dB_s.}

The bounded integrand is square integrable, and uniqueness follows from the <Itô isometry>. The drift-containing decomposition in part (b) alone would not be the required representation as a constant plus one <stochastic integral>.

Apply <Itô's formula> to $M_t=e^{N_t-\langle N\rangle_t/2}$. The quadratic-variation correction cancels the finite-variation term:
$$
dM_t=M_t\,dN_t,\qquad M_0=1.
$$
Localization of the integrand proves that $M$ is a nonnegative continuous <local martingale>.

For completeness, <conditional Fatou lemma> says that for nonnegative <random variables> $Y_n$,
$$
\mathbb E(\liminf Y_n\mid\mathcal F_s)
\leq\liminf\mathbb E(Y_n\mid\mathcal F_s).
$$
Choose a <localizing sequence> $\tau_n\uparrow\infty$ such that each stopped $M$ is a <martingale>. Fatou first gives $\mathbb EM_t\leq1$. For $s<t$, its conditional form then gives
$$
\mathbb E(M_t\mid\mathcal F_s)
\leq\liminf_n M_{s\wedge\tau_n}=M_s.
$$
Thus **$M$ is a <supermartingale>**.

A sufficient condition for it to be a true <martingale> on $[0,T]$ is <Novikov's condition>
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E\exp(\tfrac12\langle N\rangle_T)<\infty.}
$$
Under this condition the <stochastic exponential> is a uniformly integrable <martingale> on that finite interval. Requiring it for every finite $T$ gives the <martingale> property at all finite times; it does not automatically give <uniform integrability> as $t\to\infty$.

For $s<t<1$, <independence> and the Gaussian <Brownian transition density> yield the <Chapman-Kolmogorov equation>
$$
\mathbb E[p(1-t,B_t,y)\mid\mathcal F_s]
=\int_{\mathbb R}p(t-s,B_s,z)p(1-t,z,y)\,dz
=p(1-s,B_s,y).
$$
The numerator is positive and its expectation is $p(1,0,y)$, so
$$
\boxed{M_0=1,\quad\mathbb EM_t=1,\quad
\mathbb E(M_t\mid\mathcal F_s)=M_s.}
$$
These statements hold for every $t<1$, in particular on the specified closed interval $[0,T]$.

The function $h(t,z)=p(1-t,z,y)$ solves the <backward heat equation> $h_t+\tfrac12h_{zz}=0$. <Itô's formula> therefore gives
$$
dM_t=M_t\theta_t\,dB_t,\qquad
\theta_t=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\log p(1-t,B_t,y)
=\frac{y-B_t}{1-t}.
$$
On each interval ending before one this adapted continuous integrand is locally square integrable. Thus
$$
\boxed{N_t=\int_0^t\frac{y-B_s}{1-s}\,dB_s,\qquad
\langle N\rangle_t=\int_0^t\frac{(y-B_s)^2}{(1-s)^2}\,ds}
$$
gives $M_t=\exp(N_t-\langle N\rangle_t/2)$. This also follows by applying <Itô's formula> to $\log M_t$. The source's notation $t\geq0$ for this density process is restricted by its defining formula to $t<1$.

A finite-horizon form of <Girsanov theorem> is as follows. If $\theta$ is predictable, $\int_0^T\theta_s^2ds<\infty$ almost surely, and its <stochastic exponential> $M$ is a true <martingale> of mean one, then under the <probability> $d\mathbb Q=M_Td\mathbb P$ the process
$$
B_t-\int_0^t\theta_sds,\qquad0\leq t\leq T,
$$
is <Brownian motion>. The same conclusion holds on the completed natural <filtration (probability theory)>, since a strictly positive density gives equivalent probabilities.

Part (b) establishes precisely the required density <martingale> and integrand $\theta_s=\partial_x\log p(1-s,B_s,y)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{X_t=B_t-\int_0^t\frac{y-B_s}{1-s}\,ds
\text{ is }\mathbb Q\text{-Brownian on }[0,T].}
$$
In particular it is the requested <local martingale>. Equivalently, under $\mathbb Q$, $B$ solves $dB_t=(y-B_t)(1-t)^{-1}dt+dX_t$, the <Brownian bridge> <drift coefficient> toward $y$ at time one. Keeping $T<1$ avoids the singular endpoint in this density change.

Write $Z=X+iY$, with $Y$ a real <Brownian motion> started at one. For the nontrivial strip calculation assume $r>1$, so the starting point is in its interior. The first exit time is
$$
T_S=\inf\{t\geq0:Z_t\notin S\}.
$$
For a continuous Brownian path started inside, this equals the first hitting time of $Y=0$ or $Y=r$ almost surely: <Brownian motion> started at a boundary immediately visits the exterior, so using the closed strip in the definition does not change the infimum.

If $T_S>n$, then $Y_n\in[0,r]$. Its normal density is bounded by $(2\pi n)^{-1/2}$, hence
$$
\mathbb P(T_S>n)\leq\mathbb P(0\leq Y_n\leq r)
\leq\frac r{\sqrt{2\pi n}}\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus the exit time is finite almost surely.

The stopped harmonic coordinate $Y_{t\wedge T_S}$ is a <martingale> bounded between zero and $r$. <bounded convergence theorem> and <optional stopping theorem> give $\mathbb EY_{T_S}=Y_0=1$. Since the exit coordinate is zero or $r$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(Y_{T_S}=r)=\frac1r,\qquad
\mathbb P(Y_{T_S}=0)=1-\frac1r.}
$$
If $T$ is the first hit of the real axis, exit through the lower boundary implies $T<\infty$, so $\mathbb P(T<\infty)\geq1-1/r$ for every $r>1$. Letting $r\to\infty$ proves
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(T<\infty)=1.}
$$
The strip assumption is worth making explicit: at $r=1$ the initial point is the upper boundary and exit is immediate; for $0<r<1$ it starts outside the strip and the displayed interior probabilities do not apply.

<planar Brownian motion> is <recurrence of planar Brownian motion> when, almost surely, it returns to every nonempty open neighborhood infinitely often, at arbitrarily large times. This does not mean that it hits each specified point; a prescribed point is polar for <planar Brownian motion>.

<recurrence of planar Brownian motion> and <continuity> imply that all the circle-hitting times $S_k,T_k$ in the question are finite. Starting on radius $\epsilon$, it eventually visits the exterior of radius $2\epsilon$ and must cross that circle; it later visits the interior of radius $\epsilon$ and must cross back. Each duration $\Delta_k=T_k-S_k$ is strictly positive by path <continuity>, because the two radii are separated.

At $S_k$, the <strong Markov property> restarts <Brownian motion> at its current point on the inner circle. <rotational invariance of Brownian motion> makes the law of the subsequent first exit time from the radius-$2\epsilon$ disk independent of that starting angle. Its conditional distribution given the past is therefore the same fixed law at every cycle, proving that the $\Delta_k$ are independent and identically distributed.

To prove divergence of the conformal clock for any nonconstant entire $f$, choose a point $z_0$ with $f'(z_0)\neq0$. By <continuity>, there exist $\rho,c>0$ such that $|f'(z)|^2\geq c$ throughout the closed disk $|z-z_0|\leq2\rho$. Use the same <recurrence of planar Brownian motion> circle cycles centered at $z_0$, with radii $\rho,2\rho$. On each segment from the inner circle to the outer circle, the path remains in that disk, giving
$$
\int_0^\infty|f'(Z_s)|^2ds
\geq c\sum_{k\geq0}(T_k-S_k).
$$
The durations are positive iid variables. If their mean has not been computed, apply the <strong law of large numbers> to the bounded iid variables $\min(\Delta_k,1)$: their mean is strictly positive, so their sum, and hence $\sum\Delta_k$, diverges almost surely. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty|f'(Z_s)|^2ds=\infty\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$
Centering the disks at a noncritical point avoids an unjustified positive lower bound on $|f'|$ around the original origin.

Write $f=u+iv$. Harmonicity and <Itô's formula> make $u(Z_t),v(Z_t)$ continuous <local martingales>. The <Cauchy-Riemann equations> give equal brackets $\int_0^t|f'(Z_s)|^2ds$ and zero mutual bracket. Consequently <Lévy's characterization of Brownian motion> after <time change of a continuous process> makes $W$ a <planar Brownian motion> started at $f(Z_0)$, with no finite clock lifetime because the clock just proved is unbounded. The clock is strictly increasing: otherwise $f'$ would vanish along a nonconstant path interval, contradicting its isolated zeros.

For the notation in the source, $|\nabla f|^2$ is interpreted as the conformal scale $|f'|^2=|\nabla u|^2=|\nabla v|^2$. If it instead means the sum of the squared magnitudes of both complex coordinate derivatives, it is $2|f'|^2$, and the resulting $W$ runs at half the usual Brownian speed. This convention does not change divergence of the integral. The mechanism is <recurrent disk occupation makes an entire conformal clock infinite>.

Take $\lambda>0$ and let $\sigma_\lambda$ be the first time $X$ hits $\lambda$. Reflect the horizontal coordinate after $\sigma_\lambda$, leaving the vertical coordinate unchanged. The <strong Markov property> and symmetry preserve the law of <planar Brownian motion> and leave its real-axis exit time unchanged. On paths with $\sigma_\lambda<\tau$, the reflection swaps the two terminal events $X_\tau<\lambda$ and $X_\tau>\lambda$. There is no terminal atom at $\lambda$. Hence the <Brownian reflection principle> gives
$$
\mathbb P_i\!\left(\sup_{t\leq\tau}X_t>\lambda\right)
=2\mathbb P_i(X_\tau>\lambda)
=\boxed{\frac2\pi\int_\lambda^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x^2}.}
$$
Horizontal symmetry gives the same <probability> for $-\inf X>\lambda$. A union bound therefore yields
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P_i(X^*>\lambda)
\leq\frac4\pi\int_\lambda^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x^2}.}
$$
Also $X^*\geq|X_\tau|$, whose <Cauchy distribution> is $2\pi^{-1}\int_\lambda^\infty(1+x^2)^{-1}dx$. Thus the maximum tail is bounded above and below by positive constants times $\lambda^{-1}$ for large $\lambda$. The <tail integral formula for moments> for positive moments gives
$$
\mathbb E(X^*)^p=p\int_0^\infty
\lambda^{p-1}\mathbb P(X^*>\lambda)d\lambda.
$$
The large-$\lambda$ integral is finite exactly when $0<p<1$; it diverges for $p\geq1$. Near zero its upper bound by $\int_0^1p\lambda^{p-1}d\lambda$ is finite.

The vertical coordinate remains nonnegative up to exit and starts at one. The strip calculation, now with upper boundary $\lambda\geq1$, gives
$$
\mathbb P(Y^*>\lambda)=\frac1\lambda.
$$
Thus $Y^*$ has density $\lambda^{-2}$ on $[1,\infty)$, and in fact
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E(Y^*)^p=\frac1{1-p}\ (p<1),\qquad
\mathbb E(Y^*)^p=\infty\ (p\geq1).}
$$
For completeness, if the source's $p<1$ includes negative powers, $X^*$ also has all negative moments. Let $\sigma_\eta$ be the first horizontal exit from $(-\eta,\eta)$. Then
$$
\mathbb P(X^*\leq\eta)
\leq\mathbb P(\tau\leq\eta)+\mathbb P(\sigma_\eta\geq\eta)
\leq C e^{-c/\eta}\quad(0<\eta\ll1).
$$
The first bound follows from the Gaussian reflection bound for the vertical hit of zero; the second follows by splitting time into blocks of length $\eta^2$, each having a fixed positive chance of exiting the horizontal interval. This small-value estimate integrates against every negative power. At $p=0$ both moments equal one.

For $0<\alpha<2$, write $z=re^{i\theta}$ with $0<\theta<\pi$. The chosen power map sends this to $r^\alpha e^{i\alpha\theta}$, so
$$
\boxed{D=\{re^{i\theta}:r>0,\ 0<\theta<\alpha\pi\}.}
$$
At $\alpha=2$ this is the plane slit along the nonnegative real axis. For every $0<\alpha\leq2$, the map is conformal on the upper half-plane.

To check finiteness of the physical exit time, start a <Brownian motion> $B$ at $i$ and let $T$ be its real-axis exit. The <time change of a continuous process> in <conformal invariance of planar Brownian motion> is
$$
A_T=\int_0^T\alpha^2|B_s|^{2\alpha-2}ds.
$$
We know $T<\infty$ almost surely. The <Cauchy exit law from a Brownian half-plane> assigns <probability> zero to $B_T=0$. On such a path, $B_s\neq0$ throughout the compact interval $[0,T]$, because it is in the open upper half-plane before exit and has nonzero endpoint. Its modulus therefore has a positive minimum and finite maximum. The displayed integrand is bounded on that interval, proving $A_T<\infty$. The image <Brownian motion> exits $D$ at this finite changed time.

The endpoint $\alpha=0$ printed in the source is degenerate: $z^0=1$, so its image is the singleton $\{1\}$, not an open domain, and the map is not conformal. If exit from that set is defined for <Brownian motion> started at one, its exit infimum is zero. It cannot be included in a nondegenerate conformal-domain argument.

Here is the <martingale> argument for maximal comparisons, including the starting-value issue. For $P=f+ig$, the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> and <Itô's formula> show that
$$
F_t=f(Z_{t\wedge\tau})-f(i^\alpha),\qquad
G_t=g(Z_{t\wedge\tau})-g(i^\alpha)
$$
are orthogonal continuous <local martingales> with the same <quadratic variation>. Localize before large disks and before exit. The <Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequalities> then give
$$
\mathbb E(G^*)^p\leq C_p\mathbb E(F^*)^p,\qquad p>1,
$$
with a constant depending only on $p$; <Fatou's lemma> removes the localization. These are centered <martingales>. For the uncentered processes this implies
$$
\mathbb E\sup|g(Z_t)|^p
\leq C_p'\bigl(\mathbb E\sup|f(Z_t)|^p+|g(i^\alpha)|^p\bigr),
$$
since $|f(i^\alpha)|\leq\sup|f(Z_t)|$.

The source normalizes $P(0)=0$ at a boundary point, rather than the value of $g$ at the starting point. Consequently its displayed constant cannot be uniform in $\alpha$ with only $p$ dependence. To see this, take $P(z)=iz$ and $0<\alpha<\min(1/2,1/p)$. Then $f=-\operatorname{Im}z$ and $g=\operatorname{Re}z$. Inside this narrow wedge,
$$
\sup|f(Z_t)|\leq\tan(\alpha\pi)\sup|g(Z_t)|.
$$
Both $p$th moments are finite and the right one is positive; finiteness follows from the wedge radial-maximum tail of order $R^{-1/\alpha}$, justified below. The source inequality would force $C_p\geq\cot^p(\alpha\pi)$, unbounded as $\alpha\downarrow0$. At $\alpha=0$, the immediate-exit interpretation gives the direct contradiction $1\leq C_p\,0$ for the same <polynomial>.

For a fixed nondegenerate wedge, an uncentered inequality does hold with a constant allowed to depend on $\alpha$ as well as $p$. One rigorous outline for the <polynomial> class in this question is as follows. Separation of variables in a sector of radius $R$ gives, for exit through its circular arc, the expansion
$$
\mathbb P_{re^{i\theta}}(\text{hit radius }R\text{ before the sides})
=\frac4\pi\sum_{\substack{n\geq1\\n\ {\rm odd}}}
\frac1n(r/R)^{n/\alpha}\sin(n\theta/\alpha).
$$
Thus at the specified starting point the radial-maximum tail is comparable to $R^{-1/\alpha}$. Hitting any fixed interior subarc has that same power, with positive leading coefficient. If a nonzero <polynomial> has degree $d$, its <real part> has magnitude at least $cR^d$ on some fixed interior subarc for sufficiently large $R$. Therefore its maximal real-part $p$th moment is infinite whenever $dp\geq1/\alpha$. In that case the desired extended-real inequality is automatic.

The remaining <polynomials> have $d<1/(\alpha p)$, so belong to a <finite-dimensional vector space> space. On that space
$$
P\longmapsto\left(\mathbb E\sup|f(Z_t)|^p\right)^{1/p}
$$
is a genuine <norm>: if it is zero, <Brownian motion>'s positive <probability> of reaching any interior neighborhood forces $\operatorname{Re}P=0$ throughout $D$, so analyticity makes $P$ a purely imaginary constant, and $P(0)=0$ makes it zero. The corresponding imaginary-part functional is bounded on this <finite-dimensional vector space> normed space; its finiteness follows from $dp<1/\alpha$ and the radial tail bound. <equivalence of norms in finite dimensions> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E\sup|g(Z_t)|^p
\leq C_{p,\alpha}\mathbb E\sup|f(Z_t)|^p
\quad(0<\alpha\leq2).}
$$
This establishes the fixed-domain interpretation and identifies exactly why the universal centered <martingale> inequality cannot silently be applied to the printed uncentered boundary normalization. It is the distinction in <starting-value obstruction in wedge conjugate maximal inequalities>.
