= Gravitational wave
{wiki}

= Gravitational radiation
{synonym}

A <gravitational wave> is a propagating disturbance of <spacetime> <curvature> produced by changing <mass>-<energy> multipole moments. In <general relativity>, its two transverse <gravitational wave polarizations> alternately stretch and squeeze freely falling separations. The leading radiation from a slowly moving source is governed by its <mass quadrupole moment>, rather than by its total <mass> alone.

The experimental breakthrough was <GW150914>: observed on 14 September 2015 and published on 11 February 2016. The <LIGO Scientific Collaboration> and <Virgo Collaboration> reported a merging pair of <black holes>, using the two <LIGO> detectors at Hanford and Livingston; Virgo was being upgraded and did not observe this event.<Observation of Gravitational Waves from a Binary Black Hole Merger by B. P. Abbott and others>[Abstract; II Observation, pp. 1–3; received and publication dates on p. 1]{ref}

The 2017 <Nobel Prize in Physics> recognized <Rainer Weiss>, <Barry C. Barish> and <Kip S. Thorne> for contributions to <LIGO> and <gravitational-wave> observation. <Rainer Weiss> developed interferometric detection and its noise analysis; <Barry C. Barish> led the project into construction and operation; <Kip S. Thorne> developed its theoretical and astrophysical foundations. The experiment depended on a large collaboration.<Press release: The Nobel Prize in Physics 2017 by Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences>[3 October 2017 announcement, laureates and account of their complementary contributions]{ref}

= GW150914
{c}
{parent=Gravitational wave}
{wiki}

<GW150914> is the first directly detected <gravitational wave>, interpreted as a <binary black hole> inspiral, merger and ringdown. Its discovery paper reports a peak projected <gravitational-wave> strain of approximately $10^{-21}$ and a combined <matched-filter> <signal-to-noise ratio> of 24. The inferred source-frame component <masses> were $36^{+5}_{-4}$ and $29^{+4}_{-4}$ <solar masses>, with 90 percent <credible intervals>. These are model-dependent estimates from a detector network.<Observation of Gravitational Waves from a Binary Black Hole Merger by B. P. Abbott and others>[Abstract; II Observation; VI Source discussion, Table I]{ref}

The interesting measurement is a chain: <curvature> changes relative light travel times, <wave interference> turns those changes into a <photodetector> signal, calibrated records from separate sites constrain a common source, and <statistical inference> tests competing explanations. The following original models explain that chain; the plotted time series are stipulated calculations, never measured <GW150914> data.

= LIGO differential strain measurement
{c}
{parent=GW150914}
{title2=$h_D=(\delta L_x-\delta L_y)/L$}

Each <LIGO> instrument is a <Michelson interferometer> with approximately $4\,\mathrm{km}$ arms containing <Fabry–Pérot interferometers>. A stabilized $1064\,\mathrm{nm}$ <laser> illuminates suspended $40\,\mathrm{kg}$ fused-silica mirrors in <vacuum>. Four-stage suspensions and active isolation suppress ground motion; low-loss materials reduce <thermal noise>. Power recycling increases circulating <power>; signal recycling shapes the response to the <gravitational wave>.<Advanced LIGO by J. Aasi and others>[Sections 2–2.3, Table 1; 4.4 Test mass suspensions, Fig. 8; 4.5 Seismic isolation]{ref}

Why measure two perpendicular arms? In the long-<wavelength> limit, take a <gravitational wave> with <plus polarization> normally incident on ideal perpendicular arms. Its <transverse-traceless tensor> has $h_{xx}=h_+$ and $h_{yy}=-h_+$. Expanding each proper length to first order gives
$$
\frac{\delta L_x}{L}=\frac{h_+}{2},\qquad
\frac{\delta L_y}{L}=-\frac{h_+}{2},\qquad
\Delta L=\delta L_x-\delta L_y=Lh_+.
$$
Here $h_+$ is dimensionless, whereas $\Delta L$ is a <length>. A stipulated $h_+=10^{-21}$ and $L=4000\,\mathrm m$ imply $\Delta L=4\times10^{-18}\,\mathrm m$, with individual arm changes of opposite sign and magnitude $2\times10^{-18}\,\mathrm m$. These are equivalent differential arm-length changes, not a photograph of mirrors moving against a rigid external ruler. The observable compares returning light phases; <coordinate systems> can distribute the description between mirror coordinates and light propagation.

For unit arm directions $\mathbf u,\mathbf v$, define the detector <tensor> $D^{ij}=(u^iu^j-v^iv^j)/2$. If $e^+_{ij},e^\times_{ij}$ are the transverse polarization tensors, then
$$
h_D=D^{ij}h^{\rm TT}_{ij}=F_+h_++F_\times h_\times,\qquad
F_A=D^{ij}e^A_{ij}.
$$
The dimensionless factors $F_A$ encode sky direction and the polarization basis. Thus even identical sources can have different observed <amplitudes> and <wave phases>. Motion changing both arms equally cancels in this ideal differential observable, although real asymmetries can couple common disturbances into it.

Suspension supplies an inertial reference above its resonances. For example, a single ideal <harmonic oscillator> with support motion $y$ obeys $\ddot x+\omega_0^2(x-y)=0$. Its response at <angular frequency> $\omega$ is $x/y=\omega_0^2/(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)$, which decreases in magnitude as $(\omega_0/\omega)^2$ well above resonance. The mirror follows slow support drift but approximately free motion in the high-frequency limit. Real multistage suspensions also require damping and alignment control; resonant frequencies are not regions of good isolation.

<Rainer Weiss>'s 1972 report proposed suspended masses, multiple-pass optical delay lines, and a servo holding an interference fringe. Its noise analysis separately treats photon-counting fluctuations, <laser> frequency instability, mechanical <thermal noise>, <radiation pressure> and ground motion. Those delay lines should be distinguished from the resonant arm cavities of Advanced <LIGO>.<Electromagnetically coupled broadband gravitational antenna by Rainer Weiss>[Typeset transcription of 1972 report, sections 4–5.5, Fig. 1; original scan is linked from DCC record P720002]{ref}

= LIGO displacement readout and control
{c}
{parent=LIGO differential strain measurement}

An ideal single-round-trip <Michelson interferometer> converts differential <length> into <phase difference> $\delta\phi=4\pi\Delta L/\lambda$. If its dark-port <power> scale is $P_0$ and its static phase offset is $\phi_0$, then
$$
P_{\rm out}=P_0\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\phi_0+\delta\phi}{2}\right),\qquad
\delta P=\frac{P_0}{2}\sin\phi_0\,\delta\phi+O(\delta\phi^2).
$$
Exactly at a dark fringe, $\phi_0=0$, <power> is quadratic in small <displacement> and loses its sign. A deliberate offset supplies an optical reference field: interference with the small signal field produces a linear term. Equivalently, $|E_{\rm ref}+E_{\rm sig}|^2$ contains $2\operatorname{Re}(E_{\rm ref}^*E_{\rm sig})$. The <photodetector> measures <power>, not a signed <displacement> directly.

Advanced <LIGO> uses this DC homodyne principle with a small differential arm offset and an output mode cleaner. Its <feedback control> maintains optical resonance and alignment, using actuation distributed over the suspension stages.<Advanced LIGO by J. Aasi and others>[2.3 Gravitational wave readout; 4.4.2 Quadruple pendulum design; 4.8 Interferometer sensing and control]{ref} The simple formula above omits resonant storage: arm and recycling cavities give a frequency-dependent optical gain and delay, so no constant pass-count multiplier describes the entire observing band.

To see why a locked detector can still measure a disturbance, use a linear <feedback control> model. Let $x_{\rm ext}$ be the external differential <displacement>, $d_{\rm err}$ the residual readout, $d_{\rm ctrl}$ the recorded control command, $C(f)$ the sensing response and $A(f)$ the actuation response. With the convention $x_{\rm res}=x_{\rm ext}-A d_{\rm ctrl}$, reconstruction gives
$$
\widetilde x_{\rm ext}=C^{-1}\widetilde d_{\rm err}+A\widetilde d_{\rm ctrl},\qquad
\widetilde h_D=\widetilde x_{\rm ext}/L.
$$
Here tildes denote <Fourier transforms>; $C$ has readout units per metre and $A$ metres per command unit. Feedback suppresses residual motion but records the compensating command. Omitting that command would suppress the reconstructed low-frequency signal too. In the actual <GW150914> experiment, a modulated auxiliary <laser> exerted calibrated <radiation pressure> on a mirror to measure the response; periodic excitations monitored calibration.<Calibration of the Advanced LIGO detectors for the discovery of the binary black-hole merger GW150914 by B. P. Abbott and others>[II Control loop, equations (1)–(4); IV Photon calibrator; VII Time-dependent systematic errors]{ref}

= Coincidence and noise controls for GW150914
{parent=GW150914}

The modeled <matched-filter> search found an arrival difference of about $7\,\mathrm{ms}$, within the roughly $10\,\mathrm{ms}$ intersite light travel time. Background events were obtained by shifting one detector's timestamps far beyond that time and repeating the coincidence analysis. The binary search bounded the <false alarm rate> below one event per $203\,000$ years. That is an empirically estimated background rate, not a probability that the source interpretation is false.<GW150914: First results from the search for binary black hole coalescence with Advanced LIGO by B. P. Abbott and others>[Abstract; I Introduction; time-shift background analysis, Fig. 5]{ref}

The logic is to destroy a common astronomical arrival while retaining each instrument's real glitches. A <Poisson distribution> approximation would turn a background rate $r$ into $1-e^{-rT}$ for at least one equally strong noise event during live time $T$, only after accounting for the search's ranking and trials. Repeated time slides reuse data; their accumulated background duration is not an equally long series of independent new observations. Correlated disturbances also require separate controls because shifting timestamps can erase them along with a true signal.

The dedicated noise study inspected auxiliary channels and deliberately applied magnetic, acoustic and mechanical disturbances to measure their coupling into strain. For <GW150914>, checks included environmental coincidences, instrument state, and exclusion of an intentional hardware injection. It also examined potentially correlated global magnetic disturbances, which time slides alone cannot exclude.<Characterization of transient noise in Advanced LIGO relevant to gravitational wave signal GW150914 by B. P. Abbott and others>[2 Identifying noise sources, Fig. 2; 6 Validation of GW150914; Appendix B physical environment monitors]{ref} The point of an injection test is quantitative: a quiet environmental monitor is informative only if its sensitivity and coupling bound the disturbance needed to explain the candidate.

= Inferring a binary from a detector network
{parent=GW150914}

For detector $k$, model the calibrated data as $d_k(t)=h_k(t;\theta)+n_k(t)$, with a shared source parameter vector $\theta$, separate detector responses, propagation delays and <noise> $n_k$. Under an independent stationary <Gaussian process> approximation with one-sided <power spectral density> $S_k(f)$, define the noise-weighted <inner product> and <likelihood function>
$$
(a|b)_k=4\operatorname{Re}\int_{f_{\min}}^{f_{\max}}\frac{\widetilde a(f)^*\widetilde b(f)}{S_k(f)}\,df,\qquad
\log\mathcal L(\theta)=-\frac12\sum_k(d_k-h_k|d_k-h_k)_k+\mathrm{constant}.
$$
Strain <power spectral density> has units $\mathrm{Hz}^{-1}$; the displayed <inner product> is dimensionless when the <Fourier transform> integrates over seconds. A <matched filter> rewards agreement over the full evolving <wave phase>, weighting frequency bands by their noise. One conspicuous peak is insufficient: a candidate should also have the right distribution of signal across frequency bands and compatible projections in the network.

The parameter paper used coherent <Bayesian inference>, <numerical relativity>-informed inspiral–merger–ringdown models and explicit calibration uncertainties. It found a strong distance–inclination correlation; source-frame <masses> additionally require converting the detector-frame masses by a cosmological <redshift>.<Properties of the Binary Black Hole Merger GW150914 by B. P. Abbott and others>[II Parameter estimation, equations (1)–(5), II.A waveform models; III Results, p. 7, Fig. 2]{ref} Conceptually, multiply the <likelihood function> by a <prior distribution> to obtain a <posterior distribution>. Changes in waveform families, residual structure after subtraction, and checks with a generic transient reconstruction test parts of the modeling assumptions. Detection significance and parameter uncertainty answer different questions.

= Newtonian binary chirp
{c}
{parent=Gravitational wave}
{title2=$\dot f=Kf^{11/3}$}

A <Newtonian binary chirp> is a leading-order model in which loss of orbital <energy> to <gravitational radiation> makes an almost circular <binary system> shrink and its <gravitational-wave frequency> rise. Here is a derivation with explicit SI units. Let the constant component <masses> $m_1,m_2$ be in kilograms, $M=m_1+m_2$, $\mu=m_1m_2/M$, separation $a$ in metres, and orbital <angular frequency> $\Omega$ in radians per second. <Newtonian gravity>, circular motion and the <quadrupole formula> give
$$
\Omega^2=\frac{GM}{a^3},\qquad f=\frac{\Omega}{\pi},\qquad
E=-\frac{G\mu M}{2a},\qquad
P=\frac{32G^4\mu^2M^3}{5c^5a^5},\qquad \dot E=-P.
$$
The factor $\pi$ rather than $2\pi$ appears because the dominant <mass quadrupole moment> repeats twice per orbit. Since $dE/da>0$, radiative <energy> loss implies $\dot a<0$, hence
$$
\dot a=-\frac{64G^3\mu M^2}{5c^5a^3},\qquad
\dot f=Kf^{11/3},\qquad
K=\frac{96}{5}\pi^{8/3}\left(\frac{G\mathcal M}{c^3}\right)^{5/3},\qquad
\mathcal M=\mu^{3/5}M^{2/5}.
$$
The quantity $G\mathcal M/c^3$ is a time in seconds. Thus $K$ has units $\mathrm{s}^{5/3}$ and $Kf^{11/3}$ has units $\mathrm{s}^{-2}$, as required for <frequency> change per second. This model assumes weak gravity, slow relative motion, negligible <eccentricity of a conic>, no spin corrections and adiabatic evolution over an orbit. It does not describe merger or ringdown.

Separating variables gives a finite formal coalescence time $t_c$ and accumulated <wave phase> $\Phi$:
$$
\tau(f)=t_c-t=\frac{3}{8K}f^{-8/3},\qquad
f(t)=\left[\frac{8K}{3}(t_c-t)\right]^{-3/8},\qquad
\Phi(f)-\Phi(f_0)=\frac{6\pi}{5K}\left(f_0^{-5/3}-f^{-5/3}\right).
$$
Indeed $d\Phi/dt=2\pi f$; the formal divergence at $t_c$ signals extrapolation outside the approximation. Over a finite frequency interval, the number of <gravitational-wave> cycles is $N=3(f_0^{-5/3}-f_1^{-5/3})/(5K)$. This accumulated phase is why a <chirp mass> can be constrained even when distance and inclination remain entangled.

= Chirp mass
{parent=Newtonian binary chirp}
{wiki}
{title2=$\mathcal M=(m_1m_2)^{3/5}/(m_1+m_2)^{1/5}$}

The <chirp mass> is the combination of two component <masses> controlling the leading <Newtonian binary chirp>. Inverting its frequency equation gives
$$
\mathcal M=\frac{c^3}{G}\left[\frac{5}{96}\pi^{-8/3}f^{-11/3}\dot f\right]^{3/5}.
$$
Use $f$ in hertz and $\dot f$ in hertz per second to obtain kilograms. For equal component <masses> $m$, $\mathcal M=m/2^{1/5}$. A stipulated $20+20$ <solar-mass> system therefore has $\mathcal M\approx17.411$ <solar masses>. In a cosmological observation the time and frequency measured at the detector instead yield $(1+z)\mathcal M_{\rm source}$, where $z$ is <redshift>; phase evolution alone does not independently determine the source <mass> and <redshift>.

= Stipulated chirps and detector projections
{parent=Newtonian binary chirp}

To construct an original example rather than imitate <GW150914>, set $G=6.67430\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}$, $c=299\,792\,458\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, and adopt the rounded <solar mass> $M_\odot=1.98847\times10^{30}\,\mathrm{kg}$. Compare $10+10$ and $20+20$ <solar-mass> binaries starting at $20\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Stop each at the stipulated <orbital compactness> threshold
$$
x=\frac{GM}{ac^2}=\left(\frac{\pi GMf}{c^3}\right)^{2/3}=0.10,\qquad
f_{\rm cut}=\frac{c^3}{\pi GM}(0.10)^{3/2}.
$$
This is a conservative pedagogical stopping rule, not a claimed error bound: the relative speed already reaches $\sqrt{0.10}\,c$. Higher-order corrections accumulate <wave phase> well before merger, and precision inference requires better models. The two cutoffs are approximately $102.2$ and $51.1\,\mathrm{Hz}$ respectively; doubling the <chirp mass> multiplies $\dot f$ at fixed $f$ by $2^{5/3}$.

For a nearby stipulated source at distance $D$, neglect <redshift> and define inclination $\iota$ as the angle between the orbital <angular momentum> and the line of sight. The leading <quadrupole formula> yields
$$
A(f)=\frac{4(G\mathcal M)^{5/3}(\pi f)^{2/3}}{c^4D},\qquad
h_+=A(f)\frac{1+\cos^2\iota}{2}\cos\Phi,\qquad
h_\times=A(f)\cos\iota\sin\Phi.
$$
The numerator of $A$ has units $\mathrm{m^5\,s^{-4}}$, matching $c^4D$, so both polarizations are dimensionless. Its $f^{2/3}$ growth and the rising <frequency> produce the tightening, strengthening oscillations. For face-on viewing the polarization components have equal magnitudes in <phase quadrature>; for edge-on viewing the cross component vanishes, agreeing with <circular-binary quadrupole radiation>.

A physical source model must jointly explain those differences, not erase them by arbitrary rescaling. = Averaged stress-energy of transverse gravitational waves
{parent=Gravitational wave}
{title2=$t^{\rm GW}_{ab}=\frac1{32\pi G}\langle\partial_aH_{ij}\partial_bH_{ij}\rangle$}

In the weak-field, short-wavelength averaging regime, a <transverse-traceless tensor> strain $H_{ij}$ carries the displayed effective <stress-energy tensor> in units $c=1$. The average spans several wave periods while remaining small compared with the background <curvature> scale. The stress is second order in amplitude, so it is absent from a first-order vacuum <linearized Einstein equations> calculation. For a plane wave, summing the two equal transverse diagonal contributions gives flux $(16\pi G)^{-1}\langle\dot H_+^2+\dot H_\times^2\rangle$. This effective averaged stress is not an arbitrary pointwise gravitational energy <tensor>.

= Axisymmetric radiation carries no axial angular momentum
{parent=Gravitational wave}

An exact axial <Killing vector field> makes <Komar angular momentum> independent of the surrounding vacuum surface. Therefore <gravitational waves> in a configuration with exact <axisymmetry> carry no net <angular momentum> about that symmetry axis, although they can carry <energy>. This is specific to axial <angular momentum>; it is not an absence of radiation.

= Gravitational-wave frequency
{title2=$f_{\rm GW}$}
{parent=Gravitational wave}

The gravitational-wave frequency is the temporal oscillation frequency of a radiative metric perturbation. A rigid source rotating with angular frequency $\omega$ and invariant under a half-turn radiates dominantly at angular frequency $2\omega$.

= Mass quadrupole moment
{title2=$Q_{ij}$}
{parent=Gravitational wave}

The trace-free mass quadrupole moment of a mass distribution is
$$
Q_{ij}=\int \rho(\mathbf x)
\left(x_ix_j-\frac13\delta_{ij}|\mathbf x|^2\right)d^3x.
$$
It is the leading time-dependent mass multipole that can radiate in <general relativity>: mass conservation removes monopole radiation, while momentum conservation removes mass-dipole radiation.

= Stress-energy quadrupole identity
{parent=Mass quadrupole moment}
{title2=$\ddot I^{ij}=2\int T^{ij}\,d^3x$}

For a symmetric <stress-energy tensor> with <compact support> and $\partial_bT^{ab}=0$ in <Minkowski spacetime>, let $I^{ij}=\int T^{00}x^ix^j\,d^3x$. Two integrations by parts give $\dot I^{ij}=\int(T^{0i}x^j+T^{0j}x^i)\,d^3x$ and the displayed identity. Compact support removes the boundary fluxes. The support should stay bounded over the time interval used when differentiating the integrals.

= Second mass moment tensor
{parent=Mass quadrupole moment}
{title2=$I_{ij}=\int \rho(\mathbf x)x_ix_j\,d^3x$}

The second mass moment tensor records the quadratic spatial distribution of mass. Its trace-free part is the <mass quadrupole moment> $Q_{ij}=I_{ij}-\delta_{ij}I_{kk}/3$. The mechanical moment-of-inertia tensor instead equals $J_{ij}=\delta_{ij}I_{kk}-I_{ij}$. A rigid rotation $L$ transforms the second mass moment tensor as $I(t)=L(t)I(0)L(t)^T$.

= Scalar second mass moment
{parent=Second mass moment tensor}
{title2=$I=\int r^2\,dm$}

The scalar second mass moment is the <trace> of the <second mass moment tensor>. The <moment of inertia> about a specified axis instead integrates squared distance from that axis. For an isotropic distribution each axial <moment of inertia> is $2I/3$; the sum over three orthogonal axes is $2I$.

= Quadrupole formula
{parent=Mass quadrupole moment}
{wiki}

For a slowly moving isolated source, the leading gravitational-wave luminosity is
$$
P=\frac{G}{5c^5}\left\langle\dddot Q_{ij}\dddot Q_{ij}\right\rangle,
$$
where $Q_{ij}$ is the trace-free mass quadrupole moment.

= Far-zone quadrupole strain
{parent=Quadrupole formula}
{title2=$H^{\mathrm{TT}}_{ij}=\frac{2G}{r}\Lambda_{ij,kl}(\mathbf n)\ddot Q^{kl}(t-r)$}

In units $c=1$, a slowly moving isolated source has trace-free <mass quadrupole moment> $Q^{ij}=\int T^{00}(x^ix^j-\delta^{ij}|\mathbf x|^2/3)\,d^3x$. The retarded <linearized gravity> solution and the <stress-energy quadrupole identity> give the displayed physical spatial strain, with $\mathbf n=\mathbf x/r$ and the <transverse-traceless projector> $\Lambda$. If the spatial metric is written $g_{ij}=-\delta_{ij}-2E_{ij}$ for <metric signature> $(+---)$, its trace-free Lorenz-gauge coefficient before the transverse projection is $E_{ij}=G\ddot Q_{ij}(t-r)/r$. Thus $H^{\mathrm{TT}}=2E^{\mathrm{TT}}$. Far distance alone does not permit dropping source-dependent retardation: the quadrupole approximation also requires the source size to be small compared with the radiation wavelength.

= Quadrupole strain scaling of a weak self-gravitating source
{parent=Far-zone quadrupole strain}
{title2=$E=O(\epsilon^2R/r),\quad\epsilon=GM/R$}

For a weakly self-gravitating bound source of size $R$, mass $M$ and nonspherical dynamical <mass quadrupole moment> $Q=O(MR^2)$, the <virial theorem> gives $v^2=O(GM/R)=O(\epsilon)$. Its time scale is $R/v$, so $\ddot Q=O(Mv^2)$. The <far-zone quadrupole strain> then has a spatial half-strain coefficient $E=O(GMv^2/r)=O(\epsilon^2R/r)$ in units $c=1$. The physical transverse-traceless strain is $2E^{\mathrm{TT}}$. Spherical or stationary sources can have zero radiation, so this is a characteristic scale for a changing nonspherical quadrupole, not a positive lower bound. The radiation zone also requires $r\gg R/v$.

= Circular-binary quadrupole radiation
{parent=Far-zone quadrupole strain}
{title2=$\omega^2=Gm/(4R^3),\quad \omega_{\mathrm{GW}}=2\omega$}

Two equal masses $m$ orbiting their <centre of mass> at radius $R$ have separation $2R$. <Newton's law of universal gravitation> gives the displayed orbital frequency. Their <mass quadrupole moment> varies at twice that frequency, producing <gravitational radiation> with two <gravitational wave polarizations>. For inclination $\iota$, its leading plus and cross amplitudes are proportional to $1+\cos^2\iota$ and $2\cos\iota$, respectively. Face-on radiation is circularly polarized; edge-on radiation is linearly polarized.

= Orbital compactness
{parent=Quadrupole formula}
{title2=$GM/(Rc^2)$}

The dimensionless ratio of gravitational radius to orbital scale measures how relativistic a bound orbit is. In an equal-mass circular binary with individual orbital radius $R$, the leading <quadrupole formula> gives $P=(2/5)(c^5/G)[GM/(Rc^2)]^5$. Compact objects permit smaller noncontact orbits than extended stars and hence larger orbital compactness; near merger the Newtonian approximation must be replaced.

= Equal-mass circular-binary quadrupole luminosity
{parent=Quadrupole formula}
{title2=$P=\frac{2G^4M^5}{5c^5R^5}$}

For two masses $M$ at opposite points of a circular orbit, each at radius $R$ from the centre of mass, $\Omega^2=GM/(4R^3)$. The trace-free <mass quadrupole moment> oscillates at twice the orbital frequency, and its third-derivative squared contraction is $128M^2R^4\Omega^6$. The <quadrupole formula> gives the displayed phase-independent luminosity. Using separation $2R$ rather than individual radius $R$ avoids a factor-of-32 error.

= Head-on quadrupole radiation from equal masses
{parent=Quadrupole formula}
{title2=$P=m^5(1/z-1/z_0)/(15z^4)$}

For two equal <point masses> at $\pm z$ falling from rest at $\pm z_0$ under <Newtonian gravity>, in <geometrized units> one has $\ddot z=-m/(4z^2)$ and $\dot z=-\sqrt{m(1/z-1/z_0)/2}$. The only nonzero component of the <second mass moment tensor> is $I_{zz}=2mz^2$, and its third derivative is $-m^2\dot z/z^2$. The trace-free <mass quadrupole moment> and <quadrupole formula> give the displayed power. From infinity, integrating down to $z=z_c$ gives $E=2\sqrt2\,m^{9/2}/(105z_c^{7/2})$. Relative to the total initial mass $2m$ the fraction is $\sqrt2(m/z_c)^{7/2}/105$. The weak-field slow-motion approximation must be distinguished from its extrapolation to a compact endpoint.

= Quadrupole radiation from a harmonically oscillating point mass
{parent=Quadrupole formula}
{title2=$\langle P\rangle=\frac{16Gm^2L^4\omega^6}{15c^5}$}

For a <point mass> moving as $z=L\cos\omega t$ along one fixed axis, the trace-free <mass quadrupole moment> is $mL^2\cos^2\omega t\,\operatorname{diag}(-1/3,-1/3,2/3)$. Its radiating component has twice the source <angular frequency>. The <quadrupole formula> gives $\langle P\rangle=16Gm^2L^4\omega^6/(15c^5)$ for this contribution in the leading slow-motion <weak-field approximation>. Any physical driver or support has its own quadrupole contribution; this expression is for the prescribed mass alone.

= Gravitational-wave energy and angular-momentum balance
{parent=Quadrupole formula}
{title2=$\dot E=\Omega\dot J$}

For a slowly evolving circular <binary star> with separation $a$, <reduced mass> $\mu$ and orbital angular frequency $\Omega$, the leading <gravitational-wave> losses satisfy $\dot E=\Omega\dot J$. One direct check uses the standard quadrupole angular-momentum loss law
$$
\dot J_i=-\frac{2G}{5c^5}\epsilon_{ijk}\left\langle\ddot Q_{j\ell}\dddot Q_{k\ell}\right\rangle,
$$
where $Q$ is the <mass quadrupole moment> and $\epsilon_{ijk}$ is the <Levi-Civita symbol>. For $\mathbf r=a(\cos\Omega t,\sin\Omega t,0)$, put $A=\mu a^2$; then $Q_{xx}=A(1/6+\cos(2\Omega t)/2)$, $Q_{yy}=A(1/6-\cos(2\Omega t)/2)$, $Q_{xy}=A\sin(2\Omega t)/2$, and $Q_{zz}=-A/3$. Substitution gives $\dot J_z=-32GA^2\Omega^5/(5c^5)$ and the <quadrupole formula> gives $\dot E=-32GA^2\Omega^6/(5c^5)$. Their ratio proves the balance relation. The same relation follows from the rotating wave's angular harmonic number two and frequency $2\Omega$. The additional angular-momentum flux law, not the energy flux alone, is needed to justify preservation of secular circularity.

= Instantaneous quadrupole luminosity of a Kepler binary
{parent=Quadrupole formula}
{title2=$L=32G^3M^2\mu^2(v^2-11\dot r^2/12)/(5c^5r^4)$}

For a Newtonian <Kepler orbit>, let $M=M_1+M_2$ be the total <mass> and $\mu=M_1M_2/M$ the <reduced mass>; put $k=GM$, $\mathbf n=\mathbf r/r$, $u=\dot r$ and $\mathbf v_\perp=\dot{\mathbf r}-u\mathbf n$, with $v^2=\dot{\mathbf r}\cdot\dot{\mathbf r}$. In the convention $q_{ij}=\mu(3r_ir_j-r^2\delta_{ij})/2$, differentiating the acceleration $\ddot{\mathbf r}=-k\mathbf r/r^3$ gives
$$
\dddot q_{ij}=\frac{\mu k}{r^2}\left[u(\delta_{ij}-3n_in_j)-6(n_iv_{\perp j}+v_{\perp i}n_j)\right].
$$
The two terms have zero cross <tensor contraction>, with squared norms $6u^2$ and $72v_\perp^2$. Therefore $\dddot q_{ij}\dddot q_{ij}=72\mu^2k^2(v^2-11u^2/12)/r^4$. Substitution into the <quadrupole formula> in this convention, $L=4G\dddot q_{ij}\dddot q_{ij}/(45c^5)$, proves the luminosity. It is nonnegative since $v^2-11u^2/12=v_\perp^2+u^2/12$. The formula uses a weak gravitational field, slow motion and leading radiation order; orbital averaging gives a secular luminosity.

= Gravitational radiation from a rotating triaxial body
{parent=Quadrupole formula}
{title2=$P=\frac{32G}{5c^5}\Omega^6(I_1-I_2)^2$}

For a rigid body rotating around its third principal axis, the anisotropic part of its <second mass moment tensor> oscillates at angular frequency $2\Omega$. The <quadrupole formula> gives the displayed luminosity. Equal transverse principal moments remove this rotating quadrupole and its leading <gravitational wave> emission.

= Gravitational-wave energy flux
{parent=Gravitational wave}

In the radiation zone, the time-averaged energy flux of a weak wave in <transverse-traceless gauge> is
$$
\frac{dP}{d\Omega}
=\frac{c^3r^2}{32\pi G}
\left\langle\dot h^{\rm TT}_{ij}\dot h^{\rm TT}_{ij}\right\rangle.
$$
Combining this with the far-zone quadrupole field and integrating over directions gives the <quadrupole formula>.

= Newman-Penrose formalism
{c}
{parent=Gravitational wave}
{wiki=Newman–Penrose_formalism}

The Newman--Penrose formalism resolves spacetime curvature into scalars by contracting the Weyl tensor with a complex null tetrad.

= Complex null tetrad
{parent=Newman-Penrose formalism}

A complex null tetrad consists of two real null vectors and a complex-conjugate transverse pair, normalized so that the metric is reconstructed from their pairwise products.

= Newman--Penrose scalar Psi4
{title2=$\Psi_4$}
{c}
{parent=Newman-Penrose formalism}

For a null tetrad $(\ell,k,m,\bar m)$, one common curvature-sign convention defines $\Psi_4=C_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}k^\mu\bar m^\nu k^\rho\bar m^\sigma$. In an outgoing wave zone it encodes the two transverse <gravitational wave polarizations> as one complex scalar.

= Plane-fronted gravitational wave
{parent=Gravitational wave}

For
$$
ds^2=2\,du\,dv+dx^2+dy^2+H(u,x,y)\,du^2,
$$
the only potentially nonzero Ricci component is
$$
R_{uu}=-\frac12(H_{xx}+H_{yy}).
$$
The vacuum equation is therefore the transverse Laplace equation.

= Transverse tidal equation of a Brinkmann plane wave
{parent=Plane-fronted gravitational wave}
{title2=$\ddot{\boldsymbol\xi}=M(u)\boldsymbol\xi$}

For the <plane-fronted gravitational wave> $ds^2=2du\,dv+d\mathbf x^2+\mathbf x^TM(u)\mathbf x\,du^2$, synchronized neighboring <geodesics> parametrized by $u$ have transverse separation $\boldsymbol\xi$ satisfying the displayed equation. Along the central <timelike geodesic> $(u,v,\mathbf x)=(\tau,-\tau/2,0)$ the <coordinate basis> is <parallel transport>, so this is also the transverse <Jacobi field> equation in a transported frame. Vacuum requires $\operatorname{tr}M=0$, producing equal and opposite instantaneous transverse <tidal acceleration> eigenvalues. The ordinary coordinate equation $\ddot\xi^v=0$ holds on that central reference, but generally fails on off-axis reference curves.

= Harmonic quadratic plane-wave profile
{parent=Plane-fronted gravitational wave}
{title2=$\psi=a(u)(x^2-y^2)+b(u)xy$}

A homogeneous quadratic <polynomial> in the transverse coordinates $x,y$ is a <harmonic function> exactly when the sum of its $x^2$ and $y^2$ coefficients is zero. Thus it has the displayed form. For the <Minkowski metric> $du\,dv+dx^2+dy^2$, a <metric perturbation> $h=\psi\,du^2$ has zero <trace>, zero <divergence> and $\Box h=0$, since $\partial_v\psi=0$ and $\partial_x^2\psi+\partial_y^2\psi=0$. The two coefficient <functions> describe the two <gravitational wave polarizations>.

= Ricci tensor of a Brinkmann plane wave
{c}
{parent=Plane-fronted gravitational wave}
{title2=$R_{uu}=H_{xx}+H_{yy}$}

For $ds^2=-2du\,dv+dx^2+dy^2-2H(u,x,y)du^2$, the nonzero connections are $\Gamma^v_{uu}=H_u$, $\Gamma^v_{ux}=H_x$, $\Gamma^v_{uy}=H_y$, $\Gamma^x_{uu}=H_x$, and $\Gamma^y_{uu}=H_y$, with symmetric lower indices. Their traces and quadratic Ricci products vanish, leaving only the displayed Ricci component. Hence the vacuum equations require a harmonic transverse profile. The sign is tied to the metric's $-2Hdu^2$ convention.

= Homogeneous wavefront of a plane gravitational wave
{parent=Plane-fronted gravitational wave}

For a Brinkmann plane wave whose transverse profile is quadratic, Killing fields obtained from solutions of transverse oscillator equations act transitively on every constant-retarded-time wavefront. Each wavefront is therefore a homogeneous null hypersurface.

= Rosen coordinates for a plane gravitational wave
{c}
{parent=Plane-fronted gravitational wave}

Rosen coordinates put a plane wave into the transverse-metric form
$$
ds^2=-du\,dv+g_{AB}(u)dx^A dx^B.
$$
For diagonal $g_{AB}=\operatorname{diag}(f^2,g^2)$, the only potentially nonzero <Ricci tensor> component is $R_{uu}=-f''/f-g''/g$.

= Curvature of a diagonal plane wave in Rosen coordinates
{parent=Rosen coordinates for a plane gravitational wave}
{title2=$\operatorname{Ric}=-(A''/A+B''/B)du\otimes du$}

For $g=-dt^2+A(u)^2dx^2+B(u)^2dy^2+dz^2$, $u=t-z$, with nowhere-zero smooth $A,B$, take $e^0=dt$, $e^1=A\,dx$, $e^2=B\,dy$, $e^3=dz$, $\ell=du$. In signature $(-,+,+,+)$, <Cartan's first structure equation> and <metric compatibility> give the lower-index <connection 1-forms> $\omega_{01}=\omega_{13}=-(A'/A)e^1$ and $\omega_{02}=\omega_{23}=-(B'/B)e^2$. <Cartan's second structure equation> gives $\Theta_{01}=\Theta_{13}=-(A''/A)\ell\wedge e^1$ and $\Theta_{02}=\Theta_{23}=-(B''/B)\ell\wedge e^2$. All other independent <curvature 2-forms> vanish: in particular the $12$ component cancels between the time and longitudinal terms. Contracting $R^a{}_{bad}$ yields $\operatorname{Ric}=-(A''/A+B''/B)du\otimes du$. Thus the <vacuum Einstein equations> are $A''/A+B''/B=0$, while nonzero $A''/A=-B''/B$ gives opposite transverse tidal curvatures and <plus polarization> relative to these axes.

= Gravitational-wave pulse
{parent=Plane-fronted gravitational wave}

A gravitational-wave pulse has curvature supported in a bounded interval of <retarded time>. Spacetime can be flat both before and after the pulse even though freely falling bodies retain a displacement or relative velocity produced while the pulse passed.

= Gravitational-wave memory effect
{parent=Gravitational-wave pulse}
{wiki}

The gravitational-wave memory effect is a lasting change in the relative configuration of freely falling test bodies after a transient <gravitational wave> has passed.

= Displacement memory
{parent=Gravitational-wave memory effect}

Displacement memory is a persistent change in the separation of freely falling bodies after a <gravitational-wave pulse>.

= Velocity memory
{parent=Gravitational-wave memory effect}

Velocity memory is a persistent change in the relative velocity of freely falling bodies after a <gravitational-wave pulse>.
