<Almost sure convergence> means
$$
\mathbb P\{\omega:\lim_{n\to\infty}X_n(\omega)=X(\omega)\}=1.
$$
Thus a single event of probability one supports ordinary pointwise convergence. <Convergence in probability> means that for every $\epsilon>0$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(|X_n-X|>\epsilon)\longrightarrow0.}
$$
The exceptional event is allowed to change with $n$ in the second definition.

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Fix $\epsilon>0$ and use the <indicator random variables> $Y_n=1_{\{|X_n-X|>\epsilon\}}$. <Almost sure convergence> gives $\limsup_nY_n=0$ almost surely, while $0\leq Y_n\leq1$. The <reverse Fatou lemma> states that for random variables bounded above by an integrable random variable,
$$
\limsup_n\mathbb E Y_n\leq\mathbb E(\limsup_nY_n).
$$
It follows that
$$
0\leq\limsup_n\mathbb P(|X_n-X|>\epsilon)\leq0.
$$
Since $\epsilon$ was arbitrary, this proves **convergence in probability**. The integrable upper bound is essential to the application of the <reverse Fatou lemma>.

<Independent random variables> satisfy, for every finite selection of distinct indices $n_1,\ldots,n_m$ and <Borel sets> $C_1,\ldots,C_m$,
$$
\mathbb P\left(\bigcap_{j=1}^m\{X_{n_j}\in C_j\}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^m\mathbb P(X_{n_j}\in C_j).
$$
The <second Borel-Cantelli lemma> says that if events $E_n$ are independent and $\sum_n\mathbb P(E_n)=\infty$, then
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(\limsup_nE_n)=1.}
$$
Here $\limsup_nE_n=\bigcap_N\bigcup_{n\geq N}E_n$ is the event that infinitely many of the $E_n$ occur.

By <convergence in probability>, choose recursively $n_k>n_{k-1}$ so that
$$
\mathbb P(|X_{n_k}-X|>2^{-k})<2^{-k}.
$$
The corresponding <events> have a summable sequence of probabilities. The <first Borel-Cantelli lemma>, which requires no independence, therefore makes the probability of infinitely many of them zero. On the complementary event, $|X_{n_k}-X|\leq2^{-k}$ for all sufficiently large $k$, proving
$$
\boxed{X_{n_k}\longrightarrow X\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$
The recursively chosen indices are strictly increasing and hence tend to infinity.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Suppose first that <complete convergence> holds. For each positive integer $j$, apply the <first Borel-Cantelli lemma> to $\{|X_n-X|>1/j\}$. Outside a countable union of null events, only finitely many of these exceedances occur for every $j$. This is <almost sure convergence>, with no independence assumption.

For the converse, an important point is that independence of $X_n$ does not immediately imply independence of $X_n-X$. Instead use the fact that an <almost-sure limit of independent random variables is constant>. Indeed, $Z=\limsup_nX_n$ is measurable for the <tail sigma-algebra>. The <Kolmogorov zero-one law> makes each event $\{Z\leq q\}$, $q\in\mathbb Q$, have probability zero or one. Since $Z=X$ almost surely and $X$ is finite, these probabilities form the distribution function of a point mass: there is a finite constant $c$ with $X=c$ almost surely.

Now $\{|X_n-c|>\epsilon\}$ are independent events. <Almost sure convergence> says that their infinitely-often event has probability zero. The contrapositive of the <second Borel-Cantelli lemma> gives
$$
\sum_n\mathbb P(|X_n-c|>\epsilon)<\infty.
$$
Replacing $c$ by the almost surely equal $X$ leaves each probability unchanged. Thus **complete convergence and almost sure convergence are equivalent for independent sequences**.

Let $U$ have the <uniform distribution> on $(0,1)$, set $Y=1/U$, and take $X_n=Y/n$. The variable $Y$ is finite almost surely, so $X_n\to0$ almost surely. For any $\epsilon>0$ and all sufficiently large $n$,
$$
\mathbb P(|X_n|>\epsilon)=\mathbb P(U<1/(n\epsilon))=\frac1{n\epsilon}.
$$
The <harmonic series> diverges, so
$$
\boxed{\sum_n\mathbb P(|X_n|>\epsilon)=\infty.}
$$
This dependent sequence fails <complete convergence>. All its members are scalar multiples of the same nonconstant <random variable>, which explains why the independence hypothesis cannot be dropped.

A real-valued <random variable> is a <measurable function> $Y:(\Omega,\mathcal F)\to(\mathbb R,\mathcal B(\mathbb R))$: equivalently, $\{Y\leq t\}\in\mathcal F$ for every real $t$.

For $p=1$ the asserted inequality is equality. For $p>1$, let $q=p/(p-1)$. If $\mathbb E Y^p=\infty$, the inequality is automatic. Otherwise <Hölder's inequality> on the probability space gives
$$
\mathbb E Y=\mathbb E(Y\cdot1)\leq(\mathbb E Y^p)^{1/p}(\mathbb E1^q)^{1/q}
=(\mathbb E Y^p)^{1/p}.
$$
Thus $\boxed{\|Y\|_1\leq\|Y\|_p}$, using that the total measure is one.

Write $\Delta_0=X_0$ and $\Delta_j=X_j-X_{j-1}$ for $j\geq1$. If $i<j$, then $\Delta_i$ is $\mathcal F_{j-1}$-measurable. The <martingale> property and linearity of <conditional expectation> give
$$
\mathbb E(\Delta_j\mid\mathcal F_{j-1})=0.
$$
The product $\Delta_i\Delta_j$ is integrable by <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. The pull-out property of <conditional expectation>, followed by its <tower property>, therefore gives
$$
\mathbb E(\Delta_i\Delta_j)
=\mathbb E\{\Delta_i\mathbb E(\Delta_j\mid\mathcal F_{j-1})\}=0.
$$
For unbounded $\Delta_i$, the pull-out identity follows by truncation and <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. This proves <martingale-difference orthogonality>, including orthogonality to the initial value.

Expand $X_n=\sum_{j=0}^n\Delta_j$ in the Hilbert space $L^2$. Orthogonality gives the exact identity
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E X_n^2=\mathbb E X_0^2+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mathbb E(X_{j+1}-X_j)^2.}
$$
The terms are nonnegative and $\mathbb E X_0^2<\infty$, so boundedness of the left side over $n$ is equivalent to convergence of the series on the right.

Conditional on $\mathcal F_n$, put $x=X_n$. The two possible values have conditional mean
$$
(1-x)\frac x2+x\frac{1+x}2=x.
$$
The process is adapted and bounded, hence integrable. Consequently it is a <martingale>. Since $\sup_n\mathbb E|X_n|\leq1$, the <martingale convergence theorem> gives an almost surely finite limit $X_\infty$, necessarily in $[0,1]$.

Moreover $|X_n-X_\infty|^2\leq1$ and these squared differences tend to zero almost surely. The <dominated convergence theorem> gives $\mathbb E|X_n-X_\infty|^2\to0$. Thus the <Bernoulli polarization martingale> converges **almost surely and in $L^2$**.

Conditional on $\mathcal F_n$, the increment is $-X_n/2$ with probability $1-X_n$, and $(1-X_n)/2$ with probability $X_n$. Therefore

The <martingale> is bounded in $L^2$, so the increment-square series from part (a)(ii) is finite. The identity just obtained implies $\sum_n\mathbb E[X_n(1-X_n)]<\infty$, hence $\mathbb E[X_n(1-X_n)]\to0$. By <almost sure convergence> and the <dominated convergence theorem>,
$$
\mathbb E[X_\infty(1-X_\infty)]=0.

The integrand is nonnegative because $X_\infty\in[0,1]$. It must vanish almost surely, giving $X_\infty\in\{0,1\}$.

The <martingale> has constant expectation $a$. Its $L^2$ convergence implies $L^1$ convergence by <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>, and hence $\mathbb E X_\infty=a$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(X_\infty=1)=a,\qquad\mathbb P(X_\infty=0)=1-a.}
$$
The limiting law is the <Bernoulli distribution> with parameter $a$.

<Independent and identically distributed random variables> are independent as in question 1, and all have the same probability distribution. Their <tail sigma-algebra> is
$$
\mathcal T=\bigcap_{m\geq1}\sigma(X_m,X_{m+1},\ldots).
$$
An event is a tail event when it belongs to every sigma-algebra in this intersection. The <Kolmogorov zero-one law> says $\mathbb P(E)\in\{0,1\}$ for every $E\in\mathcal T$.

Use the intended <simple symmetric random walk> $S_n=\sum_{j=1}^nX_j$ with $S_0=0$; the printed lower summation index zero refers to an undefined $X_0$. For each $m$, removing the finite initial sum changes all later positions by one finite constant. This cannot change whether the lower limit is $-\infty$ or the upper limit is $+\infty$. Thus $B_-$ and $B_+$ belong to $\mathcal T$, and
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(B_-),\mathbb P(B_+)\in\{0,1\}.}
$$

A discrete <stopping time> is a random variable $T:\Omega\to\mathbb N_0\cup\{\infty\}$ such that $\{T\leq n\}\in\mathcal F_n$ for every $n$. The convention is $\inf\varnothing=\infty$.

For the <hitting time> in question,
$$
\{T_k\leq n\}=\bigcup_{j=0}^n\{S_j=k\}.
$$
Each event in the union is in $\mathcal F_j\subseteq\mathcal F_n$ because $S_j$ is adapted. This proves **$T_k$ is a stopping time** for every positive integer $k$.

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

The <optional stopping theorem> applies to $M$ at the bounded <stopping time> $n\wedge T_k$. As $M_0=0$, it yields the valid <stopped second-moment identity for a simple symmetric random walk>
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E S_{n\wedge T_k}^2=\mathbb E(n\wedge T_k).}
$$
**The printed bound on $\mathbb E T_k$ is false.** Before hitting the positive level $k$, the walk can travel arbitrarily far downwards, so $|S_{n\wedge T_k}|\leq k$ is unavailable.

Here is a valid proof of finiteness of $T_k$. For a positive integer $L$, stop instead at $\tau=\inf\{n:S_n\in\{-L,k\}\}$. The same identity and the bound $|S_{n\wedge\tau}|\leq\max(L,k)$ give
$$
\mathbb E(n\wedge\tau)\leq\max(L,k)^2.
$$
The <monotone convergence theorem> makes $\mathbb E\tau<\infty$, so $\tau<\infty$ almost surely. Applying <optional stopping> to $S$ at $n\wedge\tau$ and then using <dominated convergence> gives $\mathbb E S_\tau=0$. Since $S_\tau\in\{-L,k\}$, this means
$$
\mathbb P(S_\tau=k)=\frac L{L+k}.
$$
Consequently $\mathbb P(T_k<\infty)\geq L/(L+k)$ for every $L$, and letting $L\to\infty$ proves $T_k<\infty$ almost surely.

In fact this is the <infinite mean first passage of a simple symmetric random walk>. If $\mathbb E T_k<\infty$, the first displayed identity would bound the second moments of $S_{n\wedge T_k}$ uniformly. Their <uniform integrability> and their almost sure convergence to $k$ would imply $\mathbb E S_{n\wedge T_k}\to k$. But the <optional stopping theorem> applied to $S$ at every bounded $n\wedge T_k$ makes each expectation zero, a contradiction. Hence the correct conclusion is
$$
\boxed{T_k<\infty\ \text{almost surely},\qquad \mathbb E T_k=\infty.}
$$

Intersecting the probability-one hitting events over all positive integers shows that the <simple symmetric random walk> reaches arbitrarily large positive levels almost surely. The hitting times of levels tending to infinity also tend to infinity, since $|S_n|\leq n$. Hence $\mathbb P(B_+)=1$, and in particular $\mathbb P(B_-\cup B_+)=1$.

Alternatively, the union conclusion, the equal probabilities from symmetry, and the <Kolmogorov zero-one law> force both probabilities to equal one. Thus almost every path has arbitrarily large positive and negative excursions. A nearest-neighbor integer path must pass through zero between a positive and a negative excursion. Such crossings occur after every finite time, giving
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(B_-)=\mathbb P(B_+)=\mathbb P(A)=1.}
$$
This is recurrence of the <simple symmetric random walk>.

Apply <Itô's formula> to $\log X$ for positive initial value $x_0$. The drift correction is $-\sigma^2/2$, so
$$
\boxed{X_t=x_0\exp\{(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2)t+\sigma W_t\}.}

$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(X_T<m)=\Phi\left(\frac{\log(m/x_0)-(\mu-\sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt T}\right).}
$$
The drift here is $\mu$, because this is a physical bankruptcy probability. The <risk-neutral measure> will instead give drift $r$ for pricing. If $\sigma=0$, the value is deterministic and the default probability is the indicator of $x_0e^{\mu T}<m$; the usual positive-volatility convention is used in the remaining formulas.

If $d_1>d_2$, buy $1/100$ of stock 1 and sell $1/100$ of stock 2. Initial cost is zero; the one-period payoff is $u_1-u_2>0$ in the up state and $d_1-d_2>0$ in the down state. This is an <arbitrage>. If terminal rather than time-one arbitrage is required, reinvest the positive receipts in either positive-price stock.

For $d_1<d_2$, define the one-period <Arrow state prices>
$$
\pi_u=\frac{d_2-d_1}{u_1d_2-u_2d_1},\qquad
\pi_d=\frac{u_1-u_2}{u_1d_2-u_2d_1}.
$$
The denominator is positive because $u_1/u_2>1>d_1/d_2$. Both state prices are strictly positive and satisfy $\pi_u u_i+\pi_d d_i=1$ for $i=1,2$. Thus every portfolio's present value equals the positive state-price-weighted value of its two future payoffs.

Use the same strictly positive pricing rule at every node of the two-period tree. A nonnegative terminal payoff that is positive in at least one state has strictly positive price by backward induction, so a zero-cost <self-financing strategy> cannot generate an arbitrage. Therefore **the market is arbitrage free when $d_1<d_2$**, despite having no initially supplied bond. This is a <two-stock binomial market without a supplied bond>.

The first stock gives $0.8+0.6q=1$, hence $q=1/3$. The second gives $0.9+0.3q=1$, confirming the same answer. Thus
$$
\boxed{(q,1-q)=(1/3,2/3).}
$$
Take the successive up/down choices independent under this measure, so both stock prices are <martingales>. The martingale transition condition applies from times zero to one and one to two; the displayed $t+1$ condition in the source should be read for these two transitions.

The <exchange option> has terminal payoffs determined by the two successive up/down moves:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
\text{path}&S_1(2)&S_2(2)&(S_1(2)-S_2(2))^+\\ \hline
uu&196&144&52\\
ud,\ du&112&108&4\\
dd&64&81&0
\end{array}
$$
The two stocks span both one-step states, so this finite market is complete. The <risk-neutral pricing> uses the independent transition probabilities $q=1/3$ and $1-q=2/3$. Since the synthetic risk-free rate is zero,
$$
V_0=\frac19\,52+2\left(\frac13\frac23\right)4+\frac49\,0
=\boxed{\frac{68}{9}}.
$$
