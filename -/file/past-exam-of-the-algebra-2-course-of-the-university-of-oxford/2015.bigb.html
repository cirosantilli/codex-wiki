= Solution
{parent=i}

If $a=ub$ with $u$ a <unit in a ring>, then $a$ lies in the <principal ideal> generated by $b$. This gives $(a)\subseteq(b)$. Since $u$ has a <multiplicative inverse>, $b=u^{-1}a$, giving the reverse inclusion. Therefore
$$
\boxed{a=ub,\ u\in R^\times\quad\Longrightarrow\quad(a)=(b).}
$$
Thus <associate elements> always generate the same <principal ideal>; the <associator ring> condition asks whether equality of these <ideals> also forces the generators to differ by a <unit>.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Suppose $R$ is an <integral domain> and $(a)=(b)$. If $a=0$, then $(b)=0$, so $b=0$ and $a=1\cdot b$.

Otherwise membership in the equal <principal ideals> supplies $r,s\in R$ with $a=rb$ and $b=sa$. Substituting gives $a=rsa$, or $(1-rs)a=0$. There are no nonzero <zero divisors> in an <integral domain>, and $a\ne0$, so $rs=1$. Hence $r$ is a <unit> with inverse $s$, and $a=rb$ makes $a$ and $b$ <associate elements>. We have proved
$$
\boxed{\text{Every integral domain is an associator ring.}}
$$

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The <ideal correspondence theorem> identifies the <ideals> of $\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$ with the <ideals> of $\mathbb Z$ containing $4\mathbb Z$. The possible positive generators divide $4$, giving exactly
$$
\boxed{\{\overline0\},\qquad
(\overline2)=\{\overline0,\overline2\},\qquad
\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z.}
$$
The <units> are $\overline1$ and $\overline3$. The generators of the three displayed <ideals> are, respectively,
$$
\{\overline0\},\qquad\{\overline2\},\qquad
\{\overline1,\overline3\}.
$$
Within the last pair, multiplication by the <unit> $\overline3$ exchanges the generators. The other two sets are singletons, whose members are <associate elements> to themselves by multiplication by $\overline1$. Thus equal <principal ideals> always have associate generators: this is an <associator ring>.

However, $\overline2\ne\overline0$ and $\overline2^2=\overline0$, so $\overline2$ is a nonzero <zero divisor>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z\text{ is an associator ring but not an integral domain}.}
$$

{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

For two factors, the source's $R_1\oplus R_2$ is the <direct product of rings> with componentwise operations. Its <principal ideals> satisfy
$$
\bigl((a_1,a_2)\bigr)=(a_1)\times(a_2),
$$
One inclusion follows by projection to the factors. For the reverse inclusion, multiplication by $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ puts $(a_1,0)$ and $(0,a_2)$ in the generated <ideal>; taking <ideal> combinations separately produces every pair in $(a_1)\times(a_2)$.

Suppose $(a_1,a_2)$ and $(b_1,b_2)$ generate the same <principal ideal>. Projecting to the factors gives $(a_i)=(b_i)$ for $i=1,2$. Since each factor is an <associator ring>, there are <units> $u_i\in R_i$ with $a_i=u_ib_i$. The pair $(u_1,u_2)$ is a <unit> of the <direct product of rings>, with inverse $(u_1^{-1},u_2^{-1})$, and
$$
(a_1,a_2)=(u_1,u_2)(b_1,b_2).
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{R_1\oplus R_2\text{ is an associator ring}.}
$$
Repeating this argument proves the same assertion for every finite <direct product of rings>.

= Solution

Put $A=R/(p^k)$. A <principal ideal domain> is a <unique factorization domain>, so every nonzero element of this <quotient ring> can be written
$$
\overline a=\overline{p}^{\,e}\overline s,
\qquad 0\leq e<k,\qquad p\nmid s.
$$
Indeed, remove the largest power of the <prime element> $p$ from a representative that is not divisible by $p^k$. Since $s$ and $p^k$ have <greatest common divisor> $1$ up to a <unit>, <Bézout's identity> in the <principal ideal domain> gives $xs+yp^k=1$. Thus $\overline s$ is a <unit> in $A$.

Consequently every nonzero <principal ideal> in $A$ is generated by one of $1,\overline p,\ldots,\overline p^{\,k-1}$. Include the zero <ideal> by assigning it exponent $e=k$. These <ideals> are distinct: their inverse images in $R$ are $(1),(p),\ldots,(p^k)$, and equality of two different powers would, by cancellation in the <integral domain>, make the nonunit $p$ a <unit>.

Now suppose $(\overline a)=(\overline b)$. If this is the zero <ideal>, both generators are zero and are <associate elements>. Otherwise their exponents agree, so write
$$
\overline a=\overline p^{\,e}\overline s,\qquad
\overline b=\overline p^{\,e}\overline t
$$
with $\overline s,\overline t$ both <units>. Then
$$
\overline a=(\overline s\,\overline t^{-1})\overline b,
$$
and the multiplier is a <unit>. Thus
$$
\boxed{R/(p^k)\text{ is an associator ring for every }k\geq1.}
$$
The exponent detects the <principal ideal>, while its remaining factor is invertible; this is why <zero divisors> cause no obstruction here.

= Solution
{parent=d}

By the <first isomorphism theorem for rings>, the given surjection identifies $S$ with $R/\ker\phi$. Since $R$ is a <principal ideal domain>, write $\ker\phi=(a)$.

If $a=0$, the <quotient ring> is $R$, an <integral domain>, so part (a)(ii) applies. If $a$ is a <unit>, the <quotient ring> is the <zero ring>; its unique element is associate to itself by multiplication by its identity, so it too is an <associator ring>.

In the remaining case, <unique factorization> gives
$$
a=\varepsilon p_1^{k_1}\cdots p_r^{k_r},
$$
where $\varepsilon$ is a <unit>, the $p_i$ are pairwise nonassociate <prime elements>, and $k_i\geq1$. The <principal ideals> $(p_i^{k_i})$ are pairwise comaximal: different prime factors have <greatest common divisor> $1$, and <Bézout's identity> gives a linear combination equal to $1$. The <Chinese remainder theorem> therefore gives a <ring isomorphism>
$$
R/(a)\ \cong\ \prod_{i=1}^r R/(p_i^{k_i}).
$$
Every factor is an <associator ring> by part (c), and their finite <direct product of rings> is an <associator ring> by part (b). A <ring isomorphism> preserves <units> and <principal ideals>, so the property transfers to $S$. Hence
$$
\boxed{S\text{ is an associator ring}.}
$$

A <Euclidean domain> is a <principal ideal domain>. The <structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain> therefore gives, for every <finitely generated module> $M$ over $R$,
$$
M\cong R^r\oplus R/(d_1)\oplus\cdots\oplus R/(d_s),
$$
where the $d_i$ are nonzero nonunits and $d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_s$. The factors $d_i$ are unique up to multiplication by <units>; $r$ is uniquely determined.

The <rank of a module> is this integer $r$. Equivalently, with $K$ the <field of fractions> of $R$,
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{rank}_R M=\dim_K(K\otimes_R M)=r.}
$$
The <tensor product of modules> kills each displayed torsion summand after extending scalars to $K$, while sending $R^r$ to $K^r$, explaining the equality.

If $M$ is a <torsion-free module>, no summand $R/(d_i)$ can occur: its nonzero element $1+(d_i)$ is killed by the nonzero scalar $d_i$. Hence $s=0$ and
$$
\boxed{M\cong R^r,\quad\text{so $M$ is a free module}.}
$$
This also covers $M=0$, with $r=0$.

Let $x,y\in\mathbb Q$. Choose a positive <integer> $D$ clearing their denominators and write $x=m/D$, $y=n/D$ with $m,n\in\mathbb Z$. If $x$ and $y$ are not both zero, then
$$
nx-my=0
$$
is a relation with <integer> coefficients not both zero. If both vanish, use $1\cdot x+0\cdot y=0$. Thus every pair is <linearly dependent> over $\mathbb Z$.

A <submodule> $M\subseteq\mathbb Q$ is a <torsion-free module>: if $kz=0$ for a nonzero <integer> $k$ and a <rational number> $z$, then $z=0$. If $M$ is also a <finitely generated module>, part (a), applied to the <Euclidean domain> $\mathbb Z$, makes it a <free module>. Its <rank of a module>[rank] cannot exceed one, since two different members of a <basis of a module> would be linearly independent, contradicting the relation just proved. Since $M\ne0$, its rank cannot be zero. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb Z}M=1,\qquad M\cong\mathbb Z.}
$$
The denominator-and-<greatest common divisor> construction in part (c) gives an explicit version of this result for a <finitely generated subgroup of the rational additive group>.

Using the common denominator $70$, the three given generators become
$$
\frac25=\frac{28}{70},\qquad
\frac37=\frac{30}{70},\qquad
\frac12=\frac{35}{70}.
$$
Thus their <submodule> is contained in $(1/70)\mathbb Z$. For the reverse inclusion, the following explicit <integer> linear combination supplies the missing generator:
$$
2\cdot28-3\cdot30+35=1,
$$
so
$$
2\cdot\frac25-3\cdot\frac37+\frac12=\frac1{70}.
$$
Therefore $1/70$ belongs to the generated <submodule>, and all its <integer> multiples belong as well. We have established equality in both directions:
$$
M=\frac1{70}\mathbb Z.
$$
Finally, $k/70=0$ for an <integer> $k$ forces $k=0$, so the single generator is linearly independent. Hence the required <basis of a module> is
$$
\boxed{\left\{\frac1{70}\right\}.}
$$

Suppose a finite set of <rational numbers> $q_1,\ldots,q_m$ generated $\mathbb Q$ as a <module> over $\mathbb Z$. A positive common denominator $D$ makes every $Dq_i$ an <integer>. For every <integer> linear combination, therefore,
$$
D\sum_i n_iq_i\in\mathbb Z.
$$
The entire generated <submodule> is contained in $(1/D)\mathbb Z$. But $1/(2D)$ is a <rational number> outside that set: $1/(2D)=k/D$ would require the noninteger $k=1/2$. This contradicts generation of all of $\mathbb Q$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\mathbb Q\text{ is not finitely generated as a }\mathbb Z\text{-module}.}
$$
Each finite set of generators imposes a common denominator; the full <rational number> system has no such bound.

= Solution

For a nonzero <polynomial> $f=\sum_i a_it^i\in\mathbb Z[t]$, its <polynomial content> is the positive <greatest common divisor> of its coefficients:
$$
c(f)=\gcd(a_0,a_1,\ldots)>0.
$$
For nonzero $g\in\mathbb Q[t]$, choose a positive <integer> $n$ with $ng\in\mathbb Z[t]$ and define its <rational polynomial content> by
$$
\boxed{c(g)=\frac{c(ng)}n.}
$$
To check independence of the denominator, write $g=f/n=h/m$ with $f,h\in\mathbb Z[t]$ and $n,m>0$. Then $mf=nh$, while multiplication of all coefficients by a positive <integer> gives
$$
mc(f)=c(mf)=c(nh)=nc(h).
$$
Dividing by $mn$ proves $c(f)/n=c(h)/m$, as required.

For multiplication, write $g_i=f_i/n_i$. The integer-coefficient content identity permitted in the source gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
c(g_1g_2)
&=\frac{c(f_1f_2)}{n_1n_2}
=\frac{c(f_1)c(f_2)}{n_1n_2}\\
&=c(g_1)c(g_2).
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{c(g_1g_2)=c(g_1)c(g_2).}
$$
We will also use the normalization $g/c(g)\in\mathbb Z[t]$, with content one: if $g=f/n$, then $g/c(g)=f/c(f)$. It is a <primitive polynomial>. For a nonzero rational constant $r$, the same definition gives $c(r)=|r|$.

= Solution

The set $\widetilde P$ contains zero. It is closed under negatives because $-f\in P$ whenever $f\in P$. If $f,g\in P$ and $n,m>0$, then
$$
\frac fn+\frac gm=\frac{mf+ng}{nm}\in\widetilde P,
$$
since $P$ is an <ideal> of $\mathbb Z[t]$. Also, any $q\in\mathbb Q[t]$ can be written $q=h/d$ with $h\in\mathbb Z[t]$ and $d>0$, giving
$$
q\frac fn=\frac{hf}{dn}\in\widetilde P.
$$
This proves that $\widetilde P$ is an <ideal> of $\mathbb Q[t]$.

Clearly $P\subseteq\widetilde P\cap\mathbb Z[t]$, by taking denominator one. Conversely, if $h\in\widetilde P\cap\mathbb Z[t]$, then $nh\in P$ for some positive <integer> $n$. The condition $P\cap\mathbb Z=\{0\}$ implies $n\notin P$. Since $P$ is a <prime ideal>, $nh\in P$ forces $h\in P$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\widetilde P\cap\mathbb Z[t]=P.}
$$
In particular, $\widetilde P$ is nonzero because it contains the nonzero <ideal> $P$, and it is proper: otherwise its intersection with $\mathbb Z[t]$ would contain $1$. This is the concrete <extension of an ideal> and <contraction of an ideal> calculation for the <localization> that inverts nonzero <integers>.

= Solution

The nonzero <ideal> $\widetilde P$ is principal because $\mathbb Q[t]$ is a <principal ideal domain>. Choose a nonzero generator $h\in\mathbb Q[t]$. Part (a) gives
$$
f=\frac{h}{c(h)}\in\mathbb Z[t],\qquad c(f)=1.
$$
The nonzero rational scalar $c(h)$ is a <unit> in $\mathbb Q[t]$, so this rescaling does not change the generated <principal ideal>:
$$
\boxed{\widetilde P=(f)_{\mathbb Q[t]},\qquad c(f)=1.}
$$
Part (b) now places $f$ in $P$, since $f\in\widetilde P\cap\mathbb Z[t]$. Thus $(f)_{\mathbb Z[t]}\subseteq P$.

For the reverse inclusion, let $g\in P$ be nonzero. The displayed equality supplies $q\in\mathbb Q[t]$ with $g=fq$. Multiplicativity of <rational polynomial content> gives
$$
c(g)=c(f)c(q)=c(q).
$$
Since $g$ has <integer> coefficients, $c(g)$ is a positive <integer>. The normalization from part (a) gives $q/c(q)\in\mathbb Z[t]$, so multiplying it by the integer $c(q)=c(g)$ proves $q\in\mathbb Z[t]$. Consequently $g\in(f)_{\mathbb Z[t]}$. The zero <polynomial> belongs as well, proving
$$
\boxed{P=(f)_{\mathbb Z[t]}.}
$$
This is the divisibility descent in <Gauss lemma for polynomials>: dividing an integer-coefficient <polynomial> by a <primitive polynomial> cannot introduce a denominator into an exact quotient. Here it yields the description of <prime ideals of the integer polynomial ring above zero>. The generator has positive <polynomial degree>, since a primitive constant would be a <unit> and would put $1$ in $P$.

Take $I=(2,t)\subseteq\mathbb Z[t]$. The <ring homomorphism>
$$
\theta:\mathbb Z[t]\longrightarrow\mathbb F_2,
\qquad \theta(g)=g(0)\pmod2
$$
is surjective. Its <kernel of a ring homomorphism> is exactly $I$: a <polynomial> maps to zero precisely when its constant coefficient is even, in which case it has the form $2a+th(t)$ with $a\in\mathbb Z$ and $h\in\mathbb Z[t]$. By the <first isomorphism theorem for rings>,
$$
\mathbb Z[t]/I\cong\mathbb F_2.
$$
This <quotient ring> is a <field>, so $I$ is a <maximal ideal> and in particular a <prime ideal>.

Suppose $I=(h)$ were a <principal ideal>. Because $2\in I$, the nonzero <polynomial> $h$ divides the constant $2$, forcing its <polynomial degree> to be zero: $h=d$ for a nonzero <integer> $d$. But $t\in I$ also makes $d$ divide the coefficient $1$ of $t$, so $d=\pm1$. This would give $I=\mathbb Z[t]$, contradicting the properness of the <kernel of a ring homomorphism> $\ker\theta$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{(2,t)\text{ is a prime ideal of }\mathbb Z[t]
\text{ that is not principal}.}
$$
There is no conflict with part (c): $I\cap\mathbb Z=2\mathbb Z\ne\{0\}$. The zero-intersection hypothesis is exactly what excludes this example.
