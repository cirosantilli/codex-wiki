= Solution
{parent=a}

A <prime ideal> is a proper <ideal> $I\neq R$ such that $ab\in I$ implies $a\in I$ or $b\in I$, for every $a,b\in R$.

Suppose first that $I$ is prime. Since $I$ is proper, $1+I\neq0+I$ in the <quotient ring>. If $(a+I)(b+I)=0+I$, then $ab\in I$, so primeness gives $a+I=0+I$ or $b+I=0+I$. Thus the commutative <quotient ring> has a nonzero identity and no nonzero <zero divisors>, hence is an <integral domain>.

Conversely, if $R/I$ is an <integral domain>, its identity is nonzero, so $I\neq R$. If $ab\in I$, then $(a+I)(b+I)=0$ in $R/I$. Absence of nonzero <zero divisors> implies $a+I=0$ or $b+I=0$, namely $a\in I$ or $b\in I$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{I\text{ is prime}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ R/I\text{ is an integral domain}.}
$$
The properness condition matters: taking $I=R$ would produce the <zero ring>, which is not an <integral domain>.

= Solution

Suppose, for a contradiction, that the monic <irreducible polynomials> in the <polynomial ring> $k[t]$ form a finite list $p_1,\ldots,p_r$. The list is nonempty because $t$ itself is monic and irreducible. Put
$$
F(t)=p_1(t)\cdots p_r(t)+1.
$$
This has positive <degree>, so it is a nonzero <nonunit>. Every such <polynomial> over a <field> has an <irreducible polynomial> as a factor: repeatedly factor a reducible polynomial, with the positive degrees strictly decreasing until an irreducible factor is reached. Dividing that factor by its <leading coefficient> makes it monic; call it $q$.

By the assumed completeness of the list, $q=p_j$ for some $j$. But then $p_j$ divides both $F$ and $p_1\cdots p_r$, hence their difference $1$. A positive-degree <polynomial> cannot divide $1$. This contradiction proves **there are infinitely many monic irreducible polynomials over every field**. This is the polynomial version of <Euclid's theorem>; it does not require the field itself to be infinite.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Take a reduced fraction $a/b\in R$, with $b\neq0$. Since $\gcd(a,b)=1$, <Bézout's identity> supplies integers $u,v$ with $ua+vb=1$. As the <subring> $R$ contains all <integers>,
$$
\boxed{\frac1b=u\frac ab+v\in R.}
$$
Thus a denominator appearing in a reduced fraction in $R$ is automatically a <unit> of $R$.

Now let $I\cap\mathbb Z=d\mathbb Z$. Because $d\in I$, the <principal ideal> $dR$ is contained in $I$. For the reverse inclusion, take $x\in I$ and write $x=a/b$ in lowest terms. Multiplying by $b\in R$ gives $a=bx\in I\cap\mathbb Z=d\mathbb Z$, so $a=dc$ for some integer $c$. The preceding result gives $1/b\in R$, whence
$$
x=\frac ab=d\left(\frac cb\right)\in dR.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{I=dR.}
$$
This includes $d=0$: the same argument forces every $x\in I$ to be zero. This is the <ideals of subrings of the rational numbers> property; every such <ring> is a <principal ideal domain>.

= Solution
{parent=d}

In this <localization of a ring>, a nonzero fraction $a/b$ with odd $b$ is a <unit> exactly when $a$ is odd: its reciprocal then has odd denominator; if $a$ is even, an inverse would equate an even integer with an odd one after clearing denominators. Consequently every nonzero element has a unique form
$$
x=2^r u,\qquad r\geq0,\quad u\in R^\times.
$$
Here $r$ is the exponent of $2$ in the numerator of the reduced fraction, its nonnegative <2-adic valuation>.

Every nonzero <ideal> has the form $dR$ by part (c). Write the nonzero integer $d=2^r v$ with $v$ odd; since $v$ is a <unit>, $dR=2^rR$. Hence the nonzero proper <ideals> are exactly
$$
\boxed{2^rR=\{a/b\in R:2^r\mid a\},\qquad r=1,2,3,\ldots.}
$$
The <zero ideal> and $R=2^0R$ complete the full list. These ideals are distinct: $2^r$ does not belong to $2^{r+1}R$, since $1/2\notin R$.

The reduction map $a/b\mapsto a\bmod2$ is a well-defined surjective <ring homomorphism> $R\to\mathbb F_2$ with <kernel of a ring homomorphism> $2R$; odd denominators reduce to $1$. Thus $R/2R\cong\mathbb F_2$ is a <field>, so $2R$ is a <prime ideal> and $2$ is a <prime element>. Every nonzero <nonunit> is associated to $2^r$ with $r\geq1$. If $r\geq2$, the factorization $2^r=2\cdot2^{r-1}$ is into two <nonunits>, so it is not an <irreducible element> and therefore not a <prime element>. We conclude
$$
\boxed{\text{The sole prime element up to units is }2.}
$$
The failed step in the argument of part (b) is the guarantee that the product plus one is a nonunit and hence has an irreducible factor. Here every <prime element> is an even fraction, so a product of <prime elements> plus $1$ is an odd fraction, therefore a <unit>. Already $2+1=3$ has no prime divisor in this ring. In $k[t]$, positive degree prevented that failure.

= Solution
{parent=a}

For a nonzero integer-coefficient <polynomial> $F$, its <polynomial content> $c(F)$ is the positive <greatest common divisor> of all its coefficients; one may set $c(0)=0$. A <primitive polynomial> has content one.

For $f\in\mathbb Q[t]\setminus\{0\}$, choose a positive integer $m$ clearing its denominators, so $F=mf\in\mathbb Z[t]$. Put $d=c(F)>0$. Then
$$
f=\frac dm\frac Fd,
$$
where $f_1=F/d$ is an integer-coefficient <primitive polynomial>. This proves existence with $\alpha=d/m>0$.

For uniqueness, suppose $\alpha f_1=\beta g_1$, where both integer-coefficient polynomials are primitive and $\alpha,\beta>0$. Write $\alpha/\beta=a/b$ in lowest terms with $a,b$ positive. Then $g_1=(a/b)f_1$ has integer coefficients. Coprimality forces $b$ to divide every coefficient of $f_1$, hence $b=1$. Now $g_1=af_1$ has content $a$, so $a=1$. Therefore $\alpha=\beta$ and $f_1=g_1$.

Define the <rational polynomial content> by this unique positive multiplier. If $f=\alpha f_1$ and $g=\beta g_1$, <Gauss lemma for polynomials> says that $f_1g_1$ is primitive. Thus
$$
fg=(\alpha\beta)(f_1g_1),\qquad
\boxed{c(fg)=c(f)c(g).}
$$
The uniqueness argument also shows that the construction is independent of the chosen denominator-clearing integer $m$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Write a rational <root of a polynomial> as $\gamma=a/b$ in lowest terms with $b>0$. Substituting into the monic integer-coefficient <polynomial> and multiplying by $b^d$ gives
$$
a^d+c_{d-1}a^{d-1}b+c_{d-2}a^{d-2}b^2+\cdots+c_0b^d=0.
$$
Every term other than $a^d$ is divisible by $b$, so $b\mid a^d$. But $\gcd(a,b)=1$ implies $\gcd(a^d,b)=1$, and hence $b=1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\gamma\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
This is the monic case of the <rational root theorem>. The <monic polynomial> assumption is essential because it removes any leading coefficient that could supply prime factors of the denominator.

= Solution

Use the reduction criterion: a primitive integer-coefficient <polynomial> whose leading coefficient remains nonzero modulo a <prime number> $p$ is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$ if its reduction is irreducible over $\mathbb F_p$. This follows from <Gauss lemma for polynomials>, since a nontrivial factorization over the integers would reduce to a nontrivial factorization of the same degrees. For a monic polynomial the factors may be taken monic, so neither degree can drop.

Modulo $2$ the polynomial is $\overline f=t^4+t+1$. Its values at $0$ and $1$ are both $1$, so it has no <linear polynomial> factor. A reducible <quartic polynomial> without a <linear polynomial> factor must factor into two irreducible <quadratic polynomials>. The only monic irreducible <quadratic polynomial> over $\mathbb F_2$ is $q=t^2+t+1$: among monic <quadratic polynomials> a nonzero constant is necessary, and $t^2+1$ has a root, while $q$ has none.

But $t^2\equiv t+1\pmod q$, so $t^3\equiv1$ and $t^4\equiv t$. Consequently
$$
\overline f\equiv t+t+1=1\pmod q.
$$
Thus $q$ does not divide $\overline f$, excluding the remaining possible factorization. We obtain
$$
\boxed{t^4+t+1\text{ is irreducible over }\mathbb Q.}
$$

= Solution

<Eisenstein's criterion> states that an integer-coefficient <polynomial> is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$ if some <prime number> divides every nonleading coefficient, does not divide the leading coefficient, and its square does not divide the constant coefficient. Equivalently apply this to the primitive part when phrased for primitive polynomials.

Take $p=2$. Every nonleading coefficient of $t^5+4t+2$ is divisible by $2$ (including the zero coefficients), its <leading coefficient> is $1$, and its constant coefficient $2$ is not divisible by $4$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{t^5+4t+2\text{ is irreducible over }\mathbb Q.}
$$

= Solution

A <cubic polynomial> over a <field> is reducible if and only if it has a root in that field: a nontrivial factorization of degree three has a <linear polynomial> factor, and conversely a root supplies one by the <factor theorem>.

The <rational root theorem>, or part (b), shows that a rational root of this monic polynomial must be an integer. Such an integer must divide the constant coefficient $4$, so the only candidates are $\pm1,\pm2,\pm4$. Direct evaluation gives
$$
\begin{array}{c|rrrrrr}
a&1&-1&2&-2&4&-4\\\hline
h(a)&9&-1&20&-12&84&-76
\end{array}
$$
None vanishes. Hence there is no rational root, proving
$$
\boxed{t^3+4t+4\text{ is irreducible over }\mathbb Q.}
$$
The same no-root argument alone would not decide a <quartic polynomial>, which could factor as two <quadratic polynomials>; its sufficiency here uses degree three.

= Solution

The <structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain> applies to a <Euclidean domain> because every <Euclidean domain> is a <principal ideal domain>. In invariant-factor form, every <finitely generated module> $M$ has a decomposition
$$
\boxed{M\cong R^r\oplus R/(d_1)\oplus\cdots\oplus R/(d_s),\qquad
 d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_s,}
$$
where $r\geq0$, and each $d_i$ is a nonzero <nonunit>. The <rank of a module> $r$ is unique and the <invariant factors of a finitely generated module> $d_i$ are unique up to multiplication by units. The zero module is allowed by taking $r=s=0$.

Every <abelian group> is a <module> over $\mathbb Z$, and a <finite abelian group> is finitely generated. Since $\mathbb Z$ is a <Euclidean domain>, the theorem applies. A nonzero <free module> summand would be infinite, so a finite group has $r=0$. Choosing positive integer representatives gives the finite abelian classification
$$
\boxed{A\cong\mathbb Z/d_1\mathbb Z\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb Z/d_s\mathbb Z,
\qquad 2\leq d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_s.}
$$
The ordered integers $d_i$ are uniquely determined. Splitting each $d_i$ into prime powers using the <Chinese remainder theorem> gives the equivalent elementary-divisor form as a direct sum of cyclic prime-power groups.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Use the <Chinese remainder theorem> to separate relatively prime factors:
$$
\mathbb Z/60\mathbb Z\cong\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z\oplus\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z\oplus\mathbb Z/5\mathbb Z,
\qquad
\mathbb Z/21\mathbb Z\cong\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z\oplus\mathbb Z/7\mathbb Z.
$$
These five cyclic prime-power summands are the elementary-divisor form. Leave one $\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z$ summand aside and combine the factors of orders $4,3,5,7$, which are pairwise coprime. Their <direct sum of modules> is cyclic of order $420$. The invariant-factor canonical form is therefore
$$
\boxed{A\cong\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z\oplus\mathbb Z/420\mathbb Z,\qquad3\mid420.}
$$
Its <group order> is $3\cdot420=1260=60\cdot21$, and its <exponent of a finite group> is $420=\operatorname{lcm}(60,21)$, providing independent checks on the decomposition. Since its exponent is smaller than its order, $A$ itself is not cyclic.

= Solution
{parent=c}

We prove the <unique-subgroup criterion for a finite abelian group to be cyclic> using the invariant-factor classification from part (a). If $A$ is nontrivial, write
$$
A\cong C_{d_1}\oplus\cdots\oplus C_{d_s},\qquad 2\leq d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_s,
$$
where $C_m$ denotes a <cyclic group> of order $m$. If $s\geq2$, choose a <prime number> $p$ dividing $d_1$; then $p$ also divides $d_2$. In the first cyclic factor, the element represented by $d_1/p$ has <order of a group element> $p$, and the corresponding element in the second factor has order $p$ as well.

The two elements supported in their separate coordinates generate distinct <subgroups> of order $p$: one has zero second coordinate, and the other has zero first coordinate. Both are subgroups of $A$, and $p\mid|A|$. This contradicts the assumed uniqueness for that divisor. Hence $s\leq1$, and
$$
\boxed{A\text{ is cyclic}.}
$$
If $A$ is the trivial group, it is already cyclic, generated by its identity. Thus the argument covers every finite order.

= Solution

The <multiplicative group of a field> is abelian, so its finite <subgroup> $G$ is a <finite abelian group>. Let $d\mid|G|$, and suppose $H$ is a subgroup of order $d$. By <Lagrange's theorem>, the order of each $h\in H$ divides $d$, hence $h^d=1$. Thus $H$ is contained in the set of roots in $k$ of $t^d-1$.

This nonzero <polynomial> has degree $d$, so it has at most $d$ roots. Since the $d$ distinct elements of $H$ are already roots, they comprise the whole root set. Any second subgroup $K$ of order $d$ would also consist of $d$ roots of the same polynomial, forcing $K=H$. Therefore there is at most one <subgroup> of each order dividing $|G|$.

Part (c) now applies to give
$$
\boxed{G\text{ is cyclic}.}
$$
The root bound remains valid in positive <characteristic>, even if the formal <derivative> of $t^d-1$ vanishes: the argument counts distinct roots, so no separability assumption is needed.
