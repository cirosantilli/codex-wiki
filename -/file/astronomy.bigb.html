= Astronomy
{wiki}

Astronomy studies celestial objects and phenomena, including <stars>, <planets> and <galaxies>. <Astrometry> measures their <positions> and motion; <astrophysics> uses <physics> to explain their properties and behaviour.

Much of <astronomy> begins with incoming <light>. An image tells us which direction the <light> arrived from, rather than directly providing the source's three-dimensional <position>. Two <stars> close together on the sky may be very far apart in space. The <celestial sphere> organizes these viewing directions; locating an object on it is a different task from measuring how far away that object is. Repeated observations turn this limitation into an opportunity: the changing direction of <light> contains evidence about both <Euclidean distance> and motion.

The particularly satisfying combination is <stellar parallax> with <proper motion>. The first supplies a geometric <Euclidean distance>, while the second measures how rapidly the viewing direction changes after accounting for the observer's annual motion. Together they give a transverse <speed>, connecting tiny <angles> on an image to kilometres travelled each second. This bridge from a flat sky map to moving <stars> is one reason <astrometry> matters to the study of the <Milky Way>.

= Line-of-sight projection
{parent=Astronomy}

The map from a three-dimensional astronomical distribution to its observed sky position or integrated surface signal. For a scalar volume emissivity $j(\mathbf x)$ in simple transparent geometry, <surface brightness> is proportional to $\int j\,dl$ along the ray. Reconstructing the spatial distribution requires additional geometry or symmetry assumptions; a projected surface distribution is not a unique three-dimensional <density>.

= Astrometry
{parent=Astronomy}
{wiki}

Astrometry measures the <positions> and motions of celestial objects. Repeated <position> measurements can distinguish <stellar parallax>, caused by the observer's motion, from <proper motion> of the source across the sky.

An <astrometry> measurement needs a date and a <reference frame>, as well as an <angle>. In an <equatorial coordinate system>, <right ascension> and <declination> label two viewing directions. A catalogue's reference date states when its quoted <position> applies; comparing entries at different dates without accounting for <proper motion> can make the same <star> appear misplaced. The observational problem is to infer a consistent history from successive incoming <light> directions, while accounting for the observer's changing <position> and <measurement error>.

To see the separation clearly, consider just one angular coordinate $\theta$, with $t$ the numerical elapsed <time> in years. Assume a simplified <circular orbit> of the <Earth>, a fixed source <Euclidean distance>, constant <proper motion> in this coordinate and no other <accelerations>. Choose the direction and the starting date so that the annual <stellar parallax> projection is the <cosine> term
$$
\theta(t)=\theta_0+\mu t+p\cos(2\pi t).
$$
Here $\theta_0$ is the coordinate without the annual <stellar parallax> offset at the reference date; the observed $\theta(0)$ is $\theta_0+p$. The <proper motion> coefficient $\mu$ is quoted in <arcseconds> per year, and $p$ is the annual <stellar parallax> in <arcseconds>. This is an illustrative choice of a full-<amplitude> projection. General sky directions have different projected <amplitudes> and <phases> in their two coordinates; their apparent paths need not be <circles>. Real <astrometry> uses the observer's calculated <position> rather than imposing this single <cosine>. https://gea.esac.esa.int/archive/documentation/GDR3/Data_processing/chap_cu3ast/sec_cu3ast_cali/ssec_cu3ast_cali_source.html[ESA Gaia DR3: Astrometric source model]{ref}.

Set $\theta_0=0$, $\mu=100\,\mathrm{mas\,yr^{-1}}$ and $p=50\,\mathrm{mas}$, where $1\,\mathrm{mas}$ is one thousandth of an <arcsecond>. The model gives $\theta(0)=50$, $\theta(0.5)=0$ and $\theta(1)=150\,\mathrm{mas}$. Same-season observations one year apart differ by $100\,\mathrm{mas}$ because the annual <stellar parallax> contribution repeats. Subtracting that fitted straight trend leaves an annual signal between $-50$ and $+50\,\mathrm{mas}$. The existing <stellar parallax> distance relation then gives $20$ <parsecs>.

\Image[proper-motion-parallax-separation.png]
{title=Separating a 100-mas-per-year proper-motion trend from a 50-mas annual parallax projection}

Observing only at the same annual <phase> would recover the <proper motion> trend but leave $p$ indistinguishable from a constant offset. Sampling several seasons as well as several years separates the repeating signal from the accumulating <displacement>. The two signatures answer different questions using the same measurements.

= Orbital parallax

= Proper motion
{parent=Astrometry}
{title2=$\mu$}
{wiki}

Proper motion describes the change in a celestial object's direction on the sky due to its motion relative to the observer. Its <angular velocity> differs from the annual <stellar parallax> signal caused by the observer's orbit.

Thus <proper motion> is an <angular velocity>, rather than a <speed> in kilometres per second. It describes the relative transverse motion of source and observer in a specified <reference frame>. A <star> moving towards us can have little <proper motion>, while a nearer <star> can show larger <proper motion> than a faster but more distant one. In the example above, the observed coordinate actually decreases over the first half-year, although $\mu$ is positive: the annual <stellar parallax> excursion temporarily outweighs the steady drift. A short observed <displacement> alone therefore need not estimate <proper motion> well.

Real catalogues give two <proper motion> components. For <right ascension> $\alpha$ and <declination> $\delta$, the <angular velocities> in perpendicular directions on the local sky are
$$
\mu_{\alpha *}=\cos\delta\,\frac{d\alpha}{dt},\qquad
\mu_\delta=\frac{d\delta}{dt},\qquad
\mu_{\rm tot}=\sqrt{\mu_{\alpha *}^2+\mu_\delta^2}.
$$
The <cosine> factor accounts for the shorter east-west arc associated with a given <right ascension> increment away from the <celestial equator>. ESA's Gaia DR3 fields \c[pmra] and \c[pmdec] already contain these two components; \c[pm] is their total <proper motion>. Do not multiply \c[pmra] by another <cosine>. Components of $60$ and $80\,\mathrm{mas\,yr^{-1}}$, for example, give total <proper motion> $100\,\mathrm{mas\,yr^{-1}}$. https://gea.esac.esa.int/archive/documentation/GDR3/Gaia_archive/chap_datamodel/sec_dm_main_source_catalogue/ssec_dm_gaia_source.html[ESA Gaia DR3: Proper-motion columns and conventions]{ref}.

For nearby <stars> in the ordinary nonrelativistic approximation, <Euclidean distance> $d$ converts total <proper motion> into transverse <speed> $v_\perp$:
$$
\frac{v_\perp}{1\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}}
=4.74047\,\frac{\mu_{\rm tot}}{1\,\mathrm{arcsec\,yr^{-1}}}\,\frac{d}{1\,\mathrm{pc}}.
$$
The <small-angle approximation> gives transverse <speed> as <Euclidean distance> times <angular velocity> in <radians> per unit <time>. With <arcseconds> and <parsecs>, the <parsec> convention turns their product into <astronomical units> per year. One <astronomical unit> per Julian year of $365.25$ days is approximately $4.74047\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. The coefficient is a unit conversion, rather than a property of a particular <star>.

Taking the model's $100\,\mathrm{mas\,yr^{-1}}=0.100\,\mathrm{arcsec\,yr^{-1}}$ as the total <proper motion>, with the other component zero, gives $v_\perp=4.74047\times0.100\times20\simeq9.48\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. At the same total <proper motion>, <Euclidean distances> of $10$, $20$ and $100$ <parsecs> instead give transverse <speeds> of $4.74$, $9.48$ and $47.40\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, respectively. Their <stellar parallax> values are $100$, $50$ and $10\,\mathrm{mas}$. The same annual angular <displacement> can therefore describe quite different journeys through space.

The transverse <speed> still omits the line-of-sight <velocity>, which can be measured through the <Doppler effect> in <spectral lines>. Combining these measurements permits a three-dimensional <velocity> estimate in a consistent <reference frame>. https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Gaia/How_does_Gaia_study_the_Milky_Way[ESA: Combining proper motion, distance and radial velocity]{ref}.

Historically, <Edmond Halley>'s 1718 paper compared contemporary and ancient stellar directions and argued that some supposedly fixed <stars> had moved. Its title, "Considerations on the change of the latitudes of some of the principal fixt stars", captures the conceptual surprise: long-separated measurements could reveal motion invisible in an ordinary night's watching. Modern <astrometry> retains that insight, using repeated precise measurements to turn the apparently static sky into a record of changing <positions>. https://archive.org/details/paper-doi-10_1098_rstl_1717_0025[Halley: Original Royal Society paper, pages 736-738]{ref}. https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Gaia/How_does_Gaia_study_the_Milky_Way[ESA: Halley's 1718 comparison]{ref}.
