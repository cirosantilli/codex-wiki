= Signal transduction
{wiki}

<Signal transduction> converts a detected signal into a change inside a <biological cell>. For chemical signals, distinguish reception by a <receptor>, intracellular <signal transduction>, and the response: altered <gene expression>, <enzyme> activity or <ion channel> opening, for example. A <cell-surface receptor> can relay a <ligand>'s presence across a <cell membrane> without the <ligand> entering the <cytoplasm>. Thus a <hydrophilic> signal need not cross the membrane to influence the interior. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/9-2-propagation-of-the-signal[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 9.2: intracellular signal propagation].

For a computed example of possible <signal amplification>, suppose one activated <receptor> activates five relay <proteins>, and each relay activates twenty target <proteins> during a chosen interval. If the targets are distinct and available, $1\times5\times20=100$ targets are activated. Those assumed counts illustrate multiplication, not a measured pathway or a universal gain. Actual <signal transduction> can branch, saturate or inhibit activity. Changes in <gene expression> and changes in existing <enzyme> activity are different responses. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/9-3-response-to-the-signal[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 9.3: cellular responses].

<Signal amplification>, <signaling feedback> and <signaling adaptation> are possible properties of <signal transduction>, rather than requirements of every cascade. Intracellular relays can use <proteins> or <second messengers>; different <biological cells> can interpret the same <ligand> differently because they contain different <receptors> and relay machinery. The useful and striking idea is that recognition and response are separable: an extracellular chemical message can control many internal tasks without itself performing those tasks. See <Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others/General Principles of Cell Communication>.

An original experimental route to this separation appears in T. W. Rall and Earl W. Sutherland's 1958 work on <cAMP>. They described a heat-stable factor formed from <ATP> in separated <biological cell> preparations; it affected <enzyme> activity in other preparations where the initiating chemical signals had no direct effect. This helped distinguish an initial signal from a molecule carrying its effect to another target. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0021-9258(19)77422-5[Rall and Sutherland, Formation of a cyclic adenine ribonucleotide by tissue particles, Journal of Biological Chemistry 232 (1958), pp. 1065–1076, introduction]{ref}

= Calcium signalling
{synonym}

Regulation by changes in free intracellular <calcium ion> concentration. <Calcium signatures> are interpreted through calcium-binding sensors and downstream targets; calcium concentration is not itself the final response.

= Calcium-induced calcium release
{parent=Calcium signaling}

<Calcium-induced calcium release> is activation of release from an intracellular <calcium> store by cytoplasmic <calcium> itself. A two-pool model can represent the release <flux> as a product of <Hill function>[saturating] <functions> of store and cytoplasmic <concentrations>. Together with uptake, leakage and external <calcium> balance, this <positive feedback> can generate excitability or sustained <oscillation>; the existence of <oscillation> still depends on the full dynamical parameters.

= Two-pool calcium model
{parent=Calcium signaling}
{title2=$\dot c=r-kc-\dot c_s$}

A <two-pool calcium model> couples cytosolic <calcium> and an intracellular store. With compatible reference volumes, uptake $J_+$ moves <calcium> into the store, release $J_-$ and leakage $k_sc_s$ return it, and $\dot c_s=J_+-J_--k_sc_s$. External supply $r$ and export $kc$ give $d(c+c_s)/dt=r-kc$. Thus an <equilibrium> has $c=r/k$ independently of the internal exchange law. Distinct physical reference volumes require their appropriate volume factors before adding the balances.

= Hopf thresholds in a two-pool calcium model
{c}
{parent=Two-pool calcium model}
{title2=$f(\mu,v)=0,\quad f_v-\gamma f_u=\epsilon$}

The unique <equilibrium> has $u=\mu$ and $f=0$. Its <determinant> is $-f_v/\epsilon>0$ and <matrix trace>[trace] is $-1+(f_v-\gamma f_u)/\epsilon$. Thresholds are obtained from the two displayed <equations>, and a positive-trace interval exists only if the fast normal <derivative> rises above $\epsilon$. At a nondegenerate <matrix trace>[trace] crossing, the linear <oscillation> <frequency> is $\sqrt{-f_v/\epsilon}$. The sign of a nonlinear Hopf coefficient is needed to classify the local periodic branch; in a bounded positive planar flow, a unique <repelling equilibrium> also allows a Poincaré-Bendixson existence argument for a <periodic orbit>.

= Three-region calcium nullcline
{parent=Two-pool calcium model}
{title2=$J(u)-K(u)v^2/(1+v^2)-\delta v=0$}

For $J=\beta u^2/(1+u^2)$ and $K=u^4/(1+u^4)$ with small leak $\delta$, the positive <nullcline> is unique at each $u$ because its <derivative> with respect to $v$ is strictly negative. Its asymptotic regions are a high-store balance $v\simeq(J-K)/\delta$, a crossing balance near $J(u_c)=K(u_c)$, and a negligible-leak balance $v^2/(1+v^2)\simeq J/K$. The crossing relation is $(K_c'-J_c')(u-u_c)\simeq K_c/v^2-\delta v$. These describe one continuous curve, not three distinct equilibria at a fixed cytoplasmic <concentration>.

= Total-calcium fast fibres
{parent=Two-pool calcium model}
{title2=$w=u+\gamma v,\quad\dot w=\mu-u$}

For $\dot u=\mu-u-\gamma f/\epsilon$ and $\dot v=f/\epsilon$, total scaled <calcium> $w$ changes only on the external-input/extrusion <time> scale. Fast motion therefore follows lines $u+\gamma v=\mathrm{constant}$. The fast normal <eigenvalue> on $f=0$ is $f_v-\gamma f_u$, so $f_v<0$ alone does not establish attraction. If $v=V(u)$, the slow <equation> is $\dot u=(\mu-u)/(1+\gamma V'(u))$. Folds of this reduced description satisfy $V'=-1/\gamma$.

= Second messenger
{parent=Signal transduction}

A <second messenger> is a small intracellular <molecule> or <ion> that relays a received signal to target <proteins>. Its production, release, removal or binding changes its availability after signal reception. For a <cell-surface receptor>, distinguish the extracellular <ligand>, often called the first messenger, from the intracellular <second messenger>. An activated relay <protein> is not thereby a <second messenger>: the term identifies small chemical mediators, rather than every downstream component of <signal transduction>. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27481708/[Newton, Bootman and Scott, Second Messengers (2016): definition and classes]{ref}

Two examples clarify what changes. A <biological cell> can make <cAMP> from <ATP>; alternatively, release of stored <calcium> <ions> can raise the free <calcium> <concentration> in its <cytoplasm>. The latter need not increase the total <calcium> content of the <biological cell>. Availability to target <proteins>, not merely the total amount present, carries the message. Removal and sequestration help terminate such signals. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK10794/[Purves et al., Neuroscience, 2nd edition: second messengers and calcium storage]{ref} <The Cell: A Molecular Approach, 2nd edition by Geoffrey M. Cooper/Pathways of Intracellular Signal Transduction>{ref}

The experimental motivation is unusually tangible: can a <hormone>'s effect be transferred to machinery that barely responds to the <hormone> itself? In Rall, Sutherland and Berthet's 1957 experiment, washed cat-liver particles were incubated with <ATP> and <magnesium> <ions>, with or without <epinephrine> and <glucagon>, two <hormones>. After five minutes at $30\,{}^{\circ}\mathrm C$, the mixtures were heated in boiling water for three minutes, chilled, and separated to remove insoluble material. Small samples of the clarified extracts were transferred to a separate soluble dog-liver preparation, again supplied with <ATP> and <magnesium> <ions>. The readout was the change in <glycogen phosphorylase> activity over five minutes, not the amount of newly synthesized <protein>. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0021-9258(18)65045-8[Rall, Sutherland and Berthet (1957), Figure 4 and pp. 470–471: preparation and transfer]{ref}

The <hormone>-conditioned extract caused a larger increase in <glycogen phosphorylase> activity. Water, extract made without <hormones>, and <hormones> added directly to the soluble preparation produced smaller effects. These comparisons distinguish transferred activity from baseline activity, preparation effects and simple <hormone> carryover. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0021-9258(18)65045-8[Rall, Sutherland and Berthet (1957), Figure 4: controls and observations]{ref}

The inference is a transferable, heat-stable intermediary between the <hormone>-sensitive particles and the soluble machinery. This does not identify the intermediary merely from heat resistance, establish its direct target, or show that every <hormone> uses the same mechanism. Rall and Sutherland's 1958 paper reports the factor's identification as <cAMP> in accompanying chemical work, and follows its production from <ATP>. It also reports an important limit: washed heart and skeletal-muscle particles produced detectable <cAMP> with <epinephrine> under their test conditions, whereas brain particles produced similar amounts with and without it. Tissue and preparation matter. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0021-9258(19)77422-5[Rall and Sutherland (1958), introduction and Table IV: cAMP identity and tissue-dependent responses]{ref}

The useful and beautiful separation is between recognition and execution: an extracellular <ligand> can control intracellular <enzymes> through a chemically different intermediary. Making many <second messenger> <molecules> can support <signal amplification>, while different target <proteins> give the same mediator different consequences. A <second messenger> identifies a mode of relaying information; its presence alone does not specify the size or direction of the response. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27481708/[Newton, Bootman and Scott (2016): second messengers and their targets]{ref}

= IP3 receptor
{c}
{synonym}

An <IP3 receptor> is an intracellular <ligand>-gated <ion channel>, rather than the extracellular signal's <cell-surface receptor>. Its opening is regulated by both <IP3> and cytosolic <calcium>: <IP3> binding is not a command that fixes one constant release rate. Mammalian <IP3 receptors> have three subtypes, with possible mixed assemblies and splice variants; these need not have identical regulation or cellular roles. The 2010 authored account distinguishes established channel behavior from then-unresolved structural explanations.<IP3 Receptors: Toward Understanding Their Activation by Colin W. Taylor and Stephen C. = IP3-sensitive calcium store
{c}
{parent=Inositol trisphosphate}

An <IP3-sensitive calcium store> contains releasable <calcium> behind a membrane bearing functional <IP3 receptors>. The <endoplasmic reticulum> is an important example. Pumping <calcium> into it or exporting <calcium> across the <cell membrane> lowers free cytosolic <calcium>; intracellular redistribution and loss from the whole <biological cell> are different balances.<The Cell: A Molecular Approach, 2nd edition by Geoffrey M. Cooper/Pathways of Intracellular Signal Transduction>[Phospholipids and Ca2+, paragraph accompanying Figure 13.27: calcium pumps across plasma and ER membranes]{ref}

Finite availability matters: a release pathway cannot deliver more <calcium> than its accessible store contains unless replenishment occurs. A falling cytosolic <calcium> response can reflect reduced release, increased removal, or both. Store content, release rate and free cytosolic <concentration> therefore answer different questions. Binding to <proteins> can also change the free fraction without changing the total number of <calcium> <ions>.

= Pancreatic IP3 store fractionation experiment
{c}
{parent=IP3-sensitive calcium store}

A 1984 primary experiment localized <IP3>-evoked release using <pancreatic acinar cells> isolated from rat <pancreas> with <collagenase>. A homogenized preparation was separated by <differential centrifugation>, then by a <sucrose> step <density gradient> or precipitation with <magnesium> chloride. The readout was <IP3>-induced <calcium> release per mass of <protein> across fractions. Release tracked two <endoplasmic reticulum> markers, <RNA> content and an <enzyme> activity, across several separation methods. Marker activities for <mitochondria> and the <cell membrane> supplied contrasting comparisons: release correlated negatively with them.<Effect of inositol-1,4,5-trisphosphate on isolated subcellular fractions of rat pancreas by H. Streb and others>[Streb, Bayerdörffer, Haase, Irvine and Schulz, Effect of inositol-1,4,5-trisphosphate on isolated subcellular fractions of rat pancreas (1984), abstract: preparation, separation methods, protein-normalized release and marker correlations]{ref}

Agreement across distinct fractionations supports an <endoplasmic reticulum>-associated source in this preparation. “Nonmitochondrial” alone only excludes a candidate; positive compartment markers add localization evidence. Marker comparisons control the localization inference, rather than replacing a no-<IP3> release comparison. Fractionation may mix or damage compartments, and correlation does not itself identify the <IP3 receptor> <protein>. This <calcium> release readout also does not directly measure secretion or establish identical responses in every <biological cell>.

= Finite calcium-store release model
{parent=IP3-sensitive calcium store}

For an original <mathematical model>, imagine a sustained, imposed release pathway, with no store refill during the plotted interval. Let $S$ be releasable store <amount of substance>, $X$ excess freely dissolved cytosolic <calcium> <amount of substance>, and $Q$ the cumulative amount removed from $X$ into a separate sink. Choose $S(0)=1\,\mathrm{amol}$, $X(0)=Q(0)=0$, cytosolic <volume> $V_c=1\,\mathrm{pL}$, release <rate constant> $k=0.4\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and clearance <rate constant> $h=0.8\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. These are stipulated values, not physiological measurements or a model of <IP3 receptor> gating.

Assume fixed <volume>, uniform mixing, no binding, no reverse transfer, no outside entry and first-order loss from each donating compartment. Then
$$
\frac{dS}{dt}=-kS,\qquad \frac{dX}{dt}=kS-hX,\qquad \frac{dQ}{dt}=hX,\qquad S+X+Q=S(0).
$$
The last equality is <conservation of mass> for the tracked <calcium> across the store, mobile excess and sink together; it does not assert conservation inside a <biological cell> if the sink represents export. A constant background $c_b=0.1\,\mathrm{\mu M}$ is held separate from this excess bookkeeping. Since $1\,\mathrm{amol}/1\,\mathrm{pL}=1\,\mathrm{\mu M}$, the response is $c=c_b+X/V_c$.

= Signal amplification
{parent=Signal transduction}

<Signal amplification> is an increase in a specified downstream response relative to an initiating signal. In a molecular-count description, one activated component can generate or activate many downstream <molecules>. Repeated <catalysis> by an <enzyme> supplies a mechanism: the <enzyme> remains available for another <chemical reaction>. Successive stages can multiply the response. For a biological example, Cooper describes the <epinephrine>-to-<glycogen phosphorylase> pathway, where production of many <cAMP> <molecules> and repeated downstream <protein> <phosphorylation> provide opportunities for <signal amplification>. This is an example, not a claim that every <signal transduction> pathway has the same stages or gain. <The Cell: A Molecular Approach, 2nd edition by Geoffrey M. Cooper/Pathways of Intracellular Signal Transduction>{ref}

Here is a counting toy extending the example under <signal transduction>. Suppose $N_0$ activated <receptors> each activate $a=5$ distinct relay <proteins> during a first interval. During a subsequent interval, each relay activates $b=20$ distinct target <proteins>. Assume enough inactive relays and targets, no shared targets, and no loss of activity during the count. Then
$$
N_1=aN_0,\qquad N_2=bN_1=abN_0.
$$
For $N_0=1$, this gives $N_1=5$ and $N_2=100$: the target-to-<receptor> count <ratio> is $100$. These are assumed integer counts, not measured rates or a universal physiological amplification factor. The intervals are sequential; the equations do not specify a time course.

= Signaling feedback
{parent=Signal transduction}

<Signaling feedback> returns the effect of a downstream event to an earlier stage of <signal transduction>, closing a loop analogous to <feedback control>. Trace the causal arrows: if an earlier relay activates a later relay and the later relay changes the earlier relay's activity, the return path closes the loop. <Negative feedback> opposes the initiating change; <positive feedback> reinforces it. <Signaling feedback> is a possible property of <signal transduction>, not a requirement of every pathway.

For a schematic example, let relay $A$ activate relay $B$. If $B$ inhibits $A$, increased $A$ eventually produces an opposing influence: <negative feedback>. If $B$ activates $A$, increased $A$ produces a reinforcing influence: <positive feedback>. These signs describe effects on activity, rather than the direction in which <molecules> travel. A closed loop containing two inhibitions can have <positive feedback>, because inhibiting an inhibitor reinforces the original change. The sign belongs to the whole loop.

{title=<Negative feedback>, <positive feedback> and an <incoherent feedforward loop>}
{description=Original causal schematics. Arrows activate; flat-ended lines inhibit. The upper loops return from downstream relay $B$ to earlier relay $A$. In the lower <incoherent feedforward loop>, input $s$ drives both a fast activating branch $x$ and a slower inhibitory branch $a$; their contributions meet at readout $r$. There is no return arrow from $r$ to either branch or to $s$. Shapes and connections illustrate the distinction, rather than a measured pathway.}

<Negative feedback> can restrain excessive activation and help preserve a usable range of responses. <Positive feedback> can support a decisive transition: Alberts and colleagues describe an <enzyme> whose product increases its own activity. Such reinforcement can contribute to switchlike behavior, but a <positive feedback> arrow alone does not prove <bistability>. A different structure, an <incoherent feedforward loop>, sends opposing influences forward from the same input; inhibition there is not a downstream return. <Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others/General Principles of Cell Communication>{ref} https://cqb.pku.edu.cn/tanglab/pdf/78.pdf[Ma et al., Defining Network Topologies that Can Achieve Biochemical Adaptation, Cell 138 (2009), pp. 760–773: definitions on p. 764]{ref}

A worked <mathematical model> shows why <negative feedback> does not automatically produce complete <signaling adaptation>. Let $s$ be a fixed normalized input, $r$ a normalized response, $g\geq0$ a dimensionless <feedback gain>, and $\tau>0$ a <time constant>. Suppose response removal contributes $-r$ and a return proportional to the response contributes $-gr$. The <ordinary differential equation> is
$$
\tau\frac{dr}{dt}=s-r-gr=s-(1+g)r.
$$
Setting the <derivative> to zero gives the <equilibrium point> $r_*=s/(1+g)$. A displacement $\delta r=r-r_*$ obeys $\tau\,d(\delta r)/dt=-(1+g)\delta r$, so its decaying <exponential function> establishes <asymptotic stability>. For a unit input switched on at $t=0$, <initial condition> $r(0)=0$, $g=2$ and $\tau=1$ second,
$$
r(t)=\frac{1-e^{-3t/\tau}}{3},\qquad r(1\,\mathrm{s})\simeq0.316737,\qquad \lim_{t\to\infty}r(t)=\frac13.
$$
The <negative feedback> reduces the eventual response from $1$ to $1/3$, but this response rises monotonically and never returns to its zero baseline. Attenuation and complete <signaling adaptation> are different properties. This deliberately simple <mathematical model> specifies no molecular pathway; <integral action> provides a contrasting way to remove a lasting offset in a stable <feedback control> system. https://www.fbswiki.org/wiki/index.php/Feedback_Principles[Åström and Murray, Feedback Systems, Chapter 2: proportional and integral feedback]{ref}

= Negative feedback
{parent=Signaling feedback}

<Negative feedback> closes a causal loop whose net effect opposes an initiating change. In <signal transduction>, activation followed by a downstream inhibition of the earlier activating stage is one example of <negative feedback>. Loop sign alone does not establish <asymptotic stability> or complete <signaling adaptation>; see the worked <signaling feedback> example.

A useful biological example is <chemotaxis feedback regulation>: an <Escherichia coli> <biological cell> adjusts its response to a sustained attractant instead of remaining at its immediate response. Increased <CheA> activity promotes <CheB> <phosphorylation>; <CheB> reverses <receptor methylation>, opposing their activation. After attractant addition, reduced activity instead favors <receptor methylation> by <CheR>, restoring activity. This is a return from <receptor> activity to the machinery regulating that activity. A motivating measurement is the <chemotaxis FRET assay>. <CheY> and <CheZ> carrying yellow and cyan fluorescent tags were observed in immobilized <Escherichia coli> <biological cells> on a coverslip in a flow chamber. Adding and removing attractant changed the yellow-to-cyan emission <ratio>, reporting the association of phosphorylated <CheY> with <CheZ>. This measures a molecular readout upstream of motor behavior. Comparisons with <biological cells> lacking <CheR>, <CheB>, or both tested the dependence on <receptor> modification; these perturbations also change starting activity and sensitivity, so they are not pure changes of one <feedback gain>. <Receptor sensitivity in bacterial chemotaxis by Victor Sourjik and others>{ref}

The later <chemotaxis feedback regulation> experiment used only the <Tar receptor> and nonmetabolizable methylaspartate, supplied and removed by flow. Restricting the <receptor> type and preventing consumption of the stimulus help interpret the perturbation, but limit transfer to other <receptors> and conditions. = Delayed negative feedback model
{parent=Negative feedback}
{title2=$d$}

A <feedback delay> is a time interval between an event and its returning influence. Multiple biochemical steps can introduce an effective lag; a fixed delay is an idealization, not a universal description of <signal transduction>. To isolate timing, let $x$ be a signed displacement from an <equilibrium point> and use normalized <dimensionless variables>:
$$
\frac{dx}{dt}=-x(t)-2x(t-d),\qquad d\geq0.
$$
The local removal is $-x$ and the returning influence is negative at every delay. At $d=0$ the <ordinary differential equation> reduces to the decay already illustrated under <signaling feedback>. For $d>0$ a <delay differential equation> requires a history on $[-d,0]$, not just an <initial condition> at one instant. Negative values of $x$ are displacements, not negative chemical <concentrations>.

An <exponential function> $x=e^{\lambda t}$ gives the <characteristic equation of a delay differential equation>
$$
\lambda+1+2e^{-\lambda d}=0.
$$
Putting $\lambda=i\omega$ and equating real and imaginary parts gives $\cos(\omega d)=-1/2$ and $\omega=2\sin(\omega d)$. The first crossing with $\omega>0$ therefore occurs at
$$
\omega=\sqrt3,\qquad d_c=\frac{2\pi}{3\sqrt3}\simeq1.20920.
$$
For this particular <mathematical model>, the <equilibrium point> is <asymptotically stable> for $0\leq d<d_c$. No nonnegative real <delayed feedback characteristic root> can satisfy the equation; the first loss of <asymptotic stability> is the indicated pair of imaginary <delayed feedback characteristic roots>. Differentiating the <characteristic equation of a delay differential equation> along a root yields
$$
\frac{d\lambda}{dd}=-\frac{\lambda(\lambda+1)}{1+d(\lambda+1)},\qquad \operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{d\lambda}{dd}\right)_{d=d_c}=\frac{3}{(1+d_c)^2+3d_c^2}>0.
$$
Thus the pair moves into growth as the delay increases through $d_c$. The neutral mode at $d_c$ is a <periodic function>, but its amplitude depends on history: this linear <mathematical model> does not select an attracting <limit cycle>.

The figure plots $x(t)=e^{at}\cos(bt)$ using a <delayed feedback characteristic root> $\lambda=a+ib$ and that same formula on the history interval. At $d=0.7$ the displayed mode decays; at $d=1.5$ it grows. These are exact modes up to numerical root accuracy, not step responses with identical histories. Molecular bounds and a changed return law would be needed to interpret what happens after an unstable biological response becomes large.

= Positive feedback
{parent=Signaling feedback}

<Positive feedback> closes a causal loop whose net effect reinforces an initiating change. A loop with two inhibitory links can therefore have <positive feedback>. In <signal transduction>, a product that increases the activity of the <enzyme> making it illustrates reinforcement; the consequences depend on the full <mathematical model>, not just the loop sign. See <signaling feedback>.

A biological example is <cell-cycle activation feedback>. <Cyclin B> binds <Cdc2>, whose activity promotes activation of <Cdc25>; <Cdc25> removes inhibitory <phosphorylation> from <Cdc2>, permitting further activation. <Cdc2> also inhibits <Wee1>, an <enzyme> that inhibits <Cdc2> through <phosphorylation>. That second return contains two inhibitions and therefore also has <positive feedback>. These are regulatory paths, not a claim that every connection is a single direct molecular encounter. <Multisite phosphoregulation of Cdc25 activity refines the mitotic entrance and exit switches by Lucy X. Lu and others>{ref}

Reinforcement can preserve a decision after its initiating stimulus weakens: returning from the high state may require a lower input than entering it. The <cell-cycle hysteresis experiment> below tests this history dependence at matched <cyclin B> <concentrations>. A steep response alone is insufficient evidence of <bistability>, and finite observation times can make slow relaxation look like memory. A proposed switch must specify its input, readout and observation interval.

= Saturating positive feedback
{parent=Positive feedback}

<Saturating positive feedback> limits the reinforcing rate as activity increases. Here is an explicitly synthetic <ordinary differential equation> for normalized nonnegative input $u$, response $x$, and return strength $\beta$:
$$
\frac{dx}{dt}=f(x;u,\beta)=u+\frac{\beta x^2}{1+x^2}-x,\qquad u\geq0,\quad\beta\geq0.
$$
The return is an increasing, nonproportional function of $x$, while removal is proportional to $x$. Time and activities are <dimensionless variables>. At $x=0$ the <vector field> points into the nonnegative domain, and $dx/dt\leq u+\beta-x$ prevents unbounded growth there. This makes the toy useful for asking about a bounded switch; it supplies no molecular mechanism for the chosen rate law.

At an <equilibrium point>, $f=0$. For a simple <equilibrium point>, the sign of the <derivative>
$$
f_x=\frac{2\beta x}{(1+x^2)^2}-1
$$
determines local <asymptotic stability>. If $\beta=1$, the maximum reinforcing slope is $3\sqrt3/8<1$, so $f$ decreases strictly: every $u\geq0$ has one <asymptotically stable> <equilibrium point> despite <positive feedback>.

Three <equilibrium points> become possible only if the reinforcing slope exceeds removal's slope somewhere. Its maximum is $3\sqrt3\beta/8$, at $x=1/\sqrt3$. Consequently this toy requires
$$
\beta>\frac{8}{3\sqrt3}\simeq1.53960
$$
for two distinct folds. This is a necessary threshold for a <bistability> interval in this toy, not a universal biochemical number. At a fold, both $f=0$ and $f_x=0$, giving
$$
(1+x^2)^2=2\beta x,\qquad u=x-\frac{\beta x^2}{1+x^2}.
$$
For $\beta=1.8$, the folds are at $(x,u)\simeq(0.350003,0.153563)$ and $(0.887240,0.094405)$. Between these input values there are two <asymptotically stable> <equilibrium points> separated by an unstable one. At $u=0.12$, the three activities are approximately $0.171331$, $0.621230$ and $1.127439$; the low and high ones are stable.

A sufficiently slow increasing sweep follows the low branch to $u_{\mathrm{on}}\simeq0.153563$ and then jumps upward. A decreasing sweep follows the high branch to $u_{\mathrm{off}}\simeq0.094405$ and then jumps downward: <hysteresis>. These are limiting quasistatic thresholds, with relaxation between input changes and negligible perturbations that could cause earlier switching. Finite sweep rates and fluctuations can alter observed thresholds. A switchlike response with only one stable <equilibrium point> does not have this equilibrium <hysteresis> mechanism.

= CDK1 activity and protein abundance
{parent=Cyclin-dependent kinase 1}
{c}

<CDK1 activity and protein abundance> are different observables. A total-<CDK1> <Western blot> estimates <protein> amount under its calibration and loading assumptions. A phosphosite-specific <Western blot> asks about a particular <CDK1 phosphorylation state>. A <histone H1 kinase assay> asks how much <substrate> modification occurs under specified assay conditions. Any conversion between them needs a model and suitable controls.

An original counting example stipulates $100$ <CDK1> <molecules>, with $60$ bound to <cyclin B> in each of two hypothetical samples. In sample A, $10$ bound <molecules> are activity-permissive and $50$ are inhibited; in sample B, $40$ are activity-permissive and $20$ are inhibited. The other $40$ are unbound in both samples. Total <CDK1> and bound <CDK1> agree, but activity-permissive counts differ fourfold. These are invented counts for bookkeeping, not measured biological populations.

If each activity-permissive <molecule> contributes the same <reaction rate>, <substrate> and <ATP> remain available, and the assay stays linear in time, a simple illustrative readout is
$$
R-R_0=\alpha N_{\mathrm{active}}t.
$$
Here $R$ is the recorded signal, $R_0$ is a matched blank signal, $N_{\mathrm{active}}$ is the activity-permissive count, $t$ the reaction interval, and $\alpha$ a common signal contribution per active <molecule> per unit time. The corrected signals in the stipulated samples have <ratio> $40/10=4$, although their total <protein> amounts agree. This is an assay accounting assumption, not an <ordinary differential equation> for the <cell cycle>. The no-added-<cyclin B> baseline in the inspected experiment can itself contain catalytic activity; it need not equal an enzyme-free blank $R_0$.

<Substrate> depletion, differing inhibitory conditions or a saturated detector can break the simple proportionality. Comparing samples therefore requires matched reaction intervals, <substrate> supply, loading and detector response, rather than assuming that brighter <histone H1> labeling automatically means more total <CDK1>.

= Signaling adaptation
{parent=Signal transduction}

<Signaling adaptation> is a decline of a response toward its earlier level while the initiating <signal> remains present. Specify which response is measured and its baseline: complete <signaling adaptation> means eventual return to that baseline; partial <signaling adaptation> leaves a lasting displacement. The continuing <signal> distinguishes <signaling adaptation> from simply switching off the stimulus. <Signaling adaptation> describes behavior, while <signaling feedback> describes a causal arrangement, so neither implies the other.

By reducing a persistent response to background <ligand> <concentration>, <signaling adaptation> can make a <biological cell> sensitive to changes rather than only to absolute levels. Resetting the response helps leave room for another change over a useful range of <ligand> <concentrations>. This is valuable in <chemotaxis>: swimming <bacteria> encounter changing chemical conditions, and their <signal transduction> influences whether a run continues or a tumble occurs. <Signaling adaptation> helps separate a change encountered during motion from a lasting background. This usefulness does not imply exact return or equal sensitivity at every possible <concentration>. <Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others/General Principles of Cell Communication>{ref} https://groups.csail.mit.edu/mac/projects/amorphous/6.978/papers/doyle-PNAS-2000.pdf[Yi, Huang, Simon and Doyle, PNAS 97 (2000), pp. 4649–4653: chemotaxis and adaptation]{ref}

Here is a solvable <incoherent feedforward loop> toy that exhibits complete <signaling adaptation> without <signaling feedback>. The input $s(t)$ jumps from $0$ to $1$ at $t=0$ and remains $1$, a <Heaviside step function>. Let $x$ be a fast activating branch, $a$ a slower inhibitory branch, and $r=x-a$ a readout. The activities $s,x,a,r$ are <dimensionless variables>; physical time is in seconds. Choose <time constants> $\tau_x=1$ second and $\tau_a=5$ seconds, with <initial conditions> $x(0)=a(0)=0$:
$$
\tau_x\frac{dx}{dt}=s-x,\qquad \tau_a\frac{da}{dt}=s-a,\qquad r=x-a.
$$
Both branches read $s$ directly. Neither branch depends on $r$, and $a$ does not change $x$: the subtraction only defines the readout. Thus the slow opposing influence is feedforward, with no closed <feedback control> loop.

For $t\geq0$, solving the two <ordinary differential equations> gives
$$
x(t)=1-e^{-t/\tau_x},\qquad a(t)=1-e^{-t/\tau_a},\qquad r(t)=e^{-t/\tau_a}-e^{-t/\tau_x}.
$$
Writing $t$ for the numerical time in seconds, these are $x=1-e^{-t}$, $a=1-e^{-t/5}$ and $r=e^{-t/5}-e^{-t}$. Initially $r=0$, then $x$ rises faster than $a$, producing a pulse. At long times $x$ and $a$ both approach $1$, so $r$ returns to $0$ despite sustained $s=1$. For this upward step $e^{-t/5}\geq e^{-t}$, hence $r(t)\geq0$. The striking feature of this <signaling adaptation> toy is that both branches remain active while the readout becomes quiet: the continuing background and the response to its arrival are separable.

Find the pulse maximum by differentiating the <exponential functions>. With numerical time in seconds, the <derivative> is
$$
r'(t)=-\frac15e^{-t/5}+e^{-t}.
$$
The stationary condition is $e^{-4t/5}=1/5$, so the <natural logarithm> gives
$$
t_{\mathrm{peak}}=\frac54\ln5\simeq2.0118\ \text{seconds},\qquad r_{\mathrm{peak}}=\frac45\,5^{-1/4}\simeq0.53499.
$$
Before this time the <derivative> is positive; afterwards it is negative. Together with $r(0)=0$ and $\lim_{t\to\infty}r(t)=0$, this proves that it is the unique pulse maximum. As another numerical check, at $t=10$ seconds $x\simeq0.999955$, $a\simeq0.864665$ and $r\simeq0.135290$: the slow branch is still catching up.

The subtraction $r=x-a$ is a mathematical readout, not a claim that an actual chemical <concentration> equals this difference for arbitrary inputs. Nonnegativity was established only for the specified upward step; other input histories can give a negative signed readout. Complete <signaling adaptation> here also relies on equal eventual branch gains: changing the readout to $r=x-0.9a$ leaves $r_*=0.1$ for $s=1$. This toy illustrates cancellation and different <time constants>; it does not establish molecular implementation or robustness to arbitrary parameter changes. Ma and colleagues' 2009 study separately analyzed biochemical <mathematical models> with <negative feedback> and <incoherent feedforward loops>, identifying additional conditions for <signaling adaptation> in those models. https://cqb.pku.edu.cn/tanglab/pdf/78.pdf[Ma et al., Cell 138 (2009): adaptive architectures and their kinetic conditions]{ref}

A historical connection to <feedback control> comes from Yi, Huang, Simon and Doyle's 2000 analysis of a bacterial <chemotaxis> model. They identified <integral action> in its regulation of <receptor> activity: accumulated deviations alter a slower adjustment, which in turn changes the measured activity. At a stationary state, an adjustment that integrates a nonzero error cannot remain constant, explaining how zero error can follow. Their conclusion depended on stated biochemical assumptions and stable behavior; it was not a claim that every <biological cell> uses that mechanism. A transient pulse alone, as the <incoherent feedforward loop> toy shows, cannot identify the underlying <signaling feedback> architecture. https://groups.csail.mit.edu/mac/projects/amorphous/6.978/papers/doyle-PNAS-2000.pdf[Yi et al., Robust perfect adaptation in bacterial chemotaxis through integral feedback control, PNAS 97 (2000): Results and Discussion]{ref}
