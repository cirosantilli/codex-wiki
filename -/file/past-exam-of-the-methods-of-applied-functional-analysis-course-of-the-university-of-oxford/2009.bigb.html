The <weak derivative> condition is
$$
\boxed{\int_\Omega u\,\partial_i\phi\,dx=-\int_\Omega v\phi\,dx\qquad(\phi\in C_c^\infty(\Omega)).}
$$
Both <integrals> exist since $u,v$ are <locally integrable> and the <test function> has <compact support>. <Derivatives> are defined up to almost-everywhere equality. The space $W^{1,p}(\Omega)$ consists of $u\in L^p(\Omega)$ whose first <weak derivatives> $D_i u$ all lie in $L^p(\Omega)$. One standard <norm> is
$$
\|u\|_{1,p}=\left(\|u\|_p^p+\sum_{i=1}^n\|D_i u\|_p^p\right)^{1/p}\quad(p<\infty),\qquad
\|u\|_{1,\infty}=\max(\|u\|_\infty,\|D_1u\|_\infty,\ldots,\|D_nu\|_\infty).
$$
A sum in the last formula gives an equivalent <norm>. For restriction to $E$, extend any $\phi\in C_c^\infty(E)$ by zero to $\Omega$. Its support is compactly contained in $E$, so the extension is smooth. The identity over $\Omega$ then reduces exactly to the identity over $E$, proving locality of the <weak derivative>.

For the <jump contribution to a piecewise weak derivative>, <integration by parts> on the two sides of the joining point gives the <distributional derivative>
$$
Du=2x\,\mathbf1_{(0,1)}+\mathbf1_{(1,2)}+(a-1)\delta_1.
$$
The coefficient is the right <Sobolev trace>[trace] $1+a$ minus the left <Sobolev trace>[trace] $2$. If a <locally integrable> <weak derivative> existed, locality on each open side would identify it <almost everywhere> with the displayed regular part. Substituting that into the global test identity would force $(a-1)\phi(1)=0$ for every <test function>, hence $a=1$. Equivalently a nonzero Dirac <Dirac delta function>[point mass] cannot be represented by a <locally integrable> <derivative>.

For $a=1$ the <Dirac delta function>[point-mass] term vanishes, and both the <function> and its piecewise <derivative> are <bounded> on the finite <interval>. Thus $u\in W^{1,\infty}$ and hence in every listed $W^{1,p}$. Therefore **membership holds exactly for $a=1$**. Changing a single point value is irrelevant to Sobolev membership; it cannot remove mismatched <Sobolev trace>[one-sided traces] when $a\ne1$.

<Weak convergence> $u_j\rightharpoonup u$ means $F(u_j)\to F(u)$ for every $F\in X^*$. <weak-star topology>[Weak-star convergence] $T_j\overset*\rightharpoonup T$ means $T_j(x)\to T(x)$ for every $x\in X$, viewing $X$ as the predual. The <sequential Banach-Alaoglu theorem for a separable predual> says that every norm-bounded <sequence> in $X^*$ has a <weak-star topology>[weak-star convergent] <subsequence>. Separability is what turns the <bounded> <weak-star topology>[weak-star] <compactness> statement into this sequential form.

For the <weak-star limit of periodic rescalings>, apply it with $X=L^1(\mathbb R)$, which is separable, and $X^*=L^\infty(\mathbb R)$. The rescaled <functions> have uniform <essential supremum>[essential-supremum] bound $\|\theta\|_\infty$. Let $q=\theta-\bar\theta$ and $F(t)=\int_0^tq(s)\,ds$. Since $q$ is one-periodic with zero mean, $F$ is a <bounded> one-periodic absolutely <continuous> primitive. Thus for every finite <interval>,
$$
\int_a^b[\theta(jx)-\bar\theta],dx=\frac{F(jb)-F(ja)}j\longrightarrow0.
$$
Every <weak-star topology>[weak-star subsequential limit] $h$ therefore satisfies $\int_a^b(h-\bar\theta)=0$ for all $a<b$. To apply the supplied uniqueness fact without assuming global integrability, fix $R>0$ and <set> $v=(h-\bar\theta)\mathbf1_{[-R,R]}$. This is in $L^1(\mathbb R)$, and its <integral> over any <interval> is zero, since intersecting with $[-R,R]$ again gives an <interval>. Hence $v=0$ <almost everywhere>. Taking integer $R\to\infty$ yields $h=\bar\theta$ <almost everywhere> on the whole line.

Finally, if the full <sequence> did not converge <weak-star topology>[weak-star] to this constant, some $g\in L^1$ and some <subsequence> would have its pairing with $\theta(jx)-\bar\theta$ <bounded> away from zero. Sequential <compactness> gives a further <weak-star topology>[weak-star convergent] <subsequence>, whose <limit> must be the constant just identified, a contradiction. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\theta(j\cdot)\overset*\rightharpoonup\bar\theta\quad\text{in }L^\infty(\mathbb R).}
$$
This is averaging against integrable <test functions>, not a claim of <pointwise> or strong <norm convergence>.

For a <coercive Neumann energy with a positive reaction term>, let $\nu>0$ be the ellipticity constant and $A_0=\max_{i,j}\|a_{ij}\|_\infty$. <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>[Cauchy-Schwarz] gives
$$
|B[u,v]|\le nA_0\|\nabla u\|_2\|\nabla v\|_2+\|c\|_\infty\|u\|_2\|v\|_2
\le C\|u\|_{H^1}\|v\|_{H^1}.
$$
Bilinearity follows from the <integral> and weak-derivative <linearity>. Ellipticity and the strictly positive zeroth-order coefficient give
$$
\boxed{B[u,u]\ge\nu\|\nabla u\|_2^2+\mu\|u\|_2^2\ge\kappa\|u\|_{H^1}^2,\quad\kappa=\min(\nu,\mu)>0.}
$$
This controls constants as well as <gradients>, so no zero-mean constraint or <Neumann boundary condition>[Neumann] compatibility condition is required. Symmetry of the coefficients makes $B$ symmetric.

Write $F(v)=\int_\Omega fv$. This is a <bounded linear functional> on $H^1$, with $|F(v)|\le\|f\|_2\|v\|_{H^1}$. Consequently
$$
I(v)\ge\frac\kappa2\|v\|_{H^1}^2-\|f\|_2\|v\|_{H^1}\longrightarrow+\infty
$$
as the <norm> tends to <infinity>. The infimum is finite, and every <minimizing sequence> is <bounded>. The <Hilbert space> $H^1$ is reflexive, so a minimizing <subsequence> converges <weak convergence>[weakly] to some $u\in H^1$.

To verify lower semicontinuity directly, put $w_j=v_j-u$. Boundedness of $B$ makes $B[u,w_j]\to0$ under <weak convergence>, and symmetry gives
$$
B[v_j,v_j]=B[u,u]+2B[u,w_j]+B[w_j,w_j]\ge B[u,u]+o(1).
$$
Also $F(v_j)\to F(u)$. Therefore $I(u)\le\liminf I(v_j)$, so **the absolute minimum is attained**.

For every $h\in H^1$ the real <function> $t\mapsto I(u+th)$ has a minimum at zero. Its <derivative> there is $B[u,h]-F(h)$, yielding the required weak equation for every $h$. If two <weak solutions> exist, their difference $z$ satisfies $B[z,h]=0$ for all $h$; choose $h=z$ and use coercivity to obtain $z=0$. In fact the weak equation also gives the exact energy identity
$$
\boxed{I(v)-I(u)=\tfrac12B[v-u,v-u]\ge\tfrac\kappa2\|v-u\|_{H^1}^2.}
$$
Thus the <minimizer> and <weak solution> are both unique. The natural <boundary condition> is encoded by testing in the whole $H^1$ space, without needing <pointwise> <conormal flux of a divergence-form operator>[conormal derivatives] for rough coefficients.

The <Lax-Milgram theorem> states that a <bounded bilinear form> $a$ on a real <Hilbert space> $V$, satisfying $a(v,v)\ge\alpha\|v\|_V^2$ for some $\alpha>0$, has for each $F\in V^*$ a unique $u\in V$ with $a(u,v)=F(v)$ for all $v$. Moreover $\|u\|_V\le\|F\|/\alpha$.

For a <compact operator> $K$ on a <Hilbert space>, the <Fredholm alternative> says that $I-\lambda K$ has closed range, <finite-dimensional vector space>[finite-dimensional] <null space>[kernel] and cokernel of equal dimension, and
$$
\operatorname{Ran}(I-\lambda K)=\ker(I-\lambda K^*)^\perp\qquad(\lambda\in\mathbb R).
$$
Thus a zero <null space>[kernel] gives unique solvability for every right-hand side; a nonzero <null space>[kernel] gives solvability exactly under the adjoint <orthogonality> conditions, and then all solutions differ by a <null space>[kernel] <vector>.

Take $V=H_0^1(\Omega)$. <Gårding's inequality>[Gårding's inequality] supplies constants $a>0$, $b\ge0$ with $B[v,v]\ge a\|v\|_{H^1}^2-b\|v\|_2^2$. Therefore for $\lambda\le-b$ the shifted form $B_\lambda[u,v]=B[u,v]-\lambda(u,v)_{L^2}$ is coercive. It is <bounded> for every fixed $\lambda$, and $v\mapsto(f,v)$ is <bounded> on $V$. Lax-Milgram gives **unique weak solvability for $\lambda\le-\gamma$, with $\gamma=b$**. The inequality itself follows, for example, by absorbing the drift cross-term $\|b\|_\infty\|\nabla v\|_2\|v\|_2$ using Young's inequality and allowing a lower $L^2$ correction for $c$.

For the <compact inverse and adjoint of a coercive elliptic operator>, under the additional zero-shift coercivity assumption, define $Gg\in V$ by $B[Gg,v]=(g,v)$ for all $v\in V$, and let $K$ be this solution viewed in $L^2$. The solution operator is linear and <bounded> into $V$, and the <Rellich-Kondrachov theorem>[Rellich-Kondrachov] <compact embedding> $V\hookrightarrow L^2$ makes $K$ compact. Rewriting the weak equation as $B[u,v]=(f+\lambda u,v)$ gives
$$
\boxed{(I-\lambda K)u=Kf.}
$$
Conversely an $L^2$ solution of this operator equation lies in $V$, because it equals $K(f+\lambda u)$, and satisfies the weak <partial differential equation>[differential equation].

For constant drift coefficients and zero <Dirichlet boundary condition>[Dirichlet] <Sobolev trace>[traces], <integration by parts> gives $\int b_iD_i v\,u=-\int b_i vD_i u$. Symmetry of the principal coefficients therefore makes $B^*[u,v]=B[v,u]$ the unshifted weak form of the displayed formal adjoint. **The shifted adjoint equation requires**
$$
\boxed{B^*[u,v]-\lambda(u,v)=(f,v)\qquad(v\in V).}
$$
The <spectral shift in an adjoint weak formulation> is essential: the printed sentence omitting it is false for nonzero $\lambda$. For instance take a smooth ball, $L=-\Delta+1$, any nonzero $u\in C_c^\infty(\Omega)$ and $f=-\Delta u+u$. The printed unshifted identity holds, but $(L^*-\lambda)u=f-\lambda u\ne f$ whenever $\lambda\ne0$. This counterexample also meets zero-shift coercivity and constant-drift assumptions. The corrected identity above is used for the remainder of the argument.

Let $G_*g\in V$ solve $B^*[G_*g,v]=(g,v)$, which exists by Lax-Milgram because the diagonal coercivity is unchanged. For $p,q\in L^2$,
$$
(Kp,q)=B[Gp,G_*q]=(p,G_*q).
$$
Thus $G_*$, viewed as an $L^2$ operator, is exactly $K^*$. Applying this inverse to the corrected shifted adjoint equation gives
$$
\boxed{(I-\lambda K^*)u=K^*f,}
$$
and the converse follows from its range lying in $V$, just as before.

Finally let the original homogeneous equation have a nonzero solution. Its parameter is nonzero, since the unshifted problem is coercive. Fredholm gives solvability precisely when $(Kf,w)=0$ for every $w\in\ker(I-\lambda K^*)$. Such a $w$ satisfies $K^*w=w/\lambda$, so $(Kf,w)=(f,w)/\lambda$. Hence the necessary and sufficient condition is
$$
\boxed{\int_\Omega fw\,dx=0\quad\text{for every }w\in H_0^1(\Omega)\text{ satisfying }(L^*-\lambda)w=0\text{ weakly}.}
$$
When this holds, the solutions are an <affine subspace>[affine translate] of the original <finite-dimensional vector space>[finite-dimensional] <null space>[homogeneous solution space]. The compatibility space is the adjoint <null space>[kernel]; a drift term does not generally permit replacing it by the original <null space>[kernel].

<Banach fixed-point theorem>[Banach's fixed-point theorem] states that a map $T:Y\to Y$ on a nonempty <complete metric space> with $d(Tx,Ty)\le qd(x,y)$ for a constant $0\le q<1$ has a unique <fixed point>, approached from every starting point by iteration.

Choose $x_0$ and let $x_{n+1}=Tx_n$. Induction gives $d(x_{n+1},x_n)\le q^nd(x_1,x_0)$. For $m>n$, the triangle inequality and <geometric series> give
$$
d(x_m,x_n)\le\frac{q^n}{1-q}d(x_1,x_0).
$$
Thus the <sequence> is Cauchy and converges by completeness to some $x_*$. The contraction estimate implies <continuity>, so $Tx_*=\lim Tx_n=\lim x_{n+1}=x_*$. If $y_*$ were another <fixed point>, $d(x_*,y_*)\le qd(x_*,y_*)$ would force equality of the points. Passing $m\to\infty$ also yields the quantitative error estimate
$$
\boxed{d(x_n,x_*)\le\frac{q^n}{1-q}d(x_1,x_0).}
$$

A <weak solution> belongs to $V=H_0^1(\Omega)$ and satisfies
$$
\boxed{\int_\Omega\nabla u\cdot\nabla h\,dx=\int_\Omega f(u)h\,dx\qquad(h\in V),}
$$
with the nonlinear right-hand side defining a <bounded> <functional> on $V$. Under the stated global <Lipschitz continuity>[Lipschitz] hypothesis this requirement is automatic: $|f(s)|\le|f(0)|+\epsilon|s|$ and boundedness of $\Omega$ give $f(u)\in L^2$ whenever $u\in V$. <Continuity> of $f$ alone, without growth control, would not guarantee this for an arbitrary <Sobolev space>[Sobolev function].

For the <semilinear Poisson contraction threshold>, use the <Sobolev norm>[gradient norm] on $V$, and let $C_P$ satisfy the <Poincaré inequality> $\|v\|_2\le C_P\|\nabla v\|_2$. For $g\in L^2$, Lax-Milgram gives the <Dirichlet boundary condition>[zero-Dirichlet] Poisson solution $Pg\in V$. Testing with $Pg$ yields $\|\nabla Pg\|_2\le C_P\|g\|_2$. Define $T(v)=P(f(v))$, a self-map of the complete space $V$. For $v,w\in V$,
$$
\|T(v)-T(w)\|_V\le C_P\|f(v)-f(w)\|_2
\le\epsilon C_P^2\|v-w\|_V.
$$
Therefore **$\epsilon C_P^2<1$ is a sufficient condition** for <Banach fixed-point theorem>[Banach's theorem] to give a unique <fixed point> and hence a unique <weak solution>. There is no need to assume $f(0)=0$ or to restrict to a small ball: the <Lipschitz continuity>[Lipschitz] bound makes the map defined on all of $V$. The same estimates give
$$
\|\nabla u\|_2\le\frac{C_P|\Omega|^{1/2}|f(0)|}{1-\epsilon C_P^2}.
$$
With the optimal <Poincaré inequality>[Poincaré] constant, the threshold is $\epsilon<\lambda_1$ for the <first Dirichlet eigenvalue>; the stated sufficient condition does not depend on knowing that optimal constant.

For a <uniform contraction from a comparison function on a bounded set>, the <closed set> $M$ is complete and has diameter at most $2R$. Suppose first $R>0$. The <limit> condition supplies $r_0>0$ and $q_0<1$ such that $\psi(r)/r\le q_0$ for $0<r\le r_0$. On the remaining compact <interval> $[r_0,2R]$, if nonempty, the <continuous> ratio $\psi(r)/r$ attains a maximum $q_1<1$, since it is strictly below one at every point. Thus there is one $q<1$ with $\psi(r)\le qr$ for every distance $0\le r\le2R$ arising in $M$.

Consequently $d(Tx,Ty)\le qd(x,y)$ throughout $M$. <Banach fixed-point theorem>[Banach's theorem] applies and gives **a unique <fixed point> in $M$**. No linear structure or <compactness> of the ball is used; the compact object in the argument is only the <scalar> distance <interval>. If the <closed ball> is the singleton at radius zero, its self-map fixes that point. The estimate on the ratio near zero is what prevents the compact-interval supremum argument from approaching one at its missing endpoint.

<Brouwer fixed-point theorem>[Brouwer]'s fixed-point theorem says that every <continuous> self-map of a nonempty closed <Euclidean ball> has a <fixed point>. It does not promise convergence of repeated application. For example **$f(x)=1-x$ with starting point $x_1=0$** gives the nonconvergent alternating <sequence> $0,1,0,1,\ldots$, although $1/2$ is a <fixed point>.

For an <invariant ball from sublinear growth>, choose
$$
R\ge\max\{(2A)^{1/(1-\alpha)},\,2B\}.
$$
Then $AR^\alpha\le R/2$ and $B\le R/2$. For every $|x|\le R$ the given estimate yields $|f(x)|\le A R^\alpha+B\le R$. Thus $f$ restricts to a <continuous> self-map of the <closed ball> of this radius, and <Brouwer fixed-point theorem>[Brouwer] gives a <fixed point>. The exponent $\alpha<1$ ensures that the linear radius eventually dominates its sublinear growth; no <differentiability> or contraction estimate is needed.

One form of <Schauder fixed-point theorem>[Schauder]'s theorem says that if $M$ is a nonempty closed <bounded> convex <subset> of a <Banach space> and $T:M\to M$ is <continuous> with relatively compact image, then $T$ has a <fixed point>.

For a <Schauder solution of a small Hammerstein integral equation>, take $X=C([0,1])$ with the <supremum norm> and $M=\{u:\|u\|_\infty\le R\}$. This <set> is nonempty, closed, <bounded> and convex. Put
$$
F_R=\max_{0\le y\le1,\ |z|\le R}|f(y,z)|,\qquad G_0=\max_{x,y\in[0,1]}|G(x,y)|,
\qquad\mu_0=\frac{R}{1+G_0F_R}>0.
$$
Both maxima are finite by <continuity> on <compact sets>. Define $(T_\mu u)(x)=\mu\int_0^1G(x,y)f(y,u(y))\,dy$. <Continuity> and <uniform continuity> of the <null space>[kernel] give $T_\mu u\in X$, and for $0<\mu\le\mu_0$,
$$
\|T_\mu u\|_\infty\le\mu G_0F_R\le R,
$$
so it is a self-map. This choice of $\mu_0$ also handles the case when either maximum is zero without division by zero.

If $u_n\to u$ uniformly in $M$, <uniform continuity> of $f$ on $[0,1]\times[-R,R]$ makes $f(\cdot,u_n(\cdot))\to f(\cdot,u(\cdot))$ uniformly. The <integral> estimate then proves <continuity> of $T_\mu$ in the <supremum norm>. Moreover
$$
|(T_\mu u)(x)-(T_\mu u)(x')|\le\mu F_R\sup_y|G(x,y)-G(x',y)|,
$$
which tends to zero with $|x-x'|$ uniformly in $u\in M$. Thus the image is uniformly <bounded> and <equicontinuous>. <Arzelà-Ascoli theorem>[Arzelà-Ascoli] states that such a family on a compact <interval> has compact closure in the <supremum norm>. Hence $T_\mu(M)$ is relatively compact, and <Schauder fixed-point theorem>[Schauder] gives a <fixed point> for each $\mu$ in the specified <interval>. Therefore **a <continuous> solution with <supremum norm> at most $R$ exists**. The argument imposes no <Lipschitz continuity>[Lipschitz] hypothesis on $f$ and makes no uniqueness assertion.

Using the <dual pairing>, <monotone operator>[monotonicity] means
$$
\boxed{\langle A(u)-A(v),u-v\rangle\ge0\qquad(u,v\in X).}
$$
<strictly monotone operator>[Strict monotonicity] requires strict positivity whenever $u\ne v$. Coercivity with respect to zero, in the growth sense used for monotone-operator existence theory, means
$$
\boxed{\frac{\langle A(v),v\rangle}{\|v\|_X}\longrightarrow+\infty\qquad\text{as }\|v\|_X\to\infty.}
$$
This is a condition on all <sequences> escaping to <infinity>, not just each fixed direction with a bound depending on that direction. <strictly monotone operator>[Strict monotonicity] concerns pairs of distinct arguments; it does not by itself imply this uniform large-norm growth.

For the <log-cosh energy on a Sobolev space>, the elementary inequalities $0\le\log\cosh t\le t^2/2$ follow by integrating $|\tanh t|\le|t|$ from zero. Thus
$$
0\le I(v)\le\tfrac12(\|\nabla v\|_2^2+\|v\|_2^2)<\infty,
$$
so the energy is well-defined on $H^1$. For a fixed $v$, its proposed <derivative> is linear in the direction $h$ and <bounded>, because $|\tanh v|\le|v|$ gives
$$
|\partial_h I(v)|\le\|\nabla v\|_2\|\nabla h\|_2+\|v\|_2\|h\|_2\le\|v\|_{H^1}\|h\|_{H^1}.
$$
To justify differentiation, use $(\log\cosh t)''=\operatorname{sech}^2t\in(0,1]$. Taylor's <integral> remainder <pointwise>, combined with the exact quadratic <gradient> expansion, gives
$$
0\le I(v+h)-I(v)-\int_\Omega(\nabla v\cdot\nabla h+\tanh(v)h)\,dx
\le\tfrac12\|h\|_{H^1}^2.
$$
Every term is integrable by the displayed bounds. The remainder is quadratic in the <norm>, so the <functional> is even <Fréchet derivative>[Fréchet differentiable], and in particular <Gâteaux differentiability>[Gâteaux differentiable] with the required <derivative>. This also proves that $A(v)$ belongs to the stated <dual space>.

For $v,w\in H^1$,
$$
\langle A(v)-A(w),v-w\rangle=\|\nabla(v-w)\|_2^2+
\int_\Omega[\tanh v-\tanh w](v-w)\,dx.
$$
Both terms are nonnegative, and the integrand in the second is strictly positive wherever $v\ne w$, since $\tanh$ is strictly increasing. Distinct <Sobolev space>[Sobolev elements] differ on a <set> of positive measure, so the expression is positive. Hence **$A$ is strictly monotone**.

This gives <strict monotonicity without coercivity on H1>: it is **not coercive on $H^1$**. For <constant functions> $v_t=t$ with $t\to+\infty$, the <norm> is $t|\Omega|^{1/2}$ and
$$
\frac{\langle A(v_t),v_t\rangle}{\|v_t\|_{H^1}}=|\Omega|^{1/2}\tanh t\longrightarrow|\Omega|^{1/2},
$$
which is finite. In contrast it **is coercive on $H_0^1$**: if $\|v\|_2\le C_P\|\nabla v\|_2$, then
$$
\langle A(v),v\rangle=\|\nabla v\|_2^2+\int_\Omega v\tanh v\,dx
\ge\frac{\|v\|_{H^1}^2}{1+C_P^2}.
$$
Dividing by the <norm> proves growth to <infinity>. On this smaller space the operator is understood by restricting its <dual pairing>[dual action] to <zero-boundary Sobolev space>[zero-trace] <test functions>. Removing the constant directions by the <boundary condition> is what restores coercivity.

For the <log-cosh obstacle variational inequality>, the admissible <set> is convex: for any $v\in M$, $u+t(v-u)$ remains above the obstacle and in $H_0^1$ for $0\le t\le1$. Minimality and the right <derivative> at zero therefore give
$$
\boxed{\int_\Omega\nabla u\cdot\nabla(v-u)+\tanh(u)(v-u)\,dx\ge0\qquad(v\in M).}
$$
The one-sided sign comes from admissibility, which permits motion toward every $v\in M$ but does not permit an arbitrary negative variation at a contact point.

Now fix $\phi\in C_c^\infty(D)$. Smoothness on $D$ and <continuity> of the obstacle give a strictly positive minimum of $u-g$ on the <compact support> of $\phi$. Hence $u+t\phi$ is admissible for both signs of sufficiently small $t$. Applying the inequality to both variations yields
$$
\int_D\nabla u\cdot\nabla\phi+\tanh(u)\phi\,dx=0.
$$
<Integration by parts> gives $-\Delta u+\tanh u=0$ distributionally on $D$, and smoothness makes it <pointwise>. Thus
$$
\boxed{-\Delta u+\tanh u=0\quad\text{where }u>g.}
$$
On the contact <set> the <variational inequality>, rather than unrestricted stationarity, remains the governing statement. <Convexity> of the energy also makes the inequality sufficient for minimization; <strictly monotone operator>[strict monotonicity] implies at most one <minimizer>, although existence was already stipulated in this part.
