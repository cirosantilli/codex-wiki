Here <uniform ellipticity> means that there is a constant $\lambda>0$, independent of $x$, such that
$$
\boxed{\xi^Ta(x)\xi\geq\lambda|\xi|^2\quad\text{for almost every }x\in\Omega\text{ and every }\xi\in\mathbb R^n.}
$$
The assumption that the entries are <essentially bounded functions> also gives an upper bound $|a(x)\xi|\leq\Lambda|\xi|$ with a finite constant $\Lambda$. For this symmetric divergence-form operator, the lower bound makes its energy control the squared <Euclidean norm> of the <gradient>.

= Solution

The <space of test functions> is $\boxed{C_0^\infty(\Omega)=\{\varphi\in C^\infty(\Omega):\operatorname{supp}\varphi\text{ is a compact subset of }\Omega\}}$. The <Sobolev space> is
$$
\boxed{H^1(\Omega)=\{u\in L^2(\Omega):D_ju\in L^2(\Omega),\ j=1,\ldots,n\},}
$$
where $D_ju$ denotes a <weak derivative>, and its <Sobolev norm> is $\|u\|_{H^1}^2=\|u\|_2^2+\sum_j\|D_ju\|_2^2$. The <zero-boundary Sobolev space> is $\boxed{H_0^1(\Omega)=\overline{C_0^\infty(\Omega)}^{\,H^1}}$. This closure defines the boundary condition even when the boundary has insufficient regularity for a classical boundary value.

A <weak solution> is a function $u\in H_0^1(\Omega)$ satisfying the <weak formulation>
$$
\boxed{\int_\Omega a\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx=\int_\Omega f\varphi\,dx\quad\text{for every }\varphi\in H_0^1(\Omega).}
$$
Multiplication by a <test function> and <integration by parts> give this identity from the classical equation. Conversely it is the equation in the sense of <distributions>, with the zero boundary condition encoded by the <zero-boundary Sobolev space>. Testing only against $C_0^\infty(\Omega)$ is equivalent, by its <density> in $H_0^1$ and continuity of both sides.

Write $a(v,w)=\int_\Omega a\nabla v\cdot\nabla w$ and $A[v]=a(v,v)$. The admissible set is the closed <affine subspace> $H_g^1=g+H_0^1$. Symmetry of the coefficient matrix gives
$$
A[u+t\varphi]=A[u]+2t\,a(u,\varphi)+t^2A[\varphi],\qquad\varphi\in H_0^1.
$$
If $u$ minimizes the energy, its <first variation> vanishes: differentiating at $t=0$ gives $a(u,\varphi)=0$. Hence $u$ is a <weak solution>.

Conversely, if $u$ is a <weak solution>, every admissible $v$ has $v-u\in H_0^1$ and therefore
$$
A[v]=A[u]+2a(u,v-u)+A[v-u]=A[u]+A[v-u]\geq A[u],
$$
by <uniform ellipticity>. This is the <Dirichlet principle> for the stated symmetric energy.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

We prove existence directly through <quadratic energy minimization by the parallelogram law>. Let $m=\inf_{v\in H_g^1}A[v]$. The set contains $g$, and <uniform ellipticity> gives $0\leq m\leq A[g]<\infty$. Choose a <minimizing sequence> $v_j$ with $A[v_j]\to m$. Since $(v_j+v_k)/2\in H_g^1$, the <parallelogram law> gives
$$
A[v_j-v_k]=2A[v_j]+2A[v_k]-4A[(v_j+v_k)/2]\leq2A[v_j]+2A[v_k]-4m\longrightarrow0.
$$
The differences belong to $H_0^1$, so <uniform ellipticity> and the <Poincare inequality> imply
$$
\|v_j-v_k\|_{H^1}^2\leq\frac{1+C_P^2}{\lambda}A[v_j-v_k]\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus $(v_j)$ is a <Cauchy sequence>. Completeness of $H_0^1$, applied to $v_j-g$, produces $u\in H_g^1$ with $v_j\to u$ in $H^1$. The bounded coefficient matrix makes $a$ a <bounded bilinear form>, so $A[v_j]\to A[u]$. Consequently $A[u]=m$, and part (i) makes $u$ a <weak solution>.

For uniqueness, the difference $w$ of two <weak solutions> lies in $H_0^1$ and satisfies $a(w,w)=0$. <Uniform ellipticity> gives $\nabla w=0$ almost everywhere, and the <Poincare inequality> gives $w=0$. The argument proves existence and uniqueness without assuming the <Lax-Milgram theorem>.

For one coordinate, <integration by parts> gives $\int_\Omega(\partial_ju)\varphi=-\int_\Omega u\,\partial_j\varphi$: the <test function> vanishes outside a compact subset of $\Omega$, so there is no boundary contribution. Repeat this operation once for each of the $|\alpha|$ coordinate differentiations in the <multi-index> $\alpha$. Each operation contributes a factor $-1$, giving
$$
\boxed{\int_\Omega D^\alpha u\,\varphi\,dx=(-1)^{|\alpha|}\int_\Omega uD^\alpha\varphi\,dx.}
$$
All intermediate functions are continuous on the relevant compact support, since $u\in C^k$ and $|\alpha|\leq k$.

The function $v$ is the <weak derivative> $D^\alpha u$ if

The <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations> says that if $h\in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)$ and $\int h\varphi=0$ for every $\varphi\in C_0^\infty(\Omega)$, then $h=0$ <almost everywhere>.

If $v_1,v_2$ both represent $D^\alpha u$, subtracting their defining identities gives $\int(v_1-v_2)\varphi=0$ for every <test function>. The lemma gives $v_1=v_2$ <almost everywhere>, proving uniqueness. If $u\in C^1$, part (i) gives exactly the defining identity with $v=\partial_ju$. Since this classical derivative is locally integrable, it is the unique <weak derivative> $D_ju$, up to equality <almost everywhere>.

= Solution

Write $g=(g_1,\ldots,g_n)$ for the continuous representatives of the <weak derivatives>. Integrating the preceding identity over $B=B(0,1)$ gives
$$
|B|u_\varepsilon(x)=\int_Bu_\varepsilon(y)\,dy+\int_B\int_0^1(\rho_\varepsilon*g)(y+t(x-y))\cdot(x-y)\,dt\,dy.
$$
The first term converges to $\int_Bu$ because $u_\varepsilon\to u$ in $L^1$. For fixed $x$, all segments in the second term lie in a compact set. Continuity of $g$ implies $\rho_\varepsilon*g\to g$ uniformly on that set. Hence every $x$ has the finite limit
$$
U(x)=\frac1{|B|}\left(\int_Bu(y)\,dy+\int_B\int_0^1g(y+t(x-y))\cdot(x-y)\,dt\,dy\right).
$$
The same argument is uniform for $x$ in any fixed compact set: all the segments then lie in a common compact set. Thus $u_\varepsilon\to U$ locally uniformly, so $U$ is continuous; the local $L^1$ convergence also gives $U=u$ <almost everywhere>.

Passing to the limit in the segment identity, and taking its endpoints to be $x$ and $x+h$, gives
$$
U(x+h)-U(x)-g(x)\cdot h=\int_0^1[g(x+th)-g(x)]\cdot h\,dt=o(|h|).
$$
Therefore $U$ is <differentiable> with continuous <gradient> $g$: $\boxed{U\in C^1,\quad U=u\text{ almost everywhere},\quad\nabla U=g}$. This is the <continuous weak gradient gives a C1 representative> criterion.

The real <Lax-Milgram theorem> states: let $H$ be a <Hilbert space> and let $a:H\times H\to\mathbb R$ be a <bounded bilinear form> such that $a(v,v)\geq\alpha\|v\|_H^2$ for some $\alpha>0$ and every $v\in H$. For every bounded <linear functional> $\ell\in H^*$ there exists a unique $u\in H$ satisfying
$$
\boxed{a(u,v)=\ell(v)\quad(v\in H),\qquad\|u\|_H\leq\alpha^{-1}\|\ell\|_{H^*}.}
$$
Symmetry of $a$ is not required. The complex version replaces bilinearity by sesquilinearity and the lower bound by $\operatorname{Re}a(v,v)\geq\alpha\|v\|_H^2$.

Introduce coordinates $s=x+y$, $t=x-y$, so $dx\,dy=\tfrac12ds\,dt$. For a <test function> $\varphi(x,y)=\Phi(s,t)$,
$$
V\cdot\nabla\varphi=\mathbf1_{s>0}(\Phi_s+\Phi_t)+\mathbf1_{s<0}(\Phi_s-\Phi_t)=\Phi_s+\operatorname{sgn}(s)\Phi_t
$$
<almost everywhere>. Hence
$$
\int_{\mathbb R^2}V\cdot\nabla\varphi=\frac12\int_{\mathbb R^2}\bigl(\Phi_s+\operatorname{sgn}(s)\Phi_t\bigr)\,ds\,dt=0.
$$
The first derivative integrates to zero in $s$, and the second to zero in $t$, using compact support. Thus $\boxed{\operatorname{div}V=0}$ as a <weak divergence>. Geometrically, the jump across $x+y=0$ is $(1,-1)$, perpendicular to its normal $(1,1)$; there is no distributional flux concentrated on the interface.

Give $H_0^1$ the equivalent <Hilbert space> norm $\|u\|_H=\|\nabla u\|_2$, using the <Poincare inequality>. Define
$$
a(u,v)=\int_\Omega\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+\int_\Omega(b\cdot\nabla u)v,\qquad\ell(v)=\int_\Omega fv.
$$
The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives $|a(u,v)|\leq(1+C_P\|b\|_\infty)\|u\|_H\|v\|_H$ and $|\ell(v)|\leq C_P\|f\|_2\|v\|_H$.

For $\varphi\in C_0^\infty(\Omega)$, the definition of <weak divergence>, applied to $\varphi^2/2$, gives
$$
\int_\Omega(b\cdot\nabla\varphi)\varphi=-\frac12\int_\Omega(\operatorname{div}b)\varphi^2\geq0.
$$
The left side is continuous in the $H_0^1$ norm. Approximate any $u\in H_0^1$ by <test functions> and pass to the limit on the left to obtain $\int(b\cdot\nabla u)u\geq0$. This density argument is essential: local integrability of $\operatorname{div}b$ alone does not justify treating $(\operatorname{div}b)u^2$ as integrable for arbitrary $u\in H_0^1$.

Consequently $a(u,u)\geq\|u\|_H^2$, so $a$ is a <coercive bilinear form>. The <Lax-Milgram theorem> supplies a unique <weak solution> with $\boxed{\|\nabla u\|_2\leq C_P\|f\|_2}$. The reusable estimate is <coercivity from a drift with nonpositive weak divergence>.

The <Brouwer fixed-point theorem> says that every continuous self-map of a closed ball in $\mathbb R^n$ has a <fixed point>.

Suppose $g$ has no zero in $\overline B_R$. Then
$$
T(x)=-R\frac{g(x)}{|g(x)|}
$$
is a continuous map from $\overline B_R$ into its boundary, hence into $\overline B_R$. The <Brouwer fixed-point theorem> gives $T(x_0)=x_0$. This forces $|x_0|=R$ and
$$
g(x_0)\cdot x_0=-R|g(x_0)|<0,
$$
contradicting the assumed boundary inequality. Therefore $\boxed{g(x_0)=0\text{ for some }|x_0|\leq R}$. This is the <outward vector field zero theorem>.

= Solution

This is the <fixed point theorem for summably Lipschitz iterates>. Put $x_n=A^nx_0$ and $d=\|x_1-x_0\|$. For $n\geq1$,
$$
\|x_{n+1}-x_n\|=\|A^nx_1-A^nx_0\|\leq\alpha_nd.
$$
Consequently, for $m>n\geq1$,
$$
\|x_m-x_n\|\leq d\sum_{k=n}^{m-1}\alpha_k.
$$
Summability makes the tails tend to zero, so $(x_n)$ is a <Cauchy sequence>. Since $M$ is closed in a <Banach space>, $x_n\to x\in M$. The $n=1$ hypothesis makes $A$ a <Lipschitz map>, hence continuous; passing to the limit in $x_{n+1}=Ax_n$ gives $x=Ax$.

If $y$ is another <fixed point>, then $\|x-y\|\leq\alpha_n\|x-y\|$ for every $n$. Since $\alpha_n\to0$, eventually $\alpha_n<1$ and thus $x=y$. Finally let $m\to\infty$ in the tail estimate:
$$
\boxed{\|x-x_n\|\leq\left(\sum_{k\geq n}\alpha_k\right)\|x_1-x_0\|\quad(n\geq1).}
$$
The map itself need not be a <contraction mapping>; its successive iterates provide the summable error bound.

The space $C([a,b])$ with the <supremum norm> is a <Banach space>. Continuity of the kernel makes $K$ a <bounded linear operator>. Induction on $n$ gives
$$
A^ny=f+Kf+\cdots+K^{n-1}f+K^ny,
$$
so $A^ny-A^nz=K^n(y-z)$. The ordered integration region gives the <factorial bound for a Volterra iterate>:
$$
|(K^ny)(t)|\leq\frac{M^n(t-a)^n}{n!}\|y\|_\infty.
$$
For completeness, if this holds at $n$, then integrating $M\,M^n(s-a)^n/n!$ from $a$ to $t$ gives the bound at $n+1$; the initial case is the defining integral. Thus
$$
\|A^ny-A^nz\|_\infty\leq\alpha_n\|y-z\|_\infty,\qquad \alpha_n=\frac{[M(b-a)]^n}{n!},
$$
and $\sum_{n\geq1}\alpha_n=e^{M(b-a)}-1<\infty$. Part (b) now gives uniform convergence of every iteration sequence to the unique <fixed point>. Since $y=Ay$ is precisely the <Volterra integral equation>, this is its unique continuous solution. No restriction $M(b-a)<1$ is needed: the factorial controls all iterates on the whole finite interval.

A <weak solution> is $u\in H_0^1(\Omega)$ such that
$$
\int_\Omega\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi=\int_\Omega f(u)\varphi\qquad(\varphi\in H_0^1(\Omega)).
$$
Let $M_f=\|f\|_\infty$ and define the <Nemytskii operator> $N(v)=f\circ v$ on $L^2(\Omega)$. It satisfies $\|N(v)\|_2\leq M_f|\Omega|^{1/2}$. It is continuous in $L^2$: from any sequence $v_j\to v$ in $L^2$, every subsequence has a further subsequence converging <almost everywhere>; continuity and boundedness of $f$, followed by the <dominated convergence theorem>, give convergence of the corresponding $N(v_j)$ in $L^2$. This subsequence criterion proves continuity of the whole sequence.

For $h\in L^2$, the <Lax-Milgram theorem> defines the inverse Dirichlet solution map $Sh\in H_0^1$ by $\int\nabla(Sh)\cdot\nabla\varphi=\int h\varphi$. Testing with $Sh$ and using the <Poincare inequality> yields
$$
\|\nabla Sh\|_2\leq C_P\|h\|_2,\qquad\|Sh\|_2\leq C_P^2\|h\|_2.
$$
The <Rellich-Kondrachov compactness theorem> makes $S:L^2\to L^2$ compact. Therefore $T=S\circ N$ is a <nonlinear compact operator> and its image lies in the closed $L^2$ ball of radius $R=1+C_P^2M_f|\Omega|^{1/2}$. This ball is nonempty, closed, bounded and convex. The <Schauder fixed-point theorem> gives $u=Tu$. Since $Tu\in H_0^1$, its defining identity is exactly the required <weak formulation>. Thus **a weak solution exists**. This is the <bounded semilinear Dirichlet existence theorem>.

The displayed nonlinearity is singular at $s=-1$, so it cannot be used directly as the globally bounded function in part (b). On $s\geq0$, however,
$$
\frac12\leq f(s)=\cosh\!\left(\frac1{1+s}\right)-\frac12\sin\sqrt{1+s^2}\leq\cosh(1)+\frac12.

The <Poincare inequality> gives $u_-=0$. Hence $u\geq0$, $F(u)=f(u)$, and the denominator $1+u$ stays at least one <almost everywhere>. Thus **the original equation has a nonnegative weak solution**, with bounded right side.

Using the <dual pairing> between $X^*$ and $X$, a <monotone operator> satisfies
$$
\boxed{\langle A(u)-A(v),u-v\rangle\geq0\quad(u,v\in M).}
$$
A <nonlinear coercive operator> with respect to $u_0$ satisfies
$$
\boxed{\frac{\langle A(u),u-u_0\rangle}{\|u-u_0\|}\longrightarrow+\infty\quad\text{as }u\in M,\ \|u-u_0\|\to\infty.}
$$
The <directional derivative> is $\partial_vF(u)=\lim_{t\to0}[F(u+tv)-F(u)]/t$, when this finite limit exists through admissible points. At a boundary point of $M$ only an inward direction may be admissible; then one uses the corresponding one-sided limit.

If $A:X\to X^*$ is monotone and $s<t$, apply its defining inequality at $u+tv$ and $u+sv$. It becomes $(t-s)[\varphi(t)-\varphi(s)]\geq0$, so $\varphi$ is nondecreasing. Conversely choose $u=y$, $v=x-y$. Nondecrease from zero to one gives $\langle A(x)-A(y),x-y\rangle\geq0$. This proves the <line criterion for a monotone operator>.

The <graph area functional> is finite on $H^1$, since $\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^2}\leq1+|\nabla u|$ and $\Omega$ has finite volume. For $p=\nabla u$, $q=\nabla v$, the difference quotient of $\sqrt{1+|p+tq|^2}$ is bounded in absolute value by $|q|$. This is integrable by the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. The <dominated convergence theorem> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\partial_vF(u)=\langle A(u),v\rangle=\int_\Omega\frac{\nabla u\cdot\nabla v}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^2}}\,dx.}
$$
This defines a bounded <linear functional> of $v$, since $|\langle A(u),v\rangle|\leq|\Omega|^{1/2}\|\nabla v\|_2$.

For $a(p)=p/\sqrt{1+|p|^2}$ the <Jacobian matrix> is
$$
Da(p)=\frac{I}{\sqrt{1+|p|^2}}-\frac{pp^T}{(1+|p|^2)^{3/2}},\qquad
q^TDa(p)q=\frac{(1+|p|^2)|q|^2-(p\cdot q)^2}{(1+|p|^2)^{3/2}}\geq0.
$$
The inequality follows from the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>. Integrating this derivative along the segment between any two gradients shows $(a(p)-a(r))\cdot(p-r)\geq0$. Integrating over $\Omega$ proves that $A$ is a <monotone operator>.

It is **not coercive on $H^1(\Omega)$**. The constant functions $u_k\equiv k$ satisfy $\|u_k\|_{H^1}=k|\Omega|^{1/2}\to\infty$ but $A(u_k)=0$. Thus $\langle A(u_k),u_k\rangle/\|u_k\|_{H^1}=0$.

= Solution

The operator is **well-defined in every dimension**: $|\cos w|\leq1$ gives
$$
|\langle A(w,u),v\rangle|\leq\|\nabla u\|_2\|\nabla v\|_2\leq\|u\|_{H^1}\|v\|_{H^1}.
$$
For fixed $w$, its monotonicity condition is
$$
\langle A(w,u)-A(w,z),u-z\rangle=\int_\Omega\cos(w)|\nabla(u-z)|^2\geq0.
$$
Thus $\cos w\geq0$ <almost everywhere> is sufficient. It is also necessary, by the <positivity criterion for a scalar gradient form>. Indeed, if the bounded coefficient $c=\cos w$ is negative on a set of positive measure, choose a <Lebesgue point> $x_0$ with $c(x_0)<0$ and a nonconstant compactly supported smooth $\psi$. Set $z_\varepsilon(x)=\varepsilon\psi((x-x_0)/\varepsilon)$, supported inside $\Omega$. Then
$$
\varepsilon^{-n}\int_\Omega c(x)|\nabla z_\varepsilon(x)|^2\,dx\longrightarrow c(x_0)\int|\nabla\psi|^2<0,
$$
contradicting monotonicity. Therefore $\boxed{A(w,\cdot)\text{ is monotone exactly when }\cos w\geq0\text{ almost everywhere}}$. In particular it is not monotone for every $w$: the admissible constant $w\equiv\pi$ makes it the negative Dirichlet form.

In dimension one, the <Sobolev embedding theorem> gives $H^1(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^\infty(\Omega)$. Therefore
$$
|\langle A(w,u),v\rangle|\leq\|w\|_\infty^2\|\nabla u\|_2\|\nabla v\|_2,
$$
and, for fixed $w$,
$$
\langle A(w,u)-A(w,z),u-z\rangle=\int_\Omega w^2|\nabla(u-z)|^2\geq0.
$$
Hence **the operator is well-defined and monotone in one dimension**.

In every dimension $n\geq2$ it is **not well-defined on all of $H^1\times H^1$**. We exhibit $w=u=v\in H^1$ for which the purported pairing $\int w^2|\nabla w|^2$ is infinite. Choose a ball compactly contained in $\Omega$, put $r=|x-x_0|$, and multiply each following radial expression by a smooth <cutoff function> that equals one near $r=0$.

For $n\geq3$, set $w=r^{-\alpha}$ with $\alpha=(n-2)/3$. The local <Sobolev norm> is finite because
$$
\int_0^\delta r^{n-1-2\alpha}\,dr<\infty,\qquad\int_0^\delta r^{n-3-2\alpha}\,dr<\infty,
$$
whereas
$$
\int_{B_\delta}w^2|\nabla w|^2\,dx\asymp\int_0^\delta r^{n-3-4\alpha}\,dr=\infty,
$$
since $n-3-4\alpha=(-n-1)/3<-1$.

For $n=2$, take $w=(\log(1/r))^{1/3}$ on a sufficiently small ball. With $s=\log(1/r)$, the gradient integral is proportional to $\int^\infty s^{-4/3}\,ds<\infty$, while the weighted integral is proportional to $\int^\infty s^{-2/3}\,ds=\infty$. Its $L^2$ integral is finite because the area factor contributes exponential decay in $s$. These singular functions have the indicated <weak derivatives>; deleting a small central ball and passing to the limit in <integration by parts> leaves no boundary term.

Thus $w^2\nabla u\cdot\nabla v$ need not be integrable in dimensions at least two. This is the <squared Sobolev gradient counterexample>. With the additional restriction $w\in L^\infty$, the form is well-defined and monotone in any dimension.
