= Wave
{wiki}

A wave is a propagating or standing disturbance that can carry <energy> and momentum. Its oscillations are described by quantities such as <wavelength>, <frequency>, and <wave speed>.

= Pulse
{parent=Wave}

A pulse is a localized disturbance that passes a fixed location during a bounded or effectively bounded time interval. A <travelling wave> pulse may be written $y(x,t)=f(x-ct)$ with localized $f$. Its width and peak <amplitude> describe its extent and size; a <wave packet> additionally emphasizes a carrier and an envelope.

= Mode conversion
{parent=Wave}

Transfer of <wave energy> between distinct local branches when spatial variation couples their <polarizations> or their <dispersion relations> approach one another. Loss from one observed branch need not mean dissipation: <conservation of energy> includes every outgoing branch.

= Spherical wave
{parent=Wave}
{title2=$e^{\pm ikr}/r$}

For time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$, the three-dimensional radial solutions $e^{ikr}/r$ and $e^{-ikr}/r$ describe outgoing and incoming <spherical waves> away from the origin. Their inverse-radius amplitudes make their flux through spheres independent of radius. They form the asymptotic radial ingredients of <partial-wave scattering>.

= Waveguide
{parent=Wave}
{wiki}

A <waveguide> confines a wave transversely while allowing propagation along a longitudinal direction. Material properties and <boundary conditions> select transverse <normal modes>, whose longitudinal <dispersion relations> may have cutoffs even when the bulk material is nondispersive. Examples include an <optical fiber>, a hollow <electromagnetic waveguide>, and a <membrane waveguide>. Guiding reduces geometric spreading along the chosen route but does not eliminate absorption, scattering or leakage.

= Radiation condition for a waveguide mode
{parent=Waveguide}
{title2=$\Phi_x-iq\Phi\to0$}

For a rightward <acoustic waveguide mode> with time convention $e^{-i\omega t}$, $\omega>0$ and real $q>0$, the displayed condition selects $e^{iqx}$ and excludes waves incident from positive infinity. If $q=i\alpha$ with $\alpha>0$, the corresponding selection is the decaying factor $e^{-\alpha x}$. Boundedness alone cannot distinguish outgoing and incoming propagating waves, while requiring decay for every frequency would incorrectly remove the propagating response. The condition must match the chosen temporal phase convention.

= Acoustic waveguide mode
{parent=Waveguide}
{title2=$\phi=\Phi(x)\cos(n\pi y/H)e^{-i\omega t}$}

A separated <acoustic wave> in a straight rigid channel $0<y<H$ has transverse <Neumann boundary conditions>, giving factors $\cos(n\pi y/H)$ for integers $n\geq0$. The <velocity potential> satisfies the <wave equation>, and its axial factor obeys
$$
\Phi''+q_n^2\Phi=0,\qquad q_n^2=\omega^2/c^2-(n\pi/H)^2.
$$
Here $c$ is the <sound speed>. Real $q_n$ permits propagation; positive imaginary $q_n$ produces an <evanescent wave>. A prescribed wall <velocity> proportional to one transverse factor excites that mode alone in the linear straight-channel model. The physical field is the <real part> of the complex expression.

= Acoustic waveguide cutoff
{parent=Acoustic waveguide mode}
{title2=$\omega_{c,n}=n\pi c/H$}

For an <acoustic waveguide mode> with transverse index $n\geq1$, the <dispersion relation> $\omega^2=c^2[q^2+(n\pi/H)^2]$ permits a propagating axial <wavenumber> only above the displayed <angular frequency>. Below it the outgoing bounded response decays as $e^{-\alpha x}$, with $\alpha=\sqrt{(n\pi/H)^2-\omega^2/c^2}$. The $n=0$ mode has no positive cutoff. At cutoff, a nonzero imposed axial derivative requires $\Phi=A+Bx$, so no bounded periodic solution exists on a semi-infinite channel. Near cutoff, fixed wall-velocity forcing gives amplitudes proportional to $1/|q|$, which can invalidate <linearization>.

= Chirp
{parent=Wave}

A <chirp> is a signal whose instantaneous <frequency> changes with time.

= Transverse wave
{parent=Wave}
{wiki}

A <transverse wave> has its oscillating <vector> perpendicular to the direction of propagation. In a plane <electromagnetic wave> in vacuum, the <electric field> and <magnetic field> are transverse; their orientation is described by <polarization of waves>.

= Wave travel time on a hanging uniform cable
{parent=Transverse wave}
{title2=$t=2\sqrt{L/g}$}

At distance $x$ below the support of a flexible uniform cable of length $L$, the equilibrium <tension> is $\mu g(L-x)$, with <linear mass density> $\mu$. The local transverse speed is $\sqrt{g(L-x)}$. Integrating its reciprocal from zero to $L$ gives the displayed travel time. The speed vanishes at the free end, but the travel-time singularity is integrable. With variable <tension>, the small-amplitude <wave equation> is $\mu y_{tt}=\partial_x(Ty_x)$; its characteristic speed is still $\sqrt{T/\mu}$.

= Longitudinal wave
{parent=Wave}
{wiki}

A <longitudinal wave> has an oscillating <displacement> parallel to the direction of propagation. An <acoustic wave> in a fluid supplies a familiar example.

= Compressional wave
{parent=Longitudinal wave}
{title2=$c_p=\sqrt{(\lambda+2\mu)/\rho}$}

A compressional wave is a <longitudinal wave> whose propagation produces changes of volume or density. In an isotropic elastic solid its <displacement> is parallel to the <wavevector>; substituting that polarization into the dynamic <Navier-Cauchy equation> gives the displayed speed. In a fluid without static shear stiffness the analogous speed is $\sqrt{K/\rho}$, with the appropriate compressional <bulk modulus>. Reflection at a rigid elastic boundary can convert part of its energy into a <shear wave>.

= Polarization
{synonym}

<Polarization of waves> describes the direction and time-dependent geometry of a <transverse wave>'s <vector> during its <oscillation> at a fixed position. In three spatial dimensions, a <transverse wave> can have two independent directions of <oscillation>, both perpendicular to its direction of propagation. For <polarization of an electromagnetic wave>, the relevant <vector> is the transverse <electric field>; for a mechanical <transverse wave>, it can be the transverse <displacement>. A purely <longitudinal wave> has no choice between these transverse directions, and the orientation of a static <electric field> is not <polarization of waves>.

Choose transverse axes $x,y$ in a <Cartesian coordinate system>. A <monochromatic wave> with a fixed <phase difference> has the real <electric field>
$$
\mathbf E(t)=\bigl(A_x\cos\omega t,\ A_y\cos(\omega t+\delta)\bigr),\qquad A_x,A_y\geq0,\quad\omega>0.
$$
Here $A_x,A_y$ are <wave amplitudes>, $\omega$ is the <angular frequency>, and $\delta$ is the <phase difference> of the $y$ <oscillation> relative to the $x$ <oscillation>. The <vector>'s tip traces its path during the <wave period> $T=2\pi/\omega$. Both plotted coordinates are real <electric field> components; neither axis represents the <imaginary part> of a <complex amplitude>.

For nonzero <wave amplitudes>, $\delta=0$ or $\pi$ modulo $2\pi$ gives <linear polarization>: the <vector> moves back and forth along a fixed line. Equal nonzero <wave amplitudes> and $\delta=\pm\pi/2$ modulo $2\pi$ give <circular polarization>: the tip traces a <circle> of constant <radius>. Unequal nonzero <wave amplitudes> with $\delta=\pm\pi/2$ give a noncircular <ellipse> whose axes coincide with $x,y$. More generally, nonzero <wave amplitudes> and $\sin\delta\neq0$ give <elliptical polarization>, with <circular polarization> as a special case. If exactly one <wave amplitude> vanishes, the nonzero <wave> has <linear polarization> for every $\delta$; if both vanish, there is no nonzero <wave> to assign a <polarization of waves>. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_33.html#Ch33-S1[Feynman Lectures, I §33–1: the electric vector of light]{ref}.

For example, take $\omega=2\pi\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, so the <wave period> is $T=1\,\mathrm{s}$. The choices $(A_x,A_y,\delta)=(2,1,0)$, $(1,1,\pi/2)$ and $(2,1,\pi/2)$, with <electric field> <wave amplitudes> in $\mathrm{V/m}$, produce respectively the <line segment> $E_y=E_x/2$, the <circle> $E_x^2+E_y^2=1$ and the <ellipse> $E_x^2/4+E_y^2=1$, when coordinates are measured in $\mathrm{V/m}$. At $t=T/4$, their tips are respectively $(0,0)$, $(0,-1)$ and $(0,-1)\,\mathrm{V/m}$. The last two share a point without having the same path.

Resolving the <electric field> into two transverse components makes <polarization of waves> useful: the same <frequency> and <wave amplitudes> can describe different paths when their <phase difference> changes. An ideal linear <polarizer> selects the component along its pass axis, letting its orientation probe the direction of <linear polarization> through <Malus's law>. A fixed pair of <wave amplitudes> and a fixed <phase difference> describe one stable path; <unpolarized light> requires a statistical description rather than one fixed <ellipse>.

= Pure polarization
{parent=Polarization (waves)}

<Pure polarization> means that the transverse components of a light field have a fixed complex amplitude ratio. A single <Jones vector> describes the state; its <coherency matrix> has rank one. Removing overall intensity and a common phase leaves two real parameters, which determine its <polarization ellipse>.

= Degree of polarization
{parent=Polarization (waves)}
{wiki}
{title2=$\mathcal P=(\mu_1-\mu_2)/(\mu_1+\mu_2)$}

The <degree of polarization> quantifies how strongly a transverse optical field departs from an unpolarized mixture. Here $\mu_1\geq\mu_2\geq0$ are the <eigenvalues> of its <coherency matrix>. For two mutually uncorrelated orthogonal linear components of intensities $I_1,I_2$, $\mathcal P=|I_1-I_2|/(I_1+I_2)$. It is undefined when the total <light intensity> is zero. A signed difference is a separate convention that also identifies a preferred polarization direction.

= Coherency matrix
{parent=Polarization (waves)}
{title2=$C_{ij}=\langle E_iE_j^*\rangle$}

The optical <coherency matrix> records the second moments of transverse <complex amplitudes>. Normalize its trace to <light intensity>. Under a deterministic <Jones matrix> $M$, $C\mapsto MCM^\dagger$. <Unpolarized light> has $C=(I_0/2)I_2$, so its output intensity is $(I_0/2)\operatorname{tr}(MM^\dagger)$; it cannot be represented by one fixed <Jones vector>.

= Jones matrix
{c}
{parent=Jones calculus}
{title2=$\mathbf J_{\rm out}=M\mathbf J_{\rm in}$}

A <Jones matrix> is a complex two-by-two linear transformation of a <Jones vector> through a deterministic nondepolarizing optical element. Successive elements multiply in reverse reading order. An ideal linear <polarizer> along a real transverse <unit vector> $\mathbf a$ has matrix $\mathbf a\mathbf a^{\mathsf T}$. A common nonzero scalar phase does not change the output <polarization of waves>.

= Circular-eigenmode Jones propagator
{parent=Jones matrix}
{title2=$\mathcal J(z)=e^{-\gamma^+z}P_++e^{-\gamma^-z}P_-$}

For fixed circular <eigenvectors> $\mathbf u_\pm=(1,\mp i)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt2$, let $P_\pm=\mathbf u_\pm\mathbf u_\pm^\dagger$. A uniform optical medium with amplitude propagation coefficients $\gamma^\pm$ has the displayed <Jones matrix>. Put $\gamma=(\gamma^++\gamma^-)/2$ and $\delta=(\gamma^+-\gamma^-)/2$. Then
$$
\mathcal J(z)=e^{-\gamma z}
\begin{pmatrix}\cosh(\delta z)&-i\sinh(\delta z)\\i\sinh(\delta z)&\cosh(\delta z)\end{pmatrix}
=e^{-Kz},\qquad K=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma&i\delta\\-i\delta&\gamma\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The sum of the two modal exponentials supplies a hyperbolic cosine, not its reciprocal. The <determinant> is $e^{-2\gamma z}$, and $\mathcal J(z_1+z_2)=\mathcal J(z_1)\mathcal J(z_2)$. Real modal coefficients give <circular dichroism>; purely imaginary coefficients give lossless <circular birefringence>. Complex modal labels do not have an intrinsic numerical ordering.

= Jones vector
{c}
{parent=Jones calculus}
{title2=$\mathbf J=(J_x,J_y)^{\mathsf T}$}

A <Jones vector> records the two transverse <complex amplitudes> of a completely polarized <monochromatic wave>. Multiplication by a common nonzero complex number preserves its <polarization of waves>, while changing overall amplitude and phase. With the convention $\boldsymbol\Psi(t)=\operatorname{Re}[\mathbf J e^{i\omega t}]$, $\mathbf J=(1,2i)^{\mathsf T}$ gives $(\cos\omega t,-2\sin\omega t)$, an <ellipse> with the longer axis along $y$. Handedness labels require both a viewing direction and a naming convention.

= Jones-component ratio sensitivity
{parent=Jones vector}
{title2=$r'(0)=[J'_y(0)J_x(0)-J_y(0)J'_x(0)]/J_x(0)^2$}
{c}

For a differentiable <Jones vector> with $J_x(0)\ne0$, the ratio $r=J_y/J_x$ fixes <polarization> independently of common <amplitude> and <phase>. The title quotient derivative tests first-order <polarization> sensitivity. If $\mathbf J'(0)=a\mathbf J(0)$ for a complex scalar $a$, then $r'(0)=0$. For a lossless normalized output, differentiation of $\mathbf J^\dagger\mathbf J=1$ makes $\operatorname{Re}a=0$, so this change is a common <phase>. A vanishing component requires the complementary ratio or a basis-independent <polarization> coordinate instead.

= Elliptical polarization
{parent=Polarization (waves)}
{wiki}
{title2=$E_x^2/A_x^2+E_y^2/A_y^2-2E_xE_y\cos\delta/(A_xA_y)=\sin^2\delta$}

= Elliptically polarized wave
{synonym}

For an <elliptically polarized wave>, the tip of its transverse <vector> traces an <ellipse> at a fixed point during each <wave period>. For <polarization of an electromagnetic wave>, this is the <electric field> <vector>. With the real components in <polarization of waves>, assume $A_x,A_y>0$ and $\sin\delta\neq0$. Eliminating time gives
$$
\frac{E_x^2}{A_x^2}+\frac{E_y^2}{A_y^2}-\frac{2E_xE_y}{A_xA_y}\cos\delta=\sin^2\delta.
$$
A short derivation uses $\phi=\omega t$ and the <cosine> addition formula to obtain $E_y/A_y=(E_x/A_x)\cos\delta-\sin\phi\sin\delta$; squaring and using the <sine> and <cosine> identity $\sin^2\phi=1-\cos^2\phi$ gives the displayed equation. Outside <phase quadrature>, the mixed term gives a tilted <ellipse>; $A_x,A_y$ are component <wave amplitudes> and need not be its <semi-major and semi-minor axes>.

In <phase quadrature>, $\delta=\pm\pi/2$, the mixed term vanishes. For $(A_x,A_y)=(2,1)\,\mathrm{V/m}$, the <ellipse> is $E_x^2/4+E_y^2=1$, its <semi-major and semi-minor axes> have lengths $2$ and $1\,\mathrm{V/m}$, and its <electric field> magnitude varies between $1$ and $2\,\mathrm{V/m}$. At $\phi=\pi/4$ with $\delta=\pi/2$, the <electric field> is $(\sqrt2,-1/\sqrt2)\,\mathrm{V/m}$ and has magnitude $\sqrt{5/2}\,\mathrm{V/m}$. Equal <wave amplitudes> instead give <circular polarization>, a special case of <elliptical polarization> with constant magnitude. As $\delta$ approaches $0$ or $\pi$, the <ellipse> collapses to a <line segment> and gives <linear polarization>; this limit is degenerate rather than a nondegenerate <ellipse>.

Changing $\delta$ from $\pi/2$ to $-\pi/2$ reverses traversal of the same <ellipse>. In the displayed $x,y$ plot, $\delta=\pi/2$ moves from $(A_x,0)$ toward negative $y$ as time increases. A label such as right- or left-handed <circular polarization> additionally requires a stated viewing direction and naming convention: viewing along propagation and viewing against propagation reverse the apparent rotation. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_33.html#Ch33-S1[Feynman Lectures, I §33–1: polarization conventions]{ref}.

The <ellipse> records both the relative <wave amplitudes> and the <phase difference> of two transverse <oscillations>. This separates a change of <polarization of waves> from a change of overall <wave amplitude>: multiplying both components by the same positive factor enlarges the <ellipse> while preserving its shape and orientation.

A rotating ideal linear <polarizer> can probe this geometry. For the $2:1$ <ellipse> in <phase quadrature>, <orthogonal projection> onto a pass axis at <angle> $\theta$ gives $E_{\mathrm{out}}(t)=2\cos\theta\cos\omega t-\sin\theta\sin\omega t$, in $\mathrm{V/m}$. Averaging its square over a <wave period> gives $(4\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta)/2$, while the incoming squared <electric field> magnitude averages to $5/2$. Thus the transmitted fraction of <light intensity> is $(4\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta)/5$: it is $4/5$ at $0^\circ$, $1/2$ at $45^\circ$ and $1/5$ at $90^\circ$. There is no single incident line of <linear polarization> to insert into <Malus's law>; projecting the <electric field> and averaging its square is the appropriate generalization. This measurement cannot distinguish $\delta=\pi/2$ from $\delta=-\pi/2$, because reversing the traversal leaves all these squared averages unchanged.

= Elliptical polarization from circular and linear components
{parent=Elliptical polarization}

Two coherent waves with common propagation direction and <frequency> can combine into a <polarization ellipse>. Take circular components $E_x=A\cos\psi$, $E_y=A\sin\psi$ and add $B\cos\psi$ along $x$. The resulting semiaxes are $|A+B|$ and $|A|$. Choosing positive $A,B$ gives a nondegenerate ellipse with unequal axes. Uncorrelated relative phases do not describe the same fixed pure polarization.

= Polarization ellipse
{parent=Elliptical polarization}

The <polarization ellipse> is the curve traced by the transverse <electric field> of a monochromatic wave at a fixed point. Its orientation and signed ratio of minor to major semiaxis specify a <pure polarization>. A zero minor semiaxis gives <linear polarization>, and equal semiaxes give <circular polarization>.

= Malus's law
{c}
{parent=Polarization (waves)}
{title2=$I_{\mathrm{out}}=I_{\mathrm{in}}\cos^2\theta$}

For incident <light> with <linear polarization>, an ideal linear <polarizer> transmits
$$
I_{\mathrm{out}}=I_{\mathrm{in}}\cos^2\theta,
$$
where $I$ is <light intensity> and $\theta$ is the <angle> between the incident <electric field>'s line of <oscillation> and the <polarizer>'s pass axis. The input and output are compared in the same medium. The classical explanation is an <orthogonal projection>: for a <unit vector> $\mathbf u$ along the pass axis,
$$
\mathbf E_{\mathrm{out}}=(\mathbf E_{\mathrm{in}}\mathbin{\cdot}\mathbf u)\mathbf u.
$$
The <dot product> reduces the transmitted <wave amplitude> by $\cos\theta$. For a <plane electromagnetic wave> in the same medium, <light intensity> is proportional to squared <electric field> <wave amplitude>, giving <Malus's law>. For example, $\theta=60^\circ$ transmits one quarter of the incident <light intensity>, although the transmitted <wave amplitude> is one half. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_33.html#Ch33-S4[Feynman Lectures, I §33–4: polarizers]{ref}.

Start with <light> already in <linear polarization> along the horizontal $0^\circ$ axis and let its <light intensity> be $I_0$. Two ideal absorbing linear <polarizers> with pass axes $0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$ give $I_0\cos^2 0^\circ\cos^2 90^\circ=0$. Insert a $45^\circ$ <polarizer> between them: the first leaves $I_0$ unchanged, the middle transmits $I_0/2$ with <linear polarization> along $45^\circ$, and the last transmits $I_0/4$ with <linear polarization> along $90^\circ$. Each <angle> in <Malus's law> is relative to the preceding output, so the final step uses $90^\circ-45^\circ=45^\circ$:
$$
I_{\mathrm{out}}=I_0\cos^2 0^\circ\cos^2 45^\circ\cos^2(90^\circ-45^\circ)=\frac{I_0}{4}.
$$

\Image[polarization-through-three-filters.png]
{title=Two crossed <polarizers> transmit zero; inserting a middle <polarizer> transmits $I_0/4$ for initially horizontal <linear polarization>}
{description=Pass axes are measured from horizontal. Each blue segment represents transmitted <light intensity>, and each inset shows the pass axis in the transverse plane. The two successive $45^\circ$ changes each halve <light intensity> by <Malus's law>.}

For these ideal absorbing <polarizers>, the rejected <electric field> component transfers <energy> to the filter. In the three-filter arrangement the middle absorbs the fraction $I_0/2$, and the last absorbs $I_0/4$, leaving $I_0/4$ transmitted. <Conservation of energy> holds at each step. Inserting the middle <polarizer> changes the <linear polarization> arriving at the last one; it does not create <light>. The effect illustrates why successive <orthogonal projections> cannot generally be replaced by a single comparison of the first and last axes.

The initially horizontal assumption matters. For <unpolarized light> of <light intensity> $I_0$, the first ideal linear <polarizer> transmits $I_0/2$: averaging $\cos^2\theta$ over uniformly distributed incident orientations gives $1/2$. The same $0^\circ,45^\circ,90^\circ$ sequence then transmits $I_0/8$. This extra first-stage factor does not apply to the already horizontally polarized input in the figure.

= Transmission through a sequence of linear polarizers
{parent=Malus's law}
{title2=$I_{\rm out}=(I_{\rm in}/2)\prod_{j=2}^N\cos^2(\theta_j-\theta_{j-1})$}

For ideal <polarizers> with successive transverse transmission directions $\theta_j$, the <Jones matrix> is the rank-one <orthogonal projection> $P_j=\mathbf u_j\mathbf u_j^{\mathsf T}$. The identity $\mathbf u_j^{\mathsf T}\mathbf u_{j-1}=\cos(\theta_j-\theta_{j-1})$ reduces the transmitted <probability amplitude> analogue, here the optical <complex amplitude>, to a product of projections. For <unpolarized light>, the first element transmits half the incident <light intensity>, giving the displayed product. For a specified incident <linear polarization>, replace the first half-factor with its actual projection.

= Unpolarized light
{parent=Polarization (waves)}

<Unpolarized light> has no preferred transverse polarization direction in its time-averaged statistics. An ideal linear <polarizer> transmits half its incident <light intensity>: equal transverse components give the averaged projection factor $\langle\cos^2\theta\rangle=1/2$. This statistical description differs from the fixed deterministic phase relation of a <monochromatic wave> in a pure <polarization of waves> state.

= Coupled oscillator
{parent=Oscillator}

<Coupled oscillators> exchange <energy> through interactions. Linear equations can be transformed into <normal mode coordinates>; the spatial pattern of an applied <force> determines which <normal modes> are driven.

= Support-driven unequal-mass spring antiresonance
{parent=Coupled oscillator}
{title2=$\omega_0^2=k/m$}

For masses $m,2m$ connected in sequence to a moving support by springs $k,2k$, displacement coordinates give $m\operatorname{diag}(1,2)\ddot{\mathbf x}+k\begin{pmatrix}3&-2\\-2&2\end{pmatrix}\mathbf x=k(f,0)^{\mathsf T}$. The squared normal-mode frequencies are $(k/m)(2\pm\sqrt3)$. For support motion $f=F\sin\omega_0t$ with $\omega_0^2=k/m$, the negligible-damping steady response is $(0,-F\sin\omega_0t/2)$. The first mass has a response zero between the two resonances. Finite damping generally makes that zero imperfect.

= Pivot-driven two-mass spring antiresonance
{parent=Coupled oscillator}
{title2=$\omega_0^2=k/m$}

Two identical vertical springs connect a driven pivot to two equal masses in sequence. After subtracting the static gravitational equilibrium, the laboratory displacement equation is $m\ddot{\mathbf x}+k\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}\mathbf x=k(z,0)^{\mathsf T}$. For $z=z_0\cos\omega_0t$ and $\omega_0^2=k/m$, a particular solution is $\mathbf x=(0,-z_0\cos\omega_0t)^{\mathsf T}$. Thus the upper mass has zero driven response even though this frequency is not an eigenfrequency. The general solution also contains both free <normal modes>.

= Free-pair dynamic vibration absorber
{parent=Coupled oscillator}
{title2=$k=m\omega^2$}

A free mass $M$ driven by $F_0\cos\omega t$ can be coupled by a single <spring> of stiffness $k$ to an auxiliary free mass $m$. With no wall attachment or damping, the periodic displacement amplitude of $M$ is $A=F_0(k-m\omega^2)/\{\omega^2[Mm\omega^2-k(M+m)]\}$. Tuning $k=m\omega^2$ gives <antiresonance>: $A=0$ while the auxiliary mass has signed amplitude $-F_0/k$, supplying an exactly cancelling spring force. A nearby pole occurs at $k=Mm\omega^2/(M+m)$. For relative tuning error $\epsilon=(k-m\omega^2)/(m\omega^2)$ sufficiently small, the primary amplitude relative to its free response grows as $(M/m)|\epsilon|$. A light absorber is therefore narrowband and may require large auxiliary travel. This cancellation dissipates no energy, and undamped free modes persist unless excluded by initial conditions.

= Undamped periodic forced response is not an attracting steady state
{parent=Coupled oscillator}

In a finite-dimensional undamped linear oscillator, a bounded periodic particular solution can exist away from natural <resonances>. Adding any homogeneous <normal mode> gives another solution, and that free motion does not decay. Thus the late-time motion is not determined by the drive alone. At a resonant frequency with nonzero drive projection onto the corresponding mode, the particular solution grows secularly, so no bounded periodic steady response exists.

= End-driven two-bead string
{parent=Coupled oscillator}
{title2=$\omega_0^2=T/(2ML)$}

Place two equal <masses> $M$ on a massless <mechanical string> with spans $L,2L,L$ and uniform <tension> $T$. Fix one end and drive the other with displacement $h(t)$. Linear transverse force balance gives $\ddot y_1+\omega_0^2(3y_1-y_2)=0$ and $\ddot y_2+\omega_0^2(3y_2-y_1)=2\omega_0^2h$. The mass-normalized stiffness matrix has symmetric and antisymmetric <normal modes> with squared angular frequencies $2\omega_0^2$ and $4\omega_0^2$. The mode coordinates $(y_1+y_2)/2$ and $(y_1-y_2)/2$ separate the forced equations.

= Antiresonant node in an end-driven two-bead string
{parent=End-driven two-bead string}

= Frequency interlacing for two spring-coupled equal masses

{title2=$\omega_-<\omega_2<\omega_1<\omega_+$}

= Energy transfer after an impulse in two weakly coupled oscillators
{parent=Coupled oscillator}
{title2=$t_{\mathrm{transfer}}\sim\pi\omega_0/\kappa$}

Consider identical <harmonic oscillators> with unit <mass>, natural <angular frequency> $\omega_0>0$, and positive coupling $\kappa\ll\omega_0^2$:
$$
\ddot x_1+\omega_0^2x_1+\kappa(x_1-x_2)=0,\qquad
\ddot x_2+\omega_0^2x_2+\kappa(x_2-x_1)=0.
$$
An initial <impulse> on the first gives $x_1(0)=x_2(0)=\dot x_2(0)=0$ and $\dot x_1(0)=v$. The <normal mode coordinates> $u=x_1+x_2$ and $w=x_1-x_2$ have <angular frequencies> $\omega_0$ and $\omega_a=\sqrt{\omega_0^2+2\kappa}$. Solving their <initial value problems> proves
$$
x_1=\frac v2\left(\frac{\sin\omega_0t}{\omega_0}+\frac{\sin\omega_at}{\omega_a}\right),\qquad
x_2=\frac v2\left(\frac{\sin\omega_0t}{\omega_0}-\frac{\sin\omega_at}{\omega_a}\right).
$$
Let $\Delta=\omega_a-\omega_0\sim\kappa/\omega_0$. To leading order in $\kappa/\omega_0^2$, sum-to-product identities give the displacement <amplitude> envelopes $(|v|/\omega_0)|\cos(\Delta t/2)|$ and $(|v|/\omega_0)|\sin(\Delta t/2)|$. Thus the first nearly complete <energy> transfer occurs at $\pi/\Delta$, and localization returns at $2\pi/\Delta$. More precisely, at $t=\pi/\Delta$, the first <oscillator> has exactly zero <velocity> and residual <displacement> $\tfrac v2(1/\omega_0-1/\omega_a)\sin(\omega_0t)$, of order $|v|\kappa/\omega_0^3$.

Here transferred <energy> means <kinetic energy> plus the uncoupled quadratic <potential energy>; the coupling <potential energy> is relatively small. The fast phase need not make the receiving <oscillator> pass through zero <displacement> at the transfer time. Expanding $\Delta t$ as small is invalid on the transfer scale $t=O(1/\kappa)$. This lemma complements <absolute and signed beat envelopes> by keeping that slow phase.

= Anderson localization
{c}
{parent=Wave propagation in a random medium}
{wiki}

<Anderson localization> is the confinement of <electron> <wave functions>, or other <wave> modes, by <interference> between repeated <wave scattering> paths in static disorder. A <localized eigenstate> occupies a limited region even in a much larger sample. Disorder can therefore suppress <diffusion> without absorbing the <wave>. In 1958, <Philip Warren Anderson> studied random site <energies> and intersite coupling, finding a regime without transport. His paper develops a theoretical model; it does not report an Anderson laboratory measurement.<Absence of Diffusion in Certain Random Lattices by Philip Warren Anderson>[Anderson, 1958, pp.1492–1494, Sections I–II]{ref}

The distinction from ordinary <multiple scattering> is the retention of <wave phases>: alternative paths contribute <probability amplitudes> before their squared <modulus> is taken. Adding path probabilities alone loses the cross terms responsible for <interference>. This makes <Anderson localization> useful as a warning about transport models: repeated deflections need not produce unlimited <diffusion>. It also connects <electron> transport with <wave propagation in a random medium>, although the detailed equations and experimental diagnostics differ between <electrons>, <light> and <acoustic waves>.

<Philip Warren Anderson>'s 1977 lecture distinguishes localized magnetic moments from disorder-localized states. It credits <Nevill Francis Mott> with recognizing the significance of the <mobility edge>. A <Mott insulator> instead requires <electron> interactions and can exist without disorder.<Local Moments and Localized States by Philip Warren Anderson>[Local Moments and Localized States, pp.376–377,395–396; inspected institutional mirror https://www.nevis.columbia.edu/~haas/documents/physics_nobel_lectures/physics71-80-2.pdf]{ref}

<John Hasbrouck Van Vleck> developed <quantum mechanics> explanations of <magnetic susceptibility>, including its <temperature> dependence; this is a distinct contribution from <Anderson localization>.<Quantum Mechanics: The Key to Understanding Magnetism by John Hasbrouck Van Vleck>[Quantum Mechanics: The Key to Understanding Magnetism, pp.355–356; inspected institutional mirror https://www.nevis.columbia.edu/~haas/documents/physics_nobel_lectures/physics71-80-2.pdf]{ref}

= Weak localization
{parent=Anderson localization}

Interference of time-reversed multiple-scattering paths can enhance return probability and reduce conductance in a coherent disordered conductor with the appropriate symmetries. Magnetic fields, spin-orbit coupling and dephasing alter or suppress the correction; some symmetry classes exhibit antilocalization. This small-correction regime must be distinguished from strong <Anderson localization>.

= Mobility gap
{parent=Anderson localization}

An <energy> interval with no extended bulk <energy eigenstates> available for transport. It can contain <localized eigenstates> and have a nonzero <density of states>; a <mobility gap> need not be a <spectral gap>.

= Localized eigenstate
{parent=Anderson localization}
{title2=$H\psi=E\psi$}

A <localized eigenstate> is an <energy eigenstate> whose spatial weight remains concentrated near some position as the system grows. An exponentially <localized eigenstate> has a decaying envelope characterized by a <localization length>. A small <participation ratio> measures concentration in a chosen site basis; it does not by itself establish an exponential tail or a transport law.

An <energy eigenstate> changes only by an overall <wave phase> under time evolution, whether localized or extended. Its stationary probability distribution alone therefore does not distinguish transport mechanisms. The important distinction concerns spatial extent and how superpositions of <energy eigenstates> carry or retain a disturbance. A wave packet assembled from <localized eigenstates> with nearby centres can remain confined, whereas extended modes can support propagation.

= Localization length
{parent=Localized eigenstate}
{title2=$|\psi_n|\sim e^{-|n-n_0|a/\xi}$}

The <localization length> $\xi$ measures the decay scale of a <localized eigenstate>'s <wave amplitude> envelope. Here $a$ is the site spacing and $n_0$ its centre. With this amplitude convention, probability decays on half that scale:
$$
|\psi_n|\sim e^{-|n-n_0|a/\xi},\qquad |\psi_n|^2\sim e^{-2|n-n_0|a/\xi}.
$$
Individual random tails fluctuate; this notation describes their typical envelope, not exact equality at every site. A <localization length> much greater than a finite sample's <length> makes a state look extended inside that sample. The <participation ratio> below is an effective site count, not an independent fitted value of $\xi$.

= Participation ratio
{parent=Localized eigenstate}
{title2=$P=(\sum_n|\psi_n|^4)^{-1}$}

For an <eigenvector> normalized by $\sum_{n=1}^N|\psi_n|^2=1$, the <participation ratio> in its site basis is
$$
P=\frac{1}{\sum_{n=1}^N|\psi_n|^4},\qquad 1\leq P\leq N.
$$
It estimates how many sites carry appreciable probability. Equal weight on $K$ sites and zero elsewhere gives $P=K$, irrespective of their <wave phases>. A single occupied site gives $P=1$; uniform probability on all sites gives $P=N$. These are exact constructed examples, not claims about particular <Hamiltonian operators>.

To prove the bounds, write $p_n=|\psi_n|^2$. Since $0\leq p_n\leq1$, $\sum_np_n^2\leq\sum_np_n=1$, giving $P\geq1$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives $1=(\sum_np_n)^2\leq N\sum_np_n^2$, giving $P\leq N$. Thus the measure has useful limits without assuming an exponential shape. Comparing $P$ as $N$ increases helps distinguish a state filling the sample from one occupying a fixed region, provided <energy> selection and disorder statistics are controlled.

= Mobility edge
{parent=Anderson localization}
{title2=$E_c$}
{wiki}

A <mobility edge> separates localized from extended <energy eigenstates> on the <energy> axis. Three-dimensional disordered models can have both. In the infinite one-dimensional <tight-binding model> with independent continuous random site <energies>, all states are localized for any nonzero disorder, with probability one. This statement excludes specially correlated disorder.<Local Moments and Localized States by Philip Warren Anderson>[Local Moments and Localized States, pp.395–396, mobility edge and one-dimensional localization; institutional mirror as above]{ref}

A <mobility edge> is not a <band gap>: states can exist on both sides of $E_c$, but differ in spatial extent. For <electrons>, the location of the <Fermi energy> relative to a <mobility edge> matters for low-<temperature> <electrical conductivity>. A three-dimensional sample need not have an extended interval at every disorder strength. In particular, the finite one-dimensional calculations below neither locate a three-dimensional <mobility edge> nor establish a nonzero universal critical disorder.

= One-dimensional Anderson chain
{c}
{parent=Anderson localization}
{title2=$H=-t\sum_n(\lvert n\rangle\langle n+1\rvert+\lvert n+1\rangle\langle n\rvert)+\sum_n\epsilon_n\lvert n\rangle\langle n\rvert$}

Consider a noninteracting <electron> in a finite <tight-binding model> with open ends:
$$
H=-t\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}\bigl(\lvert n\rangle\langle n+1\rvert+\lvert n+1\rangle\langle n\rvert\bigr)+\sum_{n=1}^N\epsilon_n\lvert n\rangle\langle n\rvert,\qquad t>0.
$$
The <vectors> $\lvert n\rangle$ form an <orthonormal basis> of site states. The hopping <energy> is $t$; it is not the time coordinate. Draw each $\epsilon_n$ independently from the <continuous uniform distribution> on $[-W/2,W/2]$ and then hold it fixed. The disorder strength $W/t$ is dimensionless. Open ends mean no hopping bond connects sites $N$ and $1$; equivalently set $\psi_0=\psi_{N+1}=0$. There are no <electron> interactions, <phonons> or <wave absorption> in this model.

The <eigenvalue equation> in the interior is
$$
-t(\psi_{n-1}+\psi_{n+1})+\epsilon_n\psi_n=E\psi_n.
$$
As a simple local calculation, if a state's peak is at $n_0$, a neighbouring site with $|E-\epsilon_n|\gg t$ receives a small leading contribution $\psi_n\simeq-t\psi_{n_0}/(E-\epsilon_n)$. Several successive large mismatches multiply small factors. This explains why large disorder can restrict spreading, but is not a proof of <Anderson localization>: near-resonant denominators and alternative <interference> paths prevent treating this expansion as uniformly valid.

For the first original calculation, take $N=80$, $t=1$, $W/t=6$ and the fixed random seed $20261015$. Diagonalizing the resulting real <symmetric matrix> with `numpy.linalg.eigh` and selecting the <eigenvalue> closest to zero gives $E/t\simeq-0.05962$, a probability peak at site $29$, and $P\simeq6.0031$. The selected <eigenvector> has total probability one. This is one chosen realization, not a disorder average or a measured specimen.

{parent=Anderson localization}

<Nevill Francis Mott>'s 1977 lecture discusses <silicon> deposited in a glow discharge. Injected <electrons> provided a drift-mobility readout; <electrical conductivity> and <temperature> dependence tested localized-state transport and excitation toward a <mobility edge>. This evidence motivates a transport question beyond the closed-chain <eigenvalue problem>: how does a prepared charge disturbance move under the conditions of a real sample? The numerical figures above supply neither drift-mobility measurements nor a model of the deposition process. Finite-size concentration alone cannot reproduce an experimental <electrical conductivity> curve.

Finite <temperature> permits <phonon>-assisted hopping between <localized eigenstates> or thermal excitation toward a <mobility edge>; localized states therefore need not imply zero measured <electrical conductivity> at every nonzero <temperature>.<Electrons in Glass by Nevill Francis Mott>[Electrons in Glass, pp.405,407, localized-state hopping and thermal excitation]{ref}

Real <dephasing> destroys the relative <wave phases> needed for long-path <interference>. Observing coherent confinement requires the distance over which those <wave phases> survive to be long enough to explore the localized region, and the sample to be large enough to resolve it. <Temperature> alone is not an automatic switch: the actual inelastic processes set the relevant scales. Static elastic <wave scattering> preserves coherence within each realization. Loss of the ensemble-averaged <coherent field> through random <wave phases> is therefore not, by itself, physical <dephasing> of an individual sample.

{synonym}

For a complex random <wave amplitude>, this two-point <expectation> retains the <coherent field> and uses a <complex conjugate> on the second amplitude. It is not a normalized <wave-field correlation> coefficient. Subtracting $\mathbb E[E(\mathbf r)]\mathbb E[E(\mathbf r')]^*$ gives the centered <covariance function>. At coincident points the uncentered <wave-field correlation> equals the <mean wave intensity>. Under second-order <stationarity> it depends only on the separation and has a <spectral measure of a stationary random field>. A deterministic field is included by taking a degenerate ensemble.

= Ensemble-averaged wave intensity
{parent=Wave propagation in a random medium}
{title2=$\mathbb E[|E|^2]=|\mathbb E[E]|^2+\mathbb E[|E-\mathbb E[E]|^2]$}

= Mean wave intensity
{synonym}

For a random complex wave amplitude $E$, its mean squared amplitude is $I=\mathbb E[|E|^2]$. Expanding $E=m+(E-m)$, with $m=\mathbb E[E]$, gives
$$
 \mathbb E[|E|^2]=|m|^2+\mathbb E[|E-m|^2].
$$
The mixed terms vanish. Thus the squared <coherent field> is only one part of the mean intensity; the other is the diffuse contribution. A fixed acoustic or optical normalization converts this squared-amplitude quantity to physical <energy flux>. This ensemble mean differs from the angularly averaged radiative-transfer <mean intensity>.

= Coherent field
{synonym}

In <wave propagation in a random medium>, the coherent field is the <expectation> of the complex wave amplitude, $m(\mathbf r)=\mathbb E[E(\mathbf r)]$. The remaining field $E-m$ has zero <expectation>. This is the <coherent and diffuse wave fields> decomposition. <Statistical homogeneity> of the medium does not make $m$ spatially constant when the incident field is localized: translation symmetry applies to the response together with its incident data. The coherent component can attenuate while the total <ensemble-averaged wave intensity> remains substantial.

= Gaussian coherent-field propagation in the Markov approximation
{parent=Coherent wave field}
{c}
{title2=$m=e^{-\gamma(x-x_0)}E_{\mathrm{free}}$}

For the linear weak-index <parabolic wave equation>, a <Markov approximation> with integrated covariance $B(\eta)=\int_{\mathbb R}C(s,\eta)ds$ gives
$$
 m_x=\frac{i}{2k}m_{zz}-\gamma m,\qquad \gamma=\frac{k^2\mu^2B(0)}2.
$$
The drift follows by converting the multiplicative <Stratonovich integral> $ik\mu E\circ d\mathcal B$ to an <Itô integral> for a <Brownian field> with transverse covariance $B$. For a <Gaussian beam with one transverse coordinate> entering the random region at $x_0$, $m(x,z)=e^{-\gamma(x-x_0)}E_{\mathrm{free}}(x,z)$. The <coherent field> retains transverse beam structure. The white-noise closure needs short longitudinal correlations and the corresponding separation of propagation scales; Gaussian one-point statistics alone are insufficient.

= Weak-fluctuation memory equation for the coherent field
{parent=Coherent wave field}

For a <parabolic wave equation> with $n=1+\mu W$, a zero-mean stationary real field $W$ of unit variance, and $L_0=i\partial_z^2/(2k)$, finite-correlation perturbation theory gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
 m_x&=L_0m+\frac{ik\mu^2}{2}m\\
 &\quad-k^2\mu^2\int_{x_0}^x\int_{\mathbb R}C(x-s,z-\zeta)G_{x-s}(z-\zeta)m(s,\zeta)\,d\zeta\,ds+O(\mu^3).
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $m$ is the <coherent field>, $C$ is the <autocorrelation function of a random field> and $G$ is the one-coordinate <Fresnel propagator>. Expand the random solution once using the <Duhamel principle>, multiply by $W$ and average to obtain the memory term. The local phase term comes from the $\mu^2W^2$ term in $n^2$. The expansion is for fixed propagation distances with suitable covariance regularity and moment bounds. A <Markov approximation> is an additional scale assumption that can turn this integral equation into a local attenuation equation.

= Quadratic refractive-index shift in the coherent field
{parent=Weak-fluctuation memory equation for the coherent field}
{title2=$\partial_xm\supset ik\mu^2m/2$}

If the physical <refractive index> is $n=1+\mu W$, then $n^2-1=2\mu W+\mu^2W^2$. In the <parabolic wave equation>, the last term produces $ik\mu^2\mathbb E[W^2E]/2$ in the mean equation. For unit-variance, finite-correlation fluctuations, $\mathbb E[W^2E]=m+O(\mu)$ in a weak-fluctuation expansion, giving a coherent phase drift $ik\mu^2m/2$ to second order. This term is absent if the model deliberately linearizes $n^2-1$ to $2\mu W$. It is not obtained by squaring ideal <Gaussian white noise>, whose pointwise square is not the finite-variance random variable $W^2$.

= Doppler effect
{c}
{parent=Wave}
{wiki}
{title2=$\Delta f=f_o-f_s$}

= Doppler shift
{c}
{synonym}

The <Doppler effect> is a change between emitted and received <frequency> caused by motion of the source or receiver. An approaching siren can send more <wave crests> per second to a <microphone> than the source emits per second; a receding siren sends fewer. The source need not change its own <oscillation>. The <Doppler shift> is $\Delta f=f_o-f_s$, where $f_s>0$ is emitted <frequency> and $f_o>0$ is received <frequency>, both measured in <hertz>.

For <acoustic waves>, motion must be specified relative to the material carrying the <wave>. The <speed of sound> is measured in that material's rest <reference frame>. Moving the source changes the spacing of successive <wavefronts>; moving the receiver changes how quickly it crosses those <wavefronts>. These two mechanisms give different exact factors in the <classical Doppler effect for sound>, even for the same closing <relative velocity>.

This makes a <microphone> a motion probe: a stable tone supplies a repeating clock, and changes in arrival spacing reveal the source's motion toward or away from the receiver. An <acoustic Doppler measurement> can test the prediction directly. A passing source also illustrates why geometry matters: its speed can stay constant while the received <frequency> changes continuously, as in <Doppler frequency along a trajectory>.

The following sound model assumes <linear acoustics> in uniform stationary material with <isotropy>, constant <speed of sound> and negligible <wave dispersion>. Source and receiver speeds are below the <speed of sound>, and there is a single direct propagation path. Air motion requires transforming source and receiver <velocities> into the air's rest <reference frame>; a nonuniform flow requires a more general propagation model. At the sonic threshold the simple periodic-arrival picture fails; <moving-point acoustic wavefronts> and the <Mach cone> describe the relevant geometry, while finite <shock waves> require further physics. <Light> in <vacuum> instead requires the <relativistic Doppler effect>: the acoustic formula cannot be converted into its exact optical counterpart just by replacing the <speed of sound> with the <speed of light>.

= Doppler dipole
{c}
{parent=Doppler effect}
{title2=$\Delta T/T_0=(v/c)\cos\theta+O(v^2/c^2)$}

An observer moving through isotropic <blackbody radiation> sees a direction-dependent <temperature>. With the sky angle measured toward the velocity apex in the observer frame, $T(\theta)=T_0/[\gamma(1-\beta\cos\theta)]$, where $\beta=v/c$ and $\gamma=(1-\beta^2)^{-1/2}$. First-order expansion gives the displayed dipole. The forward sky is hotter. The <cosmic microwave background dipole> is an application.

= Rotational Doppler effect
{parent=Doppler effect}

A <frequency> change associated with rotation and exchange of <angular momentum>. If an optical element rotates at angular velocity $\Omega$ about a chosen axis and gains <angular momentum> $\Delta L$ from a <photon>, its mechanical <energy> gain is $\Omega\Delta L$ in the prescribed slow-rotation model. <Energy> conservation then gives <photon> <frequency> change $\Delta\omega=-\Omega\Delta L/\hbar$. The <rotating half-wave plate frequency shift> is a spin-polarization example.

= Rotating half-wave plate frequency shift
{parent=Rotational Doppler effect}
{title2=$|\Delta\omega|=2|\Omega|$}

A rotating ideal <half-wave plate> flips circular <polarization>. The spin change has magnitude $2\hbar$ per <photon>, so its mechanical <energy> exchange produces a <frequency> shift of magnitude $2|\Omega|$. For Jones convention $H(\theta)=R(\theta)\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)R(-\theta)$ and $Q=\operatorname{diag}(1,i)$, the combination $QH(\theta)Q=e^{i2\theta\sigma_x}$. Acting on $(1,1)^T/\sqrt2$ gives $e^{i2\theta}$; with time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$ and $\theta=\Omega t$, the output <frequency> is $\omega-2\Omega$. The opposite input eigenpolarization reverses the shift.

= Relativistic Doppler effect
{parent=Doppler effect}
{title2=$\nu=\gamma\nu'(1+\beta\cos\theta')$}

= Relativistic Doppler shift
{synonym}

The <Lorentz transformation> changes the <photon> <four-momentum> and therefore its frequency, producing the displayed formula for a boost along the first axis. Here the primed ray angle is measured in the emitting frame and $\beta=v/c$. The angle and frequency transforms must be used together: <aberration of light> changes the direction, and a photon-number angular fraction need not equal the corresponding radiant-energy fraction.

Here $c$ is the <vacuum> <speed of light>, $\gamma$ is the <Lorentz factor>, and the two <frequencies> are measured in different inertial <reference frames>. Unlike the <classical Doppler effect for sound>, there is no material rest <reference frame> for <light> in <vacuum>. For small radial recession speed $v_r\ll c$, the <wavelength> shift satisfies $\Delta\lambda/\lambda\simeq v_r/c$. This small-speed limit underlies <Doppler spectroscopy>; it is not an exact sound formula.

= Common Doppler scaling of spectral-line separations
{parent=Relativistic Doppler effect}
{title2=$\Delta\lambda_{\rm obs}=D\,\Delta\lambda_{\rm emit}$}

If two <spectral lines> come from the same uniformly moving source along the same line of sight, each <wavelength> is multiplied by the same longitudinal <Doppler factor> $D$. Subtracting $\lambda_{2,\rm obs}=D\lambda_{2,\rm emit}$ and $\lambda_{1,\rm obs}=D\lambda_{1,\rm emit}$ proves the displayed identity. Recession has $D>1$ and approach has $D<1$. Defining positive radial velocity for recession gives $\beta=(D^2-1)/(D^2+1)$. A measured increase in wavelength separation therefore cannot be reconciled with strictly approaching longitudinal motion merely by changing the speed's magnitude.

= Ultrarelativistic longitudinal Doppler factor
{parent=Relativistic Doppler effect}
{title2=$D=\gamma+\sqrt{\gamma^2-1}=2\gamma-1/(2\gamma)+O(\gamma^{-3})$}

For a forward <photon> and a positive collinear <Lorentz transformation>, the <Doppler factor> is $D=\gamma(1+\beta)$. The identity $\gamma\beta=\sqrt{\gamma^2-1}$ gives the exact expression in the title. Expanding the square root by the <binomial series> gives $D=2\gamma-1/(2\gamma)-1/(8\gamma^3)+O(\gamma^{-5})$. A leading approximation $D\sim2\gamma$ has relative error of order $\gamma^{-2}$; retaining a purported subleading term requires expanding all dependent quantities consistently. For example, $\sqrt{4\gamma^2-1}$ has subleading term $-1/(4\gamma)$ and is not the exact longitudinal <Doppler factor>.

= Equal-frequency light between equal-speed inertial observers
{parent=Relativistic Doppler effect}
{title2=$\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf v_1-\mathbf v_2)=0$}

For two <inertial frames> with equal <speed> in a laboratory, a <photon> is measured at equal <angular frequency> by their comoving observers exactly when its laboratory propagation <unit vector> $\mathbf n$ obeys the displayed condition. Indeed, measured <angular frequency> is $-k\cdot U=\gamma\omega_{\mathrm{lab}}(1-\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v/c)$, and equal laboratory <Lorentz factors> cancel. Thus the permitted directions form a <great circle> on the <unit sphere>. In the plane of the two laboratory <velocities>, only the internal <angle bisector> line remains. The <velocity> information alone fixes neither direction along that line nor coplanarity of the ray.

= Frequency ratio of corotating clocks
{parent=Relativistic Doppler effect}

For a freely propagating <photon> between emitters and receivers rotating with the same <angular velocity> in flat <spacetime>, $\nu_r/\nu_e=\gamma_r/\gamma_e$, provided the <photon> path is unobstructed. At equal radii the ratio is one. Positions and <velocities> at the actual emission and reception events must be used.

= Doppler extrema for a circularly moving source
{parent=Relativistic Doppler effect}

A source of fixed proper <frequency> and <speed> $v$ on a circle has instantaneous <frequency> extrema $\nu_0\sqrt{(1\pm\beta)/(1\mp\beta)}$ for a stationary coplanar receiver outside the circle. The extrema occur when the emission ray is tangent to the source circle, so line-of-sight <speed> reaches $\pm v$.

= Transverse Doppler shift and observation time
{parent=Relativistic Doppler effect}
{title2=$\lambda=\gamma\lambda_0(1-\beta\cos\theta)$}

Let $\theta$ be the angle between the source velocity and the emitted photon's propagation direction in the observer's frame. The <relativistic Doppler effect> gives $\nu=\nu_0/[\gamma(1-\beta\cos\theta)]$. A ray transverse in that frame has $\lambda=\gamma\lambda_0$, a redshift. A ray transverse in the source frame instead has $\cos\theta=\beta$ by <aberration of light>, and $\lambda=\lambda_0/\gamma$, a blueshift.

The time at which a passing source is closest must also be specified. Put the observer at the origin and the source at $(vt_e,d)$ at emission, with finite transverse offset $d>0$. Reception occurs at
$$
t_r=t_e+\frac{\sqrt{v^2t_e^2+d^2}}c.
$$
Emission at closest approach means $t_e=0$, a transverse observer-frame ray received at $t_r=d/c$, hence a redshift. Reception at closest approach means $t_r=0$, so $t_e=-\gamma d/c$ and the ray has $\cos\theta=\beta$, hence a blueshift. Sending $d$ to zero does not remove the distinction between these two limiting event prescriptions. https://web.mit.edu/8.286/www/quiz18/q1rp-euf18.pdf[MIT 8.286, transverse Doppler shifts, distinction between closest approach at emission and reception]{ref}

= Moving-mirror Doppler shift
{parent=Relativistic Doppler effect}
{title2=$\omega_r=\omega_i(1-\beta)/(1+\beta)$}

A mirror receding at <velocity> $u=\beta c$ from normally incident <light> produces two applications of the <relativistic Doppler effect>, one into the mirror rest frame and one back to the observer. In that rest frame, reflection preserves <frequency>. Consequently $\omega_r=\gamma^2(1-\beta)^2\omega_i=\omega_i(1-\beta)/(1+\beta)$. Equivalently, continuity of <wave phase> on the moving surface $z=ut$ gives $\omega_i-k_i u=\omega_r+k_r u$, with $k_i=\omega_i/c$ and $k_r=\omega_r/c$. For $|u|\ll c$, the fractional <frequency> change is $-2u/c$ to first order.

= Moving-mirror photon interception rate
{parent=Moving-mirror Doppler shift}
{title2=$R_{\rm hit}=R(1-\beta)$}

For a <photon> emitted from the origin and arriving at receding mirror position $x(t)$, its emission time is $t_e=t-x(t)/c$. Thus $dt_e/dt=1-\beta$ and a steady source rate $R$ gives impact rate $R(1-\beta)$ in source-frame coordinate time. This is distinct from the emission rate and from a rate per mirror <proper time>.

= Kinematic redshift
{parent=Relativistic Doppler effect}
{title2=$1+z_{\rm kin}=\sqrt{(1+v/c)/(1-v/c)}$}

A kinematic redshift is the <relativistic Doppler effect> from relative motion of emitter and receiver. With positive radial recession speed $v$, its small-velocity limit is $z_{\rm kin}=v/c+O(v^2/c^2)$. A <peculiar velocity> gives such a factor in addition to <cosmological redshift>.

= Classical Doppler effect for sound
{parent=Doppler effect}
{title2=$f_o=f_s(c-u_o)/(c-u_s)$}

Work in the material's rest <reference frame>, with constant <speed of sound> $c>0$. For one-dimensional propagation, choose the positive $x$ direction along the travelling <wave> from its emission point toward the receiver. Define $u_s=dx_s/dt$ and $u_o=dx_o/dt$ as the signed source and receiver <velocities> along that same axis. Both use the same sign convention: positive $u_s$ moves the source toward the receiver ahead, whereas positive $u_o$ moves that receiver away from the incoming <wave>. Assume $|u_s|<c$ and $|u_o|<c$, constant <velocities>, and no source-receiver crossing during the interval considered. Then
$$
f_o=f_s\frac{c-u_o}{c-u_s}.
$$

To derive this, let $T_s=1/f_s$ be the emission <wave period>. During $T_s$, one <wave crest> travels $cT_s$ while the source advances $u_sT_s$. Successive <wavefronts> along the chosen direction therefore have <wavelength>
$$
\lambda=(c-u_s)T_s=\frac{c-u_s}{f_s}.
$$
They overtake the receiver at relative speed $c-u_o$, so counting <wave crests> gives $f_o=(c-u_o)/\lambda$. This separates the source's effect on <wavelength> from the receiver's effect on crossing rate. Even with a moving source, a stationary receiver obeys $f_o\lambda=c$ because the <wave speed> through the material remains $c$.

For a stationary receiver and a source approaching at speed $v$, set $u_o=0$ and $u_s=v$: $f_o=f_sc/(c-v)>f_s$. For recession choose the positive axis along the departing <wave> toward the receiver again, so the source has $u_s=-v$: $f_o=f_sc/(c+v)<f_s$. For the deliberately chosen values $c=343\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $v=20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and $f_s=500\,\mathrm{Hz}$, the two predictions are
$$
f_{\mathrm{approach}}=500\frac{343}{323}\,\mathrm{Hz}\simeq530.96\,\mathrm{Hz},\qquad f_{\mathrm{recede}}=500\frac{343}{363}\,\mathrm{Hz}\simeq472.45\,\mathrm{Hz}.
$$
The corresponding <wavelengths> are $0.646\,\mathrm m$ and $0.726\,\mathrm m$, compared with $c/f_s=0.686\,\mathrm m$ for a stationary source. These are calculations for chosen parameters, not reported measurements.

= Doppler frequency along a trajectory
{c}
{parent=Doppler effect}
{title2=$f_o(t)=f_s(c-\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v_o(t))/(c-\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v_s(\tau))$}

In the same uniform stationary sound model, let $\tau$ be emission <time>, $t$ reception <time>, and $\mathbf x_s(\tau)$ and $\mathbf x_o(t)$ source and receiver positions. The direct-path propagation condition and its direction are
$$
c(t-\tau)=R,\qquad R=|\mathbf x_o(t)-\mathbf x_s(\tau)|>0,\qquad \mathbf n=\frac{\mathbf x_o(t)-\mathbf x_s(\tau)}{R}.
$$
The <unit vector> $\mathbf n$ points from the source's old emission position to the receiver's reception position. With <velocities> $\mathbf v_s=d\mathbf x_s/d\tau$ and $\mathbf v_o=d\mathbf x_o/dt$, taking a <derivative> of the propagation condition gives
$$
c\left(1-\frac{d\tau}{dt}\right)=\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v_o-\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v_s\frac{d\tau}{dt},\qquad f_o(t)=f_s\frac{d\tau}{dt}=f_s\frac{c-\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v_o(t)}{c-\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v_s(\tau)}.
$$
Here the <dot products> project onto the actual propagation direction. The last equality counts source <wave phase> cycles at reception for constant emitted <frequency> $f_s$. It assumes both speeds are below $c$, a unique direct arrival and smooth motion; interpreting a measured short-window <frequency spectrum> additionally requires little <frequency> change within that window. The source <velocity> must be evaluated at emission, not at reception. This delayed geometry matters when the direction changes.

For an original pass-by model, a source follows $\mathbf x_s(\tau)=(v\tau,0)$ and a stationary receiver is at $(0,b)$ with $b>0$. The source passes its closest position at emission <time> $\tau=0$. Then
$$
R=\sqrt{v^2\tau^2+b^2},\qquad t=\tau+R/c,\qquad f_o(t)=f_s\frac{c}{c+v^2\tau/R}.
$$
These parametric equations give received <frequency> against reception <time>. For $\tau<0$ the shift is positive, for $\tau>0$ negative, and the unshifted emission at $\tau=0$ reaches the receiver at $t=b/c$. Thus zero shift occurs after the source's geometric closest passage in the material's <reference frame>. Increasing $b$ spreads the transition over a longer interval even though $v$ is unchanged. Far before and after passage, the <frequency> approaches $f_sc/(c-v)$ and $f_sc/(c+v)$ respectively.
