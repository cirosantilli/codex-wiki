= Biochemistry
{wiki}

<Biochemistry> studies the <chemistry> of living systems: what their <molecules> are, how they recognize one another, and how their <chemical reactions> support the organization of a <biological cell>. It connects <biology> to <chemical kinetics>, <stoichiometry> and <thermodynamics>. A useful biochemical explanation follows both material and <energy>: which <substrate> becomes which <reaction product>, what sets the <reaction rate>, and how the transformation is driven.

Consider three connected examples. The <stereochemistry> of a <sugar> helps determine how it interacts with an <enzyme>'s <active site>; the <molecular formula> alone cannot describe that recognition. Selective <enzymes> then direct material through <metabolism>, while <ATP> can participate in <energy coupling> that drives otherwise unfavourable transformations. These are different questions about the same working system, rather than three names for its <energy> supply.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others>[§12.7, Enzyme Structure and Function, Figure 12.25: substrate recognition and induced fit]{ref}

This viewpoint is useful because it separates possible explanations for a stopped process. Missing <substrate>, inactive <enzyme>, depleted <ATP> and accumulated <reaction product> call for different interventions. Studying a <cell-free extract> can isolate some of those causes while leaving a substantial <chemical reaction network> intact.

= Dialysis
{synonym}

<Dialysis> exchanges small <solutes> between a sample and an external bath through a <semipermeable membrane>, while retaining suitably large <macromolecules>, such as <proteins>. The transport is passive <diffusion>: random motion carries permeant <molecules> both ways, with net transport down their <concentration> <gradient>. The bath is the <dialysate>. At equal permeant <solute> <concentrations>, microscopic exchange continues but net exchange stops.<Dialysis: an overview, Pierce Biotechnology technical note>[Dialysis: an overview, Tech Tip 20, p. 1, Dialysis is Diffusion Through a Semi-Permeable Membrane]{ref}

For example, a <protein> sample containing excess <sodium chloride> can lose the small <ions> to a low-<salt> <dialysate> without deliberately transferring the <protein>. Conversely, components of a new <buffer solution> can enter from the bath. This is useful before studying <enzyme kinetics>: changing the surrounding <chemical solution> can remove an interfering small <solute> while keeping the <enzyme> available for the next experiment.

\Image[biochemical-dialysis-membrane-exchange.png]
{title=Small <salt> <ions> exchange across a <semipermeable membrane> while a <protein> is retained}
{description=Original schematic of a sample bag immersed in a <dialysate>, with an enlarged membrane region. Arrows indicate exchange in both directions and an initially outward net <diffusive flux>. Symbols and openings are not to scale; the drawn gap is not a literal <molecular weight cut-off> measurement. Replacing the bath removes escaped <salt> and renews the <concentration> <gradient>.}

The striking feature is that the retained <protein> does not have to be captured and released again to change its surroundings. However, retention is conditional on the <semipermeable membrane> and the particular <macromolecule>, and <dialysis> is poorly suited to separating <proteins> of nearly the same size. The <molecular weight cut-off> is a nominal retention rating, not a sharp geometrical boundary.

= Dialysate
{parent=Dialysis (biochemistry)}

The external <chemical solution> on the other side of a <dialysis> <semipermeable membrane>. Its composition supplies the boundary condition for exchange: it can receive unwanted small <solutes> or supply desired small <solutes>. A bath replacement discards the old <dialysate> and introduces a new reservoir with a specified composition.

= Molecular weight cut-off
{parent=Dialysis (biochemistry)}

= MWCO
{c}
{synonym}

The <molecular weight cut-off> rates retention of reference <molecules>. One manufacturer's definition uses the <mass> at which approximately $90\%$ remains after seventeen-hour <dialysis>, tested with globular <proteins>. A $10\,\mathrm{kDa}$ rating uses <dalton (unit)> units: a <mass>, not a length or an absolute passage threshold.<Dialysis: an overview, Pierce Biotechnology technical note>[Tech Tip 20, p. 1, Factors Affecting Dialysis Rate: MWCO definition and globular reference molecules]{ref}

A <semipermeable membrane> has a distribution of passage sizes, and a <molecule>'s shape affects passage as well as its <mass>. Near the nominal <MWCO>, slow passage can make substantial retention compatible with eventual leakage. The <protein>-retention experiment below makes retention an observable quantity rather than a universal yes-or-no property inferred from the label.

= Buffer exchange
{parent=Dialysis (biochemistry)}

<Buffer exchange> changes the small components surrounding a retained <macromolecule> toward those of a desired <buffer solution>. Components absent from fresh <dialysate> tend to leave; supplied components can enter. Each permeant <solute> has its own exchange problem.<Dialysis: an overview, Pierce Biotechnology technical note>[Tech Tip 20, pp. 1-2, renewed gradients and Other Factors in Dialysis]{ref}

For a dilute, nonbinding permeant <solute>, an ideal equilibrium calculation approaches the bath's specified <concentration>. That approximation need not describe a <solute> bound to a <protein> or a charged mixture with additional electrical constraints. Retaining a <protein> also does not by itself establish that its <protein structure> or catalytic function survives the change of <buffer solution>.

= Repeated-bath dialysis model
{parent=Dialysis (biochemistry)}
{title2=$c_n/c_0=(V_s/(V_s+V_b))^n$}

Consider an original ideal model with sample <volume (thermodynamics)> $V_s$, bath <volume (thermodynamics)> $V_b$, and initial sample <solute> <concentration> $c_0$. Assume each <volume (thermodynamics)> is fixed, a freely permeant, nonbinding <solute>, no <chemical reactions>, complete mixing on each side, and equal <concentrations> at equilibrium. Each fresh bath initially contains none of this <solute>. The <protein> is perfectly retained in this model.

The first equilibration redistributes the initial <amount of substance> $c_0V_s$ through both compartments. Conservation gives
$$
c_0V_s=c_1(V_s+V_b),\qquad
c_1=c_0\frac{V_s}{V_s+V_b}.
$$
This denominator includes the sample itself. For the chosen $V_s=1\,\mathrm{mL}$ and $V_b=9\,\mathrm{mL}$, $c_1/c_0=1/10$, rather than $1/9$. Nine-tenths of the initial <solute> is in the bath and one-tenth remains in the sample; nothing has vanished during equilibration.

Discarding the equilibrated bath removes its <solute>. A second fresh bath starts with only $c_1V_s$ of <solute> in the entire system, so the same argument applies again. Induction gives the <geometric progression>
$$
q=\frac{V_s}{V_s+V_b},\qquad
c_n=q c_{n-1}=c_0q^n,\qquad
\frac{c_n}{c_0}=10^{-n}\quad(V_s=1,\ V_b=9).
$$
Here $n$ counts completed equilibrations with fresh baths: $n=1$ is the first bath, $n=2$ follows one replacement, and $n=3$ follows two replacements. The normalized sample <concentrations> are respectively $0.1$, $0.01$ and $0.001$. Leaving the first bath in place for longer cannot lower the ideal equilibrium value below $0.1$.

\Image[biochemical-dialysis-repeated-bath-model.png]
{title=Repeated fresh baths reduce normalized <solute> <concentration> geometrically}
{description=Original fixed-<volume (thermodynamics)> model, not experimental data. The left panel normalizes the first bath's conserved <amount of substance> by $c_0V_s$, distinguishing the sample's $0.1$ share from the bath's $0.9$ share. The right panel plots sample <solute> <concentration> divided by $c_0$ after each completed fresh-bath equilibration on a logarithmic vertical axis. The <protein> curve is normalized independently by its own initial <concentration>. The perfectly retained <protein> stays at normalized <concentration> one only because its <amount of substance> and the sample <volume (thermodynamics)> are fixed.}

The same conservation argument handles a fresh bath already containing a permeant <solute> at fixed <concentration> $b$:
$$
c_n=\frac{V_sc_{n-1}+V_bb}{V_s+V_b},\qquad
c_n-b=q(c_{n-1}-b),\qquad
c_n=b+(c_0-b)q^n.
$$
Thus the sample approaches $b$, not necessarily zero. For example, if a hypothetical new buffer component starts at zero in the sample and at $b$ in every bath, its sample <concentration> reaches $0.9b$, $0.99b$ and $0.999b$ in the three equilibrations. Outward removal and inward replacement follow the same mathematics.

Equilibrium fractions do not specify elapsed time. An illustrative well-mixed <linear ordinary differential equation> model within one bath is
$$
V_s\frac{dc_s}{dt}=-K(c_s-c_b),\qquad
V_b\frac{dc_b}{dt}=K(c_s-c_b),\qquad K>0.
$$
Here $K$ is an effective transfer coefficient with units of <volume (thermodynamics)> per time. Adding the two equations conserves $V_sc_s+V_bc_b$; subtracting their normalized forms gives
$$
\frac{d(c_s-c_b)}{dt}=-K\left(\frac1{V_s}+\frac1{V_b}\right)(c_s-c_b).
$$
The difference undergoes <exponential decay>. A bath replacement resets $c_b$, renewing the driving difference without making $c_s$ jump instantly. The plotted points are equilibrium endpoints, not a measured time course or a timetable for an arbitrary <protein>.

Real sample <volume (thermodynamics)> can change through <osmosis>; <proteins> can be lost through leakage or <adsorption> to the <semipermeable membrane>. Consequently, a measured decrease in <protein> <concentration> alone cannot distinguish dilution from loss of <protein>. Measuring recovered <volume (thermodynamics)> as well as <protein> <concentration> permits an estimate of recovered <protein> <mass>.<Dialysis: an overview, Pierce Biotechnology technical note>[Tech Tip 20, p. 2, Other Factors in Dialysis: water transport, volume change and nonspecific membrane binding]{ref}

= Dialysis salt-removal and protein-retention experiments
{parent=Dialysis (biochemistry)}

The manufacturer-issued Protein Preparation Handbook reports a paired <dialysis> comparison: $2\,\mathrm{mL}$ of <bovine serum albumin>, $0.25\,\mathrm{mg/mL}$ in $1\,\mathrm M$ <sodium chloride>, was held behind a $10\,\mathrm{kDa}$ <MWCO> membrane against $45\,\mathrm{mL}$ of <water> in a conical tube, shaken at room <temperature>. The bath was replaced after two hours. A conventional stirred-beaker treatment against $2\,\mathrm L$ of <water> supplied the comparison control. Residual <salt> was followed by sample <electrical conductivity>; results averaged two samples. This readout tests small-<ion> removal, not <protein> retention.<Protein Preparation Handbook, Thermo Fisher Scientific>[Protein Preparation Handbook, printed p. 11, Figure 9A and caption: sample preparation, 10K membrane, baths, conductivity and conventional-dialysis reference]{ref}

Its separate retention experiment used individual <proteins> at $1\,\mathrm{mg/mL}$ in saline or carbonate-bicarbonate <buffer solution>, <pH> $9.4$, with membranes rated $2$, $3.5$, $7$, $10$ and $20\,\mathrm{kDa}$. After seventeen hours at $4\,\mathrm{{}^{\circ}C}$, retained <protein> was estimated by a <bicinchoninic acid assay>. The size series and membrane series compare retention; they do not isolate shape independently of <mass>. The figure caption supplies neither a no-membrane recovery control nor a complete uncertainty analysis. These are manufacturer comparisons, not proof of universal recovery or unchanged <enzyme> function.<Protein Preparation Handbook, Thermo Fisher Scientific>[Protein Preparation Handbook, printed p. 11, Figure 8 and caption: membrane series, preparation, duration, temperature and protein assay; the separately plotted vitamin B12 uses a different optical readout]{ref}

The two experiments answer complementary questions: did the small <solute> leave, and did the desired <macromolecule> remain? In a new experiment, a bath blank and a starting <salt> standard would help interpret <electrical conductivity>, while a matched undialyzed <protein> aliquot and recovered <volume (thermodynamics)> would help distinguish handling loss from dilution. Those are proposed additional controls, not controls claimed for the published figures. Other <ions> also contribute to <electrical conductivity>, so the readout cannot generally identify <sodium chloride> alone in an unfamiliar <buffer solution>.

= Selective biochemical reaction pathways
{parent=Biochemistry}

An <enzyme> accelerates particular <chemical reactions>, with <catalytic selectivity> arising from recognition and from the relative ease of the subsequent chemistry. Strong binding alone does not guarantee fast conversion. Nor does <catalysis> change the <chemical equilibrium> for an unchanged net <chemical reaction>. These distinctions explain how <enzymes> can direct the flow of material without creating an extra source of <free energy>.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others>[§12.7, opening paragraphs and Enzyme Structure and Function: alternative mechanisms, regeneration and specificity]{ref}

For an original model of competing routes, let a <substrate> $S$ become either a desired <reaction product> $P$ or another <reaction product> $Q$:
$$
S\xrightarrow{k_P}P,\qquad S\xrightarrow{k_Q}Q.
$$
The labels represent hypothetical <chemical species> with the same elemental composition, not molecular structures. Assume fixed volume, irreversible <first-order reactions>, constant effective <rate constants>, and initially $100\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of $S$ with neither <reaction product> present. The model approximates a limited operating regime; real <enzyme kinetics> can include saturation and reverse reactions.

\Image[biochemical-competing-reaction-routes.png]
{title=Selective acceleration changes the split between competing biochemical <reaction products>}
{description=Original illustrative <chemical reaction network> and limiting product amounts from $100\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of hypothetical $S$. Chosen effective <rate constants> are $k_P=0.08$ or $0.32\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$ and $k_Q=0.02\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$. The boxes label <chemical species>; they are not molecular structures. The bars are model limits, not experimental data.}

The <reaction-rate equations> give $dn_S/dt=-(k_P+k_Q)n_S$, $dn_P/dt=k_Pn_S$ and $dn_Q/dt=k_Qn_S$, where $n$ denotes <amount of substance>. Consequently,
$$
n_S=n_0e^{-(k_P+k_Q)t},\qquad
n_P=\frac{k_P}{k_P+k_Q}(n_0-n_S),\qquad
n_Q=\frac{k_Q}{k_P+k_Q}(n_0-n_S).
$$
With $k_P=0.08$ and $k_Q=0.02\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$, the eventual split is $80\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of $P$ and $20\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of $Q$. At ten minutes, the calculated amounts are approximately $36.8$, $50.6$ and $12.6\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ for $S$, $P$ and $Q$. Selectively increasing $k_P$ fourfold changes the eventual desired fraction to $0.32/0.34=16/17\approx94.1\%$. Increasing both <rate constants> fourfold instead preserves the $80\%$ split and only shortens the conversion time. Speed and selectivity answer different questions.

The bookkeeping matters independently of that model. In ideal <alcoholic fermentation>, complete conversion of $3\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of <glucose> yields $6\,\mathrm{mmol}$ each of <ethanol> and <carbon dioxide>. Of the initial $18\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of <carbon> <chemical atoms>, $12$ remain in <ethanol> and $6$ enter <carbon dioxide>. This is a chosen material-balance example using the net <stoichiometry> explained under <Saccharomyces cerevisiae>; it gives neither an observed yield nor the mechanism of the individual steps. Equal amounts of the two <reaction products> also do not mean equal <masses>.

= ATP-driven sugar phosphorylation
{c}
{parent=Biochemistry}

<Hexokinase> provides a concrete connection between <sugar> chemistry and <energy coupling>: it transfers a terminal <phosphate> group from <ATP> to <glucose>, forming a phosphorylated <sugar> and <adenosine diphosphate>. The <enzyme> accelerates the transfer, while consuming <ATP> changes the net <chemical reaction> being carried out. The donated group reaches the <substrate> through the catalytic route.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[§7.2, First Half of Glycolysis, step 1: hexokinase and ATP-dependent glucose phosphorylation]{ref}

For a symbolic teaching model, write $S$ for an unphosphorylated <sugar>, $S\text{-}P$ for its phosphorylated form, and $P_i$ for <inorganic phosphate>. These are labels for chemical states, not complete elemental formulas. Using one consistent reference convention at fixed <pH> and <temperature>, stipulate the following molar changes in <Gibbs free energy>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
S+P_i&\longrightarrow S\text{-}P+\mathrm{H_2O},
&\Delta G_1^{\circ\prime}&=+12\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}},\\
\mathrm{ATP}+\mathrm{H_2O}&\longrightarrow\mathrm{ADP}+P_i,
&\Delta G_2^{\circ\prime}&=-32\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}},\\
S+\mathrm{ATP}&\longrightarrow S\text{-}P+\mathrm{ADP},
&\Delta G_{\mathrm{net}}^{\circ\prime}&=-20\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
All three numbers are chosen illustrative values, not measured properties of <glucose> or a particular <enzyme>. The prime marks a biochemical reference convention with <pH> held fixed; explicit proton and metal-binding bookkeeping is suppressed consistently. The first two lines are a thermodynamic decomposition: cancelling <water> and <inorganic phosphate> gives the net transfer. They do not assert that the <reaction mechanism> first releases free <inorganic phosphate> and then recaptures it.

{description=Original symbolic reaction diagram, with a schematic catalytic cycle and a separate thermodynamic sum. $S$ and $S\text{-}P$ label hypothetical <sugar> states. The cycle groups many possible microscopic steps and draws no molecular structure. Because <Gibbs free energy> is a <state function>, consistently defined reaction changes add. A working <reaction mechanism> must also connect the desired conversion to <ATP> consumption; independently allowing <ATP> <hydrolysis> beside the <sugar> does not establish <energy coupling>. The general distinction is developed under <energy coupling>. <ATP>-dependent <phosphorylation> of a membrane <protein> can likewise connect a chemical change to <active transport>.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[§6.4, Energy Coupling, Figure 6.14: transfer to the sodium-potassium pump and its conformational cycle]{ref}

Actual <Gibbs free energy> changes depend on <thermodynamic activities>. Even a favourable reference sum can be overcome by accumulation of <reaction products> or depletion of <ATP>; it supplies no prediction of the <reaction rate>. A living <biological cell> also regenerates <ATP>. Sustained operation therefore requires a connected <metabolism>, not an inexhaustible stock of a special <molecule>.

= Cell-free extract
{parent=Biochemistry}

A <cell-free extract> is material obtained from disrupted <biological cells> after removing intact <biological cells>. Its biochemical activity can be tested separately from the whole <organism>. It can contain many <enzymes> and <substrates>, together with <cofactor (biochemistry)> components; cell removal is different from purification of one component.

= Cell-free fermentation as a biochemical experiment
{parent=Cell-free extract}

The <cell-free extract> experiment under <yeast> asks whether <fermentation> requires intact living <biological cells>. In his 1907 lecture, <Eduard Buchner> described grinding <yeast> with <quartz> sand and <diatomite> in a large mortar using a heavy pestle, wrapping the dough in canvas, then squeezing it in a hydraulic press. Adding concentrated <sugar> syrup to the expressed liquid produced <carbon dioxide> bubbles and foam; separate investigations identified <ethanol>.<Cell-free fermentation by Eduard Buchner>[Cell-free fermentation, Nobel lecture delivered 11 December 1907, printed pp. 110–112, Figures 2–4 and accompanying preparation/readout text]{ref}

Activity persisted after filtration through cell-removing porcelain candles. <Toluene> inhibited live <yeast> <fermentation> while the <cell-free extract> retained activity, addressing a different objection from cell removal. These comparisons support extracted chemical machinery as the cause; bubbles alone would not identify <alcoholic fermentation>.<Cell-free fermentation by Eduard Buchner>[Printed pp. 112–114: residual-cell objection, biscuit-porcelain filtration, toluene comparison and extract activity]{ref}

The inference is activity outside intact living <biological cells>, not that <biological cells> are unnecessary for making or maintaining the machinery. Nor was this a purified single-<enzyme> mechanism: the lecture acknowledges unsuccessful purification and reports that two separated inactive fractions recovered <fermentation> when reunited. Such a reconstitution asks which components must work together, extending the initial separation of <organism> from chemical activity.<Cell-free fermentation by Eduard Buchner>[Printed p. 115: unsuccessful zymase separation; pp. 117–118: gelatine-filter fractions and restored activity on reunion]{ref}

= Metabolic pathway
{parent=Biochemistry}
{wiki}

A <metabolic pathway> is a connected sequence of <enzyme>-catalyzed <chemical reactions> in <metabolism>, with a <reaction product> of one step serving as a <substrate> for another. A pathway boundary is a choice about which transformations to count: <glycolysis> ends at <pyruvate>, whereas subsequent <fermentation> or <cellular respiration> changes the final material balance. Adding the individual <chemical equations> cancels internal intermediates and exposes the net <stoichiometry>; it does not determine the <reaction rate>.

= Glycolysis
{parent=Metabolic pathway}
{tag=Energy metabolism}
{wiki}

<Glycolysis> converts one <glucose> into two <pyruvates>, yielding two net <ATP> and reducing two <NAD+> to two <NADH> in the canonical pathway. It first spends two <ATP>, then produces four by <substrate-level phosphorylation>. The pathway itself consumes no molecular <oxygen>; sustained operation nevertheless requires regeneration of <NAD+>.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[§7.2, First Half of Glycolysis, step 5; Second Half of Glycolysis, steps 6–10; Outcomes of Glycolysis]{ref}

The useful organizing idea is three separate ledgers: <carbon> <chemical atoms>, transfers between <ADP> and <ATP>, and <redox> transfers between <NAD+> and <NADH>. Splitting the <carbon> skeleton doubles the number of downstream conversions; recycling a <cofactor (biochemistry)> permits repeated operation. These counts explain the yield without memorizing a drawing of every <enzyme>. The two net <ATP> belong to <glycolysis> alone, not to complete <cellular respiration>.

= Glycolysis carbon and ATP ledger
{c}
{parent=Glycolysis}

Follow one <glucose> through canonical <glycolysis>, with no diversion of intermediates into other <metabolic pathways>. The investment stage uses two <ATP> to prepare the six-<carbon> <substrate>. Cleavage and rearrangement provide two <glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate> <molecules>, each containing three <carbon> <chemical atoms>. Each then yields one three-<carbon> <pyruvate>, one <NADH>, and two <ATP> in the payoff stage.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[§7.2, steps 3–7 and 10; Figures 7.8–7.9 captions: investment, two glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate molecules and duplicated payoff]{ref}

\Image[glycolysis-carbon-atp-stages.png]
{title=<Glycolysis> preserves six <carbon> <chemical atoms> while four gross <ATP> repay a two-<ATP> investment}
{description=Original aggregate-stage schematic per <glucose>, not a complete <enzyme> map or measured data. Dots count <carbon> <chemical atoms>, without assigning their positions in the <molecules>. Both three-<carbon> routes perform the same payoff sequence. The running <ATP> ledger starts at zero relative to the initial inventory; negative two means expenditure from an existing pool.}

The material check is $6=2\times3$: all six <carbon> <chemical atoms> remain in the two <pyruvates>. No <carbon dioxide> has been released within this boundary. In the <ATP> ledger, each three-<carbon> route contains two <ATP>-forming transfers, so
$$
\text{gross ATP}=2\times2=4,\qquad
\text{net ATP}=4-2=2.
$$
The first pair of payoff transfers only restores the initial <ATP> inventory; the second pair creates the net gain. Four newly formed <ATP> is therefore compatible with only two additional <ATP> remaining.

For an original scaled example, stipulate complete conversion of $5\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of <glucose>. The initial $30\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of <carbon> <chemical atoms> becomes $10\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of <pyruvate>, still containing $30\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of <carbon> <chemical atoms>. The pathway spends $10\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of <ATP>, forms $20\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of <ATP>, and adds $10\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ to the <ATP> inventory before any other <ATP> use. It also reduces $10\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of <NAD+>. These are calculated ideal amounts, not observed yields; side routes or incomplete conversion change the outcome.

A common biochemical net <chemical equation> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{glucose}+2\,\mathrm{ADP}+2\,\mathrm P_i+2\,\mathrm{NAD}^{+}
\longrightarrow {}&2\,\mathrm{pyruvate}+2\,\mathrm{ATP}\\
&+2\,\mathrm{NADH}+2\,\mathrm H^{+}+2\,\mathrm{H_2O}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $\mathrm P_i$ is <inorganic phosphate>. The names group biochemical <protonation> and metal-binding states; the displayed <proton> and <water> terms use a common shorthand rather than a unique specification of every <chemical species>. The robust ledger records two net <ADP>-to-<ATP> conversions and two <NAD+>-to-<NADH> conversions. Summing the individual <chemical equations> cancels two invested <ATP> against four formed <ATP> and cancels the internal <substrates>, giving those coefficients.

= Glycolysis cofactor recycling
{parent=Glycolysis}

In the payoff stage of <glycolysis>, <oxidation> of <glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate> is coupled to <reduction> of <NAD+>. Each <NADH> carries a two-<electron> reducing equivalent. The formal half-<chemical equation>
$$
\mathrm{NAD}^{+}+\mathrm H^{+}+2e^-\longrightarrow\mathrm{NADH}
$$
makes the <electron> count explicit: two <NADH> per <glucose> carry four <electrons>. This is <redox> bookkeeping, not a claim that free <electrons> travel through the <chemical solution> or that <NADH> is itself <ATP>.

<Fermentation> can return <NADH> to <NAD+> by reducing an organic <substrate>; reduction of <pyruvate> to <lactate> is one example. In aerobic <cellular respiration>, reducing equivalents reach an <electron transport chain>, reducing molecular <oxygen>. Other forms of <cellular respiration> use other <electron acceptors>, so absence of <oxygen> does not uniquely imply <fermentation>.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[§7.2, step 6 and its NAD+ regeneration paragraph; §7.5, opening comparison of fermentation and anaerobic respiration, Lactic Acid Fermentation and Figure 7.16 caption]{ref}

The need for <NAD+> regeneration follows from a finite-pool argument. In a hypothetical closed preparation with $12\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of initially oxidized <NAD+> and no regeneration, at most $6\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of <glucose> can complete the two <NAD+>-consuming conversions per <glucose>. This is an upper bound even with excess <glucose>, not a predicted stopping time: other limitations can stop the preparation earlier. With an intact fixed <nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide> pool and no side reactions, converting <NAD+> into <NADH> preserves their sum but depletes the usable oxidized form.

<ATP> recycling imposes a second condition. <Glycolysis> needs some initial <ATP> for investment and consumes two net <ADP> and two <inorganic phosphate> per completed <glucose>. In a closed ideal preparation with only $8\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of initial <ADP>, no other source of <ADP>, and enough initial <ATP> and <inorganic phosphate>, at most $4\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of <glucose> can finish. This bound follows from the net <ADP> consumption, even though investment temporarily produces additional <ADP>. Initial investment also means that adequate final inventories alone cannot guarantee the route can start.

For continued operation at fixed carrier inventories, other <chemical reactions> must match the two net <ATP> formed per <glucose> with <ATP> use that restores <ADP> and <inorganic phosphate>, or exchange must supply those <reactants>. <ATP> <hydrolysis> coupled to cellular work is one route. Thus <glycolysis> can sustain useful <energy coupling> while neither its <ATP> inventory nor its <NADH> inventory grows indefinitely; matter cycles while <glucose> supplies fresh chemical <free energy>.

= Coupled-enzyme readout of glycolytic ATP formation
{c}
{parent=Glycolysis}

The final <substrate-level phosphorylation> in <glycolysis> provides a useful experimental handle: <pyruvate kinase> transfers a <phosphate> group from <phosphoenolpyruvate> to <ADP>, producing <pyruvate> and <ATP>. Adding <lactate dehydrogenase> couples each <pyruvate> formed to consumption of one <NADH>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{phosphoenolpyruvate}+\mathrm{ADP}+\mathrm H^+
&\longrightarrow\mathrm{pyruvate}+\mathrm{ATP},\\
\mathrm{pyruvate}+\mathrm{NADH}+\mathrm H^+
&\longrightarrow\mathrm{lactate}+\mathrm{NAD}^{+}.
\end{aligned}
$$
These equations select free ionic forms consistently: <phosphoenolpyruvate> has charge $-3$, <ADP> $-3$, <ATP> $-4$, and <pyruvate> and <lactate> each $-1$; <NAD+> has one more positive charge than <NADH>. Each line conserves charge. Cancelling <pyruvate> gives one <NADH> consumed per <ATP> formed in this coupled system. A whole-pathway <NADH> measurement has a different meaning, because the earlier <glycolysis> <redox> step produces <NADH>.

The published An Optimized Enzyme-Coupled Spectrophotometric Method for Measuring Pyruvate Kinase Kinetics reports recombinant <pyruvate kinase> prepared in <Escherichia coli>, purified by <chromatography> and <size-exclusion chromatography>. UV-transparent microplate wells contained <ADP>, <phosphoenolpyruvate>, <NADH>, <lactate dehydrogenase> and a <buffer solution> with <magnesium> <ions>. An Epoch plate reader used <spectrophotometry> to follow <absorbance> at $340\,\mathrm{nm}$ every five to ten seconds for one minute at $25\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. Its Figure 2 presents $A_0-A_t$, increasing as <NADH> is consumed. Heat-inactivated <pyruvate kinase> gave minimal background; a validated active preparation was the positive control. These comparisons support an <enzyme>-dependent readout in the prepared system, rather than a whole-<biological cell> <glycolysis> measurement.<An Optimized Enzyme-Coupled Spectrophotometric Method for Measuring Pyruvate Kinase Kinetics by Saurabh Upadhyay>[Procedure A.5–6 and B.2; Equipment item 1; Figure 2 and caption; Validation of protocol: preparation, optical readout and controls]{ref}

The same report uses $0.5\,\mathrm{cm}$ for optical path length in Procedure B.2.i, but approximately $0.3\,\mathrm{cm}$ in Data analysis §3. An absolute conversion requires the actual path length and sufficient coupling <enzyme> activity; no measured <ATP> rate is inferred here.<An Optimized Enzyme-Coupled Spectrophotometric Method for Measuring Pyruvate Kinase Kinetics by Saurabh Upadhyay>[Procedure B.2.i; Data analysis §3; General notes, items 4 and 6: path-length inconsistency and linear-range conditions]{ref}

For an original dimensional example, stipulate a blank-corrected <absorbance> decline $-dA_{340}/dt=0.024\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$, a calibrated path length $\ell=0.40\,\mathrm{cm}$ and a chosen calibration coefficient $\varepsilon=6000\,\mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}\,cm^{-1}}$. These are invented teaching inputs, not the protocol's measurements or its quoted coefficient. The <Beer-Lambert law> gives
$$
A_{340}=\varepsilon\ell c_{\mathrm{NADH}},\qquad
-\frac{dc_{\mathrm{NADH}}}{dt}
=\frac{0.024}{6000\times0.40}
=10^{-5}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,min^{-1}}.
$$
In a stipulated $0.20\,\mathrm{mL}$ reaction <volume (thermodynamics)>, this is $2\,\mathrm{nmol\,min^{-1}}$ of <NADH> consumed and, conditionally, $2\,\mathrm{nmol\,min^{-1}}$ of <ATP> formed. The conditional inference requires rapid <pyruvate> consumption by the coupling <enzyme>, no other <NADH>-consuming <chemical reactions>, and a linear, correctly calibrated optical response. The readout measures this last <glycolysis> step; it cannot establish the full two-net-<ATP>-per-<glucose> balance by itself.

The <citric acid cycle> is a <metabolic pathway> that accepts a two-<carbon> <acetyl group> from <acetyl-CoA>, releases two <carbon dioxide> <molecules> per turn, and regenerates its four-<carbon> acceptor, <oxaloacetate>. The <carbon> sequence is <oxaloacetate> $(4)$ plus an <acetyl group> $(2)$ to <citrate> $(6)$, then <isocitrate> $(6)$, <alpha-ketoglutarate> $(5)$, <succinyl-CoA> $(4)$, <succinate> $(4)$, <fumarate> $(4)$, <malate> $(4)$ and back to <oxaloacetate> $(4)$. Per <acetyl group>: three <NADH>, one <FADH2> equivalent, one <GTP> or <ATP> by <substrate-level phosphorylation>, and two <carbon dioxide>. <Succinate dehydrogenase> retains <FADH2> and transfers its <electrons> to <ubiquinone>, forming <ubiquinol>. A useful check adds the stepwise changes. The <carbon> count is $4+2-1-1=4$; the <NADH> count is $1+1+1=3$. Each <NADH> carries two <electrons>, and the <succinate dehydrogenase> route supplies another pair, so one turn exports $3\times2+2=8$ <electrons>. This arithmetic is an accounting identity, not an <ATP> yield calculation. Converting it to <ATP> requires the separate <energy coupling> and transport assumptions discussed under <oxidative phosphorylation>.

For a chosen ideal conversion of $5\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of <acetyl groups>, multiplication gives $15\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of <NADH>, $5\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of <ubiquinol>, $5\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of <GTP> and $10\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of <carbon dioxide>. The <electron> output is $40\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$. If all these <electrons> subsequently reach <oxygen> with no competing sinks, four <electrons> per <oxygen> <molecule> require $10\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of <oxygen>. That is consumption by the connected <electron transport chain>; it is not an <oxygen> term in the cycle's own <substrate> ledger.

No cycle step directly consumes molecular <oxygen>. In ordinary aerobic mitochondrial operation, however, the <mitochondrial respiratory chain> must reoxidize <NADH> and <ubiquinol> to restore the oxidized <electron carriers>. Without that outlet, a finite oxidized-carrier pool cannot sustain continued turnover. Other <electron acceptors> and other forms of <metabolism> require their own analysis.<Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others/How Cells Obtain Energy from Food>[Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others; Chapter 2, How Cells Obtain Energy from Food, The Citric Acid Cycle Generates NADH by Oxidizing Acetyl Groups to CO2: lack of direct O2 consumption and need to regenerate NAD+]{ref}

The <pyruvate dehydrogenase complex> acts upstream: one three-<carbon> <pyruvate> gives one two-<carbon> <acetyl group>, one <carbon dioxide> and one <NADH>. These are additional to the cycle ledger.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[§7.3, Breakdown of Pyruvate, steps 1–3 and Figure 7.10]{ref} Consequently, processing one <pyruvate> through both stages gives three <carbon dioxide> and four <NADH>, while the <succinate dehydrogenase> contribution and the <substrate-level phosphorylation> contribution each remain one. Counting the upstream step inside the cycle would obscure where the extra <carbon dioxide> and <NADH> arise.

= Citric acid cycle first-turn carbon tracing
{parent=Citric acid cycle}

Net <stoichiometry> does not identify individual <chemical atoms>. In the ordinary forward <citric acid cycle>, neither entering <acetyl group> <carbon> is released in that same first turn: both released <carbon dioxide> <carbon> <chemical atoms> came from the starting <oxaloacetate>. The entering <carbon> remains in regenerated intermediates.<The Citric Acid Cycle (Boston University lecture slides)>[The Citric Acid Cycle, slide numbered 4, Origin of C-Atoms in CO2: entering acetyl carbons retained and released carbons from starting oxaloacetate]{ref}

Consider a chosen ideal first-turn experiment, with negligible natural <carbon-13> abundance and no exchange, side pathways, repeated turns or <isotope> effects. Supply $10\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of initially unlabelled <oxaloacetate> and $10\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of <acetyl-CoA>, of which $6\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ has both <acetyl group> <carbon> positions labelled with <carbon-13>. The other $4\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ has neither position labelled. The label refers only to the <acetyl group>, not to <coenzyme A>.

The input contains $2\times6=12\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of labelled <carbon> <chemical atoms>. The $10\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of <citrate> initially formed contains $60\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of <carbon> <chemical atoms>, so its labelled-<carbon> fraction is $12/60=20\%$. After exactly one forward traversal per starting <oxaloacetate>, the $20\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of released <carbon dioxide> has zero label, while the regenerated $10\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of <oxaloacetate> contains all $12\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of labelled <carbon> <chemical atoms> among $40\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ total: $30\%$.

= Respiratory control ratio
{parent=Respiratory control}

For a constructed example, choose constant suspension <volume (thermodynamics)> $V=2\,\mathrm{mL}$ and <oxygen> <concentration> declines of $0.030$ and $0.006\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}\,min^{-1}}$ during the two comparison windows. $$
v=-V\frac{dc_{\mathrm O_2}}{dt},\qquad
v_{\rm ADP}=60\,\mathrm{nmol\,min^{-1}},\qquad
v_{\rm after}=12\,\mathrm{nmol\,min^{-1}},\qquad

= Chemiosmosis
{parent=Oxidative phosphorylation}
{wiki}

<Chemiosmosis> couples transmembrane <ion> movement down an <electrochemical gradient> to a chemical transformation. For mitochondrial <ATP> synthesis, <protons> return from the <intermembrane space> to the <mitochondrial matrix> through <ATP synthase>.<Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others>[Chapter 14, Electron-Transport Chains and Their Proton Pumps, Summary: inward proton flow driving ATP synthesis]{ref}

An original thermodynamic example separates the electrical and chemical contributions. At fixed <temperature> $T$, use ideal dilute <thermodynamic activities> $a$ and the <electric potential> $\psi$ on each side. The molar <electrochemical potential> difference for inward transfer of a singly charged <proton> is
$$
\Delta\widetilde\mu_{\rm in}
=RT\ln\!\left(\frac{a_{\rm matrix}}{a_{\rm outside}}\right)
+F(\psi_{\rm matrix}-\psi_{\rm outside}).
$$
Here $R$ is the <molar gas constant>, $F$ the <Faraday constant>, and $\ln$ the <natural logarithm>. The sign convention is destination minus origin: a negative difference makes inward transfer favourable. Both lower <proton> activity and a more negative <electric potential> inside favour inward movement.

Stipulate $T=300\,\mathrm K$, $a_{\rm matrix}/a_{\rm outside}=0.1$, $\psi_{\rm matrix}-\psi_{\rm outside}=-0.12\,\mathrm V$, $R=8.314\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ and $F=96485\,\mathrm{C\,mol^{-1}}$. These illustrative inputs give approximately $-5.743$ and $-11.578\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ for the chemical and electrical terms, hence $-17.321\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ per transferred <proton>. For a hypothetical device coupling three inward <protons> per <ATP> to an <ATP>-formation cost of $45\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$,
$$
\Delta G_{\rm coupled}=45+3\Delta\widetilde\mu_{\rm in}
\approx-6.964\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}.
$$
The sign follows by adding changes in the <state function> <Gibbs free energy>. It establishes thermodynamic feasibility for this chosen model, not its <reaction rate>. Three is a stipulated coupling count, not a universal <ATP synthase> <stoichiometry>; transport costs and leaks can also consume the available <free energy>.

= Respiratory uncoupling
{parent=Oxidative phosphorylation}

<Respiratory uncoupling> allows <proton> return to bypass <ATP synthase>, dissipating the <electrochemical gradient>. <Electron> transport and <oxygen> consumption can continue while less <ATP> is recovered.<Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others>[Chapter 14, Electron-Transport Chains and Their Proton Pumps, H+ Ionophores Uncouple Electron Transport from ATP Synthesis]{ref}

In the thermodynamic example under <chemiosmosis>, reducing both the activity ratio to one and the <electric potential> difference to zero removes the driving contribution. The hypothetical <ATP>-forming conversion then costs $+45\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. Continued donor <oxidation> would not restore coupling unless it rebuilt a usable <electrochemical gradient>. This distinction makes <respiratory uncoupling> useful conceptually: abundant <oxygen> consumption can coexist with poor recovery of <ATP>.

= Substrate-level phosphorylation
{parent=Energy metabolism}
{wiki}

<Substrate-level phosphorylation> forms <ATP> by <enzyme>-catalyzed transfer of a <phosphate> group from a phosphorylated <substrate> to <ADP>. In canonical <glycolysis>, two such transfers occur for each three-<carbon> route, giving four gross <ATP> per <glucose>. Unlike <oxidative phosphorylation>, these transfers do not require an <electron transport chain>.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[§7.2, steps 7 and 10: phosphate transfer to ADP; §7.5, opening comparison of fermentation with respiration]{ref}

The <pyruvate kinase> example under <coupled-enzyme readout of glycolytic ATP formation> separates the material transfer from its detection. A favourable coupled <chemical reaction> supplies the driving change in <Gibbs free energy>; the <enzyme> supplies a catalytic route. Counting <ATP>-forming transfers gives a gross yield, while subtracting upstream <ATP> use gives the net yield.

= Proteolysis
{parent=Biochemistry}
{wiki}

<Proteolysis> cleaves <peptide bonds> in <proteins>; it differs from <reduction> of <protein disulfide bonds>.

= Degron
{parent=Proteolysis}

A recognition feature that helps target a <protein> for degradation. It may depend on sequence, conformation or a regulated modification rather than being permanently exposed.

= Phosphodegron
{parent=Degron}

A <degron> whose recognition depends on <phosphorylation>. Phosphorylation can thereby couple protein turnover to a regulatory signal; not every phosphorylated motif is a degradation signal.

= Peptide mapping
{parent=Biochemistry}

<Peptide mapping> compares separated <peptides> produced by cleavage of a <protein>. Matching patterns provide a chemical comparison beyond an intact <protein>'s <gel electrophoresis> position.

= Glycosylation
{parent=Biochemistry}
{wiki}

<Glycosylation> attaches <carbohydrates> to other <molecules>, including <proteins> and <lipids>.<Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others/Transport from the ER through the Golgi Apparatus>[Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others; opening discussion and Oligosaccharide Chains Are Processed in the Golgi Apparatus]{ref}

= Protein glycosylation
{parent=Glycosylation}

<protein glycosylation> attaches a carbohydrate group to a <protein> directly or extends a protein-associated glycan. Donor supply, acceptor accessibility, <enzyme> specificity and compartmentation jointly determine the product.

= O-linked glycosylation
{parent=Protein glycosylation}
{c}

<O-linked glycosylation> attaches a sugar through an oxygen atom on the acceptor, often a <protein> hydroxyl group. Several different pathways and donor specificities fall under this description.

= N-linked glycosylation
{parent=Protein glycosylation}
{c}

<N-linked glycosylation> attaches a glycan through a nitrogen atom, commonly the side-chain amide nitrogen of asparagine. In the ER pathway, an assembled lipid-linked oligosaccharide is transferred to an appropriate <protein> acceptor.
