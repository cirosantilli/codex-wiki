= Solution

In a real <Hilbert space>, <weak convergence in a Hilbert space> means
$$
\boxed{x_n\rightharpoonup x\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\langle x_n,z\rangle\longrightarrow\langle x,z\rangle\ \text{for every }z\in X.}
$$
By the <Riesz representation theorem>, every <continuous linear functional> on $X$ has the form $u\mapsto\langle u,z\rangle$. Thus this is exactly <weak convergence>, defined by convergence under every <continuous linear functional>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Define the <continuous linear functionals> $f_n(z)=\langle x_n,z\rangle$. For each fixed $z\in X$, <weak convergence> makes $f_n(z)$ a convergent, hence bounded, scalar sequence. The <uniform boundedness principle> states that a pointwise bounded family of <bounded linear operators> from a <Banach space> to a <normed space> has uniformly bounded <operator norms>. Since a <Hilbert space> is a <Banach space>, it applies to $(f_n)$.

The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives $\|f_n\|\leq\|x_n\|$, and evaluation at $z=x_n/\|x_n\|$ gives equality when $x_n\ne0$; equality also holds at zero. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\sup_n\|x_n\|=\sup_n\|f_n\|<\infty.}
$$

By <weakly convergent sequence is bounded>, $M=\sup_n\|x_n\|^2$ is finite. Choose any $n_1$. Once $n_1<\cdots<n_k$ have been chosen, put $s_k=\sum_{j=1}^kx_{n_j}$. Since $s_k$ is fixed, <weak convergence> to zero gives $\langle s_k,x_n\rangle\to0$. Choose $n_{k+1}>n_k$ with $|\langle s_k,x_{n_{k+1}}\rangle|\leq1$. This gives the required one-sided inequality as well.

The useful estimate is for the **squared** <norm>. Indeed, $\|s_1\|^2\leq M$, and the <inner product> expansion gives
$$
\|s_{k+1}\|^2
=\|s_k\|^2+2\langle s_k,x_{n_{k+1}}\rangle+\|x_{n_{k+1}}\|^2
\leq\|s_k\|^2+2+M.
$$
By <mathematical induction>, $\|s_k\|^2\leq Mk+2(k-1)\leq(M+2)k$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\left\|\frac1m\sum_{k=1}^m x_{n_k}\right\|^2\leq\frac{M+2}{m}\longrightarrow0.}
$$
This proves <norm convergence> of the averages, the <weak Banach–Saks theorem in a Hilbert space>. The printed inequality omits the square on its left side. Our stronger estimate also proves that printed bound, since $\sqrt{(M+2)k}\leq(M+2)k$ for $k\geq1$, but the printed bound alone only bounds the averages and cannot imply their <norm convergence> to zero.

First suppose the <convex set> $K$ is closed in the <norm topology>, and $x_n\in K$ satisfies $x_n\rightharpoonup x$. Apply the <weak Banach–Saks theorem in a Hilbert space> to the <weakly null sequence> $x_n-x$. Along the resulting subsequence,
$$
z_m=\frac1m\sum_{k=1}^m x_{n_k}\longrightarrow x
$$
in <norm>. Each $z_m$ is a <convex combination> of points in $K$, so $z_m\in K$. Since $K$ is a <closed set> in the <norm topology>, $x\in K$.

Conversely, suppose $K$ is a <sequentially weakly closed set>. If $x\in\overline K$ in the <norm topology>, choose $x_n\in K$ with $\|x_n-x\|<1/n$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives $|\langle x_n-x,z\rangle|\leq\|x_n-x\|\|z\|\to0$ for every $z$, hence <weak convergence> to $x$. The assumed closure property gives $x\in K$. The empty <set> satisfies both properties too. Thus
$$
\boxed{K\text{ convex}:\quad K\text{ norm-closed}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ K\text{ sequentially weakly closed}.}
$$

The <orthogonal complement> $x^\perp$ is infinite-dimensional: otherwise $X=\operatorname{span}\{x\}\oplus x^\perp$ would be finite-dimensional. Inductively choose an <orthonormal sequence> $(e_n)$ in $x^\perp$, each new vector being orthogonal to the preceding finite <linear span>. The <Bessel inequality> gives $\sum_n|\langle e_n,z\rangle|^2\leq\|z\|^2$ for every $z\in X$, so $\langle e_n,z\rangle\to0$. Thus $(e_n)$ is a <weakly null orthonormal sequence>.

Set
$$
\boxed{x_n=x+\sqrt{1-\|x\|^2}\,e_n.}
$$
By <orthogonality>, $\|x_n\|^2=\|x\|^2+(1-\|x\|^2)=1$, while $\langle x_n-x,z\rangle\to0$ for every $z$. Hence $x_n\rightharpoonup x$. When $\|x\|=1$, this formula simply gives the constant sequence $x_n=x$. In particular, the <weak closure of the unit sphere> contains the whole <closed unit ball>.

= Solution

For each real $a$, define the <sublevel set> $K_a=\{u\in K:F(u)\leq a\}$. Continuity of $F$ and closure of $K$ make $K_a$ a <closed set> in $X$; the <convex function> inequality makes it a <convex set>. The preceding result therefore makes every $K_a$ a <sequentially weakly closed set>. Equivalently, $F$ has <weak lower semicontinuity> along sequences in $K$.

Every $K_a$ is bounded. This is immediate if $K$ is bounded. Otherwise, an unbounded $K_a$ would contain $u_n$ with $\|u_n\|\geq n$, contradicting the assumed <coercive function> condition $F(u_n)\to\infty$.

Choose $z\in K$ and put $\ell=\inf_KF\in[-\infty,F(z)]$. Choose a <minimizing sequence> $(u_n)$ entirely in $K_{F(z)}$, with $F(u_n)\to\ell$; when $\ell=-\infty$, require $F(u_n)\leq-n$ for sufficiently large $n$. It is bounded by the previous paragraph.

The <weak subsequence of a bounded Hilbert-space sequence> theorem gives $u_{n_j}\rightharpoonup u_0$. This applies even if $X$ is not separable: the closed <linear span> of the sequence is a <separable Hilbert space>; successive extraction makes every coefficient in a countable <Hilbertian basis> converge, the common <norm> bound gives square-summable limiting coefficients by the <Bessel inequality>, and approximation by finite <linear combinations> proves <weak convergence>. <Orthogonal decomposition> extends that conclusion to all test vectors in $X$. Since $K$ is a <sequentially weakly closed set>, $u_0\in K$.

For any real $a>\ell$, a tail of $(u_{n_j})$ lies in $K_a$, so $u_0\in K_a$. If $\ell=-\infty$, this would give $F(u_0)\leq a$ for every real $a$, impossible because $F$ is real-valued. Thus $\ell$ is finite; letting $a\downarrow\ell$ gives $F(u_0)\leq\ell$, while the definition of <infimum> gives the reverse inequality. Therefore
$$
\boxed{u_0\in K,\qquad F(u_0)=\min_{u\in K}F(u).}
$$
This proves <minimum of a coercive convex functional on a Hilbert space> by the <direct method in the calculus of variations>, with bounded <sublevel sets> supplying the required <weak sequential compactness in a Hilbert space>.

The <Banach-Steinhaus convergence theorem> can be stated as follows. Let $X$ be a <normed space>, $Y$ a <Banach space>, and $A_n\in\mathcal B(X,Y)$. If
$$
\sup_n\|A_n\|<\infty
$$
and $A_nd$ converges in $Y$ for every $d$ in a <dense subset> $D\subseteq X$, then $A_nx$ converges for every $x\in X$. The pointwise limit $A$ is a <bounded linear operator> and
$$
\boxed{A_nx\longrightarrow Ax\quad(x\in X),\qquad\|A\|\leq\liminf_n\|A_n\|.}
$$
If $X$ is also a <Banach space>, the uniform <operator norm> bound is necessary: pointwise convergence implies pointwise boundedness, so the <Banach-Steinhaus theorem>, namely the <uniform boundedness principle>, supplies it. The limit is convergence in the <strong operator topology>; it need not be convergence in <operator norm>.

To see the sufficient direction, write $C=\sup_n\|A_n\|$. For $d\in D$ the <triangle inequality> gives
$$
\|A_nx-A_mx\|\leq2C\|x-d\|+\|A_nd-A_md\|.
$$
First approximate $x$ by $d$, then let $m,n\to\infty$. Completeness of $Y$ gives the limit. Linearity passes to limits, and $\|Ax\|=\lim_n\|A_nx\|\leq(\liminf_n\|A_n\|)\|x\|$ gives the displayed bound. If a candidate <bounded linear operator> $A:X\to Y$ is already given and $A_nd\to Ad$ on $D$, the same approximation proves convergence to $Ax$ even when $Y$ is merely a <normed space>.

Composition with $t\mapsto t^{1+\alpha_n}$ preserves continuity, so $A_n$ maps the <space of continuous functions on a compact space> $C([0,1])$ into itself and is linear. Since $t\mapsto t^{1+\alpha_n}$ maps $[0,1]$ onto itself,
$$
\|A_nx\|_\infty=\|x\|_\infty,\qquad\|A_n\|=1.
$$
For $0<t\leq1$, the <mean value theorem> applied to $r\mapsto t^{1+r}$ gives
$$
0\leq t-t^{1+\alpha_n}\leq\alpha_n\,t|\log t|\leq\frac{\alpha_n}{e}.
$$
The same bound holds at $t=0$. Each $x\in C([0,1])$ has <uniform continuity> by the <Heine-Cantor theorem>. With its <modulus of continuity> $\omega_x(\delta)=\sup_{|s-t|\leq\delta}|x(s)-x(t)|$, we obtain
$$
\boxed{\|A_nx-x\|_\infty\leq\omega_x(\alpha_n/e)\longrightarrow0.}
$$
Alternatively, the bound proves convergence on every <polynomial>; the <Weierstrass approximation theorem> makes these a <dense subset> of $C([0,1])$, and the <Banach-Steinhaus convergence theorem> with $\|A_n\|=1$ gives the same conclusion.

The <open mapping theorem (functional analysis)> states that a surjective <bounded linear operator> $T:X\to Y$ between <Banach spaces> is an <open map>: $T(U)$ is an <open set> in $Y$ whenever $U$ is an <open set> in $X$. Equivalently, there is $\delta>0$ such that
$$
\boxed{B_Y(0,\delta)\subseteq T(B_X(0,1)),}
$$
where both displayed <balls> are open. In particular, a bijective <bounded linear operator> between <Banach spaces> has a bounded inverse, the <bounded inverse theorem>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Use the <closed unit ball> $\overline B_X$ first, and put $C=\overline{T(\overline B_X)}$. This is a <convex set> symmetric about zero. If $B_Y(y_0,r)\subseteq C$, then $-y_0\in C$, so taking midpoints gives
$$
B_Y(0,r/2)\subseteq C.
$$
Write $\delta=r/2$. By scaling,
$$
B_Y(0,2^{-j}\delta)\subseteq\overline{T(2^{-j}\overline B_X)}\qquad(j\geq1).
$$
Fix $y$ with $\|y\|<\delta/2$. Starting with $r_0=y$, recursively choose $x_j\in X$ such that
$$
\|x_j\|\leq2^{-j},\qquad
r_j=r_{j-1}-Tx_j,\qquad
\|r_j\|<2^{-j-1}\delta.
$$
At each step the previous residual lies in the open <ball> on the left of the scaled inclusion, so the definition of <closure> permits this approximation. The <series> $\sum_jx_j$ converges to some $x$ because $X$ is a <Banach space>, and the <triangle inequality> gives $\|x\|\leq\sum_j2^{-j}=1$. Continuity of $T$ gives $Tx=\lim_N\sum_{j=1}^NTx_j=y$, since $r_N\to0$. Thus
$$
B_Y(0,\delta/2)\subseteq T(\overline B_X).
$$
Scaling by $1/2$ now gives the slightly smaller open <ball>
$$
\boxed{B_Y(0,\delta/4)\subseteq T(B_X(0,1)).}
$$
This proves the assertion whether the original <unit ball> is taken open or closed. Only completeness of $X$ was used; the <normed space> $Y$ need not be complete. The argument is <geometric correction for approximate surjectivity>.

For $n\geq1$ put $C_n=\overline{T(n\overline B_X)}$, with <closure> taken in $Y$. Then
$$
T(X)=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}T(n\overline B_X)\subseteq\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}C_n.
$$
If every $C_n$ has empty <interior>, then each $C_n$ is a <nowhere dense set>, because it is closed. Each $T(n\overline B_X)$ is then a <nowhere dense set> too. Consequently $T(X)$ is a <meagre set>, that is, a set of the first category in $Y$.

Otherwise some $C_n$ has nonempty <interior>. Scaling gives nonempty <interior> of $\overline{T(\overline B_X)}$. The <geometric correction for approximate surjectivity> proved in the preceding part gives an open <ball> about zero inside $T(\overline B_X)$. Since $T(X)$ is a <vector subspace>, it then equals $Y$: scale any $y\in Y$ into this <ball> and scale its preimage back. Hence $T(X)$ is a <closed set>.

Thus, in fact, the stronger <operator range category dichotomy> holds:
$$
\boxed{T(X)=Y\quad\text{or}\quad T(X)\text{ is meagre in }Y.}
$$
In particular $T(X)$ is closed or of the first category, as required. The only substantive theorem used here is the preceding <geometric correction for approximate surjectivity>: for a <bounded linear operator> from a <Banach space> to a <normed space>, nonempty <interior> of the closure of the image of the <unit ball> implies nonempty <interior> of the image itself. Its series proof was given there, so this proof does not require the <Baire category theorem> or completeness of $Y$.

Let $J:C([0,1])\to L^1(0,1)$ send a <continuous function> to its <almost everywhere> equivalence class. The domain with the <supremum norm> and the target with the <L1 norm> are <Banach spaces>, and
$$
\|Jf\|_{L^1}=\int_0^1|f(t)|\,dt\leq\|f\|_\infty.
$$
Thus $J$ is a <bounded linear operator>, and the requested <vector subspace> is $J(C([0,1]))$.

To show this <image of a linear map> is not closed, consider the <continuous functions>
$$
f_n(t)=\min\{1,\max\{0,n(t-1/2)\}\}\qquad(n\geq2)
$$
and $g=\mathbf1_{(1/2,1)}$. Their <L1 norm> difference is the area of one triangle:
$$
\|Jf_n-g\|_{L^1}=\int_{1/2}^{1/2+1/n}\bigl(1-n(t-1/2)\bigr)\,dt=\frac1{2n}\longrightarrow0.
$$
But $g$ has no <continuous function> representative. Indeed, a <continuous function> equal to zero <almost everywhere> on $(0,1/2)$ must be identically zero there: any nonzero value would persist on an open interval of positive <Lebesgue measure>. Likewise any continuous representative would be identically one on $(1/2,1)$. Continuity at $1/2$ makes these requirements incompatible. Therefore $g\notin J(C([0,1]))$, although it lies in its <closure>.

The preceding <operator range category dichotomy> proves <continuous functions are meagre in L1>:
$$
\boxed{J(C([0,1]))\text{ is of the first category in }L^1(0,1).}
$$

Put $A=\lambda I-T$. Since $T$ is a <self-adjoint operator>, $A^*=\overline\lambda I-T$. Applying the preceding <image-kernel orthogonality for an adjoint> identities to $A^*$ gives
$$
\overline{\operatorname{Im}A}=(\ker A^*)^\perp.
$$
If $\lambda$ is real, $A^*=A$ and $\ker A=\{0\}$ because $\lambda$ is not an <eigenvalue>. If $\lambda$ is nonreal and $A^*x=0$, then $Tx=\overline\lambda x$. The scalar $\langle Tx,x\rangle$ is real by <self-adjointness>, while it equals $\overline\lambda\|x\|^2$. Its imaginary part forces $x=0$. Thus $\ker A^*=\{0\}$ in both cases, and
$$
\boxed{\overline{\operatorname{Im}(\lambda I-T)}=X.}
$$
The conjugation on $\lambda$ in the <adjoint operator> is essential when $\lambda$ is nonreal.

Here the intended space must consist of **bounded** <linear operators>, namely <Hilbert-Schmidt operators>. The printed restriction to “all linear mappings” is insufficient. To see this, choose $v\in X$ outside the algebraic <linear span> of $(e_k)$, for example $v=\sum_{k\geq1}2^{-k}e_k$. Extend the linearly independent family $\{e_k:k\geq1\}\cup\{v\}$ to a <Hamel basis>. Define a <linear functional> $\phi$ on this <Hamel basis> by $\phi(v)=1$ and zero on every other basis vector, and extend it linearly. Then the everywhere-defined <linear operator> $Rx=\phi(x)e_1$ satisfies
$$
Re_k=0\quad(k\geq1),\qquad Rv=e_1\ne0.
$$
Its printed column sum is zero although $R\ne0$. More explicitly, $v_N=\sum_{k=1}^N2^{-k}e_k\to v$ but $R(v-v_N)=e_1$ for every $N$. Thus $R$ is discontinuous; it has no everywhere-defined <adjoint operator> of the bounded kind used in the question. This proves <finite column sum does not imply boundedness>. We use $T\in\mathcal B(X)$ in the intended argument below.

By the <Parseval identity for a Hilbertian basis> for $(e_j)$, followed by the <adjoint operator> identity and then the <Parseval identity for a Hilbertian basis> for $(h_k)$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_k\|Th_k\|^2
&=\sum_k\sum_j|\langle Th_k,e_j\rangle|^2\\
&=\sum_j\sum_k|\langle h_k,T^*e_j\rangle|^2\\
&=\sum_j\|T^*e_j\|^2.
\end{aligned}
$$
The interchange is valid by <Tonelli's theorem> for nonnegative double <series>, even before finiteness is known. Repeating with $h_k=e_k$ shows that the right side also equals $\sum_k\|Te_k\|^2$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\sum_k\|Th_k\|^2=\sum_j\|T^*e_j\|^2=\sum_k\|Te_k\|^2.}
$$
In particular the <Hilbert-Schmidt norm> is independent of the <Hilbertian basis>, and $\|T^*\|_2=\|T\|_2$.

As in the preceding part, take $T$ to be a <bounded linear operator>. For $x\in X$, the <Hilbertian basis> expansion and continuity of $T$ give
$$
Tx=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^N\langle x,e_k\rangle Te_k.
$$
The <triangle inequality>, the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> for finite sums, and the <Parseval identity for a Hilbertian basis> give
$$
\begin{aligned}
\left\|\sum_{k=1}^N\langle x,e_k\rangle Te_k\right\|
&\leq\sum_{k=1}^N|\langle x,e_k\rangle|\,\|Te_k\|\\
&\leq\left(\sum_{k=1}^N|\langle x,e_k\rangle|^2\right)^{1/2}

\end{aligned}
$$
Passing to the limit and taking the supremum over the <unit ball> proves
$$
\boxed{\|T\|\leq\|T\|_2.}

Thus the <Hilbert-Schmidt norm> controls the <operator norm>. Without the boundedness interpretation, the nonzero $R$ constructed in the preceding part has zero printed column sum and makes the requested inequality false.

Use the intended space of <Hilbert-Schmidt operators>, with the given <Hilbert-Schmidt inner product> linear in its first argument. It remains to prove completeness in its <Hilbert-Schmidt norm>. Let $(T_n)$ be a <Cauchy sequence> in that <norm>. The bound $\|T_n-T_m\|\leq\|T_n-T_m\|_2$ makes it a <Cauchy sequence> in <operator norm>. The space $\mathcal B(X)$ is a <Banach space>, so $T_n\to T$ in <operator norm> for some <bounded linear operator> $T$.

For completeness, that last standard fact follows by taking $Tx=\lim_nT_nx$ for each $x$: the <Cauchy sequence> $(T_n)$ is uniformly bounded in <operator norm>, so its limit is linear and bounded, and the uniform Cauchy estimate on the <unit ball> gives <operator norm> convergence.

Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ so that $\|T_n-T_m\|_2<\varepsilon$ for $n,m\geq N$. Fix $n\geq N$ and any positive integer $K$. Since $T_me_k\to Te_k$ for each $k$, passage to the limit in a finite sum gives
$$
\sum_{k=1}^K\|(T_n-T)e_k\|^2
=\lim_{m\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^K\|(T_n-T_m)e_k\|^2
\leq\varepsilon^2.
$$
Taking the supremum over $K$ gives $\sum_k\|(T_n-T)e_k\|^2\leq\varepsilon^2$. Also,
$$
\sum_k\|Te_k\|^2
\leq2\sum_k\|T_Ne_k\|^2+2\sum_k\|(T-T_N)e_k\|^2<\infty.
$$
Thus $T\in S_2(X)$, and $\|T_n-T\|_2\leq\varepsilon$ for $n\geq N$. This proves <Hilbert-Schmidt operators form a Hilbert space>:
$$
\boxed{S_2(X)\text{ is complete in }\|\cdot\|_2,\text{ hence is a Hilbert space}.}
$$
Under the literal unrestricted “linear mappings” definition, the nonzero $R$ from part (i) satisfies $\langle R,R\rangle_{S_2(X)}=0$. The form would fail positive definiteness and would not be an <inner product>; the boundedness correction is needed here too.

Write $u(t)=e^t$ and $v(t)=e^{-t}$. Both lie in the <Hilbert space> $L^2(-\pi,\pi)$, and the <integral operator> factors as the <rank-one operator>
$$
Tf=\langle f,v\rangle u.
$$
The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives $\|Tf\|\leq\|u\|\|v\|\|f\|$, and equality is attained at $f=v/\|v\|$. Hence the <operator norm> is $\|T\|=\|u\|\|v\|$. Direct integration gives
$$
\|u\|^2=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{2t}\,dt
=\frac{e^{2\pi}-e^{-2\pi}}2=\sinh(2\pi),\qquad
\|v\|^2=\sinh(2\pi).
$$
For any <Hilbertian basis> $(h_k)$, the <Parseval identity for a Hilbertian basis> gives
$$
\|T\|_2^2=\sum_k\|\langle h_k,v\rangle u\|^2
=\|u\|^2\sum_k|\langle h_k,v\rangle|^2
=\|u\|^2\|v\|^2.
$$
This is the general formula for the <norms of a rank-one operator>. In particular $T$ is a <Hilbert-Schmidt operator>, and
$$
\boxed{\|T\|=\|T\|_2=\sinh(2\pi)=\frac{e^{2\pi}-e^{-2\pi}}2.}
$$
This computation needs no explicit <Fourier basis>. The printed optional basis formula omits the variable $t$: the intended <Hilbertian basis> is $e_n(t)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{int}$, $n\in\mathbb Z$. Taken literally, the printed expressions are constant <functions> and cannot form an <orthonormal basis> of $L^2(-\pi,\pi)$.
