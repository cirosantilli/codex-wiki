Count the pairs $(g,\omega)$ for which $g$ fixes $\omega$. Counting first by <group elements> gives $\sum_g\operatorname{Fix}(g)$, while counting first by points gives $\sum_\omega|\operatorname{Stab}(\omega)|$. On a <group orbit> of size $r$, <orbit-stabilizer theorem> makes each <stabilizer> have size $|G|/r$, so that <group orbit> contributes $|G|$ to the second sum. Dividing by $|G|$ proves <Burnside's lemma>:
$$
\boxed{\text{number of orbits}=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\operatorname{Fix}(g).}
$$
A <tetrahedron> has four vertices. A <rotational symmetry> is determined by the <image> of one vertex, with four possibilities, and the cyclic orientation of the three vertices around it, with three possibilities. All these choices are realizable, so its <rotational symmetry group of a tetrahedron> has order $12$. More explicitly, its elements are the identity, the two nontrivial <rotational symmetries> around each of four vertex-to-opposite-face axes, and the half-turn about each of three axes through opposite edge midpoints. Thus there is **one element of order 1, eight of order 3, and three of order 2**. The induced <permutations> of vertices are the <even permutations>, identifying the <group> with $A_4$.

Apply the <group action> to the <set> of <edge colourings>. A colouring is fixed by a <rotational symmetry> precisely when its colour is constant on each cycle of the induced edge <permutation>. The identity has six edge cycles and fixes $4^6$ colourings. A vertex-axis <rotational symmetry> has two cycles of length three, fixing $4^2$. An opposite-edge half-turn fixes the two edges on its axis as <sets> and exchanges the other four in two pairs, giving four cycles and $4^4$ fixed colourings. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{4^6+8\cdot4^2+3\cdot4^4}{12}=416.}
$$
Reflections are excluded because the equivalence in the source uses <rotational symmetries>.

Map the <integers> into a <field> by $n\mapsto n1$. Its <kernel (algebra)> is a <prime ideal> of $\mathbb Z$, since the <image> has no <zero divisors>. Thus the <kernel (algebra)> is either zero or $(p)$ for a <prime> $p$. In the zero case, the map extends injectively to the <rational numbers> by taking quotients of nonzero <integers>. In the other case, its <image> is the <field> $\mathbb F_p$. This proves the prime-field assertion.

A <finite field> cannot contain the infinite rational <field>, so it has <characteristic> $p$. It is a <finite-dimensional vector space> over its <prime field> $\mathbb F_p$, say of <dimension> $n\ge1$, and choosing a <basis> counts its elements as
$$
\boxed{|F|=p^n.}
$$
For existence, put $q=p^n$ and take a <splitting field> of $x^q-x$ over $\mathbb F_p$. Its <derivative> is $-1$, so it has exactly $q$ distinct <polynomial roots>. The <polynomial roots> are closed under <addition> and <multiplication> because the Frobenius identity gives $(u+v)^q=u^q+v^q$ and $(uv)^q=u^qv^q$. They include $0,1$, are closed under <additive inverses>, and for a nonzero <polynomial root> $u$, $(u^{-1})^q=(u^q)^{-1}=u^{-1}$. They therefore form a <field> of exactly $q$ elements. Indeed this <field> is the <splitting field> because it contains all <polynomial roots> and is generated by them.

In order 4, <characteristic> is 2. For $\omega\ne0,1$, the multiplicative <group> has order 3, so $\omega^3=1$ and $\omega^2\ne1,\omega$. Factorization of $x^3-1$ gives $\omega^2+\omega+1=0$. The quadratic $x^2+x+1$ has no <polynomial root> in $\mathbb F_2$ and is the <minimal polynomial>. Write $s=\omega^2=\omega+1$. The complete tables are
$$
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
+&0&1&\omega&s\\\hline
0&0&1&\omega&s\\
1&1&0&s&\omega\\
\omega&\omega&s&0&1\\
s&s&\omega&1&0
\end{array}
\qquad
\begin{array}{c|cccc}
\cdot&0&1&\omega&s\\\hline
0&0&0&0&0\\
1&0&1&\omega&s\\
\omega&0&\omega&s&1\\
s&0&s&1&\omega
\end{array}.
$$
For an element $\xi\ne0,1$ in order 8, the <tower law> makes $[\mathbb F_2(\xi):\mathbb F_2]$ divide3. It is not1, so its <minimal polynomial> is an irreducible monic cubic. Its <constant term> must be1. A cubic $x^3+ax^2+bx+1$ has no <polynomial root> at 1 exactly when $a+b=1$, and absence of a <polynomial root> is equivalent to cubic irreducibility. Consequently the two possibilities are
$$
\boxed{x^3+x+1\quad\text{and}\quad x^3+x^2+1.}
$$
Both occur: together with $x$ and $x+1$ they factor $x^8-x$ over $\mathbb F_2$, whose distinct <polynomial roots> are exactly the eight elements of the <field>. Each cubic contributes its three <polynomial roots>.

<Euler's totient function> $\phi(n)$ counts the <integers> $1\le a\le n$ <coprime> to $n$, equivalently the invertible <residue> classes modulo $n$. Among $1,\ldots,p^e$, exactly the $p^{e-1}$ multiples of $p$ are not <coprime> to the <prime power>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\phi(p^e)=p^e-p^{e-1}=p^e(1-1/p).}
$$
Factor $n=\prod_p p^{e_p}$. Its distinct prime-power factors are pairwise <coprime>, so the permitted <multiplicativity> gives
$$
\phi(n)=\prod_{p\mid n}p^{e_p}(1-1/p)
=\boxed{n\prod_{p\mid n}(1-1/p).}
$$
For $n=1$, the <empty product> is one and agrees with $\phi(1)=1$.

The <monotone convergence theorem> says that if $0\le f_n\uparrow f$ <almost everywhere> and the functions are <measurable> on a <measure space>, then
$$
\int f=\lim_n\int f_n,
$$
with infinite <integrals> permitted. The limit is <measurable> after the usual modification on any exceptional <null set>.

The <dominated convergence theorem> says that if <measurable functions> $f_n$ converge <almost everywhere> to $f$ and satisfy $|f_n|\le g$ <almost everywhere> for an integrable <nonnegative function> $g$, then $f$ is integrable and
$$
\boxed{\int|f_n-f|\longrightarrow0,\qquad\int f_n\longrightarrow\int f.}
$$
The theorem applies equally to real or complex functions. In particular domination must be by one fixed <Lebesgue integrable function>, rather than by a different bound for each $n$.

Put $G_N=\sum_{n=1}^N|f_n|$. These are nonnegative <measurable functions> increasing to $G=\sum_{n=1}^\infty|f_n|$, possibly initially with value infinity. <Monotone convergence theorem> gives
$$
\int_{\mathbb R}G=\lim_N\sum_{n=1}^N\int_{\mathbb R}|f_n|=K<\infty.
$$
An infinite value on a <set> of <positive measure> would force an infinite <integral>. Thus $G$ is finite <almost everywhere> and, after setting it to zero on its null exceptional <set> if desired, is integrable.

Where $G$ is finite, the real <series> $\sum_n f_n$ is <absolutely convergent>. Define its sum to be $h$ there and zero on the exceptional <null set>. Measurability follows as an almost-everywhere limit of <partial sums>, and $|h|\le G$ <almost everywhere> gives integrability. Every <partial sum> is dominated by $G$, so <dominated convergence theorem> yields
$$
\boxed{\int_{\mathbb R}h=\lim_N\sum_{n=1}^N\int_{\mathbb R}f_n
=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\int_{\mathbb R}f_n.}
$$
In fact the <convergence> is also in the integrable-function <norm>:
$$
\left\|h-\sum_{n=1}^Nf_n\right\|_1
\le\sum_{n>N}\|f_n\|_1\longrightarrow0.
$$

<Fubini's theorem> for the product of two <sigma-finite measure> spaces states that an integrable <measurable function> on the product has integrable sections <almost everywhere>, the section <integrals> define <Lebesgue integrable functions>, and both <iterated integrals> equal the product <integral>. The integrability hypothesis is <absolute integrability>. For nonnegative <measurable functions>, <Tonelli's theorem> gives equality of the possibly <infinite product> and <iterated integrals> without first assuming finiteness.

The displayed function is **not <Lebesgue integrable>**. Its value at the origin can be assigned arbitrarily, since a point is a <null set>. On $0<x<1$ and $0<y<x/2$,
$$
\left|\frac{x-y}{(x+y)^3}\right|
\ge\frac{x/2}{(3x/2)^3}=\frac4{27x^2}.
$$
Consequently <Tonelli's theorem> gives the divergent <lower bound>
$$
\int_0^1\int_0^1|f(x,y)|\,dy\,dx
\ge\frac2{27}\int_0^1\frac{dx}{x}=\infty.
$$
The signed <iterated integrals> illustrate why the hypothesis matters. For $x>0$, $f(x,y)=\partial_y[y/(x+y)^2]$, so <integration> first in $y$ and then in $x$ gives $1/2$. Integrating first in $x$ gives $-1/2$, using $f(x,y)=-\partial_x[x/(x+y)^2]$. Exceptional boundary sections have <measure> zero. Thus finite iterated values do not establish product integrability.

The <product topology> on $X\times Y$ has the <open> rectangles $U\times V$, with $U$ <open> in $X$ and $V$ <open> in $Y$, as a <topological basis>. The projection preimages are $p_X^{-1}(U)=U\times Y$ and $p_Y^{-1}(V)=X\times V$, so both projections are <continuous>.

If $f:Z\to X\times Y$ is <continuous>, both coordinate compositions are <continuous>. Conversely, if both are <continuous>, then
$$
f^{-1}(U\times V)=(p_X\circ f)^{-1}(U)\cap(p_Y\circ f)^{-1}(V)
$$
is <open> for every basic rectangle, proving <continuity> of $f$. Given <paths> between two chosen endpoints in each factor, their coordinatewise pair is therefore a <continuous path> in the product, proving that a product of <path-connected spaces> is <path-connected>.

For the compact-neighborhood assertion, fix $x\in K$. For each $y\in L$, choose an <open> rectangle $U_y\times V_y$ containing $(x,y)$ inside $W$. <Compactness> of $L$ chooses finitely many $V_{y_j}$ covering $L$. Put
$$
U_x=\bigcap_jU_{y_j},\qquad V_x=\bigcup_jV_{y_j}.
$$
Then $x\in U_x$, $L\subseteq V_x$, and $U_x\times V_x\subseteq W$: any point of $V_x$ belongs to one of those rectangles' second factors, while $U_x$ lies in every first factor.

<Compactness> of $K$ now selects finitely many $U_{x_i}$ covering $K$. <Set>
$$
U=\bigcup_iU_{x_i},\qquad V=\bigcap_iV_{x_i}.
$$
These are <open>, contain $K$ and $L$, and if $(u,v)\in U\times V$, choose an $i$ with $u\in U_{x_i}$ and use $v\in V_{x_i}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{K\times L\subseteq U\times V\subseteq W.}
$$
If $K$ or $L$ is empty, take the corresponding <open> factor empty and the other factor the whole space; the assertions then hold directly. No <Hausdorff> assumption on the factors is needed.

A <Hausdorff space> separates any two distinct points by disjoint <open neighborhoods>. For a subspace $A\subseteq X$, intersect such neighborhoods in $X$ with $A$ to obtain disjoint neighborhoods in the <subspace topology>. Thus subspaces inherit the <Hausdorff> property.

If $(z_1,z_2)$ lies off the diagonal of a <Hausdorff space>'s square, choose disjoint <open neighborhoods> $U$ of $z_1$ and $V$ of $z_2$. The <open> rectangle $U\times V$ contains the pair and avoids the diagonal. Therefore its complement is <open> and the diagonal is <closed>.

The map $F:X\times Y\to Z\times Z$ given by $F(x,y)=(f(x),g(y))$ is <continuous> by the coordinate criterion from E3. The equality locus is $F^{-1}(\Delta_Z)$ and is <closed> as an <inverse image> of a <closed set>.

Let $q:X\to X/{\sim}$ be the <quotient map>. If the <quotient> is <Hausdorff>, apply the same argument to $q$ in both coordinates:
$$
R=(q\times q)^{-1}(\Delta_{X/{\sim}})
$$
is <closed> in $X\times X$.

Conversely suppose $R$ is <closed> and $q$ is <open>. Take two distinct <quotient> points, with representatives $x,y$. Since $(x,y)\notin R$, an <open> rectangle $U\times V$ around it avoids $R$. The <images> $q(U)$ and $q(V)$ are <open> <quotient> neighborhoods. They are disjoint, because a <quotient> point in their <intersection> would have equivalent representatives $u\in U$, $v\in V$, giving $(u,v)\in R$. Hence the <quotient> is <Hausdorff>. Thus **closedness of the relation and openness of the <quotient map> suffice for a <Hausdorff> <quotient>**.

For a minimizing pair $(a,b)$ and any <tangent vector> $v\in T_aS_f$, take a <differentiable> <surface> <curve> $x(s)$ with $x(0)=a$, $x'(0)=v$. The function $\|x(s)-b\|^2$ has a minimum at zero, so its <derivative> $2(a-b)\cdot v$ vanishes. Varying the other point similarly proves <orthogonality> to $T_bS_g$.

For the particular <surfaces>, minimize the distance from the origin to the second <surface> first. Put $u=x^2$, $v=y^2$, so $u,v\ge0$ and $z=(u-v-6)/2$. Then
$$
\|(x,y,z)\|^2=u+v+\frac{(u-v-6)^2}{4}.

Write $I[y]=\int_a^bG(x,y,y')\,dx$. For endpoint-zero variations $\eta$, the <first variations> are
$$
\delta J(\eta)=\int_a^b(F_y\eta+F_{y'}\eta')\,dx,\qquad
\delta I(\eta)=\int_a^b(G_y\eta+G_{y'}\eta')\,dx.
$$
For a normal constrained <extremal>, choose a variation $\eta_0$ with $\delta I(\eta_0)\ne0$. Given any $\eta$, use the <implicit function theorem> to choose a small correction in the $\eta_0$ direction so that $y+t\eta+s(t)\eta_0$ preserves the constraint. Differentiating it and the stationarity condition at zero gives
$$
\delta J(\eta)=\lambda\delta I(\eta),\qquad
\lambda=\frac{\delta J(\eta_0)}{\delta I(\eta_0)}.

Integrate the derivative-of-variation term by parts. Since the variations vanish at both endpoints, the <boundary term> is zero. The <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations> then gives
$$
\boxed{(F-\lambda G)_y-\frac d{dx}(F-\lambda G)_{y'}=0.}

The regularity condition on the constraint is what permits the coefficient of $F$ to be normalized to 1. In a degenerate case, the general multiplier statement uses $\lambda_0F-\lambda_1G$ with the two coefficients not both zero; the source's displayed form is the normal case.

For the upper <function graph>, the vertical cross-section of the enclosed region has length $y(x)$, so its <area> is $J[y]=\int_0^{2a}y(x)\,dx$. A maximizing nonnegative arch therefore uses $F=y$ and $G=\sqrt{1+y'^2}$ in the normal <Euler-Lagrange equation>. It becomes
$$
1+\lambda\frac d{dx}\left(\frac{y'}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}\right)=0.
$$
Integrating once gives $y'/\sqrt{1+y'^2}=(c-x)/\lambda$. The concave upper branch has $\lambda>0$ and integrates to $y=\sqrt{\lambda^2-(x-c)^2}+d$. The equal zero endpoint heights force $c=a$ and $d=-\sqrt{\lambda^2-a^2}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\sqrt{\lambda^2-(x-a)^2}-\sqrt{\lambda^2-a^2}.}
$$
It is a <circular arc>. To find its parameter, substitute into the length <integral>: $\sqrt{1+y'^2}=\lambda/\sqrt{\lambda^2-(x-a)^2}$, giving
$$
\boxed{l=2\lambda\arcsin(a/\lambda).}
$$
This scalar equation determines $\lambda$ numerically when needed; no explicit solution is required.

The arch is a <global maximum> in its smooth <function graph> regime. For $\lambda>0$, the integrand $y-\lambda\sqrt{1+y'^2}$ is concave and <strictly concave> in its slope. Its supporting-tangent inequality, integrated against any competing <function graph> with the same endpoint values, has <first-order term> zero by the <Euler-Lagrange equation>. Thus $J[y]-\lambda I[y]\le J[y_*]-\lambda I[y_*]$. On the fixed-length class this is $J[y]\le J[y_*]$; strict slope <concavity> also gives uniqueness.

The source leaves the length range implicit. A twice <continuously differentiable> <function graph> of this form needs $\lambda>a$, giving **$2a<l<\pi a$**. At $l=2a$ the straight <function graph> is the degenerate solution. The semicircle limit $l=\pi a$ has vertical endpoint tangents and is outside that smooth <function graph> class. The elementary elimination step replaces row $i$ by row $i$ minus a multiple $m_{ij}$ of an earlier <pivot> row $j$. With <coordinate vectors> $e_i$, its premultiplication <matrix> is
$$
E_{ij}=I-m_{ij}e_ie_j^T,\qquad i>j,
$$
and its inverse is $I+m_{ij}e_ie_j^T$, since $(e_ie_j^T)^2=0$. Choose $m_{ij}$ as the current entry below the <pivot> divided by the current <pivot>. Required <pivots> are assumed nonzero, consistently with elimination without <row interchanges>.

For <dimension> 4, first eliminate positions $(2,1),(3,1),(4,1)$, then $(3,2),(4,2)$, then $(4,3)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{U=E_{43}E_{42}E_{32}E_{41}E_{31}E_{21}A.}
$$
The rightmost operation acts first, and every multiplier is computed from the <matrix> at its own stage. For general $n$, the numbers of eliminations in successive columns are $n-1,n-2,\ldots,1$, totaling $n(n-1)/2$; a zero multiplier simply gives an identity operation.

The product $E$ is unit lower triangular and $U=EA$ is <upper triangular>. Its inverse $L=E^{-1}$ is unit lower triangular, so
$$
\boxed{A=LU.}
$$
The stored multiplier in position $(i,j)$ is the corresponding subdiagonal entry of $L$, as follows by reversing the pivot-row subtractions column by column.

If $A$ is upper Hessenberg, only the first subdiagonal can be nonzero below the diagonal. At stage $j$ only position $(j+1,j)$ needs elimination. Subtracting row $j$ cannot create lower entries further down because its entries before its <pivot> are already zero. Hence only $n-1$ operations are needed. The inverse factors occur in increasing column order, and their <cross products> vanish, giving
$$
L=I+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}m_{j+1,j}e_{j+1}e_j^T,
$$
which is lower bidiagonal; $U$ is <upper triangular>, possibly dense above its diagonal.

If the <Hessenberg matrix> is also symmetric, it is tridiagonal. At each elimination step the <pivot> row has no entries past its immediate superdiagonal, so the updated <matrix> retains that upper bandwidth. Thus **$L$ is lower bidiagonal and $U$ is upper bidiagonal**, with at most two nonzero entries in each row, as required.

Exact degree is part of this definition; merely belonging to the indicated <polynomial vector spaces> would not suffice.

Distinct degrees make $\phi_0,\ldots,\phi_{\ell-1}$ <linearly independent>. There are $\ell$ of them in the $\ell$-dimensional space $\Pi_{\ell-1}$, so they form a <basis>. Any lower-degree <polynomial> is their <linear combination>, and bilinearity therefore gives $(\phi_\ell,p)=0$ for every $p\in\Pi_{\ell-1}$.

Use the orthogonal-projection characterization: $p_*$ is the <best approximation> to $v$ in a finite-dimensional subspace $W$ exactly when $v-p_*$ is <orthogonal> to $W$. Here $v=\phi_\ell$ is already <orthogonal> to $W=\Pi_{\ell-1}$, so its unique <best approximation> is zero. For $\ell\ge1$, $\phi_{\ell-1}$ is nonzero and therefore cannot be that <best approximation>. Directly,
$$
\|\phi_\ell-\phi_{\ell-1}\|^2
=\|\phi_\ell\|^2+\|\phi_{\ell-1}\|^2>\|\phi_\ell\|^2.

$$
The weight vanishes only at one point, so a nonzero <polynomial> has positive squared <norm>; the form is indeed positive definite on the <polynomial vector space>. Nonzero rescalings of individual family members give equally valid unnormalized answers.
