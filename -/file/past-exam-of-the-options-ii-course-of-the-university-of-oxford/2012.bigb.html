=== A2

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

Start with $O=(0,0)$ and $E=(1,0)$. A <constructible point> is a point obtained from these by finitely many ruler-and-compass steps. At each step one may draw the <line> through two previously obtained distinct points, or the <circle> with a previously obtained <centre> and <radius> equal to the <distance> between two previously obtained points, and take isolated <intersection> points of the drawn <lines> or <circles>. A <constructible line> is a <line> through two distinct <constructible points>; a <constructible circle> has constructible <centre> and a <radius> obtainable as such a <distance>. These conventions also permit copying a previously obtained segment with the compass.

**Finite construction from a fixed unit segment is essential:** a drawing through an arbitrary unspecified real point does not make that point constructible.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Consider an isolated <intersection> of the two curves. For two distinct <lines>, solving their <linear equations> by elimination gives coordinates in $F$ whenever there is an <intersection>.

For a <line> and a <circle>, solve the <line> for one coordinate with a nonzero <coefficient> and substitute into the <circle> equation. The resulting quadratic has <coefficients> in $F$. A real <root> lies in $F$ if its <discriminant> is a square in $F$, and otherwise in $F(\sqrt D)$ for its positive nonsquare <discriminant> $D\in F$. The other coordinate is an $F$-linear expression in that <root>. Thus both coordinates lie in the same real <quadratic extension>. A <tangent> <intersection> has <discriminant> zero and already lies in $F$.

Write two <circle> equations in normalized form $x^2+y^2+u_ix+v_iy+w_i=0$. Subtracting eliminates the quadratic terms. If the <circles> are distinct and meet, this gives a nontrivial <line>, reducing the problem to the preceding case. Concentric distinct <circles> do not intersect. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{each isolated intersection has coordinates in }F\text{ or in }F(\sqrt D),\quad [F(\sqrt D):F]=2.}
$$
The isolated-intersection qualification is implicit in the construction operation. If coincident curves were allowed and every point on their common curve counted as an <intersection> point, the printed assertion would be false: a rational <line> contains points with transcendental coordinates.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Induct through the finitely many construction steps. Initially all available coordinates lie in $\mathbb Q$. If coordinates obtained so far lie in a real <field> $F$, the <line> through two of its points has <coefficients> in $F$. A <circle> with <centre> in $F^2$ and <radius> a <distance> between two such points has equation
$$
(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2=r^2,
$$
whose <coefficients> lie in $F$, since the squared <distance> $r^2$ lies in $F$; the <radius> itself need not lie there.

Part (b) therefore puts every new <intersection> in $F$ or a real <quadratic extension> of $F$. If a step produces several points, adjoin their coordinates successively, omitting extensions that add nothing. A finite construction ends in a real <field> $K$ containing the desired coordinates, with a tower of genuine quadratic extensions
$$
\mathbb Q=F_0\subset F_1\subset\cdots\subset F_n=K.
$$
The <tower law for field extensions> yields
$$
\boxed{[K:\mathbb Q]=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}[F_{i+1}:F_i]=2^n.}
$$
A construction involving no nontrivial extension has $n=0$ and <degree> one.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

A <constructible regular polygon> can be translated, rotated, and rescaled by constructible operations to one inscribed in the unit <circle> with a vertex at $(1,0)$. Thus the real and imaginary parts of $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/p}$ are constructible. Part (c) puts both in a real <field> $K$ of <degree> $2^n$ over $\mathbb Q$. Consequently $\zeta\in K(i)$, and $[K(i):\mathbb Q]=2^{n+1}$ because $K\subset\mathbb R$.

For <prime> $p$, the supplied irreducibility of $1+x+\cdots+x^{p-1}$ shows that $[\mathbb Q(\zeta):\mathbb Q]=p-1$. The intermediate-field tower law implies that $p-1$ divides $2^{n+1}$. Every positive divisor of a <power of two> is a <power of two>, so
$$
\boxed{p=2^r+1\quad\text{for an integer }r\geq0.}
$$
This necessary condition includes $p=2$ with $r=0$. It does not assert sufficiency for arbitrary <integers> of this form.

==== a

===== Solution

The <identity element> satisfies $e*x=exe^{-1}=x$. For $g,h\in G$,
$$
(gh)*x=ghxh^{-1}g^{-1}=g*(h*x).
$$
Both <action> axioms hold, so **<conjugation> is a <group action> on $G$**.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

By definition, $g$ stabilizes $x$ exactly when $gxg^{-1}=x$. Multiplying on the right by $g$ gives the equivalent condition $gx=xg$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Stab}_G(x)=Z(x).}
$$
Thus the <stabilizer> in this <action> is the <centralizer> of the element.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $H=Z(x)$. The map $G/H\to C(x)$ sending $gH$ to $gxg^{-1}$ is well defined: multiplying $g$ on the right by an element commuting with $x$ leaves its conjugate unchanged. It is onto by the <orbit> definition. If $gxg^{-1}=hxh^{-1}$, then $h^{-1}g$ commutes with $x$, so $gH=hH$, proving <injectivity>.

Every <coset> has $|H|$ elements, so $|C(x)|=|G|/|H|$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{|G|=|Z(x)|\,|C(x)|.}
$$
This is the <orbit-stabilizer theorem> specialized to <conjugation>.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

An element $x$ is central exactly when $gx=xg$ for every $g\in G$. By part (b), this is exactly the assertion that every $g$ stabilizes $x$. That means every conjugate of $x$ is $x$; conversely, a singleton <conjugacy class> has this property for every $g$.
$$
\boxed{x\in Z(G)\iff\operatorname{Stab}_G(x)=G\iff C(x)=\{x\}.}
$$
In particular, the singleton classes are in one-to-one correspondence with the elements of the <centre>.

==== e
{scope}

===== Solution

<Conjugacy> is an <equivalence relation>: the <identity element> gives reflexivity, <conjugation> by $g^{-1}$ reverses <conjugation> by $g$, and composing conjugations gives transitivity. Its <equivalence classes> therefore partition the finite set $G$. Counting this disjoint <union> gives
$$
\boxed{|G|=\sum_{i=1}^k|C_i|.}
$$
Separating singleton classes from the others gives the <class equation> $|G|=|Z(G)|+\sum_{|C_i|>1}|C_i|$.

==== f
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $|G|=p^n$, where $n\geq1$. By part (c), each conjugacy-class size divides $p^n$ and hence is a power of $p$. Every nonsingleton class consequently has size divisible by $p$. The <class equation> implies
$$
|Z(G)|\equiv|G|\equiv0\pmod p.
$$
The <identity element> lies in the <centre>, so the <centre> is nonempty. Its size is a positive multiple of $p$, and therefore
$$
\boxed{|Z(G)|\geq p\geq2.}
$$
This proves that a nontrivial finite <p-group> has a nontrivial <centre>.

===== i

====== Solution

For an <integer> $a$ and an <odd prime> $p$, the <Legendre symbol> is
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac ap\right)=\begin{cases}0,&p\mid a,\\1,&a\not\equiv0\pmod p\text{ and }a\text{ is a square modulo }p,\\-1,&a\text{ is not a square modulo }p.\end{cases}}
$$
The value one refers to a nonzero <quadratic residue>; divisibility by $p$ is recorded separately by zero.

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

<Euler's criterion> states that
$$
\boxed{a^{(p-1)/2}\equiv\left(\frac ap\right)\pmod p.}
$$
For $p\nmid a$ this says that the power is $1$ for a <quadratic residue> and $-1$ for a <quadratic nonresidue>. When $p\mid a$, both sides are zero modulo $p$.

===== iii
{scope}

====== Solution

<Euler's criterion> gives
$$
\left(\frac{ab}p\right)\equiv (ab)^{(p-1)/2}
\equiv a^{(p-1)/2}b^{(p-1)/2}
\equiv\left(\frac ap\right)\left(\frac bp\right)\pmod p.
$$
If either factor is divisible by $p$, both asserted values are zero. Otherwise the two values lie in $\{1,-1\}$, whose elements are distinct modulo an <odd prime>. The <modular congruence> is therefore equality:
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{ab}p\right)=\left(\frac ap\right)\left(\frac bp\right).}
$$
Finally, $a\equiv b\pmod p$ means they give the same <residue class> and thus the same set of square-congruence solutions. Divisibility by $p$ is also identical. Hence $\left(\frac ap\right)=\left(\frac bp\right)$.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

The <quadratic reciprocity> states that for distinct odd <primes> $p,q$,
$$
\left(\frac pq\right)\left(\frac qp\right)=(-1)^{(p-1)(q-1)/4}.
$$
With $q=3$, it gives $\left(\frac3p\right)=(-1)^{(p-1)/2}\left(\frac p3\right)$. The nonzero square modulo three is one, so $\left(\frac p3\right)=1$ when $p\equiv1\pmod3$, and $-1$ when $p\equiv-1\pmod3$. An <odd prime> other than three is congruent to $1,5,7$, or $11$ modulo twelve. Evaluating the two signs gives
$$
\begin{array}{c|rrrr}p\bmod12&1&5&7&11\\\hline
(-1)^{(p-1)/2}&1&1&-1&-1\\
\left(\frac p3\right)&1&-1&1&-1\\
\left(\frac3p\right)&1&-1&-1&1
\end{array}
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac3p\right)=1\text{ for }p\equiv\pm1\pmod{12},\quad -1\text{ for }p\equiv\pm5\pmod{12}.}
$$

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Reducing modulo $23$ gives $189\equiv5$. Reciprocity with five has positive sign, so
$$
\left(\frac{189}{23}\right)=\left(\frac5{23}\right)=\left(\frac{23}5\right)=\left(\frac35\right)=-1,
$$
since the nonzero squares modulo five are one and four. Modulo $59$, $189\equiv12=4\cdot3$, and $59\equiv-1\pmod{12}$. Thus part (b) gives
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{189}{23}\right)=-1,\qquad\left(\frac{189}{59}\right)=1.}
$$
As $1357=23\cdot59$, a solution modulo $1357$ would in particular solve the <modular congruence> modulo $23$, contradicting the first symbol. Therefore **there is no <integer> solution modulo $1357$**.

==== Solution

The <monotone convergence theorem> states that if <measurable functions> $0\leq f_n\uparrow f$ pointwise on a <measure space>, then $\int f_n\uparrow\int f$, allowing the limit to be infinite.

Extend each restriction to $I_n$ by zero on $I\setminus I_n$. The functions $f\mathbf1_{I_n}$ are nonnegative and increase pointwise to $f$ because the <intervals> are increasing and exhaust $I$. Hence
$$
\int_I f=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{I_n}f=\sup_n\int_{I_n}f.
$$
For nonnegative $f$, <integrability> means finiteness of this <integral>, so
$$
\boxed{f\in L^1(I)\iff\sup_n\int_{I_n}f<\infty.}
$$
No <boundedness> or <closedness> of the <intervals> is required.

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

The function is nonnegative, <measurable>, and even. Direct integration gives
$$
\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-|x|}\,dx=2\int_0^\infty e^{-x}\,dx=2.
$$
Thus **it is <integrable>**, with <integral> $\boxed{2}$.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

On finite <intervals> the function is <continuous>, but
$$
\int_0^R\frac{x}{1+x^2}\,dx=\frac12\log(1+R^2)\longrightarrow\infty.
$$
Consequently its nonnegative <integral> on the half-line is infinite: **it is not <integrable>**. The divergence occurs at infinity, not at the origin.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

For every real $x$, the numerator is at most $2e^{|x|}$, while the denominator is at least $e^{2|x|}$. Therefore
$$
0\leq\frac{e^x+e^{-x}}{e^{2x}+e^{-2x}}\leq2e^{-|x|}.
$$
The upper bound is <integrable> by part (a), so the comparison theorem shows that **the function is <integrable> on $\mathbb R$**.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

We prove <absolute integrability>. On $(0,1]$, $|\sin(1/x)|\leq1$ and the denominator is at least one, so $|f(x)|\leq1$. On $[1,\infty)$, the standard inequality $|\sin t|\leq|t|$ gives
$$
|f(x)|\leq\frac1{x(1+(\log x)^2)}.
$$
<Substitution> $t=\log x$ yields
$$
\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x(1+(\log x)^2)}=\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{1+t^2}=\frac\pi2.
$$
Thus $\int_0^\infty|f(x)|\,dx\leq1+\pi/2<\infty$, and **the function is <integrable>** despite its oscillation near zero.

=== D4
{scope}
{c}

==== Solution

For a <continuously differentiable> <diffeomorphism> $\phi:J\to I$ between <intervals>, the <integration by substitution> states
$$
\int_I h(x)\,dx=\int_J h(\phi(t))|\phi'(t)|\,dt.
$$
It holds as an <identity element> of extended <integrals> for nonnegative <measurable> $h$. For signed or complex-valued <measurable> $h$, <integrability> on one side is equivalent to <integrability> after <substitution>, and the <identity element> then holds. The absolute <derivative> accounts for a decreasing <substitution>; on oriented finite <intervals> the equivalent formula uses $\phi'$ and oriented endpoints.

Put $C=\int_0^\infty(1+x^4)^{-1}\,dx=\pi/(2\sqrt2)$. The map $x=y^{-1/4}$ is a decreasing <diffeomorphism> of the positive half-line onto itself and
$$
\frac{|dx/dy|}{1+x^4}=\frac1{4y^{1/4}(1+y)}.
$$
Applying the nonnegative version before assuming finiteness proves both existence and value:
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty\frac{dy}{y^{1/4}(1+y)}=4C=\sqrt2\pi.}
$$
<Tonelli's theorem> permits either iterated <integral> of a nonnegative <measurable function> on a product of sigma-finite <measure spaces> and identifies both with its extended product <integral>. <Fubini's theorem> states that for an absolutely <integrable> <measurable function> on such a product, almost every section is <integrable>, the section-integral functions are <integrable>, and the two iterated <integrals> equal the product <integral>.

The given two-variable function is <continuous> and positive on the positive quadrant. For fixed $y>0$, substituting $t=xy^{1/4}$ gives
$$
\int_0^\infty f(x,y)\,dx=\frac{Cy^{-1/4}}{1+y}.
$$
Tonelli now gives
$$
\iint f(x,y)\,dx\,dy=C(4C)=4C^2=\frac{\pi^2}2<\infty.
$$
This proves <absolute integrability>, so Fubini is applicable.

For $x\neq1$, integrate the displayed partial fraction <identity element> in $y$ up to $R$:
$$
\int_0^R f(x,y)\,dy=\frac{\log(1+x^4R)-\log(1+R)}{x^4-1}
\longrightarrow\frac{4\log x}{x^4-1}.
$$
At $x=1$ the section <integral> is $\int_0^\infty(1+y)^{-2}\,dy=1$, agreeing with the <continuous> limit of this expression. Therefore Fubini identifies the remaining <integral> and gives
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty\frac{\log x}{x^4-1}\,dx=\frac14\cdot\frac{\pi^2}2=\frac{\pi^2}8.}
$$
The apparent <singularity> at one is removable, with limiting integrand value $1/4$. Positivity and the finite product <integral> already establish convergence at both ends.

==== a

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

The <closure> of $A$ is the <intersection> of every <closed> subset containing it:
$$
\boxed{\overline A=\bigcap\{F\subseteq X:F\text{ closed and }A\subseteq F\}.}
$$
The collection is nonempty because it contains $X$. Arbitrary <intersections> of <closed> sets are <closed>, so this is the smallest <closed> set containing $A$.

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

Every subset is contained in its <closure>, giving $\overline A\subseteq\overline{\overline A}$. On the other hand, $\overline A$ is already <closed> by its definition as an <intersection> of <closed> sets. It is therefore one of the <closed> supersets used when taking its own <closure>, so $\overline{\overline A}\subseteq\overline A$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\overline{\overline A}=\overline A.}
$$
This is the <idempotence> of the <closure> operation.

===== iii
{scope}

====== Solution

If $x\notin\overline A$, some <closed> superset $F$ of $A$ misses $x$. Its <complement> is an <open set> containing $x$ and disjoint from $A$. Conversely, if an <open set> $U$ contains $x$ and misses $A$, the <closed> set $X\setminus U$ contains $A$ and misses $x$, so $x\notin\overline A$.

Negating this equivalence gives the <neighbourhood> characterization
$$
\boxed{x\in\overline A\iff U\cap A\neq\varnothing\text{ for every open }U\ni x.}
$$

===== iv
{scope}

====== Solution

An <accumulation point> of $A$ is a point $x\in X$ such that every <open neighbourhood> of $x$ contains a point of $A$ different from $x$:
$$
\boxed{x\in A'\iff (U\setminus\{x\})\cap A\neq\varnothing\text{ for every open }U\ni x.}
$$
The point itself need not belong to $A$. Requiring a different point distinguishes accumulation from mere membership in the <closure>.

==== b
{scope}

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

In a <metric space>,
$$
\boxed{x\in A'\iff\text{there exists a sequence }a_n\in A\setminus\{x\}\text{ with }a_n\to x.}
$$
The <sequence> may equivalently be required to have pairwise distinct terms. The metric <hypothesis> makes a countable <sequence> sufficient; arbitrary <topological spaces> need not have this sequential characterization.

===== ii
{scope}

====== 1
{scope}

======= Solution

Take $A_1=\mathbb Q^2$. Every <open disk> contains infinitely many points with both coordinates rational, so every point of the plane is an <accumulation point> of $A_1$. Every disk also contains infinitely many points with an irrational first coordinate, and these lie in the <complement>. Thus
$$
\boxed{A_1'=\mathbb R^2=(\mathbb R^2\setminus A_1)'.}
$$
The infinitely-many-points observation ensures that one can avoid the <centre> of the disk itself in both cases.

====== 2
{scope}

======= Solution

Take
$$
A_2=\{(1/n,0):n\geq1\}.
$$
Its only <accumulation point> is $(0,0)$, which is absent from $A_2$. Every listed point is isolated, so none belongs to $A_2'$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{A_2'=\{(0,0)\},\qquad A_2'\not\subseteq A_2,\quad A_2\not\subseteq A_2'.}
$$
For completeness, away from the origin a sufficiently small disk meets only finitely many of the listed points and can be shrunk to exclude all but its <centre>, proving there are no further <accumulation points>.

====== 3
{scope}

======= Solution

Use three nested <sequences>:
$$
A_3=\left\{\left(\frac1n+\frac1{n^2m},\frac1{n^2m^2k}\right):n,m,k\geq1\right\}.
$$
For fixed $n,m$, letting $k\to\infty$ produces the point $(1/n+1/(n^2m),0)$. Letting $m\to\infty$ produces $(1/n,0)$, and letting $n\to\infty$ produces $(0,0)$. Accordingly, put
$$
B=\left\{\left(\frac1n+\frac1{n^2m},0\right):n,m\geq1\right\},\qquad
C=\{(1/n,0):n\geq1\}.
$$
The successive derived sets are
$$
\boxed{A_3'=B\cup C\cup\{(0,0)\},\quad A_3''=C\cup\{(0,0)\},\quad A_3^{\prime\prime\prime}=\{(0,0)\}.}
$$
To see that no limits are missing, take any <convergent sequence> of distinct points from $A_3$. If $n$ is unbounded, a subsequence has $n\to\infty$ and converges to the origin. Otherwise fix $n$ on a subsequence. Unbounded $m$ then forces limit $(1/n,0)$; with $n,m$ fixed, distinctness forces unbounded $k$, giving the corresponding point of $B$. The same argument, with one index fewer at each stage, proves the next two equalities. Distinct $n$-clusters are separated away from the origin because their first coordinates lie in the disjoint <intervals> $[1/n,1/n+1/n^2]$.

The original set has positive second coordinates, the first derived set has isolated $B$-points on the axis, the second has infinitely many $C$-points, and the third is a singleton. All three required successive inequalities follow. This is a <three-stage accumulation tower>.

==== a

===== i

====== Solution

The <quotient topology> is defined by
$$
\boxed{U\subseteq X/\mathcal R\text{ open}\iff p^{-1}(U)\text{ open in }X.}
$$
Preimages commute with arbitrary <unions> and finite <intersections> and take the empty and whole sets to the corresponding sets, so this definition indeed gives a <topology>.

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

A map is <continuous> exactly when the preimage of every <open set> is <open>. By the definition in part (i), every <open> $U\subseteq X/\mathcal R$ has <open> preimage $p^{-1}(U)$. Thus **$p$ is <continuous>**, directly from the defining property of the <quotient topology>.

===== iii
{scope}

====== Solution

If $g$ is <continuous>, so is the composition $g\circ p$. Conversely, assume the composition is <continuous>. For every <open set> $V\subseteq Y$,
$$
p^{-1}(g^{-1}(V))=(g\circ p)^{-1}(V)
$$
is <open> in $X$. The quotient definition then says that $g^{-1}(V)$ is <open> in $X/\mathcal R$. Hence $g$ is <continuous> and
$$
\boxed{g\text{ continuous}\iff g\circ p\text{ continuous}.}
$$
This is the <universal property of the quotient topology>.

===== iv
{scope}

====== Solution

Let two distinct quotient points correspond to two <equivalence classes>. By <hypothesis> those classes lie in disjoint <open> saturated sets $U,V\subseteq X$. Saturation gives $p^{-1}(p(U))=U$, so the quotient definition makes $p(U)$ <open>, and likewise $p(V)$.

The image sets contain the two quotient points and are disjoint. Indeed, a class meeting both $U$ and $V$ would be contained in each because both sets are saturated, contradicting their disjointness. Thus **the quotient is <Hausdorff>**.

===== v
{scope}

====== Solution

Define $\widetilde f:X/\mathcal F\to Y$ by $\widetilde f([x])=f(x)$. Its definition is <independent> of the representative because the classes are precisely the fibres of $f$. It is onto since $f$ is onto, and <injective> because two points with equal $f$-values lie in the same fibre.

The relation $\widetilde f\circ p=f$ and part (iii) imply <continuity> of $\widetilde f$. Since $X$ is <compact> and $p$ is <continuous>, $X/\mathcal F$ is <compact>. A <continuous> <bijection> from a <compact> space to a <Hausdorff space> is a <homeomorphism>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{X/\mathcal F\cong Y.}
$$

==== b
{scope}

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

Every iterate $g^n(x,y)=(x+n,(-1)^n y)$ is an <isometry> of the strip with its Euclidean subspace metric. Let $a,b$ lie in distinct <orbits>. Since the first-coordinate difference in $a-g^n b$ grows without bound as $|n|\to\infty$, the positive distances $d(a,g^n b)$ have a positive minimum $\delta$. Only finitely many indices can give a <distance> below any fixed bound, and none gives zero.

Choose $0<\varepsilon<\delta/3$. Saturate the relative <open balls> by setting
$$
U=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb Z}g^nB_S(a,\varepsilon),\qquad
V=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb Z}g^nB_S(b,\varepsilon).
$$
These are <open> saturated <neighbourhoods> of the two <orbits>. Any two centres $g^n a,g^m b$ are at least $\delta$ apart, since their <distance> equals $d(a,g^{m-n}b)$. Their radius-$\varepsilon$ balls therefore cannot intersect. Hence $U\cap V=\varnothing$, and part (a)(iv), an instance of <positive orbit separation implies a Hausdorff quotient>, proves
$$
\boxed{S/\mathcal R'\text{ is Hausdorff}.}
$$

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

Let $D=[0,1]\times[-1,1]$ and $q=p\circ i:D\to S/\mathcal R'$. This is <continuous>. It is onto: given $(x,y)\in S$, choose an <integer> shift bringing $x$ into $[0,1]$, with the corresponding possible sign change in $y$; the resulting representative lies in $D$.

Two representatives in $D$ are in the same <orbit> only if their first coordinates differ by an <integer>. An interior point can therefore be equivalent only to itself. Distinct <boundary> representatives have first coordinates zero and one, and the iterate $g$ identifies exactly $(0,y)$ with $(1,-y)$. Thus the fibres of $q$ are precisely the <equivalence classes> defining the <Möbius band> $M$.

The <rectangle> $D$ is <compact>, and the target is <Hausdorff> by part (i). Applying part (a)(v) to $q$ therefore yields
$$
\boxed{M=D/\mathcal R\cong S/\mathcal R'.}
$$
The <compactness> argument supplies <continuity> of the inverse without requiring a discontinuous choice of one representative per <orbit>.

====== Solution

A $C^1$ <diffeomorphism> between <open subsets> of $\mathbb R^n$ is a <bijection> $f:U\to V$ for which both $f$ and $f^{-1}$ are <continuously differentiable>. More generally, a $C^k$ <diffeomorphism> requires that regularity for both maps; a <smooth> <diffeomorphism> has <derivatives> of every order. In the $C^1$ setting used here,
$$
\boxed{f\text{ bijective},\qquad f,f^{-1}\in C^1.}
$$

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

The <inverse function theorem> states that if $f:U\to\mathbb R^n$ is $C^1$ on an <open set> and $Df(a)$ is invertible, then there are <open neighbourhoods> $U_0$ of $a$ and $V_0$ of $f(a)$ such that $f:U_0\to V_0$ is a $C^1$ <diffeomorphism>. Its inverse satisfies
$$
\boxed{D(f^{-1})(f(x))=(Df(x))^{-1},\qquad x\in U_0.}
$$
If $f$ is $C^k$ or <smooth>, the inverse has the same regularity.

===== iii
{scope}

====== Solution

The planar <implicit function theorem> states that if $F$ is $C^1$ near $(a,b)$, $F(a,b)=0$, and $F_y(a,b)\neq0$, then in some product <neighbourhood> the equation $F(x,y)=0$ is equivalent to $y=h(x)$ for a unique $C^1$ function with $h(a)=b$. Moreover,
$$
\boxed{h'(x)=-\frac{F_x(x,h(x))}{F_y(x,h(x))}.}
$$
If instead $F_x(a,b)\neq0$, exchange the coordinates to solve locally for $x$ as a function of $y$.

====== Solution

The function is <smooth>, $f(0,0)=0$, and
$$
f_x=ye^x+e^y\cos x,\qquad f_y=e^x+e^y\sin x.
$$
At the origin both <partial derivatives> equal one. In particular, $f_y(0,0)=1\neq0$, so the <implicit function theorem> supplies a <neighbourhood> in which
$$
\boxed{C=\{(x,h(x))\},\qquad h\in C^1,\quad h(0)=0.}
$$
In fact $h$ is <smooth> because $f$ is <smooth>.

===== ii

====== Solution

By the implicit <derivative> formula,
$$
h'(0)=-\frac{f_x(0,0)}{f_y(0,0)}=-1.
$$
All sufficiently near points of the curve have $y=h(x)$, and the only such point with $x=0$ is the origin itself. Therefore for the punctured approach along the curve,
$$
\boxed{\lim_{\substack{(x,y)\to(0,0)\\(x,y)\in C,\ (x,y)\neq(0,0)}}\frac yx
=\lim_{x\to0}\frac{h(x)-h(0)}x=h'(0)=-1.}
$$

====== Solution

The <chain rule> gives
$$
DG(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}
f_x(x,y)&f_y(x,y)\\
f_x(x,y^2)&2y f_y(x,y^2)
\end{pmatrix},\qquad DG(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its <determinant> is $-1$, so it is invertible. The map $G$ is <smooth> and $G(0,0)=(0,0)$. The <inverse function theorem> therefore yields an <open neighbourhood> $U$ of the origin for which $G(U)$ is <open>, contains the origin, and
$$
\boxed{G:U\to G(U)\text{ is a diffeomorphism}.}
$$

The inverse <derivative> formula, evaluated at $G(0,0)=(0,0)$, gives
$$
\boxed{D(G^{-1})(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.}

Multiplying the two displayed <matrices> in either order gives the <identity element>, verifying the <total derivative> explicitly.

==== Solution

Take a <smooth> variation $u+\varepsilon v$ with $v=0$ on the <boundary>. Differentiating under the <integral> gives the <first variation>
$$
\delta I[u](v)=\iint_R(F_uv+F_{u_\xi}v_\xi+F_{u_\zeta}v_\zeta)\,d\xi\,d\zeta.
$$
<Integration by parts> removes the <derivatives> of $v$, and the <boundary> contribution vanishes. The <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{F_u-\partial_\xi F_{u_\xi}-\partial_\zeta F_{u_\zeta}=0.}
$$
For a regular <constraint> $J[u]=c$, the <Lagrange multiplier> rule says that an extremal satisfies $\delta I=\lambda\delta J$ for a constant $\lambda$. Equivalently, apply the preceding Euler–Lagrange equation to $F-\lambda G$. Regularity means that $\delta J$ is not identically zero as a <linear functional> on the admissible variations. For the quadratic <constraint> below this holds at every nonzero admissible $u$; a zero <constraint> leaves only $u=0$.

In <polar coordinates> the constrained integrand is
$$
\mathcal L=r u_r^2+\frac1r u_\theta^2-\lambda r u^2.
$$
Its Euler–Lagrange equation is $-2\lambda r u-\partial_r(2ru_r)-\partial_\theta(2u_\theta/r)=0$, or
$$
\boxed{u_{rr}+\frac1r u_r+\frac1{r^2}u_{\theta\theta}+\lambda u=0,\qquad u(a,\theta)=0.}
$$
The <field> is single-valued and <periodic> in $\theta$. <Substitution> $u=f(r)\cos(n\theta)$ with $n\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ gives
$$
\boxed{f''+\frac1r f'+\left(\lambda-\frac{n^2}{r^2}\right)f=0,\qquad f(a)=0.}
$$
The <centre> is part of the disk, so one must also select the regular radial solution: for $n\geq1$, smoothness gives $f(r)=O(r^n)$ and the regular <derivative> behaviour; for $n=0$, $f$ is <bounded> and $f'(0)=0$. A singular solution at zero does not define an admissible <smooth> <field> on the disk.

For fixed $n$, let $c_n=\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2(n\theta)\,d\theta$, so $c_0=2\pi$ and $c_n=\pi$ for $n\geq1$. The angular <derivative> term has <integral> $n^2c_n$, including zero when $n=0$. Consequently
$$
\widehat I[f]=c_n\int_0^a\left(rf'^2+\frac{n^2}{r}f^2\right)\,dr,\qquad
\widehat J[f]=c_n\int_0^a rf^2\,dr.
$$
The one-dimensional Euler–Lagrange equation for $\widehat I-\lambda\widehat J$ is
$$
-(rf')'+\frac{n^2}{r}f=\lambda rf,
$$
exactly the same radial equation. The common factor $c_n$ cancels.

For two regular solutions with the same $n$ and different multipliers, multiply the equation for $f_1$ by $f_2$, multiply that for $f_2$ by $f_1$, subtract and integrate. The result is
$$
(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)\int_0^a rf_1f_2\,dr
=\left[r(f_1f_2'-f_1'f_2)\right]_0^a=0.
$$
At $a$, both functions vanish; at zero, regularity makes the <boundary> term tend to zero. Since the multipliers are distinct,
$$
\boxed{\int_0^a rf_1f_2\,dr=0.}
$$
This is weighted Sturm–Liouville <orthogonality> with weight $r$.

Finally, multiply one radial equation by $f$ and integrate by parts. The same endpoint conditions yield
$$
\int_0^a\left(rf'^2+\frac{n^2}{r}f^2\right)\,dr=\lambda\int_0^a rf^2\,dr.
$$
Multiplication by $c_n$ proves
$$
\boxed{\widehat I[f_i]-\lambda_i\widehat J[f_i]=0.}
$$
For any nonzero admissible solution the <energy> on the left before subtraction is positive, so its multiplier is the positive <Rayleigh quotient> $\lambda=\widehat I/\widehat J$.

===== Solution

Let $E(t)$ be the <matrix> whose columns are the moving <orthonormal basis> <vectors>. Then $EE^T=I$. Differentiating gives
$$
\dot EE^T+E\dot E^T=0,
$$
so $W=\dot EE^T$ is <skew-symmetric>. Every real <skew-symmetric> $3\times3$ <matrix> has the unique form
$$
W=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\omega_3&\omega_2\\\omega_3&0&-\omega_1\\-\omega_2&\omega_1&0\end{pmatrix},
$$
which acts on a <vector> as $Wv=\boldsymbol\omega\times v$. Since $\dot E=WE$, each column satisfies
$$
\boxed{\dot{\mathbf e}_i=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf e_i.}
$$
The <vector> $\boldsymbol\omega$ is the instantaneous <angular velocity>. It may also be written $\boldsymbol\omega=\tfrac12\sum_i\mathbf e_i\times\dot{\mathbf e}_i$.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

For <uniform density>, the <centroid> is the <volume> average of position and coincides with the <centre of mass>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf r_G=\frac1m\int_A\rho\,\mathbf r\,dV,\qquad m=\rho\operatorname{Vol}(A).}
$$
A point fixed in the body has $\mathbf r_{OQ}=\sum_iq_i\mathbf e_i$ with time-independent body coordinates $q_i$. Since $O$ is fixed, part (a) gives
$$
\mathbf v_Q=\sum_iq_i\dot{\mathbf e}_i=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r_{OQ}.
$$
The <centroid> is fixed relative to the body as well, so $\mathbf v_G=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r_{OG}$. Using $\mathbf r_{OQ}=\mathbf r_{OG}+\mathbf r_{GQ}$ therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf v_Q=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r_{OQ}=\mathbf v_G+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r_{GQ}.}
$$

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $r=(x_1,x_2,x_3)^T$ for position relative to the <centroid>. The <inertia tensor> there is
$$
\boxed{(J_G)_{ij}=\int_A\rho\bigl(|r|^2\delta_{ij}-x_ix_j\bigr)\,dV.}
$$
In particular its diagonal entries integrate the squares of distances from the corresponding axes, and its off-diagonal entries are the negative products of coordinates.

Insert $\mathbf v_Q=\mathbf v_G+\boldsymbol\omega\times r$ into $T=\tfrac12\int_A\rho|\mathbf v_Q|^2\,dV$. The cross term vanishes because $\int_A\rho r\,dV=0$. Using $|\boldsymbol\omega\times r|^2=|\boldsymbol\omega|^2|r|^2-(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot r)^2$ gives
$$
\boxed{T=\frac12m|\mathbf v_G|^2+\frac12\boldsymbol\omega^TJ_G\boldsymbol\omega.}
$$
If $a$ is the <vector> from $G$ to $O$, positions relative to $O$ are $r-a$. Expanding the defining tensor <integral> with this <substitution> again removes the terms <linear> in $r$. Thus the tensor <parallel axis theorem> is
$$
\boxed{J_O=J_G+m\bigl(|a|^2I-aa^T\bigr),\quad (J_O)_{ij}=(J_G)_{ij}+m(|a|^2\delta_{ij}-a_ia_j).}
$$
Since $O$ is fixed, $\mathbf v_G=\boldsymbol\omega\times(-a)$, so $m|\mathbf v_G|^2=m\boldsymbol\omega^T(|a|^2I-aa^T)\boldsymbol\omega$. <Substitution> into the <energy> decomposition yields
$$
\boxed{T=\frac12\boldsymbol\omega^TJ_O\boldsymbol\omega.}
$$

Choose the $z$-axis along the <cylinder> and the origin at its midpoint, so $x^2+y^2\leq b^2$ and $-a\leq z\leq a$. <Reflection symmetry> puts the <centroid> at the origin and makes all products of inertia zero. The <mass> is

====== Solution

Since $d/dx=\sqrt{m\omega/\hbar}\,d/d\xi$, dividing the <Schrödinger equation> by $\hbar\omega/2$ yields
$$
-\psi_{\xi\xi}+\xi^2\psi=\epsilon\psi,\qquad\epsilon=\frac{2E}{\hbar\omega}.
$$
For $\psi=f e^{-\xi^2/2}$, differentiation gives
$$
\psi_{\xi\xi}=e^{-\xi^2/2}\bigl(f''-2\xi f'+(\xi^2-1)f\bigr).
$$
The $\xi^2f$ terms cancel, leaving
$$
\boxed{f''-2\xi f'+(\epsilon-1)f=0,\qquad\epsilon=\frac{2E}{\hbar\omega}.}
$$

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

Differentiate the <power series> twice and align the <coefficient> of $\xi^k$. The equation becomes
$$
(k+2)(k+1)a_{k+2}+(\epsilon-1-2k)a_k=0\qquad(k\geq0).
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{a_{k+2}=\frac{2k+1-\epsilon}{(k+2)(k+1)}a_k.}
$$
Even <coefficients> are generated from $a_0$, and odd <coefficients> from $a_1$; the two <parity> <sequences> are <independent>.

===== iii
{scope}

====== Solution

A nonzero <polynomial> solution of <degree> $n$ has $a_n\neq0$ but $a_{n+2}=0$. The <recurrence> therefore forces $2n+1-\epsilon=0$. Since $\epsilon=2E/(\hbar\omega)$,
$$
\boxed{E_n=\left(n+\frac12\right)\hbar\omega,\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots.}
$$
Conversely, for this value of $\epsilon$, choose only the <parity> <sequence> containing <degree> $n$ and set the other initial <coefficient> to zero. The <recurrence> terminates at $n$, producing a nonzero <polynomial> times the decaying <Gaussian>. Thus every displayed <energy> occurs under the supplied normalizability criterion.

===== Solution

The Born <probability> is the <integral> of $|\psi_n|^2$ over the indicated <interval>. Changing variables to $\xi$ cancels the dimensional <normalization> factors and gives
$$
P_0(\ell)=\frac1{\sqrt\pi}\int_{-\ell}^{\ell}e^{-\xi^2}\,d\xi,\qquad

==== a

===== Solution

Orient the body <boundary> counterclockwise and write its <tangent> displacement as $(dx,dy)$. The outward normal from the body has displacement-weighted components $(dy,-dx)$. <Pressure> therefore exerts the <force> components $F_x=-\oint p\,dy$ and $F_y=\oint p\,dx$, or
$$
F_x-iF_y=-i\oint_{\partial B}p\,d\bar z.
$$
For steady <irrotational> incompressible flow at constant <density>, Bernoulli gives $p=p_0-\rho|w'(z)|^2/2$, with any constant far-field kinetic term absorbed into $p_0$. Its constant contribution integrates to zero. The <boundary> is a <streamline>, so $\operatorname{Im}w$ is constant there and $w'(z)\,dz$ is real. Thus $w'\,dz=\overline{w'}\,d\bar z$, giving $|w'|^2d\bar z=(w')^2dz$. This <identity element> remains valid at a <stagnation point> as both sides vanish. <Substitution> yields <Blasius force theorem>:
$$
\boxed{F_x-iF_y=\frac{i\rho}2\oint_{\partial B}(w'(z))^2\,dz.}
$$

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

The <Milne-Thomson circle theorem> states that if $f$ is an analytic complex potential with no <singularity> on or inside $|z|=a$, then
$$
w(z)=f(z)+\overline{f(a^2/\bar z)}
$$
makes $|z|=a$ a <streamline> while retaining the original exterior <singularities> and adding reflected <singularities> inside the <circle>. On the <circle> the two terms are conjugates, so the resulting <streamfunction> is zero there.

Put $A=me^{i\alpha}$ and $c=a^2/b$. Applying the formula to the exterior <planar potential doublet> gives
$$
\boxed{w(z)=\frac{me^{i\alpha}}{z-b}+\frac{me^{-i\alpha}z}{a^2-bz}.}
$$
Equivalently, up to an irrelevant constant,
$$
w(z)=\frac A{z-b}-\frac B{z-c},\qquad B=\frac{ma^2}{b^2}e^{-i\alpha}.
$$
The image <planar potential doublet> is inside the obstacle at $c<a$; the only physical <fluid> <singularity> is the prescribed one at $b$.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

The <complex velocity> obtained from part (b) is
$$
w'(z)=-\frac A{(z-b)^2}+\frac B{(z-c)^2}.
$$
For a nonzero <planar potential doublet>, $B/A=(a^2/b^2)e^{-2i\alpha}$. Its zeros therefore satisfy
$$
\frac{z-c}{z-b}=t_\pm,\qquad t_\pm=\pm\frac ab e^{-i\alpha},
$$
and hence
$$
\boxed{z_\pm=\frac{c-bt_\pm}{1-t_\pm}=a\frac{a/b\mp e^{-i\alpha}}{1\mp(a/b)e^{-i\alpha}}.}
$$
The denominators are nonzero because $a/b<1$. For either sign,
$$
|a/b\mp e^{-i\alpha}|^2=|1\mp(a/b)e^{-i\alpha}|^2,
$$
so $|z_\pm|=a$. They are distinct because $t_+\neq t_-$ and $t\mapsto(c-bt)/(1-t)$ is <injective>, its <determinant> being $c-b\neq0$. Clearing the velocity denominators gives a quadratic with these two <roots>, so there are no further finite <stagnation points>. Thus **both <stagnation points> lie on the obstacle <boundary>**.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

In the <analytic continuation> inside the <circle>, only the image location $c=a^2/b$ is singular; $b$ lies outside. Squaring the velocity gives
$$
(w')^2=\frac{A^2}{(z-b)^4}-\frac{2AB}{(z-b)^2(z-c)^2}+\frac{B^2}{(z-c)^4}.
$$
The last term has no <residue>, and the middle term has <residue>
$$
\operatorname{res}((w')^2;c)=\left.\frac{d}{dz}\left(-\frac{2AB}{(z-b)^2}\right)\right|_{z=c}
=\frac{4AB}{(c-b)^3}
=-\frac{4m^2a^2b}{(b^2-a^2)^3},
$$
because $AB=m^2a^2/b^2$ and $c-b=-(b^2-a^2)/b$. The <residue theorem> and part (a) therefore yield
$$
\boxed{F_x-iF_y=-\pi\rho\operatorname{res}((w')^2;c)=\frac{4\pi\rho m^2a^2b}{(b^2-a^2)^3}.}
$$
Thus $F_y=0$ and $F_x>0$. **The <force> points towards the <planar potential doublet> and is <independent> of its orientation**, since the factors $e^{i\alpha}$ and $e^{-i\alpha}$ cancel in $AB$.

==== Solution

A material interface obeys $D(y-\eta(x,t))/Dt=0$. In each <fluid> this gives $\phi_{jy}=\eta_t+(U_j+\phi_{jx})\eta_x$ at $y=\eta$, where $U_1=U$ and $U_2=V$. Discarding products of <perturbations> and evaluating at the undisturbed interface gives
$$
\boxed{\phi_{1y}=\eta_t+U\eta_x,\qquad\phi_{2y}=\eta_t+V\eta_x\quad(y=0).}
$$
For an inviscid <irrotational> <fluid>, unsteady Bernoulli is $\Phi_t+\tfrac12|\nabla\Phi|^2+p/\rho+gy=C(t)$, with total potential $\Phi=U_jx+\phi_j$. Subtract the undisturbed relation and linearize at the moving interface. The <pressure> <perturbation> there is $-\rho_j(\phi_{jt}+U_j\phi_{jx}+g\eta)$. With no <surface tension> the two pressures agree, giving
$$
\boxed{\rho_1(\phi_{1t}+U\phi_{1x}+g\eta)=\rho_2(\phi_{2t}+V\phi_{2x}+g\eta)\quad(y=0).}
$$
Use <complex amplitudes> and take real parts at the end. For $k>0$, solutions of <Laplace's equation> decaying away from the interface have the form
$$
\eta=Ae^{i(kx-\omega t)},\quad
\phi_1=B_1e^{ky}e^{i(kx-\omega t)},\quad
\phi_2=B_2e^{-ky}e^{i(kx-\omega t)}.
$$
Put $\Omega_1=\omega-Uk$ and $\Omega_2=\omega-Vk$. The kinematic conditions imply $B_1=-i\Omega_1A/k$ and $B_2=i\Omega_2A/k$. Substituting into the <pressure> condition gives
$$
\rho_1\left(g-\frac{\Omega_1^2}k\right)A=\rho_2\left(g+\frac{\Omega_2^2}k\right)A.
$$
A nonzero <amplitude> therefore requires
$$
\boxed{\rho_1(\omega-Uk)^2+\rho_2(\omega-Vk)^2=(\rho_1-\rho_2)gk.}
$$
To determine <stability>, let $R=\rho_1+\rho_2$ and $\overline U=(\rho_1U+\rho_2V)/R$. Completing the square yields
$$
(\omega-\overline Uk)^2=\frac{(\rho_1-\rho_2)gk}{R}-\frac{\rho_1\rho_2(U-V)^2k^2}{R^2}.
$$
For $U\neq V$, the right-hand side is negative exactly when
$$
k>\frac{(\rho_1^2-\rho_2^2)g}{\rho_1\rho_2(U-V)^2}.
$$
Then the two frequencies have opposite nonzero imaginary parts. The <root> with positive imaginary part gives <exponential growth> through $e^{-i\omega t}$. In terms of <wavelength> $\lambda=2\pi/k$, the unstable range is
$$
\boxed{\lambda<\lambda_c=\frac{2\pi\rho_1\rho_2(U-V)^2}{(\rho_1^2-\rho_2^2)g}.}
$$
This is the short-wavelength <Kelvin-Helmholtz instability> for a density-stable interface without <surface tension>. Equality is the neutral threshold; if $U=V$, the gravitational term is positive and this shear <instability> is absent.

====== Solution

Each ticket is equally likely, so the supplied sums give
$$
\mathbb E X=\frac{40}{10}=4,\qquad\mathbb E X^2=\frac{216}{10}=\frac{108}5.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E X=4,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(X)=\mathbb E X^2-(\mathbb E X)^2=\frac{28}5=5.6.}
$$
Repeated numerical values still represent different equally likely tickets; they must retain their multiplicities.

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

Replacement and mixing make the one hundred draws <independent and identically distributed>. Writing $S=\sum_{j=1}^{100}X_j$, <linearity of expectation> and additivity of variances of <independent> variables give
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E S=100\cdot4=400,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(S)=100\cdot\frac{28}5=560.}
$$

===== iii
{scope}

====== Solution

The event $S\geq500$ is contained in $|S-400|\geq100$. Thus <Chebyshev's inequality> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(S\geq500)\leq\frac{\operatorname{Var}(S)}{100^2}=\frac{560}{10000}=0.056.}
$$
The <variance> bound is rigorous and uses no <normal approximation>.

===== iv
{scope}

====== Solution

The <central limit theorem> suggests approximating $S$ by a normal variable with mean $400$ and <variance> $560$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(S\geq500)\approx1-\Phi\left(\frac{500-400}{\sqrt{560}}\right).}
$$
Since $S$ is integer-valued, the usual <continuity correction> replaces $500$ by $499.5$:
$$
\mathbb P(S\geq500)\approx1-\Phi\left(\frac{99.5}{\sqrt{560}}\right).
$$
These are approximation formulas rather than consequences of Chebyshev's bound; the <standard deviation> is $\sqrt{560}$, not $560$.

{scope}

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

<Convergence in probability> to $Y$ means that for every $\varepsilon>0$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(|Y_n-Y|>\varepsilon)\longrightarrow0.}
$$
Here the variables must be defined on a common <probability> space so that their differences are meaningful.

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

<Convergence in distribution> to $Y$ means convergence of <cumulative distribution functions> at every <continuity point> of the <limiting distribution> function:
$$
\boxed{F_{Y_n}(x)\longrightarrow F_Y(x)\quad\text{whenever }F_Y\text{ is continuous at }x.}
$$
Convergence at a jump of $F_Y$ is not required. Only the laws are involved, so this definition does not require a particular <coupling> of the variables.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Assume <convergence in probability> and fix $\varepsilon>0$. On the event $|Y_n-Y|\leq\varepsilon$, $Y\leq x-\varepsilon$ implies $Y_n\leq x$, while $Y_n\leq x$ implies $Y\leq x+\varepsilon$. Hence
$$
F_Y(x-\varepsilon)-\mathbb P(|Y_n-Y|>\varepsilon)
\leq F_{Y_n}(x)
\leq F_Y(x+\varepsilon)+\mathbb P(|Y_n-Y|>\varepsilon).
$$
Taking lower and upper limits in $n$ removes the error <probabilities>. If $x$ is a <continuity point> of $F_Y$, letting $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ squeezes both bounds to $F_Y(x)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{Y_n\to Y\text{ in probability}\Longrightarrow Y_n\to Y\text{ in distribution}.}
$$

==== Solution

A homogeneous <Poisson process> of rate $\lambda>0$ is an integer-valued <counting process> with $N_0=0$ almost surely, nondecreasing right-continuous paths with unit jumps, and <independent> <stationary increments> satisfying
$$
\boxed{N_t-N_s\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda(t-s)),\qquad0\leq s<t.}
$$
Increments over disjoint time <intervals> are <independent>. In particular,
$$
\mathbb P(N_t=k)=e^{-\lambda t}\frac{(\lambda t)^k}{k!},\qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots.
$$
The finite Poisson counts ensure that there is no explosion in finite time. The increment formula also gives <stochastic continuity>, since the <probability> of any arrival in an <interval> of length $h$ is $1-e^{-\lambda h}\to0$.

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

Since $N_3\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(3\lambda)$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(N_3\geq1)=1-\mathbb P(N_3=0)=1-e^{-3\lambda}.}
$$

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

<Stationary increments> give $N_5-N_3\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(2\lambda)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(N_5-N_3=4)=e^{-2\lambda}\frac{(2\lambda)^4}{4!}.}
$$
There is zero <probability> of an arrival at either deterministic endpoint, so endpoint inclusion does not alter the result.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Successive interarrival times $W_1=T_1$ and $W_2=T_2-T_1$ are <independent> exponential variables of rate $\lambda$. Their <joint density> is $\lambda^2e^{-\lambda(w_1+w_2)}$ for $w_1,w_2>0$. The transformation $t_1=w_1$, $t_2=w_1+w_2$ has <Jacobian determinant> one, giving
$$
\boxed{f_{T_1,T_2}(t_1,t_2)=\lambda^2e^{-\lambda t_2}\,\mathbf1_{\{0<t_1<t_2\}}.}
$$
Integrating over $0<t_1<t_2<\infty$ gives one, as a <joint density> should.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $N_3=N_2+(N_3-N_2)$. <Independent increments> make $N_2$ <independent> of the second term. Hence
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Cov}(N_2,N_3)=\operatorname{Var}(N_2)=2\lambda.}
$$
The overlapping time <interval> contributes the <covariance>; the extra unit of time contributes none.

==== e
{scope}

===== Solution

After the third arrival, the future interarrival times are <independent> of the first three interarrival times and again generate a rate-$\lambda$ <Poisson process>. Conditional on $T_3=t<2$, exactly three arrivals have occurred by time $t$, and the number of further arrivals before two is Poisson with mean $\lambda(2-t)$. Consequently, in the regular conditional-distribution sense at $t=1$,
$$
\boxed{N_2\mid(T_3=1)\ \sim\ 3+\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda).}
$$
Explicitly its conditional <mass> is zero for $k<3$ and is
$$
\mathbb P(N_2=k\mid T_3=1)=e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{k-3}}{(k-3)!},\qquad k\geq3.
$$
This conditions on the third arrival time itself, rather than merely on the event that the third arrival has happened by time one.

===== Solution

The <sample mean> has <expectation> $\theta$ and <variance> $\theta^2/n$. For $g(z)=\log z$, $g'(\theta)=1/\theta$. The first-order delta-method expansion is
$$
\log\overline Z\approx\log\theta+\frac{\overline Z-\theta}{\theta}.
$$
Taking its mean and <variance> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[\log\overline Z]\approx\log\theta,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(\log\overline Z)\approx\frac1n.}
$$
These are large-sample approximations. Retaining the quadratic Taylor term would add the leading mean correction $-1/(2n)$, but it does not change the requested first-order expression.

==== b
{scope}

===== i
{scope}

====== Solution

For nonnegative <integers> $j,k$, repeated <integration by parts> gives the <beta integral> $\int_0^1y^j(1-y)^k\,dy=j!k!/(j+k+1)!$. Multiplying the stated <density> by $y$ and using this formula yields
$$
\mathbb E Y_{(r)}=\frac{n!}{(r-1)!(n-r)!}\frac{r!(n-r)!}{(n+1)!}.
$$
Cancellation gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E Y_{(r)}=\frac r{n+1}.}
$$

===== ii
{scope}

====== Solution

The same <beta integral> with one additional power of $y$ gives
$$
\mathbb E Y_{(r)}^2=\frac{r(r+1)}{(n+1)(n+2)}.
$$
Subtracting the square of the mean yields
$$
\operatorname{Var}(Y_{(r)})=\frac{r(n+1-r)}{(n+1)^2(n+2)}.
$$
At $r=1$ and $r=n$, therefore,
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(Y_{(1)})=\operatorname{Var}(Y_{(n)})=\frac n{(n+1)^2(n+2)}.}
$$
Their equality also follows by reflecting every uniform observation via $y\mapsto1-y$.

====== Solution

For the shifted exponential model, $F(x)=1-e^{-\lambda(x-1)}$ for $x\geq1$. It is increasing, so the <probability integral transform> preserves order: $F(X_{(r)})=Y_{(r)}$. The expected uniform plotting positions are $r/(n+1)$ by part (b). Replacing the random transformed <order statistic> by this representative position gives
$$
1-e^{-\lambda(x_{(r)}-1)}\approx\frac r{n+1},\qquad
x_{(r)}\approx1+\frac1\lambda\left[-\log\left(1-\frac r{n+1}\right)\right].
$$
Thus **plotting $x_{(r)}$ against $t_r=-\log(1-r/(n+1))$ should give an approximate <line> with intercept one and slope $\boxed{1/\lambda}$**.

The replacement is a quantile-plot approximation, not an assertion that <expectation> commutes exactly with the nonlinear inverse <distribution function>. The random points need not lie exactly on the <line>.

===== ii

====== Solution

For the Pareto model, integration gives $F(x)=1-x^{-\lambda}$ on $x\geq1$. The same plotting positions suggest $1-x_{(r)}^{-\lambda}\approx r/(n+1)$. Taking logarithms gives
$$
\log x_{(r)}\approx\frac1\lambda\left[-\log\left(1-\frac r{n+1}\right)\right].
$$
Therefore **plot $\log x_{(r)}$ vertically against $t_r=-\log(1-r/(n+1))$ horizontally**. A reasonable fit should be approximately a straight <line> through the origin, with slope $\boxed{1/\lambda}$. This logarithmic transformation distinguishes the Pareto tail model from the shifted exponential plot in part (i).

{c}

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

<Independence> and normality give $\overline X\sim N(\mu,1/n)$. Consequently $Z=\sqrt n(\overline X-\mu)$ is standard normal, and
$$
\mathbb P\left(\overline X<\mu+\frac{z_\alpha}{\sqrt n}\right)=\Phi(z_\alpha)=1-\alpha.
$$
Rearranging the event gives $\mu>\overline X-z_\alpha/\sqrt n$. Thus the random <interval> $(\overline X-z_\alpha/\sqrt n,\infty)$ covers the fixed parameter with <probability> $1-\alpha$. After observing the sample, its realized <confidence interval> is
$$
\boxed{\left(\overline x-\frac{z_\alpha}{\sqrt n},\infty\right).}
$$
The coverage refers to repeated samples, not to a <posterior probability> for the fixed parameter.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

The <Neyman-Pearson lemma> concerns testing two simple hypotheses with densities $f_0$ and $f_1$ relative to a common measure. A test that rejects when $f_1(x)>k f_0(x)$, accepts when $f_1(x)<k f_0(x)$, and randomizes on equality if necessary to achieve size $\alpha$, is most powerful among all tests with size at most $\alpha$:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E_0\varphi=\alpha\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathbb E_1\psi\leq\mathbb E_1\varphi\text{ for every test }\psi\text{ with }\mathbb E_0\psi\leq\alpha.}
$$
Here $\varphi$ is the described <likelihood-ratio test> and test functions take values in $[0,1]$. In the <continuous> normal setting below the <boundary> has <probability> zero, so randomization is unnecessary.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

The <likelihood ratio> between the two normal means is
$$
\frac{L(\mu_1;x)}{L(\mu_0;x)}
=\exp\left((\mu_1-\mu_0)\sum_{i=1}^nx_i-\frac n2(\mu_1^2-\mu_0^2)\right).
$$
Since $\mu_1>\mu_0$, it is strictly increasing in $\overline x$. The Neyman–Pearson test therefore rejects for large sample means. Under the null, $\sqrt n(\overline X-\mu_0)\sim N(0,1)$, so choosing a right-tail <probability> $\alpha$ gives
$$
\boxed{c_\alpha=\mu_0+\frac{z_\alpha}{\sqrt n},\qquad C=\{x:\overline x\geq c_\alpha\}.}
$$
This has size exactly $\alpha$ because the <sampling distribution> is <continuous>.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

For every particular alternative mean $\mu_1>\mu_0$, the <likelihood ratio> is increasing in the same statistic $\overline x$. Its size-$\alpha$ threshold is fixed solely by the null distribution, so it is always the same $c_\alpha$ found in part (c), <independent> of $\mu_1$.

The single <rejection region> is therefore most powerful against every member of the composite alternative. Hence **the test is uniformly most powerful for $H_1:\mu>\mu_0$**.

==== e
{scope}

===== Solution

For the observed mean, the one-sided $p$-value is the null <probability> of a <sample mean> at least as large:
$$
p=\mathbb P_{\mu_0}(\overline X\geq\overline x).
$$
Standardization under the null gives
$$
\boxed{p=1-\Phi\bigl(\sqrt n(\overline x-\mu_0)\bigr).}
$$

==== f
{scope}

===== Solution

The <standard normal distribution> function is strictly increasing. Thus $p>\alpha$ implies
$$
1-\Phi(\sqrt n(\overline x-\mu_0))>\alpha
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\sqrt n(\overline x-\mu_0)<z_\alpha.
$$
Rearranging gives
$$
\boxed{\mu_0>\overline x-\frac{z_\alpha}{\sqrt n},\quad\text{so }\mu_0\in\left(\overline x-\frac{z_\alpha}{\sqrt n},\infty\right).}
$$
This is the confidence-interval/test duality for the matching one-sided procedures. The strict inequalities agree with the printed <open> <interval>.

==== a

===== Solution

A real square <matrix> $Q$ is <orthogonal> when $Q^TQ=I$, equivalently $Q^{-1}=Q^T$. For $c=\cos\theta$ and $s=\sin\theta$,
$$
M(\theta)^TM(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}c&-s\\s&c\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c&s\\-s&c\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}c^2+s^2&0\\0&c^2+s^2\end{pmatrix}=I.
$$
Thus **$M(\theta)$ is <orthogonal> for every allowed angle**, and indeed for every real angle.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Using <orthogonality>,
$$
\|Qx\|^2=(Qx)^TQx=x^TQ^TQx=x^Tx=\|x\|^2.
$$
Both <norms> are nonnegative, so taking square <roots> proves
$$
\boxed{\|Qx\|=\|x\|.}
$$
Thus an <orthogonal matrix> is a <Euclidean isometry> fixing the origin.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

The columns outside indices $i,j$ are unchanged coordinate <vectors>. The remaining two columns have nonzero entries only in rows $i,j$, with the $2\times2$ block $M(\theta)$. They have squared <norm> $\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1$ and mutual <inner product> zero; they are also <orthogonal> to all the unchanged columns. Hence $J^TJ=I$.

Let $r=\sqrt{x_i^2+x_j^2}$. If $r>0$, choose $\theta\in[-\pi,\pi]$ with $\cos\theta=x_i/r$ and $\sin\theta=x_j/r$. Then
$$
y_i=\cos\theta\,x_i+\sin\theta\,x_j=r,\qquad
y_j=-\sin\theta\,x_i+\cos\theta\,x_j=0.
$$
Every other coordinate is unchanged. If $r=0$, choose $\theta=0$; the same conclusions hold. Therefore the procedure can be fixed with the nonnegative <pivot> convention
$$
\boxed{y_i=\sqrt{x_i^2+x_j^2},\qquad y_j=0,\qquad y_k=x_k\ (k\neq i,j).}
$$
This is a <Givens rotation>.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $J_1,J_2,J_3$ for the actual successive <rotations> $J(1,2)$, $J(1,3)$, and $J(2,3)$, each selected using the currently transformed <matrix>. The first eliminates position $(2,1)$; the second eliminates $(3,1)$ while leaving row two unchanged. After these, the first column is $(r_{11},0,0)^T$. The final <rotation> of rows two and three preserves both zeros in that column and eliminates $(3,2)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{R=J_3J_2J_1A\text{ is upper triangular}.}
$$
A lower-triangular starting <matrix> does not <force> any further zeros above the diagonal. For example, the lower-triangular <matrix> with all entries on and below its diagonal equal to one gives, with the nonnegative <pivot> convention,
$$
R=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt3&2/\sqrt3&1/\sqrt3\\0&\sqrt{2/3}&1/\sqrt6\\0&0&1/\sqrt2\end{pmatrix},
$$
which has all upper-triangular entries nonzero.

Inverting the product of <rotations> yields the <QR factorization>
$$
\boxed{Q=J_1^TJ_2^TJ_3^T,\qquad A=QR.}
$$
The order reverses when taking the <transpose>. If $A$ is <orthogonal>, then $R$ is both <orthogonal> and <upper triangular>. Its first column forces $|r_{11}|=1$ and <orthogonality> with the other columns forces $r_{12}=r_{13}=0$. Applying the same argument to the remaining block makes $R$ diagonal with entries of modulus one.

Our elimination rule makes $r_{11}$ and $r_{22}$ positive when nonzero, so they are both one. Every <rotation> has <determinant> one; thus $\det R=\det A$ and
$$
\boxed{R=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,\det A).}
$$
Without a fixed pivot-sign convention one could only specify a diagonal sign <matrix>. The convention chosen in part (c) determines the precise signs above.

===== Solution

Subtract the two finite sums. In the shifted sum replace the summation index $k$ by $k-1$. For every interior index the new <coefficient> is
$$
(-1)^k\binom rk-(-1)^{k-1}\binom r{k-1}=(-1)^k\binom{r+1}k
$$
by <Pascal's identity>; the two endpoint <coefficients> agree as well. Therefore
$$
\boxed{Q_{(r)}(x)-Q_{(r)}(x-h)=Q_{(r+1)}(x).}
$$
For a degree-$d$ <polynomial> with <leading coefficient> $c\neq0$, the backward difference has leading term
$$
c\bigl(x^d-(x-h)^d\bigr)=cdh\,x^{d-1}+\text{lower-degree terms}.
$$
Since $h>0$, this <coefficient> is nonzero for $d\geq1$. Starting with $Q_{(0)}=P$, iteration gives <degree> $n-r$ and <leading coefficient> $c h^r n!/(n-r)!$ for $0\leq r\leq n$. The next difference of the resulting constant is zero:
$$
\boxed{\deg Q_{(r)}=n-r\ (0\leq r\leq n),\qquad Q_{(n+1)}\equiv0.}
$$

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

A <natural cubic spline> with the given <knots> is a $C^2$ function that is <polynomial> of <degree> at most three on each <interval> between consecutive <knots> and has $S''(x_0)=S''(x_4)=0$. With the usual whole-line convention, it extends affinely outside the extreme <knots>. The word natural refers to these endpoint second-derivative conditions; the <interpolation> values must be specified separately.

For $H_k(x)=(x-x_k)^3_+$, direct differentiation on either side of the <knot> gives
$$
H_k'(x)=3(x-x_k)^2_+,\qquad H_k''(x)=6(x-x_k)_+.
$$
Both one-sided limits of the function and its first two <derivatives> at $x_k$ are zero, so these formulas are <continuous> there and
$$
\boxed{H_k\in C^2(\mathbb R).}
$$
Its third <derivative> jumps from zero to six, which is permitted for a <cubic spline>.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

The finite sum of truncated cubes is $C^2$ by part (b) and is cubic on each <knot> <interval>. For $x\leq0$, every term vanishes. For $x\geq4h$, every truncated power equals its ordinary <polynomial>, so part (a) applied to $P(x)=x^3$ gives
$$
S_{(3)}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^4(-1)^k\binom4k(x-kh)^3=Q_{(4)}(x)=0.
$$
It is therefore zero on both exterior <intervals>. Its $C^2$ regularity makes the endpoint second <derivatives> zero too, proving naturality.

Evaluation at the five <knots> gives
$$
\boxed{\bigl(S_{(3)}(0),S_{(3)}(h),S_{(3)}(2h),S_{(3)}(3h),S_{(3)}(4h)\bigr)=h^3(0,1,4,1,0).}
$$
For example the three interior values in units of $h^3$ are $1$, $8-4=4$, and $27-4\cdot8+6=1$. At $4h$ the cancellation is $64-4\cdot27+6\cdot8-4=0$.

For $h=1$ an explicit graph description is
$$
S_{(3)}(x)=\begin{cases}
0,&x\leq0\text{ or }x\geq4,\\
x^3,&0\leq x\leq1,\\
4-6(x-2)^2+3|x-2|^3,&1\leq x\leq3,\\
(4-x)^3,&3\leq x\leq4.
\end{cases}
$$
It is symmetric about $x=2$, increases from zero to its maximum four at the <centre>, and decreases symmetrically back to zero. It joins the zero tails with zero first and second <derivatives>.
