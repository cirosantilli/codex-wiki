= Solution
{parent=2007/A/A2}

Every nonzero vector can be extended to a <basis>, and the invertible map between two such bases carries the first vector to the second. The action is therefore transitive. Counting ordered bases gives
$$
|G|=(8-1)(8-2)(8-4)=168.
$$
The <stabilizer> $H$ of one nonzero vector consequently has order $168/7=24$. There are three planes containing that vector, corresponding to the three lines of the two-dimensional quotient space. Adapted bases show $H$ acts transitively on these planes, so the <stabilizer> of one such plane has order $24/3=8$.

Let $Q$ be any subgroup of order eight. Its <orbit> lengths on the seven nonzero vectors are powers of two. Since their sum is odd, at least one <orbit> has length one. Fix such a vector $v$. The action on the three planes through $v$ likewise has a fixed plane $P$. Thus $Q$ is contained in the <stabilizer> of the <flag> $\langle v\rangle\subset P$, and equality follows because that <stabilizer> already has order eight. The full <group> acts transitively on these flags, so all the subgroups are conjugate.

There are $7\cdot3=21$ flags. To see they yield distinct subgroups, use the standard upper unitriangular matrices relative to an adapted <basis>. Their only common nonzero fixed vector is the first <basis> vector: varying entries in positions $(1,2)$ and $(1,3)$ forces the other two coordinates to vanish. On the quotient by that line, varying position $(2,3)$ leaves only the line of the second <basis> vector fixed. Hence this subgroup fixes exactly one full <flag>. Conjugation transfers uniqueness to each <flag> <stabilizer>, giving
$$
\boxed{21\text{ subgroups of order eight}}.
$$

= Solution

An element is algebraic over $F$ if it is a zero of some nonzero polynomial in $F[x]$. An extension is algebraic if every element is algebraic. If $[K:F]=d<\infty$, the $d+1$ elements $1,\alpha,\ldots,\alpha^d$ are linearly dependent over $F$, proving every $\alpha\in K$ is algebraic.

The converse fails for the <algebraic closure> $\overline{\mathbb Q}$ inside $\mathbb C$. All its elements are algebraic by definition, but it contains $2^{1/n}$ of degree $n$ for every positive integer $n$, since $x^n-2$ is irreducible by Eisenstein's criterion at two. A finite extension could not contain elements of unbounded degree, so this <algebraic extension> is infinite.

The cubic $x^3+x+1$ has no zero in $\mathbb F_2$, so is irreducible. Its quotient field has eight elements. Every nonzero element of that field satisfies $x^7=1$ by the order of its multiplicative <group>, so the polynomial splits there. $$

= Solution
{parent=2007/C/C2}

<Wilson's theorem> states that for a prime $p$, $(p-1)!\equiv-1\pmod p$. For odd $p$, pair each nonzero <residue> with its multiplicative inverse. Only the self-inverse residues remain unpaired; $x^2=1$ in a field gives $x=\pm1$. Their product is $-1$, proving the result. For $p=2$ the assertion is immediate.

If composite $n\ge6$ is not the square of a prime, choose a prime divisor $p$ such that $p$ and $n/p$ are distinct. Both occur among the factors of $(n-1)!$, so their product $n$ divides it. In the remaining case $n=p^2$, $p\ge3$, and the two distinct factors $p,2p$ both occur in the factorial and have product divisible by $p^2$. Thus the factorial is zero modulo every such composite $n$.

For prime $n$, Wilson's congruence makes the numerator sine squared equal $\sin^2(\pi/n)$, so its ratio is one. For composite $n\ge6$, the numerator vanishes. The only exceptional composite in the sum is four; its ratio is $\sin^2(3\pi/2)/\sin^2(\pi/4)=2$. Therefore for $x\ge4$ the sum counts the primes plus two, and subtracting two gives exactly the required prime count. All statements concern the integers $2\le n\le\lfloor x\rfloor$.

A <coordinate transformation> is a continuously differentiable bijection from the <open set> $U$ onto an open image, with continuously differentiable inverse. Equivalently one may specify a one-to-one continuously differentiable map whose <Jacobian determinant> is everywhere nonzero; the <inverse function theorem> gives the corresponding smooth inverse on its image.

The change-of-variables formula is
$$
\boxed{\int_{T(U)}f(y)\,dy=\int_U f(T(x))|\det DT_x|\,dx}.

For an integrable $f$, the transformed function with its absolute-Jacobian factor is integrable as well. The absolute value is essential because the integral does not depend on whether the coordinate change preserves <orientation>.

Use $u=x-y$, $v=x+y$. It sends the diamond-shaped open square onto $(-1,1)^2$, and the inverse Jacobian has absolute <determinant> $1/2$. On this rectangle $|u|<1$ and $v+2>1$, so the original function is measurable, bounded by one and supported on a finite-area set, proving integrability. The transformed integral separates:
$$
\int f^{2n}=\frac12\int_{-1}^1u^{2n}\,du\int_{-1}^1(v+2)^{-2n}\,dv
=\boxed{\frac{1-3^{1-2n}}{(2n+1)(2n-1)}}.
$$
The two one-dimensional integrals follow from the <fundamental theorem of calculus>; Fubini's theorem applies by the established integrability.

The <dominated convergence theorem> says that if measurable $f_n\to f$ almost everywhere and $|f_n|\le g$ for an integrable $g$, then $f$ is integrable, $\int|f_n-f|\to0$, and consequently $\int f_n\to\int f$.

First derive <Fatou's lemma> from monotone convergence. For nonnegative $u_n$, the measurable functions $v_n=\inf_{j\ge n}u_j$ increase to $\liminf u_n$, and $\int v_n\le\inf_{j\ge n}\int u_j$. Monotone convergence therefore yields $\int\liminf u_n\le\liminf\int u_n$.

Now $f$ is measurable and $|f|\le g$ almost everywhere, so is integrable. Apply <Fatou's lemma> to $u_n=2g-|f_n-f|\ge0$, which converges almost everywhere to $2g$:
$$
\int2g\le\liminf_n\left(\int2g-\int|f_n-f|\right)
=\int2g-\limsup_n\int|f_n-f|.
$$
The last integrals are nonnegative, so they tend to zero. Finally $|\int f_n-\int f|\le\int|f_n-f|\to0$. This argument also works for complex-valued functions.

At each $x>0$, eventually $x<n$, and the stipulated power limit gives $f_n(x)\to e^{-x}\sin x$. At nonpositive $x$ the functions vanish. On their positive support,
$$
|f_n(x)|\le(1-x/n)^n\le e^{-x}.

The supplied integrable exponential therefore dominates the sequence. Dominated convergence gives
$$
\lim_n\int f_n=\int_0^\infty e^{-x}\sin x\,dx=\operatorname{Im}\frac1{1-i}=\boxed{\frac12}.
$$
The final improper integral is absolutely convergent, so agrees with its <Lebesgue integral>.

<Compactness> means every open cover has a finite subcover. <Connectedness> means the space cannot be written as the union of two disjoint nonempty open sets. The <Hausdorff> property means every two distinct points have disjoint open neighbourhoods.

For the surjection $p$ to the set of equivalence classes, define a subset $V$ of the quotient to be open exactly when $p^{-1}(V)$ is open in $X$. Inverse images preserve the empty and full sets, arbitrary unions and finite intersections. Applying the topology axioms in $X$ proves this is a topology on the quotient, and it makes $p$ continuous.

The map need not be open. On $X=\mathbb R$, identify zero with one and leave every other point in its own class. The set $U=(-1/4,1/4)$ is open, but its <saturation> is
$$
p^{-1}(p(U))=U\cup\{1\},
$$
which is not open in $\mathbb R$. Thus $p(U)$ is not open in the <quotient topology>. <Continuity> of a <quotient map> does not imply openness.

= Solution
{parent=2007/E/E4/b}

The surface is a **<Klein bottle>**. Here is a cell-count justification that keeps all the arrow directions. Name the corners of the first square $a,b,c,d$, beginning at its upper left and proceeding clockwise; name those of the second $e,f,g,h$ in the same way. The single-arrow gluing identifies $a$ with $e$ and $b$ with $h$. The triple-arrow gluing identifies $d$ with $h$ and $c$ with $g$. The first square's quadruple-arrow gluing identifies $b$ with $d$ and $c$ with $a$. The second square's double-arrow gluing identifies $e,f,g$ with one another. There are exactly two resulting vertex classes:
$$
\{a,c,e,f,g\},\qquad\{b,d,h\}.
$$
There are four edge classes and two faces, so the <Euler characteristic> is $2-4+2=0$. Every edge is paired and the corner links close into circles, giving a <compact> surface without boundary; the cross-square identifications make it <connected>. It is nonorientable: the quadruple-arrow identification glues two boundary edges of the same square in the same boundary-traversal direction, so no <orientation> of that face can extend across the gluing. The classification of <compact> <connected> surfaces now identifies the nonorientable surface of <Euler characteristic> zero as the <Klein bottle>.

Geometrically, the internal identification in each square produces a <Möbius band>. The remaining single- and triple-arrow edges comprise their two boundary circles, which are glued together. The double of a <Möbius band> is another description of the same <Klein bottle>.

Differentiability at $a$ means that a linear map $L:V\to V_1$ satisfies
$$
\frac{\|f(a+h)-f(a)-Lh\|_1}{\|h\|}\longrightarrow0\quad(h\to0).
$$
This map is unique and is the total derivative $Df_a$. In finite dimensions linear maps are continuous.

The <Frobenius norm> is submultiplicative. Expanding the noncommuting cube gives
$$
(A+H)^3=A^3+A^2H+AHA+HA^2+AH^2+HAH+H^2A+H^3.
$$
The last four terms have <norm> at most $3|A||H|^2+|H|^3=o(|H|)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{Df_A(H)=A^2H+AHA+HA^2}.
$$
If $|C|<1$, the <Neumann series> $\sum_{j\ge0}(-C)^j$ converges and is the inverse of $I+C$, as follows by multiplying its partial sums and letting the final power tend to zero. Its terms from degree two onwards have <norm> at most $|C|^2/(1-|C|)$. Hence $(I+C)^{-1}=I-C+O(|C|^2)$.

For invertible $A$, write $A+H=A(I+A^{-1}H)$. Whenever $|A^{-1}||H|<1$, this is invertible, proving the <general linear group> is open. The same expansion gives
$$
(A+H)^{-1}=A^{-1}-A^{-1}HA^{-1}+O(|H|^2).
$$
Compose this inverse derivative with the cube derivative, setting $B=A^{-1}$. The result is
$$
\boxed{Dg_A(H)=-(A^{-3}HA^{-1}+A^{-2}HA^{-2}+A^{-1}HA^{-3})}.
$$
The three terms cannot generally be combined into a scalar-style $-3A^{-4}H$, because matrix factors need not commute.

This is the cubic case of the <Fréchet derivative of a negative matrix power>.

Parametrize the surface by $r(x,\theta)=(x,y(x)\cos\theta,y(x)\sin\theta)$, with $0\le\theta<2\pi$. Its tangent vectors are <orthogonal>, of lengths $\sqrt{1+y'^2}$ and $y$. Thus the area element is $y\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx\,d\theta$. Integrating over the angle gives
$$
\boxed{J[y]=2\pi\int_{x_1}^{x_2}y\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx}.
$$

{parent=2007/G/G2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2007/G/G2/ii}

Discard the constant factor $2\pi$ when forming the <Euler-Lagrange equation>. For $F=y\sqrt{1+y'^2}$ it becomes
$$
\sqrt{1+y'^2}-\frac d{dx}\frac{yy'}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}=0,
\qquad yy''=1+y'^2.
$$
The <Beltrami identity>, since $F$ has no explicit $x$ dependence, gives $F-y'F_{y'}=y/\sqrt{1+y'^2}=C>0$. Combining with the differential equation yields $y''=y/C^2$. Its exponential solution has coefficients whose product is $C^2/4$, by $y'^2=y^2/C^2-1$. Positivity therefore puts it in the form
$$
\boxed{y(x)=C\cosh((x-K)/C),\qquad C>0}.
$$
Its constants must satisfy $y_1=C\cosh((x_1-K)/C)$ and $y_2=C\cosh((x_2-K)/C)$. Depending on the endpoint data, these equations may have no solution or more than one. A constant function can satisfy a formal first-integral equation but fails the full <Euler-Lagrange equation>; it is not an extra extremal.

= Solution
{parent=2007/G/G2/iii}

The functional is unbounded above. Let $y_0$ be the positive affine interpolant between the two endpoint values, and choose a smooth nonnegative function $\phi$ that vanishes at the endpoints and is positive somewhere inside. The admissible curves $y_M=y_0+M\phi$ satisfy
$$
J[y_M]\ge2\pi\int y_M\,dx\longrightarrow\infty.
$$
**The claimed minimum conclusion does not follow and is false in general.** A stationary point may have a negative <second variation>, even though the functional is unbounded above. Here is an explicit example within the allowed positive-<graph> class.

Take the stationary profile $y(x)=\cosh x$ on $[-2,2]$, and the endpoint-zero variation $\eta(x)=\cosh x-\cosh2$. For sufficiently small perturbation parameter $s$, $y+s\eta$ remains positive and preserves both endpoint values. Put $C=\cosh2$. Differentiating the area twice and integrating its mixed term by parts gives
$$
\frac{\delta^2J[\eta]}{2\pi}=\int_{-2}^2\operatorname{sech}^2x\,(\eta'^2-\eta^2)\,dx
=4C\arctan(\sinh2)-2(1+C^2)\tanh2.
$$
This is strictly negative: the first term is less than $2\pi C$, while $(C+C^{-1})\tanh2>\pi$. For example $e^2>7$ gives $C>7/2$ and $\tanh2>24/25$, whose product already exceeds $\pi$. Thus nearby positive profiles have smaller area. This <catenoid> is **not a local minimum**.

The corrected conclusion is that any smooth positive minimizer, if attained, must have the <catenoid> form. That necessary condition is not sufficient, and existence or stability requires a separate argument.

Write $q=(x,X)^T$, $M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{pmatrix}$ and $K=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\\beta&\gamma\end{pmatrix}$. The linear equations are $M\ddot q+Kq=0$. A <normal mode> has all coordinates oscillating with one frequency and fixed relative amplitudes, $q=v\cos(\omega t+\varphi)$. Its squared frequency solves
$$
\det(K-\omega^2M)=(\alpha-a\omega^2)(\gamma-c\omega^2)-(\beta-b\omega^2)^2=0,
$$
and its amplitude vector is a nonzero null vector of $K-\omega^2M$. The assumed distinct positive roots give two normal frequencies, their positive square roots.

For the rods let $\theta_1,\theta_2$ be their small inclinations from the downward vertical. To first order, $\theta_1=x/a$ and $\theta_2=(X-x)/(2a)$. The first rod has kinetic <energy> $m\dot x^2/6$. The second has centre-of-mass horizontal velocity $(\dot x+\dot X)/2$ and angular velocity $(\dot X-\dot x)/(2a)$, giving
$$
T_2=\frac m4(\dot x+\dot X)^2+\frac m{12}(\dot X-\dot x)^2.
$$
Together these give $T=m(3\dot x^2+2\dot x\dot X+2\dot X^2)/6$. The centre-of-mass height changes give
$$
V=\frac{mga}{4}\theta_1^2+mga(\theta_1^2+\theta_2^2)
=\frac{mg}{4a}(6x^2-2xX+X^2),
$$
proving the displayed quadratic <Lagrangian>.

Here $M=(m/3)\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ and $K=(mg/(2a))\begin{pmatrix}6&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}$. Set $\lambda=2a\omega^2/(3g)$. The <determinant> equation reduces to $5\lambda^2-17\lambda+5=0$, so
$$
\boxed{\omega_\pm^2=\frac{3g}{20a}(17\pm3\sqrt{21})}.
$$
The first row of the mode equation gives $X/x=(6-3\lambda)/(1+\lambda)$. The actual rod-angle ratio is therefore
$$
\frac{\theta_2}{\theta_1}=\frac{5-4\lambda}{2(1+\lambda)}.
$$
It is positive for the smaller root and negative for the larger root. Thus **the low-frequency mode has the rods in phase, and the high-frequency mode has them out of phase**. Using the rod-angle ratio is necessary because $X$ is the displacement of the end of the second rod, not that rod's inclination.

= Solution
{parent=2007/J/J2}

In vacuum, the source-free <Maxwell equations> are $\nabla\cdot E=\nabla\cdot B=0$, $\nabla\times E=-\partial_tB$ and $\nabla\times B=c^{-2}\partial_tE$. Put $\vartheta=\omega(t-z/c)$ and denote the field amplitude by $E_0$. The corresponding travelling-wave <magnetic field> is
$$
\boxed{B=\frac{E_0}{c}(-\sin\vartheta,\cos\vartheta,0)}.
$$
Both divergences vanish because the fields have no longitudinal component and no dependence on the transverse coordinates. Direct differentiation gives $\nabla\times E=(\omega E_0/c)(\cos\vartheta,\sin\vartheta,0)=-\partial_tB$ and $\nabla\times B=(\omega E_0/c^2)(-\sin\vartheta,\cos\vartheta,0)=c^{-2}\partial_tE$.

Introducing potentials by $B=\nabla\times A$ and $E=-\nabla\phi-\partial_tA$ automatically satisfies the magnetic-divergence and Faraday equations. The remaining <Maxwell equations> become equations for these potentials, with the usual gauge freedom. For $\omega\ne0$ take $\phi=0$ and
$$
A=\frac c\omega B=\frac{E_0}{\omega}(-\sin\vartheta,\cos\vartheta,0).
$$
Differentiation verifies both potential relations.

The electromagnetic force is $Q(E+v\times B)$. Confinement also supplies a normal reaction force. For motion in a plane of constant $z$, both $v$ and $B$ are tangent to the plane, so their cross product is normal and is balanced by that reaction. It does not enter the in-plane equation $m\ddot r=QE_0(\cos\vartheta,\sin\vartheta)$.

At the fixed plane, the solution with no uniform drift is
$$
r(t)=r_0-\frac{QE_0}{m\omega^2}(\cos\vartheta,\sin\vartheta).
$$
It is circular, with
$$
\boxed{R=\frac{|QE_0|}{m\omega^2}}.
$$
Other initial data can add a constant drift velocity; the question only asks for the possibility of circular motion.

= Solution
{parent=2007/K/K3}

Orient the body boundary counterclockwise. The outward normal from the body satisfies $n\,ds=(dy,-dx)$, so pressure gives
$$
F_x-iF_y=-i\oint_Cp\,d\bar z.
$$
For steady <irrotational flow>, Bernoulli's equation makes $p+\rho|u|^2/2$ constant in the <connected> fluid region. Its constant contribution integrates to zero. Write $q=w'=u-iv$. Impermeability says the velocity is tangent to the boundary, so $q\,dz=\bar q\,d\bar z$ there. Consequently
$$
F_x-iF_y=\frac{i\rho}2\oint_C|q|^2d\bar z=\boxed{\frac{i\rho}2\oint_Cq^2\,dz},
$$
which proves Blasius' theorem with this contour convention.

The background <stream function> is the imaginary part of $w_0(z)=\alpha(z-c)^3$. Since its coefficients are real, the circle theorem gives
$$
\boxed{w(z)=\alpha\{(z-c)^3+(a^2/z-c)^3\}}.
$$
Its derivative is
$$
q=\alpha(3z^2-6cz+3c^2-3a^6z^{-4}+6ca^4z^{-3}-3c^2a^2z^{-2}).
$$

= Solution
{parent=2007/K/K4}

A material interface obeys $\eta_t+\phi_{j,x}\eta_x=\phi_{j,y}$ at its actual height. Keeping only first-order terms and evaluating at the undisturbed interface gives $\phi_{1,y}=\phi_{2,y}=\eta_t$ at $y=0$. Linearized unsteady Bernoulli gives the pressure perturbation $-\rho_j(\phi_{j,t}+g\eta)$, after absorbing time-only constants into the potentials. Pressure <continuity> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\rho_1\phi_{1,t}-\rho_2\phi_{2,t}+g(\rho_1-\rho_2)\eta=0\quad\text{at }y=0}.
$$
For $\vartheta=kx-\omega t$, choose potentials decaying away from the interface:
$$
\phi_1=\frac{A\omega}{k}e^{ky}\sin\vartheta\quad(y<0),\qquad
\phi_2=-\frac{A\omega}{k}e^{-ky}\sin\vartheta\quad(y>0).
$$
They are harmonic and their normal derivatives equal $A\omega\sin\vartheta=\eta_t$ at zero. The dynamic condition reduces to
$$
\boxed{\omega^2=gk\frac{\rho_1-\rho_2}{\rho_1+\rho_2},\qquad c=\frac\omega k=\sqrt{\frac{g(\rho_1-\rho_2)}{k(\rho_1+\rho_2)}}}.
$$
If the denser fluid is above, the squared frequency is negative. The corresponding perturbations grow or decay exponentially instead of oscillating: this is <Rayleigh-Taylor instability>, with growing rate $\sqrt{gk(\rho_2-\rho_1)/(\rho_1+\rho_2)}$ in the ideal model without surface tension.

The <central limit theorem> states that for independent identically distributed variables of finite mean $\mu$ and positive finite <variance> $\sigma^2$,
$$
\frac{\sum_{j=1}^nX_j-n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt n}\xrightarrow d N(0,1).
$$
For a continuous strictly increasing <cumulative distribution function>, its inverse is defined on $(0,1)$. Thus $\mathbb P(F(X)\le y)=\mathbb P(X\le F^{-1}(y))=y$ for $0<y<1$, proving the uniform law.

If $T=-\log(1-Y)$, then for $t\ge0$, $\mathbb P(T\le t)=\mathbb P(Y\le1-e^{-t})=1-e^{-t}$. Hence $f_T(t)=e^{-t}\mathbf1_{(0,\infty)}(t)$, an exponential density of rate one. Its first two moments are one and two, by <integration by parts>, so $\mathbb ET=1$ and $\operatorname{Var}T=1$.

Although the original variables may have different distributions, their separate transforms $Y_k=F_k(X_k)$ are independent and all uniform. Consequently $T_k=-\log(1-Y_k)$ are independent identically distributed exponentials of rate one, and the statistic is exactly
$$
Z_n=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^nT_k-n}{\sqrt n}.
$$
The stated <central limit theorem> therefore yields $\boxed{Z_n\xrightarrow d N(0,1)}$.

A <Poisson process> of rate $\lambda$ is a counting process starting at zero with independent stationary increments, with $N(t)-N(s)$ Poisson of mean $\lambda(t-s)$ for $t\ge s$, and right-continuous paths with unit jumps. Equivalently the small-interval probabilities are $\lambda h+o(h)$ for one event and $o(h)$ for two or more.

<Independence> of the two processes makes their joint increments over disjoint intervals independent. On an interval of length $h$, the sum of their independent Poisson increments has <probability generating function>
$$
e^{\alpha h(z-1)}e^{\beta h(z-1)}=e^{(\alpha+\beta)h(z-1)},
$$
so it is Poisson with mean $(\alpha+\beta)h$. Simultaneous arrivals have probability zero, so the sum retains unit jumps. It is therefore a <Poisson process> of rate $\alpha+\beta$.

The first arrival times are independent exponential variables of rates $\alpha$ and $\beta$. For positive rates,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(\text{first arrival red})=\int_0^\infty\alpha e^{-\alpha t}e^{-\beta t}\,dt=\frac\alpha{\alpha+\beta}}.
$$

Stationarity at zero gives $\pi_0=q\pi_0+q\pi_1$, hence $\pi_1=(p/q)\pi_0$. At one, $\pi_1=p\pi_0+q\pi_2$; substituting $p\pi_0=q\pi_1$ gives $q\pi_2=p\pi_1$. More generally the interior equations are $\pi_i=p\pi_{i-1}+q\pi_{i+1}$. Induction gives
$$
\pi_i=(p/q)^i\pi_0.

Since $p/q<1$, normalization of this <geometric series> gives
$$

$$
These probabilities satisfy <detailed balance> $\pi_ip=\pi_{i+1}q$, including the boundary balance with the holding probability. Thus they do define a <stationary distribution>. Irreducibility then gives its uniqueness.

Apart from its multinomial coefficient, the <likelihood> is $\prod_{ij}\theta_{ij}^{n_{ij}}$. Maximizing its logarithm subject to $\sum\theta_{ij}=1$ gives $n_{ij}/\theta_{ij}$ equal to a common multiplier, which is $n$. Hence $\widehat\theta_{ij}=n_{ij}/n$.

Under <independence> the log <likelihood> separates as $\sum_i n_{i\cdot}\log\phi_i+\sum_jn_{\cdot j}\log\rho_j$. The two normalization constraints give $\widehat\phi_i=n_{i\cdot}/n$ and $\widehat\rho_j=n_{\cdot j}/n$, so the fitted probabilities are $n_{i\cdot}n_{\cdot j}/n^2$. Therefore twice the difference of the maximized log likelihoods is
$$
\boxed{\Lambda=2\sum_{i,j}n_{ij}\log\frac{n_{ij}n}{n_{i\cdot}n_{\cdot j}}}.
$$
The unrestricted model has $rc-1$ free parameters and the <independence> model has $(r-1)+(c-1)$, leaving $(r-1)(c-1)=4$ <degrees of freedom> for the table. Under the null, the <likelihood>-ratio statistic is asymptotically chi-squared with four <degrees of freedom>. Since $25.192>18.467$, **reject <independence> at the 0.1% significance level**. This is evidence of association in the sampled variables; it does not by itself identify a causal direction.

Let $C$ be the clump size and put $q=e^{-\lambda t}$. The probability that all its members die, conditional on $C=n$, is $(1-q)^n$. Summing the <geometric series> gives
$$
\mathbb P(\text{clump dead})=\sum_{n\ge1}(1-\theta)\theta^{n-1}(1-q)^n
=\frac{(1-\theta)(1-q)}{1-\theta(1-q)}.
$$
Subtracting from one yields $\boxed{p(t,\lambda)=q/(1-\theta+\theta q)}$, for $0\le\theta<1$.

Assuming independent clumps and independent repeats, the combined <likelihood> is proportional to $p^R(1-p)^{N-R}$. For $0<R<N$ its unique maximizer is $\widehat p=R/N$. The relation between $p$ and $q$ is one-to-one, with $q=(1-\theta)p/(1-\theta p)$. For $t>0$ this gives
$$
\boxed{\widehat\lambda=\frac1t\log\frac{N-\theta R}{R(1-\theta)}}.
$$
If $R=N$, the estimate is the boundary value zero; if strictly positive rates are required, it is only a limiting supremum. If $R=0$, the <likelihood> approaches its supremum as $\lambda\to\infty$ and there is no finite estimate. These boundary cases explain when the printed logarithmic expression is finite.

Gershgorin's theorem states that every complex <eigenvalue> of a square matrix lies in at least one row disc
$$
D_i=\{z\in\mathbb C:|z-a_{ii}|\le R_i\},\qquad R_i=\sum_{j\ne i}|a_{ij}|.
$$
Let $Av=\lambda v$ with $v\ne0$, and choose an index $i$ where $|v_i|$ is maximal. Its row equation gives
$$
|\lambda-a_{ii}|\,|v_i|=\left|\sum_{j\ne i}a_{ij}v_j\right|\le\sum_{j\ne i}|a_{ij}|\,|v_i|.
$$
Dividing by the positive $|v_i|$ proves $\lambda\in D_i$. This proof applies to complex eigenvectors even when the matrix is real.

Gershgorin's second theorem states that a union of $k$ row discs separated from the union of all remaining discs contains exactly $k$ eigenvalues, counted with <algebraic multiplicity>.
