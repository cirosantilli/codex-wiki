= Solution
{parent=2009/A/A2}

The <characteristic of a field> is the least positive <integer> $r$ with $r1_K=0$, or zero if none exists. In a <finite field> a positive such <integer> exists because the sequence $0,1_K,2\cdot1_K,\ldots$ must repeat.

The <prime field>[prime subfield] is the <intersection> of all subfields of $K$, equivalently the <subfield> generated by $1_K$. In characteristic $p$ it is the copy of $\mathbb F_p$ formed by the multiples of $1_K$. Every <field automorphism> preserves $1_K$ and addition, so it fixes every element of this <subfield>.

Finally, once the order-$n$ <Frobenius automorphism> below is established, all its $n$ powers are distinct. Any automorphism fixes $F$ and is determined by its value at the generator $\lambda$. That value must be a root of the degree-$n$ <minimal polynomial> of $\lambda$, so there are at most $n$ automorphisms. Hence
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Aut}(K)=\langle\vartheta\rangle\cong C_n}.
$$

If the positive <characteristic of a field>[characteristic] were composite, say $r=uv$ with $1<u,v<r$, then $(u1_K)(v1_K)=0$ while neither factor is zero, by minimality of $r$. This contradicts a <field> having no <zero divisors>. Thus the characteristic is a **prime** $p$.

Regard $K$ as a <vector space> over its <prime field>[prime subfield] $\mathbb F_p$. Since $K$ is finite, a maximal <linearly independent> list terminates and is a finite <basis>, of some dimension $n\geq1$. Each <field> element has a unique coordinate vector in $\mathbb F_p^n$, so
$$
\boxed{|K|=p^n}.
$$

For a <simple field extension>, the powers $1,\lambda,\ldots,\lambda^{d-1}$ form a <basis> when the <minimal polynomial> has degree $d$: reduction modulo that <polynomial> spans the extension, and a relation of smaller degree would contradict minimality. Hence $[F(\lambda):F]=d$. The <field> is $K$, whose dimension is $n$, giving **$d=n$**.

= Solution
{parent=2009/A/A2/ii}

Define $\vartheta(x)=x^p$ for every $x\in K$. In characteristic $p$, all intermediate binomial coefficients vanish, so $\vartheta(x+y)=\vartheta(x)+\vartheta(y)$; multiplicativity and preservation of one are immediate. If $x^p=y^p$, then $(x-y)^p=0$ and the <field> property gives $x=y$. Thus this <field homomorphism> is injective, and finiteness makes it surjective. It is therefore a <field automorphism>, sending $\lambda$ to $\lambda^p$ as required.

Here the iteration index is a positive <integer>: $1\leq m<n$. If $\vartheta^m(\lambda)=\lambda$, then $\vartheta^m$ fixes $F$ and the generator, so it fixes all of $K$. Every element of $K$ would then solve $T^{p^m}-T=0$. A nonzero <polynomial> of degree $p^m$ over a <field> has at most $p^m$ roots, contradicting $|K|=p^n>p^m$. Thus **$\vartheta^m(\lambda)\ne\lambda$**. The printed inequality alone omits positivity of $m$; at $m=0$ the claimed inequality would be false.

The <multiplicative group of a field>[multiplicative group] $K^\times$ has order $p^n-1$, so <Lagrange's theorem for finite groups> gives $x^{p^n-1}=1$ for $x\ne0$. Including zero, $x^{p^n}=x$ for every $x\in K$. Therefore $\vartheta^n$ is the identity. No positive smaller power is the identity by part (iii), so
$$
\boxed{|\vartheta|=n}.
$$
For $n=1$ this says that Frobenius itself is the identity, as it should be.

A <group action> assigns to each $g\in G$ and $x\in\Omega$ a point $g\cdot x$ so that $1\cdot x=x$ and $(gh)\cdot x=g\cdot(h\cdot x)$. The <orbit> of $x$ is $Gx=\{g\cdot x:g\in G\}$. The action is <transitive> if there is exactly one <orbit>, equivalently any point can be carried to any other by some group element.

= ii
{parent=2009/B/B2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2009/B/B2/ii}

Let $G$ act by left multiplication on its five left cosets of $H$. This gives a <group homomorphism> $\varphi:G\to S_5$. Its <kernel> is the <normal core of a subgroup>[core of a subgroup], $\bigcap_{g\in G}gHg^{-1}$: an element fixes every coset precisely when all its conjugates lie in $H$. It is a <normal subgroup> contained in $H$, so the hypothesis forces the <kernel> to be trivial. Thus $G$ embeds in $S_5$.

The image has order $60$ and index two, hence is the <kernel> of a surjection $S_5\to C_2$. All transpositions are conjugate and generate $S_5$, so under any such surjection they must all have the same nonidentity image. This determines the map uniquely as the sign <homomorphism>. Its <kernel> is $A_5$, proving $G\cong A_5$.

To prove simplicity directly, the nonidentity <conjugacy classes> in $A_5$ have sizes $15,20,12,12$. A double transposition has centralizer of order four in $A_5$; a three-cycle has centralizer of order three; a five-cycle has centralizer of order five, yielding the two classes of size twelve. The class sizes together with the identity account for all sixty elements. A <normal subgroup> must be a <union> of these classes containing the identity, and its order must divide sixty. None of the proper nontrivial sums of these sizes with one is a divisor of sixty. Therefore **$A_5$, and hence $G$, is simple**.

= Solution
{parent=2009/B/B2/iii}

Choose an inscribed regular <tetrahedron> from one of the regular compounds of five tetrahedra in the <dodecahedron>, and let $H$ be its rotational stabilizer. Its twelve tetrahedral rotations preserve the <dodecahedron>. Concretely, with $\phi=(1+\sqrt5)/2$, use <dodecahedron> vertices $(\pm1,\pm1,\pm1)$ together with cyclic coordinate placements of $(0,\pm\phi^{-1},\pm\phi)$. The <tetrahedron> has the four cube vertices whose coordinate product is $+1$. Cyclic coordinate permutations and even numbers of coordinate sign changes preserve both vertex sets, have determinant $+1$, and give twelve distinct rotations. No other rotation can preserve this <tetrahedron>, because a regular <tetrahedron> has only twelve orientation-preserving symmetries.

Their action on its four vertices is faithful and consists exactly of the even permutations: the eight rotations through $\pm120^\circ$ induce three-cycles, the three half-turns induce double transpositions, and there is the identity. Thus **$H\cong A_4$**, independently of part (ii).

Finally a nonidentity element of $H$ has order two or three. In the <dodecahedron>'s full rotation group, all fifteen half-turns about opposite-edge axes form one <conjugacy class>, and all twenty third-turns about opposite-vertex axes form one <conjugacy class>. These geometric conjugacies follow by carrying the corresponding oriented axes to one another by rotations. A <normal subgroup> containing such an element must contain its whole class, which already has more than twelve elements. Hence no nonidentity <normal subgroup> of $G$ is contained in $H$, verifying the required extra property.

For an odd <prime number> $p$, the <Legendre symbol> $(a/p)$ is zero if $p\mid a$, is $+1$ if $a$ is a nonzero square modulo $p$, and is $-1$ otherwise.

= Solution
{parent=2009/C/C2/ii}

<Euler criterion> states that
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac ap\right)\equiv a^{(p-1)/2}\pmod p}.
$$
When $p\nmid a$, the power is congruent to $+1$ or $-1$, according as $a$ is or is not a <quadratic residue>.

= Solution
{parent=2009/C/C2/iii}

<Gauss lemma (number theory)>[Gauss lemma for quadratic residues] states: for $p\nmid a$, take the least positive residues of $a,2a,\ldots,((p-1)/2)a$ modulo $p$, and let $s$ be the number greater than $p/2$. Then
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac ap\right)=(-1)^s}.
$$

= iv
{parent=2009/C/C2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2009/C/C2/iv}

For $a=2$, the least positive residues in <Gauss lemma (number theory)>[Gauss lemma for quadratic residues] are simply $2,4,\ldots,p-1$. The number exceeding $p/2$ is
$$
s=\frac{p-1}2-\left\lfloor\frac p4\right\rfloor.
$$
For $p\equiv1,3,5,7\pmod8$, respectively, this count is even, odd, odd, even. Substituting these parities into the lemma gives
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac2p\right)=\begin{cases}1,&p\equiv1,7\pmod8,\\-1,&p\equiv3,5\pmod8.\end{cases}}
$$

{parent=2009/C/C2/v}

By multiplicativity of the <Legendre symbol> and <Euler criterion>,
$$
\left(\frac{-2}p\right)=\left(\frac{-1}p\right)\left(\frac2p\right)
=(-1)^{(p-1)/2}\left(\frac2p\right).
$$
Combining this with part (iv) yields
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{-2}p\right)=\begin{cases}1,&p\equiv1,3\pmod8,\\-1,&p\equiv5,7\pmod8.\end{cases}}
$$

= Solution
{parent=2009/C/C2/vi}

Suppose all primes congruent to $3$ modulo eight are $p_1,\ldots,p_r$, and put $N=p_1\cdots p_r$, an odd <integer>. The <integer> $N^2+2$ is odd and congruent to three modulo eight. If a prime $q$ divides it, then $N^2\equiv-2\pmod q$ and $q\nmid N$, since a common divisor of $N$ and $N^2+2$ would divide two. Thus $(-2/q)=1$, so part (v) forces $q\equiv1$ or $3\pmod8$.

If all prime factors congruent to three had even multiplicity, their product, including the factors congruent to one, would be one modulo eight. Since $N^2+2$ is three modulo eight, at least one prime factor is congruent to three and occurs to odd multiplicity. But it is none of the $p_i$, since those divide $N$. This contradiction proves **infinitely many primes are congruent to three modulo eight**.

On a product of sigma-finite <measure spaces>, <Tonelli's theorem> says that a nonnegative measurable <function> has equal product and iterated integrals, allowing $+\infty$.

<Fubini's theorem> says that if a measurable <function> is absolutely integrable on the product, almost every section is integrable, the integrals of sections are integrable, and both iterated integrals equal the product integral. In particular, to establish absolute integrability one can first apply <Tonelli's theorem> to the absolute value and show its iterated integral is finite.

{parent=2009/D/D4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2009/D/D4/ii}

Both terms of each one-dimensional section are integrable by a positive scaling substitution, since $f\in L^1(0,\infty)$. For fixed $y>0$,
$$
\int_0^1g(x,y)\,dx
=\frac1y\left(\int_0^yf(t)\,dt-\int_0^{ay}f(t)\,dt\right)
=-\frac1y\int_y^{ay}f(t)\,dt<0.
$$
For fixed $x>0$,
$$
\int_1^\infty g(x,y)\,dy
=\frac1x\left(\int_x^\infty f(t)\,dt-\int_{ax}^\infty f(t)\,dt\right)
=\frac1x\int_x^{ax}f(t)\,dt>0.
$$
The strict inequalities hold because $a>1$ and the measurable integrand is strictly positive on each nonempty interval. If $g$ were integrable on the stated product domain, <Fubini's theorem> would make both iterated integrals finite and equal. But the first order of integration would give a strictly negative value, and the other a strictly positive value. This contradiction proves **$g$ is not integrable on that product**.

A subset is <connected> when its <subspace topology> admits no separation into two disjoint nonempty <open sets>. Equivalently, its only subsets that are both open and closed in that subspace are the empty set and the entire subset.

The difference map $(z,w)\mapsto z-w$ is <continuous>, and $X$ is its <preimage> of the <open set> $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$. Thus $X$ is open in the product.

The class of $(z,w)$ is $C_z=\{z\}\times(\mathbb C\setminus\{z\})$. A punctured plane is <path-connected>: two points can be joined by a polygonal path avoiding the deleted point. Consequently every class is <connected>.

The projection $\pi$ is onto, since $(z,z+1)\in X$ for every $z$. It is <continuous> as a restricted coordinate projection. If $U$ is open in $X$, it is open in the product because $X$ itself is open. For a point $(z,w)\in U$, the product-<topology> <basis> gives open sets $V,W$ with $(z,w)\in V\times W\subseteq U$. A competitor can tie if the first row of $D^*$ is zero. This is the <Gauss-Markov theorem>; it does not assert optimality against all biased or nonlinear estimators.

{parent=2009/O}

= Solution
{parent=2009/O/O5}

For unrestricted category probabilities, the <log-likelihood> is $\sum_i n_i\log\pi_i$ subject to $\sum_i\pi_i=1$. A multiplier gives $n_i/\pi_i$ constant, hence the unique maximum, because all counts are positive and the <log-likelihood> is strictly concave:
$$
\boxed{\widehat\pi_i=n_i/n}.
$$
The <likelihood-ratio test>[likelihood ratio test] compares this saturated maximum with the maximum under the parametric model. Twice the log ratio is
$$
2\sum_i n_i\log\frac{n_i/n}{\pi_i(\widehat\theta)}=\Lambda(\widehat\theta).
$$
For $k$ bins and one regular fitted parameter, its usual large-sample reference distribution is $\chi^2_{k-2}$; reject for a sufficiently large value. Under a fully specified parameter $\theta_0$, use $\Lambda(\theta_0)$ and $\chi^2_{k-1}$. This relies on an identifiable interior fit and adequate expected bin counts.

For individual ungrouped abundances, let $S$ be the number of species and $T$ the total number of individuals. The logarithmic-series model gives
$$
\ell(\theta)=-S\log[-\log(1-\theta)]+T\log\theta+C,\qquad0<\theta<1.
$$
Its score vanishes when
$$
\boxed{\frac{-\widehat\theta}{(1-\widehat\theta)\log(1-\widehat\theta)}=\frac TS}.
$$
The left side increases continuously from one to infinity. One way to see strict increase is to set $t=-\log(1-\theta)>0$, making it $(e^t-1)/t$, whose derivative is positive because $e^t(t-1)+1$ increases from zero. The score has the sign of $T/S$ minus this increasing <function>, so this is the unique maximum. Bisection on $(0,1)$ is a robust numerical method; Newton's method is another option.

There are two fits implicit in the supplied presentation. Using the ungrouped sufficient statistics gives $\widehat\theta_{\rm full}\approx0.944228$. By contrast the displayed ten-bin deviance is minimized near **$0.93$**, so that is the table-based estimate $\widehat\theta_{\rm grouped}$. Grouping the upper tail discards its detailed abundance total; minimizing the grouped deviance maximizes the grouped likelihood, not the ungrouped likelihood used in the preceding score equation. The estimates therefore need not agree. The following table-based decisions use its grouped-fit convention.

= Solution

Interpolation means $p_n(x_i)=f(x_i)$ for every node. Define the <Lagrange basis polynomials>
$$
\ell_i(x)=\prod_{j\ne i}\frac{x-x_j}{x_i-x_j}.
$$
Distinctness of the nodes makes every denominator nonzero, and $\ell_i(x_j)=\delta_{ij}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{p_n(x)=\sum_{i=0}^nf(x_i)\ell_i(x)}
$$
is a <polynomial> of degree at most $n$ with the required values. If two such polynomials existed, their difference would have $n+1$ distinct roots while having degree at most $n$; the <polynomial> root bound makes it identically zero. Hence existence and uniqueness hold. The actual degree can be smaller than $n$, despite the conventional name.

{parent=2009/P/P3}

= Solution
{parent=2009/P/P3/b}

Multiplication by the displayed <Vandermonde matrix> maps the coefficient vector of a <polynomial> of degree at most $n$ to its vector of values at the nodes. Part (a) proves that every vector of node values is attained uniquely, so this square linear map is bijective. Therefore **$A_n$ is invertible**. Equivalently, a <kernel> vector would define a <polynomial> of degree at most $n$ with $n+1$ distinct roots and would have to vanish.

= Solution
{parent=2009/P/P3/c}

If $x$ is a node the error and the product both vanish, and any interior $\xi$ works. Otherwise put $\omega(t)=\prod_{i=0}^n(t-x_i)$ and $C=(f(x)-p_n(x))/\omega(x)$. The <function>
$$
R(t)=f(t)-p_n(t)-C\omega(t)
$$
has $n+2$ distinct zeros, at the nodes and at $x$. Repeated <Rolle's theorem> gives a point $\xi$ between the extreme zeros, and therefore in $(a,b)$, where $R^{(n+1)}(\xi)=0$. Since $p_n^{(n+1)}=0$ and $\omega^{(n+1)}=(n+1)!$, one gets $C=f^{(n+1)}(\xi)/(n+1)!$. Substitution gives
$$
\boxed{f(x)-p_n(x)=\frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}\prod_{i=0}^n(x-x_i)}.
$$

= d
{parent=2009/P/P3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2009/P/P3/d}

Every derivative of order $n+1$ of $\sin(2x)$ has absolute value at most $2^{n+1}$. The equally spaced nodes are symmetric about $1/2$. Pair each node $a\leq1/2$ with $1-a$; for $x\in[0,1]$,
$$
|(x-a)(x-(1-a))|=|(x-1/2)^2-(a-1/2)^2|\leq\frac14.
$$
If an unpaired middle node remains, its factor satisfies $|x-1/2|\leq1/2$. Consequently the complete nodal product is at most $2^{-(n+1)}$. The error formula actually gives the stronger estimate
$$
\|f-p_n\|_\infty\leq\frac1{(n+1)!}\leq\boxed{\frac{2^{\lfloor(n+1)/2\rfloor}}{(n+1)!}}.
$$
The first bound tends to zero, proving **<uniform convergence>** on the whole interval. There is no appeal to a general claim that equispaced interpolation always converges; the derivative and symmetric-product bounds are particular to this <analytic function> and these nodes.

Let $P=vv^T/(v^Tv)$, which is a nonzero symmetric <orthogonal projection>: $P^T=P$ and $P^2=P$. Then $H_{v,\alpha}=I-\alpha P$ is symmetric and

\boxed{H_{v,\alpha}\text{ is orthogonal}\iff\alpha\in\{0,2\}}.

Let $r=\|x\|>0$. Choose $c=-r$ if $x_1\geq0$ and $c=r$ if $x_1<0$, and set $v=x-ce_1$. This opposite-sign choice ensures $v\ne0$. Because $c^2=r^2$,

\boxed{H_vx=x-2v\frac{v^Tx}{v^Tv}=x-v=ce_1}.

{parent=2009/P/P4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2009/P/P4/d}

Apply successive <Householder reflections> supported on smaller trailing coordinate sets. At step $k=1,\ldots,n-2$, let $x$ be the entries in rows $k+1,\ldots,n$ of column $k$ of the current <symmetric matrix>. If $x\ne0$, part (c) gives an orthogonal reflection $H$ taking it to $ce_1$; if $x=0$, use the identity. Embed this as $Q_k=\operatorname{diag}(I_k,H)$ and replace the current matrix by $Q_k^TAQ_k$.

The first $k$ columns are unchanged by right multiplication. Left multiplication turns the tail of column $k$ into a vector supported only in row $k+1$. Previously treated columns have zero entries throughout this trailing coordinate set, so their zeros are preserved. Symmetry makes the matching row zeros appear as well. After the final step all entries below the first subdiagonal, and by symmetry above the first superdiagonal, vanish.

The product $Q=Q_1\cdots Q_{n-2}$ is orthogonal, and
$$
\boxed{Q^TAQ\text{ is tridiagonal}}.
$$
Restricting each transformation to its trailing coordinates is what preserves earlier zero patterns.
