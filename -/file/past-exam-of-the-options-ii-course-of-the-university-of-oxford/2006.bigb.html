{c}

Right multiplication gives a right action $(Hx)\cdot g=Hxg$. To obtain a <homomorphism> into <permutations> with the usual function-composition convention, use the associated left action
$$
\rho(g)(Hx)=Hxg^{-1}.
$$
This is well-defined: replacing $x$ by $hx$, $h\in H$, does not change the image <coset>. Moreover $\rho(g)\rho(k)(Hx)=Hxk^{-1}g^{-1}=\rho(gk)(Hx)$, and each map is bijective with inverse $\rho(g^{-1})$.

An element $g$ is in the <kernel> precisely when $Hxg^{-1}=Hx$ for every $x\in G$. This says $xg^{-1}x^{-1}\in H$, equivalently $g\in x^{-1}Hx$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\ker\rho=\bigcap_{x\in G}x^{-1}Hx.}
$$
This is the largest <normal subgroup> of $G$ contained in $H$.

Now the index of $H$ is $q$, so $G/\ker\rho$ embeds into $S_q$. Its order therefore divides $q!$. Since $p>q$ is <prime>, $p$ does not divide $q!$. From $|G|=pq$ it follows that $p$ divides $|\ker\rho|$. But $\ker\rho\subseteq H$ and $|H|=p$, forcing $\ker\rho=H$. Therefore **$H$ is normal**.

For the last case let $H=\langle b\rangle$. Normality makes <conjugation> by $a$ an <automorphism>, so $a^{-1}ba=b^r$ with $r\in\{1,2,3,4\}$. Applying it twice and using $a^2=1$ gives $b=b^{r^2}$, so $r^2\equiv1\pmod5$. Thus $(r-1)(r+1)\equiv0\pmod5$, and
$$
\boxed{a^{-1}ba=b\ \text{or}\ b^{-1}.}
$$
Both possibilities are allowed; the order assumptions alone do not force the inverse case.

An <irreducible polynomial> has positive degree, so its <ideal> is proper: otherwise $1=fh$ would contradict degrees. Every nonzero class $[g]$ has a representative not divisible by $f$. Irreducibility gives $\gcd(g,f)=1$, and the <Euclidean algorithm> supplies <polynomials> $u,v$ with $ug+vf=1$. Passing to the <quotient> gives $[u][g]=1$. Thus the <quotient> is a nonzero <commutative ring> in which every nonzero element has an inverse, hence **a <field>**. Constants embed injectively because no nonzero constant lies in the positive-degree <ideal> $(f)$.

First address the general splitting-field request. A <splitting field> is an extension in which the <polynomial> factors into linear factors and which is generated over the base <field> by its roots. For a degree-$m$ <polynomial>, adjoin a root of an <irreducible> factor. The <quotient> construction in the preceding parts gives an extension of degree at most $m$. Remove the resulting linear factor and repeat, now with a <polynomial> of degree $m-1$ over that extension. Induction and the <tower law> give
$$
[L:F]\leq m(m-1)\cdots1=m!.
$$
The resulting <field> is generated by the roots and is a <splitting field>. Repeated roots or inseparability can only reduce the required degrees; separability was not used.

For the specified cubic, put $a=\sqrt[3]2$ and $\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}$. Its <splitting field> is $\mathbb Q(a,\omega)$. <Eisenstein criterion> at two gives $[\mathbb Q(a):\mathbb Q]=3$. This cubic <field> is real and therefore does not contain the nonreal $\omega$, whose quadratic <polynomial> is $x^2+x+1$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{[\mathbb Q(a,\omega):\mathbb Q]=3\cdot2=6.}
$$

A <primitive root> modulo $p$ is an <integer> whose residue generates the multiplicative group $\mathbb F_p^\times$, equivalently has order $p-1$.

The <Chinese remainder theorem> states that, for pairwise coprime positive <integers> $m_1,\ldots,m_r$, the system $x\equiv a_i\pmod{m_i}$ has a unique solution modulo $M=\prod_i m_i$. For a constructive proof put $M_i=M/m_i$. Bézout's identity gives an <integer> $u_i$ with $M_i u_i\equiv1\pmod{m_i}$. Then $x=\sum_i a_iM_i u_i$ has all the required residues. If two solutions exist, their difference is divisible by every $m_i$; pairwise <coprimality> makes it divisible by their product, proving uniqueness modulo $M$.

Here $3,5,7$ divide $p-1$, and none is zero modulo $p$. Choose a <primitive root> $g$ and exponents $a,b,c$ with
$$
3\equiv g^a,\qquad5\equiv g^b,\qquad7\equiv g^c\pmod p.
$$
Use the theorem with moduli $3,5,7$ to choose $r$ such that $r\equiv a\pmod3$, $r\equiv b\pmod5$, $r\equiv c\pmod7$. Define nonzero residues
$$
n=g^r,\qquad x=g^{(r-a)/3},\qquad
y=g^{(r-b)/5},\qquad z=g^{(r-c)/7}.
$$
The exponents are <integers>; negative ones are interpreted using inverses in $\mathbb F_p^\times$. Taking <integer> representatives now gives
$$
\boxed{p\nmid n,\qquad n\equiv3x^3\equiv5y^5\equiv7z^7\pmod p.}
$$
The theorem aligns the exponent <modular congruences>, which is why a <primitive root> converts the three power conditions into a linear <modular congruence> problem.

The <monotone convergence theorem> states that if $0\leq h_n\uparrow h$ pointwise almost everywhere and the functions are <measurable>, then $\int h_n\uparrow\int h$, allowing the value infinity.

Interpret the displayed densities as zero outside $(0,1)$, so no undefined logarithm is evaluated there. Each $f_k$ is nonnegative and <measurable>. With $x=e^{-t}$,
$$
\int_0^1-x^{2k}\log x\,dx
=\int_0^\infty t e^{-(2k+1)t}dt
=\boxed{\frac1{(2k+1)^2}},
$$
where the last equality is <integration by parts>. This is finite, proving <Lebesgue integrability>.

The nonnegative partial sums satisfy, for $0<x<1$,
$$
\sum_{k=0}^Nf_k(x)=-\log x\sum_{k=0}^Nx^{2k}
\ \uparrow\ -\frac{\log x}{1-x^2}=g(x).
$$
They vanish outside the interval as well. Monotone convergence therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\int_{\mathbb R}g(x)dx=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac1{(2k+1)^2}.}
$$
The series is finite by comparison with the convergent series $\sum_{j\geq1}j^{-2}$, whose convergence follows from the <integral test>. Since $g\geq0$, this finite integral proves absolute <Lebesgue integrability> as required.

We first prove the differentiation theorem. Fix $t\in(0,b)$ and take nonzero $h\to0$ with $t+h\in(0,b)$. The <measurable> difference quotients
$$
q_h(x)=\frac{f(x,t+h)-f(x,t)}h
$$
converge pointwise to $f_t(x,t)$. The <mean value theorem> and the assumed derivative bound give $|q_h(x)|\leq g(x)$. In particular $f_t(\cdot,t)$ is <measurable> as a limit of quotients along a sequence, and its bound by $g$ proves integrability. The <dominated convergence theorem>, in its parameter-limit form, says that pointwise convergence with a common integrable bound permits convergence of the integrals. Applying it yields
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dt}\int_0^\infty f(x,t)dx
=\lim_{h\to0}\int_0^\infty q_h(x)dx
=\int_0^\infty f_t(x,t)dx.}
$$
The parameter-limit statement follows from the ordinary sequential theorem by applying it to every sequence of nonzero $h$ tending to zero.

For the specific function, $|f(x,t)|\leq e^{-x^2}$, which is integrable by the allowed fact. It is differentiable in $t$, with
$$
f_t(x,t)=-2xe^{-x^2}\sin(2xt),\qquad
|f_t(x,t)|\leq2xe^{-x^2}.
$$
The last majorant has integral one on $[0,\infty)$, using the substitution $u=x^2$. All three hypotheses therefore hold. The indicator of the parameter domain is one at every point under consideration and does not affect this derivative.

A subset is <compact> if every cover of it by open sets of the ambient space has a <finite subcover>; equivalently use open sets of its <subspace topology>. A cover of $A\cup B$ separately covers $A$ and $B$. <Compactness> of each supplies two finite collections, whose union is finite and covers $A\cup B$. If $A$ is closed in a <compact> space, add the open complement of $A$ to any <open cover> of $A$. <Compactness> supplies a <finite subcover> of the whole space, and discarding the complement leaves a finite cover of $A$.

For the enlarged space, every old open set is open in $C$ and has the same trace on $X$. The trace on $X$ of a new open set $V\cup\{\infty\}$ is $V$, which is open in $X$ because its complement is closed. Thus the induced topology on $X$ is exactly the original one.

Given an <open cover> of $C$, choose a member containing infinity. It has the form $V\cup\{\infty\}$ with $K=X\setminus V$ <compact>. The remaining cover restricted to $K$ has a <finite subcover>. Together with the chosen infinity-neighborhood, these finitely many sets cover $C$. Hence **$C$ is <compact>**, with no <Hausdorff> assumption needed.

Finally put $a=f(\infty)$. <Continuity> makes $U=f^{-1}((a-\epsilon,a+\epsilon))$ open and containing infinity. Therefore $U=V\cup\{\infty\}$ with $W=X\setminus V$ <compact> and closed in $X$. For every $x\in X\setminus W=V$ we have
$$
\boxed{|f(x)-a|<\epsilon.}
$$
This expresses convergence to the value at infinity outside <compact> closed subsets.

A <quotient map> $\pi:X\to Y$ is a <surjection> such that $U\subseteq Y$ is open if and only if $\pi^{-1}(U)$ is open in $X$. Thus the topology on $Y$ is exactly the final topology induced by $\pi$.

For an open continuous <surjection>, <continuity> gives one implication. For the other, if $\pi^{-1}(U)$ is open then $U=\pi(\pi^{-1}(U))$ is open by openness and <surjectivity>. Hence it is a <quotient map>.

If $\pi:X\to Y$ and $\sigma:Y\to Z$ are <quotient maps>, they are <surjective> and
$$
(\sigma\circ\pi)^{-1}(U)=\pi^{-1}(\sigma^{-1}(U)).
$$
This set is open exactly when $\sigma^{-1}(U)$ is open, exactly when $U$ is open. Thus **the composition is a <quotient map>**.

For the factorization, <continuity> of $g$ implies <continuity> of $f=g\circ\pi$. Conversely, if $f$ is continuous and $U$ is open in $Z$, then $\pi^{-1}(g^{-1}(U))=f^{-1}(U)$ is open. The <quotient> property makes $g^{-1}(U)$ open. This holds for every open $U$, so **$g$ is continuous**.

The two singly marked sides of the first triangle are glued with their endpoints at $A$ matched. A cross-section parallel to the remaining side has its two endpoints identified, becoming a <circle>; the apex becomes its <cone> point. Thus this <quotient> triangle is a **<disc>**, whose boundary is the doubly marked edge.

In the second triangle, the two triply marked sides run consecutively along the boundary from $E$ through $D$ to $F$. Pairing these sides in the indicated directions introduces one reversal; its boundary word is $aa b^{-1}$, where $b$ is the remaining doubly marked edge. This is a **<Möbius band>** with boundary $b$. One can obtain the usual rectangle model by cutting from the identified triple-edge seam to the midpoint of $b$ and opening the triangle.

$$
\boxed{\mathbb{RP}^2,\text{ the real projective plane}.}
$$
This description also verifies that the <quotient> is a <closed surface>, including the vertices. As a check, the vertex classes are $\{A\}$ and $\{B,C,D,E,F\}$; there are three edge classes and two faces. The <Euler characteristic> is $2-3+2=1$, consistent with the <projective plane>. The <Fréchet derivative> at $a$ is the <linear map> $Df_a:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^k$ satisfying
$$
f(a+h)=f(a)+Df_a(h)+r(h),\qquad
\frac{\|r(h)\|}{\|h\|}\longrightarrow0.
$$
It is unique. When both derivatives exist, the <chain rule> is $D(g\circ f)_a=Dg_{f(a)}\circ Df_a$.

For the square map,
$$
(A+\Delta)^2-A^2=A\Delta+\Delta A+\Delta^2.
$$
The remainder has norm at most $\|\Delta\|^2$ in a submultiplicative <matrix norm>, hence is $o(\|\Delta\|)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{DS_A(\Delta)=A\Delta+\Delta A.}
$$
<Matrix> multiplication is not commutative, so the two terms cannot generally be combined as $2A\Delta$.

For small $\|\Delta\|$, the <matrix> $A+\Delta=A(I+A^{-1}\Delta)$ remains invertible and its inverse is locally bounded, for example by the convergent <Neumann series> when $\|A^{-1}\Delta\|<1$. Applying the <resolvent identity> twice gives
$$
(A+\Delta)^{-1}
=A^{-1}-A^{-1}\Delta A^{-1}
+A^{-1}\Delta A^{-1}\Delta(A+\Delta)^{-1}.
$$
The last term is $O(\|\Delta\|^2)$ by that local bound. Hence the inverse map is differentiable, with
$$
\boxed{D\mathcal I_A(\Delta)=-A^{-1}\Delta A^{-1}.}
$$
This verifies the differentiability and its remainder, rather than only formally differentiating the identity $AA^{-1}=I$.

Write $K[y]=\int_a^bG(x,y,y')dx$. At a regular constrained extremal, the <first variation> of $K$ is not identically zero. Choose a variation $\zeta$ for which $\delta K[\zeta]\neq0$; the <implicit function theorem> supplies a correction in the $\zeta$ direction to each other variation so that $K$ remains one. Stationarity of $J$ on these corrected variations implies $\delta J=\lambda\delta K$ for one scalar $\lambda$.

Put $L=F-\lambda G$. <Integration by parts> gives, for an arbitrary twice continuously differentiable variation $\eta$,
$$
0=\delta(J-\lambda K)[\eta]
=\int_a^b\left(L_y-\frac d{dx}L_{y'}\right)\eta\,dx
+[L_{y'}\eta]_a^b.
$$
Taking variations with zero endpoint values and using the stated <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations> gives the <Euler-Lagrange equation>. The remaining endpoint values are free and independent, giving the natural boundary conditions:
$$
\boxed{F_y-\lambda G_y-\frac d{dx}(F_{y'}-\lambda G_{y'})=0,\qquad
(F_{y'}-\lambda G_{y'})(a)=(F_{y'}-\lambda G_{y'})(b)=0.}
$$
If the constraint is not regular, the general multiplier statement permits an abnormal pair $\lambda_0F-\lambda_1G$ with $\lambda_0=0$; the source's general normal-form derivation implicitly excludes that case. In its quadratic example, regularity follows automatically from normalization: $\delta K[y]=2\int wy^2=2$.

For that example $L=py'^2-qy^2-\lambda wy^2$, so the equation and endpoint conditions reduce to
$$
(py')'+qy+\lambda wy=0,\qquad py'(a)=py'(b)=0.
$$
Multiply the equation by $y$ and integrate. The boundary term $[py'y]_a^b$ vanishes, giving
$$
-\int_a^bpy'^2dx+\int_a^bqy^2dx+\lambda\int_a^bwy^2dx=0.
$$
The last integral equals one, so **$\lambda=J[y]$**, the <functional> value at the constrained extremal.

Finally multiply the equation for $y_1$ by $y_2$ and the equation for $y_2$ by $y_1$, then subtract and integrate. The derivative terms give only the vanishing boundary term $[p(y_2y_1'-y_1y_2')]_a^b$, while the $q$ terms cancel. Therefore $(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)\int_a^bwy_1y_2dx=0$. For distinct multipliers this proves
$$
\boxed{\int_a^bwy_1y_2dx=0.}
$$

Use coordinates with the downward vertical as reference. The first rod's center is at $(a/2)(\sin\theta,-\cos\theta)$, so its translational plus rotational <kinetic energy> is $ma^2\dot\theta^2/6$. The second center is
$$
r_2=a(\sin\theta,-\cos\theta)+\frac a2(\sin\phi,-\cos\phi).
$$
Its squared speed is $a^2\dot\theta^2+a^2\dot\phi^2/4+a^2\dot\theta\dot\phi\cos(\theta-\phi)$. Adding its center-of-mass rotation, using <moment of inertia> $ma^2/12$, gives the total
$$
T=\frac{ma^2}{6}[4\dot\theta^2+\dot\phi^2+3\dot\theta\dot\phi\cos(\theta-\phi)].
$$
The two center heights give $V=-mga(3\cos\theta+\cos\phi)/2$. Hence $L=T-V$ is exactly the stated <Lagrangian>.

Small stable oscillations are about the downward <equilibrium> $\theta=\phi=0$. Retaining quadratic terms gives $T_2=\tfrac12\dot q^TM\dot q$ and $V_2=\tfrac12q^TKq$, where
$$
q=\begin{pmatrix}\theta\\\phi\end{pmatrix},\quad
M=ma^2\begin{pmatrix}4/3&1/2\\1/2&1/3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
K=\frac{mga}{2}\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
A <normal mode> $q=v e^{i\omega t}$ satisfies $(K-\omega^2M)v=0$. With $\kappa=a\omega^2/g$, the <determinant> equation is
$$
(9-8\kappa)(3-2\kappa)-9\kappa^2
=27-42\kappa+7\kappa^2=0.
$$
Thus the angular frequencies are
$$
\boxed{\omega_\pm=\sqrt{\frac ga\left(3\pm\frac6{\sqrt7}\right)}.}
$$
Both squared frequencies are positive. The <amplitude> ratio is $\phi/\theta=(9-8\kappa)/(3\kappa)$: it is positive in the lower-frequency mode and negative in the higher-frequency mode. The rods therefore swing in phase in the first mode and in opposite phase in the second.

In SI units the <Maxwell's equations> in vacuum, with sources, are
$$
\nabla\cdot E=\rho/\epsilon_0,\quad \nabla\cdot B=0,\quad
\nabla\times E=-\partial_tB,\quad
\nabla\times B=\mu_0j+\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_tE.
$$
Taking the divergence of the last equation and using <Gauss's law> gives $\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot j=0$, the local <conservation of electric charge>. Thus sources that violate <charge conservation> cannot satisfy all the <Maxwell's equations>. Equivalently, with this conservation law the curl evolution preserves the electric divergence constraint if it holds initially; <Faraday's law> similarly preserves $\nabla\cdot B=0$.

With $\rho=j=0$, use $\nabla\times(\nabla\times E)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot E)-\Delta E$ and substitute the two curl equations. This gives $-\Delta E=-\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t^2E$. Applying the same calculation to $B$ yields
$$
\boxed{\Delta E-c^{-2}E_{tt}=0,\qquad
\Delta B-c^{-2}B_{tt}=0,\quad c=(\mu_0\epsilon_0)^{-1/2}.}
$$
A monochromatic <plane wave> has one <angular frequency> $\omega$ and constant-phase planes $\mathbf K\cdot r=\omega t-\text{constant}$, normal to the <wavevector> $\mathbf K$. Its <wavelength> is $2\pi/|\mathbf K|$.

For a nontrivial traveling wave with $\omega\neq0$, substitution of the displayed <electric field> into the divergence equation gives $\mathbf K\cdot e_1=\mathbf K\cdot e_2=0$. <Faraday's law> is satisfied by
$$
\boxed{B=\frac1\omega\,\mathbf K\times E,\qquad
\omega^2=c^2|\mathbf K|^2,\qquad \mathbf K\cdot e_1=\mathbf K\cdot e_2=0.}
$$
Indeed $\partial_tB=\mathbf K\times E_\phi$ and $\nabla\times E=-\mathbf K\times E_\phi$. The magnetic divergence is zero, while <Ampère's law> reduces to $|\mathbf K|^2E_\phi/\omega=(\omega/c^2)E_\phi$, giving the <dispersion relation>. The amplitudes may be arbitrary transverse vectors; they need not be mutually orthogonal or have equal lengths. Their combination describes the <polarization>.

\boxed{W(z)=\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi i}
\log\frac{(z-z_1)(z+z_1)}{(z-\bar z_1)(z+\bar z_1)}.}
$$
Additive constants and consistent logarithm branches do not affect velocity. The <quotient> has unit modulus on either wall, so the <streamfunction> is constant there and there is no normal flow.

The <vortex> moves with the regular fluid velocity after removing its own singular <field>. Since $W'=u-iv$, evaluating the three image contributions at $z_1$ gives exactly
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\bar z_1}{dt}=-\frac{i\Gamma}{2\pi}
\left(\frac1{2z_1}-\frac1{z_1-\bar z_1}-\frac1{z_1+\bar z_1}\right).}
$$
The overall minus sign and the two reflected-term minus signs here agree with the source scan.

Write $z_1=x+iy$, with $x,y>0$. Taking real and imaginary parts yields
$$
\dot x=\frac{\Gamma x^2}{4\pi y(x^2+y^2)},\qquad
\dot y=-\frac{\Gamma y^2}{4\pi x(x^2+y^2)}.
$$
Dividing gives $dy/dx=-y^3/x^3$, which integrates to
$$
\boxed{\frac1{x^2}+\frac1{y^2}=\frac1{a^2}+\frac1{b^2}.}
$$
Put $\ell=ab/\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$. The first-quadrant branch has vertical and horizontal asymptotes $x=\ell$ and $y=\ell$. Its motion is to the right and downward: from large $y$ near the vertical asymptote toward large $x$ near the horizontal one. Both coordinates stay strictly positive, so no wall is crossed.

The exact free-surface kinematic condition is $\eta_t+\phi_x\eta_x=\phi_y$ evaluated at $y=\eta$. Linearizing about a flat <surface> at rest drops products of small quantities and evaluates at $y=0$, giving $\phi_y=\eta_t$. <Bernoulli's equation> with constant atmospheric pressure gives $\phi_t+g\eta=0$ to the same order. The rigid bed has no normal flow, hence $\phi_y=0$ at $y=-h$.

In the fluid, <incompressibility> and the velocity-potential representation give $\Delta\phi=0$. For $k>0$, take
$$
\phi(x,y,t)=\frac{Ag}{\omega}
\frac{\cosh(k(y+h))}{\cosh(kh)}\sin(kx-\omega t).
$$
It is harmonic, satisfies the bed condition and the linear dynamic condition. At the <surface> its vertical derivative is $Agk\tanh(kh)\sin(kx-\omega t)/\omega$, while $\eta_t=A\omega\sin(kx-\omega t)$. Therefore the kinematic condition is equivalent to
$$
\boxed{\omega^2=gk\tanh(kh),\qquad
c=\frac\omega k=\sqrt{\frac gk\tanh(kh)}.}
$$
Finally $\tanh q=q-q^3/3+O(q^5)$. Taking the square root gives
$$
\boxed{c=\sqrt{gh}\left(1-\frac16k^2h^2+O((kh)^4)\right).}
$$
The first term is nondispersive shallow-water speed; the next term is the leading finite-depth dispersion correction.

For a <joint probability density function>, <independence> is equivalent to $f(x,y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y)$ almost everywhere. The displayed density is valid for $|\rho|<1$. Complete the square:
$$
\frac{x^2-2\rho xy+y^2}{1-\rho^2}
=x^2+\frac{(y-\rho x)^2}{1-\rho^2}.
$$
Integrating the second normal factor over $y$ gives
$$
\boxed{f_X(x)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-x^2/2}.}
$$
Dividing the <joint probability density function> by this marginal yields the conditional law
$$
\boxed{Y\mid X=x\sim N(\rho x,1-\rho^2).}
$$
For $\rho=0$ the <joint probability density function> factors and the variables are independent. If $\rho\neq0$, the <conditional mean> depends on $x$, so <independence> fails; equivalently their <covariance> is $\rho$.

Define $Z=(Y-\rho X)/\sqrt{1-\rho^2}$. In the change of variables $y=\rho x+\sqrt{1-\rho^2}z$, the Jacobian $\sqrt{1-\rho^2}$ cancels the remaining density factor, leaving the product of two <standard normal> densities. Thus $Z$ is <standard normal> and independent of $X$. One choice is
$$
\boxed{a=-\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{1-\rho^2}},\qquad
b=\frac1{\sqrt{1-\rho^2}}.}
$$
Changing both signs gives the other standardized choice. The density transformation proves <independence> directly rather than inferring it from zero <covariance> without a joint-normal justification.

The <state space> is $\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$. Write $q=1-p$. The nonzero transition probabilities are
$$
P(0,1)=1,\qquad P(j,0)=p,\quad P(j,j+1)=q\quad(j\geq1).
$$
The stationary equations give $\pi_1=\pi_0$ and $\pi_j=q^{j-1}\pi_0$ for $j\geq1$. Normalization gives $\pi_0[1+\sum_{j\geq1}q^{j-1}]=\pi_0(1+1/p)=1$, so
$$
\boxed{\pi_0=\frac p{1+p},\qquad
\pi_j=\frac{p(1-p)^{j-1}}{1+p}\quad(j\geq1).}
$$
The remaining equation $\pi_0=p\sum_{j\geq1}\pi_j$ is satisfied, confirming stationarity.

The chain is <irreducible>: every positive state can jump to zero, and zero can reach every state through consecutive immigration steps. A stationary probability distribution makes it <positive recurrent>. Returns to zero can have lengths two and three with positive probability, so it is also <aperiodic>. The Markov-chain ergodic theorem now gives the almost-sure long-run time proportion for $X_n>K$ as the stationary tail:
$$
\boxed{\sum_{j=K+1}^\infty\pi_j=\frac{(1-p)^K}{1+p}.}
$$
This time-average conclusion applies to the specified start at zero.

<Independent increments> give independent Poisson variables $N[0,s]$ and $N(s,t]$ with means $\lambda s$ and $\lambda(t-s)$. Dividing their joint probability by $\mathbb P(N[0,t]=n)$ yields
$$
\mathbb P(N[0,s]=k\mid N[0,t]=n)
=\binom nk(s/t)^k(1-s/t)^{n-k},\quad0\leq k\leq n.
$$
Thus the <conditional distribution> is <binomial distribution> with parameters $n,s/t$.

The event $T_i>s$ means that at most $i-1$ events have occurred by $s$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(T_i>s\mid N[0,t]=n)
=\sum_{k=0}^{\min(i-1,n)}\binom nk(s/t)^k(1-s/t)^{n-k}.}
$$
For $1\leq i\leq n$, the conditional event time lies in $[0,t]$. Integrate its <survival function> and substitute $u=s/t$:
$$
\mathbb E(T_i\mid N[0,t]=n)
=t\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}\binom nk\int_0^1u^k(1-u)^{n-k}du
=t\sum_{k=0}^{i-1}\frac1{n+1}
=\boxed{\frac{it}{n+1}}.
$$
The index range $i\leq n$ is implicit in the source's last assertion. For $i>n$, the $i$th event is after $t$, and independent future increments instead give expectation $t+(i-n)/\lambda$; the displayed $it/(n+1)$ formula does not apply.

Minimize $\|Y-X\theta\|^2$. Differentiation gives the <normal equations> $X^TX\widehat\theta=X^TY$. Full column rank makes $X^TX$ positive definite, so
$$
\boxed{\widehat\theta=(X^TX)^{-1}X^TY,\quad
\mathbb E\widehat\theta=\theta,\quad
\operatorname{Cov}(\widehat\theta)=\sigma^2(X^TX)^{-1}.}
$$
The expectation uses the zero error mean; the <covariance> uses $\operatorname{Cov}(\epsilon)=\sigma^2I$. With independent <Gaussian> errors of common <variance>, maximizing likelihood minimizes exactly the same residual sum of squares, so this is also the maximum likelihood estimator, whether the common <variance> is known or estimated.

For the quadratic design, decompose each group's residual sum of squares into its within-group constant plus the group size times the squared residual of its <sample mean>. The three parameter combinations $\alpha$, $\alpha+\beta+\gamma$ and $\alpha-\beta+\gamma$ can fit all three group means exactly. Therefore
$$
\widehat\alpha=\overline Y_0,\qquad
\boxed{\widehat\beta=\frac{\overline Y_1-\overline Y_{-1}}2,\qquad
\widehat\gamma=\frac{\overline Y_1+\overline Y_{-1}-2\overline Y_0}2.}
$$
The group means are independent normal variables, with variances $\sigma^2/m,\sigma^2/n,\sigma^2/n$. Hence their two stated linear combinations are jointly normal, and
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(\widehat\beta,\widehat\gamma)
=\tfrac14(\sigma^2/n-\sigma^2/n)=0.
$$
Joint normality therefore proves **the two estimators are independent**. Their variances are $\sigma^2/(2n)$ and $\sigma^2/(2n)+\sigma^2/m$, respectively. Equal replication at the two off-center temperatures is what cancels the <covariance>.

A possibly <randomized statistical test> has rejection probability $\varphi(x)\in[0,1]$ after observing $x$. Its <statistical power> at parameter $\theta$ is $\mathbb E_\theta\varphi$, and its <test size> is the supremum of that <statistical power> over the null parameter set.

For simple hypotheses with densities $f_0,f_1$, the <Neyman-Pearson lemma> states that a level-$\alpha$ test is most powerful if it rejects where $f_1>k f_0$, accepts where $f_1<k f_0$, and randomizes on equality to obtain null rejection probability $\alpha$. Here $k\geq0$ is chosen to reach that <test size>. To prove optimality, let $\varphi_*$ be this test and $\varphi$ any test of level at most $\alpha$. Pointwise,
$$
(\varphi_*-\varphi)(f_1-kf_0)\geq0.
$$
Integrating and using $\int(\varphi_*-\varphi)f_0\geq0$ gives $\mathbb E_1\varphi_*-\mathbb E_1\varphi\geq0$. This proves maximal <statistical power>. For $k>0$, equality also forces any equally powerful test to use all its allowed <test size> and agree with the indicated decisions away from the likelihood-ratio tie set, up to null sets.

For the Poisson sample, $S=\sum_iX_i\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(n\mu)$ and, for every fixed $\mu_1>\mu_0>0$, the <likelihood ratio> is
$$
e^{-n(\mu_1-\mu_0)}(\mu_1/\mu_0)^S.
$$
It increases strictly with $S$. Choose an <integer> $k$ so that $\mathbb P_{\mu_0}(S>k)\leq\alpha\leq\mathbb P_{\mu_0}(S\geq k)$, and reject for $S>k$, with boundary rejection probability
$$
\boxed{r=\frac{\alpha-\mathbb P_{\mu_0}(S>k)}
{\mathbb P_{\mu_0}(S=k)}\quad\text{when }S=k.}
$$
This has exact <test size> $\alpha$. Its rejection ordering and calibration do not depend on which $\mu_1>\mu_0$ is selected, so the lemma proves it **uniformly most powerful** for the whole one-sided alternative. Randomization is generally needed for an exact arbitrary <test size> in a discrete model.

For the approximate design, the <central limit theorem> under $\mu_0=1$ gives critical sum $n+1.645\sqrt n$. Under $\mu_1$, the sum is approximately normal with mean $n\mu_1$ and <variance> $n\mu_1$. Target <statistical power> $0.9$ therefore gives
$$
\frac{n+1.645\sqrt n-n\mu_1}{\sqrt{n\mu_1}}\approx-1.282,
$$
or $\sqrt n(\mu_1-1)\approx1.645+1.282\sqrt{\mu_1}$. Thus the requested design expression is
$$
\boxed{n\approx\left(\frac{1.645+1.282\sqrt{\mu_1}}{\mu_1-1}\right)^2.}
$$
Round upward to obtain an <integer> design. This is a <normal approximation> to the requested <statistical power>, rather than an exact discrete sample-size guarantee; the exact Poisson test above provides its exact calibration.

A real square <matrix> is orthogonal if $Q^TQ=I$. Then $\|Qx\|^2=x^TQ^TQx=x^Tx=\|x\|^2$, proving length preservation.

Define $J(i,j,\theta)$ to be identity outside rows and columns $i,j$, with block
$$
\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The block's transpose times itself is identity because $\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1$ and the off-diagonal terms cancel. Thus $J$ is orthogonal, with <determinant> one.

For $r=\sqrt{x_i^2+x_j^2}>0$, choose $\cos\theta=x_i/r$, $\sin\theta=x_j/r$. Then $y_i=r$, $y_j=0$ and every other coordinate is unchanged. If $r=0$, choose $\theta=0$. This fixes the useful **nonnegative pivot convention**.

Apply the rotations to the current <matrix> in the displayed order from right to left. First $J(1,2)$ eliminates entry $(2,1)$; then $J(1,3)$ eliminates $(3,1)$ without changing row two. Finally $J(2,3)$ eliminates $(3,2)$ while preserving the zeros in column one. Thus
$$
\boxed{R=\begin{pmatrix}*&*&*\\0&*&*\\0&0&*\end{pmatrix},\qquad
Q=J(1,2)^TJ(1,3)^TJ(2,3)^T.}
$$
Inverting the product gives $A=QR$. Even if $A$ starts lower triangular, the row rotations generally create nonzero entries above the diagonal; there is no further universal simplification. For example $A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0&0\\1&1&0\\1&1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ gives positive-pivot
$$
R=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt3&2/\sqrt3&1/\sqrt3\\0&\sqrt{2/3}&1/\sqrt6\\0&0&1/\sqrt2\end{pmatrix},
$$
whose three entries above the diagonal are all nonzero.

If $A$ is orthogonal, $R$ is both <upper triangular> and orthogonal. <Orthogonality> of its columns successively forces all off-diagonal entries to vanish and all diagonal entries to have absolute value one. The first two pivots are positive under our convention, and the rotations have <determinant> one. Therefore the precise result is
$$
\boxed{R=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,\det A).}
$$
Without a specified pivot-sign convention, the invariant statement is a diagonal <matrix> with entries $\pm1$ and product $\det A$.

A <cubic spline> is a twice continuously differentiable function whose restriction to each knot interval is a <polynomial> of degree at most three. Its second derivative is continuous and piecewise linear. Natural endpoint conditions are $C''(x_0)=C''(x_n)=0$.

Linearity of both the piecewise-polynomial and smoothness conditions, and of the endpoint equations, makes the natural splines a <vector space> $V$. A continuous piecewise linear second derivative with zero endpoint values is determined freely by its $n-1$ interior nodal values. Integrating twice adds two arbitrary affine constants. Hence
$$
\boxed{\dim V=(n-1)+2=n+1.}
$$
This count includes the case of one interval.

An interpolating <spline> has $S(x_i)=f_i$ at every node. Set $e=T-S$, so $e$ vanishes at all nodes. On each open knot interval $S^{(3)}$ is constant. Two integrations by parts give
$$
\int_{x_{i-1}}^{x_i}e''S''dx
=[e'S''-eS^{(3)}]_{x_{i-1}}^{x_i}.
$$
The terms containing $e$ vanish at nodes. When summed, the interior $e'S''$ terms cancel because $e'$ and $S''$ are continuous. The two outer terms vanish by the natural endpoint conditions. Thus
$$
\boxed{\int_{x_0}^{x_n}(T''-S'')S''dx=0.}
$$
Integrating the given square identity now yields the sharper energy decomposition
$$
\int_{x_0}^{x_n}(T'')^2dx
=\int_{x_0}^{x_n}(S'')^2dx+\int_{x_0}^{x_n}(T''-S'')^2dx.
$$
In particular **the natural interpolating <spline> minimizes the squared-curvature integral**.

If $T$ is another natural interpolant, the same minimizing argument in both directions makes the two energies equal. The final nonnegative integral then vanishes, so $T''=S''$ everywhere by <continuity>. Their difference is affine and zero at all knots, including the two distinct endpoints, so $T=S$. This proves uniqueness.

It also supplies existence if needed: nodal evaluation is a <linear map> from the $(n+1)$-dimensional space $V$ to $\mathbb R^{n+1}$. Its <kernel> is zero by the same argument with zero data and the zero competitor. It is therefore <surjective>, so every data vector has a natural <spline> interpolant.
