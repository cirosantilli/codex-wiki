A <splitting field> of $f$ over $F$ is a <field extension> $K/F$ in which $f$ is a product of linear factors and which is generated over $F$ by its roots. The generation condition rules out adjoining unrelated elements.

For existence, induct on the <degree of a polynomial>. Choose an <irreducible polynomial> divisor $p$ of $f$. The <quotient ring> $F[X]/(p)$ is a <field> because $(p)$ is a <maximal ideal>; the class $\alpha$ of $X$ is a <root> of $p$, hence of $f$. Over this <field extension>, divide out $X-\alpha$ and apply induction to the remaining <polynomial>. This gives an extension in which $f$ splits. Inside it, the <subfield> generated by all roots is a <splitting field>. The induction starts with a linear <polynomial>, whose <splitting field> is already $F$. Thus **every positive-degree <polynomial> has a splitting <field>**; being monic is unnecessary for existence.

A <composition series> is a finite chain
$$
1=G_0\triangleleft G_1\triangleleft\cdots\triangleleft G_r=G
$$
whose inclusions are strict and whose <quotient group>[quotients] $G_i/G_{i-1}$ are <simple group>[simple]. These <quotient group>[quotients] are the <composition factor>[composition factors]. The <normal subgroup> condition is relative to the next member, rather than necessarily to all of $G$.

Induct on the order of a <finite group>. The trivial <group> has the empty <composition series>. Otherwise choose a <maximal normal subgroup> $N\lneq G$, which exists because there are finitely many subgroups. The <subgroup correspondence theorem> identifies <normal subgroup>[normal subgroups] of $G/N$ with <normal subgroup>[normal subgroups] of $G$ containing $N$, so maximality makes $G/N$ a <simple group>. By induction $N$ has a <composition series>; appending $G$ gives one for $G$.

For example, the nonabelian <symmetric group> $S_3$ has
$$
1\triangleleft A_3\triangleleft S_3,
$$
with <composition factor>[composition factors] $C_3$ and $C_2$, both <abelian group>[abelian].

Every <integer> square remains a square modulo every <prime number>, including the zero class at its prime divisors. Conversely, let $a$ be an odd <integer> which is not an <integer> square. If $a$ is the negative of a square, part (i) supplies an odd $k$ with $(a/k)=-1$. In every other case, the <prime factorization> of $|a|$ has an odd exponent $t$ at some odd prime $q$. Write $a=\pm q^t b$ with $b>0$ odd and $\gcd(q,b)=1$, and apply part (ii).

In either case, factor the resulting $k$ into primes. Since its <Jacobi symbol> is a product of <Legendre symbol>[Legendre symbols] and equals $-1$, at least one prime factor $p$ has $(a/p)=-1$. Thus $a$ is not a square modulo that prime. This proves **an odd <integer> is an <integer> square exactly when it is a square modulo every prime**. If the phrase <quadratic residue> is restricted to nonzero residue classes, the final statement must instead say “a square modulo every prime”; for example, $9$ reduces to zero modulo $3$.

Use the nonnegative-part convention $h=h^+-h^-$, where $h^+=\max(h,0)$ and $h^-=\max(-h,0)$. The zero-mean assumption gives
$$
A=\int_0^\pi h^+=\int_0^\pi h^-.
$$
For $k\geq1$, write the <integral> over the $k$th period as $I_k=P_k-N_k$, where
$$
P_k=\int_0^\pi\frac{h^+(t)}{k\pi+t}\,dt,
\qquad N_k=\int_0^\pi\frac{h^-(t)}{k\pi+t}\,dt.
$$
Both $P_k$ and $N_k$ lie in the <interval> $[A/((k+1)\pi),A/(k\pi)]$. Their difference therefore satisfies the stronger estimate
$$
|I_k|\leq\frac{A}{\pi k(k+1)}\leq\frac{A}{\pi k^2}.
$$
Consequently $\sum_{k\geq1}I_k$ converges absolutely as a <series> of numbers, proving the requested limit of integrals over whole periods. This does not assert absolute integrability of the original function: the cancellation took place inside each period.

Indeed, setting $B=\int_0^\pi|h(t)|\,dt$, we have
$$
\int_{k\pi}^{(k+1)\pi}|g(x)|\,dx
\geq\frac{B}{(k+1)\pi}.
$$
The <harmonic series> diverges, so $g$ is <Lebesgue integrability>[Lebesgue integrable] only if $B=0$. Conversely $B=0$ means $h=0$ almost everywhere on one period, and hence on all periods, so $g=0$ almost everywhere. Therefore
$$
\boxed{g\in L^1(\pi,\infty)\iff h=0\text{ almost everywhere}.}
$$
This is an example of <integrating a mean-zero periodic function divided by x>.

A <connected space> has no expression as the union of two disjoint nonempty <open set>[open subsets]. A <continuous function>[continuous] <surjection> to the two-point <discrete topology>[discrete space] would pull back its singleton sets to such a separation. Conversely, a separation $X=U\sqcup V$ defines a <continuous function>[continuous] <surjection> by taking the values $0$ on $U$ and $1$ on $V$. Thus these two conditions are equivalent, including for the empty space.

If a subset $S\subseteq\mathbb R$ contains $a<c$ but omits some $b$ between them, its intersections with $(-\infty,b)$ and $(b,\infty)$ separate it. Hence every nonempty <connected space>[connected] subset is an <interval>. Conversely, let an <interval> $I$ have a separation and choose $a<b$ in its opposite sides. Restrict the associated two-valued <continuous function> to $[a,b]\subseteq I$ and regard it as a real-valued function. The <intermediate value theorem> forces the value $1/2$, a contradiction. Singletons and the empty set are <connected space>[connected] directly.

For the union assertion, let $u:X\to\{0,1\}$ be <continuous function>[continuous]. Each restriction $u|_{A_i}$ is constant because $A_i$ is <connected space>[connected]. Since every pair of the $A_i$ intersects, these constants agree, so $u$ is constant on $X$. There is no <continuous function>[continuous] <surjection>, and $X$ is <connected space>[connected]. The empty indexing case gives $X=\varnothing$ and is harmless.

To apply this to the plane, for each $t\in\mathbb R$ take the union of the horizontal line at height $t$ with the vertical axis. Its two lines are <connected space>[connected] and intersect; all such unions share the vertical axis and cover $\mathbb R^2$. Thus the plane is <connected space>[connected]. For the punctured plane, take, for every $r>0$, the circle of radius $r$ together with the positive horizontal ray. The circle is the <continuous image of a connected space>[continuous image] of $[0,2\pi]$, and intersects that <connected space>[connected] ray. These unions share the ray and cover $\mathbb R^2\setminus\{0\}$, so the punctured plane is <connected space>[connected] too. Translation gives the same conclusion after removing any one point.

Finally, suppose $g:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R$ were a <continuous function>[continuous] <injection>. Its image $I$ is a <connected space>[connected] <interval> with at least three points. Choose an interior point $c$ of $I$ and its unique preimage $p$. The restriction of $g$ to $\mathbb R^2\setminus\{p\}$ is a <continuous function>[continuous] <surjection> onto $I\setminus\{c\}$. Its domain is <connected space>[connected], whereas its image is separated by the two half-lines on either side of $c$. This contradicts preservation of <connected space>[connectedness] under <continuous image of a connected space>[continuous images]. Therefore **no continuous <injection> of the plane into the line exists**.

$$

$$
The regularity assumption excludes the exceptional case in which the constraint itself has zero <first variation> for every admissible perturbation.

Parametrize the <surface of revolution> by $r(z,\theta)=(f(z)\cos\theta,f(z)\sin\theta,z)$. The magnitude of $r_z\times r_\theta$ is $f\sqrt{1+f'^2}$. Integrating the resulting <surface area> element over $\theta$, and integrating the circular cross-sectional areas for the <volume>, gives
$$
A=2\pi\int_a^b f\sqrt{1+f'^2}\,dz,
\qquad V=\pi\int_a^b f^2\,dz.
$$
Here $f\geq0$ because it is a radius. For a regular connected closed surface, take $f>0$ in the interior; the closing endpoints can have unbounded $f'$ even when the surface itself is smooth.

After dividing $A-\lambda V$ by $\pi$, its integrand is $L=2f\sqrt{1+f'^2}-\lambda f^2$. The <Euler-Lagrange equation> and <Beltrami identity> become
$$
\sqrt{1+f'^2}-\lambda f-
\frac d{dz}\left(\frac{ff'}{\sqrt{1+f'^2}}\right)=0,
\qquad
\frac{2f}{\sqrt{1+f'^2}}-\lambda f^2=C.
$$
As $f$ tends to zero at an endpoint, $0\leq2f/\sqrt{1+f'^2}\leq2f$ also tends to zero, so $C=0$. On the positive interior this implies $\lambda>0$ and, writing $R=2/\lambda$,
$$
1+f'^2=\frac{R^2}{f^2}.
$$

$$
(f^2)''=2(f'^2+ff'')=-2,
$$
including at the maximum where $f'=0$. Integrating and using the <first integral> gives
$$
f(z)^2=R^2-(z-z_0)^2.
$$

$$
\boxed{z_0=\frac{a+b}2,\qquad R=\frac{b-a}2,\qquad\lambda=\frac2R=\frac4{b-a}.}
$$

{parent=2011/M/M5/b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2011/M/M5/b/ii}

Let $Q=H_N$ and define
$$
\pi_N=\frac1Q,\qquad
\pi_k=\frac1{(k+1)Q}\quad(1\leq k<N).
$$
The probabilities sum to $Q^{-1}(1+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}(k+1)^{-1})=1$. For $k<N$, the incoming <probability> is
$$
(\pi P)_k=\sum_{j=k+1}^{N}\frac{\pi_j}{j}
=\frac1Q\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{N-1}\frac1{j(j+1)}+\frac1N\right)
=\frac1{(k+1)Q}=\pi_k,
$$
using a telescoping sum. Similarly,
$$
(\pi P)_N=\sum_{j=1}^N\frac{\pi_j}{j}
=\frac1Q\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\frac1{j(j+1)}+\frac1N\right)
=\frac1Q=\pi_N.
$$
Thus $\pi$ is a <stationary distribution>. Every state can reach $N$ in one step, and $N$ can reach every lower positive state in one step, so the chain is <irreducible Markov chain>[irreducible]. The self-loop at $N$ has <probability> $1/N>0$, hence the chain is <aperiodic Markov chain>[aperiodic]. The convergence theorem for a finite irreducible aperiodic <Markov chain> now gives
$$
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}P(X_n=k)=\pi_k.}
$$
For $N=1$, the conclusion follows directly from the single absorbing/refilled state.

This is the limiting law of the <uniform depletion and refill Markov chain>.

The log <likelihood function> is strictly concave on the positive simplex, because its <Hessian matrix> is diagonal with entries $-n_{ij}/\theta_{ij}^2<0$. Hence this critical point is the unique global <maximum likelihood estimation>[maximum-likelihood estimate].

A <polynomial least-squares approximation> $p_n\in\Pi_n$ minimizes the weighted residual <norm>:
$$
\boxed{\|u-p_n\|=\min_{q\in\Pi_n}\|u-q\|.}
$$
Equivalently it minimizes $\int_{-1}^1x^2(u(x)-q(x))^2\,dx$. This is a best approximation within a finite-dimensional <polynomial> space, without an interpolation requirement.

= Solution
{parent=2011/P/P3/b}

If $p$ is a minimizer, then for each $q\in\Pi_n$ the quadratic
$$
\|u-p-tq\|^2=\|u-p\|^2-2t(u-p,q)+t^2\|q\|^2
$$
has a minimum at $t=0$, so $(u-p,q)=0$. Conversely, if this orthogonality holds, then for every $q\in\Pi_n$ the <Pythagorean theorem> in the <inner product space> gives
$$
\|u-q\|^2=\|u-p\|^2+\|p-q\|^2\geq\|u-p\|^2.
$$
Thus the minimizers are exactly the <orthogonal projection>[orthogonal projections] characterized by
$$
\boxed{(u-p,q)=0\quad\text{for all }q\in\Pi_n.}
$$
For existence, write $p=\sum_{j=0}^n c_jx^j$. The conditions form the <normal equations for linear least squares>[normal equations] $Gc=b$, with <Gram matrix> $G_{ij}=(x^i,x^j)$ and $b_i=(u,x^i)$. For every nonzero coefficient vector $v$,
$$
v^TGv=\int_{-1}^1x^2\left(\sum_jv_jx^j\right)^2\,dx>0.
$$
Indeed a nonzero <polynomial> is nonzero on some open <interval> away from $0$, where the weight is positive. Hence $G$ is a <positive-definite matrix> and is invertible, proving existence and uniqueness of $c$. Uniqueness also follows from the preceding <Pythagorean theorem> identity. The zero of the weight at one point therefore causes no degeneracy on continuous functions or polynomials.

Multiply $Ax=b$ by $Q^T$ to get the equivalent triangular system
$$
Rx=Q^Tb.
