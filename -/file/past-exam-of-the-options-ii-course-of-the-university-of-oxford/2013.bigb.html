A monic <quadratic polynomial> over a <field> is irreducible exactly when it has no root in that <field>. Testing the nine choices of its two coefficients in $\mathbb F_3$ gives
$$
\boxed{x^2+1,\qquad x^2+x+2,\qquad x^2+2x+2.}
$$
For clarity, a zero constant term gives a root at zero. Among constant term one, only the zero linear coefficient avoids roots at $1,2$. Among constant term two, precisely the linear coefficients one and two avoid roots. This exhausts all choices.

Put $f=x^4+x+2$. Its values at $0,1,2$ are respectively $2,1,2$, so it has no linear factor. If this quartic were reducible, absence of a linear factor would force a factorization into two irreducible quadratics. Division by the three candidates above gives remainders respectively $x,x+1,x+1$, all nonzero. Thus **$f$ is an <irreducible polynomial>**.

The <quotient by an irreducible polynomial is a field> consequently gives a <field> of $3^4=81$ elements. Its nonzero elements form a <multiplicative group of a finite field> of order $80$. In this <field>, $a^4=1+2a$. Successive squaring gives
$$
a^8=1+a+a^2,\qquad a^{16}=2+a+2a^3.
$$
The latter is not one: otherwise the nonzero <polynomial> $1+x+2x^3$ of degree below four would vanish at $a$, contradicting its <minimal polynomial>. By <Lagrange's theorem>, $(a^{16})^5=a^{80}=1$. Since five is prime, its order is exactly five. Hence one answer is
$$
\boxed{\zeta=2+a+2a^3.}
$$
Its other nonidentity powers are the remaining primitive fifth roots. It is unnecessary to prove that $a$ itself generates the entire multiplicative <group>.

The <orbit> of $s$ is $O_s=\{gs:g\in G\}$, and its <stabilizer> is $G_s=\{g\in G:gs=s\}$. The identity fixes $s$; if $g,h$ fix it then $(gh)s=g(hs)=s$; and $gs=s$ implies $g^{-1}s=s$. Thus **$G_s$ is a <subgroup>**.

A <group action> is <transitive group action>[transitive] when it has just one <orbit>: for every $s,t\in S$ there is $g\in G$ with $gs=t$.

Two <group actions> on $S_1,S_2$ are equivalent if there is a <bijection> $\phi:S_1\to S_2$ such that $\phi(gs)=g\phi(s)$ for all $g,s$. Such a bijection is <equivariant>.

The <orbit-stabilizer theorem> in this form says that the action on $O_s$ is equivalent to the left <coset> action on $G/G_s$, by $gG_s\mapsto gs$. This map is well-defined and injective because $gs=hs$ exactly when $h^{-1}g\in G_s$, and it is onto by the definition of the <orbit>. It is equivariant by multiplication.

Thus every transitive action is a <coset> action. Two such actions $G/H$ and $G/K$ are equivalent exactly when $H,K$ are conjugate: <stabilizers> at different points of one <orbit> are conjugate, and a conjugacy explicitly supplies a coset-action equivalence. This last classification is what makes counting by <orbit> types possible.

A <cyclic group> of order six has exactly one <subgroup> of each order $1,2,3,6$, and no others. Since it is abelian, these are also its four conjugacy classes of <subgroups>. Thus there is one transitive <group action> of each size $1,2,3,6$.

An action on six points is a disjoint union of its <orbits>. Its equivalence type is therefore determined by nonnegative integers satisfying
$$
n_1+2n_2+3n_3+6n_6=6.
$$
If $n_6=1$ there is one possibility. If $n_6=0$, the possibilities $n_3=0,1,2$ allow respectively $4,2,1$ choices of $n_2$, with $n_1$ then fixed. Hence
$$
\boxed{1+4+2+1=8\text{ inequivalent actions}.}
$$
This is <counting finite group actions by transitive orbit types>. Equivalently, a generator acts as a <permutation> whose cycles have lengths dividing six, and these are its eight possible cycle types on six points.

Factor $a=\prod_i q_i$ and $b=\prod_j p_j$, listing prime factors with repetitions. Coprimality ensures $q_i\ne p_j$. By the definition of the <Jacobi symbol> and <multiplicativity of the Legendre symbol>,
$$
(a/b)(b/a)=\prod_{i,j}(q_i/p_j)(p_j/q_i)
=(-1)^{\left(\sum_i(q_i-1)/2\right)\left(\sum_j(p_j-1)/2\right)}.
$$
For odd integers $r,s$,
$$
\frac{rs-1}{2}\equiv\frac{r-1}{2}+\frac{s-1}{2}\pmod2,
$$
since the difference is $(r-1)(s-1)/2$, an even integer. Inductively the two sums in the exponent are congruent to $(a-1)/2$ and $(b-1)/2$ modulo two. Hence
$$
\boxed{(a/b)(b/a)=(-1)^{\frac{a-1}{2}\frac{b-1}{2}}.}
$$
If either number is one, the empty product convention proves the same identity. This extends the prime law to arbitrary coprime positive odd denominators.

The <monotone convergence theorem> states that if $0\leq h_n\uparrow h$ pointwise for measurable <functions>, then $h$ is measurable and $\int h=\lim_n\int h_n$, allowing infinity.

Apply it to the partial sums $S_N=\sum_{n=0}^Nf_n$. They are nonnegative, measurable, and increase pointwise to $f$. Finite additivity of the <integral> and monotone convergence yield
$$
\boxed{\int_{\mathbb R}f=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=0}^N\int_{\mathbb R}f_n
=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb R}f_n.}
$$
The assumed finite sum of <integrals> makes $f$ integrable. This proves the series-integration rule needed below directly from the stated theorem, without assuming an additional interchange theorem.

For $\alpha>0$, <integration by parts> on $[0,R]$ gives
$$
\int_0^R xe^{-\alpha x}\,dx
=\frac1{\alpha^2}-e^{-\alpha R}\left(\frac R\alpha+\frac1{\alpha^2}\right).

$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty xe^{-\alpha x}\,dx=\alpha^{-2}.}
$$
Its finite value proves integrability as well as evaluating it.

\frac{xe^{-\alpha x}}{1-e^{-x}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}xe^{-(n+\alpha)x}.

On $0<x<1$, the geometric expansion gives summands $h_n(x)=x^{n+\beta-1}-x^{n-\beta}$. Their <integrals> are
$$
\int_0^1h_n(x)\,dx
=\frac1{n+\beta}-\frac1{n+1-\beta}
=\frac{1-2\beta}{n^2+n+\beta-\beta^2}.
$$
Both powers are integrable because $0<\beta<1$. If $\beta<1/2$, each $h_n$ is nonnegative, since its first exponent is smaller. Their <integrals> form a convergent nonnegative series: the $n=0$ term is finite and subsequent terms are bounded by a constant times $n^{-2}$. The proved series-integration rule applies.

If $\beta>1/2$, apply precisely that rule to $-h_n$, which are nonnegative, and negate the result. At $\beta=1/2$ all summands and the integrand are zero. Thus in every case the <integral> is absolutely integrable and
$$
\boxed{\int_0^1\frac{x^{\beta-1}-x^{-\beta}}{1-x}\,dx
=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1-2\beta}{n^2+n+\beta-\beta^2}.}
$$
The common sign of the summands is the reason no unproved theorem for integrating arbitrary signed series is needed.

A <topological space> is **<compact>** if every <open cover> of it has a finite subcover. That is, if $X=\bigcup_{i\in I}U_i$ with all $U_i$ open, finitely many of those same <open sets> already cover $X$.

But the sets are nested, so any finite intersection is the set with largest index, which is nonempty. This contradiction proves
$$
\boxed{\bigcap_iF_i\ne\varnothing.}

This is the nested-closed-set form of the <finite intersection property> for <compact> spaces.
