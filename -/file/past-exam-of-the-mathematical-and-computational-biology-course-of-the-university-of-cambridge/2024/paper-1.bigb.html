A <probability density function> must have <integral> one. For $\theta>-1$,
$$
1=A\int_0^1x^\theta\,dx=\frac{A}{\theta+1},\qquad \boxed{A=\theta+1}.
$$
The restriction **$\theta>-1$** is essential: for $\theta\leq-1$ the <integral> diverges and no positive normalizing constant exists.

= Solution

For a <moment> of order $q$ with $\theta+q>-1$, integration of the <probability density function> gives
$$
\mathbb E[X^q]=(\theta+1)\int_0^1x^{\theta+q}\,dx=\frac{\theta+1}{\theta+q+1}.
$$
Thus the <expected value> and <variance> are
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[X]=\frac{\theta+1}{\theta+2}},\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(X)=\frac{\theta+1}{\theta+3}-\left(\frac{\theta+1}{\theta+2}\right)^2
=\boxed{\frac{\theta+1}{(\theta+3)(\theta+2)^2}}.
$$
The <cumulative distribution function> on $(0,1)$ is $F(x)=x^{\theta+1}$. Its unique <median> $m$ satisfies $F(m)=1/2$, giving
$$
\boxed{m=2^{-1/(\theta+1)}}.
$$

For the <independent and identically distributed> observations, multiply the <probability density functions>. On the admissible parameter range $\theta>-1$, the <likelihood function> and <log-likelihood> are
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L(\theta)=(\theta+1)^N\prod_{i=1}^Nx_i^\theta},\qquad
\boxed{\ell(\theta)=N\log(\theta+1)+\theta\sum_{i=1}^N\log x_i}.
$$
These expressions presume that every observation lies strictly between zero and one; an observation outside that support would give zero <likelihood> under this model.

= Solution

Write $s=\sum_i\log x_i<0$. The <score function> is
$$
\ell'(\theta)=\frac{N}{\theta+1}+s.
$$
Setting it to zero gives the <maximum-likelihood estimator>
$$
\boxed{\widehat\theta=-\frac{N}{\sum_i\log x_i}-1}.
$$
This lies above $-1$. The <observed Fisher information> is
$$
\boxed{J(\theta)=-\ell''(\theta)=\frac{N}{(\theta+1)^2}>0}.
$$
Consequently the <log-likelihood> is strictly concave throughout its domain. Its stationary point is the unique global maximum, not merely a candidate local maximum. Indeed the <log-likelihood> tends to minus infinity as either $\theta\downarrow-1$ or $\theta\to\infty$.

Keep the true parameter fixed and imagine repeatedly collecting four <independent and identically distributed> observations under the same model. Compute the <maximum-likelihood estimate>, its estimated <standard error>, and the <Wald confidence interval> afresh each time. The intended coverage statement is that approximately 95% of those random <confidence intervals> contain the fixed true parameter, provided the <normal approximation> is adequate.

For the <squared-denominator population map>, a <fixed point> satisfies
$$
\bar x=\frac{r\bar x}{(b+\bar x)^2}.
$$
There is always the extinction <fixed point> $\bar x=0$. For a positive <fixed point>, divide by $\bar x$ and use $b+\bar x>0$ to obtain
$$
\boxed{\bar x=\sqrt r-b,\qquad r>b^2}.
$$
At equality the candidate merges with zero and is not positive.

= b
{parent=B2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Differentiate the <squared-denominator population map>:
$$
F'(x)=\frac{r(b-x)}{(b+x)^3}.
$$
At the positive <fixed point>, $b+\bar x=\sqrt r$, so its <fixed-point multiplier> is
$$
F'(\bar x)=\frac{2b}{\sqrt r}-1.
$$
Since $0<b/\sqrt r<1$, we have $-1<F'(\bar x)<1$. A small perturbation $\varepsilon_t$ obeys $\varepsilon_{t+1}=F'(\bar x)\varepsilon_t+O(\varepsilon_t^2)$, and hence shrinks locally. Thus **the positive <fixed point> is locally asymptotically stable** by <fixed point stability for an iteration>.

= c
{parent=B2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

This is a <stage-structured population model> with a census once per maturation interval. Only adults present at census $t$ contribute to the juvenile cohort at census $t+1$; each contributes $r$ new juveniles on average. Thus $r$ is an effective fecundity, including any survival of offspring needed to reach the next census. Juveniles present at $t$ do not contribute a separate birth term during this interval.

The term $cy_t$ consists of juveniles that survive and mature into adults by the next census, so $c$ is their survival-to-maturity fraction. The term $(1-d)z_t$ consists of surviving adults; $d$ is the adult mortality fraction per interval. All surviving juveniles leave the juvenile compartment in one step, adults remain adults, and there is no immigration or density dependence. The reproduction term is applied to adults counted at the start of the interval, rather than only to those surviving to its end.

Eliminate the juvenile variable from the <stage-structured population model>. Since $y_{t+1}=2z_t$,
$$
z_{t+2}=\tfrac34 y_{t+1}+\tfrac12z_{t+1}
=\tfrac32z_t+\tfrac12z_{t+1}.
$$
The <characteristic polynomial> of this <linear recurrence> is
$$
q^2-\tfrac12q-\tfrac32=(q-\tfrac32)(q+1),
$$
so $z_t=A(3/2)^t+B(-1)^t$. The <initial conditions> give $z_0=0$ and $z_1=(3/4)10=15/2$, hence $A+B=0$ and $(3/2)A-B=15/2$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{z_t=3\left[\left(\frac32\right)^t-(-1)^t\right],\qquad t=0,1,2,\ldots}.
$$
The alternating term records the initial imbalance between life stages; the growing $(3/2)^t$ term dominates eventually.

At a nonzero <fixed point> of the nonlinear <stage-structured population model>, the adult balance gives $d\bar z=c\bar y$. Substitute $\bar y=d\bar z/c$ into the juvenile equation and cancel $\bar z>0$:

\frac dc=\frac r{(b+\bar z)^2}.

\boxed{\bar z=\sqrt{\frac{cr}{d}}-b,\qquad
\bar y=\frac dc\left(\sqrt{\frac{cr}{d}}-b\right)}.

Both coordinates are positive precisely when **$\boxed{cr>db^2}$.**

The <Jacobian matrix> at the extinction <fixed point> is
$$
J_0=\begin{pmatrix}0&r/b^2\\c&1-d\end{pmatrix},
$$
with <characteristic polynomial>
$$
p(q)=q^2-(1-d)q-\frac{cr}{b^2}.
$$
Under $cr>db^2$, $p(1)=d-cr/b^2<0$, whereas $p(q)\to\infty$ as $q\to\infty$. Hence its positive <eigenvalue>
$$
q_+=\frac{1-d+\sqrt{(1-d)^2+4cr/b^2}}2
$$
is greater than one. Its <eigenvector> can be taken with both components positive: choose $(r/(b^2q_+),1)^T$. Thus there are biologically admissible positive perturbations that grow. **The extinction <fixed point> is unstable** by <linear stability analysis> for a <discrete dynamical system>.

Choose the <diagonal matrix> $W=\operatorname{diag}(w_1,\ldots,w_m)$. Multiplying a <vector> by $W$ scales its $i$th component by $w_i$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\|Wb-WAx\|^2=\sum_{i=1}^mw_i^2\bigl(b_i-(Ax)_i\bigr)^2}.
$$
The <Euclidean norm> objective for ordinary <linear least squares> on $(WA,Wb)$ is exactly the <weighted least squares> objective, so the sets of minimizing <vectors> coincide. Zero weights simply discard the corresponding residuals.

$$
\boxed{(WA)^T(WA)\widehat x_w=(WA)^TWb},

or equivalently
$$
\boxed{A^TW^TWA\widehat x_w=A^TW^TWb}.
$$
Here $W^TW=\operatorname{diag}(w_1^2,\ldots,w_m^2)$. A unique minimizing <vector> exists when the weighted design <matrix> has full column rank; the equations themselves remain valid without that assumption.

A <BLOSUM> <substitution matrix> is estimated from conserved, ungapped blocks of related <protein> sequences. Closely related sequences are clustered to reduce overrepresentation, and aligned <amino acid> pairs from different clusters are counted. Let $q_{ij}$ be their normalized observed pair frequency, and $e_{ij}$ the frequency expected from independent draws at the background <amino acid> frequencies. With unordered pairs, $e_{ii}=p_i^2$ and $e_{ij}=2p_ip_j$ for $i\ne j$.

For <BLOSUM62>, scores are rounded half-bit <logarithms> of these odds:
$$
\boxed{s_{ij}\simeq2\log_2(q_{ij}/e_{ij})}.
$$
A positive entry means that the aligned pair occurs more often in related sequences than the independent-background model predicts; a negative entry means less often. Adding these scores accumulates evidence across aligned positions. A score is neither a percentage identity nor a direct substitution <probability>.<The statistics of sequence similarity scores by National Center for Biotechnology Information>[The choice of substitution scores; The PAM and BLOSUM amino acid substitution matrices]{ref}

A diagonal <substitution matrix> score measures enrichment over a background match, rather than simply rewarding an identical letter. <tryptophan residues> are comparatively rare, so a W–W match has a small chance background frequency $p_W^2$. Its observed conservation produces a much larger ratio $q_{WW}/p_W^2$ than the corresponding ratio for the more common <leucine>. The <BLOSUM62> diagonal therefore awards W–W more than L–L. Both the background frequency and the observed pair frequency matter; rarity alone does not determine a diagonal score.<Amino acid substitution matrices from protein blocks by Steven Henikoff and Jorja G. An <affine gap penalty> charges a larger opening cost than extension cost, reflecting that a single insertion or deletion event can span several residues. For a gap of length $\ell\geq1$, explicitly choose
$$
g(\ell)=o+e(\ell-1),\qquad o=4,\quad e=1.
$$
The supplied <sequence alignment> has two gap runs, of lengths one and two. Its residue–residue score remains $24$, so this choice gives
$$
\boxed{S=24-g(1)-g(2)=24-4-5=15}.
$$
More generally, this <affine gap penalty> convention gives $S=24-2o-e$. There is no unique numerical rescore until opening and extension costs are chosen. A concave length-dependent <gap penalty> can make very long gaps progressively cheaper per added residue; distinct terminal <gap penalties> can accommodate incomplete sequence ends. Each choice encodes a different assumption about insertions, deletions or sequence completeness.

Use **<local sequence alignment>**, such as the <Smith-Waterman algorithm>, to compare the <globin> fragment with the matching region of each full-length target. A <global sequence alignment> would penalize long target flanks absent from the fragment. For a <linear gap penalty> the <dynamic programming> recurrence includes a restart option:

= Solution
{parent=h}

The gain comes from avoiding a full <dynamic programming> table for every target. <BLAST> searches for short, sufficiently high-scoring word matches between query and database, then extends promising seeds. Modern versions reserve expensive gapped refinement for candidates passing earlier filters. <FASTA sequence search> similarly starts from short word matches, collects promising runs along alignment diagonals, and refines or joins selected regions. Word lookup and database indexing make candidate discovery inexpensive compared with considering every pair of positions.<Basic local alignment search tool by Stephen F. Altschul and others>[pp. 403–406, word matches and hit extension]{ref}<Improved tools for biological sequence comparison by William R. Pearson and David J. Lipman>[pp. 2444–2446, FASTA search stages]{ref}

These are <heuristic algorithms>: a good <local sequence alignment> can be missed if it has no acceptable seed, contains many interruptions, or is filtered before extension. Longer words and higher seed-score thresholds usually speed the search but reduce sensitivity; shorter words and looser thresholds recover weaker similarities at greater cost. Repetitive or compositionally biased regions can create many uninformative hits and erode both speed and specificity. No guarantee remains of finding the optimal <sequence alignment> for the chosen scoring scheme.

Thus the practical work depends on word length, thresholds, database composition and the number of hits. The speed-up is substantial on typical data, but does not establish a universal improved worst-case bound over $O(mn)$: an input producing many candidate hits can still require extensive refinement.

A <phylogenetic epoch> is a time interval between successive branching events during which the number of ancestral branches is constant. Read backward from the four sampled leaves: there are four branches until the first merger, then three until the next, then two until their common root. Write the random durations as $T_4,T_3,T_2$, with <expected values> $E_k=\mathbb E[T_k]$.

In the <Yule process>, each of $k$ branches gives birth independently at rate $\lambda$ per generation. The next birth therefore has total rate $k\lambda$. Its waiting time has an <exponential distribution>, so the mean <phylogenetic epoch> duration is
$$
\boxed{E_k^{\rm Yule}=\frac1{k\lambda}}.
$$
For the ensuing calculation use $E_2=1/(2\lambda)$, $E_3=1/(3\lambda)$ and $E_4=1/(4\lambda)$. This is the inter-event waiting-time convention requested in the question, including an observation interval with four branches. Stopping the forward process immediately when the fourth branch is born would instead leave a zero terminal interval and describe a different sampling convention.

= ii
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For a haploid population with $N$ possible parents, each pair of ancestral lineages merges backward at rate $1/N$ in the <Kingman's coalescent> approximation. With $k$ lineages there are $\binom{k}{2}$ pairs. Hence the mean <phylogenetic epoch> duration is
$$
\boxed{E_k^{\rm coal}=\frac{N}{\binom{k}{2}}=\frac{2N}{k(k-1)}}.
$$
Thus $E_2=N$, $E_3=N/3$ and $E_4=N/6$. For an autosomal sample from $N$ diploid individuals, replace the number of gene copies by $2N$, giving $4N/[k(k-1)]$. A mitochondrial sample naturally uses a haploid maternal <effective population size>; the common factor cancels in the ratios below.

Under the stated topology <probabilities>, $P(A)=2/3$ and $P(B)=1/3$. Averaging the branch lengths from part (b), the expected length producing the folded $3:1$ class is
$$
B_{3:1}=\tfrac23(4E_4+2E_3+2E_2)+\tfrac13(4E_4+2E_3)

$$
R=\frac{4E_4+2E_3+\tfrac43E_2}{E_3+\tfrac23E_2}.

$$
\boxed{R_{\rm Yule}=\frac{1+2/3+2/3}{1/3+1/3}=\frac72=3.5}.

$$
\boxed{R_{\rm coal}=\frac{2/3+2/3+4/3}{1/3+2/3}=\frac83\approx2.67}.
$$
These are ratios of ensemble expected mutation counts, not the <expected value> of the random ratio in one tree. The stipulated topology weights apply to both processes, and their waiting-time rates depend only on the current number of branches.

For <Kingman's coalescent>, one possible violation is **recent population expansion** rather than a constant <effective population size>. Backward in time, coalescence is slow in the large recent population and faster in the smaller ancestral population. The resulting long external branches tend to increase the $3:1$ class relative to the $2:2$ class.

A <network null model> is an ensemble representing a specified baseline against which to assess an observed biological network. It should preserve the features that would otherwise confound the test: at least the number of <vertices> and <edges>, and often the <degree sequence>, direction, edge type, or relevant spatial constraints. It should randomize the feature whose biological organization is under investigation. An ensemble that preserves that very feature cannot test whether the feature is unusual.

For example, test whether a measured <protein-protein interaction network> contains more closed three-protein interaction groups than can be explained by the interaction counts of individual <proteins>. Define <vertices> as the measured <proteins> and an undirected <edge> as a reproducible detected interaction, using a fixed assay threshold and treatment of duplicates. Preselect the triangle count as the test statistic. Generate many simple <random graphs> by <degree-preserving edge rewiring>, rejecting <self-loops> and duplicate <edges>, with enough accepted swaps and separation to obtain a well-mixed reference sample. This retains the observed <degree sequence> while disrupting higher-order association.

Compute the same triangle count for every reference <graph>. Compare the observed count with this distribution; an empirical upper-tail <p-value> can be reported as $(1+\#\{T_{\rm null}\geq T_{\rm obs}\})/(M+1)$ for $M$ suitably sampled reference <graphs>. Report the size of the excess as well as its tail frequency, and check sensitivity to the assay threshold and any required connectivity constraint. A significant excess is evidence beyond the chosen degree baseline, while assay bias or spatial organization omitted from that baseline can still explain it.

A generative network model specifies a mechanism for producing networks, such as growth with <preferential attachment>, and asks what structures that mechanism creates. A <network null model> is defined by its role as a comparison baseline. These roles can overlap: the same random mechanism can be a proposed biological explanation in one analysis and a deliberately simplified null in another.

For the **<Erdős-Rényi model> $G(n,p)$**, choose the number $n$ of <vertices> and an edge <probability> $0\leq p\leq1$. Visit every unordered pair of distinct <vertices> once and include its <edge> independently with <probability> $p$. This construction gives a <simple graph> with expected edge count $p\binom n2$ and mean <vertex degree> $p(n-1)$.

Increasing $p$ at fixed $n$ makes the <graph> denser, increases typical <vertex degree> and local <clustering coefficient>, and tends to shorten <graph distances> and improve connectivity. Increasing $n$ at fixed $p$ also increases the mean <vertex degree>; to retain a sparse comparison one instead decreases $p$ approximately as $1/n$.

Its <degree distribution> is <binomial distribution>, approximately <Poisson distribution> in the sparse large-$n$ regime, so it is **not a <scale-free network>**. Connected realizations can have short <average shortest path length>, but their <clustering coefficients> are not elevated above the random baseline they themselves define. Thus they are **not <small-world networks> under the high-clustering convention**, although “small world” is sometimes used more loosely to mean only short distances.

For the **<Watts-Strogatz model>**, choose $n$ <vertices> on a ring, an even initial <vertex degree> $K<n$, and a rewiring <probability> $p$. Connect each <vertex> to its $K/2$ nearest neighbours on each side. Consider each original clockwise <edge> once: with <probability> $p$, retain its starting endpoint and replace its other endpoint uniformly, excluding <self-loops> and duplicate <edges>. The edge count remains $nK/2$, although individual <vertex degrees> change.

Increasing $n$ at fixed $K$ lengthens unrewired-ring <graph distances>. Increasing $K$ adds <edges> and shortens distances. Increasing $p$ introduces shortcuts, sharply reducing <average shortest path length> at first, then progressively reducing the <clustering coefficient> by destroying local triangles.

**The <small-world network> regime occurs at intermediate $p$** with sufficient shortcuts, substantial clustering, and $K\ll n$. At $p=0$ distances are long; near $p=1$ the clustering advantage disappears. The <degree distribution> remains concentrated around $K$, so this is **not a <scale-free network>**.<Collective dynamics of small-world networks by Duncan J. Watts and Steven H. Strogatz>[pp. 440–441, construction and dependence on rewiring probability]{ref}

For the **<Barabási-Albert model>**, choose a connected seed <graph> on $m_0$ <vertices>, the number $m\leq m_0$ of new <edges> per added <vertex>, and a final size $n$. A complete seed with $m_0\geq2$ suffices. Add one <vertex> at a time, attaching it to $m$ distinct existing <vertices> selected with weights proportional to their current <vertex degrees>. Exclude duplicate <edges> and <self-loops>.

Increasing $n$ creates a larger network and permits older hubs to grow. Increasing $m$ makes the <graph> denser, with asymptotic average <vertex degree> $2m$, and tends to shorten <graph distances>. The seed affects early structure and the oldest hubs; standard linear <preferential attachment> gives a robust large-size tail exponent.

Its asymptotic <degree distribution> is $P(k)\propto k^{-3}$, so **it is a <scale-free network>**. Hubs produce short <average shortest path length>, but elevated <clustering coefficients> are not automatic: $m=1$ with a tree seed gives a triangle-free <tree>, and clustering decreases with size in the standard model. **The strict clustered <small-world network> property is therefore not guaranteed.**<Emergence of scaling in random networks by Albert-László Barabási and Réka Albert>[pp. 510–511, growth, attachment and degree exponent]{ref}

The premise contains a wording error: $\lambda$ must be a nonzero **<eigenvalue>**, rather than a nonzero <eigenvector>. Let $v\ne0$ satisfy
$$
X^TXv=\lambda v,\qquad \lambda\ne0.
$$
Then $Xv\ne0$, since otherwise $X^TXv=0$ would force $\lambda v=0$. Multiplying by $X$ gives
$$
XX^T(Xv)=X(X^TXv)=\lambda(Xv).
$$
Thus **$\boxed{\lambda\text{ is an eigenvalue of }XX^T}$**, with <eigenvector> $Xv$. Conversely, applying $X^T$ maps an <eigenvector> of $XX^T$ with nonzero <eigenvalue> to one of $X^TX$.

These maps are inverses up to the factor $\lambda$ on the corresponding <eigenspaces>, so the nonzero <eigenvalues> have equal multiplicities. Their zero <eigenvalues> need not have the same multiplicity because the two <matrices> can have different sizes. For a unit <eigenvector> $v$ and real $X$, the normalized partner is $u=Xv/\sqrt\lambda$, since $\|Xv\|^2=v^TX^TXv=\lambda>0$.

$$
