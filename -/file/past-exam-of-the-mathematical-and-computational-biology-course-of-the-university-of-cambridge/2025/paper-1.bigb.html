= Solution
{parent=b}

It performs a <trial division> test for a <prime number>. An <integer> below $2$ is rejected; otherwise the function searches for a <divisor> from $2$ through $\lfloor\sqrt n\rfloor$. A <composite number> has a <prime factor> no larger than its <square root>, so finding no such <divisor> proves <primality>. For example, `mystery2(97)` returns **`True`**, while `mystery2(91)` returns **`False`**, since $91=7\cdot13$.

This describes the intended mathematical test. For extremely large <integers>, the floating-point computation `n**0.5` can round or overflow; an implementation intended for arbitrary-size <integers> should use an exact <integer square root>.

= Solution
{parent=d}

With its default argument and a nonnegative <integer> input, it prints the decimal <digits> of $n$ in order, one per line. For example, `mystery4(407)` prints **`4`, then `0`, then `7`**. <Integer division> removes the final <digit>, <recursion> handles the preceding <digits>, and the <remainder> gives the final <digit>. The function returns `None`.

There is a subtlety for a nondefault base: the recursive call omits `m`, so it resets to $10$. For $m\ge2$ and $n\ge m$, the actual output is the decimal <digits> of $\lfloor n/m\rfloor$, followed by the <remainder> $n\bmod m$. For example, `mystery4(13, 2)` prints **`6`, then `1`**, rather than the binary <digits> of $13$. Passing `m` on to the recursive call would give a consistent base-$m$ <positional notation>. The interpretation as <digits> assumes $n\ge0$ and $m\ge2$; arbitrary <integer> arguments need not satisfy those conditions.

Using the <probability density function> of the <Erlang distribution>, the <expected value> is
$$
\mathbb E X=\theta^2\int_0^\infty x^2e^{-\theta x}\,dx
=\frac1\theta\int_0^\infty u^2e^{-u}\,du
=\boxed{\frac2\theta}.
$$
Here $u=\theta x$, and two applications of <integration by parts> give $\int_0^\infty u^2e^{-u}\,du=2\int_0^\infty ue^{-u}\,du=2$. The boundary terms vanish because <exponential decay> dominates these powers.

Put $S=\sum_{i=1}^N x_i$. Since the observations are <independent random variables>, their joint <probability density function> is a product. Therefore the <likelihood function> and <log-likelihood> are
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L(\theta)=\theta^{2N}e^{-\theta S}\prod_{i=1}^N x_i},\qquad
\boxed{\ell(\theta)=2N\log\theta-\theta S+\sum_{i=1}^N\log x_i}.
$$
These expressions assume positive observed values, as occurs with <probability> one under this continuous <Erlang distribution>.

The <score function> and the <observed Fisher information> are
$$
\ell'(\theta)=\frac{2N}{\theta}-S,\qquad
j(\theta)=-\ell''(\theta)=\frac{2N}{\theta^2}>0.
$$
Solving the <score equation> gives the <maximum-likelihood estimate>
$$
\boxed{\widehat\theta=\frac{2N}{S}=\frac2{\bar x}}.
$$
The positive <observed Fisher information> means that the <log-likelihood> is strictly <concave> on $\theta>0$. Its unique <stationary point> is therefore the global maximum. Equivalently, the <score function> changes from positive to negative at this point. At the estimate, $j(\widehat\theta)=S^2/(2N)$.

For observation $j$ of bird $i$ in year $t(i,j)$, a <Gaussian linear mixed model> with crossed <random intercepts> is
$$
Y_{ij}=\beta_0+\beta_1 a_{ij}+u_i+v_{t(i,j)}+\varepsilon_{ij},
\quad
u_i\sim N(0,\tau_b^2),\quad v_t\sim N(0,\tau_y^2),\quad
\varepsilon_{ij}\sim N(0,\sigma^2),
$$
where the $u_i$, $v_t$, and $\varepsilon_{ij}$ are mutually <independent random variables>. Here $a_{ij}$ is age, $\beta_0$ is the population intercept at age zero, and $\beta_1$ is the change in conditional <expected value> per year of age. The $u_i$ capture persistent between-bird variation, the $v_t$ capture shared annual conditions, and the <regression residuals> capture remaining observation-level variation. Two observations from the same bird share <covariance> $\tau_b^2$; two from the same year share $\tau_y^2$, with both contributions present if both group labels agree. The model also assumes the <random effects> are independent of the age covariate.

= Solution

This is the <Nicholson-Bailey model> for a <host>-<parasitoid> interaction with discrete generations. The factor $r$ is the host multiplication factor in the absence of parasitism; $a$ measures effective attack or searching intensity per parasitoid; and $b$ is the number of next-generation parasitoids produced per attacked host, with any survival or unit conversion absorbed into it.

If the number of attacks on a host has a <Poisson distribution> with <expected value> $aP_t$, then the <probability> of no attack is $e^{-aP_t}$. Consequently $e^{-aP_t}$ is the surviving, unparasitized host fraction, and $1-e^{-aP_t}$ is the attacked fraction. The two update equations count host descendants from surviving hosts and parasitoid descendants from attacked hosts, respectively.

= d
{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

At an <equilibrium>, $H=rHe^{-aP}$ and $P=bH(1-e^{-aP})$. Taking $H=0$ forces $P=0$, giving extinction. If $H\ne0$, the first equation gives $e^{-aP}=1/r$ and hence
$$
\boxed{P_*=\frac{\log r}{a},\qquad H_*=
\frac{r\log r}{ab(r-1)}}\qquad(r\ne1).
$$
These are the only isolated candidates obtained from the equations. There is an exceptional parameter case: at $r=1$, $P=0$ and every $H\ge0$ is an <equilibrium>. Thus the assertion of exactly two <equilibria> needs $r\ne1$, and only the positive candidate belongs to the biological state space when $r>1$.

= Solution

Since $a,b>0$, the coexistence value $P_*=(\log r)/a$ is positive exactly when $r>1$. Under that condition, $H_*>0$ as well. Therefore the required condition is

\boxed{r>1}.

For $0<r<1$, the formal nonzero candidate has negative parasitoid density and is not biologically admissible; $r=1$ gives only the host-only line of <equilibria>.

= Solution
{parent=f}

The <Jacobian matrix> of the update map is
$$
J(H,P)=\begin{pmatrix}
r e^{-aP}&-arHe^{-aP}\\
b(1-e^{-aP})&abHe^{-aP}
\end{pmatrix},\qquad
J(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}r&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its <eigenvalues> are $r$ and zero. The extinction <equilibrium> is locally <asymptotically stable> for $0<r<1$ and unstable for $r>1$, by <linear stability analysis> for a <discrete dynamical system>. In the latter case, even a small perturbation along $P=0$ grows as $H_t=r^tH_0$.

At $r=1$, an <eigenvalue> equals one, so the strict linear criterion does not decide stability. Directly, $0\le H_{t+1}\le H_t$ and $0\le P_{t+1}\le bH_t$. Starting sufficiently close to extinction therefore keeps both coordinates close for all time, proving <Lyapunov stability> in the nonnegative state space. It is not <asymptotically stable>, since every nearby $(H,0)$ is another <equilibrium>. This distinction makes the statement “stable when $r>1$ fails” precise.

= Solution

Suppose $r>1$ and set $q=\log r/(r-1)$. Substitution of the positive <equilibrium> into the <Jacobian matrix> gives
$$
J_*=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&-r\log r/[b(r-1)]\\
b(r-1)/r&q
\end{pmatrix},\quad
\operatorname{tr}J_*=1+q,\quad
\det J_*=q+\log r=\frac{r\log r}{r-1}.
$$
Part (b) showed $f(x)<0$ whenever $x\ne1$. In particular,
$$
f(1/r)=\frac{1-r+\log r}{r}<0
$$
gives $\log r<r-1$, whereas
$$
f(r)=r-1-r\log r<0
$$
gives $\det J_*>1$. The latter already violates the <Jury stability criterion> condition $1-\det J_*>0$. More directly, the product of the two <eigenvalues> is greater than one, so at least one has <modulus> greater than one. Therefore the positive <equilibrium> is **unstable**. This is the <instability of the Nicholson-Bailey equilibrium>; parasitoid feedback in this simple model does not stabilize coexistence.

For independent <geometric random variables> supported on $1,2,\ldots$, put $S=\sum_i x_i$. Multiplication of their <probability mass functions> gives the <likelihood function>
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L(p)=p^n(1-p)^{S-n}},\qquad0<p<1.
$$
There are $n$ terminal successes and $S-n$ preceding failures.

= b
{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <beta distribution> <prior density> is proportional to $p^{a-1}(1-p)^{b-1}$. By <Bayes' theorem>, its product with the <likelihood function> gives the <posterior density>
$$
\pi(p\mid x)\propto p^{a+n-1}(1-p)^{b+S-n-1}.
$$
This is again a <beta distribution>, namely
$$
\boxed{p\mid x\sim\operatorname{Beta}(a+n,b+S-n)}.
$$
Thus the beta family is a <conjugate prior> family for the <geometric distribution>. The update is an instance of <beta-geometric conjugacy>: counts of observed successes and failures are added to the two beta parameters.

Using the <expected value> of a <beta distribution>, the <posterior mean> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[p\mid x]=\frac{a+n}{a+b+S}}.
$$
Equivalently, it is the weighted average
$$
\frac{a+b}{a+b+S}\frac{a}{a+b}
+\frac{S}{a+b+S}\frac{n}{S}
$$
of the <prior mean> and the <maximum-likelihood estimate> $n/S$.

**Direct independent sampling is preferable.** The <posterior distribution> is the standard <beta distribution> $\operatorname{Beta}(9,38)$, for which exact <posterior moments> and standard random-number generators are available. For example, draw independent $U\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(9,1)$ and $V\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(38,1)$ with the same rate, and return $U/(U+V)$. This has the required <beta distribution> and avoids <Markov chain Monte Carlo> convergence problems. A correct, converged MCMC method could sample this target, but it is unnecessarily complicated here.

Write $n_k=|C_k|$ and suppose the cluster is nonempty. Expanding the <Euclidean norm> through its <inner product> gives
$$
\|x-z\|^2=\|(x-\bar x_k)-(z-\bar x_k)\|^2
=\|x-\bar x_k\|^2-2(x-\bar x_k)^{\mathsf T}(z-\bar x_k)
+\|z-\bar x_k\|^2.
$$
Sum over $x\in C_k$. The final term does not depend on $x$, so its sum is $n_k\|z-\bar x_k\|^2$, which proves the requested identity. This expansion is the starting point for the <centroid sum-of-squares identity>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The centred observations sum to zero:
$$
\sum_{x\in C_k}(x-\bar x_k)=\sum_{x\in C_k}x-n_k\bar x_k=0.
$$
By <linearity> of the <inner product>,
$$
\sum_{x\in C_k}(x-\bar x_k)^{\mathsf T}(z-\bar x_k)
=\left(\sum_{x\in C_k}(x-\bar x_k)\right)^{\mathsf T}(z-\bar x_k)=0.
$$
This cancellation holds for every $z$.

Combining the first two parts gives the <centroid sum-of-squares identity>
$$
J_k(z)=J_k(\bar x_k)+n_k\|z-\bar x_k\|^2.
$$
The added term is nonnegative and, since $n_k>0$, vanishes exactly at $z=\bar x_k$. Thus the unique minimizing <centroid> in this step of <K-means clustering> is
$$
\boxed{z_k=\bar x_k}.
$$
An empty cluster needs a separate algorithmic convention because its <sample mean> is undefined and its objective is identically zero.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Expanding the squared <Euclidean distances> gives
$$
\|x-z_1\|^2-\|x-z_2\|^2
=-2x^{\mathsf T}(z_1-z_2)+\|z_1\|^2-\|z_2\|^2.
$$
For distinct <centroids>, choose
$$
\boxed{w=2(z_1-z_2),\qquad b=\|z_2\|^2-\|z_1\|^2}.
$$
A point assigned to $C_1$ has the displayed distance difference at most zero and therefore $w^{\mathsf T}x+b\ge0$; assignment to $C_2$ gives the reverse inequality. The separating <hyperplane> is the <perpendicular bisector> of the line segment joining the two <centroids>. Points tied between them lie on that <hyperplane>. This is the <nearest-centroid decision hyperplane>.

The requested nonzero $w$ needs the additional assumption $z_1\ne z_2$. It does not follow from the stated algorithm alone. In one dimension, take $C_1=\{-1,1\}$ and $C_2=\{0\}$, whose <centroids> are both zero. A tie rule that retains previous assignments can leave this configuration after an iteration. The inequalities at $-1,1$ imply $b\ge|w|$, while the inequality at zero implies $b\le0$, forcing $w=0$. Thus the unconditional nonzero-separator claim has this degenerate exception.
