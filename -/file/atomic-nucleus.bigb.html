= Atomic nucleus
{title2=$A=Z+N$}
{wiki}

= Nucleus
{synonym}

The central system of <protons> and <neutrons> in an atom. Its mass number $A$ is its <baryon number> in ordinary nuclear states; $Z$ is the proton number and $N$ the neutron number.

The <nucleus> contains almost all of an atom's <mass> and carries positive <electric charge> $Ze$, where $e$ is the <elementary charge>. The <atomic number> $Z$ identifies the <chemical element>; changing $N$ at fixed $Z$ gives another <isotope>. The <mass number> $A=Z+N$ counts <nucleons>, rather than specifying the exact nuclear <mass>: <nuclear binding energy> lowers the <mass> of a bound system relative to its separated constituents. The simplest <nucleus> is a single <proton>; other <atomic nuclei> can contain many <protons> and <neutrons> bound by the <nuclear force>.

A nuclear <quantum state> has properties such as <spin> and a spatial <nuclear charge distribution>. These are different questions from merely counting <nucleons>. A small-angle <elastic scattering> measurement can reveal a <nuclear charge radius>, while a wider range of <momentum transfer> tests the distribution's shape. The <Hofstadter nuclear electron scattering experiment> made this connection between scattering patterns and nuclear structure experimentally concrete.

= Nuclear matrix element
{parent=Atomic nucleus}
{title2=$\mathcal M=\langle f|\widehat O|i\rangle$}

A transition-operator <matrix element> between specified nuclear states. It combines their many-nucleon wavefunctions with the relevant interaction operator. In <double beta decay>, different assumed mechanisms and nuclear-structure calculations give different <matrix elements>, so converting a half-life bound to a neutrino-mass bound requires that input. A measured <energy> endpoint alone does not determine it.

= Nuclear radius scaling
{parent=Atomic nucleus}
{title2=$R\simeq r_0A^{1/3}$}

The approximate mass-number dependence of a sharp equivalent nuclear radius. Near-constant interior <nucleon> number density gives $A\simeq(4\pi/3)n_0R^3$, hence $R\propto A^{1/3}$. A conventional matter-radius scale is $r_0\simeq1.2\,\mathrm{fm}$, with isotope and definition dependence. A charge-weighted root-mean-square radius is a different observable: for a uniform sphere it is $\sqrt{3/5}R$. Diffuse surfaces and <neutron> skins prevent interpreting the scaling as a universal hard boundary.

= Nuclear excited state
{parent=Atomic nucleus}

A <nuclear excited state> lies above the same <nucleus>'s <ground state> in internal <energy>. <spin>, <parity> and approximate <isospin> help classify it. Single-particle rearrangements, broken pairs and collective deformation/vibration give different excitation patterns and can mix. Below particle-emission thresholds, <gamma decay> and <internal conversion> are important de-excitation routes; an <excitation energy> is not necessarily the <energy> of every emitted <gamma ray> in a cascade.

= Nuclear isomer
{parent=Nuclear excited state}

A <nuclear isomer> is a relatively long-lived metastable nuclear state. Gamma de-excitation can be hindered by a large angular-momentum change, high electromagnetic multipolarity or small overlap with lower states. Its lifetime and decay channels depend on the actual level structure; metastability is not an absolute prohibition on decay.

= Odd-odd nucleus
{parent=Atomic nucleus}

An <atomic nucleus> with odd <proton> number and odd <neutron> number. Both unpaired species can contribute to the total <nuclear spin>; <addition of angular momentum> gives possible couplings, while the <nuclear residual interaction> determines their ordering.

= Nuclear isobar
{synonym}

<Nuclear isobars> are different <nuclides> with the same <mass number> $A$ and different <atomic numbers> $Z$. They differ from <isotopes>, which have fixed $Z$. <Beta decay> connects neighboring <nuclear isobars>.

= Nuclear spin
{parent=Atomic nucleus}
{title2=$\mathbf I$}

The total <angular momentum> of an <atomic nucleus>, including the orbital and intrinsic <spin> contributions of its constituent <nucleons>.

= Nuclear structure
{parent=Atomic nucleus}

= Nuclear level scheme
{parent=Nuclear structure}

A <nuclear level scheme> represents nuclear <energy levels> and transitions among them. <Gamma-ray> <energies> constrain pairwise differences; emission counts and coincidence measurements help resolve different schemes having the same difference multiset.

= Nuclear level population balance
{parent=Nuclear level scheme}
{title2=$F_i=\sum_{j<i}C_{i\to j}-\sum_{k>i}C_{k\to i}$}

For a complete ensemble of downward <nuclear de-excitation> cascades, let $F_i$ be the direct population of excited level $i$ and $C_{i\to j}$ the total number of transitions from $i$ to $j$. Conservation of population gives the displayed outward-minus-inward formula. A negative $F_i$ rules out a proposed scheme under the assumed complete transition inventory. <Gamma-ray> counts equal transition counts only when unreported <internal conversion> and other competing paths can be neglected or corrected. Summing over all excited levels cancels internal transitions and leaves the transitions into the <ground state>.

= Nuclear de-excitation
{parent=Nuclear structure}

<Nuclear de-excitation> is a transition from a higher to a lower <nuclear energy level>. It can transfer energy to an emitted <gamma ray> or to an atomic <electron> through <internal conversion>, among other channels when thresholds permit.

= Internal conversion
{parent=Nuclear de-excitation}
{wiki}

<Internal conversion> transfers nuclear <excitation energy> directly to a bound atomic <electron>, which is emitted, rather than to a real <gamma ray>. Photon counts alone then underestimate the number of nuclear transitions unless the conversion contribution is included.

= Gamma decay
{parent=Nuclear de-excitation}
{title2=$E_\gamma\simeq E_i-E_f$}

<Gamma decay> is electromagnetic <nuclear de-excitation> by <photon> emission. It preserves <mass number> and <atomic number>, while changing the nuclear state. <Angular momentum> and <parity> constrain the allowed radiation multipoles. The <gamma ray> topic is broader than this nuclear process.

= Weisskopf single-particle estimate
{parent=Gamma decay}
{c}

Named for <Victor Weisskopf>, a Weisskopf estimate is a benchmark electromagnetic nuclear transition strength based on a simple single-particle size model. It supplies a scale against which enhancement or hindrance is measured, rather than a universal rate. The partial rate contains both the photon-energy power $E_\gamma^{2L+1}$ and the actual reduced nuclear strength. Electric and magnetic multipoles at different energies cannot be universally ranked by order alone.<Guidelines for Nuclear Structure Evaluators by Murray J. Martin>[Appendix C, printed pp.60–67]{ref}

= Gamma-cascade intensity balance
{parent=Gamma decay}
{title2=$\sum I_{\rm feed}=\sum I_{\rm deexcite}$}

For a prompt nuclear level fed in a steady decay cascade, incoming and outgoing transition populations balance. Quoted <photon> <intensities> must represent efficiency-corrected emission counts; <internal conversion>, alternative <particle decays> and unobserved feeds must either be included or explicitly excluded. Equal <photon> counts suggest a serial cascade, while a feed <intensity> equal to the sum of two branches suggests splitting. <intensities> alone do not determine the chronological order of equal-count lines.

= Nuclear level reconstruction from energy differences
{parent=Gamma-cascade intensity balance}
{title2=$E_{\gamma,i}=E_{u_i}-E_{l_i}$}

Reconstructing a nuclear gamma scheme amounts to assigning measured <photon> energies to differences between ordered level energies. Cascade <energy> sums constrain the graph, while population-flow balances constrain its orientation and feeding. Experimental central energies need not close exactly; a weighted least-squares energy-network fit uses their uncertainties consistently. Multiple graphs can satisfy the same spectrum when coincidence or lifetime information is absent.

= Nuclear gamma-ray multipole selection rules
{parent=Gamma decay}
{title2=$|J_i-J_f|\leq L\leq J_i+J_f,\quad L\geq1$}

A real <photon> emitted in <gamma decay> carries a radiation multipole of integer <angular momentum> $L\geq1$. The triangle rule gives the displayed range. An electric multipole $EL$ requires $\pi_f/\pi_i=(-1)^L$, while a magnetic multipole $ML$ requires $\pi_f/\pi_i=(-1)^{L+1}$. There is no real single-photon $E0$ decay; an electric monopole transition can instead proceed through <internal conversion> or, above threshold, pair creation. In the long-wavelength regime $kR\ll1$, increasing $L$ usually strongly suppresses the rate, but nuclear transition matrix elements and collective enhancement can make an allowed lower multipole weaker than a higher one.

= Missing gamma lines and matrix-element hindrance
{parent=Nuclear gamma-ray multipole selection rules}

An absent otherwise-fast gamma transition can support a <spin> assignment that forbids it. It is not an absolute <selection rule>: a nuclear <reduced matrix element> may be small even when <angular momentum> and <parity> allow the mode. Meaningful limits require detection thresholds, branching normalization and competing-channel information. Assuming ordinary unhindered strengths can yield a useful conventional assignment, but that assumption must be distinguished from strict observed-line constraints.

= Spin-reflection ambiguity in a gamma cascade
{parent=Missing gamma lines and matrix-element hindrance}

If a cascade determines only absolute <spin> differences relative to a known reference <spin>, reflection of all inferred <spins> around that reference can preserve the same multipole pattern. Nonnegative-spin constraints, independently known <spins>, angular correlations or <polarization> measurements may break the ambiguity. A set of line energies and lowest-multipole labels need not do so.

= Nuclear recoil
{parent=Atomic nucleus}

Movement of an <atomic nucleus> after it receives <momentum>, for example in a collision. A finite nuclear <mass> requires sharing <kinetic energy> as well as <momentum>; the fixed-target approximation neglects this motion.

= Nuclear-recoil quenching factor
{parent=Nuclear recoil}
{title2=$Q(E)=Y_{\rm nuclear}(E)/Y_{\rm electron}(E)$}

The relative light or charge yield of a <nuclear recoil> compared with an <electron recoil> at the same deposited <energy> in a specified material and field configuration. It connects an electron-equivalent <calibration> scale with a recoil-energy scale. It can depend on <energy> and measurement conditions; assuming a constant or extrapolating below <calibration> changes light-WIMP interpretations. It does not mean <energy> conservation fails: <energy> can enter channels not measured by that readout.

= Nuclear-recoil kinematics of WIMP scattering
{parent=Nuclear recoil}
{title2=$E_{R,\max}=2\mu_{\chi N}^2v^2/m_N$}

For nonrelativistic <elastic scattering> of a <WIMP> of <mass> $m_\chi$ from a stationary nucleus of <mass> $m_N$, the <reduced mass> is $\mu_{\chi N}=m_\chi m_N/(m_\chi+m_N)$. In the centre-of-momentum description, a <scattering angle> $\vartheta$ gives <momentum> transfer squared $q^2=2\mu_{\chi N}^2v^2(1-\cos\vartheta)$. The target <kinetic energy> is $E_R=q^2/(2m_N)$, giving the displayed maximum at backscattering. In SI units, using <masses> in kilograms and <velocity> in metres per second gives joules. Low recoil <energy> and a falling recoil spectrum make threshold and response <calibration> important for <dark-matter direct detection>.

= Nuclear elastic electron scattering kinematics
{parent=Nuclear recoil}
{title2=$E'=E/[1+2E\sin^2(\theta/2)/(Mc^2)]$}

An <elastic scattering> event leaves the nuclear internal <quantum state> unchanged while transferring <energy> to its motion. Let a target of <mass> $M$ initially be at rest. Neglect the <electron> rest <mass>, but retain relativistic target recoil. <Conservation of momentum> and <conservation of relativistic energy> give
$$
\hbar^2c^2q^2=E^2+E'^2-2EE'\cos\theta,
\qquad
(Mc^2+E-E')^2=M^2c^4+\hbar^2c^2q^2.
$$
Eliminating $q$ yields $Mc^2(E-E')=EE'(1-\cos\theta)$ and hence
$$
E'=\frac{E}{1+\dfrac{2E}{Mc^2}\sin^2(\theta/2)},\qquad
T_R=E-E'.
$$
The static relation $q=2E\sin(\theta/2)/(\hbar c)$ follows only when $E/(Mc^2)$ is negligible. For small recoil, $T_R\simeq\hbar^2q^2/(2M)$; this last approximation is nonrelativistic for the recoiling <nucleus>, not for the incident <electron>. The invariant spacelike transfer, expressed in energy-squared units, is
$$
Q^2=\hbar^2c^2q^2-(E-E')^2=4EE'\sin^2(\theta/2),
$$
so $Q/(\hbar c)$ and the spatial inverse length $q$ coincide only in the static limit. Recoil kinematics alone does not correct the static <differential scattering cross-section>: a finite-mass target also changes its kinematic prefactor and the appropriate current matrix elements.

= Alpha particle
{parent=Atomic nucleus}
{title2=$A=4,\quad Z=2,\quad J=I=0$}
{wiki}

The helium-four <atomic nucleus>, containing two <protons> and two <neutrons>. Its ground-state <spin> and <isospin> are zero. A quantized <cubic four-Skyrmion> supplies a corresponding nuclear state in the soliton description.

= Deuteron
{parent=Atomic nucleus}
{title2=$A=2,\quad J=1,\quad I=0$}

The bound <atomic nucleus> containing one <proton> and one <neutron>. Its ground-state <spin> is one and its isospin is zero. The appropriately quantized <toroidal two-Skyrmion> models these quantum numbers.

= Deuteron D-state admixture
{parent=Deuteron}
{title2=$P_D=\int_0^\infty |u_D(r)|^2dr$}

The <deuteron> has a dominant $\,{}^3S_1$ component and a smaller $\,{}^3D_1$ component coupled by the <nuclear tensor force>. The <electric quadrupole moment> rules out a purely isotropic <S wave (quantum scattering)> spatial state. A D-state probability depends on the chosen nuclear Hamiltonian and current approximation; it is not an independently model-free observable.

= Nuclear binding energy
{parent=Atomic nucleus}
{title2=$E_{\rm bind}=(Zm_p+Nm_n-M_A)c^2$}
{wiki}

The positive energy needed to separate a bound <atomic nucleus> into its constituent <protons> and <neutrons>. It is the mass defect times $c^2$. In a <Skyrmion model of a nucleus>, both separated nucleon masses and the multi-soliton state require a consistent quantization and calibration.

= Liquid-drop model of the nucleus
{parent=Nuclear binding energy}

= Liquid-drop model
{synonym}

A collective nuclear model that treats the nucleus as a nearly uniform fluid drop with short-range cohesive binding, surface energy and electrostatic self-energy. The <semi-empirical mass formula> incorporates these contributions, together with asymmetry and pairing terms, to describe broad binding trends. Discrete <nuclear shell> structure requires additional physics.

= Mass defect
{parent=Nuclear binding energy}
{title2=$\Delta m$}

A difference between the mass of a bound system and an appropriate sum of reference constituent masses. For a nuclear transformation, the initial-minus-final mass difference gives the available <Q value> after multiplication by $c^2$, with atomic electrons counted consistently when using neutral-atom masses.

= Binding energy per nucleon
{parent=Nuclear binding energy}
{title2=$B/A$}

<Binding energy per nucleon> divides conventional positive <nuclear binding energy> by <mass number>. Typical well-bound nuclei have values of order $8\,\mathrm{MeV}$, and the broad maximum lies near iron-group nuclei. A mass correction added to free-nucleon masses instead has the opposite sign, $E_{\mathrm{corr}}/A=-B/A$.

= Semi-empirical mass formula
{parent=Nuclear binding energy}
{wiki}

The <semi-empirical mass formula> approximates <nuclear binding energy> using volume, surface, electrostatic, neutron-proton asymmetry, and <nuclear pairing> contributions. At fixed <mass number>, its quadratic dependence on <atomic number> gives a <nuclear mass parabola>. It describes broad trends and does not resolve individual <nuclear shell> effects.

= Semi-empirical mass errors can change beta-decay thresholds
{parent=Semi-empirical mass formula}

<Beta-decay> <Q values> depend on small differences between much larger atomic <masses>. A smooth <semi-empirical mass formula> can therefore approximate absolute masses reasonably while predicting the wrong sign for a MeV-scale decay threshold. <Nuclear pairing> explains offset even-even and odd-odd mass branches, but does not guarantee that every specific even-even neighbor lies below the intervening odd-odd nucleus. Measured masses or additional <nuclear shell> corrections are needed for quantitative threshold tests near a model's residual error scale.

= Nuclear Coulomb energy
{parent=Semi-empirical mass formula}
{title2=$E_C\simeq a_C Z^2/A^{1/3}$}

<Nuclear Coulomb energy> is the positive electrostatic mass-energy contribution from proton repulsion in a <nucleus>. A uniform-charge model gives scaling $Z^2/R$, and approximately constant nuclear density gives $R\propto A^{1/3}$. It reduces conventional positive <nuclear binding energy>.

= Uniform-sphere Coulomb displacement energy
{parent=Nuclear Coulomb energy}
{title2=$\Delta E_C=3\alpha\hbar c\,\Delta(Z^2)/(5R)$}

The electrostatic self-energy of a uniformly charged sphere of sharp radius $R$ is $3Z^2\alpha\hbar c/(5R)$. For mirror nuclei, subtract the changed neutron/proton rest-mass contribution from their nuclear mass difference before identifying the residual with a <nuclear Coulomb energy> difference. The inferred $R$ is a sharp-sphere radius; the corresponding <nuclear charge radius> defined as an rms radius is $\sqrt{3/5}R$. Replacing $Z^2$ by $Z(Z-1)$ removes a proton self-interaction approximation and shifts the estimate.

= Nuclear mass parabola
{parent=Semi-empirical mass formula}
{title2=$M(A,Z)\simeq a(A)Z^2+b(A)Z+c(A)$}

At fixed <mass number> $A$, the smooth <semi-empirical mass formula> makes the atomic <mass> approximately quadratic and convex in <atomic number> $Z$. Neighboring <nuclear isobars> undergo energetically allowed <beta decay> toward a discrete local minimum, subject to the electron-mass threshold for <beta-plus decay>. For odd $A$ there is one smooth branch; for even $A$, <nuclear pairing> splits the even-even and odd-odd branches. A local minimum against a single change of $Z$ need not be stable against <double beta decay>.

= Nuclear charge distribution
{parent=Atomic nucleus}
{title2=$\rho_{\mathrm{ch}}(\mathbf r)$}

A spatial <charge density> describing the <electric charge> inside an <atomic nucleus>. In a static description centered on the <nucleus>, normalize it by
$$
\int_{\mathbb R^3}\rho_{\mathrm{ch}}(\mathbf r)\,d^3r=Ze,
$$
where $Z$ is the <atomic number> and $e>0$ the <elementary charge>. A <nuclear charge distribution> is an average over a nuclear <quantum state>, rather than a photograph of immobile <protons>. Even when the average has <spherical symmetry>, individual <nucleons> need not sit on spherical shells. The <proton charge radius> and the internal <charge density> of the <neutron> also contribute: a distribution of pointlike proton centers is an approximation to a <nuclear charge distribution>, not its definition.

The experimental attraction is that an <electron> probes <electric charge> through the <electromagnetic interaction>, without itself feeling the <nuclear force>. Varying its <momentum transfer> changes the spatial scale tested. Long wavelengths mainly constrain a <nuclear charge radius>; shorter wavelengths can distinguish a diffuse edge from a sharper one through <diffraction>. Recovering the entire <nuclear charge distribution> from a finite range of noisy scattering data remains an <inverse problem>.

= Two-parameter Fermi nuclear charge distribution
{parent=Nuclear charge distribution}
{title2=$\rho(r)=\rho_0/[1+e^{(r-c)/a}]$}

A common spherical model of a heavy <nuclear charge distribution> uses a half-density radius $c$ and surface diffuseness $a>0$. The normalization $\rho_0$ is fixed by $4\pi\int_0^\infty r^2\rho(r)\,dr=Ze$. The 90%-to-10% surface thickness is $t=4a\ln3$: solve $\rho/\rho_0=0.9$ and $0.1$ and subtract the two radii. Typical fits have an approximately constant surface thickness and a radius scaling roughly as the cube root of <mass number>. This is a spatial density model, not an assertion of a thermal <Fermi-Dirac distribution> for nuclear positions.

{parent=Nuclear charge distribution}

<Robert Hofstadter>'s Stanford installation selected <electrons> up to $190\,\mathrm{MeV}$. <Magnetic spectrometer> scans counted shielded Lucite <Cherenkov radiation> pulses per monitor charge. <Carbon-12> at $187\,\mathrm{MeV}$ and $80^\circ$ resolved $185.1\,\mathrm{MeV}$ <elastic scattering> and $180.7\,\mathrm{MeV}$ <inelastic scattering> ($4.43\,\mathrm{MeV}$ excitation). Calibration used forward-beam energy, <hydrogen> <nuclear recoil> and <carbon-12> <energy levels>. Closed entrance slits checked background.<Electron Scattering and Nuclear Structure by Robert Hofstadter>[§III(b)(1), pp. 222–223, Fig. 11; §IV(a), pp. 227–231, Figs. 14–17]{ref}

\Image[nuclear-electron-scattering-beam-and-spectrometer.png]
{title=Selecting a scattering angle and an outgoing electron momentum}
{description=Original functional schematic: separate scattering and bending planes.}

The key distinction is between an <electron> counter and an <energy> measurement. Here the counter registers particles admitted by the <magnetic spectrometer>; changing its <magnetic field> selects another outgoing <momentum>. A peak is therefore reconstructed from a scan, rather than read as one pulse whose height equals the scattered <electron> <energy>. Dividing counts by monitored incident <electric charge> compensates for changes in beam intensity. A peak's height alone is insufficient: its area and the instrument's accepted <momentum> interval determine the selected yield.

An <inelastic scattering> peak corresponds to a different final nuclear <quantum state>, so including it in the <elastic scattering> yield would answer a different structural question. Conversely, finite <energy> resolution can hide sufficiently nearby excitations beneath an apparent <elastic scattering> peak. Background subtraction, target-loss corrections, angular acceptance and counter efficiency all enter before counts become a <differential scattering cross-section>. A dip in uncorrected counts is therefore not automatically a zero of a <nuclear charge form factor>.

= Nuclear charge radius
{parent=Nuclear charge distribution}
{title2=$r_{\mathrm{ch}}=\sqrt{\langle r^2\rangle_{\mathrm{ch}}}$}

The <root mean square> radius of a <nuclear charge distribution>, referred to its center. Define its charge-weighted second moment by
$$
\langle r^2\rangle_{\mathrm{ch}}=\frac1{Ze}\int r^2\rho_{\mathrm{ch}}(\mathbf r)\,d^3r.
$$
For a spherical <charge density> with finite fourth moment, insert the <Taylor series> $\sin u/u=1-u^2/6+O(u^4)$ into the <nuclear charge form factor>:
$$
F(q)=1-\frac{q^2}{6}\langle r^2\rangle_{\mathrm{ch}}+O(q^4),
\qquad
\langle r^2\rangle_{\mathrm{ch}}=-6\left.\frac{dF}{d(q^2)}\right|_{q=0}.
$$
Thus a radius is a low-<momentum transfer> slope, not necessarily the position of a sharp edge. In this real static model,
$$
|F(q)|^2=1-\frac{q^2}{3}\langle r^2\rangle_{\mathrm{ch}}+O(q^4).
$$
The factor of two between the slopes of $F$ and $|F|^2$ matters when interpreting a <differential scattering cross-section>. A <proton charge radius> is the corresponding size concept for the <proton>; the spatial arrangement of many <nucleons> introduces an additional scale in a heavier <atomic nucleus>.

= Nuclear charge form factor
{parent=Nuclear charge distribution}
{title2=$F(\mathbf q)$}

In the static <charge density> model, define the normalized <Fourier transform>
$$
F(\mathbf q)=\frac1{Ze}\int\rho_{\mathrm{ch}}(\mathbf r)e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}\,d^3r,
\qquad F(\mathbf 0)=1.
$$
Here $\mathbf q=(\mathbf p-\mathbf p')/\hbar$ is the spatial <momentum transfer> divided by the reduced <Planck constant>: $q=|\mathbf q|$ has units of inverse length. Physical <momentum transfer> is $\hbar\mathbf q$. We keep $\hbar$ and the <speed of light> $c$ explicit; compute $q=|\mathbf p-\mathbf p'|c/(\hbar c)$, expressing the numerator in $\mathrm{MeV}$ and $\hbar c$ in $\mathrm{MeV}\,\mathrm{fm}$, to obtain $q$ in inverse <femtometres>. The spatial $q$ is distinct from the invariant <four-momentum> transfer used in <relativistic scattering cross-sections>.

For <spherical symmetry>, take the polar axis along $\mathbf q$ and use <spherical coordinates>. With $\mu$ the cosine of the polar angle,
$$
\int_{-1}^{1}e^{iqr\mu}\,d\mu=2\frac{\sin(qr)}{qr},
\qquad
F(q)=\frac{4\pi}{Ze}\int_0^\infty r^2\rho_{\mathrm{ch}}(r)\frac{\sin(qr)}{qr}\,dr.
$$
Every radial shell contributes an amplitude. At $q=0$ all shells add with the same sign; at larger $q$ their contributions can cancel. This cancellation is the origin of a <diffraction> minimum in the <nuclear charge form factor>, rather than an absence of <electric charge> in the <nucleus>.

The static <Fourier transform> interpretation is appropriate for the nuclear model and kinematic regime specified here. A fully relativistic composite system requires frame and current conventions; its electric form factor is not universally the transform of a frame-independent three-dimensional <charge density>.

= Uniform-sphere nuclear charge form factor
{parent=Nuclear charge form factor}
{title2=$F(q)=3[\sin(qR)-qR\cos(qR)]/(qR)^3$}

Consider an ideal <nuclear charge distribution> that is constant inside a ball of radius $R>0$ and zero outside:
$$
\rho_{\mathrm{ch}}(r)=\begin{cases}3Ze/(4\pi R^3),&0\le r\le R,\\0,&r>R.\end{cases}
$$
This is a deliberately sharp-edged model. Substitution into the spherical <nuclear charge form factor> gives, for $q\ne0$,
$$
F(q)=\frac3{R^3}\int_0^R r^2\frac{\sin(qr)}{qr}\,dr
=\frac3{qR^3}\left[-\frac{r\cos(qr)}q+\frac{\sin(qr)}{q^2}\right]_0^R
=3\frac{\sin(qR)-qR\cos(qR)}{(qR)^3}.
$$
At $q=0$ take the continuous limit $F(0)=1$. Direct radial integration gives $\langle r^2\rangle_{\mathrm{ch}}=3R^2/5$, and the <Taylor series> provides an independent check:
$$
F(q)=1-\frac{(qR)^2}{10}+\frac{(qR)^4}{280}+O((qR)^6).
$$
The first positive zero solves $\sin x-x\cos x=0$, equivalently $\tan x=x$, with $x=qR\simeq4.49340946$. Consequently the first <diffraction> minimum moves toward smaller <momentum transfer> as $R$ increases.

For comparison, a smooth <Gaussian function> <charge density> with the same <nuclear charge radius> is
$$
\rho_G(r)=\frac{Ze}{(2\pi\sigma^2)^{3/2}}e^{-r^2/(2\sigma^2)},\qquad
\sigma^2=R^2/5,\qquad F_G(q)=e^{-q^2R^2/10}.
$$
The <Fourier transform of a Gaussian> stays positive and has no zero. Both models have the same leading low-$q$ curvature, but they disagree at higher <momentum transfer>: at $qR=2$, the sphere gives $F\simeq0.653097$ and the <Gaussian function> gives $F_G\simeq0.670320$. Measuring only a <nuclear charge radius> cannot distinguish these two <nuclear charge distributions>.

= Nuclear elastic electron scattering cross-section
{parent=Nuclear charge form factor}
{title2=$d\sigma/d\Omega\simeq(d\sigma/d\Omega)_{\mathrm{point}}|F(q)|^2$}

For an ultrarelativistic <electron> scattered elastically by a static, spherical, spin-zero <atomic nucleus>, a plane-wave <Born approximation> retains a single electromagnetic interaction. The <Fourier transform> of its <Coulomb potential> is proportional to $ZeF(q)/q^2$. Relative to the same point charge, the scattering amplitude is therefore multiplied by $F(q)$ and the <differential scattering cross-section> by $|F(q)|^2$:
$$
\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\simeq
\left(\frac{Z\alpha\hbar c}{2E}\right)^2
\frac{\cos^2(\theta/2)}{\sin^4(\theta/2)}|F(q)|^2,
\qquad q\simeq\frac{2E}{\hbar c}\sin\frac\theta2.
$$
Here $E\gg m_ec^2$ is the incident total <electron> <energy>, $\theta$ the <scattering angle>, $d\Omega$ a <solid angle> element, and $\alpha$ the <fine-structure constant>. The cosine factor already accounts for the projectile's <spin>; a spinless projectile would give a different point-charge factor. The formula neglects <nuclear recoil>, electron rest-mass corrections, <radiative corrections> and <Coulomb distortion in electron scattering>. The latter can be substantial at large $Z\alpha$: electron waves then require treatment in the target's <Coulomb potential>, rather than undistorted plane waves.

Nonzero target <spin> can introduce magnetic and additional electric contributions, so division by this point-charge expression does not generally isolate one charge form factor for a <proton> or <deuteron>. Even for spin zero, finite <energy> resolution, recoil and <Coulomb distortion in electron scattering> can change a measured minimum. A zero of the uniform-sphere model is a clear illustration of amplitude cancellation, not a universal prediction of a zero experimental counting rate.

= Nuclear shell model
{parent=Atomic nucleus}
{wiki}

Why should adding one <neutron> be easy for one <atomic nucleus> and much less favorable for its neighbor? In the <nuclear shell model>, <nucleons> occupy discrete <energy levels> in a <nuclear mean field>. Filling all available states below a sufficiently large gap produces a <nuclear shell closure>. This connects <nuclear separation energies> with the counting of <quantum states>, and connects the <spin> of an odd <atomic nucleus> with its last unpaired <nucleon>.

The starting point is an approximate <Hamiltonian operator>
$$
H=\sum_{i=1}^{A}\left[\frac{\mathbf p_i^2}{2m_i}+U_i(r_i)-a_i(r_i)\frac{\mathbf L_i\cdot\mathbf S_i}{\hbar^2}\right]+V_{\mathrm{res}},
$$
where $U_i$ is a confining <potential energy>, $a_i$ specifies <nuclear spin-orbit splitting>, and $V_{\mathrm{res}}$ contains interactions left out of the <nuclear mean field>. This is an effective organization of the nuclear <many-body problem>, rather than a derivation of the <nuclear force>. In a spherical approximation, <addition of angular momentum> combines the <orbital angular momentum> $l$ with <spin> $s=1/2$ to give $j=l\pm1/2$; for $l=0$, only $j=1/2$ exists.

A level labelled $nlj$ has $2j+1$ magnetic substates $m_j=-j,-j+1,\ldots,j$. The <Pauli exclusion principle> permits one <neutron> in each such state and, separately, one <proton>. Thus a $j=7/2$ level holds eight <neutrons>, or eight <protons>; it does not hold sixteen identical <neutrons>. Here $n=1,2,\ldots$ counts radial levels of the same $l$, starting with the lowest; it is not the hydrogenic principal <quantum number>. The letters $s,p,d,f,g,h,i$ mean $l=0,1,2,3,4,5,6$.

= Nuclear particle-hole excitation
{parent=Nuclear shell model}

Promoting a nucleon from an occupied orbital to an unoccupied orbital creates a particle-hole configuration. Its possible angular momenta come from coupling the particle and hole, and its <parity> relative to the reference configuration is the product of their orbital <parities>. Residual interactions split and mix configurations; a measured nuclear <excitation energy> is not generally just an uncorrected independent-orbital spacing.

= Proton hole
{parent=Nuclear shell model}

A missing <proton> relative to a filled <nuclear shell>. In a closed-core approximation a single hole in a $j$ orbital has the same total <angular momentum> $j$ and orbital <parity> as the removed particle. For example, removing a $p_{1/2}$ <proton> from a spin-zero closed core gives $J^P=1/2^-$.

= Nuclear residual interaction
{parent=Nuclear shell model}
{title2=$V_{\mathrm{res}}$}

The interaction remaining after a chosen <nuclear mean field> is subtracted from the nuclear <Hamiltonian operator>. It can split configurations having the same occupied one-particle levels but different coupled <angular momentum>. Thus the ordering of nuclear orbitals alone does not determine every <odd-odd nucleus> ground-state <spin>.

= Single-particle nuclear magnetic moment
{parent=Nuclear shell model}
{title2=$\mu=g_jj\mu_N$}

In the simplest <nuclear shell model> of an odd-<mass number> <nucleus>, paired <nucleons> contribute zero total <angular momentum> and <magnetic dipole moment>. The last unpaired <nucleon> supplies the nuclear <spin> $j$ and the stretched-state moment $\mu=g_jj\mu_N$, where $g_j$ is the <Landé g-factor> and $\mu_N$ the <nuclear magneton>.

Using <addition of angular momentum> with $s=1/2$ gives
$$
g_j=g_\ell+(g_s-g_\ell)\frac{j(j+1)-\ell(\ell+1)+3/4}{2j(j+1)}.
$$
For $j=\ell+1/2$, this reduces to $\mu/\mu_N=g_\ell\ell+g_s/2$. For $j=\ell-1/2$, it gives
$$
\frac\mu{\mu_N}=\frac{2\ell-1}{2\ell+1}\left[g_\ell(\ell+1)-\frac{g_s}{2}\right].
$$
The free-nucleon orbital factors are $g_\ell=1$ for a <proton> and $0$ for a <neutron>. Residual nuclear interactions and configuration mixing can shift measured moments away from this ideal prediction. A single hole in an otherwise closed $j$ shell has the same stretched-state moment as a particle in that shell: removing the $m_j=-j$ particle leaves total $J_z=j$ and moment $g_jj\mu_N$.

= Nuclear shell
{parent=Nuclear shell model}

A nuclear shell groups single-particle <energy levels> for <protons> or <neutrons> in a <nuclear mean field>. Filling all states below a sufficiently large gap gives a <nuclear shell closure>. These shells describe quantum-state occupation rather than thin spatial layers. Their discrete filling effects explain departures from the smooth <semi-empirical mass formula>.

= Nuclear mean field
{parent=Nuclear shell model}

An effective one-<nucleon> <potential energy> representing the average influence of the other <nucleons>. The <nuclear shell model> treats motion in this field first, then includes residual interactions. The field experienced by a <proton> also includes the repulsive <Coulomb potential>; <proton> and <neutron> <energy levels> need not have identical spacings.

= Magic number
{parent=Nuclear shell model}
{wiki=Magic_number_(physics)}

= Nuclear magic number
{synonym}

A <proton> number $Z$ or <neutron> number $N$ associated with a pronounced <nuclear shell closure>. The traditional sequence is
$$
2,\quad8,\quad20,\quad28,\quad50,\quad82,\quad126.
$$
These count either species separately, rather than their sum $A=Z+N$. Calling a number magic summarizes a structural pattern; it does not promise that every <isotope> at that number is equally stable.

\Image[nuclear-magic-shell-capacities.png]
{title=Counting states between traditional nuclear shell closures}
{description=Original capacity diagram for a chosen spherical level scheme. Each orbital contributes $2j+1$ states for one species. Rows show groups and cumulative totals, not measured energies or a universal ordering within each group.}

For example, in the chosen scheme the $20$-particle core plus the eight substates of $1f_{7/2}$ gives $28$. The next group contributes $4+6+2+10=22$, giving $50$. The following two groups contribute $8+6+4+2+12=32$ and $10+8+6+4+2+14=44$, giving $82$ and $126$. Counting verifies the capacities once the grouping has been chosen; it does not establish the physical <energy> gaps that justify that grouping.

= Nuclear shell closure
{parent=Magic number}

Filling the accessible one-species <nuclear shell model> states below a substantial <energy> gap. A closed spherical $j$ level has all $m_j$ values occupied, so their sum is zero. In the independent-particle picture, a closed core therefore contributes zero total <angular momentum>; a single particle outside it, or a single missing particle inside it, supplies the leading <spin> assignment. Residual interactions can change this simple picture.

The <three-dimensional isotropic harmonic oscillator> alone has one-species shell capacities $(q+1)(q+2)$ for oscillator index $q=0,1,\ldots$, including <spin>. Summing gives closures $2,8,20,40,70,112,\ldots$, rather than the complete traditional <magic number> sequence. Strong <nuclear spin-orbit splitting> can separate a high-$j$ level from its former neighbors. Realistic radial <potential energies> and residual interactions also matter: a chosen oscillator plus an arbitrary single constant is not a demonstrated explanation of every real nuclear gap.

Traditional <nuclear shell closures> can weaken along <isotope> chains far from stability, as in neutron-rich <magnesium>.<Magic numbers disappear and expand area of nuclear deformation by RIKEN>[20 November 2013, magnesium shell weakening]{ref}

The relevant test is the actual <energy> gap and the correlated nuclear <quantum states>, rather than membership in an immutable numerical list.

= Nuclear spin-orbit splitting
{parent=Nuclear shell model}
{title2=$j=l\pm\tfrac12$}

The splitting of one-<nucleon> <energy levels> by their <spin-orbit interaction>. With $\mathbf J=\mathbf L+\mathbf S$, the identity $2\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S=\mathbf J^2-\mathbf L^2-\mathbf S^2$ gives, in a state of definite $l,s=1/2,j$,
$$
\langle\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S\rangle
=\frac{\hbar^2}{2}\left[j(j+1)-l(l+1)-\frac34\right]
=\begin{cases}
\hbar^2l/2,&j=l+1/2,\\
-\hbar^2(l+1)/2,&j=l-1/2.
\end{cases}
$$
The second branch requires $l\ge1$. For the illustrative <Hamiltonian operator> $H_{\mathrm{so}}=-a\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S/\hbar^2$ with $a>0$, the shifts are $-al/2$ and $+a(l+1)/2$. Thus the strong negative coefficient lowers the aligned $j=l+1/2$ branch. Its capacity is $2l+2$; the raised partner holds $2l$. Together they still hold $4l+2=2(2l+1)$ identical <nucleons>: the interaction rearranges states rather than creating them.

\Image[nuclear-spin-orbit-splitting-and-occupancy.png]
{title=A negative spin-orbit term lowers the larger-j nuclear level}
{description=Original isolated $d$-doublet example with $l=2$, $a>0$, and seven identical nucleons. Six occupy $d_{5/2}$ and one occupies $d_{3/2}$. The circles count magnetic substates; they do not represent spatial particle positions. Energies are chosen model shifts in units of $a$.}

In this example the $d_{5/2}$ and $d_{3/2}$ shifts are $-a$ and $3a/2$, respectively, so the gap is $5a/2$. The capacity-weighted mean shift vanishes:
$$
6(-a)+4(3a/2)=0.
$$
This is a useful check on both the sign and state counting. A seventh identical particle cannot occupy another substate of the already full lower level. In a real <atomic nucleus>, other radial and <orbital angular momentum> levels can interleave with this doublet, and a radial coupling need not give the same effective $a$ for every orbital.

= Nuclear separation energy
{parent=Nuclear shell model}

The <energy> needed to remove specified <nucleons> from a bound <atomic nucleus> in its <ground state>. It compares neighboring nuclear <masses>, and so can reveal a <nuclear shell closure> more clearly than a smoothly varying total <nuclear binding energy>.

= Proton separation energy
{parent=Nuclear separation energy}
{title2=$S_p=[M(A-1,Z-1)+m_p-M(A,Z)]c^2$}

With bare nuclear <masses>, the <proton separation energy> is the energy needed to remove a <proton>. A negative value means the daughter plus proton has lower rest <energy>. Proton emission can still have a finite lifetime because of a Coulomb or centrifugal barrier.

= Nuclear drip line
{parent=Nuclear separation energy}
{wiki}

A <nuclear drip line> marks a boundary beyond which one- or few-nucleon emission becomes energetically allowed. One- and two-particle <nuclear separation energies> can give distinct boundaries because of <nuclear pairing>.

= Neutron drip line
{parent=Nuclear drip line}

The <neutron drip line> is the neutron-rich boundary at which one- or two-neutron emission becomes energetically possible. <Neutrons> beyond that boundary are not stably bound; the absence of a Coulomb barrier often makes their emission prompt, subject to angular-momentum barriers.

= Proton drip line
{parent=Nuclear drip line}

The <proton drip line> is the proton-rich boundary at which one- or two-proton emission becomes energetically possible. It is governed by <proton separation energies>, rather than the energetics of <beta decay> alone.

= Neutron separation energy
{parent=Nuclear separation energy}
{title2=$S_n(Z,N)=B(Z,N)-B(Z,N-1)$}

Writing positive <nuclear binding energy> as $B(Z,N)$ and nuclear <mass> as $M(Z,N)$,
$$
S_n(Z,N)=\left[M(Z,N-1)+m_n-M(Z,N)\right]c^2
=B(Z,N)-B(Z,N-1).
$$
The two-<neutron> <nuclear separation energy> is $S_{2n}(Z,N)=B(Z,N)-B(Z,N-2)$. In an independent-particle example, let the last occupied <energy level> have $\epsilon_-=-8u$ and the next empty level $\epsilon_+=-3u$, for a chosen positive <energy> unit $u$. With an unchanged core and no residual interactions, removal from the filled level costs $8u$, whereas removing the first particle added above it costs $3u$: the drop $5u$ equals the one-particle gap. These are invented model inputs, not measured nuclear values. <Nuclear pairing>, rearrangement of the <nuclear mean field>, and correlations complicate that identification in real <masses>; a finite difference of <nuclear binding energies> is not automatically a bare one-particle gap.

= Nuclear pairing
{parent=Nuclear shell model}

Correlations that favor pairs of like <nucleons> coupled to zero total <angular momentum> in the simplest spherical picture. They help explain why an unpaired <nucleon> often dominates an odd nucleus's <spin>, and why neighboring <nuclear separation energies> show an odd-even staggering. <Nuclear pairing> belongs to the residual-interaction physics beyond merely filling independent <energy levels>. Mixing different occupations and deformation can also be necessary; the closed-core picture is most useful near suitable <nuclear shell closures>.

= Empirical evidence for nuclear shells
{parent=Nuclear shell model}

<Isotope> abundance, <nuclear separation energies>, <spin> and <nuclear magnetic moments> supply complementary tests: abundance concerns which <atomic nuclei> survive and accumulate, removal <energy> concerns binding, and magnetic properties test the <angular momentum> assignments. Agreement across these observables is more informative than fitting one list of numbers.

In her 1963 lecture, <Maria Goeppert Mayer> recalled excess abundances at $N=50$ and $82$. She contrasted the strong binding of the last <neutron> in <oxygen> <isotope> ${}^{16}\mathrm O$ with the weaker extra <neutron> in <oxygen> <isotope> ${}^{17}\mathrm O$, and displayed <beta decay> <energies> near $N=20$ and $50$. These are compiled empirical patterns, rather than readings from an apparatus she built.<The Shell Model by Maria Goeppert Mayer>[§2, p. 21; Figs. 2–3, p. 23]{ref}

Her 1949 letter compared observed <spins> and <nuclear magnetic moments> with orbital assignments. For example, <lead> <isotope> ${}^{207}\mathrm{Pb}$ was assigned a $p_{1/2}$ neutron hole near $N=126$, whereas <bismuth>'s odd <proton> began the next shell as $h_{9/2}$. The moments tested orbital character as well as <spin>.<On Closed Shells in Nuclei. II by Maria Goeppert Mayer>[p. 1970, discussion and Table II]{ref}

A useful comparison holds $Z$ fixed when comparing neighboring <isotopes>, and examines odd-even staggering separately from a shell discontinuity. Abundance additionally depends on production and decay history. Neither the abundance pattern nor a successful <spin> assignment directly images orbiting particles; neither fixes a unique <nuclear mean field>. The experimental techniques themselves include <mass spectrometry> and <nuclear magnetic resonance>, whose preparation and readout differ. The <Hofstadter nuclear electron scattering experiment> separately constrains nuclear size and <nuclear charge distributions>, rather than measuring these occupancies.

= Neutron-rich magnesium shell-closure test
{parent=Empirical evidence for nuclear shells}

A 2013 <RIKEN> experiment used a fast <calcium> beam on <beryllium>, selected radioactive <aluminium> and <silicon> fragments, then struck <carbon> to produce <magnesium> <isotopes> with $N=22,24,26$. Emitted <gamma rays> supplied excitation <energies>; comparison across these <isotopes> supported deformation extending beyond the $N=20$ region toward $28$. This tests the limits of traditional <magic numbers>, not merely their arithmetic. The inferred shape depends on nuclear-structure interpretation of the <energy levels>, rather than direct imaging.<Magic numbers disappear and expand area of nuclear deformation by RIKEN>[20 November 2013, beam preparation, gamma-ray readout and deformation inference]{ref}

= Goeppert Mayer and Jensen nuclear shell model history
{parent=Nuclear shell model}
{c}

The 1948 empirical synthesis preceded the 1949 strong <spin-orbit interaction> proposals. <Johannes Hans Daniel Jensen> recalled developing that idea in a discussion with colleagues, independently of <Maria Goeppert Mayer>'s work; their shared problem was to account for the observed <magic numbers> and <spins>.<Glimpses at the History of the Nuclear Structure Theory by Johannes Hans Daniel Jensen>[pp. 46–48, empirical evidence and strong spin-orbit proposal]{ref}

<Johannes Hans Daniel Jensen> emphasized that even nuclear <ground states> retain correlations and that independent-particle motion becomes less adequate at higher excitation. This helps explain why the <nuclear shell model> needs residual interactions and complementary collective descriptions.<Glimpses at the History of the Nuclear Structure Theory by Johannes Hans Daniel Jensen>[pp. 48–49, correlations and configuration mixing]{ref}

The 1963 <Nobel Prize in Physics> recognized <Maria Goeppert Mayer> and <Johannes Hans Daniel Jensen> for nuclear shell structure. <Eugene Paul Wigner>'s share recognized his contributions to nuclear and elementary-particle theory, especially fundamental <symmetry> principles, a distinct award motivation.<All Nobel Prizes by Nobel Prize Outreach>[1963 Physics: separate motivations for Wigner and for Goeppert Mayer and Jensen]{ref}

= Collective nuclear model
{parent=Atomic nucleus}

= Bohr-Mottelson-Rainwater collective model
{c}
{synonym}

A description of an <atomic nucleus> in terms of coordinated motion of many <nucleons>, especially changes in shape and orientation. The <nuclear shell model> organizes individual <nucleon> states in a <nuclear mean field>; the <collective nuclear model> organizes low-lying <energy levels> by <nuclear deformation>, <nuclear rotational bands> and <nuclear quadrupole vibrations>. These are complementary organizations of the same nuclear <many-body problem>. A deformed <nuclear mean field> can support individual-particle states while its orientation supplies a slow collective coordinate.

In his 1975 lecture, <Ben Roy Mottelson> described coupling individual-particle motion to nuclear shape coordinates, and distinguished observed rotational spectra from vibrations about spherical equilibrium. This connects the two descriptions without treating the collective coordinates as extra constituent particles.<Elementary Modes of Excitation in the Nucleus by Ben Roy Mottelson>[pp. 236–237, coupled Hamiltonian and interpretation of low-energy excitation spectra]{ref}

<James Rainwater>'s 1975 account identifies unexpectedly large <electric quadrupole moments> as a motivation for a deformable <nuclear mean field>. The challenge concerned their magnitude, even where spherical-shell trends had the correct sign.<Background for the Spheroidal Nuclear Model Proposal by James Rainwater>[p. 264, Fig. 3 and discussion of the 1949 quadrupole compilation]{ref}

A simple original <potential energy> example makes the coupling tangible. Let $q$ be a dimensionless signed shape coordinate for a chosen intrinsic configuration, $C>0$ its restoring stiffness, and $f$ a configuration-dependent coupling, both with units of <energy>. Suppose
$$
V(q)=\frac12Cq^2-fq
=\frac C2\left(q-\frac fC\right)^2-\frac{f^2}{2C}.
$$
The minimum is $q_*=f/C$, with <energy> gain $f^2/(2C)$. For chosen values $C=20u$ and $f=4u$, where $u$ is an arbitrary positive <energy> unit, $q_*=0.20$ and the gain is $0.40u$. This illustrates how a configuration can favor deformation when the core can respond. It is not a fitted nuclear surface or a claim that every configuration chooses the same shape; competing configurations and <nuclear pairing> can favor a spherical minimum.

The predictive attraction is economy: one collective scale relates several <energy levels>, while <electric quadrupole transitions> independently test their common structure. Agreement in both is much more restrictive than assigning a separate free parameter to every level. <Nuclear magnetic moments> test magnetic structure and <angular momentum> assignments; they are distinct observables from the <electric quadrupole moments> used to infer charge deformation.

= Nuclear quadrupole vibration
{parent=Collective nuclear model}
{title2=$E_n-E_0=n\hbar\omega$}

A collective oscillation of the quadrupole shape coordinates of an <atomic nucleus>. Near spherical equilibrium, the ideal harmonic description has five components, corresponding to the five magnetic substates of <angular momentum> two. Each quantum carries $I^\pi=2^+$; it is an excitation of the many-<nucleon> system rather than an extra constituent. Its frequency is $\omega$, and its <energy> spacing $\hbar\omega$ follows the <quantum harmonic oscillator>.

One quantum produces a $2^+$ level at $\hbar\omega$. Two identical quadrupole quanta can couple to $I=0,2,4$ in a symmetric state, giving a degenerate $0^+,2^+,4^+$ multiplet at $2\hbar\omega$ in the ideal model. Odd total $I$ would be antisymmetric under their exchange. Thus the first $4^+$ to first $2^+$ excitation ratio is $2$, rather than the <nuclear rotational band> value $10/3$. Real <nuclear quadrupole vibrations> can be anharmonic and their multiplets split: the ratio is a diagnostic of a limiting model, not a universal classification rule.

There is a useful state-counting check. Two identical quanta distributed among five substates span $5(5+1)/2=15$ symmetric states. The proposed multiplet contains $(2\cdot0+1)+(2\cdot2+1)+(2\cdot4+1)=1+5+9=15$. This verifies the angular-state bookkeeping independently of the chosen <energy> scale.

= Three-quadrupole-phonon multiplet
{parent=Nuclear quadrupole vibration}
{title2=$S^3(V_2)=V_0\oplus V_2\oplus V_3\oplus V_4\oplus V_6$}

Three identical quadrupole quanta occupy the third <symmetric power> of the five-dimensional spin-two space. Its dimension is $\binom73=35$, and its <irreducible representations> have $J=0,2,3,4,6$, each once. All have positive <parity>. This counts allowed angular states in the ideal harmonic <nuclear quadrupole vibration>; anharmonic interactions can split their energies.

= Coulomb excitation
{parent=Collective nuclear model}
{c}
{wiki}

Excitation of an <atomic nucleus> by the <electric field> of a passing charged projectile. For suitable collision <energies> and separations, the <electromagnetic interaction> can populate collective states without requiring contact through the <nuclear force>. <Electric quadrupole transition> strengths connect excitation yields with collective charge motion; inferring a shape additionally assumes a nuclear model. Contributions from the <nuclear force>, competing transitions and the collision kinematics must be controlled before identifying a yield with a single quadrupole strength.

= Electric-dipole excitation by a passing ion
{parent=Coulomb excitation}
{title2=$P_{ji}\simeq[2Ze^2/(\hbar bv)]^2|\langle j|x|i\rangle|^2$}

In the straight-trajectory dipole model, retain the Coulomb-coupling convention of $V(t)=Ze^2(bx+vty)/(b^2+v^2t^2)^{3/2}$. In SI units the coupling denoted $e^2$ here is $e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$. First-order <time-dependent perturbation theory> expresses a transition through the <Fourier transform> of this pulse at the transition angular frequency.

For positive $\omega$ put $\beta=\omega b/v$. The two Cartesian pulse transforms are
$$
I_x=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{b e^{i\omega t}}{(b^2+v^2t^2)^{3/2}}dt=\frac{2\beta K_1(\beta)}{bv},\qquad
I_y=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{vt e^{i\omega t}}{(b^2+v^2t^2)^{3/2}}dt=\frac{2i\beta K_0(\beta)}{bv},
$$
where $K_\nu$ is a <modified Bessel function of the second kind>. One may derive these from the Fourier transform of $(1+s^2)^{-1/2}$, differentiating in its scale and integrating its spatial derivative by parts. For $\beta\ll1$, $\beta K_1(\beta)\to1$ while $\beta K_0(\beta)\to0$, so the transverse impulse gives the probability in the title. If the $x$ <matrix element> vanishes, the smaller longitudinal term can be the leading nonzero contribution.

The conditions include $b$ large compared with the atomic extent for the <electric-dipole approximation>, a small total transition <probability> for first-order theory, and a collision time $b/v$ short compared with $1/|\omega|$. Large $\beta$ instead gives exponentially suppressed excitation in this model through the large-argument behavior of $K_\nu$.

= Even-even nucleus
{parent=Atomic nucleus}

An <atomic nucleus> with even <proton> number $Z$ and even <neutron> number $N$. The simple <nuclear pairing> picture couples the constituent pairs to zero <angular momentum>. The collective examples below concern a $0^+$ <ground state>, where the superscript denotes positive <parity>, and its associated low-lying excitations; even $A=Z+N$ alone would also allow odd $Z$ and odd $N$.

= Nuclear deformation
{parent=Atomic nucleus}

Departure of an intrinsic nuclear shape from <spherical symmetry>. Here intrinsic means referred to axes attached to a model <nuclear charge distribution>. A nuclear <quantum state> with definite <spin> need not have the same laboratory <charge density> as that intrinsic distribution. In particular, the <ground state> of a deformed <even-even nucleus> can have $I=0$, a spherical laboratory <expectation value>, and a nonzero <intrinsic nuclear quadrupole moment>.

For an illustrative uniform <charge density> inside a <spheroid>, let the semiaxes be $(a,a,c)$ and keep its <volume> equal to that of a sphere of radius $R$:
$$
a^2c=R^3,\qquad a=R\eta^{-1/3},\qquad c=R\eta^{2/3},\qquad\eta=\frac ca>0.
$$
Thus $\eta>1$ gives a <prolate spheroid>, $\eta=1$ a sphere, and $\eta<1$ an <oblate spheroid>. These are three-dimensional equal-<volume> shapes: equal areas of their drawn cross-sections would be a different constraint. Real <nuclear charge distributions> have diffuse surfaces and need not be uniform, axially symmetric or confined to one shape.

{title=Equal-volume prolate, spherical and oblate intrinsic shapes}
{description=Original <spheroid> geometry with chosen axis ratios $\eta=1.8,1,1/1.8$. Each has $a^2c=R^3$. The surfaces illustrate shape coordinates, rather than literal arrangements or photographs of <nucleons>. The indicated signs refer to the <intrinsic nuclear quadrupole moment> of positive uniform <charge density>.}

= Intrinsic nuclear quadrupole moment
{parent=Nuclear deformation}
{title2=$Q_0$}

The axial <electric quadrupole moment> of an intrinsic <nuclear charge distribution>, with the origin at its center and the $z$ axis along its symmetry axis:
$$
Q_0=\int \rho_{\mathrm{ch}}(\mathbf r)(2z^2-x^2-y^2)\,d^3r.
$$
This convention includes <electric charge> in $Q_0$; a tabulated moment divided by the <elementary charge> $e$ instead has units of area. For the uniform <spheroid>, mapping a uniform unit ball to $(ax,ay,cz)$ gives $\langle x^2\rangle=a^2/5$ and $\langle z^2\rangle=c^2/5$, so
$$
Q_0=\frac25Ze(c^2-a^2),\qquad
\frac{Q_0}{ZeR^2}=\frac25\left(\eta^{4/3}-\eta^{-2/3}\right).
$$
The formula explains the signs in the figure. It measures an anisotropy of the <nuclear charge distribution>, rather than counting individual <nucleons> on a surface. An illustrative choice $\eta=\sqrt8$ gives $a=R/\sqrt2$, $c=2R$ and $Q_0=7ZeR^2/5$, while preserving $a^2c=R^3$ exactly.

<James Rainwater>'s lecture contrasts measured laboratory <electric quadrupole moments> with intrinsic moments inferred through <Coulomb excitation>, including <even-even nuclei>. Extracting intrinsic shape therefore uses a nuclear model, rather than equating every measured moment to $Q_0$.<Background for the Spheroidal Nuclear Model Proposal by James Rainwater>[p. 266, Fig. 4 and preceding Coulomb-excitation discussion]{ref}

= Spectroscopic nuclear quadrupole moment
{parent=Nuclear deformation}
{title2=$Q_s$}

The laboratory <expectation value> of the axial <electric quadrupole moment> in the stretched nuclear <quantum state> $|I,M=I\rangle$; $M\hbar$ is the laboratory $z$ component of total <angular momentum>. It differs from the <intrinsic nuclear quadrupole moment> $Q_0$. In an axial <nuclear rotational band> with $K=0$, projection onto definite <angular momentum> gives
$$
Q_s(I)=-\frac{I}{2I+3}Q_0,\qquad Q_s(0)=0,\qquad Q_s(2)=-\frac27Q_0.
$$
Here $K\hbar$ denotes the component of total <angular momentum> along the intrinsic symmetry axis. Consequently a positive $Q_0$ for a <prolate spheroid> corresponds to a negative $Q_s(2)$ in this model. The sign of the laboratory moment alone must be interpreted together with the band assignment.

The $I=0$ result has a short <symmetry> explanation. A spin-zero state has no preferred spatial direction. Its second charge moments satisfy $\langle x^2\rangle=\langle y^2\rangle=\langle z^2\rangle$, so $\langle2z^2-x^2-y^2\rangle=0$. Equivalently, averaging an intrinsic axis uniformly over directions gives $\langle P_2(\cos\theta)\rangle=0$, where $P_2(u)=(3u^2-1)/2$ is a <Legendre polynomial>. This is a statement about a quantum <expectation value>, not a classical picture of a spinning object sweeping through orientations.

For an independent check of the $I=2$ sign, the $K=0$, $M=2$ rotor orientation probability is proportional to $|Y_2^2|^2\propto\sin^4\theta$, using a <spherical harmonic>. With $u=\cos\theta$,
$$
\frac{Q_s(2)}{Q_0}
=\frac{\int_{-1}^{1}P_2(u)(1-u^2)^2\,du}{\int_{-1}^{1}(1-u^2)^2\,du}
=\frac{-32/105}{16/15}=-\frac27.
$$
A zero static laboratory moment does not make every quadrupole observable zero. With electric multipole operator $\mathcal M_{2\mu}=\int\rho_{\mathrm{ch}}r^2Y_2^\mu\,d^3r$, define the reduced transition strength using a <reduced matrix element>:
$$
B(E2;I_i\to I_f)=\frac{|\langle I_f\Vert\mathcal M_2\Vert I_i\rangle|^2}{2I_i+1}.
$$
In the axial $K=0$ rotor, angular projection gives $B(E2;0^+\to2^+)=5Q_0^2/(16\pi)$ and $B(E2;2^+\to0^+)=Q_0^2/(16\pi)$. The factor of five is the ratio of the numbers of magnetic substates. Thus an <electric quadrupole transition> can reveal collective deformation even when $Q_s(0)=0$. These strengths determine $|Q_0|$ within the model; their squares alone cannot distinguish its sign.

= Nuclear rotational band
{parent=Atomic nucleus}
{title2=$E_I-E_0=\hbar^2I(I+1)/(2\mathcal J)$}

A sequence of nuclear <energy levels> interpreted as different total <angular momenta> built on approximately the same intrinsic structure. For an axially symmetric, positive-<parity>, $K=0$ <ground-state> band of an <even-even nucleus>, the allowed sequence is $I=0,2,4,\ldots$. The simple constant-inertia rotor has
$$
H_{\mathrm{rot}}=\frac{\widehat{\mathbf I}^{\,2}}{2\mathcal J},\qquad
\widehat{\mathbf I}^{\,2}|IM\rangle=\hbar^2I(I+1)|IM\rangle,\qquad
E_I-E_0=\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mathcal J}I(I+1).
$$
Here $\mathcal J$ is an effective <moment of inertia>, and $\hbar$ the reduced <Planck constant>. The even-$I$ restriction belongs to this particular intrinsic <symmetry> and band, rather than to all nuclear excitations. The <angular momentum operator> eigenvalue supplies $I(I+1)$, rather than the classical replacement $I^2$.

Set $\epsilon=E_2-E_0$. Since $2(2+1)=6$, the model predicts
$$
\frac{E_4-E_0}{\epsilon}=\frac{20}{6}=\frac{10}{3},\qquad
\frac{E_6-E_0}{\epsilon}=\frac{42}{6}=7.
$$
For a chosen model scale $\epsilon=90\,\mathrm{keV}$, the excitation <energies> are $0,90,300,630\,\mathrm{keV}$. The corresponding successive <gamma-ray> <energies> are approximately $90,210,330\,\mathrm{keV}$, neglecting <nuclear recoil>. Level <energies> and emitted line <energies> are different lists. These numbers illustrate the formula and are not a measured <isotope> spectrum.

The fitted $\mathcal J=3\hbar^2/\epsilon$ is a response parameter, not automatically the <moment of inertia> of a classical rigid charge distribution. <Nuclear pairing> changes this response; <Ben Roy Mottelson> connected observed inertias with correlations beyond independent-particle motion.<Elementary Modes of Excitation in the Nucleus by Ben Roy Mottelson>[continuation after p. 237, pairing correlations and Fig. 1 potential-energy surfaces]{ref}

At larger <angular momentum>, changing intrinsic structure or <nuclear pairing> can invalidate a single constant $\mathcal J$. A useful diagnostic is to compare $(E_I-E_0)/[I(I+1)]$ across several levels: constancy tests the simple formula, while a drift identifies the need for additional physics. A successful ratio alone neither specifies every microscopic motion nor measures a literal solid-body rotation.

= Nuclear rotational inertia relative to a rigid sphere
{parent=Nuclear rotational band}
{title2=$\mathcal I=\hbar^2J(J+1)/(2E_J)$}

The excitation <energy> in an ideal $K=0$ <nuclear rotational band> determines an effective <moment of inertia> through the displayed equation. Comparing it with $2MR^2/5$ tests the rigid-sphere scale. A sequence of approximately constant $E_J/[J(J+1)]$ supports the rotational interpretation; systematic variation indicates that the effective inertia changes along the band. Collective rotation need not have a rigid-body inertia, notably when <nuclear pairing> is important.

= Nuclear rotational spectroscopy evidence
{parent=Nuclear rotational band}

<Aage Niels Bohr>'s 1975 lecture presents a <plutonium> ${}^{238}\mathrm{Pu}$ spectrum deduced from the fine structure of <alpha decay> of <curium> ${}^{242}\mathrm{Cm}$. The readout was decay-line <energy>; the comparison fixed the rotor scale from the first excitation and tested the remaining levels against $I(I+1)$. The lecture reports spectroscopy, rather than a laboratory setup operated by its author.<Rotational Motion in Nuclei by Aage Niels Bohr>[p. 217, Fig. 3 caption, plutonium spectrum and one-scale comparison]{ref}

His measured <erbium> ${}^{166}\mathrm{Er}$ $K=0$ band has excitation <energies> $0$, $80.56$, $265.0$, $545.4$ and $911.2\,\mathrm{keV}$ for $I=0,2,4,6,8$. The same lecture analyzes interband <electric quadrupole transitions>, adding tests beyond level positions.<Rotational Motion in Nuclei by Aage Niels Bohr>[p. 225, Fig. 9 measured levels and following discussion of Fig. 10 transition matrix elements]{ref}

Using those first two excitations gives $265.0/80.56\simeq3.28947$, close to the ideal $10/3$, and distinct from the ideal harmonic value $2$. Fixing the rotor scale with $80.56\,\mathrm{keV}$ predicts $268.53\,\mathrm{keV}$ at $I=4$ and $563.92\,\mathrm{keV}$ at $I=6$. The measured values lie about $1.32\%$ and $3.28\%$ below these predictions. This is a useful limit of the simple model: the pattern supports a rotational interpretation while revealing departures from constant inertia. It does not by itself determine a unique <nuclear charge distribution>. Reconstructing a level scheme also requires assigning which transitions connect which states; raw peak positions alone do not provide all <spins>, <parities> or transition strengths.
