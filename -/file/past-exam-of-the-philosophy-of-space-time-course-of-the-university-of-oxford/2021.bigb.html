= Solution
{parent=1}

There are two different issues: whether a law follows logically from the other laws, and whether an experiment can test it without assuming anything else. <Newton's laws of motion> have empirical content when combined with rules for identifying <inertial frames>, measuring <mass> and specifying <forces>. An experiment tests predictions of that combination. Calling every observed <acceleration> an otherwise unspecified <force> would remove the empirical constraint rather than verify <Newton's second law>. The distinction is central to the <empirical testing of Newton's laws of motion>.

<Newton's first law> contributes the claim that a common family of <inertial frames> exists: bodies with zero net <external force> have uniform straight <motion> in those frames. It is not enough to choose coordinates which straighten the <trajectory> of just one body; an admissible frame must accommodate many independent bodies and interactions. Once that frame and the ordinary constant-positive-<mass> form of <Newton's second law> are assumed,
$$
\mathbf F= m\frac{d\mathbf v}{dt}=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathbf v(t)=\mathbf v_0.
$$
This conditional deduction does not establish the existence of the required frame, or independently identify the physical circumstances in which the net <force> vanishes. Defining an <inertial frame> and then counting a straight <trajectory> as proof of the definition would not be a test.

Nevertheless, the <law of inertia> has testable implications in a network of assumptions. Consider low-friction motion along an air track: determine <positions> at measured <times>, reduce the known residual influences, and ask whether the remaining <acceleration> approaches zero in the same laboratory frame for different bodies. Rotating the frame changes the description through <Coriolis forces> and <centrifugal forces>; this dependence must be accounted for. No finite experiment establishes that every unmeasured influence is absent, but varying the apparatus can discriminate an interaction from a frame effect. A failed prediction is evidence against some part of the package, not automatically the isolated verbal statement of <Newton's first law>.

<Newton's second law> is more than a definition if the <force law> and <mass> assignments constrain predictions before the new <acceleration> is measured. For instance, independently characterize a spring's reproducible extension response, specify the same interaction in several configurations, and compare the resulting <accelerations> for different bodies. If its strength and every <mass> are freely refitted to each outcome, agreement is empty. If they are fixed by an independently checked model and calibration, the law predicts held-out <trajectories>. Such a test remains conditional on the spring model, <initial conditions> and frame identification; this is an instance of the <Duhem problem>, not a reason to deny all empirical content.

<Newton's third law> supplies a different constraint. For two bodies exchanging <forces>, <Newton's second law> gives
$$
\frac{d}{dt}(\mathbf p_1+\mathbf p_2)
=\mathbf F_{12}+\mathbf F_{21}
$$
when there is no net <external force>. Equal-and-opposite interaction <forces> imply <conservation of momentum> for the pair. The two second-law equations alone do not require that cancellation: assigning $\mathbf F_{12}=\mathbf f$ and $\mathbf F_{21}=-2\mathbf f$ is algebraically consistent with both second-law equations while violating the third law. This is a logical countermodel, not a proposed isolated physical interaction. Measuring paired <impulses> or checking the pair's total <momentum> therefore tests an additional constraint, conditional on the assumed absence of momentum exchange with anything else.

The system boundary matters. In <electromagnetism>, matter can exchange <momentum> with the <electromagnetic field>. Instantaneous mechanical <forces> between two particles need not satisfy the simple pairwise statement, although total matter-plus-field <momentum> is conserved under the appropriate closed-system conditions. Likewise, <Noether theorem> can derive <conservation of momentum> from a translationally invariant <action>; that uses an extra symmetry assumption and does not derive the third law from the second alone.

Thus the first law is not an additional differential consequence once the full second-law framework is fixed, and the third law is not a consequence of that framework. All three can contribute to empirical tests, but none is tested in total isolation from frame, interaction and measurement assumptions. This preserves the difference between logical dependence and experimental testability.<The Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy by Isaac Newton (1846 edition)>[Axioms, or Laws of Motion: Laws I–III and the discussion following Law III]{ref}

Historically, <electromagnetism> was central to <Albert Einstein>'s 1905 argument. His opening example compares a moving magnet and a moving conductor. The same relative <motion> produces the observed induction, but a description using <electric fields> in one frame and the magnetic part of the <Lorentz force> in another seemed to divide the explanation according to which object was called stationary. The unsuccessful search for an observable preferred state of rest also motivated treating the laws of <mechanics>, <optics> and <electromagnetism> on a common footing. That establishes a strong historical connection, not a deduction that relativity applies only to electromagnetic systems.<On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies by Albert Einstein>[opening magnet–conductor example and statement of the two postulates]{ref}

The logical starting points of the kinematical argument are <Einstein's principle of relativity> and an invariant <speed of light> in vacuum, together with the assumptions needed to compare homogeneous <inertial frames> and physical measuring standards. <Einstein synchronisation> assigns distant <clock> readings by light exchange. In standard coordinates, the resulting <Lorentz transformation> for a boost along $x$ is
$$
t'=\gamma\left(t-\frac{vx}{c^2}\right),\qquad
x'=\gamma(x-vt),\qquad
\gamma=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}.
$$
Neither the derivation of this transformation nor the deductions of <relativity of simultaneity> and <time dilation> require solving <Maxwell equations> for every particular material system. They constrain the relation between measurements made in different <inertial frames>.<On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies by Albert Einstein>[sections 1–4: synchronization, the transformation, and its kinematical consequences]{ref}

There is nevertheless an empirical commitment in identifying $c$ with the speed of vacuum <light>. It is not a freely chosen numerical label: the theory says different inertial observers, using the prescribed standards, obtain the same measured value. More generally, $c$ specifies the <causal structure> of <Minkowski spacetime>. A theory could contain a massive <scalar field> with a <Lorentz-invariant> equation and no electromagnetic field at all. Its particles, <proper times> and causal propagation would still be governed by relativistic geometry. This gives a direct sense in which the framework is logically more general than <electromagnetism>.

Conversely, the presence of <Maxwell equations> alone would not establish the full relativity principle for all matter. One could append a law involving a physically preferred <timelike vector field>, giving a nonelectromagnetic sector a preferred frame while retaining the usual electromagnetic equations. The <covariant equation with a preferred timelike vector> is an explicit model of this distinction. Requiring every relevant interaction to respect <Lorentz invariance> adds content; writing its components in four-dimensional notation does not supply that content automatically.

The magnet–conductor puzzle is resolved because <electric fields> and <magnetic fields> are related parts of one <electromagnetic field>, with the <Lorentz force> transforming consistently. The measured current must be compared for the same physical arrangement, including the conductor's state of motion, rather than by demanding the same electric and magnetic components separately. This is an application of the general frame transformations to a particular interaction, not the definition of every possible relativistic interaction.

<Albert Einstein>'s later distinction between a <principle theory> and a <constructive theory> clarifies what the 1905 argument provides. It places universal restrictions on admissible laws without constructing every rod and <clock> from a particular microscopic model. A constructive explanation can additionally investigate why actual material standards approximate the required behavior. <Harvey R. Brown> and <Oliver Pooley> emphasize this dynamical question; it does not make the kinematical transformations meaningless or confine them to electromagnetic phenomena.<What Is the Theory of Relativity? by Albert Einstein>[1919 article: distinction between constructive theories and principle theories]{ref}<Minkowski space-time: a glorious non-entity by Harvey R. Brown and Oliver Pooley>[sections 3–5: rods, clocks, and dynamical explanation]{ref}

The strongest account therefore distinguishes historical motivation, the operational use of light, and the theory's universal scope. The 1905 paper addresses an electromagnetic problem through a framework whose restrictions extend to every physical process claimed to obey <special relativity>.

The age comparison is a local comparison of <clocks> at two shared <spacetime events>: departure and reunion. No convention about the simultaneity of distant events is needed to read two adjacent <clocks>. The result is determined by the <proper time> along each complete <world line>. For an ideal <clock> following a <timelike curve> in the home observer's <inertial frame>,
$$
\Delta\tau=\int_0^T\sqrt{1-\frac{\dot x(t)^2}{c^2}}\,dt.
$$
The home observer stays at $x=0$, whereas the traveler goes outward at <velocity> $v$ and returns at <velocity> $-v$. Ignoring the duration of a short turnaround gives home age $T$ and traveler age $T/\gamma$. The <twin paradox with fixed reunion events> derives this directly. There is no contradiction because the traveler does not remain in one <inertial frame> throughout the trip.

<Relativity of simultaneity> becomes essential to a different question: how can the traveler consistently describe the home <clock> as running slowly during both straight legs? The statement about a distant <clock>'s rate refers to a sequence of remote events selected as simultaneous by a specified <inertial frame>. Changing that frame changes the selected sequence. Adding the two slow-rate comparisons while silently preserving one distant-time assignment is the error.

Let turnaround be $P=(t,x)=(T/2,vT/2)$ in the home frame. Constant outward-frame time means $t-vx/c^2$ is constant. Thus the home event simultaneous with $P$ according to the outward frame occurs at
$$
t_H^-=\frac T2\left(1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}\right).
$$
For the return frame, constant time means $t+vx/c^2$ is constant, so its simultaneous home event occurs at
$$
t_H^+=\frac T2\left(1+\frac{v^2}{c^2}\right).
$$
These are two different remote <spacetime events>, even though both assignments pass through the same turnaround event. The <twin paradox simultaneity change> is
$$
t_H^+-t_H^-=\frac{v^2T}{c^2}.
$$
Each leg contributes $T/(2\gamma^2)$ to the home <clock> advance assigned within that leg's frame. Including the reassignment gives $T/\gamma^2+v^2T/c^2=T$, exactly the home reading at reunion. For the illustrative values $v=0.6c$ and $T=10$ years, the traveler's elapsed <proper time> is eight years; the two leg assignments give $3.2+3.2$ years of home advance, and the frame change supplies the missing $3.6$ years. The distant home <clock> does not physically jump.

Nor should simultaneity assignments be confused with received <light> signals. An observer sees emissions after a propagation delay and measures a <Doppler shift>. The received history remains consistent through a finite turnaround, but it is not the same sequence of events as a constant-coordinate-time slice. The <Lorentz transformation> relates the latter; signal travel times must be included separately when discussing what is seen.

An instantaneous turnaround is only an ideal limit. In the <twin paradox with finite acceleration>, the <world line> and the momentarily comoving frame vary continuously. The distant home-time assignment is $t_H(t)=t-v(t)x(t)/c^2$, so
$$
\frac{dt_H}{dt}=1-\frac{v(t)^2}{c^2}-\frac{x(t)}{c^2}\frac{dv}{dt}.
$$
The reversal at positive $x$ makes the last contribution positive. Its integrated effect replaces the ideal reassignment. By the <clock hypothesis>, the traveler's own elapsed <proper time> has no extra term depending directly on <acceleration>; physical disturbances of a particular <clock> are a separate experimental issue.

Thus <relativity of simultaneity> explains the consistency of the traveler's successive inertial descriptions. It is not a convention-dependent cause of the final age difference. The <invariance of reunion ages under clock resynchronization> makes that distinction explicit: remote time labels can change while the two reunited <clock> readings remain unchanged.

The numerical coordinates assigned to an event can be changed without changing the event. It does not follow that every relation between coordinate systems is devoid of physical content. A standard <inertial frame> includes a family of resting measuring standards and synchronized <clocks>. Its coordinates are normalized so that the <Minkowski metric> has the same standard form. The relation between two such descriptions therefore records properties of the standards and of <spacetime>, not just an arbitrary choice of names.

Write $x^\mu=(ct,x,y,z)$ and use <metric signature> $(-,+,+,+)$. A <Lorentz transformation> satisfies
$$
x'^\mu=\Lambda^\mu{}_{\nu}x^\nu,\qquad
\Lambda^T\eta\Lambda=\eta,\qquad
\eta=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,1,1).
$$
An origin translation can also be included without changing the <Minkowski interval>. The displayed condition preserves the interval between events, including the <null cone> and <proper time> along a <timelike curve>. Its physical significance lies in the claim that comparisons made with actual idealized rods and <clocks> obey this same metric structure.

A more general passive relabeling is allowed, but then the metric components must change with the labels. For example, let $t'=2t$ and leave $x,y,z$ fixed. In the new coordinates,
$$
ds^2=-\frac{c^2}{4}\,dt'^2+dx^2+dy^2+dz^2.
$$
Nothing physical has changed, but $t'$ no longer has the original clock-normalized meaning of $t$. Retaining the old metric components as well as the new labels would change the mathematical model. This distinction is part of the <physical content of a passive Lorentz transformation>: the transformation between standard inertial coordinate systems preserves a specified metric form, whereas arbitrary relabelings need not preserve that form.

For an operational example, a <clock> moving at <velocity> $v$ between two events on its <world line> accumulates
$$
\Delta\tau=\Delta t\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}
$$
in the first frame. In its own rest frame the same result is simply the interval between its local readings. The equality is a physical agreement about one <clock>, derived using the preserved <Minkowski interval>. It is not a claim that the two coordinate-time differences are identical. Similarly, an observer measures a <light> angular <frequency> through $\omega=-k_\mu u^\mu$, where $k^\mu$ is the <wave four-vector> and $u^\mu$ the observer's <four-velocity>. Transforming both sets of components leaves this scalar unchanged. Comparing two different observers gives the physical <relativistic Doppler effect> rather than a disagreement over one observer's measurement.

There is a further distinction between passive transformations of the same situation and active changes to a situation. Re-expressing a particular experiment in different coordinates is always possible if all geometric fields are transformed consistently. Preparing a second, moving copy of the apparatus is a different physical arrangement. Its relation to the first is governed by a <dynamical symmetry> only if the relevant laws and background structure have the appropriate invariance. An <active Lorentz transformation of a vector field> makes the changed field configuration explicit.

This is why mere <Lorentz covariance> of notation must not be confused with the <Lorentz invariance> of the dynamics. A physically preferred <timelike vector field> can be transformed as a vector under passive relabeling while distinguishing particular states of motion. Holding that preferred field fixed can destroy the active boost symmetry of the matter dynamics. The <covariant equation with a preferred timelike vector> is a concrete example.

Coordinates are therefore labels, but the <Lorentz transformation> relates labels tied to shared physical standards and a definite metric structure. Its measurable consequences follow from that structure and the dynamical assumptions, not from the bare act of changing labels.<On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies by Albert Einstein>[sections 1–4: operational definitions of time and length, and the transformation of moving standards]{ref}

The phrase <equivalence principle> covers several related claims. The <weak equivalence principle> concerns the universality of <free fall> for <test particles> of negligible self-gravity, with the same initial conditions and no relevant nongravitational <forces>. In a Newtonian notation,
$$
m_I\mathbf a=m_G\mathbf g
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathbf a=\frac{m_G}{m_I}\mathbf g.
$$
Composition-independent motion requires the ratio of passive <gravitational mass> to <inertial mass> to be universal. Its common value can then be absorbed into the normalization of $\mathbf g$; the empirical assertion is universality, not the numerical convention used to express the ratio.

The <Einstein equivalence principle> includes that universality and extends the claim to local nongravitational experiments through <local Lorentz invariance> and <local position invariance>. A sufficiently small freely falling laboratory should recover the nongravitational laws of <special relativity>. The <strong equivalence principle> extends the scope to local gravitational experiments and bodies with appreciable gravitational self-energy. An experiment establishing one part of the weak statement does not automatically establish these stronger claims.

The motivating elevator comparison makes both the power and the limitation clear. Release a small object in a freely falling cabin. The cabin and object share the local <free fall>, so the object initially floats relative to the cabin. In a cabin supported against <gravity>, a released object instead accelerates relative to the floor, as it does in a suitably accelerated cabin in flat <spacetime>. This suggested replacing a universal gravitational <force> by geometric <inertial motion>. It does not identify a finite planetary <gravitational field> with an accelerated frame everywhere: separated falling objects can have a measurable relative <acceleration>.

In <general relativity>, the precise local geometric statement is that one can choose <normal coordinates> at an event $p$ with
$$
g_{\mu\nu}(p)=\eta_{\mu\nu},\qquad
\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}(p)=0,\qquad
\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}(p)=0.
$$
This eliminates the connection contribution to the <geodesic equation> at that point. It does not generally set the <Riemann curvature tensor> to zero. In normal coordinates the first metric corrections away from $p$ are quadratic in the coordinate separation, and <geodesic deviation> measures the associated <tidal forces>. Equivalence is a local approximation for finite experiments whose extent and duration make these corrections negligible; it is not an assertion that curvature has been transformed away.<General Relativity by David Tong>[sections 3.3.2 and 3.3.4: normal coordinates and geodesic deviation]{ref}

There are direct experimental consequences beyond the elevator thought experiment. An <Eötvös experiment> compares bodies of different composition on a <torsion balance>, using a differential signal to test the universality of gravitational <acceleration>. <MICROSCOPE (satellite)> performed an analogous test in orbit using electrostatic control of titanium-alloy and platinum-alloy test masses. Its first published result in 2017 constrained their differential <free fall>; this was already available before the examination. It tested a specific weak-equivalence prediction, not every possible gravitational law.<The MICROSCOPE mission: first results of a space test of the Equivalence Principle by Pierre Touboul and others>[2017 abstract and experimental description: composition comparison and differential control forces]{ref} Comparisons of different <atomic clocks> can instead test <local position invariance>, including the expected <gravitational redshift>. These measurements constrain related but distinct parts of the framework.

The principle remains important as a restriction on how matter couples to gravity and as an organizing guide for experimental tests. It does not, on its own, derive the <Einstein field equations>. Universally coupling matter to a <metric tensor> still leaves choices for that metric's <action>; the <equivalence principle does not uniquely fix field equations>. For example, a metric gravitational action containing a <scalar curvature> squared term can retain the same minimal matter coupling while changing the gravitational dynamics. Selecting the <Einstein-Hilbert action> requires further dynamical assumptions and empirical success. The present role of the principle is consequently precise and limited: it supports local relativistic physics and universal matter coupling, while curvature, gravitational field equations and their quantitative predictions remain additional content.
