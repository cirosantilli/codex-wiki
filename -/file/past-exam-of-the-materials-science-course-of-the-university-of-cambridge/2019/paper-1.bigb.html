In <crystal polymorphism>, the same <chemical composition> has different <crystal structures>; for an <chemical element>, this is a form of <allotropy>. <Diamond> has a three-dimensional tetrahedral network of <covalent bonds>, giving high <hardness> and a wide <band gap>. <Graphite> has strongly bonded planar sheets, with weaker interactions between sheets. Sliding between sheets makes it a useful <solid> <lubricant>; mobile electronic states give good electrical conduction within the sheets. Their different properties come from different bonding and structures, despite both being <carbon>.

<Barium titanate> has a high-temperature cubic <perovskite structure>, with Ti centred in its <oxygen> octahedron on average. This phase is <paraelectric>. Near room <temperature> the structure is tetragonal, with relative ionic displacements that give a spontaneous <electric polarization>. This phase is <ferroelectric>: an applied <electric field> can switch between differently polarized domains. Both phases have the same composition, BaTiO$_3$; the transition near $120\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ changes <crystal symmetry> and electrical response.

The <zinc blende structure> has an FCC <sulfur> sublattice and <zinc> on half its tetrahedral sites. One conventional <unit cell> uses S at $(0,0,0),(1/2,1/2,0),(1/2,0,1/2),(0,1/2,1/2)$ and Zn at those positions displaced by $(1/4,1/4,1/4)$, reduced modulo one. The <wurtzite structure> has ABAB <sulfur> layers, instead of ABCABC layers. One hexagonal-cell convention uses S at $(0,0,0),(2/3,1/3,1/2)$ and Zn at $(0,0,u),(2/3,1/3,1/2+u)$, with ideal $u=3/8$. Looking along $[001]$ superposes S and Zn in that latter coordinate convention; the height labels distinguish them.

{title=Two-by-two zinc sulfide unit-cell projections along the stacking axes}

The closest-packed <sulfur> planes are **$\{111\}$, with four distinct plane orientations**, for <zinc blende structure>, and **$\{001\}$, with one orientation**, for <wurtzite structure>. Opposite normals describe the same unoriented plane orientation. Hexagonal four-index notation writes the latter basal family as $\{0001\}$.

= Solution
{parent=c}

**The <solid> trace is wurtzite; the dashed trace is <zinc> blende.** The isolated strong dashed peak is the cubic $111$ reflection. The <solid> trace has hexagonal $100$, $002$ and $101$ reflections in this angular interval; its central $002$ peak is the one at $28.65^\circ$, while cubic $111$ occurs at $28.53^\circ$. These assignments are consistent with the absence of lower-angle reflections.

In the ideal close-packed model, the cubic $111$ and hexagonal $002$ reflections probe the same local alternating layer geometry. Their scattering strength per suitably normalized amount of material and their angular factors are therefore very similar, but <powder diffraction multiplicity> differs: the cubic family has eight directed normals, while hexagonal $002$ has two. This explains the approximately **fourfold greater intensity** of the dashed peak. Comparing unnormalized conventional-cell <structure factors> alone would be misleading because the cells contain different numbers of formula units. Peak heights additionally depend on line widths; the multiplicity argument applies most directly to integrated intensities.

Taking Cu K$\alpha$ <wavelength> $\lambda=0.15418\,\mathrm{nm}$, <Bragg's law> gives $d=\lambda/(2\sin\theta)$. For cubic $111$, $a=\sqrt3d_{111}$ and the nearest Zn–S separation is $\sqrt3a/4=3d_{111}/4$. For ideal <wurtzite structure>, $c=2d_{002}$ and the nearest separation is $3c/8=3d_{002}/4$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{r_{\rm cubic}=0.2346\,\mathrm{nm},\qquad r_{\rm hexagonal}\simeq0.2337\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
Both have tetrahedral nearest-neighbour <coordination number> four, but different further-neighbour stacking and lattice relaxation can shift their equilibrium bond lengths. The hexagonal value assumes ideal $c/a=\sqrt{8/3}$ and $u=3/8$; a real distorted <wurtzite structure> can have unequal axial and inclined bonds. One measured $002$ spacing alone does not determine every bond length without that structural assumption.

Features only a few tens of nanometres wide lie below the ordinary visible-light <diffraction limit>, approximately $\lambda/(2\mathrm{NA})$. The microscope cannot resolve individual rods, and unpolarized illumination does not directly reveal the orientation-dependent optical response. **<Scanning electron microscopy> and <atomic force microscopy>** can resolve the surface pattern. <Transmission electron microscopy> is another possible choice if a suitably thin specimen can be prepared.

= Solution
{parent=b}

An optically <isotropic> film changes the phase of both transverse polarization components equally, so crossed <polarizers> still extinguish the transmitted light. The etched rod-and-gap composite has **<form birefringence>**: its effective <permittivity> depends on the direction of the <electric field>, even when the constituent material is intrinsically <isotropic>. In a simple subwavelength lamellar model,
$$
\epsilon_\parallel=f\epsilon_r+(1-f)\epsilon_g,\qquad
\epsilon_\perp=\left(\frac f{\epsilon_r}+\frac{1-f}{\epsilon_g}\right)^{-1}.
$$
The precise effective-medium formula changes with rod geometry, but the <anisotropy> remains. Resolving incident light along the two principal directions produces a <phase retardation>. An ideal lossless specimen between crossed <polarizers> transmits
$$
I=I_P\sin^2(2\alpha)\sin^2(\pi\Gamma/\lambda),\qquad\Gamma=(n_{\rm slow}-n_{\rm fast})t,
$$
where $I_P$ is the intensity after the first polarizer. Extinction occurs when a principal direction follows either polarizer; transmission is strongest near $45^\circ$.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

At distance $x$ from its tip, the wedge thickness is $t_q=x\tan\theta_q$. Its <optical path difference> is therefore
$$
\Gamma_q=\Delta n_q x\tan\theta_q
=0.009(5\times10^{-3})\tan(0.5^\circ)
=3.927\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm m.
$$
The perpendicular slow directions make the two retardances subtract. A white-light black compensation fringe is the zero-order cancellation, so **the film retardance has magnitude approximately $393\,\mathrm{nm}$**, with its slow direction along the rods. This interpretation assumes the observed fringe is the white-light compensation fringe; a single monochromatic dark fringe alone would have an integer-order ambiguity. Without the wedge, the <wavelength> dependence of $\sin^2(\pi\Gamma/\lambda)$ selectively transmits visible wavelengths and gives the film its <interference colour>.

= Solution

The local slow direction follows the radial rods. Between crossed <polarizers>, the angular factor is $\sin^2(2\varphi)$, so radial directions parallel to either polarizer are dark. The pattern is a **<Maltese cross in polarized-light microscopy>**, with four bright sectors separated by dark arms parallel to the polarizer directions. Its colours depend on the local <optical path difference>; tapering rods or changing fill fraction can make this vary with radius. The cross belongs to the local radial-axis pattern and does not imply a <conoscopic interference figure>.

For a disordered <face-centered cubic lattice>, equal average scattering factors on the four sites give reflections only when $h,k,l$ are all even or all odd. <Bragg's law> also requires $h^2+k^2+l^2\leq4a^2/\lambda^2$. With Fe K$\alpha$ <wavelength> $\lambda\simeq0.1936\,\mathrm{nm}$, the upper bound is $15.41$. The allowed sums are $3,4,8,11,12$, giving
$$
\boxed{111,\quad200,\quad220,\quad311,\quad222.}
$$
The next allowed family, $400$, has squared-index sum $16$ and cannot satisfy the diffraction condition for this <wavelength>. This list assumes the full geometrically accessible angular range, rather than an instrument-specific cutoff.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Successive planes normal to $[001]$ are chemically ordered Au and Cu layers. The supplied four-site cell is a convenient doubled description of the **<primitive tetragonal lattice>**: valid primitive translations are
$$
\mathbf a_1=\tfrac a2(1,1,0),\quad
\mathbf a_2=\tfrac a2(-1,1,0),\quad
\mathbf a_3=(0,0,c).
$$
They give a square basal cell of side $a/\sqrt2$, containing an Au basis atom at the origin and Cu at fractional $(1/2,1/2,1/2)$. That last vector exchanges chemical species and is not a Bravais translation. Thus a face-centred-looking atomic arrangement does not make the chemically ordered material an FCC <Bravais lattice>. This is the <L10 crystal structure>; the following indices retain the larger cell specified in the question.

Summing phase-weighted <atomic scattering factors> over the four positions gives
$$
\boxed{F_{hkl}=f_{\rm Au}[1+(-1)^{h+k}]+f_{\rm Cu}[(-1)^{h+l}+(-1)^{k+l}].}
$$
If $h,k$ have different parity, the reflection vanishes. If they have equal parity, this becomes $2[f_{\rm Au}+(-1)^{h+l}f_{\rm Cu}]$. The difference reflections reveal chemical order; they disappear when all sites instead have the same average scattering factor.

= ii
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Ordering reflections by $d_{hkl}^{-2}=(h^2+k^2)/a^2+l^2/c^2$ with $c\simeq0.9a$, the first three nonzero families are **$001$, $110$ and $111$**. The $100$ family is extinct. Their <structure factors> are
$$
\boxed{F_{001}=2(f_{\rm Au}-f_{\rm Cu}),\quad F_{110}=2(f_{\rm Au}-f_{\rm Cu}),\quad F_{111}=2(f_{\rm Au}+f_{\rm Cu}).}
$$
The <atomic scattering factors> in each expression are evaluated at that reflection's scattering vector, so equal symbolic forms need not mean equal numerical amplitudes at different angles. In particular, the low-angle superlattice peaks need not be the strongest peaks.

{parent=c}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The first peak is $001$, with $d_{001}=c$; the second is $110$, with $d_{110}=a/\sqrt2$. Applying <Bragg's law> with $\lambda=0.193604\,\mathrm{nm}$ gives
$$
\boxed{c=\frac\lambda{2\sin15.30^\circ}=0.3669\,\mathrm{nm},\qquad a=b=\frac\lambda{\sqrt2\sin20.24^\circ}=0.3957\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
Thus $c/a=0.927$, consistent with the approximate ratio used to order the reflections. These are the lengths of the supplied larger basal cell; the primitive basal <lattice parameter> is $a/\sqrt2=0.2798\,\mathrm{nm}$.

= Solution
{parent=d}

At a common test <temperature> and otherwise comparable microstructure, chemical order generally raises the <yield strength>. An ordinary FCC <dislocation> displaced by $a\langle110\rangle/2$ can interchange Au and Cu sublattices, creating an <antiphase boundary> behind it. This additional energy resists slip; paired <dislocations> can restore the order after the second member passes. The disordered phase does not pay the same ordering penalty. Comparing specimens actually tested at their different phase-stability temperatures also includes temperature-dependent mobility and recovery, so the ordering argument alone does not predict a unique measured strength ratio.

(Bi) and $(\beta\mathrm{Sn})$ are Bi-rich and Sn-rich <terminal solid solutions>, not intermetallic compounds. At the eutectic composition, cooling through $138\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ gives
$$
\boxed{L\longrightarrow(\mathrm{Bi})+(\beta\mathrm{Sn}).}
$$
The two solids grow together as a fine <eutectic microstructure>, commonly lamellar or rod-like. Below about $13\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, the tin-rich component has an equilibrium tendency to transform from metallic tetragonal <white tin> to diamond-cubic <grey tin>, with a substantial volume increase. This is <tin pest>, which can disrupt the microstructure mechanically. The dashed transition does not transform the Bi-rich phase into <tin>. Actual transformation can be slow and depend strongly on impurities and <nucleation>; crossing the line does not guarantee immediate disintegration.

{parent=4}
{scope}

For a mixture with phase compositions $x_1,x_2$, its molar <Gibbs free energy> is the straight-line combination $g=(1-f)g_1(x_1)+fg_2(x_2)$ at overall $x=(1-f)x_1+fx_2$. Minimizing over phase compositions gives equal slopes and equal tangent intercepts: the <common-tangent construction> expresses equality of both component <chemical potentials>. The equilibrium curve is the lower <convex envelope> of the candidate phase curves. Phase fractions follow the <lever rule>.

At $200\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, moving from Bi-rich to Sn-rich composition gives **$(\mathrm{Bi})$, $(\mathrm{Bi})+L$, $L$, $L+(\beta\mathrm{Sn})$, $(\beta\mathrm{Sn})$**. Approximate <liquid> coexistence compositions read from the diagram are $x_{\rm Sn}\simeq0.33$ on the left and $0.87$ on the right; the coexisting solids lie near their respective pure ends. The stable <Gibbs free energy> follows <solid> curves near the endpoints, two <common-tangent constructions> in the coexistence intervals, and the <liquid> curve between them.

At $138\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, one line is tangent to all three <Gibbs free energy> curves at approximately $x_{\rm Sn}=0.03$ for (Bi), $0.61$ for <liquid>, and $0.93$ for $(\beta\mathrm{Sn})$. Convert atomic fractions to <mass fractions> before using the diagram's weight-percent scale:
$$
w(x)=\frac{xM_{\rm Sn}}{xM_{\rm Sn}+(1-x)M_{\rm Bi}}.
$$
With $M_{\rm Sn}=118.71$ and $M_{\rm Bi}=208.98\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$, the overall $x=0.30$ is **$19.58\,\mathrm{wt\%}$ Sn**. At $150\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, the diagram gives approximately $x_B=0.028$ and $x_L=0.555$. These are about $1.61$ and $41.47\,\mathrm{wt\%}$ Sn. The <lever rule> gives
$$
\boxed{f_L=\frac{0.1958-0.0161}{0.4147-0.0161}\simeq0.45,\qquad f_B\simeq0.55.}
$$
The microstructure has primary Bi-rich crystals surrounded by remaining <liquid>. The phase compositions and fractions are approximate readings of a printed phase diagram, not exact thermodynamic values. Faster cooling generally gives finer crystals, solute segregation and departure from these equilibrium compositions.

At $125\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, primary Bi-rich crystals are surrounded by a transformed two-phase <eutectic microstructure>. Reading approximately $x_B=0.026$ and $x_S=0.94$ gives phase compositions $1.49$ and $89.90\,\mathrm{wt\%}$ Sn. Consequently,
$$
\boxed{f_{\beta\rm Sn}\simeq\frac{0.1958-0.0149}{0.8990-0.0149}=0.205,\qquad f_{\rm Bi}\simeq0.795.}
$$
These are total **phase mass fractions**, including both primary and eutectic material. They differ from the <microconstituent> fractions: just before the eutectic reaction, roughly $39\%$ by mass is <liquid> and subsequently becomes the eutectic constituent, with roughly $61\%$ primary Bi-rich material. Further slow cooling changes the phase compositions and allows secondary precipitation. Faster cooling usually refines the primary crystals and eutectic spacing and increases retained segregation; the equilibrium diagram does not specify the resulting nonequilibrium fractions uniquely.

An FCC crystal slips on $\{111\}\langle110\rangle$. For each <slip system>, <Schmid's law> gives $\tau=\sigma m$, with $m=|\hat{\mathbf l}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}|\,|\hat{\mathbf l}\cdot\hat{\mathbf s}|$. Enumerating the twelve systems for $\mathbf l=(5,1,4)$ gives the maximum at
$$
\boxed{(1\bar11)[110].}
$$
Here $\mathbf n=(1,-1,1)$ and $\mathbf s=(1,1,0)$ are perpendicular, while $\mathbf l\cdot\mathbf n=8$, $\mathbf l\cdot\mathbf s=6$ and $|\mathbf l|^2=42$. Hence $m=48/(42\sqrt6)=0.46657$. Reversing both directed slip conventions describes the same physical system.

{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Applying <Schmid's law> at first yield,
$$
\boxed{\tau_{\rm CRSS}=83.5\frac{48}{42\sqrt6}=38.96\,\mathrm{MPa}\simeq39.0\,\mathrm{MPa}.}
$$
This infers the <critical resolved shear stress> for the initially active system, assuming the supplied tensile <stress> is the relevant uniaxial <stress> at yield and the twelve FCC systems have comparable initial slip resistance.

{parent=5}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Initially, one <slip system> carries most of the deformation. Easy <dislocation glide> gives the nearly flat stage-I response, although lattice rotation and gradual <dislocation> interactions can already change the required tensile <stress>. In tension the loading direction expressed in the rotating crystal approaches the primary slip direction. Its path has the form $(5+t,1+t,4)$ until it reaches the symmetry boundary $y=z$, at $[844]=[211]$.

At that orientation, **$(1\bar11)[110]$ and $(11\bar1)[101]$** have equal <Schmid factors>, $1/\sqrt6$. Conjugate slip therefore becomes competitive. Intersections between the two <dislocation> families generate obstacles, junctions and <forest hardening>, giving the sharp stage-II rise in flow <stress>. This is the ideal single-slip-to-conjugate-slip construction; real activation stresses can be shifted by latent hardening and imperfections.

= Solution
{parent=d}

The number and spacing of primary slip planes remain fixed, so the length projected on their normal is invariant: $l_0\cos\phi_0=l_1\cos\phi_1$. The initial and boundary loading directions are $[514]$ and $[211]$, respectively. With normal $[1\bar11]$,
$$
\cos\phi_0=\frac8{\sqrt{42}\sqrt3},\qquad
\cos\phi_1=\frac2{\sqrt6\sqrt3}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\epsilon_p=\frac{\cos\phi_0}{\cos\phi_1}-1=\frac4{\sqrt7}-1=0.5119\simeq51.2\%.}
$$
This is engineering plastic <strain> in the ideal geometric model; its corresponding logarithmic <strain> is $\ln(4/\sqrt7)$. Elastic extension and changes in slip resistance are neglected, as required by this construction.

The <nuclear fuel> supplies nuclei whose <nuclear fission> releases energy. Most of that energy becomes <heat> in the fuel and neighbouring material. The reactor transfers this <heat> to a working fluid; the fuel's chemical form and geometry must also remain compatible with its thermal and irradiation environment.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

<Nuclear fuel cladding> separates the fuel and most <fission products> from the <coolant>, provides mechanical containment, and transfers <heat> across the fuel boundary. It must retain useful mechanical integrity and corrosion resistance during irradiation while avoiding excessive <neutron> absorption.

{parent=a}

= Solution

<Control rods> absorb <neutrons>. Changing their insertion changes the <neutron> balance and hence reactor <reactivity>; sufficiently strong insertion makes the chain reaction subcritical. They provide control and shutdown functions. Continued decay <heat> means that shutdown does not remove the need for cooling.

{parent=a}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

The <coolant> removes <heat> from the fuel region and carries it to a <heat> exchanger or directly to a turbine system. It can also act as a <neutron moderator> in some reactor types, but cooling and moderation are distinct functions and need not be performed by the same material.

= b
{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Energetic <neutron> collisions create <displacement cascades> and <Frenkel pairs>: displaced atoms become <self-interstitials>, leaving <vacancies>. During migration, some opposite defects recombine, while others cluster or reach sinks. Interstitials can condense into extra atomic planes bounded by <dislocation loops>; <vacancy> clustering can instead give missing-plane loops. Their subsequent growth is driven by defect absorption and <dislocation climb>.

Randomly oriented loops do not select a macroscopic shear direction in an initially <isotropic> polycrystal. That does **not** prove that every external dimension remains unchanged: interstitial and <vacancy> loops have opposite relaxation-volume contributions, and an imbalance can change the volume. Macroscopic <void swelling> requires the additional vacancy-cavity mechanism; it must not be identified automatically with the mere presence of loops. Texture, stress-biased absorption and unequal loop populations can also give anisotropic dimensional change.

= c
{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The annealed sample initially has relatively few obstacles to <dislocation glide>. After its elastic response it yields, undergoes appreciable plastic deformation and develops <strain hardening>. Irradiation produces <dislocation loops> and defect clusters that obstruct gliding <dislocations>, usually raising the <yield strength>. In a simple dispersed-obstacle model the strengthening scales as $\Delta\sigma\sim M\alpha Gb\sqrt{Nd}$, with obstacle density $N$ and diameter $d$.

Irradiated material often has less uniform plastic elongation and weaker subsequent hardening because slip localizes into channels that have cleared irradiation obstacles. Earlier <necking> or fracture can then reduce ductility. The elastic slope is much less affected than the yield <stress> in this schematic comparison; the exact strength and elongation depend on dose and <temperature>.

= d
{parent=6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

Irradiation both creates <dislocation> loops and supplies mobile point defects that allow existing <dislocations> to climb, rearrange and annihilate. At low initial <dislocation density>, net production can dominate; at high initial density, sink absorption and recovery can dominate. A simple illustrative rate equation is
$$
\frac{d\rho}{dD}=A-B\rho,\qquad
\rho(D)=\frac AB+\left(\rho_0-\frac AB\right)e^{-BD}.
$$
Thus **both initial states approach $\rho_{\rm ss}=A/B$** in this fixed-condition model. Here $D$ is irradiation dose, not a <diffusion coefficient>. The asymptote reflects a dynamic balance, not the cessation of damage. It need not be the same for different alloys, temperatures, dose rates or applied stresses.
