**A periodic crystal cannot have a fivefold crystallographic <rotation> axis.** The <crystallographic restriction theorem> permits <rotation> orders $1,2,3,4,6$ for a periodic <Bravais lattice>. A $72^\circ$ <rotation> cannot preserve such a lattice. This restriction concerns exact <symmetry> of the infinite <crystal structure>; an isolated finite cluster with fivefold <symmetry> is a different object.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

**A motif can have fivefold <symmetry>.** For example, repeat an identically oriented regular pentagonal molecular motif at every point of a square translation lattice. Each isolated motif has fivefold <rotational symmetry>, while the <crystal structure> as a whole does not: rotating about one motif fails to map the other motif centres onto <lattice points>. The <symmetry> of an <atomic basis of a crystal> need not be a <symmetry> of its <Bravais lattice>.

Each Ti has <coordination number> **six**, with oxygen neighbours forming a distorted octahedron. Each oxygen has <coordination number> **three**, with titanium neighbours forming a planar triangle. For oxygen at $(u,u,0)$, the nearest Ti positions can be taken as $(0,0,0)$ and $(1/2,1/2,\pm1/2)$; all three lie in the plane $x=y$. The triangle is not generally equilateral. Equal Ti–O bond lengths in the following calculation do not make every angle ideal.

= Solution

In the <tetragonal crystal system>, physical squared distances use $a^2(\Delta x^2+\Delta y^2)+c^2\Delta z^2$. Two Ti–O distances from the corner Ti have length $\sqrt2ua$; the other four have length $\sqrt{2a^2(1/2-u)^2+c^2/4}$. Equating both to $r$ gives
$$
a=\frac{r}{\sqrt2u},\qquad c=2r\sqrt{1-\frac{(1/2-u)^2}{u^2}}.
$$
Thus, within the equal-bond model,
$$
\boxed{a=4.596\,\mathrm{\mathring A},\qquad c=2.907\,\mathrm{\mathring A}}.
$$
The <rutile structure> remains tetragonal and its octahedral angles remain distorted; these numbers are not a claim about measured ambient-pressure lattice parameters.

The <fluorite structure> has Ce on the corner and face-centre sites of a <face-centered cubic lattice>. Oxygen occupies all eight positions $(x,y,z)$ with each coordinate independently $1/4$ or $3/4$. In projection, there are four oxygen positions $(1/4,1/4),(1/4,3/4),(3/4,1/4),(3/4,3/4)$, each representing two heights. There are four cation sites per conventional <unit cell>, hence $0.4$ Gd substitutions per cell on average. Two substitutions of Gd$^{3+}$ for Ce$^{4+}$ require one <oxygen vacancy> for <charge neutrality>, so the cell contains $0.2$ vacancies. Equivalently, the composition is $\mathrm{Ce_{0.9}Gd_{0.1}O_{1.95}}$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{n_v=\frac{0.2}{a^3}=3.461\times10^{27}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}}.
$$
This uses the supplied lattice parameter literally and treats it as the conventional fluorite-cell edge.

An <oxygen vacancy> has effective carrier charge $q_v=+2e$. Combining its density with its own <diffusion coefficient> in the <Nernst-Einstein relation> gives
$$
\sigma_{\rm ion}=\frac{n_v(2e)^2D_v}{k_BT},\qquad
\boxed{\sigma_{\rm ion}=2.00\,\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}}
$$
at $T=873.15\,\mathrm K$. The oxygen ions move oppositely to the vacancies and carry the same conventional current. This estimate assumes independently migrating vacancies, negligible correlation corrections, and negligible electronic contribution to the reported <ionic conductivity>.

Write the <Arrhenius equation> as $D=D_0e^{-Q/(k_BT)}$. Eliminating the prefactor with two temperatures gives
$$
Q=\frac{k_B\ln(D_{600}/D_{300})}{1/T_{300}-1/T_{600}}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\boxed{Q=0.795\,\mathrm{eV}=76.7\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}}.

Each missing oxide ion leaves two units of excess positive effective charge, compensated by reducing two Ce$^{4+}$ ions to Ce$^{3+}$. For $0.2$ <oxygen vacancies> per conventional <unit cell>, $0.4$ of its four cerium ions must be trivalent: **10% Ce$^{3+}$**. For the <L12 structure>, the <crystal basis structure factor> is
$$
F_{hkl}=f_{\rm Sc}+f_{\rm Al}\left[e^{i\pi(h+k)}+e^{i\pi(h+l)}+e^{i\pi(k+l)}\right].
$$
Equal parity of $h,k,l$ makes all three phase factors $+1$, hence
$$
\boxed{F_{hkl}=f_{\rm Sc}+3f_{\rm Al}}.
$$
The <electron> scattering factors are evaluated at the reflection's scattering vector.

{parent=i}

= Solution
{parent=2}

For mixed parity, one of the three pair sums is even and two are odd. Thus their phase factors sum to $-1$, giving
$$
\boxed{F_{hkl}=f_{\rm Sc}-f_{\rm Al}}.
$$
These <superlattice reflections> reveal chemical ordering. Their kinematic intensity is proportional to $|f_{\rm Sc}-f_{\rm Al}|^2$, and vanishes in the hypothetical limit of equal species scattering factors.

Keep the matrix spots and add precipitate reflections at all integer $(h,k,0)$ with $0<h^2+k^2\leq8$. Even-even spots are fundamental; every other allowed position in this zone is a <superlattice reflection>. The added positions are $100,010,110,210,120$ and their symmetry-related signs. Each position actually denotes the corresponding signed index pair, not one radial ring. The precipitate grid spacing is $1/a_p$; the nearest fundamental separation is $2/a_p=0.4878\,\mathrm{\mathring A^{-1}}$. If matrix and precipitates are coherent and similarly oriented, their fundamental spots approximately coincide, with finite mismatch potentially giving splitting or broadening. The sketch assumes common orientation, small <strain>, appreciable sampled precipitate <volume> and predominantly single scattering; <electron diffraction> can have dynamical intensity effects.

Choose an objective aperture around a precipitate <superlattice reflection>, such as $100$, which is absent from the disordered aluminium matrix. The selected beam then makes precipitates bright in <dark-field transmission electron microscopy>, while the matrix gives little intensity in that beam. This reduces excitation error and can increase precipitate brightness; the exact maximum also depends on foil thickness, <strain> and dynamical scattering. At $227^\circ\mathrm C\simeq500\,\mathrm K$, $1000/T\simeq2$. Reading the <carbon> line gives $D_C\sim2\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m^2s^{-1}}$ to graphical accuracy. With $t=60\,\mathrm s$, the characteristic <diffusion length> is

\boxed{\sqrt{Dt}\sim0.35\,\mathrm{\mu m}}.
$$
If “distance” means a one-coordinate root-mean-square displacement, use $\sqrt{2Dt}\sim0.49\,\mathrm{\mu m}$; the three-dimensional radial root-mean-square is $\sqrt{6Dt}\sim0.85\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. The graph does not justify precision between these conventions. Equal <diffusion> distance in equal time requires equal <diffusion coefficients>. The <copper> line reaches this diffusivity around $1000/T\simeq0.96$, giving **$T_{\rm Cu}\sim1.0\times10^3\,\mathrm K$, about $770^\circ\mathrm C$**. <Carbon> moves interstitially through <ferrite>, whereas <copper> self-diffusion is substitutional and vacancy-mediated, with a much higher effective activation barrier.

At $700^\circ\mathrm C$ the undercooling below the eutectoid <temperature> is small, so the free-energy driving <force> for forming <pearlite> is weak and <nucleation> is difficult. At $300^\circ\mathrm C$ the driving <force> is larger, but <carbon> <diffusion> is slow, limiting the coupled growth of <ferrite> and <cementite>. Their competition produces the nose of the <isothermal transformation diagram> near $550^\circ\mathrm C$ and $1\,\mathrm s$. A roughly linear quench from $740$–$800^\circ\mathrm C$ must pass left of this nose, requiring a rate of order **$2\times10^2$–$3\times10^2\,\mathrm{K\,s^{-1}}$**, then continue below the martensite-start region near $240^\circ\mathrm C$. The tangent to the full start curve, rather than just the nose, refines this estimate; a true continuous-cooling diagram would be preferable. <Martensite> forms by a coordinated lattice-shape change without long-range <carbon> partitioning, so its formation is a <displacive transformation>. The tetragonal-to-monoclinic transformation of <zirconia> is a nonmetallic example.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

As-quenched <martensite> generally has greater <hardness> and strength, but lower ductility and <toughness>, than ordinary pearlitic steel. Its supersaturated <carbon> and distorted lattice impede <dislocation glide>; quench <stresses> also promote cracking. <Pearlite> combines hard <cementite> lamellae with more deformable <ferrite>, with finer spacings generally giving greater strength. **Austenitize, quench to <martensite>, then temper** to obtain a useful compromise between indentation resistance and resistance to fast fracture. Tempering below the austenitizing range relieves <stresses> and develops a fine carbide-containing microstructure. It trades some maximum <hardness> for improved <toughness>; the <temperature> and time must suit the required balance.

= c
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The lamellae are **<ferrite> and <cementite>**, together forming <pearlite>. The displayed quantity is surface height in an <atomic force microscopy> image. <Nital> preferentially attacks the <ferrite>, leaving it lower and therefore darker; the narrow raised <cementite> lamellae appear bright. This is not the same contrast mechanism as brightness in an optical micrograph. Faster cooling that still permits <pearlite> formation causes transformation at greater undercooling and generally gives **finer lamellar spacing**. Cooling sufficiently rapidly to bypass diffusional transformation instead produces other products, potentially <martensite>; it does not merely keep making <pearlite> indefinitely finer.

The exponential in the <Peierls stress> makes a large core-width-to-<Burgers vector> ratio $w/b$ favourable for easy <dislocation glide>. Densely packed slip planes often have wider cores because the shear distortion can spread over more atomic rows, reducing the lattice-periodic barrier. Close-packed directions provide short lattice translations and hence small allowed Burgers vectors; their line energies, approximately proportional to $Gb^2$, are also relatively low. These effects favour dense planes and directions, but the relation between packing and core width is a physical argument rather than a consequence of substituting arbitrary independent $w,b$ into the formula. Body-centred cubic metals have densely packed directions without a truly close-packed plane, and their screw-core behaviour can require additional qualifications.

The cosine of the angle between <Burgers vector> and line direction is $1/3$, so the angle is $70.53^\circ$: **the <dislocation> is mixed**. Both vectors must lie in its <dislocation glide> plane. Their <cross product> is proportional to
$$
(1,-1,1)\times(1,1,1)=(-2,0,2),
$$
so the plane is **$(10\bar1)$**, a member of $\{110\}$. The screw component magnitude is $a/(2\sqrt3)$ and the edge component magnitude is $a\sqrt{2/3}$.

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

At small load the deformation is elastic: $\varepsilon_{\parallel}=\sigma/E$, and, using isotropic elasticity with $E=2G(1+\nu)$, $\nu=0.30$ gives lateral contraction $\varepsilon_\perp=-0.30\varepsilon_\parallel$. At maximum load,
$$
\boxed{\sigma_{\max}=\frac{12.6\,\mathrm{kN}}{\pi(2\,\mathrm{mm})^2}=1.003\,\mathrm{GPa}}.
$$
For the specified <slip system>, the tensile direction is proportional to $(-2,-3,5)$, the slip direction to $(1,-1,1)$ and the plane normal to $(1,0,-1)$. Hence the <Schmid factor> is
$$
m=\frac{|6|}{\sqrt{38}\sqrt3}\frac{|-7|}{\sqrt{38}\sqrt2}
=0.4512,\qquad \boxed{|\tau_{\max}|=0.452\,\mathrm{GPa}}.
$$
<Schmid's law> predicts glide when this reaches the <critical resolved shear stress>. Before that, unloading reverses the <strain>; after activation, glide produces permanent <strain> and may generate <work hardening> and additional slip. **The supplied data do not fix the yield load.** Neither the adjective “ultra-strong” nor $E/G$ supplies a critical shear <stress> or an absolute modulus. Other <slip systems> must also be checked before claiming which activates first. In particular, the specimen remains elastic through the maximum load if every relevant system stays below its activation threshold.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

<Burgers vector> conservation gives two candidate reactions, depending on the relative signs of the approaching segments:
$$
\mathbf b_1+\mathbf b_2=a[001],\qquad
\mathbf b_1-\mathbf b_2=a[1\bar10].
$$
Initially $b_1^2+b_2^2=3a^2/2$. The sum product has $b^2=a^2$, while the difference product has $b^2=2a^2$. Thus **the $a[001]$ product is favoured by <Frank's rule>; the $a[1\bar10]$ product is disfavoured**. The result is not annihilation, because the two supplied vectors are not opposite. The same ordering survives a simple character correction: with $\nu=0.30$, line energy proportional to $Gb^2[\cos^2\chi+\sin^2\chi/(1-\nu)]$ gives the two parent segments a combined coefficient $1.786a^2$, whereas any character of the sum product has coefficient between $a^2$ and $1.429a^2$ and any character of the difference product at least $2a^2$, comparing equal lengths and common logarithmic factors. A favourable <dislocation junction> can obstruct further glide and harden the crystal. Its actual mobility requires the junction line and allowed glide plane: the second screw can occupy several $\{110\}$ planes containing $[\bar111]$, so geometry is not unique. For instance, its $(101)$ plane intersects $(10\bar1)$ along $[010]$; an $a[001]$ junction on that line would glide geometrically on $(100)$, not either original plane. <Dislocation cross-slip>, junction unzipping or bypass can compete under load. An energy inequality alone is not a universal proof of a permanently sessile product.

The fixed support supplies the end shear <force> and the largest bending moment, $FL$. In elementary beam bending, the greatest longitudinal tensile and compressive <stresses> lie on opposite outer faces of the section at the **fixed end**, with zero longitudinal bending <stress> on the neutral axis. For a downward tip load on a horizontal <cantilever>, the upper face is in tension and the lower face in compression. Transverse shear <stress> has a different distribution, largest near the neutral axis for a rectangular section; “highest <force>” should therefore be interpreted with the <stress> component specified.

= Solution

The rectangular <second moment of area> is $I=wh^3/12$. The maximum bending <stress> in the <cantilever> is $FL(h/2)/I=6FL/(wh^2)$. If $h$ is fixed, choose the minimum allowed width $w=6FL/(\sigma_fh^2)$. Its <mass> is then
$$
m_A=\rho Lwh=\frac{6FL^2}{h}\frac{\rho}{\sigma_f}.
$$
Thus the <material performance index> to maximize is
$$
\boxed{M_A=\frac{\sigma_f}{\rho}}.
$$
For unequal tensile and compressive strengths, use the smaller permissible <stress> in this symmetric section. The derivation assumes bending-strength control, not a separate deflection, shear, buckling or existing-crack constraint.

{parent=b}

= Solution

With $w$ fixed, bending strength requires $h=\sqrt{6FL/(w\sigma_f)}$. Substitution into the <cantilever> <mass> gives
$$
m_B=L\sqrt{6FLw}\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{\sigma_f}}.
$$
The <material performance index> to maximize is therefore
$$
\boxed{M_B=\frac{\sqrt{\sigma_f}}{\rho}}.
$$
Changing the free geometric variable changes the strength exponent. A stiffness-limited problem would instead involve <Young's modulus>; the supplied modulus does not enter this strength-only index.

On a logarithmic strength–density <materials selection chart>, constant $M_A$ gives $\sigma_f=M_A\rho$, a guideline of slope one; constant $M_B$ gives $\sigma_f=M_B^2\rho^2$, of slope two. Moving each guideline upwards selects better candidates. **A suitably qualified longitudinal carbon-fibre-reinforced polymer is a promising candidate for both designs.** Composite families occupy favourable high-strength/low-density regions in published course selection charts, but a beam must meet its compressive as well as tensile allowable.<Examples of Materials Selection by Wright State University>[Unit 5, strength–density charts and composite selection examples; family comparisons are screening evidence, not universal longitudinal compression data]{ref}

For scale, an explicitly illustrative candidate with $\sigma_{\rm lim}=1\,\mathrm{GPa}$ in both required loading senses and $\rho=1600\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ would have $M_A=6.25\times10^5\,\mathrm{m^2s^{-2}}$ and $M_B=19.76\,\mathrm{Pa^{1/2}m^3kg^{-1}}$, exceeding the best supplied materials. = Solution
{parent=d}

Check stiffness and tip deflection using <Young's modulus>, failure from existing cracks using <fracture toughness> or the supplied critical energy-release rate, local crushing at supports, shear failure, buckling, fatigue and joint design. In a simple linear-elastic crack model, $K_c^2\simeq E'G_c$, so <toughness> cannot be inferred from strength alone. Wood also has grain-direction dependence, moisture sensitivity, natural defects, property scatter and time-dependent creep; the design must control grain alignment and compression-side stability. Metals raise issues of corrosion, <mass>, joining, machining, residual <stress> and heat-treatment state. Cost, availability, reproducibility, service <temperature> and end-of-life treatment can decide between otherwise feasible designs. A high merit index is a screening result, not a complete safety specification.

A <diamond anvil cell> concentrates <force> onto tiny opposing culets, reaching very high <pressure> with small samples and useful optical access through diamond; its small <volume>, <pressure> gradients, alignment demands and difficulties with some heating or sample environments can limit experiments. A <multi-anvil press> supports a much larger sample and is useful for high-temperature synthesis and bulk measurements, but usually reaches lower ultimate pressures and has more restricted optical access. Hydrostaticity, attainable <temperature> and calibration depend on the actual apparatus and <pressure> medium; neither design guarantees uniform <pressure>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Within each cubic phase, increasing <pressure> decreases $a$, so $d_{hkl}=a/\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}$ decreases and <Bragg's law> moves a given reflection to higher $2\theta$. Near $2.55\,\mathrm{GPa}$, peaks of the <rock salt structure> disappear as those of the <CsCl structure> appear, potentially with a coexistence interval. The former has an FCC translation selection rule, with $h,k,l$ all even or all odd; mixed-parity reflections vanish kinematically. The latter has primitive-cubic translations and basis factor $f_K+(-1)^{h+k+l}f_{Cl}$, so it has no FCC extinction rule. Odd-sum reflections may nevertheless be weak because the two ionic scattering factors are similar. Peak spacings, relative intensities and the cell <volume> per formula unit change discontinuously. A simple uniform shift of the entire original pattern cannot describe the structural transition.

Use only the high-pressure <CsCl structure> branch to extrapolate its <volume> per formula unit $V=a^3$. A straight-line least-squares fit to those three measurements gives approximately

V(P)=(52.285-1.300P)\,\mathrm{\mathring A^3},

with $P$ in GPa. Thus $V(10)\simeq39.29\,\mathrm{\mathring A^3}$, $a(10)\simeq3.400\,\mathrm{\mathring A}$ and $d_{111}=a/\sqrt3\simeq1.963\,\mathrm{\mathring A}$. Taking Cu K$\alpha$ wavelength $\lambda\simeq1.541\,\mathrm{\mathring A}$ in <Bragg's law> gives

\boxed{\theta\simeq23.1^\circ,\qquad 2\theta\simeq46.2^\circ}.
$$
The estimate is model-dependent: a straight-line extrapolation of $a(P)$ instead gives $2\theta\simeq46.0^\circ$. The data stop at $3.81\,\mathrm{GPa}$, so the absence of further phase transitions does not by itself determine an exact equation of state to $10\,\mathrm{GPa}$. The $111$ peak can also be weak because its basis factor is $f_K-f_{Cl}$.

The <coordination number> rises from six in the <rock salt structure> to eight in the <CsCl structure>. In the elementary hard-sphere <ionic bonding> model, accommodating eight opposite neighbours favours a larger cation/anion radius ratio: the geometrical limiting ratios are $\sqrt2-1$ for sixfold and $\sqrt3-1$ for eightfold coordination. The high-pressure change is therefore consistent with $r_K/r_{Cl}$ increasing under compression. Since
$$
\frac{d}{dP}\ln\frac{r_K}{r_{Cl}}=\kappa_{r,Cl}-\kappa_{r,K},\qquad
\kappa_r=-\frac{d\ln r}{dP},
$$
**the chloride ion is inferred to be more compressible than the potassium ion** in this radius-ratio interpretation. This is a qualitative structural argument; real ionic radii depend on coordination and electronic structure, and the transition does not uniquely determine separate microscopic compressibilities.
