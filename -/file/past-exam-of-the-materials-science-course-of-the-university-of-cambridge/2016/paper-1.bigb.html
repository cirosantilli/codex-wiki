= Solution
{parent=i}

**This is a <C-centered orthorhombic lattice>.** The translation $(1/2,1/2,0)$ takes each A molecule to another A molecule and each B molecule to another B molecule. Thus the conventional cell has two <lattice points>.

A two-molecule <crystal motif> can be chosen as A at $(0,0,0)$ and B at $(1/2,0,1/2)$. Attaching that same motif to both C-centered <lattice points> produces all four molecules, hence **eight <iodine> atoms per conventional cell**.

The centers alone appear face-centered, but $(1/2,0,1/2)$ exchanges A and B orientations. It is not a translation symmetry of the oriented molecular solid. An F-centered assignment would discard essential motif information.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

**There are $2_1$ <screw axes> parallel to $[100]$ through $(y/b,z/c)=(1/4,0),(3/4,0),(1/4,1/2),(3/4,1/2)$**, with periodic repeats.

For the first axis the operation in fractional coordinates is
$$
(x,y,z)\longmapsto(x+1/2,1/2-y,-z).
$$
It rotates by $180^\circ$ about the axis and translates by $a/2$. For example, it sends the A center at $(0,0,0)$ to the A center at $(1/2,1/2,0)$, and sends B at $(1/2,0,1/2)$ to B at $(0,1/2,1/2)$ modulo integer translations. A half-turn preserves each unoriented diatomic bond. A second application gives one full $a$ translation.

There are also ordinary twofold axes through the molecular-center rows; those should not be confused with the <screw axes> halfway between the corresponding rows.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The relevant planes contain $[100]$, so their normals lie in the $bc$ plane. The symmetry operations can be checked directly on the height-labelled molecules:

* **<Glide planes> of type $c$, parallel to $(010)$ at $y/b=1/4,3/4$**, for example
$$
(x,y,z)\longmapsto(x,1/2-y,z+1/2).
$$
Reflection reverses the bond slope, and the $c/2$ translation carries A to B with the correct height.

* **<Glide planes> of type $a$, parallel to $(001)$ at $z/c=1/4,3/4$**, for example
$$
(x,y,z)\longmapsto(x+1/2,y,1/2-z).
$$
This again exchanges A and B. Combining it with the C-centering translation gives the equivalent description with a $b/2$ glide in the same plane.

**There are no ordinary mirror planes containing $[100]$ for the illustrated generic bond inclination.** Reflection alone would reverse a bond slope without the necessary accompanying translation. A mirror perpendicular to $[100]$ is a different symmetry and is outside the requested zone.

At fixed <temperature>, a phase's <Gibbs free energy> satisfies $\mathrm dG=V\,\mathrm dp$. For the metallic-minus-molecular difference,
$$
\left(\frac{\partial\Delta G}{\partial p}\right)_T=\Delta V<0.
$$
**Increasing <pressure> favors the smaller-volume metallic phase.** With a pressure-independent <volume> approximation, $\Delta G(p)\simeq\Delta G(p_0)+(p-p_0)\Delta V$, so <pressure> can overcome an initially positive free-energy difference. The <volume> comparison explains the direction of stabilization; it does not by itself determine the transition <pressure> or the electronic origin of <metallic bonding>.

**The numerical vacancy fraction depends on what the supplied diffusivity describes.** In the usual effective mobile-vacancy calculation, the $0.9236$ carriers per cell are <oxygen vacancies>, each of effective charge $+2e$. There are eight oxygen sites, so that convention gives
$$
\boxed{f_v=\frac{0.9236}{8}=0.1155\simeq11.5\%.\quad\text{Vacancy-carrier convention}}
$$
There is a real qualification: the printed description calls the measured particles oxygen carrier <ions>. If $D$ is their tracer diffusivity and every occupied oxygen site is counted as an uncorrelated carrier, the inversion instead gives $N_O=0.9236$, formally $f_v=1-N_O/8=0.8845$. Such an enormous oxygen deficiency is inconsistent with the ordinary lightly doped fluorite picture; it is not a credible deduction of $88.5\%$ vacancies in stabilized <zirconia>.

More generally the conductivity involves a collective <diffusion coefficient>, whereas <tracer diffusion> includes jump correlations. With the explicitly defined convention $H_R=D_{\rm tr}/D_\sigma$,
$$
\sigma=\frac{n_O(2e)^2D_{\rm tr}}{H_Rk_BT},
\qquad n_O=H_R n_{\rm eff}.
$$
Without a carrier definition and the relevant correlation information, **the literal tracer data do not uniquely establish the vacancy fraction**. The consistent vacancy-carrier interpretation gives the boxed **$11.5\%$** estimate. Interpreting the same measured $D$ as an oxygen-ion tracer diffusivity requires the collective/tracer conversion above; ordinary <Nernst-Einstein relation> arithmetic cannot identify these two carrier populations. This states the model assumption needed for the numerical result without treating the physically implausible uncorrelated oxygen-ion inversion as an established material composition.

**<Eutectic solidification> is the coupled transformation of a liquid into two distinct solid phases at the <eutectic temperature> and composition**, $L\rightarrow\alpha+\beta$. At fixed <pressure> in a binary system the three-phase equilibrium is invariant. An exactly eutectic liquid can therefore finish freezing at one <temperature>, instead of over a broad liquid-plus-solid interval.

<Eutectic compositions> are useful in joining alloys, for example silver-copper <brazing> filler: the comparatively low melting <temperature> and narrow freezing interval help liquid flow into a joint and then solidify. Alternating growth of the two phases often produces a fine lamellar <eutectic microstructure>. The eutectic is a two-phase aggregate, not a new single solid phase.

**At equilibrium at room <temperature> the phases are pure Ag and pure Cu, with <mass fractions> $0.80$ and $0.20$.** A representative <solidification> structure contains primary Ag-rich grains or dendrites surrounded by a finer two-phase eutectic aggregate. The alloy shear yield <stress> can be roughly $70/0.78\simeq90$ times the stated pure-aluminium <critical resolved shear stress>. Strengthening raises the <stress> required for <dislocation glide> rather than requiring homogeneous shearing of a perfect crystal.

* **<Solid solution strengthening>:** substitutional solutes create elastic and chemical disturbances that interact with <dislocations>. A moving <dislocation> must escape or drag these disturbances.
* **<Precipitation hardening>:** suitably spaced second-phase particles obstruct glide. Small shearable precipitates resist cutting; larger nonshearable obstacles require <dislocation> bowing and bypass. Particle spacing and coherency matter, so overaging can reduce strength.
* **<Work hardening>:** prior plastic deformation increases <dislocation density>. Intersections, junctions and forest <dislocations> impede subsequent glide; a common scaling is $\Delta\tau\sim\alpha Gb\sqrt{\rho_d}$.
* **<Grain boundary strengthening>:** smaller grains shorten the distance over which <dislocations> can pile up and make slip transfer across differently oriented grains more difficult. In its ordinary regime, the <Hall-Petch relation> is $\sigma_y=\sigma_0+k_y d^{-1/2}$.

These mechanisms can coexist. The numerical comparison is an order-of-magnitude comparison of shear strengths; it is not a conversion of the alloy's shear yield <stress> directly into a tensile yield <stress>.

For FCC <aluminium>, consider $\{111\}\langle110\rangle$ slip. Normalize the tensile direction and one candidate system:
$$
\mathbf l_0=\frac{(1,2,4)}{\sqrt{21}},\qquad
\mathbf n=\frac{(-1,1,1)}{\sqrt3},\qquad
\mathbf s=\frac{(1,0,1)}{\sqrt2}.
$$
Here $\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf s=0$. Comparing all twelve systems gives the largest <Schmid factor> on $(\overline111)[101]$:
$$
m=|\mathbf l_0\cdot\mathbf n|\,|\mathbf l_0\cdot\mathbf s|
=\frac{25}{21\sqrt6}=0.48601.
$$
<Schmid's law> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\sigma_y=\frac{0.78}{0.48601}=1.605\,\mathrm{MPa}.}
$$
During ideal single slip, the tensile direction expressed in crystal coordinates rotates **towards $[101]$**, along the great-circle path joining the original axis to that slip direction. It reaches a symmetry boundary where another <slip system> becomes equally favored. This <single-slip tensile-axis rotation> concerns the crystal relative to the externally held loading direction, rather than a rotation of the laboratory loading apparatus.

A second <slip system> begins to operate as the tensile-axis rotation makes its <resolved shear stress> comparable to that on the primary system. <Dislocations> on intersecting planes interact much more strongly, giving the transition from easy single slip to rapid <work hardening>. The standard geometric estimate neglects differences between the evolving critical <stresses> of the two systems.

The primary direction is $\mathbf s\parallel[101]$. Single-slip shear carries the line joining the specimen ends, expressed in crystal coordinates, along
$$
\mathbf r(t)=(1,2,4)+t(1,0,1)=(1+t,2,4+t).
$$
The initial fundamental sector has $x<y<z$. Its first reached boundary is $x=y$, at $t=1$, giving $\mathbf r=[225]$. At that orientation $(\overline111)[101]$ and $(1\overline11)[011]$ have equal <Schmid factors>. Since the component perpendicular to the primary slip direction is unchanged by that shear, the length ratio follows directly:
$$
\frac{L}{L_0}=\frac{|(2,2,5)|}{|(1,2,4)|}
=\sqrt{\frac{33}{21}}=\sqrt{\frac{11}7}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\varepsilon_{\rm eng}=\sqrt{11/7}-1=0.2536\simeq25.4\%.}
$$
The corresponding logarithmic <strain> is $\ln(L/L_0)=0.2260$. This is an **ideal equal-critical-stress geometric onset estimate**. Real secondary slip can start earlier or later because of <latent hardening>, nonuniform deformation and grip constraints; the problem supplies no hardening law that would determine that shift.<Applied Mechanics of Solids by A. F. Bower>[§3.12.2, points10–12: tensile-axis rotation, secondary slip and stages of hardening]{ref}

$$
The qualitative contrast is nevertheless meaningful. FCC <aluminium> has close-packed <glide planes> and relatively mobile <dislocations>. <Magnesium> also has easy close-packed basal glide, although it lacks enough equivalently easy <slip systems> for arbitrary room-temperature polycrystal deformation.

BCC <iron> has no close-packed plane. Its screw-dislocation cores and lattice-friction barrier give much stronger resistance to glide; thermally activated kink motion makes the critical <stress> particularly temperature- and rate-sensitive. This is the relevant Peierls-stress explanation. A high polycrystal constraint factor cannot explain the supplied contrast, because the data are already <critical resolved shear stresses>. Neither the precise factor35 nor a universal BCC/FCC ratio follows from crystal packing alone: purity, <dislocation> structure, <temperature> and testing conditions also matter.

For a spherical homogeneous <nucleus>, the capillarity approximation gives
$$
\Delta G(r)=4\pi r^2\gamma+\frac{4\pi r^3}{3}\Delta g_V.
$$
The first term penalizes creating an interface; below the melting point the bulk free-energy change per <volume> $\Delta g_V$ is negative and favors growth. Near $T_m$, neglect the <temperature> dependence of the <latent heat>. Since the free-energy difference vanishes at $T_m$,
$$
\Delta g_V\simeq\Delta H_V\frac{\Delta T}{T_m},
\qquad\Delta T=T_m-T>0,
\qquad\Delta H_V<0\quad\text{on freezing}.
$$
Set the <radius> derivative to zero:
$$
\frac{\mathrm d\Delta G}{\mathrm dr}=8\pi r\gamma+4\pi r^2\Delta g_V=0,
\qquad
\boxed{r^*=-\frac{2\gamma}{\Delta g_V}
=-\frac{2\gamma T_m}{\Delta H_V\Delta T}.}
$$
The nonzero stationary point is a maximum, since $\Delta G''(r^*)=-8\pi\gamma<0$. Subcritical <nuclei> lower free energy by shrinking; supercritical <nuclei> lower it by growing. The positive <radius> and its inverse-supercooling dependence follow from the negative freezing <enthalpy>. This derivation assumes an isotropic, size-independent interfacial energy and a bulk-like spherical <nucleus>; it is not a molecular-scale kinetic theory.

**<Material creep> is time-dependent permanent deformation under sustained <stress>.** Its rate is often appreciable at a large <homologous temperature> $T/T_m$, because <diffusion> and obstacle-escape processes become rapid. In an activated regime a useful form is
$$
\dot\varepsilon=A\sigma^n d^{-p}\exp[-Q_c/(RT)],
$$
with additional elastic, geometric and <temperature> prefactors absorbed into $A$ for the chosen approximation. The <activation energy> belongs to the rate-controlling process: bulk <diffusion>, grain-boundary <diffusion> and <dislocation> motion need not have the same value.

* **A: power-law <dislocation> <material creep>.** <Dislocations> glide and climb around obstacles; vacancy transport enables climb and recovery. The rate is strongly stress-dependent, typically with $n$ several times greater than one. It dominates the higher-stress, hot field below the glide-strength boundary.
* **B: <Nabarro-Herring creep>.** Stress-directed <vacancy diffusion> through the grain interiors transfers material between differently stressed boundaries, extending grains along tension. In the simple equiaxed-grain model $\dot\varepsilon\propto D_V\sigma/(Td^2)$. It occupies the high-temperature, low-stress field.
* **C: <Coble creep>.** The analogous transport runs predominantly along <grain boundaries>. The characteristic scaling is $\dot\varepsilon\propto\delta D_{GB}\sigma/(Td^3)$, where $\delta$ is an effective boundary thickness. The lower activation cost of boundary <diffusion> makes it competitive at lower <temperature> than bulk-diffusion <material creep>.

Boundary sliding can accommodate grain-shape changes during diffusional <material creep>; it is not a reason to identify either <diffusion> field as <dislocation glide>. The supplied map's A/B/C placement, including the boundary between B and C, determines these assignments. Its numerical contours apply to the material state used to construct that map, not to arbitrary <grain sizes>.

**Lower the operating <temperature> or <stress>, suppress <diffusion>, and remove or stabilize the paths that accommodate <material creep>.** Increasing the melting <temperature> can reduce $T/T_m$ at a specified operating <temperature>. Chemistry that reduces diffusivity or impedes <dislocation climb> can reduce the <material creep> rate; stable precipitates or dispersoids can resist glide and climb, provided they do not dissolve or coarsen rapidly in service.

Large grains reduce both Nabarro-Herring and <Coble creep>, according to their $d^{-2}$ and $d^{-3}$ dependencies. Directional <solidification> can reduce boundaries normal to the dominant tensile <stress>, while a single crystal removes grain-boundary transport and sliding from the ordinary polycrystal mechanism inventory. It does not remove bulk <diffusion> or <dislocation> <material creep>.

This is why room-temperature grain-refinement strengthening is not automatically a high-temperature <material creep> strategy: fine grains create more boundary transport paths. Any protective <microstructure> must remain stable at the service <temperature>, and the design must also avoid <stress> concentrations and unwanted thermal loading.

At height $z$ above the free lower end, a uniform vertical filament carries the weight of length $z$. With cross-sectional area $A$,
$$
F(z)=\rho A g z,\qquad\sigma(z)=\rho g z,
\qquad\boxed{\sigma_{\max}=\rho g L.}
$$
**The maximum <stress> is at the top and is independent of diameter.** The diameter cancels because both <mass> and load-bearing area scale with $A$. Neglecting self-heating gradients, evaporation and initial fabrication <stress>, this top point sets the first creep-strain limit.

For a clearly labelled external reference estimate, take $\rho=1.93\times10^4\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. Then $\sigma_{\max}=1.89\times10^4\,\mathrm{Pa}=0.0189\,\mathrm{MPa}$; the area is $3.14\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m^2}$ and the total suspended <mass> is about $6.06\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{kg}$. The <density> is independently tabulated, not invented as a supplied 2016 value.<Atomic and nuclear properties of tungsten by the Particle Data Group>[2009 table, density entry]{ref}

Use the original map's top <temperature> axis at $2500^\circ\mathrm C$ and its **right-hand tensile-stress axis**, so no uncertain shear-stress conversion or additional <shear modulus> is required. The point lies in C, between the $10^{-9}$ and $10^{-8}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ contours, nearer $10^{-8}$. A logarithmic interpolation of the scanned contours gives about $6\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, with only graphical precision. Thus
$$
t_f\simeq\frac{0.05}{\dot\varepsilon_{\max}}
\simeq8\times10^6\,\mathrm s
\simeq2.3\times10^3\,\mathrm h,
$$
or **of order three months in this steady-rate, reference-density map estimate**. Reading only the bracketing contours gives the appropriately broad interval $5\times10^6$ to $5\times10^7\,\mathrm s$; the graph does not support an exact service-life number.

For the exact prescribed input, the answer remains
$$
\boxed{t_f\simeq\frac{0.05}{\dot\varepsilon(2500^\circ\mathrm C,\rho gL)}}.
$$
This estimate uses the cited reference <density> and the grain-size assumptions of the supplied map. <Thermal expansion> lowers <density>, and an evolved area or tertiary <material creep> changes the rate; a steady initial-rate estimate is not a guaranteed lifetime. In particular, a different filament <grain size> could require a different map. Other failure mechanisms could shorten useful life.
