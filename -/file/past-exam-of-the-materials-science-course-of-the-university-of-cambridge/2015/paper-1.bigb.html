Use fractional coordinates in a conventional cubic cell with an atom at the origin. The <diamond cubic structure> consists of an FCC <Bravais lattice> with the two-atom basis
$$
(0,0,0),\qquad(\tfrac14,\tfrac14,\tfrac14).
$$
The FCC translations within the conventional cell are $(0,0,0)$, $(0,\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$, $(\tfrac12,0,\tfrac12)$ and $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0)$. Adding the basis gives eight atoms per conventional cell. Equivalently, **two identical FCC sublattices are displaced by $a[111]/4$**. It is not a monatomic close-packed arrangement: every atom has four tetrahedrally arranged nearest neighbors rather than twelve.

In the projection, label each atom by its fractional height. The $(0,0)$ and $(1/2,1/2)$ columns have height0; $(0,1/2)$ and $(1/2,0)$ have height1/2. The additional four columns are $(1/4,1/4)$ and $(3/4,3/4)$ at height1/4, and $(1/4,3/4)$ and $(3/4,1/4)$ at height3/4. Boundary columns repeat periodically; they must not be counted as full extra atoms.

The <vertical mirrors of the diamond cubic structure> have the required orientation: their normals lie in the $(001)$ plane. In the chosen atomic-origin convention, their traces satisfy
$$
\boxed{x-y=\frac n2,\qquad x+y=\frac n2,\qquad n\in\mathbb Z,}
$$
with $x,y$ expressed as fractions of $a$. These are the two orientations of vertical $\{110\}$ mirrors and their translated copies. For example, the plane $x=y$ interchanges the first two coordinates and leaves every <diamond> site in an equivalent site. <Reflection> in $x+y=1/2$ sends $(x,y,z)$ to $(1/2-y,1/2-x,z)$, which also preserves the full three-dimensional motif.

The projected traces alone do not test a mirror: the atom heights must also agree under <reflection>. In particular, a vertical $\{100\}$ trace can appear plausible in an unlabelled projection but does not preserve all heights as a pure mirror; glide operations are different symmetry elements.

<Bond-centred inversion in diamond cubic> gives one <inversion centre> at the midpoint of a nearest-neighbor bond:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf c=(\tfrac18,\tfrac18,\tfrac18).}
$$
Mark its projected point $(1/8,1/8)$ and label its height **$z/a=1/8$**. Inversion about this point sends $\mathbf r$ to $(1/4,1/4,1/4)-\mathbf r$. It exchanges the two identical FCC sublattices and therefore preserves the crystal. An individual atom at the chosen origin is not an <inversion centre>.

The nearest-neighbor displacement is $a[111]/4$, of length $a\sqrt3/4$. Touching <hard spheres> therefore obey
$$
2r_{\rm atom}=\frac{\sqrt3a}{4},\qquad
r_{\rm atom}=\frac{\sqrt3a}{8}.
$$
There are eight atoms per conventional cubic cell, giving
$$
\boxed{f_{\rm diamond}=
\frac{8(4\pi r_{\rm atom}^3/3)}{a^3}
=\frac{\pi\sqrt3}{16}\simeq0.3401.}
$$
For monatomic FCC close packing, four atoms per cell touch along a face diagonal, so $r_{\rm atom}=a/(2\sqrt2)$ and
$$
\boxed{f_{\rm ccp}=\frac{\pi}{3\sqrt2}\simeq0.7405.}
$$
Thus the <diamond> <packing fraction> is much smaller. The tetrahedral network leaves substantial open volume. Although its <Bravais lattice> is FCC, the two-atom motif and the shorter hard-sphere contact distance prevent it from inheriting the FCC close-packing fraction.

The four FCC translations give a lattice <structure factor> that is nonzero only when $h,k,l$ are all even or all odd. The identical two-atom motif then multiplies it by
$$
1+\exp\left[\frac{i\pi}{2}(h+k+l)\right].
$$
For $(200)$ the FCC part is allowed, but the motif factor is $1+e^{i\pi}=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{F_{200}=0.}
$$
In the projection, equal numbers of atoms occur at $x/a=0,1/4,1/2,3/4$. Their $(200)$ <phases> are respectively $+1,-1,+1,-1$. The paired layers cancel exactly for equal <silicon> <scattering amplitudes>. This <diamond-basis diffraction extinction> is additional to the FCC centring rule; it is not evidence that no atoms lie on the nominal reflecting planes.

The normal to the section is $[010]$, so its in-plane principal vibration directions are $[100]$ and $[001]$. Their refractive-index difference is
$$
\boxed{\Delta n=1.45-1.32=0.13.}
$$
The slow direction is $[001]$. The optical path retardation is
$$
\Gamma=t\Delta n=(4\ {\rm \mu m})(0.13)=0.52\ {\rm \mu m}.
$$
At an angle $\alpha$ between a principal direction and the <polarizer>, ideal crossed-polar transmission has the form
$$
I(\lambda)=I_P\sin^2(2\alpha)
\sin^2\left(\frac{\pi\Gamma}{\lambda}\right),
$$
where $I_P$ is the intensity after the first <polarizer>. The two components recombine parallel to the incident <polarization> when $\Gamma=m\lambda$, and the crossed analyser removes them. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\lambda=\Gamma/m,\qquad
\lambda_{\rm visible}=520\ {\rm nm}\quad(m=1).}
$$
The next order is260nm, outside ordinary visible illumination. At exact principal-axis alignment, $\sin2\alpha=0$ and every wavelength is extinguished, explaining the black appearance rather than a special single-wavelength cancellation.

An unstressed <glass> is optically isotropic, so it does not split incident light into differently phased in-plane components; it remains dark between crossed polars. A natural explanation of the colored regions after heating is <devitrification>: anisotropic crystallites have nucleated in the <glass>. Edges and surfaces provide <heterogeneous nucleation> sites, making their greater concentration there plausible.

Each approximately uniform crystallite can give a nearly uniform <interference colour>. Regions with similar thickness and effective <birefringence> have similar retardation and therefore similar spectral colour, while differing azimuths multiply the intensity by $\sin^2(2\alpha)$ and change brightness. Identical hue does not prove identical three-dimensional crystallographic orientation.

Stress-induced <birefringence> is a possible alternative in a heat-treated <glass>. The observations alone do not uniquely prove crystallinity; rotating-stage extinction, morphology or diffraction could distinguish the explanations. The crystallite interpretation is the straightforward phase-transformation explanation, without inventing a composition or heat-treatment schedule.

= Solution

On rotating a fixed crystallite around the microscope axis, the angle $\alpha$ to the fixed <polarizer> changes. The factor $\sin^2(2\alpha)$ repeats every90degrees. Each birefringent region is therefore extinguished four times in a complete revolution and reaches maximum brightness45degrees from its own extinction positions.

Different regions need not extinguish together if their principal in-plane directions differ. For a fixed propagation direction through a uniform region, azimuthal rotation changes brightness more directly than its retardation colour: the underlying index difference and thickness remain the same. The isotropic unstressed matrix remains dark throughout rotation.

= Solution

The <quartz compensator> is a <birefringent optical compensator>: a retarder of known principal directions. With its <principal axes> at45degrees to the <polarizer> and analyser, the previously dark isotropic background acquires the compensator's own <interference colour>. The crystals are now viewed through two retarders.

When a specimen's slow axis is parallel to the compensator's slow axis, the retardations add. A90degree relative principal-axis rotation pairs the specimen's slow axis with the compensator's fast axis, and the signed retardations subtract:
$$
\Gamma_{\rm total}=\Gamma_q+\Gamma_s
\quad\hbox{or}\quad
\Gamma_{\rm total}=\Gamma_q-\Gamma_s.
$$
Thus different crystallites can shift to higher or lower <interference> orders even if their original hue was similar. In the subtracting orientation, equal magnitudes can compensate and yield extinction. These shifts identify fast and slow specimen directions.

At a general relative angle the full <polarization> transformation is required; simple scalar addition is only valid for aligned <principal axes>. The source does not specify the compensator retardation, so no unique list of resulting named colours can be assigned. The robust predictions are a changed background, orientation-dependent addition or subtraction, and possible compensation.

The materials interpretation is a cooling-rate gradient in a near-eutectoid <steel>. Before transformation, the parent <thermodynamic phase> is <austenite>. A small section can cool rapidly enough to suppress diffusional decomposition and form <martensite>, a supersaturated, highly strained transformation product. A much thicker interior cools more slowly and can transform diffusively to <pearlite>: alternating <ferrite> and <cementite> lamellae grouped into colonies. Bainite or mixed products are also possible for intermediate histories.

The likely contrast is therefore **a martensite-rich rapidly cooled region and a more pearlitic slowly cooled interior**. Brittle fracture is promoted by the highly strained martensitic state, residual <stresses> and local <stress> concentration. A larger intact interior need not share the same microstructure or local loading. The observations are consistent with this contrast, not a measurement proving pure <thermodynamic phases> everywhere.

The sketches below are generic <steel microstructures>, rather than a component design or fabrication prescription. No cooling curve, dimensions or transformation diagram is provided, so exact <thermodynamic phase> fractions, plate sizes and a universally pearlitic centre cannot be inferred.

For $N$ distinguishable lattice sites occupied by $N_i$ mutually indistinguishable atoms of each species, the number of configurations is
$$
\Omega=\frac{N!}{\prod_{i=1}^nN_i!},\qquad
N_i=X_iN,\quad\sum_iX_i=1.
$$
Using <Stirling's approximation>,
$$
\ln\Omega\simeq
N\ln N-\sum_iN_i\ln N_i
=-N\sum_iX_i\ln X_i.
$$
For one mole, $N=N_A$ and $k_BN_A=R$, so
$$
\Delta S_{\rm mix}=-R\sum_iX_i\ln X_i.
$$
<Equiatomic configurational entropy> follows when the composition has $X_i=1/n$, giving
$$
\boxed{\Delta S_{\rm mix}=R\ln n.}
$$
The reference is the unmixed configurational arrangement, and the model assumes random site occupation without <short-range order>. It counts <configurational entropy>, not all vibrational, electronic or magnetic <entropy>. A large number of elements does not by itself guarantee a stable single <solid solution>: competing <thermodynamic phases> and the <enthalpy> term also enter the <Gibbs free energy>.

A <Shockley partial dislocation> in an FCC slip plane has <Burgers vector> of the family $a\langle112\rangle/6$. Its vector must lie in the specified plane, and the two vectors must sum to the perfect <dislocation> vector.

For the first plane normal $\mathbf n_1=[\bar111]$, one allowed dissociation is
$$
\boxed{\frac a2[101]
=\frac a6[211]+\frac a6[1\bar12].}
$$
The vectors add to $a[303]/6$, and $\mathbf n_1\cdot[211]=-2+1+1=0$, $\mathbf n_1\cdot[1\bar12]=-1-1+2=0$.

For $\mathbf n_2=[111]$,
$$
\boxed{\frac a2[\bar110]
=\frac a6[\bar211]+\frac a6[\bar12\bar1].}
$$
Their sum is $a[\bar330]/6$, and both have zero dot product with $[111]$. Interchanging leading and trailing labels does not change these vector identities.

Each perfect vector has squared magnitude $a^2/2$, whereas the sum of the two partial squared magnitudes is $a^2/3$. The line-energy estimate proportional to $Gb^2$ therefore favors separation, opposed by the stacking-fault energy of the ribbon between the partials.

Use the two partials on each plane as obtained above. The four pairwise sums, in units of $a/6$, are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{first-plane vector}+\text{second-plane vector}
&\text{resultant}&|\text{resultant}|^2\\\hline
[211]+[\bar211]&[022]&8\\

[1\bar12]+[\bar211]&[\bar103]&10\\
[1\bar12]+[\bar12\bar1]&[011]&2
\end{array}
$$
The shortest resultant gives the largest line-energy reduction in the $Gb^2$ approximation:
$$
\boxed{\frac a6[1\bar12]+\frac a6[\bar12\bar1]
\longrightarrow\frac a6[011].}
$$
The initial summed squared magnitude is $a^2/3$ and the final value is $a^2/18$, a sixfold reduction in that part of the energy estimate.

The line of intersection of the two original planes is parallel to
$$
[\bar111]\times[111]=[0,2,-2]\parallel[01\bar1].
$$
Its dot product with the resultant <Burgers vector> is zero, so the junction is edge in character. A plane containing both line and <Burgers vector> has normal $[100]$, rather than an FCC close-packed $\{111\}$ glide normal. The resulting <stair-rod dislocation> is therefore sessile for ordinary glide and can form a <Lomer-Cottrell lock>. It obstructs further <dislocation> motion and promotes <work hardening>.

This energetic comparison assumes comparable line lengths and elastic prefactors. Geometry, line character and stacking-fault geometry affect a complete junction-energy calculation, but the small resultant identifies the favorable candidate requested by the vector model.

The original diagram is a <Kelvin-Voigt model>: a <spring> and a <dashpot> act in parallel. The <spring> is an ideal elastic element, $\sigma_s=E\varepsilon_s$; under its own sinusoidal <stress> its <strain> is in <phase> with <stress> and energy is stored reversibly. The <dashpot> is viscous, $\sigma_d=\eta\dot\varepsilon_d$; its <strain> under an oscillating <stress> lags by90degrees and mechanical energy is dissipated.

In parallel the <strain> is common and the branch <stresses> add:
$$
\sigma=E\varepsilon+\eta\dot\varepsilon,\qquad
\tau=\eta/E.
$$
For imposed harmonic <stress>, the <strain> amplitude and <phase> lag are
$$
|\varepsilon|=\frac{\sigma_0}{\sqrt{E^2+(\omega\eta)^2}},
\qquad
\delta=\tan^{-1}(\omega\tau).
$$
The short- and long-timescale limits follow under the printed roman labels below.

A sudden constant <stress> $\sigma_0$ gives delayed, bounded creep:
$$
\boxed{\varepsilon(t)=\frac{\sigma_0}{E}
(1-e^{-t/\tau}),\qquad t\geq0.}
$$
The <strain> is initially zero because an instantaneous change would require an infinite <dashpot> <stress>. Initially the <dashpot> carries the load, with finite <strain> rate $\sigma_0/\eta$; the <spring> gradually carries more of it as the rate decays. On unloading, the <strain> recovers exponentially to zero. The network does not produce the instantaneous <strain> jump or unbounded constant-stress creep of a series Maxwell element.

The imposed oscillating <stress> produces a small <strain>, of amplitude approximately $\sigma_0/(\eta\omega)$, nearly90degrees behind the <stress>. **The network appears stiff and predominantly viscous in this stress-controlled high-frequency limit.** It is not an ideal perfectly rigid body at finite frequency.

For a rubber-like solid, a <spring> is a first model of recoverable <elasticity>, while a viscous element can represent rate-dependent loss and delayed recovery. An immediate extension followed by bounded delayed <material creep> is represented by <series spring and Voigt-branch creep>, a <standard linear solid model>:
$$
\varepsilon(t)=\frac{\sigma_0}{E_0}
+\frac{\sigma_0}{E_1}(1-e^{-E_1t/\eta}).
$$
The first term is immediate and recoverable; the second is delayed and also recoverable. For a load held until $t=t_h$, <recovery after a finite-duration stress load> predicts an immediate drop of $\sigma_0/E_0$ on unloading, followed by
$$
\boxed{\varepsilon(t)=\frac{\sigma_0}{E_1}(1-e^{-t_h/\tau})e^{-(t-t_h)/\tau},\qquad\tau=\frac\eta{E_1},\quad t>t_h.}
$$
This supplies a direct comparison with a loading-and-unloading observation: the model returns completely to its original length, with separate immediate and delayed recovery. A single <Kelvin-Voigt model> misses the immediate compliance, while a free series <dashpot> would predict unbounded flow that an intact crosslinked rubber need not show. A <spring> alone suffices over a timescale on which the measured response is effectively immediate and elastic.

These are local small-strain analogues. Large-strain <rubber elasticity> is nonlinear, nearly incompressible and sensitive to temperature, loading history and internal damage. A small set of linear <springs> and <dashpots> cannot reproduce every amplitude, timescale or <hysteresis> loop.

No numerical rubber trace or fitted constants are supplied, so these are explicit qualitative model predictions for comparison with the practical observations.

= Solution

A physical mechanics model of wood must at least acknowledge <anisotropy>: stiffness and creep depend on loading direction. Direction-dependent elastic elements can represent different small-strain moduli, and delayed elastic branches can approximate time-dependent <strain>. A linear-solid network can therefore be a local fit under specified direction, moisture and temperature, rather than a universal scalar constitutive law.

An immediate <strain> followed by delayed <material creep> is captured locally by a <spring> plus a <Kelvin-Voigt model> branch. If residual deformation remains after unloading, a <Burgers material> adds a free series <dashpot> $\eta_0$. Under a constant load,
$$
\varepsilon(t)=\frac{\sigma_0}{E_0}+\frac{\sigma_0}{E_1}(1-e^{-t/\tau})+\frac{\sigma_0t}{\eta_0}.
$$
After a hold time $t_h$, the two elastic contributions recover and the dashpot retains
$$
\boxed{\varepsilon(\infty)=\frac{\sigma_0t_h}{\eta_0}}.
$$
Thus immediate recovery, delayed recovery and a residual set identify different roles for the components; an observed permanent set would rule out a purely recoverable linear-solid fit. A viscous dashpot does not itself model a yield threshold or structural damage. Strength loss, splitting, moisture-dependent response and nonlinear compression cannot be represented by one fixed linear <Kelvin-Voigt model>. No direction-specific measured trace is supplied, so selecting numerical parameters requires the practical data; the constitutive predictions and their limitations are the model comparison that can be made from the paper.

At equilibrium between two <thermodynamic phases> of a pure substance, their molar <Gibbs free energies> are equal. Along a coexistence line,
$$
g_1(T,P)=g_2(T,P),\qquad
\mathrm dg=-s\,\mathrm dT+v\,\mathrm dP.
$$
Subtracting the two differentials gives
$$
-(s_2-s_1)\,\mathrm dT+(v_2-v_1)\,\mathrm dP=0.
$$
For a nonzero volume change,
$$
\boxed{\frac{\mathrm dP}{\mathrm dT}
=\frac{\Delta s}{\Delta v}
=\frac{\Delta h}{T\Delta v}.}
$$
The second equality uses $\Delta g=\Delta h-T\Delta s=0$ on coexistence. Total <entropy> and volume changes may be used instead if they refer to the same amount of material. The ratio must use one consistent transformation direction, so reversing the labels changes both signs and leaves the slope unchanged. This is the general <Clapeyron equation>; the ideal-vapour approximation often called Clausius-Clapeyron makes further assumptions not needed for two solids.

A <multi-anvil press> transmits a large external load through several hard anvils onto a confined pressure medium surrounding the sample. Reducing the loaded area raises pressure, while the surrounding medium and gasket limit lateral escape. A resistive heater within the assembly can heat the sample, with insulation and temperature sensing arranged around it. It provides relatively large sample volumes and useful bulk, high-temperature experimental conditions, but access to the sample and optical paths is restricted, <pressure gradients> and calibration matter, and the maximum pressure is constrained by anvil strength and geometry.

A <diamond anvil cell> squeezes a very small sample and pressure medium in a gasket hole between opposed <diamond> culets. <Diamond> combines high strength with useful optical transparency, permitting spectroscopy and optical <pressure calibration> as well as suitable X-ray access. Heating can be external or laser-based. The very small contact area permits high pressure from a modest total force, but gives a tiny sample volume. Nonhydrostatic <stress>, <pressure gradients>, gasket deformation, heating gradients, chemical compatibility and <diamond> failure are important limitations.

Neither device generates pressure by force alone independent of area and confinement. Nor does a nominal force-to-area ratio guarantee a known hydrostatic sample pressure; experimental pressure and temperature must be calibrated. The two assemblies trade sample volume and access against pressure reach rather than one being universally preferable.

<Diamond> can be **metastable**: its <Gibbs free energy> is higher than <graphite>'s under ambient conditions, but transformation is kinetically inhibited. Conversion requires substantial rearrangement of the strong carbon-bond network and the formation and growth of a new <thermodynamic phase>, with an activation and <nucleation barrier>.

At room temperature the rate can be extremely small even though the free-energy change favors <graphite>. Thermodynamic stability specifies the preferred equilibrium state, not the time needed to reach it. Persistence of <diamond> therefore does not contradict <graphite>'s ambient equilibrium stability.

Use the same molar amount for both <thermodynamic phases>. A hexagonal cell built from two basal lattice vectors at120degrees has volume $(\sqrt3/2)a^2c$, not the volume of a three-times-larger full hexagonal prism. With four atoms per stated <graphite> cell,
$$
V_{m,g}=\frac{N_A}{4}\frac{\sqrt3}{2}
(0.246\times10^{-9})^2(0.671\times10^{-9})
\simeq5.2944\times10^{-6}\ {\rm m^3\,mol^{-1}}.
$$
<Diamond> has eight atoms per conventional cubic cell, so
$$
V_{m,d}=\frac{N_A}{8}(0.357\times10^{-9})^3
\simeq3.4250\times10^{-6}\ {\rm m^3\,mol^{-1}}.
$$
For <graphite> transforming to <diamond>,
$$
\Delta V_m\simeq-1.8693\times10^{-6}\ {\rm m^3\,mol^{-1}},
\qquad
\Delta S_m=2.45-5.69=-3.24\ {\rm J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{\mathrm dP}{\mathrm dT}
\simeq1.73\times10^6\ {\rm Pa\,K^{-1}}
=1.73\ {\rm MPa\,K^{-1}}.}
$$
The positive slope reflects <diamond>'s smaller <entropy> and volume. Higher temperature favors the higher-entropy <graphite> <thermodynamic phase>, requiring greater pressure to compensate in favor of the smaller-volume <diamond> <thermodynamic phase>.

This estimate treats the supplied ambient molar <entropies> and volumes as applicable near coexistence and neglects their changes through <thermal expansion>, <compressibility> and <heat capacity>. It gives an approximate gradient, not the transition pressure or a complete high-pressure <thermodynamic phase> boundary; an intercept would require an additional free-energy datum.

At two-phase equilibrium, the endpoints of a common tangent to the two molar free-energy curves give the coexisting compositions. They are not the separate curve minima. Reading the graph gives approximately $x_{B,\alpha}\simeq0.02$ and $x_{B,m}\simeq0.64$, with a few atomic-percent graphical uncertainty.

Let $f_\alpha$ be the fraction of atoms in the <precipitate> <thermodynamic phase>. Conservation of the overall B fraction gives
$$
0.55=f_\alpha x_{B,\alpha}+
(1-f_\alpha)x_{B,m},
$$
and hence
$$
\boxed{f_\alpha=\frac{x_{B,m}-0.55}
{x_{B,m}-x_{B,\alpha}}
\simeq\frac{0.64-0.55}{0.64-0.02}
\simeq0.145\approx15\%.}
$$

<Orowan bypass> bows a <dislocation> around an unshearable obstacle and leaves a loop. A <dislocation line-tension estimate> for a segment with tension $T_\ell$ gives the limiting shear-stress scale
$$
\Delta\tau\simeq\frac{2T_\ell}{b\ell},
$$
where $\ell$ is a free obstacle gap on the slip plane. For an FCC perfect <dislocation>,
$$
b=\frac a{\sqrt2}
=\frac{0.36}{\sqrt2}\ {\rm nm}
\simeq0.2546\ {\rm nm}.
$$
Using $T_\ell\simeq Gb^2/2$ and the simple gap estimate $\ell\simeq L-d\simeq0.34\ {\rm \mu m}$ gives
$$
\boxed{\Delta\tau\simeq\frac{Gb}{\ell}
\simeq5.5\times10^7\ {\rm Pa}.}
$$
Converting to tensile <stress> requires an orientation factor. With an explicitly assumed polycrystalline FCC <Taylor factor> $M\simeq3$,
$$
\boxed{\Delta\sigma_y\simeq M\Delta\tau
\sim(1.6\text{--}1.7)\times10^8\ {\rm Pa}\approx160\text{--}170\ {\rm MPa}.}
$$
The graph and spacing approximations do not justify a precise last digit. A favorably oriented single crystal with Schmid factor1/2 would instead give $\Delta\sigma_y\simeq2\Delta\tau$, around110MPa.

This is a line-tension estimate. A true slip-plane obstacle distribution, line character, logarithmic elastic corrections and <precipitate> size distribution are not specified. If the quoted pitch is used directly as an effective free spacing, neglecting diameter, the same simplified calculation gives $\Delta\tau\simeq19\ {\rm MPa}$ and $M\Delta\tau\simeq57\ {\rm MPa}$. The larger gap-corrected estimate explicitly uses the size inferred above; the tensile factor and spacing convention must not be silently treated as provided measurements.

= Solution

A single-domain <precipitate> has no internal domain wall available to move. <Magnetization> changes therefore require rotation, curling or reversal of a particle's <magnetization>, rather than ordinary wall displacement through that particle. Relative to a larger multidomain material this can increase <coercivity>, but the actual <coercivity> depends on <anisotropy>, shape and particle interactions. If <thermal activation> over the <anisotropy> barrier is rapid on the observation timescale, superparamagnetic behavior can instead suppress <remanence>. Ferromagnetic composition plus small size alone does not prove a permanent hard magnet.

For an elementary wall-energy comparison, imagine one planar wall through a sphere of radius $r$. Its area is $A_w=\pi r^2$. If that trial two-domain state removes essentially all of the specified stray-field energy while adding wall energy $\gamma_wA_w$, stability of the uniform state requires
$$
\gamma_wA_w\gtrsim U_{\rm stray}.
$$
With $r\simeq0.33\ {\rm \mu m}$ and the supplied $U_{\rm stray}=5\times10^{-18}\ {\rm J}$,
$$
\boxed{\gamma_w\gtrsim
\frac{5\times10^{-18}}{\pi(0.33\times10^{-6})^2}
\simeq1.5\times10^{-5}\ {\rm J\,m^{-2}}.}
$$
This is the requested scale under a full stray-energy-release and one-planar-wall approximation; it is a lower-bound estimate within that model.

A more exact comparison uses the actual energy reduction $\Delta U_{\rm stray}$ of the competing domain pattern, not necessarily the whole original stray energy:
$$
\gamma_w\gtrsim\frac{\Delta U_{\rm stray}}{A_w}
=\frac{\zeta U_{\rm stray}}{A_w},\qquad0\leq\zeta\leq1.
$$
No domain geometry, <anisotropy> energy or barrier is supplied to determine $\zeta$ or establish that the observed state is the thermodynamic ground state rather than kinetically trapped. Thus the given information alone does not establish an unconditional positive numerical lower bound independent of the competing pattern; the elementary estimate must retain its stated assumptions.
