= Wave function

{title2=$\psi(x,t)$}

= Wavefunction
{synonym}

A <wavefunction> is the position representation of a <pure state>. In the one-dimensional model used here, $\psi(x,t)$ takes values in the <complex numbers>, and the <Born rule> gives
$$
\rho(x,t)=|\psi(x,t)|^2,\qquad \Pr(a<x<b)=\int_a^b\rho(x,t)\,dx.
$$
The <probability density> $\rho$ has units of inverse length; the <wavefunction> has units of inverse square root of length. A value of $\rho$ at a point is not itself a probability: probabilities come from an <integral> over a region. For the <infinite square well> <ground state>, $\rho(L/2)=2/L$, whereas the probability of the exact single point $L/2$ is zero. The interesting question is how much probability a finite interval contains.

Two <wavefunctions> differing only by a <global phase> represent the same <pure state>. Relative <quantum phases> between components of a <quantum superposition> affect <matter-wave interference>, so retaining the full <complex number> amplitude is essential. This scalar description covers a particle without active <spin> degrees of freedom; a general <electron> <wavefunction> can have two <spin> components, with the position <probability density> obtained by adding the squared <modulus> of each component.

= Radial wavefunction
{parent=Wave function}
{title2=$R_{nl}(r)$}

<Separation of variables> in a <central potential> writes a <wavefunction> as $R_{nl}(r)Y_l^m(\theta,\phi)$. With normalized <spherical harmonics> the radial factor satisfies $\int_0^\infty r^2|R_{nl}|^2dr=1$. The reduced radial function is $u=rR$, with <normalization> $\int|u|^2dr=1$; the two functions have different behavior at the origin. For a regular Coulomb <eigenstate>, $R_{nl}\propto r^l$ near the origin.

= Single-particle orbital
{parent=Wave function}
{title2=$\phi$}

= Orbital
{synonym}

A single-particle <wavefunction> used as a mode of a many-particle description. An <orbital> can include spatial and <spin> variables; a <spatial orbital> specifies the spatial part. Orthonormal <orbitals> define <creation and annihilation operators>. Occupation of one normalized <orbital> by every <boson> gives a <Hartree product state>, while antisymmetrized occupation by <fermions> gives a <Slater determinant>.

= Single-valued wavefunction
{parent=Wave function}

An ordinary scalar <wavefunction> assigns one complex amplitude to each physical configuration. In <polar coordinates> without an additional twisted boundary condition, the points $(r,\phi)$ and $(r,\phi+2\pi)$ are identical, so the angular factor must be periodic. An angular <eigenfunction> $e^{im\phi}$ therefore requires integer $m$. This scalar orbital condition does not rule out half-integer intrinsic <spin>.

= Momentum representation in quantum mechanics
{parent=Wave function}
{title2=$\phi(k)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\int\psi(x)e^{-ikx}\,dx$}

The <Fourier transform> in the title represents a one-dimensional state in the <wavevector> basis. Its <probability density> is $|\phi(k)|^2$, normalized by $\int|\phi(k)|^2dk=1$ when $\int|\psi(x)|^2dx=1$. For physical <momentum> $p=\hbar k$, the density per unit momentum is $|\phi(p/\hbar)|^2/\hbar$. A confined stationary <energy eigenstate> need not be a <momentum eigenstate>.

= Top-hat wavefunction
{parent=Wave function}
{title2=$\psi(x)=L^{-1/2}\mathbf1_{|x|<L/2}$}

A <top-hat wavefunction> is constant on an interval and zero elsewhere. It has a uniform <position> <distribution> and a <sinc function> <momentum> <amplitude>. Its discontinuous edges place it outside the finite-kinetic-energy domain of the free <Hamiltonian>. Smooth-edged localized packets can approximate its <position> profile while having different <momentum> tails.

= Momentum variance of a discontinuous top hat
{parent=Top-hat wavefunction}
{title2=$|\widetilde\psi(p)|^2=\frac{L}{2\pi\hbar}\operatorname{sinc}^2(pL/(2\hbar))$}

The <momentum> density of a <top-hat wavefunction> decays as an oscillatory $1/p^2$ envelope. Its <momentum> second moment therefore diverges, although the <probability density> itself is normalizable. The central diffraction-lobe width is not the <standard deviation>. Even the first moment requires care: the symmetric <principal value> is zero, but the absolute first moment diverges logarithmically.

= Position representation in quantum mechanics
{parent=Wave function}
{title2=$\psi(x)=\langle x|\psi\rangle$}

The <wavefunction> is the <probability amplitude> of a <quantum state> in the generalized position <basis>. For a scalar particle on a line, the <position operator> multiplies by $x$ and the <momentum operator> acts as $-i\hbar\,d/dx$ on their appropriate domains. This makes the <canonical commutation relation> $[x,p]=i\hbar$ explicit.

= Node of a wavefunction
{parent=Wave function}
{title2=$\psi(x)=0$}

= Nodes of a wavefunction
{synonym}

A position or set of positions where a <wavefunction> vanishes. Its <Born rule> position <probability density> is also zero there. For a one-dimensional bound state, counting interior nodes distinguishes successive <energy eigenfunctions>; hard-wall endpoint zeros are usually counted separately.

= Kinetic-energy estimate from wavefunction nodes
{parent=Node of a wavefunction}
{title2=$\langle K\rangle\sim\hbar^2n^2/(ma^2)$}

A <wavefunction> with extent $a$ and many nearly equally spaced internal <nodes of a wavefunction> has local <wavevector> $k\sim\pi n/a$. Thus $\langle K\rangle=\hbar^2\int|\psi'|^2/(2m\int|\psi|^2)$ scales as $\hbar^2n^2/(ma^2)$. Exact coefficients require the envelope and <boundary conditions>. An order-one node-count offset is needed for low states: a nodeless localized state still has positive <kinetic energy> by the <Heisenberg uncertainty principle>.

= Wavefunction normalization
{parent=Wave function}
{title2=$\int|\psi|^2\,dx=1$}

Wavefunction normalization makes the total <Born rule> probability equal to one. In an <orthonormal basis> of <energy eigenstates>, $\psi=\sum_n c_n\phi_n$ is normalized precisely when $\sum_n|c_n|^2=1$.

For a proposed <infinite square well> mode $A\sin(n\pi x/L)$, the <integral> of its squared <modulus> is $|A|^2L/2$. Thus <wavefunction normalization> requires $|A|=\sqrt{2/L}$; choosing $A$ real and positive fixes a convenient <global phase>. For the equal <quantum superposition> $(\phi_1+\phi_2)/\sqrt2$, <orthogonality> makes the integrated cross term zero, leaving $1/2+1/2=1$. The cross term can nevertheless be nonzero locally: normalization constrains total probability, not the shape of its <probability density>.

= Matter wave
{parent=Wave function}
{wiki}

A matter wave is the quantum wave associated with a material particle. Its phase can interfere after coherent paths recombine.

In the position representation, a <matter wave> is described by a <wavefunction>: a <complex number> <probability amplitude>, with detection probabilities given by the <Born rule>. A localized <wave packet> combines different <momenta>; its envelope can move while its individual <quantum phase> crests move at another speed. Neither curve is a literal mechanical oscillation of material along a particle's path. The <de Broglie phase harmony> explains the historical route to $\lambda=h/p$; the <relativistic matter-wave velocities> distinguish the two speeds. The <Davisson–Germer experiment> and the <Thomson electron transmission diffraction experiment> connect that spatial <quantum phase> to measured <electron diffraction>.

= Matter-wave coherence
{parent=Matter wave}

= de Broglie phase harmony
{parent=Matter wave}
{c}

In the 1923 note *Waves and Quanta*, <Louis de Broglie> proposed a periodic process associated with <rest energy>, remaining in phase with a wave along the moving particle's path. He distinguished the internal <frequency> slowed by <time dilation> from the wave <frequency> based on total <energy>.<Waves and Quanta by Louis de Broglie>[Nature 112, p. 540, 13 October 1923, opening paragraph]{ref} The proposal developed into his 1924 thesis.<Nobel Lectures, Physics 1922–1941/Louis de Broglie - Biographical>[Nobel Lectures, Physics 1922–1941; Louis de Broglie, Biographical, printed p. 257: 1924 thesis and doctoral degree]{ref} In the 1929 lecture *The wave nature of the electron*, he reconstructed the argument using a <Lorentz transformation>.<Nobel Lectures, Physics 1922–1941/The Wave Nature of the Electron by Louis de Broglie>[Nobel Lectures, Physics 1922–1941; 12 December 1929, printed pp. 247–249: rest-frame periodicity, Lorentz transformation and momentum]{ref}

Here is that <quantum phase> argument in modern notation. Set $\omega_0=mc^2/\hbar$ in the <rest frame>. A <Lorentz transformation> to a frame where the particle has <velocity> $v$ gives
$$
t'=\gamma\left(t-\frac{vx}{c^2}\right),\qquad
\gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}},\qquad
\phi=\omega_0t'=\frac{Et-px}{\hbar},\qquad
E=\gamma mc^2,\quad p=\gamma mv.
$$
Along $x=vt$, <proper time> is $\tau=t/\gamma$, so
$$
\phi(vt,t)=\frac{(E-pv)t}{\hbar}
=\frac{mc^2t}{\gamma\hbar}=\omega_0\tau.
$$
This is the harmony: evaluating the spatial wave at the moving particle gives the slower clock phase. At fixed $x$, its <angular frequency> is instead $E/\hbar=\gamma\omega_0$. Since $k=p/\hbar$, the <de Broglie wavelength> follows as $\lambda=2\pi/k=h/p$. The proposed clock was a theoretical construction, not an observed mechanical vibration.

= Relativistic matter-wave velocities
{parent=Matter wave}
{title2=$v_{\mathrm{ph}}=E/p,\quad v_{\mathrm g}=dE/dp$}

For a one-dimensional <free particle> with $m>0$ and $p>0$, take the positive total <energy> from the <relativistic energy-momentum relation>:
$$
E(p)=\sqrt{m^2c^4+p^2c^2},\qquad
\omega=E/\hbar,\qquad k=p/\hbar.
$$
A fixed <plane wave> <quantum phase> obeys $kx-\omega t=\text{constant}$. Its <phase velocity> is therefore
$$
v_{\mathrm{ph}}=\frac{\omega}{k}=\frac{E}{p}=\frac{c^2}{v}>c.
$$
For a narrow <wave packet> centered at $p_0$, expand its <dispersion relation> as $E(p)\simeq E(p_0)+E'(p_0)(p-p_0)$. In the <Fourier inversion> <integral> building the <wave packet>, the envelope then depends on $x-E'(p_0)t$. Thus its <group velocity> is
$$
v_{\mathrm g}=\frac{d\omega}{dk}=\frac{dE}{dp}=\frac{pc^2}{E}=v<c,\qquad
v_{\mathrm{ph}}v_{\mathrm g}=c^2.
$$
The superluminal <phase velocity> follows a repeating phase marker; it does not describe a localized signal or superluminal transport of <energy>. Higher derivatives of the <dispersion relation> produce <wave packet> spreading, so the translated-envelope argument is a narrow-band approximation. For $v=0.6c$, the <Lorentz factor> is $1.25$, $p=0.75mc$ and $E=1.25mc^2$: the <group velocity> is $0.6c$ while the <phase velocity> is $5c/3$.

The total-<energy> convention matters. Define $\psi=e^{imc^2t/\hbar}\Psi$ to remove the common <rest energy> from a <wavefunction> $\Psi$ with total-<energy> time dependence. Then
$$
K=E-mc^2\simeq\frac{p^2}{2m},\qquad
\omega_{\mathrm{NR}}=K/\hbar,\qquad
\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{NR}}}{k}\simeq\frac{p}{2m},\qquad
\frac{d\omega_{\mathrm{NR}}}{dk}\simeq\frac{p}{m}.
$$
This is exactly the <free-particle momentum convention> used by the <Schrödinger equation> here. Removing a common time-dependent <global phase> changes the reported <phase velocity>, while leaving the <de Broglie wavelength>, relative spatial <quantum phases> and <group velocity> unchanged. Adding $mc^2$ again to a total <energy> would count the <rest energy> twice.

= Thomson electron transmission diffraction experiment
{parent=Matter wave}
{c}

Alongside the 1927 <Davisson–Germer experiment>, <George Paget Thomson> developed an independent transmission test. <Alexander Reid> first used celluloid; Thomson used thin metal films. A narrow <electron> beam crossed a film about $10^{-6}\,\mathrm{cm}$ thick, reaching a <photographic plate> perpendicular to the beam. Rings appeared. Accelerating <voltages> of $20$–$60\,\mathrm{kV}$ set the <de Broglie wavelengths>; metal structures known from <X-ray diffraction> allowed quantitative comparison. A downstream <magnetic field> deflected ring-forming <electrons> like those passing through holes, identifying the carriers as <electrons> rather than <X-rays>. Celluloid's uncertain structure limited its quantitative interpretation.<Nobel Lectures, Physics 1922–1941/Electronic Waves by George Paget Thomson>[Nobel Lectures, Physics 1922–1941; Electronic waves, delivered 7 June 1938, printed pp. 398–400: films, plate, voltage range, metal comparison and magnetic control]{ref}

The <Davisson–Germer experiment> uses crystal reflection and angular <electric current> scans; this transmission camera records a spatial pattern behind the specimen. Both probe <interference> in <electron diffraction>, with different specimen preparations and readouts. A developed <photographic plate> records accumulated detections, not a direct image of the <wavefunction>.

Why rings? In an ideal film containing many small <crystals> with uniformly distributed orientations, a family of <lattice planes> with spacing $d$ can satisfy <Bragg's law> at every azimuth about the incident beam. First-order <elastic scattering> then forms a cone of opening half-angle $2\theta$, where $2d\sin\theta=\lambda$. A flat detector a distance $L$ downstream cuts this cone in a circle:
$$
R=L\tan(2\theta)
=L\tan\!\left[2\arcsin\!\left(\frac{\lambda}{2d}\right)\right]
\simeq\frac{L\lambda}{d}.
$$
The last expression requires small <scattering angles>. It explains a useful control: hold the specimen and camera distance fixed, increase accelerating <voltage>, and the ring radii should shrink with the <electron accelerating-voltage wavelength>. It predicts positions, not intensities: preferred crystal orientations, film thickness and repeated <quantum scattering> can alter a real pattern.

For a chosen example, take $L=0.200\,\mathrm m$ and $d=0.200\,\mathrm{nm}$. The exact relativistic <electron accelerating-voltage wavelength> gives $\lambda=8.589\,\mathrm{pm}$ at $20\,\mathrm{kV}$ and $4.176\,\mathrm{pm}$ at $80\,\mathrm{kV}$. The corresponding exact ring radii are $8.594\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $4.176\,\mathrm{mm}$. These are calculated examples with chosen spacings and camera distance, not Thomson's measurements. The higher <voltage> lies outside the range quoted from his lecture, deliberately illustrating a prediction rather than reconstructing his data.

= Matter-wave interference
{parent=Matter wave}

Matter-wave interference occurs when coherent spatial branches of a particle's <wavefunction> recombine and their relative <quantum phase> changes the detection probabilities.

= Gravitational phase shift in matter-wave interferometry
{parent=Matter-wave interference}
{title2=$\Delta\varphi=mgA/(\hbar v)$}

For equal-speed nonrelativistic matter waves in an interferometer with signed enclosed vertical area $A$, a uniform <gravitational acceleration> gives the displayed leading-order phase difference. In a rectangular device, the horizontal paths differ in <gravitational potential energy> by $mg h$ for a time $\ell/v$, so their phase difference has magnitude $mg h\ell/(\hbar v)$. Assumptions include negligible fractional changes of speed, matched unperturbed optical paths and a consistent treatment of the beam splitters. The <Colella–Overhauser–Werner experiment> uses neutron interference to test this gravitational phase.

= de Broglie wavelength
{title2=$\lambda=h/p$}
{parent=Matter wave}
{c}
{wiki}

The <de Broglie wavelength> is the distance over which a <matter wave> accumulates one full spatial <quantum phase> cycle in its propagation direction:
$$
\lambda=\frac{h}{|\mathbf p|}=\frac{2\pi}{|\mathbf k|},\qquad \mathbf p=\hbar\mathbf k,\qquad \hbar=\frac{h}{2\pi}.
$$
Here $h$ is the <Planck constant>, $\mathbf p$ is the <momentum>, and $\mathbf k$ is the angular <wavevector>. The scalar $p$ in $\lambda=h/p$ means the positive <momentum> magnitude, not a signed component. Reversing a <momentum> reverses the <wavevector> without making the <wavelength> negative. At zero <momentum> there is no finite spatial phase period: the limiting <wavelength> diverges.

This relation makes microscopic <diffraction> accessible with particles: increasing the <momentum> shortens the <wavelength> until it is comparable with the spacings in a <crystal scattering> problem. The <electron accelerating-voltage wavelength> gives a practical way to tune this scale, while the <Davisson–Germer experiment> tests it by comparing <electron diffraction> directions with independently known crystal geometry. The same relation applies to neutral particles in a <neutron interferometer>; <electric charge> is not a requirement for a <matter wave>.

The <wavelength> belongs to the <wavefunction>, not to a sinusoidal path of a particle. A <free-particle momentum convention> makes the distinction especially clear: a <plane wave> has a rotating complex <quantum phase> but a constant <probability density>. A localized <wave packet> contains a range of <momenta> and therefore generally has a range of <wavelengths>, rather than one exact spatial period.

= de Broglie hypothesis
{parent=de Broglie wavelength}

A material particle carries a quantum <phase> whose wave vector and <angular frequency> obey $\mathbf p=\hbar\mathbf k$ and $E=\hbar\omega$. <Interference> and <diffraction> test this relation; a localized particle requires a <wave packet>.

= Free-particle momentum convention
{parent=de Broglie wavelength}
{title2=$\psi=Ae^{i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)},\quad\mathbf p=\hbar\mathbf k$}

For a <free particle> of <mass> $m>0$ in <nonrelativistic quantum mechanics>, choose the <potential energy> zero and write
$$
\psi(\mathbf r,t)=A\exp\!\left[\frac{i}{\hbar}(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf r-Et)\right],\qquad E=\frac{|\mathbf p|^2}{2m},\qquad \omega=\frac{E}{\hbar}.
$$
Applying the <momentum operator> $-i\hbar\nabla$ returns $\mathbf p\psi$; this fixes the sign convention. The <angular frequency> is measured relative to this chosen <energy> zero. Adding a constant <energy> changes only a time-dependent <global phase>, leaving the spatial <de Broglie wavelength> unchanged. No rest-energy term is included in this <nonrelativistic quantum mechanics> convention.

The <plane wave> has $|\psi|^2=|A|^2$ and <probability current> $\mathbf j=(\mathbf p/m)|A|^2$. Its real part can be drawn as a <sine> or <cosine>, but that graph is a component of a <probability amplitude>, not a transverse displacement. On infinite space this is a generalized <momentum eigenstate>, not a state satisfying <wavefunction normalization>. A narrow <wave packet> instead moves with <group velocity> $\nabla_{\mathbf k}\omega=\mathbf p/m$; the <phase velocity> $\omega/|\mathbf k|=|\mathbf p|/(2m)$ is not its transport speed.

The equality of <canonical momentum> and <kinetic momentum> here assumes a field-free region with the <magnetic vector potential> chosen zero. For a charged particle in a <magnetic field>, the <kinetic momentum> is $\boldsymbol\Pi=\mathbf p-q\mathbf A$. An arbitrary canonical phase gradient must not then be substituted into $m\mathbf v$; the <free particle> formula needs its stated assumptions.

= Electron accelerating-voltage wavelength
{parent=de Broglie wavelength}
{title2=$\lambda=h/\sqrt{2m_e eU}$}

Let an <electron> start with negligible <kinetic energy> and move through an increase $U>0$ in <electric potential>. Its <electric charge> is $q=-e$, where $e>0$ is the <elementary charge>, so the decrease in <potential energy> is $eU$. In a field-free region after acceleration,
$$
K=eU,\qquad |\mathbf p|=\sqrt{2m_e eU},\qquad \lambda=\frac{h}{\sqrt{2m_e eU}}\simeq\frac{1.2264\,\mathrm{nm}}{\sqrt{U/(1\,\mathrm V)}}.
$$
This is an <electron> formula, not a universal conversion from <voltage> to <wavelength>. It assumes negligible initial <kinetic energy>, negligible losses, and $eU\ll m_ec^2$, where $c$ is the <speed of light>. It describes the <de Broglie wavelength> outside the crystal; the <potential energy> inside a material can change the local <kinetic momentum>.

For $U=54\,\mathrm V$, take $h=6.62607015\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$, $e=1.602176634\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$, and $m_e\simeq9.1093837\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$. The rounded <electron> <mass> is consistent with the 2022 recommended constants.<CODATA Value: electron mass by National Institute of Standards and Technology>[CODATA electron mass: numerical value and 2022 adjustment label]{ref}
$$
K=8.65175\times10^{-18}\,\mathrm J=54\,\mathrm{eV},\qquad
|\mathbf p|\simeq3.97019\times10^{-24}\,\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}},\qquad
\lambda\simeq1.66895\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm m=0.166895\,\mathrm{nm}.
$$
The <electronvolt> is an <energy> unit: $54\,\mathrm{eV}$ must be converted to <joules> before combining it with $m_e$ in SI units. <Dimensional analysis> checks both steps:
$$
[eU]=\mathrm{C\,V}=\mathrm J,\qquad [\sqrt{2m_e eU}]=\sqrt{\mathrm{kg\,J}}=\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}},\qquad [h/p]=\frac{\mathrm{J\,s}}{\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}}=\mathrm m.
$$
The resulting <velocity> is about $4.3584\times10^6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, or $0.01454c$, so the nonrelativistic assumption is consistent. For a larger accelerating <voltage>, the <relativistic energy-momentum relation> gives $pc=\sqrt{K(K+2m_ec^2)}$, with $K=eU$, while $\lambda=h/p$ still holds. At $54\,\mathrm V$ the relativistic <wavelength> is shorter by about $0.00264\%$.

Explicitly, the exact <special relativity> formula and its <nonrelativistic quantum mechanics> limit are
$$
\lambda_{\mathrm{rel}}(U)
=\frac{hc}{\sqrt{eU(eU+2m_ec^2)}}
=\frac{h}{\sqrt{2m_e eU}}\left(1+\frac{eU}{2m_ec^2}\right)^{-1/2}
\underset{eU\ll m_ec^2}{\simeq}\frac{h}{\sqrt{2m_e eU}}.
$$
Here $K=eU$ is the gained <kinetic energy>; the total <energy> is $E=m_ec^2+K$. At $80\,\mathrm{kV}$, the <nonrelativistic quantum mechanics> expression gives $4.336\,\mathrm{pm}$, about $3.84\%$ larger than the exact $4.176\,\mathrm{pm}$. This difference matters when using <electron diffraction> ring positions to infer <lattice plane> spacings.

= Probability amplitude
{parent=Wave function}
{wiki}
{title2=$a_j=\langle j|\psi\rangle$}

A probability amplitude is a <complex number> that describes the contribution of a <pure state> to a specified measurement outcome. For a normalized <quantum state> $|\psi\rangle$ and a rank-one outcome associated with a normalized vector $|j\rangle$, it is the <inner product> $a_j=\langle j|\psi\rangle$. The <Born rule> gives the corresponding <probability> $p_j=|a_j|^2=a_j^*a_j$, using the <complex conjugate>. Thus an amplitude can be negative or complex, while a <probability> is real and nonnegative. A <wavefunction> is the analogous position amplitude: its squared <modulus> gives a <probability density>, which must be integrated over a region to obtain a <probability>. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-04-quantum-physics-i-spring-2016/d87c768c4e1181fde8d4366112169719_MIT8_04S16_LecNotes6.pdf[Zwiebach, MIT 8.04, lecture 6, section 2, pp. 3–4]{ref}.

The extra information is the <quantum phase>. If $a=re^{i\theta}$, its <modulus> $r$ fixes $|a|^2=r^2$, but its angle $\theta$ controls how it adds to another contribution. In the <complex plane>, picture amplitudes as arrows: add the arrows, then square the resulting length. For coherent contributions $A$ and $B$ to the same outcome,
$$
|A+B|^2=|A|^2+|B|^2+2\operatorname{Re}(A^*B).
$$
The last term is <interference>. Adding <probabilities> first loses it. Contributions with different relative <quantum phases> can reinforce or cancel even when their individual squared <moduli> are fixed.

For the <qubit> <quantum superposition> $|\psi_\phi\rangle=(\sqrt3/2)|0\rangle+(e^{i\phi}/2)|1\rangle$, project onto the plus vector of the <Hadamard basis>. By linearity, the two contributions combine as
$$
\langle+|\psi_\phi\rangle
=\underbrace{\frac{\sqrt3}{2\sqrt2}}_{A}
+\underbrace{\frac{e^{i\phi}}{2\sqrt2}}_{B}.
$$
Here $|A|^2=3/8$ and $|B|^2=1/8$, while the <interference> term is $(\sqrt3/4)\cos\phi$. At $\phi=0$, the arrows align and the plus <probability> is $1/2+\sqrt3/4\simeq0.9330127$. At $\phi=\pi$, they oppose and that <probability> is $1/2-\sqrt3/4\simeq0.0669873$; cancellation is incomplete because the lengths differ. At $\phi=\pi/2$, the arrows are perpendicular and the cross term vanishes. The minus amplitude uses $A-B$, reversing the cross term. These are alternate <projective measurements> on identically prepared copies, rather than a sequence of measurements on one copy.

\Image[quantum-relative-phase-interference.png]
{title=Relative quantum phase redistributes Hadamard-basis probabilities; a diagonal mixture stays at one half}

A <global phase> multiplies every contribution by the same factor $e^{i\gamma}$, giving
$$
|\langle j|e^{i\gamma}\psi\rangle|^2
=|e^{i\gamma}\langle j|\psi\rangle|^2
=|\langle j|\psi\rangle|^2.
$$
It therefore changes no outcome <probability>. Changing a relative <quantum phase> can change the angle between contributions and hence their <interference>. Keeping amplitudes until the last step makes <quantum circuits> and <matter-wave interference> calculable; discarding their angles prematurely discards physically testable information. The <Born rule> example compares two measurement bases explicitly.

= Born rule
{parent=Probability amplitude}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$p_j=|\langle j|\psi\rangle|^2$}

The Born rule connects a <quantum state> to measurement <probabilities>. Let $\{|j\rangle\}$ be a complete <orthonormal basis>, finite or countably infinite, for a <Hilbert space>, and let
$$
|\psi\rangle=\sum_j a_j|j\rangle,
\qquad
a_j=\langle j|\psi\rangle,
\qquad
\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1.
$$
An ideal measurement distinguishing these basis vectors returns label $j$ with <probability>
$$
p_j=|a_j|^2=|\langle j|\psi\rangle|^2.
$$
This assignment is a physical measurement rule. Its compatibility with total <probability> one follows from <orthonormality> and normalization:
$$
\sum_jp_j=\sum_j|a_j|^2
=\sum_{j,k}a_j^*a_k\langle j|k\rangle
=\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1.
$$
The <probabilities> are the squared <moduli>, not the amplitudes themselves or the squares $a_j^2$. https://quantum.cloud.ibm.com/learning/en/courses/basics-of-quantum-information/single-systems/quantum-information[Watrous, IBM Quantum Learning, Quantum information, Measuring quantum states]{ref}.

If the basis is an <eigenbasis> of an <observable>, the reported numerical value is its <eigenvalue>. Several vectors can share one <eigenvalue>; the <probability> of that value is then the sum over the corresponding basis labels. More generally, a <projective measurement> with <orthogonal projections> $P_r$ assigns
$$
p_r=\langle\psi|P_r|\psi\rangle=\|P_r|\psi\rangle\|^2,
\qquad
p_r=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho P_r)
$$
for a <pure state> and a general <density matrix> $\rho$, respectively. This also covers <mixed states> and degenerate <eigenspaces>. The <Born rule> determines outcome statistics; the ideal conditional state change is specified separately by the <Lüders rule>. See <projective measurement> for that formulation and its assumptions. https://quantum.cloud.ibm.com/learning/en/courses/general-formulation-of-quantum-information/general-measurements/formulations-of-measurements[Watrous, IBM Quantum Learning, Mathematical formulations of measurements, Projective measurements]{ref}.

For a worked example, take the normalized <qubit> <quantum state>
$$
|\psi_\phi\rangle=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}|0\rangle+\frac{e^{i\phi}}2|1\rangle.
$$
The squared <moduli> add to $3/4+1/4=1$. A <computational-basis measurement>, equivalently a measurement of the $Z$ <Pauli matrix>, has
$$
p_0=\left|\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right|^2=\frac34,
\qquad
p_1=\left|\frac{e^{i\phi}}2\right|^2=\frac14.
$$
These <probabilities> do not depend on the relative <quantum phase> $\phi$. Now choose the <Hadamard basis>
$$
|\pm\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle}{\sqrt2},
$$
whose vectors are <eigenstates> of the $X$ <Pauli matrix>, also used by the <Pauli X gate>. The new <probability amplitudes> and <probabilities> are
$$
b_\pm=\langle\pm|\psi_\phi\rangle
=\frac{\sqrt3\pm e^{i\phi}}{2\sqrt2},
\qquad
p_\pm=|b_\pm|^2
=\frac12\pm\frac{\sqrt3}{4}\cos\phi.
$$
Both <probabilities> are nonnegative and their sum is one. The relative <quantum phase> becomes visible because the two <probability amplitudes> add before their squared <modulus> is taken:
* At $\phi=0$, $p_+=(2+\sqrt3)/4\simeq0.9330127$ and $p_-\simeq0.0669873$.
* At $\phi=\pi/2$, $p_+=p_-=1/2$.
* At $\phi=\pi$, $p_+\simeq0.0669873$ and $p_-\simeq0.9330127$.

\Image[born-rule-basis-probabilities.png]
{title=The same qubit preparation has phase-independent Z probabilities and phase-sensitive X probabilities}

A <Hadamard gate> followed by a <computational-basis measurement> realizes these $X$ statistics, since $H|\pm\rangle=|0\rangle,|1\rangle$, respectively. Compare the two bases using fresh, identically prepared copies: a preceding measurement may change the state. The <quantum superposition> section shows why replacing the preparation by the <mixed state> $\operatorname{diag}(3/4,1/4)$ keeps the $Z$ statistics but gives $p_+=p_-=1/2$ for every $\phi$.

The usefulness of the <Born rule> is this conversion from <linear algebra> to experimental predictions: calculate overlaps in the basis actually measured, then take squared <moduli>. Its appealing geometry is that changing basis redistributes probability while preserving total squared <norm>. The same rule relates an <energy eigenstate> expansion to an <energy measurement> and a position <wavefunction> to a <probability density>.

Born's 1926 paper on <quantum scattering> interpreted the outgoing-wave coefficients in terms of the <probabilities> of collision outcomes; a corrective footnote specified proportionality to the square of the amplitude. This is a historical scattering formulation, rather than the general <density matrix> formula above. <Zur Quantenmechanik der Stoßvorgänge by Max Born>[Born, Zur Quantenmechanik der Stoßvorgänge, Zeitschrift für Physik 37, 863–867, 1926]{ref}; https://www.neo-classical-physics.info/uploads/3/0/6/5/3065888/born_-_qm_for_collisions_i.pdf[English translation of Born's paper, p. 3 and footnote 1]{ref}.

= Pure-state realization of a basis measurement distribution
{parent=Born rule}
{title2=$|\psi\rangle=\sum_j\sqrt{p_j}e^{i\theta_j}|e_j\rangle$}

For an <orthonormal basis> $\{|e_j\rangle\}$ and nonnegative numbers $p_j$ summing to one, the displayed <vector> is a <unit vector> and gives measurement <probabilities> $p_j$ by the <Born rule>. Conversely, every <pure state> with those <probabilities> has coefficients of these magnitudes, with arbitrary phases on the nonzero coefficients. A common change of all phases is a <global phase>; differences between them are <relative phases> that can affect measurements in another <basis>. Zero-probability coefficients vanish, so their phases have no effect.

= Quantum transition probability
{parent=Born rule}
{title2=$P_{i\to f}(t)=|\langle f|U(t)|i\rangle|^2$}

For a prepared normalized <pure state> $|i\rangle$ and a final <projective measurement> onto the normalized state $|f\rangle$, <unitary time evolution> gives the displayed <probability>, by the <Born rule>. This depends on the preparation, final measurement, and <time-evolution operator>; it does not assume a classical <Markov chain> of transitions.

= Position probability
{parent=Born rule}
{title2=$\Pr(x\in A)=\int_A|\psi|^2dx$}

For a normalized <wavefunction> $\psi(x)$, the probability of a <position measurement> lying in a measurable set $A$ is $\int_A|\psi(x)|^2dx$. It is a spatial-projection probability and differs from an <energy measurement> outcome probability.

= Born statistical scattering interpretation
{parent=Born rule}
{c}

The scattering interpretation connects an incoming preparation to the relative frequency of outgoing directions and final atomic states. <Max Born>'s 1926 paper treats a distant incoming <electron> and a <chemical atom>, expands the outgoing <wavefunction> into final atomic states, and imposes <conservation of energy>. Its translated correction footnote specifies the square of the coefficient; the modern <complex modulus> notation below is ours.<Zur Quantenmechanik der Stoßvorgänge by Max Born>[Zur Quantenmechanik der Stoßvorgänge, 1926, pp. 863–867; inspected in D. H. Delphenich's translation, pp. 2–4, especially p. 3 equation (1) and footnote 1: squared coefficient]{ref}

In his 11 December 1954 lecture, <Max Born> recalled the difficulty of interpreting bound <electrons> alone. He instead described an incident <electron> wave generating an outgoing spherical wave, with direction-dependent squared amplitude determining relative scattering probabilities. Distinct final atomic states describe excitation with corresponding <electron> <energy> loss. This is a retrospective account of an interpretation, rather than the report of a new apparatus.<The statistical interpretation of quantum mechanics by Max Born>[The statistical interpretation of quantum mechanics, lecture delivered 11 December 1954, printed pp. 261–262; inspected through the transcription at https://studyres.com/doc/8624145/the-statistical-interpretation-of-quantum-mechanics, paragraphs from the bound-electron difficulty through inelastic scattering]{ref}

The distinction from the modern <Born rule> matters. The historical question was which collision outcome occurs: a direction of motion and a final atomic state. The general <projective measurement> formula applies to arbitrary <observables>, including degenerate outcomes and <mixed states>; a <positive operator-valued measure> describes still more general detector statistics. Those formulations should not be read back as the explicit contents of the short 1926 scattering paper. Nor does the scattering interpretation itself supply a rule for the conditional <post-measurement state>.

Actual experimental settings are described in the <Davisson–Germer experiment> and the <Franck-Hertz experiment>. The former scans collected <electric current> as an <electron> beam scatters from prepared <nickel>; changing direction, accelerating <voltage> and surface preparation probes <electron diffraction>. The latter uses acceleration through mercury vapor and a retarding collector to infer discrete <energy> losses from current minima, with vapor density and retarding settings as controls. Angular resolution and surface backgrounds limit the first inference; initial <energy> spread and collision positions limit the second. These linked experiments supply the preparation, apparatus and readout; the examples below are original calculations, not Born's measured data.

= Angular scattering probability from complex amplitudes
{parent=Born statistical scattering interpretation}
{title2=$d\sigma_i/d\Omega=(v_i/v_a)|f_{ia}|^2$}

Take <nonrelativistic quantum mechanics>, a short-range interaction, a fixed heavy target, and a common projectile <mass> $m$. An incoming <plane wave> in atomic state $a$ has unit coefficient. In an open <scattering channel> $i$, the target finishes in a normalized atomic state $\chi_i$, and the projectile has positive <velocity> magnitude $v_i=\hbar k_i/m$. At large distance,
$$
\Psi\sim e^{ik_a z}\chi_a+\sum_{i\,\mathrm{open}}f_{ia}(\Omega)\frac{e^{ik_i r}}r\chi_i,\qquad
\frac{\hbar^2k_i^2}{2m}+\varepsilon_i=\frac{\hbar^2k_a^2}{2m}+\varepsilon_a.
$$
Here $\Omega$ denotes direction, $d\Omega$ is a <solid angle> element, and $\varepsilon_i$ is an internal atomic <energy>. Different $\chi_i$ are <orthogonal>. The <scattering amplitude> $f_{ia}$ has units of length. A closed <scattering channel> has no propagating outgoing <probability current> at this total <energy>.

The incoming <probability current> density is $j_{\mathrm{in}}=v_a$. The outgoing radial <probability current> density in channel $i$ is $j_{r,i}\sim v_i|f_{ia}|^2/r^2$. Multiplying by the area $r^2d\Omega$ and dividing by $j_{\mathrm{in}}$ gives the <differential scattering cross-section>:
$$
\frac{d\sigma_i}{d\Omega}=\frac{v_i}{v_a}|f_{ia}(\Omega)|^2.
$$
The $r^{-2}$ dilution cancels the detector's area growth. The <velocity> ratio remains: a squared wave coefficient alone is not generally a flux fraction. A <differential scattering cross-section> has units of area per <solid angle>, rather than being a normalized directional <probability density>. For selected scattering events in acceptance $D$, an ideal detector with equal efficiency in all retained channels instead gives
$$
\Pr(i,\Omega\in D\mid\text{selected scattering})
=\frac{\displaystyle\int_D(v_i/v_a)|f_{ia}|^2\,d\Omega}
{\displaystyle\sum_j\int_{\mathrm{selection}}(v_j/v_a)|f_{ja}|^2\,d\Omega}.
$$
The denominator specifies the event selection; it need not equal one square unit of area. Absolute rates require incident flux, target number and detector efficiency in addition to these relative weights. The next finite-mode model normalizes outgoing <probability current> to one incoming event and predicts event fractions directly.

= Two-channel scattering with flux normalization
{parent=Born statistical scattering interpretation}
{title2=$S^\dagger S=I,\quad P_i=(v_i/v_1)|c_i|^2$}

Consider one chosen model at a fixed total <energy>, with two open <scattering channels>, no absorption and no omitted outgoing modes. Channel 1 leaves the target unchanged; channel 2 excites it. Let $v_1=v>0$ and $v_2=v/2$. For the same projectile <mass>, <conservation of energy> then requires a target excitation $\Delta=3mv^2/8$. These are hypothetical parameters, not a fit to a particular atom. Each incoming or outgoing mode is normalized to unit <probability current>; the outgoing modes will have the angular profiles specified below.

Choose the <S-matrix>
$$
S=\frac15\begin{pmatrix}3&4i\\4i&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
S^\dagger S=\frac1{25}\begin{pmatrix}25&12i-12i\\-12i+12i&25\end{pmatrix}=I.
$$
This phenomenological <unitary matrix> specifies the channel conversion; it is not derived here from an interaction <potential energy>. For incidence in channel 1, its first column gives the outgoing flux amplitudes
$$
b=S\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}3/5\\4i/5\end{pmatrix},\qquad
P_1=\frac9{25},\quad P_2=\frac{16}{25}.
$$
To recover ordinary wave coefficients relative to an incident coefficient one, undo the <velocity> normalization:
$$
c_i=\sqrt{\frac{v_1}{v_i}}b_i,\qquad
c_1=\frac35,\quad c_2=\frac{4i\sqrt2}{5},\qquad
\sum_i\frac{v_i}{v_1}|c_i|^2=\frac9{25}+\frac12\frac{32}{25}=1.
$$
Thus $|c_2|^2=32/25>1$ is allowed: channel 2 travels more slowly. Its outgoing <probability current> fraction is $16/25$, not $32/25$. Renormalizing the unweighted squares would give $9/41$ and $32/41$, the wrong <probabilities>. More generally the ordinary coefficient map is $A=V^{-1/2}SV^{1/2}$, with $V=\operatorname{diag}(v,v/2)$; it obeys $A^\dagger VA=V$. The conserved quantity is the squared coefficient <norm> weighted by <velocity>, which represents <probability current>.

Now specify an original angular realization of these two outgoing modes, with <scattering angle> $\theta$ and no azimuth dependence:
$$
g_1(\theta)=\sqrt{\frac3{16\pi}}(1+i\cos\theta),\qquad
g_2(\theta)=\frac1{\sqrt{4\pi}},\qquad
\int|g_1|^2d\Omega=\int|g_2|^2d\Omega=1.
$$
The first normalization follows from $2\pi\int_{-1}^1(1+u^2)\,du=16\pi/3$, where $u=\cos\theta$. These angular profiles define the modes of this model; they are not asserted to follow from Born's approximation or an atomic <potential energy>. The joint directional <probability densities> are
$$
\frac{dP_1}{d\Omega}=\frac9{25}\frac3{16\pi}(1+\cos^2\theta),\qquad
\frac{dP_2}{d\Omega}=\frac{16}{25}\frac1{4\pi}.
$$

Suppose a detector accepts the cone $D:0\leq\theta\leq\pi/3$, with full azimuth and unit efficiency. Its angular fractions within the channels are
$$
F_1=\frac38\int_{1/2}^1(1+u^2)\,du=\frac{19}{64},\qquad
F_2=\frac12\int_{1/2}^1du=\frac14.
$$
Multiplying the angular fractions by the outgoing flux weights gives
$$
\Pr(1,D)=\frac9{25}\frac{19}{64}=\frac{171}{1600},\qquad
\Pr(2,D)=\frac{16}{25}\frac14=\frac{256}{1600}.
$$
For $1600$ independent incident events, the <expected values> are therefore $171$ and $256$ accepted detections, compared with $576$ and $1024$ over all directions. The remaining expected $1173$ events fall outside the cone. The chosen incident number makes the expectations integers; actual counts fluctuate. Conditional on a detection inside the cone, the channel <probabilities> are $171/427$ and $256/427$, rather than $9/25$ and $16/25$. Angular acceptance changes the observed channel proportions even with equal detector efficiencies.

The final atomic states are <orthogonal>, so ignoring the atomic readout adds the two channel intensities; it does not produce $|b_1g_1+b_2g_2|^2$. Coherent paths that reach the same final state can instead interfere, as the existing <probability amplitude> and <quantum superposition> examples demonstrate. The distinction is physical distinguishability of final states, not whether the detector operator chooses to display their labels. Flux normalization and angular integration make the scattering interpretation useful: they turn complex outgoing waves into conserved event weights and then into the statistics of a specified detector.

= Two-level quantum return probability
{parent=Born rule}

A normalized <quantum state> $\chi=\sqrt q\,\psi_1+e^{i\eta}\sqrt{1-q}\,\psi_2$ in two orthonormal <energy eigenstates> returns to its original one-dimensional measurement subspace with probability
$$
|\langle\chi,e^{-iHt/\hbar}\chi\rangle|^2=q^2+(1-q)^2+2q(1-q)\cos((E_1-E_2)t/\hbar).
$$
This follows by applying <unitary time evolution> to each <energy eigenstate>, then the <Born rule>. The relative phase $\eta$ cancels from the return amplitude. Degenerate energies make the probability identically one.

= Two-level observable transition probability
{parent=Born rule}
{title2=$P_{-\to+}(t)=\sin^2((E_2-E_1)t/(2\hbar))$}

For two orthonormal <energy eigenstates> $\psi_1,\psi_2$, let an <observable> have distinct nondegenerate <eigenstates> $\phi_\pm=(\psi_1\pm\psi_2)/\sqrt2$. Starting in $\phi_-$, <unitary time evolution> gives $(e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}\psi_1-e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}\psi_2)/\sqrt2$. Its <probability amplitude> along $\phi_+$ is $(e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}-e^{-iE_2t/\hbar})/2$. The <Born rule> therefore gives the stated transition probability. The energy difference determines the oscillation frequency, while a common energy shift has no effect.

= Wigner's theorem
{c}
{parent=Born rule}
{wiki}

= Wigner theorem
{synonym}
{c}

Every bijection of pure-state rays that preserves transition probabilities is induced by either a unitary or an antiunitary operator on the Hilbert space.

= Projective unitary representation
{parent=Wigner's theorem}

Because state vectors differing by a <quantum phase> represent the same ray, a unitary realization of a group action need only satisfy
$$
U(g_1)U(g_2)=e^{i\phi(g_1,g_2)}U(g_1g_2).
$$

A <projective unitary representation> is a <projective representation> whose chosen representatives are unitary operators. The phase factors disappear on rays.

= Energy measurement
{parent=Born rule}

An ideal energy measurement returns an eigenvalue of the Hamiltonian with probability equal to the squared norm of the state's projection onto its eigenspace. After obtaining a nondegenerate energy, the state is the corresponding energy eigenstate.

= Normalizable wavefunction
{parent=Wave function}

= Normalizable
{synonym}

A wavefunction is normalizable when the integral of its squared modulus is finite, so multiplication by a constant can make its total probability one.

= Probability density
{parent=Wave function}

A probability density is a nonnegative <function> whose <integral> over an event gives its probability.

= Probability flux
{parent=Probability density}
{title2=$\partial_t p+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0$}

A current in a <probability continuity equation>. For overdamped drift-diffusion, $\mathbf J=\mathbf v p-D\nabla p$. This transport definition does not require the quantum-mechanical <wavefunction> current formula.

= Radial probability density
{parent=Probability density}
{title2=$P(r)=r^2\int|\psi(r,\Omega)|^2\,d\Omega$}

The <radial probability density> $P(r)$ is defined so that $P(r)\,dr$ is the probability of finding a particle between radii $r$ and $r+dr$. The spherical volume element gives $P(r)=r^2\int|\psi(r,\Omega)|^2d\Omega$. For a spherically symmetric <wavefunction>, $P(r)=4\pi r^2|\psi(r)|^2$; for $\psi=R(r)Y_l^m(\Omega)$ with normalized <spherical harmonics>, $P(r)=r^2|R(r)|^2$. Its maximum need not coincide with the maximum of the density per unit volume: a hydrogenic $1s$ state has its largest spatial density at the origin but its most probable radius at $a_0/Z$.

= Probability current
{parent=Wave function}
{wiki}

For a nonrelativistic particle of mass $m$, the probability current is
$$
J=-\frac{i\hbar}{2m}
\left(\psi^*\nabla\psi-\psi\nabla\psi^*\right).
$$

= Current of a translated Gaussian coherent state
{parent=Probability current}
{title2=$\psi=\left(\frac\alpha\pi\right)^{1/4}e^{-\alpha(x-q)^2/2+i p x/\hbar+i\gamma},\quad j=\frac pm|\psi|^2$}

Here $\alpha>0$ and $q,p,\gamma$ are real functions of time. Differentiation in position gives $\psi_x=[-\alpha(x-q)+ip/\hbar]\psi$. The standard <probability current> is therefore
$$
j=\frac\hbar m\operatorname{Im}(\overline\psi\psi_x)=\frac pm\sqrt{\frac\alpha\pi}\,e^{-\alpha(x-q)^2}.
$$
For a <coherent state> moving in a quadratic <Hamiltonian>, the centre follows the classical equation $\dot q=p/m$, so the current is the normalized <normal distribution>[Gaussian probability density] transported at its centre velocity. The factor $\sqrt{\alpha/\pi}$ is essential: omitting it changes the current by a dimensional constant and violates the <continuity equation> for the normalized density. A spatially constant phase does not affect the current.

= Spatial phase gradient of a Schrodinger wavefunction
{parent=Probability current}
{title2=$\mathbf j=\rho\hbar\nabla\theta/m$}

Where a nonzero <wavefunction> can be written as $\psi=\sqrt\rho e^{i\theta}$, and there is no <vector potential>, substituting into the Schrodinger <probability current> gives $\mathbf j=\rho\hbar\nabla\theta/m$. The direction of <probability> flow is determined by the <phase> gradient rather than the slope of the <real part>. A <plane wave> and its <complex conjugate> have the same <real part> and density but opposite currents. In a <vector potential> the gauge-invariant kinetic <momentum> replaces $\hbar\nabla\theta$.

= Probability current of a two-dimensional Dirac particle
{parent=Probability current}
{c}
{title2=$\mathbf j=c\Psi^\dagger(\sigma_x,\sigma_y)\Psi$}

For a Hermitian local mass coefficient and $H=c\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p+V\sigma_z$, the density and current obey $\partial_t(\Psi^\dagger\Psi)+\nabla\cdot(c\Psi^\dagger\boldsymbol\sigma\Psi)=0$. A normalized $\sigma_x=-1$ interface <spinor> carries $j_x=-c\rho$ and $j_y=0$.

= Probability continuity equation
{parent=Probability current}

For a <wavefunction> satisfying the <Schrodinger equation> with a real potential,
$$
\frac{\partial|\psi|^2}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot J=0.
$$
This <continuity equation> expresses local conservation of probability.

= Conservation of quantum probability
{parent=Probability continuity equation}

If the <probability current> of a <normalizable wavefunction> vanishes at spatial infinity, integrating the <probability continuity equation> over all space shows that the total probability $\int |\psi|^2$ is constant in time.

= Gaussian wave packet
{parent=Wave function}
{c}
{wiki}

A Gaussian wave packet is a <wavefunction> whose spatial amplitude is a <Gaussian function>. Free quantum evolution preserves its Gaussian form while changing its complex width and spreading its <probability density>.

= Gaussian covariance in a harmonic potential
{parent=Gaussian wave packet}
{c}

Under a <quantum harmonic oscillator> Hamiltonian, the <Heisenberg equations of motion> give
$$
x(t)=x(0)\cos\omega t+\frac{p(0)}{m\omega}\sin\omega t,\qquad p(t)=p(0)\cos\omega t-m\omega x(0)\sin\omega t.
$$
Subtract the <expectation values>, square the first equation, and use the symmetrized <covariance> $C_0=\langle\{x-\langle x\rangle,p-\langle p\rangle\}\rangle/2$. This yields
$$
\sigma_x(t)^2=\sigma_x(0)^2\cos^2\omega t+\frac{\sigma_p(0)^2}{m^2\omega^2}\sin^2\omega t+\frac{2C_0}{m\omega}\sin\omega t\cos\omega t.
$$
These moment equations hold for any state with finite second moments; a quadratic Hamiltonian additionally preserves <Gaussian wave packets>. A <coherent state> has $\sigma_x(0)^2=\hbar/(2m\omega)$, $\sigma_p(0)^2=m\hbar\omega/2$, and $C_0=0$, so its width stays fixed while its center follows the classical orbit. A <squeezed coherent state> generally has a width that oscillates at $2\omega$.

= Free Gaussian wave packet evolution
{parent=Gaussian wave packet}
{title2=$\sigma_x(t)^2=\sigma_0^2+\hbar^2t^2/(4m^2\sigma_0^2)$}

Let the initial <Gaussian wave packet> be
$$
\psi(x,0)=(2\pi\sigma_0^2)^{-1/4}\exp\left[-\frac{(x-x_0)^2}{4\sigma_0^2}+\frac{ip_0(x-x_0)}\hbar\right].
$$
Fourier transforming, multiplying each momentum amplitude by $e^{-ip^2t/(2m\hbar)}$ and evaluating the <Gaussian integral> gives
$$
\psi(x,t)=\frac{(2\pi\sigma_0^2)^{-1/4}}{\sqrt{1+i\eta}}\exp\left[-\frac{(x-x_0-p_0t/m)^2}{4\sigma_0^2(1+i\eta)}+\frac{ip_0(x-x_0)}\hbar-\frac{ip_0^2t}{2m\hbar}\right],\qquad \eta=\frac{\hbar t}{2m\sigma_0^2}.
$$
Its <probability density> is a normalized Gaussian with center $x_0+p_0t/m$ and width $\sigma_x=\sigma_0\sqrt{1+\eta^2}$; the momentum width remains $\sigma_p=\hbar/(2\sigma_0)$. Its symmetrized position-momentum <covariance> is $C_{xp}=t\sigma_p^2/m$, so
$$
\sigma_x\sigma_p=\frac\hbar2\sqrt{1+\eta^2},\qquad \sigma_x^2\sigma_p^2-C_{xp}^2=\frac{\hbar^2}4.
$$
Thus free spreading preserves equality in the <Robertson-Schrödinger uncertainty relation> while producing a nonzero quadratic position-dependent phase and a product $\sigma_x\sigma_p$ larger than $\hbar/2$. More generally, $\sigma_x(t)^2=\sigma_x(0)^2+2tC_{xp}(0)/m+t^2\sigma_p(0)^2/m^2$, so an initially correlated Gaussian can first contract.

= Gaussian evolution in an inverted harmonic oscillator
{parent=Gaussian wave packet}
{c}
{title2=$B(t)=\frac{\tan(\phi-it)}{2\hbar}$}

For the <Hamiltonian (quantum mechanics)> $H=(p^2-x^2)/2$, a <Gaussian wave packet> $Ae^{-Bx^2}$ satisfies $\dot A=-i\hbar AB$ and $\dot B=-i/(2\hbar)-2i\hbar B^2$. The displayed solution is normalizable for real $\phi$ exactly when $\sin(2\phi)>0$; its <second moments of a complex Gaussian wave packet> are
$$
\langle x^2\rangle=\frac{\hbar[\cosh(2t)+\cos(2\phi)]}{2\sin(2\phi)},\qquad
\langle p^2\rangle=\frac{\hbar[\cosh(2t)-\cos(2\phi)]}{2\sin(2\phi)}.
$$
Both grow as $\hbar e^{2t}/[4\sin(2\phi)]$, while their difference and hence the mean <energy> remain constant.
