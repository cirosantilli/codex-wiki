= Solution
{parent=a}

Use the <commutator> convention $[x,y]=x^{-1}y^{-1}xy$, and let $[A,B]$ denote the <subgroup> generated by all such commutators with $x\in A,y\in B$. The <lower central series> is
$$
\gamma_1(G)=G,\qquad\gamma_{i+1}(G)=[\gamma_i(G),G].
$$
A <central series> has $[G_j,G]\le G_{j-1}$ for each $j\ge1$. We prove the requested inclusion by induction. At $i=0$, $\gamma_1(G)=G_n$. If $i<n$ and $\gamma_{i+1}(G)\le G_{n-i}$, then
$$
\gamma_{i+2}(G)=[\gamma_{i+1}(G),G]
\le[G_{n-i},G]\le G_{n-i-1}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\gamma_{i+1}(G)\le G_{n-i}\quad(0\le i\le n).}
$$
In particular $\gamma_{n+1}(G)=1$. The proof uses the central-series condition directly; no equality of the intermediate terms is claimed.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Suppose inductively that $G=H\gamma_i(G)$. In the <quotient> $G/\gamma_{i+1}(G)$, the image of $\gamma_i(G)$ is central. Hence this <quotient> is generated by the image of $H$ together with a central <subgroup>, so its <commutator subgroup> lies in the image of $H$. Pulling this back gives
$$
\gamma_2(G)\le H\gamma_{i+1}(G).
$$
The original assumption now gives $G=H\gamma_2(G)\le H\gamma_{i+1}(G)\le G$. Starting with $i=2$, induction proves $G=H\gamma_i(G)$ for every $i\ge2$. These products are subgroups because each $\gamma_i(G)$ is normal; no <normality> of $H$ is needed.

If $G$ is finitely generated, every <quotient>, including its <abelianization> $G/\gamma_2(G)$, is finitely generated. Conversely, lift a finite <generating set> of that <quotient> to $g_1,\ldots,g_t\in G$, and set $H=\langle g_1,\ldots,g_t\rangle$. Then $G=H\gamma_2(G)$. For a <nilpotent group>, $\gamma_{c+1}(G)=1$ for some $c$, and the established identity gives $G=H$. Thus
$$
\boxed{G\text{ finitely generated}\iff G/\gamma_2(G)\text{ finitely generated}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=c}

All commutators of $H$ with $K$ lie in the central <subgroup> $Y$ of $HK$. In the standard <commutator> identities, <conjugation> by elements of $H$ or $K$ therefore acts trivially on these commutators. Since $Y$ is abelian, their order can also be reversed. Consequently
$$
[h_1h_2,k]=[h_1,k][h_2,k],\qquad
[h,k_1k_2]=[h,k_1][h,k_2].
$$
Thus the map is a <homomorphism> in each variable separately; this does not assert that it is a <homomorphism> from the direct product jointly.

It factors through $H/C_H(K)$ in the first variable and $K/C_K(H)$ in the second. If these finite <quotient> groups have representatives $h_1,\ldots,h_a$ and $k_1,\ldots,k_b$, then the finite collection $[h_i,k_j]$ generates $Y$. Let $e$ be an exponent of $H/C_H(K)$. For every $h,k$,
$$
[h,k]^e=[h^e,k]=1.
$$
Hence $Y$ is a finitely generated <abelian group> of bounded exponent, and **$Y$ is finite**. For instance its order is at most $e^{ab}$.

Now suppose $G$ is nilpotent and $G/\gamma_2(G)$ is finite. Induct on the <nilpotency class> $c$. For $c=1$, $G$ is its own <abelianization> and is finite. If $c\ge2$, the <group> $\bar G=G/\gamma_c(G)$ has smaller class and
$$
\bar G/\gamma_2(\bar G)\cong G/\gamma_2(G),
$$
since $\gamma_c(G)\le\gamma_2(G)$. By induction $\bar G$ is finite. Apply the preceding result with $H=G=\gamma_1(G)$ and $K=\gamma_{c-1}(G)$. Their <commutator subgroup> is $Y=\gamma_c(G)\le Z(G)$. Both relevant <centralizer> quotients are finite, because their centralizers contain $\gamma_c(G)$ and $G/\gamma_c(G)$ is finite. Thus $\gamma_c(G)$ is finite. The extension of this finite <normal subgroup> by the finite <quotient> $\bar G$ is finite. The converse follows because a <quotient> of a <finite group> is finite. Therefore
$$
\boxed{G\text{ finite}\iff G/\gamma_2(G)\text{ finite}\qquad(G\text{ nilpotent}).}
$$

= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $|g|_S$ be the least length of a word in $S\cup S^{-1}$ representing $g$, and write $B_S(r)=\{g:|g|_S\le r\}$. <Polynomial growth of a group> of degree at most $d$ means that, for some constant $C$, 
$$
|B_S(r)|\le C(1+r)^d\qquad(r\ge0).
$$
In particular these balls are finite. For the usual convention $S$ is finite; finiteness of $B_S(1)$ would force that in any case.

For a finite subset $T$, set $L=\max(1,\max_{t\in T}|t|_S)$. Replacing each letter of a $T$-word by an $S$-word gives
$$
B_T(r)\subseteq B_S(Lr).
$$
Thus $|B_T(r)|\le C(1+Lr)^d\le CL^d(1+r)^d$. This proves the same degree bound for $\langle T\rangle$. If $T$ is empty the <subgroup> is trivial and the assertion is immediate.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Choose finite symmetric generating sets $S_i$ for all $G_i$, and let $L\ge1$ bound the $S$-length of their elements. If $[G_{i-1}:G_i]=\infty$, the connected locally finite <Schreier graph> of left cosets of $G_i$ in $G_{i-1}$ is infinite. Its radius-$r$ <ball> has at least $r+1$ vertices: otherwise a finite-diameter locally finite connected graph would be finite. Equivalently, we can choose $r+1$ distinct left-<coset> representatives $t_{i,0},\ldots,t_{i,r}$ of $G_i$, each of $S_{i-1}$-length at most $r$, and hence $S$-length at most $Lr$.

The $(r+1)^n$ products
$$
t_{1,j_1}t_{2,j_2}\cdots t_{n,j_n}\qquad(0\le j_i\le r)
$$
are distinct. Indeed, equality first forces equality of the $G_1$-cosets of the first factors, and hence their indices; cancellation then forces equality of the $G_2$-cosets of the second factors, and so on. Each product has $S$-length at most $nLr$. Therefore
$$
(r+1)^n\le|B_S(nLr)|\le C(1+nLr)^d.
$$
Letting $r\to\infty$ yields
$$
\boxed{n\le d.}
$$
For $n=0$ the assertion is automatic.

= Solution
{parent=c}

First establish a stronger form of the <coset>-counting argument. If $K$ is a finitely generated infinite-index <subgroup> of a <finitely generated group> $L$ of growth at most $q$, choose a finite <generating set> $T$ for $K$, and let $M$ bound its lengths in a <generating set> $S$ of $L$. The <Schreier graph> supplies $r+1$ distinct left-<coset> representatives of $K$ of $S$-length at most $r$. The corresponding translates of $B_T(r)$ are disjoint and lie in $B_S((M+1)r)$. Hence
$$
(r+1)|B_T(r)|\le C(1+(M+1)r)^q.
$$
Thus **an infinite-index finitely generated <subgroup> has growth at most $q-1$** whenever the ambient upper bound is $q$.

Every finitely generated $K\le H$ has infinite index in $G$. Applying this result with $L=G$ gives growth at most $n-1$. Minimality of $n$ among the supposed counterexamples therefore makes each such $K$ virtually nilpotent. Here $n\ge1$, since a <group> with a uniform bound on its finite word balls is finite, and finite groups are virtually nilpotent.

For each finitely generated $K\le H$, let $d(K)$ be the least nonnegative integer giving a polynomial growth upper bound. It exists and belongs to $\{0,\ldots,n-1\}$. Choose $K$ for which this integer is maximal. If $K\le L\le H$ and $L$ is finitely generated, then $d(L)\le d(K)$. Were $[L:K]$ infinite, the strengthened bound would make $K$ have growth at most $d(L)-1\le d(K)-1$, contradicting the definition of $d(K)$. If $d(K)=0$, the same inequality would force the nonempty balls of $K$ to have cardinality tending to zero, also impossible. Hence every such overgroup has $[L:K]<\infty$.

Since $K$ is virtually nilpotent, choose a nilpotent finite-index <subgroup> $N\le K$. It is finitely generated by <Schreier's lemma>. For any $h\in H$, the <subgroup> $L=\langle K,h\rangle$ is finitely generated and $[L:K]<\infty$. Pigeonholing the cosets $h^jK$ gives a positive $a$ with $h^a\in K$. Pigeonholing the cosets of $N$ in $K$ then gives a positive $b$ with $(h^a)^b\in N$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\text{For every }h\in H\text{ there is }r=ab>0\text{ with }h^r\in N.}
$$
No <normality> of $K$ in $L$ or of $N$ in $K$ is required. Using least integer upper bounds also avoids needing any further structural theorem about the exact growth degree of a <virtually nilpotent group>.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $B=H_1\times\cdots\times H_m$, with each $H_i$ a copy of $H$. The <permutation> <group> $F\le S_m$ acts on $B$ by
$$
\alpha_\sigma(h_1,\ldots,h_m)_j=h_{\sigma^{-1}(j)}.
$$
Define the <permutation wreath product> to be the <semidirect product>
$$
\boxed{H\operatorname{wr}F=H^m\rtimes_\alpha F.}
$$
Explicitly,
$$
(b,\sigma)(c,\tau)=\bigl(b\,\alpha_\sigma(c),\sigma\tau\bigr).
$$
Writing $h_i$ for the element with entry $h$ in coordinate $i$ and identity elsewhere, and writing $\sigma$ for $(1,\sigma)$, <conjugation> satisfies
$$
\sigma h_i\sigma^{-1}=h_{\sigma(i)}.
$$
Thus the top <permutation> <group> permutes the coordinate subgroups in exactly its given action. Distinct coordinate subgroups commute, including when $H$ itself is nonabelian.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Put $A=A_m$ and let $T$ be the <rooted tree> of finite words over $\{1,\ldots,m\}$. View $a\in A$ as a rooted <automorphism>: it permutes the first letter by $a$ and fixes the suffix. Denote this rooted copy by $A$ again.

For $a\in A$, define a directed tree <automorphism> $b(a)$ by putting local <permutation> $a$ at every vertex
$$
2,\quad12,\quad112,\quad1112,\quad\ldots
$$
and identity local <permutation> at every other vertex. These marked vertices are pairwise incomparable, so the prescribed actions are on disjoint rooted subtrees and define a unique <automorphism>. Equivalently its root <permutation> is trivial and its sections are
$$
b(a)=\bigl(b(a),a,1,\ldots,1\bigr).
$$
The map $a\mapsto b(a)$ is an <injective> <homomorphism>: it is the same copy of $a$ acting on each of those disjoint subtrees. Let $B=b(A)$ and define
$$
H=\langle A,B\rangle\le\operatorname{Aut}(T).
$$
Both $A$ and $B$ are finite, so $H$ is finitely generated. For $m\ge5$, $A_m$ is a nonabelian simple <group> and hence perfect. Thus $A,B\le H'$, and because they generate $H$, **$H$ is perfect**.

Every section of either defining generator belongs to $H$, so sections of every element of $H$ belong to $H$. Its root <permutation> belongs to $A$. For a tree <automorphism> $g$, write $g(iw)=\sigma(i)g_i(w)$, where $g_i$ is its section at input letter $i$. In the semidirect-product convention of part (a), define
$$
\theta(g)=\bigl(g_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},\ldots,g_{\sigma^{-1}(m)};\sigma\bigr).
$$
Composition of tree automorphisms gives exactly the wreath-product multiplication: this is why $\theta$ is a <homomorphism>. It is <injective> because an <automorphism> is determined by its first-level <permutation> and all its first-level sections. On the generators,
$$
\theta(a)=(1,\ldots,1;a),\qquad
\theta(b(a))=(b(a),a,1,\ldots,1;1).
$$
It remains to prove surjectivity; merely writing these recursions would not establish it. Choose $\sigma=(2\,3\,4)\in A$, which fixes $1$ and sends $2$ to $3$. For $a,c\in A$, the two base tuples $\theta(b(a))$ and $\theta(\sigma b(c)\sigma^{-1})$ overlap only in their first coordinate. Their <commutator> is therefore
$$
\bigl([b(a),b(c)],1,\ldots,1;1\bigr)
=\bigl(b([a,c]),1,\ldots,1;1\bigr).
$$
Since $A$ is perfect, products of these commutators give every element of the copy of $B$ localized in coordinate $1$. Canceling this first coordinate from $\theta(b(a))$ then gives $(1,a,1,\ldots,1;1)$, the rooted copy of $A$ localized in coordinate $2$. Conjugating by the transitive rooted <group> $A_m$ supplies both localized $A$ and localized $B$ in every coordinate. Since $H=\langle A,B\rangle$, the image contains the full base $H^m$. It also contains the top $A_m$. Therefore it is the entire <wreath product> and
$$
\boxed{\theta:H\overset\sim\longrightarrow H\operatorname{wr}A_m.}
$$
Finally $H$ is infinite: it contains the nontrivial rooted $A_m$, and a finite order $h=|H|$ would satisfy $h=h^m|A_m|$, which is impossible for $m\ge5$.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Let $L$ be the <subgroup> of $H\operatorname{wr}A_m$ generated by all its elements of order $3$. The top <group> $A_m$ is generated by 3-cycles, also when $m=3$, so $A_m\le L$.

Take $\sigma=(1\,2\,3)$. For any $h\in H$, the conjugate $h_1\sigma h_1^{-1}$ has order $3$, as does $\sigma^{-1}$. Thus their product belongs to $L$ and equals
$$
d_{12}(h)=h_1\sigma h_1^{-1}\sigma^{-1}=h_1h_2^{-1}.
$$
Using $\sigma^{-1}$ instead supplies $d_{13}(h)=h_1h_3^{-1}$. Since distinct coordinate groups commute,
$$
[d_{12}(h),d_{13}(k)]=[h,k]_1\in L.
$$
The hypothesis that $H$ is perfect says that products of its commutators generate $H$, so $H_1\le L$. Transitivity of $A_m$ now gives $H_i\le L$ for every coordinate. Hence $H^m\le L$ and $A_m\le L$, proving
$$
\boxed{H\operatorname{wr}A_m\text{ is generated by elements of order }3.}
$$
The proof does not require $H$ itself to be finitely generated, nor does it require $A_m$ to be perfect for $m=3,4$.
