= Solution
{parent=a}

The <absolute continuity of measures> condition is
$$
\nu\ll\kappa\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \kappa(E)=0\Longrightarrow\nu(E)=0\qquad(E\in\mathfrak M).
$$
The <Radon-Nikodym theorem> says that if both <positive measures> are <sigma-finite measures>, then this condition is equivalent to the existence of a nonnegative <measurable function> $h$, unique up to equality $\kappa$-<almost everywhere>, such that
$$
\boxed{\nu(E)=\int_Eh\,d\kappa\quad(E\in\mathfrak M).}
$$
One writes $h=d\nu/d\kappa$, the <Radon-Nikodym derivative>. Under these hypotheses $h$ is finite $\kappa$-<almost everywhere>. If $\nu$ is finite, then $h\in L^1(\kappa)$. A finite <signed measure> absolutely continuous with respect to a sigma-finite <positive measure> likewise has an integrable signed <Radon-Nikodym derivative>.

The sigma-finiteness hypothesis on the dominating <measure> cannot simply be omitted. On the Borel subsets of $[0,1]$, let $\kappa$ be <counting measure> and $\nu$ <Lebesgue measure>. Absolute continuity holds because the only $\kappa$-<null set> is empty. A <Radon-Nikodym derivative> would satisfy $h(x)=\nu(\{x\})=0$ at every point, contradicting $\nu([0,1])=1$. Here <counting measure> is not sigma-finite.

For a <signed measure> define its <total variation measure> by
$$
|\mu|(E)=\sup\left\{\sum_{r=1}^m|\mu(E_r)|:E=\bigsqcup_{r=1}^m E_r,\ E_r\in\mathfrak M\right\}.
$$
The value at the empty set is zero. For disjoint <measurable sets> $A_j$ with union $A$, any finite partition $E_r$ of $A$ gives
$$
\sum_r|\mu(E_r)|\leq\sum_j\sum_r|\mu(E_r\cap A_j)|\leq\sum_j|\mu|(A_j).
$$
Taking the <supremum> proves one inequality. In the reverse direction choose, for the first $k$ sets, finite partitions approaching their variation values. Combining these partitions and the remaining set $A\setminus\bigcup_{j\leq k}A_j$ gives
$$
|\mu|(A)\geq\sum_{j=1}^k|\mu|(A_j)-\varepsilon.
$$
If one term is infinite, its partition sums can instead be made arbitrarily large. Letting $\varepsilon\downarrow0$ and $k\to\infty$ proves **<countable additivity>**, including infinite values. Thus $|\mu|$ is a <positive measure>. This uses only <countable additivity> of $\mu$, without assuming a <Hahn decomposition>.

From now on $|\mu|(X)<\infty$, so all signed quantities below are finite. Since $|\mu|(E)\geq|\mu(E)|$, the set function
$$
\mu^+(E)=\frac{|\mu|(E)+\mu(E)}2
$$
is nonnegative. <Countable additivity> follows from that of the two terms, so $\mu^+$ is a finite <positive measure>.

Set $s=\sup\{|\mu|(M):\mu^+(M)=0\}$. This is finite. Choose measurable $M_j$ with $\mu^+(M_j)=0$ and $|\mu|(M_j)\to s$. Then
$$
M_-=\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}M_j
$$
is $\mu^+$-null by <countable subadditivity>, and <monotonicity> gives $|\mu|(M_-)\geq s$. The definition of $s$ gives the reverse inequality. Hence **$M_-$ attains the maximum**, rather than merely approximating it.

= Solution

Start with $R_0=A$. Having chosen disjoint $A_1,\ldots,A_i$, put $R_i=A\setminus\bigcup_{j\leq i}A_j$ and
$$
s_i=\sup\{\mu(B):B\in\mathfrak M,\ B\subseteq R_i\}.
$$
The empty set shows $s_i\geq0$, and finite total variation bounds it above. If $s_i>0$, choose $A_{i+1}\subseteq R_i$ with $\mu(A_{i+1})\geq s_i/2>0$; if $s_i=0$, choose $A_{i+1}=\varnothing$. This is the required recursion, including the initial step $i=0$.

Disjointness and nonnegativity give $\sum_i\mu(A_i)\leq|\mu|(A)<\infty$, hence $\mu(A_i)\to0$. Write $R=A\setminus\bigcup_i A_i$. For every measurable $B\subseteq R$,
$$
\mu(B)\leq s_i\leq2\mu(A_{i+1})\longrightarrow0.
$$
Thus every measurable subset of $R$ has nonpositive <measure>. Each finite partition of $R$ consequently has variation sum exactly $-\mu(R)$, and therefore $|\mu|(R)=-\mu(R)$. It follows that $\mu^+(R)=0$. Finally <countable additivity> gives
$$
\boxed{\mu(R)=\mu(A)-\sum_i\mu(A_i)\leq\mu(A)<0,\qquad\mu^+(R)=0.}
$$

= Solution

If $S\subseteq M_-$, then $\mu^+(S)=0$, so $\mu(S)=-|\mu|(S)\leq0$. Suppose instead that $S\cap M_-=\varnothing$ but $\mu(S)<0$. The preceding construction gives $R\subseteq S$ with $\mu^+(R)=0$ and $|\mu|(R)=-\mu(R)>0$. Then $M_-\cup R$ is $\mu^+$-null with variation strictly larger than that of $M_-$, a contradiction. Hence **$\mu(S)\geq0$ on the complement of $M_-$**. This is a <Hahn decomposition>.

On $M_+=X\setminus M_-$ every partition has nonnegative <signed measures>, so $|\mu|(E)=\mu(E)=\mu^+(E)$ for $E\subseteq M_+$. On $M_-$, $\mu^+$ vanishes. Splitting an arbitrary <measurable set> between these two sets gives
$$
\boxed{\mu^+(E)=\int_E\mathbf1_{M_+}\,d|\mu|,\qquad \frac{d\mu^+}{d|\mu|}=\mathbf1_{M_+}\quad |\mu|\text{-a.e.}}
$$
In particular $\mu^+\ll|\mu|$, and the exhibited <Radon-Nikodym derivative> verifies the <Radon-Nikodym theorem> for this pair directly.

An <outer measure> on $X$ is a function $\mu:\mathcal P(X)\to[0,\infty]$ satisfying $\mu(\varnothing)=0$, <monotonicity>, and <countable subadditivity>:
$$
\mu\left(\bigcup_jE_j\right)\leq\sum_j\mu(E_j).
$$
Its <measurable sets> are those satisfying <Caratheodory measurability criterion>:
$$
\boxed{\mathfrak M_\mu^*=\{A\subseteq X:\mu(E)=\mu(E\cap A)+\mu(E\setminus A)\text{ for every }E\subseteq X\}.}
$$
The test runs over all subsets $E$, including nonmeasurable ones.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <Caratheodory measurability criterion> yields a complete sigma-algebra $\mathfrak M_\mu^*$, and $\mu$ restricted to it is a countably additive <measure>. <Completeness> means that every subset of a measurable <null set> is measurable.

For the requested algebra proof, $X$ and $\varnothing$ plainly satisfy the criterion. Replacing $A$ by $X\setminus A$ merely exchanges the two terms, proving closure under complements. If $A,B$ are measurable, apply their splitting identities successively to any $E$:
$$
\mu(E)=\mu(E\cap A)+\mu((E\setminus A)\cap B)+\mu(E\setminus(A\cup B)).
$$
Subadditivity applied to the first two pieces implies
$$
\mu(E)\geq\mu(E\cap(A\cup B))+\mu(E\setminus(A\cup B)).
$$
The reverse inequality is outer subadditivity. Hence $A\cup B$ is measurable, proving closure under finite unions and therefore the **<algebra of sets> property**. The argument never subtracts infinite values.

A <metric outer measure> on a <metric space> $(X,d)$ is an <outer measure> satisfying
$$
\boxed{d(A,B)>0\Longrightarrow\mu(A\cup B)=\mu(A)+\mu(B).}
$$
Here $d(A,B)=\inf\{d(a,b):a\in A,b\in B\}$. Every <Borel set> is measurable for a <metric outer measure>. Consequently its measurable sigma-algebra contains the <Borel sigma-algebra>; it may be strictly larger.

For $E\subseteq\mathbb R^n$, define the <Lebesgue outer measure> by
$$
\mathscr L^*(E)=\inf\left\{\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{vol}(Q_j):E\subseteq\bigcup_jQ_j,\quad Q_j=\prod_{r=1}^n[a_{jr},b_{jr})\right\},
$$
where the boxes are bounded and $\operatorname{vol}(Q_j)=\prod_r(b_{jr}-a_{jr})$. The empty covering handles the empty set. <Monotonicity> follows by inclusion of admissible covers. Taking covers within $\varepsilon2^{-j}$ of the outer <measures> of a sequence of sets proves <countable subadditivity>; when the sum is infinite the inequality is automatic. <Lebesgue measure> is the restriction of $\mathscr L^*$ to its Caratheodory-<measurable sets>.

If $d(A,B)=\delta>0$, subdivide each box of any countable cover of $A\cup B$ into finitely many half-open boxes of diameter smaller than $\delta$, preserving its total volume. No small box can meet both $A$ and $B$. The boxes meeting $A$ cover $A$, and the disjoint family meeting $B$ covers $B$, so the original cover costs at least $\mathscr L^*(A)+\mathscr L^*(B)$. Taking the <infimum> and then using subadditivity gives
$$
\boxed{\mathscr L^*(A\cup B)=\mathscr L^*(A)+\mathscr L^*(B).}
$$
Thus the <outer measure> underlying <Lebesgue measure> is a <metric outer measure>, and in particular every <Borel set> is Lebesgue measurable.

A <regular outer measure> has, for every $E\subseteq X$, a measurable hull $H\supseteq E$ with $\mu(H)=\mu(E)$.

Suppose $\mathcal C$ is an algebra containing $\mathfrak M_\mu^*$ and $\mu$ is finitely additive on $\mathcal C$. Fix $A\in\mathcal C$ and an arbitrary $E$ of finite <outer measure>. Take a measurable hull $H$ of $E$. Both $H\cap A$ and $H\setminus A$ belong to $\mathcal C$, so
$$
\mu(E)=\mu(H)=\mu(H\cap A)+\mu(H\setminus A)\geq\mu(E\cap A)+\mu(E\setminus A).
$$
Outer subadditivity gives equality. If $\mu(E)=\infty$, outer subadditivity already forces the sum on the right to be infinite, again giving equality. Therefore $A$ satisfies the <Caratheodory criterion>. We have proved $\mathcal C\subseteq\mathfrak M_\mu^*$, so
$$
\boxed{\mathcal C=\mathfrak M_\mu^*.}
$$
This proves the <maximal additivity algebra of a regular outer measure> without any sigma-finiteness assumption.

The <Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem> identifies the continuous dual of $C(\mathbb T)$ with the finite complex <Radon measures> on $\mathbb T$: every bounded complex-<linear functional> $L$ has a unique representation
$$
\boxed{L(f)=\int_{\mathbb T}f\,d\mu,\qquad \|L\|=|\mu|(\mathbb T).}
$$
The right side is the total variation norm of the <measure>. On this compact <metric space> <Radon measures> are regular Borel <measures>. A functional is positive precisely when its representing <measure> is positive; then $\|L\|=L(1)=\mu(\mathbb T)$.

Let $m$ be normalized arclength on the <unit circle>, so in angular coordinates $dm=dt/(2\pi)$. It is a finite <Radon measure>. The given identity says that $\mu$ and $m$ represent the same <continuous linear functional> on $C(\mathbb T)$. Uniqueness in the <Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\mu(E)=m(E)=\frac{|E|}{2\pi}\quad\text{for every Borel }E\subseteq\mathbb T.}
$$
The printed phrase “<Borel sets> in $E$” is interpreted as Borel subsets $E$ of the circle.

Write $A_N(f)=(N+1)^{-1}\sum_{n=0}^N f(e^{in\xi})$. For a monomial $z^k$, $k\in\mathbb Z$, the <geometric series> gives
$$
A_N(z^k)=\begin{cases}1,&e^{ik\xi}=1,\\ \displaystyle\frac{1-e^{ik(N+1)\xi}}{(N+1)(1-e^{ik\xi})},&e^{ik\xi}\ne1.\end{cases}
$$
Thus every such average converges, and by <linearity> so does the average of every <trigonometric polynomial>. Also $|A_N(f)|\leq\|f\|_\infty$ uniformly in $N$.

Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose a <trigonometric polynomial> $P$ with $\|f-P\|_\infty<\varepsilon$. Then
$$
|A_N(f)-A_M(f)|\leq2\varepsilon+|A_N(P)-A_M(P)|.
$$
The last term tends to zero as $N,M\to\infty$. The scalar sequence is Cauchy and therefore **the limit exists for every continuous $f$ and every real $\xi$**.

Define $L_\xi(f)=\lim_NA_N(f)$. Limits preserve <linearity> and <positivity>, and $|L_\xi(f)|\leq\|f\|_\infty$, $L_\xi(1)=1$. The <Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem> supplies a unique Radon <probability measure> $\mu^\xi$. By its definition,
$$
\int f\,d\mu_N^\xi\longrightarrow\int f\,d\mu^\xi\quad(f\in C(\mathbb T)),
$$
which is exactly weak-star convergence in $C(\mathbb T)'$.

If $\xi/(2\pi)=p/q$ in lowest terms, the orbit has exactly $q$ points. Averaging a periodic sequence gives
$$
\boxed{\mu^\xi=\frac1q\sum_{r=0}^{q-1}\delta_{e^{ir\xi}}.}
$$
This includes $q=1$. If $\xi/\pi$ is irrational, then $e^{ik\xi}\ne1$ for every nonzero integer $k$. The preceding calculation gives $L_\xi(z^k)=0$ for $k\ne0$ and $L_\xi(1)=1$, exactly the integrals against normalized <Haar measure> $m$. <Density> of <trigonometric polynomials> implies equality on all <continuous functions>, and uniqueness gives
$$
\boxed{\mu^\xi=m\quad\text{when }\xi/\pi\text{ is irrational}.}
$$
These are the periodic-orbit and irrational-rotation cases of <equidistribution>.

For each $n\geq0$, choose an open arc $I_n$ about $e^{in}$ of arclength $\pi2^{-n-2}$, and put $G^1=\bigcup_{n\geq0}I_n$. It contains every point used by every empirical <measure>, whereas
$$
|G^1|\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\pi2^{-n-2}=\frac\pi2<\frac{|\mathbb T|}2.
$$
Thus **$\mu_N^1(G^1)=1$ for all $N$**. Let $K=\mathbb T\setminus G^1$. This is closed and $\mu_N^1(K)=0$ for all $N$. But $1/\pi$ is irrational, so the weak-star limit is normalized arclength and
$$
\boxed{\mu^1(K)=1-\frac{|G^1|}{2\pi}\geq\frac34>0.}
$$
Therefore the proposed implication is **false**. Weak-star convergence tests <continuous functions> and does not assert convergence of the <indicator function> of an arbitrary closed set.

= Solution

Use normalized <Haar measure> $dm=dt/(2\pi)$. A <homogeneous Banach space on the circle> is a Banach subspace $B\subseteq L^1(\mathbb T)$ with $\|f\|_1\leq\|f\|_B$, such that translations $\tau_s f(t)=f(t-s)$ preserve $B$, satisfy $\|\tau_sf\|_B=\|f\|_B$, and obey $\|\tau_sf-f\|_B\to0$ as $s\to0$.

For continuous $g$, the map $s\mapsto g(s)\tau_sf$ is continuous with values in the complete space $B$. Define its Banach-valued Riemann integral, equivalently its <Bochner integral>, by
$$
f*g=\int_{\mathbb T}g(s)\tau_sf\,dm(s).
$$
The <triangle inequality> for Riemann sums and passage to their norm limit give the canonical estimate
$$
\boxed{\|f*g\|_B\leq\int_{\mathbb T}|g(s)|\|\tau_sf\|_B\,dm(s)=\|f\|_B\|g\|_1.}
$$
The continuous inclusion into $L^1$ carries the same Riemann sums to the ordinary <convolution>, so this definition agrees <almost everywhere> with $\int f(t-s)g(s)\,dm(s)$. <Density> of <continuous functions> in $L^1$, <completeness> of $B$, and the estimate extend the operation uniquely to all $g\in L^1$. The ordinary $L^1$ <convolution> estimate identifies that extension with the same integral formula.

A <summability kernel on the circle> is a sequence of continuous periodic functions with
$$
\int k_n\,dm=1,\qquad \sup_n\|k_n\|_1=C<\infty,\qquad\int_{\delta\leq|t|\leq\pi}|k_n(t)|\,dm(t)\longrightarrow0\quad(0<\delta<\pi).
$$
The frequently used stronger condition of uniform convergence to zero off each neighborhood of zero implies the last condition. For $f$ in a homogeneous <Banach space>,
$$
\|f*k_n-f\|_B\leq\int|k_n(s)|\|\tau_sf-f\|_B\,dm(s).
$$
Given $\varepsilon>0$, translation continuity bounds the difference inside a sufficiently small neighborhood by $\varepsilon$; its integral is at most $C\varepsilon$. Outside, use $2\|f\|_B$ and the vanishing tail condition. Hence $f*k_n\to f$ in $B$.

With the indexing needed in part (c), the <Fejér kernel> is
$$
K_n(t)=\frac1{n+1}\left|\sum_{r=0}^n e^{irt}\right|^2=\sum_{|j|\leq n}\left(1-\frac{|j|}{n+1}\right)e^{ijt},\qquad n\geq0.
$$
In particular $K_0=1$. The squared-modulus expression proves $K_n\geq0$, and its constant <Fourier coefficient> proves $\int K_n\,dm=\|K_n\|_1=1$. The <geometric series> yields
$$
K_n(t)=\frac1{n+1}\frac{\sin^2((n+1)t/2)}{\sin^2(t/2)}\leq\frac1{(n+1)\sin^2(\delta/2)}\quad(\delta\leq|t|\leq\pi).
$$
Thus these are summability kernels. For $T_f:g\mapsto f*g$, part (a) gives $\|T_f\|\leq\|f\|_B$. Since $\|K_n\|_1=1$ and $T_fK_n\to f$ in $B$, the reverse bound follows:
$$
\boxed{\|T_f:L^1(\mathbb T)\to B\|=\|f\|_B.}
$$

For $n\geq1$ put $q_n(t)=2nK_{n-1}(t)\sin(nt)$. Using $\sin(nt)=(e^{int}-e^{-int})/(2i)$ and the <Fourier coefficients> of $K_{n-1}$ gives
$$
\widehat q_n(k)=-ik\quad(|k|\leq n).
$$
Indeed, when $1\leq k\leq n$ only the $e^{int}$ shift contributes, with coefficient $(-in)(1-|k-n|/n)=-ik$. For $-n\leq k\leq-1$ only the opposite shift contributes and gives $in(1-|k+n|/n)=-ik$; at $k=0$ both coefficients vanish.

If $P=\sum_{|k|\leq n}a_ke^{ikt}$, <convolution> multiplies its <Fourier coefficients> by those of $q_n$. Thus $-P*q_n=\sum ika_ke^{ikt}=P'$, proving the exact identity. Since $K_{n-1}\geq0$ and has integral one,
$$
\boxed{\|P'\|_\infty\leq\|P\|_\infty\|q_n\|_1\leq2n\|P\|_\infty.}
$$
For $n=0$ the polynomial is constant and the bound is immediate. This elementary Fejér-kernel argument proves the requested estimate; the sharp <Bernstein inequality for trigonometric polynomials> has constant $n$.
