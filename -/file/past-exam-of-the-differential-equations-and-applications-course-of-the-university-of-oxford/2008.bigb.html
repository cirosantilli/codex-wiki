= Solution
{parent=a}

Set $y=\dot x$. The <phase>-plane system is $\dot x=y$, $\dot y=-9x+\epsilon(1-4x^2)y$. The origin is its only <equilibrium>, and its <Jacobian matrix> is $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-9&\epsilon\end{pmatrix}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\lambda_\pm=\frac\epsilon2\pm i\sqrt{9-\epsilon^2/4}.}
$$
For the stated small positive $\epsilon$, the <eigenvalues> have positive real part and nonzero imaginary part: the origin is an **<unstable focus>**.

= b
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Use the <Poincaré-Lindstedt method>. Introduce $\theta=\Omega t$ and $x=x_0(\theta)+\epsilon x_1(\theta)+\cdots$. The leading equation gives $\omega_0=3$ and $x_0=A_0\cos\theta$, after fixing the arbitrary <phase>. At order $\epsilon$,
$$
9(x_1''+x_1)=6\omega_1A_0\cos\theta+3A_0(A_0^2-1)\sin\theta+3A_0^3\sin3\theta.
$$
Here $4\cos^2\theta\sin\theta=\sin\theta+\sin3\theta$. Periodicity requires the two resonant coefficients to vanish. With $A_0>0$, this gives
$$
\boxed{A_0=1,\qquad\omega_0=3,\qquad\omega_1=0.}
$$
Therefore the leading <limit cycle> is $x(t)=\cos(3t)+O(\epsilon)$, with frequency $3+O(\epsilon^2)$. For example the nonresonant first correction contains $-\epsilon\sin(9t)/24$; its homogeneous <sine>/<cosine> pieces depend on <phase> and <amplitude> conventions. The absence of a first-order frequency shift is a solvability condition, not an assumption.

= c
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Use the <method of multiple scales> with independent fast and slow variables $t$ and $\tau=\epsilon t$, and write $x_0=R(\tau)\cos\Theta$, $\Theta=3t+\Phi(\tau)$. At the next order, $x_{1,tt}+9x_1=-2x_{0,t\tau}-(4x_0^2-1)x_{0,t}$. Its resonant terms are
$$
[6R'+3R(R^2-1)]\sin\Theta+6R\Phi'\cos\Theta.
$$
Suppressing <secular terms> yields
$$
\boxed{\frac{dR_0}{d\tau}=\frac12R_0(1-R_0^2),\qquad\frac{d\Phi_0}{d\tau}=0.}
$$
The leading initial data are $\boxed{R_0(0)=4,\ \Phi_0(0)=0}$, up to adding an integer multiple of $2\pi$ to the <phase>. Thus $x(t)=R_0(\epsilon t)\cos[3t+\Phi_0(\epsilon t)]+O(\epsilon)$. The <amplitude> decreases from four towards the stable value one, consistent with the selected <limit cycle>; the zero-<amplitude> state is unstable. Order-$\epsilon$ corrections enforce the exact initial <derivative> after differentiating the slow <amplitude> term.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $L=D^2+1$ on the mixed endpoint domain $y(0)=0$, $y'(1)=0$ in $L^2(0,1)$. <Integration by parts> gives the boundary form
$$
\langle z,Ly\rangle-\langle Lz,y\rangle=[\overline z y'-\overline{z'}y]_0^1.
$$
At zero, $y'(0)$ is free, forcing $z(0)=0$; at one, $y(1)$ is free, forcing $z'(1)=0$. Therefore the <formal adjoint> expression and full adjoint endpoint conditions coincide with the original ones:
$$
\boxed{L^*=D^2+1,\qquad z(0)=0,\quad z'(1)=0.}
$$
With the usual second-order operator domain this is a <self-adjoint> problem. The consequences for its <eigenvalues> and <eigenfunctions> follow below.

= i
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}

For $Ly=-\lambda y$, <self-adjointness> gives $-\lambda\|y\|^2=\langle y,Ly\rangle=\overline{\langle y,Ly\rangle}$. Since an <eigenfunction> is nonzero, $\boxed{\lambda\in\mathbb R}$. One may also see reality directly from $\lambda\int|y|^2=\int|y'|^2-\int|y|^2$, whose boundary term vanishes under the mixed conditions.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

If $Ly_j=-\lambda_jy_j$, then $\langle y_1,Ly_2\rangle=\langle Ly_1,y_2\rangle$ gives $(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)\langle y_1,y_2\rangle=0$. Thus <eigenfunctions> belonging to distinct <eigenvalues> are **<orthogonal>**. <Normalization> can make them <orthonormal>. Equality of <eigenvalues> alone would not force arbitrary <eigenvectors> to be <orthogonal>, although in this particular scalar separated problem each <eigenspace> is one-dimensional.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The equation is $y''+(1+\lambda)y=0$. If $1+\lambda\leq0$, the <linear> or <hyperbolic> branches cannot meet both homogeneous endpoint conditions nontrivially. Otherwise $y=C\sin(kx)$, $k=\sqrt{1+\lambda}$, and $y'(1)=Ck\cos k=0$ gives $k_n=(n+1/2)\pi$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\lambda_n=(n+\tfrac12)^2\pi^2-1,\qquad\phi_n(x)=\sqrt2\sin[(n+\tfrac12)\pi x],\quad n=0,1,\ldots.}
$$
The factor $\sqrt2$ makes the <eigenfunctions> unit normalized. All the <eigenvalues> are positive, in particular none is zero.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The difference of two solutions would satisfy $Ly=0$ with $y(0)=0$, $y'(1)=0$. This would be a zero <eigenvalue> of the homogeneous problem, but part (b) shows there is none. Hence **at most one solution exists**. For continuous forcing the standard second-order existence construction also works: the free initial slope is fixed by an equation with coefficient $\cos1\ne0$. Thus for the stated regular boundary-value problem the solution is unique even with nonzero endpoint data.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Expand a forcing in the complete <orthonormal> <basis> $\phi_n$. Since $L\phi_n=-\lambda_n\phi_n$, its inverse multiplies the $n$th coefficient by $-1/\lambda_n$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{g(x,t)=-\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{\phi_n(x)\phi_n(t)}{\lambda_n}=-2\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{\sin(k_nx)\sin(k_nt)}{k_n^2-1}.}
$$
The negative sign follows from the <eigenvalue> convention $Ly=-\lambda y$. This <kernel> satisfies the mixed endpoint conditions in its first argument and $L_xg=\delta(x-t)$.

The <integral equation> says precisely $h=L^{-1}z$ with the homogeneous mixed endpoint conditions. Since the supplied $h$ satisfies those conditions, apply $L$ directly:
$$
\boxed{z(x)=Lh=h''+h=x^2-2x+2.}
$$
No <integration> is required. The absence of a zero <eigenvalue> established in (b)–(c) also proves uniqueness of this recovered forcing.

= Solution

Write the integral part as $Ky=12b\langle a,y\rangle-30a\langle b,y\rangle$, where $a(x)=e^{-x}$ and $b(x)=e^{-2x}$. The <kernel> is real, so its adjoint transposes the two arguments. Hence
$$
\boxed{L^*z(x)=z(x)+\int_0^\infty[12e^{-x-2t}-30e^{-2x-t}]z(t)\,dt.}
$$
This follows by exchanging the two <integrations> in the $L^2(0,\infty)$ <inner product>; the exponential factors make the <finite-rank operators> bounded.

= b
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Every <eigenvector> with <eigenvalue> different from one lies in $E=\operatorname{span}\{a,b\}$, because $(\lambda-1)y=Ky\in E$. The elementary overlaps are $\langle a,a\rangle=1/2$, $\langle a,b\rangle=1/3$, $\langle b,b\rangle=1/4$. Thus
$$
La=-9a+6b,\quad Lb=-\tfrac{15}2a+5b,\quad
[L]_E=\begin{pmatrix}-9&-15/2\\6&5\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its <characteristic polynomial> is $\lambda(\lambda+4)$, so the finite-<multiplicity> eigenpairs are
$$
\boxed{\lambda=0:\ y_0=5e^{-x}-6e^{-2x};\qquad\lambda=-4:\ y_{-4}=3e^{-x}-2e^{-2x}.}
$$
Each <eigenspace> has <dimension> one. On $E^\perp$, both integrals defining $K$ vanish, so $Ly=y$. Thus $\boxed{\lambda=1}$ is also an <eigenvalue>, of infinite <multiplicity>, and there are no others.

The adjoint also leaves $E$ invariant, with $L^*a=5a-15b$ and $L^*b=3a-9b$. By <adjoint in weighted coordinates>, its <matrix> in the same <basis> is $\begin{pmatrix}5&3\\-15&-9\end{pmatrix}$. Solving the two <eigenvector> equations gives
$$
\boxed{\lambda=0:\ z_0=3e^{-x}-5e^{-2x};\qquad\lambda=-4:\ z_{-4}=e^{-x}-3e^{-2x}.}
$$
The <basis> $a,b$ is not <orthonormal>, so one must not simply transpose the previous numerical <matrix> without accounting for its <Gram matrix>. Directly transposing the integral <kernel> avoids that error.

{parent=3}

= Solution
{parent=d}

The nonzero <kernel> of $L$ rules out a unique solution for every value of $c$. By the <Fredholm alternative>, solvability requires <orthogonality> of the forcing to $\ker L^*=\operatorname{span}\{3a-5b\}$. Its pairing with $6a+cb$ is
$$
\langle3a-5b,6a+cb\rangle=9+c-10-\frac{5c}4=-1-\frac c4.
$$
Thus only $c=-4$ is compatible. In that case $6a-4b=2y_{-4}$ and a particular solution is $-y_{-4}/2$. All solutions are
$$
\boxed{y(x)=-\frac32e^{-x}+e^{-2x}+C(5e^{-x}-6e^{-2x}),\qquad c=-4.}
$$
Therefore **infinitely many solutions exist for $c=-4$, no solution exists for $c\ne-4$, and no parameter value gives a unique solution**.

= Solution

Integrate the <conservation law> over $[-R,R]$: $d\int_{-R}^R\rho\,dx/dt=-t[\rho^2]_{-R}^R$. The Gaussian data and their finite-time <characteristic>/entropy evolution have vanishing far-field flux. Taking $R\to\infty$ yields $\boxed{M'(t)=0}$ and hence $\boxed{M(t)=\sqrt{2\pi}}$. After a <shock> forms, its <Rankine-Hugoniot condition> preserves the same integral <mass>; the formal multivalued <characteristic> surface itself must be replaced by the conservative <entropy solution>.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Expanding the flux gives $\rho_t+2t\rho\rho_x=0$. Along <characteristics>, $\dot\rho=0$ and $\dot x=2t\rho$. Labeling the initial point by $\xi$ gives
$$
\boxed{\rho=e^{-\xi^2/2},\qquad x=\xi+t^2e^{-\xi^2/2}.}
$$
Equivalently, <time reparametrization for a separable conservation-law flux> uses $T=t^2/2$. The projection <derivative> is $x_\xi=1-t^2\xi e^{-\xi^2/2}$. For large enough $t$ it becomes negative on an interval of positive $\xi$, although it tends to one at both far ends. The projection then folds and assigns several density values to one position. This establishes multivaluedness of the formal <characteristic> continuation, not of the admissible <shock> solution.

= iii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The first fold occurs when $t^2\max_{\xi>0}\xi e^{-\xi^2/2}=1$. <Differentiation> gives $(\xi e^{-\xi^2/2})'=e^{-\xi^2/2}(1-\xi^2)$, so the <maximum> is $e^{-1/2}$ at $\xi=1$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{t_c=e^{1/4}.}
$$
At that point the first singular projection is at $x_c=1+t_c^2e^{-1/2}=2$, with density $e^{-1/2}$.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

The two <characteristic> families are $x=\xi+t^2$, $\rho=1$ for left-state labels, and $x=\xi$, $\rho=0$ for right-state labels. The explicit time-dependent flux is $q=t\rho^2$, so the <shock> speed is
$$
\dot x_s=\frac{t(1^2-0^2)}{1-0}=t.
$$
Starting at zero gives
$$
\boxed{x_s(t)=t^2/2,\qquad\rho=1\text{ for }x<t^2/2,\quad\rho=0\text{ for }x>t^2/2.}
$$
The <characteristic speeds> are $2t$ behind and zero ahead, with <shock> speed $t$ between them. The left parabolas and right vertical lines terminate at the compressive <shock>.

= Solution

Try $\rho=t^af(\eta)$, $\eta=x/t^b$. The boundary data force $a=3$ and $f(0)=1$. The time <derivative> scales as $t^{a-1}$, advection as $t^{2a-b}$ and the source as $t^{a+c}$. Balancing all three gives $c=-1$ and $b=a+1=4$. Substitution then yields
$$
3f-4\eta f'+ff'=f,\qquad
\boxed{c=-1,\quad\eta=x/t^4,\quad(f-4\eta)f'+2f=0,\quad f(0)=1.}
$$
For the pointwise initial trace at any fixed $x>0$, the far-profile requirement is $f(\eta)=o(\eta^{3/4})$ as $\eta\to\infty$ on any smooth branch extending that far; a compact-support continuation also meets it. Solving or selecting a <shock> continuation was not required. The time-dependent source is singular at $t=0$, so the similarity reduction is stated on $t>0$.

= Solution
{parent=5}

The <square-root reduction of a quadratic eikonal equation> explains why two initial normal derivatives are possible. Set $F=p^2+q^2-4u$. <Charpit's equations> are $\dot x=2p$, $\dot y=2q$, $\dot u=2p^2+2q^2=8u$, $\dot p=4p$, $\dot q=4q$. Parametrize the initial semicircle by $(x_0,y_0)=(\cos s,\sin s)$, $-\pi/2<s<\pi/2$. Tangential compatibility gives $-p_0\sin s+q_0\cos s=0$, and $F=0$ gives $p_0^2+q_0^2=4$. Thus there are two normal-gradient choices, $p_0=2\varepsilon\cos s$, $q_0=2\varepsilon\sin s$, $\varepsilon=\pm1$.

<Integration> gives the requested parametric solutions
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}x&=[1+\varepsilon(e^{4t}-1)]\cos s,\\y&=[1+\varepsilon(e^{4t}-1)]\sin s,\\u&=e^{8t},\quad p=2\varepsilon e^{4t}\cos s,\quad q=2\varepsilon e^{4t}\sin s.\end{aligned}}
$$
For $\varepsilon=+1$, $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=e^{4t}$, so $u_+=r^2$ on the whole right half-plane. For $\varepsilon=-1$, before focusing at the origin, $r=2-e^{4t}$, so $u_-=(2-r)^2$ on $x>0$, $0<r<2$. The origin is a <caustic> for this inward family. The circle $r=2$ is approached only as <characteristic> time tends to negative infinity, where $u$ and its gradient vanish. It cannot be crossed by this initial <characteristic> chart. Thus the initial data alone permit two branches; each is locally unique only after its normal-gradient sign is chosen.

On the added half-line write $x_0=a\leq0$, $y_0=1$, $u_0=1$. Tangential <differentiation> gives $p_0=0$ and $q_0=2\delta$, $\delta=\pm1$. Its <characteristics> give
$$
\boxed{x=a,\qquad y=1+\delta(e^{4t}-1),\qquad u=e^{8t}.}
$$
The plus branch is $u=y^2$ on $x\leq0,y>0$; the minus branch is $u=(2-y)^2$ on $x\leq0,y<2$. On the positive $y$-axis, the semicircle traces are $y^2$ and $(2-y)^2$, respectively. Therefore **only matching sign choices are continuous throughout their common positive-axis coverage**. Mixed choices agree merely at the initial junction $y=1$.

For the matching-plus branch the data-covered open domain is
$$
\boxed{D_+=\{x>0\}\cup\{y>0\},\qquad
u_+=\begin{cases}x^2+y^2,&x>0,\\y^2,&x\leq0,\ y>0.\end{cases}}
$$
For the matching-minus branch it is
$$
\boxed{D_-=\{x>0,\ x^2+y^2<4\}\cup\{x<0,\ y<2\}\cup\{x=0,\ 0<y<2\}.}
$$
Its formula is $(2-\sqrt{x^2+y^2})^2$ in the right portion and $(2-y)^2$ in the left portion. The origin is excluded. The negative $y$-axis must also be a cut: the two formulas have different traces there, so including it would destroy continuity. Both matching solutions are $C^1$ across the included positive-axis seam; generally their second transverse <derivatives> differ, which is compatible with this first-order problem. Values beyond these <characteristic> charts can sometimes be continued, but are not selected by the initial data through a nonsingular <characteristic> graph.

= Solution
{parent=6}

With $A$ invertible, write the system as $\mathbf u_x+M\mathbf u_y=\mathbf d$, $M=A^{-1}B$. Real distinct <eigenvalues> of $M$ give strict <hyperbolicity>; a real complete <eigenbasis> gives the usual diagonalizable <hyperbolic> form. The <characteristic curves> satisfy $dy/dx=\lambda_i$. Left <eigenvectors> form <characteristic> dependent combinations $w_i=\ell_i\mathbf u$; for constant coefficients they obey separate <transport equations>. <Characteristic> coordinates are functions constant on those curves, such as $y-\lambda_i x$ in the constant case. For variable coefficients extra <derivatives> of the <eigenvectors> can couple the combinations; local <characteristic> directions still follow the real <matrix> pencil. If $A$ is singular one first chooses a noncharacteristic coordinate direction with an invertible coefficient combination.

For the specified constants,
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}5&3\\-6&-4\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf d=\begin{pmatrix}3\\-2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its speeds are $2$ and $-1$, with left <eigenvectors> $(2,1)$ and $(1,1)$. Hence
$$
w_+=2u+v,\quad (w_+)_x+2(w_+)_y=4,\qquad
w_-=u+v,\quad (w_-)_x-(w_-)_y=1.
$$
The transported <Riemann invariants> are $\boxed{R_+=2u+v-4x,\quad R_-=u+v-x}$, constant on $y-2x=\text{constant}$ and $y+x=\text{constant}$, respectively. Initial data give $w_+(0,s)=s-\sin(s/2)$ and $w_-(0,s)=-e^s$, so
$$
w_+=y+2x-\sin(y/2-x),\qquad w_-=-e^{y+x}+x.
$$
Invert these two combinations:
$$
\boxed{u=e^{y+x}+y+x-\sin(y/2-x),\qquad v=-2e^{y+x}-y+\sin(y/2-x).}
$$
Both <characteristic> feet must lie on the supplied positive initial half-line. Thus
$$
\boxed{y-2x>0,\qquad y+x>0,\quad\text{equivalently }y>\max(2x,-x).}
$$
Outside this sector at least one invariant needs unsupplied initial data. The closed expressions give one possible extension there but are not uniquely forced by the question's initial half-line.

= Solution
{parent=7}

For fixed source $(\xi,\eta)\in D$, define the <Dirichlet Green function> by $-\Delta_{x,y}G=\delta_{(\xi,\eta)}$ in $D$, $G=0$ on $\partial D$. It is <harmonic> away from the source and has <singularity> $-(2\pi)^{-1}\log\sqrt{(x-\xi)^2+(y-\eta)^2}$. The outward <normal vector> <derivative> is $\partial_nG=\mathbf n\cdot\nabla_{x,y}G$ at a boundary point of arc-length parameter $s$.

<Green's second identity> gives $\int_D(u\Delta G-G\Delta u)=\int_{\partial D}(u\partial_nG-G\partial_nu)\,ds$. Substituting the two PDEs and the boundary data yields
$$
-u(\xi,\eta)+\int_DfG=\int_{\partial D}g\partial_nG,\qquad
\boxed{u(\xi,\eta)=-\int_{\partial D}g\partial_nG\,ds+\int_DfG\,dx\,dy.}
$$
The singular version follows by excising a small source disk and taking its radius to zero, or directly by the distributional identity.

For the upper half-plane use the opposite-sign image source at $(\xi,-\eta)$:
$$
\boxed{G=\frac1{4\pi}\log\frac{(x-\xi)^2+(y+\eta)^2}{(x-\xi)^2+(y-\eta)^2}.}
$$
The image lies outside the domain, so it is <harmonic> inside; the true source has the required unit <singularity>, and the two distances agree at $y=0$, giving zero boundary value. On that boundary $\mathbf n=-\mathbf e_y$, so
$$
\partial_nG=-\frac\eta{\pi[(s-\xi)^2+\eta^2]}.
$$
The assumed far-field decay makes the large semicircle contribution vanish. With $f=0$, the representation becomes
$$
\boxed{u(\xi,\eta)=\frac\eta\pi\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{g(s)}{(\xi-s)^2+\eta^2}\,ds.}
$$
Finally set $s=\xi+\eta\tan\theta$, $ds=\eta\sec^2\theta\,d\theta$. The <Jacobian matrix> cancels the denominator, giving
$$
\boxed{u(\xi,\eta)=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}g(\xi+\eta\tan\theta)\,d\theta.}
$$
If $g$ is even, the substitution $s\mapsto-s$ in the <Poisson integral> gives $u(-\xi,\eta)=u(\xi,\eta)$. At $\eta=0$ this agrees with the continuous even boundary trace.

{parent=2008/B}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=8}

At a positive interior <maximum>, $u_x=0$, $u_{xx}\leq0$, and $u_t\geq0$ if the point is compared with its past. The PDE instead gives $u_t\leq-u^3<0$, a contradiction. Thus a positive global/past-strip <maximum> must come from the spatial boundary or initial-time limit. The far value is positive, so any attained global <maximum> is positive; the argument does not prohibit a negative local <maximum> in isolation.

For uniqueness put $w=u_1-u_2$. It satisfies
$$
w_t=w_{xx}-\frac{x}{2t}w_x-aw,\qquad a=u_1^2+u_1u_2+u_2^2\geq0.
$$
The <parabolic comparison principle> with zero difference data gives $w\leq0$ and then $-w\leq0$, hence $w=0$. As usual on this noncompact singular-time domain, this uses an admissible comparison class in which the initial and far-field limits justify truncation; for example bounded/subquadratic differences on finite past strips with the corresponding uniform zero traces suffice. A barrier $\delta(1+x^2+2t)$ controls spatial infinity, since $\partial_t-\partial_x^2+[x/(2t)]\partial_x+a$ applied to it is nonnegative. The printed fixed-point limits alone should not silently be substituted for all uniform/growth hypotheses of this comparison argument.

Use <drift cancellation in a parabolic similarity reduction>. For the <similarity ansatz>, <derivative> scaling gives
$$
u_t=t^{-3/2}(-f/2-\beta\eta f'),\quad u_{xx}=t^{-1/2-2\beta}f'',\quad-\frac{x}{2t}u_x=-t^{-3/2}\eta f'/2.
$$
Thus $\boxed{\beta=1/2}$, the drift terms cancel, and $f''=f^3-f/2$ with $f(\infty)=1/\sqrt2$. Use the <positive-equilibrium separatrix for a cubic profile equation>. Multiply by $f'$ and use the limiting <equilibrium> to determine the <integration> constant:
$$
\frac12(f')^2=\frac14f^4-\frac14f^2+\frac1{16},\qquad(f')^2=\frac12(f^2-1/2)^2.
$$
The branch approaching the positive far <equilibrium> satisfies $f'=(1/2-f^2)/\sqrt2$; the constant <equilibrium> is included as well. Separation gives
$$
\boxed{f(\eta)=\frac1{\sqrt2}\frac{1-Ae^{-\eta}}{1+Ae^{-\eta}},\qquad
u(x,t)=\frac1{\sqrt{2t}}\frac{1-Ae^{-x/\sqrt t}}{1+Ae^{-x/\sqrt t}}.}
$$
For a finite boundary value at $x=0$ and no interior pole, take $A>-1$. Values $-1<A<0$ describe the corresponding coth-type approach from above; $A=0$ is constant, and positive $A$ gives the tanh-type branch. The other first-integral sign cannot approach the positive <equilibrium> nontrivially as $\eta\to\infty$.

The requested deficit integral is, after $x=\sqrt t\eta$,
$$
\int_0^\infty(1/\sqrt{2t}-u)\,dx=\sqrt2\int_0^\infty\frac{Ae^{-\eta}}{1+Ae^{-\eta}}\,d\eta=\sqrt2\log(1+A).
$$
Therefore $\boxed{A=1}$, and the selected solution is $\boxed{u(x,t)=(2t)^{-1/2}\tanh[x/(2\sqrt t)]}$.
