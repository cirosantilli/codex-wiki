= Solution
{parent=a}

The characteristic <polynomial> is $r^2+2r+2=(r+1)^2+1$, with distinct complex roots $r=-1\pm i$. Taking the real and imaginary parts of the corresponding <exponential functions> gives the real <fundamental system of solutions> $e^{-x}\cos x$ and $e^{-x}\sin x$. Consequently the general real solution is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=e^{-x}(C\cos x+D\sin x),\qquad C,D\in\mathbb R.}
$$
Their <Wronskian> is $e^{-2x}\ne0$, so they span the two-dimensional real solution space.

{parent=i}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

For each $0<\xi<2\pi$, the <Green's function> is a <distribution> in $x$ satisfying
$$
\boxed{(\partial_x^2+2\partial_x+2)g(x,\xi)=\delta(x-\xi),\qquad g(0,\xi)=0,\quad g_x(2\pi,\xi)=0.}
$$
Equivalently, $g$ is a classical homogeneous solution on each side of $\xi$, satisfies the same endpoint conditions, and obeys the <Green function jump conditions>
$$
\boxed{g(\xi^+,\xi)=g(\xi^-,\xi),\qquad g_x(\xi^+,\xi)-g_x(\xi^-,\xi)=1.}
$$
To see equivalence, let $[g]$ and $[g_x]$ denote its jumps. The singular part of $L_xg$ is $[g]\delta'(x-\xi)+([g_x]+2[g])\delta(x-\xi)$. Matching a unit <Dirac delta distribution> with no <derivative> first forces <continuity>, then the unit <derivative> jump. Conversely these conditions produce exactly that <distribution>. The solution for a regular forcing is $y(x)=\int_0^{2\pi}g(x,\xi)f(\xi)\,d\xi$. The homogeneous endpoint problem has only the zero solution, which ensures uniqueness of this <kernel>.

{parent=i}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Choose a left-end solution $u(x)=e^{-x}\sin x$ and a right-end solution $v(x)=e^{-x}(\sin x+\cos x)$. They satisfy $u(0)=0$ and $v'(2\pi)=0$. Their <Wronskian>, with the order used here, is
$$
W(x)=u(x)v'(x)-u'(x)v(x)=-e^{-2x}.
$$
The <Green's function> is $u(x_<)v(x_>)/W(\xi)$, where $x_<=\min(x,\xi)$ and $x_>=\max(x,\xi)$. Explicitly,
$$
\boxed{g(x,\xi)=-e^{\xi-x}\begin{cases}
\sin x(\sin\xi+\cos\xi),&0\leq x\leq\xi,\\
\sin\xi(\sin x+\cos x),&\xi\leq x\leq2\pi.
\end{cases}}
$$
The two expressions agree at the source. Their <derivative> difference is $[u(\xi)v'(\xi)-u'(\xi)v(\xi)]/W(\xi)=1$, checking both normalization and sign. The first-order <derivative> term means the <kernel> need not be symmetric under exchange of $x$ and $\xi$.

= Solution

A <distribution> is a **<continuous linear functional> on the space of <test functions>** $\mathcal D(\mathbb R)=C_c^\infty(\mathbb R)$. A precise useful form of <continuity> is: for every compact set $K\subset\mathbb R$ there exist an <integer> $m\geq0$ and a constant $C_K$ such that
$$
\boxed{|\langle T,\phi\rangle|\leq C_K\max_{0\leq j\leq m}\sup_{x\in K}|\phi^{(j)}(x)|\quad\text{when }\operatorname{supp}\phi\subset K.}
$$
Equivalently, if a sequence of <test functions> has supports in one fixed compact set and every <derivative> tends uniformly to zero, then its images under $T$ tend to zero. Keeping a common <compact support> is part of the <test function> topology; mere pointwise convergence is insufficient.

= b
{parent=ii}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

<Linearity> follows from <linearity> of differentiation and point evaluation. For a <test function> supported in a fixed compact set $K$, its value and <derivative> vanish at any point outside $K$. Therefore
$$
|\phi'(0)+\phi(1)|\leq\sup_K|\phi'|+\sup_K|\phi|\leq2\max_{0\leq j\leq1}\sup_K|\phi^{(j)}|.
$$
This proves the <continuity> estimate with $m=1$ and $C_K=2$ for every $K$. Thus the stated mapping is a <distribution> of order at most one, supported in $\{0,1\}$. In particular, a sequence tending to zero in the <test function> topology has both $\phi_n'(0)\to0$ and $\phi_n(1)\to0$.

= Solution

The <distributional derivative> is defined by moving the <derivative> onto a <test function> and changing the sign:
$$
\boxed{\langle S',\phi\rangle=-\langle S,\phi'\rangle.}
$$
Since $\langle\delta_a,\phi\rangle=\phi(a)$, one has $\langle\delta_0',\phi\rangle=-\phi'(0)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{T=-\delta_0'+\delta_1=-\delta'(x)+\delta(x-1).}
$$
The minus sign in front of $\delta_0'$ is essential: it makes its action the positive <derivative> evaluation required here.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Set $t=\log x$, so $0<t<\pi$, and write $y(x)=x^{-2}w(t)$. Direct differentiation of this <Cauchy-Euler equation> gives
$$
x^2y''+5xy'+y=x^{-2}(w''-3w).
$$
The <eigenvalue> equation is therefore $-w''=(\lambda-3)w$ with $w(0)=w(\pi)=0$. For $\lambda-3\leq0$, the linear/hyperbolic solutions cannot satisfy both endpoint conditions except trivially. For $\lambda-3>0$, the sine solution vanishes at both endpoints exactly when its frequency is a positive <integer>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\lambda_k=k^2+3,\qquad y_k(x)=x^{-2}\sin(k\log x),\qquad k=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
Each associated <eigenspace> is one-dimensional.

**The displayed differential operator is not <self-adjoint> for the unweighted <inner product>.** Let $A=x^2\partial_x^2+5x\partial_x+1$. <Integration by parts> on compactly supported smooth functions gives its <formal adjoint>
$$
A^*f=(x^2f)''-(5xf)'+f=x^2f''-xf'-2f,
$$
which differs from $A$. One can see an explicit failure of symmetry without boundary terms: take a nonzero real compactly supported smooth $f$ in the interval and $g=xf$. Then
$$
\langle f,Ag\rangle-\langle Af,g\rangle=3\int_1^{e^\pi}x(fg'-f'g)\,dx=3\int_1^{e^\pi}xf^2\,dx>0.
$$
Both <test functions> obey the Dirichlet endpoint conditions. Changing the sign of $A$ for the eigenproblem does not repair this failure. A different, weighted <inner product> with weight $x^3$ does give the <Sturm-Liouville form> $x^3Ay=(x^5y')'+x^3y$, explaining why a real discrete <spectrum> is nevertheless natural.

Use the cosine difference identity to separate the <kernel>:
$$
k(x,t)=\cos2x\cos2t+\tfrac12\sin2x\sin2t.
$$
Thus $L$ has <image> $E=\operatorname{span}\{c,s\}$, where $c(x)=\cos2x$, $s(x)=\sin2x$. Their squared norms in $L^2(0,\pi)$ are $\pi/2$ and they are <orthogonal>. Consequently
$$
Lc=(\pi/2)c,\qquad Ls=(\pi/4)s.
$$
Every nonzero <eigenvalue> must belong to this two-dimensional <image>, so the complete list of <eigenvalues> of finite multiplicity is
$$
\boxed{\lambda=\pi/2\text{ with eigenfunctions }Ac,\qquad \lambda=\pi/4\text{ with eigenfunctions }Bs,\quad A,B\ne0.}
$$
Each has multiplicity one. On $E^\perp$ the operator vanishes, giving the zero <eigenvalue> infinite multiplicity. For example $\boxed{y(x)=1}$ is a nonzero element of the <kernel> because both relevant integrals over the interval vanish.

The forcing is $\tfrac12\sin2x$, which lies in the <image> of $L$. Since the <eigenvalue> on $\sin2x$ is $\pi/4$, a particular solution is $y_p=(2/\pi)\sin2x$. The full family is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac2\pi\sin2x+z(x),\qquad z\perp\cos2x,\quad z\perp\sin2x.}
$$
Thus **solutions exist and are not unique**: adding any element of the infinite-dimensional <kernel> leaves $Ly$ unchanged. For example every constant can be added.

= Solution
{parent=β}

Every image of $L$ is a <linear combination> $a\cos2x+b\sin2x$. The function $\cos x$ is not such a combination. Indeed these functions are continuous, so equality almost everywhere would imply equality everywhere; evaluation at the endpoints would require simultaneously $a=1$ and $a=-1$. Therefore the forcing is outside the <image> and **there is no solution**. <Orthogonality> to the <kernel> is the equivalent <image> test for this <self-adjoint> <finite-rank operator>.

Rewrite the equation as $(L-I)y=\cos x$. On $E=\operatorname{span}\{\cos2x,\sin2x\}$, the operator $L-I$ has <eigenvalues> $\pi/2-1$ and $\pi/4-1$, both nonzero. On $E^\perp$ it equals $-I$. The <orthogonal> decomposition $L^2(0,\pi)=E\oplus E^\perp$ therefore makes $L-I$ bijective with <bounded inverse>. In particular **there is exactly one solution** for this forcing. This is also the <finite-rank Fredholm alternative>: $1$ is not an <eigenvalue> of $L$, so adding the identity term removes the <image> obstruction present in $Ly=f$.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Normalize the <Laguerre differential equation> as $y''+P(x)y'+Q(x)y=0$, where $P(x)=1/x-1$ and $Q(x)=n/x$. At $x=0$, the products $xP=1-x$ and $x^2Q=nx$ are <analytic function>[analytic]. Thus zero is a <regular singular point>, with <indicial equation>
$$
\boxed{r(r-1)+r=r^2=0,\qquad r_1=r_2=0.}
$$
At $x=1$ the coefficients $P,Q$ are <analytic function>[analytic], so it is an **<ordinary point>**. No singular-point <indicial equation> is needed there.

For infinity put $z=1/x$ and $Y(z)=y(1/z)$. Then the equation becomes
$$
Y''+(z^{-1}+z^{-2})Y'+nz^{-3}Y=0.
$$
At $z=0$, the coefficient $z(z^{-1}+z^{-2})=1+1/z$ is not <analytic function>[analytic]. Therefore infinity is an **<irregular singular point>**, including when $n=0$. A terminating <polynomial> solution does not change the classification of the differential equation.

= ii
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Substitute $y=\sum_{k\geq0}a_kx^k$ and compare the coefficient of $x^k$. The two <derivative> terms give $(k+1)^2a_{k+1}$, while the remaining terms give $(n-k)a_k$. Thus
$$
\boxed{a_{k+1}=\frac{k-n}{(k+1)^2}a_k,\qquad a_0=1.}
$$
Iterating the recurrence gives
$$
\boxed{a_k=\frac{(-1)^kn!}{(n-k)!(k!)^2}=\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}\binom nk\quad(0\leq k\leq n),\qquad a_k=0\quad(k>n).}
$$
Indeed the recurrence first vanishes at $k=n$ and then remains zero. Consequently the <Laguerre polynomial> is
$$
\boxed{L_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}\binom nk x^k,}
$$
with exact degree $n$ and leading coefficient $(-1)^n/n!$. This is the regular power-series branch normalized at zero; the second branch at the repeated <indicial root> is not an additional <analytic function>[analytic] solution with this normalization.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Assume first that $n\geq m$. Substituting <Rodrigues' formula> for $L_n$ gives
$$
I_{nm}=\frac1{n!}\int_0^\infty L_m(x)\frac{d^n}{dx^n}(x^ne^{-x})\,dx
=\frac{(-1)^n}{n!}\int_0^\infty L_m^{(n)}(x)x^ne^{-x}\,dx.
$$
Here <integration by parts> was performed $n$ times. Each boundary term vanishes: at infinity a <polynomial> times $e^{-x}$ tends to zero; at zero every <derivative> of $x^ne^{-x}$ of order less than $n$ vanishes. If $n>m$, the <derivative> $L_m^{(n)}$ is zero, proving <orthogonality>. The case $m>n$ follows by exchanging the two factors.

For $m=n$, the leading coefficient found above gives $L_n^{(n)}=(-1)^n$. Therefore
$$
I_{nn}=\frac1{n!}\int_0^\infty x^ne^{-x}\,dx=\frac{\Gamma(n+1)}{n!}=1.
$$
The same conclusion includes $n=0$ directly. Thus the full <Laguerre orthonormality> relation is
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty L_n(x)L_m(x)e^{-x}\,dx=\delta_{nm}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=a}

Use the initial-curve parameter $s$ and a characteristic parameter $t$. <Charpit's equations> are
$$
\boxed{\dot x=F_p,\quad\dot y=F_q,\quad\dot u=pF_p+qF_q,\quad\dot p=-F_x-pF_u,\quad\dot q=-F_y-qF_u.}
$$
At $t=0$ prescribe $x=x_0(s)$, $y=y_0(s)$ and $u=u_0(s)$. The two initial <derivatives> $p_0,q_0$ must satisfy
$$
\boxed{F(x_0,y_0,u_0,p_0,q_0)=0,\qquad p_0x_0'+q_0y_0'=u_0'.}
$$
The second equation is the <chain rule> along the initial curve. For the usual local Cauchy construction also require the <non-characteristic> condition $x_0'F_q-y_0'F_p\ne0$. It ensures both local determination of an allowed branch of $p_0,q_0$ and local inversion of the projected characteristic coordinates.

Along a characteristic, the <chain rule> and the five equations give
$$
\frac{dF}{dt}=F_xF_p+F_yF_q+F_u(pF_p+qF_q)-F_p(F_x+pF_u)-F_q(F_y+qF_u)=0.
$$
Since $F=0$ initially, $\boxed{F\equiv0}$ along each constructed curve. With the contact compatibility $du=p\,dx+q\,dy$ propagated by this system, an invertible characteristic chart reconstructs $p=u_x,q=u_y$ and hence a local <classical solution>.

{parent=4}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Set $F=xp+q^2/4+u-1$. Parametrize the data by $x_0=s$, $y_0=0$, $u_0=1-s^2/3$. Tangential differentiation gives $p_0=-2s/3$; substitution into $F=0$ then gives $q_0^2=4s^2$. Thus the data allow **two normal-<derivative> branches**, indexed by $\varepsilon=\pm1$, with $q_0=2\varepsilon s$. For $s>0$ each branch is <non-characteristic>; the endpoint $s=0$ is degenerate.

The <Charpit's equations> reduce to $\dot x=x$, $\dot y=q/2$, $\dot p=-2p$, $\dot q=-q$ and $\dot u=xp+q^2/2$. Integrating with these initial values gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
x&=se^t,&y&=\varepsilon s(1-e^{-t}),\\
p&=-\tfrac23se^{-2t},&q&=2\varepsilon se^{-t},\\
u&=1+\tfrac23s^2e^{-t}-s^2e^{-2t},&&0<s\leq1.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Direct substitution checks $xp+q^2/4+u-1=0$, and $t=0$ recovers the initial data. The projection onto the $(x,y)$ plane has <Jacobian determinant>
$$
J=\det\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(s,t)}=\varepsilon s(2-e^t).
$$
It first vanishes at $t=\log2$. These points form the <characteristic caustic> $y=\varepsilon x/4$, $0<x\leq2$; a single <classical solution> cannot in general be continued through the fold by treating all projected <characteristic curves> as a graph.

For a precise description put $r=\varepsilon y$. On the chart containing the initial curve and lying before the fold, $t<\log2$, elimination gives
$$
s=\frac{x+\sqrt{x^2-4xr}}2,\qquad e^{-t}=s/x.
$$
The plus square-root is forced by $s/x>1/2$, rather than a free second choice. The conditions $0<s\leq1$ give the complete unfolded chart
$$
\boxed{0<x<2,\qquad 1-\frac1x\leq\varepsilon y<\frac x4.}
$$
Interior points of the domain have strict inequality at the boundary generated by $s=1$ as well. The other boundary is the <characteristic caustic>; the original endpoint $s=0$ contributes no open chart. On the portion obtained by following characteristics forward from the data, $0\leq t<\log2$, the region is more specifically
$$
\boxed{\begin{cases}0\leq\varepsilon y<x/4,&0<x\leq1,\\1-1/x\leq\varepsilon y<x/4,&1<x<2.\end{cases}}
$$
The $s=1$ boundary is $y=\varepsilon(1-1/x)$ and meets the <characteristic caustic> at $(2,\varepsilon/2)$. Negative characteristic times give the continuation across the initial segment into the opposite half-plane. Each sign branch is a distinct local solution; the initial values alone do not select one of them.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Write the system as $U_x+A(U)U_y=0$ with $U=(u,v)^T$ and
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}v&u\\1&v\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det(A-\lambda I)=(v-\lambda)^2-u.
$$
Its two <characteristic speeds> are $\lambda_\pm=v\pm\sqrt u$, so it is **strictly hyperbolic precisely when $u>0$**. At $u=0$ they coincide and the matrix is not diagonalizable; for $u<0$ the speeds are nonreal.

For $u>0$ define $R_\pm=v\pm2\sqrt u$ and $D_\pm=\partial_x+(v\pm\sqrt u)\partial_y$. Substitution from the two differential equations gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
D_\pm R_\pm
&=v_x+(v\pm\sqrt u)v_y
\ \pm u^{-1/2}[u_x+(v\pm\sqrt u)u_y]\\
&=(-u_y\pm\sqrt u\,v_y)\pm u^{-1/2}(\pm\sqrt u\,u_y-uv_y)=0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{v\pm2\sqrt u\text{ is constant on curves }\frac{dy}{dx}=v\pm\sqrt u.}
$$
These are the two <Riemann invariants>; their constancy applies within smooth regions, not across a <shock>.

= Solution

For a smooth solution, the first equation is $u_x+(uv)_y=0$. Multiplying it by $v$ and adding $u$ times the second equation gives
$$
(uv)_x+\bigl(uv^2+\tfrac12u^2\bigr)_y=0.
$$
Thus the appropriate <shallow water equations> conservation variables and <flux> are
$$
W=\begin{pmatrix}u\\uv\end{pmatrix},\qquad H(W)=\begin{pmatrix}uv\\uv^2+u^2/2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Use this conservative extension to define a <weak solution> across $y=F(x)$. A jump contributes $-F'[W]\delta(y-F(x))$ to $W_x$ and $[H]\delta(y-F(x))$ to $H_y$. Cancellation of the singular parts gives the <Rankine-Hugoniot conditions>
$$
\boxed{F'(x)\begin{pmatrix}[u]\\{[uv]}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}[uv]\\{[uv^2+u^2/2]}\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Equivalently this follows by integrating each <conservation law> over a thin space-time control region crossing the discontinuity.

The conservative extension is essential here. Piecewise smoothness by itself does not imply any jump law. Moreover the smooth second equation can also be written $v_x+(u+v^2/2)_y=0$; imposing that different <conservation law> distributionally would give a different <shock> condition. <Weak conservation laws depend on conserved variables>. The requested jump law specifically selects <mass> $u$ and <momentum> $uv$, and this is the extension used below.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Test a single discontinuity connecting the two constant initial states. With state 1 below the discontinuity and state 2 above it, their jumps are
$$
[u]=-1,\qquad[uv]=-\sqrt3,\qquad[uv^2+u^2/2]=\tfrac12-(\tfrac32+2)=-3.
$$
The first <Rankine-Hugoniot condition> gives $F'=\sqrt3$, and the second independently gives $(-\sqrt3)F'=-3$, the same value. Since the initial jump is at the origin,
$$
\boxed{F(x)=\sqrt3\,x,\qquad
(u,v)(x,y)=\begin{cases}(2,\sqrt3/2),&y<\sqrt3 x,\\(1,0),&y>\sqrt3 x,\end{cases}\quad x>0.}
$$
Values on the <shock> itself do not affect the <weak solution>. No intermediate state or rarefaction is needed: the prescribed states already lie on the same compressive <shock> curve.

For admissibility, the upper-family <characteristic speed> is $\sqrt3/2+\sqrt2$ behind the <shock> and $1$ ahead of it. They satisfy
$$
1<\sqrt3<\sqrt3/2+\sqrt2.
$$
Both lower-family speeds, $-1$ and $\sqrt3/2-\sqrt2$, are less than the <shock> speed. These are the <Lax shock admissibility conditions> for a family-plus <shock>. This is the entropy-admissible solution for the <mass>/<momentum> conservative extension specified by the preceding jump law; imposing an additional velocity <conservation law> across the same jump would describe a different problem.

= Solution

For $\eta=x/y^\beta$ the <chain rule> gives $u_y=-\beta\eta f'/y$, $u_x=y^{-\beta}f'$ and $(uu_x)_x=y^{-2\beta}[ff''+(f')^2]$. Multiplication of the equation by $y$ gives
$$
-\beta\eta f'=y^{1-2\beta}[ff''+(f')^2]+\tfrac12f.
$$
A nontrivial scale-invariant balance therefore selects $\boxed{\beta=1/2}$ and yields
$$
ff''+(f')^2+\tfrac12\eta f'+\tfrac12f=0.
$$
Its left side is the <derivative> of $ff'+\eta f/2$. The <boundary conditions> give $f(0)=1$ and $f'(0)=0$, so the integration constant is zero:
$$
f(f'+\eta/2)=0.
$$
On the positive branch before the first zero, divide by $f$ and integrate, obtaining $f=1-\eta^2/4$. Its first positive zero is $\eta_F=2$. Hence
$$
\boxed{u(x,y)=1-\frac{x^2}{4y}\quad(0\leq x\leq2\sqrt y),\qquad x_F(y)=2\sqrt y.}
$$
A direct check gives $u_y=x^2/(4y^2)$ and $(uu_x)_x+u/(2y)=x^2/(4y^2)$. The zero extension past this front is also a natural weak compact-support continuation: $u$ is continuous and the degenerate diffusive <flux> $uu_x$ tends to zero at the front. This is a <source-driven porous-medium similarity front>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Assume a <classical solution> $u\in C^2(D)\cap C(\overline D)$, as needed to interpret the boundary values. At an interior local minimum its <Hessian> is <positive semidefinite>, hence $\Delta u\geq0$. But the equation gives $\Delta u=-e^{-x^2}<0$, a contradiction. Since $D$ is bounded and $u=0$ on its boundary, a nonpositive interior value would either furnish an interior negative minimum or itself be a zero minimum. Thus $\boxed{u>0\text{ in }D}$.

For the upper bound use the barrier $w(x,y)=(1-x^2-y^2)/4$, which satisfies $-\Delta w=1$. Set $z=w-u$. On the boundary $z=w\geq0$, because the domain lies inside the unit disk, while
$$
-\Delta z=1-e^{-x^2}\geq0.
$$
The <quadratic perturbation proof of the minimum principle> gives $z\geq0$. The <strong minimum principle for elliptic operators> makes this strict in the connected interior unless $z$ is constant. Constancy is impossible since $1-e^{-x^2}$ is not identically zero on any open domain. Hence
$$
\boxed{0<u(x,y)<\tfrac14(1-x^2-y^2)\quad\text{throughout }D.}
$$
This last strong-principle step handles points with $x=0$, where the comparison forcing itself vanishes. Simple connectedness is not needed for the comparison argument.

With source point $\xi\in D$ and field point $X=(x,y)$, the <Dirichlet Green function> for the operator $-\Delta$ satisfies
$$
\boxed{-\Delta_XG(X,\xi)=\delta_\xi(X)\text{ in }D,\qquad G(X,\xi)=0\text{ on }\partial D.}
$$
Classically it is harmonic off $\xi$, with the two-dimensional normalization $G(X,\xi)=-(2\pi)^{-1}\log|X-\xi|+H(X,\xi)$, where $H$ is regular near the source. Equivalently $-\lim_{r\downarrow0}\int_{|X-\xi|=r}\partial_rG\,ds=1$. This sign convention gives the representation $u(X)=\int_DG(X,\xi)e^{-\xi_1^2}\,d\xi$.
