= Solution
{parent=a}

Define the auxiliary variable $y=\epsilon\dot x-x+x^3/3$. Differentiating gives $\dot y=\epsilon\ddot x+(x^2-1)\dot x=-x$, while rearrangement gives $\epsilon\dot x=x-x^3/3+y$. Conversely eliminating $y$ recovers the second-order equation, so this <phase plane> system is equivalent. At an <equilibrium> $\dot y=0$ forces $x=0$, and the first equation then forces $y=0$. Its <Jacobian matrix> is
$$
J=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon^{-1}&\epsilon^{-1}\\-1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det(\lambda I-J)=\lambda^2-\epsilon^{-1}\lambda+\epsilon^{-1}.
$$
Thus $\epsilon\lambda^2-\lambda+1=0$. Expanding $\sqrt{1-4\epsilon}=1-2\epsilon-2\epsilon^2-4\epsilon^3+O(\epsilon^4)$ gives
$$
\boxed{\lambda_{\rm fast}=\epsilon^{-1}-1+O(\epsilon),\qquad\lambda_{\rm slow}=1+\epsilon+O(\epsilon^2).}
$$
For sufficiently small positive $\epsilon$ both are positive: the <equilibrium> is an <unstable node>. For negative $\epsilon$ the fast root is negative and the slow root positive, so it becomes a <saddle equilibrium>.

The <nullcline> is $\boxed{y=h(x)=x^3/3-x}$. Write $F=x-x^3/3+y$. Away from it at an order-one distance, $F$ is order one on the indicated finite phase-space scales. Thus $\dot x=O(\epsilon^{-1})$ while $\dot y=-x=O(1)$: the <trajectory> changes $x$ on the <fast time in a singular perturbation> $t/\epsilon$, with only an $O(\epsilon)$ change in $y$. Equivalently $dy/dx=-\epsilon x/F=O(\epsilon)$, which explains the nearly horizontal motion.

For positive $\epsilon$, $F_x=1-x^2$ makes the branches $|x|>1$ fast attracting and the middle branch $|x|<1$ fast repelling. The arrows point right where $y>h(x)$ and left where $y<h(x)$, carrying a generic off-<nullcline> point towards an attracting branch. The reduced description changes near a fold, where $F_x=0$; the rapid/slow distinction there needs a separate transition layer.

On the attracting right branch, $x>1$ and $\dot y=-x<0$, so the motion goes from $(2,2/3)$ towards the fold $(1,-2/3)$. At essentially fixed $y=-2/3$ it jumps to the other attracting root $x=-2$. The left branch then carries it to $(-1,2/3)$, where it jumps right to $x=2$. This gives the clockwise limiting <relaxation oscillation> shown in the preceding figure.

Along a slow branch $y=h(x)$, differentiation gives $(x^2-1)\dot x=-x$. The right-branch time is
$$
T_R=\int_2^1\frac{1-x^2}{x}\,dx=\left[\log x-\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_2^1=\frac32-\log2.
$$
Reflection symmetry gives the same time on the left. The fast jumps take a time tending to zero as $\epsilon\to0^+$, as do the fold-transition corrections at leading order. Hence
$$
\boxed{T\sim2T_R=3-2\log2.}
$$
This is a leading asymptotic period, not an exact finite-$\epsilon$ equality.

For negative $\epsilon$ the fast arrows reverse: the outer branches become repelling and the middle branch attracting. On the middle branch the slow equation is $\dot x=x/(1-x^2)$, so the slow motion goes away from the <saddle equilibrium> at zero towards a fold.

At $(3,1)$, $F=3-9+1=-5$, so $\dot x>0$: the <trajectory> immediately escapes to the right. At $(1,-2)$, $F=1-1/3-2=-4/3$, so it also moves rapidly right and escapes. In each case the large-$x$ balance is $\dot x\sim x^3/(3|\epsilon|)$, with $y$ decreasing; it is not a bounded relaxation cycle.

At $(-1,-1/3)$, $F=-1$, so the initial fast motion is rightward. At nearly fixed $y=-1/3$ it approaches the attracting middle root of $x-x^3/3=1/3$, approximately $x=0.347$. It then moves slowly down/right along the middle branch towards $(1,-2/3)$, leaves that fold to the right, and eventually escapes. These are the small-negative-$\epsilon$ limiting descriptions; the fold locations and fast landing point receive small corrections. The right panel of the figure distinguishes this slow excursion from the two immediate escapes.

= Solution
{parent=A}

Put $y=e^xw$. Then $y''-2y'+y=e^xw''$ and the endpoint conditions become $w(0)=w(\pi)=0$. The transformed <eigenvalue> equation is $-w''=\lambda w$, so
$$
\boxed{\lambda_k=k^2,\qquad y_k(x)=e^x\sin(kx),\quad k=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
Zero and negative $\lambda$ yield no nonzero solution with both endpoint values zero.

For the usual unweighted $L^2(0,\pi)$ <inner product>, the operator $D^2-2D+1$ is **not <self-adjoint>**: its <formal adjoint> is $D^2+2D+1$. The mismatch is already visible on compactly supported test functions, so endpoint conditions cannot remove it. With the different weight $e^{-2x}$ one does obtain a symmetric <Sturm-Liouville form>, since $e^{-2x}(y''-2y'+y)=(e^{-2x}y')'+e^{-2x}y$. Thus the inner-product convention matters; the real spectrum alone does not prove self-adjointness in the unweighted space.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Use the complex $L^2$ <inner product> $\langle f,g\rangle=\int_0^\pi\overline f g$, linear in its second argument. Exchanging the order of integration gives
$$
\langle f,Lg\rangle=\int_0^\pi\left[\int_0^\pi\overline{f(x)}k(x,t)\,dx\right]g(t)\,dt.
$$
Since the <kernel> is real, the <adjoint operator> is therefore
$$
\boxed{L^*f(t)=\int_0^\pi k(x,t)f(x)\,dx,}
$$
or, renaming variables, $L^*f(x)=\int_0^\pi k(t,x)f(t)\,dt$. <Continuity> of the <kernel> on the compact integration square justifies these integrals and the exchange.

{parent=a}

= Solution
{parent=i}

Symmetry $k(x,t)=k(t,x)$ makes the adjoint <kernel> equal to the original <kernel>, so $L^*=L$. For a nonzero <eigenfunction> $Ly=\lambda y$,
$$
\lambda\|y\|^2=\langle y,Ly\rangle=\langle Ly,y\rangle=\overline\lambda\|y\|^2.
$$
Thus $\boxed{\lambda\in\mathbb R}$. The argument includes the zero <eigenvalue> and does not require normalizing the <eigenfunction>.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Antisymmetry $k(x,t)=-k(t,x)$ instead gives $L^*=-L$, so $L$ is <skew-adjoint>. Its quadratic form satisfies $\langle y,Ly\rangle=-\overline{\langle y,Ly\rangle}$. Hence an eigenpair satisfies
$$
\lambda\|y\|^2=-\overline\lambda\|y\|^2,\qquad\boxed{\operatorname{Re}\lambda=0.}
$$
Nonzero <eigenvalues> are purely imaginary in the complexified function space; zero is allowed as well. On a strictly real vector space this means there are no nonzero real <eigenvalues>.

Write $c(x)=\cos2x$. The separated <kernel> gives
$$
Ly=c(x)\int_0^\pi y(t)\,dt+\int_0^\pi c(t)y(t)\,dt.
$$
Thus its <image> is $E=\operatorname{span}\{1,c\}$. Since $L1=\pi c$ and $Lc=\pi/2$, its matrix on this basis is $\begin{pmatrix}0&\pi/2\\\pi&0\end{pmatrix}$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\lambda_\pm=\pm\frac\pi{\sqrt2},\qquad y_\pm(x)=1\pm\sqrt2\cos2x.}
$$
Both <eigenspaces> have dimension one, and there are no further nonzero <eigenvalues> because every such <eigenfunction> belongs to the <image>. The zero <eigenspace> is $E^\perp$, which is infinite-dimensional. One explicit nonzero example is $\boxed{y(x)=\sin2x}$, <orthogonal> to both $1$ and $\cos2x$.

The <image> of $L$ consists only of $a+b\cos2x$. If $\sin x$ were such a function, its endpoint values would give $a+b=0$ and its midpoint value $a-b=1$, forcing $a=1/2$, $b=-1/2$. Thus the forcing is outside the <image> and **no solution exists**.

Here $\sin^2x=(1-\cos2x)/2$ belongs to the <image>. Matching the two coefficients using $L(A+B\cos2x)=B\pi/2+A\pi\cos2x$ gives $A=-1/(2\pi)$, $B=1/\pi$. Consequently

= Solution
{parent=d}

The <eigenvalues> of $L$ are $\pi/\sqrt2$, $-\pi/\sqrt2$ and zero, none equal to one. On $E=\operatorname{span}\{1,\cos2x\}$ the map $L-I$ is an invertible two-dimensional operator; on $E^\perp$ it is $-I$. Hence it is invertible on all of $L^2(0,\pi)$. Thus $(L-I)y=\sin x$ has **exactly one solution**. This is the <finite-rank Fredholm alternative>; no range obstruction survives the nonresonant identity term.

= Solution
{parent=a}

The quantity inside a fixed interval changes by influx at its left endpoint minus outflux at its right endpoint:
$$
\frac d{dt}\int_a^b\rho\,dx=q(\rho(a,t))-q(\rho(b,t)).
$$
In a smooth region the <fundamental theorem of calculus> turns this into $\int_a^b[\rho_t+\partial_xq(\rho)]\,dx=0$. Since the interval is arbitrary, the local <conservation law> is $\boxed{\rho_t+\partial_xq(\rho)=0}$.

For a jump at $x=S(t)$, write the two smooth pieces using a <step function>. The singular part of $\rho_t$ is $-S'[\rho]\delta(x-S)$ and that of $\partial_xq$ is $[q]\delta(x-S)$. Cancellation gives the <Rankine-Hugoniot condition>
$$
\boxed{S'[\rho]=[q],\qquad S'=[q]/[\rho]\quad\text{when }[\rho]\ne0.}
$$
The same identity follows by applying conservation to a shrinking moving control interval across the <shock>.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

In smooth regions the characteristic equations are $\dot x=q'(\rho)=\rho^3$ and $\dot\rho=0$. If $\xi$ labels the initial point, their parametrization is
$$
x=\xi+\rho_0(\xi)^3t,\qquad\rho=\rho_0(\xi).
$$
For the two constant states, characteristics from the left have speed one and those from the right speed zero; they would overlap. The <entropy solution> replaces the overlap by a compressive <shock>. Its speed is
$$
c=\frac{q(1)-q(0)}{1-0}=\frac14.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\rho(x,t)=\begin{cases}1,&x<t/4,\\0,&x>t/4,\end{cases}\qquad S(t)=t/4.}
$$
The left parametrization $x=\xi+t$ is retained only for $x<t/4$, and the right parametrization $x=\xi$ only for $x>t/4$. The <characteristic speeds> obey $0<c<1$, so characteristics enter the <shock> from both sides.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Before intersection, the characteristic map is $x=\xi+\rho_0(\xi)^3t$, with derivative $1+3\rho_0^2\rho_0't$. Because the initial profile decreases, higher-<density> characteristics behind move faster than lower-<density> characteristics ahead. Compression therefore forms <shocks>, which absorb more characteristics. The far states remain one and zero, so a <shock> joining their limiting traces has speed tending to $[q]/[\rho]=1/4$.

The precise large-scale interpretation uses $\zeta=x/t$. The rescaled initial transition tends to a step, and entropy comparison for the convex <flux> gives
$$
\boxed{\rho(\zeta t,t)\longrightarrow1\text{ for }\zeta<1/4,\qquad \rho(\zeta t,t)\longrightarrow0\text{ for }\zeta>1/4.}
$$
One way to see the comparison is to choose distant initial points beyond which the profile is within $\delta$ of each far state. Step profiles with those nearby states bracket it; their <shock> speeds tend to $1/4$ as $\delta\downarrow0$, while their fixed initial locations disappear after division by $t$.

A fixed translation of the <shock> can persist. If the deviation from a reference step is integrable, its signed <mass> fixes a phase shift. Without such a tail assumption one should assert convergence on the $x/t$ scale and $S(t)/t\to1/4$, rather than claim that the exact line $x=t/4$ is the unshifted asymptote for every smooth initial transition.

= Solution
{parent=d}

<Mass> preservation of a nonnegative finite-<mass> profile requires $\alpha+\beta=0$. Balancing $\rho_t$ and $\partial_x(\rho^4/4)$ requires $\alpha-1=4\alpha-\beta$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\alpha=-\tfrac14,\qquad\beta=\tfrac14.}
$$
The similarity ODE is
$$
-\tfrac14(f+\eta f')+f^3f'=0,\qquad (f^4-\eta f)'=0.
$$
For a source-type profile adjoining vacuum, the integration constant is zero. The positive branch is $f=\eta^{1/3}$ for positive $\eta$, terminated by a <shock> into zero. Normalize the <mass>:
$$
1=\int_0^{\eta_F}\eta^{1/3}\,d\eta=\frac34\eta_F^{4/3},\qquad \eta_F=(4/3)^{3/4}.
$$
Thus a unit-<mass> <similarity solution> is
$$
\boxed{\rho(x,t)=t^{-1/4}\begin{cases}(x/t^{1/4})^{1/3},&0<x<\eta_Ft^{1/4},\\0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}}
$$
At the moving right edge, $S'=\eta_Ft^{-3/4}/4$ equals $\rho_L^3/4$, so the <Rankine-Hugoniot condition> is satisfied. The <characteristic speeds> satisfy $0<S'<\rho_L^3$, making it an entropy <shock>. The left edge meets vacuum continuously. As $t\downarrow0$ the <mass> concentrates at the origin; this is a source solution, not a claim that every finite-<mass> initial profile equals it at finite time.

For the general equation the <Charpit's equations> are
$$
\dot x=F_p,\quad\dot y=F_q,\quad\dot u=pF_p+qF_q,\quad\dot p=-F_x-pF_u,\quad\dot q=-F_y-qF_u.
$$
At parameter time zero set $(x,y,u)=(x_0,y_0,u_0)$ and choose $p_0,q_0$ to satisfy $F(x_0,y_0,u_0,p_0,q_0)=0$ and $p_0x_0'+q_0y_0'=u_0'$. The <chain rule> gives
$$
\frac{dF}{dt}=F_xF_p+F_yF_q+F_u(pF_p+qF_q)-F_p(F_x+pF_u)-F_q(F_y+qF_u)=0,
$$
so $F$ remains zero. The <non-characteristic> condition is $x_0'F_q-y_0'F_p\ne0$, ensuring a local graph.

For the particular equation put $F=p^3+(y-1)q+u-2$ and label the initial curve by $y_0=1+s$, $0<s\leq1$, $x_0=1$, $u_0=2+2s^3$. Compatibility gives $q_0=6s^2$ and $p_0^3=-8s^3$, hence the unique real choice $p_0=-2s$. Integrating the characteristic equations gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
x&=1+6s^2(1-e^{-2t}),&y&=1+se^t,\\
p&=-2se^{-t},&q&=6s^2e^{-2t},\\
u&=2+8s^3e^{-3t}-6s^3e^{-t},&&0<s\leq1.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The projection determinant is $J=12s^2e^t(1-2e^{-2t})$. Initially $J=-12s^2\ne0$; its first zero is $t=\tfrac12\log2$. At that fold,
$$
x=1+3s^2,\qquad y=1+\sqrt2s,\qquad\boxed{x=1+\tfrac32(y-1)^2.}
$$
To describe the entire chart attached to the data before the fold, put $Y=y-1>0$ and $w=e^{-2t}>1/2$. Elimination gives $x-1=6Y^2w(1-w)$ and
$$
w=\frac{1+\sqrt{1-2(x-1)/(3Y^2)}}2,\qquad s=Y\sqrt w.
$$
The initial-endpoint constraint $s\leq1$ therefore gives the uniquely determined chart
$$
\boxed{0<Y<\sqrt2,\qquad 7-\frac6{Y^2}\leq x<1+\frac32Y^2.}
$$
The boundary $s=1$ is included as inherited endpoint data; open-domain statements use strict inequality there. The fold is excluded. For forward characteristic times only, $0\leq t<\tfrac12\log2$, impose additionally $x\geq1$. The source excludes $s=0$, so $(1,1)$ is not a <non-characteristic> initial point. The sheet beyond the fold does not extend this initial graph uniquely.

= Solution

The coefficient matrix of $(u_y,v_y)^T$ is $A=\begin{pmatrix}v&u\\u^{-3}&v\end{pmatrix}$. Its distinct <eigenvalues> are $\lambda_\pm=v\pm1/u$ for every $u>0$, so the system is strictly hyperbolic. Define $D_\pm=\partial_x+(v\pm1/u)\partial_y$. Then
$$
D_+\left(v-\frac1u\right)=v_x+(v+1/u)v_y+u^{-2}[u_x+(v+1/u)u_y]=0:
$$
the derivative terms cancel, as do the sources $u-u$. Hence $R=v-1/u$ is constant on the plus-family characteristics.

The data give $R=0$, so throughout their <domain of dependence> $v=1/u$. The minus-family speed is then $v-1/u=0$: **the other characteristics are the horizontal lines $y=a$**. Substitution into the second PDE, or its minus-characteristic compatibility relation, gives $v_x=1/v$. Integrate at fixed $y$ using $v(0,y)=y$ and retain the positive branch:
$$
\boxed{v(x,y)=\sqrt{y^2+2x},\qquad u(x,y)=\frac1{\sqrt{y^2+2x}}.}
$$
Indeed $u_x=-u^3$, $u_y=-yu^3$, $v_y=yu$, so $vu_y+uv_y=0$; similarly $u^{-3}u_y+vv_y=-y+y=0$.

For $x\geq0$ the lower edge of the region determined solely by the given data is the plus characteristic from $(0,1)$. Write it $y=b(x)$, where the exact boundary characterization is
$$
\boxed{b'(x)=2\sqrt{b(x)^2+2x},\qquad b(0)=1.}
$$
The determined region is $\boxed{x\geq0,\ y\geq b(x)}$, with strict inequality for its interior. Both backward characteristic families there reach the supplied initial half-line. Below this boundary a backward plus characteristic would require initial data below $y=1$, which were not supplied; extending the displayed formulas there gives a possible continuation but is not forced by the data. An elementary expression for $b$ is unnecessary: its initial-value ODE defines it uniquely and the requested other family already has the explicit form $y=a$. The sketch integrates only this boundary for illustration.

At such an interior backward-in-time maximum, spatial differentiation gives $u_x=0$ and $u_{xx}\leq0$. Comparison with earlier times at the same spatial point gives the one-sided time derivative $u_y\geq0$; differentiability makes this the usual derivative. Consequently $xu_{xx}-u_y\leq0$, contradicting the strictly positive source $xy^{-2}e^{-x/y}$. Therefore **no such maximum can occur**. The temporal one-sided argument matters because the condition concerns only the past of the candidate point.

= Solution
{parent=b}

**The stated pointwise boundary limits do not imply uniqueness.** A counterexample to homogeneous uniqueness is
$$
\boxed{w(x,y)=\frac{x}{y^2}e^{-x/y}\ne0.}
$$
It is smooth for $x,y>0$. Writing $\eta=x/y$, differentiation gives
$$
xw_{xx}=\frac{\eta(\eta-2)}{y^2}e^{-\eta}=w_y.
$$
For fixed positive $y$, $w\to0$ as $x\to0$ and as $x\to\infty$; for fixed positive $x$, it also tends to zero as $y\downarrow0$. Thus if $u_0$ is one solution, $u_0+Cw$ satisfies the same equation and every printed boundary limit for any constant $C$. Part (d) supplies such a particular solution, so this is a genuine family of distinct solutions, not merely a formal obstruction.

This is <corner-source nonuniqueness for a degenerate parabolic problem>. The peak $w(y,y)=1/(ey)$ diverges towards the missing corner $(0,0)$; pointwise initial limits at each fixed $x>0$ do not exclude it. The preceding maximum argument fails to force uniqueness because a supremum over an entire past strip need not be attained or finite.

A customary bounded-solution condition restores the intended result. If the difference $z$ of two solutions is bounded on every finite time strip, subtract $\delta(1+x+y)$. Its operator $x\partial_x^2-\partial_y$ equals $\delta>0$, excluding a positive past maximum; the linear barrier controls spatial infinity, and the homogeneous initial/boundary values control the <parabolic boundary>. Letting $\delta\downarrow0$ gives $z\leq0$; applying the same argument to $-z$ gives $z=0$. This proves uniqueness in that additional bounded class, which the printed pointwise limits alone do not specify.

Choose the positive causal sign convention $\mathcal L=\partial_y-x\partial_x^2$, so the forced equation is $\mathcal Lu=-xy^{-2}e^{-x/y}$. With source $(\xi,\tau)$, a forward <Green's function> satisfies
$$
\boxed{(\partial_y-x\partial_x^2)G(x,y;\xi,\tau)=\delta(x-\xi)\delta(y-\tau),\qquad G=0\text{ for }y<\tau.}
$$
It vanishes at $x=0$, decays at spatial infinity in the chosen bounded evolution class, and has the temporal jump $G(x,\tau^+;\xi,\tau)=\delta(x-\xi)$. Away from the source it solves the homogeneous equation. Equivalently one may reverse both the Green-function operator and its source sign to use the original printed operator.

To select the physical zero-initial-data solution one also excludes extra corner-source terms of the kind found in (b). With this causal bounded convention the solution representation is
$$
u(x,y)=-\int_0^y\int_0^\infty G(x,y;\xi,\tau)\frac\xi{\tau^2}e^{-\xi/\tau}\,d\xi\,d\tau.
$$
A mere pointwise statement of the three boundary limits does not by itself provide that uniqueness convention.

= Solution

The forcing contains $x/y$, so matching it with a single similarity variable requires $\beta=1$. For $u=y^\alpha f(\eta)$, $\eta=x/y$, substitution gives
$$
xu_{xx}-u_y=y^{\alpha-1}[\eta f''+\eta f'-\alpha f].
$$
Matching the source $y^{-1}\eta e^{-\eta}$ selects $\boxed{\alpha=0,\ \beta=1}$ and gives $f''+f'=e^{-\eta}$. Multiplying the first-order equation for $f'$ by $e^\eta$ yields $f'=(\eta+C)e^{-\eta}$, so $f=-(\eta+1+C)e^{-\eta}+D$. The limits at zero and infinity give $D=0$, $C=-1$. Hence
$$
\boxed{u_0(x,y)=-\frac xy e^{-x/y}.}
$$
For fixed positive $x$, the initial limit is also zero because the exponential dominates $x/y$. The function $-\eta e^{-\eta}$ decreases to $-1/e$ at $\eta=1$ and increases thereafter. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\min u_0=-1/e,\qquad\text{attained at every }x=y>0.}
$$
The axis limits are zero, but there is no joint limit at $(0,0)$: approaching along $x=y$ gives $-1/e$. This does not violate any of the printed fixed-variable limits. It is this bounded <similarity solution> whose minimum was calculated; the unbounded corner-source additions in (b) need not have the same minimum.
