= Solution
{parent=1}

Use <dimensionless variables> $X=X_*x$, $Y=Y_*y$, $Z=Z_*z$, $t=T\tau$, with
$$
\boxed{Y_*=\frac1b,\quad Z_*=\frac g{mb},\quad X_*=\frac{lg}{rmb},\quad T=\frac{lg}{rmk}.}
$$
Substitution gives the requested system, with dots now meaning differentiation with respect to $\tau$, and
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac{ab}k,\qquad\epsilon=\frac{rm^2k}{l^2g^2},\qquad\delta=\frac{rk}{lg},\qquad\gamma=\frac{3c}{b^2}.}
$$
In particular the same scale for $Y$ makes the first two coefficients of the cubic equal in magnitude.

Its <derivative> is $f'(y)=1-2y+\gamma y^2$. For $0<\gamma<1$ its two simple zeros are
$$
\boxed{y_\pm=\frac{1\pm\sqrt{1-\gamma}}\gamma.}
$$
The cubic increases below $y_-$, decreases between the two <fold points of a critical manifold>, and increases above $y_+$. It has a <local maximum> at $y_-$ and a local minimum at $y_+$.

After a rapidly decaying <transient response>, $z\simeq f(y)$ when its relaxation is faster than the other variables. The reduced <autonomous system> is
$$
\boxed{\dot x=\alpha-y,\qquad\dot y=\frac{x-f(y)}\epsilon.}
$$
For $\epsilon\ll1$, the fast $y$ dynamics at fixed $x$ attracts to the branches where $f'(y)>0$ and repels from the middle branch. On the low branch $y<y_-<\alpha$, $x$ increases slowly. At its <fold point of a critical manifold> $x=f(y_-)$, the attracting low branch disappears and $y$ jumps upward, with nearly constant $x$, to the high branch. On that branch $y>y_+>\alpha$, $x$ decreases slowly. At $x=f(y_+)$ it jumps back to the low branch. These slow arcs and fast jumps form the singular relaxation cycle. For small positive $\epsilon$, and parameters bounded away from the <fold point of a critical manifold>-endpoint cases, the nearby attracting periodic motion has the same pattern. The <equilibrium> $(f(\alpha),\alpha)$ is on the repelling branch: its <Jacobian matrix> has <determinant> $1/\epsilon>0$ and <trace> $-f'(\alpha)/\epsilon>0$.

The limiting landing values are $3/\gamma-2y_-$ after the upward jump and $3/\gamma-2y_+$ after the downward jump, obtained from the third root of $f(y)=f(y_\pm)$. Thus the two vertical segments in the <phase plane> plot really connect roots of the same fast equation.

\Image[2007-relaxation-cycle.png]
{title=Cubic folds and the singular relaxation cycle: slow drift on attracting branches and fast jumps at nearly fixed x}

If $\gamma>1$, $f'$ is strictly positive everywhere, so the cubic is <monotone> and has no <fold points of a critical manifold>. The reduced <equilibrium> is asymptotically stable. In fact
$$
V=\tfrac12[x-f(\alpha)]^2+\tfrac\epsilon2(y-\alpha)^2,\qquad
\dot V=-(y-\alpha)[f(y)-f(\alpha)]\leq0.
$$
The only invariant trajectory in the zero-<derivative> set is the <equilibrium>, so bounded reduced solutions converge to it; <relaxation oscillations> disappear.

The stronger condition $\boxed{\delta\ll\epsilon}$ matters during a fast jump: $y$ then varies on the $\epsilon$ time scale, not on the slow unit scale. Only if $z$ adjusts faster still may one keep $z\simeq f(y)$ through that jump. Merely imposing $\delta\ll1$ would not justify this reduction on all pieces of the cycle. This is a <three-time-scale cubic relaxation oscillator>.

= Solution
{parent=2}

For zero <damping> the conserved <mechanical energy> is
$$
\boxed{H=\tfrac12\dot x^2+V(x)=\tfrac12v^2,\qquad V(x)=\tfrac12x^2-\tfrac\gamma4x^4.}
$$
For $\gamma<0$, the potential grows to infinity in both directions, so every nonstationary finite-energy trajectory is trapped between two <turning points> and is periodic; the zero-energy solution is stationary. The case $\gamma=0$ is the <harmonic oscillator>. The negative-$\gamma$ comparison extends the parameter range initially stated in the source.

For $\gamma>0$, the origin lies in a local potential well, with barriers at $x=\pm\gamma^{-1/2}$ of height $1/(4\gamma)$. A trajectory launched from the origin oscillates precisely when its initial energy is below that barrier:
$$
\boxed{|v|<\frac1{\sqrt{2\gamma}}.}
$$
At equality the trajectory approaches a barrier <equilibrium> asymptotically, rather than being periodic; for example the positive branch is $x=\gamma^{-1/2}\tanh(t/\sqrt2)$. Above the barrier the trajectory escapes the well. The source's inequality without the absolute value tacitly takes $v\geq0$; for signed initial velocity the energy criterion requires $|v|$.

Next set $\beta=\gamma=0$ and assume $\alpha>0$. The energy $H=(\dot x^2+x^2)/2$ satisfies $\dot H=-\alpha x^2\dot x^2\leq0$. This bounds the complete trajectory. The zero-dissipation set is the union of $x=0$ and $\dot x=0$, and its only invariant trajectory is the origin: a nonzero point on either axis immediately leaves it under the differential equation. The <LaSalle invariance principle> therefore gives $\boxed{x(t)\to0,\quad\dot x(t)\to0}$. If $\alpha=0$ as well, <damping> is absent and that conclusion fails for nonzero $v$; the positive order-one <damping> assumption is essential.

For the weakly nonlinear periodic motion, write $\gamma=\alpha\kappa$, set $\tau=\omega t$, $\omega=1+\alpha\omega_1+\cdots$, and expand $x=A\cos\tau+\alpha x_1+\cdots$. The first correction obeys
$$
x_1''+x_1=A(A^2/4-\beta)\sin\tau+
(2\omega_1A+3\kappa A^3/4)\cos\tau
+\frac{A^3}4\sin3\tau+\frac{\kappa A^3}4\cos3\tau.
$$
Resonant forcing at the <fundamental frequency> would generate <secular terms>. Eliminating both coefficients yields
$$
\boxed{A=2\sqrt\beta,\qquad\omega_1=-\frac{3\kappa A^2}{8},\qquad
\omega=1-\frac32\gamma\beta+O(\alpha^2).}
$$
Consequently the maximum displacement is $\boxed{2\sqrt\beta+O(\alpha)}$ for fixed positive $\beta$. A possible third-harmonic correction is $x_1=-A^3\sin3\tau/32-\kappa A^3\cos3\tau/32$, up to homogeneous <amplitude> and <phase> choices. The leading <amplitude> also balances averaged <damping>: its slow evolution is $\dot A=\alpha A(\beta/2-A^2/8)$, whose nonzero fixed point is attracting. The case $\beta=0$ is a degenerate zero-<amplitude> limit, rather than a nonzero <limit cycle>.

= Solution

Multiplication by an <integrating factor> gives
$$
\boxed{p(x)=\exp\!\left(\int^xa(s)\,ds\right),\quad r=pc,\quad q=-pb,}
$$
since $p'=ap$ and hence $(pw')'=p(w''+aw')$.

For the singular-endpoint equation, take $p=x^{-1/2}$, $r=x^{1/2}/2$, $q=0$. The <indicial equation> is $\alpha(2\alpha-3)=0$. The constant branch violates the zero endpoint value unless its coefficient vanishes; the nontrivial admissible branch therefore has $\boxed{\alpha=3/2}$. More precisely, an <eigenfunction> has $w=Cx^{3/2}+O(x^{9/2})$ and $w'=(3C/2)x^{1/2}+O(x^{7/2})$ near zero.

Integrate the <self-adjoint> equation after multiplying by $w$, initially on $[a,1]$ with $a>0$:
$$
[wx^{-1/2}w']_a^1-\int_a^1x^{-1/2}(w')^2\,dx
+\frac\lambda2\int_a^1x^{1/2}w^2\,dx=0.
$$
At zero the boundary expression is $O(x^{3/2})$, the first <integrand> is $O(x^{1/2})$, and the second is $O(x^{7/2})$. Both <integrals> converge and the <boundary term> vanishes. Division by the positive <weighted norm> gives $\boxed{\lambda=I(w)}$.

The change of coordinate $z=x^{3/2}$, $w(x)=W(z)$ gives exactly
$$
W''+\frac{2\lambda}{9}W=0,\qquad W(0)=W(1)=0.
$$
Thus $\lambda_n=9n^2\pi^2/2$ and $w_n=\sin(n\pi x^{3/2})$. To prove a minimum over trial functions, rather than just identify the least <eigenvalue>, transform the quotient itself:
$$
I(w)=\frac92\frac{\int_0^1(W')^2\,dz}{\int_0^1W^2\,dz}\geq\boxed{\frac{9\pi^2}2}.
$$
The Dirichlet <Poincaré inequality> gives the last step, with equality precisely for a nonzero multiple of $W=\sin\pi z$. The trial class is the nonzero finite-energy class with zero <Sobolev traces>; endpoint values alone without an integrability requirement do not define a finite quotient. This is the <power-coordinate reduction of a singular Sturm-Liouville quotient>.

= Solution
{parent=4}

With <diffusion> absent, the <characteristic equations for a first-order quasilinear equation> are $du/dt=0$, $dx/dt=q'(u)$. A <characteristic curve> labelled by its starting point $s$ is
$$
\boxed{u=u_0(s),\quad x=s+tq'[u_0(s)],\qquad
u(x,t)=u_0\!\left(x-tq'[u(x,t)]\right).}
$$
This determines a smooth single-valued solution while $s\mapsto x$ is one-to-one. Its <derivative> is
$$
u_x=\frac{u_0'(s)}{1+tq''[u_0(s)]u_0'(s)}.
$$
All <characteristic curves> move right because $q'>0$. Because $q''<0$, increasing portions of the initial profile have slower <characteristic curves> ahead than behind, so they steepen. The first positive <gradient catastrophe> occurs at
$$
\boxed{t_* =\inf_{s:u_0'(s)>0}\frac{-1}{u_0'(s)q''[u_0(s)]}.}
$$
This is a minimum when attained. The numerator at such a point is positive and the denominator decreases to zero from above, so $u_x\to+\infty$.

Resolve the thin transition with $X=(x-x_s(t))/\epsilon$ and $u\simeq U(X)$. The leading balance is $(-c+q'(U))U'=(D(U)U')'$, where $c=\dot x_s$. Integrating once and matching to the state behind gives
$$
\boxed{D(U)U'=F(U)=q(U)-q(u_-)-c(U-u_-).}
$$
Matching the state ahead requires $F(u_+)=0$ and gives the Rankine-Hugoniot speed
$$
\boxed{c=\frac{q(u_+)-q(u_-)}{u_+-u_-}.}
$$
The strictly <concave> graph of $F$ has zeros at the two states and lies above its chord, hence is strictly positive between them. Since $D>0$, $U'>0$ through the layer. A heteroclinic profile increasing with $X$ can therefore connect the rear state at $X=-\infty$ to the front state at $X=+\infty$ only when $\boxed{u_-<u_+}$. The <concavity> also gives $q'(u_-)>c>q'(u_+)$: <characteristic curves> enter the <viscous shock> from both sides. Reversing the states would demand a decreasing profile, contradicting the sign of $F/D$.

= Solution
{parent=5}

Write the parameter along a <characteristic curve> as $r$. <Charpit's equations> are
$$
\boxed{\dot x=F_p,\quad\dot y=F_q,\quad\dot u=pF_p+qF_q,\quad
\dot p=-F_x-pF_u,\quad\dot q=-F_y-qF_u.}
$$
At $r=0$, prescribe $x=x_0(s)$, $y=y_0(s)$, $u=u_0(s)$ and choose $p_0,q_0$ satisfying both $F(x_0,y_0,u_0,p_0,q_0)=0$ and the compatibility relation $p_0x_0'+q_0y_0'=u_0'$. Direct differentiation gives
$$
\frac{dF}{dr}=F_xF_p+F_yF_q+F_u(pF_p+qF_q)
-F_p(F_x+pF_u)-F_q(F_y+qF_u)=0.
$$
Thus the initial constraint propagates. A chosen initial-gradient branch is locally unique when
$$
\boxed{x_0'F_q-y_0'F_p\ne0.}
$$
This is simultaneously the implicit-function <determinant> for the initial gradients and the <projection map> <determinant> for the surface generated by the <method of characteristics>. Uniqueness continues while that <projection map> is invertible, the flow remains regular, and distinct <characteristic curves> do not give competing values at the same point. Multiple initial-gradient branches, <characteristic curve> initial points or a <caustic> require separate analysis.

Here the initial coordinate is $s=x$, so $p_0=3s^2/4$ and the equation forces $q_0^3=-s^3$, hence $q_0=-s$. The <projection map> <determinant> on the initial line is $3s^2$, nonzero for $0<s\leq1$. Solving the <characteristic curve> <ordinary differential equations> gives
$$
\boxed{x=se^r,\quad y=\frac32s^2(1-e^{-2r}),\quad
u=1+s^3(e^{-3r}-\tfrac34e^{-r}),\quad0<s\leq1,}
$$
with $p=3s^2e^{-2r}/4$ and $q=-se^{-r}$. These satisfy both the equation and all the noncharacteristic initial values.

The <projection map> <determinant> is $J=3s^2e^r(2e^{-2r}-1)$. Starting from $r=0$, the first <caustic> occurs at $r_c=\tfrac12\log2$. At the initial endpoint $s=0$ the <non-characteristic> condition fails, and its entire <characteristic curve> remains the single point $(0,0)$; it does not generate an open region of determination.

The maximal regular sheet connected to the initial line uses $r<r_c$. Its interior image is exactly
$$
\boxed{0<x<\sqrt2,\qquad\frac32(1-x^{-2})<y<\frac38x^2.}
$$
Indeed the inverse on this sheet is
$$
s^2=\frac{x^2+\sqrt{x^4-\tfrac83x^2y}}2,\qquad r=\log(x/s),
$$
and the inequalities say $0<s<1$ and $s>x/\sqrt2$. The upper boundary is the <caustic>; the lower boundary is the <characteristic curve> from $s=1$. Endpoints of that latter boundary can be retained as one-sided data. For forward <characteristic curves> alone, also impose $r\geq0$: this retains $y\geq0$, with lower boundary zero for $x\leq1$ and the displayed endpoint <characteristic curve> for $1<x<\sqrt2$. The other square-root branch belongs to the folded sheet after the <caustic> and does not provide a unique regular continuation from the <initial data>.

= Solution
{parent=6}

In vector form the system is $U_x+A(U)U_y=C(U)$, with
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}u&2\\2v&u\end{pmatrix},\qquad
C=\binom1{\sqrt v}.
$$
For $v>0$, its two real distinct <eigenvalues> are $\boxed{\lambda_\pm=u\pm2\sqrt v}$, with <left eigenvectors> $\ell_\pm^T=(1,\pm1/\sqrt v)$. Thus the system is strictly hyperbolic there; at $v=0$ the <eigenvalues> coincide and the matrix is defective, so <strict hyperbolicity> fails. The <characteristic directions> are $dy/dx=\lambda_\pm$.

If $\dot y=\lambda_\pm\dot x$, multiplication of $\dot U=U_x\dot x+U_y\dot y$ by the <left eigenvector> gives $\ell_\pm^T\dot U=\ell_\pm^TC\dot x$. Since $d(u\pm2\sqrt v)=du\pm dv/\sqrt v$, define
$$
R=u-2\sqrt v,\qquad S=u+2\sqrt v.
$$
Then
$$
\boxed{R_x+RR_y=0,\qquad S_x+SS_y=2.}
$$
Consequently $R$ is constant on the minus <characteristic curves>, and $S-2x$ is constant on the plus <characteristic curves>. One reconstructs $\boxed{u=(R+S)/2,\quad v=(S-R)^2/16}$, with the essential physical constraint $S\geq R$.

For the data on $y=0$, the nonnegative <square root> gives
$$
R_0(\xi)=\xi+\tfrac12-|\xi-1|,\qquad
S_0(\xi)=\xi+\tfrac12+|\xi-1|.
$$
Thus, on any regular patch, use the two footpoints determined by
$$
\boxed{y=R_0(\xi)(x-\xi),\qquad
 y=(x-\eta)^2+S_0(\eta)(x-\eta),}
$$
and put $R=R_0(\xi)$, $S=2(x-\eta)+S_0(\eta)$, selecting branches continuous from the initial point. These implicit algebraic formulas determine the local answer wherever both <characteristic curve> <projections> are regular and $S>R$.

For example, near an initial point with $x>1$, they give $R=3/2$, $S=2x-1/2$, so $\boxed{u=x+1/2,\quad v=(x-1)^2/4}$. On an initial interval $x<1$, define $H=\sqrt{9/4+4y}$. One instead has $S=H$ and
$$
\boxed{R=x-\tfrac14+\operatorname{sgn}(x_0-\tfrac14)\sqrt{(x-\tfrac14)^2-2y},\quad
u=\tfrac12(R+H),\quad v=(H-R)^2/16,}
$$
near the chosen initial point $x_0\ne1/4$. The radicands must be positive, the footpoints must stay in their indicated initial intervals, and $H>R$ must hold.

**There is no continuously differentiable solution realizing the first data set on the entire stated initial line.** At $(x,y)=(1/4,0)$, $u=3/4$, $\sqrt v=3/8$, $u_x=1$ and $v_x=-3/8$. The minus <left eigenvector> is $(1,-8/3)$ and its <eigenvalue> is zero. The differential equation there would require $\ell_-^TU_x=\ell_-^TC$, but its two sides are respectively $2$ and $0$. This is an explicit compatibility contradiction, not merely a loss of a preferred formula. Also, the proposed simple expression independent of $y$ does not solve the equation for $x<1$, because then $v_x<0$ but $\sqrt v>0$.

For the second data set, treat $x$ as the evolution variable and let $\eta$ label the initial $y$ coordinate. Then $R_0(\eta)=4\min(\eta,0)$ and $S_0(\eta)=4\max(\eta,0)$. In the positive quadrant the minus <characteristic curves> give $R=0$. The plus <characteristic curves> satisfy $S=2x+S_0(\eta)$ and $y=\eta+x^2+xS_0(\eta)$. Therefore their continuous <characteristic curve> continuation is
$$
\boxed{u=\begin{cases}
x,&0<y<x^2,\\
\dfrac{x+2x^2+2y}{1+4x},&y>x^2,
\end{cases}\qquad v=\frac{u^2}{4}.}
$$
Above the parabola the footpoint is $\eta=(y-x^2)/(1+4x)>0$; below it the plus footpoint is $\eta=y-x^2<0$. Each branch directly satisfies both equations and $v>0$. They agree continuously on $y=x^2$, but their $y$ <derivatives> are $0$ and $2/(1+4x)$, respectively. Thus **the continuation is piecewise classical and not continuously differentiable across the parabola**. The source's globally continuously differentiable formulation needs this qualification. If only positive initial $y$ is intended, the upper rational formula is a classical continuation throughout the positive quadrant, but the region $y<x^2$ requires incoming information from $y=0$ and is not determined by that positive half of the initial line alone. This illustrates <loss of differentiability from square-root hyperbolic source data>.

= Solution
{parent=7}

Set $\xi=x/t^\beta$ and $u=t^\alpha F(\xi)$. Balancing $u_t$, <diffusion> and the cubic gives $\alpha-1=\alpha-2\beta=3\alpha$, hence $\boxed{\alpha=-1/2,\quad\beta=1/2}$. The drift term cancels the $-\beta\xi F'$ contribution in $u_t$, leaving
$$
F''+\tfrac12F-\tfrac12F^3=0,\qquad F(0)=0,\quad F(+\infty)=1.
$$
Multiply by $F'$ and integrate. The limiting <equilibrium> has $F'=0$, fixing the constant and giving $(F')^2=(1-F^2)^2/4$. The increasing branch from zero to one obeys $F'=(1-F^2)/2$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{F(\xi)=\tanh(\xi/2),\qquad
u(x,t)=t^{-1/2}\tanh\!\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt t}\right).}
$$
The <first integral> also shows why another turning branch cannot reach the required limit from zero.

At a space-time interior maximum, $u_t=u_x=0$ and $u_{xx}\leq0$. The equation would then give $0=u_{xx}-u^3/2$, so $u\leq0$. Hence **a positive interior maximum is impossible**. In particular, a <global maximum> cannot be attained at an interior point, since the far-field boundary has positive values. If “maximum” instead means every <local maximum> of an arbitrary signed solution, the calculation only excludes positive local maxima; nonnegativity must be added for the broader usual interpretation. Positive boundary values alone do not impose nonnegativity on arbitrary <initial data>.

For uniqueness in the standard bounded <classical solution> class on finite time strips, set $\tau=\log t$, $\xi=x/\sqrt t$, and $v(\xi,\tau)=\sqrt t\,u(x,t)$. The equation becomes
$$
v_\tau=v_{\xi\xi}+\tfrac12v-\tfrac12v^3,
$$
with $v(0,\tau)=0$, $v(\infty,\tau)=1$ and prescribed $v(\xi,0)=f(\xi)$. For two solutions let $w=v_1-v_2$. Then
$$
w_\tau=w_{\xi\xi}+\left[\tfrac12-\tfrac12(v_1^2+v_1v_2+v_2^2)\right]w.
$$
The bracket is at most $1/2$ since $a^2+ab+b^2\geq0$. Thus $z=e^{-\tau/2}w$ solves $z_\tau=z_{\xi\xi}-Qz$ with $Q=(v_1^2+v_1v_2+v_2^2)/2\geq0$, zero <initial data> and zero boundary difference.

For completeness on the unbounded interval, compare $\pm z$ with $\eta e^{3\tau}(1+\xi^2)$ on a large rectangle. This is a strict positive <supersolution> because
$$
(\partial_\tau-\partial_{\xi\xi}+Q)e^{3\tau}(1+\xi^2)
=e^{3\tau}[3(1+\xi^2)-2+Q(1+\xi^2)]>0.
$$
Finite-strip boundedness lets its right boundary dominate $z$ when the rectangle is sufficiently wide. The <weak parabolic maximum principle> then bounds $|z|$ by this barrier. Send the width to infinity and then $\eta$ to zero. Hence $z=w=0$, giving $\boxed{\text{at most one bounded classical solution for }t>1}$. The boundedness or a suitable uniform far-field/growth condition is the usual hypothesis that makes this unbounded-domain argument valid; pointwise limits at <spatial infinity> should not silently replace it. This is <similarity reduction for a cubic absorption equation with dilating drift>.

= Solution
{parent=8}

Choose the positive <Green function> convention
$$
\boxed{-\Delta_xG(x,\xi)=\delta_\xi(x),\quad G=0\text{ on }\partial D,\quad
G(x,\xi)\sim-\frac1{2\pi}\log|x-\xi|\text{ near }\xi.}
$$
Away from its pole, $G$ is harmonic, and the <maximum principle for harmonic functions> makes it positive inside $D$. The sign of its <logarithmic singularity> is important: it is the convention for $-\Delta$, whereas a <Green function> for $+\Delta$ has the opposite sign.

<Green's second identity>, justified by removing a small disk about the pole and taking its radius to zero, gives
$$
\int_D(u\Delta G-G\Delta u)\,dx
=\int_{\partial D}(u\partial_nG-G\partial_nu)\,ds.
$$
Since $\Delta u=-f$, $\Delta G=-\delta_\xi$ and $G$ vanishes on the outer boundary, this rearranges to
$$
\boxed{u(\xi)=\int_DG(x,\xi)f(x)\,dx-\int_{\partial D}g(x)\partial_nG(x,\xi)\,ds.}
$$
The local <logarithmic singularity> is integrable, so continuous $f$ gives a well-defined volume <integral>.

In <complex coordinates> $z=x+iy$, $\zeta=\xi+i\eta$ for the <unit disk>, use
$$
G(z,\zeta)=\frac1{2\pi}\log\left|\frac{1-z\bar\zeta}{z-\zeta}\right|.
$$
The numerator has no zeros inside the disk, so its logarithm is harmonic there. On $|z|=1$, the numerator and denominator have equal modulus, giving the required zero boundary value. Differentiating in the outward radial direction at $z=e^{i\theta}$ gives
$$
\boxed{-\partial_nG(e^{i\theta},\zeta)=\frac{1-|\zeta|^2}{2\pi|e^{i\theta}-\zeta|^2}.}
$$
With zero forcing the representation therefore becomes the <Poisson integral>
$$
\boxed{u(\xi,\eta)=\frac{1-\xi^2-\eta^2}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}
\frac{g(\theta)\,d\theta}{(\xi-\cos\theta)^2+(\eta-\sin\theta)^2}.}
$$
Finally replace $\eta$ by $-\eta$ and change variable $\theta\mapsto2\pi-\theta$. Cosine stays the same, sine changes sign and the assumed boundary symmetry leaves $g$ unchanged. The <integral> is therefore invariant, so $\boxed{u(\xi,-\eta)=u(\xi,\eta)}$. Equivalently, <reflection> produces a <harmonic function> with the same Dirichlet data, and uniqueness identifies it with the original solution.
