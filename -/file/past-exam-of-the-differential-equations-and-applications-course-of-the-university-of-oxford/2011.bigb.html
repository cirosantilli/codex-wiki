= Solution
{parent=i}

A <distribution> is a linear functional on the <space of test functions> that is continuous in its test-function topology. Equivalently, for every compact $K\subset\mathbb R$ there are an integer $m$ and a constant $C_K$ such that $|\langle T,\phi\rangle|\leq C_K\max_{0\leq j\leq m}\|\phi^{(j)}\|_\infty$ whenever $\phi$ has support in $K$.

Linearity of $T_n$ follows from linearity of the <integral> and <derivative>. Since the integration interval has length $2/n$,
$$
|\langle T_n,\phi\rangle|\leq\frac n2\int_{-1/n}^{1/n}(|\phi|+|\phi'|)\,dx
\leq\|\phi\|_\infty+\|\phi'\|_\infty.
$$
This is a test-function <seminorm> estimate of order one, valid for every $K$ and even uniformly in $n$. It proves that **each $T_n$ is a distribution**.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The function $\phi-\phi'$ is continuous at zero, so its average over $[-1/n,1/n]$ tends to its value there. Consequently
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle T_n,\phi\rangle=\phi(0)-\phi'(0).
$$
The <Dirac delta> satisfies $\langle\delta,\phi\rangle=\phi(0)$, and its <distributional derivative> satisfies $\langle\delta',\phi\rangle=-\phi'(0)$. Hence the <distributional convergence> is
$$
\boxed{T_n\longrightarrow\delta+\delta'.}
$$

The characteristic <polynomial> after multiplication by four is $r^2+4r+4+\pi^2$, with roots $-2\pm i\pi$. Thus the general real solution of this constant-coefficient <ordinary differential equation> is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=e^{-2x}[A\cos(\pi x)+B\sin(\pi x)],\qquad A,B\in\mathbb R.}
$$
Equivalently the <change of dependent variable> $y=e^{-2x}v$ gives $Ly=\tfrac14e^{-2x}(v''+\pi^2v)$.

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

In the <distribution> definition, the <Green's function> is characterized, for fixed $0<\xi<1$, by
$$
\boxed{L_xg(x,\xi)=\delta(x-\xi),\quad g_x(0,\xi)+g(0,\xi)=0,\quad g(1,\xi)=0.}
$$
In the classical definition, solve $L_xg=0$ separately for $0<x<\xi$ and $\xi<x<1$, with those endpoint conditions, and impose
$$
\boxed{g(\xi^+,\xi)=g(\xi^-,\xi),\qquad g_x(\xi^+,\xi)-g_x(\xi^-,\xi)=4.}
$$
A jump in $g$ would introduce a <distributional derivative> of the <Dirac delta>, absent from the right side. Continuity eliminates it; integrating the equation across $\xi$ leaves one quarter of the derivative jump equal to one. These <Green function jump conditions> make the definitions equivalent. The forced solution is $y(x)=\int_0^1g(x,\xi)f(\xi)\,d\xi$.

{parent=b}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Under $y=e^{-2x}v$, the left <Robin boundary condition> becomes $v'(0)-v(0)=0$ and the right <Dirichlet boundary condition> becomes $v(1)=0$. Choose
$$
v_L(x)=\cos(\pi x)+\frac1\pi\sin(\pi x),\qquad v_R(x)=\sin(\pi x).
$$
Their <Wronskian> $v_Lv_R'-v_L'v_R$ is the constant $\pi$. For $y_L=e^{-2x}v_L$ and $y_R=e^{-2x}v_R$, it is $W_y(x)=\pi e^{-4x}$. The coefficient enforcing the derivative jump four is $4/W_y(\xi)$. Writing $x_< =\min(x,\xi)$ and $x_>=\max(x,\xi)$, the <Green's function> is
$$
\boxed{g(x,\xi)=\frac4\pi e^{2\xi-2x}\left[\cos(\pi x_<)+\frac1\pi\sin(\pi x_<)\right]\sin(\pi x_>).}
$$
The endpoint conditions, continuity and derivative jump follow directly from this <Wronskian> construction. Its exponential factor also shows why the kernel need not be symmetric in the unweighted inner product.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Put $t=\log x$ and $Y(t)=y(e^t)$. Then $xy''+y'=Y''/x$, so the <eigenvalue problem> reduces to
$$
Y''+(4+\lambda)Y=0,\qquad Y(0)=Y(\pi)=0.
$$
A nonzero <Dirichlet eigenfunction> therefore has $4+\lambda=n^2$ for an integer $n\geq1$. The complete family is
$$
\boxed{\lambda_n=n^2-4,\qquad y_n(x)=\sin(n\log x),\qquad n=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
The alternatives $4+\lambda\leq0$ give only the zero solution under both endpoint conditions.

The operator itself is **self-adjoint with the given unweighted inner product**, on the usual regular Dirichlet domain. Indeed $My=(xy')'+4y/x$, and <integration by parts> gives
$$
\langle f,Mg\rangle-\langle Mf,g\rangle=[x(fg'-f'g)]_1^{e^\pi}=0
$$
for functions satisfying the endpoint conditions. This is a regular <Sturm-Liouville operator> with positive leading coefficient $x$, whose Dirichlet realization is self-adjoint. The displayed generalized eigenproblem contains the additional weight $1/x$; its eigenfunctions are accordingly <orthogonal> with respect to $\int fg/x$, which is separate from the self-adjointness assertion about $M$.

= Solution

Write $v=Ly$ as
$$
v(x)=\cos x\int_x^\pi\sin t\,y(t)\,dt+\sin x\int_0^x\cos t\,y(t)\,dt.
$$
Differentiating twice, the moving-limit terms cancel at first order and yield $\boxed{v''+v=y}$ at second order. Also $v'(0)=0$ and $v(\pi)=0$. Thus this <integral operator> is the inverse of $D^2+1$ with mixed <Neumann boundary condition> and <Dirichlet boundary condition>.

For $\kappa_n=n+1/2$, $y_n(x)=\cos(\kappa_nx)$ satisfies those conditions and $(D^2+1)y_n=(1-\kappa_n^2)y_n$. The corresponding mixed homogeneous equation has no nonzero solution, so uniqueness gives
$$
\boxed{Ly_n=\lambda_ny_n,\qquad\lambda_n=\frac1{1-\kappa_n^2}=-\frac4{4n^2+4n-3}.}
$$
This verifies the asserted <eigenvalues> and <eigenfunctions> for every $n\geq0$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Since $\lambda_1=-4/5$, the <kernel of a linear map> $L+\tfrac45I$ is the span of $\psi(x)=\cos(3x/2)$. The self-adjoint <Fredholm alternative> requires $\langle f,\psi\rangle=0$ for solvability. If it holds, an arbitrary multiple of $\psi$ can be added, so a solution is not unique.

For $f=\cos x$,
$$
\int_0^\pi\cos x\cos(3x/2)\,dx=\frac65\ne0,
$$
so **case (A) has no solution**. For $f=\sin x\sin(3x/2)$,
$$
\int_0^\pi f(x)\psi(x)\,dx=\frac12\int_0^\pi\sin x\sin3x\,dx=0
$$
by sine-function <orthogonality>. Thus **case (B) has solutions, and they are nonunique**.

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For $\alpha\ne0$, the <Fredholm alternative> gives a unique solution exactly when $\alpha\ne-\lambda_n$ for all $n\geq0$, unless a resonant problem could exceptionally be compatible. Here none is: with $\kappa_n=n+1/2$,
$$
\int_0^\pi\cos x\cos(\kappa_nx)\,dx=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}\kappa_n}{\kappa_n^2-1}\ne0.
$$
At every resonance the compatibility condition therefore fails.

The case $\alpha=0$ must be checked separately because $L$ is a compact first-kind <integral operator>. For every admissible $y$, $Ly$ is continuous and $(Ly)(\pi)=0$, whereas $\cos\pi=-1$. Hence $Ly=\cos x$ has no solution. Combining these cases,
$$
\boxed{\text{A unique solution exists exactly for }\alpha\in\mathbb R\setminus\left(\{0\}\cup\left\{\frac4{4n^2+4n-3}:n\geq0\right\}\right).}
$$
In the nonresonant nonzero case the shifted eigenvalues stay away from zero, since $\lambda_n\to0$ and $\alpha\ne0$, so inversion is bounded on the Hilbert space.

= Solution
{parent=a}

After division by $x$, the <ordinary differential equation> has coefficients $P(x)=(m+1)/x-1$ and $Q(x)=n/x$. At zero, $xP\to m+1$ and $x^2Q\to0$, making zero a <regular singular point>. The <indicial equation> is
$$
\rho(\rho-1)+(m+1)\rho=\rho(\rho+m)=0,
$$
so the <indicial exponents> are $\boxed{0,-m}$, coincident when $m=0$.

At infinity put $t=1/x$ and $Y(t)=y(1/t)$. The transformed equation is
$$
Y''+\left(\frac{1-m}{t}+\frac1{t^2}\right)Y'+\frac n{t^3}Y=0.
$$
The coefficient of $Y'$ already fails the regular-singularity test at $t=0$. Thus $\boxed{x=\infty\text{ is an irregular singular point}}$, including when $n=0$.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

There is a normalization conflict in the source: the printed <Rodrigues' formula> gives the standard <generalized Laguerre polynomial> $P_n=L_n^{(m)}$ with $P_n(0)=\binom{n+m}{n}$, which is generally not one. The norm requested here corresponds to that Rodrigues normalization. A solution normalized to one at zero is $\widehat P_n=P_n/\binom{n+m}{n}$.

Set $w=x^me^{-x}$ and $p=x^{m+1}e^{-x}$. The equation becomes $(pP_n')'+nwP_n=0$. Multiply the equations for $P_n,P_k$ by the opposite <polynomial>, subtract and integrate. The boundary term $p(P_n'P_k-P_k'P_n)$ vanishes at zero and infinity, so
$$
(n-k)\int_0^\infty wP_nP_k\,dx=0.
$$
This proves <orthogonality> for $k\ne n$ under either normalization.

For the squared norm, use the printed <Rodrigues' formula> and integrate by parts $n$ times:
$$
\int_0^\infty wP_n^2\,dx
=\frac1{n!}\int_0^\infty P_nD^n(x^{n+m}e^{-x})\,dx
=\frac{(-1)^n}{n!}\int_0^\infty P_n^{(n)}x^{n+m}e^{-x}\,dx.
$$
All boundary terms vanish: the required derivatives have a positive power of $x$ at zero and exponential decay at infinity. The leading coefficient is $(-1)^n/n!$, so $P_n^{(n)}=(-1)^n$. The <Gamma function> integral gives
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty x^me^{-x}P_n(x)^2\,dx=\frac{(n+m)!}{n!}.}
$$
For the source's alternative unit-at-zero normalization the correct answer instead is
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty x^me^{-x}\widehat P_n(x)^2\,dx=\frac{(n+m)!}{n!\binom{n+m}{n}^2}.}
$$
Both cannot equal the printed norm when $m,n>0$. This distinction is the <normalization of generalized Laguerre polynomials>.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

For $m=0$ the two normalizations agree, and the <Laguerre polynomials> are orthonormal with weight $e^{-x}$. Take real $-1<h<1$. Their expansion coefficients for the requested function are
$$
c_n(h)=\frac1{1-h}\int_0^\infty e^{-x/(1-h)}L_n(x)\,dx.
$$
Substitute <Rodrigues' formula> and integrate by parts $n$ times. Differentiating $e^{-xh/(1-h)}$ transfers a factor $[h/(1-h)]^n$ after the two sign factors cancel. Therefore
$$
c_n(h)=\frac1{(1-h)n!}\left(\frac h{1-h}\right)^n\int_0^\infty x^ne^{-x/(1-h)}\,dx
=\boxed{h^n}.
$$
The integral is $n!(1-h)^{n+1}$ by the <Gamma function>. Boundary terms vanish throughout this range.

The resulting <generating function for Laguerre polynomials> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{e^{-xh/(1-h)}}{1-h}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty h^nL_n(x),\qquad |h|<1.}
$$
One can also verify convergence and equality directly: <Rodrigues' formula> gives $L_n(x)=\sum_{j=0}^n\binom nj(-x)^j/j!$, and summing $\sum_{n\geq j}\binom njh^n=h^j/(1-h)^{j+1}$ reduces the double series to the exponential on the left. The absolute-value double series converges for fixed $x$ and $|h|<1$, justifying the interchange; the formula then also holds for complex $h$ in that disk.

= Solution
{parent=i}

For zero viscosity, the <method of characteristics> gives $u=u_0(a)e^{-t}$ and $x=a+u_0(a)(1-e^{-t})$. On the sloping part of the initial profile, $u_0(a)=-2a$, so $x=a(2e^{-t}-1)$. While this map is increasing, inversion gives
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=\begin{cases}
2e^{-t},&x\leq1-2e^{-t},\\
-\dfrac{2e^{-t}x}{2e^{-t}-1},&1-2e^{-t}<x\leq0,\\
0,&x>0.
\end{cases}}
$$
The sloping interval shrinks from width one to zero while its height decreases from two to one. Its <spatial derivative> is $-2e^{-t}/(2e^{-t}-1)$ and diverges as $2e^{-t}-1\downarrow0$. Thus the first <shock formation by characteristic intersection> occurs at
$$
\boxed{t_* =\log2,\qquad x_*=0.}
$$
For $0\leq t<t_*$ the profile consists of a left plateau, a straight descending ramp ending at zero, and a right zero plateau. At $t_*$ the ramp becomes a vertical jump. This is a <damped Burgers equation>; viscosity smooths that jump into a thin transition.

{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

In the moving stretched coordinates, $\partial_t=\partial_\tau-s'(\tau)\varepsilon^{-1}\partial_\xi$ and $\partial_x=\varepsilon^{-1}\partial_\xi$. Multiplying the <damped Burgers equation> by $\varepsilon$ gives
$$
\varepsilon(u_\tau+u)+(u-s')u_\xi=u_{\xi\xi}.
$$
Discarding the order-$\varepsilon$ terms yields the leading <viscous Burgers travelling shock> equation $\boxed{(u-s')u_\xi=u_{\xi\xi}}$. Integrate across a nonconstant transition whose derivative vanishes at either end:
$$
\boxed{\left[\frac{u^2}{2}-s'u\right]_-^+=0.}
$$
Multiplication by $u$ and a further <integration by parts> give
$$
\boxed{\left[\frac{u^3}{3}-\frac{s'u^2}{2}\right]_-^+=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u_\xi^2\,d\xi<0.}
$$
The first identity is the <Rankine-Hugoniot condition>, giving $s'=(u_-+u_+)/2$. Substitution into the second makes its left side $(u_+-u_-)^3/12$, so the viscosity-selected <entropy shock> has $u_->u_+$.

After the first characteristic collapse, the shock is fed by the left constant initial state and the right zero state. Their damped values are $u_-=2e^{-\tau}$ and $u_+=0$. Therefore $s'=e^{-\tau}$. Matching to $s(\log2)=0$ yields
$$
\boxed{s(\tau)=\frac12-e^{-\tau},\qquad\tau>\log2.}
$$
These matching states are consistent with the characteristic feet: the left foot is $s-2(1-e^{-\tau})=-3/2+e^{-\tau}<-1$, while the right foot is $s>0$. The formula describes the leading thin-layer position in the small-viscosity limit.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Assume a classical solution continuous up to the relevant boundary. At an interior positive maximum, $u_t=0$ and $u_{xx}\leq0$, hence $Lu=u_t-u_{xx}+u>0$, a contradiction. At a positive maximum on the top edge $t=\tau$, away from the spatial endpoints, the left time derivative satisfies $u_t\geq0$, while again $u_{xx}\leq0$; the same contradiction applies. Thus a positive maximum can occur only on the initial or lateral <parabolic boundary>.

For two solutions with the same data, their difference $w$ satisfies $Lw=0$ and vanishes on that <parabolic boundary>. The <weak parabolic maximum principle> prohibits a positive maximum of $w$ and also of $-w$. Therefore $w=0$, proving **at most one solution**. The positive reaction term is what gives the immediate strict contradiction at a positive maximum.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Use the <Fourier transform> convention $\widehat u(k,t)=\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-ikx}u(x,t)\,dx$. Then the equation and <Dirac delta> initial condition become
$$
\partial_t\widehat u+(k^2+1)\widehat u=0,\qquad\widehat u(k,0)=1,
$$
so $\widehat u=e^{-(k^2+1)t}$. The <inverse Fourier transform> gives
$$
u(x,t)=\frac{e^{-t}}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-tk^2+ikx}\,dk.
$$
Complete the square as $-tk^2+ikx=-t(k-ix/(2t))^2-x^2/(4t)$ and use the permitted complex-shift <Gaussian integral>. Thus the <heat kernel with constant absorption> is
$$
\boxed{u(x,t)=\frac1{\sqrt{4\pi t}}\exp\left(-t-\frac{x^2}{4t}\right),\qquad t>0.}
$$
Its mass is $e^{-t}$, consistent with constant absorption, and it tends to the <Dirac delta> as a <distribution> as $t\downarrow0$.

{parent=5}

= Solution
{parent=c}

For a fixed observation point $(\xi,\tau)$, use the backward <adjoint Green function> with $L^*=-\partial_t-\partial_x^2+1$. It satisfies $L^*G=0$ for $t<\tau$, decays with its spatial derivative as $|x|\to\infty$, and has terminal <distribution> $G(x,\tau^-;\xi,\tau)=\delta(x-\xi)$. Extending it by zero for $t>\tau$ gives $L^*G=\delta(x-\xi)\delta(t-\tau)$.

For $0<t<\tau$, <integration by parts> proves
$$
\frac d{dt}\int_{\mathbb R}Gu\,dx=\int_{\mathbb R}[(-G_{xx}+G)u+G(u_{xx}-u)]\,dx=[Gu_x-G_xu]_{-\infty}^{\infty}=0.
$$
Evaluating this constant pairing at the initial and terminal times yields
$$
\boxed{u(\xi,\tau)=\int_{\mathbb R}G(x,0;\xi,\tau)f(x)\,dx.}
$$
By translating the <heat kernel with constant absorption> and reversing the time difference,
$$
\boxed{G(x,t;\xi,\tau)=\frac{\exp[-(\tau-t)-(x-\xi)^2/(4(\tau-t))]}{\sqrt{4\pi(\tau-t)}},\qquad t<\tau.}
$$
The terminal singularity is understood as a <distribution>; the representation follows for smooth decaying data and extends to standard integrable data by the heat-kernel approximation argument.

= Solution

Write $F_p,F_q$ evaluated at the fixed pair $(p(s),q(s))$. Differentiating in $\tau$ gives $x_\tau=F_p$, $y_\tau=F_q$, and $u_\tau=pF_p+qF_q$, proving $u_\tau=px_\tau+qy_\tau$.

Differentiate the other parameter. The difference of the required sides is
$$
u_s-px_s-qy_s=(u_0'-px_0'-qy_0')+\tau(p'F_p+q'F_q).
$$
The first bracket is zero by the prescribed initial compatibility condition, and the second is zero by differentiating $F(p(s),q(s))=0$. Thus $u_s=px_s+qy_s$ as well. These calculations use the differentiability of the supplied parametric functions.

If the coordinate <Jacobian determinant> $J=x_sy_\tau-y_sx_\tau$ is nonzero, the <inverse function theorem> gives local coordinates $(x,y)$. The <chain rule> then makes $(u_x-p,u_y-q)$ solve a homogeneous two-by-two <linear system> with determinant $J$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{u_x=p,\qquad u_y=q.}
$$
This is the <method of characteristics> for a first-order equation depending only on the gradient.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Take $F(p,q)=p^2+pq-1$. Parametrize the initial line by $x_0=s>0$, $y_0=0$, $u_0=2s^2$. Initial compatibility gives $p=4s$, and $F=0$ then gives $q=1/(4s)-4s$. The characteristic formula in part (a) yields
$$
\boxed{x=s+\tau\left(4s+\frac1{4s}\right),\quad y=4s\tau,\quad u=2s^2+2\tau,\qquad s>0.}
$$
Since $y>0$, take $\tau>0$. To select the branch connected smoothly to the initial data, stop each characteristic before its coordinate map folds. Its <Jacobian determinant> is
$$
J=4s-\frac{2\tau}{s},
$$
so this branch has $0<\tau<2s^2$. Eliminating $\tau=y/(4s)$ gives
$$
x-y=s+\frac{y}{16s^2},\qquad u=2s^2+\frac{y}{2s},\qquad s>\frac12y^{1/3}.
$$
For fixed $y>0$, the right side defining $x-y$ is strictly increasing on that selected branch. At its lower endpoint it equals $3y^{1/3}/4$, and it tends to infinity as $s\to\infty$. Thus it gives exactly one $s$ for every point in
$$
\boxed{y>0,\qquad x>y+\frac34y^{1/3}.}
$$
Substituting that unique $s$ into the formula for $u$ gives the required classical solution. As $y\downarrow0$ at fixed $x>0$, $s\to x$, and $u\to2x^2$ as required.

Without the pre-fold branch restriction, the algebraic equation also has a smaller positive root. That root tends to zero as $y\downarrow0$, and its $u$ tends to zero rather than $2x^2$, so it does not solve the prescribed initial-boundary problem. The fold $J=0$ is the <characteristic caustic>; uniqueness here is for the classical branch continued from the given initial data before that fold.
