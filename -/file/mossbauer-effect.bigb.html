= Mössbauer effect
{c}
{wiki}

A nucleus bound in a solid can emit or absorb a <photon> without exciting lattice vibrations, transferring momentum to the solid as a whole. The resulting recoil energy is negligible compared with the isolated-nucleus recoil, making very narrow resonant emission and absorption lines observable. The <Pound-Rebka experiment> uses this effect to resolve the small frequency shift from <gravitational redshift>.

The striking feature is the combination of a large <photon> <energy> and a tiny <natural linewidth>: an <atomic nucleus> can act as an extraordinarily selective <energy> filter. The <Mössbauer effect> supplies a narrow shared <emission line> and <absorption line> even when <nuclear recoil> would separate the corresponding free-nucleus lines. A slow mechanical motion can then tune their overlap through the <Doppler effect>. The <Pound-Rebka experiment> is an application to <general relativity>; the discovery experiment below concerns the recoil-free process itself.

= Recoil-free nuclear transition
{parent=Mössbauer effect}

= Recoilless nuclear transition
{synonym}

A <recoil-free nuclear transition> emits or absorbs a <gamma ray> while leaving the internal vibrational state of the surrounding <solid> unchanged. It belongs to the elastic part of the process: no <phonon> is created or destroyed. The <photon> still carries <momentum> $p=E_\gamma/c$, where $c$ is the <speed of light>. The emitting <solid> takes momentum $-\mathbf p$; the absorbing <solid> takes $+\mathbf p$. <Conservation of momentum> holds for each event.

Why does an isolated <atomic nucleus> pose a problem? Start with an <excited state> at rest and let $E_0$ be its transition <energy>, with nuclear <mass> $M$ and $E_0\ll Mc^2$. <Conservation of momentum> gives a recoil momentum of magnitude $p$; its nonrelativistic <kinetic energy> is therefore
$$
E_R=\frac{p^2}{2M}=\frac{E_\gamma^2}{2Mc^2}\simeq\frac{E_0^2}{2Mc^2}.
$$
By <conservation of energy>, emission spends part of $E_0$ on <nuclear recoil>, whereas absorption must supply both $E_0$ and the final <kinetic energy>. To leading order,
$$
E_{\mathrm{em}}\simeq E_0-E_R,\qquad E_{\mathrm{abs}}\simeq E_0+E_R,\qquad E_{\mathrm{abs}}-E_{\mathrm{em}}\simeq2E_R.
$$
Thus the relevant comparison is $2E_R$ against the overlapping <spectral line> widths, rather than $E_R/E_0$ alone. If $2E_R$ greatly exceeds the widths, the isolated-nucleus lines have negligible overlap. Finite <Lorentzian line shape> tails mean that this is suppression, rather than an absolute prohibition on absorption.

For a bound <atomic nucleus>, the recoil-free channel transfers <momentum> to the <center of mass> of the emitting or absorbing <solid>, with negligible <kinetic energy> $p^2/(2M_{\mathrm{solid}})$. Other channels exchange <phonons> and carry substantial <energy> into or out of the internal vibrations. A large $M_{\mathrm{solid}}$ makes the elastic channel's <energy> cost small; the vibrational <quantum state> determines its probability. Merely replacing $M$ by a large <mass> in the free-particle formula does not calculate that probability.

Here is a deliberately stipulated teaching system, independent of historical isotope data: $E_0=20\,\mathrm{keV}$, $Mc^2=10^{11}\,\mathrm{eV}$, and a single-line <natural linewidth> $\Gamma_E=10^{-7}\,\mathrm{eV}$ measured as an <energy> <full width at half maximum>. These give $E_R=0.002\,\mathrm{eV}$ and $2E_R/\Gamma_E=40{,}000$. A <solid> of $10^{12}$ such nuclear <masses> has elastic recoil <energy> $2\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm{eV}$, far below $\Gamma_E$. In this article $\Gamma_E$ is an <energy> width; the corresponding lifetime is $\tau=\hbar/\Gamma_E$, with $\hbar$ the <Planck constant> divided by $2\pi$.

= Lamb-Mössbauer factor
{c}
{parent=Recoil-free nuclear transition}
{title2=$f$}

= Recoil-free fraction
{synonym}

The <Lamb-Mössbauer factor> $f$ is the fraction in the coherent elastic line. In the harmonic-lattice approximation it is a <Debye–Waller factor>. Write the displacement of the emitting or absorbing <atomic nucleus> relative to its equilibrium site as $\mathbf u$, put $u_\parallel=\widehat{\mathbf k}\cdot\mathbf u$, and define the angular <wavenumber> $k=E_\gamma/(\hbar c)$. Choose the equilibrium site so that $\langle u_\parallel\rangle=0$. Its directional displacement <variance> is $\sigma_\parallel^2=\langle u_\parallel^2\rangle$. A harmonic <solid> in <thermal equilibrium> has a <Gaussian distribution> for this displacement, whose <characteristic function> gives the elastic amplitude
$$
\left\langle e^{iku_\parallel}\right\rangle=\exp\left(-\frac{k^2\sigma_\parallel^2}{2}\right).
$$
Taking the squared magnitude gives the intensity fraction
$$
f=\left|\left\langle e^{iku_\parallel}\right\rangle\right|^2=\exp\left(-k^2\langle u_\parallel^2\rangle\right).
$$
The factor $1/2$ belongs to the amplitude exponent; it disappears when the amplitude is squared. For an isotropic <solid>, $\langle u_\parallel^2\rangle=\langle|\mathbf u|^2\rangle/3$, so an equivalent expression is $f=\exp[-k^2\langle|\mathbf u|^2\rangle/3]$. Directional and three-dimensional mean squared displacements must not be substituted for one another without this factor.

An <Einstein solid> approximation replaces the vibrational spectrum by one positive <angular frequency> $\omega$. For the directional <quantum harmonic oscillator>, with the same effective <mass> $M$ as in $E_R$,
$$
\langle u_\parallel^2\rangle=\frac{\hbar}{2M\omega}(2\bar n+1),\qquad \bar n=\frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}-1}.
$$
Here $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant> and $T$ is <temperature>. This follows by taking the <expected value> of $u_\parallel^2$ in each oscillator <energy eigenstate>, $\hbar(n+1/2)/(M\omega)$, and averaging with the <Boltzmann distribution>. Substitution gives
$$
f(T)=\exp\left[-\frac{E_R}{\hbar\omega}\coth\left(\frac{\hbar\omega}{2k_BT}\right)\right],\qquad f(0)=\exp\left(-\frac{E_R}{\hbar\omega}\right)<1.
$$
The zero-<temperature> limit retains <zero-point energy> and a nonzero displacement <variance>. Cooling therefore increases the <recoil-free fraction> towards a limit below one for finite $E_R/(\hbar\omega)>0$. At high <temperature>, the exponent tends to $-2E_Rk_BT/(\hbar\omega)^2$. This is a single-frequency approximation to a harmonic lattice's coherent elastic weight, not a complete description of all zero-net-<energy> processes of an isolated oscillator. Real vibrational spectra, directional binding and anharmonic motion can change its predictions.

Extend the stipulated teaching system by choosing $\hbar\omega=0.020\,\mathrm{eV}$, so $\eta=E_R/(\hbar\omega)=0.1$. Then $f(0)=0.90484$, while $f=0.80542$ at $k_BT=\hbar\omega$. If source and absorber are independent, the fraction of events eligible for recoil-free emission followed by recoil-free absorption contains the product $f_s f_a$; this gives $0.81873$ for two zero-<temperature> copies. It is not the total probability of absorption, which also depends on the number of resonant <atomic nuclei>, geometry and <spectral line> overlap.

= Mössbauer velocity scan
{c}
{parent=Mössbauer effect}

Let $v$ be the source <velocity> towards the absorber, positive for approach along the <photon> direction. A first-order <Lorentz transformation> of the <photon> <energy> gives $E'=E+vp$; hence, for $|v|\ll c$,
$$
\delta E\simeq E_\gamma\frac{v}{c}.
$$
Moving the source shifts its <emission line> through the stationary <absorption line>. A minimum in transmitted <photon> counts marks their strongest overlap. The absorber is the narrow <energy> discriminator; the downstream <particle detector> only needs to count the transmitted <gamma rays>.

For a thin absorber with unsplit <Lorentzian line shapes>, the overlap is a <convolution> of the source and absorber profiles. The combined <full width at half maximum> is $W=\Gamma_s+\Gamma_a$. A simple expected transmission model is
$$
\frac{I(v)}{I_0}=1-\frac{A}{1+\left[2E_\gamma(v-v_0)/(cW)\right]^2},\qquad 0<A<1.
$$
Here $I_0$ is the off-resonance intensity, $v_0$ encodes a relative shift of the two transition <energies>, and $A$ includes the resonant absorber strength and $f_s f_a$. The convolution result can be checked by multiplying the <Fourier transforms> of two <Cauchy distributions>: their exponential decay constants add. The width of the absorption dip in <velocity> is $cW/E_\gamma$, rather than the width of one individual <spectral line> converted to <velocity>.

For the teaching system with $\Gamma_s=\Gamma_a=\Gamma_E$ and $v_0=0$, the dip's <full width at half maximum> is $2.9979\,\mathrm{mm\,s^{-1}}$. Stipulate $A=0.25$ at zero <temperature> and $10{,}000$ expected off-resonance counts per equal-duration bin: the central bin then has $7{,}500$ expected counts, and the half-depth bins have $8{,}750$. These are model expectations, without sampled counting noise. Holding all other parameters fixed while heating both lattices to $k_BT=2\hbar\omega$ multiplies $A$ by $[f(T)/f(0)]^2$. This isolates how a falling <recoil-free fraction> weakens the dip.

The thin-absorber model is deliberately limited. A thick absorber can saturate and broaden the dip; unresolved <hyperfine structure> can create multiple components. Differences in chemical surroundings can shift the centers, and uncontrolled motion can smear them. These effects must be modeled before interpreting an observed width as a <natural linewidth>. Their sensitivity also makes the <Mössbauer effect> useful: a small displacement of a narrow dip can reveal a small change in the local nuclear environment.

= Mössbauer discovery experiment
{c}
{parent=Mössbauer effect}

<Rudolf Ludwig Mössbauer> found the effect during his Heidelberg work in 1957.<Rudolf Ludwig Mössbauer, CV by Lindau Nobel Laureate Meetings>[CV, opening paragraphs: Heidelberg discovery in 1957]{ref} His 1961 lecture *Recoilless nuclear resonance absorption of gamma radiation* describes crystalline preparations using the $129\,\mathrm{keV}$ transition of the <iridium> <isotope> $^{191}\mathrm{Ir}$. Separate cryostats, lead collimation and a <particle scintillation> detector measured transmission. Liquid-air cooling of both preparations enhanced absorption; cooling only the source against a room-temperature absorber slightly reduced it. A rotating source cryostat supplied nearly constant projected velocities over selected arcs. Centimetres per second suppressed absorption relative to zero <velocity>; a nonresonant absorber supplied the comparison. This tested a narrow common emission/absorption <energy> beyond the detector's resolution. Temperature data alone had not established its width. The lecture cites the initial and direct-verification publications from 1958.<Recoilless nuclear resonance absorption of gamma radiation by Rudolf Ludwig Mössbauer>[Authorized 2012 reprint, pp. 6–8, 13–15, 17; Figs. 1.3–1.4, 1.7–1.8]{ref}
