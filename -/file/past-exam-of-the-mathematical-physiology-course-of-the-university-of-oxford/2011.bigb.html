= Solution
{parent=a}

In the <Hodgkin-Huxley model>, $V$ is the <membrane potential>, the <voltage> inside the axon relative to outside. The <space clamp> assumption makes it spatially uniform. The gating variables lie between zero and one: $m$ describes <sodium> activation, $h$ describes availability against <sodium> inactivation, and $n$ describes <potassium> activation. Thus large $h$ means channels remain available, not that inactivation is strong.

The left side $C_m\dot V$ is capacitive <electric current> per unit membrane area. The imposed $I$ is inward-positive applied <electric current>. The other terms are outward-positive ionic <electric currents>: <sodium> with <electrical conductance> $g_{Na}m^3h$, <potassium> with conductance $g_Kn^4$, and a passive leak with conductance $g_L$. Each current is its conductance times the difference between $V$ and the corresponding <reversal potential> $V_{Na}$, $V_K$ or $V_L$. The coefficients $g_{Na},g_K$ are maximal conductances; the gate factors represent <independent permissive gates in an ion channel>.

For each gate, $k_\infty(V)$ is the equilibrium opening/availability fraction at fixed <voltage> and $\tau_k(V)>0$ its <exponential relaxation time>. The equation follows from <two-state ion-channel gating>: if $\dot k=\alpha_k(V)(1-k)-\beta_k(V)k$, then
$$
\boxed{k_\infty=\frac{\alpha_k}{\alpha_k+\beta_k},\qquad\tau_k=\frac1{\alpha_k+\beta_k}.}
$$
<Voltage> feedback couples these otherwise elementary gating equations to the <membrane potential> and produces <action potentials>.

Write $M(v)=m^3(v)$. In the physically relevant strip $-v_K<v<1$, $0<n<H$, the zero-current <nullcline> satisfies
$$
\boxed{\frac{n^4}{H-n}=\frac{(1-v)M(v)}{\gamma_K(v+v_K)}.}
$$
The left side increases strictly from zero to infinity, so there is exactly one <nullcline> height $n=N(v)$ for each interior <voltage>. If $M$ is positive at the endpoints, its curve joins $(-v_K,H)$ to $(1,0)$; when low-<voltage> activation is idealized as exactly zero, the limiting left branch can lie on $v=-v_K$.

The signs of the derivatives explain its folds:
$$
g_n=4\gamma_K(v+v_K)n^3+(1-v)M(v)>0,
$$
$$
g_v=\gamma_Kn^4+(H-n)M(v)-(1-v)(H-n)M'(v),\qquad N'(v)=-g_v/g_n.
$$
At low <voltage>, <potassium>'s approximately linear contribution dominates and $g_v>0$. During rapid <sodium> activation the last negative term can dominate, giving $g_v<0$. Once activation saturates, $M'$ becomes small and $g_v>0$ again. This <folded conductance-model nullcline> has the familiar shape: the curve has **descending outer branches and an ascending middle branch** in the $(v,n)$ plane. Equivalently, an intermediate fixed $n$ can give three zeros of $g$ as <voltage> rises, with positive, negative and positive $g_v$ at those crossings.

The rapid-activation description permits this folded shape; exact fold positions depend on $M,\gamma_K,H,v_K$. The figure supplies an explicit admissible sigmoid example. Its right panel shows $g=0$ as well as the recovery <nullcline> and an oscillating trajectory.

= Solution

The <voltage> <nullcline> is $g(v,n)=I^*$ and the recovery <nullcline> is $n=n_\infty(v)$. In the relevant region $g_n>0$, so below the <voltage> curve $\dot v>0$ and above it $\dot v<0$. Below the recovery curve $\dot n>0$, while above it $\dot n<0$.

For small $\epsilon$, the outer <voltage> branches with $g_v>0$ attract fast <voltage> motion; the middle branch with $g_v<0$ repels it. Arrange the intersections so the equilibrium is on that middle branch, with recovery increasing along the high-<voltage> attracting branch and decreasing along the low-<voltage> attracting branch. The trajectory then drifts towards a fold, jumps rapidly to the other attracting branch at nearly constant $n$, and repeats. This is a <relaxation oscillation>.

The local <linear stability> calculation supports the construction. At an equilibrium,
$$
J=\begin{pmatrix}-g_v/\epsilon&-g_n/\epsilon\\n_\infty'&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\operatorname{tr}J=-g_v/\epsilon-1,\qquad\det J=\frac{g_v+g_nn_\infty'}{\epsilon}.
$$
A sufficiently steep recovery curve gives positive determinant, while $g_v<-\epsilon$ gives positive trace. Hence the equilibrium is a repeller. The bounded physical rectangle can be chosen forward invariant, and a small neighborhood of the repeller removed to give a trapping annulus. The <Poincaré-Bendixson theorem> then supplies a <periodic orbit>. These conditions can be realized because the recovery function and the applied current control the intersection and its slope; oscillation is possible, not forced for every choice.

For the illustration, $\gamma_K=0.1$, $H=0.8$, $v_K=0.2$, $\epsilon=0.01$, $I^*=0$, $M(v)=(1+e^{-25(v-0.2)})^{-1}$ and $n_\infty(v)=0.8/(1+e^{-20v})$. The rectangle $[-0.2,1]\times[0,0.8]$ has inward-pointing flow, and the displayed numerical trajectory realizes the folded-branch mechanism.

= Solution

The <nullclines> are $w=g(v)$ and $v=0$. The two outer pieces of $g$ have slope $-1$; the middle piece has positive slope
$$
\mu=\frac{1-\beta+\alpha}{\beta-\alpha}>0.
$$
The curve is continuous, with folds $(\alpha,-\alpha)$ and $(\beta,1-\beta)$. The unique equilibrium is $(0,-\alpha/(\beta-\alpha))$. Its <Jacobian matrix> has trace $\mu/\epsilon>0$ and determinant $1/\epsilon>0$, so it is unstable. The outer branches attract the fast <voltage> motion.

Let $w_L=-\alpha$ and $w_R=1-\beta$. The hypothesis $\beta-\alpha<1$ gives $0<w_L<w_R<1$. The singular <relaxation oscillation> follows this route:

* On the right branch $v=1-w>0$, $w$ slowly increases from $w_L$ to $w_R$.
* At $w=w_R$, the fast <voltage> jumps from $v=\beta$ to $v=\beta-1<\alpha$.
* On the left branch $v=-w<0$, $w$ slowly decreases from $w_R$ to $w_L$.
* At $w=w_L$, <voltage> jumps from $v=\alpha$ to $v=1+\alpha>\beta$.

The jumps are almost horizontal in the <phase plane>, because $w$ changes negligibly on the fast $O(\epsilon)$ time. Below $w=g(v)$, <voltage> increases; above it, <voltage> decreases; $w$ increases on the right of $v=0$ and decreases on the left. These signs provide a closed trapping region around the indicated route for sufficiently small positive $\epsilon$, with the unstable equilibrium excluded, giving a <periodic orbit> by the planar return-map or <Poincaré-Bendixson theorem> argument. The continuous piecewise linear field is locally Lipschitz, so solution uniqueness is available at the corners.

On the attracting right branch, the leading fast balance is $w=1-v$, so $\dot w=v=1-w$. On the left branch it is $w=-v$, so $\dot w=-w$. The slow travel times are therefore
$$
T_R=\int_{w_L}^{w_R}\frac{dw}{1-w}=\log\frac{1+\alpha}{\beta},\qquad T_L=\int_{w_R}^{w_L}\frac{dw}{-w}=\log\frac{1-\beta}{-\alpha}.
$$
Both are positive because $w_R>w_L$. The fast jumps contribute vanishing time as $\epsilon\to0$, giving the <piecewise linear relaxation-oscillator period>
$$
\boxed{T\sim\log\frac{1+\alpha}{\beta}+\log\frac{1-\beta}{-\alpha}=\log\frac{(1+\alpha)(1-\beta)}{-\alpha\beta}.}
$$
For the illustrated $\alpha=-0.25$, $\beta=0.4$, the leading period is $1.5041$ in the given nondimensional time units.

= Solution

Let $p_a,p_v,p_{LV}$ be pressures in the <arterial reservoir>, <venous reservoir> and <left ventricle>. Constant <vascular compliances> $C_a,C_v$ relate volume changes to <pressure> changes. The <ventricular elastance> relation is
$$
V_{LV}=V_0+\frac{p_{LV}}{E_{LV}(t)},
$$
where $V_0$ is the unstressed ventricular volume and $E_{LV}(t)>0$ is the time-dependent inverse compliance. Define the positive part $[x]_+=\max(x,0)$. The systemic, aortic-valve and mitral-valve flows are
$$
Q_c=\frac{p_a-p_v}{R_c},\qquad Q_a=\frac{[p_{LV}-p_a]_+}{R_a},\qquad Q_v=\frac{[p_v-p_{LV}]_+}{R_v}.
$$
Each $R$ is a positive <hydraulic resistance>. The <aortic valve> permits outflow only when ventricular <pressure> exceeds arterial <pressure>, and the <mitral valve> permits filling only when venous <pressure> exceeds ventricular <pressure>.

<Mass conservation>, with <blood> treated as incompressible, gives
$$
C_a\dot p_a=Q_a-Q_c,\qquad C_v\dot p_v=Q_c-Q_v,\qquad\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{p_{LV}}{E_{LV}}\right)=Q_v-Q_a.
$$
Substitution and multiplication of the first two equations by $R_c$ give exactly
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
R_cC_a\dot p_a&=-(p_a-p_v)+\frac{R_c}{R_a}[p_{LV}-p_a]_+,\\
R_cC_v\dot p_v&=(p_a-p_v)-\frac{R_c}{R_v}[p_v-p_{LV}]_+,\\
\frac{d}{dt}(p_{LV}/E_{LV})&=\frac{[p_v-p_{LV}]_+}{R_v}-\frac{[p_{LV}-p_a]_+}{R_a}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Adding the three original balances gives zero total volume change. This is a <three-compartment ventricular circulation model>.

= Solution

During <isovolumetric contraction>, ventricular <pressure> lies between the venous and arterial pressures, $p_v<p_{LV}<p_a$. Both valves are closed, so $Q_v=Q_a=0$ and ventricular volume remains at its end-filling value $V^+$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{p_{LV}=E_{LV}(t)(V^+-V_0).}
$$
Here $V^+$ is end-diastolic ventricular volume and $V_0$ is the zero-<pressure>/unstressed volume.

The arterial and venous reservoirs still exchange systemic <blood>:
$$
\dot p_a=-\frac{p_a-p_v}{R_cC_a},\qquad\dot p_v=\frac{p_a-p_v}{R_cC_v}.
$$
If contraction is short compared with both $R_cC_a$ and $R_cC_v$, the fractional changes are small and
$$
\boxed{p_a\simeq p_a^+,\qquad p_v\simeq p_v^+,}
$$
where the superscript values refer to the beginning of contraction. Thus <pressure> can rise rapidly in the <left ventricle> through increasing elastance while the other pressures are approximately fixed. This stage ends when $p_{LV}$ reaches $p_a$ and the <aortic valve> opens.

{parent=2}

= Solution
{parent=c}

During ejection the <mitral valve> is closed, so $Q_v=0$. Adding the arterial and ventricular balances cancels aortic flow:
$$
R_cC_a\dot p_a+R_c\frac{d}{dt}(p_{LV}/E_{LV})=-(p_a-p_v)\simeq-p_a,
$$
where the last approximation assumes $p_v\ll p_a$.

Model the rapid contraction as a switch to approximately constant systolic elastance $E_S$, with $C_s=1/E_S$ the systolic ventricular compliance. A sufficiently small aortic-valve resistance makes $p_{LV}\simeq p_a$ after a short <pressure>-equalization transient. The systolic pressure must be high enough to open the valve, $E_S(V^+-V_0)>p_a^+$. During that transient neglect systemic runoff. Conservation of combined arterial and stressed ventricular volume gives
$$
C_ap_a^++V^+-V_0=(C_a+C_s)\widehat p_a,
$$
so
$$
\boxed{\widehat p_a\simeq\frac{C_ap_a^++V^+-V_0}{C_a+C_s}.}
$$
This is an approximation across a rapid transient, not a discontinuity at finite valve resistance. The subsequent common <pressure> obeys $R_c(C_a+C_s)\dot p_a\simeq-p_a$, giving
$$
\boxed{p_a(t)\simeq\widehat p_a\exp\left[-\frac{t}{R_c(C_a+C_s)}\right].}
$$
The source's $R_C$ in this expression denotes the same systemic resistance $R_c$.

At the end of firing, the remaining ventricular volume is $V_{\mathrm{end}}\simeq V_0+C_sp_a(\Delta t_F)$. The <stroke volume> is $V^+-V_{\mathrm{end}}$, hence
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{SV}\simeq V^+-V_0-\frac{C_s}{C_a+C_s}(C_ap_a^++V^+-V_0)\exp\left[-\frac{\Delta t_F}{R_c(C_a+C_s)}\right].}
$$
The assumptions are a closed <mitral valve> throughout ejection, negligible venous <pressure>, rapid valve equalization relative to systemic runoff, and nearly constant systolic elastance during the interval measured by $\Delta t_F$. These give the <ventricular ejection pressure and stroke-volume approximation>.

For a constant solution $u(t)\equiv U$, its delayed value is also $U$. The equation becomes $0=U(1-U)$, so the only constant equilibria are $\boxed{U=0\text{ and }U=1}$. The <delay differential equation> requires the same constant history to realize each equilibrium.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

A positive solution stays positive, since
$$
u(t)=u(0)\exp\left(\int_0^t[1-u(s-\tau)]\,ds\right).
$$
Therefore the sign of $\dot u$ is exactly the sign of $1-u(t-\tau)$. Initially the given history below one makes $\dot u>0$. If $u$ crosses one upwards at time $t_0$, it continues growing until $t_0+\tau$, because the feedback still sees earlier values below one. It reaches a maximum when the delayed value crosses one. Similarly, after a downward crossing at $t_1$, it continues decreasing until $t_1+\tau$ and reaches a minimum then.

This delayed reversal makes overshoots and undershoots possible. In a derivative sketch, positive lobes persist until one delay after upward crossings, followed by negative lobes persisting until one delay after downward crossings. Sustained oscillation depends on the delay; a short delay can instead give convergence to one. The example shown has $\tau=2>\pi/2$ and a history identically $1/2$.

Put $u=1+x$. Then $\dot x=-x(t-\tau)-x(t)x(t-\tau)$, so the <linearization> is $\dot x=-x(t-\tau)$. A mode $e^{\lambda t}$ gives the <characteristic equation of a delay differential equation>
$$
\boxed{\lambda=-e^{-\lambda\tau}.}
$$
For real $\lambda\geq0$, the left side is nonnegative and the right side negative. The two real graphs therefore never meet there, as drawn in the figure. **There is no nonoscillatory exponential instability.** If stability is lost, the unstable <eigenvalues> must be a nonreal conjugate pair, producing oscillatory growth; absence of a positive real root alone does not imply stability.

= Solution

For a purely imaginary root $\lambda=i\omega$ with $\omega>0$, separating real and imaginary parts gives
$$
0=-\cos(\omega\tau),\qquad\omega=\sin(\omega\tau).
$$
Thus $\omega=1$ and $\tau=\pi/2+2\pi m$, $m\geq0$. The first crossing is
$$
\boxed{\tau_c=\pi/2,\qquad\lambda=\pm i.}
$$
For $0<\tau<\pi/2$ there is no root with nonnegative real part. Indeed, write $\lambda=a+ib$ with $a\geq0$. The real equation forces $\cos(b\tau)\leq0$, hence $|b|\tau\geq\pi/2$. But the imaginary equation gives $|b|\leq e^{-a\tau}\leq1$, contradicting $\tau<\pi/2$.

Differentiate the characteristic equation along the root:
$$
\frac{d\lambda}{d\tau}=-\frac{\lambda^2}{1+\tau\lambda},\qquad\left.\operatorname{Re}\frac{d\lambda}{d\tau}\right|_{\lambda=i,\tau=\tau_c}=\frac1{1+\tau_c^2}>0.
$$
The imaginary roots are simple because $1+i\tau_c\ne0$, and they cross into the unstable half-plane as delay increases.

One can also check the nonlinear <Hopf bifurcation> directly. With $\theta=\omega t$ and small amplitude $a$, substitute
$$
x=a\cos\theta+a^2\left(\frac15\cos2\theta+\frac1{10}\sin2\theta\right)+O(a^3),\qquad\omega=1+\nu a^2+O(a^3),\quad\tau=\tau_c+\mu a^2+O(a^3).
$$
The constant and second harmonics at order $a^2$ give the displayed coefficients. Put $d=\mu+\tau_c\nu$. At order $a^3$, absence of resonant first harmonics requires $d+1/20=0$ and $\nu+3/20=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\nu=-\frac3{20},\qquad\mu=\frac{3\pi-2}{40}>0.}
$$
The nonzero coefficient verifies a branch of small periodic solutions for $\tau>\tau_c$, consistent with the transverse <Hopf bifurcation> in the delayed logistic equation. This is the <delayed logistic Hopf threshold>.

At the crossing the angular frequency is $\omega_c=1$, so the limiting small-amplitude period is
$$
\boxed{T_c=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_c}=2\pi=4\tau_c.}
$$
This is the period of the bifurcating infinitesimal oscillation. Finite-amplitude oscillations away from the threshold need not have period exactly $4\tau$.
