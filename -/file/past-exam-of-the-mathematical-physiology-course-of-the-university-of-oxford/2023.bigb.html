= Solution
{parent=a}

Write $a=[\mathrm A]$, $b=[\mathrm B]$ and $c=[\mathrm C]$ for the three <concentrations>. The letters denote <chemical species>; the forward arrow represents association of one <molecule> of each reactant into one complex, and the reverse arrow represents dissociation. The positive constants $k_+$ and $k_-$ are the respective <reaction rate constants>. For an elementary <chemical reaction>, the <law of mass action> gives forward and reverse <reaction rates>
$$
R_+=k_+ab,\qquad R_-=k_-c.
$$
Every forward event removes one <molecule> of each reactant and adds one complex; a reverse event reverses those changes. Thus the <reaction-rate equations> are
$$
\boxed{\dot a=-k_+ab+k_-c,\qquad
\dot b=-k_+ab+k_-c,\qquad
\dot c=k_+ab-k_-c.}
$$
The units of $k_+$ are inverse <concentration> per unit time, whereas $k_-$ has units of inverse time. Adding the equations gives the independent <conservation laws>
$$
\boxed{a+c=a(0)+c(0),\qquad b+c=b(0)+c(0).}
$$
In particular $a-b$ is constant, but this is a consequence of those two <conservation laws>. The total $a+b+c$ need not be constant: association changes the number of separate <molecules>.

For the general <stoichiometry>, take $p,q$ to be positive <integers>. The net <reaction rate> and <stoichiometric vector> are
$$
R=k_+a^pb^q-k_-c,\qquad \boldsymbol\nu=(-p,-q,1)^{\mathsf T}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\dot a=-pR,\qquad \dot b=-qR,\qquad \dot c=R.}
$$
The factors $p,q$ outside $R$ count <molecules> consumed per event; the powers inside $R$ express the <law of mass action>. The corresponding <conservation laws> are $a+pc=\text{constant}$ and $b+qc=\text{constant}$. No <factorial> is required in this deterministic convention for the <reaction rate constant>.

There are four reversible transitions of the <ion channel>, each changing exactly one gate:
$$
\begin{aligned}
S_{00}&\mathrel{\mathop{\rightleftharpoons}^{\alpha}_{\beta}}S_{10},&
S_{01}&\mathrel{\mathop{\rightleftharpoons}^{\alpha}_{\beta}}S_{11},\\
S_{00}&\mathrel{\mathop{\rightleftharpoons}^{\gamma}_{\delta}}S_{01},&
S_{10}&\mathrel{\mathop{\rightleftharpoons}^{\gamma}_{\delta}}S_{11}.
\end{aligned}
$$
These are first-order <reaction-rate equations>: their <reaction rate constants> are per-gate transition rates. In the <continuous-time Markov chain> interpretation, simultaneous changes of both gates have <probability> of order $(dt)^2$, so there is no direct diagonal transition in its <infinitesimal generator matrix>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Normalize the total channel density to one. For <independent permissive gates in an ion channel>, the <probability> that the $M$ gate is open is $m$, and the <probability> that the $H$ gate is open is $h$. The joint <probability> is the product of the appropriate open or closed <probabilities>, giving
$$
\boxed{S_{ij}=m^i(1-m)^{1-i}h^j(1-h)^{1-j}.}
$$
In particular
$$
S_{00}=(1-m)(1-h),\quad S_{10}=m(1-h),\quad
S_{01}=(1-m)h,\quad S_{11}=mh.
$$
The original PDF really prints $j$, rather than $1-j$, on the final factor. That expression fails <normalization of a probability distribution> and does not give the closed-$H$ <probability> at $j=0$; the displayed correction is necessary. If $S_{ij}$ are literal densities with conserved total density $N$, multiply each expression by $N$.

The product additionally assumes initially <independent events> and independent gate transitions. The transition scheme alone permits initially correlated gates and does not force this factorization at every time. The all-closed initial preparation used below satisfies the required product assumption.

= Solution
{parent=d}

For each state, add its incoming <reaction rates> and subtract its outgoing <reaction rates>. This gives the <master equation>
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\dot S_{00}&=-(\alpha+\gamma)S_{00}+\beta S_{10}+\delta S_{01},\\
\dot S_{10}&=\alpha S_{00}-(\beta+\gamma)S_{10}+\delta S_{11},\\
\dot S_{01}&=\gamma S_{00}-(\alpha+\delta)S_{01}+\beta S_{11},\\
\dot S_{11}&=\gamma S_{10}+\alpha S_{01}-(\beta+\delta)S_{11}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Summing gives $\sum_{i,j}\dot S_{ij}=0$, verifying the <conservation law> for total channel density. Nonnegative initial densities stay nonnegative because every loss term vanishes when its own density reaches zero.

= Solution
{parent=e}

With total density one, the open <probabilities> for the individual gates are $m=S_{10}+S_{11}$ and $h=S_{01}+S_{11}$. Adding the relevant <master equations> cancels transitions of the other gate:
$$
\boxed{\dot m=\alpha(S_{00}+S_{01})-\beta(S_{10}+S_{11})
=\alpha(1-m)-\beta m,}
$$
$$
\boxed{\dot h=\gamma(S_{00}+S_{10})-\delta(S_{01}+S_{11})
=\gamma(1-h)-\delta h.}
$$
These marginal <ordinary differential equations> hold even for correlated initial gates. Their product gives $S_{11}$ when the independence assumption in part (c) holds. Solving the <first-order linear differential equations> from the all-closed initial state gives
$$
m(t)=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\bigl(1-e^{-(\alpha+\beta)t}\bigr),\qquad
h(t)=\frac{\gamma}{\gamma+\delta}\bigl(1-e^{-(\gamma+\delta)t}\bigr).
$$
Put $\lambda=\alpha+\beta=\gamma+\delta>0$. Then
$$
S_{11}(t)=S_{11,\infty}(1-e^{-\lambda t})^2,\qquad
S_{11,\infty}=\frac{\alpha\gamma}{\lambda^2}.
$$
For positive opening rates this increases strictly from zero, so its half-<equilibrium> time is uniquely determined by $(1-e^{-\lambda t})^2=1/2$:
$$
\boxed{t_{1/2}=\frac{-\log(1-2^{-1/2})}{\lambda}
=\frac{\log(2+\sqrt2)}{\lambda}.}
$$
If an opening rate is zero, the all-open <equilibrium> density is zero and a nontrivial half-rise time is undefined.

= Solution
{parent=f}

The two identical $M$ gates are independent <Bernoulli random variables>, so their open count has a <binomial distribution>. Multiplying by the independent $H$ factor gives the normalized channel densities
$$
\boxed{S_{ij}=\binom2i m^i(1-m)^{2-i}h^j(1-h)^{1-j}.}
$$
Explicitly,
$$
\begin{aligned}
S_{00}&=(1-m)^2(1-h),&S_{01}&=(1-m)^2h,\\
S_{10}&=2m(1-m)(1-h),&S_{11}&=2m(1-m)h,\\
S_{20}&=m^2(1-h),&S_{21}&=m^2h.
\end{aligned}
$$
The <binomial coefficient> $\binom21=2$ counts which of the two distinguishable $M$ gates is open. At open count $i$, the total $M$-opening rate is $(2-i)\alpha$ and the total closing rate is $i\beta$. Dividing the <expected value> of that count by two therefore recovers the same single-gate <ordinary differential equation>:
$$
\boxed{\dot m=\alpha(1-m)-\beta m,\qquad
\dot h=\gamma(1-h)-\delta h.}
$$
For the specified <initial conditions> and common rate sum $\lambda$, the all-open fraction is
$$
S_{21}(t)=\frac{\alpha^2\gamma}{\lambda^3}(1-e^{-\lambda t})^3.
$$
Thus, assuming positive opening rates,
$$
\boxed{t_{1/2}=\frac{-\log(1-2^{-1/3})}{\lambda}.}
$$
Three gates take longer to reach half their final all-open fraction than two gates: all three activation factors must build up simultaneously.

In the <Hodgkin-Huxley model>, $V$ is the intracellular-minus-extracellular <membrane potential>, $C_m$ is the <membrane capacitance> per unit area, and $I_{\mathrm{app}}$ is the applied inward <electric current> per unit area. A <space clamp> makes $V$ spatially uniform, leaving temporal <ordinary differential equations>. With outward ionic <electric current> positive, the <capacitance> balance is $C_m\dot V=I_{\mathrm{app}}-I_i$.

The variables $m,n,h$ lie between zero and one. They describe <sodium> activation, <potassium> activation and availability against <sodium> inactivation, respectively. Thus larger $h$ means less inactivation. Their voltage-dependent limiting values are $m_\infty,n_\infty,h_\infty$, and $\tau_m,\tau_n,\tau_h$ are their positive relaxation <time constants>.

Each ionic term has the <Ohm's law> form conductance times driving potential. The <sodium> term has instantaneous <electrical conductance> $g_{\mathrm{Na}}m^3h$, the <potassium> term has instantaneous <electrical conductance> $g_{\mathrm K}n^4$, and the leak term has constant <electrical conductance> $g_{\mathrm L}$. Here the $g$ constants are maximal <electrical conductances> per area, and $V_{\mathrm{Na}},V_{\mathrm K},V_{\mathrm L}$ are the corresponding <reversal potentials>. The factors $m^3h$ and $n^4$ arise from <independent permissive gates in an ion channel>. A conducting channel carries zero net ionic <electric current> at its <reversal potential>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Use the three standard reduction assumptions: $m$ relaxes much faster than $V,n,h$ and can be replaced by $m_\infty(V)$; the two slower gates have the same <time constant>, $\tau_n(V)=\tau_h(V)=\tau(V)$; and their limiting open fractions have a voltage-independent sum, $n_\infty(V)+h_\infty(V)=K$. The constant-sum assumption is essential: equality of two <time constants> by itself does not preserve a moving voltage-dependent sum.

For $q=n+h$, addition of the two gate <ordinary differential equations> gives
$$
\tau(V)\dot q=K-q.
$$
The specified initial condition is $q(0)=K$. Uniqueness for this <first-order linear differential equation> gives $q(t)=K$, hence $h=K-n$ for all time. The remaining two <ordinary differential equations> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
C_m\dot V={}&I_{\mathrm{app}}
-g_{\mathrm{Na}}m_\infty(V)^3(K-n)(V-V_{\mathrm{Na}})
-g_{\mathrm K}n^4(V-V_{\mathrm K})-g_{\mathrm L}(V-V_{\mathrm L}),\\
\tau(V)\dot n={}&n_\infty(V)-n.
\end{aligned}}
$$
These assumptions give a <planar reduction of the Hodgkin-Huxley model>. The <FitzHugh-Nagumo model> keeps its fast activation and slow <recovery variable> geometry, approximating the voltage response by a <cubic polynomial> and the recovery response by a <linear function> after <nondimensionalization>. The <polynomial> equations in part (c) are a further model simplification; they are not an exact algebraic consequence of arbitrary original gate functions.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Assume $\varepsilon>0$. At an <equilibrium>, $w=\gamma v=f(v)$, so
$$
v\bigl[(a-v)(v-1)-\gamma\bigr]=0.
$$
The nonzero roots are
$$
v_\pm=\frac{1+a\pm\sqrt{(1-a)^2-4\gamma}}2,\qquad w_\pm=\gamma v_\pm.
$$
Consequently, with $\gamma_c=(1-a)^2/4$, there are three distinct <equilibria> for $0<\gamma<\gamma_c$, two distinct <equilibria> for $\gamma=\gamma_c$, and only the origin for $\gamma>\gamma_c$. At equality the two nonzero <equilibria> coincide; for $0<\gamma<\gamma_c$ they both have $a<v<1$. Geometrically, $\gamma_c$ is the maximum of the concave quadratic $(a-v)(v-1)$.

Since $f'(0)=-a$, the <Jacobian matrix> at the origin is
$$
J_0=\begin{pmatrix}-a/\varepsilon&-1/\varepsilon\\\gamma&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\operatorname{tr}J_0=-\frac a\varepsilon-1<0,\qquad
\det J_0=\frac{a+\gamma}{\varepsilon}>0.
$$
Thus the origin is always <asymptotically stable>. Its <eigenvalues> are
$$
\lambda_\pm=\frac{-(a+\varepsilon)\pm\sqrt{(a-\varepsilon)^2-4\varepsilon\gamma}}{2\varepsilon}.
$$
Writing $\gamma_N=(a-\varepsilon)^2/(4\varepsilon)$, the origin is a <stable node> for $0<\gamma<\gamma_N$ and a <stable focus> for $\gamma>\gamma_N$. At $\gamma=\gamma_N>0$ it is a <degenerate node>: the repeated negative <eigenvalue> has only one independent <eigenvector>, since the nonzero off-diagonal entry prevents $J_0$ being a scalar matrix. If $a=\varepsilon$, then $\gamma_N=0$ and every permitted $\gamma>0$ gives a <stable focus>. This change from node to focus does not change <stability> and is not a <Hopf bifurcation>.

= Solution

Take $\gamma>\gamma_c$ and let $\varepsilon\to0^+$ with the other parameters fixed. The <nullclines> are the <cubic polynomial> graph $w=f(v)$ and the line $w=\gamma v$. In the <phase plane>, $v$ increases below the <cubic polynomial> graph and decreases above it; $w$ increases below the line and decreases above it. The fast <ordinary differential equation>, in time $s=t/\varepsilon$, freezes $w$ and gives $dv/ds=f(v)-w$.

At $w=0$, the fast roots are $0,a,1$. Their <linear stability> follows from $f'(0)=-a<0$, $f'(a)=a(1-a)>0$ and $f'(1)=a-1<0$. Thus a fixed $0<v^*<a$ falls towards the resting branch, whereas a fixed $a<v^*<1$ rises towards $v=1$. If $v^*>1$, the first fast segment instead settles down towards $1$, followed by the same recovery excursion. The threshold $a$ is the leading <singular perturbation> threshold; finite-$\varepsilon$ corrections matter for initial values approaching $a$ with $\varepsilon$.

Let
$$
d=\sqrt{1-a+a^2},\qquad
v_L=\frac{1+a-d}{3},\qquad v_R=\frac{1+a+d}{3}.
$$
These are the roots of $f'(v)=0$. The outer branches $v<v_L$ and $v>v_R$ of the <critical manifold> are attracting under fast motion; the middle branch is repelling. The full excursion has four stages.

* **Fast activation:** nearly horizontal movement towards $(1,0)$, on the $t=O(\varepsilon)$ <time scale>, apart from the usual matching tails.
* **First slow phase:** motion along $w=f(v)$ from $v=1$ down to $v=v_R$. Here $w$ rises and $v$ slowly falls, because $\dot w=\gamma v-f(v)>0$ on the positive outer branch. This takes order-one time for fixed $\gamma$.
* **Fast repolarization:** near the right <fold point of a critical manifold>, the attracting branch ends. With $w_R=f(v_R)$ nearly constant, $v$ drops to the negative root $v_J$ of $f(v_J)=w_R$. The jump takes the fast <time scale>; a thin neighborhood of the fold has an intermediate <fold delay>.
* **Second slow phase:** motion along the negative attracting branch from $v_J$ towards $0$ from below, while $w$ falls towards zero. This is the refractory recovery and has order-one duration to a fixed small cutoff; exact return to the <equilibrium> takes infinite time.

For this <cubic polynomial>, the landing point is explicit. Since $f(v)-f(v_R)=-(v-v_R)^2(v-v_J)$, comparison of the quadratic <coefficients> gives
$$
\boxed{v_J=1+a-2v_R=\frac{1+a-2\sqrt{1-a+a^2}}3<0.}
$$
Thus a graph of $v(t)$ has a rapid rise, a slowly falling elevated plateau, a rapid fall through zero to a negative undershoot, and a slow approach back to zero. The figure shows both the <phase plane> and a computed time trace. The smaller initial perturbation stays near the resting branch and produces no elevated plateau.

The four-stage description concerns the <singular limit>. Close to a simple <fold point of a critical manifold>, balancing the local quadratic activation term with the slow drift gives $\Delta v=O(\varepsilon^{1/3})$ and $\Delta t=O(\varepsilon^{2/3})$ for fixed parameters. These layers shrink compared with the slow phases and do not alter the leading time integrals below.

= Solution
{parent=e}

On an attracting branch of the <critical manifold>, $w=f(v)$ to leading order. The <chain rule> therefore gives
$$
f'(v)\dot v=\dot w=\gamma v-f(v),\qquad
dt=\frac{f'(v)}{\gamma v-f(v)}\,dv.
$$
The initial fast segment ends at $v=1$, and the first slow phase ends at the right <fold point of a critical manifold>, $v_R$. Hence
$$
\boxed{T_1=\int_1^{v_R}g(v)\,dv,\qquad
g(v)=\frac{-3v^2+2(1+a)v-a}{\gamma v-v(a-v)(v-1)},\qquad
v_1=1,\quad v_2=v_R.}
$$
On this interval $f'(v)<0$ and $\gamma v-f(v)>0$. The <integrand> is negative, but the upper limit is smaller than the lower limit, so $T_1>0$. The numerator tends to zero at the fold while the denominator remains positive; the leading <integral> is finite. Fast segments and the <fold delay> give vanishing corrections as $\varepsilon\to0^+$.

= Solution
{parent=f}

The requested second phase is the second slow recovery phase, beginning after the downward fast jump. Its starting point is $v_3=v_J<0$, fixed by $f(v_J)=f(v_R)$, with the negative root chosen. Along it the same <chain rule> gives
$$
\boxed{h(v)=\frac{f'(v)}{\gamma v-f(v)}
=\frac{-3v^2+2(1+a)v-a}{v[\gamma+a-(1+a)v+v^2]}.}
$$
The approach is to zero from below. Thus, if the printed cutoff $\beta$ is a signed value, it must satisfy $v_J<\beta<0$ and $v_4=\beta$. If $\beta$ denotes a small positive magnitude, the endpoint is instead $v_4=-\beta$. To express both conventions unambiguously, put $b=|\beta|>0$ and write
$$
\boxed{T_2=\int_{v_J}^{-b}h(v)\,dv.}
$$
There is no positive small endpoint on this leading slow branch. A positive cutoff would require a different event or finite-$\varepsilon$ oscillatory recovery.

For bounded negative $v$ and $\gamma\gg1$,
$$
h(v)=\frac1\gamma\left[-3v+2(1+a)-\frac av\right]
+O\!\left(\frac{1+|v|^{-1}}{\gamma^2}\right).
$$
<Integration> therefore gives the useful more detailed approximation
$$
T_2=\frac1\gamma\left[
\frac32v_J^2-2(1+a)v_J+a\log\frac{|v_J|}{b}
-2(1+a)b-\frac32b^2\right]
+O\!\left(\frac{1+\log(1/b)}{\gamma^2}\right).
$$
Taking also $b\ll1$, the leading logarithm is
$$
\boxed{T_2\sim\frac a\gamma\log\frac1b.}
$$
Indeed near zero, $f(v)=-av+O(v^2)$ and the slow equation is $\dot v=-(\gamma+a)v/a+O(v^2)$, so at fixed $\gamma$ the exact leading logarithmic coefficient is $a/(\gamma+a)$. Its large-$\gamma$ limit is $a/\gamma$, agreeing with the <integral>.

The order of approximation matters: first take the <singular limit> at fixed $\gamma$, then the large-$\gamma$ and small-cutoff limits. For simultaneous limits, a sufficient separation near rest is $\varepsilon\gamma\ll a^2$. Otherwise the fast and recovery <time scales> merge, and these slow-branch formulas need not describe the late-time motion.

In this <two-pool calcium model>, $c$ is the cytosolic <calcium ion> <concentration>, and $c_s$ measures <calcium> in an intracellular store, such as the <endoplasmic reticulum>. The variables in these balances are expressed using compatible effective volumes, so internal transfers enter their two equations with equal and opposite signs. If actual compartment <concentrations> use different reference volumes, the corresponding volume ratio must first be included in their balances.

The <flux> $J_+$ pumps <calcium> from the <cytosol> into the store, $J_-$ releases it back, and $k_sc_s$ is passive store leakage back to the <cytosol>. The external input $r$ supplies cytosolic <calcium>, while $kc$ exports it from the <biological cell>. Thus $k,k_s$ have units of inverse time and all five <fluxes> have units of <concentration> per time. Their directions can be sketched as
$$
\text{exterior}\ \xrightarrow{\ r\ }\ \text{cytosol }c
\ \xrightarrow{\ J_+\ }\ \text{store }c_s,\qquad
\text{store }c_s\ \xrightarrow{\ J_-+k_sc_s\ }\ \text{cytosol }c
\ \xrightarrow{\ kc\ }\ \text{exterior}.
$$
Adding the two <ordinary differential equations> verifies the total <calcium> balance
$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}(c+c_s)=r-kc.}
$$
Internal uptake, release and leakage redistribute <calcium>; only the exchanges across the <cell membrane> change this total. In particular any <equilibrium> must have $c=r/k$ when $k>0$.

= Solution

Choose positive reference <concentrations> $C,C_s$ and a reference <flux> $J_0$, and define
$$
u=\frac cC,\qquad v=\frac{c_s}{C_s},\qquad
\tau=kt,\qquad j_\pm=\frac{J_\pm}{J_0}.
$$
Overdots now mean <derivatives> with respect to the dimensionless time $\tau$. The store balance becomes
$$
\frac{kC_s}{J_0}\dot v=j_+-j_--\frac{k_sC_s}{J_0}v.
$$
The total balance becomes $\dot u+(C_s/C)\dot v=r/(kC)-u$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mu=\frac r{kC},\qquad
\gamma=\frac{C_s}{C},\qquad
\varepsilon=\frac{kC_s}{J_0},\qquad
\delta=\frac{k_sC_s}{J_0},}
$$
and
$$
\boxed{\dot u=\mu-u-\gamma\dot v,\qquad
\varepsilon\dot v=j_+-j_--\delta v.}
$$
The reference quantities are scale choices, not additional kinetic variables; typically the response functions supply natural saturation <concentrations> and a maximal uptake <flux>. The identity $\delta/\varepsilon=k_s/k$ checks the <nondimensionalization>. A small $\varepsilon$ means store exchange is fast compared with external cytosolic removal.

= Solution

At any <equilibrium>, the first balance gives $u=\mu$ and the second gives $f(\mu,v)=0$. For every fixed $u>0$,
$$
f(u,0)=\frac{u^2}{1+u^2}>0,\qquad
\lim_{v\to\infty}f(u,v)=-\infty,
$$
and
$$
\boxed{f_v(u,v)=-\frac{u^4}{1+u^4}\frac{2v}{(1+v^2)^2}-\delta<0
\quad(v\ge0).}
$$
The <intermediate value theorem> and strict <monotonicity> give precisely one positive root $v=g(u)$ for each $u>0$. Since $f_v\ne0$, the <implicit function theorem> makes this a smooth <nullcline>. For every $\mu>0$, therefore,
$$
\boxed{(u_*,v_*)=(\mu,g(\mu))}
$$
is the unique positive <equilibrium>. Graphically, draw the vertical line $u=\mu$ through the given graph: it meets the graph once, even on its steep descending section. This vertical line is the condition for simultaneous stationarity; the full $u$ <nullcline> at finite $\varepsilon$ is actually $f(u,v)=(\varepsilon/\gamma)(\mu-u)$.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Eliminate $\dot v$ from the first equation before <linearization>:
$$
\dot u=\mu-u-\frac\gamma\varepsilon f(u,v),\qquad
\dot v=\frac1\varepsilon f(u,v).
$$
All partial <derivatives> below are evaluated at $(\mu,g(\mu))$. The <Jacobian matrix> is
$$
J=\begin{pmatrix}
-1-\gamma f_u/\varepsilon&-\gamma f_v/\varepsilon\\
f_u/\varepsilon&f_v/\varepsilon
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its <trace> and <determinant> are
$$
\operatorname{tr}J=-1+\frac{f_v-\gamma f_u}{\varepsilon},\qquad
\det J=-\frac{f_v}{\varepsilon}>0.
$$
By <linear stability of a planar equilibrium>, a positive <trace> makes both real <eigenvalues> positive or gives a complex pair with positive real part. The <equilibrium> is consequently unstable precisely when
$$
\boxed{\varepsilon-f_v<-\gamma f_u.}
$$
A negative <trace> gives <asymptotic stability>; equality leaves a purely imaginary pair and requires a separate nonlinear <bifurcation> calculation. In particular, the unique positive <equilibrium> is never a <saddle equilibrium>.

= Solution
{parent=e}

Differentiate the identity $f(u,g(u))=0$ using the <chain rule>:
$$
f_u+f_vg'(u)=0,\qquad f_u=-f_vg'(u).
$$
Substitution into the <trace> gives
$$
\operatorname{tr}J=-1+\frac{f_v}{\varepsilon}\bigl(1+\gamma g'(\mu)\bigr).
$$
Because $f_v<0$, the exact instability condition is
$$
\boxed{1+\gamma g'(\mu)<\frac\varepsilon{f_v},\qquad
g'(\mu)<-\frac1\gamma+\frac{\varepsilon}{\gamma f_v}.}
$$
For fixed $\delta>0$ and fixed <equilibrium> location, the correction tends to zero with $\varepsilon$. To leading order this is
$$
\boxed{g'(\mu)<-1/\gamma.}
$$
Thus instability requires the descending <nullcline> to be steeper than a line of slope $-1/\gamma$. The sign of the finite-$\varepsilon$ correction makes the exact unstable interval slightly smaller. Since $\delta=0.001$ can make $|f_v|$ small, the approximation quantitatively requires $\varepsilon/|f_v|\ll1$ at the point in question; merely comparing $\varepsilon$ with one can be insufficient.

On the descending part of the graph, mark the two tangency points where
$$
\boxed{g'(\mu_-)=-1/\gamma=g'(\mu_+),\qquad \mu_-<\mu_+.}
$$
Choose the crossings that enclose the portion with $g'(u)<-1/\gamma$. For fixed $\mu$ strictly between these values and sufficiently small $\varepsilon$, the unique <equilibrium> is a <repelling equilibrium> by part (e). The endpoints here are leading <singular limit> values; at finite $\varepsilon$ the boundaries instead solve $f_v-\gamma f_u=\varepsilon$ and approach these tangencies when the crossings are nondegenerate.

There is a necessary qualification: such an interval exists only when $\gamma$ is large enough that the descending graph actually attains slopes below $-1/\gamma$. If $M=\max_{u>0}[-g'(u)]$, one needs $\gamma M>1$. The supplied graph has a very steep descending section, but an arbitrarily small positive $\gamma$ need not satisfy this condition. For $\delta=0.001$, numerical evaluation gives $M\simeq511.49$, so the leading threshold is $\gamma\simeq0.0019551$. A graph alone does not remove this parameter restriction.

The fast motion is especially informative. In fast time $s=\tau/\varepsilon$,
$$
\frac{du}{ds}=\varepsilon(\mu-u)-\gamma f(u,v),\qquad
\frac{dv}{ds}=f(u,v).
$$
At leading order $z=u+\gamma v$ is constant, so fast paths are straight lines with slope $dv/du=-1/\gamma$. Along such a line, the fast <eigenvalue> at the <nullcline> is
$$
f_v-\gamma f_u=f_v\bigl(1+\gamma g'(u)\bigr).
$$
It is negative on the two outer attracting segments and positive on the intervening repelling segment. The tangencies defining $\mu_\pm$ are consequently the <fold points of a critical manifold> when it is expressed in the coordinates $(z,v)$. They are not generally the maximum and minimum of $g$ itself.

For $\mu_-<\mu<\mu_+$, slow motion on an attracting segment follows
$$
\boxed{(1+\gamma g'(u))\dot u=\mu-u.}
$$
On the left attracting segment $u<\mu_-$ this moves towards increasing $u$; on the right attracting segment $u>\mu_+$ it moves towards decreasing $u$. Each segment ends at a tangency and is followed by a fast jump along a line of slope $-1/\gamma$ to the other attracting segment. This constructs the limiting <relaxation oscillation>. The figure uses $\gamma=0.01$, for which $\mu_-\simeq0.62970$ and $\mu_+\simeq1.01157$; the marked endpoints change with $\gamma$.

To justify existence of a <periodic orbit> of the full planar system, rather than just draw a formal cycle, first establish a compact <trapping region>. The positive quadrant is forward invariant: at $u=0$, $\dot u=\mu+\gamma\delta v/\varepsilon>0$, and at $v=0$, $\dot v=u^2/[\varepsilon(1+u^2)]\ge0$. Moreover
$$
\dot v\le\frac{1-\delta v}{\varepsilon}.
$$
Choose $V>1/\delta$ above the initial store value. Since $f(u,v)\ge-1-\delta V$ when $0\le v\le V$,
$$
\dot u\le\mu-u+\frac\gamma\varepsilon(1+\delta V).
$$
Thus the rectangle $0\le u\le U$, $0\le v\le V$ is a <positively invariant set> if $U>\mu+\gamma(1+\delta V)/\varepsilon$ and the initial point lies inside it. Every forward solution is bounded. There are no <equilibria> on its axes for $\mu>0$, and its sole interior <equilibrium> is a <hyperbolic equilibrium point> with both <eigenvalues> having positive real parts in the strict unstable range. A sufficiently small neighborhood of this <repelling equilibrium> has outward flow; deleting it leaves a compact <trapping region> with no <equilibria>. The <Poincaré-Bendixson theorem> then supplies a <periodic orbit>.

Hence **<periodic solutions> exist throughout the strict unstable interval**, interpreted with the finite-$\varepsilon$ correction above. This argument establishes existence, without asserting uniqueness, the criticality of possible endpoint <Hopf bifurcations>, or absence of coexisting <periodic orbits> outside the interval.
