===== Solution

A <complex derivative> computed along the real and imaginary directions gives $f'=u_x+iv_x=v_y-iu_y$. Equality yields the <Cauchy-Riemann equations>
$$
\boxed{u_x=v_y,\qquad u_y=-v_x.}
$$
The <gradient>s satisfy $\nabla u\cdot\nabla v=u_xv_x+u_yv_y=0$. Since a <gradient> is normal to a regular <level set>, the two families meet at right angles by <orthogonality> wherever $f'\ne0$. At a <critical point> the regular-curve argument fails: for $f=z^2$ both <gradient>s vanish at zero, so the assertion requires this qualification.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Set $A=x^2-y^2$ and $B=2xy$. Direct <differentiation> gives
$$
u_{xx}=e^{-B}[(4y^2-4x^2)\sin A+(2-8xy)\cos A],\qquad
u_{yy}=e^{-B}[(4x^2-4y^2)\sin A+(8xy-2)\cos A].
$$
Their sum vanishes, proving that $u$ is a <harmonic function>. More generally the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> give $u_{xx}+u_{yy}=v_{yx}-v_{xy}=0$ and $v_{xx}+v_{yy}=-u_{yx}+u_{xy}=0$; the <mixed partial derivatives> commute because a <holomorphic function> is smooth. Since $e^{iz^2}=e^{-B}(\cos A+i\sin A)$, a <harmonic conjugate> and the resulting <holomorphic function> are
$$
\boxed{v=-e^{-2xy}\cos(x^2-y^2)+C,\qquad f(z)=-ie^{iz^2}+iC,\quad C\in\mathbb R.}
$$
These expressions also verify the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> directly.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

The <chain rule> in <polar coordinates> gives $u_r=u_x\cos\theta+u_y\sin\theta$ and $u_\theta=r(-u_x\sin\theta+u_y\cos\theta)$, with analogous expressions for $v$. Substituting the <Cauchy-Riemann equations> gives
$$
\boxed{ru_r=v_\theta,\qquad rv_r=-u_\theta,\quad r>0.}
$$
The restriction excludes the coordinate singularity at the origin.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

Choose a continuous <branch of a multivalued function> of the <complex logarithm> on a simply connected region avoiding zero. Then
$$
g=e^{-\theta}e^{i\ln r}=e^{i\operatorname{Log}z},\qquad
\boxed{g'(z)=\frac{i}{z}g(z).}
$$
Alternatively $u_r=-e^{-\theta}\sin(\ln r)/r=v_\theta/r$ and $v_r=e^{-\theta}\cos(\ln r)/r=-u_\theta/r$, verifying the <Cauchy-Riemann equations>. The <holomorphy> is local to a chosen <branch of a multivalued function>: increasing $\theta$ by $2\pi$ multiplies $g$ by $e^{-2\pi}$, so this is not a single-valued <holomorphic function> on the whole punctured plane.

===== Solution

For $n\ge1$, $f=z^ne^{n/z}$ is <holomorphic> at every finite nonzero point. At zero its <Laurent series> has infinitely many negative powers, so zero is an <essential singularity>. If the <Riemann sphere> is included, $f(1/w)=w^{-n}e^{nw}$ has a <pole> of order $n$ at $w=0$, so infinity is a <pole> of order $n$.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Expanding the <exponential function> produces the <Laurent series>
$$
\boxed{f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{n^k}{k!}z^{n-k},\qquad 0<|z|<\infty.}
$$
In particular its <residue> is $a_{-1}=n^{n+1}/(n+1)!$.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

For a positively oriented circle of any radius $R>0$, the <residue theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\oint_{|z|=R}f(z)\,dz=2\pi i\frac{n^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}.}
$$
Only the $z^{-1}$ coefficient in the <Laurent series> contributes. Reversing the <orientation> changes the sign.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

If $f(z)=\sum a_jz^j$, then $f(-z)=\sum(-1)^ja_jz^j$ and its <residue> at zero is $-a_{-1}$. The circle $|z-1|=2$ encloses zero and no other finite <singularity>. Thus the <residue theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\oint_{|z-1|=2}[f(z)-f(-z)]\,dz=4\pi i\frac{n^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}}
$$
for positive <orientation>.

==== a

===== Solution

<Cauchy's integral theorem> states that a <holomorphic function> on a simply connected open region has zero <contour integral> around every closed piecewise smooth <contour> in that region. More generally the same holds for a <contour> homologous to zero in its domain of <holomorphy>. Having no singularity on the curve alone is insufficient: $1/z$ has a nonzero <contour integral> around the unit circle.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Put $z=e^{i\theta}$, so $d\theta=dz/(iz)$, $\cos2\theta=(z^2+z^{-2})/2$ and $\cos^23\theta=(z^6+2+z^{-6})/4$. The <contour integral> is
$$
\frac1{4i}\oint_{|z|=1}\frac{z^8+2z^2+z^{-4}}{z(5z^2-2z^4-2)}\,dz.
$$
The enclosed <poles> are $0$ and $\pm1/\sqrt2$. For the rational function displayed without the prefactor $1/(4i)$, the <residue> at zero is $-21/8$ and the two other <residues> sum to $27/8$. Therefore the <residue theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\cos^23\theta}{5-4\cos2\theta}\,d\theta=\frac1{4i}(2\pi i)\frac34=\frac{3\pi}{8}.}
$$
A useful independent check is the <Fourier series> $(5-4\cos\phi)^{-1}=\frac13[1+2\sum_{k\ge1}2^{-k}\cos k\phi]$; only its constant and third <Fourier coefficients> survive.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

The square root produces <branch points> at $z=\pm1$, with local behaviour proportional to $(z\mp1)^{-1/2}$. One <branch cut> can join them along $[-1,1]$. Another choice uses the two rays $(-\infty,-1]\cup[1,\infty)$. Each choice prevents a loop from enclosing just one <branch point> within a single-valued domain. For the finite-segment choice fix $\sqrt{z^2-1}\sim z$ at infinity. Infinity is then a <pole> of order three, not a <branch point>, since
$$
f(z)=z^3+\frac12z+\frac{3}{8z}+O(z^{-3}).
$$

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the <branch cut> $[-1,1]$ and the <branch of a multivalued function> normalized by $\sqrt{z^2-1}\sim z$. The <Laurent series> from the preceding solution has $z^{-1}$ coefficient $3/8$. Consequently every sufficiently large positively oriented circle gives
$$
\boxed{\oint_{C_\infty}f(z)\,dz=\frac{3\pi i}{4}.}
$$
Although $f$ grows cubically, its other <Laurent series> terms have zero <contour integral>. Choosing the opposite <branch of a multivalued function> reverses the sign; neither the <branch of a multivalued function> nor the <orientation> can be omitted from the answer.

==== e
{scope}

===== Solution

Shrink the positive outer <contour> to the <branch cut> $[-1,1]$. On its upper bank the chosen <branch of a multivalued function> has $\sqrt{x^2-1}=i\sqrt{1-x^2}$ and on the lower bank it has the negative of this value. The upper bank runs from $1$ to $-1$, and the lower bank runs from $-1$ to $1$. The endpoint circles contribute $O(\sqrt\epsilon)$ and vanish. Hence
$$
\frac{3\pi i}{4}=2i\int_{-1}^1\frac{x^4}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx,\qquad
\boxed{\int_{-1}^1\frac{x^4}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx=\frac{3\pi}{8}.}
$$
The <change of variables> $x=\sin t$ independently reduces the result to the <integral> of $\sin^4t$ over $[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$.

===== Solution

<Kepler's third law> for two <point masses> in a <Newtonian gravitational field> is
$$
\boxed{P^2=\frac{4\pi^2a^3}{G(M_1+M_2)}.}
$$
Here $a$ is the <semimajor axis> of their relative <orbit>, $P$ the <orbital period> and $G$ the <gravitational constant>. Equivalently $(P/\mathrm{yr})^2=(a/\mathrm{AU})^3/[(M_1+M_2)/M_\odot]$, to the accuracy with which these astronomical units normalize the <solar mass>.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Assume a circular <orbit> and an <orbital inclination> $i\simeq90^\circ$, as appropriate for a simple <eclipsing binary> estimate. At quadrature the two <radial velocities> are opposite and equal to the <orbital speeds>. The more massive component has the smaller amplitude. With the <centre of mass> as origin,
$$
a_1=\frac{K_1P}{2\pi},\quad a_2=\frac{K_2P}{2\pi},\quad a=a_1+a_2,\quad \frac{M_1}{M_2}=\frac{K_2}{K_1}=2.
$$
Thus $a_1=0.1103\,\mathrm{AU}$, $a_2=0.2206\,\mathrm{AU}$ and <Kepler's third law> gives
$$
\boxed{a=0.3309\,\mathrm{AU},\quad M_1=8.06M_\odot,\quad M_2=4.03M_\odot.}
$$
The <orbital plane> contains the observer's line of sight; at quadrature the two <velocities> point towards and away from that observer, as labelled in the original schematic below. For an <elliptic orbit>, the measured amplitudes instead give $a\sin i=P(K_1+K_2)\sqrt{1-e^2}/(2\pi)$; the numerical answer requires the circular assumption.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Adopt the approximate <mass-luminosity relation> $L/L_\odot\simeq(M/M_\odot)^3$ and equal available fuel fractions. The <main-sequence lifetime> then scales as $M/L$, giving
$$
\boxed{t_1\simeq1.54\times10^8\,\mathrm{yr},\qquad t_2\simeq6.16\times10^8\,\mathrm{yr}.}
$$
Using an exponent $3.5$ instead gives about $5.4\times10^7$ and $3.1\times10^8$ years; these are model-dependent estimates. At $r=3r_s=6GM/c^2$ the <Newtonian gravitational potential energy> released from infinity is $GMm/r=mc^2/6$. Half remains as <kinetic energy> in a circular <orbit>, so the radiatable <binding energy> is $GMm/(2r)$:
$$
\boxed{\eta_{\rm Newtonian}=\frac1{12}\simeq8.3\%.}
$$
Using the whole <potential energy> loss would give $1/6$, which does not subtract the final <kinetic energy>. A fully relativistic calculation for a nonrotating <black hole> gives $1-\sqrt{8/9}\simeq5.7\%$. When the surviving star exhausts <hydrogen burning>, it expands off the <main sequence> and can fill its <Roche lobe>. <Binary mass transfer> to the <black hole> can form an <accretion disk> and an <X-ray binary>; unstable <binary mass transfer> may instead produce a <common envelope>. This is conditional on the <binary star> remaining bound after the first remnant forms. The supplied <black hole> endpoint is an assumption, not a universal outcome for an isolated $8M_\odot$ star.

===== Solution

A thin spherical shell has outward <pressure force> $-4\pi r^2(dP/dr)dr$ and inward <gravitational force> $GM(r)\,4\pi r^2\rho(r)dr/r^2$. <Hydrostatic equilibrium> requires
$$
\boxed{\frac{dP}{dr}=-\frac{GM(r)\rho(r)}{r^2},\qquad \frac{dM}{dr}=4\pi r^2\rho(r).}
$$
A uniform-<density> <solar model> gives $M(r)=M_\odot(r/R_\odot)^3$, $\bar\rho=3M_\odot/(4\pi R_\odot^3)$, and after <integration> from $R_\odot$ to zero,
$$
P_c=\frac{3GM_\odot^2}{8\pi R_\odot^4}=1.34\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{Pa},\qquad
T_c=\frac{P_c\mu m_p}{\bar\rho k_B}=6.9\times10^6\,\mathrm K\quad(\mu=0.6).
$$
Thus the appropriate orders of magnitude are $10^{14}$–$10^{16}\,\mathrm{Pa}$ and $10^7\,\mathrm K$. The uniform-<density> approximation underestimates the actual <central pressure> because the Sun is centrally concentrated. <Radiation pressure> is neglected in applying the <ideal gas law>.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

A <free-fall timescale> estimate follows from $R/t^2\sim GM/R^2$:
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm dyn}\sim\sqrt{\frac{R_\odot^3}{GM_\odot}}\simeq1.6\times10^3\,\mathrm s.}
$$
Falling to a central <point mass> gives the more precise factor $\pi/(2\sqrt2)$, or $1.77\times10^3\,\mathrm s$; a distributed star changes an order-one coefficient. For any spherical <mass distribution> with radius $R$,
$$
|\Omega|=\int_0^M\frac{Gm}{r(m)}\,dm\ge\frac GR\int_0^M m\,dm=\frac{GM^2}{2R}.
$$
The inequality is strict for an extended star; equality is approached by a thin shell at $R$. For an <monatomic gas> the <virial theorem> is $2U+\Omega=0$, so half the <gravitational potential energy> released by contraction heats the star and half can be radiated. The <Kelvin-Helmholtz cooling time> is therefore of order
$$
\boxed{t_{\rm KH}\sim\frac{GM_\odot^2}{2R_\odot L_\odot}\simeq1.6\times10^7\,\mathrm{yr}.}
$$
A uniform-<density> model gives $3GM^2/(10RL)\simeq9.4\times10^6\,\mathrm{yr}$. The given bound fixes a scale, rather than an exact contraction history; both estimates are far shorter than the Sun's <main-sequence lifetime>.

The right panel of the figure in <past-exam-of-the-short-options-paper-shared-by-parts-a-and-b-of-the-physics-course-of-the-university-of-oxford/2019/s2/1/b/solution> has <luminosity> increasing upwards and <effective temperature> decreasing to the right. An old <globular cluster> contains a low-mass <main sequence>, a <main-sequence turnoff>, <subgiants>, a <red giant branch>, a <horizontal branch>, an <asymptotic giant branch> and a faint hot <white dwarf> sequence. Its high-mass <main sequence> is absent because those stars have evolved.

If a fraction $f_c=0.1$ of the star participates in <hydrogen burning>, with initial <hydrogen mass fraction> $X=0.7$, the available <nuclear binding energy> is $0.007f_cXM c^2$. Assuming a roughly constant <luminosity> and coeval stars,
$$
\boxed{t\simeq\frac{0.007f_cX(0.8M_\odot)c^2}{1.6\times10^{26}\,\mathrm W}\simeq1.39\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{yr}.}
$$
Taking the quoted core fraction as hydrogen fuel itself, $f_cX=0.1$, gives $1.98\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{yr}$. Stating the fuel convention matters: burning the whole stellar mass overestimates the age by about an order of magnitude. Evolving <luminosity> and detailed <stellar structure> are needed for an accurate age.

==== a

===== Solution

The <Milky Way> has a thin stellar <galactic disk>, radius roughly $15$–$20\,\mathrm{kpc}$ and scale height about $0.3\,\mathrm{kpc}$, containing gas, dust, young and old stars, <spiral arms> and most current <star formation>. Its stellar mass is a few $10^{10}M_\odot$. A thicker, older <galactic disk> has scale height around $1\,\mathrm{kpc}$ and stellar mass of order $10^{10}M_\odot$, with little cold gas.

The central <galactic bulge> and bar extend a few thousand <parsecs> and contain about $10^{10}M_\odot$, largely older stars. The central <supermassive black hole> has mass about $4\times10^6M_\odot$ and is negligible in the total galactic budget. A diffuse old metal-poor <stellar halo>, extending tens to over $100\,\mathrm{kpc}$, contains <globular clusters> and roughly $10^9M_\odot$ in stars. An extended <dark matter halo> reaches roughly $200\,\mathrm{kpc}$ and dominates the total mass, of order $10^{12}M_\odot$. These are approximate component scales; the gaseous <galactic halo> also extends beyond the stellar <galactic disk>.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

Treat the <gravitational field> as approximately spherical. The <circular orbit> condition gives
$$
\boxed{M(<8\,\mathrm{kpc})=\frac{v_c^2r}{G}=9.0\times10^{10}M_\odot,\qquad \Upsilon=\frac{M/M_\odot}{L/L_\odot}=4.50.}
$$
If every star had a representative mass $m$ and obeyed $L/L_\odot=(m/M_\odot)^3$, its <mass-to-light ratio> would be $(m/M_\odot)^{-2}$, giving $\boxed{m\simeq0.47M_\odot}$. This is a one-mass estimate, not an actual average inferred uniquely from the data: a <galactic stellar mass function>, stellar remnants, dust and nonstellar mass alter the relation. The flattening of the <galactic disk> also changes the spherical conversion by an order-one factor.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Assume an isotropic, stationary spherical <galaxy cluster>. Its three-dimensional <velocity dispersion> obeys $\langle v^2\rangle=3\sigma_{\rm los}^2$. For a uniform sphere, the <virial theorem> gives $3M\sigma_{\rm los}^2=3GM^2/(5R)$, hence
$$
\boxed{M_{\rm dyn}\simeq\frac{5R\sigma_{\rm los}^2}{G}=9.4\times10^{14}M_\odot.}
$$
A different <density profile> changes the coefficient, so $10^{15}M_\odot$ is the meaningful scale. For isothermal <X-ray> gas in <hydrostatic equilibrium>,
$$
M(r)=-\frac{k_BTr}{G\mu m_p}\frac{d\ln\rho_g}{d\ln r}.
$$
Using $\mu=0.6$ and a representative slope $-d\ln\rho_g/d\ln r=2$–$3$ at $1\,\mathrm{Mpc}$ gives $M_X\simeq(1.3$–$1.9)\times10^{15}M_\odot$. The <temperature> and extent alone cannot fix this coefficient without a <density profile>. The total <luminosity> is $5\times10^{12}L_\odot$, so the previously inferred <mass-to-light ratio> gives
$$
\boxed{M_\star\simeq4.50(5\times10^{12})M_\odot=2.25\times10^{13}M_\odot.}
$$

{scope}

===== Solution

Three physically distinct contributions are unseen <dark matter>, hot diffuse <intracluster gas> omitted from the summed stellar <mass>, and faint stars or <stellar remnants> missed by transferring the <Milky Way> <mass-to-light ratio> to the cluster. The first two are actual additional components; the third changes the inferred stellar budget. Departures from <virial equilibrium>, <velocity anisotropy> or extra nonthermal <pressure> can also bias the dynamical or <X-ray> estimates, although they need not remove the discrepancy.

===== Solution

Define the <canonical momentum> $p_i=\partial L/\partial\dot q_i$ and the <energy function> $E=\sum_i p_i\dot q_i-L$. Along an <Euler-Lagrange equation> solution,
$$
\frac{dE}{dt}=\sum_i\left(\dot p_i-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}\right)\dot q_i-\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}.
$$
Thus an autonomous <Lagrangian> has conserved $E$. For a <simple harmonic oscillator>, $L=m\dot x^2/2-kx^2/2$ and $\boxed{E=m\dot x^2/2+kx^2/2}$ is constant. For ordinary time-independent mechanical coordinates this is the total <mechanical energy>.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

The appropriate invariance is invariance of the <action> element $L\,dt$, including the change of the time measure. Under $\delta t=\lambda\phi$ and $\delta q_i=\lambda\psi_i$, <differentiation> of $q_i'(t')$ gives $\delta\dot q_i=\lambda(D_t\psi_i-\dot q_iD_t\phi)$. Therefore
$$
\frac{\delta(L\,dt)}{\lambda\,dt}=L_t\phi+\sum_iL_{q_i}\psi_i+\sum_i p_i(D_t\psi_i-\dot q_iD_t\phi)+L D_t\phi.
$$
Using the <Euler-Lagrange equations> and $D_tE=-L_t$, this becomes
$$
D_t\left[\sum_i p_i\psi_i-E\phi\right].
$$
An <action symmetry> therefore yields the <Noether charge>
$$
\boxed{Q=\sum_i p_i\psi_i+\left(L-\sum_i p_i\dot q_i\right)\phi,\qquad D_tQ=0.}
$$
For $\phi=0$ this reduces to the usual spatial form of <Noether's theorem>. The same on-shell calculation permits velocity-dependent generators when they define a legitimate variational <symmetry>. Merely preserving the numerical value of $L$ is insufficient for transformations of time: the <Jacobian matrix and determinant> $dt'/dt$ matters. If the <action> changes by a boundary term $\lambda D_tB$, the conserved quantity is $Q-B$.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

For a <central potential> use $L=m|\dot{\mathbf r}|^2/2-V(|\mathbf r|)$. An infinitesimal <rotation> about any constant unit <vector> $\mathbf n$ has $\phi=0$ and $\boldsymbol\psi=\mathbf n\times\mathbf r$. <Noether's theorem> gives $Q=m\dot{\mathbf r}\cdot(\mathbf n\times\mathbf r)=\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf L$, where $\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times m\dot{\mathbf r}$. Since $\mathbf n$ is arbitrary, every component of <angular momentum> is conserved. A <time-translation symmetry> has $\phi=1$, $\boldsymbol\psi=0$, giving $Q=-E$ and
$$
\boxed{\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times m\dot{\mathbf r},\qquad E=\frac12m|\dot{\mathbf r}|^2+V(r)}
$$
as conserved quantities.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

The orbiting planet produces a potential $V(r,\vartheta,\varphi-\Omega t)$. It has explicit time dependence and exerts an azimuthal <torque>, so generally $\dot E=V_t$ and $\dot L_z=-V_\varphi$ are nonzero. Nevertheless a simultaneous <time-translation symmetry> $\delta t=\lambda$ and <rotation> $\delta\varphi=\Omega\lambda$ leaves the <Lagrangian> and $dt$ unchanged. <Noether's theorem> gives $Q=\Omega p_\varphi-E$ and hence
$$
\boxed{E_J=E-\Omega L_z=\text{constant}.}
$$
Equivalently $V_t=-\Omega V_\varphi$ implies $d(E-\Omega L_z)/dt=0$. This <Jacobi integral> assumes a rigidly rotating potential with constant $\Omega$ and negligible back-reaction from the <test particle>.

==== a

===== Solution

Write the geometric <vector> as $\mathbf V=\sum_iV_i'\mathbf e_i'(t)$. A rotating orthonormal basis obeys $\dot{\mathbf e}_i'=\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf e_i'$. The <product rule> gives the transport identity
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{d\mathbf V}{dt}\right)_{\rm inertial}=\left(\frac{d\mathbf V}{dt}\right)_{\rm rotating}+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf V.}
$$
Both terms must be expressed in the same basis before adding them. If $R(t)$ maps rotating components into inertial components, the precise component equation is $d(R\mathbf V')/dt=R(\dot{\mathbf V}'+\boldsymbol\Omega'\times\mathbf V')$. The printed expression suppresses this basis conversion.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

The inertial <Lagrangian> is $L=m|\dot{\mathbf r}|^2/2-U(\mathbf r,t)$. Put $\mathbf r=R(t)\mathbf r'$ and define $U'(\mathbf r',t)=U(R(t)\mathbf r',t)$. The transport identity and invariance of the <Euclidean norm> under a <rotation> give
$$
\boxed{L'=\frac m2|\dot{\mathbf r}'+\boldsymbol\Omega'\times\mathbf r'|^2-U'(\mathbf r',t).}
$$
Its expansion contains the ordinary <kinetic energy>, $m\dot{\mathbf r}'\cdot(\boldsymbol\Omega'\times\mathbf r')$, and $m|\boldsymbol\Omega'\times\mathbf r'|^2/2$. These velocity and position terms generate the apparent forces in the rotating frame.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

Suppress primes on the angular <velocity> while retaining rotating components. The <canonical momentum> is $\partial L'/\partial\dot{\mathbf r}'=m(\dot{\mathbf r}'+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r')$. Varying $L'$ with respect to position and applying the <Euler-Lagrange equations> gives
$$
\boxed{m\ddot{\mathbf r}'=-\nabla' U'-2m\boldsymbol\Omega\times\dot{\mathbf r}'-m\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r')-m\dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}\times\mathbf r'.}
$$
The terms are the physical <force>, the velocity-dependent <Coriolis force>, the outward <centrifugal force>, and the <Euler force> caused by changing angular <velocity>. The common origin is inertial; an accelerating origin would introduce another translational apparent <force>.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

At <latitude> $\ell$, the Earth's angular <velocity> in east–north–up coordinates is $\boldsymbol\Omega=(0,\Omega\cos\ell,\Omega\sin\ell)$. Assume a small horizontal patch, constant $\Omega$, negligible vertical <velocity>, and a vertical constraint balancing effective <gravity>. Absorb the steady <centrifugal force> into that effective <gravity>. With $f=2\Omega\sin\ell$ and physical horizontal <force> per unit mass $F_x',F_y'$, the <Coriolis force> gives
$$
\boxed{\ddot x'=f\dot y'+F_x',\qquad\ddot y'=-f\dot x'+F_y',\quad A=D=0,\ B=f,\ C=-f.}
$$
Large-scale atmospheric and oceanic flows deflect to their right in the northern hemisphere and left in the southern hemisphere; their balance with a horizontal <pressure gradient> produces <geostrophic flow>. A <Foucault pendulum> has an oscillation plane that precesses relative to the ground at angular rate $-\Omega\sin\ell$. Both effects vanish at the equator within this horizontal approximation.

==== e
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $m$ be the ice mass for which the stated drag coefficients are defined, $K=k_1+k_2$ and $f=2\Omega\sin\ell$. With stationary water, the steady horizontal ice equations are
$$
0=mf v_y+k_1v_{a,x}-Kv_x,\qquad0=-mf v_x+k_1v_{a,y}-Kv_y.
$$
The <linear system> gives
$$
v_y=\frac{k_1}{K^2+(mf)^2}\left(Kv_{a,y}-mf v_{a,x}\right).
$$
For fixed wind speed $V=10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> minimizes this component when
$$

The <Scientific Revolution> is a useful name for linked transformations in European <natural philosophy> during roughly the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, rather than a single event with an agreed beginning or end. Its strongest scientific content is a change in how motion, the heavens and natural phenomena were represented and investigated: increasingly mathematical accounts, new instruments, controlled experiments and public argument altered what could count as a persuasive explanation. The conventional span from <Nicolaus Copernicus> in 1543 to <Isaac Newton> in 1687 captures important developments, but excludes relevant earlier work and later consolidation.

In <astronomy>, the <heliocentric model> changed the arrangement of the planets without initially discarding all older mathematical devices. <Johannes Kepler>'s <elliptic orbits> and empirical <Kepler's laws of planetary motion> then displaced uniform circular motion as a universal premise. Telescopic observations associated with <Galileo Galilei>, including the phases of Venus and satellites of Jupiter, challenged specific inherited cosmological claims. Their significance depended on arguments that the <telescope> reliably revealed celestial phenomena, not simply on looking through an uncontroversial window.

In <mechanics>, idealized mathematical treatments of falling bodies and projectiles helped establish quantitative relations between motion and causes. <Newton's laws of motion> and <Newton's law of universal gravitation> linked terrestrial falling bodies with celestial <orbits> in a single framework. The importance of this synthesis lay in detailed quantitative problems, such as the relation between inverse-square <force> and planetary <motion>, not merely in announcing that mathematics applied to nature.<Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica by Isaac Newton>[Books I and III, mathematical motion and the system of the world]{ref}

Experimental practices changed alongside theory. Air-pump work associated with <Robert Boyle> made carefully described apparatus, witnessed trials and replication central to disputes about air and the <vacuum>. <Francis Bacon> promoted organized inquiry, while <René Descartes> pursued a mathematical and mechanical account of nature; their differences caution against inventing a single timeless “scientific method”. Scientific societies, correspondence, printed diagrams and journals helped stabilize results and reputations beyond an individual's laboratory.

The term remains selective. Mathematics, craft knowledge, instruments and observations had long histories, including substantial contributions beyond Europe. Older and newer explanatory styles coexisted; natural philosophers did not suddenly become present-day professional scientists. Religious, courtly and commercial settings often supported the same work later narrated as a victory over tradition. The <Scientific Revolution> therefore describes a consequential reorganization of scientific questions, practices and authority, while requiring care about geographical scope, continuity and the uneven pace of change.

{scope}

==== Solution

Doubt was reasonable if it concerned the physical cause or unlimited extrapolation of <Newton's law of universal gravitation>; wholesale dismissal of the quantitative evidence was harder to justify. Early eighteenth-century investigators had to distinguish the success of an inverse-square <force law> in explaining observations from the claim that every material body directly attracts every other one without a mediating mechanism.

The strongest case for <Isaac Newton>'s framework was its unification of phenomena previously treated separately. <Kepler's third law> and nearly <elliptic orbits> could be connected to an inverse-square central <force>. Comparing terrestrial <gravity> with the <centripetal acceleration> of the Moon supported continuity between falling bodies and celestial <motion>. The <Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica by Isaac Newton> also developed treatments of interacting bodies, <tides> and the Earth's figure. These were substantive tests and organized a research programme capable of producing further quantitative comparisons.<Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica by Isaac Newton>[Book III, propositions on planetary forces, lunar gravity and universal attraction]{ref}

There were nevertheless rational reservations. A law proportional to $m_1m_2/r^2$ inferred from accessible bodies and distances did not logically establish unrestricted universality. Individual celestial predictions involved uncertain observations and approximations to difficult many-body <classical mechanics>. The Moon's motion and the Earth's shape were demanding tests whose successful treatment was not complete at the beginning of the century. A dissenter could question auxiliary assumptions, numerical accuracy or the inference from special cases without denying every success.

A deeper objection concerned explanation. In traditions requiring contact mechanisms, unexplained attraction across empty space appeared to reintroduce an occult quality. <René Descartes>'s vortices supplied a mechanical alternative, though they faced their own serious dynamical difficulties. <Newton> himself separated a mathematically established pattern from a claimed cause of <gravity>; the 1713 <General Scholium by Isaac Newton> declined to invent an unsupported physical mechanism. Treating every criticism of action at a distance as a failure to understand evidence would therefore misrepresent the available positions.<General Scholium by Isaac Newton>[1713 discussion of gravitational cause and the evidential status of hypotheses]{ref}

Later confirmations cannot be counted as evidence already possessed by an early-eighteenth-century critic. Subsequent geodetic measurements, improved lunar theory and successful comet predictions helped shift the balance, but their timing matters. Nor did retaining a mechanical alternative remain equally reasonable after every failed prediction: responsible doubt required comparative assessment and willingness to revise it. The balanced conclusion is that selective, explicit scepticism about universality or cause was defensible, while the breadth of <Newtonian mechanics>' quantitative achievements already imposed a substantial explanatory burden on rival accounts.

==== Solution

<Sadi Carnot>'s ideal engine was deeply indebted to practical <heat engines> as a source of questions and physical constraints, while its decisive result depended on deliberate idealization. In 1824 steam power was an established technology, and variations in engine economy made the conversion of heat into useful <work> a problem with immediate industrial meaning. Comparing different working substances and operating temperatures required a theory more general than the description of one machine.

Real engines supplied the essential architecture: a working fluid, heating from a hot source, expansion producing <work>, cooling or condensation, and return to an initial condition. Their losses also suggested what an ideal comparison had to remove. Friction, leakage, finite temperature differences during heat exchange and incomplete expansion waste useful performance. The idea of obtaining more work by using expansion rather than simply discarding pressure connected the argument directly to engineering experience. <Sadi Carnot>'s opening discussion explicitly places the inquiry in the context of the steam engine's practical power.<Réflexions sur la puissance motrice du feu et sur les machines propres à développer cette puissance by Sadi Carnot>[Opening discussion of steam power and the idealized sequence of heating, expansion and cooling]{ref}

The theoretical advance was to replace those irreversible operations by a closed <thermodynamic cycle>. In its modern description the <Carnot cycle> contains an <isothermal expansion> at $T_h$, an <adiabatic expansion>, an <isothermal compression> at $T_c$, and an <adiabatic compression>. The fluid returns to its initial state, so the result can be compared repeatedly rather than consuming a hidden initial store. Reversibility permits an engine to be run backwards and paired with another engine, providing a powerful limiting argument independent of detailed construction.

It is essential not to attribute the whole modern interpretation to 1824. <Sadi Carnot> reasoned within a <caloric theory> in which heat was conserved and motive power was associated with its passage from hot to cold, rather like a fall between levels. The modern <first law of thermodynamics> instead gives $W=Q_h-Q_c$, and the reversible <entropy> balance gives $Q_h/T_h=Q_c/T_c$. Thus the modern result
$$
\eta_{\rm rev}=1-\frac{T_c}{T_h}
$$
expresses a lasting structure of his argument in a later thermodynamic language; it should not be presented as his original derivation with contemporary concepts.

Actual engines therefore did more than decorate an abstract proof: they provided the phenomenon to explain, the operating ingredients and the direction in which improvements were sought. Yet no real engine executes a perfectly reversible <Carnot cycle>; finite power requires finite rates, and practical machines face material and transfer constraints. The engine is most valuable as a comparative upper limit and an argument for universality. Its historical interest lies in extracting such a general limit from engineering knowledge using an initially mistaken theory of heat, not in either pure deduction without machinery or mere generalization of workshop measurements.

==== Solution

Between about 1880 and 1920 a recognizable investigation of subatomic entities emerged from work on electrical discharge, radiation and <atomic model>. Calling it <particle physics> is retrospective: it was not yet the later high-energy specialization with accelerators, and boundaries with <atomic physics>, <nuclear physics> and chemistry remained fluid. Its formation nevertheless had both scientific and institutional causes.

Better vacuum pumps, discharge tubes, high voltages, photographic plates and sensitive electrical instruments made new phenomena experimentally reproducible. <Cathode rays> could be deflected by electric and magnetic fields, letting investigators compare their <charge-to-mass ratio> under different conditions. In 1897 <Joseph John Thomson> argued that their carriers were common constituents of matter with far smaller mass than an atom. This made a divisible atom an experimental research problem rather than simply a speculative possibility.<Cathode Rays by Joseph John Thomson>[1897 electric and magnetic deflection measurements and inference of a common corpuscular carrier]{ref}

<Wilhelm Röntgen>'s <X-rays>, <Henri Becquerel>'s spontaneous uranium radiation and the work of <Marie Curie> and <Pierre Curie> on strongly radioactive substances revealed additional emissions and transformations. Their classification by penetration, charge and deflection separated physically different entities. <Radioactive decay> introduced characteristic lifetimes and suggested that atoms could change identity. Chemical separation and quantitative electrical measurements were therefore as important as a purely mechanical picture of particles.<Radium and the New Concepts in Chemistry by Marie Curie>[1911 account of radium as an element and research on radioactive substances]{ref}

<Rutherford scattering> made the atom's internal structure accessible to an incisive quantitative probe. <Hans Geiger> and <Ernest Marsden> measured rare large deflections, and <Ernest Rutherford>'s 1911 analysis connected them to a concentrated nuclear charge rather than a diffuse one. The experimental task involved a collimated source, thin target foils and detection of individual scintillations at different angles; changing thickness and angle tested explanations of single versus multiple <scattering>. This style of inferring invisible structure from statistical distributions became characteristic of the developing field.<The Scattering of Alpha and Beta Particles by Matter and the Structure of the Atom by Ernest Rutherford>[1911 central-charge scattering model and comparison with large-angle observations]{ref}

Specialization also depended on laboratories capable of maintaining apparatus, obtaining rare sources, training assistants and students, and circulating standardized measurements. Research schools sustained linked problems across many experiments; new journals, meetings and prizes gave their practitioners a recognizable identity. Industry supplied pumps and electrical equipment, while medical interest in radiation supplied additional demand and resources. These conditions help explain why the specialization consolidated in that period rather than emerging automatically from one discovery.

The date range should not imply completion. By 1920 the <electron>, nuclear <electric charge>, radioactivity and early nuclear transformations had established a new scale of questions, but the <neutron>, mature <quantum mechanics> and accelerator-based high-energy research still lay ahead. The emerging specialty was the joint product of experimentally identifiable new objects, a distinctive toolkit and institutions able to make their investigation a continuing career.

=== 5
{scope}

==== Solution

Practising scientists can learn how evidence, instruments and disciplinary assumptions acquire authority, provided history is treated as a critical investigation rather than a collection of motivational stories. The useful question is not whether the past supplies a universal recipe for discovery, but which specific habits of reasoning become visible when earlier problems are reconstructed with the evidence their participants actually possessed.

First, history can discipline judgements about evidence. Early critics of <Newton's law of universal gravitation> faced a genuine distinction between a successful <force law> and an unexplained physical cause. Reconstructing that distinction discourages equating disagreement with stupidity. Conversely, the quantitative successes of <Newtonian mechanics> show that scepticism must answer the best available evidence, rather than invoking general uncertainty indefinitely. The transferable lesson is to separate measured regularities, modelling assumptions and causal interpretation.

Second, experiments have histories of reliability. <Cathode-ray> measurements required vacuum control, calibrated fields and competing accounts of what a discharge tube produced. <Rutherford scattering> required sensitive counting, geometrical control and a statistical interpretation of rare events. These examples make instrument performance and data selection part of the argument rather than invisible background. For current practice this supports explicit calibration, reporting uncertainty, checking alternative explanations and distinguishing a missing event from a detector's failure to register it.

Third, productive models can rest on concepts later replaced. <Sadi Carnot>'s reasoning retained an enduring reversible-engine comparison while using <caloric theory>. This encourages scientists to identify which conclusions depend on which premises, instead of accepting or rejecting a model as an indivisible whole. It does not mean false assumptions are harmless: their usefulness has to be established for particular questions and tested limits. Likewise an elegant idealization is not automatically a workable machine.

Fourth, the organization of work matters. Shared apparatus, trained technicians, correspondence and laboratory traditions affected which observations became possible and credible. Historical reconstruction can recover contributions obscured by a single-discoverer narrative and reveal why a method that worked in one laboratory failed elsewhere. Scientists can use that awareness to improve documentation, replication and credit without treating social context as a substitute for empirical evaluation.

There are limits. Historical episodes are not controlled experiments on “the scientific method”; selecting only successful cases produces <survivorship bias>, and later knowledge can make a discovery appear inevitable. Neither a slogan about revolutions nor an analogy with a famous controversy settles a present scientific dispute. Practical lessons need to be modest and specific: track assumptions, preserve evidence, inspect instruments, compare rival explanations fairly and remain alert to unrecorded work. On those terms the <history of science> offers intellectual tools for better scientific judgement, while leaving present claims answerable to present evidence.

A strictly positive gravitational <density> violates this condition: summing the update gives $\bar\Phi^{n+1}=\bar\Phi^n-4\pi G\Delta\tau\bar\rho$, an indefinitely drifting mean. Periodic gravitational calculations normally solve for $\rho-\bar\rho$ and fix the arbitrary mean <gravitational potential>. The per-step <matrix> $A$ itself remains invertible; the issue is existence of a periodic steady solution of <Poisson's equation>.

==== f
{scope}

===== Solution

For an error <Fourier mode> $e_j^n=\widehat e^n e^{i\theta j}$, the periodic <discrete Laplacian> has eigenvalue $-4\sin^2(\theta/2)/h^2$. The <backward Euler method> therefore has amplification factor
$$
\boxed{g(\theta)=\frac1{1+4r\sin^2(\theta/2)},\qquad0<g\le1\quad(r\ge0).}
$$
<Von Neumann stability analysis> thus imposes no upper limit on positive $\Delta\tau$. The constant mode has $g=1$ and is not damped; compatible forcing and a gauge condition remain necessary as discussed in part (e). Large steps can accelerate relaxation of nonconstant modes, but require a suitably accurate <linear solver> and can worsen its conditioning. Unconditional linear stability is not a statement about time accuracy or cost.

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

Put $y^{(0)}=y$ and $y^{(1)}=\dot y$. Then
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dt}\begin{pmatrix}y^{(0)}\\y^{(1)}\end{pmatrix}
=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-k^2&-2\gamma\end{pmatrix}}_{M}
\begin{pmatrix}y^{(0)}\\y^{(1)}\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The second row is the original <damped harmonic oscillator> equation and the first row defines the auxiliary <velocity>. This converts a second-order <ordinary differential equation> into two first-order equations without changing the required two initial values.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

A numerical evolution has <numerical stability> if small perturbations in initial data, forcing or arithmetic are not amplified without control over the time interval of interest. For this constant-coefficient decaying <linear system>, an absolute-stability test requires the homogeneous numerical amplification factors to have magnitude at most one, with no growing Jordan-block contribution on the unit circle. Stability is distinct from accuracy: a strongly overdamped numerical solution can be stable while representing the physical <oscillation> poorly.

==== c
{scope}

===== Solution

The characteristic <polynomial> is $\lambda^2+2\gamma\lambda+k^2=0$. Since $0<\gamma<|k|$,
$$
\lambda_\pm=-\gamma\pm i\sqrt{k^2-\gamma^2}.
$$
Forward <Euler method> has amplification eigenvalues $g_\pm=1+h\lambda_\pm$. Their squared modulus is
$$
|g_\pm|^2=(1-\gamma h)^2+(k^2-\gamma^2)h^2=1-2\gamma h+k^2h^2.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{0\le h\le\frac{2\gamma}{k^2}.}
$$
Strict inequality gives numerical damping; at the upper boundary the amplitude is constant despite physical decay, so that boundary is a poor accuracy choice. In the undamped limit $\gamma=0$, $|g|^2=1+k^2h^2>1$ for every $h>0$: forward <Euler method> is unstable for any nonzero step on an undamped <harmonic oscillator>.

==== d
{scope}

===== Solution

Backward <Euler method> has update $\mathbf y^{n+1}=(I-hM)^{-1}\mathbf y^n$ and amplification eigenvalues $g_\pm=(1-h\lambda_\pm)^{-1}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{|g_\pm|^2=\frac1{(1+\gamma h)^2+(k^2-\gamma^2)h^2}=\frac1{1+2\gamma h+k^2h^2}\le1}
$$
for every $h\ge0$. It is therefore unconditionally stable for this <linear system>. Even with $\gamma=0$, it damps every nonzero numerical mode: stability is obtained at the cost of artificial damping and phase error. Large $h$ does not accurately resolve the oscillation period.

==== e
{scope}

===== Solution

An <explicit numerical method> normally has a simple, inexpensive step and needs no simultaneous solve, but stability can force very small steps, especially for <stiff differential equations>. An <implicit numerical method> requires a linear or nonlinear solve, storage and suitable solver tolerances; its larger stability region can permit larger steps when stability is the main restriction. Accuracy, phase fidelity and preservation of physical structure still constrain the step. For this <oscillator>, backward <Euler method> is stable but dissipative, while forward <Euler method> is cheap but unstable without damping. Neither statement makes all implicit methods superior to all explicit ones; method order, problem structure and total cost determine the useful choice.
