Solutions to all three questions.<Information theory examination paper A16551W1 (2023)>[Printed pp. 2–4, Questions 1–3]{ref} All <logarithms> are base two; <information entropy> is measured in <bits> and <code rates> in <bits> per <channel> use. Terms $0\log 0$ are zero. Question 3(b) contains an incorrect hint; its requested conclusion is proved directly below for deterministic <channel codes>. The repeat-until-success protocol in Question 3(c) requires $q<1$ to terminate.

== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

**The inequality is true** for ordinary addition, and also for addition in a <group>. For any fixed $y$, the translation $x\mapsto x+y$ is <injective>, so it merely relabels the <probability masses> in the <conditional distribution>. Since $X,Y$ are <independent random variables>,
$$
H(X+Y\mid Y=y)=H(X\mid Y=y)=H(X).
$$
Averaging over $y$ and using <conditioning reduces entropy> proves <entropy monotonicity under independent addition>:
$$
\boxed{H(X+Y)\geq H(X+Y\mid Y)=H(X).}
$$
There is no requirement that $X+Y$ remain in the original <finite alphabet>. The proof also gives $H(X+Y)\geq H(Y)$ by symmetry. Independence matters: for a nonconstant $X$ and $Y=-X$, the sum is constant while $H(X)>0$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $N=|\mathcal X|\geq2$ and let $E=\mathbf1_{\{X\ne Y\}}$ be the error <indicator random variable>. Because $E$ is determined by $(X,Y)$, the <chain rule for conditional entropy> in its two orders gives
$$
H(X\mid Y)=H(X,E\mid Y)=H(E\mid Y)+H(X\mid E,Y).
$$
By <conditioning reduces entropy>, $H(E\mid Y)\leq H(E)$. On $E=0$, knowing $Y$ determines $X$, so the corresponding <conditional entropy> is zero. On $E=1$, at most $N-1$ symbols remain possible for $X$. The maximum <Shannon entropy> of a <probability distribution> on at most $N-1$ symbols is $\log(N-1)$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{H(X\mid Y)\leq H(\mathbf1_{\{X\ne Y\}})+\Pr(X\ne Y)\log(N-1).}
$$
For an arbitrary <function> $g:\mathcal X\to\mathcal X$, repeat the argument with $E_g=\mathbf1_{\{X\ne g(Y)\}}$. This is still determined by $(X,Y)$; when it is zero, $X=g(Y)$, and when it is one, $X$ has at most $N-1$ possibilities for each $Y$. Thus <Fano's inequality> gives
$$
\boxed{H(X\mid Y)\leq H(\mathbf1_{\{X\ne g(Y)\}})+\Pr(X\ne g(Y))\log(N-1).}
$$
Events of zero <probability> contribute zero to the conditional average, covering the error <probabilities> zero and one. If $N=1$, the left side and error <probabilities> are zero; the trivial conclusion is handled separately instead of writing $0\log0$ as an ordinary finite product.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For the <entropy summand> $f(x)=-x\log x$, differentiation on $(0,1)$ gives
$$
f'(x)=-\frac{\ln x+1}{\ln2},\qquad f''(x)=-\frac1{x\ln2}<0.
$$
Since $f(x)\to0$ as $x\downarrow0$, its continuous extension to $[0,1]$ is a <concave function>.

Fix $0<v<1$ and define the increment $D_v(t)=f(t+v)-f(t)$ on $[0,1-v]$. In its interior,
$$
D_v'(t)=f'(t+v)-f'(t)=-\log\frac{t+v}{t}<0.
$$
Thus the increment is decreasing, and its endpoint values are $D_v(0)=f(v)$ and $D_v(1-v)=-f(1-v)$. Therefore
$$
-f(1-v)\leq f(t+v)-f(t)\leq f(v),
\qquad
\boxed{|f(t+v)-f(t)|\leq\max\{f(v),f(1-v)\}.}
$$
The cases $v=0,1$ follow directly from the endpoint values.

To obtain the sharper half-interval bound, put $G(v)=f(v)-f(1-v)$. It has $G(0)=G(1/2)=0$, while
$$
G''(v)=\frac{2v-1}{v(1-v)\ln2}\leq0\qquad(0<v\leq1/2).
$$
Hence $G$ is a <concave function> on $[0,1/2]$ and lies above the chord joining its zero endpoints. Thus $f(v)\geq f(1-v)$ there, proving
$$
\boxed{|f(t+v)-f(t)|\leq f(v)\qquad(0\leq v\leq1/2).}
$$

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Set $N=|\mathcal X|$, $d_i=|p_i-q_i|$ and $d=\sum_i d_i=\|p-q\|_1$. If $d=0$, the two <probability mass functions> agree and the bound is zero. Otherwise every $d_i\leq d\leq1/2$. The half-interval <entropy summand> bound, with $t=\min(p_i,q_i)$ and $v=d_i$, gives
$$
|f(p_i)-f(q_i)|\leq f(d_i).
$$
By the <triangle inequality>, followed by <Jensen's inequality> for the <concave function> $f$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
|H(p)-H(q)|
&\leq\sum_{i=1}^N|f(p_i)-f(q_i)|
\leq\sum_{i=1}^Nf(d_i)\\
&\leq Nf\left(\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^Nd_i\right)
=-d\log\frac dN.
\end{aligned}
$$
This proves the requested <finite-alphabet entropy continuity bound>:
$$
\boxed{|H(p)-H(q)|\leq-\|p-q\|_1\log\left(\frac{\|p-q\|_1}{|\mathcal X|}\right).}
$$

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <independent and identically distributed> hypothesis factors the <joint probability mass function>:
$$
p_n(X_1,\ldots,X_n)=\prod_{i=1}^np(X_i),\qquad
\frac1n\log p_n(X_1,\ldots,X_n)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\log p(X_i).
$$
Symbols outside the <support of a probability distribution> $p$ never occur, almost surely. On its finite positive <probability support>, $\log p(X_i)$ is bounded, so the <strong law of large numbers> applies:
$$
\boxed{\frac1n\log p_n(X_1,\ldots,X_n)\longrightarrow
\sum_{x:p(x)>0}p(x)\log p(x)=-H(p)\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$
This is the <asymptotic equipartition property> with the sign appropriate to a <logarithm> of a <probability mass>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For a fixed estimated <probability mass function> $q$, the estimated <joint probability mass function> is $q_n(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=\prod_iq(x_i)$. If $q(x)>0$ whenever $p(x)>0$, the <strong law of large numbers> gives the <mismatched asymptotic equipartition property>:
$$
\boxed{\frac1n\log q_n(X_1,\ldots,X_n)\longrightarrow
\sum_{x:p(x)>0}p(x)\log q(x)
=-H(p,q)=-H(p)-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q)
\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$
Here $H(p,q)$ is <cross-entropy>, and the last equality is <cross-entropy equals entropy plus relative entropy>. The <Kullback-Leibler divergence> measures the extra average <logarithmic loss> from the estimated law.

If $q(x)=0$ for some $x$ with $p(x)>0$, let $a=\sum_{x:q(x)=0}p(x)>0$. The <probability> that none of the first $n$ observations belongs to this set is $(1-a)^n\to0$. Almost surely such a symbol eventually occurs, after which $q_n(X_1,\ldots,X_n)=0$ forever. Thus the limit is **$-\infty$**, consistent with infinite <cross-entropy> and infinite <Kullback-Leibler divergence>.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For every $x^n\in A^{n,\varepsilon}$, the defining <typical set> constraint implies
$$
2^{-n(H(p)+\varepsilon)}\leq p_n(x^n)\leq2^{-n(H(p)-\varepsilon)}.
$$
Let $S_n=A^{n,\varepsilon}\cap B^{n,\varepsilon}$. Summing the lower <probability mass> bound over $S_n$ gives
$$
1\geq\Pr(X^n\in S_n)
=\sum_{x^n\in S_n}p_n(x^n)
\geq |S_n|2^{-n(H(p)+\varepsilon)}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{|A^{n,\varepsilon}\cap B^{n,\varepsilon}|\leq2^{n(H(p)+\varepsilon)}.}
$$
The <sample mean> constraint can only shrink the <typical set>, so this <cardinality> upper bound holds for every positive integer $n$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For fixed $\varepsilon>0$, the <asymptotic equipartition property> gives $\Pr(X^n\notin A^{n,\varepsilon})\to0$. The <strong law of large numbers> applied to $X_i$, which are bounded because their numerical <alphabet> is finite, gives $\Pr(X^n\notin B^{n,\varepsilon})\to0$. The <union bound> therefore yields
$$
\Pr(X^n\in S_n)\geq1-\Pr(X^n\notin A^{n,\varepsilon})-\Pr(X^n\notin B^{n,\varepsilon})\longrightarrow1.
$$
Since $\delta>0$, eventually $\Pr(X^n\in S_n)\geq2^{-\delta}$. Each word in $S_n$ has <probability mass> at most $2^{-n(H(p)-\varepsilon)}$, so
$$
2^{-\delta}\leq\Pr(X^n\in S_n)
\leq |S_n|2^{-n(H(p)-\varepsilon)}.
$$
Rearranging proves
$$
\boxed{|A^{n,\varepsilon}\cap B^{n,\varepsilon}|\geq2^{n(H(p)-\varepsilon)-\delta}}
$$
for sufficiently large $n$. The key is that the factor $2^{-\delta}$ is a fixed number below one, however small $\delta$ is. This is an instance of <typical sets with an additional high-probability constraint>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

First, the binary translation of a quaternary <prefix code> is still a <prefix code>: every translated <codeword length> is even, and a binary prefix relation would split into complete two-<bit> blocks, giving a prefix relation between the original quaternary <codewords>. Binary <Huffman coding> is optimal among binary <prefix codes>, so
$$
\mathbb E|c_B(X_1)|\leq\mathbb E|c_{QB}(X_1)|.
$$
For the other direction, let $\ell_i=|c_B(x_i)|$ and round these binary <codeword lengths> to quaternary lengths $k_i=\lceil\ell_i/2\rceil$. The <Kraft inequality> gives
$$
\sum_i4^{-k_i}=\sum_i2^{-2\lceil\ell_i/2\rceil}
\leq\sum_i2^{-\ell_i}\leq1.
$$
The quaternary converse of the <Kraft inequality> supplies a quaternary <prefix code> with lengths $k_i$. Optimality of the <quaternary Huffman code> then implies
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E|c_{QB}(X_1)|
&=2\mathbb E|c_Q(X_1)|
\leq2\sum_i p_i\left\lceil\frac{\ell_i}2\right\rceil\\
&\leq\sum_i p_i(\ell_i+1)
=\mathbb E|c_B(X_1)|+1.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus **binary translation of an optimal quaternary code costs at most one extra bit per source symbol**:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E|c_B(X_1)|\leq\mathbb E|c_{QB}(X_1)|\leq\mathbb E|c_B(X_1)|+1.}
$$
The <quaternary Huffman code> uses the usual adjustment of adding zero-weight dummy leaves when needed. If the <alphabet> has one symbol, both optimal codes may use the empty <codeword>, giving the trivial endpoint.

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The specified transition law is a <binary erasure channel>. For an input <bit> with $\Pr(X=1)=a$, its <Shannon entropy> is the <binary entropy> $h_2(a)$. A non-erased output determines $X$, whereas the erasure symbol leaves its <probability distribution> unchanged because the erasure event is <independent> of $X$. Thus
$$
H(X\mid Y)=qH(X),\qquad I(X;Y)=(1-q)H(X)=(1-q)h_2(a).
$$
Maximizing the <mutual information> over the input <probability distribution>, $h_2(a)\leq1$ with equality at $a=1/2$, gives
$$
\boxed{C=1-q\quad\text{bits per channel use}.}
$$
This includes $q=0$, a noiseless binary <channel>, and $q=1$, a completely erased <channel> with zero <channel capacity>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the standard deterministic <channel encoder> and <channel decoder>, with $m$ message labels and <code rate> $R=(\log m)/n$. The <Fano's inequality> hint printed here replaces $\log(m-1)$ by $\log m-1$, which is false in general: take $X$ uniform on $\{0,1\}$ and $Y=2$ in the common <alphabet> $\{0,1,2\}$. The error <probability> is one and its <binary entropy> is zero, so the proposed bound asserts $1\leq\log3-1<1$. The correct <Fano's inequality> alone gives $(1-P_e)\log m\leq n(1-q)+h_2(P_e)$, which does not immediately give the requested additive $1/n$. The following <erasure-pattern counting bound for maximal decoding error> proves the displayed claim directly.

The case $m\leq2$ is immediate because $R\leq1/n$. Suppose $m>2$, put $L=\log m-1>0$, and let $w_0$ be the message that the <channel decoder> returns on the all-erased output. Choose $W$ uniformly among the other $m-1$ messages. Every one of these messages is decoded correctly with <probability> at least $1-\delta$, by the assumed <maximal decoding error probability> bound.

Let $S$ be the random set of unerased coordinates and $K=|S|$. For each fixed $S$ with $K=k\geq1$, at most $2^k$ output words are possible. A deterministic <channel decoder> can correctly identify at most one message per output word, so its conditional success <probability>, averaged over these $m-1$ messages, is at most $\min\{1,2^k/(m-1)\}$. When $K=0$, success is zero because $w_0$ was excluded.

For every integer $k\geq1$,
$$
\min\left\{1,\frac{2^k}{m-1}\right\}\leq\frac{k}{L}.
$$
If $k\geq L$, this is immediate. If $k<L$, write $r=m/2^k>2$. The elementary inequality $\log r\leq r-1$ for $r\geq2$ gives
$$
L=k-1+\log r\leq k+r-2\leq k(r-1)<\frac{k(m-1)}{2^k},
$$
where the middle inequality is $(k-1)(r-2)\geq0$. This proves the needed scalar bound.

Taking the <expectation> over erasure patterns, and using $\mathbb E K=n(1-q)$, yields
$$
1-\delta\leq\Pr(\widehat W=W)\leq\frac{\mathbb E K}{L}
=\frac{n(1-q)}{\log m-1}.
$$
Consequently the requested bound is valid:
$$
\boxed{R=\frac{\log m}{n}\leq\frac{1-q}{1-\delta}+\frac1n.}
$$
The use of <maximal decoding error probability> matters: excluding one message preserves the lower bound on success. The deterministic <channel decoder> convention also matters; allowing independent randomized guessing would invalidate this particular finite-length claim at $q=1$.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For $0\leq q<1$, use <repeat-until-success coding over an erasure channel>: **delete every erasure symbol from the received sequence and retain the non-erased bits in order**. Each message <bit> contributes a string of erasures followed by exactly one correct non-erased copy. The <channel encoder> advances to the next <bit> only after this successful copy, so the retained <bits> are precisely $W_1,\ldots,W_n$. Hence
$$
\boxed{d(Y)=W,\qquad\Pr(d(Y)\ne W)=0.}
$$
Indeed, $\Pr(M_j>k)=q^k\to0$, so each stage and the finite collection of $n$ stages terminate <almost surely>. The protocol uses <channel feedback>: Alice must know whether an erasure occurred before deciding whether to repeat. This feedback is implicit in the stated algorithm and is not counted as a forward <channel> use.

At $q=1$, no stage ever finishes, so there is **no terminating zero-error decoder for arbitrary messages**. This endpoint is excluded from the operational claim above.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $0\leq q<1$, the number of attempts has the <geometric distribution> with success <probability> $1-q$:
$$
\Pr(M_1=k)=q^{k-1}(1-q),\qquad k=1,2,\ldots.
$$
The <tail-sum formula for expectation> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E M_1=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Pr(M_1>k)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q^k=\frac1{1-q}.}
$$
At $q=0$, $M_1=1$ deterministically. At $q=1$, $M_1=+\infty$ and its <expectation> is $+\infty$, rather than a finite waiting time.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

For $0\leq q<1$, the total number of forward <channel> uses is $T_n=\sum_{j=1}^nM_j$, so the random <code rate> of a message with $n$ <bits> is
$$
R_n=\frac{n}{T_n}=\left(\frac1n\sum_{j=1}^nM_j\right)^{-1}.
$$
The <independent and identically distributed random variables> $M_j$ have finite <expectation> $1/(1-q)$. By the <strong law of large numbers>,
$$
\boxed{R_n\longrightarrow1-q\quad\text{almost surely}.}
$$
Thus the protocol reaches the <binary erasure channel> <channel capacity> in its asymptotic forward throughput while guaranteeing zero error on every completed message. Its random transmission length and use of <channel feedback> distinguish it from the fixed-length <channel codes> in part (b). Independent erasure indicators suffice for this calculation; the message <bits> themselves need not be <independent> or uniformly distributed. At $q=1$, the algorithm never produces a completed transmission, so $R_n$ as a completed-code rate is undefined; the physical throughput is zero.
