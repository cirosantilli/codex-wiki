== 1
{scope}

All <logarithms> have base two. In part a the <information entropy> differences and information expressions are interpreted where they are well defined; finite <joint entropy> is a sufficient hypothesis. This avoids undefined expressions such as infinity minus infinity.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <chain rule for conditional entropy> gives
$$
H(X,Y\mid Z)-H(X\mid Z)=H(Y\mid X,Z)\geq0.
$$
Indeed, for every conditioning value $(x,z)$ of positive <probability>, each summand $-p(y\mid x,z)\log_2p(y\mid x,z)$ is nonnegative. Its sum is zero exactly when the <conditional probability mass function> is concentrated at one value. Averaging over $(X,Z)$ therefore proves both the inequality and its equality criterion: **equality holds exactly when $Y=f(X,Z)$ <almost surely> for some function $f$.** Conditioning values of <probability> zero impose no restriction. This is a statement about <discrete random variables>; a <differential entropy> need not be nonnegative.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Expand the two observations in order using the <chain rule for mutual information>:
$$
I(X,Y;Z)=I(X;Z)+I(Y;Z\mid X).
$$
The remaining <conditional mutual information> is
$$
I(Y;Z\mid X)=\sum_xp_X(x)D\!\left(p_{Y,Z\mid x}\,\middle\|\,p_{Y\mid x}p_{Z\mid x}\right)\geq0.
$$
<Relative entropy nonnegativity> proves the inequality. Equality requires every positive-weight <relative entropy> to vanish, which is equivalent to $p(y,z\mid x)=p(y\mid x)p(z\mid x)$ for every $x$ of positive <probability>. Thus **equality holds exactly when $Y$ and $Z$ have <conditional independence> given $X$.** No unconditional <independence> is required.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <chain rule for information entropy> converts the two differences into <conditional entropies>. Their gap, in the order right minus left, is
$$
\begin{aligned}
&\bigl[H(X,Z)-H(X)\bigr]-\bigl[H(X,Y,Z)-H(X,Y)\bigr]\\
&\qquad=H(Z\mid X)-H(Z\mid X,Y)=I(Y;Z\mid X)\geq0.
\end{aligned}
$$
This is <additional conditioning reduces entropy>. The <relative entropy> representation of <conditional mutual information> identifies exactly when that gap vanishes: **equality holds if and only if $Y$ and $Z$ have <conditional independence> given $X$.** Knowledge of $Y$ then changes no <conditional probability> of $Z$ after $X$ is known.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

Here the asserted bound is actually **an identity, so equality always holds** whenever the displayed quantities are defined. Expand the same <mutual information> in two orders:
$$
\begin{aligned}
I(X,Y;Z)&=I(Y;Z)+I(X;Z\mid Y),\\
I(X,Y;Z)&=I(X;Z)+I(Y;Z\mid X).
\end{aligned}
$$
Equating these expressions and using <symmetry> of <mutual information> and <conditional mutual information> gives
$$
\boxed{I(X;Z\mid Y)=I(Z;Y\mid X)-I(Z;Y)+I(X;Z).}
$$
There is no further <independence> condition. Treating this part as a strict inequality would obscure its cancellation.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For <probability mass functions> on the <finite alphabet>, take <logarithms> to base two and define the <relative entropy> by
$$
\boxed{D(q\|p)=\sum_{i=1}^mq_i\log_2\frac{q_i}{p_i}.}
$$
A term with $q_i=0$ is zero, including $p_i=0$; a term with $q_i>0=p_i$ is $+\infty$. Fix $p_i>0$ and write $f_i(t)=t\log_2(t/p_i)$. For $t>0$,
$$
f_i''(t)=\frac1{t\ln2}>0,\qquad \lim_{t\downarrow0}f_i(t)=0.
$$
Thus $f_i$ is a <convex function> on $[0,\infty)$ by continuous extension to zero. For $0\leq\lambda\leq1$, applying this scalar <convexity> to each coordinate and summing proves
$$
D(\lambda q+(1-\lambda)r\|p)\leq\lambda D(q\|p)+(1-\lambda)D(r\|p).
$$
If $p$ has zero coordinates, the finite-valued domain consists of <probability mass functions> supported on $\{i:p_i>0\}$; it is convex and the same proof applies there. Outside that domain the right side is infinite whenever an offending <probability mass function> has a positive mixture weight, so the extended-valued <convexity> assertion still holds. At mixture weights zero or one the inequality is an identity, without needing the expression $0\cdot\infty$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

**Index correction:** the printed definition only permits $i\leq n$, although the required average includes $i=n+1$. Extend the same deletion formula to $1\leq i\leq n+1$. Without that extension the final summand is undefined; there is no new sample or different estimator to supply.

For a fixed outcome of the sample and a fixed symbol $x$, put $A_j=\mathbf1_{\{X_j=x\}}$. The leave-one-out <empirical distribution> then satisfies
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\widehat q_{n,i}(x)
&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}A_j-A_i\right)\\
&=\frac1n\left((n+1)\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}A_j-\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}A_i\right)
=\sum_{j=1}^{n+1}A_j.
\end{aligned}
$$
Dividing by $n+1$ gives **$\widehat p_{n+1}=(n+1)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\widehat q_{n,i}$.** Each of the $n+1$ <indicator functions> was counted exactly $n$ times before division by $n$. This <leave-one-out identity for empirical distributions> is deterministic; <independence> plays no part in this part.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

**Reference-distribution ambiguity:** $\rho$ is not defined in the paper. If it is an arbitrary <probability mass function>, the printed inequality is false. For instance, choose $p=(1,0)$ and $\rho=(1/2,1/2)$. Every <empirical distribution> equals $p$, making the printed left side one <bit> and its right side zero. This is a counterexample for every $n\geq1$.

Under the consistent interpretation **$\rho=p$**, the preceding <leave-one-out identity for empirical distributions> and <convexity of relative entropy in its first argument> give, for every sample,
$$
D(\widehat p_{n+1}\|p)
\leq\frac1{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}D(\widehat q_{n,i}\|p).
$$
Deleting any one observation from $n+1$ <independent and identically distributed random variables> leaves $n$ <independent> observations with <probability mass function> $p$. Consequently each $\widehat q_{n,i}$ has the same distribution as $\widehat p_n$. They need not be <independent> of one another. Taking <expected values> and using <linearity of expectation> therefore proves
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E D(\widehat p_{n+1}\|p)\leq\mathbb E D(\widehat p_n\|p).}
$$
Zero coordinates of $p$ are never sampled, <almost surely>, so the calculation can be restricted to its <probability support>. In particular the <expected values> are finite on this <finite alphabet>.

The discrepancy cannot be removed by silently choosing any fixed reference. Whenever $\rho$ is positive on the <probability support> of $p$, direct subtraction and $\mathbb E\widehat p_n=p$ give the <expected relative entropy decomposition>
$$
\mathbb E D(\widehat p_n\|\rho)=\mathbb E D(\widehat p_n\|p)+D(p\|\rho).
$$
Thus the valid arbitrary-reference version has $\rho$ on *both* sides; the printed mixed-reference version has an additional term whose sign prevents the proposed conclusion.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For the <maximum entropy distribution on the nonnegative integers>, use the <geometric distribution> counting failures before the first success. Write $r=a/(a+1)$ and define
$$
\boxed{g_i=(1-r)r^i=\frac1{a+1}\left(\frac a{a+1}\right)^i,\qquad i=0,1,\ldots.}
$$
The <geometric series> gives $\sum_i g_i=1$. <Termwise differentiation of a power series> applied to that <geometric series> for $0<r<1$ gives $\sum_iir^i=r/(1-r)^2$, hence $\sum_iig_i=r/(1-r)=a$.

For any competing <probability mass function> $p$ of mean $a$, its <cross-entropy> against $g$ is
$$
-\sum_{i\geq0}p_i\log_2g_i
=\log_2(a+1)+a\log_2\frac{a+1}{a}
=(a+1)\log_2(a+1)-a\log_2a.
$$
This is finite. The fact that <finite cross entropy bounds countable information entropy> must be justified without assuming in advance that $H(p)$ is finite. Use $\ln u\leq u-1$ with $u=g_i/p_i$ when $p_i>0$. Including zero terms by their limiting convention yields, for every finite $N$,
$$
\sum_{i=0}^N-p_i\log_2p_i\leq\sum_{i=0}^N-p_i\log_2g_i+\frac1{\ln2}\sum_{i=0}^N(g_i-p_i).
$$
Let $N\to\infty$. The <information entropy> partial sums increase, the <cross-entropy> partial sums converge to the finite value above, and the final mass difference tends to zero. This proves finiteness and the upper bound. We may now write
$$
D(p\|g)=H(g)-H(p)\geq0.
$$
<Relative entropy nonnegativity> has equality exactly when $p=g$. Therefore the displayed geometric <probability mass function> is the **unique maximizer**, and
$$
\boxed{H_{\max}=(a+1)\log_2(a+1)-a\log_2a\ \text{bits}.}
$$
The mean constraint is essential: without it, <uniform distributions> on longer initial intervals have unbounded <information entropy>.

=== a

==== Solution

The displayed <prefix code> has <expected codeword length> $3/2$ ternary digits per source symbol. Instead assign
$$
\boxed{c(1)=0,\qquad c(2)=1,\qquad c(3)=20,\qquad c(4)=21.}
$$
This is a <ternary prefix code>: the first two <codewords> end at distinct <tree children> of the <tree root>, and the other two start with $2$ and end at distinct <tree children> below it. No <codeword> is a prefix of another. Its <expected codeword length> is
$$
\frac12\cdot1+\frac14\cdot1+\frac18\cdot2+\frac18\cdot2=\frac54<\frac32.
$$
The reduction is **$1/4$ <trit> per source symbol**, which already disproves optimality of the displayed code. The <ternary Huffman coding> construction below will produce this same improved <code tree>.

=== b
{scope}

The <shape of minimum-cost positive-weight ternary prefix trees> arguments in ii–vi assume a <full-support finite distribution>: $p_i>0$ for every retained source symbol. The printed setup does not state this globally. Each affected solution gives the proof with that qualification and a counterexample when zero-probability symbols are allowed in arbitrary redundant <code trees>. Part i needs no such qualification.

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Exchange the two complete <codewords> while leaving the <code tree> unchanged. The <prefix code> property is preserved because the set of <codewords> is unchanged. If $p_i>p_j$ but $l_i>l_j$, the change in <expected codeword length> is
$$
(p_il_j+p_jl_i)-(p_il_i+p_jl_j)=(p_i-p_j)(l_j-l_i)<0,
$$
contradicting optimality. Hence **$p_i>p_j$ implies $l_i\leq l_j$**. This <probability monotonicity of optimal prefix-code lengths> is valid even if some <probabilities> are zero; equal <probabilities> impose no order.<A Method for the Construction of Minimum-Redundancy Codes by David A. Huffman>[p. 1099, Derived Coding Requirements: probability ordering and permissible exchange of equal-probability messages]{ref}

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

First assume a <full-support finite distribution>, as stated above. Let $L$ be the largest <codeword length> and take a <tree leaf> at depth $L$. Every existing <tree child> of its <tree parent> must also be a <tree leaf>: an internal <tree child> would have a descendant deeper than $L$. If the chosen <tree leaf> were its <tree parent>'s only <tree child>, moving its symbol to that <tree parent> would shorten its <codeword> by one, preserve the <prefix code> property, and reduce <expected codeword length> by its positive <probability>. That contradicts optimality. The <tree parent> therefore has at least two <tree children>, both <tree leaves>. A second <codeword> has the same first $L-1$ digits and a different last digit, proving the required **deepest <sibling codeword> property** for a <ternary prefix code>.

For the literal wording allowing zero <probabilities>, take $p=(1,0,0)$ and the <prefix code> $(0,10,110)$. Its <expected codeword length> is one, which is minimal: with more than one message an empty <codeword> is impossible, so the positive-probability message must have length at least one. Yet the deepest <tree leaf> $110$ has no <sibling codeword>. **Thus positivity or an additional convention removing redundant zero-weight branches is needed.**

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Suppose a <tree parent> at depth $n-1$, with $n<L$, has an unused outgoing digit. The missing <tree child> gives an empty <code tree> branch at depth $n$. Move any deepest <tree leaf> into that position, retaining all other <codewords> and deleting the abandoned empty branch. The new word has no old word as a prefix, because it lies in an unused branch, and it is not a prefix of any retained word. Its length decreases from $L$ to $n$. With a <full-support finite distribution> this reduces <expected codeword length> by $p_i(L-n)>0$, contradicting optimality. Hence all three <tree children> exist, and each has a descendant <codeword>. In fact **every internal <vertex> above depth $L-1$ has all three <tree children>**. Applying this to the <tree parent> of the specified prefix gives the claimed three extensions.

The positive-probability qualification attached only to the specified prefix does not by itself suffice. In the zero-weight example $(p,c)=((1,0,0),(0,10,110))$, choose the positive-probability word $0$, so $n=1<L=3$. There is no word starting with $2$. The deepest <tree leaf> available for relocation has <probability> zero, so the strict-improvement argument fails. This is why the <full-support finite distribution> assumption is used for the structural conclusions.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

With a <full-support finite distribution>, suppose two distinct <tree parents> at depth $L-1$ each have exactly two <tree children>, both <tree leaves>. Move one <tree leaf> from the second pair into the vacant third <tree child> slot of the first <tree parent>. Its length remains $L$, so this relocation does not change <expected codeword length>. The second <tree parent> now has a single <tree child>, which is a <tree leaf>. Replace that remaining <tree child>'s <codeword> by the <tree parent>'s word. This preserves the <prefix code> property and shortens a positive-probability <codeword> by one. <Expected codeword length> strictly decreases, a contradiction. Thus **there is at most one deepest pair with exactly two <sibling codewords>**.

If zero-probability symbols are retained without a reduction convention, the claim can fail. The <probability mass function> $(1,0,0,0,0)$ with <prefix code> $(0,100,101,110,111)$ has the optimal <expected codeword length> one, yet contains the two deepest pairs $(100,101)$ and $(110,111)$. The contraction would only shorten a zero-probability word.

==== v
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $L$ again denote the largest <codeword length>, and assume a <full-support finite distribution>. The missing-branch exchange from part iii forces every internal <vertex> at depth at most $L-2$ to have three <tree children>. At depth $L-1$, all <tree children> are <tree leaves>. Part ii rules out one <tree child> there; the hypothesis that there is no pair of two deepest <sibling codewords> rules out two <tree children>. Thus each such <tree parent> also has three <tree children>. **Every internal <vertex> has exactly three <tree children>**, which is a <full ternary tree>, called semi-complete in this paper. The same last-level argument covers $L=1$.

Without the assumption of a <full-support finite distribution> the implication is false: for $p=(1,0,0)$, the optimal <prefix code> $(0,10,110)$ has no deepest pair of two <sibling codewords>, but its <code tree> contains <vertices> with a single <tree child>. Empty unused <tree children> are not <vertices> of the <code tree>.

==== vi
{scope}

===== Solution

Assume a <full-support finite distribution>. The preceding structural arguments show that every internal <vertex> has three <tree children>, except possibly one <tree parent> at depth $L-1$ with two <tree children>. Let $N_3$ and $N_2$ count these two kinds of internal <vertex>. Part iv gives $N_2\in\{0,1\}$; no <vertex> has one <tree child>.

A finite <rooted tree> with $m$ <tree leaves> has $m+N_2+N_3-1$ <edges>, while counting outgoing <edges> gives $2N_2+3N_3$. Equating the counts yields
$$
m=1+N_2+2N_3.
$$
For an <odd integer> $m$, the right side forces $N_2$ to be an <even integer>. Since it is at most one, **$N_2=0$**. Thus no deepest pair has only two <sibling codewords>, and the optimal <code tree> is a <full ternary tree>. This leaf-count identity also directly proves that a <full ternary tree> has an <odd number> of <tree leaves>.

Again the unrestricted zero-weight version is false: $(0,10,11)$ is an optimal <prefix code> for $p=(1,0,0)$, with <odd integer> $m=3$, but its two deepest <tree leaves> form a two-sibling pair. There is no contradiction with the proof because its <tree root> has only two <tree children>, which the <full-support finite distribution> assumption would forbid above the deepest level.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For an <odd integer> $m\geq3$, <ternary Huffman coding> repeatedly merges **three** smallest weights.

Start with one <tree leaf> for each source symbol, weighted by its <probability>. While more than one weighted object remains, choose the three objects with least weights, create their common <tree parent> with weight equal to their sum, and replace the three by that <tree parent>. Newly created <tree parents> participate in subsequent choices. Each merge reduces the number of objects by two; an <odd number> of objects therefore reaches three and then one, never an unmergeable pair. The final object is the <tree root> of the <code tree>.

Label the three outgoing <edges> at each internal <vertex> by $0,1,2$ in any order. The digits along the root-to-leaf <graph path> give each symbol's <codeword>. <Tree leaves> cannot be ancestors of other <tree leaves>, so this is a <prefix code>. Ties can be resolved arbitrarily. The <expected codeword length> is measured in <trits>, and equals the sum of all merge weights, because each merge adds one digit to every <codeword> below it.

The structural reason for merging three is that the positive-weight, odd-leaf case has a <full ternary tree>. Its deepest <sibling codeword> triple can receive the three least weights by exchanging <tree leaf> labels, and contracting that triple reduces the problem by two symbols. This explains the recursive construction; a separate optimality proof is not required here.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For an <even integer> $m\geq2$, **merge the two least weights once, then repeatedly merge three least weights** until only the <tree root> remains. The initial binary merge leaves an <odd number> of weighted objects, so the preceding algorithm applies. The initial merged <tree parent> has two <tree children>; subsequent <tree parents> have three. Equivalently, add one zero-weight dummy symbol, run the odd-leaf <ternary Huffman coding> algorithm, and remove its <codeword> afterwards. The dummy contributes nothing to <expected codeword length>.

Here the first merge joins the two weights $1/8$, producing $1/4$. The three remaining weights are $1/2,1/4,1/4$, and merge directly into the <tree root>. Assigning the first two <tree root> branches to the original symbols of weights $1/2$ and $1/4$ gives
$$
\boxed{c(1)=0,\quad c(2)=1,\quad c(3)=20,\quad c(4)=21,\qquad L(c)=\frac54\ \text{trits}.}
$$
The merge-weight check gives $1/4+1=5/4$ as well. For $m=2$, the first merge is already the <tree root>; for $m=1$, outside this case with an <even number> of symbols, the usual temporary reduced-problem convention would permit an <empty word>.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For a <discrete memoryless channel> with <transition probabilities> $M(y\mid x)$, the <conditional probability> of a length-$n$ output given a length-$n$ input factors as
$$
\mathbb P(Y_1=y_1,\ldots,Y_n=y_n\mid X_1=x_1,\ldots,X_n=x_n)=\prod_{j=1}^n M(y_j\mid x_j).
$$
The same <transition probabilities> apply at each use. Its <channel capacity> is
$$
\boxed{C=\max_{p_X}I(X;Y)=\max_{p_X}\sum_{x,y}p_X(x)M(y\mid x)\log_2\frac{M(y\mid x)}{\sum_{x'}p_X(x')M(y\mid x')} .}
$$
The unit is <bits> per channel use. For <finite alphabets> the maximum exists by <continuity> on the <probability simplex>; with more general <alphabets> one uses a <supremum> when appropriate.

An $(m,n)$ <channel code> consists of an <encoder> $c:\mathcal V_m\to\mathcal X^n$ and a <decoder> $d:\mathcal Y^n\to\mathcal V_m$. It represents each of $m$ messages by $n$ input symbols and recovers a message from the $n$ observed output symbols. Its <code rate> is **$R=(\log_2m)/n$ <bits> per use**. For each message $w$, its conditional error <probability> is $\mathbb P(d(Y^n)\ne w\mid W=w)$; an average error also specifies the message <probability distribution>, while the maximum error takes the largest conditional error.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $T=g(Y)$. Because $T$ is determined by $Y$, the <chain rule for mutual information> gives
$$
I(X;Y)=I(X;Y,T)=I(X;T)+I(X;Y\mid T)\geq I(X;T).
$$
This is the <data processing inequality>. It holds for every input <probability distribution>. Maximizing each side over that same set of input <probability distributions> gives **$C_{g(Y)}\leq C_Y$**; processing cannot increase <channel capacity>.

A simple sufficient condition for equality is that **$g$ be <injective> on the reachable output <alphabet>**. Then $Y$ can be recovered from $T$, giving $H(Y\mid T)=0$ and hence $I(X;Y\mid T)=0$ for every input law. The input that achieves the original <channel capacity> therefore achieves it after processing as well. Values of $g$ outside reachable outputs are irrelevant.

<Injectivity> is sufficient rather than necessary. More precisely, for <finite alphabets> <equality in capacity data processing> holds exactly when there is an original capacity-achieving input law for which $X$ and $Y$ have <conditional independence> given $T$. Sufficiency follows from the displayed gap identity. For necessity, maximize $I(X;T)$ at an input law $p_*$; if its maximum equals $C_Y$, then $C_Y=I_{p_*}(X;T)\leq I_{p_*}(X;Y)\leq C_Y$, so both inequalities are equalities. Processing may discard output details unrelated to the input without losing <channel capacity>.

=== c
{scope}

Interpret additive noise in the standard channel sense: $Z$ is <independent> of the chosen input $X$ and its law is fixed as the input law is varied. A uniform marginal law for $Z$ alone, without this <independence> assumption, would not specify the <transition matrix>. Only reachable output symbols are counted.

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Translate the output <alphabet> by $-\min(z_1,z_2)$ and put $d=|z_2-z_1|\geq1$. The reachable outputs become
$$
\{0,1,2\}\cup\{d,d+1,d+2\},\qquad |\mathcal Y|=3+\min(d,3).
$$
For $d=1$ the two sets overlap in two points; for $d=2$ in one; for $d\geq3$ they are disjoint. In particular **$|\mathcal Y|\geq4$**, and choosing noise values $0,1$ attains four.

With rows indexed by inputs $0,1,2$ and columns by outputs $0,1,2,3$, <independence> and uniform two-point noise give
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}
1/2&1/2&0&0\\
0&1/2&1/2&0\\
0&0&1/2&1/2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Every conditional output distribution has <information entropy> one <bit>, so $I(X;Y)=H(Y)-1\leq\log_2 4-1=1$. The input <probability mass function> $(1/2,0,1/2)$ makes all four outputs equiprobable, attaining that bound. Thus
$$
\boxed{C=1\ \text{bit per use},\qquad p_X=(1/2,0,1/2).}
$$
Using a zero-probability *input* in a channel-capacity maximization is legitimate; it is unrelated to the strictly positive source weights needed for the tree-shape claims in Question 2.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Each of the three input values has two possible outputs, so their union has at most six elements. The preceding union formula also gives this bound. Choose noise values $0,3$, whose two translated input <alphabets> are disjoint. With columns ordered $0,1,2,3,4,5$ the <transition matrix> is
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}
1/2&0&0&1/2&0&0\\
0&1/2&0&0&1/2&0\\
0&0&1/2&0&0&1/2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Thus **$|\mathcal Y|=6$**. Every output determines its input, explicitly $X=Y\bmod3$, so $H(X\mid Y)=0$. Consequently $I(X;Y)=H(X)\leq\log_2 3$, with equality for a uniform input. Therefore
$$
\boxed{C=\log_2 3\ \text{bits per use},\qquad p_X=(1/3,1/3,1/3).}
$$
The extra uncertainty of which noise value occurred does not reduce information about the input: the two possible outputs for each input remain distinguishable from those of every other input.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Choose noise values $0,2$, giving exactly the outputs $0,1,2,3,4$. The <transition matrix>, in that column order, is
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}
1/2&0&1/2&0&0\\
0&1/2&0&1/2&0\\
0&0&1/2&0&1/2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
An input <probability mass function> $(1/2,1/2,0)$ makes outputs $0,1,2,3$ equiprobable, so $H(Y)=2$ and $H(Y\mid X)=1$. Hence **$C\geq1$ <bit> per use**, as required for comparison with the four-output case.

There is a strict improvement, which can also be computed exactly. <Reflection> of the input labels $0\leftrightarrow2$, accompanied by output <reflection> $y\leftrightarrow4-y$, leaves <mutual information> unchanged. <Concavity of information entropy> makes <mutual information> a concave function of the input <probability mass function> for a fixed <transition matrix>. Averaging an input law with its <reflection> therefore cannot decrease it. It suffices to consider
$$
p_X=(s/2,1-s,s/2),\qquad 0\leq s\leq1.
$$
The outputs whose labels are <even integers> have total mass $s$ and <conditional probability mass function> $(1/4,1/2,1/4)$, with <information entropy> $3/2$. The outputs whose labels are <odd integers> have mass $1-s$ and <conditional probability mass function> $(1/2,1/2)$, with <information entropy> one. Splitting first by output <parity> using the <chain rule for information entropy> yields
$$
H(Y)=h_2(s)+\tfrac32s+(1-s),\qquad I(X;Y)=h_2(s)+\frac s2.
$$
Its <derivative> is $\log_2((1-s)/s)+1/2$, and its <second derivative> is negative. The unique maximum occurs at $s=\sqrt2/(1+\sqrt2)$, giving
$$
\boxed{C=\log_2(1+\sqrt2)\approx1.271553,\qquad p_X=\left(\frac1{2+\sqrt2},\frac1{1+\sqrt2},\frac1{2+\sqrt2}\right).}
$$
This additional optimization explains the collision pattern: outputs $1,3$ identify input $1$, outputs $0,4$ identify the two extreme inputs, and only output $2$ is ambiguous.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For every input <probability mass function>, <conditional entropy> is nonnegative, so
$$
I(X;Y)=H(X)-H(X\mid Y)\leq H(X)\leq\log_2|\mathcal X|=1.
$$
Taking the maximum over input laws proves **$C\leq1$ <bit> per channel use**. This bound comes from the binary input <alphabet> itself; no specific <transition probabilities> or <decoder> are needed.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

There are two possible messages and three channel uses, giving
$$
\boxed{R=\frac{\log_2 2}{3}=\frac13\ \text{bit per use}.}
$$
Conditional on either transmitted message, a decoding error occurs exactly when at least two of the three <bits> are flipped. The <discrete memoryless channel> makes these flips <independent>, each with <probability> $\varepsilon=1/10$. Their count therefore has a <binomial distribution> with parameters $3,\varepsilon$. <Majority-vote decoding of a repetition code> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(\text{error}\mid W=a)
&=\binom32\varepsilon^2(1-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon^3\\
&=3\left(\frac1{10}\right)^2\frac9{10}+\left(\frac1{10}\right)^3
=\frac7{250}=0.028.
\end{aligned}
$$
Interchanging the two <bit> values leaves the <binary symmetric channel> and majority decision unchanged, so **$\mathbb P(\text{error}\mid W=b)=7/250$ as well**. Both the maximum conditional error and the average error, for any message prior, are therefore $0.028$. The <probability> of correct decoding for each message is $243/250=0.972$. These are finite-block error <probabilities>; a channel-capacity statement alone would not determine them.
