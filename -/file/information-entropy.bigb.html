= Information entropy
{title2=$H(X)$}

= Shannon entropy
{c}
{synonym}

For a finite <discrete random variable> with <probability mass function> $p$, its information entropy is the <expected value> of its <information content>:
$$
H(X)=-\sum_{x:p(x)>0}p(x)\log_2p(x)
=\mathbb E[-\log_2p(X)].
$$
Base-two <logarithms> give <bits>; choosing a different base rescales the units. The convention $0\log_2 0=0$ is justified by a <limit>, so outcomes of zero <probability> contribute nothing. The definition also applies to countable distributions, but the sum may be infinite.

A fair <bit> has $H=1$. A certain outcome has $H=0$. A <Bernoulli random variable> that is one with <probability> $0.1$ has $H=h_2(0.1)\approx0.469$ <bits>, because most of its outcomes are predictable. A rare outcome still has large <information content>: the outcome of <probability> $0.1$ contributes $-\log_2(0.1)\approx3.322$ <bits> of surprise when it occurs. **Entropy is the average surprise, rather than the surprise of each outcome.**

For $m$ possible outcomes, $0\leq H(X)\leq\log_2m$, with the upper bound attained exactly by the <uniform distribution on a finite set>. The <Gibbs inequality> proves this by comparing $p$ with the uniform distribution: $D(p\Vert u)=\log_2m-H(X)\geq0$. Thus increasing predictability reduces the required average description length. The four-symbol <prefix code> in <information theory> attains $H=7/4$ <bits> exactly; for general probabilities, integer codeword lengths prevent such a perfect match for every individual symbol. <Shannon's source coding theorem> explains how coding sufficiently long independent blocks approaches the entropy per symbol.

The <logarithm> is especially natural because <independent> outcomes have additive surprise: $-\log_2(pq)=-\log_2p-\log_2q$. Taking <expected values> gives additive <information entropy> for <independent random variables>; for dependent ones, the <chain rule for information entropy> replaces the second term by <conditional entropy>. This is why the same quantity can govern both a single observation and a long message.

= Entropy bound for a random-length binary string
{parent=Information entropy}
{title2=$H(M)\le H(L)+\mathbb EL$}

For a random finite binary string $M$ of length $L$, conditioning on $L=\ell$ leaves at most $2^\ell$ possible strings. Thus its <conditional entropy> is at most $\ell$. Since the length is a function of the string, the <chain rule for information entropy> gives
$$
H(M)=H(L)+H(M\mid L)\le H(L)+\mathbb EL.
$$
The term $H(L)$ cannot generally be removed. For concatenations of $m$ words from a fixed finite <decipherable code> with maximum length $d$, it is at most $\log_2(md+1)$. <Decipherable code>[Decipherability] preserves the <Shannon entropy> of the source block. For an independent source block, dividing the resulting inequality by $m$ and taking $m\to\infty$ proves the <expected codeword length> is at least the one-symbol <Shannon entropy>.

= Entropy lower bound from the largest atom
{parent=Information entropy}

For a finite <probability distribution> with largest atom $\Pi$, its base-two <Shannon entropy> satisfies $H\geq2(1-\Pi)$. If $\Pi\geq1/2$, coarsen the distribution to the indicator of a largest atom: <Shannon entropy> cannot increase under a deterministic function, so $H\geq h_2(\Pi)$. The <concavity of information entropy>[concavity] of <binary entropy> places it above the chord from $(1/2,1)$ to $(1,0)$, giving $h_2(\Pi)\geq2(1-\Pi)$. If $\Pi\leq1/2$, every nonzero probability $p$ has $-\log_2p\geq-\log_2\Pi$, so $H\geq-\log_2\Pi$. The function $-\log_2\Pi-2(1-\Pi)$ vanishes at $1/2$ and has negative derivative on $(0,1/2]$, proving the same bound. Equality in the combined bound occurs for a point mass or a distribution uniform on two points.

= Finite cross entropy bounds countable information entropy
{parent=Information entropy}
{title2=$H(p)\leq-\sum_i p_i\log_2g_i$}

Let $p$ and $g$ be <probability mass functions> on a countable <alphabet>, with $g_i>0$ whenever $p_i>0$. If the <cross-entropy> $C=-\sum_i p_i\log_2g_i$ is finite, then $H(p)$ is finite, $H(p)\leq C$, and $D(p\|g)=C-H(p)$. To avoid assuming the desired finiteness, first apply $\ln u\leq u-1$ with $u=g_i/p_i$ to each positive $p_i$. For a finite initial segment,
$$
\sum_{i=0}^N-p_i\log_2p_i\leq\sum_{i=0}^N-p_i\log_2g_i+\frac{\sum_{i=0}^N(g_i-p_i)}{\ln2}.
$$
Zero coordinates satisfy this inequality too. <Monotone convergence> of the nonnegative <information entropy> summands and convergence of both total masses give $H(p)\leq C<\infty$. Both logarithmic sums are then finite, justifying their difference and the <relative entropy> identity. This supplies a rigorous route to the <maximum entropy distribution on the nonnegative integers> without presupposing finite <information entropy> for every competing law.

= Finite-alphabet entropy continuity bound
{parent=Information entropy}
{title2=$|H(p)-H(q)|\leq d\log_2(N/d),\quad d=\|p-q\|_1\leq1/2$}

For <probability mass functions> on $N$ symbols, set $d_i=|p_i-q_i|$ and $d=\sum_id_i\leq1/2$. The <entropy summand> bound gives $|f(p_i)-f(q_i)|\leq f(d_i)$. The <triangle inequality> and <Jensen's inequality> give
$$
|H(p)-H(q)|\leq\sum_if(d_i)\leq Nf(d/N)=d\log_2(N/d).
$$
At $d=0$, use the continuous value zero. This proves uniform continuity of <Shannon entropy> on a fixed <finite alphabet>; the <alphabet> size appears explicitly in the bound.

= Entropy summand
{parent=Information entropy}
{title2=$f(x)=-x\log_2x$}

The contribution of a symbol with <probability> $x$ to <Shannon entropy>, with $f(0)=0$. It is a <concave function> because $f''(x)=-1/(x\ln2)<0$ on $(0,1)$ and its endpoint extension is continuous. Its sharp bound for pairs exactly distance $v$ apart in $[0,1]$ is
$$
\max_{0\leq t\leq1-v}|f(t+v)-f(t)|=\max\{f(v),f(1-v)\}.
$$
To prove this, the increment $f(t+v)-f(t)$ decreases with $t$, from $f(v)$ to $-f(1-v)$. For $v\leq1/2$, the maximum is $f(v)$: the difference $f(v)-f(1-v)$ vanishes at zero and one half and has second <derivative> $(2v-1)/(v(1-v)\ln2)\leq0$ between them. Hence it is nonnegative by <concavity>.

= Joint entropy
{parent=Information entropy}
{title2=$H(X_1,\ldots,X_n)$}

<Joint entropy> is the <information entropy> of an entire <random vector>, treating its coordinates together as one outcome. For <random variables> with finite <alphabets> and joint <probability mass function> $p$,
$$
H(X_1,\ldots,X_n)=-\sum_{x_1,\ldots,x_n:p(x_1,\ldots,x_n)>0}p(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\log_2 p(x_1,\ldots,x_n).
$$
It is measured in <bits>. The distinction from separate <information entropies> is whether shared uncertainty is counted once or repeatedly. Two copies of the same fair <bit> have <joint entropy> one <bit>, since only $00$ and $11$ can occur. Two <independent> fair <bits> have <joint entropy> two <bits>, since all four pairs are possible and equiprobable.

For a less symmetric example, assign <probabilities> $1/2,1/4,1/4$ to $(X,Y)=(0,0),(1,0),(1,1)$ respectively. Then
$$
\boxed{H(X,Y)=\tfrac12\cdot1+\tfrac14\cdot2+\tfrac14\cdot2=\tfrac32\text{ bits}.}
$$
Here $X$ is fair, while $\mathbb P(Y=1)=1/4$. Thus $H(X)=1$ and $H(Y)=h_2(1/4)\approx0.811278$ <bits>, where $h_2$ is <binary entropy>. Adding the separate <information entropies> would give approximately $1.811278$ <bits>, overcounting by the <mutual information> $I(X;Y)\approx0.311278$ <bits>.

The <chain rule for information entropy> makes the shared information explicit:
$$
H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y\mid X).
$$
In this example $Y$ is certain given $X=0$ and fair given $X=1$, so its average <conditional entropy> is $H(Y\mid X)=\tfrac12\cdot0+\tfrac12\cdot1=1/2$ <bit>. Sending the first coordinate changes how much uncertainty remains in the second. A <prefix code> with codewords `0`, `10`, `11` for the three possible pairs has average length $3/2$ <bits>, exactly the <joint entropy> in this stipulated law.

For finite <alphabets>,
$$
\max_iH(X_i)\leq H(X_1,\ldots,X_n)\leq\sum_iH(X_i).
$$
The lower bound follows because knowing the full <random vector> determines every coordinate; the upper bound follows from <conditioning reduces entropy> in the <chain rule for information entropy>. Equality in the upper bound means joint <independence>. The difference from that upper bound is <total correlation>, whose <noisy parity total correlation> example shows why separate pairwise comparisons need not find all the dependence.

= Cross-entropy
{parent=Information entropy}
{wiki}
{title2=$H(P,Q)=-\sum_i p_i\log q_i$}

<Cross-entropy> measures the average <logarithmic loss> incurred when outcomes follow one <probability distribution> $P$, but a model assigns <probabilities> using another <probability distribution> $Q$. For a finite <alphabet>, write their <probability mass functions> as $p_i$ and $q_i$. With base-two <logarithms>,
$$
H(P,Q)=-\sum_{i:p_i>0}p_i\log_2 q_i=\mathbb E_P[-\log_2 q(X)].
$$
The expectation is under the true or stipulated source law $P$, rather than the model $Q$. Terms with $p_i=0$ contribute nothing. If $p_i>0$ but $q_i=0$ for any outcome, the <cross-entropy> is $+\infty$: the model rules out an event that can occur. The result is measured in <bits>; <natural logarithms> give <nats>.

The useful identity is <cross-entropy equals entropy plus relative entropy>:
$$
\boxed{H(P,Q)=H(P)+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\Vert Q).}
$$
The source's <information entropy> $H(P)$ is the baseline uncertainty. The <Kullback-Leibler divergence> is the extra average cost of a mismatched model, with the true law first and model second. Thus optimizing $Q$ for a fixed $P$ reduces the mismatch, rather than changing the source's own uncertainty. For finite <alphabets>, the minimum is $H(P)$, attained exactly at $Q=P$.

Take a three-symbol source with $P=(1/2,1/4,1/4)$. A matched binary <prefix code> assigns $A\mapsto0$, $B\mapsto10$, $C\mapsto11$, with lengths $(1,2,2)$. A mismatched <prefix code> instead assigns $A\mapsto10$, $B\mapsto11$, $C\mapsto0$, with lengths $(2,2,1)$, corresponding to $Q=(1/4,1/4,1/2)$. Both <prefix codes> decode exactly, but the most common symbol is shorter in the matched code. Under the same source $P$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
H(P)&=\tfrac12\cdot1+\tfrac14\cdot2+\tfrac14\cdot2=\tfrac32,\\
H(P,Q)&=\tfrac12\cdot2+\tfrac14\cdot2+\tfrac14\cdot1=\tfrac74,\\
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\Vert Q)&=\tfrac12\log_2 2+\tfrac14\log_2 1+\tfrac14\log_2(1/2)=\tfrac14.
\end{aligned}
$$
**The mismatched code costs an extra (1/4) bit per source symbol on average.** No information is lost; the penalty is inefficient representation of this source. The <mismatch penalty in lossless coding> explains when this coding interpretation is exact.

{description=Both sets of <codewords> are <prefix codes>. The source <probabilities> are fixed at $(1/2,1/4,1/4)$. Each colored contribution is source <probability> times <codeword length>; changing which symbol receives the shortest word increases the total from $3/2$ to $7/4$ <bits> per source symbol.}

<Cross-entropy> is also useful when evaluating a <predictive distribution>. A report that places tiny <probability> on the realized outcome receives a large <logarithmic loss>, even when it usually predicts the most common outcome correctly. The loss convention is the negative of the reward convention in the <logarithmic scoring rule>. Its expected loss is uniquely minimized by the correct <probability distribution>, making it a <strictly proper scoring rule>. <Strictly Proper Scoring Rules, Prediction, and Estimation by Tilmann Gneiting and others> treats this connection in §3.1, Example 3; its discussion uses rewards, so the signs are reversed for the losses here.<Strictly Proper Scoring Rules, Prediction, and Estimation by Tilmann Gneiting and others>{ref}

<I. J. Good>'s <Rational Decisions by I. J. Good> appeared in 1952. Its published summary describes a method for encouraging accurate <probability> estimates and a connection with <information theory>. <Strictly Proper Scoring Rules, Prediction, and Estimation by Tilmann Gneiting and others> traces the <logarithmic scoring rule> at least as far back as this 1952 paper.<Rational Decisions by I. J. Good>{ref}

= Mismatched asymptotic equipartition property
{parent=Cross-entropy}
{title2=$n^{-1}\log_2q_n(X^n)\to-H(p,q)$}

For <independent and identically distributed random variables> with <probability mass function> $p$ on a <finite alphabet>, evaluate their realized strings under a fixed product model $q_n(x^n)=\prod_iq(x_i)$. If $q$ is positive on the <probability support> of $p$, the <strong law of large numbers> gives
$$
\frac1n\log_2q_n(X^n)=\frac1n\sum_i\log_2q(X_i)\longrightarrow-H(p,q)=-H(p)-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q)
$$
<almost surely>, using <cross-entropy equals entropy plus relative entropy>. If $q$ vanishes at an outcome of positive $p$ <probability>, that outcome eventually appears <almost surely>, making all subsequent product likelihoods zero and the limit $-\infty$. This matches infinite <cross-entropy>.

= Cross-entropy equals entropy plus relative entropy
{parent=Cross-entropy}
{title2=$H(P,Q)-H(P)=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\Vert Q)$}

For finite <probability mass functions>, with $q_i>0$ whenever $p_i>0$, subtract the two <expectations> of <logarithmic loss>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
H(P,Q)-H(P)
&=\sum_{i:p_i>0}p_i(\log_2 p_i-\log_2 q_i)\\
&=\sum_{i:p_i>0}p_i\log_2\frac{p_i}{q_i}
=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\Vert Q).
\end{aligned}
$$
The <Gibbs inequality> makes the difference nonnegative and zero exactly when $P=Q$. If $Q$ assigns zero <probability> to an outcome with positive <probability> under $P$, both the <cross-entropy> and the <Kullback-Leibler divergence> are infinite. The finite <information entropy> still leaves the displayed identity valid in the extended sense. In more general settings, undefined differences such as $\infty-\infty$ cannot be used as a derivation.

= Mismatch penalty in lossless coding
{parent=Cross-entropy}
{title2=$\mathbb E_P[\ell(X)]=H(P,Q)$}

Suppose a binary <prefix code> has integer <codeword lengths> $\ell_i=-\log_2 q_i$. Under a source with <probability mass function> $p_i$, its average <codeword length> is exactly <cross-entropy>, $\sum_i p_i\ell_i=H(P,Q)$. The <Kraft inequality> is satisfied because $\sum_i2^{-\ell_i}=\sum_iq_i=1$. This exact interpretation requires suitable <dyadic rational> <probabilities>; a general $-\log_2q_i$ is not an integer <codeword length>. For positive $q_i$, a <prefix code> with lengths $\lceil-\log_2q_i\rceil$ exists by the <Kraft inequality> and has average length between $H(P,Q)$ and $H(P,Q)+1$. The <ceiling function> causes the one-symbol rounding overhead. The cross-entropy term describes mismatch overhead; those are distinct costs.

= Binary cross-entropy
{parent=Cross-entropy}
{title2=$H(r,q)=-r\log_2q-(1-r)\log_2(1-q)$}

Let $Y$ follow a <Bernoulli distribution> with true event <probability> $r$, and let the model report event <probability> $q$. The <binary cross-entropy> is its expected <logarithmic loss>:
$$
H(r,q)=-r\log_2q-(1-r)\log_2(1-q).
$$
For $0<r<1$, it is minimized at $q=r$, where the value is <binary entropy> $h_2(r)$. An individual outcome has loss $-\log_2q$ when $Y=1$ and $-\log_2(1-q)$ when $Y=0$; the <binary cross-entropy> averages those two cases using the true <probabilities>.

For $r=1/4$, truthful reporting gives $h_2(1/4)\approx0.811278$ <bits>. Reporting $q=1/2$ gives one <bit>. Reporting $q=0.01$ gives
$$
H(1/4,0.01)=\tfrac14\log_2 100-\tfrac34\log_2 0.99\approx1.671839\text{ bits}.
$$
Both $q=1/4$ and $q=0.01$ select zero if one predicts only the more likely binary outcome. Their predicted <probabilities> are very different, however, and <cross-entropy> reveals the cost of underestimating the event that occurs a quarter of the time. Endpoint losses are computed using the realized outcome rather than multiplying zero by an undefined <logarithm>.

= Empirical cross-entropy
{parent=Cross-entropy}
{title2=$\widehat H_n(Q)=-n^{-1}\sum_{t=1}^n\log_2 q(X_t)$}

For observations $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ of a <categorical variable>, <empirical cross-entropy> is the average <logarithmic loss> under the reported <probability mass function> $q$. Writing the <empirical distribution> as $\hat p_i=n^{-1}\#\{t:X_t=i\}$ gives
$$
\widehat H_n(Q)=-\frac1n\sum_{t=1}^n\log_2q(X_t)
=-\sum_{i:\hat p_i>0}\hat p_i\log_2q_i=H(\widehat P_n,Q).
$$
Under <independent and identically distributed> observations from $P$ and a fixed model with positive <probability> for each possible outcome, the <law of large numbers> gives convergence to $H(P,Q)$. A finite <empirical distribution> is an estimate of $P$, rather than the true source law; unobserved outcomes need not be impossible. If the same data are used to choose $Q$, evaluating the resulting <empirical cross-entropy> on that data need not estimate its prediction performance on fresh observations without <bias>.

= Cross-entropy and maximum likelihood
{parent=Empirical cross-entropy}
{title2=$\arg\min_\theta\widehat H_n(Q_\theta)=\arg\max_\theta\prod_tq_\theta(X_t)$}

For a <parametric statistical model> with <probability mass function> $q_\theta$ and an <independent and identically distributed> model, minimizing <empirical cross-entropy> is <maximum likelihood estimation>. The <logarithm> is strictly increasing, so maximizing $\prod_tq_\theta(X_t)$ is equivalent to maximizing $\sum_t\log_2q_\theta(X_t)$. Negating and dividing by $n$ gives the displayed minimization. Restricting the model family can prevent the optimum from matching the <empirical distribution>, and a finite optimizing parameter need not exist. This algebra does not by itself establish accuracy on new data.<Strictly Proper Scoring Rules, Prediction, and Estimation by Tilmann Gneiting and others>[§1, printed p. 360: optimum-score estimation and maximum likelihood]{ref}

= Entropy monotonicity under independent addition
{parent=Information entropy}

If $U,V$ are <independent random variables> taking values in a countable additive group, conditioning on $V$ makes $U+V$ a translate of $U$. Consequently $H(U+V\mid V)=H(U)$, and <conditional entropy> gives $H(U+V)\geq H(U)$. If the two <information entropies> are finite, equality holds exactly when $U+V$ and $V$ are independent.

= q-ary entropy
{c}
{synonym}

This is the entropy in base $q$ of the distribution with one symbol of probability $1-x$ and each of the other $q-1$ symbols of probability $x/(q-1)$. It increases on $[0,1-1/q]$ from zero to one, and governs the exponential volume of a <Hamming ball>. For $q=2$ it is the <binary entropy> function.

The self-information of a positive-probability outcome is the negative logarithm of its probability. Independent outcomes have additive self-information. Its expectation is <Shannon entropy>.

= Graph entropy
{parent=Information entropy}
{wiki}
{title2=$H(G,P)=\min I(X;Y)$}

For a vertex law $P$ on a graph, choose $X\sim P$ and an <independent set> valued random variable $Y$ containing $X$ almost surely. Minimize their <mutual information> over all such joint laws. This defines graph entropy. It differs from the entropy of a proper coloring, because the random independent set need not be a deterministic color class.

= Entropy bound with one prescribed probability
{parent=Information entropy}
{title2=$H\leq h(t)+(1-t)\log_2(k-1)$}

If one probability in a $k$-point distribution equals $t$, normalize the remaining $k-1$ probabilities to $q$. Then $H=h(t)+(1-t)H(q)\leq h(t)+(1-t)\log_2(k-1)$. Equality for $t<1$ means that the remaining probabilities are equal. This combines the <binary entropy> of the distinguished event with the largest residual <Shannon entropy>.

= Epsilon-sufficient set
{parent=Information entropy}
{title2=$\Pr(X\in S)\geq1-\epsilon$}

For a finite-valued <random variable>, an epsilon-sufficient set $S$ retains probability at least $1-\epsilon$. This notion is about covering probability mass and is unrelated to a <sufficient statistic>. For an <IID random variable> source, any such set of words with fixed $\epsilon<1$ requires exponentially many words at a rate approaching the <Shannon entropy>.

= Minimum size of a sufficient set for an IID source
{parent=Epsilon-sufficient set}
{title2=$|S_n|\geq\frac{1-\epsilon}{2}2^{n(H-\delta)}$}

For finite-alphabet <independent and identically distributed random variables>, fixed $0\leq\epsilon<1$ and $\delta>0$, every <epsilon-sufficient set> $S_n$ of length-$n$ words satisfies $|S_n|\geq(1-\epsilon)2^{n(H-\delta)}/2$ for sufficiently large $n$. Intersect $S_n$ with a <typical set> whose excluded mass is at most $(1-\epsilon)/2$. The intersection retains that much probability and each word has probability at most $2^{-n(H-\delta)}$.

= Differential entropy
{parent=Information entropy}
{title2=$h(q)=-\int q(x)\log q(x)\,dx$}
{wiki}

<Differential entropy> describes the spread of a continuous <probability distribution> relative to the volume scale of its coordinates. For a real <random variable> $X$ with <probability density function> $f$, use <natural logarithms> and write
$$
\boxed{h_e(X)=-\int_{\mathbb R} f(x)\ln f(x)\,dx=-\mathbb E[\ln f(X)].}
$$
The convention is $0\ln0=0$. For a finite value, require $\int f|\ln f|<\infty$; if both positive and negative contributions are infinite, the expression is undefined. Unlike a <probability>, a <probability density> can exceed one. Consequently <differential entropy> can be negative. Base-two <logarithms> give $h_2=h_e/\ln2$, in <bits> instead of <nats>.<6.441 Information Theory (Spring 2016 lecture notes) by Yury Polyanskiy and others/Chapter 1: Information Measures: Entropy and Divergence>[§1.3, Definition 1.5, equation (1.19) and warning, printed p. 18]{ref}

This quantity does not give the exact number of <bits> needed to store a real number. A recording device supplies a resolution; <equal-width bin entropy> connects that resolution to discrete <information entropy>. The useful continuous quantity is what remains after removing the increasing cost of specifying finer bins. Its dependence on coordinates is visible already in <differential entropy of a uniform law>.

= Differential entropy of a uniform law
{parent=Differential entropy}
{title2=$h_e(U(a,a+L))=\ln L$}

For a <continuous uniform distribution> on an interval of length $L>0$, the <probability density> is $1/L$. Direct integration gives
$$
h_e(X)=-\int_a^{a+L}\frac1L\ln\frac1L\,dx=\ln L.
$$
Thus **a uniform law on an interval of length $1/2$ has differential entropy $-\ln2$**, while length two gives $\ln2$. Both laws have total <probability> one. The shorter interval has larger <probability density>, making $-\ln f(X)$ negative; this is neither a negative <probability> nor a negative discrete <information entropy>.

= Differential entropy of a Gaussian law
{parent=Differential entropy}
{title2=$h_e(N(m,\sigma^2))=\frac12\ln(2\pi e\sigma^2)$}

For a <normal distribution> with <mean> $m$ and positive <variance> $\sigma^2$, expand the <logarithm> of its <probability density function>:
$$
-\ln f(X)=\frac12\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)+\frac{(X-m)^2}{2\sigma^2}.
$$
The second term has <expected value> $1/2$, hence $h_e(X)=\tfrac12\ln(2\pi e\sigma^2)$. Translation of the <mean> has no effect, while shrinking the <standard deviation> lowers <differential entropy>. A <standard normal distribution> has approximately $1.41894$ <nats>. This connects the continuous spread calculation with <entropy power>, whose normalization matches a <Gaussian distribution>'s <variance>.

= Differential entropy under an increasing transformation
{parent=Differential entropy}
{title2=$h_b(g(X))=h_b(X)+\mathbb E\log_b g'(X)$}

Let $X$ have a <probability density function> on an open interval, and let $g$ be continuously <differentiable>, strictly increasing there, with $g'(x)>0$. The <change of variables formula> gives
$$
f_{g(X)}(g(x))=\frac{f_X(x)}{g'(x)}.
$$
If the <differential entropy> and the expected absolute <logarithm> of $g'(X)$ are finite, taking the <expectation> of the negative <logarithm> proves the displayed formula, for a fixed base $b>1$. Local stretching reduces <probability density>; its contribution to <differential entropy> is the expected logarithmic stretch. The map is invertible, so this change does not mean that the transformation destroyed information about $X$.

= Differential entropy of a squared uniform variable
{parent=Differential entropy under an increasing transformation}
{title2=$h_e(X^2)=\ln2-1,\quad X\sim U(0,1)$}

Take $X$ with <continuous uniform distribution> on $(0,1)$ and set $Y=X^2$. This is an increasing invertible transformation on that interval. Its <cumulative distribution function> is $F_Y(y)=\sqrt y$, giving
$$
f_Y(y)=\frac1{2\sqrt y},\qquad 0<y<1.
$$
Although the <probability density> diverges near zero, it is <integrable>. The logarithmic stretch is integrable too:
$$
h_e(Y)=h_e(X)+\int_0^1\ln(2x)\,dx
=\boxed{\ln2-1\approx-0.306853\text{ nats}.}
$$
The <inverse function> $X=\sqrt Y$ recovers every input in the interval. The changed <differential entropy> records a changed coordinate volume, rather than a failure of recovery.

= Differential entropy under affine rescaling
{parent=Differential entropy}
{title2=$h_e(aX+b)=h_e(X)+\ln|a|$}

For real $a\ne0$ and $b$, the <change of variables formula> gives $f_{aX+b}(ax+b)=f_X(x)/|a|$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{h_e(aX+b)=h_e(X)+\ln|a|.}
$$
The absolute value also covers a decreasing transformation. If a numerical length changes from metres to centimetres, $a=100$ and the <differential entropy> changes by $\ln100\approx4.60517$ <nats>, although the physical uncertainty is the same. The reference coordinate scale must therefore accompany an absolute <differential entropy> value.

= Equal-width bin entropy
{parent=Differential entropy}
{title2=$H_e(Q_n)-\ln n\longrightarrow h_e(X)$}

Partition $(0,1)$ into $n$ bins of width $\delta=1/n$, and let the <discrete random variable> $Q_n$ record the bin containing $X$. This is a <quantization (signal processing)> of $X$. Its <probability mass function> is
$$
p_j=\int_{j/n}^{(j+1)/n}f(x)\,dx,\qquad 0\leq j<n,
$$
and its discrete <information entropy> is $H_e(Q_n)=-\sum_jp_j\ln p_j$. Let $\bar f_j=p_j/\delta$ be the average <probability density> within bin $j$. Rearrangement gives the exact finite-bin identity
$$
H_e(Q_n)+\ln\delta=-\sum_j\delta\bar f_j\ln\bar f_j.
$$
For a <continuous> <probability density> on $[0,1]$, <uniform continuity> makes the bin averages converge uniformly to the density. Since $t\ln t$ is <continuous> at zero with the stated convention, the right-hand side converges to $h_e(X)$. Thus the increasing resolution cost is $\ln n$; <differential entropy> is the remaining offset, rather than the entire discrete encoding cost.

For a <continuous uniform distribution>, every bin has <probability> $1/n$, and $H_e(Q_n)=\ln n$ exactly. For the <continuous uniform distribution>'s squared transformation above, the exact bin probabilities are
$$
p_j=\sqrt{\frac{j+1}{n}}-\sqrt{\frac jn}.
$$
The diagram computes these masses directly, including the bin next to the integrable singularity. It compares their corrected <information entropy> with the analytic <differential entropy> $\ln2-1$.

= Relative entropy and coordinate dependence
{parent=Differential entropy}

Apply the same invertible differentiable transformation to two <probability distributions> with densities $f$ and $r$. Both transformed densities acquire the same <Jacobian determinant> factor. Write their transformed densities as $f_g$ and $r_g$. Their ratio is unchanged, and the integration measure transforms with the numerator density. Consequently their <relative entropy> is unchanged:
$$
D(f_g\Vert r_g)=D(f\Vert r).
$$
The densities describe two alternative laws in the same coordinate, with a nonzero <Jacobian determinant> on their common domain, and the expression is assumed well defined. This explains why comparing two laws can survive a change of measurement units even though either law's <differential entropy> changes. <Mutual information>, formulated as <relative entropy> from a product of marginals, has the same invariance under invertible changes to either observed coordinate.

<Differential entropy> is also the entropy term in the <evidence lower bound> for continuous <variational inference>. Its coordinate dependence must be combined consistently with that of the model density; changing units does not justify changing only one term of the objective.

= Shannon's continuous-entropy coordinate convention
{c}
{parent=Differential entropy}

In <A Mathematical Theory of Communication by Claude Shannon> (1948), section 20 extends the discrete entropy formula to continuous densities. Properties 8–9 explicitly discuss coordinate dependence, possible negative values and linear rescaling. <Claude Shannon> relates this coordinate convention to the engineering use of entropy differences, rather than treating an isolated continuous entropy as an invariant count of messages.<A Mathematical Theory of Communication by Claude Shannon>[section 20, definition and properties 8–9, reprint pp. 35 and 37–38]{ref}

= Entropy power
{parent=Differential entropy}
{title2=$N(X)=(2\pi e)^{-1}2^{2h(X)/n}$}

The <entropy power> of an $n$-dimensional <random vector> with finite <differential entropy> in bits is the <variance> per coordinate of an isotropic <Gaussian distribution> with the same <differential entropy>. The normalization makes $N(X)=\sigma^2$ when $X$ has <covariance matrix> $\sigma^2I_n$.

= Entropy power inequality
{parent=Entropy power}
{wiki}
{title2=$N(X+Y)\geq N(X)+N(Y)$}

For independent $n$-dimensional <random vectors> with densities and finite <differential entropies>, the <entropy power inequality> states
$$
2^{2h(X+Y)/n}\geq2^{2h(X)/n}+2^{2h(Y)/n}.
$$
The sum's <differential entropy> is required to be defined; a value $+\infty$ makes the inequality immediate. Equality for finite nondegenerate densities occurs for <Gaussian distributions> with proportional <covariance matrices>.

= Multiplicative entropy power inequality
{parent=Entropy power inequality}

For independent positive <random variables> $Y_1,Y_2$ with densities, finite <differential entropies> and finite $\mu_i=\mathbb E\log_2Y_i$, applying the <entropy power inequality> to $Z_i=\ln Y_i$ gives
$$
2^{2h(Y_1Y_2)}\geq2^{2\mu_2}2^{2h(Y_1)}+2^{2\mu_1}2^{2h(Y_2)}.
$$
Indeed $h(Z_i)=h(Y_i)-\mu_i$, while $h(Y_1Y_2)=h(Z_1+Z_2)+\mu_1+\mu_2$, provided the sum's <differential entropy> is defined. Positivity alone does not ensure these expressions exist: $Y=e^T$ with $T$ having <Cauchy distribution> has undefined $\mathbb E\log_2Y$.

= Min-entropy
{title2=$H_\infty(X)$}
{parent=Information entropy}
{wiki}

For a <discrete random variable> $X$ with a finite or countable <alphabet> and <probability mass function> $p_x=\mathbb P(X=x)$, define $p_{\max}=\max_xp_x$. Its <min-entropy>, using base-two <logarithms>, is
$$
H_\infty(X)=-\log_2p_{\max}
=\min_{x:p_x>0}(-\log_2p_x).
$$
The unit is the <bit>. This is the smallest <information content> of any possible outcome: it records the easiest outcome to predict, whereas <Shannon entropy> averages <information content> over all outcomes. A normalized countable <probability mass function> has an attained positive maximum, so $H_\infty(X)$ is finite even when <Shannon entropy> is infinite. Outcomes of zero <probability> cannot minimize the surprise and are omitted from the minimum; including extra impossible symbols changes neither quantity.https://www.cs.cornell.edu/courses/cs6830/2009fa/lecnotes.pdf[Pass and shelat, A Course in Cryptography, Section 8.3.2, Definition 148.1]{ref}

Suppose someone knows the <probability mass function> but receives no observation correlated with $X$, and must name exactly one outcome. Naming $x$ succeeds with <probability> $p_x$, so naming a most likely outcome is optimal:
$$
p_{\mathrm{guess}}(X)=\max_x\mathbb P(X=x)=2^{-H_\infty(X)}.
$$
Tied maxima give equally good guesses. Randomizing independently among guesses cannot improve success: if the guess has <probability mass function> $q_x$, its success <probability> is $\sum_xq_xp_x\leq p_{\max}\sum_xq_x=p_{\max}$. This is the simplest instance of <maximum a posteriori decoding>. The identity gives <min-entropy> an operational meaning: $H_\infty(X)\geq k$ <bits> means every single guess succeeds with <probability> at most $2^{-k}$, under these information assumptions.https://arxiv.org/html/0807.1338[König, Renner and Schaffner, The operational meaning of min- and max-entropy, Section I.3.1, equation (18) and the no-side-information case]{ref}

For an exact analytical example on symbols $A,B,C,D,E$, compare the two <probability mass functions>
$$
u=(1/4,1/4,1/4,1/4,0),\qquad b=(1/2,1/8,1/8,1/8,1/8).
$$
Under $u$, each possible outcome has <information content> $2$ <bits>; under $b$, $A$ has <information content> $1$ <bit> and each other outcome has $3$ <bits>. Consequently their <Shannon entropies> agree exactly,
$$
H(u)=4\frac14\cdot2=2,\qquad H(b)=\frac12\cdot1+4\frac18\cdot3=2,
$$
but $H_\infty(u)=2$ and $H_\infty(b)=1$ <bits>. A best single guess therefore succeeds with <probability> $1/4$ under $u$ and $1/2$ under $b$. The same average surprise can hide a factor of two in immediate predictability. The zero entry in $u$ contributes zero to <Shannon entropy>, using $0\log_2 0=0$.

\Image[min-entropy-guessing-equal-average.png]
{title=Two exact probability models have the same average surprise but different best single guesses}

For the <discrete uniform distribution> on $m$ possible outcomes, $H_\infty=\log_2m$ <bits> and the best single guess succeeds with <probability> $1/m$. A certain outcome has $H_\infty=0$ and success <probability> one. Thus <min-entropy> is useful when a common outcome could defeat an attempt to make outputs unpredictable, even if rare alternatives raise <Shannon entropy>.

For <cryptography>, this unconditional calculation alone does not certify security: an observer might know $X$ already. If a report $Y=X$ reveals a uniform four-outcome source, its unconditional <min-entropy> remains $2$ <bits> but guessing after the report succeeds with <probability> one. For finite classical reports, the appropriate success calculation uses <conditional probabilities> and <maximum a posteriori decoding>:
$$
p_{\mathrm{guess}}(X\mid Y)=\sum_{y:p_Y(y)>0}p_Y(y)\max_x\mathbb P(X=x\mid Y=y).
$$
The <conditional entropy> $H(X\mid Y)$ instead averages remaining surprise. A security argument must specify the observer's information and the protocol's required guarantee; neither unconditional <min-entropy> nor <Shannon entropy> supplies those assumptions.

= Information entropy dominates min-entropy
{parent=Min-entropy}

For a <discrete random variable> $X$ with <probability mass function> $p_x$, using base-two <logarithms> for both <Shannon entropy> and <min-entropy>,
$$
H(X)\geq H_\infty(X)\geq0.
$$
The first inequality says that average <information content> cannot be below the least <information content> of a possible outcome. For a finite <alphabet>, equality $H(X)=H_\infty(X)$ holds exactly when the <probability mass function> is uniform on its positive-probability outcomes. Impossible outcomes do not need to share that positive <probability>. In particular, $(1/2,1/2,0)$ has both quantities equal to $1$ <bit>, while $(1,0,0)$ has both zero. The same equality characterization holds for a countable <alphabet>: equality forces only finitely many positive-probability outcomes, because all must have the same positive mass. The uniform maximum of <Shannon entropy> is discussed in Shannon's original treatment.<A Mathematical Theory of Communication by Claude Shannon>[Shannon, A Mathematical Theory of Communication, Section 6, definition of H and properties 1–2]{ref}

For the proof, $0<p_x\leq p_{\max}$ gives $-\log_2p_x\geq-\log_2p_{\max}$; taking the <expected value> proves <information entropy dominates min-entropy>. More precisely, the nonnegative gap is
$$
H(X)-H_\infty(X)
=\sum_{x:p_x>0}p_x\log_2\frac{p_{\max}}{p_x}\geq0.
$$
It vanishes precisely when every positive term vanishes, namely $p_x=p_{\max}$ for every possible outcome. With $m$ such outcomes, normalization forces $p_{\max}=1/m$. If <Shannon entropy> is infinite, the inequality still holds and is strict, because <min-entropy> of a countable <probability mass function> is finite. No subtraction of two infinite quantities is needed.

An analytical family makes the gap visible. Give $A$ <probability> $q$ and each of $B,C,D,E$ <probability> $(1-q)/4$, with $1/5\leq q\leq1$. Then $A$ is a most likely outcome, so its <probability> is also the best single-guess success <probability>, and
$$
H_\infty(q)=-\log_2q,\qquad H(q)=h_2(q)+2(1-q),
$$
where $h_2$ is <binary entropy>. The <entropy bound with one prescribed probability> explains the second expression: it separates whether $A$ occurred from which of four equally likely alternatives occurred. At $q=1/5$, all five outcomes are uniform and both quantities are $\log_2 5\approx2.3219$ <bits>. At $q=1/2$, <Shannon entropy> is $2$ <bits> while <min-entropy> is $1$ <bit>, reproducing the biased example in <min-entropy>. At $q=1$, both are zero; the four impossible alternatives contribute nothing. For every $1/5<q<1$, the <probability mass function> is nonuniform on its possible outcomes and the gap is strictly positive.

\Image[min-entropy-guessing-inequality-family.png]
{title=An analytical five-outcome model separates average surprise from the surprise of the most likely outcome}

For finite <Shannon entropy>, exponentiating the inequality gives the useful but potentially loose bound $p_{\mathrm{guess}}(X)\geq2^{-H(X)}$. Equality holds exactly for a <discrete uniform distribution> on the possible outcomes. Thus <Shannon entropy> alone supplies a lower bound on optimal success, not the upper bound needed to promise that every guess is unlikely. <Shannon's source coding theorem> concerns average compression under its hypotheses, while <min-entropy> answers the single-guess question directly. The two quantities serve different tasks even when both are expressed in <bits>.

= Binary entropy
{parent=Information entropy}
{title2=$h_2(p)$}

The binary entropy function is the <information entropy> of a <Bernoulli random variable>:
$$
h_2(p)=-p\log_2p-(1-p)\log_2(1-p),\qquad 0\leq p\leq1,
$$
with endpoint values zero. It is symmetric under $p\mapsto1-p$, reaches one <bit> at $p=1/2$, and has
$$
h_2'(p)=\log_2\frac{1-p}{p},\qquad h_2''(p)=-\frac1{(\ln2)p(1-p)}<0
$$
in the interior. Its strict <concavity> expresses why an even mixture of two outcomes has more uncertainty than a strong bias toward either one. The curve in <mutual information> also shows how this same uncertainty becomes the loss term in <binary symmetric channel capacity>.

= Chain rule for information entropy
{parent=Information entropy}
{title2=$H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y\mid X)$}

For finite <discrete random variables>, the chain rule counts uncertainty in two stages:
$$
H(X,Y)=H(X)+H(Y\mid X).
$$
The <joint entropy> equals the first variable's <information entropy> plus the average <conditional entropy> left in the second. All <logarithms> here have base two, so the unit is the <bit>; zero-probability terms contribute zero. The identity holds without assuming <independence>.<A Mathematical Theory of Communication by Claude Shannon>[section 6, property 5, reprint p. 12: joint entropy as first entropy plus average conditional entropy]{ref}

Iterating gives $H(X_1,\ldots,X_n)=\sum_iH(X_i\mid X_1,\ldots,X_{i-1})$. The first summand means $H(X_1)$, with no earlier observations. Each later summand counts only the new uncertainty after the preceding <random variables> are known. For <independent random variables>, every term is its ordinary <information entropy>; for dependent ones, adding those ordinary <information entropies> can count shared uncertainty repeatedly. This is the reason the <chain rule for information entropy> is useful in <lossless source coding> and in calculating an <entropy rate>.

A short proof starts from the factorization of the <joint probability mass function> into a <marginal distribution> and a <conditional probability>:
$$
p_{X,Y}(x,y)=p_X(x)p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x),\qquad
-\log_2 p_{X,Y}(x,y)=-\log_2 p_X(x)-\log_2p_{Y\mid X}(y\mid x).
$$
Use this only where $p_{X,Y}(x,y)>0$, then average with those joint <probabilities>. Summing over $y$ in the first term gives $H(X)$; the second is precisely $H(Y\mid X)$. Repeating the same step with a longer <random vector> proves the iterated identity. Reversing the order also gives $H(X,Y)=H(Y)+H(X\mid Y)$: the two contributions can change with the order, but their total cannot.

= Correlated binary variables
{parent=Chain rule for information entropy}
{title2=$B=A\oplus N,\quad H(A,B)=1+h_2(p)$}

Let $A$ be a fair <bit>, let $N$ be an <independent> <Bernoulli random variable> with $\mathbb P(N=1)=p$, and set $B=A\oplus N$, where $\oplus$ is <exclusive or>. Thus $N=1$ flips the first <bit>. This constructs a <binary symmetric channel> with a fair input, for $0\leq p\leq1$. The <joint probability mass function> is
$$
\begin{array}{c|cc|c}
& B=0&B=1&\mathbb P(A=a)\\\hline
A=0&(1-p)/2&p/2&1/2\\
A=1&p/2&(1-p)/2&1/2\\\hline
\mathbb P(B=b)&1/2&1/2&1
\end{array}
$$
The column sums show that $B$ is also a fair <bit>, whatever $p$ is. Both <marginal distributions> therefore have <information entropy> one <bit>. Given $A$, only the flip decision remains unknown, giving <conditional entropy> $H(B\mid A)=h_2(p)$, where
$$
h_2(p)=-p\log_2p-(1-p)\log_2(1-p)
$$
is the <binary entropy>, with $h_2(0)=h_2(1)=0$. The <chain rule for information entropy> and the definition of <mutual information> now give
$$
\boxed{H(A,B)=1+h_2(p),\qquad I(A;B)=1-h_2(p).}
$$

Take $p=1/10$. The four joint <probabilities>, in row order, are $(0.45,0.05,0.05,0.45)$. Directly from the definition of <joint entropy>,
$$
H(A,B)=-2(0.45)\log_2(0.45)-2(0.05)\log_2(0.05)
\approx1.468995594\ \text{bits}.
$$
Conditioning on either value of $A$ gives <probabilities> $0.9$ and $0.1$ for the two values of $B$, hence
$$
H(B\mid A)=h_2(0.1)\approx0.468995594\ \text{bits},\qquad
I(A;B)\approx0.531004406\ \text{bits}.
$$
Adding $H(A)+H(B)=2$ would overcount by exactly $I(A;B)$. These are average uncertainties under the stipulated <probability distribution>; the four possible pairs are still represented by four distinct outcomes.

{title=A correlated binary pair contains one bit plus the remaining flip uncertainty}
{description=Original exact-model <joint probability mass function> for $p=0.1$. The bars compare the sum of marginal <information entropies> with the <joint entropy> from the <chain rule for information entropy>. The hatched portion is the <mutual information> counted twice by the marginal sum. Values are calculated, not empirical frequencies.}

There is another way to see the decomposition: $(A,B)\mapsto(A,A\oplus B)=(A,N)$ is a <bijection>. It merely relabels the four outcomes, so it preserves <joint entropy>. The <independence> of $A$ and $N$ then gives $H(A,B)=H(A,N)=H(A)+H(N)=1+h_2(p)$. Describing the initial <bit> and the flip decision separates shared uncertainty from new uncertainty.

At $p=0$, the <bits> are identical: $B=A$, so $H(B\mid A)=0$ and $I(A;B)=1$. At $p=1$, they are complementary: $B=1-A$, with exactly the same <conditional entropy> and <mutual information>. In either case, observing one determines the other. At $p=1/2$, all four joint <probabilities> are $1/4$; the <bits> are <independent>, $H(A,B)=2$, and $I(A;B)=0$. Perfect disagreement is therefore just as informative as perfect agreement.

\Image[entropy-chain-rule-dependence-curves.png]
{title=Identical and complementary bits both reveal one full bit of information}
{description=Original calculated curves for the constructed pair $B=A\oplus N$: <joint entropy> $1+h_2(p)$, <conditional entropy> $h_2(p)$, and <mutual information> $1-h_2(p)$, all in <bits>. The horizontal line marks the one-bit <information entropy> of either <marginal distribution>. Reflection about $p=1/2$ exchanges likely agreement with likely disagreement while preserving these quantities.}

For $p>1/2$, guessing $A=1-B$ succeeds with <probability> $p$, better than guessing $A=B$. Changing $B$ to $1-B$ replaces $p$ by $1-p$ and preserves <mutual information>, since the relabeling is invertible. What matters is how well the observation distinguishes the input, rather than how often its symbol matches the input. The same symmetry makes the <binary entropy> curve peak at <independence>, while the <mutual information> curve reaches its minimum there.

= Subadditivity of information entropy
{parent=Information entropy}

The <chain rule for information entropy> and <conditioning reduces entropy> give
$$
H(X,Y)\leq H(X)+H(Y),
$$
with equality exactly when $X$ and $Y$ are <independent random variables>[independent].

= Concavity of information entropy
{parent=Information entropy}

For probability mass functions $P_1,\ldots,P_m$ and convex weights $\alpha_i$, information entropy satisfies
$$
H\!\left(\sum_i\alpha_iP_i\right)\geq\sum_i\alpha_iH(P_i).
$$
This follows from the <concavity> of $-t\log t$.

= Maximum entropy distribution on a finite set
{parent=Information entropy}

Among <probability distributions> on a finite set of $m$ elements, the <uniform distribution> uniquely maximizes <information entropy>, attaining $\log m$.

= Maximum entropy distribution with a prescribed finite mean
{parent=Maximum entropy distribution on a finite set}

For a finite list of real values $y_i$, maximize <information entropy> $H=-\sum_i p_i\log p_i$ with $p_i>0$, $\sum_i p_i=1$, and $\sum_i p_i y_i=Y$. <Lagrange multipliers> give
$$
p_i=\frac{e^{-by_i}}{Z(b)},\qquad
Z(b)=\sum_j e^{-by_j},\qquad
Y=-\frac{d}{db}\log Z(b).
$$
The constrained second variation is $-\sum_i(\delta p_i)^2/p_i<0$ for any nonzero tangent perturbation, proving uniqueness of the entropy maximum whenever a feasible interior solution exists. The mean has derivative $-\operatorname{Var}_b(y)$, so for nonconstant $y_i$ it decreases strictly with $b$ and ranges between the largest and smallest $y_i$ as $b$ ranges over the real line. Thus a finite $b$ exists uniquely exactly when $\min_i y_i<Y<\max_i y_i$.

At an extreme mean, strictly positive probabilities are impossible unless all $y_i$ coincide. If all $y_i=y_0$, the mean constraint is redundant when $Y=y_0$, the maximum is uniform, and $b$ is not identifiable. This finite <exponential family> is an <exponential tilting> of the uniform distribution.

= Maximum entropy on a finite alphabet
{title2=$H(X)\leq\log_2m$}
{parent=Maximum entropy distribution on a finite set}

An <information entropy> on at most $m$ outcomes is at most $\log_2m$, with equality for the uniform distribution on all $m$ outcomes. For the uniform reference $u$, the <Kullback-Leibler divergence> is $D(p\|u)=\log_2m-H(p)\geq0$, proving the bound.

= Maximum entropy distribution on the nonnegative integers
{parent=Information entropy}

Among distributions on $\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ with fixed <expected value> $\mu$, the <geometric distribution> with success probability $1/(1+\mu)$ uniquely maximizes <information entropy>. Its entropy is
$$
(1+\mu)h_2\!\left(\frac1{1+\mu}\right).
$$
