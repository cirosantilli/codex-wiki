= Calorimetry
{wiki}

Calorimetry measures <heat> transfers. A <calorimeter>, a calibrated <heat capacity> and an accounting of unwanted <heat transfer> connect an observed <temperature> change to an <energy> balance, as in <heat-engine calorimetry> and <heat-pump calorimetry>.

The central idea is to measure a response that is easier to observe than the transfer itself: a known <heat capacity> turns a <temperature> change into an <energy> estimate. <Mixing calorimetry> can determine an unknown <specific heat capacity>, while a <chemical reaction> can be studied by measuring the response of its surroundings. The useful observable is a change in <temperature>, together with a stated <thermodynamic system> boundary; a hot object does not contain a separately conserved store of <heat>.

For a <closed system> with unchanged bulk <kinetic energy> and <potential energy>, let $Q_{\rm in}$ be net inward <heat> and $W_{\rm on}$ net inward <work>. The <first law of thermodynamics> gives
$$
\Delta U=Q_{\rm in}+W_{\rm on}.
$$
If the boundary includes a hot sample, receiving <water> and the participating vessel, their mutual <heat transfer> is internal. With no external input, the total storage change is minus the signed outward <heat> leakage, not the positive amount transferred from sample to <water>. A <calorimeter calibration> with an immersed <resistor> instead supplies electrical <work> across this boundary; <Joule heating> converts it into <internal energy>. Choosing only the receiving <water> as the boundary changes which transfers count as external.

The mechanical constraint decides which storage quantity the readout represents. For a <closed system> at fixed <volume (thermodynamics)>, with no other <work>, $Q_V=\Delta U$. At constant external <pressure>, with only expansion <work> $p\Delta V$, $Q_p=\Delta H$, where $H=U+pV$ is <enthalpy>. Thus the corresponding warming balances use <heat capacity at constant volume> or <heat capacity at constant pressure>. Electrical or shaft <work> must be included separately when present. These endpoint balances do not require the actual exchange to be a <reversible thermodynamic process>.

A covered cup open to atmospheric <pressure> approximately realizes the second constraint when escaping material is negligible. A sealed rigid reaction vessel realizes the first; its surrounding <water>, vessel and immersed parts form the calibrated receiver. The reaction's outward <heat> equals the receiver's gain only after losses and ignition input have been accounted for. The distinction concerns the reaction boundary, even if its receiver is open to atmospheric <pressure>.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/5.2 Calorimetry>[§5.2, paragraph beginning “If the amount of heat absorbed by a calorimeter”, and Figure 5.17: vessel storage, constant-pressure versus constant-volume apparatus and calibration]{ref}

A historical quantitative result appears in <James Prescott Joule>'s <On the Mechanical Equivalent of Heat> (1850): Table IX gives $772.692$ foot-pounds of mechanical <work> per warming of one pound of <water> by one degree Fahrenheit, with weighing reduced to vacuum. His final rounded statement specifies <water> between $55$ and $60$ degrees Fahrenheit. He acknowledged residual vibration and sound as possible upward bias. This is an original reported conversion result, not the definition of the modern <joule>; the apparatus and resting controls are described in <Joule's paddle-wheel experiment>.<On the Mechanical Equivalent of Heat by James Prescott Joule>[p. 82, Table IX and concluding paragraphs: water-series equivalent and residual losses]{ref}

= Differential scanning calorimetry
{parent=Calorimetry}

<differential scanning calorimetry> measures differences in heat flow between sample and reference during a controlled temperature scan. Transition peaks can reveal cooperative lipid-chain melting, with scan rate, composition and reversibility affecting interpretation.

= Mixing calorimetry
{parent=Calorimetry}
{title2=$m_xc_x(T_x-T_f)=(m_wc_w+C_{\rm cal})(T_f-T_w)+Q_{\rm loss,out}$}

Mixing calorimetry determines <heat capacities> by bringing bodies at different <temperatures> into thermal contact. The striking feature is that the final <temperature> is a <weighted arithmetic mean> with <heat capacities> as weights, rather than weights given by <mass>. The vessel participates in the exchange even though it is not the sample being studied.

For a hot nonreacting sample of <mass> $m_x$, initial <temperature> $T_x$ and unknown <specific heat capacity at constant pressure> $c_x$, let receiving <water> have <mass> $m_w$, initial <temperature> $T_w$ and known <specific heat capacity at constant pressure> $c_w$. Let the vessel and immersed parts start at $T_w$ and have combined <heat capacity> $C_{\rm cal}$, excluding that <water>. Assume a common final <temperature> $T_f$, approximately constant <pressure> and <heat capacities>, no <phase transition>, no <chemical reaction> and negligible external nonexpansion <work>. For signed outward <heat> loss $Q_{\rm loss,out}$, the combined <enthalpy> balance is
$$
m_xc_x(T_f-T_x)+(m_wc_w+C_{\rm cal})(T_f-T_w)=-Q_{\rm loss,out}.
$$
Hence
$$
c_x=\frac{(m_wc_w+C_{\rm cal})(T_f-T_w)+Q_{\rm loss,out}}{m_x(T_x-T_f)}.
$$
The loss is positive if the room receives net <heat>, negative if the room supplies it. If the vessel starts at a different <temperature> $T_{{\rm cal},i}$, replace its warming term by $C_{\rm cal}(T_f-T_{{\rm cal},i})$; do not assume the vessel shared the <water>'s initial <temperature>. For variable material properties, replace each $mc\Delta T$ by the corresponding <integral> $m\int c_p(T)\,dT$ and each vessel term by $\int C_{\rm cal}(T)\,dT$. The inferred constant $c_x$ then represents an average over the sample's cooling interval.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/5.2 Calorimetry>[Figure 5.14 and Example 5.4: exchange between metal and water and inference of unknown specific heat]{ref}

For a synthetic no-loss example, mix $0.100\,\mathrm{kg}$ of hot <water> at $80\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ with $0.200\,\mathrm{kg}$ of cold <water> at $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ in a vessel initially at $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. Stipulate $c_w=4180\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ and $C_{\rm cal}=100\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$. The <enthalpy> balance gives
$$
T_f=\frac{(418)(80)+(836+100)(20)}{418+836+100}\,{}^\circ\mathrm C
\approx38.523\,{}^\circ\mathrm C.
$$
The hot <water> releases $17.337\,\mathrm{kJ}$: the cold <water> receives $15.485\,\mathrm{kJ}$ and the vessel $1.852\,\mathrm{kJ}$. Omitting the vessel predicts $40\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, despite perfect insulation. These are computed numbers from stipulated properties, not laboratory observations.

For an unknown-sample example using the same cold <water> and vessel, stipulate $m_x=0.100\,\mathrm{kg}$, $T_x=80\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$ and $c_x=450\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$. With no loss, $T_f\approx22.752\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$. Inverting the full balance returns $450\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$; ignoring vessel storage returns about $402\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$. With a positive outward loss, ignoring that loss also biases the inferred $c_x$ downward under these hot-sample conditions. A small <water> <temperature> rise makes sensor offsets and loss estimates relatively influential; <propagation of uncertainty> must include the measured <temperatures>, <masses> and calibrated vessel term.

= Calorimeter
{parent=Calorimetry}
{wiki}

A calorimeter infers transferred <heat> from a measured change, such as a <temperature> change and a calibrated <heat capacity>. The <heat-engine calorimetry> example requires accounting for the vessel and <heat transfer> to the surroundings as well as the <water>.

A practical receiver has an inner vessel, insulation or an outer shield, a lid, a <thermometer> and a stirrer. Its participating <heat capacity> includes the immersed parts that follow the <water>'s <temperature>. Insulation reduces <heat transfer> to the room; stirring makes a single reading more representative of the contents. These serve different purposes: a well-insulated vessel can still have substantial <heat capacity>, and a uniform <temperature> does not imply zero external <heat transfer>.

The University of Toronto's <Heat Capacity (University of Toronto laboratory handout)> describes a <copper> inner vessel, outer shield, <thermometer> and heated metal block. Weigh the block and receiving <water>, heat the block in a bath, then transfer it quickly into enough <water> to cover it. The observed <temperature> rises, reaches a maximum, then falls. The inferred <specific heat capacity> comes from the block's cooling and the warming of both <water> and vessel. In its cooling exercise the handout deliberately exposes the inner vessel to room air, recommends readings every $15\,\mathrm s$, and uses the later common cooling to estimate the earlier loss. Starting below room <temperature> reduces exchange without guaranteeing cancellation. The handout notes limited time and thermometry for small corrections; it supplies no dataset.<Heat Capacity (University of Toronto laboratory handout)>[pp. 1–3, The Method of Mixtures and Cooling Correction, Figure 1]{ref}

For a proposed modern version, independently perform <thermometer calibration> and <calorimeter calibration>, record bath and room <temperatures>, and keep immersion depth, lid position and stirring rate fixed. Ensure the block actually reaches bath <temperature>; minimize and account for adhering hot bath <water>. Compare repeated transfers with different delays and a reference sample of known <specific heat capacity>. A resting run tests environmental <heat transfer>, while a stirred run with no added sample checks mechanical input. These are proposed controls, not claimed observations from the handout. Sensor delay, changing <heat capacity>, incomplete <thermal equilibrium> and material escaping the boundary limit the resulting estimate.

= Calorimeter calibration
{parent=Calorimeter}
{title2=$C_{\rm eff}=m_wc_w+C_{\rm cal}$}

Calorimeter calibration establishes the response of the actual measurement assembly to a known <energy> input. It determines a participating <heat capacity>, not just the empty vessel's <mass>. For an electrical calibration with an immersed <resistor>, measure <voltage> $V(t)$ across that <resistor> and <electric current> $I(t)$ through it. If that electrical <work> is deposited inside the chosen boundary, its input is
$$
E_{\rm el}=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}V(t)I(t)\,dt
=C_{\rm eff}(T_1-T_0)+Q_{\rm loss,out},\qquad
C_{\rm eff}=m_wc_w+C_{\rm cal}.
$$
For a covered receiver at constant <pressure>, $C_{\rm eff}$ describes <enthalpy> storage; for a rigid fixed-<volume (thermodynamics)> assembly, use the matching <heat capacity at constant volume>. Negligible endpoint mechanical storage and no escaping material are assumed. Thus $E_{\rm el}/(T_1-T_0)$ overestimates $C_{\rm eff}$ if outward loss is ignored. Account for lead losses outside the boundary and maintain the same <water> charge and immersed hardware in calibration and sample runs.

An alternative calibration mixes known hot and cold <water> portions. With the vessel initially at the cold portion's <temperature> $T_c$, the <mixing calorimetry> balance gives
$$
C_{\rm cal}=\frac{m_hc_w(T_h-T_f)-Q_{\rm loss,out}}{T_f-T_c}-m_cc_w.
$$
The previous synthetic <water> example returns $100\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$. The sign matters: attributing room losses to the vessel makes this estimate too large. With both $C_{\rm cal}$ and the loss unknown, one final <temperature> supplies only one equation; separate cooling information or controlled independent runs are needed.

A <calorimeter heat-loss correction> can make the electrical balance quantitative. For a second synthetic example, stipulate $0.100\,\mathrm{kg}$ of <water> with $c_w=4180\,\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$ and immersed hardware with $C_{\rm cal}=82\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$, so $C_{\rm eff}=500\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$. Supply $10\,\mathrm W$ for $300\,\mathrm s$, starting at a room <temperature> of $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, with loss coefficient $G=1\,\mathrm{W\,K^{-1}}$. The model below gives $T_1\approx24.512\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, storage $2255.942\,\mathrm J$ and outward loss $744.058\,\mathrm J$. The uncorrected ratio gives about $665\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$; subtracting the loss recovers $500\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$.

= Calorimeter heat-loss correction
{parent=Calorimeter}
{title2=$Q_{\rm loss,out}=G\int(T-T_a)\,dt$}

A calorimeter heat-loss correction estimates the signed <heat> exchanged with the room during the measurement. Under <Newton's law of cooling>, a constant room <temperature> $T_a$ and a single representative assembly <temperature> $T$ give the model
$$
C_{\rm eff}\frac{dT}{dt}=P(t)-G(T-T_a),\qquad
Q_{\rm loss,out}=G\int_{t_0}^{t_1}(T(t)-T_a)\,dt.
$$
Here $P(t)$ is deposited input <power>, and $G$ is the apparatus loss coefficient in $\mathrm{W\,K^{-1}}$. After input ceases, the <temperature> excess shows <exponential decay> with <time constant> $\tau=C_{\rm eff}/G$:
$$
T(t)-T_a=(T(t_1)-T_a)e^{-(t-t_1)/\tau}.
$$
A cooling trace determines the ratio $G/C_{\rm eff}$, not the two factors separately. Combining the cooling trace with a known electrical <energy> input supplies the missing information. For constant input $P_0$, starting at $T_a$, the heating solution is $T(t)-T_a=(P_0/G)(1-e^{-t/\tau})$; these solutions generate the calibration figure.

For <mixing calorimetry>, infer cooling behavior only after the sample, <water> and vessel approach a common <temperature>. Applying a single-body cooling model while the sample remains much hotter than the <water> can misestimate the earlier loss. The Toronto handout's area correction assumes the same loss mechanism represents both the mixing interval and subsequent cooling.<Heat Capacity (University of Toronto laboratory handout)>[p. 3, Figure 1 and the area-ratio cooling correction]{ref} A backward extrapolation to the transfer time is therefore a model-dependent estimate, not the ultimate equilibrium with the room. Extend the model or report a limitation if sensor delay, substantial <temperature gradients>, changing room <temperature>, or escaping material invalidate that assumption. In particular, the relevant $C_{\rm eff}$ after adding a sample includes its <heat capacity>; an empty-receiver cooling rate need not apply to the loaded receiver.
