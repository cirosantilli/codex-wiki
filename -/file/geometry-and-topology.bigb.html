= Geometry and topology
{wiki}

How much information about a shape do we need to keep? <Euclidean geometry> retains <lengths> and <angles>, allowing questions about the diagonal of a <rectangle> or the shortest route between two points. <Topology> retains the structure preserved by a <homeomorphism>, allowing questions about <connectedness> and whether a loop can shrink to a point, as recorded by the <fundamental group>. These are different levels of description of the same object.

For example, stretching a <circle> horizontally produces an <ellipse>. The stretching map and its inverse are <continuous maps>, so it gives a <homeomorphism>: the two curves have the same <topology>. It changes some <Euclidean distances>, however, so it is not an <isometry>. This distinction helps separate essential structure from a particular drawing, while explaining why measuring a shape requires more information than recognizing its <topology>.

The choice of geometry matters too. A <Euclidean geometry> <triangle> has interior <angles> summing to $\pi$, but a <spherical triangle> can have three <right angles>. <Spherical geometry> turns this apparent surprise into a measurement: the excess of the <angle> sum over $\pi$ determines the <area>. Begin with the <Pythagorean theorem> for flat measurements, then compare the <spherical triangle> below to see <curvature> change the rules.

= Cylinder
{disambiguate=geometry}
{parent=Geometry and topology}
{wiki=Cylinder}

A <cylinder (geometry)> is generated by translating a <plane curve> along parallel lines transverse to its plane. A <circular cylinder> uses a <circle> as its generating <plane curve>; it is right when the generators are perpendicular to that plane. The term can denote the lateral surface or, for a bounded portion with end caps, the enclosed solid. The <circular cylinder> curvature formulas describe the smooth lateral surface.

= Parametric equation
{parent=Geometry and topology}
{wiki}

A <parametric equation> expresses coordinates as <functions> of one or more independent parameters. For example, $x=\cos t$, $y=\sin t$ describes a <circle>; multiple parameters describe surfaces. A <smooth curve> is a differentiable single-parameter realization. A <reparametrization> changes the parameter without changing the geometric image.

= Embedding
{parent=Geometry and topology}
{wiki}

An <embedding> identifies one mathematical structure with a structure-preserving copy inside another. In <topology> it is a <homeomorphism> onto its image with the <subspace topology>; an algebraic <embedding> is an injective <homomorphism>. A <smooth embedding> additionally has injective <differential> everywhere, so its image is an <embedded submanifold>.

= Pencil
{disambiguate=geometry}
{parent=Geometry and topology}
{wiki}

A <pencil (geometry)> is a one-parameter linear family of geometric objects. For linearly independent homogeneous <polynomials> $F,G$ of equal degree, $sF+tG=0$, indexed by $[s:t]\in\mathbb P^1$, is a <pencil (geometry)> of <plane curves> or <hypersurfaces>. Lines through a fixed point give a simple example; a <pencil of plane curves> specializes the general construction.

= Spherical geometry
{parent=Geometry and topology}
{wiki}

<Spherical geometry> studies <lengths> and <angles> measured along the surface of a <sphere>, rather than through its interior. It is a useful model whenever travel is constrained to a round surface. Its straightest paths are <great circle> arcs: they are <geodesics>, even though they appear curved in the surrounding <Euclidean space>. Two distinct <great circles> meet at an <antipodal pair>, so the parallel-line picture of <Euclidean geometry> does not apply.

For a <sphere> of radius $R>0$, write two positions as $Ru,Rv$, with $u,v$ <unit vectors>. Their shorter surface route has <great-circle distance>
$$
d=R\arccos(u\cdot v),
$$
where the <dot product> gives the central <angle> in <radians>. The straight chord through the surrounding <Euclidean space> instead has <length> $2R\sin(d/(2R))$. For a quarter-circle separation these are $\pi R/2\approx1.571R$ and $\sqrt2R\approx1.414R$: specifying which distance we mean has a measurable effect. On the <unit sphere>, $R=1$ and <great-circle distance> equals the central <angle>.

Consider the <spherical triangle> with vertices $(R,0,0)$, $(0,R,0)$ and $(0,0,R)$, joined by the shorter <great circle> arcs. Each side has <length> $\pi R/2$. At each vertex the two outgoing <tangent vectors> point along the other coordinate axes and are <orthogonal>, so all three interior <angles> are $\pi/2$. This is the positive coordinate octant of the <sphere>, not a flat <triangle> drawn inside it.

For a convex minor <spherical triangle> with interior <angles> $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ in <radians>, the <spherical excess formula> states
$$
\mathcal A=R^2(\alpha+\beta+\gamma-\pi).
$$
Our octant therefore has <area> $R^2(3\pi/2-\pi)=\pi R^2/2$. Independently, eight congruent octants partition the <sphere>, giving $4\pi R^2/8$ again. Thus <angles> alone determine <area> when $R$ is fixed, unlike <Euclidean geometry>, where <similar triangles> can have arbitrarily different <areas>. The <Gauss-Bonnet theorem> explains the connection: the <sphere>'s constant <Gaussian curvature> $1/R^2$ times the <area> equals this excess. For <spherical triangles> whose <area> is small compared with $R^2$, the excess is small, recovering the familiar flat picture.

= Convex regular spherical polygon
{parent=Spherical geometry}
{title2=$\frac{n-2}{n}\pi<\alpha<\pi$}

A nondegenerate convex regular spherical $n$-gon contained in an <open hemisphere> has equal interior angles $\alpha$ in the displayed interval. The <spherical excess formula> gives area $n\alpha-(n-2)\pi>0$. Conversely, put equally spaced vertices at colatitude $t\in(0,\pi/2)$ around a pole. Dividing each central triangle in half gives $\cot(\alpha/2)=\cos t\tan(\pi/n)$ by the <spherical law of cosines>. As $t$ increases, the angle ranges continuously from the Euclidean limiting angle to $\pi$.

= Spherical circle
{parent=Spherical geometry}
{title2=$C(r)=2\pi R\sin(r/R)$}

On a sphere of radius $R$, a spherical circle of radius $r\in(0,\pi R)$ is the locus at <spherical distance> $r$ from its center. Its polar angle about that center is $r/R$, so parametrization by longitude has speed $R\sin(r/R)$. Its circumference is therefore $2\pi R\sin(r/R)$. For small $r$, this is $2\pi r-\pi r^3/(3R^2)+O(r^5)$, with the deficit determined by the sphere's positive <Gaussian curvature>.

= Spherical lune
{parent=Spherical geometry}

A spherical lune is the region between two great semicircles joining antipodal points. On a unit sphere a lune of opening angle $\alpha$ has area $2\alpha$, obtained by integrating area over longitude width $\alpha$. Lune area counting yields the <spherical excess formula>.

= Great-circle distance
{parent=Spherical geometry}
{title2=$d(u,v)=R\arccos(u\cdot v)$}

= Spherical distance
{synonym}

For points on a <sphere> of radius $R$, write their position <vectors> relative to the centre as $Ru,Rv$ with $u,v$ <unit vectors>. The <great-circle distance> is the shorter <great circle> arc length, $R$ times its central angle. Hence it equals $R\arccos(u\cdot v)$, including zero for identical points and $\pi R$ for antipodal points. At antipodal points the shortest arc is not unique, but its length is.

= Spherical trigonometry
{parent=Spherical geometry}
{wiki}

<Spherical trigonometry> relates side lengths and angles of <spherical triangles>. Side lengths are measured as central angles, or equivalently arc lengths on the <unit sphere>.

= Spherical law of sines
{parent=Spherical trigonometry}
{title2=$\sin A/\sin a=\sin B/\sin b=\sin C/\sin c$}

For a nondegenerate convex minor <spherical triangle>, its <scalar triple product> $\tau=u\cdot(v\times w)$ satisfies $|\tau|=\sin b\sin c\sin A$, and cyclically. Dividing by $\sin a\sin b\sin c$ proves the <spherical law of sines>.

= Spherical law of cosines
{parent=Spherical trigonometry}
{wiki}
{title2=$\cos a=\cos b\cos c+\sin b\sin c\cos A$}

For a convex minor <spherical triangle> with opposite side-angle pairs $(a,A),(b,B),(c,C)$, the displayed identity is the <spherical law of cosines>. At a vertex with unit position <vector> $u$, the two outgoing unit tangent <vectors> are $(v-(u\cdot v)u)/\sin c$ and $(w-(u\cdot w)u)/\sin b$. Taking their <dot product> gives the identity.

= Spherical triangle
{parent=Spherical geometry}

A <spherical triangle> is bounded by three <great circle> arcs. For the usual convex minor triangle, the vertices are neither antipodal nor on one <great circle>, and its sides and interior angles lie in $(0,\pi)$.

= Spherical triangle reflection group
{parent=Spherical triangle}
{title2=$|G|=\frac4{1/p+1/q+1/r-1}$}

Reflections in the sides of a spherical triangle of angles $\pi/p,\pi/q,\pi/r$ generate a finite <group> when its images tessellate the sphere. The triangle area is $\pi(1/p+1/q+1/r-1)$ by the <spherical excess formula>. Label its three vertex types and continue those labels across each reflected side; they are consistent around every vertex and, because the sphere is <simply connected>, globally. The generated group preserves these labels, so a chamber stabilizer fixes all three vertices and is trivial. Dividing sphere area by chamber area gives its order. Up to permutation the possibilities are $(2,2,n)$ for $n\geq2$, and $(2,3,3),(2,3,4),(2,3,5)$, of orders $4n,24,48,120$.

= Minkowski sum
{c}
{synonym}

The <Minkowski sum> of subsets $A,B$ of a <vector space> is $A+B=\{a+b:a\in A,b\in B\}$. <Minkowski addition> also makes sense for nonconvex sets; translations and positive dilations commute with it.

= Sum of two closed sets need not be closed
{parent=Minkowski addition}

In $\mathbb R$, the sets $X=\{n:n\ge1\}$ and $Y=\{-n+1/n:n\ge1\}$ are closed because their points have no finite accumulation point. Their <Minkowski sum> contains $1/n\to0$ but does not contain zero, since $m-n+1/n=0$ has no positive-integer solution. Compactness of one summand is a useful sufficient condition missing from this counterexample.

= Sum of a compact set and a closed set
{parent=Minkowski addition}

In Euclidean space, a <Minkowski sum> of a compact set $X$ and a closed set $Y$ is closed. From any convergent sequence $x_j+y_j$, extract a convergent subsequence of $x_j$ using compactness. The corresponding $y_j$ then converges to the difference of the two limits, which lies in $Y$. If $Y$ is also bounded, the sum is bounded and therefore compact.

= Brunn-Minkowski theorem
{c}
{parent=Minkowski addition}
{title2=$\lambda_n(A+B)^{1/n}\geq\lambda_n(A)^{1/n}+\lambda_n(B)^{1/n}$}
{wiki=Brunn–Minkowski_theorem}

= Brunn–Minkowski inequality
{c}
{synonym}

For nonempty <open sets> $A,B\subset\mathbb R^n$, the <Lebesgue measure> of their <Minkowski sum> satisfies
$$
\lambda_n(A+B)^{1/n}\geq\lambda_n(A)^{1/n}+\lambda_n(B)^{1/n}.
$$
It also holds for compact sets and in standard measurable-set formulations with the appropriate measurability qualification. Normalize both volumes to one and apply the <Prékopa–Leindler inequality> to <indicator functions>.

= Affine geometry
{parent=Geometry and topology}
{wiki}

The geometry of <affine spaces>, studying incidence and parallelism without choosing an origin or a metric.

= Affine space
{parent=Affine geometry}
{title2=$A$}
{wiki}

A nonempty set $A$ equipped with a <group action> of the additive group of a <vector space> $V$, such that for every $x,y\in A$ there is a unique $v\in V$ with $x+v=y$. There is no distinguished origin. Choosing one identifies $A$ with $V$; changing it gives a <translation>.

= Affine independence
{parent=Affine space}
{title2=$\sum_i\lambda_i p_i=0,\ \sum_i\lambda_i=0\ \Longrightarrow\ \lambda_i=0$}

= Affinely independent
{synonym}

Points $p_0,\ldots,p_k$ in an <affine space> are <affinely independent> if the only affine dependence displayed above has all coefficients zero, using any choice of origin to read the <vector> equation. Since the coefficients sum to zero, replacing the origin leaves that equation unchanged. Substituting $\lambda_0=-\sum_{i=1}^k\lambda_i$ shows that this condition is equivalent to <linear independence> of $p_1-p_0,\ldots,p_k-p_0$. It also says that an affine combination of the points has unique coefficients. In $\mathbb R^n$, at most $n+1$ points can be <affinely independent>; $n+1$ such points span a full-dimensional <simplex>.

= Integer lattice
{parent=Affine space}
{title2=$\mathbb Z^d$}

The points with <integer> coordinates in a real d-dimensional <affine space> with a chosen origin and coordinate basis. <Translations> by <integer> <vectors> and dilations by <positive integers> preserve this set. The <Gallai theorem for an integer lattice> concerns <finite colourings> of these points and <homothetic copies> of finite configurations of them.

= Homothetic copy of a finite configuration
{parent=Affine space}
{title2=$a+tF,\quad t>0$}

= Homothetic copy
{synonym}

A <translation> and positive uniform scaling of a finite configuration $F$ in a <real vector space>. It preserves the ratios and directions of differences between its points. In an <integer lattice>, the <Gallai theorem for an integer lattice> guarantees a <monochromatic> <homothetic copy> of every finite pattern in any <finite colouring>, with <positive integer> scaling factor.

= Affine combination
{parent=Affine space}
{wiki}

An <affine combination> of points $P_i$ is $\sum_i a_iP_i$ with $\sum_i a_i=1$, allowing negative coefficients. It is independent of the choice of origin: translating every point by $T$ translates the result by $T$. It also commutes with every <affine map>. When all coefficients are nonnegative, it is a <convex combination>, placing the result inside the <convex hull> of the points.

= Affine map
{parent=Affine space}
{title2=$x\mapsto Ax+b$}

A map between <affine spaces> whose difference map is linear. After choosing origins it is $x\mapsto Ax+b$, where $A$ is its <linear part of an affine map> and $b$ its translation. This definition allows noninvertible maps; invertibility requires $A$ invertible.

= Euclidean similarity
{parent=Affine map}
{c}
{title2=$S(x)=cRx+b$}

A <Euclidean similarity> is a map $S:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^n$ satisfying $|S(x)-S(y)|=c|x-y|$ for a fixed $c>0$. It has the form $S(x)=cRx+b$ with $R$ orthogonal: after subtracting $S(0)$ and dividing by $c$, the map fixes zero and preserves distances, hence inner products by polarization, so it is a linear orthogonal map. It is a contraction if $c<1$. Contracting <Euclidean similarities> define self-similar <compact sets>; the <open set condition> is a useful separation hypothesis for computing their <Hausdorff dimension>.

= Linear part of an affine map
{parent=Affine map}
{title2=$x\mapsto Ax+b$}

The map $A$ associated with an <affine map> $x\mapsto Ax+b$; it is independent of the translation $b$. An affine <Euclidean isometry> has an orthogonal linear part, and an <isometry> between <flat tori> has a lift whose linear part carries one <Euclidean lattice> onto the other.

= Half-space
{parent=Affine space}
{title2=$\{x:a^Tx\le b\},\quad a\ne0$}

A closed half-space consists of a <hyperplane> and one of its two sides in a real <affine space>. Replacing the weak inequality by a strict inequality gives an open half-space. Half-spaces are <convex sets>, and finite <intersections> of closed <half-spaces> are <linear polyhedra>.

= Translation
{disambiguate=geometry}
{parent=Affine space}
{title2=$T_v(x)=x+v$}

= Translation
{synonym}

On an <affine space> modeled on a <vector space>, the map $T_v(x)=x+v$ for a fixed vector $v$. Its <derivative> is the identity, and $T_vT_w=T_{v+w}$. Changes between <flat coordinates> on a <translation surface> have this form.

= Moduli space
{parent=Geometry and topology}
{title2=$\mathcal M$}

A space parametrizing isomorphism classes of geometric objects. Its topology and possible stabilizers are part of the definition; a <moduli space of Riemann surfaces> forgets the marking retained by <Teichmüller space>.

= SU2 character variety of the punctured torus
{parent=Moduli space}
{c}
{title2=$SU(2)^2/SU(2)\cong\overline B^3$}

The punctured torus retracts onto two circles, so its flat $SU(2)$ gauge-orbit space is $SU(2)^2/SU(2)$ under simultaneous conjugation. Write unit quaternions as $A=a+u$ and $B=b+v$. Complete orbit invariants are $(a,b,u\cdot v)$ with $(u\cdot v)^2\leq(1-a^2)(1-b^2)$. Adding $ab$ to the third coordinate identifies this space with the compact convex set of positive-semidefinite $3\times3$ real correlation matrices, a closed three-ball. The dense open subset $A\ne\pm1$ is a closed disk times an open interval; its complement is two disjoint intervals.

= Riemann surface moduli space
{synonym}
{c}

The <moduli space> of unmarked <compact> <genus>-$g$ <Riemann surfaces>. For $g\geq2$ it is the quotient $\mathcal T_g/\operatorname{Mod}(S_g)$ of <Teichmüller space> by the <mapping class group>, with its quotient topology. The thick subsets are <compact> by <Mumford's compactness theorem>.

= Punctured Riemann surface moduli dimension
{parent=Moduli space of Riemann surfaces}
{title2=$\dim_{\mathbb C}\mathcal M_{g,n}=3g-3+n$}

For stable compact <Riemann surfaces> with $n$ distinct labelled marked points, where $2g-2+n>0$, the displayed complex dimension counts independent conformal shapes and positions after conformal automorphisms are removed. On the sphere it is $n-3$, since three points fix a <Möbius transformation>. On a torus it is $1+n-1=n$: one shape modulus and $n$ positions, less one translation. For <genus> $g\ge2$, the unmarked shape dimension is $3g-3$ and continuous conformal automorphisms vanish. The shape dimension also follows from the <Riemann-Roch theorem> applied to <holomorphic quadratic differentials>.

= Mumford's compactness theorem
{c}
{parent=Moduli space of Riemann surfaces}
{title2=$\mathcal M_g^\varepsilon\text{ is compact}$}
{wiki}

The subset of the unmarked <moduli space of Riemann surfaces> with <hyperbolic systole> at least $\varepsilon>0$ is <compact> for fixed <genus> $g\geq2$. Use a <Bers pants decomposition theorem> bound, finitely many topological decomposition types and <Dehn twists> to place representatives in finitely many <compact> boxes in <Fenchel–Nielsen coordinates>.

= Foliation
{parent=Geometry and topology}
{wiki}

A foliation decomposes a <topological manifold> into connected leaves which locally look like parallel slices in product coordinate charts.

= Godbillon-Vey invariant
{parent=Foliation}
{c}
{title2=$\mathrm{gv}(E)=\int_N\eta\wedge d\eta$}

For a cooriented integrable codimension-one distribution, choose a nonvanishing defining form $\zeta$ and an auxiliary form with $d\zeta=\eta\wedge\zeta$. Changing the auxiliary form to $\eta+a\zeta$ changes $\eta\wedge d\eta$ by $-d(a\,d\zeta)$. Replacing $\zeta$ by $h\zeta$ changes the auxiliary form by $d\log|h|$, changing the three-form by the exact differential $d(-d\log|h|\wedge\eta)$. Stokes' theorem proves invariance on a closed oriented three-manifold. For the weak stable foliation of a curvature-minus-one surface geodesic flow, take $\zeta=\theta+\omega$, $\eta=\alpha$ to obtain $\mathrm{gv}=4\pi^2\chi(M)$ in the positive base/fibre orientation.

= Leaf of a regular foliation
{parent=Foliation}
{title2=$T_pL=\mathcal D_p$}

= Leaves of a regular foliation
{synonym}

A leaf is a maximal connected <integral manifold> of the smooth constant-rank <integrable distribution> defining a regular <foliation>. It carries its intrinsic manifold topology and is immersed in the ambient manifold. It need not be embedded or closed. Local <Frobenius theorem> coordinates make each leaf segment a plaque with constant transverse coordinates; <global confinement to an immersed leaf> follows by joining such plaques along tangent curves.

= Taut foliation
{parent=Foliation}
{wiki}

A codimension-one <foliation> of a closed <three-manifold> is taut if every leaf meets a closed transverse curve. In particular, the fibers of a bundle over $S^1$ form a taut <foliation>.

= Closed transversal to a foliation
{parent=Taut foliation}

= Closed transversals to a foliation
{synonym}

A closed transversal is a closed curve everywhere transverse to the leaves of a <foliation>. A single such curve meeting every leaf proves that the <foliation> is a <taut foliation>.

= Orbifold fundamental group
{parent=Orbifold}
{title2=$\pi_1^{\rm orb}(B)$}

The <orbifold fundamental group> records loops together with their local isotropy relations, so a loop around a cone point of order $m$ has its $m$th power trivial rather than being trivial itself. For an oriented closed surface of <genus> $g$ with cone orders $m_1,\ldots,m_r$, its presentation is $\langle a_1,b_1,\ldots,a_g,b_g,c_1,\ldots,c_r\mid c_i^{m_i}=1,\ \prod_j[a_j,b_j]c_1\cdots c_r=1\rangle$. A torsion-free <finite-index subgroup> of a <hyperbolic surface> <orbifold> gives a smooth locally isometric surface cover. The absence of torsion means that the <subgroup> meets no nonidentity local cone stabilizer.

= Paired-polygon construction of finite surface covers
{parent=Orbifold fundamental group}

Pair the $2s$ edges of an oriented <hyperbolic polygon> by length-preserving identifications reversing their <boundary> directions. Label the pairs by generating elements $A_1,\ldots,A_s$ of a finite <group> $T$. Glue the corresponding edge of a copy labelled $g$ to its mate in the copy labelled $gA_k$. Left multiplication of labels defines the $T$ action. A <vertex cycle of a paired polygon> has angle sum $\theta_j$ and accumulated gluing word $C_j\in T$ of order $q_j$; above it lie $|T|/q_j$ vertices, each of <cone angle> $q_j\theta_j$. The action is free away from vertices, and the vertex stabilizers are conjugates of $\langle C_j\rangle$. If the base cone has integer order $m_j=2\pi/\theta_j$ and $q_j\mid m_j$, this is an <orbifold> covering, with lifted cone order $m_j/q_j$. The lift is smooth exactly when $q_j=m_j$.

= Freeness criterion for a subgroup of a polygon-cover deck group
{parent=Paired-polygon construction of finite surface covers}
{title2=$U\cap g\langle C_j\rangle g^{-1}=\{1\}$}

In the <paired-polygon construction of finite surface covers>, a <subgroup> $U$ acts freely precisely when the displayed intersection is trivial for every vertex word $C_j$ and every $g\in T$. The quotient map $M\to U\backslash M$ is then a <normal covering map> with deck <group> $U$ and $\chi(U\backslash M)=\chi(M)/|U|$. Normality of $U$ in $T$ is instead the criterion for the intermediate <orbifold> cover $U\backslash M\to T\backslash M$ to be normal. These are different <covering maps>.

= Euler characteristic of a paired-polygon surface cover
{parent=Paired-polygon construction of finite surface covers}
{title2=$\chi(M)=|T|(1-s+\sum_jq_j^{-1})$}

For a paired $2s$-gon with $r$ base <vertex cycles of a paired polygon>, there are one face, $s$ edges and $r$ vertices on the base, so $\chi(M_0)=1-s+r$. The full finite-group construction has $|T|$ faces, $s|T|$ edges and $\sum_j|T|/q_j$ vertices, giving the displayed ordinary <Euler characteristic>. If all lifted angles are $2\pi$, the <orbifold Euler characteristic> formula gives equivalently $\chi(M)=|T|\chi_{\rm orb}(M_0)$.

= Vertex cycle of a paired polygon
{parent=Paired-polygon construction of finite surface covers}

= Vertex cycles of a paired polygon
{synonym}

A <vertex cycle of a paired polygon> is an equivalence class of vertices under the edge identifications. Its angle sum is the total angle at the resulting point of the quotient surface. Recording the edge labels encountered around that point gives a <group> word, whose order determines the number of turns required to close a lifted cycle. This explains the angle multiplier and lifted vertex count in the <paired-polygon construction of finite surface covers>.

= Smoothness criterion from cone-monodromy cycles
{parent=Orbifold fundamental group}
{title2=$\text{residual cone order}=m/\ell$}

Over a cone point of order $m$, a cycle of length $\ell$ in the <monodromy> on sheets has ramification degree $\ell$ and residual cone angle $2\pi\ell/m$. The lifted inherited <hyperbolic metric> is smooth precisely when every such cycle has length $m$. Equivalently the subgroup defining the cover meets no conjugate of a nonidentity power of a cone generator.

= Orbifold Euler characteristic
{parent=Orbifold}
{title2=$\chi_{\mathrm{orb}}$}

For a compact <topological surface> with cone-point orders $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_k$, the orbifold Euler characteristic is $\chi_{\mathrm{orb}}=\chi(\text{underlying surface})-\sum_i(1-1/\alpha_i)$. An orbifold covering multiplies this quantity by its degree.

= Euler characteristic from coset cycle counts
{parent=Orbifold Euler characteristic}
{title2=$\chi=n(2-2g_0-r)+\sum_{i=1}^r c_i$}

An $n$-sheeted surface cover of an orbifold of underlying <genus> $g_0$ with $r$ cone points has the displayed ordinary <Euler characteristic>, where $c_i$ is the number of cycles of the $i$th cone <monodromy permutation>. The ramification deficit over that point is $n-c_i$, so the formula follows by counting lifted vertices, edges and faces, or by the <Riemann-Hurwitz formula>. If every cone cycle has its full prescribed length, this equals $n\chi_{\mathrm{orb}}$.

= Inversive distance
{parent=Geometry and topology}
{wiki}

For two disjoint Euclidean spheres of radii $R_1,R_2$ whose centers are distance $d$ apart, the inversive distance
$$
\chi=\frac{d^2-R_1^2-R_2^2}{2R_1R_2}
$$
is invariant under conformal transformations. It satisfies $\chi>1$ for externally disjoint spheres.

A boundary point of the source half-plane that maps to a <polygon> vertex. If that corner has angle $\pi\alpha$, the <Schwarz-Christoffel mapping> has local behavior $F(z)-F(a)\sim C(z-a)^\alpha$ at its <prevertex>, so the <derivative> has exponent $\alpha-1$. Infinity may also be a <prevertex>, expressed in the local coordinate $1/z$.

= Power-product map to a slit half-plane
{parent=Schwarz-Christoffel mapping}
{title2=$g(z)=(z-1)^k(z+1)^{1-k}$}

For $0<k<1$, principal upper-half-plane branches give a <conformal map> onto the upper half-plane cut along $[0,\tau]$, where $\tau=2k^k(1-k)^{1-k}e^{i\pi k}$. Its <derivative> is $(z-1+2k)(z-1)^{k-1}(z+1)^{-k}$. The two prevertices $-1,1$ map to the slit base, and $1-2k$ maps to its tip. Boundary traversal goes out along the slit and back, while $g(z)=z+1-2k+O(1/z)$ at infinity. The <argument principle> gives one preimage of every point off the slit.

= Quarter-sector mapping of a circular lens
{parent=Conformal map}
{title2=$F(z)=((1+z)/(1-z))^4$}

For the lens $\operatorname{Im}z>0$ and $|z+i|<\sqrt2$, the <Möbius transformation> $w=(1+z)/(1-z)$ maps its real boundary segment to the positive real axis and its circular boundary arc to the ray with argument $\pi/4$. Its interior maps bijectively to $0<\arg w<\pi/4$. The fourth power therefore maps this lens conformally onto the upper half-plane. Composing with $(F-i)/(F+i)$ maps it onto the unit disc. The endpoints $\pm1$ are boundary points, so the pole and zero introduce no interior singularity or branching.

= Conformal group
{parent=Conformal map}
{title2=$\operatorname{Conf}(M,g)$}

The <conformal group> of a metric manifold consists of <diffeomorphisms> $f$ with $f^*g=\Omega^2g$ for a positive function $\Omega$. Its infinitesimal generators are <conformal Killing vector fields>. The choice of manifold matters: in flat Euclidean dimension greater than two, <special conformal transformations> can be regular on the <conformal compactification of Euclidean space> while having finite poles in the uncompactified space.

= Null affine parameter under a conformal rescaling
{parent=Conformal map}
{title2=$d\widetilde\lambda=\Omega^2\,d\lambda$}

If $g$ is rescaled to $\widetilde g=\Omega^2g$, a null geodesic retains its unparametrized path. For its original affine tangent $k$, the changed connection gives $\widetilde\nabla_k k=2k(\log\Omega)k$. Rescaling the tangent by $\Omega^{-2}$ cancels this term and proves the displayed affine-parameter relation, up to a constant multiplier. A conformal boundary may therefore be reached at finite unphysical affine parameter while physical affine parameter is infinite.

= Conformality
{parent=Conformal map}

The local angle-preservation property of a <conformal map>. At a regular point its <derivative> is a positive scaling times an orthogonal linear map. Consequently composition multiplies <gradient> lengths by that scaling, as follows directly from the <chain rule>.

= Conformal map of a circular lens
{parent=Conformal map}

Two intersecting <circles> bound a lens. A <Möbius transformation> sending their intersection points to zero and infinity sends the two boundary arcs to rays, and the lens to a sector. A suitable power straightens the sector to a half-plane. The chosen sector and its branch must be identified by an interior test point; merely squaring an arbitrary boundary-straightening map need not be injective on the intended domain. A <Cayley transform> then gives a conformal bijection to a disc.

= Explicit disk map of a slit half disk
{parent=Conformal map}
{title2=$u^2=\dfrac{4(z^2+1)}{z^2+16},\quad F(z)=\dfrac{u+u^{-1}+i}{u+u^{-1}-i}$}

For the upper half disk $|z|<2$ slit along $z=iy$, $0<y\le1$, squaring maps onto $|w|<4$ minus $[-1,4)$. After scaling the radius-four disc, the <disk automorphism> $v=4(w+1)/(w+16)$ sends this slit to $[0,1)$. Its <square root> with argument in $(0,\pi)$ maps onto the upper half <unit disc>. There $u+u^{-1}$ maps bijectively onto the <lower half-plane>: its <imaginary part> is $\operatorname{Im}u(1-|u|^{-2})<0$, and the two roots of its inverse equation are reciprocal, with exactly one inside the unit <circle>. The final <Möbius map> gives the displayed conformal disk <bijection>.

= Two-sided cluster set at an interior slit point
{parent=Explicit disk map of a slit half disk}
{title2=$u\to\pm2/\sqrt{21}\text{ as }z\to i/2$}

In the explicit slit map, right-side approach to $i/2$ gives the positive root, and left-side approach gives the negative root. The corresponding lower-half-plane <boundary> values are $\pm25/(2\sqrt{21})$, so the two unit-circle image values are distinct. Every other <conformal bijection> onto the disk differs by a <disk automorphism>, which extends bijectively to the unit <circle>. Thus a sequence approaching that slit point has exactly one or both of two <boundary> cluster values, according to which sides occur infinitely often.

= Conformal map of a segment complement to a punctured disk
{parent=Conformal map}
{title2=$w=\frac{\sqrt{(z-1)/(z+1)}-1}{\sqrt{(z-1)/(z+1)}+1}$}

On $\mathbb C\setminus[-1,1]$, take the <principal square root> with positive real part. The <Mobius transformation> $(z-1)/(z+1)$ has image $\bigl(\mathbb C\setminus(-\infty,0]\bigr)\setminus\{1\}$. The <principal square root> therefore maps to the right half-plane with $1$ omitted, and the final <Mobius transformation> gives exactly $0<|w|<1$. The inverse is $z=-(w+w^{-1})/2$. Adding $\infty$ to the source on the <Riemann sphere> fills the missing value $w=0$ and gives a <conformal map> onto the full disk. Without $\infty$, a loop surrounding the segment has nonzero <winding number>, so the source is not a <simply connected space> and cannot be <conformally equivalent> to a disk.

= Tangent-circle crescent conformal map
{parent=Conformal map}
{title2=$z\mapsto e^{4\pi/z}+i\mapsto (e^{4\pi/z}+i-i)/(e^{4\pi/z}+i+i)$}

For the crescent inside $|z-2i|<2$ and outside $|z-i|\leq1$, inversion $w=1/z$ gives the horizontal strip $-1/2<\operatorname{Im}w<-1/4$. The affine map $\zeta=4w+2i$ sends it to $0<\operatorname{Im}\zeta<1$, and $e^{\pi\zeta}$ maps it bijectively onto the upper half-plane. A Cayley transform then maps it onto the unit disc. Tangency, rather than a separate enclosed hole, is why this domain is simply connected.

= Conformal map of a vertical half-strip by sine
{parent=Conformal map}
{title2=$z\mapsto\sin z$}

The <sine> maps $\{-\pi/2<\operatorname{Re}z<\pi/2,\ \operatorname{Im}z>0\}$ bijectively onto the upper half-plane: its imaginary part is $\cos(\operatorname{Re}z)\sinh(\operatorname{Im}z)>0$, and the equality of two sine values forces either equal arguments modulo $2\pi$ or arguments summing to an odd multiple of $\pi$. Only equality is possible in this domain. For surjectivity, solve $t^2-2iwt-1=0$ for $t=e^{iz}$. When $\operatorname{Im}w>0$, exactly one root has modulus below one and it has positive real part. The inverse $z=-i\log t$ therefore belongs to the half-strip. Composing with $(w-i)/(w+i)$ gives a <conformal map> onto the <unit disc>.

= Conformal geometry
{parent=Conformal map}
{wiki}

<Conformal geometry> studies a <metric tensor> only up to multiplication by a nowhere-zero positive scalar function on a real slice. A conformal class determines angles in Riemannian signature and null directions in Lorentzian signature. The complexified flat four-dimensional conformal geometry is encoded by the <Klein quadric>, whose incidence relation describes null separation.

= Conformal structure
{parent=Conformal geometry}
{title2=$[g]=\{e^{2u}g:u\in C^\infty(M)\}$}

An equivalence class of <Riemannian metrics> under multiplication by positive smooth functions. Such rescalings preserve angles. On an oriented smooth surface, a conformal structure is equivalent to a <complex structure>, with local holomorphic coordinates in which its metrics are positive multiples of $dx^2+dy^2$.

= Conformal compactification
{parent=Conformal geometry}
{wiki}

A conformal compactification realizes a noncompact metric space as a dense open subset of a compact space whose metric on that subset is conformally related to the original one. Its smoothness and boundary interpretation depend on the example. <Conformal compactification of Euclidean space> adds one point to four-space; <projective Minkowski compactification> adds a null cone. A regular null-boundary <conformal completion> and a projective compactification need not have the same global topology or causal covering convention.

= Conformal boundary
{parent=Conformal compactification}
{title2=$\widetilde g=\Omega^2g,\quad\Omega=0,\quad d\Omega\ne0$}

A boundary obtained by extending the rescaled metric $\widetilde g=\Omega^2g$ smoothly and nondegenerately to a hypersurface on which $\Omega$ vanishes with nonzero derivative. Its causal character is measured using the extended <Lorentzian metric>, not the divergent physical metric. In signature $(+---)$, a timelike normal makes the boundary spacelike, a spacelike normal makes it timelike, and a null normal makes it null. These cases lead to different causal and initial-value properties. The <conformal cylinder of two-dimensional de Sitter spacetime> has spacelike past/future boundaries; the <conformal strip of two-dimensional anti-de Sitter spacetime> has two timelike boundaries.

= Conformal compactification of Euclidean space
{parent=Conformal compactification}
{title2=$\mathbb R^4\cup\{\infty\}\cong S^4$}

Inverse stereographic projection identifies Euclidean four-space with a sphere minus one point. Multiplying its flat metric by $4/(1+|x|^2)^2$ gives the round metric, which extends smoothly over the added point. In the <Klein quadric> description, the compact space is the projectivized real null cone of signature $(1,5)$. It differs topologically from <Minkowski conformal compactification>.

= Global obstruction to Wick rotation of conformal compactifications
{parent=Conformal compactification}

The <projective Minkowski compactification> has infinite cyclic fundamental group, while the <conformal compactification of Euclidean space> is $S^4$ and simply connected. Thus the affine substitution $t\mapsto it$ cannot extend to an invertible smooth identification of these real compactifications. Both are real slices of the same complex <Klein quadric>, which permits local analytic continuation without equating their global real topologies.

In affine cone coordinates $[U,V,t,\boldsymbol x]$ with $2UV+t^2-|\boldsymbol x|^2=0$ and $U=1$, the substitution is represented on the complex quadric by the rational map $[U,V,t,\boldsymbol x]\mapsto[U^2,UV+t^2,iUt,U\boldsymbol x]$. It preserves the quadric equation but is indeterminate at some infinity points, including $[0,1,0,\boldsymbol0]$. This is a direct algebraic manifestation of the obstruction to a global regular Wick map.

= Half-strip conformal map by hyperbolic cosine
{parent=Conformal map}
{title2=$\zeta=\cosh(\pi z/b)$}

For $b>0$, the <conformal map> $\cosh(\pi z/b)$ takes $\{\operatorname{Re}z>0,\ 0<\operatorname{Im}z<b\}$ bijectively onto the upper half-plane. The exponential maps this half-strip onto the upper half-plane exterior to the unit circle; then $\cosh(\pi z/b)=(w+w^{-1})/2$ is a scaled <Joukowski map>. All critical points of the entire map are $z=inb$, mapping alternately to $1$ and $-1$.

= Conformal inversion of a quadrant
{parent=Conformal map}
{title2=$w=-1/z$}

The map $w=-1/z$ sends the open second quadrant inside the unit circle bijectively to the open first quadrant outside it. Radius becomes $r^{-1}$ and argument becomes $\pi-\theta$. Its derivative $1/z^2$ is nonzero on the source, so it is a <conformal map>. Rotation combined with reciprocal inversion similarly maps other quarter-disc regions.

= Conformal map from a parabolic domain to the unit disc
{parent=Conformal map}
{title2=$C(z)=\tan^2(\pi\sqrt z/4)$}

The map $C(z)=\tan^2(\pi\sqrt z/4)$ is a <conformal map> from $\{x+iy:y^2<4(1-x)\}$ onto the <unit disc>. A <principal square root> first maps the slit parabolic region to a vertical strip, and a scaled <tangent> followed by squaring maps the strip to a slit disc. Evenness in the square-root variable gives a <holomorphic> extension across the removed axis, filling the target slit.

= Prime end
{parent=Conformal map}

A prime end describes an approach to the boundary of a <simply connected domain> through a nested sequence of <crosscuts>. A <conformal map> induces a correspondence between prime ends, even when the Euclidean boundary does not have a continuous one-to-one parametrization. This is the natural boundary interpretation of the endpoints and growing tip of a <Loewner chain>.

= Intrinsic boundary of a simply connected domain
{parent=Prime end}
{title2=$\delta D=\widehat D\setminus D$}

Choose a <conformal map> from a proper <simply connected domain> to the unit disc and pull back the topology of the closed disc. The resulting compactification is independent of the chosen map, since disc automorphisms extend to its boundary. Its intrinsic boundary distinguishes different approaches to a slit. Every <conformal map> between such domains extends to their intrinsic compactifications. This boundary agrees with the prime-end description in the simply connected setting.

= Conformal radius
{parent=Conformal map}
{title2=$\operatorname{crad}_D(z)=|\phi'(0)|$}

For a <simply connected domain> $D\ne\mathbb C$ and $z\in D$, the conformal radius is $|\phi'(0)|$, where $\phi$ maps the unit disc conformally onto $D$ with $\phi(0)=z$. Disc automorphisms fixing zero are rotations, so the value is independent of the chosen map.

= Conformal radius under a chordal Loewner flow
{parent=Conformal radius}
{title2=$\Upsilon_t=\operatorname{Im}g_t(z)/|g_t'(z)|$}

= Loewner conformal radius
{synonym}
{c}

Before the <Loewner swallowing time> of $z$, set $Z_t=g_t(z)-U_t=X_t+iY_t$ and $\Upsilon_t=Y_t/|g_t'(z)|$. Since the <conformal radius> of the <complex upper half-plane> at $Z_t$ is $2Y_t$, the transformation rule gives $2\Upsilon_t=\operatorname{crad}_{D_t}(z)$. The <Koebe quarter theorem> and the interior-disc comparison give $\operatorname{dist}(z,\partial D_t)/2\leq\Upsilon_t\leq2\operatorname{dist}(z,\partial D_t)$. Differentiating the <chordal Loewner equation> gives $\boxed{\partial_t\log\Upsilon_t=-4Y_t^2/|Z_t|^4}$. Thus this quantity decreases as the remaining <domain> shrinks.

Two plane domains are conformally equivalent when there is a bijective holomorphic map between them whose inverse is holomorphic.

= Punctured plane is not conformally equivalent to the punctured unit disc
{parent=Conformal equivalence}

A conformal map from $\mathbb C^*$ into the punctured unit disc would be bounded near its isolated singularity at zero, hence extend to a bounded entire function. The <Liouville theorem> would make it constant.

= Complex plane is not conformally equivalent to the upper half-plane
{parent=Conformal equivalence}

Composing a hypothetical conformal equivalence $\mathbb C\to\mathbb H$ with a <Cayley transform (hyperbolic geometry)> $\mathbb H\to\mathbb D$ would give a nonconstant bounded <entire function>, contradicting the <Liouville theorem>.

= Complex plane and unit disc are homeomorphic but not conformally equivalent
{parent=Conformal equivalence}

The radial map $z\mapsto z/(1+|z|)$ is a <homeomorphism> from $\mathbb C$ to the unit disc, with inverse $w\mapsto w/(1-|w|)$. A conformal equivalence in that direction would be a nonconstant bounded entire function, contradicting the <Liouville theorem>.

= Conformal equivalence between the upper half-disc and upper half-plane
{parent=Conformal equivalence}

The map
$$
z\longmapsto\left(\frac{1+z}{1-z}\right)^2
$$
maps $\{z:|z|<1,\ \operatorname{Im}z>0\}$ conformally onto the upper half-plane. The <Möbius transformation> maps the half-disc onto the first quadrant, and squaring maps that quadrant onto the upper half-plane.

= Holomorphic inverse function theorem
{parent=Conformal map}

If a holomorphic function $f$ satisfies $f'(z_0)\ne0$, then it has a holomorphic local inverse near $z_0$ and is conformal there. Its possible failures of local conformality are therefore its critical points.

= Hyperbolic-cosine half-strip map
{parent=Conformal map}

The map $z\mapsto\cosh(\pi z)$ is a conformal bijection from
$$
\{z:\Re z>0,\ 0<\Im z<1\}
$$
onto the upper half-plane. Its three boundary intervals map respectively to $(1,\infty)$, $(-1,1)$ and $(-\infty,-1)$.

A connected space cannot be expressed as the union of two disjoint nonempty open subsets.

= Connected chain of overlapping subsets
{parent=Connected space}

A sequence of <connected subsets> with each consecutive pair intersecting has connected union. If the union admitted a separation, each <connected subset> would lie entirely on one side. Consecutive intersections force consecutive subsets onto the same side, hence all subsets onto one side, contradicting that both sides of the separation are nonempty.

= Spiral accumulating on a circle
{parent=Connected space}
{title2=$Y=\{(1+t^{-1})e^{2\pi it}:t\ge1\}$}

The closure of the displayed <connected> spiral adds the unit circle. Its union with the closed unit disk is therefore connected but not path-connected. A path from the spiral to the disk would have a first contact time. Before contact its reciprocal radial offset defines a continuous parameter tending to infinity. The <intermediate value theorem> then forces sequences of path points with parameters integer and half-integer, approaching opposite circle points. This contradicts continuity at the first contact. Thus the spiral and disk form separate <path-connected components>.

= Connectedness from global regular functions
{parent=Connected space}

If a nonempty <scheme> $X$ has no nontrivial <idempotent> in $\Gamma(X,\mathcal O_X)$, then its underlying <topological space> is <connected>. Indeed, a decomposition into two nonempty clopen subsets would produce the global regular function equal to zero on one component and one on the other. In particular, $\Gamma(X,\mathcal O_X)$ being a <field> implies connectedness.

= Connected subset
{parent=Connected space}

A subset of a topological space is connected when it is connected in the subspace topology.

= Connected union with a connected hub
{parent=Connected subset}

Suppose <connected subsets> $U_j$ all meet one <connected> member $U_1$. A separation of their union would place $U_1$ entirely on one side. Each $U_j$ is also entirely on one side and intersects $U_1$, so every $U_j$ lies on the same side, contradicting nonempty separation. Thus the union is <connected>. If the subsets are <path-connected>, join two points by a <path> to $U_1$, a <path> inside $U_1$, and a <path> back out. This needs pairwise contact with the hub, not one point shared by every subset.

= Irrational-cut separation of rational slopes
{parent=Connected subset}

In a plane set with $x,y>0$ and rational ratio $x/y$, any irrational number $a$ lying between two attained ratios separates the set into the nonempty relatively open sets $x/y<a$ and $x/y>a$. This exhibits a failure of <connectedness> using the continuous slope map, even though each individual ray is <path-connected>.

= Separation of a topological space
{parent=Connected space}

A separation of a topological space $X$ is a pair of disjoint nonempty open subsets whose union is $X$. A space is <connected> exactly when it has no separation.

= Identity component of a topological group
{synonym}

The identity component is the <connected component> containing the <identity element>. It is a closed <normal subgroup>: products and inverses of connected sets through the identity remain in that component, and conjugation preserves it. = Connected components of a product space

The <product of connected spaces> is connected, so the product of the two <connected components> lies in the component of $(x,y)$. Conversely, continuous projections carry that component to connected sets in the factors, which must lie in $C_x$ and $C_y$. These two inclusions prove equality in arbitrary <topological spaces>.

= Connected subset of the real line
{parent=Connected space}

A subset of the <real numbers> is connected exactly when it is a <real interval>. If it contains $a<b$ but omits some $c\in(a,b)$, intersecting it with the two open half-lines separated by $c$ gives a separation.

= Integer-valued function criterion for connectedness
{parent=Connected space}

A topological space is connected exactly when every continuous map from it to the discrete space $\mathbb Z$ is constant. A disconnection produces a nonconstant indicator map, while the continuous image of a connected space in $\mathbb Z$ must be a singleton.

= Pairwise-intersecting connected cover
{parent=Connected space}

If connected subsets cover a space and every two of them intersect, then their union is connected. Equivalently, any continuous integer-valued function is constant on each member, and the intersections force all those constants to agree.

= Product of connected spaces

The product of any family of connected spaces is connected. For two factors, fix $y_0$ and use the connected sets
$$
(X\times\{y_0\})\cup(\{x\}\times Y),
\qquad x\in X.
$$
They cover $X\times Y$ and all contain the connected horizontal slice $X\times\{y_0\}$.

= Distance from a point to a plane

= Signed distance from a point to a plane
{parent=Distance from a point to a plane}
{title2=$s=\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p-d$}

Orient the <plane> $\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r=d$ with a <unit normal> $\mathbf n$. The signed distance of a point $\mathbf p$ from the plane is
$$
s=\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf p-d.
$$
Its absolute value is the ordinary <distance from a point to a plane>. The closest point in the plane is $\mathbf p-s\mathbf n$, so the displacement from the point to its projection is $-s\mathbf n$. Reversing the normal and $d$ reverses $s$ without changing the geometric plane or the unsigned distance. A sphere of radius $q$ centred at $\mathbf p$ has <sphere-plane intersection> radius $\sqrt{q^2-s^2}$ when $|s|<q$.

= Reciprocal spiral with its endpoint
{parent=Path-connected space}
{title2=$\{t^{-1}e^{2\pi it}:t\ge1\}\cup\{0\}$}

The map $F(s)=se^{2\pi i/s}$ for $s>0$, $F(0)=0$, is <continuous> on $[0,1]$, since $|F(s)|=s$. Its image is the displayed spiral together with its endpoint. The image is <compact> and <path-connected>: between images of $s_0,s_1$ the explicit path is $F((1-u)s_0+us_1)$.

= Rational comb with a connecting spine
{parent=Path-connected space}

The union of $\{0\}\times[0,1]$ with horizontal segments $[0,1]\times\{q\}$ for rational $q\in[0,1]$ is <path-connected>. Each point lies either on the vertical spine or on a segment meeting it. A path travels horizontally to the spine, vertically along it, and horizontally to its destination. This contrasts with disconnected rational-slope rays whose common origin has been excluded.

= Polygonal connectivity of a finite-line complement
{parent=Path-connected space}

The complement of finitely many lines in $\mathbb R^3$ is <path-connected>, and any two points can be joined by two line segments. For endpoints $a,b$ outside the lines, exclude the finitely many affine planes through each endpoint and each removed line. Choose a bend point $c$ outside all those planes. If either segment met a removed line, the bend point would lie in its associated excluded plane, a contradiction.

= Plane minus a countable set is path connected
{parent=Path-connected space}

For a <countable set> $T\subset\mathbb R^2$, join two points outside $T$ through an auxiliary point avoiding the countably many lines through either endpoint and a point of $T$. Such an auxiliary point exists: choose a line with direction outside the countable list of forbidden directions, then avoid its countably many intersections with those lines. The two segments avoid $T$, giving a <continuous path>.

A <topological embedding> $\gamma:S^1\to X$ of a <circle> into a <topological space>. On an oriented surface, an essential such curve is noncontractible; on a punctured surface, nonperipheral means that it does not surround only one puncture.

= Nonseparating curve
{parent=Simple closed curve}

A simple closed curve in a connected oriented <surface> is nonseparating when cutting along it leaves a connected <surface>. A separating curve bounds a subsurface and represents zero in first <homology>. For a nonseparating curve, an arc between the two new boundary components closes to a curve meeting it once. Its <algebraic intersection number of curves on an oriented surface> is $\pm1$, proving that the original curve represents a <primitive homology class>.

= Connected locally path-connected spaces are path-connected
{parent=Locally path-connected space}

In a <locally path-connected space>, every <path component> is <open>: a <path-connected> open neighbourhood of a point lies within that point's <path component>. In a nonempty <connected space>, a nonempty subset that is both <open> and <closed> must be the whole space. Thus a <connected space> that is <locally path-connected> is <path-connected>.

= Line-plane intersection criterion
{parent=Plane}

A line $r=b+s m$ intersects a plane $(r-a)\cdot n=0$ exactly when the scalar equation $s(m\cdot n)=(a-b)\cdot n$ can be solved. If $m\cdot n\ne0$, there is one point. If that product vanishes, the line is either contained in the plane or disjoint from it. Direction and normal vectors must be nonzero.

= Equation of a plane through three points
{parent=Plane}
{title2=$(\mathbf r-\mathbf a)\cdot[(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)\times(\mathbf c-\mathbf a)]=0$}

For three non-collinear points with position <vectors> $\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c$, a nonzero <normal vector> is
$$
\mathbf N=(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)\times(\mathbf c-\mathbf a).
$$
The <plane> is the set of $\mathbf r$ satisfying $(\mathbf r-\mathbf a)\cdot\mathbf N=0$. Expanding the <cross product> and using the <scalar triple product> gives
$$
\mathbf r\cdot(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b+\mathbf b\times\mathbf c+\mathbf c\times\mathbf a)
=\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c).
$$
Non-collinearity is essential: otherwise $\mathbf N=0$ and the equation fails to select a unique plane.

= Decidable tree for binary diagonal avoidance
{parent=Computable binary tree}

For an effective enumeration $(\varphi_e)$ of unary <partial computable functions>, admit a length-$n$ string $\sigma$ if it disagrees with every binary value of $\varphi_e(e)$ observed within $n$ steps for $e<n$. The <bounded halting predicate> makes membership decidable, and the tests are prefix compatible. Its paths are exactly the <binary diagonally noncomputable functions>. The path space is nonempty and perfect because infinitely many indices of divergent programs leave arbitrarily late bits free; no path is computable. Dead ends in this decidable presentation are essential.

= Computable pruned binary trees have computable paths
{parent=Computable binary tree}

For a nonempty decidable <binary tree of finite strings> with no terminal nodes, begin at the empty string and repeatedly choose the first immediate successor that belongs to the tree. Each finite decision terminates and some successor always exists. This produces a <total computable function> giving an infinite path. In particular, a nonempty decidable tree in which every node has two incompatible extensions cannot have only noncomputable paths.

= Perfect path space

= Cylinder-function density in Cantor space
{parent=Cantor space}

{parent=Sphere-plane intersection}

= Spherically symmetric solution of Poisson equation

= Origin-regular spherical Poisson flux law
{parent=Spherically symmetric solution of Poisson equation}
{title2=$F(x)=\frac{x}{|x|^3}\int_0^{|x|}f(s)s^2\,ds$}

For a spherically symmetric <gradient> field regular at the origin and satisfying <Poisson equation>, the <divergence theorem> on a ball determines its radial magnitude from the enclosed source integral. = Potential of a uniform spherical source
{parent=Origin-regular spherical Poisson flux law}
{title2=$\Delta f=\rho_0\mathbf1_{\{|x|<a\}}$}

For a uniform spherical source of radius $a$, the origin-regular radial <Poisson equation> gives $\nabla f=\rho_0x/3$ inside and $\nabla f=\rho_0a^3x/(3|x|^3)$ outside. Integrating radially, imposing $f\to0$ at infinity and continuity at $|x|=a$, gives
$$
f(x)=\begin{cases}\rho_0(|x|^2-3a^2)/6,&|x|\le a,\\-\rho_0a^3/(3|x|),&|x|\ge a.\end{cases}
$$
The potential and its normal derivative are continuous at the interface, so its distributional <Laplacian> has no surface delta contribution. The classical equation holds off the interface and the source equation holds almost everywhere and distributionally.

= Cotangent partial-fraction expansion
{parent=Cotangent}
{title2=$\pi\cot\pi z=z^{-1}+\sum_{n\ge1}2z/(z^2-n^2)$}

Away from integer <poles>,
$$
\pi\cot\pi z=\frac1z+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2z}{z^2-n^2}.
$$
The series is locally uniformly convergent. Integrate $\pi\cot(\pi\zeta)/(\zeta^2-z^2)$ around the square with sides $\operatorname{Re}\zeta,\operatorname{Im}\zeta=\pm(N+1/2)$. The <cotangent> is uniformly bounded there, so the integral tends to zero. The <residue theorem> then gives $\pi\cot(\pi z)/z+\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}(n^2-z^2)^{-1}=0$, which is the expansion. Differentiation generates higher integer-lattice <pole> sums. Setting $w=e^{2\pi iz}$ gives $\pi\cot\pi z=\pi i(w+1)/(w-1)$. On $0<|z|<1$, expansion of each paired summand gives $\pi\cot\pi z=z^{-1}-2\sum_{m\ge0}\zeta(2m+2)z^{2m+1}$, relating this <trigonometric function> to the <Riemann zeta function>.

= Cotangent Fourier expansion
{parent=Cotangent partial-fraction expansion}
{title2=$\pi\cot(\pi z)=-\pi i-2\pi i\sum_{r\geq1}e^{2\pi irz}$}

For $\operatorname{Im}z>0$, writing <cotangent> as $i(e^{2\pi iz}+1)/(e^{2\pi iz}-1)$ and expanding a <geometric series> gives this <Fourier series>. Differentiating the <cotangent partial-fraction expansion> and this <Fourier series> $k-1$ times yields
$$
\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}(z+n)^{-k}=\frac{(-2\pi i)^k}{(k-1)!}\sum_{r\geq1}r^{k-1}e^{2\pi irz},\qquad k\geq2.
$$

= Box-counting dimension
{parent=Fractal geometry}
{title2=$\underline{\dim}_{\mathrm M}E,\ \overline{\dim}_{\mathrm M}E$}
{wiki}

= Minkowski dimension
{c}
{synonym}

For a nonempty bounded subset $E$ of <Euclidean space>, let $N_\delta(E)$ be the least number of radius-$\delta$ balls covering $E$. Its lower and upper box-counting dimensions are the lower and upper limits of $\log N_\delta(E)/\log(1/\delta)$ as $\delta\downarrow0$. If these limits agree, their common value is its <Minkowski dimension>. A cover by $N_\delta(E)$ balls implies $|E_\delta|\lesssim_n\delta^nN_\delta(E)$ for the open $\delta$-neighborhood. Conversely, a maximal separated subset gives the reverse comparison up to fixed changes of scale. This connects small-scale covering counts with neighborhood <Lebesgue measure>.

= Box-counting dimension of separated digit sets
{parent=Box-counting dimension}
{title2=$\dim_{\mathrm M}E=\log m/\log b$}

Consider base-$b$ expansions using $m$ allowed digits with positive gaps between the corresponding first-level intervals. At depth $k$ there are $m^k$ cylinders of diameter comparable to $b^{-k}$, and separated cylinders cannot all be covered by fewer than a constant multiple of $m^k$ balls of that scale. Thus $N_{b^{-k}}(E)\asymp m^k$. Monotonicity between successive scales gives equal upper and lower <Minkowski dimensions>, both $\log m/\log b$.

= Finite similarity iterated function system

= Minkowski sausage
{c}
{parent=Finite similarity iterated function system}
{wiki}

The <Minkowski sausage> replaces a segment by eight directed unit segments whose endpoint displacement is four units. A middle straight run of length two represents two collinear unit segments. After normalizing the displacement to one, the eight <contracting Euclidean similarities> all have ratio $1/4$. The open diamond $\{|x-1/2|+|y|<1/2\}$ is an <open set condition> witness: its eight image diamonds have disjoint interiors and lie within it. Hence $8(1/4)^s=1$, giving <Hausdorff dimension> $3/2$. Treating the middle run as a single similarity of ratio $1/2$ instead defines a different iterated scheme.

= Open set condition
{parent=Self-similarity}

A finite family of contracting similarities $S_1,\ldots,S_k$ satisfies the open set condition if there is a nonempty bounded <open set> $O$ with $S_i(O)\subseteq O$ and $S_i(O)\cap S_j(O)=\varnothing$ for $i\ne j$. This permits the closures to meet. For the invariant compact set, this separation makes its <Hausdorff dimension> equal to the similarity exponent $s$ defined by $\sum_i c_i^s=1$, where $c_i$ are the contraction ratios. The level-$n$ cylinder sets supply the upper dimension bound; the separated cylinder masses with weights $c_i^s$ supply the matching mass-distribution lower bound. For the 24 maps of ratio $1/5$ retaining all but the central square, $O=(0,1)^2$ works and $s=\log24/\log5$.

= Cartesian powers of the Cantor set
{parent=Cantor set}
{c}
{title2=$\dim_HC^d=d\log2/\log3$}

For the middle-third <Cantor set> $C$ and positive integer $d$, $C^d$ is compact and <totally disconnected>. A connected subset projects to a point in each coordinate and is therefore itself a point. At level $n$, the product has $2^{dn}$ cubes of side $3^{-n}$. These covers give dimension at most $d\log2/\log3$. The product digit probability assigns each cube mass $2^{-dn}$; a ball of radius comparable to $3^{-n}$ meets only a bounded number of cubes, so its mass is at most a constant times $r^{d\log2/\log3}$. The cover-cost argument for <Hausdorff dimension> proves the matching lower bound. Thus compact totally disconnected Euclidean subsets can have arbitrarily large Hausdorff dimension when the ambient dimension is allowed to increase.

= Euclidean geometry
{c}
{parent=Geometry and topology}
{wiki}

<Euclidean geometry> describes flat <Euclidean space> using <lengths>, <angles>, and <rigid motions>. A coordinate realization uses points $x,y\in\mathbb R^n$, <Euclidean distance>
$$
d(x,y)=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-y_i)^2},
$$
and <angles> determined by the <dot product>. The coordinate axes are a convenience: <translations> and <rotations> change coordinates while preserving these measurements. This makes <Euclidean geometry> useful for comparing figures and converting measurements along different directions into a single distance.

In the <Euclidean plane>, through a point outside a line there is exactly one parallel line. This is a standard equivalent formulation of the <parallel postulate>. Euclid's fifth postulate in the <Elements> instead describes when two lines meet using the interior <angles> made by a crossing line.\a[https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/post5.html][Euclid, Elements I, Postulate 5]{ref} The assumption has consequences we often take for granted: a <triangle>'s interior <angles> sum to $\pi$, and <similar triangles> can differ in size while keeping their <angles>. Comparing with <spherical geometry> reveals that these are properties of a particular geometry.

As a computed example, the diagonal from $(0,0)$ to $(4,3)$ in a <rectangle> has <length> $\sqrt{4^2+3^2}=5$. The <Pythagorean theorem> explains why perpendicular contributions add as squares. If the directions cease to meet at a <right angle>, the <law of cosines> supplies the missing <angle>-dependent term. The beauty is that a single distance formula unites geometric constructions, coordinate calculations, and <orthogonality> in <linear algebra>.

= Parallel postulate
{parent=Euclidean geometry}
{wiki}

In the <Euclidean plane>, precisely one line through a point outside a given line is parallel to it. This formulation is equivalent to Euclid's fifth postulate within the usual axioms of plane geometry.

Its orientation-preserving <subgroup> is $SE(n)=SO(n)\ltimes\mathbb R^n$, the <semidirect product> of the <special orthogonal group> with translations. = Geometric locus
{parent=Euclidean geometry}

A <geometric locus> is the <set> of points satisfying a specified geometric or algebraic condition in an ambient space. For example, points at a fixed positive distance $r$ from $p$ form a <circle> in the plane and a <sphere> in three dimensions. In the <complex plane>, $|z-z_0|=r$ has Cartesian equation $(x-\operatorname{Re}z_0)^2+(y-\operatorname{Im}z_0)^2=r^2$, proving the <circle> description. A locus can also be a union, such as $x(y-1)=0$, which consists of two lines; domain restrictions in the original equation must be retained when simplifying it.

= Hypercube

A hypercube is the Cartesian product of $n$ equal-length closed intervals in <Euclidean space>. The centered cube $[-1,1]^n$ has $2^n$ vertices, each with coordinates in $\{-1,1\}$. Antipodal vertices determine $2^{n-1}$ body diagonals.

= Orthogonality of hypercube body diagonals
{parent=Hypercube}
{title2=$u\cdot v=n-2h$}

Two sign-vector vertices of a centered <hypercube> differing in $h$ coordinates have <dot product> $n-2h$. Thus their body diagonals can be <orthogonal> only in even dimension. A collection of mutually <orthogonal> nonzero diagonals has at most $n$ members; achieving $n$ is equivalent to the existence of a <Hadamard matrix> of order $n$. For unoriented lines the angle is determined by $|n-2h|/n$, while oriented vertex vectors retain the sign of their <dot product>.

= Point group

The <point group> of a Euclidean crystallographic group is the group of its orthogonal linear parts, equivalently its quotient by the translation subgroup. = Crystallographic restriction theorem
{parent=Point group}
{wiki}

The only integral values in $[-2,2]$ are $-2,-1,0,1,2$, which give precisely those rotation orders. Finiteness of a symmetry group alone would not impose this restriction.

= Double circular cone

= Intersection plane of parallel congruent double cones
{parent=Double circular cone}

Let two <double circular cones> have vertices $a_1,a_2$, common unit axis $n$, and semi-angle $0<\alpha<\pi/2$. Put $Q=\cos^2\alpha\,I-nn^T$ and $b=a_1-a_2\ne0$. Their equations are $(x-a_i)^TQ(x-a_i)=0$. Subtracting cancels the common quadratic term, so every intersection point satisfies
$$
(Qb)\cdot\left(x-\frac{a_1+a_2}{2}\right)=0.
$$
Since $Q$ has eigenvalues $\cos^2\alpha$ perpendicular to $n$ and $-\sin^2\alpha$ along $n$, $Qb\ne0$. Thus the intersection is contained in a plane, possibly with an empty intersection. Its unit normal is $Qb/|Qb|$, with $|Qb|^2=|b|^2\cos^4\alpha+(n\cdot b)^2(1-2\cos^2\alpha)$. Degenerate endpoint semi-angles can make the normal vanish and must be treated separately.

= Simple-cubic sphere packing
{parent=Sphere packing}

Equal spheres of radius $r$ occupy the sites of a cubic lattice of spacing $2r$. A cell contains one sphere, so its <packing fraction> is $(4\pi r^3/3)/(2r)^3=\pi/6$. This is an ideal arranged deposit rather than a claim about all settling suspensions.

= Circle packing
{parent=Geometric packing}

Arrangement of <circles> in a plane without interior overlap. The covered area fraction is its <packing fraction>. Equal-radius square packing has fraction $\pi/4$.

= Alternating-radius square circle packing
{parent=Circle packing}
{title2=$\eta=\pi(r_1^2+r_2^2)/(2d^2)$}

Place equal numbers of radii $r_1,r_2$ on alternating sites of a square nearest-neighbour lattice of spacing $d$. Nonoverlap requires $d\ge\max(r_1+r_2,\sqrt2r_1,\sqrt2r_2)$. = Injectivity of sums on two distinct rays
{parent=Euclidean ray}
{title2=$|P+Q|=|P||Q|$}

If finite point sets $P,Q$ lie on two distinct <Euclidean rays> in a plane, the map $(p,q)\mapsto p+q$ is <injective> because the two direction vectors are <linearly independent>. Thus $|P+Q|=|P||Q|$. If all points are nonzero and the rays have positive slope, every sum lies in the <open planar sector> between those rays.

= Tetrahedron centroid-to-midpoint sum of squares
{parent=Tetrahedron}

Place the <centroid> of a <tetrahedron> at the origin, so its four vertex vectors satisfy $r_1+r_2+r_3+r_4=0$. Midpoints of opposite edges have opposite vectors, hence equal distances from the centroid. For the three opposite-edge pairs, choose midpoint vectors $(r_1+r_j)/2$, $j=2,3,4$. Expanding their squared lengths and using $\sum_{j=2}^4r_j=-r_1$ gives
$$
\sum_{j=2}^4\left|\frac{r_1+r_j}{2}\right|^2=\frac14\sum_{i=1}^4|r_i|^2.
$$
The argument uses no regularity or equal-edge assumption on the tetrahedron.

= Incenter of a tetrahedron

= Sphere center from three tetrahedron face distances
{parent=Tetrahedron}
{title2=$\mathbf p=r(|\mathbf b\times\mathbf c|\mathbf a+|\mathbf c\times\mathbf a|\mathbf b+|\mathbf a\times\mathbf b|\mathbf c)/\tau$}

= Orthogonal opposite edges of a tetrahedron

Place one vertex of a <tetrahedron> at the origin and denote the other vertex vectors by $a,b,c$. Orthogonality of two pairs of <opposite edges of a tetrahedron> forces $a\cdot b=a\cdot c=b\cdot c$. = Pythagorean theorem
{c}
{parent=Euclidean geometry}
{wiki}
{title2=$a^2+b^2=c^2$}

For a <right triangle> in <Euclidean geometry> with perpendicular side <lengths> $a,b>0$ and <hypotenuse> $c$, the <Pythagorean theorem> states
$$
a^2+b^2=c^2.
$$
Geometrically, the <area> of the <square> on the <hypotenuse> equals the sum of the <areas> of the <squares> on the other two sides. Euclid's <Elements>, Book I, Proposition 47 states this <area> equality; Proposition 48 states its converse: a <triangle> satisfying the equality has a <right angle> opposite side $c$.\a[https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/propI47.html][Euclid, Elements I.47]{ref}\a[https://mathcs.clarku.edu/~djoyce/elements/bookI/propI48.html][Euclid, Elements I.48]{ref}

For $a=3$ and $b=4$, we compute $c=\sqrt{9+16}=5$. The <triangle> has <area> $ab/2=6$, and its side <lengths> form a <Pythagorean triple>. Conversely, a <triangle> measured to have sides $3,4,5$ must have a <right angle> between the sides $3$ and $4$. This makes the equality both a calculation tool and a test for perpendicularity.

The dissection below makes the general identity tangible. Both outer <squares> have side $a+b$ and contain the same four <congruent> <right triangles>. In the first arrangement, their <hypotenuses> bound a <square> of side $c$: adjacent acute <angles> together make a <right angle>. In the second, the uncovered regions are <squares> of sides $a$ and $b$. Subtracting the same total <triangle> <area> from equal outer <areas> gives
$$
c^2=(a+b)^2-4\frac{ab}{2}=a^2+b^2.
$$
For the pictured dimensions, the shared uncovered <area> is $49-24=25=9+16$.

{title=Rearranging four congruent 3-4-5 right triangles proves that the uncovered square areas satisfy $25=9+16$}

The same mechanism survives in <inner product spaces>: for <orthogonal> <vectors> $u,v$, expanding the <inner product> gives $\|u+v\|^2=\|u\|^2+\|v\|^2$. This is why <orthogonal projection> separates a squared distance into independent contributions. The familiar <right triangle> is the simplest picture of a much more reusable principle.

= Pythagorean triple
{c}
{parent=Pythagorean theorem}
{title2=$x^2+y^2=z^2$}
{wiki}

A Pythagorean triple consists of three positive <integers> satisfying $x^2+y^2=z^2$. It is primitive when their <greatest common divisor> is one. Reducing their <square numbers> modulo $3$ shows that at least one leg is divisible by $3$; reducing modulo $4$ shows that both legs cannot be odd.

= Primitive Pythagorean parametrization
{parent=Pythagorean triple}
{title2=$(a,b,c)=(2mn,m^2-n^2,m^2+n^2)$}

Every primitive <Pythagorean triple>, after exchanging its legs, has this form with $m>n>0$, $\gcd(m,n)=1$ and opposite parity. Its area is $mn(m-n)(m+n)$. The factors $m,n,m-n,m+n$ are pairwise coprime, because $m\pm n$ are odd. Conversely these conditions give a primitive triple. The parametrization follows by factoring the relatively prime integers $(c+b)/2$ and $(c-b)/2$ when $a$ is the even leg.

= Sphere

A sphere is the set of points at one fixed positive <Euclidean distance> from a center.

= Spherical shell
{parent=Sphere}
{title2=$r_1<|\boldsymbol x|<r_2$}

A spherical shell is the region between two concentric <spheres>. A thin mass shell at radius $r$, thickness $dr$ and <mass density> $\rho(r)$ has mass $4\pi r^2\rho(r)\,dr$ to first order. Decomposing a spherical body into such shells makes the <spherical shell theorem> applicable to its <gravitational potential energy>.

= Product of two spheres
{parent=Sphere}
{title2=$S^p\times S^q$}

The <Cartesian product> $S^p\times S^q$ has one cell in each of dimensions $0,p,q,p+q$, counting the two middle cells separately when $p=q$. For positive $p,q$, its integral <cohomology ring> has generators $a,b$ of degrees $p,q$, with $a^2=b^2=0$ and $ba=(-1)^{pq}ab$. The class $ab$ is a product orientation class. This follows from the <Künneth theorem>.

= Simultaneous antipodal quotient of two spheres
{parent=Product of two spheres}
{title2=$(S^p\times S^q)/\langle(-1,-1)\rangle$}

For positive $p,q$, quotient $S^p\times S^q$ by $(x,y)\mapsto(-x,-y)$. This is a free double <covering space> action. Its <closed manifold> quotient is orientable exactly when $p+q$ is even, because the product <mapping degree> is $(-1)^{p+q+2}$. If $p,q\ge2$, the product is <simply connected> and the quotient has <fundamental group> $\mathbb Z/2$. For $p=q=2$, the quotient has integral <cohomology groups> $\mathbb Z$ in degrees $0,4$, $\mathbb Z/2$ in degrees $2,3$, and zero elsewhere. Every positive-degree <cup product> is zero. These conclusions follow from <Poincare duality>, the <Euler characteristic under a finite covering>, and the <universal coefficient theorem for cohomology>.

= Surface measure on a sphere

Surface area is the two-dimensional measure induced on the unit <sphere> in three dimensions. It is invariant under orthogonal rotations. It is not the restriction of ambient three-dimensional <Lebesgue measure>, under which the <sphere> has measure zero. Normalizing by the <sphere> area gives a <probability measure>.

On a <sphere> centred at the origin, an antipodal pair is the unordered pair $\{x,-x\}$. The <antipodal map> exchanges its two points.

= Apollonius sphere
{parent=Sphere}
{c}
{title2=$|\mathbf x-\mathbf a|=\lambda|\mathbf x-\mathbf b|$}

For distinct points and $0<\lambda<1$, the distance-ratio locus is a sphere with center $(\mathbf a-\lambda^2\mathbf b)/(1-\lambda^2)$ and radius $\lambda|\mathbf a-\mathbf b|/(1-\lambda^2)$. Completing the square proves this formula. It encloses $\mathbf a$ but excludes $\mathbf b$, because their distances to the center are $\lambda R$ and $R/\lambda$. It is the zero-potential locus of two opposite-sign point charges with magnitude ratio $\lambda$. Equal magnitudes instead give a plane.

= Hemisphere
{parent=Sphere}

A hemisphere is either half of a sphere cut by a plane through its center. Its curved surface has area $2\pi r^2$. Closing that surface with its equatorial disk encloses a half-ball of volume $2\pi r^3/3$. The curved surface and enclosed solid have distinct roles in surface-cost and volume calculations.

= Open hemisphere
{parent=Hemisphere}
{title2=$\{x\in S^d:u\cdot x>0\}$}

For a nonzero vector $u$, the open hemisphere of $S^d$ with direction $u$ is $\{x\in S^d:u\cdot x>0\}$. Its boundary is excluded, so opposite <open hemispheres> are disjoint.

= General-position hemisphere count
{parent=Open hemisphere}
{title2=$N-d\geq2r-1$}

Choose $N$ points of $S^d$ such that every $d+1$ of their position vectors are <linearly independent>. Any equatorial hyperplane then contains at most $d$ points. If $N-d\geq2r-1$, at least one of each opposite pair of <open hemispheres> contains $r$ points. Such configurations exist: take distinct real parameters $t_i$ and normalize $(1,t_i,\ldots,t_i^d)$; the <Vandermonde determinant> proves the required independence. This weaker one-of-two guarantee already suffices for the topological lower bound in <Lovász theorem on Kneser graphs>.

= Gale hemisphere lemma
{c}
{parent=Open hemisphere}

For integers $n=2k+d$ with $k\geq1$ and $d\geq0$, there are $n$ labelled points of $S^d$ such that every <open hemisphere> contains at least $k$ of them. The normalized vectors $(-1)^i(1,t_i,\ldots,t_i^d)$ for increasing $t_i$ give a signed <moment curve> construction. A nonzero <polynomial> of <polynomial degree> at most $d$ cannot have enough sign changes for fewer than $k$ positive alternating sample values. Zero sample values can be perturbed negatively by a <Lagrange interpolation polynomial>. Labels allow repeated positions when $d=0$.

= Great circle
{parent=Sphere}
{wiki}

A great circle is the intersection of a <sphere> with a plane through its centre. Its shorter arcs are <geodesics> of the round <sphere>; celestial meridians and the equator are examples.

= Regions of a general-position great-circle arrangement
{parent=Great circle}
{title2=$R_N=N(N-1)+2$}

For $N\geq1$ <great circles> on a <sphere>, with no coincident circles or triple intersection, the number of open regions is $R_N=N(N-1)+2$. The first circle gives two regions. Each subsequent circle meets the previous $n-1$ circles at $2(n-1)$ distinct points and is divided into that many arcs, each splitting a previous region. Summing the increments proves the formula. If the circles are $a_i\cdot x=0$, each region has one fixed sign vector $(\operatorname{sgn}(a_i\cdot x))_i$. Conversely a realized strict sign vector has a connected region: its cone is an intersection of open <half-spaces>, and radial projection of line segments connects any two points.

= Gnomonic projection

The gnomonic projection intersects rays from a <sphere>’s centre with a tangent plane. If $\mathbf b$ is its unit normal and $\mathbf e,\mathbf n$ are tangent basis vectors, a direction $\mathbf s$ maps to $f(\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf e,\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf n)/(\mathbf s\cdot\mathbf b)$. It maps <great circle> arcs to straight lines and is singular at the tangent-plane horizon.

= Angular average
{parent=Solid angle}
{title2=$\langle F\rangle_\Omega=\frac1{4\pi}\int_{S^2}F\,d\Omega$}

The normalized <angular average> is over the unit <sphere>, with <solid angle> measure $d\Omega$. Rotational symmetry gives $\langle e^i\rangle_\Omega=0$, $\langle e^ie^j\rangle_\Omega=\delta^{ij}/3$, and $\langle e^ie^je^ke^\ell\rangle_\Omega=(\delta^{ij}\delta^{k\ell}+\delta^{ik}\delta^{j\ell}+\delta^{i\ell}\delta^{jk})/15$. The constants follow by contracting indices and using $e^ie_i=1$. These identities extract <photon angular temperature moments>. For normalized <spherical harmonics>, $\int Y_{\ell m}^*Y_{\ell' m'}\,d\Omega=\delta_{\ell\ell'}\delta_{mm'}$; their <angular average> instead has an extra factor $1/(4\pi)$.

= Incircle of a triangle
{parent=Triangle}
{title2=$r=\Delta/s$}

= Incircle
{synonym}

The <incircle of a triangle> is the <circle> inside the <triangle> tangent to all three sides. Its center is the intersection of two internal angle bisectors; its radius is $r=\Delta/s$, where $\Delta$ is the area and $s$ the semiperimeter. To prove it is the largest contained <circle>, let any candidate have center $P$, radius $\rho$ and distances $d_i\geq\rho$ from the three side lines. Splitting the <triangle> into three <triangles> with vertex $P$ gives $\Delta=\frac12\sum_i\ell_i d_i\geq s\rho$. The tangent <circle> attains equality, proving maximality. For a <right triangle> with legs $a,b$ and hypotenuse $c$, $r=(a+b-c)/2$, since $r=ab/(a+b+c)$ and $a^2+b^2=c^2$.

= Circumcircle
{parent=Triangle}

= Circumscribed circle of a triangle
{synonym}

The <circumcircle> of a nondegenerate <triangle> is the unique <circle> through its three vertices. Its center is the <circumcenter>, found by intersecting two <perpendicular bisectors>; equality of distances to all three vertices proves existence, and subtracting the squared-distance equations proves uniqueness. For a <right triangle>, the midpoint of its hypotenuse is equidistant from all vertices, so its circumradius is half the hypotenuse length.

= Borsuk conjecture
{c}
{parent=Euclidean geometry}
{wiki}

The Borsuk conjecture asserted that every bounded subset of $\mathbb R^d$ with positive diameter can be partitioned into $d+1$ subsets of strictly smaller diameter. It is false in sufficiently high dimension.

= Kahn-Kalai counterexample to the Borsuk conjecture
{c}
{parent=Borsuk conjecture}

Kahn and Kalai represented uniform subsets by quadratic sign vectors so that diameter pairs correspond to one forbidden intersection size. The <Frankl-Wilson theorem> bounds each smaller-diameter part, forcing exponentially many parts while the ambient dimension grows only quadratically.

= Quadratic sign-vector diameter formula
{parent=Kahn-Kalai counterexample to the Borsuk conjecture}
{title2=$\|u_Au_A^T-u_Bu_B^T\|_F^2=2(N^2-(4|A\cap B|-4p+3)^2)$}

Take $N=4p-1$, $|A|=|B|=2p-1$ and sign <vectors> $u_A=2\mathbf1_A-\mathbf1$. The <Frobenius inner product> of two outer products is $(u_A\cdot u_B)^2$, proving the formula. The squared <Euclidean distance> is largest precisely when $|A\cap B|=p-1$, where the inner product is minus one. The common diagonal can be deleted, leaving an isometric representation in dimension $\binom N2$ if off-diagonal coordinates are multiplied by $\sqrt2$. The <Frankl-Wilson theorem> then bounds each smaller-<diameter> class.

= Borsuk counterexample in dimension 1325
{c}
{parent=Kahn-Kalai counterexample to the Borsuk conjecture}
{title2=$d=\binom{52}{2}-1=1325$}

Use the <balanced cut-vector distance formula> with $p=13$. There are $\binom{51}{25}=247959266474052$ distinct cuts. A subset of strictly smaller <diameter> contains no pair with side intersection thirteen, so deleting their shared coordinate one and applying the <Frankl-Wilson theorem> bounds its size by $\binom{51}{12}=158753389900$. At least 1562 smaller-diameter parts are needed, exceeding $1325+1$. The cut vectors occupy an <affine hyperplane> in $\mathbb R^{1326}$, which identifies isometrically with $\mathbb R^{1325}$ and gives the stated explicit counterexample.

= Balanced cut-vector distance formula
{parent=Kahn-Kalai counterexample to the Borsuk conjecture}
{title2=$\|v_X-v_Y\|_2^2=4t(2p-t),\quad t=|X\cap Y|$}

Represent a balanced partition of $[4p]$ by the zero-one vector recording which unordered pairs lie across its cut. Complementary subsets represent the same cut; choose the representative containing coordinate one. Two such cuts differ on the pairs crossing the <symmetric difference> of their sides, giving the displayed distance. Its maximum is $4p^2$, attained exactly when $t=p$. Every cut has $4p^2$ occupied coordinates, so the vectors lie in an <affine hyperplane> of dimension at most $\binom{4p}{2}-1$.

= Volumetric covering bound
{parent=Borsuk conjecture}

The unit ball in $\mathbb R^n$ can be covered by at most $9^n$ balls of radius $1/4$. A maximal $1/4$-separated set supplies the centres, and disjoint radius-$1/8$ balls around them fit inside the ball of radius $9/8$.

= Conic section
{parent=Euclidean geometry}
{wiki}

= Directrix of a conic
{parent=Conic section}

A <directrix of a conic> is the fixed line in its focus-directrix description: distance to the <focus of a conic> is a constant multiple of distance to this line. For a <parabola> the multiple is one. The <directrix of a conic> belongs to the geometry of the <conic section>, rather than being a tangent or an asymptote.

= Focus of a conic
{parent=Conic section}

In the focus-directrix description of a noncircular <conic section>, the focus is the fixed point $F$ for which every point $X$ of the curve satisfies $|X-F|=e\,\operatorname{dist}(X,L)$, where $L$ is a <directrix of a conic> and $e$ is the <eccentricity of a conic>. For $y^2=4ax$ with $a>0$, the <focus of a conic> is $(a,0)$, the <directrix of a conic> is $x=-a$, and $e=1$.

= Eccentricity of a conic
{parent=Conic section}
{title2=$e$}

The parameter $e\geq0$ in a focus-centred <polar coordinates> equation $r=\ell/(1-e\cos\theta)$ for a <conic section>. It distinguishes an <ellipse> ($e<1$), a <parabola> ($e=1$), and a <hyperbola> ($e>1$).

= Semilatus rectum
{parent=Conic section}
{title2=$\ell$}

The distance from a focus to a <conic section> along a line perpendicular to its symmetry axis. In focus-centred <polar coordinates>, an <ellipse> may be written $r(1-e\cos\theta)=\ell$, where $e$ is its <eccentricity of a conic>.

= Parabola
{parent=Conic section}
{wiki}

A parabola is a <plane curve> whose points are equally distant from a focus and a directrix. In suitable coordinates it has equation $y=ax^2$, with $a\ne0$. Its quadratic geometry can also describe a <contour deformation> through a <saddle point>.

= Hyperbola
{title2=$x^2/a^2-y^2/b^2=1$}
{parent=Conic section}
{wiki}

A hyperbola is a <conic section> with two unbounded branches. In suitable coordinates its equation is $x^2/a^2-y^2/b^2=1$ with $a,b>0$. The unit hyperbola is parametrized by $x=\cosh t$, $y=\sinh t$ on its right branch, relating it to the <hyperbolic functions>.

= Ellipse
{parent=Conic section}
{wiki}

An ellipse is the locus of points whose distances from two fixed foci have constant sum. If the focal distance is $d$ and the sum is $c>d$, its semimajor and semiminor axes are $c/2$ and $\sqrt{c^2-d^2}/2$.

= Semi-major and semi-minor axes
{parent=Ellipse}
{wiki}
{title2=$a,b$}

The <semi-major and semi-minor axes> are the larger and smaller half-axis lengths of an <ellipse>. In principal-axis coordinates the <ellipse> has equation $x^2/a^2+y^2/b^2=1$ with $a\ge b>0$. The same terminology is used for a <hyperbola> $x^2/a^2-y^2/b^2=1$, where $a$ is half its transverse axis and $b$ half its conjugate axis. Orbital semiaxes apply these geometric quantities to <conic sections>.

= Ellipse perimeter expansion near a circle
{parent=Ellipse}
{title2=$L=2\pi(1-t/4-3t^2/64+O(t^3)),\quad t=1-x^2$}

For an <ellipse> with semiaxes $1,x$, the perimeter integral has integrand $\sqrt{1-t\cos^2\theta}$. The uniformly convergent <binomial series> near $t=0$ permits termwise integration. Integrals of $\cos^2\theta$ and $\cos^4\theta$ over a full period give the displayed first corrections. The same coefficients follow from the <Frobenius method> for the transformed perimeter equation.

= Nondegenerate ellipse
{parent=Ellipse}

A nondegenerate ellipse has two positive semiaxis lengths. It is a smooth closed curve rather than a point or line segment.

= Parity and upper bound for transverse ellipse intersections
{parent=Nondegenerate ellipse}
{title2=$|E_1\cap E_2|\in\{0,2,4\}$}

Parametrize one <ellipse> as $\gamma(t)=c+A(\cos t,\sin t)$ and substitute it into the quadratic equation $Q=0$ of the other. At a <transverse intersection>, $g(t)=Q(\gamma(t))$ has a simple zero, so its sign changes. Periodicity makes the number of zeros even. The function is a <trigonometric polynomial> of degree at most two, so multiplication by $z^2$, with $z=e^{it}$, gives a nonzero polynomial of degree at most four. Hence there are at most four distinct intersection points. Coincident ellipses are excluded by transversality.

= Focal line
{parent=Ellipse}

The focal line of an <ellipse> is the straight line through its two foci.

= Ellipsoid
{parent=Euclidean geometry}
{wiki}

An ellipsoid is a level set $(x-c)^TA(x-c)=1$ for a real symmetric <positive-definite matrix> $A$. Its principal axes are the orthonormal eigenvectors of $A$, and an eigenvalue $\lambda$ gives semi-axis length $1/\sqrt\lambda$.

= Nearest points to the origin on a positive-definite ellipsoid
{parent=Ellipsoid}
{title2=$d_{\min}=\lambda_{\max}^{-1/2}$}

For a <positive-definite symmetric matrix> $A$, the <ellipsoid> $x^TAx=1$ has nearest points to the origin exactly at $x=\lambda_{\max}^{-1/2}u$, where $u$ ranges over <unit vectors> in the largest-<eigenvalue> <eigenspace>. Indeed, the <spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices> gives $x^TAx\leq\lambda_{\max}|x|^2$, with equality precisely in that <eigenspace>. A simple largest <eigenvalue> gives two opposite nearest points; multiplicity $m>1$ gives a scaled $(m-1)$-sphere of nearest points.

= Solid ellipsoid
{parent=Ellipsoid}
{title2=$E=\{x:x^TQx<1\},\quad Q>0$}

The interior bounded by an <ellipsoid> is a solid ellipsoid. Positive coordinate dilations multiply its <symplectic capacity> by the square of the dilation factor.

= Cartesian second moment of a solid ellipsoid
{parent=Solid ellipsoid}
{title2=$\int_Dx^2\,dV=4\pi a^3bc/15$}

For the <solid ellipsoid> $D=\{x^2/a^2+y^2/b^2+z^2/c^2\le1\}$ with positive semi-axes, set $x=au$, $y=bv$, $z=cw$. The <Jacobian determinant> of this inverse coordinate map is $abc$, and $D$ becomes the unit ball. Symmetry gives $\int_{B_1}u^2\,dV=\frac13\int_{B_1}r^2\,dV=4\pi/15$, proving the displayed moment. The other two moments follow by interchanging the semi-axes; mixed moments vanish by reflection symmetry.

= John ellipsoid
{parent=Ellipsoid}
{c}
{title2=$\mathcal E$}

The <John ellipsoid> of a convex body is its inscribed <ellipsoid> of largest volume. For the <unit ball> $K$ of a $d$-dimensional <normed vector space>, change coordinates so that this <ellipsoid> is the <Euclidean unit ball>. The resulting coordinates are called <John position>. In these coordinates the <John contact decomposition> implies $B_2^d\subseteq K\subseteq\sqrt d B_2^d$, or $\|x\|_E\leq |x|\leq\sqrt d\|x\|_E$.

= John contact decomposition
{parent=John ellipsoid}
{c}
{title2=$\sum_j c_j u_j\otimes u_j=I$}

For a centrally symmetric convex body in <John position>, there are contact points $u_j\in\partial K\cap S^{d-1}$ and positive weights $c_j$ such that $\sum_jc_j u_j\otimes u_j=I$. Taking the <trace> gives $\sum_jc_j=d$. A supporting hyperplane at $u_j$ shows $|\langle u_j,x\rangle|\leq\|x\|_K$.

Here is the maximum-volume argument for the decomposition. If $I$ were outside the <convex cone> generated by the contact matrices, a <separating hyperplane> would give a <symmetric matrix> $A$ with $\operatorname{tr}A>0$ and $\langle Au,u\rangle\leq0$ at every contact. Subtract a small positive multiple of $I$ so these inequalities become strict while the <trace> remains positive. The <support function> of $(I+tA)B_2^d$ is $|(I+tA)v|$. It decreases below $1$ near all contact directions for small positive $t$; away from them, the strict gap $h_K(v)>1$ preserves containment. Its volume increases, since $\det(I+tA)=1+t\operatorname{tr}A+O(t^2)$, a contradiction. The cone is closed because its generators have <trace> $1$ and form a <compact set>; finite-dimensional <convexity> gives a finite decomposition.

= John position
{parent=John ellipsoid}
{c}

A centrally symmetric convex body is in <John position> when its <John ellipsoid> is the <Euclidean unit ball>.

= Coordinate-projection atlas of an ellipsoid
{parent=Ellipsoid}
{title2=$q_i=\varepsilon\sqrt{1-\sum_{j\ne i}q_j^2}$}

For nonzero real $a_i$, scale the ambient coordinates by $q_i=a_i r_i$. On the half where $\varepsilon q_i>0$, omitting $q_i$ is a <manifold chart> onto the <open unit ball>; its inverse restores $q_i=\varepsilon\sqrt{1-\sum_{j\ne i}q_j^2}$. These $2(n+1)$ charts form a <smooth atlas> of the <ellipsoid>, including for negative $a_i$.

= Two-chart smooth atlas of an ellipsoid
{parent=Ellipsoid}

If $D=\operatorname{diag}(a_1,\ldots,a_{n+1})$ is invertible, $x\mapsto Dx$ sends the <ellipsoid> $\sum_i a_i^2x_i^2=1$ to the unit <sphere>. On $u=(v,s)\in S^n$, the two <stereographic projections> $v/(1-s)$ and $v/(1+s)$ omit opposite poles and have inverses $(2w/(1+|w|^2),\pm(|w|^2-1)/(1+|w|^2))$, with the sign chosen for the appropriate pole. Their overlap transition is $w\mapsto w/|w|^2$, smooth and involutive on $\mathbb R^n\setminus\{0\}$. Composing their inverses with $D^{-1}$ gives a two-chart <smooth atlas> of the ellipsoid. Its <subspace topology> is <Hausdorff> and has a <second-countable space> topology, so it is a <smooth manifold>. For $n=0$ the overlap is empty and each chart is a point.

= Spherical cap area upper bound
{parent=Spherical cap}

= Isoperimetric inequality
{parent=Euclidean geometry}
{wiki}

The <isoperimetric inequality> bounds enclosed volume in terms of boundary size. In the plane it gives $4\pi A\leq L^2$, with equality for disks; higher-dimensional forms bound the boundary area of a region of prescribed volume.

= Planar isoperimetric inequality
{parent=Isoperimetric inequality}

This is the planar case of the <isoperimetric inequality>. Every bounded planar domain with a sufficiently regular boundary of length $L$ and area $A$ satisfies
$$
4\pi A\leq L^2.
$$
Equality holds exactly for circular domains.

= Hurwitz proof of the planar isoperimetric inequality
{c}
{parent=Planar isoperimetric inequality}

Parametrize a positively oriented closed <plane curve> at constant <arc length> speed on $[0,2\pi]$, and let $c_n$ be its complex-position <Fourier coefficients>. <Green's theorem> and <Parseval's identity> give $A=\pi\sum_n n|c_n|^2$ and $L^2/(4\pi^2)=\sum_n n^2|c_n|^2$. Since $n\leq n^2$ for integers, the <planar isoperimetric inequality> follows. Equality leaves only the constant and positive first harmonic, a translated circle.

= Circle equality in the planar isoperimetric inequality
{parent=Planar isoperimetric inequality}

Equality in the Cauchy-Schwarz and periodic Wirtinger bounds makes the arclength boundary parametrization a first harmonic with orthogonal equal-length coefficient vectors. The boundary is therefore a circle, and the enclosed domain is a disc.

= Isotropic tensor integral
{parent=Spherical symmetry}
{wiki}

A rotationally invariant rank-two tensor is a scalar multiple of $\delta_{ij}$; taking its trace determines the scalar.

= Quadratically weighted inverse-radius Hessian integral
{parent=Isotropic tensor integral}
{title2=$\int_{r<a}x_ix_j\partial_k\partial_l(r^{-1})\,dV=\frac{2\pi a^2}{15}(-2\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+3\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+3\delta_{il}\delta_{jk})$}

Away from the origin the <Hessian matrix> of $r^{-1}$ is $(3x_kx_l-r^2\delta_{kl})/r^5$. Multiplication by $x_ix_j$ makes the singularity locally integrable. Rotational invariance and the symmetries in each index pair reduce the integral to $A\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+B(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk})$. The Laplacian trace gives $3A+2B=0$, while contracting the two pairs as $k=i,l=j$ gives $3A+12B=\int_{r<a}2/r\,dV=4\pi a^2$. Solving these two equations yields the displayed expression.

= Null-vector cancellation of radial moments
{parent=Isotropic tensor integral}
{title2=$\int_{|x|<1}(x_1+ix_2)^n f(|x|)\,d^3x=0\quad(n\geq1)$}

Assume the radial moment integral exists. Rotation through $\phi$ about the third axis multiplies $x_1+ix_2$ by $e^{i\phi}$ and preserves the region, volume element and radial weight. The integral equals $e^{in\phi}$ times itself for every angle, so it vanishes. For degree two and four this also follows by contracting the isotropic sphere moments with the complex null vector $(1,i,0)$, whose bilinear square is zero. Odd moments vanish under inversion.

= Cartesian moments of a three-dimensional ball
{c}
{parent=Isotropic tensor integral}
{title2=$\int_{|x|\leq a}x_ix_j\,dV=\frac{4\pi a^5}{15}\delta_{ij}$}

<Spherical symmetry> makes moments over a ball <isotropic tensors>. Odd moments vanish under $x\mapsto-x$, while the second and fourth moments are
$$
\int_{|x|\leq a}x_ix_j\,dV=\frac{4\pi a^5}{15}\delta_{ij},\qquad
\int_{|x|\leq a}x_ix_jx_kx_l\,dV=\frac{4\pi a^7}{105}(\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}).
$$
For the second moment, a <tensor contraction> gives $3\alpha=\int r^2\,dV=4\pi a^5/5$. For the fourth, complete index symmetry makes the three coefficients equal and a double <tensor contraction> gives $15\beta=\int r^4\,dV=4\pi a^7/7$. These identities turn polynomial <volume integrals> into scalar radial integrals and are useful for inertia and rotational averages.

= Pullback of a mixed tensor by a diffeomorphism
{parent=Diffeomorphism}
{title2=$(\phi^*T)_p=[(d\phi_p)^{-1}]^{\otimes r}\otimes[(d\phi_p)^*]^{\otimes s}T_{\phi(p)}$}

= Mixed tensor pullback
{synonym}

For a <diffeomorphism>, pull a type $(r,s)$ <tensor field> back by the inverse differential on vector slots and the dual differential on covector slots. Invertibility distinguishes this construction from pullback of purely covariant tensors along an arbitrary smooth map. This operation preserves <tensor products> and <tensor contractions>, supplying the <flow definition of the Lie derivative of a tensor field>.

= C1 openness of diffeomorphisms
{c}
{parent=Diffeomorphism}
{title2=$\operatorname{Diff}(M)\text{ is }C^1\text{-open}$}

On a compact <smooth manifold> without boundary, every smooth self-map sufficiently $C^1$-close to a <diffeomorphism> is a <diffeomorphism>. Reducing to a map near the identity, invertibility of its differential follows uniformly by compactness. It is then a proper local <diffeomorphism>, hence a finite <covering map>. Sufficient $C^0$ closeness gives a <homotopy> to the identity on each component; its induced map on the <fundamental group> is surjective. The covering therefore has one sheet on each component, proving the assertion. This lets a nearby <Lagrangian submanifold> transverse to cotangent fibers be represented by a single global <differential one-form> graph.

= Large diffeomorphism
{parent=Diffeomorphism}

A large diffeomorphism does not approach the identity at the relevant boundary or cannot be continuously deformed to the identity within the allowed gauge transformations. It may therefore act nontrivially on physical states or boundary data.

= Large spatial diffeomorphism
{parent=Large diffeomorphism}

A large spatial diffeomorphism changes spatial coordinates by a transformation that remains nonzero at large distance. In cosmology, suitable large spatial diffeomorphisms generate zero-momentum adiabatic modes.

= Hyperbolic geometry
{parent=Geometry and topology}
{wiki}

Hyperbolic geometry studies spaces of constant negative curvature.

= Hyperbolic Pythagorean theorem
{parent=Hyperbolic geometry}
{title2=$\cosh c=\cosh a\cosh b$}

For a right-angled geodesic triangle in curvature minus one, with legs $a,b$ and hypotenuse $c$, the displayed identity replaces the Euclidean theorem. Normalize the right vertex to $i$ in the upper half-plane, one endpoint to $iy$ with $y>1$, and the other to $s+it$ with $s^2+t^2=1$ and $t>0$. The <hyperbolic distance in the Poincare half-plane> gives $\cosh a=(y+y^{-1})/2$, $\cosh b=1/t$ and $\cosh c=(1+y^2)/(2yt)$, proving the identity. For small lengths its expansion gives $c^2=a^2+b^2$ to leading order.

= Tessellation
{parent=Hyperbolic geometry}

A tessellation is a covering by tiles with pairwise disjoint interiors; a locally finite tessellation meets any compact set in only finitely many tiles. In a tessellation by congruent compact hyperbolic polygons, the adjacency graph records side-crossing steps. The area growth $\operatorname{Area}B(r)=2\pi(\cosh r-1)$ and bounded tile diameter imply exponential growth of the number of tiles within $n$ steps: a unit geodesic segment meets a uniformly bounded number of tiles, by packing congruent interior balls, so a metric ball of radius proportional to $n$ lies in the step ball. Conversely the step ball lies in a metric ball of radius proportional to $n$. The regular right-angled pentagons with four around every vertex give the tiling $\{5,4\}$.

= Hyperbolic circle in the Poincare disc
{parent=Hyperbolic geometry}
{title2=$d_D(p,z)=R$}

In the <Poincare disc model>, a <hyperbolic circle in the Poincare disc> consists of points at one fixed hyperbolic distance from a center. At center zero it is the Euclidean circle $|z|=\tanh(R/2)$, since radial hyperbolic distance is $2\operatorname{artanh}|z|$. A <Poincare disc automorphism> sending a general center to zero transports this description. Conversely every Euclidean circle entirely inside the disc is such a circle: the product of inversion in that circle and unit-circle inversion has reciprocal fixed points, one inside; sending the inner fixed point to zero makes the circle concentric with the unit circle.

= Hyperbolic space
{parent=Hyperbolic geometry}
{title2=$\mathbb H^n$}
{wiki}

The complete simply connected <Riemannian manifold> of dimension $n$ with constant sectional curvature $-1$ is hyperbolic space. In the upper half-space model its metric is $ds^2=(dx_1^2+\cdots+dx_n^2)/x_n^2$ for $x_n>0$. Its ideal boundary in dimension three is the <Riemann sphere>.

= Bounded displacement sets of hyperbolic isometries are compact
{parent=Hyperbolic space}

In the positive Hermitian determinant-one model of three-dimensional <hyperbolic space>, the base point is $I$ and a matrix $g\in SL(2,\mathbb C)$ sends it to $gg^*$. The distance obeys $2\cosh d(I,gg^*)=\operatorname{tr}(gg^*)$. Bounded displacement therefore bounds every matrix entry, and the determinant-one condition gives a closed bounded subset of a finite-dimensional real vector space. Passing to $PSL(2,\mathbb C)$ preserves compactness. Conjugating moves any chosen base point to $I$. A discrete subgroup meets each such compact set in finitely many elements, proving escape of distinct elements and proper discontinuity, including finite stabilizers.

= Minimum enclosing ball in hyperbolic space
{parent=Hyperbolic space}

For a nonempty finite set $\{p_1,\ldots,p_m\}$ in finite-dimensional <hyperbolic space>, the continuous function $R(x)=\max_jd(x,p_j)$ attains its minimum. Indeed, a bounded candidate radius confines all possible improving centers to a closed bounded ball about $p_1$. Such a ball is compact in the <Poincare ball model>, since its points remain away from the ideal boundary. Minimizing $R$ on it supplies a <closed ball> of smallest enclosing radius. More generally this argument works for any nonempty bounded set in a <proper metric space> when the supremum-distance function is finite.

= Axis of a hyperbolic-space isometry
{parent=Hyperbolic space}

An isometry of <hyperbolic space> with positive attained <translation length> has an invariant complete geodesic joining its two ideal fixed points. This is its axis. It translates along the axis by its translation length; in three dimensions it may additionally rotate around it. In upper half-space, $(z,t)\mapsto(kz,|k|t)$ with $|k|\ne1$ has the vertical axis through zero and length $|\log|k||$. Every connecting path has length at least the change in $\log t$, and a vertical segment attains the bound.

= Poincare ball model
{c}
{parent=Hyperbolic space}
{title2=$ds^2=4|dx|^2/(1-|x|^2)^2$}

The unit ball in Euclidean space with the displayed <Riemannian metric> models <hyperbolic space> of curvature minus one. Its ideal boundary is the unit sphere, and its geodesics are diameters or circle arcs orthogonal to that sphere. The metric is invariant under the conformal ball automorphisms.

= Hyperbolic extension of a Möbius transformation
{parent=Poincare ball model}

A <Möbius transformation> of the ideal two-sphere extends uniquely to an orientation-preserving <isometry> of three-dimensional <hyperbolic space>. In upper half-space, translations, complex similarities, and $z\mapsto-1/z$ extend respectively to $(z,t)\mapsto(z+b,t)$, $(\lambda z,|\lambda|t)$, and $(-\overline z,t)/(|z|^2+t^2)$. Each preserves $(|dz|^2+dt^2)/t^2$; composing these extensions constructs the general case. Ball-origin stabilizers are ordinary rotations, represented on the boundary by $SU(2)$ modulo its central signs.

= Hyperbolic hexagon
{parent=Hyperbolic geometry}

A six-sided polygon in the <hyperbolic plane> whose sides are <geodesic segments>. The especially useful right-angled case is a <right-angled hyperbolic hexagon>.

= Right-angled hyperbolic hexagon
{parent=Hyperbolic hexagon}

A geodesic hexagon in the <hyperbolic plane> with all six interior angles $\pi/2$. Any three positive alternating side lengths determine it up to <isometry>. If those sides are $\ell_i/2$, the opposite seam satisfies $\cosh s_i=(\cosh(\ell_i/2)+\cosh(\ell_j/2)\cosh(\ell_k/2))/(\sinh(\ell_j/2)\sinh(\ell_k/2))$. Two congruent such hexagons glued along the other three sides form a <pair of pants (mathematics)> with boundary lengths $\ell_1,\ell_2,\ell_3$.

= Beltrami-Klein model
{parent=Hyperbolic geometry}
{c}

The Beltrami-Klein model realizes the <hyperbolic plane> inside an open Euclidean disk, with <geodesics> given by straight chords. Its metric differs from the Euclidean metric, and angles are generally distorted. The straight chords make <geodesic convexity> especially easy to see.

= Hyperbolic surface
{parent=Hyperbolic geometry}
{title2=$K_{\rm Gaussian}=-1$}

A surface with a complete <Riemannian metric> of constant <Gaussian curvature> $-1$. Its local model is the <Poincare half-plane model>; the orientable case is a quotient by a free <properly discontinuous group action> of orientation-preserving isometries.

= Hyperbolic holonomy representation
{parent=Hyperbolic surface}
{title2=$\rho:\pi_1(\Sigma_g)\to\operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb R)$}

For a marked closed <hyperbolic surface>, the representation of its <fundamental group> whose image acts as the deck group on the <hyperbolic plane>. It is discrete and faithful, determined up to conjugation by the marking and the metric, and gives $X=\mathbb H^2/\rho(\pi_1\Sigma_g)$. For a closed orientable surface it admits determinant-one lifts. It is different from the tangent-space action of the <Riemannian holonomy group>.

= Length spectra
{synonym}

The unmarked length spectrum of a compact <hyperbolic surface> is the multiset of lengths of its closed <geodesics>, with a consistent convention for primitive curves and orientation. The marked version labels lengths by free homotopy classes. The <Selberg trace formula> relates the unmarked multiset to the <Laplace-Beltrami operator> <spectrum>.

= Marked length spectrum
{parent=Length spectrum}
{title2=$[\gamma]\mapsto\ell(\gamma)$}

For a closed <hyperbolic surface> with a specified marking of its <fundamental group>, assign to each nontrivial conjugacy class the length of its unique closed <geodesic>. Retaining the group label is stronger than retaining only the unmarked length multiset: it also records which lengths belong to intersecting curves or powers of particular curves.

= Huber spectral equivalence theorem
{c}
{parent=Length spectrum}

= Huber's theorem
{c}
{synonym}

For closed <hyperbolic surfaces> of curvature $-1$, equality of the <Laplace-Beltrami operator> <spectra>, including multiplicities, is equivalent to equality of the unmarked <length spectra> of closed <geodesics>. The <Selberg trace formula> proves the equivalence. Primitive lengths with multiplicities and the multiset including their iterates determine one another, provided the same orientation convention is used.

= Uniform finiteness of short geodesic classes
{parent=Length spectrum}

In a compact smooth family of marked closed <hyperbolic surfaces>, only finitely many <free homotopy> classes of <closed geodesics> have length below a fixed bound somewhere in the family. A uniform <bilipschitz equivalence> with a fixed metric reduces this to local finiteness of its <length spectrum>.

= Buser finite length spectrum theorem
{c}
{parent=Length spectrum}
{title2=$L=L(g,\varepsilon)$}

= Buser's finite length spectrum theorem
{c}
{synonym}

For fixed genus $g\ge2$ and systole lower bound $\varepsilon>0$, a uniform finite length cutoff $L$ determines the entire unmarked <length spectrum> of a closed <hyperbolic surface> in the thick part, including multiplicities. Equality up to $L$ forces equality everywhere. This is a finite-determination statement for spectra and does not claim that all isospectral surfaces are isometric.

= Finite spectral matching by polynomial invariants
{parent=Buser finite length spectrum theorem}

Suppose a compact parameter set carries a locally finite countable multiset of positive quantities, and a fixed injective transform of each labelled quantity is a <polynomial> in finitely many ambient coordinates. Assume only finitely many labels can have bounded value anywhere in the compact set. Then a uniform finite initial multiset determines the entire multiset. For each source label there are finitely many possible matching labels. Equality for finitely many labels in both directions is a finite union of <algebraic sets>, obtained from injective matchings and polynomial equalities. These algebraic sets decrease as more labels are included, so they stabilize by the <Hilbert basis theorem>. A compatible infinite matching in each direction follows by the <König infinity lemma>, and local finiteness makes multiplicities equal. Squared traces of <hyperbolic holonomy representations> give the application to the <Buser finite length spectrum theorem>.

= Selberg trace formula
{c}
{wiki}
{parent=Length spectrum}

For a compact <hyperbolic surface>, the Selberg trace formula equates a sum over <Laplace-Beltrami operator> <eigenvalues> to an area term and a sum over primitive closed <geodesics> and their iterates. It proves that the <Laplacian> <spectrum> and the unmarked <length spectrum>, with multiplicities, determine each other.

= Hyperbolic collar
{title2=$dr^2+\ell^2\cosh^2r\,dt^2$}
{parent=Hyperbolic surface}

A collar around a simple closed <geodesic> of length $\ell$ is an embedded annulus with metric $dr^2+\ell^2\cosh^2r\,dt^2$, where $t\in\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$. The <collar lemma> gives half-width $w=\operatorname{arcsinh}(1/\sinh(\ell/2))$. As $\ell$ tends to zero the collar gets wide, forcing crossing <geodesics> to become long.

= Shortest closed geodesic on the three-punctured sphere
{parent=Hyperbolic surface}
{title2=$\ell_{\min}=2\log(3+2\sqrt2)$}

In curvature $-1$, its length is $2\operatorname{arcosh}3$. The level-two <principal congruence subgroup> has traces congruent to two modulo four, so the smallest hyperbolic absolute <trace> is six, achieved by $\begin{pmatrix}5&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}$. The <hyperbolic translation length> formula gives the result; the shortest closed <geodesic> is nonsimple.

= Pants decomposition
{parent=Hyperbolic surface}
{title2=$\{\gamma_i\}_{i=1}^{3g-3}$}

A collection of disjoint essential <simple closed curves> that cuts a closed <genus>-$g$ surface into $2g-2$ surfaces of type <pair of pants (mathematics)>. There are $3g-3$ curves. <Geodesic> representatives are used for <Fenchel–Nielsen coordinates>.

= Elementary move of a pants decomposition
{parent=Pants decomposition}

An elementary move replaces one cuff inside the four-holed sphere or one-holed torus obtained by uniting its adjacent pants. The replacement is an essential curve meeting the old cuff minimally. It changes the <pants decomposition graph> without changing the underlying surface; the identity surface map identifies the two descriptions.

= Pants decomposition graph
{title2=$V=2g-2,\ E=3g-3$}
{parent=Pants decomposition}

The graph of a <pants decomposition> has one vertex per <pair of pants (mathematics)> and one edge per glued cuff. It is a connected trivalent multigraph, possibly with loops. A closed surface of genus $g$ gives $2g-2$ vertices and $3g-3$ edges, so its first <Betti number> is $g$. The graph records a decomposition rather than the homeomorphism class alone, and omits cuff lengths and twists.

= Bers pants decomposition theorem
{c}
{parent=Pants decomposition}
{title2=$\ell_i\leq B_g$}

For fixed $g\geq2$, a constant $B_g$ bounds all cuff lengths in some <pants decomposition> of every closed <genus>-$g$ <hyperbolic surface>. Together with <Fenchel–Nielsen coordinates> this implies <Mumford's compactness theorem>.

= Fenchel–Nielsen coordinates
{c}
{parent=Pants decomposition}
{title2=$(\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_{3g-3},\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_{3g-3})$}
{wiki}

For a fixed <pants decomposition>, the positive cuff lengths and real twist parameters give coordinates on <Teichmüller space>. With twists measured in length units, a full <Dehn twist> changes $\tau_i$ by $\ell_i$; with angle units it changes the twist by $2\pi$.

= Fenchel–Nielsen twist
{c}
{parent=Fenchel–Nielsen coordinates}
{title2=$\tau$}

After cutting a <hyperbolic surface> along a simple closed <geodesic>, reglue its two boundary copies after an arclength displacement $\tau$. The boundary length remains fixed, while the <Riemannian metric> varies. In marked <Fenchel–Nielsen coordinates>, the twist is real-valued; changing it by one boundary length corresponds to a <Dehn twist> in the marking. This construction also applies to a hyperbolic pair of pants with a fixed cone angle and two geodesic boundaries.

= Finite length coordinates for hyperbolic surfaces
{parent=Fenchel–Nielsen coordinates}
{title2=$9g-9\text{ marked geodesic lengths}$}

For a fixed <pants decomposition> of a closed <genus>-$g$ <hyperbolic surface>, its $3g-3$ cuff lengths determine all its <Y-pieces>. Two suitably chosen transverse simple closed curves at each cuff, one obtained from the other by a <Dehn twist>, determine the corresponding twist: hyperbolic seam identities express their lengths through shifted hyperbolic cosines of that twist, removing the sign ambiguity of just one transversal. Thus finitely many marked lengths determine the <Fenchel–Nielsen coordinates>. The unmarked <length spectrum> lacks these labels, which is why this fact alone does not imply global spectral uniqueness.

= Collar lemma
{parent=Hyperbolic surface}
{title2=$\sinh w\,\sinh(\ell/2)=1$}

A <simple closed curve> that is a <geodesic> of length $\ell$ on a <hyperbolic surface> has an embedded collar of half-width $w$ with $\sinh w\,\sinh(\ell/2)=1$. In circumference-one coordinates its metric is $dr^2+\ell^2\cosh^2r\,dt^2$, and its <conformal modulus of an annulus> is $(2/\ell)\arctan(\operatorname{csch}(\ell/2))\sim\pi/\ell$.

= Hyperbolic systole
{parent=Hyperbolic surface}
{title2=$\operatorname{sys}_{\rm hyp}(X)$}

The <infimum> of nonconstant closed <geodesic> lengths in curvature $-1$, with value $+\infty$ if there are none. On a <closed surface> with a <hyperbolic metric> this is a positive attained minimum, and a minimizing <geodesic> is simple. For fixed closed <genus> $g\geq2$, a common positive lower bound gives relative compactness in the unmarked <moduli space of Riemann surfaces> by <Mumford's compactness theorem>.

= Hyperbolic area
{parent=Hyperbolic surface}
{title2=$dA=dx\,dy/y^2$}

The metric area of a <hyperbolic surface>. In the <Poincare half-plane model> the density is $dx\,dy/y^2$. A finite-area torsion-free quotient of <genus> $g$ with $r$ cusps has area $2\pi(2g-2+r)$ by the <Gauss-Bonnet theorem>.

= Hyperbolic metric
{parent=Hyperbolic geometry}

A hyperbolic metric is a complete Riemannian metric of constant sectional curvature $-1$. On a surface it supplies local charts modeled on the <hyperbolic plane>.

= Poincare metric on a Riemann surface
{parent=Hyperbolic metric}
{c}
{title2=$ds_{\mathbb D}=2|dz|/(1-|z|^2)$}

A connected <Riemann surface> whose <universal cover> is the <unit disc> inherits the complete curvature-minus-one metric displayed on its cover. <Möbius transformations> preserving the disc are isometries, so <deck transformations> preserve the metric and it descends to the surface. This uniformization notion must be distinguished from the potential-theoretic existence of a Green function. <Holomorphic maps> between such surfaces contract their metric by lifting to <Schwarz-Pick theorem> on the covers.

= Hyperbolic tube around a radial segment
{parent=Poincare metric on a Riemann surface}
{title2=$\operatorname{Im}w/|w|\geq\operatorname{sech}k$}

Under the <Cayley transform between the half-plane and disk>, a radial segment ending at a boundary point becomes a vertical half-ray. If a point is within <hyperbolic distance> $k$ of that half-ray, it lies in a fixed angular sector. Indeed, in the <complex upper half-plane>, $\cosh\rho(w,iy)=(|w|^2+y^2)/(2y\operatorname{Im}w)\geq|w|/\operatorname{Im}w$. This converts bounded hyperbolic approach to a boundary point into nontangential approach, allowing <normal-family proof of angular boundary convergence> to transfer radial limits of bounded <holomorphic functions>.

= Hyperbolic derivative density
{parent=Poincare metric on a Riemann surface}
{title2=$H_g(z)=2|g'(z)|/(1-|g(z)|^2)$}

For a nonconstant <holomorphic map> into the <unit disc>, the displayed density pulls the target <hyperbolic metric> back to the source Euclidean coordinate. It is not the operator norm between two hyperbolic tangent spaces, which additionally divides by the source metric density. Away from <ramification points> $\Delta\log H_g=H_g^2$; a <ramification point> of <multiplicity> $m$ adds $2\pi m$ times its point mass to the distributional <Laplacian>.

= Hyperbolic characteristic of a disc map
{parent=Hyperbolic derivative density}
{title2=$T_{\mathbb D}(R)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{|z|<R}\log(R/|z|)H_g^2\,dA$}

This logarithmic area integral measures the accumulated pullback <hyperbolic metric> area of a <holomorphic> disc map. Its nonnegative kernel makes it increasing in $R$. <Schwarz-Pick theorem> bounds $H_g(z)$ by $2/(1-|z|^2)$, which controls its characteristic through a critical-point Jensen identity.

= Critical-point Jensen identity for the hyperbolic derivative
{parent=Hyperbolic characteristic of a disc map}
{title2=$T_{\mathbb D}(R)+N_{\rm crit}(R)=\langle\log H_g\rangle_R-\log H_g(0)$}

For $g'(0)\ne0$, apply <Jensen's formula> with the smooth curvature term to the <hyperbolic derivative density>. Its distributional <Laplacian> is $H_g^2+2\pi\sum m_a\delta_a$, giving the displayed identity with <ramification points> defined by $g'(a)=0$. If $g'(z)=cz^m+\cdots$ at zero, use $m\log R$ as the origin counting term and replace the base constant by $\log\lim_{z\to0}H_g(z)/|z|^m$. Without this regularization the basepoint-critical formula is undefined.

= Hyperbolic metric on the punctured disk

The <universal covering map> $\tau\mapsto e^{i\tau}$ from the <complex upper half-plane> to the punctured <unit disc> pulls back the metric $|dz|/(|z|\log(1/|z|))$ to $|d\tau|/\operatorname{Im}\tau$. This is its complete curvature-minus-one <hyperbolic metric>, which is different from restricting the ordinary disk metric to the punctured disk.

= Hyperbolic zero sequences on a punctured disk
{parent=Hyperbolic metric on the punctured disk}
{title2=$z_n=e^{-n^2},\qquad e^{-\rho(e^{-1},z_n)}=n^{-2}$}

The curvature-minus-one <hyperbolic metric on the punctured disk> assigns distance $2\log n$ along the radial geodesic from $e^{-1}$ to $e^{-n^2}$. = Pseudohyperbolic distance
{parent=Hyperbolic metric}
{title2=$\delta(z,w)=|(z-w)/(1-\overline wz)|$}

On the <unit disc>, the <pseudohyperbolic distance> is
$$
\delta(z,w)=\left|\frac{z-w}{1-\overline wz}\right|.
$$
It is invariant under disc <Möbius transformations>. For the curvature-minus-one <hyperbolic metric> with length element $2|dz|/(1-|z|^2)$, $\delta(z,w)=\tanh(\rho(z,w)/2)$ and $e^{-\rho(z,w)}=(1-\delta)/(1+\delta)$. The <Schwarz-Pick theorem> says that <holomorphic functions> from the disc into itself contract this distance. = Hyperbolic polygon

A <geodesic> polygon in the <hyperbolic plane> is bounded by finitely many <geodesic> segments. For curvature $-1$, its <area> is its angle defect, as given by <hyperbolic polygon area>. = Hyperbolic circle in the upper half-plane

= Hyperbolic line

In curvature minus one, a hyperbolic disc of radius $R$ has area

A(R)=4\pi\sinh^2(R/2)=2\pi(\cosh R-1).
$$
In the Poincare disc this follows by integrating $4r(1-r^2)^{-2}\,dr\,d\theta$ up to Euclidean radius $\tanh(R/2)$.

= Hyperbolic area bounds an inscribed disc

If a domain of finite <hyperbolic area> $A$ contains a hyperbolic disc of radius $r$ in curvature $-1$, then $2\pi(\cosh r-1)\leq A$, hence $r\leq\operatorname{arcosh}(1+A/(2\pi))$. For an interior point of a <hyperbolic triangle>, the open disc with radius equal to its distance from the boundary lies inside the triangle. Its area is at most $\pi$ by <Gauss-Bonnet theorem>, giving the elementary bound $r\leq\operatorname{arcosh}(3/2)$; this need not be the sharp triangle inradius bound.

r_n=\frac{4^n-1}{4^n+1}.
$$

= Geodesics of the Poincare disc
{parent=Poincaré disk model}
{c}

The geodesics are diameters and arcs of circles orthogonal to the unit circle. A radial segment is length minimizing because
$$
\int\frac{2\sqrt{\dot r^{,2}+r^2\dot\theta^{,2}}}{1-r^2}\,dt
\geq2\operatorname{artanh}r,
$$
with equality for constant angle and monotone radius. Hence the distance from the origin to $z$ is $2\operatorname{artanh}|z|$.

= Hyperbolic distance in the Poincare disc
{c}
{parent=Poincaré disk model}

For $z_1,z_2$ in the <Poincare disc model>, put

\rho=\left|\frac{z_2-z_1}{1-\overline{z_1}z_2}\right|.

Their <hyperbolic distance> is

d(z_1,z_2)=2\operatorname{artanh}\rho
=\log\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}.

= Hyperbolic geodesic cap
{parent=Hyperbolic distance in the Poincare disc}
{title2=$Q(I)$}

For a proper arc $I$ of the boundary of the <unit disc>, $Q(I)$ is the side of the <hyperbolic geodesic> between its endpoints adjacent to $I$. For short arcs this is a curved boundary cap. For arcs longer than a semicircle it contains the center. With endpoints $e^{\pm i\alpha}$ and $0<\alpha<\pi/2$, the geodesic is the circle with center $\sec\alpha$ and radius $\tan\alpha$, orthogonal to the <unit circle>. It is a diameter at $\alpha=\pi/2$.

= Geodesic-cap and Carleson-box comparison
{parent=Hyperbolic geodesic cap}

For a short arc $I$ of normalized length $\ell$, every point of its <hyperbolic geodesic cap> lies within Euclidean distance $C\ell$ of its center boundary point. Conversely, for any fixed $b>0$, the region with angular projection in $I$ and depth $1-|z|\le b\ell$ lies in $Q(\kappa I)$ for a fixed enlargement $\kappa$ depending only on $b$, when $I$ is sufficiently short. To verify this, rotate the midpoint to one and write the endpoints as $e^{\pm i\alpha}$. The cap inequality is $2\operatorname{Re}z>(1+|z|^2)\cos\alpha$. Its radial depth at the midpoint is $1-\sec\alpha+\tan\alpha$, comparable to $\alpha$ for small $\alpha$; its angular projection lies in $I$. Enlarging $\alpha$ by a sufficiently large fixed factor makes the inequality hold throughout the indicated rectangle. Large arcs use a finite cover of the disc. Thus <Carleson boxes> and <hyperbolic geodesic caps> define equivalent <Carleson measure> conditions.

The rectangle containment can also be checked directly. Write $z=re^{i\theta}$ with $|\theta|\le\pi\ell$ and $1-r\le b\ell$. Its membership in the enlarged cap is equivalent to
$$
\cos\theta-\cos(\kappa\pi\ell)>\frac{(1-r)^2}{2r}\cos(\kappa\pi\ell).
$$
For small enough $\ell$, $r\ge1/2$. The right side is at most $b^2\ell^2$, while the left side is at least $(\kappa^2-1)\pi^2\ell^2/8$, using the sine product formula and $\sin x\ge x/2$ for small positive $x$. Thus $\kappa=4+4b/\pi$ suffices.

= Equality of horizontal hyperbolic and Euclidean distances
{parent=Hyperbolic distance in the Poincare half-plane}
{title2=$b=a/(2\sinh(a/2))$}

For points $ib$ and $a+ib$ with $a,b>0$, the <hyperbolic distance> is $2\operatorname{arsinh}(a/(2b))$. It equals their Euclidean distance $a$ exactly when the displayed relation holds. The function of $a$ decreases continuously from one to zero: its derivative has the sign of $\sinh(a/2)-(a/2)\cosh(a/2)<0$. The geodesics in the upper half-plane are vertical lines and semicircles centred on the boundary axis. Indeed, the length integrand $\sqrt{1+y'^2}/y$ has the <Beltrami identity>

= Common perpendicular of ultraparallel hyperbolic lines
{parent=Geodesic in the Poincare half-plane model}

Two disjoint hyperbolic lines with four distinct ideal endpoints have a unique common perpendicular, and the segment of that perpendicular between them realizes their minimum distance. An upper-half-plane isometry can make the two lines concentric semicircles; their common perpendicular then becomes the imaginary axis.

= Configurations of three ultraparallel lines
{parent=Common perpendicular of ultraparallel hyperbolic lines}

Three pairwise <ultraparallel hyperbolic lines> may have disjoint common perpendiculars or coincident ones. In the upper-half-plane model, three semicircles of radius $1/2$ centred at $-5/2,0,5/2$ have mutually disjoint common perpendiculars. Three concentric semicircles instead have the same vertical common perpendicular. Thus pairwise ultraparallelism alone does not determine the incidence of their perpendiculars.

= Hyperbolic lines meeting the imaginary axis at a fixed angle

The hyperbolic metric in the half-plane model is conformal, so hyperbolic and Euclidean angles agree. The line meeting the imaginary axis at $iy$, $y>0$, at signed angle $\alpha\in(0,\pi)$ is represented by the Euclidean circle with centre $y\cot\alpha$ on the real axis and radius $y\csc\alpha$.

= Isometry group of the Poincare half-plane
{title2=$\operatorname{Isom}(\mathbb H^2)$}
{c}
{parent=PSL2(R)}

Every orientation-preserving isometry of the <Poincare half-plane model> lies in $PSL_2(\mathbb R)$. Every orientation-reversing isometry is the composition of one of these with the <hyperbolic reflection> $z\mapsto-\overline z$ in the imaginary axis. Equivalently, the full isometry group consists of real Möbius and anti-Möbius transformations, modulo nonzero scalar matrices.

= Transitivity on pointed hyperbolic lines
{parent=PSL2(R)}

The group $PSL_2(\mathbb R)$ acts transitively on pairs $(\ell,P)$ consisting of a hyperbolic line and a point on it. Send the two ideal endpoints of $\ell$ to $0$ and $\infty$, then use a positive dilation to move the resulting point on the imaginary axis to $i$.

= Hyperbolic reflection
{parent=Poincaré half-plane model}

Reflection in a hyperbolic line is the unique orientation-reversing hyperbolic isometry fixing that line pointwise. The product of reflections in intersecting lines is a rotation through twice their angle.

= Every hyperbolic-plane isometry is a product of at most three reflections
{parent=Hyperbolic reflection}

A reflection in the perpendicular bisector of a point and its image corrects that point. A second bisector reflection through the corrected point can correct another point without moving the first. An <isometry> fixing two distinct points is either the identity or reflection in their joining geodesic: points with prescribed distances from both centres have at most two possibilities, exchanged by that reflection. Fixing a third noncollinear point then fixes every point. This constructs a factorization with at most three <hyperbolic reflections>.

= Composition of reflections in ultraparallel hyperbolic lines
{parent=Hyperbolic reflection}

The composition of <hyperbolic reflections> in two <ultraparallel hyperbolic lines> has infinite order. Map their common perpendicular to the <imaginary axis> in the <Poincare half-plane model>. The two reflecting <hyperbolic lines> become semicircles centred at zero, with distinct radii $r_1,r_2$. Reflection in the radius-$r$ semicircle is $z\mapsto r^2/\overline z$, so their composition is $z\mapsto(r_2/r_1)^2z$. Every positive power remains nonidentity, because $(r_2/r_1)^{2k}\ne1$ for $k\ge1$.

= Hyperbolic half-turn
{parent=Poincaré half-plane model}

A hyperbolic half-turn is the unique orientation-preserving isometry of order two fixing a given point. It exchanges every pair of points lying at equal opposite distances along a geodesic through its centre.

= Hyperbolic triangle inradius bound
{parent=Hyperbolic triangle}

For curvature minus one, no <hyperbolic triangle> contains an open hyperbolic disc of radius greater than $\tfrac12\log3$. Move the disc centre to zero in the <Poincare disc model> and enclose the triangle in an ideal triangle with the same radial vertex directions. One gap between ideal directions is at least $2\pi/3$. An ideal hyperbolic triangle has all three vertices on the boundary at infinity of the <hyperbolic plane>. All its angles are zero and its <hyperbolic area> in curvature $-1$ is $\pi$, by <hyperbolic triangle area>.

= Ideal vertex

= Hyperbolic side-side-angle ambiguity
{parent=Hyperbolic triangle}
{title2=$c_\pm=\ln(3\pm\sqrt2)$}

Two corresponding side lengths and an equal nonincluded angle need not determine a <hyperbolic triangle>. Set $A=\pi/6$, $\cosh a=3/2$, $\cosh b=2$. The <hyperbolic law of cosines> has two distinct positive solutions $c=\ln(3\pm\sqrt2)$. The two triangles share $a,b,A$ but have different third sides. An included angle instead determines the third side by the cosine rule.

= Hyperbolic side-side-side congruence
{parent=Hyperbolic triangle}

Three corresponding side lengths determine a <hyperbolic triangle> up to an <isometry> of the <hyperbolic plane>. First match an ordered pair of vertices by an isometry. The third vertex lies at the intersection of two prescribed hyperbolic distance circles. There are at most two choices, interchanged by reflection in the geodesic through the matched pair; either choice can therefore be matched.

= Hyperbolic triangle area

For curvature $-1$, a triangle of angles $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ has area $\pi-\alpha-\beta-\gamma$ by Gauss--Bonnet.

= Hyperbolic polygon area

For a geodesic hyperbolic $n$-gon of curvature $-1$ with interior angles $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n$, triangulation or the Gauss--Bonnet theorem gives
$$
\operatorname{area}=(n-2)\pi-\sum_{j=1}^n\alpha_j.
$$

= Regular hyperbolic polygon with prescribed area
{parent=Hyperbolic polygon area}

In curvature $-1$, a regular $n$-gon of circumradius $R>0$ has interior angle $\alpha(R)=2\arctan(\cot(\pi/n)/\cosh R)$, by the right-triangle angle form of the <hyperbolic law of cosines>. Thus its <hyperbolic polygon area> is $(n-2)\pi-n\alpha(R)$, continuously and strictly increasing from $0$ to $(n-2)\pi$. Every area in that open interval is therefore attained at exactly one circumradius.

= Cobordism
{parent=Geometry and topology}
{wiki}

A cobordism from a closed oriented $n$-manifold $X_0$ to $X_1$ is a compact oriented $(n+1)$-manifold $W$ whose oriented boundary is $-X_0\sqcup X_1$.

= Witt bordism
{parent=Cobordism}
{c}
{title2=$\Omega_n^{\mathrm{Witt}}$}

Two closed oriented rational <Witt spaces> are Witt bordant if their signed disjoint union is the collared boundary of a compact oriented <Witt space with collared boundary>. Disjoint union gives the bordism group $\Omega_n^{\mathrm{Witt}}$.

= Witt null-bordism in dimensions one two and three
{parent=Witt bordism}
{c}

Every odd-dimensional closed oriented <Witt space> bounds its closed cone: the new vertex has odd-dimensional link, so introduces no Witt obstruction. For a closed oriented two-dimensional pseudomanifold, normalize to a <surface>, fill each <surface> component by a <handlebody>, and attach a collar of the original space using the finite normalization map. Along the singular collar arcs the links are unions of circles; at their interior starting points the links are wedges of two-spheres. These links have zero middle <intersection homology>, so the construction is a three-dimensional Witt null-bordism.

= Witt bordism classification
{parent=Witt bordism}
{c}

For $k\ge1$, the rational middle intersection pairing induces an isomorphism $\Omega_{4k}^{\mathrm{Witt}}\cong W(\mathbb Q)$. The restriction $k>0$ matters: the degree-zero group is $\mathbb Z$. In positive dimension congruent to zero modulo four, a space is Witt null-bordant precisely when its middle <intersection form> is metabolic.

= Metabolic intersection form of a four-dimensional product
{parent=Witt bordism classification}

A nontrivial product of positive-dimensional closed oriented <Witt spaces> of total dimension four has metabolic rational middle <intersection form>. For dimensions $1+3$, the two Künneth summands in degree two pair only with one another, so either is a half-dimensional isotropic subspace. For dimensions $2+2$, the outside degree-two summands pair with one another, and the $IH^1\otimes IH^1$ summand has the tensor pairing of two symplectic forms. A Lagrangian in the first symplectic factor tensored with the entire second factor is a half-dimensional isotropic subspace of that summand. Adding one outside summand gives a Lagrangian in the complete middle form. The <Witt bordism classification> then makes the product null-bordant.

= Trivial cobordism
{parent=Cobordism}
{title2=$M\times[0,1]$}

A <cobordism> is trivial when it is <diffeomorphic> to a cylinder with the prescribed incoming boundary identification. Both boundary inclusions are <homotopy equivalences> and its <relative homology> with respect to either end vanishes. It can have <critical points> for a chosen <cobordism Morse function>; those must form an acyclic relative <Morse chain complex>.

= H-cobordism
{parent=Cobordism}

A compact smooth <cobordism> is an <h-cobordism> when both boundary inclusions $M_i\hookrightarrow W$ are <homotopy equivalences>. The name does not itself impose simple connectivity. A <trivial cobordism> is an <h-cobordism> because a product cylinder retracts onto either end.

= H-cobordism theorem
{parent=H-cobordism}

A compact smooth <h-cobordism> of dimension at least six between connected simply connected closed <manifolds> is <diffeomorphic> to a product, relative to its incoming boundary. The boundary <manifolds> consequently are <diffeomorphic>. The dimension and simple-connectivity hypotheses are sufficient conditions, not necessary properties of a product cylinder. The two boundary inclusions being <homotopy equivalences> are necessary for a <trivial cobordism>.

= Framed cobordism

Framed cobordism is the <generalized cohomology theory> of stable cohomotopy. Its geometric maps have a <stable framing of a vector bundle> on their normal data. A finite covering has canonically framed zero-dimensional relative tangent data; its Gysin class of $1$ is the stable transfer of the cover. A trivial $d$-sheeted cover gives $d\cdot1$ in degree zero.

= Chern-Weil theory
{c}
{synonym}

Chern-Weil theory constructs closed characteristic forms by applying invariant polynomials to the curvature of a connection.

= Pontryagin class
{c}
{parent=Chern-Weil homomorphism}
{title2=$p_k(E)=(-1)^kc_{2k}(E\otimes_{\mathbb R}\mathbb C)$}

For a real <vector bundle> $E$, its Pontryagin classes are the even-index <Chern classes> of its complexification with the displayed alternating signs. They lie in integral <cohomology> of degrees $4k$ and obey a total-class product formula under <direct sums>. If a metric <connection on a vector bundle> has curvature matrix $R$, its first real representative is $p_1(E)=-\operatorname{tr}(R\wedge R)/(8\pi^2)$. The <Bianchi identity> makes this representative closed, and <Chern-Weil connection transgression> makes its <de Rham cohomology> class independent of the connection. This normalization gives degree-four <A-hat form> $-p_1/24$.

= A-hat form
{parent=Chern-Weil homomorphism}
{title2=$\widehat A(R)=\det{}^{1/2}\!\left(\frac{R/(4\pi i)}{\sinh(R/(4\pi i))}\right)$}

For the <vector-bundle curvature> matrix $R$ of a metric <connection on a vector bundle> on a real oriented <vector bundle>, the A-hat form is the even <differential form> defined by the displayed finite-degree <power series>, with square root having constant term one. The expression is independent of the choice of orthonormal frame. Equivalently, for formal paired curvature <Chern roots> $\pm x_j$, it is $\prod_j(x_j/2)/\sinh(x_j/2)$. The <Bianchi identity> makes it closed, and changing the connection changes it by an <exact differential form> via <Chern-Weil connection transgression>. Its degree-four part is $-p_1/24$, where $p_1=-\operatorname{tr}(R\wedge R)/(8\pi^2)$ is the first <Pontryagin class> form.

= Chern-Weil connection transgression
{parent=Chern-Weil homomorphism}
{c}
{title2=$\partial_t\operatorname{Tr}F_t^2=2d\operatorname{Tr}(\dot A_t\wedge F_t)$}

A variation of the <connection one-form> has $\dot F=D_A\dot A$. The <Bianchi identity> and graded cyclic <matrix trace> imply $\partial_t\operatorname{Tr}(F\wedge F)=2d\operatorname{Tr}(\dot A\wedge F)$. Thus the <Chern-Weil theory> four-form changes by an <exact differential form>, and its integral on a closed four-cycle is connection-independent. For a general unitary bundle, include the corresponding trace-product term in the <second Chern form>.

= Second Chern form
{c}
{parent=Chern-Weil homomorphism}

For curvature $F=dA+A\wedge A$, a standard normalization of the second Chern form is
$$
C_2=\frac1{8\pi^2}\operatorname{Tr}(F\wedge F).
$$

= Second Chern number
{title2=$c_2$}
{c}
{parent=Second Chern form}

The second Chern number is the integral of the second Chern class over an oriented four-cycle. For a suitably normalized $SU(n)$ curvature on a compactified four-manifold, it is an integer.

= Chern-Simons form
{c}
{parent=Chern-Weil homomorphism}
{wiki=Chern–Simons_form}

A <Chern-Simons form> is an odd-degree transgression form whose <exterior derivative> is an invariant polynomial in the <curvature form>. For the trace polynomial of degree $k$, $d\omega_{2k-1}=\operatorname{Tr}(F^k)$; the <Chern-Simons 3-form> is the degree-three case.

= Chern-Simons 3-form
{c}
{parent=Chern-Simons form}

= Chern-Simons three-form
{synonym}
{c}

The Chern-Simons 3-form
$$
Y=\frac1{8\pi^2}\operatorname{Tr}\left(A\wedge dA+\frac23A\wedge A\wedge A\right)
$$
satisfies $dY=C_2$.

= Chern-Simons number of a gauge field
{parent=Chern-Simons 3-form}
{c}
{title2=$N_{\rm CS}$}

= Chern-Simons number
{synonym}
{c}

For an oriented three-dimensional slice $\Sigma$, use the <skew-Hermitian> trace convention
$$
N_{\rm CS}[A]= -\frac1{8\pi^2}\int_\Sigma\operatorname{tr}\left(A\wedge dA+\frac23A\wedge A\wedge A\right).
$$
Since the differential of the integrand is $\operatorname{tr}(F\wedge F)$, a space-time slab oriented by $dt\wedge\operatorname{vol}_\Sigma$ has <instanton> charge $k=N_{\rm CS}(t_+)-N_{\rm CS}(t_-)$ when the side boundary contributes no flux. For a pure gauge $A=h^{-1}dh$, this reduces to the winding integral $(24\pi^2)^{-1}\int_\Sigma\operatorname{tr}(h^{-1}dh)^3$. <Gauge transformations> of nonzero winding can shift it by an <integer>.

= Gauge change of the Chern-Simons three-form
{parent=Chern-Simons 3-form}

For an anti-Hermitian $SU(N)$ <connection one-form>, normalize the <Chern-Simons three-form> by $1/(8\pi^2)$ and use $A^g=g^{-1}Ag+g^{-1}dg$. Its change is $-d\operatorname{Tr}(dg\,g^{-1}\wedge A)/(8\pi^2)-\operatorname{Tr}(g^{-1}dg)^3/(24\pi^2)$. On a closed three-manifold the first term integrates to zero, while the latter is an integer with the fundamental <matrix trace>. Hence the integral is defined modulo integers; integer-level exponentials are invariant under large <Yang-Mills gauge transformations>.

= Volume
{parent=Geometry and topology}
{title2=$V$}
{wiki}

Volume measures the size of a three-dimensional region. In Euclidean space it is the <Lebesgue measure> $V=\int_\Omega dx\,dy\,dz$; a layer of thickness $h$ over a planar region has $V=\int h\,dA$.

= Radian
{parent=Angle}
{wiki}
{title2=$\mathrm{rad}$}

An <angle> in <radians> is the ratio of its subtended arc <length> to the radius of a <circle>. A full turn is $2\pi$ <radians> and a <right angle> is $\pi/2$ <radians>.

= Minute and second of arc
{parent=Angle}
{wiki}

A minute of arc is one sixtieth of a degree; a second of arc is one sixtieth of a minute of arc. These units describe small astronomical <angles>, including <astronomical seeing>.

= Arcsecond
{parent=Minute and second of arc}
{title2=$1''=\pi/648000\ \mathrm{rad}$}

An arcsecond is $1/3600$ of a degree, equivalently $\pi/648000$ radians. There are approximately $206265$ arcseconds in a radian.
