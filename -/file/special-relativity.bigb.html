= Special relativity
{wiki}

= Spacetime
{parent=Special relativity}
{wiki}

Spacetime combines spatial position and time into one geometric setting for physical events. In <special relativity> it is modeled by <Minkowski spacetime>; in <general relativity> its geometry is dynamical.

= Proper time
{title2=$\tau$}
{parent=Spacetime}
{wiki}

Proper time is the time measured by a clock following a timelike worldline. With metric signature $(-,+,+,+)$ and units $c=1$,
$$
d\tau^2=-ds^2
$$
along that worldline.

= Null vector
{parent=Special relativity}
{wiki}

A nonzero spacetime vector $k$ is null when its Minkowski norm vanishes, $k^ak_a=0$. Null tangent vectors describe the local directions of light rays.

Special relativity describes inertial frames using Lorentz transformations and Minkowski spacetime.

= Lorentz factor
{title2=$\gamma$}
{parent=Special relativity}
{c}
{wiki}

For speed $v<c$, the Lorentz factor is
$$
\gamma=\frac1{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}.
$$

= Time dilation
{parent=Lorentz factor}
{wiki}

If a clock measures proper-time interval $\Delta\tau$ while moving at constant speed $v$ in an inertial frame, that frame measures $\Delta t=\gamma\Delta\tau$.

= Ultrarelativistic particle
{parent=Special relativity}

= Ultrarelativistic
{synonym}

An ultrarelativistic particle has energy much larger than its rest energy. Its dispersion relation is then approximated by $E=pc$.

= Galilean transformation
{parent=Special relativity}
{wiki}

= Galilean boost
{c}
{synonym}

For inertial frames with relative velocity $u$ along the $x$-axis, the Galilean transformation is
$$
t'=t,\qquad x'=x-ut.
$$

= Principle of Galilean relativity
{c}
{parent=Galilean transformation}

The laws of Newtonian mechanics have the same form in every <inertial frame> related by a <Galilean transformation>.

= Lorentz transformation
{title2=$\Lambda$}
{parent=Special relativity}
{c}
{wiki}

A Lorentz transformation preserves the <Minkowski metric>. A boost of speed $u$ along the $x$-axis uses $\beta=u/c$ and
$$
ct'=\gamma(ct-\beta x),\qquad
x'=\gamma(x-\beta ct).
$$

= Lorentz scalar
{c}
{parent=Lorentz transformation}
{wiki}

A Lorentz scalar is unchanged under a <Lorentz transformation>. Contracting every Lorentz index in a tensor expression produces a scalar when the constituent fields transform covariantly.

= Lorentz tensor
{c}
{parent=Lorentz transformation}
{wiki}

A Lorentz tensor transforms by one Lorentz matrix for each contravariant index and one inverse matrix for each covariant index. Complete contraction of all indices produces a Lorentz scalar.

= Lorentz group
{title2=$O(1,3)$}
{c}
{parent=Lorentz transformation}
{wiki}

The Lorentz group consists of real matrices preserving the <Minkowski metric>: $\Lambda^T\eta\Lambda=\eta$.

= Lie algebra of the Lorentz group
{title2=$\mathfrak o(1,3)$}
{c}
{parent=Lorentz group}

For $M=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,1,1)$,
$$
\mathfrak o(1,3)=\{Y\in M_4(\mathbb R):Y^TM+MY=0\}.
$$
Every such matrix has the block form
$$
Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&v^T\\v&\Omega\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
v\in\mathbb R^3,\quad\Omega^T=-\Omega,
$$
so the Lie algebra and the <Lorentz group> have dimension six. At $A\in O(1,3)$, the tangent space is $A\mathfrak o(1,3)$.

= Orthochronous Lorentz group
{title2=$O^+(1,3)$}
{c}
{parent=Lorentz group}

The orthochronous Lorentz group consists of Lorentz transformations preserving the future sheet of the unit timelike hyperboloid. Equivalently, $A_{00}>0$. This is an open subgroup of $O(1,3)$ and hence a full-dimensional submanifold.

= Proper orthochronous Lorentz group
{c}
{parent=Lorentz group}

The proper orthochronous Lorentz group is the identity component of the <Lorentz group>. Its elements have determinant one and preserve the future light cone.

= Poincare group
{c}
{parent=Lorentz group}
{wiki=Poincaré_group}

The Poincare group is the <semidirect product> of spacetime translations by the <Lorentz group>. It is the isometry group of <Minkowski spacetime>.

= Poincare algebra
{c}
{parent=Poincare group}

The Poincare algebra is generated by translations $P^\mu$ and Lorentz transformations $M^{\mu\nu}$. Its mixed commutator is $[M^{\mu\nu},P^\rho]=i(\eta^{\nu\rho}P^\mu-\eta^{\mu\rho}P^\nu)$.

= Pauli-Lubanski pseudovector
{title2=$W_\mu$}
{c}
{parent=Poincare algebra}
{wiki}

The Pauli-Lubanski pseudovector is $W_\mu=\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}M^{\nu\rho}P^\sigma/2$, up to an overall sign convention. Its square is a Casimir of the Poincare algebra and measures spin in a massive irreducible representation.

= Little group
{parent=Poincare group}
{wiki=Wigner's_little_group}

The little group of a momentum $p$ is its stabilizer in the Lorentz group. Its <unitary irreducible representation>[unitary irreducible representations] label the internal degrees of freedom of one-particle states on the orbit of $p$.

= Helicity
{title2=$\lambda$}
{parent=Little group}
{wiki}

Helicity is the projection of angular momentum onto the momentum direction. For a finite-helicity massless particle, the Pauli-Lubanski vector satisfies $W^\mu=\lambda P^\mu$.

= Continuous-spin representation
{parent=Little group}
{wiki}

A continuous-spin representation is a massless unitary irreducible representation of the Poincare group in which the translation subgroup of the $ISO(2)$ little group acts nontrivially. It contains infinitely many helicities.

= Wigner's classification
{c}
{parent=Little group}
{wiki}

= Wigner classification
{c}
{synonym}

Wigner's classification obtains irreducible positive-energy representations of the <Poincare group> by inducing from <unitary irreducible representation>[unitary irreducible representations] of the <little group> of a standard momentum.

= One-dimensional Lorentz group
{title2=$SO(1,1)$}
{c}
{parent=Lorentz group}

The determinant-one Lorentz group in one space and one time dimension has two connected components. Its identity component consists of boosts
$$
\begin{pmatrix}\cosh\varphi&\sinh\varphi\\\sinh\varphi&\cosh\varphi\end{pmatrix},
$$
and the other component is its negative.

= Null coordinates in two-dimensional Minkowski spacetime
{title2=$u=x+y,\ v=x-y$}
{parent=One-dimensional Lorentz group}

A boost of rapidity $\varphi$ acts on null coordinates by $u\mapsto e^\varphi u$ and $v\mapsto e^{-\varphi}v$, preserving $uv=x^2-y^2$.

= Retarded and advanced null coordinates
{title2=$u=t-z,\ v=t+z$}
{parent=Null coordinates in two-dimensional Minkowski spacetime}

In the $(t,z)$ plane, $u=t-z$ is retarded time and $v=t+z$ is advanced time. The radial part of the <Minkowski metric> is $-dt^2+dz^2=-du\,dv$.

= Minkowski wave operator in null coordinates
{title2=$\Box=-4\partial_u\partial_v+\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2$}
{parent=Retarded and advanced null coordinates}

For $u=t-z$ and $v=t+z$, the four-dimensional Minkowski wave operator is
$$
\Box=-4\partial_u\partial_v+\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2.
$$
Every profile depending only on one null coordinate solves the wave equation when it is uniform in the transverse coordinates.

= Nonrelativistic limit
{parent=Lorentz transformation}

The nonrelativistic limit takes characteristic speeds much smaller than $c$. Lorentz transformations then reduce to Galilean transformations at leading order.

= Relativistic velocity-addition formula
{parent=Lorentz transformation}
{wiki=Velocity-addition_formula}

For a boost of speed $V$ along $x$,
$$
u'_x=\frac{u_x-V}{1-Vu_x/c^2},
\qquad
u'_y=\frac{u_y}{\gamma_V(1-Vu_x/c^2)}.
$$

= Four-vector
{parent=Special relativity}
{wiki=Four-vector}

= Lorentz four-vector
{c}
{synonym}

A four-vector transforms as $U'^\mu=\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu U^\nu$ under every Lorentz transformation.

= Axial vector
{parent=Four-vector}
{wiki}

An axial vector, or pseudovector, transforms like a vector under proper rotations but acquires an additional sign relative to a polar vector under orientation reversal. Angular momentum and spin are axial vectors.

= Causality
{parent=Special relativity}
{wiki=Causality_(physics)}

Causality requires an effect at an event to depend only on sources in its past light cone. Relativistic field equations implement this choice through retarded Green functions.

= Minkowski spacetime
{c}
{parent=Special relativity}
{wiki}

Minkowski spacetime is the flat spacetime of special relativity.

= Minkowski metric
{c}
{parent=Minkowski spacetime}
{wiki}

With the mostly-plus convention, the Minkowski metric is $\eta_{\mu\nu}=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,1,1)$.

= Minkowski norm
{title2=$U\cdot U$}
{c}
{parent=Minkowski metric}
{wiki=Minkowski_space}

The Minkowski norm is the Lorentz-invariant quadratic form $U\cdot U=\eta_{\mu\nu}U^\mu U^\nu$.

= Light cone
{parent=Minkowski metric}
{wiki}

The light cone through an event consists of null displacement vectors. In one spatial dimension its two directions satisfy $x=\pm ct$.

= Past light cone
{parent=Light cone}

The past light cone of an event consists of earlier events connected to it by past-directed null curves. A retarded field at the event depends on sources where their worldlines intersect this cone.

= Speed of light
{title2=$c$}
{parent=Light cone}
{wiki}

The speed of light $c$ is the invariant speed of every inertial frame and the limiting speed of causal propagation in special relativity.

= Null directions
{parent=Light cone}
{wiki=Lightlike}

Null directions are tangent directions of the <light cone>; their Minkowski squared norm is zero.

= Null curve
{parent=Minkowski metric}
{wiki=Lightlike}

A null curve has tangent $u$ satisfying $g(u,u)=0$. It represents the possible spacetime path of a light signal.

= Null geodesic
{parent=Null curve}

A null geodesic is both a <null curve> and a <geodesic>. In geometric optics, light rays follow null geodesics.

= World line
{parent=Special relativity}
{wiki}

= Worldline
{synonym}

A world line is the path of an object through spacetime. A massive observer follows a <timelike curve>.

= Timelike curve
{parent=World line}

A timelike curve has an everywhere timelike tangent vector and can represent the <world line> of a massive observer.

= Subluminal speed
{parent=Special relativity}

A subluminal speed has magnitude strictly below the speed of light: $|v|<c$. Massive particles follow timelike worldlines and therefore move subluminally in every inertial frame.

= Impossibility of photon decay into two massive particles
{parent=Special relativity}

The sum of two future-pointing timelike four-momenta is timelike. It therefore cannot equal a photon's null four-momentum, which forbids spontaneous photon decay into a massive particle pair in vacuum.

= Four-momentum
{parent=Special relativity}
{wiki}

A massive particle has four-momentum
$$
P^\mu=(E/c,\mathbf p)=m\gamma(c,\mathbf v),
\qquad P^2=m^2c^2.
$$
For a massless particle, $P^\mu=(E/c,\mathbf p)$ with $E=c|\mathbf p|$ and $P^2=0$.

= Four-momentum conservation
{parent=Four-momentum}

In an isolated relativistic interaction, the sum of incoming four-momenta equals the sum of outgoing four-momenta.

= Rest energy
{title2=$E_0=mc^2$}
{parent=Four-momentum}
{wiki}

The rest energy of a particle of invariant mass $m$ is $mc^2$, the energy measured in its rest frame.

= Center-of-momentum frame
{parent=Four-momentum}
{wiki=Center-of-momentum_frame}

= Centre-of-momentum frame
{synonym}

The center-of-momentum frame is an inertial frame in which the total spatial momentum of a system vanishes. For a two-particle initial state, the momenta are equal and opposite and the total invariant energy is $\sqrt s$.

= Centre-of-momentum energy
{title2=$\sqrt s$}
{parent=Center-of-momentum frame}

The centre-of-momentum energy is the total energy measured in the <centre-of-momentum frame>. For a scattering process with total four-momentum $P$, it is the Lorentz-invariant quantity $\sqrt{P^2}=\sqrt s$ in the mostly-minus convention.

= Relativistic energy-momentum relation
{parent=Four-momentum}
{wiki=Energy–momentum_relation}

For a particle of rest mass $m$,
$$
\mathcal E^2=m^2c^4+c^2|\mathbf p|^2.
$$

= Rest mass
{title2=$m$}
{parent=Relativistic energy-momentum relation}
{wiki=Invariant_mass}

Rest mass is the invariant mass $m$ in the <relativistic energy-momentum relation>. It equals the energy measured in the particle's rest frame divided by $c^2$.

= Conservation of relativistic energy
{parent=Relativistic energy-momentum relation}
{wiki=Energy_conservation}

In an isolated relativistic collision, total four-momentum is conserved; its time component states conservation of total relativistic energy.

= Relativistic motion in a constant electric field with transverse momentum
{parent=Relativistic energy-momentum relation}

For $\mathbf E=(0,0,E)$, $\mathbf B=0$, and initial momentum $(p_0,0,0)$,
$$
p_x=p_0,\qquad p_z=qEt,\qquad
\mathcal E(t)=\sqrt{\mathcal E_0^2+c^2q^2E^2t^2}.
$$
With the initial <position> at the origin,
$$
z(t)=\frac{\mathcal E(t)-\mathcal E_0}{qE},
\qquad
x(t)=\frac{cp_0}{qE}
\operatorname{arsinh}\frac{cqEt}{\mathcal E_0}.
$$

= Relativistic force
{title2=$F=dp/dt$}
{parent=Four-momentum}
{wiki=Four-force}

For three-momentum $p=\gamma mv$, force and acceleration satisfy
$$
F=m\gamma\left(a+\frac{\gamma^2}{c^2}(v\mathbin{\cdot}a)v\right),
\qquad
a=\frac1{m\gamma}\left(F-\frac{F\mathbin{\cdot}v}{c^2}v\right).
$$

= Relativistic motion under a constant force from rest
{parent=Relativistic force}

Under a constant force $F$ with $p(0)=0$, momentum is $p=Ft$ and
$$
v(t)=\frac{Ft/m}{\sqrt{1+|F|^2t^2/(m^2c^2)}}.
$$
The speed approaches $c$ without reaching it at finite time.

= Relativistic two-body decay
{parent=Four-momentum}
{wiki}

For a rest-frame decay $1\to2+3$,
$$
E_2=\frac{m_1^2+m_2^2-m_3^2}{2m_1}c^2,
\qquad
E_3=\frac{m_1^2+m_3^2-m_2^2}{2m_1}c^2.
$$
The decay is allowed exactly when $m_1\geq m_2+m_3$.

= Mandelstam variables
{parent=Four-momentum}
{c}
{wiki}

For two-to-two scattering $A+B\to C+D$,
$$
s=(P_A+P_B)^2,\qquad
t=(P_A-P_C)^2,\qquad
u=(P_A-P_D)^2.
$$
They satisfy $s+t+u=m_A^2+m_B^2+m_C^2+m_D^2$ in units $c=1$.

= Four-velocity
{parent=Special relativity}
{wiki}

Four-velocity is the proper-time derivative of position and has invariant squared norm c^2.

= Proper-time normalization
{parent=Four-velocity}

With signature $(-,+,+,+)$ and units $c=1$, parametrizing a timelike worldline by <proper time> makes its <four-velocity> satisfy $U^aU_a=-1$.

= Four-acceleration
{parent=Special relativity}
{wiki}

Four-acceleration is the proper-time derivative of four-velocity and is orthogonal to it.

= Proper acceleration
{parent=Four-acceleration}
{wiki}

Proper acceleration is the acceleration measured in the particle’s instantaneous rest frame.

= Rindler horizon
{parent=Proper acceleration}
{c}
{wiki}

A uniformly accelerated observer has a null boundary beyond which light signals can never reach the observer.

= Relativistic causality
{parent=Special relativity}

Relativistic causality requires physical influences and controllable information to propagate only within or on the future light cone of their source.

= Spacelike separation
{parent=Relativistic causality}
{wiki}

Two events are spacelike separated when no signal traveling at or below the speed of light can connect them. Their temporal order can differ between inertial frames.

= Superluminal signalling
{parent=Relativistic causality}
{wiki=Faster-than-light_communication}

= Superluminal signal
{synonym}

Superluminal signalling would transmit controllable information outside the source event's future light cone and would violate <relativistic causality>.
