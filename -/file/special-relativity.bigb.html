= Special relativity
{wiki}

= Einstein's principle of relativity
{c}
{parent=Special relativity}

The laws of physics have the same form in all <inertial frames>; uniform absolute motion cannot be identified by experiments confined to an inertial laboratory. The invariant vacuum speed of light is a separate postulate. Together they motivate the <Lorentz transformations> of <special relativity>.

= Relativistic particle
{parent=Special relativity}
{title2=$u^\mu u_\mu=c^2$}

A relativistic massive particle is described by a timelike worldline with proper-time four-velocity $u^\mu$. In signature $(+---)$ its normalization is $u^\mu u_\mu=c^2$ and its momentum is $p^\mu=mu^\mu$. The <relativistic charged-particle action> adds electromagnetic coupling, whose variation gives the <Lorentz force>.

= Nonrelativistic particle
{parent=Special relativity}

A massive particle is nonrelativistic when its speed is small compared with the speed of light. Its energy expansion is $E=mc^2+p^2/(2m)+O(p^4/(m^3c^2))$. The leading momentum-dependent term is the classical <kinetic energy>.

= Hyperbolic motion
{parent=Special relativity}
{title2=$x^2-c^2t^2=c^2t_0^2$}

Hyperbolic motion in <special relativity> is rectilinear motion with constant <proper acceleration>. A representative positive-$x$ <world line> is $x=ct_0\cosh(\tau/t_0)$, $t=t_0\sinh(\tau/t_0)$, with proper acceleration $c/t_0$. This describes a hyperbola in spacetime, not a <hyperbolic Kepler orbit>.

= Proper length
{parent=Special relativity}
{title2=$\ell$}

Proper length is spatial length measured using the <induced metric> on a specified spacelike slice. For a moving extended object, specify the simultaneity convention; local rest lengths need not coincide with a coordinate length.

= Spacetime
{parent=Special relativity}
{wiki}

Spacetime combines spatial position and time into one geometric setting for physical events. In <special relativity> it is modeled by <Minkowski spacetime>; in <general relativity> its geometry is dynamical.

= Spacetime extension
{parent=Spacetime}

A <spacetime extension> is an isometric embedding of a spacetime as a proper open subset of a larger connected spacetime with a nondegenerate Lorentzian metric. The allowed differentiability class must be specified: analytic, smooth and low-regularity extensions are different questions. Divergent curvature can obstruct a regular extension even when an incomplete geodesic has finite affine length.

= Maximal analytic extension
{parent=Spacetime extension}

A <maximal analytic extension> of an analytic <spacetime> is an analytic <spacetime extension> admitting no further proper analytic extension as a regular spacetime of the same dimension. It is different from a <maximal Cauchy development>, which can end at a smooth <Cauchy horizon>. Additional global choices or quotients can affect uniqueness. The simply connected extension of positive-mass <Schwarzschild spacetime> through both horizons is the <Kruskal spacetime>; its <curvature singularities> remain boundaries.

= Extendible spacetime
{parent=Spacetime extension}

= Extendible
{synonym}

An <extendible spacetime> admits a <spacetime extension> in the specified regularity class. A coordinate horizon need not obstruct an extension: the exterior Schwarzschild chart extends through its horizon in regular null coordinates. Maximality refers to extensions of the given spacetime, not to the mere absence of a singularity in one coordinate chart.

= Proper time
{title2=$\tau$}
{parent=Spacetime}
{wiki}

Proper time is the time measured by a clock following a timelike worldline. With metric signature $(-,+,+,+)$ and units $c=1$,
$$
d\tau^2=-ds^2
$$
along that worldline.

= Null vector
{parent=Special relativity}
{wiki}

= Null tangent
{synonym}

A nonzero spacetime vector $k$ is null when its Minkowski norm vanishes, $k^ak_a=0$. Null tangent vectors describe the local directions of light rays.

Special relativity describes inertial frames using Lorentz transformations and Minkowski spacetime.

= Sum of future-pointing null vectors
{parent=Null vector}
{title2=$(A+B)^2=-2ab(1-\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf m)$}

In <Minkowski spacetime> with <metric signature> $(-,+,+,+)$, write two nonzero future-pointing <null vectors> as $A=a(1,\mathbf n)$ and $B=b(1,\mathbf m)$, where $a,b>0$ and $\mathbf n,\mathbf m$ are <unit vectors>. Their <Lorentzian inner product> gives
$$
(A+B)\cdot(A+B)=-2ab(1-\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf m)\leq0.
$$
Thus their sum is a future-pointing <causal vector>. It is a <null vector> exactly when the spatial directions coincide, and a <timelike vector> otherwise. The positivity of the time components is essential: adding past- and future-pointing <null vectors> can instead produce a <spacelike vector>.

= Null condition
{parent=Null vector}
{title2=$g(k,k)=0$}

The null condition for a nonzero vector $k$ in a <Lorentzian metric> is $g(k,k)=0$. Applied to the tangent of a curve it makes the curve a <null curve>. Nullity alone does not imply the <geodesic equation>; an additional zero or tangent-parallel covariant acceleration is required.

= Lorentz factor
{title2=$\gamma$}
{parent=Special relativity}
{c}
{wiki}

For speed $v<c$, the Lorentz factor is
$$
\gamma=\frac1{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}.
$$

= Length contraction
{parent=Lorentz factor}
{title2=$L=L_0/\gamma$}
{wiki}

= Lorentz contraction
{c}
{synonym}

A rod of rest length $L_0$ moving parallel to its length has simultaneous laboratory length $L_0/\gamma$, where $\gamma$ is the <Lorentz factor>. Transverse lengths are unchanged by the corresponding <Lorentz transformation>.

= Time dilation
{parent=Lorentz factor}
{wiki}

If a clock measures proper-time interval $\Delta\tau$ while moving at constant speed $v$ in an inertial frame, that frame measures $\Delta t=\gamma\Delta\tau$.

= Twin paradox
{parent=Time dilation}
{wiki}

The twin paradox compares <proper time> along the different <world lines> of a travelling observer and a stay-at-home observer who reunite. Reciprocal inertial <time dilation> produces an apparent contradiction only if the traveller's change of <inertial frame> is ignored.

= Ultrarelativistic particle
{parent=Special relativity}

= Ultrarelativistic
{synonym}

An ultrarelativistic particle has energy much larger than its rest energy. Its dispersion relation is then approximated by $E=pc$.

= Galilean transformation
{parent=Special relativity}
{wiki}

= Galilean boost
{c}
{synonym}

For inertial frames with relative velocity $u$ along the $x$-axis, the Galilean transformation is
$$
t'=t,\qquad x'=x-ut.
$$

= Principle of Galilean relativity
{c}
{parent=Galilean transformation}

The laws of Newtonian mechanics have the same form in every <inertial frame> related by a <Galilean transformation>.

= Lorentz transformation
{title2=$\Lambda$}
{parent=Special relativity}
{c}
{wiki}

A Lorentz transformation preserves the <Minkowski metric>. A boost of speed $u$ along the $x$-axis uses $\beta=u/c$ and
$$
ct'=\gamma(ct-\beta x),\qquad
x'=\gamma(x-\beta ct).
$$

= Local Lorentz transformation
{parent=Lorentz transformation}
{title2=$e'^A{}_\mu=\Lambda^A{}_B(x)e^B{}_\mu$}

An <orthonormal coframe in spacetime> can be rotated by a position-dependent Lorentz matrix without changing the spacetime <metric tensor>. Its <spin connection> transforms as $\omega'=\Lambda\omega\Lambda^{-1}-d\Lambda\,\Lambda^{-1}$. A <spinor field> transforms through a chosen spin lift $\psi'=S(\Lambda)\psi$, not through the vector matrix $\Lambda$. With $S^{-1}\gamma^A S=\Lambda^A{}_B\gamma^B$, an infinitesimal parameter gives $S=1+\tfrac14\alpha_{AB}\gamma^{AB}+O(\alpha^2)$, where $\gamma^{AB}=\tfrac12[\gamma^A,\gamma^B]$. Consistent global transition lifts require a <spin structure>; a $2\pi$ spatial rotation lifts to minus the identity on spinors.

= Relativity of simultaneity
{parent=Lorentz transformation}
{title2=$\Delta t'=-\gamma v\Delta x/c^2\quad(\Delta t=0)$}
{wiki}

Spatially separated simultaneous events in one <inertial frame> are generally not simultaneous in a relatively moving frame. The <Lorentz transformation> explicitly mixes position into time. This is essential when comparing distances between moving light pulses: one must compare their positions at the same time in the measuring frame.

= Rod containment and simultaneity
{parent=Relativity of simultaneity}

A moving rod can have both ends inside a shorter shed on a single constant-time slice of the shed frame. <Lorentz transformation> sends the two simultaneous endpoint events to different times in the rod frame; it does not imply simultaneous containment there. Entrance and first collision can be spacelike separated and occur in reversed time order. After impact, length changes must propagate through the rod causally; a perfectly rigid instantaneous stopping model is not relativistically admissible.

= Photon separation under a collinear Lorentz boost
{parent=Relativity of simultaneity}
{title2=$d'=d\sqrt{(c+v)/(c-v)}$}

For two <photons> emitted simultaneously a distance $d$ apart in one frame and moving in the same positive direction, the world-line intercept $x-ct$ transforms to $\gamma(1+v/c)(x-ct)$. Their equal-time separation in the boosted frame is therefore the displayed constant $d'$. It is different from length contraction of the emitters, because the emission events are not simultaneous after the boost.

= Lorentz invariance
{c}
{parent=Lorentz covariance}

= Lorentz invariant
{c}
{synonym}

A physical quantity is Lorentz invariant when its value is unchanged under <Lorentz transformations>. Tensor equations can be <Lorentz covariant> while their components transform. Fixed-vector gauge choices need not make covariance manifest, although physical gauge-invariant predictions retain the symmetry.

= Lorentz boost
{c}
{parent=Lorentz transformation}
{title2=$t'=\gamma(t-vx),\quad x'=\gamma(x-vt)$}

A Lorentz boost relates <inertial frames> moving at a constant relative velocity without an additional spatial rotation. In units with light speed one, the displayed transformation preserves $t^2-x^2$, with <Lorentz factor> $\gamma=(1-v^2)^{-1/2}$. Applying it to a static <classical field-theory soliton> produces a uniformly moving solution in a Lorentz-invariant theory.

= Wigner rotation
{c}
{parent=Lorentz boost}

Two noncollinear <Lorentz boosts> produce a <Lorentz transformation> with both a boost and a rotation. In the <SL(2,C)> lift, the product of two positive Hermitian boost <matrices> generally ceases to be Hermitian. Its <polar decomposition of an invertible complex matrix> separates a positive Hermitian boost factor and a unitary rotation factor. The rotation depends on both boosts and their order, and is called the Wigner rotation.

= Noncollinear boost obstruction from Pauli products
{parent=Wigner rotation}
{title2=$A'A-(A'A)^\dagger=2is's(\mathbf n'\times\mathbf n)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$}

For boost <matrices> $A=cI+s\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ and $A'=c'I+s'\mathbf n'\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$, the <Pauli matrix multiplication law> gives the displayed anti-Hermitian part. If both rapidities are nonzero and the axes are noncollinear, it cannot vanish, while either lift of a pure boost is Hermitian. Thus an additional <Wigner rotation> is necessary. A zero boost is a degenerate exception.

= Rapidity
{parent=Lorentz boost}
{title2=$\theta=\operatorname{artanh}v$}
{wiki}

For one spatial dimension and $c=1$, a massive particle has $v=\tanh\theta$, $E=M\cosh\theta$ and $p=M\sinh\theta$. A <Lorentz boost> adds a constant to all rapidities, so a rapidity difference is invariant. Rapidity is useful for <factorized scattering>, where two-body <S-matrices> depend only on that difference.

= Iterated collinear boosts with equal rapidity
{parent=Rapidity}
{title2=$u_n=c\tanh(n\alpha)$}

The <relativistic velocity-addition formula> becomes addition of <rapidities> for collinear <Lorentz boosts>. Starting from rest, applying the same positive rest-frame <velocity> increment $c\tanh\alpha$ repeatedly gives $u_n=c\tanh(n\alpha)$. For increment $c/2$, $\alpha=\tfrac12\log3$ and $u_n=c(3^n-1)/(3^n+1)$. Every finite sequence remains below the <speed of light>.

= Active Lorentz transformation of a vector field
{parent=Lorentz transformation}

An active transformation keeps the coordinate argument fixed and changes a contravariant vector field by $A'^\mu(x)=\Lambda^\mu{}_{\nu}A^\nu(\Lambda^{-1}x)$. Infinitesimally, $\delta A^\mu=\alpha\omega^\mu{}_{\nu}A^\nu-\alpha\omega^\rho{}_{\sigma}x^\sigma\partial_\rho A^\mu$. For a <Lorentz scalar> density, the spin-index term is absent, giving $\delta\mathcal L=-\alpha\omega^\rho{}_{\sigma}x^\sigma\partial_\rho\mathcal L$.

= Lorentz scalar
{c}
{parent=Lorentz transformation}
{wiki}

A Lorentz scalar is unchanged under a <Lorentz transformation>. Contracting every Lorentz index in a tensor expression produces a scalar when the constituent fields transform covariantly.

= Lorentz tensor
{c}
{parent=Lorentz transformation}
{wiki}

A Lorentz tensor transforms by one Lorentz matrix for each contravariant index and one inverse matrix for each covariant index. Complete contraction of all indices produces a Lorentz scalar.

= Lorentz group
{title2=$O(1,3)$}
{c}
{parent=Lorentz transformation}
{wiki}

The Lorentz group consists of real matrices preserving the <Minkowski metric>: $\Lambda^T\eta\Lambda=\eta$.

= Time-orientation-preserving Lorentz group in one spatial dimension
{parent=Lorentz group}
{title2=$O^{\uparrow}(1,1)$}

For $J=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$, the <matrices> satisfying $A^TJA=J$ and $A_{11}>0$ form the time-orientation-preserving <Lorentz group> in one spatial dimension. They are exactly $A=H(t)D_\varepsilon$, where $H(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh t&\sinh t\\\sinh t&\cosh t\end{pmatrix}$, $D_\varepsilon=\operatorname{diag}(1,\varepsilon)$ and $\varepsilon=\pm1$. Preservation of the <Minkowski metric> makes the first column a future-pointing unit timelike <vector>; <Minkowski inner product> orthogonality determines the second up to sign. The identity $D_\varepsilon H(s)=H(\varepsilon s)D_\varepsilon$ gives the multiplication law $(t,\varepsilon)(s,\eta)=(t+\varepsilon s,\varepsilon\eta)$ and inverse $(-\varepsilon t,\varepsilon)$.

= Lie algebra of the Lorentz group
{title2=$\mathfrak o(1,3)$}
{c}
{parent=Lorentz group}

For $M=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,1,1)$,
$$
\mathfrak o(1,3)=\{Y\in M_4(\mathbb R):Y^TM+MY=0\}.
$$
Every such matrix has the block form
$$
Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&v^T\\v&\Omega\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
v\in\mathbb R^3,\quad\Omega^T=-\Omega,
$$
so the Lie algebra and the <Lorentz group> have dimension six. At $A\in O(1,3)$, the tangent space is $A\mathfrak o(1,3)$.

= Orthochronous Lorentz group
{title2=$O^+(1,3)$}
{c}
{parent=Lorentz group}

The orthochronous Lorentz group consists of Lorentz transformations preserving the future sheet of the unit timelike hyperboloid. Equivalently, $A_{00}>0$. This is an open subgroup of $O(1,3)$ and hence a full-dimensional submanifold.

= Proper orthochronous Lorentz group
{c}
{parent=Lorentz group}

The proper orthochronous Lorentz group is the identity component of the <Lorentz group>. Its elements have determinant one and preserve the future light cone.

= Every proper orthochronous Lorentz transformation is an exponential
{parent=Proper orthochronous Lorentz group}
{title2=$L=\exp\Omega$}

Use the <Lorentz spinor double cover> $SL(2,\mathbb C)\to SO^+(1,3)$. For a lift with distinct eigenvalues $\lambda,\lambda^{-1}$, diagonalize and choose logarithms $z,-z$; this gives a traceless matrix logarithm. For a repeated eigenvalue, choose the sign of the lift so it is $I+N$, where $N^2=0$, and use $\log(I+N)=N$. The two lifts have the same Lorentz image. Exponentiating the differential of the covering map therefore expresses every proper orthochronous transformation as the exponential of a real element of the <Lie algebra of the Lorentz group>. This argument does not assert that every element of $SL(2,\mathbb C)$ itself has a traceless logarithm.

= Lorentz spinor double cover
{c}
{parent=Proper orthochronous Lorentz group}
{title2=$SL(2,\mathbb C)\longrightarrow SO^+(1,3)$}

Identify Minkowski vectors with Hermitian matrices $X=x^0I+x^a\sigma_a$. The action $X\mapsto SXS^\dagger$ preserves $\det X=(x^0)^2-|\mathbf x|^2$. It gives a surjective homomorphism from $SL(2,\mathbb C)$ to the <proper orthochronous Lorentz group> with kernel $\{I,-I\}$. Polar decomposition retracts the covering group onto <SU(2)>, making it simply connected. This cover permits half-integer spin <group representations> that do not descend to the Lorentz group itself.

= Hermitian matrix representation of Minkowski four-vectors
{c}
{parent=Lorentz spinor double cover}
{title2=$X=x_\mu\sigma^\mu$}

A real four-vector corresponds to a two-by-two Hermitian matrix with determinant equal to its Minkowski squared norm. The action $X\mapsto NXN^\dagger$ for $N\in SL(2,\mathbb C)$ preserves that determinant and the future cone. Expanding with $\tfrac12\operatorname{Tr}(\bar\sigma^\mu\sigma_\nu)=\delta^\mu{}_{\nu}$ yields $\Lambda^\mu{}_{\nu}(N)=\tfrac12\operatorname{Tr}(\bar\sigma^\mu N\sigma_\nu N^\dagger)$. The map has kernel $\{\pm I\}$ and image $SO^+(1,3)$.

= Trace form on scalar-plus-skew-Hermitian two-by-two matrices
{parent=Hermitian matrix representation of Minkowski four-vectors}
{title2=$C=aI+i\mathbf v\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma,\quad-\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(CD)=-ab+\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf w$}

The real matrix space defined by $C+C^*=\operatorname{tr}(C)I$ consists exactly of $C=aI+i\mathbf v\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$, with $a$ and the three components of $\mathbf v$ real. The <Pauli matrices> have $\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_i\sigma_j)=2\delta_{ij}$, giving the displayed real symmetric <bilinear form> of signature $(3,1)$. The real isomorphism $C\mapsto aI-\mathbf v\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ to <Hermitian matrices> preserves this form when the latter have the polarized negative-determinant form $[\operatorname{tr}(AB)-\operatorname{tr}A\operatorname{tr}B]/2$.

= Poincaré group
{c}
{parent=Lorentz group}
{wiki}

The Poincare group is the <semidirect product> of spacetime translations by the <Lorentz group>. It is the isometry group of <Minkowski spacetime>.

= Poincare algebra
{c}
{parent=Poincaré group}

The Poincare algebra is generated by translations $P^\mu$ and Lorentz transformations $M^{\mu\nu}$. Its mixed commutator is $[M^{\mu\nu},P^\rho]=i(\eta^{\nu\rho}P^\mu-\eta^{\mu\rho}P^\nu)$.

= Pauli-Lubanski pseudovector
{title2=$W_\mu$}
{c}
{parent=Poincare algebra}
{wiki}

The Pauli-Lubanski pseudovector is $W_\mu=\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}M^{\nu\rho}P^\sigma/2$, up to an overall sign convention. Its square is a Casimir of the Poincare algebra and measures spin in a massive irreducible representation.

= Pauli-Lubanski orthogonality
{parent=Pauli-Lubanski pseudovector}
{c}
{title2=$W^\mu P_\mu=0$}

In $W^\mu=-\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}P_\nu M_{\rho\sigma}/2$, contracting with $P_\mu$ gives zero because the commuting momentum products are symmetric in $\mu,\nu$ while the alternating tensor is antisymmetric. Reordering $P$ and $M$ makes no difference because the resulting metric contractions with the alternating tensor vanish.

= Massive Pauli-Lubanski Casimir
{parent=Pauli-Lubanski pseudovector}
{title2=$W^2=-m^2s(s+1)$}

For a massive positive-energy <unitary irreducible representation> of the <Poincare group> with signature $(+---)$, choose a rest frame. The <Pauli-Lubanski pseudovector> has zero time component and spatial components $mJ_i$, up to a common sign convention. Its square is therefore $-m^2\mathbf J^2$. The <SU(2)> little-group representation has <spin> $s$ and $\mathbf J^2=s(s+1)$, proving the invariant value. The mass and spin label the <massive induced representation of the Poincare double cover>.

= Massless longitudinal Pauli-Lubanski eigenvalues
{parent=Pauli-Lubanski pseudovector}
{title2=$(W_0,W_3)=(kj_3,-kj_3)$}

With signature $(+---)$ and $\epsilon_{0123}=+1$, a momentum $(k,0,0,k)$ and <helicity> $j_3$ give lower components $W_0=kj_3$ and $W_3=-kj_3$. They obey $W_\mu P^\mu=k(W_0+W_3)=0$. These longitudinal identities require only the momentum and rotation-projection eigenvalues, not a statement about transverse little-group translations.

= Massive rest-frame Pauli-Lubanski eigenvalues
{parent=Pauli-Lubanski pseudovector}
{title2=$(W_0,W_3)=(0,-mj_3)$}

With signature $(+---)$, $\epsilon_{0123}=+1$, $J_3=M^{12}$ and the lower-index definition of $W_\mu$, a rest momentum $(m,0,0,0)$ gives $W_0=0$ and $W_3=-mJ_3$ on that momentum eigenspace. A Hermitian spin projection $j_3$ consequently gives eigenvalues $0$ and $-mj_3$. Raising the third index changes its sign.

= Little group
{parent=Poincaré group}

The little group of a momentum $p$ is its stabilizer in the Lorentz group. Its <unitary irreducible representation>[unitary irreducible representations] label the internal degrees of freedom of one-particle states on the orbit of $p$.

These stabilizer representations are used in <Wigner's classification> of relativistic particle states.

= Particle polarization
{parent=Little group}
{title2=$\epsilon$}

A particle polarization specifies a physical spin state at fixed <four-momentum>. Massive spin states transform under the rotation <little group> $SO(D-1)$; finite-spin massless states transform under its transverse rotation part $SO(D-2)$. A <massive two-form field> has $\binom{D-1}{2}$ such states, while a <two-form gauge field> has $\binom{D-2}{2}$. Polarization components must be counted after field equations and any gauge equivalence are imposed.

= Massless particle little group
{parent=Little group}
{title2=$ISO(D-2)$}

The stabilizer of a nonzero null <four-momentum> has connected group $ISO(D-2)$. For ordinary finite-polarization particles its translation part acts trivially, leaving a representation of $SO(D-2)$.

= Massless induced representation of the Poincare double cover
{parent=Massless particle little group}

For a future null standard momentum $k$, choose <Lorentz transformations> $L(p)k=p$ and a <unitary irreducible representation> $d$ of its <little group>. The <Wigner rotation> $R(\Lambda,p)=L(\Lambda p)^{-1}\Lambda L(p)$ acts on the internal state. The resulting <induced representation> acts on wavefunctions with invariant measure $d^3p/(2p^0)$. Trivial little-group translations give fixed <helicity>; nontrivial translations give a <continuous-spin representation> with infinitely many polarization states.

= Finite-dimensional unitary representations of the massless little group have trivial translations
{parent=Massless particle little group}
{title2=$N_1=N_2=0$}

In a finite-dimensional unitary representation of $ISO(2)$, its two commuting Hermitian translation generators have a finite joint spectrum. Conjugating by every planar rotation rotates that spectrum. A finite set invariant under all rotations contains only the origin, so both generators vanish. An irreducible representation is then a one-dimensional rotation character, giving a <helicity> state. Nontrivial translations instead require the infinite-dimensional <continuous-spin representation>. The same argument applies to the physical double cover of this little group.

= Massless little-group generators
{parent=Massless particle little group}
{title2=$N_1=K_1-J_2,\quad N_2=K_2+J_1$}

For a standard null momentum $(E,0,0,E)$ in signature $(+---)$, define $K_i=M^{0i}$ and $J_i=\epsilon_{ijk}M^{jk}/2$. The stabilizing generators are $J_3,N_1,N_2$. The <Poincare algebra> gives $[J_3,N_1]=iN_2$, $[J_3,N_2]=-iN_1$ and $[N_1,N_2]=0$, the Lie algebra of planar Euclidean motions. Evaluating the <Pauli-Lubanski pseudovector> gives $W^2=-E^2(N_1^2+N_2^2)$.

= Massless vector particle
{parent=Massless particle little group}
{title2=$p\cdot\epsilon=0,\quad\epsilon\sim\epsilon+\lambda p$}

= Massless vector
{synonym}

An ordinary massless spin-one particle has transverse vector polarizations under $SO(D-2)$. A covariant polarization satisfies $p\cdot\epsilon=0$ and is identified under $\epsilon\mapsto\epsilon+\lambda p$. The quotient has $D-2$ positive polarizations.

= Massive particle little group
{parent=Little group}
{title2=$SO(D-1)$}

The stabilizer of a timelike <four-momentum> in $D$-dimensional <Minkowski spacetime> has connected group $SO(D-1)$. Massive particle polarizations form representations of this group.

= Massive vector particle
{parent=Massive particle little group}
{title2=$D-1\text{ polarizations}$}

= Massive vector
{synonym}

A massive spin-one particle transforms as a vector under its <massive particle little group> $SO(D-1)$. Its $D-1$ polarizations are orthogonal to its timelike <four-momentum>. Under transverse $SO(D-2)$ rotations they split into a vector and a scalar.

= Little group with mixed string boundary conditions
{parent=Little group}

The symmetry used to classify an <open string> must preserve its endpoint boundary background as well as its <momentum>. A <Neumann-Dirichlet open-string boundary condition> breaks target Lorentz transformations mixing ND and common directions with <Neumann boundary conditions>. For a D1–D25 pair with 24 ND directions, the common worldvolume has Lorentz group $SO(1,1)$, whose connected massive <little group> is trivial. The transverse $SO(24)$ remains as an internal rotation group. Thus a massive 24-component oscillator multiplet is not an $SO(25)$ vector of the full 26-dimensional massive little group; a vector of that full group would have 25 components.

= Vector-particle polarization
{parent=Little group}
{title2=$\epsilon_m$}

A massive vector in $D$ spacetime dimensions has $D-1$ polarizations perpendicular to its timelike <momentum>. A massless vector has transverse polarizations modulo its null <longitudinal polarization>, leaving $D-2$ physical <degrees of freedom>.

= Helicity
{title2=$\lambda$}
{parent=Little group}
{wiki}

Helicity is the projection of angular momentum onto the momentum direction. For a finite-helicity massless particle, the Pauli-Lubanski vector satisfies $W^\mu=\lambda P^\mu$.

= Helicity decomposition of a transverse Fourier mode
{parent=Helicity}
{title2=$m^{(\pm)}=(\hat x\pm i\hat y)/\sqrt2$}

For $\mathbf k=k\hat z$, these complex vectors form an orthonormal basis of the transverse plane using the Hermitian <inner product>. They have opposite phases under rotations about $\hat z$, so they describe the two <helicity> components. Since $\mathbf e\cdot m^{(\pm)}=\sin\theta e^{\pm i\varphi}/\sqrt2$, transverse-vector sources carry only the angular sectors $m=\pm1$ of <spherical harmonics>. Streaming by $ik\cos\theta$ preserves $m$ and couples neighboring angular degrees.

= Continuous-spin representation
{parent=Little group}
{wiki}

A continuous-spin representation is a massless unitary irreducible representation of the Poincare group in which the translation subgroup of the $ISO(2)$ little group acts nontrivially. It contains infinitely many helicities.

= Wigner's classification
{c}
{parent=Little group}
{wiki}

= Wigner classification
{c}
{synonym}

Wigner's classification obtains irreducible positive-energy representations of the <Poincare group> by inducing from <unitary irreducible representation>[unitary irreducible representations] of the <little group> of a standard momentum.

= Massive induced representation of the Poincare double cover
{parent=Wigner's classification}
{title2=$U(\Lambda,a)|p,s,\sigma\rangle=e^{i(\Lambda p)\cdot a}\sum_\tau D^{(s)}_{\tau\sigma}(W(\Lambda,p))|\Lambda p,s,\tau\rangle$}

Fix $m>0$, $k=(m,\mathbf0)$, and a rotationless <Lorentz boost> $L(p)$ taking $k$ to $p$ on the positive-energy <mass shell>. The <little group> on the <Lorentz spinor double cover> is <SU(2)>. Its spin-$s$ irreducible representation induces a unitary representation on $L^2(d^3p/(2p^0))\otimes\mathbb C^{2s+1}$. Here $W(\Lambda,p)=L(\Lambda p)^{-1}\Lambda L(p)$ is the <Wigner rotation>, lifted to <SU(2)> when necessary, and the translation convention is $U(I,a)|p\rangle=e^{ip\cdot a}|p\rangle$. The little-group cocycle proves the group law, and <Wigner's classification> gives irreducibility. Every nonnegative integer or half-integer spin is allowed on the double cover; descent to the ordinary proper orthochronous <Poincare group> requires integer spin, because its $2\pi$ rotation must act as the identity.

= One-dimensional Lorentz group
{title2=$SO(1,1)$}
{c}
{parent=Lorentz group}

The determinant-one Lorentz group in one space and one time dimension has two connected components. Its identity component consists of boosts
$$
\begin{pmatrix}\cosh\varphi&\sinh\varphi\\\sinh\varphi&\cosh\varphi\end{pmatrix},
$$
and the other component is its negative.

= Null coordinates in two-dimensional Minkowski spacetime
{title2=$u=x+y,\ v=x-y$}
{parent=One-dimensional Lorentz group}

A boost of rapidity $\varphi$ acts on null coordinates by $u\mapsto e^\varphi u$ and $v\mapsto e^{-\varphi}v$, preserving $uv=x^2-y^2$.

= Retarded and advanced null coordinates
{title2=$u=t-z,\ v=t+z$}
{parent=Null coordinates in two-dimensional Minkowski spacetime}

In the $(t,z)$ plane, $u=t-z$ is retarded time and $v=t+z$ is advanced time. The radial part of the <Minkowski metric> is $-dt^2+dz^2=-du\,dv$.

= Minkowski wave operator in null coordinates
{title2=$\Box=-4\partial_u\partial_v+\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2$}
{parent=Retarded and advanced null coordinates}

For $u=t-z$ and $v=t+z$, the four-dimensional Minkowski wave operator is
$$
\Box=-4\partial_u\partial_v+\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2.
$$
Every profile depending only on one null coordinate solves the wave equation when it is uniform in the transverse coordinates.

= Nonrelativistic limit
{parent=Lorentz transformation}

The nonrelativistic limit takes characteristic speeds much smaller than $c$. Lorentz transformations then reduce to Galilean transformations at leading order.

= Velocity-addition formula
{parent=Lorentz transformation}
{wiki}

= Relativistic velocity-addition formula
{synonym}

= Relativistic velocity addition
{synonym}

For a boost of speed $V$ along $x$,
$$
u'_x=\frac{u_x-V}{1-Vu_x/c^2},
\qquad
u'_y=\frac{u_y}{\gamma_V(1-Vu_x/c^2)}.
$$

= Four-vector
{parent=Special relativity}
{wiki}

= Lorentz four-vector
{c}
{synonym}

A four-vector transforms as $U'^\mu=\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu U^\nu$ under every Lorentz transformation.

= Position four-vector
{parent=Four-vector}
{title2=$X^a=(ct,\mathbf x)$}

The spacetime coordinates of an event combine into a <four-vector> $X^a=(ct,x,y,z)$ under Lorentz transformations between inertial frames sharing an origin. For a massive particle, differentiation with respect to <proper time> gives its <four-velocity>. Under a spacetime translation, positions acquire an additive offset, while displacement four-vectors transform linearly under the Lorentz part.

= Future-pointing timelike four-vector
{parent=Four-vector}
{title2=$P^0>|\mathbf P|$}

In <metric signature> $(+---)$, a future-pointing timelike <four-vector> has positive time component larger than its spatial magnitude. Two such vectors have strictly positive <Minkowski metric> scalar product, since $P\cdot Q=P^0Q^0-\mathbf P\cdot\mathbf Q\geq P^0Q^0-|\mathbf P||\mathbf Q|>0$. In the opposite signature the scalar-product sign reverses, while the causal condition is the same. The future timelike cone is closed under addition, which rules out a null <photon> momentum equalling a sum of two massive future <four-momenta>.

= Four-divergence
{parent=Four-vector}
{title2=$\partial_\mu V^\mu$}

The four-divergence of a <four-vector> field is the <Lorentz scalar> contraction $\partial_\mu V^\mu$. With $x^\mu=(ct,\boldsymbol x)$, it equals $c^{-1}\partial_t V^0+\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol V$. The <four-current> has zero four-divergence, expressing local charge conservation.

= Causality
{parent=Special relativity}
{wiki=Causality_(physics)}

Causality requires an effect at an event to depend only on sources in its past light cone. Relativistic field equations implement this choice through retarded Green functions.

= Minkowski spacetime
{c}
{parent=Special relativity}
{wiki}

Minkowski spacetime is the flat spacetime of special relativity.

= Time-dependent lapse can disguise flat spacetime
{parent=Minkowski spacetime}
{title2=$ds^2=-N(t)^2dt^2+dx^2,\quad T=\int N(t)\,dt$}

For positive lapse $N$, this time reparametrization gives a local <Minkowski metric>. If the new time coordinate ranges over the entire real line, it is a global isometry to Minkowski space. In particular $N=\cosh t$ gives $T=\sinh t$ and complete nonperiodic timelike geodesics. A lapse depending on the spatial coordinate instead can describe a different curved geometry; exchanging the arguments is not a harmless relabeling of the same metric.

= Complexified Minkowski spacetime
{parent=Minkowski spacetime}
{title2=$M_{\mathbb C}\cong\mathbb C^4$}

= Complexified Minkowski space
{synonym}

Allow the four affine Minkowski coordinates to take independent complex values, obtaining $M_{\mathbb C}\simeq\mathbb C^4$ with the holomorphic extension of the flat conformal metric. In two-spinor coordinates $x^{AA'}$, the metric is proportional to $\epsilon_{AB}\epsilon_{A'B'}dx^{AA'}dx^{BB'}$. The <twistor incidence relation> associates a <complex projective line> to each affine point; compactification adds the points at conformal infinity.

= Rindler wedge
{c}
{parent=Minkowski spacetime}
{title2=$X>|T|$}

= Rindler spacetime
{c}
{synonym}

The right <Rindler wedge> is the open region $X>|T|$ of Minkowski spacetime. Coordinates $T=\rho\sinh\tau$, $X=\rho\cosh\tau$, $\rho>0$, give metric $-\rho^2d\tau^2+d\rho^2+dY^2+dZ^2$. The wedge extends into Minkowski spacetime and is geodesically incomplete, while its constant-$\tau$ slices are Cauchy surfaces for the wedge itself.

= Rindler coordinates
{c}
{parent=Minkowski spacetime}
{title2=$T=\rho\sinh(\kappa t),\quad X=\rho\cosh(\kappa t)$}
{wiki}

For $\rho>0$, these coordinates cover a wedge of <Minkowski spacetime> and give $ds^2=-\kappa^2\rho^2dt^2+d\rho^2$ in two dimensions. A constant-$\rho$ observer has proper acceleration $1/\rho$ and the <Unruh effect> temperature $1/(2\pi\rho)$ in natural units. The apparent degeneration at $\rho=0$ is a coordinate horizon.

= Minkowski metric
{c}
{parent=Minkowski spacetime}
{wiki}

With the mostly-plus convention, the Minkowski metric is $\eta_{\mu\nu}=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,1,1)$.

= Minkowski interval
{c}
{parent=Minkowski metric}
{title2=$s^2=\eta_{\mu\nu}\Delta x^\mu\Delta x^\nu$}

The squared interval between two events in <Minkowski spacetime> is the quadratic form of their coordinate difference under the <Minkowski metric>. In signature $(-,+,+,+)$ it is $-c^2\Delta t^2+|\Delta\mathbf x|^2$. <Lorentz transformations> preserve this quadratic form; timelike, spacelike and null separation correspond to negative, positive and zero values.

= Light-cone coordinates
{parent=Minkowski metric}
{title2=$x^\pm$}

For mostly-plus signature, $x^+=(x^{D-1}+x^0)/\sqrt2$ and $x^-=(x^{D-1}-x^0)/\sqrt2$ give $ds^2=2dx^+dx^-+dx^Idx^I$. In this convention raising a plus or minus derivative exchanges the two labels.

= Minkowski norm
{title2=$U\cdot U$}
{c}
{parent=Minkowski metric}

The Minkowski norm is the Lorentz-invariant quadratic form $U\cdot U=\eta_{\mu\nu}U^\mu U^\nu$.

This quadratic form belongs to the geometry of <Minkowski spacetime>; the spacetime itself is a broader concept than this squared norm.

= Light cone
{parent=Minkowski metric}
{wiki}

The light cone through an event consists of null displacement vectors. In one spatial dimension its two directions satisfy $x=\pm ct$.

= Past light cone
{parent=Light cone}

The past light cone of an event consists of earlier events connected to it by past-directed null curves. A retarded field at the event depends on sources where their worldlines intersect this cone.

= Speed of light
{title2=$c$}
{parent=Light cone}
{wiki}

The speed of light $c$ is the invariant speed of every inertial frame and the limiting speed of causal propagation in special relativity.

= Null directions
{parent=Light cone}

Null directions are tangent directions of the <light cone>; their Minkowski squared norm is zero.

A direction represented by a nonzero <null vector> has zero <Minkowski norm>. Such directions form the generators of the <light cone>.

= Null curve
{parent=Minkowski metric}

A null curve has tangent $u$ satisfying $g(u,u)=0$. It represents the possible spacetime path of a light signal.

Its nonzero tangent is a <null vector> at every regular point; this distinguishes the curve from a single null direction.

= Null geodesic
{parent=Null curve}

A null geodesic is both a <null curve> and a <geodesic>. In geometric optics, light rays follow null geodesics.

= Radial null geodesic
{parent=Null geodesic}

A <radial null geodesic> in a spherically symmetric <spacetime> has zero <angular momentum> and fixed angular coordinates. Its projection is a null curve of the two-dimensional radial <Lorentzian metric>.

= World line
{parent=Special relativity}
{wiki}

= Worldline
{synonym}

A world line is the path of an object through spacetime. A massive observer follows a <timelike curve>.

= Timelike curve
{parent=World line}

A timelike curve has an everywhere timelike tangent vector and can represent the <world line> of a massive observer.

= Subluminal speed
{parent=Special relativity}

A subluminal speed has magnitude strictly below the speed of light: $|v|<c$. Massive particles follow timelike worldlines and therefore move subluminally in every inertial frame.

= Impossibility of photon decay into two massive particles
{parent=Special relativity}

The sum of two future-pointing timelike four-momenta is timelike. It therefore cannot equal a photon's null four-momentum, which forbids spontaneous photon decay into a massive particle pair in vacuum.

= Four-momentum
{parent=Special relativity}
{wiki}

= Four-momenta
{synonym}

A massive particle has four-momentum
$$
P^\mu=(E/c,\mathbf p)=m\gamma(c,\mathbf v),
\qquad P^2=m^2c^2.
$$
For a massless particle, $P^\mu=(E/c,\mathbf p)$ with $E=c|\mathbf p|$ and $P^2=0$.

= Physical momentum
{parent=Four-momentum}
{title2=$p_\perp^\mu=(\delta^\mu{}_{\nu}+u^\mu u_\nu)p^\nu$}

An observer with future-directed unit velocity $u^\mu$ measures particle energy $E=-u_\mu p^\mu$ and spatial <physical momentum> given by the orthogonal projection of its <four-momentum> in the displayed formula. With signature $(-+++)$ and $p_\mu p^\mu=-m^2$, its squared magnitude is $p_{\perp\mu}p_\perp^\mu=E^2-m^2$. Components in the observer's orthonormal spatial frame agree with $\gamma m\mathbf v$. They differ in general from coordinate <canonical momentum> components. For a comoving observer in flat FRW, a conserved conventional spatial comoving covector has locally measured magnitude divided by the scale factor.

= Center-of-mass energy
{parent=Four-momentum}
{title2=$E_{\rm com}^2=-P_\mu P^\mu$}

= Centre-of-mass energy
{synonym}

For future-directed particles with total <four-momentum> $P$ and <metric signature> $(-+++)$, the positive square root of $-P_\mu P^\mu$ is their energy in the frame where total spatial momentum vanishes. Its invariant definition remains valid at a collision in <spacetime>.

= Mass shell
{parent=Four-momentum}
{title2=$p^2=\pm m^2$}

The <mass shell> is the locus of <four-momenta> satisfying the relativistic dispersion relation for a fixed <mass> $m$. With mostly-minus <Minkowski metric>, $p^2=m^2$; with mostly-plus <Minkowski metric>, $p^2=-m^2$. The positive-energy sheet represents particle <four-momenta>. For $m=0$ this is the light cone. The condition of being <on shell> refers more generally to satisfying the field equations.

= Four-momentum conservation
{parent=Four-momentum}

In an isolated relativistic interaction, the sum of incoming four-momenta equals the sum of outgoing four-momenta.

= Relativistic collision mass threshold
{parent=Four-momentum conservation}
{title2=$W\geq c^2\sum_i\mu_i$}

If the total incoming <four-momentum> is timelike and has <centre-of-momentum energy> $W$, production of particles with <rest masses> $\mu_i$ requires $W\geq\sum_i\mu_ic^2$. In the <centre-of-momentum frame>, total final energy is a sum of $\sqrt{\mu_i^2c^4+c^2|\mathbf p_i|^2}$, each at least its rest energy. For massive products equality occurs with all at rest in that frame. A single product requires equality of the entire invariant mass, not just an inequality; a massless product cannot have nonzero energy and be at rest.

= Fixed-target production threshold
{parent=Relativistic collision mass threshold}
{title2=$E_1\geq\frac{(\sum_i\mu_i)^2-m_1^2-m_2^2}{2m_2}c^2$}

For an incident particle of <rest mass> $m_1$ and total energy $E_1$ hitting a stationary target of mass $m_2>0$, <four-momentum conservation> gives $W^2=m_1^2c^4+m_2^2c^4+2m_2c^2E_1$. Applying the <relativistic collision mass threshold> gives the displayed necessary condition. The incoming energy must also satisfy $E_1\geq m_1c^2$; other conserved quantum numbers or reaction dynamics can impose further restrictions.

= Photon fixed-target production threshold
{parent=Fixed-target production threshold}
{title2=$h\nu_{\rm thr}=\frac{(\sum_i\mu_i)^2-m_2^2}{2m_2}c^2$}

For a <photon> of energy $h\nu$ hitting a stationary target of mass $m_2$, the incoming invariant energy satisfies $W^2=m_2^2c^4+2m_2c^2h\nu$. The massive multi-product threshold is the displayed value when $\sum_i\mu_i>m_2$. If the algebraic bound is nonpositive, the lower limit of allowed positive frequencies can be zero. A single final product instead fixes the frequency by invariant-mass equality, and a final massless particle can make a nominal threshold an infimum rather than an attained value.

= Photon-induced triplet pair-production threshold
{parent=Photon fixed-target production threshold}
{title2=$E_{\gamma,\mathrm{thr}}=4m_ec^2$}

For a <photon> incident on a stationary <electron>, producing an additional electron and positron leaves three massive particles in the final state. <Four-momentum conservation> gives $W^2=m_e^2c^4+2m_ec^2E_\gamma$ for the <centre-of-momentum energy>. Its minimum is $3m_ec^2$, so the incident threshold is $4m_ec^2$. At threshold all final particles are at rest in their centre-of-momentum frame, which moves at $4c/5$ relative to the target laboratory frame. This differs from a heavy-target threshold because the electron target's recoil cannot be neglected.

= Photon excitation threshold with a soft final photon
{parent=Photon fixed-target production threshold}
{title2=$E_{\rm th}=(M^2-m^2)c^2/(2m)$}

A photon hitting a resting mass $m$ has squared total energy invariant $m^2c^4+2mc^2E$. Producing a larger mass $M$ and an outgoing photon requires this invariant to exceed $M^2c^4$ if the photon has positive energy. The displayed threshold is therefore the limiting infimum as the final photon becomes soft, not an attained physical state with a zero-energy photon. Above threshold the outgoing photon energy in the centre-of-momentum frame is positive.

= Fixed-target threshold for production of equal-mass particles
{parent=Four-momentum conservation}
{title2=$E_{\min}=\frac{N^2-2}{2}mc^2$}

An incident particle of <rest mass> $m$ and energy $E$ striking a stationary identical target has total squared invariant energy $W^2=2mc^2(E+mc^2)$. Producing $N\geq2$ final particles of the same rest mass requires $W\geq Nmc^2$, giving the threshold. Equality means no final kinetic energy in the <centre-of-momentum frame>. From the <Lorentz factor>, the corresponding incident speed is at least $cN\sqrt{N^2-4}/(N^2-2)$.

= Angular relation for two-photon electron-positron annihilation
{parent=Four-momentum conservation}
{title2=$\frac{1+\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2)}{\cos\theta_1+\cos\theta_2}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1}}$}

When an electron of Lorentz factor $\gamma>1$ annihilates with a stationary equal-mass positron, transverse <four-momentum conservation> makes the <photons>' transverse components opposite. Eliminating their positive frequencies gives the displayed relation for noncollinear <photon> angles to the incoming electron. The exactly collinear forward/backward configuration is physically allowed but makes the quotient $0/0$; its undivided cross-multiplied identity remains valid. The angle relation thus requires nondegeneracy or an appropriate limit, rather than an assertion that every allowed angle pair defines the quotient.

= Rest mass of a coalescing relativistic collision
{parent=Four-momentum conservation}
{title2=$m_3^2=m_1^2+m_2^2+2m_1m_2\gamma_1\gamma_2(1-u_1u_2/c^2)$}

When two massive particles coalesce without additional outgoing radiation, <four-momentum conservation> makes the final <four-momentum> their sum. For collinear incident <velocities>, its <rest mass> is the positive square root of the displayed invariant, and its final <velocity> equals the <center-of-momentum frame> velocity $c^2p_{\rm total}/E_{\rm total}$. Relative <kinetic energy> can become internal <rest energy>, so the final <rest mass> is generally larger than the sum of the incident <rest masses>.

= Rest energy
{title2=$E_0=mc^2$}
{parent=Four-momentum}
{wiki}

The rest energy of a particle of invariant mass $m$ is $mc^2$, the energy measured in its rest frame.

= Center-of-momentum frame

{wiki}

= Centre-of-momentum frame
{synonym}

The center-of-momentum frame is an inertial frame in which the total spatial momentum of a system vanishes. For a two-particle initial state, the momenta are equal and opposite and the total invariant energy is $\sqrt s$.

= Centre-of-momentum energy
{title2=$\sqrt s$}
{parent=Center-of-momentum frame}

The centre-of-momentum energy is the total energy measured in the <centre-of-momentum frame>. For a scattering process with total four-momentum $P$, it is the Lorentz-invariant quantity $\sqrt{P^2}=\sqrt s$ in the mostly-minus convention.

= Energy–momentum relation

= Relativistic energy-momentum relation
{synonym}

For a particle of rest mass $m$,
$$
\mathcal E^2=m^2c^4+c^2|\mathbf p|^2.
$$

= Invariant mass
{title2=$m$}
{parent=Energy–momentum relation}
{wiki}

= Rest mass
{synonym}

Rest mass is the invariant mass $m$ in the <relativistic energy-momentum relation>. It equals the energy measured in the particle's rest frame divided by $c^2$.

= Conservation of relativistic energy
{parent=Energy–momentum relation}

In an isolated relativistic collision, total four-momentum is conserved; its time component states conservation of total relativistic energy.

This is the relativistic form of <conservation of energy>, expressed through the conserved <four-momentum>.

= Relativistic motion in a constant electric field with transverse momentum
{parent=Energy–momentum relation}

For $\mathbf E=(0,0,E)$, $\mathbf B=0$, and initial momentum $(p_0,0,0)$,
$$
p_x=p_0,\qquad p_z=qEt,\qquad
\mathcal E(t)=\sqrt{\mathcal E_0^2+c^2q^2E^2t^2}.
$$
With the initial <position> at the origin,
$$
z(t)=\frac{\mathcal E(t)-\mathcal E_0}{qE},
\qquad
x(t)=\frac{cp_0}{qE}
\operatorname{arsinh}\frac{cqEt}{\mathcal E_0}.
$$

= Four-force
{parent=Four-momentum}
{title2=$K^\mu=dp^\mu/d\tau$}
{wiki}

The <four-force> is the <proper time> derivative of <four-momentum>. For constant rest mass and coordinate three-force $\mathbf F=d\mathbf p/dt$, its components are $K^\mu=\gamma(\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v/c,\mathbf F)$ in the convention $p^\mu=(E/c,\mathbf p)$. Thus it packages the power transfer and three-force into one Lorentz-covariant vector.

= Relativistic force
{title2=$F=dp/dt$}
{parent=Four-momentum}

For three-momentum $p=\gamma mv$, force and acceleration satisfy
$$
F=m\gamma\left(a+\frac{\gamma^2}{c^2}(v\mathbin{\cdot}a)v\right),
\qquad
a=\frac1{m\gamma}\left(F-\frac{F\mathbin{\cdot}v}{c^2}v\right).
$$

The associated <four-force> uses <proper time> instead of coordinate time and is a different, four-dimensional object.

= Relativistic motion under a constant force from rest
{parent=Relativistic force}

Under a constant force $F$ with $p(0)=0$, momentum is $p=Ft$ and
$$
v(t)=\frac{Ft/m}{\sqrt{1+|F|^2t^2/(m^2c^2)}}.
$$
The speed approaches $c$ without reaching it at finite time.

= Relativistic two-body decay
{parent=Four-momentum}
{wiki}

For a rest-frame decay $1\to2+3$,
$$
E_2=\frac{m_1^2+m_2^2-m_3^2}{2m_1}c^2,
\qquad
E_3=\frac{m_1^2+m_3^2-m_2^2}{2m_1}c^2.
$$
The decay is allowed exactly when $m_1\geq m_2+m_3$.

= Mass threshold after photon absorption
{parent=Relativistic two-body decay}
{title2=$M^2=m^2+2mE/c^2$}

A stationary particle of <rest mass> $m$ absorbing a <photon> of <energy> $E$ acquires total <invariant mass>
$$
M=\sqrt{m^2+2mE/c^2}.
$$
This follows from <four-momentum conservation>, since the incoming <photon> has <momentum> magnitude $E/c$. If the system splits into two equal particles, the largest kinematically permitted daughter <rest mass> is $M/2$, attained when both are at rest in the <centre-of-momentum frame>. Their common laboratory <speed> at threshold is $cE/(mc^2+E)$. Above this daughter-mass threshold there is insufficient <centre-of-momentum energy>; below it, relative <kinetic energy> is available.

= Källén function
{c}
{parent=Relativistic two-body decay}
{title2=$\kappa(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2-2xy-2xz-2yz$}

The Källén function controls two-body relativistic kinematics. In the rest frame of total invariant mass $\sqrt s$, the magnitude of each final momentum is $\sqrt{\kappa(s,m_1^2,m_2^2)}/(2\sqrt s)$. It also determines the <invariant flux factor> in a two-particle collision.

= Mandelstam variables
{parent=Four-momentum}

= Mandelstam invariant
{c}

For two-to-two scattering $A+B\to C+D$,

s=(P_A+P_B)^2,\qquad

= Scattering t-channel

{title2=$t=(p_1-p_3)^2$}

= t-channel
{c}
{synonym}

With a specified ordering of external legs, this crossed channel carries the difference between an incoming and an outgoing energy-momentum vector. A one-particle exchange <pole> requires that difference to be on the exchanged particle's <mass shell>. Ordered amplitudes in one spatial dimension can label the crossed invariant using the difference of the two rapidity-parameterized external momenta; the external-leg convention fixes which crossed invariant is named $t$.

= Scattering s-channel

= s-channel

The direct channel combines the two incoming <four-momentum> vectors. A one-particle <pole> in this channel has the intermediate particle's squared <mass> equal to $s$. Imaginary <rapidity> differences can satisfy this <mass> relation for a <bound state> even though there is no <pole> at real physical scattering <rapidity>.

= Proper acceleration for one-dimensional motion
