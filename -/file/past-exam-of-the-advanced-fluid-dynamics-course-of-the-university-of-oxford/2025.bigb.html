= Solution
{parent=a}

To first order in fluid <velocity> divided by the <speed of light>, the <Lorentz transformation of electromagnetic fields> gives $\mathbf E'=\mathbf E+\mathbf u\times\mathbf B$. Applying <Ohm's law> in the local fluid frame gives $\mathbf E+\mathbf u\times\mathbf B=\eta\mathbf J$. Neglecting the <displacement current> in <Ampère's law>, $\mathbf J=\nabla\times\mathbf B/\mu_0$. Substitution in <Faraday's law> gives
$$
\partial_t\mathbf B=\nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B)-\frac\eta{\mu_0}\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf B)
=\nabla\times(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B)+\eta_m\nabla^2\mathbf B,\qquad\eta_m=\frac\eta{\mu_0}.
$$
Here $\eta_m$ is the <magnetic diffusivity>; the paper's $\eta$ is <electrical resistivity>, rather than <magnetic diffusivity>. Expanding the curl with the <solenoidal magnetic-field constraint> gives the <resistive induction equation> in its compressible form:
$$
\boxed{\partial_t\mathbf B+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B=(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u-\mathbf B\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf u+\frac\eta{\mu_0}\nabla^2\mathbf B.}
$$
The left side is the <material derivative> of the <magnetic field>. The three right-hand terms respectively describe field-line stretching and reorientation, amplification or dilution by compression or expansion, and <magnetic diffusion>. In particular, compression has $\nabla\cdot\mathbf u<0$ and amplifies a field component perpendicular to the compression.

The assumptions are nonrelativistic bulk motion, the low-frequency approximation permitting omission of the <displacement current>, a conducting-fluid continuum, isotropic local <Ohm's law> with spatially constant scalar <electrical resistivity>, constant <magnetic permeability> $\mu_0$, and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$. Hall, electron-pressure and other non-Ohmic corrections to the generalized <Ohm's law> are excluded. <Incompressible flow> is not required: imposing it would remove the compression term.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let $E_B=\int_VB^2/(2\mu_0)\,dV$ be the total <magnetic energy>. Multiply the curl form of the <resistive induction equation> by $\mathbf B/\mu_0$ and integrate over the fixed volume. The identity
$$
\int_V\mathbf B\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf C\,dV=\int_V\mathbf C\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf B\,dV+\int_{\partial V}\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf C\times\mathbf B)\,dS
$$
follows from the <divergence theorem>. The stated zero boundary fields remove its surface term. Applying it to the motional and resistive contributions yields the <magnetic energy balance in resistive magnetohydrodynamics>:
$$
\boxed{\dot E_B=\int_V(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B)\cdot\mathbf J\,dV-\frac\eta{\mu_0^2}\int_V|\nabla\times\mathbf B|^2\,dV
=-\int_V\mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf J\times\mathbf B)\,dV-\eta\int_VJ^2\,dV.}
$$
The first term is the negative of the mechanical power delivered to the fluid by the <Lorentz force density>. It transfers <energy> between fluid motion and the <magnetic field>, with either sign. The second is the nonpositive magnetic contribution to <Joule heating>. A nonzero positive <electrical resistivity> therefore dissipates <magnetic energy> at rate $\eta\int_VJ^2\,dV$; it does not force the total <magnetic energy> to decrease when fluid motion supplies a larger positive power.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Choose a <magnetic vector potential> with $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$. The <resistive induction equation> permits the evolution equation
$$
\partial_t\mathbf A=\mathbf u\times\mathbf B-\eta_m\nabla\times\mathbf B-\nabla\phi.
$$
A <gauge transformation> changes $H=\int_V\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B\,dV$ by a surface integral of the normal <magnetic field>, which is zero here. Thus the integrated <magnetic helicity> has <gauge invariance> under the stated boundary conditions.

Differentiate both factors in $H$. Use the stated vanishing-boundary assumption to remove the helicity surface flux; this can be implemented in the usual closed-boundary gauge $\mathbf n\times\mathbf A=0$, or by extending the localized field and integrating over all space with sufficient decay. The curl <integration by parts> identity then gives
$$
\dot H=\int_V\partial_t\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B\,dV+\int_V\mathbf A\cdot\nabla\times\partial_t\mathbf A\,dV
=2\int_V\partial_t\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B\,dV.
$$
The motional term is zero pointwise because $(\mathbf u\times\mathbf B)\cdot\mathbf B=0$. The scalar-potential term integrates to zero by $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$ and the boundary condition. The <resistive magnetic-helicity balance> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\dot H=-\frac{2\eta}{\mu_0}\int_V\mathbf B\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B)\,dV.}
$$
Unlike the resistive <magnetic energy> contribution, this signed <integral> need not have a fixed sign.

= d
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

A <magnetic field> varying over a length $\ell$ has $|\nabla\times\mathbf B|$ of order $B/\ell$. Consequently, the resistive <magnetic energy> loss contains two inverse powers of $\ell$, while the <resistive magnetic-helicity balance> contains only one. Small-scale gradients can therefore dissipate substantial <magnetic energy> while retaining an approximately conserved large-scale <magnetic helicity>.

A dimensionally consistent version is the <selective decay bound for magnetic helicity>. With $D_B=\eta_m\int_V|\nabla\times\mathbf B|^2\,dV/\mu_0$, the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives
$$
|\dot H|\le2\eta_m\left(\int_VB^2\,dV\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_V|\nabla\times\mathbf B|^2\,dV\right)^{1/2}
=\boxed{2\mu_0\sqrt{2\eta_mE_BD_B}}.
$$
For bounded $E_B$ and bounded, possibly nonvanishing $D_B$ as $\eta_m\to0$, the resistive change in <magnetic helicity> tends to zero, even though resistive <magnetic energy> loss may remain finite. **Magnetic energy is preferentially dissipated, while magnetic helicity is approximately conserved in this bounded-energy, bounded-dissipation limit.**

This is not a comparison of quantities with different physical units or a universal ordering of fractional decay rates. For a single <Beltrami field> satisfying $\nabla\times\mathbf B=k\mathbf B$, both integrated quantities have fractional decay rate $2\eta_mk^2$ without fluid motion. The boundedness assumptions and distribution of <magnetic helicity> across spatial scales matter.

= Solution
{parent=a}

The <stress tensor> of the incompressible <Newtonian fluid> is $\boldsymbol\sigma=-pI+2\mu e$, where $e=(\nabla\mathbf u+\nabla\mathbf u^T)/2$ is the <rate-of-strain tensor>. The homogeneous <Stokes equations> give $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma=0$. Apply the <divergence theorem> to the fluid between the body and a surrounding sphere $S_R$. If $\mathbf n_B$ points from the body into the fluid, the fluid-domain normal on the inner boundary is $-\mathbf n_B$. Hence
$$
0=\int_{S_R}\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf n\,dS-\int_{\partial B}\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf n_B\,dS,
\qquad\boxed{\mathbf F=\int_{S_R}\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf n\,dS.}
$$
This is the <force flux at infinity in Stokes flow>. A nonzero resultant <force> must be supported by a stress of order $R^{-2}$ on a surface of area of order $R^2$. More precisely, the leading exterior solution is a <Stokeslet> with force on the fluid $\mathbf f=-\mathbf F$:
$$
\mathbf u(\mathbf x)\sim\frac{(I+\widehat{\mathbf x}\widehat{\mathbf x}^{T})\mathbf f}{8\pi\mu R},\qquad
p(\mathbf x)-p_\infty\sim\frac{\mathbf f\cdot\widehat{\mathbf x}}{4\pi R^2}.
$$
Thus $\mathbf u=O(R^{-1})$, $p-p_\infty=O(R^{-2})$ and $e=O(R^{-2})$. These are leading generic orders for a translating body with nonzero drag; the integral alone does not determine every angular component, and force-free disturbances may decay faster.

= b
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

For two <Stokes flows> in the same fluid domain with the same <dynamic viscosity>, symmetry of the <Newtonian fluid stress tensor> and <incompressibility> give the local reciprocal identity
$$
\nabla\cdot(\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf u'-\boldsymbol\sigma'\mathbf u)=0.
$$
In <index notation>, its remaining cross-gradient terms are both $2\mu e:e'$. On the remote sphere the integrated reciprocal flux tends to zero: the <velocity> is $O(R^{-1})$, the <stress tensor> is $O(R^{-2})$ and the area is $O(R^2)$. On the body, the <no-slip boundary condition> makes the two <velocities> the constants $\mathbf U,\mathbf U'$. The <Lorentz reciprocal theorem> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf U'=\mathbf F'\cdot\mathbf U.}
$$
By the <linearity of Stokes flow>, the sum of the two velocity-pressure fields satisfies the boundary conditions for body <velocity> $\mathbf U+\mathbf U'$. Uniqueness of the exterior <Stokes flow> identifies it with the third solution. Since the <stress tensor> and its surface <integral> are linear too, $\mathbf F(\mathbf U+\mathbf U')=\mathbf F(\mathbf U)+\mathbf F(\mathbf U')$; scalar multiplication follows in the same way. Thus $\mathbf F=-A\mathbf U$ for a fixed-orientation <hydrodynamic resistance matrix> $A$. Substituting arbitrary $\mathbf U,\mathbf U'$ in the reciprocal identity yields
$$
\boxed{A=A^T,\qquad\mathbf F=-A\mathbf U.}
$$
The minus sign is natural because <viscous dissipation> gives $-\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf U=2\mu\int e:e\,dV>0$ for nonzero rigid translation in an otherwise stationary fluid; $A$ is a <positive-definite matrix>.

{parent=2}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Use the <free-draining approximation>: neglect the <hydrodynamic interaction> between the widely separated spheres and neglect the connector's viscous drag. <Stokes' law> gives $\mathbf f_i=-6\pi\mu a_i\mathbf U$ for each sphere. Their resultant is
$$
\mathbf F=\mathbf f_1+\mathbf f_2=-6\pi\mu(a_1+a_2)\mathbf U,
\qquad\boxed{A\simeq6\pi\mu(a_1+a_2)I.}
$$
This is the leading two-sphere approximation, not an exact finite-separation <hydrodynamic resistance matrix>. Writing $d=|\mathbf r_2-\mathbf r_1|$ and $\mathbf n=(\mathbf r_2-\mathbf r_1)/d$, the first <Stokeslet> reflection gives, if the connector remains negligible,
$$
A=6\pi\mu(a_1+a_2)I-\frac{9\pi\mu a_1a_2}{d}(I+\mathbf n\mathbf n^T)+O\!\left(\mu\frac{(a_1+a_2)^3}{d^2}\right).
$$
The negative correction expresses reduced drag from fluid entrainment by the other sphere. A very thin but arbitrarily long rod need not have negligible total drag: its typical transverse drag scales as $\mu d/\log(d/b)$ for rod radius $b$. Thus the simple boxed result uses the intended negligibly dragging connector limit as well as $a_i/d\ll1$; the two printed size inequalities alone do not specify a uniform limit in which rod drag vanishes.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

Hold the two-sphere object fixed in a uniform stream $\mathbf V$. In the <free-draining approximation>, its drag <forces> are $\mathbf f_i=\zeta_i\mathbf V$, with $\zeta_i=6\pi\mu a_i$. The <hydrodynamic torque> about $\mathbf r_c$ is
$$
\mathbf T_c=\sum_{i=1}^2(\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_c)\times\zeta_i\mathbf V
=\left[\zeta_1\mathbf r_1+\zeta_2\mathbf r_2-(\zeta_1+\zeta_2)\mathbf r_c\right]\times\mathbf V.
$$
To make it zero for every incoming-stream direction, choose the <hydrodynamic centre of a two-sphere dumbbell>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf r_c=\frac{\zeta_1\mathbf r_1+\zeta_2\mathbf r_2}{\zeta_1+\zeta_2}=\frac{a_1\mathbf r_1+a_2\mathbf r_2}{a_1+a_2}.}
$$
It lies closer to the larger sphere, whose larger drag requires a shorter lever arm. Uniform translation does not spin an isolated sphere, so no additional isolated-sphere spin <torque> enters this leading approximation.

Finite-separation <hydrodynamic interactions> and connector drag can change the displayed location; the calculation has the same approximation as part <2/c>.

= Solution
{parent=e}

For rotation about the <hydrodynamic centre of a two-sphere dumbbell>, the sphere centres have <velocities> $\mathbf v_i=\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_c)$. Their resultant drag <force> in the <free-draining approximation> is
$$
\mathbf F=-\sum_i\zeta_i\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\mathbf r_i-\mathbf r_c)
=-\boldsymbol\Omega\times\left(\sum_i\zeta_i\mathbf r_i-(\zeta_1+\zeta_2)\mathbf r_c\right)
=\boxed{\mathbf0.}
$$
Each sphere also experiences a spin drag <torque>, but its isolated rotation causes no net <force>. Those spin contributions therefore do not alter the cancellation.

This illustrates <translation-rotation reciprocal coupling>. About any fixed origin, write the fluid's rigid-body <force> and <torque> as
$$
\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf F\\\mathbf T\end{pmatrix}=-\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\B^T&C\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf U\\\boldsymbol\Omega\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The transposed cross-blocks follow from the <Lorentz reciprocal theorem>. If pure translation causes zero <torque> about an origin for every $\mathbf U$, then $B^T=0$, hence pure rotation about that origin causes zero <force>. This implication is general for linear <Stokes flow>; the existence and leading location of such an origin here use the two-sphere geometry and the approximation in part <2/c>.
