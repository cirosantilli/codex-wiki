== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the source's <Gaussian units> and neglect the <displacement current>, as in nonrelativistic <magnetohydrodynamics>. Write $\Delta p=p_\perp-p_\parallel$ for the <pressure anisotropy>. The <pressure tensor> is $\mathsf P=p_\perp\mathsf I-\Delta p\,\mathbf b\mathbf b$, so its <force density> is
$$
-\nabla\cdot\mathsf P=-\nabla p_\perp+\nabla\cdot(\Delta p\,\mathbf b\mathbf b).
$$
<Ampère's law> and the <solenoidal magnetic-field constraint> give the <Lorentz force> decomposition
$$
\frac{\mathbf j\times\mathbf B}{c}
=\frac{(\nabla\times\mathbf B)\times\mathbf B}{4\pi}
=-\nabla\frac{B^2}{8\pi}+\frac{(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B}{4\pi}
=-\nabla\frac{B^2}{8\pi}+\nabla\cdot\frac{\mathbf B\mathbf B}{4\pi}.
$$
Adding these two <force densities> and using $\mathbf B\mathbf B=B^2\mathbf b\mathbf b$ yields
$$
\boxed{\rho\frac{D\mathbf u}{Dt}=-\nabla\left(p_\perp+\frac{B^2}{8\pi}\right)+\nabla\cdot\left[\left(\Delta p+\frac{B^2}{4\pi}\right)\mathbf b\mathbf b\right].}
$$
The first term is the <gradient> of the modified <magnetohydrodynamic total pressure>; the second is the anisotropic <Maxwell stress tensor> contribution together with the directional <pressure force>. Their effective coefficient is the <pressure-anisotropy-modified magnetic tension>, $\mathcal T=B^2/(4\pi)+\Delta p$. At fixed <magnetic field>, positive <pressure anisotropy> increases resistance to bending, while negative <pressure anisotropy> reduces it. When $\mathcal T$ becomes negative, bending experiences an amplifying <force> rather than a restoring <force>. Gradients of $\Delta p$ can also drive motion, so the curvature interpretation refers specifically to the bending contribution.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Set $\Delta p_0=p_{\perp0}-p_{\parallel0}$ and $\mathcal T_0=\Delta p_0+B_0^2/(4\pi)$. Distinguish the scalar <magnetic field> strength perturbation $\delta B$ from the vector perturbation $\delta\mathbf B$. To first order,
$$
\delta\mathbf B=\delta B\,\widehat{\mathbf z}+B_0\delta\mathbf b,
\qquad \delta b_z=0,
\qquad k_\perp\delta b_x=-k_\parallel\frac{\delta B}{B_0},
$$
where the last identity is the <solenoidal magnetic-field constraint>. For a <Fourier mode> with the stated time convention, put
$$
\delta q=\delta p_\perp+\frac{B_0\delta B}{4\pi},\qquad
\delta\mathcal T=\delta p_\perp-\delta p_\parallel+\frac{B_0\delta B}{2\pi}.
$$
The linear <momentum conservation> equation, after division by $i$, is
$$
-\omega\rho_0\mathbf u=-\mathbf k\,\delta q+k_\parallel\widehat{\mathbf z}\,\delta\mathcal T+\mathcal T_0\left[k_\parallel\delta\mathbf b+\widehat{\mathbf z}(\mathbf k\cdot\delta\mathbf b)\right].
$$
Use the <solenoidal magnetic-field constraint> in its $z$ component. The coefficient of $\widehat{\mathbf z}$ reduces to $-k_\parallel[\delta p_\parallel+\Delta p_0\delta B/B_0]$. Thus
$$
\boxed{-\omega\rho_0\mathbf u=-k_\perp\widehat{\mathbf x}\left(\delta p_\perp+\frac{B_0\delta B}{4\pi}\right)-k_\parallel\widehat{\mathbf z}\left(\delta p_\parallel+\Delta p_0\frac{\delta B}{B_0}\right)+k_\parallel\mathcal T_0\delta\mathbf b.}
$$
Both scalar <pressure> perturbations act within the $xz$ plane. The $y$ component therefore decouples, independently of their closure. The <ideal magnetohydrodynamic equations> give
$$
\partial_t\delta\mathbf B=B_0\partial_z\mathbf u-B_0\widehat{\mathbf z}\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf u,
\qquad
-\omega\delta b_y=k_\parallel u_y,
\qquad
-\omega\rho_0u_y=k_\parallel\mathcal T_0\delta b_y.
$$
Eliminating the amplitudes gives the <Alfvén wave> <dispersion relation>
$$
\boxed{\omega^2=\frac{k_\parallel^2\mathcal T_0}{\rho_0}=k_\parallel^2v_A^2\left(1+\frac{4\pi\Delta p_0}{B_0^2}\right),\qquad v_A^2=\frac{B_0^2}{4\pi\rho_0}.}
$$
For this <shear Alfvén wave>, the <velocity> and fluid displacement are along $y$, perpendicular to both $\mathbf k$ and the equilibrium <magnetic field>. Let $\boldsymbol\xi=\xi_y\widehat{\mathbf y}$; then
$$
\mathbf u=-i\omega\boldsymbol\xi,\qquad
\delta\mathbf B=ik_\parallel B_0\boldsymbol\xi,\qquad
\delta\mathbf b=ik_\parallel\boldsymbol\xi.
$$
A real snapshot $\xi_y=\xi_0\cos(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)$ bends the <magnetic field lines> sideways, with $\delta B_y=B_0\partial_z\xi_y$. There is no first-order compression or change of <magnetic field> strength: $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\xi=0$, $\delta\rho=0$ and $\delta B=0$. Scalar <pressure> perturbations are unnecessary in this transverse subsystem. For a traveling stable branch, $\delta B_y/B_0=-k_\parallel u_y/\omega$, so the <velocity> and transverse <magnetic field> perturbations are in phase or opposite phase according to the branch. At $k_\parallel=0$ the bending <force> and this <angular frequency> vanish.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For $k_\parallel\ne0$ the <Alfvén wave> becomes a <firehose instability> when its squared <angular frequency> is negative:
$$
\boxed{p_{\parallel0}-p_{\perp0}>\frac{B_0^2}{4\pi},\qquad \frac{p_{\perp0}-p_{\parallel0}}{p_{\parallel0}}<-\frac{2}{\beta},\qquad \beta=\frac{8\pi p_{\parallel0}}{B_0^2}.}
$$
Equality is marginal, rather than exponential growth. The <pressure anisotropy> must have negative sign; its absolute magnitude alone is insufficient.

For the intergalactic <plasma>, a negative relative <pressure anisotropy> of $-10^{-2}$ reaches this fluid threshold only for $\beta>200$. In particular, the literal pair $\beta=100$ and relative <pressure anisotropy> $-0.01$ is stable because the threshold is $-0.02$. Since the quoted numbers are approximate and allow larger <plasma beta>, this weakly magnetized <plasma> is susceptible, rather than necessarily unstable at every quoted value. For example, $\beta=300$ and relative <pressure anisotropy> $-0.01$ are unstable. Positive <pressure anisotropy> does not excite this branch.

For the fusion-device <plasma>, $\beta\sim10^{-2}$ requires a negative relative <pressure anisotropy> below roughly $-200$, whereas its magnitude is only about $0.1$. The stabilizing <magnetic tension> dominates, so it is not susceptible to this fluid <firehose instability> under the stated conditions. Indeed, nonnegative perpendicular <pressure> requires the relative <pressure anisotropy> to be no smaller than $-1$; at such a small <plasma beta> even this extreme cannot satisfy the threshold.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

<Magnetic flux freezing> ties a transverse fluid displacement to a bend in the <magnetic field lines>. For the uniform background, the transverse displacement equation is
$$
\rho_0\partial_t^2\boldsymbol\xi_\perp=\mathcal T_0\partial_z^2\boldsymbol\xi_\perp.
$$
For a sinusoidal bend, $\partial_z^2\boldsymbol\xi_\perp=-k_\parallel^2\boldsymbol\xi_\perp$. Positive <pressure-anisotropy-modified magnetic tension> therefore gives a restoring <force>, and <inertia> produces oscillation. In an isotropic <plasma>, this is precisely the positive <magnetic tension> $B_0^2/(4\pi)$.

Particles moving predominantly along bent <magnetic field lines> contribute a transverse <momentum flux>. Their larger parallel <pressure> opposes the restoring curvature <force> supplied by the <magnetic field> and perpendicular <pressure>. Once $p_{\parallel0}-p_{\perp0}>B_0^2/(4\pi)$, the net <force> points along the original displacement: a larger bend produces a larger outward <force>, giving positive feedback. Equivalently, the bending contribution to the quadratic <potential energy> is $\frac12\mathcal T_0\lvert\partial_z\boldsymbol\xi_\perp\rvert^2$ and has become negative.

In this static, homogeneous fluid model the unstable roots are
$$
\boxed{\omega=\pm i\gamma,\qquad \gamma=|k_\parallel|\sqrt{\frac{p_{\parallel0}-p_{\perp0}-B_0^2/(4\pi)}{\rho_0}}.}
$$
With $e^{-i\omega t}$ the root $+i\gamma$ grows. Its real <angular frequency> is zero, so the unstable pattern grows without propagation in the equilibrium rest frame; its partner decays. Advection by a background flow would change that frame-dependent statement. The growth increasing without bound as $|k_\parallel|$ increases is a limitation of this long-<wavelength> model: finite <gyroradius> and other kinetic corrections cannot be neglected at sufficiently short scales.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Label a trajectory by $\mathbf a$ and write its <flow map> as $\mathbf X(\mathbf a,t)$, with $\partial_t\mathbf X=\mathbf u(\mathbf X,t)$. An infinitesimal <material line> joining neighboring labels has tangent $\ell_i=F_{ij}\,d a_j$, where $F_{ij}=\partial X_i/\partial a_j$ is the <deformation gradient>. Differentiating at fixed labels gives
$$
\frac{D\ell_i}{Dt}=\partial_j u_i\,\ell_j,
\qquad
\boxed{\partial_t\boldsymbol\ell+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol\ell=(\boldsymbol\ell\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u.}
$$
This identity holds for an unnormalized <material line> tangent even in a compressible flow. <Incompressibility> also makes an advected <magnetic field> obey the same stretching law. A unit tangent would instead require a projection removing its change of length.

Use the source's <velocity gradient> convention $G_{ij}=\partial_i u_j$. For a <outer product> $A_{ij}=\ell_i\ell_j$, the <product rule> yields
$$
\frac{DA_{ij}}{Dt}=(\partial_k u_i)A_{kj}+A_{ik}(\partial_k u_j)
=(G^T\mathsf A+\mathsf A G)_{ij}.
$$
The same law holds for a sum or locally transported average of such <outer products>. Thus the <dyadic transport of material line elements> motivates
$$
\boxed{\overset{\triangledown}{\mathsf A}=\partial_t\mathsf A+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla\mathsf A-\mathsf A G-G^T\mathsf A.}
$$
This <upper-convected derivative> vanishes for a symmetric <tensor> transported solely by stretching of <material lines>. It subtracts both advection and geometric deformation, allowing a <constitutive equation> to isolate intrinsic relaxation or production. More generally $\mathsf A=F\mathsf A_{\rm ref}F^T$ gives $\overset{\triangledown}{\mathsf A}=F\dot{\mathsf A}_{\rm ref}F^T$. Under a time-dependent rigid rotation $Q$, its value transforms as $Q\overset{\triangledown}{\mathsf A}Q^T$, making it an <objective time derivative>. This is a suitable derivative for this transport type; symmetry alone does not make it the unique possible <objective time derivative>.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

When deformation is stopped, the <rate-of-strain tensor> and <velocity gradient> vanish, leaving $\mathsf T+\tau\partial_t\mathsf T=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathsf T(t)=\mathsf T(0)e^{-t/\tau}.}
$$
The positive constant $\tau$ is the <viscoelastic relaxation time>. For slow weak deformation, all the transport and quadratic terms are smaller than $\mathsf T$, so $\mathsf T\simeq2\mu\mathsf E$. Thus $\mu>0$ is the zero-rate <dynamic viscosity>, measured in <stress> times time. The corresponding short-time <shear modulus> of the <Maxwell fluid> response is $G_{\rm el}=\mu/\tau$. The source's $\alpha$ has units of time, since $\alpha\mathsf T\mathsf E$ must have units of <stress>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Linearize about a relaxed state $\mathsf T=0$ with constant <mass density> $\rho_0$. In the unforced <momentum conservation> equation $\rho_0D_t\mathbf u=-\nabla p+\nabla\cdot\mathsf T$, the $x$ component of the <pressure gradient> is zero for a freely evolving <unidirectional flow> independent of $x$. A prescribed external <pressure gradient> would add a forcing term.

For $\mathbf u=u(y,t)\widehat{\mathbf x}$, <incompressibility> is automatic and the only nonzero entries of the <rate-of-strain tensor> are $E_{xy}=E_{yx}=\frac12\partial_yu$. Products of <stress> and <velocity gradient> are quadratic in the small amplitude. The <constitutive equation> consequently becomes $\mathsf T+\tau\partial_t\mathsf T=2\mu\mathsf E$ to first order. With $T=T_{xy}=T_{yx}$, its shear component and the $x$ component of <momentum conservation> are
$$
\boxed{\rho_0\partial_tu=\partial_yT,\qquad T+\tau\partial_tT=\mu\partial_yu.}
$$
The diagonal <stress> components have only homogeneous relaxation at this order and vanish if initially relaxed. In particular, $\alpha$ does not enter this linear response.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

Eliminating the <shear stress> from the two linear equations gives the <Maxwell shear-wave dispersion> equation in physical space,
$$
\tau\partial_t^2u+\partial_tu=\nu\partial_y^2u,
\qquad \nu=\frac{\mu}{\rho_0},
$$
where $\nu$ is the <kinematic viscosity>. Substitution of a <Fourier mode> $e^{iky+\sigma t}$ gives
$$
\tau\sigma^2+\sigma+\nu k^2=0,
\qquad
\boxed{\sigma_\pm=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt{1-4\tau\nu k^2}}{2\tau}.}
$$
These are temporal exponents: the oscillation <angular frequency> is the imaginary part of $\sigma$, with the sign determined by the selected time convention. For $4\tau\nu k^2<1$ both exponents are real and nonpositive. At equality the repeated exponent is $-1/(2\tau)$; generic solutions include $t e^{-t/(2\tau)}$. Above the threshold the modes are damped propagating shear disturbances:
$$
\sigma_\pm=-\frac1{2\tau}\pm i\sqrt{\frac{\nu k^2}{\tau}-\frac1{4\tau^2}}.
$$
At large $|k|$ their <phase velocity> tends to $\sqrt{\nu/\tau}=\sqrt{G_{\rm el}/\rho_0}$.

For the limit $\tau\to0$ at fixed $\mu,\rho_0$ and $k$, expansion of the <square root> gives
$$
\boxed{\sigma_{\rm slow}=-\nu k^2+O(\tau),\qquad \sigma_{\rm fast}=-\frac1\tau+\nu k^2+O(\tau).}
$$
After a short initial relaxation, the surviving branch obeys the <diffusion equation> $u_t=\nu u_{yy}$ of a <Newtonian fluid>. The fast branch disappears on the vanishing <viscoelastic relaxation time>; at $k=0$ the two exponents are exactly $0$ and $-1/\tau$. This limit is not uniform in $k$: modes with $k$ of order $(\tau\nu)^{-1/2}$ still sample the oscillatory transition. If instead $G_{\rm el}=\mu/\tau$ is held fixed as $\tau\to0$, then $\mu\to0$ and the fixed-$k$ slow exponent tends to zero. The ordinary <Newtonian fluid> limit therefore specifies which material parameter is held fixed.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $g=\dot\gamma$ and use $G_{yx}=g$ for the source's <velocity gradient> convention. Seek the homogeneous stationary <stress> reached under steady <simple shear flow>. The <material derivative> of that <stress tensor> vanishes. In the $xy$ plane,
$$
G=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\g&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
E=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}0&g\\g&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The $xx$, $yy$ and $zz$ components of the <constitutive equation> give, respectively,
$$
T_{xx}-(2\tau-\alpha)gT_{xy}=0,\qquad
T_{yy}+\alpha gT_{xy}=0,\qquad T_{zz}=0.
$$
Thus, putting $D=1+\alpha(2\tau-\alpha)g^2$ and using the <shear stress> calculated below,
$$
\boxed{T_{xx}=\frac{\mu(2\tau-\alpha)g^2}{D},\qquad T_{yy}=-\frac{\mu\alpha g^2}{D},\qquad T_{zz}=0.}
$$
The out-of-plane shear components vanish on the homogeneous branch. The two <normal-stress differences> are
$$
N_1=2\tau gT_{xy}=\frac{2\mu\tau g^2}{D},\qquad
N_2=-\alpha gT_{xy}=-\frac{\mu\alpha g^2}{D},
\qquad \boxed{\frac{N_2}{N_1}=-\frac{\alpha}{2\tau}.}
$$
The ratio is defined at nonzero shear when $N_1\ne0$; at $g=0$ both differences vanish, and the displayed constant is their limiting ratio. An arbitrary isotropic <pressure> in the total <Cauchy stress tensor> cancels from both differences; it is not an arbitrary addition to the constitutively defined extra <stress> $\mathsf T$ itself.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The $xy$ component of the stationary <constitutive equation> is
$$
T_{xy}-\tau gT_{yy}+\frac{\alpha g}{2}(T_{xx}+T_{yy})=\mu g.
$$
Insert $T_{yy}=-\alpha gT_{xy}$ and $T_{xx}=(2\tau-\alpha)gT_{xy}$. The coefficient of $g^2T_{xy}$ becomes $\tau\alpha+\alpha(\tau-\alpha)=\alpha(2\tau-\alpha)$, giving
$$
\boxed{T_{xy}=\frac{\mu\dot\gamma}{1+\alpha(2\tau-\alpha)\dot\gamma^2},\qquad \eta_{\rm sh}=\frac{T_{xy}}{\dot\gamma}=\frac{\mu}{1+\alpha(2\tau-\alpha)\dot\gamma^2}.}
$$
For $C=\alpha(2\tau-\alpha)>0$, the small-rate <shear stress> is $T_{xy}=\mu\dot\gamma-\mu C\dot\gamma^3+O(\dot\gamma^5)$. At large positive rate, $T_{xy}\sim\mu/(C\dot\gamma)$ and the <shear viscosity> decreases as $\mu/(C\dot\gamma^2)$. The <shear stress> is odd in rate, reaches a positive maximum at
$$
\dot\gamma_*=C^{-1/2},\qquad T_{xy,*}=\frac{\mu}{2\sqrt C},\qquad
\frac{dT_{xy}}{d\dot\gamma}=\mu\frac{1-C\dot\gamma^2}{(1+C\dot\gamma^2)^2},
$$
and decreases thereafter. The original plot displays this homogeneous constitutive curve. Its decreasing branch can permit <shear banding>; the local model alone supplies neither an interface-selection rule nor a guaranteed stable homogeneous realization.

\Image[2016-viscoelastic-shear-response.png]
{title=Homogeneous shear stress and the two temporal branches of the linear response}
{description=The steady curve uses q equal to the shear rate times the square root of alpha times (2 tau minus alpha). The spectral panel uses K equal to the wavenumber magnitude times the square root of tau times nu. These are distinct nondimensional variables.}

This is a <Johnson-Segalman fluid> without an added <Newtonian fluid> solvent contribution. To identify conventions explicitly, set $a=1-\alpha/\tau$ and $L=G^T=E+W$, with $W$ the <spin tensor>. The derivative appearing in the <constitutive equation> becomes
$$
D_t\mathsf T-W\mathsf T+\mathsf TW-a(E\mathsf T+\mathsf TE),
$$
the <Gordon--Schowalter derivative>. Its usual slip interval $|a|\le1$ corresponds to $0\le\alpha\le2\tau$, while a nonsingular genuinely <shear-thinning> curve requires
$$
\boxed{\tau>0,\quad\mu>0,\quad0<\alpha<2\tau.}
$$
At $\alpha=0$ the model is the <upper-convected Maxwell model>; at $\alpha=2\tau$ it is the <lower-convected derivative> counterpart, and both have constant <shear viscosity> in this test. At $\alpha=\tau$ the derivative is the <Jaumann derivative>, giving the <corotational Maxwell fluid>. Merely requiring $\alpha>0$ is insufficient: for $\alpha=3\tau$ the denominator is $1-3\tau^2\dot\gamma^2$, which vanishes at $|\dot\gamma|=1/(\sqrt3\tau)$ and then gives a negative steady <shear viscosity>. There is no solvent-induced large-rate plateau in the stated equation.
