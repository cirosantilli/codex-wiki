= Solution
{parent=a}

Write the physical <magnetic field> as $\mathbf B_{\rm phys}$ and use the constant <mass density> $\rho_0$ to introduce <Alfvén velocity> units, $\mathbf B=\mathbf B_{\rm phys}/\sqrt{\mu_0\rho_0}$. If $P$ is the fluid's mechanical <pressure>, the <magnetohydrodynamic total pressure> per unit <mass density> is $p=P/\rho_0+B^2/2$. The <ideal magnetohydrodynamic equations> then reduce to
$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\partial_t\mathbf u+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u
&=-\nabla p+(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B,\\
\partial_t\mathbf B+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B
&=(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u,\\
\nabla\cdot\mathbf u&=0,\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The first equation is <momentum conservation>: the <magnetic pressure> has been included in $p$, leaving <magnetic tension> explicitly. The second is the <ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation>, expressing <magnetic flux freezing>. The last two equations impose <incompressibility> and the <solenoidal magnetic-field constraint>.

The model assumes a smooth conducting-fluid continuum with constant <mass density> and constant <magnetic permeability>, nonrelativistic <velocity>, and scales on which a single-fluid description is appropriate. Negligible <electrical resistivity> removes <magnetic diffusion>; negligible <viscosity> removes viscous <momentum diffusion>. Equivalently, the <magnetic Reynolds number> and the <Reynolds number> are large on the scales being retained. The low-frequency approximation neglects the <displacement current>; Hall and other kinetic corrections to <Ohm's law> are absent. <Incompressibility> is an imposed approximation, commonly justified when the <Mach number> is small. There is no independently evolved thermodynamic <pressure>: $p$ enforces the <incompressibility condition>. We omit external body forces; a conservative one could instead be absorbed into $p$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <magnetic second-moment tensor> is $M_{ij}=B_iB_j$. This is the magnetic dyadic called the Maxwell tensor in the paper; the isotropic part of the usual <Maxwell stress tensor> is already contained in the <magnetohydrodynamic total pressure>. The <solenoidal magnetic-field constraint> gives
$$
\partial_jM_{ij}
=B_j\partial_jB_i+B_i\partial_jB_j
=B_j\partial_jB_i.
$$
Consequently the <magnetohydrodynamic momentum equation> in part <1/a> becomes $D_tu_i=-\partial_i p+\partial_jM_{ij}$, where $D_t=\partial_t+u_n\partial_n$ is the <material derivative>. Applying its product rule and the <ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
D_tM_{ij}
&=(D_tB_i)B_j+B_i(D_tB_j)\\
&=B_nB_j\partial_nu_i+B_iB_n\partial_nu_j
=\boxed{M_{nj}\partial_nu_i+M_{in}\partial_nu_j}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the evolution uses only $\mathbf u,M,p$, together with $\partial_i u_i=0$. To determine $p$, take the <divergence> of the <magnetohydrodynamic momentum equation>. Since $\partial_t\partial_i u_i=0$ and $u_j\partial_j\partial_i u_i=0$,
$$
\boxed{\nabla^2p=\partial_i\partial_jM_{ij}
-(\partial_i u_j)(\partial_j u_i).}
$$
This is the <pressure Poisson equation for incompressible flow>, with <boundary conditions> supplied by the imposed normal <acceleration> and normal <stress>, or by periodicity or decay at infinity as appropriate. For example, the normal component of the <magnetohydrodynamic momentum equation> requires $\mathbf n\cdot\nabla p=\mathbf n\cdot(\nabla\cdot M-D_t\mathbf u)$ at a prescribed boundary. The <pressure> is determined up to a spatially constant function of <time>.

For equivalence to one resolved <magnetic field>, the initial $M$ must actually be $\mathbf B\mathbf B^T$ for a <divergence-free vector field>. In particular it is a symmetric <positive-semidefinite matrix> of <matrix rank> at most one. The tensor equations also admit more general stresses; the averaged $M$ in part <1/d> need not have <matrix rank> one.

= Solution
{parent=c}

With zero <velocity> and no time dependence, the <magnetic second-moment tensor> equation holds automatically, but the <magnetohydrodynamic momentum equation> still requires
$$
\boxed{\partial_jM_{ij}^{(0)}=\partial_i p_0.}
$$
The <magnetic tension> must therefore be a <pressure gradient>. Locally, its necessary integrability condition is
$$
\nabla\times\bigl(\nabla\cdot M^{(0)}\bigr)=0;
$$
on a simply connected region this is also sufficient for a scalar $p_0$, subject to the global <boundary conditions>. In physical units the same balance is $\nabla P_0=\mathbf J_0\times\mathbf B_{{\rm phys},0}$: the <Lorentz force density> is balanced by mechanical <pressure>, rather than necessarily vanishing. A zero mean <magnetic field> does not make the local <magnetic tension> zero, so an arbitrary tangle is not automatically a static <equilibrium>.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Take the <trace> of the statistically isotropic <magnetic second-moment tensor>. There are three spatial directions, so
$$
\langle B^2\rangle
=\langle B_iB_i\rangle
=v_A^2\delta_{ii}=3v_A^2.
$$
Choosing the nonnegative <speed>,
$$
\boxed{v_A=\sqrt{\frac{\langle B^2\rangle}{3}}
=\sqrt{\frac{\langle B_{\rm phys}^2\rangle}{3\mu_0\rho_0}}.}
$$
Thus $v_A$ is the root-mean-square value of any one Cartesian <magnetic field> component in <Alfvén velocity> units. It is not the magnitude of the mean <Alfvén velocity>, which is zero here. <Statistical isotropy> fixes a second moment even when the first moment vanishes.

= Solution
{parent=e}

Average the equations in part <1/b> and make exactly the prescribed scale-separation approximation: the retained perturbations vary on lengths much greater than $\ell$, and perturbations at the tangle scale are omitted. Products of two retained perturbations can then be neglected. Writing $\mathbf v=\delta\mathbf u$, $m=\delta M$ and $\pi=\delta p$, the <linearized equations> are
$$
\partial_tv_i=-\partial_i\pi+\partial_jm_{ij},\qquad
\partial_tm_{ij}=v_A^2(\partial_jv_i+\partial_iv_j),\qquad
\partial_iv_i=0.
$$
No unmodelled small-scale response has been inserted into this closure.

Introduce the <fluid displacement> $\boldsymbol\xi$ by $\mathbf v=\partial_t\boldsymbol\xi$ and choose $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\xi=0$. Integrating the <magnetic second-moment tensor> equation gives
$$
\boxed{m_{ij}
=v_A^2(\partial_i\xi_j+\partial_j\xi_i)+s_{ij}(\mathbf r),}
$$
where $s$ is a time-independent integration tensor set by the initial <stress>. For a pure oscillatory <normal mode>, $s=0$. Its <restoring force> is therefore $v_A^2\nabla^2\boldsymbol\xi$: the isotropic magnetic tangle has the same transverse response as an incompressible elastic medium with <shear modulus> $\rho_0v_A^2$.

For a <plane wave> proportional to $e^{i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)}$, with $\omega\ne0$, <incompressibility> gives $\mathbf k\cdot\widehat{\mathbf v}=0$. The tensor equation and <magnetohydrodynamic momentum equation> become
$$
\widehat m_{ij}
=-\frac{v_A^2}{\omega}
(k_i\widehat v_j+k_j\widehat v_i),\qquad
-i\omega\widehat v_i=-ik_i\widehat\pi+ik_j\widehat m_{ij}.
$$
Dotting the second equation with $\mathbf k$ yields $\widehat\pi=0$ for $k\ne0$, since $k_i k_j\widehat m_{ij}=0$. Substitution then gives
$$
\boxed{\omega^2=v_A^2k^2,\qquad
\widehat m_{ij}=iv_A^2(k_i\widehat\xi_j+k_j\widehat\xi_i),\qquad
\mathbf k\cdot\widehat{\boldsymbol\xi}=0.}
$$
There are two independent <transverse polarizations>. The <phase speed> is $v_A$ in every direction, and the two signs of $\omega$ give <group velocities> $\pm v_A\widehat{\mathbf k}$. These <magnetoelastic waves in an isotropic magnetic tangle> exist because a shear deformation distorts the second moment of the tangled <magnetic field>, producing a restoring <magnetic tension> even though its mean is zero.

For comparison, a uniform <magnetic field> with <Alfvén velocity> $\mathbf B_0$ gives the incompressible <Alfvén wave> relation $\omega^2=(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf B_0)^2$. Its <frequency> depends on the direction of $\mathbf k$, and <energy> propagates along $\mathbf B_0$. Here the averaged restoring response is isotropic: there is no preferred mean-field direction. Both systems resist transverse deformation through <magnetic tension>, rather than through compressive sound.

\Image[2019-magnetoelastic-wave-speeds.png]
{title=Directional phase speeds for a magnetic tangle and a uniform magnetic field with the same mean-square strength}
{description=The radial coordinate is phase speed divided by the tangle wave speed. The circular tangle curve is direction independent; the uniform-field curve is proportional to the absolute cosine of the propagation angle. These are phase-speed curves, not group-velocity trajectories.}

**The propagating branch is not the entire perturbation space.** For example, $\mathbf v=0$, $m_{ij}=f(\mathbf r)\delta_{ij}$ and $\pi=f(\mathbf r)$ solve the <linearized equations> without propagation, for any small, slowly varying $f$. Such a <static stress perturbation in magnetoelastic wave equations> balances a <pressure gradient>. Thus the requested <wave> conclusion applies to the oscillatory branch; it must not exclude stationary initial <stress> perturbations.

= Solution
{parent=f}

The common part of the <magnetohydrodynamic-viscoelastic analogy> is stretching and advection of a line element. With the component-first <velocity gradient> $L_{ij}=\partial_j u_i$, the ideal tensor equation is
$$
D_tM-LM-ML^T=0.
$$
Its left side is an <upper-convected derivative>, the same deformation law that appears for a <polymer> <conformation tensor> when its relaxation is neglected.

There are two important physical differences. First, the <solenoidal magnetic-field constraint> prevents a <magnetic field line> from having an isolated source or sink inside the fluid. A <polymer> strand can have molecular endpoints, so there is no corresponding no-endpoint constraint on the strands. Second, a stretched <polymer> has a preferred relaxed distribution of configurations and a finite <polymer relaxation time>; it can recoil relative to the solvent through molecular drag and thermal motion. An ideal <magnetic field> has no analogous local tendency to return to a prescribed coil size. Real <polymers> also have a finite contour length, a limitation absent from the ideal field-stretching law.

For example, a normalized <Hookean dumbbell conformation equation> has the dissipative extension
$$
\boxed{D_tC-LC-CL^T=-\frac1\tau(C-I).}
$$
This is local relaxation toward an isotropic thermal configuration. It remains nonzero for a spatially uniform stretched $C$. By contrast, constant <magnetic diffusivity> $\eta_m$ adds $\eta_m\nabla^2\mathbf B$ to the <resistive induction equation>. The product rule gives the <resistive evolution of the magnetic second-moment tensor>:
$$
\boxed{D_tM_{ij}-L_{in}M_{nj}-M_{in}L_{jn}
=\eta_m\nabla^2M_{ij}
-2\eta_m(\partial_nB_i)(\partial_nB_j).}
$$
The magnetic correction involves spatial gradients, vanishes for a uniform <magnetic field>, and does not create field-line endpoints: $\partial_t(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B)=\eta_m\nabla^2(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B)$. It permits slippage and <magnetic reconnection> while preserving the no-monopole constraint. After averaging, its gradient-product term generally needs additional information beyond the averaged $M$. One cannot replace it indiscriminately by the local <polymer> relaxation term, or even by $\eta_m\nabla^2M$ alone.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Work in the laboratory frame, with the fluid at rest at infinity. Set
$$
f(r)=\frac{3a}{4r}+\frac{a^3}{4r^3},\qquad
g(r)=\frac{3a}{4r^3}-\frac{3a^3}{4r^5},
\qquad \mathbf u=f\mathbf U+g(\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf x)\mathbf x.
$$
At $r=a$, $f=1$ and $g=0$, so the <no-slip boundary condition> is $\mathbf u=\mathbf U$ on the translating <sphere>. As $r\to\infty$, $\mathbf u=O(r^{-1})\to0$ and the gauge-fixed <pressure> tends to zero.

It is also possible to check the exterior <Stokes equations> directly. Differentiating the radial functions gives
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf u
=(\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf x)\left(\frac{f'}r+rg'+4g\right)=0,
$$
and
$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla^2\mathbf u
&=\left(f''+\frac{2f'}r+2g\right)\mathbf U
+\left(g''+\frac{6g'}r\right)(\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf x)\mathbf x\\
&=\frac{3a}{2}\left(\frac{\mathbf U}{r^3}
-\frac{3(\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf x)\mathbf x}{r^5}\right)
=\frac{\nabla p}{\mu}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the <Newtonian fluid stress tensor> $\sigma=-pI+\mu(\nabla\mathbf u+\nabla\mathbf u^T)$ satisfies $\nabla\cdot\sigma=0$ throughout $r>a$.

Let $\mathbf n$ point away from the common centre on each concentric <sphere>. Applying the <divergence theorem> to the intervening fluid shell, whose inner normal is $-\mathbf n$, yields
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F_{\rm drag}
=\int_{r=a}\sigma\mathbf n\,dS
=\int_{r=R}\sigma\mathbf n\,dS,\qquad R>a.}
$$
This is the <force flux at infinity in Stokes flow>. It is legitimate even though the <stress tensor> tends to zero pointwise, because the area of the large <sphere> grows as $R^2$.

For an explicit evaluation, put $A=3a/4$ and $\mathbf n=\mathbf x/R$. The leading terms are $\mathbf u=A[\mathbf U+(\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf n)\mathbf n]/R$ and $p=2\mu A(\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf n)/R^2$. Their <velocity gradient> is
$$
\partial_j u_i
=\frac{A}{R^2}\left[-U_i n_j+U_j n_i
+(\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf n)\delta_{ij}
-3(\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf n)n_i n_j\right]
+O\!\left(\frac{a^3|\mathbf U|}{R^4}\right).
$$
After symmetrizing and subtracting the <pressure> contribution,
$$
\sigma\mathbf n
=-\frac{9\mu a}{2R^2}(\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf n)\mathbf n
+O\!\left(\frac{\mu a^3|\mathbf U|}{R^4}\right).
$$
By <rotational symmetry>, $\int n_i n_j\,d\Omega=(4\pi/3)\delta_{ij}$. Hence
$$
\mathbf F_{\rm drag}
=-\frac{9\mu a}{2}\int(\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf n)\mathbf n\,d\Omega
=\boxed{-6\pi\mu a\mathbf U}.
$$
This is <Stokes's law>, with the <force> on the <sphere> opposite its <velocity>. For a component calculation with $\mathbf U=U\mathbf e_z$, the same integral is $F_z=-(9\mu aU/2)\int\cos^2\theta\,d\Omega$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Neglect the <inertia> of the <sphere> and balance the applied <force> against <Stokes drag>. The <sphere> translates with $\mathbf U=\mathbf F_1/\zeta$, where $\zeta=6\pi\mu a$. The applied <force> is also the net <force> transmitted to the fluid; the fluid's drag on the <sphere> has the opposite sign.

Translate the origin to $\mathbf r_1$. In the solution of part <2/a>, retain the terms of order $a/r$ and discard those of order $a^3/r^3$. With $\mathbf n=(\mathbf x-\mathbf r_1)/r$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf u(\mathbf x)
&\sim\frac{3a}{4r}(I+\mathbf n\mathbf n^T)\mathbf U\\
&=\boxed{\frac1{8\pi\mu r}(I+\mathbf n\mathbf n^T)\mathbf F_1}.
\end{aligned}
$$
This is the <Stokeslet>, or equivalently the <Oseen tensor> $\mathsf G(\mathbf r)=(I+\mathbf n\mathbf n^T)/(8\pi\mu r)$ acting on $\mathbf F_1$. The omitted finite-size <velocity> is of order $a^2|\mathbf F_1|/(\mu r^3)$, so the displayed term is the leading far field for $r\gg a$. The singular <Stokeslet> must not be evaluated at its own centre to obtain a particle's self mobility.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Use the deterministic <Hookean dumbbell model>, with a <zero-rest-length spring> and negligible connector drag. For $\mathbf R=\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2$, its elastic <forces> on the two beads are
$$
\mathbf F_1=-H\mathbf R,\qquad \mathbf F_2=+H\mathbf R.
$$
Here $H>0$ is the <spring constant>. No independent thermal forcing from <Brownian motion> or bead <inertia> is included. The unperturbed <linear velocity field> is $\mathbf L\mathbf r$, with $\operatorname{tr}\mathbf L=0$ by <incompressibility>.

The fluid <velocity> used in each bead's drag law is the imposed flow plus the disturbance from the *other* bead, with its own singular field omitted. At leading order in $a/R$, the evenness of the <Oseen tensor>, $\mathsf G(-\mathbf R)=\mathsf G(\mathbf R)$, gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot{\mathbf r}_1
&=\mathbf L\mathbf r_1+\mathsf G(\mathbf R)\mathbf F_2
+\frac{\mathbf F_1}{\zeta},\\
\dot{\mathbf r}_2
&=\mathbf L\mathbf r_2+\mathsf G(\mathbf R)\mathbf F_1
+\frac{\mathbf F_2}{\zeta},
\qquad \boxed{\zeta=6\pi\mu a}.
\end{aligned}
$$
These are the pair <hydrodynamic mobility matrix> relations. Their signs follow from balancing each spring <force> with $-\zeta(\dot{\mathbf r}_i-\mathbf u_{{\rm ext},i})$, the fluid drag on that bead.

Adding the equations cancels both the direct spring slips and the cross-induced <velocities>, since $\mathbf F_1+\mathbf F_2=0$. For the midpoint $\mathbf r_c=(\mathbf r_1+\mathbf r_2)/2$,
$$
\boxed{\dot{\mathbf r}_c=\mathbf L\mathbf r_c.}
$$
The midpoint therefore follows the undisturbed <linear velocity field>. Subtracting the equations instead gives the <hydrodynamic interaction of a Hookean dumbbell>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot{\mathbf R}
&=\mathbf L\mathbf R+
\left(\zeta^{-1}I-\mathsf G(\mathbf R)\right)
(\mathbf F_1-\mathbf F_2)\\
&=\boxed{\mathbf L\mathbf R-\frac{2H}{\zeta}
\left[I-\frac{\zeta}{8\pi\mu R}
(I+\widehat{\mathbf R}\widehat{\mathbf R}^T)\right]\mathbf R}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The dimensionless interaction matrix is
$$
\zeta\mathsf G(\mathbf R)
=\frac{3a}{4R}(I+\widehat{\mathbf R}\widehat{\mathbf R}^T).
$$
Its transverse and longitudinal <eigenvalues> are $3a/(4R)$ and $3a/(2R)$, respectively. Since the spring <force> is longitudinal, the separation equation can also be written
$$
\boxed{\dot{\mathbf R}
=\mathbf L\mathbf R-\frac{2H}{\zeta}
\left(1-\frac{3a}{2R}\right)\mathbf R.}
$$
The relative correction to the free-draining spring relaxation rate is therefore $3a/(2R)$, explicitly proportional to $a/R$. Entrainment by the other bead reduces their relative approach for given opposing spring <forces>.

This is a far-field <hydrodynamic interaction>, valid for $R\gg a$; it is not a near-contact law and omits higher reflections and lubrication. The printed target also fixes the spring's rest length implicitly. For an ordinary <spring> obeying <Hooke's law> with nonzero rest length $R_0$, the elastic <force> would be $-H(R-R_0)\widehat{\mathbf R}$, and every spring term above would acquire the factor $1-R_0/R$. The displayed result uses the <zero-rest-length spring> convention appropriate to the ideal <Hookean dumbbell model>.
