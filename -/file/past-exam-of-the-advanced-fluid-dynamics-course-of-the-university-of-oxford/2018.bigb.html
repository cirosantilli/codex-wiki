= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $\widehat{\mathbf u}$ and $\widehat p$ be the complex <plane wave> amplitudes and set $k_x=k_\perp$, $k_z=k_\parallel$. The <linearized Euler equations> in the rotating frame are
$$
\begin{aligned}
-i\omega\widehat u_x&=-ik_\perp\widehat p+2\Omega\widehat u_y,\\
-i\omega\widehat u_y&=-2\Omega\widehat u_x,\\
-i\omega\widehat u_z&=-ik_\parallel\widehat p,\\
k_\perp\widehat u_x+k_\parallel\widehat u_z&=0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $p$ is <pressure> per unit <mass density>, including the centrifugal potential. The last line is <incompressibility>, not an additional equation of state.

Taking the <curl> eliminates <pressure>. For constant $\boldsymbol\Omega$ and a <divergence-free vector field>, $\nabla\times(2\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\Omega)=2(\boldsymbol\Omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u$. Thus the <inertial-wave vorticity equation> gives
$$
-i\omega(i\mathbf k\times\widehat{\mathbf u})
=2i\Omega k_\parallel\widehat{\mathbf u},\qquad
\omega\mathbf k\times\widehat{\mathbf u}=2i\Omega k_\parallel\widehat{\mathbf u}.
$$
Apply $\mathbf k\times$ once more and use $\mathbf k\times(\mathbf k\times\widehat{\mathbf u})=-k^2\widehat{\mathbf u}$. For a nonzero oscillatory amplitude,
$$
-\omega k^2\widehat{\mathbf u}
=2i\Omega k_\parallel\frac{2i\Omega k_\parallel}{\omega}\widehat{\mathbf u},
$$
so the <inertial-wave dispersion relation> is
$$
\boxed{\omega^2=4\Omega^2\frac{k_\parallel^2}{k^2},\qquad
\omega_\sigma=\sigma\,2\Omega\frac{k_\parallel}{k},\quad\sigma=\pm1.}
$$
The restoring influence is the <Coriolis acceleration>. In particular $|\omega|\le2|\Omega|$. For $k_\parallel=0$, the projected linear dynamics have zero <frequency>; these are steady balanced disturbances rather than the nonzero-frequency branch used in the division above. The spatially uniform $k=0$ mode must be treated separately from this <dispersion relation>.

= Solution

The <group velocity>, which gives wave-packet and <energy> propagation, is the gradient of the <dispersion relation>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf v_g=\nabla_{\mathbf k}\omega_\sigma
=\frac{\sigma\,2\Omega}{k}\left(\widehat{\mathbf z}-\frac{k_\parallel}{k^2}\mathbf k\right).}
$$
For $\mathbf k=(k_\perp,0,k_\parallel)$ this becomes
$$
v_{g,x}=-\sigma\,2\Omega\frac{k_\perp k_\parallel}{k^3},\qquad
v_{g,z}=\sigma\,2\Omega\frac{k_\perp^2}{k^3},\qquad
\frac{v_{g,x}}{v_{g,z}}=-\frac{k_\parallel}{k_\perp}.
$$
Thus for $\epsilon=|k_\parallel|/k_\perp\ll1$, <energy> travels almost along the rotation axis, with a small horizontal component. By contrast, the normal-to-phase-front <phase velocity> $\mathbf v_{\rm ph}=\omega\mathbf k/k^2$ is almost horizontal. Indeed $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf v_g=0$, so these phase and group directions are perpendicular. Specifying which meaning of propagation is intended matters for an <inertial wave>.

For the <wave polarization>, <incompressibility> and the $y$ equation in part <1/a> give
$$
\boxed{\widehat u_x=-\frac{k_\parallel}{k_\perp}\widehat u_z,\qquad
\widehat u_y=\frac{\sigma i k}{k_\perp}\widehat u_z.}
$$
The horizontal component parallel to $\mathbf k_\perp$ is small, but $|\widehat u_y|\simeq|\widehat u_z|$: the other horizontal and vertical components are in quadrature. More exactly, introduce the orthonormal transverse basis
$$
\mathbf e_1=\widehat{\mathbf y},\qquad
\mathbf e_2=\widehat{\mathbf k}\times\widehat{\mathbf y}
=\frac1k(-k_\parallel,0,k_\perp).
$$
Then $\widehat{\mathbf u}=A(\mathbf e_1-\sigma i\mathbf e_2)$. The physical <velocity> at a fixed point traces a circle in the plane perpendicular to $\mathbf k$; its handedness reverses between branches. In the anisotropic limit this plane is nearly vertical. The <group velocity and polarization of an inertial wave> therefore have quite different geometric roles.

= Solution

Let $L_\perp=k_\perp^{-1}$ and let $U$ be the common size of horizontal and vertical <velocity> perturbations, as found in part <1/b>. The linear <inertial wave> time and nonlinear advection time are
$$
\tau_w^{-1}\sim2\Omega\frac{|k_\parallel|}{k_\perp},\qquad
\tau_{\rm nl}^{-1}\sim k_\perp U.
$$
The <critical balance in a rapidly rotating fluid> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\epsilon=\frac{|k_\parallel|}{k_\perp}
\sim\frac{k_\perp U}{2\Omega}\ll1,\qquad
\omega\sim k_\perp U\sim2\Omega\epsilon.}
$$
The <Rossby number> is of order $\epsilon$. Horizontal <geostrophic flow> balances the leading <pressure gradient> against <Coriolis acceleration>, so
$$
\boxed{u_\perp\sim u_\parallel\sim U,\quad
\delta p\sim2\Omega L_\perp U\sim U^2/\epsilon,\quad
k_\parallel\sim\epsilon k_\perp.}
$$
These are dimensionally comparable statements, rather than comparisons of a dimensional <frequency> with a dimensional <velocity>. Equivalently, use $L_\perp$ and $(2\Omega)^{-1}$ as units. Then
$$
k_\perp L_\perp=O(1),\quad
k_\parallel L_\perp=O(\epsilon),\quad
\frac{\omega}{2\Omega}=O(\epsilon),\quad
\frac{u_\perp}{2\Omega L_\perp},\frac{u_\parallel}{2\Omega L_\perp}=O(\epsilon),\quad
\frac{\delta p}{(2\Omega L_\perp)^2}=O(\epsilon).
$$
Thus rotation stays $O(1)$ in these units, while the motions evolve on the slower $O(\epsilon^{-1})$ time. Vertical advection $u_\parallel\partial_z$ is smaller than horizontal advection by $\epsilon$.

Write $\mathbf u_\perp=\mathbf u_\perp^{(0)}+\mathbf u_\perp^{(1)}+\cdots$, with $\mathbf u_\perp^{(1)}=O(\epsilon U)$, and $p=p_0+p_1+\cdots$. At leading horizontal order,
$$
0=-\nabla_\perp p_0+2\Omega\mathbf u_\perp^{(0)}\times\widehat{\mathbf z}.
$$
Define the <stream function> $\Phi=p_0/(2\Omega)$, including its horizontally uniform part in this convention. Then
$$
\boxed{\mathbf u_\perp^{(0)}=\widehat{\mathbf z}\times\nabla_\perp\Phi
=(-\partial_y\Phi,\partial_x\Phi),\qquad
\nabla_\perp\cdot\mathbf u_\perp^{(0)}=0.}
$$
The next-order <incompressibility> condition is not zero horizontal divergence:
$$
\boxed{\nabla_\perp\cdot\mathbf u_\perp^{(1)}=-\partial_z u_\parallel.}
$$
This term has size $\epsilon k_\perp U$ and is essential: its product with the large rotation rate is a leading contribution to the vertical <vorticity> equation.

To see that contribution explicitly, write $D_\perp=\partial_t+\mathbf u_\perp^{(0)}\cdot\nabla_\perp$. At the first horizontal inertial order,
$$
D_\perp\mathbf u_\perp^{(0)}=-\nabla_\perp p_1
+2\Omega\mathbf u_\perp^{(1)}\times\widehat{\mathbf z}.
$$
Taking its vertical <curl> eliminates $p_1$. The two-dimensional advection identity and the two divergence orders give
$$
D_\perp\zeta=-2\Omega\nabla_\perp\cdot\mathbf u_\perp^{(1)}
=2\Omega\partial_z u_\parallel,\qquad
\zeta=(\nabla_\perp\times\mathbf u_\perp^{(0)})_z=\nabla_\perp^2\Phi.
$$
Equivalently the <ageostrophic flow> satisfies
$$
\mathbf u_\perp^{(1)}=\frac{\widehat{\mathbf z}\times[D_\perp\mathbf u_\perp^{(0)}+\nabla_\perp p_1]}{2\Omega},
$$
whose divergence is $-D_\perp\zeta/(2\Omega)$. This explicitly verifies that the next-order divergence has the required sign.

The vertical momentum equation, at the same slow order, is
$$
D_\perp u_\parallel=-\partial_zp_0=-2\Omega\partial_z\Phi.
$$
Finally $\mathbf u_\perp^{(0)}\cdot\nabla_\perp f=\{\Phi,f\}$, where the canonical two-dimensional <Poisson bracket> is $\{f,g\}=f_xg_y-f_yg_x$. The <reduced equations for an anisotropic rotating fluid> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\partial_t\nabla_\perp^2\Phi+\{\Phi,\nabla_\perp^2\Phi\}
&=2\Omega\partial_z u_\parallel,\\
\partial_tu_\parallel+\{\Phi,u_\parallel\}
&=-2\Omega\partial_z\Phi.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The omitted vertical-advection, ageostrophic-advection and relative-vorticity stretching terms are smaller by $O(\epsilon)$ in the equations retained here. Linearizing these equations recovers $\omega^2=4\Omega^2k_\parallel^2/k_\perp^2$, the anisotropic limit of part <1/a>.

Within the <reduced equations for an anisotropic rotating fluid>, a flow that remains purely horizontal has $u_\parallel=0$ and $\partial_tu_\parallel=0$. The second reduced equation therefore requires
$$
0=-2\Omega\partial_z\Phi,\qquad
\boxed{\partial_z\Phi=0,\quad\partial_z\mathbf u_\perp^{(0)}=0.}
$$
Thus the retained horizontal motion is two-dimensional. This is a <Taylor–Proudman theorem> consequence in the slow balanced ordering; setting $u_\parallel=0$ at only one instant would not suffice, because vertical motion could subsequently develop.

The word “exactly” applies to the reduced model. It is not a theorem for every finite-<Rossby number> solution satisfying only $\Omega\gg kU$. For example, the full rotating <Euler equation> admits
$$
\mathbf u=A\cos(kz)(\cos2\Omega t,-\sin2\Omega t,0),\qquad p=\text{constant}.
$$
It is an <incompressible flow>, has zero nonlinear advection, and obeys $\partial_t\mathbf u=2\mathbf u\times\boldsymbol\Omega$. It is purely horizontal but depends on $z$, and $\Omega\gg kA$ can hold. This fast inertial oscillation lies outside the slow anisotropic <critical balance> used in part <1/c>; it shows why that ordering cannot be dropped from the conclusion.

= Solution
{parent=e}

For a uniform axial <magnetic field>, the anisotropic <shear Alfvén wave> is predominantly horizontally polarized, whereas the <pseudo-Alfvén wave> is predominantly vertically polarized. Without rotation both have characteristic <frequency> $\omega_A\sim|k_\parallel|v_A$. Pressure projection makes the effective <Coriolis acceleration> couple these two transverse <wave polarization> states at the inertial-wave rate
$$
\omega_I\sim2\Omega\frac{|k_\parallel|}{k_\perp}.
$$
Thus the useful comparison is not $\Omega$ alone, nor anisotropy alone, but
$$
\boxed{\frac{\omega_I}{\omega_A}\sim\frac{2\Omega}{k_\perp v_A}\ll1.}
$$
In this limit <rotational coupling of shear and pseudo-Alfvén waves> is negligible on an Alfvén propagation time, so the two familiar linear polarizations are decoupled to leading order. The factor $k_\parallel$ cancels: simply making both disturbances more anisotropic does not by itself remove their relative rotational coupling.

For a nonlinear reduction one must also make the rotational mixing slow compared with the nonlinear evolution being retained. Under Alfvénic <critical balance>, $|k_\parallel|v_A\sim k_\perp U$, the same inequality gives $\omega_I\ll k_\perp U$. Rapid rotation relative to the horizontal nonlinear rate can therefore coexist with leading Alfvénic decoupling in the window
$$
\boxed{k_\perp U\ll2\Omega\ll k_\perp v_A.}
$$
High <electrical conductivity> makes magnetic diffusion slow, and the corresponding inviscid approximation is also assumed. If $2\Omega\gtrsim k_\perp v_A$, rotational and magnetic restoring effects are comparable and the modes are mixed magneto-Coriolis disturbances; in the rotation-dominated limit they separate into fast inertial-like and slow magnetostrophic branches, rather than into independently evolving shear and pseudo-Alfvén polarizations.

At finite amplitude, independence is generally one-way in the usual anisotropic <ideal magnetohydrodynamics> reduction: the Alfvénic subsystem has no leading feedback from pseudo-Alfvénic perturbations, but it can still advect them. The small rotational-coupling condition removes an additional two-way coupling, rather than all nonlinear <advection>.

This is an asymptotic finite-timescale statement. The two nonrotating Alfvén polarizations are degenerate, so even a weak nonzero rotational coupling can rotate their superposition over the much longer time $\omega_I^{-1}$. Exact decoupling for arbitrarily long times does not follow from a small ratio alone. The strictly $k_\parallel=0$ nonpropagating limit is separate.

= Solution

Use the same constant <dynamic viscosity> $\mu$ for both incompressible <Stokes flows>, and let $\mathbf f^{(a)}$ be <body force> per unit volume. Their equations and <Newtonian fluid stress tensor> are
$$
\partial_j\sigma^{(a)}_{ij}+f^{(a)}_i=0,\qquad
\partial_i u^{(a)}_i=0,\qquad
\sigma^{(a)}_{ij}=-p^{(a)}\delta_{ij}+2\mu e^{(a)}_{ij},
$$
where $e^{(a)}_{ij}=(\partial_i u^{(a)}_j+\partial_j u^{(a)}_i)/2$ is the <strain-rate tensor>. Consider the cross-work flux
$$
Q_j=u_i^{(2)}\sigma^{(1)}_{ij}-u_i^{(1)}\sigma^{(2)}_{ij}.
$$
By the product rule,
$$
\partial_jQ_j
=(\partial_j u_i^{(2)})\sigma^{(1)}_{ij}
-(\partial_j u_i^{(1)})\sigma^{(2)}_{ij}
-u_i^{(2)}f_i^{(1)}+u_i^{(1)}f_i^{(2)}.
$$
The pressure terms vanish by <incompressibility>. Symmetry of the <stress tensor> removes antisymmetric <velocity gradient> contributions, leaving $2\mu e^{(1)}:e^{(2)}$ in both cross terms; they cancel. Apply the <divergence theorem>, with $\mathbf n$ outward from $V$, and rearrange:
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\int_V\mathbf f^{(1)}\cdot\mathbf u^{(2)}\,dV
+\int_{\partial V}\mathbf u^{(2)}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma^{(1)}\mathbf n\,dS
={}&\int_V\mathbf f^{(2)}\cdot\mathbf u^{(1)}\,dV\\
&+\int_{\partial V}\mathbf u^{(1)}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma^{(2)}\mathbf n\,dS.
\end{aligned}}
$$
This is the <Lorentz reciprocal theorem with body forces>. It depends on reciprocal linear viscous response in the same fluid; arbitrary unrelated constitutive laws would not supply the cross-term cancellation.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Write the supplied <velocity> in the radial form
$$
\mathbf u=f(r)\mathbf U+g(r)(\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf x)\mathbf x,\qquad
f=\frac{3a}{4r}+\frac{a^3}{4r^3},\quad
g=\frac{3a}{4r^3}-\frac{3a^3}{4r^5}.
$$
At $r=a$, $f=1$ and $g=0$, giving the <no-slip boundary condition> $\mathbf u=\mathbf U$. At infinity $\mathbf u\to0$ and the supplied gauge-fixed <pressure> tends to zero. These are the laboratory-frame conditions for a sphere translating through otherwise quiescent fluid.

The exterior <Stokes equations> can also be checked directly:
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=(\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf x)\left(\frac{f'}r+rg'+4g\right)=0,
$$
and
$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla^2\mathbf u
&=\left(f''+\frac{2f'}r+2g\right)\mathbf U
+\left(g''+\frac{6g'}r\right)(\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf x)\mathbf x\\
&=\frac{3a}{2}\left[\frac{\mathbf U}{r^3}
-\frac{3(\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf x)\mathbf x}{r^5}\right]
=\frac1\mu\nabla p.
\end{aligned}
$$
To compute <surface traction in translating-sphere Stokes flow>, let $\mathbf n=\mathbf x/a$ point from the sphere into the fluid. At the surface, $f'(a)=-3/(2a)$, $g'(a)=3/(2a^3)$ and $g(a)=0$. Hence
$$
\left.\partial_j u_i\right|_{a}
=\frac{3}{2a}\left[-U_i n_j+(\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf n)n_i n_j\right],\qquad
p(a)=\frac{3\mu}{2a}(\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf n).
$$
Substitute into the <Newtonian fluid stress tensor>. Its viscous contribution to the surface <traction> is $3\mu[-\mathbf U+(\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf n)\mathbf n]/(2a)$, so the normal part cancels the <pressure> contribution:
$$
\boxed{\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf n=-\frac{3\mu}{2a}\mathbf U.}
$$
The vector is constant over the entire sphere in the stated pressure gauge. Integrating it gives the fluid's <drag force> on the sphere,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F_H=\int_{r=a}\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf n\,dS
=-\frac{3\mu}{2a}(4\pi a^2)\mathbf U=-6\pi\mu a\mathbf U.}
$$
This is <Stokes's law>. On an exterior fluid domain the outward domain normal at its inner boundary is $-\mathbf n$; that distinction will matter in the reciprocal calculation.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Let $S_a$ be the sphere surface, $\mathbf n_s$ point from the sphere into the fluid, and write
$$
\mathbf w=\mathbf u_S-\mathbf u_\infty,\qquad
\boldsymbol\tau=\boldsymbol\sigma_S-\boldsymbol\sigma_\infty.
$$
Because the prescribed exterior <body forces> are unchanged, they cancel on subtraction: the disturbance is a homogeneous <Stokes flow> in the exterior domain. On the sphere the <no-slip boundary condition> is
$$
\mathbf w=\mathbf V+\boldsymbol\omega_s\times\mathbf x-\mathbf u_\infty,
$$
allowing an arbitrary sphere rotation $\boldsymbol\omega_s$.

Use as auxiliary flow the translating-sphere solution of part <2/b>, with arbitrary test <velocity> $\mathbf U$. Its surface <velocity> is $\mathbf U$ and its surface <traction> is $-3\mu\mathbf U/(2a)$. Applying the <Lorentz reciprocal theorem> on the exterior domain gives, after cancelling the common inner-normal minus sign,
$$
\int_{S_a}\mathbf w\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma^*\mathbf n_s\,dS
=\int_{S_a}\mathbf U\cdot\boldsymbol\tau\mathbf n_s\,dS.
$$
The outer surface terms vanish for decaying disturbances: velocities are at most $O(r^{-1})$, stresses $O(r^{-2})$, and the integrated cross-work is $O(r^{-1})$.

There are no <body forces> inside the sphere in the original ambient problem. Thus the smooth ambient <stress tensor> has zero divergence there, and
$$
\int_{S_a}\boldsymbol\sigma_\infty\mathbf n_s\,dS
=\int_{r<a}\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma_\infty\,dV=0.
$$
Also $\int_{S_a}\mathbf x\,dS=0$, so the rotation term drops out of the left side. Consequently the reciprocal identity reduces to
$$
-\frac{3\mu}{2a}\mathbf U\cdot
\left[4\pi a^2\mathbf V-\int_{S_a}\mathbf u_\infty\,dS\right]
=\mathbf U\cdot\mathbf F_H.
$$
Since $\mathbf U$ is arbitrary, the result before imposing mechanical balance is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F_H=-6\pi\mu a\left[\mathbf V-
\frac1{4\pi a^2}\int_{S_a}\mathbf u_\infty\,dS\right].}
$$
For a freely suspended sphere with negligible inertia and no applied external <force>, $\mathbf F_H=0$. Its <sphere velocity as a surface average of ambient Stokes flow> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbf V=\frac1{4\pi a^2}\int_{|\mathbf x|=a}\mathbf u_\infty(\mathbf x)\,dS.}
$$
The implicit force-free condition is essential. More generally, an external <force> $\mathbf F_{\rm ext}$ gives $\mathbf V=\langle\mathbf u_\infty\rangle_{S_a}+\mathbf F_{\rm ext}/(6\pi\mu a)$. For example, an externally pulled sphere in the zero ambient flow has nonzero <velocity>, contrary to an unrestricted surface-average-only claim. No torque-free assumption is needed for this translational identity, because the auxiliary surface traction is constant.

= Solution
{parent=d}

The undisturbed ambient solution is smooth and free of <body forces> throughout the ball $r\le a$. Its <Stokes equations> imply
$$
\mu\nabla^2\mathbf u_\infty=\nabla p_\infty,\qquad
\nabla\cdot\mathbf u_\infty=0.
$$
Taking the divergence gives $\nabla^2p_\infty=0$, and a further <Laplacian> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\nabla^4\mathbf u_\infty=\frac1\mu\nabla(\nabla^2p_\infty)=0.}
$$
Each <velocity> component obeys the <biharmonic equation> in the whole ball.

Expand a component of $\mathbf u_\infty$ at the centre and average over $\mathbf x=a\mathbf n$. The odd spherical moments vanish, while
$$
\langle n_i n_j\rangle=\frac13\delta_{ij},\qquad
\langle n_i n_j n_k n_l\rangle
=\frac1{15}(\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}).
$$
Thus the <spherical mean of a biharmonic function> gives
$$
\langle\mathbf u_\infty\rangle_{S_a}
=\left[1+\frac{a^2}{6}\nabla^2+\frac{a^4}{120}\nabla^4+\cdots\right]
\mathbf u_\infty(0)
=\mathbf u_\infty(0)+\frac{a^2}{6}\nabla^2\mathbf u_\infty(0).
$$
The truncation is exact, not just a small-sphere approximation: all terms containing two or more Laplacians vanish. One can avoid any Taylor-convergence issue by letting $M(r)=\langle\mathbf u_\infty(r\mathbf n)\rangle$. Since $\nabla^2\mathbf u_\infty$ is harmonic, its <spherical mean> is $\nabla^2\mathbf u_\infty(0)$, so
$$
M''+\frac2rM'=\nabla^2\mathbf u_\infty(0),\qquad M(0)=\mathbf u_\infty(0),\quad M'(0)=0.
$$
Integrating gives $M(r)=\mathbf u_\infty(0)+r^2\nabla^2\mathbf u_\infty(0)/6$ for every admissible $r$.

Combining with the force-free result of part <2/c> proves <Faxén's first law> in the requested form:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf V=\left.\left(1+\frac{a^2}{6}\nabla^2\right)\mathbf u_\infty\right|_{\mathbf x=0}.}
$$
The force-free qualification carries over. Local absence of ambient <body forces> and regularity inside the ball are also needed; if forcing or a singularity lies inside it, the harmonic-pressure and biharmonic-mean argument does not apply unchanged.
