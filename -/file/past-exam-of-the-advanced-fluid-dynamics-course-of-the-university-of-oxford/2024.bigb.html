= Solution
{parent=a}

Start in an inertial frame with the inviscid <magnetohydrodynamic momentum equation>,
$$
\rho D_t\mathbf v=-\nabla P+\mathbf J\times\mathbf B-\rho\nabla\Phi.
$$
Here $\rho$ is <mass density>, $P$ is the isotropic fluid <pressure>, $\mathbf v$ is inertial-frame <velocity>, $\mathbf J$ is <electric current density>, $\mathbf B$ is the <magnetic field>, and $\Phi$ is <gravitational potential> per unit <mass>. In the low-frequency approximation to <Ampère's law>, $\mathbf J=\nabla\times\mathbf B/\mu_0$. The <vector calculus identity>
$$
(\nabla\times\mathbf B)\times\mathbf B=(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B-\nabla(B^2/2)
$$
separates the <Lorentz force density> into <magnetic tension> and a <magnetic pressure> gradient. Transform the material <acceleration> to a rigid frame rotating with constant <angular velocity> $\boldsymbol\Omega_0$, using the stated frame transformation. The <rotating-frame magnetohydrodynamic momentum equation> is
$$
\boxed{\rho D_t\mathbf u=-\nabla\left(P+\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}\right)+\frac{(\mathbf B\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B}{\mu_0}-\rho\nabla\Phi-2\rho\boldsymbol\Omega_0\times\mathbf u-\rho\boldsymbol\Omega_0\times(\boldsymbol\Omega_0\times\mathbf r).}
$$
The final terms are the <Coriolis force> and <centrifugal force> densities; $\mathbf u$ is the <velocity> relative to the rotating frame, and $D_t=\partial_t+\mathbf u\cdot\nabla$ uses that frame.

This requires a smooth conducting-fluid continuum with nonrelativistic motion, a constant <magnetic permeability> $\mu_0$, the <solenoidal magnetic-field constraint>, a negligible <displacement current> and negligible net electric-charge force. Viscous forces and anisotropic fluid stresses are omitted. A time-dependent frame would add an <Euler force>. <Incompressible flow> is not needed for this force equation. Finite <electrical resistivity> does not change its displayed instantaneous magnetic force; the later frozen-field calculation additionally uses <ideal magnetohydrodynamics>. A differentially rotating fluid does not define a single rigid frame with spatially varying $\boldsymbol\Omega_0$: choose a fixed local reference rate and retain the fluid's shear in $\mathbf u$.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

In an inertial frame, a <circular orbit> at radius $R$ has radial <acceleration> $-R\Omega(R)^2\mathbf e_R$. Neglecting fluid and magnetic forces gives
$$
-\rho R\Omega(R)^2=-\rho\frac{d\Phi}{dR},\qquad\boxed{\frac{d\Phi}{dR}=R\Omega(R)^2.}
$$
In a local rotating frame with $\Omega_0=\Omega(R_0)$, the fluid is instantaneously at rest at the reference radius $R_0$. The radial <centrifugal force> $+\rho R_0\Omega_0^2$ then balances the inward <gravitational force>. Away from $R_0$, the fluid's nonzero rotating-frame <velocity> must also be retained; the entire differentially rotating disk is not at rest in that one frame.

= c
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Take outward radial <force> as positive and keep the displaced element's angular rate fixed at $\Omega_0=\Omega(R_0)$. Its imbalance is
$$
\Delta f_R=\rho\left[(R_0+\xi_R)\Omega_0^2-\Phi'(R_0+\xi_R)\right].
$$
Use the equilibrium relation from part <1/b>, $\Phi'(R)=R\Omega(R)^2$, and a first-order <Taylor expansion>:
$$
\boxed{\Delta f_R=-\rho R_0\left.\frac{d\Omega^2}{dR}\right|_{R_0}\xi_R+O(\xi_R^2),\qquad
|\Delta f_R|\simeq\rho\left|R_0\frac{d\Omega^2}{dR}\xi_R\right|.}
$$
The absolute values are needed for a magnitude. As printed, the proposed magnitude has a signed right side; it would be negative for outward displacement in a <Keplerian disk>. For decreasing $\Omega^2$, the signed imbalance above points outward and therefore amplifies that fixed-angular-rate displacement. This calculation fixes the angular rate, rather than the <specific angular momentum>; the latter is the conserved quantity for an uncoupled inviscid radial displacement and leads to the <Rayleigh centrifugal stability criterion>.

= d
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

Linearized <magnetic flux freezing> gives the Eulerian <magnetic field> perturbation
$$
\delta\mathbf B=\nabla\times(\boldsymbol\xi\times\mathbf B_0)=(\mathbf B_0\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol\xi-\mathbf B_0\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\xi.
$$
The background is uniform, and <incompressibility> gives $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\xi=0$. For a horizontal displacement proportional to $e^{ikz}$, $\delta\mathbf B=ikB_0\boldsymbol\xi$ and $\delta B_z=0$. Thus the first-order <magnetic pressure> perturbation vanishes, and the <magnetic-tension response to an incompressible displacement> is
$$
\boxed{\delta\mathbf f=\frac{B_0}{\mu_0}\partial_z\delta\mathbf B=-\frac{k^2B_0^2}{\mu_0}\boldsymbol\xi.}
$$
Bending a field line produces a restoring <force> proportional to displacement, like a spring obeying <Hooke's law>. Per unit <mass>, its squared spring <frequency> is $k^2v_A^2$, where $v_A=B_0/\sqrt{\mu_0\rho}$ is the <Alfvén speed>. The equilibrium can be independent of $z$ while the perturbation varies with $z$; neglect of background vertical structure does not set this perturbation's <wavenumber> to zero.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=e}

Work locally at $R_0$ with $\Omega=\Omega(R_0)$, and define
$$
D=\left.\frac{d\Omega^2}{d\ln R}\right|_{R_0},\qquad a=k^2v_A^2,\qquad v_A=\frac{B_0}{\sqrt{\mu_0\rho}},\qquad\kappa^2=4\Omega^2+D.
$$
Here $\kappa$ is the <radial epicyclic frequency>. Combining the radial imbalance, the <Coriolis force> and the magnetic spring gives the <spring model of magnetorotational instability>:
$$
\ddot\xi_R-2\Omega\dot\xi_\phi=-(D+a)\xi_R,\qquad
\ddot\xi_\phi+2\Omega\dot\xi_R=-a\xi_\phi.
$$
For a <normal mode> proportional to $e^{-i\omega t}$, the coefficient <matrix> is
$$
\begin{pmatrix}a+D-\omega^2&2i\Omega\omega\\-2i\Omega\omega&a-\omega^2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Setting its <determinant> to zero yields the <ideal magnetorotational dispersion relation>,
$$
\boxed{\omega^4-\omega^2(2a+4\Omega^2+D)+a(a+D)=0.}
$$
Its two values of $\omega^2$ are
$$
\omega_\pm^2=a+\frac{\kappa^2}{2}\pm\frac12\sqrt{(\kappa^2)^2+16a\Omega^2},
$$
so they are real. For $a>0$, their product is negative exactly when $a+D<0$, giving an exponentially growing <normal mode>. Conversely, $a+D>0$ also makes their sum positive and therefore gives two positive squared <frequencies>. **A fixed nonzero magnetic mode is exponentially unstable for $k^2v_A^2+D<0$, oscillatory for $k^2v_A^2+D>0$, and marginal at equality.** Stability against every allowed nonzero <wavenumber> in the unbounded ideal model requires $D\ge0$; finite-domain restrictions can remove the unstable band.

For $\Phi=-GM/R$, the equilibrium relation gives $\Omega^2=GM/R^3$, so $D=-3\Omega^2$ and $\kappa^2=\Omega^2$. Hence the <Keplerian disk> has the <magnetorotational instability> band
$$
\boxed{0<k^2v_A^2<3\Omega^2.}
$$
Without the <magnetic field>, the polynomial becomes $\omega^2(\omega^2-\kappa^2)=0$. The <Rayleigh centrifugal stability criterion> instead requires $\kappa^2>0$, equivalently $d(R^4\Omega^2)/dR>0$. A <Keplerian disk> satisfies that hydrodynamic criterion. Magnetic coupling therefore destabilizes a disk that has a restoring hydrodynamic <epicyclic oscillation>; the field couples angular-momentum exchange between elements, rather than acting only as an isolated restoring spring.

The plotted maximum is the <maximum growth rate of ideal magnetorotational instability>, $3\Omega/4$ at $k^2v_A^2=15\Omega^2/16$.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Using the given linear momentum balance, multiply by the <velocity> and integrate over the fixed volume:
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_V\frac12\rho u_iu_i\,dV=\int_Vu_i\partial_j\sigma_{ij}\,dV
=\int_{\partial V}u_i\sigma_{ij}n_j\,dS-\int_V\sigma_{ij}\partial_j u_i\,dV.
$$
This is <integration by parts> component by component, or the <divergence theorem>. The first term is boundary <stress> power. With component-first <velocity gradient> $K_{ij}=\partial_j u_i$, the second is the <double contraction> $\Phi=\sigma:K$.

For a <Newtonian fluid>, $\sigma=-pI+2\mu e$, where $e=(K+K^T)/2$ is the <rate-of-strain tensor> and $\mu\ge0$ is <dynamic viscosity>. <Incompressibility> removes the pressure contribution, and the <symmetric tensor> $e$ is orthogonal to the antisymmetric part of $K$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Phi=2\mu e:e=2\mu\sum_{ij}e_{ij}^2\ge0.}
$$
The displayed energy identity follows for the stated linear balance. A full advective momentum equation in a fixed control volume would also contribute an advective <kinetic energy> flux. The nonnegativity statement here is for a purely Newtonian viscous stress; a <viscoelastic> stress can exchange stored <free energy> with the flow.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let $K_{ij}=\partial_j u_i$, $C_{ij}=\langle R_iR_j\rangle$, $\alpha=2H/\zeta$ and $D_R=2k_BT/\zeta$. The configuration equation of the <Hookean dumbbell model> becomes
$$
\partial_t\psi=-\nabla_{\mathbf R}\cdot[(K\mathbf R-\alpha\mathbf R)\psi]+D_R\nabla_{\mathbf R}^2\psi.
$$
Here the paper's $k$ denotes the <Boltzmann constant> $k_B$. Integrating the <second moment> equation against $R_iR_j$ and discarding boundary terms at infinite separation gives
$$
\dot C_{ij}=K_{i\ell}C_{\ell j}+K_{j\ell}C_{i\ell}-2\alpha C_{ij}+2D_R\delta_{ij}.
$$
For example the spring drift differentiates each factor $R_i,R_j$, while the <Laplacian> gives $\nabla_{\mathbf R}^2(R_iR_j)=2\delta_{ij}$. The <Hookean dumbbell conformation equation> is
$$
\boxed{\dot C-KC-CK^T=\frac{4k_BT}{\zeta}I-\frac{4H}{\zeta}C.}
$$
In the row-vector convention of $\mathbf R\cdot\nabla\mathbf u$, the displayed gradient tensor is $K^T$, giving exactly the transposes in the requested equation. The dimensionless <conformation tensor> $A=HC/(k_BT)$ consequently has <upper-convected derivative> $-(A-I)/\lambda$, with <polymer relaxation time> $\lambda=\zeta/(4H)$. This normalization matters: the unscaled $C$ has equilibrium value $(k_BT/H)I$, rather than $I$.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Write the centre position as $\mathbf x$ and the bead positions as $\mathbf r_{1,2}=\mathbf x\mp\mathbf R/2$. In the <affine velocity field>, $\mathbf u(\mathbf r_{1,2})=\mathbf u(\mathbf x)\mp K\mathbf R/2$. The midpoint follows the flow, so its two slip <velocities> are
$$
\dot{\mathbf r}_{1,2}-\mathbf u(\mathbf r_{1,2})=\mp\frac12(\dot{\mathbf R}-K\mathbf R).
$$
Each bead loses mechanical power $\zeta$ times its squared slip <speed>. Adding the two powers proves the <drag dissipation of a bead-spring pair>:
$$
\boxed{D_{\rm pair}=\zeta\left|\frac{\dot{\mathbf R}-K\mathbf R}{2}\right|^2+\zeta\left|\frac{\dot{\mathbf R}-K\mathbf R}{2}\right|^2=\frac\zeta2|\dot{\mathbf R}-K\mathbf R|^2.}
$$
The factor one half comes from two equal-and-opposite bead slips, not from assigning only one bead a drag force.

= Solution
{parent=d}

**The claimed equality does not hold for the printed joint distribution.** The original PDF specifies a centered <Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution> of $\mathbf v=\dot{\mathbf R}$ independent of $\mathbf R$, with
$$
\langle v_i\rangle=0,\qquad\langle v_iv_j\rangle=\frac{2k_BT}{m}\delta_{ij}.
$$
Since the double average includes number density $n$, evaluating the <Gaussian integrals> gives
$$
\boxed{\left\langle\!\left\langle\frac\zeta2|\mathbf v-K\mathbf R|^2\right\rangle\!\right\rangle
=\frac{3n\zeta k_BT}{m}+\frac{n\zeta}{2}\operatorname{tr}(KCK^T).}
$$
The cross term vanishes because the conditional mean of $\mathbf v$ is zero. In particular, set $K=0$ and take the equilibrium configuration <probability density> $\psi_{\rm eq}\propto e^{-HR^2/(2k_BT)}$. The left side is $3n\zeta k_BT/m>0$, while $nHC:K=0$. This is a counterexample even at thermal equilibrium, so no rearrangement of the earlier <conformation tensor> equation can prove the printed statement.

The discrepancy is <thermal bead drag and heat-bath power>: gross viscous loss of microscopic thermal motion is replenished by the thermal bath. It is not identical to mechanical polymer stress power. Moreover, $\dot{\mathbf R}$ in the earlier configuration continuity equation is a <probability current velocity>, including a distribution-dependent osmotic drift; it cannot simultaneously be identified with an independently sampled centered Maxwellian velocity.

A consistent overdamped result is the <free-energy balance of a Hookean dumbbell>. Define $\mathbf q=H\mathbf R+k_BT\nabla_{\mathbf R}\log\psi$ and
$$
\mathcal F=nk_BT\int\psi\log\frac\psi{\psi_{\rm eq}}\,d\mathbf R,\qquad
\mathbf V=K\mathbf R-\frac2\zeta\mathbf q.
$$
Differentiate the <relative entropy> and use the configuration <Fokker-Planck probability current> $\psi\mathbf V$. After <integration by parts>,
$$
\dot{\mathcal F}=n\int\psi\,\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf V\,d\mathbf R
=nHC:K-\frac{2n}{\zeta}\int\psi|\mathbf q|^2\,d\mathbf R.
$$
The affine osmotic term is $nk_BT\int\nabla\psi\cdot K\mathbf R\,d\mathbf R=-nk_BT\operatorname{tr}K=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{nHC:K=\dot{\mathcal F}+\mathcal D,\qquad\mathcal D=\frac{2n}{\zeta}\int\psi|\mathbf q|^2\,d\mathbf R\ge0.}
$$
The polymer <stress tensor> $\sigma_p=n(HC-k_BTI)$ has $\sigma_p:K=nHC:K$ in an <incompressible flow>. It stores or releases <free energy> as well as dissipating it, so the total stress-work term in part <2/a> need not itself be a purely dissipative quantity for this <viscoelastic> material. Stress power equals $\mathcal D$ only for stationary <free energy>, and $\mathcal D$ uses the probability-current slip. Neither condition turns the printed independent-Maxwellian average into that expression.
