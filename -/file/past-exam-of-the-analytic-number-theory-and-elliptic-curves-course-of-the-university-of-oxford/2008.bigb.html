= Solution
{parent=2008/a/1}

A <multiplicative arithmetic function> has $f(1)=1$ and satisfies $f(mn)=f(m)f(n)$ whenever $m,n$ are coprime. For a <prime number>[prime] power $p^e$, the divisors are exactly $1,p,\ldots,p^e$, so there are $e+1$. Multiplicativity therefore gives
$$
\boxed{d(n)=\prod_{j=1}^k(e_j+1).}
$$
Fix $\varepsilon>0$. If $p\ge2^{1/\varepsilon}$, then $(e+1)p^{-\varepsilon e}\le(e+1)2^{-e}\le1$ for every <integer> $e\ge0$. There are only finitely many <prime numbers>[primes] below the threshold. For each such <prime number>[prime] the sequence $(e+1)p^{-\varepsilon e}$ tends to zero, so its supremum $C_p$ is finite and at least one. Thus
$$
\frac{d(n)}{n^\varepsilon}=\prod_{p^e\parallel n}(e+1)p^{-\varepsilon e}\le\prod_{p<2^{1/\varepsilon}}C_p=:C(\varepsilon).
$$
The product is over a fixed finite set, independent of $n$, proving $\boxed{d(n)\le C(\varepsilon)n^\varepsilon}$.

The change of divisor $k=m/d$ gives $\sigma(m)/m=\sum_{k\mid m}1/k$. List the divisors as $k_1<\cdots<k_{d(m)}$. They are distinct positive <integers>, so $k_j\ge j$ and $\sigma(m)/m\le\sum_{j\le d(m)}1/j$.

For an <integer> $K\ge1$, monotonicity of $1/x$ gives $\sum_{k=2}^K1/k\le\int_1^Kdx/x=\log K$; the same bound for real $K\ge1$ follows by taking its <integer> part. Consequently
$$
\frac{\sigma(m)}m\le1+\log d(m)\le\varepsilon\log m+1+\log C(\varepsilon).
$$
Hence the requested bound holds with $\boxed{K(\varepsilon)=1+\log C(\varepsilon)}$, including $m=1$.

For $\operatorname{Re}s>1$, the <Euler product> theorem is
$$
\boxed{F(s)=\prod_{p}\left(\sum_{e=0}^\infty f(p^e)p^{-es}\right).}
$$
The local series converge absolutely, and the product is <absolutely convergent> in the sense that the sum over <prime numbers>[primes] of the absolute deviations of the local factors from one is finite. Indeed this sum is bounded by $\sum_{n\ge2}|f(n)|n^{-\operatorname{Re}s}$. To prove the identity, expand the product over <prime numbers>[primes] at most $y$. <Absolute convergence> permits all those local expansions, and multiplicativity identifies the <coefficients>[coefficients] with $f(n)$ for <integers> whose <prime number>[prime] factors are at most $y$. Every omitted <integer> exceeds $y$, so the difference from the full <Dirichlet series> is bounded by its absolute tail beyond $y$, which tends to zero. The same tail bound on a closed right half-plane inside $\operatorname{Re}s>1$ proves local <uniform convergence>. With signed <coefficients>[coefficients] a local factor can be zero; the identity does not require claiming every such general <Euler product> is nonzero.

For $d(n^2)$, multiplicativity gives local <coefficients>[coefficients] $d(p^{2e})=2e+1$. With $z=p^{-s}$,
$$
\sum_{e\ge0}(2e+1)z^e=\frac{1+z}{(1-z)^2}=\frac{1-z^2}{(1-z)^3}.
$$
The divisor bound from (1), applied with a sufficiently small exponent, proves <absolute convergence> when $\operatorname{Re}s>1$; divergence at one follows from $d(n^2)\ge1$. Multiplying the local identities therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{d(n^2)}{n^s}=\frac{\zeta(s)^3}{\zeta(2s)}.}
$$
For $n\le x$, the inequality $n^\delta\le x^\delta$ gives $d(n^2)/n\le x^\delta d(n^2)/n^{1+\delta}$. Sum those nonnegative terms and enlarge to the whole infinite series to get the required comparison.

For real $1<s<2$, integral comparison gives $\zeta(s)\le1+(s-1)^{-1}\le2/(s-1)$, while $\zeta(2s)\ge1$. Hence $\zeta(s)^3/\zeta(2s)=O((s-1)^{-3})$ uniformly in that interval. Choosing $\delta=1/\log x$ for $x\ge3$ makes $0<\delta<1$ and $x^\delta=e$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\sum_{n\le x}\frac{d(n^2)}n=O((\log x)^3).}
$$

Each finite-interval integral $f_n(s)$ is entire in $s$: the integrand is $ (x-n)e^{-(s+1)\log x}$ and its complex derivatives may be integrated on the compact interval. On a compact subset of $\operatorname{Re}s>0$, choose $\delta>0$ with $\operatorname{Re}s\ge\delta$. Then $|f_n(s)|\le n^{-1-\delta}$. The Weierstrass uniform-convergence theorem gives convergence and holomorphy of $\sum_nf_n(s)$ throughout that half-plane.

<Integration by parts> gives
$$
sf_n(s)=-(n+1)^{-s}+\int_n^{n+1}x^{-s}\,dx.
$$
For $\operatorname{Re}s>1$, sum absolutely and use the integral over $[1,\infty)$ to obtain $s\sum_nf_n(s)=1-\zeta(s)+1/(s-1)$. Rearranging gives the first requested identity.

On $[n,n+1]$, the magnitude of $(x-n)(x-n-1)$ is at most $1/4$. On a compact subset of $\operatorname{Re}s>-1$, its integrals are bounded by a convergent constant multiple of $n^{-1-\delta}$ for some $\delta>0$. Thus $\sum_ng_n(s)$ is <holomorphic> there. The prefactor $s(s+1)$ is entire, leaving only the explicit <simple pole> at one in $\widetilde\zeta$, with <residue> one.

Differentiate $(x-n)(x-n-1)x^{-s-1}$ and integrate. Its endpoint values both vanish, giving
$$
\boxed{(s+1)g_n(s)-2f_n(s)=-\int_n^{n+1}x^{-s-1}\,dx=\frac{(n+1)^{-s}-n^{-s}}s.}
$$
At $s=0$ the last quotient has its continuous value $-\log((n+1)/n)$. For $\operatorname{Re}s>1$, telescoping over $n$ gives $(s+1)\sum_ng_n-2\sum_nf_n=-1/s$. Substitution into $\widetilde\zeta$ yields
$$
\widetilde\zeta(s)=\frac1{s-1}+1-s\sum_nf_n(s)=\frac s{s-1}-s\sum_nf_n(s)=\zeta(s).
$$
This supplies the <analytic continuation> to $\operatorname{Re}s>-1$ apart from the <pole>. Evaluating its <holomorphic> expression at zero makes the series term vanish and gives $\boxed{\zeta(0)=-1/2}$.

For $\operatorname{Re}s>1$, logarithmic differentiation of the <absolutely convergent> <Euler product> gives $-\zeta'(s)/\zeta(s)=\sum_{n\ge1}\Lambda(n)n^{-s}$, where $\Lambda(p^k)=\log p$ and it is zero otherwise. On the line $\operatorname{Re}s=c>1$, this series is absolutely bounded by $\sum_n(\log n)n^{-c}<\infty$, and $1/[s(s+1)]$ is integrable in the vertical variable. Hence <absolute convergence> justifies interchanging sum and <contour integral>. The given first Mellin kernel, with $y=x/n$, contributes $x(1-n/x)=x-n$ if $n\le x$ and zero otherwise. Thus
$$
\boxed{\psi_1(x)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}-\frac{\zeta'(s)}{\zeta(s)}\frac{x^{s+1}}{s(s+1)}\,ds.}
$$
Splitting off $1/(s-1)$ gives the desired decomposition with $f$ for $x\ge1$. There is a range misprint in the second supplied kernel: for $0<y<1$ its integral is zero, not $(y-1)^2/2$. The globally correct value is $(y-1)_+^2/2$. For $y\ge1$ it is exactly the expression used here, so the subsequent large-$x$ argument is unchanged.

Near one, write $h=s-1$ and $\zeta(s)=h^{-1}+\gamma+O(h)$. Then $-\zeta'/\zeta=h^{-1}-\gamma+O(h)$, so subtraction cancels the <pole> and $f$ has the removable value $\boxed{f(1)=-\gamma/2}$.

For a continuously differentiable $F$ with the given decay, both $F$ and $F'$ are integrable and $F(t)\to0$ at either end. <Integration by parts> on finite intervals and passage to the limit gives, for $x>1$,
$$
\int_{-\infty}^\infty F(t)x^{it}\,dt=-\frac1{i\log x}\int_{-\infty}^\infty F'(t)x^{it}\,dt.
$$
Its absolute value is at most $\|F'\|_1/\log x$, proving the estimate.

We also need uniform control of $f(c+it)$ on bounded $t$ as $c\downarrow1$. Here is the <three-four-one zero-free-region argument> supplying it. For $\sigma>1$, the <Euler product> and $3+4\cos u+\cos2u=2(1+\cos u)^2\ge0$ imply
$$
\zeta(\sigma)^3|\zeta(\sigma+it)|^4|\zeta(\sigma+2it)|\ge1.
$$
If $\zeta(1+it)$ vanished for a nonzero real $t$, holomorphy from (3) would make the left side $O((\sigma-1)^{-3+4})\to0$, since $\zeta(\sigma+2it)$ remains bounded, a contradiction. Thus there are no zeros on that line, and there are none to its right by the <Euler product>. After removal of the singularity at one, $f$ and $f'$ are bounded on the compact rectangle $1\le\operatorname{Re}s\le2$, $|\operatorname{Im}s|\le3$.

For $|t|\ge3$, put $H=-\zeta'/\zeta-1/(s-1)$. The supplied estimates give $H=O((\log|t|)^9)$ and
$$
H'=-\frac{\zeta''}{\zeta}+\left(\frac{\zeta'}{\zeta}\right)^2+\frac1{(s-1)^2}=O((\log|t|)^{18}),
$$
uniformly in $1<c<2$. Since $f=H/[s(s+1)]$, it follows that $f=O(t^{-2}(\log|t|)^9)$ and $f'=O(t^{-2}(\log|t|)^{18})$, both $O(|t|^{-3/2})$. Also $d f(c+it)/dt=if'(c+it)$. Together with the compact bound this proves both required decay bounds with constants independent of $c$.

Parametrizing the contour, the remainder in the decomposition is $x^{c+1}/(2\pi)$ times the <oscillatory integral> just bounded. Choose $c=1+1/\log x$ for $x>e$, so $x^{c+1}=ex^2$. Hence the remainder is $O(x^2/\log x)$. Finally $(x-1)^2/2=x^2/2+O(x)$, and $O(x)$ is absorbed, proving
$$
\boxed{\psi_1(x)=\frac{x^2}2+O\left(\frac{x^2}{\log x}\right).}
$$

For a nonsingular curve $y^2=x^3+Ax+B$ with <integers> $A,B$, the <Nagell–Lutz theorem> states that a finite <torsion subgroup>[rational torsion] point has <integer> coordinates and satisfies either $y=0$ or
$$
\boxed{y^2\mid4A^3+27B^2.}
$$
The point at infinity is the identity and is treated separately. Integrality is the result allowed in the source. To prove the remaining divisibility, suppose $y\ne0$. Then the doubled point is finite and also torsion, so its coordinates are integral. Its <tangent> slope is $m=(3x^2+A)/(2y)$ and its abscissa is $m^2-2x$. Thus $m^2$ is an <integer>. A <rational number> whose square is an <integer> is itself an <integer>, so $2y$ divides $3x^2+A$. In particular $y^2$ divides $\psi_1(x)=(3x^2+A)^2$. It also divides $\psi_2(x)=x^3+Ax+B=y^2$. Evaluating the permitted <polynomial> identity at the <integer> $x$ therefore proves the divisibility of $4A^3+27B^2$ by $y^2$.

For $y=0$, the point equals its negative and has order two. The theorem gives a finite set of torsion candidates, but the divisibility condition by itself does not assert that every candidate is torsion.

Set $d_m=e_m-e_{m-1}$. Starting from $d_1=0$, the inequality gives $|d_m|\le(m-1)C$. Summing from one to $m$ therefore yields
$$
\boxed{|h_x(mP)-m^2h_x(P)|\le\frac{m(m-1)}2C.}
$$
Thus $C_m=m(m-1)C/2$ works independently of $P$, including $m=1$.

\boxed{|a_m-a_n|\le\sum_{j=n}^\infty C4^{-j-1}=\frac C{3\cdot4^n}\longrightarrow0.}

Thus the sequence is <Cauchy sequence>[Cauchy] and converges by <completeness of the real numbers>. The estimate also gives a quantitative bound on its distance from the limit.

For <prime-power contraction in a formal group>, a one-dimensional <formal group> over $R$ has $F(X,Y)=X+Y+$ terms of total degree at least two with <coefficients>[coefficients] in $R$. Induction under composition therefore gives
$$
[p](X)=pX+\sum_{j\ge2}c_jX^j,\qquad c_j\in R.
$$
For $|x|<1$ this converges and the non-Archimedean inequality gives $|[p](x)|\le\max(|p||x|,|x|^2)$. The <residue> <characteristic> implies $|p|<1$; in equal <characteristic> $p$ the linear <coefficient>[coefficient] is zero, which only improves the bound.

Let $x_n=[p^n](x)$, so $x_{n+1}=[p](x_n)$. If $x=0$ there is nothing to prove. Otherwise put $\rho=\max(|p|,|x|)<1$. Inductively $|x_n|\le|x|$, and then $|x_{n+1}|\le\rho|x_n|$. Thus
$$
\boxed{|[p^n](x)|\le\rho^n|x|\longrightarrow0.}
$$
Completeness ensures convergence of the formal-series evaluations. The contraction argument in fact does not need the extra discreteness assumption.

For <prescribing a factor by elliptic-curve tripling>, <quadratic reciprocity> gives $(3/p)=1$ when $p\equiv1\pmod{12}$. Choose $r\in\mathbb F_p$ with $r^2=3$ and choose an <integer> $a$ by the <Chinese remainder theorem> so that
$$
a\equiv-3+2r\pmod p,\qquad a\equiv1\pmod q.
$$
Then $a^2+6a-3\equiv0\pmod p$ and it is four modulo $q$.

The curve $E:y^2=x^3+ax-a$ has <discriminant> $-16a^2(4a+27)$. At $p$, $a\ne0$, since otherwise $r=3/2$ would force $p=3$. Also $4a+27=15+8r$ cannot vanish: squaring $8r=-15$ would give $192=225\pmod p$, forcing $p=3$ or 11, neither in the required class. At $q$, $a=1$ makes $a(4a+27)=31$, and $q\equiv5\pmod{12}$ excludes $q=31$. Thus both reductions are nonsingular.

The point $P=(1,1)$ is on every curve in this family. Doubling it uses the invertible denominator two and gives
$$
x(2P)=\frac{a^2+6a+1}4,\qquad x(2P)-x(P)=\frac{a^2+6a-3}4.
$$
Modulo $p$ this difference vanishes, and the doubling ordinate is $-1$, so $2P=-P$ and $3P$ is the identity. Modulo $q$, taking $a=1$ gives $x(2P)=2$, so $2P$ and $P$ have distinct abscissas and their sum is a finite point.

Consequently the attempted addition computing $3P$ modulo $N$ asks to invert a denominator divisible by $p$ and not by $q$. Its numerator is nonzero modulo $p$, since the two ordinates there are minus one and one, so this is not a removable coincidence. The Euclidean gcd used for inversion returns
$$
\boxed{\gcd(a^2+6a-3,N)=p,}
$$
a proper factor. Thus the specified elliptic-curve computation successfully factors $N$.
