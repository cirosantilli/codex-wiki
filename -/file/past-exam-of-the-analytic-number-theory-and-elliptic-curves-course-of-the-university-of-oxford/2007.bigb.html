= Solution
{parent=2007/a/1/i}

The <Dirichlet convolution> is $(f_1*f_2)(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}f_1(d)f_2(n/d)$. Fix $s$ with $\sigma=\operatorname{Re}s>1$. <Absolute convergence> of the two <Dirichlet series> gives
$$
\sum_{a,b\ge1}|f_1(a)f_2(b)(ab)^{-s}|
=\left(\sum_a|f_1(a)|a^{-\sigma}\right)
\left(\sum_b|f_2(b)|b^{-\sigma}\right)<\infty.
$$
Thus the double <series> may be regrouped by $n=ab$, proving
$$
\boxed{F_1(s)F_2(s)=\sum_{n\ge1}(f_1*f_2)(n)n^{-s}.}
$$
The same estimate proves <absolute convergence> of the resulting <Dirichlet series>.

= Solution
{parent=2007/a/1/ii}

For a finite set of primes $p\le y$, multiply the absolutely convergent local <series>. Unique <prime factorization> and multiplicativity, with $g(1)=1$, give
$$
\prod_{p\le y}\sum_{e\ge0}g(p^e)p^{-es}
=\sum_{\substack{n\ge1\\\text{every prime divisor of }n\le y}}g(n)n^{-s}.
$$
The omitted <integers> all exceed $y$, so the absolute difference from $G(s)$ is at most $\sum_{n>y}|g(n)|n^{-\sigma}$, which tends to zero. Hence the finite products converge to $G(s)$:
$$
\boxed{G(s)=\prod_pG_p(s)\qquad(\operatorname{Re}s>1).}
$$
This proves the <Euler product> directly from <absolute convergence>, rather than treating an infinite formal multiplication as automatically valid.

The <Möbius function> satisfies $\mu(p^e)^2=1$ for $e=0,1$ and is zero for $e\ge2$. Therefore
$$
H_1(s)=\prod_p(1+p^{-s})=\frac{\zeta(s)}{\zeta(2s)}.
$$
The inverse local factor is $(1+p^{-s})^{-1}=\sum_{e\ge0}(-1)^ep^{-es}$. Consequently the inverse under <Dirichlet convolution> is the <Liouville function>
$$
\boxed{h_2(n)=(-1)^{\Omega(n)},}
$$
where $\Omega(n)$ counts prime factors with multiplicity. Both functions are <multiplicative functions>. At $p^e$ with $e\ge1$, their convolution is $(-1)^e+(-1)^{e-1}=0$, while its value at one is one. Multiplicativity then proves $h_1*h_2=I$ on every positive <integer>. Its <Dirichlet series> is $H_2(s)=\zeta(2s)/\zeta(s)$ for $\operatorname{Re}s>1$.

For $n\le x$, write $F(n)=F(x)-\int_n^xF'(t)dt$, multiply by $f(n)$ and sum. Interchanging a finite sum with the <integral> gives the <Abel summation formula>
$$
\sum_{n\le x}f(n)F(n)=S(x)F(x)-\int_1^xS(t)F'(t)\,dt.
$$
Apply it with $F(t)=t^{-s}$ and $S(t)=\sum_{n\le t}a_n$. If $\sigma>\alpha$, the boundary term is $O(x^{\alpha-\sigma})\to0$ and $\int_1^\infty|S(t)t^{-s-1}|dt$ converges. Therefore the <Dirichlet series> converges and
$$
A(s)=s\int_1^\infty S(t)t^{-s-1}dt=s\sum_{m\ge1}I_m(s).
$$
Choose $C$ with $|S(t)|\le Ct^\alpha$ for $t\ge1$. For $\sigma>\alpha+\delta$,
$$
|I_m(s)|\le C\int_m^{m+1}t^{\alpha-\sigma-1}dt\le Cm^{-1-\delta}.
$$
The <Weierstrass M-test> proves <uniform convergence> of $\sum I_m$ on this entire open half-plane. For fixed $s$, the same estimate with its actual $\sigma$ gives $I_m(s)=O(m^{\alpha-1-\sigma})$. Each $I_m$ is an <entire function>, since its <integral> has bounded positive $t$ range and can be differentiated in $s$ under an integrable bound on each <compact set>. Thus the locally uniform sum is <holomorphic>, and so is its product with $s$. This gives <holomorphy from polynomial growth of arithmetic partial sums> and proves holomorphy of $A$ for $\operatorname{Re}s>\alpha$. Multiplication by the unbounded factor $s$ does not assert <uniform convergence> of $A$ on a whole unbounded half-plane; local <uniform convergence> is what the holomorphy argument needs.

With $a_n=\mu(n)$ and the hypothetical bound, $A_\mu(s)$ is <holomorphic> for $\operatorname{Re}s>3/4$. On $\operatorname{Re}s>1$, the <Euler product> gives $\zeta(s)A_\mu(s)=1$. The <identity theorem> extends this as a meromorphic identity throughout the connected half-plane $\operatorname{Re}s>3/4$. Hence $\zeta$ has **no zeros in that half-plane**: at any zero, the product with the <holomorphic> $A_\mu$ would be zero. The <pole> at one is not a zero; $A_\mu$ has a compensating zero there.

The <double-angle formula> gives
$$
3\pm4\cos\theta+\cos2\theta=2(1\pm\cos\theta)^2\ge0.
$$
For $\sigma>1$, the <logarithm> of the absolutely convergent <Euler product> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
&3\log\zeta(\sigma)+4\log|\zeta(\sigma+it)|+\log|\zeta(\sigma+2it)|\\
&=\sum_p\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{3+4\cos(mt\log p)+\cos(2mt\log p)}{mp^{m\sigma}}\ge0.
\end{aligned}
$$
Exponentiation proves
$$
\boxed{\zeta(\sigma)^3|\zeta(\sigma+it)|^4|\zeta(\sigma+2it)|\ge1.}
$$
Suppose $\zeta(1+it)=0$ for $t\ne0$, of order $r\ge1$. As $\sigma\downarrow1$, its middle factor is $O((\sigma-1)^{4r})$, the first is $O((\sigma-1)^{-3})$ because the <pole> at one is simple, and the final factor is bounded because $1+2it\ne1$. The product is $O((\sigma-1)^{4r-3})\to0$, contradicting the lower bound one. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\zeta(1+it)\ne0\qquad(t\ne0).}
$$
This is the <three-four-one zero-free-region argument> specialized to the line with real part one.

For $x>0$, $(e^x-1)^{-1}=\sum_{n\ge1}e^{-nx}$. If $\sigma=\operatorname{Re}s>1$, the <integral> of the sum of absolute values is $\Gamma(\sigma)\sum n^{-\sigma}<\infty$. Therefore interchange of sum and <integral> is justified, and the substitution $u=nx$ gives
$$
J(s)=\sum_{n\ge1}\int_0^\infty x^{s-1}e^{-nx}dx
=\boxed{\Gamma(s)\zeta(s).}
$$
On the two unbounded rays of the specified <Hankel contour>, $x\ge\pi$ and $1/(e^x-1)$ decays exponentially, dominating every fixed power $x^{\sigma-1}$. The branch multiplier on the lower ray is a fixed factor $e^{2\pi i(s-1)}$. On the circular part, the chosen branch is continuous along the parametrized arc, and $e^z-1$ never vanishes because its nonzero zeros have modulus at least $2\pi$. Thus $I(s)$ exists for every $s$. The estimates are uniform when $s$ ranges over a <compact set>, including after differentiation in $s$, so $I$ is an <entire function>.

Let $C(s)=(e^{2\pi is}-1)\Gamma(s)$. The given identity shows that $I(s)/C(s)$ agrees with $\zeta(s)$ for $\operatorname{Re}s>1$ wherever the quotient is directly defined. It supplies the <meromorphic continuation> elsewhere wherever $C$ is finite and nonzero, with removable points filled by limits. At a nonpositive <integer> $-k$, the simple zero of the exponential factor cancels the supplied gamma <pole>, giving
$$
C(-k)=\frac{2\pi i(-1)^k}{k!}.
$$
At $s=-k$, the <integrals> on the two rays cancel because the integrand is single-valued. The remaining positively oriented circle has only the <pole> at zero inside it, so
$$
I(-k)=2\pi i\operatorname{Res}_{z=0}\frac{z^{-k-1}}{e^z-1}
=2\pi i\,[z^{k+1}]\frac z{e^z-1}.
$$
The coefficient at $k=0$ is $-1/2$, hence $\boxed{\zeta(0)=-1/2}$. For positive even $k$, the relevant odd coefficient has degree at least three and is zero, giving $\boxed{\zeta(-2m)=0}$ for $m\ge1$. For $k=2m-1$, the given generating function gives the coefficient $(-1)^{m-1}B_m/(2m)!$. Division by $C(-(2m-1))=-2\pi i/(2m-1)!$ yields
$$
\boxed{\zeta(1-2m)=\frac{(-1)^mB_m}{2m}\qquad(m\ge1).}
$$
The symbols $B_m$ here follow the printed generating function: they are the positive magnitudes of the usual even-index <Bernoulli numbers>, so $B_1=1/6$, $B_2=1/30$. This convention accounts for the alternating sign in the final expression.

**No prime works.** The <p-adic valuation> induces a well-defined <homomorphism>

\mathbb Q_p^*/(\mathbb Q_p^*)^p\longrightarrow\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z,

= Solution

Choose a nonzero <integer> $D$ with $D\alpha,D\beta\in\mathbb Z$. By the <Chinese remainder theorem>, choose $z_n\in\mathbb Z$ satisfying $z_n\equiv D\alpha\pmod{q^n}$ and $z_n\equiv D\beta\pmod{r^n}$. Put $x_n=z_n/D$. Then
$$
|x_n-\alpha|_q\le|D|_q^{-1}q^{-n}\to0,\qquad
|x_n-\beta|_r\le|D|_r^{-1}r^{-n}\to0.
$$
This proves the requested simultaneous approximation, even when the rational targets have denominators divisible by either prime.

A <rational sequence converging to zero at every standard absolute value> is:
$$
\boxed{y_n=\frac{n!}{1+(n!)^2}.}
$$
Its ordinary absolute value is at most $1/n!$, hence tends to zero. For a fixed prime $p$ and all sufficiently large $n$, the denominator is congruent to one modulo $p$, so $v_p(y_n)=v_p(n!)\to\infty$. Therefore $|y_n|_p\to0$ for each prime as well. The assertion concerns each fixed prime separately, rather than <uniform convergence> over all primes.

If $p=0$ as an element of $K$, the stated lower bound is zero and is immediate. Otherwise set $\rho_1=|p|^{1/(p-1)}$. The supplied <formal group> expansion has $g(0)=0$, because $[p](0)=0$. For $z\in\mathcal M$, the non-Archimedean estimates give
$$
|f(z)|=|z|,\qquad |g(z^p)|\le|z|^p,\qquad
|[p](z)|\le\max\{|p||z|,|z|^p\}.
$$
If $0<|z|<\rho_1$, then $|z|^p<|p||z|$, so the two terms cannot cancel and $|[p](z)|=|p||z|>0$. The same strict inequality persists under iteration, so no such $z$ can have finite $p$-power order. Every nonzero $p$-power torsion point therefore satisfies $|z|\ge\rho_1$.

For a point of order $p$, $[p](z)=0$ itself forces $|p||z|\le|z|^p$, proving the bound for $n=1$. For $n\ge2$, put $w=[p](z)$, of order $p^{n-1}$. Since $|z|\ge\rho_1$, the preceding maximum is $|z|^p$. By induction,
$$
|z|^p\ge|w|\ge|p|^{1/(p^{n-1}-p^{n-2})}.
$$
This proves the <formal torsion radius for prime power order>. Taking the positive $p$th root gives
$$
\boxed{|z|\ge|p|^{1/(p^n-p^{n-1})}.}
$$

For a short <Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve> with coefficients in the <p-adic integers>, a point with nonintegral $x$ has valuations $v_p(x)=-2m$ and $v_p(y)=-3m$ for some <integer> $m\ge1$: the term $x^3$ strictly dominates $Ax+B$ in the equation, so $2v_p(y)=3v_p(x)$. Its formal parameter $T=-x/y$ therefore has valuation $m\ge1$. These points, together with the identity, form the formal neighborhood $E_1(\mathbb Q_p)$ of the identity, with parameter in $p\mathbb Z_p$ and the elliptic <formal group> law over the <p-adic integers>. For a point with $x\in\mathbb Z_p$, the equation gives $y\in\mathbb Z_p$ too.

The formal neighborhood has no nontrivial torsion. Multiplication by an <integer> coprime to $p$ has <series> $[m](T)=mT+O(T^2)$ and preserves $|T|_p$, so any torsion there must have $p$-power order. If $p\ge3$, part (i) gives $|T|_p\ge p^{-1/(p-1)}>p^{-1}$, contradicting $T\in p\mathbb Z_p$.

The printed hypotheses also allow $p=2$. In that case, a nontrivial torsion point in the formal neighborhood would have order a power of two and hence yield a point of order two in that neighborhood. But every nonidentity point of order two on a short <Weierstrass equation of an elliptic curve> has $y=0$ and $x$ a root of the monic cubic over the <p-adic integers> $X^3+AX+B$. Such a root lies in the <p-adic integers>, so it cannot belong to the formal neighborhood, whose nonidentity points have nonintegral coordinates. This excludes the remaining case. This is the <torsion-free formal subgroup for a short Weierstrass equation>. Thus every affine torsion point satisfies
$$
\boxed{|x|_p\le1,\qquad|y|_p\le1.}
$$
With <good reduction>, the reduction <homomorphism> $E(\mathbb Q_p)\to\widetilde E(\mathbb F_p)$ has kernel exactly this torsion-free formal neighborhood. Its restriction to the <torsion subgroup> is therefore injective, proving the required subgroup isomorphism. The short model over the <p-adic integers> is essential to the prime-two argument; no blanket torsion-injectivity assertion for arbitrary models is needed.

\#E(\mathbb F_p)=1+\sum_x(1+\chi(f(x)))=p+1\equiv0\pmod4.
$$
Thus in both cases
$$
\boxed{4\mid\#E(\mathbb F_p).}
$$
The assumptions $p\ne2$ and $m\ne0$ ensure nonsingularity and keep the displayed two-torsion roots distinct when they exist.
