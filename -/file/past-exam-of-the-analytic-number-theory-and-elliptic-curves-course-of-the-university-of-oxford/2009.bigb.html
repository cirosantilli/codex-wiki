= Solution

{c}

<absolute convergence> depends only on $\sigma=\operatorname{Re}s$, since $|a_nn^{-s}|=|a_n|n^{-\sigma}$. Let $A$ be the set of real $\sigma$ for which $\sum|a_n|n^{-\sigma}$ converges. Comparison with a convergent member shows that $A$ is upward closed. The hypotheses give a member of $A$ and a real number outside $A$, so its <infimum> $\sigma_0$ is finite. If $\sigma>\sigma_0$, some $\tau\in A$ lies below $\sigma$, giving convergence by comparison; if $\sigma<\sigma_0$, convergence would contradict the definition of the <infimum>. Thus **$\sigma_0=\inf A$ is the <abscissa of absolute convergence of a Dirichlet series>**. This argument leaves convergence on the boundary open.

Partition $1,\ldots,n$ according to $d=n/\gcd(k,n)$. Writing $k=(n/d)j$ identifies the part indexed by $d\mid n$ with the <integers> $1\leq j\leq d$ <coprime> to $d$. There are $\phi(d)$ of them. Counting the entire partition proves the <totient divisor-sum identity>
$$
\boxed{\sum_{d\mid n}\phi(d)=n.}
$$

We use the multiplication theorem for absolutely convergent <Dirichlet series>: their product is the <Dirichlet series> of the <Dirichlet convolution> of their coefficients. <absolute convergence> justifies rearrangement of the double sum. Here $0<\phi(n)\leq n$, so its series converges absolutely for $\sigma>2$, and
$$
\zeta(s)\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{\phi(n)}{n^s}
=\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{\sum_{d\mid n}\phi(d)}{n^s}
=\sum_{n\geq1}n^{1-s}=\zeta(s-1).
$$
The <Euler product> implies $\zeta(s)\neq0$ for $\sigma>1$. Division gives
$$
\boxed{\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{\phi(n)}{n^s}
=\frac{\zeta(s-1)}{\zeta(s)}\quad(\operatorname{Re}s>2).}
$$

We finally use the <simple pole> of the <Riemann zeta function> at one, of residue one, and $0<\zeta(2)<\infty$. The quotient therefore tends to $+\infty$ as real $s\downarrow2$. If $\sum\phi(n)n^{-2}$ converged, its nonnegative terms would dominate the series at every real $s>2$, giving a uniform finite bound and a contradiction. Thus the series diverges at two; comparison then excludes <absolute convergence> at every $\sigma<2$. Combined with the convergence already proved, **the abscissa is exactly two**. This is the <totient Dirichlet series and its absolute convergence boundary>.

<summation by parts>, with $S(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)$, gives for an <integer> $N$
$$
\sum_{n\leq N}\chi(n)n^{-s}
=S(N)N^{-s}+s\int_1^N S(x)x^{-s-1}\,dx.
$$
The boundary term tends to zero when $\operatorname{Re}s>0$ because $S$ is bounded. In the original region of <absolute convergence> this proves
$$
\boxed{L(s)=s\int_1^\infty S(x)x^{-s-1}\,dx.}
$$
For a <compact subset> of $\operatorname{Re}s>0$, choose $\delta>0$ below all its real parts and bound $|s|$ there. The integral tail is bounded by a constant times $\int_N^\infty x^{-1-\delta}dx$, uniformly on that <compact set>. Its truncated integrals are <holomorphic function> in $s$, so <locally uniform convergence> proves a <analytic continuation> throughout $\operatorname{Re}s>0$. This is <holomorphy of a Dirichlet series from bounded partial sums>.

The <Euler product>, valid for $\operatorname{Re}s>1$, is
$$
L(s)=\prod_p(1-\chi(p)p^{-s})^{-1}
=\prod_{p\equiv1\ (4)}(1-p^{-s})^{-1}
\prod_{p\equiv3\ (4)}(1+p^{-s})^{-1}.
$$
Expand the logarithms of these absolutely convergent <Euler products>. Writing $\theta=mt\log p$, the logarithm of the product in the desired inequality is
$$
\sum_p\sum_{m\geq1}\frac{
3+4\chi(p)^m\cos\theta+\cos2\theta}{m p^{m\sigma}}.
$$
For odd $p$, $\chi(p)^m$ is $1$ or $-1$. In the first case the numerator is the given nonnegative trigonometric <polynomial>. In the second replace $\theta$ by $\theta+\pi$, which changes the sign of $\cos\theta$ and leaves $\cos2\theta$ unchanged. At $p=2$ the numerator is $3+\cos2\theta\geq2$. Every summand is nonnegative. Exponentiation proves
$$
\boxed{\zeta(\sigma)^3|L(\sigma+it)|^4
|\zeta(\sigma+2it)|\geq1.}
$$
This is the <three-four-one inequality for Euler products> applied to the real character modulo four.

Suppose $t\neq0$ and $L(1+it)=0$. Holomorphy gives $L(\sigma+it)=O(\sigma-1)$ as $\sigma\downarrow1$. The only pole of the <Riemann zeta function> is at one, so $\zeta(\sigma+2it)$ remains bounded, while $\zeta(\sigma)^3=O((\sigma-1)^{-3})$. The displayed product is then $O(\sigma-1)$, contradicting its lower bound. Hence **$L(1+it)\neq0$ for $t\neq0$**.

At $t=0$ the last zeta factor also has a pole, so that contradiction no longer follows from the inequality. Instead evaluate the convergent alternating series:
$$
L(1)=1-\frac13+\frac15-\cdots
=\int_0^1\frac{dx}{1+x^2}
=\boxed{\frac\pi4\neq0}.
$$
For rigor, integrate the finite geometric identity for $(1+x^2)^{-1}$; the remainder has absolute integral at most $1/(2N+3)$ and tends to zero. <summation by parts> above identifies this value with the <analytic continuation> at one.

Write $s=\sigma+it$; the source's rectangle notation $|\operatorname{Im}(t)|\leq T$ is interpreted as the intended height restriction $|t|\leq T$. We use the standard facts that $\zeta$ has no zeros on $\sigma\geq1$, has a <simple pole> at one, and is meromorphic with no other pole. The function $(s-1)\zeta(s)$ is <holomorphic function> and nonzero on the <compact set> $\{1+it:|t|\leq T\}$, including its removable value one at $s=1$. It remains nonzero in a neighborhood of this <compact set>. Together with nonvanishing to its right this gives some $0<\alpha_T<1$ such that the whole closed rectangle $\alpha_T\leq\sigma\leq2$, $|t|\leq T$ contains no zeta zero. The pole at one is harmless: $1/\zeta$ extends holomorphically there with value zero.

The linearly smoothed Perron kernel, for $y>0$ and $c>0$, is
$$
\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}
\frac{y^{s+1}}{s(s+1)}\,ds
=\begin{cases}y-1,&y>1,\\0,&0<y\leq1.\end{cases}
$$
For $y>1$ shift the <contour> left across the <simple poles> at zero and minus one, whose <residues> are $y$ and $-1$. For $y<1$ shift right, crossing no pole. At $y=1$ the integral is zero, also obtained by direct integration of $1/s-1/(s+1)$. The quadratic denominator gives absolute integrability on the original vertical line.

For $c>1$ use the absolutely convergent <Dirichlet series> $1/\zeta(s)=\sum\mu(n)n^{-s}$. Interchanging the sum and the <contour integral> is justified by $\sum|\mu(n)|n^{-c}<\infty$ and the integrable denominator. The term for $n$ is $n$ times the kernel at $y=x/n$, hence $(x-n)$ when $n<x$ and zero otherwise. Consequently
$$
\boxed{M_1(x):=\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)(x-n)
=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}
\frac{x^{s+1}}{\zeta(s)s(s+1)}\,ds.}
$$

Here is a <contour> argument that needs only the stated bound, with no unproved uniform estimate left of one. Take $x>e$, $c=1+1/\log x$, and first hold $T\geq2$ fixed. On the two tails $|t|>T$, the given bound and $x^{c+1}=ex^2$ give
$$
\left|\text{tail contribution}\right|
\leq Cx^2\int_T^\infty\frac{\log^7(2+t)}{t^2}\,dt.
$$
Shift the remaining segment to $\sigma=\alpha_T$. The integrand is <holomorphic function> inside the rectangle: the zero of $1/\zeta$ at one cancels the apparent zeta pole, and $0,-1$ lie outside. On the fixed left side its integral is $O_T(x^{1+\alpha_T})=o_T(x^2)$. On the two horizontal sides, $1/(\zeta(s)s(s+1))$ is bounded by some finite constant depending on $T$. Therefore their integrals are bounded by
$$
C_T\int_{\alpha_T}^{c}x^{\sigma+1}\,d\sigma
\leq C_T\frac{x^{c+1}}{\log x}
=o_T(x^2).
$$
It follows that
$$
\limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{|M_1(x)|}{x^2}
\leq C\int_T^\infty\frac{\log^7(2+t)}{t^2}\,dt.
$$
Now let $T\to\infty$; the integrable tail tends to zero. Thus
$$
\boxed{\sum_{n\leq x}\mu(n)(x-n)=o(x^2).}
$$
The order of limits matters: first $x\to\infty$ for each fixed rectangle, then $T\to\infty$. This is <linearly smoothed Möbius cancellation from finite zero-free rectangles>.

Work with the smooth projective cubic, including its points at infinity. For two points $P,Q$, draw their chord, using the tangent when they coincide, and let $R$ be its third intersection counted with <intersection multiplicity>. Draw the line through $R$ and $o$, again using the tangent if necessary, and let $S$ be its third intersection. Define $P+Q=S$. When $o$ is a flex, this is the familiar reflection of the first third-intersection point across the Weierstrass involution. The two-line construction also works when the chosen identity is not a flex.

To prove associativity without assuming it in the geometry, use <divisor class>. A nonsingular plane cubic has genus one. By the <Riemann-Roch theorem>, every <divisor class> of degree one contains exactly one effective <divisor on an algebraic curve> of degree one: for such a <divisor on an algebraic curve> $D$,
$$
\ell(D)-\ell(K_C-D)=\deg D=1,
$$
and $\deg(K_C-D)=-1$, so $\ell(K_C-D)=0$. Thus $\ell(D)=1$, and the nonzero section is unique up to scalar. Over $K$, a degree-one effective <divisor on an algebraic curve> defined over $K$ is a $K$-rational point. This also proves uniqueness of that point. In particular the map
$$
\iota:C(K)\longrightarrow\operatorname{Pic}^0(C),\qquad
P\longmapsto[P-o]
$$
is injective and identifies these points with the corresponding <degree-zero Picard group of a curve>: to recover the point from a class, add $[o]$ and take its unique effective representative. This is the <elliptic curve group law from Riemann-Roch>.

Let $H$ denote the <divisor class> cut out by a line. The two lines in the <chord-and-tangent group law> give
$$
P+Q+R\sim H,\qquad R+o+S\sim H.
$$
Their difference gives $P+Q\sim o+S$, hence
$$
\iota(S)=[P-o]+[Q-o].
$$
Addition of <divisor class> is associative. Therefore both $(P+Q)+R$ and $P+(Q+R)$ map to $[P-o]+[Q-o]+[R-o]$. Injectivity of $\iota$ proves **the <chord-and-tangent group law> is associative**. The same argument gives identity $o$, commutativity and inverses, and covers tangencies through the use of <intersection multiplicities>. All constructions are defined over $K$.

At $p=2$, take $x=-2$. The odd 2-adic <unit in a ring> $-7$ is congruent to one modulo eight, so it is a square in $\mathbb Z_2$. We use the standard 2-adic square criterion that an odd <unit in a ring> is a square exactly when it is one modulo eight. Its sufficiency follows by successively adjusting an odd square root modulo $2^m$ by $2^{m-1}$ to obtain the next required square congruence. Thus $y^2=-7$ has a 2-adic <integer> solution.

For $p\geq5$ other than $23$, the discriminant of $y^2=x^3-x-1$ is
$$
\Delta=-16\bigl(4(-1)^3+27(-1)^2\bigr)=-16\cdot23,
$$
so this model has <good reduction of an elliptic curve>. <Hasse's theorem on elliptic curves> gives
$$
\#E(\mathbb F_p)\geq p+1-2\sqrt p>1.
$$
Since there is just one point at infinity, there is an affine point. Smoothness means that at least one partial derivative of $y^2-x^3+x+1$ is a <unit in a ring> modulo $p$, so <Hensel's lemma> lifts the affine point to $\mathbb Z_p^2$. The last exceptional odd <prime> is also soluble: at $p=23$, $h(-2)=-7\equiv16$ has square roots $\pm4$, which are simple roots of $Y^2+7$ modulo $23$, and lift by <Hensel's lemma>.

$$
\boxed{\text{a solution in }\mathbb Z_p^2\text{ exists exactly when }p\neq3.}
$$
The same <prime> list holds if one only asks for a solution modulo $p$, but the lifting above establishes the asserted <p-adic integer> solutions. This is <local solubility of the cubic x cubed minus x minus one>.

\boxed{\gcd(13,221)=13,\qquad221=13\cdot17.}

Modulo seventeen that chord denominator remains nonzero. The differing behavior at the two <primes> is exactly what the <elliptic curve method> detects.

Choose the blocks
$$
\boxed{x_n=p^{\lfloor\log_p n\rfloor}\quad(n\geq1).}
$$
If $p^k\leq n<p^{k+1}$, then $|x_n|_p=p^{-k}$, so $x_n\to0$ in $\mathbb Q_p$. By the <ultrametric inequality>, for $N\geq M$,
$$
\left|\sum_{n=M}^{N}x_n\right|_p
\leq\max_{M\leq n\leq N}|x_n|_p\longrightarrow0
\quad(M\to\infty).
$$
The <partial sums> are <Cauchy sequence>, and <completeness> of $\mathbb Q_p$ proves convergence of $\sum x_n$.

In the real series of <p-adic absolute values>, the block $p^k\leq n<p^{k+1}$ contains $(p-1)p^k$ equal terms of size $p^{-k}$. Its sum is $p-1$, independently of $k$. Infinitely many such positive block sums force divergence. This exhibits <p-adic convergence without summable absolute values>: the criterion is that the terms tend to zero, rather than real absolute summability.

Put $U(T)=F_X(0,T)$. The <formal group> identity $F(X,0)=X$ gives $U(0)=1$, so $U$ is a <unit in a ring> in $R[[T]]$, even if $R$ has <zero divisors>.

A differential $h(T)dT$ is invariant under formal translation if
$$
h(F(X,T))F_X(X,T)=h(X)
$$
as an identity of <formal power series>. Setting $X=0$ gives $h(T)U(T)=h(0)$. Consequently every <invariant differential of a formal group law>, if it exists, must have the form $h(0)U(T)^{-1}dT$. In particular there can be only one with constant coefficient one.

Existence follows directly from associativity. Differentiate
$$
F(Z,F(X,T))=F(F(Z,X),T)
$$
with respect to $Z$ at zero, obtaining
$$
U(F(X,T))=F_X(X,T)U(X).
$$
Every displayed $U$ is a <unit in a ring>, so
$$
U(F(X,T))^{-1}F_X(X,T)=U(X)^{-1}.
$$
This is precisely invariance of $U(T)^{-1}dT$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\omega=F_X(0,T)^{-1}dT,\qquad
\{\text{invariant differentials}\}=R\omega.}
$$
Normalization means its constant coefficient is one. This proves both existence and uniqueness over an arbitrary <commutative ring>, without division by any <integer>.

Let $\omega_G=h_G(T)dT$. The pullback through the <formal group> homomorphism is
$$
f^*\omega_G=h_G(f(T))f'(T)dT.
$$
The identity $f(F(X,T))=G(f(X),f(T))$ and the <chain rule> show that this pullback is invariant under $F$: translation by $T$ before $f$ becomes translation by $f(T)$ afterward. By part (i) it is a scalar multiple of $\omega_F$. Its coefficient at zero is $h_G(0)f'(0)=f'(0)$, so
$$
\boxed{f^*\omega_G=f'(0)\omega_F.}
$$

Apply this to the multiplication-by-$p$ endomorphism $q(T)=[p](T)$. Since the linear coefficient of $F$ is $X+Y$, repeated addition gives $q'(0)=p$. If $\omega_F=h(T)dT$ with $h(0)=1$, the pullback formula gives
$$
h(q(T))q'(T)=p\,h(T),\qquad
q'(T)\in pR[[T]],
$$
because $h(q(T))$ is a <unit in a ring>. Write $q(T)=\sum_{m\geq1}a_mT^m$. Then $ma_m\in pR$ for every $m$. If $p\nmid m$, choose <integers> $u,v$ with $um+vp=1$. It follows that
$$
a_m=u(ma_m)+vp a_m\in pR.
$$
Choose $b_m\in R$ with $a_m=pb_m$ for those $m$ not divisible by $p$, and define
$$
f(T)=\sum_{p\nmid m}b_mT^m,\qquad
g(T)=\sum_{j\geq1}a_{pj}T^j.
$$
Coefficient comparison now proves
$$
\boxed{[p](T)=pf(T)+g(T^p).}
$$
No cancellation of $p$ was used, so this <prime-multiplication coefficient splitting for formal groups> remains valid when $p$ is a <zero divisor> in $R$.

Since neither $n$ nor $-n$ is a square, both cubics have exactly one rational root, zero. Thus each <torsion point of an elliptic curve> <group> has exactly one nonidentity <2-torsion>, namely $T=(0,0)$.

Neither curve has a <rational point> of order four. Indeed on $E_m:y^2=x^3+mx$ the duplication formula gives
$$
x(2P)=\frac{(x(P)^2-m)^2}{4y(P)^2}.
$$
If $2P=T$, then $P$ is neither the identity nor a <2-torsion>, so the denominator is nonzero and $x(P)^2=m$. Thus $m$ must be a rational square; an <integer> that is a rational square is an <integer> square. Applying this with $m=n$ or $m=-n$ contradicts the hypotheses.

If $3\nmid n$, both curves have <good reduction of an elliptic curve> at three and exactly four points over $\mathbb F_3$. To see this, $x^3=x$ there, so the equation is $y^2=(1+m)x$. For $m=1$ the right side runs through all <residues>, and for $m=-1$ it is always zero; either way there are three affine points. Injection of <torsion point of an elliptic curve> at this odd good <prime> bounds each <torsion element> order by four. An order-four <torsion subgroup> with just one nonzero <2-torsion> would contain an order-four point, which was excluded. Therefore in this case both <torsion subgroups> equal $\{o,T\}$.

The remaining case is $3\mid n$. The literal hypothesis $15\nmid n$ then ensures $5\nmid n$, so both curves have <good reduction of an elliptic curve> at five. Direct square counting gives
$$
\begin{array}{c|rrrr}
m\bmod5&1&2&3&4\\\hline
\#E_m(\mathbb F_5)&4&2&10&8.
\end{array}
$$
For example the square <residues> are $0,1,4$; substituting $x=0,\ldots,4$ gives this table, including the point at infinity. The pair $m=n,-n$ has <residues> $(1,4)$ or $(2,3)$ in some order. In either pair at least one reduction <group> has order two or four. \boxed{\text{at least one of }E(\mathbb Q)_{\rm tors},
F(\mathbb Q)_{\rm tors}\text{ is }\{o,(0,0)\}.}

We use <two-isogeny descent>, stating the arithmetic results involved. For
$$
E:y^2=x(x^2+ax+b),\qquad
E':Y^2=X(X^2-2aX+a^2-4b),
$$
define the <two-torsion square-class homomorphism> $\alpha_E:E(\mathbb Q)\to\mathbb Q^\times/\mathbb Q^{\times2}$ by $\alpha_E(o)=1$, $\alpha_E((0,0))=b$, and $\alpha_E((x,y))=x$ otherwise. Its kernel is the image of the dual <two-isogeny formula>; the analogous map $\alpha_{E'}$ has kernel the image of the forward <two-isogeny formula>. The <two-isogeny rank formula> is
$$
2^r=\frac{|\alpha_E(E(\mathbb Q))|\,
|\alpha_{E'}(E'(\mathbb Q))|}{4}.
$$
The images have signed squarefree representatives supported on <primes> dividing $b$ and $a^2-4b$, respectively. This <prime-support bound in two-isogeny descent> follows by <valuation> comparison: if a <prime> does not divide $b$, the <valuations> of $x$ at that <prime> must be even, whether positive or negative. A candidate squarefree class $d$ is represented precisely when its <quartic covering in a two-isogeny descent>
$$
N^2=dU^4+aU^2V^2+(b/d)V^4
$$
has the corresponding rational solution, apart from the separately included identity and two-torsion cases; clearing denominators permits <coprime> <integers> $U,V$.

Here $a=5,b=-5$, so
$$
E':Y^2=X(X^2-10X+45).
$$
On $E$ the possible classes are $1,-1,5,-5$. They all occur: the identity gives one, $T=(0,0)$ gives $-5$, and $(-1,3)$ gives $-1$. Since the image is a <group>, their product also gives five. Thus its image has order four.

On $E'$, the quadratic factor $(X-5)^2+20$ is strictly positive for real $X$. Every nonzero rational $X$-coordinate is therefore positive. The <prime-support bound in two-isogeny descent> restricts its image to $1,3,5,15$. The identity gives one and $(0,0)$ gives $45$, that is class five.

Class three is impossible. Its <quartic covering in a two-isogeny descent> would require
$$
N^2=3U^4-10U^2V^2+15V^4,\qquad\gcd(U,V)=1.
$$
Modulo three this is $N^2\equiv2U^2V^2$, so three divides $N$ and at least one of $U,V$. They cannot both be divisible. If $3\mid U$ and $3\nmid V$, the right side has <valuation> exactly one, from $15V^4$. If $3\mid V$ and $3\nmid U$, it again has <valuation> exactly one, from $3U^4$. Either case contradicts its being a square. Class fifteen is then impossible too, since multiplying it by the known image class five would give class three.

Hence the second image is exactly $\{1,5\}$, of order two. The rank formula gives
$$
2^r=\frac{4\cdot2}{4}=2,\qquad\boxed{r=1.}
$$
This proves the <rank-one elliptic curve with coefficients five and minus five> calculation, including the local obstruction that removes the spurious extra descent classes.

For $E:y^2=x^3+px$ with $p>0$, the factor $x^2+p$ is strictly positive on the real line. Thus every nonzero rational $x$-coordinate is positive. In the <two-isogeny descent> map, <prime> support restricts its <square class> to $1$ or $p$. Both are realized by the identity and $(0,0)$, although only the upper bound is needed:
$$
|\alpha_E(E(\mathbb Q))|\leq2.
$$
The two-isogenous curve is $E':Y^2=X^3-4pX$. Its <two-torsion square-class homomorphism> has signed squarefree representatives supported on the <primes> dividing $4p$. For odd $p$ these are among
$$
1,-1,2,-2,p,-p,2p,-2p.
$$
Hence the second image has at most eight elements. At $p=2$ the list reduces to $1,-1,2,-2$, so the bound by eight remains valid. The <two-isogeny rank formula> therefore gives
$$
2^r\leq\frac{2\cdot8}{4}=4,\qquad
\boxed{\operatorname{rank}E(\mathbb Q)\leq2.}
$$
In fact the same simple count yields $r\leq1$ when $p=2$. The positivity restriction on the first descent image is essential for the stated bound of two for all <primes>.

Use the <two-isogeny formula> formula with $a=\alpha$ and $b=-\alpha$. Its companion is
$$
D:v^2=u\bigl(u^2-2\alpha u+\alpha(\alpha+4)\bigr).
$$
Both curves are nonsingular because $\alpha\neq0,-4$. With $b'=\alpha(\alpha+4)$, choose
$$
\phi(x,y)=\left(x+\alpha-\frac{\alpha}{x},
y\left(1+\frac{\alpha}{x^2}\right)\right),
$$
$$
\widehat\phi(u,v)=
\left(\frac{u-2\alpha+b'/u}{4},
\frac{v(1-b'/u^2)}8\right).
$$
Each map extends across its exceptional affine points, and $\widehat\phi\phi=[2]$. On $D$, the first coordinate of the dual can also be written $v^2/(4u^2)$.

Let $P=(1,1)$ and solve $\widehat\phi(u,v)=P$. The first coordinate gives $v^2=4u^2$. Since $u\neq0$, the equation of $D$ then gives
$$
u^2-(2\alpha+4)u+\alpha(\alpha+4)=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
(u-\alpha)(u-\alpha-4)=0.
$$
The second coordinate selects the signs. The two rational preimages are exactly
$$
Q_1=(\alpha,-2\alpha),\qquad
Q_2=(\alpha+4,2(\alpha+4)).
$$
Substituting into the dual formula checks that both map to $(1,1)$. Their coordinates are nonexceptional under the assumptions, and the degree-two map has no further preimages.

The <two-torsion square-class homomorphism> $\alpha_D$ has kernel $\phi(C(\mathbb Q))$, and on these two points it is their nonzero $u$-coordinate modulo squares. Thus $Q_1$ is in the image of $\phi$ exactly when $\alpha$ is a rational square, and $Q_2$ is in that image exactly when $\alpha+4$ is a rational square.

If $P=2R$, then $\phi(R)$ is one of the two preimages, because $\widehat\phi\phi(R)=2R=P$. Conversely, if $Q_j=\phi(R)$, applying the dual shows $P=2R$. Therefore
$$
P\in2C(\mathbb Q)
\Longleftrightarrow
\alpha\in\mathbb Q^2\ \text{or}\ \alpha+4\in\mathbb Q^2.
$$
Finally, if an <integer> equals $(a/b)^2$ in lowest terms, then $b^2\mid a^2$ forces $b=1$. Thus rational squareness of these <integer> parameters is exactly <integer> squareness, and
$$
\boxed{(1,1)\in2C(\mathbb Q)
\iff\alpha\text{ or }\alpha+4\text{ is an integer square}.}
$$
This is the <two-isogeny halving criterion for the alpha family>.
