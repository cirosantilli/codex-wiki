= Solution
{parent=a}

**Conceptual scope:** the safe account below describes the functions of <spent nuclear fuel> management and the chemical basis of partitioning. It does not supply an ordered fissile-separation flowsheet, reagents, process conditions or purification instructions. In particular, the requested operational eight-step separation scheme is outside this answer's scope.

Eight useful functional objectives are: receiving and identifying the <spent nuclear fuel>; managing residual <decay heat>; providing <radioactive material confinement> and <radiation shielding>; establishing the chemical and radiological inventory; partitioning reusable constituents from waste at a conceptual level; maintaining accountable control of recovered materials; conditioning solid and liquid wastes; and retaining or treating <radioactive effluents>. These are **management and containment functions**, not a claim that the question specifies one universal eight-stage plant design. Several occur throughout a facility's operation rather than sequentially.

The chemical principle of <uranium>/<plutonium> partitioning is that an element's <oxidation state> and <complexation> affect its <chemical speciation>, <solubility> and relative affinity for immiscible phases. <solvent extraction> can therefore separate elements whose chemistry differs, even though it does not distinguish <uranium> <isotopes> by this chemical selectivity. A recovery operation must account for the accompanying <fission products>, contaminated process materials and secondary wastes; the recovered material is only one output. <radioactive material confinement>, remote handling and <nuclear material accountancy> remain necessary because chemical partitioning redistributes both radioactive and fissile inventories.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Use the thermal power when applying <nuclear fuel burnup>. No <capacity factor> is supplied, so take a full-power year of $365$ days; if the operating fraction were $u$, the annual quantities in this part would be multiplied by $u$.
$$
P_{\rm th}=\frac{1650}{0.37}=4459.459\ \mathrm{MW},\qquad
E_{\rm th}=4459.459(365)=1.627703\times10^6\ \mathrm{MW\,d\,yr^{-1}}.
$$
The corresponding initial heavy-metal <nuclear fuel> requirement is
$$
M=\frac{E_{\rm th}}{55000}=\boxed{29.5946\ \mathrm{t\,U\,yr^{-1}}}.
$$
All <nuclear fuel> and concentrate masses below are <uranium> masses, excluding oxygen. Let $F,P,W$ denote <uranium> feed, product and <enrichment tails>, and $x_f,x_p,x_w$ the <uranium-235> <mass fractions>. Conservation of total <uranium> and <uranium-235> gives
$$
F=P+W,\qquad Fx_f=Px_p+Wx_w,
$$
so
$$
\frac FP=\frac{x_p-x_w}{x_f-x_w}
=\frac{0.050-0.003}{0.007-0.003}=11.75.
$$
With $P=M$, the <uranium oxide concentrate> requirement is therefore
$$
F=11.75M=\boxed{347.7365\ \mathrm{t\,U\,yr^{-1}}},\qquad
W=318.1419\ \mathrm{t\,U\,yr^{-1}}.
$$
This is not the gross mass of oxide concentrate. Both $F-P-W=0$ and $0.007F-0.050P-0.003W=0$ provide independent balance checks.

= Solution

The recycle feed is the recovered <uranium>, not the whole initial <nuclear fuel> charge. After the stated loss it is
$$
F_r=27(1-0.015)=26.595\ \mathrm{t\,U\,yr^{-1}}.
$$
Using the same <enrichment tails> assay and the stipulated higher recycle-product assay, the <isotopic mass balance> gives
$$
P_r=F_r\frac{0.0085-0.003}{0.053-0.003}
=2.925450\ \mathrm{t\,U\,yr^{-1}},\qquad
W_r=F_r-P_r=23.669550\ \mathrm{t\,U\,yr^{-1}}.
$$
Treat the $5.3\%$ recycled product as replacing the same <uranium> mass of ordinary $5.0\%$ <nuclear fuel>: the additional <uranium-235> compensates for the supplied <uranium-236> penalty. It displaces fresh natural-uranium feed in the ratio calculated in part (b), hence
$$
\Delta F=11.75P_r=\boxed{34.37404\ \mathrm{t\,U\,yr^{-1}}}.
$$
The remaining natural feed is $313.36245\ \mathrm{t\,U\,yr^{-1}}$, a **9.885% resource saving** relative to the full-power once-through requirement. This credits one <uranium> recycle only; it adds no second-pass recycling or <plutonium> credit.

Separative work must be charged for producing the recycled <nuclear fuel> as well as credited for displaced fresh <nuclear fuel>. With <uranium-235> <mass fraction> $x$, use the <separative value function>
$$
V(x)=(1-2x)\ln\frac{1-x}{x},\qquad
S=PV(x_p)+WV(x_w)-FV(x_f).
$$
Masses in kilograms give work in kg-SWU. For ordinary fresh product the work per product kilogram is
$$
s_f=V(0.050)+10.75V(0.003)-11.75V(0.007)
=7.28741475\ \mathrm{kg\!\text{-}\!SWU\,kg^{-1}}.
$$
Thus the fresh work avoided is
$$
S_{\rm avoided}=1000P_rs_f=21318.9675\ \mathrm{kg\!\text{-}\!SWU\,yr^{-1}}.
$$
The recycled product still requires
$$
S_r=1000\big[P_rV(0.053)+W_rV(0.003)-F_rV(0.0085)\big]
=19726.2085\ \mathrm{kg\!\text{-}\!SWU\,yr^{-1}}.
$$
The **net separative-work saving** is consequently
$$
\Delta S=S_{\rm avoided}-S_r
=\boxed{1592.7590\ \mathrm{kg\!\text{-}\!SWU\,yr^{-1}}}.
$$
As a whole-cycle check, the fresh route requires $215668.0851$ kg-SWU per year, while the mixed fresh-plus-recycled supply requires $214075.3261$ kg-SWU. The net saving is only **0.7385%** of the original work. The recovered stream has a higher <uranium-235> assay than natural feed, but the higher required product assay uses most of the work credit. These equations are resource accounting and do not specify an <uranium enrichment> plant or its operation.

= Solution
{parent=d}

**Resource savings do not by themselves establish a financial saving.** The one-recycle case avoids purchasing $34374.04$ kg U in natural feed and $1592.76$ kg-SWU per year. Those are the benefits to compare with the incremental costs, not the whole value of the recovered <uranium> or the gross avoided fresh-product separative work.

Let $p_U$ be the concentrate price per kilogram of <uranium>, $p_S$ the price per kg-SWU, and $C_{\rm add}$ the annual net extra cost of recovery, recycle-specific conversion and fabrication, waste management and financing, relative to the once-through alternative. Recycling is cheaper under this simplified boundary only if
$$
\boxed{C_{\rm add}<34374.04\,p_U+1592.76\,p_S}.
$$
Equivalently, if $p_S$ and $C_{\rm add}$ were supplied, the break-even <uranium> price would be
$$
p_U^*=\frac{C_{\rm add}-1592.76p_S}{34374.04}.
$$
An avoided spent-fuel-disposal charge belongs as a credit in $C_{\rm add}$; a new <radioactive waste conditioning> or disposal liability belongs as a cost. They must not both be assigned the same sign simply because each concerns waste.

The natural-uranium saving is appreciable, but the fractional separative-work saving is small. Recovery losses and the <uranium-236> compensation limit the value of recycling. Additional radiological handling, separate fabrication campaigns, secondary waste streams, capital tied up during storage and recovery, and uncertain future disposal costs can matter. Conversely, a higher natural-uranium price, a lower incremental recovery cost or an independently justified disposal credit can favour reuse. The calculation gives a conditional comparison, **not a uniquely determined economic verdict or market-price threshold**, because no costs are supplied. <plutonium> utilisation is a different option and has not been silently credited to this uranium-only recycle.

= Solution
{parent=a}

A parent initially at rest emits an <alpha particle> and a recoiling heavy daughter. <momentum conservation> gives equal and opposite momenta, so their nonrelativistic <kinetic energies> satisfy
$$
\frac{T_d}{T_\alpha}=\frac{m_\alpha}{M_d},\qquad
T_\alpha=Q\frac{M_d}{M_d+m_\alpha},\qquad
T_d=Q\frac{m_\alpha}{M_d+m_\alpha}.
$$
Here $Q$ is the <kinetic energy> available to this branch; excitation of the daughter reduces it and may be followed by photon emission. For an illustrative $Q=5$ MeV, $m_\alpha=4\,\mathrm u$ and $M_d=236\,\mathrm u$, the energies are **$4.917$ MeV in the <alpha particle> and $83.3$ keV in the daughter**. The heavy daughter receives much less energy, but that does not imply much less atomic damage.

The fast <alpha particle> loses most of its energy through <electronic stopping>: <ionization> and <electronic excitation> along a relatively long path. It also undergoes nuclear collisions, particularly towards the end of its range, and can produce <displacement damage>. The slower, heavy, highly charged recoil couples strongly to surrounding nuclei and generates a compact <displacement cascade>. Successive knock-on atoms create <vacancies>, <interstitial defects> and clusters. Some <Frenkel pairs> recombine as the cascade relaxes; the initial collision count is therefore not the number of defects that remain.

A quantitative illustration comes from <brannerite> rather than an assumed universal <ceramic>. Simulations in the primary natural-mineral study used a $4.5$ MeV <alpha particle> and an $80$ keV daughter recoil. The corresponding ranges were about **$13$ micrometres and $23$ nanometres**. At the study's assumed $50$ eV <threshold displacement energy>, its tabulated results give roughly $560$–$571$ recoil <atomic displacements> and $91$–$100$ <alpha particle> <atomic displacements> per <radioactive decay>. The numbers depend strongly on the displacement threshold and material, but the shorter recoil path contains the dominant and much denser <ballistic radiation damage>.<Chemistry, microstructure, and alpha decay damage of natural brannerite by G. R. Lumpkin and others>[Chemical Geology 291, section 2.3 and Table 5; simulated ranges and threshold-dependent displacement counts]{ref}

In a susceptible <ceramic>, cascade cores can accumulate into an <amorphous fraction>; defects and associated relaxation can also change volume, <thermal conductivity> and mechanical response. <molecular dynamics> simulations of <zirconolite> found a strongly disordered core surrounded by more isolated <point defects>, supporting this distinction between local cascade damage and an entirely transformed specimen.<Molecular dynamics simulation of the α-recoil nucleus displacement cascade in zirconolite by L. Veiller and others>[Journal of Nuclear Materials 306, 61–72, abstract and cascade-core/point-defect results]{ref}

The <alpha particle> eventually becomes a <helium> atom after slowing and <charge exchange>. <helium> accumulation can have later microstructural effects distinct from the initial knock-on cascade. Material-dependent recombination and <annealing (materials science)> limit surviving disorder; a change in <diffraction> or <radiation-induced swelling> is not automatically <radiation-induced amorphization>, and <radiation-induced amorphization> is not automatically <dissolution> or release of the contained radionuclide.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Consider a representative bulk volume $\mathcal V$ and let $f_a(t)$ be its <amorphous fraction>. With <alpha-decay event rate density> $R$, during $dt$ the expected number of independent <alpha decay> events is $R\mathcal V\,dt$. Each produces <effective amorphization volume per decay> $V_\alpha$, but only the fraction $1-f_a$ of a randomly placed damage region was previously crystalline. The expected *new* amorphous volume is therefore
$$
d(f_a\mathcal V)=R\mathcal V\,dt\;V_\alpha(1-f_a).
$$
Dividing by $\mathcal V\,dt$ gives
$$
\frac{df_a}{dt}=RV_\alpha(1-f_a),\qquad f_a(0)=0.
$$
Separate variables and integrate:
$$
\int_0^{f_a}\frac{df}{1-f}=V_\alpha\int_0^tR\,ds,
\qquad -\ln(1-f_a)=RV_\alpha t.
$$
Hence, with <cumulative alpha-decay event density> $D=Rt$,
$$
\boxed{f_a(D)=1-e^{-DV_\alpha}=1-e^{-RtV_\alpha}}.
$$
The product $RtV_\alpha$ is dimensionless: events per cubic metre accumulated over time multiply cubic metres damaged per event. Here “dose” is an **event density**, not an absorbed energy per kilogram.

An independent <probability> interpretation reaches the same result. A particular point is covered by a number of random damage events with a <Poisson distribution> of mean $DV_\alpha$. Its <probability> of remaining crystalline is the zero-hit <probability> $e^{-DV_\alpha}$; the complementary fraction has been hit at least once. Overlapping events do not create a second copy of already amorphous material.

\Image[paper-ne4-overlap-model.png]
{title=Direct-impact amorphization as a function of dimensionless accumulated alpha-decay dose}

At small dose, $f_a\simeq DV_\alpha$; at larger dose, overlap bends the curve towards one. For example, $DV_\alpha=1$ gives $f_a=0.6321$, not full <radiation-induced amorphization>, and $f_a=0.99$ requires $DV_\alpha=\ln100=4.6052$.

The derivation assumes a homogeneous, initially crystalline material; independent random event locations; one-event <radiation-induced amorphization> of a fixed effective volume; and no recovery of that volume. It is the direct-impact model, not a model requiring several prior hits to cross a damage threshold. If $R$ varies while $V_\alpha$ stays fixed, replace $Rt$ by $\int_0^tR(s)\,ds$. If <annealing (materials science)>, material evolution or spatially correlated <actinide> distributions matter, this simple law need not hold. The subscript on $V_\alpha$ indexes an **<alpha decay> event**: it does not require the emitted <alpha particle> itself to produce the damaged volume.

= Solution
{parent=c}

The assumption is a **leading approximation for concentrated <ballistic radiation damage>**, not an exact statement that the <alpha particle> displaces no atoms. The daughter has only about $m_\alpha/M_d$ times the <alpha particle>'s <kinetic energy>, but it is much slower and loses a much larger fraction of that energy in collisions with target nuclei. Those collisions initiate closely packed recoil cascades. The <alpha particle> deposits most of its much larger energy into the electronic system over a path hundreds of times longer, with a less concentrated displacement contribution.

Thus the dense region susceptible to one-event loss of crystalline order can reasonably be associated chiefly with the daughter recoil. This explains why the effective damaged volume in the <direct-impact amorphization model> can be estimated or fitted from recoil-dominated cascades. It is the **spatial density and stopping channel of the damage**, not simply which particle carries more energy, that matters.

The approximation has a measurable limit. Turcotte's comparison of actinide-dioxide self-damage and external alpha irradiation found a nonzero alpha contribution, reporting about $19\%$ of total damage in the studied case. Comparison of diffraction-derived lattice changes with internal-reference measurements helps separate real <radiation-induced swelling> from instrument shifts, while the distinction between self-decay and external-alpha irradiation probes the omitted recoil channel.<Alpha radiation damage in the actinide dioxides by R. P. Turcotte>[BNWL-SA-5333, summary and experimental/results discussion; nonzero alpha damage and diffraction measurements]{ref}

Consequently “all <ballistic radiation damage>” is useful as the stated model assumption but should not be promoted to an exact material-independent law. <electronic excitation> can also couple to structural change, and recovery alters what survives. The derivation in part (b) remains valid for an effective one-event volume fitted consistently to the relevant regime; it does not establish that all <ceramics> or spent fuels share the same $V_\alpha$ or the same eventual <amorphous fraction>.
