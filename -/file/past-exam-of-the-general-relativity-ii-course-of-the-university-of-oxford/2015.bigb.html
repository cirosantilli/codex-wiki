= Solution
{parent=a}
{c}

A <Killing vector field> $K$ generates local <isometries>: its <flow of a vector field> $\Phi_s$ satisfies $\Phi_s^*g=g$. Equivalently, the <Lie derivative> of the <metric tensor> vanishes. For the <Levi-Civita connection>, <metric compatibility> converts the coordinate expression into the <Killing equation>:
$$
\boxed{\mathcal L_Kg=0,\qquad \nabla_\mu K_\nu+\nabla_\nu K_\mu=0.}
$$
**The <Levi-Civita connection> is an implicit extra assumption in this question.** The printed assumption of vanishing <torsion tensor> alone does not imply <metric compatibility>. For example, on <Minkowski spacetime>, take the <affine connection> whose only nonzero <Christoffel symbol> is $\Gamma^0{}_{00}=1$. The <vector field> $K=\partial_0$ preserves the <Minkowski metric>, but $K_0=-1$ gives $\nabla_0K_0=1$, contradicting the <Killing equation>. The following calculation, and the covariant formulas in part (b), use the <Levi-Civita connection>; this is the role of <metric compatibility in Killing identities>.

For the stated <curvature sign convention>, the <Ricci identity> on a <covector> has a minus sign:
$$
[\nabla_\mu,\nabla_\nu]K_\rho=-R^\sigma{}_{\rho\mu\nu}K_\sigma.
$$
Write $A_{\mu\nu\rho}=\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu K_\rho$. Differentiating the <Killing equation> gives $A_{\mu\nu\rho}=-A_{\mu\rho\nu}$. Alternately using this relation and the <Ricci identity> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
A_{\mu\nu\rho}
&=-A_{\mu\rho\nu}
=-A_{\rho\mu\nu}+R^\sigma{}_{\nu\mu\rho}K_\sigma\\
&=A_{\rho\nu\mu}+R^\sigma{}_{\nu\mu\rho}K_\sigma\\
&=A_{\nu\rho\mu}-R^\sigma{}_{\mu\rho\nu}K_\sigma+R^\sigma{}_{\nu\mu\rho}K_\sigma\\
&=-A_{\mu\nu\rho}-R^\sigma{}_{\rho\mu\nu}K_\sigma-R^\sigma{}_{\mu\rho\nu}K_\sigma+R^\sigma{}_{\nu\mu\rho}K_\sigma.
\end{aligned}
$$
The <first Bianchi identity> and antisymmetry of the last two indices of the <Riemann curvature tensor> reduce the three curvature terms to $2R^\sigma{}_{\mu\nu\rho}K_\sigma$. Consequently the <second covariant derivative of a Killing vector> is
$$
\boxed{\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu K_\rho=R^\sigma{}_{\mu\nu\rho}K_\sigma.}
$$

For a <conformal Killing vector field>, the same <metric compatibility> calculation gives
$$
\mathcal L_Xg_{\mu\nu}=\nabla_\mu X_\nu+\nabla_\nu X_\mu=2\lambda g_{\mu\nu}.
$$
Thus its <conformal Killing equation> is $\nabla_{(\mu}X_{\nu)}=\lambda g_{\mu\nu}$. Taking the <tensor contraction> with the <inverse metric>, and using $g^{\mu\nu}g_{\mu\nu}=4$, determines the <conformal Killing factor>:
$$
\boxed{\nabla_{(\mu}X_{\nu)}=\lambda g_{\mu\nu},\qquad \lambda=\frac14\nabla_\mu X^\mu.}
$$
Parentheses include the factor $1/2$ in <tensor index symmetrization>.

Use an <affine parameter> $s$ on the <geodesic>, so its <tangent vector> $U$ obeys $\nabla_UU=0$. The <product rule>, <metric compatibility>, and the <conformal Killing equation> give
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{ds}(X_\mu U^\mu)
&=U^\nu U^\mu\nabla_\nu X_\mu+X_\mu(\nabla_UU)^\mu\\
&=U^\nu U^\mu\nabla_{(\nu}X_{\mu)}
=\lambda g_{\mu\nu}U^\mu U^\nu.
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{ds}(X\cdot U)=\lambda\,g(U,U).}
$$
**It is conserved on every <null geodesic> with an <affine parameter>, but generally not on other <geodesics>.** On a <timelike geodesic> parametrized by <proper time>, $g(U,U)=-1$, so the derivative is $-\lambda$. A <Killing vector field>, or a <geodesic> on which $\lambda$ vanishes, supplies the exceptional conservation. This is the <conformal Killing geodesic identity>.

For an arbitrary parameter with $\nabla_UU=fU$, the right-hand side acquires $f(X\cdot U)$. Thus even for a <null geodesic>, the simple conserved expression presupposes an <affine parameter>.

In <Cartesian coordinates> on <Minkowski spacetime>, the <conformal Killing equation> becomes
$$
\partial_\mu X_\nu+\partial_\nu X_\mu=2\lambda\eta_{\mu\nu}.
$$
Differentiate the equations with index pairs $(\nu,\rho)$, $(\mu,\rho)$ and $(\mu,\nu)$ by $\partial_\mu$, $\partial_\nu$ and $\partial_\rho$, respectively. Adding the first two and subtracting the third, <Clairaut's theorem> yields the <flat conformal Killing integrability> formula
$$
\partial_\mu\partial_\nu X_\rho
=\eta_{\nu\rho}\partial_\mu\lambda
+\eta_{\mu\rho}\partial_\nu\lambda
-\eta_{\mu\nu}\partial_\rho\lambda.
$$
Apply $\partial^\rho$ and use $\partial^\rho X_\rho=4\lambda$. With the <d'Alembert operator> $\Box=\eta^{\alpha\beta}\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta$, this gives
$$
4\partial_\mu\partial_\nu\lambda
=2\partial_\mu\partial_\nu\lambda-\eta_{\mu\nu}\Box\lambda,
\qquad
2\partial_\mu\partial_\nu\lambda=-\eta_{\mu\nu}\Box\lambda.
$$
A <tensor contraction> with $\eta^{\mu\nu}$ gives $2\Box\lambda=-4\Box\lambda$, so $\Box\lambda=0$. Returning to the preceding equation proves the stronger statement
$$
\boxed{\partial_\mu\partial_\nu\lambda=0.}
$$
Thus the <conformal Killing factor> is an <affine function>, not an arbitrary solution of the <wave equation>.

Write the <affine function> obtained in part (iii) as $\lambda=c+2b_\mu x^\mu$, with constant $c$ and $b_\mu$. A <conformal Killing vector field> with precisely this <conformal Killing factor> is
$$
X_{\mathrm{part}}^\mu=cx^\mu+2(b\cdot x)x^\mu-x^2b^\mu,
\qquad x^2=\eta_{\alpha\beta}x^\alpha x^\beta.
$$
Direct <partial differentiation> verifies its <conformal Killing equation>. Subtracting it from any other solution leaves a <Killing vector field> $Y$. The formula in part (iii), now with $\lambda=0$, says $\partial_\mu\partial_\nu Y_\rho=0$. Therefore $Y$ is affine, and the <Killing equation> makes its linear coefficient antisymmetric after lowering an index. Every solution is consequently
$$
\boxed{X^\mu=a^\mu+\omega^\mu{}_{\nu}x^\nu+cx^\mu+2(b\cdot x)x^\mu-x^2b^\mu,
\qquad \omega_{\mu\nu}=-\omega_{\nu\mu}.}
$$
The four constant $a^\mu$ generate <translations>, the six independent $\omega_{\mu\nu}$ generate <Lorentz transformations>, $c$ generates a <dilation>, and the four $b^\mu$ generate <special conformal transformations>. Their constant, linear and quadratic terms are independent; within the linear terms, the antisymmetric coefficient cannot equal a nonzero multiple of the identity. Hence the <conformal Killing algebra of Minkowski spacetime> has
$$
\boxed{\dim\mathfrak{conf}(\mathbb R^{1,3})=4+6+1+4=15.}
$$
For $\lambda=1$, the canonical choice is the <dilation> generator $D=x^\mu\partial_\mu$. Its <flow of a vector field> solves $dx^\mu/ds=x^\mu$, giving
$$
\boxed{\Phi_s(x)=e^s x,\qquad \Phi_s^*\eta=e^{2s}\eta,\qquad \Phi_s\circ\Phi_t=\Phi_{s+t}.}
$$
One may add any <Killing vector field> without changing $\lambda=1$, so this choice is not unique. The displayed <dilation> gives a global <one-parameter group> of <diffeomorphisms>. The count of fifteen concerns independent <conformal Killing vector fields>; some <special conformal transformations> have only local flows on all of affine <Minkowski spacetime>.

A nonzero <vector field> has <hypersurface orthogonality> when it is locally normal to a family of <hypersurfaces>. In terms of its associated <differential one-form> $K^\flat=g_{\mu\nu}K^\nu dx^\mu$, the <Frobenius theorem> gives the equivalent conditions
$$
K^\flat=f\,dh,\qquad K^\flat\wedge dK^\flat=0,
\qquad K_{[\mu}\nabla_\nu K_{\rho]}=0,
$$
locally with nonzero $f$ and $dh$; square brackets denote <tensor index antisymmetrization>.

All coefficients of the <Reissner-Nordstrom metric> are independent of $t$, and $K=\partial_t$ has constant coordinate components. Thus the <Lie derivative> obeys $\mathcal L_Kg=\partial_tg=0$: $K$ is a <Killing vector field>. Moreover,
$$
K^\flat=-F(r)\,dt,\qquad dK^\flat=-F'(r)\,dr\wedge dt,
\qquad \boxed{K^\flat\wedge dK^\flat=0.}
$$
Thus $K$ has <hypersurface orthogonality>, with the $t=\text{constant}$ <hypersurfaces> providing the orthogonal family wherever this chart is regular. It is timelike where $F>0$, since $g(K,K)=-F$. Its <hypersurface orthogonality> also holds in a regular extension across a horizon; singular static <coordinates> do not invalidate the <Frobenius theorem> test.

For the three cases, distinguish a <curvature singularity> from a <coordinate singularity>. The <Reissner-Nordstrom Kretschmann scalar> is
$$
\mathcal K=R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}R^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}
=\frac{48M^2}{r^6}-\frac{96MQ^2}{r^7}+\frac{56Q^4}{r^8}.
$$
It diverges at $r=0$ when either $M$ or $Q$ is nonzero. Any positive root of $F$ instead admits <Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates>: set $v=t+\int dr/F$ to obtain
$$
ds^2=-F\,dv^2+2\,dv\,dr+r^2(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2).
$$
The <determinant> of its $(v,r)$ block is $-1$, including at $F=0$. Hence positive roots of $F$ are removable <coordinate singularities> of the static chart. The apparent degeneracy at the poles of the angular chart is also a <coordinate singularity>.

For $0<Q^2<M^2$ there are two distinct positive roots:
$$
\boxed{r_\pm=M\pm\sqrt{M^2-Q^2},\qquad F=\frac{(r-r_+)(r-r_-)}{r^2}.}
$$
Both roots are <coordinate singularities> of the static chart. In the standard extension, $r_+$ is the outer <event horizon> and $r_-$ is an inner <Cauchy horizon>. The <curvature singularity> is $r=0$. Between the horizons $F<0$, so the radial direction is timelike; for $0<r<r_-$, $F>0$ again and the central <curvature singularity> is timelike. These are the three radial regions of the <global structure of a charged spherical black hole>.<General Relativity by David Tong>[Sections 6.2.2–6.2.5, printed pages 260–268]{ref}

For completeness, if $Q=0$ and $M>0$, the <Schwarzschild spacetime> limit has just the positive horizon $r=2M$ and a spacelike <curvature singularity> at $r=0$; the formal root $r_-=0$ of $r^2F$ is not a regular inner horizon. If $M=Q=0$, the <metric tensor> is flat and the radial origin is regular in <Cartesian coordinates>.

A regular <hypersurface> $h=0$, with $dh\ne0$, is a <null hypersurface> if its normal is null:
$$
\boxed{g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu h\,\partial_\nu h=0\quad\text{on }h=0.}
$$
The raised normal is then also tangent to the <hypersurface>, and its <induced metric> is degenerate. This is a local geometric property.

In an <asymptotically flat spacetime>, choose its <future null infinity> $\mathcal I^+$. The <black hole> region and future <event horizon> are
$$
\boxed{\mathcal B=\mathcal M\setminus J^-(\mathcal I^+),\qquad \mathcal H^+=\partial J^-(\mathcal I^+).}
$$
Here $J^-(\mathcal I^+)$ is the <causal past> of <future null infinity>: the region from which a future-directed <causal curve> can escape to arbitrarily distant observers. An <event horizon> therefore depends on the global future <causal structure>, and an arbitrary <null hypersurface> need not be an <event horizon>.

In the extremal case, the squared norm of the <Killing vector field> is
$$
g(K,K)=-\left(1-\frac Mr\right)^2.
$$
For $M=|Q|>0$, it is negative for every regular $r\ne M$ and vanishes precisely at $r=M$ in the extended <spacetime>. Thus
$$
\boxed{K\text{ is null exactly on }r=M.}
$$
The static chart cannot itself test the geometry at its horizon. In the regular <Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates>, the normal to $h=r-M=0$ is $n=dr$, and
$$
g^{-1}(n,n)=g^{rr}=F,\qquad n^\sharp=\partial_v+F\partial_r.
$$
At $r=M$ this gives $n^\sharp=\partial_v=K$, a nonzero <null vector> tangent to the surface. Therefore **$r=M$ is a <null hypersurface>**, even though $F$ has the same sign on its two sides.

On the equatorial path, the <Lagrangian> per unit <mass> is
$$
L=\frac12\left(-F\dot t^2+F^{-1}\dot r^2+r^2\dot\phi^2\right)-\frac{eQ}{mr}\dot t.
$$
Both $t$ and $\phi$ are <cyclic coordinates>. The <Euler-Lagrange equations> therefore conserve their <canonical momenta>:
$$
p_t=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot t}=-F\dot t-\frac{eQ}{mr},\qquad
p_\phi=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot\phi}=r^2\dot\phi.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{E=-mp_t=mF\dot t+\frac{eQ}{r},\qquad l=p_\phi=r^2\dot\phi,\qquad \dot E=\dot l=0.}
$$
Here $E$ is the total conserved <energy> in the specified gauge of the <electromagnetic four-potential>. The paper's $l$ is the <angular momentum> per unit <mass>; the physical <angular momentum> is $ml$. The equatorial choice is consistent with the $\theta$ <Euler-Lagrange equation>, since its angular force term contains $\sin\theta\cos\theta=0$ at $\theta=\pi/2$ and the <electromagnetic four-potential> has no angular component.

With $\tau$ the <proper time> of the massive <test particle>, the <four-velocity> has squared norm $-1$:
$$
-1=-F\dot t^2+F^{-1}\dot r^2+r^2\dot\phi^2.
$$
The <conserved quantities> in part (i) give
$$
F\dot t=\frac1m\left(E-\frac{eQ}{r}\right),\qquad \dot\phi=\frac l{r^2}.
$$
Multiplying the normalization by $F$ and substituting gives the <charged-particle radial equation in Reissner-Nordstrom spacetime>:
$$
\boxed{\dot r^2=\frac1{m^2}\left(E-\frac{eQ}{r}\right)^2-F(r)\left(1+\frac{l^2}{r^2}\right).}
$$
At a radial <turning point> $r_*>0$, the left side is zero, so
$$
F(r_*)\left(1+\frac{l^2}{r_*^2}\right)
=\frac1{m^2}\left(E-\frac{eQ}{r_*}\right)^2\ge0.
$$
The parenthesis on the left is strictly positive. Hence $F(r_*)\ge0$. The factorization in part (b) now yields
$$
\boxed{r_*\ge r_+\quad\text{or}\quad 0<r_*\le r_-.}
$$
**There is no radial <turning point> between the horizons.** Indeed, for $r_-<r<r_+$, the right-hand side of the radial equation is strictly positive, independently of the <electric charge> $e$ of the <test particle>. This is the <charged-particle turning-point exclusion in Reissner-Nordstrom spacetime>.

The inequalities are necessary, not a claim that a radial <turning point> exists for every $E,l,e$. At a regular horizon crossing a <timelike vector> cannot be tangent to a <null hypersurface>, so a massive <test particle> actually has $\dot r\ne0$ there. Algebraic equality at $r_\pm$ would require $E=eQ/r_\pm$ and does not describe a regular finite-<proper time> radial <turning point> on the horizon. In the <Schwarzschild spacetime> limit $Q=0$, the positive-radius inner alternative disappears.

The <scale factor> $a(t)>0$ specifies the common time dependence of <proper distance in cosmology> between <comoving observers>. Their <comoving coordinates> remain fixed, while their separation is multiplied by $a(t)$. Thus $\dot a>0$ means expansion and $\dot a<0$ means contraction. The coordinate $t$ is <cosmic time>, measured by the <proper time> of a <comoving observer>.

The parameter $k$ fixes the sign of the <spatial curvature of an FLRW universe>. More precisely, the spatial <sectional curvature> and three-dimensional <scalar curvature> are
$$
\boxed{K_{\mathrm{spatial}}=\frac{k}{a^2},\qquad {}^{(3)}R=\frac{6k}{a^2}.}
$$
The cases $k=1,0,-1$ have locally <spherical geometry>, <Euclidean geometry>, and <hyperbolic geometry>, respectively. For $k=\pm1$, $a$ is the <curvature radius of a constant-curvature space> in the displayed normalization. Local <curvature> alone does not fix the global <topology>.

= Solution

The <Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker metric> implements the <cosmological principle>: on the scales being modelled, the spatial slices have <spatial homogeneity> and <cosmological isotropy>. There is a family of <comoving observers> whose orthogonal slices share one <cosmic time> and one <scale factor>. Each spatial slice is a <locally maximally symmetric space>, with time-independent normalized $k$.

The matter is represented by a <perfect fluid in general relativity>, at rest relative to the <comoving observers>, with <energy density> $\varrho(t)$ and <pressure> $p(t)$ depending only on <cosmic time>. Its <stress-energy tensor> is
$$
\boxed{T_{\mu\nu}=(\varrho+p)u_\mu u_\nu+p g_{\mu\nu},\qquad u=\partial_t.}
$$
There is no preferred spatial direction, so there is no <anisotropic stress> or spatial <heat flux> in that rest frame. Gravity obeys the <Einstein field equations>; the equations in this paper contain no separate <cosmological constant>, although a component with $p=-\varrho$ can represent one. An <equation of state> relating $p$ and $\varrho$ closes the evolution equations. <Spatial homogeneity> and <cosmological isotropy> are idealizations of the cosmological background; they do not assert that individual local structures are uniform.

Set $H=\dot a/a$, the <Hubble parameter>. The first supplied component of the <Einstein field equations> gives the <Friedmann acceleration equation>
$$
\frac{\ddot a}{a}=-\frac{4\pi G}{3}(\varrho+3p).
$$
Substitution into the second supplied equation gives
$$
2\left(H^2+\frac{k}{a^2}\right)
=4\pi G(\varrho-p)+\frac{4\pi G}{3}(\varrho+3p)
=\frac{16\pi G}{3}\varrho.
$$
Therefore the first <Friedmann equation> is
$$
\boxed{\dot a^2+k=\frac{8\pi G}{3}\varrho a^2.}
$$
Differentiate this equation with respect to <cosmic time>:
$$
2\dot a\ddot a=\frac{8\pi G}{3}\left(a^2\dot\varrho+2a\varrho\dot a\right).
$$
Insert the <Friedmann acceleration equation> on the left and cancel $8\pi Ga/3$, which is nonzero for $a>0$. This gives
$$
-\dot a(\varrho+3p)=a\dot\varrho+2\varrho\dot a,
\qquad
\boxed{a\dot\varrho+3(\varrho+p)\dot a=0.}
$$
This is the <cosmological continuity equation>, expressing <stress-energy conservation> for the <perfect fluid in general relativity>. No division by $\dot a$ was made, so the derivation remains valid at a <cosmological bounce> or an instant of maximum expansion.

For $a>0$, the assumed inequality and the <Friedmann acceleration equation> imply $\ddot a\le0$. Thus $a$ is a <concave function>. Let $a_0=a(t_0)>0$ and $v_0=\dot a(t_0)>0$. At every earlier time in the same regular expanding interval, $\dot a(t)\ge v_0$. Integration gives the <concavity bound on past FLRW evolution>:
$$
a_0-a(t)=\int_t^{t_0}\dot a(s)\,ds\ge v_0(t_0-t),
\qquad
\boxed{a(t)\le a_0-v_0(t_0-t).}
$$
The right-hand side reaches zero at $t=t_0-a_0/v_0$. Hence an everywhere regular positive <scale factor> cannot extend arbitrarily far into the past. With the usual implicit continuation assumption that the cosmological solution remains regular while $a>0$, it reaches $a(t_B)=0$ at a finite past boundary satisfying
$$
\boxed{0<t_0-t_B\le\frac{a_0}{v_0}=\frac1{H(t_0)}.}
$$
**Past expansion lasts at most the present inverse <Hubble parameter>.** The inequality is the essential argument, and equality is attained by a linearly growing <scale factor>.

There is a literal qualification to the claim that $a$ must reach zero: the printed hypothesis alone does not exclude an earlier <curvature singularity> with positive limiting $a$. In arbitrary fixed units, take $k=0$ and $a(t)=1+\sqrt t$ for $t>0$, and define $\varrho,p$ using the two <Friedmann equations> and the <Friedmann acceleration equation>. Then $\dot a>0$, $\ddot a<0$, and $\varrho+3p=-3\ddot a/(4\pi Ga)>0$, but $a\to1$ while the <energy density> and <pressure> diverge as $t\downarrow0$. Thus the rigorous unconditional conclusion is the finite bound on a regular past interval; reaching zero additionally requires the stated continuation assumption. Nor does a zero of $a$ alone prove a <curvature singularity>, as the <Milne model> illustrates.

For the <equation of state> $p=-\varrho$, the <cosmological continuity equation> gives $a\dot\varrho=0$. Hence $\varrho=\varrho_0$ is constant. First take the physically intended positive <energy density> and define
$$
H_\Lambda=\sqrt{\frac{8\pi G\varrho_0}{3}}>0.
$$
The <Friedmann equation> and the original <Friedmann acceleration equation> become
$$
\dot a^2+k=H_\Lambda^2a^2,\qquad \ddot a=H_\Lambda^2a.
$$
Solve the second equation as $a=Ae^{H_\Lambda t}+Be^{-H_\Lambda t}$. The first equation then requires $4H_\Lambda^2AB=k$. Choosing the time origin separately in each solution gives all positive branches:
$$
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
k&a(t)&\text{domain and finite past zero on the expanding branch}\\ \hline
0&A_0e^{\pm H_\Lambda(t-t_c)},\ A_0>0&-\infty<t<\infty;\ \text{none}\\
+1&H_\Lambda^{-1}\cosh[H_\Lambda(t-t_b)]&-\infty<t<\infty;\ \text{none}\\
-1&H_\Lambda^{-1}\sinh[H_\Lambda(t-t_B)]&t>t_B;\ a\to0\text{ at }t_B
\end{array}}
$$
For $k=-1$, there is also the time reverse $a=H_\Lambda^{-1}\sinh[H_\Lambda(t_B-t)]$ on $t<t_B$, which contracts to a future zero. For $k=+1$, the <cosmological bounce> joins contraction to expansion at the positive minimum $a(t_b)=H_\Lambda^{-1}$. Retaining $\ddot a=H_\Lambda^2a$ at the minimum excludes a spurious permanently static solution that would satisfy the first-order constraint alone.

These are <vacuum-energy FLRW solutions>: the expanding flat branch, <closed slicing of de Sitter spacetime>, and <open slicing of de Sitter spacetime>. To compare them at the same <energy density>, the figure sets $T=H_\Lambda(t-t_c)$, takes $A_0=H_\Lambda^{-1}$, and aligns the open boundary and closed minimum at $T=0$. Thus the three plotted functions are $H_\Lambda a=e^T$, $\cosh T$, and $\sinh T$, the last only for $T>0$.

Only the expanding $k=-1$ branch has a finite past zero. The expanding $k=0$ branch tends to zero only as $t\to-\infty$, and the $k=+1$ branch is bounded below. All three describe portions of <de Sitter spacetime>, with <Ricci scalar> $R=12H_\Lambda^2$ and <Kretschmann scalar> $24H_\Lambda^4$. In particular the $k=-1$ zero is a boundary of that slicing, not a <curvature singularity>. The inequality in part (d) does not apply, because $\varrho+3p=-2\varrho_0<0$.

For zero <energy density>, the remaining solutions are $a=A_0>0$ for $k=0$, and the expanding or contracting <Milne model> $a=\pm(t-t_B)>0$ for $k=-1$; no positive real solution exists for $k=+1$. If negative <energy density> is admitted mathematically, write $\alpha^2=-8\pi G\varrho_0/3>0$. Only $k=-1$ is possible, with $a=\alpha^{-1}\sin[\alpha(t-t_B)]$ for $0<\alpha(t-t_B)<\pi$, an <open slicing of anti-de Sitter spacetime>. These limiting and negative-density cases do not alter the positive-density comparison above.

Take the expanding flat branch $a(t)=Ae^{H_\Lambda t}$, with $A>0$. A <radial null geodesic in FLRW spacetime> satisfies $d\rho/dt=\pm1/a(t)$. For the incoming <past light cone> of the <spacetime event> $(t_0,\rho=0)$, integration gives
$$
\boxed{\rho(t;t_0)=\int_t^{t_0}\frac{ds}{a(s)}
=\frac1{H_\Lambda}\left(\frac1{a(t)}-\frac1{a(t_0)}\right),\qquad t\le t_0,}
$$
with arbitrary angular direction. Equivalently, <conformal time> $\eta=-1/(H_\Lambda a)$ gives $\rho=\eta_0-\eta$.

Let the reception time tend to infinity. The largest <comoving radial distance> from which a signal emitted at time $t$ can reach the central <comoving observer> is
$$
\rho_{\mathrm h}(t)=\int_t^\infty\frac{ds}{a(s)}=\frac1{H_\Lambda a(t)}.
$$
Thus the <cosmological event horizon> has constant <proper distance in cosmology>:
$$
\boxed{\rho=\frac1{H_\Lambda a(t)},\qquad D_{\mathrm h}=a(t)\rho_{\mathrm h}=H_\Lambda^{-1}.}
$$
An emitted <spacetime event> is seen at a finite time if and only if $a(t)\rho<H_\Lambda^{-1}$. Equality would require $t_0=\infty$, while an event with $a(t)\rho>H_\Lambda^{-1}$ never reaches the observer. This is a bound on events at their emission time; a fixed <comoving observer> can have earlier visible events and later invisible ones.

The boundary really is a <null hypersurface>. For $f(t,\rho)=\rho-[H_\Lambda a(t)]^{-1}$, its normal has components $\partial_tf=1/a$ and $\partial_\rho f=1$, so
$$
g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu f\partial_\nu f=-\frac1{a^2}+\frac1{a^2}=0.
$$
The finite future integral, and hence this <observer event horizon>, uses the expanding branch. For the contracting flat solution $a=Ae^{-H_\Lambda t}$, the same integral diverges and there is no future <observer event horizon> of this kind; the static zero-density solution likewise has none.
