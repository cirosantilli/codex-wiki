= Probability theory
{wiki}

Probability theory describes uncertainty through a <probability space>: possible outcomes form a <sample space>, observable questions are <events>, and a <probability measure> assigns them numbers between zero and one. The <Kolmogorov axioms> make those assignments consistent. They let us combine information about several <events>, update it through <conditional probability>, and study <random variables> without listing every possible outcome separately.

For example, roll two dice whose results are <independent random variables>, each with a <discrete uniform distribution> on $\{1,\ldots,6\}$. There are 36 equally likely ordered outcomes. Only $(1,6),(2,5),\ldots,(6,1)$ have sum seven, so that <event> has <probability> $6/36=1/6$. The sum itself is not uniformly distributed: sum two has only one outcome. Choosing a <probability distribution> for the elementary outcomes is part of the model; equal likelihood does not follow merely from counting the named possibilities.

The subject becomes especially useful when complete outcome lists are enormous or infinite. <Independence> turns a joint calculation into simpler pieces, <expected value> summarizes a <random variable>, and the <law of large numbers> explains how averages can become predictable even when individual observations are uncertain. The <central limit theorem> describes the scale and shape of many remaining fluctuations. These are distinct claims: a small <variance> of an average does not make every observation close to its <expected value>.

There is a satisfying unity here: dice, hidden causes, and <Brownian motion> all use the same rules for <events> and <conditional probability>. A model still needs justification. Its exact deductions describe what its assumptions imply; comparison with observations belongs to <statistical inference>.

= Bertrand's paradox
{c}
{synonym}

Different precise measures on random chords give different probabilities; the phrase “random chord” does not select a unique <probability distribution>. In a circle of radius $r$, a chord exceeds the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle exactly when its midpoint distance from the center is below $r/2$. Uniform midpoint area gives probability $1/4$, independent uniform endpoints give $1/3$, and uniform midpoint distance gives $1/2$. The difference is in the sampling laws, not a contradiction in <probability theory>.

= Polygon probability for independent uniform rods
{parent=Probability theory}
{title2=$1-1/(n-1)!$}

For $n\ge3$ independent lengths with the <uniform distribution> on $[0,1]$, the <probability> of forming a nondegenerate <polygon> is $1-1/(n-1)!$. The necessary and sufficient condition is that every length is less than the sum of the others. The failure events are disjoint except on sets of probability zero. The sum of $n-1$ lengths has <probability density function> $s^{n-2}/(n-2)!$ on $0<s<1$, by repeated <convolution of probability densities>. A specified rod is too long with probability $\int_0^1(1-s)s^{n-2}/(n-2)!\,ds=1/n!$; summing over the $n$ rods proves the formula. For three and four rods the probabilities are $1/2$ and $5/6$.

= Coverage of a sphere by random open hemispheres
{parent=Probability theory}
{title2=$1-\frac{N(N-1)+2}{2^N}$}

The probability that $N\geq1$ <independent> uniformly oriented open <hemispheres> cover a <sphere> is the displayed quantity. Coverage fails exactly when their normals lie in a closed hemisphere. With probability one, no three normals are coplanar and no pair is antipodal; a closed hemisphere containing the normals can then be perturbed into an open one. Condition on the unoriented axes of the normals. Their $N$ orientation signs remain independent fair bits, and the <regions of a general-position great-circle arrangement> realize exactly $N(N-1)+2$ of the $2^N$ possible sign vectors. Each such vector corresponds to normals that fit into one open hemisphere, proving the complementary probability. The argument counts equiprobable orientation choices, not equal-area regions.

= Pairwise independent events
{parent=Probability theory}

= Pairwise independence
{synonym}

<Pairwise independent events> have factorizable two-event intersections. <Independent events> require the corresponding factorization for every finite subfamily, which is stronger. If three events have common probability $s$ and every pair and triple intersect in the same event of probability $q$, pairwise independence requires $q=s^2$, while full independence additionally requires $q=s^3$. Both hold only for $s=0$ or $s=1$.

= Point process
{parent=Probability theory}

A point process is a random locally finite configuration of points, usually represented as a random counting measure. A finite <eigenvalue> configuration of a <random matrix> is an example. Multiplicities and whether the process is simple should be specified.

= Neyman-Scott process
{parent=Point process}
{c}
{wiki=Neyman–Scott_process}

A <Neyman-Scott process> places <independent> offspring clusters around points of a Poisson parent process, with an independently drawn offspring count and displacements. Poisson offspring counts make the offspring process conditionally Poisson given the parents, and therefore give a <Cox process>. Arbitrary offspring counts need not have that property.

= Intensity measure
{synonym}

The <intensity measure of a point process> $\Pi$ is the measure $\mu(A)=\mathbb E[\Pi(A)]$. It can have infinite total mass even when counts on bounded or finite-intensity sets are finite. For a <Poisson random measure>, this measure also specifies every finite-set count distribution.

= Void probability functional
{parent=Point process}
{title2=$v(A)=\mathbb P(M(A)=0)$}

The void <probability> functional assigns to a measurable set the <probability> that it contains no atom of a random point <measure>. For a <Poisson random measure> of intensity $\mu$, it is $e^{-\mu(A)}$. The <exponential void probabilities characterize a Poisson random measure> result uses simplicity; doubling every atom of a Poisson <measure> preserves void <probabilities> but changes count distributions.

= Exponential void probabilities forbid fixed atoms
{parent=Void probability functional}

If bounded intervals have void <probabilities> $e^{-\lambda(I)}$ for <Lebesgue measure> $\lambda$, a fixed deterministic point $x$ has zero chance of being an atom. Surround it by intervals of lengths tending to zero and use $\mathbb P(M(\{x\})>0)\leq1-e^{-\lambda(I)}$. This holds for each fixed endpoint, not simultaneously for all real points of a nonempty point process.

= Simple locally finite point measure
{parent=Point process}
{title2=$M=\sum_j\delta_{x_j}$}

A point <measure> is a countable sum of <Dirac measures>. It is simple if all its atoms have mass one, and locally finite if every bounded set has finite mass. Simplicity lets fine enough cells count individual points, whereas occupation indicators would lose multiplicities in a nonsimple <measure>.

= Dyadic occupancy approximation of a simple point measure
{parent=Simple locally finite point measure}
{title2=$M_n(J)=\sum_{D\in\mathcal D_n,\ D\subset J}\mathbf1_{\{M(D)>0\}}$}

For a fixed bounded interval with no atoms at its endpoints, the occupation count of fully contained half-open <dyadic intervals> increases to the true count of a <simple locally finite point measure>. Refinement cannot reduce occupancy. Finitely many distinct atoms in a bounding interval have positive minimum separation; for fine enough cells each atom lies alone, and every interior atom's cell is contained in the target interval. Thus the counts eventually agree, path by path.

= Determinantal point process
{parent=Point process}
{title2=$R_k(\mathbf x)=\det[K(x_i,x_j)]_{i,j=1}^k$}

A point process is determinantal with kernel $K$ relative to a specified reference measure if every <correlation function of a point process> is the indicated <determinant>. A rank-$m$ <finite-rank projection kernel> gives an exactly $m$-point process, whose symmetric joint density is $\det[K(x_i,x_j)]/m!$.

= Correlation function of a point process
{parent=Point process}
{title2=$R_k$}

= Correlation functions of a point process
{synonym}

The $k$th correlation <function> is the density of the $k$th <factorial> moment measure: integrating a nonnegative test <function> against $R_k$ gives the <expectation> of its sum over ordered $k$-tuples of distinct points. For exactly $m$ exchangeably labelled points with symmetric joint density $f_m$, it is $m!/(m-k)!$ times the marginal density of $k$ labels. It is not normalized to have total mass one.

= Random matrix
{parent=Probability theory}

A random <matrix> is a <matrix> whose entries are <random variables>. Its <eigenvalues> form a random configuration, and its <empirical spectral measure> records their distribution with equal mass on each <eigenvalue>. Entry assumptions and normalization determine the spectral regime.

= Wishart distribution
{c}
{parent=Random matrix}
{wiki}
{title2=$W_p(\nu,V)$}

For integer $\nu$, the sum $\sum_{j=1}^{\nu}X_jX_j^T$ of independent $N_p(0,V)$ vectors has a Wishart distribution. Its positive-definite density extends to real $\nu>p-1$ and is proportional to $|W|^{(\nu-p-1)/2}\exp[-\operatorname{tr}(V^{-1}W)/2]$. It is a <conjugate prior> for the <precision matrix> of a <multivariate normal distribution>. Its mean is $\nu V$.

The <WinBUGS> declaration `dwish(R, nu)` uses $R=V^{-1}$; the density convention is documented in https://r-nimble.org/manual/cha-writing-models.html[the NIMBLE BUGS-language manual]. For $\nu=p=3$ it is proper, while the expected inverse matrix does not exist: that expectation requires $\nu>p+1$. Specifying a Wishart prior on precision is therefore a substantive covariance assumption.

= Inverse-Wishart distribution
{parent=Wishart distribution}
{wiki}
{title2=$IW_p(\nu,R)$}

If $W\sim W_p(\nu,R^{-1})$ has the <Wishart distribution>, then $W^{-1}$ has the inverse-Wishart distribution. For $\nu>p+1$, its <expected value> is $R/(\nu-p-1)$; below this threshold the matrix mean does not exist. It commonly models a <covariance matrix>, but couples the prior behavior of variances and correlations.

= Empirical spectral measure
{parent=Random matrix}
{title2=$L_N=N^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^N\delta_{\lambda_i}$}

For an $N$-by-$N$ <Hermitian matrix>, the empirical spectral measure puts mass $1/N$ at each <eigenvalue>, counted with multiplicity. It is a <probability measure>. Integrating a test <function> against it gives the average of that <function> evaluated at the <eigenvalues>.

= Wigner matrix
{c}
{parent=Random matrix}
{title2=$X_{ij}=N^{-1/2}Y_{ij}$}

A real Wigner <matrix> is a real <symmetric matrix> whose upper off-diagonal entries are independent centered copies of a fixed <random variable>, scaled to have <variance> of order $1/N$. Diagonal conventions may vary and should be specified separately. A common version has variance-one unscaled off-diagonal entries and either zero diagonal entries or independent diagonal entries with a bounded second moment.

= Centered truncation of a Wigner matrix
{parent=Wigner matrix}
{title2=$\widehat X_{ij}=N^{-1/2}(Y_{ij}\mathbf1_{|Y_{ij}|<C}-\mathbb E[Y_{ij}\mathbf1_{|Y_{ij}|<C}])$}

Truncate the unscaled entries of a <Wigner matrix> at a fixed threshold and subtract their <expected values>. The difference between original and truncated entries is $N^{-1/2}(Z_C-\mathbb EZ_C)$ with $Z_C=Y\mathbf1_{|Y|\geq C}$, so its <variance> is bounded by $N^{-1}\mathbb E[Y^2\mathbf1_{|Y|\geq C}]$. The centering term cannot be discarded for an asymmetric entry law.

= Second moment bound for spectral truncation
{parent=Centered truncation of a Wigner matrix}

For a fixed mean-zero variance-one real entry law, <centered truncation of a Wigner matrix> satisfies $\mathbb E[N^{-1}\operatorname{Tr}(X-\widehat X)^2]\leq t(C)$, where $t(C)=\mathbb E[Y^2\mathbf1_{|Y|\geq C}]\to0$. The <spectral Lipschitz bound from Frobenius distance> and <Markov inequality> give probability at most $t(C)/\varepsilon^2$ for a spectral test-function discrepancy exceeding $\varepsilon$. Thus $t(C)<\varepsilon^3$ works uniformly in <matrix> size. A varying entry-law family requires uniform second-moment tail decay instead.

= Complex Wishart matrix
{parent=Random matrix}
{title2=$A=X^*X$}

A complex Wishart <matrix> is the Gram <matrix> of an $n$-by-$m$ <matrix> of independent centered complex <Gaussian random variables> with an indicated <variance> convention. It is a <positive semidefinite matrix>. In the standard convention with joint entry density proportional to $e^{-\operatorname{Tr}X^*X}$ and $n\geq m$, its <eigenvalue> density contains the weight $x^{n-m}e^{-x}$ and a squared <Vandermonde determinant>.

= Laguerre unitary ensemble
{c}
{parent=Complex Wishart matrix}
{title2=$\Delta(x)^2\prod_i x_i^a e^{-x_i}$}

The Laguerre unitary ensemble is the positive-eigenvalue ensemble with joint density proportional to $\prod_{i<j}(x_j-x_i)^2\prod_i x_i^a e^{-x_i}$, for $a>-1$. Integer $a=n-m\geq0$ is realized by a <complex Wishart matrix>. The <Laguerre projection kernel> expresses its <correlation functions of a point process> as determinants.

= Concentration of measure
{parent=Probability theory}

For a <metric space> with a <probability measure> $\mu$, the concentration function is $\alpha(\varepsilon)=\sup_{\mu(A)\geq1/2}(1-\mu(A_\varepsilon))$, where $A_\varepsilon$ is the metric neighbourhood of radius $\varepsilon$. A sequence exhibits <concentration of measure> if these functions tend to zero for every fixed positive radius. The metric scale is part of this definition.

= Lévy family of graphs
{c}
{parent=Concentration of measure}
{title2=$\alpha_n(\varepsilon)\to0$}

= Lévy family
{c}
{synonym}

A <Lévy family of graphs> consists of finite connected <graphs> with their uniform <probability measures> and <graph distances> divided by their diameters, whose <concentration of measure> functions vanish at every fixed positive normalized radius. Equivalently, every family occupying at least half the vertices has a radius-$\lfloor\varepsilon D_n\rfloor$ neighbourhood occupying a proportion tending to one, uniformly over such families. <Harper theorem> and <binomial distribution> tail bounds prove this for the <hypercube graphs>.

= Failure of concentration in fixed-dimensional grids
{parent=Lévy family of graphs}

Equip $[n]^d$ with uniform <probability measure> and its nearest-neighbour <graph distance> divided by $d(n-1)$, for fixed positive $d$. The <set> $\{x:x_1\leq\lceil n/2\rceil\}$ has at least half the vertices. Its radius-$\varepsilon d(n-1)$ <graph neighbourhood> still omits a proportion tending to $1/2-\varepsilon d$ when $0<\varepsilon<1/(2d)$. These grids therefore do not form a <Lévy family of graphs>.

= Concentration of discrete cubes at diameter scale
{parent=Lévy family of graphs}
{title2=$d_n(x,y)=d_{Q_n}(x,y)/n$}

Equip the <Boolean hypercube> with uniform <probability measure> and <graph distance> divided by its <graph diameter>. These spaces form a <Lévy family of graphs>. The <vertex-isoperimetric inequality in the discrete cube> reduces the complement of a positive-density <set>'s radius-$\varepsilon n$ <graph neighbourhood> to a <binomial distribution> tail. For fixed density $\delta>0$, <Chebyshev's inequality> puts a lower density quantile at least $n/2-C_\delta\sqrt n-O(1)$. After enlarging by $\varepsilon n$, the omitted mass is at most $n/[4(\varepsilon n-C_\delta\sqrt n-O(1))^2]$, which tends to zero.

= Positive association of random variables
{parent=Probability theory}
{title2=$\operatorname{Cov}(f(X),g(X))\geq0$}

= Positively associated
{synonym}

A random vector $X$ is positively associated if $\operatorname{Cov}(f(X),g(X))\geq0$ for every pair of coordinatewise nondecreasing bounded functions $f,g$. The same inequality holds when both are nonincreasing, by negating them. Repeatedly applying it to products of nonnegative increasing indicators gives $\mathbb P(\bigcap_iE_i)\geq\prod_i\mathbb P(E_i)$ for increasing events; the same is true for decreasing events. Thus associated rejection indicators have <familywise error rate> no greater than <independent> indicators with the same marginals. Pairwise positive <correlation coefficients> alone do not give this stronger property. The classical definition is in https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~brian/720/week02/esary-proschan-walkup-1967.pdf[Esary, Proschan and Walkup (1967)].

= Extreme value theory
{parent=Probability theory}
{wiki}

<Extreme value theory> studies limiting <probability distributions> of normalized <sample maxima> and related extremes. The standard limits for maxima include the <Fréchet distribution>, <negative Weibull distribution>, and <Gumbel distribution>.

= Hill estimator
{parent=Extreme value theory}
{c}
{title2=$\widehat\gamma_H=k^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^k\log(X_{(n-j+1)}/X_{(n-k)})$}

For $1\leq k<n$ and a positive threshold $t=X_{(n-k)}$, the <Hill estimator> averages the logarithmic ratios of the top $k$ <order statistics> to $t$. For a <Fréchet distribution> domain with shape $\alpha$, it estimates the reciprocal tail index $1/\alpha$. At an untied threshold it equals the <empirical distribution function> plug-in tail integral $\int_t^\infty(1-F_n(x))/(1-F_n(t))\,dx/x$. At a tied threshold the plug-in denominator counts strictly larger observations and the identity needs adjustment. Fixed $k$ does not by itself yield a consistent tail-index estimate.

= Extremal types theorem
{parent=Extreme value theory}

= Fisher–Tippett–Gnedenko theorem
{synonym}
{c}

Every non-degenerate limit in <convergence in distribution> of positively scaled and centered <sample maxima> is a <max-stable distribution>. Up to positive affine transformations its law is the <Gumbel distribution>, a <Fréchet distribution> with positive shape parameter, or a <negative Weibull distribution> with positive shape parameter. This classifies possible limits, without asserting that every <distribution function> admits a limiting normalization.

= Max-stable distribution
{parent=Extreme value theory}
{title2=$F(a_nx+b_n)^n=F(x)$}

= Max-stable
{synonym}

A non-degenerate <distribution function> is max-stable when every <sample maximum> of $n$ <independent and identically distributed random variables> can be positively scaled and centered to have the original law. Thus for every integer $n\geq1$ there are $a_n>0,b_n\in\mathbb R$ with $F(a_nx+b_n)^n=F(x)$. The <extremal types theorem> classifies these laws, up to affine changes, as the <Gumbel distribution>, <Fréchet distribution>, and <negative Weibull distribution>.

= Return period
{parent=Extreme value theory}
{title2=$T=1/p$}

For independent repeated time blocks, an event with exceedance probability $p$ has mean waiting time $1/p$ blocks by the <geometric distribution>. The return period is a long-run average waiting time, not a deterministic recurrence interval. For annual exceedance events, it is measured in years.

= Return level
{parent=Return period}
{title2=$z_T=F_{\rm annual}^{-1}(1-1/T)$}

A $T$-year return level has annual exceedance probability $1/T$ under the assumed stationary model. It is the corresponding <quantile> of annual maxima. Daily exceedance probabilities must be converted to annual event probabilities before using the annual <return period> definition.

= Return level plot
{parent=Return level}

A return level plot shows estimated <return levels> against their <return periods>, often on a logarithmic horizontal axis. Confidence curves describe uncertainty in estimated return levels. They are not prediction bands for individual extreme observations, and uncertainty typically increases when extrapolating far beyond the observation window.

= Generalized Pareto distribution
{parent=Extreme value theory}
{title2=$G_{\xi,\sigma}(y)$}
{wiki}

For scale $\sigma>0$ and shape $\xi$, the generalized Pareto distribution on nonnegative excesses has <cumulative distribution function>
$$
G_{\xi,\sigma}(y)=1-(1+\xi y/\sigma)^{-1/\xi},\qquad y\geq0,\quad1+\xi y/\sigma>0.
$$
The case $\xi=0$ is the continuous limit $1-e^{-y/\sigma}$. Positive $\xi$ gives a power-law tail, zero gives an <exponential distribution>, and negative $\xi$ gives a finite upper endpoint $-\sigma/\xi$. Its role in modelling high-threshold excesses is explained by the <Pickands-Balkema-de Haan theorem>.

= Orthogonal shape and scale for a generalized Pareto distribution
{parent=Generalized Pareto distribution}
{title2=$\eta=\sigma(1+\psi)$}

For the positive-shape <generalized Pareto distribution> with shape $\psi>0$ and scale $\sigma>0$, the expected <Fisher information matrix> for one observation is
$$
I(\psi,\sigma)=\frac1{1+2\psi}\begin{pmatrix}2/(1+\psi)&1/[\sigma(1+\psi)]\\1/[\sigma(1+\psi)]&1/\sigma^2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The <orthogonalization equation for two statistical parameters> gives $\partial_\psi\sigma=-\sigma/(1+\psi)$ at fixed new nuisance coordinate. Thus $\eta=\sigma(1+\psi)$ is a global <interest-respecting reparametrization>. In coordinates $(\psi,\eta)$, the information is diagonal with entries $(1+\psi)^{-2}$ and $[\eta^2(1+2\psi)]^{-1}$.

= Peaks-over-threshold method
{parent=Generalized Pareto distribution}

The peaks-over-threshold method fits a <generalized Pareto distribution> to exceedances of a high threshold and also estimates their frequency. Together these determine tail probabilities and <return levels>. For dependent observations, threshold exceedances may occur in clusters; <declustering of extremes> can separate extreme events before fitting a model for independent events.

= Declustering of extremes
{parent=Peaks-over-threshold method}

Declustering groups dependent threshold exceedances into extreme-event clusters and retains a representative, commonly the cluster maximum. It aims to make the events used in a <peaks-over-threshold method> approximately independent. The event rate must be estimated consistently with the retained clusters when computing <return levels>.

= Pickands-Balkema-de Haan theorem
{parent=Generalized Pareto distribution}
{c}

= Threshold excess limit theorem
{synonym}

For a distribution in an extreme-value domain of attraction, the <conditional distribution> of the excess above a high threshold is asymptotically approximated by a <generalized Pareto distribution> with a threshold-dependent scale and a limiting shape parameter. This theorem justifies the <peaks-over-threshold method>. Threshold selection must balance approximation error against the reduced number of excess observations.

= Gumbel distribution
{c}
{parent=Extreme value theory}
{title2=$\Lambda$}
{wiki}

The standard <Gumbel distribution> for maxima has <distribution function> $\Lambda(x)=e^{-e^{-x}}$ for every real $x$. The <maximum domain of attraction> is characterized by an exponential tail ratio on the scale of a <Gumbel auxiliary function>.

= Moving Gumbel cutoff in an inverse-power tail
{parent=Gumbel distribution}
{title2=$\int_a^\infty x^{-r}e^{-\lambda e^{-x}}dx\sim\frac{L^{1-r}}{r-1}-\gamma L^{-r}\quad(L=\log\lambda)$}

For fixed $a>0$ and $r>1$, the factor $\exp(-\lambda e^{-x})$ is a translated <Gumbel distribution> cumulative function, with transition near $x=L$. Integration by parts transforms the <integral> to an exponentially small boundary term plus $(r-1)^{-1}\int_0^{\lambda e^{-a}}e^{-u}(L-\log u)^{1-r}du$. Expanding the latter against its finite logarithmic moments gives the displayed two terms because $\int_0^\infty e^{-u}\log u\,du=-\gamma$, with $\gamma$ <Euler's constant>. The smooth cutoff shifts the first correction even though replacing it by a step gives the leading tail.

= Gumbel limit for gamma maxima
{c}
{parent=Gumbel distribution}

For the <Gamma distribution> with fixed positive integer shape $m$ and rate one, the <survival function> is $e^{-t}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}t^j/j!$. Its <sample maximum> centered by $\log n+(m-1)\log\log n-\log((m-1)!)$ has <convergence in distribution> to the standard <Gumbel distribution>, without an additional scale factor.

= Gumbel auxiliary function
{c}
{parent=Gumbel distribution}
{title2=$a(t)$}

A positive <Gumbel auxiliary function> makes $\overline F(t+a(t)x)/\overline F(t)\to e^{-x}$ as $t$ approaches the <right endpoint of a distribution>, for every real $x$. Under the <Von Mises conditions for extreme values>, the reciprocal <hazard function> is a valid choice.

= Negative Weibull distribution
{parent=Extreme value theory}
{title2=$\Psi_\alpha$}

= Reversed Weibull distribution
{synonym}

For $\alpha>0$, the standard <negative Weibull distribution> has <distribution function> $\Psi_\alpha(x)=e^{-(-x)^\alpha}$ for $x<0$ and one for $x\geq0$. This is the <probability distribution> of the negative of a standard positive <Weibull distribution> variable.

= Fréchet distribution
{c}
{parent=Extreme value theory}
{title2=$\Phi_\alpha$}
{wiki}

For $\alpha>0$, the standard <Fréchet distribution> has <distribution function> $\Phi_\alpha(x)=e^{-x^{-\alpha}}$ for $x>0$ and zero for $x\leq0$. Its <maximum domain of attraction> consists of <distribution functions> with an infinite <right endpoint of a distribution> and a <survival function> with <regular variation> of index $-\alpha$.

= Maximum domain of attraction
{parent=Extreme value theory}
{title2=$D(G)$}

= Maximum domains of attraction
{synonym}

A <distribution function> $F$ lies in the <maximum domain of attraction> of $G$ if $F(a_nx+b_n)^n\to G(x)$ at every continuity point of $G$ for some $a_n>0$ and real $b_n$. This expresses <convergence in distribution> of normalized <sample maxima>.

= Logarithmic-tail Fréchet normalization
{parent=Maximum domain of attraction}
{title2=$a_n\log a_n=n$}

For a <distribution function> with tail $1-F(x)=1/(x\log x)$, let $a_n>1$ solve $a_n\log a_n=n$. Then $n(1-F(a_nx))\to1/x$ for $x>0$, so the normalized <sample maximum> tends to the shape-one <Fréchet distribution>. The inequalities $n/\log n<a_n<n/(\log n-\log\log n)$ for large $n$ show $a_n\sim n/\log n$, allowing that simpler scale too.

= Von Mises conditions for extreme values
{c}
{parent=Maximum domain of attraction}

For an absolutely continuous <distribution function> with positive <probability density function> near its <right endpoint of a distribution>, write $\eta=f/(1-F)$ for its <hazard function>. The limits $t\eta(t)\to\alpha$ at an infinite endpoint or $(x_F-t)\eta(t)\to\alpha$ at a finite endpoint imply attraction to the <Fréchet distribution> or <negative Weibull distribution>, respectively. A continuously differentiable reciprocal <hazard function> with $(1/\eta)'\to0$ implies attraction to the <Gumbel distribution>, provided the <survival function> tends to zero at the endpoint.

= Probability
{title2=$\mathbb P(E)$}
{parent=Probability theory}
{wiki}

The probability of an <event> is its value under a <probability measure>, a <real number> between zero and one. It compares the mass assigned to that <event> with the unit mass of the whole <sample space>. For a finite <sample space> with equally likely outcomes, $\mathbb P(A)=|A|/|\Omega|$. For unequal outcome masses, add their individual <probabilities> instead.

For example, selecting a coin with head <probability> $1/2$ with chance $4/5$, or a coin with head <probability> $3/4$ with chance $1/5$, gives head <probability> $(4/5)(1/2)+(1/5)(3/4)=11/20$. This is the <law of total probability>: averaging over the hidden selection is part of calculating the outcome's <probability>.

= Odds
{parent=Probability}
{title2=$o=p/(1-p)$}
{wiki}

For an <event> of <probability> $0<p<1$, its odds in favor are $o=p/(1-p)$. They compare the <probability> of the <event> with that of its <complement>. Conversely $p=o/(1+o)$. For example, <probability> $1/4$ gives <odds> $1/3$, or one to three in favor. The same transformation applies to a <conditional probability>; an <odds ratio> compares these transformed <probabilities> in two groups, while a <risk ratio> compares the <probabilities> themselves.

= Tail probability
{parent=Probability}

For a real <random variable> $X$, an upper-tail probability has the form $\mathbb P(X\geq t)$ and a lower-tail probability has the form $\mathbb P(X\leq t)$. A <concentration inequality> bounds such probabilities.

= Percolation
{synonym}

Percolation theory studies random subgraphs formed by independently or dependently declaring vertices or edges open.

= Directed percolation
{parent=Percolation theory}

In <bond percolation> on a <directed graph>, independently open each <directed edge> with <probability> $p$. The out-cluster of $x$ consists of vertices reachable by open <directed paths>. In <site percolation>, independently open vertices and require every vertex of the <directed path>, including $x$, to be open. The same definitions allow a <multigraph>. The orientation matters: an infinite underlying <connected graph> need not contain an infinite out-cluster from a specified root.

= Oriented site percolation
{parent=Directed percolation}
{title2=$p_c^{\mathrm{site}}$}

Independently open vertices of a <directed graph> with common <probability> $p$, and require every vertex of an infinite directed path to be open, including its root. Conditioning the root to be open changes the survival probability by a factor $p$ and does not change the critical probability. This model gives an independent comparison process in the <independent oriented-percolation comparison for the contact process>.

= Oriented bond percolation
{parent=Directed percolation}
{title2=$p_c^{\mathrm{bond}}$}

Independently open the bonds of a <directed graph> with a common <probability> $p$, and ask whether an infinite open directed path starts at a specified root. On the oriented <square lattice>, edges run from $(x,n)$ to $(x\pm1,n+1)$ with $x+n$ even. The critical probability is the infimum of parameters with positive survival probability. The <incoming-edge coupling of site and bond percolation> compares this threshold with that of <oriented site percolation>.

= Parallel-bond ray with different site and bond thresholds
{parent=Directed percolation}

Take a <directed graph> with vertices $v_0,v_1,\ldots$ and $n+1$ parallel bonds from $v_n$ to $v_{n+1}$. This is a <locally finite graph>, although the incoming degrees are unbounded. For <bond percolation>, $\theta_b(p)=\prod_{n\ge0}(1-(1-p)^{n+1})>0$ whenever $p>0$: the failure <probabilities> are summable, so the <infinite product> is positive. For <site percolation>, survival requires every vertex of the ray to be open, which has <probability> zero for $p<1$. Thus the <survival and susceptibility thresholds for directed percolation> satisfy $p_H^b=0$ and $p_H^s=1$.

= Incoming-edge coupling of site and bond percolation
{parent=Directed percolation}

Suppose every vertex of a <directed graph> has at most $\Delta$ incoming bonds. In <bond percolation> with parameter $q$, open a site whenever at least one incoming bond is open. Incoming bond sets have different heads and hence are disjoint, so these site indicators are <independent random variables>. Supplement their <probabilities> to $r=1-(1-q)^\Delta$. Every vertex reached by a nontrivial open <directed path> is now open. Conditioning the root to be open gives $\theta_s(r)\ge r\theta_b(q)$, since a simple <directed path> from the root never uses a bond entering the root. Thus $p_H^s\le1-(1-p_H^b)^\Delta$.

= Survival and susceptibility thresholds for directed percolation
{parent=Directed percolation}
{title2=$p_H,p_T$}

For the out-cluster $C_x$ in <directed percolation>, define $p_H=\inf\{p:\mathbb P_p(|C_x|=\infty)>0\}$ and $p_T=\inf\{p:\mathbb E_p|C_x|=\infty\}$, with value $1$ when the relevant set is empty below $1$. The first is a survival threshold; the second is a <percolation susceptibility> threshold. Both depend on the root in a general <directed graph>. Exploring a <site percolation> cluster and using each fresh vertex coin for its first tested incoming bond gives a <coupling of probability distributions> with <bond percolation> such that $\theta_s(p)\le p\theta_b(p)$ and $\chi_s(p)\le p\chi_b(p)$. Consequently $p_H^b\le p_H^s$ and $p_T^b\le p_T^s$.

= Conformal invariance of planar percolation

For critical <site percolation> on the <triangular lattice>, scaling-limit crossing probabilities transform naturally under conformal maps between planar domains. This rigorously established property supports descriptions of interfaces by <SLE> and calculations of planar <percolation critical exponents>. An iterative expansion of long-range connectivity functions that separates an approximately random-walk contribution from corrections caused by loops and intersections. = Near-critical percolation power upper bound

= Right continuity of percolation probability
{parent=Percolation probability}

For independent <site percolation> or <bond percolation> on a rooted <locally finite graph>, $\theta(p)$ is right continuous on $[0,1)$. Finite-radius connection events have polynomial <probabilities> in $p$ and decrease to infinite connection. Thus $\theta$ is their infimum by <continuity from above of a measure>. This infimum of <continuous functions> is upper semicontinuous; parameter monotonicity from the <monotone coupling of Bernoulli percolation> then gives right continuity.

= Supercritical continuity of percolation probability
{parent=Right continuity of percolation probability}

Independent <site percolation> and <bond percolation> on the <cubic lattice> have continuous <percolation probability> on $(p_c,1]$. Fix $p>p_c$ and an intermediate $q\in(p_c,p)$. By <uniqueness of the infinite percolation cluster>, every origin in the infinite $p$-cluster has a finite $p$-open <graph path> to the infinite $q$-cluster. In the uniform-label <monotone coupling of Bernoulli percolation>, the finitely many labels on this <graph path> are strictly below fixed $p$ <almost surely>, so it persists at some parameter below $p$. The <coupled onset parameter for origin percolation> has no attained atom there, giving left continuity, including at $1$. Combine this with <right continuity of percolation probability>.

{synonym}

= Connective-constant lower bound for percolation

= Zhang's argument
{synonym}

= Zhang argument
{synonym}
{c}

At parameter $1/2$, primal and <dual bond percolation> on the <square lattice> have the same <probability distribution>. Suppose an <infinite percolation cluster> exists. Large boxes meet it with <probability> tending to one. The <square-root trick for positively associated events> and quarter-turn symmetry imply that each side has an infinite exterior open arm with <probability> tending to one. The same holds for dual arms. A <union bound> then gives positive <probability> of primal top and bottom arms together with dual left and right arms.

Use a primal box $[-r,r]^2$ and a dual box with sides at $\pm(r+1/2)$. Open only primal <edges> whose endpoints both lie in the primal box, preserving all outward and exterior arm <edges>. The two primal arms are joined inside. Planarity prevents the two alternating dual arms from joining: a proposed dual connection together with the inner boundary arc separates one primal infinite arm from infinity. Thus the modified configuration has two infinite dual clusters. Its positive <probability> by <finite modification of Bernoulli percolation> contradicts <uniqueness of the infinite percolation cluster>. Consequently the <percolation probability> at $1/2$ is zero. This is often called Zhang's argument.

= Three-fold symmetric primal-dual noncoexistence
{parent=Alternating arms argument at the self-dual percolation parameter}

For independent <bond percolation> on a periodic plane lattice with three-fold rotation symmetry, the primal model at $p$ and the <dual bond percolation> model at $1-p$ cannot both percolate. To adapt <Zhang's argument>, divide a large boundary circle into six arcs, repeating the division in each $120$-degree sector. Vary the division point until the failure probabilities $a,b$ of the two alternating primal arc types are comparable within a factor $(1-p)^{-2}$. Moving past a single crossing edge changes a failure probability by at most a factor $(1-p)^{-1}$, by the <FKG inequality>. If the probability of no primal exterior arm is at most $\varepsilon$, then $a^3b^3\le\varepsilon$ and both failures are at most $(1-p)^{-1}\varepsilon^{1/6}$. For the dual model, at least one arc type has failure at most $\varepsilon^{1/6}$; its three rotations have the same bound. Four alternating primal/dual arms now occur with positive probability, contradicting the <alternating exterior-arm separation lemma> and uniqueness after a finite modification. This extends the four-fold argument to the <martini lattice>.

= Alternating exterior-arm separation lemma
{parent=Alternating arms argument at the self-dual percolation parameter}

Four infinite exterior arms entering a square in alternating primal-open and dual-open order force the two primal arms to be disjoint outside the square. If they met, their finite connecting crosscut together with a boundary arc would enclose one dual arm, preventing its escape to infinity. Connecting the two primal arms through the square creates a proper doubly infinite <open path>, separating the two remaining dual infinite tails. <finite-energy property of Bernoulli percolation> can then turn this configuration into two infinite dual clusters, contradicting uniqueness. Care is needed to preserve the exterior dual tails even if the finite modification destroys their original boundary attachments.

= Monotone coupling of Bernoulli percolation
{parent=Percolation theory}

Assign every site or edge $v$ an independent $U_v\sim\operatorname{Unif}[0,1]$ and declare it open at parameter $p$ exactly when $U_v\leq p$. The resulting configurations have the correct Bernoulli laws and increase with $p$.

= Finite-box comparison for percolation parameters
{parent=Monotone coupling of Bernoulli percolation}
{title2=$\mathbb P_q(A)-\mathbb P_p(A)\leq m(q-p)$}

If an <increasing event> $A$ depends on $m$ sites or <edges>, the uniform-label <monotone coupling of Bernoulli percolation> and the <union bound> give $0\leq\mathbb P_q(A)-\mathbb P_p(A)\leq m(q-p)$ for $p\leq q$. A change of the event requires a coordinate with label in $(p,q]$. For <bond percolation> from the origin to the boundary of $[-n,n]^2$, first-hit <graph paths> use the $4n(2n+1)$ internal <edges>, so the same count bounds the parameter sensitivity.

= Coupled onset parameter for origin percolation
{parent=Monotone coupling of Bernoulli percolation}
{title2=$M=\inf\{p:I_p\}$}

In the uniform-label <monotone coupling of Bernoulli percolation>, let $I_p$ mean that the origin belongs to an <infinite percolation cluster>, and let $M=\inf\{p:I_p\}$. The occurrence parameters form an upper interval. Therefore $\{M<p\}=\bigcup_{r<p,\ r\in\mathbb Q}I_r$ and $\theta(p-)=\mathbb P(M<p)$. Since $\{M<p\}\subseteq I_p\subseteq\{M\leq p\}$, the jump is $\theta(p)-\theta(p-)=\mathbb P(I_p\cap\{M=p\})$. Occurrence at the infimum is not presumed.

= Sprinkling coupling for Bernoulli percolation
{parent=Monotone coupling of Bernoulli percolation}

For $p<q$, first sample Bernoulli percolation at parameter $p$, then independently open each remaining closed coordinate with probability $(q-p)/(1-p)$. Every coordinate is then open with probability $q$, so the union has the parameter-$q$ law. This coupling separates existing $p$-clusters from the additional sprinkled connections between them.

= Probability space
{title2=$(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mathbb P)$}
{parent=Probability theory}
{wiki}

A probability space is a <measure space> $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mathbb P)$ whose total <measure> is one. The <sample space> $\Omega$ contains the elementary outcomes; the <sigma-algebra> $\mathcal F$ contains the <events> to which the model assigns <probabilities>; the <probability measure> $\mathbb P$ supplies those values. The <sigma-algebra> lets us combine observable questions using <complements> and countable <unions> while remaining within the model.

For two independent uniform dice, take $\Omega=\{1,\ldots,6\}^2$, $\mathcal F=\mathcal P(\Omega)$, and give each ordered pair mass $1/36$. A <random variable> might record the sum, the larger result, or whether both results are <even integers>. These are different <functions> on the same <sample space>, rather than different elementary experiments. A <probability distribution> records the mass of the values of one such <random variable> and can forget information retained by the full <probability space>.

= Product probability space
{parent=Probability space}
{title2=$(\prod_iX_i,\bigotimes_i\mathcal F_i,\bigotimes_i\mu_i)$}

For finitely many <probability spaces> $(X_i,\mathcal F_i,\mu_i)$, their product <probability> space has underlying <set> $\prod_iX_i$, the <product sigma-algebra> and <product measure> $\bigotimes_i\mu_i$. The coordinate projections are jointly independent: for measurable $B_i\subseteq X_i$, the <probability> of all coordinate conditions is $\prod_i\mu_i(B_i)$. Conversely a family of independent coordinates has this product joint distribution. Product structure permits coordinate exposure and independent resampling in concentration arguments.

= Sample space
{parent=Probability space}
{title2=$\Omega$}

The <sample space> is the set $\Omega$ of possible outcomes underlying a <probability space> $(\Omega,\mathcal F,\mathbb P)$. Its measurable subsets are <events>, and the whole <sample space> has probability one.

= Almost sure event

An almost sure event is a measurable event of probability one. = Probability measure
{parent=Probability space}
{wiki}

A probability measure is a nonnegative, <countably additive> <measure> with total mass one. Thus $\mathbb P(\Omega)=1$, and for pairwise <disjoint events> $A_j$,
$$
\mathbb P\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}A_j\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mathbb P(A_j).
$$
The <complement of an event> has <probability> $1-\mathbb P(A)$. The <probability> of a <union> of overlapping <events> needs a correction: $\mathbb P(A\cup B)=\mathbb P(A)+\mathbb P(B)-\mathbb P(A\cap B)$. Counting the overlap once is the simplest instance of the <inclusion-exclusion principle>.

A <zero-probability event> need not be impossible as a member of the <sample space>. Under the <continuous uniform distribution> on $[0,1]$, every individual point has <probability> zero, while the whole interval has <probability> one. <Countable additivity> applies to countable <unions>, not arbitrary uncountable ones; there is no contradiction.

= Continuity of probability
{parent=Probability measure}
{title2=$A_n\uparrow A\text{ or }A_n\downarrow A\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathbb P(A_n)\to\mathbb P(A)$}

For increasing or decreasing sequences of <events>, the <probability> of their union or intersection, respectively, is the limit of their probabilities. Increasing events follow from <countable additivity> applied to successive disjoint differences; decreasing events follow by taking complements. This is <continuity from below of a measure> and <continuity from above of a measure> for a finite <probability measure>.

= Helly selection for distribution functions

= Law of total probability
{parent=Probability measure}
{wiki}

Let $(B_i)$ be a countable <measurable partition> of the <sample space>, with positive <probabilities>. For any <event> $A$, the law of total probability gives
$$
\mathbb P(A)=\sum_i\mathbb P(A\mid B_i)\mathbb P(B_i).
$$
The pieces $A\cap B_i$ are <disjoint events>, so add their masses and use the definition of <conditional probability>. Zero-mass partition pieces can be omitted without changing the answer.

For the coin selection in <probability>, let $B$ mean the $3/4$-head coin was selected and let $H$ mean the first toss was heads. Then $\mathbb P(H)=\mathbb P(H\mid B)\mathbb P(B)+\mathbb P(H\mid B^c)\mathbb P(B^c)=11/20$. Each conditional answer describes one possible hidden situation; its weight describes how often that situation occurs under the model. This decomposition is useful whenever a simple conditional model sits behind a more complicated marginal <probability distribution>.

= Independent sigma-algebras

= Independent events
{parent=Independent sigma-algebras}

Events $A_1,\ldots,A_n$ are mutually independent when
$$
\mathbb P\left(\bigcap_{j\in J}A_j\right)
=\prod_{j\in J}\mathbb P(A_j)
$$
for every nonempty index subset $J\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}$. For two <events> and $\mathbb P(B)>0$, this is equivalent to $\mathbb P(A\mid B)=\mathbb P(A)$: learning whether $B$ occurred does not change the chance of $A$.

For independent uniform dice, the <events> that the first result is an <even integer> and that the second result is an <even integer> each have <probability> $1/2$; their <intersection> has <probability> $9/36=1/4$. In contrast, "first result is an <even integer>" and "first result is an <odd integer>" are <disjoint events> of positive <probability>, so they cannot be <independent events>. Learning one occurred completely rules out the other.

<Pairwise independence> is weaker than mutual independence. Take two <independent> fair <bits> $X,Y$. The three <events> $X=0$, $Y=0$, and $X=Y$ each have <probability> $1/2$ and every pair has <intersection> <probability> $1/4$. Their triple <intersection> is the single outcome $(0,0)$, of <probability> $1/4$ rather than $1/8$. Checking pairs alone misses the joint constraint.

= Pairwise-independent three-event avoidance
{parent=Independent events}
{title2=$\mathbb P(\bigcap_{i=1}^3A_i^c)\geq(1-a)(1-2a)$}

It is strictly positive for $0<a<1/2$. These masses sum to one, have marginal $a$, and have pair intersections $a^2$.

= Multinomial collision proof of three-dimensional walk transience
{parent=Simple symmetric random walk}
{title2=$P(S_{2n}=0)=\frac{\binom{2n}{n}}{4^n}\sum_{i+j+k=n}\left(\frac{n!}{3^ni!j!k!}\right)^2=O(n^{-3/2})$}

For the nearest-neighbor symmetric walk on $\mathbb Z^3$, a return at time $2n$ uses $i,j,k$ positive and the same numbers of negative steps along each axis, with $i+j+k=n$. Counting arrangements gives the displayed identity. The sum of squares of the three-category <multinomial distribution> probabilities is at most their maximum. This maximum occurs at balanced counts and is $O(n^{-1})$ by <Stirling's formula>; the central binomial factor is $O(n^{-1/2})$. The total return probability is therefore summable. The expected number of visits is finite, so the probability of returning after time zero is strictly below one, proving that the origin is a <transient state>.

= Infinite mean first passage of a simple symmetric random walk
{parent=Simple symmetric random walk}
{title2=$\mathbb ET_1=\infty,\quad\mathbb P(T_1<\infty)=1$}

For a <simple symmetric random walk> starting from zero, its first <hitting time> of $1$ is almost surely finite but has infinite <expectation>. Stopping at exit from $[-m,1]$, the <optional stopping theorem> for $S_n$ gives upper-exit <probability> $m/(m+1)$, while stopping $S_n^2-n$ gives mean exit time $m$. The first <probabilities> tend to one, and the exit times are bounded above by the first passage to $1$, proving both conclusions. Finite-time stopping and bounded stopped positions justify passage to the exit time.

= Origin capture by a sufficiently sleepy planar random walk
{parent=Simple symmetric random walk}

Suppose two independent planar <simple symmetric random walks> are compared while one stays at its $j$th position throughout a deterministic time block. If each block has an unconditional probability bounded below by a positive constant that the other walk visits the origin, simultaneous origin visits occur infinitely often almost surely. <Markov-chain recurrence> gives infinitely many origin blocks for the sleepy walk. The block bound yields positive probability of infinitely many coincidences; <eventual coupling of planar symmetric random walks> then makes this future event have probability zero or one. Conditional block bounds or independence of capture attempts are not required.

= Borel-Cantelli lemma
{c}
{synonym}

If $\sum\mathbb P(A_n)<\infty$, only finitely many $A_n$ occur almost surely. For independent events, divergence implies infinitely many occur. Use a union bound for the first and $\prod(1-p_n)\le e^{-\sum p_n}$ for the second.

= Gaussian sample limsup at logarithmic scale
{c}
{parent=Borel-Cantelli lemmas}
{title2=$\limsup_{n\to\infty}X_n^2/\log n=2$}

For independent <standard normal random variables> $X_n$, the displayed <limit superior> equals two <almost surely>. The <Mills ratio> asymptotic gives, for $a>0$,
$$
\mathbb P(X_n^2>a\log n)\sim\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi a}}\frac{n^{-a/2}}{\sqrt{\log n}}.
$$
The series converges for $a>2$ and diverges for $0<a\leq2$. The two <Borel-Cantelli lemmas>, applied at countably many thresholds approaching two, give the result. In particular, the value two is also a threshold exceeded infinitely often, despite being the almost-sure <limit superior>.

= Borel-Cantelli first lemma
{c}
{parent=Borel-Cantelli lemmas}

{synonym}
{c}

For arbitrary events $A_n$, if $\sum_n\mathbb P(A_n)<\infty$, then $\mathbb P(\limsup_n A_n)=0$. The <union bound> gives $\mathbb P(\limsup A_n)\le\sum_{n\ge m}\mathbb P(A_n)\to0$. No <independence> is required.

= Tail-sum formula for expectation
{parent=Borel-Cantelli lemmas}

For a nonnegative <random variable> $X$, <Tonelli theorem> gives
$$
\mathbb E[X]=\int_0^\infty\mathbb P(X>t)\,dt.
$$
Consequently, for each $a>0$, $\mathbb E[X]=\infty$ exactly when $\sum_{n\geq1}\mathbb P(X>an)=\infty$; compare the integral on successive intervals of length $a$.

= Extremes of independent exponential variables
{parent=Borel-Cantelli lemmas}

For independent rate-one exponential variables $X_n$ and $M_n=\max_{m\leq n}X_m$,
$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{X_n}{\log n}=1,
\qquad
\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{M_n}{\log n}\geq1
$$
almost surely. The first follows by applying both Borel--Cantelli lemmas to thresholds $(1\pm\varepsilon)\log n$; the second uses
$\mathbb P(M_n\leq(1-\varepsilon)\log n)\leq e^{-n^\varepsilon}$.

= Conditional Borel-Cantelli lemma
{c}
{parent=Borel-Cantelli lemmas}

= Poisson observation scheme and rate likelihood
{parent=Poisson process}
{title2=$\widehat\lambda=N_H/H\quad\text{or}\quad n/T_n$}

A <Poisson process> observed for a fixed duration $H$ has likelihood proportional to $\lambda^{N_H}e^{-\lambda H}$. = Every second Poisson arrival has underdispersed counts
{c}
{parent=Poisson process}

For a rate-$\lambda$ <Poisson process>, keeping every second arrival gives a renewal process with independent interarrival times equal to sums of two exponential waiting times. Put $x=\lambda t$. = Proof of Wald's equation by stopping indicators
{c}
{parent=Wald's equation}

= Poisson point process
{parent=Probability theory}
{c}
{wiki}

A Poisson point process has independent counts on disjoint sets and Poisson count in each set with mean given by its intensity measure.

= Poisson line process
{parent=Poisson point process}
{c}
{title2=$\Pi\subset\{L(p,\theta):p>0,\ 0\leq\theta<2\pi\}$}

Identify an unoriented line not through the origin by $L(p,\theta)=\{z:z\cdot(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)=p\}$, with $p>0$. A <Poisson point process> on this parameter space is a random collection of lines. The <measure> $c\,dp\,d\theta$ is invariant under rotations and translations; signed coordinates $t\in\mathbb R$, $0\leq\theta<\pi$ give the same <measure> $c\,dt\,d\theta$. The number of lines meeting a disk of radius $r$ is <Poisson> with <mean> $2\pi cr$.

= Intersection point process of a Poisson line process
{parent=Poisson line process}
{title2=$\Phi=\{L\cap L':L,L'\in\Pi,\ L\ne L'\}$}

For an isotropic <Poisson line process> of intensity $c\,dp\,d\theta$, its pair intersections form a locally finite <point process>. Coincident intersections and parallel pairs have <probability> zero. Its <intensity measure> is $\pi c^2$ times area. Indeed the <Poisson factorial moment measure> and the <change of variables> from signed distances to intersection location give
$$
\mathbb E\#(\Phi\cap A)=\frac{c^2|A|}{2}\int_0^\pi\int_0^\pi|\sin(\theta-\phi)|\,d\theta\,d\phi=\pi c^2|A|.
$$
The factor $1/2$ counts unordered line pairs. Shared lines create dependence between intersections; this process is not a <Poisson point process>.

= Strict disk-void bound for a Poisson line process
{parent=Intersection point process of a Poisson line process}
{title2=$\mathbb P(\Phi\cap D_r\ne\varnothing)<1-(1+2\pi cr)e^{-2\pi cr}$}

For $c,r>0$, an intersection inside a disk requires at least two lines to meet that disk. The latter count is <Poisson> with <mean> $2\pi cr$, giving the non-strict bound first. It is strict because two lines with nearby normal angles but separated perpendicular distances can both hit the disk while intersecting outside. Choose two positive-measure parameter boxes forcing this configuration; the event of exactly one line in each box and no other disk-hitting line has positive <probability>. This also yields a void <probability> too large to equal that of a <Poisson point process> with the intersection process's <intensity measure>.

= Poisson factorial moment measure
{parent=Poisson point process}
{c}
{title2=$\alpha^{(k)}=\mu^{\otimes k}$}

For a <Poisson point process> with <intensity measure> $\mu$, ordered tuples of distinct atoms have factorial moment <measure> $\mu^{\otimes k}$. Thus for any nonnegative <measurable> function $g$,
$$
\mathbb E\sum_{z_1,\ldots,z_k\text{ distinct}}g(z_1,\ldots,z_k)=\int g\,d\mu^{\otimes k}.
$$
For $k=2$, separating diagonal terms from distinct pairs gives the second moment of a <Poisson> sum. Dividing by two instead counts unordered pairs, which is essential for geometric intersection intensities.

= Poisson mapping theorem
{parent=Poisson point process}
{c}
{title2=$f_*\Pi\sim\operatorname{PRM}(f_*\nu)$}

For a <measurable> map $f$ and a <Poisson random measure> of intensity $\nu$, image counts in disjoint sets equal original counts in their disjoint preimages. They are therefore <independent> Poisson variables with the pushforward intensity. The result is a <Poisson random measure> even if images collide; diffuse pushforward intensity gives a simple point process.

= Poisson polar projections share their total count
{parent=Poisson mapping theorem}
{c}
{title2=$\operatorname{Cov}(N_r(A),N_\omega(C))=\sigma(C)\int_A r^{d-1}\,dr$}

For unit volume intensity in the unit ball of $\mathbb R^d$, polar coordinates give intensity $r^{d-1}dr\,\sigma(d\omega)$. The separate radius and direction images are Poisson processes, but each counts the same random total number of original points. Their count <covariance> is the displayed intensity of the intersection of their two preimages. Conditional on the total count, radii and directions are <independent> samples, which does not imply unconditional <independence> of the two point processes.

= Symmetric inverse-power Poisson field on a line
{parent=Poisson point process}
{title2=$\phi(t)=\exp\{-\rho\pi m^\alpha|t|^\alpha/[\Gamma(\alpha)\sin(\pi\alpha/2)]\}$}

Place equal masses at points of a homogeneous <Poisson point process> of intensity $\rho$ on the line, and truncate their signed inverse-power forces symmetrically at radius $R$. For force $m\operatorname{sign}(x)/|x|^p$, with $\alpha=1/p\in(0,2)$, the characteristic exponent tends to $-2\rho\int_0^\infty(1-\cos(mt/x^p))\,dx$. The <cosine integral for a symmetric stable exponent> evaluates it as displayed. Thus the weak limit is a <symmetric stable distribution>. Symmetric truncation is important when the far-field force series is not absolutely summable.

= Poisson germ-grain model
{parent=Poisson point process}

A <Poisson germ-grain model> assigns a measurable region to each point of a <Poisson point process> and takes the union of those regions. A location remains uncovered exactly when no Poisson point has a region containing it, so its coverage <probability> is determined by the intensity of the corresponding set of germs and marks.

= Void probability for growing Poisson rain patches
{parent=Poisson germ-grain model}
{title2=$\mathbb P(\xi\text{ dry at }\tau)=e^{-\mu(A_{\xi,\tau})}$}

For drops with intensity $\lambda(x,t,v)$, impact time $t$ and nonnegative <volume> $v$, let their radius at time $\tau$ be $r(\tau-t,v)$. The dry <probability> at $\xi$ is
$$
\exp\left[-\int_{t\le\tau}\int_{v\ge0}\int_{|x-\xi|\le r(\tau-t,v)}\lambda(x,t,v)\,dx\,dv\,dt\right].
$$
The exponent is the intensity of drops whose patch covers the point by that time. An infinite exponent means zero dry <probability>. No spatial-homogeneity assumption is needed.

For nonnegative <measurable> $H$ and a <Poisson random measure> of sigma-finite intensity $\rho$, removing the selected point from a sum leaves an <independent> copy of the original process under the integral. = Existence of a Poisson random measure on a standard Borel space
{parent=Poisson random measure}

A <sigma-finite measure> on a <standard Borel space> is the <intensity measure of a point process> with Poisson counts. Partition the space into sets of finite intensity, draw an independent Poisson count on each piece, and conditional on that count draw independent points from its normalized intensity measure. The sum of their <Dirac measures> is the required <Poisson random measure>. Non-atomic intensity gives simplicity; local finiteness of the intensity gives local finiteness of the point configuration.

= L2 construction of compensated Poisson integrals
{c}
{parent=Poisson random measure}
{title2=$\mathbb E\left|\int f\,(\mu-\nu)\right|^2=\int|f|^2d\nu$}

For deterministic square-integrable $f$ and a <Poisson random measure> of intensity $\nu$, first define the integral for simple functions on disjoint finite-intensity sets. <Independence> and the equality of <Poisson distribution> mean and <variance> prove the displayed <isometry>. = Laplace functional of a Poisson random measure
{parent=Poisson random measure}
{c}
{title2=$\mathbb E e^{-M(f)}=\exp(-\int(1-e^{-f})\,d\mu)$}

For nonnegative measurable $f$, independent Poisson counts first prove this formula for simple functions on disjoint measurable sets. Monotone approximation gives the general formula, allowing infinite integrals with $e^{-\infty}=0$. Conversely, the formula evaluated at $f=\sum_ju_j\mathbf1_{A_j}$ for disjoint finite-intensity sets gives the joint Laplace transform of independent Poisson counts, so characterizes the law of a <Poisson random measure>.

= Count-weighted Laplace functional of a Poisson random measure
{parent=Laplace functional of a Poisson random measure}
{title2=$\mathbb E[M(A)e^{-M(f)}]=\mathbb E[e^{-M(f)}]\int_Ae^{-f}\,d\mu$}

Let $M$ be a <Poisson random measure> of intensity $\mu$, let $\mu(A)<\infty$, and let $f\geq0$ be <measurable>. Apply the <Laplace functional of a Poisson random measure> to $f+t\mathbf1_A$ and differentiate from the right at $t=0$. On the random side, differentiation is dominated by the <integrable random variable> $M(A)$; on the intensity side, by $\mathbf1_A$. This proves the displayed identity when $\int(1-e^{-f})d\mu$ is finite. When this integral is infinite, $e^{-M(f)}=0$ <almost surely>, and the same identity holds with both sides zero.

= Small-ball conditioning for a Poisson random measure
{parent=Count-weighted Laplace functional of a Poisson random measure}
{title2=$\lim_{r\downarrow0}\mathbb E[e^{-M(f)}\mid M(B(0,r))\geq1]=e^{-f(0)}\mathbb E[e^{-M(f)}]$}

For a <Poisson random measure> on $\mathbb R^d$ with <Lebesgue measure> intensity and a nonnegative compactly supported <continuous function> $f$, put $m_r=\mu(B(0,r))$ and $a_r=\int_{B(0,r)}e^{-f}d\mu$. Continuity gives $a_r/m_r\to e^{-f(0)}$. With $L=\mathbb E e^{-M(f)}$, the <count-weighted Laplace functional of a Poisson random measure> and independent inside/outside counts give respectively
$$
\frac{\mathbb E[M(B(0,r))e^{-M(f)}]}{\mathbb P(M(B(0,r))\geq1)}=L\frac{a_r}{1-e^{-m_r}},\qquad
\mathbb E[e^{-M(f)}\mid M(B(0,r))\geq1]=L\frac{1-e^{-a_r}}{1-e^{-m_r}}.
$$
Both converge to the displayed value. The conditioning is meaningful for every $r>0$; it is not conditioning on a positive-probability point at the origin.

= Exponential void probabilities characterize a Poisson random measure
{parent=Poisson random measure}

A <simple locally finite point measure> on $[0,\infty)$ with void <probability> $e^{-\lambda(A)}$ on finite unions of bounded intervals is a <Poisson random measure> with <Lebesgue measure> intensity. Void-event intersections factor, hence the occupation indicators on disjoint cells are independent. The <dyadic occupancy approximation of a simple point measure> is binomial with rare-event <probability> $1-e^{-2^{-n}}$ and converges to a Poisson count of mean the interval length. <Independence> survives joint convergence for disjoint intervals. Extension of the mean <measure> and the <Laplace functional of a Poisson random measure> to Borel functions identifies all Borel counts, not just intervals.

= Inverse-power sum over Poisson arrivals
{parent=Poisson random measure}
{title2=$X_p=\sum_iZ_i^{-p},\quad p>1$}

For unit-rate <Poisson process> arrivals $Z_i$, $X_p$ is finite almost surely when $p>1$. There are finitely many positive arrivals before time one; the expected contribution afterwards is $\int_1^\infty z^{-p}\,dz<\infty$, by the Poisson <expectation> identity proved on simple functions and extended by <monotone convergence>. Merging <independent> processes of rates $1$ and $\rho$ produces rate $1+\rho$; rescaling their arrivals by $1+\rho$ restores unit rate. Hence the sum of their inverse-power contributions has law $(1+\rho)^pX_p$. In particular two <independent> unit-rate inverse-square sums add to a variable with law $4X_2$.

= Poisson stochastic integral with finite intensity
{c}
{parent=Poisson random measure}
{title2=$\int_E f(z)\,N(dz)$}

For a <Poisson random measure> on $E$ with finite intensity $\nu(E)$, its integral of a finite-valued measurable mark function is the finite sum of the marks at its random atoms. Its <characteristic function> is $\mathbb E\exp(iu\int f\,dN)=\exp(\int(e^{iuf}-1)\,d\nu)$. On a time-mark space with intensity $ds\,\nu(dz)$ and finite mark intensity, restriction to each bounded time interval has finite intensity and defines a <compound Poisson process>. Zero-valued marks do not affect the integral and need not appear in its <Lévy measure>.

= Superposition theorem for Poisson point processes
{parent=Poisson point process}

The union of independent Poisson point processes with diffuse intensity measures $\mu_1,\ldots,\mu_m$ is a Poisson point process with intensity measure
$$
\mu_1+\cdots+\mu_m.
$$
On every finite collection of disjoint measurable sets, this follows by adding independent <Poisson distribution>[Poisson counts] and checking that the resulting counts remain independent.

= Campbell's theorem
{c}
{parent=Poisson point process}
{wiki=Campbell's_theorem_(probability)}

= Campbell theorem
{synonym}
{c}

For a Poisson point process $\Pi$ with intensity measure $\mu$ and a square-integrable measurable function $g$,
$$
\mathbb E\sum_{x\in\Pi}g(x)=\int g\,d\mu,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{x\in\Pi}g(x)\right)=\int g^2\,d\mu.
$$
The second identity follows because distinct points contribute the square of the mean, while the diagonal contribution is $\int g^2\,d\mu$.

The expectation identity holds for a general <point process> with intensity measure $\mu$ whenever the sum is integrable. The stated variance identity uses the additional <Poisson point process> assumption.

= Campbell first-moment formula
{parent=Campbell's theorem}
{c}
{title2=$\mathbb E\sum_{z\in\Pi}h(z)=\int h\,d\mu$}

For a <Poisson random measure> $\Pi$ with intensity $\mu$ and measurable nonnegative $h$, $\mathbb E\int h\,d\Pi=\int h\,d\mu$, allowing both sides to be infinite. For signed $h$, the same identity holds if $\int|h|d\mu<\infty$. This first-moment statement does not require square integrability. Applying it to a nonnegative <volume> mark gives the expected total <volume> in a marked Poisson model.

= Power-law boundary accumulation of a Poisson point process
{parent=Non-homogeneous Poisson point process}
{title2=$n^{1/(\alpha-1)}(b-X_n)\to(c/(\alpha-1))^{1/(\alpha-1)}$}

If an <intensity function of a point process> has $\lambda(x)\sim c(b-x)^{-\alpha}$ at a finite endpoint, with $c>0$ and $\alpha>1$, the cumulative intensity satisfies $F(x)\sim c(b-x)^{1-\alpha}/(\alpha-1)$. The <Poisson mapping theorem> turns $F(X_n)$ into unit-rate arrivals. The <strong law for Poisson arrival times> gives $F(X_n)/n\to1$ <almost surely>, hence the displayed accumulation rate. Reversing the coordinate gives the same argument at a left endpoint. Different singularity exponents produce different powers of the index.

= Two-sided order statistics of a boundary-singular Poisson process

For intensity $x^{-2}(1-x)^{-1}$ on $(0,1)$, the cumulative-intensity maps based at $1/2$ are

L(x)=x^{-1}-2-\log\frac{x}{1-x},\qquad F(x)=2-x^{-1}+\log\frac{x}{1-x}.

The left and right images are independent unit-rate <Poisson processes>. Labeling the original points by $X_{-k}<1/2<X_n$, with $k\ge1,n\ge0$, gives $L(X_{-k})/k\to1$ and $F(X_n)/n\to1$. Since $xL(x)\to1$ at zero and $F(x)=1-\log(1-x)+o(1)$ at one, $kX_{-k}\to1$ and $\log(1-X_n)/n\to-1$.

= Independent exponential order-statistic spacings

= Waiting time for a new record after a fixed sample
{parent=Order statistic}
{title2=$P(\tau=k)=\frac n{(n+k-1)(n+k)}$}

$$
P(\tau=k)=n\int_0^1u^{n+k-2}(1-u)\,du
=\frac n{(n+k-1)(n+k)},\qquad
P(\tau>k)=\frac n{n+k}.
$$

= De Finetti theorem
{c}
{parent=Exchangeable random variables}

= Conditional independence of exchangeable sequences
{synonym}

For an infinite sequence of real <exchangeable random variables>, conditional on its <invariant sigma-algebra of an exchangeable sequence>, the coordinates are independent and identically distributed. In particular, for Borel sets $A_0,\ldots,A_p$,
$$
\mathbb E\!\left[\prod_{k=0}^p\mathbf1_{A_k}(X_k)\mid\mathcal S\right]
=\prod_{k=0}^p\mathbb E[\mathbf1_{A_k}(X_k)\mid\mathcal S].
$$
Here is the core proof. Let $\mathcal S_m$ denote invariance under permutations of coordinates $0,\ldots,m$. <Symmetrization as conditional expectation> identifies the empirical average of each indicator with its conditional expectation on $\mathcal S_m$. The <reverse martingale convergence theorem> gives its limit conditional on $\mathcal S$. Multiplying the averages sums over all ordered coordinate tuples, including repetitions. Symmetrization of the product itself sums over the distinct tuples. The fraction of repeated tuples is at most $\binom{p+1}{2}/(m+1)$, so these bounded averages have the same limit. This proves the displayed factorization. Permutation invariance also makes the individual conditional laws identical. A regular conditional distribution on the real line packages that common law as a random probability measure, giving the equivalent mixture-of-iid formulation.

= Invariant sigma-algebra of an exchangeable sequence
{parent=Exchangeable random variables}
{title2=$\mathcal S=\{A:T_\pi^{-1}A=A\text{ for every finitely supported permutation }\pi\}$}

On the coordinate product space of a sequence, the invariant sigma-algebra consists of measurable events unchanged by every finite permutation of coordinates. Its definition is pointwise and does not depend on the probability law. An exchangeable law makes each such permutation measure preserving. Events in the <tail sigma-algebra> are invariant because any finite permutation fixes every sufficiently late coordinate. Conversely, <exchangeable invariant events are tail events modulo null sets>.

= Exchangeable invariant events are tail events modulo null sets
{parent=Invariant sigma-algebra of an exchangeable sequence}

For a real sequence of <exchangeable random variables>, every event $A$ in its <invariant sigma-algebra of an exchangeable sequence> agrees almost surely with an event of its <tail sigma-algebra>. Put $F=\mathbf1_A$. Approximate $F$ in $L^1$ by its <conditional expectation> on the first $n+1$ coordinates. Swap that block with the next $n+1$ coordinates. Invariance of $A$ and the law shows that the conditional expectation on the second block is an equally good approximation. It is measurable with respect to the tail after index $n$. The <L1 contraction of conditional expectation> then bounds $\|F-\mathbb E[F\mid\mathcal G_n]\|_1$ by twice the approximation error. The <reverse martingale convergence theorem> gives $F=\mathbb E[F\mid\bigcap_n\mathcal G_n]$ almost surely. Thresholding the latter at $1/2$ supplies the required tail event. Together with the reverse inclusion, the two sigma-algebras have identical probability completions.

Independent <gamma distributions> $S\sim\Gamma(\alpha,r)$ and $T\sim\Gamma(\beta,r)$ with a common rate yield a <Beta distribution> $S/(S+T)\sim\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)$. The <change of variables> $S=wu$, $T=w(1-u)$ has <Jacobian determinant> $w$, and factors the joint <probability density function> into a <Beta distribution> density in $u$ and a <gamma distribution> density in $w$. In particular, independent <chi-squared distributions> with $2\alpha$ and $2\beta$ degrees of freedom give the required ratio.

= Cumulative distribution function
{title2=$F_X(x)$}
{parent=Probability distribution}
{wiki}

= Distribution function
{synonym}

The cumulative distribution function of a real <random variable> $X$ is
$$
F_X(x)=\mathbb P(X\leq x).
$$
It is nondecreasing, right-continuous, tends to zero at $-\infty$, and tends to one at $+\infty$.

= Singular distribution functions have zero derivative almost everywhere
{parent=Cumulative distribution function}
{title2=$\mu\perp\lambda_1\Longrightarrow F_\mu'=0\quad\lambda_1\text{-a.e.}$}

For $F_\mu(t)=\mu((-\infty,t])$, every difference quotient at $x$ is nonnegative and bounded by $4\mu(B(x,2|h|))/\lambda_1(B(x,2|h|))$. The <spherical derivative of a singular measure vanishes>, so the two-sided <derivative> is zero <Lebesgue almost everywhere>. A singular distribution function need not be constant: differentiation almost everywhere does not recover increments without <absolute continuity of a function> of the function.

= Kolmogorov distance
{c}
{parent=Cumulative distribution function}
{title2=$d_K(F,G)=\sup_z|F(z)-G(z)|$}

The <Kolmogorov distance> between two <probability distributions> with <cumulative distribution functions> $F,G$ is $\sup_{z\in\mathbb R}|F(z)-G(z)|$.

= Lattice obstruction to normal approximation
{parent=Kolmogorov distance}

If a <cumulative distribution function> is constant between consecutive lattice points spaced by $h$ near zero, then its <Kolmogorov distance> from the <standard normal distribution function> is at least half the increase of that function across one such interval. This gives a lower bound of order $h$.

= Probability integral transform
{title2=$F(X)\sim\mathcal U(0,1)$}
{parent=Cumulative distribution function}

If $X$ has a continuous <cumulative distribution function> $F$, then $F(X)$ has the <uniform distribution> on $(0,1)$. Combined with <inverse transform sampling>, this constructs the <monotone rearrangement> $G^{-1}(F(X))$ to a target with <quantile function> $G^{-1}$.

= Sequential probability integral transform
{parent=Probability integral transform}
{title2=$U_t=F_t(Y_t)\mid Y_{<t}\sim\operatorname{Unif}(0,1)$}

For correct continuous conditional forecasts, the conditional <probability integral transform> is uniform given the entire past. Iterating conditional probabilities makes the transformed sequence independent and uniform. Uniformity and serial checks assess absolute predictive calibration using only the forecast distributions and observations.

= Uniform convergence of distribution functions to a continuous limit
{parent=Cumulative distribution function}

If distribution functions $F_n$ converge pointwise to a continuous distribution function $F$, then the convergence is uniform. Choose finitely many points whose successive $F$-increments and two tails are small, then use monotonicity to squeeze $F_n-F$ between its values at adjacent points.

= Empirical distribution function

For real observations $X_1,\ldots,X_n$, the empirical distribution function is
$$
F_n(x)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbf1_{\{X_i\leq x\}}.
$$

= Anderson–Darling test
{parent=Empirical distribution function}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$n\int\frac{(F_n-F_0)^2}{F_0(1-F_0)}dF_0$}

The test rejects for large values of this tail-weighted empirical discrepancy. For a specified continuous null, its standard limiting statistic is $\int_0^1B^0(u)^2/[u(1-u)]du$, using a <Brownian bridge>. The reciprocal <variance> weight gives tail deviations more influence than the <Cramér-von Mises criterion>. If null parameters are estimated, fitting must be included when computing or simulating its reference law.

= Cramér–von Mises criterion
{parent=Empirical distribution function}
{c}
{wiki}

This goodness-of-fit criterion integrates the squared difference between the <empirical distribution function> and a specified continuous null distribution. Under a fully specified null, the <probability integral transform> makes its law distribution-free; its limiting law is $\int_0^1B^0(u)^2du$ for a <Brownian bridge>. = Empirical distribution process
{parent=Empirical distribution function}
{title2=$\alpha_n(x)=\sqrt n\,(G_n(x)-G(x))$}

The empirical distribution process is the centred, scaled <empirical distribution function> of an independent sample with <cumulative distribution function> $G$. Its mean is zero and its <covariance> is $G(\min(x,y))-G(x)G(y)$. This follows by expanding the two indicator averages: different sample indices have zero covariance, while the same index contributes the covariance of the two nested events. The scaling by $\sqrt n$ keeps these covariances independent of $n$.

= Donsker theorem for empirical distribution functions
{c}
{parent=Empirical distribution process}
{title2=$\sqrt n(F_n-F)\rightsquigarrow\mathbb G_F$}

For an independent sample with <cumulative distribution function> $F$, the <empirical distribution process> converges in the <supremum norm> on $\ell^\infty(\mathbb R)$ to the <F-Brownian bridge>. The convergence uses <outer expectation> and inner expectation when finite-sample Borel measurability fails. It is a full function-space theorem, stronger than the <finite-dimensional Brownian bridge limit of an empirical distribution process>. No continuity of $F$ is required. The <continuous mapping theorem> gives convergence of the supremum statistic; for continuous $F$, its range contains $(0,1)$ and the bridge vanishes at zero and one, giving the <Kolmogorov-Smirnov theorem>. The function-space statement is stated here as a theorem; its tightness assertion is an additional ingredient beyond the finite-dimensional covariance calculation.

= Finite-dimensional Brownian bridge limit of an empirical distribution process
{c}
{parent=Empirical distribution process}

For fixed $x_1,\ldots,x_d$, the vector $(\alpha_n(x_j))_{j=1}^d$ converges in distribution to $(B(G(x_j)))_{j=1}^d$, where $B$ is a standard <Brownian bridge>. To prove it, write the vector as $n^{-1/2}$ times the sum of independent centred bounded indicator vectors. Their covariance matrix is $G(\min(x_i,x_j))-G(x_i)G(x_j)$. The <multivariate central limit theorem> gives a centred <multivariate normal distribution> with this covariance, exactly the bridge vector. Singular covariance matrices, including endpoint times or repeated values of $G$, cause no problem. This is a finite-dimensional assertion; a full function-space limit additionally requires tightness.

= Cauchy distribution
{title2=$\operatorname{Cauchy}(x_0,\gamma)$}
{c}
{parent=Probability distribution}
{wiki}

The Cauchy distribution with location $x_0$ and scale $\gamma>0$ has <probability density function>
$$
f(x)=\frac{1}{\pi\gamma\left(1+((x-x_0)/\gamma)^2\right)}.
$$
The standard Cauchy distribution has $x_0=0$, $\gamma=1$, <characteristic function> $e^{-|t|}$, and infinite first absolute moment.

= Cauchy sample mean has an unchanged distribution
{c}
{parent=Cauchy distribution}
{title2=$(X_1+\cdots+X_n)/n\sim\operatorname{Cauchy}(0,1)$}

Independent standard <Cauchy distribution> variables sum to a Cauchy law of scale $n$, so their sample mean has its original distribution for every sample size. This follows by induction using <convolution of probability densities>. It does not contradict the usual <weak law of large numbers>, since the Cauchy law has no finite first absolute moment and its expectation is undefined. Symmetry does not turn a divergent expectation into zero.

= Half-Cauchy distribution
{parent=Cauchy distribution}
{title2=$f(x)=2\gamma/[\pi(\gamma^2+x^2)],\quad x>0$}

The absolute value of a centered <Cauchy distribution> variable with scale $\gamma>0$ has this <probability density function>. Its positive support and doubled density distinguish it from the two-sided distribution. Its unit-scale case arises as the ratio of two independent, equally scaled <half-normal distribution> variables.

= Standard Cauchy distribution
{c}
{parent=Cauchy distribution}

The standard Cauchy distribution is $\operatorname{Cauchy}(0,1)$, with density $1/[\pi(1+x^2)]$ and <characteristic function> $e^{-|t|}$.

= Cauchy law of an infinite-horizon Brownian exponential integral
{c}
{parent=Standard Cauchy distribution}
{title2=$\int_0^\infty e^{W_s-s/2}\,dB_s\sim\operatorname{Cauchy}(0,1)$}

For independent <Brownian motions> $W,B$, the <strong law for Brownian motion> makes $\int_0^\infty e^{2W_s-s}\,ds$ finite almost surely, so bracket localization gives an almost sure limit of the <stochastic integral>. The <arctangent transform of a two-noise affine diffusion> and <endpoint convergence of a bounded angle diffusion> identify its distribution function as $1/2+\arctan(x)/\pi$. For $X\sim\operatorname{Cauchy}(x_0,\gamma)$,

In particular, the <standard Cauchy distribution> has characteristic function $e^{-|t|}$.

A Cauchy random variable is a <random variable> having a <Cauchy distribution>. For a standard Cauchy random variable $V$,

= Stability of the Cauchy distribution

If $X_1,\ldots,X_n$ are independent standard Cauchy random variables, then
$$
\frac{X_1+\cdots+X_n}{n}
$$
is again standard Cauchy. Indeed, its characteristic function is $(e^{-|t|/n})^n=e^{-|t|}$.

= Stable distribution
{parent=Cauchy distribution}
{wiki}

A probability distribution is stable when every linear combination of independent copies has the same distribution up to a location and scale change. The <Cauchy distribution> is stable with stability parameter one.

= Symmetric stable distribution
{parent=Stable distribution}
{title2=$\phi(t)=\exp(i\mu t-c|t|^\alpha),\quad0<\alpha\leq2$}

A symmetric stable distribution has this <characteristic function>, with $c>0$ and location $\mu$. At location zero it is a <strictly stable distribution>: weighted sums of <independent> copies have scale factor $(\sum_j|a_j|^\alpha)^{1/\alpha}$. The index $\alpha$ need not permit a finite <mean> or <variance>. The scale in this convention is $c^{1/\alpha}$, so the coefficient of $|t|^\alpha$ and the scale parameter should not be confused.

= Cosine integral for a symmetric stable exponent
{parent=Symmetric stable distribution}
{title2=$\int_0^\infty(1-\cos u)u^{-1-\alpha}\,du=\pi/[2\Gamma(1+\alpha)\sin(\pi\alpha/2)]$}

For $0<\alpha<2$, represent $u^{-1-\alpha}$ as $\Gamma(1+\alpha)^{-1}\int_0^\infty v^\alpha e^{-vu}\,dv$. The <Tonelli theorem> applies to the nonnegative integrand. The inner <integral> in $u$ is $1/[v(1+v^2)]$, leaving $\Gamma(1+\alpha)^{-1}\int_0^\infty v^{\alpha-1}/(1+v^2)\,dv$. Substitution $z=v^2$ and the <Euler beta function> together with the <Gamma reflection formula> give the result. The bounds at zero and infinity explain exactly the index range.

= Strictly stable distribution
{parent=Stable distribution}
{title2=$\alpha$}

A <probability distribution> is strictly stable with index $\alpha$ if $n^{-1/\alpha}(T^{(1)}+\cdots+T^{(n)})\overset d=T$ for every positive integer $n$, for <independent> copies. Here no centering term is allowed, unlike in the general definition of a <stable distribution>.

= Probability generating function
{title2=$G_X(s)=\mathbb E[s^X]$}
{parent=Probability distribution}
{wiki}

= PGF
{c}
{synonym}

For a nonnegative integer-valued <random variable> $X$, its probability generating function is
$$
G_X(s)=\mathbb E[s^X]=\sum_{n\geq0}\mathbb P(X=n)s^n.
$$
The generating function of a sum of <independent random variables> is the product of their generating functions.

= Redistribution of polynomial factors for dice
{parent=Probability generating function}

Encode a fair die with face labels $a_1,\ldots,a_s$ by $A(z)=\sum_i z^{a_i}$. Its <probability generating function> is $A(z)/s$. Two <independent> dice with polynomials $A,B$ have the same sum <probability distribution> as another pair $C,D$ if $AB=CD$ and all four dice have $s$ faces. Redistributing factors can construct unexpected examples, but each resulting polynomial must have nonnegative integer <coefficients>, no constant term if labels must be positive, and value $s$ at $z=1$. For example, reallocating $1-z+z^2$ from one copy of $z+\cdots+z^6$ to the other gives face labels $(1,2,2,3,3,4)$ and $(1,3,4,5,6,8)$.

= Dirichlet probability generating function
{c}
{parent=Probability generating function}

For a positive integer-valued <random variable> of finite support, this transform is a finite <Dirichlet series>. Its values encode ordinary moments, $q_X(-r)=\mathbb E[X^r]$, and its derivatives at zero encode logarithmic moments: $(-1)^rq_X^{(r)}(0)=\mathbb E[(\log X)^r]$. Positive support is essential to these definitions; the transform is distinct from the ordinary <probability generating function> $\mathbb E[s^X]$.

For a distribution function $F$, its generalized inverse or quantile function is
$$
F^{-1}(p)=\inf\{t:F(t)\geq p\}.
$$

= Sample quantile
{parent=Quantile function}
{title2=$\widehat F_n^{-1}(p)=X_{(\lceil np\rceil)}$}

= Empirical quantile
{synonym}

For $0<p<1$, the <sample quantile> is the generalized inverse of the <empirical distribution function>: $\inf\{x:\widehat F_n(x)\geq p\}$. With ordered observations this is $X_{(\lceil np\rceil)}$, including ties. If the population <probability density function> is continuous and positive at its unique $p$th <quantile>, the centered quantile has asymptotic variance $p(1-p)/f(F^{-1}(p))^2$ under scaling by $\sqrt n$.

= Quantile endpoint at probability one
{parent=Quantile function}
{title2=$F^{-1}(1)=\inf\{x:F(x)=1\}$}

The <quantile function> is finite at every probability strictly between zero and one, but need not be finite at one. For any unbounded-above <normal distribution>, $F(x)<1$ for every real $x$, so its generalized inverse at one is $+\infty$. <Inverse transform sampling> still produces a real random variable almost surely because a continuous <uniform distribution> puts zero mass at that endpoint. A real-valued implementation may discard endpoint draws or assign an arbitrary finite value there without changing the sampled law.

= Left continuity of the quantile function
{parent=Quantile function}
{title2=$Q(u)=\sup_{v<u}Q(v)$}

For $Q(u)=\inf\{x:F(x)\geq u\}$, $0<u<1$, the <quantile function> is a <monotone function> and is left-continuous. The <distribution function> is right-continuous, giving $Q(u)\leq x$ exactly when $u\leq F(x)$. If $\sup_{v<u}Q(v)<Q(u)$, a point between them has $F(x)<u$ and contradicts this equivalence for some $v\in(F(x),u)$. A jump just to the right of $u$ is possible when $F$ is flat on an interval.

= Quantile derivative identity
{parent=Quantile function}
{title2=$q^{\prime}(u)=1/f(q(u))$}

If $q$ is the <quantile function> of an absolutely continuous <probability distribution> with <probability density function> $f$, then $q^{\prime}(u)=1/f(q(u))$ for almost every $u\in(0,1)$. Gaps in the support may give jumps in $q$, so the identity concerns its ordinary almost-everywhere derivative. It follows by differentiating the inverse relation to the <cumulative distribution function>.

= Inverse transform sampling

= Method of inversion
{synonym}

If $U$ is uniform on $[0,1]$, then $F^{-1}(U)$ has distribution function $F$. Indeed, right continuity gives
$$
F^{-1}(p)\leq t\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p\leq F(t)
$$
apart from irrelevant endpoint cases.

= Recycling a uniform random variable after discrete sampling
{parent=Inverse transform sampling}
{title2=$V=(U-C_{J-1})/w_J$}

For positive <mixture weights> $w_j$ summing to one, set $C_j=\sum_{i\leq j}w_i$ and choose $J=j$ when $C_{j-1}\leq U<C_j$, where $U$ has a <uniform distribution>. Then $V=(U-C_{J-1})/w_J$ has a <uniform distribution> independently of $J$: for $0\leq v\leq1$, $\Pr(J=j,V\leq v)=w_jv$. This permits exact <finite mixture model> sampling with a recycled continuous <random variable>.

= Quantile coupling for convergence in distribution
{parent=Quantile function}

If $F_n\to F$ at every continuity point of $F$, then the generalized inverses satisfy
$$
F_n^{-1}(u)\to F^{-1}(u)
$$
at every continuity point of $F^{-1}$. Since a monotone function has only countably many discontinuities, evaluating all quantiles at one uniform random variable gives an almost-sure coupling of the corresponding convergence in distribution.

= Dyadic rigidity of a moment-generating function
{parent=Moment-generating function}
{title2=$M(2t)=M(t)^4$}

Suppose a positive function $M$ satisfies the displayed fourth-power identity and $\log M(h)=h^2/2+o(h^2)$ at zero. Iteration gives $\log M(t)=4^m\log M(t/2^m)$, which tends to $t^2/2$. A related first-power symmetry step is: if $\psi(t)=\psi(t/2)^2$ and $\psi(h)=1+o(h^2)$, then $\log\psi(t)=2^m\log\psi(t/2^m)\to0$. These two different dyadic exponents must not be interchanged in the <Gaussian characterization by independent sum and difference>.

= Uniform consistency of an empirical moment-generating function
{parent=Moment-generating function}
{title2=$\sup_{0\le s\le t}|\widehat m_n(s)-m(s)|\to0$}

If $X\ge0$ and $\mathbb E e^{tX}<\infty$ for some finite $t\ge0$, the estimator $\widehat m_n(s)=n^{-1}\sum_i e^{sX_i}$ satisfies $\sup_{s\in[0,t]}|\widehat m_n(s)-\mathbb E e^{sX}|\to0$ almost surely. The <dominated convergence theorem> makes the population <moment-generating function> uniformly continuous on this interval. Parameter subintervals give <function brackets> whose endpoints are ordered exponentials and whose widths are the corresponding increments of the population mean. Apply the <uniform strong law from finite L1 bracketing>. Continuity in the parameter makes the supremum measurable.

= Saddlepoint density approximation
{parent=Cumulant-generating function}
{title2=$\widehat f_{S_n}(nx)=\frac{e^{n[K(\widehat t)-\widehat t x]}}{\sqrt{2\pi nK''(\widehat t)}}$}

= Statistical saddlepoint approximation
{synonym}

For independent identically distributed continuous random variables, let $K(t)=\log\mathbb E e^{tY}$ be finite near zero. At a mean value $x$ reached by $K'(\widehat t)=x$ in the interior of that domain, the displayed leading approximation describes the density of the sum $S_n$. <Exponential tilting> by $\widehat t$ puts the sum at its tilted mean; a local normal approximation gives the denominator, and undoing the tilt gives the exponential factor. Under suitable smooth nonlattice regularity, at fixed interior $x$ the exact density is the displayed quantity times $1+O(n^{-1})$. This statistical approximation is an application of the <saddle-point approximation> to a transform inversion integral. It requires more than the existence of a few ordinary moments.

= Saddlepoint density of a Laplace sample mean
{parent=Saddlepoint density approximation}
{title2=$\widehat f_{\bar Y}(y)=\sqrt{\frac{n}{2\pi D(1+D)}}\exp\!\left[n\left\{\log\frac{1+D}{2}-D+1\right\}\right],\quad D=\sqrt{1+y^2}$}

For independent unit-scale <Laplace distributions>, the <cumulant-generating function> is $K(t)=-\log(1-t^2)$ for $|t|<1$. The <saddlepoint density approximation> solves $K'(t)=y$, giving $t=y/(1+\sqrt{1+y^2})$. At this tilt, $K''(t)=D(1+D)$ and $ty=D-1$, giving the displayed <probability density function> approximation for the <sample mean>. Its value at zero is $\sqrt{n/(4\pi)}$; it need not integrate to one before normalization.

= Lugannani-Rice saddlepoint tail approximation
{c}
{parent=Saddlepoint density approximation}

For a sample mean from an independent identically distributed sample, let $K(t)$ be the <cumulant-generating function> of one observation and solve $K'(t)=x$. Put $w=\operatorname{sgn}(t)\sqrt{2n(tx-K(t))}$ and $u=t\sqrt{nK''(t)}$. Under the smooth, nonlattice conditions for a <saddlepoint density approximation>, the corresponding approximation to the <cumulative distribution function> is
$$
\Pr(\overline X\le x)\simeq\Phi(w)+\phi(w)\left(\frac1w-\frac1u\right),
$$
where $\Phi$ and $\phi$ are the standard <normal distribution> functions. The apparent singularity at $t=0$ is removable. Write $v=K''(0)$ and $k=K'''(0)$; <Taylor expansion> gives $w=t\sqrt{nv}(1+kt/(3v)+O(t^2))$ and $u=t\sqrt{nv}(1+kt/(2v)+O(t^2))$. Thus $1/w-1/u\to k/(6\sqrt n\,v^{3/2})$. The approximation requires suitable tail regularity and is not a universal formula for distributions without a moment-generating function.

= P-star approximation
{parent=Saddlepoint density approximation}
{title2=$p^*(\widehat\theta\mid a;\theta)=c(\theta,a)|j(\widehat\theta)|^{1/2}e^{\ell(\theta)-\ell(\widehat\theta)}$}

= p* approximation
{synonym}

For a regular model with a suitable <ancillary statistic> $a$, the displayed expression approximates the conditional density of the <maximum-likelihood estimator>. The normalizing factor includes $(2\pi)^{-p/2}$ for a $p$-dimensional estimator, depending on convention. The <observed information> is evaluated at the sample's own fitted value. In a pure scale model the factor is proportional to $1/\widehat\theta$; this <Jacobian determinant> can make the normalized approximation coincide with the exact conditional density.

= P-star density for two exponential rates
{c}
{parent=P-star approximation}
{title2=$p^*(\widehat\psi,\widehat\lambda)$}

For independent samples of size $n$ from <exponential distributions> with rates $\lambda$ and $\psi\lambda$, the <maximum-likelihood estimators> are $\widehat\lambda=n/S$ and $\widehat\psi=S/T$, where $S$ and $T$ are the respective sample sums. Their normalized <P-star approximation> is exact:
$$
p(\widehat\psi,\widehat\lambda;\psi,\lambda)=\frac{n^{2n}\psi^n\lambda^{2n}}{\Gamma(n)^2\widehat\psi^{n+1}\widehat\lambda^{2n+1}}\exp\left\{-\frac{n\lambda}{\widehat\lambda}\left(1+\frac\psi{\widehat\psi}\right)\right\},\qquad \widehat\psi,\widehat\lambda>0.
$$
Indeed, $S$ and $T$ have independent <gamma distributions> with shapes $n$ and respective rates $\lambda,\psi\lambda$. The <Jacobian determinant> of their inverse transformation is $n^2/(\widehat\psi^2\widehat\lambda^3)$, giving the displayed <density>. Alternatively the square root of the determinant of the fitted <observed information> is $n/(\widehat\psi\widehat\lambda)$; multiplying by the exponential of the fitted <log-likelihood> difference gives the same shape. Its normalizing constant in the <P-star approximation> is $c_n=n^{2n-1}e^{-2n}/\Gamma(n)^2$. Integrating over $\widehat\lambda$ gives
$$
p(\widehat\psi;\psi)=\frac{\Gamma(2n)}{\Gamma(n)^2\psi}\left(\frac{\widehat\psi}\psi\right)^{n-1}\left(1+\frac{\widehat\psi}\psi\right)^{-2n},
$$
so $\widehat\psi/\psi$ has the <F-distribution> with degrees of freedom $(2n,2n)$.

= P-star density for an exponential mean
{parent=P-star approximation}
{title2=$p^*(v;\theta)\propto v^{n-1}\theta^{-n}e^{-nv/\theta}$}

For a size-$n$ sample from an <exponential distribution> of mean $\theta$, the likelihood in the <maximum-likelihood estimator> coordinate $v=\bar Y$ has difference $\ell(\theta;v)-\ell(v;v)=n\log(v/\theta)-nv/\theta+n$, and the fitted <observed information> is $n/v^2$. Their <P-star approximation> is the displayed gamma density shape. Its normalization gives exactly the sampling density $n^nv^{n-1}e^{-nv/\theta}/(\Gamma(n)\theta^n)$. This illustrates an exact transformation-model case rather than a general claim of exactness.

= Ancillary-conditioned score density
{parent=P-star approximation}
{title2=$f_U(u\mid a;\theta)\approx\sum_{v:u(v,a)=u}\frac{p^*(v\mid a;\theta)}{|\partial_vu(v,a)|}$}

Fix an <ancillary statistic> $a$ and a parameter value at which the <score function> is evaluated. Express that score as a function $u(v,a)$ of the varying <maximum-likelihood estimator> coordinate $v$. The ordinary density change-of-variables formula applied to the <P-star approximation> gives the displayed density; several monotone branches require a sum. = Polynomial tails prevent exponential tilting
{parent=Saddlepoint density approximation}

If a density has polynomially decaying tails on both half-lines, its <moment-generating function> is infinite at every nonzero real parameter: exponential growth defeats polynomial decay on the positive tail for a positive parameter, and on the negative tail for a negative parameter. Thus no open real tilting domain exists for a <saddlepoint density approximation>, even when the distribution has finite mean and variance and a <central limit theorem> applies.

= Cumulant

The $n$th cumulant is the coefficient of $t^n/n!$ in the <cumulant-generating function>. The first two <cumulants> are the <expected value> and <variance>; the third is the third <central moment>.

= Cumulant expansion
{parent=Cumulant-generating function}
{wiki}

The cumulant expansion writes
$$
\log\mathbb E[e^{tX}]
=\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{t^n}{n!}\kappa_n(X),
$$
where $\kappa_n$ is the $n$th cumulant. Applied to a fluctuating interaction, it expands the logarithm of the averaged Boltzmann weight in connected expectation values.

= Edgeworth series
{parent=Cumulant expansion}
{c}
{wiki}

= Edgeworth expansion
{synonym}
{c}

An Edgeworth expansion approximates a <probability density function> by a <normal distribution> multiplied by <Hermite polynomials> encoding higher <cumulants>. For a centered variable of <variance> $\sigma^2$ and third cumulant $\kappa_3$,
$$
p(x)=\frac{e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}
\left[1+\frac{\kappa_3}{6\sigma^3}
\operatorname{He}_3(x/\sigma)+\cdots\right],
\qquad \operatorname{He}_3(z)=z^3-3z.
$$
The correction follows from inverse-transforming the cubic term of the <cumulant-generating function>. It is an asymptotic approximation near the bulk of the distribution; truncation can give a negative result in far tails.

= Symmetric Edgeworth coverage cancellation
{parent=Edgeworth series}
{title2=$F_n(z)-F_n(-z)=2\Phi(z)-1+O(n^{-1})$}

For a standardized iid sum with a regular <Edgeworth expansion>, the first distribution correction is proportional to $\phi(t)(t^2-1)$ and is even. Subtracting the two endpoint distribution functions therefore cancels the $n^{-1/2}$ contribution. Fixed symmetric pivot cutoffs can consequently have $O(n^{-1})$ coverage error even though each one-sided approximation has error $O(n^{-1/2})$. This cancellation does not automatically hold for asymmetric or estimated random cutoffs.

= Cornish-Fisher expansion
{c}
{parent=Edgeworth series}
{wiki}
{title2=$y_\alpha=z_\alpha+\gamma_1(z_\alpha^2-1)/(6\sqrt n)+O(n^{-1})$}

The Cornish-Fisher expansion inverts an <Edgeworth expansion> to approximate <quantiles>. For a standardized iid sum, let $\gamma_1$ be the <skewness> of one summand and $z_\alpha$ the <standard normal quantile>. The first <distribution function> correction is $-\gamma_1(z^2-1)\phi(z)/(6\sqrt n)$. Substituting $y_\alpha=z_\alpha+c/\sqrt n$ and expanding $\Phi$ gives $c=\gamma_1(z_\alpha^2-1)/6$. This is a fixed-quantile asymptotic, with $\alpha$ fixed in $(0,1)$.

= Cramér theorem
{synonym}
{c}

For <independent and identically distributed random variables> with a <moment-generating function> finite on an open interval containing zero, the empirical mean satisfies a <large deviation principle> with rate function given by the <Legendre transform of a cumulant-generating function>. The exponential-moment hypothesis is part of this theorem; mere integrability does not guarantee this full large-deviation principle.

= Poisson sample-mean rate function
{parent=Cramér's theorem}
{c}
{title2=$I_P(x)=x\log(x/\lambda)-x+\lambda\quad(x\ge0)$}

For sample means of <Poisson random variables> of mean $\lambda>0$, the <cumulant-generating function> is $\lambda(e^\theta-1)$. Its <Legendre-Fenchel transform> is the displayed <good rate function>, with $0\log0=0$, and is infinite for $x<0$. Near its zero, $I_P(\lambda+h)=h^2/(2\lambda)+O(h^3)$. This local quadratic explains the <Poisson moderate deviation principle>.

= Rate function of an exponential sample mean
{parent=Cramér's theorem}
{title2=$I(x)=\lambda x-1-\log(\lambda x)\quad(x>0)$}

For <independent and identically distributed> <exponential random variables> of rate $\lambda>0$, the sample mean has <large-deviation speed> $n$ and the displayed <good rate function>, infinite for $x\le0$. The <cumulant-generating function> is $\log[\lambda/(\lambda-\theta)]$ for $\theta<\lambda$. Its <Legendre-Fenchel transform> has maximizing parameter $\theta=\lambda-1/x$. For $x\le0$, sending $\theta\to-\infty$ gives infinite cost. The cost has its unique zero at $1/\lambda$ and diverges at both ends of its positive domain.

= Exponential sample-mean upper-tail rate
{parent=Cramér's theorem}
{title2=$I(a)=a-1-\log a$}

For <independent> unit-rate <exponential random variables>, the <cumulant-generating function> is $-\log(1-\theta)$ for $\theta<1$. Its <Legendre transform of a cumulant-generating function> is $I(x)=x-1-\log x$ for $x>0$ and infinite otherwise. The <Cramér theorem> gives the upper-tail logarithmic rate $-I(a)$ for $a>1$, and zero for $0\leq a\leq1$. At the mean threshold the infima on both the open and closed upper half-lines are zero; below the mean the <strong law of large numbers> applies.

= Conditional concentration at a large-deviation threshold
{parent=Cramér's theorem}
{title2=$\mathbb P(S_n/n\in[a,a+\varepsilon)\mid S_n/n\geq a)\to1$}

For independent identically distributed real variables with everywhere finite <cumulant-generating function>, let $\mu$ be the mean. If $a\geq\mu$ and $\mathbb P(X_1>a)>0$, then $\mathbb P(S_n/n\in[a,a+\varepsilon)\mid S_n/n\geq a)\to1$ for each $\varepsilon>0$. The <Legendre transform of a cumulant-generating function> is finite at $a$, and a positive exponential parameter gives strictly greater cost at $a+\varepsilon$. A <Chernoff bound> for the farther tail divided by the <Cramér theorem> lower estimate at $a$ decays exponentially. The support condition both ensures positive conditioning probabilities and avoids a vacuous conditioning event.

= Gaussian characterization by independent sum and difference
{c}
{parent=Normal distribution}
{title2=$X+Y\perp X-Y$}

= Gaussian moment pairing theorem
{synonym}
{c}

For centered jointly <Gaussian random variables>, an <odd> product has zero expectation, and an <even> product's expectation is the sum over all pairings of the products of the pair covariances. In particular,
$$
\mathbb E[X_1X_2X_3X_4]
=\mathbb E[X_1X_2]\mathbb E[X_3X_4]
+\mathbb E[X_1X_3]\mathbb E[X_2X_4]
+\mathbb E[X_1X_4]\mathbb E[X_2X_3].
$$
For a proof, the <moment-generating function> is $\exp(t^T\Sigma t/2)$. Differentiating once in each variable at zero leaves precisely products of covariance entries indexed by complete pairings. No <independence> assumption between the coordinates is needed.

= Gaussian even-moment pairing count
{c}
{parent=Isserlis's theorem}
{title2=$\mathbb EX^{2n}=(2n-1)!!\,v^n$}

For a centered scalar <normal random variable> of <variance> $v$, the <Isserlis theorem> sums complete pairings of $2n$ factors. The first factor has $2n-1$ partners, then $2n-3$ choices remain for the first unpaired factor, giving $(2n-1)!!=(2n)!/(2^nn!)$. Odd moments vanish. Summing the <moment-generating function> gives $\exp(vs^2/2)$. This is a scalar formula, not a formula for the length of a vector.

= Gaussian series with square-summable coefficients

= Almost sure convergence criterion for an independent Gaussian series
{parent=Gaussian series with square-summable coefficients}
{title2=$\sum_ka_kZ_k\text{ converges a.s.}\iff\sum_ka_k^2<\infty$}

For <independent> standard <Gaussian random variables>, the partial sums have <characteristic functions> $\exp(-u^2v_n/2)$, where $v_n=\sum_{k\leq n}a_k^2$. A finite almost-sure limit implies convergence of these <characteristic functions> to one continuous at zero; $v_n\to\infty$ would instead give zero at every nonzero $u$. Conversely, bounded $v_n$ makes the partial sums an <martingale> bounded in <L2 space>, which converges <almost surely> and in <L2 space>.

= Threshold stopping of an independent Gaussian series
{parent=Almost sure convergence criterion for an independent Gaussian series}
{title2=$\mathbb EM_T=0\iff\sum_ka_k^2<\infty$}

Let $M_n=\sum_{k\leq n}a_kZ_k$, and stop on first reaching a fixed positive threshold. If the coefficient squares are summable, <uniform integrability> gives the stopped mean zero, with the terminal limit used on $T=\infty$. If the coefficient squares diverge, $\mathbb P(M_n\geq R)\to1/2$ for each fixed $R$. Thus $\mathbb P(\sup_nM_n=\infty)\geq1/2$. This event is unaffected by changing finitely many of the <independent> variables, so the <Kolmogorov zero-one law> makes its probability one. Hence $T<\infty$ and $M_T$ is at least the positive threshold; its <expectation>, finite or infinite, cannot be zero.

{title2=$\mathbb P(V-n\geq2\sqrt{nx}+2x)\leq e^{-x}$}

Indeed $\log\mathbb E e^{s(V-n)}=-ns-(n/2)\log(1-2s)\leq ns^2/(1-2s)$ for $0<s<1/2$. The <Chernoff bound> with $s=\sqrt{x/n}/(1+2\sqrt{x/n})$ proves the upper estimate. For the lower estimate, $\log\mathbb E e^{-s(V-n)}\leq ns^2$ for $s>0$, and optimization gives the result. The lower threshold can be negative; the <chi-squared Chernoff lower-tail bound> provides an always-positive alternative.

= Chi-squared Chernoff lower-tail bound
{parent=Chi-squared concentration inequality}
{title2=$\mathbb P(V\leq na)\leq e^{-n(a-1-\log a)/2}$}

= Conditional probability
{title2=$P(A\mid B)$}
{parent=Probability theory}
{wiki}

For <events> $A,B$ with $\mathbb P(B)>0$, conditional probability is
$$
\mathbb P(A\mid B)=\frac{\mathbb P(A\cap B)}{\mathbb P(B)}.
$$
Restrict attention to the outcomes in $B$, then renormalize their <probability measure> so its total mass becomes one. The resulting rule $A\mapsto\mathbb P(A\mid B)$ is itself a <probability measure>. The definition and its connection to <independent events> are developed in \a[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-05-introduction-to-probability-and-statistics-spring-2022/resources/mit18_05_s22_class03-prep_pdf/][MIT 18.05, Reading 3: Conditional Probability, Independence and Bayes' Theorem]{ref}.

For the two-dice model, let $A$ mean the sum is at least nine and $B$ mean the first result is six. Of the six outcomes in $B$, four belong to $A$: the second die can be 3, 4, 5, or 6. Thus **$\mathbb P(A\mid B)=2/3$**. Reversing the information gives a different answer: four of the ten outcomes in $A$ have first result six, so **$\mathbb P(B\mid A)=2/5$**. Both ratios use the same overlap; their denominators describe different information.

\Image[conditional-probability-dice.png]
{title=Conditioning selects a different denominator in the same dice outcome grid}

The definition requires positive conditioning mass. For a continuously distributed <random variable> $X$, it cannot directly define $\mathbb P(A\mid X=x)$ when $\mathbb P(X=x)=0$. A <regular conditional distribution> or <conditional density> supplies the appropriate extension, with the usual <almost everywhere> qualification. Nor does conditioning by itself justify <causal inference>: changing which outcomes are considered is different from physically changing how the experiment is generated.

= Three prisoners problem
{parent=Conditional probability}
{wiki}

A messenger names an excluded candidate while concealing the uniquely selected one. The <conditional probability> depends on the naming rule. With three equally likely selected candidates and a messenger forbidden to name the observing candidate A, let $s$ be the probability of naming B when A is selected. When C is selected the messenger must name B, and when B is selected cannot name B. Thus the probability that A is not selected, conditional on B being named, is $1/(1+s)$. Unbiased naming has $s=1/2$ and leaves this probability $2/3$; bias toward B gives $1/2$, and bias toward C gives one. Conditioning on the name is different from conditioning only on B's exclusion.

= Chain rule for probabilities
{parent=Conditional probability}
{title2=$P(A_1\cap\cdots\cap A_m)=\prod_jP(A_j\mid A_1\cap\cdots\cap A_{j-1})$}

When all conditioning events have positive <probability>, repeatedly apply the definition of <conditional probability> to factor a joint event into successive conditional factors. For a sequence of discrete responses, this yields the product of conditional masses along the actual outcome path. It requires no unconditional independence assumption.

= Disintegration theorem for a probability measure
{parent=Conditional probability}

For a <probability measure> on a standard Borel space and a measurable map $\pi$ to another standard Borel space, there is an almost-everywhere unique <measurable probability kernel> $y\mapsto\mu_y$ supported on $\pi^{-1}\{y\}$. It integrates to $\mu$, and $\int f\,d\mu_{\pi(x)}$ realizes <conditional expectation> given $\pi$. These measures are the <conditional measures of a factor>.

= Bayes' theorem
{c}
{parent=Conditional probability}
{wiki}
{title2=$\mathbb P(B\mid A)=\mathbb P(A\mid B)\mathbb P(B)/\mathbb P(A)$}

For a finite <measurable partition> $(B_j)$ with positive <probabilities>, and an <event> $A$ of positive <probability>,
$$
\mathbb P(B_j\mid A)
=\frac{\mathbb P(A\mid B_j)\mathbb P(B_j)}
{\sum_k\mathbb P(A\mid B_k)\mathbb P(B_k)}.
$$
Its beauty is that a model predicting observations from a hidden state can also describe uncertainty about the state after an observation. Each numerator is a joint <probability>; the denominator, supplied by the <law of total probability>, makes the answers sum to one. This is a direct consequence of <conditional probability>, rather than an extra axiom.

Select the coin from <law of total probability> once, then toss that same coin repeatedly, with <conditionally independent> tosses given its identity. Let $B$ denote the $3/4$-head coin, initially of <prior probability> $1/5$. After one head $H$, its <posterior probability> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(B\mid H)=\frac{(1/5)(3/4)}{(1/5)(3/4)+(4/5)(1/2)}=\frac3{11}.}
$$
The <likelihood> of a head is larger under $B$, but $B$ remains less likely than the other coin because its <prior probability> was smaller. A large <likelihood> for an observation does not itself imply a large <posterior probability> for the hypothesis.

After an ordered sequence containing $h$ heads and $t$ tails, its <likelihood ratio> between the two coins is $(3/2)^h(1/2)^t$. Bayes' theorem therefore gives <posterior odds>
$$
O_{B:B^c}=\frac14\left(\frac32\right)^h\left(\frac12\right)^t,\qquad
\mathbb P(B\mid\text{sequence})=\frac{O_{B:B^c}}{1+O_{B:B^c}}.
$$
Each head multiplies the <odds> by $3/2$ and each tail by $1/2$. Three heads give <posterior probability> $27/59$, still below one half; four give $81/145$, above one half. The finite-model update and the distinction between <prior probability>, <likelihood> and <posterior probability> are set out in \a[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-05-introduction-to-probability-and-statistics-spring-2022/mit18_05_s22_statistics.pdf][MIT 18.05, Reading 11: Bayesian Updating with Discrete Priors]{ref}.

\Image[bayes-coin-updating.png]
{title=Updating uncertainty about one selected coin after repeated observations}

The hidden selection also creates dependence. Each toss has marginal head <probability> $11/20$, but two heads have joint <probability> $(4/5)(1/2)^2+(1/5)(3/4)^2=5/16$, greater than $(11/20)^2=121/400$. Their indicator <covariance> is $1/100$. Seeing a head makes the head-favoring coin more likely, so it also raises the chance of a future head. This is <covariance induced by a shared latent variable>, despite <conditional independence> of the tosses given the coin.

Historically, Thomas Bayes's essay on an unknown success <probability> appeared in 1763 after his death, communicated by Richard Price. Its opening problem asks how observed successes and failures constrain that unknown <probability>; Price's introductory letter explains the connection to reasoning from experience. That is a more substantial inference problem than simply rearranging today's elementary identity. \a[https://www.gtfp.cs.rhul.ac.uk/pulskamp/Bayes/bayesessay-rjp.pdf][Bayes, An Essay towards solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances, 1763; original text transcribed, introductory letter and statement of the problem]{ref}

= Posterior coin count after exactly one head
{parent=Bayes' theorem}
{title2=$P(N=n\mid H=1)=n(1-r)^2r^{n-1}$}

Let $N$ have the positive-integer <geometric distribution> with success parameter $p\in(0,1)$, and conditionally toss $N$ independent coins with head <probability> $\theta\in(0,1)$. Exactly one head has conditional <probability> $n\theta(1-\theta)^{n-1}$. Combining this with the prior by <Bayes theorem> gives the displayed posterior with $r=(1-p)(1-\theta)$; its normalization uses $\sum_{n\geq1}nr^{n-1}=(1-r)^{-2}$. For $p=\theta=1/2$, the posterior is $9n/4^{n+1}$. It is also a shifted <negative binomial distribution>, with $N-1$ equal in law to the number of failures before two successes of <probability> $1-r$.

= Posterior equality for proportional likelihoods
{parent=Bayes' theorem}
{title2=$L_i'=hL_i\ \Longrightarrow\ \pi_i'=\pi_i$}

If every model's <likelihood> is multiplied by the same positive factor independent of its parameter or model index, <Bayes theorem> cancels that factor from the normalized posterior. Fixed-count Bernoulli sampling and sampling stopped at a specified positive head count give such proportional <likelihoods> for the same total heads and tails. This conclusion uses their actual sampling <likelihoods>; it is not a claim that every selection or stopping rule can be ignored.

= Posterior coin selection from repeated tosses
{parent=Bayes' theorem}
{title2=$P(B\mid H^m)=\pi p_B^m/[(1-\pi)p_A^m+\pi p_B^m]$}

If a hidden coin is selected once with prior probability $\pi$ of coin $B$, repeated tosses are <independent> conditional on that coin. <Bayes theorem> weights the prior by each all-heads likelihood. They need not be <independent> without conditioning on the chosen coin.

= Regular conditional distribution
{parent=Conditional distribution}
{title2=$K(y,B)=P(X\in B\mid Y=y)$}

A regular conditional distribution of a <random variable> $X$ given $Y$ is a <Markov kernel> $K$ such that, for all measurable sets $A,B$,
$$
P(X\in B,Y\in A)=\int_A K(y,B)\,P_Y(dy).
$$
For each $y$, $K(y,\cdot)$ is a <probability measure>; for each $B$, $K(\cdot,B)$ is measurable. Thus it assigns an actual probability distribution to a specified observation even when $P(Y=y)=0$. Such kernels exist for standard Borel state spaces, including real-valued variables, and their uniqueness is only up to $P_Y$-null sets. With a joint density and positive marginal density, the ratio of the two densities defines the usual conditional density kernel. Values outside the marginal support or on a null set are not uniquely determined by the joint law.

= Poisson-Dirichlet distribution
{parent=Random partition}
{c}
{wiki}

The Poisson-Dirichlet family consists of laws of decreasing random mass partitions $(p_i)$ with $\sum_i p_i=1$. Its two-parameter notation is $PD(\alpha,\theta)$, with $0\le\alpha<1$ and $\theta>-\alpha$. The conventional one-parameter <probability distribution> $PD(\theta)$ means $PD(0,\theta)$, for $\theta>0$, and can be constructed from normalized gamma jumps.

= One-parameter Poisson-Dirichlet distribution
{parent=Poisson-Dirichlet distribution}
{title2=$PD(\theta)=PD(0,\theta)$}

Take a <Poisson random measure> on $(0,\infty)$ with intensity $\theta e^{-u}du/u$ and arrange its atoms as $J_1>J_2>\cdots$. The sum $T=\sum_iJ_i$ is finite and positive <almost surely>, has <Gamma distribution> with shape $\theta$ and rate one, and the law of $(J_i/T)$ is $PD(\theta)$. The order is decreasing, so the first component is the largest; a size-biased selection of a component has a different law.

= Largest component of a Poisson-Dirichlet partition
{parent=One-parameter Poisson-Dirichlet distribution}
{title2=$F_\theta(a)=\mathbb P(p_1\le a)$}

For $a\ge1/2$, at most one component exceeds $a$, so $1-F_\theta(a)=\theta\int_a^1x^{-1}(1-x)^{\theta-1}dx$. Below one half several components can exceed the threshold. Put $N_a=\#\{i:p_i>a\}$ and use the finite identity $\mathbf1_{\{N_a=0\}}=\sum_m(-1)^m(N_a)_m/m!$, with the <factorial moment densities of Poisson-Dirichlet components>. It gives the full <probability distribution> as finite <inclusion-exclusion> over $x_i>a$, $\sum_i x_i<1$. In contrast, a size-biased component is beta-distributed.

= Factorial moment densities of Poisson-Dirichlet components
{parent=One-parameter Poisson-Dirichlet distribution}
{title2=$\alpha_\theta^{(m)}(dx)=\theta^m\frac{(1-\sum_i x_i)^{\theta-1}}{\prod_i x_i}\,dx$}

This density is supported where all $x_i>0$ and $\sum_i x_i<1$, and gives the expected sum over ordered distinct components of $PD(\theta)$. Apply the iterated <Mecke formula for a Poisson random measure> to $m$ selected jumps $u_i$ and the remaining gamma total $t$. With $s=t+\sum_i u_i$ and $x_i=u_i/s$, the Jacobian is $s^m$ and the integral in $s$ is $\Gamma(\theta)$, giving the density. It describes joint counts of large components, beyond the first-moment measure.

= Mean measure of Poisson-Dirichlet components
{parent=One-parameter Poisson-Dirichlet distribution}
{title2=$\alpha_\theta(dx)=\theta x^{-1}(1-x)^{\theta-1}\,dx$}

For $PD(\theta)$, $\mathbb E\sum_i\varphi(p_i)=\int\varphi\,d\alpha_\theta$ whenever the absolute integral is finite. Apply the <Mecke formula for a Poisson random measure> to a selected jump $u$ and the remaining total $t$, whose density is $t^{\theta-1}e^{-t}/\Gamma(\theta)$. Changing variables to $s=u+t$ and $x=u/s$ factors out a <Gamma function> and gives the displayed measure. Weighting it by $x$ gives a <probability> density $\theta(1-x)^{\theta-1}$, the <Beta distribution> of a size-biased component.

Alternately routing a rate-$r$ <Poisson process> produces non-Poisson streams with common interarrival law Erlang$(2,r)$. The odd-numbered stream has an initial exponential delay, while the even-numbered stream is an ordinary renewal process. Their equilibrium delay survivor is $e^{-rt}(1+rt/2)$. The probability of no odd-stream point in $(s,s+t]$ is $e^{-rt}[1+rt(1-e^{-2rs})/2]$; for the even stream replace the minus by a plus. Conditioning on the parity of $N(s)$ proves these formulas.

= Uniform-sum crossing time below one
{parent=Renewal process}
{title2=$\mathbb E N(x)=e^x\quad(0\leq x\leq1)$}

For independent <uniform distribution> increments on $(0,1)$ and $0\leq x\leq1$, let $N(x)$ be the first index at which their partial sum exceeds $x$. <First-step analysis> gives $M(x)=\mathbb E N(x)=1+\int_0^xM(s)ds$, hence $M=e^x$. Equivalently, $\mathbb P(N(x)>n)=x^n/n!$ is a simplex-volume probability and the <tail-sum formula> sums to $e^x$. The strict inequality gives $N(0)=1$ almost surely.

= Renewal measure
{parent=Renewal process}
{title2=$U=\sum_{j\geq0}K^{*j}$}

For a positive interarrival law $K$, the renewal measure $U=\sum_{j\geq0}K^{*j}$ counts the expected number of renewal epochs in a set, including the epoch at zero through $K^{*0}=\delta_0$. Its cumulative function is one plus the usual <renewal function>. Convolution against $U$ solves a <renewal equation>.

= Key renewal theorem
{parent=Renewal process}
{title2=$g*U(t)\longrightarrow(\mathbb ET)^{-1}\int_0^\infty g$}

For positive nonarithmetic interarrival times with law $K$ and mean $m\in(0,\infty]$, let $U=\sum_{j\geq0}K^{*j}$ be their <renewal measure>. If $g$ is <directly Riemann integrable>, then $\int_{[0,t]}g(t-x)U(dx)\to m^{-1}\int_0^\infty g(x)\,dx$, with $1/\infty=0$. Thus a locally bounded solution of $z=g+z*K$ has this limit through its <renewal representation>. Arithmetic laws require the corresponding lattice version.

= Renewal equation
{parent=Renewal process}
{title2=$u_n=\sum_{k=1}^n f_k u_{n-k},\ u_0=1$}

A renewal equation decomposes a process at its first renewal. For returns of a <Markov chain> to a fixed state, $f_k$ is the first-return probability and $u_n$ the probability of being at that state at time $n$. Their <probability generating functions> satisfy $U=1+FU$.

= Renewal representation
{parent=Renewal equation}
{title2=$z=g*U$}

For positive interarrivals with law $K$, the unique locally bounded solution of $z=g+z*K$ is $z=g*U=\sum_{j\geq0}g*K^{*j}$, where $U$ is the <renewal measure>. Iteration proves the identity: the residual is bounded on each compact interval by a constant times the probability that the sum of many positive interarrivals stays in that interval, which tends to zero.

= Defective renewal equation
{parent=Renewal equation}
{title2=$z=g+z*Q,\quad Q([0,\infty))<1$}

A defective renewal equation is $z(u)=g(u)+\int_{[0,u]}z(u-x)Q(dx)$ where the nonnegative kernel measure has total mass less than one. Its <renewal representation> is a convergent sum of convolutions. <Exponential tilting> can turn the kernel into a probability measure, enabling the <key renewal theorem>.

= Exponential tilt of the ruin renewal kernel
{parent=Defective renewal equation}
{title2=$k_R(x)=(\lambda/c)e^{Rx}\overline F(x)$}

The <ultimate ruin probability> satisfies a tail-forced <defective renewal equation>. Multiplying it by $e^{Ru}$ at an <adjustment coefficient> turns its kernel into the probability density $k_R$. Normalization is exactly $\lambda(M(R)-1)=cR$. The density is absolutely continuous even when the original claim law has atoms; thus it has a <nonarithmetic distribution>.

= Exponential moment of a renewal count
{parent=Renewal process}
{title2=$\mathbb E e^{\theta N(t)}$}

For nonnegative finite <independent and identically distributed random variables> as inter-arrivals with positive probability of exceeding $\varepsilon>0$, write $p=\mathbb P(X_1\geq\varepsilon)>0$. The count tail is bounded by $\mathbb P(\operatorname{Bin}(n,p)\leq\lfloor t/\varepsilon\rfloor)$, which has geometric decay times a polynomial in $n$. Hence $\mathbb E e^{\theta N(t)}<\infty$ for every fixed finite $t$ and sufficiently small $\theta>0$.

= Renewal function
{title2=$m(t)$}
{parent=Renewal process}

The renewal function is the expected number of renewals by time $t$:
$$
m(t)=\mathbb E N_t.
$$

= Elementary renewal theorem
{parent=Renewal function}

For strictly positive independent identically distributed interarrival times of finite mean $\mu$,
$$
\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb EN_t}{t}=\frac1\mu.
$$

= Renewal-reward process
{parent=Renewal process}

A renewal-reward process attaches an integrable reward $R_n$ to each i.i.d. renewal cycle of length $\xi_n$. Its cumulative reward sums completed-cycle rewards and may include reward accrued in the current unfinished cycle.

= Renewal-reward theorem
{parent=Renewal-reward process}
{wiki}

For independent identically distributed regenerative cycles with $0<\mathbb E\xi_1<\infty$ and $\mathbb E|R_1|<\infty$, the long-run reward rate is
$$
\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{Z(t)}t
=\frac{\mathbb ER_1}{\mathbb E\xi_1}
$$
almost surely, provided the unfinished-cycle reward is negligible after division by $t$.

= Long-run availability under periodic inspection
{parent=Renewal-reward theorem}

If a repaired machine has independent exponential lifetimes of rate $\lambda$ and is inspected and repaired every $m$ time units after each repair, then its cycle length is $m\lceil X/m\rceil$ and its working reward is $X$. Its long-run working proportion is
$$
\frac{1-e^{-\lambda m}}{\lambda m}.
$$

= Renewal counting law of large numbers
{parent=Renewal process}

If $0<\mathbb E\xi_1=1/\lambda<\infty$, then the strong law $S_n/n\to1/\lambda$ and the inequalities $S_{N_t}\leq t<S_{N_t+1}$ imply $N_t/t\to\lambda$ almost surely.

= Renewal interval containing a fixed time
{parent=Renewal process}

The renewal interval containing $t$ has length
$$
L(t)=S_{N_t+1}-S_{N_t}=\xi_{N_t+1}.
$$
Selection by a fixed observation time favours longer interarrival times.

= Size-biased distribution
{parent=Renewal interval containing a fixed time}
{wiki}

For a nonnegative random variable $X$ with $0<\mathbb EX<\infty$, its size-biased version $\widehat X$ is defined by
$$
\mathbb E h(\widehat X)=\frac{\mathbb E[Xh(X)]}{\mathbb EX}
$$
for bounded measurable $h$. If $X$ has density $f$, then $\widehat X$ has density $xf(x)/\mathbb EX$.

= Exponential renewal interval limit
{parent=Renewal interval containing a fixed time}

For exponential interarrival times of rate $\lambda$, the age and residual life at time $t$ converge jointly to independent rate-$\lambda$ exponential variables. Their sum therefore converges to the gamma distribution with density $\lambda^2xe^{-\lambda x}$, the size-biased exponential law.

= Stochastic length bias of a renewal interval
{parent=Renewal interval containing a fixed time}

At every deterministic $t>0$, the containing interval stochastically dominates an unselected interarrival time:
$$
\mathbb P(L(t)\geq x)\geq\mathbb P(\xi_1\geq x),\qquad x>0.
$$
Conditioning on the first interarrival gives a renewal equation; subtracting the constant tail probability leaves a renewal equation with a nonnegative forcing term.

= Residual lifetime process
{parent=Renewal process}
{wiki}

The residual lifetime is the time remaining until the next renewal.

= Renewal excess limit theorem
{parent=Residual lifetime process}

For non-arithmetic inter-renewal times with finite positive mean $\mu$, the excess $E(t)=S_{N(t)+1}-t$ converges in distribution to $E_\infty$ with
$$
\mathbb P(E_\infty>x)
=\frac1\mu\int_x^\infty\mathbb P(\xi_1>y)\,dy.
$$

= Exponential first interarrival in a renewal superposition containing a Poisson process
{parent=Renewal excess limit theorem}

If an independent superposition of a Poisson process and a non-arithmetic finite-mean renewal process is itself a renewal process, then its first interarrival is exponential. Equating the limiting excess law computed as a renewal excess with the minimum of the Poisson and second-process excesses yields an integral equation whose survival-function solutions are exponential.

= Residual lifetime Markov chain
{parent=Residual lifetime process}
{wiki}

With bounded integer lifetimes, residual lifetime decreases deterministically and resets randomly at zero.

= Equilibrium residual-life distribution
{parent=Residual lifetime process}
{wiki}

The invariant residual-life mass at i is proportional to the probability that a lifetime exceeds i.

= Equilibrium delay of an Erlang renewal process
{parent=Equilibrium residual-life distribution}

For inter-renewal times with an <Erlang distribution> of integer shape $r$ and rate $\lambda$, the mean is $r/\lambda$ and survival function is $e^{-\lambda t}\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}(\lambda t)^j/j!$. The equilibrium first-delay density is survival divided by the mean,
$$
f_e(t)=\frac\lambda r e^{-\lambda t}\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\frac{(\lambda t)^j}{j!},\qquad t>0.
$$
This is the equal mixture of Erlang distributions of shapes $1,\ldots,r$. It can be constructed by observing a Poisson clock with uniformly randomized phase modulo $r$, whose every $r$th event is a renewal.

= Law of total variance
{parent=Probability theory}
{wiki}

Variance equals the variance of a conditional mean plus the mean conditional variance.

= Laplace transforms of nonnegative random variables
{synonym}
{c}

For a nonnegative <random variable> $X$, its Laplace transform is

\psi_X(s)=\mathbb E[e^{-sX}],
\qquad s\geq0.
$$

= Second-moment expansion of a Laplace transform
{parent=Laplace transform of a nonnegative random variable}
{title2=$\mathbb Ee^{-\lambda X}=1-\lambda\mathbb EX+\lambda^2\mathbb EX^2/2+o(\lambda^2)$}

For nonnegative $X$ with finite <second moment>, <differentiation> under the <expectation> gives $\phi'(\lambda)=-\mathbb E[Xe^{-\lambda X}]$ for $\lambda>0$. The exponential Taylor remainder divided by $\lambda^2$ tends pointwise to zero and is bounded in absolute value by $X^2/2$. The <dominated convergence theorem> therefore proves the displayed expansion without requiring a third moment.

= Uniqueness theorem for Laplace transforms of nonnegative random variables
{parent=Laplace transform of a nonnegative random variable}

The <Laplace transform of a nonnegative random variable> determines its <probability law>. Indeed, $U=e^{-X}$ takes values in $(0,1]$, and $\mathbb E[U^n]=\psi_X(n)$. Thus the transform determines every moment of $U$; <moment determinacy on a compact interval> determines the law of $U$, and the injective inverse $X=-\log U$ determines the law of $X$.

= Characteristic function
{title2=$\varphi_X(t)$}
{parent=Probability theory}
{wiki=Characteristic_function_(probability_theory)}

The characteristic function of a real <random variable> $X$ is
$$
\varphi_X(t)=\mathbb E[e^{itX}].
$$
It determines the <probability distribution> of $X$.

= Characteristic-function derivative under an absolute first moment
{parent=Characteristic function}
{title2=$\phi\prime(0)=i\mathbb E X$}

If $\mathbb E|X|<\infty$, the <characteristic function> $\phi(u)=\mathbb E e^{iuX}$ satisfies $\phi'(0)=i\mathbb E X$. The bound $|(e^{iuX}-1)/u|\leq|X|$ and the <dominated convergence theorem> justify differentiation at zero. For mean zero, $\psi(u)=|1-\phi(u)|/|u|$ extends continuously by $\psi(0)=0$, and $|1-\phi(u)^n|\leq n|u|\psi(u)$ by a finite <geometric series>. This supplies a direct characteristic-function proof of the <weak law of large numbers>.

= Characteristic-function tail bound
{parent=Characteristic function}
{title2=$\mu(|y|\ge\lambda)\le\frac{\lambda}{1-\sin1}\int_0^{1/\lambda}(1-\operatorname{Re}\phi(u))du$}

Average $1-\cos(uy)$ over $0\le u\le1/\lambda$. Multiplication by $\lambda$ gives $1-\sin(y/\lambda)/(y/\lambda)$, with value zero at $y=0$. This is at least $1-\sin1$ when $|y|\ge\lambda$. <Tonelli's theorem> then gives the displayed bound for a <probability measure> with <characteristic function> $\phi$. The bound converts control of the <characteristic function> near zero into <tightness of probability measures> and is useful in proving the <Lévy continuity theorem>.

= Conditional characteristic function
{parent=Characteristic function}
{title2=$\mathbb E[e^{i\theta Y}\mid\mathcal G]$}

For a real <random variable> $Y$ and a sigma-algebra $\mathcal G$, the conditional characteristic function is $\mathbb E[e^{i\theta Y}\mid\mathcal G]$. If it equals the nonrandom <characteristic function> of a probability law for every $\theta$, then $Y$ has that law and is independent of $\mathcal G$. To verify the independence assertion, multiply the identity by any bounded $\mathcal G$-measurable test variable and use the <uniqueness theorem for characteristic functions> on the resulting finite measures. Equality for rational $\theta$ extends by continuity, so simultaneous null sets can be arranged when needed.

= Empirical characteristic function
{parent=Characteristic function}
{wiki}
{title2=$\varphi_n(u)$}

For a sample $X_1,\ldots,X_n$, $\varphi_n(u)=n^{-1}\sum_j e^{iuX_j}$ is the <characteristic function> of the sample's <empirical measure>. For <independent and identically distributed random variables>, $\mathbb E\varphi_n(u)=\varphi(u)$ and $\operatorname{Var}_{\mathbb C}\varphi_n(u)=(1-|\varphi(u)|^2)/n$, without any moment condition on the observations.

= Empirical characteristic process
{parent=Empirical characteristic function}
{title2=$\mathcal C_n(u)$}

The centered process $\mathcal C_n(u)=\sqrt n(\varphi_n(u)-\varphi(u))$ describes fluctuations of the <empirical characteristic function>. At a fixed frequency, its real and imaginary parts satisfy a <multivariate central limit theorem>. A process-level limit requires further control over the frequency index.

= Continuity of a characteristic function
{parent=Characteristic function}

Every characteristic function is uniformly continuous and satisfies $\varphi(0)=1$ and $|\varphi(t)|\leq1$. Continuity follows from the <dominated convergence theorem> applied to $e^{itX}$.

= Unit modulus of a characteristic function
{parent=Characteristic function}

If $|\varphi_X(u)|=1$, then $e^{iuX}$ is almost surely constant. Indeed,
$$
\mathbb E\left[\left|e^{iuX}-\mathbb E[e^{iuX}]\right|^2\right]
=1-|\varphi_X(u)|^2=0.
$$
Hence $uX\in\theta+2\pi\mathbb Z$ almost surely for some real $\theta$.

= Irrational unit-modulus frequencies force a point mass
{parent=Unit modulus of a characteristic function}

If $u/v$ is irrational and $|\varphi_X(u)|=|\varphi_X(v)|=1$, then $X$ is almost surely constant. For independent copies $X,X'$, both $u(X-X')$ and $v(X-X')$ lie in $2\pi\mathbb Z$ almost surely; irrationality makes the two corresponding lattices intersect only at zero, so $X=X'$ almost surely. Two independent identically distributed variables can agree almost surely only when their common law is a point mass.

= Characteristic function of a random vector
{title2=$\varphi_X(u)=\mathbb E[e^{i\langle u,X\rangle}]$}
{parent=Characteristic function}

For an $\mathbb R^d$-valued random vector, the characteristic function is $\varphi_X(u)=\mathbb E[e^{i\langle u,X\rangle}]$. It determines the joint distribution and satisfies $\varphi_{AX}(u)=\varphi_X(A^Tu)$ for every deterministic matrix $A$.

= Characteristic function of an isotropic Gaussian vector
{title2=$\exp(-\sigma^2\lVert u\rVert^2/2)$}
{parent=Characteristic function of a random vector}

If $X\sim N(0,\sigma^2I_d)$, then
$$
\varphi_X(u)=\exp\left(-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\lVert u\rVert^2\right).
$$

= Orthogonally invariant random vector
{parent=Characteristic function of a random vector}

A random vector $X\in\mathbb R^d$ is orthogonally invariant when $AX$ has the same distribution as $X$ for every $A\in O(d)$. Its characteristic function is radial and real: $\varphi_X(u)=f(\lVert u\rVert^2)$ for a continuous function $f:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$.

= Maxwell characterization of the normal distribution
{c}
{parent=Orthogonally invariant random vector}

If an orthogonally invariant random vector in dimension at least two has independent coordinates, then it is centered isotropic Gaussian, possibly degenerate at zero. Independence and rotational invariance give
$$
f(r+s)=f(r)f(s),
$$
while continuity and $f(0)=1$ imply $f(r)=e^{-\alpha r}$ for some $\alpha\geq0$. Thus $X\sim N(0,2\alpha I_d)$.

= Uniqueness theorem for characteristic functions
{parent=Characteristic function}

Two finite Borel measures on $\mathbb R$ with the same characteristic function are equal. One proof convolves both measures with centered Gaussians, applies Fourier inversion to the smooth densities, and then lets the Gaussian variance tend to zero.

= Fourier-cutoff recovery of expectations from a characteristic function
{parent=Uniqueness theorem for characteristic functions}

Let $f$ be compactly supported and continuous, let $g,\widehat g\in L^1(\mathbb R)$, and use $\widehat g(t)=\int g(x)e^{itx}\,dx$. If $g(0)\ne0$, then
$$
\mathbb E[f(Z)]
=\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow0}
\frac1{2\pi g(0)}
\int_{\mathbb R}g(\varepsilon s)\widehat f(-s)\varphi_Z(s)\,ds.
$$
This follows by <Fubini's theorem>, a change of variables, <Fourier inversion theorem>, and <dominated convergence theorem>. It gives a direct proof of the <uniqueness theorem for characteristic functions> because the right side depends only on $\varphi_Z$.

= Characteristic function of a sum of independent variables
{parent=Characteristic function}

Independence makes the characteristic function of a sum equal the product of the individual characteristic functions.

= Lévy continuity theorem
{c}
{parent=Characteristic function}
{wiki}

If the <characteristic functions> $\varphi_n$ of <probability distributions> $\mu_n$ converge pointwise to a function $\varphi$ that is continuous at zero, then $\varphi$ is a characteristic function and $\mu_n$ <convergence in distribution>[converges in distribution] to its distribution. Conversely, convergence in distribution implies pointwise convergence of the characteristic functions.

= Characteristic-function tightness bound
{parent=Lévy continuity theorem}
{title2=$\mu(|x|\geq2/h)\leq h^{-1}\int_{-h}^h(1-\operatorname{Re}\varphi(t))\,dt$}

For a <probability measure> on the real line with <characteristic function> $\varphi$, averaging $1-\cos(tx)$ over $[-h,h]$ gives $1-\sin(hx)/(hx)$. This is at least $1/2$ when $|x|\geq2/h$. Integrating gives the displayed bound. Consequently pointwise convergence of <characteristic functions> to a function continuous at zero implies <tightness of probability measures>, by the <dominated convergence theorem> on $[-h,h]$ and a separate tail bound for the finitely many initial measures.

= Weak law from a characteristic-function expansion
{parent=Lévy continuity theorem}

If <independent and identically distributed random variables> have <characteristic function> $\varphi(t)=1+iat+o(|t|)$ at zero, then their <sample mean> has <convergence in distribution> to $a$: its <characteristic function> is $\varphi(t/n)^n\to e^{iat}$. This is also <convergence in probability> by <convergence in distribution to a constant implies convergence in probability>.

= Variance normalization

Dividing a centered sum by the square root of its total variance gives unit variance and is the natural central-limit scaling.

= Cox--Ross--Rubinstein model
{c}
{parent=Probability theory}

In the Cox--Ross--Rubinstein model, a stock changes at each discrete time by an up factor $U$ or a down factor $D$, while the money-market account changes by a deterministic factor $R$. After discounting by the money-market account, the factors are $u=U/R$ and $d=D/R$. The model is arbitrage-free exactly when $D<R<U$, equivalently $d<1<u$.

= Cox-Ross-Rubinstein diffusion approximation
{c}
{parent=Cox--Ross--Rubinstein model}
{title2=$u=e^{\sigma\sqrt h},\quad d=e^{-\sigma\sqrt h},\quad B_{k+1}/B_k=e^{\rho h}$}

= Binomial diffusion approximation
{synonym}

For mesh $h$, the risk-neutral up probability is $(e^{\rho h}-d)/(u-d)$. The log-price increment has mean $(\rho-\sigma^2/2)h+O(h^2)$ and variance $\sigma^2h+O(h^2)$. The <Donsker invariance principle> gives convergence of the log-price process to Brownian motion with this drift, hence of prices to the <Black-Scholes model>. Payoff-price convergence additionally uses continuity at almost every limiting path and uniform integrability for unbounded payoffs.

= Risk-neutral probability in the Cox--Ross--Rubinstein model
{c}
{parent=Cox--Ross--Rubinstein model}

The unique equivalent martingale measure in the one-stock Cox--Ross--Rubinstein model assigns each up move probability
$$
q=\frac{R-D}{U-D}=\frac{1-d}{u-d}
$$
and each down move probability $1-q$. Under this measure the discounted stock is a <martingale>.

= Risk-neutral valuation in the Cox--Ross--Rubinstein model
{c}
{parent=Risk-neutral probability in the Cox--Ross--Rubinstein model}

For a discounted claim $H$, the arbitrage-free value process is $V_t=\mathbb E_Q[H\mid\mathcal F_t]$. A terminal payoff $h(S_T)$ has value
$$
V_t=\sum_{k=0}^{T-t}\binom{T-t}{k}q^k(1-q)^{T-t-k}
h(S_tu^kd^{T-t-k}).
$$

= Running-maximum state reduction in the Cox--Ross--Rubinstein model
{c}
{parent=Risk-neutral valuation in the Cox--Ross--Rubinstein model}

For a payoff $h(S_T,M_T)$ with $M_t=\max_{s\leq t}S_s$, the pair $(S_t,M_t)$ is a sufficient Markov state for valuation. If an independent restarted stock path has initial value $y$, terminal value $z$, and running maximum $w$, then the continued payoff is
$$
g(x,m,y,z,w)=h\left(\frac{xz}{y},\max\left\{m,\frac{xw}{y}\right\}\right).
$$

Thinning removes points from a <point process> according to a retention rule. Independent retention with probability $p(x)$ changes the intensity measure to $p(x)\mu(dx)$. For a <Poisson point process>, the retained and removed point processes are independent Poisson processes.
