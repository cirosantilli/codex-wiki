= Interpolation
{wiki}
{title2=$g(x_i)=y_i$}

Interpolation constructs a <function> agreeing exactly with prescribed values at sample points. The resulting function is an <interpolant>. Choosing a family determines how missing values are filled: <polynomial interpolation> selects a <polynomial>, while <linear interpolation> joins adjacent samples by straight segments. The <McShane extension theorem> instead permits any <Lipschitz function> with a specified bound and provides envelopes controlling all allowed choices.

= Bilinear interpolation
{parent=Interpolation}
{wiki}

On a rectangle, <bilinear interpolation> is linear in each coordinate separately and matches the four corner values. With normalized coordinates $s,t\in[0,1]$, it is $(1-s)(1-t)u_{00}+s(1-t)u_{10}+(1-s)tu_{01}+stu_{11}$. A <multigrid method> can use it to prolong a coarse correction to finer-grid nodes. It is not generally an affine function of the pair $(s,t)$, since it can contain a mixed $st$ term.

= Finite measurement interpolation freedom
{parent=Interpolation}
{title2=$g(x)=f(x)+\lambda\prod_i(x-x_i)$}

If $f(x_i)=y_i$ at finitely many distinct measured inputs, then $g(x)=f(x)+\lambda\prod_i(x-x_i)$ agrees at every measured point. Away from the roots it can disagree. This proves that the finite record alone does not determine an unrestricted function; it does not imply that all such functions satisfy a physical theory's independent constraints.

= Rational interpolation linearization
{parent=Interpolation}
{title2=$q(x_i)=f_ir(x_i)$}

For <rational interpolation> with $\deg q\leq m$ and a monic denominator $r(x)=x^\ell-\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}b_jx^j$, multiplying through gives the <linear system>
$$
\sum_{j=0}^{m}a_jx_i^j+f_i\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1}b_jx_i^j=f_ix_i^\ell.
$$
These equations are equivalent to the fraction conditions only when all $r(x_i)$ are nonzero. If $f_i=x_i^{m+1}$ and $m+\ell=n$, the coefficient <matrix> is the <Vandermonde matrix> with rows $(1,x_i,\ldots,x_i^n)$, so distinct <interpolation nodes> give a unique coefficient solution. More constructively, divide $W(x)=\prod_{i=0}^n(x-x_i)$ by $x^{m+1}$ as $W=x^{m+1}R+S$, $\deg S\leq m$. Then $r=R$, $q=-S$, since $x^{m+1}r-q$ is the unique monic degree-$n+1$ <polynomial> vanishing at all the <interpolation nodes>. The quotient may vanish at a node, so unique solvability of this <linear system> does not prove existence of a <rational interpolant>.

= Rational interpolation
{parent=Interpolation}
{title2=$q(x_i)/r(x_i)=f_i$}

Rational interpolation seeks a <rational function> $q/r$ with prescribed bounds on the <polynomial degrees> of its numerator and denominator and prescribed values at distinct <interpolation nodes>. A <rational interpolant> must have a well-defined value at each <interpolation node>. With a specified numerator-denominator representation, this requires $r(x_i)\ne0$ for every $i$. A normalization such as a <monic polynomial> denominator removes the common scaling freedom but does not guarantee existence or uniqueness. Multiplying by a zero denominator can create spurious solutions to the <rational interpolation linearization>.

= Endpoint-constrained rational approximation to a power
{parent=Rational interpolation}
{title2=$r(x)=x/[k+(1-k)x]$}

For $0<k\leq1$, impose $r(0)=0$, $r(1)=1$, and $r'(1)=k$ within the rational family $ax/(1+bx)$. Matching the first two Taylor coefficients at $x=1$ gives the displayed formula. It differs from the standard degree-$(1,1)$ <Padé approximant> to $x^k$ about one, which is $[(1-k)+(1+k)x]/[(1+k)+(1-k)x]$ and matches one further Taylor coefficient. At $k=0$, the constrained expression is interpreted by its removable constant limit.

= Extrapolation
{parent=Interpolation}

Estimating values beyond the range of the supplied sample points. For two distinct points $P,Q$, the <affine combination> $(1-t)P+tQ$ lies beyond an endpoint of their <line segment> when $t<0$ or $t>1$. The construction specifies an extension of the chosen model; it does not show that an unknown underlying <function> follows that extension.

= Interpolant
{parent=Interpolation}
{title2=$g(x_i)=y_i$}

An interpolant is a <function> satisfying the prescribed sample conditions $g(x_i)=y_i$. It agrees with the data exactly, but need not agree with an unknown underlying function between samples. The <polynomial interpolation> example shows that a family or a <polynomial degree> restriction can supply uniqueness; the <McShane extension theorem> shows a useful nonunique alternative.

= Rational interpolant
{parent=Interpolant}
{title2=$p=q/r$}

A rational interpolant is a <rational function> agreeing with given data at every <interpolation node>. If the representation is required to have no zero denominator at those nodes, this condition is part of the problem, in addition to the degree restrictions. Even when removable common factors are cancelled before evaluation, the resulting <rational function> must still take the prescribed values. For example, at the data $(0,0),(1,1)$ there is no interpolant $a/(x-b)$: the first value forces $a=0$ when its denominator is nonzero, and then the second value cannot be one. The cleared equations nevertheless have the unique coefficient solution $a=0,b=1$.

= Polynomial interpolation
{parent=Interpolation}
{wiki}
{title2=$p_n(x_i)=y_i,\quad\deg p_n\leq n$}

<Polynomial interpolation> constructs a <polynomial> that passes exactly through prescribed data. Given $n+1$ distinct <interpolation nodes> $x_0,\ldots,x_n$ and values $y_0,\ldots,y_n$, there is exactly one <interpolation polynomial> $p_n$ of <polynomial degree> at most $n$ with $p_n(x_i)=y_i$ for every $i$. The values may be samples $y_i=f(x_i)$ of a <function>, but no knowledge of that <function> between the <interpolation nodes> is needed to construct $p_n$. The <Lagrange polynomial> and <Newton polynomial> are two representations of this same answer.https://fncbook.com/polynomial/[Toby A. Driscoll and Richard J. Braun, Fundamentals of Numerical Computation, §9.1, Theorems 9.1.1–9.1.2]{ref}

For example, the three data points $(0,1),(1,2),(2,5)$ give the <quadratic polynomial>
$$
p_2(x)=x^2+1,\qquad p_2(0)=1,\quad p_2(1)=2,\quad p_2(2)=5.
$$
Thus <polynomial interpolation> supplies $p_2(1/2)=5/4$ as an intermediate value. Whether this approximates an unknown underlying <function> well is a separate question about <polynomial interpolation error>.

Both hypotheses in the uniqueness statement matter. The <Lagrange cardinal polynomials> prove existence constructively. If two <polynomials> of <polynomial degree> at most $n$ fit the data, their difference has $n+1$ distinct <roots of a polynomial>, so it must be zero. Repeated <interpolation nodes> with conflicting values have no solution, and repeated equal values provide no additional independent condition; prescribed <derivatives> instead lead to <Hermite interpolation>. Without the <polynomial degree> restriction there are infinitely many solutions:
$$
p(x)=p_n(x)+q(x)\prod_{i=0}^n(x-x_i),
$$
where $q$ is any <polynomial>. By the <factor theorem>, this describes every <polynomial> fitting the same data. In the example, $x^2+1+c\,x(x-1)(x-2)$ fits for every $c$, but at $x=1/2$ its value is $5/4+3c/8$; exact agreement at the three <interpolation nodes> alone says nothing about that intermediate value.

The usefulness of <polynomial interpolation> is that data become an explicit <function> whose values, <derivatives>, and <integrals> can be calculated. Differentiating or integrating the <Lagrange cardinal polynomials> produces <differentiation weights from nodal evaluations> or a <quadrature rule>. Exact passage through observations is the defining constraint. In <polynomial regression>, <linear least squares> instead chooses a <polynomial degree> and minimizes a sum of squared residuals, generally without passing through every observation. Interpolating noisy values reproduces their errors at the <interpolation nodes> and can amplify them between <interpolation nodes>; <polynomial interpolation> does not itself smooth measurement noise. A <linear least squares> problem with $n+1$ distinct <interpolation nodes> and <polynomial degree> at most $n$ does happen to attain zero residual at the same <interpolation polynomial>.

= Symmetric node pairing bound for interpolation on the unit interval
{parent=Polynomial interpolation}
{title2=$\sup_{0\le x\le1}\left|\prod_j(x-x_j)\right|\le2^{-m}$}

Suppose $m$ interpolation nodes in $[0,1]$ occur in pairs $a,1-a$, with the middle node $1/2$ if $m$ is odd. For each pair,
$$
|(x-a)(x-(1-a))|=|(x-1/2)^2-(a-1/2)^2|\le1/4,
$$
because both squared quantities lie between zero and one quarter. An unpaired middle factor has magnitude at most one half. Multiplication gives the displayed bound on the nodal <polynomial>.

The <polynomial interpolation> remainder therefore satisfies $\|f-p_{m-1}\|_\infty\le\|f^{(m)}\|_\infty/[2^m m!]$ for $f\in C^m[0,1]$. For $f(x)=\sin2x$, the <derivative> bound $\|f^{(m)}\|_\infty\le2^m$ gives the particularly simple error bound $1/m!$. This proves <uniform convergence> for these symmetric-node interpolants of that function. It is not a general convergence theorem for arbitrary <continuous> interpolation data.

= Interpolation property of weighted least-squares polynomial approximation
{parent=Polynomial interpolation}
{title2=$\#\{x:f(x)=p_n(x)\}\geq n+1$}

Let $f$ be a real <continuous function> on a nondegenerate compact interval and let $p_n$ be its <best approximation> among <polynomials> of <polynomial degree> at most $n$ for a positive integrable <weighted inner product>. Then $f-p_n$ has at least $n+1$ distinct zeros. Indeed, if it had at most $n$, multiply it by the <polynomial> with simple zeros at precisely its interior sign changes. The product has constant nonzero sign away from finitely many zeros, so its weighted integral is nonzero, contradicting <orthogonality> of $f-p_n$ to all <polynomials> of <polynomial degree> at most $n$.

= Newton forward interpolation
{parent=Polynomial interpolation}
{c}

For equally spaced nodes $x_0,x_0+h,\ldots,x_0+dh$, write $t=(x-x_0)/h$. The interpolating <polynomial> is $P(x)=\sum_{j=0}^d\binom tj\Delta_h^j f(x_0)$. A table of <forward differences> supplies its coefficients; for a <polynomial> of degree at most $d$, the formula reproduces the function exactly.

= Interpolation polynomial
{parent=Polynomial interpolation}
{title2=$p(x_i)=y_i$}

An interpolation polynomial for distinct nodes $x_0,\ldots,x_n$ and prescribed values $y_0,\ldots,y_n$ is a <polynomial> $p$ satisfying $p(x_i)=y_i$. There is a unique such <polynomial> of degree at most $n$: the <Lagrange interpolation polynomial> constructs it explicitly, and uniqueness follows because a nonzero degree-at-most-$n$ difference cannot have $n+1$ distinct roots. <Newton interpolation polynomials> express the same object using <divided differences>.

= Interpolation nodes from best uniform approximation
{parent=Polynomial interpolation}
{title2=$\ell_n(f)=p_n^*$}

For a real <continuous function> on a compact interval, choose its <best uniform approximation> $p_n^*$ of <polynomial degree> at most $n$. If its error is nonzero, the <Chebyshev alternation theorem> supplies $n+2$ ordered alternating extrema. The <intermediate value theorem> gives a zero of $f-p_n^*$ in each intervening interval. At these $n+1$ distinct points, the <polynomial interpolant> is exactly $p_n^*$. If the best error is zero, arbitrary distinct nodes suffice. The <Weierstrass approximation theorem> shows that these function-dependent interpolants converge uniformly. The nodes depend on the target function; the conclusion does not provide one universal node sequence for every continuous target.

= Interpolation node
{parent=Polynomial interpolation}
{title2=$x_i$}

An <interpolation node> is an argument value $x_i$ at which an <interpolation polynomial> must take a prescribed value $y_i$. The <interpolation nodes> specify where the constraints apply; the values specify how high the graph must pass there. In the three-point example, the <interpolation nodes> are $0,1,2$, while the prescribed values are $1,2,5$. Changing the <interpolation nodes> changes the <Lagrange cardinal polynomials> and the <polynomial interpolation error>, even if their number stays fixed.

= Chebyshev-Lobatto node
{c}
{parent=Interpolation node}
{title2=$x_k=\cos(k\pi/n),\quad k=0,\ldots,n$}

= Chebyshev extreme point
{c}
{synonym}

For $n\geq1$, the $n+1$ <Chebyshev-Lobatto nodes> are the <extrema> of the <Chebyshev polynomial> $T_n$ on $[-1,1]$, including both endpoints: $T_n(x_k)=(-1)^k$. Imagine $n+1$ equally spaced angles on a <semicircle> and project their points onto the horizontal axis. The <interpolation nodes> crowd near $-1$ and $1$ because equal angular steps have smaller horizontal projections there. Reversing the order gives the same set. These are distinct from the <roots of a polynomial> $\cos((2k+1)\pi/(2n+2))$ of $T_{n+1}$, which omit the endpoints. Driscoll and Braun call this endpoint-including family Chebyshev points of the second kind or Chebyshev extreme points.https://fncbook.com/python/stability-1/[Driscoll and Braun, Fundamentals of Numerical Computation, §9.3.2, equation 9.3.2]{ref}

On an interval $[a,b]$, use $x_k=(a+b)/2+(b-a)\cos(k\pi/n)/2$. The <Runge phenomenon> example compares <polynomial interpolation> at eleven <Chebyshev-Lobatto nodes> with eleven equally spaced <interpolation nodes>. The distinction matters whenever sampling locations are a choice rather than fixed observations.

= Runge phenomenon
{c}
{synonym}

The <Runge phenomenon> is the failure of increasing-degree <polynomial interpolation> at equally spaced <interpolation nodes> to converge to some smooth target <functions>, with large oscillations near the ends of the interval. It is a property of the approximation problem, and can occur even with exact arithmetic. A numerically stable evaluation method cannot remove it. Endpoint clustering, as in <Chebyshev-Lobatto nodes>, changes the approximation problem and can greatly improve it.https://fncbook.com/python/stability-1/[Driscoll and Braun, Fundamentals of Numerical Computation, §9.3.1–9.3.2]{ref}

Take $f(x)=1/(1+25x^2)$ on $[-1,1]$ and <polynomial degree> at most $10$. Use either $x_k=-1+k/5$ or $x_k=\cos(k\pi/10)$ for $k=0,\ldots,10$. Each <interpolation polynomial> passes through all eleven of its own samples, yet the first has severe endpoint oscillations and the second stays much closer to the target <function>. Evaluating both by the <barycentric interpolation formula> on $20{,}001$ equally spaced points, including the endpoints, gives sampled maximum absolute errors of approximately $1.915659$ and $0.132197$, respectively: about a $14.49$-fold reduction. These are maxima over the stated grid, not certified maxima over the continuous interval. The generator separately checks exact reproduction of all stored nodal values.

\Image[interpolation-node-placement.png]
{title=Degree-10 interpolation of the Runge function at equally spaced and Chebyshev-Lobatto nodes, with absolute errors}

The <polynomial interpolation error> formula explains why <interpolation nodes> enter the analysis. If $f$ has $n+1$ continuous <derivatives> on an interval containing $x$ and all the <interpolation nodes>, then for a suitable $\xi_x$ in that interval,
$$
f(x)-p_n(x)=\frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi_x)}{(n+1)!}\prod_{i=0}^n(x-x_i).
$$
At an <interpolation node> the error is zero directly. Away from the <interpolation nodes>, both the product and the high-order <derivative> matter. A short proof applies <Rolle's theorem> repeatedly to $f(t)-p_n(t)-[f(x)-p_n(x)]\prod_i(t-x_i)/\prod_i(x-x_i)$, which vanishes at $n+2$ distinct points.https://fncbook.com/polynomial/[Driscoll and Braun, Fundamentals of Numerical Computation, §9.1.2, Theorem 9.1.3]{ref}

The successful comparison here does not assert that <Chebyshev-Lobatto nodes> solve every approximation problem for every <function>. The <derivatives>, the <polynomial degree>, and measurement errors still matter. The <Lebesgue constant of interpolation> measures worst-case amplification of nodal errors, while the <polynomial reproduction error bound> relates interpolation to the <best uniform approximation> available at that <polynomial degree>.

{title2=$p_n(x)=\sum_{i=0}^n y_i\ell_i(x)$}

= Lagrange interpolation polynomial
{c}
{synonym}

= Lagrange interpolation
{c}
{synonym}

= Polynomial interpolant
{synonym}

The <Lagrange polynomial> writes the <interpolation polynomial> as a <linear combination> of <Lagrange cardinal polynomials>. Each one selects a single <interpolation node>, so the data values themselves become the coefficients:
$$
p_n(x)=\sum_{i=0}^n y_i\ell_i(x),\qquad
\ell_i(x)=\prod_{\substack{0\leq j\leq n\\j\ne i}}\frac{x-x_j}{x_i-x_j}.
$$
Distinct <interpolation nodes> make every denominator nonzero. At $x_j$, all terms vanish except $y_j\ell_j(x_j)=y_j$. This gives an explicit construction without solving for <monomial> coefficients. The elegance of the <Lagrange polynomial> is that it builds all constraints into the <basis>, leaving no equations to solve for the data values.

For $(x_0,x_1,x_2)=(0,1,2)$ and $(y_0,y_1,y_2)=(1,2,5)$, substitution gives
$$
p_2(x)=\frac{(x-1)(x-2)}2-2x(x-2)+\frac{5x(x-1)}2=x^2+1.
$$
At $x=1/2$, the <Lagrange cardinal polynomial> values are $3/8,3/4,-1/8$, so $p_2(1/2)=3/8+2(3/4)+5(-1/8)=5/4$. A negative <Lagrange cardinal polynomial> value is allowed: these weights sum to one but need not be <probabilities>.

The product form is especially useful for deriving <differentiation weights from nodal evaluations> and <quadrature rules>. For repeated numerical evaluation, the <barycentric interpolation formula> reorganizes the same <Lagrange polynomial> into precomputed weights and sums; it avoids evaluating every full product at every argument.

= Lagrange leading-coefficient identity
{parent=Lagrange polynomial}
{c}
{title2=$\sum_i x_i^k/\prod_{j\ne i}(x_i-x_j)=\delta_{kn}$}

For $n+1$ distinct <interpolation nodes> and an integer $0\leq k\leq n$,
$$
\sum_{i=0}^n\frac{x_i^k}{\prod_{j\ne i}(x_i-x_j)}
=\begin{cases}0,&k<n,\\1,&k=n.\end{cases}
$$
To prove this, the <Lagrange interpolation polynomial> for the data $x_i^k$ is exactly $x^k$, by uniqueness of <polynomial interpolation>. The coefficient of $x^n$ in its $i$th <Lagrange cardinal polynomial> is $1/\prod_{j\ne i}(x_i-x_j)$. Comparing the coefficients of $x^n$ gives the identity. At $k=0$, values of the constant <polynomial> $1$ are intended even at a zero node. Negative exponents are outside the assertion.

= Differentiation weights from nodal evaluations
{parent=Lagrange polynomial}
{title2=$p'(x_0)=\sum_j\ell_j'(x_0)p(a_j)$}

At distinct nodes $a_0,\ldots,a_n$, the <Lagrange interpolation polynomial> has basis $\ell_j(x)=\prod_{i\ne j}(x-a_i)/(a_j-a_i)$. Differentiating its exact expression for a polynomial of degree at most $n$ gives the displayed weights. Their uniqueness follows because the nodal evaluations form a basis of the <dual space> of that polynomial space.

= Discrete minimax interpolation on ordered nodes
{parent=Lagrange polynomial}
{title2=$p=t-\lambda r,\quad\lambda=[x^{n+1}]t/[x^{n+1}]r$}

For $n+2$ increasingly ordered nodes $y_j$, let $r_j$ be their <Lagrange cardinal polynomials>, $t=\sum f(y_j)r_j$, and $r=\sum(-1)^jr_j$. The leading coefficient of $r_j$ has sign $(-1)^{n+2-j}$, so all summands in the leading coefficient of $r$ have the same nonzero sign. There is a unique $\lambda$ canceling degree $n+1$ in $t-\lambda r$. Its errors at the nodes are $\lambda(-1)^j$, and a better degree-at-most-$n$ approximation would have a difference polynomial with at least $n+1$ roots. Thus it minimizes the maximum node error. Increasing order matters: for the permutation $(0,1/3,1,2/3)$ the alternating interpolant is already the quadratic $-1+9x(1-x)$, so the leading-coefficient assertion for $n=2$ fails.

= Lagrange cardinal polynomial
{parent=Lagrange polynomial}
{c}
{title2=$\ell_i$}

= Lagrange basis polynomial
{c}
{synonym}

For distinct <interpolation nodes> $x_0,\ldots,x_n$, the <Lagrange cardinal polynomial> $\ell_i$ has value one at its own <interpolation node> and zero at every other <interpolation node>:
$$
\ell_i(x)=\prod_{j\ne i}\frac{x-x_j}{x_i-x_j},\qquad
\ell_i(x_j)=\delta_{ij}=
\begin{cases}1,&i=j,\\0,&i\ne j.\end{cases}
$$
Here $\delta_{ij}$ is the <Kronecker delta>. These <Lagrange cardinal polynomials> form a <basis> of the <vector space> of <polynomials> of <polynomial degree> at most $n$: every such <polynomial> has the unique expansion $p(x)=\sum_i p(x_i)\ell_i(x)$. The nodal evaluation <linear functionals> $E_j(p)=p(x_j)$ form the <dual basis>, since $E_j(\ell_i)=\delta_{ij}$.

For the <interpolation nodes> $0,1,2$, the three <Lagrange cardinal polynomials> are
$$
\ell_0(x)=\frac{(x-1)(x-2)}2,\qquad
\ell_1(x)=-x(x-2),\qquad
\ell_2(x)=\frac{x(x-1)}2.
$$
Their values on the <interpolation nodes> are the rows of the <identity matrix>. In the figure, each colored curve passes through its own unit-height marker and through zero at the other two <interpolation nodes>. Multiplying the curves by the data $1,2,5$ and adding gives the <Lagrange polynomial> $x^2+1$.

\Image[lagrange-interpolation-cardinals.png]
{title=Lagrange cardinal polynomials at nodes 0, 1, 2 and their weighted sum interpolating values 1, 2, 5}

Since the constant <polynomial> one interpolates data values all equal to one, uniqueness gives $\sum_i\ell_i(x)=1$. Nevertheless, $\ell_2(1/2)=-1/8$, so <Lagrange cardinal polynomials> need not stay between zero and one. If a single data value $y_i$ changes by $\varepsilon$, the <interpolation polynomial> changes by exactly $\varepsilon\ell_i(x)$. Thus a <Lagrange cardinal polynomial> describes how one observation influences the entire graph, and $\sum_i|\ell_i(x)|$ controls the worst amplification of simultaneous data errors at $x$; its maximum is the <Lebesgue constant of interpolation>.

= Endpoint derivative signs of Lagrange cardinal polynomials
{parent=Lagrange cardinal polynomial}
{title2=$(-1)^iL_i^{(k)}(1)>0$}

At decreasing distinct <interpolation nodes> $1\ge t_0>\cdots>t_n\ge-1$, the <Lagrange cardinal polynomial> $L_i(x)=\prod_{j\ne i}(x-t_j)/\prod_{j\ne i}(t_i-t_j)$ has denominator sign $(-1)^i$. For $1\le k\le n$, its numerator's $k$th <derivative> at one is $k!$ times a sum of products of $n-k$ nonnegative factors $1-t_j$. At most one factor vanishes, and at least one product avoids it, so that sum is strictly positive. The same alternating nodal signs therefore maximize every endpoint derivative simultaneously.

= Nodal derivative norm from Lagrange cardinal polynomials
{parent=Lagrange cardinal polynomial}
{title2=$\sup_{|p(t_i)|\le1}|p^{(k)}(x)|=\sum_i|\ell_i^{(k)}(x)|$}

At distinct <interpolation nodes>, differentiating <Lagrange interpolation> gives $p^{(k)}(x)=\sum_i p(t_i)\ell_i^{(k)}(x)$. The <triangle inequality> gives the displayed upper bound, attained by prescribing each nodal value to be the sign of the corresponding <derivative> weight. For increasingly ordered real nodes, the <Markov interlacing lemma> and <monotonicity of polynomial critical points in their roots> imply that $(-1)^i\ell_i^{(k)}(x)$ has at most one sign change, ignoring zero weights. Thus an extremizer has alternating nodal signs with at most one reversal of that alternating pattern. This reduces a choice among $2^{n+1}$ sign patterns to $n+1$ candidate <polynomials>.

= Barycentric interpolation formula
{parent=Lagrange polynomial}
{title2=$p_n(x)=\dfrac{\sum_i w_i y_i/(x-x_i)}{\sum_i w_i/(x-x_i)}$}

The <barycentric interpolation formula> evaluates the same <Lagrange polynomial> using weights that depend only on the distinct <interpolation nodes>:
$$
w_i=\frac1{\prod_{j\ne i}(x_i-x_j)},\qquad
p_n(x)=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^n\frac{w_i y_i}{x-x_i}}
{\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^n\frac{w_i}{x-x_i}},\qquad x\ne x_i.
$$
At $x=x_i$, return $y_i$ directly, before dividing; the formula has <removable singularities> at the <interpolation nodes>. Multiplying every $w_i$ by the same nonzero constant leaves the answer unchanged. Driscoll and Braun derive this formula as a more efficient, numerically stable way to evaluate <polynomial interpolation>.https://fncbook.com/barycentric/[Driscoll and Braun, Fundamentals of Numerical Computation, §9.2, Theorem 9.2.1 and Algorithm 9.2.1]{ref}

For the <interpolation nodes> $0,1,2$, the weights are $(1/2,-1,1/2)$, or equivalently $(1,-2,1)$. With data $(1,2,5)$, at $x=1/2$ the denominator using the latter weights is $2+4-2/3=16/3$ and the numerator is $2+8-10/3=20/3$, giving $p_2(1/2)=5/4$ again. For eleven <interpolation nodes> in the <Runge phenomenon> figure, the generator computes the weights by the direct products above and returns stored values exactly at every <interpolation node>.

The direct products require $O(n^2)$ <arithmetic operations> once; each subsequent value uses $O(n)$ <arithmetic operations>. Here $O$ denotes <Big O notation>. At large <polynomial degree> or on poorly scaled intervals, forming these products can exceed the range of <floating-point arithmetic>; normalization or specialized weight formulas may be needed. Stable evaluation also cannot cure sensitivity intrinsic to the chosen <interpolation nodes>, measured by the <Lebesgue constant of interpolation>, or the approximation failure in the <Runge phenomenon>.

= Divided difference
{parent=Polynomial interpolation}
{wiki}
{title2=$f[x_0,\ldots,x_k]$}

For $k+1$ distinct <interpolation nodes>, the <divided difference> $f[x_0,\ldots,x_k]$ is the coefficient of $x^k$ in the <interpolation polynomial> of <polynomial degree> at most $k$ through those <interpolation nodes>. It is the <leading coefficient> when that <polynomial degree> is exactly $k$, and zero when it is smaller. Explicitly,
$$
f[x_0,\ldots,x_k]
=\sum_{j=0}^k\frac{f(x_j)}
{\prod_{i\ne j}(x_j-x_i)}.
$$
starting with $f[x_i]=f(x_i)$. The <divided differences> obey the recurrence
$$
f[x_0,\ldots,x_k]
=\frac{f[x_1,\ldots,x_k]-f[x_0,\ldots,x_{k-1}]}
{x_k-x_0}.
$$
For data $f(0)=1$, $f(1)=2$, $f(2)=5$, the first <divided differences> are $f[0,1]=(2-1)/(1-0)=1$ and $f[1,2]=(5-2)/(2-1)=3$. The second <divided difference> is $f[0,1,2]=(3-1)/(2-0)=1$, the <leading coefficient> of $x^2+1$. The first <divided difference> is the slope of a <secant line>; higher <divided differences> supply successive coefficients for the <Newton polynomial>.

= Divided difference table
{parent=Divided difference}
{title2=$D_{i,k}=f[x_i,\ldots,x_{i+k}]$}

A <divided difference table> arranges <divided differences> by starting <interpolation node> $i$ and order $k$. Its base column is $D_{i,0}=f(x_i)$; every later entry uses two neighboring entries from the previous column:
$$
D_{i,k}=\frac{D_{i+1,k-1}-D_{i,k-1}}{x_{i+k}-x_i},\qquad
1\leq k\leq n,\quad 0\leq i\leq n-k.
$$
The denominator spans all $k+1$ <interpolation nodes> of that entry, even though the numerator uses adjacent table entries. The <interpolation nodes> must be distinct; they need not be equally spaced or sorted.<NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions/3.3 Interpolation>[NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions; §3.3(iii), equation 3.3.34: initial values and divided-difference recurrence for distinct nodes]{ref}

Take the <polynomial> $f(x)=x^3-2x+1$ at the <interpolation nodes> $(-1,0,2,3)$, giving values $(2,1,5,22)$. Its <divided difference table> is
$$
\begin{array}{c|r|r|r|r}
x_i&D_{i,0}&D_{i,1}&D_{i,2}&D_{i,3}\\\hline
-1&2&-1&1&1\\
0&1&2&5&\\
2&5&17&&\\
3&22&&&
\end{array}
$$
The first <divided differences> are $(1-2)/(0+1)=-1$, $(5-1)/(2-0)=2$, and $(22-5)/(3-2)=17$. The second <divided differences> use the wider spans: $(2+1)/(2+1)=1$ and $(17-2)/(3-0)=5$. The third <divided difference> is $(5-1)/(3+1)=1$. Reading the top row gives the <Newton polynomial> coefficients $(2,-1,1,1)$, rather than all the entries in any one column.

The resulting <Newton polynomial> grows through four partial <interpolants>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
p_0(x)&=2,\\
p_1(x)&=2-(x+1)=1-x,\\
p_2(x)&=2-(x+1)+(x+1)x=x^2+1,\\
p_3(x)&=2-(x+1)+(x+1)x+(x+1)x(x-2)=x^3-2x+1.
\end{aligned}
$$
At $x=2$, $p_1(2)=-1$ misses $f(2)=5$ by $6$; the correction $(x+1)x$ supplies exactly $6$ there and vanishes at the earlier <interpolation nodes> $-1,0$. At $x=3$, $p_2(3)=10$ misses $f(3)=22$ by $12$; the correction $(x+1)x(x-2)$ supplies exactly $12$ there and vanishes at all three earlier <interpolation nodes>. Each added term therefore preserves the previous agreements while satisfying the next one.

\Image[divided-difference-triangle-newton-stages.png]
{title=Divided-difference triangle and successive Newton interpolants for the constructed polynomial x cubed minus 2x plus 1}

For $n+1$ <interpolation nodes>, the triangle contains $n+(n-1)+\cdots+1=n(n+1)/2$ recurrence steps. Once the top row is known, nested evaluation of the <Newton polynomial> uses only $n$ multiplications and $n$ additions per argument. The triangle organizes the work; the factors explain why the construction works.

= Symmetry of divided differences
{parent=Divided difference}
{title2=$f[x_{\sigma(0)},\ldots,x_{\sigma(k)}]=f[x_0,\ldots,x_k]$}

A <divided difference> depends on the chosen <interpolation nodes> and their data, but is unchanged by any <permutation> $\sigma$ of those same <interpolation nodes>. The explicit <sum> in <divided difference> makes this visible: a <permutation> merely rearranges its summands.<NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions/3.3 Interpolation>[NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions; §3.3(iii), equation 3.3.35: symmetric explicit expression]{ref}

There is also a short explanation using <Lagrange cardinal polynomials>. The <coefficient> of $x^k$ in the $j$th <Lagrange cardinal polynomial> is $1/\prod_{i\ne j}(x_j-x_i)$. Taking that <coefficient> in their weighted <sum> gives exactly the explicit <divided difference> formula. Since <polynomial interpolation> has a unique answer for a fixed set of distinct <interpolation nodes>, this <coefficient> cannot depend on their ordering. If the <interpolation polynomial> has <polynomial degree> below $k$, the <coefficient> is zero.

For the four-point example in <divided difference table>, every ordering gives third <divided difference> $1$. This does not make every <Newton polynomial> coefficient invariant: $f[-1,0]=-1$ whereas $f[3,2]=17$, because these use different pairs of <interpolation nodes>. Even at second order, $f[-1,0,2]=1$ and $f[3,2,0]=5$ use different triples. The <node order in Newton interpolation> determines which subsets occur along the top row.

= Omitted-node identities for divided differences
{parent=Divided difference}
{title2=$D_i-D_j=(x_j-x_i)f[x_0,\ldots,x_n]$}

For distinct <interpolation nodes>, write $D_i$ for the <divided difference> with $x_i$ omitted. Symmetry and the divided-difference recurrence give the displayed identity. Consequently, for $0<i<n$,
$$
D_i=\frac{x_i-x_0}{x_n-x_0}D_n+\frac{x_n-x_i}{x_n-x_0}D_0.
$$
The identity is algebraic and does not require the nodes to be ordered; the coefficients need not be nonnegative.

= Exponential divided difference
{parent=Divided difference}
{title2=$D_t(\beta,\gamma)=\int_0^te^{(t-x)\gamma+x\beta}\,dx$}

The displayed integral is $(e^{t\beta}-e^{t\gamma})/(\beta-\gamma)$ for distinct exponents and $te^{t\gamma}$ when they coincide. The latter is the continuous limit, so a zero denominator is a removable singularity. This quantity appears in <variation-of-constants formula> estimates of <matrix> evolution and avoids omitting an equal-exponent case.

= Leibniz rule for divided differences
{c}
{parent=Divided difference}

For $h=fg$,
$$
h[t_0,\ldots,t_k]=\sum_{m=0}^kf[t_0,\ldots,t_m]g[t_m,\ldots,t_k].
$$

= Newton polynomial
{c}
{parent=Divided difference}
{wiki}
{title2=$p_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n f[x_0,\ldots,x_k]\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(x-x_i)$}

= Newton interpolation polynomial
{c}
{synonym}

The <Newton polynomial> expresses the <interpolation polynomial> in products that successively vanish at all previously used <interpolation nodes>. With $y_i=f(x_i)$, the coefficients are <divided differences>:
$$
p_n(x)=f(x_0)+\sum_{k=1}^nf[x_0,\ldots,x_k]
\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(x-x_i).
$$
A triangular <divided difference> table computes all its coefficients using exactly $n(n+1)/2$ divisions. The <Newton polynomial> and the <Lagrange polynomial> have the same <polynomial degree> bound and the same nodal values, hence are equal by uniqueness of <polynomial interpolation>.

For $f(0)=1$, $f(1)=2$, $f(2)=5$, the complete <divided difference> table is
$$
\begin{array}{c|r|r|r}
x_i&f[x_i]&f[x_i,x_{i+1}]&f[x_i,x_{i+1},x_{i+2}]\\\hline
0&1&1&1\\
1&2&3&\\
2&5&&
\end{array}
$$
The first <divided differences> are $1$ and $3$, and their <divided difference> is $(3-1)/(2-0)=1$. Reading the top entries gives
$$
p_2(x)=1+1(x-0)+1(x-0)(x-1)=1+x+x(x-1)=x^2+1.
$$
The initial term fits $(0,1)$; adding $x$ also fits $(1,2)$; adding $x(x-1)$ fits $(2,5)$ while leaving the first two data values unchanged. This is the practical beauty of the <Newton polynomial>: each new correction respects all previous interpolation constraints.

If a new distinct <interpolation node> $x_{n+1}$ with value $y_{n+1}$ arrives, append one term:
$$
p_{n+1}(x)=p_n(x)+c\prod_{i=0}^n(x-x_i),\qquad
c=\frac{y_{n+1}-p_n(x_{n+1})}{\prod_{i=0}^n(x_{n+1}-x_i)}.
$$
For example, appending $(3,11)$ to the three-point data gives $c=(11-10)/(3\cdot2\cdot1)=1/6$, hence $p_3(x)=x^2+1+x(x-1)(x-2)/6$. Changing the order of the <interpolation nodes> changes the <Newton polynomial> coefficients and products, but not the final <interpolation polynomial> in exact arithmetic.

The products also permit nested evaluation analogous to <Horner's method>. If $a_k=f[x_0,\ldots,x_k]$, start with $b_n=a_n$ and work backwards by $b_k=a_k+(x-x_k)b_{k+1}$; the answer is $b_0$. The three-point example becomes $1+x[1+(x-1)]$, needing two multiplications for each evaluation. This organization is useful when adding observations incrementally or evaluating a stored <Newton polynomial> many times; the <Runge phenomenon> still depends on the <interpolation nodes> rather than on which representation is used.

To justify the update formula, its added <product> vanishes at every previous <interpolation node>. Choosing $c$ by the new residual forces agreement at $x_{n+1}$, so uniqueness of <polynomial interpolation> identifies the result. For $n\geq0$, $p_n$ has <polynomial degree> at most $n$, and the added <product> has <leading coefficient> one. Thus the new <coefficient> $c$ is exactly $f[x_0,\ldots,x_{n+1}]$. The initial state is $p_0(x)=f(x_0)$, with the <empty product> interpreted as one.<NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions/3.3 Interpolation>[NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions; §3.3(iv), equation 3.3.38 and the following new-point update paragraph]{ref}

Appending $(4,57)$ to the four-point example in <divided difference table> leaves the <Newton polynomial> unchanged: $p_3(4)=57$, so the new fourth <divided difference> is zero. If the appended value is instead $58$, the new <coefficient> is
$$
c=\frac{58-57}{(4+1)\cdot4\cdot(4-2)\cdot(4-3)}=\frac1{40},\qquad
p_4(x)=x^3-2x+1+\frac{(x+1)x(x-2)(x-3)}{40}.
$$
This <polynomial> still takes values $2,1,5,22$ at the original <interpolation nodes>, and now takes value $58$ at $4$. A zero update is a valid completed step: adding another exact sample of the original <polynomial> need not increase the <polynomial degree>.

= Node order in Newton interpolation
{parent=Newton polynomial}

Reordering the <interpolation nodes> changes the successive subsets used for the <divided differences> and the factors of the <Newton polynomial>. The final <interpolation polynomial> remains unchanged in exact arithmetic. For $f(x)=x^3-2x+1$, reversing $(-1,0,2,3)$ gives $(3,2,0,-1)$ and values $(22,5,1,2)$. The successive <divided difference> columns are now
$$
\begin{aligned}
D_{i,1}&:\quad 17,\ 2,\ -1,\\
D_{i,2}&:\quad 5,\ 1,\\
D_{i,3}&:\quad 1.
\end{aligned}
$$
For example, the first second <divided difference> is $(2-17)/(0-3)=5$. The top row becomes $(22,17,5,1)$, giving
$$
\begin{aligned}
q_0(x)&=22,\\
q_1(x)&=22+17(x-3)=17x-29,\\
q_2(x)&=22+17(x-3)+5(x-3)(x-2)=5x^2-8x+1,\\
q_3(x)&=22+17(x-3)+5(x-3)(x-2)+(x-3)(x-2)x\\
&=x^3-2x+1=p_3(x).
\end{aligned}
$$
The earlier partial <interpolants> differ because they fit different subsets: $p_2$ fits $-1,0,2$, whereas $q_2$ fits $3,2,0$. The highest <divided difference> is invariant because both complete top rows finish with the same set of four <interpolation nodes>; this is <symmetry of divided differences>.

\Image[newton-interpolation-node-order.png]
{title=Reversing the interpolation nodes changes the Newton construction but gives the same final polynomial}

This distinction is useful when checking a <divided difference table>: permuting all the <interpolation nodes> must preserve its final <divided difference>, even though the intermediate entries change. It also explains why data can be appended in their arrival order. In <floating-point arithmetic>, the subtractions and divisions may accumulate different rounding errors in different orders; exact invariance describes the mathematical <polynomial>, rather than guaranteeing bit-for-bit equality of numerical results.
