= Solution
{parent=a}

The <strong equivalence principle> extends universality of <free fall> to bodies with appreciable gravitational self-energy, and states that the outcome of any local experiment, including a gravitational experiment, in a freely falling laboratory is independent of its location and <velocity>. In a sufficiently small freely falling laboratory the external gravitational influence can be removed to first order by choosing a <local inertial frame>.

This does not remove <tidal forces> or the <Riemann curvature tensor>. The laboratory must be small compared with the length scale over which the external gravitational field varies. These qualifications distinguish a local equivalence statement from the incorrect assertion that gravity can be transformed away throughout an extended curved region.

= Solution
{parent=b}

In general coordinates the basis changes along the <world line>. Differentiating only the components of a <vector> omits that basis change, and does not transform as a <vector> under a position-dependent coordinate transformation. The appropriate rate is the <covariant derivative along a curve>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{DV^\alpha}{d\lambda}
=\frac{dV^\alpha}{d\lambda}
+\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\beta\gamma}\frac{dx^\beta}{d\lambda}V^\gamma.}
$$
For the metric-compatible torsion-free connection, the <Christoffel symbols> are determined by the <metric tensor> and its first derivatives:
$$
\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\beta\gamma}
=\frac12g^{\alpha\delta}(\partial_\beta g_{\delta\gamma}
+\partial_\gamma g_{\delta\beta}-\partial_\delta g_{\beta\gamma}).
$$
Thus the ship's <four-acceleration> is
$$
\boxed{a^\alpha=\frac{Du^\alpha}{d\tau}
=\frac{du^\alpha}{d\tau}+\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\beta\gamma}u^\beta u^\gamma.}
$$
In Cartesian coordinates of an <inertial frame> in flat <spacetime>, the connection coefficients vanish and the ordinary component derivative happens to suffice. In curved <spacetime> it suffices at a chosen event in a <local inertial frame>, but not generally along an extended trajectory.

= Solution

Use <metric signature> $(-,+,+,+)$ and $x^0=ct$. At an event in the ship's instantaneous rest <local inertial frame>, $u^\alpha=(c,0,0,0)$. Since its squared norm is a scalar,
$$
\boxed{u^\alpha u_\alpha=-c^2}
$$
in every coordinate system. Equivalently it follows directly from $ds^2=-c^2d\tau^2$ and $u^\alpha=dx^\alpha/d\tau$.

<Metric compatibility> lets us differentiate this identity covariantly:
$$
0=\frac D{d\tau}(g_{\alpha\beta}u^\alpha u^\beta)
=2u_\alpha a^\alpha,
\qquad\boxed{u_\alpha a^\alpha=0.}
$$
In the instantaneous rest <local inertial frame>, the last equation gives $a^0=0$. The spatial components are the acceleration recorded by an onboard accelerometer, $\mathbf a_{\rm rest}$, whose magnitude is $g$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{a^\alpha a_\alpha=|\mathbf a_{\rm rest}|^2=g^2.}
$$
This <proper acceleration> is zero in ideal <free fall>, even when the coordinate position has a nonzero second derivative. A coordinate acceleration and an accelerometer reading are different quantities.

Put $F(r)=1-r_s/r$ and $r_s=2GM/c^2$. For radial motion $u^\theta=u^\phi=0$. The given <Christoffel symbols> imply
$$
\boxed{a^0=\frac{du^0}{d\tau}
+\frac{2GM}{c^2r^2F}u^0u^r,}
$$
and
$$
\boxed{a^r=\frac{du^r}{d\tau}
+\frac{GM}{c^2r^2}\left[F(u^0)^2-F^{-1}(u^r)^2\right].}
$$
The <four-velocity> normalization of part <1/c> is
$$
-F(u^0)^2+F^{-1}(u^r)^2=-c^2.
$$
Its bracketed combination is therefore exactly $c^2$, independently of the instantaneous <velocity> or the thrust history. Consequently
$$
\boxed{a^r=\frac{du^r}{d\tau}+\frac{GM}{r^2}.}
$$
The derivative here is with respect to <proper time>. This expression does not say that the ship measures a gravitational acceleration $GM/r^2$ during <free fall>; setting $a^r=0$ gives its radial proper-time equation of motion in <Schwarzschild spacetime>.

= Solution
{parent=e}

For radial motion outside the <Schwarzschild event horizon>, the equations needed to determine the acceleration components are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
-F(u^0)^2+F^{-1}(u^r)^2&=-c^2,\\
-Fu^0a^0+F^{-1}u^ra^r&=0,\\
-F(a^0)^2+F^{-1}(a^r)^2&=g^2.
\end{aligned}}
$$
They are respectively the <four-velocity> norm, velocity-acceleration orthogonality, and the maximum <proper acceleration> norm. Together with part <1/d> they give the stated thrust equation; no integration of the trajectory is required. The positive radial sign corresponds to outward thrust.

Let $r_I=\sqrt{GM/g}$. A first transition from inward to outward motion requires $u^r=0$ at a minimum radius and $du^r/d\tau\ge0$ there. The largest possible radial derivative at such an event is
$$
\left.\frac{du^r}{d\tau}\right|_{u^r=0}
=g\sqrt{1-r_s/r}-\frac{GM}{r^2}.
$$
At $r=r_I>r_s$, this is
$$
\boxed{\left.\frac{du^r}{d\tau}\right|_{r_I,u^r=0}
=g\left[\sqrt{1-r_s/r_I}-1\right]<0.}
$$
Thus the ship cannot reverse its inward motion there. It cannot reverse at any smaller exterior radius either: $GM/r^2$ grows while $\sqrt{1-r_s/r}$ decreases. If it first reaches $r_I$ while falling inward, **it cannot return home using the stipulated bounded radial thrust**. If $r_I\le r_s$, it would instead have reached the black-hole horizon before that radius, which already prevents escape.

The <minimum radius for radial turnaround with bounded proper acceleration> lies strictly outside the proposed radius. Define $r_c>r_s$ by
$$
\boxed{g=\frac{GM}{r_c^2\sqrt{1-r_s/r_c}}.}
$$
The right side is the <proper acceleration> required to hover, decreases monotonically from infinity to zero, and exceeds $GM/r^2$. A genuine turnaround needs $r_{\rm turn}>r_c>r_I$; equality gives no positive initial outward acceleration at rest. A sufficiently early burn is also needed to remove the pre-existing inward <velocity>. The failure at $r_I$ follows from the turning-point condition, not from treating the moving ship as static throughout its fall.

In coordinates $(ct,r,\phi,z)$ the diagonal <metric tensor> is $\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,f^2,1)$, and only $g_{\phi\phi}$ depends on position. Substitution into the connection formula gives
$$
\boxed{\Gamma^r{}_{\phi\phi}=-ff',\qquad
\Gamma^\phi{}_{r\phi}=\Gamma^\phi{}_{\phi r}=\frac{f'}f,}
$$
with all other <Christoffel symbols> zero away from singular axes.

Use the explicit <Ricci tensor> formula and the <Einstein equation> sign printed on the source formula sheet. They give
$$
R_{rr}=\frac{f''}f,\qquad R_{\phi\phi}=ff'',\qquad
\mathcal R=g^{\alpha\beta}R_{\alpha\beta}=2\frac{f''}f.
$$
For example, in $R_{rr}$ the two terms proportional to $(f'/f)^2$ cancel. For $R_{\phi\phi}$ the derivative of $-ff'$ contributes $(f')^2+ff''$, the product terms contribute $-2(f')^2$, and the determinant term contributes $(f')^2$.

**Formula-sheet qualification.** Directly contracting the separately printed <Riemann curvature tensor> as instructed on that sheet instead gives $R_{rr}=-f''/f$, the opposite sign. For the concrete example $f(r)=\ell\sin(r/\ell)$, that contraction gives $+1/\ell^2$, whereas the explicit <Ricci tensor> formula gives $-1/\ell^2$. The two source formulas cannot both hold with the stated contraction. The calculation here uses the explicit <Ricci tensor> formula, whose pairing with the negative <Einstein equation> coupling yields the requested <stress-energy tensor>.

The desired matter tensor assumes $\Lambda=0$; otherwise the cosmological-constant contribution must also be included. With that assumption,
$$
T_{\alpha\beta}=-\frac{c^4}{8\pi G}
\left(R_{\alpha\beta}-\frac12g_{\alpha\beta}\mathcal R\right),
$$
and raising both indices gives
$$
\boxed{T^{\alpha\beta}=\frac{c^4}{8\pi G}\frac{f''}f
\operatorname{diag}(-1,0,0,1).}
$$
The static observer measures <energy density> $T^{00}=-c^4f''/(8\pi Gf)$ and equal-magnitude negative axial <pressure> when that energy is positive. The axial stress is a tension. Keeping the source's curvature sign and its negative Einstein-equation coupling together is essential to this result.

Assume $\phi$ has period $2\pi$, $f>0$ in the regular bulk, and consider a unit proper length in $z$. The proper transverse area element is $dA=f\,dr\,d\phi$, while the mass density is $T^{00}/c^2$. The <axial boundary term in the mass of a cylindrical spacetime> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\mu_{\rm bulk}=\int_0^R\frac{T^{00}}{c^2}\,2\pi f\,dr
=-\frac{c^2}{4G}\int_0^Rf''\,dr
=\frac{c^2}{4G}[f'(0^+)-f'(R^-)].}
$$
Choose the usual deficit branch $0\le\lambda<1$, so $f_{\rm out}=(1-\lambda)r$. Continuity of the induced metric requires $f(R^-)=(1-\lambda)R$. If there is no distributional thin shell at $R$, continuity of the <extrinsic curvature> also requires $f'(R^-)=1-\lambda$. A jump in $f'$ would add a surface-layer stress and cannot simply be discarded.

**Using the printed inner condition** $f'(0^+)=0$, these assumptions yield
$$
\boxed{\mu_{\rm bulk}=\frac{c^2}{4G}(\lambda-1).}
$$
This is the literal smooth-bulk answer. It is negative for the ordinary deficit range, so it must not be silently replaced by the usual positive cosmic-string mass formula.

For comparison, a regular axis with proper radial coordinate and angular period $2\pi$ requires $f(0)=0$ and $f'(0)=1$, since the circumference-to-radius ratio must approach $2\pi$. Under that different regular-axis condition the same integral gives
$$
\boxed{\mu_{\rm regular}=\frac{\lambda c^2}{4G},\qquad
\delta=2\pi\lambda=\frac{8\pi G\mu_{\rm regular}}{c^2}.}
$$
The original PDF really prints the derivative limit zero. It describes either a nonregular axis or an inner geometry different from an ordinary smooth string core; any additional singular-axis matter is not included in the bulk integral above and would need its own stress specification. Standard regular-core matching can also require an offset in the exterior radial origin. Thus the regular-axis formula is a qualified physical interpretation, not a derivation from the printed zero. For a general exterior branch replace $1-\lambda$ by $|1-\lambda|$ in the boundary derivative.

Outside the core, $f''=0$ and the metric is locally flat: set $\widetilde\phi=(1-\lambda)\phi$. Static test particles have no Newtonian-like attraction because $g_{00}$ is constant and $\Gamma^r{}_{00}=0$. Local trajectories are straight lines in locally unwrapped coordinates, so a very thin dark core is hard to identify through ordinary orbital accelerations or local <tidal forces>.

The global angular range is nevertheless $2\pi(1-\lambda)$ rather than $2\pi$. The missing angle $\delta=2\pi\lambda$ can give <gravitational lensing> with two nearly identical, essentially undistorted images of a background galaxy, and multiple such pairs aligned along the projected object. For a small deficit, a straight string and favourable geometry, their angular separation is of order $\delta D_{ls}/D_s$, with an additional inclination factor for a tilted string. The matching spectra and common line of image pairs distinguish this from an ordinary compact lens.

A moving <cosmic string> can also induce a step in the <cosmic microwave background> <temperature> across its path. That signature needs motion in addition to the static geometry supplied here. These are consequences of the exterior conical geometry; interpreting the inferred deficit as a positive mass per length requires the regular-axis/core assumptions distinguished in part <2/b>.

The <cosmic distance ladder> starts by calibrating a physical baseline. Radar echoes from planets or spacecraft give a round-trip light time and hence a distance $d=c\Delta t/2$, after accounting for orbital motion and propagation delays. Combining this with orbital geometry fixes the Earth-Sun orbital scale; directly timing a reflected pulse from the solar surface is not required.

That baseline supplies annual <stellar parallax>: for small parallax angle $p$, $d\simeq b/p$, or $d$ in parsecs equals $1/p$ when $p$ is in arcseconds and $b$ is one astronomical unit. Nearby parallax distances calibrate stellar <absolute magnitudes>. <Main-sequence fitting> extends the scale to star clusters, and the calibrated <Cepheid period-luminosity relation> gives <luminosities> for Cepheids visible in nearby galaxies.

Cepheid-host galaxies then calibrate <Type Ia supernovae>. After light-curve and colour standardization, their high <luminosities> permit distances to much more remote galaxies. For a source of calibrated <luminosity> $L$ and observed bolometric flux $F$,
$$
\boxed{d_L=\sqrt{\frac{L}{4\pi F}},\qquad
m-M=5\log_{10}\left(\frac{d_L}{10\,\mathrm{pc}}\right)}
$$
with consistent bandpass definitions and corrections. <Interstellar extinction>, composition, stellar-population differences and calibration uncertainty must be propagated through the steps. Redshift can subsequently provide a distance within a fitted cosmological model; using it alone cannot independently calibrate the <Hubble constant>.

Normalize the <scale factor> to $a(t)=R(t)/R(t_0)$, with $a(t_0)=1$. Along a radial <null geodesic>, $d\chi=c\,dt/a(t)$. Two successive wave crests sent by the same comoving emitter and received by the same comoving observer traverse the same <comoving distance>, so
$$
\int_{t_e}^{t_0}\frac{c\,dt}{a(t)}
=\int_{t_e+\delta t_e}^{t_0+\delta t_0}\frac{c\,dt}{a(t)}.
$$
To first order, $\delta t_0/a(t_0)=\delta t_e/a(t_e)$. Frequencies are inverse proper periods for the <comoving observers>, giving the <cosmological redshift>
$$
\boxed{1+z=\frac{\lambda_0}{\lambda_e}
=\frac{R(t_0)}{R(t_e)}=\frac1{a(t_e)}.}
$$
Expansion stretches wavelengths, so a spectral line of known rest wavelength gives $z=\lambda_{\rm obs}/\lambda_{\rm rest}-1$.

At small $z$, the light travel time is short compared with the expansion time. Expanding $R(t_e)$ gives $z\simeq H_0(t_0-t_e)$ and $d\simeq c(t_0-t_e)$, hence
$$
\boxed{cz\simeq H_0d,\qquad H_0=\left.\frac{\dot R}{R}\right|_{t_0}.}
$$
Combine independently calibrated <luminosity distances> with galaxy spectral redshifts and fit the slope. Nearby peculiar velocities must be allowed for, while high-redshift data require the full distance-redshift relation rather than the linear approximation.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Use standardized <Type Ia supernovae> as distance indicators and measure their redshifts. The curvature of the luminosity-distance relation constrains the <deceleration parameter>:
$$
\boxed{d_L(z)=\frac c{H_0}\left[z+\frac{1-q_0}{2}z^2+O(z^3)\right],\qquad
q_0=-\frac{\ddot R(t_0)}{R(t_0)H_0^2}.}
$$
Fainter supernovae at a given redshift imply larger distances, and comparison with the predicted curve tests accelerated expansion. In practice one fits the full expansion history over the observed redshift range and controls <luminosity> evolution, extinction and calibration, rather than relying only on a short low-$z$ series.

The <cosmological fate from local expansion data> is conditional on a matter model. For the spatially flat model of part <3/b>, if the only relevant components are pressureless matter and a constant $\Lambda$, put $A_0=\ddot R(t_0)/R(t_0)$. The two <Friedmann equations> give
$$
\boxed{\rho_{m0}=\frac{H_0^2-A_0}{4\pi G},\qquad
\Lambda c^2=H_0^2+2A_0.}
$$
Here $\Lambda$ follows the sign of the supplied <Friedmann equations>, where positive $\Lambda$ contributes positive acceleration. These fix the parameters governing subsequent evolution. Positive $\Lambda$ yields eternal late-time accelerated expansion; zero $\Lambda$ gives the flat matter solution; negative $\Lambda$ leads to eventual recollapse in this dust model. If instead one assumes dust and zero $\Lambda$ while allowing curvature, $q_0=\Omega_{m0}/2$ determines whether the matter universe is closed and recollapsing or expands indefinitely.

Without a fixed <equation of state> and its future evolution, two present derivatives do **not** uniquely fix the future. For a concrete counterexample in units with $t_0=0$, the positive scale factors $a_+(t)=1+t+t^2/2$ and $a_-(t)=1+t+t^2/2-t^3$ have the same $a,\dot a,\ddot a$ now. The former expands forever; the latter turns around and later reaches zero. Different time-dependent matter stresses can support their different flat expansion histories. Thus the printed sufficiency statement needs the cosmological-model assumption.

In an <Einstein-de Sitter universe>, take $a(t)=(t/t_0)^{2/3}$ and $t_0=2/(3H_0)$. The galaxy seen today at $z=1$ emitted the light now reaching us when $a(t_e)=1/2$, so $t_e/t_0=2^{-3/2}$. Its fixed <comoving radial distance> is
$$
\chi=c\int_{t_e}^{t_0}\frac{dt}{a(t)}
=3ct_0\left(1-\frac1{\sqrt2}\right).
$$
Put $b=1-1/\sqrt2$. A new message sent now reaches the galaxy at $t_1$, where
$$
\chi=3ct_0[(t_1/t_0)^{1/3}-1],\qquad
\frac{t_1}{t_0}=(1+b)^3.
$$
Assume an immediate reply and negligible local handling delays. The return consumes the same additional <conformal time>, giving the <Einstein-de Sitter round-trip message time>
$$
\boxed{\frac{t_2}{t_0}=(1+2b)^3=(3-\sqrt2)^3\simeq3.98781,\qquad
\Delta t=t_2-t_0\simeq2.98781t_0=\frac{1.99187}{H_0}.}
$$
For the numerical value $H_0=72\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$ supplied later in the paper, $H_0^{-1}=13.58\,\mathrm{Gyr}$ and the waiting time is about $27.1\,\mathrm{Gyr}$. The age $t_0=9.05\,\mathrm{Gyr}$ here belongs to the stipulated matter-only model, not a claim about the actual measured cosmic age.

After $n$ one-way legs, $t_n=t_0(1+nb)^3$. Since $\int_{t_0}^{\infty}dt/a(t)=\infty$, every finite number of messages is possible and there is no finite fundamental upper count. The exchange takes arbitrarily long, with successively longer intervals; infinitely many messages are not completed in finite cosmic time.

= Solution
{parent=e}

Spatial flatness constrains the total density, not matter alone. A flat model can have
$$
\boxed{\Omega_{m0}+\Omega_{\Lambda0}+\Omega_{r0}=1}
$$
with a substantial <dark energy> component. A positive <cosmological constant> has <pressure> $p_\Lambda=-\rho_\Lambda c^2$ and does not dilute like matter; it can produce the observed accelerated expansion.

If positive $\Lambda$ persists, $a(t)$ becomes exponential and the future <conformal time> interval
$$
\Delta\eta_{\rm future}=\int_{t_0}^{\infty}\frac{dt}{a(t)}
$$
is finite. For two comoving civilisations separated by $\chi$, each one-way signal consumes $\chi/c$. Thus a message completing $n$ legs at finite time requires
$$
\boxed{n\frac\chi c<\Delta\eta_{\rm future}.}
$$
Only finitely many legs, and hence finitely many full exchanges, can then occur. A reply to even the first new message requires $2\chi/c<\Delta\eta_{\rm future}$, the <cosmological round-trip communication horizon> condition. Depending on the density parameters, the initial outward message might arrive while its reply can never reach us, as in the right-hand panel of part <3/d>.

The source gives no matter and dark-energy fractions, so it does not specify an exact count for the $z=1$ pair in the real Universe. Flatness and lack of matter dominance alone do not prove a finite horizon: a radiation-dominated flat universe, for example, has divergent future <conformal time> if that power-law expansion persists. The finite-message conclusion uses persistent accelerated expansion such as positive constant $\Lambda$.

For a <photon gas> in thermal and <chemical equilibrium>, <photon> number is not a conserved thermodynamic charge. Number-changing processes can create and absorb <photons> without a corresponding conserved-number constraint, so the equilibrium <photon chemical potential> is zero.

At $T=5\times10^9\,\mathrm K$, $k_{\rm B}T\simeq0.431\,\mathrm{MeV}$. The other light species are <electrons>, <positrons>, three <neutrino> species and their antiparticles. <Neutrinos> are <ultrarelativistic>; <electrons> and <positrons> are only moderately relativistic at this particular <temperature> because $m_ec^2=0.511\,\mathrm{MeV}$. The later simple counting formulas use the intended <ultrarelativistic> approximation for them. Baryons, muons and heavier particles are not an abundant relativistic bath at this <temperature>.

Pair equilibrium implies $\mu_{e^-}+\mu_{e^+}=2\mu_\gamma=0$. Approximate charge symmetry, with the net <electron> excess tied to the much smaller baryon asymmetry, then makes both <electron> and <positron> <chemical potentials> small compared with $k_{\rm B}T$. For <neutrinos> one additionally assumes negligible lepton asymmetry. Their near-zero <chemical potentials> are not implied merely by their statistics. After <neutrino decoupling>, a distribution initially having negligible <chemical potential> retains a redshifted <Fermi-Dirac distribution>, with its own <temperature>.

Let $V$ be proper volume, let $g_i$ count the populated internal states, and take $\mu_i=0$ and $\epsilon=pc$. Spherical integration in momentum space gives
$$
\frac{N_i}{V}=\frac{g_i}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{k_{\rm B}T}{\hbar c}\right)^3
\int_0^\infty\frac{x^2\,dx}{e^x\mp1},\qquad x=\frac{pc}{k_{\rm B}T}.

Using the <fermion-to-boson thermal integral ratio>, the minus-denominator <boson> integral is $2\zeta(3)$ and the plus-denominator <fermion> integral is $3\zeta(3)/2$. Hence
$$
\boxed{N_B=\frac{g_B\zeta(3)}{\pi^2}V\left(\frac{k_{\rm B}T}{\hbar c}\right)^3,\qquad
N_F=\frac34\frac{g_F\zeta(3)}{\pi^2}V\left(\frac{k_{\rm B}T}{\hbar c}\right)^3.}

The energy integral has one extra factor $pc=k_{\rm B}Tx$:
$$
\frac{U_i}{V}=\frac{g_i}{2\pi^2}\frac{(k_{\rm B}T)^4}{(\hbar c)^3}
\int_0^\infty\frac{x^3\,dx}{e^x\mp1}.

The supplied distribution identity gives the <fermion> integral as $(1-2^{-3})\pi^4/15=7\pi^4/120$. Therefore

\boxed{U_B=\frac{g_B\pi^2}{30}V\frac{(k_{\rm B}T)^4}{(\hbar c)^3},\qquad
U_F=\frac78\frac{g_F\pi^2}{30}V\frac{(k_{\rm B}T)^4}{(\hbar c)^3}.}

\boxed{P_i=\frac{U_i}{3V}.}
$$
<Photons> have $g_\gamma=2$, <electrons> and <positrons> each have $g=2$, and each <neutrino> or antineutrino species has one populated <helicity> in the standard thermal bath.

For zero <chemical potential>, each species contributes $S_i=(U_i+P_iV)/T=4U_i/(3T)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\frac{S_B}{V}=\frac{2\pi^2}{45}g_Bk_{\rm B}

<Entropy density> scales as $T^3$ at fixed relativistic particle content; total <entropy> also includes the changing proper volume. An isolated adiabatically expanding comoving gas has conserved total <entropy> and $aT$ constant, rather than its total <entropy> falling as $T^3$.

At equal <temperature>, single <electrons> and <photons> each have two spin/polarization states. In the <ultrarelativistic> approximation,
$$
\boxed{\frac{S_{e^-}}{S_\gamma}=\frac78.}
$$
<Positrons> are a separate species with the same contribution, so $(S_{e^-}+S_{e^+})/S_\gamma=7/4$. This distinction is needed for the heating count in part <4/d>. At the exact supplied <temperature> the <electron> rest mass gives corrections to the ideal $7/8$ ratio; it is the limiting relativistic value requested by the problem.

There is also a sign convention issue in the introductory <entropy> formula. With the chemical-potential convention used in the supplied distribution, the Euler relation is $U=TS-PV+\sum_i\mu_iN_i$, so $S=(U+PV-\sum_i\mu_iN_i)/T$. Its chemical-potential term has the opposite sign to the printed one. All <chemical potentials> have been neglected here, so this source error does not change any of the above results.

= Solution
{parent=d}

As the plasma cools through electron-positron annihilation, the pairs transfer their <entropy> to the coupled <photons>. <Neutrinos> have largely decoupled and do not share that heating in the instantaneous-decoupling approximation. Before annihilation the electromagnetic <entropy> count is
$$
g_{*s,\rm before}=2+\frac78(2+2)=\frac{11}{2},
$$
while after it $g_{*s,\rm after}=2$. Conservation of electromagnetic <comoving entropy>, $g_{*s}T_\gamma^3a^3=\text{constant}$, gives the relative <photon> heating factor
$$
\boxed{\frac{(aT_\gamma)_{\rm after}}{(aT_\gamma)_{\rm before}}
=\left(\frac{11}{4}\right)^{1/3}\simeq1.401.}
$$
Equivalently $T_\gamma/T_\nu=(11/4)^{1/3}$ afterward. The exact $11/4$ count compares an asymptotic relativistic pair bath with the pair-free bath; at the supplied <temperature> some finite-electron-mass <entropy> loss has already begun.

The word “rises” requires care. The <photons> become hotter than an otherwise freely redshifting bath at the same <scale factor>; their absolute <temperature> need not rise during the expansion. In the smooth equilibrium process,
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\ln T_\gamma}{d\ln a}
=-\frac{3}{3+d\ln g_{*s}/d\ln T_\gamma}<0,}
$$
since the pair contribution grows with <temperature>. This <monotonic photon cooling during pair annihilation> is slower than $a^{-1}$ while $aT_\gamma$ increases. The factor $1.401$ compares the <photon> <temperature> with the freely redshifting <neutrino> bath, not two consecutive absolute temperatures along the expanding history.

= Solution
{parent=e}

Use the observed present <photon> <temperature> $T_{\gamma0}\simeq2.725\,\mathrm K$. This is an additional observational input, consistent with <The Temperature of the Cosmic Microwave Background by Dale J. Fixsen>[abstract: combined CMB-temperature determination]{ref}. Standard <neutrino decoupling> and electron-positron <entropy> transfer give
$$
\boxed{T_{\nu0}=\left(\frac4{11}\right)^{1/3}T_{\gamma0}\simeq1.945\,\mathrm K.}
$$
For nonrelativistic relic <neutrinos> this is the <temperature> parameter of the redshifted momentum distribution, not the <temperature> of a newly thermalized gas of massive particles.

The <photon number density> from part <4/b> is
$$
n_{\gamma0}=\frac{2\zeta(3)}{\pi^2}
\left(\frac{k_{\rm B}T_{\gamma0}}{\hbar c}\right)^3\simeq410.5\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}.
$$
One <neutrino> mass eigenstate and its antineutrino have two populated <helicity> states. Thus
$$
\boxed{n_{\nu_i}+n_{\bar\nu_i}
=\frac34\left(\frac{T_\nu}{T_\gamma}\right)^3n_\gamma
=\frac3{11}n_\gamma\simeq112\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}.}
$$
The total over three species is about $336\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$, including antineutrinos. Comoving number conservation preserves these abundances after the particles become nonrelativistic.

For the supplied $H_0=72\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}=2.33\times10^{-18}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$,
$$
\rho_c=\frac{3H_0^2}{8\pi G}\simeq9.74\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}.
$$
Assuming nonnegative other energy components, relic <neutrino> rest-mass density cannot exceed the near-critical total density. With the common per-species abundance $n_*=n_{\nu_i}+n_{\bar\nu_i}$,
$$
\rho_{\nu,\rm rest}=n_*\sum_{i=1}^3m_i\le\rho_c,
\qquad
\boxed{\sum_{i=1}^3m_i\lesssim\frac{\rho_c}{n_*}
\simeq48.8\,\mathrm{eV}/c^2\approx50\,\mathrm{eV}/c^2.}
$$
Using the total $336\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}$ would instead bound the mean mass, not the sum. This <critical-density bound on the sum of relic neutrino masses> is deliberately conservative: baryons, dark matter and <dark energy> leave less density available. It assumes the standard thermal abundance, negligible <chemical potentials>, no later dilution or extra populated states, and the idealized decoupling count. Small non-instantaneous-decoupling corrections do not matter for this estimate.
