A <T0 space> distinguishes any two distinct points by an <open set> containing one and not the other. A <Tychonoff space>, also called a completely regular <Hausdorff> space, is a $T_1$ space in which a point outside a <closed set> can be separated from it by a <continuous function>: after rescaling, $f(x)=0$ and $f|_C=1$ with values in $[0,1]$.

The <product topology> has the <subbasis of a topology>
$$
\boxed{\{\pi_\lambda^{-1}(U):\lambda\in\Lambda,\ U\text{ open in }X_\lambda\}.}
$$
A map into the product is continuous exactly when each coordinate map is continuous. One direction follows by composition with the continuous <product projections>; the other follows by taking <inverse images> of the displayed subbasic sets.

Define the <diagonal evaluation map> $\Delta(x)=(f_\lambda(x))_{\lambda\in\Lambda}$. Its coordinates are continuous, so the map is continuous by <continuity into a product space>. Point separation makes it <injective>. For a basic <open set> $B=f_\lambda^{-1}(U)$,
$$
\Delta(B)=\Delta(X)\cap\pi_\lambda^{-1}(U).
$$
Every <open subset> of $X$ is a <union> of such $B$, so its image is open in $\Delta(X)$. The inverse on the image is continuous, and $\boxed{\Delta\text{ is a topological embedding}}$. If $X$ is $T_0$, any two distinct points are distinguished by an <open set> and then by one basic neighborhood within it. Equality of all coordinates would prevent that distinction. Thus the basis hypothesis alone implies point separation in a $T_0$ space. The unsubscripted $f$ in the printed separation hypothesis is understood as the chosen coordinate $f_\lambda$.

For the <Sierpiński space> $S=\{0,1\}$ with opens $\varnothing,\{1\},S$, each open $U\subseteq X$ gives a continuous characteristic map $\chi_U:X\to S$ with $\chi_U^{-1}(\{1\})=U$. These <inverse images> form the whole <topology>, hence a basis. In a $T_0$ space they separate points, proving the embedding into $S^{\tau}$.

For a <Tychonoff space>, use all <continuous maps> $X\to[0,1]$. Given $x\in U$ with $U$ open, complete regularity supplies $f(x)=0$ and $f=1$ on $X\setminus U$. Then $f^{-1}([0,1/2))$ is an <open neighborhood> of $x$ contained in $U$. These <inverse images> form a basis. The diagonal theorem gives an embedding into a product of closed unit intervals.

**A three-point space works even without any separation axiom.** Take
$$
\boxed{Y=\{0,1,2\},\qquad\tau_Y=\{\varnothing,\{2\},Y\}.}
$$
For each open $U$ of an arbitrary space, let $g_U=2$ on $U$ and $0$ elsewhere. It is continuous. For each point $a$, let $h_a=1$ at $a$ and $0$ elsewhere. It is also continuous, because its image lies in the subspace $\{0,1\}$ of $Y$, which has the <indiscrete topology>; equivalently $h_a^{-1}(\{2\})=\varnothing$. The $g_U$ supply every <open set> as an <inverse image>, and the $h_a$ distinguish every pair of points. Using the <diagonal evaluation map> for their combined family gives a <universal three-point space for product embeddings>:
$$
\boxed{X\hookrightarrow Y^{\tau\sqcup X}.}
$$

Finally embed the given completely regular <Hausdorff> space into a cube $K=[0,1]^I$. The <Tychonoff theorem> makes $K$ <compact>; it is <Hausdorff> and hence normal. The image of the <compact subset> $C$ is <compact>, so closed in $K$. The <Tietze extension theorem> says that a continuous bounded real-valued function on a <closed subset> of a <normal space> extends continuously to the whole space. The given function on $C$ is bounded by <compactness>, so apply this theorem to its transported function on the image of $C$ in $K$. Restrict the extension to the image of $X$ and pull it back along the embedding. This yields $\boxed{F:X\to\mathbb R\text{ continuous},\ F|_C=f}$. This proves that <compact subsets of Tychonoff spaces are C-embedded>, using normality of the <compact> cube.

For $y\in Y$, every neighborhood of $y$ in the subspace is of the form $V\cap Y$ with $V$ an ambient <open neighborhood>. Since $A\subseteq Y$, this meets $A$ exactly when $V$ meets $A$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\overline A^{\,Y}=Y\cap\overline A^{\,X}.}
$$

A <Hausdorff compactification> is a <topological embedding> $h:X\to K$ with <dense> image in a <compact Hausdorff space> $K$. Define the domination preorder by
$$
\boxed{(h,K)\leq(h',K')\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \exists q:K'\to K\text{ continuous with }q\circ h'=h.}
$$
It is reflexive by the identity map and transitive by composition. Any comparison map is onto: its image is <compact> and hence closed in $K$, and contains the <dense> set $h(X)$. Two compactifications are equivalent if a <homeomorphism> $u:K\to K'$ satisfies $u\circ h=h'$.

Such a <homeomorphism> and its inverse give domination both ways. Conversely, if comparison maps exist in both directions, each composite agrees with the identity on the <dense> embedded copy of $X$. Two <continuous maps> to a <Hausdorff> space agreeing on a <dense> set agree everywhere. The comparison maps are therefore inverse <homeomorphisms>. Thus equivalence is exactly mutual domination.

The <Stone-Čech extension property> says that every <continuous map> from $X$ into any <compact Hausdorff space> extends continuously to the compactification. The extension is unique by density. For two compactifications with this property, extend each embedding through the other compactification. This gives comparison maps both ways, hence equivalence by the preceding argument.

For the <perfect-map characterization by Stone-Čech remainders>, the universal property first gives a unique continuous $F:\beta X\to\beta Y$ extending the composite $X\xrightarrow fY\hookrightarrow\beta Y$. The required remainder condition is equivalent to
$$
\boxed{F^{-1}(Y)=X.}
$$
One inclusion is already forced by the extension property; the other forbids any point of the remainder mapping into $Y$.

Suppose first that $f$ is a <perfect map>. Fix $y\in Y$ and let $K=f^{-1}(y)$, a <compact subset> of $X$ and hence a <closed subset> of $\beta X$. If $p\in F^{-1}(y)\setminus K$, normality of $\beta X$ supplies an open $U$ with $K\subseteq U$ and $p\notin\overline U$. Because $f$ is closed,
$$
V=Y\setminus f(X\setminus U)
$$
is an <open neighborhood> of $y$ and $f^{-1}(V)\subseteq U\cap X$. Choose an open $V'$ of $\beta Y$ with $V'\cap Y=V$. The <open set> $F^{-1}(V')$ contains $p$, and its <intersection> with the <dense> subset $X$ lies in $U$. Every point of that <open set> is consequently in $\overline U$, contradicting the choice of $p$. Therefore $F^{-1}(y)=K\subseteq X$ for every $y\in Y$, proving the remainder condition. This argument also handles an empty <fiber>.

Conversely suppose the remainder condition holds. Every <fiber> $f^{-1}(y)=F^{-1}(y)$ is a <closed subset> of $\beta X$, hence <compact>, and lies in $X$. To show that $f$ is closed, let $A$ be closed in $X$ and set $D=\overline A^{\,\beta X}$. The initial <closure> identity gives $D\cap X=A$. <Compactness> makes $F(D)$ closed in $\beta Y$, and the remainder condition gives
$$
f(A)=F(D)\cap Y.
$$
Indeed any preimage in $D$ of a point of $Y$ must belong to $X$ and hence to $A$. Thus $f(A)$ is closed in $Y$. Together with <compact> <fibers> this proves
$$
\boxed{f\text{ perfect}\ \Longleftrightarrow\ F(\beta X\setminus X)\subseteq\beta Y\setminus Y.}
$$

The <open sets> $U$ and $X\setminus C_\lambda$ cover $X$. A finite subcover exists by <compactness>, and taking complements yields
$$
\boxed{\bigcap_{\lambda\in\Lambda_0}C_\lambda\subseteq U}
$$
for a finite index set. This is the <finite intersection characterization of compactness> in a neighborhood form; the finite index set may be empty when $U=X$.

The <Šura-Bura lemma> states that in a <compact Hausdorff space> the <connected component> $C(x)$ of a point equals its <quasi-component>, the <intersection> of all <clopen> neighborhoods of that point. It also follows that each component has a neighborhood basis of <clopen> sets. Here is a proof of both assertions.

Let $Q$ be the <intersection> of the <clopen> neighborhoods of $x$. Every <connected set> containing $x$ lies in each such neighborhood, so $C(x)\subseteq Q$. The set $Q$ is closed and <compact>. If $Q$ were disconnected, write it as the disjoint <union> of nonempty relatively <closed sets> $A,B$, choosing $x\in A$. Both are <compact> and closed in $X$. Normality gives disjoint <open sets> $O_A,O_B$ containing them. By the finite-intersection result, a finite <intersection> $V$ of <clopen> neighborhoods of $x$ satisfies $Q\subseteq V\subseteq O_A\cup O_B$. Then $V\cap O_A$ is <clopen> in $V$, hence <clopen> in $X$, and contains $x$ while excluding $B$. This contradicts the definition of $Q$. Thus $Q$ is <connected> and equals $C(x)$. Given any <open neighborhood> of the component, the same finite-intersection argument supplies a <clopen> neighborhood inside it. This proves the neighborhood-basis form as well.

For an explicit <noncompact counterexample to the Šura-Bura lemma>, put $p=(0,0)$, $q=(1,0)$, and $\varepsilon_m=1/(m+2)$ for $m\geq1$. Let $A_m$ be the polygonal arc with successive vertices
$$
(\varepsilon_m,0),\quad(\varepsilon_m,m),\quad(1-\varepsilon_m,m),\quad(1-\varepsilon_m,0),
$$
and give $Z=\{p,q\}\cup\bigcup_{m\geq1}A_m$ its <subspace topology> from the plane. The arcs are pairwise disjoint: a higher arc has its vertical sides outside the horizontal span of any lower arc. Each $A_m$ is <compact> and relatively open in $Z$, so is <clopen>. For relative openness, all sufficiently high arcs have vertical sides close to $x=0,1$ and horizontal sides above a fixed bounded neighborhood of $A_m$; the remaining finitely many <compact> disjoint arcs have positive distance from it.

A <connected> subset containing $p$ cannot meet any of these <clopen> arcs, so it lies in $\{p,q\}$ and is just $\{p\}$. But any <clopen> neighborhood of $p$ contains the left endpoints of all sufficiently high arcs. <Connectedness> of each arc forces the entire arc into that neighborhood, and closedness then forces the limit $q$ of their right endpoints into it. Conversely the <clopen> set $Z\setminus A_m$ excludes every point of $A_m$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{C_Z(p)=\{p\},\qquad Q_Z(p)=\{p,q\}.}
$$
The <compact> set containing the endpoints has lost the connection supplied by arcs escaping to arbitrarily large height; <compactness> of the ambient space is essential to the lemma.

Now let $D=\bigcap_n C_n$ for the decreasing <compact> <connected sets> in the <compact> <Hausdorff> setting. Every finite <intersection> is one of the nonempty $C_n$, so the <finite intersection property> gives $D\ne\varnothing$. It is closed and hence <compact>. If it were disconnected, its two nonempty <compact> pieces would have disjoint <open neighborhoods> $U,V$ in $X$. Since $D\subseteq U\cup V$, the initial finite-intersection result and the decreasing property give $C_N\subseteq U\cup V$ for some $N$. This set meets both neighborhoods because it contains $D$, contradicting its <connectedness>. Thus
$$
\boxed{D\text{ is nonempty, compact, and connected}.}
$$
The argument does not need <connectedness> of the whole ambient space.

For the requested failure without ambient <compactness>, add the central ray $L=\{(1/2,t):t\geq1\}$ to the previous example and put $X=Z\cup L$. For $n\geq1$ define
$$
\boxed{C_n=\{p,q\}\cup\bigcup_{m\geq n}A_m\cup\{(1/2,t):t\geq n\}.}
$$
Each graph formed by the displayed arcs and ray is <connected>: each arc meets the ray at its top. Adding $p,q$, which are limit points of the graph, preserves <connectedness> because the <closure> of a <connected set> is <connected>. Its relative <closure> in $X$ adds exactly these two points, so $C_n$ is closed in $X$. To check that no omitted point is a further limit, a bounded neighborhood away from the lines $x=0,1$ meets only finitely many arcs; the deleted initial arcs are disjoint <compact> sets, and the deleted ray segment lies strictly below the remaining ray. All other planar limit points on $x=0,1$ are outside $X$.

The sets are decreasing and nonempty, and $X=C_1$ is <connected> and <Hausdorff>. Every individual arc and every point of finite height on the ray disappears from sufficiently late sets. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\bigcap_{n\geq1}C_n=\{p,q\},}
$$
a nonempty <compact> disconnected set. Reindexing by $n+1$ gives the source's indexing by $\omega$. The graph is unbounded, so the ambient subspace is not <compact>. This is an explicit <decreasing closed connected sets with disconnected compact intersection> example.

For a set $A$, its <definable power set> consists of the subsets of $A$ definable by a <first-order formula> over $(A,\in)$ using finitely many parameters from $A$. The quantifiers range over $A$; this is generally much smaller than its full <power set>.

The <constructible universe> is defined by <transfinite recursion>:
$$
\boxed{L_0=\varnothing,\qquad L_{\alpha+1}=\operatorname{Def}(L_\alpha),\qquad
L_\lambda=\bigcup_{\alpha<\lambda}L_\alpha\quad(\lambda\text{ a limit ordinal}),\qquad
L=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\mathrm{On}}L_\alpha.}
$$
The last <union> denotes a definable <proper class>, not a set-sized <union> over a set of all <ordinals>. At a successor stage, only definitions over the previously constructed stage are permitted.

Use <transfinite induction>. The assertion is immediate at stage zero, and at a limit stage it follows by taking the increasing <union> of the earlier stages.

Assume $L_\alpha\cap\mathrm{On}=\alpha$. Any <ordinal> $\gamma\in L_{\alpha+1}$ is a subset of $L_\alpha$, since members of the <definable power set> are such subsets. Every element of $\gamma$ is an <ordinal>, so
$$
\gamma\subseteq L_\alpha\cap\mathrm{On}=\alpha,
$$
which implies $\gamma\leq\alpha$. This gives the upper bound on the <ordinals> appearing at the successor stage.

For the reverse inclusion, the smaller <ordinals> are already in $L_\alpha$ and hence in $L_{\alpha+1}$. The remaining <ordinal> $\alpha$ itself is the definable subset $L_\alpha\cap\mathrm{On}$. The fact used here is **absoluteness of ordinalhood between a <transitive set> and the universe**: ordinalhood has a bounded definition, and bounded formulas are absolute for <transitive sets>. Thus the formula asserting that a member is an <ordinal>, interpreted over $L_\alpha$, defines exactly $\alpha$. Therefore $\alpha\in\operatorname{Def}(L_\alpha)=L_{\alpha+1}$.

This proves
$$
\boxed{L_\alpha\cap\mathrm{On}=\alpha\quad\text{for every ordinal }\alpha.}
$$
In particular each <ordinal> $\alpha$ is a member of $L_{\alpha+1}$, so $\boxed{\mathrm{On}\subseteq L}$.

**The axiom of infinity fails.** First prove $L_n=V_n$ for every <finite ordinal> $n$. The base stage is empty in both hierarchies. If $L_n=V_n$, that stage is a finite set. Every one of its subsets is definable over it with finitely many parameters, by a disjunction of equalities to the selected elements; the empty subset is defined by a false formula. Hence
$$
\operatorname{Def}(L_n)=\mathcal P(V_n)=V_{n+1}.
$$
Induction and the limit-stage definitions give
$$
\boxed{L_\omega=V_\omega,}
$$
the set of all <hereditarily finite sets>. Every member of this set is finite externally.

If this transitive structure satisfied the <axiom of infinity>, it would contain an inductive set $I$: zero belongs to $I$, and whenever $x\in I$, its successor $x\cup\{x\}$ belongs to $I$. These operations are the actual <empty set> and actual set successor, since they are absolute for this transitive structure and all relevant finite-rank sets lie in it. External induction would then place every <finite ordinal> in $I$, contradicting the finiteness of $I$.

Every individual <finite ordinal> does belong to $L_\omega$, but the set $\omega$ of all finite <ordinals> does not. The distinction between containing each <finite ordinal> and containing one inductive set is precisely the issue.

The <cardinal sum> is the <cardinality> of a disjoint <union>, and the <cardinal product> is the <cardinality> of the set of choice functions:
$$
\boxed{\sum_{\alpha<\beta}\kappa_\alpha=
\left|\{(\alpha,\xi):\alpha<\beta,\ \xi<\kappa_\alpha\}\right|,\qquad
\prod_{\alpha<\beta}\kappa_\alpha=
\left|\{f:\operatorname{dom}f=\beta,\ f(\alpha)<\kappa_\alpha\}\right|.}
$$
Here the cardinals are represented by <initial ordinals>. Tagging by $\alpha$ makes the summands disjoint even when their underlying sets overlap. For an empty index set the sum is zero and the product is one, because there is exactly one <empty function>. If any factor in a nonempty product is zero, that product is empty.

This is <König theorem for cardinal numbers>. Let $D=\bigsqcup_{\alpha<\beta}\kappa_\alpha$ and $P=\prod_{\alpha<\beta}\lambda_\alpha$. For any proposed map $g:D\to P$, consider the values of the $\alpha$th coordinates on the $\alpha$th summand:
$$
E_\alpha=\{g(\alpha,\xi)(\alpha):\xi<\kappa_\alpha\}.
$$
This set has <cardinality> at most $\kappa_\alpha<\lambda_\alpha$. Choose $y_\alpha\in\lambda_\alpha\setminus E_\alpha$, using the <axiom of choice>, and assemble $y=(y_\alpha)\in P$. For any $(\alpha,\xi)\in D$, its image disagrees with $y$ at coordinate $\alpha$. Thus $y$ is outside the image of $g$, and no such $g$ is <surjective>.

Every $\lambda_\alpha$ is nonzero. The product is nonempty by choice; if $D$ is empty, strict inequality is immediate. Otherwise <cardinal comparability principle> in ZFC says that $|P|\leq|D|$ would yield a <surjection> from $D$ onto the nonempty $P$, which the <diagonal argument> excludes. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\sum_{\alpha<\beta}\kappa_\alpha<\prod_{\alpha<\beta}\lambda_\alpha.}
$$
The diagonal coordinate is tied to the summand containing the argument; this is why a sum on the left pairs naturally with a product on the right.

The <aleph numbers> enumerate the infinite <initial ordinals> by <transfinite recursion>:
$$
\boxed{\aleph_0=\omega,\qquad\aleph_{\alpha+1}=(\aleph_\alpha)^+,\qquad

equivalently $2^{\aleph_\alpha}=\aleph_{\alpha+1}$ for every <ordinal> $\alpha$ in ZFC.

The <membership induction theorem> is the following schema, for every formula $\varphi(x)$ with any fixed set parameters:
$$
\boxed{\left[\forall x\left((\forall y\in x\,\varphi(y))\to\varphi(x)\right)\right]
\ \Longrightarrow\ \forall x\,\varphi(x).}
$$
Its hypothesis says the property is progressive under membership. In ZF it follows from the <axiom of foundation>: if a counterexample $x$ exists, take the nonempty set of counterexamples in the <transitive closure> $\operatorname{TC}(\{x\})$, which contains $x$. A membership-minimal counterexample supplied by the <axiom of foundation> has no counterexample among its elements, contradicting progressivity. The transitive <closure> ensures that all elements relevant to this argument remain in the set being considered.

Compose the two assumed isomorphisms to obtain a membership-preserving and membership-reflecting <bijection>
$$
f=\pi_2\circ\pi_1^{-1}:M_1\to M_2.
$$
Transitivity of both targets implies, for every $a\in M_1$,
$$
f(a)=\{f(b):b\in a\}.
$$
For one inclusion use preservation of membership. For the other, if $c\in f(a)$, transitivity puts $c$ in $M_2$; surjectivity gives $c=f(b)$ for $b\in M_1$, and reflection gives $b\in a$.

Apply the <membership induction theorem> to the assertion that every $a\in M_1$ is fixed. If all members $b$ of $a$ are fixed, the displayed equation gives $f(a)=a$. Thus $f$ is the identity on $M_1$. Its range is $M_2$, so $M_1=M_2$, and the definition of $f$ gives
$$
\boxed{\pi_1=\pi_2,\qquad M_1=M_2.}
$$
This proves uniqueness of the <Mostowski collapse theorem>. Existence of the two isomorphisms already supplies the membership structure needed in this argument.

Read epsilon as actual set membership restricted to $X$, the interpretation in which ambient ZF makes the relation <well-founded>. The structure's <axiom of extensionality> assumption means
$$
x,x'\in X,\quad x\cap X=x'\cap X\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=x'.
$$
By <well-founded recursion> on membership, define
$$
\boxed{\pi(x)=\{\pi(y):y\in x\cap X\}\qquad(x\in X).}
$$
The recursion theorem applies because the <axiom of foundation> makes the restricted relation <well-founded> and each predecessor collection is a set. The <axiom schema of replacement> gives the set $M=\pi[X]$.

To prove injectivity, use membership induction on $x$ for the property that $x\in X$ and $\pi(x)=\pi(x')$ with $x'\in X$ imply $x=x'$. Suppose this property holds for every $y\in x\cap X$. Equality of the two images means that each $y\in x\cap X$ has some $y'\in x'\cap X$ with $\pi(y)=\pi(y')$, so the induction hypothesis gives $y=y'$. Conversely every $y'\in x'\cap X$ has the same image as some $y\in x\cap X$, and the same induction hypothesis again makes them equal. Hence $x\cap X=x'\cap X$, and the structure's <axiom of extensionality> gives $x=x'$.

The map is onto $M$ by definition. The recursion immediately preserves membership, and its injectivity makes membership reflection valid as well. Moreover if $z\in\pi(x)$ then $z=\pi(y)$ for a predecessor $y\in X$, so $z\in M$. Therefore $M$ is transitive and $\pi$ is the required membership isomorphism. This proves the existence direction of the <Mostowski collapse theorem>.

**An arbitrary binary relation must also be <well-founded>.** <Axiom of extensionality> alone is insufficient in that interpretation: the one-element relation with a self-loop is extensional, but any collapse onto a <transitive set> would produce a set containing itself, contrary to <axiom of foundation>. The actual-membership interpretation above supplies precisely the hypothesis otherwise missing; no analogous claim is made for a general ill-founded relation.

The <cumulative hierarchy> is defined by <transfinite recursion>:
$$
\boxed{V_0=\varnothing,\qquad V_{\alpha+1}=\mathcal P(V_\alpha),\qquad
V_\lambda=\bigcup_{\alpha<\lambda}V_\alpha\quad(\lambda\text{ a limit ordinal}).}
$$
This recursion on <ordinals> is available without assuming the <axiom of foundation> for all sets. The stages are increasing and transitive, by <transfinite induction> using their definitions.

Work internally in the specified model of ZF without the <axiom of foundation>, and assume the <membership induction theorem>. Apply it to
$$
\varphi(x)\equiv\exists\alpha\in\mathrm{On}\ (x\in V_\alpha).
$$
Suppose every member $y$ of $x$ lies in some stage. Assign to each such $y$ the least <ordinal> $\alpha_y$ for which $y\in V_{\alpha_y}$. The <axiom schema of replacement> gives a set of these <ordinals>, and let $\beta$ be their supremum, taking $\beta=0$ for the empty collection. Monotonicity gives $x\subseteq V_\beta$, and therefore $x\in V_{\beta+1}$.

This proves progressivity of $\varphi$, so membership induction gives it for every set of the model. Every stage already consists of sets in that model. Consequently
$$
\boxed{V^*=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\mathrm{On}}V_\alpha.}
$$
The equality is internal to the given model; its <ordinals> and power sets are computed there.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Assume a nonempty set $a$ has no membership-minimal element, so for every $x\in a$ there is $y\in x\cap a$. Consider the formula $\varphi(x)\equiv x\notin a$.

It is progressive: if every element of $x$ is outside $a$ but $x\in a$, the assumed absence of a minimal element supplies $y\in x\cap a$, contradicting that hypothesis. The <membership induction theorem> therefore implies $x\notin a$ for every set $x$. This contradicts $a\ne\varnothing$.

Thus every nonempty set $a$ contains an element $x$ with $x\cap a=\varnothing$, exactly the <axiom of foundation>:
$$
\boxed{a\ne\varnothing\ \Longrightarrow\ \exists x\in a\ (x\cap a=\varnothing).}
$$
Only the induction schema and elementary set reasoning were needed; no prior use of <axiom of foundation> occurs in the proof.
