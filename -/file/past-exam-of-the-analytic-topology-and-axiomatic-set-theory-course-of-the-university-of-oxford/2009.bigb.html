Let $\mathscr H$ be the <set> of <separately continuous> real-valued <functions> on the plane. Take as a <subbasis of a topology>[subbasis] all <sets> $h^{-1}(U)$, where $h\in\mathscr H$ and $U$ is open in the usual <real line>. Unions of finite intersections of these <sets> form a <topology>, and every $h$ is <continuous> for it by construction. Conversely every <topology> making every $h$ <continuous> must contain the <subbasis of a topology>[subbasis], hence this entire generated <topology>. Thus **the required coarsest <topology> exists and is unique**: it is the <initial topology> of the family $\mathscr H$.

The family is a <set>, being a <subset> of $\mathbb R^{\mathbb R^2}$, so this construction has no proper-class issue. The <projection map>[coordinate projections] belong to $\mathscr H$; consequently this <topology> contains the ordinary <Euclidean topology>. This last observation will also make <convergence> in the new <topology> imply ordinary coordinatewise <convergence>.

Fix a horizontal line $L=\mathbb R\times\{c\}$. The restriction of every member of $\mathscr H$ to $L$ is <continuous> in its usual <topology>. Therefore every subbasic <open set> intersects $L$ in a usual <open set>, so the induced <topology> is no finer than the usual one. Conversely the first <projection map>[coordinate projection] is <continuous> for the <initial topology>, and its inverse images induce every usual <open set> on $L$. The two <topologies> on $L$ are equal. The second projection gives the identical argument for a vertical line.

Write $F=\overline Y^{\,X}$. For every nonzero rational $r$, the <intersection> of $Y$ with $\{r\}\times\mathbb R$ is <dense> in that vertical line, since the nonzero <rational numbers> are <dense> in the <real line>. Hence $\{r\}\times\mathbb R\subseteq F$. For each real $c$, it follows that $F\cap(\mathbb R\times\{c\})$ contains $(\mathbb Q\setminus\{0\})\times\{c\}$ and is closed in that line. This <dense subset> forces the whole horizontal line into $F$. Thus $F=X$: **$Y$ is a <countable> <dense subset>**. The two successive uses of line density are necessary; the <initial topology> is finer than the <Euclidean topology>, so Euclidean density alone would not suffice.

For a fixed nonzero second coordinate $c$, the rational expression defining $h(x,c)$ is <continuous> on the half-line where $xc>0$. On the opposite half-line it is constant. At $x=0$ the ratio tends to $1$, agreeing with the value on the other side. If $c=0$ the entire restriction is constant. By symmetry the restrictions with the first coordinate fixed are <continuous> too. Hence $h$ is <separately continuous>, and so is $h_t(x,y)=h(x-t,y-t)$ for every real $t$.

On the diagonal $D$, $h_t(t,t)=1$, while $h_t(s,s)=0$ for $s\ne t$. Therefore
$$
D\cap h_t^{-1}((1/2,3/2))=\{(t,t)\}.
$$
Each diagonal <singleton> is open in the induced <topology>; arbitrary unions show that **$D$ has the <discrete topology>**.

If $X$ were <metrizable space>[metrizable], its <countable> <dense subset> from the preceding part would make it <second countable>. Indeed balls with rational radii about <dense> points contain a <countable> <basis of a topology>[basis] after the usual smaller-ball argument. Every subspace of a <second-countable space> is <second countable>. A discrete <second-countable space> is <countable>, since each <singleton> must itself occur as a <basis of a topology>[basis] member. But $D$ is <uncountable>. This contradiction proves **$X$ is not <metrizable space>[metrizable]**.

Suppose for contradiction that $p_n=(u_n,v_n)\in Y$ converges to the origin in the <initial topology>. The <projection map>[coordinate projections] then give $u_n\to0$ and $v_n\to0$ in the usual <real line>. <Rectangular bumps detecting nonconvergence>, using anisotropic supports, avoids any unsupported regularity assertion about a radial maximum. <Set>
$$
\phi(t)=\max(1-|t|,0),\qquad r_n=|u_n|/4,\quad s_n=|v_n|/4,
$$
$$
b_n(x,y)=\phi\!\left(\frac{x-u_n}{r_n}\right)\phi\!\left(\frac{y-v_n}{s_n}\right),\qquad B(x,y)=\sup_n b_n(x,y).
$$
The radii are positive since neither coordinate of $p_n$ vanishes. Each rectangular support misses both coordinate axes, so $B$ vanishes on those axes. For a fixed $y=c\ne0$, all but finitely many vertical support intervals miss $c$, because their centres and radii tend to zero. Hence $B(x,c)$ is a <maximum>[finite maximum] of <continuous functions> of $x$, and is <continuous>. At $c=0$ it is identically zero. The same argument with the coordinates exchanged proves <separate continuity> everywhere. Thus $B$ is <continuous> for the <initial topology>.

However $B(p_n)=1$ for every $n$, whereas $B(0,0)=0$. This contradicts preservation of <convergent sequences> by a <continuous function>. Therefore **no <sequence> from $Y$ converges to the origin**.

The radial hint in the scan is not <separately continuous> for every candidate <sequence>: for $p_n=(2^{-n},2^{-2n})$, its value at the origin is zero, while its value at $(2^{-n},0)$ is at least $1-2(2^{-2n})/\sqrt{2^{-2n}+2^{-4n}}$, which tends to one. The rectangular construction supplies the needed valid proof independently of that hint.

<Urysohn's lemma> states that if $X$ is normal and $A,B$ are disjoint <closed subsets>, there is a <continuous function> $u:X\to[0,1]$ with $u|_A=0$ and $u|_B=1$. The values on the rest of $X$ remain in that interval. Interchanging $A,B$ or composing with an affine <function> gives any two prescribed real endpoint values. This is **functional separation of <closed sets>**, stronger than merely choosing disjoint open <neighbourhoods>.

A <C-embedded subset> $Y\subseteq X$ is one when every <continuous> <real-valued function> on $Y$ extends continuously to $X$. It is a <Cb-embedded subset> when this is required for every bounded <continuous> <real-valued function>, with a bounded extension. These definitions concern the <subspace topology> on $Y$.

Here is the bounded Tietze extension construction from <Urysohn's lemma>. Let $g:Y\to\mathbb R$ be <continuous> with $|g|\le M$. The case $M=0$ has the zero extension. For $M>0$, the <sets> $A=\{g\le-M/3\}$ and $B=\{g\ge M/3\}$ are disjoint and closed in $X$, since $Y$ is closed. <Urysohn's lemma> gives a <continuous> $u_0:X\to[-M/3,M/3]$ taking the endpoint values on $A,B$. On $Y$,
$$
|g-u_0|\le2M/3.
$$
Indeed on $A$ or $B$ this follows from the prescribed endpoint; on their complement both $|g|$ and $|u_0|$ are at most $M/3$.

Repeat on the residual. With $M_n=M(2/3)^n$, choose <continuous> $u_n$ satisfying $\|u_n\|_\infty\le M_n/3$ and reducing the residual on $Y$ to at most $M_{n+1}$. The <series> $F=\sum_{n\ge0}u_n$ converges uniformly by the geometric majorant. Its sum is <continuous>, has $|F|\le\sum M_n/3=M$, and restricts to $g$, since the residual tends uniformly to zero. Thus **every bounded <continuous function> extends, with its bound preserved**, proving $C^b$-embeddedness without assuming the extension theorem being proved.

Let $h:Y\to\mathbb R$ be <continuous>, without a boundedness assumption. The <homeomorphism> $\theta(t)=t/(1+|t|)$ maps the <real line> onto $(-1,1)$, with inverse $\theta^{-1}(s)=s/(1-|s|)$. The <bounded function> $g=\theta\circ h$ extends by the preceding bounded construction to a <continuous> real-valued $G$ on $X$. Since $|G|<1$ on $Y$, the strict-range modification gives a <continuous> $\widetilde G$ with range in $(-1,1)$ and the same restriction to $Y$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{H(x)=\frac{\widetilde G(x)}{1-|\widetilde G(x)|}}
$$
is <continuous> everywhere: its denominator is pointwise positive. On $Y$ it equals $\theta^{-1}(\theta(h))=h$. Thus **$Y$ is $C$-embedded**. It is important to modify the extension before applying the inverse: an extension allowed to attain $1$ or $-1$ outside $Y$ would make this formula undefined there.

A <filter base> on a <set> $X$ is a nonempty family $\mathcal F$ of nonempty <subsets> such that for every $F,G\in\mathcal F$ there is $H\in\mathcal F$ with $H\subseteq F\cap G$. Repeated application gives a member contained in any specified <finite intersection>. In particular every <finite intersection> is nonempty, so the family has the <finite intersection property>. Its upward closure $\{A\subseteq X:\exists F\in\mathcal F, F\subseteq A\}$ is the corresponding <filter>[proper filter].

For a <connected intersection of a closed filter base>, the filter-base property implies the <finite intersection property>. <Compactness>, in its closed-set formulation, therefore gives $K=\bigcap\mathcal F\ne\varnothing$. It is closed in $X$, hence <compact>.

Suppose additionally that each base member is <connected> but $K$ is disconnected. Write $K=A\cup B$ with $A,B$ disjoint nonempty <closed subsets> of $K$. They are <compact>, hence closed in the <Hausdorff space> $X$. <Normality of a compact Hausdorff space> gives disjoint <open sets> $U,V$ containing $A,B$, respectively. The <compact set> $W=X\setminus(U\cup V)$ misses $K$. For every $w\in W$ some $F_w\in\mathcal F$ misses $w$, so the <open sets> $X\setminus F_w$ cover $W$. A finite subcover gives $F_1\cap\cdots\cap F_m\subseteq U\cup V$. Choose $F_0\in\mathcal F$ inside this <finite intersection>. If $W$ is empty, choose any base member instead.

Since $F_0$ contains $K$, it meets both $U$ and $V$; but it lies in their <disjoint union>. These two relatively open nonempty pieces disconnect $F_0$, a contradiction. Thus **the <intersection> is nonempty and <connected>**. <Compactness> is used to pass from an infinite exclusion condition to a single base member.

A <Hausdorff compactification> of $X$ is an <topological embedding>[embedding] $h:X\to Y$ into a <compact Hausdorff space> with <dense> image. The complement $Y\setminus h[X]$ is the remainder; a one-point or two-point compactification has a remainder of that <cardinality>.

For one point, take the <unit circle> and
$$
h_1(x)=(\cos(2\arctan x),\sin(2\arctan x)).
$$
The angle runs over $(-\pi,\pi)$, so this is a <homeomorphism> onto the <circle> with $(-1,0)$ removed. That image is <dense>, and the <circle> is <compact> Hausdorff. For two points, take $Y=[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$ and $h_2(x)=\arctan x$. This is a <homeomorphism> onto its <dense> open interval, leaving exactly the two endpoints. Therefore **both required compactifications exist**; in the first the positive and negative ends meet, whereas in the second they remain distinct.

The <open set> $U\setminus h([-n,n])$ still contains $p$ and meets the <dense> image of $h$. Such an <intersection> point belongs to $T_n$, so $p\in\overline{T_n}$ for every $n$.

Conversely, if $p=h(x)$ choose an integer $n>|x|$. Since $h$ is an <topological embedding>[embedding], there is an open <neighbourhood> $U$ of $p$ in $Y$ whose <intersection> with $h[\mathbb R]$ is $h((-n,n))$. It misses $T_n$, so $p\notin\overline{T_n}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{Y\setminus h[\mathbb R]=\bigcap_{n\in\omega}\overline{T_n}^{\,Y}.}
$$
For the <two ends of a real-line compactification>, let $A_n^+=\overline{h((n,\infty))}$ and $A_n^-=\overline{h((-\infty,-n))}$. Each is nonempty <compact> and <connected>, being the <closure> of a <connected> image. The two nested families therefore have nonempty <connected> intersections $C^+$ and $C^-$. $$
\bigcap_n(A_n^+\cup A_n^-)=C^+\cup C^-.
$$
For if a point fails both intersections, it fails one positive member and one negative member, hence both members at the larger index by nesting.

If the remainder were a finite three-point <set>, its Hausdorff <subspace topology> would be discrete. Each nonempty <connected subset> $C^\pm$ would then be a <singleton>, so their <union> would have at most two points. This contradiction proves **there is no three-point <Hausdorff compactification> of the <real line>**. The argument does not assert that every possible remainder is finite.

The <cumulative hierarchy> is defined by <transfinite recursion> on the <ordinals>:
$$
\boxed{V_0=\varnothing,\qquad V_{\alpha+1}=\mathcal P(V_\alpha),\qquad V_\lambda=\bigcup_{\beta<\lambda}V_\beta\quad(\lambda\text{ a nonzero limit}).}
$$
The recursion theorem of <ZF> supplies each <set> stage. The whole universe is $V=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\mathrm{Ord}}V_\alpha$, a proper-class <union>. Equivalently, $x\in V_\alpha$ exactly when the rank of $x$ is less than $\alpha$, where $\operatorname{rank}(x)=\sup\{\operatorname{rank}(z)+1:z\in x\}$.

A <set> $T$ is transitive if every element of an element of $T$ is itself in $T$, equivalently each element of $T$ is a <subset> of $T$. Proceed by <transfinite induction>. The <empty set> is transitive. If $T$ is transitive and $u\in v\in\mathcal P(T)$, then $v\subseteq T$ gives $u\in T$, and transitivity of $T$ gives $u\subseteq T$. Thus $u\in\mathcal P(T)$, proving the successor stage transitive. A <union> of <transitive sets> is transitive: if $u\in v\in\bigcup_iT_i$, choose $i$ with $v\in T_i$ and infer $u\in T_i$. This proves the limit step and hence **every $V_\alpha$ is transitive**.

Take a nonzero <limit ordinal> $\lambda$ and $x\in V_\lambda$. Choose $\beta<\lambda$ with $x\in V_\beta$. Transitivity gives $x\subseteq V_\beta$. Hence every actual <subset> of $x$ belongs to $\mathcal P(V_\beta)=V_{\beta+1}$, and the actual <set> $\mathcal P(x)$ belongs to $V_{\beta+2}$. Here the absoluteness fact used is: every <bounded formula in set theory>[bounded formula] is absolute between <transitive sets> or classes with the actual membership relation. The actual <set> $\omega$ contains the <empty set> and is closed under $z\mapsto z\cup\{z\}$. These bounded membership and successor conditions are absolute in the transitive stage, so $\omega$ is an internal inductive <set>. Therefore **<Infinity> holds as well**. To prove <replacement failure at V omega plus omega>, put $M=V_{\omega+\omega}$. The <set> $\omega$ belongs to $M$, and for every $n\in\omega$ the <ordinal> $\omega+n$ also belongs to $M$. The relation saying that $y$ is obtained by taking $n$ successive <ordinal> successors of $\omega$ is definable, with parameter $\omega$, and is single valued in $M$.

For clarity this does not assume <Replacement> inside $M$. For each finite $n$ its finite computation <sequence> exists in $M$: its entries have rank below $\omega+n+1$, and the finite coding overhead still has rank below $\omega+\omega$. The <bounded formula in set theory>[bounded formula] verifying this finite successor chain is absolute between $M$ and the universe. The ordinary finite induction identifying its unique last entry uses the actual finite <ordinals>, which are the same in $M$. This justifies the internal definable <function> $n\mapsto\omega+n$ without invoking the axiom being tested.

$$
\operatorname{rank}(B)=\sup_{n<\omega}(\omega+n+1)=\omega+\omega.
$$
Every element of $V_{\omega+\omega}$ has strictly smaller rank. This contradiction proves **<Replacement> fails in $V_{\omega+\omega}$**. Each individual output is present; the missing object is the <set> collecting all outputs.

König's theorem for <cardinal numbers> states that, for a set-indexed family with $\kappa_i<\lambda_i$ at every index,
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i\in I}\kappa_i<\prod_{i\in I}\lambda_i.}
$$
Here the sum is the <cardinality> of a <disjoint union> and the product is the <cardinality> of a <Cartesian product>. Work in <ZFC>. Choose disjoint <sets> $A_i$ of sizes $\kappa_i$ and <sets> $B_i$ of sizes $\lambda_i$. For any <function> $f:\bigsqcup_i A_i\to\prod_iB_i$, its $i$th coordinate values on $A_i$ form a <subset> of $B_i$ of <cardinality> at most $\kappa_i$. Choose $b_i\in B_i$ outside that <subset>, using <choice>. Then $b=(b_i)_i$ cannot equal $f(a)$ for any $a$: if $a\in A_j$, its $j$th coordinate was specifically excluded. Thus there is no <surjection> from the <disjoint union> onto the product.

<cardinal number>[Cardinal comparability] under <choice> now implies the displayed strict inequality. Empty index <set> gives $0<1$ directly; empty individual $A_i$ cause no difficulty, since the corresponding coordinate excludes an empty image. This is the cardinal diagonal theorem, rather than the finitely branching tree result also bearing König's name.

The <generalized continuum hypothesis>[generalized continuum hypothesis] is
$$
\boxed{2^\kappa=\kappa^+\quad\text{for every infinite cardinal }\kappa.}
$$
Equivalently, for every <ordinal> $\alpha$, $2^{\aleph_\alpha}=\aleph_{\alpha+1}$. Here $2^\kappa$ is the <cardinality> of the <power set> of a <set> of <cardinality> $\kappa$, and $\kappa^+$ is the least strictly larger cardinal. The hypothesis says that no intermediate cardinal lies between an <infinite set> and its <power set>.

$$
\boxed{\aleph_\omega^{\aleph_\omega}=\aleph_{\omega+1}.}

The lower bound embeds binary-valued <functions> into $\kappa$-valued ones; the upper bound codes each value by a <subset> and combines the resulting pairs of indices. In particular the equality before applying the hypothesis is a <ZFC> equality.

Let $U_1\subseteq U_2$ be transitive classes, both interpreted with actual membership. A formula $\phi(\bar x)$ is upward absolute from $U_1$ to $U_2$ if for every tuple of parameters $\bar a$ from $U_1$,
$$
\boxed{(U_1,\in)\models\phi(\bar a)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad(U_2,\in)\models\phi(\bar a).}
$$
For proper classes this means the usual relativization of the fixed formula's <quantifiers>. Full absoluteness requires the implication in both directions. The qualification on parameters matters: the smaller class must contain every object named in the comparison.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

A sufficient syntactic condition is that $\phi$ be a <sigma-one formula in set theory>, written $\Sigma_1$: an existential block $\exists z_1\cdots\exists z_m\,\theta$ with $\theta$ bounded, meaning that each of its <quantifiers> is of the form $\forall t\in a$ or $\exists t\in a$. A <bounded formula in set theory>[bounded formula] is also called $\Delta_0$ and is absolute in both directions between transitive classes.

Prove the latter fact by <structural induction>. Atomic membership and equality have their actual meanings in both classes, and Boolean connectives preserve equivalence of truth. For a <bounded formula in set theory>[bounded quantifier] with bound $a\in U_1$, transitivity ensures that every element of $a$ belongs to $U_1$; thus its range is exactly the same in both classes, and induction applies to its matrix. This proves the bounded case. If the smaller class satisfies a $\Sigma_1$ formula, take its existential witnesses in $U_1$. They remain available in $U_2$, and bounded absoluteness preserves the matrix. Hence **$\Sigma_1$ formulas are upward absolute**. An arbitrary unbounded <universal quantifier> has no such witness-preservation argument.

Use the <bounded formula in set theory>[bounded formula]
$$
\boxed{\forall z\in x\ (z\in y).}
$$
It expresses $x\subseteq y$ with no unbounded <quantifier>. For parameters $x,y\in U_1$, transitivity puts every member of $x$ in $U_1$ already, and membership in $y$ has the same meaning in both classes. Thus the formula is $\Delta_0$ and is absolute in both directions, in particular upward absolute.

= Solution

Equality with the <union> is expressed by
$$
\boxed{\bigl(\forall z\in x\ \exists u\in y\ z\in u\bigr)\ \land\ \bigl(\forall u\in y\ \forall z\in u\ z\in x\bigr).}
$$
The first conjunct says that every member of $x$ lies in some member of $y$; the second gives the reverse inclusion. Every <quantifier> is bounded by a parameter or by an element of a previously bounded parameter. Therefore bounded absoluteness applies, proving full absoluteness and hence upward absoluteness of $x=\bigcup y$. This only compares the relation for existing parameters; it does not require either class to contain the <union> of every one of its <sets>.

= Solution

A bounded expression for the successor relation is
$$
\boxed{y\in x\ \land\ (\forall z\in y\ z\in x)\ \land\ (\forall z\in x\ (z\in y\lor z=y)).}
$$
The first two clauses put $y$ and all its elements into $x$, while the last forbids any additional member of $x$. <Extensionality> identifies this exactly with $x=y\cup\{y\}$. Since the formula is $\Delta_0$, it is absolute in both directions between the transitive classes and therefore upward absolute. This bounded description also justifies the successor computations used earlier in the rank-stage <Replacement> counterexample.

To prove that <power-set equality is not upward absolute>, use the standard <constructible universe theorem>: $L$ is a transitive <inner model> of <ZF> containing every <ordinal>, and <set> rank is absolute between $L$ and $V$. Since $V\ne L$, some rank stage contains a nonconstructible <set>. Let $\alpha$ be the least <ordinal> with $V_\alpha\nsubseteq L$. It is not zero; it cannot be a limit, since a <union> of earlier stages contained in $L$ is still contained in $L$. Thus $\alpha=\beta+1$ and $V_\beta\subseteq L$.

We need not merely this inclusion, but that $y=V_\beta$ itself belongs to $L$. Inside $L$, <ZF> constructs its own rank stage $(V_\beta)^L$, which is a <set> of $L$. By transitivity and <set-theoretic absoluteness>[rank absoluteness],
$$
(V_\beta)^L=V_\beta\cap L=V_\beta.
$$
Consequently $y\in L$. Let $x=(\mathcal P(y))^L$, the <power set> computed in $L$. Then $x\in L$ and $L\models x=\mathcal P(y)$.

By minimality there is $z\in V_{\beta+1}\setminus L$. Externally $z\subseteq V_\beta=y$, but $z\notin x$, because every member of the <set> $x\in L$ is in the <transitive class> $L$. Hence $V\not\models x=\mathcal P(y)$. Taking $U_1=L$ and $U_2=V$ proves **<power-set equality is not upward absolute>, even between transitive models of <ZF>**.

There is no contradiction with absoluteness of $z\subseteq y$: that bounded relation agrees whenever both parameters are present in the smaller class. <power-set equality is not upward absolute>[Power-set equality] quantifies over all <subsets>, including a new $z$ absent from the smaller class. The step proving $V_\beta\in L$ is essential; inclusion in a class alone would not imply membership in it.
