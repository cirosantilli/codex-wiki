Use these standard <ultrafilter> facts: a space is <compact> if and only if every <ultrafilter> on it converges; the image <ultrafilter> under a map is an <ultrafilter>; and a convergent <ultrafilter> contains every <neighborhood> of its limit.

Let $X=\prod_{\lambda\in\Lambda}X_\lambda$ with all factors <compact>. An empty factor makes $X$ empty and hence <compact>. Otherwise, given an <ultrafilter> $\mathcal U$ on $X$, push it forward by each <product projection> to obtain an <ultrafilter> $\mathcal U_\lambda$ on $X_\lambda$. Choose a limit $x_\lambda$ of each, and put $x=(x_\lambda)_\lambda$. A basic product <neighborhood> of $x$ is an intersection of finitely many inverse images of coordinate <neighborhoods>. Each inverse image belongs to $\mathcal U$, so their <finite intersection> does too. Thus $\mathcal U$ converges to $x$, proving **<compactness> of the product**. Uniqueness of coordinate limits is not needed, so the factors need not be <Hausdorff>.

For <local compactness>, suppose only the factors indexed by a finite set $F$ may be noncompact. At $x\in X$ choose, for each $\lambda\in F$, a <compact> <neighborhood> $K_\lambda$ of $x_\lambda$, with an <open neighborhood> $U_\lambda\subseteq K_\lambda$. For other indices put $K_\lambda=X_\lambda$. The <compact> product $\prod_\lambda K_\lambda$ contains the open product <neighborhood> restricting just the finitely many coordinates in $F$ to $U_\lambda$. Hence every <point> has a <compact> <neighborhood> and **the product is locally <compact>**.

The unrestricted assertion is **false**. Each factor of $\mathbb R^{\mathbb N}$ is locally <compact>, but its product is not. Any basic <neighborhood> restricts only finitely many coordinates. Choose a free coordinate $j$; its projection of that <neighborhood> is all of $\mathbb R$. A <compact> <neighborhood> containing it would consequently have <compact> image equal to $\mathbb R$, impossible. This shows directly that no <point> has a <compact> <neighborhood>.

For $x$ in a <compact Hausdorff space> $K$, its <quasi-component> $Q$ is the intersection of all <clopen sets> containing $x$. It is closed and therefore <compact>. Suppose $Q=A\sqcup B$ is a <separation of a topological space> into two nonempty relatively <closed sets>, with $x\in A$. The sets $A,B$ are <compact> and hence closed in $K$. <Normality of a compact Hausdorff space> gives disjoint <open neighborhoods> $U,V$ of $A,B$.

The <compact set> $K\setminus(U\cup V)$ is disjoint from $Q$. For each <point> $y$ of that set choose a <clopen> $C_y$ containing $x$ but not $y$. Finitely many complements cover $K\setminus(U\cup V)$, so their corresponding intersection $C$ is <clopen>, contains $Q$, and is contained in $U\cup V$. Now $C\cap U$ is <clopen> in $C$, hence <clopen> in $K$, and contains $x$ while missing $B$. This contradicts the definition of $Q$. Thus **$Q$ is <connected>**. Any <connected set> containing $x$ lies in every <clopen> <neighborhood> of $x$, so the <connected component> of $x$ equals $Q$.

For the second assertion, $D$ is closed in the ambient <Hausdorff space> $X$, and is itself <compact> <Hausdorff>. Let $C$ be a <connected component> of $D$ disjoint from $\partial_XD$. Choose $p\in C$. By the first assertion $C$ is the <quasi-component> of $p$ in $D$. For each $y\in\partial_XD$ choose a <clopen> subset of $D$ containing $p$ but not $y$. <Compactness> of $\partial_XD\subseteq D$ gives a <finite intersection> $H$ of these sets which contains $C$ and avoids the whole <boundary>. Thus $H\subseteq\operatorname{int}_XD$. It is closed in $X$, because it is closed in the <closed set> $D$, and is open in $X$, because it is relatively open in $D$ and contained in its <interior>. It is nonempty and proper, contradicting <connectedness> of $X$. Hence **every <connected component> of $D$ meets its <boundary>**.

Finally suppose a closed subset $A$ of the square has the <point>-separating <clopen> property specified in the paper. Every nonempty relatively open subset of the square contains a nontrivial <line segment>: it contains an <interior> <point> of the square and a small ball about that <point>. If such a segment lay in $A$, a <clopen> subset of $A$ separating its endpoints would induce a <separation of a topological space> of the <connected> segment. This is impossible. Therefore $A$ contains no nonempty relatively open subset of the square. Being closed, it is **<nowhere dense> in the square**, and also <nowhere dense> in $\mathbb R^2$.

Write $\alpha X=X\cup\{\infty_X\}$. Its <open sets> are the open subsets of $X$ together with sets $\alpha X\setminus K$, where $K$ is a <compact subset> of $X$. <Compact subsets> of the <Hausdorff space> $X$ are closed. This fact, and finite unions of <compact sets>, verify the topology axioms, including intersections of an old <open set> with a <neighborhood> of infinity. The inclusion $h_1:X\to\alpha X$ is a <topological embedding> with open image.

An <open cover> of $\alpha X$ has a member containing infinity; its complement is <compact> in $X$ and therefore is covered by finitely many other members. This proves <compactness>. Two <points> of $X$ can be separated in $X$. A <point> $x$ and infinity can be separated by an <open neighborhood> $U$ of $x$ contained in a <compact> <neighborhood> $K$, and $\alpha X\setminus K$. Thus $\alpha X$ is <Hausdorff>. Since $X$ is noncompact, every <neighborhood> of infinity meets $X$, so the embedded copy of $X$ is <dense>. This proves it is a **<Hausdorff> compactification**.

For a <proper map>, use the standard locally <compact> <Hausdorff> convention: it is <continuous> and inverse images of <compact sets> are <compact>. Define
$$
\boxed{\alpha f(h_1(x))=h_2(f(x)),\qquad\alpha f(\infty_X)=\infty_Y.}
$$
An open subset of $Y$ has open inverse image in $X$, hence in $\alpha X$. A basic <neighborhood> $\alpha Y\setminus K$ of infinity has inverse image $\alpha X\setminus f^{-1}(K)$, open because $f^{-1}(K)$ is <compact>. Hence $\alpha f$ is <continuous> and the diagram commutes. Any <continuous> map from a <compact space> to a <Hausdorff space> is proper: a <compact subset> of the target is closed, its inverse image is closed in the <compact> domain and therefore <compact>. This proves the required properness.

The last requested extension **does not exist**, even as a <continuous> map. Both sequences $h_1(0,n)$ and $h_1(1,n)$ tend to $\infty_{\mathbb R^2}$, since they eventually leave every <compact subset> of the plane. Their prescribed images would be the constant sequences $h_2(0)$ and $h_2(1)$. <Continuity> would force both distinct <points> to equal $g(\infty_{\mathbb R^2})$, contradicting uniqueness of limits in the <Hausdorff> compactification of the line. The original <product projection> is not proper, as its inverse image of a singleton is an unbounded line.

If $\mathcal B$ is a <countable> <topological basis> of $X$, then $\{B\cap Y:B\in\mathcal B\}$ is a <countable> <topological basis> of the subspace $Y$. Indeed every subspace-<open neighborhood> is $Y\cap U$ for an open $U$ of $X$, and a suitable basic set lies between its <point> and $U$. This proves the first assertion.

**<Urysohn's metrisation theorem>:** every <second-countable space> that is also a <T3 space> is a <metrizable space>. Here <regularity of a topological space> allows a <neighborhood> of a <point> to be shrunk to an <open neighborhood> whose <closure> is still inside the original one.

We first prove the separation facts needed in a complete proof. <Second countability> implies the <Lindelof> property: for each basic set contained in some member of an <open cover> choose one such member; these countably many members cover. In particular closed subspaces are <Lindelof>. For disjoint <closed sets> $F,G$, <regularity of a topological space> and this property give open covers $F\subseteq\bigcup_nU_n$, $G\subseteq\bigcup_nV_n$ with $\overline U_n\cap G=\varnothing$ and $\overline V_n\cap F=\varnothing$. Define
$$
U=\bigcup_n\left(U_n\setminus\bigcup_{j\leq n}\overline V_j\right),\qquad V=\bigcup_n\left(V_n\setminus\bigcup_{j\leq n}\overline U_j\right).
$$
These sets are open and contain $F,G$ respectively. They are disjoint: if a <point> belonged to a term indexed by $n$ in the first union and by $m$ in the second, the term with the larger index would have removed the <closure> of the other covering set. Thus the space is a <normal space>.

For completeness, <Urysohn's lemma> supplies <continuous> separating functions in a <normal topological space>. Its construction is as follows. Given disjoint closed $F,G$, choose <open sets> $O_r$ for dyadic $r\in[0,1]$, with $F\subseteq O_0$, $O_1=X\setminus G$, and $\overline O_r\subseteq O_s$ whenever $r<s$. Start by shrinking a <neighborhood> of $F$ inside $X\setminus G$, and insert the successive dyadic sets by the <closed-neighborhood shrinking in a normal space>. Define
$$
f(x)=\inf\bigl(\{r:x\in O_r\}\cup\{1\}\bigr).
$$
Then $f=0$ on $F$, $f=1$ on $G$, and $0\leq f\leq1$. The identities $\{f<a\}=\bigcup_{r<a}O_r$ and $\{f>a\}=\bigcup_{a<r<1}(X\setminus\overline O_r)$ prove <continuity> for $0<a<1$, with the endpoint cases immediate.

Let $\{B_n\}$ be a <countable> <topological basis>. For each pair $(n,m)$ with $\overline B_n\subseteq B_m$, choose a <continuous function> $f_{nm}:X\to[0,1]$ equal to one on $\overline B_n$ and zero outside $B_m$. These form a <countable> family. Given $x\in O$ open, choose $B_m$ with $x\in B_m\subseteq O$, shrink a <neighborhood> of $x$ inside $B_m$, and then choose $B_n$ inside that smaller <neighborhood>. Thus
$$
x\in\{f_{nm}>1/2\}\subseteq B_m\subseteq O.
$$
Consequently these functions generate the original topology. They also separate distinct <points>: use the $T_1$ <open set> $X\setminus\{y\}$ when $x\ne y$. Enumerate them as $f_j$, padding by zero functions if necessary. The map $x\mapsto(f_j(x))_j$ is a <topological embedding> into the <countable> cube. Pulling back its <metric>, or directly using
$$
\boxed{d(x,y)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2^{-j}|f_j(x)-f_j(y)|,}
$$
gives a compatible <metric>. The fact that the functions distinguish distinct points proves positivity for distinct <points>, the <triangle inequality> is termwise, and the uniformly bounded tails show that this <metric> induces exactly the topology generated by the coordinates. Empty and singleton spaces are trivially <metrizable spaces>. This proves the theorem.

For the application, a <separable> <metric space> has a <countable> <topological basis> consisting of balls with rational positive radii about a <countable> <dense> set, and it is a <T3 space>. The given product theorems make a <countable> product of these spaces <second countable> and a <T3 space>. Urysohn's theorem makes it a <metrizable space>. Finally every <second countable> space is <separable>: choose a <point> from each nonempty basic <open set>; the resulting <countable> set meets every nonempty <open set>. Hence the product is **<separable> and a <metrizable space>**.

The <transitive closure> $\operatorname{TC}(a)$ is the least <transitive set> containing $a$ as a subset. Put $a_0=a$, $a_{n+1}=\bigcup a_n$ and define
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{TC}(a)=\bigcup_{n<\omega}a_n.}
$$
Recursion and <Replacement> give the set of iterates, and Union gives its union. It contains $a=a_0$ as a subset. If $u\in v\in\operatorname{TC}(a)$, then $v\in a_n$ for some $n$, so $u\in a_{n+1}\subseteq\operatorname{TC}(a)$; hence it is transitive.

If $T$ is transitive with $a\subseteq T$, induction gives $a_n\subseteq T$ for every $n$, because $\bigcup T\subseteq T$. Therefore $\operatorname{TC}(a)\subseteq T$. This proves minimality and uniqueness. If the alternative convention asks for the least <transitive set> containing $a$ as an element, that set is $\operatorname{TC}(\{a\})$.

An $\in$-<isomorphism> is a <bijection> $f:X\to Y$ such that $x\in x'$ if and only if $f(x)\in f(x')$ for all $x,x'\in X$. When $Y$ is transitive, <surjectivity> gives the stronger recursive description
$$
f(x)=\{f(u):u\in x\cap X\}\qquad(x\in X).
$$
Indeed every member of $f(x)$ belongs to $Y$ because it is a <transitive set> and so has a preimage in $X$; the <isomorphism> property identifies exactly which preimages occur.

If $f,g$ are two such maps, possibly to different <transitive sets>, form $B=\{x\in X:f(x)\ne g(x)\}$. If $B$ were nonempty, <Foundation> would give $x\in B$ with $x\cap B=\varnothing$. All predecessors $u\in x\cap X$ then satisfy $f(u)=g(u)$, and the recursive descriptions force $f(x)=g(x)$, a contradiction. Therefore **the map and its transitive range are unique**.

The necessary and sufficient condition is that $\langle X,\in\rangle$ be **<well-founded> and extensional as a relation on $X$**. Well-foundedness says every nonempty $A\subseteq X$ has a member with no $\in$-predecessor in $A$. The <extensional relation> condition says distinct elements $x,x'\in X$ have distinct predecessor sets $x\cap X$, $x'\cap X$. Sufficiency is the <Mostowski collapse theorem>.

For necessity, an <isomorphism> transfers well-foundedness from its transitive range, where <Foundation> applies. For extensionality, if $x\cap X=x'\cap X$, the recursive descriptions give $f(x)=f(x')$, and <injectivity> gives $x=x'$. Notice that ambient <axiom of extensionality> alone does not establish this internal predecessor condition; for example $X=\{\{0\},\{2\}\}$ can have distinct elements with no predecessors in $X$.

A <cardinal number> is an <initial ordinal>: an <ordinal> not in <bijection> with any smaller <ordinal>. In $ZFC$ every set has the <cardinality> of a unique such <ordinal>.

For an infinite <cardinal> $\kappa$, its <cofinality> is the least <cardinality> of an unbounded subset of the <ordinal> $\kappa$. Equivalently,
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)=\min\{\theta:\exists\text{ a cofinal map }f:\theta\to\kappa\}.}
$$
A subset is unbounded when no <ordinal> below $\kappa$ bounds all its elements. A <cofinal> sequence can be taken increasing, and $\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)$ is an infinite <regular cardinal>.

<König theorem for cardinal numbers> says that for families of <cardinals> $\kappa_i<\lambda_i$ indexed by a set $I$,
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i\in I}\kappa_i<\prod_{i\in I}\lambda_i.}
$$
The sum is the <cardinality> of the disjoint union of sets $A_i$ of sizes $\kappa_i$, and the product is the <cardinality> of $\prod_iB_i$ for sets of sizes $\lambda_i$.

Consider any function $F:\bigsqcup_iA_i\to\prod_iB_i$. For each $i$, the set of $i$th coordinates of images of <points> from the $i$th block has <cardinality> at most $\kappa_i<\lambda_i$. Choose $b_i\in B_i$ outside it. Then $b=(b_i)_i$ is not the image of any <point>: a <point> from block $i$ has the wrong $i$th coordinate. Thus there is no <surjection> from the sum set onto the product set. <Cardinal> comparability in $ZFC$ makes the inequality strict in the indicated direction. For the empty index set the statement is $0<1$, also true. This is the diagonal argument underlying the inequality.

First derive the standard consequence of <König theorem for cardinal numbers> that every infinite <cardinal> $\rho$ satisfies
$$
\rho^{\operatorname{cf}(\rho)}>\rho.
$$
Let $\theta=\operatorname{cf}(\rho)$ and take <cofinal> <ordinals> $\alpha_i<\rho$, $i<\theta$. Put $\rho_i=\max(1,|\alpha_i|)<\rho$. Their sum is $\rho$: it is at least the size of the union of the <cofinal> <ordinals> and at most $\rho\cdot\theta=\rho$. Choose the next larger <cardinal> $\rho_i^+\leq\rho$. <König theorem for cardinal numbers> gives
$$
\rho=\sum_{i<\theta}\rho_i<\prod_{i<\theta}\rho_i^+\leq\rho^\theta.
$$
This also covers $\rho=\aleph_0$, using finite <cardinals> and their next larger finite <cardinals>.

Suppose $\lambda=\kappa^\mu$ with both $\kappa,\mu$ infinite. Infinite <cardinal> multiplication gives
$$
\lambda^\mu=(\kappa^\mu)^\mu=\kappa^{\mu\cdot\mu}=\lambda.
$$
If $\operatorname{cf}(\lambda)\leq\mu$, monotonicity of exponentiation would contradict the preceding strict inequality for $\rho=\lambda$. Thus $\operatorname{cf}(\lambda)>\mu\geq\aleph_0$. This direction does not require <GCH>.

Conversely assume <GCH> and $\operatorname{cf}(\lambda)>\aleph_0$. Every function $\omega\to\lambda$ has bounded range. For each infinite <cardinal> $\nu<\lambda$, <GCH> gives
$$
\nu^{\aleph_0}\leq(2^\nu)^{\aleph_0}=2^{\nu\cdot\aleph_0}=2^\nu=\nu^+\leq\lambda.
$$
Finite bounded ranges are also covered by the bound $\aleph_0^{\aleph_0}\leq\aleph_1\leq\lambda$. Counting functions according to an <ordinal> bound on their range now gives $\lambda^{\aleph_0}\leq\lambda\cdot\lambda=\lambda$. Constant functions give the reverse inequality. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\lambda=\lambda^{\aleph_0},}
$$
which is the required representation with $\kappa=\lambda$, $\mu=\aleph_0$. This proves both directions.

For a set $A$, its <definable power set> is
$$
\operatorname{Def}(A)=\{\{x\in A:\langle A,\in\rangle\models\varphi(x,p_1,\ldots,p_n)\}:\varphi\text{ a formula},\ p_1,\ldots,p_n\in A\}.
$$
Only finitely many parameters occur in each definition. Satisfaction for the set structure is definable, so this specifies a uniformly definable class function assigning a set to every set $A$, with $\operatorname{Def}(A)\subseteq\mathcal P(A)$. Every subset definable over the structure with its parameters occurs. The full set $A$ itself occurs by the formula $x=x$. If $A$ is transitive, then $A\subseteq\operatorname{Def}(A)$, since any $p\in A$ is defined as a subset of $A$ by $x\in p$. In that case $\operatorname{Def}(A)$ is transitive as well: a member of one of its elements lies in $A$, hence in $\operatorname{Def}(A)$.

The <constructible hierarchy> and universe are
$$
\boxed{L_0=\varnothing,\quad L_{\alpha+1}=\operatorname{Def}(L_\alpha),\quad L_\lambda=\bigcup_{\beta<\lambda}L_\beta,\quad L=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\operatorname{Ord}}L_\alpha.}
$$
The displayed properties prove by induction that levels are transitive and increasing, so $L$ is transitive. It contains every <ordinal>: inductively $L_\alpha\cap\operatorname{Ord}=\alpha$, and the subset of $L_\alpha$ consisting of <ordinals> is definable there, giving $\alpha\in L_{\alpha+1}$. Ordinalhood is absolute here because the levels are transitive. Both the level function and membership in $L$ are definable in $ZF$. This is Gödel's <constructible universe>, which can contain fewer subsets than the full universe.

The inclusion test is absolute for the transitive level $L_\beta$, since all members of $x$ and $a$ lie there. The right side is consequently a subset definable over $L_\beta$ with parameter $a$, so
$$
\boxed{S\in\operatorname{Def}(L_\beta)=L_{\beta+1}\subseteq L.}
$$
For $x\in L$, transitivity of the <constructible universe> makes internal and ambient subsethood of $a$ agree. Hence $L$ satisfies that $S$ consists exactly of all its subsets of $a$. This is the <Power Set> Axiom in $\langle L,\in\rangle$. It is not the assertion that every ambient subset of $a$ belongs to $L$.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Use the <constructible universe theorem> that $L$ satisfies $ZFC$ and <GCH>. In particular, internally it has a <bijection>
$$
b:\omega_1^L\longrightarrow\mathcal P(\omega)^L.
$$
This <bijection> belongs to $L$ and is also an actual <bijection> in the ambient universe, because its domain, range and graph are transitive-model sets with the same membership relation.

We must compare the <ordinals> rather than assume <cardinality> is absolute. Every <ordinal> below $\omega_1^L$ is <countable> in $L$, and its witnessing enumeration belongs to the ambient universe too. Thus every such <ordinal> is <countable> externally, proving $\omega_1^L\leq\omega_1$. Consequently the ambient <cardinality> of the constructible reals is at most $\aleph_1$.

If every subset of $\omega$ were constructible, the displayed <bijection> would enumerate the full ambient <power set> using at most $\aleph_1$ <points>. This contradicts the assumption $2^{\aleph_0}=\aleph_2$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\exists X\subseteq\omega\quad X\notin L.}
$$
The argument permits $\omega_1^L$ to be strictly smaller than the ambient $\omega_1$, exactly the <cardinal>-absoluteness issue highlighted in the source.
