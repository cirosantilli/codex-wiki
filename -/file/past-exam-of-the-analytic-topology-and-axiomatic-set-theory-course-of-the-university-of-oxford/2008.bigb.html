A <subset> $D$ is <dense> when its <closure> is the whole <topological space>, equivalently when every nonempty <open set> intersects $D$. <Countable> here permits a <finite set>, so finite <topological spaces> and the empty <topological space> fit the same definition.

A <topological space> is <Lindelöf> if every <open cover> has a <countable> subcover. We also prove the requested equivalence for <metrizable spaces> here, after the two source definition leaves.

Choose a compatible <metric> $d$. First suppose that $D$ is a <countable> <dense subset>. The balls $B(d_0,q)$ with centers $d_0\in D$ and positive rational radii $q$ form a <countable> <topological basis>. Indeed, if $x\in U$ and $B(x,\epsilon)\subset U$, choose $d_0\in D$ with $d(x,d_0)<\epsilon/3$ and a rational $q$ between $d(x,d_0)$ and $\epsilon-d(x,d_0)$. The <triangle inequality> gives $x\in B(d_0,q)\subset U$.

For any <open cover>, retain the <topological basis> elements which are contained in some member of the cover, and choose one covering member for each such <topological basis> element. There are only countably many choices, and the resulting subfamily covers the <topological space>, since each point has a <topological basis> <neighborhood> inside a covering member. Thus a <separable> <metrizable space> is <Lindelöf>.

Conversely, suppose the <metric space> is <Lindelöf>. For each positive <integer> $n$, the balls $B(x,1/n)$ cover it, so select a <countable> subcover with centers in a <countable set> $D_n$. The <union> $D=\bigcup_{n\ge1}D_n$ is <countable> and <dense>: if $B(x,\epsilon)$ is a nonempty <open> ball, choose $n$ with $1/n<\epsilon$. One of the selected radius-$1/n$ balls contains $x$, so its center lies in $D\cap B(x,\epsilon)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{metrizable: separable}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\text{Lindelöf}.}
$$
The <metric> hypothesis is used in producing uniform small-ball covers and a <countable> ball <topological basis>; the equivalence need not hold for general <topological spaces>.

Since $D$ is a <discrete subset> in the <subspace topology>, for each $d\in D$ choose an <open set> $U_d\subseteq Z$ with $U_d\cap D=\{d\}$. Closedness of $D$ makes $Z\setminus D$ <open>. The family

\{Z\setminus D\}\cup\{U_d:d\in D\}

is therefore an <open cover> of $Z$. A <countable> subcover exists by the <Lindelöf> property. Each point $d$ can be covered only by its own $U_d$ among this family: the <complement> misses all of $D$, and every other $U_e$ meets $D$ only at $e$. $$
\boxed{D\text{ is countable}.}
$$
Both closedness and discreteness matter: they provide the <open> <complement> and the single-point traces, respectively.

The <lower limit topology> on the <real line> is the <lower limit topology>. Every nonempty basic interval $[a,b)$ contains a rational number, so the rational numbers are a <countable> <dense subset>. By the preceding product argument, $\mathbb Q^2$ is <dense> in the square, proving its <topological separability>.

Consider the uncountable anti-diagonal
$$
D=\{(t,-t):t\in\mathbb R\}.
$$
It is a <discrete subset>: the basic rectangle $[t,t+\epsilon)\times[-t,-t+\epsilon)$ intersects $D$ only at $(t,-t)$, because both coordinates have increased weakly and their sum can remain zero only if neither increases.

It is also <closed>. At a point $(u,v)$ off the anti-diagonal, $u+v\ne0$. If this sum is positive, every sufficiently small lower-limit rectangle based at $(u,v)$ still has positive coordinate sum. If it is negative, choose $\epsilon>0$ with $2\epsilon<-(u+v)$; then $[u,u+\epsilon)\times[v,v+\epsilon)$ has negative coordinate sum throughout. These <neighborhoods> avoid $D$, so its <complement> is <open>.

If the square were <Lindelöf>, its <closed discrete subset> $D$ would be <countable> by part (c), contradicting its <bijection> with $\mathbb R$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{the Sorgenfrey plane is separable but not Lindelöf}.}
$$

For each point which belongs to at least one $U_t$, its membership-index <set> is a nonempty <subset> of $[0,1]$, so has an infimum in $[0,1]$. Assigning the value one to the other points therefore defines $f:X\to[0,1]$ everywhere.

For $0<r\le1$,
$$
\{x:f(x)<r\}=\bigcup_{t\in D,\ t<r}U_t.
$$
An index $t<r$ gives $f(x)\le t<r$. Conversely if the infimum is below $r$, some membership index must be below $r$. Thus this strict sublevel <set> is <open>.

For $0\le r<1$ we claim
$$
\{x:f(x)>r\}=\bigcup_{t\in D,\ t>r}(X\setminus\overline{U_t}).
$$
If $x\notin\overline{U_t}$ and $t>r$, then $x$ cannot belong to any $U_s$ with $s\le t$, since $U_s\subseteq U_t$ for $s<t$. Hence $f(x)\ge t>r$. Conversely, if $f(x)>r$, density of $D$ supplies $r<t<u<f(x)$ with $t,u\in D$. Since $x\notin U_u$ and $\overline{U_t}\subseteq U_u$, one has $x\notin\overline{U_t}$. This proves the identity, and its right-hand side is <open>.

The remaining sublevel and superlevel <sets>, for thresholds outside these ranges, are either empty or all of $X$. Preimages of <open> rays are consequently <open>, and these rays generate the usual real <topology>. Therefore **$f$ is <continuous>**. The <closure> inclusion is what supplies openness of the strict superlevel <sets>; mere nesting would not suffice.

<Urysohn's lemma> states that, in a <normal space>, disjoint <closed> <subsets> $A,B$ can be separated by a <continuous function> $u:X\to[0,1]$ with $u=0$ on $A$ and $u=1$ on $B$.

First use the shrinking consequence of the <separation> property of a <normal space>: if a <closed set> $F$ lies in an <open set> $G$, there is an <open> $W$ with $F\subseteq W\subseteq\overline W\subseteq G$. To prove it, separate the disjoint <closed sets> $F$ and $X\setminus G$ by disjoint <open> <neighborhoods> $W,V$. Then $\overline W\subseteq X\setminus V\subseteq G$.

Let $D$ be the dyadic rationals in $[0,1]$. <Set> $U_1=X\setminus B$, and shrink around $A$ to choose $U_0$ with $A\subseteq U_0\subseteq\overline{U_0}\subseteq U_1$. At each subsequent dyadic stage, insert between each adjacent pair $s<t$ an <open set> $U_{(s+t)/2}$ such that
$$
\overline{U_s}\subseteq U_{(s+t)/2}\subseteq\overline{U_{(s+t)/2}}\subseteq U_t.
$$
The shrinking lemma applies because the first <set> is <closed> and contained in the last <open set>. Induction gives $\overline{U_s}\subseteq U_t$ for all dyadic $s<t$. Part (a) now constructs a <continuous> $u$. Each point of $A$ lies in $U_0$, so has value zero. Each point of $B$ lies in none of the $U_t$, so has value one. This proves the lemma.

For the embedding, let $I=C(X,[0,1])$, a <set> of <continuous functions>, and define the evaluation map
$$
e:X\to\mathbb R^I,\qquad e(x)=(u(x))_{u\in I}.
$$
Every coordinate is <continuous>, so $e$ is <continuous> in the <product topology>. The <Hausdorff> assumption makes singletons <closed>. For distinct points, <Urysohn's lemma> applied to their singletons gives a coordinate with different values, so $e$ is <injective>.

To check the inverse <topology>, take $x\in O$ with $O$ <open>. Apply the lemma to $\{x\}$ and $X\setminus O$, choosing a coordinate $u$ with $u(x)=1$ and $u=0$ outside $O$. Then
$$
x\in u^{-1}((1/2,3/2))\subseteq O.
$$
This is the preimage of an <open> coordinate cylinder under $e$. Thus every original <neighborhood> is detected by coordinate <neighborhoods>, and $e^{-1}:e[X]\to X$ is <continuous>. **The evaluation map is a <topological embedding>.**

A <Hausdorff compactification> of $X$ is a pair $(h,Y)$ in which $Y$ is <compact> <Hausdorff> and $h:X\to Y$ is a <topological embedding> with <dense> image.

For a locally <compact> <Hausdorff> noncompact <topological space>, form $X^*=X\cup\{\infty\}$ with one new point. Keep all <open> <subsets> of $X$, and take the <neighborhoods> of infinity to contain <sets>
$$
\{\infty\}\cup(X\setminus K),\qquad K\subseteq X\text{ compact}.
$$
<Compact subsets> of a <Hausdorff space> are <closed>. Finite <unions> of <compact> <sets> are <compact>, so finite <intersections> of these basic <neighborhoods> are again of the same form; their <intersections> with ordinary <open sets> are <open> in $X$. This specifies a <topology> restricting to the original one on $X$.

It is <compact>: from an <open cover> choose a member containing infinity. Its <complement> is contained in some <compact> $K$, and finitely many more members cover that <compact> remainder. It is <Hausdorff>: two points of $X$ are separated as before; to separate $x\in X$ from infinity, choose an <open> <neighborhood> $U$ of $x$ lying inside a <compact> <neighborhood> $K$. The <open set> $U$ and the infinity <neighborhood> $X^*\setminus K$ are disjoint.

The inclusion of $X$ is an embedding. Every basic <neighborhood> of infinity meets $X$, because otherwise its <compact> <complement> $K$ would equal all of $X$, contrary to noncompactness. Thus $X$ is <dense> in $X^*$. This proves that **$X^*$ is the <one-point compactification>**.

A <proper filter> $\mathcal F$ on $X$ is a nonempty family of <subsets>, <closed> under finite <intersection> and passage to <supersets>, with $\varnothing\notin\mathcal F$. An <ultrafilter> is a maximal <proper filter>, equivalently one which contains, for each $A\subseteq X$, exactly one of $A$ and $X\setminus A$. The <pushforward filter> is
$$
f_*\mathcal F=\{B\subseteq Y:f^{-1}[B]\in\mathcal F\}.
$$
<Inverse images> preserve <intersections> and <complements>. Thus it is a <proper filter>, and the either-a-set-or-its-complement criterion shows it is an <ultrafilter> whenever $\mathcal F$ is one.

<Convergence of a filter> $\mathcal F\to x$ means that every <neighborhood> of $x$ belongs to $\mathcal F$. A mapping is <continuous> exactly when $\mathcal F\to x$ implies $f_*\mathcal F\to f(x)$ for all <proper filters> and points. The forward implication follows from <inverse images> of <neighborhoods>. For the converse apply the criterion to the <neighborhood> <proper filter> at $x$: each <inverse image> of a <neighborhood> of $f(x)$ must be a <neighborhood> of $x$, which is <continuity> at $x$.

A <topological space> is <compact> exactly when every <ultrafilter> converges to at least one point; equivalently every <proper filter> has a cluster point. For completeness, in a <compact space> the <closed sets> $\overline A$, $A\in\mathcal U$, have the <finite intersection property>, hence a common point $x$. Every <neighborhood> $N$ of $x$ belongs to the <ultrafilter>. Indeed, if $N$ did not, choose an <open> $V$ with $x\in V\subseteq N$. Then $V$ would not belong either, so $X\setminus V$ would belong. That <set> is <closed> and excludes $x$, contradicting the choice of $x$ in every <closure>. Conversely, if an <open cover> has no finite subcover, its <closed> <complements> generate a <proper filter>. An <ultrafilter> extension cannot converge anywhere, because each point has a covering <neighborhood> whose <complement> belongs to that <ultrafilter>. This proves the compactness criterion using the permitted extension principle.

Now suppose $b\in\overline{f[A]}$. The family
$$
\{A\cap f^{-1}[V]:V\text{ is an open neighborhood of }b\}
$$
is a <filter base> of nonempty <sets>: <closure> guarantees each <intersection> is nonempty, and finite <intersections> correspond to <intersections> of <neighborhoods>. Extend its generated <proper filter> to an <ultrafilter> $\mathcal H$. It contains $A$ and every $f^{-1}[V]$, so $f_*\mathcal H\to b$.

Conversely, if such an <ultrafilter> exists, each <neighborhood> $V$ of $b$ has $f^{-1}[V]\in\mathcal H$. Its <intersection> with $A\in\mathcal H$ is nonempty. Thus $V$ meets $f[A]$, proving
$$
\boxed{b\in\overline{f[A]}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\exists\mathcal H\text{ ultrafilter}:A\in\mathcal H,\ f_*\mathcal H\to b.}
$$
This part includes the unnumbered <proper filter> definitions and criteria preceding the printed subparts.

That limit is unique because $Y$ is <Hausdorff>: distinct limits would have disjoint <neighborhoods> both belonging to the same <proper filter>. Therefore $\Lambda$ is well-defined. Since $f[X]$ is <dense>, every $b\in Y$ lies in $\overline{f[X]}$. Part (a), with $A=X$, supplies an <ultrafilter> mapping to $b$, proving <surjectivity>.

Suppose disjoint <subsets> have disjoint image <closures>. If $\mathcal H\ne\mathcal K$, choose $A\in\mathcal H$ not in $\mathcal K$; the <ultrafilter> criterion gives $X\setminus A\in\mathcal K$. Part (a) gives
$$
\Lambda(\mathcal H)\in\overline{f[A]},\qquad
\Lambda(\mathcal K)\in\overline{f[X\setminus A]}.

The two <closures> are disjoint by assumption, so the limits differ. Thus $\Lambda$ is <injective>.

Use the <Stone-Čech extension property>: a discrete <topological space> $X$ embeds densely into a <compact> <Hausdorff space> $\beta X$, and every <continuous map> from $X$ to a <compact> <Hausdorff space> extends continuously to $\beta X$. Apply it to the <characteristic function> $\chi_A:X\to\{0,1\}$ of an arbitrary <subset> $A$; discreteness makes it <continuous>. Its extension $\widetilde\chi_A$ has <clopen> fibers, and
$$
\overline A^{\,\beta X}=\widetilde\chi_A^{-1}(1).
$$
Indeed that fiber is <closed> and contains $A$, while its openness and density of $X$ show that every point in the fiber is approached by points of $A$.

If $A$ and $B$ are disjoint, the extension of $\chi_A$ is one on $\overline A$ and zero on $\overline B$, so these <closures> are disjoint. The inclusion $X\to\beta X$ therefore satisfies the criterion from part (b), and its ultrafilter-limit map is both <surjective> and <injective>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\Upsilon(X)\longleftrightarrow\beta X,\qquad
\mathcal H\longmapsto\text{the unique limit of its image filter}.}
$$
This is a <bijection> of underlying <sets>; identifying the compatible <topology> on the <ultrafilter> <topological space> would give the familiar stronger <homeomorphism> statement, but is not needed for the requested conclusion.

In first-order <set> theory a <class in set theory> is a definable collection, written schematically $\{x:\theta(x,\vec p)\}$ using a <logical formula> and <set> parameters. A <class term> is an abbreviation for such a defining condition; it does not add a new kind of object to the <quantifiers> of <ZF>. A <class in set theory> is proper if it is not the collection of elements of any <set>.

Define the <cumulative hierarchy> by <transfinite recursion>:
$$
V_0=\varnothing,\qquad V_{\alpha+1}=\mathcal P(V_\alpha),\qquad
V_\lambda=\bigcup_{\alpha<\lambda}V_\alpha\quad(\lambda\text{ a nonzero limit ordinal}),
$$
and let $V=\{x:\exists\alpha\text{ ordinal }(x\in V_\alpha)\}$. Without <foundation> this denotes the well-founded part produced by the hierarchy; it must not simply be assumed to contain every ambient <set>. <Transfinite recursion> on <ordinals> and the relevant <set> constructions remain available in <ZF without foundation>.

Prove transitivity by <transfinite induction>. The empty stage is transitive. Suppose $T=V_\alpha$ is transitive. Then each member of $T$ is a <subset> of $T$, so $T\subseteq\mathcal P(T)$. Also $\mathcal P(T)$ is transitive: if $u\in v\in\mathcal P(T)$, then $u\in T$ and, by transitivity of $T$, $u\subseteq T$, so $u\in\mathcal P(T)$. At a limit stage, a <union> of <transitive sets> is transitive. This proves transitivity at every stage.

Finally transitivity of $V_\alpha$ itself implies that every member is a <subset> of $V_\alpha$, giving
$$
\boxed{V_\alpha\subseteq\mathcal P(V_\alpha)=V_{\alpha+1}.}
$$
These successive inclusions and the limit definition also imply $V_\alpha\subseteq V_\beta$ whenever $\alpha\le\beta$. The argument has not used <foundation>. This leaf includes the unnumbered definitions preceding the source's three claims.

A bounded <quantifier> has the form $\exists u\in v$ or $\forall u\in v$, abbreviating $\exists u(u\in v\wedge\cdots)$ or $\forall u(u\in v\to\cdots)$. A $\Sigma_0$ <logical formula>, also called $\Delta_0$, is built from atomic membership and equality <logical formulas> using Boolean connectives and only bounded <quantifiers>. For example $z\subseteq y$ abbreviates the bounded <logical formula> $\forall u\in z\,(u\in y)$. <Quantifiers> are bounded by an existing variable or parameter, not by an unrestricted <class term> such as the <power set> of a parameter unless that <set> has separately been supplied as a parameter.

Use the strict-order convention appropriate to the membership relation. A bounded <logical formula> is
$$
\begin{aligned}
TO(x)\ \equiv\;&(\forall u\in x)\,\neg(u\in u)\\
&\wedge(\forall u\in x)(\forall v\in x)(\forall w\in x)
\bigl((u\in v\wedge v\in w)\to u\in w\bigr)\\
&\wedge(\forall u\in x)(\forall v\in x)
\bigl(u=v\vee u\in v\vee v\in u\bigr).
\end{aligned}
$$
The first clause is irreflexivity, the second transitivity, and the third comparability of distinct elements. Irreflexivity and transitivity exclude $u\in v\in u$, so distinct elements cannot be ordered in both directions. These are exactly the axioms of a <strict total order> on the <set> $x$, with $\in$ as its order relation.

Every displayed <quantifier> is bounded by $x$, so **$TO(x)$ is $\Sigma_0$**. The <logical formula> does not assert that $x$ is transitive as a <set>, and no such additional assertion is needed to say that the restriction of membership to $x$ is a <total order>.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

There are two ways for a <set> to fail to be the <power set> of $y$: it may contain an object which is not a <subset> of $y$, or omit a <subset> of $y$. A single existential <logical formula> expressing these alternatives is
$$
\boxed{\phi(x,y)\equiv\exists z\Bigl[
\bigl(z\in x\wedge(\exists u\in z)\,u\notin y\bigr)
\vee\bigl(z\notin x\wedge(\forall u\in z)\,u\in y\bigr)\Bigr].}
$$
Its matrix is bounded, so this is $\Sigma_1$. Its negation is precisely the first-order power-set specification
$$
\forall z\bigl(z\in x\ \longleftrightarrow\ (\forall u\in z)\,u\in y\bigr).
$$

We prove the absoluteness fact needed to exclude a logically equivalent $\Sigma_1$ <logical formula>. If $M\subseteq N$ are <transitive sets> and all parameters lie in $M$, bounded <logical formulas> have the same truth value in $\langle M,\in\rangle$ and $\langle N,\in\rangle$. Atomic equality and membership agree because both structures use actual membership. Boolean operations preserve agreement. In a bounded <quantifier> over $v\in M$, transitivity ensures that every element of $v$ already lies in $M$, so the two structures quantify over exactly the same elements of $v$. Induction on <logical formulas> proves the claim. It follows that $\Sigma_1$ <logical formulas> are upward absolute: witnesses in $M$ remain available in $N$, and their bounded matrix retains its truth value.

Now use a finite counterexample, so no existence of a transitive model of <ZF> is assumed. Let $M=4=\{0,1,2,3\}$, the <Finite von Neumann ordinal>, and let $N=V_4$. These are <transitive sets> with $M\subseteq N$. <Set> $y=2$ and $x=3$, both elements of $M$. Inside $M$, the <subsets> of $2$ which are available as elements of the domain are exactly $0,1,2$; the remaining domain element $3$ is not a <subset> of $2$. Thus $M$ satisfies the displayed power-set specification with $x=3$.

Inside $N$, however, the <set> $\{1\}$ is also present and is a <subset> of $2$, while $\{1\}\notin3$. Thus the same specification is false in $N$. If $\neg\phi$ were logically equivalent to a $\Sigma_1$ <logical formula>, its truth in $M$ would transfer upward to $N$, a contradiction. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\neg\phi\text{ is not logically equivalent to any }\Sigma_1\text{ formula}.}
$$
This proves the requested unrestricted logical non-equivalence. The finite structures are not being claimed to satisfy all of <ZF>; logical equivalence must hold in them as well as in any models of a stronger theory.

<König theorem for cardinal numbers> states that if $\kappa_i<\lambda_i$ for each index $i\in I$, then
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i\in I}\kappa_i<\prod_{i\in I}\lambda_i.}
$$
Represent each <cardinal> by its <initial ordinal>, let $A=\{(i,\alpha):i\in I,\alpha<\kappa_i\}$, and let $B=\prod_{i\in I}\lambda_i$.

First there is an <injection> $A\to B$: encode $(i,\alpha)$ by the <function> which is zero away from $i$ and takes value $\alpha+1$ at $i$. The nonzero coordinate identifies $i$, and its value identifies $\alpha$. It is valid because $\alpha+1\le\kappa_i<\lambda_i$; an index with $\kappa_i=0$ contributes no domain points.

For strictness suppose a surjection $F:A\to B$ existed. At coordinate $i$, the values $F(i,\alpha)(i)$ for $\alpha<\kappa_i$ occupy fewer than $\lambda_i$ possibilities. Choose $b_i$ as the least <ordinal> below $\lambda_i$ absent from that <set>. The resulting $b=(b_i)\in B$ differs from $F(i,\alpha)$ at coordinate $i$ for every $(i,\alpha)\in A$, contradicting <surjectivity>.

An <injection> $B\to A$ would produce a surjection $A\to B$ by inverting on its image and assigning the zero <function> elsewhere. It is therefore impossible. The <injection> in the first direction and impossibility of the reverse give the strict <cardinal> inequality. This diagonal argument uses the canonical well-orders of the specified <cardinals> to choose missing coordinate values, rather than an unexplained arbitrary choice at that step.

For an infinite <cardinal> regarded as an <ordinal>, its <cofinality> $\theta=\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)$ is the least <cardinality> of a <cofinal subset> of $\kappa$. Equivalently there is a family $(\alpha_i)_{i<\theta}$ of <ordinals> below $\kappa$ whose <supremum> is $\kappa$, and no smaller indexed family has that property. Since an infinite <initial ordinal> is a <limit ordinal>, $\theta$ is infinite and in particular nonzero.

Use the diagonal mechanism of <König theorem for cardinal numbers> directly. Suppose $F:\kappa\to{}^{\theta}\kappa$ were <surjective>. For each $i<\theta$, the <set>
$$
E_i=\{F(\alpha)(i):\alpha<\alpha_i\}
$$
has <cardinality> at most $|\alpha_i|<\kappa$. Choose the least $b_i\in\kappa\setminus E_i$. The resulting <function> $b:\theta\to\kappa$ is not in the range: for any $\alpha<\kappa$, <cofinality> supplies $i$ with $\alpha<\alpha_i$, and then $b_i\ne F(\alpha)(i)$.

Thus no such surjection exists. Constant <functions> provide an <injection> $\kappa\to{}^{\theta}\kappa$, and an <injection> in the reverse direction would give a surjection by the same inverse-and-default construction as in part (i). Consequently
$$
\boxed{\kappa^{\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)}>\kappa.}
$$
This argument also handles $\kappa=\aleph_0$, when <cofinal> initial segments are finite; it does not incorrectly assume that smaller <cardinals> themselves must form a <cofinal> sequence in every infinite <cardinal>.

In the usual arithmetic under the <axiom of choice> of <cardinal numbers>, take the singular strong-limit <cardinal>
$$
\kappa=\beth_\omega=\sup_{n<\omega}\beth_n,\qquad
\beth_0=\aleph_0,\quad\beth_{n+1}=2^{\beth_n}.
$$
<Cantor's theorem> makes this sequence strictly increasing. It is <countable> and <cofinal> in its <supremum>, so $\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)\le\aleph_0$. No finite <subset> is <cofinal> in this <limit ordinal>, hence $\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)=\aleph_0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{2^{\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)}=2^{\aleph_0}=\beth_1<\beth_\omega=\kappa.}
$$
This construction needs no assumption such as the <Continuum hypothesis>. The conventional use of <cardinals> here includes well-orderability of these <power sets>, supplied by the <axiom of choice>. It is a separate setting from the explicit failure of well-orderability hypothesized in question 8; one must not impose choice on that later hypothesis.

For a <set> $M$, let $\operatorname{Def}(M)$ consist of the <subsets> of $M$ definable over the structure $\langle M,\in\rangle$ by a <first-order formula> with finitely many parameters from $M$. It is a <set> contained in $\mathcal P(M)$: satisfaction for a <set> structure and the <set> of <logical formula> codes give a uniform <set> construction of these definable <subsets>. If $M$ is transitive, every $a\in M$ is one such <subset>, defined over $M$ by $z\in a$, so $M\subseteq\operatorname{Def}(M)$.

Define the <constructible hierarchy> by <transfinite recursion>:
$$
L_0=\varnothing,\qquad L_{\alpha+1}=\operatorname{Def}(L_\alpha),\qquad
L_\lambda=\bigcup_{\alpha<\lambda}L_\alpha\quad(\lambda\text{ a nonzero limit ordinal}).
$$
Gödel's <constructible universe> is the definable <class in set theory>
$$
\boxed{L=\{x:\exists\alpha\text{ ordinal }(x\in L_\alpha)\}.}
$$
At successor stages only <subsets> definable over the previous stage are admitted, in contrast to the full power-set construction of the <cumulative hierarchy>. The stages are transitive and increasing, as permitted in the question, and their <union> contains every <ordinal>. Finite parameters are essential in the definition of $\operatorname{Def}$.

Fix $a\in L$. In the ambient universe form the <set>
$$
S=\{y\in\mathcal P(a):y\in L\}
$$
by <power set> and <separation>, using definability of $L$. This is the collection which must serve as the <power set> of $a$ inside $L$; it need not be the entire ambient <power set>.

For each $y\in S$, let $\alpha_y$ be the least <ordinal> with $y\in L_{\alpha_y}$. **Ambient <replacement> is used to collect the <ordinals> $\alpha_y$ into a <set>.** Their <supremum>, enlarged if necessary to include a stage containing $a$, gives an <ordinal> $\beta$ such that $a\in L_\beta$ and every $y\in S$ belongs to $L_\beta$.

Because $L_\beta$ is transitive and $a\in L_\beta$, the bounded property $y\subseteq a$ for $y\in L_\beta$ has its actual meaning when evaluated in that structure: every element of $y$ already lies in $L_\beta$, and membership is unchanged. It follows that
$$
S=\{y\in L_\beta:\langle L_\beta,\in\rangle\models y\subseteq a\}.
$$
This is a definable <subset> of $L_\beta$ using the parameter $a$, so $S\in\operatorname{Def}(L_\beta)=L_{\beta+1}\subseteq L$. Every member of $L$ which is a <subset> of $a$ is in $S$, and every member of $S$ is such a <subset>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathcal P^{L}(a)=\mathcal P^{V}(a)\cap L\in L,}
$$
which is exactly the power-set axiom in $\langle L,\in\rangle$. The <replacement> step is indispensable to this proof: definability over one stage requires first bounding all the constructible <subsets> by one common stage.

Every constructible <set> is well-orderable: the <constructible hierarchy> has a canonical well-order, obtained by ordering the stage of first appearance, <logical formula> codes and the already ordered finite parameter lists. Its restriction to any <set> belonging to $L$ is a well-order in $L$ and remains a well-order in the ambient universe. This does not imply that arbitrary ambient <sets> are well-orderable.

If $a\in L$ is finite, all its <subsets> are finite combinations of its elements and hence constructible. Moreover the <finite set> of all those <subsets> is constructible by the <finite set> operations available in $L$. Thus the full ambient <power set> $\mathcal P(a)$ belongs to $L$.

Conversely, suppose $a\in L$ is infinite and that its full ambient <power set> belonged to $L$. A well-order of $a$ supplies an <injection> $j:\omega\to a$ by taking its first countably many elements. The map
$$
\mathcal P(\omega)\to\mathcal P(a),\qquad u\longmapsto j[u]
$$
is <injective>. But $\mathcal P(a)\in L$ would make that <set> well-orderable in the ambient universe, and pulling its well-order back along this <injection> would well-order $\mathcal P(\omega)$. This contradicts the hypothesis. Hence
$$
\boxed{a\in L:\qquad\mathcal P^{V}(a)\in L\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a\text{ is finite}.}
$$
The superscript emphasizes that the source's final claim concerns the ambient <power set>. The internal <power set> $\mathcal P^L(a)$ exists in $L$ for every $a\in L$ by part (ii), including infinite ones.
