= Solution
{parent=a}

The <cosmological principle> permits a <Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker metric>. The <Friedmann equation>, with vanishing <cosmological constant>, is
$$
H^2=\frac{8\pi G}{3}\bar\rho_m-\frac{k}{a^2}.
$$
For separately conserved <pressureless matter>, the <cosmological continuity equation> gives $\bar\rho_m=\bar\rho_{m0}a^{-3}$: its rest <energy> is conserved while its physical <volume> grows as $a^3$. Define the present <critical density> and <matter density parameter> by
$$
\rho_{\mathrm{crit},0}=\frac{3H_0^2}{8\pi G},\qquad \Omega=\frac{\bar\rho_{m0}}{\rho_{\mathrm{crit},0}}.
$$
Evaluating the <Friedmann equation> today gives $-k=H_0^2(1-\Omega)>0$. Thus the model has negative <spatial curvature of an FLRW universe>; the second contribution to $H^2$ represents that <spatial curvature of an FLRW universe>, rather than another material fluid. Taking the expanding branch gives
$$
\boxed{H(a)=H_0\sqrt{\Omega a^{-3}+(1-\Omega)a^{-2}}.}
$$
Here $0<\Omega<1$. At early times, $a\ll\Omega/(1-\Omega)$, <matter domination> gives $\dot a\simeq H_0\sqrt\Omega\,a^{-1/2}$, hence, measuring <cosmic time> from the <Big Bang>,
$$
\boxed{a(t)\simeq\left(\frac32H_0\sqrt\Omega\,t\right)^{2/3}.}
$$
At late times the $a^{-2}$ term dominates the <Friedmann equation>. This <curvature-dominated universe> has $\dot a\to H_0\sqrt{1-\Omega}$ and therefore
$$
\boxed{a(t)\sim H_0\sqrt{1-\Omega}\,t.}
$$
The late expansion approaches the coasting <Milne model>. The early limit belongs to this idealized matter-only model; including <radiation in cosmology> would alter the sufficiently early evolution.

= Solution

Introduce the positive <dimensionless quantity> $x=(1-\Omega)a/\Omega$ and the time scale $A=\Omega/[H_0(1-\Omega)^{3/2}]$. The <Friedmann equation> gives the separable relation
$$
dt=\frac{da}{aH(a)}=A\sqrt{\frac{x}{1+x}}\,dx.
$$
For $x=(\cosh\theta-1)/2=\sinh^2(\theta/2)$, $\theta\geq0$, the <hyperbolic function> identities give
$$
\sqrt{\frac{x}{1+x}}\,dx
=\tanh\frac\theta2\,\frac{\sinh\theta}{2}\,d\theta
=\frac{\cosh\theta-1}{2}\,d\theta.
$$
Choose the <constant of integration> so that $t=0$ at $a=0$. The resulting <parametric equation> for the <open matter-dominated Friedmann solution> is
$$
\boxed{t=\frac{\Omega}{2H_0(1-\Omega)^{3/2}}(\sinh\theta-\theta),\qquad a=\frac{\Omega}{2(1-\Omega)}(\cosh\theta-1).}
$$
For $\theta\ll1$, the <Taylor series> give
$$
t=\frac{\Omega\theta^3}{12H_0(1-\Omega)^{3/2}}[1+O(\theta^2)],\qquad a=\frac{\Omega\theta^2}{4(1-\Omega)}[1+O(\theta^2)].
$$
Eliminating $\theta$ yields $a^{3/2}\simeq(3/2)H_0\sqrt\Omega\,t$, exactly the early <matter-dominated universe> result. For $\theta\gg1$, $\sinh\theta\sim\cosh\theta\sim e^\theta/2$, whereas $\theta/e^\theta\to0$. The <ratio> of the two parametric expressions tends to $a/t=H_0\sqrt{1-\Omega}$, recovering the <curvature-dominated universe> result including its coefficient.

The <matter density parameter> at general <scale factor> is
$$
\Omega_m(a)=\frac{\Omega a^{-3}}{\Omega a^{-3}+(1-\Omega)a^{-2}}=\frac1{1+x}.
$$
The reference to the definition of $x$ in the printed part points to the wrong equation number: the needed definition is the substitution in part (b). Write $B=(1-\Omega)/\Omega$, so $x=Ba$, $d/da=B\,d/dx$, and
$$
H=\frac{H_0(1-\Omega)^{3/2}}{\Omega}\,x^{-3/2}(1+x)^{1/2}.
$$
Expanding the <matter growth equation as a function of scale factor> first gives
$$
\frac{d^2\delta}{da^2}+\left(\frac3a+\frac{d\ln H}{da}\right)\frac{d\delta}{da}-\frac{3\Omega_m(a)}{2a^2}\delta=0.
$$
Using $d\ln H/dx=-3/(2x)+1/[2(1+x)]$ and dividing by $B^2$, we obtain
$$
\boxed{\delta''+\frac12\left(\frac3x+\frac1{1+x}\right)\delta'-\frac{3\delta}{2x^2(1+x)}=0,}
$$
where these primes denote <derivatives> with respect to $x$. For the proposed <linear cosmological density perturbation> $\delta_1=(1+x)^{1/2}x^{-3/2}$,
$$
\frac{\delta_1'}{\delta_1}=-\frac{2x+3}{2x(1+x)},\qquad
\frac{\delta_1''}{\delta_1}=\frac{8x^2+24x+15}{4x^2(1+x)^2}.
$$
The left side of the <differential equation>, divided by $\delta_1$, has numerator
$$
(8x^2+24x+15)-(4x+3)(2x+3)-6(1+x)=0
$$
over the common denominator $4x^2(1+x)^2$. This proves the result directly. Also $\delta_1'/\delta_1<0$ for every $x>0$, so it is a **decaying mode**:
$$
\boxed{\delta_1\sim x^{-3/2}\quad(x\to0),\qquad \delta_1\sim x^{-1}\quad(x\to\infty).}
$$
It is proportional to the <Hubble parameter>, an instance of the <Hubble parameter as a solution of the dust growth equation>.

To construct the regular <open dust linear growth mode>, use <reduction of order>, writing $\delta=\delta_1u$ and denoting the coefficient of $\delta'$ by $p(x)$. Since $\delta_1$ solves the <homogeneous linear differential equation>, its terms cancel and leave
$$
u''+\left(2\frac{\delta_1'}{\delta_1}+p\right)u'=0,\qquad p=\frac3{2x}+\frac1{2(1+x)}.
$$
The <integrating factor> for this first-order equation in $u'$ is $\delta_1^2e^{\int p\,dx}$, so
$$
u'=C\frac{e^{-\int p\,dx}}{\delta_1^2}
=C\frac{x^{-3/2}(1+x)^{-1/2}}{(1+x)x^{-3}}
=C\left(\frac{x}{1+x}\right)^{3/2}.
$$
Choose $C=1$ and the lower <integration> endpoint zero. This selects the mode regular at early times without adding a multiple of $\delta_1$:
$$
\boxed{\delta_2(x)=\sqrt{\frac{1+x}{x^3}}\int_0^x\left(\frac{y}{1+y}\right)^{3/2}\,dy.}
$$
Its <Wronskian> with $\delta_1$ is $\delta_1\delta_2'-\delta_1'\delta_2=x^{-3/2}(1+x)^{-1/2}>0$, proving <linear independence>.

No explicit <antiderivative> is needed for the limits. For $x\ll1$, the integrand is $y^{3/2}[1+O(y)]$, whence its <integral> is $(2/5)x^{5/2}[1+O(x)]$. For $x\gg1$, the integrand tends to one, so its <integral> divided by $x$ tends to one, while $x\delta_1\to1$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\delta_2\sim\frac25x\quad(x\to0),\qquad \delta_2\to1\quad(x\to\infty).}
$$
Early <matter domination> permits <gravitational instability> to amplify the <density contrast> in proportion to the <scale factor>. Later, the <matter density parameter> tends to zero and <Hubble friction> suppresses further amplification. Indeed, for $a\propto t$, neglecting the matter source in the <linear matter perturbation growth equation> gives $\ddot\delta+2\dot\delta/t\simeq0$, whose modes are a constant and $t^{-1}$. These agree with the late limits of the two exact modes.

In the <integral> from part (d), set $y=\sinh^2u$. Its upper endpoint is $u_x=\operatorname{arsinh}\sqrt x$, equal to $\theta/2$ in part (b). The <hyperbolic function> identity
$$
\left(\frac{y}{1+y}\right)^{3/2}dy
=2\frac{\sinh^4u}{\cosh^2u}\,du
=2(\cosh^2u-2+\operatorname{sech}^2u)\,du
$$
gives the <antiderivative>
$$
I(x)=\left[\frac12\sinh(2u)-3u+2\tanh u\right]_0^{u_x}
=\sqrt{x(1+x)}+2\sqrt{\frac{x}{1+x}}-3\operatorname{arsinh}\sqrt x.
$$
Multiplication by $\delta_1$ therefore yields
$$
\boxed{\delta_2(x)=1+\frac3x-3\sqrt{\frac{1+x}{x^3}}\operatorname{arsinh}\sqrt x
=1+\frac3x-3\sqrt{\frac{1+x}{x^3}}\ln(\sqrt x+\sqrt{1+x}).}
$$
At small $x$, cancellation between the terms leaves the <Taylor series> $\delta_2=2x/5-8x^2/35+16x^3/105+O(x^4)$. At large $x$, $\operatorname{arsinh}\sqrt x=\tfrac12\ln(4x)+O(x^{-1})$, so the correction to one is $[3-\tfrac32\ln(4x)]/x+O(\ln x/x^2)$. Both checks agree with part (d); the <integral> or the small-$x$ <Taylor series> is preferable to subtracting large nearly equal terms in a numerical evaluation near zero.

The <Helmholtz decomposition> separates the <peculiar velocity> into a <gradient field> and a <transverse vector field>:
$$
\mathbf v=\nabla V+\mathbf v_\perp,\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf v_\perp=0.
$$
With decaying or periodic spatial boundary conditions this is equivalently the <Fourier transform> projection, for $k\ne0$,
$$
v_{\perp i}(\mathbf k)=\left(\delta_{ij}-\frac{k_ik_j}{k^2}\right)v_j(\mathbf k).
$$
The <transverse projection operator> annihilates a <gradient>, because $(\delta_{ij}-k_ik_j/k^2)k_j=0$. Both forces in the linear <cosmological Euler equation> are <gradients>, with spatially homogeneous coefficients. Applying the <transverse projection operator> therefore gives
$$
\dot{\mathbf v}_\perp+H\mathbf v_\perp=0.
$$
Since $H=\dot a/a$, this is $d(a\mathbf v_\perp)/dt=0$, and hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbf v_\perp(\mathbf x,t)=\frac{a(t_i)}{a(t)}\mathbf v_\perp(\mathbf x,t_i).}
$$
At this order the scalar pressure and gravitational forces cannot generate <vorticity>; an initially vanishing <transverse vector field> remains zero. Its nonzero initial value is damped by <cosmic expansion>.

Use the source's $+---$ <metric signature> and $x^0=\eta$, with <conformal time> <derivatives> denoted by primes. For this <vector cosmological perturbation>, the <metric tensor> and <inverse metric> through first order are
$$
g_{00}=a^2,\quad g_{0i}=0,\quad g_{ij}=-a^2(\delta_{ij}-J_{ij}),\qquad
g^{00}=a^{-2},\quad g^{0i}=0,\quad g^{ij}=-a^{-2}(\delta_{ij}+J_{ij}).
$$
Spatial indices on perturbations are raised with the background <Kronecker delta>. The <Christoffel symbol> formula is
$$
\Gamma^\alpha_{\beta\gamma}=\frac12g^{\alpha\sigma}(\partial_\beta g_{\sigma\gamma}+\partial_\gamma g_{\sigma\beta}-\partial_\sigma g_{\beta\gamma}).
$$
Because $a=a(\eta)$ only, it gives
$$
\boxed{\Gamma^0_{00}=\frac12a^{-2}(2aa')=\mathcal H,\qquad \Gamma^0_{0i}=\frac12a^{-2}\partial_i(a^2)=0.}
$$
For the other requested <Christoffel symbols>, only the time <derivative> of the spatial <metric tensor> contributes:
$$
\Gamma^0_{ij}=-\frac12a^{-2}\partial_\eta g_{ij}
=\frac12a^{-2}\left[2aa'(\delta_{ij}-J_{ij})-a^2J'_{ij}\right].
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\Gamma^0_{ij}=\mathcal H(\delta_{ij}-J_{ij})-\frac12J'_{ij}.}
$$
Finally contract the first upper and first lower spatial indices, summing over $i$:
$$
\gamma^i_{ij}=\frac12(\partial_iJ_{ij}+\partial_jJ_{ii}-\partial_iJ_{ij})=\frac12\partial_jJ_{ii}.
$$
Since $J_{ii}=2\partial_iF_i=0$ by the <transverse vector field> condition on $\mathbf F$,
$$
\boxed{\gamma^i_{ij}=0.}
$$
The useful <Christoffel symbol> traces are consequently $\Gamma^\mu_{\mu0}=4\mathcal H$ and $\Gamma^\mu_{\mu j}=0$ to first order: the first uses $J'_{ii}=0$ and the second uses the contraction just proved.

Define the <momentum density> $q_i=(\bar\rho+\bar P)v_{\perp i}$, lowering its spatial label with $\delta_{ij}$, so the mixed <stress-energy tensor> obeys $T^i{}_0=q_i$ and $T^0{}_i=-q_i$. All products of $J_{ij}$ or its <derivatives> with $q_i$ are second order and must be dropped in <linear cosmological perturbation theory>.

For $\nu=0$, the three terms in <stress-energy conservation> are
$$
\partial_\mu T^\mu{}_0=\bar\rho'+\partial_iq_i,\qquad
\Gamma^\mu_{\mu\sigma}T^\sigma{}_0=4\mathcal H\bar\rho,\qquad
\Gamma^\sigma_{\mu0}T^\mu{}_\sigma=\mathcal H\bar\rho-3\mathcal H\bar P.
$$
Here the trace $J'_{ii}$ vanishes, $\Gamma^i_{00}=\Gamma^0_{i0}=0$, and $\partial_iq_i=(\bar\rho+\bar P)\partial_iv_\perp^i=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\bar\rho'+3\mathcal H(\bar\rho+\bar P)=0.}
$$
For $\nu=i$, spatial <pressure> gradients vanish because this is a purely <vector cosmological perturbation>. The first two terms are
$$
\partial_\mu T^\mu{}_i=-q_i',\qquad
\Gamma^\mu_{\mu\sigma}T^\sigma{}_i=-4\mathcal Hq_i.
$$
In the remaining contraction, the two background-connection times <momentum density> terms cancel:
$$
\Gamma^0_{ji}T^j{}_0+\Gamma^j_{0i}T^0{}_j=\mathcal Hq_i-\mathcal Hq_i=0.
$$
The other terms are $\Gamma^0_{0i}\bar\rho=0$ and $-\bar P\Gamma^j_{ji}=0$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{[(\bar\rho+\bar P)v_{\perp i}]'+4\mathcal H(\bar\rho+\bar P)v_{\perp i}=0.}
$$
Expanding this <conservation law> and using the background <cosmological continuity equation> gives the equivalent <peculiar velocity> equation, when $\bar\rho+\bar P\ne0$,
$$
\boxed{v_{\perp i}'+\left(\mathcal H+\frac{\bar P'}{\bar\rho+\bar P}\right)v_{\perp i}=0.}
$$

The $\nu=0$ result is the background <cosmological perfect-fluid continuity equation>. For a comoving region of physical <volume> proportional to $a^3$, it is precisely
$$
d(\bar\rho a^3)=-\bar P\,d(a^3),
$$
expressing local <conservation of energy> including the <pressure-volume work> done during <cosmic expansion>. A <transverse vector field> has no velocity <divergence>, and the vector spatial <metric tensor> has no trace perturbation, so neither produces a first-order compression or density change.

The spatial result is <vector-fluid momentum dilution> and integrates immediately:
$$
(a^4q_i)'=0,\qquad
\boxed{q_i(\eta)=q_i(\eta_i)\left(\frac{a_i}{a}\right)^4.}
$$
Thus, for any specified background <perfect fluid>,
$$
\boxed{v_{\perp i}(\eta)=v_{\perp i}(\eta_i)\left(\frac{a_i}{a}\right)^4\frac{\bar\rho_i+\bar P_i}{\bar\rho+\bar P}.}
$$
A statement about $\mathbf q$ alone does not fix the velocity scaling independently of the <equation of state>. For constant <equation-of-state parameter> $w\ne-1$, the <cosmological continuity equation> gives $\bar\rho\propto a^{-3(1+w)}$ and hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbf v_\perp\propto a^{3w-1}.}
$$
For <pressureless matter>, $w=0$ and $\mathbf v_\perp\propto a^{-1}$, exactly as in the Newtonian calculation in part (a). For <radiation in cosmology>, $w=1/3$ and the vector <peculiar velocity> remains constant, although its <momentum density> still falls as $a^{-4}$. More generally, for a barotropic <perfect fluid> with <cosmological adiabatic sound speed> $c_a^2=\bar P'/\bar\rho'$, the equivalent equation is $\mathbf v_\perp'+\mathcal H(1-3c_a^2)\mathbf v_\perp=0$. The Newtonian damping law describes nonrelativistic inertia; it need not apply to a relativistic <perfect fluid>. A pure <cosmological constant> has $\bar\rho+\bar P=0$ and supplies no such fluid velocity degree of freedom.

The <cosmic microwave background> consists of relic <photons> from the early hot, nearly thermal <photon-baryon fluid>. Before <cosmological recombination>, abundant free <electrons> repeatedly scatter <photons> by <Thomson scattering>. Their <mean free path> $\lambda_\gamma=(n_e\sigma_T)^{-1}$ is short and their <Thomson scattering> rate $n_e\sigma_Tc$ is much greater than the <Hubble parameter>. An earlier <photon> therefore cannot stream directly to us: subsequent scattering repeatedly changes its direction.

As <cosmic expansion> cools the plasma to about $T\simeq3000\,\mathrm K$, <electrons> and <protons> combine into neutral <hydrogen>. The free-electron abundance drops rapidly, the scattering <optical depth> to the observer becomes of order one, and <photon decoupling> occurs. Since the <cosmic microwave background> <temperature> scales as $T=T_0(1+z)$, with $T_0\simeq2.725\,\mathrm K$, this occurs at $z\simeq1100$. The small <baryon-to-photon ratio> delays neutralization to well below $13.6\,\mathrm{eV}/k_B$: the high-energy tail contains enough <photons> to ionize <hydrogen> until the <temperature> is much lower than the <hydrogen binding energy> expressed as a <temperature>. The observed <cosmic microwave background> is primarily this pre-existing radiation undergoing its last scattering; its nearly <blackbody spectrum> is then preserved by <cosmological redshift>.

For the requested estimate take a flat <matter-dominated universe>, $a(t)=(t/t_0)^{2/3}$. Restore $c$ in distances. The <comoving particle horizon> at <cosmological recombination> and the <comoving radial distance> to the <cosmic microwave background last-scattering surface> are
$$
r_{\mathrm{hor},*}=c\int_0^{t_*}\frac{dt}{a(t)}=3ct_0\sqrt{a_*},\qquad
\chi_*=c\int_{t_*}^{t_0}\frac{dt}{a(t)}=3ct_0(1-\sqrt{a_*}).
$$
The proper <particle horizon> is $a_*r_{\mathrm{hor},*}=3ct_*$ and the <angular diameter distance> is $D_A=a_*\chi_*$. Therefore the <angular particle-horizon size in a power-law universe>, taking one horizon radius as the transverse length, is
$$
\boxed{\theta_{\mathrm{hor}}\simeq\frac{r_{\mathrm{hor},*}}{\chi_*}=\frac1{\sqrt{1+z_*}-1}\simeq0.0311\,\mathrm{rad}\simeq1.78^\circ.}
$$
The angular diameter of a patch of that radius is about $3.56^\circ$. The factor of two is a radius-versus-diameter convention; either gives a horizon scale of only a few degrees. This is a <particle horizon>, rather than the smaller <sound horizon>. Including early <radiation in cosmology> changes the numerical estimate but preserves its order of magnitude.

The <horizon problem> is that the <cosmic microwave background> has almost the same <temperature> across the whole sky, including pairs of regions whose past light cones do not overlap in this noninflating model. Local thermal equilibration cannot explain that observation within this history. A sufficiently long period of <cosmic inflation> makes the <comoving Hubble radius> decrease: a region once small enough to share a causal past can expand to encompass our entire observed <cosmic microwave background last-scattering surface>. After <reheating>, it can be homogeneous across many later <particle horizon> patches. The matter-only estimate integrates from a decelerating <Big Bang>; extending the earlier history by <cosmic inflation> changes that causal conclusion.

= Solution

The four contributions have distinct physical origins. The intrinsic <photon temperature perturbation> is $\delta_\gamma/4$: a local <blackbody radiation> <energy density> satisfies $\rho_\gamma\propto T^4$, so its first-order fractional perturbation is four times the fractional <temperature> perturbation. The endpoint <cosmological Newtonian potential> term $\psi_*$ is the <gravitational redshift> between the emitting plasma and the observer; escaping a negative potential well lowers the measured <photon> <energy>. Together with the intrinsic contribution it produces the ordinary <Sachs-Wolfe effect>. The term $-\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\mathbf v_*$ is the <Doppler CMB anisotropy>, caused by the plasma's line-of-sight <peculiar velocity> at last scattering, with the source's direction convention.

Finally, $2\int d\chi\,\psi'$ is the <integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect>. A <photon> passing through a static potential well gains <energy> on entry and loses it on exit, with no net shift. When the potential evolves while the <photon> crosses, the two shifts fail to cancel. The factor two is the sum of the temporal and spatial scalar-potential contributions when <anisotropic stress> is negligible, so the two potentials are equal. The prime is a <partial derivative> at fixed <comoving coordinate>; it is not the total <derivative> along the light ray.

For a low-<cosmological redshift> galaxy sample, the intrinsic, endpoint-potential and last-scattering <Doppler CMB anisotropy> terms are localized near $z\simeq1100$, whereas the <galaxy density contrast> is measured much nearer us. In the <Limber approximation> their radial kernels have no overlap, so their leading cross correlations vanish. The late <integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect> samples the same nearby evolving potentials as the <galaxies>, giving the desired <late-time CMB-matter cross correlation>. The early <integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect> near <matter-radiation equality> is likewise radially separated.

This is the leading linear, separated-kernel description intended here. The primary <cosmic microwave background anisotropy> and late <density contrast> share primordial initial conditions, so their exact large-angle correlation need not be identically zero outside the <Limber approximation>. Additional secondary effects, such as the <Sunyaev-Zeldovich effect>, can also correlate with <galaxies> in real maps; they are absent from the stipulated linear expression.

Let $p(z)$ be the normalized galaxy <probability density> per unit <cosmological redshift>, $\int p(z)\,dz=1$, and let $b_g$ be its linear <galaxy bias>. In a spatially flat background with $c=1$, $d\chi=dz/H(z)$. Thus the projected <galaxy density contrast> is
$$
\delta_g(\hat{\mathbf n})=\int dz\,p(z)b_g\delta(\chi(z)\hat{\mathbf n},z)
=\int d\chi\,H(z)p(z)b_g\delta(\chi\hat{\mathbf n},z),
$$
so its radial kernel is $q_g=Hbp$. The <cosmological Poisson equation> in <Fourier transform> variables gives
$$
-k^2\psi(\mathbf k,a)=4\pi Ga^2\bar\rho_m\delta(\mathbf k,a)
=\frac32H_0^2\Omega_m a^{-1}\delta(\mathbf k,a).
$$
Here $\Omega_m$ denotes today's <matter density parameter>, not its time-dependent value. For a growing <linear cosmological density perturbation>, write $\delta(\mathbf k,a)=D(a)\delta_{\rm ini}(\mathbf k)$, where $D$ is the <linear growth factor>, and define the <logarithmic linear growth rate> $f=d\ln D/d\ln a$. At fixed <comoving coordinate>,
$$
\left(\frac\delta a\right)'=\mathcal H(f-1)\frac\delta a=H(f-1)\delta.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\psi'(\mathbf k,z)=\frac{3H_0^2\Omega_m}{2k^2}H(z)[1-f(z)]\delta(\mathbf k,z).}
$$
In particular, $f=1$ during <matter domination> makes the linear potential constant and eliminates this <integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect>.

To use the supplied <Limber approximation> without hiding its scale dependence in a radial kernel, take $U=\delta$, $V=\psi'$, $q_g=Hbp$, and $q_T=2$. The corresponding three-dimensional <cross-power spectrum> is
$$
P_{\delta\psi'}(k,z)=\frac{3H_0^2\Omega_m}{2k^2}H(z)[1-f(z)]P(k,z),
$$
where $P$ is the <matter power spectrum>. Substitution gives
$$
C_\ell^{gT}=3H_0^2\Omega_m\int\frac{d\chi}{\chi^2}\,\frac{H^2(z)[1-f(z)]b_gp(z)}{k^2}P(k,z)\bigg|_{k=(\ell+1/2)/\chi}.
$$
The <Limber approximation> relation $k\chi=\ell+1/2$ cancels $\chi^{-2}k^{-2}$, yielding
$$
\boxed{C_\ell^{\delta_g\,\delta T/T}=\frac{3H_0^2\Omega_m}{(\ell+1/2)^2}\int d\chi\,H^2(z)[1-f(z)]b_gp(z)P\left(\frac{\ell+1/2}{\chi},z\right),\qquad z=z(\chi).}
$$
Both powers of $H$ are necessary: one converts the redshift selection to a distance kernel, and the other differentiates the potential in <conformal time>. The factor two in the <integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect> cancels the factor $1/2$ from the <cosmological Poisson equation>. For positive <galaxy bias> and $f<1$, the predicted correlation is positive: an overdense region whose negative potential becomes shallower gives a positive integrated <temperature> shift. The result assumes linear, scale-independent growth and the Newtonian <cosmological Poisson equation>; the <Limber approximation> becomes less accurate at the very lowest angular multipoles.

The $z\simeq0.3$ sample is expected to show the stronger late <integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect> correlation, for comparable <galaxy bias> and selection quality. In the stated flat <Lambda-CDM model>, neglecting <radiation in cosmology>,
$$
\Omega_m(z)=\frac{0.3(1+z)^3}{0.3(1+z)^3+0.7},\qquad
\Omega_m(0.3)\simeq0.485,\qquad \Omega_m(4)\simeq0.982.
$$
At $z\simeq4$ the universe is nearly <matter-dominated universe>, $D\propto a$ and $f\simeq1$, so the <cosmological Newtonian potential> $\psi\propto D/a$ is nearly constant. A <photon> then receives little net <integrated Sachs-Wolfe effect> shift. At $z\simeq0.3$, <dark energy> has substantially suppressed the <linear growth factor> relative to $a$, so $f<1$ and the potentials decay appreciably.

For a quantitative indication, the approximation $f\simeq\Omega_m(z)^{0.55}$ gives $1-f\simeq0.328$ at $z=0.3$ and $1-f\simeq0.0101$ at $z=4$. Thus fractional potential evolution, $d\ln|\psi|/d\ln a=f-1$, is much larger for the nearby sample. The high-<cosmological redshift> <matter power spectrum> also has a smaller growth amplitude. The entire correlation still depends on $H$, <galaxy bias>, $p(z)$, and the <matter power spectrum> at the sampled wavenumber; redshift alone does not fix a universal numerical ratio or guarantee an ordering at every multipole. The robust expectation is
$$
\boxed{\text{the low-redshift sample is the better tracer of the late integrated Sachs-Wolfe signal.}}
$$
