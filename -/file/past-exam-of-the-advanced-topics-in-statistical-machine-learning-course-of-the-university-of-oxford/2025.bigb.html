The <marginal likelihood> integrates the <likelihood function> against the <prior density>:
$$
\boxed{Z:=p(\mathcal D)=\int_{\mathbb R^d}p(\mathcal D\mid\theta)p(\theta)\,d\theta}.
$$
It is the normalizing constant in <Bayes' theorem>. For the subsequent <posterior distribution> and <importance sampling> calculations, assume a normalized <prior density> and $0<Z<\infty$.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Write $h(\theta)=\log p(\mathcal D\mid\theta)+\log p(\theta)$. Choose an interior <maximum a posteriori estimate>, a globally maximizing <posterior mode>
$$
\mu\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\theta}h(\theta),\qquad \nabla h(\mu)=0.
$$
Suppose $h$ is twice continuously differentiable near $\mu$ and its negative <Hessian matrix> $A=-\nabla^2h(\mu)$ is a <positive-definite matrix>. The second-order <Taylor expansion> is
$$
h(\theta)\simeq h(\mu)-\frac12(\theta-\mu)^TA(\theta-\mu).
$$
Exponentiating and normalizing this local approximation gives the <Laplace approximation>
$$
\boxed{p(\theta\mid\mathcal D)\simeq\mathcal N(\theta;\mu,\Sigma),\qquad \Sigma=A^{-1}=-\bigl[\nabla^2h(\mu)\bigr]^{-1}}.
$$
Thus the approximate <mean> is the <posterior mode>, and the approximate <covariance matrix> is the inverse local curvature. This <normal approximation> is useful when most <posterior probability> is concentrated around this mode. A boundary mode, singular curvature, or several substantial modes requires a modified approximation.

{parent=a}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Evaluate <Bayes' theorem> at the <posterior mode> $\mu$:
$$
p(\mathcal D)=\frac{p(\mathcal D\mid\mu)p(\mu)}{p(\mu\mid\mathcal D)}.
$$
The <multivariate normal density> in the <Laplace approximation> has height $(2\pi)^{-d/2}|\Sigma|^{-1/2}$ at its <mean>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{p(\mathcal D)\simeq p(\mathcal D\mid\mu)p(\mu)(2\pi)^{d/2}|\Sigma|^{1/2}}.
$$
Equivalently, the approximate log <marginal likelihood> is
$$
\boxed{\log p(\mathcal D)\simeq\log p(\mathcal D\mid\mu)+\log p(\mu)+\frac d2\log(2\pi)+\frac12\log|\Sigma|}.
$$
The <determinant> factor accounts for the volume of the locally plausible parameter region, which the peak <likelihood> alone does not measure.

Put $r(\theta)=p(\mathcal D\mid\theta)p(\theta)$ and $Z=\int r(\theta)\,d\theta$. The <support condition for importance sampling> is essential: $q(\theta)>0$ almost everywhere that $r(\theta)>0$. For one draw $\theta\sim q$, the <importance weight> $W=r(\theta)/q(\theta)$ satisfies
$$
\mathbb E_q W=\int_{\{q>0\}}\frac{r(\theta)}{q(\theta)}q(\theta)\,d\theta=Z.
$$
By <linearity of expectation>,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E\widehat p_1(\mathcal D)=\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T\mathbb E_qW_t=Z=p(\mathcal D)}.
$$
This proves that it is an <unbiased estimator>. If the proposal omits a region of positive target mass, the <expected value> instead misses that region's contribution; the unconditional unbiasedness claim needs the stated <support condition for importance sampling>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

With $r$, $Z$, and $W$ as above, <independence> of the draws gives the <variance of a sample mean>:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(\widehat p_1)=\frac1T\left[\int\frac{r(\theta)^2}{q(\theta)}\,d\theta-Z^2\right]}.
$$
This formula allows infinite <variance>. To identify the optimum, write $\pi(\theta)=p(\theta\mid\mathcal D)=r(\theta)/Z$. Then
$$
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat p_1)=\frac{Z^2}{T}\left[\int\frac{\pi^2}{q}-1\right]=\frac{Z^2}{T}\int_{\{q>0\}}\frac{(\pi-q)^2}{q}\,d\theta.
$$
The last equality uses $\int\pi=\int q=1$. Its nonnegative integrand proves
$$
\boxed{q_{\rm opt}(\theta)=\pi(\theta)=p(\theta\mid\mathcal D),\qquad \min\operatorname{Var}(\widehat p_1)=0}.
$$
Indeed, all <importance weights> then equal $Z$. This is a formal optimum: evaluating the normalized <posterior density> generally already requires the unknown <marginal likelihood>.

**Poor coverage of regions with substantial posterior mass produces large weights.** In particular, excessively light proposal tails, a proposal that is too narrow, or a missed mode can make $\int r^2/q$ enormous or infinite. A proposal that is unnecessarily diffuse can also waste nearly all draws on negligible <posterior probability>. Omitting target support altogether violates unbiasedness, rather than merely increasing <variance>.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For <reciprocal importance sampling>, use the same $r$ and $Z$ but draw from the <posterior density> $\pi=r/Z$. Here the support requirement is reversed: the normalized density $q$ must vanish almost everywhere that $r=0$. Thus $q$ may be confined to a subset of the <posterior distribution>'s support. For $V=q(\theta)/r(\theta)$,
$$
\mathbb E_\pi V=\int_{\{r>0\}}\frac{q(\theta)}{r(\theta)}\frac{r(\theta)}Z\,d\theta=\frac1Z\int q(\theta)\,d\theta=\frac1Z.
$$
Consequently,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E\bigl[\widehat p_2(\mathcal D)^{-1}\bigr]=p(\mathcal D)^{-1}}.
$$
It is the estimate of the reciprocal that is an <unbiased estimator>. Taking its reciprocal does not generally produce an <unbiased estimator> of $Z$: inversion is nonlinear. In fact, when the average is strictly positive and the relevant <expectation> exists, <Jensen's inequality> gives $\mathbb E\widehat p_2\geq Z$.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

For $V=q/r$ under the <posterior density> $\pi=r/Z$,
$$
\mathbb E_\pi V^2=\frac1Z\int_{\{r>0\}}\frac{q(\theta)^2}{r(\theta)}\,d\theta.
$$
The independent-draw <variance of a sample mean> therefore yields
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}(\widehat p_2^{-1})=\frac1T\left[\frac1Z\int\frac{q^2}{r}-\frac1{Z^2}\right]=\frac1{TZ^2}\left[\int\frac{q^2}{\pi}-1\right]}.
$$
Since
$$
\int\frac{q^2}{\pi}-1=\int_{\{\pi>0\}}\frac{(q-\pi)^2}{\pi}\,d\theta\geq0,
$$
we again obtain
$$
\boxed{q_{\rm opt}=\pi,\qquad \min\operatorname{Var}(\widehat p_2^{-1})=0}.
$$
The optimum is again generally unavailable as an evaluable normalized density without knowing $Z$.

**A proposal with too much mass where the posterior density is tiny makes reciprocal weights unstable.** In particular, proposal tails that are too heavy relative to the <posterior distribution> can make $\int q^2/\pi$ infinite. This contrasts with ordinary <importance sampling>, whose dangerous denominator is $q$. Choosing a normalized $q$ restricted to a well-covered posterior region is allowed, although a very small region can make most draws contribute zero and increase <variance>. Taking $q=p(\theta)$ gives the <harmonic mean estimator of Bayesian model evidence>, illustrating why very small <likelihood> values can cause severe instability.

Use the factorization $p(\theta,\mathcal D)=p(\theta\mid\mathcal D)p(\mathcal D)$ inside the <evidence lower bound>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal L(\phi)&=\mathbb E_q\left[\log p(\mathcal D)+\log\frac{p(\theta\mid\mathcal D)}{q(\theta;\phi^2)}\right]\\
&=\log p(\mathcal D)-\operatorname{KL}\bigl(q(\theta;\phi^2)\Vert p(\theta\mid\mathcal D)\bigr).
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\log p(\mathcal D)=\mathcal L(\phi)+\mathcal J(\phi)}.
$$
The <marginal likelihood> is independent of the variational parameter $\phi$, so **maximizing the <evidence lower bound> is equivalent to minimizing the <Kullback-Leibler divergence>**. Nonnegativity of the <Kullback-Leibler divergence> also gives $\mathcal L(\phi)\leq\log p(\mathcal D)$.

= Solution

The independent coordinates make the logarithm of the <probability density> ratio a sum. For coordinate $i$, its contribution to the <Kullback-Leibler divergence> is
$$
\mathbb E_q\left[\log\frac{\sigma_i}{\phi}-\frac{\theta_i^2}{2\phi^2}+\frac{\theta_i^2}{2\sigma_i^2}\right]=\log\frac{\sigma_i}{\phi}-\frac12+\frac{\phi^2}{2\sigma_i^2},
$$
because $\mathbb E_q\theta_i^2=\phi^2$. Thus, retaining all constants,
$$
\boxed{\mathcal J(\phi)=\frac{\phi^2}{2}\left(\frac1{\sigma_1^2}+\frac1{\sigma_2^2}\right)-1+\log\frac{\sigma_1\sigma_2}{\phi^2}}.
$$
Here $\sigma_1,\sigma_2>0$ and $\phi>0$, so both <normal densities> are nondegenerate.

**The printed minimization of the <evidence lower bound> has no solution.** To distinguish that literal result from the usual <variational inference> optimum, put $S=\sigma_1^{-2}+\sigma_2^{-2}>0$. The preceding <Kullback-Leibler divergence> satisfies
$$
\mathcal J'(\phi)=S\phi-\frac2\phi,\qquad \mathcal J''(\phi)=S+\frac2{\phi^2}>0.
$$
Its unique <global minimum> occurs at $\phi^2=2/S$. Since $\mathcal L=\log p(\mathcal D)-\mathcal J$, this is the unique <global maximum> of the <evidence lower bound>:
$$
\boxed{\phi_{\rm ELBO\,max}=\sqrt{\frac{2\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2}}}.
$$
The optimal common <variance> is the <harmonic mean> of the two posterior <variances>. However, $\mathcal J(\phi)\to+\infty$ as either $\phi\downarrow0$ or $\phi\to\infty$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\inf_{\phi>0}\mathcal L(\phi)=-\infty,\qquad\text{no minimizing }\phi\text{ exists}}.
$$
The source PDF confirms the minimization wording; replacing it by maximization would give the finite value above, consistently with part (i).

In this setting a <positive-semidefinite kernel> is an inner-product similarity: there exist a real <Hilbert space> $\mathcal H$ and a <feature map> $\varphi:\mathcal X\to\mathcal H$ such that
$$
\boxed{k(x,x')=\langle\varphi(x),\varphi(x')\rangle_{\mathcal H}}.
$$
The <feature map> may be infinite-dimensional. Equivalently, $k$ is symmetric and every finite <kernel matrix> is a <positive semidefinite matrix>. The equivalence follows by forming the <canonical feature space of a positive-semidefinite kernel> and taking its <Hilbert space> completion.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For every $m\geq1$, every choice $x_1,\ldots,x_m\in\mathcal X$, and every coefficient <vector> $\alpha\in\mathbb R^m$, the required condition is
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i,j=1}^m\alpha_i\alpha_j k(x_i,x_j)\geq0}.
$$
Thus $\alpha^TK\alpha\geq0$ for the <kernel matrix> $K$. For a <feature map>, this follows from
$$
\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j\langle\varphi(x_i),\varphi(x_j)\rangle=\left\|\sum_i\alpha_i\varphi(x_i)\right\|_{\mathcal H}^2\geq0.
$$
In <kernel methods>, the term <positive-definite kernel> commonly uses this non-strict inequality. Strict positive definiteness is the stronger requirement that the expression be positive for distinct inputs and every nonzero $\alpha$; it is not required here.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The <closure property of positive-semidefinite kernels> gives three constructions, each seen directly from the <feature maps> $\varphi_1,\varphi_2$.

* **Nonnegative sums:** $k=\alpha k_1+\beta k_2$, for $\alpha,\beta\geq0$. Its <feature map> is $(\sqrt\alpha\varphi_1(x),\sqrt\beta\varphi_2(x))$ in the <Hilbert space> direct sum; expanding its <inner product> gives $k$.
* **Pointwise products:** $k(x,x')=k_1(x,x')k_2(x,x')$. In the Hilbert <tensor product>, use $\varphi(x)=\varphi_1(x)\otimes\varphi_2(x)$: the <inner product> of two such elementary tensors is the product of their two <inner products>.
* **Input-dependent weighting:** $k(x,x')=a(x)k_1(x,x')a(x')$ for any real-valued function $a$. Its <feature map> is $a(x)\varphi_1(x)$, so signs of $a$ are allowed. Nonnegative scalar multiplication is the special case of constant $a$; the weighting factor $a(x)a(x')$ is itself a rank-one <positive-semidefinite kernel>.

Another useful consequence is pullback: if $h:\mathcal X\to\mathcal Z$ and $k_0$ is a <positive-semidefinite kernel> on $\mathcal Z$, then $k_0(h(x),h(x'))$ has <feature map> $\varphi_0\circ h$.

Take the real <L2 space> $\mathcal H=L^2((0,\infty),dt)$ and the <feature map>
$$
\varphi(x)(t)=\mathbf1_{(0,x]}(t).
$$
Each <indicator function> belongs to $\mathcal H$ because its squared <L2 norm> is $x<\infty$. Their <inner product> is the length of the overlapping intervals:
$$
\boxed{\langle\varphi(x),\varphi(x')\rangle=\int_0^\infty\mathbf1_{(0,x]}(t)\mathbf1_{(0,x']}(t)\,dt=\min(x,x')=k_1(x,x')}.
$$
This realizes $k_1$ as a <positive-semidefinite kernel>; it is also the <Brownian covariance kernel>.

Let $J(t)$ be the $n\times d$ <Jacobian matrix> with row $J_i(t)=\nabla_\theta f(\theta(t),x_i)^T$. Differentiating the squared-error <loss function> gives
$$
\nabla_\theta\ell(\theta(t))=\sum_i(u_i(t)-y_i)\nabla_\theta f(\theta(t),x_i)=J(t)^T(u(t)-y).
$$
The parameter <gradient flow> is consequently $\dot\theta=J^T(y-u)$. The <chain rule> then gives
$$
\boxed{\dot u(t)=H(t)(y-u(t)),\qquad H(t)=J(t)J(t)^T},
$$
where, explicitly,
$$
\boxed{H_{ij}(t)=\left\langle\nabla_\theta f(\theta(t),x_i),\nabla_\theta f(\theta(t),x_j)\right\rangle_{\mathbb R^d}}.
$$
It is a <Gram matrix> and therefore a <positive semidefinite matrix>: $v^TH(t)v=\|J(t)^Tv\|_2^2\geq0$. These derivatives are understood wherever the network is differentiable; for piecewise differentiable activations the same calculation applies along differentiable portions of the trajectory.

Under the stated infinite-width scaling and freezing assumptions, the deterministic limiting <kernel matrix> has entries
$$
\boxed{H^*_{ij}=\mathbb E_{\theta(0)\sim P}\left\langle\nabla_\theta f(\theta(0),x_i),\nabla_\theta f(\theta(0),x_j)\right\rangle=k_{\rm NTK}(x_i,x_j)}.
$$
For a sequence of finite-width networks, this expectation is interpreted in the stated width limit. The resulting <neural tangent kernel> is a <positive-semidefinite kernel>, since every finite coefficient <vector> $a$ gives $a^TH^*a=\mathbb E\|\sum_i a_i\nabla_\theta f(\theta(0),x_i)\|_2^2\geq0$.

The prediction <gradient flow> is linear:
$$
\boxed{\dot u=H^*(y-u),\qquad u(t)=y+e^{-tH^*}(u(0)-y)}.
$$
The <matrix exponential> solves the constant-coefficient system because the residual $u-y$ satisfies $\frac d{dt}(u-y)=-H^*(u-y)$. Each positive-<eigenvalue> component decays exponentially; a component in the <null space> of $H^*$ stays at its initial residual. Thus $u(t)\to y$ for every initial prediction if $H^*$ is a <positive-definite matrix>. The limiting <kernel matrix> being deterministic does not require $u(0)$ to be deterministic.

Evaluating the negative <functional gradient> at $x_j$ yields
$$
\boxed{\frac{dg_t(x_j)}{dt}=\sum_{i=1}^nk(x_j,x_i)(y_i-g_t(x_i))}.

Thus the evaluation <vector> $v(t)=(g_t(x_1),\ldots,g_t(x_n))^T$ follows

\boxed{\dot v=K(y-v),\qquad K_{ij}=k(x_i,x_j)=H^*_{ij}}.

**An infinitely wide <neural network> in this regime follows the same training-prediction dynamics as <kernel regression> with its <neural tangent kernel> and matching initial predictions.** For this squared-error <loss function>, the relevant algorithm is <gradient flow> in the <reproducing kernel Hilbert space>. Matching predictions at arbitrary additional inputs also requires the corresponding cross-input <neural tangent kernel> to freeze and the initial predictions there to match. A finite-width <neural network> can change its <feature map> during training, so the equivalence depends on the assumptions rather than holding for every architecture and parameterization.

A <transformer block> has two principal sublayers: <multi-head attention> and a positionwise <feedforward neural network>. Each sublayer has a <residual connection> and <layer normalization>. To make the components explicit, let $X\in\mathbb R^{L\times d_{\rm model}}$ contain the representations of $L$ sequence positions. For head $h$, learned projections form
$$
Q_h=XW_h^Q,\qquad K_h=XW_h^K,\qquad V_h=XW_h^V.
$$
The <scaled dot-product attention> output is
$$
A_h=\operatorname{softmax}\left(\frac{Q_hK_h^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}+B\right)V_h,
$$
where the <softmax function> acts along each row and $B$ is an optional attention mask. For <causal self-attention>, disallowed future positions receive $-\infty$ before the <softmax function>, giving zero attention weights. In <self-attention>, queries, keys, and values all come from the same input sequence. The <multi-head attention> output concatenates the heads and projects them:
$$
\operatorname{MHA}(X)=\operatorname{Concat}(A_1,\ldots,A_H)W^O.
$$
A standard post-normalization <transformer block> can then be written
$$
\boxed{U=\operatorname{LN}(X+\operatorname{MHA}(X)),\qquad Y=\operatorname{LN}(U+\operatorname{FFN}(U))}.
$$
Here <layer normalization> normalizes the feature coordinates of each position separately, within each example, and applies learned scales and shifts. The positionwise <feedforward neural network> applies the same two affine maps and <activation function> at every position, for example
$$
\operatorname{FFN}(z)=\rho(zW_1+b_1)W_2+b_2.
$$
Thus **attention mixes information between positions, while the feedforward sublayer transforms each position's features**. The <residual connections> support propagation through a deep stack, and <dropout> is often used during training. Pre-normalization variants move <layer normalization> before the sublayer. A model's <positional encoding> supplies order information to the sequence representation or attention mechanism; a decoder in an encoder-decoder model also has a cross-attention sublayer between its <self-attention> and <feedforward neural network> sublayers.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

A <recurrent neural network> processes positions through a recurrence such as $h_t=F(h_{t-1},x_t)$. Computing $h_t$ requires $h_{t-1}$, so the standard computation is sequential across sequence positions, although different examples in a batch can be processed in parallel. Information from position $s$ to $t$ must pass through $t-s$ recurrent steps, and backpropagated <gradients> involve products of that many step <Jacobian matrices>. This creates <vanishing gradients> or <exploding gradients> and can make long-range dependencies difficult to learn. Gating can mitigate these difficulties while retaining the recurrent dependence.

In a <transformer block>, all queries, keys, and values for an available input sequence can be formed in parallel. The <self-attention> weights are computed by <matrix multiplication>, so positions within one layer can be processed together; layers themselves remain sequential. With unrestricted <self-attention>, one position can directly receive information from any other position in one layer, giving a constant-length route across distant positions rather than one proportional to their separation. With <causal self-attention>, there is the same direct route to any permitted earlier position.

**A <transformer model> supports parallel training across known sequence positions and short routes for long-range dependencies.** Even a causal model can compute all training positions together when the entire training sequence is supplied and the attention mask excludes future positions. However, ordinary autoregressive generation still generates one new token at a time. Full <self-attention> also constructs $L^2$ pairwise scores, with quadratic time and storage in sequence length for the direct implementation; a standard <recurrent neural network> has linear sequential work in sequence length at fixed hidden width. Neither architecture guarantees that a useful long-range dependency will actually be learned.

**<Gaussian process regression> supplies a <predictive distribution> and uncertainty estimates.** A <Gaussian process regression posterior> gives both a <posterior mean> and a <posterior variance>, allowing <credible intervals> for the latent function and a <posterior predictive distribution> for future noisy observations. The <kernel ridge regression> estimator by itself specifies a point estimate.

With the same <positive-semidefinite kernel> and matching regularization, the <Gaussian process regression> <posterior mean> equals the <kernel ridge regression> prediction. Thus the main additional benefit is the explicit <probability distribution> of predictions, conditional on the chosen <Gaussian process> model and its hyperparameters, rather than an automatic improvement in point-prediction accuracy.

Condition throughout on the training inputs and $x_*$. Interpret $\theta$ here as the fixed <covariance function> and noise hyperparameters. Assume observation errors $\epsilon_i$ are independent standard <normal random variables>, independent of the <Gaussian process> $f$, and write
$$
K_{ij}=k(x_i,x_j),\quad k_*=(k(x_1,x_*),\ldots,k(x_n,x_*))^T,\quad k_{**}=k(x_*,x_*),\quad C=K+\sigma^2I_n.
$$
The independent-error assumption is the standard <Gaussian process regression> interpretation of the observation model; the single-observation marginal noise law alone would not specify cross-observation noise correlations. We take $\sigma^2>0$ so that $C$ is a <positive-definite matrix>. More generally the inverse formulas below apply whenever the indicated noisy <covariance matrices> are invertible.

The <Gaussian process> prior makes $(f_*,\mathbf f)$ a <Gaussian random vector>. Adding the independent <Gaussian noise> vector to $\mathbf f$ preserves a <multivariate normal distribution>. Its <mean> is zero, its observation <covariance matrix> is $C$, and $\operatorname{Cov}(f_*,y_i)=k(x_*,x_i)$ because the noise is independent of $f_*$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}f_*\\\mathbf y\end{pmatrix}\biggm|\theta\sim\mathcal N\left(0,\begin{pmatrix}k_{**}&k_*^T\\k_*&C\end{pmatrix}\right)}.
$$
A degenerate latent <variance> is allowed; the displayed <multivariate normal distribution> then denotes a Gaussian law even if it has no full-dimensional <probability density>.

The <conditional multivariate normal distribution> gives
$$
\boxed{f_*\mid\mathbf y,\theta\sim\mathcal N(m_n(x_*),v_n(x_*))},
$$
where
$$
\boxed{m_n(x_*)=k_*^TC^{-1}\mathbf y,\qquad v_n(x_*)=k_{**}-k_*^TC^{-1}k_*}.
$$
For a direct justification, form $R=f_*-k_*^TC^{-1}\mathbf y$. It is jointly <Gaussian> with $\mathbf y$, has zero <mean>, and satisfies
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(R,\mathbf y)=k_*^T-k_*^TC^{-1}C=0,\qquad\operatorname{Var}(R)=k_{**}-k_*^TC^{-1}k_*.
$$
The <independence of uncorrelated jointly normal variables> therefore makes $R$ independent of $\mathbf y$. Conditional on $\mathbf y$, only the displayed affine shift remains, proving the result. This is the latent-function <posterior variance>; a new observation $y_*=f_*+\sigma\epsilon_*$ with fresh independent noise has <predictive variance> $v_n(x_*)+\sigma^2$.

= i

= Solution
{parent=i}

Use only the first $n-1$ observations to define
$$
\begin{aligned}
A&=(k(x_i,x_j))_{i,j<n}+\sigma^2I_{n-1},&
 b&=(k(x_i,x_n))_{i<n},\\
a&=(k(x_i,x_*))_{i<n},&
 c&=k(x_n,x_n)+\sigma^2,\\
d&=k(x_n,x_*),&k_{**}&=k(x_*,x_*).
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the full noisy <covariance matrix> is $C_n=\begin{pmatrix}A&b\\b^T&c\end{pmatrix}$ and the full cross-covariance <vector> is $(a^T,d)^T$. Set
$$
s=c-b^TA^{-1}b,\qquad M=s^{-1}.
$$
The scalar $s$ is the <Schur complement> of $A$. Substituting the block inverse into the <Gaussian process regression posterior> <variance> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
v_n(x_*)&=k_{**}-\begin{pmatrix}a^T&d\end{pmatrix}C_n^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}a\\d\end{pmatrix}\\
&=k_{**}-a^TA^{-1}a-M\left[(a^TA^{-1}b)^2-2d\,a^TA^{-1}b+d^2\right]\\
&=v_{n-1}(x_*)-M(d-b^TA^{-1}a)^2.
\end{aligned}
$$
Consequently,
$$
\boxed{c_1=\frac1{k(x_n,x_n)+\sigma^2-b^TA^{-1}b},\qquad c_2=b^TA^{-1}a},
$$
and
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}_{n-1}(f_*)-\operatorname{Var}_n(f_*)=c_1\bigl(k(x_n,x_*)-c_2\bigr)^2}.
$$
For interpretation, after the first $n-1$ observations the remaining <covariance> between $f_*$ and $f(x_n)$ is $r=d-b^TA^{-1}a$, while the <conditional variance> of the new noisy observation is $s$. Thus the reduction is the squared remaining <covariance> divided by the remaining observation <variance>.

= ii
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The <Schur complement> can be written
$$
s=\underbrace{k(x_n,x_n)-b^TA^{-1}b}_{v_{n-1}(x_n)}+\sigma^2>0,
$$
because the first term is a <conditional variance> and $\sigma^2>0$. Therefore $c_1=s^{-1}>0$ and the squared term is nonnegative, so
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}_{n-1}(f_*)\geq\operatorname{Var}_n(f_*)}.
$$
Equality holds exactly when the remaining <conditional covariance> $k(x_n,x_*)-b^TA^{-1}a$ is zero. In particular, the reduction need not be strict even at a previously unseen input.

= iii
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

**Additional observations weakly decrease the <posterior variance> at every fixed prediction input.** Apply part (ii) repeatedly to add observations one at a time. An observation whose remaining <conditional covariance> with $f_*$ is nonzero strictly decreases that <posterior variance>; one with zero remaining <conditional covariance> leaves it unchanged. Large observation noise reduces the information gained through the denominator $v_{n-1}(x_n)+\sigma^2$.

For fixed <Gaussian process> hyperparameters and a <Gaussian likelihood>, the <posterior variance> depends on the observation locations and noise level, but not on the observed numerical outputs. Hence the reduction holds for each possible new output, rather than only on average. The <predictive variance> of a future noisy observation also decreases by the same amount because its extra independent noise <variance> is fixed. Refitting the hyperparameters after adding data can change the <covariance function>; the monotonicity statement compares conditioning under one fixed model.

= iv
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

For $x_n=x_*$, the notation of part (i) gives $b=a$, $d=k_{**}$, and $c=k_{**}+\sigma^2$. Write $v=\operatorname{Var}_{n-1}(f_*)=k_{**}-a^TA^{-1}a$. The remaining <covariance> is then $v$ and the new observation's remaining <variance> is $v+\sigma^2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}_{n-1}(f_*)-\operatorname{Var}_n(f_*)=\frac{v^2}{v+\sigma^2}=\frac{\operatorname{Var}_{n-1}(f_*)^2}{\operatorname{Var}_{n-1}(f_*)+\sigma^2}}.
$$
Equivalently,
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Var}_n(f_*)=\frac{v\sigma^2}{v+\sigma^2}}.
$$
The expression is valid also when $v=0$ and $\sigma^2>0$. For $v>0$, a noiseless observation, interpreted as the limit $\sigma^2\downarrow0$, removes all remaining <variance> at the observed target.

**The source's assertion that the target input always gives the greatest reduction needs an additional hypothesis.** The reduction formula above does not require that assertion. For a counterexample, use the <linear kernel> $k(x,x')=xx'$ with $\sigma^2=1$ and target $x_*=1$. With no informative previous observations, $v=1$. This can also be arranged with $n-1=1$ by observing first at $x_1=0$, which has zero <covariance> with all latent values. Observing at the target gives reduction $1/2$, whereas observing at $x_n=2$ gives
$$
\frac{k(2,1)^2}{k(2,2)+1}=\frac45>\frac12.
$$
Indeed this <Gaussian process> is $f(x)=xZ$ for a standard <normal random variable> $Z$, so the measurement at $2$ observes the same unknown coefficient with a larger signal relative to its noise.

One sufficient extra condition is that the existing latent <posterior variance> be maximal at $x_*$ among all candidate inputs. To prove it, let $w=v_{n-1}(x)$ and $r=\operatorname{Cov}(f(x),f_*\mid\mathbf y_{1:n-1},\theta)$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives $r^2\leq vw$. If $w\leq v$, then
$$
\Delta(x)=\frac{r^2}{w+\sigma^2}\leq\frac{vw}{w+\sigma^2}\leq\frac{v^2}{v+\sigma^2}=\Delta(x_*),
$$
because $w\mapsto w/(w+\sigma^2)$ is increasing. Thus target sampling is optimal under this extra condition, but it is not implied by the paper's arbitrary-<covariance function> assumptions.
