= Kinetic theory
{title2=$f(t,x,v)$}

Kinetic theory describes many-particle systems through a distribution on <phase space>, retaining both position and <velocity>. In the number-density convention, $f(t,\mathbf x,\mathbf v)\,d^3x\,d^3v$ is the expected number of particles in a small position–<velocity> region. For identical particles of <mass> $m$, its <integrals> give the <number density> $n$ and bulk <velocity> $\mathbf u$:
$$
n(t,\mathbf x)=\int_{\mathbb R^3}f\,d^3v,\qquad n\mathbf u=\int_{\mathbb R^3}\mathbf v f\,d^3v.
$$
This $f$ is not normalized to one: its <integral> over the whole <phase space> gives the total expected particle number. Transport moves particles along trajectories, while <collision> or <diffusion> terms change the distribution. The <Boltzmann equation>, <Hamiltonian Liouville equation> and <Fokker-Planck equation> describe different physical assumptions, rather than interchangeable collision laws.

An <ideal gas> is a motivating example: the <pressure> on a wall comes from microscopic <momentum> transfers, even when the bulk <velocity> is zero. The <Maxwell-Boltzmann velocity distribution> separates this random molecular motion from bulk motion; see <ideal gas pressure from wall collisions>. The attractive feature of <kinetic theory> is that a distribution can describe two opposing populations where one average <velocity> hides almost everything. It also tells us when averaging into a <continuum approximation> loses essential information: compare the intermolecular <mean free path> with the apparatus scale using the <Knudsen number>. https://farside.ph.utexas.edu/teaching/355/Surveyhtml/node212.html[Richard Fitzpatrick, Kinetic Theory: fundamental assumptions and wall pressure]{ref}

= Kinetic theory of gases
{parent=Kinetic theory}

A microscopic account of gas properties using the motion and collisions of its constituents. For a dilute monatomic ideal gas, translational kinetic <energy> gives $p=Nm\langle v^2\rangle/(3V)$ and $U=3Nk_{\rm B}T/2$. Interactions and additional molecular modes require extensions.

= Velocity distribution
{parent=Kinetic theory}
{title2=$f(\mathbf v)$}

A function specifying how a particle population is distributed over velocities. If $f$ is a number-density distribution, $f(\mathbf v)d^3v$ counts particles per unit spatial volume in that velocity element and $\int f\,d^3v=n$. A probability-density convention instead has integral one. The normalization must be stated before adding populations or comparing their slopes.

= Velocity alignment
{parent=Kinetic theory}
{title2=$\dot{\mathbf v}_i=\sum_jK_{ij}(\mathbf v_j-\mathbf v_i)$}

An interaction making particle <velocities> approach those of their neighbors. Symmetric nonnegative weights $K_{ij}$ conserve total <momentum> for equal particle <masses> and dissipate relative <kinetic energy>. Connectivity and quantitative lower bounds on the interaction matter for eventual consensus; nonnegativity alone permits noninteracting groups.

= Mean-field closure for velocity alignment
{parent=Velocity alignment}
{title2=$p_2(z,z_\star)\simeq p_1(z)p_1(z_\star)$}

Neglect of pair correlations closes the first marginal equation of the <dissipative Liouville equation>. For unit-normalized <marginal distributions>, the acceleration is $(N-1)\mathbf L[p_1]$. With number-normalized $f=Np_1$, it is $(1-1/N)\mathbf L[f]$. The usual <mean-field alignment equation> neglects this relative $1/N$ correction. Independence at one time does not imply that interactions preserve independence.

= Mean-field alignment equation
{parent=Mean-field closure for velocity alignment}
{title2=$\partial_tf+\mathbf v\cdot\nabla_xf+\nabla_v\cdot(f\mathbf L[f])=0$}

The continuum <velocity alignment> equation with $\mathbf L[f](\mathbf x,\mathbf v)=\int K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x_\star)(\mathbf v_\star-\mathbf v)f_\star\,d^3x_\star\,d^3v_\star$. For symmetric nonnegative $K$, it conserves total <mass> and <momentum> while dissipating <kinetic energy>. Its force is affine in <velocity>, with a nonzero <velocity-space divergence>.

= Velocity-alignment entropy production
{parent=Mean-field alignment equation}
{title2=$\frac{d}{dt}\int f\log f=d\iint K\rho\rho_\star\,dx\,dx_\star$}

For the <mean-field alignment equation> in $d$ position and $d$ <velocity> coordinates, put $\rho=\int f\,d^dv$. Its force has <velocity-space divergence> $-d\int K\rho_\star\,d^dx_\star$. Consequently <integration by parts> gives the nonnegative derivative in the title, assuming suitable positivity, decay and integrability. The fine-grained <Gibbs entropy> decreases because these dynamics contract <phase space>; the interaction is dissipative.

= Velocity-alignment energy dissipation
{parent=Mean-field alignment equation}
{title2=$\dot{\mathcal E}=-\tfrac12\iint K|\mathbf v_\star-\mathbf v|^2ff_\star\,dz\,dz_\star$}

For the <mean-field alignment equation>, multiply by $|\mathbf v|^2/2$ and use <integration by parts>. Exchanging $z$ with $z_\star$ and averaging the two results gives the displayed nonpositive <kinetic energy> derivative. The factor $1/2$ accounts for counting ordered pairs twice. Total <momentum> is conserved, so the dissipated part is motion relative to the global <mean velocity>.

= Dissipative Liouville equation
{parent=Kinetic theory}
{title2=$\partial_tP+\nabla_z\cdot(bP)=0$}

Conservation of <probability> under a deterministic <characteristic flow map> with <velocity> $b(z,t)$ gives the displayed <continuity equation> on <phase space>. Along a characteristic, $dP/dt=-P\nabla_z\cdot b$. Unlike the <Hamiltonian Liouville equation>, it allows contraction of phase-space volume and increasing density along trajectories. It still preserves total <probability> when boundary fluxes vanish.

= Phase-space divergence
{parent=Dissipative Liouville equation}
{title2=$\nabla_z\cdot b$}

The <divergence> of the vector field generating a flow on <phase space>. Its time integral determines the <Jacobian determinant> of the <characteristic flow map>: $d\log|\det D\Phi_t|/dt=(\nabla_z\cdot b)(\Phi_t,t)$. A <Hamiltonian vector field> has zero phase-space divergence in <canonical coordinates>.

= Quasilinear approximation
{parent=Kinetic theory}

A weak-fluctuation closure retaining the averaged quadratic fluctuation flux while linearizing the fluctuating kinetic equation about a slowly evolving mean <phase-space distribution function>. One solves the fast linear response with the mean frozen, then inserts its covariance into the slow averaged equation. A short-memory stationary limit can yield <quasilinear diffusion>, but finite-time responses retain memory. Self-consistent collective fields enter through the <dielectric function> in homogeneous electrostatic problems.

= Quasilinear diffusion
{parent=Quasilinear approximation}
{title2=$\partial_tf_0=\partial_v(D\partial_vf_0)$}

The diffusive evolution of an averaged <phase-space distribution function> caused by weak fluctuating fields in the <quasilinear approximation>. In a stationary homogeneous one-dimensional electrostatic problem, if the potential's temporal <power spectral density> is $S_{\varphi,k}(\omega)$ with covariance $\int S_{\varphi,k}(\omega)e^{-i\omega\tau}d\omega/(2\pi)$, then
$$
D(v)=\frac{e^2}{2m_e^2}\sum_k k^2S_{\varphi,k}(kv).
$$
This follows from the real part of the one-sided time correlation along an unperturbed particle trajectory. Both signs of the spatial <wavenumber> are included. The action-space counterpart for regular <stellar orbits> is <quasilinear diffusion in action space>.

= Velocity-space diffusion
{parent=Quasilinear diffusion}
{title2=$\partial_tf=\partial_v(D(v)\partial_vf)$}

Random changes of particle <velocity> described by a <diffusion equation> in <velocity space>. For constant $D$, $\langle(\Delta v)^2\rangle=2D\Delta t$. With zero boundary flux it conserves number. For a number-normalized one-dimensional distribution, its <kinetic energy> production is $d\mathcal K/dt=-m\int vD(v)f'(v)\,dv$, which reduces to $mnD$ for constant $D$. Variable $D$ must remain inside the derivative.

= Thermal speed
{parent=Kinetic theory}
{title2=$v_{\mathrm{th},s}=\sqrt{2T_s/m_s}$}

A characteristic random particle <speed>. Here <temperature> $T_s$ is measured in <energy> units, and the factor-two convention makes a <Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution> proportional to $e^{-v^2/v_{\mathrm{th},s}^2}$. Other conventions, such as the one-dimensional standard deviation $\sqrt{T_s/m_s}$, differ by fixed factors.

= Gyrokinetics
{parent=Kinetic theory}
{wiki}

A low-<frequency> kinetic reduction averaging over fast <cyclotron motion> while allowing perpendicular <wavelengths> comparable with the <gyroradius>. It retains <finite Larmor radius effects> absent from the leading long-<wavelength> <drift-kinetic equation>.

= Finite Larmor radius effect
{parent=Gyrokinetics}
{title2=$k_\perp\rho_s$}

Modification of the <plasma> response because particles sample fields around a finite <cyclotron orbit>. Effects become order one at $k_\perp\rho_s=O(1)$; even at $k_\perp\rho_s\ll1$, the leading polarization correction can be essential to a slow <dispersion relation>.

= Drift kinetics
{parent=Kinetic theory}

The slow dynamics of charged-particle <guiding centers> after averaging over rapid <cyclotron motion>, assuming <gyroradii> much smaller than resolved perpendicular length scales. Depending on the ordering, the retained transverse motion may include just the common electric drift or also <magnetic curvature drift>, <gradient-B drift> and <polarization drift>. These choices give distinct limits, not interchangeable closures.

= Polarization drift
{parent=Drift kinetics}
{title2=$\mathbf v_{\rm pol}=\mathbf b\times D_t\mathbf u_E/\Omega_s$}

The inertial correction to the electric drift $\mathbf u_E=c\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/B^2$ in a slowly varying <magnetic field>. In the simplest uniform-field electrostatic case it is $\mathbf v_{\rm pol}=m_sc^2\partial_t\mathbf E_\perp/(q_sB^2)$. Its species-dependent <current density> supplies the perpendicular polarization response needed in many slow <plasma> instabilities.

= Gradient-B drift
{parent=Drift kinetics}
{title2=$\mathbf v_{\nabla B}=\mu\mathbf b\times\nabla B/\Omega_s$}

The transverse <guiding center> drift produced by a <magnetic field> strength <gradient>, with <first adiabatic invariant> $\mu=v_\perp^2/(2B)$ and signed <cyclotron frequency> $\Omega_s$. Together with <magnetic curvature drift>, it produces velocity-dependent transverse transport.

= Magnetic curvature drift
{parent=Drift kinetics}
{title2=$\mathbf v_\kappa=(v_\parallel^2/\Omega_s)\mathbf b\times\boldsymbol\kappa$}

The transverse <guiding center> drift associated with <magnetic field-line curvature>. The signed <cyclotron frequency> $\Omega_s=q_sB/(m_sc)$ makes oppositely charged species drift oppositely at fixed <velocity>. The resulting species-dependent response is retained in slow drift-scale instabilities.

= Drift-kinetic equation
{parent=Drift kinetics}
{title2=$\partial_tf+\dot{\mathbf R}\cdot\nabla f+\dot v_\parallel\partial_{v_\parallel}f=0$}

The collisionless equation in the title transports a gyrophase-independent <phase-space distribution function> at fixed <first adiabatic invariant>. Its leading <guiding center> position has $\dot{\mathbf R}=\mathbf u_\perp+v_\parallel\mathbf b$. The parallel acceleration includes $(q/m)E_\parallel-\mu\mathbf b\cdot\nabla B$ and, when required, terms from motion of the local frame. Both $v_\parallel$ and the parallel derivative refer to the exact perturbed <magnetic field>.

= Velocity moment
{parent=Kinetic theory}
{title2=$M_{i_1\cdots i_r}=\int v_{i_1}\cdots v_{i_r}f\,d^3v$}

Integrating a <phase-space distribution function> against products of particle <velocity> components produces its velocity moments. The zeroth gives number density, the first gives number flux, and, after multiplying by particle <mass>, the second gives <momentum flux>. Replacing $\mathbf v$ by $\mathbf v-\mathbf u$, where $\mathbf u$ is the <mean velocity>, defines central velocity moments; the second is the <pressure tensor> divided by particle <mass>.

= Third central velocity moment
{parent=Velocity moment}
{title2=$Q_{ijk}=\int (v_i-u_i)(v_j-u_j)(v_k-u_k)f\,d^3v$}

For unit particle <mass>, this symmetric third-order <tensor> transports the <pressure tensor> in a kinetic fluid description. Its contraction gives the <heat flux> $q_i=Q_{ijj}/2$. It vanishes for a <local Maxwellian>, whose odd central <velocity moments> vanish, but it need not vanish for a general anisotropic or skewed <phase-space distribution function>.

= Velocity-moment hierarchy
{parent=Velocity moment}

Taking <velocity moments> of a kinetic transport equation couples the equation for a moment of order $r$ to a spatial derivative of a moment of order $r+1$. A finite fluid description therefore requires closure. For example, the <pressure tensor> equation involves the <third central velocity moment>.

= BBGKY hierarchy
{c}
{parent=Kinetic theory}

The hierarchy obtained by integrating the $N$-particle <Hamiltonian Liouville equation> over successively fewer particle coordinates. Each marginal equation involves the next marginal, so an autonomous one-particle equation requires a closure. For a <pair potential>, its first equation couples the <phase-space distribution function> to the <two-particle distribution function>. Number-normalized factorial marginals and unit-normalized probability marginals have different explicit combinatorial factors.

= Compression work from molecular impacts
{parent=Kinetic theory}

For a slowly advancing piston in an isotropic gas, multiplying the stationary molecular arrival flux by the first-order energy gain $2muv_x$ gives $dU/dt=pSu=-p\,dV/dt$. This is the microscopic form of adiabatic compression work. Higher-order terms matter for fast piston motion.

= Angularly resolved molecular arrival flux
{parent=Kinetic theory}

For isotropic velocity directions and normalized speed density $f(v)$, the number arriving at a plane per unit area and time is $d\Gamma=\tfrac12nf(v)v\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta\,dv$, over the incoming hemisphere. The cosine weights the normal speed, and the sine comes from solid angle.

= Gas impingement flux
{parent=Kinetic theory}
{title2=$J$}

The number of gas molecules striking unit surface area per unit time. For an ideal gas in thermal equilibrium, $J=P/\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}$. A deposited monolayer additionally depends on site density, <sticking coefficient> and <desorption>.

= Kinetic estimates of gas transport coefficients
{parent=Kinetic theory}
{title2=$D\sim\overline v\lambda/3,\quad\eta\sim nm\overline v\lambda/3,\quad\kappa\sim nc_v\overline v\lambda/3$}

In a dilute <gas> whose <mean free path> $\lambda$ is short compared with macroscopic gradient lengths, <particles> carry properties from their previous <collisions> over distances of order $\lambda$. A first-gradient isotropic estimate gives a transported flux $-n\overline v\lambda\nabla a/3$ for a property $a$ carried per <particle>. Applying this reasoning to species abundance, directed <momentum> $mu_x$ and thermal <energy> with per-particle <heat capacity> $c_v$ gives <diffusivity> $D\sim\overline v\lambda/3$, <dynamic viscosity> $\eta\sim nm\overline v\lambda/3$ and <thermal conductivity> $\kappa\sim nc_v\overline v\lambda/3$. These numerical prefactors are estimates, not exact collision-integral results.

For a constant hard-sphere diameter $d$, $\lambda\propto1/(nd^2)$ and $\overline v\propto\sqrt{T/m}$. Consequently $\eta\propto\sqrt{mT}/d^2$, $\kappa\propto c_v\sqrt{T/m}/d^2$ and $D\propto\sqrt{T/m}/(nd^2)$. At fixed <pressure> the last scales as $T^{3/2}/p$. The near pressure-independence of <viscosity> and <thermal conductivity> applies only while gas-gas <collisions>, rather than apparatus walls, determine the <mean free path>. Temperature-dependent cross-sections and internal molecular excitations change the simple <power laws>.

= Thermal motion
{parent=Kinetic theory}

Thermal motion is microscopic random motion associated with a system's <temperature>; <Brownian motion> makes its effects on larger suspended grains visible.

= Speed distribution
{parent=Kinetic theory}
{title2=$f(v),\quad v\geq0$}

A <speed distribution> is the <probability distribution> of the magnitude of <velocity>. When it has a <probability density function> $f$, the fraction of particles with <speeds> between $v$ and $v+dv$ is $f(v)\,dv$, and $\int_0^\infty f(v)\,dv=1$. The <Maxwell speed distribution> describes an equilibrium <ideal gas>; the <effusive beam speed distribution> weights that population by its rate of arrival.

= Boltzmann-Grad limit
{c}
{parent=Kinetic theory}
{title2=$N a^{d-1}\sim1,\qquad N a^d\to0$}

For $N$ hard particles in a fixed macroscopic volume, shrink their diameter $a$ while keeping $Na^{d-1}$ of order one. The occupied volume fraction tends to zero, but the <mean free path> stays macroscopic. Deriving the <Boltzmann equation> in this dilute limit requires control of recollisions and propagation of an incoming independence hypothesis related to <molecular chaos for the Boltzmann equation>. Its validity is not an automatic all-time consequence of small particle size.

= Knudsen number
{c}
{parent=Kinetic theory}
{title2=$\mathrm{Kn}=\ell_{\mathrm{mfp}}/L$}

The <Knudsen number> is the <dimensionless> ratio of an intermolecular <mean free path> $\ell_{\mathrm{mfp}}$ to a specified characteristic length $L$ of the apparatus or flow. The <mean free path> measures the distance travelled between gas–gas <collisions>; $L$ measures the distance over which boundaries or macroscopic conditions matter. Wall encounters must not be counted as gas–gas <collisions> in this comparison: doing so would hide the very wall-dominated behavior we want to detect. The same gas can therefore have a small <Knudsen number> in a large vessel and a large <Knudsen number> in a tiny opening.

For a dilute <hard-sphere gas> of identical molecular diameter $d$ in <thermal equilibrium>, use the convention
$$
\ell_{\mathrm{mfp}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2\,n\pi d^2}=\frac{k_BT}{\sqrt2\,\pi d^2p},\qquad \mathrm{Kn}=\frac{k_BT}{\sqrt2\,\pi d^2pL}.
$$
Here $n$ is the <number density>, $p$ the <pressure>, $T$ the absolute <temperature>, and $k_B$ the <Boltzmann constant>; the second equality uses the <ideal gas law>. The <collision cross-section> is $\pi d^2$, and the $\sqrt2$ accounts for both partners moving with independent equilibrium <velocities>. Mixtures and velocity-dependent <collision cross-sections> require a stated molecular model and <mean free path> convention; the displayed expression is not an exact formula for every real gas. https://farside.ph.utexas.edu/teaching/355/Surveyhtml/node219.html[Fitzpatrick, Mean Free Path, equations 5.209–5.219]{ref} https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.1197[Steve T. Paik, Is the mean free path the mean of a distribution?, dilute hard-sphere equilibrium convention]{ref}

As a numerical toy example, choose $T=300\,\mathrm K$ and $d=0.37\,\mathrm{nm}$, without claiming that this is a measured molecular diameter for a particular gas. The formula gives $\ell_{\mathrm{mfp}}\simeq68.1\,\mathrm{nm}$ at $p=10^5\,\mathrm{Pa}$. It yields $\mathrm{Kn}\simeq6.81\times10^{-6}$ for a $1\,\mathrm{cm}$ vessel but $\mathrm{Kn}\simeq0.681$ for a $100\,\mathrm{nm}$ gap. At $p=1\,\mathrm{Pa}$ the same toy gas has $\ell_{\mathrm{mfp}}\simeq6.81\,\mathrm{mm}$, comparable to the centimetre vessel. Reducing either <pressure> or apparatus size can thus expose molecular transport.

Choose $L$ for the question being asked: a tube diameter compares the <mean free path> with wall separation, while tube length describes a different geometric scale. For a positive macroscopic field $q$, a local gradient scale can instead be $L_q=|q|/|\nabla q|$, where its <gradient> is nonzero. A large vessel can contain a thin region with a large local <Knudsen number>. Small geometric <Knudsen number> alone does not guarantee a slowly varying near-equilibrium flow. The time, <velocity> and space scalings must also be stated when the <Knudsen number> appears as a coefficient in a <Boltzmann equation>. https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20230013741[NASA, Comparison Between DSMC and CFD for Hypersonic Planetary Entry Simulations (2023), local gradient-length breakdown analysis]{ref}

= Rarefied gas flow approximations
{parent=Knudsen number}

For $\mathrm{Kn}\ll1$, frequent gas–gas <collisions> can establish a local <Maxwellian> before macroscopic conditions change appreciably. Under this additional near-equilibrium assumption, the <hydrodynamic limit of the Boltzmann equation> motivates a <continuum approximation> with <Navier-Stokes equations> and heat conduction. A <no-slip boundary condition> is a leading wall approximation, not a statement about individual molecular <velocities>.

With a small but finite <Knudsen number>, a useful slip approximation retains continuum bulk equations and corrects wall <velocity> and <temperature>. A <Navier slip boundary condition> can represent the leading tangential <velocity> correction, but its <slip length> must come from the relevant gas–wall interaction model. A nearby <Knudsen layer> still requires kinetic treatment if its detailed structure is wanted. Slip corrections are perturbations; extending them into order-one <Knudsen numbers> has no general accuracy guarantee. At order-one <Knudsen number>, wall and intermolecular <collisions> both matter; this transitional situation calls for a <kinetic theory> model. At $\mathrm{Kn}\gg1$, <free molecular flow> becomes a useful limiting approximation. These are scale comparisons, with accuracy depending on geometry, flow conditions and the observable, rather than universal sharp numerical boundaries. https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19860015045/downloads/19860015045.pdf#page=3[Frank G. Collins (1985), Low Density Gas Dynamic Wall Boundary Conditions, introduction]{ref}

= Knudsen layer
{c}
{parent=Rarefied gas flow approximations}
{wiki}

A <Knudsen layer> is the gas region adjoining a boundary where the molecular distribution departs from the bulk near-equilibrium description over distances of order a few <mean free paths>. Continuum slip and jump conditions describe an effective boundary for the outer solution; they do not resolve this layer. https://doi.org/10.1021/ie9600351[Rosner and Papadopoulos (1996), Jump, Slip, and Creep Boundary Conditions at Nonequilibrium Gas/Solid Interfaces]{ref}

= Free molecular flow
{parent=Rarefied gas flow approximations}
{wiki}

In <free molecular flow>, gas–gas <collisions> are negligible over the trajectories relevant to the observable. Molecules still encounter walls, whose scattering of <momentum> and <energy> must be modeled. In a long narrow tube, $\ell_{\mathrm{mfp}}$ exceeding the diameter does not by itself prove that every complete transmission path is collision-free: repeated wall reflections can create much longer paths. The approximation must be checked against those paths as well as the width. https://farside.ph.utexas.edu/teaching/355/Surveyhtml/node223.html[Fitzpatrick, Molecular Flow, wall encounters in a long tube]{ref}

{wiki}

= Molecular effusion
{synonym}

<Effusion> is molecular passage through a small opening whose dimensions, including its thickness, are much smaller than the intermolecular <mean free path>. For an equilibrium <ideal gas>, the one-way particle rate through a thin hole of area $A$ is $\dot N=A p/\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}$. This weights molecules by their wall-normal <velocity>, rather than just counting all molecules in the reservoir. https://farside.ph.utexas.edu/teaching/355/Surveyhtml/node224.html[Fitzpatrick, Molecular Effusion, equations 5.284–5.285]{ref}

= Effusive molecular energy flux
{parent=Effusion}
{title2=$F_E=2k_BT\,n\overline v/4$}

An equilibrium Maxwell-Boltzmann gas has number flux $n\overline v/4$ onto a plane. Molecules crossing the plane are weighted by their positive normal <velocity>, so their mean translational <kinetic energy> is $2k_BT$, rather than the bulk value $3k_BT/2$. The translational <energy> flux is therefore $F_E=n\overline v k_BT/2$. Internal degrees of freedom contribute their mean <energy> per crossing molecule when independent of translational <velocity>.

= Planar effusion flux
{parent=Effusion}
{title2=$\Gamma=w n_2\langle v\rangle/\pi$}

For an isotropic two-dimensional <velocity> distribution, a straight opening of width $w$ in a region of number per <area> $n_2$ loses particles at the displayed rate. The angular integral is $\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\,d\theta/(2\pi)=1/\pi$. At fixed equilibrium <kinetic energy> the <speed distribution> scales as $m^{-1/2}$, so the rate has the same scaling. A fixed second moment alone does not determine the first moment without a distribution assumption.

= Collisionless effusion
{parent=Effusion}

During <collisionless effusion>, selective loss changes the distribution of surviving particle velocities without collision-driven relaxation to a new <Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution>. The loss law depends on flight paths and on the wall-reflection rule. Speed-dependent depletion and, for specular walls, directional structure can remain.

= Effusive cooling
{parent=Effusion}
{title2=$T\propto N^{1/3}$}

If a classical monatomic gas re-equilibrates by collisions while losing atoms by <effusion>, the escaping atoms carry mean <kinetic energy> $2k_BT$. Without wall heating, <conservation of energy> gives $d(3Nk_BT/2)=2k_BT\,dN$ and therefore $T\propto N^{1/3}$. This assumes a fixed container <volume (thermodynamics)> and a small aperture in the molecular-flow regime.

= Two-vessel effusive mixing
{parent=Effusion}
{title2=$\tau_s=4V/[A_h(\overline v_{s,L}+\overline v_{s,R})]$}

For a species $s$ in two well-mixed vessels of equal <volume (thermodynamics)> $V$ joined by a thin opening of <area> $A_h$, the <effusion> law gives $\dot n_{s,L}=-(A_h/4V)(\overline v_{s,L}n_{s,L}-\overline v_{s,R}n_{s,R})$. Each species' total inventory is conserved. With fixed <temperatures> and total density sum $n_{s,L}+n_{s,R}=n_{s,\Sigma}$, the left density relaxes exponentially with <time> constant $\tau_s=4V/[A_h(\overline v_{s,L}+\overline v_{s,R})]$ to $n_{s,L}^{\infty}=n_{s,\Sigma}\sqrt{T_R}/(\sqrt{T_L}+\sqrt{T_R})$.

At equal <temperatures>, $\tau_s=2V/(A_h\overline v_s)\propto\sqrt{m_s}$, and initially separated species mix at different rates. At unequal <temperatures>, the final density ratio is $n_{s,L}/n_{s,R}=\sqrt{T_R/T_L}$ for every species, while the partial-pressure ratio is $p_{s,L}/p_{s,R}=\sqrt{T_L/T_R}$. The mass-independent final ratios do not imply mass-independent transient rates. Maintaining different <temperatures> requires external <heat> exchange even after net <particle> transfer vanishes.

= Thermal transpiration
{parent=Kinetic theory}
{wiki}

<Thermal transpiration> is gas transport driven by an imposed <temperature> difference in a rarefied connection. Gas can move from cold to hot even when the two end <pressures> start equal. If the reservoirs are closed, redistribution builds a counteracting <pressure> difference until the net transfer stops. This final stationary state is not global <thermal equilibrium>: the two <temperatures> remain different. It explains why a heated pressure sensor can read a different <pressure> from the vessel to which it is connected. The correction depends on gas, connection geometry and molecular interaction with its surfaces. https://arxiv.org/html/1308.6794[Daudé, Elandaloussi and Janssen (2014), thermal-transpiration corrections for diaphragm gauges, introduction]{ref}

The simple molecular limit is beautiful: equal opposing particle rates need not mean equal <pressures>. Consider <effusion> through a thin hole connecting two locally equilibrated reservoirs of the same gas, at $T_h>T_c$. Equating their one-way rates gives
$$
\frac{p_c}{\sqrt{T_c}}=\frac{p_h}{\sqrt{T_h}},\qquad \frac{p_h}{p_c}=\sqrt{\frac{T_h}{T_c}}.
$$
At equal <pressure>, the hotter reservoir has lower <number density>; its faster molecules do not fully compensate for that reduction in outgoing particle rate. With $T_c=300\,\mathrm K$ and $T_h=351\,\mathrm K$, the ideal zero-transfer ratio is $\sqrt{351/300}\simeq1.0817$. The hydrodynamic, zero-flow limit instead has equal <pressures> when other forces are absent. These are limiting models, not an interpolation formula for a glass capillary. https://farside.ph.utexas.edu/teaching/355/Surveyhtml/node224.html[Fitzpatrick, Molecular Effusion, equations 5.286–5.287]{ref} In a real tube, incomplete exchange of molecular <energy> with the wall can alter the limiting ratio; a <Knudsen number> does not specify that exchange. https://arxiv.org/html/1308.6794[Daudé, Elandaloussi and Janssen, §I, limitations of the ideal Knudsen pressure ratio]{ref}

= Closed-vessel thermal transpiration
{parent=Thermal transpiration}
{title2=$\Delta p=2p_0q(q-1)/(q+1)$}

Two equal sealed vessels of fixed <volume (thermodynamics)>, initially at $T_0,p_0$, exchange an <ideal gas> through a thin effusive opening. Hold one at $T_1$ and the other at $T_0$, with both interiors locally thermalized. Zero net <particle> transfer gives $p_h/\sqrt{T_1}=p_c/\sqrt{T_0}$. Writing $q=\sqrt{T_1/T_0}$, conservation of total <particle number> gives $p_h=2p_0q^2/(1+q)$ and $p_c=2p_0q/(1+q)$. Thus $\Delta p=2p_0q(q-1)/(1+q)$. The alternative $p_c=p_0$, $\Delta p=p_0(q-1)$ requires a cold reservoir whose <pressure> is externally held fixed; it is not the closed equal-volume problem.

= Thermal transpiration experiment with a glass microtube
{parent=Thermal transpiration}

Rojas Cardenas, Graur, Perrier and Méolans studied <thermal transpiration> in <helium> using a glass microtube of diameter $490\pm1\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ and length $3.05\pm0.01\,\mathrm{cm}$ in their 2012 experiment. They heated one reservoir, leaving the other near room <temperature>, and measured tube-end <temperatures> of $300$ and $351\,\mathrm K$. Three contact <thermometers> and an infrared camera checked the thermal conditions; the hot reservoir was warmer than the adjacent tube end, so heater <temperature> was not an adequate substitute for tube-end <temperature>. Two <capacitance diaphragm gauges> recorded the reservoir <pressures>; a third gauge supported calibration. https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jtst/7/3/7_437/_pdf/-char/en#page=4[Rojas Cardenas et al. (2012), An Experimental and Numerical Study of the Final Zero-Flow Thermal Transpiration Stage, §2.1]{ref}

\Image[thermal-transpiration-bypass-and-flux.png]
{title=Thermal-transpiration measurement geometry and an ideal effusion comparison}
{description=The upper panel is a simplified apparatus schematic, not a reproduction of the experimental hardware. The lower panels compare one-way rates in a thin-hole toy model with $T_c=300\,\mathrm K$ and $T_h=351\,\mathrm K$; they are not measured capillary data.}

Their earlier 2011 paper explains the measurement sequence. A wide bypass links the reservoirs in parallel with the microtube. After isolating the gas supply and pump, keep the bypass open to equalize <pressures> with the <temperature> difference already established. Closing the bypass defines $t=0$. The hot-side <pressure> rises and the cold-side <pressure> falls; ultimately their slopes approach zero at unequal <pressures>. Repeating at different initial <pressures> changes the degree of rarefaction without changing the microtube. The 2011 study also compared <helium> and <nitrogen> and imposed <temperature> differences of $50$, $60$ and $70\,\mathrm K$. https://doi.org/10.1063/1.3561744[Rojas Cardenas et al. (2011), Thermal transpiration flow: A circular cross-section microtube submitted to a temperature gradient, measurement sequence]{ref}

The quantities to record are both $p_c(t)$ and $p_h(t)$, the reservoir volumes, and the <temperatures> relevant to their particle inventories. In an idealized reservoir of fixed volume $V_i$ and uniform fixed <temperature> $T_i$, the <ideal gas law> gives $\dot N_i=[V_i/(k_BT_i)]\dot p_i$, so opposite inventory changes test particle conservation. If <temperature> changes, differentiation instead gives $\dot N_i=(V_i/k_B)[\dot p_i/T_i-p_i\dot T_i/T_i^2]$: a thermal drift can imitate particle transfer. The authors' 2011 presentation explicitly checks this thermal correction when extracting an initial <mass flow rate> from the pressure slope. https://www.itep.kit.edu/downloads/12_Rojas.pdf#page=25[Rojas et al., IUVSTA 2011 presentation, mass-flow extraction and thermal correction]{ref}

The 2012 paper compares the final <pressure> ratio across near-free-molecular through slip conditions with a kinetic model and empirical corrections. It does not establish the thin-hole square-root law at every measured <pressure>. https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jtst/7/3/7_437/_pdf/-char/en[Rojas Cardenas et al. (2012), abstract and comparison of final pressure ratios]{ref} For a replication, useful additional controls follow from the interpretation: remove the <temperature> difference to test the null signal; reverse it to test the direction; check gauge zero offsets and leaks; and wait for stable <temperatures> before closing the bypass. These are suggested controls, not claims that the cited paper performed each one. A reproducible pressure split at zero net transfer makes the failure of a single continuum wall model observable, while the <Knudsen number> organizes which molecular and apparatus scales are competing.

= Capacitance diaphragm gauge
{parent=Thermal transpiration experiment with a glass microtube}

A <capacitance diaphragm gauge> senses <pressure> through deflection of a diaphragm that changes a <capacitor>'s <capacitance>. Its head can have a different <temperature> from the vessel being measured, allowing <thermal transpiration> in the connecting tube to change the head <pressure>. https://arxiv.org/html/1308.6794[Daudé, Elandaloussi and Janssen, §I, diaphragm-gauge principle and thermal-transpiration effect]{ref}

= Molecular chaos for the Boltzmann equation
{parent=Kinetic theory}

The incoming two-particle distribution at a collision is approximated by the product of one-particle distributions. This closes the binary collision term into the quadratic <nonlinear Boltzmann collision operator>. It does not assert exact independence after collisions: outgoing pairs can be correlated, and time reversal generally converts those correlations into incoming correlations. A rigorous kinetic limit needs propagation and convergence estimates rather than a bare independence assertion.

= Unordered-pair collision rate
{parent=Kinetic theory}
{title2=$\Gamma_{ii}=K_{ii}N_i(N_i-1)/2$}

For a dilute phase-mixed population, an identical-species <collision> rate counts unordered pairs: $\Gamma_{ii}=K_{ii}N_i(N_i-1)/2$. Different species have rate $\Gamma_{ij}=K_{ij}N_iN_j$. Here $K_{ij}=\langle\sigma_{ij}v_{ij}\rangle/V$ uses the <collision cross-section> and relative speed. Destroying both identical particles gives $\dot N_i=-2\Gamma_{ii}$, not $-\Gamma_{ii}$. This distinction prevents inconsistent factors when using one kinetic kernel for several particle sizes.

= Kac model
{parent=Kinetic theory}
{c}

= Kac's model
{c}
{synonym}

This many-particle stochastic collision model rotates one uniformly selected velocity pair by a uniformly selected angle, conserving the sum of squared velocities. Its normalized angular averaging is the <Kac collision operator>, and its continuous-time evolution is the <Kac master equation>. In this version velocities range over $\mathbb R^N$ and initial energy is not restricted to one sphere; the evolution preserves the initial energy distribution.

= Relative entropy in Kac's model
{parent=Kac model}
{title2=$H_N(F)=\int F\log(F/\gamma_N)$}

Use $\gamma_N=(2\pi)^{-N/2}e^{-|v|^2/2}$ as the reference probability density. The <relative entropy> is nonnegative by writing $F=\gamma_Nu$ and integrating $u\log u-u+1$. Its expansion is $\int F\log F+(N/2)\log(2\pi)+(1/2)\int|v|^2F$. Equality holds only at $F=\gamma_N$, whereas other radial densities may still be stationary for the <Kac master equation>.

= Kac entropy production
{parent=Relative entropy in Kac's model}
{c}
{title2=$D_N(F)=-\frac d{dt}H_N(F)\geq0$}

= Kac entropy dissipation
{c}
{synonym}

For the <Kac master equation> and finite differentiable entropy, mass and energy conservation remove the non-logarithmic entropy terms. Symmetrizing each pair rotation then gives $D_N(F)=[2\pi(N-1)]^{-1}\sum_{i<j}\int\int(F\circ R_{ij,\theta}-F)\log((F\circ R_{ij,\theta})/F)\,dv\,d\theta$. The integrand is nonnegative by monotonicity of the logarithm, with the usual extended convention at zeros. Zero dissipation corresponds to radiality; it need not imply zero relative entropy.

= Kac chaos
{parent=Kac model}
{c}
{title2=$\Pi_k(F_N)\rightharpoonup f^{\otimes k}$}

A permutation-symmetric sequence of probability laws is chaotic with one-particle law $f$ when, for every fixed $k$, its $k$-coordinate <marginal distributions> converge weakly to the product law $f^{\otimes k}$ as $N\to\infty$. This is an asymptotic independence condition, not a consequence of symmetry alone. A rigorous kinetic limit also requires propagation of this property under the dynamics and sufficient convergence to pass collision <integrals>.

= Kac master equation
{parent=Kac model}
{c}
{title2=$\partial_tF_N=N(Q-I)F_N$}

= Kac's master equation
{c}
{synonym}

The jump process with total collision rate $N$ and normalized pair-angle operator $Q$ evolves by $\partial_tF_N=N(Q-I)F_N$. It preserves mass and the total squared velocity. Its one-coordinate distribution satisfies the <Kac marginal evolution equation>, and its Gaussian <relative entropy in Kac's model> decreases at the rate given by <Kac entropy production>.

= Kac marginal evolution equation
{parent=Kac master equation}
{c}
{title2=$\partial_t\Pi_1=\pi^{-1}\int(\Pi_2\circ R_\theta-\Pi_2)\,dv_2d\theta$}

For a permutation-symmetric density in the <Kac master equation>, integrate over all but the first velocity. Collisions among integrated coordinates cancel. The $N-1$ pairs containing the first coordinate give equal terms, with coefficient $N(N-1)/(2\pi\binom N2)=1/\pi$. The resulting exact evolution depends on the two-coordinate <marginal distribution>; it is not a closed equation for the first marginal without additional asymptotic independence.

= Kac collision operator
{parent=Kac model}
{c}
{title2=$Q=\binom N2^{-1}\sum_{i<j}(2\pi)^{-1}\int U_{ij,\theta}\,d\theta$}

For $N\geq2$, $U_{ij,\theta}F=F\circ R_{ij,\theta}$ uses the genuine two-coordinate rotation $(v_i,v_j)\mapsto(v_i\cos\theta+v_j\sin\theta,-v_i\sin\theta+v_j\cos\theta)$. Average it uniformly over angles and pairs to obtain $Q$. Rotation invariance of <Lebesgue measure> makes $Q$ a bounded <self-adjoint operator> of <norm> one on $L^2(\mathbb R^N)$. Each pair average is an <orthogonal projection>, but their full average is not in general a projection.

= Dirichlet form of the Kac collision operator
{parent=Kac collision operator}
{c}
{title2=$\langle F,(I-Q)F\rangle\geq0$}

For the <Kac collision operator>, expanding the squared <Hilbert space> <norm> gives $\langle F,(I-Q)F\rangle=(4\pi\binom N2)^{-1}\sum_{i<j}\int_0^{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R^N}|F(R_{ij,\theta}v)-F(v)|^2dv\,d\theta$. The nonnegative form measures failure of invariance under the pair rotations. It vanishes precisely on the <radial kernel of the Kac collision operator>.

= Radial kernel of the Kac collision operator
{parent=Dirichlet form of the Kac collision operator}
{title2=$\ker(I-Q)=L^2_{\rm radial}(\mathbb R^N)$}

Zero <Dirichlet form of the Kac collision operator> forces invariance under every pair rotation; strong continuity upgrades almost every angle to every angle. Coordinate-plane rotations generate the <special orthogonal group>, whose action is transitive on each sphere for $N\geq2$. Averaging over normalized <Haar measure> therefore identifies the invariant $L^2$ functions with <radial functions>. Conversely radial functions are fixed by every pair rotation.

= Free-transport phase mixing
{parent=Kinetic theory}
{title2=$\widehat f_t(k,\xi)=\widehat f_0(k,\xi+kt)$}

= Kinetic phase mixing
{synonym}

On the unit-period spatial circle, free streaming has solution $f_t(x,v)=f_0(x-tv,v)$. With Fourier phase $e^{-2\pi i(kx+\xi v)}$, its mixed <Fourier transform> is $\widehat f_t(k,\xi)=\widehat f_0(k,\xi+kt)$. A nonzero spatial mode of the velocity-integrated density therefore samples increasingly large velocity frequencies. The conserved spatial zero mode remains, while extra velocity regularity supplies quantitative decay of the other modes.

= Uniform phase-mixing bound from velocity derivatives
{parent=Free-transport phase mixing}
{title2=$\|\rho_t-\rho_\infty\|_\infty\leq 2\zeta(m)\|\partial_v^mf_0\|_1/(2\pi|t|)^m$}

For $m\geq2$ integrable weak velocity derivatives on a unit-period spatial circle, the <Fourier transform of a derivative> gives $|\widehat f_0(k,kt)|\leq\|\partial_v^mf_0\|_1/(2\pi|kt|)^m$ for $k\ne0$. Summing these modes uses $\sum_{k\ne0}|k|^{-m}=2\zeta(m)$. The resulting absolute Fourier summability gives the displayed uniform bound for the continuous representative of the density. For $m=1$ the same summation diverges, so this argument needs additional input.

= Mean velocity of a kinetic distribution
{parent=Kinetic theory}
{title2=$u=\frac{\int vf(x,v)\,dx\,dv}{\int f(x,v)\,dx\,dv}$}

= Mean velocity
{synonym}

The mass-normalized first velocity moment of a distribution on <phase space>. For a velocity-only distribution, omit the position integral. Nonzero finite <mass> and a finite first moment are required. For a probability distribution this is its <expected value>; its unnormalized counterpart is the <momentum>.

= Hamiltonian Liouville equation
{c}
{parent=Kinetic theory}
{title2=$\partial_tf+\nabla_vH\cdot\nabla_xf-\nabla_xH\cdot\nabla_vf=h$}

= Liouville transport equation
{c}
{synonym}

Transport a phase-space density along <Hamilton's equations>. The <Hamiltonian vector field> is $b=(\nabla_vH,-\nabla_xH)$ and the equation is $\partial_tf+b\cdot\nabla_{x,v}f=h$. Its zero <divergence> gives volume-preserving transport. For $h=0$, the density is constant along characteristics; a source is integrated by the <Duhamel formula for Hamiltonian transport>. This is distinct from the elliptic Liouville equation.

= Hyperbolic characteristic flow for an inverted oscillator
{parent=Hamiltonian Liouville equation}
{title2=$A_t=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh t&\sinh t\\\sinh t&\cosh t\end{pmatrix}$}

The <Hamiltonian> $(v^2-x^2)/2$ gives $\dot x=v$, $\dot v=x$. Its <characteristic flow map> is multiplication by $A_t$, with $A_tA_s=A_{t+s}$ and determinant one. The backward map is $A_{-t}$. This expanding-contracting linear flow preserves phase-space <Lebesgue measure> and every finite-$p$ <integral> of a transported density, despite stretching its level sets.

= Invariant-source secular growth in Hamiltonian transport
{parent=Hamiltonian Liouville equation}
{title2=$f_t=t\,h$}

If $f_0=0$ and a nonzero time-independent source is invariant along the <Hamiltonian flow>, then the <Duhamel formula for Hamiltonian transport> gives $f_t=t h$. For the nonzero-frequency oscillator, $h=e^{-H_\omega}$ is smooth and belongs to all finite <Lp spaces>, yet its resulting solution has infinite space-time norm on $(0,\infty)$.

= Finite-time Lp bound for Hamiltonian transport
{parent=Hamiltonian Liouville equation}
{title2=$\|f(t)\|_p\leq\|f_0\|_p+\int_0^t\|h(s)\|_p\,ds$}

The <Duhamel formula for Hamiltonian transport>, <phase space> preservation and <Minkowski integral inequality> give the displayed bound. A time-independent source in an <Lp space> yields finite space-time norm on every bounded interval; it need not yield integrability on infinite time.

= Spatially nonintegrable forcing in transport
{parent=Finite-time Lp bound for Hamiltonian transport}
{title2=$h=x^2+v^2$}

Smooth forcing need not be integrable in phase space. For the <hyperbolic characteristic flow for an inverted oscillator>, the source $x^2+v^2$ accumulates as $q_t=\tfrac12\sinh(2t)(x^2+v^2)-(\cosh(2t)-1)xv$. Its smallest quadratic-form eigenvalue is $(1-e^{-2t})/2>0$ for $t>0$. Starting with a nonnegative integrable <Gaussian function>, the solution is therefore unbounded and outside every finite <Lp space> at positive times. Locally time-integrable $L^p$ forcing prevents this failure by the <Minkowski integral inequality>.

= Duhamel formula for Hamiltonian transport
{c}
{parent=Hamiltonian Liouville equation}
{title2=$f(t,z)=f_0(\Phi(0,t,z))+\int_0^th(s,\Phi(s,t,z))\,ds$}

For a complete invertible <Hamiltonian flow> $\Phi(t,s)$, integrate the source along the backward characteristic ending at $z$ at time $t$. The displayed formula solves the <Hamiltonian Liouville equation>. Volume preservation makes each composition with $\Phi(s,t)$ an <isometry> of <Lp spaces>.

= Vlasov equation
{c}
{parent=Kinetic theory}
{wiki}

The <Vlasov equation> describes collisionless evolution of a phase-space <distribution function> in a self-consistent force field: $\partial_t f+\mathbf v\cdot\nabla_xf+\mathbf F\cdot\nabla_pf=0$. The force can be electromagnetic or gravitational; the <collisionless dark-matter Vlasov equation> is the cosmological gravitational specialization.
