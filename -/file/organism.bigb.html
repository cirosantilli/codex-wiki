= Organism
{wiki}

An <organism> is an individual living system, made of one <biological cell> or an integrated assembly of <biological cells>. An <Escherichia coli> cell is an entire <organism>; a <neuron> is normally a specialized part of an <animal>. <Metabolism>, regulated exchange through <cell membranes>, and inherited information connect these very different scales. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/1-2-themes-and-concepts-of-biology[OpenStax Biology 2e, 1.2: living systems and levels of organization].

Keep two questions separate: how many <biological cells> make the <organism>, and how those <biological cells> are organized. A <yeast> and an <Escherichia coli> are both <unicellular organisms>, but the <yeast> is a <eukaryote> and the <Escherichia coli> a <prokaryote>. A <plant> and an <animal> are <multicellular organisms> with <eukaryotic> cellular organization. Neither distinction ranks <organisms> as better or worse: <natural selection> acts on reproduction in particular environments.

= Unicellular organism
{parent=Organism}
{wiki}

A <unicellular organism> is an <organism> whose living body consists of one <biological cell>. That <biological cell> maintains itself, grows and participates in <reproduction>; it is not merely one specialized component of a larger body. <Escherichia coli> and ordinary free-living <Saccharomyces cerevisiae> illustrate <prokaryotic> and <eukaryotic> examples respectively. Cellular organization and the number of <biological cells> are separate questions. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/4-1-studying-cells[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 4.1: cells as units of organisms].

How can one <biological cell> do enough to sustain an <organism>? Its <cell membrane> retains useful material while <membrane transport> allows exchange; <metabolism> transforms nutrients and supplies usable <energy>; <gene expression> makes <proteins> needed to maintain and rebuild the machinery. These functions operate together inside the same boundary. In <Saccharomyces cerevisiae>, for example, <glucose> can support <alcoholic fermentation> and production of <ATP>, while the <cell cycle> and <budding> produce descendants. A single <biological cell> therefore combines chemical processing, regulated exchange and inherited organization. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26864/[Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell (2002): universal cellular machinery] and the experimental accounts under <Saccharomyces cerevisiae> and <yeast>.

Being a <unicellular organism> does not mean isolated or independent of all other life. <Unicellular organisms> can interact and cooperate in groups. A <biological colony> describes association; <clonal development> describes descent; <developmental integration> describes coordination of the developing whole. Neither shared ancestry nor a visible clump alone establishes the latter. This distinction makes single-cell experiments easier to interpret: measuring the growth of one <biological cell>, the expansion of a group and the <reproductive success> of a reproducing cluster can answer different questions. See https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2781869/[Queller and Strassmann, Beyond society: the evolution of organismality (2009): cooperation and the limits of simple organism criteria].

= Multicellular organism
{parent=Organism}
{wiki}

A <multicellular organism> has a body of multiple <biological cells> whose growth and activities are integrated into the life of the whole <organism>. Extensive <cell differentiation> is common in complex examples, but specialized <biological cells> are not a prerequisite for every simple form. Ask how the body develops, how its parts cooperate and how it produces descendants, rather than counting attached <biological cells> alone. The boundary with a <biological colony> depends on the degree of integration and the criteria being investigated; there is no universal cell-count threshold. See the Queller and Strassmann account cited under <unicellular organism>.

Specialization makes the whole capable of combinations that one general-purpose <biological cell> cannot achieve in the same way. In an <animal>, a <neuron> transmits signals while a <red blood cell> carries <oxygen>; each still requires a working <cell membrane> and <metabolism>. A mature mammalian <red blood cell> lacks a <cell nucleus> and <mitochondria>, illustrating how a useful part need not retain the equipment of a self-reproducing <organism>. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/40-2-components-of-the-blood[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 40.2: red blood cell specialization]. <Cell differentiation> generally involves different <gene expression>, rather than a different inherited <genome> for each function. <Biological development> must coordinate growth, position, interactions and specialization: increasing the number of <biological cells> is only one component. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK21046/[Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell, chapter 21 (2002): construction of multicellular bodies].

**A motivating experiment: select for a property of groups.** Ratcliff, Denison, Borrello and Travisano's 2012 study began with <Saccharomyces cerevisiae> growing as <unicellular organisms>. Daily transfer favored rapidly settling material. Their initial protocol allowed a $10\,\mathrm{mL}$ culture to settle for $45\,\mathrm{min}$, discarded the upper $9.9\,\mathrm{mL}$, and transferred the bottom $100\,\mathrm{\mu L}$ into fresh medium. After seven days, they instead sampled $1.5\,\mathrm{mL}$ and centrifuged it at $100$ times gravitational acceleration for $10\,\mathrm{s}$, again transferring the bottom $100\,\mathrm{\mu L}$. Faster settling made it more likely that a lineage would seed tomorrow's culture; the procedure selected a physical outcome without specifying how it must arise. These protocol details are explicitly recounted in https://academic.oup.com/evolut/article/73/5/1012/6727309[Gulli, Herron and Ratcliff, Evolution of altruistic cooperation among nascent multicellular organisms (2019): original settling protocol].

The primary 2012 paper reports <snowflake yeast> dominating all ten initial replicate populations by 60 transfers. To distinguish the joining of previously separate <biological cells> from <clonal development>, the researchers enzymatically separated clusters and followed individual <biological cells> for $16\,\mathrm{h}$: attached descendants rebuilt clusters, with no such joining observed. Staining localized attachments at bud scars. Time-lapse observations also showed clusters releasing smaller multicellular offspring, called propagules; small clusters grew before they reproduced. See https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3277146/[Ratcliff et al., Experimental evolution of multicellularity (2012), figures 1–3: emergence, development and reproduction].

The experimental comparisons ask different questions. Competition with the <unicellular organism> ancestor showed a reported $34\%$ advantage under settling selection. Without settling selection, the estimated $10\%$ disadvantage had a one-sided $P=0.06$, so it was suggestive rather than decisive evidence of a cost. Separately, three populations derived from one evolved isolate retained the cluster form through 35 transfers without settling selection. That persistence tests stability, not whether clusters always outperform single <biological cells>. See Ratcliff et al. (2012), figure 2 and the cluster-formation results cited above.

A 2015 follow-up added a causal test: deleting the <gene> ACE2 in the <unicellular organism> ancestor produced <snowflake yeast>, while supplying functional ACE2 to one evolved isolate restored separation into single <biological cells>. Sequence changes altering the encoded <protein> were found in ACE2 in five of ten examined lineages; the study did not identify that same sequence-change mechanism in every lineage. The deletion and restoration comparisons support a mechanism beyond the observation that clusters settle quickly. See https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms7102[Ratcliff, Fankhauser, Rogers, Greig and Travisano, Origins of multicellular evolvability in snowflake yeast (2015), figure 1].

**What follows, and what does not.** The evidence supports inherited cluster formation, <clonal development> and a reproducing collective whose properties can respond to <natural selection>. Calling these simple <multicellular organisms> recognizes those demonstrated properties; it does not turn every clump into an <animal> or establish animal-like <cell differentiation>. This laboratory route also does not reconstruct the actual ancestry of <animals> or show that complex <developmental integration> must follow. The interesting achievement is narrower and testable: a change in how descendants remain connected creates a new scale at which growth and <reproductive success> can be measured.

For the toy figure, let $p$ be the fraction of tracked units of type C before transfer and let $s_C,s_U$ be the respective <probabilities> of transfer for C and U. Assume equally weighted units and equal multiplication between transfers. When overall transfer <probability> is nonzero, conditioning on transfer gives
$$
p_{\mathrm{after}}=\frac{p s_C}{p s_C+(1-p)s_U}.
$$
With $p=1/5$, $s_C=4/5$ and $s_U=1/10$, the numerator is $4/25$ and the denominator $6/25$, giving $p_{\mathrm{after}}=2/3$. If both types have the same nonzero transfer <probability>, the fraction stays $1/5$. This is <conditional probability>, not a model of the full experimental life cycle: real cluster sizes, growth and fragmentation affect which quantity a frequency measures. In particular, a fraction of clusters is not automatically a fraction of <biological cells>.

= Germline-soma differentiation
{parent=Multicellular organism}

Differentiation between cell lineages contributing to future organisms and those mainly maintaining the current body. Restricting transmission routes can help align cellular reproduction with organismal reproduction.

= Clonal development
{parent=Multicellular organism}

<Clonal development> builds a group through successive divisions of founding <biological cells>, rather than by gathering previously separate lineages. In <snowflake yeast>, daughter <biological cells> remain attached after <budding>. Common ancestry does not imply that every descendant remains genetically identical forever: new <mutations> can arise. See the 2015 study cited under <multicellular organism>.

= Snowflake yeast
{parent=Clonal development}

<Snowflake yeast> are cluster-forming <Saccharomyces cerevisiae> with branching connections between mothers and attached daughter <biological cells>. They provide an experimental system for studying early transitions to simple <multicellular organisms>; see the protocol, observations and limits under <multicellular organism>.

= Developmental integration
{parent=Multicellular organism}

<Developmental integration> here means coordination of cellular growth, organization and function into a developing whole. It describes what the parts do together, whereas <clonal development> describes how they descend from founders. Interactions through <signal transduction> can help coordinate <cell differentiation>; see the Alberts et al. development chapter cited under <multicellular organism>.

= Cell differentiation
{synonym}

<Cell differentiation> is the acquisition of a specialized <cell state>, involving changes in structure, function and <gene expression>. A <neuron> and a <red blood cell> illustrate different outcomes: signaling and <oxygen> transport. Different functions usually do not require a different inherited <DNA> sequence for each function. The shared-<genome> principle concerns nucleated precursors and most nucleated descendants: a mature mammalian <red blood cell> has lost its <cell nucleus> and <mitochondria>, so it is not an example of two mature <biological cells> both retaining the same nuclear <DNA>. <Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[Biology 2e, §40.2, Red Blood Cells: specialization and absence of a nucleus and mitochondria at mammalian maturity]{ref}

The puzzle is how a shared set of <genes> supports different jobs. <Gene expression> changes which <RNA> and <proteins> a <biological cell> produces, and in what amounts. A <transcription factor> can regulate several <genes>; different combinations of regulators can interpret the same signal differently. Stable regulatory patterns can persist through the <cell cycle>, providing memory of developmental history. Specialization therefore usually changes use of inherited information, rather than replacing the <genome> wholesale. <Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition by Bruce Alberts and others>[Alberts et al., The Molecular Genetic Mechanisms That Create Specialized Cell Types, subsections The Expression of a Set of Genes Can Be Coordinated by a Single Protein and Stable Patterns of Gene Expression Can Be Transmitted to Daughter Cells, and Summary]{ref}

A small counting example separates sequence from activity. Suppose the same three toy <genes> are present in every nucleated <biological cell>, and independently assign each <gene> an on/off state. There are $2^3=8$ possible expression profiles with no <DNA> replacement. The figure shows only two: H and A on versus H and B on. Real <gene expression> is graded and coordinated, so this count illustrates possible descriptions, not a prediction of eight actual cell types. The interesting consequence is that a stable <cell state> can depend on how a shared record is used.

<Stem cells> combine self-renewal with production of differentiated descendants; <progenitor cells> generally have more restricted developmental options and renewal capacity. Neither term means that a <biological cell> must give rise to every cell type. <Terminal differentiation> denotes advanced specialization within a lineage, often accompanied by exit from the <cell cycle>. <Cell fate> concerns the developmental outcome of a <biological cell> or its descendants, whereas <cell state> describes its current properties. A temporary change in <gene expression> after <signal transduction> is not, by itself, proof of a new <cell fate>. <Anatomy and Physiology 2e by J. Gordon Betts and others>[Anatomy and Physiology 2e, §3.6, Stem Cells and Differentiation: renewal, restricted differentiation potential and regulation of gene expression]{ref}

= Mesenchymal-to-epithelial transition
{parent=Cellular differentiation}

A transition toward epithelial organisation and behaviour, potentially contributing to metastatic colonisation in particular cancer models.

= Epithelial-mesenchymal plasticity
{parent=Cellular differentiation}

The ability of cells to move among epithelial, mesenchymal and intermediate regulatory states. The benefit of a state can vary between dissemination, persistence and metastatic outgrowth.

= Epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition
{parent=Cellular differentiation}

A transition from an epithelial organisation toward more individually migratory cellular behaviour, involving changes in adhesion and polarity.

= Competence window
{parent=Cellular differentiation}

The developmental interval during which a cell can make a specified response to a signal. Absence of response outside the interval need not mean that the signal is absent.

= Conditional specification
{parent=Cellular differentiation}

Specification that depends on a cell's interactions with its surroundings. Neighbour exchange and controlled signal perturbation can test it causally.

= Autonomous specification
{parent=Cellular differentiation}

Specification driven by information inherited within a cell, such as asymmetrically partitioned maternal material. It can coexist with external inputs later in development.

= Cell fate
{parent=Cellular differentiation}

<Cell fate> is the developmental outcome of a <biological cell> or its descendants in a specified setting. What it normally becomes and what it can become after an experimental intervention are different questions. The <intestinal nucleus transfer experiment> tests potential after <cellular reprogramming>, rather than directly tracing the normal <cell fate> of the donor.

= Developmental commitment
{parent=Cell fate}

Persistence of a developmental choice. <cell specification> tests behaviour in a neutral environment; <cell determination> tests persistence in a viable conflicting environment.

= Cell fate plasticity
{parent=Cell fate}

Context-dependent capacity to change differentiated identity or developmental potential. Demonstrating a transition requires distinguishing conversion of a defined starting cell from selection of a rare pre-existing population.

= Intestinal stem-cell plasticity
{parent=Cell fate plasticity}

Changes in intestinal epithelial potential that allow previously committed cells to restore a renewing stem-cell compartment under particular conditions.

= Transdifferentiation
{parent=Cell fate plasticity}

Conversion between differentiated cellular identities. Whether a progenitor-like intermediate is traversed is an experimental question rather than part of a universal mechanism.

= Dedifferentiation
{parent=Cell fate plasticity}

A return from a more committed state toward a less committed or progenitor-like state. Re-expression of one marker is weaker evidence than sustained renewal and functional lineage output.

= Cell determination
{parent=Cell fate}

A commitment demonstrated by retaining a fate in a conflicting environment that is viable and supplies the alternative developmental cues.

= Cell specification
{parent=Cell fate}

A provisional developmental commitment demonstrated operationally by adopting a fate in an appropriate neutral environment. It is weaker than <cell determination>.

= Fate map
{parent=Cell fate}

A description of the normal developmental descendants of identified embryonic regions or cells. It reports realised fate, not the entire potential revealed by experimental transplantation.

= Terminal differentiation
{parent=Cellular differentiation}

<Terminal differentiation> is advanced specialization at the end of a developmental lineage. It often includes withdrawal from the <cell cycle>; the term does not mean that every nuclear <gene> needed for another <cell fate> has been deleted. A mature mammalian <red blood cell> is an especially restrictive example because it lacks a <cell nucleus>; the <intestinal nucleus transfer experiment> used nucleated donors.

= Cellular reprogramming
{parent=Cellular differentiation}

<Cellular reprogramming> changes an established <cell state> so that a different developmental program becomes accessible. Experimentally induced <cellular reprogramming> must be distinguished from the ordinary progression of <cell differentiation>. An <ovum>'s <cytoplasm> can reset aspects of a transferred <cell nucleus>; this does not mean that a specialized donor normally returns to an early developmental state in its original surroundings. <Mature cells can be reprogrammed to become pluripotent by Nobel Assembly at Karolinska Institutet>[Nobel Assembly, Mature cells can be reprogrammed to become pluripotent, Scientific background: Gurdon nuclear transfer and distinction between differentiation and reprogramming]{ref}

= Epigenetic reprogramming
{parent=Cellular reprogramming}

Large-scale changes in chromatin-associated regulatory states and <DNA methylation> during a developmental transition. Loss of methylation is not by itself a measurement of reduced transcription.

= Somatic cell nuclear transfer
{parent=Cellular reprogramming}
{wiki}

<Somatic cell nuclear transfer> introduces a body-cell <cell nucleus> into an <ovum> whose own nuclear contribution has been removed or inactivated. The donor nuclear <genome> and recipient <cytoplasm> have different origins; successful development does not show that the donor <cell nucleus> acts independently of the recipient's machinery.

= Intestinal nucleus transfer experiment
{parent=Somatic cell nuclear transfer}

**Can a specialized donor still supply information for other cell types?** The 1962 https://doi.org/10.1242/dev.10.4.622[The Developmental Capacity of Nuclei taken from Intestinal Epithelium Cells of Feeding Tadpoles by J. B. Gurdon] used mid-intestinal cells of feeding <Xenopus laevis> tadpoles. Donors were recognized by columnar shape, basal <cell nuclei> and a striated absorptive border. Dissociation used Versene solution; this made individual donors accessible. The readout was subsequent development, including normal feeding tadpoles, rather than just division or survival. <The Developmental Capacity of Nuclei taken from Intestinal Epithelium Cells of Feeding Tadpoles by J. B. Gurdon>[Gurdon (1962), Material and Methods and Normal tadpoles; inspected primary text reproduced in the indexed Paperity preview]{ref}

**Apparatus and intervention.** A microscope with <ultraviolet> illumination directed light at the surface region containing the unfertilized <ovum>'s <chromosomes>, inactivating the recipient nuclear contribution. Irradiation also softened the surrounding jelly so a glass micropipette could enter. The pipette delivered donor nuclear material into recipient <cytoplasm>. In the technique's validation, fertilizing irradiated eggs produced <haploid> embryos, supporting destruction of the maternal nuclear contribution. This is inactivation, not a diagram of surgically removing an intact <cell nucleus>. <The Egg and the Nucleus: A Battle for Supremacy by J. B. Gurdon>[Gurdon, The Egg and the Nucleus: A Battle for Supremacy, The Technique of Nuclear Transfer in Xenopus; indexed authored Nobel lecture text inspected]{ref}

**Controls ask different questions.** Donor <cell nuclei> carried a marker absent from recipients; post-<blastula> embryos carried that marker. The marker distinguished diploid donors with one <nucleolus> per <cell nucleus> from the usual two-<nucleolus> recipient background, rather than mistaking one <nucleolus> for proof of a <haploid> state. <The Egg and the Nucleus: A Battle for Supremacy by J. B. Gurdon>[Gurdon, Nobel lecture, The Technique of Nuclear Transfer in Xenopus, p. 233: one-nucleolus diploid marker and its use to establish donor nuclear origin]{ref} Interspersed transfers of <blastula> or later embryonic <cell nuclei> checked variable recipient quality. A positive embryonic-donor control thus tests the preparation, while the marker tests donor origin. Abnormal development remained common. Neither control establishes universal transfer competence. <The Developmental Capacity of Nuclei taken from Intestinal Epithelium Cells of Feeding Tadpoles by J. B. Gurdon>[Gurdon (1962), Material and Methods (nuclear marker), Results (interspersed embryonic controls), and Normal tadpoles (abnormal outcomes); primary text inspected through the indexed preview]{ref}

**Inference and limits.** A successful marked donor demonstrates that specialization had not irreversibly removed all information required to build other cell types. Failure is less decisive: transfer damage, recipient condition and incomplete <cellular reprogramming> can also prevent development. The marker identifies nuclear origin; it is not a complete <DNA> sequence comparison. The experiment tests donor nuclear potential in an altered <cytoplasm>, rather than normal donor <cell fate>, a universal capacity of every donor, or development from a mature mammalian <red blood cell>. The Nobel account also describes serial transfers through embryonic intermediates; these must not be silently combined with direct transfers into one success rate. <Mature cells can be reprogrammed to become pluripotent by Nobel Assembly at Karolinska Institutet>[Nobel Assembly, Scientific background, Gurdon's nuclear-transfer experiments and serial transplantation]{ref}

= Prokaryotic
{synonym}

A <prokaryote> is an <organism> whose <biological cells> lack a membrane-bounded <cell nucleus>. The <DNA> of its <chromosomes> occupies a <nucleoid> within the <cytoplasm>. <Bacteria> and <archaea> have this cellular organization. Both contain <ribosomes> that perform <biological translation> and a selective <cell membrane>; neither is a bag of unorganized chemicals. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/4-2-prokaryotic-cells[OpenStax Biology 2e, 4.2: prokaryotic organization].

The word <prokaryotic> describes organization, not a single exclusive <clade>. Grouping <bacteria> and <archaea> by the absence of a <cell nucleus> is useful for comparing <biological cells>, but does not establish that they share a more recent ancestor with one another than either does with <eukaryotes>. See <three-domain system> and <two-domain system> for the different ancestry models.

<Prokaryotic> does not mean membrane-free. <Cyanobacteria> contain internal <thylakoid> membranes for <oxygenic photosynthesis>, despite lacking <chloroplasts> and a <cell nucleus>. Many <prokaryotes> have circular <chromosomes>, but circular shape and a single <chromosome> are not defining requirements. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/22-2-structure-of-prokaryotes-bacteria-and-archaea[OpenStax Biology 2e, 22.2: bacterial and archaeal structures].

= Bacterium
{synonym}

<Bacteria> are a major evolutionary lineage of <organisms> with <prokaryotic> cellular organization. <Escherichia coli> and <cyanobacteria> illustrate its variety: the same broad cellular plan supports different kinds of <metabolism>. Most <bacteria> have a <cell wall> containing <peptidoglycan>; <archaea> do not have <peptidoglycan> walls. A <cell wall> and a <cell membrane> are distinct structures, and some <bacteria> lack a <cell wall>. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/22-2-structure-of-prokaryotes-bacteria-and-archaea[OpenStax Biology 2e, 22.2: bacterial envelopes].

The usefulness of recognizing <bacteria> extends beyond naming: it identifies a shared evolutionary background for comparing <genes>, <proteins> and cellular mechanisms. A familiar shape or the absence of a <cell nucleus> alone cannot distinguish <bacteria> from <archaea>; molecular comparisons, including <ribosomal RNA>, supply additional evidence.

= Eukaryotic
{synonym}

A <eukaryote> is an <organism> belonging to the lineage characterized by a membrane-bounded <cell nucleus> and extensive cellular compartmentalization. A <nuclear envelope> surrounds the nuclear <genome>; nuclear <chromosomes> are typically linear. <Ribosomes> perform <biological translation> outside the <cell nucleus>. See https://openstax.org/books/microbiology/pages/3-4-unique-characteristics-of-eukaryotic-cells[OpenStax Microbiology, 3.4: nuclear organization and protein synthesis]. Specialized <biological cells> can lose their <cell nucleus>, as mature mammalian <red blood cells> do, while remaining parts of <eukaryotic> <organisms>; see https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/40-2-components-of-the-blood[OpenStax Biology 2e, 40.2: mature red blood cells].

<Yeasts>, <plants> and <animals> are all <eukaryotes>. Typical cells in these groups have <mitochondria>, but <chloroplasts> belong to photosynthetic <plant> and <algae> lineages, not to every <eukaryote>. A <cell wall> occurs in typical <plant> and <fungus> cells but is absent from <animal> cells. These are comparisons of representative cells, not a checklist every specialized cell must satisfy. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/4-3-eukaryotic-cells[OpenStax Biology 2e, 4.3: nucleus, organelles and representative cells].

The interesting feature is controlled separation: <cell membranes> and <organelle> membranes let different cellular processes occupy distinct compartments while <membrane transport> connects them. The <evolution> of <eukaryotes> also involved partnership: <mitochondria> descend from a bacterial <endosymbiosis>. Modern <phylogenetics> supports an archaeal ancestry for the nuclear lineage, alongside this bacterial contribution; see <two-domain system>. Thus the history of a whole <eukaryote> cannot be read as the history of just one of its <genes>.

= Yeast
{parent=Fungus}
{wiki}

<Yeasts> are <fungi> whose growth is predominantly unicellular, commonly through <budding> or division into two cells. The word describes a growth form across different fungal lineages, rather than one <species> or an exclusive <clade>; some <fungi> can switch between unicellular and filamentous growth. Thus <Saccharomyces cerevisiae> is a <yeast>, but <yeast> is not another name for that one <species>. See https://booksite.elsevier.com/samplechapters/9780444521491/9780444521491.pdf[Kurtzman, Fell and Boekhout, The Yeasts, chapter 1 (2011): definition and classification].

A <yeast> cell has <eukaryotic> organization, including a <cell nucleus>, <mitochondria> and a <cell wall>. Small size and a unicellular lifestyle do not make it a member of <bacteria>. These compartments let an experimentally accessible <biological cell> retain much of the organization shared with other <eukaryotes>. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/24-1-characteristics-of-fungi[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 24.1: fungal cells and yeast growth].

**A motivating experiment: can <fermentation> continue without intact <yeast> cells?** In 1897, Eduard Buchner ground brewers' <yeast> with quartz sand and diatomite, added <water>, and pressed out a liquid extract. Adding concentrated cane <sugar> produced <carbon dioxide> after 15–60 minutes. A cell-retaining filter still yielded an active filtrate: with sterilized cane <sugar> in an icebox, <fermentation> began after about a day. Saturated lactose gave no observed <fermentation>. These are reported observations, not invented control results. See https://web.lemoyne.edu/~giunta/buchner.html[Buchner, Alcoholic fermentation without yeast cells (1897), translated excerpt hosted by Carmen Giunta].

The filtration comparison addresses the objection that surviving intact <yeast> cells caused the activity. The lactose comparison tests <substrate> dependence; it is not a no-<sugar> blank, nor a comparison at matched <sugar> <concentrations>. A proposed modern follow-up would also compare the same extract with and without added <sugar>, accounting for material already in the extract. That is an experimental suggestion, not a result attributed to Buchner. His report specifies brewers' <yeast>; it does not identify the preparation here as a pure <Saccharomyces cerevisiae> strain.

Heat treatment asks a different question: does destroying activity in the extract stop <fermentation>? Buchner's preliminary report described weak or absent activity after heating and filtration, rather than one clean universal threshold. Harden's 1914 account of the subsequent work summarized loss of activity on heating the juice to $50\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Harden also described requirements for both <enzymes> and <coenzymes>. Cell-free therefore means neither chemical-free nor a preparation containing one purified <enzyme>. See https://www.gutenberg.org/files/44985/44985-h/44985-h.htm[Arthur Harden, Alcoholic Fermentation (1914), chapters II and VI: cell-free activity and the fermenting complex].

The inference is that extracted cellular machinery can sustain these <chemical reactions> without an intact living <biological cell>. This does not show that the extract reproduces, grows or maintains itself like a <yeast>. Moreover, gas evolution alone does not identify <ethanol>: <cellular respiration> can also release <carbon dioxide>. Product identification is a separate measurement; Harden's account describes both <ethanol> and <carbon dioxide> as products of cell-free <alcoholic fermentation>. The beautiful separation is between the <organism> that produces and maintains the machinery and the machinery that catalyzes a particular transformation.

= Saccharomyces cerevisiae
{c}
{parent=Yeast}
{wiki}

= S. cerevisiae
{c}
{synonym}

<Saccharomyces cerevisiae> is a particular <species> of <yeast>, used in baking, brewing and laboratory research. During ordinary vegetative growth it reproduces by <budding>: a smaller daughter grows from the mother before separation. Laboratory strains can be maintained as either <haploid> or <diploid> <biological cells>. This makes it possible to investigate <genes> in a single <eukaryote>, then use controlled crosses and <meiosis> to connect a <genotype> to a <phenotype>. See https://smburgess.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/33/2019/08/Burgess-Powers-Mell-eLS.pdf[Burgess, Powers and Mell, Budding Yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae as a Model Genetic Organism (2017), pp. 1–2].

The practical value of <Saccharomyces cerevisiae> as a <model organism> is the combination of easy laboratory cultivation, accessible <genetics>, and cellular functions shared with other <eukaryotes>. A <haploid> strain has one set of <chromosomes>, so a changed <allele> is not accompanied by a second copy at that <genetic locus>. But a <mutation> need not produce an observable <phenotype> under every condition. The <yeast> is also a whole <organism>, not a miniature human: transferring a result requires evidence that the relevant function is shared in the target system. See https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4012490/[Duina, Miller and Keeney, Budding Yeast for Budding Geneticists (2014): cultivation, genetic tools and conserved functions].

For a concrete example, Hartwell, Culotti and Reid's 1970 work followed temperature-sensitive <Saccharomyces cerevisiae> strains with successive microscope photographs. Failure to complete a particular part of the <cell cycle> helped identify required <gene> functions. Their distinction between when a function is required and where a cell finally stops is especially instructive: the visible endpoint need not be the time of the underlying defect. See https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5271168/[Hartwell, Culotti and Reid, Genetic control of the cell-division cycle in yeast. I. Detection of mutants (1970): primary abstract]. This is a useful way to turn an observable change in a <biological cell> into a question about <genes> and causal order.

In <alcoholic fermentation>, <Saccharomyces cerevisiae> converts <sugars> into <ethanol> and <carbon dioxide>. The <carbon dioxide> helps dough rise; the <ethanol> is useful in alcoholic drinks. The <metabolism> also supports production of <ATP>, a usable cellular <energy> carrier. This is one metabolic ability of one <species>, not the definition of every <yeast>. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/7-5-metabolism-without-oxygen[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 7.5: alcohol fermentation] and the Burgess, Powers and Mell account above.

For <glucose>, the ideal net <stoichiometry> is
$$
\mathrm{C}_6\mathrm{H}_{12}\mathrm{O}_6\longrightarrow 2\mathrm{C}_2\mathrm{H}_5\mathrm{OH}+2\mathrm{CO}_2.
$$
One <molecule> of <glucose> supplies six <carbon> <chemical atoms>: four end up in the two <ethanol> <molecules> and two in the two <carbon dioxide> <molecules>. The twelve <hydrogen> and six <oxygen> <chemical atoms> also balance. This is a net material balance across several <enzyme>-catalyzed steps, not a single <chemical reaction> step or the complete bookkeeping for cellular <energy> carriers. The standard pathway accounting yields a net two <ATP> per <glucose>: the initial breakdown produces the <ATP>, while the later conversion to <ethanol> regenerates a <coenzyme> needed to keep the breakdown going. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/7-2-glycolysis[Clark, Douglas and Choi, Biology 2e, 7.2: net ATP accounting] and the section 7.5 cited above.

= Germination
{parent=Seed}
{wiki}

<Seed> <germination> is renewed <plant embryo> activity and growth. = Seed viability
{parent=Germination}

The capacity of a living <seed> to produce a developing <plant> under suitable conditions. A <seed> with <seed dormancy> can retain <seed viability>. In an ideal counting exercise, if $g$ of $N$ <seeds> complete <germination>, at least $g$ were living at the start. The other $N-g$ are unresolved: a failed <germination assay> alone cannot separate dead <seeds>, <seed dormancy> and unsuitable conditions.

= Lettuce red and far-red germination experiment
{parent=Germination}

The 1952 experiment used Grand Rapids <lettuce> <seeds> on wetted blotters in covered dishes. After 16 hours in darkness, seven lots of 200 received successive alternating exposures: one minute of red <light> at $580$–$680\,\mathrm{nm}$, then four minutes above $700\,\mathrm{nm}$, with one lot removed after each exposure. Treated lots and a dark <scientific control> remained at $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ for two days; germinated <seeds> were counted. Separate wavelength tests used a two-glass-prism <spectrograph>.<A Reversible Photoreaction Controlling Seed Germination by Harry Alfred Borthwick and others>[A Reversible Photoreaction Controlling Seed Germination, pp. 662–664: preparation, spectrograph and alternating-exposure protocol]{ref}

Table 1 shows alternating promotion and suppression according to the final exposure. Red <light> could restore promotion after far-red <light>; suppression was incomplete. This reversibility supports a reversible sensing state rather than simple cumulative illumination. The paper did not isolate or identify the pigment; the response concerns this <lettuce> preparation and timing. Its readout was a two-day <germination> count, not subsequent <seedling> performance.<A Reversible Photoreaction Controlling Seed Germination by Harry Alfred Borthwick and others>[A Reversible Photoreaction Controlling Seed Germination, p. 663 pigment-identification limitation and p. 665 Table 1]{ref}

The modern <phytochrome> description distinguishes forms $P_r$ and $P_{fr}$ that interconvert under red and far-red <light>. This provides a concrete example of <signal transduction>: <light> changes a sensing state that affects development. The response should be distinguished from <photosynthesis>, which supplies chemical resources.<Biology 2e by Mary Ann Clark and others>[Biology 2e, §30.6 The Phytochrome System and the Red/Far-Red Response, Figure 30.38: interconversion and cellular response]{ref}

= Germination assay
{parent=Germination}
{title2=$\widehat G(t)=g(t)/N$}

A specified test for <germination>, recording the endpoint, conditions and observation window. For a proposed red/far-red <light> test, use <randomization> to allocate one <seed> lot to darkness, red, red then far-red, and red then far-red then red. Match hydration, <temperature>, handling and elapsed time before counting; use sham waiting periods to equalize schedules. Use dish <replication>, retain the dark <scientific control>, and record <radicle> emergence using an observation procedure that does not itself provide an uncontrolled <light> treatment. This is a proposed design, not a claim about the 1952 paper's <randomization> or <replication>.

As an original hypothetical counting example, begin with $N=40$ <seeds> in one dish. Suppose 12 first meet the <radicle> endpoint by 24 hours, eight additional <seeds> by 48 hours, and four additional <seeds> by 72 hours. These invented event counts give
$$
g(24)=12,\qquad g(48)=12+8=20,\qquad g(72)=12+8+4=24,
$$
and cumulative <sample proportions> $\widehat G(24)=0.30$, $\widehat G(48)=0.50$ and $\widehat G(72)=0.60$. The 48-hour increment is $8/40=0.20$, not $0.50$; the latter includes earlier events. Counting the same emerged <radicle> again at each inspection would inflate the total. These numbers illustrate bookkeeping, not biological rates or treatment effects.

With a fixed denominator and irreversible classification of the first endpoint event, the <set> counted at an earlier inspection is a <subset> of the <set> counted later. Hence $0\leq\widehat G(t)\leq1$ and $\widehat G(t)$ cannot decrease. This short proof applies to cumulative <germination> counts even if a later <seedling> dies. A count of currently surviving <seedlings> has a different definition and can decrease.

For treatment comparisons, the dish may be the <experimental unit>: <seeds> in the same dish share conditions. Forty <seeds> in one dish do not supply forty independent dish replicates. Report the <sample size>, <replication>, endpoint time and denominator; a <sample proportion> by a deadline measures this assay's outcome, not an unconditional fraction with <seed viability>.
