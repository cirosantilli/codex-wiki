== 1
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

A <state function> is determined by the equilibrium state, rather than the path used to reach it. Its differential is exact and its change around a closed <thermodynamic cycle> is zero. <Internal energy>, <entropy> and <enthalpy> are examples; transferred <heat> and <work> are not <state functions>.

For slow expansion in contact with a large <thermal reservoir>, the fixed <temperature> of that reservoir maintains an <isothermal process>. For a fixed amount of <ideal gas>, <internal energy> and <enthalpy> depend only on <temperature>, so
$$
\boxed{T=\mathrm{constant},\qquad U=\mathrm{constant},\qquad H=\mathrm{constant}.}
$$
The <ideal gas law> also gives $pV=\mathrm{constant}$. For a reversible expansion the absorbed <heat> equals the <work> done by the gas, while its <entropy> increases by $n_{\rm mol}R\ln(V_2/V_1)$; constant <internal energy> does not mean zero <heat transfer>.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

With a thermally insulating boundary, $\delta Q=0$. If the slow piston motion is frictionless and reversible, it is an <isentropic process>:
$$
\boxed{S=\mathrm{constant}.}
$$
The <first law of thermodynamics> gives $dU=-p\,dV$, so expansion lowers the <internal energy> and <temperature>. For constant <heat capacity ratio> $\gamma$, $pV^\gamma$ and $TV^{\gamma-1}$ are constant combinations of <state functions>. Insulation alone does not guarantee constant <entropy>: an irreversible <adiabatic process> can produce <entropy>. The conclusion uses the intended reversible, quasistatic piston idealization.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

For steady insulated flow through a porous plug, neglect changes of bulk <kinetic energy> and gravitational <potential energy>, and allow no shaft <work>. Each amount of gas brings flow <work> $p_1V_1$ upstream and delivers $p_2V_2$ downstream. The steady <energy conservation> balance is
$$
U_1+p_1V_1=U_2+p_2V_2,\qquad \boxed{H_1=H_2.}
$$
Thus <enthalpy> is conserved in this <Joule-Thomson effect>. For an <ideal gas>, $H=H(T)$, so $T_1=T_2$ and $U_1=U_2$ as well. The <entropy> increases:
$$
\boxed{\Delta S=n_{\rm mol}R\ln\frac{p_1}{p_2}>0.}
$$
The porous-plug process is irreversible despite being slow and insulated; it is not the reversible expansion of the preceding part.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

In the one-mole <Van der Waals equation>, $b>0$ represents <excluded volume>: the volume available to molecular motion is $V-b$, rather than $V$. Subtracting $b$ in the denominator therefore increases the repulsive contribution to <pressure>. The $a>0$ term represents intermolecular attraction, which reduces the outward <momentum flux> at a wall and hence appears as the negative correction $-a/V^2$. The two minus signs do not mean that both physical effects lower <pressure>.

For a reversible <isothermal process> along a homogeneous gas branch with $V_2>V_1>b$, the <work> done by the gas is
$$
W_{\rm by}=\int_{V_1}^{V_2}\left(\frac{RT}{V-b}-\frac a{V^2}\right)dV
=\boxed{RT\ln\frac{V_2-b}{V_1-b}+a\left(\frac1{V_2}-\frac1{V_1}\right).}
$$
To find the <internal energy> change without guessing a caloric law, combine $dU=T\,dS-p\,dV$ with the <Helmholtz free energy> <Maxwell relation> $(\partial S/\partial V)_T=(\partial p/\partial T)_V$. This gives
$$
\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T=T\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}\right)_V-p=\frac a{V^2}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Delta U=a\left(\frac1{V_1}-\frac1{V_2}\right)>0.}
$$
The attractive contribution $-a/V$ becomes less negative as the gas expands: separating attracting particles requires <energy>. This differs from the <ideal gas> result $\Delta U=0$ at fixed <temperature>. The required <heat> is $Q=\Delta U+W_{\rm by}=RT\ln[(V_2-b)/(V_1-b)]$. Below the critical <temperature>, an equilibrium path crossing a liquid–gas coexistence region needs the equilibrium coexistence construction rather than the unstable homogeneous <Van der Waals isotherm>.

== 3
{scope}

=== Solution

The <Clapeyron equation> for molar <latent heat> $L$ is $dp/dT=L/[T(V_{\rm vap}-V_{\rm liq})]$. Neglect the liquid <molar volume>, treat the vapor as an <ideal gas>, and approximate $L$ as constant over the interval. Then
$$
\frac{d\ln p}{dT}\simeq\frac{L}{RT^2},\qquad
\ln\frac{p_h}{p_l}=\frac LR\left(\frac1{T_l}-\frac1{T_h}\right).
$$
Using one atmosphere as $101325\,\mathrm{Pa}$ gives
$$
\boxed{L\simeq\frac{R\ln(101325/133)}{1/1.2-1/4.2}=92.7\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}.}
$$
This is the effective molar <enthalpy of vaporization> inferred from the <integrated Clausius-Clapeyron relation>. If $L$ varies with <temperature>, the inferred value is weighted by $1/T^2$, not an unweighted arithmetic mean. Real helium is not exactly an <ideal gas> over the whole interval, and its liquid changes superfluid state within it, so this is an estimate under the stated approximations rather than a precise temperature-independent material constant.

== 4
{scope}

=== Solution

A <magnetic field> favors alignment of the <magnetic moments> of a <paramagnet>, reducing the number of substantially occupied magnetic configurations at fixed <temperature>. Its <magnetic entropy> therefore decreases as the field magnitude increases, explaining the negative sign of $(\partial S/\partial B)_T=-\alpha B/T^2$.

Choose the magnetic <Helmholtz free energy> convention $dF=-S\,dT-m\,dB$. Equality of its <mixed partial derivatives> gives the magnetic <Maxwell relation>
$$
\left(\frac{\partial m}{\partial T}\right)_B=\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial B}\right)_T=-\frac{\alpha B}{T^2}.
$$
The <magnetic moment inferred from an entropy derivative>, integrated at fixed $B$, is
$$
\boxed{m(T,B)=\frac{\alpha B}{T}+m_0(B).}
$$
The given <entropy> derivative alone cannot determine the temperature-independent function $m_0(B)$. With the usual independent-<paramagnet> condition $m\to0$ as $T\to\infty$ at fixed $B$, $m_0(B)=0$, giving <Curie's law>, $m=\alpha B/T$. The sketch uses that additional condition. The inverse-$T$ expression applies in the weak-field regime; its divergence cannot describe the actual low-<temperature> saturation of a finite set of <magnetic moments>.

\Image[2016-paramagnetic-response.png]
{title=Inverse-temperature moment at fixed magnetic field}
{description=Original <Curie's law> sketch under the condition of zero moment at infinite <temperature>. The low-temperature saturation regime is outside this approximation.}

For reversible quasistatic thermal isolation, $dS=0$. With positive field-dependent <heat capacity> $C_B=T(\partial S/\partial T)_B$,
$$
0=\frac{C_B}{T}dT-\frac{\alpha B}{T^2}dB,\qquad
\boxed{dT=\frac{\alpha B}{C_BT}\,dB.}
$$
Reducing a positive $B$ therefore lowers $T$: this is <adiabatic demagnetization>. Equivalently, reducing $B^2$ cools for either field sign. Reduced alignment would raise <magnetic entropy> at fixed $T$; cooling compensates so total <entropy> remains constant. Insulation without reversibility would allow additional <entropy production> and need not follow this adiabat exactly.

== 5
{scope}

=== Solution

Assume a stationary <temperature> profile, spatially uniform <mass density> and radioactive <heat generation rate>, constant <thermal conductivity>, isotropic solid <thermal conduction>, and blackbody emission into effectively cold surroundings. The volumetric source is
$$
q_v=\rho h=4.375\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}.
$$
Total radiogenic <power> equals the surface <blackbody radiation> loss. The <Stefan–Boltzmann law> gives
$$
\frac{4\pi R^3}{3}q_v=4\pi R^2\sigma_{\rm SB}T_S^4,\qquad
\boxed{T_S=\left(\frac{q_vR}{3\sigma_{\rm SB}}\right)^{1/4}=22.52\,\mathrm K.}
$$
For <steady radiogenic heating of a conducting sphere>, the interior <heat equation> is
$$
\frac1{r^2}\frac d{dr}\left(r^2\kappa\frac{dT}{dr}\right)+q_v=0.
$$
Regularity at the centre removes the $1/r^2$ integration term and yields $dT/dr=-q_vr/(3\kappa)$. Matching $T(R)=T_S$ gives
$$
T(r)=T_S+\frac{q_v}{6\kappa}(R^2-r^2),\qquad
\boxed{T_C=T_S+\frac{q_vR^2}{6\kappa}=371.4\,\mathrm K.}
$$
The high central <temperature> is compatible with a cold surface because finite <thermal conductivity> requires a large <temperature gradient> to carry the accumulated interior <heat>. A nonzero background radiation temperature $T_{\rm bg}$ replaces $T_S^4$ by $T_S^4-T_{\rm bg}^4$ in the surface balance. The calculation neglects convection, stored transient <heat>, changing radioactive abundances, phase changes and any substantial external irradiation.

{scope}

=== Solution

Normalize the <probability density function> on $x\ge0$:
$$
1=N\int_0^\infty e^{-\alpha x}dx=\frac N\alpha,
\qquad
\boxed{N=\alpha,\qquad \langle x\rangle=\alpha\int_0^\infty xe^{-\alpha x}dx=\frac1\alpha.}
$$
For $y=\sqrt x\ge0$, the <change of variables> is $x=y^2$, $dx=2y\,dy$. Conservation of <probability> gives the transformed <probability density function>
$$
\boxed{g(y)=2\alpha y e^{-\alpha y^2},\quad y\ge0,\qquad g(y)=0,\quad y<0.}
$$
It is a <Rayleigh distribution> with scale $\sigma=1/\sqrt{2\alpha}$. Its derivative is $g'(y)=2\alpha e^{-\alpha y^2}(1-2\alpha y^2)$, so it rises from zero, has one maximum, and then decays:
$$
\boxed{y_{\rm mode}=\frac1{\sqrt{2\alpha}}.}
$$
The <expected value> follows by putting $z=\sqrt\alpha\,y$:
$$
\langle y\rangle=2\alpha\int_0^\infty y^2e^{-\alpha y^2}dy
=\frac2{\sqrt\alpha}\int_0^\infty z^2e^{-z^2}dz
=\boxed{\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2\sqrt\alpha}.}
$$
Here $\int_0^\infty z^2e^{-z^2}dz=\sqrt\pi/4$, obtained from the <Gaussian integral> by integration by parts. In particular $\langle\sqrt x\rangle\ne\sqrt{\langle x\rangle}$, and the <mode> changes under a nonlinear variable transformation because a density includes its <Jacobian determinant>.

{scope}

=== Solution

Let the gas have a small bulk <velocity> $u_x(y)$, an approximately isotropic local thermal velocity distribution, and a <mean free path> $\lambda$ short compared with the flow-gradient scale. A molecule transports the mean streamwise <momentum> acquired near its previous collision. To first order, its nonequilibrium momentum relative to the destination layer is approximately $-m\tau_c v_y\,du_x/dy$. The resulting transverse <momentum flux> is
$$
\Pi_{xy}\simeq-nm\tau_c\langle v_y^2\rangle\frac{du_x}{dy}.
$$
Thus the <dynamic viscosity> is $\eta\simeq\rho\tau_c\langle v_y^2\rangle$. With isotropy, a representative collision time $\tau_c\sim\lambda/\langle v\rangle$, and a chosen thermal speed average, this has the form
$$
\boxed{\eta=K\rho\langle v\rangle\lambda.}
$$
The dimensionless $K$ depends on how speeds and collision times are averaged; a simple one-speed estimate gives $K=1/3$. Assumptions include dilute binary collisions, a locally equilibrated distribution, weak shear, and negligible boundary slip. The <kinetic theory> identity $p=\rho\langle v^2\rangle/3$ then gives
$$
\frac p\eta=\frac{\langle v^2\rangle}{3K\langle v\rangle^2}\frac{\langle v\rangle}{\lambda}.
$$
For the <Maxwell speed distribution>, $\langle v^2\rangle/\langle v\rangle^2=3\pi/8$, so
$$
\boxed{\frac p\eta=\frac\pi{8K}\,\nu_{\rm coll},\qquad \nu_{\rm coll}\simeq\frac{\langle v\rangle}{\lambda}.}
$$
This explains both the units of inverse time and the relation to molecular collision frequency, up to the averaging convention.

In the cylinder apparatus, distinguish the vector mean molecular <velocity>, $\langle\mathbf v\rangle=\mathbf u$, from the mean thermal speed $\langle|\mathbf v-\mathbf u|\rangle$. For a slow nearly isothermal <Couette flow>, the thermal speed is approximately position-independent, whereas the bulk mean is azimuthal and changes from the local rotating-wall speed to zero at the fixed wall. In the side gap, with $y$ measured radially from the inner wall,
$$
u_\varphi(y)\simeq\omega R\left(1-\frac yh\right),\qquad 0\le y\le h.
$$
Across either end gap, at cylindrical radius $s$, the corresponding profile is $u_\varphi(s,z)\simeq\omega s(1-z/h)$, with $z$ measured away from the moving end face. The <no-slip boundary condition> and small $h/R,h/H$ underlie these linear profiles.

The side-wall <shear stress> has magnitude $\eta\omega R/h$, producing resisting <torque> $2\pi\eta\omega R^3H/h$. **The original diagram also has a gap at both circular end faces.** At either face, the stress is $\eta\omega s/h$ and its ring contribution is
$$
d\tau_{\rm end}=s\left(\frac{\eta\omega s}{h}\right)2\pi s\,ds,
\qquad \tau_{\rm end}=\frac{\pi\eta\omega R^4}{2h}.
$$
Adding the side and both ends gives the <end-face torque in narrow-gap cylindrical viscometry>:
$$
\boxed{\tau=C\eta\omega,\qquad C=\frac\pi h\left(2R^3H+R^4\right).}
$$
The motor supplies the opposite of this viscous resisting <torque>. Corner flow and the small axle area are neglected. Retaining only the cylindrical wall would omit the $\pi R^4/h$ term shown by the actual closed-ended geometry.

For hard-sphere Ar atoms of collision diameter $d_{\rm Ar}$, $\lambda=1/(\sqrt2n\pi d_{\rm Ar}^2)$ and $\rho=nm_{\rm Ar}$. Therefore
$$
d_{\rm Ar}=\left[\frac{K m_{\rm Ar}\langle v\rangle}{\sqrt2\pi\eta}\right]^{1/2},\qquad
\langle v\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{8k_BT}{\pi m_{\rm Ar}}}.
$$
Using $m_{\rm Ar}=40m_u$, $T=273.15\,\mathrm K$ and the simple estimate $K=1/3$ gives
$$
\boxed{\langle v\rangle\simeq380\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},\qquad d_{\rm Ar}\simeq3.0\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm m.}
$$
This is a kinetic estimate with a model-dependent numerical prefactor, rather than a precise atomic size measurement. Since the density cancels and the hard-sphere collision diameter is taken constant, the prediction is $\eta\propto\sqrt T$. The collision cross-section of a real atom can vary with speed, so its exact temperature dependence need not follow this power law.

== 8
{scope}

=== Solution

For a reversible <adiabatic process> of an <ideal gas>, $\delta Q=0$ and $dU=-p\,dV$. At fixed composition and constant sample <heat capacity at constant volume> $C_V$,
$$
C_V\,dT=-\frac{n_{\rm mol}RT}{V}\,dV,
\qquad d\ln T=-\frac{n_{\rm mol}R}{C_V}d\ln V.
$$
<Mayer's relation> gives $n_{\rm mol}R=C_p-C_V$, so $TV^{\gamma-1}$ is constant and
$$
\boxed{pV^\gamma=\mathrm{constant},\qquad \gamma=\frac{C_p}{C_V}.}
$$
The finite-path power law requires approximately constant <heat capacities> as well as reversibility. When rotational or vibrational populations change appreciably, use $\int C_V(T)\,dT/T+n_{\rm mol}R\ln V=\mathrm{constant}$ instead; the local isentropic pressure exponent remains $\gamma(T)$.

For an intact heteronuclear <diatomic gas>, quantum excitation progressively activates molecular motion. With rotational and vibrational modes frozen, the three translational degrees of freedom give $C_{V,\rm m}=3R/2$ and $\gamma=5/3$. Two active rotations add $R$, giving $C_{V,\rm m}=5R/2$ and $\gamma=7/5$. One active harmonic vibration contributes a further $R$, through kinetic and potential terms, giving $C_{V,\rm m}=7R/2$ and $\gamma=9/7$.

For molecular <moment of inertia> $I=\mu_{\rm red}r_e^2$, the <rigid rotor> levels are $E_J=\hbar^2J(J+1)/(2I)$, so the rotational excitation scale is
$$
\boxed{\Theta_{\rm rot}=\frac{\hbar^2}{2Ik_B}.}
$$
The first gap corresponds to $2\Theta_{\rm rot}$, so the crossover occurs at temperatures of that order. For bond force constant $k_s$ and reduced molecular <mass> $\mu_{\rm red}$, the vibrational scale is
$$
\boxed{\Theta_{\rm vib}=\frac{\hbar\omega_{\rm vib}}{k_B}=\frac\hbar{k_B}\sqrt{\frac{k_s}{\mu_{\rm red}}}.}
$$
Typically $\Theta_{\rm vib}\gg\Theta_{\rm rot}$. Real gases may condense at the low-temperature end or dissociate and excite electronic states at the high-temperature end; the three plateau values describe the ideal intact-molecule model.

\Image[2016-diatomic-heat-capacity-ratio.png]
{title=Translational, rotational and vibrational regimes of the heat capacity ratio}
{description=Original rigid-rotor and harmonic-oscillator calculation with an illustrative ratio of vibrational to rotational temperatures of 100. The three limiting <heat capacity ratios> are marked; detailed crossover shapes are not universal.}

For the piston apparatus, mechanical equilibrium requires $p_0A=p_{\rm air}A+mg$, hence $p_0=p_{\rm air}+mg/A$. Let $x$ be a small upward displacement, so $V=V_0+Ax$. The local <isentropic compressibility> gives
$$
p-p_0\simeq-\frac{\gamma p_0A}{V_0}x,
\qquad m\ddot x=A(p-p_0)=-\frac{\gamma p_0A^2}{V_0}x.
$$
Consequently the <adiabatic gas-spring piston period> is
$$
\boxed{\tau=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{B}{\gamma}},\qquad B=\frac{mV_0}{p_0A^2}.}
$$
This neglects friction, gas inertia, leakage and dissipation, with internal equilibration much faster than the oscillation and external thermal equilibration much slower. The small oscillation probes $\gamma$ locally; it need not keep $\gamma$ constant over a large temperature interval.

With $p_{\rm air}=101325\,\mathrm{Pa}$, the supplied dimensions give $p_0=101351.4\,\mathrm{Pa}$ and $B=2.20566\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{s^2}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\gamma_{\rm NO}=\frac{4\pi^2B}{(0.0251\,\mathrm s)^2}=1.382,\qquad
\gamma_{\rm He}=\frac{4\pi^2B}{(0.0229\,\mathrm s)^2}=1.660.}
$$
Helium is close to the monatomic <ideal gas> value $5/3$; NO is close to the <diatomic gas> value $7/5$, with rotations active and vibration largely frozen. The numbers are approximate experimental inferences, rather than exact rational values.

{scope}

=== Solution

For a single-particle state of energy $\epsilon$, put $z=e^{\mu/(k_BT)}$. The equilibrium mean occupancies are
$$
\boxed{\bar n_{\rm MB}=ze^{-\epsilon/(k_BT)},\qquad
\bar n_{\rm FD}=\frac1{z^{-1}e^{\epsilon/(k_BT)}+1},\qquad
\bar n_{\rm BE}=\frac1{z^{-1}e^{\epsilon/(k_BT)}-1}.}
$$
The <Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution> is the dilute classical limit. The <Fermi-Dirac distribution> applies to identical <fermions> with at most one particle in each complete single-particle state by the <Pauli exclusion principle>. The <Bose-Einstein distribution> applies to identical <bosons> with no such upper occupation bound. The <chemical potential> fixes total particle number; these are mean state occupancies, not three separately normalized single-particle probability densities. For a number-conserving ideal <Bose gas>, $\mu$ cannot exceed the lowest single-particle energy.

Take $\mathbf k$ to be a <wavevector> and count periodic-box states with cell volume $(2\pi)^3/V$ in <momentum space>, parameterized by $\mathbf k=\mathbf p/\hbar$. Write $g_s$ for internal degeneracy. The number of states in a spherical shell is $g_sV\,4\pi k^2dk/(2\pi)^3$. Since $\epsilon=\alpha k$,
$$
\boxed{g(\epsilon)=\frac{g_sV}{2\pi^2\alpha^3}\epsilon^2,\qquad \epsilon\ge0.}
$$
Thus the one-particle <canonical partition function> is
$$
Z_1=\int_0^\infty g(\epsilon)e^{-\beta\epsilon}d\epsilon
=\frac{g_sV}{2\pi^2\alpha^3}\frac2{\beta^3}
=\boxed{aVT^b,\qquad a=\frac{g_sk_B^3}{\pi^2\alpha^3},\quad b=3.}
$$
Here $\beta=1/(k_BT)$, $g_s=1$ for one nondegenerate branch, and $g_s=2$ for the spin-half case below. The integral uses $\int_0^\infty u^2e^{-u}du=2$. For classical indistinguishable particles, $Z_N=Z_1^N/N!$, giving
$$
\boxed{U=-\partial_\beta\ln Z_N=3Nk_BT,\qquad p=k_BT\left(\frac{\partial\ln Z_N}{\partial V}\right)_{T,N}=\frac{Nk_BT}{V}.}
$$
The factor three in the energy arises from the linear <dispersion relation> and three-dimensional state counting; the usual nonrelativistic quadratic-dispersion value $3Nk_BT/2$ does not apply.

For spin-half <fermions> at $T=0$, all states up to the <Fermi energy> are occupied. With $g_s=2$,
$$
N=\frac{V\epsilon_F^3}{3\pi^2\alpha^3},\qquad
\epsilon_F=\alpha(3\pi^2n)^{1/3},\qquad n=\frac NV.
$$
Integrating the occupied energy states gives
$$
\boxed{U_0=\frac34N\epsilon_F=\frac34\alpha(3\pi^2)^{1/3}N^{4/3}V^{-1/3},\qquad
p_0=-\left(\frac{\partial U_0}{\partial V}\right)_N=\frac14n\epsilon_F=\frac{U_0}{3V}.}
$$
These are the corresponding <ultrarelativistic ideal Fermi gas> formulae with general slope $\alpha$. The nonzero ground-state <internal energy> and <degeneracy pressure> reflect the <Pauli exclusion principle>: particles cannot all occupy the lowest-momentum state. Formally extrapolating the classical equations gives $U,p\to0$, but <Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics> is not valid at fixed positive $n$ as $T\to0$.

At large $T$, the number equation gives $z\simeq N/Z_1\ll1$, so expanding the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> recovers the <Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution>. A convenient crossover scale is the <Fermi temperature>,
$$
\boxed{T_0\sim T_F=\frac\alpha{k_B}(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}.}
$$
For $T\gg T_F$, both gases have $p\simeq nk_BT$. At fixed $n$, the Fermi curve begins at $p_0=n\epsilon_F/4$, has zero initial slope, lies above the classical line, and approaches it at high temperature. For example its low-temperature expansion is $p/(n\epsilon_F)=1/4+(\pi^2/6)(T/T_F)^2+\cdots$. The plot evaluates the finite-temperature occupation integrals at fixed number density.

{title=Pressure of classical and spin-half Fermi gases with linear dispersion}

If the source's symbol $\mathbf k$ is interpreted literally as <momentum> rather than a <wavevector>, the state-space cell is $h^3/V$. In that convention replace $\alpha$ in the wavevector-based coefficients and energy scales above by $\hbar\alpha$. The dimensionless thermodynamics and the relation $p=U/(3V)$ are unchanged.

== 10
{scope}

=== Solution

The <second law of thermodynamics> states that total <entropy> of an isolated composite system cannot decrease. For a cyclic <heat engine> exchanging heat only with reservoirs at $T_h>T_c>0$, it gives $-Q_h/T_h+Q_c/T_c\ge0$. Since $W=Q_h-Q_c$,
$$
\boxed{\eta=\frac W{Q_h}\le1-\frac{T_c}{T_h}=\eta_{\max}.}
$$
The bound is attained by a reversible <heat engine>: no friction, no finite-temperature-difference heat exchange and no other <entropy production>. A <Carnot cycle> provides an implementation with two reversible isotherms and two reversible adiabats. All <temperatures> in these ratios are absolute.

Let $n_{\rm mol}$ be the fixed gas amount, $r=V_2/V_1>1$, $\Delta T=T_h-T_c$, and let $C_V$ denote the heat capacity of the whole sample. First take the <unregenerated Stirling cycle>, so that heat rejected during isochoric cooling is not stored and returned during isochoric heating. The reversible quasistatic gas work is
$$
W_{12}=n_{\rm mol}RT_h\ln r,\quad W_{23}=0,\quad
W_{34}=-n_{\rm mol}RT_c\ln r,\quad W_{41}=0,
$$
while the heat inputs are $Q_{12}=n_{\rm mol}RT_h\ln r$ and $Q_{41}=C_V\Delta T$. Hence
$$
\boxed{W=n_{\rm mol}R\Delta T\ln r,\qquad
\eta=\frac{n_{\rm mol}R\Delta T\ln r}{n_{\rm mol}RT_h\ln r+C_V\Delta T}<1-\frac{T_c}{T_h}.}
$$
The strict inequality follows because $C_V\Delta T>0$ adds heat input without additional work. Quasistatic motion alone does not make heat exchange with a single hot or cold reservoir reversible while the gas temperature changes on an isochore. Indeed, using those two reservoirs gives total external <entropy production> $C_V\Delta T(1/T_c-1/T_h)>0$ per cycle.

The strict comparison requires the no-regeneration assumption. A perfect reversible regenerator stores $C_V\Delta T$ during $2\to3$ and returns it during $4\to1$; only the isothermal heat transfers then enter the external heat budget, giving $\eta=1-T_c/T_h$ exactly. For example, perfect regeneration with $T_h=600\,\mathrm K$, $T_c=300\,\mathrm K$ and $r=2$ gives $\eta=1/2=\eta_{\max}$. Thus the four gas paths by themselves do not enforce an efficiency strictly below the Carnot bound.

For the gas itself, the four <entropy> changes are
$$
\boxed{\Delta S_{12}=n_{\rm mol}R\ln r,\quad
\Delta S_{23}=C_V\ln\frac{T_c}{T_h},\quad
\Delta S_{34}=-n_{\rm mol}R\ln r,\quad
\Delta S_{41}=C_V\ln\frac{T_h}{T_c}.}
$$
Expansion increases the available positional states; compression reverses that change. Isochoric cooling reduces thermal populations and isochoric heating restores them. The sum is zero because <entropy> is a <state function>. This does not imply zero total <entropy production> in the gas plus external reservoirs.

Taking $S_1$ as reference, the isotherms are horizontal on a $T$–$S$ diagram. The isochore through state 1 obeys $S-S_1=C_V\ln(T/T_h)$, and the isochore through state 2 is shifted right by $n_{\rm mol}R\ln r$. For the internally reversible quasistatic gas paths, $\delta Q=T\,dS$, so
$$
\boxed{\oint T\,dS=Q_{\rm net}=W.}
$$
The enclosed area is the net work per engine cycle, with a clockwise orientation. It is not the total heat input.

For the refrigerator, the stated rates give the cooling <coefficient of performance> $\mathcal C=\dot Q_c/P=1.9/0.1=19$. But the absolute reservoir temperatures are $T_c=277.15\,\mathrm K$ and $T_h=293.15\,\mathrm K$, so <Carnot's theorem> requires
$$
\boxed{\mathcal C\le\frac{T_c}{T_h-T_c}=17.3219.}
$$
At $0.1\,\mathrm{kW}$ input the largest allowed cooling rate is only $1.7322\,\mathrm{kW}$. Equivalently the stated $2.0\,\mathrm{kW}$ heat rejection gives the reservoir entropy rate
$$
\frac{2.0\,\mathrm{kW}}{293.15\,\mathrm K}-\frac{1.9\,\mathrm{kW}}{277.15\,\mathrm K}
=-0.03305\,\mathrm{W\,K^{-1}}<0,
$$
a direct violation of the <second law of thermodynamics> for this cyclic two-reservoir device.

The unregenerated neon model makes the algebraic obstruction explicit. With $C_V/(n_{\rm mol}R)=3/2$, reversing the cycle gives
$$
\mathcal C=\frac{n_{\rm mol}RT_c\ln r-C_V\Delta T}{n_{\rm mol}R\Delta T\ln r}
=\frac{T_c}{\Delta T}-\frac{3}{2\ln r}.
$$
Setting this to 19 produces $\ln r=-0.89385$, or $r=0.4091$, contradicting the required expansion ratio $r>1$. Perfect regeneration can raise performance only to the Carnot bound and does not fix the problem. **There is no physically admissible $V_2/V_1$ for the stated data.**
