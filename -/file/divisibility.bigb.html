= Divisibility
{title2=$\mid$}
{wiki}

For <integers> $d,n$, **$d$ divides $n$** when <multiplication> by some <integer> $k$ takes $d$ to $n$:
$$
d\mid n\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{there exists }k\in\mathbb Z\text{ such that }n=dk.
$$
Then $d$ is a <divisor> of $n$, and $n$ is an <integer multiple> of $d$. The notation $d\nmid n$ means that no such <integer> $k$ exists. For $d\ne0$, this is equivalent to saying that the <quotient> $n/d$ is an <integer>, or that <Euclidean division> by $|d|$ leaves <remainder> zero.

For example, $3\mid12$ because $12=3\cdot4$, whereas $5\nmid12$ because $12=5\cdot2+2$. Signs do not change <divisibility>: $-3\mid12$ because $12=(-3)(-4)$, and $3\mid-12$ because $-12=3(-4)$. In general, changing the sign of either input preserves the relation.

The definition here permits zero as either input. Every <integer> divides zero, since $0=d\cdot0$. In particular, **$0\mid0$**; however, $0\nmid n$ whenever $n\ne0$, since $0k$ is always zero. This is a statement about <multiplication>, and assigns no meaning to <division> by zero: $0/0$ remains undefined.

Two useful properties come directly from the defining <multiplication>. <Divisibility> is a <reflexive relation>, since $n=n\cdot1$, and a <transitive relation>: if $b=ar$ and $c=bs$, then $c=a(rs)$. Moreover, a shared <divisor> divides every <integer> <linear combination>:
$$
d\mid a,\quad d\mid b\quad\Longrightarrow\quad d\mid(ax+by)\qquad(x,y\in\mathbb Z).
$$
Indeed, writing $a=du$ and $b=dv$ gives $ax+by=d(ux+vy)$. For example, $6\mid12$ and $6\mid18$ imply $6\mid(18-12)$. This small observation explains why the <Euclidean algorithm> preserves common <divisors>, and why <Bézout's identity> can certify a <greatest common divisor>.

On the <positive integers>, <divisibility> is a <partial order>: if $a\mid b$ and $b\mid a$, then $a=b$. The restriction matters, since $3\mid-3$ and $-3\mid3$ although $3\ne-3$. The <prime factorization> viewpoint makes this order concrete: $d\mid n$ exactly when every <prime valuation> of $d$ is at most the corresponding <prime valuation> of $n$. Thus one relation connects equal grouping, <prime factorization>, and <modular arithmetic>.

Book VII, definitions 3 and 5 of <Euclid's Elements> describe the corresponding positive-number relation through one number measuring another and through <integer multiples>. The sign and zero conventions above belong to the present <integer> formulation, rather than to those ancient definitions. https://geometor.github.io/euclid/heath/VII/index.html[Euclid, Elements VII, definitions 3 and 5, T. L. Heath translation]{ref}

= Common divisor
{parent=Divisibility}
{title2=$d\mid a,\ d\mid b$}

A positive <integer> $d$ is a common divisor of <integers> $a,b$ when $d\mid a$ and $d\mid b$. For positive $a,b$, their largest <common divisor> is their <greatest common divisor>. The identity $a=qb+r$ implies that $a,b$ and $b,r$ have the same positive <common divisors>, because $r=a-qb$ and $a=qb+r$.

= Decimal digit-sum divisibility test
{parent=Divisibility rule}
{title2=$a\equiv\sum_i a_i\pmod d\quad(d\mid9)$}

For a nonnegative <integer> with decimal expansion $a=\sum_i a_i10^i$, the congruence $10\equiv1\pmod d$ gives $a\equiv\sum_i a_i\pmod d$ whenever $d\mid9$. Thus <divisibility> by three or nine is equivalent to <divisibility> of the sum of its <decimal digits> by the same number. More generally a base-$b$ expansion has the analogous rule for every divisor of $b-1$. This follows by repeated addition and multiplication in <modular arithmetic>; it does not require an empirical rule about decimal notation.

= Semidivisible number
{parent=Divisibility}

= Semidivisible
{synonym}

A <positive integer> $n$ lying strictly between consecutive <prime> squares $p^2<n<q^2$ that is divisible by exactly one of $p,q$. Within a fixed band, the sum is the sum of multiples of $p$ plus the sum of multiples of $q$ minus twice the sum of multiples of $pq$, an <inclusion-exclusion> calculation using <arithmetic progressions>.

= Integer multiple
{parent=Divisibility}
{title2=$n=dk,\quad k\in\mathbb Z$}

= Multiple
{synonym}

An integer multiple of an <integer> $d$ is a <product> $dk$ with $k$ an <integer>; equivalently, $d$ is a <divisor> of that <product>. The <integer multiples> of $12$ are $\ldots,-24,-12,0,12,24,\ldots$. Zero is an <integer multiple> of every <integer>, and the only <integer multiple> of zero is zero. Shared positive <integer multiples> lead to the <least common multiple>.

= Divisor
{parent=Divisibility}
{title2=$d\mid n$}
{wiki}

= Integer divisor
{synonym}

A divisor of an <integer> $n$ is an <integer> $d$ satisfying the <divisibility> relation $d\mid n$. Equivalently, $n=dk$ for some <integer> $k$. The word **positive** matters when listing or counting <divisors>: unless signs are restricted, both $d$ and $-d$ qualify.

The positive <divisors> of $12$ are
$$
1,2,3,4,6,12.
$$
Their negatives are also <divisors> of $12$, giving twelve <integer divisors> altogether. The same list of positive <divisors> works for $-12$. One has only the positive <divisor> $1$, while zero has every <integer> as a <divisor>, including zero under our <divisibility> convention. A nonzero <integer> never has zero as a <divisor>.

For a <positive integer> $n$, the positive <divisors> come in pairs $(d,n/d)$ whose <product> is $n$. For $12$, the pairs are $(1,12)$, $(2,6)$ and $(3,4)$. Each pair contains a <divisor> at most $\sqrt n$, so searching through candidate <divisors> up to the <square root> suffices to find all pairs. A pair repeats its own member only when $n$ is a <perfect square>; for $36$, the middle pair is $(6,6)$. Consequently a <positive integer> has an odd number of positive <divisors> exactly when it is a <perfect square>.

The <prime factorization> gives another complete description. Since $12=2^2\cdot3$, its positive <divisors> are precisely $2^i3^j$ with $0\leq i\leq2$ and $0\leq j\leq1$. These independent choices give $(2+1)(1+1)=6$ positive <divisors>, the value of the <divisor function> $\tau(12)$. A <prime factor> is one particular kind of <divisor>; $4$, $6$ and $12$ show that <divisors> need not be <prime numbers>.

Thinking in pairs connects exact grouping with <integer factorization>: twelve objects can form three groups of four or four groups of three. Thinking in shared <divisors> gives the <greatest common divisor>; for $12$ and $18$, the positive common <divisors> are $1,2,3,6$, and the largest is $6$.

= Divisor lattice
{parent=Divisor}

The positive divisors of an integer ordered by divisibility form a <lattice>. The meet is the <greatest common divisor>, and the join is the <least common multiple>.

= Bi-unitary divisor
{parent=Divisor}

A divisor $d$ of $n$ is bi-unitary when the greatest common unitary divisor of $d$ and $n/d$ equals $1$. For $n=p^e$, all divisors are allowed except $p^{e/2}$ when $e$ is even.

= Unitary divisor
{parent=Divisor}

A <divisor> $d$ of a <positive integer> $n$ is unitary when $\gcd(d,n/d)=1$. Each <prime power> in the <prime factorization> of $n$ must therefore be included in $d$ completely or omitted completely. Consequently, the sum of the $k$-th powers of unitary divisors is $\prod_{p^e\parallel n}(1+p^{ek})$.

= Factor pair
{parent=Divisor}

A pair of <positive integers> $(a,b)$ with $ab=n$ for a specified <positive integer> $n$. Pairing each <divisor> $a$ with $n/a$ gives $\tau(n)/2$ unordered <factor pairs> for nonsquare $n$, and $(\tau(n)+1)/2$ for a <perfect square>, where $\tau(n)$ is the <divisor function>.

= Proper divisor
{parent=Divisor}
{title2=$d\mid n,\quad 1\le d<n$}

Here a <proper divisor> of a <positive integer> $n$ means a positive <integer divisor> other than $n$ itself. Thus $1$ has no <proper divisors>, and the <proper divisors> of $12$ are $1,2,3,4,6$.

= Squarefree divisor
{parent=Divisor}

A squarefree divisor of an <integer> $n$ is a <divisor> $d\mid n$ that is a <squarefree integer>. It is specified by choosing a subset of the distinct <prime factors> of $n$.

= Greatest common divisor
{title2=$\gcd$}
{parent=Divisibility}
{wiki}

= GCD
{c}
{synonym}

For <integers> $a,b$ not both zero, their greatest common divisor is the largest positive <integer> dividing both. It is unchanged by replacing either input by its negative. The convention $\gcd(0,0)=0$ is useful, but this exceptional value is not a largest positive common <divisor>. For positive inputs, the <Euclidean algorithm> computes the answer, and <Bézout's identity> expresses it as an integer <linear combination> of the inputs.

For $12=2^2\cdot3$ and $18=2\cdot3^2$, a common <divisor> can contain at most one copy of the <prime number> two and one of the <prime number> three. Taking the smaller <prime valuation> at each <prime number> therefore gives
$$
\gcd(12,18)=2^{\min(2,1)}3^{\min(1,2)}=2\cdot3=6.
$$
The check $6=18-12$ is also a <Bézout's identity>: every common <divisor> divides this difference, and six itself divides both inputs. More generally, every common <divisor> divides the <greatest common divisor>, a stronger statement than merely being no larger than it. The companion <least common multiple> takes the larger <prime valuations>, as the diagram there illustrates. For an application, divide the <numerator> and <denominator> of the <fraction> $12/18$ by six to obtain $2/3$; these remaining <integers> are <coprime>.

= Scaling identity for greatest common divisors
{parent=Greatest common divisor}
{title2=$\gcd(km,kn)=k\gcd(m,n)$}

For positive integers $m,n,k$, the <greatest common divisor> scales by the common multiplier $k$. If $d=\gcd(m,n)$, then $kd$ is a common divisor of $km,kn$. Meanwhile <Bézout's identity> expresses $kd$ as an integer linear combination of $km,kn$, so their <greatest common divisor> divides $kd$. These two comparisons prove the identity without prime factorization.

= Coprime integers
{parent=Greatest common divisor}
{wiki}

= Coprimality
{synonym}

= Coprime
{synonym}

Two <integers> $a,b$ are **coprime** when their <greatest common divisor> is one:
$$
a,b\text{ are coprime}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\gcd(a,b)=1.
$$
For <positive integers>, this says that the only positive common <divisor> is one. For signed <integers>, use $\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(|a|,|b|)$, with <absolute values>; changing either sign leaves <coprimality> unchanged. With the convention $\gcd(0,0)=0$, zero is <coprime> precisely to $1,-1$, because $\gcd(a,0)=|a|$. Both $1,-1$ are <coprime> to every <integer>. https://darijgrinberg.gitlab.io/floor.pdf[Darij Grinberg, MIT 18.781, Section 1.2: greatest common divisors and coprimality]{ref}

The word concerns shared <prime factors>, not whether either input is a <prime number>. For instance $8=2^3$ and $9=3^2$ are <coprime> <composite numbers>, even though neither is a <square-free integer>. Conversely, $6=2\cdot3$ and $10=2\cdot5$ are <square-free integers> but are not <coprime>. Consecutive <integers> are always <coprime>: any common <divisor> of $n,n+1$ divides their difference one. Thus $14,15$ provide another pair of <coprime> <composite numbers>.

For <positive integers>, let $P(n)$ be the <prime support of an integer>. The equivalent test is
$$
\gcd(a,b)=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad P(a)\cap P(b)=\varnothing.
$$
The <set intersection> of the two <prime supports of an integer> must be the <empty set>. Indeed, any positive common <divisor> greater than one has a <prime factor>, and any shared <prime factor> is a common <divisor> greater than one. Hence <coprimality> depends on which <prime factors> occur, rather than on their <prime valuations>; replacing either input by its <radical of an integer> preserves <coprimality>.

For a list of at least two <integers>, distinguish <pairwise coprimality>, requiring every pair to be <coprime>, from <joint coprimality>, requiring only the <greatest common divisor> of the entire list to be one. For example,
$$
\gcd(6,10,15)=1,\qquad
\gcd(6,10)=2,\quad\gcd(6,15)=3,\quad\gcd(10,15)=5.
$$
No <prime factor> belongs to all three <prime supports of an integer>, yet each pair shares one. By contrast, $4,9,25$ have <pairwise coprimality>. The distinction is presented by Poritz under the names pairwise and mutually relatively prime. https://math.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Combinatorics_and_Discrete_Mathematics/Yet_Another_Introductory_Number_Theory_Textbook_-_Cryptology_Emphasis_(Poritz)/01:_Well-Ordering_and_Division/1.05:_The_Greatest_Common_Divisor[Jonathan A. Poritz, Yet Another Introductory Number Theory Textbook, Section 1.5]{ref}

One use is simplifying <fractions>. For a <fraction> $a/b$ with <integer> entries and $b>0$, divide its <numerator> and <denominator> by $d=\gcd(a,b)$ to obtain a <reduced fraction> with <coprime> entries. For example, $42/30=7/5$ since $d=6$, and $-42/30=-7/5$ uses the same $d$. A further common <divisor> would make $d$ too small, so the reduction is complete. In particular, the <reduced fraction> for zero is $0/1$.

Another use is reversible <modular arithmetic>: for an <integer modulus> $m>1$, an <integer> $a$ has a <modular inverse> precisely when $a,m$ are <coprime>. The proof outline uses <Bézout's identity>: $ua+vm=1$ makes $u$ a <modular inverse>, and any inverse relation $ua-km=1$ forces every common <divisor> to divide one. For example, $3\cdot5-2\cdot7=1$ gives $5^{-1}\equiv3\pmod7$, whereas $6$ has no <modular inverse> modulo $10$. Thus <coprimality> is the exact condition allowing division by a residue in <modular arithmetic>. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-200-principles-of-discrete-applied-mathematics-spring-2024/mit18_200_s24_lec15.pdf[Ankur Moitra, MIT 18.200, Lecture 15, Lemmas 1–2 and modular inverses]{ref}

= Pairwise coprimality
{parent=Coprime integers}
{title2=$\gcd(a_i,a_j)=1\quad(i\ne j)$}

= Pairwise coprime integers
{synonym}

A list of <integers> $a_1,\ldots,a_r$, with $r\ge2$, has <pairwise coprimality> when every two entries in distinct positions are <coprime>. This implies <joint coprimality>. For <positive integers>, it means no <prime factor> occurs in two different entries. For example, $4,9,25$ satisfy this condition.

= Joint coprimality
{parent=Coprime integers}
{title2=$\gcd(a_1,\ldots,a_r)=1$}

= Jointly coprime integers
{synonym}

A list of <integers> $a_1,\ldots,a_r$, with $r\ge2$, has <joint coprimality> when its <greatest common divisor> is one; define that <greatest common divisor> by repeated application of the two-input operation. For <positive integers>, no <prime factor> may divide every entry. Thus $6,10,15$ have <joint coprimality> but fail <pairwise coprimality>. For two entries, the conditions coincide.

= Least common multiple
{title2=$\operatorname{lcm}$}
{parent=Divisibility}
{wiki}

= LCM
{c}
{synonym}

For <positive integers> $a,b$, their least common multiple is the **smallest positive <integer> that is an <integer multiple> of both**. Write it as $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)$. A positive common <integer multiple> always exists, since $ab$ is one, but it can be larger than necessary.

For example, the positive <integer multiples> of $12$ begin $12,24,36,48,60,72$, while those of $18$ begin $18,36,54,72$. Their first positive coincidence is $36$, so $\operatorname{lcm}(12,18)=36$. Both also divide zero, but the word positive excludes zero from this minimum.

Suppose two events happen every twelve and eighteen days, and both happen at day $t=0$. Their next meeting is at day $36$, after three twelve-day intervals and two eighteen-day intervals. Through day $72$, their common event times are $0,36,72$.

\Image[least-common-multiple-periods.png]
{title=Events every 12 and 18 days, starting together, meet again every 36 days}
{description=Each row marks the nonnegative <integer multiples> of its interval from zero through 72. The highlighted columns $0,36,72$ are shared; 36 is the first positive one and hence the <least common multiple>.}

The <prime factorization> explains why this coincidence occurs. For the <integers> $12$ and $18$,
$$
12=2^2\cdot3,\qquad18=2\cdot3^2,\qquad
\operatorname{lcm}(12,18)=2^{\max(2,1)}3^{\max(1,2)}=36.
$$
A common <integer multiple> must contain at least the <prime valuations> required by each input. Taking the larger <prime valuation> at each <prime number> meets both requirements with no extra factors. The <greatest common divisor> uses the smaller <prime valuations> instead. This is the same coordinate comparison developed under <prime factorization>, applied in opposite directions.

\Image[gcd-lcm-prime-exponents.png]
{title=For 12 and 18, smaller <prime valuations> give <greatest common divisor> 6 and larger ones give <least common multiple> 36}
{description=Bars show the exponents of the <prime numbers> two and three in $12$, $18$, $6$ and $36$. The table makes the minimum and maximum in each column explicit.}

For any <positive integers> $a,b$, the <prime valuations> give
$$
\gcd(a,b)\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=ab,\qquad
\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=\frac{ab}{\gcd(a,b)}.
$$
The proof outline is one observation at each <prime number>: $\min(r,s)+\max(r,s)=r+s$. Hence the two sides have identical <prime factorizations>. Numerically, $6\cdot36=12\cdot18=216$. This also gives a calculation using the <Euclidean algorithm> for the <greatest common divisor>, without first finding the <prime factors> of both inputs.

Every positive common <integer multiple> is an <integer multiple> of the <least common multiple>, because its <prime valuations> must be at least the larger input <prime valuations>. Including negatives and zero, the common <integer multiples> of $12$ and $18$ are exactly $36k$ for <integers> $k$. For more than two <positive integers>, the same definition and rule of taking the largest <prime valuations> apply; one may also combine inputs successively. For example, $\operatorname{lcm}(12,18,20)=\operatorname{lcm}(36,20)=180$.

For nonzero signed <integers>, take <absolute values>: $\operatorname{lcm}(-12,18)=36$. We extend the definition to zero by the convention $\operatorname{lcm}(a,0)=0$, since zero is then the only common <integer multiple>; this is an extension, not a positive minimum. In particular $\operatorname{lcm}(0,0)=0$ must be handled separately from the displayed <quotient>, which would involve <division> by zero.

The <least common multiple> is useful for combining <fractions> with the smallest positive shared <denominator>. For example,
$$
\frac1{12}+\frac1{18}=\frac3{36}+\frac2{36}=\frac5{36}.
$$
Its scheduling interpretation requires care about starting times. If the events start at <integer> days $r$ and $s$, a common time must satisfy the <integer congruences> $t\equiv r\pmod{12}$ and $t\equiv s\pmod{18}$. The <Chinese remainder theorem for noncoprime moduli> says a solution exists exactly when $6\mid(r-s)$. If it exists, solutions form one <congruence class> modulo $36$, but the first meeting need not be day $36$. With starts $r=0,s=6$, the first meeting is day $24$ and subsequent meetings are $60,96,\ldots$; with $r=0,s=1$, they never meet. The beauty of the <least common multiple> is the exact shared spacing, while the <integer congruences> determine whether and where the spacing begins.

Book VII, Proposition 34 of <Euclid's Elements> constructs the <least common multiple> of two numbers, and Proposition 36 extends the construction to three by first combining two of them. In modern notation this is $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b,c)=\operatorname{lcm}(\operatorname{lcm}(a,b),c)$. These are specific ancient treatments of the construction, rather than a claim about its first discovery. https://geometor.github.io/euclid/heath/VII/34/index.html[Euclid, Elements VII.34, T. L. Heath translation: two numbers]{ref} https://geometor.github.io/euclid/heath/VII/36/index.html[Euclid, Elements VII.36, T. L. Heath translation: three numbers]{ref}
