== 1
{scope}

=== Solution

In a contractible region, the <Maxwell equations> $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$ and the <Poincaré lemma> imply the existence of a <magnetic vector potential> $\mathbf A$ such that $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$. <Faraday's law of induction> then gives
$$
\nabla\times\left(\mathbf E+\partial_t\mathbf A\right)=0.
$$
The expression in parentheses is a <gradient> there, so a scalar <electric potential> $\phi$ can be chosen with
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A,\qquad \mathbf E=-\nabla\phi-\partial_t\mathbf A.}
$$
These statements are local, or global when the domain has the required topology and the <electromagnetic fields> are regular. A nonzero flux through a topologically nontrivial closed surface can obstruct a single globally defined <vector potential>, even though local expressions remain valid. The <electric potential> and <magnetic vector potential> are not unique: $\mathbf A\mapsto\mathbf A+\nabla\chi$ and $\phi\mapsto\phi-\partial_t\chi$ leave both <electromagnetic fields> unchanged.

For the <Lorenz gauge condition> $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=-c^{-2}\partial_t\phi$, <Gauss's law> becomes
$$
-\nabla^2\phi-\partial_t(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A)=\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0},\qquad
\left(\nabla^2-\frac1{c^2}\partial_t^2\right)\phi=-\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}.
$$
The <Ampère-Maxwell law> gives
$$
\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A)-\nabla^2\mathbf A
=\mu_0\mathbf J-\frac1{c^2}\nabla\partial_t\phi-\frac1{c^2}\partial_t^2\mathbf A.
$$
Cancel the two <gradient> terms using the <Lorenz gauge condition>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\left(\nabla^2-\frac1{c^2}\partial_t^2\right)\phi=-\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0},\qquad
\left(\nabla^2-\frac1{c^2}\partial_t^2\right)\mathbf A=-\mu_0\mathbf J.}
$$
Both potential equations have the same <d'Alembert operator>, with their respective <charge density> and <current density> sources. In a source-free region these are homogeneous <wave equations> with propagation speed $c$.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

Let $\ell=2\pi r-d$ be the mean path length in the core. Neglect leakage and fringing across the narrow air gap, and assume a cross-section small compared with $r$. Continuity of normal <magnetic flux density> then gives the same tangential <magnetic field> magnitude $B$ in the core and gap. <Ampère's circuital law> for the <magnetic field strength> $H$ is
$$
H_{\rm core}\ell+H_{\rm gap}d=nI.
$$
For a linear core, $H_{\rm core}=B/(\mu_0\mu_r)$ and $H_{\rm gap}=B/\mu_0$, giving
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B_{\rm core}\simeq\mathbf B_{\rm gap}\simeq\frac{\mu_0nI}{d+\ell/\mu_r}\,\widehat{\mathbf t}.}
$$
Here $\widehat{\mathbf t}$ follows the winding's <right-hand rule>. The gap has much larger $H$ than the core when $\mu_r\gg1$, although $B$ is approximately continuous. Equivalently, the core and gap <magnetic reluctances> add in series.

On a <magnetic hysteresis> loop, <remanence> is the nonzero $B_r$ remaining at $H=0$ after magnetizing the sample. The <normal magnetic coercivity> $H_{cB}$ is the magnitude of reverse $H$ at which $B=0$ on that branch. The intrinsic <magnetic coercivity> instead refers to zero <magnetization>; these two conventions should not be confused. A comparison of coercive $H$ with remanent $B$ requires a unit conversion or a specified constitutive scale, because their <SI> units differ. A soft <ferromagnet> is understood here to have a narrow loop and small coercive field.

\Image[2016-magnetic-loop-and-reversible-law.png]
{title=Magnetic hysteresis and the reversible tanh approximation}
{description=Original illustrative <magnetic hysteresis> loop with the remanent <magnetic field> and normal coercive <magnetic field strength> marked. The dashed single-valued curve is the reversible approximation used in the calculation, rather than a hysteresis branch.}

Use the stated single-valued <constitutive equation> for the calculation. Inverting it gives $H_{\rm core}=H_m\operatorname{artanh}(B/B_m)$, so
$$
nI=\ell H_m\operatorname{artanh}(B/B_m)+\frac d{\mu_0}B.
$$
Since $\operatorname{artanh}z=z+O(z^3)$, the <small-signal response of a gapped magnetic core> is
$$
\boxed{B\simeq\kappa I,\qquad \kappa=\frac{n}{\ell H_m/B_m+d/\mu_0}=\frac{\mu_0n}{d+\mu_0\ell H_m/B_m}.}
$$
The small-field core <magnetic permeability> is $B_m/H_m$. Strictly, $B=B_m\tanh(H/H_m)$ is a reversible approximation: at $H=0$ it has $B=0$, so it cannot itself model nonzero <remanence>. The displayed loop and this constitutive approximation serve different purposes. Keeping a genuinely remanent branch would generally add a history-dependent offset; it would not imply $B=\kappa I$ through the origin.

== 3
{scope}

=== Solution

Take $d$ as the centre-to-centre separation and $\epsilon=\epsilon_0\epsilon_r$. Use the ideal lossless two-conductor <transmission line> model, with nonmagnetic surroundings and negligible internal-wire <inductance>. Opposite <line charges> $+\lambda_q$ and $-\lambda_q$ approximate the cylindrical conductors when $d\gg a$. Integrating their <electric fields>, or adding their logarithmic <electric potentials>, gives the potential difference
$$
V\simeq\frac{\lambda_q}{\pi\epsilon}\ln\frac da,
\qquad C'=\frac{\lambda_q}{V}\simeq\frac{\pi\epsilon}{\ln(d/a)}.
$$
This is the <two-wire capacitance at large separation>. For opposite <electric currents> $+I$ and $-I$, the <magnetic fields> between the wires add. The linked <magnetic flux> per unit length is approximately
$$
\Phi'\simeq\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi}\int_a^{d-a}\left(\frac1x+\frac1{d-x}\right)dx
=\frac{\mu_0I}{\pi}\ln\frac{d-a}{a}
\simeq\frac{\mu_0I}{\pi}\ln\frac da.
$$
Thus the <external inductance of a thin two-wire line> is $L'\simeq\mu_0\ln(d/a)/\pi$. The lossless <telegrapher's equations> give a forward <wave> with $V/I=\sqrt{L'/C'}$, so its <characteristic impedance> is
$$
\boxed{Z_0\simeq\frac1\pi\sqrt{\frac{\mu_0}{\epsilon_0\epsilon_r}}\ln\frac da
\simeq\frac{120\,\Omega}{\sqrt{\epsilon_r}}\ln\frac da.}
$$
The product $L'C'=\mu_0\epsilon$ also gives the propagation speed $c/\sqrt{\epsilon_r}$, an independent consistency check. The exact ideal external-field geometry replaces $\ln(d/a)$ by $\operatorname{arcosh}(d/(2a))$. If the wire separation were specified between surfaces, its centre separation would be $d+2a$, with the same leading large-separation result. Finite <electrical conductivity> and internal-wire <magnetic energy> require a more detailed line model.

{scope}

=== Solution

<Snell's law> is $n_a\sin\theta_a=n_b\sin\theta_b$, with both <angles> measured from the interface <normal vector>. Let $\psi$ be the <meridional ray>'s angle to the <optical axis> inside the core and let $n_0$ be the incident medium's <refractive index>. At the entrance face,
$$
n_0\sin\theta=n_{\rm inner}\sin\psi.
$$
At the cylindrical wall the <angle of incidence> is $i=\pi/2-\psi$. For $n_{\rm inner}>n_{\rm outer}$, the <critical angle for total internal reflection> satisfies $\sin i_c=n_{\rm outer}/n_{\rm inner}$. At the limiting accepted ray,
$$
\sin\psi_{\max}=\cos i_c=\sqrt{1-\frac{n_{\rm outer}^2}{n_{\rm inner}^2}}.
$$
Consequently the <optical fiber acceptance angle> obeys
$$
\boxed{n_0\sin\theta_{\max}=\sqrt{n_{\rm inner}^2-n_{\rm outer}^2}.}
$$
The requested expression follows for incidence from air, $n_0\simeq1$, provided the right-hand side is at most one. Strict <total internal reflection> occurs inside this limiting cone. The derivation uses the standard <meridional ray> model; skew launches need additional geometry.

If $\sqrt{n_{\rm inner}^2-n_{\rm outer}^2}>n_0$, every forward entrance angle allowed by this ideal model meets the side-wall condition, and the limiting half-angle is $\pi/2$, rather than an undefined <inverse sine>. For example, $n_{\rm inner}=1.6$, $n_{\rm outer}=1.1$, $n_0=1$ gives $\sqrt{1.35}>1$. Thus the unsaturated printed formula is not valid for every possible pair of <refractive indices>.

== 5
{scope}

=== Solution

Use the <thin lens> sign convention in which a real object on the incident side has positive object distance, a real image on the outgoing side has positive image distance, and a converging <lens> has positive <focal length>. The first image distance is
$$
v_1=\frac{f_1d_1}{d_1-f_1}.
$$
For the <image distance for two separated thin lenses>, the second <lens> has object distance $u_2=L-v_1$, which can be negative when its intermediate object is virtual. Applying the <thin lens> equation again gives
$$
\boxed{d_2=\frac{f_2u_2}{u_2-f_2}
=\frac{f_2[(L-f_1)d_1-Lf_1]}{(L-f_1-f_2)d_1+f_1f_2-Lf_1}.}
$$
The image is $d_2$ beyond the second <lens> when $d_2>0$; $d_2<0$ denotes a virtual image on its incident side. A vanishing denominator with nonzero numerator denotes an image at infinity. The rational expression also supplies appropriate limits when the first image is at infinity; for example, $d_1=f_1$ gives $d_2=f_2$ for finite nonzero <focal lengths>.

As $L\to0$, the effective <focal length> is $F=f_1f_2/(f_1+f_2)$ and
$$
\boxed{d_2=\frac{Fd_1}{d_1-F}.}
$$
For a real incident object, $d_1>0$, and positive $F$, the real-image condition is exactly $d_1>F$, yielding the requested inequality. This includes two converging <optical lens>[lenses]. The positivity assumption matters: with $f_1=f_2=-1\,\mathrm m$ and $d_1=1\,\mathrm m$, one has $F=-0.5\,\mathrm m$ and $d_1>F$, but $d_2=-1/3\,\mathrm m$ is virtual. If $f_1+f_2=0$, the touching pair has zero net optical power and produces no real image of this real object in the ideal <thin lens> model.

== 6
{scope}

=== Solution

Put the plate behind the slit at transverse coordinate $+d/2$, and let positive screen coordinate $x$ point toward that slit. The extra <optical path length> is
$$
\Delta=(n_1-1)t=0.52(200\,\mu\mathrm m)=104\,\mu\mathrm m.
$$
In the <paraxial approximation>, the covered path minus the uncovered path has total <optical path difference> $\Delta-dx/L$. Its zero determines the shifted central <interference fringe>:
$$
\boxed{x_0=\frac{L(n_1-1)t}{d}=1.04\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm m=10.4\,\mathrm{mm},}
$$
**toward the slit covered by the plate**. Moving toward that slit shortens its geometric path and compensates the extra delay in the plate.

More generally, the <phase difference> is $(2\pi/\lambda)(\Delta-dx/L)$, so every <interference fringe> translates by the same $x_0$ in this approximation. The spacing $\lambda L/d$ is unchanged, and the shift corresponds to $\Delta/\lambda$ fringe periods. For an ideal uniform transparent plate that changes only the <wave phase>, the envelope and <interferometric visibility> are unchanged. Reflection losses or unequal transmission through a real plate could additionally reduce the <interferometric visibility>; those are separate amplitude effects. No numerical <wavelength> is needed for the physical displacement of the zero-order fringe.

== 7
{scope}

=== Solution

Write $\mu=\mu_0\mu_r$ and $\epsilon=\epsilon_0\epsilon_r$ for homogeneous scalar material constants, and use $\mathbf J=\sigma\mathbf E$. Taking the <curl> of <Faraday's law of induction> and substituting the <Ampère-Maxwell law> gives
$$
\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=-\mu\partial_t(\sigma\mathbf E+\epsilon\partial_t\mathbf E).
$$
The <vector calculus identity> for a double <curl> therefore yields
$$
\boxed{\nabla^2\mathbf E-\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)=\mu\epsilon\,\partial_t^2\mathbf E+\mu\sigma\,\partial_t\mathbf E.}
$$
The second term represents <electric current> response, rather than <displacement current>. A dispersive $\sigma(\omega)$ is used in the <frequency domain>; its general time-domain counterpart is a temporal response convolution.

For the convention $e^{i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)}$, the <plane wave> equation is
$$
\left[\mathbf k\mathbf k^T+\left(\mu\epsilon\omega^2+i\mu\sigma\omega-k^2\right)\mathsf I\right]\mathbf E_0=0.
$$
Decompose the <electric field> into components parallel and perpendicular to $\mathbf k$. With $S=\mu\epsilon\omega^2+i\mu\sigma\omega$, the longitudinal eigenvalue is $S$ and each transverse eigenvalue is $S-k^2$; thus the determinant is $S(S-k^2)^2$. For a transverse <electromagnetic wave>, $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf E_0=0$ and
$$
\boxed{k^2=\frac{\omega^2}{c^2}+i\mu_0\sigma\omega}
$$
when $\mu_r=1$ and the background $\epsilon_r\simeq1$. A real dissipative <electrical conductivity> generally makes $k$ complex, with the decaying branch chosen for a passive medium.

For a cold, collisionless, unmagnetized <plasma>, treat ions as stationary and write $e>0$ for the magnitude of the <elementary charge>. The electron equation of motion is $-i\omega m_e\mathbf v=-e\mathbf E$, so
$$
\mathbf v=-\frac{ie}{m_e\omega}\mathbf E,\qquad
\mathbf J=-N_e e\mathbf v=\sigma(\omega)\mathbf E,\qquad
\boxed{\sigma(\omega)=\frac{iN_e e^2}{m_e\omega}.}
$$
This reactive response includes no collision-induced <Joule heating>. Substitution into the transverse <dispersion relation> gives
$$
\boxed{k^2c^2=\omega^2-\omega_p^2,\qquad \omega_p=\sqrt{\frac{N_ee^2}{m_e\epsilon_0}},\qquad n=\frac{ck}{\omega}=\sqrt{1-\frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2}}.}
$$
The quantity $\omega_p$ is the electron <plasma frequency>. The <refractive index> is real above this cutoff; below it, the <wave> is evanescent. The electron response is represented here by $\sigma(\omega)$, so it must not also be counted a second time in the background <permittivity>.

For a nonzero longitudinal component, taking the <dot product> of the matrix equation with $\mathbf k$ instead gives $S(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf E_0)=0$. The same cold <plasma> response makes $S=(\omega^2-\omega_p^2)/c^2$, hence
$$
\boxed{\omega^2=\omega_p^2,\qquad \omega=\omega_p\quad\hbox{for positive frequency}.}
$$
At nonzero $k$, the remaining equation forces $\mathbf E_0$ to be parallel to $\mathbf k$, and <Faraday's law of induction> gives $\mathbf B_0=0$. These are electrostatic <Langmuir waves>, with no $k$ dependence of <angular frequency> in the zero-temperature model. Equilibrium neutrality does not forbid their perturbed <charge density>; <Gauss's law> requires such a density when $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf E_0\ne0$.

== 8
{scope}

=== Solution

Let $\rho_q=3q/(4\pi a^3)$ be the uniform <charge density> and take $\boldsymbol\omega=\omega\widehat{\mathbf z}$. Rigid rotation produces <current density> $\mathbf J=\rho_q\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r$. The <magnetic dipole moment> is
$$
\mathbf m_s=\frac12\int\mathbf r\times\mathbf J\,d^3r
=\frac{\rho_q}{2}\int\left[r^2\boldsymbol\omega-\mathbf r(\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\omega)\right]d^3r.
$$
<Spherical symmetry> gives $\int r_i r_j\,d^3r=\delta_{ij}\int r^2d^3r/3$, while $\int r^2d^3r=4\pi a^5/5$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbf m_s=\frac{qa^2}{5}\boldsymbol\omega.}
$$
It is parallel to the <angular velocity> for positive $q$ and antiparallel for negative $q$. The fully signed magnitude statement is $|\mathbf m_s|=|q|\,|\omega|a^2/5$; the printed magnitude assumes positive $q$ and positive angular speed.

<Axisymmetry> permits a purely azimuthal <magnetic vector potential>, $\mathbf A=f(r)\sin\theta\,\widehat{\boldsymbol\varphi}$, with $A_r=A_\theta=0$. For the source's <associated Legendre function> convention, $P_1^1(\cos\theta)=+\sin\theta$, since its definition omits the Condon–Shortley phase. The given differential operator reduces to
$$
\widehat F[f(r)\sin\theta]=\left(f''+\frac2r f'-\frac{2f}{r^2}\right)\sin\theta.
$$
Inside the sphere, $J_\varphi=\rho_q\omega r\sin\theta$. Since the radial operator maps $r^3$ to $10r$, the particular coefficient is $\alpha_0=-\mu_0\rho_q\omega/10$. Regularity at the origin and decay at infinity give
$$
f_{\rm in}=Ar-\frac{\mu_0\rho_q\omega}{10}r^3,\qquad
f_{\rm out}=\frac C{r^2}.
$$
There is no surface sheet <electric current>, so both normal and tangential <magnetic field> components are continuous at $r=a$. Using
$$
B_r=\frac{2f(r)}r\cos\theta,\qquad
B_\theta=-\frac{f(r)+rf'(r)}r\sin\theta,
$$
this is equivalent to continuity of $f$ and $f'$. The two equations give $A=\mu_0\rho_q\omega a^2/6$ and $C=\mu_0\rho_q\omega a^5/15$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbf A_{\rm in}=\frac{\mu_0\rho_q}{30}(5a^2-3r^2)(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r),\quad r<a,}
$$
$$
\boxed{\mathbf A_{\rm out}=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{\mathbf m_s\times\mathbf r}{r^3},\quad r>a.}
$$
In the homogeneous expansion, $\alpha_1=A$, $\beta_1=C$ in their respective interior and exterior regions; all higher angular orders vanish. Indeed, matching the regular $r^\ell$ and decaying $r^{-\ell-1}$ terms with zero source at $\ell>1$ forces both coefficients to zero. The outside <magnetic field> is exactly the <magnetic dipole field> of $\mathbf m_s$, not merely its leading far-field approximation. For a positive moment along $z$, $A_\varphi=\mu_0m_s\sin\theta/(4\pi r^2)$, which is the printed cross-product magnitude expression.

For the <flux coupling of a rotating charged sphere to a circular loop>, take a loop of radius $b>a$ in the equatorial plane and use <Stokes theorem> on the regular full <vector potential>, evaluated around the rim. Its linked <magnetic flux> is
$$
\Phi_b=\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell
=2\pi b\frac{\mu_0m_s}{4\pi b^2}=\frac{\mu_0m_s}{2b}.
$$
With $m_s=\pi a^2I_0$, the <mutual inductance> defined using this effective <electric current> is
$$
\boxed{M=\frac{\Phi_b}{I_0}=\frac{\mu_0\pi a^2}{2b}.}
$$
Integrating an ideal <magnetic dipole field> through the entire disk would incorrectly use its singular expression inside the actual sphere; the rim calculation avoids that error.

For the prescribed time dependence, the quasistatic <Faraday's law of induction> result and the purely resistive loop approximation give
$$
\boxed{I_b(t)=-\frac1R\frac{d\Phi_b}{dt}=-\frac{\mu_0qa^2\omega_0\nu}{10bR}\cos(\nu t).}
$$
Positive loop <electric current> is defined to produce a positive $z$ <magnetic dipole moment>, so the minus sign is <Lenz's law>. This assumes negligible retardation and $\nu L_b\ll R$, where $L_b$ is the loop's <self-inductance>. If that is not negligible, the governing equation is instead $L_b\dot I_b+RI_b=-\dot\Phi_b$; $R$ alone is insufficient for the exact dynamic response.

{scope}

=== Solution

Let $\mathcal R=|r|^2$ be the single-mirror power reflectivity and $\theta_g$ the propagation angle inside the gas. Define $\lambda$ as the <vacuum> <wavelength>. Successive transmitted <complex amplitudes> differ by a round-trip factor $\mathcal R e^{i\delta}$, where, for real dielectric reflection coefficients whose product has positive sign,
$$
\delta=\frac{4\pi nd\cos\theta_g}{\lambda}.
$$
The <cosine> arises by comparing successive emerging rays on the same outgoing <wavefront>; it is not the literal zigzag distance $2d/\cos\theta_g$. Equivalently, the round-trip phase is $2k_zd$. If the source's $\theta$ is the angle in external air, <Snell's law> gives $n\sin\theta_g=\sin\theta$ and
$$
\delta=\frac{4\pi d}{\lambda}\sqrt{n^2-\sin^2\theta}.
$$
If $\theta$ denotes the internal angle, simply set $\theta_g=\theta$.

For identical lossless mirrors, their power transmission is $1-\mathcal R$. In an energy-normalized amplitude convention this is $|t|^2=1-\mathcal R$; more general interface amplitudes require the corresponding <wave impedance> factors. The <geometric series> of transmitted amplitudes gives
$$
\frac{I_t}{I_0}=\frac{|t|^4}{|1-\mathcal R e^{i\delta}|^2}
=\frac{(1-\mathcal R)^2}{(1-\mathcal R)^2+4\mathcal R\sin^2(\delta/2)}.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{I_t=\frac{I_0}{1+Q\sin^2(\delta/2)},\qquad Q=\frac{4\mathcal R}{(1-\mathcal R)^2}=\frac{4|r|^2}{(1-|r|^2)^2}.}
$$
This is <Fabry–Pérot transmission from multiple reflections>.<Fabry-Perot Interferometer, University of Washington Physics 331>[Theory equations (1)–(2), including the coefficient of finesse]{ref}

At <normal incidence>, for nondispersive $n$ and zero net reflection phase modulo $2\pi$, the <resonance> condition is $\delta=2\pi m$. The resonance <frequencies> and <free spectral range> are
$$
\boxed{\nu_m=\frac{mc}{2nd},\qquad \Delta\nu_{\rm FSR}=\frac{c}{2nd}.}
$$
For finite $\mathcal R<1$ the resonances have finite width, and transmission away from them is nonzero. Thus “only discrete frequencies” is an ideal high-reflectivity description of the selected resonances, not an exact statement about the support of the transmitted spectrum. For example, $\mathcal R=0.9$ at $\delta=\pi$ still gives $I_t/I_0=(0.1/1.9)^2\simeq0.00277$.

At half the unit peak, $Q\sin^2(\delta/2)=1$. Measuring the phase displacement from a resonance yields
$$
\Delta\delta_{\rm FWHM}=4\arcsin\frac1{\sqrt Q},\qquad
\boxed{\Delta\nu_{\rm FWHM}=\frac{2\Delta\nu_{\rm FSR}}\pi\arcsin\left(\frac{1-\mathcal R}{2\sqrt{\mathcal R}}\right)
\simeq\frac{c}{2nd}\frac{1-\mathcal R}{\pi\sqrt{\mathcal R}}.}
$$
The last expression is the high-reflectivity approximation, with <finesse> $\pi\sqrt{\mathcal R}/(1-\mathcal R)$. At $\mathcal R=0.9$, the full phase width is about $0.2109$ radians and $\Delta\nu_{\rm FWHM}/\Delta\nu_{\rm FSR}\simeq0.03357$. At $\mathcal R=0.1$, the minimum transmission is $0.6694$, so the trace never reaches half the peak and this <full width at half maximum> is not defined. The exact half-maximum condition requires $\mathcal R\ge3-2\sqrt2$.

\Image[2016-fabry-perot-transmission.png]
{title=Fabry–Pérot transmission for mirror reflectivities 0.1 and 0.9}
{description=Original ideal <Fabry–Pérot interferometer> calculation. The broad low-reflectivity curve stays above half maximum; the right panel resolves the high-reflectivity peak and its full width at half maximum.}

For the <absorbing Fabry–Pérot mirror phase and transmission>, write $r=\sqrt{\mathcal R}e^{i\phi_r}$ and let $\mathcal T$ be the power transmission of one mirror. <Optical absorption> makes $\mathcal R+\mathcal T<1$. The transmitted intensity becomes
$$
\boxed{\frac{I_t}{I_0}=\frac{\mathcal T^2}{(1-\mathcal R)^2}\frac1{1+\frac{4\mathcal R}{(1-\mathcal R)^2}\sin^2(\delta_{\rm tot}/2)},\qquad
\delta_{\rm tot}=\frac{4\pi\nu nd}{c}+2\phi_r.}
$$
Here the phase formula is at <normal incidence>. The peak is reduced below unity, while a change in $\mathcal R$ changes the width. The complex reflection phase also moves the resonances: if $\phi_r$ is approximately constant,
$$
\boxed{\nu_m=\frac{c}{2nd}\left(m-\frac{\phi_r}{\pi}\right).}
$$
If either $n$ or $\phi_r$ depends on <frequency>, this is an implicit condition and the <free spectral range> need not be constant. No numerical shift is determined without the mirrors' optical response.

== 10
{scope}

=== Solution

Place the two slits at transverse coordinates $+h/2$ and $-h/2$. Let the source point have coordinate $s$ and the observation point coordinate $x$, both measured in the same transverse direction. The total lower-slit path minus upper-slit path is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta\ell(s,x)={}&\sqrt{R^2+(s+h/2)^2}-\sqrt{R^2+(s-h/2)^2}\\
&+\sqrt{D^2+(x+h/2)^2}-\sqrt{D^2+(x-h/2)^2}\\
\simeq{}&h\left(\frac sR+\frac xD\right).
\end{aligned}
$$
The last line uses the <paraxial approximation> at both source and screen. The source assumptions give $w,h\ll R$; the screen expression also assumes $h,|x|\ll D$. Reversing the source-coordinate orientation would change the sign of $s/R$, without changing the uniform-source result.

At the slits, the differential path delay from a source element is $hs/R$. Across the full filament its range is $hw/R$. Keeping all these phases nearly equal therefore requires
$$
\boxed{\frac{hw}{R}\ll\lambda.}
$$
This is a sufficient near-unit-<spatial coherence> condition, rather than a necessary condition for any observable <interference>. That distinction follows quantitatively below.

Different small elements of a thermal filament are mutually incoherent, so their <wave intensities> add. The two paths from one element are coherent. If that element supplies the same intensity $I_0B(s)\,ds$ through each slit, the <superposition principle> gives
$$
dI_x=\left|\sqrt{I_0B(s)\,ds}\,e^{i\varphi_+}+\sqrt{I_0B(s)\,ds}\,e^{i\varphi_-}\right|^2
=2I_0B(s)[1+\cos\delta(s,x)]\,ds,
$$
where
$$
\boxed{\delta(s,x)=\frac{2\pi h}{\lambda}\left(\frac xD+\frac sR\right).}
$$
Integrating intensities over the incoherent source gives
$$
\boxed{I_x=2I_0\int_{-w/2}^{w/2}B(s)\left[1+\cos\left(\frac{2\pi h x}{\lambda D}+\frac{2\pi h s}{\lambda R}\right)\right]ds.}
$$
Adding complex amplitudes across different source elements instead would incorrectly assume mutual <optical coherence> of the entire filament.

For $B(s)=1/w$, define $\alpha=2\pi h/(\lambda D)$ and $\beta=\pi h/(\lambda R)$. The source's signed quantity is
$$
V_{\rm printed}(x)=\frac1w\int_{-w/2}^{w/2}\cos(\alpha x+2\beta s)\,ds
=\cos(\alpha x)\frac{\sin(\beta w)}{\beta w}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac{2\pi h}{\lambda D},\qquad \beta=\frac{\pi h}{\lambda R},\qquad I_x=2I_0\left[1+\cos(\alpha x)\frac{\sin(\beta w)}{\beta w}\right].}
$$
The continuous limit at $\beta w=0$ uses $\sin z/z\to1$.

**The printed $V$ is a signed local modulation, not the usual fringe visibility.** From the maximum and minimum of this scanned fringe pattern, the <interferometric visibility> is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal V=\frac{I_{\max}-I_{\min}}{I_{\max}+I_{\min}}=\left|\frac{\sin(\beta w)}{\beta w}\right|.}
$$
It is independent of the screen's fringe phase and is nonnegative. For a concrete distinction, take $\beta w=\pi/2$ and $\alpha x=\pi/2$: the printed $V$ is zero at that observation point, while the pattern has contrast $\mathcal V=2/\pi$. The same example has $hw/R=\lambda/2$, which is not much smaller than $\lambda$, yet substantial <interference> survives. When the signed coherence factor is negative, bright and dark fringe positions interchange; the contrast remains its absolute value. This is the distinction between <signed coherence and fringe visibility>.
