= Solution

{c}

In <field characteristic> $p$, the intermediate binomial coefficients in the $p$th power vanish. Hence
$$
(\alpha+1)^p-(\alpha+1)-a=\alpha^p+1-\alpha-1-a=0.
$$
Repeated translation gives all $p$ roots $\alpha+j$, $j\in\mathbb F_p$. They are distinct because the prime <field> embeds in $F$. They exhaust the roots by <degree>; also the formal derivative of $X^p-X-a$ is $-1$, so this is a <separable polynomial>. If one <root> belongs to $F$, they all do, and
$$
\boxed{X^p-X-a=\prod_{j\in\mathbb F_p}(X-\alpha-j).}
$$
This is the <root>-translation property of an <Artin–Schreier polynomial>.

= Solution

{c}

The intended hypothesis must be a proper monic factor, with $1\leq r\leq p-1$. Without that restriction the printed deduction is false: every <polynomial> has the displayed trivial factorization with $r=p$, even when it has no <root> in $F$.

By part 1, the roots in a <splitting field> are distinct and have the form $\alpha+j$. The <degree>-$r$ monic factor is the product of $r$ corresponding linear factors. Comparing the coefficient of $X^{r-1}$ using <Vieta's formulas> gives
$$
-a_1=\sum_{j\in J}(\alpha+j)=r\alpha+\sum_{j\in J}j,\qquad |J|=r.
$$
Both $a_1$ and the sum of prime-<field> elements lie in $F$. Since $1\leq r<p$, the scalar $r$ is nonzero and invertible in $F$. Therefore $\alpha=(-a_1-\sum_{j\in J}j)/r\in F$, and part 1 proves **the <polynomial> splits into distinct linear factors over $F$**. In particular an <Artin–Schreier polynomial> of prime <degree> is either <irreducible> or splits completely.

Reduce the monic integer <polynomial> modulo $p$. Every $u\in\mathbb F_p$ satisfies $u^p=u$, so $u^p-u-1=-1\ne0$. Thus the reduction has no <root> in $\mathbb F_p$. If it had a proper factor, part 2 would force such a <root>, a contradiction. Hence it is <irreducible> over $\mathbb F_p$.

The criterion of <irreducibility by degree-preserving reduction modulo a prime>, which follows from <Gauss's lemma>, now applies because the <polynomial> is monic and its <degree> is preserved on reduction. Consequently **$X^p-X-1$ is <irreducible> in $\mathbb Q[X]$**.

The <Burnside lemma> states that the number of <orbits> of a finite <group action> is
$$
|G|^{-1}\sum_{g\in G}|\operatorname{Fix}(g)|.
$$
For $m=2n-1\geq3$, the <dihedral group> consists of $m$ rotations $r^j$ by $2\pi j/m$, and $m$ reflections $r^js$, each across the line through one vertex and the midpoint of the opposite edge. Thus $|G|=2m=4n-2$.

For the pentagon, the identity fixes $v^5e^5$ colourings. Each of the four nonidentity rotations has a single cycle on <vertices> and a single cycle on <edges>, fixing $ve$ colourings. Each of the five reflections has one fixed vertex and two vertex pairs, and one fixed edge and two edge pairs, fixing $v^3e^3$ colourings. Applying the <Burnside lemma> to simultaneous vertex and edge colourings gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{v^5e^5+4ve+5v^3e^3}{10}.}
$$
Vertex colours and edge colours may be chosen independently; each symmetry must preserve both assignments.

\boxed{a^{(p-1)/2}\equiv\left(\frac ap\right)\pmod p.}

For $p\nmid a$, the sign is $+1$ precisely for a quadratic residue and $-1$ for a quadratic nonresidue. For $p\mid a$, both sides are zero.

A <topology> $\mathcal T$ on $X$ contains $\varnothing$ and $X$, is closed under arbitrary unions, and is closed under finite intersections. Its members are the <open sets>. A subset $F\subseteq X$ is <closed> precisely when $X\setminus F$ is open.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

The <subspace topology> on $Y$ is
$$
\mathcal T_Y=\{Y\cap U:U\in\mathcal T\}.
$$
If $Y$ is open in $X$, each such intersection is open in $X$. Conversely, an open subset of $X$ contained in $Y$ is its own intersection with $Y$. Thus **the open sets of $Y$ are exactly the open subsets of $X$ contained in $Y$** in this case.

The <closure> $\overline A$ is the intersection of all closed subsets of $X$ containing $A$. It is the smallest such closed subset. Equivalently, $x\in\overline A$ exactly when every <open neighborhood> of $x$ meets $A$.

A <Hausdorff space> is one in which any distinct points $x,y$ have disjoint open neighborhoods: there exist open sets $U,V$ with $x\in U$, $y\in V$ and $U\cap V=\varnothing$.

A map $f:X\to Y$ is <continuous> if the inverse image of every open subset of $Y$ is open in $X$:
$$
V\in\mathcal T_Y\quad\Longrightarrow\quad f^{-1}(V)\in\mathcal T_X.
$$

The <inverse function theorem> says that a continuously differentiable map on an open set, with invertible derivative at a point, restricts to a $C^1$ diffeomorphism between neighborhoods of that point and its image. The inverse derivative is the inverse <matrix> of the original derivative at the corresponding preimage.

Put $s=1+x+y\ne0$. Differentiating each coordinate gives
$$
\boxed{Df(x,y)=s^{-2}\begin{pmatrix}1+y&-x\\-y&1+x\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det Df=s^{-3}.}
$$
The determinant never vanishes on $\Omega$, so the theorem applies at every point. The image point $(1/3,1/3)$ has preimage $(1,1)$: the equations $x/s=y/s=1/3$ imply $x=y=1$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{Df^{-1}(1/3,1/3)=\left[\frac19\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}\right]^{-1}
=\begin{pmatrix}6&3\\3&6\end{pmatrix}.}
$$

For fixed $x,y$, the map $z\mapsto z^3+z+xy-1$ is strictly increasing because its derivative is $3z^2+1>0$, and it tends to opposite infinities at the two ends of the real line. The <intermediate value theorem> gives exactly one <root>, defining $g(x,y)$.

For $F(x,y,z)=z^3+z+xy-1$, $F_z=3z^2+1$ never vanishes. The <implicit function theorem> supplies a $C^1$ solution locally around every $(x,y)$. Uniqueness makes all these local functions agree on overlaps, proving $g\in C^1(\mathbb R^2)$. Implicit differentiation gives
$$
Dg(x,y)=-\frac{(y,x)}{3g(x,y)^2+1}.
$$
At $(1,1)$ the <root> is zero, so **$Dg(1,1)=(-1,-1)$**.

Independent endpoint-fixed variations of the two coordinates give the <Euler-Lagrange equations>
$$
\frac d{dt}F_{\dot u}-F_u=0,\qquad
\frac d{dt}F_{\dot v}-F_v=0.
$$
Differentiating the sphere parametrization gives the <Riemannian metric> $ds^2=a^2(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)$. Its energy functional is the stated $I$. Its stationary paths are <geodesics> with affine, constant-speed parametrization; conversely a geodesic can be so parametrized. This explains the use of energy rather than arc length.

For $F=a^2(\dot\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\dot\phi^2)/2$, the equations are
$$
\boxed{\ddot\theta-\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2=0,\qquad
\frac d{dt}(\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi)=0.}
$$
The second gives the conserved <generalized momentum> $\sin^2\theta\dot\phi=h$. Since $F$ has no explicit time dependence, its <Beltrami identity> gives constant $\dot\theta F_{\dot\theta}+\dot\phi F_{\dot\phi}-F=F$. Dividing by $a^2/2$ gives the second first integral,
$$
\boxed{\dot\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\dot\phi^2=E.}
$$
Thus $E$ is the squared speed divided by $a^2$, while $h$ records the rotational symmetry about the polar axis. The coordinate equations apply away from the polar coordinate singularities.

For <small oscillations> about equilibrium, put $M_{ij}=T_{ij}(0)$ and $K_{ij}=\partial_i\partial_jV(0)$. The potential has no linear term at equilibrium; to quadratic order the <Lagrangian> is $L_2=\dot x^{\mathsf T}M\dot x/2-x^{\mathsf T}Kx/2$. Its equations are $M\ddot x+Kx=0$. A <normal mode> $x=v e^{i\omega t}$ satisfies the generalized <eigenvalue problem> $(K-\omega^2M)v=0$. For a positive-definite mass <matrix>, <diagonalization> $M^{-1/2}KM^{-1/2}$ gives the squared frequencies and independent normal coordinates. A strictly positive Hessian gives positive frequencies; zero <eigenvalues> require separate higher-order stability analysis.

Here the spring extensions are $x_1$, $x_2-x_1$, and $-x_2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{T=\tfrac m2(\dot x_1^2+\dot x_2^2),\qquad
V=\tfrac k2[x_1^2+(x_2-x_1)^2+x_2^2].}
$$
The stiffness <matrix> is $k\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ and $M=mI$. Its eigenvectors $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ have stiffness <eigenvalues> $k$ and $3k$, giving
$$
\boxed{\omega_1=\sqrt{k/m},\quad v_1=(1,1);\qquad
\omega_2=\sqrt{3k/m},\quad v_2=(1,-1).}
$$
In the first mode the masses move together with equal displacements and the middle spring keeps its length. In the second they move oppositely with equal amplitudes, stretching and compressing the middle spring twice as much as either end spring.

For a real potential, the <time-dependent Schrödinger equation> is
$$
i\hbar\partial_t\psi=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi+V\psi.
$$
Multiply this equation by $\psi^*$, multiply its conjugate by $\psi$, and subtract. The potential terms cancel and the product rule converts the remaining terms into a divergence. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\partial_t|\psi|^2+\nabla\cdot\mathbf j=0,\qquad
\mathbf j=\frac{\hbar}{2mi}(\psi^*\nabla\psi-\psi\nabla\psi^*)
=\frac\hbar m\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\nabla\psi).}
$$
This is the <probability current> and its <continuity equation>.

Put $R(r)=\sin(kr)/r$. The supplied radial <Laplacian> formula gives $\nabla^2R=-k^2R$ for $r>0$. Also $i\hbar\partial_t\psi=E\psi$. Therefore the free-particle equation is satisfied when
$$
\boxed{k=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar.}
$$
The apparent singularity at zero is removable: $R(r)=k-k^3r^2/6+\cdots$ extends smoothly there and satisfies the same equation. Since $R$ is real and the time dependence is only a common phase, $\psi^*\nabla\psi=R\nabla R$ is real, so $\mathbf j=0$. The <density> $R^2$ is time-independent, proving the continuity equation explicitly. This is a standing spherical wave with zero net radial current.

The momentum of a <material volume> $V(t)$ is $\int_{V(t)}\rho\mathbf u\,dV$. <Newton's second law> equates its time derivative to the <pressure> force and <gravity>:
$$
\frac d{dt}\int_{V(t)}\rho\mathbf u\,dV
=-\int_{\partial V(t)}p\mathbf n\,dS+\int_{V(t)}\rho\mathbf g\,dV.
$$
Apply the given <Reynolds transport theorem> componentwise to the left side, and the <divergence theorem> to the <pressure> term. Then
$$
\int_{V(t)}\left[\rho(\partial_t\mathbf u+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u)+\nabla p-\rho\mathbf g\right]dV=0.
$$
Since this holds for arbitrary material volumes and the fields are continuous, the integrand vanishes pointwise. Dividing by positive <density> gives the <Euler equations for an inviscid fluid>
$$
\boxed{\partial_t\mathbf u+(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u=-\rho^{-1}\nabla p+\mathbf g.}
$$
At a fixed physical time $t_0$, a <streamline> is a curve tangent to $\mathbf u(\cdot,t_0)$, which may be parametrized by $d\mathbf x/ds=\mathbf u(\mathbf x(s),t_0)$. The <chain rule> gives $d f(\mathbf x(s),t_0)/ds=\mathbf u\cdot\nabla f=0$, proving constancy along it. The time is held fixed; a time-dependent streamline statement does not by itself assert material conservation along particle trajectories.

\boxed{\omega^2=gk\tanh(kh).}

```
sim1 <- function(p) {
  if (!is.numeric(p) || length(p) == 0L ||
      any(!is.finite(p)) || any(p < 0) ||
      abs(sum(p) - 1) > 1e-12) {
    stop("p must be a nonempty finite probability vector")

  cumulative <- cumsum(p)
  cumulative[length(p)] <- 1
  u <- runif(1)

}
```

```
simchain <- function(n, P) {
  if (!is.numeric(n) || length(n) != 1L || !is.finite(n) ||
      n < 1 || n != floor(n)) stop("n must be a positive integer")
  if (!is.matrix(P) || !is.numeric(P) || nrow(P) == 0L ||
      nrow(P) != ncol(P) || any(!is.finite(P)) ||
      any(P < 0) || any(abs(rowSums(P) - 1) > 1e-12)) {
    stop("P must be a finite square stochastic matrix")
  }
  k <- nrow(P)
  state <- sim1(rep(1/k, k))
  counts <- numeric(k)
  counts[state] <- 1
  if (n > 1) {
    for (step in seq_len(n - 1)) {
      state <- sim1(P[state, ])
      counts[state] <- counts[state] + 1
    }
  }
  counts / n
}
```

Conditional on the current state $i$, `sim1(P[i, ])` selects $j$ with <probability> $P_{ij}$, proving the <Markov property> with the desired <transition matrix>. Initialization is uniform by the same sampler. Dividing the visit counts by $n$ gives the requested <empirical occupation proportions of a finite Markov chain>, including a valid result when $n=1$.
