For a <finite group> action, <Burnside's lemma> gives
$$
|\Omega/G|=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}|\operatorname{Fix}(g)|.
$$
Pair each vertex with its opposite face. This correspondence is equivariant under every tetrahedral symmetry. Each pair has six possible combined color states: three face colors and two vertex colors. Thus we are coloring four positions with six symbols; the two kinds of coloring must be counted together.

The group of rotations is the <alternating group> $A_4$. The identity has four cycles and fixes $6^4$ colorings. Its three half-turns have cycle type $(2,2)$ and each fix $6^2$. Its eight third-turns have cycle type $(3,1)$ and each also fix $6^2$. Consequently the rotation count is
$$
\boxed{\frac{6^4+3\cdot6^2+8\cdot6^2}{12}=141.}
$$
Allowing mirror equivalence gives the full tetrahedral symmetry group $S_4$. Now all permutations of the four paired positions are allowed, so a coloring is determined by a <multiset> of four symbols chosen from six. <stars and bars> gives
$$
\boxed{\binom{6+4-1}{4}=\binom94=126.}
$$
For an independent Burnside check, the five cycle types of $S_4$ give numerator $6^4+6\cdot6^3+3\cdot6^2+8\cdot6^2+6\cdot6=3024$, whose quotient by 24 is 126. This is <paired face and vertex colorings of a tetrahedron>.

The <degree of a field extension> $[K:F]$ is the dimension of $K$ as a <vector space> over $F$. The <tower law> states that for finite extensions $F\subseteq E\subseteq K$,
$$
[K:F]=[K:E][E:F].
$$
If the left side is prime, one factor is one. Degree one forces equality of the corresponding fields, so **there is no proper intermediate field**.

Let $K$ have 32 elements. Its <prime field> is $\mathbb F_2$, and its dimension over that field is five, since a dimension-$d$ <vector space> over $\mathbb F_2$ has $2^d$ elements. Every element outside $\mathbb F_2$ has <minimal polynomial> of degree five: its generated intermediate field has degree greater than one and dividing five.

Every element of $K$ satisfies $x^{32}=x$, by the <multiplicative group of a field> of order 31 for nonzero elements. Hence $X^{32}-X$ splits completely over $K$ and has no repeated roots, since its derivative is minus one. Conversely any <irreducible polynomial> $q$ over $\mathbb F_2$ gives a <quotient ring> $\mathbb F_2[X]/(q)$ with 32 elements. Its defining element satisfies $X^{32}-X$, so $q$ divides that <polynomial> and therefore has all five distinct roots in $K$.

The 30 elements of $K\setminus\mathbb F_2$ are thus partitioned into disjoint five-element root sets of the <irreducible polynomials>. Their number is
$$
\boxed{\frac{32-2}{5}=6.}
$$
No classification of the six individual <polynomials> is needed; the tower degree and separability already count them all.

<Euler's theorem> states that if $\gcd(a,m)=1$, then $a^{\phi(m)}\equiv1\pmod m$, where $\phi$ is <Euler's totient function>. This includes <Fermat's little theorem> when $m$ is prime.

For each prime-power factor $p_i^{e_i}$, the integer $\phi(p_i^{e_i})$ divides the defined <least common multiple> $\lambda(m)$. <Euler's theorem> modulo that prime power therefore gives
$$
a^{\lambda(m)}
=\left(a^{\phi(p_i^{e_i})}\right)^{\lambda(m)/\phi(p_i^{e_i})}
\equiv1\pmod{p_i^{e_i}}.
$$
The prime powers are pairwise coprime, so their product divides $a^{\lambda(m)}-1$. Equivalently the <Chinese remainder theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{a^{\lambda(m)}\equiv1\pmod m.}
$$
For the specified composite integer,
$$
\lambda(561)=\operatorname{lcm}(2,10,16)=80,
\qquad560=7\cdot80.
$$
Raising $a^{80}\equiv1\pmod{561}$ to the seventh power proves
$$
\boxed{a^{560}\equiv1\pmod{561}\quad(\gcd(a,561)=1).}
$$
Thus 561 is a <Carmichael number>. We use exactly the source's definition of $\lambda$ as the <least common multiple> of prime-power totients; its validity here does not require replacing it by a smaller exponent at powers of two.

The <monotone convergence theorem> states that if measurable functions $f_n$ are nonnegative and $f_n\uparrow f$ pointwise, then $f$ is measurable and
$$
\boxed{\int f\,d\mu=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int f_n\,d\mu,}
$$
allowing either side to be infinite. The same conclusion holds under almost-everywhere <monotone convergence> after changing values on a null set. No assumption that the integrals stay bounded is required.

Use the increasing nonnegative measurable functions $f_n(x)=x^{-2}\mathbf1_{[1/n,1]}(x)$. Their pointwise limit is the given function except at irrelevant endpoint choices, and the <fundamental theorem of calculus> on $[1/n,1]$ gives

A <dominated differentiation under the integral sign> suffices. Suppose $\phi(\cdot,t)$ is measurable, $\phi(\cdot,t_0)$ is integrable at one parameter value, and for almost every $x$ the map $t\mapsto\phi(x,t)$ is continuously differentiable on an interval. If $|\partial_t\phi(x,t)|\leq H(x)$ throughout that interval for an integrable $H$, then the parameter integral is differentiable, and
$$
\frac d{dt}\int\phi(x,t)dx=\int\partial_t\phi(x,t)dx.
$$
One direct sufficient version assumes $\partial_t\phi(x,t)$ is continuous in $t$ almost everywhere and has that common domination; <dominated convergence theorem> then proves the assertion.

Here $|f(x)e^{itx}|=|f(x)|$, so the <complex-valued function> $h$ is integrable for every real $t$. For a real increment $u\neq0$, the elementary bound $|e^{iux}-1|\leq|ux|$ gives
$$
\left|f(x)e^{itx}\frac{e^{iux}-1}{u}\right|
\leq|xf(x)|.
$$
The right side is integrable by hypothesis and the integrand converges pointwise to $ixf(x)e^{itx}$. <dominated convergence theorem>, applied to real and imaginary parts, proves
$$
\boxed{\widehat f'(t)=\int_{\mathbb R}ixf(x)e^{itx}dx.}
$$
This supplies the domination explicitly rather than merely differentiating a formal <Fourier transform>.

A <metric space> is sequentially compact if every sequence in it has a <subsequence> converging to a point of the same space. The sequence $x_n=1/(n+1)$ lies in $(0,1)$, but every <subsequence> converges in $\mathbb R$ to zero. Uniqueness of limits excludes an alternative limit inside $(0,1)$, so **the open interval is not sequentially compact**.

For the product, take any sequence $(x_n,y_n)$. <sequential compactness> of $X$ gives a <subsequence> $x_{n_k}\to x\in X$. Apply <sequential compactness> of $Y$ to the corresponding sequence $y_{n_k}$ to obtain a further <subsequence> $y_{n_{k_j}}\to y\in Y$. Passing to a <subsequence> preserves the convergence of the first coordinate, and therefore
$$
d((x_{n_{k_j}},y_{n_{k_j}}),(x,y))
=d_X(x_{n_{k_j}},x)+d_Y(y_{n_{k_j}},y)\to0.
$$
Thus **$X\times Y$ is sequentially compact** with the stated sum metric. Taking the second coordinate <subsequence> inside the first one is essential; two unrelated <subsequences> would not suffice.

The required condition is $D_2F(a,b)\neq0$. The <implicit function theorem> then gives a unique local differentiable branch $y=f(x)$ with $f(a)=b$ and
$$
\boxed{f'(x)=-\frac{D_1F(x,f(x))}{D_2F(x,f(x))}.}
$$
The denominator remains nonzero after shrinking the neighborhood by <continuity>.

For the specified smooth function,
$$
F_x=y+\cos(x+y),\qquad F_y=x+\cos(x+y).
$$
At $(0,0)$ both equal one, so the condition holds and $f'(0)=-1$. Differentiate $F(x,f(x))=0$ twice:
$$
F_{xx}+2F_{xy}f'+F_{yy}(f')^2+F_yf''=0.
$$
At zero, $F_{xx}=F_{yy}=0$, $F_{xy}=1$, and $F_y=1$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{f'(0)=-1,\qquad f''(0)=2.}
$$
The additional derivatives exist because this particular $F$ is smooth; the general first-derivative theorem only requires the stated continuous first <partial derivatives>.

Let $\eta$ be a smooth <variation> with $\eta=\eta'=0$ at both endpoints, and consider $y_\epsilon=y+\epsilon\eta$. <stationary point> gives the <first variation>
$$
0=\left.\frac d{d\epsilon}J[y_\epsilon]\right|_{\epsilon=0}
=\int_{x_1}^{x_2}
\left(F_y\eta+F_{y'}\eta'+F_{y''}\eta''\right)dx.
$$
Integrate the middle term once by parts and the last term twice. All boundary terms vanish because both $\eta$ and $\eta'$ vanish there. Assuming the derivatives needed for the classical formula, the given <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations> yields
$$
\boxed{F_y-\frac d{dx}F_{y'}+
\frac{d^2}{dx^2}F_{y''}=0.}
$$
Without assuming those classical derivatives initially, the same calculation with compactly supported variations states this equation weakly; sufficient regularity then gives the classical equation.

For the specific integrand, $F_y=-2y$, $F_{y'}=0$, $F_{y''}=2y''$, so the equation is $y^{(4)}-y=0$. Its <weak solutions> here are smooth, and its general classical solution is
$$
y=A\cos x+B\sin x+C\cosh x+D\sinh x.
$$
Put $\ell=\pi/2$, $c=\cosh\ell$, $s=\sinh\ell$. The conditions at zero give $C=-A$ and $D=1-B$. Those at $\ell$ give
$$
B-Ac+(1-B)s=0,\qquad
-A-As+(1-B)c=0.
$$
Using $c^2-s^2=1$, solve to obtain $A=c/2$, $B=(1-s)/2$, $C=-c/2$, $D=(1+s)/2$. Thus an <extremal> is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac12\left[
c(\cos x-\cosh x)+(1-s)\sin x+(1+s)\sinh x
\right].}
$$
Substitution checks all four boundary values. The additive term $x^2$ in the integrand contributes no <variation> in $y$, so it does not alter this equation.

Autonomy gives conservation of <energy>, while the <cyclic coordinate> give conserved <momenta>:
$$
E=\frac A2(\dot\theta^2+\dot\phi^2\sin^2\theta)
+\frac C2(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta)^2+mga\cos\theta,
$$
$$
p_\psi=C(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta)=Cn,\qquad
p_\phi=A\dot\phi\sin^2\theta+Cn\cos\theta=h.
$$
Here $p_\psi$ is the <angular momentum> along the body's symmetry axis, and $p_\phi$ is its component along the fixed vertical. The time symmetry corresponds to $E$, not to an additional angle <momentum>.

With $u=\cos\theta$ and $-1<u<1$, solve
$$
\dot\phi=\frac{h-Cnu}{A(1-u^2)}.
$$
Insert this and the conserved spin rate $n$ into the <energy> equation, multiply by $A$, and isolate $\dot\theta$:
$$
\boxed{A^2\dot\theta^2
=\frac{A(2E-Cn^2-2mgau)(1-u^2)-(h-Cnu)^2}{1-u^2}
=\frac{P(u)}{1-u^2}.}
$$

For a fixed nonvertical angle, let
$$
V_{\rm eff}(u)=\frac{(h-Cnu)^2}{2A(1-u^2)}
+\frac12Cn^2+mgau.
$$
The <energy> is $A\dot\theta^2/2+V_{\rm eff}(\cos\theta)$. A constant angle in $(0,\pi)$ requires both $E=V_{\rm eff}(u_0)$ and $V_{\rm eff}'(u_0)=0$, by the reduced <Euler-Lagrange equation> and $\sin\theta_0\neq0$. Since
$$
P(u)=2A(1-u^2)(E-V_{\rm eff}(u)),
$$
both $P(u_0)=0$ and $P'(u_0)=0$, with $u_0\in(-1,1)$.

The source's unqualified final sentence omits the nonvertical restriction. A vertical axis has $u=\pm1$, where the displayed reduction divides by zero and the common root need not be interior. For example, the stationary upright top has $n=h=0$, $E=mga$, and
$$
P(u)=2Amga(1-u)^2(1+u).
$$
Its only repeated root is $u=1$, so it has no common root with its derivative in $(-1,1)$. Thus **the interior double-root conclusion holds for nonvertical steady precession**. The vertical configurations require separate endpoint treatment. This is <nonvertical qualification in the steady-top double-root condition>.

For a consistently oriented surface and boundary, <Ampère's law> is
$$
\oint_\Gamma\mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell
=\mu_0 I_{\rm enc}
=\mu_0\int_S\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf n\,dS.
$$
<Stokes theorem> gives the left side as $\int_S(\nabla\times\mathbf B)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS$, so the given <curl> equation proves the law.

On a circle of radius $r$ around the axis, the assumed azimuthal field is tangent and constant in magnitude. Thus the <circulation> is $2\pi rB_\theta(r)$. For $r\leq a$, the enclosed <electric current> is $I r^2/a^2$; for $r\geq a$, it is $I$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B(r)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{\mu_0 I r}{2\pi a^2}\mathbf e_\theta,&0\leq r\leq a,\\[4pt]
\dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\mathbf e_\theta,&r\geq a.
\end{cases}}
$$
The field is continuous at $r=a$ and vanishes regularly at the axis. Its direction follows the right-hand rule for positive <electric current> along $\mathbf e_z$. The radial component of the <curl> equation also gives $\partial_zB_\theta=0$, so the expression is valid at every height; no unmentioned axial dependence remains.

In steady flow, the supplied <Euler equations for an inviscid fluid> gives
$$
\nabla H=-\rho\,\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf u.

Taking its scalar product with $\mathbf u$ gives $\mathbf u\cdot\nabla H=0$. Along a <streamline> with tangent $\mathbf u$, this is precisely zero derivative of $H$. Hence **$H$ is constant along each <streamline>**, with possibly different constants on different <streamlines>.

<Milne-Thomson's circle theorem> states that if $f(z)$ is a <complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow> whose singularities lie outside $|z|=a$, then
$$
w(z)=f(z)+\overline{f(a^2/\bar z)}
$$
has the circle as a <streamline>, with the original exterior singularities unchanged. Additive constants in the potential are immaterial.

Take $f(z)=\Gamma(2\pi i)^{-1}\log(z-z_1)$, using the convention that positive $\Gamma$ is positive <circulation>. Reflection in the circle gives, up to a constant,
$$
\boxed{w(z)=\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi i}
\left[\log(z-z_1)
-\log\left(z-\frac{a^2}{\bar z_1}\right)+\log z\right].}
$$
The reflected <vortex> at $a^2/\bar z_1$ has strength $-\Gamma$, while the central term has strength $+\Gamma$. Their combined <circulation> around the cylinder is zero; this is the <circulation> normalization implicit in the requested equation.

The <vortex> moves with the <velocity> induced by the other singularities, excluding its own singular term. Since <complex velocity> is $dw/dz=u-iv$, its value at the <vortex> is $d\bar z_1/dt$. Thus
$$
\frac{d\bar z_1}{dt}
=\frac{\Gamma}{2\pi i}
\left[-\frac1{z_1-a^2/\bar z_1}+\frac1{z_1}\right]
=\boxed{\frac{i\Gamma a^2}{2\pi z_1(|z_1|^2-a^2)}.}
$$
Writing $z_1=re^{i\theta}$ and multiplying this equation by $z_1$ gives
$$
r\dot r-ir^2\dot\theta
=\frac{i\Gamma a^2}{2\pi(r^2-a^2)}.
$$
Its real part implies $\dot r=0$, and its imaginary part gives
$$
\boxed{\dot\theta=-\frac{\Gamma a^2}
{2\pi r^2(r^2-a^2)}.}
$$
The radius is constant and the angular <velocity> is constant on that orbit. For positive $\Gamma$ the orbit is clockwise with this convention. Retaining the conjugate on the time derivative is essential for the sign.

using the <Gamma function> after the scaling $u=(\lambda-t)x$. At $t\geq\lambda$ the positive integral diverges.

where $\lambda$ is the rate parameter, as in the printed <probability density function>.

A <confidence interval> is a pair of statistics $L(X),U(X)$ such that, for each parameter value $\mu$,
$$
\mathbb P_\mu\{L(X)\leq\mu\leq U(X)\}\geq1-\alpha.
$$
In repeated sampling, at least the stated fraction of intervals constructed by this rule cover the fixed unknown parameter. It is not a posterior <probability> statement about the parameter after one interval has been observed. Discrete exact constructions can have coverage greater than the nominal value.

For independent observations from a <Poisson distribution>, $S=\sum_iX_i$ has a <Poisson distribution> with mean $\lambda=n\mu$. Invert equal-tail tests of that total mean. At an observed integer $s>0$, the lower endpoint solves
$$
\mathbb P_{\lambda_L}(S\geq s)=\alpha/2
\iff F_{\lambda_L}(s-1)=1-\alpha/2,
$$
and the upper endpoint solves
$$
\mathbb P_{\lambda_U}(S\leq s)=\alpha/2
\iff F_{\lambda_U}(s)=\alpha/2.
$$
Then divide both endpoints by $n$. If $s=0$, use lower endpoint zero. Each inversion allocates at most $\alpha/2$ noncoverage <probability> to one side, giving at least $1-\alpha$ total coverage.

Here the single total count is 39. The supplied values with the correct adjacent count indices are $F_{27.7}(38)=0.975$ for the lower endpoint and $F_{53.3}(39)=0.025$ for the upper one. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mu\in[27.7,\ 53.3]\quad\text{at the stated 95\% level}.}
$$
The values 28.6 and 52.2 use the other count index and are not the endpoints of this central interval. This is <adjacent count tails in an exact Poisson confidence interval>.

The <statistical power> of a test $\varphi$ is $\beta(\theta)=\mathbb E_\theta\varphi(X)$, its rejection <probability> at parameter $\theta$. Its size is $\sup_{\theta\in H_0}\beta(\theta)$; for a simple null this is just $\beta(\theta_0)$.

The <Neyman-Pearson lemma> states that for testing a simple null <probability density function> $f_0$ against a simple alternative <probability density function> $f_1$, a test rejecting where $f_1/f_0>k$, accepting where it is below $k$, and if needed randomizing on equality to attain the desired size is most powerful among all tests of that size. It follows by integrating $(\varphi-\varphi_*)(f_1-kf_0)\leq0$ and using the size constraint.

The proposed <probability density function> is nonnegative, and
$$
\int_0^\infty f(x;\theta)dx
=\frac{\theta^2}{\theta+1}
\left(\frac1{\theta^2}+\frac1\theta\right)=1.
$$
For an independent sample, cancellation of the factors $(x_i+1)$ in the <likelihood ratio> gives
$$
\frac{L(\theta_1)}{L(\theta_0)}
=\left[\frac{\theta_1^2/(\theta_1+1)}
{\theta_0^2/(\theta_0+1)}\right]^n
\exp\left((\theta_0-\theta_1)\sum_i x_i\right).
$$
For $\theta_1<\theta_0$ this is increasing in $S=\sum_iX_i$. The most powerful test therefore has <rejection region>
$$
\boxed{S>c_\alpha,\qquad
\mathbb P_{\theta_0}(S>c_\alpha)=\alpha.}
$$
The sum has a continuous positive <probability density function> on $(0,\infty)$, so no boundary randomization is required. The threshold depends on the null and chosen size, but not on which $\theta_1<\theta_0$ was specified. The same test is therefore **a <uniformly most powerful test> for the composite alternative $0<\theta<\theta_0$**.

A <Givens rotation> acts as the identity except on coordinates $i,j$, where its block can be written
$$
\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\
-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.

The block has orthonormal columns because $\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1$ and their inner product is zero, so $J^TJ=I$. For $r=\sqrt{v_i^2+v_j^2}>0$, choose $\cos\theta=v_i/r$, $\sin\theta=v_j/r$. The new coordinates are $(r,0)$, eliminating the $j$th entry. If both entries are zero, the identity already works.

For the specified vector, one suitable matrix is

\boxed{Q=\begin{pmatrix}
4/5&0&-3/5\\3/5&0&4/5\\0&-1&0
\end{pmatrix},\qquad Qv=\begin{pmatrix}0\\5\\0\end{pmatrix}.}

Its rows are orthonormal, so it is orthogonal; in fact its <determinant> is one. The positive nonzero output component must be five because an <orthogonal matrix> preserves <Euclidean norm>.

**The matrix is not unique.** Left multiplication by any <orthogonal matrix> fixing the second coordinate vector preserves this output. For example arbitrary rotations of the first and third coordinates provide infinitely many alternatives.

<Richardson extrapolation> combines approximations with a known leading error power to cancel that error. If $A(h)=I+ch^p+O(h^{p+q})$, then $(2^pA(h)-A(2h))/(2^p-1)$ removes $ch^p$.

Let $x_j=a+jh$ for an even number $n$ of equal subintervals, with $x_n=b$. The <composite trapezoidal rule> is
$$
T_h=h\left[\frac{f_0+f_n}{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}f_j\right].
$$
The <Simpson's rule> is
$$
S_h=\frac h3\left[f_0+f_n+
4\sum_{\substack{1\leq j<n\\j\ {\rm odd}}}f_j+
2\sum_{\substack{2\leq j<n\\j\ {\rm even}}}f_j\right].
$$
Assume an error expansion $T_h=I+c_2h^2+O(h^4)$, with the same coefficient $c_2$ on refined uniform meshes; $f\in C^4([a,b])$ is sufficient for this form. The coarser rule is $T_{2h}=h[f_0+f_n+2\sum_{\rm even}f_j]$. Direct coefficient collection gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{4T_h-T_{2h}}3=S_h.}
$$
The leading $h^2$ errors cancel, leaving $S_h=I+O(h^4)$. The identity of the <numerical integration> weights is exact; the error-order conclusion additionally uses the smoothness and even-power error assumptions.

The first four jobs all have deadlines at most ten, but their total processing time is $2+4+3+2=11$. They cannot all be completed by time ten on one machine, so at least one is late in every schedule.

<Moore's algorithm> processes jobs in nondecreasing deadline order. Whenever the <electric current> on-time list first misses the new job's deadline, remove from that list a job with the largest processing time and put it on the late list. Continue, then append the rejected jobs after the on-time list. The theorem for this algorithm states that it minimizes the number of late jobs when all jobs are initially available.

The source order is already deadline order. The steps are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
\text{job added}&\text{tentative completion}&\text{action}&
\text{on-time list afterward}\\\hline
1&2\leq3&\text{keep}&1\\
2&6\leq7&\text{keep}&1,2\\
3&9\leq9&\text{keep}&1,2,3\\
4&11>10&\text{remove }2\ (p_2=4)&1,3,4\\
5&12\leq13&\text{keep}&1,3,4,5\\
6&16>15&\text{remove }5\ (p_5=5)&1,3,4,6
\end{array}
$$
After the first removal the cumulative time is seven; after the second it is eleven. Append jobs two and five. One optimal schedule is
$$
\boxed{1,3,4,6,2,5,\qquad\text{two late jobs}.}
$$
Its completion times are $2,5,7,11,15,20$. Comparing with the corresponding deadlines verifies that precisely jobs two and five are late. Moore's theorem certifies that no schedule can improve on that number.

The dual is
$$
\boxed{\text{minimize }b^Ty
\quad\text{subject to }A^Ty\geq c,\quad y\geq0.}
$$
<weak duality> says that any feasible primal $x$ and dual $y$ satisfy $c^Tx\leq b^Ty$. Indeed,
$$
c^Tx\leq(A^Ty)^Tx=y^TAx\leq y^Tb.
$$
The first inequality uses $x\geq0$ and $A^Ty-c\geq0$, and the second uses $y\geq0$ and $b-Ax\geq0$. This proves the theorem. In particular, feasible solutions with equal <objective function> values are optimal for both problems.
