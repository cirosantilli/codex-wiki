The <splitting field> in $\mathbb C$ is the smallest subfield containing $\mathbb Q$ and every <root> of the <polynomial>. The sixth roots of unity are powers of $\zeta_6=e^{i\pi/3}$, so the <splitting field> of $x^6-1$ is $\mathbb Q(\zeta_6)=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3})$.

For $x^6+x^3+1=(x^9-1)/(x^3-1)$, the roots are exactly the primitive ninth roots of unity, all powers of $\zeta_9=e^{2\pi i/9}$. Thus its <splitting field> is $\mathbb Q(\zeta_9)$. The supplied irreducibility makes the displayed degree-six <polynomial> the <minimal polynomial> of $\zeta_9$, giving
$$
\boxed{[\mathbb Q(\zeta_9):\mathbb Q]=6.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Replacing $x$ by $-x$ in the irreducible <polynomial> from part (i) gives $x^6-x^3+1$. This substitution is an invertible automorphism of $\mathbb Q[x]$, so it preserves irreducibility. The roots are the primitive eighteenth roots, and the <splitting field> is $\mathbb Q(\zeta_{18})$. They are all powers of one such <root>, so its irreducible degree-six <polynomial> gives
$$
\boxed{[\mathbb Q(\zeta_{18}):\mathbb Q]=6.}
$$
In fact this is the same <field> as in part (i): $\zeta_9=\zeta_{18}^2$ and $\zeta_{18}=-\zeta_9^5$.

A finite <soluble group> is one whose <derived series> $G^{(0)}=G$, $G^{(j+1)}=[G^{(j)},G^{(j)}]$ reaches the trivial group. Equivalently it has a finite normal series with abelian factors. If $H\leq G$, induction using <commutator> containment gives $H^{(j)}\leq G^{(j)}$ for every $j$. Thus termination for $G$ implies termination for $H$, proving **every <subgroup> of a <soluble group> is soluble**.

For $S_4$, the chain
$$
\{1\}\triangleleft V_4\triangleleft A_4\triangleleft S_4
$$
has abelian factors $V_4$, $C_3$ and $C_2$. Here $V_4$ consists of the identity and the three double transpositions. It is normal because conjugation permutes those double transpositions. Hence **$S_4$ is soluble**.

An example neither soluble nor simple is **$S_5$**. Its <subgroup> $A_5$ is proper, nontrivial and normal, so $S_5$ is not simple. The group $A_5$ is nontrivial and perfect: its generating three-cycles are commutators of double transpositions, using two additional symbols, so $[A_5,A_5]=A_5$. For example the <commutator> of $(12)(45)$ and $(23)(45)$ is a three-cycle, and conjugation and inversion give all three-cycles. Thus $A_5$ is not soluble, and <subgroup> inheritance then proves $S_5$ is not soluble either.

<Fermat's little theorem> states that for a prime $p$ and any integer $a$,
$$
\boxed{a^p\equiv a\pmod p.}
$$
If $p\nmid a$, the equivalent multiplicative form is $a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

<Wilson's theorem> states that an integer $p>1$ is prime if and only if
$$
\boxed{(p-1)!\equiv-1\pmod p.}
$$
In particular the congruence holds for every prime $p$, including two.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}
{c}

Combine the preceding congruences: $a^p\equiv a$ and $(p-1)!\equiv-1\pmod p$. Therefore $a^p+(p-1)!a\equiv a-a=0$, and $(p-1)!a^p+a\equiv-a+a=0$. This proves **both stated divisibilities for every integer $a$**, with no assumption that $a$ is coprime to $p$.

Modulo $p$, the term $p^{q-1}$ is zero and <Fermat's little theorem> gives $q^{p-1}\equiv1$, since $q\ne p$. Modulo $q$, the roles reverse. Hence the sum minus one is divisible by both distinct primes. Their coprimality gives
$$
\boxed{p^{q-1}+q^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod{pq}.}
$$

The <Lebesgue outer measure> of $E$ is the infimum of $\sum_j|I_j|$ over countable coverings by open intervals. A set is a <null set> when this infimum is zero, equivalently when it has such a cover of total length less than every prescribed positive number.

The <Cantor set> is obtained from $[0,1]$ by deleting the open middle third of every remaining interval at each stage. After $n$ stages it lies inside $2^n$ closed intervals of length $3^{-n}$. Slightly enlarging these finitely many intervals to open intervals shows its <outer measure> is at most $(2/3)^n$ for every $n$, hence zero.

For the modified construction, let $L_n$ be the total length of the $2^n$ intervals in $K_n$. The removed fraction at stage $n$ is exactly $1/(2^n+2)$, so
$$
L_{n+1}=L_n\frac{2^n+1}{2^n+2},\qquad L_0=1.
$$
The product telescopes because $2^n+2=2(2^{n-1}+1)$, giving
$$
L_n=\frac13+\frac{2}{3\cdot2^n}.
$$
Since $K\subseteq K_n$, the same finite interval-cover argument gives $m^*(K)\leq L_n$ for every $n$. Taking the infimum proves
$$
\boxed{m^*(K)\leq1/3.}
$$
Only interval lengths and the definition of <outer measure> were used; the three numbered clauses in the source describe the construction rather than separate requests to prove additional statements.

A useful <differentiation under the integral sign> theorem is this: if $f(\cdot,y)$ is integrable, $f$ is differentiable in $y$ almost everywhere, and on every <compact> parameter interval $|\partial_yf(x,y)|$ is bounded by an <integrable function> of $x$, then $F'(y)=\int_I\partial_yf(x,y)dx$, under the usual pointwise <continuity> hypotheses. It follows by applying <dominated convergence> to difference quotients. The theorem can be iterated when a corresponding bound holds for the second derivative.

On a <compact> interval $0<a\leq y\leq b$, the original integrand is bounded by $b/(1+x^4)$, using $|\sin(x^2y)|\leq bx^2$. Its first and second parameter derivatives are
$$
\frac{\cos(x^2y)}{1+x^4},\qquad-\frac{x^2\sin(x^2y)}{1+x^4},
$$
with integrable bounds $(1+x^4)^{-1}$ and $x^2/(1+x^4)$. Thus $F$ is twice differentiable. Subtracting the integrals gives
$$
F''(y)-F(y)=-\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin(x^2y)}{x^2}dx
=-\sqrt y\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin t^2}{t^2}dt.
$$
The final integral is finite: its integrand tends to one at zero and is absolutely bounded by $t^{-2}$ at infinity. Consequently
$$
\boxed{F''(y)-F(y)=c\sqrt y,\quad c=-\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin t^2}{t^2}dt,}
$$
which establishes the required parameter-independent constant without needing an explicit Fresnel integral evaluation.

The empty set is countable, and the whole space contains $a$, so both belong to the proposed closed family. The family contains the whole space and the empty set. A finite intersection of nontrivial members is $(\max_j a_j,\infty)$. A nonempty union of rays is $(\inf_j a_j,\infty)$ when the infimum is finite, and is the whole line when the infimum is $-\infty$. {parent=ii}

If $A$ has a smallest element $a_0$, any open cover has a member containing $a_0$. This member is either the whole line or a ray $(c,\infty)$ with $c<a_0$, so it already contains all of $A$. Thus a single member suffices and $A$ is <compact>.

If $A$ has no smallest element, the family $\{(a,\infty):a\in A\}$ covers $A$: each point has another point of $A$ below it. A finite subfamily has union $(\min_j a_j,\infty)$, which misses its smallest listed endpoint, itself a point of $A$. No finite subcover exists. Therefore **a nonempty subset is <compact> exactly when it has a smallest element** in this <topology>.

The full-<rank> <constraint qualification> gives a multiplier vector $\lambda\in\mathbb R^2$ such that
$$
\boxed{\nabla g(x_0)=Df(x_0)^T\lambda.}
$$
Equivalently the <gradient> of the objective lies in the span of the two constraint gradients. The point also satisfies $f(x_0)=0$.

= b
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}
{c}

The constraint intersection is nonempty, closed and bounded, hence <compact>, so extrema exist. The two constraint gradients are $(x/2,2y,2z)$ and $(1,1,-1)$. If the first were $t$ times the second, the plane condition would give $2t+t/2+t/2=0$, so $t=0$ and all coordinates would vanish, contradicting the ellipsoid equation. Their <rank> is therefore two at every feasible point, justifying the <Lagrange multiplier> equations.

These equations are
$$
(1,2,0)=\lambda(x/2,2y,2z)+\mu(1,1,-1),\quad z=x+y.
$$
Eliminating $\mu$ gives $1=\lambda(5x/2+2y)$ and $2=\lambda(2x+4y)$. The first equation makes $\lambda\ne0$; subtracting twice the first from the second forces $x=0$. Hence $z=y$, and the ellipsoid gives $y=z=\pm1/\sqrt2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{g_{\max}=\sqrt2\text{ at }(0,1/\sqrt2,1/\sqrt2),\qquad
 g_{\min}=-\sqrt2\text{ at }(0,-1/\sqrt2,-1/\sqrt2).}
$$
All candidates were found, and <compactness> ensures they include the extrema.

Take an admissible variation $u+\epsilon\eta$ with $\eta=0$ on the boundary. Differentiating the functional at an extremum gives
$$
0=\int_V(F_u\eta+F_{u_x}\eta_x+F_{u_y}\eta_y+F_{u_z}\eta_z)dV.
$$
Integration by parts removes the derivatives from $\eta$; the boundary term vanishes. The <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations> yields
$$
\boxed{\partial_xF_{u_x}+\partial_yF_{u_y}+\partial_zF_{u_z}-F_u=0.}
$$
For the specified squared-<gradient> energy this is $2\Delta u=0$, so $u$ is harmonic. The affine function $\ell=ax+by+cz$ is harmonic and satisfies the boundary data.

To verify a global minimum, write $u=\ell+v$ with zero boundary trace. Then
$$
I[u]=I[\ell]+2\int_V\nabla\ell\cdot\nabla v\,dV+\int_V|\nabla v|^2dV.
$$
The middle term vanishes by integration by parts, since $\Delta\ell=0$ and $v=0$ at the boundary. Hence $I[u]\geq I[\ell]$, and
$$
\boxed{\min I=(a^2+b^2+c^2)\operatorname{vol}V.}
$$
This proves minimality directly, rather than relying only on a necessary Euler-Lagrange equation.

The <Lagrangian> is $L=\sum_i m_i|\dot q_i|^2/2-f(r)$. Away from a collision, its equations of motion are
$$
m_1\ddot q_1=-\frac{f'(r)}r(q_1-q_2)=:F,\qquad
m_2\ddot q_2=-F,\qquad m_3\ddot q_3=0.
$$
Summing forces proves conservation of total <linear momentum>. The total <mechanical energy> $E=\sum_i m_i|\dot q_i|^2/2+f(r)$ is also conserved: its derivative is $F\cdot(\dot q_1-\dot q_2)+f'(r)\dot r=0$, because $\dot r=(q_1-q_2)\cdot(\dot q_1-\dot q_2)/r$.

For the first proposed <angular momentum>,
$$
\frac d{dt}(m_1q_1\times\dot q_1)=q_1\times F
=\frac{f'(r)}r q_1\times q_2,
$$
which need not vanish. Thus **particle one's <angular momentum> about the fixed origin is not generally conserved**. Its force is central relative to particle two, not necessarily relative to that origin.

The third particle is free, so
$$
\frac d{dt}(m_3q_3\times\dot q_3)=q_3\times m_3\ddot q_3=0.

Thus **particle three's <angular momentum> is conserved**.

For an observation point in the wire's plane, both $d\mathbf s$ and $\mathbf r-\mathbf s$ lie in that plane. Their cross product is normal to it, and the <Biot-Savart law> integrates only such normal vectors. Thus the <field> is perpendicular to the plane at every point off the wire.

At the centre, all four sides contribute in the same normal direction by the right-hand rule. A single side is distance $a$ from the centre, so its contribution is
$$
\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\int_{-a}^a\frac{a\,ds}{(a^2+s^2)^{3/2}}
=\frac{\mu_0I\sqrt2}{4\pi a}.
$$
Multiplying by four gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B_{\rm centre}=\frac{\mu_0I\sqrt2}{\pi a}\,\mathbf n,}
$$
where $\mathbf n$ is the oriented normal fixed by the current.

For incompressible planar flow define the <streamfunction> by $u=\psi_y$, $v=-\psi_x$. A <velocity potential> satisfies $u=\phi_x$, $v=\phi_y$ when the flow is irrotational; the differential condition is $v_x-u_y=0$. The <complex potential of a two-dimensional potential flow> is $w=\phi+i\psi$, with derivative $w'=u-iv$.

For $w=m\log(z-z_0)/(2\pi)$, its derivative is $m/[2\pi(z-z_0)]$. This gives outward radial velocity $m/(2\pi r)$, whose flux across a circle is $2\pi r\cdot m/(2\pi r)=m$. It is therefore the stated line source.

Superposition gives
$$
w(z)=Uz+\frac m{2\pi}[\log z-\log(z-1)],\qquad
w'(z)=U-\frac m{2\pi z(z-1)}.
$$
For positive $U,m$, the stagnation points are
$$
\boxed{z_\pm=\frac{1\pm\sqrt{1+2m/(\pi U)}}2.}
$$
They lie on the real axis, one upstream of the source and the other downstream of the sink. Streamlines are level sets of $\psi=Uy+(m/2\pi)[\arg z-\arg(z-1)]$. The dividing <streamline> passes through both stagnation points and forms a <Rankine oval> enclosing the source and sink; outside, streamlines tend to uniform horizontal flow.

Writing $z=re^{i\theta}$ gives $\phi=\Gamma\theta/(2\pi)$ and $\psi=-\Gamma\log r/(2\pi)$. Constant <streamfunction> means constant radius, so streamlines are circles. The tangential velocity is $v_\theta=\Gamma/(2\pi r)$, and its anticlockwise <circulation> is $\int_0^{2\pi}v_\theta r\,d\theta=\Gamma$.

Use an opposite vortex at $z=-1$ as the image of the vortex at one:
$$
\boxed{w(z)=-\frac{i\Gamma}{2\pi}[\log(z-1)-\log(z+1)].}
$$
On $z=iy$ the two distances are equal, so $\psi=0$ and the wall is a <streamline>. Its velocity follows from $w'=-i\Gamma/[\pi(z^2-1)]$: at the wall $u=0$ and $v=-\Gamma/[\pi(1+y^2)]$.

The <Bernoulli equation> gives $p-p_\infty=-\rho\Gamma^2/[2\pi^2(1+y^2)^2]$. Relative to the ambient pressure force, the fluid's excess force on the wall is $-\int(p-p_\infty)dy$ in the positive $x$ direction. Using the supplied integral,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F_{\rm vortex}=\frac{\rho\Gamma^2}{4\pi}\,\mathbf e_x\quad\text{per unit width}.}
$$
Thus the wall is attracted toward the fluid and vortex. Subtracting the uniform ambient pressure isolates the finite force due to the vortex.

For the continuous distribution in the question, $F$ maps the support monotonically from zero to one. For $0<t<1$, strict monotonicity gives
$$
\mathbb P(F(X)\leq t)=\mathbb P(X\leq F^{-1}(t))=t.
$$
Thus **$F(X)$ is uniform on $(0,1)$**, the <probability integral transform>.

The <order statistics> are the sample values sorted increasingly, $X_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq X_{(n)}$. Monotonicity of $F$ makes $F(X_{(i)})$ the $i$th <order statistic> of independent uniforms. Its density is $n!t^{i-1}(1-t)^{n-i}/[(i-1)!(n-i)!]$, giving
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E F(X_{(i)})=\frac{i}{n+1},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.}
$$
This follows either by beta integrals or by the equal expected lengths of the $n+1$ uniform spacings.

For the stated log-logistic cumulative distribution,
$$
\log\frac{F(x)}{1-F(x)}=(\beta-1)\log x-(\beta-1)\log\alpha.
$$
Therefore plot $\log[p_i/(1-p_i)]$ vertically against $\log X_{(i)}$ horizontally, with plotting positions $p_i=i/(n+1)$. A plausible model gives an approximately straight line, with **slope $\beta-1$ and intercept $-(\beta-1)\log\alpha$**. This specifies the axes explicitly, since reversing them would invert the slope.

For $0<r<1$, start at any $x\in\{0,\ldots,n\}$. Propose $Y$ uniformly from this same finite set and accept it with probability
$$
\boxed{\min\left\{1,\frac{\binom nY}{\binom nx}\left(\frac r{1-r}\right)^{Y-x}\right\}.}
$$
Otherwise retain $x$. The uniform proposal is symmetric, so this is the <Metropolis–Hastings algorithm> with target $\pi(\cdot\mid r)$. Detailed balance follows because accepted probability flow is proportional to $\min\{\pi(x),\pi(Y)\}$. The chain is irreducible and has positive self-transition probability, hence converges to that binomial equilibrium law. At the degenerate parameters zero or one, the target is simply a point mass and can be simulated as a constant chain.
