The right-hand side depends on $x+y$, suggesting the substitution $u=x+y$. Then $y'=u'-1$, so the <ordinary differential equation> becomes
$$
u'=2-\frac4u=\frac{2(u-2)}u.
$$
The original equation is undefined at $u=0$. If $u\ne2$, this is a <separable differential equation>:
$$
\frac{u}{u-2}\,du=2\,dx,\qquad
u+2\ln|u-2|=2x+C.
$$
Substituting back gives the implicit family
$$
\boxed{y-x+2\ln|x+y-2|=C,\qquad x+y\ne0,2.}
$$
Differentiating the implicit equation gives $y'-1+2(1+y')/(x+y-2)=0$, or $(x+y)y'=x+y-4$, verifying it on its permitted intervals.

Division by $u-2$ excluded the constant solution $u=2$, which must be restored separately:
$$
\boxed{y=2-x.}
$$
It satisfies $y'=-1=(2-4)/2$. Together these give all local real solutions on intervals where $x+y\ne0$.

\boxed{y(x)=x^2(1+\ln x),\qquad x>0.}

Let $Av=\lambda v$ with $v\ne0$, using the standard complex <Hermitian inner product>. Then
$$
v^\dagger Av=\lambda v^\dagger v.
$$
Taking the <complex conjugate> and using $A^\dagger=-A$ yields $\overline\lambda v^\dagger v=v^\dagger A^\dagger v=-\lambda v^\dagger v$. Since $v^\dagger v>0$,
$$
\boxed{\overline\lambda=-\lambda,\qquad \operatorname{Re}\lambda=0.}
$$
Thus every <eigenvalue> of an <anti-Hermitian matrix> lies on the imaginary axis, including the possible zero <eigenvalue>; each nonzero <eigenvalue> is purely imaginary.

Now take $Au=\lambda u$ and $Av=\mu v$. On one hand $u^\dagger Av=\mu u^\dagger v$. On the other hand
$$
u^\dagger Av=-(Au)^\dagger v=-\overline\lambda u^\dagger v=\lambda u^\dagger v.
$$
Consequently $(\mu-\lambda)u^\dagger v=0$. Distinct <eigenvalues> therefore imply
$$
\boxed{u^\dagger v=0,}
$$
which is the required orthogonality of the <eigenvectors>.

Apply the <vector triple product identity> $p\times(c\times d)=c(p\cdot d)-d(p\cdot c)$ with $p=a\times b$. This gives
$$
(a\times b)\times(c\times d)
=c\big((a\times b)\cdot d\big)-d\big((a\times b)\cdot c\big).
$$
For column <vectors> $a,b,d$, the <scalar triple product> is $(a\times b)\cdot d=\det(a,b,d)$; replacing $d$ by $c$ gives the other coefficient. Therefore
$$
\boxed{(a\times b)\times(c\times d)=\det(a,b,d)c-\det(a,b,c)d.}
$$
The signs follow the stated column convention and the cyclic invariance of the <scalar triple product>. No independence assumptions on the four <vectors> are needed.

To invert the complex <cosine>, let $z=\cos w$ and put $u=e^{iw}$. Since the <exponential function> is nonzero, $u\ne0$, and <Euler's formula> gives $2z=u+u^{-1}$. Multiplying by $u$ yields the <quadratic equation> $u^2-2zu+1=0$. Thus
$$
u=z\pm\sqrt{z^2-1}=z\pm i\sqrt{1-z^2}.
$$
Choosing either <square root> of $1-z^2$ merely exchanges the two signs. The two values have product one, so neither vanishes; taking a <complex logarithm> is legitimate.

For each choice of sign, all solutions of $e^{iw}=u$ satisfy $iw=\operatorname{Log}u+2\pi in$. Therefore the full inverse relation is
$$
\boxed{w=-i\operatorname{Log}\left(z\pm i\sqrt{1-z^2}\right)+2\pi n,\qquad n\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
Conversely, exponentiating any displayed value recovers one root of the <quadratic equation>, and hence $(e^{iw}+e^{-iw})/2=z$, proving completeness. With a multivalued <complex logarithm>, the $2\pi n$ term is already included and this is the source's compact formula for <inverse cosine>.

The principal <inverse cosine> requires compatible choices of <logarithm branch>, <square root> and sign. Both signs cannot represent that same principal function indiscriminately: at $z=0$, the two principal-log values are $\pi/2$ and $-\pi/2$, whereas the principal $\arccos0$ is $\pi/2$. Thus the printed $\pm$ is correct for the inverse relation, not a pair of interchangeable principal-value formulas.

Assume a physical <mass> $m>0$, <spring constant> $k>0$, and nonnegative viscous damping parameter $\gamma$. The homogeneous <damped harmonic oscillator> has <characteristic equation>
$$
r^2+\gamma r+\omega_0^2=0,\qquad\omega_0^2=\frac{k}{m}.
$$
Its roots are $r=-\gamma/2\pm\sqrt{\gamma^2/4-\omega_0^2}$. They have nonzero imaginary parts precisely when $\gamma^2<4k/m$. Defining $\Omega=\sqrt{k/m-\gamma^2/4}$, the real oscillatory solutions are
$$
\boxed{z_h(t)=e^{-\gamma t/2}\big(C\cos\Omega t+D\sin\Omega t\big),\qquad\gamma^2<\frac{4k}{m}.}
$$
For strictly decaying oscillations also require $\gamma>0$. The limiting $\gamma=0$ case is an undamped <harmonic oscillator>. Equality gives critical damping with $z_h=(C+Dt)e^{-\gamma t/2}$, and a larger positive $\gamma$ gives two real decaying exponentials rather than oscillations.

= Solution

Take $m,k,\gamma,F_0>0$ and $\omega>0$, and write $D_\omega=k-m\omega^2$ and $B_\omega=m\gamma\omega$. Substitution of $z=A\cos\omega t$ into the <forced harmonic oscillator> equation gives
$$
A D_\omega\cos\omega t-A B_\omega\sin\omega t
=F_0\cos\phi_0\cos\omega t-F_0\sin\phi_0\sin\omega t.
$$
Equating the independent <sine> and <cosine> coefficients yields
$$
\boxed{A D_\omega=F_0\cos\phi_0,\qquad A B_\omega=F_0\sin\phi_0.}
$$
Thus the required <phase matching for a displacement referenced driven oscillator> is
$$
\boxed{(k-m\omega^2)\sin\phi_0=m\gamma\omega\cos\phi_0.}
$$
For a positive <amplitude> $A$, one must also choose the correct quadrant: $\cos\phi_0=D_\omega/\sqrt{D_\omega^2+B_\omega^2}$ and $\sin\phi_0=B_\omega/\sqrt{D_\omega^2+B_\omega^2}$. Squaring and adding the coefficient equations gives
$$
\boxed{A=\frac{F_0}{\sqrt{(k-m\omega^2)^2+(m\gamma\omega)^2}}.}
$$
For arbitrary forcing <phase>, the steady response is instead $z=A\cos(\omega t+\phi_0-\delta)$, where $\delta$ has the sine and cosine just specified. A change of time origin can put this displacement into $A\cos\omega t$, but it also changes the stated forcing <phase>.

The source's requested parameter list for $A$ omits $\gamma$; in general it cannot be omitted. As a concrete counterexample, take $m=k=F_0=\omega=1$ and $\phi_0=\pi/2$. The prescribed cosine response exists for every $\gamma>0$, but $A=1/\gamma$, so fixing all four listed parameters does not fix the <amplitude>. Likewise, with fixed $\phi_0=0$, positive damping and nonzero $\omega$, the second coefficient equation prevents a nonzero pure-cosine response. The forcing <phase> is an essential part of the condition.

= Solution

In the steady periodic response, the cycle average of the <mechanical energy> derivative vanishes. The applied <average power> therefore equals the power dissipated by the viscous <force>:
$$
\overline P=m\gamma\langle\dot z^{\,2}\rangle=\frac12m\gamma A^2\omega^2.
$$
Using <10/b/solution> gives
$$
\boxed{\overline P(\omega)=\frac{F_0^2m\gamma\omega^2}{2\big[(k-m\omega^2)^2+(m\gamma\omega)^2\big]}.}
$$
The same result follows from averaging the instantaneous input <power> $F(t)\dot z(t)$, using the response <phase> relative to the drive.

For positive $m,k,\gamma$ and $\omega>0$, rewrite this as
$$
\overline P=\frac{F_0^2}{2m\gamma}\,
\frac{(m\gamma\omega)^2}{(k-m\omega^2)^2+(m\gamma\omega)^2}.
$$
The last factor is at most one, with equality precisely when $k-m\omega^2=0$. Thus the power peak occurs at $\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}$ and
$$
\boxed{\overline P_{\max}=\frac{F_0^2}{2m\gamma}.}
$$
This power-resonance <angular frequency> is exactly $\omega_0$; it need not equal the <angular frequency> maximizing displacement <amplitude>. Positive damping is essential: at $\gamma=0$ and exact resonance there is no bounded steady periodic response to nonzero forcing, so the divergent expression is not a finite-power solution.

= Solution

Use $\omega_0^2=k/m$ and the power formula from <10/c/solution>. Its normalized form is
$$
\frac{\overline P(\omega)}{\overline P_{\max}}
=\frac{\gamma^2\omega^2}{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+\gamma^2\omega^2}.
$$
It is zero at $\omega=0$, rises to one at $\omega_0$, and decays to zero as $\omega\to\infty$. The two positive half-power <angular frequencies> satisfy
$$
(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2=\gamma^2\omega^2.
$$
On the low-frequency side, $\omega_0^2-\omega_1^2=\gamma\omega_1$; on the high-frequency side, $\omega_2^2-\omega_0^2=\gamma\omega_2$. Solving these <quadratic equations> gives
$$
\boxed{\omega_1=\frac{\sqrt{4\omega_0^2+\gamma^2}-\gamma}{2},\qquad
\omega_2=\frac{\sqrt{4\omega_0^2+\gamma^2}+\gamma}{2}.}
$$
Consequently the <frequency bandwidth of viscous oscillator power absorption> obeys the stronger exact result
$$
\boxed{\omega_2-\omega_1=\gamma.}
$$
For a sharp resonance, $\gamma\ll\omega_0$, expansion gives $\omega_{1,2}=\omega_0\mp\gamma/2+O(\gamma^2/\omega_0)$, which recovers the requested approximation. The exact width above assumes the constant viscous damping coefficient in this model. Although the curve is approximately symmetric near a sharp peak, the exact half-power points satisfy $\omega_1\omega_2=\omega_0^2$ rather than exact symmetry about $\omega_0$.

For a finite <matrix>, the series defining the <matrix exponential> converges absolutely in any <matrix norm>, because $\|A^m/m!\|\leq\|A\|^m/m!$. Thus the <Hermitian adjoint> may be taken term by term. Since $A^\dagger=A$,
$$
(e^A)^\dagger=\sum_{m=0}^\infty\frac{(A^m)^\dagger}{m!}
=\sum_{m=0}^\infty\frac{(A^\dagger)^m}{m!}
=\sum_{m=0}^\infty\frac{A^m}{m!}=e^A.
$$
Therefore the <matrix exponential> of a <Hermitian matrix> is Hermitian.

If $A=\operatorname{diag}(a_1,\ldots,a_n)$ is a <diagonal matrix>, then $A^m=\operatorname{diag}(a_1^m,\ldots,a_n^m)$. Summing each entry separately gives
$$
\boxed{e^A=\operatorname{diag}(e^{a_1},\ldots,e^{a_n}),\qquad(e^A)_{ij}=\delta_{ij}e^{a_i}.}
$$
For the <Hermitian matrix> case each $a_i$ is real. The diagonal entry formula itself remains valid for complex diagonal entries.

For real orthonormal column <vectors>, $u^Tu=v^Tv=1$ and $u^Tv=v^Tu=0$. Expand using associativity of <matrix multiplication>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
(uu^T+vv^T)^2
&=u(u^Tu)u^T+u(u^Tv)v^T+v(v^Tu)u^T+v(v^Tv)v^T\\
&=uu^T+vv^T.
\end{aligned}
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{M=uu^T+vv^T\text{ is idempotent}.}
$$
More precisely, it is the <orthogonal projection> onto the span of $u,v$: it fixes both <vectors> and annihilates every <vector> orthogonal to both.

The transpose convention presumes real <vectors>, or the explicitly stated bilinear identities above. For complex <vectors> orthonormal in the <Hermitian inner product>, the correct <orthogonal projection> is $uu^\dagger+vv^\dagger$. The printed transpose formula need not work in that setting: $u=(i,0)^T$ and $v=(0,1)^T$ are Hermitian-orthonormal, but $uu^T+vv^T=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1)$ has square $I_2$, which is different from itself.

Induction from $M^2=M$ gives $M^j=M$ for every integer $j\geq1$. Separate the zeroth term of the <matrix exponential> series:
$$
\begin{aligned}
e^{\alpha M}
&=I+\sum_{j=1}^\infty\frac{\alpha^jM^j}{j!}
=I+M\sum_{j=1}^\infty\frac{\alpha^j}{j!}\\
&=I+(e^\alpha-1)M.
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore the <exponential of an idempotent linear map> is
$$
\boxed{e^{\alpha M}=I+(e^\alpha-1)M=(I-M)+e^\alpha M.}
$$
The last form shows its action directly: it multiplies the <image of a linear map> of $M$ by $e^\alpha$ and fixes its <kernel of a linear map>. No Hermiticity assumption on the <idempotent matrix> is needed.

b(a+d)=b,\qquad c(a+d)=c,
$$
or $b(\operatorname{tr}M-1)=c(\operatorname{tr}M-1)=0$. If both $b,c$ vanish, $M$ is diagonal; if either is nonzero, the <trace> must be one. Thus the <trace criterion for a two-by-two idempotent> is
$$
\boxed{M^2=M\quad\Longrightarrow\quad M\text{ is diagonal or }\operatorname{tr}M=1.}
$$
For the diagonal case, idempotence further forces $a,d\in\{0,1\}$. The stated alternative is only necessary: $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ has <trace> one but its square is $\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$, so it is not idempotent. Among two-by-two <matrices> of <trace> one, the additional condition $\det M=0$ is sufficient by the <Cayley-Hamilton theorem>, which gives $M^2-M+(\det M)I=0$.

The <linear system> has the <matrix> form
$$
\boxed{A\boldsymbol x=\boldsymbol b,\qquad
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&-3&-2\\1&-4&-2\\-3&4&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
\boldsymbol x=\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\x_3\end{pmatrix},\quad
\boldsymbol b=\begin{pmatrix}4\\2\\-1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The first equation gives $3x_2+2x_3=-4$. Substitute it into the second to obtain $x_1=2+4x_2+2x_3=x_2-2$. Substitution into the third gives $x_2+x_3=-7$. Consequently
$$
3x_2+2(-7-x_2)=-4\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x_2=10.
$$
It follows that
$$
\boxed{\boldsymbol x=(8,10,-17)^T.}
$$
Substitution verifies all three equations. Also $\det A=1\ne0$, so this <solution of a linear system> is unique.

The <characteristic polynomial> of $M$ is
$$
\det(\lambda I-M)=(\lambda-6)(\lambda-3)+2=(\lambda-4)(\lambda-5).
$$
The corresponding <eigenvectors> may be chosen as $v_4=(1,2)^T$ and $v_5=(1,1)^T$. They are linearly independent, so write $\boldsymbol x=q_4(t)v_4+q_5(t)v_5$. Substitution in the system of <ordinary differential equations> gives
$$
(q_4''+4q_4)v_4+(q_5''+5q_5)v_5=0.
$$
Independence of the <eigenvectors> yields two decoupled <harmonic oscillator equations>. Their four integration constants give the general real solution
$$
\boxed{\boldsymbol x(t)=
\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}(A\cos2t+B\sin2t)
+\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}(C\cos(\sqrt5t)+D\sin(\sqrt5t)).}
$$
Both components can be differentiated directly to verify $\boldsymbol x''+M\boldsymbol x=0$. Orthogonality of the <eigenvectors> is unnecessary: diagonalizing this nonsymmetric <matrix> requires an invertible <eigenbasis>, not an orthonormal one.

= Solution

In a real orthonormal <basis>, a <matrix> is a proper <rotation matrix> exactly when $R^TR=I$ and $\det R=1$. The first condition preserves <dot products>, and the second preserves <orientation of a vector space>.

Put $s=\sqrt3$, $p=(1+s)/3$, $q=(1-s)/3$ and $r=1/3$, so the rows of the given <matrix> are $(p,q,r),(r,p,q),(q,r,p)$. Their squared <Euclidean norms> and pairwise <dot products> are
$$
p^2+q^2+r^2=1,\qquad pq+qr+rp=0.
$$
Thus $R^TR=I$. For the sign of its <determinant>, use the circulant form:
$$
\det R=p^3+q^3+r^3-3pqr
=(p+q+r)(p^2+q^2+r^2-pq-qr-rp)=1.
$$
This proves that it is a <rotation matrix>. Its fixed axis is $(1,1,1)^T$, because each row sums to one. The <rotation trace formula> gives $\cos\theta=(\operatorname{tr}R-1)/2=\sqrt3/2$; the skew part selects a positive <angle> $\theta=\pi/6$ about the unit axis $(1,1,1)^T/\sqrt3$.

The original triangle has edge <vectors> $b-a=(-2,2,0)^T$ and $c-a=(-2,0,2)^T$. Its <area> is half the <Euclidean norm> of their <cross product>:
$$
\boxed{\mathcal A=\tfrac12\|(b-a)\times(c-a)\|=\tfrac12\|(4,4,4)^T\|=2\sqrt3.}
$$
The rotated vertices are twice the three columns of $R$:
$$
\boxed{a'=\frac23\begin{pmatrix}1+\sqrt3\\1\\1-\sqrt3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
b'=\frac23\begin{pmatrix}1-\sqrt3\\1+\sqrt3\\1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
c'=\frac23\begin{pmatrix}1\\1-\sqrt3\\1+\sqrt3\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
A <rotation matrix> preserves lengths and the <angle> between the two edge <vectors>, so it preserves their parallelogram <area>. More explicitly, $(Ru)\times(Rv)=R(u\times v)$ for a proper <rotation matrix>, and $R(1,1,1)^T=(1,1,1)^T$, so the rotated edge <cross product> is again $(4,4,4)^T$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathcal A'=2\sqrt3.}
$$
