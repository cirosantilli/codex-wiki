= Solution
{parent=1}

The <complex exponential> identity $\sin\theta=(e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta})/(2i)$ gives
$$
z=\frac{e^{2i\theta}-1}{2i},\qquad
\boxed{(\Re z)^2+(\Im z-\tfrac12)^2=\tfrac14.}
$$
Thus the <locus> is a <circle> of <radius> $1/2$ centered at $i/2$, traversed counterclockwise twice as $\theta$ runs through the specified interval. Its period as a parametrized <curve> is $\pi$. Near $\theta=0$, the <Taylor expansion> gives $\Re z=\theta+O(\theta^3)$ and $\Im z=\theta^2+O(\theta^4)$, so locally $\Im z=(\Re z)^2+O((\Re z)^4)$. There is **a smooth horizontal tangent, not a cusp**: $dz/d\theta=e^{2i\theta}$ is nonzero at each passage through the origin.

Using the <hyperbolic functions> identities $\sin(ix)=i\sinh x$ and $\cos(ix)=\cosh x$, divide by $i$ and put $u=e^x\ne0$. The equation becomes $-u/2-5/(2u)=3$, hence
$$
u^2+6u+5=(u+1)(u+5)=0.
$$
Taking all branches of the <complex logarithm> gives
$$
\boxed{x=(2k+1)\pi i\quad\text{or}\quad x=\ln5+(2k+1)\pi i,\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
If $x$ is required to be real, there are **no real solutions**, since $e^x$ cannot be negative. The complex branches are essential to the unrestricted answer.

In the <matrix product>, $(AB)_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^n a_{ik}b_{kj}$. A nonzero summand in a product of <upper triangular matrices> requires both $i\le k$ and $k\le j$. If $i>j$, no such index exists, so the product is upper triangular. If $i=j$, only $k=i$ survives, giving
$$
\boxed{(AB)_{ii}=a_{ii}b_{ii}.}
$$
This also shows inductively why the diagonal of an integer <matrix power> consists of the corresponding powers of the diagonal entries.

= Solution

For the <upper triangular matrix> $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\0&d\end{pmatrix}$, direct <matrix multiplication> gives
$$
A^3=\begin{pmatrix}a^3&b(a^2+ad+d^2)\\0&d^3\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The real diagonal entries are therefore $a=3,d=4$. Their off-diagonal multiplier is $9+12+16=37$, so $b=-74/37=-2$. A required matrix is
$$
\boxed{A=\begin{pmatrix}3&-2\\0&4\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
It is the unique real upper triangular cube root. Allowing complex diagonal cube roots produces other complex matrices; uniqueness is asserted only over the real numbers.

Subtracting twice the first equation from the second gives $(\lambda+2)z=0$. The third equation gives $x=1+\lambda y$, and substitution in the first then gives $z=(\lambda+1)y$. Consequently $(\lambda+2)(\lambda+1)y=0$. The complete real <solution set> of this <linear system> is
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c}
\lambda\notin\{-1,-2\}&(x,y,z)=(1,0,0)\\
\lambda=-1&(x,y,z)=(1-s,s,0),\quad s\in\mathbb R\\
\lambda=-2&(x,y,z)=(1-2s,s,-s),\quad s\in\mathbb R.
\end{array}}
$$
There is **no parameter value allowing both $x<0$ and $y<0$**. In either exceptional family, $y=s<0$ forces $x>0$; in the remaining case, $y=0$. The exceptional values must be handled before dividing by their factors.

The displayed <orthogonal matrix> transforms the coordinates of a fixed <vector>, rather than actively moving that vector. Write $\theta=\arctan(1/2)$. Then $P=R(-\theta)$, so the new unit basis vectors, expressed in the old <orthonormal basis>, are the columns of $P^T$:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf e'_1=\frac{(2,1)}{\sqrt5},\qquad
\mathbf e'_2=\frac{(-1,2)}{\sqrt5}.}
$$
The axes have rotated counterclockwise through $\theta$, while the coordinate column transforms by the opposite rotation. For the example point $\mathbf r=(1,0)$, its new <coordinates> are $(2,-1)/\sqrt5$. Reconstruction gives $(2\mathbf e'_1-\mathbf e'_2)/\sqrt5=(1,0)$, confirming that the point is unchanged. The middle panel of the figure in part <1> shows these axes and the signed projections. Since $P^TP=I$ and $\det P=1$, lengths and orientation of the basis are preserved.

Let $\mathbf u=(2,1)/\sqrt5$ be a unit <vector> along the fixed line. The <orthogonal projection> parallel to the line is $\mathbf u\mathbf u^T$; a <reflection> keeps that component and reverses its orthogonal complement. Therefore
$$
\boxed{A=2\mathbf u\mathbf u^T-I=\frac15\begin{pmatrix}3&4\\4&-3\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det A=-1.}
$$
Its <eigenvalues> are $+1$ along $(2,1)$ and $-1$ along $(-1,2)$. It preserves lengths and unsigned areas but reverses orientation. For example, $(1,0)$ maps to $(3/5,4/5)$, with their midpoint on the reflection line and their separation perpendicular to it; the right panel of the figure in <1> illustrates this.

= Solution
{parent=6}

Use the <characteristic polynomials of AB and BA>, which also account for the zero <eigenvalue> and <algebraic multiplicities>. The block <determinant>
$$
\det\begin{pmatrix}I&tA\\-B&I\end{pmatrix}
$$
equals both $\det(I+tAB)$ and $\det(I+tBA)$, by taking the two <Schur complements>. For $\lambda\ne0$, set $t=-1/\lambda$ and multiply by $\lambda^n$ to obtain
$$
\boxed{\det(\lambda I-AB)=\det(\lambda I-BA).}
$$
Both sides are <polynomials>, so agreement away from zero proves equality there as well. Their roots, with multiplicity, are the same. The familiar eigenvector argument $ABv=\mu v\Rightarrow BA(Bv)=\mu Bv$ works directly only when $\mu\ne0$, because then $Bv\ne0$; it should not be used to discard the zero case.

= Solution
{parent=7}

The two affine lines meet at $(a,b)=(2,1)$. Translating their intersection to the origin, $X=x-2$, $Y=y-1$, removes both constant terms and gives the <homogeneous first-order differential equation>
$$
\frac{dY}{dX}=\frac{4X+2Y}{2X-Y}.
$$
On a patch with $X\ne0$, put $Y=uX$. Then $X\,du/dX=(u^2+4)/(2-u)$, and <separation of variables> gives
$$
\arctan(u/2)-\frac12\ln(u^2+4)=\ln|X|+C.
$$
Thus one local real implicit form is
$$
\boxed{\arctan\!\frac{y-1}{2(x-2)}-\frac12\ln\!\bigl(4(x-2)^2+(y-1)^2\bigr)=C.}
$$
The <arctangent> branch must be continued on the chosen solution patch. More geometrically, set $r=\sqrt{4X^2+Y^2}$ and take a continuous local argument $\theta=\arg(2X+iY)$; then $\theta-\ln r=C$. This form extends through $X=0$ where the original slope is defined. The intersection itself is excluded because the original right-hand side is $0/0$ there, and points with $2X-Y=0$ do not define a finite derivative. No real constant-$u$ solution was lost, because $u^2+4$ has no real zero. Translating the intersection works precisely because the two affine linear forms then become homogeneous linear forms.

The <linear span> is $\{\lambda\mathbf a+\mu\mathbf b:\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb R\}$, a <vector subspace> of $\mathbb R^3$. Its <dimension> is **zero** if both vectors vanish, **one** if at least one is nonzero and they are <linearly dependent>, and **two** if they are <linearly independent>. The corresponding geometric sets are the origin, a line through the origin, and a plane through the origin.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Contract the <Levi-Civita symbol> identity $\varepsilon_{ijk}\varepsilon_{i\ell m}=\delta_{j\ell}\delta_{km}-\delta_{jm}\delta_{k\ell}$. Using <Einstein summation convention>,
$$
\begin{aligned}
|\mathbf a\times\mathbf b|^2
&=\varepsilon_{ijk}a_jb_k\varepsilon_{i\ell m}a_\ell b_m\\
&=(\delta_{j\ell}\delta_{km}-\delta_{jm}\delta_{k\ell})a_jb_ka_\ell b_m\\
&=|\mathbf a|^2|\mathbf b|^2-(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)^2.
\end{aligned}
$$
This <Lagrange identity for the cross product> includes zero and parallel vectors without a separate division by their norms.

The set is the <kernel> of the <linear map> $L(\mathbf r)=\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf r$. It contains zero, and <linearity> gives $L(\alpha\mathbf r+\beta\mathbf s)=0$ whenever $L(\mathbf r)=L(\mathbf s)=0$, proving closure under <linear combinations>. If $\mathbf v\ne0$, it is a plane through the origin; if $\mathbf v=0$, it is all of $\mathbb R^3$. **An arbitrary plane need not be a vector subspace**: for example $z=1$ omits the zero vector. An <affine subspace> and a vector subspace are not interchangeable.

The <dimension formula for a sum of subspaces> gives

\dim(\mathcal U+\mathcal V)+\dim(\mathcal U\cap\mathcal V)=4.

Since the sum lies in $\mathbb R^3$ and each plane has <dimension> two, the possibilities are

Taking <cross products> of the two independent spanning vectors gives plane <normal vectors> proportional to $(-4,1,1)$ and $(4,-6,-1)$. Their <cross product> is $(5,0,20)$, so the intersection is the line $s\mathbf d$, with $\mathbf d=(1,0,4)$. The closest point to $\mathbf p=(1,1,1)$ is its <orthogonal projection>,
$$
\mathbf p_*=\frac{\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf d}{\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf d}\mathbf d
=\left(\frac5{17},0,\frac{20}{17}\right).

The squared distance is $|\mathbf p|^2-(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf d)^2/|\mathbf d|^2=3-25/17$, giving

\boxed{d=\sqrt{\frac{26}{17}}.}

Both plane normals are orthogonal to $\mathbf d$, and $(\mathbf p-\mathbf p_*)\cdot\mathbf d=0$, checking the geometric constraints.

= Solution

The <matrix exponential> power series is absolutely convergent for a finite matrix, so taking the <conjugate transpose> term by term is valid. Since $M^\dagger=M$,
$$
U^\dagger=\left(\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{i^nM^n}{n!}\right)^\dagger
=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-i)^nM^n}{n!}=e^{-iM}.
$$
The absolute convergence also justifies the <Cauchy product>. In $e^{-iM}e^{iM}$, the coefficient of $M^n$ is proportional to $\sum_{k=0}^n(-1)^k\binom nk$, which is zero for $n>0$ and one for $n=0$, by the <binomial theorem>. Consequently $U^\dagger U=I$; the reversed product is also $I$. This proves that the exponential is <unitary> directly from the power series, without invoking diagonalization.

= Solution
{parent=b}

First check that the proposed space is closed under the stated operations: $|\alpha a_i+b_i|^2\le2\alpha^2a_i^2+2b_i^2$, and summing gives a finite result. For any finite $N$, the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives
$$
\sum_{i=1}^N|a_ib_i|
\le\left(\sum_{i=1}^Na_i^2\right)^{1/2}
\left(\sum_{i=1}^Nb_i^2\right)^{1/2}
\le\|A\|_2\|B\|_2.
$$
The left side is an increasing, bounded sequence of real numbers. Its <limit> exists, proving <absolute convergence> of $\sum_i a_ib_i$. This justifies termwise addition and scalar multiplication in the defining sum. Symmetry and <bilinearity> follow, while $\langle A,A\rangle=\sum_i a_i^2\ge0$, with equality only when every $a_i=0$. Thus it is a real <inner product> on the real <l2 sequence space>.

Work over the reals with $x\ne0$, and put $p=dy/dx$. The algebraic equation implies $p\ne0$ and
$$
y=\frac x2\left(p-\frac1p\right).
$$
Differentiate this identity and use $y'=p$; cancellation gives $dp/dx=p/x$. Hence $p=Cx$, with nonzero real $C$, and substitution gives the complete family
$$
\boxed{y=\frac C2x^2-\frac1{2C},\qquad C\ne0.}
$$
There is no extra real singular branch lost in the cancellation, since $p^2+1\ne0$. At a point $(x_0,y_0)$ with $x_0\ne0$, the permitted constants satisfy $x_0^2C^2-2y_0C-1=0$, giving
$$
C_\pm=\frac{y_0\pm\sqrt{x_0^2+y_0^2}}{x_0^2}.
$$
These are distinct, real and nonzero, so **exactly two integral curves pass through each such point**. This does not contradict an <Picard-Lindelöf theorem>: the implicit equation permits two different slopes and is not one specified explicit <first-order ordinary differential equation>. Each choice of the smooth slope branch separately defines a locally unique curve.

The two real slope roots of $p^2+fp-1=0$ are distinct because their discriminant is $f^2+4>0$. <Vieta's formulas> give $p_+p_-=-1$, so the tangent vectors $(1,p_+)$ and $(1,p_-)$ have zero <scalar product>. Whenever the coefficients define local solution curves, the two families are therefore <orthogonal trajectories>.

For the stated example at $(3,4)$, the slopes are $3$ and $-1/3$. In the family from part <a>, the constants are $1$ and $-1/9$, giving
$$
\boxed{y=\frac{x^2-1}{2},\qquad y=\frac92-\frac{x^2}{18}.}
$$
Both curves pass through $(3,4)$, and $3(-1/3)=-1$ verifies the right angle.

= Solution
{parent=a}

An <integrating factor> directly solves a linear first-order equation; a general second-order equation needs an additional reduction. There are several sufficient cases, with all divisions restricted to intervals where their denominators are nonzero.

If $A=0$ and $B\ne0$, divide by $B$ to obtain $y'+(C/B)y=D/B$. The integrating factor is $\mu=\exp(\int C/B\,dx)$ and
$$
y=\mu^{-1}\left(K+\int\mu D/B\,dx\right).
$$
If $C=0$ and $A\ne0$, put $u=y'$. Then $u'+(B/A)u=D/A$ is first order, with integrating factor $\nu=\exp(\int B/A\,dx)$; solve for $u$ and integrate once more for $y$.

A further reducible class has $C=B'-A''$, for then
$$
Ay''+By'+Cy=\frac d{dx}\bigl[Ay'+(B-A')y\bigr].
$$
One <integration> produces a first-order equation for $y$, whose integrating factor after division by $A$ is $\exp(\int(B-A')/A\,dx)$.

Multiplication can always put a regular second-order equation into self-adjoint form by choosing $w=\exp(\int(B-A')/A\,dx)$, since
$$
w(Ay''+By'+Cy)=(wAy')'+wCy.
$$
However, the remaining $wCy$ term generally prevents one integration from solving it. The <limitations of integrating factors for second-order equations> explain why this formal transformation alone is not a general solution method. These sufficient classes do not purport to classify every specially solvable coefficient choice.

Differentiate the constraint to obtain $z'=2t-x'-y'$ and eliminate $z'$ from the other equations. Adding the resulting equations gives $y'=t-x+y/2$, and substitution then gives $x'+x=2t$. Its <integrating factor> is $e^t$, so $x=2t-2+C_1e^{-t}$; the initial value fixes $C_1=4$.

The remaining equation is $y'-y/2=-t+2-4e^{-t}$. Its integrating factor is $e^{-t/2}$, giving $y=2t+(8/3)e^{-t}+C_2e^{t/2}$; the initial value fixes $C_2=0$. Finally use the constraint:
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
x(t)&=2t-2+4e^{-t},\\
y(t)&=2t+\frac83e^{-t},\\
z(t)&=t^2-4t+2-\frac{20}3e^{-t}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The <acceleration> is $(4e^{-t},(8/3)e^{-t},2-(20/3)e^{-t})$, which is not constant. Thus **a constant net force cannot maintain this motion for a particle of constant mass**, by <Newton's second law>. This conclusion concerns the net force in the stated inertial-coordinate interpretation; a time-dependent force can produce it.
