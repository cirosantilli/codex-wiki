== 1
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $z=x+iy$, with real $x,y$. Its <complex conjugate> is $z^*=x-iy$, so equating real and imaginary parts gives $4x=1$, $2y=1$. Thus the <complex plane> locus is the single point
$$
\boxed{z=\frac14+\frac i2.}
$$

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

The <modulus> obeys $|z|^4=4$, giving $|z|=\sqrt2$. The <complex argument> obeys $4\arg z=\pi/2$ modulo $2\pi$. The four distinct <complex roots> are therefore
$$
\boxed{z_k=\sqrt2\exp\left[i\left(\frac\pi8+\frac{k\pi}2\right)\right],\qquad k=0,1,2,3.}
$$
They form a square centred at the origin, on the circle of radius $\sqrt2$, with the first vertex at argument $\pi/8$.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

For $z=x+iy$, the equation becomes $\sqrt{(x-2)^2+y^2}=x$. Its right side must be nonnegative. Squaring then gives $y^2=4(x-1)$, whose points already have $x\ge1$, so no extraneous branch has been introduced:
$$
\boxed{x=1+\frac{y^2}{4}.}
$$
This is a <parabola> opening along the positive real axis, with vertex $(1,0)$, focus $(2,0)$ and directrix $x=0$. The original <modulus> equation equates distance from the focus with distance from that directrix.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

<De Moivre's theorem> states $(\cos x+i\sin x)^n=\cos(nx)+i\sin(nx)$ for integer $n$. For $n=3$, expanding the cube and taking its real part gives
$$
\cos(3x)=\cos^3x-3\cos x\sin^2x.
$$
Rearranging gives the required identity with
$$
\boxed{a=1,\qquad b=3.}
$$
No division by $\cos x$ is needed, so the proof also covers its zeros.

== 3
{scope}

=== Solution

Let $A_1,A_2,A_3$ denote the three matrices in their printed order. A <Hermitian matrix> satisfies $A^\dagger=A$, a <unitary matrix> satisfies $A^\dagger A=I$, and an <orthogonal matrix> is real with $A^TA=I$.

For the first matrix, $A_1^\dagger=A_1$ and $A_1^2=I$. It is therefore **Hermitian and unitary**. It is not real, and even allowing complex entries in the transpose test gives $A_1^TA_1=-I$, so it is not orthogonal.

The second matrix is $A_2=iI$. Thus $A_2^\dagger A_2=(-iI)(iI)=I$, whereas $A_2^\dagger=-A_2$ and $A_2^TA_2=-I$. It is **unitary only** among the listed classes.

The third matrix is real, but direct multiplication gives
$$
A_3^TA_3=\operatorname{diag}\left(1,\frac14,1\right).
$$
It is neither orthogonal nor unitary. Its unequal off-diagonal transpose entries also show $A_3^T\ne A_3$, so it is **none of the three**. The common prefactor $1/2$ applies to the central diagonal entry as well; replacing that entry by one would describe a different matrix.

== 4
{scope}

=== Solution

The matrix is real symmetric, so the <spectral theorem> guarantees an <orthonormal basis> of <eigenvectors>. Its characteristic determinant factors as
$$
\det(A-\lambda I)=-(\lambda+3)(\lambda-6)(\lambda-1).
$$
Solving the three corresponding equations for the <null space> gives the normalized <eigenvectors>
$$
\boxed{\lambda=6:\ \frac1{\sqrt5}\begin{pmatrix}2\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\lambda=-3:\ \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\lambda=1:\ \frac1{\sqrt5}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\2\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Their pairwise <inner products> vanish. With these vectors as columns,
$$
Q=\begin{pmatrix}2/\sqrt5&0&1/\sqrt5\\0&1&0\\-1/\sqrt5&0&2/\sqrt5\end{pmatrix},\qquad\boxed{Q^TAQ=\operatorname{diag}(6,-3,1).}
$$
Since $Q^TQ=I$, this also gives $A=Q\operatorname{diag}(6,-3,1)Q^T$.

== 5
{scope}

=== Solution

The <scalar triple product> is the <determinant> with the three vectors as columns. A cyclic permutation of three columns is even, while exchanging two columns reverses the determinant's sign. Consequently
$$
(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)\cdot\mathbf c=\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)=-\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf c\times\mathbf b).
$$
Equivalently, expand the first expression as $\epsilon_{ijk}a_i b_j c_k$ and interchange the last two indices, using the antisymmetry of the <Levi-Civita symbol>. This argument includes linearly dependent vectors, for which both sides vanish.

== 6
{scope}

=== Solution

Differentiating the logarithm gives
$$
\frac d{dt}\log(\sec t+\tan t)=\frac{\sec t\tan t+\sec^2t}{\sec t+\tan t}=\sec t.
$$
The real logarithm is valid where its argument is positive; $\log|\sec t+\tan t|$ gives the same derivative on the other nonsingular intervals.

For the <first-order linear differential equation>, an <integrating factor> on the interval containing $t=0$ is
$$
\mu(t)=\exp\left(\int3\tan t\,dt\right)=\sec^3t.
$$
Multiplying the equation by it yields $(x\sec^3t)'=\sec t$. Integration and $x(0)=2$ therefore give
$$
\boxed{x(t)=\cos^3t\left[2+\log(\sec t+\tan t)\right],\qquad-\frac\pi2<t<\frac\pi2.}
$$
The interval ends at singularities of the differential equation's coefficient $\tan t$; a finite limiting value of the expression would not remove those coefficient singularities.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

**No, as printed.** Each displayed vector has only two components, so neither is an element of the stated $\mathbb R^3$ without an additional embedding convention. Even if one inserts a zero third component, two vectors span at most a two-dimensional <linear subspace> and cannot form a <basis> of $\mathbb R^3$. This conclusion does not require guessing a missing third entry.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

**No.** Calling the three vectors $v_1,v_2,v_3$ in order, direct addition gives $v_3=v_1+v_2$. This nontrivial <linear dependence> prevents them from forming a <basis> of $\mathbb R^3$. The first two are not proportional, so the span has dimension two.

=== iv
{scope}

==== Solution

**Yes, over $\mathbb C$.** The column matrix is $\operatorname{diag}(1,1+i)$, with nonzero <determinant> $1+i$. Every $(z_1,z_2)\in\mathbb C^2$ has the unique expansion
$$
(z_1,z_2)=z_1(1,0)+\frac{z_2}{1+i}(0,1+i).
$$
The scalar field matters: the same space regarded as a real <vector space> has dimension four.

== 8
{scope}

=== Solution

If $Mv=\lambda v$ for a nonzero <eigenvector>, then $M^2=M$ implies $(\lambda^2-\lambda)v=0$, hence $\lambda\in\{0,1\}$. Conversely, the real-symmetric <spectral theorem> gives $M=Q\Lambda Q^T$ with orthogonal $Q$. If every diagonal entry of $\Lambda$ is zero or one, then $\Lambda^2=\Lambda$ and therefore $M^2=M$. Thus the necessary and sufficient condition is
$$
\boxed{\text{Every eigenvalue of }M\text{ is either }0\text{ or }1.}
$$
Such a symmetric <idempotent matrix> is the <orthogonal projection> onto its eigenvalue-one <eigenspace>, with its eigenvalue-zero <eigenspace> as the kernel. Diagonalizability is essential to the sufficiency argument from eigenvalues alone; symmetry supplies it here.

== 9
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Set $u=x+y$, so $u'=1+y'=1+u^2/2$. The new equation is a <separable differential equation>:
$$
\int\frac{du}{1+u^2/2}=x+C,\qquad\sqrt2\arctan\frac u{\sqrt2}=x+C.
$$
Absorbing the constant scaling into $C$, the real solutions are
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\sqrt2\tan\left(\frac x{\sqrt2}+C\right)-x.}
$$
Each solution is taken on a connected interval avoiding its tangent poles. No constant real solution for $u$ was lost by separation, because $1+u^2/2$ is strictly positive for real $u$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The equation requires $x\ne0$. Put $v=y^2$, giving $v'=2yy'$ and
$$
v'-\frac4xv=2x.
$$
An <integrating factor> is $x^{-4}$, so $(vx^{-4})'=2x^{-3}$. Integrating gives $vx^{-4}=C-x^{-2}$, hence
$$
\boxed{y^2=Cx^4-x^2,\qquad y=\pm\sqrt{Cx^4-x^2}.}
$$
Real differentiable branches require $Cx^4-x^2>0$, with one sign on each connected solution interval. At $y=0$, the original equation would demand $0=x$, contrary to $x\ne0$; the zeros of the radicand are therefore not regular solution points. In particular, $y\equiv0$ is not an additional solution.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For $b\ne-1$ and initially $y\ne0$, use the <Bernoulli power substitution> $v=y^{b+1}$. The equation becomes
$$
v'-a(b+1)v=(b+1)e^{-2x}.
$$
Write $\kappa=a(b+1)$. A <first-order linear differential equation> then gives
$$
\boxed{y^{b+1}=\begin{cases}Ce^{\kappa x}-\dfrac{b+1}{\kappa+2}e^{-2x},&\kappa\ne-2,\\e^{-2x}[C+(b+1)x],&\kappa=-2.\end{cases}}
$$
The second line is the resonant case of a <Bernoulli equation with exponential forcing>. Recover $y$ using real branches for which the original power $y^{-b}$ is defined; for example, unrestricted noninteger powers are unambiguous on $y>0$.

The exceptional exponent $b=-1$ makes the original equation linear: $y'=(a+e^{-2x})y$. Its solutions are
$$
\boxed{y=C\exp\left(ax-\frac12e^{-2x}\right).}
$$
Domain restrictions matter when the solution reaches zero. If $b<0$ and the real power at zero is defined, $y\equiv0$ is also a solution. For $-1<b<0$, <waiting-time solutions of a sublinear Bernoulli equation> may stay zero until an arbitrary $x_0$ and then become positive, with
$$
y^{b+1}=(b+1)e^{\kappa x}\int_{x_0}^{x}e^{-(\kappa+2)s}\,ds\quad(x>x_0),\qquad y=0\quad(x\le x_0).
$$
The joining derivative is zero because $1/(b+1)>1$. For $b=0$, the linear formula can pass through zero normally; for $b>0$, the original negative power excludes zero. These qualifications prevent dividing out a valid zero solution or inventing one where the equation is undefined.

== 10
{scope}

=== Solution

Dividing Newton's equation by the mass gives the stated model: $\ddot z$ is the acceleration, $\gamma\dot z$ represents viscous damping, $(k/m)z$ is the spring's restoring acceleration, and the right side is the applied driving force per unit mass. The complex notation is a convenience; the physical displacement is the real part. Put $\omega_0^2=k/m$. The homogeneous characteristic roots are
$$
s=-\frac\gamma2\pm\sqrt{\frac{\gamma^2}4-\omega_0^2}.
$$
For $m,k>0$ and damping $\gamma\ge0$, oscillations occur when $\gamma^2<4\omega_0^2$, the underdamped case of the <damped harmonic oscillator>.

For a steady response $z=Ze^{i\omega t}$, substitution gives
$$
Z=\frac{F_0/m}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2+i\gamma\omega}=re^{-i\phi}.
$$
Taking the <modulus> and phase gives
$$
\boxed{r=\frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+\gamma^2\omega^2}},\qquad\phi=\operatorname{atan2}(\gamma\omega,\omega_0^2-\omega^2).}
$$
The phase is between zero and $\pi$ for positive damping and frequency; an unqualified arctangent loses the correct quadrant above resonance.

With the real drive $F_0\cos\omega t$ and real response $r\cos(\omega t-\phi)$, the average driving <power> equals the dissipated viscous power:
$$
W_{\mathrm{av}}=m\gamma\langle\dot z^2\rangle=\frac12m\gamma r^2\omega^2=\frac{F_0^2\gamma\omega^2}{2m[(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+\gamma^2\omega^2]}.
$$
For $\gamma>0$, write this as
$$
\frac{W_{\mathrm{av}}}{F_0^2/(2m\gamma)}=\frac1{1+[(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)/(\gamma\omega)]^2}.
$$
It is largest when the squared term vanishes, giving
$$
\boxed{\omega=\omega_0,\qquad W_{\mathrm{av,max}}=\frac{F_0^2}{2m\gamma}.}
$$
This power peak is at $\omega_0$ even when the displacement-amplitude peak is shifted. The <frequency bandwidth of viscous oscillator power absorption> follows from the half-power condition $|\omega_0^2-\omega^2|=\gamma\omega$:
$$
\boxed{\omega_1=\frac{\sqrt{4\omega_0^2+\gamma^2}-\gamma}{2},\quad\omega_2=\frac{\sqrt{4\omega_0^2+\gamma^2}+\gamma}{2},\quad\omega_2-\omega_1=\gamma.}
$$
In fact the bandwidth identity is exact in this model. Sharp resonance additionally gives $\omega_{1,2}\simeq\omega_0\mp\gamma/2$. With zero damping at exact resonance, there is no bounded steady harmonic response to which these finite-power formulas apply.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Multiplication by each column of $B$ gives
$$
AB=B\operatorname{diag}(-1,2,1).
$$
Since $B$ is invertible, its columns are an <eigenbasis> and the <similarity transformation> gives
$$
\boxed{D=B^{-1}AB=\operatorname{diag}(-1,2,1),\qquad(d_1,d_2,d_3)=(-1,2,1).}
$$
There is an important source variant: the alternative scanned copy supplies an erroneous inverse with an extra factor $1/6$ and a different top-right entry. Calling that supplied matrix $C$, direct multiplication gives $(CB)_{22}=1/6$, so it cannot be $B^{-1}$. Inverting $B$ instead gives
$$
B^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&-2&2\\-2&2&2\\3&0&-3\end{pmatrix}.
$$
This is the inverse in the cleaner copy used here. Using the scanned erroneous matrix would make $CAB$ nondiagonal, so correcting the inverse is necessary before applying the stated diagonalization. The <inverse-matrix verification test> catches the error without assuming the claimed outcome.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

A power of a <diagonal matrix> acts entrywise. Thus
$$
\boxed{D^n=\operatorname{diag}((-1)^n,2^n,1).}
$$
From $A=BDB^{-1}$, adjacent $B^{-1}B$ factors cancel in repeated multiplication, giving
$$
\boxed{A^n=BD^nB^{-1}.}
$$
This is the <matrix power> rule for a <similarity transformation>, proved directly rather than by taking powers of separate noncommuting factors.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

All three <eigenvalues> are nonzero, so $D^{-1}=\operatorname{diag}(-1,1/2,1)$. The <inverse matrix> is therefore
$$
\boxed{A^{-1}=BD^{-1}B^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}4&3&-5\\1/2&1/2&-1/2\\3&3&-4\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Multiplying this matrix by $A$ gives $I$, independently checking both the sign and the fractions.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The <determinant> is multiplicative and unchanged by a <similarity transformation>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\det(A^n)=\det(B)\det(D^n)\det(B^{-1})=[(-1)\,2\,1]^n=(-2)^n.}
$$
For $n=1$, expansion of the original determinant gives
$$
\det A=(-2-6)-6(1)+(-2)(-6)=-8-6+12=-2,
$$
in agreement with the product of its <eigenvalues>.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For a positive integer $n$, the identity $\sin^2(nx)=[1-\cos(2nx)]/2$ gives
$$
\boxed{\langle f_n,f_n\rangle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sin^2(nx)\,dx=\pi,\qquad n\ge1.}
$$
The stated nonnegative indexing includes $n=0$ as a special case: $f_0\equiv0$, whose squared <norm> is zero. Thus $\langle f_0,f_0\rangle=0$; it cannot belong to a <basis>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For distinct positive integers $m,n$, the product-to-sum identity gives
$$
\sin(nx)\sin(mx)=\frac12[\cos((n-m)x)-\cos((n+m)x)].
$$
Both cosine integrals vanish over $[-\pi,\pi]$, so
$$
\boxed{\langle f_n,f_m\rangle=0\quad(m\ne n).}
$$
If one index is zero, the corresponding function is identically zero and the same answer follows immediately.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

After discarding $f_0=0$, the preceding <inner products> show that distinct sine functions are <orthogonal>; dividing each by $\sqrt\pi$ makes them <orthonormal>. More precisely, their infinite expansions form an <orthogonal basis> of the odd part of $L^2(-\pi,\pi)$ in the Hilbert-space sense. This is not a claim that every arbitrary odd function is a finite <linear combination> of sines.

Take the <inner product> of $x=\sum_{n\ge1}a_n\sin(nx)$ with $\sin(mx)$. Orthogonality gives
$$
a_m=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}x\sin(mx)\,dx=\frac2\pi\left[-\frac{x\cos(mx)}m+\frac{\sin(mx)}{m^2}\right]_0^\pi=\frac{2(-1)^{m+1}}m.
$$
Therefore the <Fourier series of a periodic sawtooth>, scaled to this interval, is
$$
\boxed{x=2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}n\sin(nx),\qquad-\pi<x<\pi.}
$$
Its periodic extension is piecewise smooth, so the <Dirichlet convergence theorem for Fourier series> gives pointwise convergence to $x$ inside the interval. At the jump endpoints of that periodic extension, the series instead converges to zero, the midpoint of the two one-sided limits.

{title=Fourier sine partial sums approximate the identity inside the interval, while their periodic extension has endpoint jumps}

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The function $x^2-1$ is an <even function>, while every sine basis function is an <odd function>. Their product is odd, so every sine coefficient is zero:
$$
\langle x^2-1,\sin(nx)\rangle=0.
$$
The function itself is not zero, and therefore **it cannot be represented by this sine basis**. The basis spans the odd subspace, whereas the requested nonzero even function lies in its <orthogonal complement>. Equivalently, any pointwise limit of odd partial sums remains odd wherever both reflected points have limits.
