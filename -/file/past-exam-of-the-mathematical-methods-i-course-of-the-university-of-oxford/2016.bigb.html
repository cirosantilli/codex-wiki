=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

The nonnegative <modulus> $r=|z|$ must be a zero of the <sine> function. The restriction $r<10$ permits $r=0,\pi,2\pi,3\pi$, since $3\pi<10<4\pi$. Thus the <locus> is **the origin and three concentric circles**, centred at the origin with radii $\pi$, $2\pi$ and $3\pi$. A circle of radius zero is just one point.

{title=Complex-plane loci: sine of the modulus vanishes on three circles and the origin; real logarithm part fixes one circle}

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

For any value of the <complex logarithm>, $\log z=\ln|z|+i\arg z$ up to adding an integer multiple of $2\pi i$. Its <real part> therefore satisfies
$$
\operatorname{Re}\log z=2\iff\ln|z|=2\iff\boxed{|z|=e^2.}
$$
The <locus> is **the circle centred at the origin with radius $e^2$**. Different logarithm values change only the imaginary part. If a particular analytic <branch of the complex logarithm> with a cut is imposed, restrict the circle to that branch's domain; the modulus equation itself is branch independent.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

The <complex sine> identity is
$$
\sin(x+iy)=\sin x\cosh y+i\cos x\sinh y.
$$
It follows either from the exponential definition of <sine> or its addition formula, using $\sin(iy)=i\sinh y$ and $\cos(iy)=\cosh y$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Re}\sin(2+3i)=\sin2\cosh3,\qquad \operatorname{Im}\sin(2+3i)=\cos2\sinh3.}
$$
The angles $2$ and $3$ in these functions are dimensionless; the real trigonometric argument is measured in radians.

== 3
{scope}

=== Solution

The scalar product $\mathbf r\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}=d$ fixes the component of the <position vector> along the given <unit normal vector>. Thus the <locus> is **a plane normal to $\hat{\mathbf n}$**, through $d\hat{\mathbf n}$. Its signed distance from the origin along that normal is $d$, and its perpendicular <distance> from the origin is $|d|$. Any <displacement> lying within the plane is <orthogonal> to $\hat{\mathbf n}$.

== 4
{scope}

=== Solution

Noncoplanarity makes the <scalar triple product> $\Delta$ nonzero, so the three displayed vectors form a <reciprocal basis>. Their scalar products obey
$$
\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf a=\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf b=\mathbf C\cdot\mathbf c=1,
$$
with all six cross-pairings zero. Therefore, assuming the nonzero $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ needed to define the three given points,
$$
\mathbf X\cdot\frac{\mathbf a}{\alpha}=\mathbf X\cdot\frac{\mathbf b}{\beta}=\mathbf X\cdot\frac{\mathbf c}{\gamma}=1.
$$
Every difference of two of these points is <orthogonal> to $\mathbf X$. The two independent differences span their <plane>, so **$\mathbf X$ is a normal to the plane**, whose equation is $\mathbf X\cdot\mathbf r=1$. Also $\mathbf X\ne0$, because its scalar product with each specified point is $1$.

== 5
{scope}

=== Solution

The equations are an <eigenvalue problem> for the <matrix>
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}2&-2&1\\2&-3&2\\-1&2&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
A nonzero solution requires $\det(\lambda I-A)=0$. Expanding gives
$$
\det(\lambda I-A)=(\lambda-1)^2(\lambda+3),\qquad \boxed{\lambda=1\ \text{or}\ -3.}
$$
<Row reduction> gives the complete <eigenspaces>:
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\lambda=1:&\quad (x,y,z)=s(2,1,0)+t(-1,0,1),\qquad s,t\in\mathbb R,\\
\lambda=-3:&\quad (x,y,z)=u(-1,-2,1),\qquad u\in\mathbb R.
\end{aligned}}
$$
For $\lambda=1$ the solutions form the <plane> $x-2y+z=0$ through the origin; for $\lambda=-3$ they form a <line> through the origin. Exclude the zero vector when requiring a nontrivial solution. The repeated <eigenvalue> $1$ has a two-dimensional <eigenspace>; there are three independent <eigenvectors> in total. This matrix is not symmetric, so its different <eigenspaces> need not be mutually <orthogonal>.

== 6
{scope}

=== Solution

Use direction $\mathbf u=(-3,-2,-1)$ and displacement $\mathbf w=(1,2,3)-(6,5,4)=(-5,-3,-1)$. The <point-to-line distance> is the height of the <parallelogram> spanned by these vectors:
$$
\boxed{D=\frac{|\mathbf w\times\mathbf u|}{|\mathbf u|}=\frac{|(1,-2,1)|}{\sqrt{14}}=\frac{\sqrt{21}}7.}
$$
Equivalently the closest line parameter is $t=(\mathbf w\cdot\mathbf u)/|\mathbf u|^2=11/7$, giving the foot $(9/7,13/7,17/7)$. Its separation from the point is <orthogonal> to $\mathbf u$ and has the same length.

== 7
{scope}

=== Solution

Measure the car's vertical <displacement> $y$ from its static <equilibrium>, so gravity's constant contribution is already balanced. Both the spring and the <viscous damper> act on motion relative to the wheel. The <base-excited oscillator> therefore satisfies
$$
M\ddot y=-k(y-y_0)-\gamma(\dot y-\dot y_0).
$$
For dimensional clarity write the road profile as $B\sin(qx)$; the printed notation sets the implicit wave number $q=1$ in the chosen length units. Constant forward speed gives forcing <angular frequency> $\omega=qv$. With complex amplitudes $Y,Y_0$, the steady response is
$$
\frac Y{Y_0}=\frac{k+i\gamma\omega}{k-M\omega^2+i\gamma\omega},\qquad
\boxed{A(v)=|B|\sqrt{\frac{k^2+\gamma^2q^2v^2}{(k-Mq^2v^2)^2+\gamma^2q^2v^2}}.}
$$
Set $q=1$ for the expression in the question's notation. Assuming $M,k>0$ and $\gamma>0$, the <transient response> decays, leaving this <steady-state response>.

For a useful sketch define $\omega_n=\sqrt{k/M}$, $r=\omega/\omega_n$ and <damping ratio> $\zeta=\gamma/(2\sqrt{kM})$. The displacement <transmissibility> is
$$
\frac A{|B|}=\sqrt{\frac{1+4\zeta^2r^2}{(1-r^2)^2+4\zeta^2r^2}}.
$$
It starts at $1$, rises to a finite maximum for finite positive damping, crosses $1$ at $r=\sqrt2$, then tends to zero. Differentiation with respect to $r^2$ gives the maximum at $r^2=2/[1+\sqrt{1+8\zeta^2}]$.

**Very small damping permits large resonant oscillations** near $\omega_n$, producing discomfort and potentially large suspension travel. At exactly zero damping the assumed decay of transients fails, and resonant forcing produces secular growth. **Very large damping makes the car follow the bumps** over a wide range of speeds, sacrificing vibration isolation: at each fixed forcing frequency, $Y/Y_0\to1$ as $\gamma\to\infty$. For any fixed finite damping the response still eventually decreases at sufficiently high frequency. These two limits should not be interchanged.

== 8
{scope}

=== Solution

Split the mixed quadratic terms evenly across a <real symmetric matrix>:
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&-2&-2\\-2&-4&2\\-2&2&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad Q_1=\mathbf r^TA\mathbf r.
$$
Its <characteristic polynomial> is
$$
\det(\lambda I-A)=(\lambda+1)(\lambda+5)(\lambda-4)=\lambda^3+2\lambda^2-19\lambda-20,
$$
so **$c=2$**. Choose the orthonormal <eigenvectors>
$$
u_1=\frac{(1,0,1)^T}{\sqrt2},\quad u_2=\frac{(-1,-4,1)^T}{3\sqrt2},\quad u_3=\frac{(-2,1,2)^T}{3},\qquad P=(u_1\ u_2\ u_3).
$$
The <orthogonal transformation> $\mathbf r=P\mathbf R$ gives
$$
\boxed{Q_1=-X^2-5Y^2+4Z^2,\qquad Q_2=X^2+Y^2+Z^2,\qquad\beta=-5,\quad\gamma=4.}
$$
Indeed $P^TP=I$ and $P^TAP=\operatorname{diag}(-1,-5,4)$; preserving $Q_2$ is precisely the length-preserving property of an <orthogonal matrix>.

The requested plane has normal $(2,1,-2)$. An <orthonormal basis> within it is
$$
e_1=\frac{(1,0,1)}{\sqrt2},\qquad e_2=\frac{(1,-4,-1)}{3\sqrt2}.
$$
Write a point of that plane as $\mathbf r=ue_1+we_2$. Restricting the <quadratic form> gives $Q_1=-u^2-(13/9)w^2$, so its section with $Q_1=-5$ is
$$
\boxed{\frac{u^2}{5}+\frac{w^2}{45/13}=1.}
$$
It is an <ellipse> centred at the origin, with <semi-axes> $\sqrt5$ along $e_1$ and $\sqrt{45/13}$ along $e_2$. Normalizing the in-plane basis is essential: coefficients in nonorthonormal coordinates would not directly give physical lengths.

The complete surface is the <one-sheet hyperboloid>
$$
\frac{X^2}{5}+Y^2-\frac{Z^2}{5/4}=1.
$$
It is connected, extends indefinitely in both directions along its axis $u_3$, and has an elliptical waist at $Z=0$ with <semi-axes> $\sqrt5$ and $1$. At larger $|Z|$ its transverse sections widen; its asymptotic <cone> is $4Z^2=X^2+5Y^2$. The specified plane is oblique to that axis, rather than the waist plane.

== 9
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For $x\ne0$, the <integrating factor> for $y'-y/x=3$ can be taken as $1/x$ on either half-line. Thus $(y/x)'=3/x$, giving
$$
\boxed{y=x\bigl(C+3\ln|x|\bigr).}
$$
The arbitrary constant may differ on the disconnected domains $x>0$ and $x<0$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The initial point lies in $x>1$, where division by $\ln x$ is valid. The <first-order linear differential equation>
$$
y'+\frac{2}{x\ln x}y=\frac1{\ln x}
$$
has <integrating factor> $(\ln x)^2$. Hence
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[y(\ln x)^2\bigr]=\ln x,\qquad y(\ln x)^2=x\ln x-x+C.
$$
The condition at $x=e$ sets $C=0$, so
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac{x(\ln x-1)}{(\ln x)^2},\qquad x>1.}
$$
The solution cannot be continued regularly through $x=1$: its numerator tends to $-1$ while the denominator tends to zero. The real logarithm also requires $x>0$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The <characteristic equation> of the homogeneous <ordinary differential equation> is $r^2-2r+5=0$, with roots $1\pm2i$. Therefore $y_h=e^x(C\cos2x+D\sin2x)$. For the forcing term, try the <particular solution> $y_p=e^{-x}(ax+b)$. Substitution gives $8a=1$, $8b-4a=0$, so $a=1/8$, $b=1/16$. The two initial conditions give $C=-1/16$ and $D=0$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac{(2x+1)e^{-x}-e^x\cos2x}{16}.}
$$
Both the value and the first derivative vanish at zero; direct differentiation verifies the inhomogeneous equation.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For the <Cauchy-Euler equation> $x^2y''-xy'+y=0$, the power trial $y=x^r$ on $x>0$ gives $(r-1)^2=0$. The repeated root supplies the logarithmic second solution. On either half-line,
$$
\boxed{y=x\bigl(C_1+C_2\ln|x|\bigr),\qquad x\ne0.}
$$
One may also obtain this by setting $y=xu$, which reduces the equation to $(xu')'=0$. Constants on opposite sides of the singular point need not agree.

== 10
{scope}

=== Solution

Expanding $R\sin(x+\phi)$ and equating coefficients gives $R\sin\phi=A$, $R\cos\phi=B$. Thus
$$
\boxed{R=\sqrt{A^2+B^2},\qquad \phi=\operatorname{atan2}(A,B)\pmod{2\pi}.}
$$
The two-argument <arctangent> fixes the quadrant; if $R=0$, the phase is arbitrary.

For the coupled <ordinary differential equations>, set $z=fg$. The first equation becomes $\dot z=-3z$, and the initial values give $z=2e^{-3t}$. The second then reduces to $f''+3f=12e^{-3t}$. Its <initial value problem> has solution
$$
\boxed{f(t)=e^{-3t}+\cos(\sqrt3t)+\sqrt3\sin(\sqrt3t)=e^{-3t}+2\cos\left(\sqrt3t-\frac\pi3\right).}
$$
Where $f\ne0$, reconstruct $g(t)=2e^{-3t}/f(t)$. The scalar function $f$ is smooth for every real $t$, but the original coupled system cannot have a finite $g$ at its first zero, about $t=1.5146$. The requested sketch up to $t=3$ can display the analytic continuation of $f$, with that distinction marked. Initially $f=2$, $f'=0$ and $f''=6$; it first rises, reaches a maximum, then decreases through zero and becomes negative within the plotted interval.

\Image[2016-coupled-ode-determinant.png]
{title=The scalar solution and matrix determinant, with the first regular rotation time and the later pole in the reconstructed variable}

For the <determinant> of $M$, separate its upper-left block from the constant last diagonal entry. Using $fg=2e^{-3t}$ gives
$$
\det M=\frac34+\frac{f(2-g)}{16}=\frac34+\frac{\cos(\sqrt3t)+\sqrt3\sin(\sqrt3t)}8.
$$
Thus $\det M=1$ precisely when $2\cos(\sqrt3t-\pi/3)=2$. The smallest positive solution is
$$
\boxed{t_*=\frac\pi{3\sqrt3}.}
$$
It occurs before the zero of $f$, so $g$ and $M$ are finite there. Put $f_*=2+e^{-\pi/\sqrt3}$. At this time the upper block is
$$
\begin{pmatrix}f_*/(2a)&-\sqrt3/2\\\sqrt3/2&a/(2f_*)\end{pmatrix}.
$$
For a <rotation matrix>, each column must have unit norm, giving $a^2=f_*^2$. Both signs are allowed by the stated assumption that $a$ is a constant:
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c}
a&\text{axis}&\text{angle about positive }z\\\hline
2+e^{-\pi/\sqrt3}&z&\pi/3\\
-(2+e^{-\pi/\sqrt3})&z&2\pi/3
\end{array}}
$$
Both have <determinant> $1$ and preserve lengths. If a positive $a$ is intended, only the first row remains; positivity is not stated in the question.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The <transpose of a matrix product> reverses factor order:
$$
\boxed{(AB)^T=B^TA^T.}
$$
For the requested two-dimensional check, put $A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ and $B=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}e&f\\g&h\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. Both sides are
$$
\begin{pmatrix}ae+bg&ce+dg\\af+bh&cf+dh\end{pmatrix}.
$$
More generally, the entry formula $(AB)_{ji}=\sum_kA_{jk}B_{ki}$ is exactly $(B^TA^T)_{ij}$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <conjugate transpose> also reverses factor order. If $U$ and $V$ are <unitary matrices>,
$$
(UV)^\dagger(UV)=V^\dagger U^\dagger UV=V^\dagger IV=I.
$$
Thus their product is <unitary>. This is closure under multiplication in the <unitary group>.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

For a <Hermitian matrix> $H$, $H^\dagger=H$. Reversing the product of $n$ identical factors has no effect, so
$$
(H^n)^\dagger=(H^\dagger)^n=H^n.
$$
Hence every positive integer power is <Hermitian>. The identical-factor condition matters; a product of different <Hermitian matrices> need not be <Hermitian>.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

If $A$ and $B$ are <Hermitian matrices>, then $(AB)^\dagger=B^\dagger A^\dagger=BA$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{AB\text{ is Hermitian}\iff AB=BA\iff[A,B]=0.}
$$
The required condition is that the matrices <commute>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

At $t=0$ the <power series> gives $\mathbf v(0)=\mathbf v_0$. Equate the coefficient of $t^n$ after differentiating the series and substituting into the <linear system of ordinary differential equations>:
$$
(n+1)\mathbf v_{n+1}=-iH\mathbf v_n.
$$
<Mathematical induction> yields $\mathbf v_n=(-iH)^n\mathbf v_0/n!$. Summing gives the <matrix exponential> solution $\mathbf v(t)=e^{-iHt}\mathbf v_0$.

For the specified diagonal <Hermitian matrix>, its powers and exponential act separately on the two components. Thus
$$
\boxed{U(t)=e^{-iHt}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{-it}&0\\0&e^{it}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf v(t)=U(t)\mathbf v_0.}
$$
This <unitary matrix> preserves $\mathbf v^\dagger\mathbf v$: the two components acquire opposite phases of unit <modulus>. The coefficient recurrence applies to any constant finite square matrix $H$; unitarity here additionally uses its <Hermitian> property.
