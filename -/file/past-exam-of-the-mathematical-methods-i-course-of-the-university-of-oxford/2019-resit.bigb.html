Since $(1-i)^3=-2-2i$, divide and multiply by the <complex conjugate> of the denominator:
$$
\boxed{\frac{i}{(1-i)^3}=\frac{i(-2+2i)}8=-\frac14-\frac14i.}

= Solution

A <complex power> requires a choice of <complex logarithm>. Taking the <principal argument> of $-e$ to be $+\pi$ gives $\operatorname{Log}(-e)=1+i\pi$, hence
$$
\boxed{(-e)^{i\pi/2}=\exp\left(\frac{i\pi}{2}(1+i\pi)\right)=i e^{-\pi^2/2}.}
$$
Thus $a=0$ and $b=e^{-\pi^2/2}$ for this principal value. If the power is interpreted as multivalued, the logarithms are $1+i(2k+1)\pi$ and the possible values are $i\exp[-(2k+1)\pi^2/2]$, $k\in\mathbb Z$. Applying an identity for real powers without specifying this branch would lose the ambiguity.

Here $i^3=-i$, so the <argument> condition concerns $z+i$. Writing $z=x+iy$, the <complex number> $x+i(y+1)$ must point along the direction $1+i$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{z=-i+s(1+i),\quad s>0,\qquad\text{equivalently }y=x-1,\ x>0.}
$$
This is an <open ray> in the <Argand plane>, rather than the whole straight <line>. Its endpoint $z=-i$ is excluded because the <argument> of zero is undefined; the opposite half-line has a different <argument>.

To solve $A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$, subtract the first row from the second, and twice the first from the third. The resulting equations give $y=b_2-b_1$, $y+z=b_3-2b_1$, and hence $z=b_3-b_1-b_2$. Substituting in the original first equation gives $x=7b_1-3b_3$. Thus the <inverse matrix> is
$$
\boxed{A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}7&0&-3\\-1&1&0\\-1&-1&1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The <determinant> is one, and multiplying in either order gives the <identity matrix>.

= Solution
{parent=4}

Use the <Leibniz formula for determinants>:
$$
\det M=\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{j=1}^n m_{j,\sigma(j)}.
$$
For a nonzero product in an <upper triangular matrix>, every selected column must satisfy $\sigma(j)\geq j$. But a <permutation> has $\sum_j\sigma(j)=\sum_jj$, so every one of these inequalities is an equality. Only the identity <permutation> can contribute. Its sign is positive and its product is $\prod_jm_{jj}$, which proves the result even if some diagonal entries vanish.

The common ratio supplies a <line> parameter $s$:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf r=\mathbf p+s\mathbf v,\qquad\mathbf p=(1,1,1),\quad\mathbf v=(2,3,4).}
$$
The nearest point is the <orthogonal projection> of the origin onto the <line>. Minimize $|\mathbf p+s\mathbf v|^2$: its <derivative> is $2\mathbf v\cdot(\mathbf p+s\mathbf v)$, so $s_*=-9/29$ and the foot is $(11,2,-7)/29$. Thus
$$
\boxed{d=\sqrt{\frac6{29}}.}
$$
The <cross product> formula $d=|\mathbf p\times\mathbf v|/|\mathbf v|$ agrees, since $\mathbf p\times\mathbf v=(1,-2,1)$.

= Solution

The last equation gives $x=1/2-y$, and the first then gives $y+3z=3/2$. Together with the middle equation this reduces the consistency question to $(1-3\alpha)z=\beta-3\alpha/2$.

If $\alpha\ne1/3$, the <linear system> has the unique solution
$$
\boxed{(x,y,z)=\frac{(6\beta-3\alpha-2,\ 3-6\beta,\ 2\beta-3\alpha)}{2(1-3\alpha)}.}
$$
If $\alpha=1/3$ and $\beta=1/2$, the middle equation repeats one third of $y+3z=3/2$, giving the <affine line> of solutions
$$
\boxed{(x,y,z)=(-1+3s,\ 3/2-3s,\ s),\qquad s\in\mathbb R.}
$$
If $\alpha=1/3$ and $\beta\ne1/2$, the two equations contradict each other and **there is no solution**. These are three possible types of solution set, rather than three individual solutions for one fixed choice of parameters.

= Solution
{parent=7}

Work on an interval with $x\ne0$ and $\cos(y/x)\ne0$, as required by the original equation. Set $v=y/x$, so $y'=v+xv'$, and obtain $xv'=3\tan v$. Where $\sin v\ne0$, <separation of variables> gives $\cot v\,dv=3\,dx/x$ and therefore
$$
\boxed{\sin(y/x)=Cx^3.}
$$
The solutions lost by division by $\tan v$ are $v=k\pi$, and they are included by $C=0$. On a fixed local branch one can write
$$
y=x\left[k\pi+(-1)^k\arcsin(Cx^3)\right],\qquad |Cx^3|<1.
$$
The integer $k$ is fixed along a connected regular branch. Implicit <differentiation> recovers the original equation whenever $x\cos(y/x)\ne0$. The boundary $|Cx^3|=1$ is excluded because the original <tangent function> has a pole there.

= Solution
{parent=8}

Eliminate $y$ using $y=1-x'$ to get $x''+x=-1$. The initial data give $x(0)=1$ and $x'(0)=0$, so the <simple harmonic motion> about the equilibrium $x=-1$ is
$$
\boxed{x(t)=-1+2\cos t,\qquad y(t)=1+2\sin t.}
$$
Indeed, $x'+y=1$ and $y'-x=1$ identically, and both initial coordinates equal one. This also describes a <circle> of <radius> two around $(-1,1)$ in the <phase plane>.

= 9
{parent=2019 resit}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=9}

The three <vectors> are <coplanar> precisely when their <scalar triple product> vanishes:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)=0.}
$$
This includes <linearly dependent> or zero <vectors>. It expresses that the <determinant> with those <vectors> as columns is zero.

For the two moving particles, distinguish closest approach at a common time from the geometric <distance> between their supporting lines. For fixed reference times, put
$$
\mathbf d_0=\mathbf a_1-\mathbf a_2-\mathbf b_1t_1+\mathbf b_2t_2,\qquad
\mathbf v=\mathbf b_1-\mathbf b_2.
$$
Their relative position is $\mathbf d_0+t\mathbf v$. If $\mathbf v\ne0$, the <closest approach of two uniformly moving particles> occurs at
$$
\boxed{t_*=-\frac{\mathbf d_0\cdot\mathbf v}{|\mathbf v|^2},\qquad
 d_{\min}^2=|\mathbf d_0|^2-\frac{(\mathbf d_0\cdot\mathbf v)^2}{|\mathbf v|^2}.}
$$
If $\mathbf v=0$, the <distance> is constant. If only $t\geq0$ is permitted, replace $t_*$ by $\max(0,t_*)$ before evaluating the <distance>.

Now suppose the velocities are nonparallel, and allow the relative starting-time offset to be chosen so that the particles reach the closest points of the two lines simultaneously. Write $\Delta=\mathbf a_2-\mathbf a_1$, $A=|\mathbf b_1|^2$, $B=\mathbf b_1\cdot\mathbf b_2$, $C=|\mathbf b_2|^2$ and $D=AC-B^2>0$. The independent <line> parameters $s_1,s_2$ at the closest points satisfy
$$
A s_1-Bs_2=\mathbf b_1\cdot\Delta,\qquad
B s_1-Cs_2=\mathbf b_2\cdot\Delta.
$$
These equations make the connecting <vector> perpendicular to both velocities. Solving the two-by-two <linear system> gives
$$
s_1=\frac{C\,\mathbf b_1\cdot\Delta-B\,\mathbf b_2\cdot\Delta}{D},\qquad
s_2=\frac{B\,\mathbf b_1\cdot\Delta-A\,\mathbf b_2\cdot\Delta}{D}.
$$
The corresponding <distance between skew lines> is $|\Delta\cdot(\mathbf b_1\times\mathbf b_2)|/|\mathbf b_1\times\mathbf b_2|$. Simultaneous arrival requires $t_1+s_1=t_2+s_2$, so $t_1-t_2=s_2-s_1$. Since the <vector triple product> gives
$$
(\mathbf b_1\times\mathbf b_2)\times(\mathbf b_1-\mathbf b_2)
=(A-B)\mathbf b_2+(C-B)\mathbf b_1,
$$
this condition is precisely
$$
\boxed{t_1-t_2=
\frac{\Delta\cdot[(\mathbf b_1\times\mathbf b_2)\times(\mathbf b_1-\mathbf b_2)]}
{B^2-AC}.}
$$
Thus the printed formula has a valid interpretation as a synchronization condition for attaining the line-distance lower bound. It is not an identity that must hold for arbitrary fixed reference times at the time of closest approach.

For a counterexample to that stronger reading, take $\mathbf a_1=0$, $\mathbf a_2=(1,0,1)$, $\mathbf b_1=(1,0,0)$, $\mathbf b_2=(0,1,0)$ and $t_1=t_2=0$. The actual squared <distance> is $2(t-1/2)^2+3/2$, minimized at $t=1/2$. The printed right-hand side is $-1$, whereas the fixed difference $t_1-t_2$ is zero. The line-distance bound is one, but it is not attained by that synchronized motion. Parallel velocities also require separate treatment because the displayed denominator then vanishes.

The <integrating factor> is $e^{2x}$, giving $(e^{2x}y)'=1+e^{2x}(x^2+2x)$. A quadratic particular solution for the <polynomial> forcing is $x^2/2+x/2-1/4$, while the resonant exponential forcing contributes $xe^{-2x}$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{y=(x+C)e^{-2x}+\frac{x^2+x}{2}-\frac14.}
$$
Substitution gives $y'+2y=e^{-2x}+x^2+2x$, and the free multiple of $e^{-2x}$ is the <homogeneous solution>.

{parent=10}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Integrate the equation without dividing by $y$:
$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{y^2}{2}\right)=1-\frac1{x+1},\qquad
\boxed{y^2=2x-2\log|x+1|+C.}
$$
This holds on a connected interval excluding $x=-1$. The real solutions are differentiable branches of this implicit curve. Where the radicand is positive, $y=\pm\sqrt{2x-2\log|x+1|+C}$, with a locally fixed sign.

A zero of $y$ can belong to a classical solution only at $x=0$, since the original equation then requires $x/(x+1)=0$. For $C=0$ near zero, $2x-2\log(1+x)=x^2+O(x^3)$; the smooth continuations have slopes $+1$ or $-1$ and switch between square-root signs across zero. Keeping the principal nonnegative <square root> across zero instead gives a cusp. Thus the implicit expression requires a smooth branch choice, not an unrestricted global choice of one square-root sign.

= Solution
{parent=i}

For finite-dimensional operators, use $(AB)^\dagger=B^\dagger A^\dagger$ and the <Hermitian> assumptions. The <commutator> satisfies $[A,B]^\dagger=-[A,B]$, so
$$
(i[A,B])^\dagger=-i(-[A,B])=i[A,B].
$$
Thus **$i[A,B]$ is <Hermitian>**. These adjoint identities are the intended matrix-level setting; for unbounded operators, domains and self-adjointness require additional analysis.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The <commutator> is <anti-Hermitian>: $[A,B]^\dagger=-[A,B]$. It is also <Hermitian> precisely when it is zero, so **it is not <Hermitian> in general; it is <Hermitian> if and only if $A$ and $B$ commute**. For example, $[\sigma_x,\sigma_y]=2i\sigma_z\ne0$ for the <Pauli matrices>.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Taking the <Hermitian adjoint> reverses the products and leaves their sum unchanged:
$$
\left(\frac{AB+BA}{2}\right)^\dagger=\frac{BA+AB}{2}.
$$
Thus **the symmetrized product is <Hermitian>**, whether or not $A$ and $B$ commute. This is the <Hermitian> <anticommutator> divided by two.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

One has $(C^\dagger C)^\dagger=C^\dagger C$, with no requirement that $C$ itself be <Hermitian>. Moreover,
$$
\langle v,C^\dagger Cv\rangle=\|Cv\|^2\geq0.
$$
Thus **$C^\dagger C$ is <Hermitian> and <positive semidefinite>**. The <positive semidefinite> property is stronger than the <Hermitian> conclusion alone.

Each displayed <Pauli matrix> has zero <trace> and <determinant> $-1$. Direct multiplication gives $\sigma_i^2=I$, hence
$$
\boxed{\det\sigma_i=-1,\quad\operatorname{tr}\sigma_i=0,\quad\sigma_i^2=I,\quad\sigma_i^{-1}=\sigma_i\qquad(i=x,y,z).}
$$
Their two <eigenvalues> are therefore $+1$ and $-1$.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Besides their squares, direct multiplication yields $\sigma_x\sigma_y=i\sigma_z$, $\sigma_y\sigma_z=i\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_z\sigma_x=i\sigma_y$. Reversing each pair changes the sign. These identities combine into the <Pauli matrix multiplication identity>
$$
\sigma_i\sigma_j=\delta_{ij}I+i\sum_k\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_k.
$$
Adding the expression with $i,j$ exchanged cancels the antisymmetric term, proving $\{\sigma_i,\sigma_j\}=2\delta_{ij}I$. Taking the <trace> instead uses $\operatorname{tr}I=2$ and $\operatorname{tr}\sigma_k=0$, proving $\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_i\sigma_j)=2\delta_{ij}$.

= iii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Expand the two <linear combinations> and use the <Pauli matrix multiplication identity>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma(\mathbf a)\sigma(\mathbf b)
&=\sum_{ij}a_i b_j\left(\delta_{ij}I+i\sum_k\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_k\right)\\
&=(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)I+i\sigma(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b).
\end{aligned}
$$
Interchanging the <vectors> reverses the <cross product>, so their <commutator> is $2i\sigma(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)$. The three <Pauli matrices> are <linearly independent>; alternatively, <trace> against $\sigma_k$ to recover each coefficient. Thus
$$
\boxed{[\sigma(\mathbf a),\sigma(\mathbf b)]=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathbf a\times\mathbf b=0.}
$$
For nonzero real <vectors>, this means they are parallel or antiparallel, equivalently <linearly dependent>. The algebra also extends to complex <vectors> using bilinear dot and cross products, with the same linear-dependence criterion; the <dot product> there has no complex conjugation.

= Solution

Use <variation of parameters>, writing $y_p=t_1u_1+t_2u_2$ and imposing $t_1'u_1+t_2'u_2=0$. <Differentiation> then gives $y_p'=t_1u_1'+t_2u_2'$. Since the $u_j$ solve the homogeneous equation,
$$
L(y_p)=t_1'u_1'+t_2'u_2'.
$$
Thus solve the two-by-two <linear system>
$$
\begin{pmatrix}u_1&u_2\\u_1'&u_2'\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}t_1'\\t_2'\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}0\\R\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its <determinant> is the nonzero <Wronskian> $W$. <Cramer's rule> gives $t_1'=-u_2R/W$ and $t_2'=u_1R/W$, and <integration> gives the stated antiderivatives. Their <integration> constants only add homogeneous solutions. The construction applies on a regular interval where the forcing and coefficients are defined and $W\ne0$.

= b
{parent=12}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Differentiate the <Wronskian> and cancel the mixed first-derivative terms:
$$
W'=u_1u_2''-u_2u_1''=u_1(-au_2'-bu_2)-u_2(-au_1'-bu_1)=-aW.
$$
This is the constant-coefficient form of <Abel's identity>. Hence $W(x)=Ce^{-ax}$, or $W(x)=W(x_0)e^{-a(x-x_0)}$. Since the <exponential function> is never zero, $W$ is either identically zero or nowhere zero on a connected interval of the homogeneous equation.

{parent=12}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Linear dependence means there are constants $c_1,c_2$, not both zero, with $c_1u_1+c_2u_2=0$. Since $u_1$ is nowhere zero, $c_2\ne0$, so $u_2=Cu_1$ and $u_2'=Cu_1'$. Substitution gives $W=u_1Cu_1'-Cu_1u_1'=0$.

The nonvanishing assumption is more than is needed for this direction. It also gives the converse directly: if $W=0$, then $(u_2/u_1)'=W/u_1^2=0$, so the ratio is constant on a connected interval. For arbitrary differentiable functions with zeros, a vanishing <Wronskian> alone needs more care; here the homogeneous linear equation also supplies an appropriate uniqueness theorem.

= Solution
{parent=d}

The forcing $\sec(2x)$ is undefined at $x=\pm\pi/4$, both inside the printed interval. Therefore **there is no classical solution on the whole stated interval**. Solve separately on the three connected components obtained by removing those two points.

Choose $u_1=\cos(2x)$ and $u_2=\sin(2x)$, whose <Wronskian> is two. <Variation of parameters> gives $t_1'=-(1/2)\tan(2x)$ and $t_2'=1/2$, so a particular solution is $\tfrac14\cos(2x)\log|\cos(2x)|+\tfrac x2\sin(2x)$. The full answer on each regular component is
$$
\boxed{y=A\cos(2x)+B\sin(2x)+\frac14\cos(2x)\log|\cos(2x)|+\frac x2\sin(2x).}
$$
The constants $A,B$ can differ between $(-\pi/2,-\pi/4)$, $(-\pi/4,\pi/4)$ and $(\pi/4,\pi/2)$. <Differentiation> gives $y''+4y=1/\cos(2x)$ wherever the forcing is defined. Although the particular solution has finite one-sided values at the singularities, its <derivative> has a logarithmic divergence, and the equation itself is undefined there. It cannot be promoted to one classical solution across the poles.
