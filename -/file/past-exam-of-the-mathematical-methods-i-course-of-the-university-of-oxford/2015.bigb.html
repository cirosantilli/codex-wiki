== 1
{scope}

=== Solution

Use <polar coordinates> $z=re^{i\theta}$, where $r=|z|\geq0$. The equation sets $r=\theta$, giving an <Archimedean spiral>. The origin is excluded because the <complex argument> of zero is undefined. With the principal <complex argument> in $(-\pi,\pi]$, the locus is
$$
\boxed{z(\theta)=\theta\cos\theta+i\theta\sin\theta,\qquad0<\theta\leq\pi.}
$$
It approaches the excluded origin along the positive real axis, passes through $i\pi/2$, and ends at $-\pi$. The choice of argument matters: the convention $[0,2\pi)$ instead gives $0<\theta<2\pi$, while allowing all values of the <complex argument> gives every turn with $\theta>0$.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

All <complex logarithms> of $i$ are $i(\pi/2+2\pi k)$ for integer $k$. By <complex exponentiation>,
$$
\boxed{i^i=\exp\!\left[-\frac\pi2-2\pi k\right],\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
Every value is positive and real. If the power is defined using the <principal logarithm>, the single value is $\boxed{e^{-\pi/2}}$. Keeping the logarithm branch explicit distinguishes this value from the full multivalued set.

== 3
{scope}

=== Solution

An <integrating factor> for the <first-order linear differential equation> is $\mu(x)=e^{\sin x}$. Multiplication gives
$$
(ye^{\sin x})'=2\sin x\cos x\,e^{\sin x}.
$$
Substitute $u=\sin x$ in the <antiderivative>: $\int2u e^u\,du=2(u-1)e^u$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{y(x)=2\sin x-2+Ce^{-\sin x},\qquad C\in\mathbb R.}
$$
Differentiating verifies the equation everywhere on the real line; no division by $\cos x$ restricts the solution.

== 4
{scope}

=== Solution

The addition formulas for the <hyperbolic functions> imply
$$
B(u)B(v)=B(u+v),\qquad B(u)=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh u&\sinh u\\\sinh u&\cosh u\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Since $M=B(y)/2$, its fourth <matrix power> is
$$
\boxed{M^4=\frac1{16}\begin{pmatrix}\cosh4y&\sinh4y\\\sinh4y&\cosh4y\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Alternatively, the orthonormal <eigenvectors> $(1,\pm1)/\sqrt2$ have <eigenvalues> $e^{\pm y}/2$. Raising these <eigenvalues> to the fourth power gives the same result by <spectral decomposition>.

== 5
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

Taking the <conjugate transpose> reverses the product order: $(AB)^\dagger=B^\dagger A^\dagger=BA$. Thus
$$
\boxed{AB\text{ is Hermitian if and only if }AB=BA.}
$$
The necessary and sufficient additional condition is that the two <Hermitian matrices> <commute>.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

Complex conjugation changes $i$ to $-i$, and transposition reverses the factors. Hence
$$
[i(AB-BA)]^\dagger=-i(BA-AB)=i(AB-BA).
$$
Thus the expression is a <Hermitian matrix> **without any additional condition**. Equivalently, the <commutator> of two <Hermitian matrices> is skew-Hermitian.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

The diagonal entries of a <Hermitian matrix> satisfy $A_{jj}=\overline{A_{jj}}$, so each is real. Consequently their sum, the <trace>, is real. **No additional condition is required.** The same conclusion follows by summing the real <eigenvalues>.

=== iv
{scope}

==== Solution

If $Au=\lambda u$ and $Av=\mu v$, the <Hermitian matrix> identity gives
$$
\lambda\langle u,v\rangle=\langle Au,v\rangle=\langle u,Av\rangle=\mu\langle u,v\rangle,
$$
because both <eigenvalues> are real. Thus <eigenvectors> belonging to **distinct eigenvalues are automatically orthogonal**. A <simple spectrum> guarantees this for an arbitrary choice of one <eigenvector> per <eigenvalue>.

Within a degenerate <eigenspace>, arbitrary independent <eigenvectors> need not be orthogonal. The <Gram-Schmidt process> always supplies an <orthonormal basis> of that <eigenspace>. Accordingly the existence of an <orthonormal eigenbasis> requires no additional condition, whereas automatic orthogonality of arbitrary independent <eigenvectors> is ensured by distinct <eigenvalues>. Scalar multiples of the same nonzero <eigenvector> are of course not mutually orthogonal.

== 6
{scope}

=== Solution

The <characteristic polynomial> is $(5-\lambda)(3-\lambda)-3=(\lambda-6)(\lambda-2)$. Corresponding normalized <eigenvectors> are
$$
\lambda=6:\quad e_6=\frac12(\sqrt3,1),\qquad
\lambda=2:\quad e_2=\frac12(-1,\sqrt3).
$$
These form an <orthonormal basis>. Write $x=u e_6+v e_2$; the <quadratic form> becomes
$$
6u^2+2v^2=4,\qquad\boxed{\frac{u^2}{2/3}+\frac{v^2}{2}=1.}
$$
The locus is an <ellipse> centred at the origin. Its minor semiaxis has length $\sqrt{2/3}$ along the direction $30^\circ$ from the positive horizontal axis; its major semiaxis has length $\sqrt2$ along $120^\circ$. The right panel of the figure in part 1 shows these principal directions.

== 7
{scope}

=== Solution

Subtract the first vertex from the remaining three to obtain edge vectors $a=(1,2,-2)$, $b=(0,1,3)$, $c=(-1,-1,0)$. Their <scalar triple product> is
$$
a\cdot(b\times c)=(1,2,-2)\cdot(3,-3,1)=-5.
$$
A <tetrahedron> occupies one sixth of the corresponding <parallelepiped>, so its <volume> is
$$
\boxed{V=\frac16|a\cdot(b\times c)|=\frac56.}
$$
The absolute value removes the orientation sign of the <determinant>.

== 8
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Leibniz formula for determinants> is unchanged by interchanging rows and columns, or equivalently replacing each <permutation> by its inverse. Thus
$$
\boxed{\det(A^T)=\det A.}
$$

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the multiplicativity of the <determinant> on $AA^{-1}=I$:
$$
\det A\,\det(A^{-1})=1,\qquad\boxed{\det(A^{-1})=\frac1{\det A}.}
$$
The denominator is nonzero because $A$ is an <invertible matrix>.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

Multiplying $A$ by $c$ multiplies each of its $n$ columns by $c$. The <multilinearity> of the <determinant> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\det(cA)=c^n\det A.}
$$
This includes $c=0$.

=== iv
{scope}

==== Solution

For an integer $c$, repeated multiplicativity of the <determinant>, with the inverse identity for negative powers, gives
$$
\boxed{\det(A^c)=(\det A)^c\quad(c\in\mathbb Z).}
$$
For an arbitrary real $c$, an invertible real <matrix> does not by itself define a unique real <matrix power>. For example, the scalar matrix $(-1)$ has no real square root. If a <matrix logarithm> $L$ has been chosen with $e^L=A$ and $A^c$ is defined as $e^{cL}$, the <matrix exponential determinant identity> gives the precise formula
$$
\boxed{\det(A^c)=e^{c\operatorname{tr}L}.}
$$
This cannot always be replaced by an independently chosen scalar branch of $(\det A)^c$. For instance, $A=-I_2$ has the complex square root $iI_2$, whose <determinant> is $-1$, while the principal scalar square root of $\det A=1$ is $+1$. For a positive-definite <symmetric matrix> with its usual positive spectral power, all <eigenvalues> are positive and the familiar formula $(\det A)^c$ holds for every real $c$.

== 9
{scope}

=== Solution

Let $P$ have columns $a,b,c$ and let $G$ have columns $A,B,C$. The cross-product formulas defining the <reciprocal basis> give
$$
G^TP=I,\qquad G=P^{-T},
$$
provided the original <scalar triple product> $T=\det P$ is nonzero. Taking <determinants> yields the <reciprocal basis involution> identity
$$
\boxed{\langle A,B,C\rangle=\det G=\frac1{\det P}=\frac1{\langle a,b,c\rangle}.}
$$
When $T=0$, the displayed reciprocal vectors are undefined, so there is no additional zero-denominator case to evaluate.

== 10
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

In one dimension the nonzero vector $b$ already spans the real line. Hence $\operatorname{span}(b,c)=\mathbb R$, and translating it by $a$ leaves
$$
\boxed{S=\mathbb R.}
$$
This is an <affine subspace> containing the origin for every allowed choice of the three vectors.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <affine subspace> is $S=a+\operatorname{span}(b,c)$. If $b,c$ are <linearly independent>, their <linear span> is all of $\mathbb R^2$, so **$S$ is the whole plane**. If they are proportional, **$S$ is the line through $a$ in direction $b$**. This line passes through the origin exactly when $a\in\operatorname{span}(b)$. Because $b$ is nonzero, the set cannot be a single point.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

If $b,c$ are <linearly independent>, **$S$ is the plane through $a$ with normal $b\times c$**. Its equation is
$$
(x-a)\cdot(b\times c)=0,
$$
so it contains the origin exactly when $a\cdot(b\times c)=0$. If $b,c$ are proportional, **$S$ is the line through $a$ in direction $b$**, containing the origin exactly when $a$ is proportional to $b$. The <linear span> of two nonzero vectors has dimension one or two, so neither a single point nor all of $\mathbb R^3$ occurs.

==== Solution

For the remaining distance calculation with $b=c$, every point on the line is $a+tb$. The nearest point to $p$ is found by <orthogonal projection> onto $b$:
$$
t_* =\frac{(p-a)\cdot b}{|b|^2},\qquad
\boxed{d(p,S)=\left|(p-a)-\frac{(p-a)\cdot b}{|b|^2}b\right|=\frac{|(p-a)\times b|}{|b|}.}
$$
Indeed, differentiating $|a+tb-p|^2$ sets its component along $b$ to zero. Its second derivative is $2|b|^2>0$, proving this is the minimum.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

A negative value of either radius makes the intersection empty. Assume henceforth $\alpha,\beta\geq0$ and put $D=|d|$. If $D=0$, the two <spheres> are concentric: their intersection is the entire sphere when $\alpha=\beta>0$, the origin when $\alpha=\beta=0$, and empty otherwise.

For $D>0$, subtract their squared equations to obtain the plane
$$
x\cdot d=\frac{D^2+\alpha^2-\beta^2}{2}.
$$
The <centre of the circle of intersection of two spheres> is $x_0=s\widehat d$, where
$$
\widehat d=\frac dD,\qquad s=\frac{D^2+\alpha^2-\beta^2}{2D}.
$$
Every point of that plane can be written $x=x_0+u$ with $u\cdot d=0$. Its remaining equation is
$$
|u|^2=\rho^2=\alpha^2-s^2
=\frac{[(\alpha+\beta)^2-D^2][D^2-(\alpha-\beta)^2]}{4D^2}.
$$
Thus **the intersection is a circle** of centre $x_0$ and radius $\rho$ if $|\alpha-\beta|<D<\alpha+\beta$; **a single point** $x_0$ at either equality; and **empty** otherwise. These include tangency to a zero-radius sphere. A negative $s$ places the circle centre beyond the first sphere's centre on the side opposite $d$.

In $\mathbb R^n$ for $n\geq2$, the same calculation gives an $(n-2)$-dimensional <sphere> in the perpendicular affine hyperplane when $\rho^2>0$, a point when $\rho^2=0$, and the empty set otherwise. In two dimensions the positive-radius case consists of two points. In one dimension the perpendicular hyperplane has only one point, so a nonconcentric intersection exists only when $\rho^2=0$. Concentric equal-radius sets coincide in every dimension.

== 11
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $D=\Omega^2-\omega^2$, $E=2\gamma\omega$, and $N=D^2+E^2$. Substituting a sine-and-cosine trial solution for the <forced harmonic oscillator> gives
$$
\boxed{x(t)=\frac A N\left[D\sin\omega t-E\cos\omega t\right],\qquad t\leq0.}
$$
The complementary solution is $e^{-\gamma t}(C\cos\nu t+S\sin\nu t)$, where $\nu=\sqrt{\Omega^2-\gamma^2}$. Every nonzero such solution is unbounded as $t\to-\infty$, so boundedness on the whole negative time axis forces $C=S=0$. This fixes the phase as well as the amplitude. In particular,
$$
x_0=x(0)=-\frac{AE}{N},\qquad v_0=\dot x(0)=\frac{AD\omega}{N}.
$$

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

After the force is removed, the <damped harmonic oscillator> evolves freely. There is no impulsive force at zero, so both displacement and velocity are continuous. Matching the values in part (i) gives
$$
\boxed{x(t)=e^{-\gamma t}\left[x_0\cos\nu t+\frac{v_0+\gamma x_0}{\nu}\sin\nu t\right],\qquad t>0.}
$$
Equivalently,
$$
x(t)=\frac{A\omega e^{-\gamma t}}N\left[-2\gamma\cos\nu t+\frac{\Omega^2-\omega^2-2\gamma^2}{\nu}\sin\nu t\right].
$$
This is bounded in positive time, completing the unique globally bounded solution. The formulas also include $\omega=0$, for which the forcing and solution both vanish.

\Image[2015-force-removal-ringdown.png]
{title=Steady forced motion before switch-off joins continuously to a freely decaying oscillation}

=== Solution

For a sinusoidal drive of variable positive angular frequency, the average absorbed <power> is proportional to
$$
\frac{\omega^2}{(\Omega^2-\omega^2)^2+4\gamma^2\omega^2}.
$$
It peaks at $\omega=\Omega$. The <absorbed-power bandwidth of a viscously damped oscillator> is the separation of its half-power frequencies: solving $|\Omega^2-\omega^2|=2\gamma\omega$ gives
$$
\omega_\pm=\sqrt{\Omega^2+\gamma^2}\pm\gamma,\qquad\boxed{\Delta\omega=2\gamma.}
$$
The usual <quality factor> is $\boxed{Q=\Omega/(2\gamma)=\Omega/\Delta\omega}$. Large $Q$ means a narrow response band relative to the resonant frequency. The post-drive motion has amplitude envelope $e^{-\gamma t}$, connecting a narrow <bandwidth> to a long <ringdown>. For weak damping, the cycle-averaged energy decays as $e^{-2\gamma t}$ and $Q$ is also $2\pi$ times stored energy divided by the energy lost per cycle. The exact instantaneous energy has an oscillatory factor, so this cycle interpretation uses the weak-damping approximation.

== 12
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

Direct multiplication of the <Pauli matrices> gives $\sigma_j^2=I$, $\sigma_1\sigma_2=i\sigma_3$ and its cyclic permutations; reversing unequal factors changes the sign. Hence, using the <Levi-Civita symbol>,
$$
\sigma_j\sigma_k=\delta_{jk}I+i\epsilon_{jk\ell}\sigma_\ell,\qquad
\boxed{[\sigma_j,\sigma_k]=2i\epsilon_{jk\ell}\sigma_\ell.}
$$
Repeated $\ell$ is summed, while the <Kronecker delta> handles the equal-index case.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

For $v=(a,b)^T$, multiplying each <Pauli matrix> between $v^\dagger$ and $v$ gives
$$
\boxed{x=\left(\operatorname{Re}(a^*b),\operatorname{Im}(a^*b),\frac{|a|^2-|b|^2}{2}\right).}
$$
All three components are real. Moreover $|x|=(|a|^2+|b|^2)/2=\|v\|^2/2$. Thus normalized two-component <spinors> map to the radius-$1/2$ version of the <Bloch sphere>; the usual unit-radius convention multiplies these coordinates by two.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

Each <Pauli matrix> is Hermitian and the coefficients $\alpha_j$ are real, so $T^\dagger=T$. Taking the <conjugate transpose> of the absolutely convergent <matrix exponential> series yields
$$
U^\dagger=e^{-iT/2}.
$$
The exponents $-iT/2$ and $iT/2$ <commute>. Therefore $U^\dagger U=e^{-iT/2+iT/2}=I$, proving that **$U$ is unitary**.

=== iv
{scope}

==== Solution

If $Tv=tv$ with $v\ne0$, induction gives $T^kv=t^kv$ for every nonnegative integer $k$. Substituting into the <matrix exponential> series gives
$$
Uv=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(i/2)^kT^kv}{k!}=e^{it/2}v.
$$
Thus **each eigenvector of $T$ is an eigenvector of $U$**, with <eigenvalue> $e^{it/2}$. Different <eigenvalues> of $T$ can produce the same exponential <eigenvalue>.

=== v
{scope}

==== Solution

The anticommuting unequal <Pauli matrices> cancel the cross terms, leaving
$$
T^2=(\alpha_1^2+\alpha_2^2+\alpha_3^2)I=|\alpha|^2I.
$$
Also $\operatorname{tr}T=0$, so its <eigenvalues> are $\pm|\alpha|$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\lambda_\pm(U)=e^{\pm i|\alpha|/2}.}
$$
For $|\alpha|>0$, the <matrix exponential for a scalar square> also gives $U=I\cos(|\alpha|/2)+iT\sin(|\alpha|/2)/|\alpha|$. At $\alpha=0$, its continuous limit is $U=I$. The two exponential <eigenvalues> coincide when $|\alpha|=2\pi k$, making $U=(-1)^kI$.

=== vi
{scope}

==== Solution

With only $\alpha_3$ nonzero, $T=\operatorname{diag}(\alpha_3,-\alpha_3)$ and
$$
U=\operatorname{diag}(e^{i\alpha_3/2},e^{-i\alpha_3/2}).
$$
For $\alpha_3\notin2\pi\mathbb Z$, the <eigenvectors> are **the nonzero multiples of $(1,0)^T$ and $(0,1)^T$**. When $\alpha_3=2\pi k$, $U=(-1)^kI$ and **every nonzero vector is an eigenvector**.

=== vii
{scope}

==== Solution

The transformed components are $a'=e^{i\alpha_3/2}a$, $b'=e^{-i\alpha_3/2}b$. Hence $(a')^*b'=e^{-i\alpha_3}a^*b$, while the two <moduli> are unchanged. Taking real and imaginary parts in the expression from part (ii) gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}x'_1\\x'_2\\x'_3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha_3&\sin\alpha_3&0\\-\sin\alpha_3&\cos\alpha_3&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_1\\x_2\\x_3\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
This is a right-handed active <rotation> through **$-\alpha_3$ about the third axis**. The minus sign follows from the positive sign in $U=e^{+iT/2}$; changing that convention reverses the rotation. A $2\pi$ spatial <rotation> changes the <spinor> sign while leaving the three-vector unchanged.

== 13
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

A <fixed point> obeys $(I-M)X=c$. Thus the necessary and sufficient existence condition is
$$
\boxed{c\in\operatorname{range}(I-M).}
$$
If $1$ is not an <eigenvalue> of $M$, then $I-M$ is an <invertible matrix> and the unique constant solution is $\boxed{X=(I-M)^{-1}c}$. If $I-M$ is singular but the equation is consistent, all constant solutions are $X_0+\ker(I-M)$ for any particular solution $X_0$. For an existing <fixed point>, $x_{k+1}-X=M(x_k-X)$; the successive difference in the question likewise obeys $\delta x_{k+1}=M\delta x_k$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Repeated substitution in the <affine iteration> gives
$$
x_k=M^{k-1}x_1+(I+M+\cdots+M^{k-2})c.
$$
Therefore the required <matrix polynomials> are
$$
\boxed{f(M)=M^{k-1},\qquad g(M)=\sum_{j=0}^{k-2}M^j.}
$$
These formulas do not require an inverse. If $I-M$ is an <invertible matrix>, multiplying the finite geometric sum by $I-M$ gives $g(M)=(I-M^{k-1})(I-M)^{-1}$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

For a <diagonalizable matrix>, write $M=P\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_j)P^{-1}$. Relative to an existing <fixed point>,
$$
x_k-X=P\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_j^{k-1})P^{-1}(x_1-X).
$$
Convergence to the **same fixed point for every initial vector** therefore holds exactly when
$$
\boxed{|\lambda_j|<1\text{ for all }j,\quad\text{equivalently }\rho(M)<1.}
$$
Here $\rho(M)$ is the <spectral radius>. Necessity follows by choosing an initial displacement in each <eigenspace>; sufficiency follows because every displayed scalar power tends to zero. The condition also makes $I-M$ an <invertible matrix>, so that <fixed point> exists uniquely for every $c$.

If instead different initial vectors may converge to different constant solutions, an <eigenvalue> $1$ is allowed: all other <eigenvalues> must have <modulus> less than one, and the component of $c$ in the <eigenspace> for $1$ must vanish. That component of the initial vector then remains unchanged. No other unit-modulus <eigenvalue> is compatible with convergence for arbitrary initial vectors.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Because $A$ is a real <symmetric matrix>, differentiating the <quadratic form> gives
$$
\nabla_v a=(A-\alpha I)v.
$$
Thus the stationary vectors are precisely $\ker(A-\alpha I)$, and at every one of them
$$
\boxed{a=0.}
$$
The zero vector is always stationary. Nonzero stationary vectors exist exactly when $\alpha$ is an <eigenvalue> of $A$, and are exactly its nonzero <eigenvectors> with that <eigenvalue>. There is no norm constraint here; introducing one would change the stationary-value problem.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The second <gradient> is $\nabla_v b=[A+B-(\alpha+\beta)I]v$. Combining its vanishing with $(A-\alpha I)v=0$ gives
$$
\boxed{v\in\ker(A-\alpha I)\cap\ker(B-\beta I).}
$$
The zero vector always works. A nonzero common stationary vector exists exactly when $A,B$ have a common <eigenvector> with respective <eigenvalues> $\alpha,\beta$; both stationary values are then zero. <Commuting symmetric matrices have a common orthonormal eigenbasis>, so commutation is sufficient to find such vectors for the paired <eigenvalues> in that basis. It does not guarantee an arbitrary prescribed pair.

Commutation is also not necessary for one common <eigenvector>. For example,
$$
A=\operatorname{diag}(1,2,3),\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}\beta&0&0\\0&0&1\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}
$$
are real <symmetric matrices> sharing $e_1$ with <eigenvalues> $1,\beta$, but $AB\ne BA$. This separates the question's common-vector condition from the stronger <simultaneous diagonalization> of the entire space.
