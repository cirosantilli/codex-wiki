== 1

=== Solution

Multiply numerator and denominator by the denominator's <complex conjugate>:
$$
\frac{3+2i}{5-4i}=\frac{(3+2i)(5+4i)}{25+16}=\frac{7+22i}{41}.
$$
Thus the <real part> is $\boxed{7/41}$ and the <imaginary part> is $\boxed{22/41}$.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

The <determinant> is $a^2-i(-i)=a^2-1$. The <adjugate matrix> formula gives
$$
\boxed{A^{-1}=\frac1{a^2-1}\begin{pmatrix}a&-i\\i&a\end{pmatrix},\qquad a\ne\pm1.}
$$
When $a=1$ or $a=-1$ we have a <singular matrix>, so no <inverse matrix> exists. This criterion holds for real or complex $a$.

== 3
{scope}

=== Solution

Write $z=x+iy$ with real $x,y$. The complex <hyperbolic sine> is $\sinh z=\sinh x\cos y+i\cosh x\sin y$. Its zero <real part> requires $\sinh x\cos y=0$. If $\cos y=0$, the magnitude of the <imaginary part> would be $\cosh x\ge1$, which cannot equal $1/2$. Therefore $x=0$ and $\sin y=1/2$. All solutions are
$$
\boxed{z=i\left(\frac\pi6+2\pi k\right)\quad\text{or}\quad z=i\left(\frac{5\pi}6+2\pi k\right),\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
On the <Argand diagram> these are two interlaced arithmetic sequences on the imaginary axis; both repeat with imaginary period $2\pi i$.

== 4
{scope}

=== Solution

The elementary <row operation> $R_2\leftarrow R_2+3R_1$ gives
$$
\begin{pmatrix}1&4&2\\0&a+12&0\\0&0&b\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The nonzero rows here have distinct <pivots>. Therefore the <matrix rank> is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{rank}A=\begin{cases}
3,&a\ne-12,\ b\ne0,\\
2,&\text{exactly one of }a=-12,\ b=0\text{ holds},\\
1,&a=-12,\ b=0.
\end{cases}}
$$
There is always at least one nonzero row, so rank zero is impossible.

== 5
{scope}

=== Solution

Rearrange as $x''-4x'+3x=10t$. The <characteristic polynomial> factors as $(r-1)(r-3)$, giving the <homogeneous solutions> $e^t,e^{3t}$. A particular <polynomial> solution $At+B$ obeys $3A=10$ and $3B-4A=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{x(t)=C_1e^t+C_2e^{3t}+\frac{10}{3}t+\frac{40}{9}.}
$$
This is the full two-constant solution of the <second-order linear differential equation>.

== 6
{scope}

=== Solution

Let $\omega=e^{i\pi/7}$, a primitive fourteenth <root of unity>. The <geometric series> gives $\sum_{k=1}^{14}\omega^k=0$. Taking the <real part> and adding the remaining fifteenth term therefore yields
$$
\boxed{S=\cos\frac{15\pi}{7}=\cos\frac\pi7.}
$$
The upper summation limit matters: the first fourteen terms complete one full cycle, and the fifteenth supplies one additional term.

== 7
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

The finite-dimensional <spectral theorem> diagonalizes the <Hermitian matrix> in its <orthonormal eigenbasis>. The <determinant> is the product of the <eigenvalues>:
$$
\boxed{\det H=\lambda_1\lambda_2.}
$$

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

The <trace> is unchanged by <similarity>, so in the same <eigenbasis>
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{tr}H=\lambda_1+\lambda_2.}
$$

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

Every <eigenvalue> of a <Hermitian matrix> is real: if $He=\lambda e$ with $e^\dagger e=1$, then $\lambda=e^\dagger He=\overline{e^\dagger He}$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Im}(\lambda_1\lambda_2)=0.}
$$

=== iv
{scope}

==== Solution

Distinct <eigenvalues> of a <Hermitian matrix> have <orthogonal> <eigenvectors>, since $\lambda_2e_1^\dagger e_2=e_1^\dagger He_2=\lambda_1e_1^\dagger e_2$. The given normalization then gives
$$
\boxed{e_1^\dagger(e_1+e_2)=1+0=1.}
$$

=== Solution

Here $M^2=-I$, so its even powers alternate between $\pm I$ and its odd powers between $\pm M$. Collecting even and odd terms of the <matrix exponential> yields
$$
R(\phi)=I\cos\phi+M\sin\phi
=\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}\cos\phi&-\sin\phi\\\sin\phi&\cos\phi\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Thus $R^TR=I$, $\det R=1$, and $R(\phi)(1,0)^T=(\cos\phi,\sin\phi)^T$. It is the <rotation matrix> for a counterclockwise <rotation> through angle $\phi$, preserving <lengths> and <orientation>.

== 9
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <substitution> $u=x+y$ turns the equation into $u'=1+u^2$. Separating and integrating gives $\arctan u=x+C$, hence
$$
\boxed{y=\tan(x+C)-x.}
$$
Each real solution is defined on an <interval> containing no pole of the <tangent>. There is no real constant-$u$ solution discarded by division, since $1+u^2>0$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <first-order linear differential equation> has <integrating factor> $e^{\sin x}$. Multiplying through gives
$$
(ye^{\sin x})'=2\sin x\cos x\,e^{\sin x}.
$$
With $u=\sin x$, the right side integrates to $2e^u(u-1)$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{y=2\sin x-2+Ce^{-\sin x}.}
$$
All coefficients are smooth, so these real solutions extend over the whole real axis.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $v=y^2$, so $v'=2yy'$. On an <interval> with $x\ne0$, the resulting <first-order linear differential equation> is $v'-4v/x=2x$. Its <integrating factor> $x^{-4}$ gives $(v/x^4)'=2/x^3$, hence
$$
\boxed{y^2=Cx^4-x^2,\qquad y=\pm\sqrt{Cx^4-x^2}.}
$$
For real differentiable graphs one needs $C>0$ and $|x|>1/\sqrt C$, with either fixed sign on a connected <interval>. The points $x=0$ are outside the original equation's domain. At $|x|=1/\sqrt C$, $y=0$ would make the left side zero while the right side is $x\ne0$, so those endpoints are also excluded. The identically zero function is not an additional solution.

=== Solution

**One scalar initial condition is needed in each case**, because each equation is first order and each regular solution has one integration constant. The usual <Picard-Lindelöf theorem> applies locally at a regular initial point. For part (c), this requires $x_0\ne0$ and $y_0\ne0$; a condition at an excluded singular endpoint cannot specify a classical solution there. Arbitrary distant boundary data also need not be compatible with one common <interval> of existence.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

A direction of the intersection <line> must be <orthogonal> to both <normal vectors>. Their <cross product> is
$$
(2,6,-3)\times(5,2,-1)=(0,-13,-26).
$$
Thus a convenient parallel <vector> is $\boxed{u=(0,1,2)}$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Eliminating $2y-z$ from the two <plane> equations gives $x=2$, followed by $2y-z=2$. Taking $y=1$ gives the point $\boxed{p=(2,1,0)}$. The entire intersection <line> is $\mathbf r=p+tu$, where $u=(0,1,2)$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <orthogonal projection> of the origin onto the <line> occurs at $t_*=-p\cdot u/|u|^2=-1/5$, giving closest point $(2,4/5,-2/5)$. Hence the <distance from a point to a line in three dimensions> is
$$
\boxed{d=\sqrt{4+\frac{16+4}{25}}=\sqrt{\frac{24}{5}}=\frac{2\sqrt{30}}5.}
$$

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For $r=0$, the <norm> equation requires $x=c$. Thus the <locus> is
$$
\boxed{\{c\}\text{ if }a\cdot(c-b)=0;\qquad\varnothing\text{ otherwise}.}
$$
This includes $a=0$, when the first condition imposes no restriction.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

First assume $a\ne0$. The first equation is a <plane>, and the second is a <sphere> centred at $c$. Define its signed normal separation and projected centre by
$$
h=\frac{a\cdot(c-b)}{|a|},\qquad q=c-\frac{a\cdot(c-b)}{|a|^2}a.
$$
The <intersection of a plane and a sphere> is
$$
\boxed{\begin{cases}
\text{a circle with centre }q\text{ and radius }\sqrt{r^2-h^2},&|h|<r,\\
\{q\},&|h|=r,\\
\varnothing,&|h|>r.
\end{cases}}
$$
The <circle> lies in the given <plane>. Its radius follows from the <Pythagorean theorem>, splitting $x-c=(x-q)+(q-c)$ into <orthogonal> components. If $a=0$, the first equation is the identity $0=0$, so the locus is the **entire sphere**, rather than a planar section.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

A <norm> is nonnegative, so it cannot equal a negative $r$. The <locus> is therefore $\boxed{\varnothing}$, irrespective of $a,b,c$.

== 11
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

Reading the coefficients in the ordered concentration <vector> gives the <linear system of ordinary differential equations>
$$
\boxed{\dot x=Mx,\qquad M=\begin{pmatrix}-\epsilon&0&\delta\\\epsilon&-\eta&0\\0&\eta&-\delta\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Its columns sum to zero, so $d(A+B+C)/dt=0$. This <three-state cyclic conversion model> conserves total concentration.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $p=\epsilon+\delta+\eta$ and $q=\epsilon\delta+\delta\eta+\eta\epsilon$. Direct expansion gives the <characteristic polynomial>
$$
\det(\lambda I-M)=(\lambda+\epsilon)(\lambda+\eta)(\lambda+\delta)-\epsilon\eta\delta
=\lambda(\lambda^2+p\lambda+q).
$$
The quadratic formula therefore gives
$$
\boxed{K=-\frac{\epsilon+\delta+\eta}{2},\qquad W=\epsilon\delta+\delta\eta+\eta\epsilon,\qquad\lambda_\pm=K\pm\sqrt{K^2-W}.}
$$
Since $p,q>0$, both nonzero <eigenvalues> have negative <real part>. They need not be real; equal rates give a complex-conjugate pair.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

The remaining <eigenvalue> is $\boxed{\lambda_3=0}$. At a <steady state>, the three cyclic fluxes agree: $\epsilon A=\eta B=\delta C$. A corresponding nonzero <eigenvector> is therefore
$$
\boxed{v_0=\begin{pmatrix}1/\epsilon\\1/\eta\\1/\delta\end{pmatrix},}
$$
or its scalar multiple $(\delta\eta,\epsilon\delta,\epsilon\eta)^T$. Its positive components express a possible equilibrium concentration ratio.

=== iv
{scope}

==== Solution

If $K^2\ne W$, the three <eigenvalues> are distinct, so a basis of <eigenvectors> gives
$$
\boxed{M'=\operatorname{diag}(-\rho_1,-\rho_2,0),\qquad
\rho_1=-K-\sqrt{K^2-W},\quad\rho_2=-K+\sqrt{K^2-W}.}
$$
The transformed coordinates then have the displayed exponential form. If $K^2<W$, this is a complex <eigenbasis>; real concentrations are recovered by conjugate-paired coefficients. A real basis instead gives a decaying two-dimensional rotation block with decay rate $-K$ and angular frequency $\sqrt{W-K^2}$.

The proposed <diagonalization> is **not valid for every positive choice of rates**. For example $\epsilon=\delta=1$, $\eta=4$ gives $K=-3$, $W=9$, with repeated <eigenvalue> $-3$ and only one corresponding <eigenvector>. More generally the <defective repeated eigenvalue in a cyclic conversion model> occurs when $K^2=W$. A <Jordan basis> then gives
$$
M'=\begin{pmatrix}K&1&0\\0&K&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
x'(t)=\begin{pmatrix}(c_1+c_2t)e^{Kt}\\c_2e^{Kt}\\c_3\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The extra factor $t$ cannot be represented by two independent pure exponential coordinates in a diagonal basis.

=== v
{scope}

==== Solution

All nonzero <eigenmodes> decay, including the $te^{Kt}$ term in the repeated-root case. Total concentration is conserved and initially equals $n$. Combining $A_\infty+B_\infty+C_\infty=n$ with the equal-flux ratios gives
$$
\boxed{A_\infty=\frac{n\delta\eta}{q},\qquad B_\infty=\frac{n\epsilon\delta}{q},\qquad C_\infty=\frac{n\epsilon\eta}{q},\qquad
q=\epsilon\delta+\delta\eta+\eta\epsilon.}
$$
These are the unique <steady state> concentrations with this total. The formula remains valid when the transient <eigenvalues> are complex or repeated.

== 12
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

A real <symmetric matrix> has $n$ freely chosen diagonal entries and $n(n-1)/2$ freely chosen entries above the diagonal. The entries below are then fixed by symmetry. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\dim\mathcal S=\frac{n(n+1)}2.}
$$

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

An explicit <basis> is
$$
\boxed{E_1=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
E_2=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
E_3=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Indeed every real <symmetric matrix> $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{pmatrix}$ has the unique <linear combination> $aE_1+cE_2+bE_3$.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

The <cyclic property of the trace> gives $\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\operatorname{tr}(BA)$, which is C1. The <linearity> of <trace> and <matrix multiplication> gives C2 and C3. Finally symmetry of $A$ gives
$$
\langle A,A\rangle=\operatorname{tr}(A^2)=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}A_{ji}=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}^2\ge0,
$$
with equality exactly when every entry is zero. Thus C4 also holds. This is the <Frobenius inner product> restricted to the real <vector space> of <symmetric matrices>.

=== iv
{scope}

==== Solution

Apply the <cyclic property of the trace> and $BB^{-1}=I$:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{tr}(B^{-1}AB)=\operatorname{tr}(ABB^{-1})=\operatorname{tr}A.}
$$
This proves <similarity invariance of trace> without requiring $A$ or $B$ to be symmetric.

=== v
{scope}

==== Solution

Taking the <trace> of the proposed <commutator> identity would give
$$
0=\operatorname{tr}(AB)-\operatorname{tr}(BA)=\operatorname{tr}I=n,
$$
a contradiction for positive integer $n$ over the real numbers. **No such finite real matrices exist.** This is the <trace of a commutator> obstruction; it also applies to complex finite matrices.

=== vi
{scope}

==== Solution

Multiplicativity of the <determinant> gives $\langle A,B\rangle=\det A\det B$. Thus **C1 holds**. For $n\ge2$, **C2 fails**: choosing $A=B=I$ and $\lambda=2$ gives $\det(2I)=2^n\ne2$. The same choice $A=B=C=I$ disproves **C3**, since $\det(B+C)=2^n\ne2$. The nonnegativity portion of **C4 holds**, because $\det(A^2)=(\det A)^2\ge0$, but its positive-definiteness clause fails: a nonzero <singular matrix> that is <symmetric>, such as $\operatorname{diag}(1,0,\ldots,0)$, has zero self-pairing.

Therefore **this is not an inner product for $n\ge2$**. For the exceptional case $n=1$, the <determinant> is the scalar entry and the pairing is ordinary multiplication of real numbers, so all four conditions hold. The <determinant pairing is not an inner product> in higher dimension because it measures volume scaling rather than a bilinear length geometry.
