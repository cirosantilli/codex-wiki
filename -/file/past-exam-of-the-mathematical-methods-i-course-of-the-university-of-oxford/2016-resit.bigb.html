=== i

==== Solution

Write $27=27e^{2\pi i n}$. The three <complex roots> are
$$
\boxed{z=3,\qquad z=-\frac32+\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}i,\qquad
z=-\frac32-\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}i.}
$$
On the <Argand diagram>, these are three equally spaced points on the circle $|z|=3$, with <arguments> $0,2\pi/3,4\pi/3$. They are isolated points, rather than the whole circle.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $z=x+iy$ and $z^*=x-iy$. The first two squared terms are <complex conjugates>, so their sum is $2[(x-2)^2-(y+1)^2]$. Also $zz^*=x^2+y^2$. The equation therefore reduces to
$$
2[(x-2)^2-(y+1)^2]+2(x^2+y^2)+6
=4(x^2-2x-y+3)=0.
$$
Thus the <locus> is the upward-opening <parabola>
$$
\boxed{y=(x-1)^2+2,}
$$
with vertex $1+2i$ and axis $x=1$ in the <Argand diagram>.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

Expanding the <determinant> along its first row gives
$$
\det A=a(a^2-bc)-b(ac-b^2)+c(c^2-ab)
=a^3+b^3+c^3-3abc.
$$
The required factorization is
$$
\boxed{(a+b+c)(a^2+b^2+c^2-ab-bc-ca).}
$$
Each factor is a <symmetric polynomial>. The second is $\tfrac12[(a-b)^2+(b-c)^2+(c-a)^2]$, making its permutation symmetry especially transparent. This factorization holds over both real and complex coefficients.

== 3
{scope}

=== Solution

Let $R=|\alpha|$, $S=|\beta|$ and $d=|\mathbf b-\mathbf a|$. For distinct centres, the two spherical surfaces intersect exactly when
$$
\boxed{|R-S|\le d\le R+S.}
$$
Strict inequalities give a nondegenerate <circle>; equality gives a single tangency point. If $d=0$, equal radii give coincident spheres and unequal radii give no intersection, so the circle-centre formula below does not apply.

Subtracting the squared sphere equations gives the <radical plane>
$$
2(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)\cdot\mathbf r
=|\mathbf b|^2-|\mathbf a|^2+\alpha^2-\beta^2.
$$
The <centre of the circle of intersection of two spheres> is the projection of either sphere centre onto this plane. Write $\mathbf c=\mathbf a+\ell(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)/d$; substitution gives $\ell=(d^2+\alpha^2-\beta^2)/(2d)$ and hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbf c=\frac12\left(\mathbf a+\mathbf b+
\frac{(\alpha^2-\beta^2)(\mathbf b-\mathbf a)}{|\mathbf b-\mathbf a|^2}\right).}
$$
The circle radius squared is $R^2-\ell^2$, providing another check of the intersection conditions.

Its centre equals $\mathbf a$ precisely when $\ell=0$, or $\boxed{\beta^2=\alpha^2+d^2}$. For a positive first radius, the intersection is then a <great circle> of the first sphere. A triangle from its centre to an intersection point and then to the second centre is right-angled at $\mathbf a$, explaining the <Pythagorean theorem> relation geometrically.

== 4
{scope}

=== Solution

Choose the cylinder centre's initial position as the coordinate origin, with downhill motion to the right and $0\le\alpha<\pi/2$. For <rolling without slipping>, the clockwise <angular speed> is $\omega=v/r$. A point initially vertically above the axis therefore has
$$
\boxed{x(t)=vt\cos\alpha+a\sin(vt/r),\qquad
y(t)=-vt\sin\alpha+a\cos(vt/r).}
$$
A different origin adds constant coordinates without changing the following <velocity> test. Setting $\theta=vt/r$ gives $\dot x/v=\cos\alpha+(a/r)\cos\theta$ and $\dot y/v=-\sin\alpha-(a/r)\sin\theta$.

For strictly upward motion, $\dot x=0$ and $\dot y>0$. The first condition fixes $\cos\theta=-(r/a)\cos\alpha$; choose the negative sine branch to maximize $\dot y$. Positivity then requires
$$
\sqrt{(a/r)^2-\cos^2\alpha}>\sin\alpha,
\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad\boxed{a>r.}
$$
At $a=r$ the relevant point is momentarily stationary, not moving upward. Thus a material point inside or on an ordinary cylinder cannot move exactly vertically upward in this motion. The mathematical extension $a>r$ describes the looping <trochoid> of a point beyond the rolling radius, illustrated in the figure.

== 5
{scope}

=== Solution

Rearrange as $y'+(x-1)y=x-1$ and put $w=y-1$. The resulting <linear differential equation> is $w'+(x-1)w=0$, so
$$
\boxed{y=1+C\exp\left[-\frac12(x-1)^2\right].}
$$
The initial condition gives $C=1$. Its graph is a <Gaussian function> above the horizontal asymptote $y=1$, symmetric about $x=1$, with its maximum $y=2$ there. It rises for $x<1$ and falls for $x>1$, since $y'=-(x-1)e^{-(x-1)^2/2}$.

== 6
{scope}

=== Solution

A plane through the three points also contains the origin exactly when their <position vectors> are linearly dependent. The <scalar triple product> is
$$
\det\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\4&5&6\\\gamma&8&7\end{pmatrix}
=27-3\gamma.
$$
Thus $\boxed{\gamma=9}$. The resulting plane is $\boxed{x-2y+z=0}$, which contains all three points and the origin. The points are not collinear: the differences $(3,3,3)$ and $(\gamma-1,6,4)$ cannot be proportional, since their last two components would require $6=4$. Therefore the plane used in the argument is well defined.

== 7
{scope}

=== Solution

For a <diagonal matrix>, each diagonal entry separately obeys $d^2+2d-15=(d-3)(d+5)=0$. Hence all four possibilities are
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{diag}(3,3),\quad\operatorname{diag}(3,-5),\quad
\operatorname{diag}(-5,3),\quad\operatorname{diag}(-5,-5).}
$$
For the second form, $X^2=I$ gives
$$
(aI+bX)^2+2(aI+bX)
=(a^2+b^2+2a)I+2b(a+1)X.
$$
Because $I,X$ are linearly independent and $b\ne0$, the $X$ coefficient forces $a=-1$; the $I$ coefficient then gives $b^2=16$. Thus
$$
\boxed{A=-I\pm4X=\begin{pmatrix}-1&\pm4\\\pm4&-1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Both constants are nonzero, as required.

== 8
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== 1
{scope}

===== Solution

For the <Bernoulli differential equation>, work on a branch where $y\ne0$ and the required powers are defined. Multiplication by $(1-m)y^{-m}$ makes the <chain rule> useful:
$$
z=y^{1-m},\qquad
\boxed{z'+(1-m)f(x)z=(1-m)g(x).}
$$
Thus $\alpha=1-m$, $h=(1-m)f$ and $k=(1-m)g$. The case $m=1$ is already linear and must be treated separately, because the proposed substitution would be constant. Solutions lost by dividing by a power of $y$, particularly $y=0$ when it solves the original equation, must also be checked separately.

==== 2
{scope}

===== Solution

Here $m=2$, so $z=1/y$ obeys $z'-2z\tan x=-\tan^2x$. An <integrating factor> is $\cos^2x$, giving
$$
(z\cos^2x)'=-\sin^2x,
\qquad z\cos^2x=C-\frac x2+\frac{\sin2x}{4}.
$$
The nonzero solutions are therefore
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac{\cos^2x}{C-x/2+\sin2x/4}.}
$$
The additional solution is $\boxed{y=0}$. Each formula is interpreted on an interval where $\tan x$ is defined and its displayed denominator is nonzero.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The given particular solution $y_p=x^2$ suggests the standard reduction of a <Riccati equation>. Substitution of $y=x^2+1/v$ gives
$$
-2x^3v'=5x^2v+2,
\qquad v'+\frac{5}{2x}v=-\frac1{x^3}.
$$
On either side of $x=0$, an <integrating factor> is $|x|^{5/2}$, so $v=(C-2\sqrt{|x|})/|x|^{5/2}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{y=x^2\frac{C-\sqrt{|x|}}{C-2\sqrt{|x|}},}
$$
along with the separately recovered particular solution $y=x^2$. The denominator must remain nonzero on the solution interval; $x=0$ is a singular point of the divided differential equation.

For the positive-$x$ branch, $y(1)=(C-1)/(C-2)$ and $y(4)=16(C-2)/(C-4)$. Nonsingular endpoint values exclude $C=2,4$, so
$$
\boxed{y(4)\bigl(3-2y(1)\bigr)=16.}
$$
The particular solution $y=x^2$ gives the same value directly. These evaluations concern one solution branch, with a common integration constant, rather than independently chosen solutions at the two points.

== 9
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

Both expressions are the same scalar:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf u^{\mathsf T}\mathbf v=\sum_i u_iv_i
=\sum_i v_iu_i=\mathbf v^{\mathsf T}\mathbf u.}
$$
This identity uses the ordinary <transpose> and remains true for complex entries. A <Hermitian inner product> uses a conjugate transpose instead and has conjugate symmetry, not this bilinear symmetry.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

The $y$ coordinate is an independent eigen-direction, while the $xz$ block is $\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$. Thus
$$
\det(A-\lambda I)=(1-\lambda)^2(3-\lambda),
$$
which gives $\det(A-I)=0$. The <eigenspaces> are
$$
\boxed{\lambda=1:\ \operatorname{span}\{(1,0,-1),(0,1,0)\};\qquad
\lambda=3:\ \operatorname{span}\{(1,0,1)\}.}
$$
Divide the two diagonal $xz$ vectors by $\sqrt2$ to obtain an <orthonormal eigenbasis>. The eigenvalue one has multiplicity two; giving only the $y$ axis would omit an independent eigenvector.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

Because $A$ is symmetric, translating $\mathbf r=\mathbf x+\mathbf a$ gives
$$
\mathbf x^{\mathsf T}A\mathbf x+(2A\mathbf a+\mathbf b)^{\mathsf T}\mathbf x
+\mathbf a^{\mathsf T}A\mathbf a+\mathbf b^{\mathsf T}\mathbf a=1.
$$
The linear term vanishes when $\boxed{2A\mathbf a+\mathbf b=0}$. Solving gives $\boxed{\mathbf a=(-1,0,0)^{\mathsf T}}$. Since $\mathbf b=-2A\mathbf a$, the remaining constant is
$$
\boxed{C=1+\mathbf a^{\mathsf T}A\mathbf a=3,\qquad
\mathbf x^{\mathsf T}A\mathbf x=3.}
$$
This is <completing the square> for a <quadratic form>.

=== iv
{scope}

==== Solution

An <orthogonal transformation> to the <eigenvectors> preserves distance from the centre and writes the surface as $u^2+v^2+3w^2=3$. It is an <ellipsoid> with <semi-axes> $\sqrt3,\sqrt3,1$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{nearest distance from }\mathbf a=1.}
$$
The original $y$ axis is one of the eigen-directions with eigenvalue one, and the centre has $a_y=0$. Consequently $y^2\le3$, attained when the other centred coordinates vanish, so $\boxed{y_{\max}=\sqrt3}$.

=== v
{scope}

==== Solution

For $A'$, the centre and constant remain $\mathbf a=(-1,0,0)$ and $C=3$. Its <eigenvalues> are $3,1,-1$, so rotated coordinates put the surface in the form $3u^2+w^2-y^2=3$. This is a <one-sheet hyperboloid>. Since $3\|\mathbf x\|^2\ge\mathbf x^{\mathsf T}A'\mathbf x=3$, the nearest centre-to-surface distance is at least one, attained in the eigen-direction with eigenvalue three. The coordinate $y$ is unbounded, because arbitrarily large $|y|$ can be balanced by $u$ or $w$. Thus **the nearest distance is one, and there is no maximum value of $y$**.

For the printed nonsymmetric $A''$, use the <symmetric part of a matrix>:
$$
S=\frac{A''+A''^{\mathsf T}}2
=\begin{pmatrix}2&0&1\\0&1&1/2\\1&1/2&2\end{pmatrix},
\qquad \mathbf r^{\mathsf T}A''\mathbf r=\mathbf r^{\mathsf T}S\mathbf r.
$$
The <skew-symmetric part> contributes zero to this scalar. The same centre solves $2S\mathbf a+\mathbf b=0$, and again $C=3$. Sylvester's positive principal minors are $2,2,5/2$, so this surface is an <ellipsoid>.

Let $\lambda_*$ be the largest <eigenvalue> of $S$. Its characteristic equation is $4\lambda^3-20\lambda^2+27\lambda-10=0$, and the <Rayleigh quotient> gives
$$
\boxed{d_{\min}=\sqrt{3/\lambda_*},\qquad
\lambda_*=\frac{5+\sqrt{19}\cos\!\left[\tfrac13\arccos\!\left(\frac{55}{19\sqrt{19}}\right)\right]}{3}.}
$$
For the maximum $y$, the <support function of a centred ellipsoid> gives the bound below. Equivalently, apply the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> in the $S$-weighted <inner product>: $y^2\le3\,\mathbf e_y^{\mathsf T}S^{-1}\mathbf e_y$. The inverse entry is $\mathbf e_y^{\mathsf T}S^{-1}\mathbf e_y=6/5$, and equality is attained for a centred vector proportional to $S^{-1}\mathbf e_y$. Thus
$$
\boxed{y_{\max}=\sqrt{18/5}.}
$$
\Image[2016-resit-quadratic-surfaces.png]
{title=Ellipsoid and hyperboloid geometry after translation to the common centre}

Using the eigenvalues of the raw nonsymmetric $A''$ would be incorrect: it is its symmetric part that determines the surface and its Euclidean geometry.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The vector $(0,1,-1)$ is an <eigenvector> with <eigenvalue> one. The other two orthogonal directions give eigenvalues two and five, leading to
$$
\boxed{V=\begin{pmatrix}
0&1/\sqrt3&2/\sqrt6\\
1/\sqrt2&1/\sqrt3&-1/\sqrt6\\
-1/\sqrt2&1/\sqrt3&-1/\sqrt6
\end{pmatrix},\qquad D=\operatorname{diag}(1,2,5).}
$$
Its columns are an <orthonormal eigenbasis>, so $V^{\mathsf T}V=I$ and $V^{\mathsf T}AV=D$. Multiplying by $V$ and $V^{\mathsf T}$ gives $\boxed{A=VDV^{\mathsf T}}$. Eigenvector signs and the joint order of columns and diagonal entries may be changed consistently.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <matrix exponential> acts entrywise on a diagonal matrix, so
$$
\boxed{e^B=\operatorname{diag}(e^3,e^4,1).}
$$
For the two-dimensional matrix $C$, direct multiplication gives $C^2=-C$, hence $C^n=(-1)^{n-1}C$ for $n\ge1$. Summing its defining <power series> gives
$$
\boxed{e^C=I+(1-e^{-1})C
=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1-e^{-1}&e^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Finally, $e^A=V\operatorname{diag}(e,e^2,e^5)V^{\mathsf T}$, so each diagonal entry is a weighted sum of these exponential eigenvalues, with weights equal to squared eigenvector components:
$$
\boxed{(e^A)_{11}=\frac{e^2}{3}+\frac{2e^5}{3},\qquad
(e^A)_{22}=(e^A)_{33}=\frac e2+\frac{e^2}{3}+\frac{e^5}{6}.}
$$

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

In a submultiplicative <matrix norm>, the exponential series is absolutely convergent. Its <Cauchy product> can therefore be grouped by the total degree:
$$
e^Ae^B=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^n\frac{A^jB^{n-j}}{j!(n-j)!}
=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac1{n!}\sum_{j=0}^n\binom nj A^jB^{n-j}.
$$
When $AB=BA$, the inner sum is $(A+B)^n$ by the <binomial theorem>. Thus $\boxed{e^Ae^B=e^{A+B}}$. The commutation assumption is used precisely in that binomial expansion; absolute convergence alone does not supply it.

== 11
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Assume $k>0$, $m>0$ and $\gamma\ge0$. The <spring constant> $k$ measures restoring <force> per displacement from the equilibrium position $x_0$. The term $-\gamma m v$ is <linear drag>, with damping rate $\gamma$ in inverse-time units. Writing $\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}$, the equation is
$$
\ddot x+\gamma\dot x+\omega_0^2(x-x_0)=0.
$$
For small positive damping it gives <underdamped> motion: oscillations about $x_0$ with a decaying amplitude. If $\gamma=0$, the motion is undamped <simple harmonic motion>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Set $\omega_d=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2/4}>0$. The real solution of the <damped harmonic oscillator> equation is $x-x_0=e^{-\gamma t/2}(A\cos\omega_dt+B\sin\omega_dt)$. The initial displacement fixes $A=a-x_0$, and release from rest gives $B=\gamma A/(2\omega_d)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{x(t)=x_0+(a-x_0)e^{-\gamma t/2}
\left(\cos\omega_dt+\frac{\gamma}{2\omega_d}\sin\omega_dt\right).}
$$
The <exponential decay> rate of the amplitude is $\gamma/2$, while the weak-damping energy decay rate is $\gamma$.

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

The amplitude envelope falls by $e^{-1/2}$ when $e^{-\gamma t/2}=e^{-1/2}$, so $t=1/\gamma$. Dividing by the period $2\pi/\omega_d$ gives
$$
\boxed{N=\frac{\omega_d}{2\pi\gamma}\simeq\frac{\omega_0}{2\pi\gamma}.}
$$
This is a cycle-count estimate in the small-damping regime, not necessarily an integer. With zero damping there is no amplitude decay and no finite such time.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

The weak-damping <mechanical energy> is proportional to the squared oscillation amplitude, so it decays as $e^{-\gamma t}$. Over one period $P=2\pi/\omega_d$, the fractional loss is $1-e^{-\gamma P}\simeq\gamma P$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\frac{E}{\Delta E}\simeq\frac1{\gamma P}
\simeq\frac{\omega_0}{2\pi\gamma}.}
$$
For the release-from-rest solution, comparison at successive corresponding turning points gives the more precise ratio $E/\Delta E=(1-e^{-\gamma P})^{-1}$. The <quality factor> is $Q\simeq2\pi E/\Delta E=\omega_0/\gamma$; the requested energy-to-loss ratio itself has the extra factor $1/(2\pi)$.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

The moving equilibrium produces a <forced harmonic oscillator>:
$$
\ddot x+\gamma\dot x+\omega_0^2x=\omega_0^2d e^{-\alpha t}.
$$
A particular solution is $F e^{-\alpha t}$, where $F=\omega_0^2d/(\alpha^2-\gamma\alpha+\omega_0^2)$. The denominator equals $(\alpha-\gamma/2)^2+\omega_d^2>0$, so it cannot vanish for real $\alpha$ under the given underdamping condition. Imposing both initial conditions gives
$$
\boxed{x(t)=F e^{-\alpha t}+(d-F)e^{-\gamma t/2}
\left[\cos\omega_dt+\frac{\gamma/2-\alpha}{\omega_d}\sin\omega_dt\right].}
$$
For $\alpha=\gamma$, $F=d$, so the entire oscillatory term vanishes:
$$
\boxed{x(t)=d e^{-\gamma t}=x_0(t).}
$$
The body is <tracking an exponentially moving equilibrium without oscillation>. There is no spring extension in this special motion: <linear drag> alone supplies $m\ddot x=\gamma^2md e^{-\gamma t}$. An underdamped homogeneous equation does not require every forced solution to contain an oscillatory transient.
