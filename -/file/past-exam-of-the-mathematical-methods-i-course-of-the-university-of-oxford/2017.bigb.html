== 1

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Since $(1+i)^2=2i$, the two <square roots> are $1+i$ and $-1-i$. The <principal square root> is $1+i$, whose <real part> is
$$
\boxed{1.}
$$
If the notation is meant to include both square-root values, their real parts are $+1$ and $-1$. Stating the principal-branch convention removes that ambiguity.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For $\delta\notin2\pi\mathbb Z$, the finite <geometric series> gives
$$
\sum_{n=1}^N e^{in\delta}
=\frac{e^{i\delta}(1-e^{iN\delta})}{1-e^{i\delta}}
=e^{i(N+1)\delta/2}\frac{\sin(N\delta/2)}{\sin(\delta/2)}.
$$
The exponential prefactor has <modulus> one. Thus
$$
\boxed{\left|\sum_{n=1}^N e^{in\delta}\right|
=\begin{cases}|\sin(N\delta/2)/\sin(\delta/2)|,&\delta\notin2\pi\mathbb Z,\\N,&\delta\in2\pi\mathbb Z.\end{cases}}
$$
At an excluded phase every summand is one, which supplies the removable limiting value directly. Here $N$ is a positive <integer>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

From the exponential definition, $\cos(iu)=\cosh u$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\cos(2i\ln2)=\cosh(\ln4)=\frac{4+1/4}{2}=\frac{17}{8}.}
$$
A cosine of a nonreal argument need not lie between $-1$ and $1$; that bound applies to real arguments.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

An <integrating factor> for $u'+P(x)u=Q(x)$ is a nonzero function $\mu(x)=\exp(\int P(x)dx)$, chosen so that $(\mu u)'=\mu Q$. Integrating this total derivative reduces the first-order <linear ordinary differential equation> to one quadrature.

For the given nonlinear equation, let $u=y^2$, so $u'=2yy'$. Multiplication by $2y$ gives $u'+u=x^2+2x$. Using the <integrating factor> $e^x$, or recognizing the particular solution $u=x^2$, yields $u=x^2+Ce^{-x}$. The initial value gives $C=1$ and selects the positive branch:
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\sqrt{x^2+e^{-x}}.}
$$
The radicand is strictly positive for every real $x$. This solution never reaches the singular denominator $y=0$ of the original equation, so multiplication by $y$ has introduced no forbidden crossing. The negative branch would violate $y(0)=1$.

== 3
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $Hv=\lambda v$ with $v\ne0$ and $H^*=H$. Then $v^*Hv=\lambda v^*v$, while taking the <complex conjugate> gives $v^*Hv=\overline\lambda v^*v$. Since $v^*v>0$, every <eigenvalue> of a <Hermitian matrix> is real.

If $Hv=\lambda v$ and $Hw=\mu w$, then
$$
\lambda\,v^*w=(Hv)^*w=v^*Hw=\mu\,v^*w.
$$
Distinct <eigenvalues> therefore force $v^*w=0$: their <eigenvectors> are orthogonal in the <Hermitian inner product>. A degenerate <eigenvalue> occurs more than once in the spectrum, counted with multiplicity. For a <Hermitian matrix>, its <algebraic multiplicity> equals its <geometric multiplicity>, so degeneracy gives an <eigenspace> of dimension greater than one; an <orthonormal basis> can be chosen within it.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

A <unitary matrix> preserves the <Hermitian inner product> and hence the <norm>. If $Uv=\lambda v$ with $v\ne0$, then
$$
\|v\|^2=\|Uv\|^2=|\lambda|^2\|v\|^2.
$$
Dividing by the positive squared norm gives
$$
\boxed{|\lambda|=1.}
$$
Unlike the Hermitian case, this constrains <eigenvalues> to the <unit circle>, not necessarily the real axis.

== 4
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Subtracting the second equation from the first gives $2x-\lambda z=-4$. With $\lambda=2$ this is $x-z=-2$, whereas the third equation requires $x-z=-2\mu$. Consistency therefore requires
$$
\boxed{\mu=1.}
$$
At that value one parameter remains free:
$$
\boxed{(x,y,z)=(t-2,\,7-5t,\,t),\qquad t\in\mathbb R.}
$$
Thus the solution is not unique. For any other $\mu$ the two required values of $x-z$ conflict and the <linear system> has no solution.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For $\lambda=1$ and $\mu=1/2$, the third equation gives $x=z-1$. Combining it with $2x-z=-4$ gives $z=-2$ and $x=-3$, after which the second equation gives $y=12$. Hence
$$
\boxed{(x,y,z)=(-3,12,-2).}
$$
The <coefficient matrix> has <determinant> $\lambda-2=-1\ne0$, proving uniqueness. Direct substitution gives the required right-hand sides $1$, $5$ and $-1$.

== 5
{scope}

=== Solution

For a <unit vector>, the radial coordinate is $r=1$ and the <spherical coordinates> give
$$
(n_x,n_y,n_z)=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta).
$$
The <Pauli matrices> therefore give
$$
\boxed{H=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&e^{-i\phi}\sin\theta\\e^{i\phi}\sin\theta&-\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The diagonal entries are real and the off-diagonal entries are <complex conjugates>, so $H^*=H$. Its trace is zero and its <determinant> is $-1$, giving <eigenvalues> $+1$ and $-1$. A convenient <orthonormal eigenbasis> is
$$
\boxed{v_+=\binom{\cos(\theta/2)}{e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)},\qquad
v_-=\binom{-\sin(\theta/2)}{e^{i\phi}\cos(\theta/2)}.}
$$
The half-angle identities verify $Hv_\pm=\pm v_\pm$, $v_\pm^*v_\pm=1$ and $v_+^*v_-=0$. Multiplication of either vector by any unit-modulus phase gives an equivalent normalized <eigenvector>. At a pole, the azimuth is undefined geometrically, but the remaining phase freedom makes the chosen expressions consistent with the same one-dimensional <eigenspaces>.

== 6
{scope}

=== Solution

With the <Einstein summation convention>, the <magnetic force> has components $F_i=q\epsilon_{ijk}v_jB_k$. Thus its matrix acting on velocity is
$$
\boxed{M_{ij}=q\epsilon_{ijk}B_k,\qquad
M=q\begin{pmatrix}0&B_z&-B_y\\-B_z&0&B_x\\B_y&-B_x&0\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The <Levi-Civita symbol> changes sign on exchanging $i,j$, so $M^T=-M$; the diagonal vanishes and the matrix has zero <trace>. For nonzero $\mathbf B$, $M\mathbf B=q\mathbf B\times\mathbf B=0$, exhibiting a nontrivial <kernel> and proving noninvertibility. If $\mathbf B=0$ or $q=0$, $M$ is the zero matrix and is again noninvertible.

Equivalently, an odd-dimensional real <antisymmetric matrix> has $\det M=\det M^T=\det(-M)=-\det M$, hence zero <determinant>. The same antisymmetry gives $\mathbf v^TM\mathbf v=0$, expressing that the magnetic force does no work.

== 7
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For $x>0$, use the <Euler differential equation> substitution $t=\ln x$, so $x=e^t$, $xy'=\dot y$ and $x^2y''=\ddot y-\dot y$. The transformed equation is
$$
\ddot y+\dot y-2y=e^t.
$$
Its <characteristic polynomial> factors as $(r-1)(r+2)$. The forcing $e^t$ coincides with a homogeneous solution, so the <method of undetermined coefficients> requires a particular solution $y_p=At e^t$. Substitution gives $3Ae^t=e^t$, hence $A=1/3$. Transforming back,
$$
y(x)=C_1x+C_2x^{-2}+\frac{x\ln x}{3}.
$$
The conditions at $x=1$ give $C_1+C_2=0$ and $C_1-2C_2+1/3=0$, so
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac{x\ln x}{3}-\frac{x}{9}+\frac1{9x^2},\qquad x>0.}
$$
Differentiating confirms both initial values and the original equation. The solution interval containing $x=1$ excludes the singular point $x=0$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Using the <complex forcing method for a real differential equation>, $\operatorname{Im}[(4x+2i)e^{ix}]=4x\sin x+2\cos x$, a suitable complex forcing is $f(x)=(4x+2i)e^{ix}$. Solve $u''+2u=f$ and take its <imaginary part>. Writing $u=p(x)e^{ix}$ reduces the equation to
$$
p''+2ip'+p=4x+2i.
$$
A linear polynomial $p=ax+b$ gives $a=4$ and $b=-6i$. Thus
$$
\boxed{y_p(x)=\operatorname{Im}[(4x-6i)e^{ix}]=4x\sin x-6\cos x.}
$$
For a direct check, $(4x\sin x)''+2(4x\sin x)=8\cos x+4x\sin x$, while $(-6\cos x)''+2(-6\cos x)=-6\cos x$. Their sum is the required forcing. Adding $A\cos(\sqrt2x)+B\sin(\sqrt2x)$ gives the full real solution if needed.

== 8
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Let the columns of the given <unitary matrix> be $u_j$, with $Mu_j=\lambda_ju_j$. Columnwise, $MU=UD$ for $D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)$. Multiplication by $U^*$ and <unitarity> give
$$
\boxed{U^*MU=D.}
$$
The premise includes an <orthonormal eigenbasis>. It is not true that an arbitrary matrix admits such a basis; over $\mathbb C$ it exists precisely for a <normal matrix>. The real symmetric inertia matrix in part (b) does satisfy the required condition.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

In the <quadratic form>, the two equal off-diagonal contributions together supply the mixed term. Comparing coefficients gives
$$
\boxed{M=I_0\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
For a nontrivial physical <moment of inertia> scale take $I_0>0$; then this <inertia tensor> is positive definite.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The normalized vectors $u_1=(1,1)^T/\sqrt2$ and $u_2=(-1,1)^T/\sqrt2$ give <eigenvalues> $I_0$ and $3I_0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{U=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
D=\begin{pmatrix}I_0&0\\0&3I_0\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The columns are orthonormal, so $U^{-1}=U^T=U^*$ and direct multiplication verifies $U^{-1}MU=D$. Signs of individual <eigenvectors> are free; the displayed signs also make $\det U=+1$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

The matrix has the standard <rotation matrix> form $\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$, with $\cos\theta=\sin\theta=1/\sqrt2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\theta=\frac\pi4\pmod{2\pi}.}
$$
With $\omega=U\omega'$, the new first axis points along the original $(1,1)$ direction and is counterclockwise from the original first axis. Specifying this coordinate convention avoids confusing rotation of the axes with the inverse transformation of component columns.

==== iv
{scope}

===== Solution

Substituting $\omega=U\omega'$ and using the diagonalization gives
$$
\boxed{T'(\omega_x',\omega_y')=\frac{I_0}{2}\omega_x'^2+\frac{3I_0}{2}\omega_y'^2.}
$$
For $I_0>0$ and energy level one in the chosen units, its <ellipse> equation is
$$
\frac{\omega_x'^2}{2/I_0}+\frac{\omega_y'^2}{2/(3I_0)}=1.
$$
The major semiaxis has length $a=\sqrt{2/I_0}$ along $u_1=(1,1)/\sqrt2$; the minor semiaxis has length $b=\sqrt{2/(3I_0)}$ along $u_2=(-1,1)/\sqrt2$. The full axis lengths are $2a$ and $2b$. The original-coordinate curve is the same ellipse tilted by $45^\circ$, elongated along $\omega_y=\omega_x$; an orthogonal coordinate change preserves lengths and does not change the ellipse itself.

== 9
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A map between <vector spaces> over the same <field> is linear when $L(av+bw)=aL(v)+bL(w)$ for every $v,w$ and scalars $a,b$. Its <kernel> is $\ker L=\{v\in V:L(v)=0\}$. It contains zero because $L(0)=0$. If $v,w\in\ker L$, then
$$
L(av+bw)=aL(v)+bL(w)=0.
$$
Thus it is closed under linear combinations and is a <vector subspace> of $V$. No injectivity or equality of the dimensions of $V,W$ is needed.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Write both <cross products> with the <Levi-Civita symbol>. Its contraction identity gives
$$
[a\times(b\times c)]_i=\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{klm}a_jb_lc_m
=(\delta_{il}\delta_{jm}-\delta_{im}\delta_{jl})a_jb_lc_m
=b_i(a\cdot c)-c_i(a\cdot b).
$$
Therefore the <vector triple product> identity is
$$
\boxed{a\times(b\times c)=(a\cdot c)b-(a\cdot b)c.}
$$
The order matters: the <cross product> is not associative, and exchanging the two nested arrangements changes the expression.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The <vector triple product> with $|u|=1$ gives
$$
f(r)=r-u(u\cdot r),\qquad\boxed{A=I-uu^T,\quad A_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-u_iu_j.}
$$
This is the <orthogonal projection onto a plane> perpendicular to $u$, so it is a <linear map>. Its <image> is the plane $u^\perp$ through the origin, while its <kernel> is the normal line $\operatorname{span}\{u\}$. In components,
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1-u_1^2&-u_1u_2&-u_1u_3\\-u_1u_2&1-u_2^2&-u_2u_3\\-u_1u_3&-u_2u_3&1-u_3^2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The equation $Ar=r$ is equivalent to $u(u\cdot r)=0$, hence to $u\cdot r=0$. Its solution set is precisely the two-dimensional plane $u^\perp$, with infinitely many nonzero vectors, not merely the normal line. These are the <eigenvectors> with <eigenvalue> one; the normal direction has <eigenvalue> zero.

== 10
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For two nonparallel vectors $v_1,v_2\in\mathbb R^2$, a nontrivial relation $av_1+bv_2=0$ would make one a scalar multiple of the other. They are therefore <linearly independent>. Two independent vectors in a two-dimensional <vector space> form a <basis>.

Likewise, three vectors in $\mathbb R^3$ that are not contained in one plane through the origin cannot obey a nontrivial <linear dependence>: such a relation would express one as a combination of the other two, or already make that pair dependent. Thus they are independent and form a <basis> of $\mathbb R^3$. The plane condition here is linear coplanarity with the origin, as required for vectors, rather than affine coplanarity of three arbitrary points.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $\Delta=v_1\cdot(v_2\times v_3)\ne0$, since the original vectors form a <basis>. The <reciprocal basis> is
$$
\boxed{u^1=\frac{v_2\times v_3}{\Delta},\qquad
u^2=\frac{v_3\times v_1}{\Delta},\qquad
u^3=\frac{v_1\times v_2}{\Delta}.}
$$
Taking the <dot product> with each $v_j$ gives $u^i\cdot v_j=\delta_{ij}$, using orthogonality of a <cross product> and cyclic symmetry of the <scalar triple product>. If $\sum_i a_i u^i=0$, taking its <dot product> with $v_j$ yields $a_j=0$. The three reciprocal vectors are therefore <linearly independent> and form a <basis> of $\mathbb R^3$.

Equivalently, if $V$ has columns $v_j$ and $U$ columns $u^i$, then $U^TV=I$, so $U=V^{-T}$. In particular any vector has coordinates $r=\sum_i(r\cdot u^i)v_i$, even when the original basis is not orthonormal.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Provided $p,q$ are <linearly independent>, their linear combinations fill a two-dimensional direction space. Adding $r_0$ translates it to the plane through $r_0$. The <unit normal> and constant are
$$
\boxed{n=\frac{p\times q}{|p\times q|},\qquad c_0=n\cdot r_0.}
$$
Then $n\cdot(r_0+t_1p+t_2q)=c_0$ for every pair of parameters. Conversely a point satisfying $n\cdot r=c_0$ differs from $r_0$ by a vector in $n^\perp=\operatorname{span}\{p,q\}$, proving that the two descriptions give exactly the same plane.

For the three points, choose $r_0=(3,1,-2)$, $p=(-4,1,6)$ and $q=(-1,-2,3)$. Their <cross product> is $(15,6,9)=3(5,2,3)$, which is nonzero. The plane therefore has equation
$$
\boxed{5x+2y+3z=11.}
$$
Each supplied point satisfies it. Its <unit normal> is $(5,2,3)/\sqrt{38}$, up to an overall sign, and $c_0=11/\sqrt{38}$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $s=(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ be the sphere centre. The circle centre is its perpendicular projection onto the plane:
$$
\boxed{h=s-(n\cdot s-c_0)n,\qquad d=|s-h|=|n\cdot s-c_0|.}
$$
For the specific plane this is
$$
d=\frac{|5x_0+2y_0+3z_0-11|}{\sqrt{38}},\qquad
h=s-\frac{5x_0+2y_0+3z_0-11}{38}(5,2,3).
$$
For a point on the section, its displacement from $h$ lies in the plane and is perpendicular to $s-h$. The <Pythagorean theorem> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{r=\sqrt{R^2-d^2}.}
$$
A genuine circle requires $d<R$. At $d=R$ the section is a single tangent point, and at $d>R$ the plane misses the sphere. A central cut $d=0$ gives a great circle of radius $R$.
