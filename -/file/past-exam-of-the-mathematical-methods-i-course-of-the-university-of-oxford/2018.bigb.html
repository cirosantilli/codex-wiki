Put $w=\operatorname{artanh}z$. The <hyperbolic tangent> identity $z=(e^{2w}-1)/(e^{2w}+1)$ gives $e^{2w}=(1+z)/(1-z)$. Taking a compatible branch of the <complex logarithm> gives
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{artanh}z=\frac12\log\frac{1+z}{1-z}.}
$$
As an identity for a single-valued <inverse hyperbolic tangent>, this requires consistent <branch cuts>; one cannot choose unrelated logarithm branches on the two sides. Near $z=0$ the branch with value zero is fixed by $\operatorname{artanh}0=0$. The full inverse relation also allows $w\mapsto w+i\pi k$, $k\in\mathbb Z$, and $z=\pm1$ are singular points.

Expand the <sine> of the <complex number> using <hyperbolic functions>:
$$
\sin(x+iy)=\sin x\cosh y+i\cos x\sinh y.
$$
Its squared <complex modulus> is
$$
|\sin(x+iy)|^2=\sin^2x\cosh^2y+\cos^2x\sinh^2y=\sin^2x+\sinh^2y\geq\sin^2x.
$$
Taking nonnegative <square roots> proves $\boxed{|\sin z|\geq|\sin x|}$. Equality holds exactly when $y=0$.

Use the <Levi-Civita symbol> and <Einstein summation convention>. The <tensor contraction> $\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{klm}=\delta_{il}\delta_{jm}-\delta_{im}\delta_{jl}$, expressed through the <Kronecker delta>, gives
$$
\bigl(a\times(b\times c)\bigr)_i=\epsilon_{ijk}a_j\epsilon_{klm}b_lc_m
=b_i a_jc_j-c_i a_jb_j.
$$
The last two contractions are <dot products>, proving the <vector triple product> identity $\boxed{a\times(b\times c)=(a\cdot c)b-(a\cdot b)c}$.

Then the displayed <tensor contraction> is $(a_ic_i)b_k-(a_ib_i)c_k=(a\cdot c)b_k-(a\cdot b)c_k$, precisely the $k$th <vector component> of $a\times(b\times c)$ by the <vector triple product> identity. The <matrix> $M$ need not be a <symmetric matrix>.

The <characteristic equation> of the <damped harmonic oscillator> is $r^2+\gamma r+\omega_0^2=0$. In the underdamped case its <roots> are $-\gamma/2\pm i\omega_d$, where $\omega_d=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2/4}$. Thus the exact real solution is $y=Ae^{-\gamma t/2}\cos(\omega_dt+\alpha)$. For $\gamma\ll\omega_0$, $\omega_d=\omega_0+O(\gamma^2/\omega_0)$, giving the requested leading approximation
$$
\boxed{y(t)\simeq y_0e^{-\gamma t/2}\cos(\omega_0t+\alpha).}
$$
For <mass> $m$ and <spring constant> $m\omega_0^2$, the <mechanical energy> is $E=\tfrac12m(\dot y^2+\omega_0^2y^2)$. Differentiating and using the <differential equation> gives $\dot E=-m\gamma\dot y^2$. In particular, <energy decay of a viscously damped harmonic oscillator> is not an exact pointwise <exponential decay>: $\dot E=0$ at a turning point. To leading order in $\gamma/\omega_0$, the <period average> is
$$
\boxed{\overline E(t)\simeq\frac12m\omega_0^2y_0^2e^{-\gamma t},\qquad \overline P_{\mathrm{loss}}\simeq\gamma\overline E.}
$$
The <quality factor> is $Q=2\pi\overline E/(\text{energy lost in one cycle})$. Using the <period> $T\simeq2\pi/\omega_0$ and the small fractional loss $\gamma T$ gives $\boxed{Q\simeq\omega_0/\gamma}$. Equivalently, the energy-decay definition $Q=\omega_0\overline E/\overline P_{\mathrm{loss}}$ yields the same leading result. Replacing $\omega_d$ by $\omega_0$ in the oscillatory phase is not uniform over arbitrarily long times; its phase error is $O(\gamma^2t/\omega_0)$.

For $n\ne1$ and a nonzero solution, set $v=y^{1-n}$. Since $v'=(1-n)y^{-n}y'$, multiplying the <Bernoulli differential equation> by $(1-n)y^{-n}$ gives the <linear ordinary differential equation>
$$
\boxed{v'+(1-n)p(x)v=(1-n)q(x).}
$$
An <integrating factor> $\mu=\exp((1-n)\int p(x)\,dx)$ gives $v=\mu^{-1}(C+(1-n)\int\mu q\,dx)$. Recover $y$ from $y^{1-n}=v$ on an appropriate real or complex branch. This substitution can omit the zero solution, which must be checked in the original equation. For $n=1$, no substitution is needed: $y'+(p-q)y=0$ is already a <linear ordinary differential equation>.

{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Work on an interval with $x\ne0$. When $n=3$, let $v=y^{-2}$. The resulting <linear ordinary differential equation> and its <integrating factor> are
$$
v'-\frac4xv=-2x^2,\qquad \mu=x^{-4},\qquad (x^{-4}v)'=-2x^{-2}.
$$
Integration gives $v=2x^3+Cx^4$. The real nonzero solutions and the separately checked zero solution are therefore
$$
\boxed{y=\pm(2x^3+Cx^4)^{-1/2}\quad\text{where }2x^3+Cx^4>0;\qquad y\equiv0.}
$$
For $n=1$, separation in the <linear ordinary differential equation> gives $y'/y=x^2-2/x$, hence
$$
\boxed{y=Cx^{-2}e^{x^3/3}.}
$$
Here $C=0$ already includes the zero solution. Division by $x$ is why these formulas are stated on intervals excluding zero; the original equation requires $y(0)=0$ if a classical solution is to extend through zero.

Write the <matrix trace> in <Einstein summation convention>:
$$
\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ji}=\sum_{j,i}B_{ji}A_{ij}=\boxed{\operatorname{tr}(BA)}.
$$
The entries are <scalars>, so their multiplication commutes, and the two summed indices may be relabelled. The argument works for square <matrices> of any finite size, and also for rectangular $A,B$ of compatible opposite shapes.

= Solution

Let $Av=\lambda v$ with $v\ne0$. A vanishing <commutator> gives $A(Bv)=B(Av)=\lambda Bv$, so $Bv$ belongs to the <eigenspace> $E_\lambda$. This is <eigenspace invariance for commuting linear maps>. A <nondegenerate eigenvalue> has a one-dimensional <eigenspace>, so $Bv=\beta v$ for a <scalar> $\beta$; $\beta=0$ is allowed. Therefore **$v$ is also an <eigenvector> of $B$**. Without the simple-<eigenvalue> condition, $B$ can mix <vectors> inside a larger <eigenspace>, and an arbitrarily chosen <eigenvector> of $A$ need not be an <eigenvector> of $B$.

= Solution
{parent=c}

For <Hermitian matrices>, $A^\dagger=A$ and $B^\dagger=B$, so
$$
[A,B]^\dagger=B^\dagger A^\dagger-A^\dagger B^\dagger=-[A,B].
$$
The <commutator> is an <anti-Hermitian matrix>. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\alpha=i,\qquad C=-i[A,B]}
$$
makes $C^\dagger=C$. More generally, any nonzero purely imaginary $\alpha$ works. For a nonzero <commutator> this is also necessary if $C$ is to be <Hermitian>: comparing the <Hermitian adjoint> equations gives $(\overline\alpha+\alpha)C=0$.

= Solution

The <characteristic polynomial> factors as $\det(\lambda I-A)=(\lambda-1)(\lambda^2-1)$. Thus $\boxed{\lambda=1\text{ twice},\quad\lambda=-1\text{ once}}$ in <algebraic multiplicity>. An <orthonormal basis> of corresponding <eigenvectors> is
$$
v_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix},\quad
v_2=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\-i\end{pmatrix}\quad(\lambda=1),\qquad
v_3=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\i\end{pmatrix}\quad(\lambda=-1).
$$
Direct multiplication verifies $Av_1=v_1$, $Av_2=v_2$, $Av_3=-v_3$. Their mutual <Hermitian inner products> vanish and each has <norm> one, which proves <linear independence>. For the repeated <eigenvalue>, <Gaussian elimination> gives
$$
A-I=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\0&-1&i\\0&-i&-1\end{pmatrix}
\ \longrightarrow\ 
\begin{pmatrix}0&1&-i\\0&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Hence $\boxed{\operatorname{rank}(A-I)=1,\quad\dim\ker(A-I)=2}$. The second equality follows from the <rank-nullity theorem> and agrees with the two-dimensional <eigenspace>. A repeated <eigenvalue> alone would not imply a two-dimensional <eigenspace> for an arbitrary <matrix>; here the <Hermitian matrix> is <diagonalizable>.

For a purely imaginary candidate $z=it$, $t\in\mathbb R$, the imaginary part of the <polynomial> is $2t(t^2-4)$. The candidate $t=0$ is not a <root>, while $t=\pm2$ both make the real part zero. The resulting <factorization> into <quadratic polynomials> gives
$$
f(z)=(z^2+4)(z^2-2z+4),\qquad
\boxed{z=2i,-2i,1+i\sqrt3,1-i\sqrt3.}
$$
The <roots of a polynomial> sum to $0+2=2$ and have product $4\cdot4=16$, as required by <Vieta's formulas> for a monic quartic with cubic coefficient $-2$ and constant coefficient $16$.

= Solution

Expand $(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^4$ using <De Moivre's theorem>, then divide its imaginary and real parts by $\cos^4\theta$. With $u=\tan\theta$, this gives
$$
\boxed{\tan4\theta=\frac{4u-4u^3}{1-6u^2+u^4}.}
$$
The quotient holds where its denominator and $\cos\theta$ are nonzero. At $\theta=\pi/8$, use the undivided real-part identity: $\cos4\theta=0$ implies $u^4-6u^2+1=0$. Thus $u^2=3\pm2\sqrt2$. Since $0<\tan(\pi/8)<1$, the smaller positive value applies:
$$
\boxed{\tan22.5^\circ=\sqrt{3-2\sqrt2}=\sqrt2-1.}
$$
This limiting step avoids treating an undefined value of $\tan4\theta$ as an ordinary finite number.

A <plane> through $r_0$ with nonzero <normal vector> $n$ consists of the <position vectors> $r$ satisfying $(r-r_0)\cdot n=0$. Thus $r\cdot n=d$, with $d=r_0\cdot n$. When $n$ is a <unit vector>, $d$ is the signed distance from the origin in the direction $n$; for an unnormalized <normal vector>, that distance is $d/\|n\|$. The displacement within the <plane> is perpendicular to $n$.

\Image[2018-plane-intersection.png]
{title=A unit <normal vector> and signed plane distance, followed by the <intersection of two planes> as a <straight line>}

The <cross product> of the two <normal vectors> is $(2,6,-3)\times(5,2,-1)=(0,-13,-26)$, so their <intersection of two planes> has direction $(0,1,2)$. Eliminating $2y-z$ gives $x=2$, and substitution then gives $z=2y-2$. Thus the Cartesian constraints and <parametric equation of a straight line> are
$$
\boxed{x=2,\quad z=2y-2;\qquad r=\begin{pmatrix}2\\0\\-2\end{pmatrix}+t\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\2\end{pmatrix},\quad t\in\mathbb R.}
$$
There is no valid division by a zero $x$ component in a symmetric ratio form; retaining $x=2$ explicitly handles that component.

Substitute the <power series> and its termwise <derivatives> into the <ordinary differential equation>. The coefficient of $x^n$, for $n\geq0$, is
$$
(n+2)(n+1)a_{n+2}+[n(n-1)-6n+12]a_n=0.
$$
The bracket factors as $(n-3)(n-4)$, so the <power-series solution of a differential equation> satisfies
$$
\boxed{a_{n+2}=-\frac{(n-3)(n-4)}{(n+1)(n+2)}a_n.}
$$
The odd and even <recurrence relations> terminate: $a_3=-a_1$, $a_5=0$, and $a_2=-6a_0$, $a_4=a_0$, $a_6=0$. Thus the full solution is $y=a_0(1-6x^2+x^4)+a_1(x-x^3)$. The two <polynomials> have independent initial data at zero, and the leading coefficient $1+x^2$ never vanishes on the real axis, so every real <solution of a differential equation> for this equation is their <linear combination> on the entire real axis. The given initial data select $a_0=0$, $a_1=1$:
$$
\boxed{y(x)=x-x^3.}
$$

The $j$th column of the <matrix representation of a linear map> is the <coordinate vector> of the image of the $j$th <basis vector>:
$$
\phi(u_j)=\sum_iA_{ij}u_i.
$$
Define $C$ by $w_j=\sum_iC_{ij}u_i$, so its columns express the new <basis vectors> in the old <basis>. For every <vector>, $[v]_u=C[v]_w$. Applying $\phi$ in either coordinate system gives $AC[v]_w=CA'[v]_w$, proving the <change of basis> formula
$$
\boxed{A'=C^{-1}AC.}
$$
To make $A'$ diagonal, choose the columns of $C$ to be <linearly independent> <eigenvectors> of $A$, expressed in the $u$ <basis>, and put their corresponding <eigenvalues> on the diagonal in the same order. This is possible precisely when $A$ is a <diagonalizable matrix> over the stated <field>; an <eigenbasis> need not exist over an arbitrary <field>.

= Solution

The <linear system of differential equations> has <matrix>
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}0&\alpha\\-\alpha&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
C=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\i&-i\end{pmatrix},\qquad C^{-1}MC=\begin{pmatrix}i\alpha&0\\0&-i\alpha\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The columns of $C$ are <eigenvectors> of $M$. The <change of basis> $v'=C^{-1}v$ therefore gives $v'_1=(v_1-iv_2)/2$, $v'_2=(v_1+iv_2)/2$, with independent <first-order linear differential equations>
$$
\dot v'_1=i\alpha v'_1,\quad \dot v'_2=-i\alpha v'_2,\qquad
v'_1(t)=K_+e^{i\alpha t},\quad v'_2(t)=K_-e^{-i\alpha t}.
$$
The initial <velocity> gives $K_+=-iv_0/2$, $K_-=iv_0/2$. Transforming back, $v=Cv'$, yields the real <velocity> <vector components>
$$
\boxed{v_1(t)=v_0\sin\alpha t,\qquad v_2(t)=v_0\cos\alpha t.}
$$
These also cover $\alpha=0$. Complex <coordinates> have decoupled a real rotation without making the physical <velocity> complex.

Adopt the convention that an <inner product> is linear in its second slot. Over $\mathbb R$, it is a <symmetric bilinear form> with $\langle v,v\rangle\geq0$, equality only for $v=0$. Over $\mathbb C$, a <Hermitian inner product> obeys
$$
\langle u,av+bw\rangle=a\langle u,v\rangle+b\langle u,w\rangle,\qquad
\langle u,v\rangle=\overline{\langle v,u\rangle},\qquad
\langle v,v\rangle>0\quad(v\ne0).
$$
These imply conjugate linearity in the first slot: $\langle au+bv,w\rangle=\overline a\langle u,w\rangle+\overline b\langle v,w\rangle$. The strict positivity condition distinguishes an <inner product> from a general <Hermitian form>.

{parent=10}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Expand the <vector> in the <orthonormal basis>, $v=\sum_jv_je_j$. Using linearity in the second slot and the <Kronecker delta> gives
$$
\boxed{\langle e_i,v\rangle=\sum_jv_j\langle e_i,e_j\rangle=v_i.}
$$
Expanding both <vectors> and using conjugate linearity in the first slot therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\langle u,v\rangle=\sum_{i,j}\overline{u_i}v_j\langle e_i,e_j\rangle=\sum_i\overline{u_i}v_i.}
$$
An <orthonormal set> that is not a <basis> would recover only the coordinates of the <orthogonal projection> onto its <linear span>; here the $n$ <vectors> span $\mathbb C^n$.

The two <reciprocal bases> satisfy $\langle u_i,w_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}$. Expanding each <vector> in its specified <basis> and using real bilinearity gives
$$
\boxed{\langle a,b\rangle=\left\langle\sum_i a_iu_i,\sum_jb_jw_j\right\rangle=\sum_i a_ib_i.}
$$
The simple sum-of-products formula holds because the two <coordinate vectors> use paired <reciprocal bases>. It does not require either <basis> to be <orthogonal>, and would generally fail if both sets of components were taken in the same nonorthogonal <basis>.

= Solution

Take the <inner product> with $u_i$ in $a=\sum_ja'_jw_j$. The <reciprocal basis> condition gives $a'_i=\langle u_i,a\rangle$. Expanding instead in the $u$ <basis> proves the <reciprocal-basis coordinate transformation>
$$
\boxed{a'_i=\sum_jG_{ij}a_j,\qquad G_{ij}=\langle u_i,u_j\rangle.}
$$
Thus $G$ is the <Gram matrix> of the original <basis>. It is a <positive-definite matrix>, so this <change of basis> is invertible.

In the specified two-dimensional Cartesian frame,
$$
u_1=(1,0),\qquad u_2=\left(\frac12,\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right).
$$
Solving the four <dot product> conditions gives
$$
\boxed{w_1=\left(1,-\frac1{\sqrt3}\right),\qquad w_2=\left(0,\frac2{\sqrt3}\right).}
$$
They are perpendicular to $u_2$ and $u_1$, respectively, with lengths adjusted so the other <dot product> is one. Equating the Cartesian components of $a=a_1u_1+a_2u_2=a'_1w_1+a'_2w_2$ gives
$$
a_x=a_1+\frac12a_2=a'_1,\qquad
a_y=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}a_2=\frac{-a'_1+2a'_2}{\sqrt3}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}a'_1\\a'_2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1/2\\1/2&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_1\\a_2\end{pmatrix},}
$$
which agrees entry by entry with the <Gram matrix> computed directly from the two unit <basis vectors>.
