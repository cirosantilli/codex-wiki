==== Solution

The <complex exponential> factors as $e^{(3+i)(a+ib)}=e^{3a-b}e^{i(a+3b)}$. Its positive real prefactor is its <modulus>, while its phase gives its <complex argument>:
$$
\boxed{|e^{(3+i)z}|=e^{3a-b},\qquad\arg(e^{(3+i)z})=a+3b\pmod{2\pi}.}
$$
For the principal <complex argument>, reduce $a+3b$ to the chosen principal interval.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

The <polar form> $1+i=\sqrt2e^{i\pi/4}$ gives $(1+i)^6=-8i$. Multiplication therefore scales the <modulus> by $8$ and rotates the <complex argument> through $-\pi/2$:
$$
\boxed{|(1+i)^6z|=8\sqrt{a^2+b^2},\qquad\arg((1+i)^6z)=\arg(a+ib)-\frac\pi2\pmod{2\pi}.}
$$
Equivalently the result is $8b-8ia$, whose <complex argument> is $\operatorname{atan2}(-a,b)$. If $a=b=0$, its <modulus> is zero and its <complex argument> is undefined.

== 3
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

The <determinant> is an <alternating multilinear map> of its rows, so exchanging two rows gives **the negative of the original determinant**: $\det A\mapsto-\det A$. This also holds when the <determinant> is zero.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

**The determinant is unchanged by transposition**: $\boxed{\det(A^T)=\det A}$. In the <Leibniz formula for determinants>, replacing each <permutation> by its inverse interchanges the row and column roles without changing its <sign of a permutation>.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

Scaling the whole <matrix> scales all $n$ rows. The <multilinearity> of the <determinant> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\det(cA)=c^n\det A.}
$$
This includes $c=0$ for $n\ge1$.

== 4
{scope}

=== Solution

Where $y\ne0$ and $x^2\ne1$, the <separable differential equation> gives
$$
\frac{y\,dy}{2y^2+1}=\frac{x\,dx}{x^2-1},\qquad
\frac14\log(2y^2+1)=\frac12\log|x^2-1|+C.
$$
The requested point selects the <implicit solution> $2y^2+1=(x^2-1)^2$. The two real branches approaching that point are
$$
\boxed{y=\pm\frac{x}{\sqrt2}\sqrt{x^2-2},\qquad x>\sqrt2.}
$$
There is an important <initial value problem> qualification: **the differential equation as printed has no classical solution through the specified initial point**. Its right side is undefined at $y=0$, and these branches have an infinite one-sided <derivative> as $x\downarrow\sqrt2$. They satisfy the <differential equation> on $x>\sqrt2$ and extend continuously to the requested point. Multiplying by the vanishing denominator produces an <implicit differential equation> with a larger domain; it does not repair the original <initial value problem>.

== 5
{scope}

=== Solution

The <characteristic polynomial> of the <Hermitian matrix> is
$$
\det(A-\lambda I)=\left(\frac32-\lambda\right)^2-\frac{25}{4}=(\lambda-4)(\lambda+1).
$$
Solving the two <eigenvector> equations and using the <Hermitian inner product> to normalize gives
$$
\boxed{\lambda_1=4,\quad v_1=\frac1{\sqrt2}\binom{i}{1};\qquad
\lambda_2=-1,\quad v_2=\frac1{\sqrt2}\binom{-i}{1}.}
$$
Indeed $v_1^\dagger v_2=0$ and both <norms> equal one. Multiplication of either <eigenvector> by any unit <phase factor> gives an equally valid normalized choice.

== 6
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

For an <idempotent matrix>, $A^k=A$ for every integer $k\ge1$. Thus any scalar <polynomial> reduces to
$$
\boxed{p(A)=p(0)I+[p(1)-p(0)]A,}
$$
or equivalently $aI+bA$. This expression describes all <polynomials> in $A$, although its two coefficients need not be unique if $A=0$ or $A=I$.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

If $Av=\lambda v$ for a nonzero <eigenvector>, <idempotence> gives $\lambda^2v=A^2v=Av=\lambda v$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\lambda\in\{0,1\}.}
$$
These are the only possible <eigenvalues>; either may be absent. In fact $v=Av+(I-A)v$ decomposes every <vector> into the <image> and <kernel> of $A$, so every <idempotent matrix> is <diagonalizable> even when it is not an <orthogonal projection>.

{scope}

=== Solution

The common real <variable> gives the <parametric equation> of the <line>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf r=(2,-1,4)+t(1,3,2),\qquad t\in\mathbb R.}
$$
Write $p=(2,-1,4)$ and $u=(1,3,2)$. The point closest to the origin is <orthogonal> to $u$, so $t_*=-p\cdot u/|u|^2=-7/14=-1/2$. Its position is $(3/2,-5/2,3)$. The <distance from a point to a line in three dimensions> is therefore
$$
\boxed{d=\sqrt{|p|^2-\frac{(p\cdot u)^2}{|u|^2}}=\sqrt{\frac{35}{2}}=\frac{\sqrt{70}}2.}
$$

== 8
{scope}

=== Solution

Subtracting the first equation from the third gives $x-z=3/2$. The second equation then becomes $(\alpha-2)x=\beta-3$, and the third fixes $y=(7-7x)/5$. The <linear system> has the following three consistency cases:
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\alpha\ne2:\quad &(x,y,z)=\left(\frac{\beta-3}{\alpha-2},\frac{7(\alpha-\beta+1)}{5(\alpha-2)},\frac{2\beta-3\alpha}{2(\alpha-2)}\right);\\
\alpha=2,\ \beta=3:\quad &(x,y,z)=\left(t,\frac{7(1-t)}5,t-\frac32\right),\quad t\in\mathbb R;\\
\alpha=2,\ \beta\ne3:\quad &\text{no solution}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The first case is a unique solution, the second is an <affine line in a vector space> of solutions, and the third is an <inconsistent linear system>.

=== Solution

Normalize the <normal vector> $(2,4,3)$, whose <Euclidean norm> is $\sqrt{29}$. The <plane> has the form
$$
\boxed{\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n=d,\qquad\mathbf n=\frac{(2,4,3)}{\sqrt{29}},\quad d=\frac3{\sqrt{29}}.}

The scalar $d$ is the signed perpendicular <distance> from the origin in the chosen <unit normal> direction; the unsigned <distance> is $|d|$. The nearest point is $d\mathbf n$.

== 10
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For $n\ge1$, use $\sin^2(nx)=[1-\cos(2nx)]/2$. The <inner product> is
$$
\boxed{\langle f_n,f_n\rangle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sin^2(nx)\,dx=\pi\quad(n\ge1).}
$$
The stated nonnegative index range also includes $n=0$. Since $f_0$ is the <zero function>, $\langle f_0,f_0\rangle=0$, so it must be removed from any proposed <basis>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The <product-to-sum formula> gives
$$
\langle f_n,f_m\rangle=\frac12\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\big[\cos((n-m)x)-\cos((n+m)x)\big]dx=\boxed{0\quad(n\ne m).}
$$
When both indices are positive the two integer <frequencies> integrate to zero. If either index is zero the integrand already vanishes. This is <Fourier orthogonality>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For positive integer indices, distinct members are <orthogonal> and each has nonzero squared <norm> $\pi$. Thus $\{\sin(nx):n\ge1\}$ is an <orthogonal basis> in the <Hilbert space> of <odd> <L2 functions> on $(-\pi,\pi)$. The zero-index member must be omitted. More precisely this is a complete <Fourier sine basis>, with generally infinite <L2 norm convergence>, rather than a <Hamel basis> with finite linear combinations. A claim about arbitrary real <functions> requires suitable integrability and a specified sense of <convergence>.

=== Solution

Taking the <inner product> with $f_m$ and using <Fourier orthogonality> isolates $a_m$. Since $x\sin(mx)$ is <even>, <integration by parts> gives
$$
\pi a_m=2\int_0^\pi x\sin(mx)\,dx=-\frac{2\pi(-1)^m}{m}.
$$
Consequently the <Fourier sine series> is
$$
\boxed{x=2\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}n\sin(nx),\qquad-\pi<x<\pi.}
$$
The <series> converges to $x$ at each interior point and in the <L2 norm>. Its periodic extension has jumps at $\pm\pi$, where the <Fourier series> instead converges to the midpoint value zero. The overshoot near those jumps is the <Gibbs phenomenon>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Every finite sum of the <sine> modes is <odd>, and so is a limit in the <L2 norm> of such sums. In contrast, $x^2-1$ is a nonzero <even function>. Its <inner product> with every <odd> <sine> mode vanishes, so all its proposed <Fourier sine series> coefficients would be zero. **It cannot be represented by this basis on the symmetric interval.** An <even> <Fourier cosine series> is the appropriate alternative there.

== 11
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $\mu=|b|^2>0$ and $\lambda=a\cdot b$. Expanding the nonnegative squared <Euclidean norm> of $c=\mu a-\lambda b$ gives
$$
0\le|c|^2=\mu^2|a|^2-2\mu\lambda^2+\lambda^2|b|^2
=\mu\big(|a|^2|b|^2-(a\cdot b)^2\big).
$$
Dividing by $\mu$ and taking nonnegative <square roots> proves the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>:
$$
\boxed{|a\cdot b|\le|a|\,|b|.}
$$
Equality holds exactly when $c=0$, equivalently when the nonzero <vectors> $a,b$ are <linearly dependent>.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

Expanding the squared <Euclidean norm> and applying the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives
$$
|a+b|^2=|a|^2+2a\cdot b+|b|^2
\le|a|^2+2|a|\,|b|+|b|^2=(|a|+|b|)^2.
$$
Both sides are nonnegative, so taking <square roots> proves the <triangle inequality>, $\boxed{|a+b|\le|a|+|b|}$. For nonzero <vectors>, equality requires a positive real proportionality factor.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

With conjugation in its first slot, a complex <inner product> has <conjugate symmetry>, <linearity> in its second slot, and <positive definiteness>. For the proposed <Hermitian inner product>, direct expansion gives
$$
\langle z,y\rangle=\overline{\langle y,z\rangle},\qquad
\langle z,\alpha y+\beta w\rangle=\alpha\langle z,y\rangle+\beta\langle z,w\rangle,\qquad
\langle z,z\rangle=\sum_j|z_j|^2\ge0.
$$
The last expression vanishes exactly for the <zero vector>. <Conjugate symmetry> and <linearity> also give <antilinearity> in the first slot: $\langle\alpha z+\beta w,y\rangle=\bar\alpha\langle z,y\rangle+\bar\beta\langle w,y\rangle$. These verify all three axioms and the associated first-slot consequence.

=== iv
{scope}

==== Solution

Using the <Hermitian inner product>, the scalar product is $1-i+2i^2=-1-i$. The two squared <norms> are $6$ and $4$. The specified <real angle in a complex inner product space> therefore has
$$
\boxed{\cos\theta=\frac{\operatorname{Re}(-1-i)}{\sqrt6\sqrt4}=-\frac1{2\sqrt6}.}
$$
The real part is essential: the complex scalar product itself need not be real.

=== v
{scope}

==== Solution

Conjugating the first <vector> gives
$$
\langle a,b\rangle=ie^{-ix}+(1-i)e^{inx},\qquad
\operatorname{Re}\langle a,b\rangle=\sin x+\cos(nx)+\sin(nx).
$$
Their squared <norms> are $3$ and $2$, independently of $x,n$. Thus the specified <real angle in a complex inner product space> obeys
$$
\boxed{\cos\theta=\frac{\sin x+\cos(nx)+\sin(nx)}{\sqrt6}.}
$$
The complex <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> ensures that this real quotient lies in $[-1,1]$.

== 12
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

In components the <scalar triple product> is $A_i\epsilon_{ijk}B_jC_k$. A cyclic <permutation> has positive <sign of a permutation>, so $\epsilon_{ijk}=\epsilon_{kij}$. Relabelling the summed indices therefore gives
$$
A_i\epsilon_{ijk}B_jC_k=\epsilon_{kij}A_iB_jC_k=(A\times B)_kC_k,
$$
which proves $A\cdot(B\times C)=(A\times B)\cdot C$. Equivalently both expressions are the same three-column <determinant>.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

Apply the <contraction of two Levi-Civita symbols> to the <cross product>:
$$
[A\times(B\times C)]_i=\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{klm}A_jB_lC_m
=(\delta_{il}\delta_{jm}-\delta_{im}\delta_{jl})A_jB_lC_m.
$$
Thus the <vector triple product identity> is
$$
\boxed{A\times(B\times C)=B(A\cdot C)-C(A\cdot B).}
$$

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

Three <vectors> form a <basis> of $\mathbb R^3$ exactly when they are <linearly independent>. The <determinant> with those <vectors> as columns is their <scalar triple product>, so the condition is
$$
\boxed{A\cdot(B\times C)\ne0.}
$$
Geometrically their <parallelepiped> must have nonzero <volume>.

=== iv
{scope}

==== Solution

If two indices of the <n-dimensional Levi-Civita symbol> are equal, exchanging them leaves the component unchanged. <Antisymmetry of a multilinear map> simultaneously negates it, so $\epsilon=-\epsilon$. Over the real numbers this implies $\boxed{\epsilon=0}$.

=== v
{scope}

==== Solution

If all $n$ indices are distinct and each lies in $\{1,\ldots,n\}$, their list is a <permutation> of $(1,\ldots,n)$. Sorting it takes some number $N$ of transpositions. The <n-dimensional Levi-Civita symbol> equals $(-1)^N$ because its sorted component is $1$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\epsilon_{i_1\cdots i_n}=\begin{cases}
0,&\text{some indices repeat},\\
+1,&(i_1,\ldots,i_n)\text{ is an even permutation},\\
-1,&(i_1,\ldots,i_n)\text{ is an odd permutation}.
\end{cases}}
$$
The <parity of a permutation> is independent of the chosen sequence of transpositions.

=== vi
{scope}

==== Solution

Reversal has one <inversion of a permutation> for every pair of indices, hence $\binom n2=n(n-1)/2$ <inversions of a permutation>. The <sign of a permutation> gives
$$
\boxed{\epsilon_{n,n-1,\ldots,1}=(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}.}
$$
It is $+1$ for $n\equiv0,1\pmod4$ and $-1$ for $n\equiv2,3\pmod4$.

=== vii
{scope}

==== Solution

Every nonzero component of the <n-dimensional Levi-Civita symbol> is $\pm1$, so its square is one. There are exactly $n!$ <permutations> of the $n$ distinct indices; all remaining index lists have a repeat and contribute zero. Hence
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i_1,\ldots,i_n=1}^n\epsilon_{i_1\cdots i_n}^{\,2}=n!.}
$$

=== viii
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $M$ be the <square matrix> whose $j$th column is $A_j$. The <Leibniz formula for determinants> is precisely the stated contraction:
$$
S=\sum_{i_1,\ldots,i_n}\epsilon_{i_1\cdots i_n}M_{i_1,1}\cdots M_{i_n,n}=\det M.
$$
A <square matrix> has zero <determinant> exactly when its columns are <linearly dependent>, proving the required equivalence. One direction also follows directly from <multilinearity> and <antisymmetry of a multilinear map>: replacing one column by a linear combination of the others leaves only terms with repeated columns, each zero. Conversely, nonzero <determinant> gives an <inverse matrix> and hence independent columns.

== 13
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

For a nonzero real solution use the <Bernoulli power substitution> $u=\sqrt[3]y$. Dividing the <differential equation> by $3u^2$ gives $u'+u/3=x/3$. The <integrating factor> $e^{x/3}$ yields
$$
u=x-3+Ce^{-x/3},\qquad
\boxed{y=(x-3+Ce^{-x/3})^3.}
$$
Direct substitution shows this formula also solves the equation at its zeros. Division temporarily discarded the additional solution $\boxed{y\equiv0}$. Since the real power $y^{2/3}$ is not <locally Lipschitz> at zero, the complete real solution family also permits <waiting-time solutions of a sublinear Bernoulli equation>: join a zero interval to a displayed branch at a root of $x-3+Ce^{-x/3}$. At a joining point the branch and its <derivative> are both zero, so the joined function satisfies the original equation. On each open nonzero interval every solution has the displayed form.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

Set $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. Wherever $r>0$, the <chain rule> and the given <implicit differential equation> give
$$
\frac{dr}{dx}=\frac{x+yy'}r=\frac{x-x+r}r=1.
$$
Thus $r=x+C$ and $C=r-x\ge0$. Squaring without losing the square-root sign restriction gives
$$
\boxed{y^2=2Cx+C^2,\quad C>0,\quad x>-C/2,}
$$
with either sign of $y$ on its <interval> of definition. These <parabolas> satisfy $x+C>0$. Their vertex $(-C/2,0)$ is a regular point as a parametrized curve but cannot belong to a differentiable graph $y(x)$ satisfying the equation: there $yy'=0$ while $-x+r=C>0$. The degenerate case $C=0$ gives the separate solution $\boxed{y=0\text{ for }x\ge0}$ in the differential-form interpretation printed in the paper. It would be lost by dividing the equation by $y$.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

For the <linear differential operator> $L=(D-\alpha)(D-\beta)$, the <exponential function> is an <eigenfunction>: $Le^{\epsilon x}=(\epsilon-\alpha)(\epsilon-\beta)e^{\epsilon x}$. Distinct $\alpha,\beta$ give the two <homogeneous solutions>, while the <method of undetermined coefficients> supplies a particular solution at the distinct forcing exponent $\gamma$:
$$
\boxed{y=C_1e^{\alpha x}+C_2e^{\beta x}+\frac{k e^{\gamma x}}{(\gamma-\alpha)(\gamma-\beta)}.}
$$

=== iv
{scope}

==== Solution

With $z=(D-\beta)y$ and $\gamma=\alpha$, the first equation is $z'-\alpha z=ke^{\alpha x}$. Multiplication by its <integrating factor> gives $(e^{-\alpha x}z)'=k$, hence
$$
\boxed{z=e^{\alpha x}(C+kx).}
$$
This holds whether or not $\beta=\alpha$. If $\beta\ne\alpha$, solving $y'-\beta y=z$ by a second <integrating factor> gives
$$
y=C_1e^{\alpha x}+C_2e^{\beta x}+\frac{kx e^{\alpha x}}{\alpha-\beta}.
$$
The extra factor $x$ is the response to the <resonance condition> at the forcing exponent; the coincident-root case requires the next part.

=== v
{scope}

==== Solution

When all three exponents equal $\alpha$, write $y=e^{\alpha x}v$. The <exponential shift rule for differential operators> gives $(D-\alpha)y=e^{\alpha x}v'$ and $(D-\alpha)^2y=e^{\alpha x}v''$. Thus $v''=k$. Two integrations give
$$
\boxed{y=e^{\alpha x}\left(C_1+C_2x+\frac{kx^2}{2}\right).}
$$
The repeated <characteristic root> supplies $e^{\alpha x}$ and $xe^{\alpha x}$ as <homogeneous solutions>; forcing at that same exponent needs the further $x^2$ factor.
