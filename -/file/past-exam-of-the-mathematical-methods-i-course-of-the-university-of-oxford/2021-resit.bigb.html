The <polar form> is $z=e^{3\pi i/4}$. Interpreting the fractional <complex exponentiation> as all its values, a candidate $w$ satisfies $w^3=z^2=-i$. The three <roots of a complex number> are
$$
w_0=i,\qquad w_1=-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}-\frac i2,\qquad w_2=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}-\frac i2.
$$
They have <arguments> $\pi/2$, $7\pi/6$ and $11\pi/6$, respectively. Since all have <modulus> one, their squared distances from $z$ in the <complex plane> are $|w-z|^2=2-2\cos(\arg w-3\pi/4)$, giving
$$
|w_0-z|^2=2-\sqrt2,\quad
|w_1-z|^2=2-\frac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{2},\quad
|w_2-z|^2=2+\frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{2}.
$$
The last is strictly largest, so the required <root of a complex number> is
$$
\boxed{w=\frac{\sqrt3-i}{2}.}
$$
Restricting <complex exponentiation> to its principal value would give only $i$; the request to choose among roots requires all three values.

The <characteristic polynomial> is
$$
\det(A-\lambda I)=(1-\lambda)(-1-\lambda)+2=\lambda^2+1.
$$
Thus the <eigenvalues> are $i$ and $-i$. There is no real <diagonalization>, because a real <diagonal matrix> would have real <eigenvalues>. Over the <complex numbers>, the two distinct <eigenvalues> give <linearly independent> <eigenvectors>, so the <matrix> is diagonalizable.

For $\lambda=i$, the first row gives $(1-i)x+2y=0$. Choose $x=2$, giving $y=-1+i$. Its standard complex <norm> is $\sqrt{|2|^2+|-1+i|^2}=\sqrt6$; the conjugate <eigenvector> belongs to $-i$. Hence normalized <eigenvectors> and a suitable <change of basis> are
$$
\boxed{\mathbf p_+=\frac1{\sqrt6}\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1+i\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf p_-=\frac1{\sqrt6}\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1-i\end{pmatrix},\qquad
P=\frac1{\sqrt6}\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\-1+i&-1-i\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Directly, $AP=P\operatorname{diag}(i,-i)$ and $\det P=-2i/3\ne0$, so
$$
\boxed{P^{-1}AP=\begin{pmatrix}i&0\\0&-i\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Normalization does not assert that these <eigenvectors> are <orthogonal>.

An <invertible matrix> is a <square matrix> $X$ admitting a <matrix inverse> $X^{-1}$ with $XX^{-1}=X^{-1}X=I$, where $I$ is the <identity matrix>. Equivalently, its associated <linear map> is bijective.

Let $X\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v$ with <eigenvector> $\mathbf v\ne0$. The <eigenvalue> cannot be zero: otherwise applying $X^{-1}$ gives $\mathbf v=0$. Apply $X^{-1}$ to the <eigenvector> equation:
$$
\mathbf v=\lambda X^{-1}\mathbf v,
\qquad\boxed{X^{-1}\mathbf v=\frac1\lambda\mathbf v.}
$$
Thus $1/\lambda$ is an <eigenvalue> of $X^{-1}$ with the same <eigenvector>.

An <exact differential> $M\,dx+N\,dy$ is $d\Phi$ for a scalar <potential function> $\Phi$, so $M=\partial\Phi/\partial x$ and $N=\partial\Phi/\partial y$. Equality $\partial M/\partial y=\partial N/\partial x$ is necessary, and is sufficient on a <simply connected space> when these <partial derivatives> are continuous.

Here, after multiplying the <ordinary differential equation> by $dx$,
$$
M=x+\frac{2x}{y^2},\qquad N=6y-\frac{2x^2}{y^3},\qquad
\frac{\partial M}{\partial y}=-\frac{4x}{y^3}=\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}.
$$
On either half-plane $y>0$ or $y<0$, integrate $M$ with respect to $x$:
$$
\Phi=\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac{x^2}{y^2}+h(y).
$$
Comparison of $\partial\Phi/\partial y$ with $N$ gives $h'(y)=6y$, hence $h(y)=3y^2$ up to a constant. The solution is therefore the family of <level sets>
$$
\boxed{\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac{x^2}{y^2}+3y^2=C,\qquad y\ne0.}
$$
Locally these define differentiable graphs $y(x)$ wherever $N\ne0$, by the <implicit function theorem>. When $N=0$, $M\ne0$ on this domain, so a finite derivative cannot satisfy $M+Ny'=0$ there; such points are <vertical tangents> of the implicit curves, rather than additional classical graph solutions.

<Differentiation> satisfies $D(af+bg)=aD(f)+bD(g)$ for constant scalars $a,b$, so it is a <linear map> on suitable <vector spaces> of differentiable functions. An arbitrary set $S$ need not even be a <vector space>, and an endomorphism interpretation also requires $D(S)\subseteq S$.

For a noninvertible example, take $S=\operatorname{span}\{1,x\}$. Then $D(a+bx)=b$ and the <kernel of a linear map> is the nonzero <linear span> of $1$, so $D:S\to S$ is not injective. The <matrix> in this <basis> is $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$.

For an invertible example, take $S=\operatorname{span}\{e^x,e^{2x}\}$. In this <basis>, $D$ has <matrix> $\operatorname{diag}(1,2)$, and
$$
\boxed{D^{-1}(ae^x+be^{2x})=ae^x+\frac b2e^{2x}.}
$$
Thus <differentiation> is not always invertible; the domain and codomain determine the answer.

For an <invertible matrix> $P$ whose columns give the new <basis> in old coordinates, the new <matrix> is $B=P^{-1}AP$. Multiplicativity of the <determinant> gives
$$
\det B=\det(P^{-1})\det A\det P=\det A,
$$
since $\det(P^{-1})\det P=1$.

To justify the <cyclic property of the trace> directly, for two <square matrices> $R,S$,
$$
\operatorname{tr}(RS)=\sum_{i,j}R_{ij}S_{ji}
=\sum_{j,i}S_{ji}R_{ij}=\operatorname{tr}(SR).
$$
Apply it with $R=P^{-1}A$ and $S=P$:
$$
\boxed{\det(P^{-1}AP)=\det A,\qquad
\operatorname{tr}(P^{-1}AP)=\operatorname{tr}(PP^{-1}A)=\operatorname{tr}A.}
$$
Thus both the <determinant> and <trace> are invariant under <change of basis>.

Use $\mathbf w=-\mathbf u-\mathbf v$ and bilinearity and antisymmetry of the <cross product>:
$$
\mathbf v\times\mathbf w=-\mathbf v\times\mathbf u-\mathbf v\times\mathbf v=\mathbf u\times\mathbf v,
\qquad
\mathbf w\times\mathbf u=-\mathbf u\times\mathbf u-\mathbf v\times\mathbf u=\mathbf u\times\mathbf v.
$$
Consequently,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=\mathbf v\times\mathbf w=\mathbf w\times\mathbf u,\qquad
\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w\}=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u,\mathbf v\}.}
$$
If $\mathbf u,\mathbf v$ are <linearly independent>, this <linear span> is the <plane> through the origin with <normal vector> $\mathbf u\times\mathbf v$. If they are dependent, all three nonzero <vectors> lie on one <line> through the origin, and their <linear span> has dimension one. For example, $\mathbf u=\mathbf v=(1,0,0)$ and $\mathbf w=(-2,0,0)$ obey the hypothesis but span only a <line>. Nonzero <vectors> alone do not force a two-dimensional <linear span>.

Label $\mathbf a$ opposite $\mathbf x_0$, $\mathbf b$ opposite $\mathbf x_1$, $\mathbf c$ opposite $\mathbf x_2$ and $\mathbf d$ opposite $\mathbf x_3$. Since the <tetrahedron> is nondegenerate, its <scalar triple product> $T=\mathbf u_1\cdot(\mathbf u_2\times\mathbf u_3)$ is nonzero. Put $s=T/|T|$ to handle either <orientation of a vector space>. Then the required outward <vectors> are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\mathbf a&=s(\mathbf u_2-\mathbf u_1)\times(\mathbf u_3-\mathbf u_1),\\
\mathbf b&=-s\,\mathbf u_2\times\mathbf u_3,\\
\mathbf c&=-s\,\mathbf u_3\times\mathbf u_1,\\
\mathbf d&=-s\,\mathbf u_1\times\mathbf u_2.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Each <cross product> has magnitude twice the area of its triangular face. The signs are fixed geometrically: $\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf u_1=-|T|<0$, so $\mathbf b$ points away from the opposite vertex; the same test with $\mathbf u_2$ and $\mathbf u_3$ handles $\mathbf c$ and $\mathbf d$. For the remaining face, the displacement toward its opposite vertex is $-\mathbf u_1$, and $\mathbf a\cdot(-\mathbf u_1)=-|T|<0$, again outward.

Expand the first <cross product>:
$$
\mathbf a=s(\mathbf u_2\times\mathbf u_3+\mathbf u_3\times\mathbf u_1+\mathbf u_1\times\mathbf u_2)
=-\mathbf b-\mathbf c-\mathbf d.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbf a+\mathbf b+\mathbf c+\mathbf d=\mathbf0.}
$$
This is <closure of outward face area vectors>, with every face area multiplied by the same factor two. Omitting $s$ would orient all four faces inward when $T<0$.

Write $\Delta=\langle\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf w\rangle=\mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w)$. The displayed definitions require $\Delta\ne0$, equivalently <linear independence> of the original <vectors>. The <vector triple product identity> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf V\times\mathbf W
&=\frac{(\mathbf w\times\mathbf u)\times(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)}{\Delta^2}\\
&=\frac{\mathbf u\bigl((\mathbf w\times\mathbf u)\cdot\mathbf v\bigr)
-\mathbf v\bigl((\mathbf w\times\mathbf u)\cdot\mathbf u\bigr)}{\Delta^2}
=\frac{\mathbf u}{\Delta}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here the first <scalar triple product> is $\Delta$ by cyclic permutation, and the second is zero because a <cross product> is <orthogonal> to both its factors. Thus
$$
\boxed{\langle\mathbf U,\mathbf V,\mathbf W\rangle
=\frac{(\mathbf v\times\mathbf w)\cdot\mathbf u}{\Delta^2}
=\frac1\Delta.}
$$
This is the <reciprocal basis involution> identity. When $\Delta=0$, the proposed <reciprocal basis> is undefined; for example, three <vectors> in the $xy$ <plane> cannot be inserted into the printed quotients.

For a genuinely second-order <linear ordinary differential equation>, assume $a_2\ne0$. Substituting the <exponential function> $f=e^{rt}$ into the <homogeneous linear differential equation> gives $a_2r^2+a_1r+a_0=0$. Oscillations arise precisely when its two <roots of a polynomial> have nonzero <imaginary parts>, namely
$$
\boxed{a_1^2-4a_0a_2<0.}
$$
In that case, writing $\omega=\sqrt{4a_0a_2-a_1^2}/(2|a_2|)>0$, the real <complementary function> is
$$
f(t)=e^{-a_1t/(2a_2)}\bigl(C_1\cos\omega t+C_2\sin\omega t\bigr).
$$
Every nonzero such solution has an oscillatory factor; the oscillation may decay, grow, or keep constant amplitude according to the sign of $a_1/a_2$. This is the <oscillatory-root criterion for a real second-order equation>; decay is not a prerequisite for oscillation.

For completeness, if $a_2=0$ and $a_1\ne0$, only real <exponential functions> occur. If $a_2=a_1=0$ and $a_0\ne0$, only $f=0$ is allowed. If all three coefficients vanish, every twice-differentiable function is allowed, including oscillatory ones; the second-order criterion does not apply to this degenerate identity.

The <homogeneous linear differential equation> has roots $(-1\pm i\sqrt3)/2$, so its <complementary function> decays:
$$
f_h=e^{-t/2}\left(C_1\cos\frac{\sqrt3t}{2}+C_2\sin\frac{\sqrt3t}{2}\right).
$$
For a <particular integral>, set $f_p=B\cos2t+C\sin2t$. Substitution gives
$$
f_p''+f_p'+f_p=(-3B+2C)\cos2t+(-2B-3C)\sin2t.
$$
The two coefficient equations are $-3B+2C=A$ and $-2B-3C=0$, so $B=-3A/13$ and $C=2A/13$. Consequently every solution obeys
$$
\boxed{f(t)=\frac A{13}(-3\cos2t+2\sin2t)+O(e^{-t/2}),\qquad t\to\infty.}
$$
The limiting behaviour is a periodic steady oscillation with amplitude $|A|/\sqrt{13}$, the long-time response of a <forced harmonic oscillator>. Unless $A=0$, there is no finite numerical limit: at $t=n\pi$ and $t=n\pi+\pi/2$, the two limiting values are $-3A/13$ and $3A/13$. For $A=0$, $f(t)\to0$.

On an interval with $x\ne0$, this is a <homogeneous first-order differential equation>. Put $y=xv(x)$, so $y'=v+xv'$. Substitution and division by $x^2$ give
$$
xv'=v^2-2v=v(v-2).
$$
The constant choices $v=0$ and $v=2$ give $y=0$ and $y=2x$. Otherwise the <separable differential equation> gives
$$
\int\frac{dv}{v(v-2)}=\int\frac{dx}{x},\qquad
\frac12\ln\left|\frac{v-2}{v}\right|=\ln|x|+C_0.
$$
Absorbing the sign and the exponential integration constant yields $(v-2)/v=Cx^2$. Relabelling the arbitrary constant then gives
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac{2x}{1+Kx^2},\qquad\text{or}\qquad y(x)=0.}
$$
The first family already includes $y=2x$ at $K=0$. Choose any interval where its denominator is nonzero. These give all solutions on intervals avoiding $x=0$. Both families extend through $x=0$ with $y(0)=0$, as required by the original <ordinary differential equation>. Because it is singular there, differentiable continuations of the nonzero family may use different $K$ on the two sides: both one-sided derivatives at zero equal $2$.

For real solutions work on $x>-1$. Dividing by $2(1+x)$ puts the <linear ordinary differential equation> into the form
$$
y'+\left(-1+\frac1{2(1+x)}\right)y=\frac{x^2}{2\sqrt{1+x}}.
$$
Its <integrating factor> is
$$
\mu(x)=\exp\left(-x+\frac12\ln(1+x)\right)=e^{-x}\sqrt{1+x}.
$$
The <product rule> therefore gives
$$
(\mu y)'=\frac12x^2e^{-x}.
$$
Twice using <integration by parts>, or differentiating the proposed antiderivative, gives $\int x^2e^{-x}\,dx=-e^{-x}(x^2+2x+2)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac{Ce^x-\tfrac12(x^2+2x+2)}{\sqrt{1+x}},\qquad x>-1.}
$$
The endpoint $x=-1$ is singular. A finite continuous extension would require $C=e/2$, but then, with $h=x+1\downarrow0$, $y=\tfrac12\sqrt h+O(h^{3/2})$. Its derivative diverges, so even this choice has no differentiable extension to that endpoint.

The zero function is a solution on any interval avoiding $x=0$. For a nonzero solution, the <Bernoulli differential equation> substitution $u=1/y$ gives $u'=-y'/y^2$ and
$$
u'-\frac ux=-x.
$$
An <integrating factor> is $1/x$ on either side of zero. Thus
$$
\left(\frac ux\right)'=-1,\qquad\frac ux=C-x,\qquad u=Cx-x^2.
$$
The full solution set is
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac1{Cx-x^2},\qquad\text{or}\qquad y(x)=0.}
$$
Use connected intervals with $x\ne0$ and, in the nonzero family, $Cx-x^2\ne0$. The separate zero solution would be lost by dividing by $y^2$.
