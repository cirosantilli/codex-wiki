=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

By <De Moivre's theorem>, $(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^3=\cos3\theta+i\sin3\theta$. Expanding the cube and equating its <real part> gives
$$
\boxed{\cos3\theta=\cos^3\theta-3\cos\theta\sin^2\theta=4\cos^3\theta-3\cos\theta.}
$$
The last form uses $\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1$; comparing the <imaginary parts> similarly gives the corresponding sine identity.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

A <root of unity> satisfies $|z|^3=1$, hence has <modulus> one. Writing $z=e^{i\phi}$, its <argument> satisfies $3\phi=2\pi k$ for some <integer> $k$. The three distinct <complex roots> are therefore
$$
\boxed{z=1,\qquad -\frac12+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i,\qquad -\frac12-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i.}
$$
They are equally spaced on the <unit circle>. Taking $k=0,1,2$ exhausts them, since adding three to $k$ repeats a root and a nonzero <polynomial> of degree three has at most three distinct roots.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Factor out the same half-angle phase from the numerator and denominator:
$$
\frac{1+e^{-i\theta}}{1-e^{-i\theta}}
=\frac{e^{-i\theta/2}(e^{i\theta/2}+e^{-i\theta/2})}{e^{-i\theta/2}(e^{i\theta/2}-e^{-i\theta/2})}
=\boxed{-i\cot\frac\theta2}.
$$
Here <Euler's formula> converts the bracketed sums to cosine and sine. Both sides require $\theta\notin2\pi\mathbb Z$; at the excluded angles the original denominator vanishes.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

For $x\ne0$, divide by $x^2$ to obtain the <linear ordinary differential equation> $y'+3y/x=x^{-2}$. An <integrating factor> is $x^3$ on either interval not containing zero. Thus $(x^3y)'=x$ and
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac1{2x}+\frac{C}{x^3},\qquad x>0\ \text{or}\ x<0.}
$$
Substituting gives $x^2y'+3xy=1$ directly. No classical solution can include $x=0$, since the original equation there would assert $0=1$; constants on the two separate intervals need not agree.

== 3
{scope}

=== Solution

The real logarithm requires $x>0$. The identically zero function is a solution and must be retained before dividing by $y$. For a nonzero solution, divide by $x$ and make the <Bernoulli differential equation> substitution $u=1/y$. This gives
$$
u'-\frac ux=-x\ln x,\qquad \left(\frac ux\right)'=-\ln x.
$$
Integration yields $u/x=-x\ln x+x+C$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{y\equiv0\quad\text{or}\quad y(x)=\frac1{Cx+x^2(1-\ln x)},\qquad x>0,}
$$
with each nonzero branch restricted to an interval where its denominator is nonzero. The right-hand side of the first-order equation is locally Lipschitz in $y$ for $x>0$, so a nonzero solution cannot cross the zero solution at a finite positive $x$.

== 4
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

An <orthogonal matrix> satisfies $A^TA=I$. Taking <determinants> and using $\det A^T=\det A$ gives $(\det A)^2=1$, so
$$
\boxed{\det A=+1\quad\text{or}\quad\det A=-1.}
$$
This calculation remains valid over the <complex numbers>: orthogonality uses the <transpose>, whereas a <unitary matrix> uses the <conjugate transpose>.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

For a <two-dimensional complex orthogonal matrix>, let $\varepsilon=\det A\in\{+1,-1\}$. Since $A^{-1}=A^T$, comparison with the two-by-two <matrix inverse> formula forces the second row to be $(-\varepsilon b,\varepsilon a)$ if the first is $(a,b)$. Thus the complete family is
$$
\boxed{A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\-\varepsilon b&\varepsilon a\end{pmatrix},\qquad a,b\in\mathbb C,\qquad a^2+b^2=1,\qquad\varepsilon=\pm1.}
$$
Conversely direct multiplication gives $A^TA=(a^2+b^2)I=I$ and $\det A=\varepsilon$, proving sufficiency. The two parameters obey a bilinear constraint, not $|a|^2+|b|^2=1$; replacing squares by absolute squares would incorrectly impose <unitarity>.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

The <hyperbolic functions> satisfy $\cosh^2\theta-\sinh^2\theta=1$, so a useful family is
$$
\boxed{a=\cosh\theta,\qquad b=i\sinh\theta,\qquad
A=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh\theta&i\sinh\theta\\-\varepsilon i\sinh\theta&\varepsilon\cosh\theta\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
For real $\theta$ this gives a one-real-parameter family of <complex orthogonal matrices>, which is enough to exhibit the requested hyperbolic parametrization. It does **not** parametrize every matrix in part (ii). For example $a=b=1/\sqrt2$ obeys the constraint but cannot have $a=\cosh\theta$ with real $\theta$, since then $a\ge1$.

Allowing complex $\theta$ gives the full parametrization: $(a-ib)(a+ib)=1$, so choose a <complex logarithm> $\theta$ with $e^\theta=a-ib$. Then $a=\cosh\theta$ and $b=i\sinh\theta$. A general complex pair therefore needs a complex parameter; the real-parameter restriction in the question should not be mistaken for a completeness claim.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

<Gaussian elimination> of the first two equations gives $x=2y-1$ and $z=3-4y$. The third then reduces to $(\alpha-2)y=\beta-3$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{ll}
\alpha=2,\ \beta\ne3:&\text{no solution},\\
\alpha=2,\ \beta=3:&\text{infinitely many solutions},\\
\alpha\ne2:&\text{one solution}.
\end{array}}
$$
In the infinite family $y=t$, $x=2t-1$ and $z=3-4t$, for $t\in\mathbb R$. This makes the distinction between an inconsistent <linear system> and a free parameter explicit; its <coefficient matrix> has <determinant> $4-2\alpha$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

With $\alpha=\beta=6$, the eliminated equation gives $4y=3$. Back substitution yields
$$
\boxed{(x,y,z)=\left(\frac12,\frac34,0\right).}
$$
The three left-hand sides are respectively $4$, $2$ and $6$, verifying the solution in the original <linear system>. Its <determinant> is $-8\ne0$, so this solution is unique.

== 6
{scope}

=== Solution

For nonparallel lines, $\mathbf n=\mathbf m_1\times\mathbf m_2$ is perpendicular to both direction vectors. Every displacement between the lines differs from $\mathbf a_2-\mathbf a_1$ by a vector in their span, so its component along $\mathbf n$ is fixed. Varying the two parameters cancels the component in that span. The <shortest distance between skew lines> is therefore
$$
\boxed{d=\frac{|(\mathbf a_2-\mathbf a_1)\cdot(\mathbf m_1\times\mathbf m_2)|}{|\mathbf m_1\times\mathbf m_2|}.}
$$
This also gives zero for intersecting nonparallel lines. For parallel lines the denominator vanishes and the correct separate formula is $| (\mathbf a_2-\mathbf a_1)\times\mathbf m_1|/|\mathbf m_1|$.

The given lines have vector forms
$$
\mathbf r_1=(2,3,-1)+t(2,1,2),\qquad
\mathbf r_2=(-2,-1,1)+u(1,2,1).
$$
Here $\mathbf m_1\times\mathbf m_2=(-3,0,3)$ and $\mathbf a_2-\mathbf a_1=(-4,-4,2)$. Their <scalar triple product> is $18$, giving
$$
\boxed{d=\frac{18}{3\sqrt2}=3\sqrt2.}
$$
As a constructive check, the closest parameters are $t=2/3$ and $u=7/3$, where the connecting vector is $(3,0,-3)$ and is perpendicular to both lines.

== 7
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

<Hooke's law> and <Newton's second law> give $m\ddot x=-kx$, so $\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}$. The initial conditions select the <simple harmonic motion>
$$
\boxed{x(t)=x_0\cos\omega_0t,\qquad
K(t)=\frac{kx_0^2}{2}\sin^2\omega_0t,\qquad
U(t)=\frac{kx_0^2}{2}\cos^2\omega_0t.}
$$
The <kinetic energy> and <elastic potential energy> exchange periodically while their sum $E_0=kx_0^2/2$ is constant.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For the <damped harmonic oscillator>, the <characteristic polynomial> is $r^2+2\gamma r+\omega_0^2$. When $0\le\gamma<\omega_0$, its roots are $-\gamma\pm i\Omega$, with $\Omega=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{x(t)=e^{-\gamma t}\bigl(A\cos\Omega t+B\sin\Omega t\bigr).}
$$
Here $A$ and $B$ are arbitrary real constants. The frequency is $\Omega$, and the amplitude envelope decays with time constant $1/\gamma$ when $\gamma>0$; the zero-damping limit recovers <simple harmonic motion>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

At <critical damping>, the <characteristic polynomial> has the repeated root $r=-\omega_0$. Its independent solutions are $e^{-\omega_0t}$ and $te^{-\omega_0t}$, giving
$$
\boxed{x(t)=(A+Bt)e^{-\omega_0t}.}
$$
The factor $t$ supplies the second independent solution that would be lost by using just one exponential. There is no oscillatory factor at this boundary between the underdamped and overdamped regimes.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The initial conditions in the underdamped solution give $A=x_0$ and $B=\gamma x_0/\Omega$. Consequently
$$
x=x_0e^{-\gamma t}\left(\cos\Omega t+\frac\gamma\Omega\sin\Omega t\right),\qquad
\dot x=-\frac{x_0\omega_0^2}{\Omega}e^{-\gamma t}\sin\Omega t.
$$
The exact <mechanical energy> is therefore
$$
E(t)=E_0e^{-2\gamma t}\left[1+\frac\gamma\Omega\sin2\Omega t+\frac{2\gamma^2}{\Omega^2}\sin^2\Omega t\right].
$$
For weak damping, $\gamma\ll\omega_0$, the bracket differs from one by a bounded relative correction of order $\gamma/\omega_0$. To leading order the energy and its half-life are
$$
\boxed{E(t)\simeq E_0e^{-2\gamma t},\qquad t_{1/2}\simeq\frac{\ln2}{2\gamma}.}
$$
The exact identity $\dot E=-2m\gamma\dot x^2\le0$ confirms <dissipation>. The small rapid variation of the exponential-normalized energy does not mean that total energy increases; the half-life formula is a weak-damping approximation, not an exact equality for arbitrary $\gamma$.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Divide the <external force> by $m$. Away from resonance a particular solution has the forcing frequency, so the general <forced harmonic oscillator> solution is
$$
\boxed{x(t)=A\cos\omega_0t+B\sin\omega_0t+
\frac{F_0}{m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)}\cos(\omega t-\phi),\qquad\omega\ne\omega_0.}
$$
The two homogeneous terms cannot be discarded in an undamped system: they do not decay. If the same initial conditions are imposed, then $A=x_0-C\cos\phi$, $B=-C(\omega/\omega_0)\sin\phi$, where $C=F_0/[m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)]$.

For $\omega\gg\omega_0$, the forced displacement approaches $-F_0\cos(\omega t-\phi)/(m\omega^2)$: its amplitude decreases quadratically and it is in antiphase with the force. This statement concerns the forced component; a freely excited component at $\omega_0$ can remain.

At <resonance> the preceding denominator is zero and the appropriate solution is
$$
\boxed{x(t)=A\cos\omega_0t+B\sin\omega_0t+
\frac{F_0t}{2m\omega_0}\sin(\omega_0t-\phi).}
$$
Its amplitude grows linearly because the external force supplies energy coherently. For $x(0)=x_0$, $\dot x(0)=0$, choose $A=x_0$ and $B=F_0\sin\phi/(2m\omega_0^2)$. Physical damping or nonlinear response ultimately limits the ideal resonant growth.

== 8
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $z=x+iy\ne0$. Since $z^{-1}=(x-iy)/(x^2+y^2)$, the two <loci> are
$$
\boxed{\left(x-\frac12\right)^2+y^2=\frac14,\qquad
x^2+\left(y+\frac12\right)^2=\frac14,\qquad z\ne0.}
$$
Thus they are radius-$1/2$ circles centred at $(1/2,0)$ and $(0,-1/2)$, each missing the origin. Their circle equations intersect at $(0,0)$ and $(1/2,-1/2)$, but only the latter belongs to the original loci. At that point the radius vectors are $(0,-1/2)$ and $(1/2,0)$, so the tangents meet at a right angle.

Every point on either circle has distance at most $1/2+1/2=1$ from the origin. Equality occurs only at $(1,0)$ on the first or $(0,-1)$ on the second. The <unit circle> is therefore internally tangent to both and crosses neither. The sketch marks the excluded origin explicitly, rather than treating it as a second intersection.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The plane has <normal vector> $\mathbf n=(2,3,6)$ of length seven. Projecting the displacement from any point on the plane onto the <unit normal> gives the <distance from a point to a plane>
$$
\boxed{d=\frac{|2x_0+3y_0+6z_0-30|}{7}.}
$$
For example the perpendicular foot is $\mathbf r_0-\mathbf n(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r_0-30)/49$. Substitution places it on the plane, and its displacement from $\mathbf r_0$ is parallel to $\mathbf n$, verifying that this is the minimum distance.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

For nonzero vectors with angle $\theta$, $|\mathbf a\times\mathbf b|=|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\sin\theta$ and $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\cos\theta$. Hence $\sin^2\theta=1-\cos^2\theta$ gives <Lagrange's identity>
$$
\boxed{|\mathbf a\times\mathbf b|^2=|\mathbf a|^2|\mathbf b|^2-(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)^2.}
$$
If either vector is zero both sides vanish, so this covers every case. Alternatively, expansion of the three <cross product> components gives the same identity without introducing an angle.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For the <quadratic form of a squared cross product>, express the two scalar terms as <quadratic forms> in $\mathbf a$:
$$
\boxed{M=|\mathbf b|^2I-\mathbf b\mathbf b^T,\qquad
M_{ij}=|\mathbf b|^2\delta_{ij}-b_ib_j.}
$$
Then $\mathbf a^TM\mathbf a=|\mathbf b|^2|\mathbf a|^2-(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)^2$. In particular, for $\mathbf b=(b_1,b_2,0)$,
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}b_2^2&-b_1b_2&0\\-b_1b_2&b_1^2&0\\0&0&b_1^2+b_2^2\end{pmatrix},\qquad\boxed{\det M=0.}
$$
The cancellation is structural: $M\mathbf b=0$, while its action on every vector perpendicular to a nonzero $\mathbf b$ is multiplication by $|\mathbf b|^2$. Its <eigenvalues> are therefore $0,|\mathbf b|^2,|\mathbf b|^2$; for $\mathbf b=0$ the whole matrix is zero.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The vectors are <linearly independent> over $F$ if $\sum_{j=1}^n c_j\mathbf v_j=0$ with $c_j\in F$ implies $c_j=0$ for every $j$. Otherwise there is a nontrivial <linear dependence>. Forming a matrix with the displayed vectors as columns gives
$$
\det\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&2&-1\\3&4&0\\2&0&4\end{pmatrix}=16\alpha-16.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\alpha=1}
$$
is the unique dependent case. At that value the explicit relation $2\mathbf v_1-\tfrac32\mathbf v_2-\mathbf v_3=0$ verifies the dependence.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the convention that the <Hermitian inner product> is conjugate-linear in its first argument and linear in its second. In <orthonormal coordinates>, $\langle v,w\rangle=\sum_i\overline{v_i}w_i$. It satisfies <conjugate symmetry>, sesquilinearity and positive definiteness. Expanding in the <orthonormal basis> gives
$$
v=\sum_i e_i\langle e_i,v\rangle,\qquad
w=\sum_j e_j\langle e_j,w\rangle.
$$
Applying the <Hermitian inner product> and $\langle e_i,e_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}$ yields
$$
\boxed{\langle v,w\rangle=\sum_i\overline{\langle e_i,v\rangle}\langle e_i,w\rangle
=\sum_i\langle v,e_i\rangle\langle e_i,w\rangle.}
$$
This identifies exactly where <complex conjugation> enters; replacing the first factor by $\langle e_i,v\rangle$ would generally be wrong under this convention.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

With a fixed <unit normal> $n$, the <Householder reflection> is $P=I-2nn^T$. It is a <linear map> because both the identity and the map $v\mapsto n(n^Tv)$ are linear. Decompose $v=v_\parallel+v_\perp$, where $v_\perp=(n\cdot v)n$ and $v_\parallel\cdot n=0$. Then
$$
Pv=v_\parallel-v_\perp.
$$
Thus the tangential component is unchanged and the normal component reverses. Its norm and absolute normal component are preserved, proving equality of the physical incidence and reflection angles measured against the appropriate normal. Angles of the incident and reflected *directed vectors* to one fixed oriented normal are instead supplementary; equality uses the conventional acute angle to the normal line, or reverses the incident direction.

Since $(nn^T)^2=nn^T$, direct multiplication gives $P^2=I$. Every <eigenvalue> therefore satisfies $\lambda^2=1$. More specifically, the tangent plane is the $+1$ <eigenspace> and the normal line the $-1$ <eigenspace>. In the stated <orthonormal basis>, with the first two vectors tangent to the plane,
$$
\boxed{[P]_{\{e_1,e_2,n\}}=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,-1).}
$$
This also proves both signs occur, rather than merely showing that any eigenvalue must belong to $\{+1,-1\}$.

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

<Matrix multiplication> is associative, so
$$
(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1})=A(BB^{-1})A^{-1}=I,\qquad
(B^{-1}A^{-1})(AB)=B^{-1}(A^{-1}A)B=I.
$$
The uniqueness of the <matrix inverse> then gives
$$
\boxed{(AB)^{-1}=B^{-1}A^{-1}.}
$$
The order is reversed; $A^{-1}B^{-1}$ does not generally work because <matrix multiplication> need not be commutative.

==== ii
{scope}

===== 1
{scope}

====== Solution

A <symmetric matrix> has $A^T=A$, without conjugation. One invertible example with nonreal off-diagonal entries is
$$
\boxed{A=\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\i&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det A=3.}
$$
It is symmetric but not <Hermitian>, illustrating the importance of the distinction over $\mathbb C$.

===== 2
{scope}

====== Solution

A <Hermitian matrix> has $A^*=A$. Choose
$$
\boxed{A=\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\-i&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det A=1.}
$$
Its off-diagonal entries are <complex conjugates> and its diagonal entries are real, so the defining condition holds. It is also invertible.

===== 3
{scope}

====== Solution

A <unitary matrix> has $A^*A=I$. For example,
$$
\boxed{A=\begin{pmatrix}0&i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad A^*A=I.}
$$
This example is invertible, has nonreal off-diagonal entries, and preserves the standard <Hermitian inner product>. Although it is also symmetric, that additional property is not required for <unitarity>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <quadratic form> has <symmetric matrix>
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\2&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\det(M-\lambda I)=\lambda^2-\lambda-6=(\lambda-3)(\lambda+2).
$$
Normalized <eigenvectors> for $3$ and $-2$ are $(2,1)/\sqrt5$ and $(-1,2)/\sqrt5$. They form the columns of the <rotation matrix>
$$
Q=\frac1{\sqrt5}\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\binom xy=Q\binom{x'}{y'},\qquad
Q^TMQ=D=\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&-2\end{pmatrix}}.
$$
Thus the new $x'$ axis is rotated counterclockwise by $\theta=\arctan(1/2)$, with
$$
\boxed{\sin\theta=\frac1{\sqrt5},\qquad f=3x'^2-2y'^2.}
$$
The opposite signs of the <eigenvalues> classify each positive level as a <hyperbola>. For the original level six its standard equation is $x'^2/2-y'^2/3=1$; for the final level one it is $3x'^2-2y'^2=1$. The printed change of level therefore changes the scale, but not the principal axes or <asymptotes>.

Setting the homogeneous quadratic part to zero gives $y'=\pm\sqrt{3/2}\,x'$. In the original coordinates put $y=mx$ in $2x^2+4xy-y^2=0$, obtaining
$$
\boxed{m=2\pm\sqrt6.}
$$
These are the slopes in the original $(x,y)$ axes; $\pm\sqrt{3/2}$ are the slopes in the rotated axes, so they must not be interchanged.
