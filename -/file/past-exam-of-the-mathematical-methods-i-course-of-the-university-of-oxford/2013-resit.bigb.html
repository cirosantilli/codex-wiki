== 1
{scope}

=== Solution

Squaring first is simpler than expanding the tenth powers. Since $(1-i)^2=-2i$ and $(1+i)^2=2i$, while $i^4=1$, the requested Cartesian forms are
$$
\boxed{\bigl((1-i)^2\bigr)^5=-32i=0-32i,\qquad
\bigl((1+i)^2\bigr)^5=32i=0+32i.}
$$
Thus the <real part> is zero in each case, and the <imaginary parts> are $-32$ and $32$. The results are <complex conjugates>, as also follows because the two inputs are <complex conjugates> and the fifth-power polynomial has real coefficients.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

Write $27i=27e^{i(\pi/2+2\pi k)}$. Every <cube root> has <complex modulus> $3$ and an angle one third of one of these <complex arguments>:
$$
\boxed{z_k=3e^{i(\pi/6+2\pi k/3)},\quad k=0,1,2.}
$$
Equivalently the three <complex roots> are
$$
\boxed{\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}+\frac32i,\qquad
-\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}+\frac32i,\qquad -3i.}
$$
They lie on the circle of radius $3$ centred at the origin in the <Argand plane>, spaced by $2\pi/3$. Further integer values of $k$ repeat these three points. Cubing any of them multiplies its angle by three and cubes its <complex modulus> from $3$ to $27$, giving $27i$.

== 3
{scope}

=== Solution

Put $z=x+iy$. The expression whose <complex argument> is constrained becomes
$$
z^2+1=(x^2-y^2+1)+2ixy.
$$
Its <principal argument> is $\pi/2$ exactly when its <real part> is zero and its <imaginary part> is **strictly positive**. Hence
$$
\boxed{y^2-x^2=1,\qquad xy>0.}
$$
The <locus> consists of the upper-right half of the upper <hyperbola> branch, $y=\sqrt{x^2+1}$ with $x>0$, and the lower-left half of the lower branch, $y=-\sqrt{x^2+1}$ with $x<0$. The vertices $z=\pm i$ are excluded because $z^2+1=0$ there and zero has no <complex argument>. The remaining halves have negative <imaginary part> and give argument $-\pi/2$, not $\pi/2$. The right panel of the original illustration marks the excluded vertices with open circles.

== 4
{scope}

=== Solution

Use <parametric equations> for the two lines,
$$
\mathbf r=\mathbf p+t\mathbf u,\quad \mathbf p=(2,3,-1),\quad\mathbf u=(2,1,2),
\qquad
\mathbf r=\mathbf q+s\mathbf v,\quad\mathbf q=(-2,-1,1),\quad\mathbf v=(1,2,1).
$$
Their <cross product> is $\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=(-3,0,3)\ne0$. Projection of the separation of the base points onto this common normal gives the <shortest distance between skew lines>:
$$
\boxed{d=\frac{|(\mathbf q-\mathbf p)\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)|}{|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v|}
=\frac{18}{3\sqrt2}=3\sqrt2.}
$$
This projection is a lower bound on every connecting segment because adding either line's direction leaves its normal component unchanged. It is attained: imposing perpendicularity of $\mathbf p+t\mathbf u-\mathbf q-s\mathbf v$ to both directions gives $t=2/3$ and $s=7/3$. The corresponding points are $(10/3,11/3,1/3)$ and $(1/3,11/3,10/3)$; their difference is $(3,0,-3)$, perpendicular to both lines and of length $3\sqrt2$.

== 5
{scope}

=== Solution

Subtracting the first equation from the third gives $4x-4z=6$, so $z=x-3/2$. The second equation then becomes $(\alpha-2)x=\beta-3$. The third fixes $y=7(1-x)/5$. Therefore the three possibilities are
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c}
\alpha\ne2 & \text{one solution},\quad
x=\dfrac{\beta-3}{\alpha-2},\ y=\dfrac75(1-x),\ z=x-\dfrac32\\[4pt]
\alpha=2,\ \beta=3 & \text{infinitely many},\quad
(x,y,z)=\left(t,\dfrac75(1-t),t-\dfrac32\right)\\[4pt]
\alpha=2,\ \beta\ne3 & \text{no solution}.
\end{array}}
$$
For the first case the coefficient <determinant> is $20(\alpha-2)\ne0$, confirming uniqueness. At $\alpha=2$ its <matrix rank> is two. The <augmented matrix> has the same <matrix rank> only for $\beta=3$, giving one <free variable in a linear system>; otherwise the <linear system> is inconsistent.

== 6
{scope}

=== Solution

A real square <orthogonal matrix> satisfies $Q^TQ=I$. A complex square <unitary matrix> satisfies $U^\dagger U=I$, where $\dagger$ denotes conjugate transpose. A <Hermitian matrix> satisfies $H^\dagger=H$. The distinction between transpose and conjugate transpose matters for imaginary entries.

For the three displayed matrices, in their source order:

* First: a <unitary matrix>, but neither an <orthogonal matrix> nor a <Hermitian matrix>.
* Second: an <orthogonal matrix>, a <unitary matrix>, and a <Hermitian matrix>.
* Third: a <Hermitian matrix>, but neither an <orthogonal matrix> nor a <unitary matrix>.

For the first matrix, $A^\dagger=-A$ and $A^2=-I$, so $A^\dagger A=I$ but $A^\dagger\ne A$. It also fails $A^TA=I$, even if complex orthogonality is considered. The second matrix is real diagonal with entries $1,-1$: its transpose, conjugate transpose and inverse all equal itself. In the third matrix the imaginary off-diagonal entries are conjugate pairs, establishing the <Hermitian matrix> condition. Its first column has squared norm $1+3=4$, so it is not a <unitary matrix>; it is not real orthogonal, and the complex bilinear first-column square is $1-3=-2\ne1$ as well.

== 7
{scope}

=== Solution

For this <homogeneous tangent differential equation>, set $u=y/x$ on an interval not containing $x=0$. Since $y'=u+xu'$, cancellation gives $xu'=2\tan u$. Away from zeros of $\tan u$, separation and <integration> give
$$
\int\cot u\,du=2\int\frac{dx}{x},\qquad
\log|\sin u|=2\log|x|+C_0.
$$
Restoring a signed constant yields the compact implicit solution
$$
\boxed{\sin(y/x)=Cx^2.}
$$
A complete real branch description is
$$
\boxed{y=x\left[k\pi+(-1)^k\arcsin(Cx^2)\right],\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
Here $C$ is real and the interval must satisfy $x\ne0$ and $|Cx^2|<1$. The strict inequality excludes points with $\cos(y/x)=0$, where the original <tangent function> is undefined. Separating divided by $\tan u$, so its zeros must be checked separately: $u=k\pi$ gives $y=k\pi x$. These are exactly the $C=0$ branches already included. The branch integer is fixed on each connected solution interval; it cannot be changed arbitrarily at a regular point.

== 8
{scope}

=== Solution

The constant term belongs on the forcing side: the equation is $y''-2y'=x+1$. Set $v=y'$ to obtain the first-order linear equation $v'-2v=x+1$. A particular solution is $v_p=-x/2-3/4$, while $v_h=Ae^{2x}$. Imposing $v(0)=0$ fixes $A=3/4$. Integrating and then using $y(0)=0$ gives
$$
\boxed{y(x)=\frac38(e^{2x}-1)-\frac{x^2}{4}-\frac{3x}{4}.}
$$
Direct <differentiation> gives $y'=3e^{2x}/4-x/2-3/4$ and $y''=3e^{2x}/2-1/2$, so $y''-2y'-1=x$. Both initial values vanish. The nonzero exponential term is required by the initial derivative, even though the forcing is polynomial.

== 9
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $D=\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)\ne0$ be the <scalar triple product>. The <reciprocal basis> satisfies $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf a'=1$ and all mismatched dot products vanish, with cyclic analogues. Apply the given <vector triple product> identity to obtain
$$
\mathbf b'\times\mathbf c'
=\frac{(\mathbf c\times\mathbf a)\times(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)}{D^2}
=\frac{D\mathbf a}{D^2}=\frac{\mathbf a}{D}.
$$
Dotting with $\mathbf a'$ gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf a'\cdot(\mathbf b'\times\mathbf c')=\frac1D.}
$$
The sign is retained: a negatively oriented original <basis> has a negatively oriented <reciprocal basis>. Equivalently, if the original vectors are columns of $B$, their reciprocals are columns of $B^{-T}$, whose <determinant> is $1/\det B$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The denominator for taking reciprocals a second time is $D'=1/D$. The preceding cross-product result therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{\mathbf b'\times\mathbf c'}{D'}=\frac{\mathbf a/D}{1/D}=\mathbf a.}
$$
Cyclic permutation gives $\mathbf b$ and $\mathbf c$. Thus taking the <reciprocal basis> twice returns the original <basis>. The matrix statement is the same <reciprocal basis involution>: $(B^{-T})^{-T}=B$. Noncoplanarity is essential, since otherwise the defining denominator and the matrix inverse do not exist.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Dot the reciprocal-basis expansion with each member of the original <basis>. All mismatched dot products vanish and the matching one equals one. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf a,\qquad
\beta=\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf b,\qquad
\gamma=\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf c.}
$$
These are coefficients in the <reciprocal basis>. They would instead involve dot products with the reciprocal vectors if $\mathbf r$ were being expanded in the original <basis>. The two coefficient conventions should not be interchanged for a nonorthonormal <basis>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the preceding coefficient identities with the three prescribed plane equations. They immediately give
$$
\boxed{\mathbf r=s\mathbf a'+t\mathbf b'+u\mathbf c'.}
$$
For example, its dot product with $\mathbf a$ is $s$, because $\mathbf a'\cdot\mathbf a=1$ while the other two terms give zero; the other equations follow cyclically. This verifies existence. If two points satisfied all three equations, their difference would be perpendicular to all three members of a <basis> and therefore zero. Hence the intersection is unique, not merely one possible intersection point.

== 10
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Use active <rotations> on column vectors and positive angles counter-clockwise when viewed from the positive axis toward the origin. Rotation about $z$ takes $\mathbf e_x$ to $(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha,0)$, $\mathbf e_y$ to $(-\sin\alpha,\cos\alpha,0)$, and leaves $\mathbf e_z$ fixed. These images are the columns of the <rotation matrix>:
$$
\boxed{R_z(\alpha)=\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha&0\\
\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha&0\\
0&0&1
\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
It satisfies $R_z^TR_z=I$ and $\det R_z=1$. Stating the viewing convention avoids confusing this active <rotation> with a passive change of axes.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

With the same active <rotation> convention, the $x$ axis remains fixed, while $\mathbf e_y$ moves toward $\mathbf e_z$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{R_x(\beta)=\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&0\\
0&\cos\beta&-\sin\beta\\
0&\sin\beta&\cos\beta
\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
For instance, a positive quarter-turn maps $\mathbf e_y$ to $\mathbf e_z$ and $\mathbf e_z$ to $-\mathbf e_y$, checking both signs. This is again an <orthogonal matrix> of <determinant> one.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The first operation acts nearest the column vector, so the composite <rotation matrix> is $M=R_z(\alpha)R_x(\beta)$:
$$
\boxed{M=\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\alpha&-\sin\alpha\cos\beta&\sin\alpha\sin\beta\\
\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha\cos\beta&-\cos\alpha\sin\beta\\
0&\sin\beta&\cos\beta
\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
At $\alpha=\beta=\pi/2$ this reduces to
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
(x,y,z)\mapsto(z,x,y).
$$
Solving $M\mathbf v=\mathbf v$ gives $v_x=v_y=v_z$. Normalization yields the fixed-axis <unit vectors>
$$
\boxed{\mathbf v=\pm\frac1{\sqrt3}(1,1,1).}
$$
Both signs describe the same unoriented axis. The operation is a cyclic permutation, and $M^3=I$; it is not generally obtained by reversing the multiplication order.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Choose the <unit vector> $\mathbf p=(1,-1,0)/\sqrt2$, which is perpendicular to $\mathbf v$. Then $M\mathbf p=(0,1,-1)/\sqrt2$. Their dot product is $-1/2$, and the <rotation matrix> preserves their norms, so
$$
\boxed{\angle(\mathbf p,M\mathbf p)=\arccos(-1/2)=\frac{2\pi}{3}.}
$$
The same unsigned angle holds for every nonzero vector perpendicular to the axis: this is the rotation plane of a three-dimensional proper <rotation>. Alternatively, $\operatorname{tr}M=0=1+2\cos\chi$ gives the same rotation angle $\chi=2\pi/3$.

== 11
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Take the <imaginary part> of <De Moivre's theorem> at exponent five. Writing $s=\sin\theta$ and $c=\cos\theta$ gives
$$
\sin5\theta=5c^4s-10c^2s^3+s^5
=5s(1-s^2)^2-10s^3(1-s^2)+s^5
=16s^5-20s^3+5s.
$$
For real $x\in[-1,1]$, put $x=\sin\theta$. The equation becomes $\sin5\theta=1$, so $\theta=\pi/10+2\pi k/5$. The distinct sine values are $1$, $(\sqrt5-1)/4$, and $-(1+\sqrt5)/4$. To establish completeness over the complex numbers as well, factor the polynomial:
$$
16x^5-20x^3+5x-1=(x-1)(4x^2+2x-1)^2.
$$
Thus all <polynomial roots>, including their <multiplicities>, are
$$
\boxed{x=1\ \text{(simple)},\qquad
x=\frac{-1+\sqrt5}{4},\ \frac{-1-\sqrt5}{4}\ \text{(each double)}.}
$$
The repeated sine values account for the double roots; there are no additional nonreal roots hidden by the trigonometric parametrization.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Set $w=(z+1)/(z-1)$, with $z\ne1$. For a nonzero integer $n$, all solutions of $w^n=i$ are
$$
w_k=\exp\left(\frac{i(\pi/2+2\pi k)}n\right),\qquad k=0,\ldots,|n|-1.
$$
This covers negative integers too: $w$ is then nonzero and $w^{|n|}=-i$. No $w_k$ equals one. Inverting the <Möbius transformation> and using half-angle identities gives the <cotangent roots of a powered Möbius transformation>:
$$
\boxed{z_k=\frac{w_k+1}{w_k-1}
=-i\cot\left(\frac{\pi/4+\pi k}{n}\right),\quad k=0,\ldots,|n|-1.}
$$
These $|n|$ solutions are distinct and purely imaginary. For $n=0$ there is no solution: every defined zeroth power is one, not $i$. For negative $n$, $z=-1$ must additionally be excluded from the equation's domain; none of the displayed roots equals it.

For the cubic, direct expansion shows
$$
(1-i)(z^3+3iz^2+3z+i)=(z+1)^3-i(z-1)^3.
$$
Since $z=1$ is not a root, the cubic is precisely the $n=3$ case. Taking $k=0,1,2$ yields
$$
\boxed{z=-i(2+\sqrt3),\qquad -i(2-\sqrt3),\qquad i.}
$$
For an algebraic check, the cubic factors as $(z-i)(z^2+4iz-1)$, which gives the same three distinct roots.

== 12
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Set $u=x+1$, so the equation is linear: $y'-y/u=1+2/u$, with $u\ne0$. An <integrating factor> is $1/u$ on either connected side of $u=0$. Thus
$$
\frac d{dx}\left(\frac yu\right)=\frac1u+\frac2{u^2},\qquad
\frac yu=\log|u|-\frac2u+C.
$$
Consequently the full real solution is
$$
\boxed{y=(x+1)\left(C+\log|x+1|\right)-2,\qquad x\ne-1.}
$$
The original equation is undefined at $x=-1$, so a continuation of the formula through that point is not a solution on an interval containing it. Constants can be chosen independently on the two separated domain components.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Again put $u=y/x$, requiring $x\ne0$. Substituting $y'=u+xu'$ gives $xu'=1+u^2$. For real $u$ the factor $1+u^2$ never vanishes, so separation loses no constant real solution:
$$
\arctan u=\log|x|+C\quad\text{locally},\qquad
\boxed{y=x\tan\left(\log|x|+C\right).}
$$
Each real solution is defined on a connected interval avoiding zero and every pole of the <tangent function>. Changing the constant by an integer multiple of $\pi$ gives the same branch. If complex-valued solutions are also allowed, the separated-out constant values $u=\pm i$ supply the additional solutions $y=\pm ix$; these do not occur in the real-valued family.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The <characteristic polynomial> of the homogeneous constant-coefficient equation is $r^2+2r+2$, with roots $-1\pm i$. Its real <complementary function> is therefore $e^{-x}(C_1\cos x+C_2\sin x)$. The forcing $e^{-x}$ is not resonant because $r=-1$ is not a root. Substituting $y_p=Ae^{-x}$ into the differential operator gives $Ae^{-x}$, so $A=1$. Hence
$$
\boxed{y=e^{-x}\left(C_1\cos x+C_2\sin x+1\right).}
$$
A useful independent check is the substitution $y=e^{-x}v$: the equation becomes $v''+v=1$, whose general solution is $v=C_1\cos x+C_2\sin x+1$.
