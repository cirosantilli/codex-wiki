For a <number field> $K$ of <degree of a field extension> $n$, an <integral basis> is a <basis> $\omega_1,\ldots,\omega_n$ over $\mathbb Q$ such that
$$
\mathcal O_K=\mathbb Z\omega_1\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb Z\omega_n.
$$
Thus every <algebraic integer> in $K$ has a unique expression with <integer> coefficients in this <basis>. Merely requiring its entries to be <algebraic integers> would not suffice: for example, $1,2\sqrt2$ is a rational <basis> of $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$ but misses the <algebraic integer> $\sqrt2$ in its integer span.

Here is a proof of the <existence of an integral basis>. Start with any rational <basis> $v_1,\ldots,v_n$. Each $v_j$ has a nonzero <integer> multiple $\alpha_j$ that is an <algebraic integer>. Indeed, if
$$
v^m+c_{m-1}v^{m-1}+\cdots+c_0=0,\qquad c_i\in\mathbb Q,
$$
choose a positive <integer> $q$ clearing all the denominators. Then $qv$ satisfies the <monic polynomial>
$$
X^m+qc_{m-1}X^{m-1}+q^2c_{m-2}X^{m-2}+\cdots+q^mc_0\in\mathbb Z[X].
$$
The $\alpha_j$ are still a rational <basis>. Put $M=\sum_j\mathbb Z\alpha_j$ and form the <matrix>
$$
T=(\operatorname{Tr}_{K/\mathbb Q}(\alpha_i\alpha_j))_{i,j},\qquad D=\det T.
$$
Its entries are <integers>, because products and sums of <algebraic integers> are <algebraic integers> and a rational <algebraic integer> is an <integer>. The <nondegeneracy of the trace form of a number field> gives $D\ne0$: if $x\ne0$, then $\operatorname{Tr}(xx^{-1})=n\ne0$.

For $x=\sum_j x_j\alpha_j\in\mathcal O_K$, each $\operatorname{Tr}(x\alpha_i)$ is an <integer>, so
$$
T(x_1,\ldots,x_n)^T\in\mathbb Z^n.
$$
The <adjugate matrix> formula for $T^{-1}$ gives $Dx_j\in\mathbb Z$. Consequently
$$
M\subseteq\mathcal O_K\subseteq D^{-1}M.
$$
The <submodule theorem for free modules over a principal ideal domain> says that every subgroup of the finite-rank <free abelian group> $D^{-1}M$ is again a <free abelian group> of rank at most $n$. Since $\mathcal O_K$ contains $M$, its rank is exactly $n$. A <basis> of this <free abelian group> is the required <integral basis>.

Write $x=r+s\sqrt d$ with $r,s\in\mathbb Q$. The <field trace> and <field norm> are
$$
T=2r,\qquad N=r^2-ds^2.
$$
The element is an <algebraic integer> if and only if $T,N\in\mathbb Z$: necessity follows from its two conjugates; sufficiency follows from the <monic polynomial> $X^2-TX+N$.

Suppose $x$ is an <algebraic integer>. Set $a=2r\in\mathbb Z$. Then
$$
d(2s)^2=T^2-4N\in\mathbb Z.
$$
Write $2s=h/k$ in lowest terms, with $k>0$. Then $k^2\mid d$, and the hypothesis that $d$ is a <square-free integer> forces $k=1$. Thus $b=2s\in\mathbb Z$, and the remaining condition is
$$
a^2-db^2\equiv0\pmod4.
$$
The only squares modulo four are zero and one. For $d\equiv2\pmod4$, an odd $b$ is impossible, after which $a$ is even. For $d\equiv3\pmod4$, an odd $b$ would require $a^2\equiv3\pmod4$, again impossible. For $d\equiv1\pmod4$, the condition is precisely $a\equiv b\pmod2$.

Conversely, $\sqrt d$ satisfies $X^2-d$, and when $d\equiv1\pmod4$, the element $\omega=(1+\sqrt d)/2$ satisfies
$$
X^2-X+\frac{1-d}{4}\in\mathbb Z[X].
$$
Their integer spans therefore consist of <algebraic integers>. In the last case,
$$
\frac{a+b\sqrt d}{2}=\frac{a-b}{2}+b\omega.
$$
This proves the <ring of integers of a quadratic field> formula. The required <integral basis> is
$$
\boxed{\begin{cases}
\{1,\sqrt d\},&d\equiv2,3\pmod4,\\
\{1,(1+\sqrt d)/2\},&d\equiv1\pmod4.
\end{cases}}
$$
Their rational <linear independence> follows from $\sqrt d\notin\mathbb Q$.

Put $\alpha=\sqrt{m_1}$, $\beta=\sqrt{m_2}$ and $F=\mathbb Q(\alpha)$. The hypotheses make $L/\mathbb Q$ a <biquadratic extension>. To check the <degree of a field extension> is four, if $\beta=x+y\alpha$ with rational $x,y$, squaring gives $xy=0$. The case $y=0$ would make $m_2$ a rational square; the case $x=0$ would make $m_2/m_1$ a rational square. Neither can occur for the specified distinct coprime <square-free integers>. Hence $[L:F]=2$.

The nonidentity <field automorphism> of $L/F$ fixes $\alpha$ and sends $\beta$ to $-\beta$. Thus, for $\gamma=(r+s\alpha)+(t+u\alpha)\beta$, the <field norm> and <field trace> are
$$
\boxed{N_{L/F}(\gamma)=(r+s\alpha)^2-m_2(t+u\alpha)^2,\qquad
\operatorname{Tr}_{L/F}(\gamma)=2(r+s\alpha)}.
$$
If $\gamma$ is an <algebraic integer>, so is its conjugate, and their sum and product are <algebraic integers>. Conversely, put $T=\operatorname{Tr}_{L/F}(\gamma)$ and $N=N_{L/F}(\gamma)$. The identity
$$
\gamma^2-T\gamma+N=0
$$
shows that $\gamma$ is integral over $\mathbb Z[T,N]$. If $T,N$ are <algebraic integers>, the <ring generated by algebraic integers> $\mathbb Z[T,N]$ is integral over $\mathbb Z$. Since <integral dependence is transitive>, $\gamma$ is an <algebraic integer>. This proves the <trace and norm integrality criterion for a quadratic extension>, including elements already in $F$.

Again put $\alpha=\sqrt{m_1}$ and $\beta=\sqrt{m_2}$. The <ring of integers> of each of the three quadratic subfields has <integral basis> $1,\alpha$, $1,\beta$, or $1,\alpha\beta$, respectively: their square-free radicands are respectively congruent to $3,2,2$ modulo four. The <field traces> of the <algebraic integer> $\gamma$ to those subfields are
$$
a+b\alpha,\qquad a+c\beta,\qquad a+d\alpha\beta,
\qquad a=2r,\ b=2s,\ c=2t,\ d=2u.
$$
They are <algebraic integers>, so comparison with each <integral basis> gives
$$
\boxed{a,b,c,d\in\mathbb Z}.
$$
This use of all three subfields is needed to control $d$ as well as the other coefficients.

The <field norm> to $\mathbb Q(\alpha)$ is
$$
N_{L/\mathbb Q(\alpha)}(\gamma)
=\frac{a^2+m_1b^2-m_2c^2-m_1m_2d^2}{4}
+\frac{ab-m_2cd}{2}\alpha.
$$
Its two coefficients must be <integers>. The second condition says $ab$ is even, since $m_2$ is even. Reducing the numerator of the first coefficient modulo four gives
$$
a^2-b^2-2(c^2+d^2)\equiv0\pmod4.
$$
Reduction modulo two first gives $a\equiv b\pmod2$. Since $ab$ is even, both $a$ and $b$ are even. The same congruence now gives $c^2+d^2\equiv0\pmod2$, or $c\equiv d\pmod2$. Therefore every <algebraic integer> has the form
$$
\gamma=\frac a2+\frac b2\alpha+\frac{c-d}{2}\beta+d w,
\qquad w=\frac{\beta+\alpha\beta}{2},
$$
with four <integer> coefficients.

For the converse, $1,\alpha,\beta$ are <algebraic integers>, and
$$
w^2=\frac{m_2(1+m_1)}4+\frac{m_2}2\alpha\in\mathbb Z[\alpha].
$$
Here $4\mid1+m_1$ and $2\mid m_2$, so both coefficients are <integers>. The <monic polynomial> $X^2-w^2$ and <integral dependence is transitive> show that $w$ is an <algebraic integer>. The four displayed generators are rationally <linearly independent>, since their coefficient <matrix> in $1,\alpha,\beta,\alpha\beta$ has <determinant> $1/2$. Hence the <integral basis for coprime biquadratic radicands congruent to three and two> is
$$
\boxed{\left\{1,\sqrt{m_1},\sqrt{m_2},\frac{\sqrt{m_2}+\sqrt{m_1m_2}}2\right\}}.
$$
In this formula $\sqrt{m_1m_2}$ denotes the product $\alpha\beta$ of the chosen square roots; this convention also handles negative radicands.

Put $\alpha=\sqrt{m_1}$, $\beta=\sqrt{m_2}$, and
$$
\omega=\frac{1+\beta}{2}.
$$
The proposed <integral basis for coprime biquadratic radicands congruent to two and one> is
$$
\boxed{\{1,\alpha,\omega,\alpha\omega\}}.
$$
Each entry is an <algebraic integer>, by the <ring of integers of a quadratic field> formula and closure under products. Their coefficient <matrix> in $1,\alpha,\beta,\alpha\beta$ has <determinant> $1/4$, so they are rationally <linearly independent>. It remains to show that they span every <algebraic integer>.

Write $\gamma=(a+b\alpha+c\beta+d\alpha\beta)/2$ initially with rational $a,b,c,d$. The <field traces> to $\mathbb Q(\alpha)$ and $\mathbb Q(\alpha\beta)$ are $a+b\alpha$ and $a+d\alpha\beta$. Since $m_1\equiv m_1m_2\equiv2\pmod4$, the corresponding <integral basis> in each subfield gives $a,b,d\in\mathbb Z$. The <field trace> to $\mathbb Q(\beta)$ is $a+c\beta$. Membership in $\mathbb Z[\omega]$ says that $2a,2c$ are <integers> of equal parity. As $a$ is already an <integer>, this forces $c\in\mathbb Z$ too.

Now the <field norm> to $\mathbb Q(\beta)$ is
$$
\frac A4+\frac{ac-m_1bd}{2}\beta,
\qquad A=a^2+m_2c^2-m_1b^2-m_1m_2d^2.
$$
By the <ring of integers of a quadratic field> formula, $A/2$ is an <integer> and has the same parity as $ac-m_1bd$. Thus $A$ is even, giving $a\equiv c\pmod2$, and
$$
A\equiv2ac-2m_1bd\equiv2ac\pmod4.
$$
Using $m_1\equiv2$, $m_2\equiv1$ and $a\equiv c\pmod2$, this becomes
$$
0\equiv A-2ac\equiv(a-c)^2-2(b^2+d^2)
\equiv-2(b^2+d^2)\pmod4.
$$
Hence $b\equiv d\pmod2$. Finally,
$$
\gamma=\frac{a-c}{2}+\frac{b-d}{2}\alpha+c\omega+d\alpha\omega,
$$
an expression with <integer> coefficients. This proves the asserted <integral basis> without assuming a general theorem about products of elements of an <integral basis>.

Write $R=\mathcal O_K$. We prove <unique factorization of ideals in a number field>, rather than assuming this conclusion as a property of a <Dedekind domain>. Three preliminary facts about $R$ will be useful. Its <additive group> is a finite-rank <free abelian group>, so every <ideal> is finitely generated and the <ascending chain condition> holds. It is an <integrally closed domain>, by <integral dependence is transitive>. Finally, every nonzero <prime ideal> is a <maximal ideal>. For this last fact, choose $0\ne a\in P$. Multiplication by $a$ is an injective endomorphism of the finite-rank <free abelian group> $R$ with nonzero <determinant>, so $R/(a)$ is finite. Consequently $R/P$ is a finite <integral domain> and hence a <field>.

First, every nonzero proper <ideal> contains a product of nonzero <prime ideals>. Otherwise choose a maximal counterexample $J$ under inclusion, using the <ascending chain condition>. It cannot be a <prime ideal>. Choose $x,y\notin J$ with $xy\in J$. The strictly larger <ideals> $J+(x)$ and $J+(y)$ each contain a product of nonzero <prime ideals>, allowing the empty product $R$ when necessary. Multiplying those products gives a product contained in
$$
(J+(x))(J+(y))\subseteq J,
$$
a contradiction.

Next fix a nonzero <prime ideal> $P$, choose $0\ne a\in P$, and choose a product of nonzero <prime ideals>
$$
P_1\cdots P_r\subseteq(a)
$$
with $r$ minimal. Because $(a)\subseteq P$, primality and maximality imply $P_j=P$ for some $j$; reorder so that $P_1=P$. Minimality provides $b\in P_2\cdots P_r$ with $b\notin(a)$, taking the remaining product to be $R$ if $r=1$. Thus
$$
x=b/a\notin R,\qquad xP\subseteq R.
$$
Define the <fractional ideal>
$$
P^{-1}=\{z\in K:zP\subseteq R\}.
$$
It contains $R$ and $x$. The <ideal> $PP^{-1}$ lies between $P$ and $R$, so it is $P$ or $R$. If it were $P$, then $xP\subseteq P$. Applying the <determinant trick> to a finite <generating set of an ideal> for $P$ gives a <monic polynomial> over $R$ satisfied by $x$. Since $R$ is an <integrally closed domain>, this would put $x$ in $R$, a contradiction. Therefore
$$
\boxed{PP^{-1}=R}.
$$
This proves that each nonzero <prime ideal> is an <invertible fractional ideal>, independently of the desired factorization theorem.

Now take a nonzero proper <ideal> $A$ and a <maximal ideal> $P$ containing it. The <ideal> $B=P^{-1}A$ satisfies
$$
A\subseteq B\subseteq R,\qquad A=PB.
$$
The first inclusion is strict: if $A=B$, the same $x\in P^{-1}\setminus R$ would stabilize $A$, and the <determinant trick> would again force $x\in R$. Repeating gives a strictly ascending chain until it reaches $R$; the <ascending chain condition> ensures this happens after finitely many steps. The identities $A=PB$ then express $A$ as a product of nonzero <prime ideals>.

For uniqueness, suppose $P_1\cdots P_r=Q_1\cdots Q_s$. Since the right-hand side is contained in $P_1$, some $Q_j$ is contained in $P_1$. Both are nonzero <maximal ideals>, so $Q_j=P_1$. Multiplication by $P_1^{-1}$ cancels this factor. Repeat. A nonempty product of proper <prime ideals> cannot equal $R$, so both products finish together. This proves uniqueness up to reordering, including repeated factors.

By the <correspondence theorem for ideals>, an <ideal> of $R/P^n$ has the form $J/P^n$ with $P^n\subseteq J\subseteq R$. If $J\ne R$, apply <unique factorization of ideals in a number field> to $J$. Every <prime ideal> $Q$ dividing $J$ contains $J$ and hence $P^n$. Primality gives $P\subseteq Q$, and maximality gives $Q=P$. Thus $J=P^i$ for some $i\geq1$.

The inclusion $P^n\subseteq P^i$ forces $i\leq n$: if $i>n$, cancellation using the <invertible fractional ideal> $P^n$ would give $R\subseteq P^{i-n}$, which is impossible. The zero <ideal> corresponds to $i=n$, while the unit <ideal> corresponds to $i=0$. Consequently the nonzero proper <ideals> are exactly
$$
\boxed{P^i/P^n,\qquad 1\leq i<n}.
$$
When $n=1$, there are none, in agreement with $R/P$ being a <field>.

For every $i\geq0$, the inclusion $P^{i+1}\subseteq P^i$ is strict: equality, followed by multiplication by the inverse of $P^i$, would give $P=R$. Hence one can choose
$$
\pi\in P^i\setminus P^{i+1}.
$$
Put $J=(\pi)+P^{i+1}$. Then
$$
P^{i+1}\subsetneq J\subseteq P^i.
$$
The classification just proved, applied modulo $P^{i+1}$, leaves no <ideal> strictly between consecutive powers. It follows that
$$
\boxed{P^i=(\pi)+P^{i+1}}.
$$
For $i=0$, the same argument says $(\pi)+P=R$, using $\pi\notin P$ and maximality.

The extension to every $r\geq1$ follows by <mathematical induction>. Suppose $P^i=(\pi)+P^{i+r}$. Multiplication of the initial equality by $P^r$ gives
$$
P^{i+r}=\pi P^r+P^{i+r+1}.
$$
Substitution yields
$$
P^i=(\pi)+\pi P^r+P^{i+r+1}=(\pi)+P^{i+r+1}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{P^i=(\pi)+P^{i+r}\quad\text{for every }r\geq1}.
$$
Taking $r=n-i$ for $0\leq i<n$ gives
$$
\boxed{P^i/P^n=(\pi+P^n)}.
$$
This is a <principal ideal> of the <quotient ring> $R/P^n$. Its zero <ideal> is generated by zero, so every <ideal> of this <quotient ring> is a <principal ideal>.

**The printed domain assertion is false.** For example, take $K=\mathbb Q$ and $I=(4)$. Then $\mathcal O_K/I=\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z$ has the nonzero <zero divisor> $2+(4)$, so it is not an <integral domain> and cannot be a <principal ideal domain>. The valid conclusion is that $\mathcal O_K/I$ is a <principal ideal ring>. This error is present in the original PDF, not just its transcription.

To prove the corrected conclusion, factor
$$
I=\prod_{j=1}^sP_j^{n_j}
$$
into powers of distinct nonzero <prime ideals>. These powers are pairwise <comaximal ideals>. Indeed, a <maximal ideal> containing two such powers would contain both underlying <prime ideals>, forcing them to be equal. The <Chinese remainder theorem> gives
$$
R/I\simeq\prod_{j=1}^s R/P_j^{n_j}.
$$
Every factor is a <principal ideal ring> by the preceding part. Every <ideal> $H$ in a finite <direct product of rings> is a product $H_1\times\cdots\times H_s$ of <ideals> in the factors: multiplication by the coordinate <idempotent elements> separates the coordinates of every element of $H$. Write $H_j=(h_j)$. Then
$$
H_1\times\cdots\times H_s=((h_1,\ldots,h_s)),
$$
so $R/I$ is a <principal ideal ring>. This proves the <principal ideal property of quotients of a Dedekind domain> in the present setting. In fact, this finite <quotient ring> is an <integral domain> precisely when $I$ is a <prime ideal>, in which case it is a <field>.

For the final deduction, choose any $0\ne a\in I$ and apply the result to the nonzero proper <ideal> $(a)$. The <ideal> $I/(a)$ of $R/(a)$ is generated by one residue class $b+(a)$, which can be represented by $b\in I$. For each $x\in I$, some $r\in R$ satisfies $x-rb\in(a)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{I=(a,b)}.
$$
This proves <two generators for an ideal in a Dedekind domain>; moreover, its first generator can be any prescribed nonzero element of $I$.

Let $d_K$ denote the <field discriminant>, and put $n=[K:\mathbb Q]\geq2$. Let $r_1$ be the number of real <field embeddings> and $r_2$ the number of conjugate pairs of nonreal <field embeddings>, so $n=r_1+2r_2$. The <Minkowski bound for ideal classes> provides a nonzero <integral ideal> $A$ satisfying
$$
1\leq N(A)\leq\left(\frac4\pi\right)^{r_2}\frac{n!}{n^n}\sqrt{|d_K|}.
$$
Rearranging gives the <Minkowski lower bound for a number-field discriminant>
$$
|d_K|\geq\left(\frac\pi4\right)^{2r_2}\left(\frac{n^n}{n!}\right)^2.
$$
Because $2r_2\leq n$ and $\pi/4<1$, this is at least
$$
\left(\frac\pi4\right)^n\left(\frac{n^n}{n!}\right)^2.
$$
Apply the <arithmetic-geometric mean inequality> to $1,2,\ldots,n$:
$$
n!\leq\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^n.
$$
It follows that
$$
|d_K|\geq\left(\frac{\pi n^2}{(n+1)^2}\right)^n
\geq\left(\frac{4\pi}{9}\right)^n>1,
$$
where $n/(n+1)\geq2/3$ for $n\geq2$ was used. Thus
$$
\boxed{|d_K|>1}.
$$
The source writes $|\Delta(K)^2|>1$. If $\Delta(K)$ denotes the embedding <determinant>, its square is $d_K$; if it denotes the <field discriminant>, the displayed result also implies the stated inequality. The conclusion therefore does not depend on that notational convention.

To compute the <Ideal class group of Q of square root thirty-five>, put $\theta=\sqrt{35}$. The <ring of integers of a quadratic field> formula gives $R=\mathbb Z[\theta]$, and the <field discriminant> is $4\cdot35=140$. Both <field embeddings> are real, so the <Minkowski bound for ideal classes> is
$$
\frac{2!}{2^2}\sqrt{140}=\sqrt{35}<6.
$$
Every <ideal class> therefore has an <integral ideal> representative of <ideal norm> at most five. By <prime ideal factorization> and multiplicativity of the <ideal norm>, the <ideal class group> is generated by <prime ideals> of <ideal norm> at most five.

Apply the <Dedekind factorization theorem> to $X^2-35$. At two and five it has a repeated linear factor, giving
$$
(2)=P_2^2,\quad P_2=(2,\theta-1),\quad N(P_2)=2;
\qquad
(5)=P_5^2,\quad P_5=(5,\theta),\quad N(P_5)=5.
$$
At three, $X^2-35\equiv X^2+1$ has no root, so $(3)$ itself is a <prime ideal> of <ideal norm> nine, outside the bound. These are all the relevant <prime ideals>: a <prime ideal> of <ideal norm> four would have to lie above two with <residue degree> two, but the factorization above two has only the single factor of degree one. Consequently $[P_2]$ and $[P_5]$ generate, and each has <order of a group element> dividing two.

The <algebraic integer> $5+\theta$ has <field norm> $25-35=-10$. It belongs to both $P_2$ and $P_5$. Hence its <principal ideal> is contained in $P_2\cap P_5=P_2P_5$. Both have <ideal norm> ten, so
$$
(5+\theta)=P_2P_5.
$$
It follows that $[P_5]=[P_2]^{-1}=[P_2]$, and the <ideal class group> is generated by one element of <order of a group element> at most two.

Finally, $P_2$ is not a <principal ideal>. If $P_2=(a+b\theta)$ with $a,b\in\mathbb Z$, the equality of <ideal norm> and absolute <field norm> would imply
$$
a^2-35b^2=\pm2.
$$
Modulo five this requires $a^2\equiv2$ or $3$, whereas squares modulo five are $0,1,4$. Thus $[P_2]\ne1$, proving
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb Q(\sqrt{35}))\simeq\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z}.
$$
This computes the ordinary <ideal class group>, with no positivity restriction on generators.

Let $\alpha=\sqrt[3]{3}$. The <polynomial> $X^3-3$ is <Eisenstein> at three, so the <number field> has <degree of a field extension> three. Use the permitted <ring of integers> $R=\mathbb Z[\alpha]$, whose <integral basis> is $1,\alpha,\alpha^2$. The <field traces> $\operatorname{Tr}(1)=3$, $\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha)=\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha^2)=0$, $\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha^3)=9$, and $\operatorname{Tr}(\alpha^4)=0$ give
$$
d_K=\det\begin{pmatrix}3&0&0\\0&0&9\\0&9&0\end{pmatrix}=-243.
$$
There is one real <field embedding> and one conjugate pair of nonreal <field embeddings>. The <Minkowski bound for ideal classes> is therefore
$$
\frac4\pi\frac{3!}{3^3}\sqrt{243}=\frac{8\sqrt3}{\pi}<5.
$$
Every <ideal class> has an <integral ideal> representative of <ideal norm> at most four, so it is enough to show that every <prime ideal> of <ideal norm> at most four is a <principal ideal>.

Modulo two,
$$
X^3-3\equiv(X+1)(X^2+X+1),
$$
where the quadratic factor is an <irreducible polynomial> over the <finite field> $\mathbb F_2$. The <Dedekind factorization theorem> gives
$$
(2)=P_2Q_2,\quad P_2=(2,\alpha-1),\quad N(P_2)=2,\quad
Q_2=(2,\alpha^2+\alpha+1),\quad N(Q_2)=4.
$$
Now
$$
N_{K/\mathbb Q}(\alpha-1)=2,
\qquad (\alpha-1)(\alpha^2+\alpha+1)=2.
$$
Thus $(\alpha-1)=P_2$, by containment and equality of <ideal norms>, and cancellation in the second identity gives $(\alpha^2+\alpha+1)=Q_2$. This explicitly handles the <prime ideal> of <ideal norm> four, as well as the one of <ideal norm> two.

Modulo three the <polynomial> is $X^3$, so
$$
(3)=P_3^3,\qquad P_3=(3,\alpha)=(\alpha),\qquad N(P_3)=3.
$$
These exhaust the <prime ideals> of <ideal norm> at most four. Every bounded representative factors into the listed <principal ideals>, and hence is a <principal ideal>. Therefore the <Class group of Q of cube root of three> is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{3}))=1}.
$$

= Solution
{parent=d}

For $p\equiv1\pmod{20}$, put $\theta=\sqrt{-p}$. Since $-p\equiv3\pmod4$, the <ring of integers of a quadratic field> formula gives $R=\mathbb Z[\theta]$. Modulo five,
$$
X^2+p\equiv X^2+1=(X-2)(X+2).
$$
The two roots are distinct, so the <Dedekind factorization theorem> gives
$$
(5)=P\overline P,\qquad
P=(5,\theta-2),\quad\overline P=(5,\theta+2),\quad
N(P)=N(\overline P)=5.
$$
In particular $P\ne\overline P$.

We prove that $[P]$ has <order of a group element> greater than $n$. Suppose instead that $P^k$ is a <principal ideal> for some $1\leq k\leq n$. Since $P^k\subseteq R$, its generator is in $R$: write $P^k=(a+b\theta)$ with $a,b\in\mathbb Z$. Taking <ideal norms> gives the positive <field norm>
$$
5^k=a^2+pb^2.
$$
But $5^k\leq5^n<p$, so $b=0$. Thus $a^2=5^k$, forcing $k$ to be even and $a=\pm5^{k/2}$. This would give
$$
P^k=(a)=(5)^{k/2}=P^{k/2}\overline P^{k/2},
$$
contrary to <unique factorization of ideals in a number field> and $P\ne\overline P$. Consequently no such $k$ exists. The <finiteness of the ideal class group> ensures that $[P]$ has finite <order of a group element>, so
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ord}([P])>n}.
$$
For every positive <integer> $n$, the allowed infinitude of <prime numbers> in the progression $1\pmod{20}$ supplies one larger than $5^n$. Applying the construction proves <unbounded element orders in imaginary quadratic ideal class groups>. In particular, this establishes elements of arbitrarily large <order of a group element>, rather than merely unbounded <class numbers>.
