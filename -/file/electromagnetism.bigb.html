= Electromagnetism
{wiki}

Electromagnetism describes how <electric charge> and <electric current> interact through the <electric field> $\mathbf E$ and <magnetic field> $\mathbf B$. The <Maxwell equations> describe the fields and their sources; the <Lorentz force> describes the response of a particle of <electric charge> $q$ and <velocity> $\mathbf v$:
$$
\mathbf F=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B),\qquad
\frac{d\mathcal E_{\mathrm{particle}}}{dt}=q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v.
$$
The <electric field> can change the particle's <energy>. The magnetic part of the <Lorentz force> is perpendicular to <velocity>, so <magnetic forces do no work>; they can bend a trajectory. These are local interactions: a particle responds to the <electromagnetic field> at its position. \a[https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_01.html][Feynman lectures, II.1: fields and forces]{ref}.

The remarkable unification is that the same <Maxwell equations> connect <Coulomb's law>, <Faraday's law of induction>, and freely propagating <electromagnetic waves>. A changing <electric field> contributes to magnetic circulation through <displacement current>; together with <Faraday's law>, this permits waves in vacuum without conducting matter. The <Poynting theorem> then explains how the fields store and transport <energy>. This connects the force on a charge to the energy carried by light.

In his 1865 paper, <James Clerk Maxwell> derived transverse disturbances and compared the propagation speed inferred from electrical measurements with independently measured light speeds. Part VI, especially §§95–97, argues that their agreement supports identifying light as an <electromagnetic wave>. This was a comparison with finite experimental accuracy, rather than a numerical identity between the historical measurements. \a[https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rstl.1865.0008][Maxwell, A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field, Philosophical Transactions 155 (1865), 459–512]{ref}; \a[https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/A_Dynamical_Theory_of_the_Electromagnetic_Field/Part_VI][transcription of Maxwell's original Part VI]{ref}.

= Thermoelectric effect
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{wiki}

A <thermoelectric effect> couples heat flow and electrical transport. Temperature differences can generate an electromotive force, while <electric currents> can transport heat. These effects concern nonequilibrium transport and must be distinguished from equilibrium contact potentials.

= Seebeck effect
{c}
{parent=Thermoelectric effect}

The <Seebeck effect> is the generation of a thermoelectric voltage by a temperature difference. Two materials with different thermopowers form a circuit whose electromotive force is the temperature integral of their Seebeck-coefficient difference, with sign set by circuit orientation. In a nondegenerate <semiconductor>, <electron> and hole transport usually give opposite thermopower signs.

= Seebeck coefficient
{c}
{parent=Seebeck effect}
{title2=$S$}

The <Seebeck coefficient> is thermoelectric voltage per temperature difference in the small-gradient limit, with its sign depending on the stated voltage and orientation convention. In a two-material loop the net electromotive force is an oriented integral $\int_{T_1}^{T_2}(S_1-S_2)\,dT$. It is a transport coefficient, so equilibrium density-of-states data alone do not determine it.

= Lorenz-gauge reduction of the electromagnetic action
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{c}

The <electromagnetic field tensor> action differs from the component-gradient potential action by $(2\mu_0)^{-1}\int \partial^bA^a\partial_aA_b\,d^4x$. Integration by parts expresses this term as a boundary integral plus $(2\mu_0)^{-1}\int(\partial_aA^a)^2\,d^4x$. With the <Lorenz gauge> and fixed or vanishing boundary data, the two actions therefore differ only by a boundary term. Varying the component-gradient action without restriction gives the potential wave equations; the Lorenz condition must be supplied to recover all <Maxwell equations>.

= Volume conductor
{parent=Bioelectromagnetism}

A <volume conductor> is a conducting three-dimensional medium through which <electric currents> flow. In the quasistatic bioelectric model, the total <current density> is $\mathbf J=\mathbf J^p-\sigma\nabla u$, comprising an impressed source current and its Ohmic return current. <Electric charge conservation> gives $\nabla\cdot(\sigma\nabla u)=\nabla\cdot\mathbf J^p$. An insulating boundary has zero normal total current.

= Geselowitz formula
{c}
{parent=Volume conductor}
{title2=$\mathbf B=\mathbf B^p-\frac{\mu_0\sigma}{4\pi}\int_S u\,\mathbf n'\times\frac{\mathbf r-\mathbf r'}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|^3}\,dS'$}

For a homogeneous <volume conductor> with an insulating boundary $S$, the <Geselowitz formula> expresses the exterior <magnetic field> as the primary <Biot-Savart law> field minus a surface contribution from the induced <electric potential>. For a <current dipole>, $\mathbf B^p=\mu_0\mathbf Q\times(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0)/(4\pi|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0|^3)$. On a spherical boundary, $\mathbf n'$ is parallel to $\mathbf r'$, so the surface term has zero radial component at the observation point.

= Spherical volume conductor model
{parent=Volume conductor}
{title2=$|\mathbf r|<a$}

A <spherical volume conductor model> approximates a conducting body by a sphere, usually with uniform or radially stratified conductivity. It provides explicit forward models for <electroencephalography> and <magnetoencephalography>. Its symmetry makes a <radial current dipole> magnetically silent outside the sphere; departures from spherical symmetry need not retain that property.

= Sarvas formula
{c}
{parent=Spherical volume conductor model}
{title2=$\mathbf B=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\nabla\frac{(\mathbf Q\times\mathbf r_0)\cdot\mathbf r}{F}$}

For the exterior of a <spherical volume conductor model>, put $P=|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0|$, $r=|\mathbf r|$ and $F=rP^2+P\mathbf r\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0)$. The displayed <Sarvas formula> follows by taking the radial component of the <Geselowitz formula> and integrating the <magnetic scalar potential> from infinity. It is independent of a spherically symmetric conductivity profile. The sign shown uses $\mathbf B=+\mu_0\nabla U/(4\pi)$; conventions defining a magnetic potential with a minus sign reverse $U$. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3823129/[Sarvas's original paper] derives the spherical-conductor field and its consequences for the inverse problem.

= Spherical current-dipole logarithmic kernel
{parent=Sarvas formula}
{title2=$\phi=\frac1b\log\frac{r+P+b}{r+P-b},\quad b=|\mathbf r_0|$}

For $r>b$ and $P=|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0|$, the displayed kernel satisfies $\phi=\int_r^\infty ds/[s|s\widehat{\mathbf r}-\mathbf r_0|]$. The limit at $b=0$ is $1/r$. Differentiating along $\mathbf Q\times\mathbf r_0$, which preserves $b$, gives $(\mathbf Q\times\mathbf r_0)\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf r_0}\phi=(\mathbf Q\times\mathbf r_0)\cdot\mathbf r/F$. Thus it generates the normalized <magnetic scalar potential> in the <Sarvas formula>.

= Maxwell–Chern–Simons one-form equation
{c}
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{title2=$d{*F}+kF=0$}

For a <differential one-form> $A$ on an oriented three-dimensional spacetime, varying $\frac12\int(F\wedge*F+kA\wedge F)$, with $F=dA$, gives $d*F+kF=0$. With signature $(-,+,+)$, ${*}^2=-1$ on one- and two-forms; writing $B=*F$ gives $dB-k*B=0$ and the identity $d*B=0$. An Abelian <gauge transformation> changes the action by $\frac k2\int_{\partial M}\Lambda F$. Thus the bulk field equation is invariant, while invariance of the integrated action depends on boundary conditions.

= Electrical circuit

An electrical circuit connects components that carry <electric current> and sustain <electric potential difference> differences. Its dynamics may couple <electrical resistance>, <inductance> and <capacitance>.

= RLC circuit
{c}
{parent=Electrical circuit}
{wiki}

An RLC circuit combines <electrical resistance>, <inductance> and <capacitance>. Stored <energy> can oscillate between magnetic and electric forms, while <Joule heating> dissipates it.

= Series RLC circuit
{parent=RLC circuit}

In a series RLC circuit, the same <electric current> $I=q'$ flows through a resistor, inductor and <capacitor>. Summing their <electric potential difference> drops gives $Lq''+Rq'+q/C=V$, and differentiating gives
$$
LI''+RI'+I/C=V'.
$$
The homogeneous <damped harmonic oscillator> has angular frequency $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$ and decay rate $\gamma=R/(2L)$.

= Resonant series RLC response
{parent=Series RLC circuit}
{title2=$I_0(t)=V_M\sin(\omega_0t)/R$}

For a <series RLC circuit> driven by $V(t)=V_M\sin(\omega_0t)$ with $R>0$ and $\omega_0^2=1/(LC)$, substitution shows that $I_0=V_M\sin(\omega_0t)/R$ solves the current equation. The inductive and capacitive terms cancel at this <resonance>. Every homogeneous contribution decays, so every solution approaches this periodic response. Its <Q factor> is $L\omega_0/R$.

= Overdamped RLC response
{parent=Series RLC circuit}

When $R^2>4L/C$, the homogeneous <series RLC circuit> has two distinct negative <characteristic roots>, $[-R\pm\sqrt{R^2-4L/C}]/(2L)$. Its current is a <linear combination> of the corresponding decaying <exponential functions>.

= Critically damped RLC response
{parent=Series RLC circuit}

When $R^2=4L/C$, the homogeneous <series RLC circuit> has one repeated <characteristic root>, $-R/(2L)$. Its current is $e^{-Rt/(2L)}(A+Bt)$, with constants determined by the initial data.

= Underdamped RLC response
{parent=Series RLC circuit}

When $R^2<4L/C$, the homogeneous <series RLC circuit> response oscillates with angular frequency $\sqrt{1/(LC)-R^2/(4L^2)}$ and envelope $e^{-Rt/(2L)}$. Its <characteristic roots> form a complex-conjugate pair with negative <real part> when $R>0$.

= Capacitor
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{wiki}

A capacitor stores opposite electric charges on two conductors separated by an insulator. Its capacitance is $C=Q/V$.

= Parallel-plate capacitor
{parent=Capacitor}
{title2=$C=\epsilon A/d$}

Two parallel conducting plates of area $A$ separated by a small gap $d$, filled by material of <permittivity> $\epsilon$, have <capacitance> $C\simeq\epsilon A/d$ when fringing fields are negligible. Indeed, the interior <electric field> is approximately $Q/(\epsilon A)$, and the <electric potential difference> is approximately $Qd/(\epsilon A)$. A narrow curved capacitor gap has the same leading local approximation when its width is much less than the curvature radius.

= Spherical capacitor
{parent=Capacitor}
{title2=$C=4\pi\epsilon_0ab/(b-a)$}

Two concentric conducting spherical shells of radii $a<b$ with charges $Q,-Q$ have an <electric field> only in the intervening vacuum: $E_r=Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)$ by <Gauss's law>. Integrating gives a potential difference $Q(1/a-1/b)/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$, and hence the displayed <capacitance>. The <electrostatic energy> is $Q^2/(2C)$, either by charging work or by integrating $\epsilon_0|E|^2/2$ over the gap.

= Capacitor energy
{title2=$U=Q^2/(2C)$}
{parent=Capacitor}

A capacitor of capacitance $C$ carrying charge magnitude $Q$ stores
$$
U=\frac{Q^2}{2C}=\frac12CV^2=\frac12QV.
$$

= Fixed-voltage capacitor energy balance
{parent=Capacitor energy}
{title2=$dU_{\rm field}=dW_{\rm mech}+V\,dQ$}

For a quasistatic change of <capacitance> at fixed voltage, the <capacitor energy> is $U=CV^2/2$ and charge is $Q=CV$. Electrical work supplied to the field is $V\,dQ$, so mechanical work is $dU-V\,dQ=-V^2dC/2$. Increasing the separation of parallel plates makes $dC<0$: mechanical work is positive even though field energy decreases, because electrical energy returns to the source.

= Electromagnetic radiation
{parent=Electromagnetism}

<Electromagnetic radiation> consists of propagating oscillations of the <electric field> and <magnetic field> that carry <energy> and <momentum>.

= X-ray
{c}
{parent=Electromagnetic radiation}

X-rays are high-frequency <electromagnetic radiation>, conventionally with wavelengths of roughly $0.01$ to $10\,\mathrm{nm}$. The precise wavelength convention varies with context. Hot accreting gas and energetic electronic processes can emit X-rays, whose short wavelengths make them useful probes of compact objects and atomic structure.

= Larmor formula
{parent=Electromagnetic radiation}
{c}
{title2=$P=\frac{q^2\mu_0a^2}{6\pi c}$}
{wiki}

The nonrelativistic radiated power of an accelerated point charge is $P=q^2\mu_0a^2/(6\pi c)$ in <SI> units. Integrating its dipole radiation pattern over solid angle gives this result; relativistic motion requires the Lienard generalization.

= Liénard radiated-power formula
{c}
{parent=Larmor formula}
{title2=$P=\mu_0q^2\gamma^6(|a|^2-|v\times a|^2)/(6\pi)$, $c=1$}

= Lienard formula
{c}
{synonym}

The relativistic generalization of the <Larmor formula> uses the invariant proper-acceleration magnitude: $P=-\mu_0q^2 A^aA_a/(6\pi)$ in signature $(+---)$. In coordinate time this is the displayed expression. For perpendicular magnetic circular motion, $\gamma m|a|=|q|vB$, giving $P=\mu_0q^4(\gamma^2-1)B^2/(6\pi m^2)$. The statement concerns total instantaneous emitted power; directional radiation flux is not itself invariant.

= Mie scattering
{parent=Electromagnetic radiation}
{c}
{wiki}

The electromagnetic scattering solution for a sphere describes the transition between <Rayleigh scattering> and <geometrical optics>. Grain-size-dependent efficiencies can produce structure in the <radiation-pressure coefficient>.

= Bremsstrahlung
{parent=Electromagnetic radiation}
{wiki}

<Bremsstrahlung> is <electromagnetic radiation> emitted when a charged particle accelerates during interaction with another charged particle or field. The microscopic process $e^-+Z\to e^-+Z+\gamma$ creates a <photon>, while its inverse absorbs one. Thus it contributes to a photon-number collision source in the <photon number current>, unlike ordinary <Compton scattering>. <Thermal bremsstrahlung> averages this emission over a thermal distribution of charged particles.

= Thermal radiation
{parent=Electromagnetic radiation}
{wiki}

Thermal radiation is <electromagnetic radiation> emitted by matter because of its temperature. Matter in <thermal equilibrium> emits a spectrum governed by its absorptivity, approaching the <Planck law> for a <blackbody>.

= Rayleigh scattering
{c}
{parent=Electromagnetic radiation}
{wiki}

Rayleigh scattering is elastic scattering by particles much smaller than the wavelength. For a fixed dielectric sphere, its scattering efficiency scales as the fourth power of the size-to-wavelength ratio.

= Linear charge density

Linear charge density is <electric charge> divided by length along a charged line, or locally $dQ/d\ell$. Its SI unit is coulombs per metre. = Electric current density

= Current dipole
{parent=Current density}
{title2=$\mathbf J^p(\mathbf r)=\mathbf Q\delta(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0)$}

A <current dipole> idealizes a localized impressed <electric current density> by the displayed <Dirac delta function> distribution. Its moment $\mathbf Q$ has units of current times length. In a homogeneous <volume conductor> of conductivity $\sigma$, its primary <electric potential> is $\Psi(\mathbf r;\mathbf r_0)=\mathbf Q\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0)/(4\pi\sigma|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0|^3)$.

= Radial current dipole
{parent=Current dipole}
{title2=$\mathbf Q\times\mathbf r_0=0$}

A <radial current dipole> has moment parallel to its displacement from the sphere centre. The <Sarvas formula> gives identically zero exterior <magnetic field> for such a source in a <spherical volume conductor model>, although its <electric potential> need not vanish. This is a nonuniqueness mechanism for the inverse problem in <magnetoencephalography>.

= Four-current
{parent=Current density}
{title2=$J^\mu=(c\rho,\boldsymbol J)$}

The four-current combines charge density and <current density> into the <four-vector> $J^\mu=(c\rho,\boldsymbol J)$. With $x^\mu=(ct,\boldsymbol x)$, its <four-divergence> vanishes by the charge <continuity equation>: $\partial_\mu J^\mu=\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol J=0$. It is the source in the covariant <Maxwell equations>.

= Surface current density
{title2=$\mathbf K$}
{parent=Current density}
{wiki}

A surface current density is electric current per unit transverse length confined to a surface. Integrating a thin layer's <current density> through its thickness gives $\mathbf K$.

= Current sheet
{parent=Surface current density}
{wiki}

A current sheet idealizes electric current as supported on a surface. It produces a jump in the tangential <magnetic field>.

= Force on a current sheet from the mean magnetic field
{parent=Current sheet}
{title2=$\mathbf f_S=\mathbf K\times(\mathbf B_++\mathbf B_-)/2$}

The <Lorentz force> per unit area on an ideal <current sheet> is computed using the mean of its limiting <magnetic fields>. The sheet's own local <magnetic field> is equal and opposite on its two sides and cancels in this mean. Equivalently, integrate the <Lorentz force density> through a thin regularized layer: its self-field contribution averages to zero, leaving the external field acting on the <surface current density>.

= Magnetic-field jump across a surface current
{parent=Current sheet}

For a surface current density $\mathbf K$ on an interface with unit normal $\mathbf n$, <Ampère's law> gives
$$
\mathbf n\times(\mathbf B_+-\mathbf B_-)=\frac{4\pi}{c}\mathbf K
$$
in Gaussian units, or $\mu_0\mathbf K$ in <SI> units.

= Surface conductivity
{title2=$\Sigma$}
{parent=Surface current density}

Surface conductivity is the conductivity of a thin layer integrated through its thickness. The sheet form of <Ohm's law> is $\mathbf K=\Sigma\mathbf E_\parallel$.

= Line current

A line current idealizes an <electric current> as flowing along a curve; its current-density volume integral reduces to $I\,d\ell$ along that curve.

= Electromotive force
{parent=Electric current}
{wiki}

Electromotive force is work supplied per unit charge around a circuit.

= Motional electromotive force
{parent=Electromotive force}
{title2=$\mathcal E_{\rm mot}=\oint_C(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\mathbf l$}

Motional electromotive force is the contribution caused by a conductor moving through a <magnetic field>. The magnetic part of the <Lorentz force> separates charges within the conductor. For a bar of length $L$ moving at velocity $v$ perpendicular to a uniform field, its magnitude is $BL|v|$; the sign follows the chosen circuit orientation. Together with the electric-field contribution it enters <Faraday's law> for moving circuits.

= Electromagnetic braking of a sliding rod
{parent=Motional electromotive force}
{title2=$\tau=mR/(B^2\ell^2)$}

A conducting rod sliding on rails separated by $\ell$ in a transverse magnetic field has induced current magnitude $B\ell v/R$ when self-inductance is neglected and resistance is $R$. The <Lorentz force> opposes motion with magnitude $B^2\ell^2v/R$, so free motion decays exponentially on the displayed time constant. <Joule heating> integrates to the rod's lost kinetic energy. Current direction follows the <Lenz's law>.

= Magnetic flux transport for a moving circuit
{parent=Motional electromotive force}
{title2=$\frac{d\Phi}{dt}=\int_S\partial_t\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf S-\oint_C(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\mathbf r$}

For a smoothly moving circuit and a divergence-free <magnetic field>, the <magnetic flux> derivative is $d\Phi/dt=\int_S\partial_t\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf S-\oint_C(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\mathbf r$. The minus sign follows by orienting the swept side surface compatibly with the two spanning surfaces. Combining this transport identity with <Faraday's law> yields the moving-loop electromotive-force law.

For an ohmic element, electrical resistance is $R=V/I$ and conductance is its reciprocal $G=I/V$. They describe the same voltage-current response using reciprocal units. The relation $V=IR$ is <Ohm's law>.

= Electrical resistance
{parent=Electrical resistance and conductance}

Electrical resistance relates voltage and current by Ohm's law $V=IR$.

= Ohm's law
{title2=$V=IR$}
{parent=Electrical resistance}
{c}
{wiki}

Ohm's law states that the potential difference across an ohmic component equals its current times its resistance: $V=IR$.

= Electrical conductivity
{parent=Ohm's law}
{title2=$\sigma$}

Electrical <electrical conductivity> relates <current density> to the local <electric field> in the rest frame of an isotropic ohmic medium: $\mathbf J=\sigma\mathbf E$. It is a bulk material coefficient, distinct from a sheet surface <electrical conductivity>. A moving conductor instead uses the <moving-conductor Ohm law>.

= Electrical resistivity
{parent=Electrical conductivity}
{title2=$\rho_{\rm el}=1/\sigma$}

Electrical resistivity is the inverse of scalar <electrical conductivity> in an isotropic Ohmic medium. In its rest frame, $\mathbf E=\rho_{\rm el}\mathbf j$, so the <electric field> does work per unit volume $\mathbf j\cdot\mathbf E=\rho_{\rm el}|\mathbf j|^2$. In <Gaussian units>, finite conductivity gives <magnetic diffusivity> $\eta=c^2/(4\pi\sigma)$; neglecting this diffusion is a distinct approximation from neglecting electron inertia in <Hall magnetohydrodynamics>.

= Uniaxial conductivity tensor
{parent=Electrical conductivity}
{title2=$\sigma=\alpha I+\gamma nn^{\mathsf T},\quad|n|=1$}

A uniaxial <electrical conductivity> has one distinguished direction $n$ and equal response in its orthogonal plane. Its eigenvalues are $\alpha$ in $n^\perp$ and $\alpha+\gamma$ along $n$, so it is invertible precisely when $\alpha(\alpha+\gamma)\ne0$. For an <electric field> perpendicular to $n$, the <electric current density> is $J=\alpha E$. Positive dissipation requires both eigenvalues to be nonnegative, a physical constraint separate from algebraic invertibility. For $D_{ij}=\epsilon_{ijk}n_k$, one has $D^2=nn^{\mathsf T}-I$, and $\sigma D^2=-\sigma$ is equivalent to $\gamma=-\alpha$; the conductivity then vanishes along $n$.

= Electrical conductor
{parent=Electrical conductivity}
{wiki}

An electrical conductor supports mobile charge carriers and hence an electric current under a field. In a band description, a partially filled band or available overlapping bands supply nearby accessible states. The contrast with a <band insulator> depends on band filling as well as spectral gaps; <semiconductors> provide carriers through thermal excitation or doping.

= Ohmic conductor
{c}
{parent=Electrical conductivity}
{title2=$\mathbf J=\sigma\mathbf E$}

A material whose <current density> is proportional to its <electric field>, with proportionality given by its <electrical conductivity>.

= Uniform current in a straight homogeneous wire
{parent=Ohmic conductor}
{title2=$\mathbf j=I\mathbf e_z/(\pi a^2)$}

In a homogeneous <Ohmic conductor>, a steady <electric current density> obeys $\nabla\cdot\mathbf j=0$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf j=0$. Uniform axial current in a straight cylinder follows if its side is insulated and its flat end faces are equipotentials. The <electric potential> then solves <Laplace's equation> with those boundary conditions, and uniqueness makes it linear in the axial coordinate. Steadiness alone does not impose these contact conditions: nonuniform end potentials can produce nonuniform current even with an insulating side. Bending also generally redistributes current; in an annular conductor the azimuthal current density is proportional to the inverse radius.

= Charge relaxation
{parent=Ohmic conductor}
{title2=$\tau=\epsilon/\sigma$}

In a homogeneous <Ohmic conductor>, the <charge continuity equation> and <Gauss's law> imply exponential decay of the bulk <charge density>, $\rho(t)=\rho(0)e^{-\sigma t/\epsilon}$. For an isolated finite body, bulk charge moves to its boundary; the accumulating <surface charge density> preserves total <electric charge>. The formula uses a constant permittivity $\epsilon$ and the corresponding free-charge convention, or $\epsilon_0$ for total charge in the vacuum form of <Gauss's law>.

= Charge relaxation in an isolated conducting cylinder
{parent=Charge relaxation}
{title2=$\rho(t)=\rho_0e^{-\sigma t/\epsilon_0}$}

A uniform cylindrical volume charge relaxes exponentially under <Ohm's law> and the <continuity equation for electric charge>. In a cylinder of radius $R$ initially carrying no surface charge, the radial conduction current deposits $\rho_s(t)=R\rho_0(1-e^{-\sigma t/\epsilon_0})/2$ on its surface. Total charge per unit length remains $\pi R^2\rho_0$, so the exterior radial <electric field> is time independent even though the interior field decays. Neglecting the deposited surface charge violates charge conservation.

= Drude model
{c}
{parent=Electrical conductivity}

The <Drude model> describes classical charge carriers accelerated by an applied electric field and randomized by collisions of constant mean waiting time $\tau$. Its mean drift obeys $\dot v+v/\tau=eE/m$, and harmonic conductivity is $ne^2\tau/[m(1-i\omega\tau)]$ in the negative-frequency exponential convention.

= High-frequency Drude plasma cutoff
{parent=Drude model}
{title2=$\omega_p^2=ne^2/(m\epsilon_0)$}

The <Drude model> relative permittivity is $1-\omega_p^2/[\omega(\omega+i/\tau)]$. At frequencies large compared with the collision rate but below $\omega_p$, its leading real part is negative and the physical spatial wave-number branch has positive imaginary part. The field therefore decays into the conductor; close to the threshold the full complex response must be retained.

= Electric field
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{wiki}

The electric field is the <force> per unit charge on a stationary test particle.

= Electric flux
{parent=Electric field}
{title2=$\Phi_E=\int_S E\cdot n\,dS$}
{wiki}

The electric flux through an oriented surface is the surface integral of the normal component of its <electric field>. For a closed surface with outward normal, <Gauss's law> makes the <electric flux> equal to enclosed charge divided by $\varepsilon_0$ in <SI> units. It depends on surface orientation and may be negative.

= Electric potential
{parent=Electric field}
{wiki}

= Electrostatic potential
{synonym}

In electrostatics the electric field is $E=-\nabla\Phi$, where $\Phi$ is the electric potential.

= Constrained electrostatic equilibrium
{parent=Electric potential}

A particle constrained to a surface can have a stable minimum of <electrostatic potential> restricted to that surface even when the unrestricted equilibrium is unstable. Away from source charges, <Laplace's equation> makes the full potential <Hessian matrix> trace zero, which rules out a positive-definite three-dimensional Hessian but does not rule out a positive-definite restriction to two dimensions. Equal positive charges at the corners of a square give a planar minimum at its centre and an unstable perpendicular direction.

= Electrostatic reciprocity identity
{parent=Electric potential}
{title2=$\int_V(\phi_1\rho_2-\phi_2\rho_1)\,dV=\varepsilon_0\int_{\partial V}(\phi_2\nabla\phi_1-\phi_1\nabla\phi_2)\cdot d\mathbf S$}

For two <electrostatic potentials> satisfying the <Poisson equation> $\Delta\phi_i=-\rho_i/\varepsilon_0$, <Green's second identity> yields the displayed reciprocity formula. The <product rule for divergence> applied to $\phi_1\nabla\phi_2-\phi_2\nabla\phi_1$ cancels the cross-gradient terms. The normal points outward from the integration region; for an exterior region this means inward toward the excluded object on its inner boundary. Boundary terms may be removed only under suitable decay or boundary conditions.

= Image charge
{parent=Electric potential}

An image charge is a fictitious point charge placed outside the physical solution region so that its potential, combined with the real sources, enforces the required conductor boundary values. The <uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem> then identifies the constructed potential with the physical one. The image need not describe the actual induced surface-charge distribution.

= Fall time of a charge toward a grounded plane
{parent=Image charge}
{title2=$t_{\rm hit}=\pi\sqrt{2\pi\varepsilon_0 m d^3}/|q|$}

The image of a charge $q$ at height $z$ is $-q$ at height $-z$. Their separation is $2z$, giving force $-q^2/(16\pi\varepsilon_0z^2)$. Starting at rest at height $d$, energy conservation gives $\dot z^2=q^2(1/z-1/d)/(8\pi\varepsilon_0m)$. Integrating the reciprocal speed to the plane yields the stated finite time in the electrostatic Newtonian model. The induced-conductor potential energy is $-q^2/(16\pi\varepsilon_0z)$; using the full image-pair energy would double the force incorrectly.

= Falling charge above a grounded conducting plane
{parent=Image charge}
{title2=$T=\pi\sqrt{2\pi\varepsilon_0md^3}/|q|$}

A real charge of height $z$ has a reflected <image charge> at $-z$. Its acceleration is $\ddot z=-\kappa/z^2$, with $\kappa=q^2/(16\pi\varepsilon_0m)$. Released from rest at height $d$, its ideal-model arrival time is
$$
T=\frac{\pi d^{3/2}}{2\sqrt{2\kappa}}.
$$
The image follows the instantaneous reflected position. The induced-charge energy carries one-half the full real-image pair interaction factor, consistent with this force.

= Image charge for a grounded conducting sphere
{parent=Image charge}
{title2=$q'=-aq/R,\quad\mathbf p'=a^2\mathbf p/R^2$}

For a charge $q$ at $\mathbf p$, $R=|\mathbf p|>a$, outside a grounded sphere of radius $a$, the displayed image gives zero potential on the sphere and decay at infinity. On $|\mathbf x|=a$, one has $|\mathbf x-\mathbf p'|=(a/R)|\mathbf x-\mathbf p|$, proving boundary cancellation. The induced force is inward, of magnitude $q^2aR/[4\pi\varepsilon_0(R^2-a^2)^2]$, from <Coulomb's law> applied to the image field. The self-field is excluded.

= Electric potential difference
{parent=Electric potential}

An electric potential difference compares the <electric potentials> at two points. In an <electrical circuit> it is the voltage across the terminals of a component. A vertex potential in an <electrical network> is specified relative to a reference vertex; terminal differences are independent of an additive change of reference.

= Point charge
{parent=Electric potential}

A point charge idealizes electric charge concentrated at one point. In vacuum, a charge $Q$ at the origin has potential $Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r)$.

= Equipotential
{parent=Electric potential}

An equipotential is a level set of the electric potential. The electric field is normal to every smooth equipotential.

= Magnetic field
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{wiki}

= Magnetic flux density
{synonym}

The magnetic field contributes the velocity-dependent term $qv\times B$ to the <Lorentz force>.

= Solenoid
{parent=Magnetic field}
{title2=$B=\mu_0NI$}

A solenoid is a helical winding of conducting wire. For an ideal very long winding with $N$ turns per unit length and negligible pitch, <Ampère's law> gives a uniform axial <magnetic field> $B=\mu_0NI$ inside and zero field outside. Finite-length end fields and the pitch correction are excluded by this approximation. The <self-inductance> of a length $l$ and cross-sectional area $A$ is $L=\mu_0N^2Al$.

= Solenoidal magnetic-field constraint
{parent=Magnetic field}
{title2=$\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$}

The <magnetic field> has zero divergence. The <ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation> preserves this constraint from initial data, since the divergence of its curl is zero.

= Magnetic field line
{parent=Magnetic field}

A magnetic field line is an integral curve tangent to the <magnetic field>. In coordinates $x^i$, it obeys $dx^i/B^i=dx^j/B^j$ wherever the relevant components are nonzero.

= Flux tube
{parent=Magnetic field line}
{wiki}

A flux tube is a bundle of <magnetic field lines> bounded by a surface everywhere tangent to the <magnetic field>. <Gauss's law for magnetism> makes the <magnetic flux> through any transverse cross-section constant along the tube. For a narrow tube with nearly uniform field magnitude $B$ and transverse area $A$, this gives $BA=\mathrm{constant}$.

= Flux-tube area
{parent=Flux tube}
{title2=$A$}

The cross-sectional area of a narrow <flux tube>, measured normal to its <magnetic field>. Constancy of the <magnetic flux> gives $A|\mathbf B|=\mathrm{constant}$ along the tube. This area controls the mass transport when flow follows the field.

= Magnetic-field-line equation
{parent=Magnetic field line}

The magnetic-field-line equation states that the tangent to a field line is parallel to $\mathbf B$. In spherical coordinates for an axisymmetric poloidal field, it can be written $dr/(r\,d\theta)=B_r/B_\theta$.

= Magnetic dipole field
{parent=Magnetic field line}

Outside a localized magnetic dipole $\boldsymbol\mu$, the vacuum field in Gaussian conventions is
$$
\mathbf B(\mathbf r)=\frac{3(\boldsymbol\mu\mathbin\cdot\mathbf r)\mathbf r}{r^5}-\frac{\boldsymbol\mu}{r^3}.
$$
For a dipole along the polar axis, $B_r=2\mu\cos\theta/r^3$ and $B_\theta=\mu\sin\theta/r^3$.

= Dipole magnetic-field line
{parent=Magnetic dipole field}

An axisymmetric dipole field line satisfies $r=C\sin^2\theta$, where $C$ is its equatorial crossing radius. This follows by integrating $dr/(r\,d\theta)=B_r/B_\theta=2\cot\theta$.

= Dipolar flux-tube area
{parent=Dipole magnetic-field line}

Near the polar axis of a <magnetic dipole field>, $B\propto r^{-3}$. Conservation of <magnetic flux> in a <flux tube> consequently gives $A(r)\propto r^3$. A <dipole magnetic-field line> intersecting a sphere of radius $R_\star$ at small polar angle $\theta_\star$ obeys
$$
\theta(r)^2\simeq\theta_\star^2\frac r{R_\star},
\qquad
A(r)\simeq\pi r^2\theta(r)^2
=\pi\theta_\star^2\frac{r^3}{R_\star}.
$$
This area is for one polar tube. The approximation requires $\theta_\star^2r/R_\star\ll1$; the full <magnetic dipole field> does not remain a narrow polar tube at arbitrarily large radius.

= Magnetostatic equilibrium
{parent=Magnetic field}
{wiki}

A magnetostatic equilibrium is a time-independent configuration in which magnetic, pressure, gravitational, and any other body-force densities balance.

= Spherical magnetostatic pressure balance
{parent=Magnetostatic equilibrium}
{title2=$\Delta_*\chi+\psi\psi'=-\mu_0r^2\sin^2\theta\,p'$}

For an axisymmetric <magnetic field> $\mathbf B=\psi\mathbf e_\phi/(r\sin\theta)+\nabla\times[\chi\mathbf e_\phi/(r\sin\theta)]$, the <poloidal magnetic flux function> gives $B_r=\chi_\theta/(r^2\sin\theta)$ and $B_\theta=-\chi_r/(r\sin\theta)$. Force balance implies locally $p=p(\chi)$ and $\psi=\psi(\chi)$ on regular connected flux surfaces. Direct calculation gives $(\nabla\times\mathbf B)\times\mathbf B=-(\Delta_*\chi+\psi\psi')\nabla\chi/(r^2\sin^2\theta)$, with $\Delta_*\chi=\chi_{rr}+\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta(\chi_\theta/\sin\theta)/r^2$. Equating this to $\mu_0\nabla p$ proves the displayed <magnetostatic equilibrium> equation, including its sign and dimensions.

= Confined linear-pressure spherical magnetic equilibrium
{parent=Spherical magnetostatic pressure balance}
{title2=$ (x^2-3)\sin x+3x\cos x=0$}

Set $\psi=\alpha\chi$, $p=p_0+P\chi$ and $\chi=F(r)\sin^2\theta$. A regular family is $F=A[\cos(\alpha r)-\sin(\alpha r)/(\alpha r)]+cr^2$, where $P=-\alpha^2c/\mu_0$. A zero <magnetic field> at radius a requires $F(a)=F'(a)=0$. For nonzero A this yields the displayed condition with $x=\alpha a$ and $c=-A[\cos x-\sin x/x]/a^2$. The boundary pressure is $p_0$, independent of angle. The trigonometric pair is tied by regularity and the radial differential equation; its coefficients are not independent.

= Magnetized isothermal atmosphere
{parent=Magnetostatic equilibrium}

A plane-parallel <magnetostatic equilibrium> with constant downward <gravitational acceleration> $g$, horizontal <magnetic field>, constant <isothermal sound speed> $c_s$, and constant <Alfvén speed> $v_a$ has
$$
H=\frac{c_s^2+v_a^2/2}{g},\qquad \rho=\rho_0e^{-z/H},\qquad p=c_s^2\rho,\qquad B=B_0e^{-z/(2H)}.
$$
Indeed the <magnetohydrodynamic total pressure> is $(c_s^2+v_a^2/2)\rho$, and its vertical derivative is $-g\rho$. The <magnetic pressure> therefore increases the <scale height> above that of an unmagnetized atmosphere.

= Cylindrical magnetostatic equilibrium
{parent=Magnetostatic equilibrium}

A cylindrical magnetostatic equilibrium is an <axisymmetric vector field> and <pressure> configuration independent of the axial coordinate. Its radial <Lorentz force density> balances the <pressure gradient>, as expressed by <cylindrical magnetostatic pressure balance>.

= Z-pinch
{c}
{parent=Cylindrical magnetostatic equilibrium}
{wiki}

In a Z-pinch, an axial <electric current> produces a <toroidal magnetic field> whose inward <magnetic tension> confines a plasma. A cylindrical <magnetostatic equilibrium> balances the resulting <Lorentz force density> against a <pressure gradient>.

= Cylindrical magnetostatic pressure balance
{parent=Cylindrical magnetostatic equilibrium}

For an <axisymmetric vector field> independent of the axial coordinate and regular on the axis, <Gauss's law for magnetism> gives $B_R=0$. The magnetostatic <Ampère-Maxwell equation> then gives $j_R=0$, $j_\phi=-cB_z'/(4\pi)$, and $j_z=c(RB_\phi)'/(4\pi R)$ in Gaussian units. Radial <magnetostatic equilibrium> is
$$
\frac d{dR}\left(p+\frac{B_z^2+B_\phi^2}{8\pi}\right)+\frac{B_\phi^2}{4\pi R}=0.
$$
For enclosed axial <electric current> $I(R)$, the magnetostatic <Ampère-Maxwell equation> gives $B_\phi=2I/(cR)$, hence
$$
\frac d{dR}\left(p+\frac{B_z^2}{8\pi}\right)
+\frac1{2\pi c^2R^2}\frac{dI^2}{dR}=0.
$$
The first term contains <pressure> and axial <magnetic pressure>; the second combines toroidal <magnetic pressure> with the hoop force from <magnetic tension>.

= Flux-tube axial field virial identity
{parent=Cylindrical magnetostatic pressure balance}
{title2=$\langle B_z^2\rangle=B_p^2-2\mu_0\langle p\rangle$}

For a regular cylindrical <flux tube> with $\mathbf B=(0,B_\phi(s),B_z(s))$, vanishing field beyond radius $s_0$, and exterior <pressure> $p_e$, define $\langle h\rangle=2s_0^{-2}\int_0^{s_0}sh(s)\,ds$ and $B_p^2=2\mu_0p_e$. Integrating <cylindrical magnetostatic pressure balance> against $s^2$ gives the displayed identity. The toroidal <magnetic pressure> and hoop <magnetic tension> cancel in this cross-sectional axial-field average. Nonnegative gas <pressure> gives $\langle B_z^2\rangle\leq B_p^2$, with strict inequality precisely when $\langle p\rangle>0$.

= Uniform-twist central axial field bound
{parent=Flux-tube axial field virial identity}
{title2=$B_z(0)^2\leq(1+\gamma^2)B_p^2$}

For a <flux tube> with $B_\phi=\gamma(s/s_0)B_z$, regularity at the axis and <cylindrical magnetostatic pressure balance> give $B_z(0)^2=B_p^2-2\mu_0p(0)+\gamma^2\langle B_z^2\rangle$. The <flux-tube axial field virial identity> and $p\geq0$ give the bound. A twisted <toroidal magnetic field> can support a central axial field above the untwisted <magnetic pressure> limit even though its cross-sectional mean obeys that limit.

= Power-law pressure-supported axial current
{parent=Cylindrical magnetostatic pressure balance}

For constant axial <magnetic field> and $p=p_0(1+R^2/a^2)^{-k}$ with $p_0>0$ and $k>0$, <cylindrical magnetostatic pressure balance> gives, with $x=R^2/a^2$,
$$
I^2(R)=2\pi c^2p_0a^2
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\frac{1-(1+kx)(1+x)^{-k}}{k-1},&k\ne1,\\
\log(1+x)+(1+x)^{-1}-1,&k=1.
\end{cases}
$$
The nontrivial <electric current> is finite at infinity exactly when $k>1$, in which case $I^2(\infty)=2\pi c^2p_0a^2/(k-1)$. Either sign of $I$ is possible because the <pressure> balance fixes its square.

= Magnetic pressure support with vanishing axial field
{parent=Cylindrical magnetostatic pressure balance}

With no axial <electric current>, <cylindrical magnetostatic pressure balance> reduces to constant $p+B_z^2/(8\pi)$. For $p=p_0e^{-R^2/a^2}$ and $B_z(0)=0$, its radial solutions are
$$
B_z=\sigma\sqrt{8\pi p_0}\sqrt{1-e^{-R^2/a^2}},
\qquad
j_\phi=-\sigma\frac{c\sqrt{8\pi p_0}}{4\pi a^2}
\frac{R e^{-R^2/a^2}}{\sqrt{1-e^{-R^2/a^2}}},
\qquad \sigma\in\{-1,1\}.
$$
As $R\downarrow0$, $B_z\sim\sigma\sqrt{8\pi p_0}R/a$ and $j_\phi\to-\sigma c\sqrt{8\pi p_0}/(4\pi a)$. These finite scalar limits do not give a <smooth function> of Cartesian position: $R=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ has a cusp at the axis, and the azimuthal unit vector has no unique limit there. Thus these data describe radial equilibrium away from the axis, but cannot satisfy smooth vector-field regularity on the axis without modifying the data.

= Motional electric field
{parent=Magnetic field}

A conductor or plasma moving with nonrelativistic velocity $\mathbf u$ through a magnetic field experiences the motional electric field $\mathbf E=-\mathbf u\times\mathbf B/c$ in Gaussian units when the electric field vanishes in its rest frame.

= London equations
{c}
{parent=Magnetic field}
{wiki}

= London equation
{c}
{synonym}

The London equations relate the superconducting <electric current> to the <electromagnetic field>: $\partial_t\mathbf J=\mathbf E/(\mu_0\lambda_L^2)$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf J=-\mathbf B/(\mu_0\lambda_L^2)$. In a gauge with constant condensate phase the second follows from $\mathbf J=-\mathbf A/(\mu_0\lambda_L^2)$; it implies exponential <magnetic field> screening.

= London penetration depth
{c}
{parent=London equations}
{title2=$\lambda_L=\sqrt{m/(\mu_0n_sq^2)}$}
{wiki}

For uniform vortex-free <superconductivity>, the <London equation> and stationary Maxwell equations give $\nabla^2\mathbf B=\mathbf B/\lambda_L^2$. A field tangential to a planar superconducting half-space decays as $\mathbf B(z)=\mathbf B(0)e^{-z/\lambda_L}$. A nonzero normal boundary field is incompatible with this translation-invariant decaying Meissner solution, because divergence-free magnetic flux would require that component to remain constant.

= Meissner effect
{c}
{parent=London equations}
{wiki}

The Meissner effect is the expulsion of magnetic field from the bulk of a superconductor. A static field decays from its surface over the London penetration depth.

= Magnetic flux
{parent=Magnetic field}
{wiki}

The magnetic flux through an oriented surface $S$ is $\Phi_B=\int_S B\mathbin{\cdot}dS$.

= Capacitance
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{wiki}

Capacitance is charge stored per potential difference, $C=Q/V$.

= Capacitance of an isolated conducting sphere
{parent=Capacitance}
{title2=$C=4\pi\epsilon R$}

An isolated conducting sphere of radius $R$ in a medium of <permittivity> $\epsilon$ has surface <electrostatic potential> $V=Q/(4\pi\epsilon R)$ relative to infinity. Thus its <capacitance> is $C=Q/V=4\pi\epsilon R$.

= Sphere-plane capacitance
{parent=Capacitance}
{title2=$C(R,h)$}

For a conducting sphere of radius $R$ at center height $h>R$ above a grounded infinite plane,
$$
C(R,h)=4\pi\epsilon R\sinh\alpha\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{\sinh(n\alpha)},
\qquad\cosh\alpha=\frac hR.
$$
The distance $h$ is measured to the center, so the surface gap is $h-R$. Relative to the <capacitance of an isolated conducting sphere>, the enhancement is $c(R/h)=C(R,h)/(4\pi\epsilon R)$.

The <method of images> gives a constructive proof. At fixed sphere potential $V_s$, start with $q_0=4\pi\epsilon RV_s$ at height $z_0=h$ and place its opposite image below the plane. Cancel that image's nonconstant sphere potential by the successive charges
$$
q_{n+1}=\frac R{h+z_n}q_n,
\qquad z_{n+1}=h-\frac{R^2}{h+z_n},
$$
with opposite mirror charges below the plane at every step. The resulting sphere potential is $V_s$ and the plane potential is zero. Since $q_n=4\pi\epsilon RV_s\sinh\alpha/\sinh((n+1)\alpha)$, summing the sphere charges gives the displayed <capacitance>. In particular $c(y)=1+y/2+O(y^2)$ for small $y$.

The exact sphere-plane boundary-value problem is also treated in \a[https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.6034][Behunin and colleagues' analysis of sphere-plane electrostatics].

= Capacitance per unit length
{parent=Capacitance}

Capacitance per unit length is the charge per unit length divided by the potential difference. A vacuum coaxial cable with radii $a<b$ has
$$
C'=\frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0}{\log(b/a)}.
$$

= Inductance
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{wiki}

Inductance measures magnetic flux linkage per current and gives the self-induced electromotive force $-L\,dI/dt$.

= Self-inductance
{parent=Inductance}
{title2=$\Lambda=LI,\quad\mathcal E_{\rm ind}=-L\dot I$}

For a circuit whose flux linkage $\Lambda$ is proportional to its current, the self-inductance is the coefficient $L$ in $\Lambda=LI$. <Faraday's law> gives the induced <electromotive force> $-L\dot I$ when $L$ is constant. In series with a resistance $R$ and a constant applied electromotive force, the current obeys $L\dot I+RI=\mathcal E_0$, with <time constant> $L/R$.

= Bound charge and bound current
{parent=Electromagnetism}

Polarization and magnetization produce the effective sources
$$
\rho_{\rm b}=-\nabla\cdot P,
\qquad
J_{\rm b}=\partial_tP+\nabla\times M.
$$
Defining $D=\epsilon_0E+P$ and $H=B/\mu_0-M$ moves these sources into the macroscopic fields.

= Bound current
{parent=Bound charge and bound current}
{title2=$\mathbf J_b=\partial_t\mathbf P+\nabla\times\mathbf M$}

The current associated with <electric polarization> and <magnetization> in matter. If the magnetic response is neglected, it is $\mathbf J_b=\partial_t\mathbf P$. It is distinguished from a free conduction current in the <Maxwell equations in matter>.

= Polarization density
{title2=$\mathbf P$}
{parent=Bound charge and bound current}
{wiki}

= Electric polarization
{synonym}

Electric polarization is electric dipole moment per unit volume. It produces bound volume and surface charge
$$
\rho_{\rm b}=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf P,
\qquad
\sigma_{\rm b}=\mathbf P\cdot\mathbf n.
$$
Across an interface, the net bound sheet charge is $(\mathbf P_1-\mathbf P_2)\cdot\mathbf n$, where $\mathbf n$ points from medium one to medium two.

= Electric displacement field
{title2=$\mathbf D$}
{parent=Polarization density}
{wiki}

The electric displacement is $\mathbf D=\epsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P$ and obeys $\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_{\rm free}$. In a linear isotropic dielectric, $\mathbf D=\epsilon\mathbf E$.

= Magnetization
{title2=$\mathbf M$}
{parent=Bound charge and bound current}
{wiki}

Magnetization is magnetic dipole moment per unit volume. It produces bound volume and surface currents
$$
\mathbf J_{\rm b}=\nabla\times\mathbf M,
\qquad
\mathbf K_{\rm b}=\mathbf M\times\mathbf n.
$$
Across an interface, the net bound sheet current is $(\mathbf M_1-\mathbf M_2)\times\mathbf n$.

= Curie temperature
{c}
{parent=Magnetization}
{title2=$T_C$}
{wiki}

The Curie temperature is the transition temperature above which a ferromagnetic material loses spontaneous <magnetization>. A hot conducting planetary core can generate a <magnetic field> through <dynamo action> without behaving as a permanent magnet. The blocking temperature at which a mineral records crustal remanence also depends on its relaxation timescale and need not equal its Curie temperature.

= Remanent magnetization
{parent=Magnetization}

= Remanence
{synonym}

Remanent magnetization is <magnetization> retained after the applied <magnetic field> has been removed. Magnetic minerals cooling in an ambient field can preserve information about that field in a planetary crust. Such a record may outlive the <planetary dynamo> which originally produced it. Crustal remanence therefore differs from a present field maintained by conducting-fluid motion.

= Magnetization current
{title2=$J_{\mathrm{mag}}^\mu$}
{parent=Magnetization}

A relativistic magnetization current has the form $J_{\mathrm{mag}}^\mu=\partial_\nu M^{\nu\mu}$ for an antisymmetric polarization-magnetization tensor. It is identically conserved because commuting derivatives contract an antisymmetric tensor.

= Magnetic field intensity
{title2=$\mathbf H$}
{parent=Magnetization}

The magnetic field intensity is $\mathbf H=\mathbf B/\mu_0-\mathbf M$ and obeys $\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_{\rm free}$ in magnetostatics. In a linear isotropic medium, $\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H$.

= Magnetic spherical-shell matching
{parent=Electromagnetism}

Across an interface with no free surface current, the normal component of $B$ and tangential component of $H$ are continuous. For an axisymmetric spherical shell, harmonic scalar potentials have dipole form $(ar+b/r^2)\cos\theta$ in each radial region.

= Maxwell stress tensor
{c}
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{wiki}

With electromagnetic momentum density $\mathbf g=\epsilon_0\mathbf E\times\mathbf B$, the momentum-flux convention
$$
\sigma_{ij}=-\epsilon_0E_iE_j+\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}E^2\delta_{ij}
-\frac1{\mu_0}B_iB_j+\frac1{2\mu_0}B^2\delta_{ij}
$$
satisfies
$$
\partial_tg_j+\partial_i\sigma_{ij}=-(\rho\mathbf E+\mathbf J\times\mathbf B)_j.
$$

= Electromagnetic momentum density
{title2=$\mathbf g=\epsilon_0\mathbf E\times\mathbf B=\mathbf S/c^2$}
{parent=Maxwell stress tensor}

The electromagnetic momentum density in vacuum is $\mathbf g=\epsilon_0\mathbf E\times\mathbf B$. It equals the <Poynting vector> divided by $c^2$, expressing the relativistic relation between energy flux and momentum density.

= Local conservation of electromagnetic momentum
{parent=Maxwell stress tensor}

With the momentum-flux sign convention used for the <Maxwell stress tensor>, Maxwell's equations imply
$$
\nabla\mathbin{\cdot}\boldsymbol\sigma
+\partial_t\mathbf g=-\mathbf f_{\rm L},
$$
where $\mathbf g$ is the <electromagnetic momentum density> and $\mathbf f_{\rm L}$ is the <Lorentz force density> exerted by the field on matter. Thus total field-plus-matter momentum is locally conserved.

= Radiation pressure on a perfectly absorbing surface
{parent=Maxwell stress tensor}

A normally incident vacuum wave of time-averaged intensity $I$ transfers momentum $I/c$ per unit area and time to a perfectly absorbing surface, so its radiation pressure is
$$
p=\frac Ic.
$$
A perfect reflector reverses the normal momentum and gives $2I/c$.

= Proca action
{c}
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{wiki}

The Proca action adds $m^2A_\mu A^\mu/2$ to the Maxwell Lagrangian. It describes a massive spin-one field and explicitly breaks the Abelian gauge symmetry of the massless theory.

= Proca equation
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{c}

A massive vector field coupled to a conserved current obeys
$$
\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}-m^2A^\nu=-\mu_0J^\nu.
$$
The mass term breaks electromagnetic gauge invariance.

= Proca field
{c}
{parent=Proca equation}

A Proca field is a massive spin-one vector field. Its field equation enforces transversality and leaves three physical polarizations.

= Proca propagator
{c}
{parent=Proca field}

For metric signature $(+---)$, the momentum-space Feynman propagator of a Proca field is
$$
\widetilde\Delta_{\mu\nu}(p)=\frac{-i}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon}\left(\eta_{\mu\nu}-\frac{p_\mu p_\nu}{m^2}\right).
$$
The longitudinal numerator term inverts the massive vector kinetic operator without gauge fixing.

= High-energy obstruction for a hard vector mass
{parent=Proca propagator}
{title2=$D_{\mu\nu}(k)=-i(g_{\mu\nu}-k_\mu k_\nu/m^2)/(k^2-m^2+i0)$}

A free <Proca field> is consistent and has three physical <particle polarizations>. The difficulty for generic interactions is the ultraviolet behavior of its <Proca propagator>: the longitudinal numerator makes part of it approach order $1/m^2$, rather than decay as $1/k^2$. Longitudinal external <polarization vectors> also behave as $\varepsilon_L^\mu=k^\mu/m+O(m/E)$. Without suitable identities and cancellations, amplitudes grow with energy and perturbative counterterms of increasing dimension are needed. Conserved-current Abelian models and the <Stueckelberg mechanism> are important exceptions; a massive interacting vector is not intrinsically inconsistent as an <effective field theory>.

= Proca time-ordering contact term
{c}
{parent=Proca propagator}
{title2=$\Delta^{\rm cov}_{ab}-\Delta^{T}_{ab}=i\delta_a^0\delta_b^0\delta^{(4)}/m^2$}

For a free massive <Proca field> in the mostly-plus <Minkowski metric>, the covariant inverse of the quadratic <action> and the canonical <time-ordered product> of vector potentials differ locally. With <Fourier transform> $e^{iq\cdot(x-y)}$, the covariant <Proca propagator> is $-i(\eta_{ab}+q_aq_b/m^2)/(q^2+m^2-i0)$, while the literal canonical <two-point correlation function> subtracts $i\delta_a^0\delta_b^0/m^2$ in momentum space. The time component is nondynamical; its physical-mode numerator is $|\boldsymbol q|^2/m^2$, rather than $-1+(q^0)^2/m^2$. The difference is an instantaneous <Dirac delta function>, not another propagating state. Both expressions agree away from coincidence. This distinction matters when identifying a covariant <Green function> with a canonical correlator in a constrained field theory.

= Transverse vector field
{parent=Proca field}

A vector field is transverse when $\partial_\mu V^\mu=0$ in spacetime, or $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf V=0$ for a spatial Fourier mode.

= Lorenz constraint in Proca theory
{parent=Proca equation}
{c}

Taking a divergence and using antisymmetry of $F^{\mu\nu}$ and current conservation gives $m^2\partial_\mu A^\mu=0$. For nonzero mass, the Lorenz condition follows from the field equation rather than from a gauge choice.

= Yukawa potential
{parent=Proca equation}
{c}
{wiki}

The static Green function of $\nabla^2-m^2$ in three dimensions is proportional to $e^{-mr}/r$. It describes an interaction screened beyond range $m^{-1}$ and tends to the Coulomb potential as $m\to0$.

= Relativistic Lorentz force
{parent=Electromagnetism}

$m\,du^\mu/d\tau=qF^\mu{}_\nu u^\nu$ gives $d\mathbf p/dt=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)$ and $d(\gamma mc^2)/dt=q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v$.

= Constant-field relativistic Lorentz-force solution
{parent=Relativistic Lorentz force}

For a constant <electromagnetic field tensor>, the <relativistic Lorentz force> is a constant-coefficient linear system on <four-velocity>, with solution
$$
u(\tau)=\exp\!\left(\frac qmF\tau\right)u(0).
$$
The electric-type blocks of $F$ generate hyperbolic rotations, while magnetic-type blocks generate ordinary rotations.

= Relativistic motion in parallel electric and magnetic fields
{parent=Constant-field relativistic Lorentz-force solution}

When uniform $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$ are parallel, the <relativistic Lorentz force> splits into independent hyperbolic motion along the fields and circular motion in the perpendicular plane. The circle is periodic in <proper time>, while electric acceleration makes the helix's longitudinal pitch grow exponentially from one proper-time cyclotron period to the next.

= Relativistic hyperbolic motion in a uniform electric field
{parent=Relativistic Lorentz force}

A particle released from rest in a constant electric field parallel to its motion follows a spacetime hyperbola and has rapidity proportional to proper time.

This force law realizes <hyperbolic motion> with constant <proper acceleration>.

= Null crossed electromagnetic field
{parent=Relativistic Lorentz force}

Perpendicular fields with $\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B=0$ and $E^2-c^2B^2=0$ form a null electromagnetic field. The constant-field Lorentz-force generator is nilpotent, so its exponential terminates as a polynomial.

Both invariants of the <electromagnetic field tensor> vanish for this field class.

= Relativistic trajectory in a constant null crossed field
{parent=Null crossed electromagnetic field}

Choose
$$
\mathbf B=B\,\mathbf e_z,\qquad
\mathbf E=cB\,\mathbf e_x,
\qquad
\Omega=\frac{qB}{m}.
$$
A particle released from rest at the origin has the proper-time trajectory
$$
t(\tau)=\tau+\frac{\Omega^2\tau^3}{6},
\qquad
x(\tau)=\frac{c\Omega\tau^2}{2},
\qquad
y(\tau)=-\frac{c\Omega^2\tau^3}{6},
\qquad z(\tau)=0.
$$
Eliminating <proper time> gives the semicubical parabola
$$
\boxed{y^2=\frac{2qB}{9mc}x^3.}
$$
This signed coefficient assumes that the positive $x$ direction is chosen along $\mathbf E$; reversing that axis changes the signs of both $x^3$ and its coefficient.

= Charged particle in a uniform magnetic field

{wiki}

A velocity cross magnetic-field force bends perpendicular motion into a circle while doing no work.

= Magnetic angular-momentum invariant
{parent=Charged particle in a uniform magnetic field}
{title2=$\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf B+\frac q2|\mathbf r\times\mathbf B|^2$}

For a particle obeying $m\dot{\mathbf v}=q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$ in a constant uniform <magnetic field>, the displayed combination is conserved. The derivative of mechanical <angular momentum> projected onto the field is canceled by the quadratic position term. This is the rotational analogue of the distinction between mechanical and <canonical momentum> in an electromagnetic field.

= Helical motion in a uniform magnetic field
{parent=Charged particle in a uniform magnetic field}

In a uniform <magnetic field>, the <Lorentz force> leaves the component of <velocity> parallel to the field constant and rotates the perpendicular component at the nonrelativistic <cyclotron frequency> $|q|B/m$. The resulting path is a <helix> around a field line.

The guiding center is the center of a charged particle's circular cyclotron orbit. In a nonuniform field it drifts slowly compared with the rapid <cyclotron frequency>.

= Electrostatics
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{wiki}

Electrostatics studies time-independent electric fields and charge distributions.

= Electrostatic interface boundary conditions
{parent=Electrostatics}
{title2=$[D_n]=\sigma_{\rm fixed}$}

Across a charged dielectric interface the tangential <electric field> is continuous and the normal displacement jump is the fixed <surface charge density>. Without a dipole sheet the <electrostatic potential> is continuous. For a charge sheet with equal <permittivity> on both sides, its normal potential derivative has jump $-\sigma_{\rm fixed}/\varepsilon$. Setting the field to zero on one side is an additional physical boundary assumption, as in a conductor or a negligible-exterior-admittance idealization.

= Debye–Hückel approximation
{c}
{parent=Electrostatics}
{title2=$(\nabla^2-\kappa_D^2)\phi=-\rho_{\rm fixed}/\varepsilon$}

The Debye–Hückel approximation linearizes mobile-ion Boltzmann densities about a neutral bulk electrolyte. It is valid for ionic potential energies small compared with $k_BT$. The inverse <Debye–Hückel screening length> satisfies $\kappa_D^2=\sum_i n_i z_i^2e^2/(\varepsilon k_BT)$. The quadratic screened charging free energy includes both <electric field> energy and the linearized ionic-response term $\varepsilon\kappa_D^2\phi^2/2$.

= Screened sinusoidal surface-charge mode
{parent=Debye–Hückel approximation}
{title2=$p=\sqrt{k^2+\kappa_D^2}$}

A lateral sinusoidal <surface charge density> with wave number $k$ in the <Debye–Hückel approximation> generates vertical exponential or hyperbolic functions with inverse length $p=\sqrt{k^2+\kappa_D^2}$. In a homogeneous electrolyte filling all space, an isolated sheet at $z_s$ produces $\phi=\sigma_k(x)e^{-p|z-z_s|}/(2\varepsilon p)$. Boundary conditions determine the normalization when the electrolyte occupies only a gap.

= Phase registration of screened charged sheets
{parent=Screened sinusoidal surface-charge mode}
{title2=$\theta_{\min}=\pi\pmod{2\pi}$}

Two equally modulated charge sheets in identical full-space electrolyte have charging energy per area $\alpha^2[1+e^{-pd}\cos\theta]/(4\varepsilon p)$, where $p=\sqrt{k^2+\kappa_D^2}$. Its phase-dependent interaction favors a half-wavelength offset, with opposite charge patches facing each other. For an electrolyte gap with negligible exterior displacement response, the energy is instead $\alpha^2[\coth(pd)+\cos\theta/\sinh(pd)]/(2\varepsilon p)$, with the same minimizing phase. Absolute energies require specified exterior <electrostatic interface boundary conditions>.

= Bjerrum length
{parent=Electrostatics}
{c}
{title2=$\ell_B$}
{wiki}

For a chosen reference charge $q$, the separation at which its Coulomb interaction energy equals $k_BT$. The charge-$q$ convention is $\ell_B=q^2/(4\pi\epsilon_{\rm abs}k_BT)$ in SI, or $\ell_B=\beta q^2/\epsilon$ in <Gaussian units>. The commonly tabulated value uses the elementary charge; changing <counterion> valence requires tracking that reference convention. It sets the strength of <counterion condensation>.

= Poisson-Boltzmann equation
{parent=Electrostatics}
{c}
{wiki=Poisson–Boltzmann_equation}

A mean-field coupling of the <Poisson equation> to an ideal <Boltzmann distribution> of mobile <ions>. In <Gaussian units> with uniform dielectric constant $\epsilon$, $\nabla^2\phi=-(4\pi/\epsilon)[\rho_f+\sum_i q_i n_{i,0}e^{-\beta q_i\phi}]$. The potential reference and chemical potentials determine the normalization factors. It neglects discrete-ion correlations and assumes local <thermal equilibrium>. A counterion-only model is not the same equation as a symmetric salt reservoir.

= Neutral cylindrical cell model
{parent=Poisson-Boltzmann equation}
{title2=$E(R)=0$}

Confine the <counterions> of a uniformly charged infinite cylinder within a coaxial cylinder of radius $R$, with total mobile line charge $\lambda$. The <electric field> vanishes at $R$, while its surface value at $a$ is fixed by the bare line charge. This gives $a\phi'(a)=2\lambda/\epsilon$ and $R\phi'(R)=0$ in <Gaussian units>. Sending $R/a\to\infty$ defines the <infinite-dilution limit of cylindrical counterions>.

= Infinite-dilution limit of cylindrical counterions
{parent=Neutral cylindrical cell model}

In a neutral counterion-only cylindrical cell, send the outer radius to infinity at fixed cylinder charge. Below the <Manning parameter> threshold, number density tends to zero at each fixed radius. Above threshold, the <cylindrical counterion-only Poisson-Boltzmann profile> retains line charge $\lambda-q/\ell_B$ locally, while the rest recedes to infinity. Therefore the limit of the full finite-cell charge integral is not the charge integral of the pointwise limiting density.

= Cylindrical counterion-only Poisson-Boltzmann profile
{parent=Poisson-Boltzmann equation}
{title2=$\Phi=2\log[1+(\eta-1)u]$}

For $u=\log(r/a)$ and $\Phi=\beta q\phi-2u$, a counterion-only <Poisson-Boltzmann equation> reduces to $\Phi''=-Ce^{-\Phi}$. Conditions $\Phi(0)=0$, $\Phi\to\infty$ and $\Phi'\to0$ give $\Phi=2\log(1+bu)$, $b=\sqrt{C/2}>0$. Matching surface <Gauss's law> gives $b=\eta-1$, so this branch requires the <Manning parameter> $\eta>1$. Its number density is $n(r)=(\eta-1)^2(a/r)^2/[2\pi\ell_Ba^2(1+(\eta-1)\log(r/a))^2]$. It leaves residual line-charge magnitude $q/\ell_B$.

= Counterion condensation
{parent=Poisson-Boltzmann equation}
{wiki}

= Manning condensation
{c}
{synonym}

Localization of a finite fraction of oppositely charged <ions> near a charged object even as the confining volume grows without bound. In the salt-free infinite-cylinder mean-field model, the <Manning parameter> must exceed one for a nonzero limiting local density. The condensed charge does not include <ions> that escape to infinity; finite-cell charge neutrality and the local infinite-volume limit must not be interchanged.

= Manning condensed fraction
{parent=Counterion condensation}
{c}
{title2=$f_c=\max(1-1/\eta,0)$}

In the mean-field infinite-dilution cylinder model, the fraction of neutralizing charge that remains localized is $f_c=\max(1-1/\eta,0)$, where $\eta$ is the <Manning parameter>. For $\eta>1$, integrating the <cylindrical counterion-only Poisson-Boltzmann profile> gives $Q_c=\lambda-q/\ell_B$. Below threshold, the local limiting density vanishes even though every finite neutral cell contains the full <counterion> charge.

= Cylindrical screening charge
{parent=Counterion condensation}
{title2=$Q(r)=2\pi\int_a^r s\rho(s)\,ds$}

The positive mobile charge per unit axial length accumulated from cylinder radius $a$ to $r$ is $Q(r)=2\pi\int_a^r s\rho(s)\,ds$. For fixed negative line charge $-\lambda$, <Gauss's law> gives signed radial <electric field> $E_r=2[-\lambda+Q(r)]/(\epsilon r)$ in <Gaussian units>. Its absolute value is the field magnitude. An integral over a local infinite-dilution profile measures condensed charge, rather than every <ion> in a finite neutral cell.

= Manning parameter
{parent=Counterion condensation}
{c}
{title2=$\eta=\lambda\ell_B/q$}

The dimensionless line-charge coupling $\eta=\lambda\ell_B/q$, with negative cylinder line charge $-\lambda$ and <counterions> of charge $q>0$. Here the <Bjerrum length> uses the same reference charge $q$. The mean-field infinite-dilution threshold is $\eta=1$. If $\ell_B$ is instead tabulated for elementary charge $e$ and <counterions> have charge $ze$, the corresponding coupling is $z\lambda\ell_B/e$.

= Coulomb's law
{parent=Electrostatics}
{c}
{wiki}

Point charges $q_1,q_2$ separated by a displacement $r$ exert the force
$$
F=\frac{q_1q_2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{r}{|r|^3}.
$$

= Coulomb potential energy
{parent=Coulomb's law}
{c}
{title2=$V(r)=q_1q_2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r)$}

= Coulomb potential
{synonym}
{c}

Two point charges have the displayed <potential energy> when it is set to zero at infinite separation. Integrating the radial force in <Coulomb's law>, or differentiating $V$ via $F_r=-dV/dr$, gives this expression. Opposite charges give an attractive negative potential, as in the <hydrogen atom>. Dividing by the test charge instead gives the source charge's scalar <electric potential>.

= Conductor in electrostatic equilibrium
{parent=Electrostatics}

The electric field vanishes inside a conductor at electrostatic equilibrium, so each connected conductor is equipotential and the exterior field is normal to its surface.

= Electrostatic boundary conditions at a conductor
{parent=Conductor in electrostatic equilibrium}

= Electrostatic boundary condition
{synonym}

A Gaussian pillbox gives
$$
(E_{\rm out}-E_{\rm in})\cdot n=\frac\sigma{\epsilon_0}.
$$
Since $E_{\rm in}=0$, the surface charge density is $\sigma=\epsilon_0E_{\rm out}\cdot n$.

= Surface charge density
{title2=$\sigma$}
{parent=Electrostatic boundary conditions at a conductor}
{wiki}

Surface charge density is electric charge per unit area. At a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium it equals $\epsilon_0\mathbf E_{\rm out}\mathbin{\cdot}\mathbf n$ for the normal pointing out of the conductor.

= Charge sharing between distant connected spheres
{parent=Conductor in electrostatic equilibrium}

Two widely separated conducting spheres of radii $R_1,R_2$ connected by a thin wire have equal potentials, so a total charge $Q$ divides as
$$
Q_i=\frac{R_i}{R_1+R_2}Q.
$$

= Charge on connected concentric spherical shells
{parent=Conductor in electrostatic equilibrium}

Two connected concentric spherical shells form one equipotential conductor. In the absence of charge in the inner cavity, all net charge lies on the exterior surface of the outer shell.

= Induced charge on a conducting sphere in a uniform electric field
{parent=Conductor in electrostatic equilibrium}

A neutral conducting sphere of radius $R$ in the field $E\widehat z$ has exterior potential and induced surface charge
$$
\Phi=-E\left(r-\frac{R^3}{r^2}\right)\cos\theta,
\qquad
\sigma=3\epsilon_0E\cos\theta.
$$
The surface integral of $\sigma$ is zero.

= Electrostatic energy
{parent=Electrostatics}
{wiki}

Electrostatic energy can be written as one half the charge-potential integral or epsilon-zero over two times the electric-field energy integral.

= Electrostatic energy of a three-plate capacitor
{parent=Electrostatic energy}
{title2=$W=\frac{\epsilon_0AV^2H}{H^2-a^2}$}

In the parallel-plate approximation, a central plate at potential $V$ between grounded plates at $z=\pm H$ forms two parallel capacitors of gaps $H+a$ and $H-a$. Summing their field energies gives the displayed formula. At fixed voltage it is minimized at $a=0$. Finite circular plates have fringe fields; the uniform-gap expression assumes those edge corrections are neglected.

= Electrostatic energy of a uniformly charged solid sphere
{parent=Electrostatic energy}
{title2=$U=\frac{3Q^2}{20\pi\epsilon_0R}$}

A solid sphere of radius $R$ and uniform <charge density> has total <electric charge> $Q$. <Gauss law> gives the radial <electric field> $E_r=Qr/(4\pi\epsilon_0R^3)$ inside and $E_r=Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)$ outside. The <electric potential> vanishing at infinity is $Q(3-r^2/R^2)/(8\pi\epsilon_0R)$ inside and $Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r)$ outside. Integrating the <electrostatic energy> density $\epsilon_0E^2/2$ over both regions gives $U=3Q^2/(20\pi\epsilon_0R)$. In a fixed-density family with $Q\propto Z$ and $R^3\propto Z$, the <electrostatic energy> scales as $Z^{5/3}$.

= Electrostatic energy of alternating-charge concentric shells
{parent=Electrostatic energy}
{title2=$W=\frac{q^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0r_1}$}

A first spherical shell with charge $q$ followed by shells of charges $2(-1)^{n+1}q$ leaves enclosed charge $(-1)^{n+1}q$ after each shell. <Gauss's law> therefore gives the same squared <electric field> $q^2/(16\pi^2\epsilon_0^2r^4)$ in every gap outside the first radius. Integrating the <electrostatic energy> density over all space gives $W=q^2/(8\pi\epsilon_0r_1)$, independent of the other shell radii.

= Electrostatic energy of a uniformly charged spherical shell
{parent=Electrostatic energy}

A thin spherical shell of radius $a$ carrying total charge $Q$ has surface potential
$$
\Phi(a)=\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0a}
$$
and electrostatic self-energy
$$
U=\frac12Q\Phi(a)=\frac{Q^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0a}.
$$

= Electrostatic potential energy of point charges
{parent=Electrostatic energy}

For distinct <point charges> $q_i$ at positions $r_i$, the electrostatic <potential energy> is
$$
U=\frac1{4\pi\epsilon_0}\sum_{i<j}\frac{q_iq_j}{|r_i-r_j|}.
$$
When some charges are fixed, the terms depending on a movable charge form its potential energy in their <electric potential>.

= Coaxial cylindrical capacitor
{parent=Electrostatic energy}

For coaxial cylinders of radii $a<b$ and length $L\gg b$, carrying charges per unit length $\pm\lambda$,
$$
E(r)=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0r}\widehat r,
\qquad
V=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\log\frac ba,
\qquad
C=\frac{2\pi\epsilon_0L}{\log(b/a)}.
$$
The field energy is $U=\lambda^2L\log(b/a)/(4\pi\epsilon_0)=QV/2$.

= Coaxial capacitor with grounded inner and outer cylinders
{parent=Coaxial cylindrical capacitor}
{title2=$C'=2\pi\epsilon_0(1/\log\lambda+1/\log(2/\lambda))$}

A driven cylindrical conductor between two grounded coaxial conductors forms two <coaxial cylindrical capacitors> in parallel. For radii $a,\lambda a,2a$, the <capacitance per unit length> is the sum $C'=2\pi\epsilon_0[1/\log\lambda+1/\log(2/\lambda)]$. Writing $u=\log\lambda$ and $L=\log2$, the function $1/u+1/(L-u)$ is strictly <convex> on $(0,L)$ and symmetric about $L/2$. Hence $C'$ has its unique minimum at $\lambda=\sqrt2$, with value $8\pi\epsilon_0/\log2$. As $\lambda=1+\delta$ approaches the inner conductor, $C'\sim2\pi\epsilon_0/\delta$, agreeing with the local <parallel-plate capacitor> approximation.

= Electric dipole

{wiki}

An electric dipole is a pair of equal and opposite charges in a small separation limit with their charge-times-separation vector held fixed.

= Electric dipole-dipole interaction
{parent=Electric dipole}

Two point dipoles separated by $\mathbf r$ have interaction proportional to
$$
\frac{\mathbf p_1\cdot\mathbf p_2}{r^3}
-\frac{3(\mathbf p_1\cdot\mathbf r)(\mathbf p_2\cdot\mathbf r)}{r^5}.
$$

The formula combines the source <electric dipole> field with the second dipole's coupling to that field.

= Electric multipole expansion
{parent=Electrostatics}

= Multipole expansion
{synonym}

= Electrostatic multipole expansion
{synonym}

For a localized charge distribution, the far <electric potential> is an inverse-radius expansion whose first terms are the total charge monopole, the electric dipole moment, and the electric quadrupole moment.

= Electric quadrupole moment
{parent=Electric multipole expansion}
{title2=$Q_{ij}=\sum_a q_a(3r_{a,i}r_{a,j}-|r_a|^2\delta_{ij})$}

The trace-free <electric quadrupole moment> is a symmetric tensor measuring the second-order term in an <electric multipole expansion>. For a localized charge distribution its contribution to the far-field <electrostatic potential> is $Q_{ij}\widehat r_i\widehat r_j/(8\pi\epsilon_0r^3)$, with summation over indices. The moment may be nonzero even when the total <electric charge> or <electric dipole moment> is nonzero; it should not be confused with an <electric quadrupole> whose lower moments vanish. Changing the origin generally changes the tensor when lower moments are nonzero.

= Electric quadrupole
{parent=Electric multipole expansion}
{wiki}

An electric quadrupole is a charge distribution whose total charge and <electric dipole moment> vanish while its second charge moment does not. Its potential decays as $r^{-3}$.

= Multipole expansion of three collinear charges
{parent=Electric multipole expansion}

For charges $-Q,NQ,-MQ$ at $z=d,0,-d$, respectively,
$$
\Phi(r,\theta)=\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[
\frac{N-M-1}{r}+\frac{(M-1)d\cos\theta}{r^2}
-\frac{(M+1)d^2P_2(\cos\theta)}{r^3}+O(r^{-4})\right].
$$
The monopole vanishes when $N=M+1$, the dipole vanishes when $M=1$, and both vanish when $(N,M)=(2,1)$.

= Dipole and quadrupole scaling limits of three collinear charges
{parent=Multipole expansion of three collinear charges}

Subject to $N=M+1$, keeping $Qd$ fixed as $d\to0$ gives a finite point-dipole limit when $M\ne1$. Keeping $Qd^2$ fixed gives a finite pure-quadrupole limit only when $M=1$; otherwise the dipole diverges.

= Transverse force from a collinear electric quadrupole
{parent=Multipole expansion of three collinear charges}

For charges $-Q,2Q,-Q$ at $z=d,0,-d$, the exact force on a charge $-Q$ at $(x,0,0)$ is
$$
F=-\frac{Q^2x}{2\pi\epsilon_0}
\left(\frac1{|x|^3}-\frac1{(x^2+d^2)^{3/2}}\right)\widehat x.
$$
It points toward the origin for every nonzero $x$.

= Magnetostatics
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{wiki}

Magnetostatics studies time-independent magnetic fields and steady currents. In vacuum its field equations are $\nabla\mathbin\cdot\mathbf B=0$ and $\nabla\mathbin\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J$.

= Magnetic scalar potential
{parent=Magnetostatics}
{title2=$\mathbf B=-\nabla\psi$}
{wiki}

In a simply connected current-free region of constant <magnetic permeability>, <Ampère's circuital law> makes the <magnetic field> curl-free, so it admits a scalar potential. The absence of magnetic monopoles then gives <Laplace's equation> for that potential. Some conventions define the potential for $\mathbf H$ instead of $\mathbf B$, differing by the constant permeability. = Gravity-equivalent magnetic surface density
{parent=Magnetic scalar potential}
{title2=$\Sigma_M=-B_{z0}/\sqrt{\pi G\mu_0}$}

For an isolated razor-thin disk, rescale its exterior <magnetic scalar potential> to satisfy $\sqrt{4\pi G/\mu_0}\mathbf B=-\nabla\Phi_M$. Comparing the normal magnetic boundary derivative with $\partial_z\Phi=2\pi G\Sigma$ identifies the displayed equivalent signed <surface density>. Both exterior potentials satisfy <Laplace's equation>. The equivalent <surface density> is a representation of the magnetic boundary data, rather than physical negative mass.

= Maxwell equations
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{c}
{wiki}

= Maxwell's equations
{c}
{synonym}

The <Maxwell equations> are the field equations of classical <electromagnetism>. In <SI> units, in vacuum with prescribed <charge density> $\rho$ and <current density> $\mathbf J$, they are
$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla\cdot\mathbf E&=\rho/\epsilon_0,
&\nabla\cdot\mathbf B&=0,\\
\nabla\times\mathbf E&=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},
&\nabla\times\mathbf B&=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $\mathbf E$ is the <electric field> in volts per metre, $\mathbf B$ the <magnetic field> in teslas, $\rho$ in coulombs per cubic metre, and $\mathbf J$ in amperes per square metre. The constants are <vacuum permittivity> $\epsilon_0$ and the vacuum value $\mu_0$ of <magnetic permeability>. “Vacuum” specifies the field laws without a material response; “source-free” additionally means $\rho=0$ and $\mathbf J=0$. Matter described by averaged fields instead requires <Maxwell equations in matter> and <constitutive relations>.

The first equation, <Gauss's law>, makes <electric charge> a source of <electric flux>. <Gauss's law for magnetism> sets the net <magnetic flux> through a closed surface to zero. The two <curl> equations, <Faraday's law> and the <Ampère-Maxwell equation>, couple changes of the <electric field> and <magnetic field>. Appropriate <initial conditions> and <boundary conditions> select a physical solution; the two <divergence> equations constrain the initial fields. \a[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-02-physics-ii-electricity-and-magnetism-spring-2007/2d2f227e6fd26ee25f366759e02f03dc_chapte13em_waves.pdf][MIT 8.02 notes, §13.3: Maxwell's equations]{ref}.

= Electromagnetic duality
{parent=Maxwell equations}
{wiki}

Vacuum <Maxwell equations> without sources are invariant under $E'=E\cos\alpha+cB\sin\alpha$, $cB'=cB\cos\alpha-E\sin\alpha$. At $\alpha=\pi/2$ this is $E'=cB$, $B'=-E/c$. Electric-conductor boundary conditions are not invariant under arbitrary duality rotations; the transformed fields must be tested separately against the wall conditions.

= Derivative-order ambiguity in covariant Maxwell potential equations
{parent=Maxwell equations}
{title2=$\nabla_b\nabla^a A^b-\nabla^a\nabla_bA^b=-C^a{}_bA^b$}

Use the negative curvature convention defined in <curvature-sign convention in Killing derivative identities>, and $C_{ab}=C^c{}_{acb}$. Covariant derivatives of a vector obey $[\nabla_b,\nabla^a]A^b=-C^a{}_bA^b$. Hence the flat-space equivalent expressions $\Box A^a-\partial_b\partial^aA^b$ and $\Box A^a-\partial^a\partial_bA^b$ acquire a difference $+C^a{}_bA^b$ under the <comma-to-semicolon prescription>. Covariantizing $F_{ab}=2\nabla_{[a}A_{b]}$ and its <Maxwell equations> gives $\Box A^a-\nabla^a(\nabla_bA^b)+C^a{}_bA^b=\mu_0J^a$. This form respects <gauge invariance>. Dropping the Ricci term is generally not gauge invariant. The two expressions coincide in a Ricci-flat <vacuum spacetime>; vanishing matter alone with nonzero <cosmological constant> does not imply Ricci flatness.

= Magnetic Galilean limit of the pre-Maxwell equations
{parent=Maxwell equations}

The magnetic nonrelativistic limit neglects displacement current and uses $\partial_t\mathbf B=-\nabla\times\mathbf E$, $\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J$, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$. Under a <Galilean transformation> $\mathbf x'=\mathbf x-\mathbf vt$, $t'=t$, its consistent field transformations are $\mathbf B'=\mathbf B$, $\mathbf J'=\mathbf J$ and $\mathbf E'=\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$. Indeed $\partial_{t'}=\partial_t+\mathbf v\cdot\nabla$ and $\nabla\times(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)=-(\mathbf v\cdot\nabla)\mathbf B$, giving covariance of the induction equation. The full charge-current transformation has $\mathbf J'=\mathbf J-\rho_e\mathbf v$; neglecting its convective correction belongs to the magnetic quasineutral approximation, not exact relativistic electrodynamics. With $\mathbf u'=\mathbf u-\mathbf v$, the combination $\mathbf E+\mathbf u\times\mathbf B$ is invariant. Vanishing electric field in the fluid rest frame therefore gives the <ideal magnetohydrodynamic induction equation>.

= Displacement current
{title2=$\mathbf J_D=\epsilon_0\partial_t\mathbf E$}
{parent=Ampère's circuital law}
{wiki}

In vacuum, <displacement current> is the term $\mathbf J_D=\epsilon_0\partial_t\mathbf E$ in the <Ampère-Maxwell equation>. It has the units of <current density>, but need not describe <electric charge> crossing the region. In <Maxwell equations in matter>, the corresponding term is $\partial_t\mathbf D$, with $\mathbf D$ the <electric displacement field>.

Consider a slowly charging <parallel-plate capacitor> with vacuum gap, plate area $A$, and charge $Q(t)$ on its positive plate. Neglect fringing and assume the charging timescale is long compared with the light-travel time across the apparatus. The nearly uniform gap <electric field> has magnitude $E=Q/(\epsilon_0A)$, so the <displacement current> through the gap is
$$
I_D=\epsilon_0 A\frac{dE}{dt}=\frac{dQ}{dt}=I.
$$
For $A=0.010\,\mathrm{m^2}$ and charging <electric current> $I=1.0\,\mathrm{mA}$, this gives $dE/dt\simeq1.13\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$ and $J_D=0.10\,\mathrm{A/m^2}$. No conducting <electric current> crosses the gap. A spanning surface cutting the wire and another passing between the plates nevertheless give the same magnetic circulation when the full <Ampère-Maxwell equation> is used. \a[https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_18.html#Ch18-S2][Feynman lectures, II.18.2: charging a capacitor]{ref}.

The local consistency check is short. Without <displacement current>, taking the <divergence> of $\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J$ would force $\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0$, allowing no local accumulation of <electric charge>. With <displacement current>, the <divergence of a curl is zero> and <Gauss's law> give
$$
0=\mu_0\left(\nabla\cdot\mathbf J+\epsilon_0\partial_t\nabla\cdot\mathbf E\right)
=\mu_0\left(\nabla\cdot\mathbf J+\partial_t\rho\right).
$$
This is precisely the <charge continuity equation>. <Electric charge conservation> fixes the required <divergence> of the added term; it does not by itself exclude other divergence-free additions. The specific <Maxwell equations> also yield the <electromagnetic wave equation>, linking this consistency correction to propagation at the <speed of light>.

= Magnetic helicity
{parent=Magnetic vector potential}
{title2=$H=\int_V\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B\,dV,\quad\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$}
{wiki}

The <magnetic helicity> integrates the <dot product> of a <magnetic vector potential> and its <magnetic field>. It measures linkage and twisting of magnetic flux. For a <solenoidal vector field> $\mathbf B$ tangent to the boundary, it is unchanged by the gauge transformation $\mathbf A\mapsto\mathbf A+\nabla\chi$, since the change is $\int_V\nabla\cdot(\chi\mathbf B)\,dV=\int_{\partial V}\chi\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf S=0$. If its density satisfies $\partial_t h=\nabla\cdot(f\mathbf B-h\mathbf u)$ with both vector fields tangent to the boundary of a fixed volume, the <divergence theorem> gives conservation of its integral. This is an instance of <helicity conservation by tangent boundary conditions>.

= Magnetic-energy lower bound from helicity
{parent=Magnetic helicity}
{title2=$E_B\geq |H_M|/(2\mu_0 C_D)$}

In a bounded regular domain, fix the global extension or circulation convention for the <magnetic vector potential>. A bounded inverse of <curl> on tangent <solenoidal vector fields> gives $\|\mathbf A\|_2\leq C_D\|\mathbf B\|_2$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> then gives $|H_M|\leq C_D\|\mathbf B\|_2^2=2\mu_0 C_DE_B$. One construction extends the tangent field by zero outside the domain and uses the whole-space <Biot-Savart law>; its kernel of size $|\mathbf x-\mathbf y|^{-2}$ is integrable over a bounded region, giving the required $L^2$ bound. Nonzero conserved <magnetic helicity> therefore prevents complete decay of <magnetic energy>.

Current helicity correlates a <magnetic field> with its <curl>, which is proportional to <electric current density>. It differs from <magnetic helicity>, which uses a <magnetic vector potential>. = Gauge dependence of helicity in an open magnetic flux tube
{parent=Magnetic helicity}
{title2=$H_m/L=\pi bCR_0^2$}

An open <flux tube> carries <magnetic flux> through its end caps. Its ordinary <magnetic helicity> can therefore change under a single-valued <gauge transformation>. For $\mathbf B=aR\mathbf e_\phi+b\mathbf e_z$ inside $R<R_0$ and zero outside, a regular family of <magnetic vector potentials> is $A_\phi=bR/2$, $A_z=C-aR^2/2$ inside and $A_\phi=bR_0^2/(2R)$, $A_z=C-aR_0^2/2$ outside. Their tangential components agree at $R_0$. Inside, $\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B=bC$, so the helicity per unit length is $\pi bCR_0^2$. Changing $C$ is the gauge transformation $\Lambda=\Delta C\,z$, and changes this result whenever $b\ne0$. The convention $A_z=0$ outside gives $C=aR_0^2/2$ and helicity per unit length $\pi abR_0^4/2$, while $C=0$ gives zero. Without an end-cap or gauge convention the ordinary helicity is not unique.

= Material transport of magnetic helicity density
{parent=Magnetic helicity}
{title2=$D_t(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B/\rho)$}

For smooth <ideal magnetohydrodynamics>, write $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$ and $\partial_t\mathbf A=\mathbf u\times\mathbf B-\nabla\varphi$. The <continuity equation> and induction equation give $D_t(\mathbf B/\rho)=(\mathbf B/\rho)\cdot\nabla\mathbf u$. The vector-potential equation becomes $D_t\mathbf A=\nabla(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf A-\varphi)-(\nabla\mathbf u)^T\mathbf A$. Contracting these two equations cancels their velocity-gradient terms and proves
$$
D_t\left(\frac{\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B}{\rho}\right)=\frac{\mathbf B}{\rho}\cdot\nabla(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf A-\varphi).
$$
Consequently the <magnetic helicity> in a material volume satisfies $dH_m/dt=\int_{\partial V}(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf A-\varphi)\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf n\,dS$. It is conserved when the <magnetic field> is tangent to the boundary. A single-valued <gauge transformation> changes $H_m$ by $\int_{\partial V}\Lambda\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf n\,dS$, which also vanishes under that condition.

\frac1{R-\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf R/c},
\qquad
\mathbf A=\frac{\mathbf v}{c^2}\phi,
$$
with every source quantity evaluated at the <retarded time>.

\frac1{c^2}\partial_t\phi+\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0,

= Electromagnetic field tensor
{title2=$F^{\mu\nu}$}
{parent=Electromagnetic four-potential}
{wiki}

The electromagnetic field tensor is
$$
F^{\mu\nu}=\partial^\mu A^\nu-\partial^\nu A^\mu.
$$
It packages the electric and magnetic fields into an antisymmetric rank-two Lorentz tensor and transforms as
$$
F'^{\mu\nu}(x')
=\Lambda^\mu{}_\rho\Lambda^\nu{}_\sigma F^{\rho\sigma}(x).
$$

= Dual electromagnetic field tensor
{parent=Electromagnetic field tensor}
{title2=$\widetilde F^{\mu\nu}$}

The dual electromagnetic field tensor is obtained by applying the <Hodge star operator> to the <electromagnetic field tensor>. For metric signature $(+---)$ and $\epsilon^{0123}=+1$, define $\widetilde F^{\mu\nu}=\tfrac12\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}F_{\rho\sigma}$. With $F^{0i}=-E_i/c$ and $F^{ij}=-\epsilon_{ijk}B_k$, it has components $\widetilde F^{0i}=-B_i$ and $\widetilde F^{ij}=\epsilon_{ijk}E_k/c$. The homogeneous <Maxwell equations> become $\partial_\mu\widetilde F^{\mu\nu}=0$.

= Maxwell Lagrangian
{c}
{parent=Electromagnetic field tensor}
{title2=$\mathcal L=-\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$}

The source-free electromagnetic <Lagrangian density> is quadratic in the <electromagnetic field tensor>. Its <Euler-Lagrange field equation> is $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=0$. Antisymmetry of $F$ makes the density invariant under <gauge transformations>, and contraction of its indices makes it a <Lorentz scalar>.

= Maxwell action
{c}
{parent=Maxwell Lagrangian}
{title2=$S_E=\tfrac14\int F_{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}\,d^4x$}

The source-free <Maxwell Lagrangian> gives $S=-\tfrac14\int F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}\,d^4x$ in mostly-minus Lorentzian signature. Its <Euclidean action> is the displayed positive quadratic functional of the <electromagnetic field tensor>. Variation, with boundary terms vanishing, yields $\partial_\mu F_{\mu\nu}=0$.

= First-order Maxwell action
{parent=Maxwell action}
{title2=$S=\frac1{16\pi}\int\sqrt{-g}\,[F^{ab}F_{ab}-2F^{ab}(\nabla_aA_b-\nabla_bA_a)]$}

Vary the antisymmetric auxiliary <electromagnetic field tensor> and the <electromagnetic four-potential> independently. The first variation sets $F_{ab}=\nabla_aA_b-\nabla_bA_a$ algebraically, and variation of the potential gives $\nabla_bF^{ab}=0$. Substitution of the algebraic equation reduces the first-order action to the usual negative quadratic <Maxwell action>. Metric variation gives the same stress tensor after eliminating the auxiliary field, because the chain-rule term multiplying its variational equation then vanishes.

= Electromagnetic Bianchi identity
{c}
{parent=Electromagnetic field tensor}

Because the <electromagnetic field tensor> is the <exterior derivative> $F=dA$ of a potential, $dF=d^2A=0$. For a torsion-free connection this is
$$
\nabla_aF_{bc}+\nabla_bF_{ca}+\nabla_cF_{ab}=0,
$$
which is the covariant form of the two homogeneous <Maxwell equations>.

= Lorentz force law
{c}
{synonym}

For <four-momentum> $p^\mu$ and <four-velocity> $u^\mu$, the covariant Lorentz-force law is
$$
\frac{dp^\mu}{d\tau}=qF^{\mu\nu}u_\nu.
$$
Its spatial and temporal components are

= Larmor theorem
{c}
{parent=Lorentz force}
{title2=$\boldsymbol\omega=-e\mathbf B/(2m)$}

For particles with the same charge-to-mass ratio, rotationally invariant mutual <central forces>, and a weak uniform <magnetic field>, a frame with the displayed <angular velocity> cancels the velocity-dependent magnetic <Lorentz force> against the <Coriolis force>. The remaining rotational and magnetic terms are quadratic in $\mathbf B$. Thus, to first order in the field, the rotating-frame equations coincide with the zero-field equations. This is the classical dynamical basis of <Larmor precession>; equality is an approximation rather than an exact removal of the magnetic field.

= Radial escape in an inverse-radius axial magnetic field
{parent=Lorentz force}
{title2=$\dot r^2=v_\perp^2-(\ell/r-c)^2,\quad c=qB_0/m$}

For an axial <magnetic field> $\mathbf B=(B_0/r)\mathbf e_z$, let $L=mr^2\dot\theta$. The <Lorentz force> gives $\dot L=-qB_0\dot r$, so $\ell=(L+qB_0r)/m$ is constant. Since <magnetic forces do no work> and $\dot z$ is constant, the transverse speed $v_\perp$ is constant and $\dot r^2=v_\perp^2-(\ell/r-c)^2$. If $c\ell>0$ and $0<v_\perp<|c|$, two finite radial <turning points> confine the motion. If $v_\perp>|c|$, an outward branch escapes to infinite radius. At equality a marginal escaping branch exists when $c\ell>0$, but $\ell=0$ gives a circular orbit instead. The transverse <escape velocity> threshold is $|qB_0|/m$; motion parallel to the <magnetic field> cannot provide transverse escape energy.

= Velocity rotation in a time-dependent axial magnetic field
{parent=Lorentz force}
{title2=$\dot x+i\dot y=V\exp\left(i\phi-\frac{iq}{m}\int_0^t B_z(s)\,ds\right)$}

Under the magnetic-only <Lorentz force>, planar velocity has constant magnitude and an accumulated phase determined by the time integral of the axial field. For $B_z(t)=\beta t$, the planar position involves <Fresnel integrals>. With initial planar velocity $(1,0)$ and zero initial position, its limit is $(\sqrt{\pi m/(4q\beta)},-\sqrt{\pi m/(4q\beta)})$. The model prescribes a magnetic-only force; a physical time-dependent field may also produce an induced electric field.

= Magnetic forces do no work
{parent=Lorentz force}
{title2=$(q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\cdot\mathbf v=0$}

The magnetic part of the <Lorentz force> is perpendicular to the particle <velocity>. Its power is therefore zero, so with no other forces the <kinetic energy> remains constant. This remains true relativistically because $dE/dt=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v$; a magnetic force can change momentum direction without changing total energy.

= Geometrization of the Lorentz force by an affine connection
{parent=Lorentz force}
{title2=$\Gamma^i{}_{tt}=-E^i,\quad\Gamma^i{}_{tj}=\epsilon^i{}_{jk}B^k$}

For smooth stationary fields on $\mathbb R^3$, set $\Gamma^i{}_{tt}=-E^i$ and $\Gamma^i{}_{tj}=\Gamma^i{}_{jt}=\epsilon^i{}_{jk}B^k$, with all other <Christoffel symbols> zero. This <torsion-free connection> makes the equation $\ddot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf E+2\mathbf B\times\dot{\mathbf x}$ a <geodesic equation> with time as <affine parameter>. Arbitrary smooth fields suffice; a metric realization imposes additional potential conditions.

In uniform perpendicular electric and <magnetic fields>, the guiding centre of a charged particle drifts with $\mathbf v_D=\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/|\mathbf B|^2$. This <velocity> is independent of the charge and mass; cyclotron motion is superposed on it.

This follows by summing the magnetic part of the <Lorentz force> over the moving charge carriers.

= Relativistic cyclotron orbit
{title2=$\omega_\tau=qB/m$}
{parent=Lorentz force}

In a constant <magnetic field> $B\widehat z$ with no <electric field>, a charged relativistic particle moves in a circle in the perpendicular plane. Its angular frequency with respect to <proper time> is
$$
\omega_\tau=\frac{qB}{m},
$$
whereas its angular frequency in an inertial coordinate time is $\omega_t=qB/(\gamma m)$. The orbit radius at speed $v$ is
$$
R=\frac{\gamma mv}{|q|B}.
$$

= Charged-particle action over one relativistic cyclotron orbit
{parent=Relativistic cyclotron orbit}

For $qB>0$, choose $A_y=Bx$ and parametrize one clockwise orbit by $x=R\cos(\omega_\tau\tau)$ and $y=-R\sin(\omega_\tau\tau)$. Over $\Delta\tau=2\pi/\omega_\tau$, the <relativistic charged-particle action> is
$$
S=-mc^2\Delta\tau+q\oint A\mathbin\cdot dx
=-\frac{\pi mc^2}{\omega_\tau}(1+\gamma^2).
$$
The same formula with $|\omega_\tau|$ applies for either orbit orientation.

For $\mathcal E=\gamma mc^2$, the temporal component of the covariant Lorentz-force equation gives

The magnetic field does no work because $\mathbf v\cdot(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)=0$.

\left(F^{\mu\rho}F^\nu{}_{\rho}

In particular, $T^{00}=\tfrac12(\epsilon_0E^2+B^2/\mu_0)$.

= Electromagnetic stress-energy divergence identity
{parent=Electromagnetic stress-energy tensor}
{title2=$\nabla_bT^{ab}=F^a{}_c\nabla_bF^{cb}/(4\pi)$}

For signature $(+---)$ in Gaussian electromagnetic units, $T^{ab}=(4\pi)^{-1}[-F^a{}_cF^{bc}+g^{ab}F_{cd}F^{cd}/4]$. The <electromagnetic Bianchi identity> gives $F^{bc}\nabla_bF^a{}_c=\nabla^a(F_{cd}F^{cd})/4$, so differentiating the stress tensor proves the displayed identity. Stress conservation implies the source-free <Maxwell equations> if the matrix $F^a{}_c$ is invertible; otherwise it only forces the field divergence into that matrix's kernel. Conversely source-free Maxwell equations always imply stress conservation.

= Gauge-compensated time translation in electromagnetism
{parent=Electromagnetic stress-energy tensor}
{title2=$\delta A_\nu=\alpha F_{0\nu}$}

With signature $(+,-,-,-)$ and $\mathcal L=-F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}/4$, combine a time translation $\alpha\partial_0A_\nu$ with the <gauge transformation> of parameter $-\alpha A_0$. Its <Noether current> is $J^\mu=-F^{\mu\nu}F_{0\nu}-\delta^\mu_0\mathcal L$, the gauge-invariant energy current. The density is $(|\mathbf E|^2+|\mathbf B|^2)/2$. On the source-free field equations it differs from the canonical translation current by $\partial_\nu(A_0F^{\mu\nu})$, an antisymmetric superpotential divergence, so total energies agree when the boundary term vanishes.

= Relativistic charge density of counterstreaming beams
{parent=Lorentz transformation of electromagnetic fields}
{title2=$\lambda'=-\gamma vI/c^2$}

Two equal opposite line charge densities moving in opposite directions have zero net line charge but a nonzero total current $I$. Under a longitudinal <Lorentz boost>, the charge-current four-vector gives $\lambda'=\gamma(\lambda-vI/c^2)=-\gamma vI/c^2$. Equivalently, for lab densities $n$ and speeds $\pm u$, their separate boosted number densities are $\gamma n(1\mp vu/c^2)$. Their imbalance creates the boosted radial <electric field>, consistently with <Lorentz transformation of electromagnetic fields> and <Gauss's law>.

= Electromagnetic jump conditions across a normally moving plane

If a plane is stationary in one frame and an observer moves normally to it with velocity $\mathbf v$, then

[B'_n]=0,
\qquad
[\mathbf E'_t]=\mathbf v\mathbin\times[\mathbf B'_t].
$$
The second relation accounts for the magnetic-field discontinuity moving through the observer's frame.

= Electromagnetic wave
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{wiki}

An <electromagnetic wave> is a propagating disturbance of the <electric field> and <magnetic field> obeying the <Maxwell equations>. In source-free vacuum its speed is
$$
c=\frac1{\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}}\simeq2.998\times10^8\,\mathrm{m/s},
$$
the <speed of light>. An <electromagnetic wave> can travel through vacuum: a conducting medium is unnecessary. A single traveling <plane electromagnetic wave> has mutually perpendicular <electric field>, <magnetic field>, and propagation direction. This statement applies to that wave; arbitrary <near and far field> configurations need not share these directions or the relation $|\mathbf B|=|\mathbf E|/c$.

The visualization compares perpendicular components on a common scale: $c\mathbf B$ has the same units as $\mathbf E$. The curves are field values along space, rather than paths traced by particles. Their common phase moves toward $+z$, while the <Poynting vector> records the associated <energy flux>.

The <electromagnetic wave equation> gives a propagation law, while the full <Maxwell equations> additionally enforce the field directions and relative amplitudes. This is why solving an independent scalar <wave equation> for each component is insufficient to construct an <electromagnetic wave>. \a[https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_20.html][Feynman lectures, II.20: solutions in free space]{ref}.

= Electromagnetic waveguide
{parent=Electromagnetic wave}

An electromagnetic waveguide confines an <electromagnetic wave> transversely while allowing propagation along its axis. Conducting or dielectric boundaries select transverse modes. In a uniform guide a mode with transverse eigenvalue $k_\perp^2$ has longitudinal wavenumber $k_x^2=k^2-k_\perp^2$; below cutoff it is evanescent. At an open <aperture>, the confined field radiates into an angular spectrum rather than retaining a single guided mode.

= Scalar-potential conducting waveguide modes
{parent=Electromagnetic waveguide}
{title2=$\gamma^2=\omega^2/c^2-k^2$}

For dependence $e^{i(kz-\omega t)}$, a <Helmholtz equation> $(\Delta_\perp+\gamma^2)\psi=0$ generates transverse-magnetic fields $e_\perp=\nabla_\perp\psi$, $e_z=-i\gamma^2\psi/k$, $b_\perp=\omega\,\widehat z\times\nabla_\perp\psi/(kc^2)$, $b_z=0$, for $k\ne0$. The conducting wall requires a <Dirichlet boundary condition> on $\psi$. A transverse-electric representation is $e_\perp=\widehat z\times\nabla_\perp\psi$, $e_z=0$, $b_\perp=-k\nabla_\perp\psi/\omega$, $b_z=i\gamma^2\psi/\omega$, with a <Neumann boundary condition>. These are families of modes, not a completeness assertion for multiply connected guides and their possible TEM fields.

= Electromagnetic aperture angular spectrum
{parent=Electromagnetic waveguide}
{title2=$\mathbf q=(\kappa,\nu,\eta),\quad\kappa=\sqrt{k^2-\nu^2-\eta^2}$}

For an aperture normal to $\mathbf e_x$, let $F_y,F_z$ be its tangential <electric field> traces extended by zero outside the aperture. With the outgoing branch of $\kappa$, the <Weyl plane-wave representation> and the curl aperture integral give a spectrum proportional to $(-(\nu\widehat F_y+\eta\widehat F_z)/\kappa,\widehat F_y,\widehat F_z)$. Each wave is transverse to $\mathbf q$. The longitudinal electric component is regenerated by oblique diffraction even when it vanishes at the input. For $G=e^{ikr}/(4\pi r)$ and a unit prefactor on the stated curl integral, the spectrum has an overall factor $1/2$; a doubled half-space Green kernel gives factor one. The normalization must follow the chosen Green kernel and equivalent-current convention.

= Rectangular electromagnetic waveguide
{parent=Electromagnetic wave}

A rectangular electromagnetic waveguide has a vacuum interior bounded by four <perfect conductor> walls parallel to its axis. The harmonic fields obey zero tangential <electric field> and zero normal <magnetic field> at the walls. <Maxwell equations> determine transverse fields from longitudinal components; a purely longitudinal oscillating <magnetic field> by itself cannot propagate, since its divergence would be nonzero.

= Waveguide cutoff frequency
{parent=Rectangular electromagnetic waveguide}
{title2=$\omega_{mn}=c\pi\sqrt{m^2/a^2+n^2/b^2}$}

For a <transverse electric mode of a rectangular waveguide>, the dispersion relation is $\omega^2=c^2(k^2+\kappa_{mn}^2)$, with $\kappa_{mn}^2=(m\pi/a)^2+(n\pi/b)^2$. Below $\omega_{mn}=c\kappa_{mn}$, the longitudinal wave number is imaginary and the mode is evanescent. If $a\ge b$, the lowest propagating cutoff is $\omega_{10}=\pi c/a$, or ordinary frequency $c/(2a)$.

= Waveguide phase and group velocities
{parent=Waveguide cutoff frequency}
{title2=$v_{\mathrm{ph}}v_g=c^2$}

Above a mode's <waveguide cutoff frequency> $\omega_c$, its <phase velocity> is $c/[1-(\omega_c/\omega)^2]^{1/2}$ and its <group velocity> is $c[1-(\omega_c/\omega)^2]^{1/2}$. Differentiate $\omega^2=c^2k^2+\omega_c^2$ to obtain the latter. Phase travels faster than $c$ while the wave packet travels slower, so the phase speed does not imply superluminal information transport.

= Transverse electric mode of a rectangular waveguide
{parent=Rectangular electromagnetic waveguide}
{title2=$\mathrm{TE}_{mn}$}

For a transverse electric mode, $E_z=0$ and $B_z=B_0(x,y)e^{i(kz-\omega t)}$. Put $\Delta=(\omega/c)^2-k^2$. <Maxwell equations> give $B_x=ik\partial_xB_z/\Delta$, $B_y=ik\partial_yB_z/\Delta$, $E_x=i\omega\partial_yB_z/\Delta$, and $E_y=-i\omega\partial_xB_z/\Delta$. The wall conditions become <Neumann boundary conditions> for $B_0$, whose separated solutions are $\cos(m\pi x/a)\cos(n\pi y/b)$. The constant $(0,0)$ candidate is excluded by Faraday's equation because its corresponding electric field vanishes.

= TE wave equation in a stratified magnetodielectric
{c}
{parent=Transverse electric polarization}
{title2=$E_{y,xx}+E_{y,zz}-\frac{\mu'}\mu E_{y,z}+\omega^2\mu\varepsilon E_y=0$}

With permittivity and permeability depending only on $z$ and time dependence $e^{i\omega t}$, a <transverse electric polarization> $\mathbf E=\widehat{\mathbf y}E_y(x,z)$ obeys the displayed equation. <Maxwell's equations> give $H_x=E_{y,z}/(i\omega\mu)$ and $H_z=-E_{y,x}/(i\omega\mu)$. = Refractive index

The refractive index is the ratio of the vacuum speed of light to a medium's phase speed. In a homogeneous isotropic medium, $n=c\sqrt{\varepsilon\mu}$ in compatible units.

= Inverse-square refractive-index profile
{parent=Refractive index}
{title2=$k^2(z)=k_0^2[a^2-b^2/(z-z_0)^2]$}

A <transverse electric polarization> with constant permeability and conserved transverse wavenumber $k_0\sin\theta_0$ satisfies $e''+[\beta^2-(\nu^2-1/4)/(z-z_0)^2]e=0$, with $\beta^2=k_0^2(a^2-\sin^2\theta_0)$ and $\nu^2=1/4+k_0^2b^2$. = Electromagnetic wave equation
{parent=Electromagnetic wave}
{title2=$\nabla^2\mathbf E-c^{-2}\partial_t^2\mathbf E=0$}
{wiki}

In source-free vacuum the <electric field> and <magnetic field> obey
$$
\nabla^2\mathbf E-c^{-2}\partial_t^2\mathbf E=0,\qquad
\nabla^2\mathbf B-c^{-2}\partial_t^2\mathbf B=0.
$$
A brief derivation takes the <curl> of <Faraday's law> and substitutes the <Ampère-Maxwell equation>. The <vector calculus> identity $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)-\nabla^2\mathbf E$, together with source-free <Gauss's law>, leaves $\nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t^2\mathbf E$. The same argument gives the <magnetic field> equation. The operator $\nabla^2$ is the <Laplacian>, applied componentwise.

= Plane electromagnetic wave
{parent=Electromagnetic wave}
{title2=$\mathbf B=\widehat{\mathbf k}\times\mathbf E/c$}
{wiki}

A monochromatic vacuum <plane electromagnetic wave> has phase $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t$, where $\mathbf k$ is the <wavevector>, $\omega>0$ the <angular frequency>, and $\omega=c|\mathbf k|$. Its field amplitudes obey $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf E_0=\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf B_0=0$ and $\mathbf B_0=\mathbf k\times\mathbf E_0/\omega$.

For the <linear polarization> and orientation in the visualization, choose a right-handed <Cartesian coordinate system> and put
$$
\mathbf E(z,t)=E_0\cos(kz-\omega t)\widehat{\mathbf x},\qquad
\mathbf B(z,t)=\frac{E_0}{c}\cos(kz-\omega t)\widehat{\mathbf y},\qquad
k>0,\quad\omega=ck.
$$
Constant phase means $z=ct+\text{constant}$, so propagation is along $+z$. The <cross product> $\widehat{\mathbf x}\times\widehat{\mathbf y}=\widehat{\mathbf z}$ fixes the positive <Poynting vector>. Both fields reverse together every half-period and are in phase. Reversing the propagation direction with the same electric orientation reverses the magnetic orientation.

These fields are spatially uniform across every transverse plane. An infinite <plane electromagnetic wave> is an idealization with infinite total <electromagnetic energy>; it is useful as a local approximation and as a building block for <superposition>. For <frequency> $f=100\,\mathrm{MHz}$, $\omega=2\pi f$ and <wavelength> $\lambda=c/f\simeq3.00\,\mathrm m$, independently of $E_0$.

= Polarization of an electromagnetic wave
{parent=Plane electromagnetic wave}

= Electromagnetic polarization
{synonym}

The polarization of an electromagnetic wave describes the path traced by its transverse electric-field vector at a fixed point. A monochromatic wave may be linearly, circularly, or elliptically polarized; in vacuum it has two independent transverse polarization states.

This specializes <polarization of waves> to electric-field oscillations.

= Faraday effect

The Faraday effect rotates the orientation of linear <polarization of an electromagnetic wave> during propagation through a magnetized medium. = Rotation measure
{parent=Faraday effect}
{title2=$\mathrm{RM}=a_0\int n_e B_\parallel\,dz$}

For the high-frequency <Faraday effect> in a cold <plasma>, the rotation measure is the coefficient in $\Delta\phi=\lambda^2\mathrm{RM}$. It is a density-weighted line integral of the line-of-sight <magnetic field>; the coefficient $a_0$ depends on the units. A sign convention for the line of sight fixes its sign. Correlations across an extended source can constrain a foreground <magnetic energy spectrum>, subject to statistical assumptions.

= Long-path projection of a magnetic correlation
{parent=Rotation measure}
{title2=$C_{\mathrm{RM}}(s)\simeq a_0^2n_e^2L\int H(k_\perp)e^{i\mathbf k_\perp\cdot\mathbf s}d^2k_\perp/(2\pi)^2$}

For constant <electron> density, common path length $L$, and a <solenoidal isotropic spectral tensor>, the rotation-measure <correlation> contains the window $|\int_0^Le^{ik_zz}dz|^2$. As $L$ becomes large compared with the <correlation length>, this window acts as $2\pi L\delta(k_z)$ within the <integral>. The transverse projector then has $zz$ component one, yielding the displayed two-dimensional <Fourier transform>. For finite $L$, the windowed integral retains longitudinal modes and endpoint corrections. The method of using such <rotation measure> correlations to infer magnetic spectra is developed by https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0302426[Enßlin and Vogt].

= Hankel inversion of a rotation-measure correlation
{c}
{parent=Long-path projection of a magnetic correlation}
{title2=$H(k)=\frac{2\pi}{a_0^2n_e^2L}\int_0^\infty rJ_0(kr)C_{\mathrm{RM}}(r)\,dr$}

Invert the two-dimensional <Fourier transform> in the <long-path projection of a magnetic correlation>, then perform the angular integration in <polar coordinates>. The angular integral is $2\pi J_0(kr)$, a <Bessel function of the first kind>, giving the displayed <Hankel transform> with the stated <Fourier-Hankel normalization for an isotropic spectrum>. The result recovers the scalar tensor coefficient $H$, whose shell-integrated <magnetic energy spectrum> has an additional factor $k^2$.

= Polarizer
{parent=Polarization of an electromagnetic wave}
{wiki}

= Polariser
{synonym}

A linear <polarizer> transmits one <linear polarization> and suppresses the orthogonal one. Two ideal crossed <polarizers> block light unless an intervening medium changes its <polarization of an electromagnetic wave>. A <twisted nematic field effect> uses this selection to convert field-driven reorientation into brightness modulation.

= Stokes parameters
{parent=Polarization of an electromagnetic wave}
{c}
{title2=$(I,Q,U,V)$}

The Stokes parameters $I,Q,U,V$ describe intensity, two linear-polarization components and circular polarization. $Q$ and $U$ depend on the chosen transverse basis. On the celestial sphere, $Q\pm iU$ transform as spin-two fields rather than as ordinary scalars.

= Linear polarization
{parent=Polarization of an electromagnetic wave}

A wave is linearly polarized when its oscillating <electric field> always lies along one fixed line. For a transverse <plane wave>, that line is perpendicular to the <wavevector>. Unlike <circular polarization>, the field oscillates along the line rather than rotating through directions.

= Circular polarization
{parent=Polarization of an electromagnetic wave}
{wiki}

An electromagnetic wave has circular polarization when its transverse <electric field> has constant magnitude and rotates once during each period. In complex-amplitude notation, two orthogonal components have equal magnitude and a relative phase of one quarter-cycle.

= Perfect conductor
{parent=Plane electromagnetic wave}
{wiki}

At the surface of a perfect conductor, the tangential <electric field> and normal <magnetic field> vanish. An incident <plane electromagnetic wave> is therefore accompanied by a reflected wave whose tangential electric field cancels the incident field at the surface.

= Scalar polarization reduction at a conducting corrugation
{parent=Perfect conductor}
{title2=$E_y|_\Gamma=0,\quad\partial_nH_y|_\Gamma=0$}

If a metal surface is $z=h(x)$ and the incident field is independent of $y$, Maxwell's equations separate into <TE polarization> and <TM polarization>. The metal condition that the tangential electric field vanish sets $E_y=0$ in the TE sector. In the TM sector $\mathbf E=i\nabla\times(H_y\widehat{\mathbf y})/(\omega\epsilon)$, so the same condition gives $(\partial_z-h'\partial_x)H_y=0$. This explains why the TE problem agrees with scalar Dirichlet acoustics while the TM problem has different rough-surface boundary forcing.

= Normal reflection of an electromagnetic wave from a perfect conductor
{parent=Perfect conductor}

At normal incidence, the reflected electric amplitude is the negative of the incident amplitude at the surface, while the reflected magnetic amplitude has the same sign. Their superposition forms a <standing wave> with an electric node and a magnetic antinode at the conductor.

= Interface conditions for electromagnetic fields
{parent=Plane electromagnetic wave}
{wiki}

Across an interface with unit normal $\mathbf n$ from medium one to medium two, <Maxwell equations> imply $\mathbf n\times(\mathbf E_2-\mathbf E_1)=0$, $\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf B_2-\mathbf B_1)=0$, $\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf D_2-\mathbf D_1)=\sigma_f$, and $\mathbf n\times(\mathbf H_2-\mathbf H_1)=\mathbf K_f$. Here $\sigma_f$ and $\mathbf K_f$ are the free surface <electric charge> and <electric current> densities.

= Dielectric-interface boundary conditions
{parent=Interface conditions for electromagnetic fields}

Without free surface charge or current, the tangential components of $E$ and $H$ and the normal components of $D$ and $B$ are continuous across an interface.

= Phase matching at a planar wave interface
{parent=Dielectric-interface boundary conditions}

Equality of the boundary fields for every tangential position and time forces equal frequency and equal tangential wavevector for incident, reflected, and transmitted plane waves.

= Snell law for electromagnetic waves
{parent=Phase matching at a planar wave interface}
{c}

For refractive indices $n_-$ and $n_+$,
$$
\theta_R=\theta_I,
\qquad
n_-\sin\theta_I=n_+\sin\theta_T.
$$

= Total internal reflection
{parent=Snell law for electromagnetic waves}
{wiki}

Total internal reflection occurs when a wave travels from higher to lower <refractive index> above the critical incidence angle, leaving no propagating transmitted plane wave normal to the interface.

= Critical angle for total internal reflection
{title2=$\theta_c$}
{parent=Total internal reflection}

For refractive indices $n_->n_+$, the critical angle satisfies $\sin\theta_c=n_+/n_-$. Above it, the transmitted normal wavenumber is imaginary and the transmitted field is an <evanescent wave>.

= Fresnel equations
{c}
{parent=Dielectric-interface boundary conditions}
{wiki}

The <Fresnel equations> give reflection and transmission amplitudes for both transverse-electric and transverse-magnetic <polarization of an electromagnetic wave> at a planar interface. They follow by applying <interface conditions for electromagnetic fields> to incident, reflected and refracted <plane electromagnetic waves>.

= Transverse-electric Fresnel reflection coefficient
{parent=Fresnel equations}

When the permeabilities agree, the signed reflected-to-incident electric-field ratio for polarization normal to the plane of incidence is
$$
r_{\rm TE}
=\frac{n_-\cos\theta_I-n_+\cos\theta_T}
{n_-\cos\theta_I+n_+\cos\theta_T}.
$$
For incidence from lower to higher refractive index it never vanishes.

= Reflected-to-transmitted transverse-electric amplitude ratio
{parent=Transverse-electric Fresnel reflection coefficient}

For equal permeabilities on the two sides of an interface, tangential-field continuity and <Snell law for electromagnetic waves> give
$$
\frac{E_R}{E_T}
=\frac12\left(1-\frac{\tan\theta_R}{\tan\theta_T}\right)
$$
for consistently signed electric amplitudes normal to the plane of incidence.

= Plane wave in an anisotropic dielectric
{parent=Plane electromagnetic wave}

For $D_i=\varepsilon_{ij}E_j$, $B=\mu H$, and a plane wave with electric polarization $e$, the source-free Maxwell equations imply
$$
k\times(k\times e)+\omega^2\mu\varepsilon e=0.
$$
If $e$ is an eigenvector of $\varepsilon$, then $e\perp k$, the dispersion relation is $|k|^2=\omega^2\mu\varepsilon_e$, and the <Poynting vector> is parallel to $k$.

= Poynting vector
{parent=Electromagnetic wave}
{c}
{title2=$\mathbf S=\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/\mu_0$}
{wiki}

In vacuum the <Poynting vector>
$$
\mathbf S=\frac1{\mu_0}\mathbf E\times\mathbf B
$$
is the local <energy flux>, measured in watts per square metre. Its <surface integral> $P=\int_A\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf n\,dA$ gives signed <power> crossing a surface with unit <normal vector> $\mathbf n$. Positive flux follows the chosen normal; for a closed surface the usual convention is outward. <Power> depends on the normal component and area, rather than just the field magnitude.

The <Poynting vector> applies to steady fields as well as <electromagnetic waves>. In a driven resistive <electrical circuit>, surrounding fields can carry <energy> into the conductor, where electrical <work> becomes <Joule heating>. This turns a circuit-level power balance into a spatial description of energy delivery. \a[https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-6-001-electromagnetic-fields-and-energy-spring-2008/0f814aee1c72517df7d7e7e96b7365e1_11.pdf][Haus and Melcher, Electromagnetic Fields and Energy, chapter 11, §§11.0–11.3]{ref}.

= Poynting theorem
{parent=Poynting vector}
{c}
{title2=$\partial_tu+\nabla\cdot\mathbf S=-\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E$}

= Poynting's theorem
{c}
{synonym}

The <Poynting theorem> is the local <energy> balance of classical <electromagnetism>. For vacuum field laws, with <current density> $\mathbf J$, the <electromagnetic energy> per unit volume is
$$
u=\frac{\epsilon_0|\mathbf E|^2}{2}+\frac{|\mathbf B|^2}{2\mu_0},
$$
and
$$
\partial_tu+\nabla\cdot\mathbf S=-\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E.
$$
Thus a decrease in local <electromagnetic energy> can supply outward <energy flux> or <work> on <electric charge>. The term $\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E$ is <power> transferred to matter per unit volume. Its sign matters: positive values remove field <energy>, while negative values describe matter supplying the field. The magnetic part of the <Lorentz force> supplies no work term.

For a fixed volume $V$, the <divergence theorem> gives the directly measurable balance
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\int_Vu\,dV
=-\int_{\partial V}\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf n\,dA
-\int_V\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E\,dV.
$$
For example, $2.0\,\mathrm W$ entering a region with $0.50\,\mathrm W$ leaving and $1.20\,\mathrm W$ transferred to matter means stored <electromagnetic energy> increases at $0.30\,\mathrm W$. In source-free vacuum only the boundary flux changes the stored <energy>; a nonzero local <Poynting vector> can still have zero net closed-surface flux.

A brief proof takes the <dot product> of the <Ampère-Maxwell equation> with $\mathbf E/\mu_0$ and uses <Faraday's law> with $\mathbf B/\mu_0$. The identity $\nabla\cdot(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)=\mathbf B\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf E)-\mathbf E\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B)$ turns the remaining terms into the <divergence> of the <Poynting vector>. \a[https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_27.html#Ch27-S3][Feynman lectures, II.27.3: local field-energy conservation]{ref}.

= Closed-surface Poynting flux of a plane wave
{parent=Poynting theorem}

For a real linearly polarized monochromatic vacuum <plane wave>, the local <Poynting vector> is proportional to $\cos^2(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)\widehat{\mathbf k}$. Its flux through a closed surface generally oscillates at $2\omega$, with zero cycle average, although particular volume geometries can cancel it identically. For a circularly polarized plane wave of constant amplitude the <Poynting vector> is spatially and temporally constant, so the net flux through every closed surface is exactly zero. A nonzero constant local energy flux must not be confused with the net closed-surface flux.

= Electromagnetic energy
{title2=$U_{\rm EM}$}
{parent=Poynting theorem}

In vacuum the electromagnetic energy density is
$$
u=\frac{\varepsilon_0E^2}{2}+\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0},
$$
and the field energy in a region is $U_{\rm EM}=\int u\,dV$.

= Magnetic energy
{parent=Electromagnetic energy}
{title2=$E_B$}

In vacuum-permeability <magnetohydrodynamics>, the <magnetic energy> in a volume is $E_B=\int B^2/(2\mu_0)\,dV$. Its volume density equals the <magnetic pressure>.

= Magnetic energy spectrum
{parent=Magnetic energy}
{title2=$E_B(k)$}

The shell-integrated magnetic energy spectrum distributes <magnetic energy> over <wavenumbers>. With a <solenoidal isotropic spectral tensor> normalized to $\widehat C_{ii}=2H(k)$ and the inverse <Fourier transform> measure $d^3k/(2\pi)^3$, one has $\mathbb E|\mathbf B|^2=\pi^{-2}\int k^2H(k)dk$. In <Gaussian units>, $E_B(k)=k^2H(k)/(8\pi^3)$ integrates to the energy density $\mathbb E|\mathbf B|^2/(8\pi)$. A spectrum per unit mass or normalized to $B^2/2$ has different constant factors. Scale-local fluctuation estimates give $\delta B_l^2$ proportional to $kE_B(k)$ at $k\sim l^{-1}$ when the spectrum is normalized consistently.

= Energy density and flux of a plane electromagnetic wave
{parent=Poynting theorem}

For a single traveling vacuum <plane electromagnetic wave>, $\mathbf B=\widehat{\mathbf k}\times\mathbf E/c$ gives equal electric and magnetic contributions to <electromagnetic energy>:
$$
u_E=u_B=\frac12\epsilon_0|\mathbf E|^2,\qquad
u=\epsilon_0|\mathbf E|^2,\qquad
\mathbf S=cu\widehat{\mathbf k}.
$$
For the fields with <linear polarization> along $x$ and magnetic orientation along $y$ specified above,
$$
\mathbf S(z,t)=\epsilon_0cE_0^2\cos^2(kz-\omega t)\widehat{\mathbf z},\qquad
\langle\mathbf S\rangle=\frac{\epsilon_0cE_0^2}{2}\widehat{\mathbf z}.
$$
Angle brackets mean an average over one period at fixed position. The factor $1/2$ comes from averaging squared <cosine>; $E_0$ is the peak amplitude, whereas the <root mean square> field is $E_{\mathrm{rms}}=E_0/\sqrt2$. The equal energy contributions oscillate together, rather than trading energy as in an <RLC circuit>. \a[https://live.ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-013-electromagnetics-and-applications-spring-2009/d3be4ea78b036a6362230fb41780cf54_MIT6_013S09_notes.pdf][MIT 6.013 course notes, §2.7.3: power and energy in uniform plane waves]{ref}.

For $E_0=100\,\mathrm{V/m}$, using $c=299792458\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and $\epsilon_0\simeq8.854\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{F/m}$,
$$
B_0=\frac{100}{c}\,\mathrm{V/m}\simeq3.34\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm T,
\qquad
\langle S_z\rangle\simeq13.3\,\mathrm{W/m^2},
\qquad
\langle u\rangle\simeq4.43\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{J/m^3}.
$$
A $0.010\,\mathrm{m^2}$ surface normal to $+z$ therefore has average crossing <power> $0.133\,\mathrm W$. This is incident <power>; the amount absorbed depends on the surface. Doubling the field amplitude quadruples the average flux.

= Poynting theorem in a linear anisotropic medium
{c}
{parent=Poynting theorem}

For symmetric time-independent constitutive tensors,
$$
u=\frac12(\varepsilon_{ij}E_iE_j+\mu_{ij}H_iH_j),
\qquad
S=E\times H,
$$
and the macroscopic Maxwell equations give $\partial_tu+\nabla\cdot S=-E\cdot J$.

= Near and far field
{parent=Electromagnetic wave}
{wiki}

The <near and far field> distinguish source-dependent electromagnetic field regions. Close to a localized source, reactive fields and induction terms contribute; sufficiently far away, the leading transverse fields decrease as the inverse distance and carry outgoing power. The <radiation zone> describes this far-field limit.

= Radiation zone
{parent=Near and far field}

In the radiation zone of a localized source, the leading electromagnetic fields decay as $1/R$, are transverse to the observation direction, and satisfy $B=\widehat R\times E/c$. Terms decaying faster than $1/R$ do not contribute to the limiting radiated power.

= Intensity of an isotropic radiator
{parent=Radiation zone}

An isotropic source of average power $P$ has intensity
$$
\langle S\rangle=\frac{P}{4\pi r^2}
$$
at distance $r$. For monochromatic vacuum radiation, its electric-field amplitude is $E_0=\sqrt{2\langle S\rangle/(\epsilon_0c)}$.

= Multipole radiation
{parent=Radiation zone}
{wiki}

<Multipole radiation> expands radiation from localized time-dependent sources into electric and magnetic multipoles. <Electric dipole radiation> is the leading electric term, while <magnetic dipole radiation> and higher electric multipoles contribute when the leading term vanishes or higher precision is needed.

= Electric dipole radiation
{parent=Multipole radiation}

For a localized source whose size is small compared with its radiation wavelength, the radiation fields of its electric dipole moment $p$ are
$$
E_{\rm rad}=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi R}\widehat R\times(\widehat R\times\ddot p(t-R/c)),
\qquad
B_{\rm rad}=\frac1c\widehat R\times E_{\rm rad}.
$$
Their total instantaneous power is
$$
\mathcal P=\frac{\mu_0}{6\pi c}|\ddot p(t-R/c)|^2.
$$

= Electric-dipole approximation for radiation
{parent=Electric dipole radiation}

= Dipole approximation for radiation
{synonym}

For a source of characteristic size $a$ and <angular frequency> $\omega$, the electric-dipole approximation requires
$$
\frac{\omega a}{c}\ll1,
$$
so the source is much smaller than its radiation wavelength. Observing the pure $1/R$ radiation field additionally requires the <radiation zone>, $R\gg c/\omega$ and $R\gg a$.

= Angular distribution of electric dipole radiation
{parent=Electric dipole radiation}

If $\theta$ is the angle between the observation direction and $\ddot p$, then
$$
\frac{dP}{d\Omega}
=\frac{\mu_0}{16\pi^2c}|\ddot p|^2\sin^2\theta.
$$
Integrating over <solid angle> uses $\int\sin^2\theta\,d\Omega=8\pi/3$ and gives the total <electric dipole radiation> power.

= Radiation damping of a harmonically oscillating charge
{parent=Electric dipole radiation}

A charge $q$ undergoing <simple harmonic motion> of <angular frequency> $\omega$ and slowly varying amplitude $A$ radiates the averaged power
$$
\langle P\rangle=\frac{\mu_0q^2A^2\omega^4}{12\pi c}.
$$
Its mechanical energy $E=mA^2\omega^2/2$ therefore has <exponential decay> rate $\gamma=\mu_0q^2\omega^2/(6\pi mc)$ and <half-life>
$$
t_{1/2}=\frac{6\pi mc\log2}{\mu_0q^2\omega^2}.
$$

= Magnetic dipole radiation
{parent=Multipole radiation}

Electromagnetic duality replaces the electric dipole $p$ by $m/c$ for a magnetic dipole moment $m$. Therefore
$$
\mathcal P=\frac{\mu_0}{6\pi c^3}|\ddot m|^2.
$$

This is the magnetic-dipole term of the <multipole radiation> expansion.

= Magnetic-dipole spin-down
{parent=Magnetic dipole radiation}

If a rigid body's inclined magnetic dipole rotates at angular speed $\Omega$, its radiated power is $C\Omega^4$. Conservation of rotational energy $I\Omega^2/2$ then gives
$$
\dot\Omega=-\frac CI\Omega^3,
\qquad
\Omega(t)^{-2}=\Omega_0^{-2}+\frac{2C}{I}t.
$$
