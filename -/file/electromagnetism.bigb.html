= Electromagnetism
{wiki}

Electromagnetism describes how <electric charge> and <electric current> interact through the <electric field> $\mathbf E$ and <magnetic field> $\mathbf B$. The <Maxwell equations> describe the fields and their sources; the <Lorentz force> describes the response of a particle of <electric charge> $q$ and <velocity> $\mathbf v$:
$$
\mathbf F=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B),\qquad
\frac{d\mathcal E_{\mathrm{particle}}}{dt}=q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v.
$$
The <electric field> can change the particle's <energy>. The magnetic part of the <Lorentz force> is perpendicular to <velocity>, so <magnetic forces do no work>; they can bend a trajectory. These are local interactions: a particle responds to the <electromagnetic field> at its position. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_01.html[Feynman lectures, II.1: fields and forces]{ref}.

The remarkable unification is that the same <Maxwell equations> connect <Coulomb's law>, <Faraday's law of induction>, and freely propagating <electromagnetic waves>. A changing <electric field> contributes to magnetic circulation through <displacement current>; together with <Faraday's law>, this permits waves in vacuum without conducting matter. The <Poynting theorem> then explains how the fields store and transport <energy>. This connects the force on a charge to the energy carried by light.

In his 1865 paper, <James Clerk Maxwell> derived transverse disturbances and compared the propagation speed inferred from electrical measurements with independently measured light speeds. Part VI, especially §§95–97, argues that their agreement supports identifying light as an <electromagnetic wave>. This was a comparison with finite experimental accuracy, rather than a numerical identity between the historical measurements. https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rstl.1865.0008[Maxwell, A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field, Philosophical Transactions 155 (1865), 459–512]{ref}; https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/A_Dynamical_Theory_of_the_Electromagnetic_Field/Part_VI[transcription of Maxwell's original Part VI]{ref}.

= Ørsted's experiment
{c}
{parent=Electromagnetism}

A nearby compass needle changes direction when a wire carries an <electric current>. Reversing the current or exchanging above/below positions reverses the magnetic action, connecting <electric currents> with <magnetic fields>. The long-wire idealization has $B=\mu_0 I/(2\pi r)$ and circular orientation given by the <right-hand rule>.

= Coil
{synonym}

An <electromagnetic coil> is a wound <electrical conductor> whose turns add their <magnetic fields> and <magnetic flux linkages>. For closely spaced equivalent turns carrying the same <electric current>, the <magnetic field> is the single-turn result multiplied by the number of turns.

= Quasistatic electromagnetic approximation
{parent=Electromagnetism}

A <quasistatic electromagnetic approximation> neglects propagation delay over a vacuum device of characteristic size $L$ when $\omega L/c\ll1$. It does not automatically discard every time <derivative>: an <Ampere-Maxwell law> calculation in a capacitor gap must retain <displacement current>. In a medium use the relevant propagation and response scales. The dominant approximately static <electric field> can be used to calculate a smaller induced <magnetic field>, provided the corrections to the dominant field remain small. Near a resonance or for a large distributed circuit, the size criterion alone may not justify an instantaneous lumped model.

= Dielectrophoresis
{parent=Electromagnetism}

Motion driven by the force on induced polarization in a nonuniform electric field. In the dipole regime its sign depends on the particle–medium polarizability contrast and its magnitude scales with particle volume.

= Clausius-Mossotti factor
{parent=Dielectrophoresis}
{c}
{title2=$\widetilde K(\omega)$}

The spherical-particle polarization-contrast factor $\widetilde K=(\widetilde\epsilon_p-\widetilde\epsilon_m)/(\widetilde\epsilon_p+2\widetilde\epsilon_m)$. Its real part controls ordinary time-averaged <dielectrophoresis> in the quasistatic dipole approximation.

<Electromagnetic reciprocity> relates the response obtained after exchanging source and receiver in a linear reciprocal medium. For suitably normalized ports it gives a symmetric scattering matrix. = Electromagnetic induction
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{wiki}

<Electromagnetic induction> includes an induced <electromotive force> associated with a changing <magnetic field> or motion of a conductor in a <magnetic field>. <Faraday's law of induction> and <motional electromotive force> specify the relevant contributions.

= Homopolar generator
{parent=Electromagnetic induction}
{title2=$\mathcal E=\omega Ba^2/2$}

For a conducting disc of radius $a$ rotating in a uniform axial <magnetic field> $B$, the shaft-to-rim <motional electromotive force> is $\int_0^a\omega rB\,dr=\omega Ba^2/2$. A current $I$ between shaft and rim gives the opposing <torque> $IBa^2/2$, making electromagnetic conversion <power> $\mathcal E I$ equal to mechanical <torque> times <angular speed>.

= Magnetic braking
{parent=Electromagnetic induction}

Braking caused by induced currents whose magnetic <force> opposes motion. <energy> removed from mechanical motion can become resistive <heat>; the <force> depends on magnetic-field gradients or boundary crossing, resistance and <inductance>.

= Resistive magnetic entry of a falling loop
{parent=Magnetic braking}
{title2=$\tau=mR/(Ba)^2$}

A rectangular loop entering a uniform <magnetic field> while only one horizontal segment is immersed has <motional electromotive force> $Bav$. Neglecting <self-inductance>, its <electric current> is $Bav/R$ and its upward <magnetic force> is $(Ba)^2v/R$. The equation $m\dot v=mg-(Ba)^2v/R$ gives exponential relaxation toward $mgR/(Ba)^2$. This entry model ends when both horizontal segments enter the field; then their net magnetic force and the rate of change of external <magnetic flux> vanish.

= Rotational magnetic damping of a square wire loop
{parent=Magnetic braking}

A thin square loop rotating about a central in-plane axis has $\mathcal I=ma^2/6$. With ohmic current and decay slow compared with a rotation, the averaged torque is $-B^2a^4\omega/(2R)$. Its angular-speed decay time is $mR/(3B^2a^2)$; its kinetic-energy decay time is half that value.

= Translational magnetic damping of a square wire loop
{parent=Magnetic braking}

A square wire loop partly in a uniform field has linear drag $B^2a^2v/R$, giving velocity-relaxation time $\tau_t=mR/(B^2a^2)$. For uniform wire of mass <density> $\rho_m$ and resistivity $\rho_e$, $\tau_t=16\rho_m\rho_e/B^2$, independent of wire dimensions.

= Electrical and mechanical time scales in magnetic braking
{parent=Magnetic braking}

The instantaneous-current approximation uses $I=\mathcal E/R$ and requires the electrical relaxation time $L/R$ to be negligible relative to the relevant mechanical changes. Otherwise circuit self-inductance changes the braking transient.

= Loop crossing a magnetic field boundary
{parent=Magnetic braking}

A square resistive loop of side $a$, moving normally across a sharp field edge at <speed> $v$, has induced current magnitude $Bav/R$ and drag magnitude $B^2a^2v/R$ while partially overlapping the field. Completely enclosed in a uniform static field it has no changing flux and, with neglected <self-inductance>, no current.

= Impulse of magnetic braking
{parent=Loop crossing a magnetic field boundary}

For a complete entry and exit of a small square loop, integrating the drag over two <distances> $a$ gives <impulse> magnitude $2B^2a^3/R$. This is independent of <speed> in the ideal sharp-edge, zero-inductance model, though dissipated <energy> is not.

= Inductive impulse outside a switched solenoid
{parent=Electromagnetic induction}

Changing the <magnetic flux> through a long <solenoid> produces an exterior azimuthal <electric field> $E_\phi(r,t)=-\dot\Phi(t)/(2\pi r)$ by <Faraday's law of induction>. A charge $q$ whose displacement is negligible during the switching receives mechanical <momentum>
$$
\Delta\mathbf p=-\frac{q\,\Delta\Phi}{2\pi r}\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}.
$$
The result assumes the usual quasistatic field approximation and neglects the particle's displacement while the pulse acts. It describes an <electromagnetic induction> effect, distinct from the static <Aharonov-Bohm effect>.

= Mutual induction
{parent=Electromagnetic induction}

<Mutual induction> is <electromagnetic induction> in one <electrical circuit> associated with a changing <electric current> in another. In a fixed linear arrangement, <mutual inductance> quantifies the coupling.

= Lorenz-gauge reduction of the electromagnetic action
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{c}

The <electromagnetic field tensor> action differs from the component-gradient potential action by $(2\mu_0)^{-1}\int \partial^bA^a\partial_aA_b\,d^4x$. Integration by parts expresses this term as a boundary integral plus $(2\mu_0)^{-1}\int(\partial_aA^a)^2\,d^4x$. With the <Lorenz gauge> and fixed or vanishing boundary data, the two actions therefore differ only by a boundary term. Varying the component-gradient action without restriction gives the potential wave equations; the Lorenz condition must be supplied to recover all <Maxwell equations>.

= Volume conductor
{parent=Bioelectromagnetism}

A <volume conductor> is a conducting three-dimensional medium through which <electric currents> flow. In the quasistatic bioelectric model, the total <current density> is $\mathbf J=\mathbf J^p-\sigma\nabla u$, comprising an impressed source current and its Ohmic return current. <Electric charge conservation> gives $\nabla\cdot(\sigma\nabla u)=\nabla\cdot\mathbf J^p$. An insulating boundary has zero normal total current.

= Geselowitz formula
{c}
{parent=Volume conductor}
{title2=$\mathbf B=\mathbf B^p-\frac{\mu_0\sigma}{4\pi}\int_S u\,\mathbf n'\times\frac{\mathbf r-\mathbf r'}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|^3}\,dS'$}

For a homogeneous <volume conductor> with an insulating boundary $S$, the <Geselowitz formula> expresses the exterior <magnetic field> as the primary <Biot-Savart law> field minus a surface contribution from the induced <electric potential>. For a <current dipole>, $\mathbf B^p=\mu_0\mathbf Q\times(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0)/(4\pi|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0|^3)$. On a spherical boundary, $\mathbf n'$ is parallel to $\mathbf r'$, so the surface term has zero radial component at the observation point.

= Spherical volume conductor model
{parent=Volume conductor}
{title2=$|\mathbf r|<a$}

A <spherical volume conductor model> approximates a conducting body by a sphere, usually with uniform or radially stratified conductivity. It provides explicit forward models for <electroencephalography> and <magnetoencephalography>. Its symmetry makes a <radial current dipole> magnetically silent outside the sphere; departures from spherical symmetry need not retain that property.

= Sarvas formula
{c}
{parent=Spherical volume conductor model}
{title2=$\mathbf B=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\nabla\frac{(\mathbf Q\times\mathbf r_0)\cdot\mathbf r}{F}$}

For the exterior of a <spherical volume conductor model>, put $P=|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0|$, $r=|\mathbf r|$ and $F=rP^2+P\mathbf r\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0)$. The displayed <Sarvas formula> follows by taking the radial component of the <Geselowitz formula> and integrating the <magnetic scalar potential> from infinity. It is independent of a spherically symmetric conductivity profile. The sign shown uses $\mathbf B=+\mu_0\nabla U/(4\pi)$; conventions defining a magnetic potential with a minus sign reverse $U$. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3823129/[Sarvas's original paper] derives the spherical-conductor field and its consequences for the inverse problem.

= Spherical current-dipole logarithmic kernel
{parent=Sarvas formula}
{title2=$\phi=\frac1b\log\frac{r+P+b}{r+P-b},\quad b=|\mathbf r_0|$}

For $r>b$ and $P=|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0|$, the displayed kernel satisfies $\phi=\int_r^\infty ds/[s|s\widehat{\mathbf r}-\mathbf r_0|]$. The limit at $b=0$ is $1/r$. Differentiating along $\mathbf Q\times\mathbf r_0$, which preserves $b$, gives $(\mathbf Q\times\mathbf r_0)\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf r_0}\phi=(\mathbf Q\times\mathbf r_0)\cdot\mathbf r/F$. Thus it generates the normalized <magnetic scalar potential> in the <Sarvas formula>.

= Maxwell–Chern–Simons one-form equation
{c}
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{title2=$d{*F}+kF=0$}

For a <differential one-form> $A$ on an oriented three-dimensional spacetime, varying $\frac12\int(F\wedge*F+kA\wedge F)$, with $F=dA$, gives $d*F+kF=0$. With signature $(-,+,+)$, ${*}^2=-1$ on one- and two-forms; writing $B=*F$ gives $dB-k*B=0$ and the identity $d*B=0$. An Abelian <gauge transformation> changes the action by $\frac k2\int_{\partial M}\Lambda F$. Thus the bulk field equation is invariant, while invariance of the integrated action depends on boundary conditions.

= Capacitor
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{wiki}

A <capacitor> stores <electrostatic energy> by separating <electric charge> between two <electrical conductors>. In the ideal two-terminal model their <electric charges> are $+Q$ and $-Q$, with a vacuum or insulating gap preventing direct transfer between them. Write $V=\phi_+-\phi_-$ for the terminal <electric potential difference>, where $\phi_+$ and $\phi_-$ are the <electric potentials> of the two <electrical conductors>. A linear <capacitor> obeys
$$
Q=CV,
$$
where its <capacitance> $C$ depends on the geometry and <permittivity>, and is independent of the applied <electric potential difference> while these stay fixed. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/8-1-capacitors-and-capacitance[OpenStax University Physics 2, §8.1: capacitors and capacitance]{ref}.

For example, the <parallel-plate capacitor> below has <capacitance> $C=88.54\,\mathrm{pF}$. At terminal <electric potential difference> $V=10\,\mathrm V$, the <electric charge> magnitude on either plate is
$$
Q=CV=(8.854\times10^{-11})(10)\,\mathrm C
=8.854\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm C=0.8854\,\mathrm{nC}.
$$
The total <electric charge> of the device is $Q+(-Q)=0$: <charge neutrality> does not imply zero <capacitor energy>. This <capacitor> stores $4.427\,\mathrm{nJ}$ in its <electric field>. The schematic in <parallel-plate capacitor> shows the separation of <electric charge>; the plots in <capacitor energy> show how storage grows with terminal <electric potential difference>.

The useful distinction is between storing net <electric charge> and storing separated <electric charge>. A <capacitor> can release <electrostatic energy> into an <electrical circuit>; in an ideal <RLC circuit>, <energy> moves between the <capacitor>'s <electric field> and the <magnetic field> associated with <inductance>. An ideal disconnected <capacitor> retains its separated <electric charges> and <capacitor energy> because there is no path for discharge.

= RC charging circuit
{c}
{parent=Capacitor}
{title2=$\tau=RC$}

An ideal constant-voltage source $V$ charging an initially uncharged <capacitor> through a series <resistor> gives $Q(t)=CV(1-e^{-t/(RC)})$ and $I(t)=(V/R)e^{-t/(RC)}$. These follow from <Kirchhoff's voltage law> and $I=dQ/dt$. A smaller <time constant> means faster fractional settling; it does not fix the initial current or final charge unless the separate values of $R$ and $C$ are specified.

= Capacitor bridging a divider and a return resistor
{parent=Capacitor}
{title2=$V_{\rm th}=2V/3,\quad R_{\rm th}=5R/3$}

Feed a node through resistance $R$ from an ideal voltage $V$, connect that node to ground through $2R$, and connect a capacitor from it to a second node grounded through $R$. The capacitor sees a <Thévenin equivalent> with open-circuit voltage $2V/3$ and resistance $(R\parallel2R)+R=5R/3$. For zero initial capacitor voltage, $I_C=2V e^{-t/\tau}/(5R)$ and $\tau=5RC/3$. The $2R$ branch current is $V/(3R)-I_C/3$, so the two branch currents coincide at $t=\tau\log(8/5)$. This illustrates why the source-side resistor alone does not determine a charging time constant.

= Capacitor current
{parent=Capacitor}
{title2=$I=C\,dV/dt$}

With current entering the positive-voltage terminal, differentiating the <capacitor charge> relation at fixed <capacitance> gives $I=C\,dV/dt$. A changing voltage drives current through the external circuit while a changing <electric field> supplies <displacement current> across an ideal vacuum gap.

= Capacitor charge
{parent=Capacitor}
{title2=$Q=CV$}

For an ideal linear <capacitor>, the <electric charge> on the plate at positive <electric potential difference> is $Q=CV$, where $C$ is its <capacitance>. The opposite plate carries $-Q$. An initial charge supplies the integration constant in an <op amp integrator>.

= Capacitor voltage
{parent=Capacitor}
{title2=$V_C=q/C$}

The <voltage> across an ideal linear <capacitor> of constant <capacitance> C is its signed plate charge divided by C. With the passive current convention, $I=C\dot V_C$.

= Capacitor voltage continuity
{parent=Capacitor voltage}
{title2=$\Delta V_C=C^{-1}\int I\,dt$}

Integrating the current-voltage relation shows that a finite <electric current> cannot change <capacitor voltage> instantaneously. An imposed impulsive current can make it jump. A prescribed instantaneous short-circuit discharge is an idealization of this exceptional fast process, not a counterexample for finite current.

= Parallel-plate capacitor
{parent=Capacitor}
{title2=$C=\epsilon A/d$}

A <parallel-plate capacitor> consists of two parallel <electrical conductors> with overlapping <area> $A$ and separation $d$. If the gap is small compared with both plate widths and is filled by a uniform material of positive <permittivity> $\epsilon$, its <capacitance> is approximately
$$
C\simeq\frac{\epsilon A}{d}.
$$
This neglects the <fringing field of a capacitor> near the plate edges. The interior <electric field> points from the plate of positive <electric charge> to the plate of negative <electric charge>, and is approximately uniform. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/8-1-capacitors-and-capacitance[OpenStax University Physics 2, §8.1: the parallel-plate approximation]{ref}.

For a worked vacuum example, stipulate $A=0.01\,\mathrm{m^2}$, $d=0.001\,\mathrm m$, and <vacuum permittivity> $\epsilon_0\approx8.854\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{F/m}$. This rounds the value in <Fundamental Physical Constants — Complete Listing by National Institute of Standards and Technology and others>. Within the approximation,
$$
C\simeq\frac{(8.854\times10^{-12})(0.01)}{0.001}\,\mathrm F
=8.854\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm F=88.54\,\mathrm{pF}.
$$
At terminal <electric potential difference> $V=10\,\mathrm V$, the <electric field> magnitude is $E\simeq V/d=10^4\,\mathrm{V/m}$, the plate <electric charge> magnitude is $Q=CV=0.8854\,\mathrm{nC}$, and the <capacitor energy> is $U=CV^2/2=4.427\,\mathrm{nJ}$. These numbers use the stipulated rounded <vacuum permittivity>; finite plates also have corrections from the <fringing field of a capacitor>.

{title=Uniform <electric field> in a <parallel-plate capacitor>}

The formula makes design changes easy to predict: doubling the overlapping <area> doubles the <capacitance>, and halving the gap doubles the <capacitance>, with <permittivity> fixed and the <fringing field of a capacitor> neglected. Doubling <permittivity> also doubles <capacitance> if the material uniformly fills the gap and remains linear. This lets a compact device store more separated <electric charge> at the same terminal <electric potential difference>.

The short derivation uses <surface charge density> $\sigma=Q/A$. In the interior, <Gauss's law> gives <electric field> magnitude $E\simeq\sigma/\epsilon=Q/(\epsilon A)$. The <electric potential difference> is $V\simeq Ed=Qd/(\epsilon A)$, so dividing $Q$ by $V$ gives $C\simeq\epsilon A/d$. A narrow curved <capacitor> gap has the same leading local approximation when its width is much less than the curvature radius.

= Tilted parallel-plate capacitor at fixed charge
{parent=Parallel-plate capacitor}
{title2=$C=\epsilon_0s^2\log[(a+\delta)/(a-\delta)]/(2\delta)$}

For square plates with a linearly varying gap from $a-\delta$ to $a+\delta$, the local-strip approximation gives the displayed <capacitance> for $|\delta|<a$. Its expansion is $C=(\epsilon_0s^2/a)[1+\delta^2/(3a^2)+O(\delta^4/a^4)]$. An isolated capacitor has fixed <electric charge>, so $U=Q^2/(2C)$ decreases as $|\delta|$ increases. A sinusoidal small tilt therefore produces a leading energy variation at twice the tilt frequency.

= Space-charge-filled parallel-plate capacitor
{parent=Parallel-plate capacitor}
{title2=$V''=-\rho/\epsilon_0$}

Between large plates at prescribed <electric potentials> and separation $d$, fixed uniform <charge density> $\rho$ gives $V(x)=V_1+(V_2-V_1)x/d+\rho x(d-x)/(2\epsilon_0)$ by <Poisson's equation>. The signed <electric field> is linear. Its magnitude can have an interior V-shaped minimum, so a sketch of signed field alone need not answer a request for field magnitude.

= Fringing field of a capacitor
{parent=Parallel-plate capacitor}

= Capacitor fringing field
{synonym}

The <fringing field of a capacitor> is the nonuniform <electric field> near the edges of its <electrical conductors>, extending outside the gap. For a <parallel-plate capacitor>, neglecting it is useful when the gap is much smaller than both plate widths; the approximation $C\simeq\epsilon A/d$ is less reliable when these lengths become comparable.

Finite plates break the translational <symmetry> of the infinite-plate model. Each <conductor in electrostatic equilibrium> is an <equipotential>, so the <electric field> must meet a smooth conducting surface normally. Near an edge, the <equipotentials> curve into the surrounding space and the <electric field> changes both direction and magnitude. The <surface charge density> adjusts to enforce the conducting <boundary conditions>; it is not uniform all the way to a sharp edge. The <electrostatic boundary conditions at a conductor> explain why zero <electric field> inside the metal does not imply zero <electric field> just outside it.

The practical issue is where the nearly uniform region ends. A probe near the centre can support $E\simeq V/d$ while a probe close to an edge detects a lateral component and a different magnitude. A total <capacitance> reading also includes <electric flux> outside the simple footprint. The <fringing field of a capacitor> can therefore matter in <permittivity> measurements even when a central <electric field> measurement looks nearly ideal. Keysight's three-<electrode> fixture diverts the fringe contribution away from the measured response.<Materials Measurement: Dielectric Materials>[Solution: fringe capacitance and the three-electrode 16451B fixture]{ref}

= Uniform-charge estimate at a capacitor corner
{parent=Fringing field of a capacitor}
{title2=$|E_n|\simeq V/(4d)$}

Four uniform square charge-sheet pairs, rotated around a common corner, contribute equal normal fields there. Their <superposition> is a large central plate pair, so each contributes one quarter of its field. This gives $|E_n|\simeq\sigma/(4\epsilon_0)\simeq V/(4d)$ when the bulk charge-voltage relation is used. It is an approximation for prescribed uniform sheets, not an exact equipotential-conductor result. Reflection in the mid-plane does give zero tangential field for either model.

= Capacitor gap aspect ratio
{parent=Fringing field of a capacitor}
{title2=$\eta=d/W$}

The <capacitor gap aspect ratio> compares the gap $d$ with a lateral plate width $W$. For rectangular plates of widths $W_x,W_z$, require both $d/W_x\ll1$ and $d/W_z\ll1$ for the leading <parallel-plate capacitor> approximation. Large <area> alone is insufficient: a long narrow strip can have large <area> and still have $d$ comparable with its smaller width. In addition, the observation point must be many gap widths from an edge. Reducing $d/W$ improves the interior approximation without making the edge itself disappear.

In a two-dimensional straight gap, write the <electric potential> as the infinite-plate solution plus a perturbation:
$$
\phi(x,y)=\frac{V}{d}y+\delta\phi(x,y),\qquad -\frac d2<y<\frac d2.
$$
The perturbation satisfies the <Laplace equation> and vanishes on the two ideal plates. <Separation of variables> in a semi-infinite strip, with $s$ the distance inward from its entrance, gives transverse modes
$$
\delta\phi_n(s,y)=a_n e^{-n\pi s/d}\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi(y+d/2)}{d}\right),\qquad n=1,2,\ldots.
$$
Thus edge disturbances have a natural penetration scale set by $d$. Modes forbidden by a particular <symmetry> have zero coefficients; this expression is an explanation of localization in an ideal strip, not a universal numerical correction to finite-plate <capacitance>. The outside <boundary conditions> determine the coefficients.

There is no single aspect-ratio cutoff guaranteeing an assigned accuracy: the requested observable, plate shape and thickness, surroundings, <dielectric> distribution, and guard geometry all matter. The small-gap approximation is local and is not uniform across an edge. It also does not justify extrapolating $C\simeq\epsilon A/d$ to arbitrarily large $d$, where finite <electrical conductors> and their external <electric field> dominate. At fixed $V$, formally shrinking an ideal gap makes the interior <electric field> grow like $1/d$; real <electric currents> across an imperfectly insulating gap, surface roughness and contact eventually invalidate that ideal limit.

= Bounded two-dimensional capacitor field model

= Concentric-sphere field grading
{parent=Spherical capacitor}
{title2=$V_2^*=\alpha V_1/(\alpha+\beta)$}

A biased intermediate concentric conductor redistributes the potential drop between two gaps. For radii $a<c<b$ and boundary voltages $V_1,V_2,0$, the peak gap fields are $\alpha|V_1-V_2|$ and $\beta|V_2|$, where $\alpha=c/[a(c-a)]$ and $\beta=b/[c(b-c)]$. = Capacitor energy
{title2=$U=Q^2/(2C)$}
{parent=Capacitor}

The <capacitor energy> is the <electrostatic energy> stored when <electric charge> is separated. For a linear <capacitor> of fixed positive <capacitance> $C$, plate <electric charge> magnitude $Q$, and terminal <electric potential difference> $V=Q/C$, it is
$$
U=\frac{Q^2}{2C}=\frac12CV^2=\frac12QV.
$$
The zero is the uncharged state. Thus the <capacitor energy> is positive for nonzero $Q$, even though the total <electric charge> of the two plates is zero. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/8-3-energy-stored-in-a-capacitor[OpenStax University Physics 2, §8.3: energy stored in a capacitor]{ref}.

For the <parallel-plate capacitor> example, <capacitance> $C=8.854\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm F$ and terminal <electric potential difference> $V=10\,\mathrm V$ give
$$
U=\frac12(8.854\times10^{-11})(10)^2\,\mathrm J
=4.427\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm J=4.427\,\mathrm{nJ}.
$$
The plate <electric charge> magnitude is $Q=0.8854\,\mathrm{nC}$. Doubling terminal <electric potential difference> with <capacitance> unchanged doubles the plate <electric charge> magnitude and quadruples the <capacitor energy>.

\Image[capacitor-charge-and-energy.png]
{title=Linear <electric charge> and quadratic <capacitor energy> versus <electric potential difference>}

The <electric charge> is a <linear function> of terminal <electric potential difference>, while <capacitor energy> is a <quadratic function>. The second curve in each plot uses twice the <capacitance>: at a given terminal <electric potential difference>, both plate <electric charge> magnitude and <capacitor energy> double. These relations let us calculate how much <energy> an <electrical circuit> can receive when a <capacitor> discharges.

A brief charging argument explains the factor $1/2$. If the plate <electric charge> magnitude has reached $q$, the terminal <electric potential difference> is $q/C$. Transferring a further amount $dq$ of <electric charge> quasistatically requires <work> $dW=(q/C)\,dq$. With the geometry and <permittivity> fixed, the <integral> is
$$
U=\int_0^Q\frac qC\,dq=\frac{Q^2}{2C}.
$$
Using $Q=CV$ gives the other two forms. This <integral> accounts for the gradually rising terminal <electric potential difference>; it is the <work> stored in the <capacitor>'s <electric field>, rather than a claim that all <energy> drawn from a fixed-voltage <battery> during charging ends up stored.

The field picture gives the same result. In the vacuum gap of a <parallel-plate capacitor>, the <electric field> has <energy density> $u=\epsilon_0 E^2/2$. Multiplying by the gap <volume> $Ad$ gives the <electrostatic energy>
$$
U\simeq\frac12\epsilon_0E^2Ad
=\frac12\frac{\epsilon_0 A}{d}V^2
=\frac12CV^2.
$$
For the worked example, $u=4.427\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{J/m^3}$ and $Ad=10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^3}$ reproduce $U=4.427\,\mathrm{nJ}$. The <electrostatic energy> resides in the <electric field> throughout the gap, despite overall <charge neutrality>.

When <capacitance> changes, specify the constraint. At fixed plate <electric charge> magnitude $Q$, increasing <capacitance> decreases <capacitor energy> $Q^2/(2C)$; doubling <capacitance> halves it. At fixed terminal <electric potential difference> $V$, increasing <capacitance> increases <capacitor energy> $CV^2/2$; doubling <capacitance> doubles it. In the latter case a connected <battery> supplies additional <electric charge> and <energy>, and there may also be mechanical <work>. Use the <fixed-voltage capacitor energy balance> to account for all transfers before inferring a <force> from changing <capacitor energy>.

= Fixed-voltage capacitor energy balance
{parent=Capacitor energy}
{title2=$dU_{\rm field}=dW_{\rm mech}+V\,dQ$}

For a quasistatic change of <capacitance> at fixed terminal <electric potential difference>, the <capacitor energy> is $U=CV^2/2$ and plate <electric charge> is $Q=CV$. Electrical <work> supplied to the <electric field> by the <battery> is $V\,dQ$, so mechanical <work> done on the <capacitor> is $dW_{\rm mech}=dU-V\,dQ=-V^2dC/2$. Increasing the separation of a <parallel-plate capacitor> makes $dC<0$: mechanical <work> is positive even though <electrostatic energy> decreases, because electrical <energy> returns to the source. Conversely, when $dC>0$, the <battery> supplies $V^2dC$: half increases <capacitor energy> and half is available as mechanical <work> done by the <electric field>. Differentiating <capacitor energy> alone at fixed terminal <electric potential difference> omits this exchange with the <battery>.

= Capacitance
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{title2=$C$}
{wiki}

The <capacitance> of a linear two-terminal <capacitor> is the constant relating its separated <electric charge> to its terminal <electric potential difference>:
$$
Q=CV,\qquad C=\frac QV\quad(V\ne0).
$$
Here $Q$ is the <electric charge> on one <electrical conductor>, the other carries $-Q$, and $V$ is their <electric potential difference> with the same ordering. Reversing the terminal ordering reverses both $Q$ and $V$ and leaves <capacitance> unchanged. An ideal passive <capacitor> has $C\ge0$; an ordinary pair of separated <electrical conductors> with positive <permittivity> has $C>0$. At $V=0$, use $Q=CV$ rather than the undefined ratio $0/0$.

For example, <electric charge> magnitude $Q=0.8854\,\mathrm{nC}$ at terminal <electric potential difference> $V=10\,\mathrm V$ gives <capacitance> $C=88.54\,\mathrm{pF}$. At $20\,\mathrm V$, the same linear <capacitor> has $Q=1.7708\,\mathrm{nC}$ and the same <capacitance>. The plots in <capacitor energy> show that <capacitance> is the <slope> of the <electric charge> versus terminal <electric potential difference> graph.

For fixed geometry and linear material response, <capacitance> depends on dimensions and <permittivity>, rather than the source's terminal <electric potential difference>. This is why <capacitance> is a useful property of a component: once it is known, $Q=CV$ predicts the separated <electric charge> for each operating terminal <electric potential difference>. The <SI> unit is the <farad>, $1\,\mathrm F=1\,\mathrm{C/V}$. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/8-1-capacitors-and-capacitance[OpenStax University Physics 2, §8.1: capacitance and its unit]{ref}.

For combinations of <capacitors>, the same terminal definition gives an equivalent <capacitance>. Two identical <capacitors> of <capacitance> $C$ connected in parallel share the same terminal <electric potential difference>, and their <electric charges> add, giving $C_{\rm eq}=2C$. Connected in series with an initially neutral isolated junction, they carry equal plate <electric charge> magnitudes and their terminal <electric potential differences> add, giving $C_{\rm eq}=C/2$. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/8-2-capacitors-in-series-and-in-parallel[OpenStax University Physics 2, §8.2: capacitors in series and in parallel]{ref}.

= Arithmetic-harmonic bounds for dielectric capacitance
{parent=Capacitance}
{title2=$\langle\varepsilon^{-1}\rangle^{-1}A/d\leq C\leq\langle\varepsilon\rangle A/d$}

For a <parallel-plate capacitor> of area $A$ and separation $d$, filled by a spatially varying positive scalar <permittivity>, neglect edge fields. At fixed terminal voltage, the physical <electric potential> minimizes $\int\varepsilon|\nabla V|^2\,d\mathcal V/2$. A linear trial potential gives the arithmetic upper bound. = Sphere-plane capacitance
{parent=Capacitance}
{title2=$C(R,h)$}

For a conducting sphere of radius $R$ at center height $h>R$ above a grounded infinite plane,
$$
C(R,h)=4\pi\epsilon R\sinh\alpha\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{\sinh(n\alpha)},
\qquad\cosh\alpha=\frac hR.
$$
The distance $h$ is measured to the center, so the surface gap is $h-R$. Relative to the <capacitance of an isolated conducting sphere>, the enhancement is $c(R/h)=C(R,h)/(4\pi\epsilon R)$.

The <method of images> gives a constructive proof. At fixed sphere potential $V_s$, start with $q_0=4\pi\epsilon RV_s$ at height $z_0=h$ and place its opposite image below the plane. Cancel that image's nonconstant sphere potential by the successive charges
$$
q_{n+1}=\frac R{h+z_n}q_n,
\qquad z_{n+1}=h-\frac{R^2}{h+z_n},
$$
with opposite mirror charges below the plane at every step. The resulting sphere potential is $V_s$ and the plane potential is zero. Since $q_n=4\pi\epsilon RV_s\sinh\alpha/\sinh((n+1)\alpha)$, summing the sphere charges gives the displayed <capacitance>. In particular $c(y)=1+y/2+O(y^2)$ for small $y$.

The exact sphere-plane boundary-value problem is also treated in https://arxiv.org/abs/1206.6034[Behunin and colleagues' analysis of sphere-plane electrostatics].

= Inductance
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{title2=$L$}
{wiki}

<Inductance> relates <electric currents> to the <magnetic flux linkage> they produce. For a single linear coil with fixed geometry and magnetic response, its <self-inductance> $L$ is the constant in
$$
\Lambda=LI.
$$
Here $\Lambda$ is <magnetic flux linkage> from the coil's own <electric current> $I$. The <SI> unit of <inductance> is the <henry (unit)>. Knowing <inductance> lets us predict the induced <electromotive force> when <electric current> changes, and the <magnetic energy> stored by an <inductor>. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/14-2-self-inductance-and-inductors[OpenStax University Physics 2, §14.2: self-inductance and inductors]{ref}.

For example, a coil of <self-inductance> $0.5\,\mathrm H$ carrying <electric current> $3\,\mathrm A$ has <magnetic flux linkage> $1.5\,\mathrm{Wb}$. If its $100$ turns link the same <magnetic flux>, each turn has <magnetic flux> $0.015\,\mathrm{Wb}$. Keeping the geometry and magnetic response fixed while doubling <electric current> doubles <magnetic flux linkage> and quadruples the <inductor>'s <magnetic energy>.

The comparison with <capacitance> is useful: a linear <capacitor> has $Q=CV$ and stores <capacitor energy> $CV^2/2$ in its <electric field>, whereas a linear <inductor> has $\Lambda=LI$ and stores <magnetic energy> $LI^2/2$ in its <magnetic field>. The <RLC circuit> brings both forms of <energy> storage together.

Integrating the <magnetic flux density> across its section gives $\Phi=\mu NIh\ln(b/a)/(2\pi)$ and hence the displayed <self-inductance>. It retains radial field variation when $b-a$ is not small. For two fully linked windings, replace $N^2$ by $N_1N_2$ to obtain their <mutual inductance>. Discrete turns, leakage and nonlinear core response limit the ideal formula.

= Radial-path approximation for a cracked toroid
{parent=Inductance of a rectangular-section toroid}
{title2=$M_d\simeq\frac{\mu_0\mu_rN_1N_2h}{2\pi}\ln\frac{b+q}{a+q},\quad q=\frac{(\mu_r-1)d}{2\pi}$}

In this <magnetic circuit> approximation, each circular path has air-gap <length> $d$, core <length> $2\pi r-d$, and the same normal <magnetic flux density> in air and core. <Ampère's law> then gives $B(r)\simeq\mu_0\mu_rNI/[2\pi r+(\mu_r-1)d]$; integrating across the section gives the displayed <mutual inductance>. Even $d\ll a$ can strongly reduce inductance when $\mu_rd/a$ is appreciable. This is a path estimate, not an exact local field solution in the slit geometry. It neglects <magnetic fringing> and redistribution between paths. In particular, a literal field proportional to $\widehat{\boldsymbol\phi}/(r+q)$ everywhere in a current-free core is not curl-free; transverse corrections are required. Narrow-gap circuit approximations and their limitations are discussed in <Electromagnetic Fields and Energy by Hermann A. Haus and James R. Melcher/Magnetic Circuits>[§9.7, Example 9.7.1; paragraph following the example]{ref}.

= Inductance of a toroid with an oblique narrow air gap
{parent=Inductance of a toroidal winding}
{title2=$L\simeq\mu_0N^2A/[\ell/\mu+t\sin\theta]$}

Let a slender <toroid> have core cross-section $A$, mean length $\ell$ and large relative <magnetic permeability> $\mu$. Cut a narrow gap of perpendicular width $t$ whose faces make angle $\theta$ with the local core direction. Continuity of normal <magnetic flux density> gives $B_{g,n}=B\sin\theta$; continuity of tangential <magnetic field intensity> gives $H_{g,t}=B\cos\theta/(\mu\mu_0)$. The gap field is nearly normal to the faces for large $\mu$. Its effective flux area is $A/\sin\theta$, so its leading <magnetic reluctance> is $t\sin\theta/(\mu_0A)$. Adding core reluctance gives the displayed <self-inductance>. At $45^\circ$, $L\simeq\mu_0N^2A/(\ell/\mu+t/\sqrt2)$ and $H_g\simeq NI/(t+\sqrt2\ell/\mu)$. This retains $\mu t/\ell$ while neglecting fringing and corrections of relative order $t/\ell$ or $1/\mu$.

= Mutual inductance
{parent=Inductance}
{title2=$M$}

For two fixed linear coils, <mutual inductance> $M$ relates <magnetic flux linkage> in one coil to <electric current> in the other: $\Lambda_{21}=MI_1$. The induced <electromotive force> is $\mathcal E_2=-M\,dI_1/dt$ with consistent winding orientations. For reciprocal coils, $|M|=k\sqrt{L_1L_2}$, where $L_1,L_2$ are <self-inductances> and $0\le k\le1$ measures magnetic coupling. Its <SI> unit is the <henry (unit)>.

= Mutual inductance of a straight wire and square coil
{parent=Mutual inductance}
{title2=$\mathcal M=\frac{\mu_0Na}{2\pi}\ln\frac{s+a}{s}$}

An N-turn square coil of side a lies in the plane of a long straight wire, with nearest edge at perpendicular distance s and parallel to the wire. Integrating $B=\mu_0I/(2\pi r)$ across the coil gives flux-linkage magnitude $\Lambda=\mu_0NaI\ln[(s+a)/s]/(2\pi)$. Its coefficient of I is the <mutual inductance> magnitude; its sign depends on the selected loop normal and current directions. <Flux> through one turn and N-turn <flux> linkage must be distinguished.

= Force on a resistive coil beside an alternating wire
{parent=Mutual inductance of a straight wire and square coil}

In a fixed square coil beside a long wire, neglect <self-inductance> compared with resistance. A changing wire current induces coil current proportional to $dI/dt$. <Forces> on the two perpendicular sides cancel, and the unequal parallel-side fields give magnitude $\mu_0Na|Ii|[1/s-1/(s+a)]/(2\pi)$. When $I=I_0\sin\omega t$, the signed <force> alternates at twice the drive <frequency> and has zero cycle mean, while Joule <dissipation> is positive with mean $\mathcal M^2\omega^2I_0^2/(2R)$. A fixed coil has no mechanical work despite its time-dependent <force>.

= Alternating-current induction levitation
{parent=Mutual inductance}

A resistive loop with <self-inductance> $L_r$ coupled by <mutual inductance> $M(z)$ to an imposed <alternating current> obeys $L_r\dot I_r+RI_r=-M\dot I_s$. For $I_s=I_0\cos\omega t$, averaging the magnetic <force> $I_sI_rM'(z)$ gives $-I_0^2\omega^2ML_rM'/[2(R^2+\omega^2L_r^2)]$. It is repulsive where $MM'<0$. The <self-inductance> supplies the current component opposite to the source current; a purely resistive, zero-inductance loop has zero average force in this model.

= Coupled inductors
{parent=Mutual inductance}

<Coupled inductors> are <inductors> whose <magnetic flux linkages> depend on each other's <electric currents>. A two-circuit linear model uses two <self-inductances> and a <mutual inductance>.

= Inductance matrix
{parent=Coupled inductors}
{title2=$\boldsymbol\lambda=\mathbf L\mathbf i$}

An <inductance matrix> relates <magnetic flux linkages> to <electric currents> in a linear model of <coupled inductors>. For reciprocal two-coil coupling, $\mathbf L=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}L_1&M\\M&L_2\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. Its stored <magnetic energy> is $\mathbf i^T\mathbf L\mathbf i/2$. With $L_1,L_2>0$, it is a <positive-definite matrix> exactly when $M^2<L_1L_2$. Equality is a singular limiting model requiring separate treatment.

= Magnetic flux linkage
{parent=Inductance}
{title2=$\Lambda=\sum_{k=1}^N\Phi_k$}

= Flux linkage
{synonym}

<Magnetic flux linkage> adds the signed <magnetic fluxes> through the turns of a coil:
$$
\Lambda=\sum_{k=1}^N\Phi_k,\qquad
\Phi_k=\int_{S_k}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf S.
$$
Choose each <oriented surface> $S_k$ consistently with positive <electric current> around its turn using the <right-hand rule>. If all $N$ turns link approximately the same <magnetic flux> $\Phi$, then $\Lambda\approx N\Phi$; equality holds when every turn links exactly the same <magnetic flux>. Uniform <magnetic field> perpendicular to identical flat turns of <area> $A$ gives $\Phi=BA$. For a stipulated $100$-turn coil with <magnetic flux> $0.015\,\mathrm{Wb}$ per turn, <magnetic flux linkage> is $1.5\,\mathrm{Wb}$, not $0.015\,\mathrm{Wb}$. The turn count is dimensionless. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/14-2-self-inductance-and-inductors[OpenStax University Physics 2, §14.2: flux through the turns of a coil]{ref}.

=1\,\frac{\mathrm{V\,s}}{\mathrm A}.

A coil with <self-inductance> $1\,\mathrm H$ has <magnetic flux linkage> $1\,\mathrm{Wb}$ at <electric current> $1\,\mathrm A$. = Self-inductance
{parent=Inductance}
{title2=$\Lambda=LI,\quad\mathcal E_{\rm ind}=-L\dot I$}

<Self-inductance> is <inductance> associated with a coil's own <electric current>, rather than <magnetic flux> supplied by another coil. In the linear model, fixed geometry and magnetic response give constant $L$ in $\Lambda=LI$. With the turn orientations defined in <magnetic flux linkage>, <Faraday's law of induction> gives the self-induced <electromotive force>
$$
\mathcal E_{\rm ind}=-\frac{d\Lambda}{dt}=-L\frac{dI}{dt}.
$$
The final equality requires constant $L$; if the geometry changes, use $-d\Lambda/dt$. The minus sign expresses <Lenz's law>: the induced <electromotive force> opposes the change in <electric current>. It does not always oppose the <electric current> itself. When positive <electric current> decreases, the induced <electromotive force> acts in its direction. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/14-2-self-inductance-and-inductors[OpenStax University Physics 2, §14.2: self-induced emf and its direction]{ref}.

For terminal sign bookkeeping, label the ideal <inductor>'s terminals $a,b$, choose positive <electric current> entering $a$ and leaving $b$, and define $v_L$ as the terminal <electric potential difference> drop from $a$ to $b$ in the lumped <electrical circuit> model. This passive convention gives
$$
v_L=+L\frac{dI}{dt}=-\mathcal E_{\rm ind}.
$$
Thus a coil with <self-inductance> $0.5\,\mathrm H$ and increasing <electric current> $dI/dt=12\,\mathrm{A/s}$ has terminal drop $v_L=6\,\mathrm V$ and induced <electromotive force> $\mathcal E_{\rm ind}=-6\,\mathrm V$. These signs describe the same process with different reference conventions. The <RL circuit> uses the terminal-drop convention throughout.

A finite terminal <electric potential difference> cannot make the <electric current> of an ideal <inductor> with constant $L>0$ jump. Integrating its <differential equation> gives
$$
I(t_2)-I(t_1)=\frac1L\int_{t_1}^{t_2}v_L(t)\,dt.
$$
If $v_L$ stays bounded, the <integral> tends to zero as the interval shrinks, ensuring <continuity> of <electric current>. A step in terminal <electric potential difference> changes $dI/dt$ immediately, while <electric current> remains continuous. At constant <electric current>, the self-induced <electromotive force> is zero, although the <inductor>'s <magnetic energy> can remain nonzero.

= External inductance of a thin two-wire line
{parent=Self-inductance}
{title2=$L'_{\rm ext}\simeq\mu_0\ln(d/a_w)/\pi$}

Two long round wires of radius $a_w\ll d$ carrying opposite <electric currents> form a loop with the displayed leading external <self-inductance> per unit length. Integrating their summed <magnetic field> across a connecting ribbon gives the logarithm; magnetic-energy integration gives the same leading value. The zero-radius limit diverges. Uniform direct current inside two nonmagnetic round wires contributes another $\mu_0/(4\pi)$, while an ideal surface current has no interior contribution.

= Inductor
{parent=Self-inductance}
{wiki}

An <inductor> is an <electrical circuit> component designed to provide <self-inductance>, often a coil such as a <solenoid>. An ideal linear <inductor> has constant $L>0$ and zero <electrical resistance>. Its stored <magnetic energy>, with zero chosen at zero <electric current>, is
$$
U=\frac12LI^2.
$$
For <self-inductance> $0.5\,\mathrm H$ and <electric current> $3\,\mathrm A$, this gives $U=2.25\,\mathrm J$. Unlike <Joule heating>, ideal storage is recoverable: the <inductor> can return <energy> to an <electrical circuit> when its <electric current> decreases. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/14-3-energy-in-a-magnetic-field[OpenStax University Physics 2, §14.3: energy stored by an inductor]{ref}.

The short derivation uses the terminal convention in <self-inductance>. The <power> absorbed by an ideal <inductor> is
$$
P_L=v_L I=LI\frac{dI}{dt}=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12LI^2\right).
$$
Starting from zero <electric current>, the <integral> of this <power> gives
$$
U=\int_0^t P_L(t')\,dt'=\int_0^I Li\,di=\frac12LI^2.
$$
Positive $P_L$ increases stored <magnetic energy>; negative $P_L$ returns <energy>. At constant <electric current> $3\,\mathrm A$ in the example, $P_L=0$ and induced <electromotive force> is zero, but $U=2.25\,\mathrm J$ still resides in the <magnetic field>. The <battery> in the steady <RL circuit> supplies <Joule heating> in the <electrical resistance>, rather than further increasing the <inductor>'s <magnetic energy>.

A real coil also has <electrical resistance>, so its <Joule heating> must be included separately from ideal <inductor> storage. The constant-$L$ formulas assume a fixed linear magnetic response.

= Inductive transient

= Inductor current continuity

= Source-fed parallel resistor-inductor switching
{parent=Inductor}
{title2=$\tau=L/(R_1\parallel R_2)$}

An ideal <voltage source> $V_0$ and series <resistor> $R_1$ feed parallel branches $L$ and $R_2$. <Thévenin's theorem> gives $R_{\rm th}=R_1R_2/(R_1+R_2)$ and $V_{\rm th}=V_0R_2/(R_1+R_2)$. Starting with zero <inductor> current, $i_L=(V_0/R_1)(1-e^{-t/\tau})$ and $i_2=V_0e^{-t/\tau}/(R_1+R_2)$. Switch-on heat in $R_2$ is $LV_0^2/[2R_1(R_1+R_2)]$. Disconnecting the source leaves the stored <magnetic energy> $Li_L^2/2$ to dissipate through $R_2$, with <time constant> $L/R_2$.

= Energy stored in a nonlinear inductor

For a reversible device with flux linkage $\Psi$, electrical power is $I\dot\Psi$, so stored energy changes by $dU=I\,d\Psi$. This reduces to $U=LI^2/2$ only for a fixed linear <inductance>. <Hysteresis> makes energy path dependent and introduces loss.

= Depolarization field

The <electric field> produced by the <bound charge> of a polarized body is its <depolarization field>. = Depolarization factor of a dielectric ellipsoid

= Magnetization
{title2=$\mathbf M$}
{parent=Bound charge and bound current}
{wiki}

<Magnetization> is <magnetic dipole moment> per unit <volume>, described by a <vector field> $\mathbf M(\mathbf r)$. Average the microscopic <magnetic dipole moments> in a small region of <volume> $\Delta V$ around the <point> $\mathbf r$:
$$
\mathbf M(\mathbf r)\simeq\frac{1}{\Delta V}\sum_{i\in\Delta V}\mathbf m_i,
\qquad
\mathbf m_{\rm total}=\int_V\mathbf M\,dV.
$$
The averaging region should contain many microscopic constituents while being small compared with the distance over which the macroscopic <magnetization> varies. With <number density> $n$ of identical constituents, this becomes $\mathbf M=n\langle\mathbf m\rangle$. <Magnetic dipole moment> has <SI> units $\mathrm{A\,m^2}$, so <magnetization> has units $\mathrm{A/m}$: <amperes> per metre. The average is a <vector> sum, not a sum of magnitudes.<Classical Electromagnetism: An intermediate level course by Richard Fitzpatrick/Magnetization>[Classical Electromagnetism: An intermediate level course by Richard Fitzpatrick; Magnetization, equations (856)–(857) and the surrounding averaging definition]{ref}

Alignment can change <magnetization> without changing the magnitude of any individual <magnetic dipole moment>. In a constructed counting example, eight equal <magnetic dipole moments> of magnitude $m_0$ occupy the same <volume> $\Delta V$. Six along $+z$ and two along $-z$ give $\mathbf M=4m_0\widehat{\mathbf z}/\Delta V$; turning the two opposing <magnetic dipole moments> to $+z$ doubles <magnetization> to $8m_0\widehat{\mathbf z}/\Delta V$. Both arrangements have the same sum of magnitudes, $8m_0$. Changing the individual <magnetic dipole moments> provides another possible response; their alignment and their magnitudes are distinct ingredients. Microscopic <spin> contributes <magnetic dipole moment> without being a literal rotating charged ball.

\Image[magnetization-dipole-vector-averaging.png]
{title=<Magnetization> depends on the <vector> average of <magnetic dipole moments>}
{description=Original eight-<magnetic-dipole-moment> counting model, with equal arrow lengths and equal averaging <volumes>. Reorienting two arrows doubles the net <magnetic dipole moment> without enlarging any individual <magnetic dipole moment>. The small count illustrates the sum; a macroscopic averaging region contains many more constituents.}

Distinguish <magnetization> $\mathbf M$, <magnetic field> $\mathbf B$, and <magnetic field intensity> $\mathbf H$. In <SI>,
$$
\mathbf B=\mu_0(\mathbf H+\mathbf M),\qquad
[\mathbf M]=[\mathbf H]=\mathrm{A/m},\qquad [\mathbf B]=\mathrm T.
$$
Here $\mu_0$ is the <vacuum permeability>, and $\mathrm T$ denotes the <tesla (unit)>. <Magnetization> describes matter's <magnetic dipole moment> density; <magnetic field> enters the <Lorentz force>; <magnetic field intensity> separates the free <electric current> contribution in the macroscopic equations. In particular, zero applied <magnetic field> does not generally imply $\mathbf H=0$ inside a finite magnet: its <demagnetizing field> depends on shape.<Classical Electromagnetism: An intermediate level course by Richard Fitzpatrick/Magnetization>[Classical Electromagnetism: An intermediate level course by Richard Fitzpatrick; Magnetization, equations (860)–(864): current decomposition and definition of H]{ref}

For stationary <electric polarization>, <magnetization> produces <bound current> in the <volume> and on its bounding <surface>:
$$
\mathbf J_{\rm b}=\nabla\times\mathbf M,
\qquad
\mathbf K_{\rm b}=\mathbf M\times\mathbf n.
$$
The <curl> gives a <current density> in $\mathrm{A/m^2}$; $\mathbf K_{\rm b}$ is a <surface current density> in $\mathrm{A/m}$. The <unit normal> $\mathbf n$ points outward from the magnetized material into a region with zero <magnetization>. Across an interface between two magnetized media, the net bound <current sheet> is
$$
\mathbf K_{\rm interface}=(\mathbf M_1-\mathbf M_2)\times\mathbf n,
$$
where the <unit normal> points from medium 1 to medium 2. Both faces contribute: medium 2 has the opposite outward <normal vector>. This fixes the <cross product> sign. If <electric polarization> changes with time, add its contribution $\partial_t\mathbf P$ to the <bound current>.<Classical Electromagnetism by Richard Fitzpatrick/Permanent Ferromagnets>[Classical Electromagnetism by Richard Fitzpatrick; Permanent Ferromagnets, equation (716): volume and outward-surface magnetization currents]{ref}

A short proof outline explains why both contributions are needed. Write $\mathbf M_{\rm ext}=\mathbf M\,\chi_V$, where $\chi_V$ is the <indicator function> of the material's <volume>. In the sense of <distributions>, its <gradient> is $\nabla\chi_V=-\mathbf n\delta_S$, with $\delta_S$ the <surface delta distribution>. The <product rule> for the <curl> gives
$$
\nabla\times(\chi_V\mathbf M)
=\chi_V\nabla\times\mathbf M+(\mathbf M\times\mathbf n)\delta_S.
$$
Thus a constant interior <magnetization> can have zero interior <curl> and still produce a nonzero boundary <electric current>. Equivalently, neighboring ideal microscopic <current loops> cancel along shared edges while uncompensated edges remain on the outside. This <bound current> representation predicts the same macroscopic <magnetic field> as the <magnetization> distribution; it does not require a wire or free charge transport around the magnet. Its usefulness is that the familiar <Biot-Savart law> can now calculate the <magnetic field> from a specified <magnetization>.

A time-dependent average <magnetization> need not vary smoothly under a smooth drive. The <Barkhausen effect> resolves collective jumps through their induced <electromotive force>; <Barkhausen pulse area> explains how that derivative readout relates to the underlying change.

= Uniformly magnetized cylinder
{parent=Magnetization}
{title2=$\mathbf M=M\widehat{\mathbf z},\quad\mathbf K_{\rm b}=M\widehat{\boldsymbol\phi}$}

Prescribe constant <magnetization> $\mathbf M=M\widehat{\mathbf z}$, with $M>0$, inside a <circular cylinder> of <radius> $R$ and <length> $L$, surrounded by zero <magnetization>. In <cylindrical coordinates>, the outward <unit normal> on the curved <surface> is $\widehat{\mathbf r}$, so
$$
\mathbf J_{\rm b}=\mathbf0\quad\text{in the interior},\qquad
\mathbf K_{\rm b}=M\widehat{\mathbf z}\times\widehat{\mathbf r}
=M\widehat{\boldsymbol\phi}\quad\text{on the side}.
$$
The endcaps have outward <unit normals> $+\widehat{\mathbf z}$ and $-\widehat{\mathbf z}$. Their <cross products> with <magnetization> vanish, so $\mathbf K_{\rm b}=0$ on each endcap. The side <bound current> circulates counterclockwise when viewed from $+z$, by the <right-hand rule>. Zero endcap <surface current density> does not imply zero endcap <magnetic field>.

For an original example, let $M=800\,\mathrm{A/m}$, $R=0.020\,\mathrm m$, and $L=0.10\,\mathrm m$. The <volume> and total <magnetic dipole moment> are
$$
V=\pi R^2L=4\pi\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^3},\qquad
\mathbf m=MV\widehat{\mathbf z}
=0.032\pi\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}\widehat{\mathbf z}
\approx0.100531\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}\widehat{\mathbf z}.
$$
Check the result independently using the equivalent <bound current>: a side strip of axial <length> $dz$ forms a <current loop> with $dI=M\,dz$ and <magnetic dipole moment> $d\mathbf m=\pi R^2M\,dz\,\widehat{\mathbf z}$. Its <integral> over $L$ gives the same $M\pi R^2L\widehat{\mathbf z}$. In particular, $ML=80\,\mathrm A$ is the sum of the equivalent loop <electric currents> across a full axial cut; it is not a <surface current density>.

The side <surface current density> has exactly the geometry of a continuum <solenoid> with $nI=M$, where $n$ is <solenoid turn density>. In the infinite-cylinder idealization with no applied <magnetic field>, the <long-solenoid approximation> therefore becomes
$$
\mathbf B_{\rm in}=\mu_0M\widehat{\mathbf z},\qquad
\mathbf B_{\rm out}=\mathbf0,\qquad\mathbf H=\mathbf0.
$$
The <limit> is useful well away from the ends of a sufficiently long <circular cylinder>. A finite <circular cylinder> instead has fringing <magnetic field> and a <demagnetizing field>. For this prescribed uniform <magnetization>, integrate the <on-axis magnetic field of a circular current loop>, or substitute $nI=M$ in the <on-axis magnetic field of a finite solenoid>, to obtain the central <magnetic field>:
$$
B_z(0)=\mu_0M\frac{L}{\sqrt{L^2+4R^2}}.
$$
Our example has $L/(2R)=2.5$ and $B_z(0)/(\mu_0M)=5/\sqrt{29}\approx0.92848$, rather than unity. With the rounded <vacuum permeability> $\mu_0\approx4\pi\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\,m/A}$, this is approximately $0.9334\,\mathrm{mT}$, compared with the infinite-cylinder value $1.0053\,\mathrm{mT}$. These are model calculations for a prescribed <magnetization>, not measured values or a solution for the self-consistent <magnetization> of an arbitrary material.

= Vibrating-sample magnetometer
{parent=Magnetization}
{wiki}

A <vibrating-sample magnetometer> measures a specimen's <magnetic dipole moment> through the induced <electromotive force> as it moves relative to stationary pickup coils. Dividing that <magnetic dipole moment> by the specimen's independently determined <volume> gives its average <magnetization>.

In <Simon Foner>'s 1959 apparatus, the specimen was weighed and glued to a mount. A loudspeaker drive moved it perpendicular to an applied uniform <magnetic field> between magnet poles; stationary coils detected the induced <electromotive force>. A reference magnet on the same vibrating assembly generated a second coil <electromotive force>. Adjusting the reference <voltage divider> to an electrical null compared the specimen signal with this reference. The <measurement calibration> substituted a magnetically saturated pure <nickel> sphere of known <magnetic dipole moment>; a specimen-removed reading corrected the mount's contribution. The calibrated signal inferred the specimen's <magnetic dipole moment>, not its local <magnetization> map. <Measurement uncertainty> in the standard, positioning, finite specimen size, coil noise, and mechanical coupling limited the inference.<Versatile and Sensitive Vibrating-Sample Magnetometer by Simon Foner>[Versatile and Sensitive Vibrating-Sample Magnetometer, pp. 549–550, §I and Figs. 1–2; p. 553, §IV and Fig. 9; p. 554, §§V.A–B; p. 556, §§V.G and VI.A.1]{ref}

The readout follows <Faraday's law of induction>. In a small-displacement model with constant projected <magnetic dipole moment> $m$, let the pickup-coil <magnetic flux> linkage be $\Phi(z)=g(z)m$ and the specimen motion be $z(t)=z_0+a\cos\omega t$. Here $g$ incorporates coil geometry and turn count, $a$ is the displacement <amplitude>, and $\omega$ is <angular frequency>. To first order in displacement, with $g'(z_0)$ the <derivative> at the mean position,
$$
V(t)=-\frac{d\Phi}{dt}\simeq m\,g'(z_0)a\omega\sin\omega t.
$$
This is an original idealized readout relation: a larger induced <electromotive force> can mean larger <magnetic dipole moment>, greater motion, or a changed coil position. A synchronously moving reference allows common motion factors to cancel in the ratio of induced <electromotive forces>. The <measurement calibration> and specimen-removed control are therefore integral to the measurement. A single <amplitude> reading cannot by itself distinguish microscopic alignment from a change in individual <magnetic dipole moments>.

= Paramagnetism
{parent=Magnetization}
{wiki}
{title2=$\chi>0$}

In a linear isotropic regime, a paramagnetic material has positive <magnetic susceptibility>: its induced <magnetization> is aligned with the applied <magnetic field intensity>. <Molecular oxygen> supplies an example discussed through <molecular orbital theory> in the <covalent bond> article.

= Paramagnetic material
{parent=Paramagnetism}

= Paramagnet
{synonym}

A material exhibiting <paramagnetism>. Its induced <magnetization> points along the applied <magnetic field> in the linear-response regime, giving positive <magnetic susceptibility>. Unlike a <ferromagnet>, it has no spontaneous ordered moment in that phase. The inverse-temperature response of independent localized moments is one model, rather than a definition that includes all microscopic mechanisms of <paramagnetism>.

= Two-state Curie law
{parent=Paramagnetism}
{title2=$C=\mu_0n\mu^2/k_B$}

For independent magnetic moments constrained to $\mu_z=\pm\mu$, the one-site <partition function> is $2\cosh[\mu\mu_0H/(k_BT)]$. The weak-field <magnetization> is $M=\mu_0n\mu^2H/(k_BT)$, so the SI <magnetic susceptibility> obeys $\chi=C/T$ with $C=\mu_0n\mu^2/k_B$. If $\mu=gS\mu_B$, this involves $S^2$, not the unrestricted-spin factor $S(S+1)/3$. The constraint on allowed moment orientations must be specified before using a <Curie law> coefficient.

= Curie response of an arbitrary independent spin
{parent=Paramagnetism}
{c}

At fixed positive <temperature> and weak field, independent spin-$J$ moments have total moment $M=N(g\mu_B)^2J(J+1)B/(3k_BT)$. This is the <Curie law>; the corresponding <density> divides by the sample volume.

= Brillouin magnetization formula
{parent=Paramagnetism}
{c}

For independent distinguishable moments with angular <momentum> $J$, define $y=g\mu_BB/(k_BT)$. The one-site <partition function> is $Z=\sinh[(J+1/2)y]/\sinh(y/2)$. Its logarithmic derivative gives $\langle\mu_z\rangle=g\mu_B[(J+1/2)\coth((J+1/2)y)-\tfrac12\coth(y/2)]$. At $J=1/2$ this reduces to $(g\mu_B/2)\tanh(y/2)$.

= Brillouin function
{parent=Brillouin magnetization formula}
{c}
{title2=$B_J(x)$}

The <Brillouin function> gives the normalized <magnetization> of independent angular-momentum-$J$ moments. It is odd, increasing, has slope $(J+1)/(3J)$ at zero and tends to $\pm1$ at large positive or negative argument. Explicitly,
$$
B_J(x)=\frac{2J+1}{2J}\coth\!\left(\frac{2J+1}{2J}x\right)-\frac1{2J}\coth\!\left(\frac{x}{2J}\right).
$$
It follows by differentiating the logarithm of $\sum_{m=-J}^J\exp(mx/J)$, the one-ion <partition function>. Its limiting value gives <magnetic saturation>, and its initial slope gives the <Curie law>.

= Thermally activated paramagnetism
{parent=Paramagnetism}

Paramagnetism arising when thermal excitation populates a magnetic manifold above a less magnetic or nonmagnetic ground manifold. Competing thermal population and orientational randomization can produce a maximum in susceptibility at an intermediate temperature.

= Curie–Weiss law
{parent=Paramagnetism}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$\chi=C/(T-\Theta)$}

The <Curie-Weiss law> approximates the <magnetic susceptibility> of interacting localized moments by $\chi=C/(T-\Theta)$ in a suitable paramagnetic temperature range. A positive Weiss temperature often reflects predominantly ferromagnetic interactions, while a negative value often reflects antiferromagnetic interactions. It is not a universal exact law through an ordering transition, and crystal-field effects or itinerant carriers can require another description.

= Weiss temperature
{parent=Curie–Weiss law}
{c}
{title2=$\Theta$}

The fitted temperature $\Theta$ in $\chi=C/(T-\Theta)$. It summarizes the leading high-temperature influence of interactions and need not equal the ordering temperature.

= Spin-one-half paramagnetic magnetization
{parent=Paramagnetism}
{title2=$M=n\mu\tanh[\mu B/(k_BT)]$}

For independent localized two-state moments of projections $\pm\mu$ along a field, the one-ion partition function is $Z_1=2\cosh[\mu B/(k_BT)]$. Its thermal mean gives $M=n\mu\tanh[\mu B/(k_BT)]$. A $J=1/2$ ion has $\mu=g_J\mu_B/2$, so the special value $\mu=\mu_B$ additionally assumes $g_J=2$. At weak field the SI volume susceptibility is $\chi_H=\mu_0n\mu^2/(k_BT)$, neglecting demagnetizing corrections.

= Spin-state population and net polarization
{parent=Spin-one-half paramagnetic magnetization}
{title2=$p_+=(1+M/M_s)/2$}

For moment projections $\pm\mu$, $p_+=(1+\tanh x)/2$ but $M/M_s=\tanh x$, with $x=\mu B/(k_BT)$. At zero field half the moments occupy each projection while the net <magnetization> is zero. An effective Curie moment is also distinct from the saturated projection used in this two-state model.

= Independent-spin magnetic entropy
{parent=Spin-one-half paramagnetic magnetization}

For independent moments with magnetic energies $\mp\mu B$, $Z=(2\cosh x)^N$ and $S=Nk_B[\ln(2\cosh x)-x\tanh x]$, where $x=\mu B/(k_BT)$. This counts the magnetic orientations only, excluding translational and lattice <entropy>.

= Curie temperature
{c}
{parent=Magnetization}
{title2=$T_C$}
{wiki}

The Curie temperature is the transition temperature above which a ferromagnetic material loses spontaneous <magnetization>. A hot conducting planetary core can generate a <magnetic field> through <dynamo action> without behaving as a permanent magnet. The blocking temperature at which a mineral records crustal remanence also depends on its relaxation timescale and need not equal its Curie temperature.

= Remanent magnetization
{parent=Magnetization}

= Remanence
{synonym}

Remanent magnetization is <magnetization> retained after the applied <magnetic field> has been removed. Magnetic minerals cooling in an ambient field can preserve information about that field in a planetary crust. Such a record may outlive the <planetary dynamo> which originally produced it. Crustal remanence therefore differs from a present field maintained by conducting-fluid motion.

= Magnetization current
{title2=$J_{\mathrm{mag}}^\mu$}
{parent=Magnetization}

A relativistic magnetization current has the form $J_{\mathrm{mag}}^\mu=\partial_\nu M^{\nu\mu}$ for an antisymmetric polarization-magnetization tensor. It is identically conserved because commuting derivatives contract an antisymmetric tensor.

= Magnetic field intensity
{title2=$\mathbf H$}
{parent=Magnetization}

The magnetic field intensity is $\mathbf H=\mathbf B/\mu_0-\mathbf M$ and obeys $\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_{\rm free}$ in magnetostatics. In a linear isotropic medium, $\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H$.

= Ferromagnetism
{parent=Magnetization}
{wiki}

<Ferromagnetism> is collective magnetic order with <spontaneous magnetization> below a <Curie temperature>. Interactions favor aligned <magnetic moments>; the <Ising model> represents a two-orientation version with positive coupling.

A <magnetic domain> can possess <spontaneous magnetization> even when other domains cancel its contribution to the whole specimen. Compare this spatial cancellation between domains with <antiferromagnetism>, where opposing <magnetic sublattices> compensate within the ordered structure, and <ferrimagnetism>, where their contributions are generally unequal. The <magnetic sublattice> diagram makes the three local patterns explicit.

Smoothly sweeping an applied <magnetic field> can reveal abrupt <magnetization> changes through the <Barkhausen effect>. A <pickup coil> detects the resulting changes of <magnetic flux>, making collective <magnetic domain-wall> motion accessible as an electrical <signal>.

= Stoner model
{parent=Ferromagnetism}
{c}

An itinerant mean-field model in which exchange splitting competes with the kinetic cost of unequal spin populations. Its instability condition is a suitably normalized interaction parameter times the Fermi-level density of states exceeding unity. It is distinct from a localized-spin exchange Hamiltonian for an insulator.

= Ferromagnetic ordering
{parent=Ferromagnetism}

Formation of <magnetic order> with a spontaneous uniform <magnetization>. A large positive <Pauli paramagnetism> alone does not establish this: an instability caused by interactions is needed for spontaneous <magnetization> in zero applied <magnetic field>.

= Hard magnetic material
{parent=Ferromagnetism}

A <hard magnetic material> strongly resists reversal of its <magnetization>, giving high coercivity and useful remanence for permanent magnets. The relevant hardness is magnetic, not a mechanical strength rating.

= Soft magnetic material
{parent=Ferromagnetism}

A <soft magnetic material> changes its <magnetization> relatively easily and has a low coercive field. High useful permeability and small <hysteresis> losses favour such materials in electromagnets and alternating-field cores. “Soft” describes magnetic response rather than mechanical softness; electrically resistive <ferrimagnets> can also be magnetically soft.

= Ferromagnet
{parent=Ferromagnetism}

A <ferromagnet> is a material that exhibits <ferromagnetism>. Its <magnetic domains> can cancel the net <magnetization> even while each domain remains ordered.

= Magnetic domain
{parent=Ferromagnetism}
{wiki}

A <magnetic domain> is a region sharing an ordered magnetic orientation. Different <magnetic domains> can reduce the total <magnetic moment> without erasing the local <magnetic order>. A zero total <magnetization> therefore does not by itself establish <antiferromagnetism>.

= Single-domain ferromagnetic particle
{parent=Magnetic domain}

A ferromagnetic particle with no internal domain wall. <Magnetization> reversal can proceed by rotation or nonuniform modes; <thermal activation> and <anisotropy> determine blocking and <coercivity>. Small size alone does not guarantee a permanent hard magnet.

= Magnetic domain wall
{parent=Magnetic domain}
{wiki=Domain_wall_(magnetism)}

A <magnetic domain wall> separates differently oriented <magnetic domains>. Its motion changes their <volumes> and average <magnetization>. It is a magnetic <domain wall>.

An original two-<magnetic-domain> model makes the collective change explicit. Suppose opposite regions have <magnetizations> $+M_s$ and $-M_s$ along the measuring axis, where $M_s$ is the local <saturation magnetization>. If a <magnetic domain wall> sweeps <volume> $\Delta V$ from the negative region into the positive one, in a specimen of fixed <volume> $V$, then
$$
\Delta m=2M_s\Delta V,\qquad
\Delta M=\frac{2M_s\Delta V}{V}.
$$
The factor two comes from replacing $-M_s$ by $+M_s$. The local magnitude $M_s$ stays fixed while the average <magnetization> changes; the event size depends on the swept <volume>, rather than a universal single-<spin> increment.

= Wall and stray-energy comparison in a magnetic particle
{parent=Magnetic domain wall}
{title2=$\gamma_wA_w\geq\Delta U_{\rm stray}$}

A trial domain pattern costs wall energy $\gamma_wA_w$ and can release stray energy $\Delta U$. For a stable uniform ground state, $\gamma_wA_w\geq\Delta U$ within that trial model. Substituting the entire initial stray energy assumes that it is nearly all removed; a numerical lower bound independent of the domain geometry does not follow from single-domain observation alone.

= Magnetic domain-wall pinning
{parent=Magnetic domain wall}

Spatial variations in <energy>, including <crystal defects>, can trap a <magnetic domain wall>. This is <magnetic domain-wall pinning>. Increasing the applied <magnetic field> can release the wall into a collective jump.

= Barkhausen effect
{c}
{parent=Ferromagnetism}
{wiki}

= Barkhausen noise
{c}
{synonym}

The <Barkhausen effect> consists of abrupt <magnetization> changes during a smooth applied <magnetic field> sweep. Its inductive <signal> is <Barkhausen noise>, often arising from collective <magnetic domain-wall> motion.<The Barkhausen effect by Gianfranco Durin and Stefano Zapperi>[§I, p. 2: wall-motion interpretation]{ref}

The distinction is between a smooth drive and an intermittent material response. A smooth macroscopic <hysteresis> curve can hide smaller jumps. By <Faraday's law of induction>, a <pickup coil> responds to the rate of change of linked <magnetic flux>, so a rapid jump can be conspicuous even when the total change is small. A pulse is not one <chemical atom> flipping: neither its height nor the number of audible clicks counts individual <electron> <spins>. Qualitative crackling alone does not establish quantum <spin>; collective switching also admits a classical model with trapped interfaces. Compare the distinct mechanical readout of <angular momentum> transfer in the <Einstein-de Haas effect>.

= Inductive Barkhausen experiment
{c}
{parent=Barkhausen effect}

<Heinrich Barkhausen>'s 1919 report used <iron>, a 300-turn <pickup coil>, moving magnet, amplified telephone readout and galvanometer. Removing <iron> corrected drive pickup. <The Barkhausen effect by Gianfranco Durin and Stefano Zapperi>[Appendix A, p. 46: later translation of the 1919 apparatus and blank control]{ref}

A modern primary experiment by <Gianfranco Durin> and <Stefano Zapperi> used annealed <iron>–<silicon> strips and amorphous alloys. A 60 cm <solenoid> swept a triangular <magnetic field intensity>; a 50-turn <pickup coil>, wound within 1 mm, sensed the specimen. A preamplifier with a 3–20 kHz <low-pass filter> preceded sampling. Measurements selected the central <hysteresis> region dominated by <magnetic domain-wall> motion. Above-background threshold crossings defined event durations; integrating between crossings defined event sizes. These are reported procedures, rather than data generated for the diagrams.<Scaling exponents for Barkhausen avalanches in polycrystals and amorphous ferromagnets by Gianfranco Durin and Stefano Zapperi>[pp. 1–2: apparatus, specimens and threshold integration]{ref}

\Image[barkhausen-pickup-coil-experiment.png]
{title=<Barkhausen effect>: swept <magnetic field>, stationary specimen and inductive readout}
{description=Original functional schematic of the modern measurement principle. The specimen and <pickup coil> remain stationary; no torsional suspension or mechanical rotation is needed. The empty matching coil, difference stage and drive monitor show proposed controls, not an assertion that every component was present in the cited 2000 apparatus. For this stationary implementation, secure a thin <ferromagnet> and its <pickup coil> inside a longer drive <solenoid>, keeping their relative positions fixed. Drive the <solenoid> with a slow triangular <electric current>, monitor that <electric current>, and record the amplified, filtered induced <electromotive force> with a digitizer. A speaker may make suitable frequencies audible, but the voltage record is needed for quantitative analysis. Slow variation of the drive does not require smooth <magnetization>. A triangular drive supplies an approximately constant applied-field sweep rate along each ramp; it does not guarantee constant instantaneous $dM/dt$.

A proposed control sequence makes the inference testable. First record the electronics background with no drive. Repeat the same sweep with the specimen removed, retaining coil geometry: <mutual induction> from the drive can itself induce a voltage. A matching empty <pickup coil> wired in opposition can suppress common drive pickup, after checking its matching. Compensation of air flux by an opposed coil is an established method.<The Barkhausen effect by Gianfranco Durin and Stefano Zapperi>[§II.A.1, p. 4: opposed-coil compensation]{ref} Then restore the specimen, repeat both sweep directions, reduce sweep rate, and compare the same <hysteresis> region with fixed <amplifier> gain, filter and threshold. Monitor drive irregularities and mechanical vibration; either can generate spurious pulses. These controls are recommendations for the schematic, rather than undocumented controls attributed to the modern paper.

A genuine material contribution should differ from the blank-coil record and change reproducibly with the magnetic preparation and sweep region. Quieting the drive tests drive-correlated pickup, but a held field need not eliminate all delayed material motion. Reversing the sweep is a consistency check on signed changes, not a demand that individual events retrace. A changing pulse count under a different filter or threshold cannot by itself establish changed microscopic dynamics. The useful inference is intermittent collective <magnetization> change within the instrument's <signal bandwidth>, with spatial detail left unresolved.

= Barkhausen pulse area
{c}
{parent=Barkhausen effect}
{title2=$\int e\,dt=-\Delta\lambda$}

Write the <magnetic flux linkage> of the stationary <pickup coil> as $\lambda=\sum_{n=1}^N\Phi_n$, in <SI> units of <weber (unit)>. If every turn links the same <magnetic flux> $\Phi$, then $\lambda=N\Phi$; the turn count is dimensionless. With a fixed winding and terminal convention, <Faraday's law of induction> gives the induced <electromotive force>
$$
e(t)=-\frac{d\lambda}{dt},\qquad
\int_{t_a}^{t_b}e(t)\,dt=-[\lambda(t_b)-\lambda(t_a)].
$$
This is the <fundamental theorem of calculus> applied to <magnetic flux linkage>. Its units are $\mathrm{V\,s}=\mathrm{Wb}$, not <volts> alone. Reversing the leads reverses the sign. In a high-input-<electrical-resistance> voltage measurement the input approximately follows this <electromotive force>; coil loading and the <frequency response> of the electronics require separate calibration.

Separate direct drive linkage from material linkage: $\lambda=\lambda_{\rm drive}+\lambda_{\rm material}$. Even an empty <pickup coil> has $-d\lambda_{\rm drive}/dt$. In an ideal long, uniformly coupled specimen with negligible <demagnetizing field> correction and <eddy current> shielding, a useful material approximation is
$$
\lambda_{\rm material}=\mu_0 N A_s M,\qquad
\int e_{\rm material}\,dt=-\mu_0 N A_s\Delta M.
$$
Here $A_s$ is specimen cross-sectional <area>, not the larger coil aperture; $\mu_0$ is <vacuum permeability> and $M$ the axial <magnetization> sampled by the coil. The units check directly: $(\mathrm{V\,s/(A\,m)})(\mathrm{m^2})(\mathrm{A/m})=\mathrm{V\,s}$. Finite geometry and nonuniform <magnetization> generally require a calibrated coupling in place of $\mu_0NA_s$. The exact linkage identity survives that failure of the uniform approximation.

As an original illustrative calculation, stipulate $N=100$, $A_s=10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}$, $\Delta M=10^3\,\mathrm{A/m}$ and rounded $\mu_0=4\pi\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{V\,s/(A\,m)}$. Then
$$
\Delta\lambda=4\pi\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{Wb}
\approx0.125664\,\mu\mathrm{Wb},\qquad
\int e_{\rm material}\,dt\approx-0.125664\,\mathrm{mV\,ms}.
$$
A uniform change over $0.10\,\mathrm{ms}$ would give a rectangular pulse of $-1.25664\,\mathrm{mV}$; over $0.20\,\mathrm{ms}$ it gives $-0.628319\,\mathrm{mV}$ with the same area. Thus pulse height mixes change size with change speed. These numbers describe an ideal model, not observed pulses or material constants.

= Discrete-jump model of Barkhausen noise
{c}
{parent=Barkhausen effect}
{title2=$M(t)=M_0+rt+\sum_i\Delta M_i\Theta(t-t_i)$}

An explicit constructed model separates slow <magnetization> drift from discrete collective jumps:
$$
M(t)=M_0+rt+\sum_i\Delta M_i\Theta(t-t_i),\qquad
\frac{dM}{dt}=r+\sum_i\Delta M_i\delta(t-t_i).
$$
Here $\Theta$ is the <Heaviside step function>, $\delta$ the <Dirac delta function>, $r$ a smooth drift rate, and $t_i$ the jump times. The ideal discontinuities stand for events shorter than the resolved timescale; actual <magnetic domain walls> move over finite times. This equation stipulates jump sizes rather than deriving them from atomic <spin>. With $K=\mu_0NA_s$ in the preceding geometry, subtracting the smooth background leaves $e_{\rm jump}=-K\sum_i\Delta M_i\delta(t-t_i)$.

Let a stable <linear filter> have causal <impulse response> $h(t)$, normalized by $\int h(t)\,dt=1$, and let $G$ be a constant dimensionless <voltage gain>. The output is a <convolution>:
$$
v_{\rm out}(t)=G(h*e_{\rm jump})(t)
=-GK\sum_i\Delta M_i h(t-t_i).
$$
A simple <low-pass filter> has $h(t)=\Theta(t)e^{-t/\tau}/\tau$, with <time constant> $\tau>0$. Its output pulse has peak proportional to $\Delta M_i/\tau$ and signed area $-GK\Delta M_i$. Integrating the <convolution> proves area preservation because $\int h=1$; a wider filter response lowers peaks and lengthens tails. This ideal filter models resolution, rather than claiming the cited instrument had exactly this response.

\Image[barkhausen-discrete-jumps-filtered-voltage.png]
{title=Constructed <magnetization> jumps and filtered <Barkhausen noise> voltage}
{description=Calculated model, not measured traces. The top panel stipulates three jumps of $1000$, $500$ and $500\,\mathrm{A/m}$ at $1.0$, $3.0$ and $3.2\,\mathrm{ms}$, with no smooth drift. The middle panel applies unit-gain exponential <low-pass filters> with $\tau=0.10$ and $0.50\,\mathrm{ms}$ and $K=4\pi\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{V\,s\,m/A}$. The bottom panel shows cumulative signed <integrals> for the broader filter, with and without an additional zero-DC stage of <time constant> $1.0\,\mathrm{ms}$.}

The nearby positive jumps in the figure have additive negative pulse areas even when their tails overlap. For $\tau=0.50\,\mathrm{ms}$ the first of the pair still has $e^{-0.2/0.5}=e^{-0.4}\approx0.67032$ of its initial height when the second arrives. Thresholding can merge those responses into one detected event; limited <signal bandwidth> also obscures their separation. The total signed area still records the sum if the complete response is integrated, but cannot identify how that sum was partitioned into events. Equal simultaneous jumps are exactly indistinguishable from a single jump of their summed size in this linear measurement. Oppositely signed simultaneous changes can cancel.

Area preservation requires the stated electronics. A zero-DC stage after the <low-pass filter> has response
$$
q(t)=\delta(t)-\frac{\Theta(t)}{T}e^{-t/T},\qquad
\int q(t)\,dt=0.
$$
Consequently $\int G(q*h*e_{\rm jump})(t)\,dt=0$ over the complete response: a main pulse is followed by a compensating tail. Integrating only a threshold-selected lobe, clipping the record, or saturating the <amplifier> breaks the simple pulse-area inference. Recovering a <magnetization> change from such a readout needs the calibrated <frequency response> and an appropriate reconstruction. Neither the audible crackle nor the displayed voltage is a direct staircase of <magnetization>.

= Radiation-force density without Ohm's law
{parent=Maxwell stress tensor}
{title2=$\overline f_x=-\partial_x[\mu_0\overline{H_z^2}/2+\epsilon_0\overline{E_y^2}/2]$}

= Electromagnetic momentum density
{title2=$\mathbf g=\epsilon_0\mathbf E\times\mathbf B=\mathbf S/c^2$}
{parent=Maxwell stress tensor}

The electromagnetic momentum density in vacuum is $\mathbf g=\epsilon_0\mathbf E\times\mathbf B$. It equals the <Poynting vector> divided by $c^2$, expressing the relativistic relation between energy flux and momentum density.

= Local conservation of electromagnetic momentum
{parent=Maxwell stress tensor}

With the momentum-flux sign convention used for the <Maxwell stress tensor>, Maxwell's equations imply
$$
\nabla\mathbin{\cdot}\boldsymbol\sigma
+\partial_t\mathbf g=-\mathbf f_{\rm L},
$$
where $\mathbf g$ is the <electromagnetic momentum density> and $\mathbf f_{\rm L}$ is the <Lorentz force density> exerted by the field on matter. Thus total field-plus-matter momentum is locally conserved.

= Radiation pressure on a perfectly absorbing surface
{parent=Maxwell stress tensor}

A normally incident vacuum wave of time-averaged intensity $I$ transfers momentum $I/c$ per unit area and time to a perfectly absorbing surface, so its radiation pressure is
$$
p=\frac Ic.
$$
A perfect reflector reverses the normal momentum and gives $2I/c$.

= Proca action
{c}
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{wiki}

The Proca action adds $m^2A_\mu A^\mu/2$ to the Maxwell Lagrangian. It describes a massive spin-one field and explicitly breaks the Abelian gauge symmetry of the massless theory.

= Proca equation
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{c}

With <Minkowski metric> signature $(+---)$ and source coupling $-\mu_0J_\mu A^\mu$ added to the <Proca action>, a massive vector field coupled to a <conserved current> obeys
$$
\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}+m^2A^\nu=\mu_0J^\nu.
$$
In vacuum this gives $(\Box+m^2)A^\nu=0$ after imposing the dynamically derived <Lorenz constraint in Proca theory>. The explicit mass term breaks the gauge invariance of the massless Maxwell theory.

= Proca field
{c}
{parent=Proca equation}

A Proca field is a massive spin-one vector field. Its field equation enforces transversality and leaves three physical polarizations.

= Longitudinal-vector annihilation through a scalar contact interaction
{parent=Proca field}
{title2=$\sigma_{LL}\sim g^4s/(32\pi m_A^4)$}

For a vertex $2ig^2\eta_{\mu\nu}$ and two identical outgoing real <scalar> <particles>, the longitudinal-pair amplitude is $g^2(s-2m_A^2)/m_A^2$. The high-energy <cross section> is the displayed expression, including the final-state factor $1/2!$. An <unpolarized cross section>[unpolarized] incoming pair averages over nine <spin> states and has leading <cross section> $g^4s/(288\pi m_A^4)$. A written interaction $g^2A_\mu A^\mu h^2$ instead gives vertex $4ig^2\eta_{\mu\nu}$ and four times these <cross sections>. The distinction separates a vertex normalization from <polarization> averaging and identical-particle phase-space counting.

= Complex Proca Hamiltonian
{parent=Proca field}
{title2=$\mathcal H_{\rm red}=|\pi|^2+|\nabla\cdot\pi|^2/M^2+|G_{ij}|^2/2+M^2|\mathbf B|^2$}

For <Minkowski metric> $(+---)$ and $\mathcal L=-G_{\mu\nu}^*G^{\mu\nu}/2+M^2B_\mu^*B^\mu$, use coordinates $B^\mu,B^{*\mu}$. The spatial <canonical momenta> are $\pi_i=G^{*0i}$, $\pi_i^*=G^{0i}$; the temporal momenta vanish. Constraint preservation gives $\partial_i\pi_i=M^2B_0^*$. Eliminating $B_0$ and its conjugate gives the positive reduced <Hamiltonian density> displayed above, up to a spatial surface term. In particular the massive <Gauss law> has a mass-dependent right-hand side; setting it to zero removes a physical longitudinal <particle polarization>.

= Proca propagator
{c}
{parent=Proca field}

For metric signature $(+---)$, the momentum-space Feynman propagator of a Proca field is
$$
\widetilde\Delta_{\mu\nu}(p)=\frac{-i}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon}\left(\eta_{\mu\nu}-\frac{p_\mu p_\nu}{m^2}\right).
$$
The longitudinal numerator term inverts the massive vector kinetic operator without gauge fixing.

= Ultraviolet and unitary-gauge limits of the Proca propagator
{parent=Proca propagator}
{title2=$D_\xi=-i[P_T/(k^2+M^2)+\xi P_L/(k^2+\xi M^2)]$}

In mostly-plus signature, the <unitary gauge> limit $\xi\to\infty$ leaves the longitudinal <Proca propagator> term $-iP_L/M^2$. Its ultraviolet scaling is constant in the <momentum> magnitude, whereas finite $\xi$ gives $1/k^2$ scaling. Thus taking the gauge-parameter limit before the ultraviolet limit obscures ordinary power-counting <renormalizability>. A conserved external current annihilates the longitudinal term, so the exchange between two such currents still falls as $1/k^2$. The issue does not make a free massive-vector <Gaussian integral> inconsistent.

= High-energy obstruction for a hard vector mass
{parent=Proca propagator}
{title2=$D_{\mu\nu}(k)=-i(g_{\mu\nu}-k_\mu k_\nu/m^2)/(k^2-m^2+i0)$}

A free <Proca field> is consistent and has three physical <particle polarizations>. The difficulty for generic interactions is the ultraviolet behavior of its <Proca propagator>: the longitudinal numerator makes part of it approach order $1/m^2$, rather than decay as $1/k^2$. Longitudinal external <polarization vectors> also behave as $\varepsilon_L^\mu=k^\mu/m+O(m/E)$. Without suitable identities and cancellations, amplitudes grow with energy and perturbative counterterms of increasing dimension are needed. Conserved-current Abelian models and the <Stueckelberg mechanism> are important exceptions; a massive interacting vector is not intrinsically inconsistent as an <effective field theory>.

= Proca time-ordering contact term
{c}
{parent=Proca propagator}
{title2=$\Delta^{\rm cov}_{ab}-\Delta^{T}_{ab}=i\delta_a^0\delta_b^0\delta^{(4)}/m^2$}

For a free massive <Proca field> in the mostly-plus <Minkowski metric>, the covariant inverse of the quadratic <action> and the canonical <time-ordered product> of vector potentials differ locally. With <Fourier transform> $e^{iq\cdot(x-y)}$, the covariant <Proca propagator> is $-i(\eta_{ab}+q_aq_b/m^2)/(q^2+m^2-i0)$, while the literal canonical <two-point correlation function> subtracts $i\delta_a^0\delta_b^0/m^2$ in momentum space. The time component is nondynamical; its physical-mode numerator is $|\boldsymbol q|^2/m^2$, rather than $-1+(q^0)^2/m^2$. The difference is an instantaneous <Dirac delta function>, not another propagating state. Both expressions agree away from coincidence. This distinction matters when identifying a covariant <Green function> with a canonical correlator in a constrained field theory.

= Transverse vector field

A vector field is transverse when $\partial_\mu V^\mu=0$ in spacetime, or $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf V=0$ for a spatial Fourier mode.

Taking a divergence and using antisymmetry of $F^{\mu\nu}$ and current conservation gives $m^2\partial_\mu A^\mu=0$. For nonzero mass, the Lorenz condition follows from the field equation rather than from a gauge choice.

The static Green function of $\nabla^2-m^2$ in three dimensions is proportional to $e^{-mr}/r$. It describes an interaction screened beyond range $m^{-1}$ and tends to the Coulomb potential as $m\to0$.

= Relativistic Lorentz force

$m\,du^\mu/d\tau=qF^\mu{}_\nu u^\nu$ gives $d\mathbf p/dt=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)$ and $d(\gamma mc^2)/dt=q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v$.

= Constant-field relativistic Lorentz-force solution
{parent=Relativistic Lorentz force}

For a constant <electromagnetic field tensor>, the <relativistic Lorentz force> is a constant-coefficient linear system on <four-velocity>, with solution
$$
u(\tau)=\exp\!\left(\frac qmF\tau\right)u(0).
$$
The electric-type blocks of $F$ generate hyperbolic rotations, while magnetic-type blocks generate ordinary rotations.

= Magnetic-dominated crossed-field orbit
{parent=Constant-field relativistic Lorentz-force solution}
{title2=$V=E/B$}

Uniform perpendicular fields with $E^2<c^2B^2$ admit a subluminal <Lorentz boost> eliminating the <electric field>. A laboratory particle initially at rest follows a circle in the adapted frame and a stretched cycloid in the laboratory. The mean drift <velocity> is $\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/B^2$, while its laboratory kinetic <energy> is periodic rather than unbounded.

= Electric-dominated crossed-field orbit
{parent=Constant-field relativistic Lorentz-force solution}
{title2=$V=c^2B/E$}

Uniform perpendicular fields with $E^2>c^2B^2$ admit a subluminal <Lorentz boost> along $\mathbf E\times\mathbf B$ that eliminates the <magnetic field>. The transformed motion has constant transverse <momentum> and hyperbolic <acceleration> in the electric direction. An initially stationary laboratory particle generally has nonzero transverse <velocity> in this adapted frame; dropping it gives the wrong laboratory trajectory.

= Relativistic motion in parallel electric and magnetic fields
{parent=Constant-field relativistic Lorentz-force solution}

When uniform $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$ are parallel, the <relativistic Lorentz force> splits into independent hyperbolic motion along the fields and circular motion in the perpendicular plane. The circle is periodic in <proper time>, while electric acceleration makes the helix's longitudinal pitch grow exponentially from one proper-time cyclotron period to the next.

= Relativistic hyperbolic motion in a uniform electric field
{parent=Relativistic Lorentz force}

A particle released from rest in a constant electric field parallel to its motion follows a spacetime hyperbola and has rapidity proportional to proper time.

This force law realizes <hyperbolic motion> with constant <proper acceleration>.

= Null crossed electromagnetic field
{parent=Relativistic Lorentz force}

Perpendicular fields with $\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B=0$ and $E^2-c^2B^2=0$ form a null electromagnetic field. The constant-field Lorentz-force generator is nilpotent, so its exponential terminates as a polynomial.

Both invariants of the <electromagnetic field tensor> vanish for this field class.

= Relativistic trajectory in a constant null crossed field
{parent=Null crossed electromagnetic field}

Choose
$$
\mathbf B=B\,\mathbf e_z,\qquad
\mathbf E=cB\,\mathbf e_x,
\qquad
\Omega=\frac{qB}{m}.
$$
A particle released from rest at the origin has the proper-time trajectory
$$
t(\tau)=\tau+\frac{\Omega^2\tau^3}{6},
\qquad
x(\tau)=\frac{c\Omega\tau^2}{2},
\qquad
y(\tau)=-\frac{c\Omega^2\tau^3}{6},
\qquad z(\tau)=0.
$$
Eliminating <proper time> gives the semicubical parabola
$$
\boxed{y^2=\frac{2qB}{9mc}x^3.}
$$
This signed coefficient assumes that the positive $x$ direction is chosen along $\mathbf E$; reversing that axis changes the signs of both $x^3$ and its coefficient.

= Uniform magnetic field with axial harmonic confinement

The cylindrical <vector> potential $A_\theta=\mu r$ gives a uniform axial <magnetic field> $2\mu$, while <scalar potential> $\lambda z^2$ supplies harmonic axial confinement for positive charge and positive $\lambda$. Transverse motion is cyclotron motion about a fixed guiding centre, and axial motion is an independent harmonic <oscillation>. A circle centred on the cylindrical origin has $\dot\theta=-\Omega_c$ for positive charge.

= Commensurate cyclotron and axial oscillations

A rational ratio of axial and <cyclotron frequencies> makes a nondegenerate combined orbit close after an integer number of turns. For ratio $p/q$ in lowest terms it closes after $q$ transverse periods and $p$ axial periods. An integer ratio is a sufficient special case. = Magnetic angular-momentum invariant

For a particle obeying $m\dot{\mathbf v}=q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$ in a constant uniform <magnetic field>, the displayed combination is conserved. The derivative of mechanical <angular momentum> projected onto the field is canceled by the quadratic position term. This is the rotational analogue of the distinction between mechanical and <canonical momentum> in an electromagnetic field.

In a uniform <magnetic field>, the <Lorentz force> leaves the component of <velocity> parallel to the field constant and rotates the perpendicular component at the nonrelativistic <cyclotron frequency> $|q|B/m$. The resulting path is a <helix> around a field line.

= On-axis entry into a solenoidal tube

A particle entering a uniform axial <magnetic field> on the tube axis follows a transverse circle through that entry point. = Magnetostatics
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{wiki}

Magnetostatics studies time-independent magnetic fields and steady currents. In vacuum its field equations are $\nabla\mathbin\cdot\mathbf B=0$ and $\nabla\mathbin\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J$.

= Exterior magnetic field of a wire with an eccentric cylindrical cavity
{parent=Magnetostatics}

A uniform <electric current density> outside a parallel cylindrical cavity can be constructed by the <superposition principle>: add a full cylinder carrying that density and a displaced cylinder carrying its negative. At an exterior point, each contribution is the external <magnetic flux density> of its own net <electric current>. The combined <magnetic flux density> generally lacks <rotational symmetry> even though <Ampère's law> fixes its <line integral> around the whole wire.

= Uniform magnetic field in an off-axis cylindrical cavity
{parent=Magnetostatics}
{title2=$\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J\times\mathbf b/2$}

Subtract a cylinder of <current density> $\mathbf J$ occupying the cavity from a complete current-carrying cylinder. Inside each cylinder, $\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J\times\mathbf r/2$. The difference is independent of observation point and equals $\mu_0\mathbf J\times\mathbf b/2$, where $\mathbf b$ is the offset of the cavity axis. This assumes parallel axes, uniform <current density> in the remaining metal, and negligible end effects.

= Magnetic dipole
{parent=Magnetostatics}
{wiki}

A localized magnetic source described at leading nonvanishing dipole order by a <magnetic dipole moment>. A small current loop is one classical model. The source object, its moment vector and the resulting <magnetic dipole field> are distinct concepts. At large distance from a source with nonzero dipole moment, higher multipoles give smaller corrections; if the dipole moment vanishes, a higher-order term can lead instead.

= Small-angle magnetic dipole oscillation
{parent=Magnetic dipole}
{title2=$T=2\pi\sqrt{J/(m_BB)}$}

A <magnetic dipole> of fixed magnitude $m_B$, free to rotate about an axis with <moment of inertia> $J$, has <potential energy> $-m_BB\cos\theta$ in a uniform <magnetic field>. Near stable alignment, $J\ddot\theta=-m_BB\theta$, so its small-angle period is $2\pi\sqrt{J/(m_BB)}$. This neglects damping, bearing friction and any other restoring <torque>.

= Oscillation of a uniformly wound coil
{parent=Small-angle magnetic dipole oscillation}

For a light nonmagnetic rod of length $l$ wound uniformly with $N$ thin turns of mean radius $a$, the <magnetic dipole moment> is $m_B=NI\pi a^2$. Wire of cross-sectional <area> $A_w$ and <density> $\rho$ has <mass> approximately $M=2\pi aN\rho A_w$. The transverse central <moment of inertia> is $J=M(l^2/12+a^2/2)$ in the thin-winding approximation. Consequently the oscillation period in a transverse <magnetic field> does not depend on the number of turns: wire inertia and magnetic moment both scale with $N$.

= Magnetic tweezers
{parent=Magnetic dipole}

A generic instrument for <force> and <torque> uses a magnetically responsive bead in a controlled <magnetic field>, with optical tracking. Translational <force> requires a field gradient. Permanent or anisotropic moments can feel orienting <torque>; an isotropic induced moment parallel to the field has no such <torque>. This definition concerns the physical instrument, without biological applications.

= Transverse-fluctuation calibration of a tensioned tether
{parent=Magnetic tweezers}

For a tether with extension $\ell$ and tensile load $F$, a small transverse coordinate costs $Fx^2/(2\ell)$. = Finite-exposure correction to fluctuation force calibration
{parent=Transverse-fluctuation calibration of a tensioned tether}
{title2=$\langle\bar x^2\rangle/\langle x^2\rangle=2(s-1+e^{-s})/s^2$}

For an equilibrium overdamped harmonic coordinate with covariance $\langle x(t)x(0)\rangle=V e^{-|t|/\tau}$, a camera exposure $\Delta$ records the average over that interval. Integrating the covariance twice gives $\operatorname{Var}(\bar x)=2V\Delta^{-2}\int_0^\Delta(\Delta-u)e^{-u/\tau}du$. The displayed reduction follows with $s=\Delta/\tau$. In the tether calibration $F=k_BTh/V$, ignoring exposure averaging overestimates the inferred <force>. Independent localization-noise <variance> instead adds to the measured <variance> and biases it in the opposite direction. Other filters require their actual response functions.

= Induced magnetic bead force
{parent=Magnetic tweezers}

For a linear isotropic unsaturated effective susceptibility contrast $\Delta\chi$, the induced-moment energy is $-V\Delta\chi B^2/(2\mu_0)$, giving $\mathbf F=V\Delta\chi\nabla B^2/(2\mu_0)$. The one-half differs from the fixed permanent-dipole energy because the applied field creates the moment. Demagnetization and saturation are absorbed into or replace the assumed response model.

= Magnetic field of a rotating charged solid cylinder
{parent=Magnetostatics}

An infinite solid cylinder with radius $R$, prescribed uniform volume <charge density> $\rho$, and <angular velocity> $\omega\hat{\mathbf z}$ has <current density> $\mathbf J=\rho\omega r\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$. Treat a shell at radius $a$ and thickness $da$ as a <solenoid> carrying surface current $\rho\omega a\,da$. With no applied background <magnetic field> and permeability $\mu$,
$$
\mathbf B(r)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{\mu\rho\omega}{2}(R^2-r^2)\hat{\mathbf z},&r<R,\\0,&r>R.\end{cases}
$$
The same result follows from <Ampère's circuital law>, since $-dB_z/dr=\mu\rho\omega r$ inside.

= Magnetic circuit
{parent=Magnetostatics}
{wiki}

A <magnetic circuit> approximates a narrow core and its gaps by sections carrying a common <magnetic flux> $\Phi$. With negligible leakage and approximately uniform <magnetic flux density> across each section, <Gauss's law for magnetism> gives $B=\Phi/A$. The <magnetostatic Ampère law> then gives $NI=\int H\,dl=\Phi\int dl/(\mu_0\mu_rA)$. This requires an appropriate material relation; constant <magnetic permeability> is an approximation for an actual magnetic core.

= Uniformly magnetized ring with a narrow gap
{parent=Magnetic circuit}
{title2=$H_g=lM/(l+a),\quad H_r=-aM/(l+a)$}

A rod of magnetic path length $l$ and tangential <magnetization> $M$ closed by a gap of width $a$ has constant flux in the one-dimensional <magnetic circuit> approximation. Equal cross sections and negligible leakage give $H_g=H_r+M$, while absence of free current gives $lH_r+aH_g=0$. Their solution is the displayed pair. Gap fringing or an unspecified cross-section change requires a more detailed field model.

= Gap field of a tapered electromagnet
{parent=Magnetic circuit}
{title2=$H_g=NI/[h+\mu_r^{-1}(Lr^2/R^2+2sr/R)]$}

A uniform-radius magnetic arc of <length> $L$ and <radius> $R$, two linear tapers of <length> $s$ to <radius> $r$, and an unfringed vacuum gap of <length> $h$ have the displayed gap field. Here $\mu_r=\mu/\mu_0$ and $B_g=\mu_0H_g$. Tapering concentrates flux, but at fixed winding current the gap-dominated limit is $H_g\simeq NI/h$ regardless of pole <area>. Saturation and leakage prevent unrestricted field enhancement.

= Magnetic fringing
{parent=Magnetic circuit}

<Magnetic fringing> is the spreading of <magnetic field lines> near an air gap or pole edge, so the gap's effective <magnetic flux> area exceeds its geometrical pole-face <area>. A gap much shorter than its transverse dimensions permits a leading uniform-field approximation. High core <magnetic permeability> does not by itself justify neglecting <magnetic fringing> for a wide gap.

= Magnetic reluctance
{parent=Magnetic circuit}
{title2=$\mathcal R=\int dl/(\mu_0\mu_rA)$}
{wiki}

The <magnetic reluctance> of a linear section of <magnetic circuit> is the ratio of the winding's integrated <magnetic field intensity> to the <magnetic flux>, with units $\mathrm{A\,Wb^{-1}}$. For uniform length $\ell$, cross-sectional <area> $A$, and relative <magnetic permeability> $\mu_r$, $\mathcal R=\ell/(\mu_0\mu_rA)$. Series sections carrying the same <magnetic flux> have additive <magnetic reluctances>, and $NI=\Phi\sum_i\mathcal R_i$.

= Small-signal response of a gapped magnetic core
{parent=Magnetic reluctance}
{title2=$\kappa=\mu_0n/[d+\mu_0\ell/\mu_{\rm core}]$}

For a uniform core of path length $\ell$, a narrow air gap $d$, and $n$ winding turns, <Ampère's circuital law> gives $nI=\ell H_{\rm core}+dB/\mu_0$ when leakage and fringing are negligible. If a reversible branch has differential <magnetic permeability> $\mu_{\rm core}=dB/dH$, linearization gives $dB/dI=\kappa$. An offset in the operating branch changes the bias field, not this differential formula. For $B=B_m\tanh(H/H_m)$ at the origin, $\mu_{\rm core}=B_m/H_m$.

= Reluctance of a conical magnetic pole
{parent=Magnetic reluctance}
{title2=$\mathcal R_{\rm taper}=s/(\mu\pi Rr)$}

For a linear-radius taper from $R$ to $r$ over <length> $s$, constant absolute permeability $\mu$, and a common approximately axial flux, integrate $dx/[\mu\pi\{R-(R-r)x/s\}^2]$ to obtain the displayed reluctance. Leakage, fringing, nonuniform transverse fields and saturation limit this one-dimensional magnetic-circuit approximation.

= Magnetic scalar potential
{parent=Magnetostatics}
{title2=$\mathbf B=-\nabla\psi$}
{wiki}

In a simply connected current-free region of constant <magnetic permeability>, <Ampère's circuital law> makes the <magnetic field> curl-free, so it admits a scalar potential. The absence of magnetic monopoles then gives <Laplace's equation> for that potential. Some conventions define the potential for $\mathbf H$ instead of $\mathbf B$, differing by the constant permeability. Boundary conditions, including imposed fields at infinity, determine the potential up to an additive constant.

= Gravity-equivalent magnetic surface density
{parent=Magnetic scalar potential}
{title2=$\Sigma_M=-B_{z0}/\sqrt{\pi G\mu_0}$}

For an isolated razor-thin disk, rescale its exterior <magnetic scalar potential> to satisfy $\sqrt{4\pi G/\mu_0}\mathbf B=-\nabla\Phi_M$. Comparing the normal magnetic boundary derivative with $\partial_z\Phi=2\pi G\Sigma$ identifies the displayed equivalent signed <surface density>. Both exterior potentials satisfy <Laplace's equation>. The equivalent <surface density> is a representation of the magnetic boundary data, rather than physical negative mass.

A magnetic vector potential satisfies B equal to its curl and is defined up to a gradient.

= Euler potentials
{c}
{parent=Magnetic vector potential}
{title2=$\mathbf B=\nabla\alpha\times\nabla\beta$}

Two scalar labels constant along a <magnetic field line> are Euler potentials when their crossed <gradients> give the <magnetic field>. The <magnetic vector potential> $\mathbf A=\alpha\nabla\beta$ proves that this representation is a <solenoidal vector field>. Conversely, two independent local line labels yield $\mathbf B=f(\alpha,\beta)\nabla\alpha\times\nabla\beta$; replacing $\beta$ by $\int^\beta f(\alpha,b)\,db$ absorbs the factor. This is a local construction; a global regular single-valued pair requires compatible <magnetic field line> topology. Fixing boundary labels preserves their paired-footpoint connections in an ideal variation.

= Coulomb-gauge vector potential of a long solenoid
{c}
{parent=Magnetic vector potential}

For an ideal infinitely long <solenoid> with interior <magnetic field> $B_0\hat{\mathbf z}$ and radius $R$, an axisymmetric <Coulomb gauge> representative of the <magnetic vector potential> is
$$
\mathbf A=A_\phi(r)\hat{\boldsymbol\phi},\qquad A_\phi(r)=\begin{cases}B_0r/2,&r<R,\\B_0R^2/(2r),&r>R.\end{cases}
$$
This follows from $2\pi rA_\phi=\Phi(r)$ by <Stokes theorem>. The exterior has zero <curl> but nonzero circulation around the enclosed <magnetic flux>. The <Coulomb gauge> condition alone does not eliminate every residual <gauge transformation>; symmetry and regularity select this customary representative.

= Magnetic helicity
{parent=Magnetic vector potential}
{title2=$H=\int_V\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B\,dV,\quad\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$}
{wiki}

The <magnetic helicity> integrates the <dot product> of a <magnetic vector potential> and its <magnetic field>. It measures linkage and twisting of magnetic flux. For a <solenoidal vector field> $\mathbf B$ tangent to the boundary, it is unchanged by the gauge transformation $\mathbf A\mapsto\mathbf A+\nabla\chi$, since the change is $\int_V\nabla\cdot(\chi\mathbf B)\,dV=\int_{\partial V}\chi\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf S=0$. If its density satisfies $\partial_t h=\nabla\cdot(f\mathbf B-h\mathbf u)$ with both vector fields tangent to the boundary of a fixed volume, the <divergence theorem> gives conservation of its integral. This is an instance of <helicity conservation by tangent boundary conditions>.

= Magnetic helicity boundary term
{parent=Magnetic helicity}
{title2=$\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B=-\tfrac12\partial_t(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B)-\tfrac12\nabla\cdot(\phi\mathbf B+\mathbf E\times\mathbf A)$}

For regular globally defined electromagnetic potentials, use $\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi-\partial_t\mathbf A$, $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$ and the homogeneous <Maxwell equations> to prove the displayed identity. With vanishing spatial surface flux, its spacetime integral is minus one half the change of <magnetic helicity>. Zero integral therefore needs matching temporal helicity boundaries, not merely localized <electric fields> and <magnetic fields>. The Gaussian potential $a(t)e^{-r^2}(-y,x,\lambda)$ has helicity $2\lambda(\pi/2)^{3/2}a(t)^2$ and supplies an explicit spatially localized counterexample when $a$ changes from zero to one.

= Resistive magnetic-helicity balance
{parent=Magnetic helicity}
{title2=$\dot H=-2\eta_m\int_V\mathbf B\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf B)\,dV$}

For a <magnetic vector potential> $\mathbf A$, a <solenoidal> <magnetic field> $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$ and constant <magnetic diffusivity> $\eta_m$, write $\partial_t\mathbf A=\mathbf u\times\mathbf B-\eta_m\nabla\times\mathbf B-\nabla\phi$. In a fixed closed volume with zero helicity surface flux, <integration by parts> makes the two differentiated factors in $H=\int_V\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B\,dV$ equal; the scalar-potential boundary term vanishes when $\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf n=0$. One sufficient boundary gauge is $\mathbf n\times\mathbf A=0$; another standard setting is integration over all space with sufficient decay. Orthogonality of $\mathbf u\times\mathbf B$ to $\mathbf B$ gives the displayed identity. Unlike the resistive <magnetic energy> loss, $\dot H$ is not sign definite. The zero normal <magnetic field> also makes $H$ <gauge invariance> under single-valued smooth gauge transformations.

= Selective decay bound for magnetic helicity
{parent=Resistive magnetic-helicity balance}
{title2=$|\dot H|\leq2\mu_0\sqrt{2\eta_mE_BD_B}$}

With the closed-boundary assumptions of the <resistive magnetic-helicity balance>, define the nonnegative resistive <magnetic energy> loss $D_B=\eta_m\int_V|\nabla\times\mathbf B|^2\,dV/\mu_0$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives the displayed bound. Consequently, in a family of fields with bounded $E_B,D_B$ as $\eta_m\to0$, resistive <magnetic helicity> loss tends to zero even when $D_B$ remains finite. This is an absolute conservation estimate; it is not a universal comparison of fractional decay times. For a single <Beltrami field> with $\nabla\times\mathbf B=k\mathbf B$ and no motion, both $E_B$ and $H$ have the same fractional decay rate $2\eta_mk^2$.

= Magnetic-energy lower bound from helicity
{parent=Magnetic helicity}
{title2=$E_B\geq |H_M|/(2\mu_0 C_D)$}

In a bounded regular domain, fix the global extension or circulation convention for the <magnetic vector potential>. A bounded inverse of <curl> on tangent <solenoidal vector fields> gives $\|\mathbf A\|_2\leq C_D\|\mathbf B\|_2$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> then gives $|H_M|\leq C_D\|\mathbf B\|_2^2=2\mu_0 C_DE_B$. One construction extends the tangent field by zero outside the domain and uses the whole-space <Biot-Savart law>; its kernel of size $|\mathbf x-\mathbf y|^{-2}$ is integrable over a bounded region, giving the required $L^2$ bound. Nonzero conserved <magnetic helicity> therefore prevents complete decay of <magnetic energy>.

= Gauge dependence of helicity in an open magnetic flux tube
{parent=Magnetic helicity}
{title2=$H_m/L=\pi bCR_0^2$}

An open <flux tube> carries <magnetic flux> through its end caps. Its ordinary <magnetic helicity> can therefore change under a single-valued <gauge transformation>. For $\mathbf B=aR\mathbf e_\phi+b\mathbf e_z$ inside $R<R_0$ and zero outside, a regular family of <magnetic vector potentials> is $A_\phi=bR/2$, $A_z=C-aR^2/2$ inside and $A_\phi=bR_0^2/(2R)$, $A_z=C-aR_0^2/2$ outside. Their tangential components agree at $R_0$. Inside, $\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B=bC$, so the helicity per unit length is $\pi bCR_0^2$. Changing $C$ is the gauge transformation $\Lambda=\Delta C\,z$, and changes this result whenever $b\ne0$. = Material transport of magnetic helicity density
{parent=Magnetic helicity}
{title2=$D_t(\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B/\rho)$}

For smooth <ideal magnetohydrodynamics>, write $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$ and $\partial_t\mathbf A=\mathbf u\times\mathbf B-\nabla\varphi$. The <continuity equation> and induction equation give $D_t(\mathbf B/\rho)=(\mathbf B/\rho)\cdot\nabla\mathbf u$. The vector-potential equation becomes $D_t\mathbf A=\nabla(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf A-\varphi)-(\nabla\mathbf u)^T\mathbf A$. Contracting these two equations cancels their velocity-gradient terms and proves
$$
D_t\left(\frac{\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B}{\rho}\right)=\frac{\mathbf B}{\rho}\cdot\nabla(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf A-\varphi).
$$
Consequently the <magnetic helicity> in a material volume satisfies $dH_m/dt=\int_{\partial V}(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf A-\varphi)\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf n\,dS$. It is conserved when the <magnetic field> is tangent to the boundary. A single-valued <gauge transformation> changes $H_m$ by $\int_{\partial V}\Lambda\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf n\,dS$, which also vanishes under that condition.

= Magnetic moment
{synonym}

A <magnetic dipole moment> is a <vector> that summarizes the strength and orientation of a localized magnetic source. For a planar <current loop>, it is the <electric current> times the <vector area>: $\mathbf m=IA\widehat{\mathbf n}$. The <right-hand rule> fixes the unit <normal vector> $\widehat{\mathbf n}$: curl the fingers of the right hand along the <conventional current>, and the thumb points along $\mathbf m$. This single <vector> predicts both the distant <magnetic dipole field> and the turning effect of an imposed <magnetic field>.<University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/11.5 Force and Torque on a Current Loop>[§11.5, equations 11.20–11.22: loop moment, winding count and torque]{ref}

The <SI> unit is <ampere> times square metre, $\mathrm{A\,m^2}$, equivalently <joule> per <tesla (unit)>. A <magnetic dipole moment> is neither a <magnetic field> nor a <magnetic monopole>: a closed <current loop> can have a nonzero <magnetic dipole moment> while its net outward <magnetic flux> through any closed surface remains zero, as required by <Gauss's law for magnetism>.

For a localized, steady <electric current density> $\mathbf J(\mathbf r)$, the more general definition is
$$
\mathbf m=\frac12\int_{\mathbb R^3}\mathbf r\times\mathbf J(\mathbf r)\,d^3r.
$$
Here the <cross product> is integrated over the complete source, including return paths. Steadiness requires $\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0$, and localization requires sufficiently rapid decay for the boundary terms to vanish. Under these assumptions the <localized steady-current moment identities> give $\int\mathbf J\,d^3r=0$: shifting the origin by $\mathbf a$ changes the proposed <magnetic dipole moment> by $-\tfrac12\mathbf a\times\int\mathbf J\,d^3r=0$. An isolated open wire segment does not satisfy these assumptions. <Classical Electromagnetism by Richard Fitzpatrick/Localized Current Distribution>{ref}.

= Barnett effect
{c}
{parent=Magnetic dipole moment}
{tag=Magnetization}
{wiki}

The <Barnett effect> is the change of a body's <magnetization> caused by mechanical rotation. Turning the whole specimen can bias the internal <angular momentum> carrying its <magnetic moments>, even with zero applied <magnetic field>. It is the reciprocal physical question to the <Einstein-de Haas effect>: impose rotation and measure <magnetization>, rather than change <magnetization> and observe mechanical motion. The relation between the two becomes especially transparent when the same signed <gyromagnetic ratio> connects <magnetic moment> and internal <angular momentum>.

<Samuel Jackson Barnett> published https://journals.aps.org/pr/abstract/10.1103/PhysRev.6.239[Magnetization by Rotation] on 1 October 1915 in Physical Review. The <Barnett rotation magnetometry experiment> below provides an inspected modern example. The important distinction is between an equivalent term in a <Hamiltonian> and the actual external <magnetic field> measured by a <magnetometer>: the first biases the material; its resulting <magnetization> produces the second.

= Barnett equivalent magnetic field
{c}
{parent=Barnett effect}
{title2=$B_\Omega=\Omega/\gamma$}

= Barnett field
{c}
{synonym}

Let the specimen rotate uniformly with <angular velocity> $\boldsymbol\Omega=\Omega\widehat{\mathbf z}$. In its <rotating reference frame>, the internal <Hamiltonian> contains
$$
\mathcal H_{\rm rot}=\mathcal H_0-\Omega J_z.
$$
Here $J_z$ is the internal <angular momentum operator> about the rotation axis, and $\mathcal H_0$ contains the internal interactions and any imposed <magnetic field> expressed in that frame. It does not include the separately treated rigid body's <rotational kinetic energy>. For a sector with <magnetic moment> $m_z=\gamma J_z$, the rotational term has exactly the form of a <magnetic dipole potential energy>:
$$
-\Omega J_z=-m_zB_\Omega,\qquad
B_\Omega=\frac{\Omega}{\gamma}.
$$
This <Barnett equivalent magnetic field> has <tesla (unit)> units, with the angular convention for the signed <gyromagnetic ratio>: $\Omega$ is in radians per second, not revolutions per second. Its sign matters. For an electronic contribution with $\gamma<0$, positive $\Omega$ favors positive $J_z$ and negative $m_z$. A sector with $\gamma>0$ has the opposite magnetic sign. Equal $\Omega$ need not correspond to equal <Barnett fields> for sectors having different <gyromagnetic ratios>.

A short derivation fixes the sign independently of any material model. Put $U(t)=\exp(-\mathrm i\Omega tJ_z/\hbar)$ and transform a laboratory <quantum state> by $|\psi_{\rm rot}\rangle=U^\dagger|\psi_{\rm lab}\rangle$. The <Schrödinger equation> gives
$$
\mathcal H_{\rm rot}
=U^\dagger\mathcal H_{\rm lab}U-\mathrm i\hbar U^\dagger\dot U
=\mathcal H_0-\Omega J_z.
$$
The transformed laboratory term is denoted by $\mathcal H_0$; a laboratory perturbation not stationary in the rotating frame can make it time dependent. Establishing a steady thermal response also requires exchange of internal <angular momentum> with the rotating body, for example through <spin-lattice relaxation>. A mere relabeling of coordinates cannot polarize isolated, unrelaxed <spins>.

The <Barnett field> is a useful description of <spin-rotation coupling>, not a claim that empty laboratory space acquires a uniform <magnetic flux density> $\Omega/\gamma$. In particular, inserting it into the <Lorentz force> on an unrelated test <electric charge> is unjustified. A nearby <magnetometer> detects the specimen's actual stray <magnetic field> and backgrounds, rather than directly detecting this internal <Hamiltonian> parameter.

= Independent spin Barnett response
{c}
{parent=Barnett effect}
{title2=$\langle J_z\rangle=(\hbar/2)\tanh(\hbar\Omega/(2k_BT))$}

Consider an explicitly stipulated dilute <independent spin-one-half two-level system> with zero applied <magnetic field>. Each constituent has $J_z=\pm\hbar/2$ and $m_z=\gamma J_z$, with constant signed <gyromagnetic ratio> $\gamma$. Ignore interactions and the specimen's own <magnetic field>. At positive <temperature> $T$, <thermal equilibrium> with the rotating lattice gives <energy levels> $E_\pm=\mp\hbar\Omega/2$. Define the dimensionless variable $x=\hbar\Omega/(2k_BT)$, where $\hbar$ is the <reduced Planck constant> and $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant>. The <partition function> and <Boltzmann distribution> give
$$
Z=e^x+e^{-x}=2\cosh x,\qquad
p_+=\frac{e^x}{Z},\qquad p_-=\frac{e^{-x}}{Z}.
$$
Taking the <expected value> gives
$$
\langle J_z\rangle=\frac\hbar2\tanh x,\qquad
\langle m_z\rangle=\gamma\frac\hbar2\tanh x,\qquad
M_z=n\gamma\frac\hbar2\tanh x,
$$
where $n$ is <number density>. This is the rotational version of the <independent-moment magnetic response>. The <angular momentum> aligns with positive rotation even when the <magnetic moment> points oppositely. The <probability> ratio $p_+/p_-=e^{2x}$ provides a direct population check of the sign.

For $|x|\ll1$, expand the <hyperbolic tangent> to obtain
$$
M_z\simeq\frac{n\gamma\hbar^2}{4k_BT}\Omega
=\frac{n\gamma^2\hbar^2}{4k_BT}B_\Omega.
$$
The slope against $B_\Omega$ is positive for either sign of $\gamma$, while the slope against $\Omega$ has the sign of $\gamma$. At fixed $T>0$, reversing $\Omega$ reverses both means, and $\Omega=0$ gives zero. At fixed nonzero $\Omega$, the formal $T\to0^+$ limit saturates $|\langle J_z\rangle|$ at $\hbar/2$; the linear approximation must not be extrapolated there. At $T\to\infty$, the response vanishes. These limits describe the toy <canonical ensemble>, whose equilibration is assumed.

= Barnett response of a magnetic material
{c}
{parent=Barnett effect}

The two-state calculation is not a general formula for a <ferromagnet>. <Exchange interactions>, <magnetic domains>, <magnetic domain-wall pinning>, <magnetic anisotropy>, and <hysteresis> can determine which response is accessible. Both <spin angular momentum> and <orbital angular momentum> can contribute; a single constant <gyromagnetic ratio> may be only a restricted effective description. Rotation sets an internal bias, while the material and its preparation determine <magnetization>. The <Barkhausen effect> illustrates why a smooth bias can produce jumps instead of the toy smooth curve.

For a limited, reversible, isotropic linear model, suppose the same intrinsic <volume magnetic susceptibility> $\chi_v$ responds to the applied bias and to $B_\Omega/\mu_0$. For a uniformly magnetized <ellipsoid> along a principal axis, include its <demagnetizing factor> $N_d$:
$$
H_{\rm int}=H_{\rm app}-N_dM_z,\qquad
M_z=\chi_v\left(H_{\rm app}-N_dM_z+\frac{B_\Omega}{\mu_0}\right).
$$
Solving this <linear equation> yields
$$
M_z=\frac{\chi_v}{1+N_d\chi_v}
\left(H_{\rm app}+\frac{\Omega}{\mu_0\gamma}\right).
$$
Here $H_{\rm int}$ and $H_{\rm app}$ are actual <magnetic field intensities>; the added rotational bias is an equivalent drive in the constitutive response. In <SI>, dimensionless $\chi_v$ multiplies an intensity in $\mathrm{A/m}$, so $M_z=\chi_v\Omega/\gamma$ would omit $\mu_0$ and have the wrong units. A susceptibility defined instead as $\partial M/\partial B$ has different units. State the convention before comparing formulas.

At zero applied <magnetic field intensity>, a stipulated $\chi_v=3$ gives prefactor $3/2$ in a <sphere>, with $N_d=1/3$, and a prefactor approaching $3$ in a long axial <ellipsoid>. This is a geometry check of the assumed linear model. A real cylinder does not have an exactly uniform <demagnetizing field>, and an irreversible <ferromagnet> cannot in general be represented by this single linear equation. Inferring an intrinsic <gyromagnetic ratio> requires an independently calibrated material response and a geometry-dependent conversion of the measured stray <magnetic field>.

= Einstein-de Haas effect
{c}
{parent=Magnetic dipole moment}
{wiki=Einstein–de_Haas_effect}

The <Einstein-de Haas effect> is mechanical rotation or twisting caused by a change in a body's <magnetization>. The microscopic carriers of <magnetic dipole moment> also carry <angular momentum>: changing their net orientation can transfer <angular momentum> to the body. A freely suspended <ferromagnet> can therefore turn when its <magnetization> reverses. A suspension instead stores part of the response as an elastic twist. This makes a microscopic property accessible through a macroscopic <angle>.

The central measurement compares a change of <magnetic dipole moment> with transferred <angular momentum>, giving a <gyromagnetic ratio>. It complements an ordinary <magnetic dipole torque> measurement: even with the applied <magnetic field> parallel to the suspension axis, so that an ideal axial <magnetic dipole moment> has $\mathbf m\times\mathbf B=\mathbf0$, changing <magnetization> can drive an axial twist. The external drive supplies <energy>; <conservation of angular momentum> still requires accounting for the suspension and any external axial <torque>.

= Magnetomechanical angular momentum balance
{parent=Einstein-de Haas effect}
{title2=$\tau_{\rm EdH}=-\dot m_z/\gamma$}

Let $m_z=\int_V M_z\,dV$ be the axial <magnetic dipole moment>, where $M_z$ is the axial <magnetization>. Write $J_z$ for the internal <angular momentum> associated with that <magnetic dipole moment>, and $I\dot\theta$ for the body's rotational <angular momentum>, with <moment of inertia> $I$. In a regime admitting a constant signed <gyromagnetic ratio> $m_z=\gamma J_z$, axial <conservation of angular momentum> gives
$$
\frac{d}{dt}(I\dot\theta+J_z)=\tau_{\rm ext},
\qquad I\ddot\theta=\tau_{\rm ext}-\frac{\dot m_z}{\gamma},
\qquad \tau_{\rm EdH}=-\frac{\dot m_z}{\gamma}.
$$
Here $\tau_{\rm EdH}$ names the internal reaction <torque> on the body, not an additional external <torque>. The external term includes the suspension. The model assumes that the changing internal <angular momentum> is transferred to the measured mechanical motion, with other storage or escape channels negligible on the observation timescale. A changing or non-collinear mixture of <orbital angular momentum> and <spin angular momentum> need not admit a single constant $\gamma$.

During a reversal fast compared with the mechanical response, with negligible external angular <impulse>, integrating the balance gives
$$
\Delta L_{\rm body}=-\Delta J_z=-\frac{\Delta m_z}{\gamma}.
$$
For $m_z:+m_s\to-m_s$ and an electronic $\gamma<0$, the body acquires $\Delta L_{\rm body}=2m_s/\gamma<0$: its initial rotation is toward $-z$ by the <right-hand rule>. Reversing the reversal reverses the impulse. Over a complete periodic cycle, $\Delta m_z=0$, so the net reaction angular <impulse> vanishes; a resonant alternating response does not imply steadily accumulating <angular momentum>.

The <orbital angular momentum> prediction can be derived without a material model. A charge $q$ traveling around a circle of <radius> $r$ at <angular frequency> $\omega$ has loop <electric current> $q\omega/(2\pi)$ and <magnetic dipole moment> $m=q\omega r^2/2$. Its <orbital angular momentum> is $L=m_e\omega r^2$, hence, for an <electron> with <electric charge> $-e$,
$$
\mathbf m_L=-\frac{e}{2m_e}\mathbf L,
\qquad \gamma_L=-\frac{e}{2m_e}.
$$
The <electron magnetic moment> associated with intrinsic <spin> instead obeys
$$
\mathbf m_S=-g_s\frac{e}{2m_e}\mathbf S,
\qquad \gamma_S=-g_s\frac{e}{2m_e},\qquad g_s\simeq2.
$$
Here $e>0$ is the <elementary charge>, $m_e$ is the <electron mass>, and $g_s$ is the <electron spin g-factor>.<The magnetic moment of the electron by Polykarp Kusch>[pp. 299–300: orbital and intrinsic moments and the Dirac value]{ref} Thus the same change of <magnetic dipole moment> corresponds to about half as much transferred <angular momentum> in the pure-<spin> model as in the <orbital angular momentum> model. This factor of two is a relation between <magnetic dipole moment> and <angular momentum>, not evidence that an <electron> is literally a rotating charged sphere. In a material with both contributions, the inferred coefficient is a material-dependent magnetomechanical <gyromagnetic ratio>.

They compensated Earth's <magnetic field>, tilted the coils, and rotated the suspension attachment to test unwanted <magnetic dipole torques>. Their paper acknowledges a failed <phase> determination and claims only ten-percent precision for the revised measurement. Its agreement with the orbital <gyromagnetic ratio> prediction was not a direct discovery of <spin>.<Experimental proof of the existence of Ampère's molecular currents by Albert Einstein and Wander Johannes de Haas>[§6, pp. 706–707: phase failure; §7, pp. 707–711: resonance, precision]{ref} Comparing that orbital <gyromagnetic ratio> prediction with the <electron spin g-factor> explains why a successful qualitative demonstration of <angular momentum> transfer can coexist with an inadequate quantitative interpretation. The general control problem follows directly from the model. Unwanted <magnetic dipole torque> can drive the same mechanical <resonance> as the desired reaction <torque>. In the ideal geometry $m_z$ and $B_z$ alone produce no axial <cross product>, but transverse <magnetic dipole moments>, stray transverse <magnetic fields> and imperfect alignment can. Nulling stray <magnetic fields> and deliberately varying alignment test this contribution. Merely observing a large resonant twist cannot identify its cause.

For a nearly instantaneous magnetic response, a sinusoidal <magnetization> makes $m_z$ follow the drive while $-\dot m_z/\gamma$ is in <phase quadrature>. An ordinary magnetic contribution proportional to $m_z$ is then distinguishable by <phase>, after removing the mechanical <frequency response>. <Hysteresis> or delayed magnetic response can spoil this simple separation. The measured <magnetization>, rather than the coil <electric current> alone, must set the reference. Mechanical vibration, <magnetostriction> coupled through imperfect geometry, and changing suspension properties require separate checks; none is eliminated simply by reversing the coil <electric current>.

= Calibrated Einstein-de Haas torsion response
{c}
{parent=Einstein-de Haas effect}
{title2=$H_\tau(\omega)=(\kappa-I\omega^2+\mathrm i b\omega)^{-1}$}

An original reduced model makes the calibration explicit. Let the <torsion pendulum> have restoring <torque> $-\kappa\theta$, with <torsion constant> $\kappa$, and <viscous damping> <torque> $-b\dot\theta$, with $b>0$. Allow an unwanted axial <torque> $\tau_p(t)$:
$$
I\ddot\theta+b\dot\theta+\kappa\theta
=-\frac{\dot m_z}{\gamma}+\tau_p(t),\qquad
\omega_0=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{I}},\qquad
Q=\frac{I\omega_0}{b}.
$$
Here $\omega_0$ is the undamped natural <angular frequency> and $Q$ the <quality factor>. The <forced harmonic oscillator> response must be included before a twist can be interpreted as a <torque>.

Using the <complex amplitude> convention $m_z(t)=\operatorname{Re}(\widetilde m e^{\mathrm i\omega t})$ and similarly for $\theta$ and $\tau_p$, differentiation multiplies by $\mathrm i\omega$. Substitution gives
$$
H_\tau(\omega)=\frac{1}{\kappa-I\omega^2+\mathrm i b\omega},
\qquad
\widetilde\theta=H_\tau(\omega)
\left(-\frac{\mathrm i\omega}{\gamma}\widetilde m+\widetilde\tau_p\right).
$$
This is the torque-to-twist <transfer function>. With $\widetilde\tau_p=0$ and real positive $\widetilde m=m_1$, the twist <amplitude> is
$$
A_{\rm EdH}(\omega)=\frac{\omega m_1/|\gamma|}{\sqrt{(\kappa-I\omega^2)^2+(b\omega)^2}}.
$$
For $\gamma<0$, the twist <phase> relative to $m_z$ is $\pi/2-\operatorname{atan2}(b\omega,\kappa-I\omega^2)$. At $\omega=\omega_0$ it is zero, although the reaction <torque> leads $m_z$ by $\pi/2$. A <phase> measured at the mirror is not automatically the <phase> of the applied <torque>.

A fixed-<amplitude> reference <torque> $\tau_c\cos\omega t$ measures $|H_\tau|$ directly. The <Einstein-de Haas effect> instead contains an extra factor $\omega$ when $m_1$ stays fixed. Consequently its twist <amplitude> peaks exactly at $\omega_0$ in this model, whereas the fixed-<torque> peak is at $\omega_0\sqrt{1-1/(2Q^2)}$ when $Q>1/\sqrt2$. To verify the first statement, divide the squared denominator of $A_{\rm EdH}$ by $\omega^2$: the quantity to minimize is $(\kappa/\omega-I\omega)^2+b^2$, minimized at $\omega_0$. Both response curves tend to zero at high <frequency>; only the magnetomechanical curve also tends to zero at zero <frequency>.

For a fully stipulated example, choose $I=2.00\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}$, $\kappa=8.00\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm{N\,m/rad}$, $b=8.00\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N\,m\,s/rad}$, $\gamma=-1.76\times10^{11}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}\,T^{-1}}$, and $m_1=1.76\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{A\,m^2}$. These teaching inputs give $\omega_0=2.00\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, $Q=5.00$, reaction <torque> <amplitude> $2.00\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm{N\,m}$ at $\omega_0$, and
$$
A_{\rm EdH}(\omega_0)=\frac{m_1}{|\gamma|b}=1.25\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{rad}.
$$
An <optical lever> at distance $D=1.00\,\mathrm m$ gives small-displacement spot <amplitude> $x\simeq2DA=25.0\,\mu\mathrm m$; its full peak-to-peak width is $4DA=50.0\,\mu\mathrm m$. For a fast reversal from $+m_1$ to $-m_1$, the same inputs give $\Delta L_{\rm body}=-2.00\times10^{-15}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s}$ and initial $\Delta\dot\theta=-1.00\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{rad/s}$, provided the external angular <impulse> is negligible.

One independent mechanical calibration adds known <moment of inertia> $\Delta I$, keeping $\kappa$ unchanged. If $T_a,T_b$ are the undamped <oscillation periods> before and after adding it, then
$$
T_a^2=\frac{4\pi^2I}{\kappa},\qquad
T_b^2=\frac{4\pi^2(I+\Delta I)}{\kappa},\qquad
\kappa=\frac{4\pi^2\Delta I}{T_b^2-T_a^2}.
$$
For $\Delta I=I$ in the stipulated example, $T_a=\pi\,\mathrm s$ and $T_b=\sqrt2\pi\,\mathrm s$, recovering the chosen $I$ and $\kappa$. Real damped periods require a <damping> correction: a free decay has angular envelope $e^{-bt/(2I)}$ and oscillation <angular frequency> $\sqrt{\kappa/I-(b/(2I))^2}$. Measuring that decay determines $b/(2I)$. Calibrating the optical scale, mechanical response and change of <magnetic dipole moment> separately is what makes the inferred <gyromagnetic ratio> meaningful.

A <current loop> is a closed path carrying <electric current>. For a thin wire following a closed <curve> $C$, the <volume integral> defining its <magnetic dipole moment> becomes a <line integral>:
$$
\mathbf m=\frac I2\oint_C\mathbf r\times d\mathbf r=I\mathbf A,
\qquad
\mathbf A=\frac12\oint_C\mathbf r\times d\mathbf r.
$$
The <vector area> $\mathbf A$ is $A\widehat{\mathbf n}$ for a planar <current loop>. For a nonplanar <current loop>, use the <vector area>, rather than the scalar <area> of an arbitrarily chosen spanning surface. Reversing the <electric current> reverses $\mathbf m$ without changing the geometry. <Classical Electromagnetism by Richard Fitzpatrick/Localized Current Distribution>{ref}.

= Magnetic dipole torque
{parent=Magnetic dipole moment}
{title2=$\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf m\times\mathbf B_{\mathrm{ext}}$}

An externally imposed <magnetic field> that is nearly uniform over a rigid <current loop> produces the <torque>
$$
\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf m\times\mathbf B_{\mathrm{ext}},
\qquad
U=-\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf B_{\mathrm{ext}}=-mB\cos\theta.
$$
The <angle> $\theta$ is measured between the <magnetic dipole moment> and the external <magnetic field>, not between the plane of the <current loop> and the <magnetic field>. The <magnetic dipole potential energy> uses a <dot product>, whereas the <torque> uses a <cross product>. The <torque> is largest when the <magnetic dipole moment> is perpendicular to the <magnetic field>; parallel alignment minimizes the <potential energy>.<University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others/11.5 Force and Torque on a Current Loop>[§11.5, equations 11.22–11.23: vector torque and orientation energy]{ref}

For the sign convention $\mathbf B_{\mathrm{ext}}=B\widehat{\mathbf x}$ and $\mathbf m=m(\cos\theta\widehat{\mathbf x}+\sin\theta\widehat{\mathbf y})$, positive rotation increases $\theta$ and
$$
\tau_z=-mB\sin\theta=-\frac{dU}{d\theta}.
$$
Thus at $0<\theta<\pi$ the <torque> turns the <magnetic dipole moment> toward the external <magnetic field>. Both parallel and antiparallel alignment have zero <torque>, but they are respectively a <stable equilibrium> and an <unstable equilibrium>. With no <damping>, a freely released <current loop> can undergo <oscillation> around the <stable equilibrium>; settling requires <damping>.

In the twelve-turn example, take $B=0.020\,\mathrm T$ and $\theta=60\degree$. The predicted <torque> is $\tau_z=-2.35\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{N\,m}$ and the <magnetic dipole potential energy> is $U=-1.36\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm J$. The unit check is $(\mathrm{A\,m^2})\mathrm T=\mathrm{N\,m}=\mathrm J$; <torque> and <energy> have the same <physical dimensions> but different physical meanings.

A short derivation starts with the <magnetic force on a current-carrying wire>, $d\mathbf F=I\,d\mathbf r\times\mathbf B_{\mathrm{ext}}$. In an exactly uniform <magnetic field>, the net <force> is $I(\oint_Cd\mathbf r)\times\mathbf B_{\mathrm{ext}}=0$, but its <torque> need not vanish: opposite sides produce a turning <couple>. Integrating $\mathbf r\times d\mathbf F$ gives $\mathbf m\times\mathbf B_{\mathrm{ext}}$. Integrating $dU=-\tau_z\,d\theta$ at fixed $m$ and $B$ gives the orientation <potential energy>. This is the mechanical orientation <potential energy> for a maintained <electric current>, with the external source held fixed; the <energy> accounting for field and power supply is a separate problem. A varying external <magnetic field> requires corrections across a finite <current loop>, and can also give a net <force>.

The PHYWE guide <Magnetic moment in the magnetic field> makes the <magnetic dipole moment> measurable. The test <current loop> is mounted on a <torsion dynamometer>. The guide varies $I_H$, the number of turns, the test <electric current>, diameter, and the <angle> between the <magnetic dipole moment> and <magnetic field>. The leads hang loosely and are twisted together to suppress extra <torque>; the instrument zero is checked frequently because moving leads can shift it. The readout is a balance of <torques>. According to the manufacturer's <Torsion dynamometer 0.01 N operating instructions>, a metallic band supplies the restoring <torque>. where $N_H$ and $R_H$ are the winding count and radius of each field-producing <Helmholtz coil>, distinct from $N$ and $R$ of the test <current loop>. = Far-field approximation for a current loop
{parent=Magnetic dipole moment}
{title2=$\mathbf B\simeq\mu_0[3(\mathbf m\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})\widehat{\mathbf r}-\mathbf m]/(4\pi r^3)$}

Far outside a localized steady source of size $a$, at distance $r\gg a$, its <multipole expansion> starts with the <magnetic dipole moment> if that moment is nonzero. In <vacuum>, using <SI> units,
$$
\mathbf A_{\mathrm{dip}}(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{\mathbf m\times\mathbf r}{r^3},
\qquad
\mathbf B_{\mathrm{dip}}(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r^3}\left[3(\mathbf m\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})\widehat{\mathbf r}-\mathbf m\right],
$$
where $\mu_0$ is the <vacuum permeability>, $\widehat{\mathbf r}=\mathbf r/r$, and the <magnetic field> is the <curl> of the <magnetic vector potential>. The <magnetic dipole field> falls as $r^{-3}$, and the <magnetic vector potential> as $r^{-2}$. The <localized steady-current moment identities> remove the putative $r^{-1}$ term in the <magnetic vector potential>. No <magnetic monopole> is needed. If different <current loops> cancel the total <magnetic dipole moment>, a higher term of the <multipole expansion> supplies the leading distant <magnetic field>. <Classical Electromagnetism by Richard Fitzpatrick/Localized Current Distribution>{ref}.

The approximation is not valid at the center of a finite <current loop>. For a circular <current loop> of <radius> $R$ and <electric current> $I$, the exact <on-axis magnetic field of a circular current loop> and the distant approximation are
$$
B_z(z)=\frac{\mu_0IR^2}{2(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}},
\qquad
B_{z,\mathrm{dip}}(z)=\frac{\mu_0IR^2}{2|z|^3}.
$$
Their ratio is $B_z/B_{z,\mathrm{dip}}=(1+R^2/z^2)^{-3/2}$: at $|z|=10R$, the approximate <magnetic field> exceeds the exact <magnetic field> by about $1.50\%$ relative to the exact value. Near the source, retain its geometry rather than replacing it with a point source specified only by its <magnetic dipole moment>.

= Spin-orbit g-factor ratio from two P states
{parent=Electron magnetic moment}
{title2=$g_s/g_L=(4R-2)/(R+1)$}

An original reduced model makes the cancellation explicit. Assume <LS coupling>, neglect nuclear <spin>, and write the electronic <magnetic dipole moment> as $-\mu_B(g_L\mathbf L+g_s\mathbf S)/\hbar$. For $L=1$, $S=1/2$, the projection used to derive the <Landé g-factor> gives
$$
g_{3/2}=\frac{2g_L+g_s}{3},\qquad
g_{1/2}=\frac{4g_L-g_s}{3}.
$$
These coefficients follow by substituting $J=3/2$ or $1/2$ into the angular-momentum projection formula; neither level measures pure <spin> alone. In a weak uniform <magnetic field>, adjacent electronic projections have spacing $h f_J=g_J\mu_B B$ when $g_J>0$. Therefore
$$
R=\frac{f_{3/2}}{f_{1/2}}=\frac{g_{3/2}}{g_{1/2}}
=\frac{2+x}{4-x},\qquad x=\frac{g_s}{g_L},\qquad
\boxed{x=\frac{4R-2}{R+1}.}
$$
The common <magnetic field> and <Bohr magneton> cancel. The ratio determines $g_s/g_L$, not $g_s$ without further knowledge of $g_L$. For $g_L=1$, it gives $a_e=(R-2)/(R+1)$. Small departures from $R=2$ are magnified differently from small departures of $g_s$ from $2$; the two fractional changes should not be identified.

With the stipulated inputs $g_L=1$, $g_s=2.0024$ and $\mu_BB/h=100\,\mathrm{MHz}$, the two electronic frequencies are $66.5867$ and $133.4133\,\mathrm{MHz}$. Their ratio is approximately $2.0036043$, and inversion recovers $a_e=0.0012$. These are ideal electronic lines, not historical <gallium> data. Nuclear <spin>, mixing between levels and other deviations from <LS coupling> require a more complete model in an actual atom.

= Drift cancellation in a g-factor comparison
{parent=Spin-orbit g-factor ratio from two P states}

Even a shared field does not cancel if it is sampled at different times. Write $f_A(t)=g_A C(t)$ and $f_B(t)=g_B C(t)$, where $C(t)=\mu_BB(t)/h$. As an original proposed diagnostic, bracket the $B$-state reading at $t=0$ with $A$-state readings at $t=\pm\tau$:
$$
R_{\rm bracket}=
\frac{f_B(0)}{\tfrac12[f_A(-\tau)+f_A(+\tau)]}.
$$
If $C(t)=C_0(1+bt)$, the denominator is exactly $g_A C_0$, so $R_{\rm bracket}=g_B/g_A$. A single earlier $A$ reading instead gives $R_{\rm unpaired}=(g_B/g_A)/(1-b\tau)$. For the preceding numerical model and $b\tau=0.001$, this unpaired comparison falsely gives approximately $a_e=0.00186649$; interpolation recovers $0.0012$. The sequence illustrates cancellation and is not asserted to be Kusch and Foley's exact historical timing protocol.

= Magnetic field of a rotating charged spherical body
{parent=Magnetic dipole moment of a rigidly rotating charge distribution}

$$

= Biot-Savart law
{parent=Electromagnetism}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$d\mathbf B=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\frac{d\boldsymbol\ell'\times\widehat{\mathbf R}}{R^2}$}

For a steady <current density> $J$, the <magnetic field> is
$$
B(r)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int
\frac{J(r')\times(r-r')}{|r-r'|^3}\,d^3r'.
$$

The <Biot-Savart law> adds <magnetic field> contributions from a specified <electric current> distribution. The <vectors> $\mathbf r'$ and $\mathbf r$ locate source and observer; $\mathbf r-\mathbf r'$ points toward the observer. Here $\mu_0$ is <vacuum permeability>. For a thin wire carrying <electric current> $I$, the <volume integral> becomes a <line integral>:
$$
\mathbf B(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}
\int_C\frac{d\boldsymbol\ell'\times(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')}
{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|^3},\qquad
 d\mathbf B=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{I\,d\boldsymbol\ell'\times\widehat{\mathbf R}}{R^2},
\quad \mathbf R=\mathbf r-\mathbf r'.
$$
Here $d\boldsymbol\ell'$ follows positive <conventional current>; $\widehat{\mathbf R}$ is the <unit vector> toward the observer. With fixed path orientation, reversing the <electric current> replaces $I$ by $-I$. The <cross product> and <right-hand rule> fix the direction. Reversing the source-to-observer displacement would give the wrong sign.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume II by Richard Feynman and others>[II.14–6, equations (14.37)–(14.38); II.14–7, equations (14.42)–(14.43), including the cross-product order]{ref}

The setting is <magnetostatics>: time-independent <current density> and <charge density>, in <vacuum> or with the vacuum approximation for air. <Electric charge conservation> then gives $\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0$. A finite segment is part of a complete <electrical circuit>; its endpoints cannot continuously accumulate <electric charge> in a steady state. The instantaneous integral above does not describe switching transients or <electromagnetic radiation>. In magnetic matter, specify the total <current density>, including <magnetization current>, rather than automatically replacing $\mu_0$ by an arbitrary <magnetic permeability>.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume II by Richard Feynman and others>[II.13–4, equations (13.12)–(13.13), steady-current and closed-circuit discussion; II.13–5, internal currents in magnetic matter]{ref}

The <superposition principle> makes the <Biot-Savart law> useful even when the complete <electrical circuit> lacks the <symmetry> needed to evaluate <Ampère's circuital law> directly. Integrate a straight piece, a curved piece, and the return path separately, then add their <magnetic fields> as <vectors>. The beauty is that a local oriented <current element> produces both the circulating <magnetic field of a finite straight wire> and the reinforcing <on-axis magnetic field of a circular current loop>.

An actual laboratory procedure is documented in WPI Physics Department's https://users.wpi.edu/~kwu/Teaching/PH2651/biot-savart-law_MField_2017.pdf[Magnetic Field Mapping], §§1.2–1.2.3 and the single-coil checklist on p. 9. Preparation locates the active area of a <Hall effect> sensor using a small magnet. A field meter reads the component normal to that area. The wire apparatus is a wall-mounted square loop selectable between one and five conductors. The coil connects to a power supply and <ammeter>; its diameter and turn count are recorded. Align the sensor and scan distance. The control is a current-off background reading at each position, subtracted from the current-on reading. The inference compares corrected <magnetic field> with position and the predicted geometry dependence. Active-area displacement, sensor tilt, background variation, and finite coil width limit that comparison. These are instructions, not reported measurements.<Magnetic Field Mapping by WPI Physics Department>[pp. 2–5, §§1.2–1.2.3: preparation, sensor orientation, wire background subtraction and single-coil apparatus; p. 9, single-coil checklist]{ref}

A further proposed control is to reverse $I$ at fixed sensor position and orientation. If the background and meter offset stay unchanged, the odd part isolates the <magnetic field> from the <electric current>:
$$
\frac{B_{\rm read}(+I)-B_{\rm read}(-I)}2=B_{\rm current}.
$$
This is a deduction from the <superposition principle>, not a reported WPI measurement. The measured component, sensor position, and complete return path must still match the model.

= Magnetic-field cancellation of an arc and tangent leads
{parent=Biot-Savart law}
{title2=$\theta(h)=2\sqrt{1+(h/R)^2}$}

For a circular arc of radius $R$ carrying clockwise <electric current> $I$ and subtending angle $\theta$, with two tangent semi-infinite leads extending on the crossing side of the arc, the axial <magnetic field> at height $h$ above the centre is
$$
B_z(h)=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi R}\left[\frac2{1+(h/R)^2}-\frac{\theta}{[1+(h/R)^2]^{3/2}}\right].
$$
The crossing is insulated. The positive term is from the two leads and the negative term from the arc, with $+z$ out of the clockwise-current plane. The <Biot-Savart law> yields the lead integral $\int_0^\infty R(R^2+h^2+s^2)^{-3/2}\,ds=R/(R^2+h^2)$. Cancellation of the axial component gives the title formula, expanding to $\theta=2+h^2/R^2+O(h^4/R^4)$ in <radians>. This is cancellation of one component away from the plane, not necessarily the entire <magnetic field>.

= Current element
{parent=Biot-Savart law}
{title2=$I\,d\boldsymbol\ell'$}

A <current element> is the oriented infinitesimal wire contribution $I\,d\boldsymbol\ell'$ inside the <Biot-Savart law>. For uniform axial <current density> across <wire cross-sectional area> $A$, $I=JA$ and $d^3r'=A\,d\ell'$ imply $\mathbf J\,d^3r'=I\,d\boldsymbol\ell'$. This reduces a <volume integral> to a <line integral> in the thin-wire approximation. It is an integration contribution, not an isolated steady source. <Conventional current> runs opposite the drift of negatively charged <electrons>.

= Magnetic field of a finite straight wire
{parent=Biot-Savart law}
{title2=$\mathbf B=-\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi a}\left[\frac{s}{\sqrt{a^2+s^2}}\right]_{s_1}^{s_2}\widehat{\mathbf z}$}

Take a wire segment on the $y$ axis, with source positions $\mathbf r'=(0,s,0)$ for $s_1\le s\le s_2$, and positive <electric current> along $+y$. Observe at $\mathbf r=(a,0,0)$, where $a>0$ is the perpendicular distance to the wire's supporting line. Choose $x$ rightward and $y$ upward, so $+z$ points out of the page. Then
$$
d\boldsymbol\ell'=\widehat{\mathbf y}\,ds,\qquad
\mathbf R=a\widehat{\mathbf x}-s\widehat{\mathbf y},\qquad
d\boldsymbol\ell'\times\mathbf R=-a\widehat{\mathbf z}\,ds.
$$
Thus the <magnetic field> points into the page by the <right-hand rule>. The <Biot-Savart law> and an elementary <antiderivative> give
$$
\mathbf B=-\frac{\mu_0 I a}{4\pi}\widehat{\mathbf z}
\int_{s_1}^{s_2}\frac{ds}{(a^2+s^2)^{3/2}}
=-\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi a}
\left[\frac{s}{\sqrt{a^2+s^2}}\right]_{s_1}^{s_2}\widehat{\mathbf z}.
$$
For an original worked example, choose $s_1=-0.12\,\mathrm m$, $s_2=0.12\,\mathrm m$, $a=0.05\,\mathrm m$, and $I=3\,\mathrm A$. Use $\mu_0\approx4\pi\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\,m/A}$ for these rounded calculations. Each endpoint contributes $0.12/0.13=12/13$, so
$$
\mathbf B=-\frac{(10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\,m/A})(3\,\mathrm A)}{0.05\,\mathrm m}
\frac{24}{13}\,\widehat{\mathbf z}
\approx-11.08\,\mu\mathrm T\,\widehat{\mathbf z}.
$$
These are calculated inputs and output, not laboratory data. The two endpoint <angles> measured from the perpendicular each have <sine> $12/13$; this recovers the equivalent endpoint-angle formula without depending on a diagram's angle-sign convention.

For a segment centered on the perpendicular foot, $s_1=-L$, $s_2=L$. Its <magnetic field> magnitude is
$$
B=\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi a}\frac{L}{\sqrt{a^2+L^2}}.
$$
The <limit> $L/a\to\infty$ gives $B_\infty=\mu_0I/(2\pi a)$, consistent with <Ampère's circuital law> for an infinite straight wire. In the example, $B_\infty=12\,\mu\mathrm T$ and $B/B_\infty=12/13$: the infinite-wire value overestimates the segment value by $1/12$, or about $8.33\%$ of the segment value. For $a\gg L$, the isolated segment contribution instead behaves as $B\sim\mu_0I(2L)/(4\pi a^2)$. The complete localized <electrical circuit> has a different far-field behavior: return-path contributions cancel this leading segment term, leaving a <magnetic dipole field> when its <magnetic dipole moment> is nonzero. Segment and whole-circuit limits must be distinguished.

The <SI> units check directly: $\mu_0I/a$ has units $(\mathrm{T\,m/A})\mathrm A/\mathrm m=\mathrm T$, the <tesla (unit)>. The apparent divergence as $a\to0$ is a failure of the thin-wire model near the conductor, not a prediction of infinite <magnetic field> inside a real wire. This calculation gives only this segment's contribution; include the return wires before comparing with an apparatus.

= On-axis magnetic field of a circular current loop
{parent=Biot-Savart law}
{title2=$B_z(z)=\frac{\mu_0IR^2}{2(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}$}

A circular loop of radius $R$ carrying <electric current> $I$ has, on its axis,
$$
B(z)=\frac{\mu_0IR^2}{2(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\,\hat z,
$$
where the sign of $\hat z$ is fixed by the <right-hand rule>. At its centre, $B(0)=\mu_0I/(2R)$.

Put the loop in the $xy$ plane, centered at the origin, with positive <electric current> counterclockwise when viewed from $+z$. The <right-hand rule> then gives the central <magnetic field> along $+z$. Parameterize source positions by the <angle> $\phi$ and observe at $(0,0,z)$:
$$
\mathbf r'=(R\cos\phi,R\sin\phi,0),\qquad
d\boldsymbol\ell'=R(-\sin\phi,\cos\phi,0)\,d\phi,
\qquad \mathbf R=(-R\cos\phi,-R\sin\phi,z).
$$
Here $R$ without bold type is the loop <radius>, whereas $\mathbf R$ is the source-to-observer displacement. Their <cross product> is
$$
d\boldsymbol\ell'\times\mathbf R
=(Rz\cos\phi,Rz\sin\phi,R^2)\,d\phi.
$$
The denominator $|\mathbf R|^3=(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}$ is constant around the loop. The <integrals> of <sine> and <cosine> over a full turn vanish, while the axial contribution adds to $2\pi R^2$. Substitution into the <Biot-Savart law> proves the on-axis formula. <Symmetry> cancels transverse components; it does not cancel the axial components.

For an original one-turn example in the same vacuum approximation, choose $R=0.04\,\mathrm m$ and $I=2\,\mathrm A$. With $\mu_0\approx4\pi\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{T\,m/A}$,
$$
B(0)=10\pi\,\mu\mathrm T\approx31.42\,\mu\mathrm T,\qquad
\frac{B(z)}{B(0)}=\left(1+\frac{z^2}{R^2}\right)^{-3/2}.
$$
At $z=0.03\,\mathrm m$, the source-to-observer distance is $0.05\,\mathrm m$, so $B(z)/B(0)=64/125=0.512$ and $B(z)\approx16.08\,\mu\mathrm T$. At $z=-0.03\,\mathrm m$, the <magnetic field> has the same magnitude and still points along $+z$. The point has crossed the loop plane, but the loop's <electric current> has not reversed. Reversing the <electric current> reverses the <magnetic field> on both sides.

For $|z|\ll R$, a <Taylor expansion> gives $B(z)/B(0)=1-3z^2/(2R^2)+O(z^4/R^4)$: the center is a smooth maximum, with zero first <derivative>. For $|z|\gg R$,
$$
\mathbf B(z)\sim\frac{\mu_0IR^2}{2|z|^3}\widehat{\mathbf z}
=\frac{\mu_0m}{2\pi|z|^3}\widehat{\mathbf z},\qquad
m=I\pi R^2.
$$
This is the axial <magnetic dipole field> of <magnetic dipole moment> $\mathbf m=I\pi R^2\widehat{\mathbf z}$.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume II by Richard Feynman and others>[II.14–5, equations (14.33)–(14.36): current times loop area and magnetic dipole field]{ref} A compact closed loop has an inverse-cube axial far field, in contrast to the inverse-distance field of the infinite straight wire. The <SI> units of the exact loop formula are $\mu_0IR^2/(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}\sim\mu_0I/\text{length}$, hence <tesla (unit)>.

For $N$ coincident turns carrying the same <electric current>, the <superposition principle> multiplies the ideal result by $N$. A real coil has radial width and axial <length>: if those are not negligible, integrate or sum over its individual turns. This is why a single measured coil diameter and a turn count may not determine the full spatial <magnetic field> precisely. A collection of loops becomes a <solenoid>; integrating their axial contributions gives the <on-axis magnetic field of a finite solenoid>.

<Diamagnetism> is a magnetic response in which the induced <magnetization> opposes the applied <magnetic field intensity>. In a linear isotropic description, $\mathbf M=\chi\mathbf H$ with negative <magnetic susceptibility> $\chi$.

= Langevin diamagnetic susceptibility
{parent=Diamagnetism}
{c}
{title2=$\chi=-\mu_0 nZe^2\langle r^2\rangle/(6m_e)$}

For identical isotropic closed-shell atoms of <number density> $n$, with $Z$ electrons and mean squared radius $\langle r^2\rangle$ per electron, the quadratic <magnetic field> energy is $Ze^2B^2\langle r^2\rangle/(12m_e)$. It follows from the $e^2\mathbf A^2/(2m_e)$ term with $\mathbf A=\mathbf B\times\mathbf r/2$. Differentiating the energy with respect to field and using $B\simeq\mu_0H$ yields the negative SI volume <magnetic susceptibility>. For <helium>, $Z=2$; combining it with the independent spin-one-half nuclear response $\mu_0n\mu_a^2/(k_BT)$ gives a density-independent sign-change scale $T_0=3m_e\mu_a^2/(e^2k_B\langle r^2\rangle)$.

= Larmor-Langevin susceptibility
{c}
{parent=Diamagnetism}
{title2=$\chi=-\mu_0ne^2\langle r^2\rangle/(6m)$}

The dimensionless SI <volume magnetic susceptibility> of number <density> $n$ of independent spherical bound <electrons> with <mass> $m$ and electron-weighted <mean-square orbital radius>. In a uniform <magnetic field>, the quadratic minimal-coupling <Hamiltonian> gives $\Delta E=e^2B^2\langle x^2+y^2\rangle/(8m)$. Spherical symmetry gives $\langle x^2+y^2\rangle=2\langle r^2\rangle/3$; differentiating twice with respect to $B$ gives the displayed negative response. Atomic orbitals use the bare <electron mass>, whereas a shallow donor envelope within an <effective mass> approximation uses that envelope's <effective mass>.

= Diamagnetic levitation in an inverse-height field

A small weakly susceptible <sphere> has induced <magnetic energy> $-\chi VB^2/(2\mu_0)$ and vertical <force> $\chi VBB'/\mu_0$. In field magnitude $B=B_0r_0/z$, balancing <gravity> gives the title height for $\chi<0$. The vertical <force> varies as $z^{-3}$, giving a restoring derivative at that equilibrium. = Magnetic order
{parent=Magnetism}

<Magnetic order> is a reproducible spatial organization of microscopic <magnetic moments>. A net <magnetization> describes their sum; a <staggered magnetization> describes an alternating pattern. These are different <order parameters>. For example, an alternating row of equal up and down arrows has zero sum but a definite pattern, whereas randomly oriented arrows can have a small sum without that pattern.

= Magnetic sublattice
{parent=Magnetic order}

A <magnetic sublattice> groups sites with a common ordered <magnetic moment> in a description of <magnetic order>. In the simplest two-sublattice description, assign <magnetizations> $\mathbf M_A$ and $\mathbf M_B$ per total specimen <volume>, so that
$$
\mathbf M=\mathbf M_A+\mathbf M_B,\qquad
\mathbf L=\mathbf M_A-\mathbf M_B.
$$
Here $\mathbf L$ is one normalization of the <staggered magnetization>; conventions may include a factor $1/2$. Using the same total <volume> for both contributions is essential. Equal <magnetic moment> per site does not imply equal sublattice contributions if the site populations differ.

= Magnetic neutron diffraction
{parent=Magnetic order}

<Magnetic neutron diffraction> uses scattering of <neutrons> by ordered <magnetic moments> to infer <magnetic order>. <Neutrons> also scatter from <atomic nuclei>, so a pattern can contain nuclear and magnetic contributions. A <Bragg scattering> peak encodes periodicity; it is not a photograph of an individual <spin>.

A minimal original calculation makes the extra periodicity visible. Consider identical nuclear scatterers at positions $x_n=na$, with equal neighboring separation $a$, and ordered <magnetic moments> alternating between $+m_A$ and $-m_B$. A two-site repeating block of <length> $2a$ has nuclear and magnetic <scattering amplitudes>, apart from common geometric factors,
$$
F_N(q)=b(1+e^{iqa}),\qquad
F_M(q)=m_A-m_Be^{iqa},\qquad
q=\frac{\pi h}{a},\quad h\in\mathbb Z.
$$
Here $b$ is the common nuclear scattering coefficient and $q$ the component of the scattering <wave vector> along the row. The magnetic expression assumes equally scattering point <magnetic moments> perpendicular to the scattering <wave vector>; real magnetic scattering measures the perpendicular component and has a <momentum>-dependent amplitude. This one-dimensional calculation is a periodicity illustration, not a finite-<temperature> solution for a chain.

For even $h$, $F_N=2b$ and $F_M=m_A-m_B$. For odd $h$, $F_N=0$ and $F_M=m_A+m_B$. Thus equivalent opposing <magnetic sublattices>, $m_A=m_B$, cancel in forward scattering but reinforce at the odd positions of the doubled period. The <intensity (physics)> is proportional to the squared magnitude of the <scattering amplitude>, so changing an amplitude's sign does not make a negative <intensity (physics)>.

Extra magnetic peaks can therefore occur in a <ferrimagnet> too. = Magnetic structure factor
{parent=Magnetic neutron diffraction}

The <structure factor> for <magnetic neutron diffraction> sums the <magnetic form factor> and the <magnetic moment> component perpendicular to the <scattering vector> over the magnetic <atomic basis>. For unpolarized <neutrons>, the magnetic contribution to the coherent <diffraction> <wave intensity> is proportional to $|\mathbf F_M|^2$. A periodic change of sign of <magnetic moments> can shift its allowed peaks away from the nuclear <reciprocal lattice vectors> without changing nuclear positions.

{parent=Magnetic neutron diffraction}

Their Fig. 1 records scattered-neutron <intensity (physics)> versus counter angle. They indexed these with a magnetic <unit cell> having twice the chemical cell edge. Samuel Smart>[Shull and Smart, Detection of Antiferromagnetism by Neutron Diffraction (1949), Fig. 1 and accompanying paragraphs; original reprint inspected at Carnegie Mellon University]{ref}

The source was Oak Ridge's graphite-moderated reactor. In Early Development of Neutron Scattering (1994), <Clifford Shull> describes how the early two-axis instruments used a monochromating <crystal> to select the incident-neutron <wavelength>; a shielded neutron counter rotated around the specimen to record angular <intensity (physics)>. The connection to the measured angle is the <elastic Bragg scattering condition>. If the incident and outgoing <wave vectors> have equal magnitude $2\pi/\lambda$ and make an angle $2\theta$, their difference has magnitude

$$
A longer real-space period supplies additional, smaller <reciprocal lattice> spacings, which can put magnetic reflections at new counter angles. = Exchange interaction
{parent=Magnetism}
{wiki}

The <exchange interaction> is an effective interaction between <spins> originating in the quantum mechanics of <electrons>. Its sign convention must accompany any formula. For the illustrative classical fixed-length <spin> pair used here, write
$$
E=J\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2=JS^2\cos\theta.
$$
With this plus-sign convention, $J>0$ favors antiparallel <spins>: $E(\pi)=-JS^2$, compared with $E(0)=+JS^2$. The <energy> difference is $2JS^2$. If instead the <Hamiltonian> is written with an overall minus sign, the same physical preference has a negative coupling. This prevents a sign convention in the <Ising model> from being mistaken for a disagreement about the ordering.

= Indirect exchange
{parent=Exchange interaction}

Magnetic moments can interact through intermediate orbitals or itinerant carriers rather than direct overlap alone. <Superexchange> uses virtual charge transfer, the <RKKY interaction> uses conduction-electron spin response, and <double exchange> gains carrier kinetic energy through spin-dependent hopping. More than one mechanism can operate in the same solid, with different signs and ranges.

= RKKY interaction
{parent=Indirect exchange}
{c}

A local moment induces conduction-electron spin polarization, which mediates a second-order coupling to another moment. The static electronic spin susceptibility fixes the spatial interaction. For a three-dimensional spherical Fermi surface its leading large-distance form oscillates as $\cos(2k_FR)/R^3$, up to the overall sign convention and prefactor. The sign alternates with separation. Other band structures and dimensions change this form. <Indirect Exchange Coupling of Nuclear Magnetic Moments by Conduction Electrons by M. A. Ruderman and Charles Kittel>[1954, original conduction-electron-mediated magnetic coupling]{ref}

= Exponential orbital-tail estimate of exchange
{parent=Exchange interaction}
{title2=$|t(d)|\sim P(d)e^{-\kappa d},\quad J\sim4|t|^2/U$}

For localized orbitals with an exponential tail $e^{-\kappa r}$, overlap-mediated hopping over separation $d$ inherits an exponentially small factor $e^{-\kappa d}$, multiplied by geometry-dependent polynomial factors. A kinetic-exchange scale then falls approximately as $e^{-2\kappa d}$. Comparisons must use actual nearest-neighbour distances, not interchange <lattice> constants between different crystal structures. The estimate is not a replacement for a screened solid-state orbital calculation.

= Conduction-electron-mediated exchange
{parent=Exchange interaction}

A localized <magnetic moment> can polarize a metal's <conduction electrons>, whose response changes the energy of a second moment. This indirect <exchange interaction> can be long ranged and oscillate in sign with separation because of the <Fermi surface>. In the three-dimensional isotropic free-electron asymptotic limit its range dependence is proportional to $\cos(2k_Fr)/r^3$, with coupling-dependent prefactor and sign convention. The original calculation for nuclear moments used their contact <hyperfine interaction> with conduction electrons; it was motivated by nuclear magnetic resonance line widths in metallic silver.<Indirect Exchange Coupling of Nuclear Magnetic Moments by Conduction Electrons by M. A. Ruderman and Charles Kittel>[1954 abstract and introduction: second-order hyperfine coupling, indirect spin interaction and broad natural-silver resonance lines]{ref}

= Double exchange
{synonym}

Double exchange couples localized magnetic moments through actual carrier hopping between sites of different valence. Strong local <Hund's coupling> makes hopping energetically easier when neighboring core spins align, lowering the carrier <kinetic energy>. In the classical strong-coupling limit, a common effective hopping is $t_{\rm eff}=t\cos(\theta/2)$ for relative spin angle $\theta$; aligned cores maximize its magnitude. Unlike insulating <superexchange>, this mechanism directly connects carrier motion and magnetic alignment.<Interaction between the d-Shells in the Transition Metals. II. = Coulomb exchange splitting of two orbitals
{c}
{parent=Exchange interaction}
{title2=$E_{\pm}=\epsilon_a+\epsilon_b+J\pm K$}

Let $a,b$ be distinct orthonormal spatial orbitals occupied by two identical <spin-one-half> particles, with a weak repulsive <Coulomb interaction>. The symmetric and antisymmetric spatial combinations have first-order <energy> shifts $J+K$ and $J-K$, where $J$ is the direct Coulomb integral and
$$
K=g\int d^3r\,d^3r'\,\frac{a^*(\mathbf r)b(\mathbf r)b^*(\mathbf r')a(\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|},\qquad g>0.
$$
The Coulomb kernel has positive <Fourier transform> $4\pi/|\mathbf k|^2$, so $K\ge0$. <Fermionic exchange symmetry> pairs the symmetric spatial function with the <spin-one-half singlet state> and the antisymmetric one with the <spin-one-half triplet state>. For these fixed orbitals the triplet lies lower by $2K$ when $K>0$. This does not compare arbitrary singlets and triplets with different orbital occupations; for negligible orbital overlap the splitting can vanish.

= Superexchange
{parent=Exchange interaction}
{wiki}

<Superexchange> couples localized moments through virtual hopping via intermediate, often nonmagnetic, ligand orbitals. Half-filled orbitals sharing an effective hopping amplitude $t$ give an antiferromagnetic scale $J\simeq4|t|^2/U$ in the repulsive one-band limit. Ligand geometry, orbital occupation and exchange within intermediate states can change the balance and allow ferromagnetic coupling. Real charge motion can remain gapped while these virtual processes couple an insulating magnet's spins.

= Three-site charge-transfer superexchange
{parent=Superexchange}
{title2=$J\sim4t_{pd}^4/(\Delta^2U_d)$}

In a minimal <hole> description of a metal–ligand–metal path, two <holes> initially occupy separate metal orbitals and the ligand has no <hole>. Creating a virtual ligand <hole> costs a charge-transfer <energy> $\Delta$. Two hops generate an effective metal–metal hopping of order $t_{pd}^2/\Delta$; two more hops through metal <double occupancy> generate the antiferromagnetic scale in the title. Ligand <double occupancy> adds a channel with denominator $2\Delta+U_p$. Orbital geometry and intra-atomic exchange determine when the antiferromagnetic channel dominates.

= Direct exchange
{parent=Exchange interaction}

<Direct exchange> comes from Coulomb matrix elements between overlapping localized electronic orbitals together with the antisymmetry required by the <Pauli exclusion principle>. It produces a spin-dependent energy without being a classical dipole interaction. Its competition with kinetic processes and orbital occupation helps determine whether neighbouring moments prefer parallel or antiparallel alignment.

= Direct Coulomb exchange versus kinetic exchange
{parent=Direct exchange}

A direct intersite Coulomb exchange integral acts without an intermediate doubly occupied state. In a repulsive one-band <Hubbard model>, by contrast, virtual hopping into a doubly occupied state lowers the singlet <energy> by $4t^2/U$ relative to the triplet. The latter is antiferromagnetic kinetic exchange, even when hopping is directly between neighbouring magnetic orbitals. Ligand-mediated <superexchange> introduces an intermediate ligand orbital as well.

= Antiferromagnetism
{parent=Magnetism}
{wiki}

= Antiferromagnet
{synonym}

= Antiferromagnetic
{synonym}

<Antiferromagnetism> is ordered compensation of <magnetic moments>. In the collinear two-sublattice model, $\mathbf M_A=-\mathbf M_B\ne0$: net <magnetization> vanishes but <staggered magnetization> remains. <Louis Néel> developed this possibility.<Magnetism and the local molecular field by Louis Néel>[§5, pp.324–326: opposing ordered sublattices]{ref}

For an original local <energy> check, perturb the antiparallel pair in <exchange interaction> by writing $\theta=\pi-\delta$. Then
$$
E=-JS^2\cos\delta=-JS^2+\frac12JS^2\delta^2+O(\delta^4).
$$
For $J>0$, a small relative rotation costs positive <energy>. This verifies a pair's preference; it does not prove a bulk ordering <temperature>. A whole <crystal> must satisfy many bonds simultaneously, and <thermal fluctuations> and quantum effects matter. The <Heisenberg antiferromagnet> and <antiferromagnetic Ising model> specify different assumptions about those collective degrees of freedom.

Why is the distinction useful? A measurement of zero <magnetization> cannot decide between an ordered alternating arrangement, a disordered ensemble, and oppositely magnetized <magnetic domains>. A spatial probe such as <magnetic neutron diffraction> tests the pattern directly. The <manganese monoxide neutron diffraction experiment> is a particularly clear example: the order adds a new periodic scattering signal even though the net <magnetic moment> cancels.

= Néel temperature
{c}
{parent=Antiferromagnetism}
{title2=$T_N$}
{wiki}

The <Néel temperature> marks loss of long-range <antiferromagnetism>. A <Curie temperature> concerns <ferromagnetism> or <ferrimagnetism>; <magnetic compensation temperature> instead denotes cancellation within an ordered <ferrimagnet>.<Magnetism and the local molecular field by Louis Néel>[§5, pp.324–326; §§8,11–12: ordering versus compensation]{ref}

In the simple collinear description, compare $|\mathbf L|$ across $T_N$, rather than expecting $|\mathbf M|$ to switch from nonzero to zero. Zero-field <magnetization> may be zero on both sides. A nonzero <magnetic susceptibility> is also compatible with <antiferromagnetism>: an applied <magnetic field> can disturb the cancellation. The <uniform response of a two-sublattice Ising antiferromagnet> provides a model calculation.

= Ferrimagnetism
{parent=Magnetism}
{wiki}

= Ferrimagnet
{synonym}

<Ferrimagnetism> has opposing, generally unequal ordered <magnetic sublattices>, with net <magnetization> $|M_A-M_B|$ in a collinear model. <Louis Néel> used it to explain magnetic oxides.<Magnetism and the local molecular field by Louis Néel>[§8, pp.328–330: unequal sublattice contributions]{ref}

The counting example in <magnetic sublattice> gives $3(2m_0)-3(m_0)=3m_0$. Unequal populations also suffice: four sites carrying $+m_0$ and two carrying $-m_0$ give $2m_0$, even with identical individual magnitudes. Thus one must compare total sublattice contributions per common <volume>, rather than merely asking whether two atomic species carry different <magnetic moments>.

Both <ferromagnetism> and <ferrimagnetism> can produce a macroscopic magnet. Their internal organization differs: the latter retains opposing contributions. This makes <ferrimagnetism> a useful bridge between net <magnetization> and microscopic <magnetic order>: reducing one opposing contribution can increase the net sum, even though the total sum of moment magnitudes decreases.

= Magnetic compensation temperature
{parent=Ferrimagnetism}
{title2=$T_{\rm comp}$}

= Magnetization compensation temperature
{synonym}

At a <magnetic compensation temperature>, $M_A=M_B\ne0$: an ordered <ferrimagnet> has zero net <magnetization>. Such cancellation need not occur in every material.<Magnetism and the local molecular field by Louis Néel>[§8, Fig.7; §§11–12, pp.335–338: cancellation within an ordered phase]{ref}

For an original stipulated example, let $t=T/T_C$, with $0\le t\le1$, and prescribe sublattice magnitudes per common <volume> by
$$
M_A(t)=M_0(1-t),\qquad M_B(t)=\frac32M_0(1-t)^2,\qquad M_0>0.
$$
Take A along $+z$ and B along $-z$, retaining a fixed sublattice orientation convention. These are toy functions, not measured curves or a self-consistent <mean-field approximation>. Their difference factors exactly:
$$
M_z(t)=M_A-M_B=\frac{M_0}{2}(1-t)(3t-1).
$$
At $t=0$, $M_z=-M_0/2$. At $t=1/3$, both magnitudes equal $2M_0/3$ and $M_z=0$: this is compensation with finite order. At $t=1/2$, $M_z=M_0/8>0$. At $t=1$, both magnitudes vanish: that endpoint represents the stipulated <Curie temperature>, not another compensation point. For $t>1$ stipulate both ordered magnitudes zero; do not extrapolate the polynomials into negative sublattice magnitudes.
