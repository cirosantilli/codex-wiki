= Solution
{parent=a}

Write $\psi(s)=\log\mathbb E e^{sX}$ for the <cumulant-generating function>, and let $D_+=\{s>0:\psi(s)<\infty\}$. This set is nonempty. In fact, finiteness of the <moment-generating function> at some $\eta>0$ and $-\eta$ gives
$$
\mathbb E e^{\eta|X|}\leq\mathbb E e^{\eta X}+\mathbb E e^{-\eta X}<\infty,
$$
so $X$ is an <integrable random variable>. Put $m=\mathbb EX$.

For the upper tail, use the following one-sided <convex conjugate> and its <strict generalized inverse of a nondecreasing function>:
$$
I(x)=\sup_{s\in D_+\cup\{0\}}\{sx-\psi(s)\},\qquad
\psi_X^{*-1}(t):=\inf\{x\in\mathbb R:I(x)>t\},\quad t>0.
$$
Here $\psi(0)=0$, and $I$ is nondecreasing because every slope in its defining <supremum> is nonnegative. For $x\geq m$, negative $s$ cannot increase the conjugate: $\psi(s)\geq sm$ gives $sx-\psi(s)\leq s(x-m)\leq0$, and $s=0$ already gives zero. Thus $I$ agrees with the full <Legendre transform of a cumulant-generating function> on its upper-tail branch. Using a <strict generalized inverse of a nondecreasing function> handles constant <random variables> and bounded supports without assuming a strictly increasing, everywhere finite conjugate. Directly from the definition,
$$
\{x:I(x)>t\}
=\bigcup_{s\in D_+}\left(\frac{t+\psi(s)}s,\infty\right),
\qquad
\boxed{\psi_X^{*-1}(t)=\inf_{s\in D_+}\frac{t+\psi(s)}s.}
$$
This threshold is finite: any fixed $s\in D_+$ gives a finite upper bound, while $e^u\geq1+u$ gives $\mathbb E e^{s(X-m)}\geq1$ and hence $\psi(s)\geq sm$, providing the lower bound $m$.

We now derive the needed <Chernoff bound>. For $s\in D_+$ and any real $x$, the pointwise inequality $e^{sX}\geq e^{sx}\mathbf1_{\{X>x\}}$ implies
$$
\mathbb P(X>x)\leq e^{\psi(s)-sx}.
$$
Let $q=\psi_X^{*-1}(t)$. For every $\varepsilon>0$, the <infimum> formula supplies an $s\in D_+$ such that $(t+\psi(s))/s<q+\varepsilon$. Thus $\mathbb P(X>q+\varepsilon)\leq e^{-t}$. As $\varepsilon\downarrow0$, these events increase to $\{X>q\}$; <continuity from below of a measure> proves
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P\{X>\psi_X^{*-1}(t)\}\leq e^{-t}.}
$$
The strict event and the <strict generalized inverse of a nondecreasing function> avoid any assumption that the optimizing $s$ exists. For example, if $X=m$ almost surely, the threshold is $m$ for every $t>0$, and the probability is zero.

Let $M_n=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_i$. For $0<s<1/c$, the exponential of a maximum is bounded by a sum, giving
$$
e^{sM_n}\leq\sum_{i=1}^n e^{sX_i},\qquad
\mathbb E e^{sM_n}\leq n\exp\!\left(\frac{\nu s^2}{2(1-cs)}\right).
$$
Applying <Jensen's inequality> to the <logarithm> yields
$$
\mathbb EM_n\leq\frac1s\log\mathbb E e^{sM_n}
\leq\frac{\log n}{s}+\frac{\nu s}{2(1-cs)}.
$$
For $n>1$, put $a=\sqrt{2\log n/\nu}$ and choose $s=a/(1+ca)$. This gives $1-cs=1/(1+ca)$ and therefore
$$
\frac{\log n}{s}+\frac{\nu s}{2(1-cs)}
=\frac{\log n}{a}+c\log n+\frac{\nu a}{2}
=\sqrt{2\nu\log n}+c\log n.
$$
For $n=1$, let $s\downarrow0$ in the preceding upper bound, obtaining $\mathbb EX_1\leq0$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_i\leq\sqrt{2\nu\log n}+c\log n\quad(n\geq1).}
$$
The proof uses only the individual <moment-generating function> bounds for <sub-Gamma random variables in the right tail>; it requires no <independence>. Under the paper's literal one-sided definition, the negative part need not be integrable. The positive part of $M_n$ is integrable by the displayed exponential bound; if $\mathbb EM_n=-\infty$, the conclusion holds in the extended sense. Otherwise the application of <Jensen's inequality> is ordinary. Equivalently, truncate $M_n$ below and pass to the limit.

Use <closed balls> with centres belonging to the space being covered. The <covering number> is
$$
\boxed{N(\varepsilon;R,\rho)=\min\left\{|A|:A\subseteq R,\ R\subseteq\bigcup_{a\in A}\overline B_\rho(a,\varepsilon),\ |A|<\infty\right\},}
$$
with value $\infty$ if there is no finite <metric net>, and value zero for the empty space.

With this intrinsic-centre convention, **the proposed monotonicity under subsets is false**. Give $R=\{-1,0,1\}$ the usual distance, take $S=\{-1,1\}$, and set $\varepsilon=3/2$. A ball centred at $0$ covers $R$, so $N(3/2;R,\rho)=1$. The two points of $S$ are distance $2$ apart, so a ball centred in $S$ contains only its centre, and $N(3/2;S,\rho)=2$. These values are also valid with <open balls>.

The obstruction is that the useful centre $0$ disappears on passing to the subspace. If centres were allowed anywhere in a fixed ambient space, monotonicity would hold. With intrinsic centres, one instead has
$$
\boxed{N(2\varepsilon;S,\rho)\leq N(\varepsilon;R,\rho).}
$$
Indeed, discard covering balls missing $S$ and choose one point of $S$ from each remaining ball. The <triangle inequality> shows that every point of its intersection with $S$ is within $2\varepsilon$ of the chosen point. This proves the replacement bound whenever the right side is finite; otherwise it is automatic.

We give a finite <chaining> argument, keeping the constants explicit. If $\delta=0$, the <metric space> has just the point $t_0$ and both sides vanish. Suppose $\delta>0$, and write
$$
\varepsilon_k=2^{-k}\delta,\qquad N_k=N(\varepsilon_k;T,d),\quad k\geq1.
$$
Choose a smallest <metric net> $T_k\subseteq T$ of radius $\varepsilon_k$, and maps $\pi_k:T\to T_k$ satisfying $d(t,\pi_k(t))\leq\varepsilon_k$. Set $T_0=\{t_0\}$ and $\pi_0(t)=t_0$. Since $T$ is finite, choose $K$ with $\varepsilon_K$ smaller than the minimum distance between distinct points; then $T_K=T$ and $\pi_K(t)=t$.

For each $k$, retain only the pairs actually occurring along these approximation paths:
$$
E_k=\{(\pi_k(t),\pi_{k-1}(t)):t\in T\},\qquad m_k=|E_k|.
$$
The <triangle inequality> gives $d(u,v)\leq\varepsilon_k+\varepsilon_{k-1}=3\varepsilon_k$ for $(u,v)\in E_k$; for $k=1$ the same argument uses $d(t,t_0)\leq\delta=\varepsilon_0$. Also
$$
m_1\leq N_1,\qquad m_k\leq N_kN_{k-1}\leq N_k^2\ (k\geq2),\qquad \log m_k\leq2\log N_k.
$$
Here only monotonicity in the radius for the same space $T$ is used. No monotonicity under subsets is assumed.

For every pair in $E_k$, the increment has the <sub-Gamma random variable in the right tail> bound with common parameters $9\nu\varepsilon_k^2$ and $3c\varepsilon_k$: for $0<s<(3c\varepsilon_k)^{-1}$,
$$
\log\mathbb E e^{s(X_u-X_v)}
\leq\frac{\nu d(u,v)^2s^2}{2(1-cd(u,v)s)}
\leq\frac{9\nu\varepsilon_k^2s^2}{2(1-3c\varepsilon_ks)}.
$$
Identical pairs give the zero increment, which obeys the same bound. Both orientations have finite <exponential moments>, so all increments are <integrable random variables>. Differentiating their two upper bounds at zero also gives $\mathbb E(X_u-X_v)=0$. Applying the maximum bound from part (b), including its $m_k=1$ case, gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E\max_{(u,v)\in E_k}(X_u-X_v)
&\leq3\varepsilon_k\sqrt{2\nu\log m_k}+3c\varepsilon_k\log m_k\\
&\leq6\sqrt\nu\,\varepsilon_k\sqrt{\log N_k}+6c\varepsilon_k\log N_k.
\end{aligned}
$$
The <telescoping sum> along each path is exact:
$$
X_t-X_{t_0}=\sum_{k=1}^K(X_{\pi_k(t)}-X_{\pi_{k-1}(t)}).
$$
Take the <supremum>, bound it by the sum of the maxima, and take <expected values>. This proves
$$
\mathbb E\sup_{t\in T}(X_t-X_{t_0})
\leq6\sqrt\nu\sum_{k=1}^K\varepsilon_k\sqrt{\log N_k}
+6c\sum_{k=1}^K\varepsilon_k\log N_k.
$$
For either $f(u)=\sqrt{\log N(u;T,d)}$ or $f(u)=\log N(u;T,d)$, the function is nonnegative and nonincreasing. Thus
$$
\int_{\varepsilon_{k+1}}^{\varepsilon_k}f(u)\,du
\geq\frac{\varepsilon_k}{2}f(\varepsilon_k),\qquad
\sum_{k=1}^K\varepsilon_k f(\varepsilon_k)
\leq2\int_0^{\delta/2}f(u)\,du.
$$
Combining the estimates yields the required <metric entropy> bound:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E\left[\sup_{t\in T}X_t-X_{t_0}\right]
\leq12\sqrt\nu\int_0^{\delta/2}\sqrt{\log N(u;T,d)}\,du
+12c\int_0^{\delta/2}\log N(u;T,d)\,du.}
$$
In particular, **$C=12$ and $C'=12$ work**. The square-root and linear <metric entropy> terms arise respectively from the two terms in the sub-Gamma maximum bound. Because the chain ends exactly at $T$, no limiting sample-path regularity assumption is needed.

Work for an arbitrary realization of the sequence, and set $u_t=X_t^TV_{t-1}^{-1}X_t$. In the <Loewner order>,
$$
V_{t-1}=\lambda I+\sum_{s<t}X_sX_s^T\succeq\lambda I.
$$
By the <spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices>, every <eigenvalue> of $V_{t-1}$ is at least $\lambda$. The <matrix inverse> therefore has eigenvalues at most $\lambda^{-1}$, and
$$
0\leq u_t\leq\frac{\|X_t\|_2^2}{\lambda}\leq1.
$$
For $0\leq u\leq1$, put $g(u)=2\log(1+u)-u$. Since $g(0)=0$ and $g'(u)=(1-u)/(1+u)\geq0$, we have $u\leq2\log(1+u)$. The <matrix determinant lemma> now gives
$$
\det V_t=\det V_{t-1}(1+u_t).
$$
Taking <logarithms> and using a <telescoping sum>,
$$
\boxed{\sum_{t=1}^n|X_t|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}^2
=\sum_{t=1}^n u_t
\leq2\sum_{t=1}^n\log(1+u_t)
=2\log\frac{\det V_n}{\det V_0}.}
$$
This <elliptical potential bound> holds for every realization and every $n$, without a probabilistic exceptional event. The condition $\lambda\geq1$ is what ensures $u_t\leq1$.

Let $a_1,\ldots,a_d>0$ be the <eigenvalues> of the <positive-definite matrix> $V_n$. Its <trace> satisfies
$$
\sum_{j=1}^d a_j=\operatorname{tr}V_n
=d\lambda+\sum_{t=1}^n\|X_t\|_2^2\leq d\lambda+n.
$$
The <arithmetic-geometric mean inequality> gives
$$
\det V_n=\prod_{j=1}^d a_j
\leq\left(\frac{\sum_j a_j}{d}\right)^d
\leq\left(\lambda+\frac nd\right)^d.
$$
Since $\det V_0=\lambda^d$, taking the <logarithm> proves
$$
\boxed{\log\frac{\det V_n}{\det V_0}\leq d\log\left(1+\frac{n}{d\lambda}\right).}
$$
The determinant is largest, for a fixed trace, when the eigenvalues are equal; this is the geometric content of the estimate.

Use the single event
$$
\mathcal E=\bigcap_{t\geq0}\{\theta_\star\in\Theta_t\},\qquad\mathbb P(\mathcal E)\geq1-\delta.
$$
Fix a realization in $\mathcal E$. The action space is nonempty and <compact>, since it is a closed subset of the finite-dimensional unit ball. The <Lipschitz continuous> reward function is continuous, so there is an optimal action $x_\star\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{x\in\mathcal X}\mu(\langle x,\theta_\star\rangle)$. Because the same reward function is used at every time, the <pseudoregret> is $R_n=\sum_{t=1}^n r_t$, where
$$
r_t=\mu(\langle x_\star,\theta_\star\rangle)-\mu(\langle X_t,\theta_\star\rangle)\geq0.
$$
The pair $(x_\star,\theta_\star)$ is feasible in the optimization defining $(X_t,\theta_t)$. This <optimism in the face of uncertainty> gives
$$
\mu(\langle x_\star,\theta_\star\rangle)\leq\mu(\langle X_t,\theta_t\rangle),
\qquad
r_t\leq L|\langle X_t,\theta_t-\theta_\star\rangle|.
$$
For any <positive-definite matrix> $V$, the ordinary <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> applied to $V^{-1/2}x$ and $V^{1/2}z$ proves
$$
|\langle x,z\rangle|\leq|x|_{V^{-1}}|z|_V.
$$
Both $\theta_t$ and $\theta_\star$ belong to the same <confidence ellipsoid> $\Theta_{t-1}$. The <triangle inequality> for the <norm> associated with this <weighted Euclidean inner product> therefore gives
$$
|\theta_t-\theta_\star|_{V_{t-1}}
\leq|\theta_t-\widehat\theta_{t-1}|_{V_{t-1}}
+|\widehat\theta_{t-1}-\theta_\star|_{V_{t-1}}
\leq2\beta_{t-1}(\delta),
$$
and hence $r_t\leq2L\beta_{t-1}(\delta)|X_t|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}$. Monotonicity of $\beta_t(\delta)$ and another <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> now yield
$$
\begin{aligned}
R_n&\leq2L\beta_n(\delta)\sum_{t=1}^n|X_t|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}\\
&\leq\boxed{2L\beta_n(\delta)\sqrt{n\sum_{t=1}^n|X_t|_{V_{t-1}^{-1}}^2}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
This reasoning holds on $\mathcal E$ for every $n$ simultaneously, proving the asserted probability. In combination with part (a), it also gives
$$
R_n\leq2L\beta_n(\delta)\sqrt{2nd\log\left(1+\frac n{d\lambda}\right)}.
$$
Only the supplied simultaneous <confidence set> coverage and the optimistic choice are needed here. No claim that the displayed linear estimator is automatically a suitable estimator for a nonlinear link is required: its coverage was an explicit hypothesis.

Let $\mathcal G_t=\sigma(X_1,\ldots,X_t)$. Under $P_{\theta_\star}$, the true <probability density function> is positive and finite at every observation almost surely. Define the sequential <likelihood ratio>
$$
M_0=1,\qquad
M_t=\prod_{s=1}^t\frac{p_{\widehat\theta_{s-1}}(X_s)}{p_{\theta_\star}(X_s)}.
$$
A zero numerator sets the product to zero thereafter. Thus $M_t$ is a well-defined nonnegative process, and $L_t(\theta_\star)=\log M_t$, with $\log0=-\infty$.

The next observation is <independent> of $\mathcal G_{s-1}$, while $\widehat\theta_{s-1}$ is <measurable> with respect to that <sigma-algebra>. Its <conditional expectation> is therefore
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_{\theta_\star}\left[\left.
\frac{p_{\widehat\theta_{s-1}}(X_s)}{p_{\theta_\star}(X_s)}\right|\mathcal G_{s-1}\right]
&=\int_{\{p_{\theta_\star}>0\}}
p_{\widehat\theta_{s-1}}(x)\,d\mu(x)\\
&\leq1.
\end{aligned}
$$
Consequently $\mathbb E(M_s\mid\mathcal G_{s-1})\leq M_{s-1}$; induction also gives $\mathbb EM_s\leq1$. Thus $(M_t)$ is a nonnegative <supermartingale> for the <filtration> $(\mathcal G_t)$. It need not be a <martingale>: an alternative distribution can put mass outside the support of the true distribution.

The <maximal inequality for a nonnegative supermartingale>, the corresponding form of the <Ville inequality>, gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P_{\theta_\star}\{\exists t\geq0:\theta_\star\notin\Theta_t\}
&=\mathbb P_{\theta_\star}\{\exists t\geq0:L_t(\theta_\star)\geq\log(1/\delta)\}\\
&\leq\mathbb P_{\theta_\star}\{\sup_{t\geq0}M_t\geq1/\delta\}\\
&\leq\boxed{\delta\mathbb EM_0=\delta}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The strict inequality in the definition of the <confidence set> correctly becomes a non-strict crossing event.

There is a minor definitional omission in the source: for false candidates $\theta$, the printed sum of <log-likelihood ratios> can involve $0/0$ or both infinite signs. To define the entire <confidence set> without a positivity assumption, use the logarithm of the ratio of the two full products, $\prod_s p_{\widehat\theta_{s-1}}(X_s)$ and $\prod_s p_\theta(X_s)$, taking $0/a=0$ for $a>0$, $a/0=+\infty$ for $a>0$, and, for example, $0/0=1$. This is a measurable extension of the printed formula wherever that formula is defined. The choice at $0/0$ cannot affect coverage of the truth, since its full denominator is positive almost surely. Alternatively, strict positivity of every density on the common support removes the omission.

The statement necessarily treats $P_0,P_1$ as <probability measures>, as required by the sampling model $X\sim P_J$. The word "measures" in the introduction must be read with this normalization. Use the paper's convention $H^2(P,Q)=\tfrac12\int(\sqrt p-\sqrt q)^2\,d\mu$ for the <squared Hellinger distance> throughout this question.

A measurable binary <hypothesis test> $\psi$ is determined by the <measurable set> $A=\{x:\psi(x)=1\}$. Under the uniform prior on the two hypotheses, its error probability is
$$
\mathbb P\{\psi(X)\neq J\}
=\frac12\bigl(P_0(A)+P_1(A^c)\bigr)
=\frac12\bigl(1-[P_1(A)-P_0(A)]\bigr).
$$
Since the two measures have equal total mass,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sup_A[P_1(A)-P_0(A)]
&=\int(p_1-p_0)_+\,d\mu\\
&=\frac12\int|p_1-p_0|\,d\mu
=V(P_0,P_1).
\end{aligned}
$$
Indeed, the integral over any set is at most the integral of the positive part, and equality holds for $A=\{p_1>p_0\}$. This is also the <total variation distance>. The <likelihood ratio> rule choosing hypothesis $1$ precisely on that set therefore attains the <infimum>, proving
$$
\boxed{\inf_\psi\mathbb P\{\psi(X)\neq J\}=\frac12\bigl[1-V(P_0,P_1)\bigr].}
$$
Arbitrary choices on the tie set $\{p_1=p_0\}$ leave the error unchanged. This is the exact link between <total variation distance> and optimal binary <statistical hypothesis testing>.

Put $h=H^2(P_0,P_1)$ with the paper's normalized <Hellinger distance>. The <Hellinger affinity> is
$$
A(P_0,P_1)=\int\sqrt{p_0p_1}\,d\mu=1-h,\qquad0\leq A(P_0,P_1)\leq1.
$$
The last bound follows from the <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> and the unit integrals of the two <probability density functions>. Using a common dominating <probability measure>, for example $(P_0+P_1)/2$, makes the product calculation immediate. The <probability density functions> of the <product measures> factor, and <Tonelli's theorem> gives
$$
A(P_0^n,P_1^n)
=\int\prod_{i=1}^n\sqrt{p_0(x_i)p_1(x_i)}\,d\mu^{\otimes n}(\mathbf x)
=A(P_0,P_1)^n.
$$
The <squared Hellinger distance> is independent of the choice of common dominating measure, so this calculation applies to the original distributions. We obtain the exact identity and then apply <Bernoulli's inequality>:
$$
\boxed{H^2(P_0^n,P_1^n)=1-(1-h)^n\leq nh=nH^2(P_0,P_1).}
$$
For completeness, the scalar bound follows by induction: $(1-h)^1=1-h$, and multiplying $(1-h)^n\geq1-nh$ by the nonnegative $1-h$ gives $(1-h)^{n+1}\geq1-(n+1)h+nh^2\geq1-(n+1)h$.

Use two translated <uniform distributions>, with parameters $0$ and $a=1/(2n)$. Their distance in the usual <metric> is $a$, so take the separation radius $\delta=a/2=1/(4n)$ and the <loss function> $\Phi(r)=r^2$. The supplied testing reduction, equivalently the <Le Cam two-point lemma>, and part (a)(i) imply
$$
m_n\geq\delta^2\frac{1-V(U_0^n,U_a^n)}2
=\frac{a^2}{8}\bigl[1-V(U_0^n,U_a^n)\bigr].
$$
To see the reduction directly, turn any <estimator> into a binary <hypothesis test> by choosing the closer of $0$ and $a$. If the test is wrong, the absolute estimation error is at least $a/2$. The worst <quadratic risk> is at least its average over these two parameters, giving precisely this bound.

Under <Lebesgue measure> on $\mathbb R^n$, the two sample <probability density functions> are the indicators of the cubes $[0,1]^n$ and $[a,a+1]^n$. Their intersection is $[a,1]^n$, of volume $(1-a)^n$. Therefore
$$
1-V(U_0^n,U_a^n)
=\int\min(p_0^{(n)},p_a^{(n)})\,d\mathbf x
=(1-a)^n\geq1-na=\frac12,
$$
where the inequality is <Bernoulli's inequality>. Consequently
$$
\boxed{m_n\geq\frac{a^2}{16}=\frac1{64n^2},\qquad C=\frac1{64}.}
$$
This also fits the <Hellinger distance> approach: the two one-sample affinities give $H^2(U_0,U_a)=a$, while equal-height indicator densities imply $V(U_0^n,U_a^n)=H^2(U_0^n,U_a^n)$. Part (a)(ii) thus yields the same bound $V\leq na=1/2$. The change of support produces <squared Hellinger distance> proportional to $a$, explaining the parameter scale $a\asymp n^{-1}$ and <quadratic risk> scale $n^{-2}$.

Use the first <order statistic> and correct its bias:
$$
\widehat\theta(\mathbf X)=X_{(1)}-\frac1{n+1},\qquad X_{(1)}=\min_{1\leq i\leq n}X_i.
$$
This is a <measurable function> of the sample. Under $U_\theta^n$, write $Z=X_{(1)}-\theta$. It lies in $[0,1]$, and <independence> gives
$$
\mathbb P_\theta(Z>u)
=\prod_{i=1}^n\mathbb P_\theta(X_i>\theta+u)
=(1-u)^n,\qquad0\leq u\leq1.
$$
For a bounded nonnegative <random variable>, integrating the identities $Z=\int_0^1\mathbf1_{\{Z>u\}}\,du$ and $Z^2=\int_0^1 2u\mathbf1_{\{Z>u\}}\,du$ proves the <tail integral formula for moments> by <Tonelli's theorem>. Hence
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_\theta Z&=\int_0^1(1-u)^n\,du=\frac1{n+1},\\
\mathbb E_\theta Z^2&=2\int_0^1u(1-u)^n\,du
=2\left(\frac1{n+1}-\frac1{n+2}\right)
=\frac2{(n+1)(n+2)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus $\widehat\theta$ is an <unbiased estimator>. Its <quadratic risk> is the <variance> of $Z$, independent of $\theta$:
$$
\mathbb E_\theta(\widehat\theta-\theta)^2
=\frac2{(n+1)(n+2)}-\frac1{(n+1)^2}
=\frac{n}{(n+1)^2(n+2)}.
$$
Taking the <supremum> over all real $\theta$ and then the <infimum> over <estimators> gives
$$
\boxed{m_n\leq\frac{n}{(n+1)^2(n+2)}\leq\frac1{n^2},\qquad C'=1.}
$$
The final inequality follows from $n^3\leq(n+1)^2(n+2)$. Together with part (i), this proves that the <minimax risk> has order $n^{-2}$ for all $n\geq1$. In the last line of the source, the request for the upper-bound constant calls it $C$; here it is denoted $C'$ consistently with the upper-bound inequality.
