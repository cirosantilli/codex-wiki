= Newton's law of universal gravitation
{c}
{title2=$\mathbf F=-GMm\mathbf r/|\mathbf r|^3$}
{wiki}

<Newton's law of universal gravitation> gives the attractive <force> between two <point masses>. With source <mass> $M$ at the origin and relative <position> $\mathbf r$ of <mass> $m$, write $r=|\mathbf r|>0$:
$$
\mathbf F_{M\to m}=-\frac{GMm}{r^3}\mathbf r,\qquad |\mathbf F_{M\to m}|=\frac{GMm}{r^2}.
$$
Here $G$ is the <gravitational constant>. The minus sign points toward $M$; the magnitude follows an <inverse-square law>, so doubling the separation quarters the <force>. The opposite <force> on $M$ satisfies <Newton's third law>. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/13-1-newtons-law-of-universal-gravitation[OpenStax: mutual gravitational forces]{ref}

Both <masses> accelerate. Applying <Newton's second law> to each in the isolated <two-body problem> gives
$$
\ddot{\mathbf r}=-\frac{\mu}{r^3}\mathbf r,\qquad \mu=G(M+m).
$$
The relative <standard gravitational parameter> includes both <masses>; replacing it by $GM$ requires the <test particle> approximation $m\ll M$.

Historically, Newton's Book III, Proposition VII argues that gravitational attraction applies to all bodies and scales with their <masses>; its second corollary states the <inverse-square law> for individual particles. https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Mathematical_Principles_of_Natural_Philosophy_(1729)/Proposition_7:_Gravity_tending_to_all_Bodies[Newton, Principia, Book III, Proposition VII, Motte's 1729 translation]{ref}

= Axial and transverse gravity of a uniform rod
{parent=Newton's law of universal gravitation}
{title2=$F_\parallel=GMm/(b^2-a^2),\quad F_\perp=GMm/[r\sqrt{r^2+a^2}]$}

Integrate Newtonian point-force elements along a uniform thin rod of total <mass> $M$ and half-length $a$. The first title formula applies on the axis outside the endpoint, $b>a$; the second applies on its perpendicular bisector. Both approach the point-mass result at large distance, but at equal distance their ratio is $1+3a^2/(2b^2)+O(a^4/b^4)$. A nonuniform linear <mass> profile cannot be replaced by these formulas using only total <mass> and <length>.

= Circular orbit perpendicular to a uniform rod
{parent=Axial and transverse gravity of a uniform rod}
{title2=$v(r)=\sqrt{GM/a}[1+(r/a)^2]^{-1/4}$}

For a light test <mass> in the rod's perpendicular-bisector plane, axial <symmetry> makes the attraction radial. Equating $mv^2/r$ to the transverse <force> gives the title <speed>. Far outside the rod it is Keplerian, proportional to $r^{-1/2}$. Well inside the half-length but outside its physical thickness, the rod approximates an infinite line and the circular <speed> is nearly constant, $\sqrt{GM/a}$. Extrapolation to an orbit inside the material or a zero-radius orbit is outside this thin-rod model.

= Gravitational-electrostatic balance of equal charged spheres
{parent=Newton's law of universal gravitation}
{title2=$q^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)=GM^2$}

For two identical spheres sufficiently far apart, <Coulomb's law> and <Newton's law of universal gravitation> both give inverse-square forces. Equal-sign charges therefore balance attraction when the displayed charge-to-mass relation holds. For uniform mass density $\rho$, substituting $M=4\pi\rho a^3/3$ fixes the radius. Changing the separation does not restore balance if the charge-to-mass ratio is wrong. Spherically symmetric charge makes the monopole result exact outside each sphere; polarization can require a far-field approximation.

= Free fall
{parent=Newton's law of universal gravitation}
{wiki}

Free fall is motion under <gravity> alone: there is no supporting <normal force>, engine thrust or appreciable <drag force>. In the <Newtonian mechanics> model, a <test particle> of <mass> $m$ obeys $m\ddot{\mathbf r}=m\mathbf g(\mathbf r)$, hence $\ddot{\mathbf r}=\mathbf g(\mathbf r)$. The <mass> cancels. Bodies released at the same <position> with the same <velocity> follow the same path in this model, even if their <gravitational weights> differ. https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/free-falling-objects/[NASA Glenn: free-falling objects and the cancellation of mass]{ref}

A dropped stone is one example; a coasting satellite is another. Free fall does not require motion straight down or decreasing distance to the attracting body. An ideal <circular orbit> has constant radius while its <velocity> continually turns under <centripetal acceleration>. The <circular orbit as continuous free fall> connects the familiar drop with orbital motion.

Near a surface, treating the <gravitational acceleration> as constant gives a useful first prediction. Over larger heights, the <inverse-square law> changes the acceleration during the fall. <Radial free fall from rest> uses <conservation of mechanical energy> to account for that change exactly, within the specified <Newtonian gravitational field>. The contrast matters: the simplest formula is an approximation to a physical model, rather than a law with the same numerical $g$ everywhere.

History also makes the idealization tangible. Galileo's *Two New Sciences* (1638), Third Day, Theorem II, derives distance proportional to the square of time for uniformly accelerated motion from rest; its first corollary gives successive distances in the ratios $1,3,5,7,\ldots$ over equal time intervals. This is a kinematic statement, predating the gravitational <inverse-square law>. https://galileoandeinstein.phys.virginia.edu/tns_draft/tns_160to243.html[Galileo, Two New Sciences, Third Day, Theorem II and Corollary I; Crew and de Salvio's 1914 translation, pp. 174–176]{ref}

On Apollo 15's final lunar surface excursion, David Scott released a hammer and a feather together. The near-<vacuum> removed the large difference in <drag force> familiar on Earth. NASA's report describes their arrival together within the accuracy of the release; this demonstration illustrates the <free fall> prediction without establishing infinite experimental precision. https://science.nasa.gov/resource/the-apollo-15-hammer-feather-drop/[NASA's original footage and Joe Allen's account in the Apollo 15 Preliminary Science Report, p. 2-11]{ref}

= Free fall in a constant gravitational field
{parent=Free fall}
{title2=$s(t)=\tfrac12gt^2,\quad v(t)=gt$}

Take downward <displacement> $s$ as positive, assume a constant <gravitational acceleration> $g>0$, and release a <point mass> from rest at $s=0$. Integrating $\ddot s=g$ twice gives
$$
v(t)=gt,\qquad s(t)=\frac12gt^2.
$$
For a downward distance $h>0$, the arrival time and <speed> are
$$
t_{\mathrm{fall}}=\sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}},\qquad v_{\mathrm{arrival}}=\sqrt{2gh}.
$$
For the model values $h=20\,\mathrm m$ and $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, these are approximately $2.0193\,\mathrm s$ and $19.8091\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Doubling the height multiplies both quantities by $\sqrt2$; it does not double them. The same <speed> follows from the <work-energy theorem>, $mgh=mv^2/2$.

For intervals of length $\Delta t$, the successive downward <displacements> are
$$
s((j+1)\Delta t)-s(j\Delta t)=\frac12g(\Delta t)^2(2j+1),\qquad j=0,1,2,\ldots.
$$
Thus equal spacing in time produces increasingly separated positions. This is the odd-number pattern in Galileo's result. It assumes constant <acceleration> and release from rest; a nonzero initial <velocity> adds a linear term to $s(t)$.

The approximation can be estimated for a spherical source of radius $R$ and <standard gravitational parameter> $\mu$. Writing $g_0=\mu/R^2$, the gravitational magnitude at height $h$ is
$$
\frac{g(R+h)}{g_0}=\left(1+\frac hR\right)^{-2}.
$$
For $h/R\ll1$, a <Taylor expansion> gives $g(R+h)/g_0=1-2h/R+O((h/R)^2)$. Small height relative to the source's radius makes nearly constant gravity plausible; negligible <drag force> remains a separate requirement.

= Radial free fall from rest
{parent=Free fall}
{title2=$v(r)=\sqrt{2\mu(1/r-1/r_0)}$}

Let a <test particle> start at rest at radius $r_0$ outside a fixed spherically symmetric source. The <spherical shell theorem> gives the exterior <Newtonian gravitational potential> $\Phi(r)=-\mu/r$, where $\mu=GM$ is the source's <standard gravitational parameter>. Zero initial transverse <velocity> makes the motion radial. <Conservation of mechanical energy> per unit <mass> gives
$$
\frac{\dot r^2}{2}-\frac{\mu}{r}=-\frac{\mu}{r_0},\qquad
\dot r=-\sqrt{2\mu\left(\frac1r-\frac1{r_0}\right)}.
$$
The minus sign selects inward motion; the positive square root is its <speed>. For a fall from $r_0=2R$ to the surface $r=R$, that <speed> is $\sqrt{\mu/R}$. In the limit of release from rest infinitely far away, the familiar <free-fall speed> is $\sqrt{2\mu/r}$, equal in magnitude to the local <escape velocity>. A finite starting radius gives a smaller speed.

The arrival time follows from the same first integral. Set $r=r_0\cos^2\eta$. Then
$$
t(\eta)=\sqrt{\frac{r_0^3}{2\mu}}\left(\eta+\sin\eta\cos\eta\right),\qquad
\eta=\arccos\sqrt{\frac r{r_0}},\qquad 0\leq\eta<\frac\pi2.
$$
For arrival at $R>0$ this is finite, and the field formula is used only outside the source. For $r_0=2R$, $\eta=\pi/4$ gives
$$
t_{\mathrm{arrival}}=\left(1+\frac\pi2\right)\sqrt{\frac{R^3}{\mu}}\approx2.570796\sqrt{\frac{R^3}{\mu}}.
$$
Keeping the smaller initial gravitational magnitude $g(r_0)=\mu/(4R^2)$ constant would predict $\sqrt8\,\sqrt{R^3/\mu}$, about $10.0\%$ longer. The actual <acceleration> increases during the fall.

= Rotational deflection of a falling body
{parent=Free fall}

In an Earth-fixed <rotating reference frame>, the horizontal displacement of a freely falling body receives contributions from <Coriolis acceleration> and <centrifugal acceleration>. A short-fall approximation substitutes the unperturbed downward <velocity> into the Coriolis term and the Earth's surface <position> into the centrifugal term. If <gravitational acceleration> excludes centrifugal acceleration, both contributions must be included explicitly.

= Equatorial Coriolis deflection in free fall
{parent=Rotational deflection of a falling body}
{title2=$x_E=\Omega_E(2h)^{3/2}/(3\sqrt g)$}

A body released from rest relative to <Earth> at the equator has unperturbed downward <velocity> $v_U=-gt$. The <Coriolis acceleration in local Earth coordinates> gives eastward acceleration $a_E=-2\Omega_Ev_U=2\Omega_Egt$. Integration gives $x_E=\Omega_Egt^3/3$ and impact time $t_f=\sqrt{2h/g}$, proving the title formula. This is first order in <Earth's rotation>, neglecting air resistance and the height dependence of the effective local <gravitational acceleration>. For a short fall at latitude $\varphi$, its leading eastward term is multiplied by $\cos\varphi$.

= Newtonian gravitational field
{parent=Newton's law of universal gravitation}
{c}
{title2=$\mathbf g$}

The <Newtonian gravitational field> is the gravitational <force> per unit <mass> of a <test particle>. For a <point mass> $M$ at the origin,
$$
\mathbf g(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mathbf F}{m}=-\frac{GM}{r^3}\mathbf r,\qquad g(r)=|\mathbf g|=\frac{GM}{r^2}.
$$
The <test particle>'s <mass> cancels: different small <masses> have the same <gravitational acceleration> at the same <position>. In <SI>, the <Newtonian gravitational field> has units $\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}=\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. The same exterior expression holds for a spherically symmetric source, by the <spherical shell theorem>.

Choose a reference radius $r_0>0$, set $g_0=GM/r_0^2$, and use <nondimensionalization> with $z=r/r_0$. Then $g/g_0=z^{-2}$: at $z=1,2,4$, the magnitudes are $1,1/4,1/16$. This makes the spatial variation visible without specifying a planet. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/13-1-newtons-law-of-universal-gravitation[OpenStax: inverse-square attraction and spherical sources]{ref}

= Gravitational field of an infinite plane sheet
{parent=Newtonian gravitational field}
{title2=$|g|=2\pi G\Sigma$}

An infinite plane sheet of surface <mass density> $\Sigma$ has constant gravitational-field magnitude $2\pi G\Sigma$ on either side, directed toward the sheet. A Gaussian pillbox has equal inward <flux> on its two caps and enclosed <mass> $\Sigma A$, so $-2gA=-4\pi G\Sigma A$. A uniform infinite slab has the same exterior formula with $\Sigma=\rho h$. Translating an infinite sheet does not change its exterior field, whereas adding a slab changes its surface <mass density>.

= Tidal water loading of a spring balance
{parent=Gravitational field of an infinite plane sheet}
{title2=$\Delta x=x_0\,2\pi G\rho h/g$}

A <mass> above a sufficiently wide estuary experiences additional downward <gravity> when extra water is present. Approximating the tide as a plane slab of <density> $\rho$ and added thickness $h$ gives $\Delta g=2\pi G\rho h$. A linear <spring> with initial extension $x_0=mg/k$ changes by $\Delta x=(m/k)\Delta g=x_0\Delta g/g$. This isolates direct water attraction; it omits finite shoreline geometry, buoyancy, structural motion and terrestrial tides.

= Axial gravitational field of a uniform ring
{parent=Newtonian gravitational field}
{title2=$g_z=-GMz/(a^2+z^2)^{3/2}$}

A uniform thin ring of radius $a$ and <mass> $M$ has axial <gravitational field> $g_z=-GMz/(a^2+z^2)^{3/2}$. Every ring element is the same distance from the axial point; transverse contributions cancel and axial projection supplies the factor $z/\sqrt{a^2+z^2}$. This also follows by differentiating the axial potential $\Phi=-GM/\sqrt{a^2+z^2}$. Near the centre $g_z\simeq-GMz/a^3$, whereas far away it approaches the point-mass field.

= Gravitational field of a finite uniform line segment
{parent=Newtonian gravitational field}
{title2=$g_r=-GM/[r\sqrt{r^2+a^2}]$}

Spread <mass> $M$ uniformly along $-a<z<a$ on an axis, with <linear mass density> $\lambda=M/(2a)$. In its perpendicular bisector plane, axial field components cancel, and the radial component is
$$
g_r=-G\lambda r\int_{-a}^a\frac{dz}{(r^2+z^2)^{3/2}}=-\frac{GM}{r\sqrt{r^2+a^2}}.
$$
This is an exact <line mass> model and a thin-rod approximation for a physical cylinder. A centred <circular orbit> in this symmetry plane has $v^2=GM/\sqrt{R^2+a^2}$. Thus $v\to\sqrt{GM/a}=\sqrt{2G\lambda}$ for $R/a\to0$, and $v\sim\sqrt{GM/R}$ for $R/a\to\infty$. For a finite-width rod, the first limit is useful only while its transverse size remains much smaller than $R$.

= Gravitational field of an infinite uniform cylinder
{parent=Newtonian gravitational field}
{title2=$g_r=-2G\lambda/r\quad(r>b)$}

A circular infinite cylinder of radius $b$ and uniform <mass density> $\rho$ has <linear mass density> $\lambda=\pi b^2\rho$. A coaxial <Gaussian surface> of length $L$ has inward radial <gravitational field> and no end-cap <flux>. <Gauss's law for gravity> gives $2\pi rL g_r=-4\pi G\lambda L$ outside, hence the displayed result. Inside, enclosed <linear mass density> is $\pi r^2\rho$, so $g_r=-2\pi G\rho r$. An exterior <circular orbit> has <speed> $\sqrt{2G\lambda}$ independent of radius. The exterior formula presupposes circular axial symmetry; area alone would not specify the near field of a noncircular rod.

= Plumb-line deflection from cylindrical gravity
{parent=Gravitational field of an infinite uniform cylinder}
{title2=$d\simeq2GL\lambda/(rg)$}

A pendulum of length $L$ in vertical <gravity> $g$ is weakly attracted horizontally by an added long circular cylinder of line <density> $\lambda$. Outside it, $g_h=2G\lambda/r$ at axis distance $r$. Small-angle equilibrium gives horizontal deflection $d\simeq Lg_h/g=2GL\lambda/(rg)$. Removing the cylinder's <mass> reverses the change. It is a before/after differential estimate; finite length, a nonhorizontal radial direction and mechanical motion require corrections.

= Diameter-rod oscillation in cylindrical gravity
{parent=Gravitational field of an infinite uniform cylinder}
{title2=$\omega^2=2\pi G\rho$}

A thin rod of length $2R$ and <linear mass density> $\lambda$ slides along a diameter of an infinite uniform source cylinder of radius $R$. Neglect the narrow hole's perturbation of the source field. If the rod centre is displaced by $x$, $F(x)=\lambda\int_{x-R}^{x+R}g_y(y)\,dy$. The endpoint derivative gives $F'(0)=\lambda[g_y(R)-g_y(-R)]=-4\pi G\rho\lambda R$. Dividing the restoring coefficient by rod <mass> $2R\lambda$ gives the displayed small-oscillation <frequency>.

= Gauss's law for gravity
{c}
{parent=Newtonian gravitational field}
{title2=$\oint_S\mathbf g\cdot d\mathbf S=-4\pi GM_{\rm enc}$}

The outward <flux> of the <Newtonian gravitational field> through a closed surface is $-4\pi G$ times the enclosed <mass>. A sphere around a <point mass> establishes the coefficient; <superposition> extends the integral law to general <mass distributions>. The minus sign expresses attractive gravity. A symmetric <Gaussian surface> can turn the flux relation into a local field calculation, but the integral law alone does not imply that the field is uniform on an arbitrary surface.

= Uniform gravitational field in an off-center spherical cavity
{parent=Newtonian gravitational field}
{title2=$g=-4\pi G\rho c/3$}

Remove a spherical void centered at $c$ from a uniform sphere centered at the origin. At points inside the void, <superposition> combines the sphere's interior field $-4\pi G\rho x/3$ with the removed sphere's field $+4\pi G\rho(x-c)/3$. Their sum is the displayed constant <Newtonian gravitational field>. A second removed mass outside this void adds a nonuniform contribution.

= Cavity equilibrium destabilized by a second void
{parent=Uniform gravitational field in an off-center spherical cavity}

A uniform <Newtonian gravitational field> inside one spherical void can be cancelled at a point by the repulsive field representing another removed sphere. The latter field has a <Jacobian matrix> proportional to $I-3\hat r\hat r^T$: its axial eigenvalue is negative and both transverse eigenvalues positive. The equilibrium is restoring along the axis but unstable transversely. A zero force is thus not enough for stable rest.

= Newtonian gravitational stress tensor
{parent=Newtonian gravitational field}
{c}
{title2=$T_{ij}$}

For a <Newtonian gravitational potential> obeying the <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity>, define $T_{ij}=[\partial_i\Phi\partial_j\Phi-\delta_{ij}|\nabla\Phi|^2/2]/(4\pi G)$. Commutation of mixed <partial derivatives> gives $\partial_jT_{ij}=\rho\partial_i\Phi$. Thus the gravitational force density can be put in conservative form as $-\partial_jT_{ij}$. The sign is chosen so that $T_{ij}$ is added to the momentum flux; reversing the stress convention reverses the divergence sign.

= Gravitational stress contribution to angular-momentum transport
{parent=Newtonian gravitational stress tensor}
{title2=$rT_{r\phi}$}

In cylindrical coordinates, the <Newtonian gravitational stress tensor> gives radial <angular-momentum flux> $rT_{r\phi}=r(\partial_r\Phi)(r^{-1}\partial_\phi\Phi)/(4\pi G)$. Nonaxisymmetric structure can correlate the field components and transport <angular momentum>. The isotropic negative-pressure part has zero off-diagonal component, so it gives no direct radial transport. This separates a shear flux from a contribution to normal force balance.

= Self-gravity
{parent=Newtonian gravitational field}

Self-gravity is the <gravitational acceleration> produced by a system's own <mass density>. It is obtained by solving the <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity> for that density, rather than treating the <Newtonian gravitational potential> as an externally fixed field. It competes with <pressure> and rotational support in <gravitational instability of an astrophysical disk>.

= Standard gravitational parameter
{parent=Newtonian gravitational field}
{title2=$\mu$}
{wiki}

= Gravitational parameter
{synonym}

The product $\mu=GM$ of the <gravitational constant> and a central <mass> sets the strength of the exterior <Newtonian gravitational field>. Its dimensions are length cubed divided by time squared. In the exact relative <two-body problem>, the parameter is $G(M_1+M_2)$; using $GM$ for one source assumes the <test particle> approximation.

The combination is useful because orbital and falling-body observations constrain $\mu$ directly. A gravitational <circular orbit> of radius $a$ has <oscillation period> $T=2\pi\sqrt{a^3/\mu}$, so measuring $a$ and $T$ gives $\mu=4\pi^2a^3/T^2$. That measurement alone does not separately determine $G$ and $M$. Similarly, <radial free fall from rest> has a natural time scale $\sqrt{r_0^3/\mu}$.

Other interactions require a different model. Inverse-square <radiation pressure> can reduce the effective attraction for a dust <Kepler orbit>; calling that effective coefficient a gravitational parameter must not hide the added <force>.

= Tidal tensor
{title2=$T_{jk}$}
{parent=Newtonian gravitational field}

The acceleration tidal tensor is $T_{jk}=\partial_k g_j=-\partial_j\partial_k\Phi$ for <gravitational acceleration> $\mathbf g=-\nabla\Phi$. It describes differential acceleration $\Delta\mathbf g\simeq T\Delta\mathbf r$. Some authors instead call the positive <Hessian matrix> $\partial_j\partial_k\Phi$ the tidal tensor, reversing this sign convention. For a point mass and the positive potential convention $\mathbf g=\nabla\psi$, the tensor is $Gm(3\mathbf R\mathbf R^{\mathsf T}/R^5-\mathbf1/R^3)$.

The <tidal tensor> is the <Jacobian matrix> of the <Newtonian gravitational field>, rather than the <gravitational acceleration> itself. Its entries have units $\mathrm{s^{-2}}$: multiplying by a <displacement> produces an <acceleration>. For a smooth <Newtonian gravitational potential>, the <tidal tensor> is a <symmetric matrix>, since mixed <partial derivatives> commute. A spatially uniform <gravitational acceleration> has zero <tidal tensor>, however large that <acceleration> is.

= Newtonian tidal eigenvalues outside a point mass
{parent=Tidal tensor}
{c}
{title2=$\operatorname{eig}T=(2,-1,-1)GM/r^3$}

For $\Phi=-GM/r$, the acceleration <tidal tensor> is
$$
T_{ij}=-\partial_i\partial_j\Phi=
\frac{GM}{r^3}(3n_i n_j-\delta_{ij}).
$$
Its radial eigenvalue is $2GM/r^3$ and its two transverse eigenvalues are $-GM/r^3$. This stretches a small freely falling cloud radially and compresses it transversely. The zero trace expresses the vacuum <Poisson equation> outside the source; within matter the trace is $-4\pi G\rho$.

= Corotating point-mass tidal field
{parent=Tidal tensor}
{title2=$T_{\rm eff}=\Omega^2\operatorname{diag}(3,0,-1)$}

Use local axes radial, tangential in the orbital plane, and perpendicular to that plane around a <circular Kepler orbit> with $\Omega^2=GM/R^3$. The gravitational <tidal tensor> is $\Omega^2\operatorname{diag}(2,-1,-1)$. A uniformly corotating frame adds the gradient of <centrifugal acceleration>, $\Omega^2\operatorname{diag}(1,1,0)$, giving the displayed effective tensor for stationary offsets. Thus the radial differential acceleration is $3GMa/R^3$. These are accelerations, or forces per unit test mass; an actual differential force includes that mass. Moving offsets also experience <Coriolis acceleration>.

= Point-mass tidal stretching and squeezing
{parent=Tidal tensor}
{title2=$T=\mu(3\mathbf n\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}-I)/R^3$}

For a source <point mass> $M$ at the origin, let $\mathbf R=R\mathbf n$, where $\mathbf n$ is an outward <unit vector>, and let $\mu=GM$ be the source's <standard gravitational parameter>. Differentiating $g_i=-\mu R_i/R^3$ gives the <tidal tensor>
$$
T_{ij}=\frac{\mu}{R^3}(3n_i n_j-\delta_{ij}),\qquad T=\frac{\mu}{R^3}(3\mathbf n\mathbf n^{\mathsf T}-I),
$$
where $\delta_{ij}$ is the <Kronecker delta> and $I$ is the <identity matrix>. In an <orthonormal basis> with first axis radial, the <tidal tensor> is
$$
T=\frac{\mu}{R^3}\begin{pmatrix}2&0&0\\0&-1&0\\0&0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The radial <eigenvalue> $2\mu/R^3$ separates nearby freely falling <test particles>; the two transverse <eigenvalues> $-\mu/R^3$ bring them together. The farther radial <test particle> falls inward less rapidly, so its <acceleration> relative to the nearer one points outward. Transverse <gravitational accelerations> converge toward the source. Thus an initially resting small cloud stretches radially and squeezes in both transverse directions. These are instantaneous relative <accelerations>; the subsequent shape also depends on initial <velocities> and the changing <tidal tensor> along the cloud's <free fall>.

Outside the source, the <matrix trace> is $2\mu/R^3-\mu/R^3-\mu/R^3=0$. This matches $\operatorname{tr}T=-\nabla^2\Phi=0$ in a region with zero <mass density>, by the <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity>. Zero <matrix trace> means no leading change of a small initially resting cloud's <volume> at order $t^2$, not that each separation stays fixed. Inside matter, the same sign convention gives $\operatorname{tr}T=-4\pi G\rho$ instead.

= Quadrupolar point-mass tidal potential
{parent=Tidal tensor}
{title2=$\psi_{\mathrm{tid}}=\tfrac12r_iT_{ij}r_j$}

Subtracting the constant and uniform-acceleration terms from the point-mass <relative potential> gives $\psi_{\mathrm{tid}}=Gm_p[3(\mathbf r\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r}_p)^2-r^2]/(2r_p^3)$, with force convention $\nabla\psi$. Its <tidal tensor> is $Gm_p(3\widehat{\mathbf r}_p\widehat{\mathbf r}_p^T-I)/r_p^3$. This is the leading term for target size small compared with separation.

= Tidal disruption
{parent=Tidal tensor}

Disruption of a self-gravitating body when differential gravitational <acceleration> across it exceeds its binding <acceleration>. For a body of radius $R$, <mass> $m$ at distance $r$ from a much more massive body $M$, the competing scales are $GMR/r^3$ and $Gm/R^2$; their equality gives $r\sim R(M/m)^{1/3}$, up to geometry and internal-structure factors. The <tidal tensor> gives the directional differential <acceleration>. A <tidal disruption event> near a black hole is a specific astronomical application.

= Tidal force
{parent=Tidal tensor}
{wiki}

Differential gravitational <acceleration>, multiplied by the affected <mass>, across a spatially extended body. In the linear approximation the <acceleration> difference is $T\Delta\boldsymbol r$, where $T$ is the <tidal tensor>. Stellar <tidal forces> limit the domain of a <circumplanetary orbit>; sufficiently strong ones can produce <tidal disruption>.

To isolate a <tidal force>, subtract the <gravitational acceleration> shared by the body as a whole. Let a reference <test particle> follow $\ddot{\mathbf R}=\mathbf g(\mathbf R)$, and let another have <position> $\mathbf R+\mathbf x$. In a <translating accelerated reference frame> with parallel, nonrotating axes,
$$
\ddot{\mathbf x}=\mathbf g(\mathbf R+\mathbf x)-\mathbf g(\mathbf R)=T(\mathbf R)\mathbf x+O\!\left(\frac{\mu|\mathbf x|^2}{R^4}\right)
$$

= Exact tidal acceleration between separated masses
{parent=Tidal force}
{title2=$\Delta a=2\mu L/[R^3(1-L^2/(4R^2))^2]$}

Stipulate a fixed source <point mass> $M=10^{12}\,\mathrm{kg}$, two equal <test particles> of <mass> $m=1\,\mathrm{kg}$, and $G=6.67430\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}$. This is a chosen model, not a measurement of a particular object. Place the <test particles> on the outward radial axis at $R-L/2$ and $R+L/2$, with midpoint distance $R=100\,\mathrm m$ and separation $L=2\,\mathrm m$. Neglect their mutual attraction, other sources and all supporting <forces>.

Each signed radial <gravitational acceleration> is inward:
$$
a_-=-\frac{\mu}{(R-L/2)^2}\approx-0.0068098153\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}},\qquad a_+=-\frac{\mu}{(R+L/2)^2}\approx-0.0065427899\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}.
$$
Their separation has outward relative <acceleration>
$$
\ddot L=a_+-a_-=\mu\left[\frac{1}{(R-L/2)^2}-\frac{1}{(R+L/2)^2}\right]=\frac{2\mu L}{R^3}\left(1-\frac{L^2}{4R^2}\right)^{-2}.
$$
The exact value is $2.6702540\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. The midpoint <tidal tensor> predicts $2\mu L/R^3=2.6697200\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, an underestimate of $0.019999\%$ relative to the exact value. For these equal $1\,\mathrm{kg}$ <masses>, the difference of external <forces> has the same numerical value in the <SI> unit of <force>, the <newton (unit)>. It is not an additional mutual <force> between the <test particles>.

The exact formula requires $L<2R$ and describes the instantaneous relative <acceleration>. Because the two sample <positions> are symmetric about the midpoint, the <Taylor expansion> error relative to the linear result starts at order $(L/R)^2$; a one-sided comparison with a reference point generally has relative error of order $L/R$. If either <test particle> enters an extended source, use that source's interior <Newtonian gravitational field> instead of extrapolating the singular <point-mass> model.

= Gravity magnitude versus tidal gradient
{parent=Tidal force}
{title2=$g=\mu/R^2,\quad \lambda_r=2\mu/R^3$}

$$
\frac{\Delta a}{g}\simeq\frac{2L}{R}.
$$
A small body can therefore experience strong common <gravitational acceleration> with a comparatively small <tidal force>. A measurement of $g$ alone does not determine the <tidal tensor>: sources at different distances can have the same $g$ but different spatial variation.

= Gravitational constant
{title2=$G$}
{parent=Newtonian gravitational field}
{wiki}

The <gravitational constant> $G$ sets the strength of <Newton's law of universal gravitation>. Two <point masses> $M$ and $m$ a center-to-center distance $r$ apart attract with <force> magnitude
$$
F=\frac{GMm}{r^2}.
$$
For fixed <masses> and separation, doubling $G$ would double the <force>. For bodies with <spherical symmetry>, the same exterior formula follows from the <spherical shell theorem>; for arbitrary extended bodies, add the attractions of their <mass density> elements instead. The <gravitational constant> connects a laboratory attraction to the <Newtonian gravitational field> controlling a <Kepler orbit>.

The <gravitational constant> $G$ and local <gravitational acceleration> $g$ are different quantities. Applying <Newton's second law> to a <test particle> gives $g=F/m=GM/r^2$ for one spherical source. The source's <mass> and the observer's <position> change $g$; $G$ remains the same parameter of the law. A local falling-body experiment therefore measures a field, not $G$ alone. On rotating <Earth>, an instrument fixed to the surface also responds to <centrifugal acceleration>, while surrounding <mass density> and altitude change the gravitational contribution.

<Dimensional analysis> explains the units. Denote the base <physical dimensions> of <length>, <mass> and <time> by $\mathsf L,\mathsf M,\mathsf T$. Since <force> has dimension $\mathsf M\mathsf L\mathsf T^{-2}$,
$$
[G]=\frac{[F][r]^2}{[M][m]}=\mathsf L^3\mathsf M^{-1}\mathsf T^{-2},\qquad G\text{ has SI unit }\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}.
$$
By contrast, $[g]=\mathsf L\mathsf T^{-2}$ and the <standard gravitational parameter> $\mu=GM$ has dimension $\mathsf L^3\mathsf T^{-2}$. A small numerical value of a dimensional constant depends on the units chosen: it is the resulting <force> for specified physical <masses> and separation that conveys the laboratory scale.

NIST's current complete listing gives
$$
G=6.67430(15)\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}
$$
for the *2022 CODATA adjustment*, the latest adjustment listed when checked on 14 October 2026. Parentheses indicate a <standard uncertainty> $u(G)=0.00015\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}$, approximately $22$ parts per million relative to $G$. The adjustment year is 2022; NIST's May 2024 data update is a different date.<Fundamental Physical Constants — Complete Listing by National Institute of Standards and Technology and others>[2022 CODATA adjustment, Newtonian constant of gravitation row: value, uncertainty and units]{ref} <Fundamental Physical Constants — Complete Listing by National Institute of Standards and Technology and others>[Current adjustment heading, next-adjustment notice and data-content update]{ref}

For an original scale calculation, stipulate two $1\,\mathrm{kg}$ <point masses> separated by $1\,\mathrm m$ and insert this recommended $G$: $F=6.67430\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm N$. The corresponding <acceleration> of either <mass> is $6.67430\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ at that instant. Reducing the separation to $0.10\,\mathrm m$ multiplies both by $100$. These are ideal model predictions, not observations; their tiny scale explains why stray <forces> matter.

Being a constant of a physical law does not make the numerical value exact. The <SI> fixes the numerical values of seven defining constants, including the <speed of light> and <Planck constant>; $G$ is determined experimentally. Exact unit definitions still require physical realizations with <measurement uncertainty>: calibrating a <length> or a <mass> does not become error-free.<The International System of Units (SI), ninth edition, SI Brochure>[English §§1.3 and 2.2–2.2.1, printed pp. 122–124: realization, seven defining constants and exact numerical values]{ref}

= Gravitational mass degeneracy
{parent=Gravitational constant}
{title2=$\mu=GM$}

An <orbit> around an isolated source constrains its <standard gravitational parameter> $\mu=GM$ rather than independently measuring the source's <mass> and the <gravitational constant>. In the <test particle> approximation, a <circular orbit> of radius $a$ and <orbital period> $T$ satisfies
$$
\frac{4\pi^2a}{T^2}=\frac{GM}{a^2},\qquad \mu=\frac{4\pi^2a^3}{T^2}.
$$
The left equation equates the required <centripetal acceleration> to the <gravitational acceleration>. More generally, a <Kepler orbit> depends on the same $\mu$. In the exact relative <two-body problem>, replace $GM$ by $G(M+m)$.

For any positive dimensionless number $q$, replacing $G$ by $qG$ and $M$ by $M/q$ leaves $GM$ and every predicted <test particle> <trajectory> unchanged. This is the <gravitational mass degeneracy>: more observations of the same <orbit> can refine $GM$ without separating its factors. A laboratory source whose <mass> is calibrated independently supplies the missing information.

Consequently an astronomical <mass> expressed in kilograms as $M=\mu/G$ inherits <measurement uncertainty> from $G$, even if $\mu$ is known much more precisely. To first order,
$$
\frac{\delta M}{M}=\frac{\delta\mu}{\mu}-\frac{\delta G}{G}.
$$
When the relative <standard uncertainty> of $\mu$ is negligible, that of $M$ is approximately $u(G)/G$. Conversely, the ratio of two source <masses> inferred using the same $G$ is $M_1/M_2=\mu_1/\mu_2$, so this common factor cancels. That distinction explains how excellent <orbit determination> can coexist with less precise absolute astronomical <masses>.

= Gravitational constant measurement sensitivity
{parent=Gravitational constant}
{title2=$G_{\mathrm{inferred}}=Fr^2/(Mm)$}

A laboratory determination of the <gravitational constant> must connect a sensor's reading to a gravitational signal from independently characterized <masses>. For an ideal two-<point-mass> <force> measurement, invert the model:
$$
G_{\mathrm{inferred}}=\frac{Fr^2}{Mm},\qquad \frac{\delta G}{G}\simeq\frac{\delta F}{F}+2\frac{\delta r}{r}-\frac{\delta M}{M}-\frac{\delta m}{m}.
$$
These are signed small changes obtained by taking the <logarithmic derivative>, not an instruction to add <standard uncertainties> with signs. For independent small input uncertainties, linear <propagation of uncertainty> instead gives
$$
\left(\frac{u(G)}G\right)^2\simeq\left(\frac{u(F)}F\right)^2+4\left(\frac{u(r)}r\right)^2+\left(\frac{u(M)}M\right)^2+\left(\frac{u(m)}m\right)^2.
$$
Correlated inputs require their <covariances> too. In particular, common geometric calibration errors must not be treated as independent simply because several distances were recorded.

For a concrete model check, hold the actual <force> and <masses> fixed but overestimate a true $r=0.100\,\mathrm m$ by $1\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. The exact inferred ratio is
$$
\frac{G_{\mathrm{inferred}}}{G_{\mathrm{true}}}=(1+10^{-5})^2=1.0000200001.
$$
The resulting bias is $20.0001$ parts per million, already comparable with the relative <standard uncertainty> of the recommended <gravitational constant>. A sensor can resolve very small changes while its inferred answer remains limited by source geometry. The example concerns one idealized separation; it is not an uncertainty estimate for any actual experiment.

Making the source <mass> larger or bringing it closer strengthens the signal, but also makes detailed <mass density> and geometry important. = Gravitational potential of a sinusoidal sheet
{parent=Newtonian gravitational potential}
{title2=$\Phi=-\frac{2\pi G\Sigma_k}k\cos(kx)e^{-k|y|}$}

A sinusoidal surface-density component $\Sigma_k\cos(kx)$ on $y=0$, with $k>0$ and translational invariance in the remaining direction, produces the displayed <gravitational potential>. <Laplace's equation> away from the sheet separates into a cosine in $x$ and a decaying exponential in $|y|$. Continuity across the sheet excludes a potential jump, while integrating <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity> across it gives $[\Phi_y]_{0^-}^{0^+}=4\pi G\Sigma_k\cos(kx)$. For a potential $C\cos(kx)e^{-k|y|}$ that derivative jump is $-2kC\cos(kx)$, giving $C=-2\pi G\Sigma_k/k$.

General sheet distributions are built by superposing their <Fourier modes>; decay alone does not restrict a harmonic potential to a single mode.

= Newtonian gravitational potential of a uniform solid sphere
{parent=Newtonian gravitational potential}
{c}
{title2=$\Phi(r)=-GM(3R^2-r^2)/(2R^3)\quad(r\leq R)$}

For radius $R$, constant <mass density> $\rho$ and $M=4\pi\rho R^3/3$, normalize the <Newtonian gravitational potential> to zero at infinity. Then
$$
\boxed{\Phi(r)=\begin{cases}-\dfrac{GM}{2R^3}(3R^2-r^2),&0\leq r\leq R,\\-GM/r,&r\geq R.\end{cases}}
$$
By the <spherical shell theorem>, each spherical shell contributes the potential of a point mass outside it and a constant inside it. Summing inner and outer shells gives $\Phi(r)=-4\pi G\rho[r^2/3+(R^2-r^2)/2]$ inside. Equivalently, angular integration of the free-space <Green function of the Laplacian> gives $\int_{-1}^1(r^2+r'^2-2rr'\mu)^{-1/2}d\mu=[r+r'-|r-r'|]/(rr')$. The resulting radial integral has the same value.

The potential and its first radial derivative are continuous at $R$. The interior <gravitational acceleration> is $-\nabla\Phi=-(GM/R^3)\mathbf r$, and $\nabla^2\Phi=4\pi G\rho$. At the centre $\Phi(0)=-3GM/(2R)$ while its gradient vanishes.

= Planetary quadrupole coefficient
{parent=Newtonian gravitational potential}
{title2=$J_2$}

The dimensionless coefficient $J_2$ describes the leading axisymmetric departure of a planet's external <Newtonian gravitational potential> from a monopole: $\Phi=-GM_p/r+[GM_pJ_2R_p^2/r^3]P_2(\cos\vartheta)$, where $\vartheta$ is colatitude from the spin axis. An oblate mass distribution has positive $J_2$. The <disturbing function> is minus the nonspherical potential term. On a circular inclined <Kepler orbit>, $\cos\vartheta=\sin I\sin M$, so <orbit averaging> gives $\overline{\mathcal R}_{J_2}=GM_pJ_2R_p^2a^{-3}(1/2-3\sin^2I/4)$. The exact circular nodal rate is $-3nJ_2(R_p/a)^2\cos I/2$; its small-tilt limit is $-3nJ_2(R_p/a)^2/2$.

= Oblate-quadrupole epicyclic precession
{parent=Planetary quadrupole coefficient}
{title2=$\kappa^2=n^2(1-3\alpha^2/R^2),\quad\nu^2=n^2(1+9\alpha^2/R^2)$}

For $\Phi=-\mu/r+\alpha^2\mu(3z^2-r^2)/r^5$, a nearly circular equatorial orbit has $n^2=\mu/R^3$ and azimuthal frequency $\Omega_c^2=n^2(1+3\alpha^2/R^2)$. The <radial epicyclic frequency> is $\kappa^2=R\,d\Omega_c^2/dR+4\Omega_c^2$, and the <vertical epicyclic frequency> is $\nu^2=\partial_z^2\Phi|_{z=0}$. Radial stability requires $R^2>3\alpha^2$. The <nodal precession> rate $\Omega_c-\nu$ is negative and the <apsidal precession> rate $\Omega_c-\kappa$ positive. These sign statements concern small eccentricity and inclination. For an arbitrary inclined orbit, the averaged <argument of periapsis> instead evolves as $(3/2)n\alpha^2[5\cos^2i-1]/[a^2(1-e^2)^2]$ and can regress.

= Axisymmetric logarithmic gravitational potential
{parent=Newtonian gravitational potential}
{title2=$\Phi=\tfrac12V^2\log(R^2+z^2/q^2)$}

An axisymmetric logarithmic <Newtonian gravitational potential> has spheroidal equipotential surfaces and a <flat galaxy rotation curve>: $R\Phi_R(R,0)=V^2$. The singular model is defined away from the origin; a fixed reference length makes the logarithm dimensionless. Its central $r^{-2}$ <mass density> cusp is locally integrable, but its total <mass> diverges at large radius.

= Density positivity for an axisymmetric logarithmic potential
{parent=Axisymmetric logarithmic gravitational potential}
{title2=$q^2\geq1/2$}

The <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity> gives
$$
\rho=\frac{V^2}{4\pi Gq^2}\frac{R^2+(2-q^{-2})z^2}{(R^2+q^{-2}z^2)^2}.
$$
For $V>0$, nonnegative <mass density> away from the origin is equivalent to $q^2\geq1/2$. Strict positivity requires $q^2>1/2$. This condition does not by itself guarantee a nonnegative <galactic distribution function>.

= Power-law gravitational potential
{parent=Newtonian gravitational potential}
{title2=$\Phi=-Ar^{-\beta}$}

A radial <Newtonian gravitational potential> $\Phi=-Ar^{-\beta}$ produces inward acceleration $A\beta r^{-\beta-1}$. The point-mass case has $\beta=1$ and $A=GM$. Treating the <power-law gravitational potential> as prescribed does not require it to be sourced by the flowing gas.

= Gravitational quadrupole potential of a point-mass binary
{parent=Newtonian gravitational potential}
{title2=$\Phi_2=-G\mu[3(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf r)^2-x^2r^2]/(2x^5)$}

For a <binary star> viewed far from its <centre of mass>, let $\mathbf r$ be the separation and $\mu$ the <reduced mass>, $M=M_1+M_2$ the total <mass>, $r=|\mathbf r|$ and $x=|\mathbf x|$. The <Taylor expansion> of the point-mass <Newtonian gravitational potential> has zero dipole because $\sum_A M_A\mathbf x_A=0$. Its second moment is $\sum_A M_Ax_{Ai}x_{Aj}=\mu r_i r_j$, so
$$
\Phi=-\frac{GM}{x}-\frac{G\mu}{2x^5}\left[3(\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf r)^2-x^2r^2\right]+O(GMr^3/x^4).
$$
This holds for an instantaneous separation in any orbit, not only a <circular orbit>. The <mass quadrupole moment> convention $Q_{ij}=\mu(r_ir_j-r^2\delta_{ij}/3)$ gives $\Phi_2=-3GQ_{ij}x_ix_j/(2x^5)$.

= Flattened power-law gravitational potential
{parent=Newtonian gravitational potential}
{title2=$\Phi=\Phi_0[R_0/(R^2+\lambda^2z^2)^{1/2}]^\beta$}

A flattened power-law <Newtonian gravitational potential> has equipotential spheroids $R^2+\lambda^2z^2=\text{constant}$. For $\Phi_0<0$ and $0<\beta<1$, its midplane <circular orbit> angular frequency satisfies $\Omega^2=-\beta\Phi_0R_0^\beta R^{-\beta-2}$.

= Density positivity for a flattened power-law potential
{parent=Flattened power-law gravitational potential}

Applying the <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity> to a <flattened power-law gravitational potential> yields
$$
\rho=-\frac{\beta\Phi_0R_0^\beta}{4\pi G}\frac{(\lambda^2-\beta)R^2+\lambda^2[2-(\beta+1)\lambda^2]z^2}{(R^2+\lambda^2z^2)^{(\beta+4)/2}}.
$$
For $0<\beta<1$, nonnegative <mass density> everywhere outside the origin requires and is ensured by $\beta\leq\lambda^2\leq2/(\beta+1)$. The central density singularity is locally integrable and carries no point mass, although the total mass diverges at large radius in this scale-free model.

= Newtonian potential of a point mass
{title2=$\Phi(r)=-GM/r$}
{parent=Newtonian gravitational potential}
{c}

= Gravitational potential
{synonym}

The <Newtonian potential of a point mass> $M$ at the origin is
$$
\Phi(r)=-\frac{GM}{r},\qquad r>0,\qquad \Phi(\infty)=0.
$$
It is the free-space <Green function of the Laplacian> with the normalization of the <Poisson equation for Newtonian gravity>. Differentiating gives inward <gravitational acceleration> $-GM/r^2$. With $z=r/r_0$, the normalized <Newtonian gravitational potential> is $\Phi/(GM/r_0)=-1/z$; at $z=1,2,4$ it equals $-1,-1/2,-1/4$.

For a radially outward launch from $r_0$, <conservation of mechanical energy> in the fixed-source <test particle> approximation gives the minimum <escape velocity>:
$$
\frac{E}{m}=\frac{v_0^2}{2}-\frac{GM}{r_0}=0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_{\mathrm{esc}}=\sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r_0}}.
$$
At this threshold the <speed> tends to zero only at infinite separation. The model neglects air resistance, external <tidal forces>, and <general relativity>. <University Physics Volume 1 by William Moebs and others/13.3 Gravitational Potential Energy and Total Energy>{ref}

A <circular orbit> at $r_0$ instead requires tangential <speed> $v_c=\sqrt{GM/r_0}$. Choosing units with $G=M=r_0=1$ gives $g_0=1$, $v_c=1$, and $v_{\mathrm{esc}}=\sqrt2\simeq1.41421$. A radial launch at <speed> $1$ reaches $r=2$ and returns; the same <speed> directed tangentially gives a <circular orbit>. <University Physics Volume 1 by William Moebs and others/13.4 Satellite Orbits and Energy>{ref}

= Self-gravity in a thin spherical shell expansion
{parent=Newtonian potential of a point mass}
{title2=$W_{\rm self}=-GM_s^2/(2R)$}

A shell of fixed mass $M_s$ and radius $R$ has self-energy $-GM_s^2/(2R)$. Its inward generalized force opposing an increase of radius is $GM_s^2/(2R^2)$. Including this in a swept-gas shell threshold changes an interior-only factor $f(1-f)$ to $f(1-f/2)$ when the original total mass is held fixed.

= Gravitational potential of a truncated inverse-radius sphere
{parent=Newtonian potential of a point mass}
{title2=$\Phi(r)=-k^\prime(2r_0-r)$}

The sphere $\rho=k^\prime/(2\pi Gr)$ for $r<r_0$ has $M(r)=k^\prime r^2/G$ and $\Phi(r)=-k^\prime(2r_0-r)$, with zero potential at infinity. Its finite central potential contrasts with the logarithmic central divergence of a truncated inverse-square density.

= Axisymmetric gravitational potential of a displaced cylindrical interface
{parent=Newtonian potential of a point mass}
{title2=$\psi_{\rm in}=-4\pi G\rho R K_0(kR)I_0(kr)\eta$}

Consider a uniform-density cylinder with an axisymmetric radial <Fourier mode> $\eta=\epsilon e^{ikz}$. Its displaced boundary gives a first-order <surface mass density> $\rho\eta$, although the interior density perturbation vanishes. The gravitational <Poisson equation> reduces to <Laplace equation> away from the interface. Regularity internally and decay externally give $\psi_{\rm in}=AI_0(kr)e^{ikz}$ and $\psi_{\rm out}=BK_0(kr)e^{ikz}$. Continuity at $R$ and the derivative jump $\psi'_{\rm out}-\psi'_{\rm in}=4\pi G\rho\epsilon e^{ikz}$, together with $I_0(\kappa)K'_0(\kappa)-I'_0(\kappa)K_0(\kappa)=-1/\kappa$, give the title amplitude. The prime differentiates the <modified Bessel function> argument. This axial mode is different from an azimuthal mode on a <gravitational potential of a displaced cylindrical interface>.

= Spherically symmetric potential
{parent=Newtonian gravitational potential}

A spherically symmetric potential depends only on distance $r$ from one point. Its force is central, so angular momentum is conserved.

= Newtonian gravitational potential energy
{c}
{parent=Newtonian gravitational field}

Two point masses $m_1,m_2$ separated by distance $r$ have <potential energy>
$$
V(r)=-\frac{Gm_1m_2}{r}.
$$
The corresponding attractive force is $-\nabla V$.

This is the Newtonian point-mass specialization of <gravitational energy>, with the reference value chosen at infinite separation.

= Spherical binding-energy mass integral
{parent=Newtonian gravitational potential energy}
{title2=$W=-\dfrac G2\int_0^\infty M(r)^2r^{-2}\,dr$}

Assembling a spherical mass distribution in shells gives <gravitational potential energy> $W=-G\int M(r)\,dM(r)/r$. Integration by parts gives the displayed identity when $M(r)^2/r$ vanishes at both endpoints. It includes each interacting pair once; no extra factor of one half belongs in the original shell-assembly integral.

= Minimum-separation binding-energy bounds
{parent=Newtonian gravitational potential energy}
{title2=$B_0\leq rU\leq A_0$}

For positive <masses>, let $r$ be the actual minimum pairwise separation and use positive binding <energy> $U=\sum_{j<k}Gm_jm_k/r_{jk}$. If $m_{(1)},m_{(2)}$ are the smallest <masses>, then $Gm_{(1)}m_{(2)}\leq rU\leq G\sum_{j<k}m_jm_k$. Thus $U^{-1}$ is comparable to $r$ with constants depending only on the masses.

= Negative-energy minimum-separation upper bound
{parent=Minimum-separation binding-energy bounds}
{title2=$r\leq A_0/|E|$}

With $E=T-U<0$ and $T\geq0$, the positive binding <energy> satisfies $U\geq|E|$. The <minimum-separation binding-energy bounds> imply $r\leq A_0/|E|$. At least one pair stays close; this does not bound every particle's distance or exclude a <negative-energy gravitational collision>.

= Cavendish experiment
{parent=Newtonian gravitational field}
{c}
{wiki}

The <Cavendish experiment> makes the attraction predicted by <Newton's law of universal gravitation> measurable between laboratory <masses>. Two small test <masses> hang at the ends of a horizontal rod in a <Cavendish torsion balance>; nearby larger source <masses> exert a tiny <torque>. A thin suspension fiber supplies an opposing <torque> as it twists. Measuring its twist and calibrating its <torsion constant> lets an experimenter infer the gravitational <force> and, in a modern analysis, the <gravitational constant>. https://www.phys.uconn.edu/~hamilton/phys258/N/cav.pdf[University of Connecticut, Physics 258/259, The Cavendish Experiment: apparatus and equations of motion, pp. 1–3]{ref}

The beautiful feature is that the two horizontal <forces> can cancel as vectors while their <torques> add. The instrument converts weak attraction into a rotation, with sensitivity set by the fiber's <torsion constant>. Earth's much larger downward <gravitational weight> is supported by the suspension rather than supplying the intended <torque> about its vertical axis. A laboratory measurement can therefore probe the same <Newtonian gravitational field> that governs the <two-body problem>.

Henry Cavendish's original paper was *Experiments to determine the Density of the Earth*, published in *Philosophical Transactions*, volume 88 (1798), pp. 469–526, and read on 21 June 1798. On p. 469 he credits John Michell with the apparatus idea and explains that Michell died before using it; the instrument reached Cavendish through Francis John Hyde Wollaston. Cavendish's stated aim was Earth's mean <mass density>. Calling the experiment a determination of the <gravitational constant> is a modern interpretation, not Cavendish's own formulation of his result. https://www.famaf.unc.edu.ar/~wolf/FisicaI/Phil.%20Trans.%20R.%20Soc.%20Lond.-1798-Cavendish-rstl.1798.0022.pdf[Cavendish (1798), original paper, opening p. 469; DOI 10.1098/rstl.1798.0022]{ref}

The connection between Earth's mean <mass density> and the <gravitational constant> follows in a spherical, nonrotating model of <Earth>. With Earth <mass> $M_E$, radius $R_E$, and surface <gravitational acceleration> $g$, the <spherical shell theorem> and <Newton's law of universal gravitation> give
$$
g=\frac{GM_E}{R_E^2},\qquad \overline\rho_E=\frac{3M_E}{4\pi R_E^3},\qquad G=\frac{3g}{4\pi R_E\overline\rho_E}.
$$
Thus a local comparison of gravitational attractions, together with knowledge of Earth's size and surface <gravitational acceleration>, reaches a property of the entire planet. These are modern equations explaining the connection, not equations quoted from Cavendish's paper.

For a worked example, use an explicitly simplified two-near-pair model. Each test <point mass> is $m$, each source <point mass> is $M$, and each <lever arm> is $L$, so the rod's full <length> is $2L$. Neglect the rod's <mass>. At zero twist put the test <masses> at $(-L,0)$ and $(L,0)$, and the sources at $(-L,-r)$ and $(L,r)$. Here $r$ is the near-pair center separation. Ignore each source's attraction on the test <mass> at the opposite end. Evaluate the two near-pair <forces> at zero twist and treat their <torque> as constant for a small twist, requiring $L|\theta|\ll r$. This is a teaching model, not a precision model of Cavendish's actual apparatus.

By <Newton's law of universal gravitation>, each retained <force> has magnitude $F=GmM/r^2$. The left <force> points down and the right <force> points up. Using <torque> about the suspension axis, both contributions are counterclockwise when viewed from above:
$$
\tau_{z,\mathrm{left}}=(-L)(-F)=LF,\qquad \tau_{z,\mathrm{right}}=LF,\qquad |\tau_g|=2L\frac{GmM}{r^2}.
$$
The net retained horizontal <force> is zero, yet the net <torque> is positive. Moving the sources to $(-L,r)$ and $(L,-r)$ reverses both <forces> and hence the <torque>. In a real apparatus, the opposite source also attracts each test <mass>; that correction is explicitly included in the university derivation. https://www.phys.uconn.edu/~hamilton/phys258/N/cav.pdf[University of Connecticut, The Cavendish Experiment, p. 2, equation (1): opposite-source correction]{ref}

For illustrative parameters, take $m=0.010\,\mathrm{kg}$, $M=1.0\,\mathrm{kg}$, $r=0.050\,\mathrm m$, $L=0.10\,\mathrm m$, and <torsion constant> $\kappa=1.0\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N\,m/rad}$. Use $G=6.67430\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}$, the central value in NIST's 2022 CODATA table; that table gives standard uncertainty $0.00015\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}$. This is an input for the example, not a measurement made with these assumed parameters. <Fundamental Physical Constants — Complete Listing by National Institute of Standards and Technology and others>{ref}
$$
F=\frac{(6.67430\times10^{-11})(0.010)(1.0)}{(0.050)^2}=2.66972\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm N,\qquad |\tau_g|=2(0.10)F=5.33944\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N\,m}.
$$
This sub-nanonewton <force> produces a measurable twist in the <Cavendish torsion balance>. The displayed digits make the toy calculation reproducible; they do not describe experimental precision.

= Cavendish torsion balance
{parent=Cavendish experiment}
{c}

A <Cavendish torsion balance> is a <torsion balance> arranged to measure gravitational <torque> from nearby source <masses>. Its suspended horizontal rod rotates about a vertical fiber. In the linear elastic regime, a twist <angle> $\theta$, measured from the fiber's untwisted orientation, gives a restoring <torque> $\tau_{\mathrm{fiber}}=-\kappa\theta$, where $\kappa>0$ is the <torsion constant>. The minus sign is essential: the fiber resists the twist. Under an approximately constant gravitational <torque>, the rotational equation using <moment of inertia> $I$ and <angular acceleration> $\ddot\theta$ is
$$
I\ddot\theta=\tau_g-\kappa\theta,\qquad \theta_{\mathrm{eq}}=\frac{\tau_g}{\kappa}.
$$
At <equilibrium>, the two <torques> balance. The <angle> is a displacement from the fiber's reference orientation, not the largest excursion during an <oscillation>. https://www.phys.uconn.edu/~hamilton/phys258/N/cav.pdf[University of Connecticut, The Cavendish Experiment, pp. 2–3: restoring torque and equilibrium deflection]{ref}

For the toy parameters in the <Cavendish experiment>, the predicted <equilibrium> twist is
$$
\theta_{\mathrm{eq}}=\frac{5.33944\times10^{-11}}{1.0\times10^{-8}}=0.00533944\,\mathrm{rad}\simeq0.3059^\circ.
$$
Here $L|\theta_{\mathrm{eq}}|/r\simeq0.0107$, consistent with using a small geometric displacement in the toy model, but not an estimate of its total <measurement error>. Reversing the source positions ideally takes the <equilibrium> <angle> from $+\theta_{\mathrm{eq}}$ to $-\theta_{\mathrm{eq}}$: the magnitude of the change is $2|\theta_{\mathrm{eq}}|\simeq0.6119^\circ$. A stable zero offset then cancels in the difference. A real reading may track a reflected light spot; the <law of reflection> doubles a mirror's rotation in the outgoing beam, so the spot's angular change must not be confused with the rod's <angle>. https://sciencedemonstrations.fas.harvard.edu/presentations/cavendish-experiment[Harvard Natural Sciences Lecture Demonstrations, Cavendish Experiment: mirror readout and source reversal]{ref}

How can the fiber's <torsion constant> be found without already knowing the gravitational <force>? Measure the <oscillation period> and calculate the <moment of inertia>. For the same toy rod, neglecting its <mass> and treating both test <masses> as <point masses>, $I=mL^2+mL^2=2mL^2$. With the source <torque> absent or treated as constant, let $\phi=\theta-\theta_{\mathrm{eq}}$ be a small departure from <equilibrium>. Neglect <damping> and any change of gravitational <torque> with <angle>. The rotational equation becomes <simple harmonic motion>:
$$
I\ddot\phi+\kappa\phi=0,\qquad \phi(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\delta),\qquad \omega=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{I}},\qquad T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac I\kappa},\qquad \kappa=\frac{4\pi^2I}{T^2}.
$$
Here $A$ is the angular amplitude, $\delta$ specifies the initial phase, and $\omega$ is the <angular frequency>. A softer fiber gives a larger <equilibrium> twist but also a longer <oscillation period>: greater sensitivity costs observation time. An undamped <oscillation> does not settle at <equilibrium>; <damping> is needed for settling. https://www.phys.uconn.edu/~hamilton/phys258/N/cav.pdf[University of Connecticut, The Cavendish Experiment, p. 3, equation (7): period calibration neglecting damping]{ref}

The toy <moment of inertia> and undamped <oscillation period> are
$$
I=2(0.010)(0.10)^2=2.0\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2},\qquad T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2.0\times10^{-4}}{1.0\times10^{-8}}}\simeq888.5766\,\mathrm s\simeq14.81\,\mathrm{min}.
$$
Timing this <oscillation period> would recover the assumed <torsion constant>. The units check explains the result: <torque> divided by <torsion constant> is an <angle> in <radians>, and $I/\kappa$ has units of <time> squared when <radians> are treated as dimensionless in <SI>.

{title=Undamped small torsional oscillations calibrate the restoring torque: a softer fiber has a longer period}

Combining the <equilibrium> twist with the period calibration, the two-near-pair model would infer the <gravitational constant> as
$$
G=\frac{\kappa|\theta_{\mathrm{eq}}|r^2}{2LmM}=\frac{4\pi^2Lr^2|\theta_{\mathrm{eq}}|}{MT^2}.
$$
The test <mass> cancels in the second expression because it enters both the gravitational <torque> and the toy <moment of inertia>. If the measured quantity is the magnitude $|\Delta\theta|$ of the change between opposite source positions, use $|\theta_{\mathrm{eq}}|=|\Delta\theta|/2$. Substituting the toy twist and <oscillation period> returns the input $G$; it is a consistency check, not independent experimental evidence.

The sensitivity of a <Cavendish torsion balance> also explains its practical difficulty. Air motion can exert unwanted <torque>; unequal <temperatures> can drive that motion. Cavendish discusses this effect on p. 471 and describes observing from outside a closed room to avoid disturbing the instrument. https://www.infinitoteatrodelcosmo.it/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/cavendish-1997-xxi-experiments-to-determine-the-density-of-the-earth.pdf[Cavendish (1798), original paper, p. 471: temperature-driven air currents and remote observation]{ref} Mechanical vibrations can excite the same slow <oscillations> used to calibrate the <torsion constant>, while changes in the fiber or source separations alter the inferred <force>. Precision therefore depends on resolving the <equilibrium> <angle>, calibrating the full suspended <moment of inertia> and <torsion constant>, and modeling the actual source geometry. The two-near-pair model omits opposite-source attraction, rod contributions, finite-size effects, <damping>, and the variation of gravitational <torque> as the rod turns. https://sciencedemonstrations.fas.harvard.edu/presentations/cavendish-experiment[Harvard Cavendish demonstration: sensitivity, settling, and separation-dependent measurement]{ref}
