== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

In a frame rotating with <angular velocity> $\boldsymbol\Omega$, a particle of relative <velocity> $\mathbf v$ experiences the <Coriolis force>
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F_C=-2m\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v.}
$$
For nearly horizontal atmospheric motion, the relevant vertical <rotation> component is $\Omega\sin\ell$, with latitude $\ell$. In the northern hemisphere the horizontal deflection is to the right of the motion. A low-pressure center produces an inward pressure-gradient force; an anticlockwise circulation has an outward <Coriolis force> to oppose it. A high-pressure center instead supports clockwise circulation, with inward <Coriolis force> opposing the outward pressure-gradient force.

The sense reverses in the southern hemisphere. There, anticlockwise circulation is anticyclonic and corresponds to high <atmospheric pressure>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{Earth northern hemisphere: low anticlockwise, high clockwise; Jupiter's Great Red Spot: high pressure.}}
$$
This is the large-scale rotating-fluid interpretation; <friction>, centrifugal <acceleration> and departures from ideal balance modify actual wind profiles. NASA's description independently identifies the southern, counterclockwise <Great Red Spot> as a high-pressure system.<The Great Red Spot: An Ancient Storm in Jupiter's Atmosphere by National Aeronautics and Space Administration>[Description comparing the southern-hemisphere counterclockwise Great Red Spot with low-pressure terrestrial hurricanes]{ref}

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $\mathsf R(\alpha)$ denote the planar <rotation matrix>, and choose the initial cup center on the positive $x$ axis, as in the requested equations. Its <trajectory> is $\mathbf C(t)=R(\cos\Omega t,\sin\Omega t)$. The handle rotates relative to the radial line at signed rate $\phi$, so its absolute <angular velocity> is $\nu=\Omega+\phi$. A positive recurrence time is $2\pi/|\phi|$; the sign records <rotation> direction.

Writing $\mathbf q=(x_1,y_1)$, the relation is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf r=\mathbf C+\mathsf R(\nu t)\mathbf q,}
$$
or explicitly
$$
x=R\cos\Omega t+x_1\cos\nu t-y_1\sin\nu t,
\qquad
y=R\sin\Omega t+x_1\sin\nu t+y_1\cos\nu t.
$$
One obtains the same result by rotating cup coordinates through $\phi t$ into the radially aligned axes, then rotating through $\Omega t$ into the inertial axes.

At $\phi=-\Omega$, $\nu=0$, and
$$
\boxed{x=R\cos\Omega t+x_1,\qquad y=R\sin\Omega t+y_1.}
$$
The axes maintain a fixed orientation in inertial space while their origin accelerates around a circle. This is translation without <rotation> of the cup frame; it is not an inertial frame because the origin accelerates. In particular its inertial correction is a spatially uniform fictitious force, rather than a <Coriolis force> from <rotation> of these axes.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Treat the marble as a frictionless point particle; no size, rolling constraint or <friction> law is supplied. Neglect its vertical <kinetic energy> as requested, but retain the gravitational <potential energy> $mgz=m\omega^2(x_1^2+y_1^2)/2$.

For completeness keep the general constant cup <rotation> rate $\nu=\Omega+\phi$. In cup components the center's <velocity> is $\mathbf w=R\Omega(\sin\phi t,\cos\phi t)$. With $\mathsf J(x,y)=(-y,x)$, the horizontal <velocity> norm is $|\dot{\mathbf q}+\nu\mathsf J\mathbf q+\mathbf w|$. Thus
$$
\boxed{L=\frac m2\left[(\dot x_1-\nu y_1+R\Omega\sin\phi t)^2+
(\dot y_1+\nu x_1+R\Omega\cos\phi t)^2\right]
-\frac{m\omega^2}{2}(x_1^2+y_1^2).}
$$
In the nonrotating translating case $\phi=-\Omega$, this reduces to
$$
\boxed{L=\frac m2\left[(\dot x_1-R\Omega\sin\Omega t)^2+
(\dot y_1+R\Omega\cos\Omega t)^2\right]-\frac{m\omega^2}{2}(x_1^2+y_1^2).}
$$
The time-only constant $mR^2\Omega^2/2$ may be dropped. Integrating the cross-velocity term by parts gives an equivalent <Lagrangian>
$$
L'=\frac m2(\dot x_1^2+\dot y_1^2)-\frac{m\omega^2}{2}(x_1^2+y_1^2)
+mR\Omega^2(x_1\cos\Omega t+y_1\sin\Omega t).
$$
It differs by a <total derivative> and a time-only term, so gives the same <trajectories> but different expressions for the <canonical momenta>. The small-vertical-velocity approximation is typically consistent at small surface slope $\omega^2|\mathbf q|/g$; it need not remain valid during unlimited resonant growth.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For the original <Lagrangian> of <1/c/solution>, differentiation with respect to the generalized <velocities> gives
$$
\boxed{p_{x_1}=m(\dot x_1-\nu y_1+R\Omega\sin\phi t),\qquad
p_{y_1}=m(\dot y_1+\nu x_1+R\Omega\cos\phi t).}
$$
In contrast, using $L'$ in the nonrotating case gives $p'_{x_1}=m\dot x_1$, $p'_{y_1}=m\dot y_1$; this is the expected <momentum> shift under adding a <total derivative>.

The <Euler-Lagrange equations> in the general cup frame are
$$
\ddot{\mathbf q}+2\nu\mathsf J\dot{\mathbf q}+(\omega^2-\nu^2)\mathbf q
=R\Omega^2(\cos\phi t,-\sin\phi t).
$$
These include the Coriolis and centrifugal terms when the cup axes really rotate. Set $z_q=x_1+iy_1$ to obtain
$$
\ddot z_q+2i\nu\dot z_q+(\omega^2-\nu^2)z_q=R\Omega^2e^{-i\phi t}.
$$
For $\omega^2\ne\Omega^2$, its complete solution is
$$
\boxed{z_q(t)=A e^{i(\omega-\nu)t}+B e^{-i(\omega+\nu)t}
+\frac{R\Omega^2}{\omega^2-\Omega^2}e^{-i\phi t},}
$$
where the two complex constants provide the four real initial data. The driving denominator is independent of cup-axis <rotation>: <rotation> changes the coordinate description, not the physical trap frequency.

In the requested special case $\nu=0$, an equivalent real form is
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
x_1(t)&=a_x\cos\omega t+b_x\sin\omega t+D\cos\Omega t,\\
y_1(t)&=a_y\cos\omega t+b_y\sin\omega t+D\sin\Omega t,
\qquad D=\frac{R\Omega^2}{\omega^2-\Omega^2}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
For example $a_x=x_1(0)-D$, $b_x=\dot x_1(0)/\omega$, $a_y=y_1(0)$ and $b_y=[\dot y_1(0)-D\Omega]/\omega$ determine the constants.

At <resonance> $\omega^2=\Omega^2$ with nonzero $\Omega$, replace the particular term by
$$
\boxed{z_{q,\rm res}(t)=-\frac{iR\Omega}{2}\,t\,e^{-i\phi t}.}
$$
In the nonrotating cup frame this has components $(R\Omega t\sin\Omega t/2,-R\Omega t\cos\Omega t/2)$, plus the homogeneous solution. Secular growth eventually invalidates a finite cup or the neglected vertical <kinetic energy>. If the ride does not rotate, its forcing vanishes and ordinary free oscillations remain.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

A <constant of motion> is a function $I(\mathbf q,\mathbf p,t)$ whose <total derivative> vanishes along every physical <trajectory>. It may depend explicitly on time; arbitrary functions of conserved quantities are also conserved, so a count normally concerns a specified independent set or symmetry generators.

For a fixed-time infinitesimal variation $\delta q_i=\epsilon\xi_i(\mathbf q,t)$, suppose $\delta L=\epsilon\,dB/dt$. On a solution of the <Euler-Lagrange equations>,
$$
\frac{\delta L}{\epsilon}=\sum_i\left(L_{q_i}\xi_i+p_i\dot\xi_i\right)
=\frac d{dt}\sum_ip_i\xi_i.
$$
Therefore <Noether theorem> gives
$$
\boxed{I=\sum_i p_i\xi_i-B,\qquad \frac{dI}{dt}=0.}
$$
For a transformation also changing time by $\delta t=\epsilon\tau$, the <action> variation includes $L\,d(\delta t)/dt$, and the transformed <velocity> varies by $\epsilon(\dot\xi_i-\dot q_i\dot\tau)$. The same <integration by parts> gives the general point-symmetry charge
$$
\boxed{I=\sum_ip_i\xi_i-H\tau-B.}
$$
Thus a <time-translation symmetry> gives the <energy>, a spatial translation gives the corresponding total <momentum>, and a <rotation> gives its angular-momentum generator. The required symmetry is a symmetry of the <action>, allowing a boundary term; demanding that the numerical value of the <Lagrangian> remain strictly unchanged is unnecessarily restrictive. Conversely, explicit time dependence of a <Lagrangian> generally prevents conservation of its <energy> even while other charges survive.<Classical Dynamics by David Tong>[Chapter 2, section 2.6 and 2.6.1, continuous symmetries, conserved charges and Noether's theorem]{ref}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Assume positive, pairwise distinct masses and generic nonzero $\omega,\lambda$. Let $p_{x_i}=m_i\dot x_i$, and similarly for $y,z$. The three uncoupled $x$ oscillators each conserve
$$
\boxed{E_{x_i}=\frac{p_{x_i}^2}{2m_i}+\frac12m_i\omega^2x_i^2\qquad(i=1,2,3).}
$$
Uniform translation of all $y_i$ conserves $P_y=\sum_i p_{y_i}$. The $z_1,z_2$ interaction permits their common translation, while $z_3$ is entirely free, giving two independent <momenta> $P_{z,12}=p_{z_1}+p_{z_2}$ and $p_{z_3}$. The interacting $z_1,z_2$ subsystem is autonomous and has its own conserved <energy>. A useful basic collection is therefore
$$
\boxed{P_y,\quad P_{z,12},\quad p_{z_3},\quad
E_{z,12}=\frac{p_{z_1}^2}{2m_1}+\frac{p_{z_2}^2}{2m_2}+V(z_1-z_2),\quad E_{x_1},E_{x_2},E_{x_3}.}
$$
The total <energy> is generally not conserved. With $\kappa=\lambda(m_1-m_2)$, its derivative is $\dot H=-\kappa\sin(\kappa t)U_{12}(y_1-y_2)$, which is generically nonzero.

**Unequal masses do not remove every <rotation> charge in the oscillator sector.** Put $X_i=\sqrt{m_i}x_i$, $P_i=p_{x_i}/\sqrt{m_i}$. This is a <canonical transformation> and makes that sector an <isotropic harmonic oscillator>. Its <rotations> conserve
$$
\boxed{\mathcal J_{ij}=X_iP_j-X_jP_i
=\sqrt{\frac{m_i}{m_j}}x_ip_{x_j}-\sqrt{\frac{m_j}{m_i}}x_jp_{x_i}.}
$$
These <mass-weighted oscillator symmetries> exist already at distinct masses. The energies and three <rotation> charges are not all functionally independent; the three-oscillator sector has at most five independent time-independent integrals on regular <trajectories>.

There are further simple explicitly time-dependent constants, such as $x_i\cos\omega t-(\dot x_i/\omega)\sin\omega t$ and $x_i\sin\omega t+(\dot x_i/\omega)\cos\omega t$, and center-of-mass boost constants. For example $MY-tP_y$ is constant for $M=\sum_im_i$ and $Y=\sum_im_iy_i/M$. These illustrate why an unrestricted raw number of conserved functions is not meaningful.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

At $m_1=m_2$, the explicitly time-dependent coefficient becomes one. This restores <time-translation symmetry>, and the total <energy> becomes conserved. No further analogous restoration occurs merely from setting $m_3$ equal to them: the three arbitrary interaction functions still distinguish their arguments and particle labels.

If the question counts the extra **ordinary unweighted <rotations>** of the $x$ coordinate labels, the conventional generator count is as follows. For $m_1=m_2\ne m_3$, <rotations> of $(x_1,x_2)$ preserve both the kinetic and oscillator terms, so one ordinarily displayed <rotation> charge joins the restored <energy>: two displayed generators beyond the elementary list. For three equal masses, the full ordinary <rotation> group of $(x_1,x_2,x_3)$ has three generators; together with <energy> this gives four, or two more than in the preceding equal-pair case.

There is a necessary qualification to interpreting those numbers as genuinely new independent conservation laws. The weighted charges in <2/a/solution> deform continuously to those same ordinary <rotation> charges at equality. For example $\mathcal J_{12}$ is conserved for every positive $m_1,m_2$ and becomes $x_1p_{x_2}-x_2p_{x_1}$ when the masses agree. Thus
$$
\boxed{\begin{gathered}
\text{Restricted ordinary-coordinate generator count: }2\text{ and }4.\\
\text{New generic energy conservation: }1\text{ at }m_1=m_2;\quad
0\text{ further at }m_3=m_1.
\end{gathered}}
$$
The restriction must be stated if using the first count. Equal <mass> does not create three previously absent independent oscillator integrals. If $\lambda=0$, <energy> conservation was already present; special choices of the interaction functions can add further symmetries and are excluded by the generic assumption.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

With $m_1=m_2=m_3=m$, the explicit <energy> and ordinary <rotation> generators just discussed are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
H={}&\sum_i\frac{p_{x_i}^2+p_{y_i}^2+p_{z_i}^2}{2m}
+\frac{m\omega^2}{2}\sum_i x_i^2\\
&+U_{12}(y_1-y_2)+U_{13}(y_1-y_3)+V(z_1-z_2),\\
J_{12}&=x_1p_{x_2}-x_2p_{x_1},\\
J_{23}&=x_2p_{x_3}-x_3p_{x_2},\qquad
J_{31}=x_3p_{x_1}-x_1p_{x_3}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The <energy> is constant because the drive factor no longer depends on time. For each <rotation> charge, differentiating and using $\ddot x_i=-\omega^2x_i$ gives $\dot J_{ij}=m(x_i\ddot x_j-x_j\ddot x_i)=0$.

These are angular <momenta> in the auxiliary three-dimensional space whose coordinates are the particle labels $(x_1,x_2,x_3)$. They are not the physical spatial <angular momentum> $\sum_i\mathbf r_i\times\mathbf p_i$; generic $U_{12},U_{13},V$ do not have physical rotational invariance. They are precisely the equal-mass representatives of the weighted charges already present in <2/a/solution>. The new <energy> integral supplies the autonomous relative-$y$ dynamics not present in the driven case.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For an <action functional> $S[q]=\int_{t_i}^{t_f}L(q,\dot q,t)dt$, vary a path while fixing its endpoint coordinates. <Integration by parts> gives
$$
\delta S=\left[\sum_i\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}\delta q_i\right]_{t_i}^{t_f}
+\int_{t_i}^{t_f}\sum_i\left[\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}-\frac d{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}\right]\delta q_i\,dt.
$$
The endpoint term vanishes. Since the interior variations are arbitrary, stationarity gives
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}=0.}
$$
Define <canonical momentum> $p_i=\partial L/\partial\dot q_i$. For a regular <velocity> Hessian, these relations can be inverted for $\dot q(q,p,t)$, and the <Legendre transform> defines
$$
\boxed{H(q,p,t)=\sum_ip_i\dot q_i-L(q,\dot q,t).}
$$
Its differential is $dH=\sum_i(\dot q_i\,dp_i-L_{q_i}\,dq_i)-L_tdt$. Inserting the <Euler-Lagrange equations> already suggests the canonical equations.

To derive them directly from an <principle of stationary action>, vary the first-order <action> $I[q,p]=\int(\sum_ip_i\dot q_i-H)dt$ with independent $q,p$ and fixed endpoint $q$. Integrating $p_i\delta\dot q_i$ by parts gives
$$
\delta I=\int\sum_i\left[(\dot q_i-H_{p_i})\delta p_i+(-\dot p_i-H_{q_i})\delta q_i\right]dt.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\dot q_i=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i},\qquad
\dot p_i=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}.}
$$
The endpoint <momenta> need not be fixed. A singular <velocity> Hessian instead requires a constrained <Hamiltonian> formulation; the ordinary Legendre inversion is an assumption, not an identity valid for every <Lagrangian>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For $n$ degrees of freedom, <phase space> is the $2n$-dimensional space of <canonical coordinates> $(q_1,\ldots,q_n,p_1,\ldots,p_n)$. Initial data at one point determine a <trajectory> under the <Hamilton equations>.

<Liouville's theorem> in <Hamiltonian> mechanics states that this flow preserves the canonical volume element $d\Gamma=\prod_i dq_i\,dp_i$. Its <divergence> is
$$
\nabla_{q,p}\cdot(\dot q,\dot p)
=\sum_i\left(\frac{\partial^2H}{\partial q_i\partial p_i}-\frac{\partial^2H}{\partial p_i\partial q_i}\right)=0.
$$
If $J(t)$ is the <Jacobian determinant> of the flow from initial data, the determinant evolution formula gives $\dot J=J\nabla\cdot(\dot q,\dot p)=0$. Since $J(0)=1$, it remains one, proving preservation of every advected phase volume. The proof also applies to smooth explicitly time-dependent Hamiltonians.

For an ensemble density $\rho(q,p,t)$, probability conservation and the same vanishing <divergence> give
$$
\boxed{\partial_t\rho+\sum_i\left(\dot q_i\partial_{q_i}\rho+\dot p_i\partial_{p_i}\rho\right)=0,
\quad\text{or}\quad \partial_t\rho+\{\rho,H\}=0.}
$$
Thus the fine-grained density is constant along the <Hamiltonian> <trajectories>, although the ensemble may shear, stretch and develop fine structure. This is not a claim that coordinate-space volume or every lower-dimensional projection is conserved.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Solving $\dot x=p/m$, $\dot p=-m\omega^2x$ gives the linear phase flow
$$
\begin{pmatrix}x(t)\\p(t)\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\omega t&\sin\omega t/(m\omega)\\
-m\omega\sin\omega t&\cos\omega t\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}x_0\\p_0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its <Jacobian determinant> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial(x(t),p(t))}{\partial(x_0,p_0)}
=\cos^2\omega t+\sin^2\omega t=1.}
$$
Hence every patch keeps its area $dx\,dp$, directly verifying <Liouville's theorem>. The <energy> ellipses are also invariant, but area preservation applies to arbitrary patches, not just whole <energy> shells.

In scaled <canonical coordinates> $X=\sqrt{m\omega}\,x$, $P=p/\sqrt{m\omega}$ the flow is a rigid clockwise <rotation>, $(X,P)=(X_0\cos\omega t+P_0\sin\omega t,-X_0\sin\omega t+P_0\cos\omega t)$. The following original plot follows an off-center patch and shows equal areas at successive times.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For a truly isolated <Hamiltonian> bunch, <Liouville's theorem> preserves the fine-grained volume in its full $6N$-dimensional <phase space>. <Hamiltonian> manipulation cannot increase its full phase-space density by compressing all canonical position and <momentum> directions simultaneously. Merely focusing the beam to a smaller transverse spot generally increases angular or <momentum> <divergence>; it does not by itself reduce <beam emittance>.

The distinction between full volume and projected spreads matters. Coupling transverse and longitudinal degrees of freedom can exchange emittance or move spread into other coordinates and correlations, so a projected transverse quantity may decrease without reducing the complete phase volume. That is redistribution, not cooling of every degree of freedom. In linear uncoupled optics, preservation of each transverse canonical area makes the spot-size/<divergence> tradeoff particularly direct.

Actual cooling allows the beam to exchange <energy> and entropy with other systems. In <electron cooling>, a colder comoving electron population receives the protons' random <kinetic energy> and is subsequently renewed or cooled. In <stochastic cooling>, a pickup measures deviations and a downstream kicker applies feedback corrections; the electronics, measurement noise and environment belong to the enlarged system. CERN's description identifies exactly this pickup-and-kicker arrangement.<Stochastic cooling by CERN>[Feedback and corrections: pickup measurement of sample motion and downstream electric-field correction]{ref}

Thus these methods relax the idealization that the proton bunch alone is isolated. Including the cooling apparatus does not require the complete closed dynamics to violate <Liouville's theorem>. For protons, direct radiative damping is much weaker than for electrons, so stochastic or electron cooling is a more relevant practical suggestion than relying on strong synchrotron damping.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Fix an initial event $(q_i,t_i)$. For each reachable final event $(q_f,t_f)$, evaluate the <action> on a classical path satisfying those endpoints. On a chosen local branch this produces Hamilton's principal function $S(q_f,t_f)$, rather than a functional of every possible path. Multiple classical paths may make this function multivalued, so the derivation is local to a branch.

Allow the final event to vary. The integrated interior variation vanishes by the <Euler-Lagrange equations>. The endpoint variation must distinguish the total coordinate change from the change at fixed time: $\delta q(t_f)=\delta q_f-\dot q_f\delta t_f$. Consequently
$$
\delta S=\sum_ip_{i,f}(\delta q_{i,f}-\dot q_{i,f}\delta t_f)+L_f\delta t_f
=\sum_ip_{i,f}\delta q_{i,f}-H_f\delta t_f.
$$
Hence $p_i=\partial S/\partial q_i$ and $\partial S/\partial t=-H$. Eliminating the <momenta> yields the <Hamilton-Jacobi equation>
$$
\boxed{\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}+H\left(q,\frac{\partial S}{\partial q},t\right)=0.}
$$
A <complete integral of the Hamilton-Jacobi equation> depends on $n$ suitable independent constants for $n$ coordinates; differentiating it with respect to those constants supplies the conjugate integration constants needed to reconstruct <trajectories>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The required <Hamilton-Jacobi equation> is
$$
\boxed{S_t+\frac1{2m}(S_x^2+S_y^2+S_z^2)+mgz=0.}
$$
Because $x,y$ and time are cyclic in the <Hamiltonian>, use separation $S=ax+by-Et+W(z)$. The remaining equation is
$$
(W')^2=2m(E-mgz)-a^2-b^2\equiv Q(z).
$$
For $g\ne0$, each sign branch has the separated complete integral
$$
\boxed{S=ax+by-Et-\frac{s}{3m^2g}[2m(E-mgz)-a^2-b^2]^{3/2},\qquad s=\pm1.}
$$
It is real in the classically allowed region $Q\ge0$, and $p_z=s\sqrt Q$. At a turning point one matches the ascending and descending branches. If $g=0$, the integral is simply $W=p_zz$ with constant $p_z$.

A convenient equivalent complete integral, expressed using initial-momentum constants $\boldsymbol\alpha$, is globally polynomial in time:
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
S={}&\alpha_xx+\alpha_yy+(\alpha_z-mgt)z
-\frac{\alpha_x^2+\alpha_y^2+\alpha_z^2}{2m}t\\
&+\frac{\alpha_zg}{2}t^2-\frac{mg^2}{6}t^3.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Substitution verifies that its time derivative cancels the kinetic term and $mgz$. It avoids a square-root branch change when extracting the complete <trajectory>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Apply Jacobi's reconstruction to the polynomial complete integral in <4/b/solution>. Its parameter derivatives are constant:
$$
\beta_x=S_{\alpha_x}=x-\frac{\alpha_x}{m}t,\qquad
\beta_y=y-\frac{\alpha_y}{m}t,\qquad
\beta_z=z-\frac{\alpha_z}{m}t+\frac12gt^2.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{x(t)=\beta_x+\frac{\alpha_x}{m}t,\quad
y(t)=\beta_y+\frac{\alpha_y}{m}t,\quad
z(t)=\beta_z+\frac{\alpha_z}{m}t-\frac12gt^2.}
$$
At $t=0$ the constants $\boldsymbol\beta$ are the initial positions and $\boldsymbol\alpha$ are the initial <momenta>. Direct differentiation of $S$ gives $p_x=\alpha_x$, $p_y=\alpha_y$, $p_z=\alpha_z-mgt$, confirming the same motion. The six constants provide arbitrary initial data; the solution covers both ascent and descent, not merely one local square-root branch of the separated <action>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

An autonomous <Hamiltonian> system with $n$ degrees of freedom is <Liouville integrable> if it has $n$ conserved functions $I_1,\ldots,I_n$, including the <Hamiltonian>, whose differentials are independent on a regular open set and which are in involution:
$$
\boxed{dI_1\wedge\cdots\wedge dI_n\ne0,\qquad\{I_i,I_j\}=0.}
$$
The equations can then be reduced locally to quadratures. The statement about action-angle coordinates on invariant tori additionally requires compact connected regular common level sets; integrable unbounded motion need not be periodic or lie on a torus.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

For the uniform-gravity <Hamiltonian>, take the three integrals $p_x,p_y,H$. Since the potential does not depend on $x,y$,
$$
\{p_x,p_y\}=0,\qquad\{p_x,H\}=-\partial_xH=0,\qquad\{p_y,H\}=0.
$$
For $g\ne0$, the $mg\,dz$ term in $dH$ makes these three differentials independent. Thus the system is <Liouville integrable>, as the explicit separated solution already demonstrates.

Replacing $mgz$ by a sufficiently smooth arbitrary function $f(z)$ preserves the same commuting integrals. They are independent wherever $p_z$ and $f'(z)$ do not vanish simultaneously. The vertical motion reduces to
$$
\boxed{\frac{p_z^2}{2m}+f(z)=E_z,\qquad
 t-t_0=\pm\sqrt{\frac m2}\int_{z_0}^{z}\frac{d\zeta}{\sqrt{E_z-f(\zeta)}}.}
$$
The horizontal motion remains uniform. Thus an elementary antiderivative is unnecessary: the system is still integrable by a one-dimensional quadrature on regular branches, joined at turning points. Critical equilibria may be singular common level sets without destroying generic integrability. If $f$ is constant, the three <momenta> themselves give an everywhere independent commuting set, avoiding the rank loss of $p_x,p_y,H$ at $p_z=0$.
