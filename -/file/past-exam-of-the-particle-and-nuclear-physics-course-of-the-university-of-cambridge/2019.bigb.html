= Solution
{parent=1}

Count <strangeness> before drawing a <weak interaction> diagram. An <Omega baryon> has $S=-3$, a <Lambda baryon> has $S=-1$, a negatively charged <kaon> has $S=-1$, and a <pion> has $S=0$. Thus the two <nonleptonic baryon decays> require
$$
\Delta S(\Lambda^0K^-)=+1,\qquad \Delta S(\Lambda^0\pi^-)=+2.
$$
The <strong interaction> cannot supply either net change of <strangeness>.

\Image[2019-omega-weak-diagrams.png]
{title=One-W and two-W quark diagrams for the two Omega decay channels}

In the upper <Feynman diagram>, one initial <strange quark> undergoes the <weak charged current> transition
$$
s\longrightarrow u+W^-,\qquad W^-\longrightarrow d+\bar u.
$$
The remaining two <strange quarks> are <spectator quarks>. Combine the outgoing $u,d$ and one spectator $s$ into the <Lambda baryon>, and the remaining $s$ with $\bar u$ into the <kaon>. This uses one <W boson> propagator and two weak <interaction vertices>. Its <Cabibbo-Kobayashi-Maskawa matrix> factor has magnitude $|V_{us}V_{ud}|\simeq\sin\theta_C\cos\theta_C$.

For the lower <Feynman diagram>, let the upper initial $s$ first emit a $W^-$ and become a virtual $u$, then emit a $W^+$ and become a $d$. The $W^+$ is absorbed by a second initial $s$, turning it into a $u$; the third initial $s$ remains a <spectator quark>. The emitted $W^-$ produces $d\bar u$. The complete <quark flavour> flow is therefore
$$
\begin{aligned}
s_1&\longrightarrow u^*+W^-,&u^*&\longrightarrow d+W^+,\\
s_2+W^+&\longrightarrow u,&W^-&\longrightarrow d+\bar u,\\
s_3&\longrightarrow s.
\end{aligned}
$$
The final $d,u,s$ form the <Lambda baryon> and the other $d\bar u$ form the <pion>. <Electric charge conservation> holds at every vertex. Internal lines are virtual; this is a contribution to one <decay amplitude>, not a sequence of freely propagating on-shell constituent-quark decays.

This is a possible lowest-weak-order diagram for a <second-order strangeness-changing weak decay>: it has two <W boson> propagators and four weak <interaction vertices>. Its <Cabibbo-Kobayashi-Maskawa matrix> factor has magnitude $|V_{us}|^2|V_{ud}|^2$ in the displayed light-quark topology. Other contributions can exist, but a single charged-current insertion cannot turn two net strange quarks into nonstrange ones. No extra <loop Feynman diagram> is compulsory: the displayed two-W tree already realizes the required <quark flavour> change.

At low <energies>, one-W exchange produces one power of the <Fermi constant> in an effective <decay amplitude>; the second process requires an additional weak insertion. Hadronic <matrix elements> and propagators supply the accompanying dimensions, so a numerical ratio cannot be obtained by simply squaring a dimensionful constant. The robust comparison is
$$
\boxed{\Gamma(\Omega^-\to\Lambda^0\pi^-)\ll\Gamma(\Omega^-\to\Lambda^0K^-).}
$$
The dominant reason is the extra weak order, with additional <Cabibbo suppression> in this representative topology. It is not a comparison between a strong decay and a weak decay.

In both <semileptonic decays>, an active <quark> emits a virtual <W boson>, which produces an <electron> and an <electron antineutrino>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
n:&\quad d\to u+W^-,\quad W^-\to e^-+\bar\nu_e,\\
\Lambda^0:&\quad s\to u+W^-,\quad W^-\to e^-+\bar\nu_e.
\end{aligned}
$$
The unchanged $u,d$ <spectator quarks> combine with the produced $u$ into the final <proton>. The relevant <Cabibbo-Kobayashi-Maskawa matrix> elements are $V_{ud}\simeq\cos\theta_C$ and $V_{us}\simeq\sin\theta_C$.

\Image[2019-beta-cabibbo-diagrams.png]
{title=Neutron and Lambda semileptonic decay diagrams and their Cabibbo factors}

Use the quoted <masses> in <energy> units, equivalently <natural units> $c=1$, and define the energy releases before subtracting the electron rest energy as
$$
\Delta_n=939.6-938.3=1.3\,\mathrm{MeV},\qquad
\Delta_\Lambda=1115.7-938.3=177.4\,\mathrm{MeV}.
$$
The prescribed <density of states> ansatz uses precisely these differences to the fifth power. A <Cabibbo-angle extraction from semileptonic decay rates> also needs a common reduced hadronic strength. In that approximation the partial decay rates, measured in $\mathrm s^{-1}$, are
$$
r_n=K\cos^2\theta_C\,\Delta_n^5,\qquad
r_\Lambda=K\sin^2\theta_C\,\Delta_\Lambda^5.
$$
The supplied <mean lifetimes> describe all decays, so multiply their inverses by the relevant <branching fractions>:
$$
r_n=\frac{1}{880\,\mathrm s}=1.13636\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm s^{-1},\qquad
r_\Lambda=\frac{8.32\times10^{-4}}{2.63\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm s}
=3.16350\times10^6\,\mathrm s^{-1}.
$$
Taking their ratio cancels $K$ and gives
$$
\tan^2\theta_C
=\frac{r_\Lambda}{r_n}\left(\frac{\Delta_n}{\Delta_\Lambda}\right)^5
=\frac{8.32\times10^{-4}\times880}{2.63\times10^{-10}}
\left(\frac{1.3}{177.4}\right)^5
=0.0588300.
$$
Choose the conventional first-quadrant <Cabibbo angle>:
$$
\boxed{\theta_C=\arctan\sqrt{0.0588300}=0.237954\,\mathrm{rad}\simeq13.6^\circ.}
$$
The <Lambda baryon> partial rate is much larger despite its smaller weak flavour coupling because the fifth-power <phase space> factor is enormous.

There is an assumption beyond the stated <density of states> ansatz: the neutron and Lambda reduced hadronic strengths have been set equal. If their ratio is $H=K_\Lambda/K_n$, the same data instead imply $\tan^2\theta_C=0.0588300/H$. Without specifying $H$, the angle is not uniquely determined. Unequal <hadronic form factors>, finite <electron mass> and recoil make the displayed common-strength result an exercise-level estimate, rather than a precision measurement.

Use the leading lepton-orbital classification of a <beta decay transition>. For <lepton>-pair <orbital angular momentum> $l$, <Fermi transitions> have pair <spin> $S=0$ and total lepton <angular momentum> $J=l$; <Gamow-Teller transitions> have $S=1$ and $J=|l-1|,\ldots,l+1$. <Addition of angular momentum> requires
$$
|J_i-J_f|\leq J\leq J_i+J_f,
$$
while the nuclear <parity> change requires $\pi_f/\pi_i=(-1)^l$. The least allowed $l$ gives the order: $l=0$ means an <allowed beta decay>, and $l=n>0$ an $n$th-<forbidden beta decay>. Fermi-type and Gamow-Teller-type below refer to these spin-independent and spin-dependent contributions, including their higher-orbital generalizations.

* For $^{129}\mathrm I\to{}^{129}\mathrm{Xe}$, the nuclear <spin> changes by $2$ and <parity> is unchanged. An $l=0$ <lepton> pair cannot carry $J\geq2$; $l=1$ would have the wrong <parity>. At $l=2$, <Fermi transitions> can carry $J=2$, and <Gamow-Teller transitions> can carry $J=1,2,3$, including the permitted transfers $2$ and $3$. Therefore this is a **second-forbidden, nonunique transition, with both Fermi-type and Gamow-Teller-type contributions permitted**. The quantum numbers do not determine their actual relative matrix elements.
* For $^{73}\mathrm{Kr}\to{}^{73}\mathrm{Br}$, $\Delta J=1$ and <parity> is unchanged. Already $l=0,S=1$ carries $J=1$. The $l=0$ operator for a <Fermi transition> has rank zero and cannot connect these unequal nuclear <spins>. This is an **allowed Gamow-Teller transition**. The decrease of <atomic number> makes the nuclear conversion proton-to-neutron; the <angular momentum> classification applies to either the energetically available <beta-plus decay> or <electron capture> branch.
* For $^{158}\mathrm{Yb}\to{}^{158}\mathrm{Tm}$, $0^+\to2^-$ requires transfer $J=2$ and a <parity> change. The lowest possible orbital order is $l=1$. The $S=0$ pair for a <Fermi transition> then has only $J=1$, whereas the $S=1$ pair for a <Gamow-Teller transition> can attain $J=2$. Thus this is a **first-forbidden unique Gamow-Teller-type transition**. Here unique means the maximal transfer $J=l+1$ at the leading orbital order, as in a <unique forbidden beta decay>.
* For $^{22}\mathrm{Mg}\to{}^{22}\mathrm{Na}$, $0^+\to0^+$ permits the scalar operator for a <Fermi transition> with $l=0$. The allowed <Gamow-Teller transition> operator has rank one, and the triangle rule excludes $0\to0$. Interpreting the printed logarithm in the usual <comparative half-life> convention gives
$$
ft=10^{3.48}\,\mathrm s=3.02\times10^3\,\mathrm s.
$$
This small value supports the expected **superallowed Fermi transition** between <isobaric analog states>. The $0^+\to0^+$ quantum numbers alone would establish allowed Fermi character, but would not by themselves establish the large analog-state overlap implied by superallowed strength.

In the order listed, the concise classification is
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{ll}
1:\ \mathrm{F+GT},\ \text{second forbidden};&2:\ \mathrm{GT},\ \text{allowed};\\
3:\ \mathrm{GT},\ \text{first forbidden unique};&4:\ \mathrm{F},\ \text{superallowed}.
\end{array}}
$$

**Dating principle**. Atmospheric <cosmic rays> produce secondary <neutrons>, which can generate <carbon-14> through $^{14}\mathrm N(n,p){}^{14}\mathrm C$. <Carbon> then enters atmospheric <carbon dioxide> and living organisms. While an organism exchanges carbon with its environment, its <carbon-14> to <carbon-12> ratio approximately tracks that reservoir. After exchange stops, surviving <carbon-14> undergoes <beta-minus decay>, whereas the stable <carbon> remains. On the closed-sample, constant-initial-ratio model, <radioactive decay> gives
$$
N_{14}(t)=N_{14}(0)e^{-\lambda t},\qquad
\mathcal A(t)=\lambda N_{14}(t)=\mathcal A_0e^{-\lambda t}.
$$
Thus <radiocarbon dating> infers elapsed time from the loss of an isotope ratio or of <radioactive activity> per fixed carbon mass. It dates cessation of carbon exchange, which need not equal the date an object was manufactured or buried. Actual calendar dating also requires <radiocarbon calibration> and control of contamination and reservoir differences.

**Mean lifetime**. For <exponential decay>, the <half-life> obeys $e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}}=1/2$, while the <mean lifetime> is $\tau=1/\lambda$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\tau=\frac{5730}{\ln2}\,\mathrm{yr}=8.26664\times10^3\,\mathrm{yr}.}
$$
Using $1\,\mathrm{yr}=365.25\times86400\,\mathrm s$ gives $\lambda=3.83325\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm s^{-1}$ and $\tau=2.60875\times10^{11}\,\mathrm s$.

**The mass minimum and beta stability**. Treat the supplied <masses> as nuclear <rest energies> in $\mathrm{MeV}$, and omit <nuclear pairing> as instructed. At fixed <mass number> $A$, the volume and surface terms of the <semi-empirical mass formula> are constant. Expanding the remaining terms gives a convex <nuclear mass parabola>,
$$
M(A,Z)=C(A)+\left(\frac{a_C}{A^{1/3}}+\frac{4a_A}{A}\right)Z^2
+\left(m_p-m_n-4a_A\right)Z.
$$
The continuous minimum in <atomic number> is therefore
$$
\frac{\partial M}{\partial Z}
=m_p-m_n+\frac{2a_CZ}{A^{1/3}}-\frac{4a_A(A-2Z)}{A}=0,
\qquad
Z_* =\frac{4a_A+m_n-m_p}{2a_CA^{-1/3}+8a_A/A}.
$$
At $A=14$, the quadratic coefficient is $7.01302\,\mathrm{MeV}$ and $Z_*=6.79450$. The nearest integer is $Z=7$; the <nuclear isobar> of minimum <mass> is <nitrogen-14>. Maximizing <nuclear binding energy> alone would omit the neutron-proton rest-mass difference; its continuous optimum is slightly different, but also selects integer $Z=7$ here.

The values most useful for checking neighboring decay thresholds are
$$
\begin{array}{c|rrrr}
Z&5&6&7&8\\ \hline
M(14,Z)\;(\mathrm{MeV})&13065.584&13047.427&13043.296&13053.192.
\end{array}
$$
Consequently <carbon-14> can lower its nuclear <mass> by changing a <neutron> into a <proton>:
$$
\boxed{{}^{14}_6\mathrm C\longrightarrow{}^{14}_7\mathrm N+e^-+\bar\nu_e
\quad\text{(beta-minus decay).}}
$$
For bare nuclear <masses>, the <Q value> must include the emitted <electron mass>. Taking $m_ec^2=0.511\,\mathrm{MeV}$,
$$
Q_{\beta^-}^{\mathrm{model}}=M(14,6)-M(14,7)-m_ec^2
=4.13070-0.511=3.61970\,\mathrm{MeV}>0.
$$
The opposite proton-to-neutron conversion would lead to $Z=5$, whose nuclear <mass> is $18.1567\,\mathrm{MeV}$ higher than that of <carbon-14>. Neither <beta-plus decay> nor <electron capture> can bridge that gap by supplying an electron-mass correction. For the <nitrogen-14> daughter, both neighboring nuclear <masses> are higher: $Z=6$ by $4.13070\,\mathrm{MeV}$ and $Z=8$ by $9.89535\,\mathrm{MeV}$. Even <electron capture> to $Z=6$ has $Q=0.511-4.13070<0$. Thus the daughter has **no further energetically allowed single beta decay in this model**; physical <nitrogen-14> is stable.

These threshold numbers belong to the supplied smooth <semi-empirical mass formula>, not to an accurate prediction of the <carbon-14> electron spectrum or its long <half-life>. <Semi-empirical mass errors can change beta-decay thresholds>. Also, the instruction to omit <nuclear pairing> is a model simplification: even $A=14$ does not make physical pairing vanish, and even-even carbon and odd-odd nitrogen need not have the same pairing correction. No omitted pairing coefficient is guessed here.

**Activity of modern carbon**. Neglect the small contribution of other stable <carbon> <isotopes> when counting atoms in a gram. The <Avogadro constant> gives
$$
N_{12}\simeq\frac{1\,\mathrm g}{12\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}}
(6.02214076\times10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}})
=5.01845\times10^{22},
\qquad
N_{14}=1.3\times10^{-12}N_{12}=6.52399\times10^{10}.
$$
The expected <radioactive activity> of that one gram is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal A_0=\lambda N_{14}=0.25008\,\mathrm{Bq}
\simeq0.25\,\mathrm{Bq},}
$$
or approximately $15.0$ decays per minute per gram of carbon. A <becquerel> is one decay per second; the normalization is per gram of carbon, rather than per gram of the entire organic sample.

**Artefact age**. Its remaining activity fraction is $0.13/0.25008=0.51983$. Inverting the <exponential decay> law,
$$
\boxed{t=\frac{1}{\lambda}\ln\frac{\mathcal A_0}{\mathcal A}
=8266.64\,\mathrm{yr}\times\ln\frac{0.25008}{0.13}
=5408.44\,\mathrm{yr}\simeq5.4\times10^3\,\mathrm{yr}.}
$$
This is the <radiocarbon age> on the supplied constant-initial-activity, $5730$-year <half-life> model. It is not a calibrated calendar date; the sample's historical initial ratio and any later carbon exchange are not supplied.

**Angular momentum and parity**. Work in the <rho meson> <rest frame>. Since each <pion> has zero <spin>, their combined intrinsic <spin> is $S=0$ and their total <angular momentum> is entirely relative <orbital angular momentum>:
$$
\mathbf J_\rho=\mathbf L+\mathbf S_1+\mathbf S_2=\mathbf L,
\qquad J_\rho=1\ \Longrightarrow\ L=1.
$$
The <parity selection rule for a two-body decay> gives
$$
P_\rho=P_{\pi}^2(-1)^L=(-1)^2(-1)^L=(-1)^L.
$$
The supplied $P_\rho=-1$ requires odd $L$, so both conservation laws agree:
$$
\boxed{L=1,\qquad P_{\pi\pi}=-1.}
$$
Here the statement about two-pion decays is understood as the dominant <strong interaction> channel; the assertion that it occurs literally always is an idealization, since other interactions can produce other channels.

**Exchange symmetry**. <Pions> are spin-zero <bosons>, so the complete two-particle <wavefunction> must have <bosonic exchange symmetry>, including its spatial and <isospin> factors. The unique spin-zero product is symmetric. Exchanging the particle slots reverses the relative position, so an $L=1$ spatial state has <orbital parity> $(-1)^L=-1$. Its <isospin> state must therefore be antisymmetric to make the product symmetric.

For two <neutral pions>, the internal charge state $|\pi^0\pi^0\rangle$ is symmetric under exchange. There is no antisymmetric internal factor to compensate odd $L$. Equivalently, the odd-$L$ projection of two identical spin-zero neutral particles vanishes. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\rho\to\pi^0\pi^0\ \text{is forbidden by Bose exchange symmetry}.}
$$
This argument does not depend on treating <isospin> as an exact symmetry of nature.

**All nine isospin states**. In the following <two-pion isospin decomposition>, let $|ab\rangle=|\pi^a\rangle_1\otimes|\pi^b\rangle_2$ denote ordered particle slots; each of $a,b$ is $+,0,-$. The second label in $|I,q\rangle$ is the total <electric charge> in units of the <elementary charge>. For this pion system it equals the total isospin projection $I_3$.

<Addition of angular momentum> for two <isospins> of one gives $1\otimes1=2\oplus1\oplus0$. Start from the highest-weight <quantum state> $|2,2\rangle=|++\rangle$ and apply the sum of the two <ladder operators>. The <ladder recurrence for Clebsch-Gordan coefficients> gives the normalized symmetric quintet:
$$
\begin{aligned}
|2,2\rangle&=|++\rangle,\\
|2,1\rangle&=\frac{|+0\rangle+|0+\rangle}{\sqrt2},\\
|2,0\rangle&=\frac{|+-\rangle+|-+\rangle+2|00\rangle}{\sqrt6},\\
|2,-1\rangle&=\frac{|0-\rangle+|-0\rangle}{\sqrt2},\\
|2,-2\rangle&=|--\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$
The normalized <quantum state> orthogonal to $|2,1\rangle$ in the two-dimensional charge-one subspace is antisymmetric. Lowering it gives the antisymmetric triplet:
$$
\begin{aligned}
|1,1\rangle&=\frac{|+0\rangle-|0+\rangle}{\sqrt2},\\
|1,0\rangle&=\frac{|+-\rangle-|-+\rangle}{\sqrt2},\\
|1,-1\rangle&=\frac{|0-\rangle-|-0\rangle}{\sqrt2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The remaining charge-zero <quantum state> must be orthogonal to both displayed charge-zero states and annihilated by the total <ladder operators>. This gives the symmetric singlet:
$$
\boxed{|0,0\rangle=\frac{|+-\rangle+|-+\rangle-|00\rangle}{\sqrt3}.}
$$
Together the quintet, triplet and singlet contain $5+3+1=9$ orthonormal states, accounting for the full $3\times3$ charge-product space. Multiplying an entire <isospin multiplet> by a common <quantum phase> changes none of these physical conclusions.

**Rho isospin and strong decay modes**. Only the $I=1$ <two-pion isospin decomposition> is antisymmetric under exchange; $I=0,2$ are symmetric. The required $L=1$ spatial state therefore selects $I_{\pi\pi}=1$. Conservation of <isospin> by the <strong interaction>, in its usual approximation, implies
$$
\boxed{I_\rho=1.}
$$
The three charge states have the corresponding strong two-pion channels
$$
\boxed{\rho^+\to\pi^+\pi^0,\qquad
\rho^0\to\pi^+\pi^-,\qquad
\rho^-\to\pi^-\pi^0.}
$$
The ordered antisymmetric combinations above describe the internal part; multiplying by the odd spatial state gives the required symmetric complete <bosonic> state. The neutral $I=1$ state contains no $|00\rangle$ component, in agreement with the independent identical-particle argument.

**Charge conjugation**. <Charge conjugation> preserves <mass> and reverses <electric charge>. A charged <rho meson> is therefore changed into a distinct state of opposite charge, so neither $\rho^+$ nor $\rho^-$ is itself a charge-conjugation eigenstate. The neutral state can be an eigenstate. Acting on its two-pion internal state interchanges the charge labels:
$$
\mathcal C\frac{|+-\rangle-|-+\rangle}{\sqrt2}
=\frac{|-+\rangle-|+-\rangle}{\sqrt2}
=-\frac{|+-\rangle-|-+\rangle}{\sqrt2}.
$$
The charged-pion <quantum phase> conventions cancel between the two particles. Thus the <two-pion charge-conjugation parity> is $C=(-1)^L=-1$, and conservation by the <strong interaction> fixes
$$
\boxed{\mathcal C|\rho^0\rangle=-|\rho^0\rangle;\qquad C(\rho^0)=-1.}
$$
Only $\rho^0$ among the individual charge states is a <charge conjugation> eigenstate.

**Conservation laws**. Use <natural units> $c=1$ and write $p_i^\mu=(E_i,\mathbf p_i)$ with the $+---$ metric. In the parent's <rest frame>, which is the final-state <centre-of-momentum frame>, $P^\mu=(M,\mathbf0)$. <Four-momentum conservation> and the <relativistic energy-momentum relation> give
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i=1}^n E_i=M,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^n\mathbf p_i=\mathbf0,}
\qquad
E_i^2-\mathbf p_i^2=m_i^2,\quad E_i\geq m_i.
$$
A real massless daughter has strictly positive <energy>. The kinematic threshold is $M\geq\sum_i m_i$; below, no decay satisfying these conditions exists. The endpoint discussion assumes open <phase space>, $M>\sum_i m_i$, to avoid collapsed threshold configurations.

**Two-body energies**. <Momentum conservation> requires $\mathbf p_2=-\mathbf p_1$, so both daughters have the same momentum magnitude $p$. Their on-shell relations imply
$$
E_1^2-p^2=m_1^2,\qquad E_2^2-p^2=m_2^2,
$$
and subtraction eliminates $p$:
$$
(E_1-E_2)(E_1+E_2)=m_1^2-m_2^2.
$$
Since $E_1+E_2=M$, solving the sum and difference equations gives the <relativistic two-body decay> result
$$
\boxed{E_1=\frac{M^2+m_1^2-m_2^2}{2M},\qquad
E_2=\frac{M^2+m_2^2-m_1^2}{2M}.}
$$
As a check, their common momentum magnitude is
$$
p=\frac{\sqrt{[M^2-(m_1+m_2)^2][M^2-(m_1-m_2)^2]}}{2M}
=\frac{\sqrt{\kappa(M^2,m_1^2,m_2^2)}}{2M},
$$
where $\kappa$ is the <Källén function>. It vanishes at the massive two-body threshold and is real on the allowed side. If the symbols $M,m_i$ denote <masses> in kilograms rather than rest <energies>, multiply the displayed energy formulas by $c^2$ and the displayed momentum formula by $c$.

**Minimum energy in three-body decay**. The on-shell inequality already gives $E_1\geq m_1$. For $m_1>0$, the bound is attainable by putting daughter 1 at rest and letting daughters 2 and 3 recoil back to back:
$$
\mathbf p_1=0,\qquad\mathbf p_2=-\mathbf p_3=\mathbf k,\qquad
\sqrt{k^2+m_2^2}+\sqrt{k^2+m_3^2}=M-m_1.
$$
The last equation has a solution because $M-m_1>m_2+m_3$; its left side increases continuously from $m_2+m_3$ to infinity. Thus
$$
\boxed{E_1^{\min}=m_1\quad(c=1),\qquad E_1^{\min}=m_1c^2\quad\text{with units restored}.}
$$
If $m_1=0$, the same formula describes the lower boundary $E_1\to0^+$. It is an <infimum>, not an attained physical zero-energy massless particle: this is a <soft-particle endpoint>.

**Maximum energy from the recoil mass**. Group daughters 2 and 3 into a recoil system with <four-momentum> $Q=p_2+p_3=P-p_1$ and squared <invariant mass> $s_{23}=Q^2$. Directly in the parent <rest frame>,
$$
s_{23}=(P-p_1)^2=M^2+m_1^2-2ME_1,
\qquad
E_1=\frac{M^2+m_1^2-s_{23}}{2M}.
$$
Maximizing $E_1$ therefore means minimizing the recoil <invariant mass>. This approach does not assume a recoil rest frame when that system is null.

If both recoil daughters have positive <masses>, use their pair <rest frame>. Their total <energy> there is at least $m_2+m_3$, with equality when both are at rest relative to one another. Equivalently, their future-directed <four-velocities> satisfy $p_2\cdot p_3\geq m_2m_3$, giving
$$
s_{23}=m_2^2+m_3^2+2p_2\cdot p_3\geq(m_2+m_3)^2.
$$
Equality means the two recoil daughters have a common <velocity> in the parent frame: their momenta are parallel and $\mathbf p_2/m_2=\mathbf p_3/m_3$. Daughter 1 recoils in the opposite direction. Hence
$$
\boxed{E_1^{\max}=\frac{M^2+m_1^2-(m_2+m_3)^2}{2M}.}
$$
At this endpoint their total <energy> and its sharing are
$$
E_{23}=\frac{M^2-m_1^2+(m_2+m_3)^2}{2M},\qquad
E_2=\frac{m_2}{m_2+m_3}E_{23},\qquad
E_3=\frac{m_3}{m_2+m_3}E_{23}.
$$
These explicitly exhibit a permitted configuration attaining the bound when both recoil <masses> are positive.

If exactly one recoil daughter is massless, say $m_3=0$ and $m_2>0$, work in the <rest frame> of daughter 2. For any positive photon-like daughter energy there,
$$
s_{23}=m_2^2+2m_2E_3^{(2)}>m_2^2.
$$
The lower recoil-mass bound is approached only as $E_3^{(2)}\to0^+$. Thus the same general formula with $m_3=0$ is the **<supremum>** of $E_1$ for a genuine three-body event, rather than an attained maximum. Interchanging 2 and 3 covers the other mixed case. The distinction is important because the paper's category “at least one has mass” includes this <soft-particle endpoint> as well as the attainable two-massive-particle case.

If both recoil daughters are massless, instead compute
$$
s_{23}=2E_2E_3(1-\cos\theta_{23})\geq0.
$$
It can be exactly zero at nonzero $E_2,E_3$ when their momenta are parallel. Then $|\mathbf Q|=E_2+E_3$, and <momentum conservation> makes $|\mathbf p_1|=|\mathbf Q|$. Solving $E_1+|\mathbf p_1|=M$ and $E_1^2-|\mathbf p_1|^2=m_1^2$ gives
$$
\boxed{E_1^{\max}=\frac{M^2+m_1^2}{2M},\qquad
|\mathbf p_1|=E_2+E_3=\frac{M^2-m_1^2}{2M}.}
$$
Both recoil daughters can have positive <energies>: set $E_2=x|\mathbf p_1|$ and $E_3=(1-x)|\mathbf p_1|$ for any $0<x<1$. This endpoint is attained without a soft daughter, even though their combined <four-momentum> is null and has no <rest frame>.

Combining the <three-body decay energy endpoints>, the closed kinematic range is
$$
\boxed{m_1\leq E_1\leq\frac{M^2+m_1^2-(m_2+m_3)^2}{2M}\quad(c=1).}
$$
For <masses> in ordinary units, multiply both endpoint expressions by $c^2$. Open endpoints occur precisely in the soft limits described above. These bounds specify kinematic possibilities; the <decay amplitude> can still vanish at a boundary, so a possible endpoint need not have a nonzero event density.
