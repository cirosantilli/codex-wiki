A positive-parity $K=0$ <nuclear rotational band> in an <even-even nucleus> has $J=0,2,4,\ldots$ and excitation <energies>
$$
E_J=\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mathcal I}J(J+1).
$$
The four nonzero levels give $E_J/[J(J+1)]=15.17,14.85,14.48,14.01\ \mathrm{keV}$. These approximately constant coefficients, together with the even-spin positive-<parity> sequence, support a rotational interpretation. The roughly eight-percent decline means a constant-inertia rigid rotor is only an approximation: the effective <moment of inertia> increases along the sequence.

Using the lowest excited level gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal I=\frac{3\hbar^2}{91\ \mathrm{keV}}
=2.29\times10^{-54}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}.}
$$
Using the highest level instead gives $2.48\times10^{-54}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$, showing the scale of the departure from a constant inertia. This is a comparison of <nuclear rotational inertia relative to a rigid sphere>, not evidence for an exactly spherical rotating intrinsic state.

For a conventional size estimate, explicitly take a uniform sharp <radius> $R=r_0A^{1/3}$ with $r_0\simeq1.2\ \mathrm{fm}$ and <mass> $M\simeq A m_u$. These are standard scale approximations, not additional measured properties of this isotope supplied by the paper. They give $R\simeq6.70\ \mathrm{fm}$ and
$$
\mathcal I_{\rm rigid}=\frac25MR^2\simeq5.19\times10^{-54}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2},\qquad
\boxed{\frac{\mathcal I}{\mathcal I_{\rm rigid}}\simeq0.44
\left(\frac{1.2\ \mathrm{fm}}{r_0}\right)^2.}
$$
Thus the fitted rotational inertia is substantially smaller than the rigid-body scale. <Nuclear pairing> provides a physical reason collective nuclear inertia need not equal the classical rigid-body value. The comparison's numerical <radius> dependence is explicit; it is not an independently determined <radius>.

A physical <baryon> is a <colour singlet>. Its three-quark colour factor is antisymmetric, proportional to $\epsilon_{abc}|abc\rangle$. In the hypothetical scalar-quark theory, the <quarks> obey <Bose-Einstein statistics>, so the complete three-particle state must be symmetric under exchange. At $L=0$ the spatial factor is symmetric, and three zero spins supply no nontrivial <spin> factor. The flavour factor must therefore be antisymmetric to cancel the antisymmetry of the colour factor.

There is exactly one completely antisymmetric state made from three flavour basis states: the <flavour singlet of three scalar quarks>,
$$
|F\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt6}\bigl(|uds\rangle+|dsu\rangle+|sud\rangle
-|usd\rangle-|dus\rangle-|sdu\rangle\bigr).
$$
Equivalently, $\dim\bigwedge^3\mathbb C^3=1$. A repeated flavour gives zero under complete antisymmetrization. **Only one ground-state flavour singlet, containing $uds$ and having $J^P=0^+$, remains.** Keeping fermionic exchange antisymmetry while changing the spins to zero would contradict the assumed <spin-statistics theorem>. The standard antisymmetric colour factor and the ordinary-quark flavour decomposition are documented in the quark-model review.<Review of Particle Physics by Particle Data Group (2022)>[<Quark> Model, §15.5, equations (15.22)–(15.24)]{ref}

Use <natural units> and invariant squared center-of-mass <energy> $s$. For a massless incident <neutrino> and an initially stationary <electron>,
$$
s=(p_\nu+p_e)^2=m_e^2+2m_eE_\nu.
$$
At the production threshold the final massless <neutrino> can have arbitrarily small center-of-mass <energy>, so the lower endpoint is $s=m_\ell^2$. The <charged-current neutrino-electron threshold> is consequently
$$
\boxed{E_{\nu,\min}=\frac{m_\ell^2-m_e^2}{2m_e}.}
$$
For the three flavours, using precisely the masses given in this part, the answers are **$0$ for $\nu_e$**, **$1.0994\times10^4\ \mathrm{MeV}\simeq11.0\ \mathrm{GeV}$ for $\nu_\mu$**, and **$3.0898\times10^6\ \mathrm{MeV}\simeq3.09\ \mathrm{TeV}$ for $\nu_\tau$**. The zero threshold for the <electron> flavour is an infimum for elastic scattering; it does not imply a nonzero interaction rate at zero incident <energy>. The large heavy-lepton thresholds arise because most laboratory <energy> becomes center-of-mass motion against a very light target.

Evidence for <quarks> comes from several kinds of observation that test different aspects of their interpretation. The light <meson> and <baryon> spectra organize into approximate flavour multiplets with the charges and strangeness expected for $q\bar q$ and $qqq$. In particular, the <baryon octet> and <baryon decuplet> encode the three light <quark flavours> and their <spin> couplings. This is an economical account of hadron quantum numbers, but spectroscopy alone would not establish that the constituents behave as localized scattering centers.

<Deep inelastic scattering> supplies that second test. In energetic <electron>–<proton> scattering the outgoing lepton measures the <energy> and <momentum> transferred through a virtual <photon>, while the broken-up target is summed over. Approximate <Bjorken scaling> means the structure functions depend primarily on $x=Q^2/(2P\cdot q)$ rather than varying rapidly with the resolving <momentum> scale $Q^2$. It indicates nearly pointlike constituents. In the leading spin-half <parton model>,
$$
F_2(x)=x\sum_q e_q^2[q(x)+\bar q(x)],\qquad F_2(x)=2xF_1(x).
$$
The charge-squared weights and the second relation test the constituents' fractional charges and spin-half nature. Scaling violations reflect <quark> and <gluon> radiation rather than a failure of the constituent picture.<Review of Particle Physics by Particle Data Group (2022)>[Structure Functions, §§18.1–18.3, deep-inelastic kinematics and leading parton expressions]{ref}

<Particle jets> give complementary final-state evidence. A high-energy <quark> produces a collimated spray of hadrons because <confinement> prevents it from emerging as an isolated particle. Back-to-back jets fit <quark>–antiquark production; three-jet events show hard <gluon> radiation. Their angular correlations test a spin-one radiated parton. This distinguishes constituents of hadrons from a literal observation of free fractional-charge particles.

Three independent arguments identify an additional threefold colour degree of freedom. First, the spin-aligned $uuu$ state of a ground-state $\Delta^{++}$ would have symmetric space, <spin> and flavour factors. The <Pauli exclusion principle> requires an antisymmetric additional factor. The <three-quark colour singlet> supplies it. The same construction accommodates the spin-aligned $sss$ state of the $\Omega^-$ without altering its measured quantum numbers.

Second, the inclusive <hadronic R ratio>, away from thresholds and narrow resonances, is at leading electromagnetic order
$$
R=\frac{\sigma(e^+e^-\to\text{hadrons})}{\sigma(e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-)}
=N_c\sum_{q\ {
m accessible}}e_q^2.
$$
Each <quark flavour> has $N_c$ distinguishable colour final states. With $N_c=3$, the predicted plateaux for three, four and five accessible flavours are **$2$, $10/3$ and $11/3$**; without the factor three they would be a third as large. The measured inclusive normalization is therefore a colour-counting test, with radiative and threshold corrections included in precision comparisons.<Review of Particle Physics by Particle Data Group (2022)>[Plots of <Cross Sections> and Related Quantities, inclusive <electron>–<positron> hadronic <cross sections> and R plots]{ref}

\Image[paper-1-colour-counting.png]
{title=Leading colour-counting predictions for the hadronic R ratio; these are model values, not plotted measurements}

Third, the neutral pion's two-photon amplitude contains a quark-loop colour sum, proportional to $N_c(e_u^2-e_d^2)$; its rate and the other evidence are consistent with three colours. <Quantum chromodynamics> identifies this degree of freedom as a gauge charge, with eight <gluons>. Observed hadrons are colour singlets; the evidence for colour is its quantitative and symmetry consequences, not visible colours or isolated coloured <quarks>.

= Solution

The common energetic principle behind <nuclear fission> and <nuclear fusion> is the <nuclear binding energy>. If the products are more tightly bound in total than the reactants, their smaller rest <mass> releases
$$
\boxed{Q=\left(\sum_i M_i-\sum_f M_f\right)c^2
=\sum_f B_f-\sum_i B_i.}
$$
The two binding-energy expressions assume that the same numbers of <protons> and <neutrons> are counted on both sides; emitted free nucleons have zero nuclear binding. The <energy> appears mainly as product <kinetic energy>, with radiation and subsequent decays contributing according to the reaction.

The <binding energy per nucleon> rises rapidly through light nuclei, has a broad maximum in the iron/nickel region, and decreases more slowly for heavier nuclei. Combining suitable light nuclei can therefore increase their total binding, whereas splitting a sufficiently heavy nucleus into moderately heavy fragments can do the same. This is an <energy> comparison, not a rule that every fusion or fission channel is exothermic: the actual nuclear masses fix the sign of $Q$.

\Image[paper-1-binding-curve.png]
{title=Schematic binding-energy trend explaining why light-nucleus fusion and heavy-nucleus fission can release energy}

In <nuclear fission>, the <liquid-drop model> illustrates the competition between attractive short-range nuclear cohesion and long-range <proton> repulsive <Coulomb interaction>. Deformation increases surface area and costs surface <energy>, but separating the charge can reduce <nuclear Coulomb energy>. Their competition creates a <fission barrier>. An excited nucleus may pass over the barrier; <spontaneous fission> can occur through <quantum tunnelling>. The resulting fragments are often excited and neutron-rich, so they may emit prompt <neutrons> and <photons> and undergo later <beta decay>. The typical total <energy> released by a heavy-nucleus fission is of order a few hundred MeV, rather than an electronic-scale <energy>. Its size follows from an approximately MeV-per-nucleon binding gain summed over a heavy nucleus. A <neutron> from one fission can induce another; a <nuclear chain reaction> is the repeated physical process, distinct from the energy-release condition for one event.

In <nuclear fusion>, the initial charged nuclei repel one another. Exothermicity does not remove this entrance <Coulomb barrier>. A reaction requires sufficient overlap of their <wavefunctions>, achieved by energetic encounters and <quantum tunnelling>. For nonresonant low-energy charged-particle fusion, a useful separation is
$$
\sigma(E)=\frac{S(E)}E e^{-2\pi\eta_C},\qquad
\eta_C=\frac{Z_1Z_2\alpha c}{v_{\rm rel}}.
$$
The <astrophysical S-factor> isolates the nuclear part from much of the rapidly varying Coulomb penetration probability. In a thermal population, the competition between the decreasing high-energy population and increasing penetration produces the <Gamow peak>. This explains how fusion can proceed below the classical barrier without requiring the mean thermal <energy> to equal its height.

Both processes conserve total <energy>, charge, <baryon> number and <angular momentum>. Their very different entrance barriers and <mass> ranges explain why a favorable $Q$ value by itself does not specify a reaction rate. Short-range attraction gives approximately saturated nuclear binding, while surface, Coulomb, symmetry and pairing terms account for the broad trend and its isotope-dependent departures.

The <deuteron> is the bound <proton>–<neutron> system. Its ground state has $J^P=1^+$ and <isospin> $T=0$, with binding <energy> approximately $2.225\ \mathrm{MeV}$ and no excited bound state. The small binding compared with characteristic nuclear <energy> scales produces an extended tail. With <reduced mass> $\mu\simeq m_N/2$, the asymptotic radial wavefunction decays as $e^{-\kappa r}$, where
$$
\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{2\mu B}}\hbar,
\qquad \kappa^{-1}\simeq4.3\ \mathrm{fm}.
$$
The decay length is not a sharp deuteron <radius> or the force range. It illustrates weak binding outside the region of strong attraction.

The observed total <spin> is consistent with aligned nucleon spins, $S=1$, and a predominantly $L=0$ spatial state, $\,{}^3S_1$. A central attraction identical in both spin–isospin channels would not distinguish this channel from a spin-singlet <proton>–<neutron> channel. In fact the triplet channel binds while the corresponding singlet channel does not. A realistic <nuclear force> must therefore distinguish these spin–isospin channels, for example through a term $V_\sigma(r)\boldsymbol\sigma_p\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma_n$, whose eigenvalues are $+1$ and $-3$ in triplet and singlet states. The absence of a bound dineutron and diproton reinforces the need to distinguish channels; <proton>–<proton> Coulomb repulsion is an additional effect, not a complete explanation of the singlet nuclear interaction.

The clearest evidence against a purely central force is the nonzero <electric quadrupole moment>. A pure isotropic S-wave charge distribution would give zero. The <deuteron quadrupole evidence for a tensor force> instead points to a <nuclear tensor force>,
$$
V_T(r)S_{12},\qquad
S_{12}=3(\boldsymbol\sigma_p\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})
(\boldsymbol\sigma_n\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})
-\boldsymbol\sigma_p\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma_n.
$$
It depends on how the spins point relative to the line joining the particles. It preserves total $J$ but couples orbital angular momenta differing by two, producing
$$
|d\rangle=\frac{u(r)}r|{}^3S_1\rangle+\frac{w(r)}r|{}^3D_1\rangle.
$$
Both terms have $J^P=1^+$, and their interference permits a quadrupole moment. The 1939 theoretical analysis explicitly connected the measured quadrupole moment with such directional dependence.<The Electric Quadrupole Moment of the Deuteron and the Field Theory of Nuclear Forces by Christian Møller and Léon Rosenfeld>[Accessible abstract, Nature 144, 476–477]{ref}

The <nuclear magnetic moment> provides a second check. A pure aligned-spin S wave would give approximately $\mu_p+\mu_n\simeq0.880\mu_N$, close to but larger than the observed deuteron value approximately $0.857\mu_N$. The discrepancy is consistent with non-S-wave structure and interaction-current corrections; it is not by itself a model-independent measurement of a D-state probability. The <deuteron D-state admixture> depends on the adopted Hamiltonian and electromagnetic current. The magnetic moments and <mass> differences used for these scales are tabulated physical constants.<Fundamental Physical Constants — Complete Listing by National Institute of Standards and Technology and others>[2022 CODATA adjustment, <proton>, <neutron> and deuteron magnetic moments and rest-mass energies]{ref}

A useful schematic interaction therefore contains central, spin-dependent and tensor terms, and possibly <spin>–orbit terms in other channels. Long-range <one-pion exchange> naturally supplies a tensor component, with its finite range set by the pion <mass>. **The deuteron establishes an attractive triplet channel and a noncentral tensor interaction; a spin-independent central potential is insufficient.**

In the <Breit-Wigner resonance cross section>, $E$ is the total <centre-of-momentum energy>, $E_0$ the resonance <energy>, and $p$ either incident particle's center-of-momentum <momentum> magnitude. The total <decay width> is $\Gamma=\hbar/\tau$, while $\Gamma_i$ and $\Gamma_f$ are partial widths into the entrance and specified exit channels. Their ratios to $\Gamma$ are the corresponding <branching fractions>. The factor $g$ averages over incident <spin> states and sums over the resonance's magnetic substates.

In <natural units>, $\hbar=c=1$, the resonant partial-wave cross-sectional scale is an area proportional to the squared reduced de Broglie wavelength, $1/p^2$. Equivalently a partial-wave scattering amplitude carries one factor $1/p$, and its squared modulus supplies $1/p^2$. In SI area units the same scale is $(\hbar/p)^2$; if $p$ is expressed in <energy> units, multiply the final inverse-energy-squared answer by $(\hbar c)^2$.

For unpolarized spin-half incident <muons>, the <unpolarized resonance spin factor> is
$$
g=\frac{2J+1}{(2s_{\mu^-}+1)(2s_{\mu^+}+1)}
=\frac{2J+1}{4}.
$$
Hence **$g_Z=3/4$ and $g_H=1/4$**. The outgoing tau spins do not supply another independent multiplicity here: their allowed final states are already included in $\Gamma_f$.

Near a narrow resonance, treat the smooth prefactor $p^{-2}$ and the partial widths as constant across the line. At the peak the denominator is $\Gamma^2/4$. Half its height occurs when $(E-E_0)^2+\Gamma^2/4=\Gamma^2/2$, namely at $E=E_0\pm\Gamma/2$. Thus **the full width at half maximum is $\Gamma$** within the Breit-Wigner narrow-line approximation. A varying prefactor or convolution with the beam-energy distribution can modify an observed line shape.

\Image[paper-1-higgs-diagrams.png]
{title=Tree production and decay, fermion triangles, and the W-loop topologies for Higgs diphoton decay}

The production amplitude has a <muon> pair joined to a Higgs propagator through their <Yukawa coupling>, followed by the bottom-quark pair at a second Yukawa vertex. The separate $H\to b\bar b$ graph is a tree-level Higgs–<fermion> vertex. There are no tree-level $Hgg$ or $H\gamma\gamma$ vertices in the renormalizable <Standard Model>: their lowest nonzero graphs are loops. A <quark> triangle, dominated by the top contribution, produces two <gluons>. Charged-fermion triangles contribute to two <photons>; charged-W loops also contribute and are dominant in the Standard Model amplitude. <Photon> permutations and the W contact topology belong to the full amplitude; the diagram shows these topologies rather than claiming one triangle alone is the entire gauge-invariant W contribution.<Review of Particle Physics by Particle Data Group (2022)>[Status of Higgs <Boson> Physics, §11.2.4 and discussion on physical page 5 of loop-induced <gluon> and <photon> couplings]{ref}

At leading order the <Higgs decay to a fermion pair> has
$$
\Gamma_f=N_c^f\frac{m_f^2m_H}{8\pi v^2}
\left(1-\frac{4m_f^2}{m_H^2}\right)^{3/2}.
$$
Each <quark> contributes three distinguishable colour states, whereas a lepton has one. Neglecting the small phase-space factors for these masses, radiative corrections and the scale dependence of <quark> masses, the requested ratio is
$$
\boxed{\Gamma_\tau:\Gamma_c:\Gamma_b
=m_\tau^2:3m_c^2:3m_b^2
=3.24:6.75:75
=1:2.083:23.15.}
$$
The numerical masses here are those printed with question 3; the tau and <electron> approximations deliberately differ from those in question 1. Omitting the <quark> colour multiplicity would give the wrong relative rates.

Use the same leading-order assumptions as in part a. Let $K=m_H/(8\pi v^2)$, so the partial widths are $K$ times their mass-squared and colour weights. The stated three-channel branching sum fixes
$$
\frac K\Gamma=\frac{0.67}{m_\tau^2+3m_c^2+3m_b^2}
=\frac{0.67}{84.99\ \mathrm{GeV^2}}.
$$
The <Higgs branching-fraction normalization from mass-weighted channels> therefore gives
$$
B_{\mu\mu}=\frac{0.67(0.106)^2}{84.99}=8.858\times10^{-5},\qquad
B_{b\bar b}=\frac{0.67(75)}{84.99}=0.59125.
$$
No numerical value of $v$ is needed. At resonance, $g=1/4$ and $p\simeq m_H/2$, neglecting the incident <muon> <mass> in the kinematic factor. The <peak resonance cross section and branching fractions> relation gives
$$
\sigma_{\rm peak}=\frac{4\pi g}{p^2}B_{\mu\mu}B_{b\bar b}
\simeq\frac{4\pi}{m_H^2}B_{\mu\mu}B_{b\bar b}
=\boxed{4.21\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{GeV^{-2}}.}
$$
Use the supplied rounded $\hbar c=0.197\ \mathrm{GeV\,fm}$, rather than silently substituting a different conversion:
$$
\boxed{\sigma_{\rm peak}=1.63\times10^{-39}\ \mathrm{m^2}
=1.63\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm b\simeq16.3\ \mathrm{pb}.}
$$
This is the ideal unsmeared peak for the simplified inputs, not a beam-averaged collider event rate. Finite <energy> spread, radiation, polarization and radiative corrections require additional treatment.

The entrance <Yukawa coupling> is proportional to the incident lepton <mass>, so the partial Higgs width into that lepton pair is proportional to its <mass> squared. With the question's masses,

\frac{\Gamma_{\mu\mu}}{\Gamma_{ee}}\simeq
\left(\frac{106\ \mathrm{MeV}}{0.5\ \mathrm{MeV}}\right)^2

The inequality $m_H<2m_W$ forbids two on-shell W <bosons> in a two-body final state. It does not forbid the four-fermion states reached through a virtual W propagator. The physical channel is
$$
\boxed{H\longrightarrow WW^*\longrightarrow4\ \text{fermions},}
$$
with at least one W off shell. The virtual particle need not satisfy $p^2=m_W^2$; the final stable particles still obey total four-momentum conservation. The W propagator and reduced <phase space> suppress the rate relative to an above-threshold two-on-shell-W decay, but do not make it zero. The label $H\to WW$ commonly includes this off-shell contribution to <Higgs decay to two W bosons>.

<Beta decay> converts a <neutron> to a <proton> or vice versa through the <weak interaction>, leaving the <mass> number fixed:
$$
n\to p+e^-+\bar\nu_e,\qquad
p\to n+e^++\nu_e.
$$
Inside a nucleus the full initial and final nuclear masses determine whether either process is possible. <Electron capture>, $p+e^-\to n+\nu_e$, is the related bound-electron process. The emitted charged-lepton spectrum is continuous because <energy> is shared with the <neutrino> and nuclear recoil.

For <allowed beta decay>, the lepton <wavefunctions>' leading spatial terms are constant over the nucleus. A <Fermi transition> has $\Delta J=0$ and no <parity> change. A <Gamow-Teller transition> has $\Delta J=0,1$ and no <parity> change, excluding $0\to0$. Thus a same-parity transition with nonzero initial and final spins and $\Delta J=0$ can contain both contributions. First-forbidden transitions change <parity> and permit $|\Delta J|=0,1,2$; the $|\Delta J|=2$ case is <unique forbidden beta decay>. Higher forbiddenness requires higher powers of the small lepton <momentum> times nuclear size. “Forbidden” denotes suppression of the leading allowed operator, not an impossible decay.<Nuclear Data Sheets General Policies by National Nuclear Data Center>[Physical PDF page 3, General Policies — Theory, Beta Transitions]{ref}

<Fermi's golden rule> expresses a rate as a squared nuclear <matrix element> times leptonic <phase space>. Neglecting charged-lepton <mass> and Coulomb distortion, a constant allowed <matrix element> gives
$$
\lambda\propto |M|^2\int_0^Q E^2(Q-E)^2dE
=\frac{|M|^2Q^5}{30}.
$$
This is the approximate <Sargent rule>, $\lambda\propto Q^5$ for comparable allowed strengths; it is not an exact law across arbitrary transitions. Finite <electron> <mass>, the <Fermi function>, forbidden shape factors and nuclear structure change the comparison. A microscopic first-forbidden unique operator introduces additional <momentum> factors.

Using bare nuclear masses $M_i,M_f$, the kinetic-energy release is
$$
Q_{\beta^-}=(M_i-M_f-m_e)c^2,
\qquad Q_{\beta^+}=(M_i-M_f-m_e)c^2.
$$
The daughter is different in the two cases: its charge is $Z+1$ for beta minus and $Z-1$ for beta plus. The relevant <mass> difference must exceed one <electron> <mass>. For neutral atomic masses, to leading order in small electronic binding corrections, the corresponding expressions are $Q_{\beta^-}=(M_i^{\rm at}-M_f^{\rm at})c^2$ and $Q_{\beta^+}=(M_i^{\rm at}-M_f^{\rm at}-2m_e)c^2$. <Electron capture> is energetically possible approximately when $M_i^{\rm at}>M_f^{\rm at}$, with available shell binding <energy> and daughter excitation included. Nuclear masses and atomic masses must not be mixed.

The mirror nuclei have interchanged <neutron> and <proton> numbers. Neglect their strong-interaction binding difference and describe them with the same uniformly charged sharp sphere of <radius> $R$. The <nuclear Coulomb energy> is
$$
E_C(Z)=\frac35\frac{Z^2e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0R}
=\frac35\frac{Z^2\alpha\hbar c}{R}.
$$
Changing one <neutron> into a <proton> decreases the nucleon rest-mass sum by $m_n-m_p=1.294\ \mathrm{MeV}/c^2$, but raises the Coulomb <energy>. The diagram gives $(M_F-M_O)c^2=2.25\ \mathrm{MeV}$ for nuclear masses, so
$$
2.25=-1.294+\frac35(9^2-8^2)\frac{\alpha\hbar c}{R}.
$$
With the standard electromagnetic conversion $\alpha\hbar c\simeq1.44\ \mathrm{MeV\,fm}$, the <uniform-sphere Coulomb displacement energy> estimate is
$$
\boxed{R=\frac{(3/5)(17)(1.44)}{3.544}\ \mathrm{fm}\simeq4.14\ \mathrm{fm}.}
$$
This $R$ is the uniform sphere's outer <radius>. If “charge <radius>” means the rms <nuclear charge radius>, the same distribution gives
$$
\boxed{\sqrt{\langle r^2\rangle}=\sqrt{3/5}\,R\simeq3.21\ \mathrm{fm}.}
$$
The numerical result is an estimate within the charge-density model, not a model-independent <radius> measurement. A discrete-proton correction replaces $Z^2$ by $Z(Z-1)$, giving $\Delta[Z(Z-1)]=16$ and $R\simeq3.90\ \mathrm{fm}$. The main answer above follows the literal continuum charged-sphere model. Strong isospin-breaking, nonuniform density and changes in the valence-nucleon distribution also limit the extraction.

Read the <spin> and <parity> from the actual diagram: N and Ne have $1/2^-$ ground states; O and F have $5/2^+$ ground states. The N→O beta-minus branch and Ne→F beta-plus branch therefore both have $|\Delta J|=2$ with a <parity> change: **both are first-forbidden unique**. The F→O beta-plus branch has $5/2^+\to5/2^+$: **it is allowed and can contain both Fermi and Gamow-Teller contributions**.

The Ne ground-state branch is **$0.2\%=0.002$**, as explicitly printed on the diagram, not $0.2$ as a probability. The prose leaves off its percent sign. Interpret the supplied “lifetimes” as mean lifetimes $\tau$, so each partial decay rate is $\lambda_j=B_j/\tau$, with no extra $\ln2$. Then
$$
\boxed{\lambda_{N\to O}=\frac{0.016}{6.1}=2.623\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}},}
$$
$$
\boxed{\lambda_{F\to O}=\frac1{95}=1.053\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}},\qquad
\lambda_{Ne\to F}=\frac{0.002}{0.15}=1.333\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}.}
$$
A <decay width> in <energy> units is $\Gamma_j=\hbar\lambda_j$, rather than a rate. Thus the corresponding partial <energy> widths, in the same order N, F, Ne, are **$1.73\times10^{-24}$, $6.93\times10^{-24}$ and $8.78\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{MeV}$**. If a different convention used the supplied numbers as half-lives, all these rates and widths would acquire $\ln2$; their ratios would be unchanged. The literal mean-lifetime convention is used throughout.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

The bracketed diagram energies are nuclear, so subtract one <electron> rest <energy> from each parent-to-daughter <energy> difference. The three kinetic-energy endpoints are
$$
Q_N=9.190-0.511=8.679\ \mathrm{MeV},\quad
Q_{Ne}=16.26-2.25-0.511=13.499\ \mathrm{MeV},\quad
Q_F=2.25-0.511=1.739\ \mathrm{MeV}.
$$
The observed partial-rate ratios are $\lambda_N/\lambda_{Ne}=0.19672$ and $\lambda_N/\lambda_F=0.24918$.

To obtain the simple Sargent-law estimates, define an effective strength by $\lambda=C|M_{\rm eff}|^2Q^5$, using the same normalization $C$. Then
$$
\boxed{\frac{|M_{\rm eff,N}|^2}{|M_{\rm eff,Ne}|^2}
\simeq0.19672\left(\frac{13.499}{8.679}\right)^5=1.79,}
$$
$$
\boxed{\frac{|M_{\rm eff,N}|^2}{|M_{\rm eff,F}|^2}
\simeq0.24918\left(\frac{1.739}{8.679}\right)^5
=8.05\times10^{-5}.}
$$
Thus the two forbidden branches have effective strengths of the same broad order, whereas the N ground-state branch is highly suppressed relative to the allowed F branch after this approximate fifth-power kinematic scaling. The first comparison involves mirror transitions with related nuclear configurations; order-of-magnitude similarity is reasonable, without expecting exact equality from this estimate.

These effective strengths must not be confused with precisely extracted microscopic reduced nuclear <matrix elements>. Both N and Ne ground-state branches are first-forbidden unique, for which the <first-forbidden unique beta-decay phase space> contains an additional factor proportional to $p_e^2+E_\nu^2$. In the massless, no-Coulomb limit this gives $Q^7$, so using the same microscopic unique-forbidden normalization for those two branches instead yields
$$
\boxed{\frac{|M_{U,N}|^2}{|M_{U,Ne}|^2}
\simeq0.19672\left(\frac{13.499}{8.679}\right)^7=4.33.}
$$
Comparing a unique-forbidden reduced operator directly with an allowed reduced operator also requires a convention for its powers of length and <momentum>. The number $8.05\times10^{-5}$ is consequently an effective Sargent-normalized suppression, not a universal dimensionless ratio between different operator ranks. Electron-mass and opposite-sign Coulomb corrections matter, particularly for the low-energy F branch. The data justify approximate strength comparisons; they do not fix precision nuclear <matrix elements> without these extra ingredients.

= Solution
{parent=v}

The state marked X in F is the $1/2^-$ analogue at $11.20\ \mathrm{MeV}$ excitation. Its absolute nuclear <energy> relative to O is $2.25+11.20=13.45\ \mathrm{MeV}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{Q_X=16.26-13.45-0.511=2.299\ \mathrm{MeV}.}
$$
It is a same-spin, same-parity <allowed beta decay> between <isobaric analog states>. The large analogue Fermi overlap makes it a **superallowed analogue transition**, in the broad analogue-state terminology. Since $J=1/2$ rather than zero, this classification does not exclude a Gamow-Teller contribution.

For a transparent numerical estimate, take the Fermi part to dominate both this branch and the reference F-ground-state decay, neglect Coulomb and electron-mass corrections in their phase-space ratio, and use the <Sargent rule>. For $T_z=(N-Z)/2$, Ne has $T=T_z^{\rm magnitude}=3/2$ and goes from $T_z=-3/2$ to $-1/2$ in its analogue multiplet. The <isospin ladder strength in analogue beta decay> is therefore three. The F→O ground-state mirror transition in $T=1/2$ has Fermi strength one. Thus the <analogue beta-decay branching estimate> gives
$$
\lambda_X\simeq\frac3{95\ \mathrm s}\left(\frac{2.299}{1.739}\right)^5
=0.128\ \mathrm{s^{-1}},\qquad
\boxed{B_X\simeq(0.15\ \mathrm s)\lambda_X=0.0191\simeq1.9\%.}
$$
This percent-level result is an estimate under the stated assumptions, not a unique consequence of the level diagram. More generally, if $f$ denotes an allowed phase-space integral and $S=g_V^2|M_F|^2+g_A^2|M_{GT}|^2$, then
$$
B_X=\frac{\tau_{Ne}}{\tau_F}\frac{f_X}{f_F}\frac{S_X}{S_F}.
$$
The diagram fixes the Fermi strengths but not both axial strengths. Including them and proper <positron> phase-space functions changes the numerical result. Assuming equal total allowed strengths instead of the Fermi-only ratio three would give approximately $0.64\%$; that alternative makes a different nuclear-structure assumption. The rate enhancement from removing the forbiddenness explains why the X branch can be appreciable despite its much smaller endpoint than the Ne ground-state branch.
