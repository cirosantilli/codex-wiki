In the <nuclear shell model>, a level with total <angular momentum> $j$ has $2j+1$ substates for each <nucleon> species. The first three levels accommodate $2+4+2=8$ <protons> or eight <neutrons>. <nuclear pairing> makes a filled shell or a pair of like <nucleons> contribute $J=0$, and an orbital has spatial <parity> $(-1)^\ell$.

For <oxygen-17>, eight <protons> and eight <neutrons> form a closed core; the remaining <neutron> occupies $1d_{5/2}$, so **$J^P=5/2^+$**. For <nitrogen-15>, eight <neutrons> close their shell, while the seventh <proton> occupies $1p_{1/2}$. Equivalently it is a <proton hole> relative to the closed eight-proton shell, giving **$J^P=1/2^-$**. <beryllium-10> is an <even-even nucleus>; pairing its four <protons> and six <neutrons> gives **$J^P=0^+$**.

<boron-10> needs an additional qualification. The fifth <proton> and fifth <neutron> each occupy $1p_{3/2}$. <addition of angular momentum> permits
$$
J=0,1,2,3,\qquad P=(-1)^{1+1}=+1.
$$
The experimentally correct ground-state assignment is **$3^+$**, corresponding to aligned unpaired $j=3/2$ contributions in this simple description. However, one-particle level ordering alone does not select it over the other couplings: their splitting requires the <nuclear residual interaction>. Assuming a preference for the aligned proton-neutron coupling gives the intended prediction; without that assumption, the strictly deducible result is the allowed positive-parity multiplet. The evaluated <ground state> is $3^+,T=0$, with a $1^+,T=0$ level above it.<Energy levels of boron-10 by TUNL (2004)>[Table 10.18, ground state and first excited state, physical page 1]{ref}

Use <natural units> and write the common light <constituent quark mass> as $m$, and the strange one as $M$. For two <spin one-half> constituents,
$$
\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2=\frac12\left[S(S+1)-\frac32\right]
=\begin{cases}-3/4,&S=0,\\+1/4,&S=1.\end{cases}
$$
The relative hyperfine contributions are $-3$ and $+1$. Absorb their normalization and spatial overlap into pair-dependent parameters $h_{\ell\ell},h_{\ell s},h_{ss}$. The <meson hyperfine mass splitting> model gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
m_\pi&=2m-3h_{\ell\ell},&m_\rho&=2m+h_{\ell\ell},\\
m_K&=m+M-3h_{\ell s},&m_{K^*}&=m+M+h_{\ell s},\\
m_\phi&=2M+h_{ss}.
\end{aligned}
$$
A common color-magnetic coefficient gives $h_{ij}\propto1/(m_i m_j)$, but its numerical value is not needed.

In the specified <Eta octet state> used for the <eta meson> the light-flavor probability is $1/3$ and the strange-flavor probability $2/3$. Assuming a flavor-diagonal <mass> operator,
$$
m_\eta=\frac13(2m-3h_{\ell\ell})+\frac23(2M-3h_{ss})
=\frac23m+\frac43M-h_{\ell\ell}-2h_{ss}.
$$
Inserting the five <mass> expressions into the proposed combination makes the coefficient of $h_{\ell s}$ equal to $4-4=0$. Those of $m,M,h_{\ell\ell},h_{ss}$ are $2/3,4/3,-1,-2$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{m_\eta=-\frac13m_{\pi^0}-2m_{\rho^0}+4m_{K^{*0}}+\frac43m_{K^0}-2m_\phi.}
$$
This is an identity within the assumed <mass> operator and octet flavor assignment. It is not an exact relation among measured <masses>: <eta mixing> and additional spin-independent binding effects are outside those assumptions.

For <neutrino-electron elastic scattering>, work in the laboratory frame with a stationary <electron> and a massless incoming <neutrino> of <energy> $E$. If the <electron> receives <kinetic energy> $T$ and <momentum> $\mathbf p$, its <relativistic energy-momentum relation> gives
$$
p^2=(m+T)^2-m^2=T^2+2mT.
$$
<conservation of energy> fixes the outgoing <neutrino> <energy> to $E-T$, and <conservation of momentum> fixes its <momentum> to $\mathbf k-\mathbf p$. Its zero <mass> therefore requires
$$
(E-T)^2=E^2+p^2-2Ep\cos\theta.
$$
Substitution and cancellation give
$$
(E+m)T=E\sqrt{T(T+2m)}\cos\theta.
$$
For $T>0$, this requires $\cos\theta>0$; squaring must not introduce a backward-electron branch. Squaring, cancelling $T$ and solving gives
$$
\boxed{T=\frac{2mE^2\cos^2\theta}{(E+m)^2-E^2\cos^2\theta}.}
$$
The expression increases with $\cos^2\theta$ on the physical interval $0\leq\theta\leq\pi/2$. Maximum transfer occurs for a forward <electron> and a backward outgoing <neutrino>:
$$
T_{\max}=\frac{2E^2}{m+2E}
=\frac{2(0.86)^2}{0.511+2(0.86)}\,\mathrm{MeV}
=\boxed{0.663\,\mathrm{MeV}.}
$$
The remaining $0.197\,\mathrm{MeV}$ is carried by the <neutrino>. An elastic collision cannot transfer the entire incident <energy> to a free massive <electron>.

A <muonic atom> binds a negative <muon> in the nuclear <Coulomb potential>. The <muon> is a <spin one-half> <lepton> with the <electron>'s charge but about 207 times its <mass>. For a point <nucleus> of <mass> $M_N$ and charge $Ze$, the <reduced mass> and nonrelativistic hydrogenic scales, with <fine-structure constant> $\alpha$, are
$$
\mu_r=\frac{m_\mu M_N}{m_\mu+M_N},\qquad
a_\mu=\frac{\hbar}{Z\alpha\mu_r c},\qquad
E_n=-\frac{\mu_r c^2(Z\alpha)^2}{2n^2}.
$$
For a heavy <nucleus> $\mu_r\simeq m_\mu$, giving a radius roughly $1/207$ of the corresponding electronic radius and transition <energies> roughly 207 times larger. For hydrogen, <nuclear recoil> matters: $m_\mu\simeq106\,\mathrm{MeV}/c^2$ gives $\mu_r\simeq95\,\mathrm{MeV}/c^2$ and $a_\mu\simeq284\,\mathrm{fm}$ rather than the infinite-nuclear-mass value about $255\,\mathrm{fm}$.

A slowed negative <muon> is captured into an excited orbit and undergoes a <radiative cascade>, ejecting atomic <electrons> and emitting muonic X-rays. Inner orbits penetrate the <nuclear charge distribution>, particularly at large $Z$. Replacing a point charge by an extended charge makes the central attraction less singular and shifts states with substantial nuclear overlap. Measured transition <energies> consequently constrain the <nuclear charge radius>. Sensitivity is particularly strong for $S$ states, whose contact density scales as $(Z\alpha\mu_r)^3$.

An instructive experiment is <Randolf Pohl>'s team's muonic-hydrogen spectroscopy. A stopped-muon target prepared a metastable $2S$ population; a tunable pulsed laser drove a $2S\to2P$ transition, followed by a detectable $2P\to1S$ X-ray. Scanning laser frequency located a resonance whose interpretation included the <proton> finite-size contribution to the <Lamb shift>. Delayed detection selected the long-lived population against prompt cascade backgrounds. Laser-off or detuned measurements provide a background control; <energy> and time selection alone would not establish a laser-induced resonance.<The size of the proton by Randolf Pohl and others>[Nature 466, pp. 213–216, Fig. 1 experimental principle and Figs. 4–5 time spectra and resonance]{ref}

\Image[paper-1-muonic-scales.png]
{title=<reduced mass> changes the orbital scale and nuclear-size sensitivity}
{description=Original calculated hydrogenic radial probabilities, plotted against physical radius with probability measured per logarithmic interval. The Bohr radii are stated alongside; these are point-nucleus model curves, not measured nuclear densities.}

Extracting a radius requires <quantum electrodynamics>, including <vacuum polarization>, recoil and nuclear-structure corrections. A free <muon> decays on a microsecond timescale; a bound negative <muon> can instead undergo <muon capture>, for example $\mu^-p\to n\nu_\mu$. These processes limit observation time. In heavy atoms, relativistic motion and nuclear overlap make the simple Bohr formulas only qualitative. **Muonic spectroscopy converts a smaller orbital scale into enhanced sensitivity to nuclear charge structure**, provided atomic and nuclear corrections are controlled.

The <nuclear Fermi gas model> treats <protons> and <neutrons> as two species of <spin one-half> <fermions> moving approximately independently in a confining <nuclear mean field>. At a <temperature> small compared with their <Fermi temperature>, the <Pauli exclusion principle> fills the lowest available <momentum> states. It does not allow all <nucleons> to sit at zero <momentum>.

For species $i=p,n$, two <spin> states occupy each momentum-space cell $(2\pi\hbar)^3/V$. Counting a filled sphere gives
$$
N_i=\frac{2V}{(2\pi\hbar)^3}\frac{4\pi p_{F,i}^3}{3},\qquad
p_{F,i}=\hbar(3\pi^2 n_i)^{1/3},\qquad E_{F,i}=\frac{p_{F,i}^2}{2m_N},
$$
where $n_i=N_i/V$. The <Fermi energy> is measured from the mean-field well bottom; it need not equal the <energy> needed to remove a <nucleon> into vacuum. Occupied-state averaging gives
$$
E_{\mathrm{kin}}=\frac35(NE_{F,n}+ZE_{F,p}),\qquad P_i=\frac25 n_iE_{F,i}.
$$
Even at zero <temperature> there is <kinetic energy> and <degeneracy pressure>. As an explicitly illustrative input, total density $n=0.16\,\mathrm{fm}^{-3}$ and $N=Z$ give $p_Fc\simeq263\,\mathrm{MeV}$ and $E_F\simeq37\,\mathrm{MeV}$. These numbers illustrate the formula; they are not additional data supplied by the paper.

Separate <proton> and <neutron> seas explain a kinetic contribution to <nuclear symmetry energy>. At fixed $n$, put $\delta=(N-Z)/A$ and define $E_F$ at $n_p=n_n=n/2$. Then
$$
\frac{E_{\mathrm{kin}}}{A}
=\frac35E_F\frac{(1+\delta)^{5/3}+(1-\delta)^{5/3}}2
=\frac35E_F+\frac13E_F\delta^2+O(\delta^4).
$$
Increasing one species' population raises its <Fermi surface> and costs <energy>, favoring similar populations before electrostatic and interaction terms are included.

<pauli blocking> suppresses collisions deep in a filled sea because scattering needs unoccupied final states. An energetic external probe can knock a <nucleon> into an empty state and reveal its initial <Fermi motion>, making the model useful for broad inclusive scattering distributions. A finite surface can be approximated using a position-dependent <nuclear mean field>.

A free gas cannot produce nuclear binding or saturation: those require an attractive interaction and its density dependence. The smooth gas picture also omits discrete <nuclear shells>, <nuclear spin-orbit splitting>, <nuclear pairing>, short-distance correlations and deformation. It cannot assign all ground-state <spins> from a <momentum> sphere. **Its useful prediction is the scale and occupancy of <nucleon> motion, with a surrounding mean field supplied separately.**

The <Standard Model> is a <gauge theory> with group $SU(3)_c\times SU(2)_L\times U(1)_Y$, matter <quarks> and <leptons>, gauge bosons and a <Higgs field>. It combines <Quantum chromodynamics> with the <electroweak interaction>. Its successes do not make its field content a complete account of nature. Established missing phenomena should be distinguished from theoretical questions about its parameters.

<neutrino oscillation> demonstrates nonzero mass-squared differences and mixing of flavor states. The minimal renormalizable <Standard Model> has no <right-handed neutrino> and no <neutrino mass> term. The atmospheric experiment compared zenith-angle distributions of muon-like and electron-like events in a large water <Cherenkov detector>. A direction-dependent muon-neutrino deficit, with different path lengths through Earth and comparison samples, discriminated against a uniform flux-normalization error.<Evidence for oscillation of atmospheric neutrinos by Y. Fukuda and others>[Abstract and zenith-angle distributions, Physical Review Letters 81, pp. 1562–1567 (1998)]{ref} One extension adds <right-handed neutrinos> and small Dirac Yukawa couplings; another has a <Majorana mass term> and <seesaw mechanism>, with light eigenvalue of order $m_D^2/M$. Oscillations alone do not decide whether <neutrinos> are <Majorana neutrinos>.

<dark matter> is inferred from gravitational observations such as galactic motions and <gravitational lensing>; <Standard Model> particles do not supply the required dominant cold component. Active <neutrinos> contribute some matter but cannot constitute all the cold <dark matter>. A new stable weakly interacting particle, an <axion>, or other hidden-sector states are possible explanations, with different scattering, production and astrophysical signals. **Dark-matter evidence does not identify a particular new particle.** The institutional account distinguishes gravitational evidence from proposed particle candidates.<Dark matter by CERN>[Gravitational evidence and candidate-particle discussion]{ref}

The matter-antimatter imbalance poses a different problem. Dynamical <baryogenesis> needs <Sakharov conditions>, including baryon-number violation, <CP violation> and departure from <thermal equilibrium>. Although the <Standard Model> contains relevant ingredients in principle, they do not generate the observed asymmetry in its conventional thermal history with measured parameters. Additional CP phases, a stronger electroweak transition, or <leptogenesis> from heavy <Majorana neutrinos> are possible extensions. Each needs a quantitative mechanism.

<gravity> is absent as a dynamical quantum interaction, motivating <quantum gravity> and unification. The <hierarchy problem> asks why the Higgs <mass> scale is small compared with much higher scales when scalar <masses> receive sensitive quantum corrections. <supersymmetry> can cancel particular contributions between partner fields if its breaking pattern is suitable; compositeness gives another interpretation of the Higgs sector. Neither motivation proves that the corresponding new particles exist. The <Strong CP problem> asks why the allowed <QCD> CP-violating parameter is so small; an <axion> mechanism is one possible answer.

A <grand unified theory> embeds the separate gauge factors in a larger group and can relate couplings or <fermion> representations, sometimes predicting <proton decay>. Its tests differ from dark-matter or neutrino-mass searches. **A useful extension must explain a specified deficiency, preserve successful low-energy predictions and supply distinguishable experimental consequences.** Attractive new symmetries alone are not evidence.

= Solution

Assume <allowed beta decay>, so the leading <matrix element> has negligible lepton-momentum dependence. Neglect <nuclear recoil> and <neutrino mass>. Let $Q$ be the <kinetic energy> available to the <electron> and <antineutrino>, $E_0=m_e+Q$, and $E=\sqrt{p^2+m_e^2}$. The <neutrino> <energy> and <momentum> magnitude are $k=E_0-E$.

<Fermi's golden rule> weights accessible final states. After angular integration, the momentum-space factors are $p^2\,dp$ and $k^2\,dk$. The energy-conserving <Dirac delta function> removes the <neutrino> integral:
$$
d\Gamma=Cp^2\,dp\int_0^\infty k^2\,dk\,\delta(E_0-E-k).
$$
The constant includes the allowed nuclear <matrix element>, weak-coupling and angular factors. Including the usual <Fermi function> for electron-nucleus Coulomb distortion gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\Gamma}{dp}=C F(Z,E)\,p^2(E_0-\sqrt{p^2+m_e^2})^2,\quad
0\leq p\leq\sqrt{Q(Q+2m_e)}.}
$$
For the kinetic-energy spectrum, $dp/dT=E/p$ instead yields
$$
\frac{d\Gamma}{dT}=C F(Z,E)\,pE(Q-T)^2.
$$
The two distributions differ by a Jacobian; forgetting it changes the shape.

A pure fifth-power law additionally neglects <electron> <mass> and Coulomb distortion and keeps the nuclear <matrix element> fixed. Then $E=p$, $E_0\simeq Q$, and
$$
\Gamma=C\int_0^Q p^2(Q-p)^2\,dp
=CQ^5\int_0^1x^2(1-x)^2\,dx
=\boxed{\frac{C}{30}Q^5.}
$$
Two <momentum> powers come from each final particle and one from the remaining integration. This is the origin of the <Sargent rule>. **Exact $Q^5$ dependence requires the relativistic-electron, constant-matrix-element limit.** For $Q\ll m_e$, the same approximation without Coulomb distortion has $pE\simeq m_e\sqrt{2m_eT}$ and instead scales as $m_e^{3/2}Q^{7/2}$. Forbidden-transition factors, nuclear-structure changes and Coulomb distortion also modify the simple law.

The negative <muon> emits a virtual $W^-$ and becomes a <muon neutrino>; the boson creates an <electron> and an <electron antineutrino>:
$$
\mu^-\to\nu_\mu+W^{-*}\to\nu_\mu+e^-+\bar\nu_e.
$$
Both vertices are <weak charged currents>.

\Image[paper-1-muon-decay.png]
{title=<muon> decay through a virtual charged weak boson}
{description=Original tree diagram. Arrows indicate fermion-number flow, including the reversed arrow on the outgoing <electron antineutrino>.}

Typical transfers satisfy $|q^2|\lesssim m_\mu^2\ll M_W^2$, so the propagator factor $(M_W^2-q^2)^{-1}$ expands as $M_W^{-2}$ plus small corrections. Contracting this short-distance exchange produces the local <Fermi interaction>, with strength contained in $G_F$. This effective description does not assume that the real W <mass> vanishes. The same treatment applies to leptonic tau decays with the relevant <masses> changed.<Review of Particle Physics by Particle Data Group (2022)>[Muon Decay Parameters, §57.1 introduction and §57.2]{ref}

In GeV units, $m_\mu=0.106\,\mathrm{GeV}$ and $\hbar=6.582\times10^{-25}\,\mathrm{GeV\,s}$. Thus
$$
\Gamma_\mu=\frac{(1.166\times10^{-5})^2(0.106)^5}{192\pi^3}\,\mathrm{GeV}
=3.0562\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm{GeV},
$$
$$
\boxed{\tau_\mu=\frac{\hbar}{\Gamma_\mu}=2.154\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm s.}
$$
The modest difference from a precision experimental lifetime is expected from the rounded <mass> and omitted radiative and final-electron-mass corrections.

Use an <inclusive spectator model>: one heavy constituent decays through the <Fermi interaction>, and sum the open charged-current channels. In <weak-decay channel counting>, each massless final <lepton> pair gives basic width
$$
\Gamma_0(m)=\frac{G_F^2m^5}{192\pi^3},\qquad
\tau=\frac{\hbar}{C\Gamma_0(m)}.
$$
A <quark> pair contributes three colors multiplied by its squared <Cabibbo-Kobayashi-Maskawa matrix> element. Do not multiply by three just because the initial <quark> has three colors: its lifetime is the width of one initial state, averaged over initial color when appropriate. Define
$$
S_u=|V_{ud}|^2+|V_{us}|^2=0.9893,\qquad
S_c=|V_{cd}|^2+|V_{cs}|^2=0.9893.
$$

For the <tau lepton>, two leptonic channels contain an <electron> or a <muon>; light hadronic channels have $d\bar u$ or $s\bar u$. Therefore
$$
C_\tau=2+3S_u=4.9679,\qquad
\Gamma_\tau=2.0103\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{GeV},\qquad
\boxed{\tau_\tau\simeq3.27\times10^{-13}\,\mathrm s.}
$$
No charmed <hadron> can be produced: the lightest charmed <meson> exceeds the tau <mass>. <Review of Particle Physics by Particle Data Group (2022)>[D-zero and D-plus listings, physical page 1 of each, masses 1864.84 and 1869.66 MeV]{ref} The supplied charm-quark parameter is not a free asymptotic final-particle <mass>. The leading count neglects <electron> and <muon> <masses> and light hadronic threshold effects.

For the <charm quark>, $c\to sW^{+*}$ and $c\to dW^{+*}$ dominate. The emitted current has two light leptonic channels and the same light hadronic channels as above; a tau cannot be produced:
$$
C_c=S_c(2+3S_u)=4.91474347,\qquad
\Gamma_c=8.5232\times10^{-13}\,\mathrm{GeV},\qquad
\boxed{\tau_c^{\mathrm{spectator}}\simeq7.72\times10^{-13}\,\mathrm s.}
$$

For the <bottom quark>, retain $b\to cW^{-*}$ with the supplied $V_{cb}$ and neglect the much smaller $b\to u$ channel. The emitted current can produce three <lepton> flavors and two up-type <quark> families, with three colors for each family. In particular $b\to c\bar cs$ is open at the given <masses>, although suppressed by <phase space>. Counting each open channel as massless gives
$$
C_b=|V_{cb}|^2[3+3S_u+3S_c]=0.01429728,
$$
$$
\Gamma_b=1.0204\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{GeV},\qquad
\boxed{\tau_b^{\mathrm{spectator}}\simeq6.45\times10^{-13}\,\mathrm s.}
$$
Setting Cabibbo sums to one recovers the familiar counts $5$, $5$ and $9|V_{cb}|^2$. Despite its larger <mass>, the <bottom quark> is slowed by its small interfamily mixing factor.

These are leading estimates rather than precise lifetimes of free <quarks>. <confinement> makes charm and bottom observable in <hadrons>, whose lifetimes involve spectator interactions, <Quantum chromodynamics> corrections and bound-state effects. <mass> corrections are significant for bottom: even the single-charm channel with massless <leptons> has the <three-body weak-decay mass correction>
$$
f(r)=1-8r+8r^3-r^4-12r^2\ln r,\qquad
r=(m_c/m_b)^2=0.09,\qquad f(r)\simeq0.520.
$$
Tau and double-charm channels have different suppressions, so multiplying the entire width by this one factor is inconsistent. Accurate <hadron> lifetimes require those channel-dependent factors and hadronic dynamics; the exercise supports the explicitly stated leading counts.

An <S wave (quantum scattering)> has $L=0$ and positive <parity>. The <deuteron>'s $J=1$ therefore requires a <spin-one-half triplet state>, $S=1$. The two-nucleon <isospin> description makes this the antisymmetric $T=0$ channel. Its existence proves sufficient attraction to bind in the $\,{}^3S_1$-dominated channel.

A <spin one-half> pair could also form $S=0$. The absence of a bound $\,{}^1S_0$ <deuteron> indicates that the singlet interaction does not bind, not that it is everywhere repulsive. Together with triplet binding, this shows that a spin-independent central attraction is insufficient. No radial excited bound level also constrains the strength and range: the attraction supports only a shallow low-energy <bound state>.

For identical <neutrons> or <protons>, two-particle <isospin> has $T=1$ and is symmetric. At even $L$, spatial exchange is symmetric, so the <Pauli exclusion principle> requires an antisymmetric <spin> state, $S=0$. A low-energy $nn$ or $pp$ pair cannot occupy the <deuteron>'s $S=1,T=0$ channel. With approximate <charge independence of nuclear forces>, their singlet <strong interaction> corresponds to the unbound neutron-proton singlet; proton-proton electrostatic repulsion further disfavors binding. **There is no bound <dineutron> or <diproton> in these physical low-energy channels.** The inference uses charge independence and singlet-channel behavior; the <deuteron>'s quantum numbers alone do not constrain every imaginable potential.

In a pure $L=0,S=1$ stretched state both <spins> point along the total <angular momentum>, with no orbital contribution. The <nuclear magnetic moment> would be
$$
\mu_d^{(S)}=\mu_p+\mu_n=(2.793-1.913)\mu_N=0.880\mu_N.
$$
The measured $0.857\mu_N$ differs. More decisively, a pure <S wave (quantum scattering)> spatial state is isotropic and has zero <electric quadrupole moment>. Its nonzero value requires a nonspherical component of the quantum charge distribution.

Positive <parity> allows even $L$; coupling $S=1$ to $J=1$ permits $L=0$ and $L=2$. The state contains a <deuteron D-state admixture>:
$$
|\psi_d\rangle=\sqrt{1-P_D}\,|{}^3S_1\rangle+\sqrt{P_D}\,|{}^3D_1\rangle,
$$
with radial <wavefunctions> implicit. A <nuclear tensor force> couples the orbital components. The quadrupole moment involves their interference as well as a D-state contribution, making it sensitive to a small admixture. This is a quantum angular correlation, not two particles permanently fixed in space.

In the nonrelativistic one-body magnetic-moment approximation,
$$
\frac{\mu_d}{\mu_N}=(1-P_D)\mu_s+P_D\left(\frac34-\frac12\mu_s\right),
\qquad \mu_s=\frac{\mu_p+\mu_n}{\mu_N}=0.880.
$$
The D-state coupling has $\langle S_z\rangle=-1/2$ and $\langle L_z\rangle=3/2$ at $J_z=1$; the <proton> supplies approximately half the relative orbital magnetic moment. Thus
$$
P_D\simeq\frac{0.880-0.857}{\frac32(0.880-\frac12)}
=\boxed{0.040\ \text{in this approximation}.}
$$
The robust conclusion is **a predominantly <S wave (quantum scattering)> state with a small tensor-induced <D wave> component**. The 4% estimate is model dependent: exchange currents and other corrections also affect the magnetic moment, and a <wavefunction> component probability is not itself a uniquely measured observable.<Modern Theory of Nuclear Forces by Evgeny Epelbaum and others>[§I.F, Observables and not-so observable quantities; §II.B, spin-dependent nuclear interactions]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=d}

Use the relative coordinate and <reduced mass>
$$
\mu=\frac{m_pm_n}{m_p+m_n},\qquad \mu c^2=469.459\,\mathrm{MeV}.
$$
Take $V=-V_0$ inside, $V=0$ outside, and $E=-B$. For $V_0>B>0$, define inverse lengths
$$
k=\frac{\sqrt{2\mu c^2(V_0-B)}}{\hbar c},\qquad
\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{2\mu c^2B}}{\hbar c}.
$$
The radial equation becomes $u''+k^2u=0$ inside and $u''-\kappa^2u=0$ outside. Finiteness of the full <wavefunction> at the origin imposes $u(0)=0$; normalizability excludes the growing exterior exponential. Hence
$$
u(r)=A\begin{cases}
\sin(kr),&0\leq r\leq b,\\
\sin(kb)e^{-\kappa(r-b)},&r\geq b.
\end{cases}
$$
Continuity of $u$ was built in. Continuity of its derivative at the finite step gives
$$
\boxed{k\cot(kb)=-\kappa,\qquad k^2+\kappa^2=\frac{2\mu c^2V_0}{(\hbar c)^2}.}
$$
For the nodeless <ground state>, $\pi/2<kb<\pi$ selects the first branch. Higher roots would correspond to different wells with more <bound states>. Normalization is
$$
A^{-2}=\frac b2-\frac{\sin(2kb)}{4k}+\frac{\sin^2(kb)}{2\kappa}.
$$

For the requested weak-binding estimate, $B/V_0\to0$ makes $\kappa/k\to0$. On the first branch $kb\to\pi/2$, and $k^2\simeq2\mu c^2V_0/(\hbar c)^2$. The leading threshold estimate is
$$
\boxed{V_0\simeq\frac{\pi^2(\hbar c)^2}{8\mu c^2b^2}
=\frac{\pi^2(197.3)^2}{8(469.459)(2.1)^2}\,\mathrm{MeV}
=23.2\,\mathrm{MeV}.}
$$
This is the zero-binding threshold of a <spherical square well>, so nonzero positive binding needs a depth above it. Corrections start at order $\sqrt B$, not just at order $B$: setting $kb=\pi/2+\epsilon$ gives $\epsilon\simeq\kappa b/(\pi/2)$ and
$$
V_0\simeq V_{\rm threshold}+\frac{(\hbar c)^2\kappa}{\mu c^2b}+O(B)
\simeq32.3\,\mathrm{MeV}+O(B).
$$

The actual numbers permit an accuracy check: $\kappa=0.231608\,\mathrm{fm}^{-1}$ and $\kappa b=0.486377$ are not extremely small. Retaining the exterior decay, solve
$$
x\cot x=-0.486377,\qquad \pi/2<x<\pi.
$$
This gives $x=kb=1.830503$, and the full square-well result is
$$
V_0=B+\frac{(\hbar c)^2x^2}{2\mu c^2b^2}
=\boxed{33.73\,\mathrm{MeV}\quad\text{without weak-binding truncation}.}
$$
Neglecting $B$ only in the interior kinetic relation while retaining the measured exterior decay instead estimates $V_0\simeq31.50\,\mathrm{MeV}$. These approximations should be identified: 23.2 MeV is the leading $B\to0$ threshold, whereas matching the supplied nonzero binding requires a boundary phase shift and a deeper well.

= Solution
{parent=e}

In <neutron-proton radiative capture>, the initial pair at rest has total <energy> $Mc^2$, where $M=m_p+m_n$. The emitted <photon> carries <momentum>, requiring equal and opposite <nuclear recoil>. Some <binding energy> becomes <deuteron> <kinetic energy>.

Exact two-body kinematics in <natural units> gives
$$
M=E_\gamma+\sqrt{m_d^2+E_\gamma^2},\qquad E_\gamma=\frac{M^2-m_d^2}{2M}.
$$
Since $m_d=M-B$,
$$
E_\gamma=B-\frac{B^2}{2M},\qquad \boxed{B-E_\gamma=\frac{B^2}{2M}.}
$$
With the stated rounded <masses>,
$$
B-E_\gamma=\frac{(2.224)^2}{2(938.272+939.565)}\,\mathrm{MeV}
=0.00131699\,\mathrm{MeV}
=\boxed{1.317\,\mathrm{keV}}.
$$
The <photon> <energy> is $2.222683\,\mathrm{MeV}$. At leading order the difference equals the nonrelativistic recoil estimate $p_\gamma^2/(2m_d)\simeq B^2/(2m_d)$; exact kinematics fixes the small distinction between using $m_d$ and $M$ in that approximation.
