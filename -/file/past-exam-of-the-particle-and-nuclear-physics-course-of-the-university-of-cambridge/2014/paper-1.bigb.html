In <time> $\Delta t$ the particle advances $\beta c\Delta t$, while a light wavelet travels $c\Delta t/n$. Coherent addition along the direction of emitted <photons> gives the <Cherenkov cone angle>
$$
\boxed{\cos\alpha=\frac1{n\beta},\qquad n\beta>1.}
$$
The angle is measured between <photon> propagation and the particle velocity. It is complementary to the angle between the conical <wavefront> surface and the trajectory; confusing those two angles exchanges sine and cosine. At threshold the <photon> cone closes, $\alpha\to0$.

= Solution

The decay is energetically open, but two spin-zero <pions> have total <angular momentum> equal to their relative <orbital angular momentum> $L$. Conserving the parent <spin> requires $L=1$. Interchanging two identical <neutral pions> multiplies this orbital state by $(-1)^L=-1$, whereas their total state must be symmetric by Bose statistics. There is no different <spin> or species label to supply another minus sign. Thus **the two-neutral-pion final state is forbidden**. Its <parity> for $L=1$ would actually be $(-1)^2(-1)^1=-1$, matching the parent; the decisive obstruction is identical-boson <symmetry>, not the <energy> or the shared quark-wavefunction component.

Away from thresholds and narrow <resonances>, photon-mediated annihilation gives one colour copy proportional to $Q_q^2$ relative to the <muon> cross-section. The accessible flavours are $u,d,s,c$, each with three colours, so
$$
\boxed{R\simeq3\sum_{q=u,d,s,c}Q_q^2
=3\left(\frac49+\frac19+\frac19+\frac49\right)=\frac{10}{3}\simeq3.3.}
$$
Bottom production is closed. QCD radiative corrections raise the leading parton-model result by a relative term beginning at $\alpha_s/\pi$; the requested estimate does not require a supplied value of $\alpha_s$.

A useful convention for the <semi-empirical mass formula> is
$$
B(A,Z)=a_vA-a_sA^{2/3}-a_c\frac{Z(Z-1)}{A^{1/3}}
-a_a\frac{(A-2Z)^2}{A}+\delta(A,Z),
$$
where $B>0$ is the binding <energy>. The nuclear <mass> is $Zm_p+(A-Z)m_n-B/c^2$. Atomic-mass comparisons instead use hydrogen-atom <masses> and account for <electron> binding at the desired accuracy. No numerical fit coefficients are specified here.

The volume term represents short-range nuclear attraction with saturation: each <nucleon> has a limited number of close neighbours. The surface term subtracts the binding lost by <nucleons> at the boundary. Coulomb repulsion penalizes a large <proton> fraction. The asymmetry term penalizes unequal <neutron> and <proton> populations, reflecting the cost of filling different <Fermi seas> and nuclear interactions. Pairing favours even-even nuclei and penalizes odd-odd nuclei; odd-$A$ nuclei have zero pairing term in this approximation. For example, one may choose $\delta=\pm a_pA^{-1/2}$; an alternative fitted power requires a correspondingly different coefficient.

At fixed $A$, the smooth <mass> dependence on $Z$ is approximately parabolic. Neglecting the neutron-proton <mass> difference, pairing and small linear-in-$Z$ Coulomb corrections, minimization gives
$$
Z_{\rm stable}\simeq\frac{A}{2+(a_c/2a_a)A^{2/3}}.
$$
Thus the preferred <neutron excess> grows with <mass number>. The surface penalty disfavors very light systems, while the growing Coulomb penalty reduces the binding per <nucleon> of very heavy systems; this explains broad fusion and fission <energy> trends. The formula is a bulk approximation: shell closures, deformation and small separation-energy differences can give important exceptions. A fitted average binding trend is not a reliable substitute for a measured decay threshold near a boundary of stability.

= Solution
{parent=b}

High-energy elastic <electron scattering> probes nuclear charge through the <electromagnetic interaction>. Resolve the elastic peak with a spectrometer, measure its angular dependence and normalize by beam charge, target thickness and <detector acceptance>. In a plane-wave, approximately static treatment,
$$
\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\simeq\left(\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\right)_{\rm point}|F(q)|^2,
\qquad F(q)=\frac1{Ze}\int\rho_{\rm ch}(\mathbf r)e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}d^3r.
$$
Here $q$ is <physical momentum> transfer divided by $\hbar$, not the <momentum> itself. The low-$q$ slope, $F(q)=1-q^2\langle r^2\rangle/6+\cdots$, determines the charge r.m.s. radius. A sharp uniform sphere has
$$
F(q)=3\frac{\sin(qR)-qR\cos(qR)}{(qR)^3},\qquad
\langle r^2\rangle=3R^2/5.
$$
Its first <diffraction> minimum is at $qR\simeq4.493$, illustrating how angular structure determines size. Radii approximately follow $R\propto A^{1/3}$, but a sharp radius, a surface half-density radius and an r.m.s. radius are different definitions.

A motivating experimental implementation is Hofstadter's magnetic-spectrometer measurement: selecting outgoing <electron> <momentum> separates <elastic scattering> from nuclear excitation, rather than treating every scattered <electron> as an elastic event. Beam-energy and angle calibration, empty-target or blocked-acceptance backgrounds, and target-thickness checks matter. Finite resolution mixes nearby excitations; multiple scattering and Coulomb distortion require correction before a precision density is inferred.<Electron Scattering and Nuclear Structure by Robert Hofstadter>[§III(b), pp.222–223; §IV(a), pp.227–231, spectrometer scans and elastic/inelastic separation]{ref}

Shape information comes from quadrupole moments, Coulomb-excitation transition strengths and rotational spectra. An intrinsic axial moment $Q_0=\int\rho(3z^2-r^2)d^3r$ is positive for a prolate charge <distribution> and negative for an oblate one. A laboratory quadrupole moment is not identical to $Q_0$: a rotationally averaged spin-zero state can have zero laboratory moment despite intrinsic deformation. Collective rotational levels approximately follow $E_J=\hbar^2J(J+1)/(2\mathcal I)$, while measured $E2$ strengths test the associated charge collectivity. Muonic-atom X-rays and <isotope shifts> supply complementary charge-radius constraints; hadronic probes provide additional, more interaction-dependent matter and <neutron> information. Neither a charge radius alone nor a finite-range form-factor measurement uniquely fixes the entire nuclear shape.

= Solution

An alpha emitter has an alpha-like cluster coupled to a daughter nucleus. Its positive decay <energy> is $Q=[M_P-M_D-M_\alpha]c^2$; atomic <masses> may be used consistently because the <electron> counts balance. The daughter and alpha share this <energy> according to <momentum> conservation. The attractive nuclear interior and repulsive exterior <Coulomb potential> produce a quasibound state with a barrier, so positive $Q$ does not imply rapid classical escape.

For daughter charge $Z_de$, the exterior barrier is approximately $C/r$, $C=2Z_de^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$, with outer turning radius $b=C/Q$. For zero <orbital angular momentum>, the leading WKB <probability> is
$$
T\sim\exp\left[-\frac2\hbar\int_R^b\sqrt{2\mu(C/r-Q)}\,dr\right],
\qquad\lambda\simeq\nu P_{\rm pre}T,\qquad t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{\lambda}.
$$
Here $R$ is a nuclear matching radius, $\mu$ the <reduced mass>, $\nu$ an interior assault <frequency> and $P_{\rm pre}$ a preformation factor. The barrier factor alone cannot determine the latter two. Nonzero emitted <orbital angular momentum> adds a <centrifugal barrier> and can strongly hinder a transition.

The dominant <action> varies roughly as $Z_d/\sqrt Q$, explaining the Geiger-Nuttall relation and the enormous spread of alpha <half-lives>. <angular momentum> and <parity> constrain the emitted <partial wave>: a spin-zero positive-parity alpha requires $|J_P-J_D|\le L\le J_P+J_D$ and $\pi_P=\pi_D(-1)^L$. Fine-structure alpha groups populate different daughter levels and therefore have different kinetic energies and penetrabilities. Shell structure and alpha clustering also change preformation <probabilities>, so equal $Q$ values do not guarantee equal lifetimes. Escape conserves the total <energy> throughout tunnelling; the particle does not borrow a measured <energy> from the barrier.

Set $c=1$ and use metric $(+---)$. In the <rest frame> of B, $p_B=(m_B,\mathbf0)$ and $p_C=p_B+p_t$. Since $p_t^2=0$,
$$
m_C^2=m_B^2+2m_BE_t,
\qquad\boxed{|\mathbf p_C|=|\mathbf p_t|=\frac{m_C^2-m_B^2}{2m_B}.}
$$
The denominator is $m_B$ because this is the B <rest frame>, not the C <rest frame>. In this frame C and $t$ have equal, parallel three-momenta.

The second decay has $\mathbf p_A=-\mathbf p_s$ in the B <rest frame>. Using $p_A^2=m_A^2$ and $p_s^2=0$ gives
$$
\boxed{|\mathbf p_A|=|\mathbf p_s|=\frac{m_B^2-m_A^2}{2m_B},\qquad
E_A=\frac{m_B^2+m_A^2}{2m_B}.}
$$
This is an ordinary two-body decay in its parent's <rest frame>.

Let $q=(m_C^2-m_B^2)/(2m_B)$ and $p=(m_B^2-m_A^2)/(2m_B)$. In the B frame these are the energies of $t$ and $s$. If $\theta$ is their relative angle,
$$
m_{st}^2=2pq(1-\cos\theta),\qquad
\boxed{(m_{st}^2)_{\min}=0,\quad
(m_{st}^2)_{\max}=\frac{(m_C^2-m_B^2)(m_B^2-m_A^2)}{m_B^2}.}
$$
The minimum has parallel massless particles; the maximum has antiparallel ones. The isotropic decay allows both limiting directions. The bounds are <Lorentz invariant> even though the B frame makes their calculation simple.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Since $\mathbf p_A=-\mathbf p_s$, the same angle gives $m_{At}^2=m_A^2+2q(E_A+p\cos\theta)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{(m_{At}^2)_{\min}=\frac{m_A^2m_C^2}{m_B^2},\qquad
(m_{At}^2)_{\max}=m_A^2+m_C^2-m_B^2.}
$$
The minimum now occurs when $s$ and $t$ are antiparallel. The eventwise identity $m_{At}^2+m_{st}^2=m_C^2+m_A^2-m_B^2$ independently checks the opposite ordering of the two endpoints.

Use $K^-=s\bar u$ and $p=uud$. In the first reaction a <proton> $u$ can join the incoming $\bar u$ to form the $u\bar u$ component of the <neutral pion>, leaving $uds$. In the second, a $u\bar u$ pair can annihilate into a <gluon> which creates $s\bar s$; the remaining $u$ joins $\bar s$ in the outgoing <kaon>, leaving $dss$. Thus
$$
\boxed{X^0=uds,\qquad Y^-=dss.}
$$
<baryon number> is one in both cases. Strong <strangeness> conservation provides a separate check: the first <baryon> has <strangeness> $-1$; the second has <strangeness> $-2$ because the outgoing $K^+$ carries $+1$. The <Feynman diagrams> show representative <quark-line flavour rearrangement> and pair-annihilation topologies. Bound <hadrons> require nonperturbative binding, so a single drawn <gluon> is not an exclusive cross-section calculation.

= Solution
{parent=d}

The radiative $X^0$ decay conserves <strangeness> and proceeds electromagnetically. A strong $\Lambda\pi$ decay is closed because even the lighter neutral-pion threshold exceeds the supplied $X^0$ <mass>. The $Y^-$ decay has about $65\,\mathrm{MeV}$ of available <energy>, but changes <strangeness> from $-2$ to $-1$, so it requires the <weak interaction>. Its lifetime is therefore ordinarily much longer than the electromagnetic lifetime of $X^0$; no numerical lifetime follows from these <masses> alone.

Both particles are <baryons> with half-integer <spin>. A radiative transition to <spin> $1/2$ can couple through <photon> multipoles $L\ge1$, giving $J_X=L\pm1/2$. Electromagnetic <parity> is conserved, but without the electric/magnetic multipolarity neither $J_X$ nor its <parity> is fixed. In the weak $Y^-$ decay, coupling orbital $L$ to the final $\Lambda$ <spin> gives $J_Y=L\pm1/2$; <parity conservation> cannot be imposed on that weak <amplitude>. The supplied modes therefore do not determine unique spin-parity assignments.

The <masses> and flavours identify the familiar candidates as $\Sigma^0$ and $\Xi^-$. Their established ground-state assignments are both $1/2^+$, consistent with the lowest-orbital <quark model>, but this is additional empirical/model information rather than a consequence of the two <decay channels> alone.<Review of Particle Physics by Particle Data Group (2022)>[Sigma-zero and Xi-minus listings, each p.1, established quantum numbers and decay information]{ref}

Assume the quoted <intensities> represent relative emitted <photon numbers>, with efficiency corrections implicit, as required for population-flow bookkeeping. The prominent branch obeys $I_1=I_2+I_4$, and $I_2=I_3$ identifies a serial two-photon branch. The energies obey $E_2+E_3\simeq E_4$ and $E_1+E_4\simeq E_5$ within their uncertainties. The weak fifth line directly spans D to A. Reversing which end contains the line-1 step and interchanging the serial line-2/line-3 order produces the four possibilities.

Take A as zero. A <gamma-ray energy-network fit> to the five <energy> differences gives the following approximate <excitation energies> in keV:
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|rrr}
\text{arrangement}&\Delta E_{AB}&\Delta E_{AC}&\Delta E_{AD}\\\hline
\mathrm I&133.02&269.27&615.10\\
\mathrm{II}&133.02&478.85&615.10\\
\mathrm{III}&136.25&482.08&615.10\\
\mathrm{IV}&345.83&482.08&615.10
\end{array}}
$$
The line connections, in the order $1,2,3,4,5$, are respectively: I: $(B\to A,C\to B,D\to C,D\to B,D\to A)$; II: $(B\to A,D\to C,C\to B,D\to B,D\to A)$; III: $(D\to C,B\to A,C\to B,C\to A,D\to A)$; IV: $(D\to C,C\to B,B\to A,C\to A,D\to A)$.

For I and II, the B population entering through lines 2 or 3 and line 4 exits through line 1. For III and IV, line 1 feeds C and its <intensity> splits into the direct line 4 and serial lines 2/3. The small direct D-to-A branch bypasses these intermediate balances. The central measured energies do not obey every sum exactly; all four fitted arrangements have $\chi^2\simeq0.81$ for two <energy> constraints. Assigning the weakest line's central <energy> as an exact <excitation energy> would discard the more precise cascade measurements.

= Solution

The symbols $EL$ and $ML$ <mean> electric and magnetic radiation multipoles of order $L$. The orders $L=1,2,3$ are called dipole, quadrupole and octupole. They describe the angular structure of charge/current radiation and the total <angular momentum> carried by the radiation; they do not change the <photon>'s intrinsic <spin> from one. A real single <photon> has no monopole radiation mode.

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}

An electric multipole has radiation <parity> $(-1)^L$ and a magnetic multipole <parity> $(-1)^{L+1}$. The radiation <quantum numbers> are therefore
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|cccccc}
\text{mode}&E1&E2&E3&M1&M2&M3\\\hline
J_\gamma^P&1^-&2^+&3^-&1^+&2^-&3^+
\end{array}.}
$$
Equivalently, an $EL$ transition requires $\pi_f/\pi_i=(-1)^L$, while an $ML$ transition requires $\pi_f/\pi_i=(-1)^{L+1}$.

{parent=b}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For comparable transition energies and ordinary single-particle nuclear strengths in the long-wavelength regime, the usual qualitative ordering is **$E1$, $M1$, $E2$, $M2$, $E3$, $M3$ from faster to slower**. Each increase of multipole order is strongly suppressed, and magnetic radiation is usually weaker than electric radiation of the same order. More precisely,
$$
\lambda(\sigma L)\propto E_\gamma^{2L+1}B(\sigma L),\qquad \sigma=E\text{ or }M.
$$
The nuclear reduced transition strength $B$ is essential: collective enhancement, cancellation and <selection rules> can overturn this benchmark. It is not a universal ranking between arbitrary transitions of different energies. The weak measured M3 branch is consistent with high-multipole suppression, but its absolute lifetime cannot be obtained from its arbitrary-unit <intensity> alone.<Guidelines for Nuclear Structure Evaluators by Murray J. Martin>[Appendix C, printed pp.60–67, transition rates and Weisskopf single-particle estimates]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=c}

For a single-photon transition, <angular momentum addition> requires $|J_i-J_f|\le L\le J_i+J_f$, $L\ge1$, together with the electric/magnetic <parity> rules above. Thus a known final state and a measured multipole restrict both the <spin> difference and <parity> of the emitting level. For example, an E1 line reverses <parity> and allows $\Delta J=0,1$ except $0\to0$; a mixed M1+E2 line preserves <parity> and must satisfy both angular triangles. An E2 line preserves <parity> and allows <spin> differences up to two.

Line energies identify connected levels, while calibrated <intensities> and coincidences test cascade flow and branching. If a proposed assignment permits a fast low-order transition to a lower state, an experimentally stringent absence of that line argues against the assignment. Nevertheless, **an allowed transition can have a small or vanishing nuclear <matrix> element**. Pure E2 radiation alone does not prove that M1 is forbidden by <angular momentum>. Detection sensitivity, efficiency and overlap must be considered, and in real nuclei <internal conversion> can remove <photons>; this question explicitly excludes that competing channel. Lifetimes or reduced-strength estimates make a missing-line argument substantially stronger than a bare selection-rule statement.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Use arrangement III. Lines 2 and 4 connect B and C to A, line 3 connects C to B, line 1 connects D to C, and line 5 connects D to A. Every measured multipole preserves <parity>, so **B, C and D all have positive <parity>**. The two mixed M1+E2 transitions to $J_A=7/2$ strictly imply
$$
J_B,J_C\in\{5/2,7/2,9/2\}.
$$
The measured E2 C-to-B and D-to-C transitions require $|J_C-J_B|\le2$ and $|J_D-J_C|\le2$, with their angular sums at least two. The M3 D-to-A line gives $|J_D-7/2|\le3$ and $J_D+7/2\ge3$. These inequalities, with half-integer <spins>, are the strict observed-line constraints. They do not alone fix unique <spins>.

The customary low-multipole-dominance inference goes further. If lower-order allowed radiation would not be accidentally hindered, a pure M3 D-to-A decay indicates $|J_D-7/2|=3$, giving $J_D=1/2$ or $13/2$. Its E2 connection to C then forces $J_C=5/2$ or $9/2$, respectively. Treating the pure E2 C-to-B transition similarly as a <spin> change of two selects
$$
\boxed{(J_B^P,J_C^P,J_D^P)
=(\tfrac{9}{2}^{+},\tfrac{5}{2}^{+},\tfrac{1}{2}^{+})
\quad\text{or}\quad(\tfrac{5}{2}^{+},\tfrac{9}{2}^{+},\tfrac{13}{2}^{+}).}
$$
In either assignment the missing D-to-B line changes <spin> by four. Its lowest parity-preserving multipole is E4, so it is naturally much weaker than the observed E2 D-to-C branch. This spin-reflection ambiguity around the reference <spin> is not resolved by the spectra under this approximation.

The boxed alternatives are **conditional on ordinary, unhindered lower-multipole strengths**. If accidental or structural hindrance is allowed, other <spins> satisfying the preceding inequalities remain possible: an upper limit on an unobserved lower multipole does not mathematically prove its selection-rule prohibition. The supplied <intensities> and absence of other comparable lines support the usual assignments but do not supply measured reduced strengths sufficient to eliminate that qualification.
