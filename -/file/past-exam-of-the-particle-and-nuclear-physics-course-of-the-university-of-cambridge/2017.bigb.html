Use the <inverse beta decay threshold>, with the charged-lepton <mass> generalized to $m_\ell$. For a stationary <proton> and a massless incident <antineutrino>, the <Mandelstam invariant> is $s=m_p^2+2m_pE_\nu$. At threshold the final particles have no relative momentum in their <centre-of-momentum frame>, so $s_{\rm thr}=(m_n+m_\ell)^2$. Therefore
$$
E_{\nu,\rm thr}=\frac{(m_n+m_\ell)^2-m_p^2}{2m_p}=(m_n-m_p+m_\ell)+\frac{(m_n-m_p+m_\ell)^2}{2m_p}.
$$
Using the supplied, rounded <masses> gives
$$
\boxed{E_{\bar\nu_e,\rm thr}=1.813\,\mathrm{MeV},\qquad E_{\bar\nu_\mu,\rm thr}=113.1\,\mathrm{MeV}.}
$$
The second term is the recoil correction; setting the threshold equal just to the final rest-mass excess would miss it.

Reactor <electron antineutrinos> have energies of a few million <electronvolts>. They can produce a <positron> through <inverse beta decay> above the first threshold, but cannot produce a <muon>. Thus reactor <neutrino oscillations> are measured primarily as **electron-antineutrino disappearance**, through the event rate and energy spectrum relative to the unoscillated prediction. An oscillated <muon antineutrino> cannot be identified by the proposed muon-production channel at reactor energies; disappearance alone does not distinguish all possible final flavours.

The separate <proton> and <neutron> shell capacities are $2j+1=2,4,2,6,\ldots$. Filled shells and paired particles have zero net <spin> and <magnetic dipole moment>. The <single-particle nuclear magnetic moment> is $\mu=g_jj\mu_N$, with orbital factors $g_\ell^p=1$, $g_\ell^n=0$ and $s=1/2$.

For nitrogen, the eight <neutrons> form a closed shell. The seventh <proton> occupies $1p_{1/2}$, so $\ell=1$, $j=1/2$ and the <Landé g-factor> gives
$$
g_j=\frac43g_\ell-\frac13g_s=\frac43-\frac{5.586}{3}=-0.52867.
$$
Hence the <nuclear shell model> predicts
$$
\boxed{\mu({}^{15}\mathrm N)=-0.2643\,\mu_N.}
$$
Its single-particle <spin> and <parity> is $1/2^-$. The negative moment results from the proton spin being predominantly opposed to the total <angular momentum> in this $j=\ell-1/2$ level.

For oxygen, eight <protons> and the first eight <neutrons> are paired. The ninth <neutron> occupies $1d_{5/2}$, with $\ell=2$, $j=5/2=\ell+1/2$. Since its orbital factor is zero, $g_j=g_n/(2\ell+1)=-3.826/5$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mu({}^{17}\mathrm O)=-1.913\,\mu_N,\qquad J^P=5/2^+.}
$$
These are ideal single-particle predictions in units of the <nuclear magneton>, not fits incorporating nuclear configuration mixing.

For negligible final <fermion> masses, the <chiral Z-boson partial width> is proportional to $g_L^2+g_R^2$. Both channels have one color state. An <electron> has $Q=-1$, $T_{3L}=-1/2$ and $T_{3R}=0$, giving
$$
g_L^e=-\frac12+0.23=-0.27,\qquad g_R^e=0.23.
$$
For one active <neutrino> species, $Q=0$, $T_{3L}=1/2$ and there is no coupled right-handed neutrino field, so $g_L^\nu=1/2$, $g_R^\nu=0$. The common coupling normalization and <phase space> cancel in the ratio:
$$
\frac{\Gamma(Z\to\nu_e\bar\nu_e)}{\Gamma(Z\to e^-e^+)}=\frac{(1/2)^2}{(-0.27)^2+(0.23)^2}=1.9873.
$$
The same total <decay width> appears in the two <branching fractions>, so
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Br}(Z\to\nu_e\bar\nu_e)=1.9873\times3.36\%=6.68\%.}
$$
There is no factor of three for a single specified flavour. Summing three active <neutrino> flavours would give an invisible <branching fraction> of approximately $20.0\%$ in this approximation.

**What is measured.** A monoenergetic <electron> beam is directed at a thin target. A movable <magnetic spectrometer> selects the scattering angle and outgoing momentum, separating <elastic scattering> from nuclear excitation and other backgrounds. Incident beam current and target thickness supply the normalization of the <differential scattering cross-section>. The <Hofstadter nuclear electron scattering experiment> is a classic implementation of this electromagnetic probe.

For a heavy, approximately spin-zero <atomic nucleus>, neglecting recoil and treating the electron in the <Born approximation>, the point-charge result is multiplied by the <nuclear charge form factor>:
$$
\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\simeq\frac{Z^2\alpha^2\cos^2(\theta/2)}{4E^2\sin^4(\theta/2)}|F(q)|^2,\qquad q\simeq2E\sin\frac\theta2.
$$
Here $q$ is the magnitude of the spatial <momentum transfer> in <natural units>. The cosine factor includes the <electron> <spin>. Dividing the elastic data by the point-charge factor isolates $|F(q)|^2$ within these approximations. Recoil, magnetic terms for targets with spin, <radiative corrections> and particularly <Coulomb distortion in electron scattering> must be included when significant.

**From scattering to size and shape.** For a spherical <nuclear charge distribution>,
$$
F(q)=\frac{4\pi}{Ze}\int_0^\infty r^2\rho_{\rm ch}(r)\frac{\sin(qr)}{qr}\,dr,\qquad F(0)=1.
$$
Thus the <nuclear charge form factor> is a normalized <Fourier transform>. Its low-$q$ expansion,
$$
F(q)=1-\frac{q^2\langle r^2\rangle_{\rm ch}}6+\cdots,\qquad |F(q)|^2=1-\frac{q^2\langle r^2\rangle_{\rm ch}}3+\cdots,
$$
measures the <nuclear charge radius> $r_{\rm ch}=\sqrt{\langle r^2\rangle_{\rm ch}}$. Larger <momentum transfers> resolve the density profile through <diffraction> maxima and minima. For a uniform sphere of sharp radius $R$, the <uniform-sphere nuclear charge form factor> is $3[\sin(qR)-qR\cos(qR)]/(qR)^3$; its first zero is at $qR\simeq4.493$, while $r_{\rm ch}=\sqrt{3/5}R$.

A practical extraction fits a family of densities to all measured cross sections, rather than treating a few measured values of $|F|^2$ as a complete density reconstruction. Finite angular coverage, resolution and the missing sign information make this an <inverse problem>.

**Observed pattern.** Apart from the lightest <nuclei>, the central density is approximately constant; the size grows approximately as $R\simeq1.2A^{1/3}\,\mathrm{fm}$ and there is a diffuse surface. The coefficient describes an equivalent sharp nuclear radius, rather than the root-mean-square radius itself. Heavy <nuclear charge distributions> are often described by the <two-parameter Fermi nuclear charge distribution>, $\rho(r)=\rho_0/[1+\exp((r-c)/a)]$, with half-density radius $c$ scaling roughly as $A^{1/3}$ and diffuseness $a$ around $0.5\,\mathrm{fm}$. The surface thickness is then about $4a\ln3\simeq2.2\,\mathrm{fm}$. Shell effects, deformation and the lightest systems give departures from this smooth pattern. These qualitative features are established by the <electron scattering> density measurements.<Electron Scattering and Nuclear Structure by Robert Hofstadter>{ref}

\Image[2017-pnp-charge-density-models.png]
{title=Original sharp-edge and diffuse nuclear charge models, showing how a surface profile changes the <nuclear charge form factor>}

**Complementary probes.** Transition energies in a <muonic atom> are especially sensitive to nuclear size because the heavy negative <muon> orbits close to the charge. Electronic <isotope shifts>, after separating nuclear-mass and finite-size contributions, measure changes in $\langle r^2\rangle$. Both require atomic-structure calculations. Electromagnetic excitation and transition strengths constrain deformation and charge multipoles. Hadronic scattering can also constrain nuclear dimensions, but carries uncertainties from the <strong interaction> and probes matter rather than purely electric charge.

**Theory.** <Quantum chromodynamics> is a local, non-Abelian <gauge theory> with gauge group <SU(3)> acting on <color charge>. Its matter fields are spin-half <quarks> of six flavours, each in three colors; its eight massless spin-one <gluons> are the gauge fields. Unlike the neutral <photon>, <gluons> themselves carry gauge charge. Consequently there are three-gluon and four-gluon vertices as well as the quark-gluon vertex. A compact <Lagrangian density> is
$$
\mathcal L_{\rm QCD}=\sum_f\bar q_f(i\gamma^\mu D_\mu-m_f)q_f-\frac14G_{\mu\nu}^aG^{a\mu\nu},
$$
where $D_\mu$ is the gauge <covariant derivative> and the field strength contains the nonlinear term $g_sf^{abc}G_\mu^bG_\nu^c$.

The <running coupling> is weak at high momentum scales. At one loop, for $n_f$ active flavours,
$$
\alpha_s(Q^2)\simeq\frac{4\pi}{(11-2n_f/3)\ln(Q^2/\Lambda_{\rm QCD}^2)}.
$$
The positive coefficient $11-2n_f/3$ produces <asymptotic freedom>: short-distance, large-$Q$ processes admit <perturbation theory>. The gluon self-interaction is crucial to its sign. At low scales the coupling grows and isolated colored particles are not observed; the physical spectrum consists of color-singlet <hadrons>, the phenomenon of <confinement>. A hard-produced <quark> or <gluon> radiates and undergoes <hadronization>, giving a collimated <jet>. The one-loop formula is not valid at $Q\sim\Lambda_{\rm QCD}$ and is not, by itself, a mathematical proof of <confinement>.

**Experimental tests.** Different measurements test different ingredients of this account:

* <Deep inelastic scattering> resolves approximately pointlike spin-half constituents. Approximate <Bjorken scaling> supplies the starting parton picture, while the measured logarithmic departures from scaling agree with QCD evolution of <parton distribution functions>.
* The <hadronic R ratio> in <electron-positron annihilation> counts squared quark charges multiplied by three colors, with calculable strong corrections. It tests color multiplicity as well as the quark charge assignments.
* Two-jet events identify a quark-antiquark hard topology. Three-jet events test hard gluon radiation, $e^+e^-\to q\bar qg$; their angular correlations support a spin-one <gluon>. Four-jet correlations and radiation patterns further probe the non-Abelian color factors and gluon self-interactions.
* Determinations of $\alpha_s$ from processes at widely separated scales follow the same <running coupling>, rather than independent interaction strengths. Jet rates, event shapes, hadronic weak-boson decays and high-energy hadron collisions provide complementary tests.
* <Lattice QCD> calculations reproduce many hadronic masses and other low-energy quantities from the same underlying theory, connecting its strongly coupled regime to the observed spectrum.

The convincing evidence is the quantitative consistency across these probes, with a common color group and coupling evolution. A jet by itself is evidence for a hard parton; its rates and correlations supply the more specific tests of <quantum chromodynamics>.<Asymptotic Freedom: From Paradox to Paradigm by Frank Wilczek>{ref}

**Production and identification.** At a hadron collider, initial partons carry fractions of the proton momenta; observable production rates combine parton-level amplitudes with <parton distribution functions>.

* <Top quark> pairs are produced through $gg\to t\bar t$ and $q\bar q\to g^*\to t\bar t$. Single top production uses the <weak charged current>, for example $qb\to q't$ through a virtual <W boson>. The dominant decay is $t\to W^+b$, and similarly for the antiquark. The subsequent <W boson> decays produce dilepton, lepton-plus-jets and all-hadronic channels. Reconstructing the W and b systems constrains the top mass. The top decays before forming an ordinary long-lived top hadron.
* An on-shell <W boson> is produced by quark-antiquark annihilation, for example $u\bar d\to W^+$. Other sources include top decay and boson-pair production; at a lepton collider, $e^+e^-\to W^+W^-$ provides a clean pair channel. The <W boson> decays to $\ell\nu_\ell$ or to a kinematically accessible quark pair. Neglecting masses and strong corrections, the three lepton channels and two up-type quarks with three colors give approximately one-third leptonic and two-thirds hadronic branching. The leptonic modes combine a charged lepton with missing transverse momentum; the transverse-mass distribution constrains $m_W$.
* <Higgs boson> production includes gluon fusion $gg\to H$ through a heavy-quark loop, vector-boson fusion $qq\to qqH$, associated $q\bar q\to WH,ZH$, and $t\bar tH$ production. At a lepton collider, $e^+e^-\to ZH$ is another example. For a Higgs mass near $125\,\mathrm{GeV}$, important decays include $b\bar b$, $WW^*$, $gg$, $\tau^+\tau^-$ and $ZZ^*$. The less frequent $\gamma\gamma$ mode and $ZZ^*\to4\ell$ mode give especially clean reconstructed mass peaks. The asterisk denotes an off-shell daughter boson; two on-shell W or Z bosons are inaccessible at that Higgs mass.

**Why the three masses form a test.** At tree level, <electroweak symmetry breaking> gives $m_W=gv/2$ and $m_Z=\sqrt{g^2+g'^2}\,v/2$. The <Higgs mechanism> relates masses to the vacuum field, but it does not by itself predict the top Yukawa coupling or the Higgs self-coupling, so the three masses are not fixed independently of measured parameters.

The stronger test comes from <radiative corrections>. With accurately measured $\alpha$, $G_F$ and $m_Z$, the <electroweak precision test with the W, top and Higgs masses> uses
$$
m_W^2\left(1-\frac{m_W^2}{m_Z^2}\right)=\frac{\pi\alpha}{\sqrt2G_F(1-\Delta r)}.
$$
The top-bottom loop contributes approximately $\Delta\rho=3G_Fm_t^2/(8\sqrt2\pi^2)$, while the Higgs contribution has a characteristic logarithmic mass dependence. Hence direct measurements of $m_t$ and $m_H$ turn the precision calculation into a prediction for $m_W$. Comparing that prediction with the direct W-mass measurement overconstrains the <Standard Model>; disagreement beyond experimental and theoretical uncertainties could indicate additional particles or interactions in the loops. The test concerns the relation among independently measured quantities, rather than merely the existence of the three particles.

**Alpha lifetimes and competing beta decays.** The <Geiger-Nuttall law> follows approximately from <quantum tunnelling> through the daughter’s <Coulomb barrier>. Increasing $Q_\alpha$ shortens the barrier and greatly increases the decay rate; increasing the daughter charge makes the barrier harder to penetrate. A useful local parametrization is
$$
\log_{10}\frac{T_\alpha}{\mathrm y}\simeq a\frac{Z_d}{\sqrt{Q_\alpha/\mathrm{MeV}}}+b,\qquad Z_d=Z-2.
$$
Using the supplied *nuclear* masses, no electron-mass subtraction is needed for <alpha decay>. The calculated <Q values> are
$$
\begin{array}{c|rrrr}
\text{parent}&{}^{232}\mathrm{Th}&{}^{228}\mathrm{Th}&{}^{228}\mathrm{Ra}&{}^{228}\mathrm{Ac}\\\hline
Q_\alpha/\mathrm{MeV}&4.07063&5.51445&4.07063&4.67610
\end{array}.
$$
Calibrate the two constants to the two supplied even-even thorium <half-lives>. Their daughter charges are both 88, so
$$
a=\frac{\log_{10}(1.4\times10^{10}/1.9)}{88(1/\sqrt{4.07063}-1/\sqrt{5.51445})}\simeq1.606,\qquad b\simeq-59.92.
$$
This gives the order-of-magnitude estimates
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
T_\alpha({}^{228}\mathrm{Ra})&\sim4\times10^8\,\mathrm y,&\lambda_\alpha&\sim6\times10^{-17}\,\mathrm s^{-1},\\
T_\alpha({}^{228}\mathrm{Ac})&\sim5\times10^4\,\mathrm y,&\lambda_\alpha&\sim4\times10^{-13}\,\mathrm s^{-1}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Here $\lambda_\alpha=\ln2/T_\alpha$. The known <beta-minus decay> rates are approximately $3.8\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm s^{-1}$ and $3.2\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm s^{-1}$, respectively. Thus the reference alpha <branching fractions> are only about $1.6\times10^{-8}$ and $1.4\times10^{-8}$. Beta decay overwhelmingly removes the parents before alpha emission, explaining why the alpha branches are not seen in the stated chain.

These estimates are local extrapolations, not precision predictions. In particular, actinium is odd-odd, so extrapolating an even-even <Geiger-Nuttall law> neglects nuclear-structure and angular-momentum hindrance. Such effects can further suppress its alpha branch.

**Allowed daughter spin and parity.** The even-even parent in its <ground state> and the <alpha particle> both have $J^P=0^+$. The total final <angular momentum> must be zero, so coupling daughter spin $J_Y$ to relative orbital momentum $L$ requires $J_Y=L$. <Parity conservation> gives $+1=\pi_Y(+1)(-1)^L$. The <alpha-decay angular-momentum and parity selection rule> is therefore
$$
\boxed{J_Y^P=0^+,1^-,2^+,3^-,4^+,\ldots.}
$$
These are the <natural parity> states. Restricting the list to even spin would be too strong; even-spin rotational bands are a further structural specialization.

**Ground-state line and recoil.** The <nuclear alpha-decay recoil correction> gives
$$
T_\alpha=Q_\alpha\frac{M_d}{M_d+m_\alpha}.
$$
For the thorium ground-state decay, this predicts $T_\alpha\simeq4.0004\,\mathrm{MeV}$, with daughter recoil about $70.2\,\mathrm{keV}$. This is close to the highest measured line, $4.0112\,\mathrm{MeV}$: the difference is $10.8\,\mathrm{keV}$, or $0.27\%$. Conversely that line implies $Q_\alpha\simeq4.0816\,\mathrm{MeV}$. The quoted nuclear masses have only five decimal places in atomic mass units; rounding three such entries can shift their mass difference by up to about $14\,\mathrm{keV}$. Thus the requested approximate agreement is supported, but the rounded table does not reproduce the line energy exactly. Dropping daughter recoil would give an error around $70\,\mathrm{keV}$ instead.

**Excited levels.** Relative to the highest line, the two alpha-energy deficits are $62.7$ and $201.2\,\mathrm{keV}$. Multiplying by the common recoil factor $1+m_\alpha/M_d\simeq232/228$ gives
$$
\boxed{E_{x,1}\simeq63.8\,\mathrm{keV},\qquad E_{x,2}\simeq204.7\,\mathrm{keV},\qquad\frac{E_{x,2}}{E_{x,1}}=3.209.}
$$
A deformed even-even <nucleus> has a ground-state <nuclear rotational band> with $E_J\simeq J(J+1)/(2\mathcal J)$ and $J^P=0^+,2^+,4^+,\ldots$. Its $4^+$ to $2^+$ excitation ratio is $20/6=10/3=3.333$, with the observed ratio about $3.7\%$ below this ideal value. The likely assignments are therefore **$2^+$ and $4^+$ rotational excitations**, with modest departures from a rigid constant-inertia rotor. The ratio supports this interpretation; it is not a unique spin measurement by itself.

**Sargent comparison.** For <beta-minus decay> using nuclear masses, subtract the emitted electron rest mass:
$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_\beta({}^{228}\mathrm{Ra})&=(227.98280-227.98220-0.00055)\,\mathrm u=0.046575\,\mathrm{MeV},\\
Q_\beta({}^{228}\mathrm{Ac})&=(227.98220-227.97937-0.00055)\,\mathrm u=2.12381\,\mathrm{MeV}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The simple $\lambda\propto Q^5$ version of <Sargent's rule> would predict
$$
\frac{\lambda_\beta(\mathrm{Ac})}{\lambda_\beta(\mathrm{Ra})}\simeq\left(\frac{2.12381}{0.046575}\right)^5=1.97\times10^8.
$$
The measured rate ratio is the inverse half-life ratio,
$$
\boxed{\frac{\lambda_\beta(\mathrm{Ac})}{\lambda_\beta(\mathrm{Ra})}=\frac{5.75\times365.25\times24}{6.1}=8.26\times10^3.}
$$
The naive fifth-power estimate is about **$2.4\times10^4$ times larger**. It correctly predicts the much faster actinium decay but fails quantitatively. The small radium endpoint is far below the electron rest mass, violating the negligible-mass approximation behind the fifth power; Coulomb distortion of the emitted electron, different nuclear matrix elements and different daughter excitations also matter. The rounded mass difference makes the very small radium $Q$ especially uncertain. The appropriate comparison of individual branches uses the actual endpoint-dependent statistical factor and <comparative half-life>, not a universal fifth power of the ground-state release.

**Beta selection rules and the four observed branches.** The radium parent has $0^+$, so the ground-state $0^+\to3^+$ transition is a <unique forbidden beta decay> of second order: it preserves parity and requires three units of angular momentum. For the increasing daughter excitation energies, the endpoints decrease. The assignments are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|l}
\text{daughter state}&\text{endpoint}/\mathrm{keV}&\text{intensity}&\text{classification}\\\hline
3^+\text{ ground state}&\text{about }46.6&\text{not listed}&\text{second-forbidden unique}\\
1^-\text{ first excited}&39.5&10\%&\text{first-forbidden nonunique}\\
1^+\text{ second excited}&39.1&40\%&\text{allowed Gamow-Teller}\\
1^-\text{ third excited}&25.6&20\%&\text{first-forbidden nonunique}\\
1^+\text{ fourth excited}&12.7&30\%&\text{allowed Gamow-Teller}
\end{array}.
$$
For $0^+\to1^+$, zero lepton orbital angular momentum and lepton-pair spin one give an <allowed beta decay> of <Gamow-Teller transition> type. The $0^+\to1^-$ branches need odd lepton orbital angular momentum, with lowest value one, and are <forbidden beta decays> of first order; they are not unique because they do not have the maximal transfer $\Delta J=2$ at that order.

The absence of a listed ground-state branch is consistent with its stronger forbiddenness. The two allowed branches carry $70\%$ in total, and the nearly equal-endpoint pair favours the allowed branch by four to one. These trends support the classification. However, first-forbidden branches still carry $30\%$, and the lower-endpoint first-forbidden branch is stronger than the higher-endpoint one. Thus selection-rule labels alone do not explain all intensities: nuclear overlaps and the endpoint-dependent <phase space> are indispensable. “Forbidden” means suppressed at leading order, not impossible.

**Continuum amplitudes.** The leading <quantum electrodynamics> diagram is <electron-positron annihilation> into a virtual <photon>, followed by conversion to a muon pair. Replacing the outgoing muons with a quark-antiquark pair gives the quark-level hadronic diagram. The first two panels below show these processes.

\Image[2017-pnp-annihilation-and-decays.png]
{title=Original <Feynman diagrams> for electromagnetic pair production, vector-quarkonium annihilation, and the open-bottom decay topology}

The <Feynman rules> give, up to signs and factors of $i$,
$$
\mathcal M=\frac{e^2}{s}[\bar v_e\gamma^\mu u_e][\bar u_\mu\gamma_\mu v_\mu].
$$
The two vertices supply $e^2$, and the photon <propagator> supplies $1/s$. Each external current scales as $\sqrt s$, so the complete amplitude is of order $e^2$, and its squared magnitude is of order $e^4\propto\alpha^2$. The two-body <relativistic scattering cross-section> supplies the remaining $1/s$ from flux and <phase space>. Integrating the angular distribution yields $\sigma_{\mu\mu}=4\pi\alpha^2/(3s)$. Keeping only the squared propagator while ignoring the external currents would incorrectly predict $1/s^2$.

A quark of charge $Q_fe$ contributes $N_cQ_f^2$ times the massless muon rate. With $u,d,s,c,b$ open and $N_c=3$, the <hadronic R ratio> is
$$
\boxed{R=3\left[2\left(\frac23\right)^2+3\left(\frac13\right)^2\right]=\frac{11}{3}.}
$$
This is the requested massless leading-QED continuum approximation. Strong corrections, physical hadron thresholds, resonance contributions and Z exchange are separate effects; the pure photon formula is not a complete high-energy electroweak cross section throughout the entire stated range.

**Efficiency normalization.** Read the original PDF plot as a continuum of about $0.8\,\mathrm{nb}$ and a maximum around $1.7\,\mathrm{nb}$ at $E\simeq10.58\,\mathrm{GeV}$. The supplied conversion constant gives
$$
1\,\mathrm{GeV}^{-2}=(0.1975)^2\,\mathrm{fm}^2=3.9006\times10^5\,\mathrm{nb}.
$$
Hence near the peak,
$$
\sigma_{\mu\mu}\simeq0.778\,\mathrm{nb},\qquad\sigma_{\rm cont}^{\rm QED}\simeq\frac{11}{3}\sigma_{\mu\mu}=2.85\,\mathrm{nb}.
$$
If $C$ multiplies the displayed cross sections, the required scale is
$$
\boxed{C\simeq\frac{2.85}{0.8}\simeq3.6,\qquad\epsilon\simeq C^{-1}\simeq0.28.}
$$
Here $\epsilon$ is the implied <detection efficiency> under the common-scale assumption. Using continuum readings $0.75$–$0.85\,\mathrm{nb}$ gives approximately $C=3.4$–$3.8$, illustrating the graph-reading precision. The nearby open-bottom threshold also limits the precision of the ideal continuum approximation.

**Resonance quantum numbers and widths.** Direct formation through a single photon requires
$$
\boxed{J^P=1^-\quad\text{and, for neutral quarkonium, }J^{PC}=1^{--}.}
$$
These are the quantum numbers of the electromagnetic current. This concerns single-photon formation; associated production or two-photon reactions need not obey this restriction.

For a <Breit-Wigner resonance cross section>, subtract the continuum before finding the <full width at half maximum>. The plotted excess is about $0.9\,\mathrm{nb}$, so its half-height occurs at a total displayed cross section near $1.25\,\mathrm{nb}$, not $0.85\,\mathrm{nb}$. The crossings are roughly $10.57$ and $10.59\,\mathrm{GeV}$, with a separation around $0.02$–$0.025\,\mathrm{GeV}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{M_{\Upsilon(4S)}\simeq10.58\,\mathrm{GeV},\qquad\Gamma\simeq20\text{–}25\,\mathrm{MeV}.}
$$
For spin-one formation from unpolarized electrons and positrons, $g=3/4$ and $p\simeq M/2$. With $\Gamma_{\rm had}\simeq\Gamma$, the <peak resonance cross section and branching fractions> relation becomes
$$
\sigma_{\rm res,peak}=\frac{12\pi}{M^2}\frac{\Gamma_{ee}}\Gamma.
$$
The corrected excess is $C(1.7-0.8)\simeq3.2\,\mathrm{nb}$. Taking $\Gamma\simeq24\,\mathrm{MeV}$ as a representative graph reading gives
$$
\Gamma_{ee}=\frac{\left[\frac{3.2}{3.9006\times10^5}\right]\,(10.58)^2\,(0.024)}{12\pi}\,\mathrm{GeV}\simeq5.9\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{GeV}.
$$
Thus the plot supports
$$
\boxed{\Gamma_{ee}\sim0.5\text{–}0.6\,\mathrm{keV}.}
$$
The width and electronic partial width are estimates from the printed graph under the prescribed Breit-Wigner and efficiency assumptions; these are not precision world-average values. A scale correction changes the peak height and inferred partial width, but not the peak position or width.

**Why the lower Upsilon states are narrow.** In <electromagnetic production and decay of vector quarkonium>, every $nS$ state can annihilate as $b\bar b\to\gamma^*\to e^+e^-$. The electronic width probes the wavefunction at the origin, $\Gamma_{ee}\propto\alpha^2Q_b^2|\psi_{nS}(0)|^2/M_n^2$. The radial overlaps differ, but the same short-distance annihilation mechanism operates, so electronic widths remain of comparable order.

The hadronic mechanism changes sharply at $2m_B\simeq10.560\,\mathrm{GeV}$. The first three <Upsilon mesons> lie below it and cannot produce an on-shell B-meson pair. Their leading purely strong annihilation channel is $b\bar b\to ggg$, followed by <hadronization>: a color-singlet pair cannot annihilate to one gluon, and two gluons cannot match its negative charge-conjugation parity. Three-gluon annihilation carries three powers of $\alpha_s$ in the rate and requires quark-antiquark overlap. Electromagnetic annihilation to light quarks and, for excited states, transitions to lower bottomonium plus pions or photons also contribute.

The fourth state lies above the pair threshold and can instead undergo $\Upsilon(4S)\to B\bar B$ through light-pair creation, keeping the b and anti-b in separate outgoing <mesons>. The final panel depicts this quark-line topology; it represents a nonperturbative strong decay, not a free on-shell b quark radiating a real gluon. For two pseudoscalar B mesons the pair is in a P wave to give $1^-$. Although close to threshold, this open strong channel has a much larger rate than the annihilation channels. Consequently a total width of tens of MeV can coexist with an electronic width below a keV; below threshold the total widths are smaller by hundreds or more, while their electronic widths remain of similar order.

**Hyperfine estimate.** For two spin-half constituents,
$$
\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2=\frac12[S(S+1)-3/2]=\begin{cases}-3/4,&S=0,\\+1/4,&S=1.\end{cases}
$$
The <meson hyperfine mass splitting> therefore raises the vector mass above the pseudoscalar mass. To infer its coefficient from only one measured mass, adopt the intended simple constituent ansatz that the spin-independent baseline is $m_b+m_q=5.31\,\mathrm{GeV}$. Then
$$
5.280=5.310-\frac{3A}{4(5.0)(0.31)},\qquad\boxed{A\simeq0.062\,\mathrm{GeV}^3.}
$$
For the spin-one partner,
$$
\boxed{M_{B^*}\simeq5.310+\frac{A}{4(5.0)(0.31)}=5.320\,\mathrm{GeV},\qquad M_{B^*}-M_B\simeq40\,\mathrm{MeV}.}
$$
The estimate is the same for $\bar bu$ and $\bar bd$ with the supplied equal light-quark masses. The splitting is below $m_\pi$, so the strong decay $B^*\to B\pi$ is closed. An electromagnetic transition is open and dominates over weak decay:
$$
\boxed{B^*\to B\gamma.}
$$
It preserves electric charge, giving the corresponding charged or neutral B meson; the photon energy is approximately $40\,\mathrm{MeV}$, slightly reduced by recoil. Strictly, the statement that the mass merely *includes* a spin-spin term does not fix $A$: an independent spin-independent binding energy would leave the baseline unknown. The numerical prediction above explicitly uses the additive constituent-mass approximation.
