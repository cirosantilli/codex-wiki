= Solution
{parent=a}

The <diphoton invariant mass> follows by adding the two <photon> four-momenta: $M^2c^4=2E_1E_2(1-\cos\theta)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{M=126.65\,\mathrm{GeV}/c^2}.
$$
For <correlated diphoton mass resolution>, at fixed angle, $dM/M=(dE_1/E_1+dE_2/E_2)/2$. If the two quoted fractional errors are independent one-standard-deviation uncertainties,
$$
\frac{\sigma_M}{M}=\frac12\sqrt{0.1^2+0.1^2},\qquad
\boxed{M=(127\pm9)\,\mathrm{GeV}/c^2}.
$$
An unknown common energy-scale error would be correlated: with <correlation coefficient> $\rho$, the fractional variance is $(0.1^2+0.1^2+2\rho\,0.1^2)/4$. Fully correlated scale errors give 10%, rather than 7.1%. A <mass> consistent with a candidate is not by itself evidence establishing its identity.

For the <neutral strange B meson>, a charged-current tree amplitude has $\bar b\to\bar cW^+$ followed by $W^+\to c\bar s$, while the initial $s$ is a spectator. The $c\bar c$ pair forms the <j-psi-meson> and the $s\bar s$ pair forms the <phi meson>. Charge and <quark> flavour flow are conserved at each vertex except for the permitted weak flavour change. <antiquark> arrows point opposite their physical propagation direction.

\Image[paper-1-bs-decay.png]
{title=Original quark-level charged-current contribution to the neutral strange B-meson decay; arrows show fermion flow}

Useful reconstruction modes are **$J/\psi\to\mu^+\mu^-$ or $e^+e^-$**, through a <virtual photon>, and **$\phi\to K^+K^-$**, a strong decay. The latter is the largest individual $\phi$ mode, with $K_L^0K_S^0$ also important. For the $J/\psi$, the <inclusive branching fraction versus reconstruction mode> distinction matters: hadronic decays dominate inclusively, principally through three <gluons>; each charged-lepton pair accounts for about 6%, not most decays. The charm pair lies below open-charm threshold. These distinctions are confirmed by the official particle listings.<Review of Particle Physics by Particle Data Group (2022)>[J/psi(1S) listing, p. 2, decay modes; phi(1020) listing, p. 4, decay modes]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=a}

A <nuclear excited state> has internal <energy> above its <ground state>, with levels labelled by <spin> and <parity> $J^\pi$ and, where useful, approximate <isospin>. In the shell model, nucleons occupy orbitals split by <spin-orbit coupling>; <nuclear particle-hole excitation> and broken pairs produce excited configurations. An orbital has <parity> $(-1)^l$, and an even-even paired <ground state> is commonly $0^+$. Residual interactions mix configurations, so shell spacings are not a complete spectrum.

Collective motion gives distinct patterns. A deformed <nucleus> can have a rotational band with $E_J-E_0=\hbar^2J(J+1)/(2\mathcal I)$, so a simple even-even $0^+,2^+,4^+$ band has $E_4/E_2=10/3$. Near spherical shape, a quadrupole vibration gives a one-phonon $2^+$ excitation; two such <phonons> can couple to $0^+,2^+,4^+$ near twice that <energy> in the harmonic limit. Anharmonicity and coupling to single-particle motion modify these ideal patterns.

De-excitation occurs by gamma emission, <internal conversion>, or particle emission if energetically open. <photon> multipolarity $\lambda\ge1$ must satisfy the angular-momentum triangle rule. Electric multipoles change <parity> by $(-1)^\lambda$ and magnetic multipoles by $(-1)^{\lambda+1}$; a single <photon> cannot connect $0\to0$. Large multipolarity or a small transition matrix element can make a long-lived <nuclear isomer>. Gamma energies, coincidence cascades, angular correlations and lifetimes constrain level schemes; inelastic scattering and transfer reactions supply complementary <spin>/configuration information. One observed line alone need not identify a unique level assignment.

= Solution

<parity> reverses spatial coordinates and <polar vectors> such as <momentum>, while leaving <axial vectors> such as <angular momentum> unchanged. The correlation $\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf p_e$ is therefore <parity> odd. For polarized beta-emitting <nuclei> one can measure
$$
W(\theta)\propto1+A\mathcal P\frac vc\cos\theta,
$$
where $\theta$ is measured from <nuclear polarization>, $\mathcal P$ its magnitude and $A$ a decay-dependent coefficient. A nonzero <spin>–<momentum> asymmetry is incompatible with <parity> invariance.

The <cobalt-60> experiment polarized <nuclei> at low temperature and compared beta emission along and opposite the polarization axis. Preferential emission on one side established weak-interaction <parity violation>. Reversing polarization tests whether the asymmetry follows nuclear orientation; warming the sample removes polarization, while gamma anisotropy monitors its loss. Detector acceptance, backgrounds and magnetic deflection of charged particles must be separated from a true emission asymmetry.<Experimental Test of Parity Conservation in Beta Decay by Chien-Shiung Wu and others>[1957, polarized cobalt-60 experiment and temperature/polarization checks]{ref}

In the modern <weak charged current>, the <V-A interaction> couples to left-chiral <fermion> fields. In the massless limit this selects left-helicity <neutrinos> and right-helicity <antineutrinos>. Massive charged <leptons> need not be pure-helicity states, consistent with the factor $v/c$. Strong and <electromagnetic interactions> conserve <parity> to the usual experimental approximation; <parity violation> alone should not be confused with <CP violation> or a failure of CPT.

= Solution

The electroweak <gauge group> is $SU(2)_L\times U(1)_Y$. Left-handed <fermions> form weak-isospin doublets and right-handed charged <fermions> are singlets under $SU(2)_L$. Before <symmetry> breaking the <gauge fields> are $W^1,W^2,W^3$ and $B$, with couplings $g,g'$. A Higgs-doublet <vacuum expectation value> breaks the <symmetry> to electromagnetic $U(1)$.

In units $\hbar=c=1$, the physical combinations and tree-level relations are
$$
W^\pm=\frac{W^1\mp iW^2}{\sqrt2},\quad
A=B\cos\theta_W+W^3\sin\theta_W,\quad
Z=-B\sin\theta_W+W^3\cos\theta_W,
$$
$$
e=g\sin\theta_W=g'\cos\theta_W,\qquad
M_W=gv/2,\qquad M_Z=\frac v2\sqrt{g^2+g'^2},\qquad
\boxed{M_W=M_Z\cos\theta_W}.
$$
The <photon> remains massless. <charged currents> change members of weak doublets, whereas the $Z$ mediates <weak neutral currents> with parity-dependent couplings. At low energies $W$ exchange reduces to the Fermi contact interaction, $G_F/\sqrt2=g^2/(8M_W^2)$; the apparent weak suppression reflects the massive mediator. This unifies the gauge description, not the numerical equality of all low-energy couplings.<A Model of Leptons by Steven Weinberg>[1967, gauge structure, symmetry breaking and vector-boson mass relations]{ref}

Neutral-current <neutrino> interactions and subsequent observation of massive weak vector bosons tested this structure. In a neutral-current <neutrino> experiment, an isolated hadronic interaction without an accompanying charged <lepton> is a candidate signal; entering neutral hadrons and missed charged-current <leptons> are important backgrounds. Gargamelle's neutral-current evidence supplied a direct experimental check beyond ordinary <beta decay>.<Observation of neutrino-like interactions without muon or electron in the Gargamelle neutrino experiment by F. J. Hasert>[1973, event topology and background analysis]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=3}

For the lowest spatial state, <orbital angular momentum> is zero and the spatial <wavefunction> is symmetric. A <three-quark colour singlet> is antisymmetric, proportional to $\epsilon_{abc}|abc\rangle$. The <Pauli exclusion principle> for <quarks> therefore requires a symmetric spin–flavour factor. Colour and flavour are different degrees of freedom even though both use three-dimensional group representations.

The <tensor product> flavour decomposition is $3\otimes3\otimes3=10_S\oplus8_M\oplus8_M\oplus1_A$. Three spin-one-half particles have a symmetric spin-$3/2$ quartet and mixed-symmetry spin-$1/2$ sectors. The symmetric <spin> quartet combines with symmetric flavour factors to give a <baryon decuplet>. Mixed <spin> and mixed flavour factors instead combine to a symmetric <spin>–flavour state, giving one physical ground-state <baryon octet>, not two independent ground-state octets. Equivalently the <symmetric spin-flavour SU6 representation> has dimension $56=10\times4+8\times2$. The antisymmetric flavour singlet would require a fully antisymmetric three-spin state, which does not exist for only two single-quark <spin> components.

The spin-one-half <baryon octet> contains $(p,n)$, $(\Sigma^+,\Sigma^0,\Sigma^-)$, $\Lambda$ and $(\Xi^0,\Xi^-)$. The spin-three-halves <baryon decuplet> contains the four $\Delta$ charge states, three $\Sigma^*$ states, two $\Xi^*$ states and $\Omega^-$. Both have positive <parity> in this $L=0$ approximation. Strange-quark <mass> breaks the approximate <flavour symmetry> and splits multiplets.

In the <baryon hyperfine mass formula>, the constituent-mass sum accounts phenomenologically for common confinement <energy>. A colour-magnetic <spin>–<spin> interaction contributes a contact-type term proportional to $\mathbf S_i\cdot\mathbf S_j/(m_im_j)$: each magnetic moment supplies one inverse <mass>. The common colour and spatial-overlap factors are absorbed into $A'$. These are effective constituent <masses>, not short-distance current-quark <masses>. Use dimensionless <spin> operators with $\hbar=1$, as implicit in the supplied formula and coefficient.

Label the identical up <quarks> 1 and 2. Their flavour and $L=0$ spatial factors are symmetric, and their colour pair is antisymmetric, so their <spin> is a triplet, $S_{12}=1$. Thus
$$
\langle\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2\rangle
=\frac12\left[1(1+1)-\frac34-\frac34\right]=\frac14.
$$
Coupling the strange <spin> gives
$$
\langle(\mathbf S_1+\mathbf S_2)\cdot\mathbf S_3\rangle
=\frac12\left[J(J+1)-2-\frac34\right]
=\begin{cases}-1,&J=1/2,\\1/2,&J=3/2.\end{cases}
$$
The two up–strange denominators are identical. Hence
$$
\boxed{M_{1/2}=2m_u+m_s+A'\left(\frac1{4m_u^2}-\frac1{m_um_s}\right)},\qquad
\boxed{M_{3/2}=2m_u+m_s+A'\left(\frac1{4m_u^2}+\frac1{2m_um_s}\right)}.
$$
With the supplied <masses> expressed in GeV units, the common <mass> is $1.261\,\mathrm{GeV}/c^2$. The predicted values are
$$
\boxed{M(\Sigma^+)=1.177\,\mathrm{GeV}/c^2,\qquad
M(\Sigma^{*+})=1.377\,\mathrm{GeV}/c^2}.
$$
They are about $13$ and $3\,\mathrm{MeV}/c^2$ below the stated measurements, respectively. The predicted splitting is $201\,\mathrm{MeV}/c^2$, compared with $190\,\mathrm{MeV}/c^2$. The agreement is useful for such a simple model, but a common overlap constant neglects flavour-dependent spatial structure and other residual interactions.

For the weak decay, one contribution is $s\to uW^-$, $W^-\to d\bar u$. The two spectator up <quarks> combine with $d$ into the <proton>, and the new $u\bar u$ supplies the <neutral pion>'s corresponding component. This changes <strangeness>. In the strong decay, a <gluon> creates $d\bar d$; rearrangement gives $uds$ and $u\bar d$ without changing <strangeness>.

\Image[paper-1-sigma-decays.png]
{title=Original weak and strong quark-level contributions to the two charged Sigma-baryon decays; final groupings show the hadrons}

The strong channel conserves <parity>: the positive-parity daughter <baryon> and negative-parity <pion> require odd relative <orbital angular momentum>, with a $P$ wave sufficient. The weak channel need not conserve <parity>. Using the <mean lifetime> relation $\tau=\hbar/\Gamma$,
$$
\boxed{\tau(\Sigma^+)\simeq8.2\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm s,\qquad
\tau(\Sigma^{*+})\simeq1.8\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm s}.
$$
The widths differ by about $4.5\times10^{12}$, principally because one is weak and flavour changing and the other is an open strong decay. They are not comparable transitions differing only by a small <spin> factor.

= Solution
{parent=4}

At energies far below the $W$ <mass>, replace its propagator by a constant. The modern <V-A interaction> form of Fermi's contact theory has, in $\hbar=c=1$ units,
$$
\mathcal H_{\rm eff}=\frac{G_FV_{ud}}{\sqrt2}
[\bar\psi_p\gamma^\mu(g_V-g_A\gamma^5)\psi_n]
[\bar\psi_e\gamma_\mu(1-\gamma^5)\psi_\nu]+\mathrm{h.c.}
$$
Nucleons are treated nonrelativistically within nuclear states. To leading order in their velocity, the vector charge and axial spatial current give the two nuclear operators
$$
M_F=\langle f|\sum_k\tau_k^+|i\rangle,\qquad
\mathbf M_{GT}=\langle f|\sum_k\boldsymbol\sigma_k\tau_k^+|i\rangle,
$$
where $\tau^+$ here means neutron-to-proton conversion. **(i)** The scalar Fermi operator permits $\Delta J=0$, including $0\to0$, without <parity> change. The rank-one Gamow–Teller operator permits $\Delta J=0,1$ without <parity> change, excluding $0\to0$. Both terms can contribute when the same nuclear states permit them; their strengths contain nuclear matrix elements, not just <phase space>.

**(ii)** Expand the <lepton> waves across the <nucleus> in powers of their momenta times nuclear radius. When $pR/\hbar\ll1$, the constant spatial term gives allowed transitions. If its matrix element vanishes by <spin>/<parity> selection, higher terms supply <lepton> <orbital angular momentum>: “forbidden” means suppressed, not impossible. First-forbidden terms change <parity> and can have $\Delta J=0,1,2$; the rank-two $\Delta J=2$ case is unique first-forbidden. Higher orders carry additional small spatial factors and different energy-dependent shape factors. The contact approximation alone does not imply the allowed approximation.

For a single branch, neglect recoil and <neutrino> <mass> and first assume an energy-independent nuclear shape factor. <Fermi's golden rule> integrates the <neutrino> <phase space> with $E_\nu=Q-T$, while the <electron> <momentum> volume is proportional to $p^2dp$. Including Coulomb distortion,
$$
N(p)\,dp=C\,p^2F(p)(Q-T)^2\,dp,\qquad
\boxed{N'(p)=\sqrt C\,(Q-T)}.
$$
This <momentum-bin Kurie ordinate> is linear in <kinetic energy> and vanishes at its endpoint. If instead using counts per unit <kinetic energy>, $dN/dT\propto F(p)pE_e(Q-T)^2$ in $c=1$ units; converting with $dT/dp=p/E_e$ recovers the supplied momentum-bin definition. No extra factor of $E_e$ should be left inside that definition of $N'$.

The graph's horizontal coordinate is **$T/(m_ec^2)$**, not $T$ in MeV. Its intercept is approximately $3.9$–$4.0$, so
$$
\boxed{Q=T_{\max}\simeq2.0\,\mathrm{MeV}}.
$$
For a ground-to-ground branch between bare <nuclei>, the emitted <electron>'s rest <energy> must also be supplied:
$$
\boxed{[M_{\rm nuc}(^{91}\mathrm Y)-M_{\rm nuc}(^{91}\mathrm{Zr})]c^2
\simeq Q+m_ec^2\simeq2.5\,\mathrm{MeV}}.
$$
For neutral atomic <masses> the <mass> difference instead equals approximately $Q\simeq2.0\,\mathrm{MeV}$, neglecting small electron-binding corrections. The graph supports this approximate precision, not a precise evaluated <isotope> <mass>. Interpreting a branch to an excited daughter would require adding its <excitation energy>; the ground-state comparison assumes the ground-state branch intended here.

The <shell-model prediction for yttrium-91 beta decay> follows from shell filling: the <proton> orbitals above 28 fill $2p_{3/2}$ with four <protons> and $1f_{5/2}$ with six, reaching 38. Yttrium has its 39th <proton> in $2p_{1/2}$, giving $J_i^\pi=1/2^-$. Its 52 <neutrons> include the closed 50-neutron core and a pair in $2d_{5/2}$, coupled to zero in the simple paired <ground state>. Zirconium has 40 paired <protons> and 51 <neutrons>, leaving an unpaired $2d_{5/2}$ <neutron>, so $J_f^\pi=5/2^+$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{|\Delta J|=2,\quad\pi_f=-\pi_i:\qquad\text{first-forbidden unique beta decay}}.
$$
The single-particle change is <neutron> $d_{5/2}$ to <proton> $p_{1/2}$; the <parity> change and rank two prevent an allowed Fermi or Gamow–Teller contribution.

**The straight plotted line is a constant-shape idealization.** For the shell-model transition just inferred, a leading unique-forbidden shape factor contains $C(T)\propto p_e^2+p_\nu^2$ in $c=1$ units, with Coulomb/finite-size refinements. Consequently the uncorrected quantity specified in the paper is generally proportional to $(Q-T)\sqrt{C(T)}$, not exactly linear. A consistent <shape-corrected Kurie plot> also divides its ordinate by $\sqrt{C(T)}$. The source's exact straight-line construction and its forbidden-transition inference cannot both be exact with a Coulomb-only $F$; the approximate graphical endpoint can still be read as requested. Allowed and unique-forbidden spectral shapes are experimentally distinguishable.<Shapes of Allowed and Unique First-Forbidden β-Ray Spectra: 114In, 42K, 86Rb, 90Sr, and 90Y by H. Daniel and others>[1964 abstract; separate allowed and unique-forbidden spectrum measurements]{ref}
