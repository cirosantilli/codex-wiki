= Solution
{parent=a}

Treat the quoted maximum as the resonant <Z boson> contribution described by the <Breit-Wigner resonance cross section>. The <electron> and <positron> each have <spin> $1/2$, while the <Z boson> has <spin> one, so
$$
g=\frac{2(1)+1}{[2(1/2)+1]^2}=\frac34.
$$
Neglect the <electron mass> at this <centre-of-mass energy>. Each incident momentum magnitude is then $p_i=E_0/2$ in <natural units>. Write $B_e=\Gamma_{ee}/\Gamma$. At the resonance peak the denominator is $\Gamma^2/4$, and the same partial <decay width> appears for production and decay:
$$
\sigma_{\rm peak}=\frac{\pi g}{p_i^2}\frac{\Gamma_{ee}^2}{\Gamma^2/4}
=\frac{12\pi}{E_0^2}B_e^2.
$$
The <peak resonance cross section and branching fractions> relation contains $B_e^2$, because the electronic channel occurs at both ends of the resonant process.

For an area in ordinary units, restore $(\hbar c)^2$. The original PDF prints $\hbar c=197.5\,\mathrm{MeV}$, which is dimensionally incomplete: the intended standard conversion is $197.5\,\mathrm{MeV\,fm}=0.1975\,\mathrm{GeV\,fm}$. Also,
$$
2.0\,\mathrm{nb}=2.0\times10^{-37}\,\mathrm{m^2}
=2.0\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{fm^2}.
$$
Consequently
$$
B_e=\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{\rm peak}E_0^2}{12\pi(\hbar c)^2}}
=\sqrt{\frac{(2.0\times10^{-7})(91.2)^2}{12\pi(0.1975)^2}},
\qquad
\boxed{B(Z^0\to e^+e^-)=0.03363\simeq3.4\%.}
$$
This estimate uses the supplied resonance model. The full physical elastic <electron>-<positron> cross section also contains nonresonant <photon> exchange and interference; an experimental use of the formula must isolate the resonant contribution.

= Solution

For <gamma decay>, the <nuclear gamma-ray multipole selection rules> require the radiation's <angular momentum> $L$ to satisfy the triangle inequality
$$
|J_i-J_f|\leq L\leq J_i+J_f.
$$
Here $J_i=J_f=5/2$, so $0\leq L\leq5$. A real emitted <photon> has no monopole radiation mode, leaving $L=1,2,3,4,5$.

The two nuclear <quantum states> have the same <parity>. An electric multipole has parity factor $(-1)^L$, while a magnetic multipole has factor $(-1)^{L+1}$. Thus electric radiation requires even $L$ and magnetic radiation odd $L$:
$$
\boxed{M1,\quad E2,\quad M3,\quad E4,\quad M5.}
$$
In particular, an $E0$ nuclear transition cannot emit the single real <photon> requested here; processes such as <internal conversion> are different decay channels.

For usual nuclear <gamma-ray> energies, $kR\ll1$, where $k=E_\gamma/(\hbar c)$ and $R$ is the nuclear size. Higher <multipole radiation> orders bring extra powers of this small parameter, so **$M1$ and $E2$ are the likely important contributions**, with $M3$, $E4$ and $M5$ generally much smaller. Often $M1$ is dominant, but an allowed $M1$ <matrix element> can be hindered and an $E2$ transition can be collectively enhanced. The <quantum numbers> alone do not fix a numerical relative contribution or an unconditional ordering of the $M1$ and $E2$ rates.

The four tree-level diagrams for <electron-positron annihilation into W-boson pairs> are
$$
\boxed{s\text{-channel }\gamma^*,\quad s\text{-channel }Z^*,\quad
 t\text{-channel }\nu_e,\quad s\text{-channel }h^*.}
$$

\Image[2018-ww-production-diagrams.png]
{title=The four tree-level Standard Model diagrams for electron-positron annihilation into W pairs}

The first two diagrams contain the <electromagnetic interaction> and <weak interaction> neutral electron currents and the <gauge boson self-interaction> vertices $\gamma W^+W^-$ and $ZW^+W^-$. The third has two <weak charged current> vertices joined by an <electron neutrino> propagator. In the fourth, the incoming <electron>-<positron> pair couples to the <Higgs boson>, which then couples to $W^+W^-$.

The small factor is at the initial Higgs-electron vertex. With the usual electroweak vacuum value $v\simeq246\,\mathrm{GeV}$, <electron Yukawa suppression of Higgs production> gives
$$
y_e=\frac{\sqrt2m_e}{v}\simeq2.9\times10^{-6},\qquad
 g_{hee}=\frac{m_e}{v}\simeq2.1\times10^{-6}.
$$
These are tiny compared with ordinary <electroweak interaction> gauge couplings. The <decay amplitude> for the <Higgs boson> diagram carries this small coefficient, and its separate squared contribution carries its square. Thus **the Higgs diagram is negligible because the electron Yukawa coupling is proportional to the electron mass**. It vanishes in the massless-electron approximation, even though the <Higgs boson>-<W boson> coupling itself is not small. The remaining three gauge diagrams must be added coherently, including their interference.

= Solution

**Smooth-model fission energy**. Use <natural units> $c=1$, so the quoted nuclear <masses> are expressed as rest <energies>. Conservation of <mass number> and <atomic number> fixes the second fragment to $(A_2,Z_2)=((1-y)A,(1-y)Z)$. First omit <nuclear pairing> to compare a continuous range of $y$. Pairing can subsequently add $\delta_1+\delta_2-\delta_{\rm parent}$ for integer fragments; it has no smooth parity assignment for arbitrary real $y$.

In the parent-minus-daughters mass difference, the <nucleon> <rest energy> and volume terms cancel. The <nuclear symmetry energy> also cancels because both fragments have the same $Z/A$:
$$
\frac{(A_1-2Z_1)^2}{A_1}+\frac{(A_2-2Z_2)^2}{A_2}
=[y+(1-y)]\frac{(A-2Z)^2}{A}.
$$
The remaining <liquid-drop fission energy> is
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
Q(y)={}&a_SA^{2/3}\left[1-y^{2/3}-(1-y)^{2/3}\right]\\
&+\frac{a_CZ^2}{A^{1/3}}\left[1-y^{5/3}-(1-y)^{5/3}\right].
\end{aligned}}
$$
Splitting increases the <surface energy> cost, but reduces <nuclear Coulomb energy>. Both contributions are symmetric under $y\leftrightarrow1-y$, so the symmetric split is stationary. Symmetry alone would not establish a maximum, since the two contributions compete.

For a full check, put $S=a_SA^{2/3}$, $x=a_CZ^2/(a_SA)$ and $t=y^{1/3}+(1-y)^{1/3}$. On $0\leq y\leq1/2$, $t$ increases from $1$ to $2^{2/3}$. Expressing the two fractional-power sums through $t$ gives
$$
\frac QS=1+x-\frac{t^2+2/t}{3}+\frac{x}{9}(t^5-5t^2-5/t),
\qquad
\frac{d(Q/S)}{dt}=\frac{t^3-1}{9t^2}\left[5x(t^3-1)-6\right].
$$
The derivative can change sign only from negative to positive, so any interior stationary point in $t$ is a minimum. The largest value is therefore either the no-split endpoint $Q=0$ or the symmetric split. Thus <symmetric fission maximizes the positive liquid-drop energy release>: whenever this smooth model permits an exothermic split, its largest release occurs at $y=1/2$.

There is a qualification to the paper's unqualified wording. For arbitrary low $Z^2/A$, the symmetric split need not maximize $Q$; indeed it is a local minimum when $Z^2/A<(2/5)a_S/a_C=10$. The symmetric-maximum conclusion is valid in the heavy, fission-favorable regime relevant here, rather than a universal property of the formula.

At $y=1/2$,
$$
Q_{\rm sym}=-a_SA^{2/3}(2^{1/3}-1)
+\frac{a_CZ^2}{A^{1/3}}(1-2^{-2/3}).
$$
Requiring $Q_{\rm sym}>0$ gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{Z^2}{A}>\frac{a_S}{a_C}
\frac{2^{1/3}-1}{1-2^{-2/3}}
=\frac{18.0}{0.72}\frac{0.259921}{0.370039}
\simeq17.6.}
$$

**Barrier and fragment yields**. Positive final-state <Q value> does not remove the <fission barrier> along a deformation path. <Surface energy> initially rises as the nucleus deforms; <spontaneous fission> then requires collective <quantum tunnelling> unless enough excitation is present. Its rate is exponentially sensitive to barrier height, width and collective inertia. Thus nuclei close to the exothermicity threshold can have immeasurably long spontaneous-fission <mean lifetimes>, while sufficiently heavier nuclei have smaller effective <fission barriers> or larger <quantum tunnelling> probabilities.

The <fission-fragment mass distribution> of low-excitation actinide fission is usually double peaked: complementary fragments have mass fractions roughly $0.4$ and $0.6$ rather than both exactly $1/2$. <Nuclear shell model> effects and the fission dynamics help favor asymmetric fragments. Maximizing the final smooth-model energy release is not a calculation of the fragment yield. The following yield curve is schematic, with no measured counts implied:

**Capture excitation energies**. Capturing a negligible-energy neutron forms a compound nucleus with the rest energy of the target plus the free <neutron>. Relative to the compound <ground state>, the <neutron-capture excitation energy> is therefore
$$
E^*=M(A,92)+m_n-M(A+1,92)
=B(A+1,92)-B(A,92)=S_n(A+1,92),
$$
where $B$ is positive <nuclear binding energy> and $S_n$ the compound nucleus's <neutron separation energy>. The free <neutron> <rest energy> cancels in the binding-energy difference. With the supplied <semi-empirical mass formula>,
$$
\begin{aligned}
S_n={}&a_V-a_S[(A+1)^{2/3}-A^{2/3}]\\
&-a_CZ^2[(A+1)^{-1/3}-A^{-1/3}]\\
&-a_A\left[\frac{(A+1-2Z)^2}{A+1}-\frac{(A-2Z)^2}{A}\right]
+\delta(A+1,Z)-\delta(A,Z).
\end{aligned}
$$
At $Z=92$, <uranium-235> has odd $A$, while <uranium-236> is even-even. The pairing contribution is $+33.5(236)^{-3/4}=+0.55637\,\mathrm{MeV}$. By contrast <uranium-238> is even-even and <uranium-239> has odd $A$, giving $-33.5(238)^{-3/4}=-0.55286\,\mathrm{MeV}$. The individual contributions are
$$
\begin{array}{c|rr}
\text{Contribution (MeV)}&{}^{235}\mathrm U+n&{}^{238}\mathrm U+n\\\hline
\text{Volume}&15.80000&15.80000\\
\text{Surface}&-1.94320&-1.93502\\
\text{Coulomb}&1.39680&1.37342\\
\text{Asymmetry}&-9.15424&-9.51287\\
\text{Pairing}&0.55637&-0.55286
\end{array}
$$
Thus the model predicts
$$
\boxed{E^*({}^{236}\mathrm U)=6.65573\,\mathrm{MeV}\simeq6.66\,\mathrm{MeV},\qquad
E^*({}^{239}\mathrm U)=5.17268\,\mathrm{MeV}\simeq5.17\,\mathrm{MeV}.}
$$
Of the $1.48305\,\mathrm{MeV}$ predicted difference, $1.10922\,\mathrm{MeV}$ comes from the change in <nuclear pairing>. It is the primary reason the even-even capture product is more strongly excited; the smooth terms account for the remaining $0.37383\,\mathrm{MeV}$.

**Thermal versus fast-neutron fission**. The last comparison uses the supplied observed capture excitations, $6.5$ and $4.8\,\mathrm{MeV}$, rather than replacing them by the preceding smooth-model estimates. Interpreting the quoted $6.2$ and $6.6\,\mathrm{MeV}$ activation energies as the barriers in the respective capture channels gives
$$
\boxed{6.5>6.2:\quad\text{thermal capture on }{}^{235}\mathrm U\text{ can lead to prompt fission},}
$$
$$
\boxed{4.8<6.6:\quad\text{thermal capture on }{}^{238}\mathrm U\text{ is below the fission barrier}.}
$$
A simple <neutron-induced fission threshold> estimate for the second channel requires about $6.6-4.8=1.8\,\mathrm{MeV}$ additional centre-of-mass kinetic energy. Sub-barrier tunnelling is possible, but it does not give the large thermal-neutron fission probability of the first channel.

The barrier labels in the original PDF need care: it assigns them to $^{235}\mathrm U$ and $^{238}\mathrm U$, whereas the states formed after capture are $^{236}\mathrm U^*$ and $^{239}\mathrm U^*$. The comparison above uses the intended capture-channel interpretation. If the quoted numbers are literally barriers of the uncaptured target nuclei, the compound-nucleus barriers are missing and this numerical comparison does not follow from those data alone.

In a thermal reactor, <neutron moderation> slows fission neutrons so the large low-energy fission cross section of <uranium-235> can support a <nuclear chain reaction>. Fuel composition, moderator choice and geometry must balance production against capture and leakage; capture resonances of <uranium-238> during slowing are important losses from that neutron population. The reaction rate per target nucleus depends on the spectrum through $\int\phi(E)\sigma_f(E)\,dE$, where $\phi(E)$ is differential <neutron flux> per unit <energy>. A fast spectrum can access the <uranium-238> fission channel and changes this balance. Also, <uranium-238> is a <fertile material>: capture followed by two <beta-minus decays> can produce <plutonium-239>, a <fissile material>, so capture can contribute to breeding even when it causes no immediate fission.

= Solution

**Colour and identical-quark spin**. A spatial ground-state <baryon> has a symmetric $\ell=0$ spatial <wavefunction>. For $uuu$, $ddd$ or $sss$, the <quark flavour> state is also symmetric, and the total-<spin> $3/2$ state of three spin-one-half <quarks> is symmetric. Without another degree of freedom, the whole state would be symmetric under exchange of identical <fermions>, contrary to the <Pauli exclusion principle>.

A <three-quark colour singlet> supplies a completely antisymmetric factor,
$$
|1_c\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt6}\epsilon_{abc}|abc\rangle,
$$
using the three <color charge> states. The remaining spatial-spin-flavour factor can then be symmetric. These baryons therefore provide evidence for an additional internal <degree of freedom> with at least three states, realized as colour in <quantum chromodynamics>.

For the $uu$ pair in $uus$, the spatial and <quark flavour> factors are symmetric under exchange of the two up quarks, while the colour factor is antisymmetric. The pair's <spin> state must consequently be symmetric. The <identical-quark pair spin in a ground-state baryon> is
$$
\boxed{S_{uu}=1,}
$$
a <triplet state>. This holds whether the complete baryon has total <spin> $1/2$ or $3/2$.

**Origin of the pair interaction**. The terms in the <baryon hyperfine mass formula> are effective colour-magnetic <spin-spin interactions>, arising from gluon exchange between the quarks' colour-magnetic moments. Each magnetic moment brings an inverse <constituent quark mass>, explaining the factor $1/(m_im_j)$. The constant $A'$ absorbs the <strong coupling constant>, colour factors and the spatial overlap. A common $A'$ is a constituent-model approximation.

**Spin algebra and the two masses**. Set $\hbar=c=1$, so each dimensionless <spin angular momentum> operator satisfies $\mathbf S_i^2=3/4$. Label the two up quarks 1 and 2 and the strange quark 3. Squaring $\mathbf S_{12}=\mathbf S_1+\mathbf S_2$ gives
$$
\langle\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2\rangle
=\frac12\left[S_{12}(S_{12}+1)-\frac34-\frac34\right]
=\frac14.
$$
Next square the total <spin> $\mathbf J=\mathbf S_{12}+\mathbf S_3$:
$$
\langle\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_3+\mathbf S_2\cdot\mathbf S_3\rangle
=\frac12\left[J(J+1)-2-\frac34\right]
=\begin{cases}-1,&J=1/2,\\1/2,&J=3/2.\end{cases}
$$
Substituting these pair sums into the <baryon hyperfine mass formula> yields
$$
\boxed{M_{uus,1/2}=2m_u+m_s+\frac{A'}4
\left(\frac1{m_u^2}-\frac4{m_um_s}\right),}
$$
$$
\boxed{M_{uus,3/2}=2m_u+m_s+\frac{A'}4
\left(\frac1{m_u^2}+\frac2{m_um_s}\right).}
$$
Their difference is $3A'/(2m_um_s)$. Repeating the argument for the identical $ss$ pair in $uss$ gives
$$
M_{uss,1/2}=m_u+2m_s+\frac{A'}4\left(\frac1{m_s^2}-\frac4{m_um_s}\right),\qquad
M_{uss,3/2}=m_u+2m_s+\frac{A'}4\left(\frac1{m_s^2}+\frac2{m_um_s}\right).
$$
Thus the model predicts <equal Sigma and Xi hyperfine splittings>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{M(\Sigma^*)-M(\Sigma)}{M(\Xi^*)-M(\Xi)}=1.}
$$
For a like-flavour comparison using exactly the supplied masses, the $uus$ splitting is $1383-1189=194\,\mathrm{MeV}$ and the $uss$ splitting is $1532-1315=217\,\mathrm{MeV}$, giving $194/217=0.894$. Averaging within the supplied <isospin multiplets> instead gives $192/215=0.893$. The predicted equality captures the common scale but misses the observed ratio by about $11\%$ in this table. Different spatial overlaps, and hence different effective $A'$ values, can account for departures from the common-coefficient approximation.

**Radiative decay and recoil**. In a <radiative Sigma-to-Lambda transition>, both baryons retain valence content $uds$. A <photon> couples to an electrically charged constituent while the final quarks are projected onto the lower <baryon>'s spin-flavour <wavefunction>. The leading up- and down-quark diagrams are shown below; their contributions must be combined coherently. In the simplest model, the strange-quark spin operator alone cannot change the $ud$ pair from its Sigma triplet to the Lambda singlet. The transition is an $M1$ <multipole radiation> transition, consistent with $1/2^+\to1/2^+$.

\Image[2018-baryon-decay-diagrams.png]
{title=Quark diagrams for Sigma-to-Lambda photon emission and an example strong Delta decay}

For the photon energy, let $M_\Sigma=1193\,\mathrm{MeV}$ and $M_\Lambda=1116\,\mathrm{MeV}$ in <natural units>. <Four-momentum conservation> gives equal opposite final momenta of magnitude $E_\gamma$, so
$$
M_\Sigma=E_\gamma+\sqrt{M_\Lambda^2+E_\gamma^2},
\qquad
\boxed{E_\gamma=\frac{M_\Sigma^2-M_\Lambda^2}{2M_\Sigma}
=74.5151\,\mathrm{MeV}\simeq74.5\,\mathrm{MeV}.}
$$
The recoil <kinetic energy> of the <Lambda baryon> is $77-74.5151=2.4849\,\mathrm{MeV}$; assigning the entire mass difference to the <photon> would miss it.

**Which baryons decay strongly?** The <open strong-decay thresholds for baryons> require both sufficient energy and conserved <quark flavour>, including <strangeness>. The dominant strong decayers in the supplied table are
$$
\boxed{\Delta^-,\Delta^0,\Delta^+,\Delta^{++};\qquad
\Sigma^{*-},\Sigma^{*0},\Sigma^{*+};\qquad\Xi^{*-},\Xi^{*0}.}
$$
The <Delta baryon> channels $\Delta\to N\pi$ have threshold near $940+140=1080\,\mathrm{MeV}$, below $1232\,\mathrm{MeV}$. The excited <Sigma baryons> can decay as $\Sigma^*\to\Lambda\pi$ or $\Sigma\pi$, and the excited <Xi baryons> as $\Xi^*\to\Xi\pi$, all with open thresholds and conserved <strangeness>. Their positive-parity baryon states can emit the negative-parity <pion> in an odd relative <orbital angular momentum>, with $L=1$ already allowing total $J=3/2$.

The last diagram illustrates $\Delta^{++}(uuu)\to p(uud)+\pi^+(u\bar d)$: a <gluon> creates a $d\bar d$ pair and the quarks regroup into the two hadrons. No quark changes flavour at the gluon vertices. This is a representative strong pair-creation topology; quantitative hadronic decay rates require bound-state dynamics.

The <Omega baryon> is an important exception among the spin-$3/2$ states. Its $S=-3$ cannot become the $S=-2$ of $\Xi\pi$ through the <strong interaction>. Its flavour-conserving $\Xi K$ threshold is at least $1315+500=1815\,\mathrm{MeV}$, above its $1673\,\mathrm{MeV}$ mass, so it decays weakly. The ground-state strange octet also has no open strong channel: for example $\Sigma\to\Lambda\pi$ needs at least $1256\,\mathrm{MeV}$, and $\Lambda\to NK$ or $\Xi\to\Lambda K$ needs still more than the relevant initial mass. The neutral Sigma decays electromagnetically as above; the neutron and the other unstable octet members decay weakly, while the proton is stable within these processes.

= Solution

**The nuclear determination of $|V_{ud}|$**. A $0^+\to0^+$ <beta decay transition> has no nuclear <parity> change and can proceed with zero lepton <orbital angular momentum>. The allowed scalar operator gives a <Fermi transition>; the rank-one <Gamow-Teller transition> operator cannot connect total nuclear spins zero to zero. The strong isobaric-analog transitions used for precision measurements are <superallowed beta decays>. A generic $0^+\to0^+$ assignment alone would not guarantee superallowed strength if the states were nonanalog.

At the <quark> level the <weak charged current> converts $d\leftrightarrow u$ and carries $V_{ud}$ in the amplitude. The nuclear Fermi operator is an <isospin> ladder operator, giving
$$
\mathcal M\propto G_FV_{ud}M_F,\qquad
\boxed{\Gamma\propto G_F^2|V_{ud}|^2|M_F|^2f.}
$$
Here $f$ denotes the known <phase space> dependence, with the dimensional factors included in the proportionality constant. In the isospin-symmetric limit, $M_F$ between analog states is fixed by isospin algebra, for example $|M_F|^2=2$ for the usual $T=1$ analog transitions. A measured partial <half-life> and the computed <phase space> factor give the <comparative half-life>; after <radiative corrections> and <isospin> breaking corrections, its inverse determines $|V_{ud}|^2$. The decay rate measures a magnitude squared, not the complex phase of $V_{ud}$.

**Ds decay diagrams and CKM comparisons**. The original PDF has $K^+\bar K^0$, with $\bar K^0=s\bar d$. The local TeX drops that overbar; using $K^0=d\bar s$ instead would give a different flavour channel. Use the PDF's <neutral antikaon> throughout.

In the representative <Ds nonleptonic tree amplitudes>, the <Ds meson> spectator is $\bar s$. The elementary weak transitions are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{Channel}&\text{Quark transition}&\text{CKM magnitude}\\\hline
K^+\pi^0&c\to dW^+,\quad W^+\to u\bar d&|V_{cd}V_{ud}|\\
K^+\bar K^0&c\to sW^+,\quad W^+\to u\bar d&|V_{cs}V_{ud}|\\
\phi\pi^+&c\to sW^+,\quad W^+\to u\bar d&|V_{cs}V_{ud}|
\end{array}
$$

\Image[2018-ds-nonleptonic-diagrams.png]
{title=The three Ds spectator tree diagrams, using the neutral-antikaon channel in the original PDF}

In the first two channels, the produced $u$ joins the spectator $\bar s$ to form $K^+$; the other quark joins $\bar d$. For the first channel this uses the $-d\bar d/\sqrt2$ component of the <neutral pion>. In the third, $s\bar s$ forms the <phi meson> and $u\bar d$ forms $\pi^+$. The two possible final pairings have different colour contractions and hadronic <matrix elements> even when their weak CKM factors coincide.

The purely CKM rate suppression of $c\to d$ relative to $c\to s$ is
$$
\boxed{\left|\frac{V_{cd}}{V_{cs}}\right|^2
=\left(\frac{0.225}{0.974}\right)^2=0.0533638.}
$$
Thus $K^+\pi^0$ is singly <Cabibbo suppressed>, while $K^+\bar K^0$ and $\phi\pi^+$ are Cabibbo-favored at this level. If reduced hadronic strengths and <phase space> effects were equal, including the neutral-pion component would give
$$
\left[\frac{B(K^+\pi^0)}{B(K^+\bar K^0)}\right]_{\rm simple}
=\frac12\left|\frac{V_{cd}}{V_{cs}}\right|^2=0.0266819.
$$
The supplied <branching fractions> instead give
$$
\frac{B(K^+\pi^0)}{B(K^+\bar K^0)}=\frac{6.3\times10^{-4}}{2.9\times10^{-2}}=0.0217241,
\qquad
\frac{B(K^+\pi^0)}{B(\phi\pi^+)}=0.0140,
$$
$$
\frac{B(K^+\bar K^0)}{B(\phi\pi^+)}=0.64444.
$$
The first observed ratio is $0.814$ times the neutral-pion-corrected simple estimate, requiring only an order-one remaining hadronic and <phase space> factor. Relative to $\phi\pi^+$, that remaining factor is $0.525$. The two Cabibbo-favored channels differ by a factor $0.644$ despite equal CKM magnitudes. The CKM hierarchy therefore explains why one channel is much rarer, but it does not calculate the three exclusive <branching fractions>; <spin>, <phase space>, <color charge> and <strong interaction> final-state effects also matter.

**What spin and parity establish**. In either pseudoscalar-pseudoscalar final state, the total intrinsic <spin> is zero. Conservation of <angular momentum> for a spin-zero parent forces $L=0$, so the final <parity> is
$$
P_f=(-1)(-1)(-1)^0=+1,
$$
opposite to the parent $0^-$ <Ds meson>. Consequently
$$
\boxed{D_s^+\to K^+\pi^0,\ K^+\bar K^0\text{ require parity violation and are weak decays}.}
$$
For a <vector meson> plus a <pion>, the intrinsic final <spin> is one. Coupling to total $J=0$ requires $L=1$, giving $P_f=(-1)(-1)(-1)^1=-1$, equal to the parent parity. Thus **spin and parity alone permit a parity-conserving $\phi\pi^+$ transition**. That is the limited sense in which it is not necessarily weak from this information alone. In reality it is also weak: the initial charm flavour disappears, which the <strong interaction> and <electromagnetic interaction> conserve.

**W branching fractions**. In the massless-daughter, tree-level estimate, each leptonic channel contributes one common unit of <decay width>. Each accessible quark pair contributes $3|V_{ij}|^2$ because of the three final colours. The <charged-current W branching-fraction counting> denominator is
$$
D=3+3\sum_{i=u,c}\sum_{j=d,s,b}|V_{ij}|^2
=9.000897\simeq9.
$$
Only $u$ and $c$ are accessible up-type quarks; the large $V_{tb}$ is irrelevant because a <W boson> cannot decay into the heavier <top quark>. Hence
$$
\boxed{B(W^-\to e^-\bar\nu_e)=\frac1D\simeq\frac19=11.1\%.}
$$
A final <bottom quark> arises in $W^-\to b\bar u$ or $b\bar c$, so
$$
\boxed{B(W^-\text{ containing }b)=\frac{3(|V_{ub}|^2+|V_{cb}|^2)}D
=\frac{3(0.004^2+0.041^2)}{9.000897}
=5.66\times10^{-4}\simeq0.057\%.}
$$
This counts the primary decay channels; finite daughter masses and <QCD> corrections refine the approximation.

**The requested top-lifetime estimate and its limitation**. Under the instructed equal-coefficient <Sargent's rule> scaling, $\Gamma\propto m^5$ and the <mean lifetime> is inversely proportional to that fifth power. Converting both masses to GeV gives
$$
\boxed{\tau_t^{(m^5)}=\tau_\mu\left(\frac{m_\mu}{m_t}\right)^5
=2.2\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm s\left(\frac{0.106}{175}\right)^5
=1.79\times10^{-22}\,\mathrm s.}
$$
This is the requested order-of-magnitude estimate, assuming the same effective coefficient for the two total decay rates. It corresponds to $c\tau\simeq54\,\mathrm{fm}$ and is longer than the <hadronization time>, roughly a few $10^{-24}\,\mathrm s$. Taken literally, it would allow top-flavored hadrons to form; it does not establish the physical statement that the top decays before hadronizing.

The extrapolation fails because <Sargent's rule> uses a low-energy <Fermi interaction> approximation, whereas $m_t>m_W+m_b$ permits $t\to bW$ with an on-shell <W boson>. The physical leading <decay width> instead scales as
$$
\Gamma(t\to bW)\simeq\frac{G_Fm_t^3|V_{tb}|^2}{8\pi\sqrt2}
(1-x)^2(1+2x),\qquad x=\frac{m_W^2}{m_t^2}.
$$
Using $m_W\simeq80.4\,\mathrm{GeV}$, the <Fermi constant> $G_F=1.16638\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{GeV^{-2}}$ and $|V_{tb}|=0.999$ gives about $1.55\,\mathrm{GeV}$ at the supplied top mass. Then $\tau_t=\hbar/\Gamma_t$, with $\hbar=6.58212\times10^{-25}\,\mathrm{GeV\,s}$, gives $\tau_t\simeq4.2\times10^{-25}\,\mathrm s$. This corrected regime explains <top-quark decay before hadronization>.<Review of Particle Physics by Particle Data Group (2022)>[Top Quark review, section 61.2, equation 61.1 and comparison of lifetime with hadron formation]{ref}
