= Chemical reaction
{wiki}

A <chemical reaction> interconverts <chemical species>, as defined by the https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/C01033[IUPAC Gold Book, chemical reaction]{ref}. The consumed species are <reactants>; the formed species are <reaction products>. In ordinary <chemical reactions>, <chemical atoms> are rearranged while each <chemical element>'s total atom count is conserved. For a complete <chemical equation>, net <electric charge> is conserved too.

For example, the balanced <chemical equation>
$$
2\mathrm{H}_2+\mathrm{O}_2\longrightarrow2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}
$$
describes conversion of <molecular hydrogen> and <molecular oxygen> into <water>. Both sides contain four <hydrogen> <chemical atoms> and two <oxygen> <chemical atoms>. Three <reactant> <molecules> become two <reaction product> <molecules>: atom counts are conserved, but molecule counts need not be. See https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/4-1-writing-and-balancing-chemical-equations[Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, balancing chemical equations].

The coefficients describe net consumption and formation, without asserting that three <molecules> collide in one step. A balanced <chemical equation> supplies quantitative constraints; it does not determine how quickly the <chemical reaction> occurs, or whether <chemical equilibrium> prevents complete conversion. Those questions need further information. Keeping these roles distinct makes <stoichiometry> useful even when the microscopic details are complicated.

= Hydration reaction
{parent=Chemical reaction}

Addition of water to a molecular species. A carbonyl compound can form a geminal diol by hydration, distinct from a solvent hydration shell.

= Salt metathesis
{parent=Chemical reaction}

Exchange of ionic partners, often used to replace a <metal> halide by an <anionic ligand> while an alkali-metal halide forms. Atom and charge balance distinguish it from an oxidative-addition reaction.

= Pericyclic reaction
{parent=Chemical reaction}

A concerted reorganization of bonding through a cyclic orbital interaction, including electrocyclic reactions. The participating orbitals and their electronic occupation determine the relevant symmetry selection rule.

= Suprafacial reaction
{parent=Pericyclic reaction}

A reaction using the same face of a participating orbital array at both reacting termini.

= Sigmatropic rearrangement
{parent=Pericyclic reaction}

A <pericyclic reaction> that relocates a sigma bond while reorganizing an adjacent pi system.

= Cycloreversion
{parent=Pericyclic reaction}

The reverse of a <cycloaddition>, splitting a ring into unsaturated fragments.

= Retro hetero-Diels-Alder reaction
{parent=Cycloreversion}

A <retro hetero-Diels-Alder reaction> is the reverse of a <Diels-Alder reaction> involving a heteroatom-containing partner. For a six-membered ring with sequence $O-A-B=C-D-E$, cleavage of $O-A$ and $D-E$ can give $A=B-C=D$ and $E=O$. Whether the <cycloreversion> is concerted or proceeds through ionic intermediates depends on the substrate and conditions.

= Woodward-Hoffmann rules
{c}
{parent=Pericyclic reaction}

Orbital-symmetry rules associated with <Robert Burns Woodward> and <Roald Hoffmann> for concerted reactions. Ground-state and photochemically excited occupations can have different allowed stereochemical modes; an allowed mode does not determine every kinetic detail or guarantee a large reaction rate.

= Electrocyclic reaction
{parent=Pericyclic reaction}

Interconversion of a conjugated pi system and a ring by formation or cleavage of one sigma bond at the pi-system termini, accompanied by pi-bond redistribution. Terminal rotation connects starting geometry with product stereochemistry.

= Electrocyclic ring closure
{parent=Electrocyclic reaction}

Formation of a ring by concerted conversion of terminal pi-system overlap into a new sigma bond. Thermal four-pi closure is conrotatory, subject to the appropriate electronic-state assumptions.

= Anionic eight-pi electrocyclization
{parent=Electrocyclic reaction}

Ring closure of a seven-carbon conjugated anion with eight pi <electrons>; thermal orbital-symmetry selection favors disrotatory motion.

= Electrocyclic selection rule
{parent=Electrocyclic reaction}

For ordinary closed-shell systems, a thermal $4n$-electron electrocyclic process is conrotatory and a thermal $4n+2$-electron process is disrotatory. The corresponding simple photochemical rules are reversed. These rules relate terminal orbital phases and occupation to allowed concerted bond formation or cleavage.

= Disrotatory motion
{parent=Electrocyclic reaction}

= Disrotatory
{synonym}

Terminal rotation in opposite senses under a common viewing convention during an <electrocyclic reaction>.

= Conrotatory motion
{parent=Electrocyclic reaction}

= Conrotatory
{synonym}

Terminal rotation in the same rotational sense under a common viewing convention during an <electrocyclic reaction>. It is distinguished from <disrotatory motion>; looking from opposite ends without changing conventions can reverse an apparent clockwise label.

= Cyclopropanation
{parent=Chemical reaction}

Formation of a three-membered carbon ring, for example by transferring a carbene-like carbon across an alkene.

= Intramolecular cyclopropanation
{parent=Cyclopropanation}

Cyclopropanation between reactive groups tethered within one molecular framework. The tether constrains approach geometry, can form an additional ring simultaneously, and can influence which alkene face reacts.

= Rearrangement reaction
{parent=Chemical reaction}

= Dienone-phenol rearrangement
{parent=Rearrangement reaction}

An acid-promoted skeletal migration of a cyclohexadienone followed by rearomatization to a phenol. Carbonyl protonation activates the conjugated system; migration from a suitably placed quaternary carbon and subsequent proton loss can change ring connectivity while restoring aromaticity.

= Dearomatizing spirocyclization of a phenoxide
{parent=Dienone-phenol rearrangement}

A para-tethered <phenoxide> can react through its carbon-nucleophilic resonance contributor. Intramolecular displacement of a tether leaving group at the para carbon creates a spiro ring and a cyclohexadienone. Subsequent acid-induced bond migration to a neighbouring carbon can expand the attached ring and restore the phenol. The two-stage sequence can therefore give a final substitution position different from the initial carbon attack.

= Hydrosilylation
{parent=Chemical reaction}

Addition of Si–H across an unsaturated bond. For an <alkene>, the net products acquire one C–H and one C–Si bond. Alternative catalytic mechanisms can insert the <alkene> into either a <metal>–<hydride> or a <metal>–silyl bond; these alternatives should not be conflated.

= Chalk-Harrod mechanism
{parent=Hydrosilylation}
{c}

A <hydrosilylation> cycle involving Si–H <oxidative addition>, <alkene> insertion into the <metal>–<hydride> bond, and C–Si <reductive elimination>. This defines the conventional version; insertion into a <metal>–silyl bond describes a modified alternative.

= Isomerization
{parent=Chemical reaction}

Interconversion of <isomers> without changing the overall molecular formula. Conformational interconversion can be kinetically slow when rotation is hindered.

= Tautomerization
{parent=Chemical reaction}

Interconversion of constitutional forms by <proton transfer> accompanied by redistribution of pi bonds. Keto–<enol> interconversion is a familiar example.

= Selectivity
{parent=Chemical reaction}

Preferential formation of one product or reaction at one site. <chemoselectivity>, <regioselectivity> and stereoselectivity describe different comparisons.

= Chemoselectivity
{parent=Selectivity}

Preferential reaction of one functional group or bond type in a <molecule> containing alternatives. It depends on reagents, conditions and reaction time rather than being an absolute property of a functional group.

= Decarboxylation
{parent=Chemical reaction}

Loss of carbon dioxide from a compound. Malonic-acid derivatives can decarboxylate through cyclic proton-transfer/bond-cleavage arrangements that generate a carbonyl-stabilized product.

= Malonic acid decarboxylation
{parent=Decarboxylation}

Heating a substituted malonic acid can release carbon dioxide through a six-membered cyclic transition arrangement. The initial enol-like carboxylic-acid product tautomerizes to a substituted acetic acid.

= Cycloaddition
{parent=Chemical reaction}

A reaction that joins unsaturated components while forming a ring and new sigma bonds. Its transition-state entropy and activation volume depend on both molecular reorganization and the surrounding medium.

= Diels-Alder reaction
{parent=Cycloaddition}
{c}

A concerted cycloaddition of a conjugated <diene> and a <dienophile> that makes a six-membered ring with two new <sigma bonds> and one retained alkene. The internal <diene> atoms become the residual alkene atoms.

= Diels-Alder endo preference
{parent=Diels-Alder reaction}
{c}

= Endo rule
{synonym}

A common kinetic preference for an orientation with dienophile substituents directed beneath the diene pi system. Steric and orbital interactions compete, so endo preference is not an absolute rule.

= Ethylene equivalent
{parent=Diels-Alder reaction}

A reagent combination that installs the two saturated carbons of an ethylene cycloaddition while allowing more favourable reactivity. A removable electron-withdrawing substituent can activate the <dienophile> and be removed later.

= Endo selectivity
{parent=Diels-Alder reaction}

A preference for the cycloaddition orientation in which relevant <dienophile> substituents lie under the <diene> framework in the transition geometry. It is distinct from regiochemistry and is not an unconditional guarantee of one product.

= Dienophile
{parent=Diels-Alder reaction}

The unsaturated partner that combines with a conjugated <diene> in a Diels–Alder reaction. Electron-withdrawing substituents often enhance reactivity in a normal-electron-demand process.

= Mesylation
{parent=Chemical reaction}

<Mesylation> installs a methanesulfonyl group on a heteroatom, often alcohol <oxygen>. An alcohol attacks electrophilic <sulfur> of a sulfonylating reagent, and base removes the O–H <proton>.

= Hydroalumination
{parent=Chemical reaction}

<Hydroalumination> adds an Al–H equivalent across an unsaturated bond. Syn and directed trans pathways give different alkenyl-aluminium geometries; protonolysis can preserve that stereochemical information.

= Directed trans-hydroalumination
{parent=Hydroalumination}

Oxygen-directed anti addition of <aluminium> and <hydrogen> across an <alkyne>, giving a stereodefined vinylaluminium species.

= Dihydroxylation
{parent=Chemical reaction}

<Dihydroxylation> installs hydroxyl groups on adjacent <alkene> carbons. <osmium tetroxide> gives a <syn addition> pathway through a <cyclic osmate ester>.

= Cyclic osmate ester
{parent=Dihydroxylation}

A <cyclic osmate ester> contains the newly formed <carbon>–<oxygen> bonds of an osmium-mediated <alkene> <dihydroxylation>. Both <oxygen> atoms attach on the same face; hydrolytic cleavage of metal–<oxygen> bonds releases the diol.

= Transacetalization
{parent=Chemical reaction}

<Transacetalization> exchanges acetal alkoxy groups through acid-catalyzed cleavage and reformation. Protonated alkoxy departure gives an oxocarbenium species that a new alcohol can capture.

= Syn addition
{parent=Chemical reaction}

A <syn addition> attaches both new groups from the same face of an unsaturated unit. Formation of a <cyclic osmate ester> explains syn <dihydroxylation>.

= Anti addition
{parent=Chemical reaction}

An <anti addition> places the two newly attached groups on opposite faces of an unsaturated unit. Backside opening of a cyclic <bromonium ion> is a standard example.

= Desilylation
{parent=Chemical reaction}

<Desilylation> removes a silyl group. Attack by a <nucleophile> at <silicon> can pass through a pentacoordinate <silicon> arrangement before Si–O cleavage; the liberated <oxygen> may be trapped by a new <electrophile>.

= Oxidative addition
{parent=Chemical reaction}

In a standard <oxidative addition>, a bond X–Y is cleaved and both fragments become <metal> ligands, increasing formal <metal> <oxidation state> by two. A B–Br bond can give a <metal>–boryl bond and a <bromide ligand> without cleaving a <borazine> ring.

= Oxidative hydrogen migration
{parent=Oxidative addition}

A metal-assisted pathway in which cleavage of a substrate C-H bond transfers H to an electrophilic ligand while new metal-carbon bonding forms. In directed Fischer-carbene insertion, it can precede carbon-carbon <reductive elimination>.

= Beta-hydride elimination
{parent=Chemical reaction}

<Beta-hydride elimination> transfers hydrogen from the beta <carbon> of a <metal>-bound alkyl to the <metal> while forming an <alkene>. It can release a growing <polymer> chain and compete with <migratory insertion>. Its accessibility depends on geometry, available sites and bond energetics.

= Migratory insertion
{parent=Chemical reaction}

In <migratory insertion>, adjacent ligands combine while remaining attached to the <metal> in the product. In <alkene> insertion into M–R, R forms a bond to one <alkene> <carbon> while the other <carbon> acquires the M–C bond. <metal> <oxidation state> need not change.

= Carbopalladation
{parent=Migratory insertion}

Addition of a carbon–palladium bond across an unsaturated bond.

= Methyl abstraction
{parent=Chemical reaction}

<Methyl abstraction> transfers a <metal>-bound methyl group to an <electrophile>. A trityl cation can convert a neutral metallocene dimethyl into a cationic monomethyl, forming $\mathrm{Ph_3CMe}$ and opening a site for an <alkene ligand>.

= Hydroboration
{parent=Chemical reaction}

Addition of a B–H bond across an unsaturated bond. For many ordinary alkenes, concerted syn addition places boron mainly on the less substituted carbon; subsequent oxidation can replace C–B by C–O with retention.

= Condensation reaction
{parent=Chemical reaction}

= Hydride transfer
{parent=Chemical reaction}

A <hydride transfer> moves hydrogen with its bonding <electron> pair from donor to acceptor. Reduced nicotinamide cofactors transfer hydride from their ring C4; this is not transfer of a hydrogen radical.

= Conjugate addition
{parent=Chemical reaction}

<Conjugate addition> attacks a beta position in a conjugated electrophile and redistributes pi bonding, often generating an <enolate>. <proton> transfer then supplies a saturated carbonyl product.

= Nucleophile-catalyzed alpha functionalization of an enone
{parent=Conjugate addition}

A <nucleophile> adds reversibly at the beta <carbon> to form an <enolate>, which is functionalized before elimination restores the <alkene>.

= 1,6-Conjugate addition
{parent=Conjugate addition}

A <1,6-conjugate addition> attacks the delta <carbon> of an extended conjugated <electrophile>. The resulting extended <enolate> can be protonated at different sites; <proton> transfer or equilibration can favor a product retaining <carbonyl>–<alkene> conjugation.

= Conjugate reduction
{parent=Chemical reaction}

A <conjugate reduction> adds a reducing equivalent at the beta position of a conjugated carbonyl system. Hydride delivery followed by protonation can reduce C=C while retaining C=O, unlike direct carbonyl-to-alcohol reduction.

= Keto-enol tautomerism
{parent=Chemical reaction}

<Keto-enol tautomerism> interconverts carbonyl and <enol> forms by <proton> transfer and pi-bond redistribution. Solvent-mediated relays avoid an unrealistic isolated intramolecular <proton> jump.

= Reductive elimination
{parent=Chemical reaction}

In a standard two-ligand <reductive elimination>, two metal-bound anionic fragments couple to form a bond while metal <oxidation state> decreases by two. The reacting ligands commonly need adjacent positions; favorable bond formation and metal reduction energetics determine whether the formal step actually occurs.

= Dissociative substitution
{parent=Chemical reaction}

A <dissociative substitution> loses a ligand before binding the incoming replacement. A saturated 18-electron carbonyl can first form a 16-electron intermediate; the dissociation barrier and intermediate rearrangement influence rates and product geometry.

= Cis effect
{parent=Dissociative substitution}

The carbonyl <cis effect> is preferential labilization of a ligand cis to another ligand in many six-coordinate metal carbonyls. Site preference in five-coordinate intermediates and transition states matters. Isotopic exchange can distinguish departure sites more directly than substitution-product geometry alone.

= Electrophilicity
{parent=Chemical reaction}

= Electrophilic
{synonym}

The susceptibility of a species or atomic site to accepting an electron pair from a <nucleophile>. It depends on structure, charge and reaction conditions.

= Electrophile
{parent=Chemical reaction}
{wiki}

An <electrophile> accepts an electron pair in forming a bond. Reaction selectivity depends on both the electrophile and its interacting <nucleophile>.

= Organic reaction
{parent=Chemical reaction}

= Arbuzov reaction
{parent=Organic reaction}
{c}

= Michaelis-Arbuzov reaction
{c}
{synonym}

Reaction of a trialkyl phosphite with an alkyl halide to give a phosphonate and an alkyl halide byproduct. With a haloacetate it prepares a phosphonoacetate used in the <Horner-Wadsworth-Emmons reaction>.

= Baeyer-Villiger oxidation
{parent=Organic reaction}
{c}

Insertion of <oxygen> beside an <aldehyde> or <ketone> <carbonyl>, commonly with a <peroxy acid>. Cyclic <ketones> give ring-expanded <lactones>. The migrating group retains its configuration, but competing migrations can give different constitutional products.

= C-glycosylation
{parent=Organic reaction}
{c}

Formation of a <carbon>–<carbon> bond at an <anomeric carbon>. In <electrophilic aromatic substitution> on a suitable heteroarene, <carbon> attack on an activated glycosyl donor is followed by <deprotonation> to restore <aromaticity>.

= Bromoetherification
{parent=Organic reaction}

Addition of bromine and an <oxygen> <nucleophile> across an <alkene> to form a bromoether. Intramolecular attack on a <bromonium ion> can close a ring; tether length, charge distribution and anti opening determine the resulting connectivity.

= Selenoxide elimination
{parent=Organic reaction}

A syn elimination in which <oxidation> of an organoselenide permits intramolecular beta-hydrogen removal and C–Se cleavage, producing an <alkene>.

= 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition
{parent=Organic reaction}

A <cycloaddition> joining a three-atom 1,3-dipole to a two-atom unsaturated unit and forming a five-membered ring. The atom order of the dipole fixes which heteroatoms become adjacent.

= Mitsunobu reaction
{parent=Organic reaction}
{c}

Substitution of an <alcohol> using a phosphine and an azodicarboxylate reagent with a suitable acidic <nucleophile>. Carboxylate displacement normally inverts a stereogenic <alcohol> <carbon>; subsequent <ester> <hydrolysis> can unveil the inverted <alcohol>.

= Manganese(III)-mediated radical cyclization
{parent=Organic reaction}

<oxidation> of an <enol> or <enolate> can generate a carbon-centred <radical> that adds intramolecularly to an <alkene>. Further <oxidation> or <oxygen> capture determines the final functional group.

= Radical-polar crossover
{parent=Organic reaction}

A sequence in which <electron> transfer converts a <radical> intermediate into a cationic or anionic intermediate, enabling subsequent polar chemistry.

= Iodoetherification
{parent=Organic reaction}

Intramolecular capture of an alkene-derived iodonium <ion> by an <alcohol> produces a cyclic <ether> and a <carbon>–iodine bond. Backside opening gives an anti relationship between the new C–O and C–I bonds.

= Alpha-ketol rearrangement
{parent=Organic reaction}

= Acyloin rearrangement
{synonym}

A 1,2-carbon migration interconverts adjacent tertiary alpha-hydroxy <carbonyl compounds>. Formation of a new <carbonyl> at the original carbinol <carbon> accompanies conversion of the original <carbonyl> into an <alcohol>. Unlike dehydration, the net process preserves both <oxygens>.

= Kornblum oxidation
{parent=Organic reaction}
{c}

<oxidation> of an alkyl halide or a related electrophile by <dimethyl sulfoxide>. An alkoxysulfonium intermediate undergoes base-promoted elimination to a <carbonyl compound> and dimethyl sulfide.

= Nitrone cycloaddition
{parent=Organic reaction}

A <1,3-dipolar cycloaddition> joining a <nitrone> to an <alkene>. Intramolecular tethering controls regioselectivity and relative approach; the <nitrone> <nitrogen>–<oxygen> bond can later be cleaved reductively.

= Aziridinium-mediated ring expansion
{parent=Organic reaction}

A neighbouring <tertiary amine> displaces a leaving group to form a three-membered ammonium ring; attack at the original ring <carbon> cleaves its old C–N bond and incorporates the side-chain <carbon> into an expanded <nitrogen> ring.

= Hydroacylation
{parent=Organic reaction}

Addition of an aldehydic C–H bond across an unsaturated bond, creating a <ketone>. Intramolecular <alkene> <hydroacylation> commonly constructs a five-membered ring via an acyl–metal <hydride>, <alkene> insertion and <reductive elimination>.

= Palladium-catalysed hydrodehalogenation
{parent=Organic reaction}

Replacement of an organic halide by <hydrogen> through a <palladium> catalytic cycle. <oxidative addition>, <hydride> delivery and <reductive elimination> can preserve the <stereochemistry> of an alkenyl fragment.

= Hydrozirconation
{parent=Organic reaction}

Addition of a <zirconium>–<hydrogen bond> across an <alkene> or <alkyne>. <Schwartz reagent> commonly gives a syn addition and places <zirconium> at the less substituted terminus; reversible insertion can influence regioselectivity.

= Dearomatization
{parent=Organic reaction}

A <chemical reaction> that converts an aromatic ring into a nonaromatic product.

= Wacker oxidation
{parent=Organic reaction}
{c}

Palladium-catalyzed oxidation of an alkene to a <carbonyl> compound using water as the oxygen source in the usual catalytic cycle.

= Cross-coupling reaction
{parent=Organic reaction}

A carbon–carbon or carbon–heteroatom coupling between constitutionally different organic partners.

= Kumada coupling
{parent=Cross-coupling reaction}
{c}

A transition-metal-catalyzed carbon–carbon coupling of an <Grignard reagent> with an organic halide.

= Cyclodehydration
{parent=Organic reaction}

Ring formation accompanied by elimination of water.

= 1,3-dipole
{parent=Organic reaction}

A three-atom dipolar electronic system participating in a \[3+2\]-<cycloaddition>.

= Nef reaction
{parent=Organic reaction}
{c}

Conversion of a primary or secondary nitro-derived <nitronate> into a <carbonyl> compound by suitable hydrolytic conditions. The nitro-bearing <carbon> becomes an aldehyde <carbon> for a primary nitroalkane or a <ketone> <carbon> for a secondary one.

= Olefination
{parent=Organic reaction}

Conversion of a <carbonyl> or related precursor into an alkene. Atom mapping and E/Z selectivity depend on the chosen reagent class.

= Wittig reaction
{parent=Olefination}
{c}

Reaction of a <phosphorus ylide> with a <carbonyl> compound to form an alkene. The <carbonyl> <carbon> and ylidic <carbon> become the two alkene atoms; the <oxygen> is transferred to phosphorus.

= Wittig methylenation
{parent=Wittig reaction}
{c}

A <Wittig reaction> with $\mathrm{Ph_3P{=}CH_2}$ that replaces a <carbonyl> <oxygen> by $\mathrm{{=}CH_2}$. On a cyclic <ketone> it installs an exocyclic methylene without inserting a new ring atom.

= Z-selective Wittig olefination
{parent=Wittig reaction}
{c}

Conditions favouring a Z alkene in a <Wittig reaction>, often using a nonstabilized <ylide> under kinetic, salt-controlled conditions. Isolation or verification is still required when complete stereoselectivity is not established.

= Reductive amination
{parent=Organic reaction}

Formation of a <carbon>–<nitrogen> bond by <carbonyl> condensation followed by imine/iminium reduction. Conditions and amine identity determine the substitution pattern at <nitrogen>.

= Alpha-methylenation
{parent=Organic reaction}

Installation of an exocyclic or terminal methylene at the alpha position of a <carbonyl> compound. Hydroxymethylation/dehydration or Mannich-base elimination can provide examples.

= Dehydration
{parent=Organic reaction}

Net loss of <water>. A <nitroaldol reaction> can first give an <alcohol>; eliminating water from that product is a separate reaction rather than an automatic part of the addition mechanism.

= Demercuration
{parent=Organic reaction}

Replacement of a <carbon>–<mercury (element)> bond by a <carbon>–<hydrogen bond> in a synthetic sequence. Its detailed mechanism need not be identical to that of the preceding <oxymercuration>.

= Oxymercuration
{parent=Organic reaction}

<electrophilic> activation of an <alkene> by <mercury (element)>(II), followed by nucleophilic opening of a bridged <mercurinium ion>. <water> usually attacks the more substituted <carbon>; subsequent <demercuration> replaces C–Hg with C–H.

= Henry reaction
{parent=Organic reaction}
{c}

= Nitroaldol reaction
{synonym}

Addition of a nitroalkane-derived <nitronate> to an <aldehyde> or <ketone> gives a beta-nitro <alcohol> after <protonation>. Subsequent <dehydration> is possible under some conditions but is a separate step.

= Neighboring group participation
{parent=Organic reaction}

= Neighbouring group participation
{synonym}

Intramolecular assistance by a group near a reacting centre. Temporary bridging can alter rate, connectivity, <stereochemistry> or apparent retention; an intermediate with equivalent paths can erase an original <stereochemical> distinction.

= Semipinacol rearrangement
{parent=Organic reaction}

Formation of a <carbonyl> accompanies migration of an adjacent C–H or C–C bond and departure of a <leaving group>. An <alkoxide>-assisted <hydride> migration can convert a suitably aligned <halohydrin> into a <ketone>.

= Benzannulation
{parent=Organic reaction}

Formation of a new <benzene> ring by joining <carbon> fragments, often followed by elimination and <tautomerization> that establish <aromaticity>.

= Nitroalkane benzannulation
{parent=Benzannulation}

A nitroalkane <carbon> can join the two ends of a five-<carbon> conjugated <ketone> framework. <carbon>–<carbon> bond formation, <thiolate> displacement and <nitrite> elimination can build a substituted <phenol>. The nitro-bearing <carbon>, rather than the nitro <nitrogen>, enters the <aromatic ring>.

= Claisen condensation
{parent=Organic reaction}
{c}

<carbon> acylation of an <ester> <enolate> followed by <deprotonation> of the beta-dicarbonyl product. <ketone>-<enolate acylation> by an <acid (chemistry)> <chloride> is a closely related acyl-substitution strategy, although it is not the classical <ester> self-condensation.

= Ozonolysis
{parent=Organic reaction}

Oxidative cleavage of an alkene by ozone, followed by workup. A reductive workup can yield carbonyl compounds; sodium borohydride can reduce the resulting aldehydes and ketones to alcohols.

= Beckmann rearrangement
{parent=Organic reaction}
{c}

Activated oximes rearrange to amides, with migration of the carbon substituent anti to the leaving group. A cyclic ketoxime can therefore expand a ring to a lactam.

= Beckmann fragmentation
{c}
{parent=Beckmann rearrangement}

Fragmentation of an activated <oxime> by cleavage of a carbon–carbon bond anti to its N–O leaving group, yielding a nitrile and a carbon electrophile or a product derived from it. Stabilization of a developing carbocation can favour fragmentation over migration. It differs from the usual <Beckmann rearrangement>, which inserts nitrogen into the migrating carbon–carbon bond and yields an amide after hydrolysis.

= Antiperiplanar migration in the Beckmann rearrangement
{parent=Beckmann rearrangement}

A <carbon> <substituent> opposite the activated N–O leaving group migrates onto <nitrogen> as the N–O bond cleaves. The <oxime> geometry therefore determines which bond is replaced by a <carbon>–<nitrogen> bond.

= Protodehalogenation
{parent=Organic reaction}

Replacement of a carbon-bound halogen by hydrogen. The mechanism and selectivity depend on the reducing agent, substrate and medium; ordinary aryl nucleophilic substitution need not be involved.

= Protodebromination
{parent=Protodehalogenation}

Replacement of a carbon-bound bromine by hydrogen. In suitable bromophenols, acid-promoted ipso protonation and reductive trapping of departing bromine provide a plausible polar mechanism.

= Malonic ester synthesis
{parent=Organic reaction}

Alkylation of a malonate ester enolate, followed by ester hydrolysis and decarboxylation, produces a substituted carboxylic acid. Two sequential alkylations replace the two original active-methylene hydrogens.

= Regioselectivity
{parent=Organic reaction}

= Markovnikov addition
{parent=Regioselectivity}
{c}

The conventional addition orientation in which the incoming <hydrogen> goes to the <alkene> <carbon> already bearing more hydrogens, in the ordinary applicable unsymmetrical-alkene setting.

= Anti-Markovnikov addition
{parent=Regioselectivity}
{c}

An addition whose regiochemical outcome is opposite to the conventional Markovnikov orientation. <hydroboration> followed by <oxidation> places OH at the less substituted <alkene> <carbon>.

= Electrophilic substitution
{parent=Organic reaction}

= Weitz-Scheffer reaction
{parent=Organic reaction}
{c}

= Weitz-Scheffer epoxidation
{c}
{synonym}

The <Weitz-Scheffer epoxidation> of an electron-poor alkene uses hydroperoxide addition followed by intramolecular closure and O–O bond cleavage. A planar achiral enone in an achiral environment can give a racemic epoxide pair.

= Robinson annulation
{parent=Organic reaction}
{c}
{wiki}

A <Robinson annulation> combines a <Michael addition> with an intramolecular <aldol reaction> and dehydration. Tracking the atoms shows which carbonyl survives and which new bond closes the six-membered ring.

= Elimination reaction
{parent=Organic reaction}
{wiki}

= Dehydrohalogenation
{parent=Elimination reaction}

Loss of a hydrogen halide to form a carbon–carbon multiple bond. Along a chlorinated polymer backbone, repeated elimination can create unsaturated or conjugated segments rather than exclusively changing ester side groups.

= E2 elimination
{parent=Elimination reaction}
{c}

A concerted elimination in which a base removes a beta hydrogen as a leaving group departs and a pi bond forms. Orbital alignment and substrate structure affect the allowed pathway.

= Trans-diaxial elimination
{parent=E2 elimination}

<antiperiplanar conformation> elimination in a <cyclohexane> chair with axial <leaving group> and axial beta <hydrogen>.

= E1 elimination

= Precipitation
{disambiguate=chemistry}
{parent=Chemical reaction}
{wiki}

<Precipitation (chemistry)> is formation of a <solid> from a <chemical solution>; the formed <solid> is a <precipitate>. Dissolved <chemical species> enter a separate <thermodynamic phase>. A newly formed <solid> can create a surface for <heterogeneous catalysis>; observing it does not establish the composition or catalytic role of that surface.

For example, mixing suitable <chemical solutions> of <calcium chloride> and <sodium carbonate> can form <calcium carbonate>:
$$
\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}(aq)+\mathrm{CO}_3^{2-}(aq)\longrightarrow\mathrm{CaCO}_3(s).
$$
Here $aq$ means dissolved in <water>, and $s$ marks the <solid>. Both sides contain one <calcium> <chemical atom>, one <carbon> <chemical atom>, and three <oxygen> <chemical atoms>; net <electric charge> is $+2-2=0$ on the left and zero on the right. The arrow describes the net change when <precipitation (chemistry)> occurs, rather than promising complete removal of either <ion>. The <calcium carbonate precipitation experiment> makes this phase separation visible.<Carbon Captured Chalk by Tony Butterfield>[Carbon Captured Chalk, Summary and Background for Teachers: sodium carbonate and calcium chloride form calcium carbonate]{ref}

The attraction is that an invisible dissolved inventory becomes a recoverable <solid>. <Net ionic equations> track that transfer; the <saturation criterion for precipitation> determines whether it is favored under specified conditions. This combination supports separations and chemical analysis.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others>[Chemistry 2e, §4.2, Precipitation Reactions and Solubility Rules: separations and analytical uses]{ref}

= Net ionic equation
{parent=Precipitation (chemistry)}

A <net ionic equation> retains the <chemical species> changed by a <chemical reaction> and cancels <spectator ions> present unchanged on both sides. Dissolved <strong electrolytes> are represented by their separated <ions>, while a <solid> product retains its <chemical formula>. Cancellation preserves both elemental inventories and <electric charge conservation>.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others>[Chemistry 2e, §4.2, Precipitation Reactions and Solubility Rules: molecular and net ionic equations for silver chloride]{ref}

For the <calcium carbonate> example, the complete ionic account is
$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}(aq)+2\mathrm{Cl}^-(aq)+2\mathrm{Na}^+(aq)+\mathrm{CO}_3^{2-}(aq)\\
&\qquad\longrightarrow\mathrm{CaCO}_3(s)+2\mathrm{Na}^+(aq)+2\mathrm{Cl}^-(aq).
\end{aligned}
$$
Canceling the <sodium> <cations> and <chloride> <anions> yields the <net ionic equation> above. Neither cancellation nor the conventional <sodium chloride> formula implies that the remaining dissolved <ions> have become paired <molecules>.

= Solubility product
{parent=Precipitation (chemistry)}
{title2=$K_{\mathrm{sp}}$}

= Solubility product constant
{synonym}

The <solubility product> is the <equilibrium constant> for dissolution of a specified <solid> into its dissolved <ions>. For a pure bulk AB <solid> at the chosen <temperature>, <pressure> and <standard states>,
$$
\mathrm{AB}(s)\rightleftharpoons\mathrm A^+(aq)+\mathrm B^-(aq),\qquad K_{\mathrm{sp}}=a_{\mathrm A}a_{\mathrm B}.
$$
The pure bulk <solid>'s <thermodynamic activity> is one in this convention. Coexisting <solid> and <chemical solution> at <chemical equilibrium> give this equality. If all available <solid> dissolves, the final <chemical solution> may instead be undersaturated; do not impose equality merely because a salt was initially added.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/15.1 Precipitation and Dissolution>[Chemistry 2e, §15.1, The Solubility Product: saturated solution with excess solid and dissolution equilibrium]{ref}

Using the <thermodynamic activity> definition $a_i=\gamma_i c_i/c^\circ$, with <activity coefficients> $\gamma_i$ and reference <molar concentration> $c^\circ$, gives the independent conversion
$$
K_{\mathrm{sp}}=\gamma_{\mathrm A}\gamma_{\mathrm B}\frac{c_{\mathrm A}c_{\mathrm B}}{(c^\circ)^2},\qquad K_{\mathrm{sp},c}=\frac{K_{\mathrm{sp}}(c^\circ)^2}{\gamma_{\mathrm A}\gamma_{\mathrm B}}.
$$
Thus $K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ is <dimensionless>, while the conditional concentration form $K_{\mathrm{sp},c}$ has units $\mathrm M^2$ for this one-to-one model. Treating $K_{\mathrm{sp},c}$ as constant requires a justified fixed or approximately fixed <activity coefficient> product. Changing <ionic strength> can change that product even at fixed <temperature>; changing <temperature> can change the <equilibrium constant> itself. Specify the <solid> <thermodynamic phase> too: different <crystal polymorphs> need not have the same <solubility product>.

= Molar solubility

{title2=$s$}

The <molar solubility> $s$ is the <amount of substance> of a specified <solid> dissolved per <volume (thermodynamics)> of saturated <chemical solution>, under stated conditions. It counts dissolved formula units, not the sum of all dissolved <ion> <concentrations>. For AB with no initial A or B and no competing <chemical reactions>, one-to-one <stoichiometry> gives $c_{\mathrm A}=c_{\mathrm B}=s$. For an AB$_2$ <solid>, the corresponding <concentrations> are $s$ and $2s$.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/15.1 Precipitation and Dissolution>[Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others; §15.1, Ksp and Solubility: dissolution coefficients relate molar solubility to individual ion concentrations]{ref}

= Solubility product and material balance
{parent=Solubility product}

A <solubility product> constrains a product of <thermodynamic activities>; the available inventories determine which combinations of <concentrations> are possible. Their intersection determines the <molar solubility>. Consider an original, abstract AB <solid>, with the preceding one-to-one dissolution <chemical equation>. Stipulate $c^\circ=1\,\mathrm M$, $\gamma_{\mathrm A}=\gamma_{\mathrm B}=1$ and $K_{\mathrm{sp},c}=10^{-4}\,\mathrm M^2$ at fixed <temperature> and <pressure>. Thus $K_{\mathrm{sp}}=10^{-4}$ is <dimensionless>. These are chosen model parameters, not data for an identified material.

Initially, a completely dissociated soluble XB <salt> supplies B <anions> at <molar concentration> $c_0\geq0$ and X <cations> at the same <molar concentration>; A is absent. All three <ions> have unit <electric charge> magnitude. X remains a <spectator ion>, and only AB can form a <solid>. Assume constant <volume (thermodynamics)>, no additional <chemical reactions>, and negligible contributions from <self-ionization of water>. Dissolving AB raises both reacting-ion <concentrations> by the same $s$:
$$
c_{\mathrm A}=s,\qquad c_{\mathrm B}=c_0+s,\qquad c_{\mathrm X}=c_0.
$$
<Charge neutrality> is satisfied: $c_{\mathrm A}+c_{\mathrm X}-c_{\mathrm B}=s+c_0-(s+c_0)=0$. Adding B alone without its accompanying <cation> would omit the initial <electric charge> balance.

In the plane of the two reacting-ion <concentrations>, this inventory constraint is the <line> $c_{\mathrm B}=c_{\mathrm A}+c_0$. Saturation supplies the <hyperbola> $c_{\mathrm B}=K_{\mathrm{sp},c}/c_{\mathrm A}$ for $c_{\mathrm A}>0$. Their positive intersection solves a <quadratic equation>:
$$
s(s+c_0)=K_{\mathrm{sp},c},\qquad
s_* =\frac{\sqrt{c_0^2+4K_{\mathrm{sp},c}}-c_0}{2}
=\frac{2K_{\mathrm{sp},c}}{\sqrt{c_0^2+4K_{\mathrm{sp},c}}+c_0}.
$$
The <quadratic formula> supplies two <roots of a polynomial>; only the positive one is a physical <concentration>. Uniqueness also follows because $s(s+c_0)$ strictly increases for $s\geq0$, starting at zero and growing without bound. The second expression, obtained by multiplying by the conjugate, avoids subtraction of nearly equal numbers at large $c_0$.

For pure <water>, $c_0=0$ gives $s_*=\sqrt{K_{\mathrm{sp},c}}=0.010\,\mathrm M$. For $c_0=0.030\,\mathrm M$,
$$
s_*\approx0.003027756\,\mathrm M,\qquad
c_{\mathrm B}\approx0.033027756\,\mathrm M.
$$
Their product is $10^{-4}\,\mathrm M^2$, although neither <concentration> equals the pure-<water> value. This is the useful geometrical distinction: an inventory <line> and an <equilibrium constant> specify different constraints.

Check the <solid> inventory before imposing saturation. If the initially added AB has <amount of substance> $n_0$ in <volume (thermodynamics)> $V$, the saturated branch requires $n_0>Vs_*$ for excess <solid> to remain. For example, choose $n_0=0.0020\,\mathrm{mol}$ and $V=0.100\,\mathrm L$. In pure <water>, $0.0010\,\mathrm{mol}$ dissolves and the same <amount of substance> remains as <solid>. At $c_0=0.030\,\mathrm M$, approximately $0.0003027756\,\mathrm{mol}$ dissolves, leaving $0.0016972244\,\mathrm{mol}$ as <solid>. In this model, an insufficient supply instead gives
$$
s=\min\{n_0/V,s_*\}.
$$
If $n_0/V<s_*$, all AB dissolves and the <ion product> is below the <solubility product>. At $n_0/V=s_*$, the last <solid> disappears exactly at saturation.

= Common-ion effect
{parent=Solubility product}

The <common-ion effect> is the change in a <chemical equilibrium> caused by adding an <ion> already participating in it. In the fixed-<activity coefficient> AB model, adding the common B <anion> lowers the <molar solubility>. The shared <ion> participates regardless of which <salt> supplied it.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/15.1 Precipitation and Dissolution>[Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others; §15.1, Common Ion Effect: adding an ion also produced by dissolution shifts the solubility equilibrium]{ref}

The preceding <solubility product and material balance> give a short proof without a qualitative slogan. Taking a <derivative> of $s_*^2+c_0s_*=K_{\mathrm{sp},c}$ at fixed $K_{\mathrm{sp},c}$ gives
$$
(2s_*+c_0)\frac{ds_*}{dc_0}+s_*=0,\qquad
\frac{ds_*}{dc_0}=-\frac{s_*}{2s_*+c_0}<0.
$$
Thus the <molar solubility> decreases continuously as $c_0$ increases. At $c_0=0$, the <derivative> is $-1/2$; for a very large common-ion background, the rationalized expression approaches $K_{\mathrm{sp},c}/c_0$. The <solubility product> is fixed within this model even while the <molar solubility> changes.

Neglecting $s_*$ beside $c_0$ replaces $s_*(c_0+s_*)=K_{\mathrm{sp},c}$ by $c_0s_{\mathrm{app}}=K_{\mathrm{sp},c}$. This approximation always overestimates the exact <molar solubility>, since it neglects the B <anions> supplied by AB itself. For $c_0>0$, its <relative error> has an exact simple form:
$$
s_{\mathrm{app}}=\frac{K_{\mathrm{sp},c}}{c_0}
=s_*+\frac{s_*^2}{c_0},\qquad
\frac{s_{\mathrm{app}}-s_*}{s_*}=\frac{s_*}{c_0}.
$$
At $c_0=0.030\,\mathrm M$, $s_{\mathrm{app}}=0.003333333\,\mathrm M$, approximately $10.1\%$ too high. At $c_0=0.100\,\mathrm M$, $s_*=0.000990195\,\mathrm M$ and $s_{\mathrm{app}}=0.001000000\,\mathrm M$, approximately $0.990\%$ too high. A small <solubility product> by itself does not justify the approximation: the common-ion background must be large compared with the resulting <molar solubility>.

In a real <chemical solution>, changes in <ionic strength> and <activity coefficients>, or competing <chemical reactions>, can invalidate a constant $K_{\mathrm{sp},c}$ even when this bound is met.

= Solubility product measurement by titration
{parent=Solubility product}

How can an invisible dissolved inventory be measured? The unsigned laboratory handout https://www.webassign.net/question_assets/ucscgencheml1/updated_labs2020/lab-10-procedure-2020.pdf[Solubility Product Constant, Ksp, for Calcium Hydroxide by Acid-Base Titration], Procedure, starts with saturated <calcium hydroxide>, supplied by the teacher or prepared from <calcium> and <water>. Its apparatus includes filter paper, a funnel, stoppered flasks, a <volumetric pipette>, a volumetric flask and a <burette>. Two successive rounds of <filtration (chemistry)> remove particles. The filtrate is stoppered to reduce <carbon dioxide> exposure. A $10.00\,\mathrm{mL}$ <aliquot> is diluted with deionized <water> and titrated with standardized <hydrochloric acid>; <bromothymol blue> gives a pale-green <titration endpoint>. The readout is the difference between final and initial <burette> readings; four consistent trials are required.<Solubility Product Constant, Ksp, for Calcium Hydroxide by Acid-Base Titration, University of California, Santa Cruz>[pp. 3–7, Procedure: gravity filtration steps 2–7, HCl preparation steps 8–14, and titrations steps 15–19]{ref}

Unlike AB, <calcium hydroxide> has one-to-two dissolution <stoichiometry>:
$$
\mathrm{Ca(OH)_2}(s)\rightleftharpoons\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}(aq)+2\mathrm{OH}^-(aq),\qquad
K_{\mathrm{sp},c}=[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}][\mathrm{OH}^-]^2.
$$
For <molar solubility> $s$ in the ideal model with no background <calcium> or <hydroxide>, no additional <chemical reactions>, and negligible <self-ionization of water>, $[\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}]=s$ and $[\mathrm{OH}^-]=2s$. Hence $K_{\mathrm{sp},c}=4s^3$, with units $\mathrm M^3$; it is not the AB constant used in the figures. The <neutralization> <chemical equation> is $\mathrm H^++\mathrm{OH}^-\longrightarrow\mathrm H_2\mathrm O$. Let $C_H$ be the known <titrant> <molar concentration>, $v$ its delivered <volume (thermodynamics)> at the <equivalence point>, and $V_a$ the original <aliquot> <volume (thermodynamics)>. Then the inventory calculation is
$$
h=[\mathrm{OH}^-]=\frac{C_Hv}{V_a},\qquad
s=\frac h2,\qquad K_{\mathrm{sp},c}=\frac{h^3}{2}.
$$
Dilution of the <aliquot> changes its <concentration> during the <acid-base titration>, but preserves its initial <amount of substance> of <hydroxide>. Therefore $V_a$, rather than the diluted flask <volume (thermodynamics)>, belongs in the denominator.

The handout identifies two biases: carried-over <calcium hydroxide> <solid> dissolves during <acid-base titration>, consuming extra <titrant>; <carbon dioxide> consumes <hydroxide> and forms <calcium carbonate>. Filtration and stoppering address these effects, while repeat trials test consistency. Its review questions also ask about dissolved ion pairs, which filtration cannot remove.<Solubility Product Constant, Ksp, for Calcium Hydroxide by Acid-Base Titration, University of California, Santa Cruz>[pp. 2–4, filtration and CO2 discussion; p. 9, Discussion Questions and Review Topics, ion pairs and dissolved CO2]{ref}

Further controls would compare a <water> blank, independently verify <titrant standardization>, and record the saturation <temperature>; these are proposed checks, not reported measurements. A <titration endpoint> estimates the <equivalence point> and may introduce a systematic offset. Even perfect inventory measurement does not determine <activity coefficients>. Recovering the <dimensionless> thermodynamic <solubility product> additionally requires
$$
K_{\mathrm{sp}}=\gamma_{\mathrm{Ca}}\gamma_{\mathrm{OH}}^2\frac{K_{\mathrm{sp},c}}{(c^\circ)^3}.
$$
This explains both the power and the limit of the experiment: <acid-base titration> measures a dissolved inventory, while the link from that inventory to a thermodynamic <equilibrium constant> still depends on a chemical model.

= Saturation criterion for precipitation
{parent=Solubility product}
{title2=$Q_{\mathrm{sp}}\gtrless K_{\mathrm{sp}}$}

Relative to the specified <solid>, $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}<K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ favors <dissolution> if that <solid> is available; $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}>K_{\mathrm{sp}}$ favors <precipitation (chemistry)>; equality marks saturation. These comparisons identify a driving direction favored by <thermodynamics>, not a <reaction rate>.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/15.1 Precipitation and Dissolution>[Chemistry 2e, §15.1, Predicting Precipitation: unsaturated, saturated and supersaturated cases]{ref}

A proof outline comes from the reaction <Gibbs free energy> for dissolution:
$$
\Delta_rG=RT\ln\!\left(\frac{Q_{\mathrm{sp}}}{K_{\mathrm{sp}}}\right).
$$
Here $R$ is the <molar gas constant> and $T>0$ the absolute <temperature>. If $Q_{\mathrm{sp}}>K_{\mathrm{sp}}$, the <logarithm> is positive, making the reverse transfer into <solid> favorable. At equality the bulk driving difference vanishes. Equality can also occur at the boundary where the last <solid> disappears, but it is not a universal constraint on every solid-free <chemical solution>.

A <supersaturated solution> has an <ion product> above the relevant <solubility product>. = Precipitation after equal-volume mixing
{parent=Solubility product}

Consider an original toy AB <salt> model with $K_{\mathrm{sp},c}=10^{-8}\,\mathrm M^2$ stipulated at fixed <temperature> and fixed <activity coefficient> product. This is a conditional concentration constant, not measured data for <calcium carbonate> or any identified material. A stock contains A <cations> and X <anions>; another contains Y <cations> and B <anions>. All four <ions> have unit <electric charge> magnitude. Only AB can form a <solid>; X and Y remain <spectator ions>. Assume full dissociation, no other <chemical reactions>, and a mixed <volume (thermodynamics)> equal to the sum of the initial <volumes>.

Mix two stocks, each of <volume (thermodynamics)> $V$,, each at <molar concentration> $C$ of its indicated <salt>. Before any <precipitation (chemistry)>, the mixed <volume (thermodynamics)> is $2V$, so
$$
c_{\mathrm A,0}=c_{\mathrm B,0}=\frac{CV}{2V}=\frac C2,\qquad Q_{c,0}=\frac{C^2}{4}.
$$
Multiplying the two stock <concentrations> would overestimate the mixed <ion product> by a factor of four. The saturation threshold is $C=2\sqrt{K_{\mathrm{sp},c}}=2\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm M$.

If $C=10^{-4}\,\mathrm M$, mixing gives $Q_{c,0}=2.5\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm M^2<K_{\mathrm{sp},c}$: no AB <precipitate> is favored. If $C=4\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm M$, mixing gives $Q_{c,0}=4\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm M^2>K_{\mathrm{sp},c}$. Suppose <nucleation> occurs and bulk AB <solid> remains at <chemical equilibrium>. Equal starting inventories and one-to-one <stoichiometry> require equal remaining <concentrations>, hence
$$
c_{\mathrm A,eq}=c_{\mathrm B,eq}=\sqrt{K_{\mathrm{sp},c}}=10^{-4}\,\mathrm M,\qquad
\frac{n_{\mathrm{AB},s}}{2V}=\frac C2-10^{-4}\,\mathrm M=10^{-4}\,\mathrm M.
$$
For two $10\,\mathrm{mL}$ portions in the second case, each reacting <ion> starts with $4\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$; the final $20\,\mathrm{mL}$ <chemical solution> retains $2\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of each, and $2\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$ of AB enters the <solid>. The respective X and Y inventories stay at $4\,\mathrm{\mu mol}$. Exactly half of each reacting inventory precipitates in this toy model.

= Oxidation-reduction reaction
{synonym}

A <redox> <chemical reaction> couples <oxidation> and <reduction>. In the <electrode> examples here, <oxidation> releases <electrons> and <reduction> accepts them. <Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others>[§4.2, “Oxidation-Reduction Reactions”: paired electron loss and gain]{ref}

$$

= Redox electron balance
{parent=Redox}
{title2=$n_{e,\mathrm{lost}}=n_{e,\mathrm{gained}}$}

Consider the reacting <zinc> and <copper> species in the <zinc-copper displacement experiment>. Split the net <chemical equation> into two <half-reactions>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{oxidation:}\quad&\mathrm{Zn}(s)\longrightarrow\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}(aq)+2e^-,\\
\text{reduction:}\quad&\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}(aq)+2e^-\longrightarrow\mathrm{Cu}(s).
\end{aligned}
$$

The <zinc> <oxidation state> rises from $0$ to $+2$; the <copper> <oxidation state> falls from $+2$ to $0$. Add the <half-reactions>: two released <electrons> match two accepted <electrons>. Cancelling $2e^-$ from both sides gives<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others>[§4.2, “Balancing Redox Reactions via the Half-Reaction Method”, steps 6–7: matching and cancelling exchanged electrons]{ref}
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{Zn}(s)+\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}(aq)\longrightarrow\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}(aq)+\mathrm{Cu}(s).}
$$
Thus the closed net <chemical reaction> has zero net production of free <electrons>. Cancellation does not destroy <electrons>: it removes a matched internal exchange from the written <chemical equation>. The <half-reactions> alone are incomplete descriptions of the whole reacting system. In a <galvanic cell>, the exchange can pass through a wire; in the <zinc-copper displacement experiment>, the reacting materials meet at the same metal surface. The equations do not require a reservoir of free <electrons> in the bulk <chemical solution>.

= Oxidation number
{synonym}

An <oxidation state> is an <electron>-bookkeeping assignment to a <chemical atom>: its <electric charge> number after the <ionic approximation of bonds>. It helps identify <oxidation> and <reduction> and determine <stoichiometry>, even for <covalent bonds> without separate charged <ions>. * <Water>, $\mathrm{H{-}O{-}H}$: <oxygen> receives both O–H <bond pairs> plus its two <lone pairs>, giving eight assigned <valence electrons> and $6-8=-2$. Each <hydrogen> receives none, giving $1-0=+1$.

* <Sodium hydride>, $\mathrm{NaH}$: <hydrogen> is more <electronegative> than <sodium>. The <formula unit> is assigned <sodium> $+1$ and <hydrogen> $-1$, consistent with the <hydrogen anion>. The usual $+1$ <hydrogen> shortcut fails here.

= Oxidation state sum rule
{parent=Oxidation state}
{title2=$\sum_i\mathrm{OS}_i=z$}

Sum <oxidation states> over all <chemical atoms> of the specified <molecule>, <ion> or <formula unit>, including multiplicities:
$$
\sum_i\mathrm{OS}_i=z,\qquad q=ze.
$$
Here $q$ is the net <electric charge> and $e$ the positive <elementary charge>. For a neutral entity the sum is zero; for an <ion> it equals its signed <electric charge> number $z$. The reason is simple: the allocation assigns every <valence electron> exactly once, so the sum is the neutral <electron> budget minus the actual <electron> count.

For <water>, $2(+1)+(-2)=0$; for <hydrogen peroxide>, $2(+1)+2(-1)=0$. In <permanganate>, $\mathrm{MnO}_4^-$, assigning each <oxygen> $-2$ gives $x+4(-2)=-1$, so <manganese> has <oxidation state> $+7$. This is not a free $\mathrm{Mn}^{7+}$ <ion>: the entire <permanganate> <anion> has net <electric charge> $-e$. The sum rule supplies a constraint; without additional assignments it cannot determine every individual <oxidation state>.

An increase in <oxidation state> records <oxidation>; a decrease records <reduction>. Thus $\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}\to\mathrm{Fe}^{3+}$ raises the <iron> <oxidation state> by one, while $\mathrm{MnO}_4^-\to\mathrm{Mn}^{2+}$ lowers the <manganese> <oxidation state> from $+7$ to $+2$. Five <iron> <ions> supply the five-<electron> inventory required per <permanganate> <ion>.

In an acidic <chemical solution>, the complete <half-reactions> are
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}&\longrightarrow\mathrm{Fe}^{3+}+e^-,\\
\mathrm{MnO}_4^-+8\mathrm H^++5e^-&\longrightarrow\mathrm{Mn}^{2+}+4\mathrm H_2\mathrm O.
\end{aligned}
$$
First balance <oxygen> with four <water> <molecules>, then <hydrogen> with eight <protons>, and finally <electric charge> with five <electrons>. Multiply the <iron> <half-reaction> by five and add; the exchanged <electrons> cancel:
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{MnO}_4^-+8\mathrm H^++5\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}\longrightarrow\mathrm{Mn}^{2+}+4\mathrm H_2\mathrm O+5\mathrm{Fe}^{3+}.}
$$
Both sides contain one <manganese>, four <oxygen>, eight <hydrogen> and five <iron> <chemical atoms>, with net <electric charge> $+17e$. The $\mathrm H^+$ notation represents the acidic aqueous <proton> inventory; it does not imply bare <protons> moving independently of <water>. These <half-reactions> and their sum are the acidic balance used in the documented <permanganate-iron titration>.<Iron Tablet Determination Analytical Competition (Royal Society of Chemistry resource)>[Iron Tablet Determination Analytical Competition, “Setting the Scene”: both half-reactions and the overall equation]{ref}

= Redox titration
{parent=Redox}

A <redox titration> adds a measured <amount of substance> of one <reactant> to determine another through a known <redox> <chemical equation>. The <burette> reading becomes an amount through <concentration>; the balanced <chemical equation> supplies the amount ratio. A colour endpoint additionally needs a chemical explanation, as in the <permanganate-iron titration>.

The useful bridge is an <electron> inventory. If each sample entity supplies $z_A$ <electrons> and each <titrant> entity accepts $z_T$, matching the formal exchanged <amount of substance> of <electrons> gives
$$
z_A n_A=z_T n_T,\qquad n_A=\frac{z_T}{z_A}c_TV_{\mathrm{eq}}.
$$
Here $c_T$ is the <titrant> <molar concentration> and $V_{\mathrm{eq}}$ the <volume (thermodynamics)> attributable to the sample at the <equivalence point>. These are net <half-reaction> counts, not a claim that free <electrons> accumulate in the flask. The <redox> conversion must be sufficiently complete and selective for the stated <stoichiometry> to identify $n_A$.

This is why a small visible signal can reveal a much larger invisible inventory: the signal locates a stopping point, while <titrant standardization> and <stoichiometry> turn delivery into <amount of substance>. The <titration endpoint> estimates the <equivalence point>; repeatable colour alone establishes neither correct <titrant> <concentration> nor chemical selectivity. The <permanganate-iron endpoint and blank model> separates these assumptions.

= Permanganate-iron titration
{parent=Redox titration}

A proposed modern teaching measurement puts a <pipette>-measured portion of an <iron> $\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}$ <chemical solution> in a <conical flask>, acidifies with excess dilute <sulfuric acid>, and adds standardized <potassium permanganate> from a clamped <burette> while swirling. The documented endpoint principle is that added purple <permanganate> is consumed until the first small excess leaves a pale purple colour; no separate colour indicator is required. Insufficient acidity can produce brown <manganese dioxide>, invalidating the assumed five-<electron> <half-reaction>.<Iron Tablet Determination Analytical Competition (Royal Society of Chemistry resource)>[“Setting the Scene”, final two paragraphs: burette, conical flask, decolourisation, endpoint and excess sulfuric acid]{ref}

Record initial and final <burette> readings; their difference is the delivered <volume (thermodynamics)> $V$. The following controls are proposed additions to test the inference. Run a reagent blank with the same <water>, <sulfuric acid> and endpoint criterion but no sample, obtaining $V_b$. Use a separately established <potassium permanganate> <concentration> $c$, clear the <burette> tip of air, and repeat sample portions. A known added <amount of substance> of $\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}$ should increase consumption by one fifth of that amount. A blank estimates reagent demand and the small endpoint excess; it cannot remove an interfering sample <reactant>.

Assuming complete, selective reaction according to the <acidic permanganate-iron balance>, the corrected inference is
$$
n(\mathrm{Fe}^{2+})=5c(V-V_b).
$$
With $c$ in $\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, use $V$ and $V_b$ in litres. As an original stipulated calculation, not an experimental result, take $c=0.02000\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, $V=12.60\,\mathrm{mL}$ and $V_b=0.10\,\mathrm{mL}$. Then the <permanganate> consumption is $0.2500\,\mathrm{mmol}$ and the inferred $\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}$ amount is $1.250\,\mathrm{mmol}$. In a $25.00\,\mathrm{mL}$ sample portion this corresponds to $0.05000\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$.

In this example the corrected <volume (thermodynamics)> is $12.50\,\mathrm{mL}=0.01250\,\mathrm L$. The <acidic permanganate-iron balance> gives $z_A=1$ for $\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}$ and $z_T=5$ for <permanganate>. Thus $0.2500\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of <permanganate> accepts $1.250\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of formal <electrons>, exactly what $1.250\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of $\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}$ supplies. The <amount of substance> ratio is five to one, despite equality of the exchanged <electron> amounts.

The denominator is the original $25.00\,\mathrm{mL}$ <aliquot>, not its final <volume (thermodynamics)> after adding <sulfuric acid>, <water> and <titrant>. Such <dilution> changes <concentrations> in the flask without changing the initial sampled <amount of substance> of $\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}$. Omitting the $0.10\,\mathrm{mL}$ <titration blank> would instead give $1.260\,\mathrm{mmol}$ and $0.05040\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, an overestimate of $0.80\%$ relative to the corrected constructed value. The displayed digits express the stipulated calculation, rather than establish its <measurement uncertainty>.

The endpoint detects residual <permanganate>, not the <iron> identity directly. Other sample <reactants> that consume <permanganate> raise the apparent $\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}$ amount; prior <oxidation> of sample $\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}$ to $\mathrm{Fe}^{3+}$ lowers it. A recovery check probes response to the added <iron> but does not prove absence of every interference. The readout determines the reacting $\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}$ inventory under the stated conditions, rather than total <iron> in an arbitrary mixture. No measured titres are asserted here.

= Permanganate-iron endpoint and blank model
{parent=Permanganate-iron titration}

The apparatus and motivating measurement are described in <permanganate-iron titration>; the following is an original account of its possible errors. Assume correct <titrant standardization>, the <acidic permanganate-iron balance>, quantitative conversion of the sampled $\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}$, and additive contributions expressed as equivalent <titrant> <volumes>. Write
$$
V=V_{\mathrm{Fe}}+B_s+D_s+A+I,\qquad
V_b=B_b+D_b,\qquad
V_{\mathrm{Fe}}=\frac{n_{\mathrm{Fe}}}{5c}.
$$
Here $V$ is the sample <titre>, $V_b$ the <titration blank>, $B_s,B_b$ background reagent consumption, and $D_s,D_b$ signed offsets of the observed <titration endpoints> from the respective <stoichiometry> conditions after background consumption. The subscripts distinguish sample and blank. $I$ is additional sample-specific <permanganate> demand, absent from the blank; $A$ is apparent delivery used to fill an initially air-containing <burette> tip rather than enter the flask. Assume the blank's tip is already filled; the sample should also have $A=0$ after proper preparation. These terms are a diagnostic model, not measured components of the stipulated $0.10\,\mathrm{mL}$ blank.

\Image[redox-titration-endpoint-burette-and-blank.png]
{title=<Burette> delivery, <titration endpoint> and <titration blank> describe different parts of the inference}
{description=Original schematic and additive <volume (thermodynamics)> model, not a photograph or measured <titration curve>. The numerical decomposition $12.60=12.50+0.10\,\mathrm{mL}$ assumes matched background and endpoint contributions and no tip-air or sample-specific demand. Boxes are not to scale; the blank's reagent consumption and endpoint offset are not separately specified. The exact <equivalence point> has no guaranteed visible colour flag.}

Subtracting the two accounts proves the error relation
$$
\widehat n_{\mathrm{Fe}}=5c(V-V_b)=n_{\mathrm{Fe}}+5c\Delta V,\qquad
\Delta V=(B_s-B_b)+(D_s-D_b)+A+I.
$$
The simple inference is valid when this residual is negligible. Matching <water> and <sulfuric acid> tests the background terms, but does not by itself establish $D_s=D_b$: flask composition and viewing conditions can differ. A coloured sample can mask residual <permanganate>, changing the assigned <titration endpoint> without a corresponding <redox> consumption. A sample-specific <reducing agent> instead contributes positive $I$ by consuming <permanganate>. A reagent <titration blank> cannot measure either effect just by omitting the sample.

A transient purple streak before mixing is not the mixed-flask <titration endpoint>. Conversely, the <equivalence point> is the five-to-one inventory match, without a promised visible flag. In the ideal selective model, a persistent excess colour requires some <permanganate> beyond that match. No particular drop size, waiting time or endpoint offset is stipulated here; the same stated colour criterion must be applied to sample and blank, and its transferability checked.

The model also predicts error direction. If a tip bubble disappears during sample delivery, $A>0$ makes the inferred <amount of substance> too high because final minus initial <burette> reading includes liquid that stayed in the tip. Conditioning the tip before the first reading prevents this particular discrepancy; subtracting a separate <titration blank> is not an instrument repair. Repeated <aliquots> test scatter, a known added $\mathrm{Fe}^{2+}$ inventory tests the five-to-one response, and an independent <titrant standardization> tests $c$. These are the proposed controls from <permanganate-iron titration>, with distinct diagnostic roles; none alone proves absence of all sample interference.

= Saponification
{parent=Hydrolysis}
{wiki}

<Saponification> is <hydrolysis> of an <ester> under basic conditions. The <conductometric saponification assay> follows <ethyl acetate> consumption by <hydroxide>, producing <ethanol> and an <acetate ion>; its equal-starting-<concentration> case provides a practical <second-order reaction> example.

<Saponification> is <ester> <hydrolysis> promoted by a <chemical base>, with net consumption of <hydroxide> to form a <carboxylate> and an <alcohol>. For a <carboxylic acid>-derived <ester>, the net <chemical equation> is
$$
\mathrm{RCOOR'}+\mathrm{OH^-}\longrightarrow\mathrm{RCOO^-}+\mathrm{R'OH}.
$$
The <hydroxide> is a <reactant>, rather than merely a regenerated <catalyst>.<Organic Chemistry by John McMurry/21.6 Chemistry of Esters>[Organic Chemistry by John McMurry; §21.6, Conversion of Esters into Carboxylic Acids: Hydrolysis; Figure 21.8 discussion of carboxylate formation]{ref}

For <ethyl acetate>, the balanced <chemical equation> becomes
$$
\mathrm{CH_3COOC_2H_5}+\mathrm{OH^-}\longrightarrow\mathrm{CH_3COO^-}+\mathrm{C_2H_5OH}.
$$
Both sides contain four <carbon> <chemical atoms>, nine <hydrogen> <chemical atoms>, three <oxygen> <chemical atoms>, and net <electric charge> $-1$. A <sodium> <ion> supplied by <sodium hydroxide> is a <spectator ion>; the <net ionic equation> omits it without losing <electric charge conservation>.

= Saponification with limiting hydroxide
{parent=Saponification}

Consider a hypothetical closed, well mixed <chemical solution> initially containing $20\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of <ethyl acetate>, $12\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of <hydroxide>, and no <acetate ions> or <ethanol>. One millimole is $10^{-3}$ <mole>. Assume complete conversion as far as the one-to-one <chemical equation> allows, with no competing <chemical reactions>. This is an ideal <stoichiometry> calculation, not a measured yield.

Let the <extent of reaction> be $\xi$, expressed in millimoles. The four values of <amount of substance>, in the order <ethyl acetate>, <hydroxide>, <acetate ion>, <ethanol>, are
$$
\bigl(20-\xi,\;12-\xi,\;\xi,\;\xi\bigr)\,\mathrm{mmol},\qquad 0\leq\xi\leq12.
$$
The upper bound follows because both <reactant> quantities must be nonnegative. Thus <hydroxide> is the <limiting reagent>, and the ideal endpoint $\xi=12$ leaves
$$
8\,\mathrm{mmol}\ \text{ethyl acetate},\quad
0\,\mathrm{mmol}\ \text{hydroxide},\quad
12\,\mathrm{mmol}\ \text{acetate},\quad
12\,\mathrm{mmol}\ \text{ethanol}.
$$
The <carboxylate> formed equals the <hydroxide> consumed, while unreacted <ester> remains. Adding more <hydroxide> raises the <stoichiometry> bound; merely waiting longer does not change that bound. To convert these values of <amount of substance> into <concentrations>, a <volume> is also required. The endpoint does not specify the <reaction rate> or time needed to approach it.

= Energy coupling
{parent=Chemical reaction}
{title2=$\Delta G_{\mathrm{net}}=\Delta G_1+\Delta G_2$}

<Energy coupling> links an <endergonic reaction> to an <exergonic reaction> through a <reaction mechanism>, so that the combined transformation has a favourable change in <Gibbs free energy>. <ATP> <hydrolysis> is a common driving <chemical reaction>. A shared <reaction intermediate>, produced in one step and consumed in another, can physically connect the transformations; merely placing two independent <chemical reactions> in the same <chemical solution> does not establish <energy coupling>. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26838/[Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: coupled reactions and activated intermediates].

\Image[atp-energy-coupling.png]
{title=<Energy coupling>: $+20-30.5=-10.5\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ under the same stipulated conditions}
{description=Original diagram of cumulative changes in <Gibbs free energy> as two contributions are added. The levels are bookkeeping steps, not a <reaction mechanism>, actual <reaction intermediates>, elapsed time, or <activation energy> barriers. A <reaction mechanism> must enforce the joint transformation for the net favourable value to drive the desired conversion.}

For a computed example, stipulate an <endergonic reaction> $\mathrm A\longrightarrow\mathrm B$ with $\Delta G_1^{\circ\prime}=+20\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ under precisely the same biochemical <standard state> conditions as the quoted <ATP> <hydrolysis> value. Assume a <reaction mechanism> couples one <ATP> <hydrolysis> to each A-to-B conversion. The formal sum is
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm A&\longrightarrow\mathrm B,
&\Delta G_1^{\circ\prime}&=+20\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}},\\
\mathrm{ATP}+\mathrm{H_2O}&\longrightarrow\mathrm{ADP}+\mathrm P_i,
&\Delta G_2^{\circ\prime}&=-30.5\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}},\\
\mathrm A+\mathrm{ATP}+\mathrm{H_2O}&\longrightarrow\mathrm B+\mathrm{ADP}+\mathrm P_i,
&\Delta G_{\mathrm{net}}^{\circ\prime}&=-10.5\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The sum $+20+(-30.5)=-10.5$ gives an <exergonic reaction>. The $+20$ is a chosen teaching value, not a measured property of an identified substance. Outside these reference conditions, add the actual $\Delta G_1$ and $\Delta G_2$, each evaluated using consistent <thermodynamic activities>; mixing a standard value for one <chemical reaction> with an unrelated cellular value for the other gives no justified prediction.

Why do the changes in <Gibbs free energy> add? <Gibbs free energy> is a <state function>. Adding consistent reaction changes cancels any shared <reaction intermediate> and leaves the difference between the combined <reactants> and combined <reaction products>. This explains the thermodynamic accounting; it does not construct the <reaction mechanism>. In many <ATP>-dependent routes, <phosphorylation> transfers a <phosphate> group to a <substrate> or an <enzyme>, and subsequent transformation consumes that modified material. The shared material is what connects the steps. Other <reaction mechanisms> use <ATP> differently. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26838/[Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell: ATP-driven biosynthesis].

The useful achievement is that a common regenerated <nucleotide> can connect many different processes in <metabolism>. The net negative change in <Gibbs free energy> permits the coupled conversion, but supplies no <reaction rate>: <enzymes> can lower <activation energy> barriers without changing the endpoint difference. <Energy coupling> changes which overall <chemical reaction> is being considered; <catalysis> accelerates an available route. See https://openstax.org/books/biology-2e/pages/6-2-potential-kinetic-free-and-activation-energy[OpenStax Biology 2e, 6.2: free-energy changes and reaction barriers].

A historical landmark is Fritz Lipmann's https://doi.org/10.1002/9780470122464.ch4[1941 essay, Metabolic Generation and Utilization of Phosphate Bond Energy]{ref}. Its sections on the <phosphate> cycle, generation and utilization of phosphate-bond <energy>, and group transfer organized these processes around a reusable chemical carrier. The historical terminology should be read with the modern distinction between net <hydrolysis> and the cost of breaking a <chemical bond>.

= Chemical equation
{parent=Chemical reaction}
{wiki}

A <chemical equation> represents a <chemical reaction> using <chemical formulas>, with <reactants> on the left and <reaction products> on the right. A balanced <chemical equation> has the same number of <chemical atoms> of each <chemical element> on both sides. For <molecular hydrogen> and <molecular oxygen> forming <water>,
$$
2\mathrm{H}_2+\mathrm{O}_2\longrightarrow2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}.
$$
The coefficients $2,1,2$ give the relative numbers of <molecules>; the coefficient $1$ is normally omitted. Count <hydrogen> as $2\times2=4$ on the left and $2\times2=4$ on the right, and <oxygen> as $1\times2=2$ on the left and $2\times1=2$ on the right. See https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/4-1-writing-and-balancing-chemical-equations[Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, §4.1: writing and balancing chemical equations].

{title=Two <molecular hydrogen> <molecules> and one <molecular oxygen> <molecule> account for two <water> <molecules>}
{description=Exactly four <hydrogen> and two <oxygen> <chemical atoms> occur on each side. The circles and connecting lines are an invented schematic: they indicate grouping and <chemical bonds>, without representing actual <bond lengths>, <bond angles> or a <reaction mechanism>.}

To balance the initial expression $\mathrm H_2+\mathrm O_2\longrightarrow\mathrm H_2\mathrm O$, first put $2$ before <water> to match the two <oxygen> <chemical atoms>. This creates four <hydrogen> <chemical atoms> on the right, so put $2$ before <molecular hydrogen>. Changing a coefficient multiplies the entire <chemical formula>. Changing a subscript changes composition and usually the <chemical substance>: $2\mathrm H_2\mathrm O$ is two <water> <molecules>, whereas $\mathrm H_2\mathrm O_2$ is one <hydrogen peroxide> <molecule>. Never balance a <chemical equation> by changing its subscripts.

The bookkeeping follows from conservation of the <atomic nuclei> in ordinary, non-nuclear <chemical reactions>; <chemical bonds> and the distribution of <electrons> can change. For a <chemical equation> involving <ions>, total <electric charge> must also match. For example, the existing <self-ionization of water> equation has zero total <electric charge> on each side, since the $+1$ of <hydronium> cancels the $-1$ of <hydroxide>. See <Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/2.6 Ionic and Molecular Compounds> (§2.6: nuclei and ions).

The same $2:1:2$ ratio relates changes in <amount of substance>: two <moles> of <molecular hydrogen> consumed per <mole> of <molecular oxygen> correspond to two <moles> of <water> formed. A <mole> is a defined unit, not one <molecule>; the little whole-number picture is an abstract counting model, not a claim that a macroscopic sample contains just five <molecules>. See <molecular count and amount of substance> for the exact conversion and <SI base unit: mole by Bureau International des Poids et Mesures> for the definition.

This is why a balanced <chemical equation> is useful: <stoichiometry> turns the same atom accounting into relations between measurable quantities. It does not determine <reaction rate>, the final conversion set by <chemical equilibrium>, or the actual yield of recovered <reaction products>. The <limiting reagent> gives an upper bound under the specified <chemical reaction>.

= Thermal dissociation
{parent=Chemical reaction}

Thermal dissociation is temperature-driven breaking of a molecule into simpler fragments. The <chemical equilibrium> abundance depends on pressure as well as temperature: reactions increasing particle number are generally favored at low pressure. In an <exoplanet atmosphere>, reduced <water> on a very hot dayside does not by itself establish a low planetary oxygen abundance.

= Stoichiometry
{parent=Chemical reaction}
{wiki}
{title2=$\nu_i$}

<Stoichiometry> relates the quantities consumed and formed in a <chemical reaction>. Its coefficients are ratios of entity counts, or equivalently of <amount of substance>, rather than ratios of <masses>. For the <water> <chemical equation>, consuming two <molecular hydrogen> <molecules> per <molecular oxygen> <molecule> gives the same $2:1$ ratio as consuming two <moles> of <molecular hydrogen> per <mole> of <molecular oxygen>. Multiplying every entity count by the same factor preserves the ratio. See https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/4-3-reaction-stoichiometry[Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, reaction stoichiometry].

Coefficients and subscripts do different jobs. The coefficient in $2\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}$ counts two <water> <molecules>; the subscript says that each contains two <hydrogen> <chemical atoms>. Changing coefficients scales quantities. Changing a subscript changes the specified <chemical substance>.

\Image[stoichiometry-particle-counts.png]
{title=The same stoichiometric ratio at molecular and molar scales}
{description=The upper schematic counts individual <molecules>; the lower line multiplies each count by the <Avogadro constant> times one <mole>. Circle sizes and positions are illustrative.}

This distinction catches a common counting error: one <mole> of <water> <molecules> contains two <moles> of <hydrogen> <chemical atoms> and one <mole> of <oxygen> <chemical atoms>. Always specify what is counted.

<Stoichiometry> records reactant and product multiplicities in a <chemical reaction> or a <chemical reaction network>. The change caused by one reaction is its <stoichiometric vector>; multiplying by the reaction rate gives its contribution to the concentration equations. For two identical reactants the rate-constant convention absorbs any combinatorial factor.

= Molecular count and amount of substance
{parent=Stoichiometry}
{title2=$N=nN_{\mathrm A}$}

The molecular count $N$ is a dimensionless integer; the <amount of substance> $n$ has unit <mole>, symbol $\mathrm{mol}$. They satisfy
$$
N=nN_{\mathrm A},\qquad N_{\mathrm A}=6.02214076\times10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol}^{-1}.
$$
The <Avogadro constant> is exact by the modern definition of the <mole>: see <SI base unit: mole by Bureau International des Poids et Mesures>. Thus two <water> <molecules> correspond to $2/N_{\mathrm A}$ <moles>, whereas two <moles> of <water> contain $1.204428152\times10^{24}$ <molecules>. A <mole> is a unit for counting specified entities, which may also be <chemical atoms>, <ions> or <electrons>.

= Extent of reaction
{parent=Stoichiometry}
{title2=$\xi$}
{wiki}

For a closed system with one specified <chemical reaction>, the <extent of reaction> $\xi$ records progress from $\xi=0$ through $n_i=n_{i,0}+\nu_i\xi$. Here $n_i$ is the <amount of substance> of species $i$, and the signed <stoichiometric vector> has negative entries for <reactants> and positive entries for <reaction products>. For the <water> <chemical equation> in the order $\mathrm{H}_2,\mathrm{O}_2,\mathrm{H}_2\mathrm{O}$, it is $(-2,-1,2)$. The normalization of $\xi$ depends on the chosen coefficients. See https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/E02283[IUPAC Gold Book, extent of reaction].

With particle numbers instead of molar amounts, a molecular extent $\xi_N$ obeys $dN_i=\nu_i\,d\xi_N$, and the <chemical potentials> are measured per particle. At fixed <temperature> and <volume>, the <Helmholtz free energy> then satisfies $dF=(\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i)\,d\xi_N$. An interior <chemical equilibrium> requires $\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i=0$; the molar convention gives the same relation with chemical potentials per mole.

= Limiting reactant
{synonym}

The <limiting reagent> is the <reactant> exhausted first if the specified <chemical reaction> proceeds to the maximum conversion permitted by <stoichiometry>. With initial values of <amount of substance> $n_{i,0}$ for the <reactants> and positive consumption coefficients $a_i$, the largest forward <extent of reaction> is $\xi_{\max}=\min_i(n_{i,0}/a_i)$. Compare these quotients, rather than simply choosing the smallest initial quantity. This calculation predicts the maximum <reaction product> quantity and identifies which <reactant> supply must increase to raise it. See https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/4-4-reaction-yields[Flowers et al., Chemistry 2e, limiting reactants].

Start with $3\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <molecular hydrogen>, $1\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <molecular oxygen> and no <water>. Assume complete conversion by the displayed <chemical reaction>, without competing reactions. Then
$$
\xi_{\max}=\min\left(\frac{3\,\mathrm{mol}}2,\frac{1\,\mathrm{mol}}1\right)=1\,\mathrm{mol}.
$$
The <molecular oxygen> is the <limiting reagent>. Consumption of $2\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <molecular hydrogen> and $1\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <molecular oxygen> forms $2\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <water>, leaving $1\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <molecular hydrogen>. Initially there are $1.806642228\times10^{24}$ <molecular hydrogen> <molecules> and $6.02214076\times10^{23}$ <molecular oxygen> <molecules>; after conversion there are $1.204428152\times10^{24}$ <water> <molecules> and $6.02214076\times10^{23}$ leftover <molecular hydrogen> <molecules>.

As a check, the initial inventory has $6\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <hydrogen> <chemical atoms> and $2\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <oxygen> <chemical atoms>. The final <water> contains $4\,\mathrm{mol}$ of <hydrogen> <chemical atoms>, and leftover <molecular hydrogen> supplies the remaining $2\,\mathrm{mol}$. The atom totals balance even though the total <amount of substance> of <molecules> falls from four to three <moles>.
