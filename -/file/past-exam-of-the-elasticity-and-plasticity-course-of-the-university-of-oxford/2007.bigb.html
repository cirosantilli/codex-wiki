= Solution
{parent=1}

The in-plane <equilibrium> equations are $\tau_{xx,x}+\tau_{xy,y}=0$ and $\tau_{xy,x}+\tau_{yy,y}=0$. On a <simply connected> patch, each <divergence-free> row has a <streamfunction>: write $\tau_{xx}=\phi_y$, $\tau_{xy}=-\phi_x=-\psi_y$, $\tau_{yy}=\psi_x$. Symmetry gives $\phi_x=\psi_y$, so another potential exists with $A_y=\phi$, $A_x=\psi$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\tau_{xx}=A_{yy},\qquad\tau_{yy}=A_{xx},\qquad\tau_{xy}=-A_{xy}.}
$$
If two such <Airy stress functions> give the same <stress>, their difference has zero <Hessian> and is therefore an arbitrary <affine function> $a+bx+cy$ on a connected domain. This is the full nonuniqueness for a fixed <stress>. The existence argument is local, or global on a <simply connected> domain; domains that are <multiply connected regions> require the associated force and moment period conditions for a single-valued global potential. <Equilibrium> alone should not silently erase that topological qualification.

For <plane strain> put $s=e_{xx}+e_{yy}$. The <constitutive equation> gives $\tau_{xx}+\tau_{yy}=2(\lambda+\mu)s$. Consequently
$$
e_{xx}=\frac{\tau_{xx}-\lambda s}{2\mu},\quad
e_{yy}=\frac{\tau_{yy}-\lambda s}{2\mu},\quad
e_{xy}=\frac{\tau_{xy}}{2\mu},\quad
s=\frac{\Delta A}{2(\lambda+\mu)}.
$$
The <displacement> <Saint-Venant compatibility equations> $e_{xx,yy}+e_{yy,xx}-2e_{xy,xy}=0$ then becomes
$$
0=2\mu(e_{xx,yy}+e_{yy,xx}-2e_{xy,xy})
=\frac{\lambda+2\mu}{2(\lambda+\mu)}\Delta^2A.
$$
For stable elastic moduli the coefficient is nonzero, so $\boxed{\Delta^2A=0}$. If $D$ is bounded and smooth and $A=\partial_nA=0$ on its boundary, integrating twice by parts gives $0=\int_DA\Delta^2A\,dV=\int_D(\Delta A)^2\,dV$. Thus $A$ is harmonic with zero boundary value and $\boxed{A=0}$ by uniqueness of the <Dirichlet problem>. The normal-<derivative> condition is what removes the extra fourth-order <boundary terms>.

For the pressurized <annulus>, substituting the radial <constitutive equations> into <equilibrium> gives $u''+u'/r-u/r^2=0$, so $u=Cr+D/r$. The stresses are
$$
\tau_{rr}=2(\lambda+\mu)C-\frac{2\mu D}{r^2},\qquad
\tau_{\theta\theta}=2(\lambda+\mu)C+\frac{2\mu D}{r^2}.
$$
Using gauge stresses relative to ambient, the boundaries are $\tau_{rr}(a)=-P$, $\tau_{rr}(b)=0$. Therefore
$$
2(\lambda+\mu)C=\frac{Pa^2}{b^2-a^2},\qquad
2\mu D=\frac{Pa^2b^2}{b^2-a^2},
$$
and
$$
\boxed{|\tau_{rr}-\tau_{\theta\theta}|=\frac{2Pa^2b^2}{(b^2-a^2)r^2},\qquad
\max_{a\leq r\leq b}|\tau_{rr}-\tau_{\theta\theta}|=\frac{2Pb^2}{b^2-a^2}.}
$$
The maximum occurs at the bore. Once the chosen <yield criterion> is reached, <Hooke's law> cannot describe the total <strain> alone: write total <strain> as elastic plus <plastic strain> and supplement the elastic <stress> law with a yield condition, a <plastic flow rule> and any required <work hardening> law. A plastic region starts near the bore and must be matched to the remaining elastic region. The exact conversion between a principal-<stress> difference and the quoted <yield stress> depends on whether the material criterion is Tresca or von Mises. This is the <elastic stress concentration in a pressurized thick cylinder>.

= Solution

Decomposing a plane <displacement> into components parallel and perpendicular to its <wavevector> gives compressional and shear speeds
$$
\boxed{c_p=\sqrt{(\lambda+2\mu)/\rho},\qquad c_s=\sqrt{\mu/\rho},\qquad
k_p=\omega/c_p,\quad k_s=\omega/c_s.}
$$
The incident <polarization> is longitudinal. Its reflected <longitudinal wave> reverses the vertical <wavevector> component while retaining its horizontal component. The converted <shear wave> has <wavevector> $k_s(\sin\beta,-\cos\beta)$ perpendicular to <polarization> $(\cos\beta,\sin\beta)$.

Every term must have the same <phase> along the rigid boundary. Thus
$$
\boxed{k_s\sin\beta=k_p\cos\alpha,\qquad
\sin\beta=\frac{c_s}{c_p}\cos\alpha.}
$$
Choose $\cos\beta>0$ so the reflected <shear wave> propagates downward into $y<0$. At $y=0$, both <displacement> components must vanish for every boundary <phase>:
$$
(1+R_1)\cos\alpha+R_2\cos\beta=0,\qquad
(1-R_1)\sin\alpha+R_2\sin\beta=0.
$$
Solving this two-by-two system yields
$$
\boxed{R_1=\frac{\sin(\alpha-\beta)}{\sin(\alpha+\beta)},\qquad
R_2=-\frac{\sin2\alpha}{\sin(\alpha+\beta)}.}
$$
For a stable isotropic material $0<c_s/c_p<1$. An incident wave directed toward the boundary has $0<\alpha<\pi$; choosing $\beta\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$ by the sine relation gives real reflected waves throughout this range and no zero denominator. In the usual convention restricting incident horizontal propagation to positive $x$, this is $0<\alpha\leq\pi/2$. Grazing endpoints have no incoming normal energy flux and are limiting cases. There is no <critical angle> for incident compressional-to-shear conversion here, because the converted wave is slower; the opposite incident-wave type has different critical-angle behaviour. At <normal incidence> $\alpha=\pi/2$, $\beta=0$, $R_1=1$ and $R_2=0$, cancelling the incident <displacement> with the reversed longitudinal <polarization>. This is <compressional-wave reflection from a rigid elastic boundary>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The <tension> term $Tw_{xx}$ is the transverse restoring force from a nearly straight, tensioned rod. The bending term $-EIw_{xxxx}$ is the force from Euler-Bernoulli flexure, where $E$ is <Young's modulus> and $I$ the <second moment of area>. The right side is transverse <inertia>. In the displayed one-dimensional equation $\rho$ denotes <linear mass density>; if it denotes volumetric <density> instead, the inertial coefficient must include cross-sectional area.

For a <Fourier mode>, $w_{xx}=-k^2w$, $w_{xxxx}=k^4w$ and $w_{tt}=-\omega^2w$, giving
$$
\boxed{\rho\omega^2=Tk^2+EIk^4.}
$$
For $k>0$ the <phase speed> is $c_p=\omega/k=\sqrt{T/\rho+EIk^2/\rho}$, strictly increasing with $k$ when $EI>0$ and $T\geq0$. Thus shorter <wavelengths> propagate faster in <phase>. The <group velocity> $c_g=(T+2EIk^2)/\sqrt{\rho(T+EIk^2)}$ is also increasing, so the statement is consistent with wave-packet transport.

At a simple support the <displacement> and <bending moment> vanish: $w=0$ and $EIw_{xx}=0$ at $x=0,L$. The <eigenmodes> are $\sin(n\pi x/L)$ for $n=1,2,\ldots$. Setting $T=0$ in the <dispersion relation> therefore yields
$$
\boxed{\omega_n=\sqrt{\frac{EI}\rho}\frac{n^2\pi^2}{L^2}.}
$$
The integer is positive for a nonzero mode; $n=0$ gives the identically zero sine function, and negative integers repeat the same modes. <Bending stiffness> makes the frequencies quadratic in mode number, unlike a purely tensioned string.

= Solution
{parent=i}

For antiplane <displacement> $u=(0,0,w)$ the only nonzero <strain> components are $e_{xz}=w_x/2$ and $e_{yz}=w_y/2$. Commutation of <mixed partial derivatives> gives the necessary local compatibility condition
$$
\boxed{\partial_ye_{xz}-\partial_xe_{yz}=0.}
$$
The supplied <stress> is symmetric, independent of $z$ and trace-free. Its first two <equilibrium> equations are automatic; its third gives
$$
\partial_x\left(-\frac{\mu y}{x^2+y^2}\right)+
\partial_y\left(\frac{\mu x}{x^2+y^2}\right)=0
$$
away from the origin. <Antiplane shear> has zero trace, so isotropic <Hooke's law> reduces to $\boxed{e=\tau/(2\mu)}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{w_x=-\frac y{x^2+y^2},\qquad w_y=\frac x{x^2+y^2}.}
$$
Locally this integrates to $w=\arg(x+iy)+C$, represented on a suitable branch by $\arctan(y/x)+C$. The <strain> condition is satisfied at every point of the <punctured plane>, but the <displacement> cannot be made globally single-valued there:
$$
\boxed{\oint_{r=R}(w_x\,dx+w_y\,dy)=2\pi.}
$$
A <gradient> of a globally single-valued smooth <displacement> would instead integrate to zero around any <closed curve>. Local compatibility is therefore not sufficient on this <multiply connected region>.

A physical construction is a Volterra <screw dislocation>: cut the cylinder along a radial half-plane ending on its axis, shift one cut face relative to the other parallel to the axis, and rejoin. The axial mismatch is the <Burgers vector>. In the given normalization its magnitude is $2\pi$; with a dimensional mismatch $b$ one uses $w=b\arg(x+iy)/(2\pi)$. An intact pristine solid cannot reach this state by a smooth single-valued elastic deformation alone. The axis is a <dislocation core> where the continuum elastic field is singular and must be regularized by microscopic structure or <plastic deformation>. On the punctured domain the differential condition holds locally; globally its nonzero <circulation> detects the defect. In a distributional description, that incompatibility is concentrated on the axis. This is <local strain compatibility versus screw-dislocation circulation>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Write $e_{xx}=u_{x,x}$, $e_{yy}=u_{y,y}$ and $2e_{xy}=u_{x,y}+u_{y,x}$. Differentiating twice and cancelling the <mixed partial derivatives> gives
$$
\boxed{e_{xx,yy}-2e_{xy,xy}+e_{yy,xx}=0.}
$$
A convenient set of six three-dimensional <Saint-Venant compatibility equations> is
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
e_{11,22}+e_{22,11}-2e_{12,12}&=0,\\
e_{22,33}+e_{33,22}-2e_{23,23}&=0,\\
e_{33,11}+e_{11,33}-2e_{13,13}&=0,\\
e_{11,23}+e_{23,11}-e_{12,13}-e_{13,12}&=0,\\
e_{22,13}+e_{13,22}-e_{12,23}-e_{23,12}&=0,\\
e_{33,12}+e_{12,33}-e_{13,23}-e_{23,13}&=0.
\end{aligned}}
$$
They follow by inserting $e_{ij}=(u_{i,j}+u_{j,i})/2$ and commuting <derivatives>. They form the six symmetric components of the incompatibility <tensor> $C_{ij}=\epsilon_{ik\ell}\epsilon_{jmn}e_{\ell n,km}$, up to the indicated signs and permutation of components.

They obey the three differential identities $\partial_iC_{ij}=0$, because a symmetric pair of <derivatives> contracts with an antisymmetric <Levi-Civita symbol>. Thus **there are three independent differential constraints**, represented symmetrically by six component equations; they are not six unrelated constraints on the six <strain> functions. For example, at a nonzero <wavevector> directed along the third axis, compatibility imposes $e_{11}=e_{22}=e_{12}=0$ while $e_{13},e_{23},e_{33}$ can arise from the three <displacement> components. This confirms <rank> three. One cannot simply discard the same arbitrary three component equations in every direction; their independence is differential, with the stated identities. On a <simply connected> domain these are the local <Saint-Venant compatibility equations> for <strain> to be a symmetric <displacement> <gradient>, while domains that are <multiply connected regions> require global closure conditions as in the screw example. This is <Saint-Venant strain compatibility and differential identities>.

= Solution
{parent=4}

Take the trace of the constitutive relation at zero <stress>. It gives $0=(2\mu+3\lambda)e_{kk}-3c\alpha(T-T_0)$. With the observed <stress>-free volume <strain> this requires
$$
\boxed{c=K=\lambda+\frac{2\mu}3.}
$$
This is the <bulk modulus>. A stable solid resists uniform compression, so $\boxed{K>0}$; equivalently the <elastic energy> of a nonzero pure dilation is positive. The <thermal strain> is isotropic, $e^{\rm th}_{ij}=\alpha(T-T_0)\delta_{ij}/3$, because $\alpha$ here is the volumetric, not linear, <thermal expansion> coefficient.

Let $\beta=K\alpha$. Using $e_{ij}=(u_{i,j}+u_{j,i})/2$, <stress> divergence is
$$
\partial_j\tau_{ij}=\mu\Delta u_i+(\lambda+\mu)\partial_i(\nabla\cdot u)-\beta\partial_iT.
$$
Zero <body force> and <equilibrium> therefore give
$$
\boxed{\mu\Delta u+(\lambda+\mu)\nabla(\nabla\cdot u)=\beta\nabla T,\qquad
\beta=\alpha(\lambda+2\mu/3).}
$$
For an applied <body force> per unit volume $b$, <equilibrium> is $\nabla\cdot\tau+b=0$, so its right-hand side becomes $\beta\nabla T-b$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{b=+\beta\nabla T}
$$
cancels the effective thermal forcing in the <displacement> equation. Per unit mass the force is $\beta\nabla T/\rho$. This sign follows from the divergence of the <thermal stress> $-\beta(T-T_0)I$, which is $-\beta\nabla T$. Cancelling that divergence is distinct from removing the <stress tensor> itself: boundary tractions and thermal compatibility still matter.

For spatially uniform heating with the entire boundary fixed, $u=0$ is the unique <equilibrium> <displacement> in a stable elastic solid, and
$$
\boxed{\tau_{ij}=-K\alpha(T-T_0)\delta_{ij}.}
$$
The <stress> is compressive <hydrostatic stress> for a temperature rise. The fixed boundary supplies the tractions that prevent <thermal expansion>. If $T$ varies spatially and no compensating <body force> is imposed, fixing the boundary alone does not generally force the interior <displacement> to vanish: one must solve the displayed forced <Navier-Cauchy equation> with $u=0$ on the boundary, then recover the <stress>. The simple uniform hydrostatic expression should not be asserted for arbitrary temperature fields. This is <uniform thermal stress in a fully constrained solid>.
