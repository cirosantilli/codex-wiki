= Solution
{parent=a}

The <infinitesimal strain tensor> is the symmetric part of the <displacement gradient tensor>:
$$
\boxed{e_{ij}=\frac12\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}+\frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i}\right).}
$$
Its diagonal entries are infinitesimal normal extensions; twice an off-diagonal entry is the corresponding engineering shear <strain>. An infinitesimal rigid rotation has an antisymmetric <displacement gradient tensor> and therefore contributes no linearized <strain>.

= Solution
{parent=i}

For a surface with unit normal $\mathbf n$, <traction> is $t_i=\tau_{ij}n_j$. Thus $\tau_{ij}$ is the $i$th force-per-area component on a plane whose positive normal is $\mathbf e_j$. Diagonal entries are normal stresses and off-diagonal entries are shear stresses, with tensile <normal stress> positive in this convention.

The symmetric <strain> has six independent components and so does the symmetric <stress>. A general <linear map> between these two six-dimensional spaces therefore has
$$
\boxed{6\times6=36\text{ independent constitutive coefficients}.}
$$
Equivalently choose the minor symmetries $C_{ijkl}=C_{jikl}=C_{ijlk}$. Components antisymmetric in the <strain> indices have no observable effect and are omitted from this representative.

A further distinction is important: if a <strain energy density> exists, $C_{ijkl}=\partial^2W/(\partial e_{ij}\partial e_{kl})$ has the major symmetry $C_{ijkl}=C_{klij}$. Then the six-by-six map is symmetric and has $6\cdot7/2=21$ independent coefficients. Thus **36 follows from the two stated tensor symmetries alone; 21 additionally uses the energy/reciprocity assumption**, as in the variational setting later in the paper. These are the <minor and major symmetries of linear elasticity>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Differentiating the <displacement> gives
$$
\mathcal E=\alpha\operatorname{diag}(1,-\nu,-\nu),\qquad\operatorname{tr}\mathcal E=\alpha(1-2\nu).
$$
The <isotropic> <stress> is diagonal, with
$$
\tau_{xx}=\alpha[\lambda(1-2\nu)+2\mu],\qquad
\tau_{yy}=\tau_{zz}=\alpha[\lambda(1-2\nu)-2\mu\nu].
$$
The two transverse <traction>-free conditions set the latter entries to zero. Hence $\lambda=2\nu(\lambda+\mu)$ and
$$
\boxed{\nu=\frac\lambda{2(\lambda+\mu)},\qquad\tau=\operatorname{diag}(E\alpha,0,0),\qquad E=\frac{\mu(3\lambda+2\mu)}{\lambda+\mu}.}
$$
Substitution shows $E=2\mu(1+\nu)$; solving for the two <Lamé parameters> gives
$$
\boxed{\mu=\frac E{2(1+\nu)},\qquad\lambda=\frac{\nu E}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)}.}
$$
To determine the allowed interval without assuming signs not given, put $K=\lambda+2\mu/3>0$. Then $\nu=(3K-2\mu)/[2(3K+\mu)]$, so
$$
1+\nu=\frac{9K}{2(3K+\mu)}>0,\qquad\frac12-\nu=\frac{3\mu}{2(3K+\mu)}>0.
$$
Therefore $\boxed{-1<\nu<1/2}$. The endpoints would require a degenerate <elastic modulus>, so they are excluded by the strict material inequalities.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The perturbation preserves the prescribed <displacement> because $\mathbf w=0$ on the boundary. <Strain> varies as $e_{ij}(\mathbf u+\alpha\mathbf w)=e_{ij}(\mathbf u)+\alpha(w_{i,j}+w_{j,i})/2$. Taylor expansion of the <strain energy density> gives
$$
U[\mathbf u+\alpha\mathbf w]-U[\mathbf u]
=\alpha\int_B\left[\tau_{ij}\frac{w_{i,j}+w_{j,i}}2-\rho g_iw_i\right]dV+O(\alpha^2).
$$
Symmetry of the <stress> makes its contraction with the two symmetric terms equal, hence
$$
\boxed{U[\mathbf u+\alpha\mathbf w]-U[\mathbf u]=\alpha\int_B(\tau_{ij}w_{i,j}-\rho g_iw_i)\,dV+O(\alpha^2).}
$$
A <minimizer> must have zero <first variation> for both signs of $\alpha$. Integrating by parts and using the zero boundary variation yields $\int_B(-\partial_j\tau_{ij}-\rho g_i)w_i\,dV=0$. The <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations>, applied to arbitrary compactly supported variations, gives
$$
\boxed{\partial_j\tau_{ij}+\rho g_i=0,\quad\text{or }\nabla\cdot\tau+\rho\mathbf g=0.}
$$
This is the static momentum equation. Fixed-<displacement> data remove the surface variation; prescribed <tractions> would instead appear through boundary work in the potential energy.

<Plane strain> sets all <strains> involving $z$ to zero, including $e_{zz}=0$, while $e_{xx}=u_x$, $e_{yy}=v_y$ and $e_{xy}=(u_y+v_x)/2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\tau=\begin{pmatrix}
(\lambda+2\mu)u_x+\lambda v_y&\mu(u_y+v_x)&0\\
\mu(u_y+v_x)&\lambda u_x+(\lambda+2\mu)v_y&0\\
0&0&\lambda(u_x+v_y)
\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
In particular, $\tau_{zz}$ generally does not vanish: the suppressed out-of-<plane strain> requires a reaction <stress>. Setting that entry to zero would instead be a plane-<stress> assumption.

= Solution
{parent=i}

The <Airy stress function> determines the in-<plane stress> through its second derivatives. If two functions give the same <stress>, their difference $B$ has $B_{xx}=B_{xy}=B_{yy}=0$. On a connected domain this implies that its gradient is constant. Therefore
$$
\boxed{A\mapsto A+ax+by+c}
$$
is exactly the local affine gauge freedom. It also preserves biharmonicity. An imposed decay condition for $A$ can fix this freedom; adding an arbitrary biharmonic function would usually change the physical <stress> and is not a <gauge transformation>.

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The source uses the forward Fourier phase $e^{ikx}$, so $\widehat{\partial_x A}=-ik\widehat A$. For a nonzero <wavenumber> put $q=|k|$. Biharmonicity becomes $(\partial_y^2-q^2)^2\widehat A=0$. The decaying solutions are $(C+Dy)e^{-qy}$. The normal-<stress> condition gives $-k^2\widehat A(k,0)=-\widehat P(k)$, hence $C=\widehat P/k^2$. The shear condition gives $\widehat A_y(k,0)=0$, so $D=qC$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\widehat A(k,y)=\frac{1+qy}{k^2}\widehat P(k)e^{-qy},\qquad k\ne0.}
$$
Its second derivatives are $\widehat{A_{xx}}=-\widehat P(1+qy)e^{-qy}$ and $\widehat{A_{yy}}=\widehat P(qy-1)e^{-qy}$. The given <strain>/<stress> inversion therefore yields
$$
2\mu\widehat v_y=\widehat P(2\nu-1-qy)e^{-qy}.
$$
Integrating in $y$ with the stated decaying-mode condition gives
$$
\boxed{2\mu\widehat v=\left(y+\frac{2(1-\nu)}q\right)\widehat P(k)e^{-qy},\qquad k\ne0.}
$$
This is the <Fourier solution of a loaded elastic half-space>.

The zero <wavenumber> needs separate treatment: these displayed divisions by $k^2$ and $|k|$ are not formulas at $k=0$. In particular a nonzero resultant $\widehat P(0)=\int P\,dx$ is incompatible with an ordinary decaying zero-mode <displacement> of the stipulated kind; two-dimensional net loading produces a logarithmic <displacement> reference issue. Balanced loads or an appropriate distributional/reference-<displacement> formulation are needed for a global transform solution. The nonzero modes and the <displacement>-gradient kernel below remain the correct formal calculation; the singular zero mode must not be silently assigned a finite value.

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Multiplication of the preceding <displacement> formula by $-ik$ gives
$$
\boxed{2\mu\widehat{v_x}=\widehat P\left[-ikye^{-qy}-2i(1-\nu)\frac{k}{q}e^{-qy}\right].}
$$
For the supplied kernels, $\widehat{f_{1,x}}=-ike^{-qy}$ and $\widehat f_2=i(k/q)e^{-qy}$. The <convolution theorem> therefore gives
$$
2\mu v_x=P*\left[yf_{1,x}-2(1-\nu)f_2\right].
$$
Since $f_{1,x}(s,y)=-2ys/[\pi(s^2+y^2)^2]$ and $f_2(s,y)=s/[\pi(s^2+y^2)]$, combine the two fractions and divide by two:
$$
\boxed{\mu v_x(x,y)=-\frac1\pi\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{(1-\nu)s^2+(2-\nu)y^2}{(s^2+y^2)^2}\,P(x-s)s\,ds.}
$$
The sign is tied to the positive forward Fourier phase used here. This is a <displacement> derivative, so an additive <displacement> reference drops out. At $y>0$ the kernel is regular near $s=0$; the usual decay/integrability of the applied load controls the tails. A boundary-limit singular integral, if wanted at $y=0$, would require its own principal-value interpretation.

= Solution

For dry <friction>, the tangential contact force opposes sliding and has limiting magnitude $\mu_fN_c$, where $N_c\geq0$ is the compressive <normal force> and $\mu_f=\tan\phi$. Sticking requires $|F|\leq\mu_fN_c$; impending sliding reaches equality.

The cohesionless <Mohr-Coulomb failure criterion> applies the same local condition on internal planes: yield begins when some plane has $|\tau_{\rm shear}|=N_c\tan\phi$. In the tension-positive Cauchy convention used here, $N_c=-N$ for a compressive plane, so
$$
\boxed{|F|\leq-N\tan\phi\quad(N\leq0),\qquad |F|=-N\tan\phi\text{ at yield}.}
$$
A cohesive material would add a positive intercept, but the later formulas in this paper are explicitly the zero-<cohesion> case. The model does not supply a tensile strength law.

= b
{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <Mohr circle> has center $C=(\tau_{xx}+\tau_{yy})/2$ on the normal-<stress> axis and radius $R=\sqrt{(\tau_{xx}-\tau_{yy})^2/4+\tau_{xy}^2}$. In the compressive regime $C<0$, yield occurs when the circle first touches either <friction> line $F=\pm(-N)\tan\phi$. The perpendicular distance of its center from either line is $|C|\sin\phi$. Tangency therefore gives $R^2=C^2\sin^2\phi$, or $C^2-R^2=C^2\cos^2\phi$. But $C^2-R^2=\tau_{xx}\tau_{yy}-\tau_{xy}^2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{4(\tau_{xx}\tau_{yy}-\tau_{xy}^2)=(\tau_{xx}+\tau_{yy})^2\cos^2\phi.}
$$
The compressive sign restriction remains part of this equivalence: squaring alone would also produce algebraic branches that do not represent cohesionless compressive <friction>.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Insert the two constitutive stresses into the radial component of the <Cauchy momentum equation>. After collecting terms, $(\lambda+2\mu)(u_r''+u_r'/r-u_r/r^2)=0$, so $u_r=Ar+B/r$. Its stresses are
$$
\tau_{rr}=2(\lambda+\mu)A-2\mu B/r^2,\qquad\tau_{\theta\theta}=2(\lambda+\mu)A+2\mu B/r^2.
$$
The far pressure gives $A=-p_\infty/[2(\lambda+\mu)]$. At the tunnel wall, compressive bracing imposes $\tau_{rr}(a)=-P$, so $B=(P-p_\infty)a^2/(2\mu)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\tau_{rr}=-p_\infty-\frac{a^2}{r^2}(P-p_\infty),\quad\tau_{\theta\theta}=-p_\infty+\frac{a^2}{r^2}(P-p_\infty),}
$$
$$
\boxed{u_r=-\frac{p_\infty r}{2(\lambda+\mu)}+\frac{(P-p_\infty)a^2}{2\mu r}.}
$$
With no shear and $\tau_{\theta\theta}=k\tau_{rr}$, divide the yield identity by the nonzero $\tau_{rr}^2$ to obtain $4k=(1+k)^2\cos^2\phi$. Solving its quadratic gives
$$
\boxed{k_+=\frac{1+\sin\phi}{1-\sin\phi},\qquad k_-=\frac{1-\sin\phi}{1+\sin\phi}.}
$$
To locate first yield, the <stress>-circle center is the constant $-p_\infty$, while its radius is $|P-p_\infty|a^2/r^2$. The radius is maximal at $r=a$, so that is where it first meets the <friction> envelope as the bracing departs from the hydrostatic value. At the wall $\tau_{rr}=-P$ and $\tau_{\theta\theta}=P-2p_\infty$; hence $k=(2p_\infty-P)/P$ and
$$
\boxed{P=\frac{2p_\infty}{1+k}.}
$$
For lowering bracing from $P=p_\infty$, the hoop <stress> becomes more compressive, so $k=k_+>1$ and $\boxed{P_{\rm crit}=p_\infty(1-\sin\phi)}$. Increasing bracing instead selects $k_-$ and reaches the other threshold $p_\infty(1+\sin\phi)$. Stating the loading direction removes any ambiguity between the two reciprocal <stress> ratios. These conclusions describe first yield of the elastic field, not the later plastic-zone solution.
