= Solution
{parent=a}

The <deformation gradient> is the <derivative> of the <deformation map> with respect to the <reference configuration> coordinates, at fixed time:
$$
\boxed{F_{iA}(X,t)=\frac{\partial x_i}{\partial X_A}(X,t),\qquad dx=F\,dX.}
$$
Its <determinant> $J=\det F$ is the local ratio of current to reference <volume>: $dv=J\,dV$ for an orientation-preserving <deformation in continuum mechanics>. Thus <elastic incompressibility> is the pointwise constraint $\boxed{J=1}$, rather than a requirement that each individual <principal stretch> be one.

= b
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Choose the outward <unit normal> $n$ to the <boundary> of the chosen current material subbody. The <traction> $t(x,n)$ is the contact <force> exerted on that subbody per unit current <area> at the <boundary> point. Reversing the subbody side reverses $n$ and $t$.

By <Cauchy's stress theorem>, the dependence on $n$ is linear. With the usual column-vector convention the <Cauchy stress tensor> satisfies
$$
\boxed{t(x,n)=\tau(x)n,\qquad t_i=\tau_{ij}n_j.}
$$
The same point has different <traction> vectors on differently oriented planes, but a single <Cauchy stress tensor> encodes all of them. In the absence of body couples, <conservation of angular momentum> makes $\tau$ symmetric.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

In the <current configuration>, <balance of linear momentum> is the <Cauchy momentum equation>
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{div}_x\tau+\rho b=\rho\left(\partial_t v+(v\cdot\nabla_x)v\right).}
$$
Here $\tau$ is the <Cauchy stress tensor>, $\rho$ is current <mass density>, $b$ is <body force> per unit <mass>, and $v(x,t)$ is the <velocity> of the material particle currently at $x$. The expression in parentheses is the <material acceleration>. The <divergence> is taken in current spatial coordinates, with $(\operatorname{div}\tau)_i=\partial_j\tau_{ij}$.

Equivalently the right side is $\rho\partial_t^2x(X,t)$ evaluated at the particle, because this is the same <material acceleration> in <Lagrangian coordinates>. For the subsequent static inflation with negligible <body force>, the equation reduces to $\operatorname{div}\tau=0$.

= d
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

Write $B=A+H$ for the reference outer radius and $b=a+h$ for the current outer radius. The inner material normal is $-e_r$. A fluid <pressure> $P$ applies <traction> $-Pn=Pe_r$ there, so the radial <Cauchy stress tensor> component must be negative:
$$
\boxed{t_r(a)=-P,\qquad t_r(b)=0.}
$$
The second condition uses zero external gauge <pressure>. Both faces also have zero shear <traction>; the axisymmetric diagonal <stress> field satisfies those conditions automatically. The axial constraint is <plane strain>, so supports may supply an axial reaction; it does not imply zero axial <stress>.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=e}

For the axisymmetric <deformation map>, take $\theta=\Theta$, $z=Z$, and $r=r(R)$. A reference cylindrical shell of unit axial length has <volume> $2\pi R\,dR$, and the corresponding current shell has <volume> $2\pi r\,dr$. <Elastic incompressibility> therefore gives $r\,dr=R\,dR$.

Integrating from the inner surface, where $r(A)=a$, gives $r^2-a^2=R^2-A^2$. The positive radius is consequently
$$
\boxed{r(R)=\sqrt{R^2+a^2-A^2},\qquad A\leq R\leq B.}
$$
The outer surface is not an additional independent unknown: $\boxed{b=\sqrt{B^2+a^2-A^2}}$, and hence $h=b-a$.

= f
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=f}

Use orthonormal cylindrical bases in the <reference configuration> and <current configuration>. The <deformation gradient> maps a radial increment $dR$ to $r'dR$, an azimuthal arc $R\,d\Theta$ to $r\,d\Theta$, and an axial increment $dZ$ to itself. Therefore
$$
\boxed{F=r' e_r\otimes E_R+\frac rR e_\theta\otimes E_\Theta+e_z\otimes E_Z.}
$$
Differentiating the radius relation gives $r'=R/r$. Its three <principal stretches> are
$$
\boxed{\lambda_r=\frac Rr,\qquad\lambda_\theta=\frac rR,\qquad\lambda_z=1.}
$$
Their product is one, as required by <elastic incompressibility>. The symbols called “strains” in the source are these stretch ratios; engineering <strain> would instead subtract one. The axial stretch is one because this is <plane strain>.

= g
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=g}

The multiplier enforcing <elastic incompressibility> contributes an arbitrary hydrostatic <stress>. Denote it by $q(r)$ to distinguish it from the applied <pressure> $P$. For the <neo-Hookean solid>, the <Cauchy stress tensor> is $\tau=-qI+\mu FF^T$. Thus its principal components are
$$
\boxed{t_r=-q+\mu\lambda_r^2=-q+\mu\frac{R^2}{r^2},\qquad
t_\theta=-q+\mu\lambda_\theta^2=-q+\mu\frac{r^2}{R^2}.}
$$
The axial component is $t_z=-q+\mu$, despite the unit axial stretch. The quantity $q$ is a <Lagrange multiplier> determined together with equilibrium and the <traction> conditions; it is not obtained by differentiating the reduced stretch <energy> while ignoring its constraint.

In particular the <stress> difference eliminates this multiplier:
$$
\boxed{t_\theta-t_r=\mu\left(\frac{r^2}{R^2}-\frac{R^2}{r^2}\right).}
$$

= h
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=h}

The radial equilibrium equation gives $dt_r/dr=(t_\theta-t_r)/r$. Set $C=a^2-A^2$, so that $R^2=r^2-C$. Substitution of the <neo-Hookean solid> <stress> difference eliminates both $q$ and $t_\theta$:
$$
\boxed{\frac{dt_r}{dr}=\frac\mu r\left[\frac{r^2}{r^2-C}-\frac{r^2-C}{r^2}\right]
=\frac{\mu C(2r^2-C)}{r^3(r^2-C)},\qquad a<r<b.}
$$
This is a first-order <ordinary differential equation> for the radial <stress>, parametrized by the unknown inner radius. For inflation $C>0$, its right side is positive, so $t_r$ increases from the negative inner <pressure> to zero at the outer surface. The denominator stays positive because $r^2-C=R^2\geq A^2>0$.

= i
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}

Integrate the radial equilibrium equation across the thickness, retaining the <integral> rather than evaluating its <antiderivative>. The <traction> conditions give $t_r(b)-t_r(a)=P$. Hence $a$ must satisfy
$$
\boxed{P=\mu\int_a^{\sqrt{(A+H)^2+a^2-A^2}}
\frac1r\left[\frac{r^2}{r^2-a^2+A^2}-\frac{r^2-a^2+A^2}{r^2}\right]\,dr.}
$$
All endpoints and coefficients here depend only on $a$ and the specified material and reference geometry. This is the required implicit <pressure>–radius relation for <inflation and extension of an incompressible tube> with unit axial stretch. In particular $a=A$ gives zero integrand and $P=0$. Once an admissible root is selected, the radial <stress> follows by integrating from the outer face, $t_r(r)=-\int_r^b(dt_r/ds)\,ds$, and the remaining <stress> components follow from the <constitutive equation>.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Substituting the sinusoidal <plane wave> into the <Navier-Cauchy equation> gives $\Delta u=-u$, $u_{tt}=-c^2u$, and $\nabla\operatorname{div}u=-k(k\cdot a)\sin(k\cdot X-ct)$ because $|k|=1$. Equality of the amplitudes is therefore
$$
\boxed{\rho c^2a=\mu a+(\lambda+\mu)k(k\cdot a).}
$$
This is an <eigenvalue problem> for the <acoustic tensor> $\mu I+(\lambda+\mu)k\otimes k$. Choosing a nonzero <amplitude> parallel to $k$ gives $\rho c^2=\lambda+2\mu$; choosing any nonzero <amplitude> perpendicular to $k$ gives $\rho c^2=\mu$. For positive <mass density> and stable <Lamé parameters>, both squared speeds are positive. These choices construct nonzero solutions explicitly, proving existence.

= b
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Resolve each <amplitude> by the <orthogonal projections>
$$
a_L=(a\cdot k)k,\qquad a_T=a-(a\cdot k)k.
$$
The <acoustic tensor> acts by $\lambda+2\mu$ on the longitudinal line and by $\mu$ on its perpendicular plane. Thus
$$
\boxed{c_L=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda+2\mu}{\rho}},\qquad c_T=\sqrt{\frac\mu\rho}.}
$$
The longitudinal component is a <longitudinal wave>, while the two independent transverse polarizations are <transverse waves>. Each projection obeys its own <wave equation>, and <superposition> adds the corresponding solutions.

A general planar disturbance is therefore a sum of the two types. For a single sinusoid with a common speed, both projections cannot be nonzero unless the two speeds coincide: projecting the <eigenvalue equation> gives $(\rho c^2-\lambda-2\mu)a_L=0$ and $(\rho c^2-\mu)a_T=0$. This distinguishes <superposition> of <waves> with different speeds from a mixed polarization traveling rigidly at one speed.

= c
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Divide the <Navier-Cauchy equation> by <mass density>. Since $\mu/\rho=c_T^2$ and $(\lambda+\mu)/\rho=c_L^2-c_T^2$, it becomes
$$
\boxed{u_{tt}=c_T^2\Delta u+(c_L^2-c_T^2)\nabla\operatorname{div}u.}
$$
Equivalently, using the <vector calculus> identity $\Delta u=\nabla\operatorname{div}u-\nabla\times(\nabla\times u)$,
$$
\boxed{u_{tt}=c_L^2\nabla\operatorname{div}u-c_T^2\nabla\times(\nabla\times u).}
$$
The <divergence> and <curl> consequently propagate at $c_L$ and $c_T$, respectively.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

Use the complex <amplitude> and take its <real part> at the end; the equations are linear with real coefficients. Let $M=\lambda+2\mu$, $\omega=kc$, and $v=(A,B)^T$. The differential operators on this <exponential function> are $\partial_X=ik$, $\partial_Y=-b$, and $\partial_t=-i\omega$. Substitution gives
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
\mu b^2-Mk^2+\rho k^2c^2&-i(\lambda+\mu)kb\\
-i(\lambda+\mu)kb&Mb^2-\mu k^2+\rho k^2c^2
\end{pmatrix}\binom AB=0.
$$
The <determinant> factors as
$$
\mu M\left[b^2-k^2+\frac{\rho k^2c^2}{M}\right]
\left[b^2-k^2+\frac{\rho k^2c^2}{\mu}\right].
$$
Consequently choose the positive decay roots
$$
\boxed{b_1=k\sqrt{1-c^2/c_L^2},\qquad b_2=k\sqrt{1-c^2/c_T^2}.}
$$
The <Rayleigh wave decay exponents> must both give strict decay into the half-space. A genuine nonzero <Rayleigh wave> combines both, as the free-surface equations below also show. Thus $b_1,b_2>0$ requires $\boxed{0<c<\min(c_T,c_L)}$. In a stable ordinary isotropic solid $c_T<c_L$, so it is slower even than the <transverse wave>. The roots depend on the candidate speed as well as the material constants and <wavenumber>; the speed is fixed by the <boundary condition>.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=e}

For the $b_1$ root the <elastic wave polarization> is parallel to the complex <derivative> vector $(ik,-b_1)$, giving
$$
\boxed{B_1=\frac{i b_1}{k}A_1.}
$$
For the $b_2$ root the field has zero <divergence>, $ikA_2-b_2B_2=0$, giving
$$
\boxed{B_2=\frac{ik}{b_2}A_2.}
$$
These relations also follow by inserting each decay root into either row of the <matrix>. The complete decaying <displacement field> has complex components
$$
U(Y)=A_1e^{-b_1Y}+A_2e^{-b_2Y},\qquad
V(Y)=\frac{ib_1}{k}A_1e^{-b_1Y}+\frac{ik}{b_2}A_2e^{-b_2Y},
$$
multiplied by $e^{ik(X-ct)}$, followed by the <real part>. The first constituent is irrotational and the second is divergence-free; both are needed to cancel the two surface <tractions>.

= Solution
{parent=f}

The material occupies $Y\geq0$, so its outward <unit normal> at the surface is $n=-e_Y$. A free surface has zero <traction>, $\tau n=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\tau_{XY}(X,0,t)=0,\qquad\tau_{YY}(X,0,t)=0,\qquad\tau_{ZY}(X,0,t)=0.}
$$
The third condition is automatic for the two-dimensional <displacement field> with zero third component and no dependence on the third coordinate. The first two impose zero shear and normal <stress> at the free surface.

{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=g}

Write the in-plane <displacement field> as $(u,v)$. The <infinitesimal strain tensor> gives $2e_{XY}=u_Y+v_X$, $e_{YY}=v_Y$, and $\operatorname{tr}e=u_X+v_Y$. The two nontrivial zero-<traction> conditions become
$$
\boxed{u_Y+v_X=0,\qquad\lambda u_X+(\lambda+2\mu)v_Y=0\quad(Y=0).}
$$
Equivalently for the <complex amplitudes> they are $U'(0)+ikV(0)=0$ and $ik\lambda U(0)+(\lambda+2\mu)V'(0)=0$. Because the <constitutive equation> is real and linear, vanishing of these complex <amplitudes> enforces the conditions at every position and time.

= h
{parent=2}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=h}

Substituting both profiles into the shear <traction> condition gives
$$
2b_1A_1+\frac{b_2^2+k^2}{b_2}A_2=0.
$$
For the normal <traction>, use $Mb_1^2=Mk^2-\rho k^2c^2$ and $b_2^2+k^2=k^2(2-c^2/c_T^2)$. Removing the common nonzero factors yields the second equation. Thus
$$
\boxed{\begin{pmatrix}
2b_1&(b_2^2+k^2)/b_2\\
(b_2^2+k^2)/k&2k
\end{pmatrix}\binom{A_1}{A_2}=0.}
$$
A nonzero <displacement field> requires the <determinant> to vanish:
$$
\boxed{(b_2^2+k^2)^2=4k^2b_1b_2.}
$$
Let $s=c^2/c_T^2$ and $\eta=c_T^2/c_L^2$. Cancelling $k^4$ gives the <Rayleigh secular equation>
$$
\boxed{(2-s)^2=4\sqrt{1-s}\sqrt{1-\eta s},\qquad0<s<1.}
$$
Squaring and excluding the zero root gives $s^3-8s^2+(24-16\eta)s-16(1-\eta)=0$; any candidate must still satisfy the unsquared equation and the decay inequalities. For stable isotropic elasticity $0<\eta<3/4$, this cubic has one root in $(0,1)$: its values at zero and one have opposite signs, and its strictly negative second <derivative> on this interval prevents a second crossing back to a positive final value. Both sides of the unsquared equation are positive there, so this root is valid.

The root $s=0$ of the squared equation is not a propagating surface <wave>: at $c=0$, the two roots coincide, and the null combination $A_2=-A_1$ in this representation produces zero <displacement field>. As a numerical example, $\lambda=\mu$ gives $\eta=1/3$ and $c\simeq0.9194c_T$. The surface-wave speed is independent of $k$ in this homogeneous half-space model.

= Solution

Let $s$ be <arc length>, so <inextensibility> makes $r'=d_3$ a <unit tangent vector>. The <curvature> is $\kappa=\theta'$, hence $m'=EI\theta''e_z$ for uniform <bending stiffness>. <Balance of linear momentum> in static equilibrium gives a constant resultant $n=Fe_x+Ge_y$.

Taking the <cross product> directly,
$$
d_3\times n=(\cos\theta\,e_x+\sin\theta\,e_y)\times(Fe_x+Ge_y)
=(G\cos\theta-F\sin\theta)e_z.
$$
The scalar component of <conservation of angular momentum> therefore becomes
$$
\boxed{EI\theta''+G\cos\theta-F\sin\theta=0.}
$$
Together with $x'=\cos\theta$ and $y'=\sin\theta$, this <Euler elastica> equation determines the centerline once the appropriate <boundary conditions> are supplied.

{parent=3}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Put $P=-F>0$. With $G=0$, the <Euler elastica> equation is $EI\theta''+P\sin\theta=0$. <Linearization> about the straight configuration gives $EI\theta''+P\theta=0$. Under the <boundary conditions> explicitly stated, $\theta(0)=\theta(L)=0$, a nonzero solution requires
$$
\boxed{P_n=EI\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2,\qquad
\theta_n(s)=C\sin\frac{n\pi s}{L},\qquad n=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
Thus **with only the stated <boundary conditions>**, the first critical compressive force is $\boxed{F_c=-\pi^2EI/L^2}$. The corresponding linear transverse <displacement field>, with $y(0)=0$, is
$$
y_n(s)=\frac{CL}{n\pi}\left(1-\cos\frac{n\pi s}{L}\right),
$$
and $x(s)=s$ to first order. These are the successive small-amplitude <buckling> modes. The nonlinear branches retain the <simple pendulum> equation; if the angular amplitude is $\theta_m$ and $m=\sin(\theta_m/2)$, fitting $n$ half-periods into the rod gives $P=4EI n^2K(m)^2/L^2$, which tends to $P_n$ as $m\downarrow0$. Here $K$ is the <complete elliptic integral of the first kind>, with $m$ its modulus, not its squared parameter.

**<Endpoint position compatibility for the Euler elastica> adds a condition when fully fixed clamps have equal transverse height.** If the word “clamped” also imposes $y(L)=y(0)$, then $\int_0^L\theta_n\,ds=0$. With $G=0$, this retains only even $n=2j$, yielding
$$
\boxed{P_j=4j^2\pi^2EI/L^2,\qquad P_{\rm first}=4\pi^2EI/L^2.}
$$
The same exclusion is exact on the nonlinear branches: integrating the elastica equation gives $P\int\sin\theta\,ds=-EI[\theta'(L)-\theta'(0)]$, and an odd number of half-periods reverses the nonzero endpoint slope. Hence odd $n$ changes the endpoint height. The conventional fixed-clamp first <Euler buckling> load is the second boxed value, whereas the source's explicitly listed <boundary conditions> alone give the first. Additional clamped modes with a nonzero transverse reaction require releasing the imposed $G=0$. Load-controlled buckling also permits axial shortening; an exactly inextensible rod whose endpoint separation is fixed equal to its full length has no room to buckle.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Set $\ell=\sqrt{EI/F}$. The <Euler elastica> equation becomes $\theta''=\ell^{-2}\sin\theta$. Multiplying by $\theta'$ gives the <first integral>
$$
\frac{EI}{2}(\theta')^2+F\cos\theta=C.
$$
Straight asymptotic ends require $\theta'\to0$ and a horizontal tangent, so $C=F$. This is the <separatrix> of a <simple pendulum> with its angle shifted by $\pi$. A nonconstant solution moves from $\theta=0$ to $2\pi$, with the same physical tangent at both ends, taking infinite <arc length> to approach either limit. One explicit representative is
$$
\boxed{\theta(s)=4\arctan e^{s/\ell}.}
$$
Using $\cos\theta=1-2\operatorname{sech}^2(s/\ell)$ and $\sin\theta=-2\operatorname{sech}(s/\ell)\tanh(s/\ell)$ and integrating $r'=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ gives the <tensile elastica loop>
$$
\boxed{x(s)=s-2\ell\tanh(s/\ell),\qquad y(s)=2\ell\operatorname{sech}(s/\ell).}
$$
This solution is nontrivial, tends to horizontal straight tails, and has finite excess <bending energy>. It is also necessarily self-intersecting. Indeed $y$ is even and $x$ is odd. For $z>0$, let $f(z)=z-2\tanh z$. Near zero $f(z)<0$, while $f(2)>0$, so there is a root $z_*\in(0,2)$. At the two distinct parameters $s=\pm\ell z_*$, both points have $x=0$ and the same $y>0$. Numerically $z_*\simeq1.91501$.

Any nonconstant smooth solution with the same straight asymptotic conditions has this <first integral>. Between consecutive horizontal orientations, separating variables gives the displayed solution up to translation, reflection, and reversal. It cannot pass through a straight equilibrium at a finite parameter, by uniqueness of the <ordinary differential equation>. Thus all such nontrivial branches have the same loop crossing. This is an ideal zero-thickness rod solution; a physical rod with self-contact excluded cannot realize the crossing without adding contact forces.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Take the clamp at $s=0$ with $x(0)=y(0)=\theta(0)=0$. An end <force> without an applied end moment gives the free-end condition $\theta'(1)=0$. With $F=0$, the <Euler elastica> equation is $EI\theta''+G\cos\theta=0$. For $|G|/(EI)\ll1$, $\theta$ is small, so the leading equation is
$$
EI\theta''=-G.
$$
Integrating with the two <boundary conditions> gives
$$
\boxed{\theta(s)=\frac G{EI}\left(s-\frac{s^2}{2}\right).}
$$
To first order, $x'=\cos\theta=1+O(G^2)$ and $y'=\sin\theta=\theta+O(G^3)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{x(s)=s+O(G^2),\qquad
y(s)=\frac{G}{6EI}s^2(3-s)+O(G^3),\quad0\leq s\leq1.}
$$
Equivalently $y(x)=Gx^2(3-x)/(6EI)$ at leading order, the familiar <cantilever> response. Because $G<0$, the deflection is downward. The free-end <displacement field> and slope are $y(1)=G/(3EI)$ and $\theta(1)=G/(2EI)$. The remainder notation holds with fixed positive $EI$ and nondimensional unit rod length.
