= Solution
{parent=1}

An <eigenvalue> of an <observable> in a known corresponding <eigenstate> is a simple example satisfying the <Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen criterion of reality>: its value is predictable with certainty. For example, a <spin angular momentum> component in a prepared spin-up eigenstate has a definite predicted value. For a distant system, <Albert Einstein>, <Boris Podolsky> and <Nathan Rosen> invoked locality: a measurement performed on a sufficiently separated, noninteracting partner should not disturb that system. In the relativistic version here, the measurement events have <spacelike separation>.

The <Pauli matrices> obey $[\sigma_z,\sigma_x]=2i\sigma_y$, so
$$
\boxed{[S_z,S_x]=i\hbar S_y\ne0.}
$$
Their nondegenerate <eigenstates> are incompatible: an $S_z$ eigenstate has equal probabilities for the two $S_x$ outcomes, and conversely. Thus no single <quantum state> has both components sharply determined. The <Robertson uncertainty relation> gives $\Delta S_z\Delta S_x\ge\hbar|\langle S_y\rangle|/2$, but its right-hand side may be zero; that alone is not a proof that simultaneous zero uncertainties exist. The absence of a common eigenstate supplies the stronger conclusion for this spin-half pair.

For the specified <Bell state>, Bob obtains $+\hbar/2$ or $-\hbar/2$ with probability $1/2$ when he measures $S_z$. Conditional on his result, Alice obtains the same sign with certainty if she measures $S_z$. Under the EPR locality premise, this certainty supplies an element of reality for her $S_z$; it is a conditional prediction after Bob learns his own random result, not a premeasurement prediction of its sign.

The normalized $S_x$ eigenstates are $|+\rangle=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$ and $|-\rangle=(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$. Expanding their <tensor products> gives
$$
|++\rangle+|--\rangle=|00\rangle+|11\rangle.
$$
Consequently the same <Bell state> has equal, perfectly correlated $S_x$ outcomes. Bob's two outcomes again each have probability $1/2$, and the same locality-and-certainty argument applies separately to Alice's $S_x$.

The disputed step is to combine predictions conditional on two incompatible choices into two simultaneous, measurement-independent pre-existing values. Quantum theory provides the two conditional ensembles, not a joint sharp assignment of $S_x$ and $S_z$. This is <quantum steering>. Bob cannot measure both incompatible observables sharply on the same member of the pair. Nor does conditional steering allow signalling: ignoring his result leaves Alice's <reduced density matrix> equal to $I/2$ for either measurement choice, by the <no-communication theorem>.

To test the stronger claim of a <local hidden-variable theory>, Alice needs several correlation settings, not just perfect agreement along the two common axes. For the <CHSH inequality>, choose observables with outcomes $\pm1$:
$$
A_0=\sigma_z,\quad A_1=\sigma_x,\qquad
B_0=\frac{\sigma_z+\sigma_x}{\sqrt2},\quad
B_1=\frac{\sigma_z-\sigma_x}{\sqrt2}.
$$
In the given <Bell state>, $\langle\sigma_z\otimes\sigma_z\rangle=\langle\sigma_x\otimes\sigma_x\rangle=1$ and the crossed correlations vanish. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathcal S=\langle A_0B_0\rangle+\langle A_0B_1\rangle+\langle A_1B_0\rangle-\langle A_1B_1\rangle=2\sqrt2,}
$$
exceeding the local bound $|\mathcal S|\le2$. The bound assumes <Bell local causality> and <measurement independence>, with the appropriate treatment of sampling and settings.

Experimental <Bell tests> support violations rather than that local assignment. The <Aspect switching experiment> changed analyzer settings during separated photon flights, while leaving sampling and switching limitations. By the time of this examination, the <Delft electron-spin Bell test> had used electron spins at stations $1.3\,\mathrm{km}$ apart, efficient spin readout and rapid random basis choices. The combination addressed both detection and locality loopholes, providing evidence against the tested local model class.<Experimental loophole-free violation of a Bell inequality by Bas Hensen and others>[2015 abstract: separated electron spins, efficient readout and fast random settings]{ref}

This does **not** prove that every conceivable notion of reality is false, or that noncommutation alone rules out every hidden-variable theory. A nonlocal theory can evade the local premise. What Alice can challenge is Bob's claim that locality, measurement-independent simultaneous values and all observed correlations can be retained together. An ordinary measurement disturbance at Bob's lab is not by itself a local explanation of Alice's conditional ensemble.

Use the thin <singular value decomposition> $a=UdV^\dagger$, with $U^\dagger U=I_N$, $V$ unitary and $d=\operatorname{diag}(d_k)$ nonnegative. Expanding its entries gives
$$
a_{ij}=\sum_kU_{ik}d_kV_{jk}^*,\qquad
|\psi\rangle=\sum_kd_k\left(\sum_iU_{ik}|i_A\rangle\right)\left(\sum_jV_{jk}^*|j_B\rangle\right).
$$
The columns of $U$ and of $V^*$ are orthonormal, establishing the <Schmidt decomposition>. **Bob's basis uses the complex-conjugated columns of $V$.** Omitting that conjugation would not reproduce the coefficient matrix. The squared <Schmidt coefficients> are the <eigenvalues> of $a^\dagger a$; equivalently $d^2=V^\dagger a^\dagger aV$ and $d=U^\dagger aV$. The two <reduced density matrices> are $aa^\dagger$ and $(a^\dagger a)^T$, which have the same nonzero eigenvalues.

The printed coefficient $0.41231$ slightly rounds a normalized state: each squared norm is $0.9999995361$. Normalize before calculating an entropy. For the two states, the resulting <Gram matrices> are approximately
$$
G_\Phi=\begin{pmatrix}0.6200003&0.2649241-0.2661551i\\0.2649241+0.2661551i&0.3799997\end{pmatrix},\quad
G_\Psi=\begin{pmatrix}0.6200003&-0.0649240+0.1461551i\\-0.0649240-0.1461551i&0.3799997\end{pmatrix}.
$$
For a trace-one two-dimensional <Hermitian matrix> with diagonal entry $u$ and off-diagonal entry $c$, its eigenvalues are
$$
\lambda_{0,1}=\frac12\left(1\pm\sqrt{(2u-1)^2+4|c|^2}\right).
$$
Sort them decreasingly and take square roots:
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
& (d_0,d_1)&(\lambda_0,\lambda_1)&S(\rho_A)\text{ in bits}\\\hline
\Phi&(0.945641,0.325211)&(0.894238,0.105762)&0.487000\\
\Psi&(0.836625,0.547776)&(0.699941,0.300059)&0.881363
\end{array}}
$$
The <Von Neumann entropy> is $S=-\lambda_0\log_2\lambda_0-\lambda_1\log_2\lambda_1$. **$\Psi$ is more entangled:** its Schmidt weights are closer to equal and its reduced-state entropy is larger. Neither state is a product state or maximally entangled on its two-dimensional support.

By <Nielsen's pure-state conversion theorem>, deterministic <LOCC> conversion requires the input vector of squared <Schmidt coefficients> to be majorized by the output vector. Here $0.699941<0.894238$ and both sums are one, so $\lambda(\Psi)\prec\lambda(\Phi)$. Therefore **$\Psi\to\Phi$ is possible deterministically; $\Phi\to\Psi$ is not.** Entanglement can be discarded by local operations but cannot increase deterministically for these pure inputs. Probabilistic concentration is a separate question and is allowed below.<Conditions for a class of entanglement transformations by Michael A. Nielsen>[1999 primary theorem: deterministic pure-state conversion and majorization]{ref}

There is a dimensional qualification in the proposed measurement. Write $P=|0'_A\rangle\langle0'_A|+|1'_A\rangle\langle1'_A|$. The two supplied <measurement operators> obey
$$
M_1^\dagger M_1+M_2^\dagger M_2=P.
$$
Thus their effects form a <POVM on the occupied support>, where $P$ is the identity. Alice's full space is three-dimensional, so these two operators alone do not sum to $I_3$. A full-space completion is $M_3=|2'_A\rangle\langle2'_A|$. It has zero probability on either input, so it changes none of the requested outcomes. Strictly, the POVM consists of the effects $E_r=M_r^\dagger M_r$; the specified $M_r$ additionally fix a <quantum instrument> and therefore the conditional states.

In the appropriate <Schmidt basis> for either input, write $|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{\lambda_0}|00\rangle+\sqrt{\lambda_1}|11\rangle$. The two outcomes have probabilities
$$
\boxed{p_1=\lambda_0\sin^2\theta+\lambda_1\cos^2\theta,\qquad
p_2=\lambda_0\cos^2\theta+\lambda_1\sin^2\theta,}
$$
and normalized conditional states
$$
|\psi_1\rangle=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_0}\sin\theta|00\rangle+\sqrt{\lambda_1}\cos\theta|11\rangle}{\sqrt{p_1}},\qquad
|\psi_2\rangle=\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_0}\cos\theta|00\rangle+\sqrt{\lambda_1}\sin\theta|11\rangle}{\sqrt{p_2}}.
$$
These formulas give both outcomes for each state by substituting its row of the table. For example,
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
&p_1&p_2\\\hline
\Phi&0.894238\sin^2\theta+0.105762\cos^2\theta&1-p_1\\
\Psi&0.699941\sin^2\theta+0.300059\cos^2\theta&1-p_1
\end{array}
$$
The two inputs have different Schmidt bases; the primed measurement basis is adapted separately to the state, as the question specifies, not one common unexamined laboratory basis.

For the $M_1$ outcome to be a <maximally entangled state>, its two amplitudes must be equal. Hence
$$
\tan\theta=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_0}},\qquad
\sin^2\theta=\lambda_1,\qquad
\boxed{p_1^{\rm Bell}=2\lambda_0\lambda_1.}
$$
The requested numerical results are
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c}
&\theta\text{ in radians}&p_1^{\rm Bell}\\\hline
\Phi&0.331235&0.189153\\
\Psi&0.579704&0.420047
\end{array}}
$$
Thus **$\Psi$ has the larger success probability**, consistent with its greater initial entanglement. The $M_2$ branch cannot be balanced in the allowed angle range for either nonmaximal input: it would require $\tan\theta=\sqrt{\lambda_0/\lambda_1}>1$.

The success probabilities belong to this particular <binary Schmidt filter>. They are not the optimum over every possible <quantum instrument>. The diagonal success operator $\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{\lambda_1/\lambda_0},1)$ instead gives the standard <single-copy entanglement concentration> probability $2\lambda_1$. Postselection can increase entanglement in the retained branch, while the outcome-averaged entanglement cannot increase under <LOCC>.

A <weak measurement> couples an <observable> only weakly to a meter, so the pointer distributions associated with its different eigenvalues overlap strongly. One trial supplies little information and a small disturbance; an ensemble is needed to estimate a small mean shift. It is not an exact determination of a previously hidden eigenvalue with no disturbance.

For pointer position $q$ and conjugate momentum $p$, a short <von Neumann measurement interaction> has unitary $U=\exp(-igA\otimes p/\hbar)$. An eigenvalue $a$ translates the pointer by $ga$. The weak regime requires those translations to be small relative to the pointer width. Without postselection the first-order mean displacement is $g\langle A\rangle$ for an initially uncorrelated, suitably centered meter.

If the system is prepared in $|i\rangle$ and postselected in $|f\rangle$, the unnormalized meter state is
$$
\langle f|U|i\rangle|\varphi\rangle\simeq\langle f|i\rangle\left(1-\frac{ig}{\hbar}A_w p\right)|\varphi\rangle,\qquad
\boxed{A_w=\frac{\langle f|A|i\rangle}{\langle f|i\rangle}.}
$$
For a real centered Gaussian pointer with no position–momentum correlation, the conditional position shift is $g\operatorname{Re}A_w$; the imaginary part affects the conjugate momentum distribution. The <weak value> can be complex or have a real part outside the observable's eigenvalue interval, because it is a conditional transition-amplitude ratio, not an eigenvalue.

An example apparatus uses photon polarization as the two-level system, a weak polarization-dependent transverse displacement as the interaction, and an output polarizer for <postselection>. Repeated position detections estimate the selected beam's displacement. Nearly orthogonal selection can make the conditional shift large, but also reduces the accepted sample and eventually invalidates the first-order expansion. The original weak-value proposal emphasized preselection, a weak coupling and postselection, rather than an anomalous result from an ordinary sharp spin measurement.<Weak values by Yakir Aharonov, David Z. Albert and Lev Vaidman>[1988 primary paper: preselected and postselected spin ensemble and conditional weak value]{ref}

Weak coupling limits <quantum measurement backaction> per trial; it does not abolish the information–disturbance tradeoff. Repeating weak observations can accumulate substantial backaction, and discarding rejected trials cannot automatically improve the information available per prepared system. Conditional pointer amplification and a statistically improved parameter estimate are distinct claims.

<Quantum teleportation> transfers an unknown <qubit> state using a previously shared <Bell pair>, local operations and two classical bits. It requires no description of the unknown complex amplitudes and produces no extra copy.

Let the input be $|\chi\rangle_S=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$, and let Alice and Bob share $|\Phi^+\rangle_{AB}$. Alice applies a <controlled-NOT gate> from $S$ to $A$, then a <Hadamard gate> to $S$. Expanding the circuit gives the <Bell-basis teleportation identity> in computational-output form:
$$
|\chi\rangle_S|\Phi^+\rangle_{AB}\longmapsto\frac12\sum_{a,b\in\{0,1\}}|a\rangle_S|b\rangle_A\,X^bZ^a|\chi\rangle_B.
$$
Her <projective measurement> yields each bit pair $(a,b)$ with probability $1/4$. She sends the two classical bits, and Bob applies **$Z^aX^b$** to recover $|\chi\rangle$. The order is the inverse of the displayed conditional operator; interchanging these Pauli corrections changes only an irrelevant global phase when both bits are one.

Before receiving the bits, Bob has
$$
\rho_B=\frac14\sum_{a,b}X^bZ^a|\chi\rangle\langle\chi|Z^aX^b=\frac I2.
$$
Thus teleportation respects the <no-communication theorem> and cannot transmit the usable unknown state faster than the classical message. Alice's input has been measured, and the shared entanglement resource has been consumed, in agreement with the <no-cloning theorem>. The protocol transfers the state, not the material particle initially carrying it.

A physical implementation must distribute and preserve an entangled pair, implement a Bell-basis measurement and transmit its result to a controllable output system. Imperfect entanglement, lost outcomes and noisy corrections reduce <quantum fidelity>; a heralded probabilistic experiment must report its success probability separately from conditional fidelity. Ideal deterministic teleportation assumes every Bell outcome can be identified and corrected.

<Quantum error correction> encodes a <logical qubit> into a larger <Hilbert space>, diagnoses a specified error family with an <error syndrome>, and reverses it without learning the protected logical amplitudes. It must preserve arbitrary superpositions, not just distinguish two classical codewords.

The <bit-flip repetition code> encodes
$$
\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle\longmapsto\alpha|000\rangle+\beta|111\rangle.
$$
Measure the commuting <stabilizer generators> $Z_1Z_2$ and $Z_2Z_3$ using ancillary qubits. Their outcomes reveal the parity relations, with the <three-qubit bit-flip syndrome table>
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{error}&(Z_1Z_2,Z_2Z_3)&\text{recovery}\\\hline
I&(+1,+1)&I\\
X_1&(-1,+1)&X_1\\
X_2&(-1,-1)&X_2\\
X_3&(+1,-1)&X_3
\end{array}}
$$
Both codewords have the same parity checks, so the syndrome does not reveal $\alpha$ or $\beta$. For example, the $X_2$ error produces $\alpha|010\rangle+\beta|101\rangle$; applying $X_2$ restores the encoded superposition. A coherent combination of correctable errors is also recoverable after the syndrome is recorded in an ancilla, by linearity.

This three-qubit code corrects one bit flip, not every one-qubit error. A physical $Z_1$ changes the relative logical phase without a nontrivial bit-flip syndrome. General single-qubit noise is a linear combination of $I,X,Y,Z$; protecting arbitrary single-qubit errors therefore needs a code such as the <Shor code>, the <Steane code> or the <five-qubit code>, with the corresponding enlarged syndrome structure.

The general exact-recovery criterion is the <Knill--Laflamme condition>. For code projector $P$ and error operators $E_r$, there must be constants $C_{rs}$ such that
$$
\boxed{PE_r^\dagger E_sP=C_{rs}P\quad\text{for every }r,s.}
$$
It expresses that the syndrome information can depend on the error but not on the logical state. Encoding entangles several physical qubits; it does not create independent copies of an unknown input, so it respects the <no-cloning theorem>. Fault-tolerant implementations must also prevent noisy syndrome extraction and recovery gates from spreading an initially correctable error into an uncorrectable one.
