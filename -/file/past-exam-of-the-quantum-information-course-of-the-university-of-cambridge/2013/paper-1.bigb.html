<Outcome determinism> means that a complete hidden state $\lambda$, together with the chosen measurement settings, fixes each outcome. The observed <probabilities> can still arise from an ensemble of unknown $\lambda$. This differs from deterministic unitary evolution of a <wavefunction>, which does not by itself specify a unique measurement outcome. <Quantum nonlocality> means failure of a Bell-local account of the correlations, under assumptions including <measurement independence>; it is stronger than the existence of entanglement and does not imply controllable faster-than-light signaling.

Use the first ket for the <positron> and the second for the <electron>. Before either final <beam splitter>, the first pair of splitters and annihilation produce
$$
|\Psi\rangle=-\frac12|\gamma\rangle
+\frac i2|u^+v^-\rangle+\frac i2|v^+u^-\rangle+\frac12|v^+v^-\rangle.
$$
The <photon> state $|\gamma\rangle$ is orthogonal to the surviving charged-particle sector. Its <probability> is $1/4$, while the remaining sector has squared norm $3/4$. The states below retain that <photon> amplitude and are normalized over **all trials**, without conditioning on nonannihilation. For compactness write $|cc\rangle=|c^+c^-\rangle$, and similarly for $|cd\rangle,|dc\rangle,|dd\rangle$.

= Solution

With both final splitters removed, the path labels map directly into detector labels:
$$
\boxed{|\Psi_{RR}\rangle=-\frac12|\gamma\rangle+\frac12\left(i|cd\rangle+i|dc\rangle+|dd\rangle\right).}
$$
In particular $P_{RR}(C^+,C^-)=0$, because joint occupation of the two intersecting arms has already annihilated. Each of the other three charged-particle coincidences has unconditional <probability> $1/4$.

Apply the final splitter only to the first ket. The $|dd\rangle$ amplitudes from $i|uv\rangle$ and $|vv\rangle$ cancel, giving
$$
\boxed{|\Psi_{IR}\rangle=-\frac12|\gamma\rangle+\frac1{2\sqrt2}\left(-|cc\rangle+2i|cd\rangle+i|dc\rangle\right).}
$$
Thus detection at $D^+$ occurs only in the coincidence $D^+,C^-$, with <probability> $1/8$. The other two nonzero coincidence probabilities are $P(C^+,C^-)=1/8$ and $P(C^+,D^-)=1/2$. The cancellation is quantum <interference> between indistinguishable surviving alternatives.

Interchanging the two wings gives
$$
\boxed{|\Psi_{RI}\rangle=-\frac12|\gamma\rangle+\frac1{2\sqrt2}\left(-|cc\rangle+i|cd\rangle+2i|dc\rangle\right).}
$$
Now detection at $D^-$ occurs only together with $C^+$, with <probability> $1/8$. The remaining nonzero coincidence probabilities are $P(C^+,C^-)=1/8$ and $P(D^+,C^-)=1/2$.

Applying both final splitters gives
$$
\boxed{|\Psi_{II}\rangle=-\frac12|\gamma\rangle+\frac14\left(-3|cc\rangle+i|cd\rangle+i|dc\rangle-|dd\rangle\right).}
$$
The charged-particle coincidence probabilities are $9/16,1/16,1/16,1/16$ in the displayed order, and the annihilation <probability> remains $1/4$. In particular
$$
\boxed{P_{II}(D^+,D^-)=\frac1{16}.}
$$
Conditioning on surviving pairs would change this number to $(1/16)/(3/4)=1/12$; it would not change the amplitudes of the unconditioned all-trials state above.

= Solution
{parent=1}
{disambiguate=Hardy contradiction}

In a <deterministic local hidden-variable model>, write $C^+(R,\lambda)$ or $D^+(I,\lambda)$ without an argument for the remote splitter setting. That omission is the <parameter independence> assumption: the local response for a fixed complete hidden state does not change when the other wing changes its apparatus. <Measurement independence> also requires the hidden-state ensemble to be independent of the freely selected settings, allowing the same $\lambda$ assignments to be compared across configurations.

The zero probabilities derived above then constrain those assignments, apart from sets of hidden states of zero measure:

* Case $RR$ requires $C^+(R,\lambda)C^-(R,\lambda)=0$.
* Case $IR$ requires $D^+(I,\lambda)=1\Rightarrow C^-(R,\lambda)=1$.
* Case $RI$ requires $D^-(I,\lambda)=1\Rightarrow C^+(R,\lambda)=1$.

For a trial with $D^+(I,\lambda)D^-(I,\lambda)=1$, the last two implications <force> $C^+(R,\lambda)=C^-(R,\lambda)=1$, contradicting the first condition. Thus these deterministic local assignments predict zero <probability> for the double-$D$ event, whereas quantum theory predicts $1/16$. This is <Hardy's paradox>, an inequality-free ideal contradiction. With imperfect apparatus the zero-event conditions become small nonzero rates and require a statistical inequality rather than declaring a contradiction from one imperfect detection.<Quantum mechanics, local realistic theories, and Lorentz-invariant realistic theories by Lucien Hardy>[1992 abstract: a two-particle proof of Bell's theorem without inequalities]{ref}

The logical contradiction combines counterfactual **hidden-variable assignments**; it does not assign simultaneously observed path properties to a <quantum state>. Nor does it allow a message to be sent by changing a distant splitter: the observable local marginal obeys <quantum no-signalling>. For example, fixing the plus-side splitter to $I$ gives $P(D^+)=1/8$ for either minus-side setting in the results above.

Set $N=2^n$, $a=N^{-1/2}=\sin t$ and $c=\cos t$, with $N>1$. The ordered vectors $|w\rangle,|s\rangle$ are normalized but <nonorthogonal>, since $\langle w|s\rangle=a$. Their coordinate <Gram matrix> is therefore
$$
S=\begin{pmatrix}1&a\\a&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
An operator matrix in this basis is obtained from the coefficients in the images of its basis vectors, not by treating those vectors as orthogonal measurement outcomes. Directly applying the two <reflection operators> gives
$$
\hat U_s|w\rangle=-|w\rangle+2a|s\rangle,\quad\hat U_s|s\rangle=|s\rangle,
$$
$$
\hat U_w|w\rangle=-|w\rangle,\quad\hat U_w|s\rangle=|s\rangle-2a|w\rangle.
$$
Their columns are consequently
$$
\boxed{U_s=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\2a&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
U_w=\begin{pmatrix}-1&-2a\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
These are physical unitary operators even though the coordinate matrices are not unitary in the ordinary Euclidean coordinate norm: they preserve $S$, so $U^\dagger SU=S$.

The <Grover iteration in a nonorthogonal basis> is
$$
G=U_sU_w=\begin{pmatrix}1&2a\\-2a&1-4a^2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
It has determinant one and trace $2-4a^2=2\cos2t$, hence <eigenvalues> $e^{\pm2it}$. For example, the invertible <matrix> of <eigenvectors>
$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\-a+ic&-a-ic\end{pmatrix}
$$
satisfies
$$
\boxed{G^r=M\begin{pmatrix}e^{2irt}&0\\0&e^{-2irt}\end{pmatrix}M^{-1}.}
$$
This diagonalization is exact for $N>1$; no large-$n$ approximation is needed.

To extract an actual <probability> using the <Born rule>, introduce the orthonormal unmarked superposition $|b\rangle=(|s\rangle-a|w\rangle)/c$. In the ordered basis $|w\rangle,|b\rangle$, the same iteration is the rotation
$$
G_{\rm orth}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos2t&\sin2t\\-\sin2t&\cos2t\end{pmatrix},\qquad
|s\rangle=\sin t\,|w\rangle+\cos t\,|b\rangle.
$$
After $r$ iterations,
$$
|\psi_f\rangle=\sin((2r+1)t)|w\rangle+\cos((2r+1)t)|b\rangle,
\qquad
\boxed{P_r=\sin^2((2r+1)t).}
$$
Equivalently the original coordinates are $[\sin(2rt)/c,\ \cos((2r+1)t)/c]^T$. The overlap with $|w\rangle$ is the first coordinate **plus $a$ times the second**, recovering the same amplitude. Squaring just the first coordinate would be incorrect in a nonorthogonal basis.

For large $n$, $t\simeq N^{-1/2}$, and dropping the initial angle $t$ gives the requested approximation $P_r\simeq\sin^2(2rt)$. The first near-unit success occurs at the integer closest to $\pi/(4t)-1/2$:
$$
\boxed{r_{\rm opt}=\operatorname{nearest\ integer}\left(\frac\pi{4\arcsin(N^{-1/2})}-\frac12\right)
\sim\frac\pi4\sqrt N.}
$$
One can choose $r_{\rm opt}=\lfloor\pi/(4t)\rfloor$, with either neighbor equivalent in a tie. Further iterations overshoot and reduce success rather than monotonically improving it. The special $N=1$ problem needs no search and the two-vector coordinate construction becomes singular.

\Image[paper-1-grover-success.png]
{title=Grover search success: exact initial-angle offset and overshooting the first maximum}

For an unstructured search, a reversible <Boolean quantum oracle> implements a phase change on the marked entry, while the diffusion reflection about a uniform state is known. Repeating the pair of reflections amplifies the marked amplitude and measurement yields a candidate that can be checked by another oracle query. The bounded-error query count is $O(\sqrt N)$ for one marked item, compared with classical $\Theta(N)$ queries. It is a quadratic **oracle-query** speedup: state preparation, the cost of implementing the oracle and access to a classical database still matter. The method does not reveal an unknown marked value without the promised query access.<A fast quantum mechanical algorithm for database search by Lov Grover>[1996 abstract and search construction]{ref}

<Quantum complementarity> concerns experimental arrangements or observables that cannot be combined into one sharp classical description. For a <qubit>, the <Pauli operators> $X$ and $Z$ have incompatible eigenbases: a definite $Z$ value gives equal probabilities for the two $X$ outcomes. One can measure either observable sharply on a given preparation, but a joint measurement cannot reproduce both sharp projective measurements on every state. Complementarity is not merely ignorance of two simultaneously definite quantum properties.

In a balanced two-path <interferometer>, <which-path information> reduces the <interference> available when the paths recombine. If the path becomes entangled with detector states,
$$
|\Psi\rangle=\frac{|u\rangle|E_u\rangle+e^{i\varphi}|v\rangle|E_v\rangle}{\sqrt2},
$$
the path <reduced density matrix> has off-diagonal magnitude $|\langle E_u|E_v\rangle|/2$. Its <interference visibility> is $V=|\langle E_u|E_v\rangle|$. For equally likely pure marker states, optimal path distinguishability is $D=\sqrt{1-|\langle E_u|E_v\rangle|^2}$, giving $D^2+V^2=1$ in this ideal model. Identical marker states preserve full <interference>; orthogonal ones permit perfect path discrimination and erase the unconditional fringes.

A concrete optical realization puts different <polarizations> on the two arms of a <Mach-Zehnder interferometer>. A detector that ignores <polarization> sees reduced <interference> because <polarization> records the path. Projecting the outgoing light onto a common <polarization> erases that information in the selected subensemble and can recover conditional fringes; summing all outcomes again removes them. This is a physical change in which alternatives remain distinguishable, not a retrospective alteration of earlier detections.

<Quantum decoherence> arises when different system alternatives correlate with distinguishable environmental states. For an initially uncorrelated state, a measurement-like <unitary time evolution> can produce
$$
(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle)|E_0\rangle
\longmapsto\alpha|0\rangle|E_0(t)\rangle+\beta|1\rangle|E_1(t)\rangle.
$$
Taking the environmental <partial trace> gives
$$
\rho_S(t)=\begin{pmatrix}
|\alpha|^2&\alpha\beta^*\langle E_1(t)|E_0(t)\rangle\\
\alpha^*\beta\langle E_0(t)|E_1(t)\rangle&|\beta|^2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The environmental overlap suppresses locally observable <quantum coherence>. Scattered <photons>, surrounding gas and uncontrolled apparatus variables can carry away such records. The interaction often singles out robust states forming a <pointer basis>. Decoherence can be much faster than thermal <energy> relaxation because recording a macroscopic distinction need not transfer much <energy>.

The overall state can remain pure and evolve unitarily even when the system alone looks like a classical mixture. Discarding environmental information is therefore not, by itself, a proof of fundamental <wavefunction> collapse or a solution to the definite-outcome <measurement problem>. A small controlled environment can return coherence, as in the <single-qubit environment dephasing model>; practical irreversibility arises when recovering the many dispersed records becomes inaccessible. In an interferometer, varying gas <pressure> or an independently controllable path marker tests the dependence of fringe contrast on this environmental coupling. Quantum computation must suppress or correct such unwanted system–environment entanglement to preserve the intended <interference>.

= Solution

<Quantum cryptography> includes <quantum key distribution>, which uses quantum transmissions and authenticated public discussion to establish a secret random key. In <BB84>, the sender independently chooses a random <bit> and either the computational or Hadamard basis, sending one of $|0\rangle,|1\rangle,|+\rangle,|-\rangle$. A receiver independently chooses one of those two bases. They announce bases after receipt, retain matching-basis positions and reveal a random sample of those bits to estimate noise or eavesdropping. Ideal uniform independent basis choices retain about half of the detected signals before testing and further processing.<Quantum cryptography: Public key distribution and coin tossing by Charles H. Bennett and Gilles Brassard>[1984 key-distribution protocol; original proceedings pp. 175–179]{ref}

The <no-cloning theorem> prevents an adversary from copying arbitrary unknown nonorthogonal signal states perfectly. In a simple intercept–resend attack, the adversary chooses a random protocol basis. On half of the sifted positions it is the wrong basis, and the resent state then yields an erroneous receiver bit with <probability> one half. Thus the <BB84 intercept-resend error rate> is
$$
\boxed{Q=\frac12\cdot\frac12=\frac14}
$$
if every signal is intercepted, or $Q=q/4$ when an independently selected fraction $q$ is attacked this way. This is an illustrative attack calculation, not a security proof against all possible quantum attacks.

After parameter estimation, classical error reconciliation aligns the remaining keys; <privacy amplification> compresses them to remove the adversary's bounded information, including information leaked during public reconciliation. The classical channel requires <message authentication>, otherwise an attacker could impersonate each party to the other. Real sources, losses, multiphoton pulses and detector behavior must fit the security model. A low observed error rate alone is insufficient to certify arbitrary hardware. Finally the protocol distributes a key; encrypting a later message is a separate step.
