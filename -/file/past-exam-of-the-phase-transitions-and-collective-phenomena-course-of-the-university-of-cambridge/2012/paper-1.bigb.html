= Solution
{parent=a}

Use $s=|t|$, $x=h/s^\Delta$ and separate scaling functions above and below the transition. Up to a nonsingular conversion between physical and dimensionless field, <magnetization scaling-function matching> gives
$$
m=-\partial_h f_s=s^{2-\alpha-\Delta}g_{m,\pm}(x),\qquad g_{m,\pm}=-g'_{f,\pm}.
$$
On the ordered side take $h\to0^+$ after the <thermodynamic limit>, selecting one <pure thermodynamic phase>. A finite nonzero $g_{m,-}(0^+)$ implies
$$
\boxed{\beta=2-\alpha-\Delta.}
$$
The critical isotherm must have a finite nonzero limit at fixed small $h$ as $s\to0$. Thus $g_m(x)\sim C\operatorname{sgn}(x)|x|^{1/\delta}$ at large $|x|$. Cancellation of the residual power of $s$ requires
$$
2-\alpha-\Delta-\frac\Delta\delta=0,\qquad
\boxed{\delta=\frac{\Delta}{2-\alpha-\Delta},\qquad\Delta=\beta\delta.}
$$
The absolute value and branch choice matter: spontaneous zero-field <magnetization> is below $T_c$, whereas the symmetry-unbroken <thermodynamic phase> has $m(t,0)=0$. The <field crossover exponent> is not the <scaling dimension> of the <magnetization> field.

A further field derivative of the <magnetization> scaling form gives
$$
\chi=\partial_hm=s^{2-\alpha-2\Delta}g'_{m,\pm}(x).
$$
At zero field, on a branch with a finite nonzero susceptibility <amplitude>, comparison with $\chi\sim s^{-\gamma}$ yields
$$
\boxed{\gamma=2\Delta-2+\alpha=\Delta-\beta.}
$$
Consequently $\alpha+2\beta+\gamma=2$ and $\gamma=\beta(\delta-1)$, the Rushbrooke and <Widom scaling relations>. Regular analytic terms do not change the leading divergent susceptibility.

At fixed zero field, $\partial_T=T_c^{-1}\partial_t$. Therefore, away from $t=0$ on either branch,
$$
c_{h,s}=-T\left(\frac{\partial^2 f_s}{\partial T^2}\right)_h
=-\frac{T}{T_c^2}(2-\alpha)(1-\alpha)g_{f,\pm}(0)s^{-\alpha}.
$$
Thus **the singular <heat capacity> has exponent $\alpha$**. An arbitrary analytic background must be added to obtain the full <heat capacity>. Vanishing branch <amplitudes> or the marginal case $\alpha=0$ need care: a finite jump is compatible with this power counting, while a logarithmic singularity requires a logarithmic refinement of the assumed homogeneous form.

<Hyperscaling> adds the physical assumption that the singular <free energy> associated with one <correlation volume> is of order $k_BT_c$:
$$
f_s\xi^d\sim k_BT_c,\qquad f_s\sim\xi^{-d}.
$$
At fixed scaled field $x=h/s^\Delta$, this predicts $f_s\sim s^{d\nu}$, whereas the given thermodynamic form has power $2-\alpha$. Hence
$$
\boxed{d\nu=2-\alpha.}
$$
The product $g_f(x)[g_\xi(x)]^d$ can be a finite dimensionless function of fixed $x$; it need not be constant. The homogeneity of $\xi$ alone is insufficient: the correlation-volume free-energy assumption supplies the connection. Above an <upper critical dimension>, a <dangerously irrelevant coupling> can invalidate this <argument>; at a marginal dimension logarithms may qualify pure <powers>.

The susceptibility is the integrated <connected correlation function>, up to a nonsingular field-normalization factor:
$$
\chi\propto\int d^dx\,G_c(\mathbf x),\qquad
G_c(\mathbf x)=\langle m(\mathbf x)m(0)\rangle-\langle m\rangle^2.
$$
Above the transition at zero field the subtraction vanishes. In an ordered <thermodynamic phase> it is essential; integrating the disconnected constant would incorrectly give a <volume> divergence unrelated to the desired response.

With a microscopic cutoff $a$ and $r=|\mathbf x|$, radial integration and $u=r/\xi$ give
$$
\chi_s\propto S_d\int_a^\infty r^{1-\eta}g(r/\xi)\,dr
=S_d\xi^{2-\eta}\int_{a/\xi}^\infty u^{1-\eta}g(u)\,du.
$$
If $g(0)$ is finite, its large-distance tail decays sufficiently rapidly and $\eta<2$, the dimensionless integral approaches a finite nonzero constant. Thus
$$
\boxed{\chi_s\sim\xi^{2-\eta}\sim s^{-(2-\eta)\nu},\qquad\gamma=(2-\eta)\nu.}
$$
Short-distance contributions give a regular background. This is the <Fisher scaling relation>; it concerns the fluctuating connected part of the source <correlation> ansatz.

<Spontaneous symmetry breaking> means that a <pure thermodynamic phase> is less symmetric than the <Hamiltonian> even when the selecting external field is removed. For example,
$$
\mathbf m_0=\lim_{\mathbf h\to0^+}\lim_{V\to\infty}\langle\mathbf m\rangle_{V,\mathbf h}\ne0.
$$
The direction of the infinitesimal field is held fixed in this limit. Reversing the limits in a finite symmetric sample normally gives zero. For the <XY model>, an ordered state chooses one direction from a circle of energetically equivalent directions.

A <Goldstone mode> is a long-wavelength fluctuation along this continuously degenerate family. A uniform angle rotation costs no <energy>; a slowly varying one costs only <gradient> <energy>. In the stated static phase-only model the quadratic kernel is $\bar K q^2$, tending to zero as $q\to0$. **There is one independent soft <order-parameter phase> coordinate for broken $O(2)$ rotation.** The static <Hamiltonian> does not specify a dynamical dispersion $\omega(q)$; that requires a dynamical law. Magnitude fluctuations instead have a restoring local curvature and have been excluded from this low-energy model.

For an <infrared-regulated phase covariance>, fix the uniform <order-parameter phase> zero mode and introduce ultraviolet and <infrared cutoffs> before taking limits. With the Fourier convention
$$
\theta(\mathbf x)=\int\frac{d^dq}{(2\pi)^d}\,\theta_{\mathbf q}e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x},
$$
the dimensionless quadratic <Hamiltonian> diagonalizes as
$$
\beta H=\frac{\bar K}{2}\int\frac{d^dq}{(2\pi)^d}q^2\theta_{\mathbf q}\theta_{-\mathbf q}.
$$
Completing the square with a source gives $Z[J]/Z[0]=\exp[\tfrac12\int JGJ]$, with inverse kernel
$$
\langle\theta_{\mathbf q}\theta_{\mathbf q'}\rangle
=\frac{(2\pi)^d\delta^{(d)}(\mathbf q+\mathbf q')}{\bar Kq^2},\qquad
G(\mathbf x)=\frac1{\bar K}\int\frac{d^dq}{(2\pi)^d}\frac{e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x}}{q^2}.
$$
There is no additional $k_BT$ factor: it is already included in $\bar K$ because the supplied <Hamiltonian> is $\beta H$.

It is simplest to evaluate the real-space dependence by its defining Green equation, $-\bar K\nabla^2G=\delta^{(d)}$. Rotational invariance gives $r^{d-1}G'(r)=$ constant for $r>0$. Integrating over a small <sphere> fixes $-\bar KS_dr^{d-1}G'(r)=1$, where $S_d=2\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2)$. Integrating once more,
$$
\boxed{G(r)=-\frac{r^{2-d}}{(2-d)S_d\bar K}+C,\qquad d\ne2.}
$$
In two dimensions, take the limit after absorbing the divergent $r$-independent piece into $C$:
$$
\boxed{G(r)=-\frac1{2\pi\bar K}\log(r/a)+C.}
$$
For $d\le2$, $G$ itself has an infrared-divergent constant in the infinite system; it is not a finite thermodynamic <covariance> without a regulator. The physical <phase-difference variance> is finite at fixed separation and cancels that constant. These are smooth <spin-wave> <correlations>; compact-field <vortex> sectors are considered separately below.

The <magnetization> correlator is an average of a <phase difference>, not merely its lowest Taylor expansion. <Gaussian phase averaging> gives
$$
C_m(r)=\bar m^2\langle\cos[\theta(\mathbf x)-\theta(0)]\rangle
=\bar m^2\exp\!\left[-\frac12D(r)\right],
$$
where
$$
D(r)=\langle[\theta(\mathbf x)-\theta(0)]^2\rangle
=\frac2{\bar K}\int\frac{d^dq}{(2\pi)^d}\frac{1-\cos(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x)}{q^2}
=2[G(0)-G(r)].
$$
The coincident value is ultraviolet-regularized at a microscopic scale $a$. At large $r$:

* For $d>2$, the infrared integral for $G(0)$ converges. Choosing $G(\infty)=0$, $D(\infty)=2G(0)<\infty$, so **$C_m(\infty)=m_0^2=\bar m^2e^{-G(0)}>0$** within the low-temperature <spin-wave> <thermodynamic phase>.
* For $d=2$, $D(r)=(\pi\bar K)^{-1}\log(r/a)+O(1)$, hence **$C_m(r)\propto r^{-1/(2\pi\bar K)}\to0$**.
* For $0<d<2$, the leading growth is $D(r)=2r^{2-d}/[(2-d)S_d\bar K]+O(1)$, hence **$C_m(r)\propto\exp\{-r^{2-d}/[(2-d)S_d\bar K]\}\to0$**. In particular $d=1$, $S_1=2$, gives $C_m(r)\propto e^{-r/(2\bar K)}$.

Two dimensions can still have <quasi-long-range order> and a thermodynamic transition; zero limiting <magnetization> does not forbid them. The $d>2$ <spin-wave> result describes an admissible low-temperature ordered <thermodynamic phase>, not order at arbitrarily high <temperature>. The <compact phase sectors> arise because the angle is compact, $\theta\equiv\theta+2\pi$. A circuit avoiding a defect core can have integer winding
$$
\boxed{q=\frac1{2\pi}\oint\nabla\theta\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
Smooth single-valued small fluctuations remain in the zero-winding sector. Vortices with $q\ne0$ cannot be unwound without a core or a boundary crossing; the smooth <Gaussian> theory misses those sectors. For $\theta=q\arg(x+iy)$ outside a core of <radius> $a$,
$$
\beta E_q=\frac{\bar K}{2}\int_a^L2\pi r\,dr\,\frac{q^2}{r^2}+\beta E_c
=\pi\bar Kq^2\log(L/a)+\beta E_c.
$$
The positional <entropy> of a freely placed defect is approximately $S/k_B=2\log(L/a)$. For unit charge the corresponding heuristic free-energy cost is
$$
\beta F_1\simeq(\pi\bar K-2)\log(L/a)+\beta E_c.
$$
Actual bulk configurations obey net-charge constraints, with vortex-antivortex pairs attracted logarithmically. At low <temperature> they remain bound; they renormalize the <phase stiffness> but do not screen away the algebraic <correlations>. Heating can unbind the pairs into a screening <vortex> plasma with short-range <correlations>: this is the <Berezinskii-Kosterlitz-Thouless transition>.

A coarse-grained scalar <order parameter> $\phi(\mathbf x)$ describes a short-range Ising-like transition with sign <symmetry>. Write the dimensionless Landau-Ginzburg functional as
$$
\mathcal H[\phi]=\frac{F[\phi]}{k_BT}
=\int d^dx\left[\frac c2(\nabla\phi)^2+\frac r2\phi^2+\frac u4\phi^4-h\phi\right],\qquad c,u>0.
$$
Here $r=a_t t$, with the relevant critical-mass shift understood when fluctuations are included. Higher local <powers> and <gradients> are negligible only within a justified long-distance expansion. The <partition function> is $Z=\int\mathcal D\phi\,e^{-\mathcal H}$ with a microscopic cutoff. The local functional is not already the fluctuation-integrated thermodynamic <free energy>.

<Mean-field theory> replaces this integral by its stable uniform saddle, $r\phi_0+u\phi_0^3=h$. At $h=0$,
$$
\phi_0=0\quad(r>0),\qquad\phi_0=\pm\sqrt{|r|/u}\quad(r<0),\qquad
f_{\min}-f_0=\begin{cases}0&r>0,\\-r^2/(4u)&r<0.\end{cases}
$$
The branch curvature is $r_L=r+3u\phi_0^2$: it is $r$ above and $2|r|$ below. Hence
$$
\chi_+=r^{-1},\quad\chi_-=(2|r|)^{-1},\quad
\xi=\sqrt{c/r_L},\quad h=u\phi_0^3\text{ at }r=0.
$$
Together with the finite heat-capacity jump from $f_{\min}$, this yields
$$
\boxed{\beta_{\rm MF}=\tfrac12,\quad\gamma_{\rm MF}=1,\quad\delta_{\rm MF}=3,
\quad\nu_{\rm MF}=\tfrac12,\quad\eta_{\rm MF}=0,\quad\alpha_{\rm MF}=0.}
$$
The equal-$|t|$ susceptibility <amplitude> ratio is two. Uniform saddles also miss <domain walls> and spatial texture. For an $n$-component <order parameter>, <symmetry> allows a vector generalization; ordered transverse directions additionally carry Goldstone fluctuations.

To include small fluctuations set $\phi=\phi_0+\delta\phi$. The <Gaussian> action is $\tfrac12\int[c(\nabla\delta\phi)^2+r_L(\delta\phi)^2]$, giving
$$
G(q)=\langle|\delta\phi_q|^2\rangle_{\rm spectral}=\frac1{cq^2+r_L},\qquad
\delta f=\frac12\int^{\Lambda}\frac{d^dq}{(2\pi)^d}\log(cq^2+r_L)+\text{normalization}.
$$
The displayed $G(q)$ is a spectral <covariance> per Fourier delta, avoiding an extra <volume> factor. As $r_L\to0$, a growing <correlation length> lets increasingly large correlated regions fluctuate. The singular <Gaussian> heat correction contains $\int d^dq/(cq^2+r_L)^2$, which is infrared singular for $d<4$ and logarithmic at four. This signals failure of a simple saddle expansion; the <Gaussian> heat exponent obtained by extrapolation is not the exact interacting <critical exponent>.

The <Ginzburg criterion> compares the fluctuation of a correlation-volume average with $\phi_0^2$, rather than using all ultraviolet fluctuations at a point. Keeping $q\lesssim\xi^{-1}$ below the transition gives, up to finite convention-dependent constants,
$$
\langle(\delta\phi)_\xi^2\rangle
\sim\int_{|q|\lesssim\xi^{-1}}\frac{d^dq}{(2\pi)^d}\frac1{cq^2+2|r|}
\sim c^{-d/2}|r|^{(d-2)/2}.
$$
Dividing by $\phi_0^2=|r|/u$ yields
$$
\boxed{\mathcal G\sim u c^{-d/2}|r|^{(d-4)/2}\ll1.}
$$
For $d<4$, writing $r=a_t t$ gives a nonuniversal <Ginzburg reduced-temperature window>
$$
\boxed{|t|\gg\mathrm{Gi},\qquad
\mathrm{Gi}\sim\left[u c^{-d/2}a_t^{(d-4)/2}\right]^{2/(4-d)}.}
$$
Inside $|t|\lesssim\mathrm{Gi}$, interacting fluctuations must be treated. For example, in three dimensions $\mathrm{Gi}\sim u^2/(c^3a_t)$ in this normalization. Above four dimensions the long-wavelength ratio vanishes at criticality; at four it is marginal and interaction-generated logarithms require further analysis.

<Renormalization> compares the same long-distance physics at different observation scales. The starting object is a microscopic or cutoff <statistical Hamiltonian> $\mathcal H_g$ with every symmetry-allowed coupling $g$. Because a critical <correlation length> is much larger than a lattice spacing, repeatedly eliminating short-distance detail can reveal scale-invariant behavior.

In a real-space transformation choose a <normalized blocking kernel> $B(S|s)$ for retained variables. Define
$$
e^{-\mathcal H_{g'}(S)}=\sum_sB(S|s)e^{-\mathcal H_g(s)},\qquad\sum_SB(S|s)=1.
$$
Summing over $S$ proves exact preservation of $Z$ before truncation. The transformed <Hamiltonian> generally gains longer-range and many-body interactions and a field-independent constant. Observables must be blocked consistently. In momentum-shell language split $\phi=\phi_<+\phi_>$, integrate $\Lambda/b<|q|<\Lambda$, then use $x'=x/b$ and a field rescaling to restore cutoff and kinetic normalization. **Coarse-graining, rescaling and tracking generated interactions are distinct steps.**

Repeated transformations generate a flow $g'=R_b(g)$ with composition $R_bR_c=R_{bc}$ when the same exact coarse-graining family is used. Losing degrees of freedom generally makes this a semigroup, despite its conventional group name. A <renormalization-group fixed point> is invariant after rescaling. At a continuous transition the large-distance theory approaches a critical <renormalization-group fixed point>; finite <correlation length> instead eventually flows into an ordinary <thermodynamic phase> regime.

Linearize in scaling coordinates $u_i$:
$$
u'_i=b^{y_i}u_i.
$$
For increasing observation <length>, $y_i>0$ is relevant and must be tuned, $y_i<0$ is irrelevant, and $y_i=0$ is marginal and needs nonlinear analysis. Discrete multipliers $b^{y_i}$ must be compared with one, not with zero. The stable manifold forms the <critical surface> after relevant directions have been set to zero.

After subtracting regular additive contributions, ordinary <hyperscaling> gives
$$
f_s(t,h)=b^{-d}f_s(b^{y_t}t,b^{y_h}h).
$$
Choosing $b=|t|^{-1/y_t}$ or choosing the field as scale yields
$$
\boxed{\nu=1/y_t,\quad\Delta=y_h/y_t,\quad
2-\alpha=d/y_t,\quad\beta=(d-y_h)/y_t,\quad
\gamma=(2y_h-d)/y_t,\quad\delta=y_h/(d-y_h).}
$$
For a critical two-point function $G(r)\sim r^{-(d-2+\eta)}$, the <order-parameter> <scaling dimension> is $x_m=(d-2+\eta)/2$, and its <conjugate field> has $y_h=d-x_m=(d+2-\eta)/2$. This recovers the Q1 identities while distinguishing the gap exponent $\Delta$ from the field's <scaling dimension> $y_h$.

As a concrete dimensional check, start from a scalar <gradient> action. Restoring its coefficient uses $\phi'(x')=b^{(d-2)/2}\phi_<(bx')$, so at the <Gaussian fixed point>
$$
r'=b^2r,\qquad u'=b^{4-d}u,\qquad h'=b^{(d+2)/2}h.
$$
Quartic interactions are relevant below four dimensions. They can produce an interacting <Wilson-Fisher fixed point>, whose exponents differ from naive <Gaussian> counting. Above four, the quartic coupling is irrelevant at the <Gaussian fixed point> but dangerous for ordered observables: $\phi_0^2=-r/u$ is singular when $u$ is dropped. Thus the simple two-variable <hyperscaling> formula is not universal above the <upper critical dimension>.

Different microscopic systems can flow to the same long-distance <renormalization-group fixed point>, explaining <universality> of exponents and normalized scaling forms. <Critical temperatures>, metric factors and regular <free energies> remain nonuniversal. Dimension, <order-parameter> <symmetry>, interaction range and additional soft variables constrain the <universality class>. Truncations, perturbative expansions and numerics approximate the flow; they must not be confused with the exact elimination definition.<Statistical Field Theory by David Tong>[§3.1–§3.3: coarse-graining, scaling, relevance and dangerously irrelevant interactions]{ref}

For a ferromagnetic nearest-neighbour <Ising model> on a chain let $s_i=\pm1$, $K=J/(k_BT)>0$ and $h=H/(k_BT)$. The dimensionless <Hamiltonian> is $-K\sum_i s_is_{i+1}-h\sum_i s_i$. In one dimension, decimating every other spin closes exactly within the nearest-neighbour family, including its additive constant. This is the exact chain building block of the <Migdal-Kadanoff approach>; <bond moving> becomes an additional approximation in higher-dimensional lattices.

For <exact zero-field Ising-chain decimation>, sum over an intermediate spin $\sigma$ between two retained spins:
$$
W(s,s')=\sum_{\sigma=\pm1}e^{K(s\sigma+\sigma s')}
=2\cosh[K(s+s')]=A(K)e^{K'ss'}.
$$
Parallel and antiparallel end spins give $Ae^{K'}=2\cosh2K$ and $Ae^{-K'}=2$. Hence
$$
\boxed{K'=\tfrac12\log\cosh2K,\qquad
A=2\sqrt{\cosh2K},\qquad\tanh K'=(\tanh K)^2.}
$$
For an even periodic chain, $Z_N(K)=A^{N/2}Z_{N/2}(K')$. Rescaling the lattice spacing $2a$ back to $a$ completes the <renormalization> step. Decimation of $b$ successive links gives $\tanh K'=(\tanh K)^b$, as follows by multiplying the <transfer matrix> $b$ times.

Let $v=\tanh K$. Then $v'=v^2$ on $0\le v\le1$. The infinite-temperature <renormalization-group fixed point> $v=0$ attracts every $0\le v<1$; the zero-temperature <renormalization-group fixed point> $v=1$ is unstable to heating, with slope two. There is no intermediate ferromagnetic <renormalization-group fixed point>. Equivalently $K'\simeq K^2$ for small $K$, while $K'\simeq K-\tfrac12\log2$ for large finite $K$. Thus **there is no finite-temperature ordered <thermodynamic phase> in the short-range chain**.

The <domain-wall scaling coordinate of an Ising chain> near $T=0$ is the domain-wall <Boltzmann factor> $w=e^{-2K}$. Its recursion and the zero-field <correlation length> are
$$
w'=\frac{2w}{1+w^2}\simeq2w,\qquad
\langle s_0s_n\rangle=(\tanh K)^{|n|},\qquad
\boxed{\frac\xi a=-\frac1{\log\tanh K}\sim\frac1{2w}=\frac12e^{2J/(k_BT)}.}
$$
Since $\xi(K')/a=[\xi(K)/a]/2$, the recursion has the correct <length> scaling. Its exponent is one with respect to $w$; **it is not a finite power of physical <temperature> about $T_c=0$**. In the <thermodynamic limit>, a bond is a <domain wall> with <probability> $(1-\tanh K)/2\sim e^{-2K}$, giving the same exponentially large <length> scale. Their finite <energy> allows <entropy> to proliferate walls at any positive <temperature>.

{title=Exact Ising-chain decimation and length}
{description=Original one-dimensional recursion v'=v squared, finite-temperature iterates flowing to zero and the exact correlation length with its low-temperature asymptote. The coordinate v is tanh(J/kBT); no finite-temperature critical point is introduced.}

The <Ising-chain decimation constant> matters for thermodynamics. With dimensionless <free energy> per spin $f=-\lim N^{-1}\log Z_N$,
$$
f(K)=-\tfrac12\log A(K)+\tfrac12 f(K'),\qquad
\boxed{f(K)=-\log(2\cosh K).}
$$
The second expression follows independently from transfer <eigenvalues> $2\cosh K$ and $2\sinh K$ and satisfies the recursion. Multiplying it by $k_BT$ restores the physical <free energy>. Dropping $A$ would preserve normalized retained-spin <probabilities> but lose the eliminated-spin <entropy>.

<Finite-field Ising-chain decimation> is also exact. Use the symmetric <transfer matrix>
$$
T=\begin{pmatrix}e^{K+h}&e^{-K}\\e^{-K}&e^{K-h}\end{pmatrix},\quad W=T^2,
$$
and read the new couplings from
$$
\boxed{K'=\frac14\log\frac{W_{++}W_{--}}{W_{+-}^2},\qquad
h'=\frac12\log\frac{W_{++}}{W_{--}},\qquad
A=(W_{++}W_{--}W_{+-}^2)^{1/4}.}
$$
Here $W_{++}=e^{2K+2h}+e^{-2K}$, $W_{--}=e^{2K-2h}+e^{-2K}$ and $W_{+-}=2\cosh h$. The latter off-diagonal entry, the field convention split between adjacent bonds and the constant must all be tracked consistently. Near the zero-temperature zero-field <renormalization-group fixed point> $h'\simeq2h$, while at $K=0$ the independent-spin field is unchanged.

In the higher-dimensional <Migdal-Kadanoff approximation>, moving bonds creates chains that can be decimated. With <bond moving> first on a $d$-dimensional hypercubic lattice and <length> factor $b$, the strengthened links have $b^{d-1}K$, giving the convention
$$
K'=\operatorname{artanh}\!\left[\tanh^b(b^{d-1}K)\right].
$$
For $d=1$ no transverse bonds move, so this reduces to the exact chain result. In higher dimensions it discards geometric <correlations> and is generally approximate on the original lattice; moving and decimating in another order yields another approximation, not a contradictory exact chain formula.<Position Space Renormalization Group by Mehran Kardar>[lecture 13, printed pp. 79–81, equations V.18–V.27: exact chain; lecture 14, printed pp. 86–87, equations V.51–V.54: bond-moving convention]{ref}
