= Solution
{parent=1}

<Weak convergence> means $\ell(u_j)\to\ell(u)$ for every <bounded linear functional> $\ell\in X^*$. <Norm convergence> implies this by $|\ell(u_j-u)|\leq\|\ell\|\|u_j-u\|$. The converse fails for coordinate unit vectors in $\ell^2$: their <norms> are one, but pairing with any square-summable <sequence> tends to zero, so they are weakly null.

For the <subsequence> assertion apply <Gram-Schmidt process> to a countable dense subset to choose a countable <orthonormal basis> $(e_m)$ of the <separable Hilbert space> and a bound $M$ for the <sequence>. Repeated <subsequence> selection and diagonalization make every coefficient $\langle u_{j_k},e_m\rangle$ converge to a scalar $a_m$. <Bessel inequality> gives $\sum_{m=1}^N|a_m|^2\leq M^2$ for every $N$, so $u=\sum_ma_me_m$ exists in $H$ with <norm> at most $M$. Pairings with finite <linear combinations> of the basis converge. Approximating any other test vector by such combinations and using the common <norm> bound proves **$u_{j_k}\rightharpoonup u$**. The finite-dimensional case uses the same argument with a finite basis.

Apply this to every <subsequence> of the bounded $L^2$ <sequence>. Any <weakly convergent> further <subsequence> has limit $v$ with $\int_a^bv=0$, since interval indicators are $L^2$ tests. The permitted fact gives $v=0$ almost everywhere. If the whole <sequence> failed to converge weakly to zero, some fixed test functional and <subsequence> would stay a positive distance from zero. Its <weakly convergent> further <subsequence> would then contradict the preceding conclusion. Thus **the entire <sequence> converges weakly to zero in $L^2(0,1)$**.

The first <weak derivative> $D_ju=v$ is characterized by $\int_\Omega u\,\partial_j\phi=-\int_\Omega v\phi$ for every $\phi\in C_c^\infty(\Omega)$. The <fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations> in the locally integrable form says that a <function> pairing to zero with every such test is zero almost everywhere. Applied to the difference of two candidate derivatives, it proves uniqueness. The <Sobolev space> consists of $u\in L^p$ with all first <weak derivatives> in $L^p$, with <norm>
$$
\boxed{\|u\|_{W^{1,p}}=\left(\|u\|_p^p+\sum_{j=1}^n\|D_ju\|_p^p\right)^{1/p}.}
$$
For the one-dimensional tent, <integration by parts> on each side of one has cancelling junction terms because the <function> is continuous. Thus **$u'=1$ on $(0,1)$ and $u'=-1$ on $(1,2)$**, almost everywhere. Both it and its derivative are bounded, so it belongs to every stated <Sobolev space>; its displayed <norm> to the power $p$ is $2/(p+1)+2$.

For $u=x_1/r$ in the punctured ball, ordinary differentiation gives
$$
\boxed{D_ju=\delta_{1j}/r-x_1x_j/r^3.}
$$
The <function> is bounded and $|D_ju|\leq2/r$, so polar integration gives finite derivative $L^p$ <norms> when $\int_0^1r^{n-1-p}dr<\infty$, namely $p<n$. To verify these are <weak derivatives> across the origin, integrate by parts outside a ball of radius $\varepsilon$ against a compactly supported smooth test. The inner boundary term is at most a fixed constant times $\varepsilon^{n-1}$, since $u$ is bounded. It tends to zero for $n>1$. The volume terms converge by local integrability of the displayed derivative. Thus **$u\in W^{1,p}(B)$ for $1\leq p<n$ with exactly these derivatives**. Its value at the origin can be chosen arbitrarily.

= Solution

Bounded coefficients and Cauchy-Schwarz give $|B[u,v]|\leq C\|u\|_{H^1}\|v\|_{H^1}$. <Uniform ellipticity> gives $B[u,u]\geq\theta\|Du\|_2^2$ because $c\geq0$. <Poincaré inequality> $\|u\|_2\leq C_P\|Du\|_2$ holds on $H_0^1$ of a bounded domain, so
$$
\boxed{B[u,u]\geq\nu\|u\|_{H^1}^2,\qquad\nu=\theta/(1+C_P^2)>0.}
$$
A <weak solution> is $u\in H_0^1$ satisfying $B[u,\phi]=\int f\phi$ for every $\phi\in H_0^1$. The energy is finite by boundedness of $B$ and the forcing functional, and
$$
I(v)\geq\frac\nu2\|v\|_{H^1}^2-\|f\|_2\|v\|_{H^1}\geq\frac\nu4\|v\|_{H^1}^2-C_f.
$$
It is bounded below and a <minimizing sequence> is bounded in the <Hilbert space> $H_0^1$. Its <closed linear span> is <separable>, so part 1 supplies a <weakly convergent> <subsequence> with limit $u$. Symmetry and nonnegativity of $B$ show
$$
B[v_j,v_j]=B[u,u]+2B[u,v_j-u]+B[v_j-u,v_j-u]\geq B[u,u]+o(1).
$$
The forcing term converges by <weak convergence>. Thus $I(u)\leq\liminf I(v_j)$, proving attainment. <First variation> in every zero-trace direction gives the weak equation. If $u,w$ are two solutions, test their difference equation with $u-w$ and use coercivity to get $u=w$. Hence **there is exactly one <weak solution>, and it is the unique energy minimizer**. Indeed $I(v)-I(u)=\tfrac12B[v-u,v-u]$ directly verifies the minimizing property.

= Solution

The <spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators> used here concerns a compact <self-adjoint operator>: its nonzero <eigenvalues> are real, have finite multiplicity and can accumulate only at zero; the orthogonal complement of its kernel is the closed span of its orthonormal eigenvectors. Self-adjointness is essential for this eigenbasis statement.

Let $Kf$ be the unique zero-parameter <weak solution>. Uniqueness gives linearity. Testing with $Kf$ and using coercivity gives $\|Kf\|_{H^1}\leq\nu^{-1}\|f\|_2$. The <Rellich-Kondrachov theorem> makes the embedding $H_0^1(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^2(\Omega)$ compact on this bounded $C^1$ domain, so $K$ is compact on $L^2$. Symmetry gives
$$
\langle Kf,g\rangle=B[Kg,Kf]=\langle f,Kg\rangle.
$$
It is self-adjoint and positive. It is injective: if $Kf=0$, the weak equation pairs $f$ to zero with all $H_0^1$ tests, dense in $L^2$, so $f=0$. Moreover $\langle Kf,f\rangle=B[Kf,Kf]>0$ for $f\ne0$.

The parameter equation is equivalent to $u=K(f+\lambda u)$, that is
$$
\boxed{u-\lambda Ku=Kf.}
$$
A solution of this operator equation in $L^2$ lies in $H_0^1$, because its right-side representation is $K(f+\lambda u)$, and the equivalence follows in both directions. For $\lambda\leq0$, the form $B[u,v]-\lambda\langle u,v\rangle$ remains coercive; the preceding minimization argument gives a unique <weak solution>.

The <spectral theorem> now supplies a complete <orthonormal basis> $(\phi_j)$ of $L^2$ with $K\phi_j=\kappa_j\phi_j$, $\kappa_j>0$, and $\kappa_j\to0$. The elliptic <eigenvalues> are $\lambda_j=1/\kappa_j>0$, with finite multiplicity and tending to infinity, and $L\phi_j=\lambda_j\phi_j$ weakly. If $\lambda$ is not one of them, then
$$
\boxed{u=\sum_j\frac{\langle f,\phi_j\rangle}{\lambda_j-\lambda}\phi_j.}
$$
This converges even in $H_0^1$: its squared energy <norm> is $\sum_j\lambda_j|\langle f,\phi_j\rangle|^2/|\lambda_j-\lambda|^2$, finite because the multiplier is bounded over $j$. Partial sums solve the weak equation with the corresponding $L^2$ projections of $f$. Passing to the limit proves the equation for every test; the same coefficients force uniqueness. Thus the expansion represents **the unique <weak solution> away from the elliptic <eigenvalues>**.

Use $\|w\|_{W^{1,\infty}}=\|w\|_\infty+\|w'\|_\infty$. Fix $u$. For $w$ small, all triples $(x,u+tw,u'+tw')$, $0\leq t\leq1$, lie in a fixed <compact set> up to null sets. The continuous derivatives $f_u,f_v$ are uniformly continuous there, with a modulus $\omega(s)\to0$ as $s\to0$. The integral form of the mean-value expansion gives a remainder bounded by
$$
(b-a)\omega(\|w\|_{W^{1,\infty}})(\|w\|_\infty+\|w'\|_\infty).
$$
The proposed linear derivative is bounded because its coefficient <functions> are bounded on that <compact set>. The remainder is little-oh of the <norm>, proving <Fréchet differentiability> and
$$
\boxed{DI(u)w=\int_a^b[f_v(x,u,u')w'+f_u(x,u,u')w]dx.}
$$
The derivative $u'$ only needs to exist almost everywhere; the uniform compact-range bound handles its measurable representative.

DI(u)\phi=\int_0^1x^2(u'\phi+u\phi')dx=\int_0^1x^2(u\phi)'dx=-\int_0^12xu\phi\,dx.

A Lipschitz representative is then identically zero and cannot have terminal value one. Therefore **there are no admissible solutions of the stationarity equation**.

Along the reference curve put $p=f_{vv}$, $r=f_{uv}$, $s=f_{uu}$. The <second variation> is
$$
q(\phi)=\delta^2I(\bar u)[\phi,\phi]=\int_a^b(p\phi'^2+2r\phi\phi'+s\phi^2)dx.
$$
Strict positivity means $q(\phi)>0$ for every nonzero zero-trace variation. Under the given regular Legendre condition $p>0$, the no-conjugate-point criterion also supplies a uniform coercive lower bound. The <Jacobi equation> is $-(pz'+rz)'+rz'+sz=0$. A point $c>a$ is conjugate to $a$ when a nonzero solution vanishes at both $a$ and $c$. In this regular setting, absence of <conjugate points> in $(a,b]$ is equivalent to existence of a Jacobi solution positive throughout $[a,b]$.

Choose such a positive solution $z$. The given square identity extends from smooth compactly supported variations to $H_0^1$ by density and boundedness of its coefficients. With $v=\phi/z$ it reads $q(\phi)=\int pz^2v'^2$. The positive minima of $p,z$ bound this below by $c\|v'\|_2^2$. A zero-trace <function> obeys $\|v\|_2\leq C\|v'\|_2$, while $\phi'=zv'+z'v$ and $\phi=zv$ give $\|\phi\|_{H^1}\leq C_1\|v'\|_2$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{q(\phi)\geq c_1\|\phi\|_{H^1}^2>0\quad(\phi\ne0).}
$$
In particular the required <second variation> is positive for all nonzero Lipschitz zero-trace variations.

The <Jacobi equation> is $z''+\lambda^2z=0$. For $|\lambda|<\pi$ the <function> $z=\cos(\lambda(x-1/2))$ is positive on the closed interval. The preceding square identity gives
$$
I(u)=\int_0^1z^2[(u/z)']^2dx,
$$
with $z=1$ if $\lambda=0$. As in part (a), its positive minimum and bounded derivative provide a constant $\gamma>0$ with $I(u)\geq\gamma\|u\|_{H^1}^2$. The <second variation> at zero is $2I$, so is positive, and **zero is the unique <global minimizer>**.

For a fixed zero-trace $u$, positivity of $\int u'^2-\lambda^2\int u^2$ for all $\lambda^2<\pi^2$ gives, by letting $\lambda^2\uparrow\pi^2$,
$$
\boxed{\int_0^1u'^2\geq\pi^2\int_0^1u^2.}
$$
This derivation did not assume the sharp inequality in advance. For equality, the nonnegative quadratic form $Q(u)=\int(u'^2-\pi^2u^2)$ has $Q(u)=0$. Expanding $Q(u+t\phi)\geq0$ for both signs of $t$ forces $\int u'\phi'-\pi^2u\phi=0$ for every test. Thus $u''+\pi^2u=0$, and the zero endpoint values give **exactly $u(x)=C\sin(\pi x)$**, including $C=0$. These <functions> do give equality.

A <global minimizer> satisfies $I(\bar u)\leq I(v)$ for every admissible $v$.

A <point of convexity> $s$ of a differentiable scalar <function> is one where its tangent supports the entire graph: $g(q)\geq g(s)+g'(s)(q-s)$ for every $q\in\mathbb R$. The <Weierstrass necessary condition> for strong minimality, under its differentiability assumptions in the slope variable, is
$$
f(x,\bar u,q)-f(x,\bar u,\bar u')-f_v(x,\bar u,\bar u')(q-\bar u')\geq0\quad(q\in\mathbb R)
$$
for almost every $x$. In other words the reference slope is a <point of convexity> of the slope integrand. The later example is smooth, so these derivative-based conditions apply there.

Write $g(v)=-\cos(\pi v/2)$. For the stipulated $0<\delta<1$, the displayed tent is continuous at its junction, has zero endpoint values, and its slopes are $\delta$ and $\delta-4$. At both slopes $g'$ has the same value $c=(\pi/2)\sin(\pi\delta/2)>0$, and $g''=(\pi^2/4)\cos(\pi v/2)$ is strictly positive on common sufficiently small neighborhoods of them. <Taylor's theorem> gives, for small $\|\phi'\|_\infty$,
$$
I(u^\delta+\phi)-I(u^\delta)\geq c\int_0^1\phi'+c_0\int_0^1\phi'^2=c_0\int_0^1\phi'^2.
$$
Thus **$u^\delta$ is a strict <weak local minimizer of a variational integral>**. It is not a <strong local minimizer of a variational integral>: the global supporting-tangent requirement at either slope has nonzero slope $c$, whereas $g$ is bounded. Letting the trial slope tend to positive infinity violates that requirement. The <Weierstrass necessary condition> therefore fails.

The opening parameter range $\delta\in(0,1)$ is essential. The subsequent phrase “for each $\delta>0$” must be read within it; taken literally beyond that range it is false. At $\delta=2$ the tent slopes are $2,-2$, where $g=1$ and $g''<0$, so this is not a weak minimum.

The <De la Vallée-Poussin criterion> characterizes <uniform integrability> by existence of a nonnegative increasing <convex function> $\Psi$ with $\Psi(s)/s\to\infty$ and a uniform bound on $\int\Psi(|v_j|)$. On a finite interval this gives a <weakly convergent> <subsequence> in $L^1$ by the <Dunford-Pettis theorem>.

After discarding a finite prefix of the <minimizing sequence> if needed, put $H(t)=\Phi(t)-M\geq0$. Its derivative <sequence> has $\int H(u_j')\leq C$. The superlinear bound directly gives <uniform integrability>: for $H(t)\geq A|t|$ outside $[-R_A,R_A]$,
$$
\int_E|u_j'|\leq R_A|E|+C/A.
$$
If the convex version of the criterion is used, choose increasing $R_m\to\infty$ so $H(t)\geq m^2|t|$ for $|t|\geq R_m$, and set $\Psi(s)=\sum_{m\geq1}(s-R_m)_+$. This is locally a finite sum, convex and increasing. It is superlinear because for fixed $N$ and $s\geq2R_N$, $\Psi(s)\geq Ns/2$. If exactly $m$ terms are active, $\Psi(s)\leq ms\leq H(t)$ for $s=|t|$, so its integrals have the same bound. The criterion therefore supplies $u_{j_k}'\rightharpoonup v$ in $L^1$.

Define $u(x)=\alpha+\int_a^xv$. Interval-indicator tests give $u_{j_k}(x)\to u(x)$ for every $x$, including $b$, so its endpoint values are correct and $u\in W^{1,1}$. The common derivative $L^1$ bound makes these <functions> uniformly bounded. <Uniform integrability> also gives common equicontinuity, since an increment is bounded by the derivative integral on the intervening interval. The <Arzelà-Ascoli theorem> yields uniform convergence to the same identified limit, and in particular
$$
\boxed{u\in\mathcal A,\quad u_{j_k}'\rightharpoonup u'\text{ in }L^1,\quad u_{j_k}\to u\text{ in }L^1.}
$$
Here the printed admissible space is $W^{1,1}$, so this <compactness> argument gives precisely the needed membership.

The assumed weak-star limit is the constant $m=\theta a+(1-\theta)b$. Each integer rescaling has exactly $j$ full periods on $(0,1)$, so a change of variables on those periods gives
$$
\int_0^1f(u_j(x))dx=\theta f(a)+(1-\theta)f(b).
$$
Apply the asserted lower semicontinuity to this <sequence>. The integral of $f$ of its constant limit is $f(m)$, giving
$$
\boxed{f(\theta a+(1-\theta)b)\leq\theta f(a)+(1-\theta)f(b).}
$$
This holds for every real $a,b$ and $0<\theta<1$; the endpoint values of $\theta$ give equality. Therefore **$f$ is convex**.
