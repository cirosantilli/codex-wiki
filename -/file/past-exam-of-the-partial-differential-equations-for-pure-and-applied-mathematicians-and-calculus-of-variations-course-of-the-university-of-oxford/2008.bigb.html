= Solution
{parent=1}

A <continuous embedding> $X\hookrightarrow Y$ is an injective continuous linear inclusion, so $\|x\|_Y\leq C\|x\|_X$ for a fixed constant. On a finite-measure domain, <Holder inequality> gives
$$
\boxed{\|u\|_q\leq|\Omega|^{1/q-1/p}\|u\|_p\quad(1\leq q\leq p\leq\infty),}
$$
with $1/\infty=0$. For finite $p>q$ apply <Holder inequality> to $|u|^q\cdot1$ with exponents $p/q$ and $p/(p-q)$; equality of exponents is immediate, and $p=\infty$ follows by integrating the supremum bound.

<Weak convergence> $u_j\rightharpoonup u$ means $\ell(u_j)\to\ell(u)$ for every <bounded linear functional> $\ell\in X^*$. The <Riemann-Lebesgue lemma> says that the <Fourier transform> of an integrable function tends to zero at high frequency. Since $L^2(0,1)\subset L^1(0,1)$, it makes $\cos(j\pi x)$ weakly null in $L^2$. Its absolute value is at most one, so $u_jv_j\in L^2$. For a test $\phi\in L^2$,
$$
\left|\int u_j(v_j-v)\phi\right|\leq\|v_j-v\|_2\|\phi\|_2\to0,
$$
while $\int u_jv\phi\to0$ by the <Riemann-Lebesgue lemma> applied to $v\phi\in L^1$. Hence **$u_jv_j\rightharpoonup0$**. Taking $w_j=u_j=\cos(j\pi x)$ instead gives $u_jw_j=(1+\cos(2j\pi x))/2\rightharpoonup1/2$, so two weakly null factors need not have a weakly null product.

For $\bar z_j=j^{1/p}\mathbf1_{(0,1/j)}$ and $p'=p/(p-1)$, <Holder inequality> gives
$$
\left|\int\bar z_jg\right|\leq\left(\int_0^{1/j}|g|^{p'}\right)^{1/p'}\to0\quad(g\in L^{p'}).
$$
Thus **$\bar z_j\rightharpoonup0$ in $L^p$**. In contrast, if $z_j$ had a weak $L^1$ limit $z$, testing on every interval strictly away from zero would give integral zero for $z$, hence $z=0$ <almost everywhere> by the permitted fact. Testing against the constant function one would give $\int z=1$, a contradiction. Therefore **$z_j$ has no weak $L^1$ limit**.

= Solution
{parent=2}

Choose a smooth compactly supported <mollifier> of integral one. On every compact subset of $\Omega$, and for sufficiently small $\varepsilon$, each translated <mollifier> is an allowed test, so the assumption gives $w*\rho_\varepsilon=0$. The standard <mollifier> approximation theorem gives $w*\rho_\varepsilon\to w$ in local $L^1$. Thus **$w=0$ <almost everywhere>**.

For a multi-index $\alpha$, the <weak derivative> $v=D^\alpha u$ is the locally integrable function satisfying
$$
\boxed{\int_\Omega uD^\alpha\phi=(-1)^{|\alpha|}\int_\Omega v\phi\quad(\phi\in C_0^\infty(\Omega)).}
$$
Two such functions have zero pairing difference against every test, so the first result proves uniqueness. A compactly supported smooth test on an open subset $E$ extends by zero to one on $\Omega$, proving restriction of the derivative to $E$.

Put $w=D^\beta u$. For every test $\phi$ the defining identity applied to $D^\alpha\phi$ gives
$$
(-1)^{|\alpha|}\int wD^\alpha\phi=(-1)^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}\int uD^{\alpha+\beta}\phi.
$$
Thus these two <distributions> are identical; one is represented by a locally integrable function if and only if the other is. Their <weak derivatives> then agree, proving **$D^\alpha(D^\beta u)=D^{\alpha+\beta}u$** with exactly the asserted existence equivalence.

On $(0,2)$ the displayed piecewise affine function is continuous at one. <Integration by parts> separately on the two intervals shows its first <weak derivative> is one on $(0,1)$ and minus one on $(1,2)$; the first boundary terms cancel by continuity. The derivative of that step function is $-2\delta_1$. This <distribution> is not represented by a locally integrable function: tests of height one supported in shrinking neighborhoods of one have pairing $-2$ with it, whereas their integrals against any fixed local $L^1$ function tend to zero. Hence **there is a first <weak derivative> but no locally integrable second <weak derivative>**.

= Solution
{parent=3}

The <weak formulation> is
$$
\boxed{\int_\Omega[Du\cdot D\phi+(u^3-u^2)\phi]dx=-\int_\Omega f\phi\,dx\quad(\phi\in H_0^1(\Omega)).}
$$
The boundary condition is encoded by $u\in H_0^1$. The <Sobolev embedding> in dimension three gives $H_0^1\hookrightarrow L^6$, hence also $L^4,L^3,L^2$ on the bounded domain. All energy terms and all the displayed pairings are therefore finite.

Pointwise, $t^4/4-t^3/3\geq t^4/8-C$ for a constant $C$, since the difference is a quartic with positive leading coefficient. The <Poincaré inequality> and <Young inequality> give $\int fv\geq-\tfrac14\|Dv\|_2^2-C_f$. Consequently
$$
I(v)\geq\frac14\|Dv\|_2^2+\frac18\|v\|_4^4-C_0.
$$
Thus the <infimum> is finite and a <minimizing sequence> is bounded in $H_0^1$. Extract a weakly convergent subsequence there. The <Rellich-Kondrachov theorem> states that on a bounded $C^1$ domain in dimension three the embedding $H^1\hookrightarrow L^q$ is compact for $q<6$. A subsequence therefore converges strongly in $L^2,L^3,L^4$ to the same limit $u$. The potential terms and forcing converge, while the gradient <norm> is weakly lower semicontinuous. The weak limit retains zero trace. Hence $I(u)\leq\liminf I(v_j)$ and **$u$ attains the absolute minimum**.

For any $\phi\in H_0^1$, the function $t\mapsto I(u+t\phi)$ has a minimum at zero. Differentiating its polynomial terms gives $\int[Du\cdot D\phi+(u^3-u^2+f)\phi]=0$. These differentiations are justified by the Sobolev integrability already noted. This is the <weak formulation>, proving **every energy minimizer is a <weak solution>**.

= Solution
{parent=4}

The <Lax-Milgram theorem> gives, for a bounded <coercive bilinear form> on a real <Hilbert space> and every <bounded linear functional>, a unique vector representing that functional through the form, with the inverse bound supplied by coercivity. The <Fredholm alternative> for a <compact operator> $K$ says that $I+\mu K$ is invertible if its kernel is zero; in general its range is closed and consists exactly of vectors orthogonal to $\ker(I+\mu K)^*$ in the Hilbert-space case.

<Uniform ellipticity> gives $\sum a_{ij}\xi_i\xi_j\geq\theta|\xi|^2$ for some $\theta>0$. Bounded coefficients make $B_\lambda$ bounded on $H^1$, and for $\lambda<-\gamma$,
$$
B_\lambda[u,u]\geq\theta\|Du\|_2^2+(-\gamma-\lambda)\|u\|_2^2\geq\min\{\theta,-\gamma-\lambda\}\|u\|_{H^1}^2.
$$
The forcing $v\mapsto\int fv$ is bounded on $H^1$. The <Lax-Milgram theorem> therefore gives **a unique <weak solution> for every $\lambda<-\gamma$**.

When $c=0$, choose the shift $\lambda_0=-1$ and let $Kf$ be the unique solution of $B[Kf,v]+\int(Kf)v=\int fv$. Thus $K=(L+I)^{-1}$ in the weak sense. The equation $Lu=f$ is equivalent to $u=K(f+u)$, or
$$
\boxed{(I-K)u=Kf.}
$$
Conversely a solution in $L^2$ of this operator equation lies in $H^1$ because it equals $K(f+u)$, and satisfies the variational equation. The assumed boundedness $K:L^2\to H^1$, followed by the compact Rellich embedding $H^1\hookrightarrow L^2$, proves <compactness> of $K$ as an $L^2$ operator.

By the supplied kernel assumption, $\ker(I-K)$ consists of constants. Also $K1=1$. Since $K$ is a <self-adjoint operator>, the <Fredholm alternative> condition is $Kf\perp1$, and
$$
\langle Kf,1\rangle=\langle f,K1\rangle=\int_\Omega f.
$$
Therefore **a <weak solution> exists exactly when the mean of $f$ is zero**, and solutions differ by a constant. On a disconnected domain the actual kernel would consist of constants on each component and the condition would apply separately on each; the question's given kernel assumption specifies the single-constant case.

For a general coefficient matrix, the stated variational definition encodes zero conormal flux $(aDu)\cdot n=0$. It agrees with ordinary zero normal derivative only under additional conditions on $a$. The argument above uses the <weak formulation> explicitly defined in the paper, so does not require that stronger boundary identification.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

A <strictly convex function> satisfies that inequality strictly whenever $x\ne y$ and $0<t<1$.

For necessity of the strict supporting-tangent property, <convexity> gives a nonnegative gap along a segment from $z_0$ to $z$. If the gap at its other endpoint were zero, <convexity> of the gap and its zero initial value would force it to be zero all along the segment. That would make $F$ affine on this nontrivial segment, contrary to <strict convexity>. Conversely let $z=(1-t)x+ty$ with distinct endpoints. Apply the strict supporting inequality at $z$ to both endpoints, multiply by $1-t$ and $t$, and add. The linear terms cancel, yielding <strict convexity>. Thus
$$
\boxed{F\text{ strictly convex}\iff F(z)>F(z_0)+DF(z_0)\cdot(z-z_0)\text{ for }z\ne z_0.}
$$
For strict <Jensen's inequality>, put $m=(b-a)^{-1}\int_a^bu$. The supporting gap $F(u)-F(m)-DF(m)\cdot(u-m)$ is nonnegative everywhere and strictly positive where $u\ne m$. That set has positive measure because $u$ is not <almost everywhere> constant. Its integral is therefore strictly positive. The linear term integrates to zero, giving the requested **strict integral <Jensen inequality>**.

For the unlabelled Euler-Lagrange assertion, use its integrated form $f'(u')=c$ <almost everywhere>. This follows from the <first variation> $\int f'(u')\phi'=0$ for all compactly supported tests. If $u\in C^1$, the left side is continuous and so equals $c$ everywhere. Its derivative values lie in the finite fiber $(f')^{-1}(c)$, while the continuous image of the interval under $u'$ is connected. Thus $u'$ is constant. Endpoint values fix it to $m=(\beta-\alpha)/(b-a)$, giving exactly the affine $\bar u$. Conversely that affine function has constant $f'(\bar u')$, so is a solution. This proves **uniqueness among $C^1$ solutions**; the scalar integrand is the one-dimensional case of the notation in the source.

If $f$ is strictly convex, <Jensen's inequality> applied to $u'$ gives
$$
I(u)\geq(b-a)f\left(\frac1{b-a}\int_a^bu'\right)=(b-a)f(m)=I(\bar u).
$$
Equality in the strict inequality can occur only when $u'$ is <almost everywhere> constant. <Absolute continuity> and the endpoints then give $u=\bar u$. Hence **the affine function is the unique <global minimizer>**.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

A <global minimizer> has $I(\bar u)\leq I(v)$ for every admissible $v$. Global minimality implies strong local minimality, which implies weak local minimality. The reverse implications are not valid in general.

Where $f_v$ exists, the <strengthened Weierstrass condition> says that for some $\delta>0$, the excess
$$
E(x,y,p,q)=f(x,y,q)-f(x,y,p)-f_v(x,y,p)(q-p)
$$
is nonnegative for every $x\in[a,b]$, every trial slope $q\in\mathbb R$, and all $y,p$ with $|y-\bar u(x)|<\delta$, $|p-\bar u'(x)|<\delta$. This is a neighborhood condition in height and reference slope, not just an inequality on the reference curve.

The classical <Weierstrass sufficient condition>, with the usual smooth integrand regularity in addition to continuity, states that an admissible $C^1$ curve satisfying the <Euler-Lagrange equation>, a coercively positive <second variation>, and this strengthened condition is a strong local minimizer. A strict excess away from the reference slope gives the corresponding strict conclusion. The differentiability assumptions are part of that classical sufficient theorem; continuity alone does not define its first and second variations.

**Zero is a strict weak local minimizer and is not a strong local minimizer.** Write $D=\int_0^1u'^2$. For zero endpoint values, <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> applied to
$$
u(x)=(1-x)\int_0^xu'(t)dt-x\int_x^1u'(t)dt
$$
gives $|u(x)|^2\leq x(1-x)D$, hence $\int_0^1x^2u^2\leq D/20$. For sufficiently small slopes, Taylor expansion gives $\tfrac12[\sin(1+u'^2)-\sin1]\geq(\tfrac12\cos1-\varepsilon)u'^2$. Therefore
$$
I(u)-I(0)\geq\left(\frac12\cos1-\varepsilon-\frac1{20}\right)D>0
$$
for a suitably small fixed $\varepsilon$ and every nonzero sufficiently weakly close competitor.

For strong minimality take $a=\sqrt{3\pi/2-1}$ and $u_N(x)=a\operatorname{dist}(x,N^{-1}\mathbb Z)$. Their amplitudes tend to zero but slopes are $\pm a$, so $\sin(1+u_N'^2)=-1$. Thus $I(u_N)\leq-1/2<I(0)=\sin1/2$. Uniform closeness therefore does not prevent a decrease in energy.

= Solution
{parent=a}

With coefficients evaluated along $(x,\bar u,\bar u')$, put $p=f_{vv}$, $r=f_{uv}$ and $s=f_{uu}$. The <second variation> is
$$
q(\phi)=\delta^2I(\bar u)[\phi,\phi]=\int_a^b(p\phi'^2+2r\phi\phi'+s\phi^2)dx.
$$
The <Jacobi equation> in divergence form is $-(pz'+rz)'+rz'+sz=0$; if $r$ is differentiable it is $-(pz')'+(s-r')z=0$. A <conjugate point> $c>a$ admits a nonzero Jacobi solution vanishing at $a,c$.

There are two positivity conventions worth distinguishing. Strict pointwise positivity means $q(\phi)>0$ for each nonzero fixed-endpoint variation. Coercive positivity means $q(\phi)\geq c\int\phi'^2$ with one $c>0$ for all such variations; the endpoint Poincaré estimate makes this equivalent to a positive lower bound in the full $H^1$ <norm>. Under the regular Legendre hypothesis $p>0$, the necessary and sufficient Jacobi criterion is **no <conjugate point> in $(a,b]$**; in that regular case it gives both strict and coercive positivity. Equivalently the <Jacobi equation> has a solution positive on $[a,b]$, and the square identity
$$
q(\phi)=\int_a^bpz^2[(\phi/z)']^2dx
$$
gives positivity and a uniform derivative lower bound by the positive minimum of $z$ and boundedness of $z,z'$. Conversely a conjugate Jacobi field, extended by zero beyond its second zero, is a nonzero Lipschitz variation with $q=0$, contradicting either positivity convention.

Coercivity itself forces $p>0$: local high-frequency variations make the leading coefficient at least the coercivity constant. Mere strict positivity need not force it, as $q(\phi)=\int\phi^2$ shows. If that weaker convention is intended without regularity, a complete criterion is $p\geq0$, no <conjugate point> for every regularized form $q_\eta=q+\eta\int\phi'^2$ with $\eta>0$, and no nonzero zero-endpoint Lipschitz <weak solution> of the original <Jacobi equation>. The regularized criteria imply $q_\eta\geq0$, hence $q\geq0$ by passage to the limit. For a nonnegative quadratic form, $q(\phi)=0$ forces all mixed terms with test variations to vanish, giving exactly the weak Jacobi kernel; excluding it gives strict positivity. Necessity follows in reverse. This specifies the degenerate case without imposing an unstated strict Legendre condition.

For weak local minimality, sufficient conditions are the <Euler-Lagrange equation> and a coercively positive <second variation> with the stated smoothness. Taylor's remainder is bounded by $\varepsilon\|\phi\|_{H^1}^2$ for small $W^{1,\infty}$ perturbations, so the positive quadratic term dominates it. These are the classical **weak minimum sufficient conditions**.

At zero the quadratic form is
$$
\delta^2I(0)[\phi,\phi]=2Q_\lambda(\phi),\qquad Q_\lambda(u)=\int_0^1(u'^2+\lambda u^2).
$$
For $\lambda\geq0$ it dominates $\int u'^2$. For $-\pi^2<\lambda<0$ set $\mu=\sqrt{-\lambda}<\pi$ and $z(x)=\cos(\mu(x-1/2))$, positive on the closed interval and satisfying $z''+\mu^2z=0$. The Jacobi identity gives $Q_\lambda(u)=\int z^2[(u/z)']^2$. To obtain a uniform lower bound, let $m=\min z>0$, $K=\max|z'|$ and $v=u/z$. Since $\max z=1$ and $\int v^2\leq\tfrac12\int v'^2$,
$$
\int u'^2\leq2K^2\int v^2+2\int v'^2\leq(K^2+2)\int v'^2,
$$
so $Q_\lambda(u)\geq m^2\int u'^2/(K^2+2)$.

Finally $I(u)=Q_\lambda(u)+O(\int u^4)$ near zero, because $\sin^2u=u^2+O(u^4)$. The remainder is at most a constant times $\|u\|_\infty^2\int u^2\leq\tfrac12\|u\|_\infty^2\int u'^2$. It is dominated by the coercive bound for small perturbations. Thus **the <second variation> is positive and zero is a strict weak local minimizer for every $\lambda>-\pi^2$**. This argument has not assumed the sharp <Poincaré inequality> that is to be deduced next.

The continuous superlinear function $\Phi$ tends to positive infinity at both ends, so it has a finite global minimum $M$. Hence $I(u)\geq M(b-a)$ for every admissible function. An affine function with the prescribed endpoints is Lipschitz and has finite energy by continuity of the integrand on its compact range. Thus
$$
\boxed{-\infty<M(b-a)\leq l<\infty,}
$$
where the strict left inequality simply records finiteness of $M$.

= Solution

A <minimizing sequence> has an energy upper bound $C$. Set $H(t)=\Phi(t)-M\geq0$. Then $\int H(u_j')\leq C-M(b-a)=C_1$. Since $H(t)/|t|\to\infty$, for every $A>0$ there is $R_A$ such that $H(t)\geq A|t|$ for $|t|>R_A$. Consequently, for every measurable $E$,
$$
\int_E|u_j'|\leq R_A|E|+C_1/A.
$$
Taking first $A$ large and then $|E|$ small proves <uniform integrability>; taking $E=(a,b)$ also gives a uniform $L^1$ bound. The <Dunford-Pettis theorem> on a finite-measure space identifies bounded <uniformly integrable> subsets of $L^1$ with relatively weakly compact ones. Thus a subsequence satisfies
$$
\boxed{u_{j_k}'\rightharpoonup v\text{ in }L^1(a,b).}
$$

= Solution
{parent=iii}

**The <compactness> conclusion justified by the hypotheses is $u\in W^{1,1}$ with the prescribed traces; the printed claim $u\in W^{1,\infty}$ needs additional regularity.** Define
$$
u(x)=\alpha+\int_a^xv(t)dt.
$$
Then $u\in W^{1,1}$ and $u'=v$. Weak $L^1$ convergence tested against $\mathbf1_{(a,x)}$ gives $u_{j_k}(x)\to u(x)$ for every $x$, including the endpoint trace at $b$. <Uniform integrability> of the derivatives makes the functions equicontinuous: their increment over an interval is bounded by the integral of $|u_j'|$ on that interval, uniformly small for short intervals. Their fixed initial trace and common derivative $L^1$ bound also make them uniformly bounded. The <Arzelà-Ascoli theorem> therefore upgrades the convergence to <uniform convergence>, and in particular
$$
\boxed{u_{j_k}\to u\text{ in }L^1(a,b),\qquad u'=v,\quad u(a)=\alpha,\ u(b)=\beta.}
$$
<Uniform integrability> does not bound the derivative in $L^\infty$. The natural compact class here is absolutely continuous functions, rather than <Lipschitz functions>. This distinction can affect attainment: smooth strictly convex superlinear integrands can have non-Lipschitz minimizing limits, so it is not repaired merely by adding <convexity> in the last variable. If one additionally has a uniform Lipschitz bound on the <minimizing sequence>, weak testing on measurable subsets passes that bound to $v$ and proves the printed $u\in\mathcal A$. Alternatively one can formulate the direct method from the outset in the fixed-trace $W^{1,1}$ class. The proof above gives all the asserted convergence and trace conclusions without assuming the missing bound.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Provided the limit belongs to the original admissible class, the definition of the <infimum> and the assumed inequality give
$$
\boxed{l\leq I(u)\leq\liminf_k I(u_{j_k})=l,\qquad I(u)=l.}
$$
The needed fundamental property is sequential lower semicontinuity for the convergence in the <compactness> argument: uniform, or strong $L^1$, convergence of functions together with weak $L^1$ convergence of derivatives. The extra integrand hypothesis in the classical <Tonelli existence theorem> is <convexity> in the derivative variable $v$ for every fixed $(x,u)$, together with the stated coercivity and continuity. In one dimension the resulting integral is lower semicontinuous under the uniform/weak-derivative convergence above.

This lower-semicontinuity assertion does not itself supply the missing Lipschitz membership in (iii). With the natural fixed-trace $W^{1,1}$ admissible class and its own <infimum>, it completes Tonelli's direct existence argument. Retaining the printed Lipschitz class requires the additional membership estimate; changing admissible classes can otherwise encounter the <Lavrentiev phenomenon>.

= Solution

The affine competitor $1-x$ has finite energy and the energy is nonnegative. A <minimizing sequence> therefore has bounded $\int u_j'^2$. Its fixed value at zero gives $|u_j(x)|\leq1+\|u_j'\|_2$, so it is also bounded in $L^2$ and hence in $W^{1,2}$. The estimate
$$
|u_j(x)-u_j(y)|\leq\|u_j'\|_2|x-y|^{1/2}
$$
gives uniform <equicontinuity>. Extract a weakly convergent subsequence in $W^{1,2}$ and, by the <Arzelà-Ascoli theorem>, a uniformly convergent subsequence to the same limit $u$. The endpoint values are retained. <Uniform convergence> on a common bounded range gives $\int u_j^{2008}\to\int u^{2008}$, while the derivative <norm> is weakly lower semicontinuous. Consequently
$$
I(u)\leq\liminf_jI(u_j)=\inf_{\mathcal A}I,
$$
so **the global minimum is attained in the stated $W^{1,2}$ class**. In fact the gradient term is strictly convex on this fixed-endpoint affine class and $t^{2008}$ is convex, so the minimizer is unique as well.
