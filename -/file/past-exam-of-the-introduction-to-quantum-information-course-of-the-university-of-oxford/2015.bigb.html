= Solution

{c}

Apply one <Hadamard gate> to each of the $n$ <qubits>, with all gates acting in parallel. This <quantum circuit> implements the <tensor product> $H^{\otimes n}$; it needs $n$ single-qubit gates and one layer. For one <qubit>,
$$
H|x_j\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt2}\sum_{y_j=0}^1(-1)^{x_jy_j}|y_j\rangle.
$$
Taking the <tensor product> of these expressions multiplies the <probability amplitudes> and adds their exponents, giving
$$
\boxed{H^{\otimes n}|x\rangle=2^{-n/2}\sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^n}(-1)^{x\cdot y}|y\rangle.}
$$
Here the <dot product> is evaluated using <modular arithmetic> modulo two.

\Image[2015-hadamard-network.png]
{title=The <Hadamard transform> applies one <Hadamard gate> to each <qubit> in a parallel layer}

In particular, an initially zero <quantum register> becomes the <uniform quantum superposition>
$$
\boxed{H^{\otimes n}|0^n\rangle=2^{-n/2}\sum_x|x\rangle.}
$$
This is useful at the start of a <quantum algorithm> because a subsequent <quantum oracle> acts on all these components coherently. Later <quantum interference> can reveal a global property of the function. Measuring immediately would merely produce a uniformly random <binary string>; the useful resource is the preserved <quantum coherence>, rather than the ability to read every function value in one run.

Write $\oplus$ for bitwise <exclusive or>. Both <quantum registers> begin in $|0^n\rangle$. After the first <Hadamard transform> their joint <quantum state> is
$$
2^{-n/2}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^n}|x\rangle|0^n\rangle.
$$
The <quantum oracle> $U_f$ maps each component $|x\rangle|0^n\rangle$ to $|x\rangle|f(x)\rangle$. By <linearity>, the required <quantum state> is therefore
$$
\boxed{|\Psi\rangle=2^{-n/2}\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^n}|x\rangle|f(x)\rangle.}
$$
The different first-register <computational-basis states> are <orthogonal>, so there are $2^n$ orthogonal joint components of squared <probability amplitude> $2^{-n}$, even though some second-register values coincide. Thus the displayed <quantum state> is normalized.

An observed value $k$ must lie in the <image> of $f$. Choose either <preimage> $x_k$. The <two-to-one function> promise and $s\ne0^n$ imply that its <preimages> are exactly $x_k$ and $x_k\oplus s$. A <computational-basis measurement> of the second <quantum register> projects the joint <quantum state> onto the unnormalized vector
$$
2^{-n/2}\bigl(|x_k\rangle+|x_k\oplus s\rangle\bigr)|k\rangle.
$$
By the <Born rule>, $\Pr(k)=2^{1-n}$. Dividing by its square root gives the conditional first-register <pure state>
$$
\boxed{|\psi_k\rangle=\frac{|x_k\rangle+|x_k\oplus s\rangle}{\sqrt2}.}
$$
Choosing the other <preimage> merely interchanges the two summands. The result is a <Simon coset state>, retaining <quantum coherence> between the two inputs that share the measured function value. Outcomes outside the <image> have <probability> zero and hence no conditional state to normalize.

Apply the <Hadamard transform> to the conditional <quantum state> from <1/c>. Since $(x_k\oplus s)\cdot z=x_k\cdot z+s\cdot z$ modulo two, its output is
$$
H^{\otimes n}|\psi_k\rangle
=\frac1{\sqrt{2^{n+1}}}\sum_z(-1)^{x_k\cdot z}\bigl[1+(-1)^{s\cdot z}\bigr]|z\rangle.
$$
If $s\cdot z=1$, the two <probability amplitudes> cancel by destructive <quantum interference>. If $s\cdot z=0$, they add, giving
$$
\boxed{\Pr(z\mid k)=
\begin{cases}
2^{1-n},&z\cdot s=0,\\
0,&z\cdot s=1.
\end{cases}}
$$
Thus **every observed string obeys** $\boxed{z\cdot s=0}$. This <Simon Fourier sampling> is uniform on the $(n-1)$-dimensional <vector subspace> $s^\perp$ over the two-element <field>. Indeed, the nonzero <linear functional> $z\mapsto z\cdot s$ has a <kernel> of dimension $n-1$ by the <rank-nullity theorem>. The distribution does not depend on $k$, so measuring or discarding the second <quantum register> gives the same statistics for the first.

= Solution

Each run of <Simon's algorithm> uses **one oracle call** and returns one uniform sample from $s^\perp$. Collect samples as rows of a <matrix> $Z$ and solve $Zs=0$ over the two-element <field> using <Gaussian elimination>. Once the rows have <matrix rank> $n-1$, the <kernel> is $\{0,s\}$, so the promised nonzero vector is uniquely determined. Dependent samples, including $z=0$, must be allowed for; exactly $n-1$ runs need not suffice.

For a rough quantitative estimate, put $d=n-1$. When the existing samples span an $r$-dimensional <vector subspace> of $s^\perp$, the next sample increases the <matrix rank> with <probability> $1-2^{r-d}$. The <random spanning of a binary vector space> estimate gives
$$
\mathbb E Q=\sum_{r=0}^{d-1}\frac1{1-2^{r-d}}
=d+\sum_{j=1}^d\frac1{2^j-1}<d+2=n+1.
$$
Alternatively, $m=d+t$ samples fail to span with <probability> less than $2^{-t}$: failure means some nonzero <linear functional> on the $d$-dimensional space vanishes on every sample, and a <union bound> gives $(2^d-1)2^{-m}$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{Q_{\rm quantum}=O(n)\text{ for fixed error},\qquad
Q_{\rm quantum}=O\bigl(n+\log(1/\varepsilon)\bigr)\text{ for error }\varepsilon.}
$$
The <Gaussian elimination> requires only a polynomial amount of classical work, for example $O(n^3)$ bit operations for $O(n)$ samples. The degenerate case $n=1$ needs no calls because the promise already specifies $s=1$.

Classically, distinct inputs with equal function values reveal $s=x\oplus x'$. Uniformly sampling distinct inputs finds such a <function collision> after roughly $\sqrt{2^n}$ calls, by the <birthday square-root scale>. For $q$ sampled inputs, the expected number of colliding pairs is $\binom q2/(2^n-1)$. The usual randomized classical <query complexity> of <Simon's problem> is therefore exponential in $n$, on the scale
$$
\boxed{Q_{\rm classical}=\Theta(2^{n/2})\text{ for bounded error},\qquad
Q_{\rm quantum}=O(n).}
$$
A straightforward exhaustive classical search instead uses $O(2^n)$ calls. The square-root collision estimate is the sharper classical comparison; the quantum advantage concerns access to the promised <quantum oracle>, rather than an explicit table of all function values.

For any normalized vector $|u\rangle$, the <Born rule> assigns the <probability> $\langle u|\rho|u\rangle$ to the corresponding rank-one <orthogonal projection>. This must be nonnegative for every $u$, which is exactly <positive semidefiniteness>. For any complete <orthonormal basis> $\{|j\rangle\}$, the outcome <probabilities> must sum to one:
$$
\sum_j\langle j|\rho|j\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}\rho=1.
$$
Thus a <Hermitian matrix> represents a physical <density matrix> only if
$$
\boxed{\rho\geq0,\qquad\operatorname{Tr}\rho=1.}
$$
These conditions are also sufficient in finite dimension. The <spectral theorem> writes $\rho=\sum_jp_j|u_j\rangle\langle u_j|$ with $p_j\geq0$ and $\sum_jp_j=1$, describing a <quantum state ensemble> of <pure states> with valid mixing <probabilities>. A <Hermitian matrix> alone could have negative <eigenvalues>, hence negative measurement <probabilities>.

Expand the given <pure state> in the ordered <computational basis> $00,01,10,11$:
$$
|\psi\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt3}|00\rangle-\frac1{\sqrt6}|01\rangle
+\frac1{\sqrt3}|10\rangle+\frac1{\sqrt6}|11\rangle,
\qquad
v=\frac1{\sqrt6}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt2\\-1\\\sqrt2\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The coefficients have squared magnitudes $1/3,1/6,1/3,1/6$, summing to one. The <density matrix> is the <outer product> $vv^\dagger$, not the diagonal matrix of these four <probabilities>: its off-diagonal entries retain <quantum coherence>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
=\frac16\begin{pmatrix}
2&-\sqrt2&2&\sqrt2\\
-\sqrt2&1&-\sqrt2&-1\\
2&-\sqrt2&2&\sqrt2\\
\sqrt2&-1&\sqrt2&1
\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
The <outer product> is a <Hermitian matrix> and is <positive semidefinite>: $u^\dagger\rho u=|v^\dagger u|^2\geq0$. Its <trace> is one, and $\rho^2=\rho$, as required for a <pure state>.

Let $a_{ij}$ be the coefficient of $|ij\rangle$. The <partial trace> contracts the repeated index of the discarded <qubit>:
$$
(\rho_1)_{ii'}=\sum_{j=0}^1a_{ij}\overline{a_{i'j}},
\qquad
(\rho_2)_{jj'}=\sum_{i=0}^1a_{ij}\overline{a_{ij'}}.
$$
For the first <qubit>, each diagonal entry is $1/3+1/6=1/2$ and the upper off-diagonal entry is $1/3-1/6=1/6$. For the second <qubit>, the diagonal entries are $2/3$ and $1/3$, while the upper off-diagonal entry is $-1/\sqrt{18}+1/\sqrt{18}=0$. Thus the two <reduced density matrices>, each in <computational basis> order $0,1$, are
$$
\boxed{\rho_1=\operatorname{Tr}_2\rho=\begin{pmatrix}1/2&1/6\\1/6&1/2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\rho_2=\operatorname{Tr}_1\rho=\begin{pmatrix}2/3&0\\0&1/3\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Both have <eigenvalues> $2/3,1/3$ and <trace> one, so both are <mixed states> even though the joint <quantum state> is pure. Their different <eigenvectors> are visible in the useful rewrite
$$
|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{\frac23}|+\rangle|0\rangle-\sqrt{\frac13}|-\rangle|1\rangle,
\qquad |\pm\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}.
$$
Absorbing the minus sign into one local basis vector makes this a <Schmidt decomposition> with nonnegative coefficients. It also checks both <partial traces> and exhibits the <entanglement> responsible for the mixed local descriptions.

For a <Hermitian matrix> $A$, the <spectral theorem> gives $A=U\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_d)U^\dagger$, with real <eigenvalues> and a <unitary matrix> $U$. Hence
$$
A^\dagger A=A^2=U\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1^2,\ldots,\lambda_d^2)U^\dagger,
\qquad
\sqrt{A^\dagger A}=U\operatorname{diag}(|\lambda_1|,\ldots,|\lambda_d|)U^\dagger.
$$
The square root here is the unique <positive square root of an operator>. Taking the <trace>, which is unchanged by <unitary similarity>, proves
$$
\boxed{\|A\|_{\rm tr}=\sum_{j=1}^d|\lambda_j|.}
$$
For a <density matrix>, all <eigenvalues> are nonnegative and sum to one. Its <trace norm> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\|\rho\|_{\rm tr}=\operatorname{Tr}\rho=1.}
$$
The absolute values cannot be omitted for a general <Hermitian matrix>, since positive and negative <eigenvalues> can cancel in its <trace> but never in its <trace norm>.

= Solution

With no access to the discarded <qubit>, the two preparations are exactly the <reduced density matrices> in <2/c>, with equal prior <probabilities>. Their difference is
$$
\Delta=\rho_1-\rho_2=\frac16\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad \Delta^2=\frac1{18}I.
$$
Since $\operatorname{Tr}\Delta=0$, its <eigenvalues> are $\pm\sqrt2/6$. The <trace norm> and <trace distance> are consequently
$$
\|\Delta\|_{\rm tr}=\frac{\sqrt2}{3},
\qquad
\boxed{T(\rho_1,\rho_2)=\frac{\sqrt2}{6}.}
$$
The <Holevo–Helstrom theorem> gives the optimum
$$
\boxed{P_{\rm success}^{\rm max}=\frac12\left(1+\frac{\sqrt2}{6}\right)
=\frac12+\frac{\sqrt2}{12}\simeq0.617851.}
$$
To establish attainability, rather than only an upper bound, measure the <Hermitian operator>
$$
B=\frac{X-Z}{\sqrt2},\qquad
X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},
$$
where $X,Z$ are <Pauli matrices>. Since $\Delta=(\sqrt2/6)B$, report **first qubit for the positive outcome; second qubit for the negative outcome**. The corresponding <orthogonal projections> are $E_\pm=(I\pm B)/2$. Their success <probability> is
$$
\frac12\operatorname{Tr}(E_+\rho_1)+\frac12\operatorname{Tr}(E_-\rho_2)
=\frac12+\frac12\operatorname{Tr}(E_+\Delta)
=\frac12+\frac{\sqrt2}{12},
$$
which reaches the bound. Identical <eigenvalues> of the two <density matrices> do not make them indistinguishable: their eigenvectors differ, and this <quantum measurement> detects that difference.

Use the conventional <phase gate> $P_\varphi=\operatorname{diag}(1,e^{i\varphi})$ in <computational basis> order $0,1$. The single-qubit <Hadamard gate> and the successive <quantum states> are
$$
\boxed{H=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix},}
\qquad
|0\rangle\xrightarrow{H}\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}
\xrightarrow{P_\varphi}\frac{|0\rangle+e^{i\varphi}|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}.
$$
The final <Hadamard gate> recombines the two components, giving
$$
\boxed{|\psi_{\rm out}\rangle=
\frac{1+e^{i\varphi}}2|0\rangle+\frac{1-e^{i\varphi}}2|1\rangle
=e^{i\varphi/2}\left(\cos\frac\varphi2|0\rangle-i\sin\frac\varphi2|1\rangle\right).}
$$
The leading factor in the last expression is a <global phase>. The <Born rule> therefore yields
$$
\boxed{P_0(\varphi)=\left|\frac{1+e^{i\varphi}}2\right|^2
=\frac{1+\cos\varphi}{2}=\cos^2\frac\varphi2.}
$$
At $\varphi=0$ the output is certainly zero, whereas at $\varphi=\pi$ it is certainly one. The <phase gate> changes no intermediate outcome <probabilities>; the final <Hadamard gate> converts a relative <quantum phase> into an observable <quantum interference> fringe.

Write $c=\langle e_0|e_1\rangle=ve^{i\alpha}$. The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives $0\leq v\leq1$ because the two environment states are normalized. Immediately after the interaction, the joint <quantum state> is
$$
|\Psi\rangle=\frac{|0\rangle|e_0\rangle+e^{i\varphi}|1\rangle|e_1\rangle}{\sqrt2}.
$$
Applying the last <Hadamard gate> gives
$$
|\Psi_{\rm out}\rangle=\frac12\left[
|0\rangle\bigl(|e_0\rangle+e^{i\varphi}|e_1\rangle\bigr)
+|1\rangle\bigl(|e_0\rangle-e^{i\varphi}|e_1\rangle\bigr)\right].
$$
Since the environment is unobserved, the <Born rule> uses the squared <norm> of the environment-valued coefficient of $|0\rangle$:
$$
P_0=\frac14\left[2+e^{i\varphi}\langle e_0|e_1\rangle
+e^{-i\varphi}\langle e_1|e_0\rangle\right].
$$
Thus the <Hadamard interference with environment overlap> formula is
$$
\boxed{P_0(\varphi,v,\alpha)=\frac{1+v\cos(\varphi+\alpha)}2.}
$$
The plus sign follows from the explicitly chosen <phase gate> and the order $\langle e_0|e_1\rangle$ of the <inner product>. Equivalently, before the last <Hadamard gate> the <partial trace> over the environment gives
$$
\rho=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}
1&v e^{-i(\varphi+\alpha)}\\
v e^{i(\varphi+\alpha)}&1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The <complex environment overlap in qubit dephasing> both reduces the <quantum coherence> by $v$ and adds a relative <quantum phase> $\alpha$. Orthogonal environment states have $v=0$ and give $P_0=1/2$ for every $\varphi$. If $v=1$, the <quantum interference> fringe has full contrast and is only shifted; the <qubit> has not become mixed. For $v=0$, $\alpha$ is immaterial.

Moving the interaction to between the first <Hadamard gate> and the <phase gate> gives
$$
\frac{|0\rangle|e_0\rangle+|1\rangle|e_1\rangle}{\sqrt2}
\xrightarrow{P_\varphi\otimes I}
\frac{|0\rangle|e_0\rangle+e^{i\varphi}|1\rangle|e_1\rangle}{\sqrt2},
$$
the identical joint <quantum state>. Therefore **the expression is unchanged**. This is the <commutation of computational-basis dephasing and phase gates>; it uses the specified population-preserving interaction with the same environment states, rather than an arbitrary model of <quantum decoherence>.

Normalize the second <qubit> as $|-\rangle=(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$. Since a <Pauli X gate> changes $|-\rangle$ to $-|-\rangle$, the <Boolean quantum oracle> acts as
$$
U_f|x\rangle|-\rangle=(-1)^{f(x)}|x\rangle|-\rangle.
$$
This <quantum phase kickback> leaves the second <qubit> uncorrelated with the first. Put $b=f(0)\oplus f(1)$: $b=0$ for a <constant function> and $b=1$ for a <balanced Boolean function>. Apart from the irrelevant <global phase> $(-1)^{f(0)}$, the first <qubit> and environment reach
$$
\frac{|0\rangle|e_0\rangle+(-1)^b|1\rangle|e_1\rangle}{\sqrt2}.
$$
The population-preserving interaction commutes with the <quantum oracle> on this answer-register input, so its placement before or after the oracle, between the two <Hadamard gates>, gives the same result. Substitute $\varphi=\pi b$ into <3/b>:
$$
\boxed{P(0\mid b)=\frac{1+(-1)^b v\cos\alpha}{2},\qquad
P(1\mid b)=\frac{1-(-1)^b v\cos\alpha}{2}.}
$$
The printed <Deutsch algorithm> uses the rule **zero means <constant function>; one means <balanced Boolean function>**. With that rule, the <Deutsch algorithm with pure dephasing> succeeds for either class with
$$
\boxed{P_{\rm correct}^{\rm printed}=\frac{1+v\cos\alpha}{2}.}
$$
For real nonnegative environment overlap this is $(1+v)/2$, ranging from certainty at $v=1$ to random guessing at $v=0$. For general complex overlap, a coherent shift can invert the printed answer: for example $v=1,\alpha=\pi$ makes the printed rule always wrong. If the overlap is known but the printed <quantum measurement> is retained, choose the better labeling of its outcomes; this gives
$$
\boxed{P_{\rm correct}^{\rm relabeled}=\frac{1+|v\cos\alpha|}{2}.}
$$
There is a further improvement if a known $\alpha$ can be compensated by the <phase gate> $P_{-\alpha}$ before the final <Hadamard gate>. It removes the coherent shift without reversing any actual <quantum decoherence>, and yields
$$
\boxed{P_{\rm correct}^{\rm compensated}=\frac{1+v}{2}.}
$$
This is optimal when only the first <qubit> is accessible, with equal prior <probabilities> for the two classes. Before the final <Hadamard gate>, their <reduced density matrices> are
$$
\rho_b=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&(-1)^b\overline c\\(-1)^bc&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The difference $\rho_0-\rho_1$ has <eigenvalues> $\pm v$, giving <trace distance> $v$. The <Holevo–Helstrom theorem> therefore bounds the success <probability> by $(1+v)/2$, exactly attained by the compensated procedure. A known phase shift and loss of coherence have different effects: at $v=0$ the two <density matrices> coincide and no measurement on the available <qubit> can reveal the answer.
