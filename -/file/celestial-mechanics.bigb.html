= Celestial mechanics
{wiki}

Celestial mechanics studies motion under gravitational forces.

= Orbital speed
{parent=Celestial mechanics}
{wiki}
{title2=$v$}

Orbital <speed> is the magnitude of the <velocity> of an orbiting body in a specified reference frame. For a Newtonian <circular orbit> around a point source of <mass> $M$, equating $v^2/r$ to $GM/r^2$ gives $v=\sqrt{GM/r}$. In a curved <spacetime>, a speed inferred from coordinate radius and coordinate time generally differs from a speed measured by a local observer. For a circular orbit in the <Schwarzschild metric>, a static local observer measures $v_{\rm local}=r|d\phi/dt|/\sqrt{1-2GM/(c^2r)}$; $r|d\phi/dt|$ uses the clock normalization at infinity instead.

= Radial orbit time transformation
{parent=Celestial mechanics}
{title2=$d\tau/dt=(q/r)dq/dr$}

For a central-force radial <energy> equation, choose a monotone radius $q=q(r)$ and clock $\tau$ such that $d\tau/dt=(q/r)dq/dr$. Then $q'=(r/q)\dot r$, and multiplying the old <energy> equation by $r^2/q^2$ preserves the centrifugal form $L^2/(2q^2)$. A split of the remaining terms into constant <energy> and a new <Newtonian gravitational potential> produces a transformed radial orbit.

= Angular reconstruction of a radial orbit transformation
{parent=Radial orbit time transformation}
{title2=$d\bar\theta/d\tau=L/q^2$}

A transformed radial equation containing $L^2/(2q^2)$ becomes a full planar central-force orbit by choosing $d\bar\theta/d\tau=L/q^2$. For $d\tau/dt=(q/r)dq/dr$ and old <angular velocity> $L/r^2$, this gives $d\bar\theta/d\theta=(r/q)dq/dr$. Keeping the old angle generally fails to preserve the stated transformed <angular momentum>.

= Kepler–isochrone radial transformation
{c}
{parent=Radial orbit time transformation}
{title2=$r=\sqrt{\bar E/(\lambda E)}(\sqrt{\bar r^2+b^2}-b)$}

With $a=(1-\lambda)GM$, $h=\lambda E>0$ and $\bar E>0$, set $\bar E q^2=hr^2+ar$. If $a\geq0$, its positive-radius root is $r=\sqrt{\bar E/h}(\sqrt{q^2+b^2}-b)$, with $b=a/(2\sqrt{h\bar E})$. The transformed <Newtonian gravitational potential> is an additive constant plus $-G\bar M/(b+\sqrt{q^2+b^2})$, the <spherical isochrone model>. The <mass> sign and branch conditions must be checked.

= Attractive branch of the Kepler–isochrone transformation
{parent=Kepler–isochrone radial transformation}

For bound old <Kepler orbits>, $E<0$, $\lambda<0$ and $\bar E>0$ give a positive radius, positive isochrone scale $b$, and positive transformed <mass>. The shifted <energy> is $\bar E-k=\bar E/\lambda<0$. For unbound old orbits with $E>0$, $1/2<\lambda\leq1$ gives an attractive unbound isochrone branch. The case $\lambda=0$ is handled by the separate <Kepler–harmonic radial duality>.

= Kepler–harmonic radial duality
{c}
{parent=Radial orbit time transformation}
{title2=$r=(\bar E/GM)\bar r^2$}

Taking $\bar E=GM\,r/q^2>0$ in a <radial orbit time transformation> gives $r=(\bar E/GM)q^2$ and $\bar\Phi(q)=-E(\bar E/GM)^2q^2$. Bound <Kepler orbits> with $E<0$ map to a confining <harmonic oscillator>; positive <energy> maps to an inverted oscillator. Full angular reconstruction halves the original orbital angle.

= Two-body problem
{parent=Celestial mechanics}
{wiki}

The two-body problem determines the motion of two particles interacting only with one another. For a central force it separates into uniform centre-of-mass motion and one relative-coordinate problem with the reduced mass.

= Equal-mass Coulomb circular orbit
{parent=Two-body problem}
{title2=$T=2\pi\sqrt{ms^3/(2k_eq^2)}$}

For two particles of equal <mass> $m$ and opposite charges $\pm q$ separated by $s$, the <centre of mass> lies halfway between them. In a nonradiating classical model, each radius is $s/2$ and $k_eq^2/s^2=m\omega^2s/2$. Hence $\omega^2=2k_eq^2/(ms^3)$ and $T=2\pi\sqrt{ms^3/(2k_eq^2)}$. Equivalently, relative motion has <reduced mass> $m/2$. Radiation and quantum binding invalidate an indefinitely stable classical orbit for actual microscopic charges.

= Laboratory paths of an equal-mass binary
{parent=Two-body problem}

For equal masses and relative <position> $\mathbf r$, the individual positions are $\mathbf r_A=\mathbf R-\mathbf r/2$ and $\mathbf r_B=\mathbf R+\mathbf r/2$, where the <center of mass> has uniform <velocity> in an isolated system. Bound <inverse-square force> motion makes $\mathbf r$ an <ellipse>; each mass follows a half-size ellipse in the center-of-mass frame. Adding a nonzero center-of-mass drift generally makes their laboratory <trajectories> nonclosed. The relative orbital period is not necessarily a period of either laboratory position.

= Equal-mass circular binary
{parent=Two-body problem}
{title2=$\omega^2=Gm/(4a^3)$}

Two equal point <masses> can move around the same fixed circle of radius $a$ in diametrically opposite positions. Their stationary <centre of mass> is the circle's center. A separation $2a$ gives gravitational <force> $Gm^2/(4a^2)$ on each <mass>, and <Newton's second law> equates it to $ma\omega^2$. The common <angular speed> is therefore fixed by the displayed relation. Opposite tangential <velocities> produce the same sense of angular rotation.

= Negative-energy gravitational collision
{parent=Two-body problem}
{title2=$t_{\mathrm{coll}}=\pi\sqrt{a^3/[8G(m_1+m_2)]}$}

Two point <masses> released from rest at separation $a$ have negative <mechanical energy> $-Gm_1m_2/a$ but collide in time $\pi\sqrt{a^3/[8G(m_1+m_2)]}$. Their separation approaches zero along a regular trajectory before collision. The <energy> sign alone therefore does not supply a positive lower separation bound.

= Asymptotic minimum speed of an unbound Kepler orbit
{parent=Kepler orbit}
{title2=$v_\infty=\sqrt{2E/m}$}

For a positive-mass object in the inverse-square attractive field with potential zero at infinity, energy conservation gives $v^2(r)=2E/m+2GM/r$. Along the outgoing unbound branch, speed decreases toward the displayed limiting value. It is positive for a hyperbola and zero for a parabola. This is an infimum approached at infinite separation, not a finite-radius stopping point or the local <escape velocity>.

= Pericentre-speed qualification of Kepler orbit classes
{parent=Kepler orbit}
{title2=$v_p=k\sqrt{GM/r_p},\quad k\ge1$}

At a genuine <pericentre>, the radial velocity vanishes and the radial acceleration is nonnegative. The radial equation therefore requires $v_p^2\ge GM/r_p$. In terms of the displayed factor k, a circle has k=1, a noncircular ellipse has $1<k<\sqrt2$, a parabola has $k=\sqrt2$ and a hyperbola has $k>\sqrt2$. If $0<k<1$ at a tangential turning point, that point is the apocentre, despite the negative orbital energy. Bound-energy classification alone does not establish which turning point is being specified.

= Kepler velocity hodograph
{c}
{parent=Kepler orbit}
{title2=$\boldsymbol v=(\mu_g/h)(-\sin f,\cos f+e)$}

In axes directed along the <periapsis> and transverse to it, a <Kepler orbit> with gravitational parameter $\mu_g$ and <specific angular momentum> $h$ has the displayed <velocity>. The <velocity> tip traces a circle of radius $\mu_g/h$ centred at $(0,e\mu_g/h)$. To derive it, differentiate $r=h^2/[\mu_g(1+e\cos f)]$, use $r^2\dot f=h$, and resolve $\dot r\widehat{\boldsymbol r}+r\dot f\widehat{\boldsymbol\theta}$. A rotation of the spatial axes rotates the entire hodograph by the same angle.

= Apsidal speed sum for an attractive inverse-square orbit
{parent=Kepler orbit}
{title2=$v_{\min}+v_{\max}=2Q/h$}

For unit mass with <inverse-square potential> $-Q/r$, $Q>0$, and positive conserved <angular momentum> $h$, a bounded orbit has $-Q^2/(2h^2)\le E<0$. Its <periapsis distance> and <apoapsis distance> are roots of $|E|r^2-Qr+h^2/2=0$, so their sum is $Q/|E|$ and product $h^2/(2|E|)$. Speed is largest at the <periapsis> and smallest at the <apoapsis>, because $v^2=2(E+Q/r)$ decreases with $r$. Radial velocity is zero there, so the two speeds are $h/r_{\min}$ and $h/r_{\max}$. Their sum is $h(r_{\min}+r_{\max})/(r_{\min}r_{\max})=2Q/h$, independent of $E$, including the circular limit.

= Velocity kick on a circular Kepler orbit
{parent=Kepler orbit}
{title2=$a'/a=(1-2\gamma\cos\theta-\gamma^2)^{-1}$}

For an instantaneous in-plane change of <velocity> of magnitude $\gamma v_K$ on a <circular orbit>, with angle $\theta$ measured from the forward tangential direction, the new <specific orbital energy> gives $a'/a=(1-2\gamma\cos\theta-\gamma^2)^{-1}$. The <specific angular momentum> gives $e'^2=1-(1-2\gamma\cos\theta-\gamma^2)(1+\gamma\cos\theta)^2$. The signed <semi-major axis> becomes negative for an unbound <hyperbolic Kepler orbit>.

= Kick-orbit distribution
{parent=Velocity kick on a circular Kepler orbit}
{title2=$f_c(c)=1/(\pi\sqrt{1-c^2})$}

Uniform planar kick angle $\theta$ gives $c=\cos\theta$ with probability density $1/(\pi\sqrt{1-c^2})$. This maps onto a one-dimensional curve in <orbital eccentricity>–<semi-major axis> space. The two radial-kick signs share that curve but have opposite tangential components of the <eccentricity vector>. The resulting density is enhanced near tangential-kick endpoints.

= Kepler radial energy equation
{c}
{parent=Kepler orbit}
{title2=$\frac12\dot r^2+\frac{L^2}{2r^2}=E+GM/r$}

For unit <mass> in $\Phi=-GM/r$, <conservation of angular momentum> splits the <velocity> norm into $\dot r^2+L^2/r^2$. <Conservation of energy> then gives $\tfrac12\dot r^2+L^2/(2r^2)=E+GM/r$. It separates radial dynamics from the reconstruction of the orbital angle.

= Two-body orbital energy
{parent=Kepler orbit}
{title2=$E=\mu\varepsilon$}

= Binary orbital energy
{synonym}

In the centre-of-mass frame of two Newtonian point masses, total orbital energy is reduced mass times <specific orbital energy>: $E=\mu[|\dot{\mathbf r}|^2/2-GM/r]$. The factor $\mu$ multiplies both kinetic and potential contributions, while $GM$ is the relative gravitational parameter.

= Polar equation of a Kepler orbit
{parent=Kepler orbit}
{title2=$r=p/(1+e\cos f)$}

For an attractive <inverse-square force>, $r=p/(1+e\cos f)$, where $f$ is <true anomaly>, $e$ is <orbital eccentricity>, and $p=h^2/\mu$ is the semilatus rectum. The <specific angular momentum> relation $\dot f=h/r^2$ converts this geometric equation into an angular evolution law.

= Circumplanetary orbit
{parent=Kepler orbit}

An <orbit> about a <planet>. Stellar tides, <radiation pressure> and other perturbations can limit its stability; a sufficiently small <semi-major axis> compared with the <Hill sphere> permits a local approximately planet-centred <Kepler orbit>.

= Planet-synchronous orbit
{parent=Circumplanetary orbit}
{title2=$T_{\rm orb}=P_{\rm spin}$}

= Synchronous circumplanetary orbit
{synonym}

A <circumplanetary orbit> whose <orbital period> equals the central planet's spin period. Its <semi-major axis> follows from <Kepler's third law>. Matching periods alone does not fix the satellite above one ground location; that additionally requires a <planet-stationary orbit>.

= Planet-stationary orbit
{parent=Planet-synchronous orbit}

= Stationary circumplanetary orbit
{synonym}

A prograde, equatorial <circular orbit> with <orbital period> equal to the planet's spin period. In the ideal model its satellite remains above one equatorial ground location. Nonzero <orbital inclination> or <orbital eccentricity> destroys exact stationarity even when the periods agree.

= Radiation pressure on circumplanetary dust
{parent=Circumplanetary orbit}

The leading uniform stellar <radiation pressure> is a differential <acceleration> relative to a <planet>, of scale $\beta GM_\star/a_p^2$. Relative to planetocentric gravity at dust radius $a$, its magnitude is
$$
\eta_{\rm rad}=\beta\frac{M_\star}{M_p}\left(\frac a{a_p}\right)^2.
$$
Thus lying inside a <Hill sphere> alone does not guarantee small radiative perturbations. Approximately circular tightly bound dust requires this ratio sufficiently small, as well as weak tides; the uniform radiative <force> can otherwise excite <orbital eccentricity> or unbind grains.

= Shadowing of circumplanetary dust
{parent=Radiation pressure on circumplanetary dust}

A <planet> can block stellar radiation during part of a <circumplanetary orbit>. Then the full-orbit constant-flux averages for <radiation pressure> and <Poynting–Robertson drag> require modification. The detailed shadow duty cycle depends on the <orbital inclination>, stellar angular extent and dust radius.

= Circumstellar orbit
{parent=Kepler orbit}

An <orbit> about a <star>. Dust on such an <orbit> can experience <radiation pressure> and <Poynting–Robertson drag>, in addition to gravitational perturbations.

= Binet equation
{parent=Kepler orbit}
{c}
{wiki}

Binet’s equation rewrites a central-force orbit using $u(\theta)=1/r$. If $\ell=mr^2\dot\theta$ is angular momentum and the potential is $V(r)$, then
$$
u''+u=\frac{m}{\ell^2u^2}\frac{dV}{dr}\bigg|_{r=1/u}.
$$

= Circular orbit through an inverse-fifth-power singularity
{parent=Binet equation}
{title2=$c=8mh^2R^2,\quad T=2\pi R^2/|h|$}

For an attractive <central force> of magnitude $c/r^5$, a circle of radius $R$ through the force center has polar equation $r=2R\cos\theta$. With <specific angular momentum> $h$, substitution into the <Binet equation> gives $c=8mh^2R^2$. Consequently $R$ is inversely proportional to the magnitude of the <angular momentum>, and the angular-time integral gives $T=2\pi R^2/|h|\propto R^3$. This is the finite travel time along the punctured circle from one approach to the singular center to the next; the force law alone does not specify continuation through the collision.

= Orbit equation for combined inverse-square and inverse-cube attraction
{parent=Binet equation}
{title2=$u''+(1-B/l^2)u=A/l^2$}

For radial acceleration $-A/r^2-B/r^3$, conserved <specific angular momentum> $l$ and $u=1/r$ reduce the <Binet equation> to the displayed linear equation. If $l^2>B$, its angular oscillation frequency is $\sqrt{1-B/l^2}$; attraction proportional to $r^{-3}$ changes the apsidal angle. A zero of $u$ ends the physical positive-radius branch and corresponds to escape, not passage to a negative radius. A spatial self-intersection need not be a periodic return because <velocity> may differ.

= Apsidal invariant on an unbound central-force branch
{parent=Orbit equation for combined inverse-square and inverse-cube attraction}
{title2=$\psi_{\rm peri}=\psi-\operatorname{Arg}(u-u_c-iu'/\nu)/\nu$}

For $u=u_c[1+e\cos\nu(\psi-\psi_0)]$ with $\nu>0$ and $e\geq1$, the positive-radius scattering branch has $|\nu(\psi-\psi_0)|<\arccos(-1/e)\leq\pi$. Thus the principal complex argument in the displayed expression is continuous throughout that branch, unlike at an apocentre of a bound orbit. Its derivative is $\nu$, so the angle modulo $2\pi$, or the associated unit apsidal vector in the orbital plane, is a stationary <integral of motion> fixing the periapsis direction. Together with energy and three components of <specific angular momentum>, it gives five regular scattering labels even for irrational $\nu$. Periodicity is therefore necessary for five orbit labels in recurrent bound motion, not in general for escaping trajectories.

= Closed orbits with an inverse-cube perturbation
{parent=Orbit equation for combined inverse-square and inverse-cube attraction}
{title2=$\nu=\sqrt{1-2\mu a/L^2}\in\mathbb Q_{>0}$}

In the <gravitational potential> $-\mu(1/r+a/r^2)$, a regular noncircular bound <orbit> requires $L^2>2\mu a$ and has $u=\mu(L^2-2\mu a)^{-1}[1+e\cos\nu(\psi-\psi_0)]$ with $0<e<1$. Each radial period advances the angle by $2\pi/\nu$. The full position and <velocity> return after finitely many radial periods precisely when $\nu$ is rational. Irrational $\nu$ gives a dense rosette on the two-frequency <invariant torus>. Rationality permits a fifth <orbit> label fixing the apsidal phase on that resonant <angular momentum> family, but does not give global superintegrability for all nearby angular momenta. When $a=0$, every regular bound <Kepler orbit> is closed and the <Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector> supplies the globally defined additional integral.

= Inverse-cube perturbation of Kepler apsidal motion
{parent=Orbit equation for combined inverse-square and inverse-cube attraction}
{title2=$\Delta\varpi\simeq-\pi C/[\mu a(1-e^2)]$}

For radial acceleration $-\mu/r^2+C/r^3$, the <Binet equation> gives $u''+(1+C/h^2)u=\mu/h^2$, where $h$ is <specific angular momentum>. A regular bound orbit has $h^2+C>0$, $a=-\mu/(2\mathcal E)$ and $e^2=1+2\mathcal E(h^2+C)/\mu^2$. Its radius is $a(1-e^2)/[1+e\cos(\alpha\theta)]$, with $\alpha=\sqrt{1+C/h^2}$. The <apsidal precession> per radial period is $2\pi(\alpha^{-1}-1)$, which reduces to the displayed expression for a small perturbation. Negative $C$ produces prograde precession; positive $C$ produces retrograde precession. The dimensionless coefficient is $C/(\mu a)$, since $C$ has units of length to the fourth power divided by time squared.

= Inferring an inverse-cube perturbation from apsidal precession
{parent=Inverse-cube perturbation of Kepler apsidal motion}
{title2=$\gamma\simeq p\,\Delta\varpi/\pi$}

For radial acceleration $-GM(1+\gamma/r)/r^2$, conserved <specific angular momentum> $h$ gives an angular radial frequency $\alpha=\sqrt{1-GM\gamma/h^2}$ through the <Binet equation>. Successive pericentres differ by $2\pi/\alpha$, so a small perturbation produces <apsidal precession> $\Delta\varpi\simeq\pi GM\gamma/h^2$. Writing the unperturbed semilatus rectum as $p=h^2/(GM)$ gives the displayed inverse relation. For a nearly circular orbit of period $P$, use $p\simeq[GM(P/(2\pi))^2]^{1/3}$. Positive $\gamma$ means prograde precession; $\gamma$ is a length. A measured rate per century must first be converted to an angle per orbit.

= Inferring an inverse-square force from Kepler laws
{parent=Binet equation}
{title2=$a_r=-l^2/(Ar^2)$}

Constant <areal velocity> gives constant specific <angular momentum> $l=r^2\dot\theta$, hence zero transverse acceleration and a <central force>. For a focus-based <ellipse> $u=1/r=(1+\varepsilon\cos\theta)/A$, one has $u_{\theta\theta}+u=1/A$. The <Binet equation> then gives the displayed radial acceleration, proving an attractive <inverse-square law>. An orbit's shape alone does not specify its acceleration without a time law.

= Damped Binet equation
{parent=Binet equation}
{title2=$u^{\prime\prime}+\frac{k}{h}u^{\prime}+u=\frac{p}{h^2}$}

An attractive <inverse-square force> together with radial drag $-k\dot r/r^2$ preserves <angular momentum> $h=r^2\dot\theta$ but modifies the <Binet equation> for $u=1/r$ by a first derivative. For $k/|h|<2$ and $h\ne0$, the reciprocal radius is a decaying oscillation about $p/h^2$. Forward angular distance $\operatorname{sgn}(h)(\theta-\theta_0)$ makes its damping sign explicit for either orbital orientation.

= Circularization under radial inverse-square drag
{parent=Damped Binet equation}
{title2=$r\longrightarrow h^2/p,\quad\dot E=-k\dot r^2/r^2$}

A bounded trajectory in the <damped Binet equation> with nonzero <angular momentum> asymptotically becomes circular. Energy loss is nonnegative in magnitude and vanishes only for zero radial velocity. The centrifugal effective <potential energy> excludes collision; bounded radius forces indefinitely increasing forward angular distance, so the reciprocal-radius oscillation decays. Escaping trajectories are excluded by the boundedness hypothesis.

= Shifted inverse-radius potential
{parent=Binet equation}

For
$$
V(r)=-\frac{km}{r-r_0},
\qquad r>r_0,
$$
Binet’s equation is
$$
u''+u=\frac{K}{(1-r_0u)^2},
\qquad K=\frac{km^2}{\ell^2}.
$$
Its circular orbits solve $K=u(1-r_0u)^2$; the branch $u<1/(3r_0)$ is stable and the branch $u>1/(3r_0)$ is unstable.

= Orbital eccentricity
{parent=Kepler orbit}
{wiki}

Orbital eccentricity distinguishes circular, elliptic, parabolic, and hyperbolic conics.

= Eccentricity vector
{title2=$\mathbf e$}
{parent=Orbital eccentricity}
{wiki}

For an attractive <Kepler orbit> with gravitational parameter $GM$, <velocity> $\mathbf v$ and <specific angular momentum> $\mathbf h=\mathbf r\times\mathbf v$, the eccentricity vector is
$$
\mathbf e=\frac{\mathbf v\times\mathbf h}{GM}-\widehat{\mathbf r}.
$$
Its magnitude is the <orbital eccentricity>, and it points toward <periapsis> for a noncircular orbit. It is a normalized attractive <Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector>; it remains constant under the pure inverse-square force.

= Perturbed Kepler-orbit conservation laws
{parent=Eccentricity vector}

If the relative acceleration is $\ddot{\mathbf r}=-GM\mathbf r/r^3+\mathbf f$, with constant masses, then the <specific orbital energy> $\varepsilon$, <specific angular momentum> $\mathbf h$ and <eccentricity vector> obey
$$
\dot\varepsilon=\mathbf v\mathbin\cdot\mathbf f,\qquad
\dot{\mathbf h}=\mathbf r\times\mathbf f,\qquad
GM\dot{\mathbf e}=\mathbf f\times\mathbf h+\mathbf v\times(\mathbf r\times\mathbf f).
$$
The inverse-square-force terms cancel in each derivative. These identities express perturbing work and torque, and track the instantaneous change of the ellipse's shape and orientation.

= Thermal eccentricity distribution
{title2=$n(e)=2e$}
{parent=Orbital eccentricity}

An isotropic population of bound Kepler orbits has eccentricity probability density $n(e)=2e$ on $0\leq e<1$. It follows from the fixed-energy angular-momentum measure $n(L)dL\propto LdL$ and $L=L_c\sqrt{1-e^2}$.

= Delaunay phase-volume proof of the thermal eccentricity distribution
{c}
{parent=Thermal eccentricity distribution}
{title2=$P(e'<e)=e^2$}

For bound <Kepler orbits>, the <Delaunay variables> obey $0<G<L$, $-G<H<G$, and three periodic angles. A <canonical transformation> preserves <phase space> volume. At fixed $L$, integrating $H$ supplies the measure $2G\,dG$. Since $G=L\sqrt{1-e^2}$, the fraction with <orbital eccentricity> less than $e$ is $L^{-2}\int_{L\sqrt{1-e^2}}^L2G\,dG=e^2$. Thus the <probability density function> is $2e$, independently of any normalizable energy-only <phase-space distribution function>. A <Boltzmann distribution> is unnecessary. Even the time-dependent density $F(E(L))[1+\eta\cos(l-n(L)t)]$, $|\eta|<1$, has this same marginal because integrating the <mean anomaly> removes its oscillatory part; an eccentricity marginal alone does not imply equilibrium.

= Finite normalization of an energy-only Kepler distribution
{parent=Delaunay phase-volume proof of the thermal eccentricity distribution}
{title2=$N=(2\pi)^3\int_0^\infty L^2F(-\mu^2/(2L^2))\,dL$}

For bound <Kepler orbits> with gravitational parameter $\mu$, integrating the <Delaunay variables> over $0<G<L$, $-G<H<G$ and their three full angle periods gives the displayed population. A globally defined <thermal eccentricity distribution> requires this integral to converge, or an explicit finite action range. Merely having finite $F(E)$ at every energy does not suffice: $F=1$ yields an infinite integral at large $L$. At each fixed energy, or on every finite normalized action band, the cumulative eccentricity fraction remains $e^2$.

= Gravitational scattering angle
{parent=Hyperbolic Kepler orbit}
{wiki}

The gravitational scattering angle is the change between incoming and outgoing asymptotic velocity directions.

= Non-colliding gravitational velocity-kick bound
{parent=Gravitational scattering angle}
{title2=$|\Delta\boldsymbol v|_{\max}$}

For a local two-body hyperbolic encounter with central parameter $\mu$ and allowed centre separation $r_{\min}\geq r_0$, <conservation of energy> gives equal asymptotic speeds. The <gravitational scattering angle> therefore implies
$$
|\Delta\boldsymbol v|=\frac{2v_\infty}{1+r_{\min}v_\infty^2/\mu}
\leq\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r_0}}=\frac{v_{\rm esc}(r_0)}{\sqrt2}.
$$
Put $z=v_\infty\sqrt{r_{\min}/\mu}$ and use $2z/(1+z^2)\leq1$. The optimizing grazing <orbit> has $z=1$, <orbital eccentricity> two and deflection $\pi/3$. For finite spherical bodies $r_0$ is the sum of their radii; excluding actual contact makes the upper bound a supremum. Without a finite exclusion radius the ideal point-mass problem has no finite bound.

= Gravitational assist
{parent=Hyperbolic Kepler orbit}

In a gravitational assist, a small body's speed relative to a moving massive body is deflected with nearly unchanged magnitude. Transforming back to the inertial frame can transfer orbital energy between the bodies.

= Barker equation
{parent=Parabolic trajectory}
{c}

For a <parabolic trajectory> with <periapsis> radius $r_p$, put $D=\tan(f/2)$, where $f$ is <true anomaly>. The Barker equation is $t-t_p=\sqrt{2r_p^3/(GM)}(D+D^3/3)$. Since $r=r_p(1+D^2)$, it is equivalent to the <radius-time relation for a parabolic Kepler orbit>. The sign of $D$ distinguishes approach from recession; $t_p$ is the <periapsis> time.

= Radius-time relation for a parabolic Kepler orbit
{parent=Barker equation}
{title2=$(r-r_p)(r+2r_p)^2=\tfrac92GM(t-t_p)^2$}

On a nonradial zero-<energy> <Kepler orbit>, conserved <specific angular momentum> $h$ gives periapsis $r_p=h^2/(2GM)$ and $\dot r^2=2GM(r-r_p)/r^2$. Integrating with $s=\sqrt{r-r_p}$ yields $t-t_p=\pm\sqrt{2/(GM)}(r+2r_p)\sqrt{r-r_p}/3$. Squaring gives the displayed version of the <Barker equation>, valid on both incoming and outgoing branches. The time origin $t_p$ is passage through <periapsis>, not an arbitrary point on the orbit.

= Pericentre-to-crossover flight time
{parent=Barker equation}
{title2=$n_pt_x\simeq2^{7/8}(q/a_p)^{3/8}/3$}

A <parabolic Kepler orbit> obeys $t=\sqrt{2q^3/(GM)}(D+D^3/3)$ with $D=\tan(f/2)$. At the <high-eccentricity angular-speed crossover>, $q\ll r_x$, so $n_pt_x\simeq(\sqrt2/3)(r_x/a_p)^{3/2}=2^{7/8}(q/a_p)^{3/8}/3$. The small time fraction explains why an eccentric orbit turns through almost $\pi$ of <true anomaly> while a distant perturber barely moves.

= Orbital element

= Radial-velocity semi-amplitude
{parent=Orbital element}

= Argument of periapsis
{parent=Orbital element}
{title2=$\omega$}
{wiki}

= Argument of pericentre
{synonym}

= Argument of pericenter
{synonym}

The <argument of periapsis> is the angle in the <orbital plane> from the <ascending node> to <periapsis>, measured in the direction of motion. It differs from the <longitude of periapsis> $\varpi=\Omega+\omega$. For an inclined orbit this sum is a conventional longitude, not generally the azimuth of the projected periapsis. The argument is undefined for a circular orbit and the node-based description is singular when the orbit and reference plane coincide.

= True longitude
{parent=Orbital element}
{title2=$\ell_{\rm true}=\Omega+\omega+f$}

True longitude is the conventional orbital angle formed from the <longitude of ascending node>, argument of <periapsis> and <true anomaly>. For a coplanar <Kepler orbit> it is the actual polar longitude of the position vector. For an inclined <orbital plane> the sum of angles is generally not the projected azimuth. The <mean longitude> uses <mean anomaly> $M$ in place of $f$, so $\ell_{\rm true}-\lambda=f-M$. For small <orbital eccentricity>, <Kepler's equation> gives $E=M+e\sin M+O(e^2)$, while the eccentric-to-true anomaly relation gives $f=E+e\sin E+O(e^2)$; hence $f-M=2e\sin M+O(e^2)$. This periodic difference matters when separating a slow <resonant argument> from a rapidly varying geometric angle.

= Orbital plane
{parent=Orbital element}

The plane containing a central-force <Kepler orbit>, perpendicular to its conserved <specific angular momentum>. Its orientation can be specified by an <orbital inclination> and an <orbital node>. Perturbing <forces> can change that orientation.

= Osculating orbital element
{parent=Orbital element}

An <orbital element> of the <Kepler orbit> having the same instantaneous position and <velocity> as a perturbed trajectory. Its elements vary as the perturbing <force> acts. Their interpretation depends on the central <gravitational parameter> used, for example whether static <radiation pressure> is included in the unperturbed attraction.

= Astrocentric osculating-element oscillations
{parent=Osculating orbital element}
{title2=$\delta a/a=2\varepsilon\cos\Delta,\quad(e_r,e_\theta)=(2\varepsilon\cos\Delta,\varepsilon\sin\Delta)$}

A circular distant barycentric <Kepler orbit> around a circular star–planet binary need not have constant star-centred <osculating orbital elements>. Let $\mu$ be the planet's fraction of the binary <mass>, $\alpha=a_{\rm pl}/a\ll1$, and $\varepsilon=\mu/\sqrt\alpha\ll1$. Subtracting the stellar reflex <velocity> produces radial and tangential perturbations $(-v_0\varepsilon\sin\Delta,v_0\varepsilon\cos\Delta)$, where $\Delta$ is the planet's longitude minus the particle's longitude. The <vis-viva equation> gives the displayed <semi-major axis> variation. The <eccentricity vector> components follow from $e_r=rv_\theta^2/(GM_\star)-1$ and $e_\theta=-rv_rv_\theta/(GM_\star)$. The errors are $O(\mu+\varepsilon^2)$; the spatial translation itself is only $O(\mu\alpha)$. These are changes of instantaneous orbital description, even when the assumed barycentric trajectory is fixed.

= Phase-mixed orbit
{parent=Orbital phase}

A phase-mixed orbit has a probability distribution uniform in its canonical angle variable. For a Kepler ellipse, this means uniform mean anomaly and therefore probability proportional to time spent in each orbital segment.

= Line density on a Kepler orbit
{parent=Phase-mixed orbit}
{title2=$\lambda_\ell$}

= Line densities on a Kepler orbit
{synonym}

Uniform <mean anomaly> implies a line probability inversely proportional to the orbital speed. For small <orbital eccentricity> and <true anomaly> $f$, it is proportional to $1-e\cos f$.

= Cross-sectional-area current
{parent=Line density on a Kepler orbit}
{title2=$J_\sigma$}

The <geometric cross-section> passing a fixed point of a steady orbital wire per unit time is $J_\sigma=\lambda_\sigma v$, where $\lambda_\sigma=d\sigma/ds$. Conservation of fragments requires $J_\sigma$ constant and $\lambda_\sigma\propto1/v$. For total area $\sigma_{\rm tot}$ and <orbital period> $P$,
$$
J_\sigma=\frac{\sigma_{\rm tot}}P.
$$
With the <fractional luminosity of a phase-mixed eccentric wire>, this becomes $J_\sigma=2fh$. The current describes transported area, not the swept area enclosed by the <orbit>.

= Phase-mixed radial probability of a Kepler orbit
{parent=Phase-mixed orbit}
{title2=$p_r(r)$}

For a <Kepler orbit> with <pericentre distance> $q$, <apocentre distance> $Q$, and <semi-major axis> $a=(q+Q)/2$, a uniform <mean anomaly> gives
$$
p_r(r)=\frac{r}{\pi a\sqrt{(r-q)(Q-r)}},\qquad q<r<Q.
$$
The two radial passages per <orbital period> are included, and $\int_q^Qp_r(r)\,dr=1$.

= Orbital-frame rotation from inclination and node
{parent=Orbital inclination}
{title2=$R_z(\Omega)R_x(I)$}

In a right-handed reference frame, the <rotation matrix> from an <orbital plane> frame whose first axis points along the <ascending node> is $R_z(\Omega)R_x(I)$. Its columns are the orbital frame's <orthonormal basis> expressed in reference coordinates. The <unit vector> normal is $(\sin\Omega\sin I,-\cos\Omega\sin I,\cos I)$; its <dot product> with another such normal gives the cosine of their <mutual inclination>.

= Mutual inclination

The mutual inclination is the angle between two orbital planes, equivalently the angle between consistently oriented orbital normal vectors.

= Mutual inclination of nearly edge-on orbits
{parent=Mutual inclination}
{title2=$I_m^2\simeq(\Delta I)^2+(\Delta\Omega)^2$}

For two nearly <edge-on orbits> with small <orbital inclination> difference $\Delta I$ and small <longitude of ascending node> difference $\Delta\Omega$, expanding the <dot product> of their normals gives $I_m^2=(\Delta I)^2+(\Delta\Omega)^2+O(\delta^4)$, with angles in radians and all offsets from edge-on of order $\delta$. Thus equality of observed sky-plane inclinations alone does not imply aligned <orbital planes>.

= Invariable plane
{parent=Orbital inclination}
{wiki}

The invariable plane is perpendicular to a system's total <angular momentum>. For an isolated planetary system, it is fixed while individual orbital planes precess.

= Inclination to the invariable plane of two orbital planes
{parent=Invariable plane}

For two groups carrying <angular momenta> $L_a,L_b$ with <mutual inclination> $\Delta I$, resolving total <angular momentum> parallel and perpendicular to the second group's normal gives its <orbital inclination> relative to the <invariable plane>:
$$
I_b=\operatorname{atan2}(L_a\sin\Delta I,L_b+L_a\cos\Delta I).
$$
For a positive denominator, $\tan I_b=\sin\Delta I/(L_b/L_a+\cos\Delta I)$. The larger-angular-momentum group lies closer to the <invariable plane>.

An orbit is edge-on to an observer when its orbital plane contains the line of sight, so its inclination to the plane of the sky is $90^\circ$.

= Mean motion

Mean motion is the average orbital angular frequency. A Kepler orbit about gravitational parameter $\mu$ has $n^2a^3=\mu$.

= Orbital period
{title2=$T$}
{parent=Mean motion}
{wiki}

The orbital period is the time required to complete one orbit. It is related to <mean motion> by $T=2\pi/n$.

= Low circular-orbit period measures mean density
{parent=Orbital period}
{title2=$P^2=3\pi/(G\bar\rho)$}

For a spherical body of radius $R$, mass $M$ and <mean density> $\bar\rho=3M/(4\pi R^3)$, a low <circular orbit> has period $P\simeq2\pi\sqrt{R^3/(GM)}$, by equating the gravitational acceleration from <Newton's law of universal gravitation> to the <centripetal acceleration>. Substitution proves the displayed relation. Thus similar low-orbit periods indicate similar mean densities, even for bodies with very different masses and radii. At altitude $h$, the right side is multiplied by $(1+h/R)^3$; the simple density inference requires $h\ll R$ and neglects rotation, nonsphericity and other gravitating bodies.

= Synodic period
{title2=$T_{\rm syn}$}
{parent=Mean motion}
{wiki}

The synodic period is the interval between successive repetitions of the relative orbital configuration of two bodies. For coplanar prograde mean motions $n_1,n_2$, it is $2\pi/|n_1-n_2|$.

= Specific orbital energy
{title2=$\varepsilon$}
{parent=Kepler orbit}
{wiki}

The specific orbital energy is the mechanical energy per unit mass,
$$
\varepsilon=\frac{v^2}{2}-\frac{\mu}{r}=-\frac{\mu}{2a}.
$$
Its sign distinguishes bound elliptic, marginal parabolic, and unbound hyperbolic Kepler orbits.

= Orbit-averaged drag work
{parent=Specific orbital energy}

A weak perturbing relative acceleration $\mathbf f$ changes <specific orbital energy> at rate $\dot\varepsilon=\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf f$. Since $\varepsilon=-\mu/(2a)$ for a <Kepler orbit>,
$$
\dot a=\frac{2a^2}{\mu}\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf f.
$$
For a circular <Kepler orbit> under $\mathbf f=-\kappa\mathbf v+\mathbf g$ with constant $\mathbf g$, the constant-force term averages to zero over an unperturbed orbit. Thus $\langle\dot a\rangle=-2\kappa a$. This is a leading-order orbital average; a strong constant force can distort the orbit instead.

= Vis-viva equation
{parent=Specific orbital energy}
{wiki}

For a <Kepler orbit> with <semi-major axis> $a$, the <vis-viva equation> is
$$
v^2=\mu\left(\frac2r-\frac1a\right).
$$
For the convention that a <hyperbolic Kepler orbit> has positive semi-major-axis magnitude $a$, its final term instead has a plus sign.

= Specific angular momentum
{title2=$h$}
{parent=Kepler orbit}
{wiki}

The specific angular momentum is angular momentum per unit mass, $\mathbf h=\mathbf r\times\mathbf v$. For a planar Kepler orbit its magnitude is $h=r^2\dot\theta=\sqrt{\mu a(1-e^2)}$.

= High-eccentricity angular-speed crossover
{parent=Specific angular momentum}
{title2=$r_x=\sqrt{h/n_p}$}

For a nearly radial <Kepler orbit> with <pericentre> $q$, <specific angular momentum> is $h\simeq\sqrt{2GMq}$. Relative to a circular reference orbit of radius $a_p$ and <mean motion> $n_p$, the instantaneous azimuthal rate changes from faster to slower at $r_x=\sqrt{h/n_p}\simeq a_p(2q/a_p)^{1/4}$. In a <rotating reference frame> the polar angle has a turning point at this radius. The <true anomaly> there differs from $\pi$ by approximately $2\sqrt{q/r_x}$.

= Periapsis angular speed
{title2=$\dot\theta_{\rm peri}$}
{parent=Specific angular momentum}

The <specific angular momentum> of an elliptic <Kepler orbit> is $h=\sqrt{\mu a(1-e^2)}$. At <periapsis>, $r=a(1-e)$, so
$$
\dot\theta_{\rm peri}=\frac h{r^2}=n\frac{\sqrt{1+e}}{(1-e)^{3/2}}.
$$
The <mean motion> understates instantaneous angular speed near <periapsis>. A particle in an <exterior mean-motion resonance> can briefly overtake the planet in angle despite completing full orbits more slowly.

= Circular Kepler orbit
{parent=Kepler orbit}

A circular Kepler orbit at radius $r$ has speed $v_K=\sqrt{\mu/r}$ and mean motion $n=\sqrt{\mu/r^3}$.

= Synchronous circular orbit
{parent=Circular Kepler orbit}
{title2=$r=(GMT^2/4\pi^2)^{1/3}$}

A satellite remaining above a fixed point of a uniformly rotating spherical planet must have a prograde equatorial <circular Kepler orbit> with angular speed $2\pi/T$. Balancing gravitational acceleration and centripetal acceleration gives the displayed radius and speed $v=2\pi r/T$. The satellite's own mass cancels. A physically realizable external orbit additionally requires this radius to exceed the planet's radius.

= Geostationary orbit
{parent=Synchronous circular orbit}
{wiki}

A <geostationary orbit> is a prograde equatorial <circular Kepler orbit> whose <orbital period> equals one <sidereal day>, so its satellite remains above a fixed location on <Earth>'s equator. It is the terrestrial case of a <planet-stationary orbit>. Its radius follows from <Newton's law of universal gravitation> and circular <acceleration>: $r=(GM_ET^2/(4\pi^2))^{1/3}$, approximately $42164\ \mathrm{km}$ from <Earth>'s centre. Its altitude above the surface is smaller by <Earth>'s radius.

= Hohmann transfer orbit
{parent=Kepler orbit}
{c}
{wiki}

= Hohmann transfer
{c}
{synonym}

A Hohmann transfer uses two tangential impulses and one half of an elliptic Kepler orbit to move between two coplanar circular orbits.

= Reversed circularization burn in a Hohmann transfer
{parent=Hohmann transfer orbit}
{title2=$q=[2\sqrt{2/(1+\alpha)}-1]^2$}

An outward transfer from radius $R$ to $\alpha R$ arrives with speed $v_a/v_c=\sqrt{2/(1+\alpha)}$, where $v_c$ is the desired outer circular speed. Reversing the same circularization <impulse> leaves speed ratio $v_w/v_c=2\sqrt{2/(1+\alpha)}-1$, possibly signed. Its square $q$ determines the new <apocentre> <orbit>: $e=1-q$ and $r_p=\alpha Rq/(2-q)$. The <orbit> intersects a spherical body of radius $R_b$ if $r_p<R_b$. A negative signed speed means retrograde motion, while $q=0$ is the degenerate radial-fall limit.

= Hohmann transfer impulse fractions
{parent=Hohmann transfer orbit}
{c}
{title2=$\Delta v_1/v_0=\sqrt{2\eta/(1+\eta)}-1$}

For a <Hohmann transfer orbit> from radius $r_0$ to $\eta r_0$, with $\eta>1$ and initial circular speed $v_0=\sqrt{GM/r_0}$, conservation of <orbital energy> and <angular momentum> gives transfer endpoint speeds $v_p/v_0=\sqrt{2\eta/(1+\eta)}$ and $v_a/v_0=\sqrt{2/[\eta(1+\eta)]}$. The required prograde impulses are
$$
\frac{\Delta v_1}{v_0}=\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{1+\eta}}-1,\qquad
\frac{\Delta v_2}{v_0}=\frac1{\sqrt\eta}-\sqrt{\frac2{\eta(1+\eta)}}.
$$
If the spacecraft mass is constant and fuel use is proportional to impulse magnitude, these also give the fuel fractions relative to a budget with total speed change $v_0$. Their sum is a sum of magnitudes, even though the burns occur at different positions and directions.

= Hohmann transfer time
{parent=Hohmann transfer orbit}
{c}
{title2=$t=\pi\sqrt{(r_1+r_2)^3/(8GM)}$}

A <Hohmann transfer orbit> between coplanar <circular Kepler orbits> of radii $r_1,r_2$ traverses half an <elliptic orbit> of <semi-major axis> $a=(r_1+r_2)/2$. <Kepler's third law> therefore gives $t=\pi\sqrt{a^3/(GM)}$. Relative to the initial <orbital period> $T_1$, $t=(T_1/2)[(r_1+r_2)/(2r_1)]^{3/2}$. Radii must be measured from the attracting centre, rather than from its surface.

= Restricted three-body problem
{parent=Celestial mechanics}
{wiki}

The restricted three-body problem follows a negligibly massive particle in the gravitational field of two massive bodies whose motion is unaffected by the particle.

= Circular restricted three-body problem
{parent=Restricted three-body problem}
{wiki}

In the circular restricted three-body problem the two massive bodies move on circular orbits about their barycentre. The problem is autonomous in their uniformly rotating frame.

= Hill equations
{c}
{parent=Circular restricted three-body problem}

The unforced planar <equation of motion in a rotating frame> near a circular <Kepler orbit> has components $\ddot x-2n\dot y-3n^2x=0$ and $\ddot y+2n\dot x=0$. They describe <epicyclic motion> and <Keplerian shear>; mutual gravity adds close-encounter forcing.

= Free solution of Hill equations
{parent=Hill equations}

= Orbital elements from Hill coordinates
{parent=Free solution of Hill equations}

The oscillatory integration constants determine the <orbital eccentricity> vector, the constant radial offset determines the <semi-major axis>, and the constant along-track offset fixes the <mean longitude>. The radial and tangential epicycle amplitudes have ratio $1:2$.

= Photogravitational restricted three-body problem
{parent=Circular restricted three-body problem}

The photogravitational restricted three-body problem adds radial <radiation pressure> from one or both luminous primaries. If the pressure-to-gravity ratio of primary $i$ is $\beta_i$, its effective attraction on the test particle is multiplied by $1-\beta_i$.

= Lagrange point
{c}
{parent=Circular restricted three-body problem}
{wiki}

A Lagrange point is an equilibrium of the <circular restricted three-body problem> in the frame rotating with the two massive bodies.

= Effective-potential geometry of Lagrange points
{parent=Lagrange point}

The <rotating-frame effective potential> of the <circular restricted three-body problem> has negative singular wells at both primaries. Its three <collinear Lagrange points> are planar saddles, and its two <triangular Lagrange points> are planar local maxima for nonzero primary masses. These geometric labels do not replace a dynamical stability analysis including the <Coriolis force>.

= Collinear Lagrange point
{c}
{parent=Lagrange point}

The three collinear Lagrange points $L_1,L_2,L_3$ lie on the line through the two massive bodies.

= Small-mass-ratio collinear Lagrange points
{parent=Collinear Lagrange point}
{title2=$x_{L_1,L_2}=1\mp(\alpha/3)^{1/3}+O(\alpha^{2/3})$}

In barycentric coordinates with unit primary separation, a small secondary mass fraction $\alpha$ gives distances $(\alpha/3)^{1/3}$ from that secondary to the two neighboring <Lagrange points> at leading order. This is the dimensionless <Hill radius>. The opposite exterior <L3 Lagrange point> has coordinate $-1-5\alpha/12+O(\alpha^2)$.

= Inner Lagrange point
{parent=Collinear Lagrange point}
{title2=$L_1$}

= L1 point
{c}
{synonym}

The <Lagrange point> lying between the components of a <circular restricted three-body problem>. The critical effective potential through this saddle defines their <Roche lobes>; gas passing through it can initiate <Roche-lobe overflow>.

= L2 Lagrange point
{parent=Collinear Lagrange point}
{title2=$L_2$}
{c}

The collinear <Lagrange point> beyond the smaller primary, away from the larger one, is $L_2$. For a small mass ratio it lies approximately one <Hill radius> from the secondary and has a growing linear mode.

= Linearization at L2
{parent=L2 Lagrange point}
{title2=$B=\mu_1/r_1^3+\mu_2/r_2^3$}

In unit binary separation and mean motion, displacements obey $\ddot X-2\dot Y=(1+2B)X$ and $\ddot Y+2\dot X=(1-B)Y$, where $B=\mu_1/r_1^3+\mu_2/r_2^3$ at $L_2$. The characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^4+(2-B)\lambda^2+(1+2B)(1-B)$. The small-secondary limit has $B\to4$.

= L2 escape e-folding time
{parent=Linearization at L2}
{title2=$\tau=[n_{\rm bin}\sqrt{1+2\sqrt7}]^{-1}$}
{c}

For a small secondary-to-primary mass ratio, the unstable exponent at the outer collinear equilibrium is $n_{\rm bin}\sqrt{1+2\sqrt7}$. Its inverse is approximately $0.0634$ binary periods. Reaching a finite target distance takes this time multiplied by the logarithm of the ratio of final to initial unstable amplitudes.

= L3 Lagrange point

The $L_3$ Lagrange point lies beyond the more massive primary, on the side opposite the secondary.

= Triangular Lagrange point

The triangular Lagrange points $L_4,L_5$ complete equilateral triangles with the two massive bodies.

A scale for the combined gravitational influence of two small orbiting bodies relative to their common host <star>. It is often used to express the separation of neighboring <planetary embryos>. For equal masses and nearly equal orbital radii it exceeds the individual <Hill radius> by $2^{1/3}$.

= Jacobi constant

= Orbital resonance
{parent=Celestial mechanics}
{wiki}

An orbital resonance occurs when orbital frequencies satisfy an approximately integer relation, allowing perturbations to accumulate coherently.

= Mean-motion resonance
{parent=Orbital resonance}
{wiki}

A mean-motion resonance has $jn_2-kn_1\simeq0$ for small integers $j,k$. Its slow resonant angle combines mean longitudes with apsidal or nodal longitudes.

= Interior eccentricity-type mean-motion resonance
{parent=Mean-motion resonance}

For an eccentric inner body and circular outer perturber near the reduced $(m+n):m$ period ratio, the leading <resonant argument> is $\phi=(m+n)\lambda_{\rm out}-m\lambda_{\rm in}-n\varpi_{\rm in}$. Its <disturbing function> amplitude is of order $e_{\rm in}^n$. At a mean <conjunction>, $\phi=n(\Lambda-\varpi_{\rm in})$; at actual alignment the exact expression is $(m+n)x-my$ with $x=f_{\rm in}$ and $y=M_{\rm in}(x)$.

= Inclination-type mean-motion resonance
{parent=Mean-motion resonance}

= Inclination resonance
{synonym}

An inclination-type mean-motion resonance has a leading <resonant argument> containing a <longitude of ascending node>. Reflection symmetry forces the total nodal coefficient in each disturbing-function harmonic to be even, so the leading resonant strength is an even power of $\sin(I/2)$.

= Exterior mean-motion resonance
{parent=Mean-motion resonance}

In an exterior mean-motion resonance, the resonant small body orbits outside the perturbing planet and therefore has lower mean motion. A $(p+q):p$ exterior resonance has $n/n_p\simeq p/(p+q)$.

= Resonant semi-major axis
{parent=Exterior mean-motion resonance}
{title2=$a_r=a_p((p+q)/p)^{2/3}$}

The nominal <semi-major axis> of an exterior $(p+q):p$ <mean-motion resonance> follows from $(p+q)n_r=pn_p$ and <Kepler's third law>. Apsidal precession displaces the actual libration center because stationarity of the <resonant argument> requires $(p+q)n-pn_p-q\dot\varpi=0$.

= Radiation-pressure shift of a mean-motion resonance
{parent=Exterior mean-motion resonance}
{title2=$a/a_p=(1-\beta)^{1/3}((j+1)/j)^{2/3}$}

A grain with <radiation-pressure coefficient> $\beta<1$ feels effective central <gravitational parameter> $GM_\star(1-\beta)$. Combining its <mean motion> with the planet's <mean motion> at an exterior-type $(j+1):j$ frequency commensurability gives the displayed <semi-major axis>. The commensurability lies geometrically exterior only for $j<\sqrt{1-\beta}/(1-\sqrt{1-\beta})$. Radiation changes the grain's attraction, so the same frequency ratio need not imply the usual radial ordering.

= First-order mean-motion resonance
{parent=Mean-motion resonance}

A first-order mean-motion resonance has $|j-k|=1$. For small eccentricity its leading eccentric resonant term is linear in eccentricity.

= Resonance-overlap encounter estimate
{parent=First-order mean-motion resonance}
{title2=$j_c\sim\mu^{-2/7}$}

A local weak <gravitational assist> estimate for a nonradiating circular grain gives $\delta\epsilon\simeq32\mu^2\epsilon^{-5}$, where $\mu=M_p/M_\star$. <Conjunction-longitude sensitivity to an encounter> then gives $|\delta\Lambda|\simeq128\pi\mu^2/(3\epsilon^7)$. Requiring the change to exceed one full turn and using $\epsilon\simeq2/(3j)$ gives $j_c\simeq(2/729)^{1/7}\mu^{-2/7}$. This motivates loss of coherent resonant conjunctions, rather than proving that every initial phase escapes. The coefficient depends on the encounter convention and the approximations; near-planet <Hill equations> require a consistent rotating-frame shear instead of mixing local inertial and rotating velocities.

= Resonant argument
{parent=Mean-motion resonance}

= Resonant angle
{synonym}

A resonant argument is a slow angular combination whose libration diagnoses resonance. For an exterior $j:(j-1)$ eccentric resonance it can be $\phi=j\lambda-(j-1)\lambda_p-\varpi$.

A resonant argument is an integer combination of orbital angles whose slow variation can diagnose <mean-motion resonance>. Its libration, rather than circulation through every angle, characterizes trapping in the resonance.

= Resonant conjunction geometry
{parent=Resonant argument}

For an eccentric outer body and circular inner perturber, the exterior <resonant argument> is $\phi=(p+q)\lambda_{\rm out}-p\lambda_{\rm in}-q\varpi$. At equal <mean longitudes> $\Lambda$, <conjunction> phases are
$$
\Lambda_j=\varpi+\frac{\phi+2\pi j}{q},\qquad j=0,\ldots,q-1.
$$
The <synodic period> and coprime $p,q$ determine the visitation order, with advance $2\pi p/q$. For actual true alignment, write $x=f$ and $y=M(f)$ for the eccentric body's <true anomaly> and <mean anomaly>. Then $\phi=(p+q)y-px$ instead of $qx$. The evenly spaced directions are a leading-small-eccentricity approximation.

= Phase protection of interior integer resonances
{parent=Resonant conjunction geometry}
{title2=$\phi=k\lambda_p-\lambda-(k-1)\varpi$}

In a high-<orbital eccentricity> interior $k:1$ <mean-motion resonance>, the <resonant argument> $\phi=k\lambda_p-\lambda-(k-1)\varpi$ places successive rotating-frame <apocentres> at $\pi-[\phi+(2j+1)\pi]/k$. Avoiding <astronomical conjunction> at these slow outer excursions favors $\phi=0$ for $k=2$ and $\phi=\pi$ for $k=3$. This geometrical phase protection suggests stable <resonant-argument libration> centres; actual dynamical stability requires the resonant perturbation problem.

= Conjunction-pattern precession near a mean-motion resonance
{parent=Resonant conjunction geometry}
{title2=$\dot\Lambda$}

With constant <mean motions> and $P_c/P_b=(1+\delta)(p+q)/p$, mean <conjunctions> advance by $\Delta\Lambda=2\pi/(P_c/P_b-1)$ each <synodic period>. Tracking one branch every $q$ <conjunctions> and subtracting $2\pi p$ gives
$$
\dot\Lambda=\frac{q\Delta\Lambda-2\pi p}{qP_{\rm syn}}
=-\frac{2\pi p\delta}{qP_b(1+\delta)}.
$$
The usual formula $-2\pi p\delta/(qP_b)$ keeps only first order in $\delta$. A labelled branch takes $qP_b(1+\delta)/(p|\delta|)$ to turn once, whereas the full set of $q$ interchangeable directions repeats after $P_b(1+\delta)/(p|\delta|)$. Finite <orbital eccentricity> introduces true-conjunction corrections.

= Conjunction-longitude sensitivity to an encounter
{parent=Conjunction-pattern precession near a mean-motion resonance}
{title2=$\delta\Lambda=\Lambda'(\epsilon)\delta\epsilon$}

For an exterior circular grain with <radiation-pressure coefficient> $\beta$, set $q=\sqrt{1-\beta}$ and $\epsilon=a/a_p-1$. Its next <conjunction> occurs after the <synodic period>, during which the planet's unwrapped <mean longitude> advances by $\Lambda=2\pi/[1-q(1+\epsilon)^{-3/2}]$. A weak <planetary scattering> encounter changes this by $\delta\Lambda\simeq-3\pi q(1+\epsilon)^{-5/2}\delta\epsilon/[1-q(1+\epsilon)^{-3/2}]^2$. Unwrapped longitude counts turns; reducing it modulo $2\pi$ before comparison would erase this measure of timing sensitivity.

= Resonant-argument libration
{parent=Resonant argument}

Resonant-argument libration is bounded oscillation of a resonant argument about a stable equilibrium value instead of circulation through all angles.

= Pendulum approximation of a mean-motion resonance
{parent=Resonant-argument libration}
{title2=$\ddot\phi=K\sin\phi$}

= Resonant pendulum approximation
{synonym}

Keeping one resonant harmonic and treating <orbital eccentricity> as fixed reduces <mean-motion resonance> dynamics to a <physical pendulum> equation. For the inner first-order term $\mathcal R=(GM_2/a_2)f(\alpha)e\cos\phi$, leading <Lagrange planetary equations> give $\ddot\phi\simeq3j^2\mu_2n^2\alpha f e\sin\phi$. Around its stable phase, the small-amplitude libration frequency is $jn\sqrt{3\mu_2\alpha|f|e}$. Evolution of eccentricity, other harmonics, and approach to a <separatrix> change this approximation.

= Resonant pendulum energy
{parent=Pendulum approximation of a mean-motion resonance}
{title2=$E=\dot\phi^2/2+\omega_0^2(1+\cos\phi)$}

For $\ddot\phi=\omega_0^2\sin\phi$ with constant $\omega_0$, multiplication by $\dot\phi$ gives the conserved energy $E=\dot\phi^2/2+\omega_0^2(1+\cos\phi)$. The stable center is $\pi$; the <separatrix> has $E=2\omega_0^2$. Below it the <resonant argument> librates and above it the angle circulates. This is an invariant of the constant-coefficient <pendulum approximation of a mean-motion resonance>, not of arbitrary coupled <orbital element> evolution.

= Asymmetric resonant-argument libration
{parent=Resonant-argument libration}

Libration about a resonant phase distinct from the symmetric values zero and $\pi$. For example, in a negative-Kepler-curvature reduction with disturbing potential $R=A\cos\phi+B\cos2\phi$, $A>0$, $B>A/4$, stationary points obey $\sin\phi(A+4B\cos\phi)=0$. At $\pi$, $R''=A-4B<0$, so it is unstable, whereas at $\cos\phi=-A/(4B)$ one has $R''=4B-A^2/(4B)>0$, giving two stable asymmetric centres. Actual branch thresholds depend on the resonance coefficients and evolving <orbital elements>.

= Symmetric libration centres of an eccentricity resonance
{parent=Resonant-argument libration}

In the weak, non-crossing, leading-eccentricity planar model, exterior orders one to three have positive resonant <disturbing function> coefficients $B_q$. Interior eccentricity-type coefficients of these orders have sign $(-1)^q$. A local resonant <Hamiltonian> $H=-AJ^2/2-Be^q\cos\phi$, with $A>0$, gives $\ddot\phi=ABe^q\sin\phi$. <Linear stability analysis> therefore selects $\phi=\pi$ for $B>0$ and $\phi=0$ for $B<0$. Exterior symmetric centres are $\pi$; interior centres are $0,\pi,0$ for orders one, two and three. These are conditional leading-harmonic predictions, not universal centres at arbitrary <orbital eccentricity>; <asymmetric resonant-argument libration> changes the picture.

= Low-eccentricity correction to a first-order resonance center
{parent=Symmetric libration centres of an eccentricity resonance}
{title2=$3(p+1)^2ne^3-C_r$}

In the planar exterior first-order constant-coefficient model, differentiating the apsidal-precession term retains a contribution proportional to $C_r^2/e^2$. At the zero-phase fixed point its linear angular curvature is $3(p+1)^2nC_re-C_r^2/e^2$. Hence zero is a saddle for $3(p+1)^2ne^3>C_r$, but is a center when this inequality reverses. The usual constant-eccentricity <pendulum approximation of a mean-motion resonance> omits this term and requires $C_r/(ne^3)\ll1$.

= Resonance protection
