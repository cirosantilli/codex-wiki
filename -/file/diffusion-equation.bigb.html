= Diffusion equation
{wiki}

A diffusion equation describes smoothing caused by a flux down spatial gradients.

= Scalar integrating factor for a reaction-diffusion equation
{parent=Diffusion equation}
{title2=$v_t=v_{xx}+a(t)v,\quad v=e^{\int a(t)dt}u$}

A spatially uniform time-dependent reaction coefficient can be removed from a linear <diffusion equation> by a scalar <integrating factor>. If $A'(t)=a(t)$ and $v=e^{A(t)}u$, substitution gives $u_t=u_{xx}$. Initial data and boundary values must also be divided by $e^{A(t)}$. A nonzero boundary profile can then be removed by <boundary lifting> before applying <separation of variables>. This reduction distinguishes growth of the whole amplitude from decay of the spatial diffusion modes.

= Squared diffusion-residual action
{parent=Diffusion equation}
{title2=$S[h]=\int(h_t-\nu\nabla^2h)^2\,d^dx\,dt$}

For constant $\nu$, variation of this positive functional gives $(\partial_t+\nu\nabla^2)(\partial_t-\nu\nabla^2)h=0$, or $h_{tt}-\nu^2\nabla^4h=0$. The <diffusion equation> $h_t=\nu\nabla^2h$ gives zero-residual minima, but is only a subset of the stationary equations of the squared functional. Each nonzero spatial <Fourier mode> has both growing and decaying temporal solutions in the stationary equation. Boundary data decide which stationary paths are admissible. In two spatial dimensions, the bulk action is invariant under the anisotropic <scale invariance> $x\mapsto bx$, $t\mapsto b^2t$, $h\mapsto h$.

= Time reversal of the squared diffusion-residual action
{parent=Squared diffusion-residual action}

For a real scalar height field, <integration by parts> gives
$$
(h_t-\nu\nabla^2h)^2=h_t^2+\nu^2(\nabla^2h)^2+\nu\partial_t|\nabla h|^2-2\nu\nabla\cdot(h_t\nabla h).
$$
The bulk expression is even under <time reversal>. The original density is not pointwise even, but changes by a <total divergence>; its stationary equation is time-reversal invariant. The first-order <diffusion equation> selected by zero residual is not invariant under ordinary time reversal at fixed positive $\nu$. Boundary conditions or the probabilistic interpretation can distinguish the two time directions even when the higher-order stationary equation does not.

= Robin diffusion with a growing mode
{c}
{parent=Diffusion equation}
{title2=$u_t=u_{xx},\quad u_x(0)=u(0),\quad u_x(1)=u(1)$}

These <Robin boundary conditions> give the spatial <Sturm-Liouville operator> $-d^2/dx^2$ one negative <eigenvalue>, $-1$, with <eigenfunction> $e^x$. The other eigenvalues are $n^2\pi^2$, with eigenfunctions $V_n=n\pi\cos(n\pi x)+\sin(n\pi x)$, $n\geq1$; there is no zero eigenvalue. Hence the <diffusion equation> has a growing contribution $c_0e^{t+x}$ in addition to decaying modes.

There is no contradiction with ordinary diffusive smoothing. The outward <normal derivative> is $-u_x$ at the left endpoint and $u_x$ at the right endpoint, so imposing the same coordinate derivative at both ends does not impose dissipative outward-normal data at both ends. The energy identity is $\tfrac12\partial_t\int u^2=[uu_x]_0^1-\int u_x^2$, whose boundary term is $u(1)^2-u(0)^2$ here and can supply energy. Orthogonal projection of initial data $f$ gives $c_0=2\int_0^1fe^x\,dx/(e^2-1)$; it vanishes precisely when the growing mode is absent.

The reflected boundary conditions $u_x(0,t)=-u(0,t)$ and $u_x(1,t)=-u(1,t)$ have the same spectrum, because reflection $x\mapsto1-x$ reverses the coordinate derivative. Here the growing eigenfunction is $e^{-x}$ and the decaying functions are $W_n=n\pi\cos(n\pi x)-\sin(n\pi x)$, giving
$$
u(x,t)=c_0e^{t-x}+\sum_{n\geq1}c_ne^{-n^2\pi^2t}W_n(x).
$$
For square-integrable initial data $f$, <orthogonal projection> gives
$$
c_0=\frac{2}{1-e^{-2}}\int_0^1e^{-x}f(x)dx,\qquad c_n=\frac{2}{n^2\pi^2+1}\int_0^1W_n(x)f(x)dx.
$$
The norms are $(1-e^{-2})/2$ and $(n^2\pi^2+1)/2$. The spatial <Sturm-Liouville problem> has no zero eigenvalue and exactly one negative eigenvalue for $-D^2$, namely $-1$. The energy boundary term now reads $u(0)^2-u(1)^2$, so the left endpoint can supply energy. The growing mode disappears exactly when $\int_0^1e^{-x}f(x)dx=0$.

= Drift-diffusion equation with a potential
{parent=Diffusion equation}
{title2=$\partial_t c=\partial_x[D(\partial_xc+\beta V'c)]$}

For positive <diffusivity> $D(x)$, a <diffusion equation> with potential-driven drift has <diffusive flux> $J=-D(c_x+\beta V'c)$. On a finite interval with zero <flux> at both endpoints, the total amount is conserved. The substitution $c=e^{-\lambda t}e^{-\beta V}y$ converts the decay <eigenvalue problem> to the <Sturm-Liouville problem>
$$
-(py')'=\lambda wy,\qquad p=De^{-\beta V},\quad w=e^{-\beta V},\quad py'=0\text{ at the endpoints}.
$$
The <Rayleigh quotient> is $\lambda=\int p|y'|^2/\int w|y|^2\geq0$. Its zero <eigenfunction> is constant, giving the <stationary solution> $c_{\rm eq}=C e^{-\beta V}$. This is a spatial <Boltzmann distribution>; $D$ changes relaxation rates but cancels out of the zero-<flux> equilibrium profile. For a sufficiently regular positive $D$ and bounded $V$, the constant eigenfunction is the unique zero mode up to scale.

= Diffusive time scale
{parent=Diffusion equation}
{title2=$t_D\sim L^2/\kappa$}

A concentration varying over length $L$ has Laplacian of order its amplitude divided by $L^2$. Balancing the time derivative with $\kappa\Delta\chi$ in the <diffusion equation> gives the displayed relaxation time. A <multiple-scale expansion> with spatial scale $\varepsilon x$ therefore uses slow time $\varepsilon^2t$ when the effective <diffusivity> is of order one.

= Variable-coefficient conservative diffusion equation
{parent=Diffusion equation}
{title2=$u_t=(a(x)u_x)_x$}

With positive diffusion coefficient $a(x)$, this <diffusion equation> expresses conservation with flux $-a(x)u_x$. For a differentiable coefficient, its spatial operator is $a u_{xx}+a'u_x$, rather than either $a u_x$ or $a u_{xx}$ alone. A <symmetric half-grid diffusion consistency> discretization uses the difference of the two adjacent interface fluxes, preserving that divergence structure. Suitable <Dirichlet boundary conditions> make the spatial operator dissipative in the <L2 norm>.

= Infinite propagation speed
{parent=Diffusion equation}

Infinite propagation speed means that a disturbance initially confined to a bounded region can affect arbitrarily distant points after any positive time. The strictly positive <heat kernel> gives this behavior for a nontrivial nonnegative diffusion solution. It contrasts with the <finite propagation speed> of the <wave equation>; a very small distant response is still mathematically nonzero.

= Periodic homogenization of a diffusion equation
{parent=Diffusion equation}

For $u_t=\partial_x[a(x/\epsilon)u_x]$ with positive periodic $a$, the one-dimensional homogenized diffusivity is the harmonic mean $a_{\rm eff}=\langle a^{-1}\rangle^{-1}$. Microscopic flux continuity produces this harmonic rather than arithmetic averaging.

= Porous medium equation
{title2=$u_t=\Delta(u^m)$}
{parent=Diffusion equation}
{wiki}

The porous medium equation $u_t=\Delta(u^m)$ with $m>1$ is a <nonlinear partial differential equation> whose diffusivity vanishes at $u=0$. Unlike the <heat equation>, it has compactly supported solutions with finite propagation speed.

= Spherical nonlinear-diffusion source profile
{parent=Porous medium equation}
{title2=$r_0=(75Mkt)^{1/5}$}

For $C_t=k\nabla\cdot(C\nabla C)$ in three dimensions and total mass $4\pi M$, the nonnegative radial source solution is $C=M^{2/5}(kt)^{-3/5}f(r/(Mkt)^{1/5})$, with $f(\xi)=(75^{2/5}-\xi^2)_+/10$. Integrating the similarity equation once gives $ff'=-\xi f/5$, and normalization $\int_0^\infty\xi^2f\,d\xi=1$ fixes the compact-support radius. The flux vanishes at the moving front, so the profile solves the equation weakly and approaches the prescribed point mass as time tends to zero.

= Compact radial cubic-diffusion profile
{parent=Porous medium equation}
{title2=$F(\xi)=\sqrt{(1-\xi^2)_+}$}

For the planar diffusion equation $n_t=D_0\nabla\cdot[(n/n_0)^2\nabla n]$, a mass-preserving radial <similarity solution> has $n=n_0\lambda^{-2}F(r/(r_0\lambda))$. Substitution reduces the equation to $(\xi F^2F')'=-(\xi^2F)'$ when $\lambda^5\dot\lambda=D_0/r_0^2$. Zero central flux and $F(0)=1$ give the compact profile shown above, with $\lambda^6=6D_0t/r_0^2$. Its height decays as $t^{-1/3}$ and its radius grows as $t^{1/6}$; its total mass is $2\pi n_0r_0^2/3$.

= Finite propagation in porous-medium diffusion
{parent=Porous medium equation}

In a <porous medium equation> with exponent $m>1$, the effective <diffusion coefficient> vanishes at zero density. A nonnegative compactly supported source profile can therefore retain a finite front for every finite positive time. For example, the <radial cubic-diffusion source profile> has support radius proportional to $t^{1/6}$. This notion concerns a moving diffusion front; it does not require the constant characteristic cone used for a <wave equation>.

= Forced filling similarity for power-law diffusion
{parent=Porous medium equation}

Consider a <nonlinear diffusion equation> with uniform supply, $\phi h_t=D_m(h^m h_x)_x+R$, on $x>0$, with an absorbing boundary $h(0,t)=0$ and initially $h=0$. Far from the boundary, $h=Rt/\phi$. Balancing the <time derivative>, supply, and diffusion gives
$$
h=\frac{Rt}{\phi}F(\eta),\qquad \eta=\frac{x}{\ell(t)},\qquad \ell(t)=\left[\frac{D_mR^m t^{m+1}}{\phi^{m+1}}\right]^{1/2}.
$$
The <similarity solution> satisfies
$$
(F^mF')'+1-F+\frac{m+1}{2}\eta F'=0,\qquad F(0)=0,\qquad F(\infty)=1.
$$
The outward boundary <volume flux per unit width> is $Q=R\ell c_m$, where $c_m=\lim_{\eta\downarrow0}F^mF'$. Integrating the equation gives
$$
c_m=\frac{m+3}{2}\int_0^\infty(1-F)\,d\eta.
$$
Thus the growing region of depleted storage fixes the discharge prefactor, and $Q\propto t^{(m+1)/2}$.

= Separable draining profile for power-law diffusion
{parent=Porous medium equation}

For $\phi h_t=D_m(h^m h_x)_x$ on $0<x<L$, with $h(0,t)=0$ and zero right-hand <volume flux>, a <separation of variables> gives
$$
h=\left(\frac{\phi L^2}{mD_m\tau}\right)^{1/m}F(x/L),\qquad \tau=t+t_0,
$$
where the positive profile satisfies
$$
(F^mF')'+F=0,\qquad F(0)=0,\qquad F'(1)=0.
$$
Its boundary <volume flux per unit width> is
$$
Q=\frac{D_m}{L}\left(\frac{\phi L^2}{mD_m\tau}\right)^{(m+1)/m}c_m,\qquad c_m=\lim_{\xi\downarrow0}F^mF'=\int_0^1F\,d\xi.
$$
These profiles are exact separated solutions and describe the leading long-time discharge for a broad class of positive initial data. The virtual origin $t_0$ depends on the initial profile; it does not make the separated solution an exact representation of every initial condition.

= Barenblatt solution
{c}
{parent=Porous medium equation}
{wiki}

The Barenblatt solution is a mass-preserving <similarity solution> of the <porous medium equation>. For $u_t=\beta(uu_x)_x$ in one dimension it has the form
$$
u(x,t)=\left[A t^{-1/3}-\frac{x^2}{6\beta t}\right]_+.
$$

= Radial cubic-diffusion source profile
{parent=Barenblatt solution}
{title2=$F(\xi)=\sqrt{[1-\xi^2]_+}$}

For the planar <porous medium equation> $n_t=[D_0/(3n_0^2)]\Delta(n^3)$, the mass-$Q$ point-source <similarity solution> is $n=n_0\lambda^{-2}\sqrt{[1-r^2/(r_0^2\lambda^2)]_+}$, with $\lambda=(6D_0t/r_0^2)^{1/6}$ and $r_0^2=3Q/(2\pi n_0)$. Its front grows as $t^{1/6}$ and its central density falls as $t^{-1/3}$. The <mass conservation> integral uses the planar area element $2\pi r\,dr$. Although the density slope diverges at the front, its diffusion flux vanishes there, giving a compactly supported <weak solution> with initial measure $Q\delta^{(2)}$.

= Planar volume-conserving nonlinear-diffusion similarity
{parent=Barenblatt solution}
{title2=$h_t=A(h^nh_x)_x$}

For $n,A>0$, the one-dimensional <porous medium equation> $h_t=A(h^nh_x)_x$ has a volume-preserving <similarity solution> $h=[c_n(R^2-x^2)/(At)]_+^{1/n}$, where $c_n=n/[2(n+2)]$ and $R\propto t^{1/(n+2)}$. On a reflecting half-line with conserved area $M$, $R=[M(At/c_n)^{1/n}/I_n]^{n/(n+2)}$, where $I_n=\tfrac12 B(\tfrac12,1+1/n)$. For a whole symmetric release use half its total area as $M$. The <Beta function> fixes normalization, while finite-front zero <volume flux> fixes the edge condition.

= Semicircular pulse under cubic diffusion
{parent=Planar volume-conserving nonlinear-diffusion similarity}
{title2=$L\propto t^{1/4},\quad H\propto t^{-1/4}$}

= Cubic diffusion pulse
{synonym}

The source-type <similarity solution> of $h_t=D(h^2h_x)_x$ with whole-line conserved area $\mathcal V$ is $h=H(t)[1-x^2/L(t)^2]_+^{1/2}$, where $L=2(\mathcal V/\pi)^{1/2}(Dt)^{1/4}$ and $H=(\mathcal V/\pi)^{1/2}(Dt)^{-1/4}$. Its <volume flux> vanishes at the finite front, while the velocity tends to $\dot L=L/(4t)$. A reflecting half-line uses twice its prescribed area in these formulas.

= Two-dimensional Barenblatt profile for quadratic porous-medium diffusion
{parent=Barenblatt solution}

For the radially symmetric equation
$$
n_t=D\nabla\cdot(n\nabla n)
$$
in two dimensions, the mass-$N$ similarity solution has the compactly supported form
$$
n(r,t)=\left(\frac N{Dt}\right)^{1/2}
\left[\frac{x_0^2-x^2}{8}\right]_+,
\qquad
x=\frac r{(NDt)^{1/4}},
$$
where mass normalization gives $x_0=(16/\pi)^{1/4}$. Its support radius grows as $t^{1/4}$ and its occupied area grows as $t^{1/2}$.

= Fick's first law
{title2=$\mathbf J=-D\nabla C$}
{parent=Diffusion equation}
{c}

<Fick's laws> describe diffusion through a flux law and <conservation of mass>. The first law says that <diffusive flux> points down the concentration <gradient>:
$$
\mathbf J=-D(C)\nabla C.
$$
Combining it with local conservation gives
$C_t=\nabla\cdot(D(C)\nabla C)$.

= Heat equation
{parent=Diffusion equation}
{wiki}

The heat equation
$$
u_t=D\nabla^2u,
\qquad D>0,
$$
models diffusion with constant diffusivity $D$.

= Heat balance with volumetric heat generation
{parent=Heat equation}
{title2=$c_{\rm vol}T_t=\nabla\cdot(k\nabla T)+H$}

Integrating <energy> conservation over a fixed material volume gives storage equal to incoming conductive <heat flux> plus internally generated <heat>. Here $c_{\rm vol}$ is <volumetric heat capacity>, with units $\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}\,K^{-1}}$. For a mass-specific <heat capacity>, the <density> factor must be included. Constant isotropic conductivity reduces the divergence to $k\nabla^2T$; variable conductivity must remain inside the divergence.

= Annual ground-temperature wave
{parent=Heat equation}
{title2=$T-T_0=-T_Ae^{-z/\delta}\cos(\omega t-z/\delta)$}

A sinusoidal surface <temperature> applied to a homogeneous semi-infinite conductor produces a periodic depth profile with penetration length $\delta=\sqrt{2\kappa/(\omega C)}$. It follows by substituting a complex exponential into $C\partial_tT=\kappa\partial_z^2T$ and taking the root that decays at depth. A depth $z$ attenuates the amplitude by $e^{-z/\delta}$ and delays the phase by $z/\delta$. This is <diffusion> with phase delay, rather than a nondispersive travelling wave.

= Fixed-temperature and fixed-flux heat equation on an interval
{parent=Heat equation}
{title2=$u_t=\nu u_{xx},\quad u(0,t)=0,\quad k u_x(L,t)=H$}

For positive thermal <diffusivity> $\nu$, <thermal conductivity> $k$ and length $L$, subtract the <stationary solution> $w(x)=Hx/k$. The remainder satisfies the <heat equation> with a homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary condition> at zero and a homogeneous <Neumann boundary condition> at $L$. The <mixed Dirichlet-Neumann modes on an interval> are $\sin(k_nx)$, where $k_n=(n+1/2)\pi/L$. Thus for an initial <temperature> profile $u_0$,
$$
u(x,t)=\frac{Hx}k+\sum_{n=0}^\infty b_n\sin(k_nx)e^{-\nu k_n^2t},\qquad b_n=\frac2L\int_0^L\left(u_0(x)-\frac{Hx}k\right)\sin(k_nx)dx.
$$
For zero initial <temperature>, $b_n=-2H(-1)^n/(kLk_n^2)$. The series gives the initial profile in the usual <Fourier series> sense and a smooth solution for $t>0$. If $H\ne0$, the zero initial profile does not satisfy the prescribed endpoint derivative at $t=0$: the <temperature> can be continuous there without its spatial <derivative> extending continuously to that corner. The sign $k u_x(L,t)=H>0$ describes incoming <heat flux>, since the outward conductive <heat flux> is $-k u_x$.

= Heat flow from a triangular initial temperature
{parent=Heat equation}
{title2=$T(x,t)=\sum_{n\geq1}8H\sin(n\pi/2)\sin(n\pi x/L)e^{-D(n\pi/L)^2t}/(\pi^2n^2)$}

On $0<x<L$ with zero-temperature endpoints, let the initial <temperature> rise linearly from zero to height $H$ at $L/2$ and then fall linearly to zero. <Integration by parts> on the two halves gives <Fourier sine series> coefficients $8H\sin(n\pi/2)/(\pi^2n^2)$. Under the <heat equation> $T_t=DT_{xx}$ with $D>0$, each coefficient decays by $e^{-D(n\pi/L)^2t}$, giving the displayed solution. Only odd spatial modes occur; absolute coefficient summability proves <uniform convergence> for $t\geq0$ via the <Weierstrass M-test>. This spatial profile also occurs in a <midpoint-plucked fixed-end string>, where the time factors oscillate instead of decaying.

= Heat flow from a triangular initial temperature with insulated endpoints
{parent=Heat flow from a triangular initial temperature}
{title2=$T=H/2-(4H/\pi^2)\sum_{j\geq0}\cos(2(2j+1)\pi x/L)e^{-D[2(2j+1)\pi/L]^2t}/(2j+1)^2$}

On a bar of length $L$, let the initial <temperature> increase linearly from zero at either endpoint to height $H$ at the midpoint. With thermal diffusivity $D>0$ and insulated <Neumann boundary conditions>, a <Fourier cosine series> gives the displayed solution. Its constant term is the initial <integral average> $H/2$. <Integration by parts> on the two straight segments gives zero coefficients for odd modes and modes divisible by four; the remaining coefficients are $-4H/[\pi^2(2j+1)^2]$. Nonconstant modes decay, leaving the uniform temperature $H/2$, consistent with conservation of the total heat. This contrasts with the zero-temperature boundary solution in <heat flow from a triangular initial temperature>, where heat can leave the bar and the limit is zero. The initial endpoint slopes do not satisfy the insulated condition, so derivatives need not converge to their initial endpoint values as $t\downarrow0$; the series satisfies the Neumann condition for every $t>0$ and recovers the continuous initial profile.

= Cooling of an initially uniform square
{parent=Heat equation}
{title2=$T\sim(16T_0/\pi^2)\sin(\pi x/\ell)\sin(\pi y/\ell)e^{-2\kappa\pi^2t/\ell^2}$}

For a square of side $\ell$ initially at constant temperature $T_0$, held at zero boundary temperature for $t>0$, the <heat equation> $T_t=\kappa\Delta T$ with $\kappa>0$ has solution
$$
T=\frac{16T_0}{\pi^2}\sum_{\substack{m,n\geq1\\m,n\text{ odd}}}\frac{\sin(m\pi x/\ell)\sin(n\pi y/\ell)}{mn}\,e^{-\kappa\pi^2(m^2+n^2)t/\ell^2}.
$$
The <double Fourier sine series> of the initial constant gives the coefficients. The spatial modes are <Dirichlet Laplacian eigenfunctions>, so <separation of variables> gives their decay rates. At late times the $(1,1)$ mode dominates, giving the displayed profile; the next nonzero decay exponent is $10\kappa\pi^2/\ell^2$. At early times the series approaches the constant at each interior point and in <L2 space>, while the boundary remains at zero. An instantaneous change to the boundary condition therefore need not produce a solution continuous at the initial boundary in space-time.

= Periodic diffusion on a circle
{parent=Heat equation}
{title2=$u(x,t)=\bar f+\sum_{n\geq1}(a_n\cos nx+b_n\sin nx)e^{-Dn^2t}$}

For a circle of circumference $2\pi$, the <heat equation> $u_t=Du_{xx}$ has periodic matching of both $u$ and $u_x$ at $x=\pm\pi$. For $D>0$, the displayed <Fourier series> solves the initial-value problem, with $\bar f=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_{-\pi}^\pi f(x)dx$ and the usual real <Fourier coefficients>. Every nonconstant <normal mode> decays, leaving the conserved mean. In particular $f(x)=(\pi-|x|)^2$ gives $\bar f=\pi^2/3$, $a_n=4/n^2$ and $b_n=0$. The initial cusp is smoothed for every positive time.

= Periodic sine with an endpoint-correcting linear term
{parent=Periodic diffusion on a circle}
{title2=$b_n=2(-1)^n\sin L/\{n\pi[1-(n\pi/L)^2]\}$}

For $L>0$, extend $f_L(x)=\sin x-x\sin L/L$ from $[-L,L]$ periodically. The linear term makes both endpoint values zero and the endpoint first <derivatives> match. The function is <odd>, so its <Fourier series> has only sine terms, with the displayed <Fourier coefficients>. Indeed, for $k_n=n\pi/L\ne1$,
$$
\int_0^L\sin x\sin(k_nx)dx=\frac{(-1)^nk_n\sin L}{1-k_n^2},\qquad \int_0^Lx\sin(k_nx)dx=-\frac{L(-1)^n}{k_n};
$$
subtracting and multiplying by $2/L$ proves the formula. The coefficients decay as $O(n^{-3})$. At $L=m\pi$, the single coefficient $b_m$ has removable <limit> $1$, with all others zero. Multiplying $b_n$ by $e^{-\kappa k_n^2t}$ solves the corresponding <heat equation> on the <circle>.

= Heat equation with a single stationary sine forcing
{parent=Heat equation}

On $0<x<L$, let $u_t=\nu u_{xx}+G\sin(n\pi x/L)$, with $\nu>0$, zero <Dirichlet boundary conditions>, and zero initial data. Write $k_n=n\pi/L$. Its stationary solution is $u_s=G\sin(k_nx)/(\nu k_n^2)$. Subtracting it removes the forcing from the <partial differential equation> and leaves a <heat equation> with initial data $-u_s$.

Because the forcing is a single spatial <eigenfunction>, <separation of variables> gives the entire solution without mixing modes:
$$
u(x,t)=\frac{G}{\nu k_n^2}(1-e^{-\nu k_n^2t})\sin(k_nx).
$$
Its integral is $G[1-(-1)^n](1-e^{-\nu k_n^2t})/(\nu k_n^3)$; <even> modes have zero signed net integral. Removing forcing from the evolution equation does not remove its amplitude from the initial data for the transient.

= Steady temperature of a uniformly heated cylindrical wire
{parent=Heat equation}
{title2=$T(\rho)=T_{\rm air}+\frac{Ha}{2\gamma}+\frac{H(a^2-\rho^2)}{4\kappa}$}

For a long homogeneous circular wire of radius $a$, uniform volumetric heating $H$, constant <thermal conductivity> $\kappa$ and negligible end losses, the steady <heat equation> is $\kappa\rho^{-1}(\rho T')'+H=0$. Regularity at the axis excludes a <logarithm> term, giving $T=T_s+H(a^2-\rho^2)/(4\kappa)$. If the surface obeys <Newton's law of cooling>, $-\kappa T'(a)=\gamma(T_s-T_{\rm air})$, then $T_s=T_{\rm air}+Ha/(2\gamma)$. Here $\gamma$ has units $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}K^{-1}}$ and $H$ has units $\mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}$. For uniform <electric current> $I$ and constant <electrical conductivity> $\sigma$, <Joule heating> gives $H=I^2/(\sigma\pi^2a^4)$.

= Fundamental diffusion mode
{parent=Heat equation}
{title2=$T(x,t)=e^{-\nu_0t}R_0(x)$}

For a <diffusion equation> with fixed homogeneous <boundary conditions>, a fundamental diffusion mode is an <eigenfunction> of its nonnegative decay operator belonging to the smallest decay <eigenvalue> $\nu_0$, when such an <eigenvalue> exists. It is the slowest decaying separated <normal mode>. With a discrete <spectrum> and an isolated lowest <eigenvalue>, initial data having a nonzero projection onto its <eigenspace> approach this decay law at late times. Strongly vanishing <thermal diffusivity> can destroy existence of a lowest mode, as illustrated by the <loss of a spectral gap under strongly degenerate radial diffusion>.

= Heat equation on a rectangle with two insulated sides
{parent=Heat equation}
{title2=$T=\sum_{n\geq1,m\geq0}A_{nm}\sin(n\pi x/a)\cos(m\pi y/b)e^{-\lambda\pi^2(n^2/a^2+m^2/b^2)t}$}

On $0<x<a$, $0<y<b$, impose zero <Dirichlet boundary conditions> at $x=0,a$ and zero <Neumann boundary conditions> at $y=0,b$. <Separation of variables> for $T_t=\lambda\Delta T$ gives the displayed sine-cosine expansion. The zero cosine mode must be included: its norm is $b$, whereas the positive cosine modes have norm $b/2$. Thus $A_{n0}=2(ab)^{-1}\int\!\int T_0\sin(n\pi x/a)$ and $A_{nm}=4(ab)^{-1}\int\!\int T_0\sin(n\pi x/a)\cos(m\pi y/b)$ for $m\geq1$. Expanding $x(a-x)$ gives sine coefficient $4a^2[1-(-1)^n]/(n^3\pi^3)$. Initial data $x(a-x)\sin^2(2\pi y/b)$ therefore involve only $m=0,4$ and odd $n$. Their leading long-time mode is $4a^2\sin(\pi x/a)e^{-\lambda\pi^2t/a^2}/\pi^3$.

= Reaction-diffusion spectral decay threshold
{parent=Heat equation}
{title2=$\kappa<\pi^2,\quad\kappa_h=4h^{-2}\sin^2(\pi h/2)$}

On the unit interval with zero <Dirichlet boundary conditions>, $u_t=u_{xx}+\kappa u$ has sine-mode growth rates $\kappa-j^2\pi^2$. Every finite-energy initial condition decays exactly when the largest rate is negative, namely $\kappa<\pi^2$. A centered <finite difference> grid with $h=1/(M+1)$ has eigenvectors $\sin(j\pi m/(M+1))$ and growth rates $\kappa-4h^{-2}\sin^2(j\pi/(2(M+1)))$. Its all-data decay threshold is the displayed $\kappa_h$, strictly less than $\pi^2$ and approaching it from below. Equality leaves a stationary lowest mode; exceeding the threshold allows growth.

= Spherical reaction-diffusion growth threshold
{parent=Reaction-diffusion spectral decay threshold}
{title2=$\gamma>\pi^2D/a^2$}

Thus <diffusion> leakage suppresses growth below the displayed threshold, while the mode is stationary at equality. The expression at $r=0$ uses its continuous limit. In a linear <neutron> multiplication model this is the competition between bulk production and surface loss.

= Heat evolution of bounded data need not converge at large times
{parent=Heat equation}

Bounded smooth initial data alone do not guarantee a pointwise large-time limit for the <heat equation> on Euclidean space. In four dimensions, $g(x)=(1+|x|^2)^i$ has modulus one. At the origin its <heat kernel> average is asymptotic to $4^i\Gamma(2+i)t^i$, which oscillates indefinitely. Initial data vanishing at infinity do have heat averages tending to zero. This distinction matters when using <Duhamel principle> to construct a stationary solution for a time-independent source.

= Dirichlet heat-reaction threshold on the unit square

= Normal-coordinate divergence-form heat parametrix
{parent=Heat kernel expansion}
{title2=$B_k(x)=-\int_0^1s^{k-1}(LB_{k-1})(sx)\,ds$}

For $L=-\partial_j(g^{ij}\partial_i)$ in <geodesic normal coordinates>, the <Gauss lemma> gives $g^{ij}x_i=x_j$. Substituting $F=(4\pi t)^{-n/2}e^{-|x|^2/(4t)}\sum_{k\geq0}t^kB_k$ into $(\partial_t+L)F=0$ therefore yields $(x\cdot\nabla+k)B_k=-LB_{k-1}$, with $B_0=1$. Integration along radial rays gives the displayed formula. Every coefficient is smooth at the centre. Any smooth solution of $(x\cdot\nabla+k)h=0$ is zero for $k>0$, since $s^kh(sx)$ is constant and tends to zero at $s=0$. This proves uniqueness. In this special divergence-form case $L1=0$, so every $B_k$ for $k>0$ is zero: the Gaussian itself solves the equation exactly on the coordinate ball. The <positive Laplace-Beltrami operator> instead has a metric volume-density term and a nonconstant leading amplitude.

= Heat-kernel solution
{parent=Heat kernel}

For initial data $u_0$ on the real line, Fourier transformation or convolution with the fundamental solution gives
$$
u(x,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\frac{e^{-(x-\xi)^2/(4Dt)}}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}
u_0(\xi)\,d\xi.
$$

For a <bounded function> $u_0$, the <convolution> is well defined at every positive time because the <heat kernel> has integral one. The <error-function diffusion front> uses a <Heaviside step function> <initial condition> and gives one smoothed interface. The <heat equation with interval-indicator initial data> instead has two interfaces; subtracting their two <error functions> produces the finite-interval profile.

= Heat-kernel convergence from a conditionally convergent Gaussian integral
{parent=Heat-kernel solution}
{title2=$\int_{\mathbb R}\varphi(\xi)e^{-\xi^2/(4T)}\,d\xi\text{ converges}$}

Let $\varphi$ be continuous and suppose that the displayed <improper integral> converges separately at both infinite endpoints for some $T>0$. Absolute convergence is not required. Then its <heat-kernel solution> exists, is smooth and solves the <heat equation> for $0<t<T$, and tends to $\varphi(x)$ as $t\downarrow0$ at each fixed $x$.

To prove the smoothing claim, take a primitive $H$ of $\varphi(\xi)e^{-\xi^2/(4T)}$. The assumed convergence makes $H$ bounded. For $q(x,\xi,t)=K_t(x-\xi)e^{\xi^2/(4T)}$, <integration by parts> gives
$$
\int\varphi(\xi)K_t(x-\xi)\,d\xi=-\int H(\xi)q_\xi(x,\xi,t)\,d\xi.
$$
The endpoint term vanishes. The coefficient of $\xi^2$ in the exponent of $q$ is $-(T-t)/(4tT)<0$. Its <derivatives> therefore have Gaussian decay on every compact subset of $\mathbb R\times(0,T)$, justifying <differentiation under the integral sign> in the absolutely convergent right-hand expression. Since $q_t=q_{xx}$, it satisfies the <heat equation>.

For the initial limit, apply the same construction to $\varphi(\xi)-\varphi(x)$ and split at $|\xi-x|=\delta$. <Continuity> controls the local part by its small oscillation times the unit kernel mass. On each remaining tail, $q$ is monotone for sufficiently small $t$: its maximum lies at $\xi=xT/(T-t)$, approaching $x$. If the relevant primitive has absolute bound $M$, <integration by parts> bounds that tail by $2M$ times the value of $q$ at its finite endpoint. For either endpoint $\xi=x\pm\delta$, that value tends to zero like $t^{-1/2}e^{-\delta^2/(4t)}$ times a fixed constant. Both tails vanish, proving the limit. Separate endpoint convergence matters here; merely assuming a symmetric <Cauchy principal value> does not provide the bounded primitive used in this proof.

= Heat-kernel convergence for Gaussian-integrable initial data
{parent=Heat-kernel solution}
{title2=$K_t*\varphi\to\varphi$}

Suppose $\varphi$ is continuous at $x$ and, for some $T>0$,
$$
\int_{\mathbb R}|\varphi(\xi)|e^{-\xi^2/(4T)}\,d\xi<\infty.
$$
For $0<t<T$, its <convolution> with the <heat kernel> $K_t(s)=(4\pi t)^{-1/2}e^{-s^2/(4t)}$ is smooth and solves the <heat equation>. On every <compact set> in $\mathbb R\times(0,T)$, spatial or time <derivatives> of the <heat kernel> are bounded by the displayed integrable weight times a constant: their <polynomial> factors are absorbed by the strictly narrower Gaussian. This justifies <differentiation under the integral sign>.

Moreover, $\int K_t=1$, so
$$
(K_t*\varphi)(x)-\varphi(x)=\int K_t(x-\xi)\bigl(\varphi(\xi)-\varphi(x)\bigr)\,d\xi.
$$
Inside $|\xi-x|<\delta$, <continuity> makes the difference uniformly small. Outside that interval, for $t\leq T/4$, the inequality $\xi^2\leq2(\xi-x)^2+2x^2$ gives
$$
K_t(x-\xi)e^{\xi^2/(4T)}
\leq\frac{e^{x^2/(2T)}}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}e^{-\delta^2/(8t)}.
$$
The right side tends to zero, proving that the weighted tail contribution vanishes; the constant term $\varphi(x)$ is controlled by the vanishing Gaussian tail mass. Thus $(K_t*\varphi)(x)\to\varphi(x)$ as $t\downarrow0$. In particular, continuous data of <polynomial growth> satisfy this hypothesis, even when they are unbounded. This extends the <Gaussian approximate identity> argument beyond bounded data.

= Constant-flux tracer inlet solution
{parent=Advection-diffusion equation}

For a <passive scalar> on a half-line, impose constant total solute flux at its inlet, with initially zero <concentration> and constant positive <advection> speed $U$ and <mass diffusivity> $\mathcal D$. A <Laplace transform> in time gives

With $\xi=Ux/\mathcal D$, $\vartheta=U^2t/\mathcal D$ and $z_\pm=(\xi\pm\vartheta)/(2\sqrt\vartheta)$, its inverse is

$$
The <complementary error function> describes the smeared front. This is a flux boundary condition, not the <constant-concentration inlet solution>. At large axial <Péclet number>, both have the leading advancing front $\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}(z_-)$.

= Gamma impulse solution for linearly increasing diffusivity
{parent=Advection-diffusion equation}
{title2=$p(z,t)$}

On $z>0$, consider $p_t+a p_z=D(zp_z)_z$ with $a,D>0$, zero scalar flux $ap-Dzp_z$ at the endpoints for $t>0$, and unit initial impulse at the origin. Put $r=a/D$ and $\eta=z/(Dt)$. The normalized <similarity solution> is
$$
p(z,t)=\frac{\eta^r e^{-\eta}}{Dt\,\Gamma(1+r)}.
$$
The ansatz $p=(Dt)^{-1}f(\eta)$ gives $[\eta f'+(\eta-r)f]'=0$. Endpoint decay sets this integrated constant to zero and hence $f\propto\eta^re^{-\eta}$; the <gamma function> normalizes its <integral> to one. Its scale is $Dt$, so it converges weakly to a unit impulse as $t\downarrow0$. Its <gamma distribution> shape is $1+r$, its maximum occurs at $z=at$, and its <expected value> is $(a+D)t$. For width proportional to $z$, the associated <horizontally averaged plume concentration> is proportional to $z^{r-1}e^{-z/(Dt)}$: its interior maximum is $(a-D)t$ when $a>D$, its supremum occurs at the origin when $a=D$, and it is singular there when $0<a<D$. The physical finite source regularizes this ideal-origin behaviour.

= Weighted Neumann heat kernel with constant drift
{parent=Advection-diffusion equation}
{title2=$K_\beta(x,y,t)$}

On $(0,L)$, the <differential operator> $\mathcal L=\partial_x^2+\beta\partial_x=e^{-\beta x}\partial_x(e^{\beta x}\partial_x)$ with homogeneous <Neumann boundary conditions> is <self-adjoint> in the <weighted inner product> with weight $e^{\beta x}$. Its <eigenfunctions> and nonnegative decay rates are
$$
\phi_0=1,\quad\lambda_0=0,\qquad
\phi_k=e^{-\beta x/2}\left[\cos(q_kx)+\frac\beta{2q_k}\sin(q_kx)\right],\quad
\lambda_k=q_k^2+\frac{\beta^2}4,\quad q_k=\frac{k\pi}L.
$$
Their squared <norms> are $N_0=(e^{\beta L}-1)/\beta$ and $N_k=L[1+\beta^2/(4q_k^2)]/2$. The <Sturm-Liouville eigenfunction expansion> gives
$$
K_\beta(x,y,t)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{e^{-\lambda_kt}\phi_k(x)\phi_k(y)}{N_k}.
$$
This kernel acts against the measure $e^{\beta y}dy$, not Lebesgue measure alone. It is symmetric in $x,y$; the transition density against Lebesgue measure is $e^{\beta y}K_\beta(x,y,t)$.

= Resolvent kernel for Neumann advection-diffusion on an interval
{parent=Weighted Neumann heat kernel with constant drift}
{title2=$\mathcal R_p(x,y)$}

For $L>0$, constant $\beta>0$ and $\operatorname{Re}p>0$, on $(0,L)$ put $b=\beta/2$, $\kappa=\sqrt{p+b^2}$ with the <principal square root>, and
$$
u_p(x)=e^{-bx}\left[\cosh(\kappa x)+\frac b\kappa\sinh(\kappa x)\right],\quad
v_p(x)=e^{-b(x-L)}\left[\cosh(\kappa(L-x))-\frac b\kappa\sinh(\kappa(L-x))\right].
$$
These satisfy $(p-\partial_x^2-\beta\partial_x)u_p=(p-\partial_x^2-\beta\partial_x)v_p=0$ and the left and right homogeneous <Neumann boundary conditions>, respectively. The weighted <Wronskian> gives
$$
D_p=e^{\beta x}(u_p'v_p-u_pv_p')=e^{bL}\frac p\kappa\sinh(\kappa L),\qquad
\mathcal R_p(x,y)=\frac{u_p(\min(x,y))v_p(\max(x,y))}{D_p}.
$$
The first <derivative> jumps by $-e^{-\beta y}$, so the resolvent integrates against $e^{\beta y}\,dy$. Its time <Laplace transform> interpretation is $\mathcal R_p=\int_0^\infty e^{-pt}K_\beta(t)dt$. The factor $p$ records the zero <eigenvalue> and constant stationary <eigenfunction>.

= Nonlinear diffusion equation
{parent=Diffusion equation}

= Nonlinear diffusion
{synonym}

A nonlinear diffusion equation lets diffusivity depend on the evolving field; the porous-medium equation is a standard example.

= Quadratic profiles for nonlinear diffusion with constant loss
{parent=Nonlinear diffusion equation}
{title2=$h_t=(hh_x)_x-1$}

For the displayed <nonlinear diffusion equation>, a quadratic ansatz $h=A(t)+B(t)x^2$ reduces the <partial differential equation> exactly to
$$
B'=6B^2,\qquad A'=2AB-1.
$$
Indeed $(hh_x)_x=2AB+6B^2x^2$, and equality of the constant and quadratic <coefficients> proves the reduction. For nonzero curvature, integration gives $B=-1/[6(t-t_0)]$. On $\tau=t-t_0>0$, the remaining <linear ordinary differential equation> is $A'+A/(3\tau)=-1$, whose <integrating factor> is $\tau^{1/3}$. Thus, including spatial translation,
$$
h(x,t)=C\tau^{-1/3}-\frac{(x-x_0)^2}{6\tau}-\frac34\tau.
$$
All three constants $C,x_0,t_0$ are arbitrary, on a domain with $\tau>0$. When the quadratic coefficient vanishes, the more general affine ansatz gives $h=px+(p^2-1)t+q$.

A narrower ansatz $h=a t^{-1/3}(1-x^2/t^{2/3})-bt$ fixes two coefficients together and permits exactly $(a,b)=(1/6,3/4)$ or $(0,1)$. The zero value of $a$ is a valid spatially constant branch, not an algebraic exception to discard. These finite-parameter families are exact solutions; they are not the complete general solution of the nonlinear <partial differential equation>, and their sums need not be solutions.

= Logarithmic diffusion
{parent=Nonlinear diffusion equation}
{title2=$u_t=\kappa\Delta\log u$}

For a positive density $u$, logarithmic diffusion is the <nonlinear diffusion equation> $u_t=\kappa\Delta\log u$, equivalently $u_t=\kappa\operatorname{div}(u^{-1}\nabla u)$. Its <diffusion coefficient> is inversely proportional to the density. In one dimension, a mass-$M$ <self-similar solution> is
$$
u(x,t)=\frac{2\kappa t}{x^2+(2\pi\kappa t/M)^2}.
$$
Its integral is $M$, and substitution verifies the equation. This is $M$ times a <Cauchy distribution> density of scale $2\pi\kappa t/M$. It tends weakly to $M$ times the <Dirac delta distribution> as $t\downarrow0$; the slow algebraic tails illustrate the enhanced diffusion where density is small.

= Principal diffusion coefficients
{parent=Nonlinear diffusion equation}
{title2=$c(s),\quad c(s)+sc\prime(s)$}

For flux $c(|\nabla u|)\nabla u$, the <derivative> matrix is $c(s)I+c'(s)\nabla u\otimes\nabla u/s$ with $s=|\nabla u|>0$. Its tangential and normal coefficients are $c(s)$ and $c(s)+sc'(s)$. Positivity of the scalar conductance alone therefore does not guarantee forward parabolicity.

= Perona-Malik equation
{c}
{parent=Nonlinear diffusion equation}
{title2=$u_t=\operatorname{div}(c(|\nabla u|)\nabla u)$}

The Perona-Malik equation uses decreasing diffusivity to smooth low-gradient image regions while reducing transport across edges. For $r=|\nabla u|>0$, its principal diffusion coefficients are $c(r)$ tangent to a level set and $c(r)+rc'(r)$ normal to it. A negative normal coefficient allows formal sharpening but causes forward-backward <ill-posedness>. Positivity of $c$ alone is insufficient for parabolicity.

= Forward-backward threshold for gradient-weighted exponential diffusion
{parent=Perona-Malik equation}
{title2=$F'(p)=|p|e^{-p^2/(2\lambda^2)}(2-p^2/\lambda^2)$}

For one-dimensional flux $F(p)=p|p|e^{-p^2/(2\lambda^2)}$, the <nonlinear diffusion equation> is $u_t=F'(u_x)u_{xx}$. The sole one-dimensional member of the <principal diffusion coefficients> is positive for $0<|u_x|<\sqrt2\lambda$, negative above $\sqrt2\lambda$, and zero at the two endpoints. Negative diffusion gives formal sharpening with <ill-posedness> through high-frequency growth. The extra factor $|p|$ matters: the unweighted exponential conductance has threshold $\lambda$ instead.

= Regularized Perona-Malik diffusion
{parent=Perona-Malik equation}
{title2=$u_t=\operatorname{div}(c(|\nabla(G_\sigma*u)|)\nabla u)$}

Computing conductance from a smoothed <gradient> gives $u_t=\operatorname{div}(c(|\nabla(G_\sigma*u)|)\nabla u)$. The <image edge> detector is nonlocal, while the local highest-order diffusion has positive coefficient when conductance is positive. A smooth kernel, lower conductance bound and appropriate regularity/boundary hypotheses support a well-posed regularized filter.

= Two-dimensional Barenblatt solution with diffusivity proportional to concentration
{c}

For $C_t=k\nabla\cdot(C\nabla C)$ with total mass $2\pi M$,
$$
C(r,t)=\sqrt{\frac{M}{kt}}
\left(\frac1{\sqrt2}-\frac18\frac{r^2}{\sqrt{Mkt}}\right)_+.
$$
Its compact support has radius $r_0(t)=(32Mkt)^{1/4}$.

The substitution

C(x,t)=e^{at}G(x,\tau(t)),
\qquad
\tau(t)=
\begin{cases}
(e^{at}-1)/a,&a\ne0,\\
t,&a=0,

reduces $C_t=k\nabla\cdot(C\nabla C)+aC$ to

= Reaction-diffusion equation
{synonym}

A reaction-diffusion system combines local reaction kinetics with spatial diffusion,

= Cubic fast-slow reaction-diffusion model
{parent=Reaction–diffusion system}
{title2=$u_t=Du_{xx}+u(1-u)(u-\alpha)-w+I,\quad w_t=\varepsilon(u-\beta w)$}

This nondimensional two-variable <reaction–diffusion system> combines a fast cubic local response with slow negative feedback. With $D>0$, $0<\alpha<1$, $\beta>0$ and $\varepsilon\ll1$, its spatially uniform rest state at $I=0$ is linearly stable: the Jacobian has <trace> $-\alpha-\varepsilon\beta$ and <determinant> $\varepsilon(1+\alpha\beta)$. For suitable parameters a finite perturbation can nevertheless cause a large excursion followed by recovery. Frozen recovery gives a <bistable cubic reaction-diffusion equation>; the full coupled travelling-wave boundary-value problem determines whether front pairs form a pulse. This is a generic excitable-medium formulation of the <FitzHugh-Nagumo model>, without assigning any biological variable or application.

= Diffusion-driven stationary instability
{parent=Reaction–diffusion system}

For reaction Jacobian $J=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$ with $\operatorname{tr}J<0$ and $\det J>0$, a Fourier mode evolves under $J-q^2\operatorname{diag}(D_u,D_v)$. Its determinant is $D_uD_vq^4-(D_va+D_ud)q^2+\det J$. A finite unstable band requires $D_va+D_ud>0$ and $(D_va+D_ud)^2>4D_uD_v\det J$, with an allowed domain mode in that band.

= Equal diffusion cannot destabilize a stable reaction Jacobian
{parent=Diffusion-driven stationary instability}

If both <diffusion> coefficients equal $D$, each spatial mode has matrix $J-Dq^2I$. Its eigenvalues are those of $J$ shifted by the negative real number $Dq^2$. Thus a reaction-stable homogeneous state remains stable under this equal-<diffusion> linear mechanism.

= Removing a variable scalar reaction rate by an integrating factor
{parent=Reaction–diffusion system}
{title2=$u(x,t)=e^{-\int_0^ta(s)ds}v(x,t)$}

For $u_t=u_{xx}-a(t)u$, the displayed substitution converts the equation exactly into the <heat equation> $v_t=v_{xx}$. The scalar prefactor preserves homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions>. Consequently each spatial <eigenfunction> $X_n$ with $X_n''=-\lambda_nX_n$ acquires temporal factor $\exp[-\lambda_nt-\int_0^ta(s)ds]$. The same algebra works with an evolution variable named $y$; calling that variable spatial does not alter the <separation of variables>. For $a(t)=1+\sin t$, the prefactor normalized to one at zero is $e^{-t+\cos t-1}$.

= Positivity of the first Dirichlet reaction-diffusion coefficient
{parent=Reaction–diffusion system}
{title2=$a_1=\sqrt{2/L}\int_0^Lf(x)\sin(\pi x/L)\,dx>0$}

For nonnegative nonzero integrable initial data $f$ on $(0,L)$, the first coefficient in the normalized <Fourier sine series> is strictly positive, because the first sine <eigenfunction> is positive throughout the interval. For $\rho_t=D\rho_{xx}+\gamma\rho$ with zero <Dirichlet boundary conditions>, the first decay rate is $\lambda_1=D\pi^2/L^2-\gamma$. Every higher rate exceeds it by $D\pi^2(n^2-1)/L^2$, so smoothing for positive time and the spectral gap imply $e^{\lambda_1t}\rho(x,t)\to a_1\sqrt{2/L}\sin(\pi x/L)$ uniformly. Thus all interior points share the growth/decay threshold $\gamma=D\pi^2/L^2$; the endpoints remain zero. At equality the limiting profile is nonzero and stationary.

= Dirichlet reaction-diffusion growth threshold in a cuboid
{c}
{parent=Reaction–diffusion system}
{title2=$s_{111}=\gamma-\mu\pi^2(L_x^{-2}+L_y^{-2}+L_z^{-2})$}

For $n_t=\mu\Delta n+\gamma n$ in a rectangular box with zero <Dirichlet boundary conditions>, <separation of variables> gives spatial <eigenfunctions> $\sin(p\pi x/L_x)\sin(q\pi y/L_y)\sin(r\pi z/L_z)$ and temporal rates $\gamma-\mu\pi^2(p^2/L_x^2+q^2/L_y^2+r^2/L_z^2)$. The largest rate is the displayed one. Every nonzero nonnegative initial density has positive projection onto this first <eigenfunction>, so its sign determines eventual exponential growth or decay; equality leaves a stationary first mode. At fixed volume, write $a_j=L_j^{-2}$: their product is fixed, and the <arithmetic-geometric mean inequality> minimizes their sum when all three are equal. Thus the cube maximizes the growth rate among rectangular boxes of fixed volume; making a box sufficiently thin suppresses growth.

= Bistable cubic reaction-diffusion equation
{parent=Reaction–diffusion system}
{title2=$u_t=Du_{xx}+u(1-u)(u-r)$}

The scalar equation $u_t=Du_{xx}+u(1-u)(u-r)$ with $D>0$ and $0<r<1$ has two stable homogeneous phases $u=0,1$ and an unstable intervening equilibrium $u=r$. Indeed the reaction derivatives at these three equilibria are $-r$, $-(1-r)$ and $r(1-r)$, respectively. A <travelling wave> can connect the two stable phases. At the equal-depth value $r=1/2$, its decreasing <heteroclinic orbit> is stationary. Away from this value the phases have unequal potential depths and the front propagates.

= Logistic front of a bistable cubic equation
{parent=Bistable cubic reaction-diffusion equation}
{title2=$U=[1+e^{(x-ct-q_0)/\sqrt{2D}}]^{-1}$}

For the decreasing front connecting $1$ to $0$, the balanced stationary <travelling-wave reduction of a reaction-diffusion system> has first integral $DU_x^2/2-U^2(1-U)^2/4=0$. Choosing the negative derivative gives $U_x=-U(1-U)/\sqrt{2D}$ and hence $U=[1+e^{(x-q)/\sqrt{2D}}]^{-1}$. This logistic profile remains an exact <travelling wave> when the reaction parameter differs from $1/2$: using $z=(x-ct-q_0)/\sqrt D$, its derivative is $U_z=-U(1-U)/\sqrt2$, and the wave equation is satisfied for $c=\sqrt{D/2}(1-2r)$. Thus the speed changes with the potential bias while the shape, apart from <translation>, does not.

= Basally forced quadratic activator-inhibitor model
{parent=Reaction–diffusion system}
{title2=$(u_*,v_*)=(1+r,(1+r)^2)$}

A quadratic <activator> can have both basal production and activation inhibited by a second species. A normalized example has reaction rates $f(u,v)=1+ru^2/v-u$ and $g(u,v)=q(u^2-v)$, with $r,q>0$ and positive concentrations. At its <spatially homogeneous equilibrium>, $v=u^2$, so $u_*=1+r$ and $v_*=(1+r)^2$. Writing $a=(r-1)/(r+1)$, its reaction <Jacobian matrix> is $J=\begin{pmatrix}a&-r/(1+r)^2\\2q(1+r)&-q\end{pmatrix}$. Thus $\operatorname{tr}J=a-q$ and $\det J=q>0$, giving linear <asymptotic stability> when $q>a$ and instability when $q<a$. Basal production makes $a$ change sign at $r=1$; a positive activation feedback needed for a conventional <Turing instability> requires $r>1$.

= Weak focus at the Hopf threshold of a basal activator-inhibitor model
{parent=Basally forced quadratic activator-inhibitor model}
{title2=$\ell_1=(a-1)/8<0$}

At $q=a=(r-1)/(r+1)>0$, the reaction <eigenvalues> are $\pm i\sqrt a$. This linear marginal case is a weakly attracting focus, not a nonlinear center. To check it directly, put $u=(1+r)(1+x)$, $v=(1+r)^2(1+y)$, $h=(1+a)/2$, $\beta=(1-a)/2$, $\omega=\sqrt a$, and $X=x$, $Y=(hy-a x)/\omega$. The equations become $\dot X=-\omega Y+F_2+F_3+O(4)$, $\dot Y=\omega X+G_2+G_3+O(4)$, with
$$
F_2=(\beta X-\omega Y)^2/h,\quad F_3=-(aX+\omega Y)(\beta X-\omega Y)^2/h^2,\quad G_2=\omega(hX^2-F_2),\quad G_3=-\omega F_3.
$$
On the <unit circle> write $A=\cos\theta F_2+\sin\theta G_2$, $B=\cos\theta F_3+\sin\theta G_3$, $C=\cos\theta G_2-\sin\theta F_2$. In <polar coordinates>, $d\varrho/d\theta=(A/\omega)\varrho^2+(B/\omega-AC/\omega^2)\varrho^3+O(\varrho^4)$. The quadratic angular average vanishes. Integrating the quadratic correction over a whole cycle contributes its final squared integral, also zero, leaving the cubic average. Using the even trigonometric moments gives $\langle B\rangle=a/4$ and $\langle AC\rangle/\omega=(a+1)/8$. The <Poincaré map> is therefore $\varrho\mapsto\varrho+[2\pi/\omega](a-1)\varrho^3/8+O(\varrho^4)$. Since $0<a<1$, it contracts sufficiently small positive radii and proves nonlinear <asymptotic stability> at the threshold.

= Turing threshold of a basally forced quadratic activator-inhibitor model
{parent=Basally forced quadratic activator-inhibitor model}
{c}
{title2=$k_c^2=\sqrt{q/p}$}

Add diffusivities $1,p$ to the <basally forced quadratic activator-inhibitor model>. A <Fourier mode> with $s=k^2$ has matrix $J-s\operatorname{diag}(1,p)$ and <determinant> $D(s)=ps^2+(q-pa)s+q$. When $q>a$, its <trace> is negative for every $s\geq0$. A positive-growth mode therefore occurs precisely when $D(s)<0$ for some positive $s$. The parabola has a positive minimizer only if $pa>q$, and its minimum is negative exactly when $(pa-q)^2>4pq$. Thus the ordinary <Turing instability> requires $a>0$, $q>a$ and $p>q/(\sqrt{1+a}-1)^2$. At onset, $k_c^2=(pa-q)/(2p)=\sqrt{q/p}$ and the critical <wavelength> is $2\pi(p/q)^{1/4}$. A bounded spatial domain further requires an allowed mode inside the unstable band.

= Inhibitor
{synonym}

An inhibitor in an activator-inhibitor <reaction-diffusion system> suppresses the <activator>. Its diffusion and reaction feedback can stabilize a spatially uniform state while allowing some nonzero <wavenumbers> to grow through a <Turing instability>.

= Activator in a reaction-diffusion system
{parent=Reaction–diffusion system}

= Activator
{synonym}

An activator in an activator-inhibitor <reaction-diffusion system> is a species whose local feedback enhances activation. Near a homogeneous equilibrium, positive self-feedback is reflected in the corresponding diagonal entry of the reaction <Jacobian matrix>. Cross-coupling to an <inhibitor> and differing diffusion rates can generate a <Turing instability>.

= Quadratic activator-inhibitor model
{parent=Reaction–diffusion system}
{title2=$u_t=u_{xx}+u^2/v-bu,\quad v_t=dv_{xx}+u^2-v$}

For positive concentrations and positive parameters, this <reaction-diffusion system> has homogeneous equilibrium $(u_*,v_*)=(1/b,1/b^2)$ and reaction <Jacobian matrix> $\begin{pmatrix}b&-b^2\\2/b&-1\end{pmatrix}$. Its trace is $b-1$ and determinant is $b$, making the equilibrium reaction-stable exactly when $0<b<1$. Stable complex eigenvalues permit damped oscillations, which must be distinguished from sustained periodic oscillations.

= Turing threshold of the quadratic activator-inhibitor model
{parent=Quadratic activator-inhibitor model}
{c}
{title2=$0<b<1,\quad db>3+2\sqrt2$}

A mode with squared <wavenumber> $q$ in the <quadratic activator-inhibitor model> has negative trace $b-1-(1+d)q$ when $0<b<1$. Its determinant is $D(q)=dq^2+(1-db)q+b$. A positive minimum location and a negative minimum require $db>1$ and $(db-1)^2>4db$, equivalent to $db>3+2\sqrt2$. At onset, the double determinant root is $q_c=(1+\sqrt2)/d$. In a bounded domain an admissible nonzero spatial mode must additionally lie in the open negative-determinant band.

= Positive-quadrant Dulac multiplier for quadratic activation
{parent=Quadratic activator-inhibitor model}
{title2=$\nabla\cdot(u^{-2}(f,g))=(b-1)/u^2$}

For the diffusionless <quadratic activator-inhibitor model>, choose the <Dulac function> $u^{-2}$. Then $u^{-2}f=1/v-b/u$ and $u^{-2}g=1-v/u^2$, giving divergence $(b-1)/u^2$. For $0<b<1$ it is strictly negative throughout the simply connected positive quadrant. <Green's theorem> rules out a periodic orbit: its boundary flux would be zero because the rescaled vector field is tangent to the orbit, while its interior divergence integral would be negative.

= Spatially homogeneous equilibrium
{parent=Reaction–diffusion system}

A spatially homogeneous equilibrium of a <reaction-diffusion system> is a constant state $u_*$ satisfying $f(u_*)=0$. Since its spatial derivatives vanish, its stability to homogeneous perturbations is determined by the <Jacobian matrix> $Df(u_*)$; diffusion can additionally destabilize nonconstant modes.

= Morphogen reaction-diffusion equation

A morphogen reaction-diffusion equation models a spatial concentration by diffusion and local production or decay. Linearization at a homogeneous equilibrium $C=0$ replaces $f(C)$ by $f'(0)C$.

It is a transport-and-reaction model for a <morphogen>, rather than the identity of the signaling molecule concept.

= Mixed Dirichlet-Neumann modes on an interval

X_n(x)=\sin(k_nx),

k_n=\frac{(n+1/2)\pi}{L},\quad n\geq0.

For $C_t=DC_{xx}+aC$ with $a>0$, $C(0,t)=0$, and $C_x(L,t)=0$, every mode decays exactly when

L<\frac\pi2\sqrt{\frac Da}.

= Fast-inhibitor elimination in a reaction-diffusion system

If a fast inhibitor satisfies $0=v_{xx}+u-v$, then its spatial <Fourier transform> obeys

\widehat v(k)=\frac{\widehat u(k)}{1+k^2}.
$$
Substitution produces a nonlocal scalar equation for the activator.

= Dispersion relation after fast-inhibitor elimination

For activator diffusion $D$, local linear decay $r$, and coupling $\rho(u-v)$, eliminating the fast inhibitor gives

\sigma(k)=-Dk^2-r+\rho\frac{k^2}{1+k^2}.

Its pattern-forming threshold is

\rho_c=(\sqrt r+\sqrt D)^2,

A <Turing pattern> is a spatially structured state produced by interactions between local reactions and diffusion. A <Turing instability> explains linear pattern onset when a homogeneous equilibrium is stable without diffusion but unstable to a nonzero spatial mode.

= Turing instability
{parent=Turing pattern}
{c}

A Turing, or diffusion-driven, instability occurs when a spatially homogeneous equilibrium is stable to homogeneous perturbations but unstable to a nonzero spatial Fourier mode after unequal diffusion is introduced.

= Fast-inhibitor cubic activator growth rate
{parent=Turing instability}
{title2=$\sigma(k)=-r-Dk^2+\rho k^2/(1+k^2)$}

For $u_t=Du_{xx}-u(u-r)(u-1)-\rho(v-u)$ and $0=v_{xx}-v+u$, a <Fourier mode> perturbation about zero has $v=u/(1+k^2)$ and <growth rate> $\sigma(k)=-r-Dk^2+\rho k^2/(1+k^2)$. If $\rho>D$, its maximum is $-r+(\sqrt\rho-\sqrt D)^2$ at $k^2=\sqrt{\rho/D}-1$; if $\rho\leq D$, its maximum is $-r$ at zero. Replacing $(u,v,r)$ by $(1-u,1-v,1-r)$ gives the corresponding criterion at the other homogeneous equilibrium.

= Two-species diffusion-driven instability criterion
{parent=Turing instability}

= Two-species Turing criterion
{synonym}

Let a stable two-species reaction equilibrium have Jacobian
$$
J=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix},
\qquad \operatorname{tr}J<0,
\qquad \det J>0,
$$
and diffusion matrix $\operatorname{diag}(D_u,D_v)$. A mode with $q=k^2$ has determinant
$$
\det(J-qD)=\det J-(D_va+D_ud)q+D_uD_vq^2.
$$
Since its trace decreases with $q$, diffusion-driven instability occurs exactly when
$$
D_va+D_ud>0,
\qquad
(D_va+D_ud)^2>4D_uD_v\det J.
$$

= Impossibility of a two-species Turing instability at equal diffusivities
{parent=Two-species diffusion-driven instability criterion}

When $D_u=D_v=D$, every spatial Fourier mode replaces the reaction Jacobian $J$ by $J-Dk^2I$. Each eigenvalue is shifted left by $Dk^2$, so diffusion cannot destabilize a stable homogeneous equilibrium.

= Near-unity diffusivity ratio for a Turing instability
{parent=Two-species diffusion-driven instability criterion}

For reaction Jacobian
$$
\begin{pmatrix}-1&-1\\1+\delta&1-\delta\end{pmatrix},
$$
the critical diffusivity ratio is
$$
d_c=
\left(
\frac{\sqrt{2\delta}+\sqrt{1+\delta}}{1-\delta}
\right)^2
=1+2\sqrt{2\delta}+O(\delta).
$$
It approaches one as the stable reaction system approaches marginality.

= Brusselator
{parent=Reaction–diffusion system}
{c}
{wiki}

The Brusselator is a two-species autocatalytic reaction model. In the parametrization
$$
f(u,v)=\alpha-(\beta+1)u+u^2v,
\qquad
g(u,v)=\beta u-u^2v,
$$
its positive homogeneous equilibrium is $(u_*,v_*)=(\alpha,\beta/\alpha)$.

= Hopf coefficient of the Brusselator
{parent=Brusselator}
{title2=$l_1=-(a+2)/8$}

For $u'=1-(b+1)u+au^2v$, $v'=bu-au^2v$, the equilibrium $(1,b/a)$ has a <Hopf bifurcation> at $b=1+a$. Coordinates $x=u-1$, $y=-\sqrt a[(u-1)+(v-b/a)]$ give the threshold equations $x'=-\sqrt a\,y+(1-a)x^2-2\sqrt a\,xy-ax^3-\sqrt a\,x^2y$, $y'=\sqrt a\,x$. In the usual planar radial normalization the cubic coefficient is $-(a+2)/8<0$, proving a nondegenerate supercritical bifurcation. The leading small-amplitude angular frequency is $\sqrt a$.

= Brusselator trapping region
{parent=Brusselator}
{c}

For the spatially homogeneous Brusselator with parameters $r,s>0$, set $\alpha=r/(1+s)$. The quadrilateral
$$
\alpha\leq x,\qquad
0\leq y\leq\frac s\alpha,\qquad
x+y\leq r+\frac s\alpha
$$
is a <trapping region>. The four inward-pointing tests use $\dot x\geq0$ on $x=\alpha$, $\dot y\geq0$ on $y=0$, $\dot y\leq0$ on $y=s/\alpha$, and $\dot x+\dot y=r-x\leq0$ on the sloping edge.

= Brusselator periodic-orbit criterion
{parent=Brusselator}
{c}

The spatially homogeneous Brusselator has its unique positive equilibrium at $(r,s/r)$. Its Jacobian there has determinant $r^2$ and trace $s-1-r^2$. If $s-1>r^2$, the equilibrium is a repeller inside the <Brusselator trapping region>; the <Poincare-Bendixson theorem> then supplies a periodic orbit.

= Turing threshold of the Brusselator
{parent=Brusselator}
{c}

When $u$ and $v$ have diffusivities $D$ and $1$, respectively, and the homogeneous Brusselator equilibrium is stable, a <Turing instability> occurs precisely when
$$
\beta>(1+\alpha\sqrt D)^2.
$$
At onset,
$$
D_c=\frac{(\sqrt\beta-1)^2}{\alpha^2},
\qquad
k_*^2=\frac{\alpha^2}{\sqrt\beta-1}.
$$

= Discrete-mode Turing threshold for the Brusselator
{parent=Turing threshold of the Brusselator}
{title2=$b_T=\min_{k\ne0\ \mathrm{allowed}}[1+a^2D_u/D_v+D_uk^2+a^2/(D_vk^2)]$}

On a specified finite domain, only its boundary-compatible wavenumbers can grow. Solve the <Brusselator> mode determinant for its neutral value of $b$ and minimize over those nonzero modes. Minimizing over all continuous $k$ instead gives the usual threshold and $k_c^2=a/\sqrt{D_uD_v}$. The finite-mode minimum must still precede the homogeneous threshold to describe a <Turing instability>.

= Brusselator Turing-before-Hopf diffusivity condition
{parent=Turing threshold of the Brusselator}
{c}
{title2=$D_v/D_u>((\sqrt{1+a^2}+1)/a)^2$}

For the <Brusselator>, the stationary spatial threshold is $b_T=(1+a\sqrt{D_u/D_v})^2$ and the homogeneous oscillatory threshold is $b_H=1+a^2$. A genuine <Turing instability> appears first only if $b_T<b_H$, which rearranges to the displayed diffusivity-ratio bound. Equality gives simultaneous stationary spatial and homogeneous oscillatory neutral modes. Otherwise homogeneous loss of stability occurs first.
