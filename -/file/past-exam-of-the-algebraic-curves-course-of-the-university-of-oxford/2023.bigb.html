Solutions to all three questions of the <University of Oxford> Part B <algebraic curve> paper, sat on 6 June 2023. Source: https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/request_for_mathematics_examinat/response/3129786/attach/3/FOI%20202507%20740%20Data.pdf#page=218[Oxford's released examination PDF, compilation pages 218–220; paper pages 1–3]. Curves in Question 1 are understood to be <reduced plane curves>; the qualification concerning lines in 2(a), and the unnecessary restriction on the half-period values in 3(d), are discussed where they arise.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Write $C=V(P)$, where $P\in\mathbb C[x,y,z]$ is a <squarefree polynomial> and a <homogeneous polynomial> of degree $d$. A <projective point> $p\in C$ is a <singular point> precisely when
$$
P_x(p)=P_y(p)=P_z(p)=0.
$$
If $p=[p_0:p_1:p_2]$ is a <nonsingular point of an algebraic curve>, its <projective tangent line> is
$$
\boxed{T_pC=\{[x:y:z]:P_x(p)x+P_y(p)y+P_z(p)z=0\}.}
$$
The coefficients are not all zero, and the <Euler theorem for homogeneous functions> gives $p_0P_x(p)+p_1P_y(p)+p_2P_z(p)=dP(p)=0$, so the line passes through $p$. Changing the representative of the <projective point> only rescales the equation.

The strong form of <Bézout's theorem> says that two <projective plane curves> $C,D$ of degrees $d,e$, with no common <irreducible component>, have finitely many intersections and
$$
\boxed{\sum_{p\in C\cap D}I_p(C,D)=de.}
$$
Each <intersection multiplicity> is a positive integer. At $p\in C\cap D$,
$$
\boxed{I_p(C,D)=1\iff p\text{ is nonsingular on both curves and }T_pC\ne T_pD.}
$$
This is the criterion for a <transverse intersection of plane curves>. In particular, an intersection at a <singular point> of either curve contributes at least two.

= Solution
{parent=b}

If $d=1$, $C$ is a line and has no <singular points>. Suppose $d>1$ and take $P$ to be an <irreducible polynomial>. First, $P_x$ is not the zero <polynomial>: otherwise $P\in\mathbb C[y,z]$, and a <homogeneous polynomial> in two variables of degree greater than one factors into linear factors over $\mathbb C$, contrary to irreducibility.

Let $D=V(P_x)$ be the <polar curve> in this coordinate direction. Since $\deg P_x=d-1<d$, the <irreducible polynomial> $P$ cannot divide $P_x$. Thus $C,D$ have no common <irreducible component>, and <Bézout's theorem> applies. One may count repeated factors of $P_x$ with their multiplicities; alternatively, passing to its reduced equation only decreases $\deg D$.

Every <singular point> $p$ of $C$ satisfies $P_x(p)=0$ and has $I_p(C,D)\geq2$ by part (a). Consequently, if $s$ is the number of distinct <singular points>,
$$
2s\leq\sum_{p\in C\cap D}I_p(C,D)\leq d(d-1),
\qquad
\boxed{s\leq\frac{d(d-1)}2.}
$$
This argument also proves that the <singular locus> is finite.

= Solution
{parent=c}

There is a <nonsingular point of an algebraic curve> on $C$, since part (b) makes its <singular locus> finite whereas a <projective curve> has infinitely many complex points. Apply a <projective transformation> taking one such point to $p=[1:0:0]$, and use $P$ for the transformed <homogeneous polynomial>. As in part (b), $P_x$ is nonzero and $D=V(P_x)$ has no common <irreducible component> with $C$.

The <Euler theorem for homogeneous functions> and its differentiated identities give
$$
P_x(p)=dP(p)=0,
\qquad
P_{xx}(p)=(d-1)P_x(p)=0,
$$
and
$$
P_{xy}(p)=(d-1)P_y(p),
\qquad
P_{xz}(p)=(d-1)P_z(p).
$$
Since $p$ is nonsingular on $C$, at least one of $P_y(p),P_z(p)$ is nonzero. Because $d>1$, the <polar curve> $D$ is also nonsingular at $p$, and these identities show that $T_pD=T_pC$. Hence $I_p(C,D)\geq2$.

This additional intersection is separate from all the <singular points> of $C$. Thus <Bézout's theorem> gives
$$
2s+2\leq\sum_{q\in C\cap D}I_q(C,D)\leq d(d-1),
\qquad
\boxed{s\leq\frac{d(d-1)}2-1.}
$$
The improvement comes from making the <polar curve> tangent at a chosen nonsingular point, thereby reserving at least two of the available intersections.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Let $d_i=\deg C_i$. Under the <reduced plane curve> convention, the distinct <irreducible components> have $\sum_i d_i=d$. Away from intersections with other components, a point of $C_i$ is nonsingular on the union exactly when it is nonsingular on $C_i$. For example, a local equation of the union is the equation of $C_i$ multiplied by a nonvanishing factor. At an intersection of two components, differentiating their product makes every first <partial derivative> vanish. Therefore
$$
\operatorname{Sing}(C)=\bigcup_i\operatorname{Sing}(C_i)\ \cup\ \bigcup_{i<j}(C_i\cap C_j).
$$

Part (b) bounds the number of <singular points> of $C_i$ by $d_i(d_i-1)/2$. For each distinct pair of <irreducible components>, <Bézout's theorem> bounds the number of distinct intersections by $d_id_j$. The <cardinality> of a union is at most the sum of the <cardinalities>, even if some points have been counted several times. Hence
$$
\begin{aligned}
\#\operatorname{Sing}(C)
&\leq\sum_i\frac{d_i(d_i-1)}2+\sum_{i<j}d_id_j\\
&=\frac12\left(\left(\sum_i d_i\right)^2-\sum_i d_i\right)
=\boxed{\frac{d(d-1)}2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The reduced convention matters. If one instead allows the equation $L^2=0$ as a nonreduced curve and uses its equation to test singularity, every point on its supporting line has vanishing first <partial derivatives>. A finite bound would then be false.

= Solution
{parent=e}

Take a union of $d$ distinct lines in <general position for projective lines>, meaning that no three pass through one point. Each pair contributes one distinct <singular point>, and every other point lies on just one nonsingular line. Thus the union has <degree of a projective curve> $d$ and exactly
$$
\boxed{\binom d2=\frac{d(d-1)}2}
$$
distinct <singular points>. For $d=1$, this gives a line with zero <singular points>, as required.

For a concrete construction, choose distinct $t_1,\ldots,t_d\in\mathbb C$ and set
$$
L_i:\quad y=t_i x+t_i^2z,
\qquad
C:\quad\prod_{i=1}^d(y-t_ix-t_i^2z)=0.
$$
For $i\ne j$, the intersection is $[-(t_i+t_j):-t_it_j:1]$. Substitution into the equation of $L_k$ gives $-(t_k-t_i)(t_k-t_j)$, which is nonzero when $k$ is distinct from $i,j$. Thus there are no triple intersections, including at infinity.

= Solution

A <nonsingular point of an algebraic curve> $p$ is an <inflection point of an algebraic plane curve>, or <flex>, if its <projective tangent line> has contact of order at least three:
$$
I_p(C,T_pC)\geq3.
$$
Here the <projective tangent line> must not be a component of $C$. Ordinary tangency has contact order two.

Let $C=V(F)$, where $F$ is a <homogeneous polynomial> of degree $d\geq2$. Its <Hessian curve> is defined by
$$
H_F=\det\begin{pmatrix}
F_{xx}&F_{xy}&F_{xz}\\
F_{yx}&F_{yy}&F_{yz}\\
F_{zx}&F_{zy}&F_{zz}
\end{pmatrix}=0.
$$
Each entry has degree $d-2$, so a nonzero $H_F$ has degree $3(d-2)$. By the <Hessian criterion for a flex>, the <flexes> are exactly the points of $C\cap V(H_F)$. The stated no-common-component assumption and <Bézout's theorem> therefore give
$$
\boxed{\#\{\text{distinct flexes of }C\}\leq3d(d-2).}
$$
This counts distinct points; higher contact can use more than one unit of <intersection multiplicity> at a single point.

For completeness, the <Hessian criterion for a flex> follows by putting $p=[0:0:1]$ and $T_pC=\{y=0\}$, with $F_y(p)\ne0$. The <Euler theorem for homogeneous functions> gives $F_{xz}(p)=F_{zz}(p)=0$ and $F_{yz}(p)=(d-1)F_y(p)$, whence
$$
H_F(p)=-(d-1)^2F_y(p)^2F_{xx}(p).
$$
The restriction $F(x,0,1)$ already has vanishing constant and linear terms. Its quadratic term vanishes exactly when the <intersection multiplicity> with the tangent is at least three, proving the criterion.

For $d\geq3$ the bound is the actual <maximum number of flexes of a smooth plane curve>: a general nonsingular curve has only contact of order three, and the corresponding intersections with its <Hessian curve> are simple. One way to see genericity is to vary a point and a line through it, a three-dimensional family. Contact of order at least four imposes four independent linear conditions on the coefficients of a degree-$d$ equation for $d\geq3$. Thus the locus of equations with such contact is a proper exceptional locus. Locally, if the curve is $y=h(x)$, its <Hessian curve> restricted to $C$ is a nonvanishing factor times $h''(x)$; contact exactly three gives a simple zero.

A nonsingular <plane conic> has no <flexes>, agreeing with the formula at $d=2$. A line is excluded from this formulation: its tangent is itself and its <Hessian matrix> is zero, so the no-common-component hypothesis fails. The formula is therefore intended for $d\geq2$, rather than giving the meaningless negative value at $d=1$.

= Solution

For $F=x^3+y^3+3xz^2$, the first <partial derivatives> are
$$
F_x=3(x^2+z^2),\qquad F_y=3y^2,\qquad F_z=6xz.
$$
Their simultaneous vanishing would force $y=0$, $xz=0$ and $x^2+z^2=0$, hence $x=y=z=0$, which is not a <projective point>. This also verifies that the given <plane cubic> is nonsingular.

The <Hessian matrix> and its <determinant> are
$$
\operatorname{Hess}F=6\begin{pmatrix}x&0&z\\0&y&0\\z&0&x\end{pmatrix},
\qquad H_F=216y(x^2-z^2).
$$
Thus the <Hessian curve> is the union of $y=0$, $x=z$ and $x=-z$. On $y=0$, the equation of $C$ reduces to $x(x^2+3z^2)=0$, giving
$$
\boxed{[0:0:1],\quad[\mathrm i\sqrt3:0:1],\quad[-\mathrm i\sqrt3:0:1].}
$$
On $x=z$ one must have $z\ne0$, so put $z=x=1$ and obtain $y^3=-4$. On $x=-z$ similarly put $z=1$, $x=-1$, obtaining $y^3=4$. Choose $r\in\mathbb C$ with $r^3=4$ and let $\omega=\exp(2\pi\mathrm i/3)$. The remaining points are
$$
\boxed{[1:-r\omega^j:1],\quad[-1:r\omega^j:1],\qquad j=0,1,2.}
$$
All nine listed <projective points> are distinct. They exhaust the intersection with the <Hessian curve>, so the <Hessian criterion for a flex> proves that these, and only these, are the <flexes> in this example. There are none at infinity: $z=0$ and $H_F=0$ would force $x=y=0$ on $C$.

= Solution
{parent=c}

First make the invertible linear change of <homogeneous coordinates>
$$
U=-y,\qquad V=\sqrt3\,z,\qquad W=x.
$$
The original equation becomes
$$
V^2W=U^3-W^3=(U-W)(U-\omega W)(U-\omega^2W),
\qquad \omega=\exp(2\pi\mathrm i/3).
$$
This takes the <flex> $[0:0:1]$ to the point at infinity $[0:1:0]$ of a <Weierstrass model>.

Put $h=\omega-1$ and choose $s\in\mathbb C$ with $s^2=h^3$. Both are nonzero. Define
$$
X=\frac{U-W}{h},\qquad Y=\frac V{s},\qquad Z=W.
$$
Since $U=hX+Z$, the three factors on the right are $hX$, $h(X-Z)$ and $h(X-\lambda Z)$, where
$$
\boxed{\lambda=\frac{\omega^2-1}{\omega-1}=1+\omega=\frac{1+\mathrm i\sqrt3}{2}.}
$$
The resulting equation is precisely
$$
\boxed{Y^2Z-X(X-Z)(X-\lambda Z)=0,}
$$
the <Legendre form of an elliptic curve>. In particular, $\lambda\ne0,1$.

Combining the two changes gives an explicit <projective transformation> from the original coordinates:
$$
\begin{pmatrix}X\\Y\\Z\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}
-1/h&-1/h&0\\
0&0&\sqrt3/s\\
1&0&0
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its <determinant> is $-\sqrt3/(hs)\ne0$, so this is a valid transformation of the whole <projective plane>, including the points where an affine coordinate would be undefined.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Let $(r_0,r_1,r_2)$ be any ordering of the three distinct numbers $0,1,\lambda$. Set $h=r_1-r_0$ and choose $s\ne0$ with $s^2=h^3$. The <projective transformation>
$$
\begin{pmatrix}X\\Y\\Z\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}
1/h&0&-r_0/h\\
0&1/s&0\\
0&0&1
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix}
$$
has the required matrix form. As $x=hX+r_0Z$ and $y=sY$, substitution gives
$$
Y^2Z=X(X-Z)(X-\widetilde\lambda Z),
\qquad
\boxed{\widetilde\lambda=\frac{r_2-r_0}{r_1-r_0}.}
$$
The choice $s^2=h^3$ is exactly what cancels the coefficient introduced by the change in $x$.

The six orderings give the <Legendre parameter orbit>:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
(r_0,r_1,r_2)&\widetilde\lambda\\\hline
(0,1,\lambda)&\lambda\\
(1,0,\lambda)&1-\lambda\\
(0,\lambda,1)&1/\lambda\\
(\lambda,0,1)&(\lambda-1)/\lambda\\
(1,\lambda,0)&1/(1-\lambda)\\
(\lambda,1,0)&\lambda/(\lambda-1)
\end{array}
$$
Every entry is different from $0,1$ when $\lambda\ne0,1$.

There are no further possibilities for a matrix of the stated form. Its <determinant> is $acd$, so $a,c,d$ must be nonzero. On $z=1$ it induces the affine change $u\mapsto(a/d)u+b/d$ and scales $v=y/z$ by $c/d$. It preserves the line $y=0$, whose three intersections with the <plane cubic> have $u$-coordinates $0,1,\lambda$. Their images must have coordinates $0,1,\widetilde\lambda$. Choosing the two roots sent to $0,1$ determines the affine change and leaves exactly the six orderings above.

For general $\lambda$ the six values are distinct. They coincide in some special cases: $\lambda\in\{-1,2,1/2\}$ gives three values, and $\lambda^2-\lambda+1=0$ gives two. In particular, for the value obtained in part (c), the two possible values are $(1\pm\mathrm i\sqrt3)/2$. This does not contradict the paper's qualification that there are six for general $\lambda$.

A <divisor on an algebraic curve> is a finite formal sum
$$
D=\sum_{p\in C}n_pp,\qquad n_p\in\mathbb Z.
$$
Its <degree of a divisor> is $\deg D=\sum_pn_p$. It is an <effective divisor>, written $D\geq0$, when every $n_p\geq0$.

A <meromorphic differential on a Riemann surface> is a one-form represented in every local <holomorphic> coordinate $t$ by $a(t)\,dt$, where $a$ is a <meromorphic function>. Under a coordinate change $t=t(u)$, its coefficient becomes $a(t(u))t'(u)$, so these expressions agree on overlapping charts. Since $t'(u)$ is a nonvanishing <holomorphic function> for a valid coordinate change, the order of a zero or <pole> of the differential is independent of coordinate. Equivalently, for a fixed nonconstant <meromorphic function> $h$, any such differential can be written $f\,dh$ with $f$ meromorphic.

A <canonical divisor> is the <divisor on an algebraic curve>
$$
K=(\eta)=\sum_{p\in C}\operatorname{ord}_p(\eta)p
$$
of a nonzero <meromorphic differential on a Riemann surface> $\eta$. If $\eta'=f\eta$, then $(\eta')=(\eta)+(f)$, so all <canonical divisors> are related by <linear equivalence of divisors>. Their degree is
$$
\boxed{\deg K=2g-2.}
$$

The <Riemann-Roch theorem> states that, for every <divisor on an algebraic curve> $D$ and any <canonical divisor> $K$ on the nonsingular <projective curve> $C$ of <genus> $g$,
$$
\boxed{\ell(D)-\ell(K-D)=\deg D+1-g,}
\qquad
\ell(D)=\dim_{\mathbb C}\mathcal L(D).
$$
The term $\ell(K-D)$ measures the failure of the naive count of conditions on <meromorphic functions>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Choose a nonzero <meromorphic differential on a Riemann surface> $\eta$ and write $K=(\eta)$. Multiplication defines a linear map
$$
\mathcal L(K)\longrightarrow\operatorname{HD}(C),\qquad f\longmapsto f\eta.
$$
It takes values in the <holomorphic differential forms> because $(f\eta)=(f)+K\geq0$. It is injective since $\eta\ne0$. It is surjective because, for any <holomorphic differential form> $\omega$, the ratio $\omega/\eta$ is a <meromorphic function> and has $(\omega/\eta)+K=(\omega)\geq0$. Thus this map identifies $\operatorname{HD}(C)$ with the <canonical Riemann-Roch space>.

Furthermore, $\mathcal L(0)=\mathbb C$: a <meromorphic function> with no <poles> is <holomorphic> on the <compact Riemann surface> $C$, and hence constant by the <maximum modulus principle>. Applying the <Riemann-Roch theorem> with $D=0$ gives
$$
1-\ell(K)=1-g.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\dim_{\mathbb C}\operatorname{HD}(C)=\ell(K)=g.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=c}

The generator $\eta=f\,dh$ is a nonzero <holomorphic differential form>. Its <canonical divisor> $(\eta)$ is an <effective divisor>, since there are no <poles>. In <genus> one, the <degree of a divisor> formula in part (a) gives
$$
\deg(\eta)=2g-2=0.
$$
An <effective divisor> of degree zero has every coefficient zero. Thus
$$
\boxed{(\eta)=0,\qquad f\,dh\text{ has no zeros anywhere on }C.}
$$
This proves <nonvanishing of holomorphic differentials in genus one>. It concerns the differential itself: a zero or <pole> of the particular coefficient $f$ may be cancelled by the local behavior of $dh$.

= Solution
{parent=d}

An explicit answer, initially written where $yz\ne0$, is the <meromorphic differential on a Riemann surface>
$$
\boxed{\eta=\frac{d(x/z)}{y/z}=\frac{z\,dx-x\,dz}{yz}.}
$$
The ratios $x/z$ and $y/z$ are functions on $C$, so the expression does not depend on the representative of its <homogeneous coordinates>. It is a scalar multiple of an <invariant differential on an elliptic curve>.

On the <complex torus> $\mathbb C/\Lambda$, the differential $dw$ descends from $\mathbb C$ because translation by any element of the <period lattice> leaves $dw$ unchanged. It is <holomorphic> and nowhere zero. The given <biholomorphism> satisfies, wherever the displayed quotient is initially defined,
$$
\Phi^*\eta=\frac{d\wp(w)}{\wp'(w)}=dw.
$$
Consequently, $\eta=(\Phi^{-1})^*(dw)$ extends across every apparent denominator zero and across $[0:1:0]$ as a nonzero <holomorphic differential form> on all of $C$.

One can also check the apparent exceptional points directly. On $z=1$, put $P(X)=4\prod_i(X-e_i)$, so $Y^2=P(X)$. At $Y=0$ the roots are distinct, and differentiating gives
$$
\frac{dX}{Y}=\frac{2\,dY}{P'(X)},
$$
which is regular and nonvanishing there. At $[0:1:0]$, use $u=x/y$, $v=z/y$. The equation is $v=4\prod_i(u-e_iv)$, so $u$ is a <local parameter on a smooth algebraic curve> and $v=4u^3+O(u^5)$. Hence
$$
\eta=v\,d(u/v)=du-\frac{u}{v}\,dv=(-2+O(u^2))\,du,
$$
also regular and nonvanishing.

Only distinctness of the $e_i$ is needed. The paper additionally writes $e_i\ne0$, which is not true for every <period lattice>: for $\Lambda=\mathbb Z+\mathrm i\mathbb Z$, the symmetry $\wp(\mathrm iw)=-\wp(w)$ makes the half-period value at $w=(1+\mathrm i)/2$ zero, since $\mathrm iw\equiv w\pmod\Lambda$. This unnecessary restriction has no effect on the answer.

= Solution
{parent=e}

First fix distinct $q_1,\ldots,q_k$ and let $Q=q_1+\cdots+q_k$. A <negative-degree divisor has no sections>: if $0\ne f\in\mathcal L(D)$, then $(f)+D$ is an <effective divisor> of degree $\deg D$, since a <principal divisor> has degree zero, so $\deg D$ cannot be negative. As $\deg K=0$ and $\deg(K-Q)=-k<0$, the <Riemann-Roch theorem> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\ell(Q)=\deg Q+1-g+\ell(K-Q)=k.}
$$

Now choose distinct $p_1,\ldots,p_k$ outside the support of $Q$, and put $P=p_1+\cdots+p_k$. A function with the required zeros and <poles> must be a nonzero element of $\mathcal L(Q-P)$. Conversely, if $0\ne f\in\mathcal L(Q-P)$, then $(f)+Q-P$ is an <effective divisor> of degree zero, hence is zero. It follows that $(f)=P-Q$, giving precisely the required simple zeros and simple <poles>, with no others. Thus the existence question is exactly whether $\mathcal L(Q-P)\ne0$.

Take a <basis> $f_1,\ldots,f_k$ of the <Riemann-Roch space> $\mathcal L(Q)$. Evaluation at the $p_i$ defines the <linear map>
$$
E_P:\mathcal L(Q)\longrightarrow\mathbb C^k,
\qquad f\longmapsto(f(p_1),\ldots,f(p_k)),
$$
whose <kernel> is $\mathcal L(Q-P)$. The <evaluation criterion for a prescribed principal divisor> becomes
$$
\mathcal L(Q-P)\ne0
\iff
\Delta(p_1,\ldots,p_k):=\det(f_j(p_i))_{1\leq i,j\leq k}=0.
$$
The crucial point is that this <determinant> does not vanish identically; a count of unknowns and equations alone would not prove this.

To verify it, begin with $V_0=\mathcal L(Q)$, of <dimension> $k$. Whenever $V_{j-1}\ne0$, choose a nonzero $f\in V_{j-1}$. Its zeros are finite. Choose $p_j$ outside those zeros, the support of $Q$ and the previously chosen points. Evaluation at $p_j$ is then a nonzero linear functional on $V_{j-1}$, so
$$
V_j=\{h\in V_{j-1}:h(p_j)=0\},
\qquad \dim V_j=\dim V_{j-1}-1.
$$
After $k$ steps, $V_k=0$. Thus there exists a tuple of distinct points with $\Delta\ne0$.

The entries of this <determinant> vary <holomorphically> away from the $q_i$, and are <rational functions> on the nonsingular <projective curve>. Its vanishing is therefore a proper closed exceptional locus on this configuration space; its complement is open and dense in the <Zariski topology>. For a generic choice of the $p_i$, $E_P$ is invertible and
$$
\boxed{\ell(Q-P)=0,\quad\text{so no function with the prescribed zeros and poles exists}.}
$$
This holds for every fixed tuple of distinct $q_i$, which proves the assertion for generic choices of both sets of points. At $k=1$, it is especially transparent: $\mathcal L(q_1)=\mathbb C$, so no nonzero member can vanish at a distinct $p_1$.
