= Pressure
{title2=$P=F_\perp/A$}
{wiki}

Pressure measures the compressive <normal force> per unit <surface area>. For a uniformly loaded surface of <surface area> $A$, the magnitude of the perpendicular <force> is $F_\perp=PA$; locally, $P=dF_\perp/dA$. Its <SI> unit is the pascal, $1\,\mathrm{Pa}=1\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}$. A <force> parallel to the surface contributes shear <stress>, rather than <pressure>. In a <gas>, the steady macroscopic <force> comes from many microscopic transfers of <momentum>; <ideal gas pressure from wall collisions> makes this connection explicit. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/2-2-pressure-temperature-and-rms-speed[OpenStax University Physics Volume 2, section 2.2: microscopic origin of pressure]{ref}

For a computed example, a uniform <pressure> of $100\,\mathrm{kPa}$ on a piston of <surface area> $10\,\mathrm{cm^2}=10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^2}$ produces a perpendicular <force> of $100\,\mathrm N$. If the opposite face is exposed to $80\,\mathrm{kPa}$, the net <force> due to the two <pressures> is $(100-80)\times10^3\times10^{-3}=20\,\mathrm N$, directed toward the lower-<pressure> side. The <pressure> on either face and their difference answer different questions; <gauge pressure> records a difference from a specified reference, often <atmospheric pressure>.

A scalar <pressure> describes a <gas> in local <thermodynamic equilibrium> with <isotropic> random particle <velocities>: every wall orientation has the same normal <force> per unit <surface area>. Without this <isotropy>, normal <stresses> can depend on direction and a <pressure tensor> is needed. Thus random motion alone is insufficient; a preferred direction matters.

In <thermodynamics>, <pressure> and <volume (thermodynamics)> are <thermodynamic conjugate variables>. For a <simple compressible system>, reversible expansion delivers <work> $\delta W=P\,dV$, and at fixed <entropy> $S$ and particle number $N$,
$$
P=-\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_{S,N},
$$
where $U$ is <internal energy>. A large <pressure> therefore means both a large wall <force> per unit <surface area> and a large amount of expansion <work> per small increase in <volume (thermodynamics)>.

= Multi-anvil press
{parent=Pressure}

A high-pressure apparatus in which several anvils compress a relatively large specimen. Compared with a <diamond anvil cell>, it typically offers greater sample volume for synthesis and bulk measurements, with less optical access and a lower ultimate pressure range.

= Pressure sensor
{parent=Pressure}

A pressure sensor converts a <pressure> into a recorded signal. An <absolute pressure> sensor uses a <vacuum> reference; a <gauge pressure> sensor uses a specified reference <pressure>. A closed <gas> syringe and an <absolute pressure> sensor can test <Boyle's law> after allowing the <temperature> to stabilize.

= Atmospheric pressure
{parent=Pressure}
{wiki}
{title2=$p_{\rm atm}$}

<Atmospheric pressure> often provides the reference for a <gauge pressure> measurement: $p_g=p_{\rm abs}-p_{\rm atm}$. The absolute <pressure> is measured relative to an ideal <vacuum>, whereas zero <gauge pressure> means equality to the chosen reference. The actual <atmospheric pressure> can vary; it must be specified when converting between these descriptions. <University Physics Volume 1 by William Moebs and others>[OpenStax: atmospheric, gauge and absolute pressure]{ref}.

= Kinematic pressure
{parent=Pressure}
{title2=$p/\rho_0$}

Dividing a <pressure> disturbance by the constant reference <mass density> $\rho_0$ gives a quantity whose <gradient> enters the <Boussinesq equations> directly as an <acceleration>. Its units are squared <velocity>. Distinguish this convention from physical <pressure> when calculating <energy flux> or comparing <wave polarization> formulae.

= Pressure tensor
{parent=Pressure}
{title2=$P_{ij}$}

In the local rest frame of a gas, the <pressure tensor> is the directional <momentum flux> $P_{ij}=\int p_iv_jf(\mathbf p)\,d^3p$, where $f$ is the <phase-space distribution function> per unit <momentum> volume and $\mathbf v$ is particle velocity. For an isotropic distribution, $P_{ij}=P\delta_{ij}$, recovering the <kinetic pressure of an isotropic gas>. An anisotropic distribution can exert different normal <pressures> along different directions.

= Gas pressure
{title2=$p_{\rm gas}$}
{parent=Pressure}

Gas pressure is the mechanical pressure contributed by material particles. For a classical ideal gas, $p_{\rm gas}=nk_BT$.

= Radiation pressure
{title2=$p_{\rm rad}$}
{parent=Pressure}
{wiki}

<Radiation pressure> is the normal <momentum flux> transferred by <electromagnetic radiation> to a surface. A <photon> travelling in <vacuum> carries <momentum> $p_\gamma=E_\gamma/c=h\nu/c$, where $E_\gamma$ is its <energy>, $\nu$ its <frequency>, $h$ the <Planck constant> and $c$ the <speed of light>. <Photon absorption> transfers that <momentum>; <specular reflection> reverses its normal component. This lets <light> exert a <force> even when almost no <energy> becomes <heat> in the receiver.

Two useful formulas describe different preparations. A directed beam normally incident on a stationary perfect absorber gives <pressure> $I/c$, and on a stationary perfect mirror gives $2I/c$. Here $I$ is <light intensity>, the incident <power> per beam cross-sectional <area> perpendicular to propagation, in $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. An <isotropic> <photon gas> has <pressure> $u/3$, where $u=U/V$ is its radiation <internal energy> per <volume (thermodynamics)>, in $\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}$. Stored <energy density> and travelling <energy flux> must be distinguished: an <isotropic> field can have zero net <energy flux> and positive <radiation pressure>.

The physical appeal is that the same <momentum conservation> connects a single <photon> collision, a mirror's measurable <force>, and a radiation-filled cavity's <equation of state>. The directional calculation below complements <radiation pressure on a perfectly absorbing surface>; the equilibrium spectrum and its <temperature> dependence belong to <blackbody radiation> and the <photon gas>.

= Proper-time acceleration by reflected laser light
{parent=Radiation pressure}
{title2=$\beta(\tau)=\tau/(\tau+\tau_0)$}

An ideal receding mirror reflecting a source beam of constant <power> $P$ receives <force> $(2P/c)(1-\beta)/(1+\beta)$ after including both Doppler-shifted reflected <momentum> and reduced impact rate. For fixed <rest mass> $M$, use $dt/d\tau=\gamma$ in the <momentum> equation. It reduces to $d\beta/d\tau=(1-\beta)^2/\tau_0$, where $\tau_0=Mc^2/(2P)$. Starting from rest gives $\beta=\tau/(\tau+\tau_0)$. This continuum mirror model neglects individual-photon recoil and finite beam interception.

= Radiation pressure of a directed beam
{parent=Radiation pressure}
{title2=$p_\perp=(a+2R)I\cos^2\theta/c$}

For normal incidence in <vacuum>, a uniform beam delivers <energy> $IA\,\Delta t$ to an illuminated <surface area> $A$ during time $\Delta t$. A perfect absorber receives <momentum> $IA\,\Delta t/c$, so <force> divided by <surface area> gives $p_{\rm abs}=I/c$. A perfect stationary mirror sends the <photons> back with reversed <momentum>, so <momentum conservation> gives twice the normal transfer, $p_{\rm refl}=2I/c$. These are time-averaged <pressures>; the microscopic arrivals need not be uniform.

\Image[radiation-pressure-beam-photon-transfer.png]
{title=<Photon> <momentum> transfer to an absorber and a mirror}
{description=Original idealized normal-incidence diagram. Blue arrows show the incoming <photon> <momentum>; orange shows the reflected <photon> <momentum>. The green arrow is the <momentum> delivered to the receiver, not another outgoing <photon>. The mirror reverses the incoming <momentum> and therefore receives twice as much.}

For a stipulated model <light intensity> $I=1000\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ and the exact <SI> <speed of light> $c=299792458\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$,
$$
p_{\rm abs}=3.335640952\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{Pa}\simeq3.3356\,\mu\mathrm{Pa},\qquad
p_{\rm refl}=6.671281904\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{Pa}\simeq6.6713\,\mu\mathrm{Pa}.
$$
These are computed predictions, not measured data or a universal sunlight value. If the beam fully covers a normally oriented <surface area> $A=0.010\,\mathrm{m^2}$, the intercepted <radiant flux> is $\Phi=IA=10\,\mathrm W$ and the absorber's <force> is $\Phi/c\simeq33.356\,\mathrm{nN}$. Using the total source <power> would be wrong if only part of its beam reaches the receiver. The absorption prediction assumes complete <photon absorption>, while the reflection prediction assumes complete <specular reflection>; transmission, <scattering of light> and directed thermal <photon emission> require their own outgoing <momentum> accounting.

Measure the <angle of incidence> $\theta$ between the incoming path and the surface's normal line, with $0\leq\theta<\pi/2$. There are two separate <cosine> factors. A fixed actual <surface area> $A$ intercepts only its <projected area> $A\cos\theta$, so $\Phi=IA\cos\theta$. Each incoming <photon> supplies normal <momentum> $(E_\gamma/c)\cos\theta$ on absorption, or twice that on <specular reflection>. Thus, for <radiometric absorptance> $a$ and reflected <power> fraction $R$,
$$
F_\perp=(a+2R)\frac{\Phi}{c}\cos\theta,\qquad
p_\perp=\frac{F_\perp}{A}=(a+2R)\frac{I}{c}\cos^2\theta.
$$
This model assumes transmitted <light> continues straight in the same medium and carries away its original <momentum>. It omits thermal recoil and assumes a stationary receiver. At $\theta=60^\circ$, fixed beam <light intensity> therefore gives one quarter of the normal-incidence <pressure>. For fixed intercepted <radiant flux> $\Phi$, however, the normal <force> has only one <cosine> factor. Equivalently, if the given quantity is receiving-surface <irradiance> $E_{\rm inc}=I\cos\theta$, then $p_\perp=(a+2R)E_{\rm inc}\cos\theta/c$. The distinction matters when interpreting an experiment's angle scan. <Use of radiation pressure for measurement of high-power laser emission by Paul A. Williams and others>[Williams and collaborators, Use of radiation pressure for measurement of high-power laser emission, equation (1): force expressed using intercepted optical power and the incidence-angle cosine]{ref}

Absorption also transfers a tangential <momentum> component at oblique incidence. It contributes a tangential <force>, rather than normal <pressure>. Ideal <specular reflection> preserves the tangential <momentum> component, so it transfers only normal <momentum>.

= Radiation pressure of an isotropic photon gas
{parent=Radiation pressure}
{title2=$p_{\rm rad}=u/3$}

For radiation in <vacuum> with <isotropy>, <energy> is distributed equally per <solid angle>: write $u_\Omega=u/(4\pi)$ for <energy density> per unit <solid angle>. Let $\theta$ be the <angle> between a ray's path and the wall normal. Its normal <velocity> $c\cos\theta$ determines the arrival rate, while normal <momentum> per <energy> is $\cos\theta/c$. Their product is $\cos^2\theta$. For a perfectly reflecting cavity wall,
$$
p_{\rm rad}=2\int_{\rm incoming\ hemisphere}u_\Omega\cos^2\theta\,d\Omega
=\frac{2u}{4\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}d\varphi\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta
=\frac u3.
$$
The leading two is the normal <momentum> reversal; the incoming <hemisphere> contains half of the field's directional <energy density>. This is also the normal component of the bulk <pressure tensor>, $P_{nn}=\int_{4\pi}u_\Omega\cos^2\theta\,d\Omega=u/3$. <Isotropy> makes all three diagonal components equal, while their sum is $u$ because the squared direction components sum to one. The factor one third therefore depends on three-dimensional angular geometry, not on the shape of the <blackbody radiation> spectrum.

\Image[radiation-pressure-isotropic-angular-average.png]
{title=<Isotropic> radiation: angular <energy> weights and normal <momentum flux>}
{description=Original angular model of a reflecting cavity wall. The left panel shows representative incoming paths, not a literal two-dimensional <photon gas>. The right panel uses the correct three-dimensional <solid angle> weight, normalized on an incoming <hemisphere>: $\sin\theta$. The normal <pressure> contribution adds $\cos^2\theta$, giving total weight $\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin\theta\cos^2\theta\,d\theta=1/3$.}

A perfectly absorbing wall exposed to this incoming <hemisphere> receives only $u/6$ from absorption. If it is in <thermal equilibrium> with the <isotropic> radiation, its outgoing emission supplies another $u/6$ of recoil, recovering $u/3$. A cold absorber before equilibrium is a different boundary condition. Similarly, the one-way incident <energy flux> is $cu/4$, whereas the net <energy flux> across a plane in the undisturbed <isotropic> field is zero. Dividing $cu/4$ by $c$ misses the extra normal-<momentum> projection and does not give the cavity <pressure>.

For a stipulated isotropic radiation <energy density> $u=6\,\mu\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}$, the bulk <pressure> is $2\,\mu\mathrm{Pa}$ regardless of its spectral shape. In <thermal equilibrium>, <blackbody radiation> supplies $u=a_{\rm rad}T^4$ with <radiation constant> $a_{\rm rad}=4\sigma/c$, so $p_{\rm rad}=a_{\rm rad}T^4/3$; <Stefan–Boltzmann law> defines $\sigma$. The equilibrium <photon gas> derives this same <equation of state> from its <free energy>.<Statistical Physics by David Tong/3 Quantum Gases>[§3.2, pressure immediately after the photon free-energy formula: $p=E/(3V)$]{ref}

= Radiation-pressure laser power measurement
{parent=Radiation pressure}
{title2=$\Phi=cF_\perp/[(a+2R)\cos\theta]$}

A 2013 experiment by <Paul A. Williams> and collaborators mounted a 25-mm dielectric mirror on a commercial <force-restoration balance>. Removing its pan-weight compensation spring allowed horizontal <force> sensing with a horizontal <laser> path. They measured mirror <optical reflectance> and varied incidence from about $7^\circ$ to $45^\circ$. A 1071-nm <laser>, capable of 530 W, was switched at 0.1 Hz. Digitized <mass>-equivalent readings were converted to <force> using $g$; <quadrature demodulation> isolated the switched signal from drift. <Use of radiation pressure for measurement of high-power laser emission by Paul A. Williams and others>[Williams and collaborators, pp. 2–3, Figures 1–3 and equation (2): preparation, apparatus and readout]{ref}

<Measurement calibration> used a 20-g <mass>, with checks down to 1 mg. The optical <forces> lay below that checked range, limiting the accuracy claim. An angle scan tested the predicted <cosine> dependence at fixed intercepted <power>. Replacing the mirror with a <carbon nanotube> coated absorber increased absorbed <power> while reducing the predicted optical <force>; no modulation peak was detected. This bounded gas-heating artifacts under those conditions, rather than proving their universal absence. The fitted slope was about 7% below $2/c$. Inferring <laser> <power> requires calibrated <force>, <optical reflectance>, <radiometric absorptance> and <angle of incidence>, with <measurement uncertainty> for each. <Use of radiation pressure for measurement of high-power laser emission by Paul A. Williams and others>[Williams and collaborators, pp. 2–4, Figures 3, 5–7: calibration limits, angle and absorber controls, and reported slope discrepancy]{ref}

An equation of state is a relation among thermodynamic state variables such as pressure, volume, temperature, energy, and particle number.

= Compressibility factor
{parent=Equation of state}
{title2=$Z=p/(nk_BT)$}

The <compressibility factor> is the ratio $p/(nk_BT)$, where $n$ is <number density>. A classical <ideal gas> has $Z=1$. The <virial expansion> writes $Z=1+B_2n+B_3n^2+\cdots$ for a dilute <gas>; this dimensionless ratio is distinct from <isothermal compressibility>, which is a response <derivative> with units of inverse <pressure>.

= Barotropic stellar equation of state
{parent=Equation of state}
{title2=$p=p(\rho)$}

= Stellar barotropic equation of state
{synonym}

A <perfect fluid in general relativity> stellar model in which pressure depends only on energy density, $p=p(\rho)$. A positive derivative makes this relation locally invertible and turns outward-decreasing pressure into outward-decreasing density. This general relation need not have the constant ratio $p/\rho$ used for a constant cosmological fluid parameter.

= Clausius gas
{parent=Equation of state}
{c}

A Clausius gas with covolume parameter $b$ obeys $P(V-Nb)=Nk_BT$. It is the repulsive-volume part of a <Van der Waals equation> without the attractive term.

= Van der Waals equation
{c}
{parent=Equation of state}
{wiki}

The <Van der Waals equation> is a simple <real gas> <equation of state> that can also describe a <liquid-gas critical point>. With particle count $N$ fixed, write the <volume per particle> as $v=V/N$:
$$
p=\frac{k_BT}{v-b}-\frac{a}{v^2},\qquad v>b,\quad a>0,\quad b>0,
$$
or equivalently $(p+aN^2/V^2)(V-Nb)=Nk_BT$. Here $p$ is <pressure>, $T$ is absolute <temperature>, and $k_B$ is the <Boltzmann constant>. In <SI>, $v$ and $b$ have units $\mathrm{m^3}$ per particle, $a$ has units $\mathrm{Pa\,m^6}=\mathrm{J\,m^3}$, and $k_B$ has units $\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$; the particle count is dimensionless. The pole at $v=b$ excludes smaller <volume (thermodynamics)> values from this model.

The $b$ correction represents <excluded volume>; the attraction term lowers <pressure> at fixed <temperature> and particle <number density> $1/v$. This is a <mean-field theory>, replacing detailed intermolecular structure by two parameters. At $v\gg b$ with $a/v^2\ll k_BT/v$, it approaches the <ideal gas law> $pv=k_BT$. It supplies a useful qualitative route from a dilute <gas> to <phase coexistence>, without a claim of quantitative accuracy for every <fluid>.<Thermodynamics and Statistical Mechanics by Richard Fitzpatrick/Van der Waals Gas>[opening explanation and equation 6.156: excluded volume and attractive pressure correction; Fitzpatrick uses molar volume]{ref}

Do not mix this particle convention with the <molar volume> convention elsewhere in this article. With <amount of substance> $n_{\rm mol}=N/N_A$, <Avogadro constant> $N_A$, and <molar gas constant> $R=N_Ak_B$, one has $v_m=N_Av$, $b_m=N_Ab$, and $a_m=N_A^2a$, so $p=RT/(v_m-b_m)-a_m/v_m^2$. The molar parameters have units $\mathrm{m^3\,mol^{-1}}$ and $\mathrm{Pa\,m^6\,mol^{-2}}$, respectively.

<Johannes Diderik van der Waals> introduced this <equation of state> in 1873, connecting molecular attraction with departures from the dilute <ideal gas law>. Its historical significance is the unified treatment of <gases> and <liquids>, rather than a universally exact fit.<Johannes Diderik van der Waals, Facts by Nobel Prize Outreach>[Nobel Prize Outreach, Johannes Diderik van der Waals, Work: equation introduced in 1873 and molecular attraction]{ref}

= Van der Waals gas
{parent=Van der Waals equation}
{c}

A mean-field fluid model with excluded-volume and attractive-interaction parameters, described by the <Van der Waals equation>. Its pressure law alone does not fix every thermal property: the <caloric closure of the van der Waals equation> supplies the additional internal-energy assumption.

= Isothermal free-energy change of a Van der Waals gas
{parent=Van der Waals gas}
{title2=$\Delta F=-RT\ln[(V_2-b)/(V_1-b)]+a(1/V_1-1/V_2)$}

For the one-mole <Van der Waals equation> with constant parameters, integrate $dF=-p\,dV$ along an isotherm to obtain the displayed difference. The attractive contribution has a positive change on expansion, whereas the translational term lowers free <energy>. Thermodynamic consistency independently gives $\Delta U=a(1/V_1-1/V_2)$ and $\Delta S=R\ln[(V_2-b)/(V_1-b)]$. These homogeneous-branch expressions are not a substitute for equilibrium convexification through a subcritical coexistence region.

= Caloric closure of the van der Waals equation
{parent=Van der Waals equation}

The thermodynamic identity $(\partial U/\partial V)_T=T(\partial P/\partial T)_V-P$ fixes the volume-dependent term $-aN^2/V$ but leaves an arbitrary function of <temperature>. Classical monatomic mean-field modelling adds $3Nk_BT/2$, giving $U=3Nk_BT/2-aN^2/V+C$.

= Internal energy of a van der Waals gas
{parent=Van der Waals equation}
{title2=$U(V,T)=h(T)-a/V$}

For fixed <amount of substance>, thermodynamic consistency gives
$$
\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T
=T\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V-P.
$$
Indeed, the <first law of thermodynamics> gives $U_V|_T=T S_V|_T-P$, and the <Helmholtz free energy> <Maxwell relation> gives $S_V|_T=P_T|_V$. For the fixed-amount convention $P=RT/(V-b)-a/V^2$ with temperature-independent $a,b$, substitution gives $U_V|_T=a/V^2$. Integration yields $U=h(T)-a/V$. If the dilute-limit reference is $U\to cT$ as $V\to\infty$ for every $T$, then $h(T)=cT$.

The same formula may be interpreted molarly if $V,U,a,b$ all use that convention. Mixing molar and total quantities changes the amount factors. The attraction controls the volume correction to <internal energy>, while excluded volume alone does not contribute to this derivative when $a,b$ are independent of temperature.

= Entropy excess of a van der Waals gas
{parent=Van der Waals equation}
{title2=$\Delta S^{\mathrm{ex}}=R\ln(1-b/V)$}

For one mole with temperature-independent parameters $a,b$, the <equation of state> is $P=RT/(V-b)-a/V^2$. A <Maxwell relation> gives $(\partial S/\partial V)_T=R/(V-b)$, so the volume dependence is $R\ln(V-b)$. Matching the <ideal gas> entropy at infinite volume fixes the excess at the same $V,T$ to $R\ln(1-b/V)$. The attraction contributes $-a/V$ to the <Helmholtz free energy> but no excess entropy when $a$ is independent of temperature. A temperature-dependent attraction parameter would change this conclusion.

= Van der Waals molar equation
{c}
{parent=Van der Waals equation}
{title2=$p=RT/(v_m-b_m)-a_m/v_m^2$}

The <molar volume> $v_m=V/n_{\rm mol}$ is the <volume (thermodynamics)> per <amount of substance>, not the total container <volume (thermodynamics)> or the <volume per particle>. In the molar convention of the <Van der Waals equation>, take constant positive parameters
$$
[a_m]=\mathrm{Pa\,m^6\,mol^{-2}},\qquad
[b_m]=\mathrm{m^3\,mol^{-1}},\qquad
[R]=\mathrm{Pa\,m^3\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}.
$$
Then
$$
p=\frac{RT}{v_m-b_m}-\frac{a_m}{v_m^2},\qquad v_m>b_m,
$$
or, for any fixed <amount of substance>,
$$
\left(p+\frac{a_m n_{\rm mol}^2}{V^2}\right)
(V-n_{\rm mol}b_m)=n_{\rm mol}RT.
$$
Both terms in the first equation are <pressures>. The total excluded <volume (thermodynamics)> is $n_{\rm mol}b_m$: using $V-b_m$ would subtract quantities with different units. Changing the sample size at fixed <molar volume> and <temperature> changes $V$ and $n_{\rm mol}$ proportionally, while leaving <pressure> unchanged. This is a useful unit and extensivity check before fitting a <real gas>.

For the molar <Van der Waals critical point>, differentiate with respect to <molar volume> at fixed <temperature>:
$$
\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial v_m}\right)_T
=-\frac{RT}{(v_m-b_m)^2}+\frac{2a_m}{v_m^3},\qquad
\left(\frac{\partial^2 p}{\partial v_m^2}\right)_T
=\frac{2RT}{(v_m-b_m)^3}-\frac{6a_m}{v_m^4}.
$$
Setting both to zero gives $RT_c=2a_m(v_{m,c}-b_m)^2/v_{m,c}^3$ and $RT_c=3a_m(v_{m,c}-b_m)^3/v_{m,c}^4$. Equating them and dividing by the nonzero factors yields $2v_{m,c}=3(v_{m,c}-b_m)$, so
$$
v_{m,c}=3b_m,\qquad
T_c=\frac{8a_m}{27Rb_m},\qquad
p_c=\frac{a_m}{27b_m^2}.
$$
Here $v_{m,c}$ has units $\mathrm{m^3\,mol^{-1}}$, $T_c$ has units $\mathrm K$, and $p_c$ has units $\mathrm{Pa}$. This is the molar version of the particle derivation below: $v_{m,c}=N_Av_c$, with the same physical $T_c$ and $p_c$. If one writes $v_c=3b$, $T_c=8a/(27Rb)$ and $p_c=a/(27b^2)$ without subscripts, then all of $v_c,b,a$ in those formulas must be molar quantities.

Now define the dimensionless reduced variables explicitly as $x=v_m/v_{m,c}$, $t=T/T_c$ and $q=p/p_c$. Substituting the three critical values cancels $a_m,b_m,R$ and gives
$$
q=\frac{8t}{3x-1}-\frac{3}{x^2}.
$$
Because $v_m/v_{m,c}=v/v_c$, these are exactly the reduced variables in the <Van der Waals isotherm> discussion. The <Van der Waals law of corresponding states> is therefore independent of whether one starts with particle or molar units. It predicts a critical ratio $p_cv_{m,c}/(RT_c)=3/8$, providing a third check on any proposed parameter fit.

= Molar Van der Waals worked example
{c}
{parent=Van der Waals molar equation}

Choose a hypothetical <fluid> with molar constants $a_m=0.360\,\mathrm{Pa\,m^6\,mol^{-2}}$ and $b_m=4.00\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^3\,mol^{-1}}$, using $R=8.314462618\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$. These stipulated constants are not a fit to <carbon dioxide>. The molar critical formulas give
$$
v_{m,c}=120.0\,\mathrm{cm^3\,mol^{-1}},\qquad
T_c\simeq320.7263\,\mathrm K,\qquad
p_c=8.333333\,\mathrm{MPa}.
$$
At $T=360\,\mathrm K$ and $v_m=240\,\mathrm{cm^3\,mol^{-1}}$, first convert $v_m$ to $2.40\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^3\,mol^{-1}}$. The repulsive-volume term and attractive correction are
$$
\frac{RT}{v_m-b_m}=14.966033\,\mathrm{MPa},\qquad
\frac{a_m}{v_m^2}=6.250000\,\mathrm{MPa},
$$
so $p=8.716033\,\mathrm{MPa}$. The <ideal gas law> would instead give $RT/v_m=12.471694\,\mathrm{MPa}$. The <excluded volume> raises <pressure> relative to that ideal value, but attraction more than offsets the increase here. Neither correction alone describes this example.

For $n_{\rm mol}=0.500\,\mathrm{mol}$, the same state occupies $V=n_{\rm mol}v_m=120\,\mathrm{cm^3}$. The total-volume formula gives $n_{\rm mol}b_m=20\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ and the same $8.716033\,\mathrm{MPa}$. Halving the sample has halved both its physical <volume (thermodynamics)> and its excluded <volume (thermodynamics)>, while retaining its <pressure>. Conversely, if the two critical measurements $T_c,p_c$ are used to determine model constants, inversion gives
$$
b_m=\frac{RT_c}{8p_c},\qquad
a_m=\frac{27R^2T_c^2}{64p_c}.
$$
An independent critical <molar volume> then tests the prediction $v_{m,c}=3RT_c/(8p_c)$; agreement with two fitted numbers does not ensure agreement with the third.

At $t=0.9$, the <Van der Waals liquid-gas coexistence> example below becomes $T\simeq288.6537\,\mathrm K$, $p_*\simeq5.391653\,\mathrm{MPa}$, $v_{m,l}\simeq72.40823\,\mathrm{cm^3\,mol^{-1}}$ and $v_{m,g}\simeq281.8611\,\mathrm{cm^3\,mol^{-1}}$. For a mean <molar volume> of $120\,\mathrm{cm^3\,mol^{-1}}$, the <lever rule> gives gas <mole fraction> $f_g\simeq0.227219$. Its <volume fraction> is instead $f_gv_{m,g}/v_m\simeq0.533703$: less than one quarter of the molecules occupy more than half the <volume (thermodynamics)>. The flat equilibrium <pressure> corresponds to changing these proportions. It does not describe a homogeneous sample sliding along the unstable loop.

= Van der Waals law of corresponding states
{c}
{parent=Van der Waals equation}

The <Van der Waals equation> becomes independent of its material parameters when <pressure>, <temperature> and molecular <volume> are divided by their critical values: $\bar p=8\bar T/(3\bar v-1)-3/\bar v^2$. This explains the common reduced isotherms within this model, without claiming exact agreement for all real fluids.

= Van der Waals isotherm
{c}
{parent=Van der Waals equation}
{title2=$p(v,T)$}

A <Van der Waals isotherm> is the <pressure>–particle <volume (thermodynamics)> curve at a fixed <temperature>. Put $x=v/v_c$, $t=T/T_c$, and $q=p/p_c$, using the <Van der Waals critical point> values below. Then the existing <Van der Waals law of corresponding states> becomes
$$
q(x,t)=\frac{8t}{3x-1}-\frac{3}{x^2},\qquad x>\frac13.
$$

For a uniform <thermodynamic phase>, the <isothermal compressibility> is
$$
\kappa_T=-\frac1v\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial p}\right)_T
=-\frac{1}{v(\partial p/\partial v)_T}.
$$
Thus the upward-sloping portion of a subcritical <Van der Waals isotherm> has $\kappa_T<0$: compression would reduce <pressure>, allowing a density disturbance to grow. It is mechanically unstable. The <spinodal points> bound that portion; setting the <derivative> $\partial q/\partial x$ to zero gives their curve $t=(3x-1)^2/(4x^3)$. Negative slope alone guarantees only local mechanical stability: portions between the <spinodal> and the <phase coexistence> endpoints have <metastability>. = Van der Waals critical point
{c}
{parent=Van der Waals equation}
{title2=$(v_c,T_c,p_c)$}

The <liquid-gas critical point> of the <Van der Waals equation> occurs where its two subcritical stationary points merge into a horizontal <inflection point>. The conditions are $(\partial p/\partial v)_T=(\partial^2p/\partial v^2)_T=0$. The short calculation is
$$
-\frac{k_BT_c}{(v_c-b)^2}+\frac{2a}{v_c^3}=0,\qquad
\frac{2k_BT_c}{(v_c-b)^3}-\frac{6a}{v_c^4}=0.
$$
Dividing these relations gives $2/(v_c-b)=3/v_c$, hence
$$
v_c=3b,\qquad T_c=\frac{8a}{27bk_B},\qquad p_c=\frac{a}{27b^2}.
$$
Substitution gives the <Van der Waals law of corresponding states> and the parameter-independent ratio $p_cv_c/(k_BT_c)=3/8$. That ratio belongs to this <equation of state>; reducing a real <fluid>'s variables does not enforce it.<Statistical Physics by David Tong>[§5.1.3, equations 5.7–5.8: critical values, reduced equation and compressibility ratio]{ref}

An exact rearrangement of the reduced critical <Van der Waals isotherm> makes its flatness visible:
$$
q(x,1)-1=-\frac{3(x-1)^3}{x^2(3x-1)}.
$$
The first nonzero departure near $x=1$ is $-3(x-1)^3/2$. On the critical particle <volume (thermodynamics)> approached from $t>1$, $(\partial q/\partial x)_{x=1}=-6(t-1)$ and
$$
\kappa_T=\frac{1}{6p_c(t-1)}.
$$
The divergent <isothermal compressibility> and cubic flattening explain why the critical region is mechanically soft. These are <mean-field theory> predictions; <thermal fluctuations> limit their use for real <fluids> very close to a <thermodynamic critical point>.

= Critical-isochore compressibility of a van der Waals gas
{parent=Van der Waals critical point}
{title2=$\kappa_T=4b/[3R(T-T_c)]$}

For molar equation $p=RT/(v-b)-a/v^2$, the critical values are $v_c=3b$ and $T_c=8a/(27Rb)$. At $v=v_c$, $(\partial p/\partial v)_T=-R(T-T_c)/(4b^2)$. Thus $\kappa_T=-v_c^{-1}(\partial v/\partial p)_T=4b/[3R(T-T_c)]$ on the stable branch above $T_c$. The mean-field compressibility exponent is one; the continuation below the <critical temperature> is unstable rather than an equilibrium negative compressibility.

= Van der Waals liquid-gas coexistence
{c}
{parent=Van der Waals equation}

Below $T_c$, the equilibrium <liquid> and <gas> have the same <temperature>, <pressure> $p_*$, and per-particle <chemical potential>. For macroscopic bulk <thermodynamic phases>, neglect interface contributions. The <Maxwell equal-area construction> determines $v_l$ and $v_g$ by
$$
p(v_l,T)=p(v_g,T)=p_*,\qquad
\int_{v_l}^{v_g}[p(v,T)-p_*]\,dv=0.
$$
It expresses equality of <chemical potentials>, since $d\mu=v\,dp$ at fixed <temperature>. Equivalently, the equilibrium <Helmholtz free energy> per particle follows a common <tangent line> between the two endpoint states, lying below the homogeneous continuation between them. The construction uses that mathematical continuation to compare endpoints; it is not a physical compression path through negative <isothermal compressibility> states.<Statistical Physics by David Tong>[§5.1.1, equation 5.5, Maxwell Construction and figures 36–37: chemical-potential equality and flat coexistence intervals]{ref}

Here is a reproducible calculated example at $t=0.9$. With $q_*=p_*/p_c$, the homogeneous intersections solve the <cubic equation>
$$
3q_*x^3-(q_*+8t)x^2+9x-3=0.
$$
The <Maxwell construction> gives
$$
q_*\simeq0.646998352,\qquad
(x_l,x_m,x_g)\simeq(0.603401903,\ 1.090526633,\ 2.348842376).
$$
All three <roots of a polynomial> satisfy $x>1/3$. Only the smallest and largest are the <phase coexistence> endpoints; the middle one has negative <isothermal compressibility>. Equal <pressures> at three admissible values of <volume (thermodynamics)> therefore do not imply three stable <thermodynamic phases>. An analytic area check is
$$
\frac{8t}{3}\ln\frac{3x_g-1}{3x_l-1}
+3\left(\frac1{x_g}-\frac1{x_l}\right)-q_*(x_g-x_l)=0.
$$
At full numerical precision, the residual is below $10^{-11}$ in reduced units; independent numerical integration gives the same cancellation. At $t=0.9$, the <spinodal points> are instead $x\simeq0.718597189$ and $1.528504964$.

The equilibrium plateau changes the <phase fraction> rather than the endpoint densities. For a gas particle fraction $f_g=N_g/N$ and mean particle <volume (thermodynamics)> $\bar v=V/N$, the <lever rule> reads
$$
\bar v=(1-f_g)v_l+f_gv_g,\qquad
f_g=\frac{\bar v-v_l}{v_g-v_l}.
$$
Thus $\bar v=v_c$ at $t=0.9$ gives $f_g\simeq0.227219$: this is a particle fraction, not the <volume fraction> occupied by <gas>. Differentiating equality of <chemical potentials> along this model's <phase coexistence curve> yields the same <Clapeyron equation> explained below, $dp_*/dT=\ell/[T(v_g-v_l)]$, with <latent heat> $\ell$ per particle. Both endpoint differences vanish as the <Van der Waals critical point> is approached; the equation's critical limit must be taken as a ratio.

The <Maxwell construction> presumes a pure bulk <fluid> at <thermodynamic equilibrium>. Its endpoint equality does not specify how rapidly a real compression reaches equilibrium. A homogeneous <metastable> sample can persist while <nucleation> is delayed; the <spinodal> marks loss of local mechanical stability in this <mean-field theory>, rather than the equilibrium onset of condensation. <Surface tension>, finite sample size and impurities can affect the observed transition. These qualifications matter when comparing a mathematical <Van der Waals isotherm> with a visible <liquid meniscus>.

= Low-temperature liquid volume of a van der Waals gas
{parent=Van der Waals liquid-gas coexistence}
{title2=$V_l\sim b+RTb^2/a$}

For one mole, liquid–vapour <phase coexistence> requires equal <pressure> and <chemical potential>. At $\tau=RTb/a\ll1$, the saturation pressure is exponentially small. Neglecting it in the dense-phase <equation of state> gives $RTV_l^2-aV_l+ab=0$. The root near the excluded volume is
$$
V_l\simeq\frac{a}{2RT}\left(1-\sqrt{1-4RTb/a}\right)
=\frac{2b}{1+\sqrt{1-4\tau}}
=b(1+\tau+2\tau^2+\cdots).
$$
This is an asymptotic low-temperature result, not an exact coexistence volume at every subcritical temperature. Away from that limit, solve equal pressure together with the <Maxwell equal-area construction>.

A steel screw pressurized <water> in a strong <copper> tube; movable <mercury (element)> transmitted the load. A second <capillary tube> held air as a <pressure> gauge. Flowing <water> around the visible <capillary tubes> controlled <temperature>; a <thermometer> read the bath. A <pressure measurement> determines <pressure> relative to a specified reference using an instrument such as a <manometer> or <pressure transducer>. <Gauge pressure>, <absolute pressure> and differential <pressure> require different references; <measurement calibration> and <measurement uncertainty> determine how the reading can be interpreted.

= High-pressure experimental metrology
{parent=Pressure measurement}

High-pressure experimental <metrology> connects three tasks: producing <pressure>, retaining it without leakage, and assigning a calibrated value to the <pressure> experienced by a sample. A large applied <force> alone establishes none of the last two. The useful result is a reproducible material property at specified <pressure>, <temperature> and <stress> conditions, with stated <measurement uncertainty>.

<Percy Williams Bridgman> identified leakage as an earlier experimental limit. His 1946 lecture describes packing that tightens as <pressure> rises, ultimately leaving vessel strength as a limit.<General Survey of Certain Results in the Field of High-Pressure Physics by Percy Williams Bridgman>[General survey of certain results in the field of high-pressure physics, delivered 11 December 1946, p. 53 opening and Figure 1 caption]{ref}

The <Bridgman unsupported-area seal> turns the very <pressure> that would drive a leak into an extra seating <force>. Its appeal is a feedback between load and sealing, rather than an assumption of perfectly fitted rigid surfaces. The <piston-cylinder pressure apparatus> below explains that feedback; <pressure calibration> then separates the applied load from the sample's inferred <pressure>.

= Piston-cylinder pressure apparatus
{parent=High-pressure experimental metrology}
{title2=$p-p_{\rm ext}\simeq F/A$}

In a functional model, a driven <piston> reduces the chamber's <volume (thermodynamics)>. A surrounding cylinder carries the wall <stress>; a deformable packing or gasket closes a potential leak path around a moving or removable closure. A sample and a separate <pressure sensor> occupy the chamber. A transmitting <liquid> can load the sample on all sides; the sensor's signal passes to an external readout. This is a division of experimental functions, not a scale reconstruction of one historical machine.

For a slowly moving frictionless drive <piston> of effective <surface area> $A$, external <pressure> $p_{\rm ext}$ and inward applied <force> $F$, <Newton's second law> at mechanical balance gives
$$
F+p_{\rm ext}A-pA=0,\qquad p-p_{\rm ext}=F/A.
$$
Thus $20\,\mathrm{kN}$ on a stipulated $20\,\mathrm{mm^2}$ effective <surface area> corresponds ideally to $1\,\mathrm{GPa}$ of differential <pressure>. These are illustrative numbers, not apparatus specifications. The effective <surface area> is the one associated with the <pressure> difference, not necessarily an external ram's visible cross section. With resisting <friction> $F_f$ during inward motion, the balance becomes $p-p_{\rm ext}=(F-F_f)/A$. During outward motion the sign of the <friction> contribution reverses. An applied-load reading can therefore show <hysteresis> even when the chamber's actual <pressure> is well defined.

A <resistance pressure gauge> instead supplies an internal signal. In a generic electrical readout, measure an <electric potential difference> $V_e$ with a known <electric current> $I$ and use <Ohm's law> to infer <electrical resistance> $R=V_e/I$. This describes a possible readout principle; the lecture passage cited below identifies the sensor material and calibration landmarks without specifying this wiring.

= Unsupported-area seal
{synonym}

Consider an ideal pressure-driven closure plug with a large chamber-facing <projected area> $A_p$. Its opposite side seats against a smaller annular packing <surface area> $A_{\rm seal}$. The remaining projected part $A_u=A_p-A_{\rm seal}>0$ is the unsupported area: it has no opposing packing contact in that plane. Here $p$ is the chamber <pressure> relative to the exterior. Let $q$ denote the mean axial compressive contact <stress> on the packing, not the chamber's <hydrostatic stress>.

{title=<Bridgman unsupported-area seal>: unequal <surface areas> make the packing load itself}

The drive <piston> that pressurizes the chamber is a separate experimental function.}

Neglect <friction>, the plug's <weight>, and other axial reactions. The plug's <Newton's second law> in static balance is
$$
pA_p-qA_{\rm seal}=0,\qquad
q=p\frac{A_p}{A_{\rm seal}}
=p\left(1+\frac{A_u}{A_{\rm seal}}\right)>p.
$$
The load is the same on both sides; its smaller supporting <surface area> raises the packing's mean contact <stress>. For a stipulated ratio $A_p/A_{\rm seal}=1.25$ and $p=1\,\mathrm{GPa}$, the ideal seating <stress> is $q=1.25\,\mathrm{GPa}$. Its excess over chamber <pressure> is $0.25\,\mathrm{GPa}$. Reducing $A_u$ to zero removes this amplification. This is the elementary <force> balance behind self-energizing seating, consistent with the lecture's Figure 1 caption.<General Survey of Certain Results in the Field of High-Pressure Physics by Percy Williams Bridgman>[p. 53, Figure 1 caption: packing compression maintained above liquid pressure; the formula here is an idealized derivation]{ref}

The relation is a model, not a universal sealing law. The mean axial contact <stress> need not equal the local contact <stress> along every possible leakage path. Confinement and <plasticity in continuum mechanics> determine whether the packing closes a gap or instead extrudes into it. <Friction> diverts load into other surfaces; material <plasticity in continuum mechanics> changes contact geometry; the vessel must carry its own <stress>. A finite initial seating load may also be needed before chamber <pressure> can develop. An arbitrarily small $A_{\rm seal}$ does not yield arbitrarily useful sealing: the assumptions fail when local deformation, extrusion or structural failure takes over.

= Hydrostatic stress
{parent=High-pressure experimental metrology}
{title2=$\boldsymbol{\sigma}=-p\mathbf I$}

With tension taken positive, <hydrostatic stress> is the <stress tensor> $\boldsymbol{\sigma}=-p\mathbf I$, where $\mathbf I$ is the <identity matrix>. Every plane has the same compressive normal <stress>, and no tangential traction. More generally,
$$
p=-\frac13\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol{\sigma},\qquad
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=-p\mathbf I+\mathbf s,\qquad
\operatorname{tr}\mathbf s=0,
$$
where $\mathbf s$ is <deviatoric stress>. Two samples can share the same mean <pressure> while experiencing different <deviatoric stresses>. A stationary <liquid> cannot sustain equilibrium shear <stress>; a <solid> transmitting medium can support it. Consequently, a load divided by <surface area> does not by itself establish hydrostatic sample conditions. A transmitting medium's state at the actual <pressure> and <temperature>, and contact between sample and walls, matter.

Bridgman's Figure 4 compares mild <steel> fractured in tension at <atmospheric pressure> with <steel> stretched further in a <liquid> at $25000\,\mathrm{kgf\,cm^{-2}}$.<General Survey of Certain Results in the Field of High-Pressure Physics by Percy Williams Bridgman>[p. 56, Figure 4 caption: atmospheric-pressure tensile fracture versus greater reduction of area in pressurized liquid; historical kg/cm² rendered explicitly as force per area]{ref} The imposed tension remains a directional load: the experiment combines confinement with deformation, rather than measuring a <hydrostatic stress> alone. Its comparison motivates recording the loading path and the surrounding medium as well as <pressure>; it does not establish a universal fracture threshold for all <steels>.

= Pressure calibration
{parent=High-pressure experimental metrology}
{title2=$p=g(R,T,\text{history})$}

<Pressure calibration> assigns <pressure> values to a sensor's output by comparison with a reference under specified conditions. It is not the same as making the output more finely resolved. A smooth <electrical resistance> curve can be repeatable yet have an incorrect <pressure> scale.

Bridgman used pressure-dependent <electrical resistance> of <manganin> and established fixed points, including a <bismuth> transition. He reported poorer accuracy above the better calibrated range.<General Survey of Certain Results in the Field of High-Pressure Physics by Percy Williams Bridgman>[p. 56, pressure-measurement paragraph: manganin gauge, bismuth fixed point, and different accuracy estimates below and above 30000 kg/cm²]{ref}

A fixed-point specimen and a property-measurement sample have distinct roles: one helps assign the scale, while the other is studied on that scale. The reference transition must itself have an assigned <pressure> at the relevant <temperature>. Agreement at a single point does not determine a nonlinear sensor response elsewhere. Comparing increasing and decreasing <pressure> checks <hysteresis>; returning to a reference checks drift. These are checks one can design from the measurement model, not additional historical controls asserted for the lecture's experiments.

= Resistance pressure gauge
{parent=Pressure calibration}
{title2=$R=R(p,T,\text{history})$}

A <resistance pressure gauge> is a <pressure sensor> whose <electrical resistance> is calibrated against <pressure>. Even a simple local response needs both <pressure> and <temperature>. For an original illustrative model define
$$
y=\frac{R}{R_0}-1=\alpha p+\beta(T-T_0)+h,\qquad
p=\frac{y-\beta(T-T_0)-h}{\alpha},
$$
where $R_0$ is the reference <electrical resistance> at $p=0$, $T=T_0$, and zero history offset. The <derivatives> $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are local sensitivities with units $\mathrm{Pa^{-1}}$ and $\mathrm{K^{-1}}$, respectively; $h$ is a dimensionless history offset. Constant sensitivities are an approximation over a chosen range, not material constants valid at every <pressure>.

Choose synthetic coefficients $\alpha=0.020\,\mathrm{GPa^{-1}}$ and $\beta=2.0\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$, with $T-T_0=10\,\mathrm K$ and $h=0$. A measured ratio $R/R_0=1.022$ then gives $p=(0.022-0.002)/0.020=1.00\,\mathrm{GPa}$. Ignoring the <temperature> change gives $1.10\,\mathrm{GPa}$, a $0.10\,\mathrm{GPa}$ error. This model is deliberately stipulated; these numbers are not measured <manganin> coefficients or Bridgman's data.

If two histories differ by $\Delta h=0.0004$, their outputs at the same <pressure> differ by $0.0004R_0$; interpreting both with a single history-free curve gives an apparent <pressure> separation $\Delta h/\alpha=0.020\,\mathrm{GPa}$. $$
For $u_y=10^{-4}$, $u_T=0.5\,\mathrm K$, and $u_h=10^{-4}$, this is $u_p=0.00866\,\mathrm{GPa}$. A sensor calibrates its local environment; it cannot by itself certify the absence of <deviatoric stress> throughout the sample.

= Diamond anvil culet
{parent=Diamond anvil cell}
{title2=$A_c=\pi d_c^2/4$}

A <diamond anvil culet> is the small flat contact face at the tip of a <diamond> anvil. Its diameter $d_c$ determines its nominal <surface area> $A_c$. The <gasket> hole has a different <surface area> $A_h$; neither should be confused with the much larger drive mechanism. For an ideal static axial balance, $F$ is the compressive <force> on either anvil. The two equal opposing loads do not produce $2F$ through a single contact.

For an original chosen geometry, $F=1000\,\mathrm N$ and $d_c=0.300\,\mathrm{mm}$ give
$$
A_c=7.0686\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m^2},\qquad
\bar q=F/A_c=14.147\,\mathrm{GPa}.
$$
Here $\bar q$ is the mean axial contact <stress> over the culet, not a measured chamber <pressure>. Using a $0.100\,\mathrm{mm}$ hole instead would give $F/A_h=127.324\,\mathrm{GPa}$, nine times larger. That substitution falsely assigns the entire applied <force> to the hole.

To see the missing load path, idealize the culet as a coplanar hole region plus a surrounding contact annulus. With a hydrostatic chamber <pressure> $p$ and axial resultant $F_g$ carried by the <gasket>, its <force> balance is
$$
F=pA_h+F_g.
$$
If the stipulated chamber <pressure> is $5.40\,\mathrm{GPa}$, the hole carries only $42.4\,\mathrm N$ and the <gasket> carries $957.6\,\mathrm N$. These are model loads, not measurements. Real <gasket> deformation and anvil <elasticity> alter the local contact <stress>; the scalar balance cannot predict $p$ without additional information. This is why internal <pressure calibration> matters even when applied <force> is accurately known.

= Ruby fluorescence pressure calibration
{synonym}

The <ruby pressure scale> converts a shift of a <ruby> <fluorescence> <spectral line> into <pressure>. Excitation <light> produces a <fluorescence emission spectrum>; fitting its $R_1$ line gives a <wavelength> $\lambda_{R_1}$. The reference $\lambda_{R_1,0}$ must be measured or assigned at specified reference <pressure> and <temperature>. The difference $\Delta\lambda$ is an observable; interpreting it as <pressure> requires a <pressure calibration> and suitable <stress> conditions.

The original 1972 report found sharp $R_1,R_2$ emission shifting toward lower <photon> <energy> as <pressure> increased; it tentatively associated broadening with nonhydrostatic conditions.<Pressure Measurement Made by the Utilization of Ruby Sharp-Line Luminescence by Richard A. Forman and others>[Abstract: direction of R-line shifts and tentative nonhydrostatic interpretation]{ref} In use, excitation rejection, a calibrated <wavelength> axis and a fitted background matter because leakage or background can move an apparent peak. A brighter trace improves counting statistics but does not alone establish a correct <pressure> scale.

= Ruby R-line shift
{parent=Ruby pressure scale}
{title2=$\Delta E=hc\,\Delta(1/\lambda)$}

The <ruby R-line shift> compares the same emission feature under two conditions. It is distinct from the <Stokes shift>, which compares absorption and emission features. In <ruby>, <chromium> <ions> in the <corundum> <crystal> provide the <electronic transitions>; compression changes their local environment and transition <energies>.<Stress Measurements in Alumina by Optical Fluorescence: Revisited by Robert F. Cook and Chris A. Michaels>[Introduction and hydrostatic-pressure discussion: chromium fluorescence in alumina and stress-sensitive R lines]{ref}

For consistently defined <vacuum> <wavelengths>, the reciprocal relation gives
$$
\Delta(1/\lambda)=\frac{1}{\lambda_0+\Delta\lambda}-\frac1{\lambda_0}
=-\frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda_0(\lambda_0+\Delta\lambda)}.
$$
Thus a positive <wavelength> shift means a negative reciprocal shift, and $E=hc/\lambda$ gives lower <photon> <energy>; $h$ is the <Planck constant> and $c$ the <speed of light>. For <spectroscopic wavenumber>, use <vacuum> <wavelength> and keep that convention consistent. A calibration specified in air <wavelength> must retain its convention or be converted consistently. The reciprocal relation is nonlinear: a line exactly linear in $\Delta\lambda$ is not exactly linear in $\Delta(1/\lambda)$.

For a purely mathematical check with chosen <vacuum> <wavelengths> $\lambda_0=700.0\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $\Delta\lambda=2.0\,\mathrm{nm}$, the reciprocal shift is $-40.7000\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. The small-shift approximation $-\Delta\lambda/\lambda_0^2$ gives $-40.8163\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, about $0.286\%$ too large in magnitude. These chosen values illustrate the conversion; they are not measured <ruby> lines or an additional <pressure calibration>.

= Ruby calibration against sodium chloride
{parent=Ruby pressure scale}

The 1975 experiment mixed powdered <sodium chloride> and 0.5%-<chromium> <ruby> in a 4:1 volume ratio. An Inconel <gasket>, initially $0.15\,\mathrm{mm}$ thick with a $0.3\,\mathrm{mm}$ hole, held the powder and 4:1 <methanol>–<ethanol>. At $25\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$, <X-ray diffraction> rings on film $12\,\mathrm{cm}$ away gave salt compression; a reference <equation of state> assigned <pressure>. After decreasing load, they waited at least five hours and bracketed diffraction exposures with $R_1$ readings to check drift.<Calibration of the pressure dependence of the R1 ruby fluorescence line to 195 kbar by Gasper J. Piermarini and others>[pp. 2774–2775, Experimental Procedures]{ref}

The recommended linear slope was $2.740\,\mathrm{GPa\,nm^{-1}}$, with a $95\%$ fit <confidence interval> of $\pm0.016\,\mathrm{GPa\,nm^{-1}}$, to $19.5\,\mathrm{GPa}$. Reference-scale systematic <measurement uncertainty> remained separate. Above about $10.4\,\mathrm{GPa}$ the medium was only quasihydrostatic; separate range fits checked consistency.<Calibration of the pressure dependence of the R1 ruby fluorescence line to 195 kbar by Gasper J. Piermarini and others>[pp. 2775–2776, Table I and recommended calibration; p. 2778, pressure-distribution discussion]{ref}

The logical chain is compression to reference <pressure>, paired with optical shift, then a fitted response. <X-ray diffraction> supplies the reference comparison during calibration; subsequent <ruby> readings transfer that scale to other samples. The reference <equation of state> is therefore part of the meaning of the reported <pressure>. Changing it can move all calibrated readings together, even if the optical measurements repeat perfectly. A <regression residual> tests agreement with the chosen reference within the sampled conditions; it cannot independently certify that reference.

= Ruby pressure uncertainty worked example
{parent=Ruby pressure scale}
{title2=$u_p^2=D^2u_a^2+a^2(u_\lambda^2+u_0^2+k_T^2u_T^2)$}

Construct a synthetic local calibration to show the calculation independently of the historical data. Choose reference pairs $(\Delta\lambda_i,p_i)=(1.00,2.69),(2.00,5.42),(3.00,8.09)$, with shifts in $\mathrm{nm}$ and <pressures> in $\mathrm{GPa}$. The reference zero is already subtracted. Treat the shifts as exact for this fit and give the three reference <pressures> equal weights. Minimizing the <ordinary least squares> objective
$$
S(a)=\sum_i(p_i-a\Delta\lambda_i)^2
$$
and setting its <derivative> to zero gives
$$
a=\frac{\sum_i\Delta\lambda_i p_i}{\sum_i(\Delta\lambda_i)^2}
=\frac{37.80}{14.00}=2.70\,\mathrm{GPa\,nm^{-1}}.
$$
The <regression residuals> are $(-0.01,0.02,-0.01)\,\mathrm{GPa}$. Their weighted moment $\sum_i\Delta\lambda_i(p_i-a\Delta\lambda_i)$ vanishes, as required by the <least-squares normal equations>. An unknown reference offset requires fitting an <intercept> instead; forcing it to zero can bias the slope. If shift errors matter, this elementary fit is insufficient.

\Image[ruby-pressure-calibration-uncertainty.png]
{title=Synthetic <ruby pressure scale>: fitting the response and propagating <measurement uncertainty>}
{description=Original stipulated reference points and calculations, not measured historical calibration. The left panel shows the fitted slope and a chosen reading. The right panel separates independent contributions to <standard uncertainty>; bar lengths are standard contributions, which combine by sums of squares. The assigned slope <standard uncertainty> includes a hypothetical reference-scale contribution and is not estimated from the three residuals.}

Use the local measurement model
$$
p=aD,\qquad D=\lambda-\lambda_0-k_T(T-T_0).
$$
Here $p$ denotes <pressure> relative to the reference, $a$ is calibrated sensitivity, and $k_T$ is a local <wavelength> sensitivity to <temperature> at fixed <pressure>. Choose $a=2.70\,\mathrm{GPa\,nm^{-1}}$, $k_T=0.010\,\mathrm{nm\,K^{-1}}$ and a reading $\lambda-\lambda_0=2.00\,\mathrm{nm}$ at $T=T_0$. The inferred <pressure> is $5.40\,\mathrm{GPa}$. These coefficients and readings are stipulated, not measured <ruby> constants. With the same <pressure>, a $10\,\mathrm K$ increase in <temperature> would add $0.100\,\mathrm{nm}$ to the shift in this model; neglecting it would overestimate <pressure> by $0.270\,\mathrm{GPa}$.

Assign independent <standard uncertainties> $u_a=0.020\,\mathrm{GPa\,nm^{-1}}$, $u_\lambda=u_0=0.010\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $u_T=0.50\,\mathrm K$ for the <temperature> difference. Here $u_a$ is an independently assigned calibration uncertainty, not an estimate from the <regression residuals> above; treat $k_T$ as exact in this example. First-order <propagation of uncertainty> follows by differentiating the measurement model:
$$
dp=D\,da+a\,d\lambda-a\,d\lambda_0-a k_T\,d(T-T_0),
$$
$$
u_p^2=D^2u_a^2+a^2\left(u_\lambda^2+u_0^2+k_T^2u_T^2\right).
$$
The four standard contributions are $0.0400,0.0270,0.0270,0.0135\,\mathrm{GPa}$, giving $u_p=0.0569\,\mathrm{GPa}$. This is a <standard uncertainty>, not automatically a $95\%$ interval. Correlated reference and loaded <wavelength> readings require the corresponding <covariance> term; common wavelength-axis drift may cancel in their difference. Uncertain $k_T$, nonzero $T-T_0$, model inadequacy and unresolved <deviatoric stress> require a broader budget. Taking more optical readings reduces some random errors while leaving a common reference-scale error.

= Nonhydrostatic stress in ruby pressure measurement
{parent=Ruby pressure scale}

A single optical shift represents <pressure> only to the extent that the calibration's mechanical and thermal conditions apply. Under <hydrostatic stress>, all directions have the same compressive normal <stress>. Under <deviatoric stress>, differently oriented <ruby> grains can respond differently; spatially varying <pressure> can also superpose shifted lines. These mechanisms can produce different optical signals at equal mean <pressure>.<Stress Measurements in Alumina by Optical Fluorescence: Revisited by Robert F. Cook and Chris A. Michaels>[Sections 1–2: stress-sensitive fluorescence shifts and crystallographic stress components]{ref}

The 2001 account describes scanning local line positions and measuring line width over an extended region. The former resolves spatial differences; the latter supplies a rapid warning.<High Pressure X-Ray Crystallography With the Diamond Cell at NIST⁄NBS by Gasper J. Piermarini>[Section 4.2, Methods I and II]{ref}

Neither a broadened line nor a narrow one uniquely determines the <stress tensor>. Instrument resolution, <temperature> variation and unresolved overlapping signals can also affect width. A small observed region can have a narrow line while differing from another region. Compare locations, reference traces and instrument settings before treating width as evidence for a particular mechanical explanation. These are proposed diagnostic checks, not additional historical controls attributed to the 1975 experiment.

<Temperature> is another independent input. A useful local expansion is $\Delta\lambda\simeq s_p p+s_T(T-T_0)$: one measured shift cannot determine both unknowns. Measure or control <temperature>, or supply a second independently calibrated observable. The coefficients need not remain constant across a large range. Even when a pressure sensitivity transfers over some <temperatures>, the reference line, line width and practical readout quality can change. 

The historical high-temperature account reports degrading <pressure measurement> quality above about $300\,{}^\circ\mathrm C$.<High Pressure X-Ray Crystallography With the Diamond Cell at NIST⁄NBS by Gasper J. Piermarini>[Section 4.1, high-temperature cell and readout-quality discussion]{ref}
