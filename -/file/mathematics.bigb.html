= Mathematics
{wiki}

Mathematics studies patterns in quantity, structure, space, and change by giving objects precise <mathematical definitions> and examining what follows from them. <Arithmetic> starts with operations on <numbers>; <algebra> organizes relations and transformations; <geometry and topology> studies shapes and the structures preserved by maps; <analysis> studies <limits> and continuous change. These fields connect because the same structure can appear in different forms.

A small example shows the value of changing viewpoint. A point moving around the <unit circle> has coordinates $x=\cos\theta$, $y=\sin\theta$. Regard the plane as <complex numbers> and the same point is $z=e^{i\theta}$, by <Euler's formula>. Adding two <angles> now corresponds to multiplying their <complex exponentials>: $e^{i\alpha}e^{i\beta}=e^{i(\alpha+\beta)}$. A geometric rotation has become an algebraic operation.

The connection reaches <calculus> too. Differentiating twice gives $x''+x=0$ and $y''+y=0$, so the two coordinates solve the <harmonic oscillator equation>. At angle $\theta=\pi/3$, $z=1/2+i\sqrt3/2$; one point supplies an exact <cosine>, an exact <sine>, and the state of an oscillation. The <Pythagorean trigonometric identity> records that the <modulus> stays one. Seeing these as descriptions of one object makes each formula easier to remember and reuse.

\Image[circle-exponential-oscillator.png]
{title=Uniform motion around the unit circle connects complex exponentials and real oscillations}

This is why a useful mathematical explanation provides both a reliable <mathematical proof> and a way to see what the result is doing. In his 1994 essay, <William Thurston> discusses mathematical progress in terms of advancing understanding, and uses the many interpretations of a <derivative> to illustrate this aim. https://arxiv.org/abs/math/9404236[Thurston, On proof and progress in mathematics, sections 1–2]{ref} A diagram, a computed example, and a concise argument can make different parts of the same idea accessible.

Applications ask us to select a <mathematical model> of a physical or other system. Its assumptions determine which conclusions transfer to that system. Within mathematics, the same practice of making assumptions explicit lets a result about <groups>, <vector spaces>, or <probability distributions> apply to many concrete examples. The beauty is the combination of precision and reuse: an argument can reveal more than the particular case that first suggested it.

= Postfix notation
{synonym}

Place each operator after its operands. A binary application encodes its first subtree, then its second subtree, then the operator, a <postorder traversal>. A <stack machine> evaluates the tokens in one pass, popping the right operand before the left one. Reversing a <Polish notation> token list also reverses operand order: prefix subtraction `- a b` would become `b a -` rather than the required `a b -`.

= Prefix notation

At any price vector, the total value of aggregate excess demand is zero when consumers exhaust their budgets. After normalization to a price <simplex>, choosing a price that maximizes the value of a candidate excess-demand vector is a useful <Kakutani fixed-point theorem> construction.

= Exchange economy
{parent=General equilibrium theory}
{wiki}

An <exchange economy> specifies consumers, their <preference relations>, their consumption sets and their initial endowments. A <consumption allocation> is feasible when aggregate consumption equals aggregate endowment; free-disposal models instead allow a coordinatewise inequality. These conventions must be distinguished when specifying supporting prices.

= Regular economy
{synonym}

An <exchange economy> is regular when the derivative of its reduced <aggregate excess demand> has full rank at every equilibrium. For two goods and normalized prices $(p,1)$, this means $Z_p(p)\ne0$ at every zero of first-good excess demand. Regular equilibria are locally isolated and persist smoothly under small parameter changes.

= Parametric regularity of exchange equilibria
{parent=Regular exchange economy}
{title2=$D_{(p,t)}Z\ne0\text{ on }Z^{-1}(0)$}

If zero is a <regular value> of the joint smooth <aggregate excess demand> $Z(p,t)$, almost every parameter gives a <regular economy>. The <regular level set theorem> makes the zero set a manifold. Its projection to parameter space fails to have full rank exactly where the derivative with respect to prices is singular; <Sard theorem> makes these exceptional parameter values a <null set>.

= Aggregate excess demand
{parent=Exchange economy}
{title2=$z(p)=\sum_i x_i(p,p\cdot\omega_i)-\sum_i\omega_i$}

<Aggregate excess demand> subtracts total endowment from the sum of consumers' <demand correspondences>. A market-clearing price satisfies $z(p)=0$. Under <Walras law>, one equation is redundant after price normalization.

= Price quasi-equilibrium
{synonym}

A <price quasi-equilibrium with transfers> is an exactly feasible <consumption allocation> $f$ and a nonzero price vector $p$ for which, almost everywhere, every strictly preferred bundle has cost at least $p\cdot f(a)$. The transfer-adjusted wealth is $p\cdot f(a)$; transfers $p\cdot(f(a)-\omega(a))$ sum to zero. Equal-cost strict improvements may remain, which distinguishes a quasi-equilibrium from a utility-maximizing equilibrium. Nonnegative prices need extra assumptions if goods may be undesirable.

= Atomless support of a Pareto allocation
{parent=Price quasi-equilibrium with transfers}

For an <atomless measure> of consumers with measurable, complete, <transitive> and continuous <preference relations>, every exactly feasible <Pareto efficient> <consumption allocation> admits nonzero quasi-equilibrium supporting prices. Countably many rational strict-improvement directions remove all exceptional null sets simultaneously. If their <convex hull> contained zero, sufficiently small disjoint cohorts could make those improvements in exactly balancing proportions, violating <Pareto efficiency>. A <supporting hyperplane theorem> then supplies the prices. Individual <convexity> of preferences is unnecessary.

= Demand correspondence
{parent=Exchange economy}
{title2=$x(p,w)$}

A <demand correspondence> assigns a nonempty set of chosen consumption bundles to each price and wealth pair. Every chosen bundle satisfies the <budget constraint>. A <utility function> rationalizes the <demand correspondence> when the chosen bundles are exactly its maximizers over the budget set.

= Revealed preference
{parent=Demand correspondence}
{wiki}

<Revealed preference> infers rankings from choices: a chosen bundle is directly revealed at least as desirable as any bundle affordable in the same budget. Indirect comparisons use chains of such observations.

= Indirect revealed preference
{parent=Revealed preference}
{title2=$R=(R_D\cup\Delta)^*$}

The weak <indirect revealed preference> is the <reflexive closure> of the <transitive closure of a relation> of direct revealed comparisons. Equivalently, it consists of finite chains, including chains of length zero. The diagonal convention is essential if the relation is to be an intersection of complete weak <preference relations> on all alternatives.

= Congruence axiom
{parent=Indirect revealed preference}

The <congruence axiom> says that an affordable alternative indirectly revealed at least as desirable as a chosen alternative must also be chosen. For a chosen bundle $x$ and an affordable unchosen bundle $y$, the axiom rules out $yRx$; because $xRy$, every strict-part-preserving completion ranks $x$ strictly above $y$.

= Consumption allocation
{parent=Exchange economy}
{title2=$f:A\to\mathbb R_+^L$}

In an economy with a <measure space> of consumers, a <consumption allocation> is a <measurable>, <integrable function> assigning each consumer a feasible bundle. Exact aggregate feasibility means $\int_Af\,d\mu=\int_A\omega\,d\mu$.

= Scale invariance

= Dilatation
{parent=Scale invariance}
{title2=$x'=e^{-\alpha}x,\ \phi'(x')=e^{\alpha\Delta}\phi(x)$}

A simultaneous rescaling of spacetime coordinates and a field with specified weight $\Delta$. Its infinitesimal variation at a fixed argument is $\bar\delta\phi=\alpha(\Delta\phi+x^\mu\partial_\mu\phi)$. For a canonical real <scalar field> in $D$ spacetime dimensions, $\Delta=(D-2)/2$ keeps the kinetic <action> invariant. Prescribing $\Delta=1$ therefore restricts kinetic invariance to $D=4$. In four dimensions the classical massless quartic <scalar field theory> has a conserved <dilatation current> $j^\mu=\phi\partial^\mu\phi+x_\nu T^{\mu\nu}$.

= CRT
{synonym}
{c}

The Chinese remainder theorem says that <remainders> modulo pairwise <coprime> positive <integers> can be specified independently. For $m_1,\ldots,m_r$ pairwise <coprime> and $M=m_1\cdots m_r$, every prescribed list $a_1,\ldots,a_r$ determines **one <congruence class> modulo $M$** satisfying $x\equiv a_j\pmod{m_j}$ for all $j$. It determines a class, rather than one unrestricted <integer>: adding a multiple of $M$ preserves every remainder.

For example, combine $x\equiv2\pmod3$ and $x\equiv3\pmod5$. Write $x=2+3k$ from the first condition. The second becomes $3k\equiv1\pmod5$. Since three has <modular inverse> two modulo five, $k\equiv2\pmod5$, giving **$x\equiv8\pmod{15}$**. The <integers> $8$, $23$ and $-7$ all work. The fifteen possibilities modulo fifteen correspond to the fifteen pairs in a three-by-five grid.

\Image[chinese-remainder-residue-grids.png]
{title=Coprime moduli fill every residue pair; shared factors impose a compatibility condition}

The theorem also preserves arithmetic: reducing an <addition> or a <multiplication> modulo each $m_j$ gives the same tuple as performing that operation on the corresponding <congruence classes>. In the language of <quotient rings>, reduction is a <ring isomorphism>
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb Z/M\mathbb Z&\longrightarrow\prod_{j=1}^r\mathbb Z/m_j\mathbb Z,\\
[x]_M&\longmapsto([x]_{m_1},\ldots,[x]_{m_r}).
\end{aligned}
$$
One large residue is thus an exact encoding of several smaller residues. This is useful for splitting arithmetic into smaller calculations and then reconstructing the result; MIT's modular arithmetic notes develop both the reconstruction and the product representation. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-200-principles-of-discrete-applied-mathematics-spring-2024/mit18_200_s24_lec15.pdf[Moitra, MIT 18.200, Lecture 15, section 4, Chinese remainder theorem]{ref} For a concrete multiplication, $7$ corresponds to $(1,2)$ and $8$ to $(2,3)$ modulo $(3,5)$. Their componentwise product is $(2,1)$, which reconstructs to $11\pmod{15}$, agreeing with $56\equiv11\pmod{15}$.

A constructive formula explains the independence. For $m_j>1$, put $M_j=M/m_j$ and choose $u_j$ with $M_ju_j\equiv1\pmod{m_j}$, using a <modular multiplicative inverse>. Then $e_j=M_ju_j$ is one modulo $m_j$ and zero modulo every other $m_i$. Therefore
$$
x\equiv\sum_{j=1}^r a_je_j\pmod M
$$
has the desired remainders. A modulus equal to one imposes no restriction and can be omitted. Uniqueness follows because a difference divisible by every $m_j$ is divisible by their product when they are pairwise <coprime>. This short construction gives a reusable algorithm as well as an existence statement.

The <coprime> hypothesis explains why every cell in the left grid is attainable. With <integer moduli> six and eight, a pair must agree modulo their <greatest common divisor>, two. There are twenty-four compatible pairs, rather than forty-eight arbitrary pairs; each corresponds to a class modulo the <least common multiple>, twenty-four. The <Chinese remainder theorem for noncoprime moduli> states the resulting compatibility rule.

= Common power residues from coprime exponent congruences
{parent=Chinese remainder theorem}
{title2=$n\equiv3x^3\equiv5y^5\equiv7z^7\pmod p,\quad p\equiv1\pmod{105}$}

Choose a <primitive root> $g$ modulo $p$ and write the nonzero coefficients $3,5,7$ as $g^a,g^b,g^c$. The <Chinese remainder theorem> gives an exponent $r$ congruent to $a,b,c$ modulo $3,5,7$, respectively. Then $n=g^r$ and the three roots $g^{(r-a)/3}$, $g^{(r-b)/5}$, $g^{(r-c)/7}$ give the displayed common nonzero residue. This technique converts multiplicative power constraints into simultaneous additive <modular congruences> for exponents.

= Consecutive integers divisible by prescribed prime powers
{parent=Chinese remainder theorem}
{title2=$q_i^{a_i}\mid n+i\quad(1\leq i\leq r)$}

Let $q_1,\ldots,q_r$ be distinct <prime numbers> and $a_1,\ldots,a_r$ positive <integers>. There are infinitely many positive <integers> $n$ with $q_i^{a_i}\mid n+i$ for every $i$. Indeed the moduli $q_i^{a_i}$ are pairwise <coprime>, so the <Chinese remainder theorem> solves $n\equiv-i\pmod{q_i^{a_i}}$ simultaneously. Adding multiples of their product gives arbitrarily large positive solutions. This allows long runs in which every <integer> has a large prescribed <prime power> divisor, even though neighboring <integers> are <coprime>.

= Chinese remainder theorem for noncoprime moduli
{parent=Chinese remainder theorem}
{c}
{title2=$a\equiv b\pmod{\gcd(m,n)}$}

For positive <integer moduli> $m,n$, the simultaneous <integer congruences> $x\equiv a\pmod m$ and $x\equiv b\pmod n$ are solvable exactly when $a\equiv b\pmod{\gcd(m,n)}$. When solvable, they determine a unique <congruence class> modulo $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)$. To see the condition, put $x=a+mk$: the remaining equation is the <linear congruence> $mk\equiv b-a\pmod n$, which is solvable exactly when the <greatest common divisor> divides $b-a$.

For example, $x\equiv1\pmod6$ and $x\equiv3\pmod8$ give $6k\equiv2\pmod8$, hence $3k\equiv1\pmod4$ and $k\equiv3\pmod4$. Thus **$x\equiv19\pmod{24}$**. Changing the second remainder to two makes the conditions incompatible: the first requires an <odd integer>, while the second requires an <even integer>. Article 32 of <Carl Friedrich Gauss>'s *Disquisitiones arithmeticae* gives a successive construction for simultaneous remainders, including the <greatest common divisor> and <least common multiple> that enter this general case. https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Disquisitiones_arithmeticae/Sectio_secunda[Gauss, Disquisitiones arithmeticae, section II, article 32; text from the 1870 collected edition]{ref}

= Chinese remainder theorem for ideals
{c}
{parent=Chinese remainder theorem}
{title2=$R/(I_1\cdots I_r)\cong\prod_iR/I_i$}

For pairwise comaximal <ideals> in a <commutative ring>, reduction has the displayed <ring isomorphism>. The kernel is their intersection, equal to their product. Choose $e_i\in\prod_{j\ne i}I_j$ with $e_i\equiv1\pmod{I_i}$; then $\sum_i a_ie_i$ lifts any prescribed residue tuple. The equality between intersection and product follows from $1=u+v$, $u\in I$, $v\in J$, which writes each $x\in I\cap J$ as $xu+xv\in IJ$.

= Prime-power fraction decomposition
{parent=Chinese remainder theorem}
{title2=$a/b=n+\sum_i q_i/p_i^{n_i}$}

For a positive integer $b=\prod_i p_i^{n_i}$, every rational number $a/b$ has a unique representation $a/b=n+\sum_iq_i/p_i^{n_i}$ with $n\in\mathbb Z$ and $0\leq q_i<p_i^{n_i}$. With $B_i=b/p_i^{n_i}$, the coefficients satisfy $q_i\equiv aB_i^{-1}\pmod{p_i^{n_i}}$. Their existence and uniqueness use <modular multiplicative inverses> and the <Chinese remainder theorem>.

= Lagrange theorem for polynomial congruences
{c}
{parent=Chinese remainder theorem}

A nonzero polynomial of degree $d$ over a field has at most $d$ roots. In particular, if a polynomial congruence $f(x)\equiv0\pmod p$ has degree $d$ and not all coefficients are divisible by the prime $p$, it has at most $d$ incongruent solutions modulo $p$.

= Chinese remainder theorem for unit groups
{parent=Chinese remainder theorem}
{c}

For coprime $m,n$, reduction gives $(\mathbb Z/mn\mathbb Z)^\times\cong(\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z)^\times\times(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^\times$.

= Multiplicative order under the Chinese remainder theorem
{parent=Chinese remainder theorem for unit groups}

The order of a unit modulo a product of coprime moduli is the least common multiple of the orders of its components.

= Mathematical model
{parent=Mathematics}
{wiki}

A mathematical model represents selected features of a system with mathematical objects and relations so that its behavior can be analyzed or predicted. Choosing the representation is part of the work: specify the quantities tracked, the equations or <probability distributions> connecting them, and the conditions under which those connections are expected to apply. A <mathematical proof> can establish a consequence of the model; comparing that consequence with observations tests its suitability for the intended use.

For a <simple pendulum>, the angle $\theta(t)$ and <gravitational acceleration> $g$ give the ideal equation $\ddot\theta+(g/\ell)\sin\theta=0$. Its assumptions include a point mass, fixed <length> $\ell$, uniform <gravitational acceleration>, and no <damping>. The <small-angle approximation> gives the simpler <harmonic oscillator equation> and <oscillation period> $T_0=2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}$. A larger <amplitude> changes the full model's period; drag requires a <damped pendulum>. The simpler model remains useful when its omitted effects are small for the question being asked.

A model need not track every detail. A <Bernoulli process> models repeated success/failure observations using one <probability> and an independence assumption; a <Gaussian process> specifies a <mean> and a <covariance function> to model related measurements. Each representation enables calculations by retaining some distinctions and omitting others. Useful simplification therefore comes with a stated domain of use and quantities against which its predictions can be checked.

= Parameter
{parent=Mathematical model}

A <parameter> specifies a member of a family of mathematical models and is held fixed while a particular solution evolves. Varying it can change equilibrium existence, stability or long-term dynamics, producing a <bifurcation>. A model <parameter> differs from its evolving state variables and from an initial condition, even though all may be numerical inputs to a computation.

= Mathematical model parameter
{parent=Mathematical model}
{title2=$\mu$}

A <mathematical model parameter> is a quantity held fixed while solving a particular instance of a model, but allowed to vary between instances. Changing a <mathematical model parameter>[parameter] can change <equilibrium point>[equilibria], their <lyapunov stability>[stability] and the qualitative behavior of trajectories. <mathematical model parameter>[Parameter] constancy does not mean probabilistic independence.

A transport-only closed <box model> preserves total inventory, even when its transport <matrix> is nonsymmetric.

= Unequal charges above a grounded conducting plane
{parent=Method of images}

For real <point charges> $Q$ and $-2Q$ at $(0,b)$ and $(a,b)$ above the plane $y=0$, the <image charges> are $-Q$ and $+2Q$ at the reflected positions. The image charges enforce constant <electric potential> on the plane. The physical forces follow by summing ordinary <Coulomb's law> forces on each real charge; the electrostatic-energy factor of one half for induced charges does not multiply this force. The smaller positive charge can have an upward total force component, even though its own image attracts it downward.

= Electric dipole above a grounded conducting plane
{parent=Method of images}
{title2=$U=-p^2(1+\cos^2\theta)/(64\pi\epsilon_0a^3)$}

An ideal <electric dipole> at height $a$ above a grounded plane has an image <electric dipole moment> with reversed tangential component and unchanged normal component. If $\theta$ is its angle to the normal, its conductor-response <potential energy> is the displayed expression. The factor one half in $U=-\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E_{\rm im}/2$ accounts for induced <surface charge>. Therefore its downward <force> magnitude is $3p^2(1+\cos^2\theta)/(64\pi\epsilon_0a^4)$, and its orienting <torque> follows from $-\partial_\theta U$. The two normal orientations are stable against tilting; the parallel orientation is unstable. The net induced plane <electric charge> is zero for a neutral source, although its local <surface charge density> is nonzero.

= Image charges in a sixty-degree conducting wedge
{parent=Method of images}

For two grounded conducting half-planes meeting at angle $\pi/3$, place a <point charge> $q$ at radius $d$ on the bisector $\theta=\pi/6$. The <method of images> closes after six copies: positive copies at angles $\pi/6+2k\pi/3$ and negative copies at $-\pi/6+2k\pi/3$, for $k=0,1,2$. Only the first positive copy is real. Opposite-sign reflected pairs make the <electric potential> vanish on both faces; the <uniqueness theorem for the electrostatic potential> proves the construction. Summing the five <image-charge> <electric fields> gives the <electrostatic force> $\mathbf F=-q^2(5/4-1/\sqrt3)\widehat{\mathbf e}_r/(4\pi\epsilon_0d^2)$, towards the intersection. The coefficient follows from distances $d$, $\sqrt3d$, and $2d$ to the adjacent negative, nonadjacent positive, and opposite negative copies.

= Image line charge for a grounded conducting cylinder
{parent=Method of images}
{title2=$r_{\rm image}=a^2/b$}

A real infinite <line charge> $\lambda$ lies at distance $b>a$ from the axis of a grounded conducting cylinder of radius $a$. An image line $-\lambda$ at radius $a^2/b$ lies inside the excluded conductor. On the circle, real and image distances obey $r_1/r_2=b/a$, so $\phi=(\lambda/(2\pi\epsilon_0))\ln[(b/a)r_2/r_1]$ vanishes there. The image field solves the physical exterior problem with bounded far potential. Its additive constant matters; the interior image field is not a physical field inside the conductor.

= Cylinder surface charge induced by an exterior line
{parent=Image line charge for a grounded conducting cylinder}
{title2=$\sigma=-\frac{\lambda(b^2-a^2)}{2\pi a(b^2+a^2-2ab\cos\theta)}$}

For the real line at radius $b>a$ and polar angle measured toward it, the exterior radial field gives $\sigma(\theta)=-\lambda(b^2-a^2)/[2\pi a(b^2+a^2-2ab\cos\theta)]$. Its integral around the circumference is $-\lambda$ per unit axial length. At $b=2a$, this becomes $-3\lambda/[2\pi a(5-4\cos\theta)]$. The nearer surface has larger induced charge magnitude.

= Image force on a line charge above a conducting plane
{parent=Method of images}

With no imposed external field, a line of charge density $\lambda$ at height $h$ above a conducting plane has an image $-\lambda$ a distance $2h$ away. The force per unit length is $-\lambda^2\hat{\mathbf z}/(4\pi\epsilon_0h)$. It is always attractive. The image field gives the force directly; a factor one-half used in some image-energy calculations does not multiply this force.

= Finite image construction for a planar wedge
{parent=Method of images}
{title2=$\arg z\in(0,\pi/N),\quad z_k^+=z_0e^{2\pi ik/N},\quad z_k^-=\overline{z_0}e^{2\pi ik/N}$}

For a positive integer $N$, a point source in a grounded planar wedge of opening $\pi/N$ can be supplemented by $2N-1$ images. Keep positive sources at $z_0e^{2\pi ik/N}$ and negative sources at $\overline{z_0}e^{2\pi ik/N}$, $k=0,\ldots,N-1$, counting the actual source as the first positive one. Reflection in either boundary pairs equal distances with opposite signs, so the logarithmic <electrostatic potential> vanishes on both rays. Every image lies outside the open physical wedge.

The identity $\prod_k(z-z_0e^{2\pi ik/N})=z^N-z_0^N$ compresses the image sum to
$$
\Phi(z)=-\frac e{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\log\left|\frac{z^N-z_0^N}{z^N-\overline{z_0}^{\,N}}\right|.
$$
Here the normalization is that of a two-dimensional point source, equivalently a charge per unit length in a translationally invariant three-dimensional problem. For $N=3$, a source on the bisector with unit radius has $z_0^3=i$, giving the ratio $(z^3-i)/(z^3+i)$. The <method of images> works finitely here because successive boundary reflections generate a finite rotation group; arbitrary wedge angles need not have a finite image construction.

= Neumann image lattice for a point source in a strip
{parent=Method of images}
{c}
{title2=$V=\frac{q}{2\pi}\left[\log r_0+\sum_{n\geq1}\log\frac{r_nr_{-n}}{(2nb)^2}\right]+C$}

For $b>0$, the <Poisson equation> $\Delta V=q\delta^{(2)}(x,y)$ in $-b<x<b$ with homogeneous <Neumann boundary conditions> on both edges admits the even-in-$y$ <method of images> solution in the title, where $r_n^2=(x-2nb)^2+y^2$. All image strengths are $q$. Pairing the images at $\pm2nb$ and subtracting their constant logarithms makes each summand $O(n^{-2})$, giving local <uniform convergence> away from the source. The unrenormalized sum of logarithms does not converge.

Differentiating gives
$$
\partial_yV=\frac{q}{2\pi}\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}\frac{y}{(x-2nb)^2+y^2}.
$$
The corresponding $x$-derivatives cancel in reflected pairs on $x=\pm b$. For $y>0$, integration across the strip yields a telescoping <arctangent> sum:
$$
\int_{-b}^b\partial_yV\,dx=\frac{q}{2\pi}\lim_{N\to\infty}2\arctan\frac{(2N+1)b}{y}=\frac q2.
$$
One closed form, up to an additive constant, is $V=(q/4\pi)\log[\cosh(\pi y/b)-\cos(\pi x/b)]$. Its near-source <logarithmic singularity> has coefficient $q/(2\pi)$. Conditions at large $|y|$ or a symmetry class must be specified for a uniqueness claim; side <Neumann boundary conditions> alone allow additional homogeneous <harmonic functions>.

= Cylinder-plane image charges
{parent=Method of images}
{title2=$c=\sqrt{d^2-a^2},\quad C'=2\pi\varepsilon_0/\operatorname{arcosh}(d/a)$}

An infinite conducting cylinder of radius $a$ at centre height $d>a$ above a grounded plane has the same exterior <electric potential> as opposite <line charges> $\pm\lambda$ at heights $\pm c$. If $R_+$ and $R_-$ are distances from the positive and negative lines, $V=\lambda\ln(R_-/R_+)/(2\pi\varepsilon_0)$. The plane has $R_-=R_+$; the cylinder has $R_-/R_+=(d+c)/a$. Its voltage is therefore $\lambda\operatorname{arcosh}(d/a)/(2\pi\varepsilon_0)$, giving the displayed <capacitance per unit length>. The <uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem> fixes the exterior field. A Gaussian contour surrounding the cylinder encloses the positive image line, so $\lambda$ is also its physical charge per unit length.

= Electrostatic attraction per unit length of a cylinder above a plane
{parent=Cylinder-plane image charges}
{title2=$f=\lambda^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\sqrt{d^2-a^2})$}

For the <cylinder-plane image charges> geometry, the attractive force is the displayed quantity per unit length. One derivation uses the effective energy including the voltage source: at fixed cylinder voltage $V$, the force toward increasing centre height is $F_d=V^2(dC'/dd)/2$. Since $C'=2\pi\varepsilon_0/\operatorname{arcosh}(d/a)$ decreases with $d$, this force points toward the plane. For $d\gg a$, $\lambda\simeq2\pi\varepsilon_0V/\ln(2d/a)$ and $f\simeq\pi\varepsilon_0V^2/[d\ln^2(2d/a)]$. A total force additionally requires the cable length.

= Spherical Neumann dipole images
{c}
{parent=Method of images}
{title2=$u=\Psi(\mathbf r;\mathbf r_0)+\lambda^{3/2}\Psi(\mathbf r;\lambda\mathbf r_0)+\sqrt\lambda\int_0^\lambda\Psi(\mathbf r;t\mathbf r_0)\,dt$}

For a <current dipole> in a homogeneous insulating sphere of radius $a$, let $\lambda=r^2/a^2$ and let $\Psi$ be its primary <electric potential>. = Unfolding a reflected interval trajectory
{parent=Method of images}

Reflecting an interval repeatedly across its endpoints maps a reflected path to a free path in an image interval. A return trajectory from $q\in(0,L)$ ends at $q+2rL$ after an even number of wall reflections, or $-q+2rL$ after an odd number. An <infinite square well> has reflection amplitude $-1$, so the corresponding <Dirichlet heat kernel on an interval> is the difference of direct and mirrored free kernels. Individual straight paths illustrate the geometry; the <path integral> sums all time-sliced paths with the same endpoints.

= Urysohn's metrisation theorem
{c}
{synonym}

Every <second-countable space> that is also a <T3 space> is a <metrizable space>. First <regularity of a topological space> and the <Lindelöf> property yield a <normal space>: cover each of two disjoint <closed sets> by countably many <open sets> whose closures avoid the other, and subtract the first $n$ opposite closures from the $n$th <neighborhood>. The resulting unions are disjoint <open neighborhoods>. <Urysohn's lemma> then gives, for each pair of basic sets $\overline B_n\subseteq B_m$, a <continuous function> equal to one on the smaller <closure> and zero outside the larger set. There are countably many such pairs. The shrinking form of <regularity of a topological space> shows these functions generate the topology, and $T_1$ makes them separate <points>. Their coordinate map embeds the space into the <countable> cube, with compatible <metric> $d(x,y)=\sum_j2^{-j}|f_j(x)-f_j(y)|$. The bounded tails prove compatibility with the <product topology>. Thus a <countable> base converts <separation axioms> into a numerical distance.

= Metrizable separability and the Lindelöf property
{parent=Metrizable space}

A <metrizable space> is <separable> exactly when it is <Lindelöf>. A <countable> <dense subset> gives a <countable> <topological basis> of balls with rational radii, hence the open-cover property. Conversely, choose <countable> subcovers of all radius-$1/n$ ball covers; their centers form a <countable> <dense subset>.

= Compact Nakayama lemma
{c}
{parent=Nakayama lemma}
{title2=$M/(p,T)M\text{ finite}\Longrightarrow M\text{ finitely generated over }\Lambda$}

For a compact continuous <module> over the <Iwasawa algebra of a Zp-extension>, lifts of a basis of $M/(p,T)M$ generate $M$. Their generated image is compact. The quotient $Q$ satisfies $Q=(p,T)Q$; in every finite continuous quotient, $(p,T)$ acts nilpotently, since the group action is pro-$p$. Thus all finite quotients of $Q$ vanish, and compactness gives $Q=0$.

= Graded Nakayama lemma
{parent=Nakayama lemma}

For a positively <graded ring> with degree-zero part a <field>, put $R_+=\bigoplus_{d>0}R_d$. A bounded-below <graded module> $M$ with $M=R_+M$ is zero: a nonzero homogeneous element of least degree would have to be a sum of positive-degree coefficients times elements of lower degree. Hence homogeneous lifts of a basis of $M/R_+M$ generate $M$.

= Waiting time for a word in independent nonuniform symbols
{parent=Pattern waiting time}
{title2=$\mathbb E\tau_w=\sum_{k\in B(w)}\bigl(\prod_{j=1}^k p(w_j)\bigr)^{-1}$}

Here $w$ is a finite <word over an alphabet>, every letter of the word has positive probability, and $B(w)$ is the set of lengths of its <borders of a word>, including its full length. Start a unit-capital gambler before every draw, betting successively on the word's letters and dividing surviving capital by the required letter probability. Total capital minus the number of entrants is a <martingale>. At the first full match, the surviving gamblers correspond exactly to the borders, giving the displayed formula by <optional stopping>. Disjoint blocks of full word length give a <geometric tail bound from a uniform escape probability>, so the waiting time is integrable; bounded word length and positive letter probabilities give bounded <martingale> increments and justify stopping.

= Rational root theorem

= Rodrigues rotation formula
{parent=Mathematics}
{c}
{wiki}

For a real <angle> $\alpha$ and a unit <vector> $\mathbf n$, the <rotation> about $\mathbf n$ sends
$$
\mathbf x\longmapsto\cos\alpha\,\mathbf x+\sin\alpha\,(\mathbf n\times\mathbf x)+(1-\cos\alpha)\mathbf n(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf x).
$$
The <vector triple product identity> gives the equivalent expression $\mathbf x+\sin\alpha\,\mathbf n\times\mathbf x+(1-\cos\alpha)\mathbf n\times(\mathbf n\times\mathbf x)$. To see why it is a <rotation>, decompose $\mathbf x$ into its parallel and perpendicular components relative to $\mathbf n$. The parallel component is fixed, while the perpendicular component becomes $\cos\alpha\,\mathbf x_\perp+\sin\alpha\,\mathbf n\times\mathbf x_\perp$, the usual plane <rotation>. It is also the <matrix exponential> of the <linear operator> $H\mathbf x=\alpha\mathbf n\times\mathbf x$: the identity $H^3=-\alpha^2H$ reduces its <exponential series> to these three terms.

= Simultaneous diagonalization
{parent=Mathematics}
{wiki}

A finite family of pairwise commuting diagonalizable operators on a finite-dimensional complex vector space is simultaneously diagonalizable. In particular, commuting Hermitian operators admit an orthonormal common eigenbasis.

= Eigenvalue addition in a common eigenbasis
{parent=Simultaneous diagonalization}
{title2=$Av_i=\alpha_iv_i,\ Bv_i=\beta_iv_i\ \Longrightarrow\ (A+B)v_i=(\alpha_i+\beta_i)v_i$}

If <linear operators> $A,B$ have a common <eigenbasis> $(v_i)$, then $A+B$ has the corresponding <eigenvalues> $\alpha_i+\beta_i$, counting <algebraic multiplicity>. This follows directly from <linearity> on each <basis vector>. The pairing is determined by the common <eigenvectors>; it is not the collection of all pairwise sums of arbitrary <eigenvalues>.

= Commuting symmetric matrices have a common orthonormal eigenbasis
{parent=Simultaneous diagonalization}

For commuting real <symmetric matrices> $A,B$, each <eigenspace> of $A$ is invariant under $B$: $A(Bv)=B(Av)=\lambda Bv$. The restriction of $B$ to that <eigenspace> is again symmetric. The <spectral theorem> therefore supplies an <orthonormal eigenbasis> for $B$ inside each <eigenspace> of $A$. Combining these <bases> gives a common <orthonormal eigenbasis> of the whole space. Conversely, two <symmetric matrices> diagonal in the same <orthonormal basis> <commute>.

= Simple spectrum gives simultaneous diagonalization
{parent=Simultaneous diagonalization}

Suppose $D$ has $n$ distinct <eigenvalues> over a field and $C$ commutes with $D$. Each <eigenspace> of $D$ is one-dimensional. Since <commuting maps preserve eigenspaces>, $C$ acts as a scalar on each of them. The <eigenvectors> of $D$ give a common <eigenbasis> and hence <simultaneous diagonalization>. For real matrices, the result gives a real <eigenbasis> only if the <eigenvalues> of $D$ are real; otherwise it applies over $\mathbb C$.

= Joint spectrum
{parent=Simultaneous diagonalization}
{title2=$\operatorname{Spec}(A_1,\ldots,A_r)$}

For commuting diagonalizable operators $A_1,\ldots,A_r$ on a finite-dimensional <vector space>, the joint spectrum is the set of tuples $(a_1,\ldots,a_r)$ for which there is a common nonzero <eigenvector> $v$ with $A_iv=a_iv$. Commuting self-adjoint operators on a finite-dimensional <inner product space> admit a simultaneous <orthonormal eigenbasis>.

= Spectral mapping theorem
{parent=Mathematics}

= Holomorphic spectral mapping theorem
{parent=Spectral mapping theorem}
{title2=$\sigma(f(x))=f(\sigma(x))$}

For a <holomorphic function> near the spectrum, the spectrum of its functional-calculus value is exactly the image of the original spectrum. If a scalar is absent from that image, the reciprocal of the shifted function is holomorphic on a smaller spectral neighborhood and supplies an inverse. If it is attained at $\lambda$, a divided difference factors $f(x)-f(\lambda)$ through $x-\lambda$. These factors commute, so invertibility of their product would contradict $\lambda\in\sigma(x)$.

= Resolvent spectral mapping identity
{parent=Spectral mapping theorem}
{title2=$\sigma((A-z)^{-1})\setminus\{0\}=\{(\lambda-z)^{-1}:\lambda\in\sigma(A)\}$}

For a closed <linear operator> and $z$ in its resolvent, the displayed identity follows by factoring $(A-z)^{-1}-wI$ for $w\ne0$. Its inverse exists exactly when $A-(z+1/w)I$ has a bounded inverse. Products involving $A$ are bounded after composing with a resolvent, since $A(A-\lambda)^{-1}=I+\lambda(A-\lambda)^{-1}$. Zero may be in the bounded resolvent's <spectrum> when $A$ is unbounded.

= Logistica speciosa
{parent=History of mathematics}

Calculation with species or general magnitudes, represented by letters, in <François Viète>'s analytical programme.<In artem analyticem isagoge by François Viète>[Folio 5r]{ref}

= Logistica numerosa
{parent=History of mathematics}

Numerical calculation, contrasted with <logistica speciosa> in <François Viète>'s analytical programme.<In artem analyticem isagoge by François Viète>[Folio 5r]{ref}

= Universal arithmetic
{parent=History of mathematics}

A historical description of general calculation, appearing in the title of <Arithmetica universalis by Isaac Newton>. It is not a guarantee that every law of ordinary numerical <arithmetic> applies to every <algebraic structure>.

= Symbolical algebra
{parent=History of mathematics}

In <George Peacock>'s terminology, a science of symbolic operations whose laws are considered independently of a single numerical interpretation.<A Treatise on Algebra by George Peacock>[1830 preface]{ref}

= Arithmetical algebra
{parent=History of mathematics}

In <George Peacock>'s terminology, symbolic calculation subject to the meanings and restrictions of <arithmetic>.<A Treatise on Algebra by George Peacock>[1830 preface]{ref}

= Adequality
{parent=History of mathematics}

A procedure associated with <Pierre de Fermat> for <extrema> and <tangents>. Compare expressions at $a$ and $a+e$, remove common terms, divide by a remaining power of $e$, and suppress terms still containing $e$. Its interpretation must be distinguished from ordinary equality at finite $e$ and from a modern <limit> definition.<Fermat's rule for maxima and minima by the Garden of Archimedes>{ref}
