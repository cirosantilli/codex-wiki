= Solution
{parent=i}

For this <concave recruitment map>, the extinction state is invariant because $g(0)=0$. Near extinction, $N_{t+1}=g'(0)N_t+O(N_t^2)$ with multiplier above one, so small positive populations initially grow. The <strict concavity> $g''<0$ means marginal recruitment decreases as population increases. More precisely, for $N>0$,
$$
\frac{d}{dN}\frac{g(N)}N=\frac{Ng'(N)-g(N)}{N^2}<0,
$$
since $g(N)=\int_0^Ng'(s)\,ds>Ng'(N)$. Thus per-capita recruitment decreases and the graph bends downwards from an initial slope greater than that of the identity line.

A positive <fixed point>, if present, is the unique positive intersection with $g(N)=N$: the strictly decreasing ratio can cross one at most once. The sketch shows the usual saturating example $g(N)=3N/(1+N)$, which crosses at $N=2$.

**The three assumptions alone do not guarantee this crossing.** For example $g(N)=2N+1-e^{-N}$ satisfies all three and remains strictly above $N$ for every $N>0$. An extra large-population restriction, such as $g(N)<N$ somewhere, supplies existence. Keeping this distinction avoids inventing a <carrying capacity> that is not implied by the printed hypotheses.

= Solution

Let $N_*>0$ be a <fixed point> and put $N_t=N_*+\eta_t$. <Taylor expansion> gives $\eta_{t+1}=g'(N_*)\eta_t+O(\eta_t^2)$, so the linear perturbation is $\eta_t=g'(N_*)^t\eta_0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{|g'(N_*)|<1}
$$
is the condition for linear asymptotic stability. A multiplier in $(0,1)$ gives monotone decay; one in $(-1,0)$ gives alternating decay. If its magnitude exceeds one, perturbations grow. Magnitude one is nonhyperbolic and needs nonlinear analysis.

<Strict concavity> and $g(N_*)/N_*=1$ already give $g'(N_*)<1$. Thus the remaining stability requirement is **$g'(N_*)>-1$**. If $g$ is additionally nonnegative on the entire half-line, <strict concavity> prevents its <derivative> becoming negative there, since it would then force $g$ eventually negative. Under that stronger global population-map assumption the positive <fixed point> is automatically stable. The printed local slope and <concavity> information, without that global nonnegativity requirement, does not by itself rule out a flip instability.

With <constant-effort harvesting>, the constant-effort model is
$$
\boxed{N_{t+1}=g(N_t)-EN_t.}
$$
The extinction multiplier becomes $g'(0)-E$, so extinction is <linearly stable> when $g'(0)-1<E<g'(0)+1$. At $E_c=g'(0)-1$ its multiplier crosses $+1$. Values large enough to make the next population negative lie outside the physical validity of this untruncated harvest model.

Any positive <fixed point> $N_E$ solves $g(N_E)/N_E=1+E$. Since that ratio is strictly decreasing, there is at most one such point, and it decreases with effort. Differentiating the fixed-point equation yields
$$
\frac{dN_E}{dE}=\frac{N_E}{g'(N_E)-1-E}<0.
$$
If the unharvested positive branch exists, it continues for $0\leq E<E_c$ and approaches zero as $E\uparrow E_c$. This follows because its required ratio lies between $1$ and $g'(0)$; its intersection lies between zero and the unharvested <fixed point>. There is no positive <fixed point> for $E\geq E_c$.

The positive-state multiplier is $m_E=g'(N_E)-E<1$ by <concavity>. It is stable precisely when
$$
\boxed{-1<g'(N_E)-E<1.}
$$
Crossing $-1$ can change stability through a flip, but the direction of change is not fixed by the three assumptions: $dm_E/dE=g''(N_E)N_E'-1$ has no prescribed sign. In the illustrated saturating example, increasing effort eventually removes the positive branch at extinction. The finite upper stability bound for the algebraic extinction map should not be interpreted as a physically meaningful recovery of a population at unrealistically large harvest effort.

A steady population $N_*$ is a root $h(N_*)=0$. To linearize one needs $h$ differentiable near that root, an extra local regularity assumption beyond the source's continuity. Setting $N=N_*+\eta$ gives $\dot\eta=h'(N_*)\eta+o(\eta)$, hence $\eta(t)=\eta(0)e^{h'(N_*)t}$ at linear order.

Thus **$h'(N_*)<0$ gives linear asymptotic stability and $h'(N_*)>0$ gives instability**. If $h'(N_*)=0$, <linearization> is inconclusive. In a one-dimensional <phase line>, the more general nonlinear test is whether the <vector field> points towards the root from both sides. At the physical boundary $N=0$, only the right-hand side matters. Continuity alone does not guarantee uniqueness of trajectories, so a standard well-defined flow also presumes a local uniqueness condition, such as local Lipschitz continuity.

= Solution

The four nonnegative <equilibria> are $0,a_1,a_2,a_3$. Since all factors are positive below $a_1$, the sign of $h(N)$ on consecutive intervals is
$$
(0,a_1):+,qquad(a_1,a_2):- ,qquad(a_2,a_3):+,qquad(a_3,\infty):-.
$$
The <phase line> therefore points away from zero, towards $a_1$, away from $a_2$, and towards $a_3$. Equivalently $h'(0)=a_1a_2a_3>0$, and at each simple positive root $a_i$, $h'(a_i)=-a_i\prod_{j\ne i}(a_j-a_i)$. Their signs alternate negative, positive, negative.

Hence **extinction and $a_2$ are unstable, while $a_1$ and $a_3$ are <asymptotically stable>**. Initial positive populations below $a_2$ tend to $a_1$; those above $a_2$ tend to $a_3$; an initial population exactly at $a_2$ remains there. This is <bistability> with a threshold separating the two basins.

= Solution

Put $P(N)=(a_1-N)(a_2-N)(a_3-N)$. The harvested <vector field> is $N[P(N)-E]$. Define the extinction threshold $E_T=P(0)=a_1a_2a_3$, and let $N_M\in(a_2,a_3)$ be the positive local maximum of $P$, with $E_M=P(N_M)>0$. Writing $s_1=a_1+a_2+a_3$, $s_2=a_1a_2+a_1a_3+a_2a_3$ gives
$$
N_M=\frac{s_1+\sqrt{s_1^2-3s_2}}3.
$$
Positive <equilibria> are intersections of $P(N)$ with the horizontal line $E$. A lower stable branch in $(0,a_1)$ exists exactly for $0<E<E_T$. A pair in $(a_2,a_3)$ exists exactly for $0<E<E_M$: its smaller member has $P'>0$ and is unstable; its larger member has $P'<0$ and is stable. At a positive root the linear <eigenvalue> is $NP'(N)$, which proves these claims. Extinction has <eigenvalue> $E_T-E$, becoming stable for $E>E_T$.

The <harvested bistable population thresholds> determine the behaviour. If $E_M<E_T$, the upper stable population and its unstable separator first disappear in a <saddle-node bifurcation>. Populations then tend to the remaining lower stable state until that branch reaches extinction at $E_T$; beyond it extinction attracts every positive initial state. If $E_T<E_M$, the lower stable branch reaches extinction first. For $E_T<E<E_M$, extinction and the upper positive state are both stable, separated by the middle unstable root. Increasing effort through $E_M$ then destroys the remaining positive <attractor>, causing a sudden collapse to extinction. If $E_T=E_M$, both events occur at the same effort at different populations.

At $E=E_T$, near zero the leading term is $P'(0)N^2<0$, so extinction attracts from the physical right side algebraically, despite its zero linear <eigenvalue>. At $E=E_M$, the double positive root is semistable: locally $\dot N\simeq-\tfrac12N_M|P''(N_M)|(N-N_M)^2$, attracting from above and repelling from below. These critical cases matter for exact initial data. The figure shows examples of both strict threshold orderings.

The <law of mass action> makes each elementary <reaction rate> proportional to the product of its reactant <concentrations>, with the stoichiometric change determining its contribution. Let $A=A_0$ be maintained constant, and let $D_X,D_Y>0$ be the <diffusion coefficients>. The first reaction consumes two $X$ molecules, the second creates one net $X$, the third consumes one each of $X,Y$, and the fourth consumes $Y$ while creating $X$. <Fick's laws> for <diffusion> give
$$
\boxed{X_t=D_XX_{xx}-2k_1X^2+k_2A_0X-k_3XY+k_4A_0Y,\qquad Y_t=D_YY_{xx}-k_3XY-k_4A_0Y.}
$$
Here the standard deterministic convention takes the event rate of the first reaction as $k_1X^2$, so its loss is $2k_1X^2$. A convention absorbing a combinatorial factor into $k_1$ leads to the same nondimensional model after consistently redefining that rate constant. The maintained reactant $A$ makes this an open chemical model; there is no <conservation law> requiring $A$ to decrease with reaction progress.

The <equal-diffusivity Fisher reduction> uses equal <diffusion coefficients> and the invariant relation $v=1-u$. The second equation then requires $u_\tau=\delta u(1-u)+u_{\xi\xi}$, while the first becomes $u_\tau=(1-\beta)u(1-u)+u_{\xi\xi}$. They agree when
$$
\boxed{\beta=1-\delta,\qquad v=1-u.}
$$
This reduction requires compatible initial and boundary data on that invariant relation. Tuning a parameter alone does not force arbitrary solutions of the two-field system onto it. For positive physical $\beta$, the displayed normalization also requires $0<\delta<1$. More generally the line $v=C(1-u)$ works when $\beta C=1-\delta$; the same positivity restriction remains for $C>0$.

Let $z=\xi-c\tau$ and $u=U(z)$ with $U(-\infty)=0$, $U(+\infty)=1$. The profile equation is $U''+cU'+\delta U(1-U)=0$. Multiplication by $U'$ and integration gives
$$
c\int_{-\infty}^\infty(U')^2\,dz=-\delta\int_0^1U(1-U)\,dU=-\frac\delta6.
$$
Consequently **$c<0$: the increasing front moves left**, with the $u=1$ state invading $u=0$. <Linearization> at its leading edge gives real monotone exponents only when $|c|\geq2\sqrt\delta$, yielding the usual Fisher front speed range. A convenient exact example is
$$
U(z)=\left(1+e^{-\sqrt{\delta/6}\,z}\right)^{-2},\qquad c=-5\sqrt{\delta/6}.
$$
Substitution verifies it and the figure shows its leftward displacement. The source uses $x$ for the end limits after defining $\xi$; they denote the same spatial coordinate here.

**Yes.** An immobile $Y$ <concentration> can still change locally through reaction while diffusing $X$ transports activation. Vanishing <diffusion> of one component does not prohibit a propagating reaction front.

In the invariant special case $v=0$, the first equation is already <Fisher's equation> with growth coefficient one, so it has travelling fronts regardless of $D$. A <one-diffuser reaction front> also gives a coupled example with nonzero $v$. For $z=\xi-c\tau$, the equations with $D=0$ are

A uniform <steady state> satisfies $f(u^*,v^*)=g(u^*,v^*)=0$. Set $u=u^*+\tilde u$, $v=v^*+\tilde v$ and keep only first-order terms in the perturbations. Since the base state is constant, its <Laplacian> vanishes. <Taylor expansion> gives
$$
\boxed{\partial_t\binom{\tilde u}{\tilde v}=\mathcal D\nabla^2\binom{\tilde u}{\tilde v}+J\binom{\tilde u}{\tilde v},\quad\mathcal D=\begin{pmatrix}D_u&0\\0&D_v\end{pmatrix},\quad J=\begin{pmatrix}f_u&f_v\\g_u&g_v\end{pmatrix}_{(u^*,v^*)}.}
$$
The zero-flux <boundary conditions> linearize to homogeneous Neumann conditions, $\partial_n\tilde u=\partial_n\tilde v=0$. The off-diagonal entries of the Jacobian describe the cross-effects of the two chemicals, while the diagonal entries describe local self-response.

A diffusion-driven instability means that the homogeneous reaction <equilibrium> is <asymptotically stable> against spatially uniform perturbations, but adding <diffusion> makes at least one admissible spatially nonuniform mode grow. Thus the <eigenvalues> of $J$ must have negative real parts, while $J-k^2\mathcal D$ has an <eigenvalue> with positive real part for some positive <Neumann Laplacian> <eigenvalue> $k^2$ on the actual domain.

The instability is driven by different <diffusion> responses acting together with reaction feedback. It is not merely an <equilibrium> that was already unstable without <diffusion>. On a bounded domain the permitted <wave numbers> are discrete, so an algebraically unstable continuous band must also contain an actual spatial eigenmode.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Write $a=f_u$, $b=f_v$, $c=g_u$, $d=g_v$, $\Delta=ad-bc$. Stability without <diffusion> requires $a+d<0$ and $\Delta>0$. For a mode with $q=k^2$, the characteristic equation is
$$
\lambda^2-[a+d-(D_u+D_v)q]\lambda+h(q)=0,\quad h(q)=D_uD_vq^2-(D_va+D_ud)q+\Delta.
$$
For positive diffusivities the <trace> stays negative for every $q\geq0$, so growth can occur only if $h(q)<0$. Put $S=D_va+D_ud$. This upward-opening quadratic has a negative interval at positive $q$ exactly when $S>0$ and $S^2>4D_uD_v\Delta$, or
$$
\boxed{a+d<0,\quad\Delta>0,\quad0<2\sqrt{D_uD_v\Delta}<D_ud+D_va.}
$$
If $D_u=D_v=D_0$, the right side is $D_0(a+d)<0$, impossible. Equivalently <diffusion> would merely subtract $D_0q$ from both reaction <eigenvalues>. Thus **unequal diffusivities are necessary**.

The potentially unstable band has endpoints
$$
q_\pm=\frac{S\pm\sqrt{S^2-4D_uD_v\Delta}}{2D_uD_v},\qquad0<q_-<q_+.
$$
These are necessary continuous-wave-number conditions. Actual instability on the specified bounded domain additionally requires an allowed $q$ strictly between them. The strict inequalities exclude neutral onset and presume both diffusivities are positive, as in an ordinary two-chemical <diffusion> model.

After relabelling the chemicals if necessary, take $f_u>0$ and $g_v<0$. At least one diagonal entry must be positive because $D_vf_u+D_ug_v>0$, whereas their sum is negative. Since their product is negative and $\det J>0$, the off-diagonal product must be negative and sufficiently large in magnitude. The two possibilities are
$$
\boxed{J\sim\begin{pmatrix}+&-\\+&-\end{pmatrix}\quad\hbox{or}\quad J\sim\begin{pmatrix}+&+\\-&-\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
In the first, $u$ activates its own production and stimulates $v$, while $v$ suppresses $u$: an activator-inhibitor mechanism. In the <substrate-depletion pattern mechanism>, $v$ promotes the autocatalytic species $u$, which consumes or suppresses $v$: a substrate-depletion mechanism. These are different biological interaction signs, not merely two drawings of the same feedback. Suitable relative magnitudes and sufficiently different diffusivities are still required; the sign pattern alone does not guarantee a <Turing instability>.

For Neumann boundaries the correct <eigenfunctions> include zero indices:
$$
\phi_{n_x,n_y}=\cos(n_x\pi x/L_x)\cos(n_y\pi y/L_y),\qquad n_x,n_y\in\{0,1,2,\ldots\}.
$$
Their squared <wave number> is $q=(n_x\pi/L_x)^2+(n_y\pi/L_y)^2$. The constant pair $(0,0)$ is reaction-stable. Instability requires $q_-<q<q_+$, using the band derived above. If both side lengths are large, the wave-number spacings are small enough that admissible modes can enter the band in either direction, including mixed modes. This does not mean the single $(1,1)$ mode necessarily stays unstable as lengths tend to infinity: its <wave number> eventually tends to zero below the band. Rather, increasingly fine wave-number spacing permits some suitable higher integers.

<Thin-domain Turing mode selection> applies if $L_y<\pi/\sqrt{q_+}$, every mode with $n_y\geq1$ has $q\geq(\pi/L_y)^2>q_+$ and is stable. A sufficiently large $L_x$ can nevertheless admit an integer $n_x\geq1$ with $q_-<(n_x\pi/L_x)^2<q_+$ and $n_y=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\hbox{a sufficiently thin domain suppresses transverse variation but can retain longitudinal instability}.}
$$
The printed range starting both indices at one excludes these purely longitudinal modes and cannot imply the requested conclusion. It also omits the constant mode. Further, each allowed spatial <eigenfunction> generally has two temporal <eigenvalue> branches of $J-q\mathcal D$, with coefficients along their <eigenvectors>; a single unrestricted scalar $\lambda(q)$ is shorthand for those branches rather than a complete general expansion.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Domain geometry can select biological pattern dimension even when the same reaction parameters and <diffusion coefficients> are used. A sufficiently wide developing tissue can admit instabilities with variation in two spatial directions; nonlinear development may produce spots, labyrinths or other two-dimensional patterns. A thin strip suppresses transverse unstable modes, allowing primarily bands whose <concentration> varies along its long axis and is nearly uniform across its width.

This is a mechanism for shape-dependent pattern selection during growth: increasing tissue width can admit additional transverse modes without changing the local chemical interactions. Linear instability predicts initial wavelengths and permitted directions, not the eventual stable spot or stripe pattern; nonlinear saturation and boundary geometry determine that later outcome. Zero-flux boundaries also mean these modes arise through internal redistribution and reaction rather than externally prescribed chemical <concentrations>.

Let $F(x,y)=(y,x-\Omega y-x^3-x^2y)$. Every term changes sign when both variables change sign, so $F(-x,-y)=-F(x,y)$. Consequently the <derivative> of $(-x(t),-y(t))$ equals $F(-x(t),-y(t))$, proving the stated <rotational symmetry>.

**This is equivariance under a half-turn, not time reversibility.** <Reversibility of an autonomous differential equation> requires a reverser $R$ satisfying $F(Rz)=-DR(z)F(z)$, so that $Rz(-t)$ is a solution. The half-turn $R=-I$ would require $F(-z)=F(z)$, the opposite of what holds. The usual mechanical candidate $R(x,y)=(x,-y)$ also fails when damping is present: its second equation would require $(\Omega+x^2)y=-(\Omega+x^2)y$ identically. The <symmetry> relates two orbits with the same direction of time, and does not reverse their arrows.

The planar <divergence> is $\nabla\cdot F=-(\Omega+x^2)<0$ for $\Omega>0$. Since the plane is simply connected, the <Bendixson-Dulac theorem> excludes any nonconstant <periodic orbit>: the flux across a closed trajectory would be zero because the <vector field> is tangent, whereas <Green's theorem> makes it the strictly negative integral of the <divergence> over the enclosed region.

Alternatively define $E=y^2/2-x^2/2+x^4/4$. Direct differentiation gives
$$
\boxed{\dot E=-(\Omega+x^2)y^2.}
$$
A nonconstant periodic solution would require the integral of this strictly dissipative expression over a period to vanish. That forces $y\equiv0$ and hence an <equilibrium>, a contradiction. This energy argument identifies the damping mechanism behind the geometric criterion.

= Solution

The <equilibria> satisfy $y=0$ and $x-x^3=0$, giving $(0,0)$ and $(\pm1,0)$. At the origin,
$$
J_0=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&-\Omega\end{pmatrix},\qquad\lambda_\pm=\frac{-\Omega\pm\sqrt{\Omega^2+4}}2.
$$
Their product is $-1$, so **the origin is a <saddle equilibrium> for every real $\Omega$**.

At either nonzero <equilibrium>,
$$
J_\pm=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-2&-(\Omega+1)\end{pmatrix},\qquad\lambda^2+(\Omega+1)\lambda+2=0.
$$
The <determinant> is positive and the <trace> is $-(\Omega+1)$. Thus both <equilibria> are attracting for $\Omega>-1$, repelling for $\Omega<-1$, and have purely imaginary <eigenvalues> $\pm i\sqrt2$ at $\Omega=-1$. More precisely they are <stable focus>[stable foci] for $-1<\Omega<-1+2\sqrt2$ and <stable nodes> for $\Omega>-1+2\sqrt2$; they are un<stable focus>[stable foci] for $-1-2\sqrt2<\Omega<-1$ and un<stable nodes> for $\Omega<-1-2\sqrt2$. At the two discriminant-zero endpoints they are repeated-eigenvalue <degenerate nodes>, with a nondiagonalizable Jacobian. At $\Omega=-1$, <linearization> alone does not determine nonlinear stability or prove a nonlinear centre.

= Solution

At $\Omega=-2$, the origin has stable and unstable <eigenvalues> $1-\sqrt2$ and $1+\sqrt2$; the two nonzero <equilibria> are <unstable focus>[repelling foci] with <eigenvalues> $(1\pm i\sqrt7)/2$. A compatible portrait has one attracting closed orbit surrounding all three <equilibria>. Its total enclosed index is $+1+1-1=+1$, as a <periodic orbit> requires. Its half-turn image is also attracting, so uniqueness of the attracting cycle makes that cycle half-turn symmetric. Orbits spiral away from either <focus equilibrium> and approach the surrounding cycle; the stable separatrices approach the saddle instead. The figure integrates representative trajectories and shows the local saddle directions.

These trajectories cannot escape to infinity. A <Liénard boundedness function> supplies one explicit boundedness argument using the Liénard coordinate $z=y+F(x)$ with $F(x)=x^3/3-2x$ and $g(x)=x^3-x$. Then $\dot x=z-F(x)$ and $\dot z=-g(x)$. Put $V=x^4/4-x^2/2$ and $W=V+z^2/2-xz$. This function is coercive after addition of a constant. Its <derivative> obeys
$$
\dot W=-gF-z^2+Fz+xg\leq-\tfrac12z^2-\frac5{18}x^6+\frac83x^4-x^2,
$$
using $Fz\leq(z^2+F^2)/2$. The right side is negative outside a <compact> region, yielding bounded forward motion.

In the displayed portrait, generic non-equilibrium initial states tend to the cycle; points on the saddle's <stable manifold> tend to the origin; and initial data exactly at a repelling <focus equilibrium> remain there. The <Poincaré-Bendixson theorem> excludes a chaotic <compact> limit set for this smooth planar flow. Strictly, the assumption gives uniqueness only of an attracting <periodic orbit>: it does not by itself exclude exceptional nonattracting periodic or separatrix limit sets. Those would need additional global information if one wanted a classification stronger than the compatible portrait requested.

First assume $\omega_0\ne0$ and regard $\theta$ modulo $2\pi$. The section $\theta=0$ has return time $T=2\pi/|\omega_0|$. Solving the resonantly forced oscillator from this section gives
$$
x(t)=x_0\cos(\omega_0t)+\frac{y_0}{\omega_0}\sin(\omega_0t)+\frac{\gamma}{2\omega_0}t\sin(\omega_0t).
$$
At $t=T$, the sine vanishes and the cosine is one, while differentiating the secular term adds $\gamma T/2$ to $y$. Hence the <Poincaré map> is
$$
\boxed{P(x,y)=(x,y+\gamma T/2).}
$$
For $\gamma>0$, every iterate has $y_n=y_0+n\gamma T/2\to\infty$. Thus every oscillator trajectory is unbounded, regardless of its initial amplitude. For an arbitrary section phase $\theta_0$, the return map translates by $(\gamma T\sin\theta_0/(2\omega_0),\gamma T\cos\theta_0/2)$, which is still nonzero. At $\gamma=0$ the map is the identity, and all $(x,y)$ motions are bounded harmonic oscillations, including the zero-amplitude <equilibrium>.

**The zero-frequency case allowed by the printed parameter range is different.** If $\omega_0=0$, then $\theta=\theta_0$ is constant and $x=x_0+y_0t+\tfrac12\gamma\cos\theta_0t^2$. There is no phase-crossing Poincaré section. For $\gamma>0$, choosing $\cos\theta_0=0$ and $y_0=0$ gives bounded <equilibria>, contradicting an unqualified “all trajectories” statement. For $\gamma=0$ at zero frequency, free-particle motion is unbounded unless $y_0=0$. The <resonance> conclusion therefore requires the natural nonzero-frequency assumption.

At $r=1$ and $r=3$ the relevant multiplier has magnitude one, so <linearization> is inconclusive. At $r=1$, $x_{n+1}=x_n-x_n^2$ attracts small positive points to zero nonlinearly.

Factoring $f^2(x)-x$ and removing its fixed-point factors gives $r^2x^2-r(r+1)x+(r+1)=0$. Its distinct roots are
$$
\boxed{x_\pm=\frac{r+1\pm\sqrt{(r-3)(r+1)}}{2r},\qquad r>3.}
$$
They map to each other, giving a genuine <two-cycle> only beyond $r_0=3$. The cycle multiplier is
$$
M=f'(x_+)f'(x_-)=4+2r-r^2.
$$
Solving $|M|<1$ in $r>3$ gives
$$
\boxed{3<r<1+\sqrt6.}
$$
At $r=3$, the two roots coalesce with $x_*=2/3$, where the fixed-point multiplier is $-1$: a supercritical <period-doubling bifurcation> creates the attracting <two-cycle>. At the upper endpoint its multiplier reaches $-1$, marking the next loss of <linear stability>. The <two-cycle> is superstable at $r=1+\sqrt5$, where $M=0$. At exactly $r=3$ the <fixed point> remains weakly attracting through higher-order terms: for $\eta=x-2/3$, the second iterate is $\eta\mapsto\eta-18\eta^3-27\eta^4$, showing alternating perturbations decay slowly.

For a nearby orbit, linearized separation satisfies $\delta_{i+1}=f'(x_i)\delta_i$. Repeated use of the <chain rule> gives $\delta_n=\delta_0\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}f'(x_i)$ to first order. Taking the logarithm of its magnitude and dividing by $n$ gives the exponential growth rate
$$
\boxed{\lambda=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\log\left|\frac{\delta_n}{\delta_0}\right|=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\log|f'(x_i)|.}
$$
The infinitesimal-perturbation limit is taken before interpreting long-time growth; finite separations eventually leave the linear regime. The limit is assumed to exist, with a limsup used when needed.

On a $p$-cycle, blocks of $p$ successive <derivatives> repeat. If $M=(f^p)'(x_0)=\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}f'(x_i)$, the remainder of fewer than $p$ terms has vanishing contribution to the long-time average and
$$
\boxed{\lambda=\frac1p\log|M|.}
$$
Linear attraction means $|M|<1$, giving $\lambda<0$. A superstable cycle has $M=0$, meaning at least one <derivative> in the cycle vanishes. Its infinitesimal return multiplier is zero and the corresponding logarithmic exponent is $-\infty$. This concerns the <derivative> cocycle; actual finite perturbations can converge by higher-order rather than literally vanishing in one step.

The Lorenz <vector field> has constant <divergence> $\nabla\cdot F=-\sigma-1-b$. <Liouville formula for a fundamental matrix> for the <derivative> of the flow therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\det D\Phi_t=e^{-(\sigma+1+b)t},\qquad V(t)=V(0)e^{-(\sigma+1+b)t}.}
$$
This concerns transported three-dimensional phase volume, not the Euclidean distance between each pair of trajectories.

A quasiperiodic orbit with the usual smooth two-frequency <torus> closure would lie on an invariant embedded two-torus. That <torus> bounds a solid <torus> of positive finite volume in $\mathbb R^3$. Since the <vector field> is tangent to its boundary, its outward flux is zero, contradicting the <divergence theorem>, whose volume integral is $-(\sigma+1+b)V<0$. This <negative-divergence obstruction to invariant tori> excludes such quasiperiodic tori. The argument uses global negative <divergence> and the enclosed volume; saying only that an attracting <torus> has zero volume would not exclude it.

At an <equilibrium> the sum of <eigenvalues> is $-(\sigma+1+b)<0$, so they cannot all have positive real parts: no <equilibrium> is a fully repelling source. For a <periodic orbit> of period $T$, one <Floquet multiplier> equals one in the flow direction. The product of the other two is $e^{-(\sigma+1+b)T}<1$, so they cannot both have modulus greater than one: no <periodic orbit> is fully repelling either. Saddle <equilibria> and saddle <periodic orbits> remain possible; contraction of volume does not imply contraction in every direction.

At $r=1$ its <eigenvalues> are $0,-(\sigma+1),-b$: <linearization> has a neutral direction and cannot alone prove nonlinear stability. Given the source's global attraction for $r<1$, there are no nontrivial <limit cycles> or persistent chaotic invariant dynamics in that regime; all initial states tend to the origin.

To establish the local pitchfork, one needs the simple <eigenvalue> to cross zero, the two symmetry-related branches to exist and acquire the expected local stability, and nondegeneracy of the nonlinear coefficient. The <derivative> of the crossing <eigenvalue> is $\sigma/(\sigma+1)>0$. At $C_\pm$, the <characteristic polynomial> is
$$
\boxed{\lambda^3+(\sigma+b+1)\lambda^2+b(\sigma+r)\lambda+2\sigma b(r-1)=0.}
$$
For $r>1$ sufficiently close to one, all coefficients are positive and $(\sigma+b+1)b(\sigma+r)>2\sigma b(r-1)$, so the cubic <Routh-Hurwitz criterion> makes both new <equilibria> stable. A useful operational definition of <chaos> for a deterministic flow is bounded, nonperiodic long-time dynamics on an <invariant set> with <sensitive dependence on initial conditions>: arbitrarily close starting states can separate by a fixed macroscopic amount. Boundedness distinguishes recurrent irregular dynamics from simple exponential escape; aperiodicity excludes a <limit cycle>; sensitivity distinguishes chaotic unpredictability from a regular quasiperiodic signal. These are properties of the asymptotic dynamics, not a short irregular transient. One precise topological convention, <Devaney chaos> on a <compact> <invariant set>, instead specifies <topological transitivity>, dense <periodic orbits>, and sensitive dependence. Transitivity expresses indecomposability of the recurrent motion, while dense <periodic orbits> mean every region contains unstable recurrent structure. The convention should be stated because these formulations are not identical in complete generality.

An <attractor> is an <invariant set> approached by trajectories from a neighbourhood or positive-measure basin, according to the adopted <attractor> convention. Calling it strange refers to irregular, typically <fractal geometry> rather than a smooth point, cycle or <torus>. **Strangeness alone does not force <chaos>.** <Strange nonchaotic attractors> provide counterexamples: geometrically irregular attracting sets can lack positive exponential separation. Primary examples appear in https://doi.org/10.1016/0167-2789(84)90282-3[Strange attractors that are not chaotic, Physica D 13 (1984), pages 261–268]{ref}. This geometric convention does not assert that every such example occurs in the autonomous three-variable Lorenz family.

Two complementary numerical diagnostics are to estimate the largest <Lyapunov exponent> and to construct a return map. For the first, integrate the <variational equation> $\dot\eta=DF(x(t))\eta$ alongside the Lorenz trajectory, repeatedly normalize $\eta$, and average the logarithms of the accumulated stretch factors after discarding a transient. A positive converged exponent suggests sensitive exponential separation; the full spectrum should include a zero flow exponent and have sum $-(\sigma+1+b)$, providing checks. For the second, record transverse crossings of a Poincaré section or successive maxima of a state variable, then look for the stretching and folding of an aperiodic return set rather than one point or a finite cycle. Nearby-orbit separation or a broadband <power spectrum> can add evidence, but plots alone do not prove <chaos>. Repeat with smaller integration step, longer runs and several <initial conditions> to distinguish numerical error, long transients and coexistence of <attractors> from persistent dynamics.
