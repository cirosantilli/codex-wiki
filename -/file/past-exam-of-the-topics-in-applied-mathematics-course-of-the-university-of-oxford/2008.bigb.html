Write $P(N)=f(N,0)$. Its three positive <zeros of a function> are $1/2,1,2$, and expansion and <differentiation> give
$$
P(N)=-N^3+\frac72N^2-\frac72N+1,\qquad P'(N)=-3N^2+7N-\frac72.
$$
Thus its <local minimum> and maximum occur respectively at
$$
N_m=\frac{7-\sqrt7}{6},\qquad N_M=\frac{7+\sqrt7}{6},
$$
with values
$$
P(N_m)=\frac5{54}-\frac{7\sqrt7}{108}\simeq-0.0788913,\qquad
P(N_M)=\frac5{54}+\frac{7\sqrt7}{108}\simeq0.264076.
$$
The <function graph> approaches the value $1$ as $N\downarrow0$, decreases through its first zero to the minimum, increases through its second zero to the maximum, and then decreases through $2$ to $-\infty$. This fixes the shape and all axis crossings of the <cubic polynomial>.

Changing $a$ translates the <function graph> vertically without moving either <stationary point>. For $a=1/2$ both extrema are above the horizontal axis: their heights are approximately $0.421109$ and $0.764076$. For $a=-1/2$ both are below it, at approximately $-0.578891$ and $-0.235924$. The <function graph> for $a=1/2$ therefore has only its final positive-axis crossing, whereas the <function graph> for $a=-1/2$ has only its first positive-axis crossing. The original plots mark the extrema and also show the <equilibrium point> branches used below.

$$
e_{t+1}=-\frac34e_t+O(e_t^2).
$$
For sufficiently small nonzero deviations the sign reverses each step and their magnitudes contract. **The local approach is an alternating, geometrically damped oscillation**, with asymptotic contraction factor $3/4$ per iterate.

First establish a uniform upper bound. Direct subtraction gives
$$
F(N)-\frac32=-\frac N2(2N^2-7N+7)<0\qquad(N>0),
$$
because the <quadratic polynomial> has positive leading coefficient and <discriminant> $49-56=-7$. The assumed positivity of $F(N_0)$ therefore places $N_1$ in $(0,3/2)$.

This interval is <forward invariant>. On $[0,1/2]$ and on $[1,3/2]$, the three factors in $P(N)$ have nonnegative product, hence $F(N)\ge1/2$. On $[1/2,1]$ the product is nonpositive, but
$$
|P(N)|=(N-\tfrac12)(1-N)(2-N)\le\frac12\frac12\frac32=\frac38,
$$
so $F(N)\ge1/8>0$. Together with the upper bound this proves $F((0,3/2))\subset(0,3/2)$. Induction now gives
$$
\boxed{N_t>0\quad\text{for every }t\ge0.}
$$
The hypothesis on the first image is essential for initial values outside that <invariant interval>.

For an autonomous scalar differential equation, <linear stability> of a simple <equilibrium point> $N_e$ is determined by the <derivative> $f_N(N_e,a)=P'(N_e)$: a negative <derivative> attracts nearby solutions and a positive <derivative> gives <repulsion in a dynamical system> of nearby solutions.

Here $f(0,a)=1+a\ge1/2>0$ throughout the prescribed <mathematical model parameter> range, while $f(N,a)\to-\infty$ as $N\to\infty$. If there is only one <equilibrium point>, the <function graph> must cross the axis on one of its decreasing branches, outside the interval between its two extrema. The crossing is simple and $P'(N_e)<0$. Thus **the unique <equilibrium point> is linearly <asymptotically stable>**. At a <saddle-node bifurcation> there are two distinct <equilibrium points>, one of them double, so that case does not fall under the one-equilibrium hypothesis.

**Yes: there is a quasistatic <hysteresis> loop.** <Equilibrium points> lie on the <mathematical model parameter> curve $a=-P(N)$. Its two <saddle-node bifurcations> occur at the <stationary points> computed in part (a), at <mathematical model parameter> values
$$
a_{\rm down}=-P(N_M)=-\frac5{54}-\frac{7\sqrt7}{108}\simeq-0.264076,
$$
$$
a_{\rm up}=-P(N_m)=-\frac5{54}+\frac{7\sqrt7}{108}\simeq0.0788913.
$$
Between these values there are two <attracting> branches separated by a <repelling> branch. Starting below $a_{\rm down}$ and increasing $a$ slowly, a solution follows the lower <attracting> branch. This branch persists through the <bistability> interval and ends only at $a_{\rm up}$, where it meets the <repelling> branch in a <saddle-node bifurcation>. The solution then moves to the upper <attracting> branch.

If the direction of <mathematical model parameter> change is reversed, the upper branch persists until $a_{\rm down}$, where a different <saddle-node bifurcation> forces the downward transition. The two transition values differ, and both lie inside the allowed range $[-1/2,1/2]$. Thus the <biological population> selected at an intermediate <mathematical model parameter> value depends on its prior branch. This is the <equilibrium point> <hysteresis> shown in the branch diagram; finite-rate passage can additionally delay the jumps near the <saddle-node bifurcations>.

The term $rIS$ is a mass-action incidence rate: each <infective individual> makes potentially transmitting contacts with <susceptible individuals>, and the total rate is proportional to the product of the two <compartment> sizes. An <infection> transfers one animal from $S$ to $I$, accounting for opposite signs in their equations. The <per-capita mortality rate> of an <infective individual> is $a$, giving transfer $aI$ from $I$ to $R$.

There are no births, recoveries or additional deaths in this model. Adding the three equations gives $\dot S+\dot I+\dot R=0$, so the total initial <biological population> is conserved when the dead <compartment> is included. Before <quarantine>, $S$ decreases whenever $I,S>0$; after effective isolation no further transmission is permitted.

Write $S_0=S_{initial}$ and $I_0=I_{initial}$. Until <quarantine> and while $I,S>0$, division of the equations gives
$$
\frac{dI}{dS}=-1+\frac{a}{rS}.
$$
<Integration> from the initial state yields the <first integral>
$$
I+S-\frac ar\log S=I_0+S_0-\frac ar\log S_0.
$$
Let $S_*$ be the <susceptible> count at the first time $I=I_*$. It is the <root of an equation> reached on the decreasing-$S$ outbreak trajectory of
$$
\boxed{I_*+S_*-\frac ar\log S_*=I_0+S_0-\frac ar\log S_0.}
$$
For a strictly rising threshold crossing this is the larger admissible <root of an equation>, with $a/r<S_*<S_0$; if the threshold equals the peak, $S_*=a/r$. The assumption that <quarantine> is reached selects the physically relevant branch and excludes a merely hypothetical declining-branch crossing after an earlier one.

Since there is no <incubation period> here, all removals from $S$ have already become <infection cases> by <quarantine>. No further <susceptible individuals> are <infected> after isolation. The requested number among the initially <susceptible individuals> is therefore
$$
\boxed{S_0-S_*.}
$$

The delayed influx $rI(t-\tau)S(t-\tau)$ represents animals exposed one <incubation period> earlier who enter the <infective individual> <compartment> now. They die at the instantaneous rate $aI(t)$ once in that <compartment>. The displayed bookkeeping removes an animal from $S$ at the same delayed time that it enters $I$, preserving $S+I+R$.

For this bookkeeping, $S$ must include the exposed animals which have not yet completed <incubation period>. If $S$ were instead defined as strictly unexposed <susceptible individuals>, its loss term would be the current exposure rate and a separate latent compartment would be needed. The calculations here follow the printed <delay differential equation>. A full initial-value problem for a <delay differential equation> also needs a history on $[-\tau,0]$; the following <small-delay expansion of an epidemic transfer rate> concerns a smooth compatible history and the regular regime assumed by the question.

In the first-order <Taylor expansion> correction it suffices to use the zeroth-order <derivatives>. They give
$$
\dot I\,S+I\dot S=(rIS-aI)S+I(-rIS)=IS\{r(S-I)-a\}.
$$
The delayed product is consequently
$$
I(t-\tau)S(t-\tau)=IS+\tau IS\{a+r(I-S)\}+O(\tau^2).
$$
Multiplying by $r$ and substituting into the two transfer equations produces
$$
\boxed{f(I,S,r,a)=r\{a+r(I-S)\},\qquad g(I,S,r,a)=-r\{a+r(I-S)\}.}
$$
The opposite signs of $f$ and $g$ are also required by <conservation law> of $S+I+R$: this correction changes the timing of the transfer between $S$ and $I$, while leaving the instantaneous death term unchanged.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

At the retained order define $K=1+\tau r(I-S)$. Neglecting terms of order $a\tau$ in the incidence correction gives
$$
\dot I=rISK-aI,\qquad \dot S=-rISK.
$$
Dividing and expanding consistently,
$$
\frac{dI}{dS}=-1+\frac{a}{rSK}
=-1+\frac{a}{rS}+O(a\tau,\tau^2).
$$
Thus the <susceptible> count $S_*$ at the first threshold crossing obeys the same retained-order equation as before:
$$
I_*+S_*-\frac ar\log S_*=I_0+S_0-\frac ar\log S_0.
$$
It is important to distinguish completed case transfers at <quarantine> from all <infections> already initiated. Let the <quarantine> time be $T$. Isolation prevents exposures after $T$, but animals exposed during $(T-\tau,T)$ still finish their <incubation period> during $(T,T+\tau)$. Their number, in the printed bookkeeping, is
$$
E_* =\int_{T-\tau}^{T}rI(s)S(s)\,ds
=\tau rI_*S_*+O(\tau^2).
$$
The <infection cases> transferred by time $T$ among the initial $S$ class number $S_0-S_*$. Adding those pending <infection cases> gives the eventual number who catch the <infectious disease>:
$$
\boxed{S_0-S_*+\tau rI_*S_*\quad\text{to the retained order}.}
$$
This adds only <infections> caused by exposures before isolation; it does not allow secondary transmission after <quarantine>. If one asks instead only for the transfers completed by the instant of <quarantine>, the answer is $S_0-S_*$. The distinction is necessary because the model keeps incubating animals in its $S$ <compartment>.

An unbiased <random walk> of <bacteria> leads, on the continuum scale, to <diffusion> with coefficient $D$, represented by $DB_{XX}$. The <cell proliferation> rate $\nu GB$ is proportional both to the available <biological growth factor> concentration and to the <bacterial density>. Consumption of <biological growth factor> at rate $\mu GB$ gives its negative reaction term.

There is no <diffusion> term for $G$ because the <biological growth factor> is attached to the substrate rather than freely transported. There is also no <chemotactic drift> in this model: the specified motion is random, while the <biological growth factor> changes <cell proliferation> rather than directional movement. Both reaction terms vanish if either <bacteria> or <biological growth factor> are absent.

Choose a positive reference <biological growth factor> concentration $G_{ref}$, for example its <concentration> ahead of the <travelling wave>. Set
$$
T=T_0t,\quad X=X_0x,\quad G=G_{ref}g,\quad B=B_0b,
$$
where
$$
\boxed{T_0=\frac1{\nu G_{ref}},\qquad X_0=\sqrt{\frac D{\nu G_{ref}}},\qquad B_0=\frac{\nu G_{ref}}\mu.}
$$
The <chain rule> then transforms the equations into
$$
b_t=\frac{DT_0}{X_0^2}b_{xx}+\nu G_{ref}T_0gb,\qquad g_t=-\mu B_0T_0gb.
$$
Each displayed coefficient is one with the chosen scales. Hence the <dimensionless> system is
$$
\boxed{b_t=b_{xx}+gb,\qquad g_t=-gb.}
$$
The free choice of $G_{ref}$ expresses the freedom to normalize the as-yet unspecified <concentration> scale.

Take profiles $b=b(y)$ and $g=g(y)$ with $y=x-ct$, and use a prime for <differentiation> with respect to $y$. The <chain rule> gives $b_t=-cb'$, $b_{xx}=b''$ and $g_t=-cg'$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{b''+cb'+gb=0,\qquad cg'=gb.}
$$
These are coupled <ordinary differential equations> for a right-moving <travelling wave> with speed $c>0$. A first-order formulation, using $v=b'$, is $b'=v$, $v'=-cv-gb$, $g'=gb/c$. In particular a positive profile has $g'>0$, consistent with depleted substrate behind the <travelling front> and fresh <biological growth factor> ahead.

Substitute $gb=cg'$ into the <bacterial> profile equation. This gives
$$
b''+cb'+cg'=0,
$$
which integrates once to
$$
b'+c(b+g)=C.
$$
At the asymptotic constant states the profile <derivative> tends to zero. The state ahead of the <travelling wave>, where $b\to0$ and $g\to1$, therefore gives $C=c$. The state behind it, where $g\to0$ and $b\to b_0$, gives $C=cb_0$. Since $c>0$,
$$
\boxed{b_0=1,\qquad b'+c(b+g)=c.}
$$
This <first integral> expresses the matching between biomass produced and <biological growth factor> consumed after the chosen <nondimensionalization>.

At the leading edge $b\to0$ and $g\to1$. The linearized <bacterial> equation is
$$
b''+cb'+b=0,
$$
with <characteristic exponents>
$$
\lambda_\pm=\frac{-c\pm\sqrt{c^2-4}}2.
$$
For $0<c<2$ they form a <nonreal> <complex conjugate> pair. A nonzero profile approaching the <travelling front> then has an exponentially decaying oscillatory leading term, and repeatedly changes sign. This is incompatible with the assumed positive <bacterial density>. The leading edge must therefore have real <characteristic exponents>, requiring
$$
\boxed{c\ge2.}
$$
At $c=2$ the repeated <characteristic exponent> is $-1$ and <polynomial> factors in the asymptotic profile may occur. The argument supplies a necessary lower bound; it is not an independent existence proof for every speed satisfying it.

The <first integral> and $b_0=1$ yield the linear identity $b'+cb=c(1-g)$. Multiply by the <integrating factor> $e^{cy}$ and integrate from $-\infty$. Since the bounded limit $b(-\infty)=1$ makes $e^{cy}b(y)\to0$ there,
$$
b(y)=c\int_{-\infty}^{y}e^{-c(y-s)}(1-g(s))\,ds
=1-c\int_{-\infty}^{y}e^{-c(y-s)}g(s)\,ds.
$$
Positivity of $g$ makes the last <integral> nonnegative, and strictly positive at any finite point of a strictly positive <travelling wave>. Thus
$$
\boxed{0<b(y)<b_0=1\quad\text{for finite }y,}
$$
with equality approached only at the far-behind boundary. This bound uses the <first integral> rather than an unsupported assumption that the <bacterial> profile is monotone.

For the modified strain the <travelling wave> equations are
$$
b''+cb'+gb(1-\eta b)=0,\qquad cg'=gb.
$$
Integrating the second across the entire <travelling wave> gives $\int_{\mathbb R}gb\,dy=c$, since the <biological growth factor> profile still goes from $0$ to $1$. Integrating the first, using the constant asymptotic states and vanishing <derivative> at infinity, gives
$$
cb_1=\int_{\mathbb R}gb(1-\eta b)\,dy
=c-\eta\int_{\mathbb R}gb^2\,dy.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{b_1=1-\frac\eta c\int_{\mathbb R}g(y)b(y)^2\,dy<1=b_0.}
$$
The subtracted <integral> is strictly positive because the profiles are positive and $\eta>0$. It is finite: $b$ is bounded by continuity and its finite endpoint limits, and $\int gb=c$. The additional density-dependent inhibition therefore reduces the <bacterial density> left behind; positivity of $b_1$ is part of the assumed positive-wave solution.

The term $\alpha\nabla^2q$ is cell <diffusion> arising from random movement. <Chemotaxis> up the <concentration gradient> gives the cell <flux> $\beta q\nabla c$, whose negative <divergence> is the transport contribution $-\beta\nabla\cdot(q\nabla c)$. The reaction term $q(1-q)$ gives growth governed by the <logistic differential equation> with <dimensionless> <carrying capacity> one.

The <chemoattractant> itself diffuses, is produced by cells at rate $\kappa\eta q$, and decays at rate $\kappa c$. The homogeneous <Neumann boundary conditions> suppress the normal <diffusive> <flux> of each quantity. They also suppress the normal <chemotactic cell flux> because the normal <derivative> of $c$ vanishes. Thus the rectangular boundary is impermeable, and the spatially integrated cell count changes through <cell proliferation> rather than transport across that boundary.

For spatially homogeneous <perturbations> write $q=1+u$ and $c=\eta+v$. The linearized reaction equations are
$$
\begin{pmatrix}\dot u\\\dot v\end{pmatrix}
=J\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\boxed{J=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\\kappa\eta&-\kappa\end{pmatrix}.}

This <triangular matrix> has <eigenvalues> $-1$ and $-\kappa$, both negative. Hence the <homogeneous steady state> is linearly <asymptotically stable> against homogeneous <perturbations>. Even when $\kappa=1$, where the two <eigenvalues> coincide and a <polynomial> factor may occur in the <matrix exponential>, all such <perturbations> still decay.

For the <cosine> mode in the question, the <Laplacian> acts by multiplication by $-s$, where
$$
s=k_{n,m}^2=\pi^2\left(\frac{n^2}{L_1^2}+\frac{m^2}{L_2^2}\right).

These modes obey the <Neumann boundary conditions>. In the <chemotactic> term, put $q=1+u$, $c=\eta+v$. Then $\nabla c=\nabla v$, and $q\nabla c=\nabla v+u\nabla v$; the second product is a <quadratic polynomial> in <perturbation> amplitude. Thus the <linearized equations> are
$$
u_t=\alpha\nabla^2u-\beta\nabla^2v-u,\qquad
v_t=\nabla^2v+\kappa\eta u-\kappa v.
$$
Substituting the modal time dependence gives
$$
\lambda\begin{pmatrix}q_{n,m}\\c_{n,m}\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}-1-\alpha s&\beta s\\\kappa\eta&-\kappa-s\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}q_{n,m}\\c_{n,m}\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Separating this <matrix> into its transport part and the reaction <Jacobian matrix> gives the required form, with the same $J$ as above.

For the modal <matrix> $M(s)$, $s\ge0$, its <trace> is always negative:
$$
\operatorname{tr}M(s)=-(1+\kappa)-(\alpha+1)s<0.
$$
Its <determinant> is
$$
D(s)=(1+\alpha s)(\kappa+s)-\beta\kappa\eta s
=\alpha s^2+(1+\alpha\kappa-\beta\kappa\eta)s+\kappa.
$$
The proposed inequality is exactly $\beta\kappa\eta<\alpha\kappa+1$. It makes the coefficient of $s$ positive, while the leading and constant coefficients are already positive. Therefore $D(s)>0$ for every $s\ge0$, independent of which discrete wave numbers the domain allows.

A real two-by-two <matrix> with negative <trace> and positive <determinant> has both <eigenvalues> with negative <real part>: if they are real they have positive product and negative sum, and if <nonreal> their common <real part> is half the <trace>. Every mode therefore decays, proving **<linear stability> for all domain sizes** under the stated sufficient condition.

= Solution
{parent=5}

A <fixed point> $x_*$ satisfies $f(x_*,\mu)=x_*$. A <perturbation> obeys $u_{n+1}=m u_n+O(u_n^2)$, where the <periodic-orbit multiplier> is $m=f_x(x_*,\mu)$. It is linearly <attracting> for $|m|<1$ and <repelling> for $|m|>1$; at $m=\pm1$ nonlinear terms decide <Lyapunov stability>. A crossing through $+1$ can produce a saddle-node, transcritical or <pitchfork bifurcation>, depending on the <mathematical model parameter> dependence and <symmetry>. A crossing through $-1$ generically gives a <period-doubling bifurcation>. A real one-dimensional <function> cannot have the <complex conjugate> pair needed for a <Neimark–Sacker bifurcation>.

For a genuine <period-two orbit>, study the <return map> $f\circ f$ at either point. By the <chain rule> its <periodic-orbit multiplier> is
$$
\boxed{m_2=f_x(x_1,\mu)f_x(x_2,\mu).}
$$
Its modulus determines <linear stability> by the same criterion, now per two <iterates>.

For the <real quadratic iteration map>, the fixed-point equation gives
$$
x_\pm=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt{1+4\mu}}2,\qquad \mu\ge-\frac14.
$$
The <derivative> is $-2x$. The lower branch $x_-$ is <repelling> whenever $\mu>-1/4$. The upper branch satisfies $|-2x_+|<1$ exactly for
$$
\boxed{-\frac14<\mu<\frac34.}
$$
At $\mu=-1/4$ the two <fixed points> merge in a <saddle-node bifurcation>. With $u=x+1/2$ at that <mathematical model parameter>, $u'=u-u^2$, so the point attracts from the right and is <repelling> from the left. At $\mu=3/4$, $x_+=1/2$ has <periodic-orbit multiplier> $-1$ and undergoes a <period-doubling bifurcation>. At the threshold itself $u'= -u-u^2$ and the two-step return is $u''=u-2u^3-u^4$, proving weak <asymptotic stability> there despite the inconclusive linear test.

Factor the second-iterate equation:
$$
f(f(x),\mu)-x=-(x^2+x-\mu)(x^2-x+1-\mu).
$$
Removing the fixed-point factor yields the <periodic orbit> points
$$
x_1=\frac{1-\sqrt{4\mu-3}}2,\qquad x_2=\frac{1+\sqrt{4\mu-3}}2.
$$
They are distinct and exchanged by $f$ exactly when $\mu>3/4$; at equality they coincide with the <fixed point>. Their product is $1-\mu$, hence their <periodic-orbit multiplier> is $4(1-\mu)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\text{the two-cycle exists for }\mu>\frac34,\quad\text{and is linearly attracting for }\frac34<\mu<\frac54.}
$$
It is <repelling> for $\mu>5/4$ and reaches its next <period-doubling bifurcation> threshold at $\mu=5/4$. At that exact endpoint it is weakly <attracting>: if $f^2$ is expanded about either <periodic orbit> point as $v'=-v+Av^2+Bv^3+O(v^4)$, then $A=2\mu-6x_i^2$, $B=-4x_i$, and $A^2+B=20\pm10\sqrt2>0$. Its second return is $v''=v-2(A^2+B)v^3+O(v^4)$.

At $\mu=2$ put $h(y)=4\sin^2(\pi y)-2=-2\cos(2\pi y)$. The double-angle identity gives
$$
f(h(y),2)=2-4\cos^2(2\pi y)=-2\cos(4\pi y)=h(2y\bmod1).
$$
This verifies the printed relation to the <Bernoulli shift>. Precisely, it is a <semiconjugacy>: $h(y)=h(1-y)$, so it is not invertible on the circle. To obtain a genuine interval <topological conjugacy>, use the <homeomorphism> $H(u)=-2\cos(\pi u)$ from $[0,1]$ onto $[-2,2]$, and the full <tent map>
$$
T(u)=\begin{cases}2u,&0\le u\le1/2,\\2-2u,&1/2\le u\le1.\end{cases}
$$
The same identity proves $f(H(u),2)=H(T(u))$.

The full <tent map> has <dense periodic points>, is <topologically mixing> and has <sensitive dependence on initial conditions>. To see these properties directly, its $n$th iterate has $2^n$ monotone branches on dyadic intervals of length $2^{-n}$, each mapping onto $[0,1]$. Every nonempty open interval contains such a branch domain, and a later iterate covers all of $[0,1]$, proving <topological mixing>. On any branch, the <function graph> of the iterate has a <fixed point> by the <intermediate value theorem>, so <periodic points> occur in every open interval. Given any initial point and any neighborhood, some iterate of that neighborhood covers the interval; one can choose a nearby point whose image differs from the initial point's image by at least a fixed positive distance. This proves <sensitive dependence on initial conditions>. <Uniform continuity> of the inverse <homeomorphism> transfers these properties to $f$ on $[-2,2]$.

Consequently the <real quadratic iteration map> is <chaotic> on its <invariant interval> in the Devaney sense: **<topological transitivity>, <dense periodic points> and <sensitive dependence on initial conditions>**, with all these orbits bounded. There are also aperiodic orbits: <periodic points> form a countable union of finite sets of <roots of an equation> of the equations $f^n(x)=x$, whereas the interval is uncountable. The <semiconjugacy> alone would not justify treating $h$ as invertible; the tent-map argument supplies the missing rigorous transfer.

= Solution

For <integers> $k,l$,
$$
A\begin{pmatrix}x+k\\y+l\end{pmatrix}-A\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}2k+l\\k+l\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb Z^2.
$$
Thus reducing coordinates <modulo one> gives a well-defined <function> on the <torus> $\mathbb R^2/\mathbb Z^2$. Its <determinant> is one and its inverse <matrix> is the <integer matrix> $\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}$, so it induces a <bijection> of the <torus>. The <Jacobian determinant> one makes this <bijection> <area-preserving>.

Its <eigenvalues> solve $\lambda^2-3\lambda+1=0$, giving
$$
\boxed{\lambda_+=\frac{3+\sqrt5}{2}>1,\qquad\lambda_-=\frac{3-\sqrt5}{2}=\lambda_+^{-1}<1.}
$$
There are therefore expanding and contracting <eigendirections> and no <eigenvalue> on the <unit circle>: the <function> is a <hyperbolic toral automorphism>. The <Lyapunov exponents> per iterate are
$$
\boxed{\chi_+=\log\frac{3+\sqrt5}{2},\qquad\chi_-=-\log\frac{3+\sqrt5}{2}.}
$$
They sum to zero, as required by <area preservation>.

The four vertices of the unit <square> map to $(0,0),(2,1),(3,2),(1,1)$, the vertices of an area-one <parallelogram>. To reduce it <modulo one>, cut it by the <integer> grid. For each piece in the cell $[j,j+1]\times[k,k+1]$, translate by $(-j,-k)$. Up to boundary identifications these translated pieces partition the unit <square>, because the inverse <integer matrix> gives exactly one <torus> preimage to every point. The colored pieces in the original diagram show the same pieces before and after these translations.

For the <Fibonacci> identity, at $n=1$ the claimed <matrix> is $\begin{pmatrix}F_2&F_1\\F_1&F_0\end{pmatrix}=A$. If the identity holds at $n$, multiplication on the right by $A$ gives
$$
A^{n+1}=\begin{pmatrix}2F_{2n}+F_{2n-1}&F_{2n}+F_{2n-1}\\2F_{2n-1}+F_{2n-2}&F_{2n-1}+F_{2n-2}\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}F_{2n+2}&F_{2n+1}\\F_{2n+1}&F_{2n}\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Each equality uses $F_{j+1}=F_j+F_{j-1}$, with the specified indexing $F_0=F_1=1$. This proves the formula for every positive <integer> $n$.

A <fixed point> satisfies $(A-I)v\in\mathbb Z^2$. Since $A-I=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ has an inverse <integer matrix>, this forces $v\in\mathbb Z^2$, so **the origin is the only <fixed point> <modulo one>**.

For period dividing two,
$$
(A^2-I)v=\begin{pmatrix}4&3\\3&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb Z^2.
$$
The second congruence gives $y=-3x\pmod1$; inserting it into the first gives $5x=0\pmod1$. Thus the complete list is
$$
(x,y)=\left(\frac j5,-\frac{3j}5\bmod1\right),\qquad j=0,1,2,3,4.
$$
The first is the fixed origin. Direct application of $A$ exchanges the other four in exactly two pairs:
$$
\boxed{\{(1/5,2/5),(4/5,3/5)\},\qquad\{(3/5,1/5),(2/5,4/5)\}.}
$$
This both verifies the <periodic orbits> and excludes additional ones.

Finally, if $v$ is fixed by $F^p$, then $(A^p-I)v=m$ for an <integer> vector $m$. The <eigenvalues> of $A^p-I$ are $\lambda_+^p-1$ and $\lambda_-^p-1$, neither zero. Hence its inverse exists and has <rational> entries by the <adjugate matrix> formula for an <integer matrix>. Therefore $v=(A^p-I)^{-1}m$ has <rational> coordinates. The <derivative> of the <return map> is $A^p$, with <eigenvalues> $\lambda_+^p>1$ and $0<\lambda_-^p<1$. **Every <periodic point> is a hyperbolic <saddle fixed point of a map> and is <unstable>**, although <perturbations> in its <stable> <eigendirection> contract.

= Solution
{parent=7}

For $r>0$, differentiate $r^2=x^2+y^2$ and use the two differential equations:
$$
\dot r=\frac{x\dot x+y\dot y}{r}=r(a-f(x,y)),\qquad
\dot\theta=\frac{x\dot y-y\dot x}{r^2}=1.
$$
Substituting $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$ gives the required equations in <polar coordinates>. The origin is handled in <Cartesian coordinates>, where it is an <equilibrium point>; an angular coordinate is not defined there.

A <Lyapunov function> near an <equilibrium point> is a continuously differentiable <positive-definite scalar function> $V$ whose <derivative> along solutions is nonpositive. This implies local <Lyapunov stability> under the usual comparison bounds; if its <derivative> is negative definite, the <Second Lyapunov theorem> gives local <asymptotic stability>. Here take
$$
V(x,y)=\frac12(x^2+y^2),\qquad \dot V=r^2(a-f(x,y)).
$$
If $a<f(0,0)$, continuity gives a small ball and $\delta>0$ such that $f(x,y)-a\ge\delta$ inside it. Consequently $\dot V\le-2\delta V$. A sufficiently small initial sublevel set stays inside the ball, and $V(t)\le V(0)e^{-2\delta t}$. **The origin is <asymptotically stable>**, in fact locally <exponentially stable>. If $a>f(0,0)$, continuity instead gives $\dot V\ge2\delta V$ in a small ball. Every nonzero initial point there exits that ball in forward time, proving **<instability> of the origin**. These inequalities hold along all local solutions; continuity of $f$ alone need not supply uniqueness for a general <vector field>.

If $f=g(r)$, a nonzero constant radius $r_0$ satisfying $a=g(r_0)$ gives a <periodic orbit> of period $2\pi$, since $\dot\theta=1$. Linearizing the scalar radial equation there gives
$$
\dot\rho=-r_0g'(r_0)\rho+O(\rho^2).
$$
Thus the <periodic orbit> is <orbitally asymptotically stable> for $g'(r_0)>0$ and <unstable> for $g'(r_0)<0$. The direction along the <periodic orbit> is neutral, as expected for an autonomous <periodic orbit>; <orbital asymptotic stability> means <attraction in a dynamical system> to the orbit with a possible phase shift.

For the specified radial response,
$$
g(0)=\frac32,\qquad g'(r)=\frac{-2(r-1)}{[1+(r-1)^2]^2}.
$$
The origin is therefore <stable> for $0<a<3/2$ and <unstable> for $a>3/2$. The nonzero-radius equation has <roots of an equation>
$$
\boxed{r_\pm=1\pm\sqrt{\frac{2-a}{a-1}},\qquad1<a\le2,}
$$
retaining only positive radii. For $1<a<3/2$, the <square root> exceeds one, so only $r_+>1$ exists and $g'(r_+)<0$: the <periodic orbit> is <unstable>. For $3/2<a<2$, both radii are positive; $0<r_-<1$ gives $g'(r_-)>0$ and a <stable> <periodic orbit>, while $r_+>1$ remains <unstable>.

As $a$ increases through $3/2$, the <stable> small-radius <periodic orbit> emerges from the origin while the origin loses <Lyapunov stability>. This is the supercritical <Hopf-type transition with a nonsmooth radial response> requested. It follows directly from the explicit radial dynamics. There is a regularity qualification: $g(r)=3/2+r/2+O(r^2)$ near zero, so the <vector field> in <Cartesian coordinates> contains $x\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ and $y\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ terms. It is continuously differentiable but not generally twice differentiable at the origin, and the <periodic orbit> radius grows as $r_-\sim2(a-3/2)$ rather than the square-root law of a generic smooth <Hopf normal form>. One should therefore use the explicit computation here rather than invoke a smooth <Hopf bifurcation> theorem with unmet hypotheses. At $a=3/2$ itself the origin is still weakly <attracting>, because $a-g(r)<0$ for small positive $r$.

= Solution
{parent=8}

Let $S(x,y,z)=(-x,-y,z)$. In the first two components of the <vector field>, replacing $x,y$ by their negatives changes the sign of the whole component; in the third, the product $xy$ is unchanged. Thus $F(Sv)=SF(v)$, establishing the <symmetry>, with solutions mapped to solutions by $S$.

At an <equilibrium point>, positivity of $a$ makes $y=x$. The remaining equations give $x(2c-a-z)=0$ and $z=x^2/b$. Hence
$$
\boxed{O=(0,0,0),\qquad P_\pm=(\pm\sqrt{b(2c-a)},\ \pm\sqrt{b(2c-a)},\ 2c-a),}
$$
where the two nonzero <equilibrium points> exist when $0<a<2c$. They merge with the origin at $a_p=2c$, in a <pitchfork bifurcation>.

At the origin the <Jacobian matrix> and its <eigenvalues> are
$$
J_O=\begin{pmatrix}-a&a&0\\c-a&c&0\\0&0&-b\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\lambda_z=-b,\quad\lambda_\pm=\frac{c-a\pm\sqrt{c^2+6ac-3a^2}}2.
$$
At $a=2c$ these are $0,-c,-b$. To see the local <pitchfork bifurcation> dynamics more explicitly put $\delta=2c-a$, $y=x+v$, and use $z=x^2/b+\cdots$ on the <center manifold>. The equations become
$$
\dot x=av,\qquad \dot v=\delta x+(c-a)v-xz,\qquad
\dot z=x^2+xv-bz.
$$
At $\delta=0$, invariance at cubic order gives $v=-x^3/(bc)+\cdots$, hence the reduced equation is
$$
\dot x=2\delta x-\frac2b x^3+\text{higher-order terms}.
$$
It has the supercritical <pitchfork bifurcation> form as $\delta$ increases, with the two branches $x\simeq\pm\sqrt{b\delta}$ <attracting> near onset while the origin becomes <unstable>.

For $0<a<2c$, the <determinant> of the planar block is $a(a-2c)<0$, so the origin has one positive and two negative <eigenvalues> and is a <saddle equilibrium>. At $a=c$, the planar <eigenvalues> are explicitly $\pm c$. For $a>2c$, the planar <trace> $c-a$ is negative and its <determinant> positive, so the origin is <asymptotically stable>. To distinguish its type put
$$
a_*=c\left(1+\frac{2\sqrt3}{3}\right).
$$
For $2c<a<a_*$ both planar <eigenvalues> are real negative, giving a <stable node>. At $a=a_*$ they coincide at $-c/\sqrt3$; the planar block is not a scalar <matrix>, so it is a defective <stable node>. For $a>a_*$ they are <nonreal> with negative <real part>, giving a <stable> spiral in the planar block together with the <stable> third direction. This refines the requested node-versus-saddle classification.

The origin cannot have a <Hopf bifurcation>: a planar imaginary pair would require <trace> zero, namely $a=c$, but the <determinant> there is $-c^2<0$, so the pair is real and of opposite signs.

For either nonzero <equilibrium point> put $s=\pm\sqrt{b(2c-a)}$. The <Jacobian matrix> is
$$
J_\pm=\begin{pmatrix}-a&a&0\\-c&c&-s\\s&s&-b\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its <characteristic polynomial> is
$$
p(\lambda)=\lambda^3+A\lambda^2+B\lambda+C,\qquad
A=a+b-c,\quad B=bc,\quad C=2ab(2c-a).
$$
A nonzero imaginary <root of an equation> $i\omega$ requires, by separating real and imaginary parts, $\omega^2=B$ and $AB=C$. Equivalently
$$
2a^2-3ac+bc-c^2=0,
$$
so the candidate <Hopf bifurcation> values are
$$
\boxed{a_h=\frac{3c\pm\sqrt{17c^2-8bc}}4.}
$$
At such an admissible value the factorization is $p(\lambda)=(\lambda+A)(\lambda^2+bc)$, giving the pair $\pm i\sqrt{bc}$ and a negative third <eigenvalue> $-A$, since $A=C/B>0$ on the nonzero branches.

The <mathematical model parameter> restrictions matter. A real candidate requires $b/c\le17/8$, and the nonzero <equilibrium points> also require $0<a_h<2c$. The upper-sign value is admissible for $0<b/c<17/8$; the lower-sign value additionally requires $b/c>1$. For fixed $b,c$, <differentiation> of the characteristic equation at the imaginary <root of an equation> gives the <eigenvalue crossing speed>
$$
\left.\frac{d\operatorname{Re}\lambda}{da}\right|_{a_h}
=-\frac{b(4a_h-3c)}{2\{bc+(a_h+b-c)^2\}}.
$$
Thus the <transverse eigenvalue crossing> condition holds except at the coincident-root case $b/c=17/8$, $a_h=3c/4$. At a <transverse eigenvalue crossing>, a generic nonzero <first Lyapunov coefficient> gives a <Hopf bifurcation>. The displayed radical is the spectral threshold; it does not justify an unconditional generic-Hopf claim for all positive <mathematical model parameters>, nor does it by itself exclude a degenerate <Hopf bifurcation> point. For example $b>17c/8$ yields no real candidate at all. The cubic <Routh-Hurwitz stability criterion> gives local <Lyapunov stability> on the side with $A>0$ and $AB-C>0$, where
$$
AB-C=b(2a^2-3ac+bc-c^2).
$$

For the modified equation, the planar block at the origin is $\begin{pmatrix}-a&a\\c&c\end{pmatrix}$, with <determinant> $-2ac<0$ for the stipulated positive <mathematical model parameters>. Thus the origin is always a <saddle equilibrium> for nonzero $a,c$ in that <mathematical model parameter> range. The nonzero <equilibrium points> now satisfy $y=x$, $z=2c$ and $x^2=2bc$, so they are
$$
(\pm\sqrt{2bc},\ \pm\sqrt{2bc},\ 2c).
$$
For $b,c>0$ these branches stay a positive distance from the origin and do not merge with it as $a$ varies. The origin also has no zero <eigenvalue> for $a,c>0$. Therefore **the <pitchfork bifurcation> is absent in the modified system**.
