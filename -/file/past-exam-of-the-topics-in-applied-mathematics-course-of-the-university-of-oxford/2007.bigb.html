= Solution
{parent=i}

The <iteration of a map> is $F(u)=u/(2+u^2)$. For every $u>0$, $0<F(u)<u/2$, so $0<u_n\leq2^{-n}u_0$ and $\boxed{u_n\to0}$. A cobweb alternates vertical steps to the graph and horizontal steps to the diagonal, approaching the origin monotonically.

\Image[2007-population-cobweb.png]
{title=Resource-limited population cobweb: every positive orbit approaches extinction}

More precisely $u_{n+1}/u_n\to1/2$. The product $2^nu_n=u_0\prod_{j<n}(1+u_j^2/2)^{-1}$ has a finite strictly positive limit because $\sum_ju_j^2<\infty$. Thus $\boxed{u_n\sim C(u_0)2^{-n}}$ for a positive constant depending on the initial <biological population>.

{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

A positive <fixed point> requires $\beta+u_*^\gamma=\alpha$, so it exists exactly when $\boxed{\alpha>\beta}$ and is $u_*=(\alpha-\beta)^{1/\gamma}$. Differentiating the map at it gives
$$
\boxed{F'(u_*)=1-\gamma\left(1-\frac\beta\alpha\right).}
$$
For $1<\gamma\leq2$ and $0<\beta/\alpha<1$, this <fixed-point multiplier> is strictly between $-1$ and $1$. Hence the positive <fixed point> is linearly asymptotically stable, including $\gamma=2$; positivity of $\beta$ makes the lower bound strict.

= iii
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The <fixed-point multiplier> decreases continuously with $\gamma$ and first reaches the <unit circle> at $-1$. Solving $1-\gamma_*(1-\beta/\alpha)=-1$ gives
$$
\boxed{\gamma_*=\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-\beta}>2.}
$$
It is stable for $1<\gamma<\gamma_*$ and unstable for $\gamma>\gamma_*$. At the critical value <linear stability> is inconclusive. The crossing at $-1$ is the local signature of a <period-doubling bifurcation>, rather than a crossing through $+1$.

= iv
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

At low density the per-generation growth factor is approximately $\alpha/\beta$. The power $\gamma$ controls how sharply crowding reduces recruitment or survival when resources become limiting; at high density $F(u)\sim\alpha u^{1-\gamma}$. Larger $\gamma$ therefore means a stronger <overcompensation in a population model>.

The flip threshold says that sufficiently strong density feedback can destabilize a constant positive <biological population> and create alternating generations, under the usual nondegeneracy conditions. **A local loss of fixed-point stability alone does not establish <chaos>**, nor does it prove every ecological system with stronger crowding must follow the same route.

= v
{parent=1}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=v}

A <fixed point> of $F$ is also a <fixed point> of $F\circ F$. By the <chain rule>,
$$
\boxed{(F\circ F)'(u_*)=F'(F(u_*))F'(u_*)=[F'(u_*)]^2.}
$$
If the original <fixed-point multiplier> has modulus greater than one, its square also exceeds one, so the same <fixed point> is unstable for the twice-iterated map. New stable <fixed points> of the twice-iterated map, if present, must be different points; they can represent a genuine <two-cycle> of the original map.

<Law of mass action> assigns net <substrate> gain $k_0s$ to autocatalytic replication, <substrate>-<enzyme> binding flux $k_1se$, dissociation flux $k_{-1}c$ and product-formation flux $k_2c$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\dot s=k_0s-k_1se+k_{-1}c,\quad
\dot c=k_1se-(k_{-1}+k_2)c,\quad
\dot e=-k_1se+(k_{-1}+k_2)c.}
$$
Complex breakdown to product restores free <enzyme> but does not restore <substrate>. Product <concentration>, if required, satisfies $\dot p=k_2c$.

{parent=2}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Adding the last two equations gives $d(e+c)/dt=0$. The initial conditions therefore imply $\boxed{e+c=e_0}$, or $e=e_0-c$. Substitute this in the first two balances:
$$
\boxed{\dot s=(k_0-k_1e_0)s+(k_{-1}+k_1s)c,\qquad
\dot c=k_1e_0s-(k_1s+k_{-1}+k_2)c.}
$$
The <enzyme> inventory is conserved even while <substrate> can grow autocatalytically.

= Solution

Use $s=s_0u$, $c=e_0v$ and slow time $\boxed{\tau=k_1e_0t}$. Dividing the equations by their respective <concentration> and <time scales> gives
$$
\boxed{\epsilon=\frac{e_0}{s_0},\quad
K=\frac{k_{-1}+k_2}{k_1s_0},\quad\lambda=\frac{k_2}{k_1s_0},\quad
\eta=\frac{k_0}{k_1e_0}.}
$$
Thus $u'=\eta u-u+uv+(K-\lambda)v$ and $\epsilon v'=u-uv-Kv$. Notice that $K>\lambda>0$ and the physical <enzyme> fraction satisfies $0\leq v\leq1$. The initial values are $u(0)=1$, $v(0)=0$ for this particular normalization; the threshold analysis compares different initial <substrate> abundances using a fixed reference scale.

= Solution
{parent=a}

At equilibrium $v=u/(u+K)$ and adding $u'+\epsilon v'$ gives $0=\eta u-\lambda v$. The positive equilibrium is
$$
\boxed{u_e=\frac\lambda\eta-K,\quad v_e=1-\frac{\eta K}\lambda,\quad
0<\eta<\frac\lambda K,\qquad
0<k_0<\frac{k_1e_0k_2}{k_{-1}+k_2}.}
$$
This condition also gives $\eta<1$. The <nullclines> are $v=u/(u+K)$ and $v=(1-\eta)u/(u+K-\lambda)$. The second lies above the first for $0<u<u_e$ and below it for $u>u_e$. Below the first <nullcline> $v'>0$; above it $v'<0$. Below the second <nullcline> $u'<0$; above it $u'>0$.

At the origin the Jacobian has <trace> $\eta-1-K/\epsilon<0$ and <determinant> $(\lambda-\eta K)/\epsilon>0$. Its off-diagonal entries are positive, making the <eigenvalues> real, so this is an attracting node. At the positive equilibrium the slow reduced <derivative> is $\eta-\lambda K/(u_e+K)^2=\eta v_e>0$, while the fast <derivative> is $-(u_e+K)/\epsilon<0$; equivalently the full Jacobian <determinant> is negative. Thus the positive point is a <saddle equilibrium>. Its <stable manifold> separates extinction from unbounded <substrate> growth.

= b
{parent=iv}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

After an <initial layer> of duration $O(\epsilon)$ in slow time, $v\simeq u/(u+K)$ while $u$ changes only slightly. The reduced <substrate> equation is
$$
\boxed{u'\simeq\eta u-\frac{\lambda u}{u+K}
=\frac{\eta u(u-u_e)}{u+K}.}
$$
Below $u_e$ it decreases to zero; above $u_e$ it increases without bound. The exact threshold for initial $v=0$ is the intersection of the <saddle equilibrium>'s <stable manifold> with that axis. For small $\epsilon$ it is
$$
\boxed{u_*\simeq u_e=\frac{k_2e_0}{k_0s_0}-\frac{k_{-1}+k_2}{k_1s_0}.}
$$
In dimensional initial-<substrate> units the leading threshold is $s_0u_*\simeq k_2e_0/k_0-(k_{-1}+k_2)/k_1$. Comparing the actual initial value $u(0)=1$ to this threshold determines its fate; the reference scale can be held fixed when varying the initial <concentration>.

The initial sequestration produces an $O(\epsilon)$ correction if desired. Write the <stable manifold> as $u=u_e+\epsilon\phi(v)+O(\epsilon^2)$. The invariance equation at leading order gives $\phi'(v)=F(u_e,v)/G(u_e,v)=-(1-\eta)$ and $\phi(v_e)=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{u_*=u_e+\epsilon(1-\eta)v_e+O(\epsilon^2),}
$$
for fixed <parameters> away from degenerate limits. The positive correction reflects the <substrate> consumed in building complexes from the initially <enzyme>-free-complex state.

The physical strip $u\geq0$, $0\leq v\leq1$ is invariant, and the off-diagonal <derivatives> are nonnegative there, so ordered initial <substrate> values remain ordered. There are only the attracting origin and the <saddle equilibrium>. With <Dulac function> $1/u$, the divergence of the weighted vector field is $-(K-\lambda)v/u^2-(1+K/u)/\epsilon<0$, excluding <periodic orbits> in its interior. The <saddle equilibrium> <separatrix> therefore gives the unique threshold on the initial axis: below it solutions tend to extinction, at it they tend to the <saddle equilibrium>, and above it they escape to infinity. Growth is at most exponential, so this is $u\to\infty$ as time tends to infinity, not a <finite-time blowup>. This is <autocatalytic enzyme clearance with a saddle threshold>.

The travelling coordinate has $A_t=-ca'$, $A_{xx}=a''$. Therefore the profile equation is
$$
\boxed{a''+ca'+f(a)=0,\qquad f(a)=\frac{a(\eta^2-a^2)}{1+a^2},\quad
 a(-\infty)=\eta,\ a(+\infty)=0.}
$$
The wave moves right with speed $c$. It joins a populated equilibrium behind to an empty equilibrium ahead.

= b
{parent=i}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The phase-plane equations are $a'=b$, $b'=-cb-f(a)$. Nonnegative equilibria require $b=0$ and $f(a)=0$, hence only $\boxed{(0,0),\ (\eta,0)}$. At the origin $f'(0)=\eta^2$, so the linear <eigenvalues> solve
$$
\lambda^2+c\lambda+\eta^2=0,\qquad
\boxed{\lambda_\pm=(-c\pm\sqrt{c^2-4\eta^2})/2.}
$$
For $0<c<2\eta$ the roots are complex with negative real part, giving a <stable focus>. For $c>2\eta$ both are negative real numbers, giving a <stable node>. Equality is the repeated-root threshold and is not covered by either strict classification.

= Solution

A nonconstant trajectory approaching a <stable focus> winds around it infinitely often. Its $a$ coordinate therefore changes sign repeatedly near zero. Since the <biological population> profile must remain nonnegative, such a trajectory cannot represent the wave. The only nonnegative trajectory avoiding that winding at the origin would be the equilibrium itself, which cannot join the populated state. Thus $\boxed{\text{no nonnegative front for }0<c<2\eta}$.

= Solution

On $b=-\eta a$ with $a>0$,
$$
\frac{db}{da}=\frac{-cb-f(a)}b=-c+\frac{f(a)}{\eta a}
=-c+\frac{\eta^2-a^2}{\eta(1+a^2)}\leq-c+\eta<-\eta.
$$
Thus $\boxed{db/da<-\eta}$. Since $a'=b<0$, comparing slopes alone can reverse one's intuition about crossing direction. More directly, for $H=b+\eta a$ the inward test is
$$
H'=b'+\eta a'=\eta(c-\eta)a-f(a)\geq\eta(c-2\eta)a>0,
$$
so the sloping edge also points into $H\geq0$.

{parent=ii}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

The top edge points down by (a), the sloping edge points upward in the inward normal sense by (b), and on $a=\eta$ the velocity $a'=b\leq0$ points left. At $a=0$ the region contains only the equilibrium $(0,0)$. Thus the closed triangle is forward invariant.

The given unstable <saddle equilibrium> branch enters its interior from $(\eta,0)$ and stays in this compact region. Along it $a'=b\leq0$, so $a$ decreases and has a limit. An interior limiting orbit with $b\ne0$ would keep changing $a$, while an interior point with $b=0$, $0<a<\eta$ has $b'<0$ and is not an equilibrium. Hence the only possible forward limit is $(0,0)$. Ordinary-differential-equation uniqueness prevents reaching and leaving an equilibrium in finite profile coordinate. The whole orbit therefore joins $(\eta,0)$ at $y=-\infty$ to $(0,0)$ at $y=+\infty$ with $a\geq0$ and $a'=b\leq0$.

Consequently $\boxed{\text{a nonnegative monotone front exists for every }c>2\eta}$. This proves the requested strict-speed result without claiming an unproved conclusion at the repeated-root threshold. It is a <trapping triangle for a saturating monostable invasion front>.

At a homogeneous equilibrium, add the two reaction equations: $f+g=1-b=0$, so $b=1$. Then $f=0$ gives $a=3/4$. Thus $\boxed{(a_*,b_*)=(3/4,1)}$.

{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The reaction Jacobian is
$$
\boxed{J=\begin{pmatrix}-b^2&-2ab\\b^2&-1+2ab\end{pmatrix}_{(3/4,1)}
=\begin{pmatrix}-1&-3/2\\1&1/2\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Its <trace> is $-1/2$ and <determinant> is $1$, so both <eigenvalues> have negative real parts. Explicitly they are $(-1\pm i\sqrt{15})/4$. Therefore the homogeneous reaction equilibrium is a <stable focus> in the absence of diffusion.

= iii
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The cosine modes satisfy both zero-flux endpoint conditions and $\partial_x^2\cos(k_nx)=-k_n^2\cos(k_nx)$. Linearizing the reaction terms gives $f_a\delta a+f_b\delta b$ and $g_a\delta a+g_b\delta b$. Cancelling the common mode factor yields
$$
\boxed{\lambda\binom{a_n}{b_n}=\left[J-\begin{pmatrix}k_n^2&0\\0&Dk_n^2\end{pmatrix}\right]\binom{a_n}{b_n},\qquad k_n=n\pi/L.}
$$
The <derivatives> are exactly the entries of the matrix computed in (ii). The zero mode reproduces the stable homogeneous perturbation; positive integers give the possible spatial instabilities.

= iv
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Put $q=k_n^2$. The mode matrix has <trace> $-1/2-(1+D)q<0$, so a growing <eigenvalue> occurs exactly when its <determinant> is negative:
$$
\boxed{\Delta(q)=Dq^2+(D-1/2)q+1<0.}
$$
This upward quadratic has two positive roots only if $D<1/2$ and $D^2-5D+1/4>0$. Within $0<D<1$ this is
$$
\boxed{0<D<D_c=\frac{5-\sqrt{24}}2.}
$$
For such $D$, define
$$
\boxed{q_\pm=\frac{1/2-D\pm\sqrt{D^2-5D+1/4}}{2D}.}
$$
The unstable modes satisfy $\boxed{\sqrt{q_-}<k_n<\sqrt{q_+}}$. These are strict inequalities: at either endpoint the leading <eigenvalue> is zero rather than positive.

= v
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=v}

If $D\geq1/2$, both nonconstant coefficients of $\Delta(q)$ are nonnegative, so it is positive for $q\geq0$. If $D_c<D<1/2$, its <discriminant> is negative, so it is again strictly positive. Therefore $\boxed{D>D_c\implies\text{no diffusion-driven instability}}$.

At $D=D_c$ the double root can only give a neutral zero <eigenvalue> if it coincides with an allowed spatial mode; it still gives no positive growth rate. This is the finite-<wavenumber> onset of a <Turing instability>, not an oscillatory crossing since the mode <trace> stays negative.

= vi
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=vi}

Small $L$ makes every nonzero $k_n=n\pi/L$ large, placing them above the finite instability band, where diffusion dominates reaction. The homogeneous zero mode is already stable.

For $0<D<D_c$, the first possible band entry as $L$ increases is $n=1$ crossing $k_1=\sqrt{q_+}$. Hence the critical length is
$$
\boxed{L_c=\frac\pi{\sqrt{q_+}}
=\pi\sqrt{\frac{2D}{1/2-D+\sqrt{D^2-5D+1/4}}}.}
$$
At equality the mode is neutral; it is unstable just above this value, while $\pi/\sqrt{q_+}<L<\pi/\sqrt{q_-}$. Thus strictly positive growth has infimum $L_c$, with no attained smallest length if “unstable” means $\operatorname{Re}\lambda>0$. The conventional threshold answer is $L_c$. Larger modes cannot enter earlier because their first-entry lengths are $nL_c$.

A <saddle-node bifurcation> creates or destroys two equilibria when a real <eigenvalue> passes through zero with a nonzero quadratic normal-form term. With a <parameter> orientation chosen so the pair appears for $\mu>0$, use
$$
\boxed{\dot q=\mu-q^2.}
$$
There are no equilibria for $\mu<0$, a double nonhyperbolic equilibrium at zero, and equilibria $q=\pm\sqrt\mu$ for $\mu>0$. The positive branch is stable and the negative branch unstable in the active direction; additional hyperbolic directions can make them a node and a <saddle equilibrium>. “Supercritical” for a fold depends on this chosen <parameter> orientation; unlike the pitchfork and Hopf cases it is not an intrinsic distinction between the stability of two possible cubic branches.

= b
{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The supercritical pitchfork <normal form of a dynamical system> is
$$
\boxed{\dot q=\mu q-q^3.}
$$
The symmetric equilibrium $q=0$ is stable for $\mu<0$ and unstable for $\mu>0$. Two new stable equilibria $q=\pm\sqrt\mu$ emerge on the unstable side of the original branch. Reflection <symmetry> $q\mapsto-q$ preserves the pair. The new equilibria approach zero continuously as the <parameter> reaches the threshold.

= c
{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

A <Hopf bifurcation> occurs when a simple pair of <complex conjugate> <eigenvalues> crosses the imaginary axis at nonzero frequency. A supercritical <normal form of a dynamical system> is
$$
\boxed{\dot z=(\mu+i\omega_0)z-|z|^2z,\qquad\omega_0\ne0.}
$$
Writing $z=re^{i\theta}$ gives $\dot r=\mu r-r^3$ and $\dot\theta=\omega_0$. A stable <periodic orbit> of radius $\sqrt\mu$ is born for $\mu>0$ as the equilibrium loses stability. In contrast, a subcritical Hopf has an unstable small <periodic orbit> on the side where the equilibrium is still stable.

= Solution
{parent=5}

The Lorenz equilibria satisfy $y=x$, $z=x^2/b$ and $x(r-1-z)=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{O=(0,0,0),\qquad C_\pm=(\pm\sqrt{b(r-1)},\pm\sqrt{b(r-1)},r-1)\quad(r>1).}
$$
At the origin the third <eigenvalue> is $-b$ and the other two solve $\lambda^2+(\sigma+1)\lambda+\sigma(1-r)=0$. Hence the origin is stable for $r<1$, has one zero <eigenvalue> at $r=1$, and becomes a <saddle equilibrium> with one unstable direction for $r>1$. The <symmetry> $(x,y,z)\mapsto(-x,-y,z)$ interchanges the new branches.

The nonzero-equilibrium characteristic polynomial is
$$
\boxed{P(\lambda)=\lambda^3+(\sigma+b+1)\lambda^2+b(\sigma+r)\lambda+2\sigma b(r-1).}
$$
Near $r=1$ its Routh-Hurwitz inequalities are strict, so both new branches are stable. More explicitly, center-manifold projection at $r=1$ gives
$$
\dot q=\frac\sigma{\sigma+1}(r-1)q-\frac\sigma{b(\sigma+1)}q^3+
O((r-1)^2q,(r-1)q^3,q^5).
$$
The linear coefficient crosses positively and the cubic coefficient is negative. Therefore **the equilibrium <bifurcation> is a supercritical pitchfork at $r=1$**, not a <saddle equilibrium>-node.

For completeness, the later stability boundary satisfies
$$
(\sigma+b+1)(\sigma+r)=2\sigma(r-1).
$$
If $\sigma\leq b+1$, the nonzero branches remain linearly stable for all $r>1$. If $\sigma>b+1$, they lose stability at
$$
\boxed{r_H=\frac{\sigma(\sigma+b+3)}{\sigma-b-1},\qquad
\omega_H^2=b(\sigma+r_H).}
$$
At that point $P=(\lambda+\sigma+b+1)(\lambda^2+\omega_H^2)$ and
$$
\left.\frac{d\operatorname{Re}\lambda}{dr}\right|_H
=\frac{b(\sigma-b-1)}{2[\omega_H^2+(\sigma+b+1)^2]}>0.
$$
This establishes a genuine transverse Hopf crossing. It is **subcritical** for these positive <parameters>, so it is not the supercritical Hopf <normal form of a dynamical system> from (c). One way to check its nonlinear sign is the cubic Hopf formula. At $C_+$ write $s=\sqrt{b(r_H-1)}$ and
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}-\sigma&\sigma&0\\1&-1&-s\\s&s&-b\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B(v,w)=(0,-v_1w_3-v_3w_1,v_1w_2+v_2w_1).
$$
For right and adjoint eigenvectors $Aq=i\omega_Hq$, $A^*p=-i\omega_Hp$, $\langle p,q\rangle=1$, the cubic coefficient has the sign of
$$
\operatorname{Re}\langle p,
B(\bar q,(2i\omega_HI-A)^{-1}B(q,q))
-2B(q,A^{-1}B(q,\bar q))\rangle.
$$
Substitution and clearing positive factors reduces its sign to that of the following polynomial in $h=\sigma-b-1>0$:
$$
9h^4+2(9b+28)h^3+4(5b^2+29b+31)h^2+
4(b+1)(b+2)(5b+14)h+8(b+1)^2(b+2)^2.
$$
Every term is positive. The radial cubic is therefore positive, giving the unstable small cycle on the stable-equilibrium side. There is no <saddle equilibrium>-node among the equilibrium branches for $\sigma,b,r>0$: the only zero-<eigenvalue> branch intersection is the <symmetry>-forced pitchfork. This does not rule out separate global <bifurcations> of <periodic orbits>.

= Solution

The equilibria are $\boxed{(0,0),\ (\pm\sqrt{2/3},0)}$. The Jacobian is $J=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\2-9x^2&-1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$. At the origin it has <eigenvalues> $1,-2$ and tangent eigenvectors $(1,1)$ and $(1,-2)$, so the origin is a <saddle equilibrium>. At either nonzero point the <eigenvalues> are $\boxed{(-1\pm i\sqrt{15})/2}$, giving stable foci.

The <energy>-like function $H=y^2/2-x^2+3x^4/4$ has $\dot H=-y^2\leq0$. It is coercive at large distance, so trajectories remain bounded; there are no <periodic orbits> because their <energy> could not return to its starting value unless $y\equiv0$. The <stable manifold> of the <saddle equilibrium> separates the two attracting basins. Its unstable branches leave the origin and approach the right and left foci, respectively. Their negative <energy> near the origin prevents crossing back through $x=0$.

For either invariant manifold write $y=h(x)=sx+cx^3+O(x^5)$. Odd <symmetry> rules out even powers. Tangency to the vector field requires $h'(x)h(x)=2x-h(x)-3x^3$. Matching coefficients yields $s^2+s-2=0$ and $c(4s+1)=-3$. Hence
$$
\boxed{W^u:\ y=x-\frac35x^3+O(x^5),\qquad
W^s:\ y=-2x+\frac37x^3+O(x^5).}
$$
These are local graphs, not globally exact cubic curves.

= Solution
{parent=i}

At a differentiable <fixed point>, linear perturbations advance by the <Jacobian matrix>. A hyperbolic **<source fixed point of a map>** has both <eigenvalues> of modulus greater than one, so nearby perturbations expand under forward <iteration of a map>. Real signs can cause alternating directions and a complex pair can cause spiral expansion. <Eigenvalues> on the <unit circle> are excluded from this linear classification.

= ii
{parent=7}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

A hyperbolic **<sink fixed point of a map>** has both Jacobian <eigenvalues> strictly inside the <unit circle>. Its iterates locally attract nearby initial states; complex <fixed-point multipliers> can make the approach spiral. Zero <fixed-point multipliers> are strongly contracting and can occur for <noninvertible maps>. A <fixed-point multiplier> of modulus one requires nonlinear or exact analysis.

= iii
{parent=7}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

A hyperbolic **<saddle fixed point of a map>** has one <eigenvalue> of modulus less than one and one greater than one. The corresponding stable and unstable directions persist locally as <invariant sets> or manifolds under the usual smoothness and invertibility assumptions. For a <noninvertible map> a zero stable <fixed-point multiplier> is possible, and the word <saddle fixed point of a map> describes forward contraction in one direction and expansion in the other; backward dynamics may not be unique.

= Solution

A <fixed point> $X_*$ satisfies $F(X_*)=X_*$. The <Jacobian matrix> is $DF=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}f_x&f_y\\g_x&g_y\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$; its <eigenvalues> are the linear <fixed-point multipliers> used in (i)–(iii).

For the <Lozi map>, $y=bx$ and $x=1+bx-a|x|$. At a positive point this gives $(1+a-b)x=1$, and at a negative point $(1-a-b)x=1$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{X_+=\frac{(1,b)}{1+a-b}\quad\text{when }b<1+a,\qquad
X_-=\frac{(1,b)}{1-a-b}\quad\text{when }b>1-a.}
$$
There is no <fixed point> at $x=0$, because then $y=0$ and the first component would be one. On an equality boundary the corresponding scalar equation is $0=1$, so that branch is absent, not a line of <fixed points>.

For $x\ne0$, $DF=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}-a\operatorname{sgn}x&1\\b&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$. If $a=0$, there is exactly one <fixed point> $(1,b)/(1-b)$ unless $b=1$, when there is none. Its <eigenvalues> satisfy $\lambda^2=b$. It is a <sink fixed point of a map> for $|b|<1$ and a <source fixed point of a map> for $|b|>1$. At $b=-1$, the affine map about its <fixed point> is a quarter turn, $DF^2=-I$ and $F^4=I$, so it is neutral, not attracting. At $b=1$ no <fixed point> exists. The positive/negative branch labels follow the sign of $1-b$.

If $b=0$, $X_+=(1/(1+a),0)$ exists for $a>-1$ with <fixed-point multipliers> $-a,0$. It is a <sink fixed point of a map> for $-1<a<1$ and a forward <saddle fixed point of a map> for $a>1$. At $a=1$ the <fixed-point multiplier> $-1$ is neutral; on the invariant axis the interval $0\leq x\leq1$ is mapped by $x\mapsto1-x$, giving <two-cycles> and the fixed midpoint. The negative point $X_-=(1/(1-a),0)$ exists only for $a>1$ and has <fixed-point multipliers> $a,0$, again a forward <saddle fixed point of a map>. For $a\leq-1$ there is no <fixed point>. At $a=b=0$, both <fixed-point multipliers> vanish and two iterates send all initial points to the <fixed point>.

For a genuine <two-cycle> let the first coordinates be $x_1,x_2$ and put $h=1-b$. The two equations are $hx_2=1-a|x_1|$, $hx_1=1-a|x_2|$. If both first coordinates had the same sign $s$, subtracting would give $(h-as)(x_2-x_1)=0$. Since distinct cycle points must have different first coordinates, this contradicts $a^2\ne h^2$. If one coordinate were zero, the remaining equations would instead force $a=\pm h$, also excluded. Hence $\boxed{x_1x_2<0}$.

Choose $x_1>0>x_2$. Solving $ax_1+hx_2=1$, $hx_1-ax_2=1$ gives
$$
\boxed{x_1=\frac{a+h}{a^2+h^2},\qquad x_2=\frac{h-a}{a^2+h^2},\qquad
X_1=(x_1,bx_2),\quad X_2=(x_2,bx_1).}
$$
The required signs hold exactly when $\boxed{a>|1-b|}$. This is the unique <two-cycle> up to interchanging its two points, under the stipulated nondegeneracy. Borderline <parameter> lines can carry different degenerate families and were explicitly excluded. These are <Lozi fixed points and the alternating-sign two-cycle>.

= Solution
{parent=8}

On the invariant <biological population> interval $0\leq x\leq1$, the <fixed points> are $0$ and $x_*=1-1/\mu$ when meaningful. The zero <fixed-point multiplier> is $\mu$: extinction attracts for $0\leq\mu<1$. At $\mu=1$, the nonzero branch crosses the origin in the full real-line family, giving a <transcritical bifurcation>. The nonzero point lies in the <biological population> interval for $\mu>1$ and has <fixed-point multiplier> $2-\mu$, so it is stable for $1<\mu<3$ and loses stability through $-1$ at $\mu=3$.

Factoring $F^2(x)-x$ and removing the <fixed points> gives the <two-cycle>
$$
\boxed{x_\pm=\frac{\mu+1\pm\sqrt{(\mu-3)(\mu+1)}}{2\mu},\qquad\mu>3.}
$$
Its two-step <fixed-point multiplier> is
$$
\boxed{F'(x_+)F'(x_-)=-\mu^2+2\mu+4.}
$$
It lies between $-1$ and $1$ exactly for $3<\mu<1+\sqrt6$. At the upper endpoint it crosses $-1$, creating the next period doubling. The <two-cycle> amplitude tends to zero like $\sqrt{\mu-3}$ at its birth, so the first flip is supercritical. At the equality endpoints linear <fixed-point multipliers> alone do not establish <asymptotic stability>.

{title=Logistic fixed points and the first stable two-cycle through the second period-doubling threshold}

<Period-doubling cascade> universality concerns the continuation of this <period-doubling cascade> in smooth unimodal maps with a quadratic maximum. After suitable rescalings their repeated two-step maps approach the same limiting shape; successive <parameter> spacings have the Feigenbaum ratio approximately $4.6692$ and the spatial rescaling has magnitude approximately $2.5029$. These are asymptotic statements involving later doublings, not numbers that can be established from the first two thresholds alone. The accumulation near $\mu\simeq3.56995$ is outside the first interval considered here. One should not claim <chaos> throughout the stable <two-cycle> interval.

Four useful dynamical properties to distinguish are **sensitivity to initial conditions**, **<topological transitivity> or mixing**, **<dense periodic points>**, and **<aperiodic orbits> with persistent complex evolution**. They concern the <invariant set>, not an assertion that every orbit must be aperiodic. A positive <Lyapunov exponent> for typical initial data is an additional quantitative diagnostic.

At $\mu=4$, set $x_0=\sin^2\theta_0$. The double-angle identity gives the exact solution
$$
\boxed{x_n=\sin^2(2^n\theta_0),\qquad\theta_0=\arcsin\sqrt{x_0}.}
$$
For a direct topological proof use the <homeomorphism> $h(y)=\sin^2(\pi y/2)$ of $[0,1]$. Then $F_4\circ h=h\circ T$, where the full <tent map> is
$$
T(y)=\begin{cases}2y,&0\leq y\leq1/2,\\2(1-y),&1/2\leq y\leq1.\end{cases}
$$
Every branch doubles interval length until it crosses a fold; the finite binary cylinders have length $2^{-n}$ and map onto the whole interval after $n$ steps. Thus every open interval eventually has an iterate covering the full interval: the map is topologically mixing and sensitive. Repeating a finite left/right branch word gives a periodic itinerary; arbitrarily long prescribed prefixes locate such periodic points in arbitrarily small cylinders, proving density. Nonperiodic binary itineraries yield uncountably many <aperiodic orbits>. Conjugacy transfers these properties to $F_4$, proving chaotic behaviour while retaining its dense special <periodic orbits>.

As a metric check, its <invariant measure> has density $1/[\pi\sqrt{x(1-x)}]$ and its typical <Lyapunov exponent> is $\log2>0$. This is a typical-orbit assertion, not a claim about the <derivative> along every exceptional critical or preperiodic orbit. The explicit trigonometric solution and tent-map conjugacy explain why deterministic <iteration of a map> can nevertheless amplify uncertainty.
