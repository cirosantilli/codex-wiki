=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For nonzero <momentum>, physical <helicity> is the <Spin>[spin projection] along the direction of motion,
$$
\boxed{\hat h=\frac{\mathbf S\cdot\mathbf p}{|\mathbf p|}=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf p}&0\\0&\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf p}\end{pmatrix},\qquad h=\pm\frac12.}
$$
The convention without $1/2$ labels the same two <eigenspaces> by $\pm1$. Write $\hat{\mathbf p}=(\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\cos\theta)$. <Normalization>[Normalized] <Spinor>[two-component eigenvectors] are
$$
\chi_+=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\theta/2)\\e^{i\varphi}\sin(\theta/2)\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\chi_-=\begin{pmatrix}-e^{-i\varphi}\sin(\theta/2)\\\cos(\theta/2)\end{pmatrix},\qquad
(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\hat{\mathbf p})\chi_\pm=\pm\chi_\pm.
$$
Each can carry an arbitrary <phase>. Before imposing an <energy> equation, the general <Dirac spinor>[four-component] <helicity> <eigenvector> is $(a\chi_\pm,b\chi_\pm)^{\mathsf T}$, with arbitrary complex $a,b$; unit <Norm> imposes $|a|^2+|b|^2=1$. For a <Energy eigenvalue>[positive-energy] free <electron>, the <Dirac equation> fixes their ratio and gives
$$
\boxed{u_\pm(\mathbf p)=\sqrt{\frac{E+m}{2E}}\begin{pmatrix}\chi_\pm\\\displaystyle\pm\frac{|\mathbf p|}{E+m}\chi_\pm\end{pmatrix},\qquad u_\pm^\dagger u_\pm=1.}
$$
Multiplying by an arbitrary overall <Probability amplitude>[amplitude] and the common <plane wave> supplies the corresponding free <helicity> eigenstate. <Helicity> has no preferred direction at $\mathbf p=0$. For a massive particle a <Lorentz boost>[boost] can overtake its <momentum> and change its <helicity>; it should not be identified with <Lorentz invariance>[Lorentz-invariant] <chirality>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $\chi=(\sqrt5/3,2/3)^{\mathsf T}$, so $\chi^\dagger\chi=1$. The <Pauli matrices>[Pauli identity] $(\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p)^2=|\mathbf p|^2I$ gives
$$
\psi^\dagger\psi=|N|^2\left(1+\frac{|\mathbf p|^2}{(E+m)^2}\right)=|N|^2\frac{2E}{E+m},
$$
using $E^2=|\mathbf p|^2+m^2$. Thus unit <spinor> <probability density> is obtained with
$$
\boxed{N=\sqrt{\frac{E+m}{2E}}.}
$$
An arbitrary constant <phase> is immaterial. For the alternative <Relativistic normalization of a one-particle state>[Lorentz normalization] $u^\dagger u=2E$, choose $N=\sqrt{E+m}$. Neither convention makes an infinite <plane wave> <Square-integrable function>[square-integrable]; a box-normalized state includes an additional $V^{-1/2}$, or one forms a normalizable <wave packet>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

In the supplied <Dirac representation of the gamma matrices>[Dirac representation], $\gamma^5$ exchanges the upper and lower blocks. Therefore
$$
P_L=\frac{1-\gamma^5}{2}=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}I&-I\\-I&I\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Define $\eta=[I-\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf p/(E+m)]\chi$. Acting with this <Projection operator>[projector] gives
$$
\boxed{\psi_L=P_L\psi=\frac N2\begin{pmatrix}\eta\\-\eta\end{pmatrix}e^{i(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf r-Et)}.}
$$
For completeness, its two independent components are
$$
\eta_1=\frac{\sqrt5}{3}\left(1-\frac{p_z}{E+m}\right)-\frac{2(p_x-ip_y)}{3(E+m)},\qquad
\eta_2=-\frac{\sqrt5(p_x+ip_y)}{3(E+m)}+\frac23\left(1+\frac{p_z}{E+m}\right).
$$
The equal-and-opposite block structure verifies $\gamma^5\psi_L=-\psi_L$. Projection isolates a <Chirality>[chiral component] of the massive <Dirac field>[Dirac] solution; that component alone generally does not satisfy an independent massive <Dirac equation>, since the <mass> couples both <chiralities>.

{scope}

==== Solution

Expand the original <Spinor>[two-spinor] as $\chi=c_+\chi_++c_-\chi_-$. <Orthogonality> gives
$$
c_+=\frac{\sqrt5}{3}\cos\frac\theta2+\frac23e^{-i\varphi}\sin\frac\theta2,\qquad
c_-=-\frac{\sqrt5}{3}e^{i\varphi}\sin\frac\theta2+\frac23\cos\frac\theta2.
$$
Let $r=|\mathbf p|/(E+m)$ and $w_\pm=(\chi_\pm,-\chi_\pm)^{\mathsf T}/\sqrt2$. These $w_\pm$ are <Normalization>[normalized], <Chirality>[left-chiral] <helicity> <eigenvectors>. Then
$$
\boxed{\psi_L=\sum_{\zeta=\pm1}\frac N{\sqrt2}c_\zeta(1-\zeta r)w_\zeta\,e^{i(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf r-Et)}.}
$$
The two contributions have squared norms
$$
\boxed{\|\psi_{L,+}\|^2=|c_+|^2\frac{1-\beta}{2},\qquad
\|\psi_{L,-}\|^2=|c_-|^2\frac{1+\beta}{2},\qquad \beta=|\mathbf p|/E.}
$$
These are weights relative to the original <Normalization>[normalized] state; divide by their sum if conditioning on a <Normalization>[normalized] <Chirality>[left-chiral] projection. In the ultrarelativistic limit $N\to1/\sqrt2$, $r\to1$, the <Helicity>[positive-helicity] <Probability amplitude>[amplitude] is suppressed as $m/(2E)$, while the <Helicity>[negative-helicity] <Probability amplitude>[amplitude] tends to $c_-$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\psi_L\longrightarrow c_-w_-e^{i(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf r-Et)}.}
$$
For <momentum> specifically along $+z$, $c_+=\sqrt5/3$ and $c_-=2/3$: the original <helicity> <probabilities> are $5/9,4/9$, the <Chirality>[left-chiral] <Norm> is $1/2-\beta/18$, and its limiting weight is $4/9$. The source does not specify that <momentum> direction, so these numerical <probabilities> are a specialization, not the general answer.

{scope}

==== Solution

The <Weak charged current>[charged weak current] contains $\bar\psi\gamma^\mu P_L\psi$. For an ultrarelativistic external <fermion>, it selects <Helicity>[negative helicity]; its <antifermion> partner has <Helicity>[positive helicity]. Wrong-helicity <Charged current>[charged-current] <Probability amplitudes>[amplitudes] vanish in the massless limit and are suppressed by powers of $m/E$ at finite <mass>. The active <neutrino> is consequently <Helicity>[left-helicity] and its <antineutrino> <Helicity>[right-helicity] in this limit.

This is a <Charged current>[charged-current] statement, not a prohibition on every <Helicity>[right-helicity] Standard Model <fermion>. Electromagnetic and strong vector interactions act on both <chiralities> and conserve <helicity> along a massless <fermion> line. A <Neutral current>[neutral-current] $Z$ vertex also couples to both charged-fermion <chiralities> with unequal strengths. For example, a <Helicity>[right-helicity] <electron> can scatter electromagnetically: it is not excluded from the Standard Model. In <Vector current>[vector-current] pair <annihilation> the massless <fermion> and <antifermion> have opposite <helicities>; a purely <Charged current>[charged-current] vertex keeps only the left-fermion/right-antifermion choice. <Yukawa interaction>[Yukawa interactions] connect left and right fields and must be distinguished from these <Vector current>[vector-current] selection rules.

The squared invariant <Probability amplitude>[amplitude] weights each accessible final configuration. The final <On shell>[on-shell] <momentum> measure counts the available states, and the four-dimensional <Dirac delta function>[delta function] imposes <energy> and <Conservation of momentum>[momentum conservation]:
$$
d\Phi_n=(2\pi)^4\delta^{(4)}\left(P-\sum_jp_j\right)\prod_j\frac{d^3p_j}{(2\pi)^3\,2E_j}.
$$
To prove its <Lorentz invariance> for proper orthochronous transformations, write
$$
\boxed{\frac{d^3p}{2E_{\mathbf p}}=d^4p\,\delta(p^2-m^2)\Theta(p^0).}
$$
The four-volume measure has unit <Lorentz transformation>[Lorentz Jacobian], $p^2$ is invariant, and the future <mass shell> is preserved. Likewise $\delta^{(4)}(\Lambda Q)=\delta^{(4)}(Q)$ because $|\det\Lambda|=1$. The product therefore proves that $d\Phi_n$ is invariant. <Spin> averages and sums belong in the <Probability amplitude>[amplitude] as appropriate; identical final particles require a <symmetry> divisor if the integration covers a fully labelled <Lorentz-invariant phase space>[phase space].

The initial $1/(2E_i)$ factors come from relativistic <normalization> of the incoming states. They are not individually invariant, so the displayed coordinate-time <transition rate> must not be called a <Lorentz scalar> merely because its final <Lorentz-invariant phase space>[phase space] is invariant. A one-particle decay has proper width $\Gamma_0=(2M_0)^{-1}\int|\mathcal M|^2d\Phi_n$ in its rest frame, while its laboratory rate is $\Gamma_{\mathrm{lab}}=(M_0/E_0)\Gamma_0$. A scattering <cross section> instead uses the invariant incident flux $4\sqrt{(p_1\cdot p_2)^2-m_1^2m_2^2}$. This distinguishes invariance of <Lorentz-invariant phase space>[phase space] from transformation of a measured rate.

==== Solution

The charged two-pion and three-pion channels can be assigned to the same spin-zero <kaon> by their parent <mass> and lifetime. For two spinless <pions>, total <angular momentum> zero forces their relative <orbital angular momentum> to zero, so the product of their <Intrinsic parity>[intrinsic parities] gives
$$
\boxed{P_{2\pi}=(-1)^2(-1)^0=+1.}
$$
For three spinless <pions>, use Jacobi orbital angular <momenta> $l,L$. Coupling them to total zero requires $l=L$, and hence
$$
\boxed{P_{3\pi}=(-1)^3(-1)^{l+L}=-1.}
$$
Thus both channels cannot conserve <parity> in decays of one parent with a definite <parity>. The <Weak interaction>[weak decay] interaction permits the observed coexistence.

<Dalitz plots> identify the three-body events, determine their internal <kinematics>, and allow <Probability amplitude>[amplitude] and <Angular momentum>[angular-momentum analyses] through their populations and <resonance> structures. Together with the <spin> and parent-identity evidence, they support the <parity> assignment. They are not a standalone parity-odd observable: reversing all three <momenta> leaves every $s_{ij}$, and therefore every point on the plot, unchanged. A flat distribution by itself cannot establish <parity violation> without the channel and <spin> information.

A <Unitary matrix>[unitary] three-by-three <matrix> has nine real parameters: three <rotation> angles and six <phases>. For <Dirac field>[Dirac] leptons, the independent field rephasings remove five <phases>, because one common <phase> acts trivially. Three angles and one physical <CP violation>[Dirac phase] remain. For <Majorana neutrino>[Majorana] <neutrinos> two additional <phases> cannot be removed, but they cancel from ordinary <Neutrino oscillation>[oscillation] <probabilities>.

\boxed{U_{13}(\delta)=\begin{pmatrix}c_{13}&0&s_{13}e^{-i\delta}\\0&1&0\\-s_{13}e^{i\delta}&0&c_{13}\end{pmatrix}.}

The lower-row <phase> factors in the resulting full <matrix> are $e^{+i\delta}$, opposite to the $e^{-i\delta}$ in $U_{e3}$. ==== Solution

At low charged-lepton <energy>, collisional <ionization> and <Elementary excitation>[excitation] dominate over radiation. A slowing <positron> also eventually annihilates, usually yielding <photons>. Low-energy <photons> lose <energy> through <Compton scattering> and, at sufficiently low <energy>, <Photoelectric effect>[photoelectric absorption]; <pair production> is kinematically unavailable below its threshold.

The <electron> <Critical energy of an electromagnetic shower>[critical energy] $E_c$ is the <energy> where mean <bremsstrahlung> and collisional losses are equal. With $-(dE/dx)_{\mathrm{rad}}\simeq E/X_0$ and a slowly varying collisional loss,
$$
\boxed{\left|\frac{dE}{dx}\right|_{\mathrm{rad},E_c}=\left|\frac{dE}{dx}\right|_{\mathrm{coll},E_c}.}
$$
Above this <energy>, radiation and <Pair production>[pair creation] multiply <Electromagnetic shower>[shower] particles efficiently. Below it, charged particles mainly deposit their <energy> by collisions and the <Electromagnetic shower>[shower] dies out. <Critical energy of an electromagnetic shower>[Critical energy] is material dependent; it is not a new photon-production threshold.

==== Solution

In the <binary branching model of an electromagnetic shower>, after $n$ ideal equal-sharing generations, the model has $N_n=2^n$ particles of <energy> $E_n=E_0/2^n$, at depth $nX_0$. Stop the branching when $E_n\le E_c$. Thus
$$
\boxed{n_{\mathrm{stop}}=\left\lceil\log_2\frac{E_0}{E_c}\right\rceil
=\lceil\log_2(10^4)\rceil=14.}
$$
The continuous estimate is $13.29X_0$. At 13 generations the particles still have approximately $12.2\,\mathrm{MeV}$, whereas at 14 they have approximately $6.10\,\mathrm{MeV}$. The ideal branching model therefore suggests roughly $13$–$14X_0$ before multiplication stops.

This is not an accurate containment criterion. Real conversion and radiation distances fluctuate, <bremsstrahlung> <energy> sharing is broad rather than always one half, some energetic branches penetrate beyond the average, and particles below <Critical energy of an electromagnetic shower>[critical energy] still have stopping and absorption ranges. The simplified stopping depth is closer to its own model's shower-maximum scale than a depth containing all deposited <energy>. A <Particle calorimeter>[calorimeter] needs additional longitudinal depth to control leakage and event-to-event tails. These effects explain why approximately $25X_0$ can be required even though the equal-sharing estimate is smaller. The toy model does not predict the realistic <Electromagnetic shower>[shower] maximum or leakage fraction quantitatively.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

The principal passive-material requirement is a short <radiation length> in centimetres, normally obtained using a dense, high-atomic-number <Sampling calorimeter>[absorber] such as lead or tungsten. It produces and contains an <electromagnetic shower> in a compact detector. Mechanical construction, transverse spread, cost, <Radiation damage>[radiation tolerance] and inactive gaps also affect the choice, but the <Sampling calorimeter>[absorber] is selected for <Electromagnetic shower>[shower] formation rather than signal production.

<Argon>[Liquid-argon] gaps are a suitable active sampling medium. <Ionization> <electrons> drift in an <electric field> and induce a measurable charge signal. Advantages include <Radiation damage>[radiation tolerance], stable response and useful spatial segmentation. Disadvantages include cryogenic infrastructure, high-voltage and purity requirements, signal collection times and electronic-noise control. Only the active fraction is measured directly, so fluctuations of <energy> shared between <Sampling calorimeter>[absorber] and active gaps introduce a sampling contribution to the resolution. A high active fraction and fine sampling help, at the price of construction complexity and additional channels.

=== f
{scope}

==== Solution

For independent contributions the parametrization means
$$
\boxed{\sigma^2(E)=A^2E+B^2+C^2E^2.}
$$
The <Statistical fluctuation>[stochastic] coefficient $A$ includes fluctuations in the sampled <Electromagnetic shower>[shower] fraction, the finite number of signal quanta, and intrinsic <Electromagnetic shower>[shower] fluctuations. A number of detected quanta proportional to $E$ has fluctuations proportional to $\sqrt E$, giving relative resolution proportional to $E^{-1/2}$. The <noise> coefficient $B$ describes an approximately fixed energy-equivalent readout uncertainty, including <electronic noise> and, for specified running conditions, <Collision pileup>[Pile-up] contributions. The constant term $C$ represents residual fractional nonuniformity, <Calorimeter calibration>[calibration] and response variations, imperfect correction for inactive material, and leakage effects over the fitted range. The simple three-term model is an approximation; leakage and <Collision pileup>[Pile-up] need not follow these powers exactly.

A <homogeneous calorimeter> uses the showering material itself as the active medium, for example a <Scintillator>[scintillating crystal]. It removes the absorber-versus-active sampling fluctuation and can have a much smaller <Statistical fluctuation>[stochastic] term, but it does not eliminate <Poisson distribution>[signal statistics] or intrinsic <Electromagnetic shower>[shower] fluctuations. Its <noise> and constant terms are not automatically smaller: they depend on light collection, <Calorimeter calibration>[calibration], temperature, radiation damage, electronics and geometry. <Homogeneous calorimeter>[Homogeneous detectors] can offer excellent <energy> resolution and compact construction, but large crystal volumes can be costly and fine longitudinal segmentation difficult. <Sampling calorimeter>[Sampling detectors] often provide economical large coverage and flexible segmentation with robust <Sampling calorimeter>[absorbers], at the cost of sampling fluctuations and inactive material. If <energy> is expressed in GeV, $A$ has units $\mathrm{GeV}^{1/2}$, $B$ has units GeV and $C$ is dimensionless.

Assume first an ultrarelativistic <Charged current>[charged-current] interaction with negligible <electron> <mass> in the centre-of-mass <spin> analysis. Choose the incident <antineutrino> along $+z$. Its right <helicity> gives <spin> $+z$; the interacting <Helicity>[left-helicity] <electron> moves along $-z$ and also has <spin> $+z$. The total initial projection is $+1$. The final <antineutrino> and selected <electron> likewise have total <spin> $+1$ along the outgoing <antineutrino> direction. The spin-one <rotation> therefore gives
$$
\mathcal M\propto d^1_{11}(\theta^\star)=\frac{1+\cos\theta^\star}{2},\qquad
\boxed{\frac{d\sigma_{\mathrm{CC}}}{d\cos\theta^\star}\propto(1+\cos\theta^\star)^2.}
$$
An unpolarized <electron> target changes the <normalization> through the <spin> average, not this selected-current shape. For <electron> <antineutrinos> the <Charged current>[charged-current] diagram is the $s$-channel $W^-$ graph. A <Fermi interaction>[contact approximation] further requires $m_e^2\ll s\ll m_W^2,m_Z^2$.

**The full Standard Model also has <Neutral current>[neutral-current] $Z$ exchange.** In the contact limit it supplies a right-electron contribution as well as interference in the left-electron channel. With $g_L=1/2+\sin^2\theta_W$, $g_R=\sin^2\theta_W$, the full high-energy shape is proportional to $g_L^2(1+\cos\theta^\star)^2/4+g_R^2$. Thus the pure squared angular form is an explicitly stated <Charged current>[charged-current] approximation, not an exact prediction for all Standard Model contributions. At <momentum> transfers comparable to the <Electroweak gauge boson>[weak-boson] <masses>, propagators must also be retained.<Electroweak Model and Constraints on New Physics by J. Erler and A. Freitas (2024)>[§10.3.1, equation (10.30) and following electron-neutrino charged-current shift: both left- and right-electron weights]{ref}

==== 2
{scope}

===== Solution

Neglecting the <electron> <mass> in the centre-of-mass <kinematics>, take $k=(E^*,0,0,E^*)$, $p=(E^*,0,0,-E^*)$, and $k'=(E^*,E^*\sin\theta^\star,0,E^*\cos\theta^\star)$. Then $p\cdot k=2E^{*2}$ and $p\cdot k'=E^{*2}(1+\cos\theta^\star)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{y=1-\frac{p\cdot k'}{p\cdot k}=\frac{1-\cos\theta^\star}{2},\qquad 0\le y\le1.}
$$
The constant <Jacobian determinant>[Jacobian] $|d\cos\theta^\star/dy|=2$ gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\sigma_{\mathrm{CC}}}{dy}\propto(1-y)^2,\qquad
\frac1{\sigma_{\mathrm{CC}}}\frac{d\sigma_{\mathrm{CC}}}{dy}=3(1-y)^2.}
$$
Including both Standard Model chiral channels in the <Fermi interaction>[contact approximation] instead gives $d\sigma/dy\propto g_L^2(1-y)^2+g_R^2$. The right-electron term is flat in $y$.

In the electron-rest laboratory frame $p=(m_e,\mathbf0)$, the invariant definition immediately gives
$$
\boxed{y=\frac{m_e(E_\nu-E'_\nu)}{m_eE_\nu}=\frac{E_\nu-E'_\nu}{E_\nu}.}
$$
This last equality is exact. With <electron> <mass> retained in the centre-of-mass relation, the angular formula becomes $y=(s-m_e^2)(1-\cos\theta^\star)/(2s)$, approaching the boxed massless relation at high <energy>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Treat a <nucleon> as incoherent collinear spin-half <quarks> and <antiquarks> carrying fractions $x$ of its <momentum>. Neglect their <masses> and transverse motion, use a <Charged current>[charged-current] four-fermion interaction, and take $Q^2\ll m_W^2$. The subprocess <energy> is $\hat s\simeq xs$. <Antineutrino> scattering on a <quark> has the same selected-current <helicity> factor $(1-y)^2$ as in part a; crossing to an <antiquark> changes the allowed <spin> pairing and gives a flat distribution. Multiplying the elementary strength by the respective <Parton distribution functions>[parton densities] gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{d^2\sigma(\bar\nu N)}{dx\,dy}=\frac{G_F^2sx}{\pi}\left[(1-y)^2q(x)+\bar q(x)\right].}
$$
Here $q,\bar q$ are the effective <Parton distribution function>[weak flavour combinations] with the <Isoscalar target>[isoscalar] proton-neutron average and <CKM matrix>[CKM weights] absorbed into their definitions. With only light <Flavor eigenstates>[flavours] and <Charge symmetry of nucleon parton distributions>[charge symmetry], the relevant up and down combinations coincide on an <Isoscalar target>[isoscalar target]. Rescaling those density definitions requires rescaling the prefactor consistently.

The equation is a leading-order scaling result: it neglects logarithmic <QCD> evolution, heavy-quark thresholds, <Higher twist>[higher-twist] and <Deep inelastic scattering>[target-mass] corrections, <Nuclear parton distribution function>[nuclear modifications], and <W boson propagator>[weak-propagator] suppression. The current produces $\mu^+$ for an incident <muon> <antineutrino>. At higher <momentum> transfer the weak propagator supplies a factor approximately $[m_W^2/(m_W^2+Q^2)]^2$, and at low <energy> <resonance> and quasielastic scattering invalidate a pure <Deep inelastic scattering>[DIS] description.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For a <nucleon> at rest and beam <energy> well above its <mass> scales, $s\simeq2m_NE$. If the leading <Parton distribution functions>[parton distributions] are approximately independent of beam <energy>, integration over $x,y$ therefore makes both total <cross sections> approximately proportional to $E$. The plotted plateaus are evidence for scattering on approximately pointlike constituents in this range, with the departures expected from <QCD> evolution and threshold effects.

Define the momentum-weighted contents $Q=\int_0^1xq(x)dx$ and $\bar Q=\int_0^1x\bar q(x)dx$. Since $\int_0^1(1-y)^2dy=1/3$, the two <cross sections> obey
$$
\frac{\sigma_\nu}{E}\propto Q+\frac{\bar Q}{3},\qquad
\frac{\sigma_{\bar\nu}}{E}\propto\frac Q3+\bar Q.
$$
Read the original high-energy plateaus as approximately $0.67$ and $0.33$ in the plotted $10^{-38}\,\mathrm{cm^2/GeV}$ units. With $R=\sigma_{\bar\nu}/\sigma_\nu\simeq1/2$ and $r=\bar Q/Q$,
$$
R=\frac{1/3+r}{1+r/3},\qquad
\boxed{r=\frac{3R-1}{3-R}\simeq0.20.}
$$
Using the literal readings $0.33/0.67$ gives $r=0.1905$, so twenty percent is an appropriate graphical estimate, not a precision fit. A purely valence target would have $R=1/3$, while a fully symmetric sea would give $R=1$.

The <neutrino-antineutrino DIS sea momentum ratio> is a ratio of <momentum> fractions in the effective <Flavor eigenstate>[flavour] combinations, not an unweighted count of <antiquarks>. The cross-section integral contains $xq(x)$: it cannot determine $\int\bar q/\int q$ without additional assumptions about their shapes. A distribution growing at small $x$ can have a large number integral while its <momentum> integral remains finite.

The dominant <semileptonic decay> is $t\to bW^+\to b\ell^+\nu_\ell$. Replacing $b$ by $s$ or $d$ weights the <Probability amplitude>[amplitude] by $V_{ts}$ or $V_{td}$ rather than $V_{tb}$. Neglecting the down-type <masses>,
$$
\boxed{B(t\to bW^+)\simeq\frac{|V_{tb}|^2}{|V_{td}|^2+|V_{ts}|^2+|V_{tb}|^2}=0.9983}
$$
with the source's rounded <matrix> entries. For the $W$, three leptonic widths and two open <Up quark>[up-type quark] rows with three colours give nine common width units. Thus, ignoring <QCD> and final-mass corrections,
$$
\boxed{B(t\to b\ell^+\nu_\ell)\simeq\frac{0.9983}{9}\simeq11.1\%\quad\text{per lepton flavour}.}
$$
Summing all three <Flavor eigenstates>[flavours] gives approximately one third; summing only <electron> and <muon> modes gives approximately $22.2\%$. These are per-top <branching ratios>, not the separate <branching ratio> of a specified two-top event topology.

An <Lepton isolation>[isolated] high-transverse-momentum charged lepton, <missing transverse momentum> from the <neutrino>, and a tagged <Bottom-quark jet tagging>[bottom jet] form a much cleaner signature than an entirely hadronic final state amid <QCD> <jets>. <Electrons> and <muons> are the easiest charged-lepton <Flavor eigenstates>[flavours] to identify cleanly. <Tau lepton>[Tau] decays add <neutrinos> and often hadrons, making identification and reconstruction more difficult. ==== Solution

Top decay occurs before a <Hadronization>[colour-bound] hadron can form. Using representative scales $\Gamma_t\sim1$–$1.5\,\mathrm{GeV}$ and $\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}\sim0.2\,\mathrm{GeV}$,
$$
\boxed{\tau_t\sim\frac\hbar{\Gamma_t}\sim5\times10^{-25}\,\mathrm s
<\tau_{\mathrm{had}}\sim\frac\hbar{\Lambda_{\mathrm{QCD}}}\sim3\times10^{-24}\,\mathrm s.}
$$
Thus <top quarks> do not live long enough to form ordinary top-containing mesons. The decay products still participate in <QCD> radiation and <hadronization>, so “free decay” here means decay before binding, not a stable observable free <Color charge>[coloured] asymptotic particle.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The weak <charged current> couples an <Up quark>[up-type quark] to the rotated down field $d'_{iL}=\sum_jV_{ij}d_{jL}$. A <neutral current> is <Flavor eigenstate>[flavour] universal within equal-charge <quarks>. Rotating it into the <mass> basis therefore gives
$$
\sum_i\bar d'_{iL}\gamma^\mu d'_{iL}
=\sum_{j,k}\bar d_{jL}\gamma^\mu(V^\dagger V)_{jk}d_{kL}
=\sum_j\bar d_{jL}\gamma^\mu d_{jL}.
$$
The off-diagonal terms vanish by <CKM matrix>[CKM unitarity], so no tree-level <Flavor-changing neutral current>[flavour-changing neutral current] is generated. In the two-generation description, the orthogonal strange-charm partner completes the <rotation> whose single down-strange combination alone would have produced an off-diagonal neutral coupling. The complete multiplet supplies the cancellation.

At <Loop diagram>[loop] level, a flavour-changing <Probability amplitude>[amplitude] has the structure
$$
\mathcal A_{a\to b}\propto\sum_iV_{ai}V_{bi}^*F(m_i^2/m_W^2),\qquad a\ne b.
$$
If all internal <masses> were equal, $F$ would be common and $\sum_iV_{ai}V_{bi}^*=0$. Subtracting an arbitrary common value makes the suppression explicit:
$$
\boxed{\mathcal A_{a\to b}\propto\sum_iV_{ai}V_{bi}^*\left[F(m_i^2/m_W^2)-F(m_{\mathrm{ref}}^2/m_W^2)\right].}
$$
This is the <Glashow-Iliopoulos-Maiani mechanism>. Unequal <masses> leave small loop-level <Flavor-changing neutral currents>[flavour-changing neutral currents], so the statement that they are never observed would be too strong: the mechanism suppresses them and forbids them at tree level. The cancellation requires complex <CKM> products; the rounded table of absolute values alone must not be summed as if every <phase> were positive.

{scope}

==== Solution

The literal $t\to qZ\gamma$ final state has a three-vertex <Loop diagram>[loop] using the <quartic W-photon-Z gauge coupling>[Standard Model quartic vertex] $W^+W^-Z\gamma$. The two other vertices are $t\to d_iW^+$ and $d_iW^+\to q$, with $q=u,c$. The internal <Down quark>[down-type quark] joins those two vertices, while two charged-vector propagators join them through the quartic vertex emitting both neutral bosons. The bottom panel of the figure shows this topology, satisfying the three-vertex hint without dropping the <photon>. Its additional electromagnetic coupling is contained in the four-leg vertex; counting vertices alone does not determine coupling order.

The companion single-$Z$ <Penguin diagram>[penguin] diagram illustrates $t\to qZ$: it instead attaches the neutral boson to the internal <quark> line and has three cubic vertices. These are different final states. A complete <Gauge invariance>[gauge-invariant] <Probability amplitude>[amplitude] requires all companion attachments; for $qZ\gamma$ these include <photon> radiation from charged lines as well as the quartic graph.

Both <Flavor eigenstate>[flavour] sums have the form
$$
\mathcal A(t\to qZ[\gamma])\propto\sum_{i=d,s,b}V_{ti}V_{qi}^*F_i,
$$
where the <Loop diagram>[loop] function also depends on the final-state <kinematics>. After cancellation of a mass-independent part by <GIM mechanism>, the <Bottom quark>[bottom-quark] <mass> gives the largest surviving contribution: $m_b^2\gg m_s^2,m_d^2$, while $|V_{tb}V_{cb}|\simeq0.042$ is not unusually small compared with the strange combination. The larger $|V_{cb}|$ than $|V_{ub}|$ then favours final <Charm quark>[charm]. Schematically,
$$
\boxed{t\to cZ\gamma\text{ with virtual }b\text{ is the favoured flavour combination},\qquad
\frac{|\mathcal A_c|^2}{|\mathcal A_u|^2}\sim\frac{|V_{cb}|^2}{|V_{ub}|^2}\simeq110.}
$$
The same <Flavor eigenstate>[flavour] preference applies to the separate two-body modes $t\to cZ$ and $t\to c\gamma$. This estimates a relative <Flavor eigenstate>[flavour] preference, not an absolute <branching ratio>. <Loop diagram>[Loop] factors and mass-difference suppression remain, so these channels are fully compatible with <GIM mechanism>[GIM]. A resolved extra <photon> also introduces another electromagnetic coupling and three-body <Lorentz-invariant phase space>[phase space]; an experimental $Z\gamma$ definition needs photon-energy and <Particle isolation>[isolation] cuts to distinguish hard radiation from the soft limit.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

For a $t\to cZ$ search, the cleanest neutral-boson reconstruction is $Z\to e^+e^-$ or $\mu^+\mu^-$: require an <Lepton isolation>[isolated] opposite-sign same-flavour pair near the $Z$ <mass> and a <jet> forming the top <mass> with that pair. In top-pair events, tagging the other top through its ordinary $bW$ decay provides a <Bottom-quark jet tagging>[bottom jet] and, if its $W$ is leptonic, another <Lepton isolation>[isolated] lepton and <missing transverse momentum>. Kinematic reconstruction and heavy-flavour information help reject vector-boson-plus-jets and other backgrounds. Hadronic or invisible $Z$ decays have higher rate but less distinctive backgrounds or incomplete <mass> reconstruction.

For the literal $t\to cZ\gamma$ channel, additionally require a prompt <particle isolation>[isolated] <photon> and reconstruct the top with $Z+\gamma+$<jet>. For the alternative $t\to c\gamma$, use an <Particle isolation>[isolated] energetic <photon> and <jet> near the top <mass>, again exploiting the other top as a tag. These are search signatures; no present-day observation claim is needed to interpret the historical question.
