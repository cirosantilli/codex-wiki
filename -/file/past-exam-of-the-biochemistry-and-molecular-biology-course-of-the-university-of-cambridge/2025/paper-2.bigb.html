For a primary <sigma factor> of the $\sigma^{70}$ family, distinguish the following regions. Region 1.1 restrains inappropriate <DNA> binding by free <sigma factor> and must move out of the downstream-<DNA> channel during formation of the open <RNA polymerase> complex; region 1.2 contributes to recognition around the discriminator downstream of the $-10$ element. Domain 2 binds the <RNA polymerase> core, recognizes the $-10$ <gene promoter> element and helps melt the duplex and stabilize the exposed non-template strand. Domain 3 recognizes an extended $-10$ element; its region 3.2 projects toward the active-centre channel, helps position template and initiating substrates, and must be displaced as the nascent <RNA> grows. Domain 4 recognizes the $-35$ element through a <helix-turn-helix> motif and contacts the <RNA polymerase> beta flap and some <transcription activators>. **Domains 2 and 4 supply the principal $-10$ and $-35$ sequence recognition, with the other regions controlling opening and productive initiation.** This domain map concerns the $\sigma^{70}$ family, rather than every structurally distinct bacterial <sigma factor>.<Structure of the bacterial RNA polymerase promoter specificity sigma subunit by Elizabeth A. Campbell and others>[Abstract and structural analysis of sigma domains 2, 3 and 4]{ref}

Incubate a labelled precursor <messenger RNA> containing an authentic $3'$-processing signal with a <HeLa> nuclear extract, then resolve the products by <denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis>. With <ATP>, the upstream cleavage product acquires a heterogeneous <poly(A) tail>. Replace <ATP> with an appropriate non-incorporable analogue, such as alpha,beta-methylene-ATP: a discrete product ending at the normal cleavage site still appears, but the extended tail does not. Thus **cleavage can occur when <polyadenylation> is blocked**; it is not cleavage brought about by synthesis of the tail.<Accurate cleavage and polyadenylation of exogenous RNA substrate by Claire L. Moore and Phillip A. Sharp>[Abstract: cleavage without polyadenylation in the presence of alpha,beta-methylene-ATP]{ref}

Conversely, supply an <RNA> substrate already ending at the normal cleavage site: addition of <ATP> and the appropriate <poly(A) polymerase> machinery can extend its free $3'$ hydroxyl without any new endonucleolytic cut.<The AAUAAA sequence is required both for cleavage and for polyadenylation of simian virus 40 pre-mRNA in vitro by David Zarkower and others>[Abstract: efficient polyadenylation of pre-cleaved RNA substrates in nuclear extract]{ref} Compare precursor, pre-cleaved substrate, no-extract and no-<ATP> controls, and map the product ends to distinguish correct processing from non-specific degradation. **These are separable reactions, normally coordinated in one processing complex.** Their separability does not imply that they use completely independent recognition factors.

A detectable <burst kinetics> phase requires rapid formation and detection of an initial <reaction product>, followed by a slower step that regenerates competent <enzyme>. A common <hydrolysis> mechanism is
$$
E+S\rightleftharpoons ES\xrightarrow{k_2}E^*+P_1\xrightarrow{k_3}E+P_2,
$$
where $E^*$ can be a covalent <acyl-enzyme intermediate>. With excess <substrate>, rapid binding and $k_2\gg k_3$, the first turnover rapidly releases $P_1$ and traps much of the <enzyme> as $E^*$. Subsequent turnovers wait for the slow regeneration step. **The measured product must report the fast step:** monitoring only $P_2$ need not reveal a burst. Fast mixing and sufficient time resolution are needed, while spontaneous <substrate> <hydrolysis>, depletion and <enzyme> inactivation must be excluded.

For an illustrative saturated two-step cycle, initially unmodified <enzyme> concentration $E_T$ gives
$$
[P_1](t)=\frac{E_Tk_2k_3}{k_2+k_3}t+E_T\left(\frac{k_2}{k_2+k_3}\right)^2\left(1-e^{-(k_2+k_3)t}\right).
$$
The second term approaches a burst amplitude near $E_T$ when $k_2\gg k_3$, followed by the slower steady-state slope. A covalent intermediate is a useful example, not a logical requirement for every possible burst mechanism.

The enzyme <adenylate kinase> rapidly interconverts the adenine <nucleotides>:
$$
2\mathrm{ADP}\rightleftharpoons\mathrm{ATP}+\mathrm{AMP},\qquad K=\frac{[\mathrm{ATP}][\mathrm{AMP}]}{[\mathrm{ADP}]^2}.
$$
For approximately fixed conditions and near-equilibrium activity ratios,
$$
\frac{[\mathrm{AMP}]}{[\mathrm{ATP}]}=K\left(\frac{[\mathrm{ADP}]}{[\mathrm{ATP}]}\right)^2.
$$
**The AMP:ATP ratio responds quadratically to ADP:ATP.** Doubling ADP:ATP therefore quadruples AMP:ATP. In working <skeletal muscle>, <ATP> is also buffered by <creatine kinase> and continuing <ATP> production, so its fractional change is relatively small. The initially smaller <AMP> pool can undergo a much larger fractional increase, providing a sensitive signal of energetic stress to regulators such as <AMP-activated protein kinase>. This concerns sensitivity of relative changes; it does not mean that the absolute <AMP> concentration exceeds <ATP>.

The <mitochondrial fission-fusion cycle> is continuous remodeling of a mitochondrial population by division and merger. In mammalian <biological cells>, <mitofusins> promote fusion of outer mitochondrial membranes, while <OPA1> supports fusion of inner mitochondrial membranes. Fusion mixes matrix contents and permits functional complementation between <mitochondria>. Genetic disruption and rescue of <mitofusins> demonstrated their requirement for normal mitochondrial fusion.<Mitofusins Mfn1 and Mfn2 coordinately regulate mitochondrial fusion and are essential for embryonic development by Hsiuchen Chen and others>[Abstract and Results: mitofusin loss, fragmented mitochondria and rescue]{ref}

For fission, recruited <Drp1> assembles at constrictions and uses <GTP>-dependent remodeling to help divide the <organelle>.<Dynamin-related protein Drp1 is required for mitochondrial division in mammalian cells by Elena Smirnova and others>[Abstract and Results: Drp1 recruitment and division defects]{ref} **The balance controls network shape, distribution and quality control.** Division can help distribute <mitochondria> to daughter <biological cells> and segregate damaged portions for <mitophagy>; fusion can restore mixed contents. These are regulated competing events in a network, not an obligatory synchronized sequence that every <mitochondrion> completes on a fixed timetable.

A <steroid hormone> crosses the <lipid bilayer> and binds an intracellular <nuclear receptor>. For a cytoplasmic <glucocorticoid receptor>, ligand binding alters association with <molecular chaperones>, exposes nuclear-targeting functions and promotes accumulation in the <cell nucleus>. Other <steroid hormone> receptors already have substantial nuclear localization. The activated <receptor> binds specific <hormone response elements> in <DNA>, often as a dimer, and changes recruitment of <transcription coactivators> or <transcription corepressors>. These factors alter local <chromatin> and recruitment or activity of the <RNA polymerase II> machinery. **The result is selective activation or repression of target <genes>**, followed by altered <messenger RNA> and <protein> abundance; the <hormone> itself does not act as an <RNA polymerase> or directly rewrite the <DNA> sequence.

<Fucci> uses two fluorescent <protein> fusions carrying cell-cycle-regulated degradation signals: a red/orange <Cdt1> fragment accumulates mainly in <G1 phase>, whereas a green <geminin> fragment accumulates through <S phase>, <G2 phase> and much of <M phase>. Opposing activities of <SCF ubiquitin ligase> and <anaphase-promoting complex> regulate reporter degradation through the <ubiquitin-proteasome system>; the fluorophores report those regulated stabilities rather than simply measuring total endogenous <Cdt1> and <geminin>.<Visualizing spatiotemporal dynamics of multicellular cell-cycle progression by Asako Sakaue-Sawano and others>[Abstract and reporter-design experiments]{ref}

The basic two-color version does not independently resolve every phase, and a color transition is an indirect readout of <ubiquitin ligase> activity. An <antibody-drug conjugate> couples an <antibody> against a tumor-associated surface <antigen> to a potent cytotoxic payload through a suitable chemical linker. Binding enriches delivery to <biological cells> displaying the target. Many designs undergo receptor-mediated <endocytosis>, trafficking through <endosomes> to a <lysosome>, and release of an active drug through linker cleavage or degradation of the <antibody>. The released payload can disrupt <microtubules> or damage <DNA>, killing the target <biological cell>. **Specificity comes from targeted delivery combined with controlled payload release.** It is relative rather than absolute: normal <biological cells> may share the <antigen>, and a membrane-permeable released payload can also affect neighboring <biological cells>. Successful action therefore depends on antigen abundance, accessibility, internalization, linker stability and payload sensitivity.

The <hydrogenosomes> and <mitosomes> are specialized mitochondrion-related <organelles> found in various anaerobic or oxygen-limited <eukaryotes>. **Typical <hydrogenosomes> support anaerobic <ATP> production**, converting substrates such as <pyruvate> into products including <acetate>, <carbon dioxide> and molecular <hydrogen>. <Substrate-level phosphorylation> supplies <ATP>, while <hydrogenase> disposes of reducing equivalents as <hydrogen>; oxygen is not the terminal acceptor of a conventional respiratory chain in this pathway.<Hydrogenosome, a cytoplasmic organelle of the anaerobic flagellate Tritrichomonas foetus, and its role in pyruvate metabolism by Donald G. Lindmark and Miklós Müller>[Abstract and biochemical characterization of hydrogenosomal pyruvate metabolism]{ref}

**The <mitosomes> retain selected essential metabolic functions but do not generate <ATP> by <oxidative phosphorylation>.** In <Giardia>, their role includes <iron-sulfur cluster> assembly and <protein> maturation, demonstrated using organelle markers and biochemical reconstitution.<Mitochondrial remnant organelles of Giardia function in iron-sulphur protein maturation by Jorge Tovar and others>[Abstract: localization and iron-sulfur protein maturation]{ref} Their functions are lineage-dependent: <Entamoeba histolytica> <mitosomes> compartmentalize a <sulfate activation> pathway.<Mitosomes in Entamoeba histolytica contain a sulfate activation pathway by Fumika Mi-ichi and others>[Abstract and Results: mitosome fractionation, protein localization and activated sulfate products]{ref} Thus neither organelle class should be described simply as a nonfunctional <mitochondrion>.

The catalytic <serine residue> supplies a <nucleophile>: its side-chain oxygen attacks the carbonyl carbon of the <beta-lactam> ring. This generates a tetrahedral intermediate, followed by opening of the ring and formation of a covalent <acyl-enzyme intermediate>. For the <Hanes-Woolf plot>, the slope is $1/V_{\max}$, the vertical intercept is $K_m/V_{\max}$, and the horizontal intercept is $-K_m$. <Hexokinase> consumes exported <ATP> in the reaction
$$
\mathrm{glucose+ATP\longrightarrow glucose\ 6\!\! -\!phosphate+ADP}.
$$
The <adenine nucleotide translocator> imports regenerated <ADP>, permitting <ATP synthase> to use the <proton-motive force>. Increased proton return reduces the opposing gradient and allows faster activity of the <electron transport chain> and oxygen consumption: this is <respiratory control>. The first <hexokinase> additions therefore increase demand and respiration. **By about the fourth addition, demand no longer limits the measured flux**: the respiratory and transport system has reached its available capacity, so more <hexokinase> cannot increase it. This does not imply that <hexokinase> ceases functioning, and its zero incremental control at maximal demand does not make it dispensable for maintaining that demand.

= Solution

At low <ATP> demand, the <proton-motive force> is high and much of the remaining oxygen consumption supports <proton leak>. Increasing leak conductance then creates a substantial extra demand for proton pumping and strongly changes respiratory flux. At high phosphorylation demand, proton return through <ATP synthase> is large, the <proton-motive force> is lower, and leak contributes a much smaller part of total proton return. **Control of oxygen consumption shifts toward substrate supply and the respiratory machinery, so the leak <flux control coefficient> becomes small.** The plot's value near zero does not mean that the physical leak current vanishes; the separate maximal-rate table likewise gives a small nonzero coefficient, $0.04$.

The <mitochondrial dicarboxylate carrier> is an inner-membrane transport <protein> that exchanges suitable dicarboxylates, including <succinate> and <malate>, with counter-substrates such as <phosphate>. = Solution
{parent=f}

The reported coefficients add to
$$
0.29+0.04+0.33+0.17+0.03+0=0.86.
$$
For a complete set of independently scaled activities controlling a steady-state flux, the <flux summation theorem> gives $\sum_i C_i^J=1$, where $C_i^J=\partial\ln J/\partial\ln E_i$. One way to see this is to scale all reaction capacities by the same factor: steady-state concentrations can remain the same while all fluxes scale by that factor, so the summed logarithmic sensitivities equal one. **The listed processes therefore leave a net coefficient of about $0.14$ unaccounted for under the model assumptions.** Measurement uncertainty, imperfect inhibitor specificity or incompatible measurements could also contribute to the discrepancy. Control is distributed; neither the sum nor the largest individual coefficient identifies a single universal rate-limiting step.
