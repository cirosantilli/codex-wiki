The <photon> relation gives $\lambda=hc/E=2.4797\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm m$. The diffraction-limited angular scale of an <aperture> of diameter $D$ is therefore
$$
\boxed{\theta\sim\lambda/D=2.48\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{rad}.}
$$
For a uniformly illuminated circular <aperture>, the <Rayleigh criterion> instead gives $1.22\lambda/D=3.03\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{rad}$. Both express the same requested estimate. This is an ideal <diffraction limit>, conditional on the effective diameter behaving as a coherent imaging <aperture>; <photon energy> and diameter alone do not specify the performance of a practical gamma-ray telescope.

Let $T$ be the common <tension> and $\mu_j$ the <linear mass densities>. The <characteristic impedance of a stretched string> is $Z_j=T/c_j=\sqrt{T\mu_j}$. Continuity of <displacement> and transverse <force> at a massless junction gives $1+r=t$ and $Z_1(1-r)=Z_2t$. Consequently
$$
r=\frac{Z_1-Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2}=\frac{1-\sqrt3}{1+\sqrt3},\qquad
\boxed{\mathcal R=|r|^2=(2-\sqrt3)^2=0.07180.}
$$
**The reflected <power> is $7.18\%$.** The minus sign of $r$ represents a phase reversal, not negative <power>. Both incident and reflected <waves> travel in the first string, so their <power> ratio is simply the squared displacement-amplitude ratio. As a check, $\mathcal T=(Z_2/Z_1)|t|^2=0.92820$ and $\mathcal R+\mathcal T=1$.

= Solution
{parent=3}

Compare a directly transmitted ray emerging at $A$ with a ray that makes one extra round trip inside the <Fabry–Pérot interferometer> and emerges at $C$. Both emerge parallel at angle $\theta$ to the plate normal. The extra internal distance is $2d/\cos\theta$, and their emergence points differ laterally by $AC=2d\tan\theta$. The first ray has already travelled $AC\sin\theta$ towards a common outgoing <wavefront> when the second reaches $C$. The <air-gap optical path difference in a Fabry–Pérot interferometer> follows because <optical path length> equals geometric path length in the air-gap approximation:
$$
\Delta=\frac{2d}{\cos\theta}-2d\tan\theta\sin\theta
=\frac{2d(1-\sin^2\theta)}{\cos\theta}
=\boxed{2d\cos\theta}.
$$
The subtraction is essential: comparing distances only inside the plates would compare different outgoing <wavefronts>. The corresponding propagation <phase difference> is $2\pi\Delta/\lambda$. Coating reflection phases can add a separate phase offset; they do not change this geometric <optical path difference>.

Count two <electron spin> states in each reciprocal-space cell of volume $(2\pi)^3/V$. Filling a <Fermi sphere> at zero <temperature> gives
$$
n=\frac{2}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{4\pi k_F^3}{3},\qquad
k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}=1.360\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}.
$$
The <free electron model> then gives
$$
\boxed{E_F=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}(3\pi^2n)^{2/3}
=1.129\times10^{-18}\,\mathrm J\simeq7.05\,\mathrm{eV}.}
$$
The <energy> zero is the bottom of the model's <conduction band>. The supplied <carrier density> already incorporates the one-electron-per-atom assumption; it must not be multiplied by another atomic count.

Write $a=V_c^{1/3}$ for the primitive <cubic lattice> spacing. With <wavevector> convention $e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}$, reciprocal-lattice primitive vectors have length $2\pi/a$. The first <Brillouin zone> is the cube $-\pi/a\leq k_j\leq\pi/a$, hence
$$
\boxed{V_{\mathrm{BZ}}=\left(\frac{2\pi}{a}\right)^3=\frac{(2\pi)^3}{V_c}.}
$$
It has the same volume as any primitive reciprocal unit cell. A reciprocal coordinate defined in cycles per metre would instead give $1/V_c$; that is a different convention from this $k$-space convention.

Take $m,k,b>0$, and define $\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}$ and $\gamma=b/(2m)$. Substitution of $e^{rt}$ into the unforced equation gives $r^2+2\gamma r+\omega_0^2=0$. The roots determine whether the return is oscillatory.

For return after a <displacement> in this ideal model, **<critical damping>, case (b), is the usual comfort choice**: it removes repeated bouncing while avoiding the slow relaxation mode of strong <overdamping>. For release from rest it gives a monotone return with the fastest asymptotic decay available without oscillation. This criterion does not prove optimal comfort for every road profile or initial <velocity>; actual comfort also depends on <acceleration>, road forcing and suspension travel.

For the forced response, set $\Omega=2\pi u/d$ and use $x_{\rm ss}=\operatorname{Re}(\widehat x e^{i\Omega t})$. Then
$$
(k-m\Omega^2+ib\Omega)\widehat x=kA,\qquad
\boxed{x_{\rm ss}(t)=X(\Omega)\cos(\Omega t-\delta),}
$$
$$
X(\Omega)=\frac{kA}{\sqrt{(k-m\Omega^2)^2+b^2\Omega^2}},\qquad
\delta=\operatorname{atan2}(b\Omega,k-m\Omega^2).
$$
The general forced solution adds the appropriate homogeneous transient from (a), (b) or (c). Initial data were not specified, so its constants cannot be fixed. Positive <damping> removes this transient at long times. This solution uses the particular driving <force> in the supplied equation; it does not silently replace it by a different moving-support model.

Maximising $X$ is minimising its squared denominator $D$. Differentiation gives
$$
\frac{dD}{d\Omega}=2\Omega[-2mk+2m^2\Omega^2+b^2],\qquad
\Omega_r^2=\frac{k}{m}-\frac{b^2}{2m^2}.
$$
A nonzero <displacement> <resonance> exists only if $b^2<2mk$. Here $\Omega_r^2=0.175\,\mathrm{s^{-2}}$, and therefore
$$
\boxed{u_r=\frac{d}{2\pi}\sqrt{0.175}=0.666\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\simeq2.40\,\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}},}
$$
$$
\boxed{X_{\max}=\frac{kA}{b\sqrt{k/m-b^2/(4m^2)}}=0.0616\,\mathrm m.}
$$
The low-frequency limit is $X(0)=A$, whereas $X\to0$ at large $\Omega$. The parameters give <damping ratio> $\zeta=b/(2\sqrt{mk})=0.456$, consistent with an underdamped system and the existence of this amplitude maximum.

For $b^2>4mk$, define $\beta=\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}$. The two <characteristic roots> are real and negative:

For a <monochromatic wave>, a constant phase $kx-\omega t$ moves at <phase velocity> $v_p=\omega/k$. For a narrow <wave packet> around $k$, expansion $\omega(k+\Delta k)\simeq\omega(k)+(d\omega/dk)\Delta k$ shows that its envelope moves at <group velocity> $v_g=d\omega/dk$. The envelope interpretation requires a sufficiently narrow spectrum and weak distortion; it is not a general identification with a causal signal front. Since $\omega=kv_p$ and $\lambda=2\pi/k$,
$$
v_g=v_p+k\frac{dv_p}{dk}
=\boxed{v_p-\lambda\frac{dv_p}{d\lambda}}.
$$
For the <gravity-capillary wave>, put $s=\sigma/\rho$ and take $k>0$. Direct differentiation gives
$$
v_p=\sqrt{g/k+sk},\qquad
v_g=\frac{g+3sk^2}{2\sqrt{gk+sk^3}},\qquad
\boxed{\frac{v_g}{v_p}=\frac{g+3sk^2}{2(g+sk^2)}
=1-\frac{g/k^2-s}{2(g/k^2+s)}.}
$$
Thus $v_g=v_p$ when $sk_*^2=g$, or
$$
\boxed{\lambda^*=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{\rho g}}=2.89\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm m.}
$$
At this <wavelength> the gravity and surface-tension restoring terms contribute equally. Also $d(v_p^2)/dk=-g/k^2+s=0$ with positive <second derivative>: this is the <minimum phase speed of a gravity-capillary wave>, $v_{p,\min}=(4g\sigma/\rho)^{1/4}=0.214\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Locally the carrier crests and narrow envelope travel together. **The medium has not become nondispersive**: $d^2\omega/dk^2=k_*d^2v_p/dk^2\ne0$ there, so a finite <wave packet> can still spread. In the gravity-dominated limit $v_g/v_p\to1/2$; in the capillary-dominated limit it tends to $3/2$.

= Solution
{parent=B8}

Take the <aperture> in $z=0$ and the observation point $P=(0,0,b)$, with $b>0$. Under <scalar diffraction theory>, the <Huygens-Fresnel principle> sums <complex amplitudes> from area elements, rather than summing their <intensities>. Apart from a common normalization,
$$
\psi(P)\propto\iint h(x,y)K(\theta)\frac{e^{ikR}}R\,dx\,dy,
\qquad R=\sqrt{b^2+x^2+y^2},\quad k=2\pi/\lambda.
$$
Here $h$ is the complex <aperture> transmission, $b$ is the axial distance, and $\theta$ is the angle of the secondary ray to the <aperture> normal. The <obliquity factor> $K$ describes the direction dependence of the secondary contribution; one Kirchhoff convention is $K=(1+\cos\theta)/2$ for normal illumination. Other scalar boundary approximations have slightly different angular weights, all tending to unity near the axis.

Put $r^2=x^2+y^2$. The <paraxial approximation> gives $R\simeq b+r^2/(2b)$ in the phase and $1/R\simeq1/b$ in the amplitude. Removing common $e^{ikb}/b$ yields
$$
\boxed{\psi(P)\propto\iint h(x,y)K(\theta)
\exp\left[\frac{i\pi(x^2+y^2)}{\lambda b}\right]dx\,dy.}
$$
Assumptions are uniform normally incident plane-wave illumination, coherent monochromatic propagation, <aperture> dimensions large enough for the scalar description, $r/b\ll1$, and phase accuracy $kr^4/(8b^3)\ll1$ over the relevant <aperture>. The last condition justifies dropping the quartic distance term; geometric smallness alone does not guarantee phase accuracy.

\Image[paper-2-fresnel-zones.png]
{title=Axial Fresnel geometry and phasor addition with alternate half-period zones blocked}

For a circular <aperture>, $h=1$ for $r\leq a$ and zero elsewhere. Rotational symmetry and $s=r^2$ give $dx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\varphi$, $r\,dr=ds/2$, and
$$
\psi\propto2\pi\int_0^a K(\theta)e^{i\pi r^2/(\lambda b)}r\,dr
=\pi\int_0^{a^2}K(\theta)e^{i\pi s/(\lambda b)}ds.
$$
The constant $\pi$ can be absorbed into the proportionality factor, producing the required one-dimensional <integral>.

At a focus a distance $f$ away, half-period-zone boundaries obey
$$
\sqrt{f^2+r_n^2}-f=\frac{n\lambda}{2},\qquad
r_n^2=n\lambda f+\frac{n^2\lambda^2}{4}\simeq n\lambda f.
$$
With slowly varying obliquity and illumination, each zone has almost the same area and its integrated <phasor> has the opposite sign to its neighbour. A <zone plate> blocks alternate zones, so the remaining <phasors> add instead of nearly cancelling. Choosing an open central zone, the first three open regions have outer boundaries $n=1,3,5$:
$$
\boxed{r_1=0.100\,\mathrm{mm},\qquad r_3=0.173\,\mathrm{mm},\qquad r_5=0.224\,\mathrm{mm}.}
$$
These are outer radii, not zone widths; the second open region is the annulus between $r_2$ and $r_3$.

For the specified outer radius, $R^2/(\lambda f)=49$: there are approximately 49 half-period zones, of which 25 odd zones are open. Normalize to the field $\psi_0$ of the unobstructed <plane wave>. The normalized Fresnel <aperture> <integral> is
$$
\frac{\psi}{\psi_0}=\frac{1}{i\lambda f}\int h\,e^{i\pi r^2/(\lambda f)}dA.
$$
For any odd open annulus this gives $2$, so the whole plate gives $\psi/\psi_0\simeq50$ and
$$
\boxed{I_{\rm focus}/I_{\rm unobstructed}\simeq4(25)^2=2.50\times10^3.}
$$
The <axial intensity gain of an amplitude zone plate> grows as the square of the number of open zones. Treating the number of zones continuously gives $49^2\simeq2.40\times10^3$, the same accuracy as an estimate. This large local <intensity> gain is consistent with <conservation of energy> because light is concentrated into a small focal spot. It is not a <power> efficiency; the ideal first-order <power> efficiency of a binary amplitude <zone plate> is only $1/\pi^2$.<Zone Plates by Janos Kirz and David Attwood>[X-Ray Data Booklet, §4.4, first-order efficiency paragraph]{ref}

“Without the <zone plate>” has here meant removing the finite plate and its outer stop, giving the unobstructed incident <wave>. If instead a clear circular <aperture> of radius $R$ is retained, its axial field is $\psi_{\rm clear}/\psi_0=1-e^{i49\pi}=2$, so **the ratio to that finite-aperture reference is $2500/4=625$**. The two reference experiments differ; neither ratio should be confused with total transmitted <power>.

A <Michelson interferometer> divides a source beam at a partially reflecting <beam splitter>. The two perpendicular arms terminate in <mirrors> whose reflected beams return to the splitter and overlap at the detector. The recombined fields must have overlapping <wavefronts> and compatible <polarization of waves>. Moving one <mirror> by $\Delta L$ changes the round-trip <optical path difference> by $2\Delta L$, so $N$ passing fringes determine $\Delta L=N\lambda/2$. Conversely a known <mirror> <displacement> measures <wavelength>. Scanning the delay can determine <temporal optical coherence> and, by <Fourier transform spectroscopy>, spectral structure. A compensating plate may equalize glass traversal, particularly for broadband illumination.

\Image[paper-2-michelson.png]
{title=Michelson optical arrangement and the calculated top-hat-spectrum interferogram}

For equal real peak field amplitudes, the superposition is
$$
E(t)=a\cos\omega t+a\cos(\omega t+\phi)
=2a\cos(\phi/2)\cos(\omega t+\phi/2).
$$
Time averaging the square over many optical cycles gives $\langle E^2\rangle=2a^2\cos^2(\phi/2)=a^2(1+\cos\phi)$. With $\phi=kx$ after absorbing a constant port-dependent phase into the zero of $x$,
$$
\boxed{I(x)=I_0[1+\cos(kx)].}
$$
Here $I_0$ is the sum of the two separate-arm <intensities>; maximum <intensity> is $2I_0$ and the ideal minimum is zero. Equal amplitudes give unit <interference visibility>.

For a stationary source with a flat <intensity> spectrum, different spectral components add in time-averaged <intensity>. Keeping their individual two-arm interference terms and defining $\operatorname{sinc}z=\sin z/z$ gives
$$
I(x)=C\int_{k_0-\Delta k/2}^{k_0+\Delta k/2}[1+\cos(k'x)]\,dk'
=C\Delta k\left[1+\cos(k_0x)\operatorname{sinc}\left(\frac{x\Delta k}{2}\right)\right].
$$
One must not coherently add unrelated source <frequencies> as if they had a fixed common phase. The sinc envelope is the <Fourier transform> of the rectangular spectral <intensity>.

Numerically, $\nu_0=5.00\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and $\Delta\nu=1.00\times10^{13}\,\mathrm{Hz}$. The annotated interferogram uses
$$
\boxed{\frac{I(x)}{C\Delta k}=1+\cos\left(\frac{2\pi\nu_0x}{c}\right)
\operatorname{sinc}\left(\frac{\pi\Delta\nu x}{c}\right).}
$$
It is even in $x$, has central value 2 and background 1, and its carrier-fringe period in <optical path difference> is $c/\nu_0=0.600\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. The first envelope zeros are at **$x=\pm c/\Delta\nu=\pm30.0\,\mathrm{\mu m}$**, corresponding to <mirror> shifts of $\pm15.0\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ from equal paths. Side lobes persist beyond the zeros; a negative sinc reverses fringe phase. The <intensity> remains between $1-|\operatorname{sinc}|$ and $1+|\operatorname{sinc}|$, not between the signed sinc curves with an assumed fixed ordering.

= Solution

The <free electron model> replaces the microscopic ionic potential and interacting electronic motion by independent mobile <electrons> in a uniform neutralizing background, with approximately parabolic dispersion $\epsilon=\hbar^2k^2/(2m^*)$. Its quantum version fills states according to <Fermi-Dirac statistics> and the <Pauli exclusion principle>. Scattering is represented by a <momentum relaxation time> $\tau$, rather than derived from the model's ideal free trajectories. This model explains a <Fermi energy> much larger than room-temperature thermal <energy>, an <electronic heat capacity> proportional to <temperature> at low <temperature>, and a Drude <electrical conductivity>. With appropriate scattering assumptions it also yields the <Wiedemann-Franz law>. It cannot by itself explain <band gaps>, detailed band anisotropy, strong correlations, or the microscopic <temperature> dependence of $\tau$.

The inertial term is <mass> times the rate of change of mean <drift velocity>. The electric <force> is $eE$, taking the printed $e$ as the signed <electron> charge, and $-\gamma\langle v\rangle=-m^*\langle v\rangle/\tau$ describes loss of directed momentum to scattering. <electrons> retain large microscopic <velocities>; this <damping> is of their mean directed motion. In a steady DC field,
$$
\langle v\rangle=\frac{e\tau}{m^*}E,\qquad
j=ne\langle v\rangle,\qquad
\boxed{\sigma_0=\frac{ne^2\tau}{m^*}.}
$$
Both charge factors are negative for <electrons>, so the conductivity is positive. If $e$ instead denotes a positive charge magnitude, the <force> and current equations each require a minus sign and give the same result.

For AC response choose physical fields as <real parts> of <phasors> proportional to $e^{-i\omega t}$. Substitution gives
$$
(-i\omega+1/\tau)\widehat v=\frac{e}{m^*}\widehat E,\qquad
\boxed{\sigma(\omega)=\frac{\sigma_0}{1-i\omega\tau}.}
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Re}\sigma=\frac{\sigma_0}{1+(\omega\tau)^2},\qquad
\operatorname{Im}\sigma=\frac{\sigma_0\omega\tau}{1+(\omega\tau)^2}.}
$$
The <real part> decreases from $\sigma_0$ to zero. The <imaginary part> starts at zero, reaches $\sigma_0/2$ at $\omega\tau=1$, and falls as $1/\omega$. The <real part> falls as $1/\omega^2$ at high <frequency>. With an $e^{+i\omega t}$ convention, only the sign of the <imaginary part> changes. Positive $\operatorname{Re}\sigma$ gives positive cycle-averaged dissipation $\langle jE\rangle=\operatorname{Re}\sigma\,|\widehat E|^2/2$.

\Image[paper-2-drude-chain.png]
{title=Drude conductivity and the dispersion and density of states of a monatomic harmonic chain}

The supplied DC conductivity determines the transport time:
$$
\tau=\frac{m_e\sigma_0}{ne^2}=2.49\times10^{-14}\,\mathrm s.
$$
Inertia matters when $\omega\tau\sim1$, giving **$\omega_c\simeq4.02\times10^{13}\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$**, or **$\nu_c=\omega_c/(2\pi)\simeq6.40\times10^{12}\,\mathrm{Hz}$**. Quoting the former number as hertz would introduce an erroneous factor of $2\pi$.

Write the real <displacement> as $U_n(t)=\operatorname{Re}[u e^{i\omega t-iqna}]$, choosing real positive peak amplitude $u$. Nearest-neighbour harmonic springs store <energy> in their extension $U_n-U_{n+1}$, each spring counted once. Therefore the instantaneous physical <energy> is
$$
\boxed{E=\frac12M\sum_n\dot U_n^2+\frac12\alpha\sum_n(U_n-U_{n+1})^2.}
$$
The printed complex <displacement> is a calculation device: squaring the complex <phasor> itself would not produce physical <energy>. The chain sketch in the accompanying figure shows <masses> at <equilibrium> spacing $a$, with longitudinal <displacements> along the chain.

The two spring <forces> on site $n$ give
$$
M\ddot U_n=\alpha(U_{n+1}+U_{n-1}-2U_n).
$$
Substituting the normal-mode <phasor> and cancelling its nonzero amplitude gives
$$
-M\omega^2=\alpha(e^{iqa}+e^{-iqa}-2),\qquad
\boxed{\omega^2=\frac{4\alpha}{M}\sin^2\left(\frac{qa}{2}\right).}
$$
The long-wavelength limit is acoustic, $\omega\simeq a\sqrt{\alpha/M}|q|$, and the first <Brillouin zone> ends at $|q|=\pi/a$. The maximum <angular frequency> is **$\omega_m=2\sqrt{\alpha/M}$**.

Time averaging uses $\langle[\operatorname{Re}(ze^{i\omega t})]^2\rangle=|z|^2/2$. Consequently
$$
\frac{\langle T\rangle}{N}=\frac14M\omega^2u^2,\qquad
\frac{\langle V\rangle}{N}=\frac14\alpha u^2|1-e^{-iqa}|^2
=\frac12\alpha(1-\cos qa)u^2.
$$
The <dispersion relation> makes these two contributions equal, giving
$$
\boxed{\langle E\rangle/N=\frac14M\omega^2u^2+
\frac12\alpha(1-\cos qa)u^2=\frac12M\omega^2u^2.}
$$
This is equality of time-averaged kinetic and <potential energies> in a harmonic <normal mode>; the instantaneous <energy> partition need not be equal.

Use <periodic boundary conditions> on length $L=Na$ to count modes: $q_j=2\pi j/L$. The density on the signed $q$ axis is $L/(2\pi)$. Using <folded wave-number counting for a monatomic chain> combines $q$ and $-q$ at positive magnitude and counts both directions, so the convention used by the requested expression is
$$
\boxed{g(q)=\frac{2L}{2\pi}=\frac{Na}{\pi},\quad 0<q<\pi/a.}
$$
This is a large-$N$ continuum density, not a second independent branch. It gives $N$ modes after integrating over that half-zone; endpoint counting changes only finite-size corrections.

For positive $q$, $\omega=\omega_m\sin(qa/2)$ and
$$
\frac{d\omega}{dq}=\frac a2\sqrt{\omega_m^2-\omega^2},\qquad
\boxed{g(\omega)=g(q)\left|\frac{dq}{d\omega}\right|
=\frac{2N}{\pi\sqrt{\omega_m^2-\omega^2}},\quad0<\omega<\omega_m.}
$$
The <density of states of a monatomic harmonic chain> vanishes outside the band, is constant to leading order near zero, and has an integrable <Van Hove singularity> at the upper band edge where <group velocity> vanishes. Normalization checks the directional counting:
$$
\int_0^{\omega_m}g(\omega)\,d\omega
=\frac{2N}{\pi}[\arcsin(\omega/\omega_m)]_0^{\omega_m}=N.
$$

= Solution
{parent=D12}

A completely filled <valence band> carries no net current: its occupied crystal-momentum states have <velocities> that cancel. Near a valence-band maximum, an almost full band is conveniently described by the few missing <electrons>, or <electron holes>. Removing an <electron> of charge $-e_0$, with $e_0>0$, changes the charge relative to the filled band by $+e_0$. This is a <quasiparticle> description of electronic motion, not a mobile positive ion or a <Positron>.

For an isotropic parabolic maximum, $\epsilon_v(\mathbf k)=E_v-\hbar^2k^2/(2m_h^*)$. Assign the missing-electron state momentum $\mathbf k_h=-\mathbf k_e$ and hole <energy>
$$
\epsilon_h(\mathbf k_h)=E_v-\epsilon_v(-\mathbf k_h)
=\frac{\hbar^2k_h^2}{2m_h^*}.
$$
It has positive <effective mass> $m_h^*$. Its <velocity> equals the <velocity> of the missing <electron> state, and its positive-charge current replaces the negative of that state's electronic current. Semiclassical motion is thus $\hbar\dot{\mathbf k}_h=+e_0(\mathbf E+\mathbf v_h\times\mathbf B)$. With <momentum relaxation time> $\tau_h$, the low-field mobility is $\mu_h=e_0\tau_h/m_h^*$ and
$$
\boxed{\sigma_h=e_0p\mu_h=\frac{p e_0^2\tau_h}{m_h^*}.}
$$
If <electrons> also contribute, $\sigma=e_0(p\mu_h+n\mu_e)$: the two species' longitudinal conductivities add.

A <Hall bar> separates carrier concentration from mobility. Apply <current density> $j_x$, <magnetic field> $B_z$, and measure transverse <electric field> $E_y$. For one isotropic hole population, transverse <force> balance gives $E_y=v_xB_z$, so
$$
\boxed{R_H=\frac{E_y}{j_xB_z}=\frac1{e_0p}>0,\qquad
p=\frac1{e_0R_H},\qquad \mu_h=\sigma R_H.}
$$
For thickness $t$, the Hall-voltage magnitude is $|V_H|=IB/(e_0pt)$. A longitudinal <four-terminal resistance measurement> supplies $\sigma$ independently. Use <ohmic contacts>, known conducting thickness, controlled <temperature> and low enough current to avoid self-heating. Reversing <magnetic field> removes even-in-field longitudinal offsets from contact misalignment; reversing current also helps reject thermoelectric offsets. These are central controls, rather than merely taking one transverse voltage reading.<Resistivity and Hall Measurements by National Institute of Standards and Technology>[Sample Geometry; Resistivity Measurements; Definitions for Hall Measurements; Hall Measurements and Hall Calculations]{ref}

The simple inference assumes one carrier type and a known <Hall scattering factor>. More generally $R_H=r_H/(e_0p)$, so $\sigma R_H$ measures <Hall mobility> rather than automatically the drift mobility. With two carrier populations at weak field,
$$
R_H=\frac{p\mu_h^2-n\mu_e^2}{e_0(p\mu_h+n\mu_e)^2}.
$$
Its sign alone need not identify the more numerous carrier. A Hall experiment also cannot determine $m_h^*$ and $\tau_h$ separately: their ratio sets mobility. <cyclotron resonance> supplies an independent <mass> measurement by sweeping <frequency> or <magnetic field> through $\omega=e_0B/m_c$ and detecting resonant absorption. The measured <cyclotron effective mass> can depend on band anisotropy and need not equal every curvature <mass>; the isotropic parabolic model identifies them. Its linewidth provides a scattering-time estimate only with a specified <damping> model.

\Image[paper-2-holes.png]
{title=Positive-curvature hole energy, a Hall-bar measurement, and an acceptor level in a semiconductor}

<semiconductor doping> generates holes by adding <acceptor impurities>. In <silicon>, a substitutional group-III atom such as <boron> provides one fewer valence <electron> than <silicon>. A shallow acceptor level lies slightly above the valence-band edge: accepting a valence <electron> makes the dopant negatively ionized and leaves a mobile hole in the <valence band>. The impurity stays fixed in the lattice. <charge neutrality> and the nondegenerate <equilibrium> mass-action law give
$$
p+N_D^+=n+N_A^-,\qquad np=n_i^2.
$$
For fully ionized, uncompensated acceptors in the extrinsic regime, $p\simeq N_A$ and $n\simeq n_i^2/N_A$; with fully ionized donor compensation, $p\simeq N_A-N_D$ while holes remain dominant. The <Fermi level> moves towards the <valence band>. At low <temperature>, <semiconductor carrier freeze-out> suppresses mobile <electron holes>; at high <temperature> intrinsic electron-hole generation can dominate. Increasing dopant density also changes scattering, so a conductivity increase alone cannot distinguish increased <carrier density> from changed mobility. Temperature-dependent Hall and conductivity measurements separate these effects, subject to the carrier and scattering qualifications above.

= Solution

For a small longitudinal disturbance, let the <equilibrium> pressure and <mass density> be $p_0,\rho_0$, their perturbations be $p',\rho'$, and the fluid <velocity> be $v$. Linearizing <conservation of mass> and the inviscid momentum equation in one dimension gives
$$
\partial_t\rho'=-\rho_0\partial_xv,\qquad
\rho_0\partial_tv=-\partial_xp'.
$$
When compression is adiabatic, the <equation of state> gives $p'=c_s^2\rho'$ with $c_s^2=(\partial p/\partial\rho)_s$. Differentiating and eliminating $v$ yields
$$
\partial_t^2\rho'=c_s^2\partial_x^2\rho',\qquad
\boxed{c_s=\sqrt{\gamma p_0/\rho_0}=\sqrt{\gamma RT/M_{\rm mol}}}
$$
for an <ideal gas> with heat-capacity ratio $\gamma=C_p/C_v$ and molar <mass> $M_{\rm mol}$. Compression raises <temperature> as well as density, explaining the factor $\gamma$ compared with the isothermal value $\sqrt{p_0/\rho_0}$.

A rightgoing plane <sound wave> has pressure and particle <velocity> in phase, $p'=\rho_0c_sv$, and mean <intensity> $\langle p'v\rangle=\widehat p^{\,2}/(2\rho_0c_s)=\rho_0c_s\widehat v^{\,2}/2$ for peak amplitudes. Particle <displacement> is a quarter-cycle out of phase with particle <velocity>: particles oscillate locally while the compression pattern transports <energy>. This continuum linear description requires <wavelength> much larger than the <mean free path>, small pressure perturbations and negligible viscous attenuation. Adiabatic propagation requires heat exchange across the compressed region to be slow on the oscillation time; strong thermal coupling would instead favour an isothermal response.

= Solution

The <Q factor> measures stored <energy> relative to loss: $Q=2\pi E/(\text{energy lost per cycle})$, or $Q=\omega_0 E/P_{\rm loss}$ in the weak-loss limit. For viscous <damping> $m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=0$, write $\gamma=b/(2m)$, $\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}$ and $\omega_d=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}$. The conventional oscillator parameter is
$$
\boxed{Q=\frac{m\omega_0}{b}=\frac{\omega_0}{2\gamma}.}
$$
In free ringdown, fit the amplitude envelope $A(t)=A_0e^{-\gamma t}$. Its decay time is $\tau_A=1/\gamma$, whereas the cycle-averaged <energy> envelope decays on $\tau_E=1/(2\gamma)$; thus $Q=\omega_0\tau_A/2=\omega_0\tau_E$. Equivalently the <logarithmic decrement> between same-sign peaks is $\delta=\ln(A_n/A_{n+1})=2\pi\gamma/\omega_d$. It gives **$Q\simeq\pi/\delta$ for weak <damping>**, or the exact parameter relation $Q=\sqrt{1/4+(\pi/\delta)^2}$. Fit multiple peaks above the noise floor, measure the damped period, and verify an exponential envelope rather than assuming all amplitude loss is viscous.

For a driven measurement, allow transients to settle and scan <frequency> at fixed <force> amplitude. Near <resonance> and at high $Q$, the response is Lorentzian in <displacement> <power>, with half-power points at <displacement> amplitudes $1/\sqrt2$ of the maximum. Their angular-frequency separation gives **$Q\simeq\omega_0/\Delta\omega$**. Halving the amplitude is not a half-power criterion.

The <absorbed-power bandwidth of a viscously damped oscillator> can be found exactly. Since
$$
\overline P(\Omega)=\frac{bF_0^2\Omega^2}{2[(k-m\Omega^2)^2+b^2\Omega^2]},
$$
its maximum is at $\Omega=\omega_0$, and the half-power <frequencies> satisfy $|k-m\Omega^2|=b\Omega$. Hence
$$
\Omega_\pm=\frac{\sqrt{b^2+4mk}\pm b}{2m},\qquad
\boxed{\Omega_+-\Omega_-=b/m,\quad Q=\omega_0/(\Omega_+-\Omega_-).}
$$
For broad <resonances>, <displacement> and absorbed-power peaks differ, so the measured observable must be specified. Frequency-dependent drive, backgrounds or several overlapping modes can otherwise masquerade as a changed <Q factor>.

= Solution

For propagation along $z$, a <transverse wave> has two perpendicular <displacement> components. With $\phi=kz-\omega t$, write $u_x=A\cos\phi$, $u_y=B\cos(\phi+\delta)$. Their trajectory at a fixed point obeys
$$
\boxed{\left(\frac{u_x}{A}\right)^2+\left(\frac{u_y}{B}\right)^2
-2\frac{u_xu_y}{AB}\cos\delta=\sin^2\delta.}
$$
The general nondegenerate trajectory is an ellipse: <elliptical polarization>. For $\delta=0$ or $\pi$, the components remain in phase or antiphase and the trajectory collapses to a line. A single nonzero component is also linearly polarized. Equal amplitudes $A=B$ with $\delta=\pm\pi/2$ give <circular polarization>. Unequal quadrature amplitudes give an ellipse aligned with the chosen axes, while general <phase differences> rotate its axes.

\Image[paper-2-polarization.png]
{title=Linear, circular and elliptical transverse-wave trajectories at a fixed spatial point}

For the displayed convention and $\delta=+\pi/2$, at $z=0$ the vector starts at $+x$ and moves towards $+y$ as time increases. Reversing $\delta$ reverses its rotation. Right- and left-handed labels require a stated viewing and phase convention, so rotation is defined explicitly here. A fixed ellipse is a coherent relationship between transverse components; unpolarized light instead has fluctuating orientation and phase relationships on the measurement timescale.

A linear polarizer transmits a linearly polarized beam with <intensity> proportional to $\cos^2\theta$, allowing its direction to be measured by an analyser. <circular polarization> transmits equal <intensity> through any linear analyser, but that test alone does not distinguish it from unpolarized light. A <quarter-wave plate> converts a suitable coherent circular state to <linear polarization>, allowing the combination to discriminate them. For light the <electric field> defines the usual polarization; the <magnetic field> remains perpendicular to it and to propagation. Longitudinal sound in a homogeneous gas lacks the two transverse degrees of freedom needed for these polarization states.
