= Solution

Let the infinite outer walls be at $x=0,D$, the finite steps at $x=a,b$, and the three <potential energy> values be $V_L,V_B,V_R$. The original drawing specifies the ordering
$$
V_L<E_0<E_1<V_R<E_2<V_B.
$$
The <energy eigenstates> can be chosen real. The <Dirichlet boundary conditions> are $\psi(0)=\psi(D)=0$; <wavefunction matching at a finite potential step> requires both $\psi$ and $\psi'$ to be continuous at $a,b$. In particular, neither finite step acts as an additional hard wall.

The <time-independent Schrödinger equation> gives
$$
\psi''=\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(V-E)\psi.
$$
Thus a <wavefunction> is sinusoidal in a <classically allowed region>, with <wavenumber> $k=\sqrt{2m(E-V)}/\hbar$, and a <linear combination> of <exponential functions> in a <classically forbidden region>, with inverse decay length $q=\sqrt{2m(V-E)}/\hbar$. Its <curvature> changes sign relative to $\psi$ at a step crossing $E$; its <slope> does not jump.

By the <Sturm oscillation theorem>, the three <energy eigenstates> have exactly **zero, one, and two interior nodes**, respectively. Choose their <global phases> so that their slopes at the left wall are positive:

* $E_0$: a positive lobe in the left-hand <potential well>, followed by a nonoscillatory tail through the barrier and right-hand region. The tail reaches zero at the right wall. There are no interior <nodes>.
* $E_1$: two lobes of opposite sign in the left-hand <potential well>, with one interior <node>, followed by a nonoscillatory tail of the sign of the second lobe. Both the middle and right regions are <classically forbidden regions>.
* $E_2$: sinusoidal behavior in both outer regions, joined through the nonoscillatory barrier. There are two interior <nodes> in total. The right-hand <wavelength> is longer than the left-hand <wavelength>, because $E_2-V_R<E_2-V_L$.

For $E_0,E_1$, the right-hand piece is proportional to $\sinh[q_R(D-x)]$, rather than a pure decaying exponential: this enforces the finite-distance hard wall exactly. The barrier pieces likewise contain both exponential solutions. Their small tails are an instance of <quantum tunnelling>.

The <protons> at the <Large Hadron Collider> are ultrarelativistic: their <energy> is much greater than their <rest energy>, about $0.938\,\mathrm{GeV}$. The <relativistic energy-momentum relation> therefore gives $pc\simeq E$. Using the <de Broglie wavelength>,
$$
\lambda=\frac hp\simeq\frac{hc}{E}
=\frac{1.240\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{eV\,m}}{6.5\times10^{12}\,\mathrm{eV}}
=1.91\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm m.
$$
Thus the conventional <de Broglie wavelength> estimate is **a distance of order $10^{-19}\,\mathrm m$**.

There is a conventional factor of $2\pi$ in interpreting this estimate. <Spatial resolution from momentum transfer> is often expressed as $\ell\sim\hbar c/Q$, where $Q$ is the transferred <momentum> multiplied by the <speed of light>. Taking $Q$ of order the beam <energy> gives
$$
\ell\sim\frac{197.3\,\mathrm{MeV\,fm}}{6.5\times10^6\,\mathrm{MeV}}
=3.0\times10^{-20}\,\mathrm m.
$$
Both conventions describe the same characteristic scale up to $2\pi$. The beam <energy> alone does not give an exact experimental <spatial resolution>: a particular collision probes a scale set by its actual <momentum transfer>.

The <Born rule> uses the squared <modulus> of the <wavefunction>, not its real part. Write $\psi=A[\sin x+i(1-\cos x)]$. Then
$$
|\psi|^2=|A|^2\bigl[\sin^2x+(1-\cos x)^2\bigr]
=2|A|^2(1-\cos x).
$$
<Wavefunction normalization> requires
$$
1=2|A|^2\int_0^{2\pi}(1-\cos x)\,dx=4\pi|A|^2,
\qquad |A|^2=\frac1{4\pi}.
$$
Integrating the <probability density> over the required interval gives the dimensionless <probability>
$$
\boxed{P=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi/2}(1-\cos x)\,dx
=\frac{\pi/2-1}{2\pi}
=\frac14-\frac1{2\pi}\simeq0.09085.}
$$
Thus approximately **$9.08\%$** of <position measurements> fall in this interval.

Let $M$ denote an incident <muon> and $S$ a detector signal. The selected sample is conditioned on $S$. The <law of total probability> gives
$$
P(S)=P(S\mid M)P(M)+P(S\mid M^c)P(M^c)
=(0.90)(0.95)+(0.20)(0.05)=0.865.
$$
By <Bayes' theorem>, the contamination, namely the <conditional probability> that a selected particle is not a <muon>, is
$$
\boxed{P(M^c\mid S)=\frac{(0.20)(0.05)}{0.865}
=0.01156\simeq1.16\%.}
$$
For example, per $10\,000$ incident particles the expected selected counts are $8550$ true <muons> and $100$ other particles. The denominator is the $8650$ selected particles; the rejected <muons> are outside this sample.

For the $110\,\mathrm{Hz}$ fundamental alone, the <Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem> places the limiting <sampling rate> at twice that <frequency>:
$$
\boxed{f_s\gtrsim2(110\,\mathrm{Hz})=220\,\mathrm{samples\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
Use a rate strictly above this boundary for a sinusoid of unknown <phase>: exactly two samples per cycle can, for a particular <phase>, all fall at zero. If higher <Fourier harmonics> are retained, the <sampling rate> must instead exceed twice the highest retained <frequency>, or those components must be removed to prevent <aliasing>.

For a straightforward <discrete Fourier transform> estimate, <Fourier frequency resolution> obeys $\Delta f\sim1/T_{\mathrm{obs}}$. Resolving $0.07\,\mathrm{Hz}$ therefore suggests
$$
\boxed{T_{\mathrm{obs}}\gtrsim\frac1{0.07\,\mathrm{Hz}}=14.3\,\mathrm s.}
$$
At the limiting <sampling rate> this is about $f_sT_{\mathrm{obs}}\simeq3143$ samples. These are the usual sampling and spectral-resolution estimates. The $14.3\,\mathrm s$ value is not a universal lower bound on single-tone <frequency estimation>: fitting a sinusoidal model with sufficiently low noise can estimate its <frequency> more precisely than the spacing of <discrete Fourier transform> bins. The question gives no noise level or estimator, so a sharper universal minimum time cannot be deduced.

= Solution

Use the <canonical commutation relations> $[\hat x,\hat p_x]=[\hat y,\hat p_y]=i\hbar$, with cross-coordinate <commutators> zero. For either coordinate,
$$
[\hat a,\hat a^\dagger]=2\alpha\beta\hbar,
\qquad
\hat a^\dagger\hat a=\alpha^2\hat x^2+\beta^2\hat p_x^2-\alpha\beta\hbar.
$$
Thus $2\alpha\beta\hbar=1$. To obtain the required <Hamiltonian>, the ratio of its <position operator> and <momentum operator> coefficients must be preserved:
$$
\frac{\alpha^2}{\beta^2}=m^2\omega^2,
\qquad \alpha=m\omega\beta,
$$
where the positive root follows from the requested signs. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\alpha_x=\alpha_y=\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}},
\qquad \beta_x=\beta_y=\frac1{\sqrt{2m\hbar\omega}}.}
$$
The <annihilation operators> are dimensionless; $\alpha$ has units of inverse length and $\beta$ has units of inverse <momentum>. Operators belonging to different coordinates commute automatically. Writing the <number operators> as $\hat N_j=\hat a_j^\dagger\hat a_j$, substitution gives
$$
\boxed{\hat H=\hbar\omega(\hat N_x+\hat N_y+1).}
$$
The constant is the sum of the two <zero-point energies> of this <two-dimensional isotropic harmonic oscillator>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

For either <number operator> $\hat N=\hat a^\dagger\hat a$, positivity follows from
$$
\langle u|\hat N|u\rangle=\|\hat a|u\rangle\|^2\ge0.
$$
Hence an <eigenvalue> $\nu$ cannot be negative. The <commutators>
$$
[\hat N,\hat a]=-\hat a,\qquad [\hat N,\hat a^\dagger]=\hat a^\dagger
$$
show that $\hat a|\nu\rangle$, if nonzero, is an <eigenstate> with <eigenvalue> $\nu-1$. For a normalized <eigenstate>, $\|\hat a|\nu\rangle\|^2=\nu$.

If $\nu$ were not an integer, successive nonzero lowerings would eventually reach an <eigenstate> with $0<\nu'<1$. Its next lowering would have nonzero <norm> but a negative <eigenvalue>, contradicting positivity. The ladder must therefore terminate at $\nu=0$, with $\hat a|0\rangle=0$. Conversely, successive applications of the <creation operator> never vanish, because
$$
\|\hat a^\dagger|n\rangle\|^2=\langle n|(\hat N+1)|n\rangle=n+1>0.
$$
The normalizable bottom state exists explicitly: solving $\hat a\psi_0=0$ gives $\psi_0(x)\propto\exp[-m\omega x^2/(2\hbar)]$. Thus the <number operator> has exactly the non-negative integer <eigenvalues>.

Since the two coordinate <number operators> commute, use the product <eigenstates> $|n_x,n_y\rangle$. Their <energy eigenvalues> are
$$
\boxed{E_{n_x,n_y}=\hbar\omega(n_x+n_y+1),\qquad n_x,n_y=0,1,2,\ldots.}
$$
At fixed $N=n_x+n_y$, the allowed pairs are $(0,N),(1,N-1),\ldots,(N,0)$. Thus the <energy level> $E_N=(N+1)\hbar\omega$ has <quantum degeneracy> $N+1$, and the <ground-state energy> is $\hbar\omega$.

= Solution
{parent=c}

The <number-operator> <commutators> established above imply that $\hat a_x^\dagger|n\rangle$ is proportional to $|n+1\rangle$ and $\hat a_x|n\rangle$ to $|n-1\rangle$. Their <norms> determine the magnitudes of the proportionality constants:
$$
\|\hat a_x^\dagger|n\rangle\|^2
=\langle n|\hat a_x\hat a_x^\dagger|n\rangle=n+1,
\qquad
\|\hat a_x|n\rangle\|^2
=\langle n|\hat a_x^\dagger\hat a_x|n\rangle=n.
$$
Choose the <phase> of the normalized ground <eigenstate> once, then define recursively
$$
|n+1\rangle=\frac{\hat a_x^\dagger|n\rangle}{\sqrt{n+1}},
\qquad
|n\rangle=\frac{(\hat a_x^\dagger)^n}{\sqrt{n!}}|0\rangle.
$$
This removes the otherwise arbitrary relative <phases> of the <eigenstates>. The <Hermitian adjoint> relation then fixes the lowering coefficient with the same positive sign, giving
$$
\boxed{\hat a_x^\dagger|n\rangle=\sqrt{n+1}|n+1\rangle,
\qquad \hat a_x|n\rangle=\sqrt n|n-1\rangle.}
$$
At $n=0$, the second expression means the <zero vector>, $\hat a_x|0\rangle=0$; it does not introduce a physical state $|-1\rangle$.

Set $\hat B=\hat a_x^\dagger\hat a_y-\hat a_y^\dagger\hat a_x$. Taking the <Hermitian adjoint> reverses the order of products, so $\hat B^\dagger=-\hat B$. <Complex conjugation> also reverses the sign of $-i$, and therefore
$$
\hat Q^\dagger=i\hbar\hat B^\dagger=-i\hbar\hat B=\hat Q.
$$
Thus $\hat Q$ is a <self-adjoint operator> on the usual <oscillator> states.

Using the coefficients in part (a), products containing two <position operators> or two <momentum operators> cancel in the difference. Cross-coordinate operators commute, leaving
$$
\hat B=2i\alpha\beta(\hat x\hat p_y-\hat y\hat p_x)
=\frac i\hbar(\hat x\hat p_y-\hat y\hat p_x).
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\hat Q=\hat x\hat p_y-\hat y\hat p_x=\hat L_z.}
$$
It is the component of <orbital angular momentum> perpendicular to the plane, with units $\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}}$ (equivalently $\mathrm{J\,s}$). The <rotational symmetry> of the <two-dimensional isotropic harmonic oscillator> makes this a conserved <observable>; directly, each term in $\hat B$ preserves $N_x+N_y$, so $[\hat H,\hat Q]=0$.

The specified <energy> requires $n_x+n_y=2$, so the relevant <energy eigenspace> has the <orthonormal basis>
$$
|2,0\rangle,\quad |1,1\rangle,\quad |0,2\rangle.
$$
Applying the <creation and annihilation operators> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat Q|2,0\rangle&=i\sqrt2\hbar|1,1\rangle,\\
\hat Q|1,1\rangle&=-i\sqrt2\hbar|2,0\rangle+i\sqrt2\hbar|0,2\rangle,\\
\hat Q|0,2\rangle&=-i\sqrt2\hbar|1,1\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus its <matrix> in this ordered <basis> is
$$
Q=\hbar\begin{pmatrix}
0&-i\sqrt2&0\\
i\sqrt2&0&-i\sqrt2\\
0&i\sqrt2&0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Writing $q=\hbar\lambda$, the <characteristic polynomial> gives $\lambda(\lambda^2-4)=0$. The possible <angular momentum> <eigenvalues> are therefore
$$
\boxed{q=-2\hbar,\quad0,\quad+2\hbar.}
$$
For completeness, normalized simultaneous <energy> and <angular momentum> <eigenstates> are
$$
\begin{aligned}
|q=+2\hbar\rangle&=\tfrac12\bigl(|2,0\rangle+i\sqrt2|1,1\rangle-|0,2\rangle\bigr),\\
|q=0\rangle&=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\bigl(|2,0\rangle+|0,2\rangle\bigr),\\
|q=-2\hbar\rangle&=\tfrac12\bigl(|2,0\rangle-i\sqrt2|1,1\rangle-|0,2\rangle\bigr).
\end{aligned}
$$
Each value occurs once in this <energy eigenspace>. Equivalently, <circular ladder operators of a two-dimensional oscillator> give $\hat H=\hbar\omega(\hat N_++\hat N_-+1)$ and $\hat L_z=\hbar(\hat N_+-\hat N_-)$. With $N_++N_-=2$, the difference can only be $2,0,-2$.

For a <particle in a ring>, the arc-length coordinate is $s=R\phi$. The tangential <kinetic energy> is
$$
H=\frac{p_s^2}{2m}=\frac{L_z^2}{2mR^2}.
$$
The constraint removes radial motion; the constant <potential energy> along the ring can be chosen as zero. The tangential <momentum operator> is $\hat p_s=-i\hbar\partial_s$, or equivalently the <angular momentum operator> is $\hat L_z=-i\hbar\partial_\phi$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\hat H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2mR^2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2}.}
$$
Its physical domain has periodic <boundary conditions>, $\psi(\phi+2\pi)=\psi(\phi)$ and $\psi'(\phi+2\pi)=\psi'(\phi)$. These make the <Hamiltonian> <self-adjoint> and reflect that $\phi$ and $\phi+2\pi$ describe the same point. No magnetic flux or additional angle-dependent <potential energy> is present.

= Solution

Solving the <time-independent Schrödinger equation> with $\psi\propto e^{ik\phi}$ gives $E=\hbar^2k^2/(2mR^2)$. The periodic <boundary condition> requires $e^{2\pi ik}=1$, hence $k=n\in\mathbb Z$. With the angular integration measure $d\phi$, <wavefunction normalization> gives
$$
\int_0^{2\pi}|A|^2\,d\phi=1,
$$
so a convenient <orthonormal eigenbasis> is
$$
\boxed{u_n(\phi)=\frac{e^{in\phi}}{\sqrt{2\pi}},
\qquad E_n=\frac{\hbar^2n^2}{2mR^2},\qquad n\in\mathbb Z.}
$$
The $n=0$ <ground state> is nondegenerate. For each $|n|>0$, $u_n$ and $u_{-n}$ span a two-dimensional <energy eigenspace>; real <sine> and <cosine> combinations are an equally valid <basis>. These opposite-circulation <eigenstates> have <angular momentum> $n\hbar$ and $-n\hbar$.

If instead the <wavefunction> is normalized with respect to physical arc length $ds=R\,d\phi$, its expression is $e^{in\phi}/\sqrt{2\pi R}$. The two conventions describe the same <probability>, since $|u_n|^2d\phi=|u_n/\sqrt R|^2ds$. The remaining parts use $d\phi$.

= Solution

Put $\epsilon=\hbar^2/(2mR^2)$. The two-particle product <energy eigenstates> are
$$
u_{n_1}(\phi_1)u_{n_2}(\phi_2),\qquad E=\epsilon(n_1^2+n_2^2).
$$
Because the particles are distinguishable, $(n_1,n_2)$ and $(n_2,n_1)$ are distinct when $n_1\ne n_2$. Also, the signs of nonzero integers distinguish opposite <angular momenta>. The <quantum degeneracy> is therefore the number of ordered integer pairs at each sum of squares.

The first distinct <energy levels> are $0,\epsilon,2\epsilon,4\epsilon,5\epsilon,\ldots$. This ordering matters: $4\epsilon$ is the third excited level, while $5\epsilon$ is the fourth excited level.

* At $E=0$, only $(0,0)$ occurs: **$g_0=1$**.
* At the first excited level, $E=\epsilon$, the pairs are $(\pm1,0)$ and $(0,\pm1)$: **$g_1=4$**.
* At the fourth excited level, $E=5\epsilon$, the pairs are $(\pm1,\pm2)$ and $(\pm2,\pm1)$, with independent signs: **$g_4=8$**.
* At $E=25\epsilon$, the pairs are $(\pm5,0),(0,\pm5)$, contributing four, and $(\pm3,\pm4),(\pm4,\pm3)$, contributing eight: **$g_{25}=12$**.

These counts concern <energy eigenspaces>; arbitrary <quantum superpositions> within each one do not create additional independent states.

= Solution

Identical <spin-one-half> particles are <fermions>. By <fermionic exchange symmetry>, the complete spatial-and-spin <wavefunction> must change sign when the two particle labels are exchanged. Define the normalized antisymmetric <spin-one-half singlet state>
$$
\chi_S=\frac{|\uparrow\rangle_1|\downarrow\rangle_2-|\downarrow\rangle_1|\uparrow\rangle_2}{\sqrt2},
$$
and the three symmetric <spin-one-half triplet states>
$$
\chi_+=|\uparrow\rangle_1|\uparrow\rangle_2,\qquad
\chi_0=\frac{|\uparrow\rangle_1|\downarrow\rangle_2+|\downarrow\rangle_1|\uparrow\rangle_2}{\sqrt2},\qquad
\chi_-=|\downarrow\rangle_1|\downarrow\rangle_2.
$$
The <Hamiltonian> is independent of <spin>, so these spin functions carry no additional <energy>.

Both particles can occupy $u_0$ if their <spin> factor is antisymmetric. The unique <ground-state> <wavefunction> is
$$
\boxed{\Psi_g(\phi_1,\phi_2)=u_0(\phi_1)u_0(\phi_2)\chi_S
=\frac1{2\pi}\chi_S,\qquad E_g=0,\qquad g_g=1.}
$$
Two equal-spin particles could not share this orbital, by the <Pauli exclusion principle>.

At the first excited <energy>, $E=\epsilon=\hbar^2/(2mR^2)$, one orbital is $u_0$ and the other is $u_s$ with $s=+1$ or $-1$. Define symmetric and antisymmetric normalized spatial functions
$$
\Phi_s^{\pm}(\phi_1,\phi_2)
=\frac{u_0(\phi_1)u_s(\phi_2)\pm u_s(\phi_1)u_0(\phi_2)}{\sqrt2}
=\frac{e^{is\phi_2}\pm e^{is\phi_1}}{2\pi\sqrt2}.
$$
For each $s$, all allowed <wavefunctions> are spanned by
$$
\boxed{\Phi_s^+\chi_S,\qquad
\Phi_s^-\chi_+,\quad\Phi_s^-\chi_0,\quad\Phi_s^-\chi_-,\qquad s=\pm1.}
$$
There are $2(1+3)$ <orthonormal> states, hence **first-excited-state degeneracy $g=8$**. In each product, exactly one factor is antisymmetric, ensuring the required total <fermionic exchange symmetry>.

The original apparatus has two small masses at distance $L/2$ from the suspension axis. =\frac{2\pi^2(0.038\,\mathrm{kg})(0.100\,\mathrm m)^2}{(498.2\,\mathrm s)^2}

The <torsion constant> is a restoring <torque> per unit <angle>. In dimensional calculations the radian is dimensionless.

Using first-order <propagation of uncertainty>, with the quoted period uncertainty interpreted as a <standard uncertainty>,
$$
\sigma_\kappa=\left|\frac{d\kappa}{dT}\right|\sigma_T
=\frac{2\kappa}{T}\sigma_T,
\qquad \frac{\sigma_\kappa}{\kappa}=\frac{2(6.0)}{498.2}=0.02409.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\kappa=(3.022\pm0.073)\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{N\,m\,rad^{-1}}.}

= Solution

All four <angle> measurements have the same <standard uncertainty>, so <ordinary least squares> minimizes
$$
S(c)=\sum_{i=1}^4(y_i-cx_i)^2.
$$
<Differentiation> gives
$$
\frac{dS}{dc}=-2\sum_i x_i(y_i-cx_i)=0,
\qquad \frac{d^2S}{dc^2}=2\sum_i x_i^2>0.
$$
Thus the unique minimum is
$$
\boxed{\hat c=\frac{\sum_i x_iy_i}{\sum_i x_i^2}
=\frac{\overline{xy}}{\overline{xx}}.}
$$
The overbars denote <sample means> over the four observations. Numerically,
$$
\sum_i x_iy_i=542.888740\,\mathrm{m^{-2}},\qquad
\sum_i x_i^2=3.00072844\times10^7\,\mathrm{m^{-4}},
$$
so
$$
\boxed{\hat c=1.80919\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2},\qquad
\hat G=\frac{\kappa\hat c}{mML}=9.59\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}.}
$$
The fixed $x_i$ have negligible uncertainty. Assuming <independent> errors in the four $y_i$, <propagation of uncertainty> in quadrature gives
$$
\sigma_{\hat c}^2=\sum_i\left(\frac{\partial\hat c}{\partial y_i}\right)^2\sigma_y^2
=\frac{\sigma_y^2\sum_i x_i^2}{(\sum_i x_i^2)^2}
=\frac{\sigma_y^2}{\sum_i x_i^2}.
$$
Hence
$$
\boxed{\sigma_{\hat c}=3.65\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m^2},\qquad
\hat c=(1.809\pm0.037)\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2}.}
$$
If the period measurement is <independent> of the <angle> measurements, including its calibration uncertainty gives
$$
\frac{\sigma_G}{\hat G}=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_{\hat c}}{\hat c}\right)^2+
\left(\frac{\sigma_\kappa}{\kappa}\right)^2}=0.0314,
\qquad \hat G=(9.59\pm0.30)\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}.
$$
This last uncertainty includes the stated random measurement errors only. Whether the model describes the data must be checked before treating it as a reliable measurement of the <gravitational constant>.

Assume independent <normally distributed> <angle> errors with known <standard deviation> $\sigma_y=0.002$. The relevant <chi-squared test> uses the sum of squared standardized residuals,
$$
\chi^2=\sum_i\frac{(y_i-y_{\mathrm{fit},i})^2}{\sigma_y^2}.
$$
For the model through the origin, the predictions and residuals, expressed in milliradians, are
$$
\begin{array}{c|rrrr}
r\ (\mathrm{mm})&16&18&22&32\\\hline
\hat c x\ (\mathrm{mrad})&70.6715&55.8392&37.3800&17.6679\\
y-\hat c x\ (\mathrm{mrad})&-4.6715&-0.8392&6.6200&7.3321
\end{array}
$$
Consequently $\chi_0^2=30.0280$. There are $4-1=3$ <statistical degrees of freedom>. The upper-tail <P-value> is approximately $1.36\times10^{-6}$; equivalently the value greatly exceeds the $5\%$ critical value $7.815$. The zero-intercept model is strongly rejected under the stated errors.

For the alternative <linear regression>, minimize $\sum_i(y_i-c'x_i-d)^2$. Setting both partial <derivatives> to zero gives the <normal equations>, whose solution is
$$
\hat c'=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{S_{xx}},
\qquad \hat d=\bar y-\hat c'\bar x,
\qquad S_{xx}=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2.
$$
For these data $\bar x=2508.83699\,\mathrm{m^{-2}}$, $\bar y=0.0475$, and $S_{xx}=4.830232\times10^6\,\mathrm{m^{-4}}$. This yields
$$
\boxed{\hat c'=1.37074\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2},\qquad
\hat d=0.0131105=13.1105\,\mathrm{mrad}.}
$$
The new predictions are $(66.6548,55.4171,41.4315,26.4966)\,\mathrm{mrad}$, with residuals $(-0.6548,-0.4171,2.5685,-1.4966)\,\mathrm{mrad}$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\chi_1^2=2.35998,\qquad \nu=4-2=2,\qquad p=0.307.}
$$
The affine model is compatible with the data. Allowing for its additional fitted parameter, the decrease $\Delta\chi^2=27.668$ has a <chi-squared distribution> with one <statistical degree of freedom> under the nested zero-intercept hypothesis, corresponding to $p\simeq1.44\times10^{-7}$. Thus **the model with a fitted intercept provides the better description**, rather than merely lowering the residual by adding a parameter.

With the measurement variance treated as known, the coefficient uncertainties are
$$
\sigma_{c'}=\frac{\sigma_y}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}=9.10\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m^2},
\qquad
\sigma_d=\sigma_y\sqrt{\frac14+\frac{\bar x^2}{S_{xx}}}=0.0024925.
$$
Hence **$d=(13.11\pm2.49)\,\mathrm{mrad}$**, about $5.26$ <standard deviations> above zero. A positive constant <systematic error> in the angular zero, or an additional nearly constant <torque>, could account for it. Its presence is evidence against the ideal zero-intercept model; four points alone do not identify the physical cause uniquely. A calibration error in $\kappa$ only rescales the inferred <gravitational constant> and cannot remove this residual pattern.

Using the fitted slope after allowing for this offset would give $G'=\kappa\hat c'/(mML)=(7.27\pm0.51)\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}$, with the period and slope errors combined in quadrature. This is conditional on the constant-offset model.

= Solution
{parent=D9}

Time dependence in <quantum mechanics> describes how a prepared <quantum state> changes and thereby changes the <probabilities> of later measurements. For an isolated system, the <Schrödinger picture> assigns this evolution to a state vector $|\psi(t)\rangle$, governed by the <time-dependent Schrödinger equation>
$$
i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}|\psi(t)\rangle=\hat H(t)|\psi(t)\rangle.
$$
The <Hamiltonian> is a <self-adjoint operator>; it has units of <energy>, while $\hbar$ has units of <action>, so $\hat H/\hbar$ has units of inverse time. For a particle with <potential energy> $V(\mathbf r,t)$, this becomes
$$
i\hbar\partial_t\psi(\mathbf r,t)
=\left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2+V(\mathbf r,t)\right]\psi(\mathbf r,t).
$$
Given a suitable initial <wavefunction> and the physical <boundary conditions>, this first-order equation determines its subsequent propagation. Its <linearity> means that the evolution of a <quantum superposition> is the corresponding <quantum superposition> of evolved states.

For a real <potential energy>, this equation and its <complex conjugate> imply the local <continuity equation>
$$
\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\mathbf j=0,
\qquad \rho=|\psi|^2,
\qquad \mathbf j=\frac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\nabla\psi).
$$
Here $\rho$ is the <probability density> and $\mathbf j$ the <probability current>. Vanishing net boundary flux preserves <wavefunction normalization>, as required by the <Born rule>.

The <time-evolution operator> packages the solution for every initial state:
$$
|\psi(t)\rangle=U(t,t_0)|\psi(t_0)\rangle,
\qquad i\hbar\partial_tU(t,t_0)=\hat H(t)U(t,t_0),
\qquad U(t_0,t_0)=I.
$$
Because $\hat H^\dagger=\hat H$, differentiation gives
$$
\frac d{dt}(U^\dagger U)
=\frac i\hbar U^\dagger\hat H U-\frac i\hbar U^\dagger\hat H U=0.
$$
Therefore $U^\dagger U=I$: this is <unitary time evolution>, preserving <inner products> and all state <norms>. It is reversible, with $U(t,t_0)^{-1}=U(t_0,t)=U(t,t_0)^\dagger$, and obeys the composition law $U(t_2,t_0)=U(t_2,t_1)U(t_1,t_0)$.

For a time-independent <Hamiltonian>, the solution is the <operator exponential>
$$
\boxed{U(t,t_0)=\exp[-i\hat H(t-t_0)/\hbar].}
$$
If the <Hamiltonian> varies with time, the analogous simple exponential of its integral is valid when $[\hat H(t),\hat H(t')]=0$ for all relevant times. Otherwise <time ordering> is essential:
$$
U(t,t_0)=\mathcal T\exp\left[-\frac i\hbar\int_{t_0}^t\hat H(t')\,dt'\right].
$$
The meaning is explicit in the first terms of the ordered series,
$$
U=I-\frac i\hbar\int_{t_0}^t\hat H(t_1)\,dt_1
-\frac1{\hbar^2}\int_{t_0}^t dt_1\int_{t_0}^{t_1}dt_2\,\hat H(t_1)\hat H(t_2)+\cdots:
$$
the later-time operator stands on the left. In the position representation, the same propagation is expressed by the <quantum-mechanical propagator>
$$
K(\mathbf r,t;\mathbf r',t_0)=\langle\mathbf r|U(t,t_0)|\mathbf r'\rangle,
\qquad
\psi(\mathbf r,t)=\int K(\mathbf r,t;\mathbf r',t_0)\psi(\mathbf r',t_0)\,d^3r'.
$$
Its initial value is the <Dirac delta function> $\delta^3(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')$.

A time-independent <Hamiltonian> has <energy eigenstates> satisfying $\hat H|n\rangle=E_n|n\rangle$. A single such initial state evolves as
$$
|\psi_n(t)\rangle=e^{-iE_n(t-t_0)/\hbar}|n\rangle.
$$
It is a <stationary state>: its <global phase> changes, but its <probability density> and the distributions of time-independent <observables> do not. Its <energy> is sharp. Any <quantum superposition> entirely within one degenerate <energy eigenspace> also acquires one common <global phase> and is stationary. Stationarity does not require zero <probability current>; for instance, $u_n(\phi)=e^{in\phi}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ on the ring has a constant density and persistent circulation when $n\ne0$.

An arbitrary normalized state can instead contain different <energy eigenvalues>:
$$
|\psi(t)\rangle=\sum_n c_ne^{-iE_n(t-t_0)/\hbar}|n\rangle,
\qquad \sum_n|c_n|^2=1.
$$
A <continuous spectrum> requires corresponding integrals. For the discrete case, an <energy measurement> gives $E_n$ with fixed <probability> $|c_n|^2$ (summed over degeneracies), but relative <quantum phases> change with time. Consequently
$$
|\psi(\mathbf r,t)|^2=\sum_{m,n}c_m^*c_n\phi_m^*(\mathbf r)\phi_n(\mathbf r)
 e^{i(E_m-E_n)(t-t_0)/\hbar}.
$$
The <interference> terms can oscillate at <angular frequencies> $(E_m-E_n)/\hbar$. For two populated <energy levels>, this supplies a characteristic period $2\pi\hbar/|E_m-E_n|$ when the <interference> term is nonzero. Thus fixed energy populations do not imply a stationary spatial distribution.

For a normalized state and an <observable> $\hat A(t)$, its <expectation value> is
$$
\langle A\rangle_t=\langle\psi(t)|\hat A(t)|\psi(t)\rangle.
$$
This is the statistical <mean> over repeated preparations and measurements. In an <eigenbasis> of <energy eigenstates> for a time-independent <Hamiltonian> and a time-independent $\hat A$,
$$
\langle A\rangle_t=\sum_{m,n}c_m^*c_n A_{mn}e^{i(E_m-E_n)(t-t_0)/\hbar}.
$$
Only <matrix elements> connecting different <energies> can generate these oscillations. If $[\hat A,\hat H]=0$, its <expectation value> is constant for every initial state, provided it has no explicit time dependence.

Differentiating the <expectation value> and substituting the <Schrödinger equation> for both the bra and ket proves the general <Ehrenfest theorem>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac d{dt}\langle A\rangle
&=\langle\dot\psi|\hat A|\psi\rangle+
\left\langle\frac{\partial\hat A}{\partial t}\right\rangle+
\langle\psi|\hat A|\dot\psi\rangle\\
&=\frac i\hbar\langle\hat H\hat A-\hat A\hat H\rangle+
\left\langle\frac{\partial\hat A}{\partial t}\right\rangle.
\end{aligned}
$$
Equivalently,
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dt}\langle A\rangle
=\frac1{i\hbar}\langle[\hat A,\hat H]\rangle+
\left\langle\frac{\partial\hat A}{\partial t}\right\rangle.}
$$
These manipulations require the state to lie in the relevant <operator domains>. Taking $\hat A=\hat H$ gives $d\langle H\rangle/dt=\langle\partial_t\hat H\rangle$, so <energy> is conserved under a time-independent <Hamiltonian>.

For $\hat H=\hat p^2/(2m)+V(\hat x)$, the <canonical commutation relation> gives
$$
[\hat x,\hat H]=\frac{i\hbar}{m}\hat p,
\qquad [\hat p,\hat H]=-i\hbar V'(\hat x).
$$
Hence the particularly useful position-and-momentum form of <Ehrenfest theorem> is
$$
\boxed{\frac d{dt}\langle x\rangle=\frac{\langle p\rangle}{m},
\qquad \frac d{dt}\langle p\rangle=-\langle V'(x)\rangle.}
$$
It resembles <Newton's second law>, but the mean <force> generally differs from the <force> at the mean position. If a narrow <wave packet> samples a region where the <force> varies little, $\langle V'(x)\rangle\simeq V'(\langle x\rangle)$ gives approximate classical motion. For a quadratic <potential energy> this replacement is exact. In particular the <quantum harmonic oscillator> obeys
$$
\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\langle x\rangle=-\omega^2\langle x\rangle,
\qquad
\langle x\rangle_t=\langle x\rangle_{t_0}\cos\omega\tau+
\frac{\langle p\rangle_{t_0}}{m\omega}\sin\omega\tau,
\qquad \tau=t-t_0.
$$
The centroid follows the classical <harmonic oscillator>, although this does not specify the full evolving <wavefunction> or its width.

The <Heisenberg picture> expresses the same physics by assigning evolution to <observables> instead:
$$
\hat A_H(t)=U^\dagger(t,t_0)\hat A_S(t)U(t,t_0),
\qquad |\psi_H\rangle=|\psi(t_0)\rangle.
$$
The <expectation value> is unchanged, and differentiation yields $d\hat A_H/dt=(i/\hbar)[\hat H_H,\hat A_H]+U^\dagger(\partial_t\hat A_S)U$. Thus state propagation, evolving <expectation values>, and the classical correspondence in <Ehrenfest theorem> are mutually consistent descriptions of the same <unitary time evolution>.
