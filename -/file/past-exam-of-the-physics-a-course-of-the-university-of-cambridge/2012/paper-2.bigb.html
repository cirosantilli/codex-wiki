= Solution
{parent=1}

The <Arago spot> is a bright axial point inside the geometrical shadow of a circular opaque object. Under coherent illumination, contributions from points around its circular edge have equal optical path to an axial observation point and interfere constructively. It is a <Fresnel diffraction> effect, so geometrical rays alone miss it. In the ideal paraxial plane-wave model, <Babinet's principle> gives $U_{\rm disk}=U_0-U_{\rm aperture}=U_0e^{ika^2/(2z)}$ on axis for disk radius $a$; thus **the axial intensity equals the unobstructed intensity** in that model. The whole shadow is not bright: off-axis phases differ and form a localized central feature. Finite source size, poor coherence or an imperfect edge reduce its visibility.

= Solution

For <elliptical polarization from circular and linear components>, use coherent waves of the same <frequency>, propagation direction and fixed relative phase. Let the circular component be $A(\widehat{\mathbf x}\cos\psi+\widehat{\mathbf y}\sin\psi)$ and the linear component $B\widehat{\mathbf x}\cos\psi$, with $A,B>0$. Their sum has $E_x=(A+B)\cos\psi$ and $E_y=A\sin\psi$, so
$$
\boxed{\frac{E_x^2}{(A+B)^2}+\frac{E_y^2}{A^2}=1}.
$$
The tip of the <electric field> traces an ellipse with unequal semiaxes $A+B$ and $A$. Coherence is necessary: averaging unrelated phases does not create this fixed <polarization ellipse>.

Reading the time axis, the rapid oscillation has period about $2\ \mathrm s$, while successive envelope maxima are about $10\ \mathrm s$ apart. The envelope stays nonzero and has a slowly sinusoidal modulation about a positive mean. The corresponding amplitude-modulation model is $y=[A+B\cos(2\pi\,0.1t)]\cos(2\pi\,0.5t)$, with $A>|B|$. Product-to-sum gives
$$
y=A\cos(2\pi\,0.5t)+\frac B2\cos(2\pi\,0.6t)+\frac B2\cos(2\pi\,0.4t).
$$
Thus the intended positive-frequency components are **$0.4$, $0.5$ and $0.6\ \mathrm{Hz}$**. The envelope <frequency> $0.1\ \mathrm{Hz}$ is not itself a separate additive displacement component. These values use the illustrated sinusoidal-modulation interpretation; a finite qualitative trace alone does not uniquely specify every possible spectral component.

= Solution

At long <wavelength>, the <acoustic phonon branch> moves the two atoms in a cell nearly in phase: $u_M\simeq u_m$. In the $q\to0$ limit this is rigid translation and $\omega\to0$. The <optical phonon branch> moves them oppositely, with stationary cell <centre of mass>: $Mu_M+mu_m=0$. Its $q=0$ <frequency> is finite because adjacent unlike atoms change their separation. For unequal masses the lighter atom has the larger optical displacement. Three-dimensional crystals have three acoustic branches and, with two atoms per primitive cell, three optical branches; longitudinal and transverse directions describe displacement relative to the <wavevector>. The name optical does not mean that every <optical phonon branch> is infrared-active: coupling also depends on effective charges and symmetry.

Let $z_c$ be the number of <conduction electrons> per atom. Then $n=z_c n_a$ and the Drude relations give $\sigma=ne\mu=ne^2\tau/m_e$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\mu=\frac{5.24\times10^{-3}}{z_c}\ \mathrm{m^2\,V^{-1}\,s^{-1}},\qquad
\tau=\frac{2.98\times10^{-14}}{z_c}\ \mathrm s}.
$$
Under an explicit one-electron-per-atom assumption, **$\mu=5.24\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^2\,V^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$ and $\tau=2.98\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm s$**. The carrier count is not specified: monatomic does not by itself imply monovalent, so the boxed dependence on $z_c$ is the general result supported by the data. The collision time here is the transport <relaxation time> inferred within the free-electron model, not a measurement of every microscopic scattering event.

= Solution

For a narrow packet, <phase velocity> is $u_p=\omega/k$ and <group velocity> is $u_g=d\omega/dk$. Set $b=\sigma/\rho_1$. The uncovered surface has
$$
u_p^2=\frac gk+bk,\qquad
\frac{u_g}{u_p}=\frac{g+3bk^2}{2(g+bk^2)}.
$$
Equality occurs at $k_*^2=g/b$, also the minimum of $u_p$. Numerically,
$$
\boxed{k_*=368.9\ \mathrm{m^{-1}},\quad\lambda_*=1.703\ \mathrm{cm},\quad
u_p=u_g=(4gb)^{1/4}=0.2305\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}}.
$$
The long-wave gravity limit has $u_g/u_p\to1/2$; the short-wave capillary limit has $u_g/u_p\to3/2$.

A compact packet subsequently spreads because different <wavenumbers> have different group speeds. In the gravity regime $\omega\simeq\sqrt{gk}$, so smaller $k$ and longer waves move ahead. Individual crests move twice as fast as the envelope, appearing at its rear and disappearing at its front. In the capillary regime $\omega\simeq\sqrt b\,k^{3/2}$, shorter waves move ahead; the envelope overtakes its slower individual crests, which drift backward relative to it. Thus both the <wavelength> ordering within the spread packet and the crest motion relative to its envelope reverse.

For the sheet define $c=a\rho_2/\rho_1=8.0\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm m$. The <elastic-cover frequency ratio for water waves> is
$$
\boxed{F(k)=\frac{\omega_s}{\omega_0}
=\left[\frac{1+Bk^4/(\rho_1g+\sigma k^2)}{1+ck}\right]^{1/2}}.
$$
At small $k$, $F=1-ck/2+O(k^2)$: sheet inertia lowers the <frequency>, while bending first enters at higher order. At sufficiently large $k$, bending dominates the restoring force and sheet inertia dominates the extra <mass>, giving $F\sim\sqrt{B/(\sigma c)}\,k^{1/2}$. The curve initially dips below one, reaches a minimum, then rises and crosses one. This asymptote describes the supplied ideal dispersion law; arbitrarily large $k$ eventually violates a thin-sheet continuum model.

For a <pinned-square surface displacement mode>, $k_{nm}=\pi\sqrt{n^2+m^2}/L$ with $n,m\geq1$. The fundamental has $k_{11}=74.05\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$. The uncovered <frequency> is $4.3729\ \mathrm{Hz}$ and $F(k_{11})=0.997051$, so
$$
\boxed{f_s=4.3600\ \mathrm{Hz},\qquad\Delta f=-0.0129\ \mathrm{Hz}}.
$$
This uses a square two-dimensional surface mode, not the one-dimensional $\pi/L$ estimate. A clamped plate or additional hydraulic <boundary conditions> would require a different mode calculation.

= Solution
{parent=B7}

The angle in the source ray sketch is inside the gap. Two extra reflections add a full round trip. Comparing adjacent emerging rays on a common outgoing phase plane subtracts the lateral-path contribution, so the <optical path difference> is $2\mu d\cos\theta$, rather than $2\mu d/\cos\theta$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\delta=2k_0\mu d\cos\theta},\qquad \delta=2\pi m
$$
at transmission maxima. The two internal reflection phases add to an integer full cycle in the stated ideal symmetric-coating model. <Snell's law>, $\mu\sin\theta=\mu_0\sin\theta_{\rm out}$, converts to the external direction. A fixed internal angle defines a cone and hence an equal-inclination ring in a <focal plane>.

For identical lossless mirrors the first transmitted amplitude has magnitude $(1-R)$ times the incident amplitude. Each additional round trip multiplies it by $Re^{i\delta}$, because $R$ is an intensity <reflection coefficient> for one mirror but the product of the two amplitude reflection magnitudes. Summing the <geometric series> gives
$$
\boxed{T_e=\left|\frac{1-R}{1-Re^{i\delta}}\right|^2
=\frac{(1-R)^2}{1+R^2-2R\cos\delta}}.
$$
The maximum is one in this symmetric lossless limit; absorption, unequal mirrors or additional incoherent reflections change it. At fixed angle and negligible material dispersion, $m\lambda=2\mu d\cos\theta$ and adjacent orders differ by one. For $m\gg1$,
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{FSR}_\lambda\simeq\frac{\lambda^2}{2\mu d\cos\theta}},\qquad
\delta\lambda\simeq\frac{\mathrm{FSR}_\lambda}{\mathcal F}.
$$

<order-safe angular scanning of a Fabry–Pérot spectrum> should cover the approximately $38\ \mathrm{nm}$ spectral span within one order while making the instrumental width smaller than $1\ \mathrm{nm}$. Near <normal incidence>, requiring the smallest approximate FSR to exceed the span gives $\mu d\lesssim5.3\ \mathrm{\mu m}$, and $\mathcal F$ should exceed roughly $40$. These are design bounds, not a unique optimum. One suitable choice is
$$
\boxed{\mu d=5.0\ \mathrm{\mu m},\qquad R=0.95,\qquad\mathcal F\simeq61.2}.
$$
The physical spacing is $d=5.0\ \mathrm{\mu m}/\mu$; an air gap gives $d\simeq5\ \mathrm{\mu m}$. Approximate FSRs near <normal incidence> are $40.1\ \mathrm{nm}$ at $633\ \mathrm{nm}$ and $44.9\ \mathrm{nm}$ at $670\ \mathrm{nm}$, with widths about $0.65$ and $0.73\ \mathrm{nm}$. For a concrete angular scan choose order $m=14$. The $670\ \mathrm{nm}$ pair lies near internal $\theta=20.3^\circ$ and the $633\ \mathrm{nm}$ line near $27.6^\circ$; their widths within this scan remain below $1\ \mathrm{nm}$. Restricting to this calibrated order window avoids the other-order $633\ \mathrm{nm}$ ring near $18.3^\circ$. Index dispersion and unknown extra source lines would need calibration or order-sorting filters.

The resolving <power> is approximately $m\mathcal F$: many coherent cavity round trips create a much longer effective optical delay than one traversal. A Michelson instrument with the same available physical delay has less resolving <power> unless its mirror travel provides a correspondingly long maximum <optical path difference>. Mirror separation errors spread the round-trip phase; keeping them below a linewidth requires roughly
$$
\boxed{|\Delta d|\ll\frac{\lambda}{2\mu\mathcal F\cos\theta}}.
$$
For this design the scale is about $6\ \mathrm{nm}$ in an air gap. Both surface flatness and parallelism over the illuminated area, as well as angular collimation and coating uniformity, must support the intended <finesse>.

The Fraunhofer approximation drops the aperture-dependent quadratic propagation phase. For illuminated extent $b$ and observation distance $R$, a sufficient paraxial condition is $b^2/(\lambda R)\ll1$, with coherent nearly plane incident illumination. Collimation followed by observation in a lens's back <focal plane> produces the same angular Fourier pattern without an astronomical propagation distance.

Let $(x,y)$ label the <aperture> field $\psi_\Sigma$ and let $(x_0,y_0)$ be transverse coordinates of the distant detector, with $R$ the axial separation. Expanding the propagation distance retains a common phase and the cross term $-(x_0x+y_0y)/R$, while dropping the <aperture>'s quadratic term. Summing the scalar Huygens contributions gives a field proportional to the <Fourier transform> at $(k x_0/R,k y_0/R)$. In one dimension, writing $q=kx_0/R$, $\widetilde\psi(q)=\int\psi(x)e^{-iqx}dx$ gives
$$
\boxed{\psi(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\int\widetilde\psi(q)e^{iqx}\,dq}.
$$
Remove the known propagation prefactor and any detector-plane quadratic phase before applying this inverse transform. Recovery needs the complex diffracted field, including phase; its intensity alone is insufficient for this inversion.

The <whole-aperture Fraunhofer distance of a coherent array> uses the full ten-dish extent, which is about $b=9D+25\ \mathrm m=7.225\ \mathrm{km}$. Thus $b^2/\lambda\simeq8.7\times10^8\ \mathrm m$, and a conventional boundary $2b^2/\lambda$ is about $1.7\times10^9\ \mathrm m$. **Far-field propagation must be of order a million kilometres or more**, with a stronger inequality for accurate Fraunhofer behaviour. Applying the condition to only one dish or one spacing would miss the array extent.

For equal synchronized dish fields, the array sum is $\sum_{j=0}^9e^{-ij k_0D\sin\theta}$. Its intensity is
$$
\boxed{I(\theta)=I_1(\theta)
\left[\frac{\sin(10\beta)}{\sin\beta}\right]^2,\qquad
\beta=\frac{k_0D\sin\theta}{2}}.
$$
Near the centre, $\sin\theta\simeq\theta$ and the single-dish envelope $I_1(\theta)$ is nearly constant, giving the requested approximation. A uniformly illuminated circular dish instead supplies the broader envelope $[2J_1(\pi d\sin\theta/\lambda)/(\pi d\sin\theta/\lambda)]^2$, with first zero near $1.22\lambda/d=2.93\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{rad}$. It cannot be ignored over the entire pattern.

Principal array maxima have $\sin\theta_m=m\lambda/D$, height $100I_1(\theta_m)$, with spacing approximately $7.5\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{rad}=15.5$ arcseconds. There are narrow sidelobes and many grating orders within the dish envelope because $D\gg d$. The central maximum's first zeros obey $10\beta=\pm\pi$. By reception/transmission reciprocity, the source must therefore move through
$$
\boxed{\Delta\theta\simeq\frac{\lambda}{10D}=7.50\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{rad}=1.55\text{ arcseconds}}
$$
from zero-order maximum to first minimum. The equatorial geometry fixes the sweep direction; no elapsed time is requested.

= Solution
{parent=B9}

The string speed is $c=\sqrt{T/\rho}$. The <characteristic impedance> is the ratio of the forward-wave transverse force to transverse velocity, $Z=T/c=\sqrt{T\rho}$. With peak displacement amplitude $A$, the mean <power> is
$$
\boxed{\langle P\rangle=\tfrac12Z\omega^2A^2}.
$$
Displacement and transverse force are continuous at a massless junction. Since $Z_2=2Z_1$, the displacement amplitudes are $r=(Z_1-Z_2)/(Z_1+Z_2)=-1/3$ and $t=2Z_1/(Z_1+Z_2)=2/3$. Taking the incident amplitude real, the light-string motion is
$$
y=A\left[\cos(kx-\omega t)-\tfrac13\cos(kx+\omega t)\right]
=\frac{2A}{3}\cos(kx-\omega t)+\frac{2A}{3}\sin kx\sin\omega t.
$$
In this <standing-and-travelling decomposition of a reflected string wave>, the first term travels and the second stands; the decomposition is not a separation into independent incident-power fractions because these fields interfere. The reflected wave has a phase reversal. <power> coefficients and standing-wave ratio are
$$
\boxed{\mathcal R=|r|^2=\frac19,\quad
\mathcal T=\frac{Z_2}{Z_1}|t|^2=\frac89,\quad
\mathrm{SWR}=\frac{1+|r|}{1-|r|}=2}.
$$

For a uniform inserted matching section, unequal exterior impedances require a <quarter-wave impedance transformer>: $Z_M=\sqrt{Z_1Z_2}$ and $k_Ma=(2j+1)\pi/2$. At equal <tension> this means **middle density $2\rho_0$ and an odd-quarter-wavelength length in that section**. Reflections at its two ends then cancel at the design <frequency>. An arbitrary density or an arbitrary half-wavelength segment cannot perfectly match these unequal exterior strings; the ideal match is frequency-selective.

<amplitude scaling along a slowly tapered string> instead follows from matching over an adiabatic <frequency> range. The source fixes <frequency>, while conserved <power> and the final <characteristic impedance> determine amplitude. In the heavy string,
$$
\boxed{f_2=f_1,\qquad A_2=A_1/\sqrt2,\qquad\lambda_2=\lambda_1/2}.
$$
Its <wave speed> halves. The no-reflection approximation needs small fractional <characteristic impedance> change over each local <wavelength>, rather than length alone for a taper containing abrupt features.

= Solution
{parent=C10}

In a periodic crystal potential, atomic levels broaden into bands of allowed <bloch states>. Coupling between states of the same symmetry opens <avoided crossings>, including Bragg-coupled states in the nearly free-electron picture. Bonding and antibonding combinations provide a complementary tight-binding picture. A <semiconductor> has a filled <valence band> and an empty <conduction band> at zero <temperature> separated by a forbidden <energy> interval; thermal excitation, light and doping provide mobile <electrons> and holes. The gap depends on the crystal's <periodic potential>, bonding and symmetry, not on a classical collision barrier. In a direct gap, the relevant extrema coincide in <wavevector>.

For the stated parabolic edges, take $E_v(k)=-\hbar^2k^2/(2m_h^*)$ and $E_c(k)=E_g+\hbar^2k^2/(2m_e^*)$. Negligible <photon> <momentum> and no <phonon> make the transition nearly vertical in $E$ versus $k$. <parabolic-band optical energy sharing> divides the excess <photon> <energy> $0.32\ \mathrm{eV}$ in inverse proportion to the effective masses:
$$
K_e=0.32\frac{0.4}{0.14+0.4}=0.2370\ \mathrm{eV},\qquad
K_h=0.32\frac{0.14}{0.14+0.4}=0.0830\ \mathrm{eV}.
$$
Thus the initial and final <electron> energies, relative to the valence-band maximum, are
$$
\boxed{E_i=-0.0830\ \mathrm{eV},\qquad E_f=0.4170\ \mathrm{eV}}.
$$
Their difference is $0.5000\ \mathrm{eV}$. The selected $|k|$ is about $9.33\times10^8\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$; either sign gives the same <energy> in this symmetric approximation.

A shallow donor supplies an <electron> in a screened impurity potential, so its localized level lies just below the conduction edge. A shallow acceptor binds a hole and has an electron-occupation level just above the valence edge; thermal ionization supplies mobile holes. The level positions are measured relative to the relevant band edge, and deep impurities need not obey this hydrogenic approximation.

For the supplied bound-carrier <mass> and unit effective ionic charge, <dielectric screening> and effective <mass> change the hydrogen binding <energy> to
$$
\boxed{E_b=13.6\frac{0.1}{12^2}\ \mathrm{eV}=9.44\ \mathrm{meV}}.
$$
This corresponds to $E_b/k_B\simeq110\ \mathrm K$ and an effective Bohr radius about $6.35\ \mathrm{nm}$. The binding is shallow compared with the <band gap> and typical atomic binding. Trivalent denotes chemical valence, not an unscreened hydrogenic charge of three. An acceptor physically binds a hole; the question's stated $0.1m_e$ is used as its stipulated bound-carrier <mass> rather than silently replacing it by the separate band-edge $0.4m_e$.

= Solution
{parent=C11}

With periodic <boundary conditions>, each $k$ state occupies volume $(2\pi)^3/V$ and has two spin states. Filling a sphere gives $n=k_F^3/(3\pi^2)$ and
$$
\boxed{E_F=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}(3\pi^2n)^{2/3}},\qquad
D(E)=\frac1{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m_e}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt E,
\quad \boxed{D(E_F)=\frac{3n}{2E_F}}.
$$
Here $D$ is <density of states> per volume and per <energy>; the extensive $g(E)$ is $VD(E)$. Both spin states are already included.

For divalent magnesium, $n=8.6\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$, giving
$$
\boxed{E_F=7.10\ \mathrm{eV},\qquad
D(E_F)=1.13\times10^{47}\ \mathrm{J^{-1}m^{-3}}
=1.82\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{eV^{-1}m^{-3}}}.
$$
At $T\ll T_F$, only <electrons> within about $k_BT$ of the <Fermi surface> change occupancy. The Sommerfeld <heat capacity> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{C_e}{V}=\frac{\pi^2}{3}k_B^2D(E_F)T
=71.1\,T\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}\,K^{-1}}}.
$$
Equivalently $C_{e,m}=0.995\,T\ \mathrm{mJ\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$, with $T$ in kelvin, so at room <temperature> it is about $0.299\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$. A <temperature> was not specified for the estimate; the linear coefficient is the general result.

At sufficiently low <temperature>, an ordinary nonsuperconducting metal has $C\simeq\gamma T+\beta T^3$: <electrons> dominate at the lowest <temperatures>, acoustic <phonons> produce the cubic contribution, and the lattice approaches $3R$ per mole as <temperature> exceeds its characteristic Debye scale. Anharmonic expansion can distinguish measured $C_p$ from $C_V$. In the room-temperature regime, $T/T_F\simeq0.0036$ since $T_F\simeq8.24\times10^4\ \mathrm K$, so <electron> excitation is strongly Pauli-limited. The free-electron estimate is only about $1.2\%$ of the classical $3R$ lattice scale. Calling it negligible means small relative to the lattice contribution, not identically zero or negligible as $T\to0$. Real band structure and interactions can alter the coefficient.

= Solution
{parent=D12}

For $m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=F_0\cos\omega t$, define $\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}$ and $\beta=b/(2m)$. The parameter relating <quality factor and viscous damping regimes> is
$$
\boxed{Q=\frac{\omega_0}{2\beta}=\frac{m\omega_0}{b}}.
$$
At weak damping it also equals $2\pi$ times stored <energy> divided by <energy> lost per cycle. The stored-energy convention and <frequency> matter: this cycle-based interpretation should not be extended to free motions that never complete a cycle.

For $\beta<\omega_0$ the free motion is $e^{-\beta t}[A\cos\omega_dt+B\sin\omega_dt]$, where $\omega_d=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\beta^2}$. Thus **<underdamping> means $Q>1/2$**. High $Q$ gives many oscillations per amplitude-decay time, narrow <resonance> and strong <frequency> selectivity; a lightly loaded tuning fork, optical cavity or low-loss electrical <resonator> provides examples. The amplitude decay time is $\tau_A=1/\beta=2Q/\omega_0$, while the slowly averaged <energy> decays with $\tau_E=1/(2\beta)=Q/\omega_0$. These times differ by a factor of two.

At <critical damping>, $\beta=\omega_0$ and $Q=1/2$, with $x=(A+Bt)e^{-\omega_0t}$. There is no ringing; a simple critically damped position indicator can settle without oscillation. For $\beta>\omega_0$ and $Q<1/2$, there are two real negative decay rates $-\beta\pm\sqrt{\beta^2-\omega_0^2}$. Increasing very strong drag slows the long-time return, whose rate approaches $\omega_0^2/(2\beta)$. A strongly viscously damped mechanical indicator illustrates this regime. These classifications concern the linear homogeneous equation, not every possible nonlinear damping law.

The driven displacement amplitude is
$$
A(\omega)=\frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+4\beta^2\omega^2}}.
$$
The <displacement-resonance threshold of a viscous oscillator> requires $\beta<\omega_0/\sqrt2$, or **$Q>1/\sqrt2$**; it lies at $\sqrt{\omega_0^2-2\beta^2}$. Some underdamped systems therefore have no such maximum. Average absorbed <power> instead peaks at $\omega_0$ for this viscous model. Its half-power frequencies are $\sqrt{\omega_0^2+\beta^2}\pm\beta$, so their separation is $2\beta$ and $Q=\omega_0/\Delta\omega$. For broad response this ratio remains a useful parameter but not a claim of many free oscillations. The familiar displacement-linewidth version is a high-$Q$ approximation.

A <series RLC circuit> has $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$ and $Q=\omega_0L/R$ for the charge oscillation. Resistance dissipates <energy>, inductance provides the inertial analogue and capacitance the restoring analogue. Ringdown and a calibrated half-power scan independently estimate loss; parasitic resistance, loading and radiation change the measured value. A large $Q$ increases <frequency> selectivity but also lengthens settling time, so it is not universally the best instrumental choice.

= Solution
{parent=a}

A three-phonon normal process conserves crystal <wavevector> without a reciprocal-lattice shift, $\mathbf k_1+\mathbf k_2=\mathbf k_3$. An <Umklapp process> instead has
$$
\boxed{\mathbf k_1+\mathbf k_2=\mathbf k_3+\mathbf G,\qquad \mathbf G\ne0}.
$$
The crystal can absorb the reciprocal-lattice <momentum>; after reduction to the first <Brillouin zone> the <phonon> drift transporting heat is relaxed. A normal process redistributes <phonons> while preserving their total <crystal momentum>, so it is not by itself the same resistive mechanism. <energy> must also be conserved in either process.

At low <temperature>, large-wavevector <phonons> are rare, suppressing <Umklapp scattering>. Boundary and defect scattering then limit the <mean free path>; with a nearly constant boundary-limited path, $\kappa_{\rm ph}\simeq(C_{\rm ph}/V)v\ell/3$ grows as $T^3$. As <temperature> increases, <heat capacity> rises but <Umklapp scattering> shortens $\ell$, often producing a <electrical conductivity> maximum. At sufficiently high <temperature> a simple anharmonic model has $C_{\rm ph}$ nearly constant and $\tau_U^{-1}\propto T$, giving $\kappa_{\rm ph}\propto T^{-1}$. These are regime estimates, not a universal law for every material or size.

= Solution

For degenerate carriers with approximately elastic scattering and a common transport <relaxation time>, $\sigma=ne^2\tau/m^*$ and $\kappa_e=(C_e/V)v_F^2\tau/3$. Using $C_e/V=\pi^2nk_B^2T/(2E_F)$ and $v_F^2=2E_F/m^*$ gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{\kappa_e}{\sigma T}=L_0,\qquad
L_0=\frac{\pi^2k_B^2}{3e^2}=2.44\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{W\,\Omega\,K^{-2}}}.
$$
This is the Wiedemann–Franz law. The same mobile <electrons> carry charge and heat, but Pauli statistics set the <electronic heat capacity> and hence the <Lorenz number>. The relation applies to electronic <thermal conductivity>, not automatically to total <electrical conductivity> including <phonons>. Inelastic <electron-phonon scattering> can relax <energy> and charge currents differently; nondegeneracy and strong <energy> dependence of scattering also limit the simple form.

A meaningful test combines a four-terminal electrical measurement with a steady thermal-gradient measurement on the same sample and <temperature> range. Contact heat resistance, parasitic heat leaks, <phonon> conduction and sample geometry must be controlled before interpreting a measured Lorenz ratio as an electronic departure from the law.

= Solution
{parent=c}

A carrier in a locally parabolic isotropic band obeys $m^*\dot{\mathbf v}=q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$, so
$$
\boxed{\omega_c=\frac{|q|B}{m_c},\qquad m_c=\frac{|q|B_{\rm res}}\omega}.
$$
Illuminate a high-mobility crystal with a transverse alternating <electric field>, sweep a calibrated static <magnetic field> at known <microwave> or far-infrared <frequency>, and detect resonant absorbed <power>. Cooling lengthens the <relaxation time>; resolving the <resonance> generally needs $\omega_c\tau\gg1$. Varying field direction measures band anisotropy, while <circular polarization> distinguishes the two senses of rotation for opposite carrier charges. This is orbital motion rather than <electron> spin <resonance>.

The classic silicon/germanium measurements used single crystals near $4\ \mathrm K$, light modulation and circularly polarized <microwaves>, with directional scans to infer anisotropic carrier-energy surfaces.<Cyclotron Resonance of Electrons and Holes in Silicon and Germanium Crystals by Gene Dresselhaus and others>[Abstract: low-temperature single crystals, light modulation, circular polarization and anisotropy]{ref} Carrier generation, calibration, scattering linewidth and inhomogeneity must be separated from a genuine <mass> shift.

In a general band the cyclotron <mass> is an orbit property, $m_c=\hbar^2(\partial A_k/\partial E)/(2\pi)$ for the appropriate field-normal orbit. It need not equal every component of the band-curvature <mass> tensor. For an ellipsoidal parabolic band with field along a principal $z$ direction it is $\sqrt{m_xm_y}$. Thus a single <resonance> does not determine a whole effective-mass tensor without angular information.
