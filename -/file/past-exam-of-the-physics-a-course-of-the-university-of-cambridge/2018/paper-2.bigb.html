= Solution
{parent=A1}

For a <photon>, $E=hc/\lambda$. Using <Planck constant> $h=6.62607015\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$, <speed of light> $c=2.99792458\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and $1\,\mathrm{eV}=1.602176634\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm J$ gives
$$
\lambda=\frac{hc}{2.5\,\mathrm{eV}}=4.96\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm m.
$$
The <Rayleigh criterion> for a circular <aperture> of diameter $D$ is $\theta_R=1.22\lambda/D$, set by the first zero of its <Airy disk>. Thus the ideal <diffraction-limited resolution> is
$$
\boxed{\theta_R=2.52\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{rad}},\qquad
\boxed{s=R\theta_R=5.04\times10^{10}\,\mathrm m}.
$$
The conversion to transverse separation uses the <small-angle approximation>. An order-of-magnitude estimate $\lambda/D$ instead gives $2.07\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{rad}$ and $4.13\times10^{10}\,\mathrm m$; the distinction is the chosen resolution criterion. These are <aperture> limits, before instrumental aberrations and detector sampling.

= Solution
{parent=A2}

Both the incoming and outgoing spherical <wavefronts> contribute to the path excess. At <aperture> radius $u$, with axial distances $s$ before and $r$ after the <aperture>,
$$
\Delta\ell=\sqrt{s^2+u^2}+\sqrt{r^2+u^2}-(s+r)
\simeq\frac{u^2}{2}\left(\frac1s+\frac1r\right).
$$
The outer edge of the $n$th <Fresnel half-period zone> has $\Delta\ell=n\lambda/2$. Hence
$$
u_n^2=\frac{n\lambda sr}{s+r},\qquad
u_2=\sqrt{\frac{2(400\times10^{-9})(0.5)(0.5)}{1.0}}.
$$
Therefore **the outer radius is** $\boxed{u_2=4.47\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm m=0.447\,\mathrm{mm}}$. The <paraxial approximation> is excellent because $u_2\ll s,r$. Treating the illumination as a <plane wave> would incorrectly omit the source-side path curvature.

= Solution
{parent=A3}

At normal incidence between lossless media with real positive <acoustic impedances> $Z_1,Z_2$, continuity of pressure and normal particle <velocity> gives the pressure transmission amplitude $t_p=2Z_2/(Z_1+Z_2)$. Since the mean <acoustic intensity> is $|p|^2/(2Z)$, the fraction of incident <energy> transmitted is
$$
\mathcal T=\frac{Z_1}{Z_2}|t_p|^2=\frac{4Z_1Z_2}{(Z_1+Z_2)^2},\qquad
\mathcal R=\left(\frac{Z_2-Z_1}{Z_2+Z_1}\right)^2=1-\mathcal T.
$$
This is <acoustic reflection and transmission at an interface>. With the supplied <acoustic impedances>,
$$
\boxed{\mathcal T_{\rm air\to body}=9.81\times10^{-4}=0.0981\%},\qquad
\boxed{\mathcal T_{\rm water\to body}=0.99767=99.77\%}.
$$
A thin water-based gel layer excludes air gaps and approximately matches the body's <acoustic impedance>. About $99.90\%$ of incident <energy> would otherwise reflect at the air-body interface. The coupling is also needed on the return journey, so it strongly improves the detectable echo. These figures concern interface transmission, before absorption within tissue; the gel is approximated by the stated water value.

= Solution
{parent=A4}

Take nearest-neighbour separation $d$, a common <spring constant> $K$, and one scalar displacement per atom. For a monatomic chain, the <harmonic approximation near a potential minimum>, <Newton's second law> and a <normal mode> give
$$
m\ddot u_j=K(u_{j+1}+u_{j-1}-2u_j),\qquad
u_j=u_0e^{i(kjd-\omega t)},\qquad
\boxed{\omega=2\sqrt{K/m}\,|\sin(kd/2)|}.
$$
The <monatomic-chain dispersion relation> has one <acoustic phonon branch>, with $\omega\simeq d\sqrt{K/m}|k|$ near the origin. The first <Brillouin zone> ends at $\boxed{k=\pm\pi/d}$, where $\omega=2\sqrt{K/m}$ and the <group velocity> vanishes.

For alternating <masses> $m,M$, choose a two-atom cell of length $2d$. The coupled <normal modes> give the <diatomic-chain dispersion relation with equal springs>,
$$
\boxed{\omega_\pm^2=K\left(\frac1m+\frac1M\right)\pm K\sqrt{\left(\frac1m+\frac1M\right)^2-\frac4{mM}\sin^2(kd)}}.
$$
The first <Brillouin zone> now ends at $\boxed{k=\pm\pi/(2d)}$. The minus branch is the <acoustic phonon branch>; its two atoms move together at long <wavelength>. The plus branch is the <optical phonon branch>; at $k=0$ the atoms move oppositely with a stationary centre of <mass> and $\omega_+^2=2K(1/m+1/M)$. For $M>m$ the edge frequencies are $\sqrt{2K/M}$ and $\sqrt{2K/m}$, with a gap between them.

If $M=m$, the two branches reduce to $2\sqrt{K/m}|\sin(kd/2)|$ and $2\sqrt{K/m}|\cos(kd/2)|$, touching at $\sqrt{2K/m}$. This is <zone folding in an equal-mass diatomic chain>: identical sites actually have a <primitive unit cell> of length $d$. A doubled cell folds the monatomic branch but opens no gap. Equal masses alone do not specify all possible spring models; the sketches assume equal nearest-neighbour <spring constants>.

= Solution
{parent=A5}

Under the one-carrier <Drude model>, the magnitude of the <Hall coefficient> gives an effective <carrier density>
$$
n_H=\frac1{e|R_H|}=2.60\times10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}.
$$
Convert the material <mass density> to $1850\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$ and its <molar mass> to $0.009\,\mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}}$. The atomic <number density> is
$$
n_{\rm at}=\frac{1850}{0.009}N_A=1.238\times10^{29}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}},\qquad
\boxed{\frac{n_H}{n_{\rm at}}=0.210\ \text{effective carriers per atom}}.
$$
Here $N_A=6.02214076\times10^{23}\,\mathrm{mol^{-1}}$ is the <Avogadro constant>. The positive <Hall coefficient> gives a hole-like response in a one-carrier interpretation. The <Hall carrier count versus valence> distinction matters: <beryllium> has two <valence electrons>, but the result is neither two free <electrons> nor a literal fractional atom donating charge. Its <energy bands> and <Fermi surface> invalidate the simple isotropic one-carrier inference. Electron-like and hole-like contributions with different <charge carrier mobilities> can yield this sign and effective count.

= Solution

Substituting $x=Ae^{pt}$ into the <damped harmonic oscillator> equation gives
$$
p^2+\gamma p+\omega_0^2=0,\qquad p_\pm=-\frac\gamma2\pm\sqrt{\frac{\gamma^2}{4}-\omega_0^2}.
$$
For nonnegative <viscous damping>, the cases are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\gamma>2\omega_0 &: \quad x=C_+e^{p_+t}+C_-e^{p_-t} &&\text{overdamping},\\
\gamma=2\omega_0 &: \quad x=(C_1+C_2t)e^{-\omega_0t} &&\text{critical damping},\\
0\leq\gamma<2\omega_0 &: \quad x=e^{-\gamma t/2}[C_1\cos\omega_dt+C_2\sin\omega_dt] &&\text{underdamping},
\end{aligned}}\qquad \omega_d=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2/4}.
$$
The PDF's request to express these conditions in terms of $\gamma/m$ is dimensionally inconsistent with its definition $\gamma=b/m$: $\gamma$ already has units $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. With the unnormalised equation $m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=0$, the equivalent comparisons are $b/m\gtrless2\omega_0$, or $b^2\gtrless4mk$. Also $\omega_0$ is the undamped natural <angular frequency>, not in general the driven displacement peak.

{parent=B6}

= Solution
{parent=b}

For the steady response of a <forced harmonic oscillator>, write $x=\operatorname{Re}(Ae^{i\omega t})$ and use the same <complex amplitude> convention for the driving <force>. Substitution gives
$$
[-\omega^2+i\gamma\omega+\omega_0^2]A=F_0/m,\qquad
\boxed{A=R(\omega)F_0,\quad R(\omega)=\frac1{m(\omega_0^2-\omega^2+i\gamma\omega)}}.
$$
Thus the response magnitude and displacement <phase> relative to the <force> are
$$
|R|=\frac1{m\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+\gamma^2\omega^2}},\qquad
\phi=-\operatorname{atan2}(\gamma\omega,\omega_0^2-\omega^2).
$$
The <phase> goes from $0$ at low <frequency>, through $-\pi/2$ at $\omega_0$, towards $-\pi$ at high <frequency>. The static magnitude is $1/(m\omega_0^2)=1/k$, while the high-frequency magnitude is approximately $1/(m\omega^2)$.

Define the <quality factor> by $Q=\omega_0/\gamma$. If $Q>1/\sqrt2$, the displacement peak occurs at $\omega_r=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2/2}$; for $Q\leq1/\sqrt2$ the magnitude decreases monotonically from its static value. For $Q\gg1$ there is a tall narrow peak close to $\omega_0$, of height approximately $Q/k$ and width approximately $\gamma$. At very small $Q$ the resonant peak disappears and the response is broad. The plotted functions are normalized as $k|R|$, at fixed $m,k$.

The original diagram is a <series RLC circuit>, so the same <electric current> $i=\dot q$ passes through all three elements. Choose capacitor <electric charge> $q$ positive on the plate into which positive $i$ enters. <Kirchhoff's voltage law> gives
$$
V(t)=V_L+V_R+V_C=L\frac{di}{dt}+Ri+\frac qC=L\ddot q+R\dot q+\frac qC.
$$
Dividing by <inductance> $L$ gives
$$
\boxed{\ddot q+\frac RL\dot q+\frac1{LC}q=\frac{V(t)}L}.
$$
The correspondence to the <forced harmonic oscillator> is $m\leftrightarrow L$, $b\leftrightarrow R$, $k\leftrightarrow1/C$, and $x\leftrightarrow q$. The stored <energy> is $L i^2/2+q^2/(2C)$; its balance is $\dot E=Vi-Ri^2$, confirming the damping sign.

{parent=B6}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

The capacitor-charge <complex amplitude> is
$$
q_0=\frac{V_0/L}{1/(LC)-\omega^2+i(R/L)\omega}.
$$
For fixed driving-voltage <amplitude>, maximizing $|q_0|$ minimizes
$$
D(\omega)=\left(\omega_0^2-\omega^2\right)^2+\gamma^2\omega^2,\qquad
\omega_0^2=\frac1{LC},\quad\gamma=\frac RL.
$$
Differentiate:
$$
\frac{dD}{d\omega}=2\omega[-2(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)+\gamma^2].
$$
The positive-frequency minimum therefore has
$$
\boxed{\omega_{\rm res}=\sqrt{\frac1{LC}-\frac{R^2}{2L^2}}},\qquad
R^2<\frac{2L}{C}.
$$
This is the <resonance> of charge, or capacitor voltage. If the stated inequality fails, their maximum is at zero <frequency>, and no positive displacement-resonance peak exists. The <electric current> has the additional factor $\omega$, and instead peaks exactly at $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$ for every $R>0$.

= Solution
{parent=e}

The precise <bandwidth> depends on the output: this is the distinction between <displacement and current bandwidths of a series RLC circuit>. For current output, or voltage across the <resistor>, the <electrical impedance> gives
$$
|i|^2=\frac{|V_0|^2}{R^2+(\omega L-1/(\omega C))^2}.
$$
The half-power points satisfy $|\omega L-1/(\omega C)|=R$. Their positive roots are
$$
\omega_\pm=\frac{\sqrt{R^2+4L/C}\pm R}{2L},\qquad
\boxed{\Delta\omega=\omega_+-\omega_-=R/L},\qquad
\boxed{\Delta f=R/(2\pi L)}.
$$
Changing <capacitance> shifts the centre without changing this absolute width. For the charge response in part (d), the exact half-height points instead obey
$$
\omega_\pm^2=\omega_0^2-\gamma^2/2\pm\gamma\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2/4};
$$
when both exist their separation only approaches $\gamma=R/L$ at large <quality factor>. Therefore the printed capacitance-independence claim is exact for current or resistor-voltage output and an approximation for the preceding charge resonance.

For a narrow <band-pass filter>, use current or resistor-voltage output and make $R/L$ small, counting source, load and inductor losses in the total <electrical resistance>. Increase $Q=\sqrt{L/C}/R$; if a specified centre <frequency> is to remain fixed, adjust $C$ with $L$ to keep $LC$ fixed. Capacitor-voltage output alone is not a band-pass response because it has a nonzero DC limit.

= Solution

In <Fraunhofer diffraction>, the relative propagation <phase> across the <aperture> is linear in <aperture> coordinates. If $b$ is its largest transverse size, the neglected quadratic path term must be small: for an observer at distance $R$,
$$
\boxed{\frac{b^2}{\lambda R}\ll1}.
$$
For a finite-distance point source a distance $s$ away, also require $b^2/(\lambda s)\ll1$ if the illumination is to be treated as a <plane wave>. Numerical factors depend on whether $b$ is a radius or full width and on the permitted <phase> error; the condition expresses a small <Fresnel number>.

One can place both source and screen sufficiently far away, or collimate a point source with a <lens (optics)> and observe the <aperture> in the back focal plane of a second <lens (optics)>. In the latter arrangement rays of each outgoing direction focus at one point, with $X\simeq f\theta_x$; the lens removes the need for a physically distant screen. A laser beam approximately uniform across the <aperture> can supply the incident <plane wave>. The sketches distinguish an actual far field from its focal-plane equivalent.

{parent=B7}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let the <aperture> occupy the $z=0$ plane, with coordinates $(x,y)$ and opening $\Sigma$. Write $\psi_\Sigma(x,y)$ for the incident scalar <complex amplitude> and $h(x,y)$ for its dimensionless amplitude transmission, including any imposed <phase>. The observation point has transverse coordinates $(X,Y)$, corresponding to the PDF's $(x_0,y_0)$, and axial distance $R$. Also $k=2\pi/\lambda$ is the <wavenumber> and $i^2=-1$.

The <Huygens-Fresnel principle> expresses the propagated field, up to common prefactors and an approximately constant obliquity factor, as
$$
\psi_P\propto\iint_\Sigma \psi_\Sigma h\,\frac{e^{ikr}}r\,dx\,dy,\qquad
r=\sqrt{R^2+(X-x)^2+(Y-y)^2}.
$$
In the <paraxial approximation>, expand the path as
$$
r\simeq R+\frac{X^2+Y^2}{2R}-\frac{Xx+Yy}{R}+\frac{x^2+y^2}{2R}.
$$
The last term is negligible in the <Fraunhofer diffraction> limit, while the first two terms and $1/r\simeq1/R$ are common across the <aperture>. Factoring them out leaves
$$
\boxed{\psi_P\propto\iint_\Sigma\psi_\Sigma(x,y)h(x,y)e^{-ik(Xx+Yy)/R}\,dx\,dy}.
$$
It is the two-dimensional <Fourier transform> of the transmitted <aperture> field, evaluated at transverse <wavevector> $(kX/R,kY/R)$. For appreciable observation angles the corresponding exact directional components are used rather than $X/R,Y/R$.

Represent equal narrow slits at $x=\pm D/2$ by two contributions with equal <complex amplitudes>. In a direction $\theta$ the path difference is $D\sin\theta$, so
$$
\psi(\theta)\propto e^{iqD/2}+e^{-iqD/2}=2\cos(qD/2),\qquad q=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\sin\theta.
$$
Therefore <double-slit interference> gives
$$
\boxed{I(\theta)\propto\cos^2\!\left(\frac{\pi D\sin\theta}{\lambda}\right)}.
$$
The bright <interference fringes> satisfy $D\sin\theta=m\lambda$, with a central maximum. In the small-angle region,
$$
\boxed{\Delta\theta\simeq\lambda/D=4.00\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{rad}=4.00\,\mathrm{mrad}}.
$$
Dark fringes lie halfway between bright ones. Exactly, spacing is uniform in $\sin\theta$, not in $\theta$ at large angles. The first panel of the pattern figure below shows the narrow-slit result.

= Solution

A relative <phase> $\pi$ changes the sign of one slit field. Their sum becomes
$$
\psi(\theta)\propto e^{iqD/2}-e^{-iqD/2}=2i\sin(qD/2),\qquad
\boxed{I(\theta)\propto\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\pi D\sin\theta}{\lambda}\right)}.
$$
The central <interference fringe> is now dark. The maxima move to $D\sin\theta=(m+1/2)\lambda$, while the minima occur at $D\sin\theta=m\lambda$. Thus the pattern shifts by half a fringe, $2.00\,\mathrm{mrad}$ near the axis, but **its angular fringe separation remains** $\boxed{4.00\,\mathrm{mrad}}$. This assumes the glass changes <phase> uniformly and introduces negligible absorption or beam deflection.

= Solution

For a rectangular slit centred at $x_c$, the one-dimensional <Fraunhofer diffraction> integral is
$$
\int_{x_c-a/2}^{x_c+a/2}e^{-iqx}\,dx
=a\operatorname{sinc}(qa/2)e^{-iqx_c},\qquad \operatorname{sinc}z=\frac{\sin z}{z}.
$$
The two equal <apertures> have centres at $\pm D/2$; the glass subtracts their fields. Therefore
$$
\psi\propto a\operatorname{sinc}(qa/2)\left(e^{iqD/2}-e^{-iqD/2}\right),\qquad
\boxed{I\propto\sin^2(qD/2)\operatorname{sinc}^2(qa/2)}.
$$
This is <phase-reversed double-slit diffraction>: the narrow-slit <interference> factor is multiplied by the <single-slit diffraction> envelope. In the limit $a\to0$, the normalized envelope tends to one, recovering part (d).

The <interference> minima and envelope minima obey, respectively,
$$
\boxed{\sin\theta_m=m\lambda/D=0.004m},\quad m\in\mathbb Z;\qquad
\boxed{\sin\theta_n=n\lambda/a=0.020n},\quad n\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}.
$$
Near the axis these are $\theta_m\simeq4m\,\mathrm{mrad}$ and envelope zeros $\theta_n\simeq20n\,\mathrm{mrad}$. Here $D/a=5$, so every envelope zero already coincides with an <interference> minimum. The envelope strongly reduces the outer fringes; its central value is nonzero, but the total intensity still vanishes at $\theta=0$ because the two fields cancel. The sketch uses the small-angle coordinate; the equations above specify the exact angular zeros.

In <division of amplitude>, one incident beam is split into two or more parts at a partially reflecting interface; they follow distinct paths and are recombined. The <Michelson interferometer> and <thin-film interference> use this principle. In <division of wavefront>, spatially distinct parts of the same <wavefront> are selected and allowed to overlap, as in <double-slit interference>. Both require adequate <optical coherence>; <wavefront> division particularly exposes the need for <spatial optical coherence> between the selected regions.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The beam splitter sends transmitted and reflected portions into perpendicular arms. Each mirror returns its beam to the splitter, where the two returning fields recombine towards the detector. A collimating <lens (optics)> provides approximately plane illumination. A compensating plate of the same glass thickness as the beam splitter can equalise the dispersive glass paths, which matters especially for broadband light.

\Image[paper-2-b8-michelson-layout.png]
{title=Michelson interferometer with a movable mirror, detector, beam splitter and compensating plate}

If the arm lengths are $\ell_1,\ell_2$, the geometric <optical path difference> in air is $x=2(\ell_1-\ell_2)$, up to a chosen constant and any additional material paths. Moving one mirror by $d$ changes $x$ by $2d$. Fixed reflection <phases> can be absorbed into the reference position of a bright or dark fringe.

= Solution

Take the two interfering <complex amplitudes> to have equal intensities $I_0/2$ and relative <phase> $kx$. The <interference> cross term then gives
$$
I=\frac{I_0}{2}+\frac{I_0}{2}+2\sqrt{\frac{I_0}{2}\frac{I_0}{2}}\cos(kx)
=\boxed{I_0[1+\operatorname{Re}(e^{ikx})]}.
$$
Thus the mean intensity is $I_0$, the maximum is $2I_0$, and the minima are
$$
\boxed{x=(m+\tfrac12)\lambda},\qquad m\in\mathbb Z.
$$
The <optical path difference> period is $\lambda$ and the mirror-displacement period is $\lambda/2$. The assumed equal intensities refer to the two fields at the chosen output, with matching <polarization of waves> and full <optical coherence>. They set the normalization; they should not additionally be interpreted as all incident light entering that output port. A physical lossless <Michelson interferometer> has a complementary second output.

= Solution
{parent=d}

The two <sodium> lines are distinct optical <frequencies>, so their rapidly oscillating mutual cross terms average to zero at the detector. Assuming equal line strengths and negligible individual linewidths, add their separate <interferograms>. Write $\bar k=(k_1+k_2)/2$ and $\Delta k=|k_1-k_2|$. With $I_0$ the total mean output intensity,
$$
I(x)=I_0\left[1+\frac12(\cos k_1x+\cos k_2x)\right]
=I_0[1+\cos(\bar kx)\cos(\Delta kx/2)].
$$
For mirror displacement $d=x/2$ this becomes
$$
\boxed{I(d)=I_0[1+\cos(2\bar kd)\cos(\Delta kd)]},\qquad
\mathcal V(d)=|\cos(\Delta kd)|.
$$
This is <spectral doublet from Michelson visibility revivals>. Since $\Delta\lambda\ll\bar\lambda$, $\Delta k\simeq2\pi\Delta\lambda/\bar\lambda^2$. Numerically,
$$
\boxed{\Delta d_{\rm fringe}\simeq\bar\lambda/2=294.65\,\mathrm{nm}},\qquad
\boxed{\Delta d_{\rm revival}=\pi/\Delta k\simeq0.2894\,\mathrm{mm}}.
$$
The first <interference visibility> zero occurs at $\boxed{d=0.1447\,\mathrm{mm}}$, with further zeros at odd multiples of that distance. Visibility is restored at integer multiples of $0.2894\,\mathrm{mm}$. The *signed* envelope has period $0.5788\,\mathrm{mm}$: alternate high-visibility packets have opposite fringe <phase>. Unequal line strengths leave a nonzero minimum visibility, and finite linewidths introduce a further decaying envelope; the wavelength data alone specify neither effect.

= Solution
{parent=e}

For mutually incoherent spectral components, the detected intensities add. If $S(\nu)$ describes the mean output spectral intensity, the <interferogram> is
$$
I(x)=\int_0^\infty S(\nu)\,d\nu+\int_0^\infty S(\nu)\cos(2\pi\nu x/c)\,d\nu.
$$
Subtract the constant background, scan the mirror with a calibrated displacement, and take the <Fourier transform> with respect to delay $\tau=x/c=2d/c$. This is <Fourier transform spectroscopy>. The transform recovers the spectrum subject to detector response, finite scan length and any window function. Sampling the displacement finely enough to resolve the shortest optical fringes avoids aliasing.

Using the usual reciprocal-maximum-delay resolution estimate, $\delta\nu\simeq c/x_{\max}$, and $|\delta\nu|\simeq c\delta\lambda/\lambda^2$, gives
$$
x_{\max}\simeq\frac{\lambda^2}{\delta\lambda}=2.50\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm m,
\qquad
\boxed{d_{\max}=x_{\max}/2\simeq1.25\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm m=12.5\,\mathrm{mm}}.
$$
The mirror displacement is measured from zero <optical path difference>. This <delay-range resolution in Fourier transform spectroscopy> is a conventional estimate: a specified window and resolution definition introduce a numerical factor. For example, a symmetric rectangular delay window has its first instrumental zero at $\delta\nu=1/(2\tau_{\max})$, which would give $6.25\,\mathrm{mm}$ under that particular criterion. The paper does not specify a line-profile criterion; the standard $12.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ estimate states its convention explicitly.

= Solution

The symbol $\rho$ here is <linear mass density>, with units $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}}$. A short element $[x,x+\delta x]$ has transverse <force>
$$
F_y=T\sin\theta(x+\delta x)-T\sin\theta(x)
\simeq T[\Psi_x(x+\delta x,t)-\Psi_x(x,t)],
$$
because small slopes give $\sin\theta\simeq\tan\theta=\Psi_x$. Its <mass> is $\rho\delta x$. Applying <Newton's second law>, dividing by $\delta x$, and taking the limit gives the <wave equation on a string>,
$$
\rho\Psi_{tt}=T\Psi_{xx},\qquad
\boxed{\Psi_{tt}=v^2\Psi_{xx},\quad v=\sqrt{T/\rho}}.
$$

A characteristic <wave impedance> relates an effort-like quantity to its conjugate flow-like quantity in a single travelling wave: for a mechanical wave these are <force> and <velocity>, and their product is <power>. The sign depends on the propagation and <force> conventions. Use the transverse <force> transmitted towards increasing $x$, $F=-T\Psi_x$. $$
This is the positive <characteristic impedance of a stretched string>. A leftgoing component has $F/u=-Z$ with the same <force> orientation. Its units differ from pressure-based <acoustic impedance> because it uses <force>, rather than <force> per area.

Use the quantum <free electron model>: conduction <electrons> move as independent particles in a three-dimensional box of <volume> $V$, with a uniform positive background replacing the ionic lattice potential. Neglect the detailed periodic potential and explicit electron-electron interactions; the interior one-electron <dispersion relation> is $\epsilon=\hbar^2k^2/(2m_e)$. The boundary confines the particles, and its detailed shape does not affect the bulk state count in a sufficiently large sample.

The <electrons> are spin-$1/2$ <fermions>: each spatial <quantum state> accommodates two opposite spin states, with occupations given by the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> and the <Pauli exclusion principle>. A fixed number $N$ of conduction <electrons> and thermal <equilibrium> are assumed. Collisions can be represented by a phenomenological <momentum relaxation time> for transport, but are unnecessary for the equilibrium state counting here. These assumptions distinguish the quantum model from a classical Maxwell-Boltzmann <electron> gas.

Impose periodic <boundary conditions> on a large cubic box. The allowed <wavevectors> occupy a lattice of cell volume $(2\pi)^3/V$ in <wavevector> space. Including the two spin states, the number of states with magnitude at most $k$ is
$$
\mathcal N(k)=2\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{4\pi k^3}{3}=\frac{Vk^3}{3\pi^2}.

Using $k=\sqrt{2m_e\epsilon}/\hbar$ and differentiating gives the total, spin-summed <three-dimensional free-electron density of states>,
$$
\boxed{g(\epsilon)=\frac{V}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m_e}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt\epsilon},\qquad \epsilon\geq0.
$$
It counts states per unit <energy>; divide by $V$ for states per unit <volume> per unit <energy>. At zero <temperature>, the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> fills all states up to the <Fermi energy>, giving
$$
N=\int_0^{E_F}g(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon
=\frac{V}{3\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m_eE_F}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}.
$$
Writing $n=N/V$ gives
$$
\boxed{k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}},\qquad
\boxed{E_F=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}(3\pi^2n)^{2/3}},\qquad
\boxed{g(E_F)=\frac{3N}{2E_F}}.
$$
The <energy> zero is the bottom of the free-electron band. The <electron Fermi temperature> is $T_F=E_F/k_B$.

A <Bloch state> in a crystal experiences a periodic lattice potential. Its band <dispersion relation> $\epsilon(k)$ encodes the lattice response, so an <effective mass> allows a wavepacket's acceleration under a weak applied <force> to be described without explicitly following the microscopic periodic <forces>.

In one dimension the wavepacket <group velocity> and semiclassical equation for <crystal momentum> are
$$
v_g=\frac1\hbar\frac{d\epsilon}{dk},\qquad \hbar\dot k=F.
$$
Differentiating the first equation with respect to time gives
$$
a=\frac1\hbar\frac{d^2\epsilon}{dk^2}\dot k
=\frac{F}{\hbar^2}\frac{d^2\epsilon}{dk^2}.
$$
Defining $F=m^*a$ yields
$$
\boxed{m^*=\hbar^2\left(\frac{d^2\epsilon}{dk^2}\right)^{-1}}.
$$
This <band effective mass> is controlled by <band curvature>, not the slope. A band minimum has positive $m^*$; a band maximum has negative <electron> $m^*$ and is often more conveniently described with positive-mass <electron holes>. In three dimensions the inverse <effective mass> is generally a tensor, $(m^{*-1})_{ij}=\hbar^{-2}\partial_{k_i}\partial_{k_j}\epsilon$. The scalar formula assumes motion along a specified direction in an isolated band, weak fields and negligible interband transitions; the bare <electron> <mass> has not physically changed.

= Solution

**Doping changes the mobile carrier population by introducing controlled impurity levels.** In an <intrinsic semiconductor>, the filled <valence band> is separated from the nearly empty <conduction band> by a <band gap> $E_g$. Thermal excitation produces an electron-hole pair, so the <electron> and hole <number densities> obey $n=p=n_i$. A <electron hole> represents a missing valence-band <electron> and behaves as a mobile positive <charge carrier>. For nondegenerate bands in thermal <equilibrium>,
$$
n=N_Ce^{-(E_C-E_F)/(k_BT)},\qquad
p=N_Ve^{-(E_F-E_V)/(k_BT)},\qquad
np=N_CN_Ve^{-E_g/(k_BT)}=n_i^2.
$$
Here $N_C,N_V$ are the <effective density of states>, $E_C,E_V$ the band edges, and $E_F$ denotes the <chemical potential>, conventionally called the <Fermi level>. The <semiconductor mass-action law> holds in this nondegenerate equilibrium regime.

In a tetrahedrally bonded elemental <semiconductor> such as <silicon>, a substitutional group-V atom has an extra outer <electron> relative to the host. It is a <donor impurity>: a shallow level just below $E_C$ can ionise, releasing an <electron> to the <conduction band> and leaving a fixed positively charged donor. The resulting <n-type semiconductor> has <electrons> as majority carriers and holes as <minority carriers>. Conversely a group-III substitution supplies one fewer bonding <electron>. An <acceptor impurity> can take an <electron> from the <valence band>, leaving a mobile <electron hole> and a fixed negative acceptor; the resulting <p-type semiconductor> has holes as majority carriers.

The impurity state is shallow because the <electron> or hole responds to a screened Coulomb potential and an <effective mass>, rather than the bare atomic potential. A simple hydrogenic donor estimate is
$$
E_C-E_D\simeq\frac{m^*}{m_e}\frac{13.6\,\mathrm{eV}}{\epsilon_r^2},
$$
where $\epsilon_r$ is the relative <permittivity>. This is a useful scaling model, with real band anisotropy and central-cell effects omitted. Dopants are fixed in the lattice; their ionisation changes mobile carrier numbers without making a uniformly doped bulk sample carry a large net <electric charge>.

The <charge neutrality> condition is
$$
n+N_A^-=p+N_D^+.
$$
When shallow dopants are almost fully ionised and $N_D-N_A\gg n_i$, an n-type region has $n\simeq N_D-N_A$ and $p\simeq n_i^2/n$. A p-type region similarly has $p\simeq N_A-N_D$ and $n\simeq n_i^2/p$. Thus compensation by the opposite impurity type reduces the majority-carrier density; the total dopant concentration is not generally the mobile <carrier density>. At sufficiently high impurity density the <degenerate semiconductor> regime requires the full <Fermi-Dirac distribution> and these exponential approximations fail.

There are three useful <temperature> regimes. At low <temperature>, <dopant freeze-out> leaves many shallow levels un-ionised. In the extrinsic regime, the dopants are largely ionised and set the majority-carrier density. At high enough <temperature>, intrinsic pair creation dominates and $n,p$ approach $n_i$. The <Fermi level> moves towards the <conduction band> with donor doping and towards the <valence band> with acceptor doping. The <electrical conductivity> is
$$
\boxed{\sigma=e(n\mu_e+p\mu_h)}.
$$
Doping therefore controls conductivity and its carrier type, but ionised impurities also scatter carriers and can reduce <charge carrier mobility>. <Carrier density> and mobility must both be considered.

**A p-n junction forms when p-type and n-type regions are joined within a semiconductor.** Initially the concentration gradients drive <diffusion current>: <electrons> diffuse from the n region to the p region and holes in the opposite direction. Recombination removes mobile majority carriers near the join, exposing fixed positive donors on the n side and fixed negative acceptors on the p side. This forms a <depletion region> and an <electric field> directed from n to p. Its <drift current> opposes diffusion. At equilibrium the net current vanishes and the <Fermi level> is uniform, although the band edges vary spatially through <band bending>.

For an abrupt nondegenerate junction with fully ionised dopants, its <built-in potential> is
$$
\boxed{V_{\rm bi}=\frac{k_BT}{e}\ln\frac{N_AN_D}{n_i^2}}.
$$
Under the <depletion approximation>, the space-charge densities are $-eN_A$ on a p-side width $w_p$ and $+eN_D$ on an n-side width $w_n$. Integrating <Poisson equation> and imposing zero field outside gives $N_Aw_p=N_Dw_n$ and
$$
W=w_p+w_n=\sqrt{\frac{2\epsilon_sV_{\rm bi}}e\left(\frac1{N_A}+\frac1{N_D}\right)}.
$$
Most of the depletion width therefore extends into the more lightly doped side. The schematic shows the impurity levels before contact and the resulting space charge and potential after contact; there is no externally extractable equilibrium battery voltage from the built-in field alone.

\Image[paper-2-d12-doping-junction.png]
{title=Shallow donor and acceptor levels, and charge, field and potential in an equilibrium abrupt p-n junction}

A forward bias, with p positive relative to n, reduces the barrier from $V_{\rm bi}$ towards $V_{\rm bi}-V$ and injects <minority carriers> across the junction. In the ideal low-injection, diffusion-controlled regime this produces the <Shockley diode equation>, $I=I_s[e^{eV/(k_BT)}-1]$. Reverse bias increases the barrier and the <depletion region>; only a small thermally generated minority-carrier current flows before breakdown. Reverse-biased depletion regions also give a voltage-dependent <junction capacitance>. Real devices have recombination, leakage, series resistance and breakdown mechanisms beyond the ideal law. **Controlled doping creates majority and minority carriers, and their junction produces a depletion barrier that makes rectification possible.**

The <convolution> of two <aperture> functions in one dimension is
$$
(f*g)(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(u)g(x-u)\,du.
$$
With <Fourier transform> convention $\widehat f(q)=\int f(x)e^{-iqx}dx$, the <convolution theorem> states
$$
\boxed{\widehat{f*g}(q)=\widehat f(q)\widehat g(q)},\qquad
\boxed{\widehat{fg}(q)=\frac1{2\pi}(\widehat f*\widehat g)(q)}.
$$
The first identity follows by substituting the convolution integral, putting $v=x-u$, and separating the resulting double integral into $\int f(u)e^{-iqu}du$ times $\int g(v)e^{-iqv}dv$. For an absolutely integrable pair the interchange is justified by absolute convergence; finite optical <apertures> satisfy suitable integrability assumptions.

In <Fraunhofer diffraction>, the observed field is the <Fourier transform> of the *transmitted complex <aperture> field*. For two identical slits, let $g(x)$ be one slit and use a pair of <Dirac delta> distributions to locate the centres:
$$
t(x)=g*[\delta(x-D/2)+\delta(x+D/2)].
$$
Consequently $\widehat t(q)=2\widehat g(q)\cos(qD/2)$. For a rectangular slit, $\widehat g=a\operatorname{sinc}(qa/2)$, giving a <single-slit diffraction> envelope times the <double-slit interference> factor in intensity. A $\pi$ <phase> reversal replaces the plus between the deltas by a minus, giving $\sin(qD/2)$ instead, as in B7.

More generally, an $N$-slit array is a single-aperture function convolved with a finite comb. Its field contains the factor
$$
\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{-iqjD}=e^{-iq(N-1)D/2}\frac{\sin(NqD/2)}{\sin(qD/2)}.
$$
This separates the narrow <diffraction grating> maxima from the individual-aperture envelope. The same reasoning holds in two transverse dimensions. Multiplying an existing <aperture> by an additional finite mask instead convolves their field transforms and can broaden features. These are operations on complex fields: intensity is the squared modulus of the final field and cannot generally be substituted into the field-level <convolution theorem>.

In <thin-film interference>, coherent beams reflected at the two faces of a transparent film overlap. Let the film have <refractive index> $n_f$, thickness $t$, and internal ray angle $\theta_f$, determined by <Snell's law>. For vacuum <wavelength> $\lambda_0$, the propagation part of the relative <phase> is
$$
\boxed{\delta_{\rm prop}=\frac{4\pi n_ft\cos\theta_f}{\lambda_0}}.
$$
Although the internal round-trip ray length is $2t/\cos\theta_f$, the two emergent rays must be compared on the same outgoing <wavefront>. Subtracting their external lateral path contribution gives the effective <optical path difference> $2n_ft\cos\theta_f$, explaining the cosine rather than its reciprocal.

Reflection also changes <phase>. At normal incidence in lossless positive-index media, the electric-field <Fresnel equations> give $r_{ij}=(n_i-n_j)/(n_i+n_j)$: reflection towards higher index reverses sign, whereas reflection towards lower index does not. If the two interfering reflections differ by one such <phase> reversal, their total relative <phase> is $\delta_{\rm prop}+\pi$ modulo $2\pi$. Then reflected maxima and minima obey
$$
\boxed{2n_ft\cos\theta_f=(m+\tfrac12)\lambda_0\quad\text{maxima}},\qquad
\boxed{2n_ft\cos\theta_f=m\lambda_0\quad\text{minima}}.
$$
If there are zero or two reversals, the conditions interchange. These bright/dark conditions assume overlapping beams with adequate <temporal optical coherence>; complete cancellation further requires equal amplitudes. In transmitted light, a lossless complete film has complementary intensity variations because reflected plus transmitted <power> is conserved.

For multiple internal reflections at normal incidence, summing the geometric series gives the exact film reflection amplitude
$$
r_{\rm film}=\frac{r_{01}+r_{12}e^{2i\beta}}{1+r_{01}r_{12}e^{2i\beta}},\qquad
\beta=\frac{2\pi n_ft}{\lambda_0},\qquad \mathcal R=|r_{\rm film}|^2.
$$
This includes both the amplitude mismatch and repeated-beam interference. A normal-incidence antireflection coating on substrate index $n_s>n_0$ can cancel reflection at one wavelength when $n_f=\sqrt{n_0n_s}$ and $t=\lambda_0/(4n_f)$: both interface reflections reverse sign and the film's round-trip propagation adds $\pi$, with equal interface amplitudes. A varying thickness or angle selects different maxima for different wavelengths, producing the colours of transparent films in white light. Broad bandwidth and large incidence angles generally prevent perfect cancellation across all wavelengths.

= Solution
{parent=c}

A <dispersion relation> specifies the allowed <angular frequency> $\omega(k)$ of a <wavevector> $k$. A single harmonic wave $A\cos(kx-\omega t)$ has constant-phase surfaces moving at the <phase velocity>,
$$
\boxed{v_p=\omega/k}.
$$
For two nearby components, use the identity
$$
\cos(k_1x-\omega_1t)+\cos(k_2x-\omega_2t)
=2\cos\!\left(\frac{\Delta k\,x-\Delta\omega\,t}{2}\right)
\cos(\bar kx-\bar\omega t).
$$
The slowly varying envelope moves at $\Delta\omega/\Delta k$. Taking a narrowband limit gives the <group velocity>,
$$
\boxed{v_g=\frac{d\omega}{dk}=v_p+k\frac{dv_p}{dk}}.
$$
More generally, expand $\omega(k)$ around the central <wavevector> of a wavepacket. The linear term translates its envelope at $v_g$; the quadratic and higher terms produce <group velocity dispersion> and packet distortion. This interpretation needs a sufficiently narrow spectrum and weak enough absorption and distortion for an identifiable envelope to persist.

For light in a transparent material, $k=n(\omega)\omega/c$, so
$$
\boxed{v_p=\frac cn},\qquad
\boxed{v_g=\frac{c}{n+\omega\,dn/d\omega}
=\frac{c}{n-\lambda_0\,dn/d\lambda_0}}.
$$
The derivative is evaluated at the wavepacket's central <frequency> and $\lambda_0$ is the vacuum <wavelength>. In a nondispersive medium with constant $n$, the two velocities coincide and a packet can propagate without dispersion. In normal optical dispersion $dn/d\lambda_0<0$, the displayed model gives $v_g<v_p$.

The two velocities can differ even without material dispersion: a waveguide mode with $\omega^2=\omega_c^2+c^2k^2$ has $v_p=\omega/k>c$ and $v_g=c^2k/\omega<c$, with $v_pv_g=c^2$. The <phase velocity> is not a signal speed. Near strongly absorbing resonances an anomalous derivative can also make the <group velocity> exceed $c$ or become negative; reshaping then prevents its simple identification with information transport. Relativistic causality constrains the signal front, not the speed of an arbitrary moving <phase> or distorted intensity maximum.
