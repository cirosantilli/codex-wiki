For a <normalizable> state take $a>0$. <inverse construction of a quantum potential> gives $V=E+\hbar^2\Psi''/(2m\Psi)$. <differentiation> yields $\Psi''/\Psi=16a^2x^6-12ax^2$, so the chosen <potential energy> zero fixes $E=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{V(x)=\frac{8\hbar^2a^2}{m}x^6-\frac{6\hbar^2a}{m}x^2,\qquad E=0.}
$$
This <energy> lies above the negative minima of the <potential energy>; its zero value does not mean zero <kinetic energy>. The <nodeless eigenfunction> is the <ground state>.

The <Sturm oscillation theorem> gives **three <nodes of a wavefunction>** for the fourth <bound state>. <reflection symmetry> and <nondegeneracy of one-dimensional bound states> make it **odd**: one <node of a wavefunction> is at the centre, and the other two lie at opposite positions. In each flat allowed region the <wavefunction> is a <sinusoid>, with $k=\sqrt{2m(E-V)}/\hbar$; its <wavelength> is shortest in the central, deepest region. Outside the well it decays exponentially, with opposite signs on the two sides. Both $\Psi$ and $\Psi'$ remain continuous at every finite step, as required by <wavefunction matching at a finite potential step>; only the curvature jumps. In particular, the <wavefunction> need not vanish at a step.

The <canonical commutation relation> is $[p_x,x]=-i\hbar I$. Applying the <commutator product rule> on a common <operator domain>,
$$
[p_x^2,x]=p_x[p_x,x]+[p_x,x]p_x
=\boxed{-2i\hbar p_x}.
$$
Equivalently, applying both sides to a twice differentiable <test function> gives $-\hbar^2[(xf)''-xf'']=-2\hbar^2f'$, the same result.

The central estimate is $z=3.2743$. <propagation of uncertainty> must include the occurrence of $x$ in both numerator and denominator. With angular <derivatives> measured per <radian>,
$$
z_x=\frac{\sec^2(x+y)}{\tan x}-\frac{\tan(x+y)}{\sin^2x}=-3.5382,\qquad
z_y=\frac{\sec^2(x+y)}{\tan x}=6.6497.
$$
Interpreting the two quoted errors as independent <standard deviations>, convert them to <radians> before calculating
$$
\sigma_z^2=z_x^2(4\pi/180)^2+z_y^2(3\pi/180)^2,
\qquad \boxed{z\simeq3.27\pm0.43}.
$$
If the quoted errors are instead absolute bounds, the first-order worst-case estimate is $|\delta z|\lesssim |z_x|\delta x+|z_y|\delta y=0.60$. Correlated errors add $2z_xz_y\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)$ to the <variance>. Treating $x+y$ and $x$ as independent would lose their shared <measurement uncertainty>.

In the <high-resolution quantization approximation>, a smooth input distribution varies little across one conversion interval, so <rounding> to the nearest level gives an approximately <uniform quantization error> $\epsilon\in[-\Delta V/2,\Delta V/2]$. Therefore
$$
\langle\epsilon\rangle=0,\qquad
\langle\epsilon^2\rangle=\frac1{\Delta V}\int_{-\Delta V/2}^{\Delta V/2}\epsilon^2\,d\epsilon
=\frac{\Delta V^2}{12},\qquad
\boxed{\epsilon_{\rm rms}=\frac{\Delta V}{\sqrt{12}}}.
$$
The input must stay within the <analogue-to-digital converter>'s range. A large <standard deviation> alone does not guarantee <uniform quantization error>: an input concentrated exactly on widely separated conversion levels has zero error. Truncation without a half-step correction also has a different mean and <root mean square>.

= Solution

For each mode in <Cartesian coordinates>, $[a_i,a_j^\dagger]=\delta_{ij}$ and $a_i^\dagger a_i=H_i/(\hbar\omega)-1/2$. Consequently the <two-dimensional isotropic harmonic oscillator> has
$$
\boxed{H=\hbar\omega(N_x+N_y+1)},\qquad N_i=a_i^\dagger a_i,\qquad E_{kl}=\hbar\omega(k+l+1).
$$
The normalized actions of the <creation and annihilation operators> are
$$
a_x|k,l\rangle=\sqrt{k}\,|k-1,l\rangle,\qquad
a_y^\dagger|k,l\rangle=\sqrt{l+1}\,|k,l+1\rangle,\qquad
a_x|0,l\rangle=0.
$$
Invert their definitions as $x=\sqrt{\hbar/(2m\omega)}(a_x+a_x^\dagger)$ and $p_x=i\sqrt{m\hbar\omega/2}(a_x^\dagger-a_x)$, and similarly for $y$. <linear operators> belonging to different coordinates commute, so substitution into $L_z=xp_y-yp_x$ gives
$$
L_z=\frac{i\hbar}{2}\big[(a_x+a_x^\dagger)(a_y^\dagger-a_y)-(a_y+a_y^\dagger)(a_x^\dagger-a_x)\big]
=\boxed{-i\hbar(a_x^\dagger a_y-a_y^\dagger a_x)}.
$$
Each term transfers one excitation between the two modes: $[N_x+N_y,a_x^\dagger a_y]=(1-1)a_x^\dagger a_y=0$, and likewise for its adjoint. Thus $[H,L_z]=0$ and, with no explicit time dependence, $dL_z/dt=(i/\hbar)[H,L_z]=0$. This is <conservation of angular momentum> under <planar rotations>.

Finally $L_z|1,0\rangle=i\hbar|0,1\rangle$ and $L_z|0,1\rangle=-i\hbar|1,0\rangle$. Hence
$$
\boxed{L_z\big(|1,0\rangle+i|0,1\rangle\big)
=\hbar\big(|1,0\rangle+i|0,1\rangle\big)}.
$$
The <angular momentum> is $+\hbar$; division by $\sqrt2$ performs <wavefunction normalization> on this state without changing its <eigenvalue>. It is one excitation of the positive circular mode.

= Solution

A pure <stationary state> of a time-independent <Hamiltonian operator> is $\Psi(\mathbf r,t)=\psi(\mathbf r)e^{-iEt/\hbar}$. Its <probability density> and all expectations of time-independent <observables> are constant. This does not require zero <probability current>.

For real scalar <potential energy> and no <vector potential>, substitute $i\hbar\dot\Psi=-(\hbar^2/2m)\nabla^2\Psi+V\Psi$ and its <complex conjugate> into $\dot\rho=\Psi^*\dot\Psi+\dot\Psi^*\Psi$. The potential terms cancel:
$$
\dot\rho=\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\big(\Psi^*\nabla^2\Psi-\Psi\nabla^2\Psi^*\big)
=-\nabla\cdot\left[\frac{\hbar}{2im}(\Psi^*\nabla\Psi-\Psi\nabla\Psi^*)\right].
$$
Thus the <probability current> is $\mathbf j=(\hbar/m)\operatorname{Im}(\Psi^*\nabla\Psi)$. In a <stationary state> $\nabla\cdot\mathbf j=0$: flow may circulate without changing the <density>.

Here only the azimuthal <phase> contributes an imaginary logarithmic <derivative>. Since $\nabla\phi=\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}/(r\sin\theta)$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf j=\frac{\hbar|\Psi|^2}{mr\sin\theta}\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}
=\frac{\hbar A^2}{m}r e^{-r/a_0}\sin\theta\,\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}}.
$$
There is no radial or polar flow; the azimuthal current is independent of $\phi$, so its <divergence> vanishes. The apparent coordinate singularity at $\sin\theta=0$ cancels in the final expression.

The z-component of <orbital angular momentum> follows from the moment of the <mass> current:
$$
\langle L_z\rangle
=\int mr\sin\theta\,j_\phi\,dV
=\hbar A^2
\left(\int_0^\infty r^4e^{-r/a_0}dr\right)
\left(\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,d\theta\right)
\left(\int_0^{2\pi}d\phi\right)
=\boxed{\hbar}.
$$
Indeed $A^2(24a_0^5)(4/3)(2\pi)=1$. This agrees with $L_z=-i\hbar\partial_\phi$ acting on the <wavefunction>.

Write $\gamma=ge/(2m)$, retaining the printed <magnetic moment> convention. The displayed two-dimensional <matrices> are <Pauli matrices>, and the <spin> <Hamiltonian operator> is
$$
\boxed{H=-\frac{\gamma\hbar}{2}
\begin{pmatrix}B_z&B_x-iB_y\\B_x+iB_y&-B_z\end{pmatrix}}.

For an x-directed field let $\omega=\gamma B_x/2$. The <Schrödinger equation> gives $i\dot c_+=-\omega c_-$ and $i\dot c_-=-\omega c_+$. With $c_+(0)=1,c_-(0)=0$, these equations yield
$$
\boxed{|\Psi(t)\rangle=\cos(\omega t)|+z\rangle+i\sin(\omega t)|-z\rangle,\qquad
\omega=\frac{geB_x}{4m}}.
$$
Equivalently, $U(t)=e^{i\omega t\sigma_x}=I\cos\omega t+i\sigma_x\sin\omega t$. If $e$ denotes a positive <elementary charge> magnitude, a free <electron> is conventionally assigned a minus sign in its <magnetic moment>; that alternative reverses the rotation sense. The expressions here consistently use the moment specified in the paper.

Put $\kappa=e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$. Substitution of $\psi=R(r)\Phi(\phi)$ into the <time-independent Schrödinger equation>, followed by division by $R\Phi$ and multiplication by $r^2$, separates the angular dependence:
$$
\frac{r^2R''+rR'}R+\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}(Er^2+\kappa r)
=-\frac{\Phi''}{\Phi}=m^2.
$$
Thus $\Phi=e^{im\phi}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ and
$$
rR''+R'+\left[\frac{2\mu}{\hbar^2}(Er+\kappa)-\frac{m^2}{r}\right]R=0.
$$
The <single-valued wavefunction> condition, $\Phi(\phi+2\pi)=\Phi(\phi)$, requires **$m\in\mathbb Z$**. The regular <boundary condition> at the origin selects $R\sim r^{|m|}$. Accordingly use $s=|m|$ in the radial ansatz; the printed $x^m$ notation describes the $m\geq0$ representatives.

For the <two-dimensional Coulomb bound state>, substitution of $R=x^s e^{-x/2}G$ with the stipulated radial and <energy> scales gives
$$
xG''+(2s+1-x)G'+(N-s-\tfrac12)G=0.
$$
The coefficient of $x^n$ in the <power series> is
$$
(n+1)(n+2s+1)a_{n+1}
+(N-s-\tfrac12-n)a_n=0,
\qquad
\boxed{a_{n+1}=\frac{n+s+\tfrac12-N}{(n+1)(n+2s+1)}a_n}.
$$
The regular solution otherwise has an exponentially growing contribution at large $x$, incompatible with being <normalizable>. Termination at degree $n_r\geq0$ therefore requires
$$
\boxed{N=n_r+|m|+\tfrac12,\quad
E_{n_rm}=-\frac{\mu\kappa^2}{2\hbar^2(n_r+|m|+\tfrac12)^2}}.
$$
The resulting $G$ is proportional to a <Generalized Laguerre polynomial>.

Define $R_{\rm y}=\mu\kappa^2/(2\hbar^2)$, the hydrogenic Rydberg <energy> for the stipulated <mass>. The first three distinct two-dimensional <energies> are **$-4R_{\rm y},-4R_{\rm y}/9,-4R_{\rm y}/25$**, whereas in three dimensions they are **$-R_{\rm y},-R_{\rm y}/4,-R_{\rm y}/9$**. Both series approach zero from below, but the planar <ground state> is four times more tightly bound. Without <spin> the respective <quantum degeneracies> are $1,3,5$ and $1,4,9$; if individual degenerate states are counted, the second and third planar states already share the first excited <energy>. This comparison retains the supplied $1/r$ interaction; it does not replace it by the logarithmic <electrostatic potential> of a genuinely two-dimensional electromagnetic theory.

= Solution
{parent=C10}

<identical particles> have the same intrinsic properties. Exchanging their bookkeeping labels cannot distinguish a physical preparation or change a measurable prediction. This is stronger than merely failing to track classical trajectories. For ordinary <bosons> and <fermions>, the many-particle <wavefunction> has respectively <bosonic exchange symmetry> or <fermionic exchange symmetry> under every <particle exchange operator>, acting on spatial and <spin> coordinates together. The <Spin-statistics theorem> connects integer <spin> to <bosons> and half-integer <spin> to <fermions>; this connection uses relativistic quantum-field assumptions, rather than following from the nonrelativistic <Schrödinger equation> alone.

For <one-particle states> $u,v$ satisfying <orthonormality>, the two alternatives form a <quantum superposition>:
$$
\Psi_\pm(1,2)=\frac{u(1)v(2)\pm v(1)u(2)}{\sqrt2}.
$$
Its <probability density> contains an exchange interference term, absent from an incoherent mixture of labelled alternatives. The antisymmetric expression vanishes when both <one-particle states> are identical: **one complete <one-particle state> can contain at most one <fermion>**. This <Pauli exclusion principle> explains <electron shell> filling, the electronic <Fermi sea> and <degeneracy pressure>. Opposite <spins> are different <one-particle states>, so two <electrons> may share an <atomic orbital>. Spatially separated <wave packets> with negligible overlap can often be treated as distinguishable for local measurements; the underlying exchange symmetry remains.

In <thermal equilibrium>, occupation of a level of <energy> $\epsilon$ obeys
$$
\bar n_{\rm B}=\frac1{e^{(\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT)}-1},\qquad
\bar n_{\rm F}=\frac1{e^{(\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT)}+1}.
$$
<bosons> can accumulate macroscopically in one state, giving <Bose-Einstein condensation>; <fermions> instead fill successive states up to the <Fermi energy> at zero <temperature>. When occupation is dilute, both expressions reduce to the <Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution>. The identical-particle classical <partition function> contains $1/N!$, avoiding spurious <entropy> from permutations and the <Gibbs paradox>. Exchange effects become conspicuous when the <thermal de Broglie wavelength> is comparable to the interparticle spacing.

An operational test is the <Hong-Ou-Mandel effect>. Down-converted <photon> pairs enter separate ports of a balanced <beam splitter>; indistinguishable alternatives for one <photon> per output cancel. Scanning the relative arrival delay restores coincidences away from overlap. Matching <polarization>, spectral filtering and spatial alignment controls distinguishability; measuring delayed accidental coincidences controls background. The observed dip tests two-photon interference, and its finite depth must not automatically be interpreted as a failure of bosonic symmetry. The original apparatus varied beam-splitter position and subtracted a separately measured accidental background.<Measurement of Subpicosecond Time Intervals between Two Photons by Interference by C. K. Hong and others>[pp. 2044–2046, Fig. 1, Eq. (2), coincidence-background procedure and Fig. 2]{ref}

= Solution

For <observables> $A,B$, nonzero $[A,B]$ obstructs a complete common <eigenbasis>. In a finite-dimensional system, commuting <Hermitian operators> can be simultaneously diagonalized; <quantum degeneracy> may require choosing an appropriate basis inside the degenerate <eigenspace>. Noncommuting <observables> can still share particular <eigenvectors>, so incompatibility is not a claim that every individual state must have nonzero spreads in both quantities.

The <Robertson uncertainty relation> follows by applying <Cauchy–Schwarz inequality> to $(A-\langle A\rangle)|\psi\rangle$ and $(B-\langle B\rangle)|\psi\rangle$:
$$
\boxed{\Delta A\,\Delta B\geq\frac12|\langle[A,B]\rangle|}.
$$
In particular $[x,p_x]=i\hbar$ gives $\Delta x\,\Delta p_x\geq\hbar/2$. These are preparation spreads, not necessarily <measurement errors>. A zero <commutator> expectation can make this bound zero even when the <linear operators> do not commute.

Measurement order can matter: preparing $|+z\rangle$, measuring $S_x$, then measuring $S_z$ gives both z outcomes with <probability> $1/2$ when the intermediate result is ignored. A direct z measurement would give $+\hbar/2$ certainly. Finally the <Heisenberg equation of motion> $dA/dt=(i/\hbar)[H,A]+\partial A/\partial t$ relates commutation to conservation: an <observable> with no explicit time dependence and $[H,A]=0$ is conserved. For <unbounded self-adjoint operators> these statements require the appropriate common domains; a formal commutator calculation alone need not justify simultaneous spectral measurements.

= Solution

In a <classically forbidden region>, the <Schrödinger equation> has evanescent solutions rather than forcing the <wavefunction> to vanish. Matching at both sides of a finite barrier permits nonzero transmission. For a broad barrier the <WKB approximation> gives
$$
T\simeq C\exp\left[-2\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\frac{\sqrt{2m(V(x)-E)}}{\hbar}\,dx\right],
$$
where $C$ is a matching prefactor. **Transmission is exponentially sensitive to barrier width and height.** A single decaying <exponential function> carries no <probability current>; the matched scattering solution includes the combination needed for transmitted current. <energy> is conserved throughout.

In <alpha decay>, a quasibound alpha particle penetrates the nuclear <Coulomb barrier>. Multiplying the penetration <probability> by an assault <frequency> estimates the decay rate; the strong dependence on alpha <energy> explains the wide range of lifetimes. In a <scanning tunnelling microscope>, a <voltage> drives <electron> <quantum tunnelling> between a conducting tip and sample. Approximately $I\propto V e^{-2\kappa d}$, so constant-current feedback resolves changes in gap width, with electronic <density of states> also affecting the image. Piezoelectric motion, vibration isolation and stable <voltage> are necessary; a bright feature is not automatically a taller atom. Atomic-scale imaging was demonstrated with a vacuum-tunnelling microscope in the original surface study.<Surface Studies by Scanning Tunneling Microscopy by Gerd Binnig and others>[Abstract and pp. 57–61: atomic-scale steps and surface reconstruction]{ref} In a <Josephson junction>, <Cooper pair> <quantum tunnelling> through a weak link gives $I=I_c\sin\varphi$, allowing supercurrent without an applied <voltage> and phase-sensitive interference. These are distinct applications of barrier penetration and coupling with <quantum coherence>, not particles borrowing <energy> to climb over a barrier.

Addition of two spin-one-half angular momenta gives total <spin> $S=0$ or $S=1$. The normalized <singlet state> and <triplet states> are
$$
|0,0\rangle=\frac{|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle-|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle}{\sqrt2},
\qquad
|1,1\rangle=|\uparrow\uparrow\rangle,\quad
|1,0\rangle=\frac{|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle+|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle}{\sqrt2},
\quad |1,-1\rangle=|\downarrow\downarrow\rangle.
$$
They have $S^2$ <eigenvalues> $0$ and $2\hbar^2$, and $S_z=M\hbar$. The <singlet state> is antisymmetric under a <particle exchange operator>, whereas all three triplets have symmetric spin-exchange parity. For two identical <electrons> the full state must be antisymmetric: a <singlet state> pairs with a spatial state with symmetric exchange parity, a <triplet state> with an antisymmetric spatial state. Thus **two <electrons> in the same <atomic orbital> must form a <singlet state>**.

The identity $\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2=(S^2-S_1^2-S_2^2)/2$ gives $-3\hbar^2/4$ in the <singlet state> and $+\hbar^2/4$ in the <triplet state>. Hence an <exchange interaction> distinguishes their <energies>, with ordering determined by its coupling sign. The <singlet state> has perfect opposite-spin correlations along any common measurement axis and is <entangled>. The $M=0$ <triplet state> is also <entangled>, while the $M=\pm1$ <triplet states> are <product states> along the chosen axis.

<Bayes' theorem> updates a parameter distribution using measured data:
$$
\boxed{p(\theta\mid D)=\frac{p(D\mid\theta)p(\theta)}{\int p(D\mid\theta')p(\theta')\,d\theta'}}.
$$
The <likelihood> describes the measurement process, the <prior distribution> encodes information before the present data, and the denominator normalizes the <posterior distribution>. Include instrumental <noise> and known selection effects in the <likelihood>. <nuisance parameters>, such as an uncertain background, should be jointly inferred and marginalized, rather than silently fixed.

For $n$ independent readings with a <Gaussian distribution> with known common <variance> $\sigma^2$ and <prior distribution> $\theta\sim N(\mu_0,\tau_0^2)$, completing the square gives
$$
\tau_n^{-2}=\tau_0^{-2}+n\sigma^{-2},\qquad
\mu_n=\tau_n^2\big(\mu_0\tau_0^{-2}+n\bar x\,\sigma^{-2}\big).
$$
The <posterior distribution> is $N(\mu_n,\tau_n^2)$: <prior distribution> and data contribute in proportion to precision. A broad <prior distribution> recovers the usual estimate $\bar x$ with <standard deviation> $\sigma/\sqrt n$. Report a <posterior distribution> estimate and <credible interval>, specify the <prior distribution>, and examine sensitivity when the data are weak. A <credible interval> is a <posterior distribution> <probability> statement about the parameter conditional on the model; it is not defined by the repeated-sampling coverage of a <confidence interval>.

= Solution

Uniform samples at $t_n=n/f_s$ cannot distinguish <frequencies> separated by integer multiples of $f_s$:
$$
e^{2\pi i(f+kf_s)t_n}=e^{2\pi ift_n},\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.
$$
For a real <cosine>, opposite <frequencies> also coincide. Thus an unknown high-frequency component can appear in the sampled record at its folded <frequency> $f_{\rm alias}\in[0,f_s/2]$. For example, **2 Hz and 8 Hz <cosines> give identical samples at 10 samples per second**. This ambiguity is <aliasing>, rather than <uniform quantization error>.

\Image[paper-2-aliasing.png]
{title=Two signals, identical samples}

The <Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem> permits reconstruction of an ideally <bandlimited function> of <bandwidth> $B$ when $f_s>2B$. Use an analogue <anti-aliasing filter> before conversion and allow a transition band between the desired passband and the <frequencies> that fold into it. Sampling faster relaxes the filter requirement. Digital filtering after undersampling cannot separate already identical sample sequences. Finite observation time, <clock jitter>, <noise> and imperfect filter rejection impose further practical limits.

Identify the physical coupling that causes a <systematic error> and change the apparatus so that it can be measured or cancelled. <measurement calibration> against known reference standards at several points reveals zero and gain errors; blanks and background runs measure additive contributions. Carry uncertainties of the standards and fitted corrections into the final result.

Reversal provides a particularly transparent test. If $D_+=S+b$ and $D_-=-S+b$, the combination $(D_+-D_-)/2$ isolates $S$ while $(D_++D_-)/2$ measures the <measurement zero offset>. Interleave and repeat the reversals so drift can be detected. The cancellation assumes a stable <measurement zero offset> and a genuinely odd signal; a common gain error remains. <null measurements> and bridge comparisons can reduce dependence on detector gain. Swapping sensors or leads exposes asymmetry; checking alignment, shielding, grounding and <temperature> control tests specific unwanted couplings. Varying signal amplitude or measurement <frequency> can reveal heating, nonlinear response or pickup.

Use independent measurement principles where possible, and test the predicted response to each deliberate change. **Repeated readings alone do not remove a fixed <systematic error>.** Correct identified significant effects and retain uncertainty for imperfectly known corrections; complete elimination cannot generally be demonstrated. Type A and Type B classify how uncertainty is evaluated, rather than simply meaning <random measurement errors> and <systematic errors>.<Guidelines for Evaluating and Expressing the Uncertainty of NIST Measurement Results by Barry N. = Solution

An <ideal operational amplifier> draws no current at either input. With stable <operational-amplifier negative feedback> in its unsaturated linear range, infinite <open-loop gain> also gives a <virtual short>, $V_+=V_-$. The amplifier adjusts its output to enforce that equality; it is not a conducting connection between the inputs. Here $V_+=0$, so the ideal summing node is a <virtual ground>. <Kirchhoff's current law> gives $V_{\rm in}/R_1=-V_{\rm out}/R_2$, hence **the ideal gain is $-R_2/R_1$**.

For the <finite-input-resistance inverting amplifier> let the inverting-node <voltage> be $v$, and continue to neglect <output impedance>. The controlled output and node-current balance are
$$
V_{\rm out}=-Av,\qquad
\frac{v-V_{\rm in}}{R_1}+\frac{v-V_{\rm out}}{R_2}+\frac{v}{R_{\rm in}}=0.
$$
Eliminating $v$ gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{V_{\rm out}}{V_{\rm in}}
=-\frac{R_2/R_1}{1+\dfrac1A\left(1+\dfrac{R_2}{R_1}+\dfrac{R_2}{R_{\rm in}}\right)}}.
$$
Dividing the denominator by $R_2$ gives the requested resistance-sum form. The ideal result follows whenever $|A|\gg 1+R_2/R_1+R_2/R_{\rm in}$; taking $R_{\rm in}\to\infty$ alone is insufficient if $A$ is small. At finite <frequency> $A$ and the gain are <phasor> ratios, subject to feedback stability.

With equal <resistors> and sufficiently large loop gain, **$V_{\rm out}\simeq -V_{\rm in}$**. This gives a controlled sign reversal or 180-degree <phase> inversion, useful for subtraction and opposite-phase signal paths, with amplitude set by a <resistor> ratio rather than uncertain <open-loop gain>. The source still sees approximately $R_1$, so this is not a high-input-impedance <voltage> follower. For the ideal-input, single-pole model, the <noise gain of an operational amplifier> is two and the closed-loop <bandwidth> is approximately half the <gain-bandwidth product>. Output swing, <slew rate> and load constraints must also permit linear operation.

A representative bipolar-input 741 amplifier has **$R_{\rm in}$ of order $10^6\,\Omega$**; the LM741 specification gives a typical $2\,\mathrm{M\Omega}$ at $25^\circ\mathrm C$. This is an illustrative device specification, not an identification of an unspecified practical-class part. Other input architectures can have much larger <electrical resistance>, and finite input <capacitance> makes the <input impedance> <frequency> dependent.<LM741 Operational Amplifier by Texas Instruments>[SNOSC25D, §6.5, p. 5, input-resistance row and its test conditions]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=b}

The <open-loop gain> is large and roughly constant at low <frequency>, then falls as internal <poles> become important. A dominant-pole approximation is
$$
\boxed{A(f)\simeq\frac{A_0}{1+if/f_p}}.
$$
Above $f_p$, but before higher <poles> dominate, $|A|\propto f^{-1}$, a slope of $-20\,\mathrm{dB}$ per decade, with increasing <phase> lag. The approximate unity-gain <frequency> is $f_T=A_0f_p$. A 741-class amplifier has typical low-frequency gain about $2\times10^5$; this is the <open-loop gain>, distinct from the feedback-set signal gain. The finite-gain formula at the parent level explains why the closed-loop response eventually rolls off.<LM741 Operational Amplifier by Texas Instruments>[§6.5, p. 5, large-signal voltage-gain row]{ref}
