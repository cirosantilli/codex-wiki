= Solution

For weak <damping>, the <ringdown comparison bound for two resonator modes> gives $I(t)=I(0)e^{-\omega t/Q}$. Interpreting the stated loudness comparison as an <intensity> comparison,
$$
\frac{I_L(2)}{I_H(2)}=2\exp\!\left[-2\left(\frac{2\pi(700)}{Q_L}-\frac{2\pi(6000)}{10^5}\right)\right]<1.
$$
Therefore **the information gives an upper bound**, not a unique value:
$$
\boxed{Q_L<\frac{2\pi(700)}{2\pi(6000)/10^5+\ln2/2}\simeq6.08\times10^3.}
$$
Equality would describe equal <intensities> at two seconds; the stated strict inequality allows every smaller positive $Q_L$ consistent with the weak-damping model.

By linear <scalar diffraction theory>, complementary <aperture> transmissions $t$ and $1-t$ give $U_t+U_{1-t}=U_{\rm open}$ under identical illumination. In <Fraunhofer diffraction>, away from the direct forward beam $U_{\rm open}=0$, so $U_{1-t}=-U_t$: **the complementary screens have identical off-axis diffraction <intensities>**. This is <Babinet's principle>. Their <complex amplitudes>, rather than their <intensities>, add. The direct beam is the exception; real conducting-edge vector effects are outside this ideal scalar statement.

A conventional <body-centred cubic> cell contains two atoms, so its edge $a$ obeys $\rho a^3=2(23m_u)$. Taking $m_u=1.66054\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}$ gives $a\simeq4.29\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm m$. For a <free-electron Fermi circle in a square lattice>, include two spin states per <wavevector>. A square cell of side $a$ supplies $z/a^2$ <electrons> per unit area; counting the disk of occupied states gives
$$
\frac{z}{a^2}=\frac{2\pi k_F^2}{(2\pi)^2},\qquad k_F=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi z}}a.
$$
The <first Brillouin zone> is $|k_x|,|k_y|\leq\pi/a$. For $z=1$, **$k_F=2.507/a$**, so the circular <Fermi surface> lies wholly inside this square and encloses half its area. The figure uses the stated free-electron approximation; the lattice symmetry alone does not prescribe an exact circular surface.

= Solution

The restoring, <damping> and <electric forces> on the <electron> give $m_e\ddot x=-kx-b\dot x-eE(t)$. Dividing by $m_e$ gives a <Lorentz oscillator model> with
$$
\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m_e}=\boxed{1.785\times10^{15}\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}},\qquad
\gamma=b/m_e=\boxed{6.264\times10^{12}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}}.
$$
For the $e^{i\omega t}$ convention, substitute $x=\operatorname{Re}(x_0e^{i\omega t})$. Then $x_0=-eE_0/[m_e(\omega_0^2-\omega^2+i\gamma\omega)]$ and
$$
\boxed{v_0=i\omega x_0=\frac{-i\omega eE_0}{m_e(\omega_0^2-\omega^2+i\gamma\omega)}.}
$$
The time-averaged dissipated <power> is
$$
P=\frac{be^2|E_0|^2}{2m_e^2}\frac{\omega^2}{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+\gamma^2\omega^2},\qquad
\frac P{P_{\max}}=\frac{\gamma^2}{(\omega_0^2/\omega-\omega)^2+\gamma^2}.
$$
For positive <frequency> the squared term is minimized to zero only at **$\omega=\omega_0$**. Thus the <power> maximum is exactly at the undamped <natural frequency>, even though the <displacement> maximum is slightly shifted. Here $P_{\max}=e^2|E_0|^2/(2b)$. The half-power equation is $|\omega_0^2-\omega^2|=\gamma\omega$, whose two positive roots are
$$
\omega_\pm=\frac{\sqrt{4\omega_0^2+\gamma^2}\pm\gamma}{2},\qquad
\boxed{\Delta\omega=\omega_+-\omega_-=\gamma.}
$$
This is the <absorbed-power bandwidth of a viscously damped oscillator>, exact within the model. Near <resonance> the profile is approximately a <Lorentzian line shape>. With $c=2.99792458\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$,
$$
\boxed{\lambda_{\rm peak}=\frac{2\pi c}{\omega_0}=1055.2\,\mathrm{nm}},\qquad
\boxed{\Delta\lambda=2\pi c\left(\frac1{\omega_-}-\frac1{\omega_+}\right)=\frac{2\pi c\gamma}{\omega_0^2}=3.70\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
For this <wavelength linewidth of a damped-oscillator power resonance> we used $\omega_+\omega_-=\omega_0^2$. The half-power <wavelength> endpoints are not exactly symmetric around $\lambda_{\rm peak}$, although that distinction is tiny here.

\Image[paper-1-oscillator.png]
{title=Absorption resonance and release of a polarized oscillator}
{description=Original model curves. Frequency is offset from resonance in linewidth units. The release panel uses the supplied mechanical parameters and a unit initial displacement.}

In a constant field the settled <displacement> is $x_s=-eE_{\rm dc}/k$. On sudden removal, with zero initial <velocity>, the free motion is
$$
x(t)=x_s e^{-\gamma t/2}\left[\cos\Omega t+\frac{\gamma}{2\Omega}\sin\Omega t\right],\qquad
\Omega=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2/4}.
$$
The <electron> oscillates about its original equilibrium, losing the previously stored spring <energy>. The period is **$3.52\,\mathrm{fs}$**, the amplitude decay time is **$2/\gamma=0.319\,\mathrm{ps}$**, and the cycle-averaged <energy> decay time is **$1/\gamma=0.160\,\mathrm{ps}$**. This is the effective classical model supplied, not a prediction of the full hydrogen line <radiation spectrum>.

= Solution

A <wave packet> is a localized <superposition> of nearby <wavenumbers>. Its rapid carrier crests have <phase velocity> $v_p=\omega/k$; the slowly varying envelope translates at the <group velocity>. Dispersion means that $\omega(k)$ is not simply a constant times $k$, so different carrier components have different <phase velocities>. Expand around $k_0$:
$$
u(x,t)=\operatorname{Re}\!\int A(k)e^{i[kx-\omega(k)t]}\,dk
\simeq\operatorname{Re}\!\left\{e^{i(k_0x-\omega_0t)}\int A(k_0+\kappa)e^{i\kappa[x-\omega'(k_0)t]}\,d\kappa\right\}.
$$
The integral is an envelope depending on $x-\omega'(k_0)t$, so **$v_g=d\omega/dk$** at the central <frequency>. Neglected curvature causes packet spreading. For a <deep-water gravity wave>, $\omega=\sqrt{gk}$, hence $v_p=\sqrt{g/k}$ and
$$
\boxed{v_g=\tfrac12\sqrt{g/k}=v_p/2=\frac{gT}{4\pi}.}
$$
For <crest crossings through a dispersive wave packet>, an instantaneous group containing six crests has length approximately $L=6\lambda$. A stationary ship takes $L/v_g$ to encounter the group; its crest-arrival <frequency> is $v_p/\lambda$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{N=\frac{v_p}{\lambda}\frac L{v_g}=12.}
$$
In the envelope frame the ship moves backwards at $v_g$ while crests move forwards at $v_p-v_g=v_g$. Their relative speed is $2v_g$, explaining why counting a snapshot and counting crossings differ.

\Image[paper-1-wave-group.png]
{title=Six crests in a packet and twelve ship crossings}
{description=Original narrow-band deep-water model. The right panel is in the envelope frame; only crest trajectories intersecting the ship while it is inside the packet are drawn.}

To estimate <deep-water swell distance from period-dependent arrival times>, assume a localized storm emits both periods at the same time and place, propagation is in deep water, and currents and refraction can be neglected. If its distance is $D$, the arrival lag obeys
$$
86400=D\left(\frac1{v_g(15\,\mathrm s)}-\frac1{v_g(20\,\mathrm s)}\right)
=\frac{4\pi D}{g}\left(\frac1{15}-\frac1{20}\right).
$$
With $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, **$D=15g(86400)/\pi\simeq4.05\times10^6\,\mathrm m$**, about **4050 km**. The corresponding travel times are three and four days. A moving or long-lived storm with frequency-dependent emission times would invalidate the unique distance inference.

= Solution
{parent=B8}

At screen coordinate $X$, the propagation length from <aperture> coordinate $x$ is $[D^2+(X-x)^2]^{1/2}$. Its expansion has a constant term, a term linear in $x$ giving the observation angle, and a quadratic term of order $x^2/(2D)$. For $|x|\leq d/2$, the quadratic <wave phase> is at most of order $\pi d^2/(4\lambda D)$. Requiring it to be much smaller than one gives the sufficient condition **$D\gg d^2/\lambda$**; higher terms are then smaller in the paraxial geometry. In the <lens> arrangement, put the source at the first <lens>'s front focus, illuminate the grating with its collimated output, and put the screen at the second <lens>'s back <focal plane>. This obtains the angular <Fraunhofer diffraction> pattern without a physically very distant screen.

\Image[paper-1-grating-layout.png]
{title=Two-lens arrangement for a grating diffraction pattern}
{description=Original schematic ray construction. Equal outgoing ray slopes meet at a common back-focal-plane position; distances and angles are illustrative.}

The slit pitch is $p=1/(200\,\mathrm{mm^{-1}})=5.00\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. Normal-incidence maxima satisfy $p\sin\theta=m\lambda$. Thus $\sin\theta_3=0.276$ and, using rectilinear focal-plane geometry,
$$
\boxed{x_3=\pm f\tan\theta_3=\pm86.1\,\mathrm{mm}.}
$$
The paraxial result $x_3\simeq fm\lambda/p=\pm82.8\,\mathrm{mm}$ is a roughly 4% lower approximation; an actual wide-angle <lens>'s mapping depends on its design. The angular result does not depend on that convention.

For $N$ uniformly illuminated equal slits of width $w$, the diffraction <intensity> has the form
$$
I(\theta)=Cw^2\operatorname{sinc}^2\!\left(\frac{\pi w\sin\theta}{\lambda}\right)
\left[\frac{\sin(N\pi p\sin\theta/\lambda)}{\sin(\pi p\sin\theta/\lambda)}\right]^2,\quad \operatorname{sinc}u=\frac{\sin u}u.
$$
Here $C$ is unchanged when the incoming <intensity> is fixed. The broad <single-slit diffraction> envelope multiplies the narrow grating orders. At a principal maximum,
$$
I_m=CN^2w^2\operatorname{sinc}^2(\pi mw/p).
$$
The <fixed-illumination grating peak change with slit width> gives the changes from $w=1.0$ to $1.5\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ are
$$
\boxed{I_{0,\rm new}/I_{0,\rm old}=2.25},\qquad
\boxed{I_{3,\rm new}/I_{3,\rm old}=\left[\frac{\sin(0.9\pi)}{\sin(0.6\pi)}\right]^2=0.1056.}
$$
The central peak increases by 125%, while the third-order peak decreases by about 89.4%. Separate normalizations to each central peak would hide this effect. The following width-specific solutions and profiles use a common incoming normalization.

= Solution

The <wave impedance> is the travelling-wave field ratio $Z=E/H$; for the stated nonmagnetic media it is $Z_0/n$. A <reflection coefficient> is a reflected-to-incident <complex amplitude> ratio with an explicitly chosen field orientation. With this <reflection-amplitude sign convention>, using the question's formula for incidence inside silicon gives
$$
\boxed{r=\frac{Z_0/3.5-Z_0}{Z_0/3.5+Z_0}=-\frac59},\qquad R=|r|^2=\frac{25}{81}.
$$
With a common tangential electric-field direction, the usual electric-field <Fresnel equations> instead give $r_E=(Z_2-Z_1)/(Z_2+Z_1)=+5/9$ for this incidence. The printed formula corresponds to the opposite reflected-field sign convention, for example the magnetic amplitude convention. It gives the same <reflectance> and the same round-trip factor $r^2$.

Each extra internal round trip multiplies the transmitted amplitude by $r^2e^{i\delta}$, with $\delta=4\pi nd/\lambda$. A <geometric series> therefore gives
$$
A_{\rm out}=A_{\rm first}\sum_{j=0}^\infty(r^2e^{i\delta})^j=\frac{A_{\rm first}}{1-r^2e^{i\delta}},\qquad
\boxed{I\propto\frac1{1+R^2-2R\cos\delta}.}
$$
This is <Fabry–Pérot transmission from multiple reflections>. Assuming lossless silicon with wavelength-independent $n$, the full <intensity> transmittance between air regions is
$$
T=\frac{(1-R)^2}{(1-R)^2+4R\sin^2(\delta/2)}.
$$
Maxima have $2nd/\lambda=j$, minima $2nd/\lambda=j+\tfrac12$. Here $2nd=7000\,\mathrm{nm}$, so within the specified tuning range the only maximum is **$1400\,\mathrm{nm}$**, and the two minima are **$1555.6\,\mathrm{nm}$** and **$1272.7\,\mathrm{nm}$**. The contrast is
$$
\boxed{\frac{I_{\max}}{I_{\min}}=\left(\frac{1+R}{1-R}\right)^2=\left(\frac{53}{28}\right)^2=3.583.}
$$

\Image[paper-1-etalon.png]
{title=Transmission contrast and resonance narrowing with reflectivity}
{description=Original ideal lossless symmetric-etalon curves. The coating example uses chosen R=0.90, unchanged optical thickness and zero additional round-trip phase.}

Identical highly reflecting coatings increase the <finesse>: transmission maxima become narrower and the <intensity> between them falls. Ideal symmetric lossless coatings retain $T_{\max}=1$; absorption or unequal mirrors reduce it. The illustrated maxima stay fixed only because additional reflection <wave phases> are held unchanged. Real coating <wave phases> can also shift the <resonances>.

= Solution
{parent=C10}

For this calculation define the net <wavevector> gained by the <neutron> as $\mathbf Q=\mathbf k_f-\mathbf k_i$, the opposite of a common sample-transfer convention. Since $\mathbf Q\perp\mathbf k_i$,
$$
E_f-E_i=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_n}\bigl[(\mathbf k_i+\mathbf Q)^2-k_i^2\bigr]=\frac{\hbar^2Q^2}{2m_n}.
$$
The <perpendicular neutron recoil and phonon absorption> condition therefore requires **$\omega(Q)=\hbar Q^2/(2m_n)$**. In the supplied periodic dispersion, interpret its $q$ as this transfer $Q$ in the <extended-zone representation of a phonon dispersion>. Put $x=Qa/2$ and $C=m_nav/\hbar\simeq15.84$. Nonzero intersections obey
$$
x^2=C|\sin x|.
$$
There is no root in $0<x\leq\pi/2$: $\sin x\geq2x/\pi$ there and $C>\pi^2/4$. On $(\pi/2,\pi)$ the ratio $x^2/\sin x$ increases strictly from $\pi^2/4$ to infinity, so exactly one root occurs. Nor can there be a higher root: $x\geq\sqrt C$ gives $x^2\geq C\geq C|\sin x|$, with strict inequality at the possible boundary. For $\pi\leq x\leq\sqrt C$, use $|\sin x|\leq x-\pi$; $x^2-C(x-\pi)>0$ throughout this short interval, since its minimum there is $C(1+\pi-\sqrt C)>0$. Thus the only nonzero magnitude satisfies
$$
\boxed{\pi/a<Q<2\pi/a.}
$$
Solving the exact dispersion, which is permitted instead of the linear approximation, gives
$$
\boxed{Q=1.069\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}},\quad \omega=3.610\times10^{12}\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}},\quad f=0.575\,\mathrm{THz}.}
$$
A straight chord between the zone edge and the next zone centre gives $\omega\simeq4v/a-2vQ/\pi$. Its intersection solves $(\hbar/2m_n)Q^2+(2v/\pi)Q-4v/a=0$, yielding $Q\simeq1.006\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ and $f\simeq0.508\,\mathrm{THz}$ as a less accurate estimate.

\Image[paper-1-phonon-transfer.png]
{title=Neutron recoil intersects a periodic acoustic branch}
{description=Original dispersion and recoil curves using the supplied parameters. The nonzero exact intersection lies in the second extended-zone interval; the dashed chord is the permitted linear approximation.}

The reduced-zone <phonon> <wavevector> is $q_{\rm red}=Q-2\pi/a=-1.873\times10^9\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ along the chosen positive transfer direction. The difference is a reciprocal-lattice vector, not an additional independent <phonon> outside the first zone. At low <temperature> the absorption probability contains the <Bose-Einstein distribution> factor $n_B(\omega)=[e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT}-1]^{-1}$, which tends to zero. Here $\hbar\omega/k_B\simeq27.6\,\mathrm K$, so this absorption channel is strongly suppressed well below that scale. <elastic scattering> and kinematically allowed spontaneous <phonon> creation need not disappear.

For <elastic scattering>, **$E_f=E_i$** and **$\mathbf k_f-\mathbf k_i=\mathbf G$**, where $\mathbf G$ is a <reciprocal lattice vector>. In the ideal static crystal, coherent scattering obeys this lattice selection rule; the crystal as a whole can take the mechanical recoil with negligible <energy>. <crystal momentum> is defined modulo $\hbar\mathbf G$, not as unconstrained free-particle <momentum>.

For annihilation of a <phonon> $(\mathbf q,\omega)$,
$$
\boxed{E_f=E_i+\hbar\omega,\quad\mathbf k_f=\mathbf k_i+\mathbf q+\mathbf G.}

= Solution

For the <electronic and lattice heat-capacity crossover in a monovalent metal>, to obtain a numerical estimate from $E_F$ alone, assume monovalent potassium is a three-dimensional <free electron gas> with a parabolic band. Including both spins, the molar <density of states> is $g(E_F)=3N_A/(2E_F)$. Using the supplied approximate coefficient $3$, rather than silently replacing it by $\pi^2/3$, gives
$$
C_{el}=\frac92R\frac{k_B T}{E_F}.
$$
Equating it to the stated low-temperature <Debye heat capacity> yields the nonzero crossover
$$
\boxed{T=\left[\frac{15}{8\pi^4}\frac{k_B}{E_F}\theta_D^3\right]^{1/2}=0.911\,\mathrm K.}
$$
We used $k_B=8.61733\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{eV\,K^{-1}}$ and $E_F=2\,\mathrm{eV}$. This is self-consistently far below $\theta_D=100\,\mathrm K$ and the <Fermi temperature>. Using the exact Sommerfeld coefficient instead gives $0.954\,\mathrm K$; that is a change of approximation, not a discrepancy in the algebra. Without a band/DOS assumption, the supplied <Fermi energy> does not by itself determine $g(E_F)$.

= Solution
{parent=a (internal energy)}

For independent conduction-electron states labelled by band, <wavevector> and spin, combine those labels into $j$. Then
$$
\boxed{U_{el}=\sum_j\epsilon_j f_{\rm FD}(\epsilon_j;\mu,T)=\int g(\epsilon)\,\epsilon\frac{d\epsilon}{e^{(\epsilon-\mu)/k_BT}+1}.}
$$
Here $g$ counts the same spin and band states as the sum, and $\mu(T)$ is fixed by the number constraint $N=\int g f_{\rm FD}\,d\epsilon$. The <energy> zero is a fixed band reference. Use the total DOS for total <internal energy> or the molar DOS for molar <internal energy>; these conventions must not be mixed.

= Solution
{parent=b (internal energy)}

In the harmonic approximation, sum over <wavevector> $\mathbf q$ and <phonon> branch $s$:
$$
\boxed{U_{la}=\sum_{\mathbf q,s}\hbar\omega_{\mathbf q s}\left[\frac1{e^{\hbar\omega_{\mathbf q s}/k_BT}-1}+\frac12\right].}
$$
The occupation factor is the <Bose-Einstein distribution> with zero <phonon> <chemical potential>; $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$ is the <zero-point energy>. A temperature-independent zero-point term does not contribute to the <heat capacity> in this fixed-frequency approximation.

= Solution
{parent=a (heat capacity)}

Only states within an <energy> shell of width roughly $k_BT$ around $E_F$ appreciably change occupation at low <temperature>. There are roughly $g(E_F)k_BT$ such states and each excitation costs <energy> of order $k_BT$. Thus $\Delta U\propto g(E_F)(k_BT)^2$, giving **$C_{el}\propto g(E_F)k_B^2T$**. This is the <thermal-shell estimate of electronic heat capacity>; filled states far below $E_F$ are blocked by the <Pauli exclusion principle>. A smooth DOS and fixed particle number give the more precise Sommerfeld result $C_{el}=(\pi^2/3)g(E_F)k_B^2T$ to leading order.

= Solution
{parent=b (heat capacity)}

For three-dimensional acoustic modes, $\omega\propto q$ and the mode count in a spherical shell is proportional to $q^2dq$, giving <phonon> DOS proportional to $\omega^2$. Thermally occupied modes have $\hbar\omega$ of order $k_BT$, so $U_{la}-U_0\propto\int\omega^3n_B(\omega)\,d\omega\propto T^4$. Differentiation gives **$C_{la}\propto T^3$**. At $T\gg\theta_D$, all $3N$ harmonic modes are classically populated; each has mean <energy> $k_BT$ by equipartition. Thus the <molar heat capacity> tends to **$3R$**, the <Dulong-Petit law>. Anharmonic expansion and electronic contributions are not included in that lattice limit.

In <Fourier transform spectroscopy>, a scanning <Michelson interferometer> measures <intensity> against <optical path difference> $\ell$ rather than selecting one narrow <wavelength> at a time. After subtraction of the constant background, an ideal stationary source gives an <interferogram>
$$
J(\ell)=\int_0^\infty S(\nu)\cos(2\pi\nu\ell/c)\,d\nu.
$$
A cosine <Fourier transform> recovers the spectral <power> density $S(\nu)$ after instrumental calibration. A mirror <displacement> $x$ changes the round-trip path by $2x$; the zero-path-difference burst aligns the scan, and a reference laser can calibrate the <displacement>.

Finite maximum path difference $L$ multiplies the <interferogram> by a window and convolves the recovered <radiation spectrum> with that window's <Fourier transform>. Thus **$\delta\nu$ is of order $c/L$**; precise factors depend on scan range and resolution convention. Uniform path samples of separation $\Delta\ell$ need **$\nu_{\max}<c/(2\Delta\ell)$** to avoid <aliasing> without bandpass information. <interferogram apodization> reduces sidelobes at the price of broader lines. The broad accepted optical throughput can be advantageous; the multiplex signal-to-noise advantage depends on detector noise and is not universal in photon-noise-limited measurements. Source drift during a scan, finite beam divergence, imperfect sampling and <wave phase> errors require control. The finite-window reasoning is the <delay-range resolution in Fourier transform spectroscopy>.<Introduction to Fourier Transform Spectroscopy by Julius Cohen>[NBSIR 86-3339, chapters 13–16, printed pp.51–80: basic equation, apodization, resolution and sampling]{ref}

A finite <optical pupil> spreads a point into a <point spread function>. In <incoherent imaging> an extended <intensity> distribution is convolved with that function; its <Fourier transform> is the <optical transfer function>, which attenuates and ultimately removes high spatial <frequencies>. For an ideal circular pupil the point response is an <Airy disk>. The <Rayleigh criterion> for two equally bright incoherent points is
$$
\boxed{\theta_R\simeq1.22\lambda/D},\qquad \Delta x_R\simeq1.22\lambda f/D.
$$
A microscope's object-space scale is of order $\lambda/(2\,\mathrm{NA})$, with the numerical factor depending on illumination and the criterion; $\mathrm{NA}=n\sin\alpha$ measures the accepted ray cone. A larger pupil or shorter <wavelength> improves the diffraction scale. Aberrations, <defocus>, detector pixel sampling, motion, background and <photon> noise can worsen practical performance. Merely magnifying the same blurred image adds no information. Coherent sources require amplitude <superposition> instead of the incoherent <intensity> rule. Rayleigh resolution is a convention for separating a pair, distinct from the precision of localizing one isolated point; information does not abruptly vanish below it.

Boundary conditions quantize the transverse <wavevector> of an <electromagnetic waveguide>. For an ideal hollow rectangular conducting guide of sides $a,b$, filled with a nondispersive medium of wave speed $u$, a <transverse electric mode of a rectangular waveguide> or <transverse magnetic mode of a rectangular waveguide> obeys
$$
\boxed{\omega^2=\omega_c^2+u^2\beta^2},\qquad
\omega_c=u\pi\sqrt{(m/a)^2+(n/b)^2}.
$$
The allowed integer pairs differ: TE permits one zero index, but not both; TM requires both positive. Here $\beta$ is the longitudinal propagation constant. Below cutoff $\beta$ is imaginary, so the mode is evanescent. Above cutoff,
$$
v_p=\frac\omega\beta=\frac{u}{\sqrt{1-(\omega_c/\omega)^2}},\qquad
v_g=\frac{d\omega}{d\beta}=u\sqrt{1-(\omega_c/\omega)^2},\qquad
\boxed{v_pv_g=u^2.}
$$
The positive branch approaches the straight line $\omega=u\beta$ far above cutoff, while near cutoff its slope tends to zero. Different <frequencies> acquire different group delays and a pulse can spread. A <wave phase> speed above $u$ does not convey superluminal information; in this ideal guide <energy> transport follows the <group velocity>. A single hollow conductor cannot support a TEM mode; two-conductor guides can have a zero-cutoff TEM branch. Material dispersion, dielectric fibres and lossy boundaries require their actual <dispersion relation> and need not preserve the product rule.

Joining p-type and n-type regions initially allows majority carriers to diffuse across and recombine. Exposed negative acceptor charge on the p side and positive donor charge on the n side form a <depletion region>. Its <electric field> points from n to p and balances <diffusion> at equilibrium. The <Fermi level> is then spatially constant, while the conduction and <valence band> edges bend across the <built-in potential>; this equilibrium barrier cannot supply continuous <power> to a load.

<forward bias> makes p positive relative to n, reducing the barrier from $qV_{bi}$ to $q(V_{bi}-V)$. <electrons> enter p material and holes enter n material, becoming <minority carriers> whose <diffusion> and <recombination> sustain the current. <reverse bias> enlarges the barrier and leaves a much smaller minority-carrier current until breakdown. The ideal <diffusion> model gives **$I=I_0[e^{qV/k_BT}-1]$**, the <Shockley diode equation>; depletion-region <recombination>, series resistance and breakdown limit this approximation. Minority-carrier injection on both sides is essential to the junction theory.<The Theory of p-n Junctions in Semiconductors and p-n Junction Transistors by William Shockley>[1949, pp.435–436: rectification, electron and hole currents, and injection]{ref}

<forward bias> injects <electrons> and holes into a common active region. Their <electron-hole recombination> can emit a <photon> as an <electron> falls from a conduction-band state into an empty valence-band state, with **$h\nu$ approximately the <band gap> $E_g$** near the band edges. A <direct band gap> makes this radiative transition much more probable than an indirect gap requiring simultaneous <phonon> participation. Carrier confinement improves their overlap; <nonradiative recombination> and <photon> trapping reduce the external efficiency. This is spontaneous electroluminescence and does not require <population inversion>. The biased carriers have separate <quasi-Fermi levels>, rather than one global equilibrium <Fermi level>. The band-diagram downward arrow shows <photon> emission. Absorption of <photons> with $h\nu\gtrsim E_g$ creates electron-hole pairs, the upward band-diagram arrow. In the <depletion region> the built-in field drives <electrons> towards n material and holes towards p material; <diffusion> also delivers carriers generated within a <diffusion length> outside that region. Selective contacts collect the two signs. Illumination maintains a nonequilibrium <quasi-Fermi-level splitting>, allowing a voltage and useful current through an external load; it is the light, rather than the equilibrium barrier, that supplies the <energy>.

Taking forward diode current as positive, an ideal illuminated junction has
$$
\boxed{I=I_0(e^{qV/k_BT}-1)-I_{ph}},\qquad
\boxed{I_{sc}=-I_{ph},\quad V_{oc}=\frac{k_BT}{q}\ln(1+I_{ph}/I_0).}
$$
<power> is delivered where $VI<0$ in this convention. Reflection, below-gap transmission, carrier thermalization, <recombination> and resistive losses limit real conversion; even an ideal single junction has radiative detailed-balance limits.<Detailed Balance Limit of Efficiency of p-n Junction Solar Cells by William Shockley and Hans-Joachim Queisser>[1961, Journal of Applied Physics 32, pp.510–519: radiative detailed-balance limit]{ref}
