Use the unitary position-to-momentum <Fourier transform> $\phi(p)=(2\pi\hbar)^{-1/2}\int\psi(x)e^{-ipx/\hbar}\,dx$. Integration gives
$$
\phi(p,0)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar a}}\frac{2\hbar}{p}\sin\!\left(\frac{pa}{2\hbar}\right)=\boxed{\sqrt{\frac{a}{2\pi\hbar}}\,\operatorname{sinc}\!\left(\frac{pa}{2\hbar}\right)},
$$
where $\operatorname{sinc}u=\sin u/u$ with its continuous value one at zero. The amplitude is real and even, with central maximum $\sqrt{a/(2\pi\hbar)}$, zeros at $p=2n\pi\hbar/a$ for nonzero integer $n$, and alternating-sign sidelobes decaying as $1/|p|$.

{source=paper-1-top-hat-momentum.py}

The <probability density> is $|\phi|^2$, not the signed <Sinc function> amplitude. Its integral is one by the unitarity of the <Fourier transform>.

For the oppositely travelling components, write $u=c_1e^{ikx}$ and $v=c_2e^{-ikx}$ after removing the common time phase. Then $\partial_x\psi=ik(u-v)e^{-i\omega t}$. In $\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\partial_x\psi)$ the <interference> terms contribute no imaginary part, so
$$
\boxed{J=\frac{\hbar k}{m}(|c_1|^2-|c_2|^2).}
$$
A standing-wave <interference> pattern can therefore change density without adding a cross-term to the net current.

For **<barrier-top scattering>**, the stationary equation inside the barrier is $-\hbar^2\psi_2''/(2m)=0$. Thus its spatial part is linear: $\psi_2=(bx+c)e^{-i\omega t}$. Take incident amplitude one, $k=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$ and $\omega=E/\hbar$. Continuity of the spatial <wave function> and its derivative at the two finite steps gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
e^{-ika}+re^{ika}&=-ab+c,& ik(e^{-ika}-re^{ika})&=b,\\
te^{ika}&=ab+c,& ikte^{ika}&=b.
\end{aligned}
$$
Solving these four equations yields
$$
\boxed{r=-\frac{ika\,e^{-2ika}}{1-ika},\qquad t=\frac{e^{-2ika}}{1-ika}.}
$$
The equal exterior velocities mean flux ratios are squared amplitude ratios:
$$
\boxed{T=|t|^2=\frac1{1+k^2a^2}=\left(1+\frac{2mEa^2}{\hbar^2}\right)^{-1},\qquad R=\frac{k^2a^2}{1+k^2a^2},\qquad R+T=1.}
$$
This is the threshold limit of finite-barrier scattering, not exponential decay with a nonzero interior decay constant.

The interior constants are
$$
\boxed{b=\frac{ik e^{-ika}}{1-ika},\qquad c=e^{-ika}.}
$$
Since $\operatorname{Im}[(bx+c)^*b]=\operatorname{Im}(c^*b)=k/(1+k^2a^2)$,
$$
\boxed{J_2=\frac{\hbar k/m}{1+k^2a^2}=J_{\rm incident}T.}
$$
It is positive and independent of <position>, matching the transmitted flux. Although the classical kinetic energy inside is zero, the complex linear quantum <wave function> carries conserved <probability current>; its phase varies with <position>.

Differentiating the <expectation value> and using $\partial_t|\psi\rangle=-i\hat H|\psi\rangle/\hbar$ and $\partial_t\langle\psi|=i\langle\psi|\hat H/\hbar$ gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\langle\hat A\rangle}{dt}=\frac i\hbar\langle[\hat H,\hat A]\rangle+\left\langle\frac{\partial\hat A}{\partial t}\right\rangle.}
$$
The second term means explicit operator time dependence, distinct from the state's evolution.

The <commutator product rule> follows by adding and subtracting $\hat B\hat A\hat C$:
$$
[\hat A,\hat B\hat C]=\hat A\hat B\hat C-\hat B\hat C\hat A=[\hat A,\hat B]\hat C+\hat B[\hat A,\hat C].
$$
For the **<mathematical induction> step**, let $\hat D=[\hat A,\hat B]$ and use $[\hat A,\hat D]=0$. The case $n=1$ is immediate. If $[\hat A^m,\hat B]=m\hat D\hat A^{m-1}$, then
$$
[\hat A^{m+1},\hat B]=\hat A^m\hat D+[\hat A^m,\hat B]\hat A=(m+1)\hat D\hat A^m.
$$
Thus the claimed identity holds for every positive integer. Commutation of $\hat B$ with $\hat D$ is not needed for this particular step. This is the <central-commutator differentiation rule>. Termwise application to a convergent <power series>, on a common domain where the operations are justified, gives
$$
\boxed{[F(\hat A),\hat B]=\sum_{n\geq1}na_n\hat D\hat A^{n-1}=\hat D F'(\hat A).}
$$

For <position> and <momentum>, $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar\hat I$. Hence $[\hat p^2,\hat x]=-2i\hbar\hat p$, $[V(\hat x),\hat x]=0$ and $[V(\hat x),\hat p]=i\hbar V'(\hat x)$. Substitution gives **<Ehrenfest theorem>**:
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\langle\hat x\rangle}{dt}=\frac{\langle\hat p\rangle}{m},\qquad\frac{d\langle\hat p\rangle}{dt}=-\langle V'(\hat x)\rangle.}
$$
The mean <momentum> changes by the mean <force>. It does not follow for a general nonlinear <Potential energy> that $\langle V'(\hat x)\rangle=V'(\langle\hat x\rangle)$: a closed classical trajectory for the mean <position> requires an additional condition, such as a quadratic <Potential energy> or a sufficiently localized <wave packet>.

Expanding with the <angular momentum> <commutators> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
[\hat L_z,\hat L_\pm]&=[\hat L_z,\hat L_x]\pm i[\hat L_z,\hat L_y]=\pm\hbar\hat L_\pm,\\
\hat L_-\hat L_+&=(\hat L_x-i\hat L_y)(\hat L_x+i\hat L_y)=\hat L_x^2+\hat L_y^2+i[\hat L_x,\hat L_y]=\hat L^2-\hat L_z^2-\hbar\hat L_z.
\end{aligned}
$$
For $\hat L_z|\psi\rangle=\alpha\hbar|\psi\rangle$, commuting $\hat L_z$ past a <ladder operator> gives
$$
\hat L_z\hat L_\pm|\psi\rangle=(\alpha\pm1)\hbar\hat L_\pm|\psi\rangle.
$$
Thus the raised or lowered vector, **if nonzero**, is an <eigenvector> with the shifted <eigenvalue>; an <angular momentum ladder endpoint> can instead be annihilated. Also $[\hat L^2,\hat L_\pm]=0$, so $\hat L^2\hat L_\pm|\psi\rangle=\Lambda\hbar^2\hat L_\pm|\psi\rangle$ for every nonzero ladder result.

For <orbital angular momentum>, $\hat L_z=-i\hbar\partial_\phi$ gives an azimuthal factor $e^{i\alpha\phi}$. Single-valuedness under $\phi\mapsto\phi+2\pi$ requires **integer $\alpha$**. Positivity of ladder norms gives
$$
\|\hat L_+|\Lambda,m\rangle\|^2=\hbar^2[\Lambda-m(m+1)],\qquad
\|\hat L_-|\Lambda,m\rangle\|^2=\hbar^2[\Lambda-m(m-1)]
$$
for normalized states. A fixed finite $\Lambda$ therefore has upper and lower endpoints. Writing the upper endpoint as $m=\ell$, its vanishing raising norm gives $\Lambda=\ell(\ell+1)$; the lower endpoint is $m=-\ell$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\hat L^2|\ell,m\rangle=\ell(\ell+1)\hbar^2|\ell,m\rangle,\quad \hat L_z|\ell,m\rangle=m\hbar|\ell,m\rangle,\quad m=-\ell,-\ell+1,\ldots,\ell,}
$$
with $\ell=0,1,2,\ldots$. This integer conclusion is for <orbital angular momentum>; <spin> representations can also have half-integer labels.

For the specified Gaussian-radial state, use a <Cartesian p-orbital decomposition>: write $x+y+z=r(\sin\theta\cos\phi+\sin\theta\sin\phi+\cos\theta)$. Its angular factor is
$$
\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{3}}\left[(1+i)Y_{1,-1}+\sqrt2Y_{1,0}+(-1+i)Y_{1,1}\right].
$$
Only $\ell=1$ occurs. The squared angular coefficients are $2,2,2$ before common normalization, so each allowed $m$ has probability one third. Therefore the simultaneous outcome is
$$
\boxed{P(L^2=2\hbar^2,L_z=\hbar)=\frac13.}
$$
The common normalized radial factor cancels from this ratio. The standard symmetric $m$ sequence used above resolves the duplicated central positive entry in the printed list.

Direct <matrix multiplication> gives

\begin{array}{c|cc}
&|\uparrow\rangle&|\downarrow\rangle\\\hline
\hat S_x&\frac\hbar2|\downarrow\rangle&\frac\hbar2|\uparrow\rangle\\
\hat S_y&\frac{i\hbar}2|\downarrow\rangle&-\frac{i\hbar}2|\uparrow\rangle\\
\hat S_z&\frac\hbar2|\uparrow\rangle&-\frac\hbar2|\downarrow\rangle
\end{array}
$$
The normalized $S_x$ <eigenvectors> are $(|\uparrow\rangle\pm|\downarrow\rangle)/\sqrt2$, and the $S_y$ <eigenvectors> are $(|\uparrow\rangle\pm i|\downarrow\rangle)/\sqrt2$, with <eigenvalues> $\pm\hbar/2$ in each case.

For $N=|a|^2+|b|^2>0$, allowing for a possibly unnormalized input pair of coefficients,
$$
\boxed{\langle\hat S_z\rangle=\frac\hbar2\frac{|a|^2-|b|^2}{N},\qquad\langle\hat S^2\rangle=\frac{3\hbar^2}{4}.}
$$
For a normalized state $N=1$. The second result follows because each squared <Spin operator> matrix equals $\hbar^2\hat I/4$.

The tilted component is $\hat S_{z'}=(\hbar/2)\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&-\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$. Its <eigenvalues> remain $\pm\hbar/2$, with one real phase convention for the normalized <eigenvectors>
$$
\boxed{|+z'\rangle=\cos(\theta/2)|\uparrow\rangle+\sin(\theta/2)|\downarrow\rangle,\qquad|-z'\rangle=-\sin(\theta/2)|\uparrow\rangle+\cos(\theta/2)|\downarrow\rangle.}
$$
Projecting the initial $|\uparrow\rangle$ onto them gives **$P_+=\cos^2(\theta/2)$** and **$P_-=\sin^2(\theta/2)$**. Thus
$$
\boxed{\langle\hat S_{z'}\rangle=\frac\hbar2(P_+-P_-)=\frac\hbar2\cos\theta.}
$$
A smaller <expectation> does not imply a smaller individual <spin> <eigenvalue>; it describes the probabilities of the same two measurement outcomes.

= Solution
{parent=C10}

**<identical particles> have the same intrinsic properties**, including mass, charge and <spin>. Their labels are bookkeeping devices rather than measurable identities. When <wave functions> overlap, <quantum mechanics> supplies no observable trajectory that permanently identifies “particle 1” independently of “particle 2”. A many-particle description must therefore treat exchange consistently. The <Hamiltonian> and physical <observables> are invariant under relabelling <identical particles>.

For two particles, the <exchange operator> obeys $\hat P_{12}^2=\hat I$. The usual three-dimensional <symmetrization postulate> selects states symmetric under exchange for <bosons> and antisymmetric for <fermions>. In relativistic quantum theory, the <Spin-statistics theorem> associates integer <spin> with <bosons> and half-integer <spin> with <fermions> under its locality and positivity assumptions; the connection is not derived merely from $\hat P_{12}^2=\hat I$ in elementary nonrelativistic mechanics. For orthonormal one-particle states $u$ and $v$, the two-particle spatial combinations are
$$
\Psi_\pm(1,2)=\frac{u(1)v(2)\pm v(1)u(2)}{\sqrt2}.
$$
The sign concerns the **complete state**, including <spin> and other internal variables, not necessarily the spatial factor alone.

The antisymmetric state vanishes if both <fermions> occupy the same one-particle state: this is the **<Pauli exclusion principle>**. For $N$ <fermions> in orthonormal <spin orbitals> $\chi_j$, the normalized <Slater determinant> $\Psi=(N!)^{-1/2}\det[\chi_j(i)]$ supplies the required exchange sign. Bosonic occupation has no corresponding upper limit, and symmetrized products describe many <bosons>. Fermionic occupation is zero or one per complete one-particle state; two electrons can still occupy the same spatial <atomic orbital> with opposite <spin>.

<Spin> and spatial symmetry are linked. A two-electron <spin singlet state> is antisymmetric, so its spatial factor must be symmetric. A symmetric <triplet state> instead requires an antisymmetric spatial factor. Exchange then changes pair correlations and the energies available when interactions depend on particle separation, even without introducing a new classical “exchange <force>”. It contributes to atomic shell structure, bonding and magnetic behaviour.

\Image[paper-1-exchange-correlations.png]
{title=Symmetric and antisymmetric two-particle spatial states have different joint-position correlations}
{source=paper-1-exchange-correlations.py}

In an occupation-number description, bosonic <creation and annihilation operators> satisfy $[\hat a_i,\hat a_j^\dagger]=\delta_{ij}$, whereas fermionic operators satisfy $\{\hat c_i,\hat c_j^\dagger\}=\delta_{ij}$ and anticommute among themselves. The latter imply $(\hat c_i^\dagger)^2=0$. This formalism implements the same exchange constraints without assigning permanent particle labels.

For noninteracting particles in <thermal equilibrium>, the mean occupation of a state of energy $\epsilon$ is
$$
\bar n_B=\frac1{e^{(\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT)}-1},\qquad\bar n_F=\frac1{e^{(\epsilon-\mu)/(k_BT)}+1}.
$$
<Fermi-Dirac statistics> produces a filled Fermi sea at zero <temperature> and <degeneracy pressure>; <Bose-Einstein statistics> permits macroscopic <Ground state> occupation when the density of available states and thermodynamic conditions allow condensation. The different statistics therefore have measurable collective consequences, not just different notation.<Statistical Physics by David Tong>[Chapter 3, §§3.5–3.6: Bose and Fermi distributions, condensation and degenerate Fermi gases]{ref}

When phase-space occupation is dilute, both distributions reduce to the <Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics> limit. Likewise, well-separated localized <wave packets> have negligible overlap and small exchange corrections, so effectively labelled classical trajectories can become a useful approximation. In two spatial dimensions more general exchange statistics are possible; the standard boson/fermion account here concerns ordinary three-dimensional particles and their total states.

For the nonrelativistic Coulomb <Hamiltonian> with reduced electron-proton mass $\mu$, separation in <Spherical coordinate system> gives **$\psi_{n\ell m}(r,\theta,\phi)=R_{n\ell}(r)Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)$**. The <quantum numbers> obey $n\geq1$, $0\leq\ell\leq n-1$ and $-\ell\leq m\leq\ell$. These are joint <eigenstates> of energy, $L^2$ and $L_z$, with the latter two <eigenvalues> $\ell(\ell+1)\hbar^2$ and $m\hbar$.

The Coulomb bound-state energies and characteristic length are
$$
\boxed{E_n=-\frac{\mu e^4}{2(4\pi\epsilon_0)^2\hbar^2n^2},\qquad a_\mu=\frac{4\pi\epsilon_0\hbar^2}{\mu e^2}.}
$$
For example, $\psi_{100}=(\pi a_\mu^3)^{-1/2}e^{-r/a_\mu}$ is spherically symmetric and has no radial node. In general the radial part contains an exponential times a <generalized Laguerre polynomial>, with $n-\ell-1$ radial nodes. The <spherical harmonic> specifies angular structure and azimuthal phase; real linear combinations of degenerate $m$ states give familiar directional orbitals.

The leading Coulomb energy depends on $n$ alone, giving $n^2$ spatial states for each principal shell, or $2n^2$ including electron <spin>. <Fine structure>, <Lamb shift> and <Hyperfine structure> lift parts of this ideal degeneracy. An <atomic orbital> is a probability amplitude, not a classical planetary path.<Quantum Mechanics by David Tong>[Section 4: separation of the Coulomb problem and hydrogenic states]{ref}

**<Quantum tunnelling> is supported by transport and decay through barriers that classical particles of the same energy cannot traverse.** Three complementary examples are:

* In **<alpha decay>**, an alpha cluster escapes the attractive nuclear region through a Coulomb barrier. The barrier penetration factor depends exponentially on a forbidden-region action, helping explain the strong dependence of lifetimes on emitted alpha energy. This is evidence from systematic decay rates, rather than direct imaging of an alpha inside the barrier.
* A **<scanning tunnelling microscope>** measures current across a narrow <vacuum> gap between a conducting tip and sample. At small bias an approximate dependence is $I\propto e^{-2\kappa s}$ with $\kappa\simeq\sqrt{2m\Phi}/\hbar$. The strong gap-distance sensitivity enables atomic-scale imaging and local spectroscopy. Electronic <density of states> and barrier height also affect the current, so constant-current contrast is not an unqualified geometric atom map.<Surface Studies by Scanning Tunneling Microscopy by Gerd Binnig and others>[Vacuum-tunnelling measurement and surface imaging]{ref}
* A heavily doped **<Tunnel diode>** has a narrow p-n barrier. Bias changes the overlap of occupied and empty states across it; interband tunnelling produces a characteristic peak and <Negative differential resistance> interval in its current-voltage curve. The measured voltage dependence agrees with the changing band overlap, providing a solid-state transport example.<New Phenomenon in Narrow Germanium Junctions by Leo Esaki>[Germanium-junction tunnelling characteristic]{ref}

Together these test barrier penetration in nuclear decay, vacuum-gap transport and semiconductor transport. Their detailed currents depend on state supply and matching as well as the barrier transmission probability.

For the <Quantum harmonic oscillator> define
$$
\hat a=\frac{m\omega\hat x+i\hat p}{\sqrt{2m\hbar\omega}},\qquad\hat a^\dagger=\frac{m\omega\hat x-i\hat p}{\sqrt{2m\hbar\omega}}.
$$
The <Canonical commutation relation> gives $[\hat a,\hat a^\dagger]=1$, and
$$
\boxed{\hat H=\hbar\omega(\hat a^\dagger\hat a+\tfrac12),\qquad[\hat H,\hat a^\dagger]=\hbar\omega\hat a^\dagger,\qquad[\hat H,\hat a]=-\hbar\omega\hat a.}
$$
Thus the <creation operator> and <annihilation operator> raise or lower the energy by one quantum. Positivity of $\hat a^\dagger\hat a$ and termination of downward stepping give $E_n=\hbar\omega(n+1/2)$, $n=0,1,\ldots$. Normalized actions are $\hat a|n\rangle=\sqrt n|n-1\rangle$ and $\hat a^\dagger|n\rangle=\sqrt{n+1}|n+1\rangle$. The annihilated <ground state> obeys
$$
\psi_0(x)=\left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4}e^{-m\omega x^2/(2\hbar)},\qquad|n\rangle=\frac{(\hat a^\dagger)^n}{\sqrt{n!}}|0\rangle.
$$
The nonzero <Ground state> energy is the <zero-point energy>, consistent with position-momentum <quantum uncertainty>. Since $\hat x=\sqrt{\hbar/(2m\omega)}(\hat a+\hat a^\dagger)$, a perturbation linear in <position> connects only neighbouring number states. The algebra is especially useful for <matrix elements> and <selection rules>.<Quantum Mechanics by David Tong>[Section 2: harmonic oscillator ladder construction]{ref}

The ideal voltage-feedback <Operational amplifier> has **zero input currents** and, while stable <negative feedback> holds it in its linear operating range, **equal input voltages**: $i_+=i_-=0$ and $v_+=v_-$. The first rule follows from infinite <input impedance>; the second is the large-open-loop-gain limit of $v_o=A(v_+-v_-)$. Equal input <electric potential> is a <virtual short>, not an electrically conducting connection.

For an <inverting amplifier> with the noninverting input grounded, $v_-=0$ and node-current balance gives $v_i/R_i=-v_o/R_f$, hence **$v_o=-R_fv_i/R_i$**. For a <noninverting amplifier>, the feedback divider gives $v_-=v_oR_g/(R_f+R_g)=v_i$, hence **$v_o=(1+R_f/R_g)v_i$**.

{title=Ideal inverting and noninverting negative-feedback configurations}
{source=paper-1-op-amp-feedback.py}

One should check output swing, input common-mode range, <slew rate>, <Gain-bandwidth product> and stability before using these limits in a real circuit. Saturation, positive feedback or operation beyond <bandwidth> invalidates the equal-voltage rule; finite input bias currents and offset voltage can also matter.<Operational-amplifier negative feedback by Analog Devices>[University electronics course, Chapter 3: high-gain feedback assumptions and practical limits]{ref}

Thermal agitation in a <resistor> produces **<Johnson-Nyquist noise>**, even without an applied mean current. In the classical low-frequency regime, its one-sided open-circuit voltage spectrum is $S_V=4k_BTR$ and its equivalent short-circuit current spectrum is $S_I=4k_BT/R$. In an <Effective noise bandwidth> $B$,
$$
\boxed{v_{\rm rms}=\sqrt{4k_BTRB},\qquad i_{\rm rms}=\sqrt{4k_BTB/R}.}
$$
For a matched load, available thermal noise power is $k_BTB$. A <resistor> and load at the same <temperature> exchange noise power without a net heat flow.

Cooling reduces the rms amplitude as $\sqrt T$ if <Electrical resistance> and <bandwidth> remain fixed. A cryogenic bath or refrigerator, <thermal anchoring> and controlled heat input can maintain a steady low <temperature>. Radiation shields, an insulating <vacuum>, thermally anchored leads and limited measurement dissipation reduce heat leaks and self-heating. <Temperature> sensing and stable <feedback control> reduce slow temperature-induced drift; keeping <temperature> constant does not remove microscopic <thermal fluctuations>.

\Image[paper-1-thermal-noise.png]
{title=At fixed resistance and bandwidth, thermal-noise power scales with temperature and rms amplitude with its square root}
{source=paper-1-thermal-noise.py}

<Bandwidth> restriction, suitable impedance and a low-noise amplifier complement cooling. Warm leads, contacts and the amplifier can otherwise dominate the observed noise. At fixed mean current, <shot noise> is not generally reduced merely by cooling, and low-frequency excess noise need not follow the Johnson law. At frequencies with $hf\gtrsim k_BT$, quantum noise expressions replace the classical approximation.<Op-amp Noise by Analog Devices>[Distinction between resistor thermal noise, amplifier noise and current-noise contributions]{ref}

Uniform <signal sampling> at rate $f_s$ multiplies a continuous signal by a time-domain <Dirac comb>, giving frequency-domain replicas spaced by $f_s$:
$$
X_s(f)=f_s\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}X(f-kf_s).
$$
If a baseband signal is restricted to $|f|\leq B$ and **$f_s>2B$**, these replicas can be separated by an ideal reconstruction filter. Frequencies differing by integer multiples of $f_s$ have the same sampled phase progression; for real signals they fold into the interval from zero to $f_s/2$. This ambiguity is **<aliasing>**.

{source=paper-1-sampling-alias.py}

For example, $0.8f_s$ and $0.2f_s$ cosines produce identical sample values. Apply an analogue <Anti-aliasing filter> before digitization and choose enough sample-rate margin for its finite transition band. A digital filter applied afterward cannot distinguish components already folded onto one another. Out-of-band noise also aliases unless filtered. The sample rate sets temporal/spectral coverage; <quantization (signal processing)> resolution sets amplitude precision and is a separate limitation.
