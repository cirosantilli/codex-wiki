= Solution
{parent=1}

For the <deep-water gravity wave>, the <phase velocity> and <group velocity> follow from its <dispersion relation>:
$$
v_p=\frac{\omega}{k}=\sqrt{\frac gk},\qquad v_g=\frac{d\omega}{dk}=\frac12\sqrt{\frac gk}=\frac{v_p}{2}.
$$
The <wave packet> travels at $v_g$, while the individual crests travel at $v_p$. Let its effective length be $D\simeq5\lambda$, where $\lambda=2\pi/k$ is the carrier <wavelength>. A fixed observer is inside the <wave packet> for $\Delta t=D/v_g$, and crests arrive with <wave period> $\lambda/v_p$. Thus
$$
\boxed{N_{\rm passing}=\frac{D/v_g}{\lambda/v_p}=5\frac{v_p}{v_g}=10\text{ crests}.}
$$
Crests enter the rear of the moving <wave packet> and disappear at its front, so the five visible at one instant are not five permanently identifiable crests. The count uses the intended narrow-band, approximately unchanged <wave packet> and neglects the precise visibility of its end crests.

A bubble is a thin water layer surrounded by air. At a locally flat patch, let its thickness be $d$, its <refractive index> be $n$, and the internal angle of <refraction> be $\theta_t$. The two principal reflected <light> contributions are the reflection at the first surface and the contribution that enters the layer, reflects at its second surface and leaves again. The first reflection, from lower to higher <refractive index>, introduces a $\pi$ <phase shift>; the second does not. Their <optical path difference> is $2nd\cos\theta_t$, so their <phase difference> is, modulo $2\pi$,
$$
\Delta\phi=\frac{4\pi nd\cos\theta_t}{\lambda_0}+\pi,
$$
where $\lambda_0$ is the <vacuum> <wavelength>. <Constructive interference> in reflected <light> therefore occurs approximately when
$$
\boxed{2nd\cos\theta_t=(m+\tfrac12)\lambda_0,\qquad m=0,1,2,\ldots.}
$$
<Destructive interference> occurs at $2nd\cos\theta_t=m\lambda_0$. In the weak-reflection approximation, the reflected <light intensity> at each <wavelength> is proportional to $\sin^2(2\pi nd\cos\theta_t/\lambda_0)$. This is a smooth spectral filter, with broad maxima and minima: it does not select three pure <wavelengths> corresponding to display primaries. Ordinary illumination contains many visible <wavelengths>, and the eye combines their remaining contributions through <color vision>. The result is a mixture of colours, often pastel or desaturated.

The layer thickness varies over the bubble because of drainage and flow; the bubble's curvature also changes the local angle of <refraction> and the viewed <optical path difference>. The spectral filter therefore changes continuously across the surface and in time. Very thin regions become dark because the two reflected contributions nearly cancel across the visible <optical spectrum>. <Thin-film interference> explains both the changing colours and this dark limit.

Model the room's decaying <acoustic waves> as a weakly damped <resonator>. The <quality factor from ringdown> is related to stored <energy> $E$ by
$$
Q=\omega\frac{E}{-dE/dt},\qquad E(t)=E(0)e^{-\omega t/Q},\qquad A(t)=A(0)e^{-\omega t/(2Q)},
$$
where $A$ is the pressure <wave amplitude> and $\omega=2\pi f$. The measured <frequency> $f=440\,\mathrm{Hz}$ gives $\omega\simeq2.76\times10^3\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$. If the stated $5\,\mathrm{s}$ is interpreted as an approximate <wave amplitude> <exponential relaxation time>,
$$
\boxed{Q\simeq\frac{\omega\tau_A}{2}=\pi(440\,\mathrm{Hz})(5\,\mathrm{s})\simeq6.9\times10^3,}
$$
an estimate of order $10^4$. Taking $5\,\mathrm{s}$ as an <energy> <exponential relaxation time> instead gives $Q\simeq1.4\times10^4$.

There is a real convention ambiguity: “heard to die away” gives neither an initial sound level nor an audibility threshold, so it does not determine either <exponential relaxation time>. More generally, if the <energy> falls by a factor $E(0)/E(t_d)$ in $t_d$,
$$
Q=\frac{2\pi f t_d}{\ln[E(0)/E(t_d)]}.
$$
In room <acoustics>, a <reverberation time> $T_{60}$ usually denotes a <decibel> drop of $60\,\mathrm{dB}$, or an <energy> factor $10^6$. If $5\,\mathrm{s}$ means $T_{60}$, the corresponding estimate is instead $\boxed{Q\simeq2\pi(440)(5)/(6\ln10)\simeq1.0\times10^3}$. The simple decay-time estimate and the conventional <reverberation time> estimate should not be silently equated.

The three <primitive lattice translation vectors> join a cube corner to the three adjacent face centres. Thus the <Bravais lattice> is a <face-centered cubic lattice>, with conventional cubic side $a$.

Write the given <primitive lattice translation vectors> as $\mathbf a_1,\mathbf a_2,\mathbf a_3$. Their <primitive unit cell> <volume> is
$$
\Omega=\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)=\frac{a^3}{4},\qquad
\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3=\frac{a^2}{4}(-1,1,1).
$$
The <reciprocal lattice> convention $\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf b_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}$ then gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\mathbf b_1&=\frac{2\pi}{a}(-1,1,1),\\
\mathbf b_2&=\frac{2\pi}{a}(1,-1,1),\\
\mathbf b_3&=\frac{2\pi}{a}(1,1,-1).
\end{aligned}}
$$
Each <reciprocal lattice> vector has units $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ when $a$ is in metres. Integer combinations generate a <body-centered cubic lattice> of conventional cubic side $4\pi/a$; the point $(2\pi/a)(1,1,1)$ is its body centre. Directly taking the nine <dot products> checks the signs and the $2\pi$ normalization.

Let $N=L/a$ be the integer number of <primitive unit cells>. A <Bloch state> can be written as $\psi_k(x)=e^{ikx}u_k(x)$, with $u_k(x+a)=u_k(x)$. The <periodic boundary condition> $\psi_k(x+L)=\psi_k(x)$ therefore requires
$$
e^{ikL}=1,\qquad k_j=\frac{2\pi j}{L},\qquad j\in\mathbb Z.
$$
The spacing of allowed <wavevectors> is $\Delta k=2\pi/L$. The first <Brillouin zone> has width $2\pi/a$, so
$$
\boxed{N_k=\frac{2\pi/a}{2\pi/L}=\frac La=N.}
$$
For an exact endpoint convention take a half-open <Brillouin zone>, such as $[-\pi/a,\pi/a)$. Its two endpoints differ by a <reciprocal lattice> vector and must not both be counted as distinct values of <crystal momentum>. There are exactly $N$ inequivalent integers $j$ modulo $N$, irrespective of whether $N$ is even or odd. This is <state counting in a Bloch band>: one allowed <wavevector> per <primitive unit cell> per <energy band>. A separate twofold <spin> multiplicity gives $2N$ electronic states per <energy band>, without doubling the number of <wavevectors>.

Use transverse displacement $y(x,t)$, small slopes and constant <tension> $T$. For a short string element of length $dx$, <Newton's second law> gives $\rho_L dx\,y_{tt}=T[y_x(x+dx)-y_x(x)]$. Hence the <wave equation on a string> is
$$
y_{tt}=c_L^2y_{xx},\qquad c_L=\sqrt{\frac T{\rho_L}},
$$
where $\rho_L$ is the <linear mass density>, in $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}}$.

The <mechanical impedance> of a rightgoing <travelling wave> is the transverse driving <force> divided by the transverse <velocity> at the driven section. Choose the <force> exerted by the material to the left on the material to the right, $F_y=-Ty_x$. For $y=f(x-c_Lt)$, the transverse <velocity> is $v_y=y_t=-c_Ly_x$. Thus the positive <characteristic impedance of a stretched string> is
$$
\boxed{Z_L=\frac{F_y}{v_y}=\frac T{c_L}=\rho_Lc_L=\sqrt{T\rho_L},\qquad [Z_L]=\mathrm{N\,s\,m^{-1}}=\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
A leftgoing component has $F_y/v_y=-Z_L$ with this fixed orientation of <force>. Specifying the orientation avoids confusing that propagation sign with a negative material <mechanical impedance>.

Place the junction at $x=0$ and use the <complex amplitude> convention $e^{-i\omega t}$. With incident displacement <wave amplitude> $A_i$, write
$$
y_L=A_i(e^{ik_Lx}+r e^{-ik_Lx})e^{-i\omega t},\qquad
y_R=A_i t e^{ik_Rx}e^{-i\omega t}.
$$
Here $r$ and $t$ are displacement <wave reflection> and <transmission coefficients>, and $k_j=\omega/c_j$. The junction is assumed massless, with no externally applied transverse <force>, and both strings have the common equilibrium <tension> $T$. Continuity of displacement and transverse <force> gives
$$
1+r=t,\qquad Tk_L(1-r)=Tk_Rt.
$$
Since $Tk_j=\omega Z_j$, these become $1+r=t$ and $Z_L(1-r)=Z_Rt$. Solving,
$$
\boxed{\frac{A_r}{A_i}=r=\frac{Z_L-Z_R}{Z_L+Z_R},\qquad
\frac{A_t}{A_i}=t=\frac{2Z_L}{Z_L+Z_R}.}
$$
A larger right-hand <mechanical impedance> produces a reflected displacement with reversed sign, as expected in the fixed-end limit $Z_R\to\infty$. Equal <mechanical impedances> give $r=0$ and $t=1$.

The instantaneous rightward <energy flux> along a string is the transverse <force> times the transverse <velocity>,
$$
P=-Ty_xy_t.
$$
For a rightgoing <time-harmonic wave> of peak displacement <wave amplitude> $|A|$, averaging over a <wave period> gives
$$
\langle P\rangle=\frac12Tk\omega|A|^2=\frac12Z\omega^2|A|^2.
$$
A leftgoing <time-harmonic wave> has the opposite signed <energy flux>. The positive ratios of reflected and transmitted <power> to incident <power> are therefore
$$
\mathcal R=|r|^2=\frac{(Z_L-Z_R)^2}{(Z_L+Z_R)^2},\qquad
\boxed{\mathcal T=\frac{Z_R}{Z_L}|t|^2=\frac{4Z_LZ_R}{(Z_L+Z_R)^2}.}
$$
All explicit <angular frequency> factors cancel. In the ideal <wave equation on a string>, $T$ and the two <linear mass densities> are constant, so the <mechanical impedances> have no <frequency> or <wavelength> dependence. Consequently $\mathcal T$ is independent of <wavelength> even though the two string <wavelengths> generally differ.

Finally,
$$
\boxed{\mathcal R+\mathcal T=
\frac{(Z_L-Z_R)^2+4Z_LZ_R}{(Z_L+Z_R)^2}=1.}
$$
This is <conservation of energy> at a lossless, massless junction. The transmitted displacement <wave amplitude> can exceed the incident one when $Z_R<Z_L$; the factor $Z_R/Z_L$ is essential when comparing <power>.

Let the middle segment occupy $0<x<l$. Normalize the incident displacement <complex amplitude> to one, suppress the common time factor $e^{-i\omega t}$, and choose
$$
\begin{aligned}
y_L(x)&=e^{ik_Lx}+r e^{-ik_Lx},&&x<0,\\
y_M(x)&=a e^{ik_Mx}+b e^{-ik_Mx},&&0<x<l,\\
y_R(x)&=t e^{ik_R(x-l)},&&x>l.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $r$ is the reflected <complex amplitude> at $0$, $a$ and $b$ are the rightgoing and leftgoing middle-segment <complex amplitudes> referenced to $0$, and $t$ is the outgoing <complex amplitude> referenced to $l$. The middle <wavenumber> is $k_M=\omega/c_M$, and
$$
\gamma=e^{ik_Ml},\qquad |\gamma|=1
$$
is the propagation <phase factor> across the lossless middle segment.

At each junction, continuity of displacement and transverse <force> means continuity of $y$ and $Ty_x$. Using $Tk_j=\omega Z_j$ gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
1+r&=a+b,\\
Z_L(1-r)&=Z_M(a-b),\\
\gamma a+\gamma^{-1}b&=t,\\
Z_M(\gamma a-\gamma^{-1}b)&=Z_Rt.
\end{aligned}}
$$
These <boundary conditions> also include all multiple reflections inside the middle segment: $a$ and $b$ are the total travelling components, not just the first waves to cross a junction.

= Solution

Put $\delta=k_Ml$. Eliminating the middle-segment <complex amplitudes> using the right-hand <boundary conditions> gives
$$
1+r=t\left(\cos\delta-i\frac{Z_R}{Z_M}\sin\delta\right),\qquad
Z_L(1-r)=t\left(Z_R\cos\delta-iZ_M\sin\delta\right).
$$
Hence the displacement <reflection coefficient> is
$$
r=\frac{(Z_L-Z_R)\cos\delta+i(Z_M-Z_LZ_R/Z_M)\sin\delta}
{(Z_L+Z_R)\cos\delta-i(Z_M+Z_LZ_R/Z_M)\sin\delta}.
$$
For finite positive real <mechanical impedances>, its denominator cannot vanish at real $\delta$. The real and imaginary parts of the numerator must therefore vanish separately:
$$
(Z_L-Z_R)\cos\delta=0,\qquad (Z_M^2-Z_LZ_R)\sin\delta=0.
$$
This gives all three possibilities.

If the exterior strings differ, $Z_L\ne Z_R$, then $\cos\delta=0$, so $\sin\delta\ne0$ and
$$
\boxed{Z_M=\sqrt{Z_LZ_R},\qquad l=\frac{(2n+1)\lambda_M}{4},\quad n=0,1,2,\ldots.}
$$
The middle segment is a <quarter-wave impedance transformer>. Its two reflected contributions cancel at these odd quarter-<wavelength> thicknesses. Here $\lambda_M=2\pi/k_M$ is specifically the <wavelength> in the middle string; the matching is not generally valid at every <frequency>.

If $Z_L=Z_R$ but $Z_M$ differs, the second condition requires $\sin\delta=0$, giving
$$
\boxed{l=\frac{n\lambda_M}{2},\quad n=1,2,\ldots.}
$$
A whole number of half-<wavelengths> reproduces the exterior <mechanical impedance> at the input. The formal $n=0$ limit is zero <frequency> or zero segment length, rather than a finite-<wavelength> resonance.

Finally, if $Z_L=Z_M=Z_R$, both conditions hold identically: $\boxed{r=0\text{ at every wavelength}}$. Since the system is lossless, each of these cases also gives unit transmitted <power>.

The expression is a <scalar diffraction theory> integral for a <monochromatic wave>. The complex quantity $\psi_P$ represents a field <complex amplitude>, with physical field obtained by taking the real part after multiplication by $e^{-i\omega t}$. The <light intensity> is proportional to $|\psi_P|^2$. The coordinates $x,y$ label transverse positions in the aperture plane, $\Sigma$ is its transmitting region, and $h(x,y)$ is the incident field multiplied by the aperture's complex transmission. The <wavenumber> is $k=2\pi/\lambda$ in the propagation medium, and $R$ is the axial propagation distance if $h$ already includes the incident field.

To derive the <Fresnel diffraction>, start from <Huygens-Fresnel principle> contributions with propagation factor $e^{iks}/s$. With the coordinate origin at the projection of $P$ into the aperture plane,
$$
s=\sqrt{R^2+x^2+y^2}=R+\frac{x^2+y^2}{2R}-\frac{(x^2+y^2)^2}{8R^3}+\cdots.
$$
The <paraxial approximation> requires $\rho/R\ll1$, where $\rho^2=x^2+y^2$. Replace the slowly varying $1/s$ and the obliquity factor by their axial values, but retain the quadratic variation of <wave phase>. Neglect of the next <wave phase> term requires $k\rho^4/(8R^3)\ll1$ over the relevant aperture. Thus, apart from a common constant,
$$
\psi_P\simeq\frac{e^{ikR}}{i\lambda R}\iint_\Sigma h(x,y)e^{ik(x^2+y^2)/(2R)}\,dx\,dy.
$$
The quadratic <wave phase> may be of order one or much larger; setting it to zero would require the stronger conditions for <Fraunhofer diffraction>. The scalar treatment also neglects the detailed vector field at aperture edges and assumes dimensions large enough compared with <wavelength> for this approximation to be useful.

For a finite-distance axial point source at distance $p$ and an observer at distance $q$, the incident field contributes its own quadratic <wave phase>. If $h$ instead denotes only the aperture transmission, the combined curvature is described by an effective distance
$$
\boxed{\frac1{R_{\rm eff}}=\frac1p+\frac1q,\qquad R_{\rm eff}=\frac{pq}{p+q}.}
$$
These are two equivalent conventions for distributing the incident <wave phase> between $h$ and the exponential. Both distances must be included in the later calculation.

Write $p$ and $q$ for the axial source and observation distances, and put $R_{\rm eff}=pq/(p+q)$. The aperture is $-d/2<x,y<d/2$. Under the <Fresnel diffraction>, the spherical incident <wave amplitude> is effectively constant over this small aperture, while its quadratic <wave phase> must be retained. Therefore the two transverse <integrals> separate:
$$
\psi_P=K\left[\int_{-d/2}^{d/2}\exp\left(\frac{i\pi x^2}{\lambda R_{\rm eff}}\right)dx\right]^2,
$$
where $K$ contains the source strength, the propagation distances and a common <phase factor>.

Use the dimensionless variable $u=x\sqrt{2/(\lambda R_{\rm eff})}$ and define the <optical normalization of Fresnel integrals>
$$
F(w)=C(w)+iS(w)=\int_0^w e^{i\pi u^2/2}\,du,\qquad
w=\frac d2\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda R_{\rm eff}}}.
$$
The oddness of $F$ makes the bracket $\sqrt{2\lambda R_{\rm eff}}F(w)$, so
$$
\boxed{\psi_P=2K\lambda R_{\rm eff}[C(w)+iS(w)]^2.}
$$
This <square-aperture Fresnel diffraction> normalization is especially useful for comparing with no aperture. In the same <Fresnel diffraction>, the unrestricted one-dimensional <Fresnel integral> is $\sqrt{\lambda R_{\rm eff}}e^{i\pi/4}$; its square is $i\lambda R_{\rm eff}$. Thus, writing $\psi_0$ for the unobscured field at the same point,
$$
\boxed{\frac{\psi_P}{\psi_0}=-2i[C(w)+iS(w)]^2,\qquad
\frac{I_P}{I_0}=4[C(w)^2+S(w)^2]^2.}
$$
The normalization has the correct no-aperture limit because $C(\infty)=S(\infty)=1/2$ in this convention.

Let the source remain at $(0,0,-p)$ and let the observation point be $(X,Y,q)$. The <paraxial approximation> to the source-to-aperture-to-observer <optical path length> is
$$
p+q+\frac{x^2+y^2}{2p}+\frac{(X-x)^2+(Y-y)^2}{2q}.
$$
Completing the squares gives
$$
p+q+\frac{X^2+Y^2}{2(p+q)}
+\frac{(x-x_0)^2+(y-y_0)^2}{2R_{\rm eff}},\qquad
x_0=\frac{pX}{p+q},\quad y_0=\frac{pY}{p+q}.
$$
The point $(x_0,y_0)$ is where the straight source-observer line intersects the aperture. Define the four dimensionless edge positions
$$
u_{x,\pm}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda R_{\rm eff}}}\left(\pm\frac d2-x_0\right),\qquad
u_{y,\pm}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda R_{\rm eff}}}\left(\pm\frac d2-y_0\right).
$$
On the <Cornu spiral> $F(u)=C(u)+iS(u)$, draw the directed chord from $F(u_{x,-})$ to $F(u_{x,+})$, and a second directed chord from $F(u_{y,-})$ to $F(u_{y,+})$. Denote their complex differences by $D_x$ and $D_y$. The <complex amplitude> is proportional to their product: multiply their lengths and add their arguments, including the common propagation <phase factor>.

More explicitly, normalize by the unobscured field $\psi_0(X,Y)$ at this same observation point. The constant <wave phase> from completing the square is then already included in $\psi_0$, and
$$
\boxed{\frac{\psi(X,Y)}{\psi_0(X,Y)}=\frac{D_xD_y}{2i},\qquad
\frac{I(X,Y)}{I_0(X,Y)}=\frac{|D_x|^2|D_y|^2}{4}.}
$$
Each directed chord is a one-dimensional <Fresnel diffraction> factor. One must multiply the two factors, rather than add the chords or use just one chord length for the square aperture. Setting $X=Y=0$ recovers $D_x=D_y=2F(w)$ and the on-axis answer.

The crystal has <periodic potential> $V(x+a)=V(x)$. By <Bloch's theorem>, an <energy eigenstate> may be chosen as
$$
\psi_k(x)=e^{ikx}u_k(x),\qquad u_k(x+a)=u_k(x).
$$
Expand the periodic factor in a <Fourier series>:
$$
u_k(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}C_{k,n}e^{inG_1x},\qquad G_1=\frac{2\pi}{a}.
$$
Multiplication by $e^{ikx}$ gives the stated plane-wave expansion. Here $x$ is position, $a$ is the <primitive unit cell> length, $G_1$ is the primitive <reciprocal lattice> <wavenumber>, $n$ runs over every integer, and $C_{k,n}$ are <complex numbers> specifying the component <wave amplitudes>. The <wavevector> $k$ labels the <Bloch state> modulo $G_1$; $\hbar k$ is its <crystal momentum>, rather than generally its measured free-space <momentum>.

The component $e^{i(k+nG_1)x}$ is a <plane wave> of <wavevector> $k+nG_1$. All these components acquire the same <phase factor> $e^{ika}$ under $x\mapsto x+a$, since $e^{inG_1a}=1$. Their mixing therefore respects the crystal's translation symmetry. The coefficients and <energy eigenvalue> follow from the <Schrödinger equation>; the expression is not a claim that the coefficients are equal or that only finitely many are nonzero. The <energy band> index is suppressed in the notation, so different <energy bands> at a common $k$ generally have different coefficient sets.

= Solution

Choose the <Fourier series> convention
$$
V(x)=\sum_{j\in\mathbb Z}V_j e^{ijG_1x},\qquad
V_j=\frac1a\int_0^aV(x)e^{-ijG_1x}\,dx.
$$
For a real <potential energy>, $V_{-1}=V_1^*$. The symbol $V(G_1)$ means the <Fourier coefficient> $V_1$, with units of <energy>, rather than the pointwise value of $V(x)$ at a position $G_1$.

Retain the two <plane waves> $|1\rangle=L^{-1/2}e^{ikx}$ and $|2\rangle=L^{-1/2}e^{i(k-G_1)x}$ in the <nearly-free electron model>. Their kinetic <energies> are $\epsilon_1=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)$ and $\epsilon_2=\hbar^2(k-G_1)^2/(2m)$. The <matrix representation of a linear map> for the <quantum Hamiltonian> projected onto this two-dimensional <basis> is
$$
H=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_1+V_0&V_1\\V_1^*&\epsilon_2+V_0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Thus $\det(H-EI)=0$ gives
$$
(E-V_0-\epsilon_1)(E-V_0-\epsilon_2)-|V_1|^2=0.
$$
Solving this <quadratic equation> yields
$$
\boxed{E_\pm=V_0+\frac{\epsilon_1+\epsilon_2}{2}
\pm\frac12\sqrt{(\epsilon_1-\epsilon_2)^2+4|V_1|^2}.}
$$
Choosing the zero of <potential energy> so that its spatial average is $V_0=0$ reproduces the requested expression. If that average has not been removed, both <energy bands> are shifted by $V_0$; no <band gap>, <group velocity> or <effective mass> is changed by this constant shift.

The two kinetic <energies> coincide at $k=G_1/2$. Their coupling gives an <avoided crossing> with <band gap> $2|V_1|$. The result is exact within the projected two-wave <quantum Hamiltonian>. It is an approximation to the full crystal problem, appropriate near this crossing for a weak <periodic potential> whose other <Fourier coefficients> do not cause strong mixing with remote <plane waves>. Small $|V_1|$ alone would not justify ignoring arbitrarily large other <Fourier coefficients>.

Set $G=G_1$, $\kappa=\Delta k$, $U=|V_1|>0$ and $E_B=\hbar^2G^2/(8m)$. The two kinetic <energies> satisfy
$$
\frac{\epsilon_1+\epsilon_2}{2}=E_B+\frac{\hbar^2\kappa^2}{2m},\qquad
\frac{\epsilon_1-\epsilon_2}{2}=\frac{\hbar^2G\kappa}{2m}.
$$
Consequently the <nearly-free electron dispersion near a one-dimensional band gap> becomes
$$
E_\pm\left(\frac G2+\kappa\right)=V_0+E_B+\frac{\hbar^2\kappa^2}{2m}
\pm\sqrt{U^2+\left(\frac{\hbar^2G\kappa}{2m}\right)^2}.
$$
The <Taylor expansion> $\sqrt{U^2+s^2}=U+s^2/(2U)+O(s^4/U^3)$ gives
$$
\boxed{E_\pm=V_0+E_B\pm U+
\left(\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\pm\frac{\hbar^4G^2}{8m^2U}\right)(\Delta k)^2
+O((\Delta k)^4).}
$$
For the paper's <energy> convention, set $V_0=0$. There is no linear or cubic term because each branch is an even function of displacement from the crossing in this two-wave approximation. Each coefficient multiplying $(\Delta k)^2$ has units of <energy> times length squared, as required.

The expansion requires
$$
\left|\frac{\hbar^2G\Delta k}{2m}\right|\ll U,
$$
a more restrictive condition than merely $|\Delta k|\ll G$ when the <band gap> is small. If $U=0$, the square root is proportional to $|\Delta k|$ and this analytic expansion of the energy-ordered branches is unavailable.

Twice the quadratic coefficient from the previous part is the <band curvature> $E_\pm''(G_1/2)$. Thus the <band effective masses> are
$$
\frac1{m_\pm^*}=\frac1{\hbar^2}E_\pm''\left(\frac{G_1}2\right)
=\frac1m\pm\frac{\hbar^2G_1^2}{4m^2U},\qquad
\boxed{m_\pm^*=\frac{m}{1\pm\hbar^2G_1^2/(4mU)}=\frac{m}{1\pm2E_B/U}.}
$$
Here $U=|V(G_1)|$ and $E_B=\hbar^2G_1^2/(8m)$. In the weak-potential regime $U\ll E_B$,
$$
\boxed{m_+^*\simeq+\frac{4m^2U}{\hbar^2G_1^2},\qquad
m_-^*\simeq-\frac{4m^2U}{\hbar^2G_1^2}.}
$$
Both have units of <mass>; their magnitudes are much smaller than $m$ in this asymptotic regime. The upper <energy band> has a local minimum and positive <effective mass>, while the lower <energy band> has a local maximum and negative <effective mass>. The exact lower-branch sign changes at $U=2E_B$ in the two-wave formula; the intended weak-coupling limit lies below that threshold.

The physical meaning follows from the semiclassical equations for an <electron> <wave packet> in one <energy band>:
$$
v=\frac1\hbar\frac{dE}{dk},\qquad \hbar\dot k=F,\qquad \dot v=\frac F{m^*}.
$$
An upper-band <electron> near its minimum accelerates in the direction of the applied <force>, whereas a lower-band <electron> near its maximum accelerates opposite to it. At the extremum itself the <group velocity> is zero. A nearly filled lower <energy band> is conveniently described by <electron holes> with positive <electric charge> $+e$ and positive <effective mass> $m_h^*=-m_-^*$. Negative curvature is a response of a crystal <energy eigenstate>, not a negative bare <electron mass>. These statements assume slow forcing that does not induce transitions across the <band gap>.

= Solution

In a <metal>, a partially filled <energy band> or overlapping <energy bands> supplies <electrons> with nearby unoccupied states. At <thermal equilibrium>, oppositely directed <group velocities> are equally populated and the net <electric current> vanishes. An applied <electric field> distorts the occupation distribution, producing a small mean <drift velocity>. A filled <energy band> alone has no such low-field intraband response because its velocity contributions cancel over the entire <Brillouin zone>.

In the <Drude model>, let $n$ be the mobile <electron> <number density>, let $e>0$ be the <elementary charge>, and let $\tau$ be the <momentum relaxation time>. The mean motion obeys
$$
m\frac{d\mathbf v_d}{dt}=-e\mathbf E-\frac{m\mathbf v_d}{\tau}.
$$
The steady state is $\mathbf v_d=-e\tau\mathbf E/m$. The <current density> is opposite to the <electron> <drift velocity>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf J=-ne\mathbf v_d=\sigma\mathbf E,\qquad
\sigma=\frac{ne^2\tau}{m},\qquad \rho_{\rm el}=\frac{m}{ne^2\tau}.}
$$
This gives microscopic <Ohm's law>, with <electrical conductivity> $\sigma$ in $\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}$ and <electrical resistivity> $\rho_{\rm el}$ in $\Omega\,\mathrm m$. For a uniform wire of length $\ell$ and cross-sectional <area> $A$, its <electrical resistance> is $R=\rho_{\rm el}\ell/A$. The dissipated <power> per <volume> is $\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E=\sigma E^2$, the <Joule heating> supplied by the external source.

The <free electron model> with <Fermi-Dirac statistics> improves the microscopic picture. The <Pauli exclusion principle> fills states up to the <Fermi energy> at low <temperature>. For a spherical, parabolic <energy band> with two <spin> states,
$$
k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3},\qquad E_F=\frac{\hbar^2k_F^2}{2m},\qquad v_F=\frac{\hbar k_F}{m}.
$$
Only states near the <Fermi surface> change their occupation in a weak-field response; the large equilibrium <Fermi velocity> is not the small <drift velocity>. The same $ne^2\tau/m$ formula results for this isotropic parabolic model when $\tau$ is evaluated for transport near the <Fermi surface>. In a crystal, the <energy band> <group velocity>, <effective mass> and possibly direction-dependent scattering replace the simplest bare-particle picture. The <mean free path> is approximately $\ell_{\rm mfp}=v_F\tau$.

A perfectly periodic static lattice is already included in <Bloch states> and does not by itself give a finite <electrical resistivity>. <Momentum> relaxation comes from defects, impurities, boundaries, and <electron-phonon scattering>. Independent scattering mechanisms approximately add their rates, giving <Matthiessen's rule>,
$$
\frac1\tau\simeq\frac1{\tau_{\rm imp}}+\frac1{\tau_{\rm ph}}+\cdots,\qquad
\rho_{\rm el}(T)\simeq\rho_0+\rho_{\rm ph}(T).
$$
The approximately temperature-independent $\rho_0$ is the <residual resistivity>. In ordinary simple metals, the phonon contribution is approximately proportional to <temperature> at high <temperature>; a three-dimensional acoustic-phonon transport model gives a $T^5$ contribution well below the relevant <Debye temperature>. Other mechanisms can dominate in particular materials or regimes, so neither power law is universal.

The classical <Drude model> incorrectly treats the equilibrium carrier statistics. <Fermi-Dirac statistics> explains the small <electronic heat capacity>, while electrons carrying both charge and heat give the approximate <Wiedemann-Franz law> $\kappa_e/(\sigma T)=\pi^2k_B^2/(3e^2)$ for degenerate carriers with appropriate nearly elastic scattering. Both the simple <electrical conductivity> formula and this thermal relation depend on the assumptions about <energy bands> and scattering, rather than being exact for all <metals>.

The <Debye model> approximates a three-dimensional crystal as an elastic continuum supporting quantized <normal modes>, or <phonons>. At long <wavelengths>, the <acoustic phonon branches> have approximately linear <dispersion relations> $\omega_s(k)=v_s k$: one longitudinal branch and two transverse branches. Replace their complicated actual spectra by these linear forms, then introduce a cutoff chosen to preserve the total $3N$ vibrational degrees of freedom for $N$ atoms. This is an approximation to the spectrum, not a claim that a real crystal has no non-acoustic branches.

For <periodic boundary conditions> in <volume> $V$, each allowed <wavevector> occupies $(2\pi)^3/V$ in the space of <wavevectors>. Counting a spherical shell for each branch gives the <density of states> per unit <angular frequency>
$$
g(\omega)=\frac{V\omega^2}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac1{v_l^3}+\frac2{v_t^3}\right).
$$
An effective <speed of sound> $v_D$ can be defined by $3/v_D^3=1/v_l^3+2/v_t^3$. In the single-speed <Debye model>, choose $\omega_D=v_Dk_D$ so that
$$
\int_0^{\omega_D}g(\omega)\,d\omega=3N.
$$
Thus the <Debye wavevector>, <Debye frequency> and <Debye temperature> satisfy
$$
\boxed{k_D=\left(\frac{6\pi^2N}{V}\right)^{1/3},\qquad
\omega_D=v_Dk_D,\qquad \Theta_D=\frac{\hbar\omega_D}{k_B},\qquad
g(\omega)=\frac{9N\omega^2}{\omega_D^3}\quad(0<\omega<\omega_D).}
$$
Here $g$ has units of seconds when <angular frequency> is measured in $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$; its integral is the dimensionless number of <normal modes>. The spherical cutoff replaces the real <Brillouin zone> by an equal-count continuum construction.

Each <phonon> mode is a <quantum harmonic oscillator>. Since <phonon> number is not conserved at <thermal equilibrium>, the <chemical potential> is zero and its <Bose-Einstein distribution> gives mean thermal <energy> $\hbar\omega/[e^{\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}-1]$. The <zero-point energy> is independent of <temperature> in the harmonic, fixed-volume approximation and does not contribute to the <heat capacity at constant volume>. Excluding that constant term,
$$
U(T)=\int_0^{\omega_D}\frac{g(\omega)\hbar\omega}{e^{\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}-1}\,d\omega
=9Nk_BT\left(\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\right)^3
\int_0^{\Theta_D/T}\frac{x^3}{e^x-1}\,dx.
$$
Differentiating the first <integral> at fixed <volume> avoids losing the temperature-dependent upper limit in the second one. The resulting <Debye heat capacity> is
$$
\boxed{C_V=9Nk_B\left(\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\right)^3
\int_0^{\Theta_D/T}\frac{x^4e^x}{(e^x-1)^2}\,dx.}
$$
For $T\ll\Theta_D$, extend the upper limit to infinity. Expanding $1/(e^x-1)=\sum_{r\ge1}e^{-rx}$ and integrating term by term gives $\int_0^\infty x^3/(e^x-1)\,dx=6\zeta(4)=\pi^4/15$. Therefore
$$
U\simeq\frac{3\pi^4}{5}Nk_B\frac{T^4}{\Theta_D^3},\qquad
\boxed{C_V\simeq\frac{12\pi^4}{5}Nk_B\left(\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\right)^3.}
$$
This $T^3$ law follows from the three-dimensional $\omega^2$ <density of states> and the availability only of low-<frequency> modes at low <temperature>. It is a central success of the <Debye model>.

For $T\gg\Theta_D$, the <Bose-Einstein distribution> reduces to the classical <equipartition theorem>: every mode contributes thermal <energy> $k_BT$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{U\simeq3Nk_BT,\qquad C_V\longrightarrow3Nk_B,}
$$
the <Dulong-Petit law>, or $3R$ per mole of atoms. The simple model omits detailed <phonon dispersion relation>, distinct <optical phonon branches>, and anharmonic effects such as <thermal expansion>. In a <metal>, the low-<temperature> total <heat capacity> additionally contains a term approximately proportional to $T$ from <electrons>, so the lattice $T^3$ term should not be mistaken for the whole measurement.
