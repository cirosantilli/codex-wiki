= Solution
{parent=1}

Write the one-electron Coulomb energies as $E_n=-C/n^2$, $C>0$. The <Pauli exclusion principle> allows $2n^2$ <electrons> in shell $n$, including <spin>. The ground configuration therefore has two <electrons> in $n=1$ and seven in $n=2$, giving $E_g=-C(2+7/4)=-15C/4$. The least energy-increasing promotion is from $n=2$ to $n=3$:
$$
E_e=-C\left(2+\frac64+\frac19\right)=-\frac{65C}{18},\qquad
\boxed{\frac{E_e}{E_g}=\frac{26}{27}.}
$$
Rearranging the partly occupied degenerate $n=2$ shell does not produce a higher <energy>. Both total energies are negative, so this ratio is below one even though $E_e>E_g$.

Choose the centre of the <finite square well> as the origin and its edges as $\pm a$. The first three bound <wavefunctions> have **even, odd, even <parity>**, with respectively **zero, one and two interior <nodes>**. They oscillate inside and decay exponentially outside, with $\kappa_n=\sqrt{2m(U-E_n)}/\hbar$. Both the <wavefunction> and its first <derivative> are continuous at each finite potential step. The higher <bound states> have longer external decay <lengths> because they lie closer to the barrier height. Overall sign is arbitrary.

Confinement to <length> $L$ gives a <momentum> scale $p\sim\hbar/L$ by the <Heisenberg uncertainty principle>. Using the alpha-particle <mass> $m_\alpha\simeq6.64\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}$, its speed scale is $v\sim\hbar/(m_\alpha L)$. Counting encounters with either wall,
$$
\boxed{\nu\sim\frac vL\sim\frac{\hbar}{m_\alpha L^2}
\simeq1.6\times10^{20}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}.}
$$
This is an order-of-magnitude collision-rate estimate, not a classical trajectory of a stationary <quantum state>. Taking $\Delta x\Delta p=\hbar/2$, a different geometric interpretation of the well size, or successive returns to the same wall changes the coefficient by factors of order unity; the robust scale is $10^{20}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$.

= Solution
{parent=B6}

Set $k=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$ and $\kappa=\sqrt{2m(U-E)}/\hbar$. For unit incident <amplitude>, use $\psi=e^{ikx}+r e^{-ikx}$ on the left and $\psi=t_s e^{-\kappa x}$ on the right; the growing exponential is excluded at infinity. Continuity at zero gives $1+r=t_s$ and $ik(1-r)=-\kappa t_s$. Thus
$$
r=\frac{k-i\kappa}{k+i\kappa}=e^{i\delta},\qquad |r|^2=1,
\qquad\boxed{\delta=-2\arctan(\kappa/k)=-2\arccos\sqrt{E/U}.}
$$
The phase is chosen in $(-\pi,0)$ for $0<E<U$. The <evanescent wave> is $\psi(x)=2k\,e^{-\kappa x}/(k+i\kappa)$ for $x>0$. Its <probability density> is nonzero, but its <probability current> $j=(\hbar/m)\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\psi')$ is zero. Penetration into a forbidden region therefore does not require transmitted flux through a semi-infinite step.

For a finite rectangular barrier, matching both interfaces gives
$$
\mathcal T=\left[1+\frac{U^2}{4E(U-E)}\sinh^2(\kappa a)\right]^{-1}
\simeq\frac{16E(U-E)}{U^2}e^{-2\kappa a}.
$$
The approximation requires **$\kappa a\gg1$**, or a width much greater than the evanescent penetration <length>. The tunnelling exponent is $-2a\sqrt{2m(U-E)}/\hbar$; its factor of two converts an <amplitude> decay to a <probability> decay. For the <electron> parameters, $\kappa=1.984\times10^9\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ and $\kappa a=19.84$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathcal T\simeq3e^{-39.68}=1.75\times10^{-17}.}
$$
Keeping only the exponential gives $5.83\times10^{-18}$; the matched-interface prefactor accounts for the factor of three.

= Solution
{parent=B7}

In <spherical coordinates>, the angular <Laplacian> has <spherical harmonics> as <eigenfunctions>, $L^2Y_{lm}=\hbar^2l(l+1)Y_{lm}$ and $L_zY_{lm}=\hbar mY_{lm}$. Separation leaves
$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left[R''+\frac2rR'-\frac{l(l+1)}{r^2}R\right]-\frac{C}{r}R=ER,
\qquad C=\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}.
$$
Regularity and normalizability give <principal quantum number> $n=1,2,\ldots$, orbital <quantum number> $l=0,\ldots,n-1$, and <magnetic quantum number> $m=-l,\ldots,l$. Here the $m$ in the kinetic coefficient is the <electron mass>, distinct from the magnetic label.

For the proposed angular factor, $\cos\theta\propto Y_{10}$, so $l=1$ and <magnetic quantum number> zero. Direct differentiation gives $\nabla^2(re^{-\alpha r}\cos\theta)=(\alpha^2-4\alpha/r)\psi$. The $1/r$ term in the <Schrödinger equation> vanishes when $2\hbar^2\alpha/m=C$. With <Bohr radius> $a_0=\hbar^2/(mC)$,
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac1{2a_0},\qquad E=-\frac{\hbar^2\alpha^2}{2m}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{8ma_0^2}\simeq-3.40\,\mathrm{eV},\qquad l=1.}
$$
<wavefunction normalization> uses $\int d\Omega\cos^2\theta=4\pi/3$ and $\int_0^\infty r^4e^{-2\alpha r}dr=24/(2\alpha)^5$, giving
$$
\boxed{A=\sqrt{\alpha^5/\pi}=\frac1{4\sqrt{2\pi}\,a_0^{5/2}}.}
$$
An arbitrary constant phase is allowed. This is the $2p_z$ state. Under spatial inversion, $r$ is unchanged and $\cos\theta$ changes sign, so its <parity> <eigenvalue> is **$-1$**, also given by $(-1)^l$.

Rotational invariance <means> rotated states have the same <energy>; it does not require each member of a degenerate <energy> eigenspace to be invariant. <rotations> mix the three $l=1$ magnetic substates. An orbital oriented along $z$ can therefore have an anisotropic <probability density> while the <Hamiltonian> remains spherically symmetric.

= B8
{parent=Section B}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=B8}

The <angular momentum commutation relations> are $[L_x,L_y]=i\hbar L_z$ and cyclic permutations. The product rule yields $[L_z,L_x^2]=i\hbar(L_yL_x+L_xL_y)$ and $[L_z,L_y^2]=-i\hbar(L_xL_y+L_yL_x)$, so **$[L_z,L^2]=0$**. Simultaneous <eigenstates> have <eigenvalues> $\hbar^2l(l+1)$ and $\hbar m$; <orbital angular momentum> has integer $l\ge0$ and $m=-l,\ldots,l$.

By cyclic <symmetry>, $L^2$ commutes with every component and hence every squared component. Therefore $[H,L^2]=0$: an <energy> eigenbasis can be chosen with definite $l$. For unequal transverse coefficients, $L_z$ need not commute with $H$, so definite $m$ is not generally available.

Expanding the <ladder operators> gives $L_+^2+L_-^2=2(L_x^2-L_y^2)$ and $L^2-L_z^2=L_x^2+L_y^2$. Comparing coefficients gives
$$
\boxed{\alpha=\frac{a_x-a_y}{4},\qquad\beta=\frac{a_x+a_y}{2},\qquad\gamma=a_z.}
$$
For $l=1,m=0$, both squared <ladder operators> vanish, since two raising or lowering steps would leave the multiplet. Thus $E_0=2\beta\hbar^2=\hbar^2(a_x+a_y)$. In the remaining basis $\{|1,1\rangle,|1,-1\rangle\}$ the <Hamiltonian> is
$$
\hbar^2\begin{pmatrix}\beta+\gamma&2\alpha\\2\alpha&\beta+\gamma\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its symmetric and antisymmetric combinations have energies $\hbar^2(a_x+a_z)$ and $\hbar^2(a_y+a_z)$, respectively. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\{E_{l=1}\}=\hbar^2\{a_x+a_y,\ a_y+a_z,\ a_z+a_x\}.}
$$
The energies are related by permutation of the principal axes, not generally equal. Two coincide when the corresponding coefficients coincide; all three become $2a\hbar^2$ in the isotropic limit.

= Solution
{parent=B9}

Use $[x,p]=i\hbar$. Acting on a smooth test <wavefunction>, $[f(x),p]\psi=f(-i\hbar\psi')+i\hbar(f\psi)'=i\hbar f'\psi$. Take $f$ real and use the <self-adjoint operator> domain with vanishing integration-by-parts boundary terms. Writing $Q=p-if$ gives
$$
H=\frac{Q^\dagger Q}{2m}=\frac{p^2+f^2-\hbar f'}{2m},
\qquad\boxed{V(x)=\frac{f(x)^2-\hbar f'(x)}{2m}.}
$$
The <energy> form is $\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle=\|Q\psi\|^2/(2m)\ge0$, so every normalizable <energy eigenstate> has $E\ge0$. If a normalizable zero-energy state exists, its nonnegative norm must vanish, $Q\psi_0=0$. Hence
$$
\hbar\psi_0'+f\psi_0=0,\qquad
\boxed{\psi_0(x)=N\exp\left[-\frac1\hbar\int_0^xf(t)dt\right].}
$$
Zero <energy> is an additional existence assumption, not a consequence of factorization alone.

For $\psi_1=Q^\dagger\psi_0$, the <commutator> $[Q,Q^\dagger]=2\hbar f'$ gives
$$
H\psi_1=\frac\hbar m Q^\dagger(f'\psi_0)
=-\frac{i\hbar^2}{m}f''\psi_0+\frac\hbar m f'\psi_1.
$$
Thus **$\alpha(x)=-i\hbar^2f''(x)/m$ and $\beta(x)=\hbar f'(x)/m$** in the requested relation. Generally these are multiplication functions, not constant coefficients, and $\psi_1$ is not automatically an <energy eigenstate>.

If $f(x)=cx^n+\cdots$, its integral has leading term $cx^{n+1}/(n+1)$. Decay at both infinities on the full real line requires **odd $n$ and positive <leading coefficient> $c$**. Odd degree alone is not sufficient. For $f=kx$ take $k=m\omega>0$. Then $H=H_{\rm SHO}-\hbar\omega/2$, $\alpha=0$ and $\beta=\hbar\omega$. After normalization and removal of an irrelevant phase,
$$
\boxed{\psi_0=\left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4}e^{-m\omega x^2/(2\hbar)},\quad
\psi_1=\sqrt{\frac{2m\omega}{\hbar}}\,x\psi_0,\quad
E_0^{\rm SHO}=\frac12\hbar\omega,\quad E_1^{\rm SHO}=\frac32\hbar\omega.}
$$
The corresponding factorized-Hamiltonian energies are zero and $\hbar\omega$; the constant shift must be restored for the stated oscillator <Hamiltonian>.

= Solution

Each electromagnetic <normal mode> is a <quantum harmonic oscillator> with $E_n=(n+1/2)h\nu$. For $x=h\nu/(k_BT)$ its <canonical partition function> and occupation <probabilities> are
$$
Z_\nu=\frac{e^{-x/2}}{1-e^{-x}},\qquad
P_n=(1-e^{-x})e^{-nx},\qquad
\langle n\rangle=\frac1{e^x-1}.
$$
The <geometric distribution> is normalized and gives thermal mode <energy> $h\nu/(e^x-1)$. The zero-point term $h\nu/2$ is not part of the thermally emitted black-body <intensity>. The equilibrium <photon> <chemical potential> is zero because <photon number> is not conserved.

Counting two transverse <polarizations> gives $8\pi\nu^2/c^3$ modes per volume per <frequency> interval. Thus the spectral <energy density> is $u_\nu=8\pi h\nu^3/[c^3(e^x-1)]$. In an isotropic radiation field, <spectral radiance> is $B_\nu=cu_\nu/(4\pi)$. Transforming the spectral measure, $B_\lambda=B_\nu|d\nu/d\lambda|$, gives
$$
\boxed{B_\lambda(T)=\frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\frac1{e^{hc/(\lambda k_BT)}-1}.}
$$
Here <intensity> <means> <radiance> per <wavelength> and solid angle; the hemispheric spectral exitance of a black surface is $M_\lambda=\pi B_\lambda$. The extra Jacobian is essential: the <wavelength> peak is not found by simply converting the <frequency> peak using $\lambda=c/\nu$.

At short <wavelength> the exponential suppresses radiation. At long <wavelength> the Rayleigh-Jeans limit is $B_\lambda\simeq2ck_BT/\lambda^4$; using classical equipartition at every <frequency> instead would produce the <ultraviolet catastrophe>. The <wavelength> maximum solves $5(1-e^{-x})=x$, with nonzero root $x=4.9651$, yielding <Wien's displacement law>. For the specified <temperature>,
$$
\boxed{\lambda_{\max}=\frac{2.898\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m\,K}}{2.7\,\mathrm K}
\simeq1.07\,\mathrm{mm}.}
$$
Integrating the hemispheric flux gives the Stefan-Boltzmann law, $M=\sigma T^4\simeq3.01\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. The plot uses the specified ideal <temperature>, rather than substituting a later measured value. The physical black-body description of the <cosmic microwave background> is tested by comparing the sky spectrum with an absolute reference blackbody and separating instrumental and Galactic contributions.<the-cosmic-microwave-background-spectrum-from-the-full-cobe-firas-data-set-by-dale-j-fixsen-and-others>[abstract; FIRAS comparison with a precise blackbody and limits on spectral residuals]{ref}

For a normalized <top-hat wavefunction> of width $L$ centred at zero, $\psi(x)=L^{-1/2}$ inside the interval and zero outside. Its <position> <distribution> has $\langle x\rangle=0$ and $\Delta x=L/\sqrt{12}$. With Fourier convention $(2\pi\hbar)^{-1/2}$, the <momentum> <amplitude> is
$$
\widetilde\psi(p)=\sqrt{\frac L{2\pi\hbar}}\,
\operatorname{sinc}\left(\frac{pL}{2\hbar}\right),\qquad
\operatorname{sinc}z=\frac{\sin z}{z}.
$$
The <momentum> density has long oscillatory $1/p^2$ tails. Consequently $\int p^2|\widetilde\psi|^2dp$ diverges: **the ideal discontinuous top hat has infinite r.m.s. <momentum> spread**. Its symmetric <momentum> density has formal principal-value <mean> zero, but no finite absolute first moment either. The finite-variance form $\Delta x\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$ is therefore not saturated and cannot be evaluated using the width of the central diffraction lobe as $\Delta p$.

The first <momentum> zeros occur at $p=\pm2\pi\hbar/L$, showing the inverse-width scale. Smoothing the edges sufficiently gives a finite <momentum> <variance> and restores the ordinary finite-variance uncertainty comparison; sharper edges require larger high-momentum components. It is the ideal jump, not a violation of the uncertainty principle, that causes the divergence.

= Solution
{parent=b}

In a <classically forbidden region> the local <wavevector> becomes imaginary. For a slowly varying barrier, the WKB <transmission probability> has leading exponential
$$
\mathcal T\sim e^{-2G},\qquad
G=\frac1\hbar\int_{r_1}^{r_2}\sqrt{2\mu[V(r)-Q]}\,dr,
$$
where $r_1,r_2$ are turning points and $\mu$ is the <reduced mass>. This is a <probability> exponent; the wave <amplitude> has only half that exponent. Near a turning point one uses matching formulas rather than the unmodified local WKB expression.

For <alpha decay>, an attractive nuclear interior supports a quasibound state behind the repulsive <Coulomb barrier> of the daughter nucleus. For zero <orbital angular momentum>, write the exterior potential as $C/r$, $C=2Z_de^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$, with nuclear matching radius $R$ and outer turning point $b=C/Q>R$. The exterior <action> can be integrated:
$$
G=\frac{\sqrt{2\mu Q}\,b}{\hbar}
\left[\arccos\sqrt{R/b}-\sqrt{(R/b)(1-R/b)}\right].
$$
The decay rate is approximately $\lambda=\nu P_{\rm pre}e^{-2G}$ and the <half-life> is $\ln2/\lambda$. Here $\nu$ is the interior assault-frequency scale and $P_{\rm pre}$ is the alpha preformation <probability>. They are model-dependent; barrier penetration alone does not determine them. Nonzero <orbital angular momentum> adds a <centrifugal barrier>.

For $R/b\ll1$, the leading dependence is $G\propto Z_d/\sqrt Q$. Thus a modest increase in decay <energy> can cause an enormous increase in decay rate, giving the <Geiger-Nuttall law> relating logarithmic <half-life> to inverse square-root <energy> along a suitable isotope sequence. Tunnelling conserves the total <energy>: the escaping <alpha particle> does not acquire <energy> from the barrier.

= Solution

<electron spin> is intrinsic <angular momentum>, independent of an orbital <wavefunction>'s $\mathbf r\times\mathbf p$. It has $s=1/2$, so
$$
\boxed{S^2=\frac34\hbar^2,\qquad S_z=\pm\frac\hbar2,\qquad
S_i=\frac\hbar2\sigma_i,\qquad[S_i,S_j]=i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}S_k.}
$$
A spatial orbital therefore carries a two-component <spinor> degree of freedom. Noncommuting components do not describe an ordinary <vector> with simultaneously definite projections; measurements along one axis give only the two allowed values. Even a spherically symmetric $l=0$ orbital retains <electron spin>.

The associated magnetic moment is $\boldsymbol\mu=-g_e e\mathbf S/(2m_e)$ with $e>0$ and $g_e\simeq2$. A <spinor> changes sign under a $2\pi$ <rotation> and returns under $4\pi$; that sign is observable only relative to another coherent <amplitude>, not as an isolated <global phase>. A literal classically rotating charged sphere does not account for this fixed half-integer <representation>, component algebra and <spinor> <rotation> law. Classical <angular momentum> provides useful analogies for torque and precession, but no ordinary classical orientation reproduces the full intrinsic quantum <spin> structure.

Define $s_i=\sin[2\pi(t_i-T)/P+\pi]$ and use the independent quoted Gaussian errors $\sigma=10\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, with period and <exoplanet transit> epoch fixed. The model is linear in $K,V_0$ despite its sinusoidal <time> dependence. Minimize $\chi^2=\sum_i(V_i-Ks_i-V_0)^2/\sigma^2$. With $N=5$ the <normal equations> are
$$
\begin{pmatrix}\sum s_i^2&\sum s_i\\\sum s_i&N\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}K\\V_0\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}\sum s_iV_i\\\sum V_i\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Using $\sum s_i=-0.741472$ and $\sum s_i^2=2.824633$, the solution is
$$
\boxed{K=59.26\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},\qquad
V_0=-15348.41\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The residuals in observation order are $(3.67,11.01,2.74,-7.90,-9.51)\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Therefore $\chi^2_{\min}=2.950$ for $5-2=3$ <degrees of freedom>, giving reduced $\chi^2=0.983$. The upper-tail <probability> is about $0.40$: **the fit is consistent with the stated errors**, although five points do not establish a unique physical explanation.

For known errors the <covariance matrix> is $\sigma^2(X^TX)^{-1}$. Equivalently, profiling out the fitted offset gives
$$
\sigma_K^2=\frac{\sigma^2}{\sum_i(s_i-\bar s)^2},\qquad
\boxed{\sigma_K=6.07\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
This is the $\Delta\chi^2=1$ one-parameter uncertainty, not the curvature with $V_0$ incorrectly held fixed. Propagating it through the supplied <mass> relation, and treating its coefficient and $P$ as fixed,
$$
\boxed{m_p=(0.347\pm0.035)\,M_J.}
$$
The symbol $\pm nP$ in the <exoplanet transit> epoch denotes periodic copies of the same <exoplanet transit> phase, not an uncertainty <distribution> to propagate. No independent error for the empirical <mass> coefficient was supplied.

Additional independent observations retain positive weight even with twice the error: each has one quarter of the previous per-observation weight. Their information adds to the existing information <matrix>, so the formal <mass> uncertainty **decreases for informative additional orbital phases and cannot increase** under this fixed-error model. It can remain unchanged in a degenerate phase configuration that supplies no further <amplitude> information after fitting the offset. The conclusion assumes the same correct model and independent errors, not a new fitted error rescaling or unmodelled systematic effects.

= Solution
{parent=a}

In an <operational-amplifier negative feedback> loop, an <operational amplifier> has large <open-loop gain> $A$, with $V_o=A(V_+-V_-)$. Feeding a fraction $\beta V_o$ back to the inverting input opposes an output increase. For a non-inverting <signal> input,
$$
\boxed{\frac{V_o}{V_i}=\frac A{1+A\beta}\simeq\frac1\beta\quad\text{when }|A\beta|\gg1.}
$$
Its sensitivity to <open-loop gain> changes is reduced by $d\ln A_{\rm cl}/d\ln A=1/(1+A\beta)$. Thus <resistor> ratios can set a reproducible gain despite device variation; feedback also suppresses internal distortion and reduces <output impedance>. Gain is traded for usable <bandwidth>, subject to the amplifier's <frequency> response.

For the non-inverting connection, place $R_f$ between output and inverting input and $R_g$ between that input and ground. Then $\beta=R_g/(R_f+R_g)$, giving gain $1+R_f/R_g$. For an inverting connection, ground the non-inverting input, inject the <signal> through $R_{\rm in}$ and return $R_f$ from output to the inverting <node>. Small differential input and negligible input current then give gain $-R_f/R_{\rm in}$. The near-zero <node> is a <virtual ground>, not a physical ground connection.

The feedback must remain stable at the <frequencies> where <loop gain> matters. Extra phase lag can turn the intended opposition into oscillation; adequate <phase margin>, supply headroom, input common-mode range, output-current capability and <slew rate> are required. Feedback neither prevents clipping nor removes all input <noise>.<Operational-amplifier negative feedback by Analog Devices>[Chapter 3, §§3.2.2–3.2.4, gain stability, loop gain and frequency dependence]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=b}

A <lock-in amplifier> multiplies the measured <signal> by a synchronized reference and <low-pass filters> the product. For $s(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)+n(t)$ and reference $2\cos\omega t$, the <signal> product is $A\cos\phi+A\cos(2\omega t+\phi)$. Averaging away the second harmonic gives $X=A\cos\phi$ in this peak-amplitude convention. A second reference $-2\sin\omega t$ gives $Y=A\sin\phi$, so
$$
\boxed{A=\sqrt{X^2+Y^2},\qquad\phi=\operatorname{atan2}(Y,X).}
$$
A single quadrature could read zero for a substantial <signal> $90^\circ$ from its reference. Two quadratures avoid that blind spot; the phase is undefined at zero <amplitude>. Instrument RMS calibrations can include a different constant normalization.

The reference shifts the wanted <frequency> to zero, where a narrow low-pass <bandwidth> can select it. Uncorrelated broadband <noise> averages down, whereas a simple amplifier would enlarge <signal> and <noise> together. Longer averaging improves narrow-band sensitivity but slows the response to changing <amplitude>. The <signal> must remain phase coherent with the reference; <frequency> mismatch creates a beat instead of a constant output. <interference> at the same reference <frequency> survives, so shielding and independent background measurements remain necessary.<Model SR830 DSP Lock-In Amplifier manual by Stanford Research Systems>[printed pp.3-1–3-3, multiplication, dual-phase detection and RMS-versus-peak conventions]{ref}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Sampling at $t_n=n/f_s$ makes a <frequency> $f$ indistinguishable from $f+kf_s$, since the extra phase is $2\pi kn$. For a real <sinusoid>, reflection to $|kf_s-f|$ also gives an alias, up to phase. For example, a $50\,\mathrm{Hz}$ cosine sampled at $80\,\mathrm{Hz}$ has exactly the samples of a $30\,\mathrm{Hz}$ cosine. A measured low-frequency feature can therefore originate above the instrument's <Nyquist frequency>.

To reconstruct an arbitrary baseband <signal> limited to <bandwidth> $B$, sample strictly above $2B$ and use an analogue <anti-aliasing filter> before the sampler. A practical filter needs a transition band, so merely choosing a nominal rate $2B$ is insufficient. <frequency> components already folded together cannot in general be separated by later digital filtering. <aliasing> from insufficient sampling is distinct from <spectral leakage> caused by a finite observation window.
