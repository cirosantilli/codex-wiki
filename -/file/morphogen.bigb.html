= Morphogen
{wiki}

A <morphogen> is a signal whose spatial <concentration> helps specify different <biological cell> fates or behaviors during <biological development>. A <morphogen gradient> provides a positional cue: <biological cells> at different distances from a source experience different local <concentrations> and can follow different developmental pathways. A spatial <concentration> variation alone does not establish that a substance is a <morphogen>; its graded developmental effects matter. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26825/[Alberts et al., Molecular Biology of the Cell, 4th edition (2002): morphogens as graded developmental signals].

The beautiful idea is that one distributed signal can help organize several regions of a developing body. A continuous <morphogen gradient> can supply the input for distinct domains of <gene expression>, rather than requiring a separate signal for every position. A <morphogen response threshold> is a useful idealization of that interpretation: high, intermediate and low <concentrations> are assigned different outcomes. The <morphogen reaction-diffusion equation> describes one way to generate a <morphogen gradient>; the equation is a model of transport and reaction, while the <morphogen> is the signal being transported.

Detection and interpretation also depend on the receiving <biological cell>. For an extracellular <morphogen>, <receptors> and <signal transduction> connect the local <concentration> to a response; <receptor binding occupancy> is only one step in that connection. The same <morphogen gradient> can be interpreted differently in different tissues because their developmental context differs. Thus a <morphogen response threshold> summarizes a particular response in a particular context, rather than being a universal property of the signal. See https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK9968/[Scott F. Gilbert, Developmental Biology, 6th edition (2000): positional information and context-dependent interpretation].

An important experimental example came in 1988. Wolfgang Driever and Christiane Nüsslein-Volhard used antibodies to examine the bicoid <protein> in early fruit-fly embryos. They reported a <concentration> profile approximately described by an <exponential function>, highest at the front, with the <protein> concentrated in <cell nuclei>. Their abstract proposed <diffusion> from localized <messenger RNA> and dispersed degradation as a likely origin of the <morphogen gradient>, rather than proving a unique transport mechanism. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3383244/[Driever and Nüsslein-Volhard, A gradient of bicoid protein in Drosophila embryos, Cell 54 (1988), 83–93: primary abstract]{ref}

In a companion study, Driever and Nüsslein-Volhard genetically changed the amount and distribution of bicoid <messenger RNA>, measured the resulting <protein> <morphogen gradient>, and compared developmental maps. Increased local bicoid <protein> levels shifted prospective anterior structures toward the rear; decreased levels shifted them toward the front. This linked a measured <concentration> profile to positional effects in <biological development>. The toy calculations below illustrate that logic with arbitrary numbers, not measured bicoid parameters. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3383245/[Driever and Nüsslein-Volhard, The bicoid protein determines position in the Drosophila embryo in a concentration-dependent manner, Cell 54 (1988), 95–104: primary abstract]{ref}

= Morphogen gradient
{parent=Morphogen}
{title2=$c(x)$}

A <morphogen gradient> is a spatially varying <concentration> profile of a <morphogen>. The profile $c(x)$ specifies the local amount; its mathematical <gradient>, $c'(x)$ in one dimension, specifies how rapidly that amount changes with position. In the simplest positional interpretation, a <biological cell> responds to its local <concentration>, without having to measure the <gradient> across itself. Production, transport and removal together determine a <morphogen gradient>.

For a worked example, take a stationary one-dimensional tissue occupying $x\geq0$, constant <diffusion coefficient> $D>0$, and uniform <first-order reaction> removal at rate $k>0$. At <steady state>, this specialization of the <morphogen reaction-diffusion equation> gives the <ordinary differential equation> and <boundary conditions>
$$
D\frac{d^2c}{dx^2}-kc=0,\qquad c(0)=c_0,\qquad \lim_{x\to\infty}c(x)=0.
$$
Here $D$ has units of length squared per time, $k$ has units of inverse time, and $c_0$ is a prescribed source <concentration>. The <Dirichlet boundary condition> fixes the <concentration> at $x=0$. It does not prescribe a source <flux>; any required <flux> follows from the solution. These assumptions define a toy <morphogen gradient>, not a universal account of transport during <biological development>.

Define the <diffusion length> $\ell=\sqrt{D/k}$. Substitution into the <ordinary differential equation> shows that its general solution is $A e^{x/\ell}+B e^{-x/\ell}$. The vanishing <boundary condition> at infinity forces $A=0$, and the source <Dirichlet boundary condition> forces $B=c_0$. Hence
$$
c(x)=c_0e^{-x/\ell},\qquad \ell=\sqrt{D/k}.
$$
Over every additional <diffusion length>, the <concentration> falls by the same factor $e^{-1}$. Faster <diffusion> increases $\ell$; faster removal decreases $\ell$. This makes the <diffusion length> a useful bridge between microscopic transport and the spatial reach of a <morphogen gradient>.

Choose $c_0=100$ arbitrary <concentration> units and $\ell=100\,\mu\mathrm m$, where $1\,\mu\mathrm m=10^{-6}\,\mathrm m$. Then $c(x)=100e^{-x/(100\,\mu\mathrm m)}$: at $x=0$, $100\,\mu\mathrm m$ and $200\,\mu\mathrm m$, the <concentrations> are $100$, approximately $36.79$ and approximately $13.53$. The following diagram combines this <morphogen gradient> with the idealized responses calculated under <morphogen response threshold>.

= Morphogen source-diffusion-degradation model
{parent=Morphogen gradient}
{title2=$\lambda=\sqrt{D/k}$}

An idealized model balancing localized production, <diffusion> and degradation. In one dimension at <steady state>, away from the source, $Dc^{\prime\prime}-kc=0$ gives $c(x)=c_0e^{-x/\lambda}$ with $\lambda=\sqrt{D/k}$. Tissue geometry and time-dependent source distributions can invalidate a simple point-source interpretation.

= Morphogen response threshold
{parent=Morphogen}
{title2=$\theta$}

A <morphogen response threshold> is a <concentration> level used to distinguish developmental responses to a <morphogen>. In an idealized threshold model, otherwise equivalent <biological cells> exposed to $c\geq\theta$ take one response and those exposed to $c<\theta$ take another. Two <morphogen response thresholds> can assign three bands: fate A for $c\geq50$, fate B for $20\leq c<50$, and fate C for $c<20$, all in the toy arbitrary <concentration> units. The A/B/C labels specify a response rule, not named cell types or a claim that every <morphogen> produces sharp boundaries. See the positional-information model in Gilbert's reference under <morphogen>.

For the decreasing <morphogen gradient> $c(x)=c_0e^{-x/\ell}$ and $0<\theta<c_0$, solve $c(x_\theta)=\theta$ using the <natural logarithm>:
$$
x_\theta=\ell\ln\!\left(\frac{c_0}{\theta}\right).
$$
With $c_0=100$ and <diffusion length> $\ell=100\,\mu\mathrm m$, the <morphogen response thresholds> $50$ and $20$ give
$$
x_{50}=100\ln2\,\mu\mathrm m\approx69.3147\,\mu\mathrm m,\qquad x_{20}=100\ln5\,\mu\mathrm m\approx160.9438\,\mu\mathrm m.
$$
Thus fate A occupies $0\leq x\leq x_{50}$, fate B occupies $x_{50}<x\leq x_{20}$, and fate C occupies $x>x_{20}$ in this idealization. The equality convention belongs to the response rule. If $\theta=c_0$, the crossing is at the source; if $\theta>c_0$, that <morphogen response threshold> is never reached anywhere in the tissue.

Now double the prescribed source <concentration> from $c_0$ to $2c_0$, keeping $D$, $k$, <diffusion length> $\ell$ and a fixed <morphogen response threshold> $0<\theta<c_0$ unchanged. The whole <morphogen gradient> doubles, but the boundary moves by an additive distance:
$$
\Delta x_\theta=\ell\ln\!\left(\frac{2c_0}{\theta}\right)-\ell\ln\!\left(\frac{c_0}{\theta}\right)=\ell\ln2.
$$
For $\theta=20$, the crossing moves from $160.9438\,\mu\mathrm m$ to $100\ln10\,\mu\mathrm m\approx230.2585\,\mu\mathrm m$, a shift of $69.3147\,\mu\mathrm m$. Every fixed <morphogen response threshold> below the original source <concentration> shifts by this same distance in the toy model. The example compares two <Dirichlet boundary conditions>, $c(0)=100$ and $c(0)=200$; doubling an experimentally supplied source <flux> is a different boundary specification.

These calculations are useful because they separate changes in source <concentration>, <diffusion length> and response rule. Their limits are equally concrete: growing tissues change the geometry, a <transient response> can precede <steady state>, molecular fluctuations can blur a boundary, and <signaling feedback> or altered transport can change the <morphogen gradient> or its interpretation. A local instantaneous <morphogen response threshold> need not capture exposure duration, <signaling adaptation> or the receiving <biological cell>'s state. Experiments on cultured human <biological cells> found different responses to the level and rate of change of developmental signals, illustrating why signal dynamics can matter. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6398983/[Heemskerk et al., Rapid changes in morphogen concentration control self-organized patterning in human embryonic stem cells, eLife 8 (2019), e40526: primary experiments]{ref} Quantitative measurements of bicoid <protein> and its downstream <gene expression> also make precision a question for experiment, rather than something guaranteed by drawing threshold lines. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2253670/[Gregor et al., Probing the limits to positional information, Cell 130 (2007), 153–164: primary measurements]{ref} <Morphogen gradients> provide one mechanism for spatial patterning in <biological development>, alongside other signals and interactions.
