Make heterogeneous <electron transfer> fast relative to delivery of reactant, usually by applying sufficient <overpotential> so that its surface concentration approaches zero. Use excess <supporting electrolyte> to suppress migration. For purely diffusional transient transport, suppress uncontrolled <convection>; for a <rotating disk electrode>, controlled <convection> supplies the bulk while <diffusion> across the thin interfacial boundary layer limits the current. A limiting current that no longer increases with driving potential is the characteristic test; a large current alone is not sufficient.

The <midpoint potential> $E_m$ characterizes the redox couple under specified solution conditions: it is the potential at which oxidized and reduced forms have equal activities, or equal concentrations in the corresponding formal-potential convention. For a reversible cyclic voltammogram it is estimated by $(E_{pa}+E_{pc})/2$. It approximates the formal redox potential when the usual reversibility and <diffusion> assumptions apply; it is not the potential halfway along a scan chosen arbitrarily.

All three marked points are at strongly oxidizing potentials, where X is depleted at the electrode: $c_X(0)\simeq0$. Assuming only X initially in bulk, $c_{X^+}(\infty)=0$ and $c_X(\infty)=c_b$. Oxidation produces X$^+$ at the interface.

* **A:** the largest steady limiting current corresponds to the fastest rotation and thinnest <diffusion> boundary layer. X rises steeply from zero to $c_b$; X$^+$ falls steeply from its surface value to zero.
* **B:** slower rotation gives a thicker layer and shallower gradients, hence a smaller limiting current.
* **C:** the nonrotating electrode gives the peaked, subsequently decaying current. Its <diffusion> layer continues to broaden with time, approximately as $\sqrt{Dt}$, giving the broadest and shallowest profiles at the indicated late-scan point.

For equal <diffusion coefficients>, the two concentration profiles are complementary, $c_X+c_{X^+}=c_b$. More generally equality of their interfacial molar fluxes requires $D_Xc_X'(0)=-D_{X^+}c_{X^+}'(0)$, so the product surface concentration need not equal $c_b$. A and B are approximately steady hydrodynamic profiles; C is a time-dependent <diffusion> profile.

Event a probes <electron transfer> between the semiquinone and dianion. Benzoquinone's smaller framework localizes charge and undergoes a greater change in bonding and solvation on this reduction; a larger effective reorganization barrier can make its heterogeneous transfer insufficiently fast for Nernst equilibrium on the $50\,\mathrm{mV\,s^{-1}}$ time scale. A useful electrode-surface scheme for the proposed <ECEC mechanism> is
$$
\mathrm{AQ}\xrightarrow{+e^-}\mathrm{AQ^{\bullet-}}
\xrightarrow{+CO_2}\mathrm{AQCO_2^{\bullet-}}
\xrightarrow{+e^-}\mathrm{AQCO_2^{2-}}
\xrightarrow{+CO_2}\mathrm{AQ(CO_2)_2^{2-}}.
$$
Electron-transfer arrows occur at the electrode, whereas both capture steps occur in the adjacent solution layer. CO$_2$ is attacked by an oxygen-centered nucleophilic component of the reduced quinone, forming an O-carbonate linkage rather than simply physically adsorbing on neutral AQ.

At potentials more negative than $-2.0\,\mathrm V$, direct second reduction of any unbound semiquinone is also accessible: $\mathrm{AQ^{\bullet-}}\to\mathrm{AQ^{2-}}$, followed by carbonate formation. Thus the fully reduced unbound and mono-adduct species can feed the bis-adduct, and the drawing should include CO$_2$ transport into the layer and adduct transport away. ECEC is the proposed sequence; competing EECC transfer/capture ordering is possible at such negative potentials.

Write $\mathrm{Me=CH_3}$. The alternating-vertex cubane A is $\mathrm{Li_4Me_4}$; replacing one or two methyl vertices gives $\mathrm B=\mathrm{Li_4Me_3X}$ and $\mathrm C=\mathrm{Li_4Me_2X_2}$. Balanced redistributions are

\mathrm{A+LiX\rightleftharpoons B+MeLi},\qquad
\mathrm{A+2LiX\rightleftharpoons C+2MeLi}.

For the structures, place Li on one tetrahedral set of cube corners and Me/X on the complementary set, joining adjacent unlike vertices. Useful isotope-selection factors include spin and quadrupole moment, <gyromagnetic ratio>, natural abundance or enrichment, sensitivity at the available field, line widths and relaxation, chemical-shift dispersion and the couplings needed for structural assignment. Both lithium isotopes are quadrupolar, but $^6$Li has a much smaller quadrupole moment and often much narrower lines, allowing inequivalent sites and carbon-lithium couplings to be resolved. Enrichment compensates its low natural abundance and removes isotope-mixture complexity.

Fully enriched $^7$Li would generally be more sensitive but broader in these aggregates. Its chemical-shift pattern would remain chemically similar, although isotope shifts and dynamics can make small differences. Resolved H-Li couplings increase in frequency approximately with $\gamma_7/\gamma_6=2.67$ and have different multiplicities because $I$ changes from $1$ to $3/2$: $n$ equivalent Li nuclei give $2nI+1$ lines. If such long-range proton couplings remain unresolved, the main visible difference can instead be broadened lines. One should not invent resolved multiplets absent from the printed spectra.

The zero-ppm signals E are plausibly solvated lithium halide, predominantly LiBr or LiI and their solution ion-pair/aggregate forms. Structural symmetry predicts one Li environment in A, two in B in a $3:1$ ratio, two in C in a $2:2$ ratio, and one in an ideal symmetric D.

The natural lithium assignment is therefore:

* **Br mixture:** A at approximately $1.68\,\mathrm{ppm}$ (43.4); B at approximately $0.80$ (16.2, three Li) and $1.55$ (5.4, one Li); D at $0.73$ (10.3, broad); E at $0.00$ (24.8).
* **I mixture:** A at approximately $1.68$ (59.9); B at approximately $1.10$ (8.4, three Li) and $1.55$ (2.8, one Li); C at approximately $1.0$ and $0.35$ (0.6 each); D at $0.97$ (2.3, broad); E at $0.00$ (3.0). The close C/D signals require care about overlap.

The major proton peak near $-2.08\,\mathrm{ppm}$ is A. The next peak near $-2.15$ to $-2.16$ is naturally assigned to B; in the iodide mixture the smaller peaks near $-2.22$ and $-2.28$ distinguish C and D. Smaller D aggregates can exchange Li or ligands with free halide-containing species more rapidly, giving intermediate-exchange broadening at the two specifically identified broad lithium resonances.

**There is a quantitative inconsistency in the printed integrations under these ideal single-species assignments.** For example the Br lithium B integrals give molecular amount proportional to $5.4$, while A gives $43.4/4=10.85$. Their proton areas should consequently obey $H_B/H_A=9(5.4)/[12(10.85)]=0.373$, whereas either small proton integral gives $17/66=0.258$. No common proton-versus-lithium calibration removes that ratio discrepancy. Similarly the I B/A ratio predicted from Li is $0.140$, compared with printed $9/90=0.100$.

Thus the symmetry-based assignment above is useful, but an exact simultaneous quantitative assignment satisfying every printed reliable integral is not established by these data. It would require corrected integrals, an explicitly overlapped signal or an additional speciation model; the discrepancy should not be concealed by treating independently normalized spectra as automatically consistent.

In the slowly exchanging cubane A each methyl carbon is bonded to three equivalent $^6$Li nuclei with $I=1$. Its single carbon environment gives a **septet**, with first-order intensities $1:3:6:7:6:3:1$.

In B the three methyl vertices remain equivalent by the ideal threefold symmetry, but each carbon sees two equivalent Li of one type and one Li of the other. The carbon resonance is therefore a **quintet of triplets** (15 first-order lines if both couplings are resolved and unequal): the equivalent pair gives $1:2:3:2:1$, and the third Li gives $1:1:1$. Accidental equality of couplings can collapse this to a septet.

D has one methyl-carbon environment coupled to two equivalent Li, giving a **quintet** $1:2:3:2:1$. E has no methyl carbon. These patterns assume <proton decoupling>, resolved $^1J_{CLi}$, ideal aggregate symmetry and slow exchange; without <proton decoupling> each methyl-carbon pattern is additionally split by its three attached protons.

The first transformation is an <Achmatowicz rearrangement>, followed by protection and selective reduction; the last is a <Johnson-Claisen rearrangement>.

* Butyllithium removes the furan 2-proton, giving a 2-lithiofuran. Its carbon attacks the <aldehyde> <carbonyl>; <protonation> of the resulting alkoxide gives the furfuryl alcohol. Carbon-carbon bond formation occurs at the lithiated position.
* Electrophilic oxygen transfer from mCPBA to the electron-rich furan gives an oxidized, ring-opened intermediate. Opening the oxygenated furan gives a hydroxy enedicarbonyl: the original furfuryl hydroxyl then attacks the remote <aldehyde> intramolecularly. This expands the five-membered furan into a six-membered <lactol> containing an enone <carbonyl>. The original furfuryl oxygen becomes the pyran ring oxygen; oxidation of the furan supplies the new <carbonyl> and <lactol> functionality.
* Ag$_2$O/MeI converts the <lactol> OH into its methyl <acetal>: oxygen attacks methyl iodide by <SN2 reaction>, with iodide removal and proton transfer. This protects the anomeric position.
* LiAlH$_4$ delivers hydride to the remaining <ketone> <carbonyl>, followed by <protonation>. The methyl <acetal> survives these basic reducing conditions, leaving the depicted allylic alcohol.
* Under weak-acid catalysis, that allylic OH replaces an ethoxy group of triethyl orthoacetate. Loss of another ethanol molecule, with removal of a methyl proton, gives the mixed <ketene acetal> $\mathrm{CH_2=C(OEt)-O-(allyl)}$. A six-electron, chair-like <Claisen rearrangement> breaks the allyl-O bond, forms a bond between the terminal allyl carbon and the ketene-acetal CH$_2$, and moves the ring double bond one position. <Carbonyl> formation gives the $\mathrm{-CH_2CO_2Et}$ substituent.

The <acetal> protects the other oxygenated position during hydride reduction. The orthoester transfers an acetate-derived two-carbon unit through a <ketene acetal>; it does not directly substitute the allylic OH with an intact acetate ion.

Propionic acid is a weak, relatively high-boiling acid: its normal boiling point is about $141^\circ\mathrm C$, compared with about $118^\circ\mathrm C$ for acetic acid. It provides mild proton catalysis while remaining in the hot orthoester mixture more effectively. This is a practical catalyst choice, not an absolute mechanistic prohibition on acetic acid; acetic-acid-catalysed orthoester rearrangements are also possible.

This is a thermal <Claisen rearrangement> followed by intramolecular <acetal> exchange. Identify the six-atom array as the cyclopentenone enol-ether C=C–O–CH$_2$–C=C unit. In its concerted $[3,3]$ rearrangement:

* The O–CH$_2$ bond breaks.
* A new C-C bond joins the other end of the cyclopentenone enol double bond to the cyclohexene carbon bearing CH(OEt)$_2$.
* The former ether-bearing cyclopentene carbon becomes a <ketone>, and the cyclohexene double bond becomes the exocyclic methylene.

The intermediate is thus a substituted cyclopentane-1,3-dione, with the <acetal> tether now correctly placed for six-membered-ring closure. Its enol oxygen attacks the <acetal> carbon, replacing OEt and releasing ethanol. Enolization gives the conjugated cyclic enol ether drawn in the product; the second ethoxy group remains.

A chair-like <Claisen rearrangement> transition structure with the phenyl-bearing part of the cyclohexane directed outward avoids severe axial crowding. Suprafacial bond formation and the pre-existing configuration give the two indicated ring-junction hydrogens on opposite faces: the left H is a wedge and the right H a dash in the supplied orientation. Closure favors the <acetal> epimer with outward OEt, drawn as a wedge, while the original Ph remains dashed. This is substrate-controlled diastereoselection followed by conformationally selective <acetal> closure, rather than independent arbitrary assignment of three centres.

The Coulomb interaction has leading scale $-1/r$, and the exchange pair energy carries the usual factor $1/2$ to avoid double counting, giving exchange energy per electron $-1/(2r)$.

This energy-per-electron tail should not be confused with the exchange or exchange-correlation potential, whose leading coefficient is generally $-1/r$ in the corresponding exact finite-system setting. Local energy-density representations also require a specified gauge; the question uses the conventional exchange-hole interpretation.
