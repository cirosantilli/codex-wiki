= Solution

<Quantum tunnelling> is penetration of, or transmission through, a finite <potential barrier> into a <classically forbidden region>, where the total <energy> is less than the <potential energy>. The <wavefunction> generally remains nonzero there, so the <Born rule> assigns a nonzero <probability> of detection. This does not require an increase in total <energy>; it is the classical relation between a definite position and a definite <kinetic energy> that cannot be imposed on the <quantum state>. For a bound <quantum harmonic oscillator>, the relevant quantity is the <probability> of finding the particle beyond its <classical turning points>; it is not an escaping transmitted beam.

= b
{parent=25}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

For the reduced <quantum harmonic oscillator>, $V(q)=q^2/2$ and the ground-state <energy> is $E_0=1/2$. Thus the <classical turning points> satisfy $q^2/2=1/2$, giving $q=\pm1$. Normalizing the <wavefunction> gives $\phi_0(q)=\pi^{-1/4}e^{-q^2/2}$, because $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-q^2}\,dq=\sqrt\pi$. The <Born rule> therefore gives the <harmonic-oscillator forbidden-region probability>
$$
P(|q|>1)=\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_1^\infty e^{-q^2}\,dq
\simeq\frac{2(0.1394)}{\sqrt\pi}=\boxed{0.1573\simeq15.7\%}.
$$
The factor two includes both tails. Using the exact <complementary error function> gives $\operatorname{erfc}(1)=0.1572992$. A stationary real <wavefunction> has zero <probability current>, so this result describes forbidden-region occupancy, not continuous loss of the bound particle.

Inside the <infinite square well>, the <Hamiltonian (quantum mechanics)> is purely the <kinetic energy> operator,
$$
\boxed{\hat H=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2},\qquad 0<x<L.}
$$
Its admissible <wavefunctions> obey the <boundary conditions> $\psi(0)=\psi(L)=0$ and vanish outside the interval. Equivalently write $\hat H=\hat p^2/(2m)+V(x)$ with $V=0$ inside and an infinite confining wall outside. The <boundary conditions> are part of the specification of this <Hamiltonian (quantum mechanics)>; the differential expression alone does not determine the spectrum.

{parent=26}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Twice differentiating gives $d^2\sin(kx)/dx^2=-k^2\sin(kx)$, so inside the <infinite square well>
$$
\hat H\sin(kx)=\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}\sin(kx).
$$
The <boundary condition> at zero is automatic. The other requires $\sin(kL)=0$, hence $kL=n\pi$. A nonzero <eigenfunction> can therefore be labelled by
$$
\boxed{k_n=\frac{n\pi}{L},\qquad E_n=\frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2},\qquad n=1,2,3,\ldots.}
$$
If desired, its normalized form is $\sqrt{2/L}\sin(n\pi x/L)$. The positive lowest <energy> follows from confinement and the required spatial variation of a nonzero <wavefunction>.

Within a specified <electron configuration> in the <LS coupling> regime, <Hund's rules> select the lowest <atomic term> as follows. First maximize the total <electron spin> $S$ subject to the <Pauli exclusion principle>. Among the terms having that $S$, choose the largest total orbital <angular momentum> $L$. Finally, <spin-orbit coupling> places the smallest allowed $J=|L-S|$ lowest for a subshell less than half full, and the largest $J=L+S$ lowest for a subshell more than half full. At half filling the maximum-spin term has $L=0$, hence $J=S$. These rules rank terms within configurations; the exceptional neutral-atom configurations of <chromium> and <copper> must also be used.

For the <character of a localized orbital basis>, take the <trace> of the transformation <matrix>: both permutation of centres and signs of <atomic orbitals> matter. The two metal centres lie on the principal axis and remain fixed under rotation about $z$. At either metal the $(p_x,p_y)$ <basis> has the <matrix>
$$
R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The full four-dimensional <group representation> is block diagonal with two such blocks. Consequently $\chi(R)=4\cos\theta$, and for the $60^\circ$ rotation of the <D6h molecular point group>, **$\chi(C_6)=4\cos(\pi/3)=2$**.

Fix the positive lobes of the real <atomic orbitals> along the printed positive axes, and write $\phi_1=p_x(1)$, $\phi_4=p_x(4)$, $\phi_5=p_y(5)$ and $\phi_8=p_y(8)$. A coordinate model consistent with the figure is $r_1=(0,a,-b)$, $r_4=(0,-a,-b)$, $r_5=(a,0,b)$ and $r_8=(-a,0,b)$, with $a,b>0$. For the <D2d molecular point group>, take the two transverse twofold axes along $x+y$ and $x-y$, with vertical mirrors $xz$ and $yz$. The $A_2$ <character table> row on $(E,2S_4,C_2,2C_2',2\sigma_d)$ is $(1,1,1,-1,-1)$. The <character projection operator> is
$$
P_{A_2}=\frac18\sum_{g\in D_{2d}}\chi_{A_2}(g)^*\,U(g).
$$
The eight signed images needed for this projection are
$$
\begin{array}{c|rrrrrrrr}
g&E&S_4^+&S_4^-&C_2&C_2(x+y)&C_2(x-y)&\sigma(xz)&\sigma(yz)\\\hline
U(g)\phi_1&\phi_1&\phi_8&-\phi_5&-\phi_4&\phi_5&-\phi_8&\phi_4&-\phi_1\\
\chi_{A_2}(g)&1&1&1&1&-1&-1&-1&-1
\end{array}
$$
so $P_{A_2}\phi_1=(\phi_1-\phi_4-\phi_5+\phi_8)/4$. Under the standard orthonormal <atomic orbital> approximation its squared norm is $4/16=1/4$. Therefore the normalized <symmetry-adapted linear combination> is
$$
\boxed{\Phi_{A_2}=\tfrac12(\phi_1-\phi_4-\phi_5+\phi_8).}
$$
An overall minus sign describes the same <quantum state>; changing one local <atomic orbital> phase requires changing its coefficient too. If intersite <orbital overlap> were retained, normalization would instead use the overlap <matrix>.

= Solution

Two contributions must be separated: the underlying contraction of the metal ion across the series and the configuration-dependent <crystal field stabilization energy>. Increasing <effective nuclear charge> across the relevant divalent ions generally contracts the metal orbitals and favours shorter metal–chlorine bonds. Superimposed on this, a weak-field tetrahedral chloride environment places the $e$ set below the $t_2$ set, at $-0.6\Delta_t$ and $+0.4\Delta_t$ relative to the barycentre. The high-spin occupancies and corresponding <crystal field stabilization energies> are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|r}
d\text{ count}&\text{occupancy}&\mathrm{CFSE}/\Delta_t\\\hline
6&e^3t_2^3&-0.6\\
7&e^4t_2^3&-1.2\\
8&e^4t_2^4&-0.8\\
10&e^4t_2^6&0
\end{array}
$$
Because the <tetrahedral crystal field splitting> increases on bringing the ligands inward, the stabilization is especially effective at shortening the $d^7$ bond. Adding the next <electron> to the upper $t_2$ set reduces that stabilization; in a simple sigma-bonding description this set also has more metal–ligand antibonding character. That change explains the upward deviation from $d^7$ to $d^8$ despite the general contraction trend. By $d^{10}$ the <crystal field stabilization energy> is zero, but the background contraction can still produce the shortest bond in the plotted set. Thus **the $d^7$ shortening and $d^8$ rebound reflect orbital filling, while the overall decrease reflects contraction**. A zero <crystal field stabilization energy> does not mean no chemical bonding. Absolute distances also involve <covalent bonds>, repulsion and ligand interactions; this qualitative model does not determine them numerically. No inference about the missing $d^9$ distance is required.

For a self-adjoint <Hamiltonian (quantum mechanics)> bounded below, every nonzero admissible trial <wavefunction> satisfies the <quantum variational principle>
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E[\psi]=\frac{\langle\psi|\hat H|\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}\ge E_0.}
$$
Here $E_0$ is the lowest exact <energy>, and the trial function must obey the physical <boundary conditions> and have finite relevant integrals. Equality holds for a <wavefunction> in the ground-state eigenspace. The quotient is unchanged by normalization or any nonzero overall scalar.

= b
{parent=33}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Expand the trial <wavefunction> in a complete <orthonormal set> of exact <eigenfunctions>, $\psi=\sum_n c_n\phi_n$, with $\hat H\phi_n=E_n\phi_n$ and $E_n\ge E_0$. The coefficients may depend on any chosen variational parameters. <Orthogonality> removes the cross terms in both integrals, giving
$$
\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=\sum_n|c_n|^2,\qquad
\langle\psi|\hat H|\psi\rangle=\sum_n E_n|c_n|^2.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E[\psi]-E_0=\frac{\sum_n(E_n-E_0)|c_n|^2}{\sum_n|c_n|^2}\ge0.}
$$
This proves the <quantum variational principle>. The denominator is positive because the trial function is nonzero. Equality requires every occupied exact state to have $E_n=E_0$, allowing for <ground-state degeneracy>. With a continuous part of the spectrum, the corresponding nonnegative spectral integral gives the same conclusion.

Write $g=e^{-q^2/2}$ and $f=2q^2-a$. Product differentiation for the reduced <quantum harmonic oscillator> gives
$$
\hat H(fg)=\left[-\tfrac12 f''+qf'+\tfrac12 f\right]g
=\left(5q^2-2-\frac a2\right)g.
$$
For an <eigenfunction>, this must equal $E(2q^2-a)g$ at every $q$. Matching the $q^2$ coefficients gives $E=5/2$, and matching constants then gives $5a/2=2+a/2$. Hence **$a=1$ and $E=5/2$**. This is the even $n=2$ oscillator state. Its normalized <wavefunction> is $\phi_2=(2q^2-1)e^{-q^2/2}/(\sqrt2\,\pi^{1/4})$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Direct evaluation of the <Rayleigh quotient> exposes a problem with the requested minimization. Using the supplied <Gaussian integrals>,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\langle\psi|\psi\rangle&=\sqrt\pi(a^2-2a+3),\\
\langle\psi|\hat H|\psi\rangle&=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}(a^2-2a+11),\\
\mathcal E(a)&=\frac{a^2-2a+11}{2(a^2-2a+3)}
=\frac12+\frac4{(a-1)^2+2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus
$$
\mathcal E'(a)=\frac{-8(a-1)}{[(a-1)^2+2]^2},\qquad
\mathcal E'(1)=0,\qquad \mathcal E''(1)=-2.
$$
**The <eigenfunction> value $a=1$ is a maximum, not a minimum.** Indeed $\mathcal E(1)=5/2$, whereas $\mathcal E(0)=11/6$ is already lower. There is no finite minimizing $a$ in this family: its infimum is $1/2$, approached as $|a|\to\infty$, when the normalized trial <wavefunction> approaches the ground state. This is consistent with the <quantum variational principle>. The original printed instruction in this subpart is therefore inconsistent as an unconstrained minimization; a stationary <Rayleigh quotient> need not be a minimum.

The intended excited state can instead be obtained by imposing an <excited-state variational constraint>. <Orthogonality> to the ground state requires
$$
\langle g|\psi\rangle=\sqrt\pi(1-a)=0,
$$
which fixes **$a=1$**. Every member of the family is even, so it is already orthogonal to the odd $n=1$ state. With the ground state excluded, the lowest allowed oscillator <energy> is therefore $E_2=5/2$. In the exact oscillator <basis>, $\psi$ is proportional to $\sqrt2\,\phi_2+(1-a)\phi_0$, making both the maximum and the <orthogonality> correction transparent.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Exact solutions of the <Schrödinger equation> are unavailable for most interacting or realistic molecular systems. The <quantum variational principle> turns the search for a ground-state <wavefunction> into optimization of a computable <energy> quotient in a tractable trial family. The result is an upper bound: enlarging a nested trial space can only lower or preserve the optimized <energy>, providing a systematic convergence check. Trial functions can incorporate <boundary conditions> and known <symmetry> before calculation. Near an exact normalized ground state, a small orthogonal <wavefunction> error changes the <energy> only to second order, which explains why useful energies can result from imperfect trial functions. This does not ensure that every other observable is equally accurate, nor that a restricted trial family captures correlation adequately. Excited states require <orthogonality> or appropriate <symmetry> restrictions; unrestricted minimization seeks the ground state, as the preceding calculation demonstrates.

Let $u=r-r_e$ measure displacement from the equilibrium <bond length> and put the minimum of <potential energy> at zero. The harmonic model and the <Morse potential> are
$$
V_{\rm harm}(r)=\frac12ku^2,\qquad
V_{\rm Morse}(r)=D_e(1-e^{-\alpha u})^2.
$$
The <harmonic approximation> is a symmetric parabola and has no dissociation limit. The <Morse potential> has a steep compressed side and a shallower extended side, approaching $D_e$ as $r\to\infty$. Matching the curvatures at the minimum gives $k=2D_e\alpha^2$. With $\widetilde\omega$ denoting a <spectroscopic wavenumber> rather than an angular frequency, the <energy> levels are
$$
\begin{aligned}
E_v^{\rm harm}&=hc\widetilde\omega(v+\tfrac12),\quad v=0,1,2,\ldots,\\
E_v^{\rm Morse}&=hc\widetilde\omega\left[(v+\tfrac12)-x_e(v+\tfrac12)^2\right],\quad v+\tfrac12<\frac1{2x_e}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $x_e$ is the dimensionless <spectroscopic anharmonicity constant>. The harmonic spacing is constant, while the <Morse oscillator> spacing is $E_{v+1}-E_v=hc\widetilde\omega[1-2x_e(v+1)]$ and decreases as the levels approach dissociation. Only finitely many <Morse oscillator> levels are bound. The exactly solvable potential was introduced to model this anharmonic behaviour.<Diatomic Molecules According to the Wave Mechanics. II. Vibrational Levels by Philip M. Morse>[Physical Review 34 (1929), pp. 57–64, potential and bound-level solution]{ref}

For the harmonic model put $q=\sqrt{\mu\omega/\hbar}\,u$, with $\omega=2\pi c\widetilde\omega$, and $N=\pi^{-1/4}$. The first three normalized reduced-coordinate <wavefunctions> are
$$
\phi_0=Ne^{-q^2/2},\qquad
\phi_1=\sqrt2\,Nq e^{-q^2/2},\qquad
\phi_2=\frac{N}{\sqrt2}(2q^2-1)e^{-q^2/2}.
$$
Their numbers of nodes are zero, one and two, and their parities are even, odd and even. The $v=2$ nodes are at $q=\pm1/\sqrt2$. The diagrams below use analytic curves and arbitrary illustrative scales, with both potentials given the same local curvature.

Write $s=v+1/2$ and complete the square in the <Morse oscillator> <energy>:
$$
E(s)=hc\widetilde\omega(s-x_es^2)
=\frac{hc\widetilde\omega}{4x_e}
-hc\widetilde\omega x_e\left(s-\frac1{2x_e}\right)^2.

The finite bound-level sequence approaches the dissociation limit at the vertex of this extrapolated parabola. Thus the <bond potential-well depth> is
$$
\boxed{D_e=\frac{hc\widetilde\omega}{4x_e}.}
$$
The continuous vertex is a convenient way to identify the limit; it is not an additional normalizable bound level at the threshold.

The <ground-state dissociation energy> starts at the lowest vibrational level, rather than the minimum of the potential. The <zero-point energy> of the <Morse oscillator> is
$$
E_0=hc\widetilde\omega\left(\frac12-\frac{x_e}{4}\right)=D_e(2x_e-x_e^2).

$$
This distinction matters whenever a dissociation <energy> is inferred from a vibrational spectrum.

The strong band is the **$v=0\to1$ vibrational fundamental**, and the weaker band near twice its <wavenumber> is the **$v=0\to2$ first vibrational overtone**. At room temperature these high-frequency oscillators have overwhelmingly ground-state vibrational populations. In the harmonic-potential, linear-dipole limit the infrared <selection rule> is $\Delta v=\pm1$; <anharmonicity> and nonlinear dipole dependence make the overtone weakly allowed. A $1\to2$ <hot band> would lie near, and slightly below, the fundamental <wavenumber>, rather than near twice it.

The operator $\hat J_z$ describes the $z$ component of <angular momentum>, whereas $\hat J^2$ describes its total squared magnitude. For a <spherical harmonic> $Y_{JM}$ the corresponding <eigenvalues> are
$$
\boxed{\hat J_zY_{JM}=M\hbar Y_{JM},\qquad
\hat J^2Y_{JM}=J(J+1)\hbar^2Y_{JM}.}
$$
Thus the magnitude is $\hbar\sqrt{J(J+1)}$, not $J\hbar$. For ordinary orbital rotation $J=0,1,2,\ldots$ and $M=-J,-J+1,\ldots,J$. The simultaneous values are possible because these two operators commute.

Normalization of a <wavefunction> on the unit sphere uses solid angle $d\Omega=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi$, not the flat measure $d\theta\,d\phi$. Since $|e^{-i\phi}|=1$,
$$
\int|\psi|^2d\Omega=\int_0^{2\pi}d\phi\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,d\theta
=2\pi\int_{-1}^{1}(1-u^2)\,du=\frac{8\pi}{3},
$$
where $u=\cos\theta$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Psi(\theta,\phi)=\sqrt{\frac3{8\pi}}\sin\theta\,e^{-i\phi}.}
$$
With the usual phase convention this is the <spherical harmonic> $Y_{1,-1}$. An arbitrary global unit-modulus factor would not change normalization or any observable.

The azimuthal derivative is $\partial_\phi\psi=-i\psi$, so $\hat J_z\psi=(-i\hbar)(-i)\psi=-\hbar\psi$. For the polar part of the angular Laplacian,
$$
\frac1{\sin\theta}\partial_\theta(\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta\psi)
=\frac{\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta}{\sin\theta}e^{-i\phi},
\qquad
\frac1{\sin^2\theta}\partial_\phi^2\psi=-\frac1{\sin\theta}e^{-i\phi}.
$$
Their sum is $-2\sin\theta e^{-i\phi}=-2\psi$, hence $\hat J^2\psi=2\hbar^2\psi$. Comparing these two <eigenvalues> with $M\hbar$ and $J(J+1)\hbar^2$ gives **$J=1$, $M=-1$**. The sign of $M$ follows from both minus signs in the $\hat J_z$ calculation.

For a <linear rigid rotor>, dividing the <angular momentum> squared <eigenvalue> by twice the <moment of inertia> gives

Express the <rotational constant> as a <spectroscopic wavenumber> $\widetilde B=h/(8\pi^2cI)$. The rotational absorption <selection rule> $\Delta J=+1$ gives

\widetilde\nu_{J\to J+1}=2\widetilde B(J+1).
$$

The drawn $x,y$ axes pass through opposite sites, rather than along the square edges. In the <D4h molecular point group> class order $(E,2C_4,C_2,2C_2',2C_2'',i,2S_4,\sigma_h,2\sigma_v,2\sigma_d)$, an <s orbital> contributes $+1$ whenever its centre is fixed and zero otherwise. Thus
$$
\chi_\Gamma=(4,0,0,2,0,0,0,4,2,0).
$$
For example, a transverse twofold rotation about the printed $x$ axis fixes sites 1 and 3, while its diagonal counterpart exchanges all four sites. Reflection in the square plane fixes every site. Taking <character inner products>, with class sizes $(1,2,1,2,2,1,2,1,2,2)$, gives
$$
\boxed{\Gamma=A_{1g}\oplus B_{1g}\oplus E_u.}
$$
These have dimensions $1+1+2=4$. The $B_{1g}$ label is tied to the printed diagonal-axis convention; rotating the transverse coordinate labels by $45^\circ$ would interchange $B_{1g}$ and $B_{2g}$.

Treat the site <atomic orbitals> $s_1,\ldots,s_4$ as orthonormal, as in the <Hückel method>. A normalized set of <symmetry-adapted linear combinations> is
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\Phi_{A_{1g}}&=\tfrac12(s_1+s_2+s_3+s_4),\\
\Phi_{B_{1g}}&=\tfrac12(s_1-s_2+s_3-s_4),\\
\Phi_{E_u,x}&=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(s_3-s_1),\\
\Phi_{E_u,y}&=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(s_2-s_4).
\end{aligned}}
$$
The first combination is unchanged by every operation; the alternating combination has the transformation signs of $x^2-y^2$ for the printed axes. The last two transform together like $(x,y)$. The squared coefficients sum to one in each case, and their pairwise inner products vanish. Any orthogonal rotation of the two $E_u$ combinations is an equally valid <basis> for that degenerate subspace.

Use a common Coulomb integral $\alpha$ on each site, a resonance integral $\beta$ on each adjacent pair, zero interaction across a diagonal, and the identity overlap <matrix>. In site order $(1,2,3,4)$ the <Hückel method> gives
$$
H=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta&0&\beta\\\beta&\alpha&\beta&0\\0&\beta&\alpha&\beta\\\beta&0&\beta&\alpha\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Applying it to the four <symmetry-adapted linear combinations> gives
$$
\boxed{E_{A_{1g}}=\alpha+2\beta,\qquad E_{E_u}=\alpha\ \text{(twice)},\qquad E_{B_{1g}}=\alpha-2\beta.}
$$
For the conventional bonding sign $\beta<0$, the all-in-phase combination is lowest and the alternating combination is highest. The $E_u$ combinations have canceling nearest-neighbour interactions and are nonbonding within this model.

The long edge is $3$–$4$. In the orthonormal pair <basis> $(a,b)$, its weaker resonance integral $n\beta$ changes the second diagonal element. The two short side edges contribute an inter-pair <matrix element> $\beta$, giving
$$
H_{A_1}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha+\beta&\beta\\\beta&\alpha+n\beta\end{pmatrix}.
$$
With $\lambda=(E-\alpha)/\beta$, its secular equation is
$$
(1-\lambda)(n-\lambda)-1=0,
\quad\lambda^2-(n+1)\lambda+(n-1)=0.
$$
The <eigenvalues> are therefore
$$
\boxed{E_{A_1}=\alpha+\frac\beta2\left[n+1\pm\sqrt{n^2-2n+5}\right].}
$$
At $n=1$ they become $\alpha+2\beta$ and $\alpha$, agreeing with the square limit. <Symmetry> permits mixing of the two $A_1$ pair functions; treating each pair as an isolated bond would miss the off-diagonal term.

Put $D=\sqrt{n^2-2n+5}=\sqrt{(n-1)^2+4}$. For $0<n<1$, $D>n+1$, since $D^2-(n+1)^2=4(1-n)>0$. Remembering $\beta<0$, the two lowest trapezium <molecular orbital> energies are
$$
\epsilon_1=\alpha+\frac\beta2(n+1+D)\quad(A_1),\qquad
\epsilon_2=\alpha+\frac\beta2(D-n-1)\quad(B_1).
$$
The remaining levels lie at $2\alpha-\epsilon_2$ and $2\alpha-\epsilon_1$. Filling the lowest spatial orbitals with opposite-spin pairs gives
$$
\boxed{\begin{array}{c|cc}
&\text{square}&\text{trapezium}\\\hline
2\ \text{electrons}&2\alpha+4\beta&2\alpha+\beta(n+1+D)\\
4\ \text{electrons}&4\alpha+4\beta&4\alpha+2\beta D
\end{array}}
$$
For the square four-electron case, two <electrons> occupy the lowest orbital and two occupy the degenerate $E_u$ set. Their summed one-electron <energy> is fixed here, but this <Hückel method> calculation alone does not decide the spin or correlation within that open shell.

= ii
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For two <electrons>, $n+1+D<4$ whenever $0<n<1$: both sides of $D<3-n$ are positive and squaring reduces the inequality to $n<1$. Since $\beta<0$, the trapezium then has a higher total electronic <energy> than the square. **The two-electron model favours the square.** For four <electrons>, $D>2$, so $2\beta D<4\beta$: **the four-electron model favours the distortion toward a trapezium.** The lower split middle orbital is occupied while the upper one is empty.

The distinction is especially clear near the square. Write $\delta=1-n>0$; then $D=2+\delta^2/4+O(\delta^4)$ and
$$
\begin{aligned}
E_2^{\rm trap}-E_2^{\rm sq}&=\beta[-\delta+\delta^2/4+O(\delta^4)]>0,\\
E_4^{\rm trap}-E_4^{\rm sq}&=\tfrac12\beta\delta^2+O(\delta^4)<0.
\end{aligned}
$$
In particular the four-electron lowering in this specified one-edge model is quadratic near the square; a first-order lowering of the entire occupied sum should not be asserted merely from the middle-level splitting. This <Hückel square-to-trapezium distortion> is an electronic comparison under the stated fixed-$\alpha$, fixed-short-edge-$\beta$ assumptions. Nuclear repulsion, other bonding contributions and the structural cost of distortion are omitted, so an actual equilibrium geometry or optimum $n$ is not determined by this calculation alone.
