= Solution

The <isolobal analogy> compares fragments through their <frontier molecular orbitals>: their number, approximate energies, shapes, symmetries and <electron> occupancies should correspond. It does not require the fragments to have the same total number of <electrons> or the same constituent elements. If the orbitals available for forming new bonds correspond, analogous ways of joining the fragments become plausible.

For example, **$\mathrm{CH_3^{\bullet}}$ and $\mathrm{Mn(CO)_5^{\bullet}}$ are isolobal**. The <methyl radical> has one singly occupied approximately outward-pointing orbital; removing one <carbonyl ligand> from an <octahedral molecular geometry> leaves an analogous frontier orbital on the 17-electron $\mathrm{Mn(CO)_5}$ fragment. Joining two such fragments pairs the two <electrons>: the organic example is the C–C bond of <ethane>, and the organometallic example is the Mn–Mn bond of $\mathrm{Mn_2(CO)_{10}}$. The analogy predicts a bonding pattern, rather than equality of bond energies or reaction rates.<Building Bridges Between Inorganic and Organic Chemistry (Nobel Lecture) by Roald Hoffmann>[1982, discussion of the methyl and metal-carbonyl fragment analogy]{ref}

Use <Wade's rules> with the metal included as a skeletal vertex. Each BH vertex supplies two skeletal <electrons>, each CH supplies three, and the two additional <hydrogen> atoms supply two more. The $\mathrm{Ru(CO)_3}$ fragment supplies $8+3(2)-12=2$ skeletal <electrons> after the metal's non-skeletal orbitals have been accounted for. Thus
$$
N_{\mathrm{skeletal}}=3(2)+2(3)+2+2=16,\qquad \mathrm{SEP}=8=6+2.
$$
The six-vertex cluster is therefore **nido, with a pentagonal-pyramidal skeleton**: Ru is the apex, bonded to the five vertices of the planar $\mathrm{B_3C_2}$ base, and the three <carbonyl ligands> project away from that base. This is the six-vertex fragment obtained by removing an equatorial vertex from a seven-vertex <pentagonal bipyramid>. The five ring atoms carry their terminal B–H or C–H bonds; the two additional <hydrogens> belong to the hydrogen-rich open-face bonding framework. The skeletal <electron> count does not, by itself, locate every <bridging hydride>, so a unique detailed <hydrogen> arrangement should not be inferred from it.

= Solution
{parent=b}

There are **two carbon-placement isomers**, represented by the cyclic base sequences $\mathrm{CCBBB}$ and $\mathrm{CBCBB}$. The <carbon> vertices are adjacent in the first and separated by one <boron> vertex in the second. Rotation or reversal of a sequence does not produce another constitutional isomer.

Each arrangement has a <reflection symmetry> that exchanges a pair of <boron> sites while leaving the third distinct. Consequently each can give **two $^{11}\mathrm B$ environments, with site populations $2:1$**, in a <proton-decoupled NMR> spectrum. This assumes that the <hydrogen> and <carbonyl> arrangements preserve, or rapidly average to, the corresponding skeletal symmetry. Two peaks alone neither choose between the two skeletons nor establish a unique <hydrogen> arrangement; accidental overlap or <fluxionality> can also reduce the number of observed signals.

= Solution
{parent=c}

The usual qualitative prediction is the **carbon-separated $\mathrm{CBCBB}$ isomer**. It has four B–C ring edges and one B–B edge, whereas the adjacent-carbon isomer has two B–C, two B–B and one C–C edge. Separating the more electron-rich <carbon> vertices distributes skeletal <electron> density more evenly and avoids concentrating both <carbon> vertices on one edge, a useful <carborane> stability heuristic.

This is a thermodynamic suggestion, not a consequence of the peak count. <hydrogen> placement, metal–ring interactions and entropy can affect the ordering. Establishing the preferred isomer experimentally would require reversible interconversion and an equilibrium composition, rather than simply identifying the product formed fastest.

**A <triangular prism>** has exactly the required nine metal–metal edges: six edges around two triangular faces and three edges joining corresponding vertices of those faces. It naturally offers two symmetry-distinct edge families, of sizes six and three.

With twelve $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ ligands, neutral $\mathrm{Re_6Cl_{12}}$ has total rhenium oxidation number $+12$, hence six formally Re(II), $d^5$ centres. After treating the Re–Cl interactions as ligand bonds, the available metal $d$ <electrons> are
$$
6(7-2)=30\ \mathrm{electrons}=15\ \mathrm{bonding\ pairs}.
$$
Two localized <bond order> assignments use those fifteen pairs:
$$
\begin{array}{c|cc|c}
&\text{six triangular edges}&\text{three prism edges}&\text{total bond order}\\
\mathrm{I}&1&3&6+9=15\\
\mathrm{II}&2&1&12+3=15
\end{array}
$$
In scheme I the three inter-triangle edges should be shorter; in scheme II the six <triangle> edges should be shorter. Both give each Re five <electrons>' worth of metal–metal bond-order contributions: two singles plus a triple, or two doubles plus a single. **The <electron> count permits both schemes**; identifying which family is shorter distinguishes them at this level. Actual cluster <molecular orbitals> are delocalized, so these integer <bond orders> summarize bonding rather than asserting isolated textbook multiple bonds on every edge.

= Solution
{parent=a}

The key is coupling <redox equilibrium> to <hydrolysis> and <precipitation (chemistry)>. Ce(IV) has a much greater tendency than Ce(III) to form a hydrated <cerium dioxide> solid. Raising <pH> therefore preferentially stabilizes the oxidized form. In the presence of dissolved <oxygen> a representative net reaction is
$$
4\mathrm{Ce^{3+}}+\mathrm{O_2}+6\mathrm{H_2O}
\rightleftharpoons4\mathrm{CeO_2(s)}+12\mathrm{H^+}.
$$
Removing $\mathrm{H^+}$ drives this reaction towards Ce(IV). Writing the solid as hydrated oxide changes its water content, not this redox argument.

The <Nernst equation> makes the <pH> effect explicit. At $25^\circ\mathrm C$, the reduction $\mathrm{CeO_2+4H^++e^-\to Ce^{3+}+2H_2O}$ has a <pH> slope approximately $-0.236\ \mathrm{V}$ per <pH> unit at fixed Ce(III) activity. The <oxygen>/water reduction has slope about $-0.059\ \mathrm{V}$ per <pH> unit. Raising <pH> thus makes oxidation of Ce(III) by <oxygen> increasingly favourable within the relevant phase-stability region. Conversely strong acid favours soluble Ce(III) relative to the oxide and makes Ce(IV) a stronger <oxidizing agent>. **<pH> changes the redox driving force; an <electron> acceptor or donor is still required**, and slow <oxygen> evolution or oxidation kinetics can prevent immediate equilibration.

**Not as a general aqueous separation reaction for the other <lanthanides>.** Most strongly favour the trivalent state in ordinary water chemistry. Ce(IV) is unusually accessible because it has the empty $4f$ shell and is strongly stabilized by oxide formation. Some other <lanthanides> have accessible alternative <oxidation states>, such as Eu(II), and higher oxides of Pr or Tb can exist under suitable conditions; this does not imply that they undergo the same Ce(III)/Ce(IV) pH-controlled aqueous oxidation.

{parent=4}

= Solution
{parent=c}

A natural feed can contain <thorium> and <uranium>, as well as other <lanthanides>. Th(IV) hydrolyses readily and can accompany the cerium precipitate; <uranium> speciation depends on its <oxidation state> and the ligands present. Raising <pH> too far also precipitates other rare-earth hydroxides. Even before their bulk <precipitation (chemistry)> thresholds, adsorption and <coprecipitation> on a high-area hydrated cerium oxide can contaminate the solid. Local high-pH zones during base addition and incomplete Ce(III) oxidation make the separation less reproducible.

Thus **a cerium-rich precipitate need not be pure cerium**. A sensible test measures the Ce <oxidation state>, analyses both filtrate and washed solid, and includes thorium-containing and thorium-free mixtures; otherwise low dissolved cerium could be mistaken for selective purification.

= Solution
{parent=d}

A better controlled route is **selective <solvent extraction> in acid, followed by selective stripping**, rather than precipitating everything near overlapping <hydrolysis> thresholds. Oxidize Ce(III) to Ce(IV) in a controlled acidic nitrate medium, extract its nitrate complexes into an organic donor phase, and separate the phases. Then reduce the extracted Ce(IV) to Ce(III) and back-extract it into an aqueous phase. Trivalent rare earths are less strongly extracted under suitable conditions.

This still needs a <thorium> separation: <tributyl phosphate> can coextract Th(IV). One demonstrated strategy deliberately coextracts Ce(IV) and Th(IV), then uses reducing <peroxide> solution to strip Ce as Ce(III), followed by a separate <thorium> strip. The appropriate controls are distribution measurements for each ion at the chosen acid concentration, a cerium oxidation-state check, and a complete mass balance over extraction and stripping. **Selectivity comes from combining <oxidation state> and <complexation>, not from assuming that one extraction removes every contaminant.**<Coextraction and successive selective stripping of cerium(IV) and thorium from monazite leach solutions with tributyl phosphate (TBP) by A. H. Ali>[2017, abstract: coextraction from nitric acid and successive <peroxide>/water stripping]{ref}

Disconnect the tertiary <alcohol> at its bond to the alkenyl group. The required <carbonyl> partner is $\mathrm{MeCOCH(Me)Et}$; the other partner is an **E-configured $\mathrm{PhCH_2CH{=}CH}$ organometallic reagent**. This disconnection fixes the <alkene> geometry before the <alcohol> is made.

First form the ethyl-side <enolate> of <butan-2-one> under equilibrating conditions, then trap it with ethyl iodide and isolate the required monoalkylated <ketone>:
$$
\mathrm{MeCOCH_2Me}\longrightarrow\mathrm{MeCOCH(Me)Et}.
$$
The incoming group is Et: methylation would give a <carbon> skeleton one <carbon> too short. Because an unsymmetrical <ketone> can form two <enolates> and undergo repeated alkylation, regioisomer separation and controlled conversion are part of this step.

Independently perform a halo-<Wittig reaction> on phenylacetaldehyde using $\mathrm{Ph_3P{=}CHBr}$ and isolate **E-$\mathrm{PhCH_2CH{=}CHBr}$** from any geometrical mixture. <magnesium> insertion in dry ether gives the corresponding <vinylmagnesium halide>; add the isolated <ketone> at low temperature and protonate the resulting <alkoxide>. Addition to C=O preserves the pre-existing C=C geometry:
$$
(E)\text{-}\mathrm{PhCH_2CH{=}CHMgBr}
+\mathrm{MeCOCH(Me)Et}
\longrightarrow
(E)\text{-}\mathrm{PhCH_2CH{=}CHC(OH)(Me)CH(Me)Et}.
$$
**The E <alkene> and the complete fifteen-carbon skeleton are obtained.** No absolute configuration is specified at the newly formed <alcohol> centre, so an unselected stereoisomeric mixture there is acceptable. An alternative <carbon>–<carbon> disconnection is <acetylide> addition to the same <ketone> followed by a controlled trans-selective <alkyne> reduction; the vinyl-reagent route avoids relying on reduction of a tertiary propargylic <alcohol>.

= Solution

Open the <lactone> formally at its acyl–<oxygen> bond, then replace the resulting secondary <alcohol> by its <ketone> precursor. With $R=\mathrm{CH(Me)CH(Me)_2}$, the useful sequence of retrosynthetic intermediates is
$$
\text{lactone}\ \Longleftarrow\
\mathrm{HO_2CCH(Me)CH_2CH_2CH(OH)R}
\ \Longleftarrow\
\mathrm{EtO_2CCH(Me)CH_2CH_2COR}.
$$
Disconnect the last compound as a <Michael addition> product of the methyl-side <enolate> of $\mathrm{RCOCH_3}$ and <ethyl methacrylate>:
$$
\mathrm{RCOCH_3+CH_2{=}C(Me)CO_2Et}
\longrightarrow
\mathrm{RCOCH_2CH_2CH(Me)CO_2Et}.
$$
The substituted <carbon> of the methacrylate becomes the methyl-bearing <carbon> alpha to the <lactone> <carbonyl>. The <ketone> <carbon> becomes the oxygen-bearing ring <carbon>. This checks the six-membered ring connectivity.

The required branched methyl <ketone> itself comes from <butan-2-one> by ethyl-side <aldol addition> to <acetone>, followed by <dehydration> and selective <hydrogenation>:
$$
\mathrm{MeCOCH_2Me}
\longrightarrow\mathrm{MeCOCH(Me)C(OH)Me_2}
\longrightarrow\mathrm{MeCOC(Me){=}CMe_2}
\longrightarrow\mathrm{MeCOCH(Me)CHMe_2}.
$$
Choose and isolate the aldol regioisomer shown. For the subsequent Michael step, use a bulky base at low temperature to favour its less hindered methyl-side <kinetic enolate>. Reduce the resulting <ketone> selectively with $\mathrm{NaBH_4}$ while retaining the <ester>, hydrolyse the <ester>, then acidify and promote intramolecular <esterification> with water removal. **The hydroxy acid closes to the required six-membered <lactone>.** There is no specified stereochemical relationship to enforce, but a stereochemically pure target would require an additional selectivity or resolution step.

= Solution

An <acetal> <retrosynthesis> separates the target into **trans-cyclohexane-1,2-diol and cyclohexyl methyl <ketone>**. Both fragments can be prepared using separate portions of <cyclohexene>.

For the diol, <epoxidation> with a <peroxy acid> such as mCPBA gives <cyclohexene> oxide. Acid-catalysed opening with water occurs by backside attack on the protonated <epoxide>, giving the **anti, hence trans, diol**. A syn dihydroxylation would give the wrong relative configuration.

For the <ketone>, add HBr to another portion of <cyclohexene> to obtain bromocyclohexane, form its <grignard reagent> with Mg in dry ether, and add <acetonitrile>. Hydrolyse the resulting <magnesium> imine salt to $\mathrm{C_6H_{11}COMe}$. A <nitrile> is useful here because addition followed by <hydrolysis> stops at the <ketone> instead of giving the double addition associated with an <ester>.

Finally combine the isolated trans diol and <ketone> with catalytic acid and remove water to form the cyclic <ketal>. The <ketone> <carbon> becomes the <acetal> <carbon> bearing Me and cyclohexyl; the two original diol C–O bonds retain their relative configuration. **Anti <epoxide> opening supplies the required trans relationship before <ketal> formation.** <ketal> formation is an equilibrium and the trans-fused product may be less favourable than an unstrained <acetal>, so effective water removal and isolation are necessary; substituting a cis diol is not an acceptable shortcut.

The five-membered enone-like <carbon> framework can be obtained by an intramolecular <aldol condensation> of
$$
\mathrm{MeCOCH_2CH(CN)CH(CN)CH_2COMe}.
$$
Enolization at an internal methylene next to one <ketone> followed by attack on the opposite <ketone> closes a five-membered ring. <dehydration> leaves one acetyl group attached to one <alkene> <carbon> and one methyl group on the other. The two nitrile-bearing <carbons> remain adjacent and their original cis relationship is retained if <epimerization> is avoided.

Now reverse a reductive <ozonolysis>: the diketone arises by opening a six-membered <cyclohexene> whose <alkene> <carbons> each carry Me. That <cyclohexene> is the <Diels-Alder reaction> product of **2,3-dimethylbuta-1,3-diene and <maleonitrile>**. The latter is the supplied cis dinitrile. The <stereospecificity> of the cycloaddition puts the two CN groups on the same face.

Thus the forward plan is **Diels–Alder addition, reductive <ozonolysis>, then five-membered aldol ring closure and <dehydration>**. Use a reductive ozone work-up that stops at the diketone. During the final step, avoid prolonged strongly basic heating because the <carbons> bearing CN can be deprotonated and epimerized. Achiral reagents give the racemic pair with the required cis relationship; resolution would be necessary if only the depicted <enantiomer> were required. The diene supplies six <carbons> and <maleonitrile> supplies four, accounting for all ten <carbons> without an unexplained <carbon> deletion.

For the CO and NCO cases, the added fragment acts as a <Lewis base> at <boron>, rather than becoming a new skeletal vertex. **Both have a triangular $\mathrm{B_3}$ framework**. A useful localized drawing has three $\mathrm{BH_2}$ groups, one B–H–B bridge between two of them, and the donor attached to the third <boron>. The <boron>–<boron> framework includes <three-centre two-electron bonds>; its edges should not all be read as ordinary independent two-electron single bonds.

For the neutral <carbonyl> adduct, attach CO through <carbon>, giving $\mathrm{B{\leftarrow}C{\equiv}O}$ at the donor-bearing $\mathrm{BH_2}$ site. For the anionic isocyanate adduct, attach $\mathrm{NCO^-}$ through <nitrogen>, giving a B–N=C=O connectivity and the overall $-1$ charge. In both drawings the <hydrogen> count is $3(2)+1=7$. Weak or asymmetric additional B–H contacts can distort this idealized drawing; a hydrogen-rich <borane> is not fully characterized by a polygon alone. The <carbonyl> triborane structure was established in the original <borane> study.<Boranes. XXXVII. Reactions of triborane(7) and the structure of triborane(7) carbonyl by James D. Glore and others>[1973, pp.2175–2178, structural assignment of the <carbonyl> adduct]{ref}

The rhodium fragment is instead a skeletal building block. Its neutral <electron> count is $9+2(2)+1=14$, so it contributes two skeletal <electrons>, like a BH vertex. Combining it with $\mathrm{B_3H_7}$ gives $3(2)+4+2=12$ skeletal <electrons>, or six pairs, for four vertices. A consistent low-vertex structure is **a tetrahedral $\mathrm{RhB_3}$ skeleton**, with the $\mathrm{RhH(PPh_3)_2}$ fragment capping the <boron> <triangle>. Keep the terminal Rh–H ligand distinct from the seven <hydrogens> on the <borane> portion. The latter can again be represented by three terminal $\mathrm{BH_2}$ groups and a B–H–B bridge. The total formula then contains eight <hydrogens> outside the phenyl groups. Treating Rh as a pendant one-centre <Lewis base> would miss its skeletal role.

Neutral counting assigns five <electrons> to <cyclopentadienyl ligand> and two to CO. Thus each $\mathrm{FeCpCO}$ fragment has $8+5+2=15$ <valence electrons> and contributes three skeletal <electrons>; each $\mathrm{CoCpCO}$ fragment has sixteen and contributes four:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm A:\quad&4(15)-12(4)=12\ \mathrm{skeletal\ electrons}=6\ \mathrm{pairs},\\
\mathrm B:\quad&3(16)-12(3)=12\ \mathrm{skeletal\ electrons}=6\ \mathrm{pairs}.
\end{aligned}
$$
For A the six-pair four-vertex count is the nido count derived from a five-vertex <trigonal bipyramid>. Removing an axial vertex leaves a **<tetrahedron>**: the three equatorial vertices plus the remaining axial vertex. For B the corresponding arachno fragment is obtained by removing both axial vertices from that same parent, leaving a **three-metal <triangle>**. Small clusters require specifying the parent and removed vertices; mechanically calling every nido fragment a square pyramid is wrong.

The Fe tetramer is indeed known to have a tetrahedral $\mathrm{Fe_4}$ core, with four face-capping CO groups. The <carbon> atoms of those <carbonyls> and the iron atoms form interpenetrating tetrahedra, giving a cubane-like Fe–C connectivity. Bridging rather than terminal CO changes the distribution of local donation, not the total <electron> count above.<Structural Characterization of the Cyclopentadienyliron Carbonyl Tetramer, Fe4(eta5-C5H5)4(CO)4. For a neutral <borane> $\mathrm{B_nH_m}$ with one terminal B–H bond assigned to each vertex, the skeletal <electron> number is $3n+m-2n=n+m$; an overall charge $q-$ adds $q$ <electrons>. Suitable twelve-electron correspondences are
$$
\boxed{\mathrm C=B_4H_8,\qquad D=[B_3H_8]^- .}
$$
For C, four BH contributions plus four additional <hydrogens> give $8+4=12$ <electrons>. For D, three BH contributions plus five additional <hydrogens> and the negative charge give $6+5+1=12$. They map to the tetrahedral and triangular low-vertex skeletons respectively. The neutral formal alternative for D is $\mathrm{B_3H_9}$, also with twelve skeletal <electrons>.

The <electron counting> correspondence alone must not be read as evidence that the neutral <boranes> are isolable stable bulk materials. Low-vertex hydrogen-rich <boranes> can be transient; **the triangular octahydrotriborate anion is the established practical three-boron example**, whereas neutral $\mathrm{B_3H_9}$ is a formal analogy rather than an equally established preparative product. The requested skeletal correspondence should be distinguished from a claim about comparable stability or isolation conditions.<Reactions of the octahydrotriborate(1-) anion, B3H8-, with some complexes of cobalt(I), cobalt(II), rhodium(I), and iridium(I), and the characterization of the borallyl complex IrIII(eta3-B3H7)(CO)H(PPh3)2 by Norman N. For E, each BH supplies two skeletal <electrons> and each <phosphorus> vertex supplies three after retaining a <lone pair>. Hence $10(2)+2(3)=26$ <electrons>, or thirteen pairs, matching a twelve-vertex <closo cluster>. **Place the two adjacent P vertices on a <regular icosahedron>.** The remaining ten <boron> vertices fall into four symmetry classes: two sites adjacent to both P vertices, four adjacent to exactly one, two adjacent to neither but opposite the common-neighbour pair, and two opposite the P pair. The populations are **$2:4:2:2$**, so four <boron> resonances are expected. Interchanging the identical <phosphorus> vertices is allowed when determining chemical equivalence.

For F, a ten-vertex <bicapped square antiprism> offers a consistent closo model. There are eight BH vertices, one B vertex bearing the $\mathrm{NEt_3}$ donor instead of H, and one P vertex. Their skeletal contributions are respectively $16$, $3$ and $3$, giving **22 <electrons>, eleven pairs**. The donor-bearing B contribution follows from B plus the donated <lone pair> minus the exoskeletal B–N bond, $3+2-2=3$.

One explicit arrangement consistent with six <boron> signals puts P at one axial cap and the $\mathrm{NEt_3}$ substituent on one B vertex of the nearer square. The remaining <mirror plane> through that B, the opposite vertex of its square, and the cap axis leaves three B sites individually fixed: the donor-bearing B, its opposite square vertex and the other axial cap. The other six B sites form three exchanged pairs. Thus the nine <borons> give **three singleton environments and three two-site environments**, six signals in all. The original compound has a crystallographically characterized positional assignment; the spectrum alone asks for a compatible structure and does not establish a unique positional isomer. A <single-crystal X-ray diffraction> determination or assigned heteronuclear correlations would distinguish alternatives.<Synthesis of polyhedral phosphaboranes: crystal structure of 6-(C2H5)3N·2-PB9H8 by John L. = Solution

Strong ligand-to-metal <pi donation> is favoured by **an occupied ligand <p orbital> and a suitable empty metal <d orbital>** of matching symmetry, with reasonably similar energies and good spatial overlap. Small donor atoms such as O or N can supply short bonds and efficient overlap; oxo, imido and nitrido ligands are familiar examples. Early <transition metals> in high <oxidation states>, often $d^0$, are particularly favourable because the metal acceptor orbitals are vacant and relatively low in energy. More spatially extended $4d$ and $5d$ orbitals can strengthen the overlap.

Metal-to-ligand <pi backbonding> is the complementary case: electron-rich, low-oxidation-state metals donate occupied <d orbitals> into low-lying ligand <antibonding orbitals>, as for CO. In both directions, filling a metal–ligand pi-antibonding orbital offsets the multiple-bond stabilization. The relevant prediction is orbital occupancy and overlap, rather than the presence of a formal double-bond line in a drawing.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Use the stated local axes: $z$ is radial along O–Ru, $x$ is tangential within the Ru–O plane, and $y$ is perpendicular to that plane. The <oxygen>'s three $sp^2$ hybrids make the in-plane sigma framework. Its remaining **$p_y$ orbital** is perpendicular to the plane and can overlap with **one $d_{yz}$ orbital on each Ru**.

Choose the local orbital phases so their O-facing lobes agree. Of the three metal orbitals, only
$$
D=\frac{d_{yz}^{(1)}+d_{yz}^{(2)}+d_{yz}^{(3)}}{\sqrt3}
$$
has the appropriate combination to interact with <oxygen> $p_y$. The other two independent combinations have coefficients summing to zero and are nonbonding with respect to the central <oxygen>. Mixing $D$ with $p_y$ gives one lower pi-bonding orbital and one upper pi-antibonding orbital. Each Ru also retains its $d_{xy}$ and $d_{xz}$ orbitals as nonbonding with respect to this central-oxo pi interaction. Thus the complete low-energy diagram contains **one bonding level, eight metal-nonbonding levels, and one antibonding level**. The eight consist of two $d_{yz}$ combinations, three $d_{xy}$ and three $d_{xz}$ orbitals. They need not be exactly degenerate once other ligands are considered.

For the neutral formula as printed, oxide contributes $-2$ and six acetates contribute $-6$. The Ru oxidation numbers therefore sum to $+8$, giving sixteen metal d <electrons> overall, formally two Ru(III) and one Ru(II), possibly delocalized. Including the <oxygen> pi pair gives eighteen <electrons>, filling the <bonding orbital> and all eight <nonbonding orbitals>, leaving pi* empty. If the often encountered singly charged cation were intended instead, there would be seventeen <electrons> and one singly occupied nonbonding level. This charge distinction changes occupancy, not the orbital connectivity.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The three $d_{xz}$ orbitals are in the Ru–O plane and can overlap directly along the Ru–Ru edges. Their locally rotated lobes have nonzero edge-directed overlap, producing metal–metal bonding and antibonding combinations; a simple <triangle> model resolves the three orbitals into a singlet and a doublet. The phases and ordering depend on the chosen local basis, so the important feature is the **complete set of three combinations**.

In the occupancy obtained in (i), this $d_{xz}$ set contains six <electrons>. All three combinations are filled: the stabilization of the bonding combinations is offset by occupation of their antibonding partners. Equivalently, the sum of the three eigenvalues of a pure off-diagonal interaction matrix is zero. Hence **there is no net Ru–Ru <bond order> from this filled set in the stated model**. Overlap alone is not evidence of a net metal–metal bond. This conclusion neglects higher-order mixing and the interactions explicitly excluded from the qualitative diagram.

**Nitrogen-bound $\mathrm{Eu{-}NCS}$** is favoured. Eu(III) is a small, highly charged <hard Lewis acid>, whereas the <nitrogen> end of <thiocyanate> is harder than its more polarizable sulfur end. **Water** is the preferred solvent ligand in the qualitative prediction. Its <oxygen> is a hard donor, it is less sterically demanding than ethanol, and it can occupy a crowded Eu(III) coordination sphere. Two bidentate <bipyridine> ligands provide four N donors and three terminal NCS ligands provide three more. Adding one water gives **$n=1$ and <coordination number> eight**:
$$
\mathrm{Eu(NCS)_3(bpy)_2(H_2O)}.
$$
Eight-coordinate arrangements, distorted between familiar ideal polyhedra, are plausible for a sterically crowded Eu(III) centre. A second water would give <coordination number> nine, which is not forbidden for a <lanthanide>; the requested value is a chemically motivated prediction, not something fixed by charge balance. Diffraction or a direct hydration-number measurement is needed to establish it uniquely.

= Solution

An N-bound <thiocyanate> need not make an exactly linear Eu–N–C linkage. Eu(III) bonding is predominantly electrostatic, with weak directional covalent contributions from the shielded $4f$ orbitals. There is consequently less energetic reward for enforcing a precisely collinear metal–donor arrangement than in a strongly directional transition-metal <pi bond>. In the crowded eight-coordinate sphere, bending the external NCS groups can reduce ligand–ligand repulsion while retaining favourable Eu–N distances. **Angles of $150^\circ$–$160^\circ$ are compatible with bent N coordination**; they do not require changing the linkage to sulfur. This concerns the Eu–N–C angle, not bending the internal N–C–S skeleton.

The <bipyrimidine> ligand has two distinct N,N-chelating pockets. It can use one pocket as a terminal <bidentate ligand>, or use both to bridge two Eu centres. Take all three nitrates per Eu to be bidentate O,O donors.

A consistent structure for B is **$\mathrm{Eu(NO_3)_3(bpm)_2}$**, with two terminal, singly chelating bpm ligands. Six nitrate <oxygens> plus four bpm <nitrogens> give **<coordination number> ten**. The unused pocket of each terminal bpm points away from that Eu centre.

For C, use a dimer **$\mathrm{Eu_2(NO_3)_6(bpm)_3}$**. Place one bpm between the metals, chelating each Eu through a different N,N pocket. Add one terminal bidentate bpm to each Eu and three bidentate nitrates to each Eu. Each metal again has $2+2+6=10$ donor atoms, while the overall Eu:bpm ratio is $2:3$. In this model all four <nitrogens> of the bridging bpm are used, but only two of each terminal bpm are used.

Both structures have their accessible coordination sites occupied by multidentate ligands, and the <chelate effect> favours retention of those donors over adding or exchanging a solvent molecule. This accounts for the absence of coordinated solvent while allowing a higher <coordination number> than the <thiocyanate> example. Stoichiometry alone does not exclude polymeric alternatives or different nitrate binding modes; the monomer/dimer pair is an explicit internally consistent prediction.

Disconnect the <amide> into **2-propylhexanoic acid and N-methyl-2-(4-hexylphenyl)ethylamine**. This keeps the <nitrogen> substituents distinct: one is Me and the other is the two-carbon aryl side chain. The acid's alpha <carbon> must bear both n-butyl and n-propyl groups.

For the acid fragment, esterify the supplied <hexanoic acid>, form its <ester enolate> with LDA, and alkylate with 1-iodopropane:
$$
\mathrm{EtO_2CCH_2(nBu)}
\longrightarrow\mathrm{EtO_2CCH(nPr)(nBu)}.
$$
<hydrolysis> gives 2-propylhexanoic acid; conversion with $\mathrm{SOCl_2}$ gives the <acyl chloride> for the final coupling. Limit the alkylating reagent and isolate the monoalkylated <ester> to avoid overalkylation.

For the aryl fragment, use another portion of <hexanoic acid> to make hexanoyl chloride. A <Friedel-Crafts acylation> of <bromobenzene> with this reagent and $\mathrm{AlCl_3}$ gives ortho and para products; isolate the para bromoaryl <ketone>. A <Wolff-Kishner reduction> converts $\mathrm{ArCO(CH_2)_4Me}$ into $\mathrm{ArCH_2(CH_2)_4Me}$ without deliberately hydrogenolyzing the aryl bromide. Acylation followed by <carbonyl reduction> installs an unrearranged n-hexyl group; direct Friedel–Crafts alkylation is a less reliable way to do that.

Convert the retained aryl bromide into its <grignard reagent>, open <ethylene oxide>, and work up to obtain $\mathrm{p\!-\!HexC_6H_4CH_2CH_2OH}$. Oxidize the primary <alcohol> to $\mathrm{p\!-\!HexC_6H_4CH_2CHO}$ under conditions that stop at the <aldehyde>, then perform <reductive amination> with <methylamine> and <sodium triacetoxyborohydride>. Excess <methylamine> helps favour the desired secondary amine over further alkylation. Finally acylate that secondary amine with the isolated <acyl chloride>, using a separate base to capture HCl.

**The final <amide> has an N-methyl group, the para n-hexyl phenethyl group, and the required n-butyl/n-propyl acyl branching.** The <carbon> check is $9$ in the acyl fragment plus $1+2+6+6=15$ in the amine fragment, giving $24$ overall. No absolute <stereochemistry> is specified at the acid alpha <carbon>.

Disconnect the internal **Z <alkene> by a non-stabilized <Wittig reaction>**:
$$
\mathrm{MeO_2C(CH_2)_5CH{=}PPh_3}
\quad+\quad
\mathrm{OHCCH(nPr)CH_2CH{=}CH_2}.
$$
The <aldehyde> <carbon> becomes the right-hand internal <alkene> <carbon>; the ylide <carbon> becomes the left-hand one. Both the methyl <ester> and the remote terminal <alkene> survive the olefination.

Make the <aldehyde> from pentanal by <Stork enamine reaction>: form its <pyrrolidine> <enamine>, react at <carbon> with allyl bromide, then hydrolyse to $\mathrm{OHCCH(nPr)CH_2CH{=}CH_2}$. The original alpha <carbon> of pentanal now bears allyl and n-propyl substituents. Control the alkylation to avoid multiple substitutions.

The other branch needs a three-carbon extension of the supplied four-carbon bromo <alcohol>. Protect the OH of $\mathrm{HO(CH_2)_4Br}$ as a <tetrahydropyranyl ether>. Convert the bromide into an <organocuprate> and add it conjugatively to <methyl acrylate>:
$$
\mathrm{THPO(CH_2)_4{-}Cu}
+\mathrm{CH_2{=}CHCO_2Me}
\longrightarrow\mathrm{THPO(CH_2)_6CO_2Me}.
$$
Here the organocopper formula is shorthand for a suitable cuprate preparation, not a claim that an isolated monomer is necessary. The acrylate contributes two methylenes and the <ester> <carbonyl>. Remove the THP group, replace the resulting terminal OH by Br, and react with $\mathrm{PPh_3}$ to form $\mathrm{[Ph_3PCH_2(CH_2)_5CO_2Me]^+Br^-}$. Deprotonate adjacent to <phosphorus> to generate the non-stabilized ylide. This proton is much more acidic than the <ester> alpha protons, but controlled base stoichiometry and low temperature remain important.

Add the allylated <aldehyde> under conditions favouring the usual kinetic **<Z-selective Wittig olefination>**, and separate any E isomer. The result is
$$
\boxed{(Z)\text{-}\mathrm{MeO_2C(CH_2)_5CH{=}CHCH(nPr)CH_2CH{=}CH_2}.}
$$
The four-carbon starting <alcohol> plus the three-carbon acrylate backbone supplies the seven-carbon acid-derived chain; the <ester> Me gives an eighth <carbon>. Pentanal plus allyl gives the other eight. The total is sixteen <carbons>, including the terminal <alkene>. A two-carbon extension would produce the wrong chain length.

Disconnect the N–cyclohexyl bond as a late <reductive amination> of a primary amine with <cyclohexanone>. Disconnect the exocyclic methylene as a <Wittig methylenation> of a substituted <cyclohexanone>. The useful ring precursor is **3-methyl-5-substituted cyclohex-2-en-1-one**, accessible by a <Robinson annulation>. The pendant substituent throughout ring construction should be $(\mathrm{CH_2})_4\mathrm{NHBoc}$.

Start with <nucleophilic substitution> of the supplied bromo <ester> by cyanide:
$$
\mathrm{Br(CH_2)_3CO_2Me}
\longrightarrow\mathrm{NC(CH_2)_3CO_2Me}.
$$
Reduce the <nitrile> selectively to the primary amine while retaining the <ester>, for example by controlled catalytic <hydrogenation> with <Raney nickel> in the presence of ammonia, and trap the amine promptly as its Boc <carbamate>. Avoid heating or concentrating the free amino <ester>, which can cyclize to a <lactam>. <lithium aluminium hydride> would be unsuitable at this stage because it also reduces the <ester>.

Partial reduction of the protected <ester> with DIBAL at low temperature gives $\mathrm{BocNH(CH_2)_4CHO}$. The <carbon> of the original <nitrile> is now the methylene nearest <nitrogen>; the original <ester> <carbonyl> is the <aldehyde> <carbon>. Condense this <aldehyde> with <acetone> and dehydrate to $\mathrm{BocNH(CH_2)_4CH{=}CHCOMe}$.

Now add the <enolate> of a second <acetone> molecule in a <Michael addition>. With $R=(\mathrm{CH_2})_4\mathrm{NHBoc}$, the resulting chain is
$$
\mathrm{MeCOCH_2CH(R)CH_2COMe}.
$$
Intramolecular <aldol condensation> gives **3-Me-5-R-cyclohex-2-en-1-one**. The beta methyl derives from the <carbonyl> end attacked in the ring closure; the pendant aminoalkyl chain remains at C5. Hydrogenate the ring C=C selectively, retaining the <ketone>, then methylenate the <ketone> with preformed $\mathrm{Ph_3P{=}CH_2}$. Do the <hydrogenation> before installing the exocyclic methylene so that it is not hydrogenated away.

Remove Boc under brief mild conditions, checking for acid-induced exocyclic <alkene> isomerization. <reductive amination> of the resulting primary amine with <cyclohexanone>, followed by selective imine reduction, installs the secondary N-cyclohexyl group. Use controlled stoichiometry to suppress tertiary amine formation. **The methyl and four-carbon aminoalkyl substituents occupy the required 1,3-related ring positions, with the exocyclic methylene at the former <carbonyl>.** No ring <stereochemistry> is specified. The target contains eighteen <carbons>: six ring <carbons>, one ring methyl, one methylene, four in the linker, and six in the N-cyclohexyl group.

The key stereochemical disconnection is the **cis-1,3-disubstituted cyclopentane**, not a direct functionalization of two arbitrary cyclopentane positions. A <Diels-Alder reaction> of <cyclopentadiene> with <nitroethylene> fixes both ends of the new two-carbon bridge on the same face of the original five-membered ring.

Isolate the useful nitronorbornene adduct, convert its secondary nitro group to a <ketone> by a <Nef reaction>, and then hydrogenate the remaining ring <alkene> to norcamphor. Performing the nitro-to-ketone conversion before catalytic <hydrogenation> avoids simply reducing the nitro group to an amine.

Use a <Baeyer-Villiger oxidation> to insert <oxygen> between the <carbonyl> <carbon> and its adjacent bridgehead <carbon>. Bridgehead migration is favoured over migration of the competing methylene; isolate the required <lactone> regioisomer if necessary. This opens the way to a hydroxy cyclopentyl side chain without breaking the original five-carbon ring. In the resulting bridged <lactone>, one original <cyclopentadiene> terminus is bonded to O and the other bears the $\mathrm{CH_2CO}$ segment.

Reduce the <lactone> selectively with DIBAL to its <lactol>. Ring opening of that <lactol> gives the <aldehyde> equivalent **cis-3-hydroxycyclopentyl-$\mathrm{CH_2CHO}$**. The ring OH and the two-carbon <aldehyde> side chain are cis because their original attachments were the two ends of the Diels–Alder bridge; neither the migration nor <lactol> opening inverts those cyclopentane <carbons>.

Perform a <Horner-Wadsworth-Emmons reaction> on the <lactol>/<aldehyde> equilibrium using $\mathrm{(EtO)_2P(O)CH_2CO_2R}$, where $R=\mathrm{CH(Me)CH_2CH(Me)_2}$. Preform the phosphonate anion and allow for consumption of base by the <lactol> OH. Standard stabilized-phosphonate conditions favour the required **E-$\mathrm{CH{=}CHCO_2R}$** unit. No further <hydrogenation> should follow it.

The required <ester> substituent can be prepared by reducing 4-methylpentan-2-one to 4-methylpentan-2-ol, esterifying with bromoacetic acid, and performing an <Arbuzov reaction> with triethyl phosphite to make the phosphonoacetate. No absolute configuration is indicated at that alcohol-derived centre.

**Bridge-derived cis ring <stereochemistry> plus E-selective phosphonate olefination gives both specified relationships.** Achiral cycloaddition and subsequent reagents give a racemic pair with this relative configuration; isolation of one absolute <enantiomer> would require resolution or an asymmetric variant. The acid fragment contains nine <carbons>: five from <cyclopentadiene>, two from <nitroethylene> and two from the phosphonoacetate. The <ester> alkyl group adds six, giving fifteen overall. The atom tracing in the original schematic in question 8 shows why <oxygen> insertion retains the five-membered ring rather than producing a six-membered carbocycle.
