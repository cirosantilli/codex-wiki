= Orthogonal polynomial
{wiki}
{title2=$\langle p_n,p_k\rangle_w=h_n\delta_{nk}$}

An <orthogonal polynomial> family chooses <polynomials> that do not overlap under a specified <inner product>. For real <polynomials> on an interval $(a,b)$, use the <weighted inner product>
$$
\langle p,q\rangle_w=\int_a^b p(x)q(x)w(x)\,dx,\qquad \|p\|_w^2=\langle p,p\rangle_w.
$$
Assume $a<b$, the weight $w$ is measurable and positive <almost everywhere>, and its absolute moments $\int_a^b |x|^k w(x)\,dx$ are finite for every <integer> $k\geq0$. These conditions make the <integrals> finite and give every nonzero <polynomial> positive squared <norm>. A sequence $p_0,p_1,\ldots$ is an <orthogonal polynomial> family when $p_n$ has real <coefficients> and <polynomial degree> exactly $n$, and $\langle p_n,p_k\rangle_w=0$ for $n\ne k$.<NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions/18 Orthogonal Polynomials>[NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions; Koornwinder, Wong, Koekoek, Swarttouw and Reinhardt, NIST DLMF Chapter 18, §18.2(i), equations 18.2.1 and 18.2.1_5: interval orthogonality and finite absolute moments]{ref}

Here $h_n=\|p_n\|_w^2>0$ and $\delta_{nk}$ is the <Kronecker delta>. <Orthogonality> does not require $h_n=1$: an <orthonormal set> is obtained only after replacing $p_n$ by $p_n/\sqrt{h_n}$. Multiplying each member by any nonzero real constant preserves <orthogonality>, but changes its <norm> and the corresponding expansion <coefficient>. The <monic orthogonal polynomial> convention instead fixes each leading <coefficient> to one.

The first four <Legendre polynomials>, named for <Adrien-Marie Legendre>, use $w=1$ on $[-1,1]$ and the normalization $P_n(1)=1$:
$$
P_0(x)=1,\qquad P_1(x)=x,\qquad P_2(x)=\frac{3x^2-1}{2},\qquad P_3(x)=\frac{5x^3-3x}{2}.
$$
Their <orthogonality> and squared <norms> are
$$
\int_{-1}^1 P_n(x)P_k(x)\,dx=\frac{2}{2n+1}\delta_{nk}.
$$
Thus their <Gram matrix> has a positive diagonal and zero entries elsewhere, although their <function graphs> cross and overlap on the page. <Orthogonality> measures an <integral> of products, rather than separation of <function graphs>.<NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions/18 Orthogonal Polynomials>[NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions; Koornwinder and collaborators, NIST DLMF Chapter 18, Table 18.3.1, Legendre row: interval, unit weight, squared norm and normalization]{ref}

\Image[legendre-polynomial-shapes-and-gram-matrix.png]
{title=The first four Legendre polynomials and their exact integral Gram matrix: orthogonal, with unequal squared norms}

For these four <polynomials>, a short verification expands each product and uses $\int_{-1}^1x^{2j+1}\,dx=0$ and $\int_{-1}^1x^{2j}\,dx=2/(2j+1)$. For example, $\langle P_0,P_2\rangle=(3(2/3)-2)/2=0$ and $\langle P_1,P_3\rangle=(5(2/5)-3(2/3))/2=0$. The same calculation gives the diagonal entries $2,2/3,2/5,2/7$. More generally, apply the <Gram-Schmidt process> to the <monomial basis> $1,x,x^2,\ldots$: at step $n$, subtract projections onto the earlier <polynomials>. The result retains its leading term, has degree $n$, and has positive <norm>, proving existence without a differential equation. Different degrees also give <linear independence>, so $p_0,\ldots,p_m$ form a <basis> of the <polynomials> of degree at most $m$.

This <basis> turns <least-squares solutions> for <functions> into independent scalar calculations, as the <decoupled coefficients of orthogonal polynomial projections> show. In a <spectral method>, increasing the degree enlarges the approximation space while keeping earlier projection <coefficients> fixed. Weighted <integrals> also connect the same <orthogonal polynomials> to <Gaussian quadrature>: its nodes are <polynomial roots> of a member of the family. The projection problem minimizes an integrated squared error; <Gaussian quadrature> uses the family to choose evaluation points for an <integral>.

= Orthogonal polynomials for the absolute-value weight
{parent=Orthogonal polynomial}
{title2=$\phi_0=1,\quad\phi_1=x,\quad\phi_2=x^2-\tfrac12$}

For the <inner product> obtained by integrating with weight $|x|$ on $[-1,1]$, odd moments vanish and the moment of $x^{2m}$ is $1/(m+1)$. The first <monic orthogonal polynomials> are $1$, $x$ and $x^2-1/2$. <Positive definiteness> follows because the weight is positive <almost everywhere> and a nonzero <polynomial> cannot vanish <almost everywhere>.

= Parity of monic orthogonal polynomials for an even weight
{parent=Orthogonal polynomial}
{title2=$\phi_n(-x)=(-1)^n\phi_n(x)$}

Assume a positive even weight on a symmetric domain has finite <polynomial> moments. Subtracting the <orthogonal projection onto a finite-dimensional subspace> of $x^n$ onto the lower-degree <polynomials> gives the unique monic <orthogonal polynomial>. Reflection produces another such <monic polynomial>, $(-1)^n\phi_n(-x)$, so uniqueness gives the parity identity. Every coefficient with the opposite parity to its degree therefore vanishes.

= Orthonormal polynomial
{parent=Orthogonal polynomial}
{title2=$(\phi_r,\phi_s)=\delta_{rs}$}

An orthonormal family of <orthogonal polynomials> also gives each member unit <norm> in the chosen weighted <inner product>. Its pairwise inner products are the <Kronecker delta>. Dividing every nonzero <orthogonal polynomial> by its <norm> produces such a family; the sign or complex phase still requires a convention, such as a positive real leading coefficient.

= Degree ordering in an orthonormal polynomial basis
{parent=Orthonormal polynomial}
{title2=$\deg p_k=k$}

An <orthonormal basis> of the <polynomial> space $\Pi_n$ need not be indexed by <polynomial degree>. Permuting the normalized <Legendre polynomials>, for example, preserves <orthonormality> but changes the degrees attached to the labels. Results about the number of <polynomial roots> of $p_k$ require the extra condition $\deg p_k=k$, or that the <polynomials> arise by applying the <Gram-Schmidt process> to $1,x,x^2,\ldots$ in that order. Under this <vertex degree>[degree] ordering, $p_k$ is <orthogonal> to every <polynomial> of lower <polynomial degree> because those earlier <basis> members span $\Pi_{k-1}$.

For a positive integration weight on a nondegenerate <interval>, this implies that $p_k$ has $k$ distinct <polynomial roots> in the <open interval>. If fewer than $k$ roots change sign there, multiply $p_k$ by the product of their linear factors. The product has constant nonzero sign except at its finitely many zeros, so its weighted integral cannot vanish, contradicting <orthogonality> to this lower-<vertex degree>[degree] <polynomial>. The usual <Gaussian quadrature> construction therefore uses the <vertex degree>[degree]-ordered family; arbitrary relabelling is not enough.

= Parity of orthogonal polynomials for an even weight
{parent=Orthogonal polynomial}
{title2=$p_k(-x)=(-1)^kp_k(x)$}

For a positive even weight on a symmetric interval, any <orthogonal polynomial> of degree $k$ has the parity of $k$. Induct on $k$. The reflected polynomial $q_k(x)=(-1)^kp_k(-x)$ has the same leading coefficient. By substitution $x\mapsto-x$ in the <weighted inner product> and the inductive parity of all lower polynomials, it is orthogonal to them. Hence $q_k-p_k$ has lower degree and is orthogonal to the entire space containing it. Its squared norm is zero, forcing $q_k=p_k$. The base case is a constant. This proof needs neither monic nor unit-norm normalization; it only compares polynomials with the same leading coefficient.

= Rodrigues' formula
{parent=Orthogonal polynomial}
{c}
{wiki}

A Rodrigues formula constructs an <orthogonal polynomial> by differentiating a weight multiplied by a power. The <Rodrigues formula for Legendre polynomials> uses a derivative of $(x^2-1)^l$. For <generalized Laguerre polynomials>,
$$
L_n^{(\alpha)}(x)
=\frac{x^{-\alpha}e^x}{n!}\frac{d^n}{dx^n}
\left(e^{-x}x^{n+\alpha}\right).
$$

= Decoupled coefficients of orthogonal polynomial projections
{parent=Orthogonal polynomial}
{title2=$c_n=\langle f,p_n\rangle_w/h_n$}

Let $f$ be a real <function> with $\|f\|_w^2=\int_a^b f(x)^2w(x)\,dx<\infty$, and let $V_m$ be the <vector space> of real <polynomials> of degree at most $m$. Its <orthogonal projection> is the <least-squares polynomial in an orthogonal-polynomial basis>
$$
q_m=\sum_{n=0}^m c_np_n,\qquad c_n=\frac{\langle f,p_n\rangle_w}{h_n},\qquad h_n=\langle p_n,p_n\rangle_w.
$$
Each <coefficient> depends on just one <weighted inner product> with $f$ and one squared <norm>. In particular, the <coefficients> $c_0,\ldots,c_m$ stay the same when the allowed <polynomial degree> increases. This statement assumes a fixed weight, interval and normalization of the <orthogonal polynomials>.

To see why the <least-squares normal equations> decouple, first write a trial <polynomial> as $q=\sum_{j=0}^m a_j\phi_j$ in any <basis> of $V_m$. The residual must be <orthogonal> to each permitted direction, giving
$$
\sum_{j=0}^m G_{ij}a_j=b_i,\qquad G_{ij}=\langle\phi_j,\phi_i\rangle_w,\qquad b_i=\langle f,\phi_i\rangle_w.
$$
The <Gram matrix> usually couples the unknown <coefficients>. With $\phi_j=p_j$, it becomes $G_{ij}=h_i\delta_{ij}$, leaving the separate equations $h_i a_i=b_i$. For the <monomial basis> on $[-1,1]$ with $w=1$, by contrast, $G_{ij}$ is zero when $i+j$ is odd and $2/(i+j+1)$ when $i+j$ is even. Distinct even powers, or distinct odd powers, remain coupled.

There is also a proof of the minimum that uses no <differentiation>. The defining <coefficients> give $\langle f-q_m,p_n\rangle_w=0$ for $0\leq n\leq m$. Thus $f-q_m$ is <orthogonal> to all of $V_m$. For every $v\in V_m$, the <Pythagorean theorem in an inner-product space> gives
$$
\|f-v\|_w^2=\|f-q_m\|_w^2+\|q_m-v\|_w^2.
$$
The second term is nonnegative and vanishes exactly when $v=q_m$, proving that the <least-squares solution> is unique. This is an integrated squared-error optimum; it does not assert a smallest error at every point or a smallest <uniform norm>.

For $N>m$, the nested <orthogonal projections> satisfy the exact residual budget
$$
\|f-q_m\|_w^2=\|f-q_N\|_w^2+\sum_{n=m+1}^{N}c_n^2h_n.
$$
Indeed, $q_N-q_m$ is a <linear combination> of mutually <orthogonal polynomials>, and $f-q_N$ is <orthogonal> to it. Every newly retained component removes exactly $c_n^2h_n$ from the squared error. No infinite expansion or completeness assumption is needed for this finite identity. The <Legendre least-squares projections of a cubic> make every term explicit.

= Legendre least-squares projections of a cubic
{c}
{parent=Decoupled coefficients of orthogonal polynomial projections}
{title2=$f(x)=1+x+x^2+x^3$}

Take the <polynomial> $f(x)=1+x+x^2+x^3$ on $[-1,1]$ with unit weight. Using the four <Legendre polynomials> above, direct <integration> gives
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c|c|c}
n&h_n&\langle f,P_n\rangle&c_n&c_n^2h_n\\\hline
0&2&8/3&4/3&32/9\\
1&2/3&16/15&8/5&128/75\\
2&2/5&4/15&2/3&8/45\\
3&2/7&4/35&2/5&8/175
\end{array}
$$
For example, $\langle f,P_2\rangle=\tfrac12\int_{-1}^1(3x^4+2x^2-1)\,dx=4/15$: the omitted odd terms have zero <integral>. Dividing by $h_2=2/5$ gives $c_2=2/3$. Substituting all four <coefficients> proves the exact identity
$$
f=\frac43P_0+\frac85P_1+\frac23P_2+\frac25P_3.
$$

The successive <orthogonal projections> onto degrees at most $m$ are therefore
$$
\begin{array}{c|l|c}
m&q_m(x)&E_m=\|f-q_m\|_2^2\\\hline
0&4/3&608/315\\
1&4/3+8x/5&352/1575\\
2&1+8x/5+x^2&8/175\\
3&1+x+x^2+x^3&0
\end{array}
$$
Here $\|g\|_2^2=\int_{-1}^1g(x)^2\,dx$ is the squared <L2 norm>, without division by the interval length. Each row retains the previous <Legendre polynomial> <coefficients> and appends one new term. The ordinary power <coefficients> can change: adding $\tfrac23P_2$ changes the constant from $4/3$ to $1$, and adding $\tfrac25P_3$ changes the linear <coefficient> from $8/5$ to $1$. Independence belongs to the chosen <orthogonal polynomial> coordinates.

\Image[legendre-nested-projections-and-residual-budget.png]
{title=Nested Legendre least-squares projections of a cubic and the exact squared residual contributed by each omitted mode}

The full squared <L2 norm> is $\|f\|_2^2=192/35$, equal to $32/9+128/75+8/45+8/175$. The residual budgets are the omitted terms:
$$
E_0=\frac{128}{75}+\frac8{45}+\frac8{175}=\frac{608}{315},\qquad E_1=\frac8{45}+\frac8{175}=\frac{352}{1575},\qquad E_2=\frac8{175},\qquad E_3=0.
$$
For a direct check independent of summing the budgets, $f-q_2=x^3-3x/5$, and integrating its square gives $2/7-12/25+6/25=8/175$. This residual is <orthogonal> to $1,x,x^2$, exactly the conditions for the best quadratic <least-squares solution>. An <orthogonal projection> need not be a <polynomial interpolant>: for example, $q_2(1)=18/5$ while $f(1)=4$. The approximation allocates error across the interval according to the chosen <weighted inner product>.

= Krawtchouk polynomials
{c}
{synonym}

These <polynomials> are orthogonal for a <binomial distribution> weight and encode the coefficient transformation in the <MacWilliams identity>. Their <generating function> follows by expanding the two factors:
$$
\sum_{s=0}^nK_s(t;n,q)z^s=(1-z)^t(1+(q-1)z)^{n-t}.
$$
For binary <linear codes>, $q=2$. Expanding the factors gives $K_s(t;n,2)=\sum_j(-1)^j\binom tj\binom{n-t}{s-j}$, where <binomial coefficients> outside their usual integer range are zero. Orthogonality follows by multiplying <generating functions> and summing over $t$ with weight $\binom nt(q-1)^t$: the result is $q^n(1+(q-1)zw)^n$, whose coefficient of $z^rw^s$ is zero for $r\ne s$ and $q^n(q-1)^r\binom nr$ for $r=s$.

= Orthogonality forces many interior zeros
{parent=Orthogonal polynomial}

If a nonzero continuous real function on an interval is orthogonal to every <polynomial> of degree below $n$, it has at least $n$ distinct interior zeros. If it had only finitely many zeros and fewer than $n$ sign changes, multiply it by the product of linear factors at its sign-changing zeros. The product has constant sign and is nonzero on an <open interval>, so its integral cannot vanish. Orthogonality to this degree-below-$n$ <polynomial> contradicts that conclusion. Infinitely many interior zeros already satisfy the assertion. Orthogonal <polynomials> of respective degrees $0,\ldots,n-1$ form a <basis> of the needed <polynomial> space.

= Zernike polynomial
{c}
{synonym}

The real Zernike polynomials form an <orthogonal polynomial> basis on the unit <Euclidean disk>, with radial factor $R_n^m(\rho)$ and angular factor $\cos m\phi$ or $\sin m\phi$. Here $0\leq m\leq n$ and $n-m$ is <even>. They decompose <wavefront errors> into modes useful for optical correction. Normalizations differ, so coefficients should specify the chosen convention.

= Zernike spherical mode
{c}
{parent=Zernike polynomials}
{title2=$Z_4^0=6\rho^4-6\rho^2+1$}

The unnormalized rotationally symmetric mode is $Z_4^0(\rho)=6\rho^4-6\rho^2+1$. It describes balanced primary <spherical aberration>. The lower-degree terms make it orthogonal to constant piston and quadratic defocus under the disk area measure: direct integration gives $\int_0^1 Z_4^0\rho\,d\rho=0$ and $\int_0^1 Z_4^0(2\rho^2-1)\rho\,d\rho=0$.

= Monic orthogonal polynomial
{parent=Orthogonal polynomial}

A monic orthogonal polynomial has leading coefficient one. For a fixed positive weight, there is a unique monic orthogonal polynomial of each degree.

= Three-term recurrence for monic orthogonal polynomials
{parent=Monic orthogonal polynomial}

For monic orthogonal polynomials,
$$
p_{n+1}(x)=(x-\alpha_n)p_n(x)-\beta_np_{n-1}(x),
$$
where
$$
\alpha_n=\frac{\langle xp_n,p_n\rangle}{\langle p_n,p_n\rangle},
\qquad
\beta_n=\frac{\langle p_n,p_n\rangle}{\langle p_{n-1},p_{n-1}\rangle}.
$$

= Generating function of the physicists' Hermite polynomials
{parent=Hermite polynomial}
{title2=$\sum_{n\geq0}H_n(t)z^n/n!=e^{2tz-z^2}$}

For the <Hermite polynomials> in the Rodrigues normalization $H_n=(-1)^ne^{t^2}D^ne^{-t^2}$, differentiating gives $H_{n+1}=2tH_n-H_n'$ with $H_0=1$. Induction gives
$$
H_n(t)=n!\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{(-1)^j2^{n-2j}t^{n-2j}}{j!(n-2j)!}.
$$
Insert this into the exponential generating <series> and reindex $n=m+2j$. The result factors into $\sum_m(2tz)^m/m!$ times $\sum_j(-z^2)^j/j!$, proving the displayed identity. Equivalently Taylor expansion of $e^{-(t-z)^2}$ in $z$ gives the same formula. This <generating function> keeps the normalization distinct from the <probabilists' Hermite polynomial> convention.

= Generating-function proof of Hermite orthogonality
{parent=Generating function of the physicists' Hermite polynomials}
{title2=$\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-t^2}H_m(t)H_n(t)dt=\sqrt\pi\,2^nn!\,\delta_{mn}$}

Multiply two copies of the <generating function of the physicists' Hermite polynomials> and integrate against $e^{-t^2}$. Completing the square gives
$$
\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-t^2}e^{2ty-y^2}e^{2tz-z^2}dt=\sqrt\pi e^{2yz}.
$$
Comparing coefficients gives the displayed <orthogonality> and normalization. Equivalently differentiation in $y,z$ at zero justifies coefficient extraction by the Gaussian integrable bound on compact parameter ranges. Hence the unnormalized <Hermite functions> $e^{-t^2/2}H_n(t)$ have <norm> $\pi^{1/4}\sqrt{2^nn!}$. This proves their mutual <orthogonality>; <completeness> of that family is an additional assertion.

= Gaussian polynomial operator
{c}
{parent=Hermite polynomial}
{title2=$Tf=f''-2xf'-2f$}

On $\mathbb C[x]$, equip <polynomials> with the <inner product>
$$
\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\overline{f(x)}g(x)e^{-x^2}\,dx.
$$
The <linear operator>
$$
Tf=\frac{d}{dx}\left(e^{x^2}\frac{d}{dx}(e^{-x^2}f)\right)
=f''-2xf'-2f
$$
preserves the space $V_n$ of <polynomials> of <polynomial degree> at most $n$. <Integration by parts>, whose boundary terms vanish by Gaussian decay, gives
$$
\langle Tf,g\rangle=-\langle f',g'\rangle-2\langle f,g\rangle
=\langle f,Tg\rangle.
$$
Thus $T$ equals its <adjoint on an inner product space>. If a nonzero <polynomial> of degree $n$ is an <eigenvector>, its leading coefficient forces its <eigenvalue> to be $-2(n+1)$.

One can construct all these <eigenvectors> without solving a <differential equation>. The one-dimensional space $L_n=V_n\cap V_{n-1}^{\perp}$ is invariant: for $u\in V_{n-1}$ and $v\in L_n$, the displayed identity gives $\langle u,Tv\rangle=\langle Tu,v\rangle=0$. Its unit vector of positive real leading coefficient is therefore an <eigenvector> of degree $n$. These choices are exactly the <Gram-Schmidt process> applied to $1,x,x^2,\ldots$, producing normalized <Hermite polynomials>. Their first three members are
$$
g_0=\pi^{-1/4},\qquad g_1=\sqrt2\pi^{-1/4}x,\qquad
g_2=\frac{2x^2-1}{\sqrt2\pi^{1/4}}.
$$
Every <polynomial> is a finite linear combination of these orthonormal <eigenvectors>.

= Hermite polynomial derivative recurrence
{parent=Hermite polynomial}
{c}
{title2=$H_n'=2nH_{n-1}$}

For the physicists' <Hermite polynomials> $H_n=(-1)^ne^{x^2}D^ne^{-x^2}$, the displayed recurrence holds for $n\geq1$, while $H_0'=0$ separately. Put $u=e^{-x^2}$. Differentiating $u'+2xu=0$ exactly $n$ times using the <Leibniz rule> yields
$$
(D+2x)D^nu=-2nD^{n-1}u.
$$
Differentiating the defining product for $H_n$ then gives
$$
H_n'=(-1)^ne^{x^2}(D+2x)D^nu
=2n(-1)^{n-1}e^{x^2}D^{n-1}u=2nH_{n-1}.
$$
The nonnegative integer convention is essential for the derivative-based definition; no negative-order derivative or $H_{-1}$ is needed.

= Hermite function
{c}
{parent=Hermite polynomial}
{title2=$H_n(x)=\frac{e^{-x^2/2}}{\pi^{1/4}\sqrt{2^nn!}}(-1)^ne^{x^2}\frac{d^n}{dx^n}e^{-x^2}$}

The normalized Hermite functions multiply the physicists' <Hermite polynomials> by a Gaussian. They form an <orthonormal basis> of $L^2(\mathbb R)$ and satisfy $(-d^2/dx^2+x^2)H_n=(2n+1)H_n$. With $a=(x+d/dx)/\sqrt2$ and $a^*=(x-d/dx)/\sqrt2$, they are $(a^*)^nH_0/\sqrt{n!}$ and the <quantum harmonic oscillator> is $2a^*a+1$. For completeness, if $u$ is orthogonal to all of them, all moments of $u(x)e^{-x^2/2}$ vanish. Its transform $\int u(x)e^{-x^2/2}e^{zx}\,dx$ is entire by <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality>, has every derivative zero at zero, and hence vanishes identically. Uniqueness of the <Fourier transform> gives $u=0$.

= Hermite functions are Fourier eigenfunctions
{parent=Hermite function}
{c}
{title2=$\mathcal F(H_n(x)e^{-x^2/2})=(-i)^nH_n(x)e^{-x^2/2}$}

Use the unitary <Fourier transform> with phase $e^{-i\lambda x}$. It commutes with $-d^2/dx^2+x^2$, because differentiation transforms to multiplication by $i\lambda$ and multiplication by $x$ transforms to $i\,d/d\lambda$. The <Hermite differential equation> has a unique polynomial solution of each degree up to scale, and multiplying by the Gaussian gives a <Hermite function>. Transforming a polynomial times this Gaussian again gives a polynomial times the Gaussian of the same degree; comparison of leading coefficients gives the eigenvalue $(-i)^n$.

= Mehler formula for Hermite functions
{c}
{parent=Hermite function}
{title2=$\sum_{n\geq0}t^nH_n(x)H_n(y)=\frac{\exp\!\left(\frac{4xyt-(1+t^2)(x^2+y^2)}{2(1-t^2)}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi(1-t^2)}}$}

For $|t|<1$, this identity sums the <Hermite function> expansion into a Gaussian. One proof takes the <Bargmann-Fock space> inner product of the generating functions $\pi^{-1/4}\exp(-x^2/2+\sqrt2 xz-z^2/2)$ at $\sqrt t\,z$. Writing $z=u+iv$, the two resulting real <Gaussian integrals> have quadratic coefficients $1+t$ and $1-t$ and linear coefficients $\sqrt{2t}(x+y)$ and $i\sqrt{2t}(x-y)$. Evaluation gives the formula. Setting $t=e^{-2s}$ and multiplying by $e^{-s}$ gives the <harmonic oscillator transition kernel> of $e^{-s(-d^2/dx^2+x^2)}$.

= Probabilists' Hermite polynomial
{title2=$\mathrm{He}_n$}
{parent=Hermite polynomial}
{c}

The probabilists' Hermite polynomials are
$$
\mathrm{He}_n(x)=(-1)^ne^{x^2/2}\frac{d^n}{dx^n}e^{-x^2/2}.
$$
They are orthogonal for the standard Gaussian weight and satisfy $\mathrm{He}_{n+1}=x\mathrm{He}_n-n\mathrm{He}_{n-1}$.

This is the Gaussian-probability normalization of the <Hermite polynomial> family.

= Hermite polynomial martingale
{c}
{synonym}

The space-time <probabilists' Hermite polynomial> $H_n(x,t)=t^{n/2}\mathrm{He}_n(x/\sqrt t)$ extends polynomially to $t=0$ and solves $\partial_tH_n+\frac12\partial_x^2H_n=0$. Therefore $H_n(B_t,t)$ is a <martingale> for a standard <Brownian motion>, by the <Itô formula> and Gaussian <moments>.
