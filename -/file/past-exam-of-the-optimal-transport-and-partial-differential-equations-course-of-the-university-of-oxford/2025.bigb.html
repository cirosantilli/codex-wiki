= Solution
{parent=a}

Compact <support of a measure> gives finite second <absolute moments> for both <probability measures>. Since the source satisfies <absolute continuity of measures> with respect to <Lebesgue measure>, <Brenier's theorem> identifies the quadratic <Kantorovich optimal transport problem> with the <Monge optimal transport problem>:
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}d_2^2(\mu,\nu)&=\min_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int|x-y|^2\,d\pi(x,y)\\&=\min_{S_\#\mu=\nu}\int|x-S(x)|^2\,d\mu(x)=\int|x-T(x)|^2\,d\mu(x).\end{aligned}}
$$
Here $S_\#\mu$ is the <pushforward measure>. The optimal <transport plan> is unique and equals $(\operatorname{Id},T)_\#\mu$. The optimal <transport map> is **unique up to a $\mu$-null set**, with
$$
\boxed{T=\nabla u\quad\mu\text{-almost everywhere},\qquad u\text{ convex}.}
$$
The <gradient> of this <convex function> is <c-cyclically monotone> for $c(x,y)=|x-y|^2$ on a full-measure set. In particular, summing the two supporting inequalities for the <convex function> gives $(T(x)-T(x'))\cdot(x-x')\geq0$ wherever both <gradients> exist. No regularity of the target beyond the stated <probability measure> assumptions is needed.

Write $P=\omega\omega^T$. Since $P^T=P$ and $P^2=P$, the <orthogonal reflection> $R_\omega=I-2P$ satisfies $R_\omega^TR_\omega=I$ and $R_\omega^{-1}=R_\omega$. Thus, for every <Borel set> $E$, invariance under the <orthogonal group> gives
$$
(R_\omega{}_\#\mu)(E)=\mu(R_\omega^{-1}E)=\mu(E),\qquad\boxed{R_\omega{}_\#\mu=\mu.}
$$

**The spherical average must use normalized surface measure without an additional area factor.** Let $d\sigma$ be the normalized <surface measure on a sphere>, and set
$$
A\eta(r)=\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}\eta(r\theta)\,d\sigma(\theta).
$$
The correct <radial disintegration of an orthogonally invariant measure> is
$$
\boxed{\int\eta(x)\,d\mu(x)=\int_{[0,\infty)}A\eta(r)\,d(\Phi_\#\mu)(r).}
$$
Indeed, take normalized <Haar measure> $H$ on the compact <orthogonal group> $O(d)$. Invariance and <Fubini's theorem> give
$$
\int\eta\,d\mu=\int\!\int_{O(d)}\eta(Qx)\,dH(Q)\,d\mu(x).
$$
Right invariance of <Haar measure> and transitivity of the <orthogonal group> on each <sphere> show that the inner average depends only on $r=|x|$. Averaging it once more over $x=r\theta$, using invariance of <surface measure on a sphere> and <Fubini's theorem>, identifies its value as $A\eta(r)$. At $x=0$ the same identity reads $\eta(0)=\eta(0)$. The definition of <pushforward measure> now proves the boxed formula. This argument also works for invariant <probability measures> with an <atom of a measure> at the origin.

For the printed normalization, choose a <smooth cutoff function> $\eta$ equal to one on a ball containing the <support of a measure> $\mu$. Its left integral is one, whereas the printed right integral is $\sigma_d$. Thus the extra $\sigma_d$ cannot be retained. Equivalently, with unnormalized area measure $dS$, the average is $\sigma_d^{-1}\int\eta(r\theta)\,dS(\theta)$.

**The reflection-averaging hint also fails in general dimension.** For $x=e_1$,
$$
\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}R_\omega e_1\,d\sigma(\omega)=\left(1-\frac2d\right)e_1,
\qquad\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}\omega\,d\sigma(\omega)=0.
$$
The first equality uses $\int\omega_i\omega_j\,d\sigma=\delta_{ij}/d$, which follows from coordinate reflections, coordinate permutations, and $\sum_i\omega_i^2=1$. Taking a <test function> equal to the first coordinate near the unit <sphere> disproves the hint when $d\ne2$. Averaging over the full <orthogonal group>, as above, proves the required corrected result without that hint.

Write $d\mu=f(x)\,dx$, with $f\geq0$. Applying <polar coordinates> and <Fubini's theorem> gives, for bounded nonnegative measurable $h$,
$$
\int h\,d\widetilde\mu=\int_0^\infty h(r)g(r)\,dr,\qquad g(r)=\sigma_d r^{d-1}\int_{\mathbb S^{d-1}}f(r\theta)\,d\sigma(\theta)\quad(r>0).
$$
Also $\widetilde\mu(\{0\})=\mu(\{0\})=0$ by <absolute continuity of measures>. Extending $g$ by zero on the negative half-line proves
$$
\boxed{\widetilde\mu\ll\mathcal L^1.}
$$

Both radial <pushforward measures> have compact <support of a measure>, so <Brenier's theorem> on the line supplies a unique optimal <transport plan> and a <transport map> $\tau$, unique $\widetilde\mu$-almost everywhere, with
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}d_2^2(\widetilde\mu,\widetilde\nu)&=\min_{s_\#\widetilde\mu=\widetilde\nu}\int_0^\infty|r-s(r)|^2\,d\widetilde\mu(r)\\&=\int_0^\infty|r-\tau(r)|^2\,d\widetilde\mu(r).\end{aligned}}
$$
In one dimension, the <gradient> of a <convex function> is a <nondecreasing function>. Nonnegativity holds $\widetilde\mu$-almost everywhere because $\tau_\#\widetilde\mu=\widetilde\nu$ is carried by $[0,\infty)$. To choose a nonnegative, nondecreasing representative everywhere, let $F,G$ be the radial <cumulative distribution functions> and use the <monotone rearrangement>
$$
\boxed{\tau(r)=G^{-1}(F(r)),\qquad G^{-1}(u)=\inf\{s\geq0:G(s)\geq u\}\quad(0<u<1).}
$$
At $F(r)=0$ set $\tau(r)=0$, and at $F(r)=1$ use the largest point of the <support of a measure> $\widetilde\nu$. These choices preserve monotonicity and boundedness. Since $F$ is continuous, $F(R)$ has the <uniform distribution> on $(0,1)$ when $R$ has law $\widetilde\mu$, and the <quantile function> $G^{-1}$ sends this <uniform distribution> to $\widetilde\nu$. Thus this representative has the required <pushforward measure>, even if the target has atoms.

Choose the bounded, nonnegative <nondecreasing function> $\tau$ from part (d), and define
$$
h(r)=\int_0^r\tau(s)\,ds,\qquad U(x)=h(|x|).
$$
The <function> $h$ is <convex> and nondecreasing. For $0\leq a\leq1$, the <triangle inequality>, monotonicity, and <convexity> give
$$
U((1-a)x+ay)\leq h((1-a)|x|+a|y|)\leq(1-a)U(x)+aU(y).
$$
Hence $U$ is a <convex function>. By the <gradient of a radial function> formula and the <fundamental theorem of calculus>,
$$
\nabla U(x)=\tau(|x|)\frac{x}{|x|}
$$
for $x\ne0$ whenever $h'(|x|)=\tau(|x|)$. A <monotone function> has at most countably many discontinuities, so part (d) makes the exceptional radii $\widetilde\mu$-null. The origin is also $\mu$-null. Define $\mathcal T(0)=0$; thus $\mathcal T=\nabla U$ $\mu$-almost everywhere.

For every $Q$ in the <orthogonal group>, $\mathcal T(Qx)=Q\mathcal T(x)$, including at zero. Consequently $\mathcal T_\#\mu$ is invariant under the <orthogonal group>. Moreover $|\mathcal T(x)|=\tau(|x|)$ away from the null origin, and composition of <pushforward measures> gives
$$
\Phi_\#(\mathcal T_\#\mu)=\tau_\#(\Phi_\#\mu)=\widetilde\nu=\Phi_\#\nu.
$$
Part (c) therefore gives $\mathcal T_\#\mu=\nu$. The <Knott–Smith optimality criterion> makes a <transport map> given by a <convex function>'s <gradient> optimal for the quadratic <transport cost>. Uniqueness from <Brenier's theorem> yields
$$
\boxed{T(x)=\mathcal T(x)=\tau(|x|)\frac{x}{|x|}\quad\mu\text{-almost everywhere}.}
$$
Since directions agree, this also identifies the optimal value:
$$
\boxed{d_2^2(\mu,\nu)=\int_0^\infty|r-\tau(r)|^2\,d\widetilde\mu(r)=d_2^2(\widetilde\mu,\widetilde\nu).}
$$

Put $D=d_2(\mu,\nu)$ and $z_t(x,y)=(1-t)x+ty$. The <marginal distributions> of the optimal <transport plan> $\Pi$ give $\mu_0=\mu$ and $\mu_1=\nu$. Also
$$
\int|z_t|^2\,d\Pi\leq(1-t)\int|x|^2\,d\mu+t\int|y|^2\,d\nu<\infty,
$$
so $\mu_t$ has a finite second <absolute moment>. For $0\leq s<t\leq1$, the <pushforward measure> $(z_s,z_t)_\#\Pi$ is a <coupling> of $\mu_s,\mu_t$. Therefore
$$
d_2(\mu_s,\mu_t)^2\leq\int|z_t-z_s|^2\,d\Pi=(t-s)^2D^2.
$$
Apply the <triangle inequality> for the <second Wasserstein distance> along $\mu_0,\mu_s,\mu_t,\mu_1$:
$$
D\leq d_2(\mu_0,\mu_s)+d_2(\mu_s,\mu_t)+d_2(\mu_t,\mu_1)\leq sD+(t-s)D+(1-t)D=D.
$$
All inequalities are equalities, including when $D=0$. Thus this <displacement interpolation> is a constant speed <metric geodesic>:
$$
\boxed{d_2(\mu_s,\mu_t)=|t-s|d_2(\mu,\nu).}
$$

Set $q=y-x$ and $z_t=x+tq$. The definition of <pushforward measure> gives the <confinement energy> $\mathcal V[\mu_t]=\int V(z_t)\,d\Pi$. The <chain rule> and <differentiation under the integral sign>, whenever justified, give
$$
\frac d{dt}\mathcal V[\mu_t]=\int\nabla V(z_t)\cdot q\,d\Pi,\qquad\boxed{\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\mathcal V[\mu_t]=\int q^TD^2V(z_t)q\,d\Pi.}
$$
For example, bounded <Hessian matrix> is sufficient: it gives $|\nabla V(z)|\leq C(1+|z|)$, and the first two differentiated integrands are bounded by $C'(1+|x|^2+|y|^2)$. The <dominated convergence theorem> applies because $\Pi$ has finite second <absolute moments>. Compactly supported endpoints also suffice because all interpolating points lie in one compact set.

**The printed growth assumption alone does not ensure a classical second derivative.** Here is a counterexample within the stated assumptions. On the line take the bounded nonnegative <smooth function>
$$
V(x)=1-\cos(2\pi x^3),\qquad p_n=cn^{-4}\quad(n\geq1),\qquad c^{-1}=\sum_{n\geq1}n^{-4},
$$
and the <probability measures> $\mu=\sum_np_n\delta_{n-1/2}$, $\nu=\sum_np_n\delta_{n+1/2}$. Both have finite second <absolute moments>. Their translation <transport plan> is optimal: its cost is one, while every <coupling> has cost at least the square of its mean displacement, also one. At $t_0=1/2$, $z_{t_0}=n$, so $F(t)=\mathcal V[\mu_t]$ satisfies $F(t_0)=0$. Each summand is nonnegative, and the <Fatou lemma> gives
$$
\liminf_{h\to0}\frac{F(t_0+h)+F(t_0-h)-2F(t_0)}{h^2}\geq\sum_np_nV''(n)=36\pi^2c\sum_n1=\infty.
$$
Thus the requested classical identity requires an additional integrability assumption on the <Hessian matrix>; quadratic growth controls the energy but not its <second derivative>.

For a <confinement potential> $V$ that is a $\lambda$-<strongly convex function>, the derivative formula, where valid, yields
$$
\boxed{\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\mathcal V[\mu_t]\geq\lambda\int|q|^2\,d\Pi=\lambda D^2.}
$$
The underlying <displacement convexity> conclusion is valid under the printed assumptions without that extra differentiation hypothesis. Apply <convexity> of $V(x)-\lambda|x|^2/2$ pointwise and integrate:
$$
\boxed{\mathcal V[\mu_t]\leq(1-t)\mathcal V[\mu]+t\mathcal V[\nu]-\frac\lambda2t(1-t)D^2.}
$$
The same argument on every subinterval makes $t\mapsto\mathcal V[\mu_t]-\lambda D^2t^2/2$ <convex>, so the second derivative lower bound also holds in the sense of <distributions>.

Use an <independent> copy $(x',y')$ of $(x,y)$ under the <product measure> $\Pi\otimes\Pi$. Write $q=y-x$, $q'=y'-x'$, and
$$
r_t=z_t-z'_t=(1-t)(x-x')+t(y-y'),\qquad a=q-q'.
$$
Since the <pushforward measure> of $\Pi\otimes\Pi$ under $(z_t,z'_t)$ is $\mu_t\otimes\mu_t$, the <interaction energy> is
$$
\mathcal W[\mu_t]=\frac12\iint W(r_t)\,d\Pi(x,y)\,d\Pi(x',y').
$$
Two applications of the <chain rule> give
$$
\boxed{\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\mathcal W[\mu_t]=\frac12\iint (q-q')^TD^2W(z_t-z'_t)(q-q')\,d\Pi\,d\Pi'.}
$$
The factor $1/2$ is retained. This classical identity requires <differentiation under the integral sign> to be justified, just as in part (b). Bounded <Hessian matrix> of $W$ is sufficient, since $|q-q'|^2\leq2|q|^2+2|q'|^2$ is integrable; compactly supported endpoints are another sufficient case. The printed quadratic-growth condition alone does not provide this domination.

The failure can occur for the <interaction energy> too. Take the even <interaction potential> $W(x)=1-\cos(2\pi x^3)$ and replace the endpoint <probability measures> in part (b) by $\frac12\delta_0+\frac12\mu$ and $\frac12\delta_0+\frac12\nu$. Keep the origin fixed and translate every other atom by one. This coupling is increasing on the line, hence is optimal: its assigned values extend to a nondecreasing <transport map>, which is the <gradient> of a <convex function>. Differences between translated atoms remain integers, so their mutual interaction vanishes. Only interactions with the origin contribute, giving $\mathcal W[\mu_t]=F(t)/4$, with $F$ from part (b). It therefore also lacks a finite classical <second derivative> at $t=1/2$.

Define the <barycenter of a measure> by $m_\mu=\int x\,d\mu(x)$. Under $\Pi$, the mean displacement is $\bar q=m_\nu-m_\mu$, and optimality gives $\int|q|^2\,d\Pi=D^2$. Expanding the square under the <product measure> yields the <variance identity for independent copies>:
$$
\frac12\iint|q-q'|^2\,d\Pi\,d\Pi'=\int|q|^2\,d\Pi-\left|\int q\,d\Pi\right|^2=D^2-|m_\nu-m_\mu|^2.
$$
In the classical regime of part (c), the lower bound on the <Hessian matrix> of the <interaction potential> therefore gives
$$
\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\mathcal W[\mu_t]\geq\lambda\left(D^2-|m_\nu-m_\mu|^2\right).
$$
For equal <barycenters>, this becomes
$$
\boxed{\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\mathcal W[\mu_t]\geq\lambda D^2.}
$$

For the full stated class of <probability measures>, prove the <displacement convexity> inequality directly. Pointwise strong <convexity> of $W$ gives
$$
W((1-t)(x-x')+t(y-y'))\leq(1-t)W(x-x')+tW(y-y')-\frac\lambda2t(1-t)|q-q'|^2.
$$
Integrating, including the $1/2$ in the <interaction energy>, and using the displayed <variance identity for independent copies> gives
$$
\mathcal W[\mu_t]\leq(1-t)\mathcal W[\mu]+t\mathcal W[\nu]-\frac\lambda2t(1-t)\left(D^2-|m_\nu-m_\mu|^2\right).
$$
All terms are finite by quadratic growth and finite second <absolute moments>. Equal <barycenters> give the claimed $\lambda$-<displacement convexity>. Also $m_{\mu_t}=(1-t)m_\mu+tm_\nu$, so this <metric geodesic> remains in the prescribed fixed-mean class. Applying the same argument to subintervals proves the second derivative bound in the sense of <distributions> even when a classical derivative has not been justified. The mean restriction is natural: translating all particles together leaves their pairwise differences and hence the <interaction energy> unchanged.

Set
$$
K(z)=-J\nabla W(z)=(W_y(z),-W_x(z)),\qquad L=\max\left\{1,\sup_z\|D^2W(z)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\right\}.
$$
Symmetry here means $W(-z)=W(z)$, so $\nabla W(0)=0$ and $K(0)=0$. The bounded <Hessian matrix> and the fact that $J$ is an <orthogonal matrix> give
$$
|K(z)-K(z')|\leq L|z-z'|,\qquad |K(z)|\leq L|z|.
$$
Thus the particle system is $\dot X^i=N^{-1}\sum_jK(X^i-X^j)$. On the configuration space with norm $\|X\|_{\max}=\max_i|X^i|$, its vector field has <Lipschitz constant> at most $2L$:
$$
|F_i(X)-F_i(Y)|\leq\frac L N\sum_j\left(|X^i-Y^i|+|X^j-Y^j|\right)\leq2L\|X-Y\|_{\max}.
$$
The <Picard-Lindelöf theorem> gives local existence and uniqueness. Linear growth and the <Gronwall inequality> give $\|X(t)\|_{\max}\leq e^{2Lt}\|X(0)\|_{\max}$, which rules out escape to infinity in finite time and extends the solution to every $t\geq0$.

The associated <empirical measure> is
$$
\boxed{\mu_t^N=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\delta_{X^i(t)}.}
$$
Here each $\delta_{X^i(t)}$ is a <Dirac measure>. The finite first <absolute moment> follows from $\int|x|\,d\mu_t^N=N^{-1}\sum_i|X^i(t)|<\infty$. Pairing equal particle labels defines a <coupling>, so
$$
d_1(\mu_t^N,\mu_s^N)\leq\frac1N\sum_i|X^i(t)-X^i(s)|\longrightarrow0\quad(t\to s).
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\mu^N\in C([0,\infty),\mathcal P_1(\mathbb R^2)).}
$$

For a candidate curve $\rho$, define the <velocity field> by the <convolution>
$$
b_\rho(t,x)=K*\rho_t(x)=-\int J\nabla W(x-z)\,d\rho_t(z).
$$
It is well defined with a finite first <absolute moment>, because $|K(x-z)|\leq L(|x|+|z|)$. Equality of mixed <partial derivatives> gives
$$
\operatorname{div}K=\partial_xW_y-\partial_yW_x=0,\qquad\operatorname{div}b_\rho=0.
$$
The <Hessian matrix> bound justifies moving a spatial derivative through this <convolution>. For smooth densities, the <transport equation> consequently becomes the <continuity equation>
$$
\boxed{\partial_t\rho+\operatorname{div}(b_\rho\rho)=0.}
$$
This form defines the equation for singular <probability measures> as well. With initial <probability measure> $\rho_0$, a <weak solution> is a curve in $C([0,\infty),\mathcal P_1)$ such that every $\varphi\in C_c^1([0,\infty)\times\mathbb R^2)$ satisfies
$$
\boxed{\int_0^\infty\!\int\left[\partial_t\varphi(t,x)+b_\rho(t,x)\cdot\nabla\varphi(t,x)\right]d\rho_t(x)\,dt+\int\varphi(0,x)\,d\rho_0(x)=0.}
$$
Using smooth <test functions> gives the same definition by approximation on compact sets. Continuity in the <Wasserstein distance> bounds first <absolute moments> on compact time intervals, so the displayed integrals exist.

For the <empirical measure>, the <chain rule> and the particle equations give
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac d{dt}\int\varphi(t,x)\,d\mu_t^N(x)&=\frac1N\sum_i\left[\partial_t\varphi(t,X^i)+\nabla\varphi(t,X^i)\cdot\dot X^i\right]\\
&=\int\left[\partial_t\varphi+b_{\mu^N}\cdot\nabla\varphi\right]d\mu_t^N.
\end{aligned}
$$
Integrating in time, with zero terminal contribution because $\varphi$ has compact <support>, gives precisely the weak identity and the correct initial <empirical measure>. Thus **$\mu^N$ is a global weak solution**. The self-interaction term vanishes since $K(0)=0$.

**A finite first moment suffices for the velocity, but may not suffice for the printed potential.** For example, $W(z)=|z|^2/2$ has all the stated properties. If $\mu$ is the law of $(S,0)$ with density $2s^{-3}$ on $s\geq1$, then $\mathbb ES=2$ but $\mathbb ES^2=\infty$, and $(W*\mu)(x)=\infty$ for every $x$. Thus the literal definition of $V$ needs a finite second <absolute moment>, or another hypothesis ensuring integrability of $W$.

For the specified class $\mathcal P_1$, define the <velocity fields> directly by $b_\mu=K*\mu$ and $b_{\widehat\mu}=K*\widehat\mu$. Equivalently, use the <renormalized interaction potential>
$$
V_{\mathrm{ren}}(t,x)=\int\left[W(x-z)-W(-z)\right]d\mu_t(z).
$$
The bounded <Hessian matrix> and $\nabla W(0)=0$ imply
$$
|W(x-z)-W(-z)|\leq L|x||z|+\frac L2|x|^2.
$$
Thus $V_{\mathrm{ren}}$ is finite, is twice continuously differentiable in space, and has $\nabla V_{\mathrm{ren}}=\nabla W*\mu$, by dominated <differentiation under the integral sign>. Its rotated negative <gradient> is exactly $b_\mu$. Whenever $W*\mu$ is finite, the two potentials differ only by an $x$-independent constant and give the same <transport equation>.

We check the hypotheses for <characteristic representation of a measure-valued continuity equation>. For every $x,y$,
$$
|b_\mu(t,x)-b_\mu(t,y)|\leq L|x-y|,\qquad |b_\mu(t,x)|\leq L\left(|x|+m_1(\mu_t)\right),\qquad m_1(\mu_t)=\int|z|\,d\mu_t(z).
$$
For any <coupling> $\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu_t,\widehat\mu_t)$, the <Lipschitz condition> on $K$ also gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
|b_\mu(t,x)-b_{\widehat\mu}(t,x)|&=\left|\int[K(x-z)-K(x-\widehat z)]\,d\gamma(z,\widehat z)\right|\\
&\leq L\int|z-\widehat z|\,d\gamma(z,\widehat z).
\end{aligned}
$$
Taking the infimum over <couplings> proves the uniform bound
$$
\boxed{\sup_x|b_\mu(t,x)-b_{\widehat\mu}(t,x)|\leq Ld_1(\mu_t,\widehat\mu_t).}
$$
Applying the same estimate at two times of one curve proves time continuity of $b_\mu$, uniformly in $x$. Also $|m_1(\mu_t)-m_1(\mu_s)|\leq d_1(\mu_t,\mu_s)$, so $m_1(\mu_t)$ is bounded on compact time intervals. Spatial <partial derivatives> of $b_\mu$ are continuous and bounded by $L$, using dominated convergence in space and <weak convergence of probability measures> in time for the bounded continuous kernel $DK$. All corresponding facts hold for $\widehat\mu$.

The <characteristic representation of a measure-valued continuity equation> says that a continuous <velocity field>, continuously differentiable and globally <Lipschitz> in space and with locally uniform linear growth, has a global <characteristic flow map> $X_\mu(t;s,a)$ solving
$$
\frac d{dt}X_\mu(t;s,a)=b_\mu(t,X_\mu(t;s,a)),\qquad X_\mu(s;s,a)=a.
$$
For each $f\in\mathcal P_1$, its <pushforward measure> is the unique <weak solution> in $C([0,\infty),\mathcal P_1)$ of the associated <continuity equation>. The hypotheses have all been verified above, and the fields have zero <divergence>, so these are also the two required <transport equations>. Explicitly,
$$
\boxed{\rho_t=X_\mu(t;0,\cdot)_\#f,\qquad\widehat\rho_t=X_{\widehat\mu}(t;0,\cdot)_\#f.}
$$
For completeness, the <Gronwall inequality> gives
$$
|X_\mu(t;0,a)|\leq e^{Lt}|a|+L\int_0^te^{L(t-s)}m_1(\mu_s)\,ds,
$$
so these <pushforward measures> have finite first <absolute moments>. Integrating $|X_\mu(t;0,a)-X_\mu(s;0,a)|$ against $f$ proves continuity in the <Wasserstein distance>. The <chain rule> proves the weak identity. For uniqueness at a fixed terminal time $t$, use the backward <test function> $\varphi(s,x)=\psi(X_\mu(t;s,x))$, where $\psi$ is continuously differentiable with compact <support>. It satisfies $\partial_s\varphi+b_\mu\cdot\nabla\varphi=0$ and has compact spatial <support> uniformly for $0\leq s\leq t$, by the backward linear-growth bound. The weak identity with temporal cutoffs therefore gives $\int\psi\,d\rho_t=\int\psi(X_\mu(t;0,a))\,df(a)$, identifying every <weak solution> with the displayed <pushforward measure>.

Finally compare the two <characteristic curves> with the same initial point $a$. Put $\Delta_t=X_\mu(t;0,a)-X_{\widehat\mu}(t;0,a)$. The spatial <Lipschitz condition> and the boxed velocity estimate give
$$
\frac d{dt}|\Delta_t|\leq L|\Delta_t|+Ld_1(\mu_t,\widehat\mu_t),\qquad\Delta_0=0,
$$
for almost every time; the integral form gives the same conclusion at zeros of $\Delta$. The <Gronwall inequality> implies
$$
|\Delta_t|\leq L\int_0^te^{L(t-s)}d_1(\mu_s,\widehat\mu_s)\,ds.
$$
The <pushforward measure> $(X_\mu(t;0,\cdot),X_{\widehat\mu}(t;0,\cdot))_\#f$ is a <coupling> of $\rho_t,\widehat\rho_t$. Integrating the last bound against the <probability measure> $f$ proves
$$
\boxed{d_1(\rho_t,\widehat\rho_t)\leq L\int_0^te^{L(t-s)}d_1(\mu_s,\widehat\mu_s)\,ds.}
$$
The same constant $L$ depends only on the uniform bound for the <Hessian matrix> of $W$, and is independent of the prescribed curves and their common initial data.
