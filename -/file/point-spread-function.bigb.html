= Point spread function
{wiki}
{title2=$\operatorname{PSF}$}

= PSF
{c}
{synonym}

A <point spread function> is an imaging system's response to an <optical point source>. Here it means the distribution of image <light intensity>: an ideal point object becomes a finite spot, often with faint rings, because of <diffraction>. An unresolved <star> and a sufficiently small bead emitting <fluorescence> are practical approximations to an <optical point source>. Their image tells us how nearby object details will overlap, and reveals blurring from <optical aberrations>, <defocus>, and, for an <optical telescope>, <astronomical seeing>.

A useful convention separates shape from throughput. At a fixed image plane, let $p(\mathbf x)\geq0$ be the <point spread function> with $\int_{\mathbb R^2}p(\mathbf x)\,d^2\mathbf x=1$. Its units are inverse <area>. A point delivering detected <radiant flux> $F$ at image position $\mathbf x_0$ produces <light intensity> $Fp(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0)$; this <point spread function normalization> makes the <integral> over the image equal to $F$. A profile divided by its own peak is convenient for comparing widths, but is a different <point spread function normalization> and does not by itself compare total <radiant flux>.

In <incoherent imaging>, different object points have negligible mutual <spatial optical coherence> over the measurement, so their image <light intensities> add. If the response also obeys <translation invariance> over the chosen <field of view>, the expected continuous image is the <convolution>
$$
I(\mathbf x)=\tau\int_{\mathbb R^2}O(\mathbf u)p(\mathbf x-\mathbf u)\,d^2\mathbf u=\tau(O*p)(\mathbf x).
$$
Here $O$ is the object brightness distribution expressed in image-scaled coordinates, with <magnification> already included, and $\tau$ is constant transmission. For $O(\mathbf u)=F_0\delta(\mathbf u-\mathbf u_0)$, where $\delta$ is the two-dimensional <Dirac delta function>, this gives $I=\tau F_0p(\mathbf x-\mathbf u_0)$. A changing response across the <field of view> instead requires a position-dependent <integral kernel> $p(\mathbf x;\mathbf u)$; one measured <point spread function> is then only a local calibration.

In <coherent imaging>, the <superposition principle> applies to the complex <wave amplitudes>. With <amplitude spread function> $h$, the corresponding ideal model is
$$
E_{\rm image}=h*E_{\rm object},\qquad I_{\rm image}=|E_{\rm image}|^2,\qquad p=\frac{|h|^2}{\int_{\mathbb R^2}|h|^2\,d^2\mathbf x}.
$$
Thus $|h*E|^2$ generally differs from $|h|^2*|E|^2$: coherent contributions produce <wave interference> terms that depend on their relative <wave phases>. Their time average vanishes for mutually incoherent emitters, which explains the intensity <convolution>. Partial <optical coherence> requires the correlations between object points as well.<MIT 2.71 and 2.710 Optics, Spring 2014 by Nicholas X. Fang/Review for Optics>[pp. 7–8, §E(a–b): coherent field and incoherent intensity imaging]{ref}

\Image[point-spread-two-source-overlap.png]
{title=Ideal circular-pupil point responses and the overlap of two mutually incoherent sources}

The figure is an original <diffraction> model, not measured data. All panels have the same total <radiant flux>: each member of a pair contributes half the single-source <radiant flux>. Dashed curves show the separate <light intensity> contributions; the solid curve is their sum. The <point spread functions> have tails, so even sources satisfying the <Rayleigh criterion> overlap. As separation decreases, the image changes continuously; there is no abrupt disappearance of all information at one separation.

A motivating measured example is <Measurement of point-spread function (PSF) for confocal fluorescence microscopy by InCheon Song and others> (2005). A $543.8\,\mathrm{nm}$ <laser>, delivered through an <optical fiber>, excited fluorescent beads on a slide through a $100\times$, <numerical aperture> $1.3$ oil-immersion objective. A moving stage scanned the bead; emitted <light> returned through the objective, an emission filter and a pinhole to a <photodiode>. The readout was <fluorescence> <light intensity> against scanned position. Under the same setup, nominal bead <diameters> $0.17$, $0.5$, and $1\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ produced progressively broader measured spots. The size comparison controls for the finite object: the measured response is the <convolution> of bead emission with the effective <point spread function>, rather than a pure point response. Inverting this <convolution> model gave a response compared with the theoretical one. The smaller bead gave a weaker <signal> relative to <noise>; uncertainty in actual bead <diameter> also affected the restored width. These are limits of the calibration. In <confocal microscopy>, excitation and pinhole detection jointly determine the effective <point spread function>; this experiment does not measure the ideal widefield <Airy disk> plotted above.<Measurement of point-spread function (PSF) for confocal fluorescence microscopy by InCheon Song and others>[§2 and Fig. 1, pp. 2–3: apparatus; §3.2 and Figs. 4–5, pp. 4–5: bead-size comparison and limitations]{ref}

For a new calibration, useful additional controls are to measure detector background, avoid saturating the <photodetector>, repeat the scan to check stability, and compare isolated beads at several positions in the <field of view>. Record <wavelength>, <numerical aperture>, pinhole setting, sampling interval and <optical focus>. These are practical recommendations, not extra measurements claimed for the published experiment.

= Encircled energy
{parent=Point spread function}
{title2=$E(R)$}

<Encircled energy> is the fraction of total image <radiant flux> within a circle of radius $R$ around the centre of a <point spread function>.

= Point spread function normalization
{parent=Point spread function}
{title2=$\int p\,d^2\mathbf x=1$}

<Point spread function normalization> specifies how the response is scaled. Unit-<integral> scaling separates image shape from total <radiant flux>; unit-peak scaling compares shapes but does not make their total <radiant fluxes> equal.

= Optical point source
{parent=Point spread function}

An <optical point source> has spatial extent negligible relative to the imaging scale being tested. The <Dirac delta function> is its idealization; a finite bead or unresolved <star> only approximates it.

= Incoherent imaging
{parent=Point spread function}

In <incoherent imaging>, contributions from distinct object points add as <light intensities> because their mutual <spatial optical coherence> is negligible. A single <convolution> additionally requires <translation invariance> of the <point spread function>.

= Coherent imaging
{parent=Point spread function}

In <coherent imaging>, complex <wave amplitudes> add with their relative <wave phases> preserved. The measured <light intensity> includes <wave interference> between the contributions.

= Coherent imaging cutoff
{parent=Coherent imaging}
{title2=$\nu_c$}

For an ideal on-axis coherent circular-pupil system the field transfer cutoff is $\nu_c=\mathrm{NA}/\lambda$. Incoherent intensity transfer extends to $2\mathrm{NA}/\lambda$. Field and intensity transfer functions must be distinguished.

= Amplitude spread function
{parent=Coherent imaging}
{title2=$h$}

The <amplitude spread function> is the complex field response to an <optical point source>. Its squared magnitude, with a specified <point spread function normalization>, gives the intensity <point spread function>.

= Airy disc
{c}
{synonym}

The <Airy disk> is the central bright region, up to the first dark ring, in the scalar <diffraction> pattern of a uniformly illuminated, unobscured circular <aperture>. The whole pattern includes the weaker surrounding rings; its radius is distinct from its <full width at half maximum>.

For a circular <optical pupil> of <diameter> $D$ under normal, uniform monochromatic <plane wave> illumination, <Fraunhofer diffraction> gives
$$
\frac{I(\theta)}{I_0}=P(u)=\left[\frac{2J_1(u)}{u}\right]^2,\qquad u=\frac{\pi D\sin\theta}{\lambda},\qquad P(0)=1.
$$
Here $I_0$ is the on-axis <light intensity>, $\theta$ is the <angle> from the <optical axis>, $\lambda$ is the <wavelength> in the propagation medium, and $J_1$ is the order-one <Bessel function of the first kind>. The <paraxial approximation> replaces $\sin\theta$ by $\theta$, measured in <radians>. The first positive zero $j_{1,1}=3.831705970\ldots$ gives
$$
\sin\theta_1=\frac{j_{1,1}}{\pi}\frac{\lambda}{D},\qquad \theta_1\simeq1.219669891\frac{\lambda}{D}\simeq1.22\frac{\lambda}{D}.
$$
This is the angular radius of the <Airy disk>; the angular diameter is twice as large. The <Airy disk derivation> explains the origin of the <Bessel function of the first kind> and of the numerical coefficient.<Oscillations and Waves by Richard Fitzpatrick/Two-Dimensional Fourier Optics>[equations 10.78–10.85 and Table 10.1: circular-aperture response and first zero]{ref}

\Image[airy-disk-radial-intensity.png]
{title=Ideal circular-aperture intensity: central disk, dark rings and radial profile}

The image and both radial curves show the same calculated <light intensity>, divided by its peak. The image's <logarithmic scale> makes the weak rings visible; its colour is a display choice. The linear curve shows how small the ring peaks are compared with the centre. A dark ring marks a zero of <wave amplitude>, produced by cancellation across the <optical pupil>; it is not the edge of all transmitted <light>. The first <Airy disk> contains about $83.78\%$ of the total <radiant flux>, as calculated in <Airy disk normalization and encircled energy>.

For an original design example, take an ideal <optical lens> in air with <focal length> $f=100\,\mathrm{mm}$, circular <optical pupil> <diameter> $D=5.00\,\mathrm{mm}$ and <wavelength> $\lambda=550\,\mathrm{nm}$. In the <focal plane>, the radial coordinate $r\simeq f\theta$ gives
$$
\theta_1\simeq1.34164\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{rad},\qquad r_1\simeq1.219669891\frac{\lambda f}{D}=13.4164\,\mathrm{\mu m}.
$$
The first-zero diameter is $26.8327\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. Solving $P(u_{1/2})=1/2$ gives $u_{1/2}=1.616339948\ldots$, so the diameter measured by <full width at half maximum> is only
$$
w_{1/2}=\frac{2u_{1/2}}{\pi}\frac{\lambda f}{D}\simeq1.028993970\frac{\lambda f}{D}=11.3189\,\mathrm{\mu m}.
$$
Thus a quoted “spot diameter” is incomplete without its definition. Doubling $D$ halves both widths at fixed <wavelength> and <focal length>; increasing <magnification> alone changes the image scale without improving object-space discrimination.

The <Airy disk> supplies a calculable reference for a <point spread function>, allowing <diffraction> to be distinguished from <defocus> and <optical aberrations>. Its beauty is that one circular boundary determines an entire sequence of bright and dark rings through a <Fourier transform>. The ideal formula assumes an unobscured pupil, uniform illumination, ideal <optical focus>, a single <wavelength>, and a scalar description with small output <angles>. A blocked centre, nonuniform pupil transmission or a significant pupil <wave phase> error changes the response. High <numerical aperture> may require <polarization of an electromagnetic wave> and a vector field description. The <circular-aperture diffraction laboratory experiment> tests the far-field pattern directly; the <Rayleigh criterion> uses its first zero to define a two-source convention.

= Airy disk derivation
{c}
{parent=Airy disk}
{title2=$A(u)=2J_1(u)/u$}

Write $a=D/2$ and <wavenumber> $k=2\pi/\lambda$. The pupil <Fourier transform>, normalized by pupil <area>, gives the <Fraunhofer diffraction> <wave amplitude>. In pupil <polar coordinates>,
$$
A(u)=\frac1{\pi a^2}\int_0^a\int_0^{2\pi}e^{-ik\rho\sin\theta\cos\phi}\rho\,d\phi\,d\rho=2\int_0^1tJ_0(ut)\,dt,\qquad u=ka\sin\theta.
$$
The angular average is $J_0$ because $J_0(z)=(2\pi)^{-1}\int_0^{2\pi}e^{-iz\cos\phi}\,d\phi$. The <Bessel function of the first kind> identity $d\{zJ_1(z)\}/dz=zJ_0(z)$ then reduces the radial <integral> to $A(u)=2J_1(u)/u$. Taking the squared magnitude yields the <Airy disk> <light intensity> profile.<Oscillations and Waves by Richard Fitzpatrick/Two-Dimensional Fourier Optics>[equations 10.78–10.84: angular integral, radial integral and Bessel identities]{ref}

The apparent division by zero is removable. The <power series>
$$
J_1(u)=\frac u2-\frac{u^3}{16}+\frac{u^5}{384}+O(u^7)
$$
gives $A(u)=1-u^2/8+u^4/192+O(u^6)$ and $P(u)=1-u^2/4+5u^4/192+O(u^6)$. At the centre, every pupil contribution has the same <wave phase>, so their normalized sum is one. The first dark ring instead corresponds to $J_1(u)=0$ with $u>0$, rather than to the removable zero of $J_1$ at the origin.

= Airy disk normalization and encircled energy
{c}
{parent=Airy disk}
{title2=$E(U)=1-J_0(U)^2-J_1(U)^2$}

Peak normalization $P(0)=1$ does not make the <integral> of a <point spread function> equal to one. In the <paraxial approximation> to the <focal plane>, define $b=\pi D/(\lambda f)$ and $u=br$. The unit-<integral> <point spread function normalization> is
$$
p(r)=\frac{b^2}{4\pi}P(br),\qquad \int_{\mathbb R^2}p(r)\,d^2\mathbf r=1.
$$
Here $p$ has units of inverse <area>; multiplying by the detected <radiant flux> gives image-plane <irradiance>. Its <encircled energy>, meaning the fraction inside radius $R=U/b$, is
$$
E(U)=\int_0^{U/b}2\pi r p(r)\,dr=\frac12\int_0^UuP(u)\,du=1-J_0(U)^2-J_1(U)^2.
$$
This is an <integral> over <area>, so its radial weight is $u$. An unweighted <integral> of a line through the centre is a different quantity.

A short proof uses the <Bessel function of the first kind> identities $J_0'=-J_1$ and $J_1'=J_0-J_1/u$. They give
$$
\frac{d}{dU}\left(1-J_0(U)^2-J_1(U)^2\right)=\frac{2J_1(U)^2}{U}=\frac U2P(U).
$$
Both expressions for $E$ vanish at zero. Since these <Bessel functions of the first kind> tend to zero as $U\to\infty$, $E(\infty)=1$, establishing the normalization. At the first dark ring,
$$
E(j_{1,1})=1-J_0(j_{1,1})^2\simeq0.837784869.
$$
A circular detector region ending at the first zero therefore misses about $16.22\%$ of the ideal <radiant flux>, even though the surrounding rings have low peak <light intensity>. This matters when converting measured counts into total source brightness or choosing an image aperture for a <photodetector>.

= Circular-aperture diffraction laboratory experiment
{parent=Airy disk}

An actual teaching setup is described in <Laboratory Exercise No. 4: Diffraction, MIT 6.237, Fall 2024>, for MIT's 6.237 Modern Optics Project Laboratory, Fall 2024. Preparation includes predicting the aperture-size dependence and the distinction between <Fraunhofer diffraction> and <Fresnel diffraction>. A helium–neon <laser> supplies $632.8\,\mathrm{nm}$ <light>; the far-field setup uses a planar incident <wavefront>, a diffracting object and a distant screen, with beam expansion when needed. Figure 1 also marks an optional <CCD> camera. Exercise 4.7 asks students to sketch the circular-aperture pattern, measure the <Airy disk> diameter between correctly identified boundaries, and infer the aperture size. No measured values are supplied. As a regime comparison, exercise 4.9 varies the circular opening's position near a microscope objective <optical lens>, observes near-field patterns and compares them with the far-field case. This tests whether a change in propagation geometry changes the pattern. The coherent illumination measures one aperture's response, rather than the discrimination of two mutually incoherent <optical point sources>.<Laboratory Exercise No. 4: Diffraction, MIT 6.237, Fall 2024>[pp. 1–2, introduction, PL4.1 and Fig. 1; p. 5, §4.7; p. 6, §4.9: apparatus, readout and regime comparison]{ref}

For a quantitative interpretation, let $L$ be the aperture-to-screen distance and $d_{\rm spot}$ the first-zero diameter on the screen. In the <paraxial approximation>, the independent calculation is
$$
d_{\rm spot}\simeq2L\theta_1=2\frac{j_{1,1}}\pi\frac{\lambda L}{D},\qquad D\simeq2\frac{j_{1,1}}\pi\frac{\lambda L}{d_{\rm spot}}.
$$
For an explicitly hypothetical measurement, $L=1.80\,\mathrm m$ and $d_{\rm spot}=5.60\,\mathrm{mm}$ at $632.8\,\mathrm{nm}$ would imply $D\simeq0.496\,\mathrm{mm}$. The scale $D^2/\lambda\simeq0.389\,\mathrm m$ is smaller than $L$, but only by a factor of about $4.6$; this alone gives limited confidence in a high-precision <Fraunhofer diffraction> approximation. It is not a reported MIT measurement.

Additional recommended controls are to keep <wavelength> fixed, record $L$, centre and uniformly illuminate the <aperture>, and repeat with another $D$. Compare $d_{\rm spot}/L$, which should vary inversely with $D$. A camera readout should include blocked-beam background and avoid saturation. A scaled repetition at larger $L$ tests residual <Fresnel diffraction>; nonuniform illumination, <optical aberrations>, detector sampling and uncertainty in locating a dark ring limit the inferred $D$. The screen pattern's apparent bright edge depends on display brightness and is not a substitute for the first-zero boundary.

= Defocus

<Defocus> is the mismatch between the receiving plane and the intended <optical focus>. Changing the plane changes the <point spread function> even when the <optical lens> is otherwise ideal.

= Optical transfer function
{parent=Point spread function}
{wiki}
{title2=$H(\boldsymbol\nu)=\widehat p(\boldsymbol\nu)$}

The <optical transfer function> is the <Fourier transform> of the normalized intensity <point spread function> for <incoherent imaging> with <translation invariance>, using <spatial frequency> in cycles per unit length. It has $H(\mathbf0)=1$. For an ideal circular pupil it is the normalized pupil autocorrelation, with cutoff $2\mathrm{NA}/\lambda_0$ in object coordinates: no overlap remains when two pupil copies are shifted apart by more than one pupil <diameter>.<MIT 2.71 and 2.710 Optics, Spring 2014 by Nicholas X. Fang/Review for Optics>[p. 8, §E(b): OTF as Fourier transform of intensity PSF and pupil autocorrelation]{ref}

= Michelson contrast
{parent=Optical transfer function}
{title2=$C$}

For a periodic intensity distribution, $C=(I_{\max}-I_{\min})/(I_{\max}+I_{\min})$. It should not be confused with a resist contrast parameter.

= Modulation transfer function
{parent=Optical transfer function}
{title2=$M(\nu)$}

The magnitude of the normalized intensity optical transfer function. It describes transfer of sinusoidal intensity contrast in a linear, shift-invariant incoherent imaging model.

= Diffraction-limited system
{parent=Point spread function}
{wiki}

A <diffraction-limited system> has an optical response close to the ideal <diffraction> response for its specified <optical pupil>, <wavelength> and <optical focus>. Improving the <optical aberrations> further makes little difference to that response. This describes the optics: a poorly sampled or noisy detector can still deliver an image with much worse usable <diffraction-limited resolution>.

The <optical pupil> determines which <wave amplitudes> and <wave phases> contribute to an image point. In a scalar, <paraxial approximation> at the <focal plane> of an <optical lens> in air, write the pupil transmission as $T(u,v)e^{i\phi(u,v)}$, after removing the ideal focusing phase. The <amplitude spread function> is proportional to its scaled <Fourier transform>:
$$
h(x,y)\propto\iint T(u,v)e^{i\phi(u,v)}\exp\left[-\frac{2\pi i}{\lambda f}(ux+vy)\right]\,du\,dv.
$$
Here $(u,v)$ are pupil coordinates, $(x,y)$ are image coordinates, $f$ is <focal length>, and $\lambda$ is the <wavelength> in air. For a uniformly transmitting, unobscured circular <optical pupil> at ideal <optical focus>, the result is an <Airy disk> with surrounding rings. A blocked centre, support structures, or nonuniform transmission changes the ideal <point spread function>; “diffraction limited” does not specify one universal spot shape. The existing <Fourier model of a Lyot coronagraph> illustrates how pupil and focal masks deliberately alter this response.<MIT 2.71 and 2.710 Optics, Spring 2014 by Nicholas X. Fang/Review for Optics>[pp. 6–8, §§D(c), E: lens Fourier transform and pupil transfer function]{ref}

<Optical aberrations> add pupil <wave phase> errors. <Defocus> contributes a quadratic pupil <wave phase> in the <paraxial approximation>, redistributing <light intensity> from the best-focused spot. The <Strehl ratio> compares the peak with the ideal response for the same pupil and total <radiant flux>. A broad <optical spectrum> produces a weighted sum of wavelength-dependent <point spread functions>; <chromatic aberration> can additionally change <optical focus> with <wavelength>. Thus the operating conditions belong with any claim that a system is diffraction limited.

The <numerical aperture> is $n\sin\alpha$, where $n$ is the <refractive index> of the medium between object and objective, and $\alpha$ is the half-<angle> of the admitted cone of <light>. = Diffraction-limited resolution
{parent=Diffraction-limited system}
{title2=$\theta\sim\lambda/D$}

<Diffraction-limited resolution> is a scale for distinguishing details with a specified ideal <diffraction> response and a specified criterion. For two equally bright, mutually incoherent <optical point sources> imaged through a uniformly transmitting, unobscured circular <optical pupil>, the <Rayleigh criterion> places the centre of one <Airy disk> at the first zero of the other. At small <angles> in air this separation is
$$
\theta_{\rm R}=\frac{j_{1,1}}{\pi}\frac{\lambda}{D}\simeq1.22\frac{\lambda}{D},\qquad j_{1,1}\simeq3.831706.
$$
Here $D$ is pupil <diameter>, $\lambda$ is <wavelength> in air, and $j_{1,1}$ is the first positive zero of the order-one <Bessel function of the first kind>. This is the radius to the first zero, not the diameter of the central spot. <Fraunhofer diffraction> gives the radial peak-normalized <light intensity>
$$
P(q)=\left[\frac{2J_1(q)}q\right]^2,\qquad q=\frac{\pi D\theta}{\lambda},\qquad P(0)=1.
$$
The removable value at zero follows from $J_1(q)\sim q/2$. The pupil <Fourier transform> reduces to a radial <integral> by circular symmetry, which produces the <Bessel function of the first kind>; its first zero supplies the factor $1.22$.<Oscillations and Waves by Richard Fitzpatrick/Two-Dimensional Fourier Optics>{ref}

In the usual scalar circular-pupil approximation for a <fluorescence microscope>, the corresponding object-plane <Rayleigh criterion> is
$$
r_{\rm R}=\frac{j_{1,1}}{2\pi}\frac{\lambda_0}{\mathrm{NA}}\simeq0.61\frac{\lambda_0}{\mathrm{NA}},\qquad q=\frac{2\pi\mathrm{NA}\,r}{\lambda_0}.
$$
Here $\lambda_0$ is the vacuum emission <wavelength>, and the object-side <numerical aperture> already includes the medium's <refractive index>. Do not divide $\lambda_0$ by $n$ again. For small cone <angles> in air, $\mathrm{NA}\simeq D/(2f)$ and image-plane radius $f\theta_{\rm R}$ agrees with this form. The assumptions include a single <wavelength>, ideal <optical focus>, and mutually incoherent emitters. High <numerical aperture> can require a vector description with <polarization of an electromagnetic wave> and emitter orientation; <confocal microscopy> has its own excitation-and-detection response. The same $0.61$ formula therefore does not describe every microscope or illumination method.<Resolution by Michael W. Davidson>{ref}

\Image[circular-pupil-point-spread-scale.png]
{title=Doubling numerical aperture halves the ideal circular-pupil point-response scale at fixed wavelength}

In this original model, $\lambda_0=550\,\mathrm{nm}$ and <numerical apertures> $0.25$ and $0.50$ give first-zero radii $1.342\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ and $0.671\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. Each <point spread function> is divided by its own peak to compare shape. The pupil axes are transverse <wave vector> components multiplied by $\lambda_0/(2\pi)$, so pupil radius equals <numerical aperture>. A wider pupil accepts a wider range of transverse <wave vectors>, producing a narrower image. Increasing <magnification> alone enlarges the same spot and does not reduce its object-plane radius.

The <Rayleigh criterion> is a useful visual convention, not an absolute information barrier. For equal incoherent sources with total detected <radiant flux> $F$ and separation vector $\mathbf s$, the ideal image is
$$
I(\mathbf x)=\frac F2\left[p(\mathbf x-\mathbf s/2)+p(\mathbf x+\mathbf s/2)\right].
$$
Below the <Rayleigh criterion> this still differs from $Fp(\mathbf x)$; for small $\mathbf s$, symmetry cancels the linear change and the first change is quadratic in separation. Estimating two unknown source positions is then increasingly sensitive to <noise>, background, relative brightness and uncertainty in the <point spread function>. Localizing one isolated source and recovering an arbitrary extended object are different inference tasks. Measurements that separate optical modes can also extract separation information differently from direct image <light intensity> measurement.<Quantum Theory of Superresolution for Two Incoherent Optical Point Sources by Mankei Tsang and others>{ref}

There remains a real limit for arbitrary images in this model: the <optical transfer function> is zero above the transmitted <spatial frequency> band. Its cutoff for scalar <incoherent imaging> is $\nu_c=2\mathrm{NA}/\lambda_0$ in object-plane cycles per unit length. The <Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem> therefore suggests object-referred <detector pixel> pitch $p_{\rm pix}\leq\lambda_0/(4\mathrm{NA})$, with finer sampling providing margin. Finite <detector pixels> also integrate over their <area>, suppressing contrast; coarse sampling can cause <aliasing>. Finer sampling cannot alone recover <spatial frequencies> removed by the optics, and <Fourier deconvolution> amplifies <noise> where the <optical transfer function> is small. <Photon shot noise>, <read noise>, <optical aberrations> and <defocus> can all worsen practical discrimination. A useful resolution claim therefore reports the object model, criterion, <wavelength>, <numerical aperture>, sampling and <signal-to-noise ratio in photon counting>.

= Rayleigh criterion
{c}
{parent=Diffraction-limited resolution}
{title2=$\theta_{\rm R}=1.22\lambda/D$}

The <Rayleigh criterion> calls two equal, mutually incoherent <optical point sources> just resolved when one circular-pupil <Airy disk>'s centre coincides with the other's first zero. It is a specified discrimination convention, distinct from <full width at half maximum> and from precision in estimating one source's position.

For this circular <optical pupil>, the <Rayleigh criterion> uses angular separation $s_{\rm R}=\theta_1\simeq1.22\lambda/D$, or $s_{\rm R}=r_1\simeq1.22\lambda f/D$ in the <paraxial approximation> to the <focal plane>. The <point spread functions> still overlap.<Oscillations and Waves by Richard Fitzpatrick/Two-Dimensional Fourier Optics>[equations 10.85–10.87: circular-aperture first-zero scale and Rayleigh convention]{ref}

For the <Airy disk> design example with $D=5.00\,\mathrm{mm}$, $f=100\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $\lambda=550\,\mathrm{nm}$, the angular convention gives $s_{\rm R}=1.34164\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{rad}$, or about $27.67$ <arcseconds>, and the image-plane separation is $13.4164\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. Two equally bright, mutually incoherent <optical point sources> at separations $0.7$, $1$ and $1.5$ times this scale would have image separations $9.391$, $13.416$ and $20.125\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ respectively. These calculated cases are compared in <incoherent two-source Airy profile>.

The <Rayleigh criterion> is useful because it ties an easily recognized two-peak convention to a predictable optical scale. It does not assert that all information vanishes below that separation. Inferring two unknown positions also depends on <photon shot noise>, background, unequal brightness and uncertainty in the <point spread function>; locating a single isolated source is a different task. A theoretical analysis shows that alternative optical measurements can estimate the separation of two incoherent <optical point sources> below this scale.<Quantum Theory of Superresolution for Two Incoherent Optical Point Sources by Mankei Tsang and others>[abstract: linear optics and photon counting can estimate sub-Rayleigh separation]{ref}

The mutual <optical coherence> condition is essential. In <incoherent imaging>, time-averaged <light intensities> add. In <coherent imaging>, <wave amplitudes> add before squaring, leaving a <wave interference> term controlled by relative <wave phase>. Two holes illuminated by the same coherent <laser> therefore do not implement the equal-incoherent-source convention merely because their individual patterns resemble <Airy disks>. The coherent single-aperture laboratory test establishes the response of each aperture; it does not establish the pair's mutual incoherence.

= Incoherent two-source Airy profile
{parent=Rayleigh criterion}
{title2=$Q_s(x)=\tfrac12\{P(b(x-s/2))+P(b(x+s/2))\}$}

Let $x$ measure position along the line joining two mutually incoherent <optical point sources> in the <focal plane>, with equal detected <radiant fluxes> and separation $s$. Extend the even <Airy disk> profile $P(u)$ to signed $u$, and put $b=\pi D/(\lambda f)$ as in <Airy disk normalization and encircled energy>. Relative to the peak of one source carrying the pair's total <radiant flux>, their summed <light intensity> is
$$
Q_s(x)=\frac12P\bigl(b(x-s/2)\bigr)+\frac12P\bigl(b(x+s/2)\bigr),\qquad bs_{\rm R}=j_{1,1}.
$$
The two-dimensional <point spread functions> underlying these line profiles all have the same total <radiant flux>. The line-profile <integrals> themselves need not equal one.

\Image[rayleigh-two-source-intensity.png]
{title=Equal incoherent sources at 0.7, 1 and 1.5 times the Rayleigh separation}

The dashed curves show individual contributions; the solid curve is their sum. All panels use the same brightness scale, and the vertical markers indicate the true source centres. At $s=s_{\rm R}$, the other source contributes zero at each centre, so $Q_{s_{\rm R}}(\pm s_{\rm R}/2)=1/2$. At the midpoint,
$$
Q_{s_{\rm R}}(0)=P(j_{1,1}/2)\simeq0.367515831.
$$
The midpoint is therefore about $26.50\%$ below the source-centre peak. This is a calculation for this particular <Airy disk> and equal-incoherent-source convention, not a universal experimental threshold. At $0.7s_{\rm R}$ the two central peaks merge; at $1.5s_{\rm R}$ the valley is deeper. Changing separation continuously changes the profile, without a discontinuity at the <Rayleigh criterion>.

To see how information survives when the central peaks merge, set $z=bx$ and $\delta=bs$. A symmetric <Taylor expansion> gives
$$
Q_s(x)=P(z)+\frac{\delta^2}{8}P''(z)+O(\delta^4).
$$
The linear terms cancel because the source positions are symmetric. At the midpoint, the <Airy disk derivation> gives $Q_s(0)=1-\delta^2/16+O(\delta^4)$. Thus a close pair's shape differs from that of one source, but only quadratically at leading order. Detecting this small change becomes sensitive to <noise> and response calibration. For unequal flux fractions $\alpha$ and $1-\alpha$, the intensity instead becomes $\alpha P(b(x-s/2))+(1-\alpha)P(b(x+s/2))$; its valley and peak positions need not match the equal-source example.

= Spatial resolution element
{parent=Diffraction-limited resolution}
{title2=$\Delta\vartheta\sim\lambda/D$}

A <spatial resolution element> is one independently resolved angular or spatial scale under a specified resolution criterion. For an ideal telescope of <diameter> $D$, its angular scale is of order $\lambda/D$ and its angular area of order $(\lambda/D)^2$. A <photodetector> <detector pixel> or <spaxel> can be smaller than a resolution element without adding independent spatial information.

= Strehl ratio
{c}
{parent=Point spread function}
{wiki}
{title2=$S=I_{\rm peak}/I_{\rm ideal,peak}$}

The Strehl ratio compares the peak of an observed <point spread function> with the ideal <diffraction-limited system> peak for the same pupil and total flux. It measures how strongly <wavefront errors> redistribute light away from the central peak.

= Maréchal approximation
{c}
{parent=Strehl ratio}
{title2=$S\simeq e^{-\sigma_\phi^2}$}

For small residual phase errors, $S\simeq e^{-\sigma_\phi^2}$, where $\sigma_\phi=2\pi h_{\rm rms}/\lambda$. The variance is taken after removing irrelevant constant phase. This approximation to the <Strehl ratio> is most useful near the <diffraction limit of a telescope>.
