= Chemical bond
{wiki}

A <chemical bond> associates <chemical atoms> or groups of <chemical atoms> into a sufficiently stable structure to treat it as a distinct entity. The concept covers connections within <molecules> and in extended solids; see the https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/CT07009[IUPAC Gold Book, chemical bond]. It is useful because composition alone does not specify how the <chemical atoms> are connected, and those connections help determine a <chemical substance>'s properties and possible <chemical reactions>.

In a <covalent bond>, <electrons> are shared between <chemical atoms>. Two <hydrogen> <chemical atoms> can form a <molecular hydrogen> <molecule>, with its <electrons> interacting with both <atomic nuclei>. In the usual description of this diatomic <molecule>, the <potential energy> as a function of nuclear separation has a minimum: moving the nuclei too close or too far raises the <energy>. See <Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/7.2 Covalent Bonding>.

Separating bonded <chemical atoms> requires <energy>; forming the corresponding <chemical bonds> releases <energy>. Whether an overall <chemical reaction> releases <energy> depends on both the <chemical bonds> broken and those formed. The lines in the <stoichiometry> diagram indicate connections schematically, rather than literal rods or a sequence of microscopic events.

= Siloxane bond
{parent=Chemical bond}

An Si–O–Si linkage. <hydrolysis> produces <silanol> groups; their condensation can regenerate an Si–O–Si linkage.

= Delta bonding
{parent=Chemical bond}

Bonding with two nodal planes containing the internuclear or coordination axis. <metal> $d_{xy}$ and $d_{x^2-y^2}$ orbitals can interact with ring combinations of matching delta symmetry; symmetry alone does not imply a strong bond.

= Bonding trends in second-period single bonds
{parent=Chemical bond}

Compact valence orbitals favor strong carbon-carbon bonding, but neighboring lone-pair repulsion can weaken nitrogen-nitrogen bonds. Bond strengths depend on <orbital overlap>, polarity and electronic occupancy together; atom size alone is insufficient.

= Bond stretching force constant
{parent=Chemical bond}
{title2=$k$}

In a local harmonic approximation, the vibrational wavenumber obeys $\widetilde\nu=(2\pi c)^{-1}\sqrt{k/\mu}$. Comparisons at fixed <reduced mass> therefore use squared frequency ratios. A local stretching force constant is not identical to a <bond-dissociation energy>.

= Resonance in chemistry
{parent=Chemical bond}

Representation of an electronic structure using multiple Lewis contributors with the same nuclear connectivity. The contributors are not separate species interconverting through ordinary <chemical reactions>.

{parent=Multicentre bonding}

A <hydrogen atom> associated with more than one skeletal centre. Removal as a <proton> must be distinguished from <hydride> transfer; the cluster charge and <electron> count change differently in the two operations.

= Wade's rules
{synonym}
{c}

An electron-counting approach relating <skeletal electron pairs> to a cluster vertex count $n$. A <closo> deltahedron has $n+1$ pairs; <nido> and <arachno> frameworks have $n+2$ and $n+3$. The count excludes electrons in externally directed bonds or <lone pairs>. It is a structural guide with a defined domain, not a rule that every molecule with the same total valence count has the same shape.

= Skeletal electron count
{parent=Polyhedral skeletal electron pair theory}
{title2=$N_{\rm skel}$}

The number of electrons allocated to the connected cluster framework after separating external bonds and appropriate <lone pairs>. For $\mathrm{B_nH_m^{q-}}$ with one terminal B–H bond per vertex it is $n+m+q$.

= Skeletal electron pair
{parent=Skeletal electron count}
{title2=$\mathrm{SEP}$}

A pair of electrons allocated to cluster bonding. <closo>, <nido> and <arachno> counts distinguish the number of missing vertices relative to a closed parent polyhedron.

= Metal-metal bond
{parent=Chemical bond}

A <metal-metal bond> joins two <metal> centers directly through overlap of their <atomic orbitals>. A single bond contributes one <electron> to the local count at each <metal> in conventional <electron counting>. <Bridging ligands> can accompany direct bonding, but a short bridged separation alone does not establish its <bond order>.

= Negative hyperconjugation
{parent=Chemical bond}

<Negative hyperconjugation> is donation from an occupied lone-pair or related orbital into an antibonding sigma orbital. Nitrogen-to-P–X sigma-star donation contributes to <phosphazene> bonding. Short P–N bonds therefore do not require a model dominated by phosphorus $3d$ pi orbitals.

= Conjugated system
{parent=Chemical bond}

Adjacent compatible p <atomic orbitals> allow <electron density> to delocalize across more than two atoms. <Resonance contributors> describe aspects of this shared electronic structure.

= Pi conjugation
{parent=Conjugated system}

Overlap between adjacent p-type <atomic orbitals> permits delocalized electronic states across more than one <chemical bond>. A cyclic path with suitable occupancy can support <aromaticity>; an external <lone pair> can act as a donor without contributing an extra independent pair to every ring count.

= Pi electron
{parent=Pi conjugation}

An <electron> occupying a pi-type <molecular orbital>. In a conjugated planar <molecule>, the pi subspace is commonly built from <p orbitals> perpendicular to the molecular plane; its electron population excludes the sigma framework.

= Bond-length alternation
{parent=Conjugated system}
{title2=$\beta_S-\beta_L$}

Alternation between shorter and longer <chemical bonds> along a <conjugated system>. In the <Hückel method>, unequal nearest-neighbour couplings split the frontier <molecular orbitals>. An electronic <energy> gain must be compared at a stated constraint on the mean coupling and against the geometric distortion cost.

= Bond-dissociation energy
{parent=Chemical bond}
{wiki}
{title2=$D$}

A <bond-dissociation energy> specifies the <energy> needed for a stated dissociation into separated fragments. An <energy> from the lowest vibrational state, an <energy> from the bottom of a <potential energy> well, and a finite-temperature molar dissociation <enthalpy> require different conventions; do not silently mix them in an <electronegativity> formula.

Which fragments, in which <quantum states>, and at which <temperature>? For a neutral gaseous <molecule> with a specified two-electron <covalent bond>, the usual thermochemical convention is <homolysis>,
$$
\mathrm{A{-}B}(g)\longrightarrow\mathrm A^{\bullet}(g)+\mathrm B^{\bullet}(g).
$$
Each fragment receives one of the two bonding <electrons>; the dots denote unpaired <electrons>. The final fragments are isolated from each other, with their own relaxed <molecular geometry> and specified electronic <quantum states>. The <energy> cost includes this relaxation, rather than merely stretching the initial <chemical bond> while freezing everything else. For a neutral parent, <heterolysis> instead produces $\mathrm A^+(g)+\mathrm B^-(g)$, or the opposite assignment of <electric charges>; those are different final states and different <energies>. A value for gaseous neutral fragments cannot be transferred to <ions>, a <chemical solution>, or a <solid> without accounting for those changed conditions.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology, fifth edition (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry/bond-dissociation energy>[definition of bond-dissociation energy: molar enthalpy for homolysis of a specified molecular entity]{ref}

The distinction is useful beyond terminology. <Pauling electronegativity> compares consistently defined <bond-dissociation energies>; <photodissociation> can reveal a specified ground-state threshold; a thermal <chemical reaction> balance requires compatible <enthalpies>. A threshold is also different from an <activation energy>: it compares initial and final <quantum states>, whereas a barrier can obstruct the path between them.

= Bond potential-well depth
{parent=Bond-dissociation energy}
{title2=$D_e$}

For a <diatomic molecule>, let $V(r)$ be the electronic <potential energy> at <bond length> $r$, with the <atomic nuclei> treated as fixed while finding the electronic <energy>. Choose zero at infinitely separated <chemical atoms> in the specified electronic <quantum states>. If the minimum occurs at $r_e$, the <bond potential-well depth> is
$$
D_e=V(\infty)-V(r_e)=-V(r_e).
$$
The bottom of this curve describes an equilibrium <bond length>, not an attainable motionless vibrational <quantum state>. Changing the zero of <potential energy> shifts both endpoints equally and leaves $D_e$ unchanged. A <quantum harmonic oscillator> approximates the curve near its minimum but rises without limit, so that local approximation alone cannot supply a dissociation asymptote. A <Morse potential> supplies both a minimum and a finite separated-fragment limit.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry/dissociation energy>[definition: subscripts e and 0 distinguish the potential-energy minimum and ground state]{ref}

= Ground-state dissociation energy
{parent=Bond-dissociation energy}
{title2=$D_0$}

The <ground-state dissociation energy> starts at the lowest vibrational <quantum state>, with the lowest allowed rotational <quantum state> and the fragment states explicitly specified. For a <diatomic molecule> whose fragments are ground-state <chemical atoms>, omitting any additional rotational correction,
$$
D_0=D_e-E_{\rm ZP},
$$
where $E_{\rm ZP}$ is the vibrational <zero-point energy> measured upward from the well minimum. The initial <molecule> already has this <energy>, so less additional <energy> is needed to reach the same final limit.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry/bond-dissociation energy in theoretical chemistry>[definition of bond-dissociation energy in theoretical chemistry: D0 = De minus zero-point vibrational energy]{ref}

For fragments that are themselves <molecules>, include their <zero-point energies> too. Define $D_e$ as the electronic <energy> difference between separately relaxed products and reactant; then, with consistent rotational conventions,
$$
D_0=D_e+\sum_{\rm fragments}E_{\rm ZP}-E_{\rm ZP,parent}.
$$
Thus “subtract the parent's <zero-point energy>” is sufficient for atomic products but generally incomplete for molecular products. An excited final <quantum state> raises its channel threshold by its excitation <energy>; that threshold does not become the ground-product $D_0$ just because a detector selects it.

= Bond-dissociation enthalpy
{parent=Bond-dissociation energy}
{title2=$\Delta_{\rm diss}H^{\circ}(T)$}

A <bond-dissociation enthalpy> is the molar <enthalpy> change for the specified <homolysis> at <temperature> $T$, with reactant and products in specified <standard states>. Its usual units are $\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. The superscript $\circ$ specifies <standard states>; it does not fix $T$ to room <temperature>.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology, fifth edition (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry/bond-dissociation enthalpy>[definition and symbols: standard molar enthalpy for homolysis, Δdiss H°]{ref}

Write $\delta h_i(T)=H_{m,i}^{\circ}(T)-H_{m,i}^{\circ}(0)$ for each species' molar thermal <enthalpy> increment. With $D_0$ an <energy> per parent <molecule>,
$$
\Delta_{\rm diss}H^{\circ}(T)=N_{\rm A}D_0+\delta h_A(T)+\delta h_B(T)-\delta h_{AB}(T).
$$
Here $N_{\rm A}$ is the <Avogadro constant>. The thermal increments include translational <kinetic energy>, <molecular rotation>, thermally excited <molecular vibration> and electronic states when populated, and the $pV$ contribution to <enthalpy>. They are additional to the <zero-point energies> already included in $D_0$. If values of <standard enthalpy of formation> at the same $T$ are available, <Hess's law> gives the same quantity as $\Delta_fH^{\circ}(A)+\Delta_fH^{\circ}(B)-\Delta_fH^{\circ}(AB)$.

Units alone do not identify the convention. Multiplying either $D_e$ or $D_0$ by the <Avogadro constant> gives molar <energy>, but does not add thermal <enthalpy>. One <electronvolt> per dissociation corresponds to $96.485332\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. A <spectroscopic wavenumber> $\widetilde\nu$ represents <energy> $hc\widetilde\nu$, using the <Planck constant> $h$ and <speed of light> $c$; $1\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ corresponds to $0.011962657\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$.<Fundamental Physical Constants — Complete Listing by National Institute of Standards and Technology and others>[SI defining constants h, c, e and Avogadro constant; conversions calculated from them]{ref}

= Stipulated bond dissociation model
{parent=Bond-dissociation energy}

Take an invented <diatomic molecule> with a <Morse potential>, setting the separated-atom <energy> to zero:
$$
V(r)=D_e\left[\left(1-e^{-a(r-r_e)}\right)^2-1\right],\qquad
D_e=2.0000\,\mathrm{eV},\qquad A=\hbar\omega_e=0.2000\,\mathrm{eV}.
$$
Here $a$ is an inverse length, $r_e$ the equilibrium <bond length>, and $\omega_e=a\sqrt{2D_e/\mu}$, with <reduced mass> $\mu$. These stipulated parameters describe a simple model, not a measured <chemical substance>. For the one-dimensional <Morse oscillator>, <energy eigenvalues> above the minimum are
$$
\epsilon_v=A\left(v+\tfrac12\right)-\frac{A^2}{4D_e}\left(v+\tfrac12\right)^2,
\qquad v=0,1,\ldots,\quad v+\tfrac12<\frac{2D_e}{A}.
$$
Consequently,
$$
E_{\rm ZP}=\frac A2-\frac{A^2}{16D_e}=0.09875\,\mathrm{eV},\qquad
D_0=2.0000-0.09875=1.90125\,\mathrm{eV}.
$$
The harmonic estimate $A/2=0.1000\,\mathrm{eV}$ is slightly larger than this model's exact <zero-point energy>. The physical bound-level restriction matters: the quadratic expression is not an infinite ladder of vibrational <quantum states>.

{description=Original stipulated <Morse potential> with $D_e=2\,\mathrm{eV}$ and $A=0.2\,\mathrm{eV}$. The horizontal coordinate is $a(r-r_e)$; the horizontal $v=0$ line is an <energy eigenvalue>, not a classical trajectory or a measured spectrum.}

For a separate thermal illustration using this same stipulated $D_0$, assume an <ideal gas>, fully active <molecular rotation> of the parent, negligible thermal <molecular vibration>, and negligible electronic excitation for all species. Each monatomic product contributes $\delta h=5RT/2$; the <diatomic molecule> contributes $7RT/2$. Thus the net increment is $3RT/2$, with <molar gas constant> $R$. At $T=300\,\mathrm K$,
$$
N_{\rm A}D_e\approx192.971\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}},\qquad
N_{\rm A}D_0\approx183.443\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}},\qquad
\Delta_{\rm diss}H^{\circ}\approx187.184\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}.
$$
This last result follows from the stated thermal model, not a universal correction for every <chemical bond>. Frozen rotations, accessible excited states, different fragment composition, or a condensed <phase> change the thermal increments.

= Sequential bond-dissociation energies
{parent=Bond-dissociation energy}
{title2=$D_1,D_2,\ldots$}

Equivalent <chemical bonds> in an intact <molecule> need not have equal successive <bond-dissociation enthalpies>. Once one <chemical bond> is removed, the next reactant has a different electronic <quantum state> and may relax to a different <molecular geometry>. The successive steps compare different species. This distinction also explains why a tabulated average <chemical bond> strength is not necessarily the cost of the first cleavage.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/7.5 Strengths of Ionic and Covalent Bonds>[“Bond Strength: Covalent Bonds”, methane paragraph: equivalent initial bonds and unequal sequential breaking costs]{ref}

For an invented species $AB_2$, stipulate the following gas-phase steps, with all <enthalpies> at the same <temperature> and the same <standard states>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
AB_2(g)&\longrightarrow AB(g)+B(g),&D_1&=300\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}},\\
AB(g)&\longrightarrow A(g)+B(g),&D_2&=400\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The symbols stand for hypothetical species with consistently chosen electronic states. They are not values for <water> or another measured <molecule>. By <Hess's law>, the total <enthalpy of atomization> for $AB_2(g)\to A(g)+2B(g)$ is $700\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$; the <arithmetic mean> per broken <chemical bond> is $350\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. Neither individual step equals that mean. Nor must the second step be cheaper: here it is deliberately more expensive. An exact sequence sums exactly; substituting averages taken from other <molecules> generally gives only an estimate.

= Homolysis
{parent=Chemical bond}

<Homolysis> splits a <covalent bond> with one bonding <electron> assigned to each fragment. A neutral closed-shell <molecule> thereby produces fragments with unpaired <electrons>, each a <radical (chemistry)>. The usual <bond-dissociation enthalpy> uses this product convention.

= Heterolysis
{parent=Chemical bond}

<Heterolysis> assigns both bonding <electrons> to one fragment. For an initially neutral <molecule>, the resulting <cation> and <anion> define a different dissociation channel from <homolysis>; their isolated gas-phase <energy> differs from that of solvated <ions>.

= Photodissociation
{parent=Chemical bond}
{wiki}

<Photodissociation> separates a <molecule> into fragments after <photon absorption>. For an isolated parent with a single absorbed <photon>, <conservation of energy> gives
$$
h\nu+E_{\rm initial,int}=D_0+E_{\rm products,int}+E_{\rm recoil}.
$$
Internal excitation <energies> here are relative to the parent and product ground states used to define $D_0$; $E_{\rm recoil}$ is the total product translational <kinetic energy> in the parent's <centre of mass> frame. Measuring one product's <kinetic energy> is insufficient by itself. For two fragments of <masses> $m_A,m_B$, <conservation of momentum> gives $E_{\rm recoil}=E_A(m_A+m_B)/m_B$ when $A$ is detected. Unknown product excitation can conceal part of the available <energy>.

= Quantum yield
{parent=Photodissociation}
{title2=$\Phi$}

The number of specified photochemical events per absorbed photon. A wavelength-dependent channel yield must be included when converting absorption into a chemical rate.

= Photolysis rate coefficient
{parent=Photodissociation}
{title2=$J=\int F_\lambda\sigma_\lambda\Phi_\lambda\,d\lambda$}

= Photolysis coefficient
{synonym}

The first-order rate coefficient for a specified <photodissociation> channel. It integrates spectral <actinic flux>, absorption <cross section> and channel <quantum yield>. Absorption without dissociation does not contribute to that channel's rate. Its units are inverse time.

= Ozone-column photolysis shielding
{parent=Photolysis rate coefficient}

In a direct-beam model with an effective <ozone> cross section $\sigma$, removal of an overlying column $\Delta N$ multiplies the transmitted <photodissociation> flux by $e^{\sigma\Delta N}$. A real <photodissociation> coefficient integrates wavelength-dependent cross section, <quantum yield> and <actinic flux>, including scattering and the local radiation field.

= Photofragment-ion imaging
{parent=Photodissociation}
{wiki}

<Photofragment-ion imaging> detects fragment <velocities> by ionizing neutral products and mapping their motion onto a position-sensitive detector using an <electric field>. In a velocity-mapping implementation, the image radius measures transverse speed under fixed lens settings. Selecting an arrival-time slice helps extract the central plane of the three-dimensional distribution. Species identification and electronic <quantum state> selection are essential for turning a radius into a particular dissociation threshold.

= Nitrogen dissociation threshold from fragment images
{parent=Photofragment-ion imaging}

A <molecular nitrogen> experiment used a $0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ pulsed nozzle and two skimmers. A $5\,\mathrm{cm}$ tube raised the rotational <temperature>. Opposed vacuum-ultraviolet <laser> pulses excited selected molecular levels and, about $10\,\mathrm{ns}$ later, selectively ionized excited <nitrogen> fragments. A <microchannel plate detector>, phosphor screen and camera recorded radii.<Accurate measurements of the bond dissociation energies of 14N2, 14N15N and 15N2 by Liya Lu and others>[Lu, Jiang and Gao, §2, printed manuscript pp. 2–3: nozzle, tube, pump/probe and imaging readout]{ref}

The measured squared radii were fitted against molecular excitation <energies>, referenced to the parent <ground state>:
$$
E_j=D_{0,\rm channel}+\kappa R_j^2.
$$
The intercept at zero radius gave the excited-product threshold; the fitted slope absorbed the detector scale. A wavemeter monitored the excitation source; scanning the probe covered the recoil <Doppler shift>. Subtracting the atomic excitation <energies> converted the intercept to ground-product $D_0$.<Accurate measurements of the bond dissociation energies of 14N2, 14N15N and 15N2 by Liya Lu and others>[§1: image-radius equation; §2: wavemeter and probe scan; §3 and Figure 4: fits; §4: atomic excitation correction]{ref}

An apertured grounded plate and detector timing rejected faster competing channels. Selecting fragment electronic states distinguished nearby thresholds. Agreement of $D_e$ across <isotopes> after <zero-point energy> correction tested consistency. The method still requires correctly assigned levels, adequate near-threshold signal and stable imaging; a signal drop alone can reflect another channel or a barrier.<Accurate measurements of the bond dissociation energies of 14N2, 14N15N and 15N2 by Liya Lu and others>[§2: shielding plate and timing; §3: state-selected channels; §4 and Table 2: isotope consistency and limitations]{ref}

This measurement determines a state-resolved <ground-state dissociation energy>. Obtaining a finite-<temperature> <bond-dissociation enthalpy> requires the thermal increments above; obtaining a <bond potential-well depth> requires the <zero-point energy> correction. The three quantities describe different starting conditions for the same specified separation.

= Bond length
{parent=Chemical bond}
{wiki}
{title2=$r$}

A <bond length> describes the separation of the <atomic nuclei> of two bonded <chemical atoms>. Distinguish an equilibrium geometry from an instantaneous or experimentally averaged separation. Methods of determining <molecular geometry> can use different averaging conventions, so reported <bond lengths> need their measurement context. See the https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/BT07003[IUPAC Gold Book: bond length]. Changing a <bond length> is one possible <reaction coordinate>.

= Equilibrium bond length
{parent=Bond length}
{title2=$r_e$}

The nuclear separation at the minimum of an electronic potential-energy curve. It differs from effective separations inferred from vibrationally averaged <rotational constants>.

= Partial atomic charge
{parent=Chemical bond}

A partial atomic charge assigns an <electric charge> to a <chemical atom> by partitioning the actual <electron density>. The result depends on the partitioning convention and need not be an integer. It differs from <formal charge>, which divides bonding <electrons> equally in a specified <Lewis structure>.

= Electron pair
{parent=Chemical bond}
{wiki}

An <electron pair> consists of two <electrons> with opposite <spins> sharing the same spatial part of their <quantum state>. The <Lewis structure> distinction between a <bond pair> and a <lone pair> records whether that pair is assigned to a connection between <chemical atoms> or to one <chemical atom>.

= Hydrogen bond
{parent=Chemical bond}
{wiki}
{title2=$X-\mathrm H\cdots Y$}

A <hydrogen bond> is an attractive association involving a <hydrogen> attached to another <chemical atom>, and an accepting atom or group. In the IUPAC 2011 definition, the atom $X$ in $X-\mathrm H$ is more <electronegative> than <hydrogen>, and evidence of bond formation is required. The familiar strong examples use O–H or N–H groups, but restricting the concept to only nitrogen, oxygen and fluorine misses weaker cases. A short distance alone is not the entire definition.https://publications.iupac.org/pac/pdf/2011/pdf/8308x1637.pdf[Arunan et al., Definition of the hydrogen bond, IUPAC Recommendations 2011]{ref}

\Image[water-hydrogen-bond-roles.png]
{title=Two water molecules play different hydrogen-bond roles while retaining their own covalent O–H bonds}

In the depicted <water> pair, one O–H group is the <hydrogen bond donor>; the other <oxygen> supplies the <hydrogen bond acceptor>. The solid O–H line denotes a <covalent bond> within a <water> <molecule>; the dotted H···O line denotes the <hydrogen bond> between them. One <water> <molecule> can play both roles in a larger arrangement because it has O–H groups and <lone pairs>.

This makes <hydrogen bonds> useful for understanding organization beyond a single <molecular formula>. They connect <water> <molecules> and contribute to <protein> folding and recognition between <molecules>. The same types of <chemical atoms> can support different associations depending on which donor and acceptor sites are exposed and how they are oriented. Alberts and colleagues discuss these noncovalent interactions in https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26883/[Molecular Biology of the Cell, the chemical components of a cell].

<Hydrogen bonds> are often directional, with an approximately straight donor–H–acceptor arrangement favourable in familiar cases; strength and preferred geometry depend on the system. The IUPAC definition uses converging experimental or theoretical evidence rather than one universal distance threshold. The author-hosted https://ipc.iisc.ac.in/~ea/iupac/iupac/IUPAC_finalreport_Nov_2009TG.pdf[2009 task-group draft, §§3.1–3.3] explains the definition and typical criteria leading to the recommendation.

{parent=Hydrogen bond}

<Hydrogen-bond complementarity> matches donor and acceptor positions in a binding interface. Two strong sites need not cooperate if their spacing or direction is incompatible; preorganization can favor a multiply <hydrogen>-bonded complex.

Comparison of competing donor–acceptor pairings using their interaction strengths.

= Hydrogen bonding
{parent=Hydrogen bond}

Formation of <hydrogen bonds>, either within a molecule or between interacting molecules.

= Functional group interaction profile
{parent=Hydrogen bond}
{title2=$\Delta\Delta G^\circ(\alpha,\beta)$}

A <functional group interaction profile> maps the free-energy contribution of a solute donor–acceptor contact after accounting for competing solvent contacts. Solvent polarity and preferential solvation determine favourable and unfavourable regions.

= Hydrogen bond donor
{parent=Hydrogen bond}
{title2=$X-\mathrm H$}

A <hydrogen bond donor> is the $X-\mathrm H$ group supplying the attached <hydrogen> in a <hydrogen bond> $X-\mathrm H\cdots Y$. The donor atom $X$, or the whole <molecule> containing it, may also be called the donor. The terminology does not imply transfer of a free <proton>: in the ordinary <water> pair, the O–H <covalent bond> remains. Familiar donors include O–H groups in <water> and N–H groups in <ammonia>. See https://people.reed.edu/~glasfeld/Chem391/notes/ChIMF_2018.pdf[Andrew Glasfeld, Intermolecular Attraction, pp. 12–13: donor and acceptor roles].

\Image[hydrogen-bond-donor-acceptor-site-map.png]
{title=<Hydrogen bond donor> and <hydrogen bond acceptor> roles depend on attached <hydrogen>, <protonation>, and <chemical resonance>}
{description=Toy site map for ordinary O–H and N–H associations, not measured geometry or an exhaustive list of <hydrogen bonds>. $R$ and $R'$ denote groups attached through <carbon>. Coloured labels identify possible sites, rather than count the number of simultaneous contacts.}

The attached <hydrogen> is essential to this role. At the <nitrogen> site, an unprotonated <amine> $R_3\mathrm N$ can be a <hydrogen bond acceptor>, but has no N–H <hydrogen bond donor>. <Protonation> gives $R_3\mathrm{NH}^+$: the new N–H group can donate, while that <nitrogen> no longer has the accepting <lone pair>. This is a change of chemical form, not a permanent label attached to a <chemical element>. Here $R$ denotes a group attached through <carbon>; the statement concerns the <nitrogen> site and does not rule out weaker C–H associations elsewhere. See https://openstax.org/books/organic-chemistry/pages/24-3-basicity-of-amines[McMurry, Organic Chemistry, 24.3: amine protonation] and https://people.reed.edu/~glasfeld/Chem391/notes/ChIMF_2018.pdf[Glasfeld: protonated amines as donors].

An <amide> with an N–H <covalent bond> can donate even though its <nitrogen> is ordinarily not an accepting site. For example, in $R-\mathrm C(=\mathrm O)-\mathrm{NH}-R'$, N–H is a <hydrogen bond donor> and the <carbonyl group>'s <oxygen> is a <hydrogen bond acceptor>. Replacing the remaining N–H by another group attached through <carbon> removes this N–H donor without removing the accepting <oxygen>. The analogous division of roles in a <peptide bond> helps explain <protein> structure. See https://people.reed.edu/~glasfeld/Chem391/notes/ChIMF_2018.pdf[Glasfeld: amide roles and biological interactions]. The useful question is therefore which site supplies the <hydrogen>, rather than whether the whole substance is simply “hydrogen bonding”.

= Hydrogen-bond donor parameter
{parent=Hydrogen bond donor}

An empirical measure of donor strength on a specified scale, including weak interactions when the scale defines them. A small nonzero value does not require a conventional O–H or N–H group.

= Hydrogen bond acceptor
{parent=Hydrogen bond}

A <hydrogen bond acceptor> supplies the electron-rich atom or group $Y$ interacting with the attached <hydrogen> of a <hydrogen bond donor> in $X-\mathrm H\cdots Y$. An <oxygen> <lone pair> in <water> is the familiar example. Acceptance describes the association, not necessarily acquisition of a <proton>. A single <molecule>, such as <water> or <ammonia>, can offer both <hydrogen bond donor> and <hydrogen bond acceptor> sites. See https://people.reed.edu/~glasfeld/Chem391/notes/ChIMF_2018.pdf[Glasfeld: water and nitrogen-containing accepting sites].

A <lone pair> drawn in a <Lewis structure> is a useful clue, not a universal test. At an ordinary neutral <amide> <nitrogen>, the nominal <lone pair> participates in <chemical resonance> with the <carbonyl group>, so this <nitrogen> is ordinarily excluded from lists of <hydrogen bond acceptors>; the <carbonyl group>'s <oxygen> remains an accepting site. <Protonation> changes the test again: $\mathrm{NH}_4^+$ has four N–H <covalent bonds> and no <nitrogen> <lone pair>, whereas <ammonia> has three and one. These distinctions follow the actual chemical form, and accepting ability also depends on the partner and whether it can approach the site. See https://openstax.org/books/organic-chemistry/pages/24-3-basicity-of-amines[McMurry: amide resonance and protonation] and https://people.reed.edu/~glasfeld/Chem391/notes/ChIMF_2018.pdf[Glasfeld: limitations of nitrogen accepting sites].

Conversely, an accepting group need not supply an atom-centred <lone pair>. The following <phenol>–<benzene> experiment probes an O–H donor interacting with the ring's distributed $\pi$ <electron density>.

{title=<Infrared spectroscopy> distinguishes free and associated <phenol>: schematic experimental logic, no plotted or simulated spectra}

Saggu, Levinson and Boxer (2011) used <infrared spectroscopy> to compare $200\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ <phenol> in different <solvents>. The O–H stretching peak was at $3611\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ in $\mathrm{CCl}_4$ and $3555.9\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ in <benzene>. To distinguish association from a general <solvent> effect, they diluted the ring-containing <chemical solutions> with $\mathrm{CCl}_4$: two peaks appeared, with the free peak nearly fixed while the associated peak depended on the partner. Replacing the O–H <hydrogen> with <deuterium> reproduced the ordering of shifts for different partners. Thus the perturbed O–H group identifies the <hydrogen bond donor>; the ring supplies the <hydrogen bond acceptor>. A peak shift alone does not specify a unique <molecular geometry>. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3202663/[Saggu, Levinson and Boxer, Direct measurements of electric fields in weak OH···π hydrogen bonds, JACS 133 (2011), Figure 1, Table 1 and dilution controls]{ref}

The roles make recognition between <molecules> intelligible: exposed <hydrogen bond donors> need compatible <hydrogen bond acceptors> in reachable positions. A count of sites is a starting description, not a prediction of binding strength. In <water>, forming a contact between two partners can also replace their existing <hydrogen bonds> to <water>; the <Gibbs free energy> of association concerns the whole change. See https://people.reed.edu/~glasfeld/Chem391/notes/ChIMF_2018.pdf[Glasfeld, pp. 15–16: competition with solvent interactions].

= Valence electron
{parent=Chemical bond}
{wiki}

A <valence electron> belongs to the electronic arrangement used to describe a <chemical atom>'s <chemical bonds> and ordinary <chemical reactions>. For the neutral <hydrogen>, <carbon>, <nitrogen>, and <oxygen> <chemical atoms> used here, the <valence electrons> occupy the outermost occupied <electron shell>. Their respective <electron configurations> are $1s^1$, $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^2$, $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^3$, and $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^4$, giving one, four, five, and six <valence electrons>. See https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/6-4-electronic-structure-of-atoms-electron-configurations[Chemistry 2e, 6.4: electron configurations and valence electrons].

<Lewis structures> show these <valence electrons> while omitting the inner <electrons>. Consequently, eight drawn <valence electrons> in <water> do not mean eight total <electrons>: neutral <water> has ten, with two inner <oxygen> <electrons> omitted. Neutral <ammonia> likewise has ten total <electrons> but eight <valence electrons>; neutral <carbon dioxide> has twenty-two total <electrons> but sixteen <valence electrons>.

This distinction makes the small counts useful: connections and possible <lone pairs> can be organized without representing every <electron> or solving the entire many-<electron> <wavefunction>. The outermost-<electron shell> shortcut applies to the examples above, rather than serving as a universal definition for all <chemical elements>; the electronic arrangement relevant to <chemical bonds> can also involve other <electron subshells>.
