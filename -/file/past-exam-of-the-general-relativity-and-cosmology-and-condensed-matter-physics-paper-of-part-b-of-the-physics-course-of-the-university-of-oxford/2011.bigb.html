==== Unlettered 4
{scope}

===== Solution

The https://www-thphys.physics.ox.ac.uk/people/AndreiStarinets/TUTORIALS_NOTES_GR/gr-cosmology-exam-paper-2011-solutions.pdf#page=27[official notes, PDF p. 27, handwritten p. 20] derive the early-time <photon> count, its vanishing limit, and the failure of <thermal equilibrium> when the count is of order one. Two details need supplementing.

First, their constant $N_{\gamma0}$ is the normalization obtained by extrapolating the early-time <particle horizon> formula to $z=0$. It is not the count within today's actual <particle horizon>. Let $N_0=N_\gamma(t_0)$ and let $r_{h0}$ be that horizon's physical radius. Using the paper's early-time approximation gives
$$
\boxed{1+z_*\simeq N_0^{1/3}\frac{c}{H_0r_{h0}}\sqrt{1+z_{\rm eq}},\qquad N_\gamma(z_*)=1.}
$$
For a present matter-dominated approximation, $r_{h0}\simeq2c/H_0$. Alternatively, retaining both components in the flat model exactly, $\Omega_R=1/(2+z_{\rm eq})$, and integrating the <particle horizon> gives
$$
r_{h0}=\frac{2c}{H_0}\frac{\sqrt{2+z_{\rm eq}}}{\sqrt{2+z_{\rm eq}}+1},\qquad
\boxed{1+z_*\simeq\frac{N_0^{1/3}}2\left(\sqrt{2+z_{\rm eq}}+1\right).}
$$
The second boxed formula uses the exact two-component normalization and the radiation-dominated early-time limit. Its large-$z_{\rm eq}$ leading term agrees with the first expression and $r_{h0}\simeq2c/H_0$.

Second, a <particle horizon> containing only order-one <photons> cannot support a smooth, locally representative radiation density: discrete-particle fluctuations are of the same order as the mean, and a single photon's direction is strongly anisotropic. Thus the fluid description assuming <spatial homogeneity> and <cosmological isotropy> is not self-consistent on that causal scale. This does not by itself disprove an ensemble-averaged cosmological symmetry; it limits the extrapolation of the smooth <thermal equilibrium> description.

===== Solution

For the mutually orthogonal direct <primitive lattice translation vectors> $\mathbf a_i$, define the <reciprocal lattice> basis by
$$
\boxed{\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf b_j=2\pi\delta_{ij},\qquad \mathbf b_i=\frac{2\pi\mathbf a_i}{|\mathbf a_i|^2}.}
$$
Thus $\mathbf G=\sum_i h_i\mathbf b_i$, with integer $h_i$, satisfies $\exp(i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf R)=1$ for every <lattice vector> $\mathbf R$.

==== Unlettered 2
{scope}

===== Solution

Write $\mathbf r=\sum_i s_i\mathbf a_i$. The <electron density> is periodic with period one in each $s_i$, so its three-dimensional <Fourier series> has integer harmonics:
$$
\rho(\mathbf r)=\sum_{h,k,l\in\mathbb Z}\rho_{hkl}e^{2\pi i(hs_1+ks_2+ls_3)}
=\boxed{\sum_{\mathbf G}\rho_{\mathbf G}e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf r}.}
$$
If $\Omega$ is a <primitive unit cell> of volume $\Omega_c$, <Fourier orthogonality> gives
$$
\rho_{\mathbf G}=\frac1{\Omega_c}\int_\Omega\rho(\mathbf r)e^{-i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf r}\,d^3r.
$$
Conversely, translation by $\mathbf a_i$ multiplies a general <plane wave> by $e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf a_i}$. Periodicity permits only $\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf a_i\in2\pi\mathbb Z$, precisely the <reciprocal lattice vectors>. For a physical bounded periodic <electron density>, the expansion exists in the usual square-integrable sense.

==== Unlettered 3
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the <scattering vector> $\mathbf q=\mathbf k_{\rm in}-\mathbf k_{\rm out}$; reversing its sign conjugates the following amplitudes without changing the intensities. In the <kinematic approximation>, the <atomic scattering factor> of atom $s$ is
$$
f_s(\mathbf q)=\int n_s(\mathbf u)e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf u}\,d^3u.
$$
Atoms at $\mathbf R+\mathbf d_s$ therefore give the <scattering amplitude>
$$
\boxed{A(\mathbf q)=A_e(\mathbf q)\underbrace{\sum_s f_s(\mathbf q)e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf d_s}}_{F(\mathbf q)}\underbrace{\sum_{\mathbf R}e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf R}}_{S_L(\mathbf q)}.}
$$
Here $A_e$ is the single-electron amplitude; $F$ is the <unit cell> <structure factor>, and $S_L$ is the geometrical lattice sum. If the crystal has $N_i$ repeats along $\mathbf a_i$, the latter factor is a product of <geometric series>, whose magnitudes contain
$$
\left|\frac{\sin(N_i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf a_i/2)}{\sin(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf a_i/2)}\right|.
$$
The sharp constructive peaks occur when every $\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf a_i=2\pi h_i$. Thus **a permitted reflection has wavevector change equal to a <reciprocal lattice vector>**, with $F(\mathbf G)\ne0$ and the additional elastic condition $|\mathbf k_{\rm out}|=|\mathbf k_{\rm in}|$. A finite crystal broadens these peaks; it does not impose an exact delta-function selection on every scattered photon.

==== Unlettered 4
{scope}

===== Solution

In the conventional cubic <unit cell>, the four <face-centered cubic lattice> positions contribute
$$
S_{\rm FCC}=1+e^{i\pi(k+l)}+e^{i\pi(h+l)}+e^{i\pi(h+k)}.
$$
The two-atom <atomic basis of a crystal> gives the <zinc blende structure factor>
$$
\boxed{F_{hkl}=S_{\rm FCC}\left[f_{\rm Ga}+f_{\rm As}e^{i\pi(h+k+l)/2}\right].}
$$
Mixed-parity <Miller indices> have $S_{\rm FCC}=0$. With all indices even or all odd, $S_{\rm FCC}=4$. For real <atomic scattering factors>,
$$
|F_{hkl}|^2=16\begin{cases}
f_{\rm Ga}^2+f_{\rm As}^2,&h,k,l\text{ all odd},\\
(f_{\rm Ga}+f_{\rm As})^2,&h,k,l\text{ all even},\ h+k+l=0\pmod4,\\
(f_{\rm Ga}-f_{\rm As})^2,&h,k,l\text{ all even},\ h+k+l=2\pmod4.
\end{cases}
$$
Hence **all same-parity reflections are generally permitted in GaAs**, although the last class is weak when the two <atomic scattering factors> nearly coincide. These are the structural selection rules; a particular experiment must also satisfy the <elastic Bragg scattering condition>.

==== Unlettered 5
{scope}

===== Solution

In the electron-count estimate $f_{\rm Ga}\simeq31$, $f_{\rm As}\simeq33$, the <structure factors> are $F_{111}=4(31-33i)$ and $F_{200}=4(31-33)$. Thus a single-directed-reflection estimate is
$$
\frac{|F_{111}|^2}{|F_{200}|^2}=\frac{31^2+33^2}{(31-33)^2}=512.5.
$$
A <powder X-ray diffraction> peak collects all equivalent reflections. The <powder diffraction multiplicities> are eight for $\{111\}$ and six for $\{200\}$, so the multiplicity-and-electron-count estimate is
$$
\boxed{\frac{I_{\{111\}}}{I_{\{200\}}}\simeq\frac86\frac{31^2+33^2}{(31-33)^2}=6.83\times10^2.}
$$
At $(200)$ the two sublattice amplitudes are opposite in phase and nearly cancel; at $(111)$ their relative phase is a quarter cycle, so this cancellation does not occur. In <silicon>, the two <atomic scattering factors> are identical and the <diamond cubic structure> has **an exactly forbidden $(200)$ reflection**, while $(111)$ remains allowed in the <kinematic approximation>.

The numerical ratio neglects the angular dependence of the <atomic scattering factors>, the <Lorentz-polarization factor> and thermal-motion corrections. No wavelength or measurement geometry is supplied to refine those factors. This is therefore a stated structural estimate, rather than a unique precision ratio of measured peak areas.

{scope}

==== Unlettered 1
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $a$ be a stable equilibrium separation: $V'(a)=0$, $K=V''(a)>0$. With bond extension $\delta=x-a$, the <Taylor expansion> is
$$
V(a+\delta)=V(a)+\tfrac12K\delta^2+\tfrac16V'''(a)\delta^3+\cdots.
$$
The <harmonic approximation> applies when the typical thermal and zero-point bond fluctuations satisfy $|\delta|\ll a$ and the higher terms are negligible, for example $|V'''(a)\delta|\ll3K$ and $|V''''(a)\delta^2|\ll12K$. The relevant small quantity is the relative displacement of neighbouring atoms. **Small bond excursions about a stable minimum give an amplitude-independent <spring constant> $K=V''(a)$.** Large thermal excursions, bond breaking or appreciable <anharmonicity> invalidate the approximation.

==== Unlettered 2
{scope}

===== Solution

With atomic displacement $u_j$, the harmonic potential energy is $\tfrac12K\sum_j(u_{j+1}-u_j)^2$. <Newton's second law> gives
$$
m\ddot u_j=K(u_{j+1}+u_{j-1}-2u_j).
$$
For a periodic chain of $N$ atoms, its <normal modes> are $u_j=Ue^{i(kja-\omega t)}$, with $k=2\pi s/(Na)$ and one representative per <Brillouin zone>. Substitution yields the <monatomic-chain dispersion relation>
$$
-m\omega^2=K(e^{ika}+e^{-ika}-2),\qquad
\boxed{\omega(k)=2\sqrt{\frac Km}\left|\sin\frac{ka}{2}\right|.}
$$
A real general displacement is a superposition of these <normal modes> and their complex conjugates. The $k=0$ mode is a rigid translation. At small $|k|$, the <acoustic phonon branch> is linear, with <speed of sound>
$$
\boxed{v=\lim_{k\to0^+}\frac{d\omega}{dk}=a\sqrt{\frac Km}.}
$$
For $0<k<\pi/a$, the <group velocity> is $a\sqrt{K/m}\cos(ka/2)$ and vanishes at the zone boundary. The dashed initial tangent in the sketch has slope $v$.

==== Unlettered 3
{scope}

===== Solution

For the <Lennard-Jones potential>,
$$
V'(x)=\frac{24\epsilon}{x}\left[\left(\frac\sigma x\right)^6-2\left(\frac\sigma x\right)^{12}\right].
$$
Its minimum and harmonic <spring constant> are
$$
a=2^{1/6}\sigma,\qquad
K=4\epsilon\left(\frac{156\sigma^{12}}{a^{14}}-\frac{42\sigma^6}{a^8}\right)=\frac{72\epsilon}{a^2}.
$$
Using the <monatomic-chain dispersion relation>,
$$
\boxed{v=\sqrt{\frac{72\epsilon}{m}},\qquad
\omega_{\max}=2\sqrt{\frac Km}=\frac2{2^{1/6}\sigma}\sqrt{\frac{72\epsilon}{m}},\qquad f_{\max}=\frac{\omega_{\max}}{2\pi}.}
$$
The maximum is at $|k|=\pi/a$. Taking the <argon> atomic mass as $m\simeq39.95\,\mathrm u=6.63\times10^{-26}\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $\epsilon=1.602\times10^{-21}\,\mathrm J$ gives
$$
a\simeq0.382\,\mathrm{nm},\qquad K\simeq0.792\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}},
$$
$$
\boxed{v\simeq1.32\times10^3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},\qquad\omega_{\max}\simeq6.91\times10^{12}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}},\qquad f_{\max}\simeq1.10\,\mathrm{THz}.}
$$
These are the stipulated one-dimensional nearest-neighbour model estimates; a three-dimensional <argon> crystal has additional neighbours and <phonon polarizations>.

==== Unlettered 1
{scope}

===== Solution

Normalize the <plane waves> in a periodic crystal of area or volume $\mathcal V$. The <Fourier coefficients> of the <periodic potential> obey
$$
\langle\mathbf k|V|\mathbf k'\rangle=\frac1{\mathcal V}\int_{\mathcal V}V(\mathbf r)e^{-i(\mathbf k-\mathbf k')\cdot\mathbf r}\,d\mathbf r=V_{\mathbf k-\mathbf k'}.
$$
Translation of the integral by any <lattice vector> $\mathbf R$ leaves it invariant but multiplies it by $e^{-i(\mathbf k-\mathbf k')\cdot\mathbf R}$. A nonzero result therefore requires $\mathbf k-\mathbf k'$ to be a <reciprocal lattice vector>.

Near an isolated <Bragg scattering> degeneracy, ordinary nondegenerate <perturbation theory> fails because the free energies coincide. Retain the resonant <plane waves> $|\mathbf k\rangle$, $|\mathbf k+\mathbf G\rangle$ and apply <degenerate perturbation theory>. Absorb the constant $V_{\mathbf0}$ into the energy origin. Since a real potential has $V_{-\mathbf G}=V_{\mathbf G}^*$, the projected <Schrödinger equation> has coefficient matrix
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
\epsilon_0(\mathbf k)-E&V_{-\mathbf G}\\
V_{\mathbf G}&\epsilon_0(\mathbf k+\mathbf G)-E
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
A nonzero eigenstate requires
$$
\boxed{[\epsilon_0(\mathbf k)-E][\epsilon_0(\mathbf k+\mathbf G)-E]-|V_{\mathbf G}|^2=0.}
$$
Off-resonant <plane waves> give higher-order energy shifts omitted in this leading <nearly-free electron model>.

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $u=V_{10}<0$ and
$$
E_X=\frac{\hbar^2\pi^2}{2m_ea^2}.
$$
At $X=(\pi/a,0)$ the two degenerate <plane waves> have momenta $(\pi/a,0)$ and $(-\pi/a,0)$ and coupling $u$. Thus
$$
\begin{vmatrix}E_X-E&u\\u&E_X-E\end{vmatrix}=0,\qquad
\boxed{E_{X,1}=E_X+u,\quad E_{X,2}=E_X-u.}
$$
Their symmetric and antisymmetric combinations are the lower and upper states respectively. The local <band gap> is $2|u|$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Put $w=V_{11}<0$. At $M=(\pi/a,\pi/a)$, the free energy is $2E_X$. The four cyclic combinations in the supplied <secular equation> give the <four-plane-wave splitting at a square-zone corner>
$$
\boxed{E_{M,1}=2E_X+w+2u,\qquad E_{M,2}=E_{M,3}=2E_X-w,\qquad E_{M,4}=2E_X+w-2u.}
$$
For example, the normalized coefficient vectors in the source's cyclic basis are $(1,1,1,1)/2$, $(1,0,-1,0)/\sqrt2$, $(0,1,0,-1)/\sqrt2$, and $(1,-1,1,-1)/2$. Because $u<w<0$, these are ordered as written: a lower singlet, a central doublet and an upper singlet. The sketch puts the $X$ and $M$ energies on one common scale; its representative negative couplings are stated in the caption.

==== Unlettered 2
{scope}

===== Solution

There are two <electrons> per <primitive unit cell>; a spin-degenerate <energy band> accommodates exactly that number. A <semiconductor> results when the first band is full and does not overlap the second. In the leading two-wave/four-wave approximation, compare the first-band maximum at $M$ with the second-band minimum at $X$:
$$
\Delta_{\rm ind}=E_{X,2}-E_{M,1}
=(E_X-u)-(2E_X+w+2u)=-E_X-w-3u.
$$
This is an <indirect band gap>; a nonzero local splitting at $X$ alone is insufficient. At the onset,
$$
\boxed{V_{10,\rm crit}=-\frac{E_X+V_{11}}3.}
$$
With $a=0.3\,\mathrm{nm}$, $\hbar^2/(2m_e)=0.03810\,\mathrm{eV\,nm^2}$ gives $E_X\simeq4.178\,\mathrm{eV}$, hence
$$
\boxed{V_{10,\rm crit}\simeq-1.326\,\mathrm{eV}.}
$$
For $V_{10}$ more negative than this value the estimated <indirect band gap> is positive; at equality the bands just touch. This is the onset estimate within the source's <nearly-free electron model> truncation, not an exact critical potential after coupling all <plane waves>.

===== Solution

The <Brillouin function> is odd, increases monotonically through zero, and approaches $\pm1$ for $x\to\pm\infty$. Its small-$x$ slope is $(J+1)/(3J)$. For $x\gg1$, the magnetic ions overwhelmingly occupy the largest field-aligned moment projection, so the <magnetization> reaches <magnetic saturation>:
$$
\boxed{M\longrightarrow M_s=ng\mu_BJ.}
$$
The sketch shows several $J$ values; all have the same limiting values, while their slopes differ.

\Image[2011-brillouin-functions.png]
{title=Brillouin functions approaching magnetic saturation}

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The $1/x$ terms in the <Brillouin function> cancel. Keeping the linear terms gives
$$
B_J(x)=\frac{(2J+1)^2-1}{12J^2}x+O(x^3)=\frac{J+1}{3J}x+O(x^3).
$$
Consequently,
$$
M\simeq\frac{n(g\mu_B)^2J(J+1)}{3k_BT}B.
$$
Use the dimensionless SI <magnetic susceptibility> $\chi=\partial M/\partial H_{\rm applied}$ and $B_{\rm applied}=\mu_0H_{\rm applied}$, as in the source's molecular-field convention. The <Curie law> is
$$
\boxed{\chi=\frac CT,\qquad C=\frac{\mu_0n(g\mu_B)^2J(J+1)}{3k_B}.}
$$
If susceptibility is instead defined as $\partial M/\partial B$, its coefficient is $C/\mu_0$ and is dimensionful. The SI convention above makes the subsequent <Curie-Weiss law> and $\lambda$ consistent.

==== Unlettered 1
{scope}

===== Solution

For positive $\lambda$, a fluctuation of <magnetization> produces a <Weiss molecular field> in the same direction, reinforcing alignment. The <mean-field self-consistency equation> is
$$
M=M_sB_J\!\left[\frac{g\mu_BJ\mu_0}{k_BT}(H_{\rm applied}+\lambda M)\right].
$$
Linearizing in the paramagnetic phase gives
$$
M=\frac CT(H_{\rm applied}+\lambda M),\qquad
\boxed{\chi=\frac C{T-\lambda C}=\frac C{T-T_C},\qquad T_C=\lambda C=\frac{\mu_0\lambda n(g\mu_B)^2J(J+1)}{3k_B}.}
$$
At zero applied field, the right-hand side's slope at $M=0$ is $T_C/T$. Below $T_C$ this exceeds one, and the saturation of the <Brillouin function> allows stable nonzero solutions of opposite sign: spontaneous <ferromagnetism>. Above $T_C$, only the zero-field solution $M=0$ remains stable. The stated linear susceptibility is the paramagnetic branch for $T>T_C$; it must not be continued as a negative susceptibility below the transition.

==== Unlettered 2
{scope}

===== Solution

Using the supplied <iron> parameters gives
$$
C=\frac{\mu_0(8.5\times10^{28})(2\mu_B)^2(1)(2)}{3k_B}\simeq1.77\,\mathrm K,\qquad
\boxed{\lambda=\frac{1043}{C}\simeq5.88\times10^2.}
$$
An internal <Weiss molecular field> depends on <temperature> through $M(T)$. Its low-temperature saturation estimate uses $M_s=ng\mu_BJ\simeq1.58\times10^6\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}$:
$$
\boxed{B_{e,\rm sat}=\mu_0\lambda M_s=\frac{3k_BT_C}{g\mu_B(J+1)}\simeq1.16\times10^3\,\mathrm T.}
$$
At and above $T_C$ the zero-applied-field <magnetization> vanishes in this model, so this is a saturation estimate, not the field at $T_C$.

Use the paper's stipulated <face-centered cubic lattice>, which has twelve nearest neighbours. The saturated one-ion alignment scale is
$$
(g\mu_BJ)B_{e,\rm sat}=\frac{3J}{J+1}k_BT_C\simeq0.135\,\mathrm{eV}.
$$
Dividing the effective field among twelve equivalent neighbours gives the per-bond <exchange interaction> scale
$$
\boxed{E_{\rm bond}\simeq\frac{g\mu_BJ B_{e,\rm sat}}{12}\simeq1.12\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm{eV}=11.2\,\mathrm{meV}.}
$$
To specify the convention, write the <Heisenberg model> as $\mathcal H=-\mathcal J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\mathbf J_i\cdot\mathbf J_j$, with dimensionless angular-momentum operators and each bond counted once. Then $g\mu_BB_e=12\mathcal J\langle J_z\rangle$, so $\mathcal J=3k_BT_C/[12J(J+1)]\simeq11.2\,\mathrm{meV}$ here. A convention with $-2\mathcal J'$ instead has $\mathcal J'=5.62\,\mathrm{meV}$. The total saturated interaction energy per ion is $-12\mathcal J J^2/2\simeq-67.4\,\mathrm{meV}$; the factor of one half avoids counting each bond twice.
