= Energy
{wiki}

Energy is a conserved scalar quantity associated with time-translation symmetry.

= Chemical energy
{parent=Energy}

<Energy> accounted for through a system's chemical composition and state. A <galvanic cell> can convert part of the change associated with a <redox> into electrical <work>; the system boundaries determine which thermal losses also enter the balance.

= Calorie
{parent=Energy}

An <energy> unit with several historical definitions. The thermochemical small <calorie> is exactly $4.184$ <joules>; a kilocalorie is one thousand such <calorie> units. A calorimetric calculation must specify its unit convention.

= Nuclear power
{parent=Energy}
{wiki}

Useful power generated from nuclear energy release, usually controlled <nuclear fission> in a reactor supplying heat to a power cycle. Operational carbon emissions, full fuel-cycle impacts, accident risks, waste and decommissioning are distinct assessment dimensions. Replacing existing output requires comparison of both annual energy and dependable capacity.

= Renewable energy
{parent=Energy}
{wiki}

Energy obtained from naturally replenished flows or stocks on a human timescale. Replenishment does not by itself prove low life-cycle emissions or negligible environmental impact. Variability, geographic availability and conversion equipment matter when a source is integrated into an energy system.

= Wave power
{parent=Renewable energy}
{wiki}

Useful power extracted from surface-water waves. Resource variability, extreme-storm survivability, offshore access, moorings and electrical connections affect practical performance. Its potential and costs are distinct from those of <tidal power>, even when both use marine locations.

= Tidal power
{parent=Renewable energy}
{wiki}

Power extracted from tidal water-level differences or tidal currents. Astronomical timing is predictable, but output is not constant. Basin geometry, flow changes caused by extraction, environmental effects and connection infrastructure constrain practical resources; a local undisturbed-flow power density is not a whole-channel extractable-energy estimate.

= Solar power
{parent=Renewable energy}
{wiki}

Conversion of sunlight into useful power through photovoltaic or solar-thermal systems. A first areal estimate is annual mean irradiance times conversion efficiency and system-performance factors. Storage, transmission, land use, night-time operation and seasonal balance require separate accounting.

= Wind power
{parent=Renewable energy}
{wiki}

Conversion of atmospheric kinetic energy into useful power, commonly by turbines driving generators. Annual energy equals nameplate capacity times <capacity factor> times elapsed time, while the contribution to reliable peak supply is a separate system property. Weather correlation, transmission, flexibility and curtailment affect large-scale integration.

= Bioenergy
{parent=Renewable energy}
{wiki}

= Biomass energy
{synonym}

Energy recovered from biologically produced material, for example by combustion or conversion into fuels. Sustainable regrowth can recycle biogenic carbon, but land-use change, cultivation, processing, transport and regrowth time belong in its <life-cycle assessment>. Harvested energy is incident solar energy times area, elapsed time and annual conversion efficiency; dry and wet mass yields require different heating-value conventions.

= Energy recovery
{parent=Energy}

Returning <energy> from a used flow or beam to an upstream supply or a useful reservoir. An <electron> beam decelerated before collection returns much of its <acceleration> <energy>; the net input then covers unrecovered <energy> and auxiliary losses.

= Energy storage
{parent=Energy}

Storing recoverable <energy> for later use. <Energy> capacity, maximum <power>, duration, <round-trip efficiency> and degradation are independent dimensions of performance; a high specific <energy> need not imply high <power> or efficiency.

= Round-trip efficiency
{parent=Energy storage}
{title2=$\eta_{\rm rt}=E_{\rm out}/E_{\rm in}$}

The ratio of useful <energy> recovered on discharge to the <energy> supplied during charging for a complete storage cycle. Generation efficiency alone excludes the preceding charging losses.

= Hydrogen storage
{parent=Energy storage}

Storage of <hydrogen> as a chemical-energy carrier. Comparing electricity-storage systems requires including production, containment and reconversion; the fuel heating value alone does not specify electrical <round-trip efficiency>.

= Pumped-storage hydroelectricity
{parent=Energy storage}
{title2=$E=\eta\rho Vgh$}

= Pumped storage
{synonym}

Water is pumped to an upper reservoir and later discharged through a turbine. For approximately constant head, useful discharge <energy> is $\eta\rho Vgh$ while <power> is $\eta\rho Qgh$; reservoir <volume> and flow rating set different properties.

= Primary energy
{parent=Energy}

<Energy> measured at the resource or initial-input stage before downstream conversion to final useful <energy>. Comparisons with electrical output must state the accounting convention and avoid treating a primary-energy thermal input as the same service as delivered electricity.

= Kilocalorie
{parent=Energy}
{title2=$1\,\mathrm{kcal}=4184\,\mathrm J$}

A unit of <energy> equal to one thousand thermochemical calories, or $4184\,\mathrm J$. In dietary usage the capitalized Calorie commonly denotes a kilocalorie.

= Energy harvesting
{parent=Energy}

Collection of ambient light, heat flow, thermal cycling, motion or other available <energy> into a usable electrical supply. Available average power, conversion loss and peak-load buffering must be distinguished.

= Mechanical energy harvesting
{parent=Energy}

Conversion of mechanical motion or deformation into electrical <energy>.

= Piezoelectric energy harvesting
{parent=Mechanical energy harvesting}

Electrical generation from mechanically induced <electric polarization> in a piezoelectric element.

= Piezoelectric polymer
{parent=Piezoelectric energy harvesting}

A polymer exhibiting <piezoelectricity>. A compliant, low-density film can accept larger bending or strain than a brittle ceramic, while low <permittivity> can yield a large <voltage> coefficient despite a modest charge coefficient. Extractable <energy> still depends on mechanical input, <capacitance> and electrical loading.

= Piezoelectric ceramic
{parent=Piezoelectric energy harvesting}

A ceramic material used for <piezoelectricity>, often offering large charge response and electromechanical coupling with comparatively high stiffness. Brittle fracture, mechanical impedance matching and mounting conditions can limit a vibration harvester. A high charge coefficient alone is not a device <power> ranking.

= Electromagnetic energy harvesting
{parent=Mechanical energy harvesting}

Conversion of mechanical motion into electrical output through changing <magnetic flux>.

= Electrostatic energy harvesting
{parent=Mechanical energy harvesting}

Conversion using changes in <capacitance> of a charged or electrically biased structure.

= Specific energy
{parent=Energy}
{title2=$\varepsilon_{\rm sp}$}

Energy per unit mass, with units such as $\mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}$ or $\mathrm{Wh\,kg^{-1}}$. Active-material, cell and packaged-system mass bases can give very different values.

= Ragone plot
{parent=Specific energy}
{c}

A logarithmic comparison of <specific power> against <specific energy> for storage or conversion devices. With consistent <mass> and <energy> units, constant-duration lines satisfy $p_s=e_s/t$. The plotted capability depends on operating conditions and <mass> convention.

= Q value
{c}
{parent=Energy}
{title2=$Q=E_{\mathrm{rest},i}-E_{\mathrm{rest},f}$}

The <Q value> of a reaction or <particle decay> is the initial total rest <energy> minus the final total rest <energy>. Positive $Q$ releases <kinetic energy>; a negative $Q$ requires energy supplied by incident motion or another source. Atomic and bare nuclear <mass> conventions must be distinguished when counting <electrons> in <beta decay>.

= Energy function
{parent=Energy}

For a Lagrangian $L(q,\dot q,t)$, its energy function is
$$
E=\sum_i\dot q_i\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}-L.
$$
It is conserved along Euler-Lagrange trajectories when $L$ has no explicit time dependence.

= Mechanical energy
{title2=$E=T+V$}
{parent=Energy}
{wiki}

Mechanical energy is the sum of <kinetic energy> and <potential energy>. It is conserved when all forces are conservative and the potential has no explicit time dependence.

= Conservation of mechanical energy
{parent=Mechanical energy}
{title2=$T+V=\mathrm{constant}$}

For constant masses, a time-independent <potential energy> $V$ and forces $\boldsymbol F_i=-\nabla_iV+\boldsymbol R_i$ with zero total extra power $\sum_i\boldsymbol R_i\cdot\boldsymbol v_i=0$, <Newton's second law> gives $dT/dt=-dV/dt$. Thus <mechanical energy> is conserved. Smooth stationary constraint reactions and a purely magnetic <Lorentz force> supply zero power. This is more restrictive than <conservation of energy> in general, which may include thermal or other internal forms.

= Kinetic energy conservation
{parent=Conservation of mechanical energy}

Total <kinetic energy> is unchanged between the incoming and outgoing states of an <elastic collision>. This does not require each body's <kinetic energy> to be unchanged, nor that <kinetic energy> remain constant during temporary elastic deformation. In general <mechanical energy> includes <potential energy>, so conservation of <mechanical energy> alone does not imply constant <kinetic energy>.

= Work
{parent=Energy}
{title2=$W=\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r$}
{wiki=Work_(physics)}

<Work> measures the <energy> transferred by a <force> as its point of application moves. For a <point mass> following a path $C$, add the contribution of the <force> along each small <displacement>:
$$
W=\int_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r
=\int_{t_i}^{t_f}\mathbf F(t)\cdot\mathbf v(t)\,dt.
$$
This <line integral> uses the actual path, and the <dot product> selects the component of <force> along the motion. A large <force> perpendicular to every small <displacement> does zero <work>; holding something still also does zero mechanical <work> on it, even if holding it feels tiring. The <SI> unit is $\mathrm J=\mathrm{N\,m}$. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/7-1-work[OpenStax University Physics 1, §7.1: Work]{ref}

The usefulness of <work> is that it connects a description in terms of <forces> and distances to one in terms of <energy>. The <work-energy theorem> gives the change in <kinetic energy> from the total net <work>, often without solving for the entire motion. The <work> of one selected <force> equals that change only when the other <forces> contribute zero total <work>.

= Pressure-volume work
{parent=Work}
{title2=$\delta W=P\,dV$}

The <work> done by a uniform <pressure> $P$ on an expanding boundary is $P\,dV$. With <internal energy> $U$, the <first law of thermodynamics> for an adiabatically expanding region reads $dU=-P\,dV$. For a homogeneous cosmological <perfect fluid>, $U=\rho V$ and $V\propto a^3$ give the <cosmological continuity equation> $\dot\rho+3H(\rho+P)=0$.

= Vacuum-window implosion energy estimate
{parent=Pressure-volume work}
{title2=$a_0\simeq\Delta p A/M,\quad v_{\mathrm{rms}}\lesssim\sqrt{2p_0V/M}$}

When a <vacuum> vessel's window shatters, a nearly atmospheric initial pressure difference gives inward pressure <force> $\Delta p A$ on frontal <area> $A$. Dividing by the glass <mass> $M$ estimates its initial collective <acceleration>. An ideal atmospheric pressure-work budget $p_0V$ for vessel <volume> $V$ would give a collective <kinetic energy> estimate $Mv_{\mathrm{rms}}^2/2\lesssim p_0V$ if assigned entirely to the fragments. This is a model-dependent energy budget, not a universal bound on every fragment's <speed>: air motion, fragmentation, pressure equalization and wall impacts alter the transfer, and unequal sharing can accelerate a small fragment faster than the collective estimate.

= Work done by a constant force
{parent=Work}
{title2=$W=Fd\cos\theta$}

For a fixed <force> $\mathbf F$ and endpoint <displacement> $\Delta\mathbf r$, the <line integral> reduces to a <dot product>:
$$
W=\mathbf F\cdot\Delta\mathbf r=Fd\cos\theta,
\qquad F=|\mathbf F|,\quad d=|\Delta\mathbf r|.
$$
Here $\theta$ is the <angle> between the <force> and the <displacement>. For straight motion, $d$ is also the distance traveled. A $3\,\mathrm N$ <force> acting along a $4\,\mathrm m$ <displacement> does $12\,\mathrm J$ of <work>. Turning the same <force> perpendicular to the <displacement> gives $\cos(\pi/2)=0$ and zero <work>; reversing it gives $\cos\pi=-1$ and $-12\,\mathrm J$. At $\theta=\pi/3$, the <work> is $6\,\mathrm J$. These signs describe <energy> transferred to or from the moving body. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/7-1-work[OpenStax University Physics 1, §7.1: Constant-force work]{ref}

= Work-energy theorem
{parent=Work}
{title2=$W_{\mathrm{net}}=\Delta T$}

For a <point mass> of constant <mass> $m$ observed in an <inertial frame>, the net <work> along its actual path equals its change in <kinetic energy>:
$$
W_{\mathrm{net}}=\int_{t_a}^{t_b}\mathbf F_{\mathrm{net}}(t)\cdot\mathbf v(t)\,dt
=\int_a^b\mathbf F_{\mathrm{net}}\cdot d\mathbf r
=\frac12m|\mathbf v_b|^2-\frac12m|\mathbf v_a|^2.
$$
The middle expression is a <line integral>; the <dot product> selects the component of <force> along the <displacement>. Positive net <work> increases <speed>, negative net <work> decreases it, and zero net <work> leaves the endpoint <speeds> equal. Equal endpoint <speeds> do not imply equal <velocities>: a perpendicular <force> can continually change direction while doing zero <work>. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter13.pdf[MIT mechanics, Chapter 13, section 13.11]{ref}

The bridge to <Newton's second law> is short: take the <dot product> of $m\dot{\mathbf v}=\mathbf F_{\mathrm{net}}$ with <velocity>. The <chain rule> gives $d(m|\mathbf v|^2/2)/dt=\mathbf F_{\mathrm{net}}\cdot\mathbf v$, equating the rate of change of <kinetic energy> to instantaneous <power>. Integrating gives the theorem. No <conservative force> assumption entered this argument; <friction> also contributes to the net <work>.

For the <harmonic oscillator> with <spring> stiffness $k=2\,\mathrm{N/m}$, release a <mass> $m=0.5\,\mathrm{kg}$ from rest at $x=0.3\,\mathrm m$. The <spring> exerts $F=-kx$. Its <work> before the first passage through $x=0$ is
$$
W=\int_{0.3\,\mathrm m}^{0}(-kx)\,dx
=\frac12k(0.3\,\mathrm m)^2=0.09\,\mathrm J.
$$
Thus $mv^2/2=0.09\,\mathrm J$ gives <speed> $0.6\,\mathrm{m/s}$ there. The <velocity> is negative on this passage because the <position> is decreasing. The <energy> calculation alone determines its magnitude, so the direction still requires information about the motion.

For a time-independent <potential energy> $V$, the <conservative force> contributes <work> $-\Delta V$. Separating all other <work> as $W_{\mathrm{other}}$ gives $\Delta(T+V)=W_{\mathrm{other}}$. Consequently <conservation of mechanical energy> requires zero net other <work>; the <work-energy theorem> itself does not require that conservation. With <damping>, <mechanical energy> decreases even though the theorem remains valid. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-01sc-classical-mechanics-fall-2016/mit8_01scs22_chapter14.pdf[MIT mechanics, Chapter 14: mechanical energy and nonconservative work]{ref}

= Power
{parent=Work}
{title2=$P=dW/dt$}
{wiki=Power_(physics)}

<Power> describes how quickly <energy> is transferred. For mechanical <work>, the instantaneous <power> supplied by a <force> is
$$
P(t)=\frac{dW}{dt}=\mathbf F(t)\cdot\mathbf v(t),
\qquad W=\int_{t_i}^{t_f}P(t)\,dt.
$$
The <SI> unit is $\mathrm W=\mathrm{J/s}=\mathrm{N\,m/s}$, distinct from the <velocity> unit $\mathrm{m/s}$. The italic symbol $W$ for <work> and the upright unit $\mathrm W$ for <power> mean different things. Two processes can transfer the same <energy> while requiring different <powers>: doing $12\,\mathrm J$ of <work> in $2\,\mathrm s$ requires an average $6\,\mathrm W$, whereas doing it in $4\,\mathrm s$ requires an average $3\,\mathrm W$. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/7-4-power[OpenStax University Physics 1, §7.4: Power]{ref}

= Electric power
{parent=Power}
{title2=$P=VI$}

The instantaneous rate of <energy> transfer through a circuit port is $P(t)=V(t)I(t)$, with positive <electric current> entering the positive <voltage> terminal for the passive sign convention. Peak <phasors> in sinusoidal steady state give $\langle P\rangle=\tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(\widetilde V\widetilde I^*)$. The factor $1/2$ is absent for <root mean square> <phasors>.

= Specific power
{parent=Power}
{title2=$P/m$}

Power output or input divided by the relevant mass, commonly expressed in watts per kilogram. Device-level and active-material values cannot be compared without stating their different mass bases.

= Peak power
{parent=Power}
{title2=$P_{\rm pk}$}

The maximum instantaneous <power> in a specified pulse.

= Instantaneous power
{parent=Power}
{title2=$P(t)=\mathbf F(t)\cdot\mathbf v(t)$}

<Instantaneous power> is <power> at a particular moment. Consider a <point mass> with <mass> $m=2\,\mathrm{kg}$, initially at rest, acted on by a constant net <force> $F=3\,\mathrm N$ along a straight path. <Newton's second law> gives constant <acceleration> $a=F/m=1.5\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, hence <velocity> $v(t)=at$ and
$$
P(t)=Fat=(4.5\,\mathrm{W/s})t.
$$
The <instantaneous power> starts at zero even though the <force> is nonzero, because the initial <velocity> is zero. At distance $4\,\mathrm m$, the <kinetic energy> example below gives <speed> $\sqrt{12}\,\mathrm{m/s}$, so the final <instantaneous power> is $3\sqrt{12}\,\mathrm W\approx10.3923\,\mathrm W$. Negative <instantaneous power> means the selected <force> is removing <energy> at that moment.

= Average power
{parent=Power}
{title2=$\overline P=W/(t_f-t_i)$}

<Average power> divides the <work> over a nonzero interval by the elapsed <time>. For the same $2\,\mathrm{kg}$ <point mass> driven from rest through $4\,\mathrm m$ by a net $3\,\mathrm N$ <force>, the <displacement> is $x(t)=at^2/2$. Thus
$$
t_f=\sqrt{\frac{2(4\,\mathrm m)}{1.5\,\mathrm{m/s^2}}}
=\sqrt{16/3}\,\mathrm s\approx2.309401\,\mathrm s,
\qquad \overline P=\frac{12\,\mathrm J}{t_f}
\approx5.19615\,\mathrm W.
$$
This <average power> is half the final <instantaneous power> because $P(t)$ is a <linear function> of <time>, starting at zero. Its <graph of a function> bounds a <triangle> of area $P(t_f)t_f/2=12\,\mathrm J$; the horizontal line at <average power> encloses a <rectangle> with the same area. In general, an endpoint value of <instantaneous power> does not determine <average power>. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/7-4-power[OpenStax University Physics 1, §7.4: Average and instantaneous power]{ref}

= Kinetic energy
{parent=Energy}
{title2=$K=\frac12mv^2$}
{wiki}

<Kinetic energy> is the <energy> associated with motion. For a nonrelativistic <point mass> of <mass> $m>0$, measured in a specified <inertial frame>,
$$
K=\frac12m\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v=\frac12mv^2,
\qquad v=|\mathbf v|.
$$
Here $\mathbf v$ is <velocity> and $v$ is <speed>; $T$ is another common symbol for <kinetic energy>. The <dot product> makes $K$ nonnegative regardless of the direction of <velocity>. At fixed <mass>, doubling <speed> quadruples <kinetic energy>: a $2\,\mathrm{kg}$ <point mass> at $2\,\mathrm{m/s}$ has $4\,\mathrm J$, whereas at $4\,\mathrm{m/s}$ it has $16\,\mathrm J$. The <SI> units are $\mathrm{kg\,m^2/s^2}=\mathrm J$. Different <reference frames> can assign different <velocities> and therefore different <kinetic energies>. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/7-2-kinetic-energy[OpenStax University Physics 1, §7.2: Kinetic energy]{ref}

A short outline explains why <kinetic energy> has this formula. In a nonrelativistic <inertial frame>, constant <mass> and <Newton's second law> give, using the <chain rule>,
$$
\frac{dK}{dt}=m\mathbf v\cdot\frac{d\mathbf v}{dt}
=\mathbf F_{\mathrm{net}}\cdot\mathbf v.
$$
Integrating over <time> proves the <work-energy theorem>, $W_{\mathrm{net}}=K_f-K_i$. The net <force> is the sum of all <forces>, and $W_{\mathrm{net}}$ is their total <work>, not merely the <work> of a chosen driving <force>. This route to final <speed> is useful when the <forces> as <functions> of <position> are known but the full motion as a function of <time> would be harder to find. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/7-3-work-energy-theorem[OpenStax University Physics 1, §7.3: Work–energy theorem]{ref}

In the running example, a $2\,\mathrm{kg}$ <point mass> starts at rest and receives a constant net $3\,\mathrm N$ <force> through $4\,\mathrm m$. Its net <work> is $12\,\mathrm J$, so
$$
K_f=12\,\mathrm J,
\qquad v_f=\sqrt{\frac{2K_f}{m}}
=\sqrt{12}\,\mathrm{m/s}\approx3.4641\,\mathrm{m/s}.
$$
At any intermediate distance $x\ge0$, $K(x)=(3\,\mathrm N)x$ and $v(x)=\sqrt{2Fx/m}$: <kinetic energy> is a <linear function> of distance, while <speed> is proportional to the <square root> of distance. If a net opposing $3\,\mathrm N$ braking <force> subsequently acts for $4\,\mathrm m$, it does $-12\,\mathrm J$ of <work> and brings this <point mass> to rest. Negative net <work> decreases <kinetic energy>; it does not make <kinetic energy> negative. Beyond the stopping point the motion must be reconsidered, since the <point mass> cannot keep moving forward with a negative $K$.

<Kinetic energy> also makes the exchange with <potential energy> intuitive: a falling body's loss of <gravitational potential energy> can appear as increased <kinetic energy>. The formula above applies when <speed> is small compared with the <speed of light>; at higher <speeds>, <special relativity> changes the expression. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_04.html[Feynman Lectures I, chapter 4, §§4–2 and 4–3: Energy of position and motion]{ref}

= Specific kinetic energy
{parent=Kinetic energy}
{title2=$K/m=|\mathbf v|^2/2$}

<Kinetic energy> per unit <mass>. For an acceleration field $\mathbf a$, its change along an actual trajectory is $\Delta(|\mathbf v|^2/2)=\int\mathbf a\cdot d\mathbf r$, by the <chain rule>. This differs by a mass factor from the work integral of a physical force.

= Kinetic energy correction factor
{parent=Kinetic energy}
{title2=$\alpha=\frac{\int_Au^3\,dA}{A\overline u^3}$}

For unidirectional flow through area $A$ with mean speed $\overline u$, the outgoing kinetic-energy flux is $\tfrac12\rho A\alpha\overline u^3$, where $\alpha=(A\overline u^3)^{-1}\int_Au^3dA$. Cubing the mean speed generally differs from averaging the cube. A uniform profile has $\alpha=1$; circular laminar <Hagen-Poiseuille flow> has $\alpha=2$.

= Relativistic kinetic energy
{parent=Kinetic energy}
{title2=$K=(\gamma-1)mc^2$}

For a free particle of <rest mass> $m$, subtract its <rest energy> $mc^2$ from its total <energy> $\gamma mc^2$, where $\gamma$ is the <Lorentz factor>. The low-<speed> <binomial series> gives $K=mv^2/2+3mv^4/(8c^2)+O(mv^6/c^4)$, recovering classical <kinetic energy>.

= Rigid rotation minimizes kinetic energy at fixed angular momentum
{parent=Kinetic energy}
{title2=$K\geq L^2/(2I)$}

For rotational motion about a fixed axis, let $r$ be cylindrical radius, $v$ the signed tangential <velocity>, and $dm$ the mass measure. Define the <kinetic energy>, axial <angular momentum>, and <moment of inertia> by
$$
K=\frac12\int v^2\,dm,\qquad L=\int rv\,dm,\qquad I=\int r^2\,dm>0.
$$
The <Cauchy-Schwarz inequality> gives $L^2\leq(\int v^2dm)(\int r^2dm)=2KI$. More precisely, direct expansion gives the nonnegative energy gap
$$
K-\frac{L^2}{2I}=\frac12\int\left(v-\frac LI r\right)^2dm.
$$
Thus the minimum at fixed $L$ and fixed mass distribution occurs exactly when $v=(L/I)r$ almost everywhere with respect to $dm$. This means constant <angular velocity> $\Omega=L/I$, or <solid-body rotation>. If the profile is continuous, the equality holds at every point in the mass support.

For a uniform unit-density <cylinder> of radius $a$, height $h$, and <volume> $V=\pi a^2h$, the <moment of inertia> is $I=Va^2/2$. Therefore $K\geq L^2/(Va^2)$, with equality for $\omega(r)=2L/(Va^2)$. Multiplying by a uniform nonunit <density> changes the mass measure and $I$ consistently.

= Kinetic energy decomposition about the center of mass
{parent=Kinetic energy}
{title2=$T=\frac12M|\dot R|^2+\frac12\sum_i m_i|\dot\rho_i|^2$}

= König theorem for kinetic energy
{c}
{synonym}

Writing particle positions relative to the <center of mass> as $\rho_i=r_i-R$ makes $\sum_i m_i\rho_i=0$, so the cross term in the squared <velocities> vanishes. The total <kinetic energy> splits into center-of-mass translation and relative motion as displayed. For a <rigid body>, the latter is $\tfrac12 I\omega^2$, where $I$ is the <moment of inertia> about the instantaneous rotation axis through the <center of mass>.

= Unit-direction kinetic-energy identity
{parent=Kinetic energy}
{title2=$v^2=(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)^2-r^2\mathbf u\cdot\ddot{\mathbf u}$}

For a twice differentiable position away from the origin, write $\mathbf r=r\mathbf u$ with radial <unit vector> $\mathbf u$. Its unit-length constraint gives $\mathbf u\cdot\dot{\mathbf u}=0$ and $\mathbf u\cdot\ddot{\mathbf u}=-|\dot{\mathbf u}|^2$, separating radial and directional contributions to speed and <kinetic energy>. The identity is kinematic and does not require a particular force law.

= Kinetic energy density
{parent=Kinetic energy}

The kinetic energy density of a continuum with mass density $\rho$ and velocity $\mathbf v$ is $\rho|\mathbf v|^2/2$. For a displacement field, the material velocity is its time derivative.

= Rotational kinetic energy
{title2=$K=\frac12I\omega^2$}
{parent=Kinetic energy}
{wiki}

<Rotational kinetic energy> is the <kinetic energy> of a <rigid body>'s rotation. For pure rotation about an axis fixed in an <inertial frame>,
$$
K_{\mathrm{rot}}=\frac12I\omega^2,
\qquad I=\int r_\perp^2\,dm,
\qquad \omega=|\boldsymbol\omega|.
$$
Here $r_\perp$ is the perpendicular distance of a <mass> element $dm$ from that axis, $I$ is the <moment of inertia> about that same axis, and $\omega$ is the common <angular speed>, measured in <radians> per second. The <rigid body> assumption fixes the relative positions of the <mass> elements; they share <angular velocity> but have different <speeds> $v=\omega r_\perp$. Summing their <kinetic energies> $dm\,v^2/2$ gives the formula. This is a nonrelativistic model; deformation or internal motion can contribute additional <kinetic energy>.<University Physics Volume 1 by William Moebs and others/10.4 Moment of Inertia and Rotational Kinetic Energy>[§10.4]{ref}

For a computed example, take a uniform disc and an ideal thin ring, each of <mass> $M=2\,\mathrm{kg}$ and radius $R=0.30\,\mathrm m$, rotating about its central perpendicular axis at <angular speed> $10\,\mathrm{rad/s}$. The <moment of inertia of a uniform disc> is $MR^2/2=0.090\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}$, while the ring has <moment of inertia> $MR^2=0.180\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}$. Their <rotational kinetic energies> are therefore $4.5\,\mathrm J$ and $9.0\,\mathrm J$. The ring stores twice the <energy> at the same <angular speed>, because more <mass> lies farther from the axis. At the same nonzero <rotational kinetic energy>, its <angular speed> would instead be $1/\sqrt{2}$ times the disc's. This makes radial placement useful for storing <energy> in a rotating wheel, although a real design must also withstand the stresses of rotation.

For fixed-axis rotation with constant <moment of inertia>, write the signed rotation rate as $\Omega=\dot\theta$, with <angle> $\theta$ in <radians>. The axial component of net external <torque> obeys $\tau_{\mathrm{axis}}=I\dot\Omega$, so the <work-energy theorem> becomes
$$
\frac{dK_{\mathrm{rot}}}{dt}=\tau_{\mathrm{axis}}\Omega,
\qquad \Delta K_{\mathrm{rot}}=\int\tau_{\mathrm{axis}}\,d\theta.
$$
An opposing <torque> removes <rotational kinetic energy>; bearing <friction> must be included in the net <torque>. The signed $\Omega$ matters when the direction reverses. Rotation about a fixed axis need not be about a <principal axis>: the scalar <energy> formula still holds, but transverse bearing <torques> can be needed to maintain that axis.

For general <rigid body> motion, use the <inertia tensor> $\mathsf I_G$ about the <centre of mass> $G$:
$$
K_{\mathrm{total}}=\frac12M|\mathbf V_G|^2+\frac12\boldsymbol\omega^{\mathsf T}\mathsf I_G\boldsymbol\omega,
\qquad \mathbf L_G=\mathsf I_G\boldsymbol\omega.
$$
This is the <kinetic-energy decomposition about the centre of mass>. In a <principal body frame>, the rotational term is $\tfrac12(I_1\omega_1^2+I_2\omega_2^2+I_3\omega_3^2)$, using the <principal moments of inertia>. Away from a <principal axis>, <angular momentum> and <angular velocity> need not be parallel. A freely rotating asymmetric <rigid body> can change the direction of <angular velocity> relative to the body even while <rotational kinetic energy> remains constant; <Euler equations for a torque-free rigid body> govern that motion. One constant axial <moment of inertia> is then insufficient.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume I by Richard Feynman and others/Chapter 20: Rotation in space>{ref}

= Potential energy
{parent=Energy}
{title2=$\mathbf F=-\nabla V$}
{wiki}

<Potential energy> assigns <energy> to a system's arrangement, so a change of arrangement accounts for the <work> done by a <conservative force>. For a time-independent <potential energy> $V(\mathbf r)$,
$$
\mathbf F=-\nabla V,
\qquad W_{\mathrm{conservative}}=V_i-V_f=-\Delta V.
$$
The <gradient> points toward increasing <potential energy>; the <force> points toward decreasing <potential energy>. Only differences matter: adding a constant to $V$ changes neither the <force> nor its <work>. This endpoint rule follows by substituting $-\nabla V$ into the <line integral> for <work>. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/8-2-conservative-and-non-conservative-forces[OpenStax University Physics 1, §8.2: Conservative forces and potential energy]{ref}

Near <Earth>'s surface, stipulate constant <gravitational acceleration> $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ and measure height $h$ upward from a chosen zero. Then <gravitational potential energy> is $V=mgh$. Raising a $2\,\mathrm{kg}$ <mass> by $0.5\,\mathrm m$ increases its <gravitational potential energy> by $9.81\,\mathrm J$, while <gravity> does $-9.81\,\mathrm J$ of <work>. These are computed model values, not measurements of a particular lift. An ideal <spring> obeying <Hooke's law> instead has $V=kx^2/2$, with <displacement> $x$ from its relaxed state and <spring constant> $k$: $k=100\,\mathrm{N/m}$ and $x=0.2\,\mathrm m$ give $V=2\,\mathrm J$ relative to $x=0$. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/8-1-potential-energy-of-a-system[OpenStax University Physics 1, §8.1: Gravity and spring examples]{ref}

= Gravitational height and distance along a body axis
{parent=Potential energy}
{title2=$V=Mgz_{\mathrm{CM}}$}

In uniform <gravity> a body's <gravitational potential energy> is its mass times <gravitational acceleration> times the Cartesian height of its <centre of mass>. If that height is represented by an axis distance $h$ and tilt angle $\theta$, it is $h\cos\theta$ and the energy becomes $Mgh\cos\theta$. Treating $h$ simultaneously as a Cartesian height would introduce an extra, unjustified cosine.

= Lennard-Jones potential
{parent=Potential energy}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$U(r)=4\epsilon[(\sigma/r)^{12}-(\sigma/r)^6]$}

Named after <John Lennard-Jones>, this is a pair <potential energy> combining steep short-range repulsion with nonretarded <London dispersion force> attraction. Its minimum is $U=-\epsilon$ at $r=2^{1/6}\sigma$, found by differentiation. The power twelve is a convenient model for repulsion, rather than an exact consequence of the <Pauli exclusion principle>. The power six in the attractive energy follows from second-order coupling of atomic <electric dipoles>, before retardation matters.

= Harmonic potential
{parent=Potential energy}
{title2=$V(x)=\tfrac12kx^2$}

A quadratic <potential energy> about a stable <equilibrium>. In one dimension $k>0$ is the restoring stiffness and the <force> is $-dV/dx=-kx$. A <mass> $m$ undergoing undamped motion has <angular frequency> $\sqrt{k/m}$. A smooth potential with a nondegenerate local minimum is approximately harmonic for sufficiently small <displacements>; higher-order terms describe departures from <simple harmonic motion>.

= Potential well
{parent=Potential energy}
{wiki}

A region of lower <potential energy> surrounded by higher <potential energy>; a particle must gain sufficient <energy> to escape classically.

= Double-well potential
{parent=Potential well}

A <potential energy> landscape with two local minima separated by a barrier. In a symmetric one-dimensional case, its two lowest <wavefunctions> are an <even function> and an <odd function>, with their <energies> split by <quantum tunnelling>. A <two-mode approximation for a Bose gas> retains these two <orbitals> when higher <energy levels> are sufficiently remote.

= Half-line square-root potential well
{parent=Potential well}
{title2=$V(x)=a\sqrt x,\quad x>0,\quad\psi(0)=0$}

A confining <Quantum Hamiltonian> on $x>0$ with $a>0$, potential $a\sqrt x$ and a <Dirichlet boundary condition> at zero has natural length $\ell=(\hbar^2/(2ma))^{2/5}$ and <energy> $E_*=(\hbar^2a^4/(2m))^{1/5}$. Rescaling $x=\ell y$ gives $H/E_*=-d^2/dy^2+\sqrt y$, so every <energy eigenvalue> scales as $E_*$. The potential <derivative> diverges at the wall even though the potential itself is finite there.

= Repulsive inverse-square potential
{parent=Potential energy}
{title2=$V(r)=c/r^2,\quad c>0$}

This <potential energy> generates a repulsive <central force> $F_r=-V'(r)=2c/r^3$. It is an inverse-square potential but an inverse-cube force; the powers must not be confused with the Coulomb or gravitational potential proportional to $1/r$.

= Scattering in a repulsive inverse-square potential
{parent=Repulsive inverse-square potential}
{title2=$r=r_{\min}\sec[\beta(\theta-\theta_0)]$}

For $V(r)=c/r^2$ with $c>0$, nonzero physical <angular momentum> $h$ and positive <energy> $E$, put $\beta=\sqrt{1+2mc/h^2}>1$ and $r_{\min}=\sqrt{(c+h^2/(2m))/E}$. Direct integration of the <effective potential> radial equation gives the displayed <orbit>. It has one radial minimum and two straight-line asymptotes separated by polar angle $\pi/\beta$. The repulsive scattering deflection is $\pi-\pi/\beta$; no bounded or circular orbit exists at finite positive energy.

= Gravitational energy
{parent=Potential energy}
{title2=$E_{\rm grav}$}
{wiki}

= Gravitational potential energy
{synonym}

The potential energy associated with gravitational interactions. For a self-gravitating spherical body with a fixed dimensionless density profile, $E_{\rm grav}=-C GM^2/R$ for a positive profile-dependent constant $C$; a uniform sphere has $C=3/5$. The $R^{-1}$ scaling competes with the same radius dependence of ultrarelativistic <electron degeneracy pressure> in a white dwarf, giving the <Chandrasekhar mass> scale.

= Available potential energy
{parent=Potential energy}
{title2=$A$}

The portion of a fluid system's <potential energy> that can be released by admissible rearrangement relative to a stable reference state. In a uniformly stratified incompressible fluid, the quadratic density is $A=\rho_0N^2\xi_z^2/2=\rho_0b^2/(2N^2)$, where $\xi_z$ is vertical <fluid displacement> and $b$ the buoyancy perturbation. It is positive for stable stratification and measures the restoring energy of <internal gravity waves>.

= Background potential energy
{parent=Available potential energy}
{title2=$P_b$}

The background potential energy is the <gravitational potential energy> of the statically stable state obtained by adiabatically sorting a fluid's parcels. The difference $A=P-P_b$ is <available potential energy>. Pure rearrangement changes $A$ but not $P_b$; irreversible density mixing can increase $P_b$. Molecular exchange with <internal energy> must be separated when defining an irreversible mixing rate.

= Potential function
{parent=Potential energy}

A potential function $V$ assigns a <scalar> to each point of a configuration or spatial domain. In mechanics it represents <potential energy>, and its negative <gradient> gives the corresponding <conservative force>.

= Central potential
{parent=Potential energy}
{wiki}

A central potential depends only on distance from a fixed centre, $V(\mathbf x)=V(|\mathbf x|)$. Its force is radial, and rotational symmetry implies conservation of <angular momentum>.

= Newton's theorem of revolving orbits
{parent=Central potential}
{c}

Adding an inverse-cube <force> can preserve radial motion while rescaling angular motion. For potential $-A/r+B/r^2$ and <angular momentum> $L$, the radial <effective potential> equals that of $-A/r$ with effective <angular momentum> $\sqrt{L^2+2mB}$ when this is real.

= Conservative force
{parent=Potential energy}
{wiki}

A force is conservative when its work depends only on the endpoints. On a simply connected domain this is equivalent to $F=-\nabla V$ for a <potential energy> $V$.

= Nonconservative force
{parent=Conservative force}

A nonconservative force has path-dependent work. On a simply connected domain, a continuously differentiable force is nonconservative exactly when its curl does not vanish identically.

= Inverse-square potential
{title2=$V(r)=-k/r$}
{parent=Potential energy}

Here <inverse-square potential> means the <potential energy> associated with an attractive <inverse-square law> for a <central force>: the <potential energy> itself varies as inverse distance. For $k>0$ and <position> $\mathbf r$ with $r=|\mathbf r|>0$,
$$
V(r)=-\frac{k}{r},\qquad V(\infty)=0,\qquad \mathbf F=-\nabla V=-\frac{k}{r^2}\widehat{\mathbf r}=-\frac{k\mathbf r}{r^3}.
$$
The <gradient> makes the distinction explicit: doubling $r$ halves the magnitude of <potential energy> but quarters the magnitude of <force>. The neighboring <repulsive inverse-square potential> instead has <potential energy> proportional to $r^{-2}$ and an inverse-cube <force>; the two naming conventions must be distinguished.

For <Newton's law of universal gravitation>, $k=GMm$, where $G$ is the <gravitational constant>. Treat the attracting <mass> $M$ as an isolated fixed <point mass>, or as a body with <spherical symmetry> and exterior <test particle> of constant <mass> $m\ll M$. The <spherical shell theorem> gives the same exterior <Newtonian gravitational potential energy> $U=-GMm/r$ without requiring uniform <mass density>. Here $r$ is measured from the centre, and the formula applies outside the body; it does not describe its interior. The <Newtonian gravitational potential> per unit <mass> is $\Phi=U/m=-GM/r$.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume I by Richard Feynman and others/Chapter 13: Work and Potential Energy (A)>[§13–2, equations 13.11–13.12: radial work and conserved energy; §13–4: gravitational field outside a sphere]{ref}

The negative sign records binding relative to infinite separation, rather than a negative <kinetic energy>. Moving from $r_1$ to $r_2>r_1$ increases <potential energy> by
$$
\Delta V=k\left(\frac1{r_1}-\frac1{r_2}\right)>0,\qquad W_{\mathrm{gravity}}=-\Delta V.
$$
Because the <force> is a <conservative force>, this <work> depends on the endpoints, including for a curved path. Integrating $dV/dr=k/r^2$ with zero at infinity gives $V=-k/r$; adding a constant leaves the <force> unchanged but shifts the numerical escape <energy> threshold by that same constant. This is why the reference at infinity must accompany an <escape velocity> calculation.

For an exact synthetic comparison, stipulate the <standard gravitational parameter> $\mu=GM=8\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}$ and a $1\,\mathrm{kg}$ <test particle>. At $r=2\,\mathrm m$, $U=-4\,\mathrm J$ and the inward <force> has magnitude $2\,\mathrm N$; at $r=4\,\mathrm m$, $U=-2\,\mathrm J$ and the inward <force> has magnitude $0.5\,\mathrm N$. The increase is $2\,\mathrm J$ of <potential energy>. These stipulated parameters are an arithmetic example, not observations of a particular body. The <escape-energy balance diagram> uses the same parameters.

= Angular momentum and the escape energy threshold
{parent=Inverse-square potential}
{title2=$\varepsilon=\tfrac12\dot r^2+\tfrac{h^2}{2r^2}-\tfrac\mu r$}

In the isolated model, <conservation of energy> and <angular momentum conservation> reduce the exterior <test particle>'s motion to
$$
\varepsilon=\frac{E}{m}=\frac{\dot r^2}{2}+\frac{h^2}{2r^2}-\frac{\mu}{r},\qquad h=|\mathbf r\times\mathbf v|,\qquad \mu=GM.
$$
Here $\varepsilon$ is <specific orbital energy> and $h$ is <specific angular momentum>. The <effective potential> is $\Phi_{\mathrm{eff}}=h^2/(2r^2)-\mu/r$. A zero radial <velocity> is a radial <turning point>, and need not mean zero total <speed>: at the <periapsis> or <apoapsis> of a nonradial <Kepler orbit>, tangential <velocity> remains. Thus the vertical gap between $\varepsilon$ and $-\mu/r$ represents total <kinetic energy> per unit <mass>, whereas the gap above $\Phi_{\mathrm{eff}}$ represents its radial part.

For $h>0$, the <orbital eccentricity> obeys $e^2=1+2\varepsilon h^2/\mu^2$. Negative $\varepsilon$ gives an <elliptic orbit>, including a <circular orbit> at its minimum allowed <energy>; zero $\varepsilon$ gives a <parabolic trajectory>; positive $\varepsilon$ gives a <hyperbolic Kepler orbit>. The <effective potential> tends to zero from below at infinity, so its outer region creates no extra positive escape barrier. This explains why <escape velocity> depends on <energy> even though <angular momentum> changes the path. The $h=0$ case is radial motion, rather than a nondegenerate <parabola> or <hyperbola>; inward radial motion can end in collision.

= Falling bodies and lunar motion as tests of gravity
{parent=Inverse-square potential}

One motivating laboratory demonstration compares two balls initially at the same height. A switch releases one vertically when the other leaves a horizontal track; arranging their paths to meet gives a collision as the readout of equal downward <displacement> over equal <time>. Equal initial height and synchronized release control the comparison. This tests the local independence of horizontal motion and <free fall>, under nearly constant <gravitational acceleration>; it does not measure an <inverse-square law>.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume I by Richard Feynman and others/Chapter 7: The Theory of Gravitation>[§7–4, figure 7–3: synchronized release and midair collision]{ref}

A complementary astronomical comparison uses the <Moon>'s distance and <orbital period> to infer inward <centripetal acceleration>, $a=4\pi^2r/T^2$, and compares it with surface <gravitational acceleration> scaled by $(R/r)^2$. The approximately sixtyfold distance ratio gives the approximately $1/3600$ prediction. The inference links terrestrial <free fall> to a lunar <orbit>. Distance calibration matters; the <circular orbit> approximation and the <Sun>'s <tidal force> limit this elementary comparison.<The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Volume I by Richard Feynman and others/Chapter 7: The Theory of Gravitation>[§7–4: lunar distance, period, and the inverse-square comparison]{ref}

= Lunar laser ranging
{parent=Inverse-square potential}

<Lunar laser ranging> gives a more discriminating <orbit> readout. An observatory directs short <laser> pulses at a <retroreflector> array on the <Moon>, and a detector records returning <photons>. Round-trip <time>, statistically combined over several returns, constrains distance; <pressure> and <temperature> accompany the readout. The fitted model includes <Newtonian gravity>, terrestrial and lunar rotation, geophysical effects, and corrections from <general relativity>. Comparing <orbit determination> residuals across stations and model choices checks consistency. The inspected study reports $1.8\,\mathrm{cm}$ weighted <root mean square> residuals for its 2005–2011 ranges, rather than for a bare isolated <inverse-square potential>. It tests the <equivalence principle> using the <Earth> and <Moon>'s response to the <Sun>; it is not a direct <escape velocity> measurement. Uneven coverage near new and full <Moon>, <radiation pressure>, and thermal expansion of reflector mounts limit that inference.<Lunar Laser Ranging Tests of the Equivalence Principle by James G. Williams and others>[§§2–3, pp. 3–6: pulse timing, normal points, model and figure 1; §4, pp. 7–8: consistency checks and radiation/thermal effects]{ref}

= Retroreflector
{parent=Lunar laser ranging}

A <retroreflector> returns incident <light> approximately toward its source. Corner-cube arrays make the <lunar laser ranging> round trip possible.

= Corner reflector
{parent=Retroreflector}
{wiki}

Three mutually perpendicular mirrors reverse a ray's direction when it encounters all three. With <orthonormal> normals $a,b,c$, write the reflections as $I-2P_a$, $I-2P_b$, $I-2P_c$. Their <orthogonal projection matrices> have pairwise products zero and sum to $I$ by the <resolution of the identity in an orthonormal basis>. The product of the three reflections is therefore $-I$. It reverses every incident direction independently of the order in which the mirrors are met. Real apertures restrict which incoming rays actually reach all three reflecting faces. Such <retroreflectors> provide the returning-light geometry in <lunar laser ranging>.

= Exponential cusp potential
{parent=Potential energy}

The even potential
$$
V(x)=V_\infty(1-e^{-|x|/\lambda})
$$
has a cusp at the origin and approaches the finite value $V_\infty$. Motions below that value oscillate between symmetric turning points; motions above it escape.

= Escape velocity
{parent=Potential energy}
{wiki}
{title2=$v_{\mathrm{esc}}=\sqrt{2GM/r}$}

= Escape speed
{synonym}

The <escape velocity> is the least initial <speed> that permits a freely coasting body to move arbitrarily far from an isolated attracting body. Despite its conventional name, it is a <speed>, not a unique <velocity> vector. For a fixed <point mass> $M$, or outside a body with <spherical symmetry>, set <Newtonian gravitational potential energy> zero at infinity. An exterior <test particle> of constant <mass> $m\ll M$ then has
$$
U(r)=-\frac{GMm}{r},\qquad E=\frac12mv^2-\frac{GMm}{r},\qquad v_{\mathrm{esc}}(r)=\sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}=\sqrt{\frac{2\mu}{r}}.
$$
Here $\mu=GM$ is the <standard gravitational parameter>, and $r$ is distance from the centre, not height above the surface. Set $r=R$ for surface <escape velocity>. Evaluate <velocity> in the body's rest <inertial frame>. Neglect <drag>, later <rocket thrust>, other bodies' <gravity>, and relativistic effects.<University Physics Volume 1 by William Moebs and others/13.3 Gravitational Potential Energy and Total Energy>[§13.3, equations 13.4–13.6 and “Escape velocity”: infinity reference, small test mass and exterior launch]{ref}

<Conservation of energy> gives the threshold by requiring $E\geq0$: at infinity the <potential energy> tends to zero and the <kinetic energy> cannot be negative. If $E=0$, the outward motion loses <speed> continuously toward zero. If $E>0$, the asymptotic <speed> is $v_\infty=\sqrt{2E/m}=\sqrt{v_0^2-v_{\mathrm{esc}}(r_0)^2}$. If $E<0$, infinity is inaccessible. For a radial outward launch, its outer <turning point> is $r_{\max}=-GMm/E$. For nonradial motion use <angular momentum and the escape energy threshold> to distinguish zero radial <velocity> from zero total <speed>.

Direction still determines the <orbit> and whether the body hits an obstacle. An initially inward <velocity> can cause collision even when $E\geq0$. For an isolated spherical body, an exterior outward launch, or a tangential launch at or above <escape velocity>, has an outward escape branch without intersecting the body. A <parabolic trajectory> is the nonradial threshold path; a <hyperbolic Kepler orbit> has positive <energy>. The formula specifies their required local <speed>, not their orientation.<University Physics Volume 1 by William Moebs and others/13.3 Gravitational Potential Energy and Total Energy>[§13.3, “Energy and gravitationally bound objects”: scalar energy and finite-body collisions]{ref}

= Escape by jumping from a uniform-density asteroid
{parent=Escape velocity}
{title2=$r_{\max}=\sqrt{RH}$}

For a spherical nonrotating asteroid of the same mean <mass density> as a reference planet of radius R and surface <gravity> g, the <mass> scales as r cubed. Its surface <escape speed> therefore obeys $v_{\rm esc}^2=2gr^2/R$. A take-off <speed> $v_J$ permits escape for $r\leq v_J\sqrt{R/(2g)}$. If that same take-off <speed> gives ballistic height H above the reference planet, $v_J^2=2gH$, and the limiting asteroid radius is $\sqrt{RH}$. A chosen H is an explicit performance estimate, not a universal human constant.

= Molecular escape-speed temperature estimate
{parent=Escape velocity}

Equating the molecular root-mean-square speed to the surface <escape velocity> gives $3k_BT/m=2GM/R$, or $T=2mGM/(3k_BR)$. This compares speed scales; the high-speed tail can escape below that <temperature>, and molecular dissociation can invalidate the assumed species at high <temperature>.

= Escape-energy balance diagram
{parent=Escape velocity}

Use the synthetic <standard gravitational parameter> $\mu=8\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}$ and launch distance $r_0=2\,\mathrm m$. Then <escape speed> is $2\sqrt2\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Three outward radial launches give exact comparisons: $v_0=2\,\mathrm{m/s}$ has <specific orbital energy> $-2\,\mathrm{J/kg}$ and stops outward at $r=4\,\mathrm m$; $v_0=2\sqrt2\,\mathrm{m/s}$ has zero <specific orbital energy>; $v_0=2\sqrt3\,\mathrm{m/s}$ has <specific orbital energy> $+2\,\mathrm{J/kg}$ and asymptotic <speed> $2\,\mathrm{m/s}$. The second case has <speed> $2\,\mathrm{m/s}$ at $r=4\,\mathrm m$. Doubling $r_0$ divides <escape speed> by $\sqrt2$, whereas doubling $M$ at fixed $r_0$ multiplies it by $\sqrt2$. Changing the <test particle>'s <mass> changes $E$ but cancels from the <escape speed>.

= Tangential launch trajectories across the escape threshold
{parent=Escape velocity}
{title2=$r=\tfrac{h^2/\mu}{1+e\cos f}$}

The same initial distance can lead to strikingly different <orbits>. Let $s=v_0/\sqrt{\mu/r_0}\geq1$ and launch tangentially in one <orbital plane>. Then <periapsis> is the launch point, <specific angular momentum> is $h=r_0v_0$, and
$$
\frac{\varepsilon}{\mu/r_0}=\frac{s^2}{2}-1,\qquad e=s^2-1,\qquad \frac{r}{r_0}=\frac{s^2}{1+(s^2-1)\cos f},
$$
where $f$ is <true anomaly>. These follow by combining the initial <specific orbital energy> with the <orbital eccentricity> relation and the <Kepler orbit> equation. With $\mu=8\,\mathrm{m^3/s^2}$ and $r_0=2\,\mathrm m$, the initial <speeds> $2$, $\sqrt6$, $2\sqrt2$, and $2\sqrt3\,\mathrm{m/s}$ give $e=0,1/2,1,2$: a <circular orbit>, an <elliptic orbit> with <apoapsis distance> $6\,\mathrm m$, a <parabolic trajectory>, and a <hyperbolic Kepler orbit>. The two open paths have asymptotic <speeds> zero and $2\,\mathrm{m/s}$ respectively. Their different directions at large distance are part of the <Kepler orbit>, not an extra scalar escape threshold.

= Zero-energy escape takes infinite time
{parent=Escape velocity}
{title2=$r(t)=[r_0^{3/2}+\tfrac32\sqrt{2\mu}\,t]^{2/3}$}

“Arrives at infinity with zero <speed>” is an asymptotic statement. For outward radial motion at zero <specific orbital energy>,
$$
\dot r=\sqrt{\frac{2\mu}{r}},\qquad t(r)=\frac{2}{3\sqrt{2\mu}}\left(r^{3/2}-r_0^{3/2}\right),\qquad r(t)=\left(r_0^{3/2}+\frac32\sqrt{2\mu}\,t\right)^{2/3}.
$$
Every finite distance takes finite <time>, but $t(r)\to\infty$ as $r\to\infty$. There is no instant of rest at an infinitely distant destination. Differentiating the displayed solution gives $\ddot r=-\mu/r^2$, independently checking <Newton's second law>. In the synthetic example, reaching $r=4\,\mathrm m$ takes exactly $(4-\sqrt2)/3\,\mathrm s\approx0.861929\,\mathrm s$. For nonzero <specific angular momentum>, the <Barker equation> gives the corresponding infinite-<time> limit along a <parabolic trajectory>. Positive-<energy> escape also takes infinite <time> to reach infinity; it retains nonzero asymptotic <speed>.

= Escape speed, circular speed, and propulsion
{parent=Escape velocity}

For a <circular orbit>, <gravity> supplies <centripetal acceleration>, giving $v_c^2/r=\mu/r^2$. Thus
$$
v_c=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r}},\qquad v_{\mathrm{esc}}=\sqrt2\,v_c,\qquad \varepsilon_c=-\frac{\mu}{2r}.
$$
A body in a <circular orbit> is bound despite its continuous <free fall>. At the same $r$, escape needs twice its <kinetic energy> per unit <mass>. An instantaneous forward tangential <velocity> increment from the <circular orbit> requires $\Delta v=(\sqrt2-1)v_c$, rather than the full <escape speed> from rest. In the synthetic example this is exactly $2(\sqrt2-1)\,\mathrm{m/s}\approx0.828427\,\mathrm{m/s}$.<University Physics Volume 1 by William Moebs and others/13.4 Satellite Orbits and Energy>[§13.4, “Circular Orbits” and “Energy in Circular Orbits”: centripetal balance and negative total energy]{ref}

<Escape speed> is a coasting threshold, not a required continuously maintained <speed> or a measure of <rocket propellant>. A powered vehicle can start at rest and acquire <energy> gradually. The <rocket equation> relates an ideal <velocity> increment to <rocket exhaust speed> and <rocket mass ratio>; <rocket thrust>, <drag>, and the timing and direction of propulsion affect a real ascent. Rotation also changes the relation between surface-relative <velocity> and the <inertial frame> used in the calculation.

Reaching another body is a finite-destination <spacecraft trajectory> problem. In the synthetic radial example, a launch at $2\,\mathrm{m/s}$ reaches $r=4\,\mathrm m$ while remaining bound. A spacecraft can similarly encounter the <Moon> on a bound geocentric transfer; actual planning must include the <Moon>'s <gravity>, target motion, arrival <velocity>, and any propulsion needed for capture. Escaping <Earth> alone does not imply escaping the <Sun>, because the relevant <reference frame> and attracting <mass> have changed. The isolated formula describes one gravitational well; a full mission requires more than its scalar threshold.

= Spacecraft tracking and the limits of the escape model
{parent=Escape velocity}

Operational evidence comes from reconstructing a spacecraft's motion. A ground antenna transmits a coded signal; the spacecraft returns it. Corrected round-trip <time> constrains distance, while the <Doppler effect> of a coherent carrier constrains the line-of-sight <relative velocity>. NASA's navigation account describes calibration of onboard turnaround delays before launch, calibration of ground delays before use, and corrections for the <terrestrial atmosphere>. Measurements over multiple stations and epochs constrain the <orbit>; range and <Doppler effect> alone do not directly give all transverse motion. Navigators compare a fitted <spacecraft trajectory> with later tracking and remeasure after propulsion to assess the maneuver. The account identifies <radiation pressure> and imperfect propulsion as disturbances. This provides practical checks of a dynamical model and its corrections, rather than a laboratory measurement of a universal <escape speed>. The simple <inverse-square potential> predicts an unpowered exterior arc; accurate navigation includes additional <forces> and measurement delays.<Basics of Space Flight (2017 edition) by Dave Doody/Chapter 13: Navigation>{ref}

= Spacecraft trajectory
{parent=Escape velocity}

A <spacecraft trajectory> is the path traced by a spacecraft's <position> over <time>. An unpowered exterior arc can approximate a <Kepler orbit>; other bodies' <gravity>, <radiation pressure>, and propulsion can change it.

= Orbit determination
{parent=Spacecraft trajectory}

<Orbit determination> infers a body's <position> and <velocity> from observations and an <equation of motion>. It is an <inverse problem>; a <residual of an inverse problem> compares an observed readout with its modeled prediction.

= Escape and circular speeds inside an isolated uniform sphere
{parent=Escape velocity}
{title2=$v_e^2=V_c^2(3r_0^2/r^2-1)$}

For a sphere of radius $r_0$ and uniform <mass density> $\rho_0$, with no surrounding <mass> and <gravitational potential> zero at infinity, the <spherical shell theorem> gives $V_c^2=4\pi G\rho_0r^2/3$ inside. Matching to the exterior point-mass potential gives $\Phi(r)=-(2\pi G\rho_0/3)(3r_0^2-r^2)$. Since $v_e^2=-2\Phi$, the displayed relation follows for $0<r\leq r_0$. Exterior shells leave the interior <circular speed> unchanged but deepen the potential and change the <escape velocity>; a uniform core alone is insufficient to determine escape.

= Equipotential surface
{parent=Potential energy}

An equipotential surface is a level set of a scalar potential. Its gradient and the corresponding conservative force are normal to every smooth level surface.

= Equipotential curve
{parent=Equipotential surface}
{title2=$\phi(x,y)=C$}

An equipotential curve is a planar level curve of a scalar potential. Where the gradient is nonzero, its tangent is perpendicular to that gradient. In <potential flow>, it is therefore perpendicular to the <velocity field>; this distinguishes equipotential curves from <streamlines>.

= Turning point
{parent=Energy}

A turning point of one-dimensional motion is a position where the kinetic energy and instantaneous velocity vanish before the direction of motion reverses.

= Binding energy
{parent=Energy}
{wiki}

<Binding energy> is the <energy> required to separate a <bound state> into specified free constituents. For an <electron> bound in a <chemical atom>, neglecting this cost is an assumption of the free-<electron> <Compton scattering> model.

= Gravitational binding energy
{parent=Binding energy}
{wiki}
{title2=$\Omega=-\int_0^M Gm/r(m)\,dm$}

In <Newtonian gravity>, assembling a spherical mass distribution from shells gives the gravitational <potential energy> in the title formula, with zero at infinite separation. Its magnitude measures the gravitational contribution to binding. The full energy accounting of a hot self-gravitating body also includes its <internal energy>; the <virial theorem> relates the two in equilibrium. A uniform-density <sphere> has $\Omega=-3GM^2/(5R)$.

= Gravitational binding-energy lower bound for a spherical star
{parent=Gravitational binding energy}
{title2=$|\Omega|\ge GM^2/(2R)$}

If all mass of a spherical body lies within radius $R$, then $r(m)\le R$ in the shell-assembly <integral> for its <gravitational binding energy>. Therefore $|\Omega|=\int_0^M Gm/r(m)dm\ge(G/R)\int_0^M m\,dm=GM^2/(2R)$. The inequality is strict for an extended interior mass distribution; a thin shell gives equality. Dividing by a stipulated <luminosity> gives a lower bound on that reservoir-to-luminosity ratio. A <Kelvin-Helmholtz cooling time> also needs a structure model and the accessible fraction of gravitational release.
