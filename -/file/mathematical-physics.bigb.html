= Background structure
{parent=Mathematical physics}

Geometric or field data held fixed when a theory's dynamical degrees of freedom vary. The <Minkowski metric> is background structure in ordinary <special relativity>; the spacetime <metric tensor> is dynamical in <general relativity>. A fixed timelike vector field can introduce a preferred direction even when an equation is written using <tensor notation>.

= Time scale
{parent=Mathematical physics}
{title2=$\tau$}

A <time scale> is a characteristic duration over which a specified variable or process changes appreciably. Comparing <time scales> helps choose a dimensionless time and identify a <singular perturbation>. An exponential relaxation $x-x_*=A e^{-t/\tau}$ has relaxation <time constant> $\tau$; a nonlinear process can have several distinct <time scales>.

= Compactification
{synonym}

Compactification makes some spatial dimensions compact, so modes along them acquire discrete momenta. At energies below the first nonzero compact-mode mass, a <dimensional reduction> to the zero modes gives a lower-dimensional <effective field theory>. The compact volume fixes the gravitational kinetic coefficient in an unwarped product background.

= Brane-world scenario
{parent=Compactification (physics)}

In a brane-world model some fields, typically ordinary matter and gauge fields, are localized on a lower-dimensional <brane>, while gravity propagates through the higher-dimensional bulk. Compact volume or a warped metric can then separate the observed gravitational scale from local matter scales. A model must specify field localization, boundary conditions and stabilization rather than identifying a brane with an arbitrary compactification.

= Periodic electrostatic Green function with one compact dimension
{parent=Kaluza-Klein theory}
{title2=$\phi(r,z)=\frac{q}{2\pi r}\frac{\sinh(r/R)}{\cosh(r/R)-\cos(z/R)}$}

For the <Poisson equation> $\nabla_4^2\phi=-4\pi Rq\,\delta^3(\mathbf x)\delta_{2\pi R}(z)$ on $\mathbb R^3\times S^1$, expand the periodic delta as $(2\pi R)^{-1}\sum_ne^{inz/R}$. The $n$th <Kaluza-Klein mode> has radial potential $q e^{-|n|r/R}/(2\pi r)$, since $(\nabla_3^2-m^2)e^{-mr}/r=-4\pi\delta^3(\mathbf x)$. Summing the geometric series gives the displayed <Green function>. At equal compact coordinate it reduces to $q\coth(r/(2R))/(2\pi r)$. Thus the interaction energy changes from $q_1q_2/(2\pi r)$ at large separation to $q_1q_2R/(\pi r^2)$ at small separation, with normalization fixed by the stated Poisson equation.

= Retarded circle-compactified massless propagator
{parent=Kaluza-Klein theory}
{title2=$G_{\rm ret}=R^{-1}\sum_ne^{2\pi in\Delta w/R}G_{\rm ret}^{(4,m_n)},\quad m_n=2\pi|n|/R$}

For a massless field on Minkowski space times a circle of circumference $R$, the compact delta distribution expands as $R^{-1}\sum_ne^{2\pi in\Delta w/R}$. The noncompact <retarded Green function> in each Fourier sector is that of a four-dimensional field with mass $m_n$. Equivalently, the time-frequency denominator is $\mathbf k^2+m_n^2-(\omega+i0)^2$, putting both poles below the real axis. At equal compact positions the sum has one zero mode and a doubly counted nonzero mass tower.

= Static Green function across a circle compactification
{parent=Retarded circle-compactified massless propagator}
{title2=$G_{\rm stat}(r,0)=[4\pi Rr]^{-1}\coth(\pi r/R)$}

At zero frequency, sum the <Kaluza-Klein tower> of three-spatial-dimensional Yukawa kernels: $[4\pi Rr]^{-1}(1+2\sum_{n\geq1}e^{-2\pi nr/R})$. The geometric series gives the displayed function. It behaves as $1/(4\pi^2r^2)$ for $r\ll R$, the four-spatial-dimensional Coulomb kernel, and as $1/(4\pi Rr)$ for $r\gg R$, the three-spatial-dimensional kernel with a zero-mode normalization factor.

= Maxwell reduction on a circle
{parent=Kaluza-Klein theory}
{title2=$m_n=|n|/r$}
{c}

A real five-dimensional <Maxwell field> on a circle of radius $r$ has a <Fourier series> with coefficients $A_M^{(n)}$, $A_M^{(-n)}=(A_M^{(n)})^*$. Its mixed field-strength component is $F_{\mu5}^{(n)}=\partial_\mu A_5^{(n)}-inA_\mu^{(n)}/r$. The nonzero modes transform as $\delta A_\mu^{(n)}=\partial_\mu\Lambda^{(n)}$, $\delta A_5^{(n)}=in\Lambda^{(n)}/r$. Thus $A_5^{(n)}$ is a <Stueckelberg mechanism> field and the gauge-invariant massive vector is $A_\mu^{(n)}-r\partial_\mu A_5^{(n)}/(in)$. Its mass is $|n|/r$ and it has three physical polarizations. The zero modes instead consist of a massless four-dimensional <Maxwell field> and a real massless <scalar field>; the latter cannot be removed by a periodic infinitesimal <gauge transformation>. A positive Fourier level and its negative level are conjugate, not independent real fields.

= Kaluza-Klein monopole
{parent=Kaluza-Klein theory}
{c}

A gravitational magnetic monopole for a compact circle, whose fibre is nontrivially twisted over a surrounding two-sphere. With flat spectator directions its nonsingular local geometry is a <Taub–NUT space>. Reducing along the M-circle yields the <D6-brane>. The magnetic dual of the IIB circle vector is instead a five-brane in nine noncompact dimensions.

= Radius stabilization
{parent=Radion}
{title2=$V\prime(R_0)=0,\quad m_R^2>0$}

<Radius stabilization> fixes the size of a <compactification> dynamically rather than choosing it as an undetermined parameter. If the four-dimensional <low-energy effective action> contains a <radion> $R$ with positive kinetic coefficient $Z(R)$ and <scalar potential> $V(R)$, a stable radius $R_0$ requires $V'(R_0)=0$ and $m_R^2=V''(R_0)/Z(R_0)>0$. This matters for the <hierarchy problem>: the volume or warp factor explains a hierarchy only if a consistent dynamics selects and maintains the required geometry.

= Goldberger–Wise stabilization mechanism
{parent=Radius stabilization}
{c}
{title2=$kL\simeq\epsilon^{-1}\log(v_0/v_L)$}

The <Goldberger–Wise stabilization mechanism> generates a <radion> potential in the <Randall–Sundrum model> using a weakly backreacting bulk <scalar field>. On $0\leq y\leq L$, its static profile obeys $\chi''-4k\chi'-m_\chi^2\chi=0$, hence $\chi=Ae^{(4+\epsilon)ky}+Be^{-\epsilon ky}$, where $\epsilon=\sqrt{4+m_\chi^2/k^2}-2$. Boundary potentials approximately fix $\chi(0)=v_0$, $\chi(L)=v_L$. For $\epsilon\ll1$ and $kL\gg1$, the leading radius-dependent potential is proportional to $e^{-4kL}(v_L-v_0e^{-\epsilon kL})^2$. Its leading minimum satisfies $kL\simeq\epsilon^{-1}\log(v_0/v_L)$. Thus a modest ratio of boundary values can generate a large warp exponent. Corrections shift this minimum; the approximation requires small <scalar field> backreaction, and the four-dimensional <cosmological constant> still needs adjustment.

= Randall–Sundrum model
{c}
{parent=Compactification (physics)}
{wiki}
{title2=$ds^2=e^{-2k|y|}\eta_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu dx^\nu+dy^2$}

A warped five-dimensional geometry redshifts masses of fields localized on the distant <brane>: $m_{\rm IR}=e^{-kL}m_0$. For the usual doubled orbifold interval, integration of the <Einstein-Hilbert action> gives $M_{\rm Pl}^2=M_5^3(1-e^{-2kL})/k$. Exponential redshift can generate a large separation of observable scales with a moderately sized dimensionless distance $kL$. Stabilizing the <radion> remains a dynamical requirement.

= High-energy braneworld expansion
{parent=Randall–Sundrum model}
{title2=$H^2=\frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho\left(1+\frac{\rho}{2\lambda}\right)+\frac{\mathcal C}{a^4}$}

For a spatially flat, tuned one-brane background without energy transfer to the bulk, the <Israel junction conditions> produce the displayed modified <Friedmann equation>. Positive <brane tension> $\lambda$ gives ordinary expansion when $\rho\ll\lambda$, while the quadratic density correction enhances expansion at high energy. <Big Bang nucleosynthesis>, early acoustic evolution and primordial spectra can constrain this change. Spatial curvature, a residual cosmological constant and bulk energy exchange require their additional terms rather than this special formula.

= Dark radiation on a cosmological brane
{parent=High-energy braneworld expansion}
{title2=$\rho_{\mathcal E}\propto\mathcal C a^{-4}$}

Projected bulk <Weyl tensor> can contribute a radiation-scaling term to the brane <Friedmann equation>, even without ordinary brane photons. For an anti-de Sitter black-hole bulk the integration constant is related to its mass. This <dark radiation on a cosmological brane> changes the expansion rate tested by <Big Bang nucleosynthesis> and <cosmic microwave background anisotropy>; it should not automatically be identified with a freely specifiable thermal particle species.

= Gravity localization in a noncompact warped dimension
{parent=Randall–Sundrum model}
{title2=$M_4^2=M_5^3\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-2k|y|}\,dy=M_5^3/k$}

In a one-brane warped background, the proper extra dimension can be infinite while the four-dimensional <graviton> zero mode has finite norm. The <Einstein-Hilbert action> weighs its four-dimensional curvature term by the warp factor integral shown above, finite for $k>0$. Thus the proper length is not the effective gravitational volume. This differs from an unwarped noncompact direction, whose volume integral diverges. It also differs from the two-brane hierarchy mechanism, where finite brane separation redshifts masses on the distant brane. The noncompact construction is established in https://arxiv.org/abs/hep-th/9906064[the original single-brane warped-gravity paper].

= Warped-brane proper acceleration
{parent=Gravity localization in a noncompact warped dimension}
{title2=$|a|=1/L$}

An inertial observer of a fixed-$y$ flat brane in metric $dy^2+e^{-2y/L}\eta_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu dx^\nu$ is accelerated relative to the higher-dimensional bulk. Normalization and $\Gamma^y_{\mu\nu}=e^{-2y/L}\eta_{\mu\nu}/L$ give $a^y=-1/L$. This is its invariant bulk <proper acceleration>, not a nonzero intrinsic four-dimensional acceleration on the flat induced brane metric.

= Large extra dimensions

= Stone-Čech compactification
{c}
{parent=Compactification (physics)}
{title2=$\beta X$}

For a <completely regular Hausdorff space>, the Stone-Čech compactification is the weak-star closure of the evaluation functionals in the dual unit ball of $C_b(X)$. The <Banach-Alaoglu theorem> gives compactness; complete regularity makes evaluation a <topological embedding>. Every bounded real <continuous function> extends by $\widetilde f(p)=p(f)$, yielding an isometric isomorphism $C_b(X)\cong C(\beta X)$. For a continuous map into a <compact Hausdorff space> $K$, the adjoint of pullback sends this closure into the closed evaluation copy of $K$, yielding the unique continuous extension. Uniqueness follows from density. It is a universal compactification, not a homeomorphism between $X$ and the whole dual space.

= Stone-Čech extension property
{parent=Stone-Čech compactification}
{c}

Every <continuous map> from a space to a <compact Hausdorff space> extends continuously to its <Stone-Čech compactification>. Uniqueness follows from density of the original space and the <Hausdorff> property of the target. Two <Hausdorff compactifications> with this property have comparison maps both ways; their composites fix the <dense> original space and therefore are identity maps, proving equivalence of the compactifications.

= Clopen separation in discrete Stone-Čech compactifications
{parent=Stone-Čech compactification}
{title2=$\beta X=U\mathbin{\dot\cup}V$}

In the <Stone-Čech compactification> of a <discrete space>, the closure of each subset of $X$ is <clopen>, because its indicator extends to a continuous zero-one-valued function. For disjoint closed sets $A,B\subseteq\beta X$, choose $u=0$ on $A$ and $u=1$ on $B$ by <Urysohn's lemma>. Then $U=\overline{\{x\in X:u(x)<1/2\}}$ and $V=\beta X\setminus U$ are disjoint clopen sets containing $A$ and $B$, respectively. Density proves these containments.

= Discrete Stone-Čech compactifications are extremally disconnected
{parent=Stone-Čech compactification}

For a <discrete space> $X$ and $D\subseteq X$, the continuous extension of $\mathbf1_D$ to $\beta X$ remains idempotent by density, so it takes values in $\{0,1\}$. Its one-set is the clopen closure of $D$. If $O$ is any <open set> in $\beta X$, density gives $\overline O=\overline{O\cap X}$, so this closure is clopen as well. Thus $\beta X$ is an <extremally disconnected space>. For infinite $X$, this does not imply that every subset of $\beta X$ has open closure.

= Planck mass from compactification volume

{parent=Compactification (physics)}

For an unwarped product metric with fixed compact volume $V_n$, integrating the higher-dimensional <Einstein-Hilbert action> gives $M_{\rm Pl}^2=M_*^{n+2}V_n$ when the action coefficient is $M_D^{D-2}/2$. = Kaluza-Klein mode
{c}
{parent=Compactification (physics)}
{title2=$m_\ell=2\pi|\ell|/L$}

A Kaluza-Klein mode is a coefficient in a field expansion in eigenfunctions on compact extra dimensions. On a circle of circumference $L$, momentum is $2\pi\ell/L$, and a higher-dimensional massless field has lower-dimensional squared mass $(2\pi\ell/L)^2$. Nonzero modes give exponentially suppressed static-potential corrections at distances much larger than $L$.

= Kaluza-Klein tower
{c}
{parent=Kaluza-Klein mode}
{title2=$m_n^2=M^2+n^2/R^2$}

For a field periodic on a circle of radius $R$, a <Fourier series> gives modes with quantized internal momentum $n/R$. Each mode is a lower-dimensional field with the displayed squared mass. The entire sequence is the tower; retaining only modes below a chosen energy gives an <effective field theory>. The tower is distinct from any individual <Kaluza-Klein mode>.

= Kaluza-Klein compactification scale
{c}
{parent=Kaluza-Klein tower}
{title2=$m_{\mathrm{KK}}\sim R^{-1}$}

The compactification scale is the first nonzero internal-mode mass. On a circle it equals $1/R$ for a higher-dimensional massless field. A zero-mode <dimensional reduction> applies at energies well below it. Warping, nontrivial geometry and boundary conditions can change the relation between the first mass and a geometric radius.

= Large separation does not impose a Kaluza-Klein low-energy limit
{parent=Kaluza-Klein compactification scale}

A pointlike pulse can contain frequencies above the compactification gap even when its travel time and distance greatly exceed the circle size. Its <retarded circle-compactified massless propagator> can resolve winding images and massive-mode oscillations. Suppressing those modes requires low-frequency or long-wavelength sources/measurements, or suitable smearing; mere coordinate separation is not that restriction. The <static Green function across a circle compactification> gives a precise long-distance zero-mode limit without this ambiguity.

= Kaluza-Klein charge quantization
{c}
{parent=Kaluza-Klein mode}
{title2=$q_n=n/R,\quad m_n^2=M^2+n^2/R^2$}

For a periodic scalar on a circle of circumference $2\pi R$, its <Fourier series> has modes $\psi_n(x)e^{inz/R}$. In the fixed-radius metric $ds_5^2=\gamma_{ij}dx^idx^j+(dz+A_idx^i)^2$, the five-dimensional <Klein-Gordon equation> becomes $(D_iD^i-M^2-n^2/R^2)\psi_n=0$ with $D_i=\nabla_i-inA_i/R$. Thus the geometric gauge charge is quantized in units $1/R$ in units $\hbar=1$, or the conserved compact momentum in units $\hbar/R$. A rescaling to a canonically normalized electromagnetic potential changes the corresponding numerical unit of physical electric charge.

= Static potential across a compact circle
{parent=Kaluza-Klein mode}
{title2=$U(r)=C\pi\coth(\pi r/L)/(Lr)$}

On three noncompact spatial dimensions times a circle of circumference $L$, the <method of images> gives $\sum_{j\in\mathbb Z}[r^2+j^2L^2]^{-1}=\pi\coth(\pi r/L)/(Lr)$ at equal compact coordinates. This interpolates from $1/r^2$ at short distances to $\pi/(Lr)$ at long distances. A gravitational circle-radius scalar can change the long-range coefficient if it remains unstabilized.

= Dimensional reduction
{parent=Compactification (physics)}

Dimensional reduction retains fields independent of chosen internal coordinates. In a flat-torus zero-mode reduction, a metric supplies a metric, internal-component vectors and internal metric scalars; a <massless p-form gauge field> supplies lower-rank fields with internal indices. Fluxes, twists or projections can alter the spectrum and preserved <supercharges>.

= Toroidal reduction of a p-form gauge field
{parent=Dimensional reduction}
{title2=$A_p\mapsto\binom{D-4}{p-r}\text{ four-dimensional }r\text{-forms}$}

The zero-mode <dimensional reduction> on a flat $(D-4)$-torus splits a <massless p-form gauge field> by the number $r$ of spacetime indices. Each sector has $\binom{D-4}{p-r}$ copies and $\binom2r$ local polarizations per copy. Comparing coefficients in $(1+t)^{D-4}(1+t)^2$ gives $\sum_r\binom{D-4}{p-r}\binom2r=\binom{D-2}{p}$, proving polarization matching. Four-dimensional three- and four-form potentials contribute no local propagating states.

= Added mass
{parent=Mathematical physics}
{wiki}

Added mass is the effective inertia contributed by fluid accelerated together with an immersed body.

= Oscillation frequency with fluid added mass
{parent=Added mass}
{title2=$\omega^2=k_{\rm restoring}/(m+m_a)$}

Fluid <added mass> increases inertia but need not alter the same restoring <force>. A submerged spring's constant <buoyancy> shifts equilibrium, whereas a pendulum's <buoyancy> reduces its restoring <torque>. Thus an unbounded-fluid sphere has spring <frequency> factor $\sqrt{\rho/(\rho+\rho_f/2)}$ and downward-pendulum factor $\sqrt{(\rho-\rho_f)/(\rho+\rho_f/2)}$. The latter stable configuration requires $\rho>\rho_f$.

= Added mass of a circular cylinder
{parent=Added mass}
{title2=$m_a=\rho\pi a^2$}

An infinitely long <circular cylinder> of radius $a$ translating across its axis through otherwise stationary, inviscid incompressible fluid has <added mass> $m_a=\rho\pi a^2$ per unit length. The decaying, circulation-free <velocity potential> is $\phi=-Ua^2\cos\theta/r$. In moving centre coordinates, the fixed-laboratory derivative is $\phi_t=-\dot U a^2\cos\theta/r-U^2a^2\cos(2\theta)/r^2$. The <unsteady Bernoulli equation> gives $p-p_\infty=\rho a^2\dot U\cos\theta/r+\rho U^2a^2\cos(2\theta)/r^2-\rho U^2a^4/(2r^4)$. Integrating $-pa\cos\theta$ around $r=a$ yields $F_x=-\rho\pi a^2\dot U$; the transverse force vanishes. Taking a derivative at fixed centre coordinates would omit the necessary convective correction.

= Added mass of a sphere
{parent=Added mass}

A sphere of volume $V$ translating through unbounded inviscid incompressible fluid has fluid kinetic energy
$$
K_f=\frac12\left(\frac{\rho V}{2}\right)U^2.
$$
Its added mass is therefore one half of the displaced fluid mass.

= Acceleration of a freely falling sphere with added mass
{parent=Added mass of a sphere}

A sphere of density $\rho_s$ falling in inviscid fluid of density $\rho$ has effective inertia $(\rho_s+\rho/2)V$ and net weight $(\rho_s-\rho)Vg$. Hence
$$
\dot U=\frac{\rho_s-\rho}{\rho_s+\rho/2}g.
$$

= Radial frequency in a power-law central potential
{parent=Central force}
{title2=$\omega_r/|\omega_\theta|=\sqrt{k+2}$}

For <central potential> $V(r)=\beta r^k$ and nonzero <angular momentum> $J$, a <circular orbit> satisfies $\beta k r_0^{k+2}=J^2/m$, so attraction requires $\beta k>0$. Twice differentiating the radial <effective potential> $V+J^2/(2mr^2)$ gives $V_{\mathrm{eff}}''(r_0)=(k+2)J^2/(mr_0^4)$. Its <harmonic approximation> therefore has radial <angular frequency> $\omega_r=\sqrt{k+2}|J|/(mr_0^2)$, whereas $|\omega_\theta|=|J|/(mr_0^2)$. This proves the ratio and shows stability precisely when $k>-2$. At $k=-2$ the tuned <effective potential> is flat; at $k<-2$ the radial disturbance grows exponentially. Rationality of this local <frequency> ratio is only a necessary condition for all nearby <orbits> to close; <Bertrand's theorem> makes a stronger finite-amplitude assertion.

= Finite-time collision in an inverse-cube central force
{parent=Central force}
{title2=$h^2=2k\ \Longrightarrow\ \ddot r=0$}

For <potential energy> $\Phi(r)=-mk/r^2$, the inward <central force> has magnitude $2mk/r^3$. With conserved specific <angular momentum> $h=r^2\dot\theta$, the radial equation is $\ddot r=(h^2-2k)/r^3$. At the critical value $h^2=2k$, this reduces to $\ddot r=0$. An inward initial radial <velocity> $-v$, with $v>0$ and $r(0)=a$, therefore gives $r=a-vt$ until collision at $t_*=a/v$.

For $h>0$, integration of $\dot\theta=h/(a-vt)^2$ gives $\theta-\theta(0)=(h/v)(1/r-1/a)$. Thus the <orbit> is a <hyperbolic spiral> and the <particle> completes infinitely many turns before the finite collision time. The tangential <speed> $h/r$ diverges. The <potential energy> and <kinetic energy> individually diverge with opposite signs, while their sum remains the finite conserved <mechanical energy> $mv^2/2$.

= Rosette orbit
{parent=Central force}
{title2=$r(\theta)=r(\theta+2\pi/\nu)$}

A planar <orbit> whose <radius> oscillates as its <polar angle> increases, producing successive lobes around the force center. For nonconstant $r$ with fundamental angular radial period $2\pi/\nu$ and nonzero <specific angular momentum>, the full <position> and <velocity> repeat precisely when $\nu$ is a <rational number>, provided the <radius> is bounded above and away from zero. If $\nu=p/q$ in lowest terms, $p$ radial periods advance the angle by $2\pi q$; $dt=r^2\,d\theta/h$ then gives a finite time period. This proves closure rather than merely a spatial self-intersection.

= Bertrand's theorem
{c}
{parent=Central force}
{wiki}

<Joseph Bertrand>'s theorem states that, among smooth attractive <central potentials> admitting stable <circular orbits>, requiring every <bound orbit> to be closed singles out the inverse-distance <potential energy> and the isotropic quadratic <potential energy>, up to additive constants. Rational radial-to-angular <frequency> ratios near a <circular orbit> provide only a necessary local closure test; finite-amplitude closure is the additional content. In $U=Br^n$, the radial-to-angular <frequency> ratio near a stable circle is $\sqrt{n+2}$, and stability requires $nB>0$ and $n>-2$. The all-bound-orbit cases are $n=-1$ and $n=2$.

= Radial oscillation of an elastic-string orbit
{parent=Central force}
{title2=$\Omega^2=(k/m)(4-3L/r_0)$}

For a taut elastic string with potential $k(r-L)^2/2$, a circular orbit has $\omega_0^2=k(r_0-L)/(mr_0)$. A radial impulse preserves angular momentum, so the curvature of the fixed-angular-momentum effective potential gives $\Omega^2=k/m+3\omega_0^2$. The ratio is three at $r_0=6L/5$ and approaches two at $r_0\gg L$. Rational linearized frequency ratios describe leading small-amplitude closed sketches without proving exact closure at finite amplitude.

= Radial-impulse circularization of a harmonic central orbit
{parent=Central force}

For $\mathbf F=-A\mathbf r$, the turning radii obey $L^2=mAr_{\min}^2r_{\max}^2$. A radial impulse preserves <angular momentum>. Its circular orbit therefore has $r_c^4=L^2/(mA)$, or $r_c=\sqrt{r_{\min}r_{\max}}$. Cancel the radial velocity where the original orbit crosses $r_c$.

= Inverse-cube central force
{parent=Central force}

A radial <force> proportional to $r^{-3}$. For potential $V=B/r^2$, its outward radial component is $2B/r^3$: positive $B$ is repulsive and negative $B$ is attractive.

= Inverse-cube central orbit classification
{parent=Inverse-cube central force}

For nonzero <angular momentum> $L$, put $u=1/r$ and $\lambda=1+2mB/L^2$. The <Binet equation> is $u_{\theta\theta}+\lambda u=0$. Positive $\lambda$ gives cosine scattering branches, zero gives linear reciprocal radius, and negative gives exponential combinations, with <energy> distinguishing sinh, exponential and cosh branches. Only intervals with $u>0$ are physical.

= Marginal circular orbit under an inverse-cube force
{parent=Inverse-cube central orbit classification}

At $B=-L^2/(2m)$ the centrifugal and <force> potentials cancel. With zero radial <speed>, any radius is a circular solution, but there is no radial restoring <force>; radial-velocity perturbations are not confined.

= Pedal equation for a central-force orbit
{parent=Central force}
{title2=$p^{-2}=2m(E-V)/h^2$}

Let $p$ be the perpendicular distance from the force centre to the tangent of a regular planar <orbit>. Conserved physical <angular momentum> has magnitude $|h|=mpv$, while conserved <energy> is $E=mv^2/2+V(r)$. Eliminating speed gives the displayed equation when $h\ne0$. For a differentiable polar curve, $p=r^2/\sqrt{r^2+(dr/d\theta)^2}$, connecting the geometric tangent distance to the radial energy equation.

= Compton wavelength
{parent=Mathematical physics}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$\lambda_C=h/(mc)$}

The <Compton wavelength> of a <subatomic particle> of <rest mass> $m>0$ is $\lambda_C=h/(mc)$, where $h$ is the <Planck constant> and $c$ the <speed of light>. It is the <wavelength> of a <photon> whose <energy> $hc/\lambda_C$ equals that <subatomic particle>'s <rest energy> $mc^2$. It is a characteristic scale, rather than a measured geometric radius of the <subatomic particle>.

The reduced <Compton wavelength> is $\bar\lambda_C=\hbar/(mc)=\lambda_C/(2\pi)$, with $\hbar=h/(2\pi)$. Keep that factor of $2\pi$: the <Compton scattering> <wavelength> shift below uses $h/(m_ec)$, not $\hbar/(m_ec)$. Here we choose the rounded <electron> value $\lambda_C=2.426\,\mathrm{pm}$, where $1\,\mathrm{pm}=10^{-12}\,\mathrm m$; the maximum single-collision <wavelength> increase is $2\lambda_C=4.852\,\mathrm{pm}$.

Why this scale? A <photon> of <wavelength> $\lambda$ has <momentum> magnitude $h/\lambda$. At $\lambda\sim\lambda_C$ that <momentum> is comparable with $mc$, so transfer of an appreciable fraction of it can make the recoiling <subatomic particle> relativistic. Even for longer <wavelengths>, <Compton scattering> can resolve the small <energy> transferred into <electron> <kinetic energy>.

= Compton recoil kinematics
{c}
{parent=Compton scattering}
{title2=$c\mathbf p_e=E\hat{\mathbf x}-E'\hat{\mathbf n}$}

Choose the incident <photon> direction as $+x$ and the plane of the <collision> as the $xy$ plane, with the outgoing <photon> above the $x$ axis. The <electron> initially has zero <momentum>. <Conservation of energy> and <momentum conservation>, using the relativistic <energy-momentum relation>, give
$$
E+m_ec^2=E'+\sqrt{m_e^2c^4+c^2|\mathbf p_e|^2},
\qquad
c\mathbf p_e=(E-E'\cos\theta)\hat{\mathbf x}-E'\sin\theta\hat{\mathbf y}.
$$
Squaring the <electron> <energy> and the <momentum> magnitude in these two equations yields
$$
(m_ec^2+E-E')^2
=m_e^2c^4+E^2+E'^2-2EE'\cos\theta.
$$
Cancel common terms to obtain $m_ec^2(E-E')=EE'(1-\cos\theta)$. Division by $EE'>0$ gives $1/E'-1/E=(1-\cos\theta)/(m_ec^2)$. Substitution of $\lambda=hc/E$ proves both <Compton scattering> formulas. Using the nonrelativistic approximation $T_e=|\mathbf p_e|^2/(2m_e)$ here would lose the exact relativistic result.

Let $\phi$ be the nonnegative <electron> recoil angle below the incident direction. For $0<\theta<\pi$, the <vector components> above imply
$$
\tan\phi=\frac{E'\sin\theta}{E-E'\cos\theta}
=\frac{\cot(\theta/2)}{1+E/(m_ec^2)}.
$$
This assumes exactly the same initially stationary free <electron> and single outgoing <photon>. At $\theta=\pi$, the <electron> moves forward with $\phi=0$; at $\theta=0$ it stays at rest, so its recoil direction is undefined even though the limiting angle as $\theta\downarrow0$ is $\pi/2$. At $\theta=90^\circ$ in the chosen example, $c\mathbf p_e=(70,-61.566)\,\mathrm{keV}$ and $\phi\simeq41.33^\circ$.

= Kompaneets equation
{c}

= Energy conservation does not uniquely determine equations of motion
{parent=Conservation of energy}

For constant symmetric mass and stiffness matrices, $E=\tfrac12\dot q^{\mathsf T}M\dot q+\tfrac12q^{\mathsf T}Kq$ satisfies $\dot E=\dot q^{\mathsf T}(M\ddot q+Kq)$. = Dimensional analysis
{parent=Mathematical physics}
{wiki}

Dimensional analysis checks the <physical dimensions> of quantities and identifies combinations unchanged by the choice of units. It catches impossible equations and reveals scaling before a full dynamical calculation. Adding two quantities requires the same <physical dimension>; replacing metres by centimetres must change numerical values consistently while preserving physical predictions.

For the ideal <simple pendulum>, suppose its <oscillation period> $T$ depends only on mass $m$, <length> $\ell$, <gravitational acceleration> $g$, and dimensionless angular <amplitude> $\theta_0$. Write $T=m^a\ell^bg^c F(\theta_0)$. Since $[m]=M$, $[\ell]=L$, and $[g]=LT^{-2}$, matching <physical dimensions> gives $a=0$, $b+c=0$, and $-2c=1$. Consequently
$$
T=\sqrt{\frac\ell g}\,F(\theta_0).
$$
The angular <amplitude> is dimensionless when expressed in <radians>. Dimensional analysis establishes the length/gravity scaling and the absence of a mass factor under these assumptions, but cannot determine $F$. The <harmonic oscillator equation> gives $F\to2\pi$ at small <amplitude>; the full <simple pendulum> supplies its finite-amplitude correction. A model with drag or a finite massive support has additional parameters, so the same dimensional argument does not automatically apply.

The result is useful for comparison: at fixed angular <amplitude> and $g$, lengths in the ratio $1:4$ give periods in the ratio $1:2$. The ratio $T\sqrt{g/\ell}$ should be the same for both in this ideal model. A failure to collapse onto the same value points to measurement error or an omitted parameter. Correct <physical dimensions> are a necessary consistency check, while the coefficient and the adequacy of the model require more information.

= Nondimensionalization
{parent=Dimensional analysis}

Rescaling dimensional quantities by chosen characteristic scales produces <dimensionless variables>. The remaining dimensionless ratios identify independent parameters and possible asymptotic regimes.

In the <simple pendulum> equation, choose the time scale $t_* =\sqrt{\ell/g}$ and put $\tau=t/t_*$. A solution can therefore be calculated once and rescaled to many physical lengths and gravity values with the same dimensionless initial data.

{title2=$U_h(r)=h^2/(2r^2)+gf(r)$}

A particle constrained to a stationary smooth <surface of revolution> $z=f(r)$ under uniform <gravity> conserves $h=r^2\dot\theta$ and <mechanical energy> per unit <mass>. Substitute $\dot z=f'(r)\dot r$ and $\dot\theta=h/r^2$ into that <energy> to obtain

$$
This proves the radial reduction. Simple roots of $E-U_h$ are <turning points>; a stationary point $U_h'(r_0)=0$ supports an exact <circular orbit>. At a local maximum of $U_h$, another trajectory at the same <energy> can approach the orbit asymptotically as a <separatrix>. Existence of roots elsewhere is insufficient for confinement: their dynamically accessible ordering relative to the launch radius matters.

= Electromagnetic damping
{parent=Mathematical physics}
{wiki}

Motion-dependent induced current can produce a magnetic force opposing motion and dissipating energy resistively.

= Rotational damping of a resistive loop in an oscillating field
{parent=Electromagnetic damping}
{title2=$\langle\tau_\theta\rangle=-A^2B_0^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\theta/(2R)$}

For negligible <self-inductance>, a loop with <electrical resistance> $R$ obeys $I=-\dot\Phi_{\rm ext}/R$. With $\Phi_{\rm ext}=AB(t)\cos\theta$, this gives $I=-(A/R)\dot B\cos\theta+(A/R)B\sin\theta\,\dot\theta$. Its <magnetic torque> is
$$
\tau_\theta=\frac{A^2}{R}B\dot B\sin\theta\cos\theta-\frac{A^2}{R}B^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\theta.
$$
For $B=B_0\sin\omega t$ and slow mechanical motion, the first term averages to zero while the second gives the displayed opposing torque. A stationary loop nevertheless experiences an alternating torque at $2\omega$ and generally executes small forced motion. The zero leading mean aligning torque is not a claim of exactly zero instantaneous motion or of no higher-order effect.

= Orientation torque on a flux-conserving loop
{parent=Flux conservation in a superconducting loop}

= Positive Hessian of a layered travel-time functional
{parent=Fermat principle}

For three unit-height layers with positive speeds $u,v,w$, the straight-segment travel time is $t=\sqrt{1+x_1^2}/u+\sqrt{1+(x_2-x_1)^2}/v+\sqrt{1+(X-x_2)^2}/w$. Its <Hessian matrix> has form $\begin{pmatrix}p+q&-q\\-q&q+r\end{pmatrix}$ with positive $p,q,r$. Its leading minor is positive and its determinant is $pq+pr+qr>0$. Hence this time function is strictly convex, and its stationary path is the unique minimum.

= Parabolic light rays in a reciprocal-square-root speed profile
{parent=Fermat principle}
{title2=$y=y_0-\frac{k(x-x_c)^2}{4(1-ky_0)}$}

For speed $c(y)=c_0/\sqrt{1-ky}$, with $1-ky>0$, the elapsed-time integrand is $\sqrt{(1-ky)(1+(y')^2)}/c_0$. The <Beltrami identity> gives $(1-ky)/(1+(y')^2)=1-ky_0$, where $y_0$ is the turning height. Integration yields the displayed <parabola>. If the endpoints are $(\pm x_0,0)$, symmetry gives $x_c=0$ and $(kx_0)^2=4ky_0(1-ky_0)$. Hence $kx_0\leq1$, and the two algebraic turning heights are $[1\pm\sqrt{1-k^2x_0^2}]/(2k)$. Only heights lying within the specified optical layer are admissible.

= Conserved transverse optical momentum
{parent=Fermat principle}
{title2=$p=\mu(x)y'/\sqrt{1+y'^2}$}

For a positive <refractive index> depending only on $x$, <Fermat principle> applied to $\int\mu(x)\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx$ has $y$ as a <cyclic coordinate>. The <Euler-Lagrange equation> therefore makes the displayed quantity constant along a stationary ray. When $\mu^2>p^2$, its slope is $y'=p/\sqrt{\mu^2-p^2}$. If $\theta$ is the angle to the $x$ axis, this is conservation of $\mu\sin\theta$, the stratified-medium form of <Snell's law>. For fixed endpoint values, the integrand has second derivative $\mu(1+y'^2)^{-3/2}>0$ with respect to $y'$. Its strict <convexity> and the constant momentum show, by the tangent-line inequality and integration, that any such ray is the unique minimum among competing graph paths with the same endpoints.

= Two optical rays in a square-root refractive-index profile
{parent=Fermat principle}
{title2=$y=h+(x-x_0)^2/(4h),\quad h>0$}

For <refractive index> $n(y)=c\sqrt y$ with $c>0$, the optical-length integrand is $c\sqrt{y}\sqrt{1+y'^2}$. The <Beltrami identity> gives $\sqrt y/\sqrt{1+y'^2}=\sqrt h$, while the full <Euler-Lagrange equation> gives $2yy''=1+y'^2$. Together these yield the displayed parabolas and exclude a spurious constant solution of the squared first integral. Equal-height endpoints $(\pm L,Y)$ force $x_0=0$ and $h^2-Yh+L^2/4=0$. There are two positive stationary rays for $Y>L$, one coalesced ray for $Y=L$, and none of this smooth family for $Y<L$. For $L=1,Y=5/4$, the two paths are $y=1+x^2/4$ and $y=1/4+x^2$. <Fermat principle> requires stationary optical length, not a global minimum over every competing path.

= Ray in a linear-speed medium
{parent=Fermat principle}
{title2=$(x-h)^2+y^2=R^2$}

For light speed $c(y)=y>0$, the travel-time integrand is $\sqrt{1+y'^2}/y$. The <Beltrami identity> gives $y\sqrt{1+y'^2}=R$, so nonvertical rays are semicircles centred on $y=0$. Boundary endpoints at $y=0$ have infinite travel time: along any rectifiable path entering positive height, $ds/y\geq |dy|/y$ and the integral diverges. These semicircles are therefore ideal-endpoint <hyperbolic geodesics>, not finite-time optical minimizers joining zero-speed endpoints.

= Ray in an exponential-speed medium
{parent=Fermat principle}
{title2=$e^{\lambda y}=\cos(\lambda x)/\cos(\lambda a)$}

For positive $\lambda$ and light speed $c(y)=c_0e^{\lambda y}$, the <Fermat principle> uses the time integrand $c_0^{-1}e^{-\lambda y}\sqrt{1+y'^2}$. The <Beltrami identity> sets $e^{-\lambda y}/\sqrt{1+y'^2}$ constant. Solving this first integral gives the displayed symmetric ray between $(\pm a,0)$ when $0<\lambda a<\pi/2$. Its travel time is $2\sin(\lambda a)/(c_0\lambda)$. The ray's central height diverges as $\lambda a\uparrow\pi/2$, while its travel time tends to $2/(c_0\lambda)$.

= Offset uniformly charged spherical shells
{parent=Spherical shell theorem}
{title2=$r=\sqrt{x^2+a^2/4}$}

Place a fixed shell of radius $a$ and charge $-q$ at $(0,a/2)$ and a fixed shell of radius $2a$ and charge $+q$ at $(0,-a/2)$. On the positive $x$ axis their centre distances coincide. The electrostatic <shell theorem> makes the <electric field> vanish until $x=\sqrt3a/2$, gives magnitude $k_eq/(x^2+a^2/4)$ until $x=\sqrt{15}a/2$, and gives $k_eqa/(x^2+a^2/4)^{3/2}$ thereafter. = Uniform-Earth chord oscillator
{parent=Spherical shell theorem}
{title2=$\omega_0^2=4\pi G\rho/3$}

Inside a homogeneous spherical <mass>, <gravity> is proportional to position. Projection onto a fixed straight chord gives $\ddot y=-\omega_0^2y$, independently of its perpendicular offset from the <sphere> centre. Release from one surface endpoint at rest reaches the opposite endpoint after $\pi/\omega_0$. The transverse <force> is supplied by the tunnel constraint. This idealization neglects removed tunnel <mass>, rotation, density variation and dissipative <forces>.

= Critical gravity-tunnel settling time
{parent=Uniform-Earth chord oscillator}
{title2=$b_c=2m\omega_0,\quad y/y_s=(1+\omega_0t)e^{-\omega_0t}$}

Adding a velocity-proportional <force> $-b\dot y$ produces a <damped harmonic oscillator>. The fastest non-oscillatory release from rest is critical <damping>, with the repeated-root solution in the title. Reaching fraction $\varepsilon$ of the initial displacement requires $(1+s)e^{-s}=\varepsilon$, $s=\omega_0t$. At one percent, $s=6.638352\ldots$, rather than $\log100$. Exact arrival at equilibrium takes infinite time; the tolerance reference must be stated.

= Gravity of a concentric hollow sphere
{parent=Spherical shell theorem}
{title2=$g(r)=GM(<r)/r^2$}

For constant <mass density> $\rho$ on $a<r<b$, the <shell theorem> gives zero <gravitational field> for $r\le a$, inward magnitude $4\pi G\rho(r^3-a^3)/(3r^2)$ for $a<r<b$, and $4\pi G\rho(b^3-a^3)/(3r^2)$ outside. These formulas agree continuously at both boundaries. The empty cavity has zero <gravitational field> but generally nonzero constant <gravitational potential>.

= Hollow-sphere gravitational transit
{parent=Spherical shell theorem}
{title2=$t_{\rm cavity}=\sqrt{3/(2\pi G\rho)}$}

For a spherical shell of uniform <mass density> $\rho$, inner radius $a$ and outer radius $2a$, suppose a negligible-width radial tunnel permits a small <mass> $m$ to fall from rest at the outer surface. The enclosed <mass> at radius $r$ within the material is $4\pi\rho(r^3-a^3)/3$. The <spherical shell theorem> gives inward <gravitational acceleration> $g(r)=4\pi G\rho(r-a^3/r^2)/3$. Integrating from $a$ to $2a$ gives released <gravitational potential energy> $4\pi G\rho ma^2/3$, hence speed $a\sqrt{8\pi G\rho/3}$ at the cavity. The cavity has zero <gravitational field>, so the diameter transit time is the title expression, independent of $a$ and $m$.

= Termination of the Coulomb radial series

= Probability concentration of a circular Coulomb eigenstate
{parent=Hydrogen atom}
{title2=$\psi_\ell\propto r^\ell\sin^\ell\theta\,e^{i\ell\phi-r/[(\ell+1)a]}$}

For a nonnegative integer $\ell$, this <Coulomb potential> <energy eigenstate> has principal quantum number $\ell+1$ and maximal magnetic quantum number. Put $b=(\ell+1)a$. Including the spherical <volume element>, its radial <probability density> is a <gamma distribution> proportional to $r^{2\ell+2}e^{-2r/b}$; its polar density is proportional to $\sin^{2\ell+1}\theta$, while its azimuth is uniform. Radial and polar variables are independent. Their moments satisfy
$$
\mu=\mathbb E[r]=\frac{2\ell+3}{2}b,\qquad\frac{\operatorname{Var}(r)}{\mu^2}=\frac1{2\ell+3},\qquad\mathbb E[\cos^2\theta]=\frac1{2\ell+3}.
$$
The radial identities follow by successive <integration by parts>; the angular identity follows from $\int_0^\pi\sin^{j+2}\theta\,d\theta=(j+1)\int_0^\pi\sin^j\theta\,d\theta/(j+2)$.

Set $h=(2\ell+3)^{-1/3}$. <Chebyshev's inequality> and <Markov's inequality> give $\mathbb P(|r/\mu-1|>h)\leq h$ and $\mathbb P(|\cos\theta|>h)\leq h$. By the <union bound>, outside an event of probability at most $2h$, the point lies at distance $O(\mu h)$ from the equatorial circle of radius $\mu$. The <quantum state> therefore concentrates near a classical circular orbit in relative units as $\ell$ grows. The absolute orbit radius and absolute transverse width both grow; this statement does not assert localization in a fixed narrow tube.

= Hydrogen radial wavefunction in the first excited shell
{parent=Hydrogen atom}
{title2=$R_{20},R_{21}$}

Writing $\rho=r/a_0$, the normalized <radial wavefunctions> are $R_{20}=(2-\rho)e^{-\rho/2}/(2\sqrt2a_0^{3/2})$ and $R_{21}=\rho e^{-\rho/2}/(\sqrt{24}a_0^{3/2})$. Radial <normalization> uses $\int r^2|R|^2dr=1$. The first function has one positive <radial node> at $2a_0$; the second has none. Their behavior at the origin differs from that of the reduced radial function $rR$ and from the <radial probability density> $r^2|R|^2$.

= Quadratic anisotropic perturbation of hydrogen
{parent=Hydrogen atom}
{title2=$V'=\alpha(x^2+y^2)+\beta z^2$}

An axially symmetric quadratic <potential energy> perturbation preserves <parity> and the $z$ component of <orbital angular momentum>. For $1s,2s,2p_0,2p_{\pm1}$, its first-order <energy> shifts are respectively $(2\alpha+\beta)a_0^2$, $14(2\alpha+\beta)a_0^2$, $(12\alpha+18\beta)a_0^2$, and $(24\alpha+6\beta)a_0^2$. They follow by multiplying the <hydrogen radial second moment> by the angular average of $\alpha+(\beta-\alpha)\cos^2\theta$, whose $\cos^2\theta$ averages are $1/3,1/3,3/5,1/5$. Negative $\beta$ makes the globally extended potential unbounded below; the shifts then describe local perturbative <resonance poles> or a potential stabilized at large distance.

= Hydrogen radial second moment

= Hydrogen ground-state probability beyond the classical turning point

The nonrelativistic hydrogen ground <energy> is $-e^2/(8\pi\epsilon_0a_0)$. Requiring nonnegative classical <kinetic energy> restricts radius to $r\leq2a_0$. = Nodeless Coulomb radial eigenstate
{parent=Hydrogen atom}
{title2=$R=A r^\ell e^{-r/[a_0(\ell+1)]}$}

For the nonrelativistic point-Coulomb hydrogen <Hamiltonian>, the lowest radial state at fixed <orbital angular momentum> $\ell$ has <principal quantum number> $n=\ell+1$ and no positive-radius <wavefunction nodes>. With <Bohr radius> $a$, define $A_l=a[i p_r/\hbar-(l+1)/r+1/((l+1)a)]/\sqrt2$. For regular radial <bound states> the radial inner product and compatible domains give the displayed <factorization>. = Noninteracting Coulomb-shell filling

= Finite proton-shell correction to hydrogen ground energy
{parent=Hydrogen atom}

Model a <proton> as a uniformly charged thin <spherical shell> of radius $b$. Write $C=e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$. The <shell theorem> gives an electron <potential energy> $-C/b$ inside the shell and $-C/r$ outside. Relative to a point <Coulomb potential>, the perturbation is consequently $C(1/r-1/b)$ for $r<b$ and zero otherwise.

For the <hydrogen atom> <ground state>, <time-independent perturbation theory> gives the <first-order energy correction>
$$
\Delta E=\frac{4C}{a_0^3}\int_0^b e^{-2r/a_0}\left(r-\frac{r^2}{b}\right)\,dr
=\frac{2Cb^2}{3a_0^3}+O\left(\frac{Cb^3}{a_0^4}\right).
$$
Since $E_{1s}=-C/(2a_0)$, the signed fractional change is $\Delta E/E_{1s}=-4b^2/(3a_0^2)+O((b/a_0)^3)$. The correction $\Delta E$ is positive: distributing the positive charge weakens the attraction near the origin and reduces the magnitude of the binding energy. Here $a_0$ is the <Bohr radius> and $b\ll a_0$ is the small-radius approximation.

{title2=$\Omega_{\rm HI}=\frac{H_0m_H}{c\rho_{\rm crit,0}}\int Nf_X(N,z)\,dN$}

Let $f_X=\partial^2\mathcal N/(\partial N\partial X)$ count neutral <hydrogen> absorbers per <column density> and <absorption distance>. The summed column per unit proper light-path length is the mean neutral <number density>. Since $d\ell=c\,dz/[(1+z)H(z)]$ and $dX/dz=H_0(1+z)^2/H(z)$, that density is $(H_0/c)(1+z)^3\int Nf_X\,dN$. Dividing the proper <mass density> by $(1+z)^3$ gives the comoving density in the displayed formula. The denominator is today's <critical density>; including neutral helium requires a stated mass correction. For a distribution per redshift, replace $H_0f_X$ by $H(z)f_z/(1+z)^2$.

{parent=Absorber incidence and comoving number density}

= Random-orientation absorbing-disk cross-section
{parent=Lyman-alpha absorption}
{title2=$\langle\sigma_{\rm abs}\rangle=\pi R^2/2$}

A circular, opaque, geometrically thin disk has projected area $\pi R^2|\cos i|$. For isotropically oriented normals, $|\cos i|$ is uniform on $[0,1]$, so the mean interception area is half the face-on area. A nonunit covering fraction multiplies this cross-section. Finite thickness, orientation-dependent absorption thresholds and incomplete coverage can change it; this is an average over randomly placed disks, not the inclination distribution conditional on an observed interception.

= Lyman-alpha emission
{parent=Lyman-alpha line}
{c}
{title2=$2p\to1s+\gamma$}

Radiative decay from the <hydrogen> $2p$ level to $1s$ produces a Lyman-alpha photon. = Bohr model
{c}
{parent=Hydrogen atom}
{wiki}

The Bohr model assumes circular electron orbits with quantized <orbital angular momentum> $N\hbar$. It reproduces the hydrogen energy levels, but <wavefunctions> in quantum mechanics describe spatial probability distributions rather than definite trajectories. In particular, spherically symmetric <Coulomb bound states> have zero <orbital angular momentum>.

The puzzle is the coexistence of reproducible <spectral lines> and stable <chemical atoms>. A classical circular <orbit> in a <Coulomb potential> can have any negative <energy>: <Coulomb's law> alone selects no special radius or emitted <frequency>. Moreover, its <electron> has <centripetal acceleration>. The <Larmor formula> then predicts positive radiated power, so <conservation of energy> makes the <orbit> shrink. Simply drawing concentric circles explains neither stability nor a discrete <spectrum>.

<Niels Bohr> postulated nonradiating <stationary states> and emission with $h\nu=E_i-E_f$. His circular-orbit condition is $L=n\hbar$. These assumptions override classical radiation; the emitted <frequency> need not equal an orbital <frequency>.<On the Constitution of Atoms and Molecules, Part I by Niels Bohr>[Part I, pp. 7–8, 15: postulates]{ref}

= Circular-orbit derivation of the Bohr model
{parent=Bohr model}
{title2=$r_n=n^2\hbar^2/(\mu\kappa),\quad E_n=-\mu\kappa^2/(2\hbar^2n^2)$}

Consider a single <electron> of <mass> $m_e$ and a point <atomic nucleus> of <mass> $M$ and positive <electric charge> $Ze$, with $e>0$ the <elementary charge>. Use nonrelativistic <classical mechanics>, omit external fields and interactions with other <electrons>, and write
$$
\mu=\frac{m_eM}{m_e+M},\qquad
\kappa=\frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0},\qquad
\hbar=\frac{h}{2\pi}.
$$
Here $\mu$ is the <Coulomb two-body reduced mass>, $\epsilon_0$ the <vacuum permittivity>, and $h$ the <Planck constant>. The separation $r$ and relative speed $v$ describe the <two-particle relative coordinate>; neither body is silently pinned in place. The <electron>'s own radius about the <centre of mass> is $Mr/(M+m_e)$.

For the assumed circular <orbit>, <Coulomb's law> provides the required <centripetal acceleration>, and the imposed <orbital angular momentum> condition is
$$
\frac{\mu v^2}{r}=\frac{\kappa}{r^2},\qquad
L=\mu vr=n\hbar,\qquad n=1,2,3,\ldots.
$$
Substitute $v=n\hbar/(\mu r)$ into the first equation and cancel $r^{-2}$ to obtain
$$
r_n=\frac{n^2\hbar^2}{\mu\kappa},\qquad
v_n=\frac{\kappa}{n\hbar}.
$$
The <kinetic energy> is $K=\mu v^2/2=\kappa/(2r)$ and the <Coulomb potential energy> is $U=-\kappa/r$, with zero <energy> at infinite separation. Hence
$$
E_n=K+U=-\frac{\kappa}{2r_n}
=-\frac{\mu Z^2e^4}{2(4\pi\epsilon_0)^2\hbar^2n^2}.
$$
This derives the <energy> sequence from the stated circular model; the modern <Schrödinger equation> derivation is the distinct <series-termination quantization of the Coulomb radial equation>. The radius above scales as $n^2/Z$, whereas the <binding energy> scales as $\mu Z^2/n^2$. The <Bohr radius> article supplies the corresponding <Coulomb potential> length scale. The nonrelativistic assumption requires $v_n\ll c$, with $c$ the <speed of light>.

For an original numerical example choose $Z=1$, $M=1.67262192369\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}$, $m_e=9.1093837015\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$, $e=1.602176634\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$, $h=6.62607015\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$, $\epsilon_0=8.8541878128\times10^{-12}\,\mathrm{F\,m^{-1}}$, and $c=299792458\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Treat these as stipulated constants for the calculation. They give $\mu/m_e=0.99945568$, $r_1=0.0529465\,\mathrm{nm}$, $E_1=-13.598287\,\mathrm{eV}$ and $v_1/c\simeq0.00730$, where $\mathrm{eV}$ denotes an <electronvolt>. At $n=2$, $r_2=4r_1$ and $E_2=E_1/4$; the ground-state <ionization energy> is $-E_1$.

Taking $M\to\infty$ gives $\mu\to m_e$. A finite nuclear <mass> reduces every <binding energy> by $\mu/m_e$ and increases each separation by $m_e/\mu$. This follows from relative motion, not a fitted correction to individual <spectral lines>. Replacing the <atomic nucleus> by a heavier <isotope> changes the same factor, providing another check of the model.

= Discharge spectra and the Bohr model
{parent=Bohr model}

In https://doi.org/10.1080/14786441308634955[On the Constitution of Atoms and Molecules, Part I] (1913), pp. 8–9, <Niels Bohr> interprets existing <hydrogen> discharge and astronomical spectra. Electrical discharge in a vacuum tube removes <electrons>; recombination supplies <spectral lines>. This is a theoretical interpretation, without a reported <spectrograph> of his own.<On the Constitution of Atoms and Molecules, Part I by Niels Bohr>[Part I, section 2, pp. 8–9: existing spectra]{ref}

An actual optical obstacle appears in <Theodore Lyman>'s measurements: <hydrogen> discharge light entered an evacuated <spectrograph> directly through a slit, avoiding a transmitting window. A concave reflection <diffraction grating> dispersed the light onto photographic film. Pumping preserved the vacuum; a second slit supplied a known spectral displacement for <wavelength> calibration. Gas contamination and vacuum leaks limited the measurement. This apparatus resolved ultraviolet <spectral lines>; it did not image <orbits>.<Theodore Lyman, Pioneer of Ultraviolet Vacuum Spectroscopy by Paul Horowitz>[Paul Horowitz, Theodore Lyman, Pioneer of Ultraviolet Vacuum Spectroscopy, pp. 3–4, figures 2–5: windowless discharge connection, grating, film, pumping and dual-slit calibration]{ref}

For a separate original calibration exercise, suppose an idealized <spectrograph> returns synthetic vacuum line positions $x_n=\lambda_{n\to2}+b$ for $n=3,4,5,6$, with chosen additive offset $b=0.200\,\mathrm{nm}$. Assume its dispersion scale is already known and only this offset is uncertain. Compare the finite-<mass> prediction with the fixed-nucleus prediction $\lambda_n^{(\infty)}=(\mu/m_e)\lambda_{n\to2}$. Calibrate each model using only the $3\to2$ line: $\widehat b=x_3-\lambda_3^{\rm model}$. Then hold that offset fixed and test the other lines with residuals
$$
d_n=x_n-\widehat b-\lambda_n^{\rm model}.
$$
For the finite-<mass> model every $d_n=0$. The fixed-nucleus model has $\widehat b=0.55733\,\mathrm{nm}$ and residuals $d_4=-0.09264\,\mathrm{nm}$, $d_5=-0.12100\,\mathrm{nm}$, $d_6=-0.13400\,\mathrm{nm}$. Algebraically $d_n=(1-\mu/m_e)(\lambda_n-\lambda_3)$: one additive calibration cannot absorb a multiplicative change of the <wavelengths>. Allowing an unknown multiplicative dispersion factor as well would make the two models degenerate in this exercise, so independent scale calibration matters.

= Regular origin condition for a Coulomb radial wavefunction
{synonym}

For the usual three-dimensional <Coulomb potential> <Hamiltonian operator>, a separated <radial wavefunction> $R(r)$ uses the reduced function $u=rR$, whose radial <norm> has measure $dr$. The regular physical <operator domain> imposes $u(0)=0$. Near the origin the leading radial equation gives the <indicial equation> $c(c-1)-\ell(\ell+1)=0$ for $u\sim r^c$, with roots $c=\ell+1$ and $c=-\ell$.

The regular root gives $R\sim r^\ell$. The other root gives $R\sim r^{-\ell-1}$, and its radial <probability> integral $\int_0^\varepsilon r^2|R|^2dr\sim\int_0^\varepsilon r^{-2\ell}dr$ diverges for $\ell\geq1$. At $\ell=0$, $R\sim1/r$ is locally <square-integrable> in three dimensions, but violates $u(0)=0$ and has divergent <kinetic energy> integral $\int_0^\varepsilon r^2|R'|^2dr\sim\int_0^\varepsilon r^{-2}dr$. Thus local <wavefunction normalization> alone is insufficient to select the physical branch in this exceptional case; the standard Coulomb energy domain supplies the missing condition.

= Radial momentum operator

= Stark effect
{c}
{parent=Mathematical physics}
{wiki}

An <electric field> shifts and can split the <energy eigenvalues> of an <atom (Physics)>. This is the Stark effect; its <hydrogen atom> ground-state case is quadratic in a weak field.

The observable in <Stark effect> <spectroscopy> is a change in a <spectral line>, rather than a direct reading of an individual <energy eigenvalue>. If an upper <energy eigenstate> $u$ emits a <photon> into a lower <energy eigenstate> $l$, the <Planck constant> $h$ relates its <frequency> to the difference:
$$
h\nu(\mathcal E)=E_u(\mathcal E)-E_l(\mathcal E),
\qquad
h\,\Delta\nu=\Delta E_u-\Delta E_l.
$$
Here $\mathcal E$ denotes the applied <electric field> component along a chosen axis. Both levels can move, and <electric-dipole selection rules for hydrogen> decide which pairs produce observable <spectral lines>. This makes <Stark effect> <spectroscopy> a probe of <energy eigenstates> and their <electric dipole moments>: the slope $-\partial E/\partial\mathcal E$ is the corresponding dipole component by the <Hellmann-Feynman theorem>. The comparison with <Zeeman splitting> is useful: there an applied <magnetic field> supplies the perturbation.

= Quadratic Stark effect
{parent=Stark effect}
{title2=$\delta E\propto |\mathbf E|^2$}

The quadratic <Stark effect> is an <energy level> shift whose leading term is quadratic in an applied <electric field>. For a nondegenerate <energy eigenstate> of definite parity, the diagonal <matrix element> of an odd electric-dipole perturbation vanishes. <Second-order nondegenerate perturbation theory> gives
$$
\delta E_n^{(2)}=\sum_{k\ne n}\frac{|\langle k|V|n\rangle|^2}{E_n^{(0)}-E_k^{(0)}}.
$$
Every term is negative for the ground state. If field reversal is implemented by the <parity operator>, an analytic nondegenerate energy branch is even in the field. This symmetry explains the absence of odd powers; it does not apply without qualification to a degeneracy split linearly by the field.

= Linear Stark effect in the first excited hydrogen eigenspace
{c}
{parent=Stark effect}
{title2=$\Delta E=0,0,\pm3ea_0\mathcal E$}

Consider a fixed <proton> and one <electron>, omitting <spin>, <fine structure> and the <Lamb shift>. Let $e>0$ be the <elementary charge>, $a_0$ the <Bohr radius> in this fixed-nucleus model, and $\mathbf E=\mathcal E\widehat{\mathbf z}$. Because the <electron>'s <electric dipole moment> is $\mathbf d=-e\mathbf r$, its <electric-dipole interaction> is
$$
H'= -\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf E=e\mathcal E z.
$$
The <first excited hydrogen eigenspace> has a common unperturbed <energy eigenvalue> $E_2$. Write $|s\rangle=|2,0,0\rangle$ and $|p_m\rangle=|2,1,m\rangle$ in the <hydrogen energy eigenbasis>. The <electric-dipole selection rules for hydrogen> leave only the $s,p_0$ coupling within this <eigenspace>. Each diagonal <matrix element> vanishes by <parity>, but that does not make the entire perturbation vanish. <Degenerate perturbation theory> requires its <eigenvalues> in the whole <eigenspace>. With the usual real <wavefunction> phases the off-diagonal position <matrix element> is $\langle s|z|p_0\rangle=-3a_0$.<Quantum Mechanics by Richard Fitzpatrick/Linear Stark Effect>[Quantum Mechanics by Richard Fitzpatrick; Fitzpatrick, Linear Stark Effect, equations (7.82)–(7.86)]{ref}

The factor three can be checked directly. Set $\rho=r/a_0$ and use normalized radial <wavefunctions> and <spherical harmonics>:
$$
R_{20}=\frac{(2-\rho)e^{-\rho/2}}{2\sqrt2\,a_0^{3/2}},
\qquad
R_{21}=\frac{\rho e^{-\rho/2}}{2\sqrt6\,a_0^{3/2}},
\qquad
Y_{00}=\frac1{\sqrt{4\pi}},
\qquad
Y_{10}=\sqrt{\frac3{4\pi}}\cos\theta.
$$
The angular <integral> is $\int Y_{00}^*\cos\theta Y_{10}\,d\Omega=1/\sqrt3$. Including $z=r\cos\theta$ and the radial volume factor $r^2$ then gives
$$
\langle s|z|p_0\rangle
=\frac{a_0}{24}\int_0^\infty\rho^4(2-\rho)e^{-\rho}\,d\rho
=\frac{a_0}{24}(2\cdot4!-5!)=-3a_0.
$$
Reversing the <quantum phase> of $|p_0\rangle$ reverses this sign, with no change to the predicted <energy eigenvalues>.

Thus the nontrivial two-dimensional block and its <determinant> equation are
$$
H'_{s,p_0}=e a_0\mathcal E
\begin{pmatrix}0&-3\\-3&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\det(H'_{s,p_0}-\Delta E I)
=(\Delta E)^2-9e^2a_0^2\mathcal E^2=0.
$$
Consequently, for the displayed sign convention,
$$
|\psi_-\rangle=\frac{|s\rangle+|p_0\rangle}{\sqrt2},
\quad \Delta E_-=-3ea_0\mathcal E,
\qquad
|\psi_+\rangle=\frac{|s\rangle-|p_0\rangle}{\sqrt2},
\quad \Delta E_+=+3ea_0\mathcal E.
$$
The two remaining <energy eigenstates> $|p_{+1}\rangle,|p_{-1}\rangle$ have zero first-order shifts. The slopes correspond to opposite <electric dipole moments>, $\langle d_z\rangle=+3ea_0$ and $-3ea_0$. The <electric field> selects combinations of opposite <parity> that already have these dipole expectations at leading order. This is why applying <first-order nondegenerate perturbation theory> separately to $2s$ and $2p_0$, and obtaining two zero diagonal entries, misses the splitting.

The approximation requires $ea_0|\mathcal E|$ small compared with the separation to other <principal quantum numbers>. For real <hydrogen atoms>, it also requires the relevant <electric-dipole interaction> to dominate the small splittings omitted here, including the <Lamb shift>. At sufficiently small <electric fields> a pre-existing nonzero $2s$–$2p$ separation must be retained before taking the limit. A simple two-state illustration with unperturbed separation $\delta>0$ has shifts relative to its mean
$$
E_\pm-\overline E=\pm\sqrt{(\delta/2)^2+(3ea_0\mathcal E)^2}.
$$
For $3ea_0|\mathcal E|\ll\delta$, the lower branch moves by $-(3ea_0\mathcal E)^2/\delta+O(\mathcal E^4)$ relative to its zero-field value. At $\delta=0$ the splitting is linear. This interpolation explains the importance of the <hydrogen energy-level degeneracy>; it is not a complete model of the <fine structure> multiplet.

= Stark effect of the hydrogen ground state
{c}
{parent=Stark effect}

The even, spherically symmetric <hydrogen atom> ground state has no permanent <electric dipole>, so a uniform <electric field> gives no first-order energy shift. The <electric-dipole selection rules for hydrogen> allow odd-parity excited states to mix in at second order, yielding a negative shift quadratic in the field.

Here there is a <nondegenerate energy eigenvalue>, so <second-order nondegenerate perturbation theory> applies. With $H'=e\mathcal E z$, it gives
$$
\Delta E_{1s}^{(2)}
=e^2\mathcal E^2\sum_{k\ne1s}
\frac{|\langle k|z|1s\rangle|^2}{E_1-E_k}
=-\frac12\alpha_{1s}\mathcal E^2,
\qquad
\alpha_{1s}=\frac92\,4\pi\epsilon_0a_0^3.
$$
The notation includes integration over the <continuous spectrum>, as well as a sum over discrete <energy eigenstates>. All denominators are negative because $1s$ is the <ground state>. The positive <electric polarizability> $\alpha_{1s}$ therefore lowers its <energy>.<Quantum Mechanics by Richard Fitzpatrick/Quadratic Stark Effect>[Quantum Mechanics by Richard Fitzpatrick; Fitzpatrick, Quadratic Stark Effect, equations (7.57) and (7.65): quadratic convention and exact polarizability]{ref}

An independent way to see the coefficient avoids truncating that sum. Define $E_{\rm h}=e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0a_0)$ and $f=ea_0\mathcal E/E_{\rm h}$. In coordinates $\boldsymbol\rho=\mathbf r/a_0$, the dimensionless unperturbed <Hamiltonian operator> and normalized <wavefunction> are $h_0=-\nabla_\rho^2/2-1/\rho$ and $\psi_0=\pi^{-1/2}e^{-\rho}$, with <energy eigenvalue> $-1/2$. Writing the first-order <wavefunction> correction as $f\chi$, substitution into the <time-independent Schrödinger equation> gives
$$
(h_0+1/2)\chi=-\rho\cos\theta\,\psi_0,
\qquad
\chi=-\tfrac12\rho(\rho+2)\cos\theta\,\psi_0.
$$
The second equality can be checked by differentiation. Its <parity> makes $\chi$ orthogonal to $\psi_0$. The resulting <integral> is
$$
\frac{\Delta E_{1s}^{(2)}}{E_{\rm h}}
=f^2\langle\psi_0|\rho\cos\theta|\chi\rangle
=-\frac{f^2}{6}\bigl(\langle\rho^3\rangle_0+2\langle\rho^2\rangle_0\bigr)
=-\frac94 f^2,
$$
since the normalized radial <probability density> is $4\rho^2e^{-2\rho}$, giving $\langle\rho^2\rangle_0=3$ and $\langle\rho^3\rangle_0=15/2$. Converting back to <SI> units gives the stated <electric polarizability>. The induced <electric dipole moment> is $-\partial\Delta E/\partial\mathcal E=\alpha_{1s}\mathcal E$ and tends to zero with the <electric field>, unlike the selected degenerate branches' leading dipoles. Both calculations describe weak-field level shifts; a spatially uniform static <electric field> also allows eventual <quantum tunnelling> and <ionization>, which these real <energy> corrections do not calculate.

= Kepler's laws
{c}
{synonym}

Kepler's laws describe three connected features of an <elliptic orbit>: its shape, how fast it is traversed, and how its size determines its <orbital period>. <Kepler's first law> gives an <ellipse> with the attracting centre at one <focus of a conic>; <Kepler's second law> gives equal swept areas in equal times; <Kepler's third law> relates the squared <orbital period> to the cubed <semi-major axis>. Together they turn a drawing of a <Kepler orbit> into a prediction of where the bodies will be and when they will return. https://science.nasa.gov/learn/basics-of-space-flight/chapter3-3/[NASA, Basics of Space Flight: Kepler's laws]{ref}

Here the <mathematical model> is an isolated <two-body problem> in <Newtonian gravity>, with <point masses> $M,m>0$ and a bound, noncolliding <elliptic orbit>. Let $\mathbf r=\mathbf r_m-\mathbf r_M$ be the relative displacement, $r=|\mathbf r|$ the separation, and $\mu=G(M+m)$ the <gravitational parameter>, where $G$ is the <gravitational constant>. <Newton's law of universal gravitation> then gives
$$
\ddot{\mathbf r}=-\frac{\mu}{r^3}\mathbf r.
$$
The relative <Kepler orbit> has <semi-major axis> $a$ and <orbital eccentricity> $0\le e<1$. Both bodies move around their shared <astronomical barycenter>, the system's <centre of mass>. In its rest frame their displacements are $\mathbf r_m=M\mathbf r/(M+m)$ and $\mathbf r_M=-m\mathbf r/(M+m)$. Their individual <semi-major axes> therefore add to $a$; the relative <ellipse>'s attracting <focus of a conic> represents zero separation, rather than a body fixed in space.

One example will connect all three laws. After <nondimensionalization>, choose $a=1$, $e=0.6$ and $\mu=1$. The <semi-minor axis> is $b=a\sqrt{1-e^2}=0.8$, while the <periapsis distance> and <apoapsis distance> are
$$
r_{\rm p}=a(1-e)=0.4,\qquad r_{\rm a}=a(1+e)=1.6.
$$
Thus $a=(0.4+1.6)/2=1$ measures the <ellipse>'s size even though the separation changes fourfold. The <specific angular momentum> will be $0.8$, the two endpoint <speeds> $2$ and $0.5$, and the <orbital period> $2\pi$. These are values in the chosen scaled units, not measurements of a particular <planet>.

<Johannes Kepler> published <Kepler's first law> and <Kepler's second law> in *Astronomia Nova* in 1609 and <Kepler's third law> in *Harmonices Mundi* in 1619. These publication dates are documented by https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/4642[NASA Scientific Visualization Studio, Kepler's laws]{ref}. Their appeal is that three short statements organize both geometry and timing; <Newtonian gravity> supplies a common dynamical explanation.

= Kepler's first law
{c}
{parent=Kepler's laws of planetary motion}
{title2=$r=a(1-e^2)/(1+e\cos f)$}

The bound relative <Kepler orbit> is an <ellipse> with the attracting centre at one <focus of a conic>. In <polar coordinates>, its separation is the displayed function of <true anomaly> $f$, the angle measured from <periapsis>. For $a=1$ and $e=0.6$, it becomes $r=0.64/(1+0.6\cos f)$, giving $0.4$ at $f=0$ and $1.6$ at $f=\pi$. Setting <orbital eccentricity> to zero gives a <circular orbit>. This specifies the path; <Kepler's second law> specifies how it is traversed.

= Kepler's third law
{c}
{parent=Kepler's laws of planetary motion}
{title2=$T^2=4\pi^2a^3/[G(M+m)]$}

= Kepler third law
{c}
{synonym}

For an isolated <two-body problem> in <Newtonian gravity>, the relative <elliptic orbit>'s <orbital period> $T$ obeys
$$
T^2=\frac{4\pi^2a^3}{G(M+m)}=\frac{4\pi^2a^3}{\mu}.
$$
Here $a$ is the relative <semi-major axis>, and $M+m$ is the sum of the two <masses>. The separation $r$ varies during an <elliptic orbit>, so replacing $a$ by $r$ works only for a <circular orbit>, where $r=a$. Likewise, either body's individual <semi-major axis> about the <centre of mass> must first be converted to the relative one. When $m$ is negligible compared with $M$, the denominator can be approximated by $GM$.

For fixed <gravitational parameter>, the <orbital period> depends on the <semi-major axis> and is independent of <orbital eccentricity>. Our $a=1$, $\mu=1$ example gives $T=2\pi$ both for $e=0.6$ and for a <circular orbit> with $e=0$. The eccentric body's rapidly changing <speed> compensates for its different path. A compact way to see the cancellation uses <Kepler's second law>: divide the <ellipse>'s area $\pi ab$ by its <areal velocity> $h/2$. With $b=a\sqrt{1-e^2}$ and <specific angular momentum> $h=\sqrt{\mu a(1-e^2)}$, the result is $2\pi\sqrt{a^3/\mu}$.

Compare two separate <Kepler orbits> with the same <gravitational parameter>, $a_1=1$ and $a_2=4$:
$$
\frac{T_2}{T_1}=\left(\frac{a_2}{a_1}\right)^{3/2}=4^{3/2}=8.
$$
With $\mu=1$, their <orbital periods> are $2\pi$ and $16\pi$; measured in units of $T_1$, these are $1$ and $8$. Quadrupling the size therefore takes eight times as long to complete a circuit. This <power law> makes comparisons possible without first choosing physical units.

The same relation can be inverted to weigh a system: $M+m=4\pi^2a^3/(GT^2)$. An observed relative <semi-major axis> and <orbital period> determine the total <mass>, while additional information is needed to separate the two <masses>. This is useful for <binary stars>, as discussed in https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/orbits-and-keplers-laws/[NASA, Orbits and Kepler's Laws: measuring masses]. Geometry and a clock together become a weighing instrument.

= Kepler's second law
{c}
{parent=Kepler's laws of planetary motion}

= Law of areas
{synonym}

The relative displacement of a <Kepler orbit> sweeps equal areas about the attracting <focus of a conic> in equal times. A short interval near <periapsis> covers a wide angle at small separation; the same interval near <apoapsis> covers a narrow angle at large separation. The swept areas are equal even though the travelled distances are different. In particular, the sectors have curved <ellipse> boundaries: triangles formed by joining their endpoints only approximate the swept areas for short intervals.

Let $h=|\mathbf r\times\dot{\mathbf r}|$ be the relative <specific angular momentum>. The <areal velocity> is
$$
\frac{dA}{dt}=\frac12|\mathbf r\times\dot{\mathbf r}|=\frac h2.
$$
A <central force> exerts zero <torque> about its centre, so <conservation of angular momentum> makes this rate constant. This explanation also applies to other <central forces>; the <ellipse> shape specifically comes from <Newtonian gravity>.

In the $a=1$, $e=0.6$, $\mu=1$ example, <specific angular momentum> is $h=\sqrt{\mu a(1-e^2)}=\sqrt{0.64}=0.8$, giving <areal velocity> $0.4$. At <periapsis> and <apoapsis>, the <velocity> is perpendicular to the relative displacement, so $h=rv$. The endpoint <speeds> are consequently
$$
v_{\rm p}=\frac{0.8}{0.4}=2,\qquad v_{\rm a}=\frac{0.8}{1.6}=0.5.
$$
The fourfold <speed> difference is consistent with the fourfold separation difference. Away from these endpoints, $h/r$ gives only the transverse component of <velocity>; the full <speed> follows from the <vis-viva equation>.

To actually place equal-time markers, use <Kepler's equation>. Write <mean anomaly> as $\mathcal M=nt$, with $t=0$ at <periapsis> and <mean motion> $n=\sqrt{\mu/a^3}=1$. The different symbol $\mathcal M$ avoids confusing this angle with the <mass> $M$. Solve for <eccentric anomaly> $E$ in <radians>, then compute the relative position:
$$
\mathcal M=E-e\sin E,\qquad x=a(\cos E-e),\qquad y=b\sin E.
$$
This timing procedure is explained in https://pwg.gsfc.nasa.gov/stargaze/Smotion.htm[NASA educational archive, How Orbital Motion is Calculated].

= Two-patch vector potential for a radial monopole
{parent=Magnetic monopole}
{title2=$A_\pm=\frac{\pm1-\cos\theta}{r\sin\theta}e_\phi,\quad\nabla\times A_\pm=e_r/r^2$}

The radial <vector field> $B=e_r/r^2$ on punctured three-dimensional <Euclidean space> has flux $4\pi$ through every centered <sphere>. It has no <vector potential> smooth on an entire enclosing sphere: the <Stokes theorem> on a closed surface would force zero flux. Two overlapping patches suffice. In <spherical coordinates> take $A_+=(1-\cos\theta)e_\phi/(r\sin\theta)$, regular away from the negative $z$ axis, and $A_-=-(1+\cos\theta)e_\phi/(r\sin\theta)$, regular away from the positive $z$ axis. Their <curls> both equal $B$.

The Cartesian forms $A_+=(-y,x,0)/[r(r+z)]$ and $A_-=(y,-x,0)/[r(r-z)]$ show that the first is smooth at the north pole and the second at the south pole. On the overlap their difference is $A_+-A_-=2\nabla\phi$, where the <azimuth> $\phi$ is defined locally. Its multivaluedness around the axis explains the circulation difference $4\pi$. Splitting the sphere along a latitude, applying the <Stokes theorem> with $A_+$ on the north and $A_-$ on the south, and using their opposite boundary orientations recovers the total flux. A <coordinate singularity> in the tangential spherical basis does not by itself make a vanishing smooth vector field singular at a pole.

= Pendulum with a uniform rod and an endpoint mass
{parent=Physical pendulum}
{title2=$T=2\pi\sqrt{l(m+M_r/3)/[g(m+M_r/2)]}$}

A uniform rigid rod of length $l$ and <mass> $M_r$, pivoted at one end with a <point mass> $m$ at the other, has pivot <moment of inertia> $I=l^2(m+M_r/3)$. Its gravitational <potential energy> is $-gl(m+M_r/2)\cos\theta$. The planar <Euler-Lagrange equation> is $I\ddot\theta+gl(m+M_r/2)\sin\theta=0$, giving the displayed small-amplitude <oscillation period>. The limit $M_r=0$ gives a <simple pendulum>, whereas $m=0$ gives the uniform-rod <physical pendulum>. Thinness does not specify a rod's mass, so both contributions must be stated or explicitly neglected.

= Perforated-disc pendulum
{parent=Physical pendulum}
{title2=$\omega^2=g|\sum_i m_i x_i|/I_O$}

For a plane lamina suspended at O, treat removed pieces as negative <masses> and attached pieces as positive ones. Add their <moments of inertia> about O using the <parallel axis theorem>, and add their first moments to locate the <centre of mass>. If the first moments lie along one axis, the gravitational restoring coefficient is $g|\sum_i m_i x_i|$. Dividing it by the total inertia gives the small-<oscillation> angular frequency squared. Both the restoring moment and inertia change when material is moved.

= Edge-hinged cube with a central spherical cavity
{parent=Physical pendulum}
{title2=$\Omega/\omega=\sqrt{(1-\pi/6)/(1-3\pi/20)}$}

For a uniform cube of <mass> $M$ and side $a$ hinged along an edge, the centre is $a/\sqrt2$ from the hinge and $I_h=2Ma^2/3$. Removing a concentric <sphere> of <radius> $a/2$ removes <mass> $\pi M/6$ and hinge inertia $\pi Ma^2/10$, without shifting the centre. Combining the reduced weight and reduced inertia in $\omega^2=Mgd/I_h$ proves the displayed <frequency> ratio. Updating only the inertia would miss the change of gravitational restoring <torque>.

= Minimum-period offset of a physical pendulum
{parent=Physical pendulum}

= Angular-diameter ratio under a collinear boost

= Angular size under a collinear relativistic boost

= Laboratory frame

The <laboratory frame> is the <reference frame> in which an experiment's measuring apparatus is at rest. It need not be the <centre-of-momentum frame>: a target at rest in the laboratory can be struck by a projectile carrying nonzero total <momentum>.

= Inertial force
{synonym}

An inertial force is an additional term used to express <Newton's second law> in an accelerating <reference frame>. For origin acceleration $\mathbf A$ and axes with <angular velocity> $\boldsymbol\Omega$, the apparent force on a particle is
$$
\mathbf F_{\rm inertial}=-m\mathbf A-2m\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v'-m\dot{\boldsymbol\Omega}\times\mathbf r-m\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r).
$$
Here $\mathbf r$ and $\mathbf v'$ are measured relative to that frame. The rotation terms are the <Coriolis force>, <Euler force> and <centrifugal force>. Their values depend on the frame rather than on an additional physical interaction.

= Translating accelerated reference frame
{parent=Reference frame}
{title2=$m\ddot{\mathbf R}=\mathbf F-m\ddot{\mathbf a}$}

If the origin of a nonrotating <reference frame> has inertial <position> $\mathbf a(t)$ and a particle has relative <position> $\mathbf R$, then $\mathbf r=\mathbf R+\mathbf a$. <Newton's second law> becomes $m\ddot{\mathbf R}=\mathbf F-m\ddot{\mathbf a}$. The effective inertial <force> is therefore $-m\ddot{\mathbf a}$. No rotation-dependent terms arise because the coordinate axes remain parallel to the inertial axes.

= Inertial frame
{parent=Reference frame}
{wiki}

= Inertial frame of reference
{synonym}

An inertial frame is a <reference frame> in which a force-free particle moves with constant <velocity>.

= Inertial motion
{parent=Inertial frame}
{title2=$\nabla_u u=0$}

Motion with zero <acceleration> relative to the theory's inertial <affine connection>. In an <inertial frame>, its spatial trajectory is uniform and straight. The geometric statement refers to the connection, not to a chosen <coordinate system>; force-free motion supplies its physical interpretation. In <general relativity> the corresponding freely falling trajectories are <timelike geodesics>, with local inertial coordinates rather than a global <inertial frame> in a curved region.

= Rest frame
{parent=Inertial frame}
{title2=$\mathbf p=0$}

The rest frame of a massive particle is an <inertial frame> in which its spatial <momentum> is zero. Its <four-momentum> is then $(mc,\mathbf0)$ and its energy is $mc^2$. Such a frame exists for a future-directed timelike four-momentum: the <Lorentz transformation> with boost velocity $\mathbf v=c^2\mathbf p/E$ makes the spatial momentum zero, and the <mass shell> relation implies $|\mathbf v|<c$. A massless particle has no rest frame, because $E=c|\mathbf p|$ would require a boost with speed $c$.

= Instantaneous rest frame
{parent=Rest frame}

An inertial frame in which a selected body's velocity vanishes at one event on its worldline. For an accelerated body it is a local construction: rest frames at different events need not coincide, and simultaneity slices chosen at one event do not equal laboratory equal-time slices.

= Local inertial frame
{parent=Inertial frame}

A local inertial frame is a coordinate frame in which, at one chosen spacetime event, the <metric tensor> is Minkowskian and the <Levi-Civita connection> vanishes. Freely falling experiments confined to a sufficiently small neighbourhood therefore obey the laws of <special relativity> up to tidal corrections.

= Rotating-axes theorem
{parent=Rotating reference frame}
{title2=$(d\mathbf a/dt)_I=(d\mathbf a/dt)_R+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf a$}

The <time derivative> of a <vector> in inertial axes equals its component derivative in rotating axes plus the change of the rotating basis itself. Each basis vector obeys $\dot{\mathbf e}_j=\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf e_j$, proving the displayed identity by differentiating $\mathbf a=\sum_ja_j\mathbf e_j$. = Rotating saddle trap
{parent=Rotating reference frame}

= Artificial gravity in a rotating ring

= Geodesic equation in a uniformly rotating Minkowski chart
{parent=Rotating reference frame}
{title2=$\ddot r=p_\phi^2/(m^2r^3),\quad p_\phi=-mr^2(\dot\phi+\omega\dot t)$}

Set the inertial cylindrical angle to $\Phi=\phi+\omega t$. The <Minkowski metric> becomes $ds^2=(c^2-\omega^2r^2)dt^2-2\omega r^2dt\,d\phi-dr^2-r^2d\phi^2-dz^2$. For a massive <geodesic> parametrized by <proper time>, its <cyclic coordinates> give conserved covariant <four-momentum> components $p_t=m[(c^2-\omega^2r^2)\dot t-\omega r^2\dot\phi]$, $p_\phi=-mr^2(\dot\phi+\omega\dot t)$ and $p_z=-m\dot z$. Their combination gives $\dot t=(p_t-\omega p_\phi)/(mc^2)$. The radial <Euler-Lagrange equation> gives the displayed coordinate <acceleration>, although the free worldline is straight in inertial coordinates. The conserved inertial <energy> is $p_t-\omega p_\phi$, and the inertial axial <angular momentum> is $-p_\phi$.

= Centrifugal governor
{parent=Rotating reference frame}
{wiki}

A mechanism in which rotating weights move outward as <angular speed> increases, allowing their displacement to indicate or regulate a machine's speed. The steady configuration follows the balance of actual applied <forces> and <centripetal acceleration>, or equivalently of applied <forces> and <centrifugal force> in a <rotating reference frame>. Geometry and the restoring mechanism determine the speed-displacement relation.

= Gravity-restored centrifugal governor bifurcation
{parent=Centrifugal governor}
{title2=$\cos\theta_{\rm eq}=\omega_0^2/\Omega^2,\quad\omega_0^2=g/l$}

For total ball mass $m$, arm length $l$ and imposed constant shaft speed $\Omega$, the reduced effective potential is $V_{\rm eff}/(ml^2)=-\Omega^2\sin^2\theta/2-\omega_0^2\cos\theta$. Its equilibrium equation is $\sin\theta(\omega_0^2-\Omega^2\cos\theta)=0$. The downward state is stable for $|\Omega|<\omega_0$ and loses linear stability above it; nonzero stable openings then satisfy the displayed relation. Extending the angle to signed values gives a <supercritical pitchfork bifurcation>. Squared <angular frequencies> of <small oscillations> are $\omega_0^2-\Omega^2$ at the downward state and $\Omega^2-\omega_0^4/\Omega^2$ at the open states. At threshold the quadratic term vanishes but the quartic potential is positive, so zero linear frequency does not imply finite-amplitude free motion.

= Spring-loaded centrifugal governor
{parent=Centrifugal governor}
{title2=$\omega^2=[g+2kL(1-\cos\theta)/m]/(L\cos\theta)$}

Consider two equal balls of <mass> $m$, four light rods each of length $L$, a fixed upper pivot and a light sliding lower collar. Each upper and lower rod makes angle $\theta$ with the spindle. A <spring> of <spring constant> $k$ is uncompressed at $\theta=0$ and is compressed by collar motion. Its compression is $\delta=2L(1-\cos\theta)$. Collar balance gives $2T_l\cos\theta=k\delta$, while vertical ball balance gives $(T_u-T_l)\cos\theta=mg$. Radial <Newton's second law> gives $(T_u+T_l)\sin\theta=m\omega^2L\sin\theta$. Eliminating the rod <tensions> proves the displayed relation for nonzero $\theta$.

= Energy balance in a uniformly rotating frame
{parent=Rotating reference frame}
{title2=$\dot E=(\partial_t\phi)_{r'}$}

For uniform <angular velocity> $\omega$, potential $\phi$, and additional <forces> doing no work in the rotating frame,
$$
E=\frac m2|v'|^2+\phi-\frac m2|\omega\times r|^2,
\qquad \dot E=(\partial_t\phi)_{r'}.
$$
The <Coriolis force> does no work; the <centrifugal potential> accounts for the remaining apparent <force>. This energy is conserved precisely when the potential has no explicit time dependence in the rotating coordinates. An inertial time-independent <conservative force> need not meet that condition unless its potential is also invariant under the frame's rotation. The balance prevents applying a rotating-frame conservation law to a nonaxisymmetric stationary inertial potential.

= Particle on a uniformly rotating inclined plane
{parent=Rotating reference frame}

For a smooth plane at inclination $\theta$ rotating about a vertical axis at constant <angular velocity> $\omega$, choose rotating coordinates $\xi$ horizontally and $\eta$ uphill, with their cross product the upward <normal vector>. A sliding particle under gravity satisfies
$$
\ddot\xi=\omega^2\xi+2\omega\dot\eta\cos\theta,\qquad\ddot\eta=\omega^2\eta\cos^2\theta-2\omega\dot\xi\cos\theta-g\sin\theta.
$$
The <normal reaction> is $N=m(g\cos\theta-2\omega\dot\xi\sin\theta+\omega^2\eta\sin\theta\cos\theta)$ while contact is maintained. The <Coriolis force> does no work in the rotating frame, giving the conserved specific <energy>
$$
\frac12(\dot\xi^2+\dot\eta^2)+g\eta\sin\theta-\frac{\omega^2}{2}(\xi^2+\eta^2\cos^2\theta).
$$
The last term is the <centrifugal potential>; this quantity need not equal the inertial <mechanical energy>.

= Bead on a horizontal hoop rotating about its rim
{parent=Rotating reference frame}
{title2=$\ddot\theta=-\omega^2\sin\theta\cos\theta$}

For a smooth horizontal circular hoop of radius $a$, rotated at constant <angular velocity> $\omega$ about a vertical axis through a rim point, measure $\theta$ at that pivot from the diameter toward the bead. In rotating <Cartesian coordinate system> coordinates, $\mathbf r=a(1+\cos2\theta,\sin2\theta,0)$. The tangential component of the <centrifugal acceleration> gives the displayed equation; the <Coriolis acceleration> is normal to the hoop. The farthest point $\theta=0$ is stable for $\omega\ne0$, with <small oscillation> angular frequency $|\omega|$. The pivot itself is unstable. This geometry differs from a vertical rotating hoop and has no tangential gravitational term.

= Rotating-frame derivative formula
{parent=Rotating reference frame}

If a frame rotates with <angular velocity> $\boldsymbol\Omega$ relative to an inertial frame, the derivatives of any vector $\mathbf a(t)$ satisfy
$$
\left(\frac{d\mathbf a}{dt}\right)_{\rm inertial}
=\left(\frac{d\mathbf a}{dt}\right)_{\rm rotating}
+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf a.
$$
Applying this formula twice to a <position> gives the <Coriolis acceleration>, <Euler acceleration>, and <centrifugal acceleration> terms.

= Coriolis force
{title2=$-2m\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v$}
{c}
{parent=Rotating reference frame}
{wiki}

The Coriolis force is the apparent <force> proportional to a particle's <velocity> relative to a <rotating reference frame>. For constant <angular velocity> $\boldsymbol\Omega$ and particle <mass> $m$,
$$
\mathbf F_{\rm cor}=-2m\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_{\rm rot},
\qquad
\mathbf v_{\rm rot}=\left(\frac{d\mathbf r}{dt}\right)_{\rm rot}.
$$
Here $\mathbf v$ in the title formula means $\mathbf v_{\rm rot}$, not the <velocity> measured in an <inertial frame>. The <Coriolis force> belongs to the chosen <reference frame>: a particle stationary in that <rotating reference frame> has no <Coriolis force>, even though it can have a nonzero <velocity> in the laboratory. Other <reference frames> assign different <angular velocities> and relative <velocities>, and hence different <Coriolis forces> to the same motion.

The <cross product> makes the <Coriolis force> perpendicular to both $\boldsymbol\Omega$ and $\mathbf v_{\rm rot}$, with magnitude $2m\Omega|\mathbf v_{\rm rot}|\sin\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the <angle> between them. Apply the <right-hand rule> to $\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_{\rm rot}$ and then reverse its direction. Motion parallel to the rotation axis gives zero <Coriolis force>; reversing the relative <velocity> reverses the <Coriolis force>.

The appeal of this description is practical: axes attached to the <Earth> or a turning apparatus can be kept fixed on the page, provided <Newton's second law> includes the extra terms. A <free particle in rotating coordinates> shows exactly how a straight laboratory <trajectory> can become curved, while <Coriolis acceleration in local Earth coordinates> gives a useful terrestrial scale. The <rotating-table demonstration of Coriolis deflection> makes the two viewpoints visible in one experiment.

= Coriolis bank angle for eastward flight
{parent=Coriolis force}
{c}
{title2=$\tan\theta=2\Omega v\sin\lambda/g$}

For a locally eastward aircraft <velocity> at northern latitude $\lambda$, the horizontal <Coriolis acceleration> is southward with magnitude $2\Omega v\sin\lambda$. Banking to direct part of the lift northward cancels this <acceleration>. If vertical lift balances weight, the <force> ratio gives the displayed angle. Including vertical <Coriolis acceleration> replaces $g$ by $g-2\Omega v\cos\lambda$. This is a local rotational correction, not a full navigation model of motion along a curved latitude circle.

= Polar projectile Coriolis deflection
{parent=Coriolis force}
{title2=$d=\Omega u\cos\theta\,(2u\sin\theta/g)^2$}

In a flat uniform-gravity model at the North pole, <Earth's rotation> is vertical. To first order in angular <speed>, the horizontal component $u\cos\theta$ gives constant transverse <Coriolis acceleration> $-2\Omega u\cos\theta$ toward the right of the aiming direction. The return flight time is $2u\sin\theta/g$, proving the displayed miss distance. At the maximum unperturbed range, $\theta=45^\circ$, it becomes $\sqrt2\Omega u^3/g^2$. <curvature>, varying gravity, air drag and second-order rotational terms are excluded.

= Transverse miss on a rotating platform
{parent=Coriolis force}
{title2=$d\simeq4\omega a^2/v$}

A projectile thrown across a rotating disk's diameter at <speed> $v$ relative to the thrower inherits the thrower's tangential <velocity>. During its approximate flight time $2a/v$, a receiver at the opposite rim moves tangentially in the opposite direction. The relative sideways <displacement> is consequently $4\omega a^2/v$ to leading order in $a\omega/v$. Integrating the <Coriolis force> in the disk's frame gives the same result.

= Wrong-sign Coriolis steering error
{parent=Coriolis force}
{title2=$x_E\simeq4\Omega R^2[\Lambda-\sin\Lambda]/v$}

In a leading meridional-track approximation, an aircraft starts southward from the equator at constant speed $v$, with latitude $\lambda=-vt/R$. If lateral steering applies the <Coriolis force> rather than its negative, the two eastward effects add: $\ddot x_E=-4\Omega v\sin\lambda$. With zero initial cross-track velocity and displacement, double integration gives the displayed local-plane offset at southern latitude $-\Lambda$. For small $\Lambda$, it is $2\Omega R^2\Lambda^3/(3v)$. This assumes steering is a force transverse to the actual velocity while thrust and drag balance along it. It is not compatible with a literally fixed velocity vector. A spherical calculation also includes the transport of the local east and north basis, changing finite-latitude corrections.

= Coriolis force does no work
{parent=Coriolis force}
{c}
{title2=$\mathbf F_{\rm cor}\cdot\mathbf v_{\rm rot}=0$}

The instantaneous <power> of the <Coriolis force>, evaluated with the <velocity> in the same <rotating reference frame>, vanishes:
$$
\mathbf F_{\rm cor}\cdot\mathbf v_{\rm rot}
=-2m(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_{\rm rot})\cdot\mathbf v_{\rm rot}=0.
$$
This follows from the perpendicularity of the <cross product> and the <dot product> definition of <power>. The <Coriolis force> alone does no <work> and cannot change the rotating-frame <kinetic energy> $m|\mathbf v_{\rm rot}|^2/2$. It can change the direction of the relative <velocity>. Actual <forces> and the <centrifugal force> can still change that <kinetic energy>, as the <free particle in rotating coordinates> illustrates. Conservation of an appropriate total <energy> has the additional conditions stated under <energy balance in a uniformly rotating frame>.

= Coriolis acceleration
{title2=$-2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v$}
{c}
{parent=Coriolis force}

The Coriolis acceleration is the <Coriolis force> divided by the particle's <mass>:
$$
\mathbf a_{\rm cor}=-2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf v_{\rm rot}.
$$
It is therefore independent of <mass> for a specified relative <velocity> and <angular velocity>. This is one contribution to the <acceleration> measured in a <rotating reference frame>. For a fixed common origin with an <inertial frame> and constant $\boldsymbol\Omega$, the full <equation of motion in a rotating frame> is
$$
\underbrace{\ddot{\mathbf r}_{\rm rot}}_{\text{full rotating acceleration}}
=\frac{\mathbf F_{\rm real}}m
\underbrace{-2\boldsymbol\Omega\times\dot{\mathbf r}_{\rm rot}}_{\text{Coriolis}}
\underbrace{-\boldsymbol\Omega\times(\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r)}_{\text{centrifugal}}.
$$
All <vectors> here are expressed in the rotating axes; $\mathbf F_{\rm real}$ is the sum of actual <forces>. An accelerating origin adds a translational apparent <force>, and time-dependent <angular velocity> also adds the <Euler acceleration>.

The factor two follows directly from the <rotating-frame derivative formula>. First $\mathbf v_{\rm lab}=\dot{\mathbf r}_{\rm rot}+\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r$. Differentiate again in the <inertial frame>: differentiating $\dot{\mathbf r}_{\rm rot}$ contributes one $\boldsymbol\Omega\times\dot{\mathbf r}_{\rm rot}$, and differentiating $\boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf r$ contributes another. Move both terms to the rotating side of <Newton's second law> to obtain the minus sign and the factor two. The <centrifugal acceleration> comes from the remaining double <cross product>.

= Free particle in rotating coordinates
{parent=Coriolis acceleration}
{title2=$\mathbf r_{\rm rot}=(ut\cos\Omega t,-ut\sin\Omega t)$}

Consider a ball moving horizontally with no net horizontal <force> in the laboratory. Treat its horizontal motion as that of a free particle: $\mathbf r_{\rm lab}=(ut,0)$ with constant $u>0$. Axes rotating counterclockwise at constant <angular velocity> $\boldsymbol\Omega=\Omega\mathbf e_z$ about the launch point assign it the <position>
$$
\mathbf r_{\rm rot}=
\begin{pmatrix}\cos\Omega t&\sin\Omega t\\-\sin\Omega t&\cos\Omega t\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}ut\\0\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}ut\cos\Omega t\\-ut\sin\Omega t\end{pmatrix}.
$$
This is an exact <rotation matrix> transformation, with the axes coinciding at $t=0$. Taking $u=0.25\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $\Omega=0.8\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$, and $0\leq t\leq3\ \mathrm s$ gives the two views below. At $t=3\ \mathrm s$, the laboratory <position> is $(0.75,0)\ \mathrm m$, but the rotating <position> is approximately $(-0.5530,-0.5066)\ \mathrm m$. Both assign the same distance $0.75\ \mathrm m$ from the origin.

\Image[coriolis-free-ball-two-frames.png]
{title=One free ball: straight laboratory trajectory and curved rotating-coordinate trajectory}

The entire rotating <acceleration> must include the <centrifugal acceleration> as well as the <Coriolis acceleration>. To see the balance explicitly, write $c=\cos\Omega t$ and $s=\sin\Omega t$. Direct <differentiation> gives
$$
\mathbf v_{\rm rot}=u(c-\Omega ts,-s-\Omega tc),
\qquad
\ddot{\mathbf r}_{\rm rot}=u(-2\Omega s-\Omega^2tc,-2\Omega c+\Omega^2ts).
$$
The two apparent contributions are
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf a_{\rm cor}&=u(-2\Omega s-2\Omega^2tc,-2\Omega c+2\Omega^2ts),\\
\mathbf a_{\rm cf}&=u\Omega^2t(c,-s),\\
\ddot{\mathbf r}_{\rm rot}&=\mathbf a_{\rm cor}+\mathbf a_{\rm cf}.
\end{aligned}
$$
At launch $\mathbf r=0$, so only the <Coriolis acceleration> contributes to the initial bending. Later the <centrifugal acceleration> also contributes; the full curved <trajectory> cannot be explained by keeping only the <Coriolis force>. In particular, $|\mathbf v_{\rm rot}|^2=u^2(1+\Omega^2t^2)$ grows even though the <Coriolis force does no work>: the rotating-frame <kinetic energy> increase is supplied by <work> from the <centrifugal force>. The laboratory <kinetic energy> remains $mu^2/2$.

= Coriolis acceleration in local Earth coordinates
{parent=Coriolis acceleration}
{c}
{title2=$a_E=2\Omega\sin\varphi\,v_N-2\Omega\cos\varphi\,v_U$}

Use local east, north, and up <unit vectors> $(\mathbf e_E,\mathbf e_N,\mathbf e_U)$, with $\mathbf e_E\times\mathbf e_N=\mathbf e_U$. In a spherical-<Earth> approximation at <geographical latitude> $\varphi$, the <angular velocity> has components $\boldsymbol\Omega=(0,\Omega\cos\varphi,\Omega\sin\varphi)$. Expanding the <cross product> for relative <velocity> $(v_E,v_N,v_U)$ gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
a_E&=2\Omega\sin\varphi\,v_N-2\Omega\cos\varphi\,v_U,\\
a_N&=-2\Omega\sin\varphi\,v_E,\\
a_U&=2\Omega\cos\varphi\,v_E.
\end{aligned}
$$
These are <Coriolis acceleration> components, not the total <acceleration>. For horizontal motion, the horizontal part is $(fv_N,-fv_E)$, where the <Coriolis parameter> is $f=2\Omega\sin\varphi$. Thus northward motion in the northern hemisphere has an eastward <Coriolis acceleration>, to the right of its <velocity>; in the southern hemisphere the horizontal turning is to the left. At the equator $f=0$, but the full <Coriolis acceleration> need not vanish: vertical or eastward motion still couples through $\Omega\cos\varphi$.

For a numerical example, take the <Earth> rotation rate $\Omega=7.292115\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, <geographical latitude> $\varphi=45^\circ$, and an initially northward relative <velocity> $\mathbf v_{\rm rot}=20\mathbf e_N\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Then
$$
f\simeq1.03126\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}},
\qquad
a_E=fu\simeq0.0020625\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}.
$$

= Schrödinger equation
{parent=Mathematical physics}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$i\hbar\partial_t\psi=H\psi$}

The <Schrödinger equation> governs the evolution of a <quantum state> between instances of <measurement in quantum mechanics>. For a particle of <mass> $m>0$ moving in a prescribed real <potential energy> $V(\mathbf r,t)$, its position <wavefunction> satisfies
$$
i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=H(t)\psi,
\qquad H(t)=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2+V(\mathbf r,t),
\qquad \hbar=\frac{h}{2\pi}.
$$
Here $h$ is the <Planck constant>, $\nabla^2$ is the <Laplace operator>, and the <Hamiltonian operator> combines <kinetic energy> with <potential energy>. Specify an initial <wavefunction>, an <operator domain> and any <boundary conditions>: the differential expression alone does not completely specify a physical model. The <Born rule> then turns the evolving <probability amplitude> into position <probabilities> through $\rho=|\psi|^2$ and integration over a region.

This scalar <Schrödinger operator> describes <nonrelativistic quantum mechanics>, with relevant <momenta> much smaller than $mc$, where $c$ is the <speed of light>. It omits the constant rest <energy> $mc^2$, assumes fixed particle number, and suppresses active <spin> dynamics and coupling to a <magnetic vector potential>. A free <electron> with a fixed, uncoupled <spin> state can still use this scalar model; the <Pauli equation> supplies the additional <spin> components and magnetic coupling when needed. A prescribed real $V$ describes a closed evolution model or an approximation in which the source of that <potential energy> is treated as external. Dissipation, particle creation and a general <measurement in quantum mechanics> need further modelling.

For a time-independent <self-adjoint> <Hamiltonian operator>, the <spectral theorem> defines <unitary time evolution> by
$$
\psi(t)=e^{-iHt/\hbar}\psi(0),\qquad
\|\psi(t)\|^2=\|\psi(0)\|^2.
$$
Initial states in the <operator domain> satisfy the differential equation in the <Hilbert space> sense; the <unitary operator> also evolves all other states in that <Hilbert space>. Time-dependent <Hamiltonian operators> need suitable regularity and domain hypotheses; their evolution is not generally the exponential of $-itH(t)/\hbar$.

The local reason for conservation is especially tangible. Multiply the <Schrödinger equation> by the <complex conjugate> $\psi^*$, subtract its conjugate multiplied by $\psi$, and use the fact that the real <potential energy> terms cancel. For smooth <wavefunctions> this gives the <probability continuity equation>
$$
\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\mathbf j=0,\qquad
\mathbf j=\frac{\hbar}{m}\operatorname{Im}(\psi^*\nabla\psi).
$$
The <divergence theorem> identifies the change in probability inside a region with minus the outward <probability current>. Decay at infinity, or appropriate probability-conserving <boundary conditions>, preserves <wavefunction normalization>. Conservation of total probability does not require a stationary <probability density>.

Its usefulness comes from being a <linear partial differential equation>: <quantum superpositions> of solutions are solutions, while different <quantum phases> can produce <interference>. For a fixed <Hamiltonian operator>, the <time-independent Schrödinger equation> finds the <energy eigenstates>; each then acquires just its own time-dependent <quantum phase>. One <eigenvalue problem> therefore organizes both allowed <energies> and the evolution of arbitrary <quantum superpositions>, with a <continuous spectrum> included when required. This is the attractive bridge between a spatial wave problem and a clock: <energy> differences determine the rate at which relative <quantum phases> change. It makes atomic <spectral lines>, <quantum tunnelling> and <quantum scattering> calculable from a specified model.

For an explicit example without confinement, take a <free particle> on the whole line, $V=0$, initially in a <Gaussian wave packet> with position <standard deviation> $\sigma_0$ and mean <momentum> $\hbar k_0$:
$$
\psi(x,0)=\frac{e^{-x^2/(4\sigma_0^2)+ik_0x}}{(2\pi\sigma_0^2)^{1/4}},\qquad
t_s=\frac{2m\sigma_0^2}{\hbar},\quad \tau=\frac{t}{t_s},\quad v_0=\frac{\hbar k_0}{m}.
$$
Unlike a single <plane wave>, this <wavefunction> satisfies <wavefunction normalization>. A <Fourier transform> writes it as a <quantum superposition> of <plane waves>. Their <dispersion relation> is $\omega(k)=\hbar k^2/(2m)$, so each Fourier component gains the factor $e^{-i\hbar k^2t/(2m)}$. Completing the square in the resulting <Gaussian integral> gives
$$
\psi(x,t)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\sigma_0^2)^{1/4}\sqrt{1+i\tau}}
\exp\!\left[-\frac{(x-v_0t)^2}{4\sigma_0^2(1+i\tau)}+ik_0x-\frac{i\hbar k_0^2t}{2m}\right],
$$
with the square root chosen continuously from $t=0$. Its position <probability density> and <standard deviation> are
$$
\rho(x,t)=\frac{\exp[-(x-v_0t)^2/(2\sigma_t^2)]}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_t},\qquad
\sigma_t=\sigma_0\sqrt{1+\tau^2}.
$$
The centre moves with the <group velocity> $\omega'(k_0)=v_0$ and the <wave packet> spreads because different <wavenumbers> have different <group velocities>. This is unitary <wave dispersion>, without a random <force> or loss of probability. Independently, the initial <momentum> <standard deviation> is $\Delta p=\hbar/(2\sigma_0)$ and the initial position-momentum <covariance> vanishes. Free evolution gives $x(t)=x(0)+p(0)t/m$ in the <Heisenberg picture>, hence
$$
\sigma_t^2=\sigma_0^2+\frac{t^2}{m^2}(\Delta p)^2
=\sigma_0^2+\frac{\hbar^2t^2}{4m^2\sigma_0^2}.
$$
This independently checks the spreading law. The <Gaussian integral> of $\rho$ is one at every time; the decreasing peak is exactly offset by increasing width.

As hypothetical preparation parameters, choose a free <electron> with $\sigma_0=1\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $k_0=2\,\mathrm{nm}^{-1}$. Using $\hbar\simeq1.054571817\times10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}$ and $m\simeq9.1093837\times10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}$ gives
$$
t_s\simeq17.276\,\mathrm{fs},\qquad
v_0\simeq2.31535\times10^5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},\qquad
\Delta p\simeq5.27286\times10^{-26}\,\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}.
$$

The mean <kinetic energy> stays
$$

$$
It includes both mean motion and the <momentum> spread. The mean speed is about $7.72\times10^{-4}c$ and the <momentum> spread corresponds to $1.93\times10^{-4}c$, making the nonrelativistic model accurate for the appreciably occupied part of this ideal <Gaussian distribution>. = Time-independent Schrödinger equation
{title2=$H\psi=E\psi$}
{parent=Schrodinger equation}

For a time-independent <Hamiltonian operator> $H$, an <energy eigenstate> satisfies
$$
H\psi=E\psi.
$$
For a one-dimensional particle of <mass> $m$ in a real <potential energy> $U(x)$,
$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi''+U(x)\psi=E\psi.
$$
Its full time-dependent <wavefunction> is $\Psi(x,t)=\psi(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar}$: substitution in the <Schrödinger equation> checks both the minus sign and the factor $\hbar$. Thus an <energy eigenstate> has stationary position <probability density>, even though its <quantum phase> evolves. The <boundary conditions> and <operator domain> are part of this <eigenvalue problem>, selecting physically allowed solutions; the <infinite square well> and <radial Schrodinger equation for the hydrogen atom> give complementary confined examples.

For a complete discrete <orthonormal basis> of <energy eigenstates>, <linearity> gives $\Psi(x,t)=\sum_n c_n\psi_n(x)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$, with $\sum_n|c_n|^2=1$. Cross terms in $|\Psi|^2$ oscillate at <angular frequencies> $(E_n-E_m)/\hbar$. A <continuous spectrum> requires the corresponding spectral integrals; for a <free particle> on the whole line, individual <plane waves> are generalized <eigenfunctions>, and a normalized <Gaussian wave packet> is their integral <quantum superposition>. Adding a spatially constant real $C$ to $U$ shifts every <energy eigenvalue> by $C$ and multiplies the full state by $e^{-iCt/\hbar}$, a <global phase>; <energy> differences, position <probabilities> and <probability current> are unchanged.

= Inverse construction of a quantum potential
{parent=Time-independent Schrödinger equation}
{title2=$V=E+\hbar^2\psi''/(2m\psi)$}

For a real, sufficiently smooth, nonvanishing <wavefunction> of a particle with constant <mass>, the <time-independent Schrödinger equation> determines the <potential energy> through the displayed logarithmic-curvature formula. An additive choice of <energy> origin fixes the remaining constant. For $\psi=e^{-ax^4}$ with $a>0$ and $V(0)=0$, it gives $E=0$ and $V=(\hbar^2/2m)(16a^2x^6-12ax^2)$. At a node the quotient must instead be interpreted by a limit; an arbitrary trial <wavefunction> can produce singularities rather than a regular potential.

= Classically forbidden region
{parent=Time-independent Schrödinger equation}
{title2=$E<V(x)$}

A <classically forbidden region> would require negative classical <kinetic energy>, but a quantum <wavefunction> need not vanish there. For constant <potential energy>, it is $Ae^{qx}+Be^{-qx}$ with $q=\sqrt{2m(V-E)}/\hbar$. Boundary matching determines both coefficients. Beside a hard wall at $D$, a nonzero forbidden-region solution is proportional to $\sinh[q(D-x)]$, and therefore has no interior <nodes>. Across a finite barrier, both exponentials are generally needed; a pure decaying exponential is appropriate only when the other boundary condition permits it.

= Classically allowed region
{parent=Time-independent Schrödinger equation}
{title2=$E>V(x)$}

For a particle of fixed <energy> $E$, a <classically allowed region> has positive classical <kinetic energy> $E-V(x)$. For a constant <potential energy>, the stationary <wavefunction> is a combination of $e^{ikx}$ and $e^{-ikx}$, where $k=\sqrt{2m(E-V)}/\hbar$ is the <wavenumber>. In a varying <potential energy> this local value underlies the <WKB approximation> away from turning points.

= Wavefunction matching at a finite potential step
{parent=Time-independent Schrödinger equation}
{title2=$\psi(a^-)=\psi(a^+),\quad\psi'(a^-)=\psi'(a^+)$}

For a finite jump in a one-dimensional <potential energy>, with constant <mass> and no <delta potential>, both the <wavefunction> and its first spatial <derivative> are continuous. Integrating the <time-independent Schrödinger equation> across a vanishing interval makes the jump in $\psi'$ vanish, since the integral of $(V-E)\psi$ vanishes. A jump in $\psi$ itself would produce a derivative of a <Dirac delta function> in $\psi''$, with no matching term in the equation. The second <derivative> can jump. This matching allows transmission and <quantum tunnelling>; a finite step is not a hard wall.

= Bound-state matching in a symmetric stepped well
{parent=Wavefunction matching at a finite potential step}

In an even, piecewise constant potential, integrate outward from the centre with either even <boundary conditions> $\psi'(0)=0$ or odd <boundary conditions> $\psi(0)=0$. Match the <wavefunction> and its <derivative> at every finite step and impose <exponential decay> outside. The resulting discrete <energies> alternate in <parity>, with the fourth <bound state> odd and containing three <nodes of a wavefunction>. There is no hard-wall zero at a finite step; the second <derivative> changes according to $\psi''=2m(V-E)\psi/\hbar^2$.

= Quantum quarter-wave matching layer
{parent=Wavefunction matching at a finite potential step}
{title2=$k_1^2=k_0k_2,\quad k_1a=\pi/2$}

For equal-mass one-dimensional scattering between regions with real wavenumbers $k_0>k_2>0$, insert a layer of width $a$ and wavenumber $k_1$. For a purely right-moving incident wave of amplitude $A$ and no reflection, continuity at the first edge gives interior amplitudes $C=A(1+k_0/k_1)/2$ and $D=A(1-k_0/k_1)/2$. If $k_1a=\pi/2$, continuity at the second edge gives a transmitted edge amplitude $F=i(k_0/k_1)A$ and the matching condition $k_1^2=k_0k_2$. Thus $F/A=i\sqrt{k_0/k_2}$ and the transmitted <probability current> equals the incident current, despite their unequal amplitudes.

For potentials $0,V_1,V_2$ with $E>V_2>V_1>0$, the condition is $(E-V_1)^2=E(E-V_2)$, or $E=V_1^2/(2V_1-V_2)$, requiring $V_2/2<V_1<V_2$. It is the quantum-wave counterpart of a <quarter-wave impedance transformer>: the intermediate wavenumber is the geometric mean and the two reflected amplitudes cancel.

A Schrödinger operator is a differential operator of the form
$$
H=-a\Delta+V,
\qquad a>0,
$$
where $\Delta$ is the <Laplace operator> and $V$ is a <potential function>. Its <eigenvalue problem> is the <time-independent Schrodinger equation>.

A <self-adjoint operator> of this form with $a=\hbar^2/(2m)$ supplies the <Quantum Hamiltonian> of a particle in the potential $V$.

Matching a wavefunction and its derivative across finite potential discontinuities, together with decay or wall conditions, permits only discrete bound-state energies.

= Madelung equations
{c}
{parent=Time-dependent Schrodinger equation}
{wiki}
{title2=$\psi=R e^{iS}$}

Writing the <wavefunction> in modulus and dimensionless <quantum phase> gives a <probability continuity equation> and a <Hamilton-Jacobi equation> with a <quantum potential>:
$$
\partial_t(R^2)+\nabla\cdot\left(R^2\frac\hbar m\nabla S\right)=0,\qquad
\hbar S_t+\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}|\nabla S|^2+V-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\nabla^2R}{R}=0.
$$
The phase equations hold locally away from <wavefunction> nodes. They follow by equating real and imaginary parts of the <time-dependent Schrödinger equation>.

Two bound-state solutions of the same one-dimensional time-independent <Schrodinger equation> have constant <Wronskian>. Their decay at infinity makes this constant zero, so the two solutions are linearly dependent. Every bound-state energy is therefore <nondegenerate energy eigenvalue>[nondegenerate].

= Tidal bore
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{wiki}

An incoming tide can form a moving surge that propagates upstream against a river's current. A discontinuous idealization is a <shock wave> of the <shallow water equations>, while weaker or dispersive bores can have an undular wave train. A boundary overtaking upstream-propagating <characteristic curves> warns that a smoothly attached quasi-steady description may fail; the appropriate discontinuity must satisfy the conservative jump conditions of the chosen model.

= Saint-Venant equation
{c}
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{title2=$h_t+(hu)_x=0,\quad u_t+uu_x=-g\eta_x-\tau_b/(\rho h)$}

The one-dimensional open-channel <shallow water equations> express conservation of water volume and depth-averaged <momentum>. Here $h$ is <water depth>, $u$ the mean <velocity>, $\eta$ the free-surface elevation and $\tau_b$ the resisting bed <shear stress>. The elevation includes the bed profile, so its gradient already includes gravitational driving by bed slope. A constant volume flux in space and time forces $h_t=0$ through the first equation; an unsteady reduction that imposes that flux while retaining momentum relaxation is consequently an approximation to this full system.

= Constant-flux river momentum relaxation
{parent=Saint-Venant equation}
{title2=$u_t+[Q/(F^2u^2)-u]u_X=(1-u^3/Q)/(\varepsilon F^2)$}

This scalar <quasilinear partial differential equation> results by imposing $h=Q/u$ in a frictional river momentum equation and measuring $X$ upstream. It retains a relaxation toward $u_0=Q^{1/3}$ and carries information at speed $c(u)=Q/(F^2u^2)-u$. Near equilibrium the relaxation time is $\varepsilon F^2u_0/3$. A slowly moving downstream boundary can generate an attached <boundary layer> when $c(u)$ exceeds its speed throughout the relaxation. The model retains momentum dynamics only: exactly constant flux in both original <Saint-Venant equations> would instead force time-independent depth.

= Cutoff frequency in an exponentially widening shallow-water channel
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{title2=$\omega_c=\mu\sqrt{gH}/2$}

In a quasi-one-dimensional inviscid channel with depth $H$ and width $b(x)=b_0e^{\mu x}$, the linear <shallow water equations> are $\eta_t+(H/b)(bu)_x=0$ and $u_t+g\eta_x=0$. Combining them gives $\eta_{tt}=gH(\eta_{xx}+\mu\eta_x)$.

For a time dependence $e^{i\omega t}$, write the elevation amplitude as $N(x)=e^{-\mu x/2}V(x)$. The spatial equation becomes
$$
V''+\left(\frac{\omega^2}{gH}-\frac{\mu^2}4\right)V=0.
$$
There is a travelling spatial phase only when the bracket is positive, so the <cutoff frequency> is $\omega_c=\mu\sqrt{gH}/2$. Above it, an outgoing wave has a phase proportional to $\omega t-kx$ and amplitude proportional to $e^{-\mu x/2}$; below it the spatial response is evanescent. Geometric widening introduces the cutoff even though a constant-width channel has the nondispersive speed $\sqrt{gH}$.

= Wall influence on a finite-pool dry-bed dam break
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{title2=$x_b(t)=2c_0t-3a(c_0t/a)^{1/3}$}

A flat pool of depth $h_0$ between a wall at $-a$ and a removed dam at zero initially develops the <shallow water equations> rarefaction $u=2(c_0+x/t)/3$, $c=(2c_0-x/t)/3$, where $c_0=\sqrt{gh_0}$. It has $Q_+=u+2c=2c_0$ and front $x=2c_0t$. Its right-running characteristics satisfy $x_+=2c_0t+Ct^{1/3}$, obtained by integrating $x_+'-x_+/(3t)=4c_0/3$.

The first rarefaction signal reaches the wall at $(-a,a/c_0)$. The right-running characteristic through that event is the displayed curve. To its right the original fan remains valid; to its left reflected wall data alter the invariant. Continuing the original fan to the wall after that time would give nonzero wall velocity, contradicting impermeability. This is a concrete <domain of dependence> calculation for a nonlinear hyperbolic system.

= Smooth upstream tidal-wave admissibility
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{title2=$1<H\leq4,\quad F<(H-1)/(H+H^{3/2})$}

For frictional wide-channel <shallow water equations>, a smooth increasing <wave> connecting $(h,u)=(1,1)$ to <water depth> $H>1$ and moving upstream has $c=(H^{3/2}+1)/(H-1)$ and $h'=[h^3-(1+c-ch)^2]/[h^3-(1+c)^2F^2]$. Positivity of the denominator at the upstream endpoint requires $F<1/(1+c)$. For the numerator put $r=\sqrt h$, $R=\sqrt H$: its nontrivial factor is $(r+1)(r-R)(r-R/(R-1))$. The endpoint at $H$ is the first positive downstream zero exactly when $R\leq2$, giving the amplitude restriction. At $H=4$ that zero is double; otherwise the profile cannot cross an intervening equilibrium.

= Shallow-water shock into stationary unit-depth water
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{title2=$s=\sqrt{h(h+1)/2},\quad u=(h-1)\sqrt{(h+1)/(2h)}$}

For the dimensionless <shallow water equations> with gravity coefficient one, let the left state be $(h,u)$, $h>0$, and the right state $(1,0)$. The <Rankine-Hugoniot conditions> are $s(h-1)=hu$ and $shu=hu^2+(h^2-1)/2$. For $h\ne1$ and $s>0$, eliminating $u$ gives the displayed formulas. The <characteristic speeds> are $u\pm\sqrt h$ and $\pm1$. Because $u-\sqrt h<s$ always, the positive branch can only be an admissible family-plus shock. Its remaining <Lax shock admissibility conditions>, $1<s<u+\sqrt h$, both reduce to $h>1$. At $h=1$ there is no jump and the labelled interface has no determined shock speed.

= Variable-width shallow-water characteristics
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{title2=$D_\pm(u\pm2c)=\mp cu\,b'/b$}

For a rectangular channel with stationary width $b(x)$ and constant <reduced gravity> $g'$, <volume conservation> and <momentum conservation> give $h_t+uh_x+hu_x=-hu b'/b$ and $u_t+uu_x+g'h_x=0$. With $c=\sqrt{g'h}$ the <characteristic curves> have speeds $u\pm c$. The combinations $u\pm2c$ obey the displayed compatibility equations; they are <Riemann invariants> only when the width source term vanishes.

= Boundary-driven shallow-water wave steepening
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{title2=$x_b=2gH/(3u_b'(0))$}

For an outgoing <simple wave> into water initially at rest at depth $H$, the incoming <Riemann invariant> is $u-2\sqrt{gh}=-2\sqrt{gH}$. Thus $\sqrt{gh}=\sqrt{gH}+u/2$ and the outgoing <characteristic speed> is $v=\sqrt{gH}+3u/2$. A boundary characteristic emitted at time $s$ is $x=v(s)(t-s)$. Its envelope satisfies $t=s+v/v'$ and $x=v^2/v'$. At a zero-velocity onset with positive acceleration, the leading-edge intersection is the displayed distance. Later <characteristic curves> can form a shock earlier elsewhere in a sufficiently strong waveform.

= Momentum correction for lateral mass addition
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{title2=$uu_x=-g(h+H)_x+(Q_x/A)(u_a-u)$}

In a sectionally uniform flow, a volume source $Q_x$ per downstream length brings axial velocity $u_a$. <Momentum conservation> gives the displayed source correction, with $A$ the wetted area. The head derivative is $[h+H+u^2/(2g)]_x=Q_x(u_a-u)/(gA)$. Removing fluid at the local velocity preserves the head in this approximation. Adding fluid at rest does not: its acceleration reduces that head. The volume source alone cannot specify the momentum exchange without a velocity assumption.

= Shallow-water specific energy
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{title2=$E=h+u^2/(2g)$}

For a steady rectangular-channel flow with discharge $Q=bhu$, the <shallow-water specific energy> relative to the bed is $E=h+Q^2/(2gb^2h^2)$. At fixed discharge and width, $E_h=1-F^2$, where the <Froude number> satisfies $F^2=Q^2/(gb^2h^3)$. The minimum is $E_c=3h_c/2$, with $h_c=(Q^2/(gb^2))^{1/3}$. Without dissipation, $E+H$ is the conserved <Bernoulli equation> head for bed elevation $H$.

= Variable-section channel characteristics
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{title2=$c^2=gS/S_h$}

For cross-sectional area $S(x,h)$ and cross-sectionally uniform velocity, <volume conservation> gives $S_h(h_t+uh_x)+Su_x+uS_x=0$, while hydrostatic inviscid momentum gives $u_t+uu_x+gh_x=0$. Their <characteristic speeds> are $u\pm c$. With $D_\pm=\partial_t+(u\pm c)\partial_x$, the robust compatibility equation is $(g/c)D_\pm h\pm D_\pm u=-ucS_x/S$. Replacing $D_\pm h$ by $D_\pm c/c_h$ requires $c_x=0$ at fixed height; otherwise an explicit geometry term remains.

= Standing modes in an exponentially tapered channel
{parent=Variable-section channel characteristics}
{title2=$k_n^2=(n\pi/L)^2+\beta^2/4$}

For constant long-wave speed and $S_0\propto e^{\beta x}$, a standing free-surface amplitude obeys $\eta''+\beta\eta'+k^2\eta=0$. The transformation $\eta=e^{-\beta x/2}Y$ gives $Y''+(k^2-\beta^2/4)Y=0$. Impermeable ends at $0,L$ impose $\eta'=0$, yielding the displayed nonzero-frequency eigenvalues and $\eta_n\propto e^{-\beta x/2}[\cos(n\pi x/L)+\beta L\sin(n\pi x/L)/(2n\pi)]$. A separate zero-frequency mode changes the uniform water level.

= Hydraulic jump
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{wiki}

A hydraulic jump connects shallow <supercritical flow> to deeper <subcritical flow> while conserving discharge and momentum and dissipating mechanical energy. Its <Rankine-Hugoniot condition> uses the channel's actual area and integrated <hydrostatic pressure>, which depend on cross-sectional geometry.

= Hydraulic jump head loss
{parent=Hydraulic jump}
{title2=$\Delta H=\frac{(h_2-h_1)^3}{4h_1h_2}$}

In a stationary rectangular-channel <hydraulic jump>, orient the discharge $q=hu>0$ from side 1 to side 2. <Momentum conservation> gives $q^2=gh_1h_2(h_1+h_2)/2$ for unequal depths. The specific mechanical <energy> is $B=u^2/2+gh$, so
$$
B_1-B_2=\frac{g(h_2-h_1)^3}{4h_1h_2}.
$$
Dividing by $g$ gives the displayed loss of head, measured as a length. The mechanical <energy flux> lost is $\rho q(B_1-B_2)$, so a dissipative jump must have $h_2>h_1$. With $R=h_2/h_1>1$, the squared <Froude numbers> are $R(1+R)/2>1$ upstream and $(1+R)/(2R^2)<1$ downstream. Thus the <shallow water equations> connect <supercritical flow> to <subcritical flow> through the admissible jump.

= Hydraulic bore
{parent=Hydraulic jump}

A hydraulic bore is a propagating abrupt depth change described as a moving <hydraulic jump>. In the <shock frame>, an admissible rightward through-flow goes from <supercritical flow> to <subcritical flow>, with the appropriate <characteristic curves> entering the jump.

= Piston-driven shallow-water bore
{parent=Hydraulic bore}
{title2=$F^2v^2=1+\frac{3}{2(v-1)}+\frac1{2(v-1)^2}$}

A wall moving at constant <speed> $U>0$ into stationary water of depth $h_0$ produces a <hydraulic bore> of speed $V>U$ in the uniform-state <shallow water equations> model. Behind the bore the fluid <velocity> equals $U$ and its depth $H$ obeys $H/h_0=V/(V-U)$. Put $F=U/\sqrt{gh_0}$ and $v=V/U$. The moving <Rankine-Hugoniot conditions> give $F^2v=[(H/h_0)^2-1]/2$, hence the displayed equation for $v>1$. Its left side is a <strictly increasing function> from $F^2$ to infinity, while its right side is a <strictly decreasing function> from infinity to one. The <intermediate value theorem> therefore gives exactly one admissible $V$ for every $F>0$.

= Prismatic-channel shallow water equations
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{title2=$A_t+(Au)_x=0,\quad(Au)_t+(Au^2+P)_x=0$}

In a channel whose transverse width $b(z)$ does not depend on downstream position, the wetted area is $A(h)=\int_0^h b(z)\,dz$ and the integrated <hydrostatic pressure> force divided by density is $P(h)=g\int_0^h(h-z)b(z)\,dz$. With cross-sectionally uniform velocity, <volume conservation> and <momentum conservation> give the displayed <shallow water equations>. Since $P'(h)=gA(h)$, the two <characteristic speeds> are $u\pm c$ with $c^2=gA/A'$. For $b(z)=Cz^p$, $c^2=gh/(p+1)$ and the <Riemann invariants> are $u\pm2(p+1)c$.

= Dry-bed dam break with cubic wetted area
{parent=Prismatic-channel shallow water equations}
{title2=$c^2=gh/3,\quad J_\pm=u\pm6c$}

A channel with width $b(z)=\alpha z^2$ has wetted area $A=\alpha h^3/3$. Its inviscid <shallow water equations> have characteristic speeds $u\pm c$ and the displayed <Riemann invariants>. Releasing a reservoir of depth $H$ at rest into a dry channel gives a left-family <rarefaction wave>: $c=(6c_0-x/t)/7$, $u=6(c_0+x/t)/7$, $h/H=[(6-x/(c_0t))/7]^2$ between $x=-c_0t$ and $x=6c_0t$, where $c_0=\sqrt{gH/3}$. This width law differs from a geometrically parabolic trough, whose width is proportional to $\sqrt z$.

= Finite-reservoir reflection of a cubic-area dam-break rarefaction
{parent=Dry-bed dam break with cubic wetted area}
{title2=$x_r=6c_0t-7c_0t_1^{2/7}t^{5/7},\quad t_1=\ell/c_0$}

An impermeable wall at $x=-\ell$ first receives the upstream-moving head at $t_1$. The leading downstream <characteristic curve> of the reflected adjustment obeys $\dot x=u+c=(12c_0+5x/t)/7$ in the unmodified <rarefaction wave>, giving the displayed curve. It stays behind the inviscid dry tip $x_f=6c_0t$ at every finite time. The finite reservoir drains and its upstream depth decreases, while only the region behind $x_r$ has yet received the wall signal. Bottom drag or other tip physics can change the front law.

= Triangular-channel dry-bed dam break
{parent=Prismatic-channel shallow water equations}
{title2=$h/h_0=[(4-x/(c_0t))/5]^2,\quad c_0=\sqrt{gh_0/2}$}

In a prismatic triangular channel with wetted area proportional to $h^2$, the <Riemann invariants> are $u\pm4c$, with $c^2=gh/2$. Releasing water initially at rest into a dry channel produces a <rarefaction wave> with $u+4c=4c_0$ and $x/t=u-c$. Hence $c=(4c_0-x/t)/5$, $u=4(c_0+x/t)/5$ and the displayed depth profile for $-c_0t<x<4c_0t$. The dry front advances at $4c_0$ before finite-reservoir or varying-channel effects become important.

= Parabolic-channel dry-bed dam break
{parent=Prismatic-channel shallow water equations}
{title2=$h/H=(3-x/(c_0t))^2/16$}

For water initially at rest at depth $H$ on one side of a dam and a dry bed on the other, a channel with $b(z)=C\sqrt z$ has $c_0=\sqrt{2gH/3}$. The centered <rarefaction wave> has $u+3c=3c_0$ and $x/t=u-c$. Thus $c=(3c_0-x/t)/4$ and $u=3(c_0+x/t)/4$ within $-c_0t<x<3c_0t$. The reservoir remains undisturbed to the left and the bed dry to the right. This is an outer <shallow-water approximation> before finite-reservoir boundary signals return.

= Shallow-water dispersion relation
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{title2=$\omega^2=gh\kappa^2$}

For linear surface gravity waves with wavelength much larger than the depth, $\omega^2=gh\kappa^2$. It follows from $\omega^2=g\kappa\tanh(\kappa h)$ when $\kappa h\ll1$, and the wave speed is $\sqrt{gh}$.

= Rigid-lid approximation
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{title2=$\nabla_h\cdot\mathbf u=0\quad(H=\mathrm{constant})$}

= Rigid lid
{synonym}

The rigid-lid approximation suppresses <free surface> displacement in a fluid layer while retaining a variable pressure that enforces the mass constraint. At constant depth it gives horizontally nondivergent motion. In a one-layer <quasi-geostrophic approximation>, removing free-surface stretching eliminates the $-\psi/R_D^2$ term from <shallow-water quasi-geostrophic potential vorticity>. This is also the small-horizontal-scale limit compared with the <Rossby deformation radius>, when that term is negligible rather than exactly absent.

= Rigid-lid pressure in two-layer flow
{parent=Rigid-lid approximation}
{title2=$\chi=f_0(\psi_1-\psi_2)/g\prime$}

The <rigid-lid approximation> removes external surface displacement from volume conservation while retaining its pressure as a constraint multiplier. It does not mean zero horizontal pressure gradient. In two-layer geostrophic flow, $f_0\psi_1=\Pi$ and $f_0\psi_2=\Pi-g\prime\chi$, so the upper-layer depth anomaly is $\chi=f_0(\psi_1-\psi_2)/g\prime$. This pressure difference supplies internal potential-vorticity coupling.

= Variable-buoyancy shallow water equations
{parent=Shallow water equations}

A vertically mixed <gravity current> with horizontally varying <reduced gravity> $b$ obeys $h_t+(hu)_x=0$ and $(hu)_t+(hu^2+bh^2/2)_x=0$. Consequently the <material acceleration> is $Du/Dt=-b h_x-hb_x/2$. Advected density variables add repeated contact <characteristic curves> of speed $u$ to the gravity-wave <characteristic curves> of speeds $u\pm\sqrt{bh}$.

The shallow-water approximation applies when a fluid layer's vertical scale is much smaller than its horizontal scale. Vertical acceleration is then negligible to leading order, pressure is <hydrostatic pressure>, and the horizontal velocity can be represented by a depth average.

A <seiche> is a standing oscillation of a body of water. Its <natural frequencies> depend on the basin geometry, <water depth> and the boundary conditions at its closed walls or open connections.

= Quarter-wave basin resonance
{parent=Seiche}

An ideal uniform shallow basin, closed at one end and held at zero displacement at its open end, has its lowest <standing wave> with a displacement <node> at the opening and an <antinode> at the closed end. Its <wavelength> is four times its length. Real mouths need not satisfy this perfectly reflecting idealization.

= Linearized shallow water equations
{parent=Shallow water equations}

For a homogeneous layer of mean depth $H_0$ on an f-plane, linearization about rest gives
$$
u_t-fv=-g\eta_x,\qquad
v_t+fu=-g\eta_y,\qquad
\eta_t+H_0(u_x+v_y)=0.
$$

= Variable-Coriolis shallow-water height equation
{parent=Linearized shallow water equations}
{title2=$\mathcal F_y=\partial_t^2+f(y)^2$}

On a <beta plane> with $f=f_0+\beta y$, set $\mathcal F_y=\partial_t^2+f(y)^2$. Exact elimination of the <depth-integrated shallow-water transports> gives
$$
\mathcal F_y\left[\nabla_h^2\eta-\frac{\mathcal F_y\eta}{c^2}\right]_t=\beta(\eta_{xtt}+2f\eta_{yt}-f^2\eta_x).
$$
The essential commutator is $\partial_y(\mathcal F_yV)=\mathcal F_yV_y+2f\beta V$. Freezing undifferentiated $f$ factors to $f_0$ is a local approximation. The slow-wave limit gives the <shallow-water Rossby-wave dispersion relation> with a negative zonal phase velocity for $\beta>0$.

= Depth-integrated shallow-water transport
{parent=Linearized shallow water equations}
{title2=$(U,V)=H(u,v)$}

For a depth-independent horizontal velocity in a layer of reference depth $H$, the linear transports are $U=Hu$, $V=Hv$. They have dimensions of area per time and satisfy the surface <continuity equation> $\eta_t+U_x+V_y=0$. Their momentum equations are obtained by multiplying the depth-independent velocity equations by $H$.

= Depth independence of hydrostatic shallow-water flow
{parent=Linearized shallow water equations}

In a homogeneous layer under the <hydrostatic approximation>, the horizontal <pressure gradient> is independent of depth. The depth derivatives of velocity obey $(u_z)_t-fv_z=0$, $(v_z)_t+fu_z=0$. If the motion starts without shear, these derivatives remain zero. Hydrostatic pressure alone does not eliminate a pre-existing freely oscillating shear.

= Rotating shallow-water height equation with variable depth
{parent=Linearized shallow water equations}
{title2=$\partial_t[\eta_{tt}+f^2\eta-g\nabla_h\cdot(H\nabla_h\eta)]+fgH_y\eta_x=0$}

For linear rotating <shallow water equations> with fixed depth $H(y)$ and constant <Coriolis parameter> $f$, elimination of the horizontal velocity gives
$$
\partial_t\left[\eta_{tt}+f^2\eta-g\nabla_h\cdot(H\nabla_h\eta)\right]+fgH_y\eta_x=0.
$$
At a depth step this is interpreted distributionally, with continuity of surface elevation and normal volume flux. Because velocity elimination raises the time order, initial height alone is insufficient; the original velocity and continuity equations determine the remaining initial derivatives.

= Geostrophic balance
{parent=Shallow water equations}
{wiki}

A steady horizontal pressure gradient can balance the Coriolis force:
$$
\mathbf f\times\mathbf u=-g\nabla\eta.
$$

= Validity criteria for geostrophic balance
{parent=Geostrophic balance}
{title2=$U/(|f|L)\ll1,\quad (|f|\tau)^{-1}\ll1$}

The <Rossby number> compares advective acceleration with <Coriolis acceleration>, and the second ratio compares local time dependence with it. Both should be small when pressure and Coriolis terms dominate horizontal momentum. Surface stress, sharp curvature, small scales, rapid evolution and the near-equatorial region can invalidate the approximation. The vertical <hydrostatic equilibrium> assumption is a separate scale approximation.

= Atmospheric isobar
{parent=Geostrophic balance}

An <atmospheric isobar> is a line of equal <pressure> on a specified atmospheric map. Its spacing indicates the horizontal <pressure gradient> at that map's reference level; a closer spacing means a larger gradient for the same contour interval.

= Gradient wind
{parent=Geostrophic balance}

The <gradient wind> retains the centripetal <acceleration> of a curved flow as well as the <Coriolis force> and horizontal <pressure gradient force>. For cyclonic circular flow in the northern hemisphere, $v^2/r+fv=\rho^{-1}dp/dr$, with $r$ increasing away from the low-pressure centre.

= Anticyclonic gradient-wind branches
{parent=Gradient wind}

For a conventional Northern-Hemisphere anticyclone let $G=-p_r/\rho>0$. <Gradient wind> balance gives $V^2/r-fV+G=0$, with $V_\pm=fr[1\pm\sqrt{1-4G/(f^2r)}]/2$. The minus branch approaches the <geostrophic wind> at weak forcing; the plus branch is anomalous. Reality requires $G\le f^2r/4$, so a smooth central pressure maximum must have a radial pressure force vanishing at least linearly toward its centre.

= Geostrophic wind
{parent=Geostrophic balance}
{title2=$\mathbf u_g=(\rho f)^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf z}\times\nabla_h p$}

The <geostrophic wind> is the horizontal flow for which the <pressure gradient force> balances the <Coriolis force>. It runs along <atmospheric isobars> and has speed proportional to their <pressure gradient>, at fixed <density> and nonzero <Coriolis parameter>. Curvature, <friction> and small spatial scales can invalidate that approximation.

= Geostrophic wind with linear drag
{parent=Geostrophic wind}
{title2=$\mathbf u=[f\hat{\mathbf z}\times\nabla_hp-r\nabla_hp]/[\rho(r^2+f^2)]$}

A steady horizontal parcel with drag acceleration $-r\mathbf u$ obeys $0=-\nabla_hp/\rho-f\hat{\mathbf z}\times\mathbf u-r\mathbf u$. Inverting the horizontal rotation-plus-drag matrix gives the title. For $r>0$, the velocity has a component toward lower pressure and a component along <atmospheric isobars>. For $p_x=0$, $f>0$, $p_y>0$, both $u,v$ are negative. The cross-isobar angle is $\arctan(r/f)$, and the speed is smaller than the corresponding frictionless wind. A vertically resolved turbulent stress is not generally equivalent to one constant $r$.

= Pressure work in a steady cross-isobaric wind
{parent=Geostrophic wind with linear drag}
{title2=$-\rho^{-1}\mathbf u\cdot\nabla_hp=r|\mathbf u|^2$}

Dot the steady linear-drag momentum equation with the velocity. The <Coriolis force does no work>, so pressure-gradient work balances frictional dissipation. In a strictly geostrophic flow the wind is along isobars and pressure work vanishes. Cross-isobar motion supplies the positive pressure work needed to maintain a dissipative equilibrium.

= Meridional-pressure-gradient geostrophic wind
{parent=Geostrophic wind}
{title2=$u_g=-p_y/(\rho f),\quad v_g=0$}

With $x$ eastward and $y$ northward, a purely meridional <pressure gradient> gives the velocity in the title. For $f>0$, pressure increasing northward gives westward wind, leaving lower pressure to the wind's left. Reversing the pressure gradient reverses the velocity. At a fixed gradient and density the speed scales as $1/|\sin\phi|$; the formal equatorial divergence signals breakdown of <geostrophic balance>, rather than a real infinite wind.

= Conservation law
{parent=Mathematical physics}
{wiki}

A conservation law equates local time change of a density with the divergence of its flux.

= Entropy condition
{parent=Conservation law}
{title2=$\partial_t\eta(u)+\partial_xQ(u)\leq0$}

For a scalar <conservation law> $u_t+q(u)_x=0$, an entropy condition restricts its <weak solutions> to physically admissible ones. For every convex entropy function $\eta$, define an entropy flux by $Q'=\eta'q'$. The displayed inequality is imposed in the distributional sense. Positive <diffusion> explains this choice: for $u_t+q(u)_x=\epsilon[D(u)u_x]_x$ with $D>0$, the entropy balance is $\eta(u)_t+Q(u)_x=\epsilon[\eta'(u)D(u)u_x]_x-\epsilon\eta''(u)D(u)u_x^2$. The last term is nonpositive, and an appropriate vanishing-diffusion limit yields the entropy inequality. For a strictly concave flux and a single <shock>, this selects states with incoming <characteristics>, rather than a discontinuity whose characteristics spread outward.

= Flux
{parent=Conservation law}
{wiki}

A flux records transport of a quantity through a surface per unit time. A flux density $\mathbf j$ gives total transport rate $\int_S\mathbf j\cdot\mathbf n\,dS$, where $\mathbf n$ is the unit <normal vector>. A <conservation law> relates its <divergence> to the local change in the quantity's density.

= Fick's laws
{parent=Flux}
{c}
{wiki}

Fick's laws describe transport down a concentration <gradient> and the resulting change of concentration with time. Combining <Fick's first law> with a <conservation law> gives the <diffusion equation>.

= Diffusive flux
{parent=Flux}
{title2=$\mathbf j=-D\nabla u$}

A diffusive flux transports a density $u$ down its <gradient> according to <Fick's first law>. For scalar <diffusion coefficient> $D>0$, $\mathbf j=-D\nabla u$ and the <conservation law> $u_t+\operatorname{div}\mathbf j=0$ gives a <diffusion equation>. If $D$ depends on $u$, this becomes a <nonlinear diffusion equation>.

= Local conservation law
{parent=Conservation law}
{title2=$\partial_t\rho+\partial_x j=0$}

A density and flux satisfy a differential balance whose spatial integral is conserved when the boundary flux vanishes. Rapid decay provides this boundary condition for dispersive equations on the real line.

= Volume conservation
{parent=Conservation law}

Volume conservation states that the volume of incompressible material can change only through boundary fluxes or specified sources and sinks. In a one-dimensional shallow layer of depth $h$ and depth-averaged speed $u$, it has the form $h_t+(hu)_x=s$.

= Mass flux
{parent=Conservation law}

Mass flux is the <mass> crossing a unit area per unit time. In a one-dimensional flow it is $\rho u$. Integrating its normal component over a surface gives the <mass flow rate>.

= Mass loss rate
{parent=Mass flux}
{title2=$\dot M=-dM_\star/dt$}

The mass loss rate is the positive rate at which a body loses mass. For a steady spherical <stellar wind>, the <continuity equation> gives $\dot M=4\pi r^2\rho u_r$, constant across spheres enclosing the source. It is positive for outward radial flow; an accretion convention often uses a separate positive inward rate.

= Momentum flux
{parent=Conservation law}

Momentum flux includes advected momentum and stress. In a one-dimensional inviscid flow it is $p+\rho u^2$.

= Kinetic energy flux of a uniform jet
{parent=Momentum flux}
{title2=$\dot K=\rho Au^3/2$}

A steady uniform incompressible jet has <mass flow rate> $\rho Au$, <momentum flux> $\rho Au^2$ and kinetic-energy flux $\rho Au^3/2$. These use the area and velocity at the same cross section. A nonuniform profile instead requires integrating powers of local speed; using mean speed in a cubic flux without a profile correction is generally wrong.

= Energy flux
{parent=Conservation law}

Energy flux is the energy crossing a unit area per unit time. For a one-dimensional inviscid gas with internal-energy density $W$, it is
$$
u\left(p+W+\frac12\rho u^2\right).
$$

The continuity equation expresses local conservation as time change plus flux divergence equal to zero.

<Salinity> measures dissolved salt content. A transport model must specify its units; salt mass fraction is one convenient convention. = Standing wave
{parent=Mathematical physics}
{wiki}

A <standing wave> results from the <superposition principle> applied to equal-<frequency>, equal-<amplitude> <waves> travelling in opposite directions. Its spatial <nodes of a standing wave> remain fixed while its <displacement> oscillates in time.

For a pure <standing wave>, the two <travelling waves> also have equal <amplitudes>: their <interference> leaves a fixed <mode shape>, rather than a profile moving along the string. Choose the position origin and time origin so that their transverse <displacements> are
$$
y_+(x,t)=a\sin(kx-\omega t),\qquad y_-(x,t)=a\sin(kx+\omega t).
$$
Here $a>0$ is each constituent's <amplitude>, $k=2\pi/\lambda$ is the <wavenumber>, $\lambda$ is the <wavelength>, and $\omega=2\pi f$ is the <angular frequency> corresponding to <frequency> $f$. Their <wave speed> is $c=\omega/k$. The <superposition principle> and the <sine addition formula> give
$$
y=y_++y_-=2a\sin(kx)\cos(\omega t).
$$
The spatial factor stays put; the temporal factor changes its size and sign. Other constant <wave phases> shift the position or time origin. Unequal constituent <amplitudes> generally leave moving <displacement> at the would-be <nodes of a standing wave>, rather than this exact factorization.<40.75 -- Standing waves on cord (UCSB Department of Physics)>[Standing waves on cord, interference explanation and sum-of-sines derivation]{ref}

For an original ideal-string example, set $L=1\,\mathrm m$, $c=20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, and choose the third <normal mode>. Then $k=3\pi\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$, $\omega=60\pi\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, and $f=30\,\mathrm{Hz}$ by <fixed-end standing wave frequencies>. With each constituent <amplitude> $a=5\,\mathrm{mm}$,
$$
y(x,t)=0.010\,\mathrm m\;\sin\left(\frac{3\pi x}{1\,\mathrm m}\right)\cos\left(\frac{60\pi t}{1\,\mathrm s}\right).
$$
Its <nodes of a standing wave> are at $x=0,1/3,2/3,1\,\mathrm m$ and its <antinodes> at $x=1/6,1/2,5/6\,\mathrm m$. These are constructed model parameters, not experimental readings.

\Image[standing-wave-phase-snapshots.png]
{title=Opposite travelling waves add to a standing third mode; permanent nodes survive every phase snapshot}

At $t=1/120\,\mathrm s$, every point has zero <displacement>, but most have nonzero transverse <velocity>: the string is crossing its straight configuration. The black dots alone mark permanent <nodes of a standing wave>. At an <antinode> the greatest transverse <speed> is $2a\omega=0.6\pi\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, much smaller than the chosen $20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ <wave speed>. One is motion of string material; the other is propagation of the constituent profiles.

The beauty of a <standing wave> is that motion reveals geometry: fixed <nodes of a standing wave> turn a moving disturbance into a spatial ruler. Their separation measures <wavelength>, and <boundary conditions> turn the continuous choice of <wavenumber> into a discrete set of <normal modes>. This is useful both for interpreting string vibrations and for understanding how a bounded <wave equation> selects its <eigenfrequencies>.

= Standing-and-travelling decomposition of a reflected string wave
{parent=Standing wave}

For real negative displacement reflection amplitude $r=-a$, with $0<a<1$, use
$$
A[\cos(kx-\omega t)-a\cos(kx+\omega t)]
=(1-a)A\cos(kx-\omega t)+2aA\sin kx\sin\omega t.
$$
This separates a travelling field and a standing field algebraically. Their individual <powers> cannot be treated as independent incident-power fractions because the two fields interfere. Reflected and transmitted <power> should instead be calculated from travelling-wave amplitudes with the appropriate <characteristic impedance> factors.

= Pinned-square surface displacement mode
{parent=Standing wave}
{title2=$k_{nm}=\pi\sqrt{n^2+m^2}/L$}

A surface displacement constrained to vanish on all four sides of an ideal square has sine factors $\sin(n\pi x/L)\sin(m\pi y/L)$, with $n,m\geq1$. The fundamental therefore has <wavenumber> $\sqrt2\pi/L$. This differs from the impermeable-wall condition in a closed tank, which permits cosine modes with a zero index. For an elastic plate a simply supported edge also supplies its bending <boundary condition>; clamping imposes a different problem.

= Two-mode string recurrence
{parent=Standing wave}
{title2=$\omega_1t,\omega_2t\in2\pi\mathbb Z$}

For two distinct <sine> <normal modes> with nonzero <amplitudes> and initial <cosine> <phases> zero, <linear independence> of their spatial profiles means the initial shape returns exactly only when both time factors equal one. A rational <frequency> ratio $\omega_2/\omega_1=p/q$ in lowest terms gives first positive recurrence $2\pi q/\omega_1$. An irrational ratio gives no exact positive recurrence, although arbitrarily close two-mode returns exist. These statements refer to the complete spatial profile, not a displacement at one observation point.

= Standing wave node spacing
{parent=Standing wave}
{title2=$\Delta x=\lambda/2$}

A <node of a standing wave> must have $y(x,t)=0$ for every time, not merely at one snapshot. For $y=2a\sin(kx)\cos(\omega t)$, with $a,k,\omega>0$, set $t=0$ to see that this requires $\sin(kx)=0$. Thus
$$
x_j=\frac{j\pi}{k}=\frac{j\lambda}{2},\qquad x_{j+1}-x_j=\frac{\lambda}{2},\qquad j\in\mathbb Z.
$$
An <antinode> instead maximizes the oscillation <amplitude> $2a|\sin(kx)|$ and lies halfway between successive <nodes of a standing wave>, at $x=(2j+1)\lambda/4$. Neighbouring <antinodes> are also separated by $\lambda/2$; the distance from a <node of a standing wave> to its nearest <antinode> is $\lambda/4$. These distances refer to the string's longitudinal coordinate, not its curved arc length.

Within one interval between <nodes of a standing wave>, all moving points have the same <oscillation phase>. Adjacent intervals have opposite signs of $\sin(kx)$, so their <phase difference> is $\pi$: one loop rises while the next falls. The <oscillation phase> at an exact <node of a standing wave> is undefined because its <amplitude> is zero. A ruler measures the spacing of the fixed pattern; it does not track a moving <wave crest>.

= Fixed-end standing wave frequencies
{parent=Standing wave}
{title2=$k_n=n\pi/L,\quad f_n=nc/(2L)$}

For the ideal uniform <wave equation on a string>, the <wave speed> is $c=\sqrt{\tau/\mu}$, where $\tau$ is constant <tension> and $\mu$ is <linear mass density>. Assume small transverse slopes, negligible bending stiffness and no <damping>. Fix both ends at zero <displacement>, giving the homogeneous <Dirichlet boundary conditions> $y(0,t)=y(L,t)=0$. Our <standing wave> already satisfies the first. For a nonzero <amplitude>, the second requires $\sin(kL)=0$, hence
$$
k_n=\frac{n\pi}{L},\qquad \lambda_n=\frac{2L}{n},\qquad \omega_n=ck_n,\qquad f_n=\frac{\omega_n}{2\pi}=\frac{nc}{2L},\qquad n=1,2,3,\ldots.
$$
The value $n=0$ gives zero <displacement> in this sine family, not a vibrating <normal mode>. Each positive $n$ fits $n$ half-<wavelengths> into the string: it has $n$ <antinodes>, $n-1$ interior <nodes of a standing wave>, and two endpoint <nodes of a standing wave>. For $L=1\,\mathrm m$ and $c=20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $f_n=10n\,\mathrm{Hz}$; the third <normal mode> has $f_3=30\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and <wavelength> $2/3\,\mathrm m$.

{title=Third-mode nodes and antinodes, with the discrete frequency spectrum of a one-metre ideal string at wave speed twenty metres per second}

The equally spaced <eigenfrequencies> follow from this string's uniform <wave speed> and fixed-end <boundary conditions>. They are not a universal spectrum for every vibrating object. Changing an endpoint constraint changes the allowed <mode shapes>; changing <tension> changes <wave speed>. At fixed $L$ and $\mu$, quadrupling $\tau$ doubles every $f_n$. The more general construction of arbitrary initial motion belongs to <normal mode> and <wave equation on a string>.

= Standing wave phase and energy
{parent=Standing wave}
{title2=$\langle P\rangle_t=0$}

A stationary <mode shape> does not imply absence of <energy>. Write $A=2a$ and $y=A\sin(kx)\cos(\omega t)$. For the ideal <wave equation on a string>, the <kinetic energy> and elastic <potential energy> per unit reference length add to
$$
e=\frac{\mu}{2}y_t^2+\frac{\tau}{2}y_x^2,
\qquad P=-\tau y_x y_t.
$$
Here $P$ is signed <power> crossing a position on the string, positive to the right; $e$ is an <energy density> per length, not per volume. Differentiation and $\mu y_{tt}=\tau y_{xx}$ give the local <conservation of energy> identity $\partial_t e+\partial_x P=0$. Substitution into $P$ gives
$$
P(x,t)=\tau A^2k\omega\sin(kx)\cos(kx)\sin(\omega t)\cos(\omega t).
$$
It can be nonzero away from a <node of a standing wave> or an <antinode>, but its time average over an <oscillation period> is zero. Energy flows locally back and forth even though this pure <standing wave> transports no net <energy> across a section over a complete cycle. At a <node of a standing wave>, $y_t=0$, so <kinetic energy> density is zero, but $y_x$ need not vanish: elastic <potential energy> density can be positive there.

For a fixed-end <normal mode>, integration over $0\leq x\leq L$ gives
$$
K(t)=\frac{\mu A^2\omega^2L}{4}\sin^2(\omega t),\qquad U(t)=\frac{\tau A^2k^2L}{4}\cos^2(\omega t),\qquad E=K+U=\frac{\mu A^2\omega^2L}{4}.
$$
The last equality uses $\tau k^2=\mu\omega^2$. At a straight-string snapshot, $U=0$ while $K=E$; at an extreme snapshot, $K=0$ while $U=E$. This is <normal-mode energy exchange>. A driven real string with <damping> requires continuing input <power> to replace lost <energy>, so a visually stationary pattern does not establish zero average supplied <power>.
