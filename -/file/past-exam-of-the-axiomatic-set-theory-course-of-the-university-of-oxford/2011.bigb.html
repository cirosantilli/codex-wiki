The <cumulative hierarchy> is the <transfinite recursion>
$$
\boxed{V_0=\varnothing,\qquad V_{\alpha+1}=\mathcal P(V_\alpha),\qquad V_\lambda=\bigcup_{\alpha<\lambda}V_\alpha\quad(\lambda\text{ a nonzero limit ordinal}).}
$$
Here $\mathcal P$ is the <power set>. The class $V=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\mathrm{Ord}}V_\alpha$ consists of all sets in <ZF>, by the <axiom of foundation> and <axiom schema of replacement>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Two facts about a <transitive set> $A$ drive the <transfinite induction>. First, $A\subseteq\mathcal P(A)$: every $x\in A$ is a subset of $A$. Second, $\mathcal P(A)$ is a <transitive set>: if $x\in y\in\mathcal P(A)$, then $x\in A$, hence $x\subseteq A$, hence $x\in\mathcal P(A)$.

Starting with $V_0=\varnothing$, these facts prove the successor step. At a <limit ordinal> $\lambda$, the union of the earlier increasing <transitive sets> is transitive: if $x\in y\in V_\lambda$, some $\alpha<\lambda$ contains $y$ in $V_\alpha$, and transitivity puts $x$ there too. The union contains every earlier stage. Thus the induction gives both transitivity and $V_\beta\subseteq V_\alpha$ whenever $\beta\leq\alpha$.

Finally $V_\alpha\subseteq V_\alpha$, so $V_\alpha\in\mathcal P(V_\alpha)=V_{\alpha+1}$. The <axiom of foundation> gives $V_\alpha\notin V_\alpha$. This witnesses strictness:
$$
\boxed{V_\alpha\text{ is transitive},\qquad V_\alpha\subsetneq V_{\alpha+1}.}
$$
This is the <transitivity and strict growth of the cumulative hierarchy>.

Fix $x\in V_\delta$. Since $\delta$ is a <limit ordinal>, choose $\beta<\delta$ with $x\in V_\beta$. Transitivity gives $x\subseteq V_\beta$. Every subset $u\subseteq x$ therefore belongs to $V_{\beta+1}=\mathcal P(V_\beta)$, and consequently
$$
\mathcal P(x)\subseteq V_{\beta+1},\qquad\mathcal P(x)\in V_{\beta+2}\subseteq V_\delta.
$$
The last inclusion uses $\beta+2<\delta$, since a finite number of successor steps remains below a <limit ordinal>. In particular every subset of $x$ is also an element of the <transitive set> $V_\delta$.

Take $y=\mathcal P(x)$. For any $u\in V_\delta$, transitivity ensures that the internal statement $u\subseteq x$ agrees with the external one. Hence
$$
(V_\delta,\in)\models\forall u\,(u\in y\leftrightarrow u\subseteq x).
$$
Such a $y\in V_\delta$ exists for every $x\in V_\delta$, so **$(V_\delta,\in)$ satisfies the <axiom of power set>**. This <power-set axiom at limit ranks> actually holds for every nonzero <limit ordinal>; the stated restriction $\delta>\omega$ is sufficient.

Every <finite ordinal> belongs to $V_\omega$, so $\omega\subseteq V_\omega$ and $\omega\in\mathcal P(V_\omega)=V_{\omega+1}$. However, $\omega\notin V_\omega$, whose elements are the <hereditarily finite sets>.

Suppose $y\in V_{\omega+1}$ were an internal <power set> of $\omega$. Internally $\omega\subseteq\omega$, and $\omega$ is an available element of the structure. Thus the <axiom of power set> would require $\omega\in y$. But $y\in\mathcal P(V_\omega)$ means $y\subseteq V_\omega$, which would put $\omega\in V_\omega$, a contradiction. Therefore
$$
\boxed{(V_{\omega+1},\in)\not\models\mathrm{PowerSet}.}
$$
This proves <power-set failure at the first rank above V omega> by an internal obstruction, including the subset $\omega$ itself.

A <bounded formula in set theory>, or $\Sigma_0$ formula in the <Lévy hierarchy>, uses only <bounded quantifiers> $\exists u\in v$ and $\forall u\in v$, with the usual Boolean connectives. A <Sigma-one formula in set theory> has the form
$$
\exists u_1\cdots\exists u_m\,\theta(\mathbf u,\mathbf x),
$$
where $\theta$ is a <bounded formula in set theory>; harmless logically equivalent rearrangements are allowed. A <Pi-one formula in set theory> is defined with a universal unbounded block instead.

A <Delta-one formula modulo ZF> is a <first-order formula> $\varphi(\mathbf x)$ for which there are such $\Sigma_1$ and $\Pi_1$ formulas $\sigma,\pi$ with
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{ZF}\vdash\forall\mathbf x\,(\varphi\leftrightarrow\sigma),\qquad\mathrm{ZF}\vdash\forall\mathbf x\,(\varphi\leftrightarrow\pi).}
$$
Equivalently, both $\varphi$ and $\neg\varphi$ have <Sigma-one formulas in set theory> provably equivalent to them in <ZF>. These are levels of the <Lévy hierarchy>, with bounds by sets.

= Solution

Begin with <bounded formulas in set theory>. Atomic membership and equality have the same interpretation on shared elements of $U_1\subseteq U_2$, each a <transitive class>. Boolean connectives preserve agreement. For a bounded quantifier with bound $a\in U_1$, every member of $a$ lies in $U_1$ by transitivity. Its quantifier range is therefore exactly the same in both structures. <Structural induction> proves <set-theoretic absoluteness> for every <bounded formula in set theory>.

A <Sigma-one formula in set theory> is upward absolute: witnesses in $U_1$ are still witnesses in $U_2$, and the bounded matrix agrees. A <Pi-one formula in set theory> is downward absolute, by negation or by restricting its universal quantifiers.

For the given <Delta-one formula modulo ZF> $\varphi$, select a $\Sigma_1$ equivalent $\sigma$ and a $\Pi_1$ equivalent $\pi$. Both structures satisfy <ZF>, so both equivalences hold in both structures. Upward absoluteness of $\sigma$ proves the forward implication, and downward absoluteness of $\pi$ proves the reverse implication. Thus <Delta-one absoluteness> gives
$$
\boxed{U_1\models\varphi(\mathbf a)\iff U_2\models\varphi(\mathbf a)\qquad(\mathbf a\in U_1^n).}
$$

There is a <bounded definition of omega>. Transitivity has the bounded definition $\operatorname{Trans}(x)\equiv\forall u\in x\,\forall v\in u\,(v\in x)$. An <ordinal> is a <transitive set> of <transitive sets> on which membership is a strict total order; this too has a <bounded formula in set theory>, since <ZF> supplies well-foundedness by the <axiom of foundation>.

Define the bounded predicate
$$
L(x)\equiv\operatorname{Ord}(x)\land x\ne\varnothing\land\forall y\in x\,\exists z\in x\,(y\in z).
$$
For an <ordinal>, this says that it is a nonzero <limit ordinal>. It follows in <ZF> that
$$
\boxed{x=\omega\iff L(x)\land\forall y\in x\,\neg L(y),}
$$
since $\omega$ is the least nonzero <limit ordinal>. The right side is a <bounded formula in set theory>; consequently it is absolute between the specified <transitive models>. This also shows directly that those models have the same $\omega$.

Use the convention that the <transitive closure> $\operatorname{TC}(x)$ is the least <transitive set> containing $x$ as a subset. In <ZF>, <transfinite recursion> and <axiom schema of replacement> construct
$$
x_0=x,\qquad x_{n+1}=\bigcup x_n,\qquad t=\bigcup_{n<\omega}x_n.
$$
Then $x\subseteq t$. If $a\in b\in t$, some $n$ has $b\in x_n$, whence $a\in x_{n+1}\subseteq t$. Thus $t$ is transitive. If another <transitive set> $s$ contains $x$, induction gives $x_n\subseteq s$ for every $n$, and hence $t\subseteq s$. This proves existence and uniqueness of the <transitive closure>.

Let $W(w)$ be the <bounded definition of omega> above. The displayed construction can be expressed as
$$
\exists w\,\exists f\,\exists r\,\bigl[W(w)\land f:w\to r\land\operatorname{ran}(f)=r\land f(0)=x\land\forall n\in w\,(f(n+1)=\bigcup f(n))\land y=\bigcup r\bigr].
$$
Here the brackets abbreviate a <bounded formula in set theory>: use the permitted bounded function and union predicates, bound the values of $f$ by $r$, and bound each successor index by $w$. Ordered-pair graph access is bounded; $\operatorname{ran}(f)=r$ says every value in $r$ occurs at an index in $w$. Any auxiliary sets used for the graph coding may be put in the initial existential block. Thus this is a <Sigma-one formula in set theory> defining $y=\operatorname{TC}(x)$.

Denote this formula by $T(x,y)$. Existence and uniqueness give the <ZF> equivalence
$$
\neg T(x,y)\iff\exists z\,(T(x,z)\land z\ne y).
$$
The right side is again $\Sigma_1$, after combining existential blocks. Consequently $T$ is a <Delta-one formula modulo ZF>, so <Delta-one absoluteness> applies. Therefore **<transitive closure is Delta-one definable> and is ZF-absolute**. If closure is instead required to contain $x$ as an element, apply exactly this construction to $\{x\}$; the absoluteness conclusion is unchanged.

Use the usual <cardinal arithmetic> with the <axiom of choice>. The <König theorem for cardinal numbers> states that for any index set $I$ and <cardinal numbers> $\kappa_i<\lambda_i$,
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i\in I}\kappa_i<\prod_{i\in I}\lambda_i.}
$$
Represent the <cardinal numbers> as <initial ordinals>. Let $A=\{(i,\alpha):i\in I,\alpha<\kappa_i\}$ and $B=\prod_{i\in I}\lambda_i$. There is an <injection> $A\to B$: send $(i,\alpha)$ to the function whose $i$th coordinate is $\alpha+1$ and whose other coordinates are zero. The unique nonzero coordinate identifies $i$, and its value identifies $\alpha$. All values are allowed because $\alpha+1\leq\kappa_i<\lambda_i$.

For any proposed <surjection> $F:A\to B$, its $i$th row has at most $\kappa_i$ elements, so
$$
\{F(i,\alpha)(i):\alpha<\kappa_i\}\ne\lambda_i.
$$
Choose $b_i$ to be the least omitted <ordinal>. The function $b=(b_i)_{i\in I}$ belongs to $B$ and differs from $F(i,\alpha)$ at coordinate $i$, for every $(i,\alpha)\in A$. Thus $F$ is not surjective. Cardinal comparison now gives the strict inequality. If $I=\varnothing$, the conclusion is $0<1$, also valid.

For an infinite <cardinal number> $\kappa$, its <cofinality> is
$$
\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)=\min\{|A|:A\subseteq\kappa,\ \sup A=\kappa\},
$$
viewing $\kappa$ as an <initial ordinal>. Equivalently it is the smallest <cardinal number> which is the domain size of a <cofinal map> into $\kappa$.

Put $\lambda=2^\kappa$ and $\mu=\operatorname{cf}(\lambda)$. Suppose $\mu\leq\kappa$, and choose cofinal <ordinals> $\alpha_i<\lambda$ for $i<\mu$. Put $a_i=|\alpha_i|<\lambda$. Their union is $\lambda$, so <infinite cardinal arithmetic> gives
$$
\lambda\leq\sum_{i<\mu}a_i\leq\lambda\mu=\lambda.
$$
Apply the <König theorem for cardinal numbers> to $a_i<a_i^+$. Each <successor cardinal> $a_i^+$ is at most $\lambda$, whence
$$
\lambda=\sum_{i<\mu}a_i<\prod_{i<\mu}a_i^+\leq\lambda^\mu\leq(2^\kappa)^\kappa=2^{\kappa\cdot\kappa}=2^\kappa=\lambda.
$$
Here the last equalities use the <currying law for cardinal exponentiation> and <square of an infinite cardinal>. This contradiction proves the <cofinality of a power-set cardinal> bound
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{cf}(2^\kappa)>\kappa.}
$$

Put $\mu=\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)$ and choose cofinal <ordinals> $\alpha_i<\kappa$, $i<\mu$. With $a_i=|\alpha_i|$, the same union comparison and <infinite cardinal arithmetic> give $\sum_{i<\mu}a_i=\kappa$. Since $a_i<\kappa$, their <successor cardinals> satisfy $a_i^+\leq\kappa$. The <König theorem for cardinal numbers> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\kappa=\sum_{i<\mu}a_i<\prod_{i<\mu}a_i^+\leq\kappa^\mu=\kappa^{\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)}.}
$$
This <König bound for the Gimel function> applies to regular and <singular cardinals> alike; choosing cofinal <ordinals> also covers successor <cardinal numbers>.

Take the <Beth number>
$$
\boxed{\kappa=\beth_\omega=\sup_{n<\omega}\beth_n,\qquad\beth_0=\aleph_0,\quad\beth_{n+1}=2^{\beth_n}.}
$$
<Cantor's theorem> makes this sequence strictly increasing. It is a countable cofinal sequence in $\kappa$, while no finite set is cofinal in the <limit ordinal> $\kappa$. Hence $\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)=\aleph_0$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{2^{\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)}=2^{\aleph_0}=\beth_1<\beth_\omega=\kappa.}
$$
Thus <Beth omega is a singular strong limit above the continuum>. No <continuum hypothesis> is needed for the example.
