= Solution
{parent=a}

A <homogeneous hyperelastic material> has a stored energy per reference <volume> $W(F)$ depending on the local <deformation gradient> but not explicitly on the material point. Its first Piola-Kirchhoff response is
$$
\boxed{\widehat T(F)=DW(F),\qquad T_{i\alpha}=\partial W/\partial F_{i\alpha}.}
$$
A superposed rigid rotation changes the observer's description without changing the energy of the <deformation>. <Material frame indifference> is therefore
$$
\boxed{W(QF)=W(F)\quad(F\in\mathbb M_+^{3\times3},\ Q\in\mathrm{SO}(3)).}
$$
This left-rotation invariance is distinct from a <material isotropy> assumption on right rotations. Differentiate it in an arbitrary matrix direction $H$:
$$
DW(QF):(QH)=DW(F):H.
$$
The <Frobenius inner product> then gives $Q^T\widehat T(QF)=\widehat T(F)$, proving $\widehat T(QF)=Q\widehat T(F)$.

For the converse, that stress covariance makes the <derivative> in $F$ of $W(QF)-W(F)$ zero. The positive-<determinant> matrices form a <connected set>, as also follows from <polar decomposition>, so this difference is a constant $c(Q)$ independent of $F$. Composition gives $c(Q_1Q_2)=c(Q_1)+c(Q_2)$. For fixed $F$, <continuity> of $W$ and <compactness> of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ make $c$ bounded. But $c(Q^n)=nc(Q)$ for every positive integer, forcing $c(Q)=0$. Thus **stress covariance and frame indifference of the energy are equivalent**. <Compactness> removes the possible additive energy constant in the converse.

= Solution
{parent=b}

For small <displacement> <gradients> write $F=I+H$. <Taylor expansion> around the stress-free reference state gives
$$
W(I+H)=W(I)+\frac12C_{i\alpha j\beta}H_{i\alpha}H_{j\beta}+o(|H|^2),
\qquad C_{i\alpha j\beta}=D^2W(I)_{i\alpha j\beta}.
$$
The linear term vanishes because $DW(I)=0$. Drop the irrelevant constant and retain the quadratic term:
$$
\boxed{W^{\mathrm{lin}}(H)=\frac12C_{i\alpha j\beta}H_{i\alpha}H_{j\beta}.}
$$
The major symmetry $\boxed{C_{i\alpha j\beta}=C_{j\beta i\alpha}}$ follows from equality of mixed second <derivatives>. For a skew matrix $K$, stress covariance along $e^{tK}$ gives
$$
\widehat T(e^{tK})=e^{tK}\widehat T(I)=0.
$$
Differentiation at zero yields $D\widehat T(I)[K]=C[K]=0$. Thus $C_{i\alpha j\beta}K_{j\beta}=0$ for every skew matrix, which forces the input minor symmetry $C_{i\alpha j\beta}=C_{i\alpha\beta j}$. Combining it with the major symmetry gives the output minor symmetry as well:
$$
\boxed{C_{i\alpha j\beta}=C_{\alpha i j\beta}=C_{i\alpha\beta j}.}
$$
These identify reference and spatial indices at $F=I$. Decompose $H=e+K$ into symmetric and skew parts. Since $C[K]=0$ and the major symmetry also eliminates the other cross term,
$$
\boxed{W^{\mathrm{lin}}(H)=\tfrac12C[e,e],\qquad e=(H+H^T)/2.}
$$
Thus infinitesimal rotations cost no energy in the quadratic approximation.

= Solution
{parent=c}

As a function of the full <deformation gradient>, use $e=\operatorname{sym}(F-I)$. Let $Q=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1)$, a proper rotation. Assumed full frame indifference would give $\widetilde W(Q)=\widetilde W(I)=0$. For $F_s=I+s(Q-I)$, its infinitesimal strain is $s\operatorname{sym}(Q-I)$. Quadratic homogeneity therefore gives
$$
\widetilde W(F_s)=s^2\widetilde W(Q)=0.
$$
Choose $s=1/4$. Then
$$
\boxed{F_s=\operatorname{diag}(1,1/2,1/2),\qquad
\det F_s=1/4\ne1,\qquad\widetilde W(F_s)=0.}
$$
The <homogeneous deformation> $y(X)=F_sX$ changes <volume> but has zero energy. This illustrates why a nondegenerate small-strain quadratic energy cannot also be imposed as a fully frame-indifferent finite-<deformation> energy.

= Solution
{parent=a}

A <deformation> maps a material point in the reference body to its current position, $y:\Omega\to\mathbb R^3$, with <deformation gradient> $F=Dy$. A <homogeneous deformation> has constant <gradient> and is affine, $y(X)=FX+b$.

If $\det F>0$, the matrix $F^TF$ is symmetric positive definite. The matrix-square-root theorem says that any such matrix has a unique symmetric positive definite square root, obtained by taking positive square roots in its orthogonal <spectral decomposition>. Set
$$
\boxed{U=(F^TF)^{1/2},\qquad R=FU^{-1}.}
$$
Then $R^TR=U^{-1}F^TFU^{-1}=I$ and $\det R=\det F/\det U>0$, so $R\in\mathrm{SO}(3)$ and $F=RU$. If also $F=\widetilde R\widetilde U$, then $F^TF=\widetilde U^2$; <uniqueness> of the positive square root gives $\widetilde U=U$ and then $\widetilde R=R$. This proves **existence and <uniqueness> of <polar decomposition>**.

The principal stretches are the positive <eigenvalues> of $U$, equivalently the <singular values> of $F$ or square roots of the <eigenvalues> of $F^TF$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

For $R>0$, differentiation gives $\partial_jR=\theta_j$ and $\partial_j\theta_i=(\delta_{ij}-\theta_i\theta_j)/R$. Hence
$$
\boxed{Dy=\frac rR(I-\theta\otimes\theta)+r'\theta\otimes\theta.}
$$
The two matrices are the <orthogonal projections> onto the tangent plane of the reference sphere and its radial line. The <gradient> multiplies the radial direction by $r'$ and each tangent direction by $r/R$. With positive $r,r'$, it is itself symmetric positive definite, so its <eigenvalues> are its principal stretches:
$$
\boxed{\lambda_1=r',\qquad\lambda_2=\lambda_3=r/R.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=c}

The <determinant> condition is $r'(r/R)^2=1$, or $r^2r'=R^2$. Integration gives $r^3=R^3+C$ and the outer boundary value fixes $C=t^3-1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{r(R)=(R^3+t^3-1)^{1/3}.}
$$
For $t>1$, $r(R)$ tends to the positive cavity radius $(t^3-1)^{1/3}$ as $R\downarrow0$. Its tangential stretches grow like that radius divided by $R$, so $|F|\to\infty$. The <deformation> is singular at the origin and is interpreted as a finite-energy Sobolev <deformation>, with a point value irrelevant to its energy.

Since $r'=R^2/r^2$ and the <determinant> penalty is zero almost everywhere,
$$
\boxed{E_{\mathrm{cav}}(t)=4\pi\int_0^1\left(\frac{R^6}{r(R)^4}+2r(R)^2\right)dR.}
$$
For fixed $t>1$, the first integrand is $O(R^6)$ near zero and the second is bounded. Thus the energy is finite despite the unbounded <gradient>. In particular the <gradient> is square integrable, as required for this competitor.

For large $t$, $r(R)/t\to1$ uniformly on $[0,1]$. The first <integral> is $O(t^{-4})$, while $\int_0^1r(R)^2dR\sim t^2$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{E_{\mathrm{cav}}(t)\sim8\pi t^2.}
$$
The spherical <volume> factor $R^2dR$ is what makes the $R^{-1}$ tangential-<gradient> singularity integrable in three dimensions.

= Solution
{parent=d}

Choose the homogeneous dilation $\boxed{r(R)=tR}$, which has the required endpoint values. For a radial field, put $J=r'r^2/R^2$. The radial energy density, after omitting its constant angular factor, is $R^2[r'^2+2r^2/R^2+(J-1)^2]$. Its Euler equation simplifies to
$$
R^2r''+2Rr'-2r+r^2J'=0.
$$
The chosen dilation has $r''=0$, $J=t^3$ constant, and satisfies it. More directly, the full first Piola-Kirchhoff response for the given energy is
$$
T(F)=2F+2(\det F-1)\operatorname{cof}F.
$$
At $F=tI$ this is $[2t+2(t^3-1)t^2]I$, constant throughout the body. Its <divergence> vanishes, so the <deformation> satisfies the full force-free equilibrium equations as well.

Its energy is
$$
\boxed{E_{\mathrm{hom}}(t)=\frac{4\pi}{3}[3t^2+(t^3-1)^2].}
$$
This grows like $(4\pi/3)t^6$, whereas the same-boundary cavitating competitor has energy asymptotic to $8\pi t^2$. Therefore **for sufficiently large $t$, the homogeneous equilibrium is not a global energy minimum in the finite-energy class admitting the cavitating <deformation>**.

The admissible class matters: the competitor from the preceding part is not continuous at the center. This comparison is in that Sobolev class, not a claim about minimization restricted to smooth cavity-free <deformations>.

= Solution
{parent=a}

An <antiplane shear>[antiplane displacement] has the form $v(x_1,x_2,x_3)=(0,0,u(x_1,x_2))$, independent of the out-of-plane coordinate. Only $e_{13}=e_{31}=u_{x_1}/2$ and $e_{23}=e_{32}=u_{x_2}/2$ are nonzero, so its strain energy per unit out-of-plane thickness is $\mu|\nabla u|^2/2$.

The equilibrium minimizes total potential energy
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E_s(u)=\int_{\omega\setminus K_s}
\left(\frac\mu2|\nabla u|^2-fu\right)dx,\qquad
u\in H^1(\omega\setminus K_s),\quad u=u_D\text{ on }\Gamma_D.}
$$
Here $f$ is the out-of-plane body-force component; the two <crack> faces have independent <traces>. For a variation $\eta$ with zero outer-boundary <trace>, stationarity gives
$$
\mu\int\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta=\int f\eta.
$$
Integrating by parts first for interior <test functions> and then for variations free on either <crack> face yields
$$
\boxed{-\mu\Delta u=f\text{ in }\omega\setminus K_s,\qquad
u=u_D\text{ on }\Gamma_D,\qquad\mu\partial_nu=0\text{ on both crack faces}.}
$$
These are the weak equilibrium equation, prescribed <displacement>, and zero <traction>. The tip behavior is selected by finite energy; it is not an additional prescribed tip value.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Use $-\pi<\theta_s<\pi$, with the <crack> along the negative radial axis. In the coefficient convention used here, the leading <crack>-tip expansion is
$$
\boxed{u^{(s)}=u_{\mathrm{reg}}+k_sR_s^{1/2}\sin(\theta_s/2),\qquad
\nabla u_{\mathrm{reg}}=o(R_s^{-1/2}).}
$$
The square-root term is harmonic and has zero angular <derivative> on both <crack> faces. Regular contributions may include a constant and bounded-<gradient> terms under smooth loading.

If $E(s)$ is the minimized elastic-plus-load potential, the <energy release rate> is $\boxed{G(s)=-dE(s)/ds}$. With this particular square-root coefficient normalization,
$$
\boxed{G(s)=\frac{\pi\mu}{4}k_s^2.}
$$
For example, the small-circle energy flux is $\int[\mathcal W n_1-\mu u_r u_{x_1}]R_s\,d\theta_s$. The singular term gives $\mathcal W=\mu k_s^2/(8R_s)$, $u_r=k_s\sin(\theta_s/2)/(2\sqrt{R_s})$ and $u_{x_1}=-k_s\sin(\theta_s/2)/(2\sqrt{R_s})$. Integration over $(-\pi,\pi)$ produces the stated factor. Other stress-intensity normalizations change that factor.

For toughness $G_c>0$, local stability against infinitesimal extension means $\boxed{G(s)\leq G_c}$, since the <derivative> of $E(s)+G_c(s+1)$ is nonnegative. Global energetic stability against every extension is the stronger condition $E(s)\leq E(r)+G_c(r-s)$ for all $r\geq s$.

In full isotropic linearized elasticity,
$$
\boxed{T^{\mathrm{lin}}=\lambda\operatorname{tr}(e)I+2\mu e.}
$$
For the antiplane field this becomes
$$
T^{\mathrm{lin}}=\mu\begin{pmatrix}0&0&u_{x_1}\\0&0&u_{x_2}\\u_{x_1}&u_{x_2}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
|T^{\mathrm{lin}}|^2=2\mu^2|\nabla u|^2.
$$
Ahead of the tip, $\theta_s=0$, the singular <gradient> is $(0,k_s/(2\sqrt{R_s}))$. Thus
$$
\boxed{|T^{\mathrm{lin}}|_{\theta_s=0}
=\frac{\mu|k_s|}{\sqrt2}R_s^{-1/2}+o(R_s^{-1/2}).}
$$
This is stronger than the printed remainder $o(R_s^{-1})$. If $k_s$ denotes a nonnegative intensity, it has the printed leading coefficient; for a signed singular coefficient its absolute value is essential because the left side is a <norm>.

= Solution
{parent=c}

For a differentiable <crack> path, the local quasistatic Griffith laws with constant positive toughness are
$$
\boxed{\dot\ell(t)\geq0,\qquad
G(\ell(t),t)\leq G_c,\qquad
\dot\ell(t)[G_c-G(\ell(t),t)]=0.}
$$
They express irreversibility, local stability, and energy balance during growth. A strictly subcritical release rate forces the <crack> to stop.

Fix a <crack> parameter $s$ and let $u(s,t)$ denote the equilibrium <displacement> with the prescribed time-dependent loads. The scalar equilibrium problem is linear and has a unique solution for its fixed Dirichlet data. Both body force and boundary <displacement> scale by $t$, so
$$
\boxed{u(s,t)=t\,u(s,1).}
$$
The minimized potential energy is quadratic under this scaling: the strain term scales by $t^2$ and so does the load term $-f(t)u(s,t)$. Hence $E(s,t)=t^2E(s,1)$ and
$$
\boxed{G(s,t)=t^2G(s,1).}
$$
Equivalently the <crack>-tip coefficient scales by $t$ and its squared energy-release formula gives the same result.

Put $s_0=\ell(0)$. Finiteness and <continuity> of $G(s,1)$ make it bounded by some finite $M\geq0$ on a neighborhood of $s_0$. <Continuity> of $\ell$ supplies a positive interval on which $\ell(t)$ stays in that neighborhood. Shorten it to $[0,t_0]$ so that $t_0^2M<G_c$. Then $G(\ell(t),t)\leq t_0^2M<G_c$, and complementarity forces $\dot\ell(t)=0$ throughout. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\ell(t)=\ell(0)\quad(0\leq t\leq t_0).}
$$
No differentiating an unknown <crack> path or assuming its length already constant is needed; its <continuity> first gives the local release-rate bound.
