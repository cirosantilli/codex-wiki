= Solution
{parent=a}

A <homogeneous hyperelastic material> has a <strain energy density> per unit reference <volume> $W(F)$ depending on the <deformation gradient>, with no explicit dependence on the <reference configuration>. For orientation-preserving gradients $F\in GL^+(3)$, its first <Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor> is the <derivative>:
$$
\boxed{\widehat T(F)=D_FW(F),\qquad T_{iA}=\frac{\partial W}{\partial F_{iA}}(F).}
$$
Equivalently the infinitesimal stored-energy change is $DW(F)[H]=T:H$. Reference and spatial indices are distinguished here by upper- and lowercase index letters.

The <material symmetry group> may be written $G_W=\{H\in GL^+(3):W(FH)=W(F)\text{ for every }F\in GL^+(3)\}$. A volume-preserving relabelling convention restricts the candidate $H$ to $SL(3)$; the <material isotropy> criterion is the same. The material has **<material isotropy>** when $SO(3)\subseteq G_W$, meaning $W(FQ)=W(F)$ for every proper <rotation> $Q$. If only rotational <symmetries> are included in the definition of the group, <material isotropy> means that group equals $SO(3)$. This right-rotation invariance is distinct from <material frame indifference>, the left-rotation invariance $W(QF)=W(F)$.

The <polar decomposition in continuum mechanics> gives the unique factorizations
$$
\boxed{F=RU=VR,\qquad R\in SO(3),\quad U=(F^TF)^{1/2},\quad V=(FF^T)^{1/2},}
$$
where $U,V$ are <positive-definite symmetric matrices>. Their common positive <eigenvalues> $\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3$ are the **<principal stretches>**, equivalently the <singular values> of $F$. They describe the local stretch magnitudes after separating out a rigid <rotation>.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Define the invariants by the <characteristic polynomial>
$$
\det(tI-B)=t^3-I_Bt^2+II_Bt-III_B.
$$
Because $B=FF^T$ has positive <eigenvalues> $\lambda_j^2$, its invariants are the <elementary symmetric functions> in those <eigenvalues>:
$$
I_B=\lambda_1^2+\lambda_2^2+\lambda_3^2,\qquad
II_B=\lambda_1^2\lambda_2^2+\lambda_1^2\lambda_3^2+\lambda_2^2\lambda_3^2,\qquad
III_B=\lambda_1^2\lambda_2^2\lambda_3^2.
$$
The <Frobenius norm> gives $|F|^2=\operatorname{tr}(F^TF)=\sum_j\lambda_j^2$. Write $J=\det F>0$. Since $\operatorname{cof}F=JF^{-T}$, its <singular values> are $J/\lambda_j$, so
$$
|\operatorname{cof}F|^2=J^2\sum_j\lambda_j^{-2}
=\lambda_2^2\lambda_3^2+\lambda_1^2\lambda_3^2+\lambda_1^2\lambda_2^2.
$$
Finally $\det B=\det F\det F^T=J^2$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{I_B=|F|^2,\qquad II_B=|\operatorname{cof}F|^2,\qquad III_B=(\det F)^2.}
$$
These identities also give $I_B=\operatorname{tr}B$ and $II_B=((\operatorname{tr}B)^2-\operatorname{tr}(B^2))/2$.

= Solution

Take a <singular value decomposition> $F=Q_1DQ_2^T$, with $D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3)$ positive and $Q_1,Q_2\in SO(3)$. Such a choice is possible because $\det F>0$. Left <material frame indifference> removes $Q_1$, and right <material isotropy> removes $Q_2$, giving $W(F)=W(D)$.

Moreover $W(D)$ is unchanged when the positive diagonal entries are permuted. Every <permutation> can be implemented by a signed <permutation matrix> of <determinant> one; the signs disappear in conjugating a <diagonal matrix>. Thus the energy depends only on the unordered <principal stretches>.

The three invariants determine precisely this unordered triple: the roots of $t^3-I_Bt^2+II_Bt-III_B$ are $\lambda_1^2,\lambda_2^2,\lambda_3^2$, and their positive <square roots> give the stretches. Consequently, on the set of admissible invariant triples, define $h(I_B,II_B,III_B)$ to be $W(D)$. The preceding argument proves that this is well defined and that
$$
\boxed{W(F)=h(I_B,II_B,III_B).}
$$
Not every positive triple is realizable by three positive roots. Since the requested statement only asserts existence of a <function> on $(0,\infty)^3$, extend $h$ arbitrarily on the nonrealizable triples; no choice there affects the formula for a <deformation in continuum mechanics>. Smooth invariant representations, where needed for differentiating the <constitutive equation>, are understood locally on the realizable set.

= Solution

Retain the <material frame indifference> assumption used in part (c), in addition to <material isotropy>. Put $p=u_{,1}$, $q=u_{,2}$ and $s=p^2+q^2$. For this <antiplane shear>,
$$
F=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\p&q&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&p\\0&1&q\\p&q&1+s\end{pmatrix},\qquad J=1.
$$
The invariant identities give $I_B=II_B=3+s$ and $III_B=1$. Hence the restriction of the <strain energy density> is
$$
\boxed{W(Dy)=w(|Du|^2),\qquad w(s)=h(3+s,3+s,1).}
$$
<Antiplane energy does not determine the full stress>: the restriction gives one <scalar> energy along antiplane gradients. It does not determine the full <stress> derivative with respect to every entry of $F$.

Indeed, using the <isotropic hyperelastic stress invariant representation> and differentiating the energy, with $h_j$ denoting the corresponding <partial derivatives>, gives
$$
DW=2h_1F+2h_2(I_BI-B)F+2III_Bh_3F^{-T}.
$$
Using $S=J^{-1}TF^T$, the general objective <isotropic> <Cauchy stress tensor> is
$$
\boxed{S=\frac2J\left[h_1B+h_2(I_BB-B^2)+III_Bh_3I\right].}
$$
All <derivatives> are evaluated at the current invariant triple. The extra terms generally remain nonzero on antiplane gradients, so **the printed assertion that the full <stress> must be $\beta B$ does not follow**.

A stress-free, objective <isotropic> counterexample is the compressible <neo-Hookean solid> $W(F)=\frac\mu2(|F|^2-3)-\mu\log J$, with $\mu>0$. Its derivative is $T=\mu F-\mu F^{-T}$, so on antiplane gradients
$$
S=\mu(B-I).
$$
Take $u(x_1,x_2)=x_1$. Then $B_{22}=1$ but $S_{22}=0$, whereas $B_{13}=1$ and $S_{13}=\mu$. The diagonal entry would force $\beta=0$, contradicting the shear entry. Thus this is not a matter of choosing the right <scalar> coefficient.

For the additional one-invariant constitutive assumption $W(F)=\widehat w(I_B)$, the stronger formula does hold, with
$$
\boxed{\beta(I_B,III_B)=\frac{2\widehat w'(I_B)}{\sqrt{III_B}}.}
$$
This is a valid special case, rather than a consequence of <material isotropy> alone. For the general restricted energy, the shear entries are still fixed: $S_{31}=2w'(s)u_{,1}$ and $S_{32}=2w'(s)u_{,2}$, since $w'=h_1+h_2$ along $(3+s,3+s,1)$. The full <normal stress> needs the constitutive information that the printed claim omits. If <material isotropy> alone is interpreted without <material frame indifference>, even the first reduction to $|Du|^2$ needs that additional <material frame indifference> assumption.

= Solution

In the classical smooth setting, a <deformation in continuum mechanics> is a sufficiently regular, <injective> map $y:\Omega\to\mathbb R^3$ with $\det Dy>0$. <Injectivity> excludes material interpenetration, and a positive <determinant> preserves orientation and local <volume>. We use $C^1$ regularity for these kinematics and additional differentiability where stress-divergence identities are applied.

<Cauchy's stress theorem> states that the contact <force> on an oriented surface element at a current point depends on the point and its <unit normal>, rather than on the shape of the chosen subbody. Thus the contact <force> on the <boundary> of a current subbody $v$ has the form $\int_{\partial v}t(z,n)\,da$. Static <force> balance, for a <body force> $b$ per unit current <volume>, is
$$
\int_{\partial v}t(z,n)\,da+\int_vb(z)\,dv=0
$$
for every subbody. A shrinking pillbox gives $t(z,-n)=-t(z,n)$; a shrinking tetrahedron then gives linear dependence on the normal, $t(z,n)=S(z)n$. Applying the <divergence theorem> and using arbitrary subbodies gives
$$
\boxed{t(z,n)=S(z)n,\qquad\operatorname{div}S+b=0.}
$$
If <body force> is specified per unit mass instead, replace $b$ by $\rho b$. <Symmetry> of $S$ follows additionally from <conservation of angular momentum> in the absence of body couples; it is not a consequence of <force> balance alone.

= Solution
{parent=b}

The surface element relation is <Nanson's formula>. The <force> on a corresponding pair of current and reference surface elements must agree:
$$
t\,da=(S\circ y)n\,da=(S\circ y)\operatorname{cof}F\,N\,dA=T N\,dA.
$$
Therefore the first <Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor> is
$$
\boxed{T=(S\circ y)\operatorname{cof}F=J(S\circ y)F^{-T}.}
$$
It measures <force> per reference <area>, with a current <force> direction and a reference normal direction.

In components, differentiate $T_{iA}=S_{ij}(y(x))(\operatorname{cof}F)_{jA}$. The <Piola identity> says $\partial_A(\operatorname{cof}Dy)_{jA}=0$. One proof writes the <cofactor matrix> as $\frac12\varepsilon_{jkl}\varepsilon_{ABC}y_{k,B}y_{l,C}$; differentiating in $A$ produces symmetric second <derivatives> contracted against antisymmetric epsilon symbols, hence zero. The <chain rule> consequently gives
$$
\partial_AT_{iA}=\frac{\partial S_{ij}}{\partial y_k}(y)F_{kA}(\operatorname{cof}F)_{jA}
=J\frac{\partial S_{ij}}{\partial y_j}(y),
$$
since $F_{kA}(\operatorname{cof}F)_{jA}=J\delta_{kj}$. Thus, with the coordinate composition made explicit,
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Div}_xT=J(\operatorname{div}_yS)\circ y.}
$$
The distinction between reference <divergence> and current <divergence> is essential to this identity.

= Solution
{parent=c}

For $R=|x|>0$, $DR=\theta$ and $D\theta=(I-\theta\otimes\theta)/R$. Differentiating the <spherically symmetric deformation> gives
$$
\boxed{F=r'(R)\theta\otimes\theta+\frac{r(R)}R(I-\theta\otimes\theta).}
$$
For an orientation-preserving <spherically symmetric deformation> with positive radius, $r>0$ and $r'>0$. The radial direction and its perpendicular plane are <eigenspaces> of this positive <symmetric matrix>, so
$$
\boxed{\lambda_1=r'(R),\qquad\lambda_2=\lambda_3=r(R)/R.}
$$
At a positive <diagonal matrix> $D$, every diagonal <rotation> with two minus signs commutes with $D$. The supplied equivariance relation therefore forces $\widehat T(D)$ to be a <diagonal matrix>: each off-diagonal entry changes sign under one such <rotation> while $D$ stays fixed. Varying a positive diagonal entry shows that the diagonal entries of $\widehat T(D)$ are $\Phi_{,j}$. <Permutation> <symmetry> of $\Phi$ implies $\Phi_{,2}=\Phi_{,3}$ when $\lambda_2=\lambda_3$.

Choose a <rotation> whose first column is $\theta$. Applying equivariance to $F=QDQ^T$ now gives
$$
\boxed{T=\Phi_{,1}\theta\otimes\theta+\Phi_{,2}(I-\theta\otimes\theta),}
$$
with the <derivatives> evaluated at $(r',r/R,r/R)$. Thus the <stress> has one radial and one repeated tangential principal value, independently of the choice of tangent basis.

= Solution
{parent=d}

The <Green-Lagrange strain tensor> has <eigenvalues> $(\lambda_j^2-1)/2$. Its <Frobenius norm> and trace are invariant under the orthogonal principal-axis change, so
$$
\boxed{W(F)=\frac\mu4\sum_{j=1}^3(\lambda_j^2-1)^2+\frac\lambda8\left(\sum_{j=1}^3\lambda_j^2-3\right)^2.}
$$
For the homogeneous dilation $F=\rho I$, put $K=\mu+3\lambda/2$. Then $E=(\rho^2-1)I/2$. Differentiating the energy through $E$ gives the <second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor> $2\mu E+\lambda\operatorname{tr}(E)I=K(\rho^2-1)I$, and multiplication by $F$ gives the <first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor>. Thus
$$
\boxed{T=\rho K(\rho^2-1)I,\qquad S=J^{-1}TF^T=\frac{K(\rho^2-1)}\rho I.}
$$
The normals of the reference and deformed spheres point in the same radial direction. As $\rho\downarrow0$, the reference <traction> magnitude $|T N|$ tends to zero, while the current <traction> magnitude $|S n|$ tends to infinity, provided $K>0$, as for physically stable <Lamé parameters>. The negative signs of the <stress> coefficients for $0<\rho<1$ indicate compression.

There is no contradiction: the current <area> is $\rho^2$ times the reference <area>, so the same <force> divided by the vanishing current <area> has the larger <stress>. Indeed $T N=\rho^2S n$. The limiting map at $\rho=0$ is not itself an admissible <deformation in continuum mechanics>; the statement concerns the approach through positive $\rho$. A degenerate choice $K=0$ would not have the asserted growth without bound.

= Solution
{parent=a}

The <displacement field> is $u(x)=y(x)-x$, and its <gradient> is $Du=Dy-I$. <Linear elasticity> expands about an undeformed stress-free reference state, taking $y=x+\epsilon v$ with $|Du|\ll1$ and external loads of the same small order. A rigid translation need not itself be small; it is the <deformation gradient> that controls the <infinitesimal strain tensor>.

For $W\in C^2$ with $DW(I)=0$, a <Taylor series> gives
$$
W(I+H)=W(I)+\frac12\mathbb A_{iA jB}H_{iA}H_{jB}+o(|H|^2),\qquad
\mathbb A_{iA jB}=\frac{\partial^2W}{\partial F_{iA}\partial F_{jB}}(I).
$$
The constant $W(I)$ can be set to zero. The <tensor> $\mathbb A$ is the <elasticity tensor>. Commutation of continuous <mixed partial derivatives> proves its major <symmetry>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb A_{iA jB}=\mathbb A_{jB iA}.}
$$
<Material frame indifference> at a stress-free reference also makes the quadratic energy depend only on the <infinitesimal strain tensor> $e=(H+H^T)/2$, leading to the usual minor <symmetries> after spatial and reference indices are identified.

In this scaling, an external <force> of order $\epsilon$ may be evaluated on the reference geometry: its change due to the order-$\epsilon$ displacement contributes only at order $\epsilon^2$. Thus the leading forces are fixed <dead loads>. Likewise $F=I+O(\epsilon)$, $J=1+O(\epsilon)$ and $S=O(\epsilon)$, so the Piola transformation gives $T=J S F^{-T}=S+O(\epsilon^2)$. Hence **Cauchy and first Piola stresses agree to linear order**. This reasoning uses the usual small-load, stress-free linearization; an order-one prestress or leading follower-load effect requires a different expansion.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let the <boundary> split into a displacement part $\Gamma_D$ and a <traction> part $\Gamma_N$. The linearized stored <elastic energy> functional is
$$
\boxed{I^{\mathrm{lin}}[u]=\frac12\int_\Omega\mathbb A_{iA jB}u_{i,A}u_{j,B}\,dx.}
$$
Its corresponding total potential subtracts the work of fixed body and surface loads:
$$
\Pi[u]=I^{\mathrm{lin}}[u]-\int_\Omega f\cdot u\,dx-\int_{\Gamma_N}g\cdot u\,dA.
$$
The problem in the <calculus of variations> is to minimize $\Pi$ among admissible displacements with $u=u_D$ on $\Gamma_D$. For a stable quadratic <elastic energy> this is the usual energy minimization formulation. Its stationarity condition suffices to derive equilibrium.

Vary by $u+sv$, with $v=0$ on $\Gamma_D$. Major <symmetry> combines the two differentiated quadratic terms, giving
$$
\int_\Omega S^{\mathrm{lin}}:Dv\,dx=\int_\Omega f\cdot v\,dx+\int_{\Gamma_N}g\cdot v\,dA,
\qquad S^{\mathrm{lin}}_{iA}=\mathbb A_{iA jB}u_{j,B}.
$$
<Integration by parts>, first with test functions compactly supported in the interior, gives $-\operatorname{Div}S^{\mathrm{lin}}=f$. Then arbitrary traces on $\Gamma_N$ give the natural <traction> condition. The complete <boundary value problem> is
$$
\boxed{-\operatorname{Div}S^{\mathrm{lin}}=f\text{ in }\Omega,\qquad
S^{\mathrm{lin}}N=g\text{ on }\Gamma_N,\qquad u=u_D\text{ on }\Gamma_D.}
$$
For a <homogeneous hyperelastic material> the <elasticity tensor> is constant. In <isotropic> <linear elasticity> the <constitutive equation> is $S^{\mathrm{lin}}=\lambda\operatorname{div}u\,I+\mu(Du+Du^T)$, giving the familiar <Navier-Cauchy equation> $-\mu\Delta u-(\lambda+\mu)\nabla\operatorname{div}u=f$.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Taking traces of the <constitutive equation> gives
$$
\lambda\operatorname{tr}e_\lambda=\frac13\left(\operatorname{tr}S_\lambda-2\mu\operatorname{tr}e_\lambda\right).
$$
The assumed finite limits of <stress> and <strain> make the right side bounded. Therefore $\operatorname{tr}e_\lambda=O(\lambda^{-1})$, and the limiting <strain> satisfies $\operatorname{tr}e=0$. The permitted interchange of <derivatives> and limits gives $\operatorname{tr}e=\operatorname{div}u$. Define
$$
q=\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\lambda\operatorname{tr}e_\lambda=\tfrac13\operatorname{tr}S,\qquad p=-q.
$$
Then the limit <stress> is $S=-pI+2\mu e$. Passing to the limit in <force> balance and using $2\operatorname{Div}e=\Delta u+\nabla\operatorname{div}u$ gives
$$
\boxed{-\mu\Delta u+\nabla p=f,\qquad\operatorname{div}u=0\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad u=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega.}
$$
The <pressure> acts as the <Lagrange multiplier> enforcing the <volume> constraint. The <differential equations> determine it up to an additive constant, while a specified absolute limit <stress> can fix that constant.

Nonlinear <elastic incompressibility> is $\det Dy=1$. Expanding the <determinant> for $Dy=I+Du$ gives $\det(I+Du)=1+\operatorname{div}u+O(|Du|^2)$, so its first-order form is precisely $\operatorname{div}u=0$. The volumetric energy term $\lambda(\operatorname{div}u)^2/2$ becomes increasingly expensive as $\lambda$ grows, while the fixed positive $\mu$ leaves <shear strain> possible. Thus **the infinite-bulk-stiffness limit is the <incompressible limit of isotropic linear elasticity>**, with the same equilibrium operator as the steady <Stokes equations>.
