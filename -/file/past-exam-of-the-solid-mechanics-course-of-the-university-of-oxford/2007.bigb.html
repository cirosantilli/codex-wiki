= Solution
{parent=i}
{c}

We first derive the <stress> and energy facts used in both parts. The Cauchy <traction> $s=Tn$ is <force> per unit area in the deformed configuration. The first Piola-Kirchhoff <traction> $s_R=T_RN$ is <force> per unit reference area. Their <force> vectors are measured in the same spatial coordinates, but the area measures and the normals used to specify the surface are different.

Parametrize a reference surface by $x=x(u,v)$. Its oriented area vector is $(x_u\times x_v)\,du\,dv$. Under deformation, the two tangents become $Fx_u,Fx_v$. The cross-product identity
$$
(Fa)\times(Fb)=(\operatorname{cof}F)(a\times b)=JF^{-T}(a\times b)
$$
therefore proves <Nanson's formula> $n\,da=JF^{-T}N\,dA$. Equality of the physical <force> on corresponding surface patches now gives
$$
T_RN\,dA=Tn\,da=JT F^{-T}N\,dA.
$$
Since this holds for every reference <normal>,
$$
\boxed{T_R=JT F^{-T},\qquad T=J^{-1}T_RF^T.}
$$
The <first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor> is generally not <symmetric>, whereas the <Cauchy stress tensor> is <symmetric> in the absence of couple stresses.

For the stated energy on orientation-preserving gradients, <differentiation> gives
$$
T_R=DW(F)=\mu\bigl(F-J^{-2}\operatorname{cof}F\bigr)
=\mu(F-J^{-1}F^{-T}).
$$
A stress-free state satisfies $FF^T=J^{-1}I$. Taking <determinants> yields $J^2=J^{-3}$, so $J=1$ and $FF^T=I$: precisely $F\in SO(3)$. These states are also the global energy <minimizers>. If $v_i>0$ are the <singular values>, the <arithmetic-geometric mean inequality> gives $|F|^2\geq3J^{2/3}$, with equality only when the <singular values> are equal. The function $3J^{2/3}/2+J^{-1}$ decreases up to $J=1$ and increases thereafter, with <minimum> $5/2$. Thus $W\geq5\mu/2$, with equality precisely at <rotations>. A constant shift of the energy can set this <minimum> to zero without changing the <stress>.

For the given <simple shear>,
$$
F=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
F^{-T}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\-1&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
T_R=\mu\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
For a <unit normal> $N$, $|T_RN|^2=\mu^2(N_1^2+N_2^2)=\mu^2(1-N_3^2)$. Hence **every reference <plane> whose <normal> lies in the $e_1,e_2$ <plane> is maximizing**:
$$
\boxed{N=(\cos\alpha,\sin\alpha,0),\quad
x_1\cos\alpha+x_2\sin\alpha=0,\qquad\max|s_R|=\mu.}
$$
There is a family of maximizing planes, rather than one distinguished <normal>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

Using $J=1$ and the preceding <first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor>,
$$
T=T_RF^T=\mu\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its <eigenvalues> are $\mu(1+\sqrt5)/2$, $\mu(1-\sqrt5)/2$, and zero. Because it is <symmetric>, its largest <traction> magnitude on a unit spatial <normal> is the largest absolute <eigenvalue>. Put $\varphi=(1+\sqrt5)/2$. The positive <eigenvalue> has the strictly largest modulus, with <normal>
$$
n=\pm\frac{(\varphi,1,0)}{\sqrt{\varphi^2+1}}.
$$
The question asks for the <plane> in the <reference configuration>, so this spatial <normal> must be pulled back. <Nanson's formula> gives $N$ parallel to $F^Tn$, and
$$
F^T(\varphi,1,0)^T=(\varphi,\varphi+1,0)^T
=\varphi(1,\varphi,0)^T.
$$
Therefore the required <plane> and maximum are
$$
\boxed{x_1+\varphi x_2=0,\qquad
\max|s|=\mu\varphi=\frac{\mu(1+\sqrt5)}2.}
$$
Either sign of its <unit normal> describes the same <plane>. Maximizing over reference normals is equivalent to maximizing over spatial normals because the map $N\mapsto F^{-T}N/|F^{-T}N|$ is a <bijection> of unit normals.

= Solution

A <dead-load traction> is prescribed <force> per unit reference area whose vector is independent of the deformation. It is imposed through $T_RN=t_R$ on the reference boundary. It does not rotate or rescale with the current surface as a follower load would.

<Elastic incompressibility> imposes $\det F=1$ and adds a scalar <Lagrange multiplier> to the constitutive law. With the <pressure> sign convention used here,
$$
T_R=DW(F)-p\,\operatorname{cof}F=DW(F)-pF^{-T},\qquad
T=DW(F)F^T-pI.
$$
The <pressure> field is determined by <equilibrium> and <boundary conditions>, rather than by the <strain energy density> alone.

For the diagonal <homogeneous deformation>, $F=\operatorname{diag}(v_1,v_2,v_3)$ and $v_1v_2v_3=1$. <Isotropy> makes $DW(F)$ diagonal here, with entries $\Phi_i$, so
$$
(T_R)_{ii}=\Phi_i-\frac p{v_i}.
$$
The six face conditions are exactly $T_Re_i=\tau e_i$. Thus $\Phi_i-p/v_i=\tau$ for each $i$, or
$$
\boxed{v_1\Phi_1-\tau v_1=v_2\Phi_2-\tau v_2
=v_3\Phi_3-\tau v_3=p,\qquad v_1v_2v_3=1.}
$$
Necessity follows from the <boundary conditions>. Conversely these equations give a constant <pressure> and constant <stress> satisfying those conditions, while $\operatorname{Div}T_R=0$ because the <stress> is constant and there is no <body force>. This proves sufficiency as well.

For the particular energy, $\Phi_i=\mu(v_i-v_i^{-2})$. Put $S=v_1+v_2+v_3$ and $Q=v_1v_2+v_1v_3+v_2v_3$. <Elastic incompressibility> gives $v_i^{-1}=v_jv_k$, hence
$$
v_i\Phi_i-\tau v_i
=\mu(v_i^2-v_jv_k)-\tau v_i
=(\mu S-\tau)v_i-\mu Q.
$$
Equality for every pair is therefore equivalent to $(\mu S-\tau)(v_i-v_j)=0$. If all stretches are equal, their positive product one gives $v_1=v_2=v_3=1$, with $p=-\tau$. Otherwise $S=\tau/\mu$. Conversely any positive triple with this sum and product one solves the equations, with $p=-\mu Q$. Thus **the complete solutions within the stated homogeneous diagonal ansatz are the unit triple and the stated sum-product family**.

By the <arithmetic-geometric mean inequality>, $S\geq3$, with equality only at the unit triple. Consequently $\tau\leq3\mu$ admits only that triple, including equality, when the two descriptions coincide.

To prove infinitely many for $s=\tau/\mu>3$, choose $v_1=t$ near one and set
$$
v_{2,3}=\frac{s-t\pm\sqrt{(s-t)^2-4/t}}2.
$$
At $t=1$ the discriminant is $(s-1)^2-4>0$; it stays positive on an interval about one, and both roots remain positive. Every such $t$ gives a different admissible triple. Hence
$$
\boxed{\tau\leq3\mu:\text{ one triple};\qquad
\tau>3\mu:\text{ infinitely many triples}.}
$$
The degeneracy has an energy explanation: under the product <constraint> the <strain energy density> is $\mu S^2/2$, and the total potential under <dead loading> is $\mu S^2/2-\tau S$. It depends on the three stretches only through their sum. Its <minimum> over attainable $S\geq3$ moves from $S=3$ to the whole level set $S=\tau/\mu$ when the load exceeds $3\mu$.

= Solution
{parent=3}
{c}

For a <hyperelastic material> $T_R(F)=DW(F)$. <Material frame indifference> of this first Piola-Kirchhoff law means
$$
\boxed{T_R(QF)=Q\,T_R(F)\quad(Q\in SO(3)).}
$$
The spatial <force> components rotate under a superposed rigid <rotation>, whereas the reference <normal> does not.

If $W(QF)=W(F)$, differentiate with respect to $F$ in direction $H$ to obtain $DW(QF):(QH)=DW(F):H$, hence the displayed <stress> transformation. Conversely, assume that <stress> transformation. For fixed $Q$, the <derivative> of $W(QF)-W(F)$ with respect to $F$ vanishes. The positive-determinant <matrix> domain is <connected>, as follows from <polar decomposition> and the <connectedness> of $SO(3)$ and of the <positive-definite> <cone>. Thus $W(QF)-W(F)=c(Q)$, independent of $F$. Iterating gives
$$
W(Q^nF)=W(F)+n c(Q)\qquad(n\in\mathbb Z).
$$
The assumed lower bound on $W$ forces $c(Q)=0$: either nonzero sign would tend to minus infinity for one direction of integer $n$. This proves the required equivalence and explains the use of the lower-bound hypothesis.

In the frame-indifferent setting, <isotropy> means invariance under reference <rotations> as well: $W(FQ)=W(F)$ for $Q\in SO(3)$. The distinction matters: left <rotations> encode changes of observer, and right <rotations> encode material symmetry.

An orientation-preserving <singular value decomposition> is $F=R\operatorname{diag}(v_1,v_2,v_3)S$, with $R,S\in SO(3)$ and $v_i>0$. <Material frame indifference> and <isotropy> remove $R,S$, leaving a function $\Phi$ of the stretches. Permuting the stretches using signed permutation <rotations> does not change the value, so $\Phi$ is <symmetric>. Conversely a <symmetric> stretch function is unchanged by both left and right <rotations>, in particular is <isotropic>.

The <eigenvalues> of $B=FF^T$ are $v_i^2$. Its three principal invariants are
$$
I=\operatorname{tr}B=\sum_iv_i^2,\quad
II=\tfrac12[(\operatorname{tr}B)^2-\operatorname{tr}(B^2)]
=\sum_{i<j}v_i^2v_j^2,\quad III=\det B=\prod_iv_i^2.
$$
They determine the unordered positive <eigenvalues> as the roots of $\lambda^3-I\lambda^2+II\lambda-III=0$. Therefore a <symmetric> stretch function defines a well-defined $h(I,II,III)$ on the admissible invariant triples. Conversely a function of these invariants is a <symmetric> stretch function, proving the equivalence of all three descriptions.

The printed domain of $\Phi$ must be $(0,\infty)^3$, since it takes three arguments. Also <isotropy> alone as right invariance would not imply the spectral representation without <material frame indifference>: $\operatorname{tr}(A FF^T)$ for a fixed nonscalar <symmetric> <positive-definite> $A$ is right invariant but not generally objective. The equivalence here uses the frame-indifferent constitutive setting.

For the specific energy, <Newton identities> for the three <eigenvalues> gives $\sum_i v_i^6=I^3-3I\,II+3III$, while $v_1v_2v_3=J=\sqrt{III}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{W=\alpha I+\beta(I^3-3I\,II+3III)+\gamma III^{-1/2}.}
$$
To differentiate it, use
$$
D_FI=2F,\qquad D_FII=2(IF-BF),\qquad
D_FIII=2J\operatorname{cof}F.
$$
These follow from $I=|F|^2$, the displayed <trace> formula for $II$, and $III=J^2$. The three partial derivatives of $h$ are $h_I=\alpha+3\beta I^2-3\beta II$, $h_{II}=-3\beta I$ and $h_{III}=3\beta-\gamma III^{-3/2}/2$. The <chain rule> therefore gives
$$
T_R=(2\alpha-6\beta II)F+6\beta I\,BF
+(6\beta J-\gamma J^{-2})\operatorname{cof}F.
$$
Finally $-6\beta II=-3\beta(\operatorname{tr}B)^2+3\beta\operatorname{tr}(B^2)$, and $BF=FF^TF$. This is precisely the requested expression, with the coefficient of $F$ separated from the $BF$ and <cofactor> terms. The positive sign of the $6\beta J$ term comes from the $3\beta III$ contribution in the energy.

= Solution
{parent=4}
{c}

Use a temperature-dependent <strain energy density> $W(F,\vartheta)$ with different minimizing <deformation gradients> in the two <crystal> phases. Above the transition temperature its wells describe <austenite>; below it the wells are unions $SO(3)U_i$ of <martensitic variants>. Left rotational orbits reflect <material frame indifference>, while the <crystal>'s discrete material symmetries relate the variants. Minimizing the integral of this <elastic energy>, together with loading and boundary <constraints>, models the change of lattice shape. Compatibility restricts which low-energy gradients can meet continuously; fine compatible mixtures can accommodate macroscopic boundary shapes.

For the jump condition, write the two affine pieces as $Ax+c_+$ and $Bx+c_-$. <Continuity> on the interface means
$$
(A-B)x+(c_+-c_-)=0\qquad(x\cdot N=k).
$$
Taking differences along the interface gives $(A-B)t=0$ for every <tangent vector> $t$ with $t\cdot N=0$. Decompose any vector $v$ as $t+(v\cdot N)N$. Then
$$
(A-B)v=(v\cdot N)(A-B)N.
$$
With $a=(A-B)N$ this proves
$$
\boxed{A-B=a\otimes N.}
$$
Conversely this <rank-one connection> permits <continuity> by choosing $c_+-c_-=-ka$. A coherent <twin> interface is such a compatible planar boundary between different <martensitic variants>, possibly after a rigid <rotation> of one variant. The rank-one condition is therefore the geometric compatibility condition for a simple <twin>.

For the unrotated pair, direct subtraction gives
$$
U_1-U_2=2\delta e_3\otimes e_1,
$$
so $R=I$ is one solution, with reference interface <normal> $e_1$.

Put $d=1+\delta^2$, $c=(1-\delta^2)/d$ and $s=2\delta/d$. Since $c^2+s^2=1$, the stated $\overline R$ is a <proper rotation>. Multiplication gives
$$
\overline R U_2=
\begin{pmatrix}c+\delta s&0&-s\\0&1&0\\s-\delta c&0&c\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-s\\0&1&0\\\delta&0&c\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Here $c+\delta s=1$ and $s-\delta c=\delta$. Consequently
$$
U_1-\overline R U_2
=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&s\\0&0&0\\0&0&1-c\end{pmatrix}
=\boxed{\frac{2\delta}{1+\delta^2}(e_1+\delta e_3)\otimes e_3.}
$$
This is the second <rank-one connection>, now with <normal> $e_3$. Because $\delta>0$, the two <rotations> are distinct. The permitted assertion that there are at most two proves completeness.

Finally suppose $QU_1$ is rank-one <connected> to $\overline R U_2$, with $Q\in SO(3)$. Left multiplication of their difference by $Q^T$ preserves <rank> and gives $U_1-Q^T\overline R U_2$. The classification just proved forces $Q^T\overline R$ to be either $I$ or $\overline R$, hence $Q=\overline R$ or $Q=I$. The two requested matrices are therefore
$$
\boxed{U_1\quad\text{and}\quad\overline R U_1.}
$$
Their connections are explicit: the first was calculated above, and $\overline R U_1-\overline R U_2=2\delta(\overline R e_3)\otimes e_1$. If desired the second <matrix> is
$$
\overline R U_1=
\begin{pmatrix}c-\delta s&0&-s\\0&1&0\\s+\delta c&0&c\end{pmatrix}.
$$
