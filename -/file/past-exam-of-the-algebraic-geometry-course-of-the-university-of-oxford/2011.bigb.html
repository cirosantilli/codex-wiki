=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

An <affine variety> in $\mathbb A_k^n$ is the common <zero set> of a collection of <polynomials> in $k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$, with its <Zariski topology>. We use the convention of this question that such a <variety> need not be <irreducible topological space>[irreducible]. Its <vanishing ideal> and <coordinate ring> are
$$
\boxed{I(X)=\{f:f(p)=0\text{ for every }p\in X\},\qquad A(X)=k[x_1,\ldots,x_n]/I(X).}
$$
The quotient identifies <polynomials> that give the same function on $X$, so its elements are exactly <polynomial functions> on $X$.

For affine embeddings $X\subseteq\mathbb A^n$ and $Y\subseteq\mathbb A^m$, a <morphism of affine varieties> is a map $F=(f_1,\ldots,f_m)$ with $f_i\in A(X)$ and <image> in $Y$. <Pullback> of <polynomial functions> defines the contravariant <homomorphism>
$$
\boxed{F^\#:A(Y)\longrightarrow A(X),\qquad [g]\longmapsto g\circ F.}
$$
It is well defined because any <polynomial> vanishing on $Y$ pulls back to zero on $X$, and it preserves addition, multiplication, one, and the constants in $k$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <radical of an ideal> is
$$
\boxed{\sqrt I=\{f\in R:f^N\in I\text{ for some integer }N\geq1\}.}
$$
The <ideal> is <radical> if $I=\sqrt I$. Its <zero set> is $V(I)=\{a\in k^n:f(a)=0\text{ for every }f\in I\}$, an <affine variety> by definition.

Every $f\in I$ vanishes at every <point> of $V(I)$, so $I\subseteq I(V(I))$. If $g^N\in I(V(I))$, then $g(a)^N=0$ in the <field> $k$ for every $a\in V(I)$, so $g(a)=0$ for each such <point>. Hence $g\in I(V(I))$ and
$$
\boxed{I(V(I))\text{ is a radical ideal containing }I.}
$$

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Part (b) immediately gives $\sqrt I\subseteq I(V(I))$. For the reverse inclusion, let $h\in I(V(I))$. The case $h=0$ is immediate, so assume $h\neq0$. <Noetherianity> gives finite generators $I=(f_1,\ldots,f_r)$.

Use the <Rabinowitsch trick>: in the <polynomial ring> with one auxiliary variable, form
$$
J=(f_1,\ldots,f_r,1-th)\subseteq k[x_1,\ldots,x_n,t].
$$
Apply the assumed maximal-ideal description to <polynomial rings>, including this <ring> in $n+1$ variables. If $J$ were proper, it would lie in the <maximal ideal> of some <point> $(a,b)$. Then $a\in V(I)$ and $1-bh(a)=0$, impossible because $h$ vanishes on $V(I)$. Therefore $J$ is the whole <ring>, and there is an identity
$$
1=\sum_{i=1}^r P_i(x,t)f_i(x)+Q(x,t)(1-th(x)).
$$
Substitute $t=h^{-1}$ in the <localization> $R_h$. The last term vanishes; clearing the finitely many powers of $h$ from the remaining denominators yields $h^N\in I$ for some $N$. Thus $h\in\sqrt I$ and
$$
\boxed{I(V(I))=\sqrt I.}
$$
This proves the strong <Hilbert Nullstellensatz> from the polynomial-ring weak form by converting nonvanishing into an extra equation.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Here <subvariety> means a closed affine <subvariety>. Suppose first that $F^\#$ is onto, and write $X\subseteq\mathbb A^n$, $Y\subseteq\mathbb A^m$. Put $J=\ker F^\#$ and $Z=V_Y(J)$. Choose $g_i\in A(Y)$ with $F^\#g_i=x_i|_X$ and let $G:Z\to\mathbb A^n$ have components $g_i|_Z$.

If $h\in I(X)$, then $F^\#(h(g_1,\ldots,g_n))=h|_X=0$, so $h(g_1,\ldots,g_n)\in J$. It vanishes on $Z$, proving $G(Z)\subseteq X$. The chosen lifts give $G\circ F=\operatorname{id}_X$, and $F(X)\subseteq Z$ by the definition of $J$.

Let the components of $F$ be <polynomial functions> $f_1,\ldots,f_m$ on $X$. For each target coordinate $y_j$, the element $y_j-f_j(g_1,\ldots,g_n)$ has zero <pullback>, hence belongs to $J$. It vanishes on $Z$, giving $F\circ G=\operatorname{id}_Z$. Both maps have <polynomial> components, so $F$ is an <isomorphism> onto the <closed subvariety> $Z$.

Conversely, if $F:X\to Z\subseteq Y$ is an <isomorphism> onto a <closed subvariety>, <restriction> $A(Y)\to A(Z)$ is <surjective>, and the <pullback> $A(Z)\to A(X)$ of that <isomorphism> is an <isomorphism>. Their composition is $F^\#$, proving
$$
\boxed{F^\#\text{ onto}\iff F\text{ is a closed embedding}.}
$$
The word closed matters: an <isomorphism> onto a merely locally <closed subset> need not induce a <surjective> coordinate-ring map. For example $\mathbb G_m\hookrightarrow\mathbb A^1$ induces $k[t]\hookrightarrow k[t,t^{-1}]$.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

The parametrization lands on the curve because $(t^3)^4=(t^4)^3$. Its <pullback> has <image>
$$
\operatorname{im}F^\#=k[t^3,t^4]\subset k[t]=A(\mathbb A^1).
$$
Every <monomial> in this subalgebra has exponent $3a+4b$ for nonnegative integers $a,b$. No such exponent is one, so no <linear combination> of these <monomials> is $t$. Thus $F^\#$ is not <surjective> and **$F$ is not an <isomorphism>**.

It is nevertheless <bijective> on <points>. Away from the origin, $x\neq0$ and $t=y/x$ gives $t^3=x$ and $t^4=y$ using $x^4=y^3$; the origin comes only from $t=0$. The rational inverse cannot extend regularly at the origin: if $t=P(t^3,t^4)/Q(t^3,t^4)$ with $Q(0)\neq0$, then $tQ(t^3,t^4)$ has a nonzero degree-one term, whereas $P(t^3,t^4)$ has none.
$$
\boxed{F\text{ is bijective, but its inverse is not regular at the cusp}.}
$$
This distinguishes a set-theoretic <bijection> from an <isomorphism> of <varieties> in every <characteristic>.

This is a <bijective monomial cusp parametrization with nonregular inverse>.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <projective variety> is a Zariski-closed <subset> of <projective space> $\mathbb P_k^n$, equivalently the common <zero set> of <homogeneous polynomials>. <Homogeneity> makes their vanishing independent of the nonzero scalar used to represent a projective <point>.

A <morphism of varieties>[morphism] is a map locally given by regular coordinate functions in <affine charts>. Equivalently, near each <point> of a projective source embedded in $\mathbb P^n$, a <morphism of varieties>[morphism] to $\mathbb P^m$ can be written
$$
\boxed{[x]\longmapsto[F_0(x):\cdots:F_m(x)]}
$$
using <homogeneous polynomials> of a common degree that are not all zero on that <neighbourhood>. The expressions must agree on overlaps, and for a specified target <variety> their values must lie in it. A single global tuple is a sufficient description when available, but is not part of the general definition.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Segre embedding> is
$$
\boxed{\sigma_{n,m}([x_0:\cdots:x_n],[y_0:\cdots:y_m])=[x_iy_j]_{0\leq i\leq n,\ 0\leq j\leq m}.}
$$
Rescaling the two representatives multiplies every target coordinate by the same scalar, and at least one <product> is nonzero, so the map is well defined. In affine source charts its coordinate ratios are regular <products>, so it is a <morphism of varieties>[morphism].

Arrange target coordinates into a <matrix> $Z=(z_{ij})$. Every <image> <matrix> has <rank> one and satisfies all homogeneous equations
$$
z_{ij}z_{k\ell}-z_{i\ell}z_{kj}=0.
$$
Conversely, a nonzero <matrix> satisfying these equations has <rank> one. Choose $z_{i_0j_0}\neq0$ and <set> $x_i=z_{ij_0}$, $y_j=z_{i_0j}/z_{i_0j_0}$. The <minors> imply $z_{ij}=x_iy_j$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{im}\sigma_{n,m}=V(\text{all }2\times2\text{ minors}),}
$$
a <projective variety>. The two projective factors are uniquely recoverable from a nonzero column and row. This also shows <injectivity> and supplies the local inverse formulas used in the next part.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Choose projective embeddings $X\subseteq\mathbb P^n$ and $Y\subseteq\mathbb P^m$. Define their <product> as the Segre <image> of the <set> of pairs $(x,y)$, with its induced <variety> structure.

It is closed in the Segre <variety>. To see this directly, for every homogeneous $f\in I(X)$ impose the equations $f(z_{0j},\ldots,z_{nj})=0$ on every column, and for every homogeneous $g\in I(Y)$ impose $g(z_{i0},\ldots,z_{im})=0$ on every row. For a <rank-one matrix> $z_{ij}=x_iy_j$, the column equations are $y_j^{\deg f}f(x)=0$. A nonzero $y_j$ therefore forces $x\in X$. The row equations similarly force $y\in Y$. Thus these homogeneous equations together with the <minors> cut out exactly $X\times Y$, proving it is projective.

On a chart where $z_{i_0j_0}\neq0$, the projections have formulas
$$
\pi_X([Z])=[z_{0j_0}:\cdots:z_{nj_0}],\qquad
\pi_Y([Z])=[z_{i_00}:\cdots:z_{i_0m}].
$$
These <homogeneous coordinate> tuples are nonzero on the chart and agree on overlaps, so both projections are <morphism of varieties>[morphisms].

Given <morphism of varieties>[morphisms] $\alpha:Z\to X$ and $\beta:Z\to Y$, define $\gamma(z)=\sigma_{n,m}(\alpha(z),\beta(z))$. Locally write their coordinates as $[a_0:\cdots:a_n]$ and $[b_0:\cdots:b_m]$. Then $\gamma$ has coordinates $[a_ib_j]$, which are regular with a nonzero coordinate near each <point>. Hence $\gamma$ is a <morphism of varieties>[morphism] and
$$
\boxed{\pi_X\circ\gamma=\alpha,\qquad\pi_Y\circ\gamma=\beta.}
$$
The two projections determine the underlying pair uniquely, so this <morphism of varieties>[morphism] is unique. This is the <product> <universal property>, and it makes the construction independent up to canonical <isomorphism> of the chosen embeddings.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $S=k[x_0,\ldots,x_n]$ and let $B=S/I(X)$ be the <homogeneous coordinate ring> of a nonempty <projective variety>. Its <Hilbert function> is $h_X(t)=\dim_k B_t$. For all sufficiently large integers $t$, this function agrees with a <polynomial> $P_X(t)$, the <Hilbert polynomial>.

The <dimension of an algebraic set>[dimension] $r$ is the largest length of a strict chain of nonempty <irreducible topological space>[irreducible] closed <subvarieties> of $X$. Equivalently $r=\dim B-1=\deg P_X$. The degree of the embedded <variety> is the integer specified by the leading term
$$
\boxed{P_X(t)=\frac{\deg X}{r!}t^r+\text{lower-degree terms}.}
$$
Geometrically it is the number of <intersection> <points> with a general <linear subspace> of codimension $r$, counted with <intersection multiplicities>. The <polynomial>'s degree is the <dimension of an algebraic set>[dimension] $r$; the <variety>'s degree is the <leading coefficient> multiplied by $r!$. Keeping those two meanings separate is important in the next computation.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

The <homogeneous coordinate ring> of the Segre <image> is the subalgebra $k[x_iy_j]\subseteq k[x_0,\ldots,x_n,y_0,\ldots,y_m]$, grading each <product> $x_iy_j$ as degree one. Its degree-$t$ part is precisely the space of <polynomials> bihomogeneous of bidegree $(t,t)$.

Indeed, every <product> of $t$ generators has those two degrees. Conversely, any <monomial> of bidegree $(t,t)$ can pair its $t$ individual $x$ factors with its $t$ individual $y$ factors, expressing it as a <product> of generators. The distinct resulting <monomials> are <linearly independent>. Counting them gives
$$
\boxed{P_X(t)=\binom{t+n}{n}\binom{t+m}{m}.}
$$
In fact this agrees with the <Hilbert function> for every integer $t\geq0$. Its <leading coefficient> is $1/(n!m!)$, so the two degree quantities are
$$
\boxed{\deg P_X=n+m,\qquad\deg X=\frac{(n+m)!}{n!m!}=\binom{n+m}{n}.}
$$
Thus the Segre <variety> has <dimension of an algebraic set>[dimension] $n+m$ and embedded projective degree $\binom{n+m}{n}$.

This gives the <Segre Hilbert polynomial and degree> directly from its graded <monomials>.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <quasi-projective variety> is a locally <closed subset> of <projective space>: equivalently an <open subset> of some <projective variety>, with the induced <algebraic variety> structure. <Affine varieties> are examples, via the standard <affine chart> in <projective space>.

It is <irreducible topological space>[irreducible] if it is nonempty and cannot be written as the <union> of two proper <closed subsets>. Equivalently, every two nonempty <open subsets> meet, or every nonempty <open subset> is <dense>. Closed and open are interpreted in the induced <Zariski topology> of $X$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For a <multiplicatively closed set> $S$ containing one, the <localization of a ring> $S^{-1}R$ consists of fractions $r/s$ with $r\in R,s\in S$, under the equivalence
$$
\boxed{\frac rs=\frac{r'}{s'}\iff\exists u\in S:\ u(rs'-r's)=0.}
$$
The factor $u$ is needed when the <ring> has <zero divisors>. Addition and multiplication are
$$
\frac rs+\frac{r'}{s'}=\frac{rs'+r's}{ss'},\qquad
\frac rs\frac{r'}{s'}=\frac{rr'}{ss'}.
$$
The canonical map sends $r$ to $r/1$ and makes every element of $S$ a <unit>. It is universal with this property: a <ring homomorphism> $\phi:R\to T$ taking $S$ into <units> extends uniquely by $r/s\mapsto\phi(r)\phi(s)^{-1}$. Usually zero is excluded from $S$; if it is included, this construction is the <zero ring>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

A <regular function> on $U$ is a function $f:U\to k$ locally representable, in an <affine chart> of the ambient <projective space>, as a quotient $a/b$ of <polynomial functions> with denominator nonzero on that <neighbourhood>. Equivalently it is locally a quotient of homogeneous forms of the same degree. The equal-degree condition makes the quotient independent of the chosen projective representative.

The <ring> $\mathcal O_{X,p}$ is the <ring> of <germs> of such functions near $p$. Two representatives $(U,f)$ and $(V,g)$ define the same <germ> when they agree on some smaller <neighbourhood> of $p$. Addition and multiplication use a common <neighbourhood>. It is a <local ring>: its unique <maximal ideal> consists of <germs> vanishing at $p$, and any <germ> nonzero at $p$ has a regular reciprocal on a smaller <neighbourhood>.
$$
\boxed{\mathfrak m_p=\{[f]_p:f(p)=0\},\qquad\mathcal O_{X,p}/\mathfrak m_p\cong k.}
$$

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

A <morphism of varieties>[morphism] $F:X\to Y$ is a <continuous> map in the Zariski topologies such that for every open $V\subseteq Y$ and every <regular function> $h$ on $V$, the <pullback> $h\circ F$ is regular on $F^{-1}(V)$.

Equivalently, near every <point>, choose <affine charts> of source and target; the target coordinates of $F$ must then be <regular functions> of the source coordinates. These local conditions agree on overlaps. In particular a <morphism of varieties>[morphism] is defined at every <point> of its source, not merely on a <dense> <open subset>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

A <rational map> from <irreducible topological space>[irreducible] $X$ to $Y$ is an equivalence class of <morphism of varieties>[morphisms] $F_U:U\to Y$ defined on nonempty <open subsets> $U\subseteq X$. Two representatives are equivalent if they agree on a nonempty <open subset> of their <intersection>.

<irreducible topological space>[Irreducibility] makes all these nonempty opens <dense> and ensures their <intersections> are nonempty, so the equivalence expresses agreement at general <points>. A <rational map> need not extend across all of $X$, and it need not be dominant. An <injective> <homomorphism> of <function fields> additionally requires a <dominant morphism of affine varieties>, or the corresponding condition on a <rational map>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

<Polynomial functions> are regular, giving an <injective> map $A(X)\hookrightarrow\mathcal O_X(X)$; <injectivity> follows from quotienting by the full <vanishing ideal>. To prove <surjectivity>, let $f$ be globally regular and consider the denominator <ideal>
$$
J=\{g\in A(X):gf\text{ is a polynomial function on }X\}.
$$
It is an <ideal> because sums and <polynomial> multiples preserve this property.

Fix $p\in X$. Locally $f=a/b$ on a <neighbourhood> $V$ of $p$, with $b$ nonzero there. Principal affine opens form a <basis>, so choose $h\in A(X)$ with $h(p)\neq0$ and $D(h)\subseteq V$. Then
$$
(hb)f=ha
$$
as functions on all of $X$: on $D(h)$ it follows from the local fraction, and off $D(h)$ both sides vanish because $h=0$. Hence $hb\in J$ and $(hb)(p)\neq0$.

Thus $J$ has no common zero on $X$. By the weak Nullstellensatz for $A(X)$, every proper <ideal> lies in a <maximal ideal> belonging to a <point> of $X$; therefore $J$ cannot be proper. We get $1\in J$, so $f$ itself is <polynomial>. This proves
$$
\boxed{\mathcal O_X(X)\cong A(X).}
$$
The argument works for reducible <affine varieties> as well: it uses functions and denominator <ideals> rather than assuming a single <fraction field>.

This is the <denominator ideal proof for global regular functions>.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

For any <affine variety> and <point> $p$, with $\mathfrak m_p=\{f\in A(X):f(p)=0\}$, the standard <localization> description is
$$
\boxed{\mathcal O_{X,p}\cong A(X)_{\mathfrak m_p}.}
$$
Also $\mathcal O_X(D(g))\cong A(X)_g$ for a <principal open subset>. One way to see the latter is to realize $D(g)$ as the <affine variety> $\{(x,z):g(x)z=1\}$; its <coordinate ring> is $A(X)[z]/(gz-1)=A(X)_g$ and part (d) applies.

For this <cusp> use $x=t^3,y=t^2$, identifying $A(X)$ with $k[t^2,t^3]$ inside $k(t)$. The parametrization gives exactly the relation $x^2=y^3$; its kernel is generated by this binomial. For example reduce a <polynomial> modulo $x^2-y^3$ to $a(y)+xb(y)$; substitution has even powers from $a(t^2)$ and odd powers at least three from $t^3b(t^2)$, so it can vanish only when both <polynomials> are zero.

Write $g=x-y=t^2(t-1)$. The <open set> corresponds to $t\neq0,1$, and
$$
\mathcal O_X(U)=k[t^2,t^3]_{t^2(t-1)}.
$$
To simplify this <ring> without assuming the normalization coordinate is present, observe that in $A(X)$
$$
g^2=(x-y)^2=y(y^2-2x+y).
$$
Thus $y$ becomes a <unit> after inverting $g$, with inverse $(y^2-2x+y)/g^2$. Consequently $t=x/y$ belongs to the <localization>. Since $g=t^2(t-1)$ is a <unit>, both $t$ and $t-1$ become <units>. Conversely every original generator lies in the corresponding two-point <localization> of $k[t]$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathcal O_X(U)=k[t,t^{-1},(t-1)^{-1}].}
$$
Finally $p=(1,1)$ corresponds to $t=1$. It lies outside $U$, so compute its <local ring> from $X$, at $\mathfrak m=(x-1,y-1)$. The element $y$ is a <unit> there, hence $t=x/y$ belongs to $A(X)_{\mathfrak m}$. Every <polynomial> in $t$ nonzero at one is then a <unit>. Conversely a denominator from $A(X)\setminus\mathfrak m$ remains nonzero at $t=1$. These inclusions prove
$$
\boxed{\mathcal O_{X,p}=\bigl(k[x,y]/(x^2-y^3)\bigr)_{(x-1,y-1)}\cong k[t]_{(t-1)}.}
$$
This <smooth> <point> has the ordinary one-variable <local ring>, even though the global curve has a <cusp> at its origin.

The <localization> calculation describes <regular functions on a cusp with two points removed>.
