= Electrical circuit

= Electric circuit
{synonym}

An electrical circuit connects components that carry <electric current> and sustain <electric potential difference> differences. Its dynamics may couple <electrical resistance>, <inductance> and <capacitance>.

= Internal resistance
{parent=Electrical circuit}
{title2=$R_s$}

An effective source resistance in a specified circuit model: a source with <electromotive force> $\mathcal E$ drives $I=\mathcal E/(R_s+R_L)$ through a resistive load $R_L$. A biological source or electrochemical interface can require a frequency-dependent or nonlinear model instead.

= Capacitor bridge with series loss and a parallel standard arm
{parent=Electrical circuit}

Consider two <voltage dividers> across a common source, with impedances $Z_0,Z_1$ in one branch and $R_1,Z_p$ in the other. Equal midpoint <voltage> <phasors> imply $Z_0Z_p=R_1Z_1$. If $Z_0=R_0+1/(i\omega C_0)$, $Z_1=1/(i\omega C_1)$ and $Z_p=(1/R+i\omega C)^{-1}$, then separating real and imaginary parts proves $R_0=R_1C/C_1$ and $C_0=RC_1/R_1$. The topology, especially the parallel standard arm, is essential.

= Maxwell bridge
{parent=Electrical circuit}
{c}
{wiki}

A null <electrical circuit> for measuring a lossy <inductor> against a standard <capacitor>. If opposite arms are $R_x+i\omega L_x$ and $(1/R_1+i\omega C)^{-1}$, and the remaining arms are $R_2,R_3$, balance requires their products to agree. Consequently $R_x=R_2R_3/R_1$ and $L_x=CR_2R_3$. Two independent real adjustments are necessary; changing the source <frequency> does not change the ideal balance conditions.

= Equal-ratio RC bridge
{parent=Electrical circuit}
{title2=$\omega_0^{-2}=R_x^2C_x(C-C_x)$}

A bridge driven between top and bottom nodes has equal <resistors> on its left arms, upper-right <electrical impedance> $Z=R_x/(1+i\omega R_xC_x)$ and lower-right <electrical impedance> $W=R_v+1/(i\omega C)$. A zero detector <electric current> requires $Z=W$. Equating <real parts> and <imaginary parts> gives $R_v=R_x(1-C_x/C)$ and $\omega_0^{-2}=R_x^2C_x(C-C_x)$, requiring $0<C_x<C$. Its open-circuit right-minus-left junction <voltage> is $V_0(W-Z)/[2(W+Z)]$, whereas a connected ideal <ammeter> has zero terminal <voltage> at every <angular frequency>. This distinction separates a null-current measurement from an unloaded voltage measurement.

= Duty cycle
{parent=Electrical circuit}
{title2=$D=t_{\rm active}/t_{\rm cycle}$}

The fraction of an operating cycle spent in a specified active state. For two constant-current states, average current is $\bar I=DI_a+(1-D)I_s$; wake-up transients require an additional <energy> term.

= Duty cycling
{parent=Duty cycle}

Alternating an active mode with a lower-power mode to reduce average demand. Sleep current and transition overhead prevent average power from falling indefinitely in direct proportion to active time.

= Short circuit
{parent=Electrical circuit}
{wiki}

An ideal <short circuit> fixes zero <electric potential difference> across a terminal pair, corresponding to zero load <electrical impedance>. At the end of a lossless <transmission line>, its voltage-wave <reflection coefficient> is $-1$ and the terminal <voltage> is zero. The available current is still limited by source and circuit impedances; real short circuits can dissipate substantial <Joule heating>.

= Open circuit
{parent=Electrical circuit}

An ideal <open circuit> permits no conduction <electric current> through a terminal pair, corresponding to infinite load <electrical impedance>. At the end of a lossless <transmission line>, its voltage-wave <reflection coefficient> is $+1$ and the terminal current is zero. Real open terminals may retain parasitic <capacitance> and radiation loss.

= Virtual ground
{parent=Electrical circuit}

A circuit node held close to zero <electric potential> by active feedback, without being directly connected to the reference conductor. In an ideal linear <operational amplifier> with its non-inverting input grounded and stable negative feedback, $V_-=V_+=0$ while neither input draws <electric current>. External branch currents meeting the inverting node therefore obey <Kirchhoff's current law> without flowing into the amplifier input. Equality of the input potentials fails when the output saturates or the feedback ceases to enforce linear operation.

= Wien bridge
{c}
{parent=Electrical circuit}

An alternating-current <electrical circuit> comparing a parallel <resistor>-<capacitor> arm with a series <resistor>-<capacitor> arm. Let the upper-left arm have <electrical impedance> $Z_1=R_1/(1+i\omega R_1C_1)$, the lower-left arm $Z_2=R_2+1/(i\omega C_2)$, and the upper-right and lower-right arms have <electrical resistances> $R_3,R_4$. For input between the upper and lower nodes and an unloaded difference measurement between the arm junctions, <voltage divider> balance requires $Z_2/Z_1=R_4/R_3$. Separating real and imaginary parts gives
$$
\omega^2R_1R_2C_1C_2=1,\qquad
\frac{R_4}{R_3}=\frac{R_2}{R_1}+\frac{C_1}{C_2}.
$$
The balance <angular frequency> is positive for positive components. Equal $R_1=R_2=R$ and $C_1=C_2=C$ require $\omega=1/(RC)$ and $R_4=2R_3$. Reversing which reactive arm is upper changes the divider ratios, so the connections must always be specified.

= Wien bridge band-pass response
{c}
{parent=Wien bridge}
{title2=$H=-[3+i(u-u^{-1})]^{-1}$}

Use the equal-component <Wien bridge> with upper-left parallel arm and lower-left series arm. Let $A$ be the left arm junction, $C$ the upper input node, and $V_i$ the upper-minus-lower input <electric potential difference>. With $u=\omega RC$, the unloaded transfer ratio $H=(V_A-V_C)/V_i$ is the displayed expression. Its <band-pass filter> magnitude is $[9+(u-u^{-1})^2]^{-1/2}$, maximal at $u=1$ with $H=-1/3$. One continuous <wave phase> is $\phi=-\pi-\arctan[(u-u^{-1})/3]$, tending from $-\pi/2$ at low positive <frequency> to $-3\pi/2$ at high <frequency>. Reversing the measured output polarity multiplies $H$ by $-1$ and shifts its phase by $\pi$ without changing its magnitude.

= Two-port network
{parent=Electrical circuit}
{wiki}

A <two-port network> relates voltage and current, or incoming and outgoing travelling-wave amplitudes, at two pairs of electrical terminals. Power-normalized scattering parameters obey $b_i=\sum_jS_{ij}a_j$. In a reciprocal network with equal reference impedances, $S_{12}=S_{21}$. A load reflection at port 2 can return through both transmission coefficients to port 1.

= Parallel circuit
{parent=Electrical circuit}

A connection in which branches share both terminal nodes and therefore the same <electric potential difference>. Branch <electric currents> add. For sinusoidal signals, the total <electrical admittance> is the sum of branch <electrical admittances>; ideal <capacitances> add.

= LC circuit
{c}
{parent=Electrical circuit}
{wiki}
{title2=$\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$}

An ideal <electrical circuit> exchanges <energy> between an <inductor> and a <capacitor>. With no <electrical resistance> or applied drive, the <electric charge> obeys $Lq''+q/C=0$, giving <angular frequency> $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$. A <series RLC circuit> adds dissipation and can include an external drive.

= Parallel LC circuit
{parent=LC circuit}
{title2=$Z=j\omega L/(1-\omega^2LC)$}

An ideal <inductor> and <capacitor> in parallel have <electrical admittance> $1/Z=1/(j\omega L)+j\omega C$ for <phasors> with time dependence $e^{j\omega t}$. The branch currents cancel at <angular frequency> $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$, giving zero total <electrical admittance> and an infinite limiting <electrical impedance>. Below this <resonance> its <electrical reactance> is positive; above it the <electrical reactance> is negative. Losses make the peak finite.

= Series-fed parallel LC voltage response
{parent=LC circuit}
{title2=$H(\omega)=\{1-iR[1/(\omega L)-\omega C]\}^{-1}$}

An ideal <voltage> source drives a series <resistor> feeding a parallel <inductor> and <capacitor>. In the $e^{i\omega t}$ convention, the branch <voltage> ratio is the title expression. It is a band-pass response with unit peak at $1/\sqrt{LC}$, phase from $+\pi/2$ through zero to $-\pi/2$, and half-power bandwidth $1/(RC)$. At <resonance> the input current vanishes while equal and opposite <inductor>/<capacitor> currents remain finite. Reactive branch-current magnitudes must be combined as <phasors>, not added as positive scalars.

= Diode
{parent=Electrical circuit}
{wiki}

A <diode> is a two-terminal component with an asymmetric <current-voltage characteristic>: reversing its terminal <electric potential difference> need not reverse its <electric current> with the same magnitude. Name the terminals <diode anode> $A$ and <diode cathode> $K$. Define $V_D=\phi_A-\phi_K$ using their <electric potentials>, and take positive <electric current> $I_D$ from $A$ to $K$. The bar in the <diode> symbol marks $K$. Positive $V_D$ is the forward direction; negative $V_D$ is the reverse direction.<Functions of Rectifier Diodes by Toshiba Electronic Devices & Storage Corporation>[Functions of Rectifier Diodes, opening paragraph and Fig. 2-1(a): terminal names, polarity and symbol]{ref}

The asymmetry makes a <rectifier> possible: a <sinusoid> can drive <electric current> through a <resistor> on one half of each cycle. An ordinary <resistor> obeying <Ohm's law> instead reverses its <electric current> when the applied <electric potential difference> reverses. The useful idea is directional selection; the <ideal diode model> captures it, while the <Shockley diode equation> describes a smooth <exponential function> rather than an abrupt switch.

= Diode anode
{parent=Diode}
{title2=$A$}

The terminal of a <diode> at which positive forward <electric current> enters, with the orientation used in the <ideal diode model>.

= Diode cathode
{parent=Diode}
{title2=$K$}

The terminal of a <diode> at which positive forward <electric current> leaves. The bar in the <diode> symbol identifies this terminal.

= Ideal diode
{synonym}

The <ideal diode model> sets the conducting <electric potential difference> to zero and the blocking <electric current> to zero. With the stated orientation,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{conducting:}&\quad V_D=0,\quad I_D\geq0,\\
\text{blocking:}&\quad I_D=0,\quad V_D\leq0.
\end{aligned}
$$
<The Junction Diode by W. Marshall Leach, Jr.>[The Junction Diode, §1.12.1, printed p. 30: zero forward voltage and zero reverse current idealization]{ref}

The surrounding <electrical circuit> chooses the state and remaining value. The two branches meet at $V_D=I_D=0$. Their product vanishes, so the ideal <diode> dissipates no <power>: $P_D=V_DI_D=0$.

The <ideal diode model> omits forward drop, reverse leakage, <diode reverse breakdown> and switching delay.

= Shockley diode equation
{c}
{parent=Diode}
{wiki}
{title2=$I_D=I_S(e^{V_D/(nV_T)}-1)$}

For a <p-n junction> at fixed <temperature>, the <Shockley diode equation> models its <electric current> as
$$
I_D=I_S\left(\exp\frac{V_D}{nV_T}-1\right),\qquad V_T=\frac{k_BT}{q}.
$$
Here $I_S>0$ is the <diode saturation current>, $n>0$ the <diode ideality factor>, $V_T$ the <thermal voltage>, $k_B$ the <Boltzmann constant>, and $q>0$ the <elementary charge>.<The Junction Diode by W. Marshall Leach, Jr.>[The Junction Diode, §1.2, printed p. 4, equation (1.1) and parameter definitions]{ref}

For an original model example, choose $I_S=1\,\mathrm{nA}$, $n=2$, and $T=300\,\mathrm K$. Using $k_B=1.380649\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J/K}$ and $q=1.602176634\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$ gives $V_T=25.8520\,\mathrm{mV}$. Then $V_D=0.600\,\mathrm V$ gives $I_D=0.109591\,\mathrm{mA}$, whereas $V_D=0.800\,\mathrm V$ gives $I_D=5.24450\,\mathrm{mA}$. These are predictions of the declared model. There is no universal turn-on <electric potential difference>: solving the <Shockley diode equation> for the value needed at a chosen <electric current> gives
$$
V_D=nV_T\ln\left(1+\frac{I_D}{I_S}\right),\qquad I_D>-I_S.
$$
At $I_D\gg I_S$, increasing $V_D$ by $0.100\,\mathrm V$ multiplies the <electric current> by approximately $e^{0.100/(nV_T)}=6.91772$. At $V_D=-0.300\,\mathrm V$ the same model gives $I_D=-0.996979\,\mathrm{nA}$. Its reverse branch approaches $-I_S$; it cannot describe <diode reverse breakdown>.

A constructed extension adds internal series <electrical resistance> $R_s$ to a junction whose <electric potential difference> is $V_j$. Applying the <Kirchhoff cycle law> gives
$$
V_D=V_j+I_DR_s
=nV_T\ln\left(1+\frac{I_D}{I_S}\right)+I_DR_s.
$$
For $R_s=20\,\Omega$ and the same $I_S,n,T$, sustaining $I_D=5.24450\,\mathrm{mA}$ needs $V_D=0.904890\,\mathrm V$: $0.800\,\mathrm V$ across the junction plus $0.104890\,\mathrm V$ across its internal <electrical resistance>. The <derivative>
$$
\frac{dV_D}{dI_D}=\frac{nV_T}{I_D+I_S}+R_s
$$
shows why the <current-voltage characteristic> becomes nearly a <straight line> when the internal series <electrical resistance> dominates. This is still a fixed-<temperature> model; self-heating can change $I_S$, $n$ and $V_T$. Real <diodes> also have leakage and charge-storage effects omitted here.<The Junction Diode by W. Marshall Leach, Jr.>[The Junction Diode, §§1.3, 1.8 and 1.11: temperature dependence, charge storage and series bulk resistance]{ref}

= Diode minority-carrier diffusion current
{parent=Shockley diode equation}
{title2=$I_S=e\mathcal A n_i^2[D_n/(L_nN_A)+D_p/(L_pN_D)]$}

For a long, nondegenerate, ideal <p-n junction> under low injection, the <diode saturation current> is set by <minority carriers> supplied from the neutral regions. Their equilibrium densities are $n_{p0}=n_i^2/N_A$ and $p_{n0}=n_i^2/N_D$. Solving the steady diffusion and recombination equations gives $I_S=e\mathcal A(D_nn_{p0}/L_n+D_pp_{n0}/L_p)$, where $\mathcal A$ is junction area, $D_n,D_p$ are diffusion coefficients, and $L_n,L_p$ are diffusion lengths. Reverse bias suppresses opposite majority-carrier injection exponentially while collecting this minority supply. Thus $I_S$ is approximately bias independent before breakdown, but depends strongly on <temperature> through $n_i^2$ and on transport, doping, and geometry.

= Extracting a semiconductor band gap from diode saturation current
{parent=Diode saturation current}

In a diffusion-dominated <p-n junction>, $I_s=C(T)n_i^2$ with a transport prefactor determined by minority-carrier diffusion and dopant densities. Combining this with the <intrinsic carrier concentration> gives, for fixed gap and effective masses,
$$
\log\frac{I_s}{C(T)T^3}=\text{constant}-\frac{E_g}{k_BT}.
$$
The slope against $1/T$ is $-E_g/k_B$. A measured <diode ideality factor> helps identify the transport regime: generation or recombination current can have a different dependence on $n_i$, so its activation slope cannot automatically be interpreted with the diffusion formula.

= Diode characteristic measurement
{parent=Diode}

The New Jersey Institute of Technology laboratory uses a <silicon> 1N4148 <diode>. For its forward <current-voltage characteristic>, the supply operates in current-source mode; a benchtop <voltmeter> independently reads the <diode> <electric potential difference>. Small <electric current> steps resolve the steep branch. For reverse measurements, a sensitive <ammeter> sits in series; the handout suggests a $10$–$100\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ series <resistor>. Reversing the applied polarity supplies the comparison. Meter placement must avoid counting the <voltmeter>'s own <electric current>, and the zero reading may have an offset. The same <diode> is then measured with an Agilent U2722A source measure unit for graphical comparison.<Part B Semiconductor Devices: Diodes 1 by New Jersey Institute of Technology>[Part B, Experiments with Semiconductor Diodes, items 1 and 2(a): preparation, current control, independent voltage readout, reverse series resistor and same-device comparison]{ref}

The prescribed analysis uses a <logarithmic scale> for forward <electric current> to infer $I_S$ and $n$ from the straight region. Large-<electric current> departures can indicate series <electrical resistance>. The handout cautions that the accessible range may not determine it precisely; meter loading also matters. This is a measurement procedure, without a published set of device readings.<Part B Semiconductor Devices: Diodes 1 by New Jersey Institute of Technology>[Experiments with Semiconductor Diodes, item 1, parameter extraction and high-current trend-line limitations; Report and Analysis]{ref}

The reason for the <logarithmic scale> follows directly from the <Shockley diode equation>. If $I_D\gg I_S$ and series <electrical resistance> is negligible,
$$
\ln\left(\frac{I_D}{I_0}\right)\approx\ln\left(\frac{I_S}{I_0}\right)+\frac{V_D}{nV_T},
$$
where $I_0>0$ is a fixed reference <electric current> making the <logarithm> dimensionless. The slope is $1/(nV_T)$, so extracting $n$ needs the junction <temperature>. Fitting only an appropriate <interval> matters: a straight-looking subset cannot establish the model at all <electric currents>.

= Half-wave rectification
{synonym}

A <half-wave rectifier> with one <diode> and a <resistor> load passes one half of each input cycle.<The Junction Diode by W. Marshall Leach, Jr.>[The Junction Diode, §1.12.1, printed p. 30: alternate-half-cycle conduction into a resistor load]{ref}

For an original ideal example, let an ideal <voltage source> supply the <sinusoid> $v_S(t)=V_p\sin(\omega t)$, with $V_p=5\,\mathrm V$ and <angular frequency> $\omega>0$. Connect its positive reference terminal to the <diode anode>, the <diode cathode> to the upper terminal of a load <resistor> with <electrical resistance> $R_L=1\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$, and the lower terminal to the source return. Let $v_L$ be the upper-minus-lower load <electric potential difference>, and $i_L$ the downward load <electric current>. Assume the <ideal diode model>, zero source <electrical resistance>, and no filter <capacitor>, <inductor> or switching delay.

The <Kirchhoff cycle law> gives $v_S=V_D+v_L$, and <Ohm's law> gives $v_L=R_Li_L$. If $v_S>0$, the blocking assumption would give $V_D=v_S>0$, contradicting the <ideal diode model>, so the <diode> conducts with $V_D=0$. If $v_S<0$, conduction would give $i_L=v_S/R_L<0$, also forbidden, so the <diode> blocks. At the crossings both values vanish. Thus
$$
v_L(t)=\max\{0,V_p\sin(\omega t)\},\qquad i_L(t)=\frac{v_L(t)}{R_L}.
$$
The peak load <electric current> is $5\,\mathrm{mA}$. The output is unidirectional but still varies in <time>, reaching zero throughout every negative half-cycle. Over a <period of a function> $2\pi/\omega$, write the <wave phase> as $\theta=\omega t$. Its <average value of a function> and <root mean square> follow from two <integrals>:
$$
\overline v_L=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^\pi V_p\sin\theta\,d\theta=\frac{V_p}{\pi}=1.59155\,\mathrm V,\qquad
v_{L,\mathrm{rms}}=\left(\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^\pi V_p^2\sin^2\theta\,d\theta\right)^{1/2}=\frac{V_p}{2}=2.500\,\mathrm V.
$$
Consequently the average load <power> is $v_{L,\mathrm{rms}}^2/R_L=6.25\,\mathrm{mW}$, not $\overline v_L^{\,2}/R_L$. The ideal <diode> has zero loss; the <resistor> converts supplied <energy> into <Joule heating>. The same switching argument predicts $v_L=\min(0,v_S)$ when the <diode> is reversed. Directional selection changes the waveform and its <average value of a function>; it does not by itself produce a constant output.

= Blue light-emitting diode
{parent=Light-emitting diode}

Blue transitions motivate <wide-bandgap semiconductors>, but a large <band gap> alone cannot guarantee efficient emission. <Gallium nitride> has a <direct band gap>; <indium gallium nitride> composition tunes the transition <energy>.https://www.kapitza.ras.ru/~glazkov/students/Nobel-akasaki-lecture.pdf[§2, printed pp. 8–10: direct/indirect gaps and nitride composition range]{ref}

For an emitted <photon>, with <frequency> $\nu$ and vacuum <wavelength> $\lambda$,
$$
E_\gamma=h\nu=\frac{hc}{\lambda},\qquad
\lambda=\frac{hc}{E_\gamma},
$$
where $h$ is the <Planck constant> and $c$ the vacuum <speed of light>. Multiplying <energy> by <wavelength> gives $hc=1239.841984\,\mathrm{eV\,nm}$. Thus chosen <wavelengths> $450\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $630\,\mathrm{nm}$ correspond to $2.75520\,\mathrm{eV}$ and $1.96800\,\mathrm{eV}$: blue requires more <energy> per <photon> than red. A stipulated transition <energy> $3.40\,\mathrm{eV}$ instead gives $364.659\,\mathrm{nm}$, in the <ultraviolet>. These are conversions, not measured emission lines.

For near-edge emission, $E_\gamma\approx E_g$ estimates the <photon> <energy> from the emitting material's <band gap>. <Quantum-well> confinement or impurity transitions can change it. The 1994 <blue light-emitting diode> used <zinc>-doped <indium gallium nitride>, emitting below its near-edge transition.<Candela-class high-brightness InGaN⁄AlGaN double-heterostructure blue-light-emitting diodes by Shuji Nakamura and others>[p. 1688, Fig. 2 discussion: Zn-related emission]{ref}

The measured forward terminal <electric potential difference> $V_f$ is also not fixed by $E_g/q$, where $q$ is the <elementary charge>. It depends on <electric current>, <temperature>, contacts and series <electrical resistance>. Even the simple <Shockley diode equation> with series <electrical resistance> has $V_f=V_j+IR_s$, whereas an optical transition does not acquire an extra <energy> $qIR_s$ merely because the contact drop increases. Consequently, extracting a <band gap> from one terminal-voltage reading is not justified.

= GaN blue LED development
{c}
{parent=Blue light-emitting diode}

<Isamu Akasaki> chose <metalorganic vapour-phase epitaxy> and <sapphire>, which tolerated hot <ammonia>. Large <lattice mismatch> hindered film growth. In 1985, <Hiroshi Amano> and <Isamu Akasaki>'s group developed an <aluminium nitride> <epitaxial buffer layer> deposited before hotter <gallium nitride> growth. The buffer supplies initial growth sites; growing islands join into a continuous film. = Light-emitting diode efficiency
{parent=Light-emitting diode}

Efficiency has several denominators. Specify a steady <electric current> $I>0$, positive terminal <electric potential difference> $V_f$, and a simplified narrow emission <spectrum> with <photon> <energy> $E_\gamma$. Assume at most one radiative <photon> per injected <electron> and <electron hole> pair. Let $\eta_{\rm inj}$ be the fraction of the electrical pair-supply rate $I/q$ reaching the active layer, $\eta_{\rm rad}$ the fraction of those pairs recombining radiatively, and $\eta_{\rm ext}$ the fraction of internally emitted <photons> that escape. Then counting the successive events gives
$$
\dot N_{\rm active}=\eta_{\rm inj}\frac Iq,\qquad
\dot N_{\rm internal}=\eta_{\rm rad}\dot N_{\rm active},\qquad
\dot N_{\rm out}=\eta_{\rm ext}\dot N_{\rm internal}.
$$
Define <internal quantum efficiency> here conditionally on pairs reaching the active layer: $\eta_{\rm IQE}=\eta_{\rm rad}$. The internal <photon> yield per electrical pair is instead $\eta_{\rm inj}\eta_{\rm IQE}$. Stating this convention matters when another definition folds injection into <internal quantum efficiency>. The <external quantum efficiency> and <wall-plug efficiency> are
$$
\eta_{\rm EQE}=\frac{\dot N_{\rm out}}{I/q}
=\eta_{\rm inj}\eta_{\rm IQE}\eta_{\rm ext},\qquad
\eta_{\rm WPE}=\frac{P_{\rm opt}}{IV_f}
=\eta_{\rm EQE}\frac{E_\gamma}{qV_f},\qquad
P_{\rm opt}=\dot N_{\rm out}E_\gamma.
$$
These are <photon>-count and <energy>-flow identities for the declared model. For a broad <spectrum>, replace $E_\gamma$ by the mean escaping <photon> <energy>, weighted by photon number, when computing optical <power>.

A stipulated example chooses $I=20\,\mathrm{mA}$, $V_f=3.20\,\mathrm V$, $\lambda=450\,\mathrm{nm}$, $\eta_{\rm inj}=0.90$, $\eta_{\rm IQE}=0.80$ and $\eta_{\rm ext}=0.50$. For every 100 electrical pairs, 90 reach the active layer, 72 emit internally, and 36 <photons> escape. Thus $\eta_{\rm EQE}=0.36$, not $0.80$. Using $q=1.602176634\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$ gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
I/q&=1.24830\times10^{17}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1},\\
\dot N_{\rm out}&=4.49389\times10^{16}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1},\\
P_{\rm opt}&=19.8375\,\mathrm{mW},\qquad IV_f=64.0\,\mathrm{mW},\\
\eta_{\rm WPE}&=0.309960.
\end{aligned}
$$
The <dimensional analysis> is transparent: $\mathrm{A/C}=\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, $\mathrm{J/s}=\mathrm W$, and $\mathrm{CV}=\mathrm J$. The optical and electrical efficiencies differ because $E_\gamma/q=2.75520\,\mathrm V$ differs from the chosen $V_f$.

The factors suggest different improvements. Better confinement can reduce injection leakage; suppressing <nonradiative recombination> raises the radiative fraction; improving <light extraction efficiency> helps already-emitted <photons> escape. A <photon> trapped by <total internal reflection> has already passed the radiative step and can still be lost through absorption. Improving extraction therefore need not change the internal radiative fraction. 

<Shuji Nakamura>'s lecture cautions that why <indium gallium nitride> emits efficiently despite many defects was not completely settled in that account.https://journals.aps.org/rmp/pdf/10.1103/RevModPhys.87.1139[§IV.B, pp. 1144–1145: high defect density, localization hypothesis and stated uncertainty]{ref}

As an independent consistency check on the 1994 reported values, treating its broad <spectrum> as monochromatic at the $450\,\mathrm{nm}$ peak gives
$$
\eta_{\rm EQE}\approx\frac{(1.5\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm W)q}{(0.020\,\mathrm A)(hc/(450\,\mathrm{nm}))}=0.02722.
$$
This agrees at the quoted precision with the reported $2.7\%$. Its optical/electrical <power> ratio is instead $1.5\,\mathrm{mW}/(20\,\mathrm{mA}\times3.6\,\mathrm V)=2.0833\%$. The first estimate uses a peak in place of a photon-number-weighted mean, so agreement is a consistency check rather than an exact reanalysis of the measured <spectrum>.

= Phosphor-converted white light-emitting diode
{parent=White light-emitting diode}

To isolate conversion losses, suppose a converter receives 100 blue <photons> at $\lambda_b=450\,\mathrm{nm}$, absorbs a fraction $a=0.60$, and has <fluorescence quantum yield> $\Phi=0.90$: 0.90 converted <photons> per absorbed blue <photon>. Stipulate that every converted <photon> has $\lambda_c=560\,\mathrm{nm}$, that unabsorbed blue is transmitted, and that both outputs escape without further losses. The output is then 40 blue and 54 converted <photons>. The total photon yield is $0.94$, while the retained optical <energy> fraction is
$$
\frac{P_{\rm after}}{P_{\rm before}}
=(1-a)+a\Phi\frac{\lambda_b}{\lambda_c}
=0.40+0.60\times0.90\times\frac{450}{560}
=0.833929.
$$
The lower <energy> of each converted <photon> matters even if $\Phi=1$. Combining this stipulated converter with the preceding chosen <light-emitting diode> gives $P_{\rm after}=16.5430\,\mathrm{mW}$ and an overall optical/electrical <power> ratio $0.258485$. These chosen narrow lines and photon counts illustrate conversion accounting; they do not establish a white appearance, color quality or <luminous efficacy>. Those require the full <spectrum> and a model of human visual sensitivity.

= Operational amplifier
{parent=Electronic amplifier}
{wiki}

= Op amp
{synonym}

An <operational amplifier> is a differential <electronic amplifier> whose unloaded <voltage> is modeled in its linear regime by $v_o=A(s)(v_+-v_-)$. Its large <open-loop gain>, large <input impedance> and small <output impedance> make <negative feedback> useful for setting a reproducible <transfer function> with external components. Supply voltages bound the available output swing; the linear model does not apply after saturation.

= Op amp differentiator
{parent=Operational amplifier}
{title2=$V_{\rm out}=-RC\,dV_{\rm in}/dt$}

For an <ideal operational amplifier> with grounded non-inverting input, connect an input <capacitor> $C$ to the inverting input and a feedback <resistor> $R$ from output to that input. The <virtual ground> and <Kirchhoff's current law> give $C\dot V_{\rm in}=-V_{\rm out}/R$. The ideal response amplifies high-frequency noise, so practical differentiators restrict its operating band and maintain feedback stability.

= Summing amplifier
{parent=Operational amplifier}
{title2=$V_o=-R_f\sum_jV_j/R_j$}

An inverting <operational amplifier> can combine currents from several input <resistors> at one <virtual ground>. <Kirchhoff's current law> and negligible amplifier input current give the displayed weighted sum. Replacing the feedback resistance by a <capacitor> gives a weighted <op amp integrator>. The formulas assume stable <negative feedback> and operation within the amplifier's linear output range.

= Noise gain of an operational amplifier
{parent=Operational amplifier}
{title2=$G_{\rm n}=1/\beta$}

The closed-loop gain from an equivalent differential input-<voltage> disturbance to the output is the noise gain. For an ideal-input <inverting amplifier>, $\beta=R_1/(R_1+R_2)$ and $G_{\rm n}=1+R_2/R_1$, although the <voltage gain> is $-R_2/R_1$. A dominant-pole amplifier has approximate closed-loop <bandwidth> $f_T/G_{\rm n}$ when loop gain is large at low frequency and the circuit is stable.

= Non-inverting operational amplifier
{parent=Operational amplifier}
{title2=$K=1+R_f/R_g$}

An operational-amplifier configuration applying the signal to the non-inverting input and returning a fraction of output to the inverting input. In stable unsaturated ideal operation, zero input current and equal input voltages give the displayed <voltage gain>. The feedback <resistor> $R_f$ connects output to the inverting input and $R_g$ connects that input to the reference <wavefunction node>.

= Amplifier saturation
{parent=Operational amplifier}

In <amplifier saturation>, the requested output lies outside the amplifier's linear operating range, such as when supply rails limit output voltage. The <negative feedback> <virtual short> approximation then need not hold. Output swing, current drive and recovery behaviour depend on the device and load; finite <slew rate> is a separate dynamic limit even when the output has not reached a rail.

= Operational-amplifier negative feedback
{parent=Operational amplifier}
{title2=$A_{\rm cl}=A/(1+A\beta)$}

Return a fraction of the output to the inverting input so that it opposes the input difference. Large stable <loop gain> makes the <closed-loop gain> depend mainly on the external feedback ratio rather than the uncertain <open-loop gain>. Frequency-dependent phase lag can destabilize the loop; saturation and finite <slew rate> also limit the approximation.<Operational-amplifier negative feedback by Analog Devices>[Chapter 3, §§3.2.2–3.2.4]{ref}

= Closed-loop gain
{parent=Operational-amplifier negative feedback}
{title2=$A_{\rm cl}=A/(1+A\beta)$}

The <closed-loop gain> is the input-to-output transfer after closing the feedback connection. In the elementary noninverting voltage model it tends to $1/\beta$ for large stable <loop gain>, rather than retaining the uncertain <open-loop gain> $A$. A frequency-dependent transfer must be checked for stability before that approximation is used.

= Loop gain
{parent=Operational-amplifier negative feedback}
{title2=$L(\omega)=A(\omega)\beta(\omega)$}

The <loop gain> is the product of the forward amplifier response and the return-network fraction, with the summing-junction minus sign kept explicit. Closed-loop poles are determined by $1+L=0$ in this convention. Large low-frequency magnitude controls gain error, while frequency-dependent phase affects stability.

= Phase margin
{parent=Loop gain}
{title2=$\mathrm{PM}=\pi+\arg L(\omega_c),\quad |L(\omega_c)|=1$}

The <phase margin> measures the remaining phase lag before a negative-feedback loop reaches the critical phase $-\pi$ at unity loop magnitude, using a continuous phase convention. Positive adequate margin is a useful stability and transient-response design diagnostic for standard single-crossover amplifier loops. More general unstable open-loop systems and multiple crossovers require the full stability criterion.

= Noninverting amplifier
{parent=Operational amplifier}

A voltage-feedback <operational amplifier> driven at its noninverting input, with a feedback divider $R_f,R_g$ at its inverting input, has ideal gain $1+R_f/R_g$ under stable <negative feedback>.

= Inverting amplifier
{parent=Operational amplifier}

A voltage-feedback <operational amplifier> with its noninverting input grounded and input and feedback resistances $R_i,R_f$ has ideal gain $-R_f/R_i$. <Virtual short> and negligible input current give the result by node-current balance.

= Inverting RC high-pass amplifier
{parent=Inverting amplifier}
{title2=$H(\omega)=-i\omega R_fC/(1+i\omega R_iC)$}

An ideal <operational amplifier> with a series input <resistor> and <capacitor>, resistive feedback and a grounded noninverting terminal realizes the displayed <high-pass filter>. <Virtual ground> and <Kirchhoff's current law> give $H=-R_f/[R_i+1/(i\omega C)]$. Its <phase> is $-\pi/2-\tan^{-1}(\omega R_iC)$ in the $e^{i\omega t}$ convention. Equal resistances do not make its <phase> <frequency> independent.

= Finite-input-resistance inverting amplifier
{parent=Inverting amplifier}
{title2=$G=-\dfrac{R_2/R_1}{1+(1+R_2/R_1+R_2/R_{\rm in})/A}$}

With the noninverting input grounded, a differential <input impedance> $R_{\rm in}$ draws current from the summing node. For zero <output impedance>, $V_o=-Av$ and <Kirchhoff's current law> gives $(v-V_i)/R_1+(v-V_o)/R_2+v/R_{\rm in}=0$. Eliminating $v$ yields the displayed <voltage gain>. The resistor-ratio result requires $|A|\gg1+R_2/R_1+R_2/R_{\rm in}$ as well as stable, unsaturated operation. The same expression applies to small-signal <phasors> with complex $A(f)$.

= Inverting two-pole band-pass filter
{parent=Inverting amplifier}
{title2=$H(s)=-K s\tau_1/[(1+s\tau_1)(1+s\tau_2)]$}

An ideal inverting <operational amplifier> with series $R_1,C_1$ input and parallel $R_2,C_2$ feedback has the displayed <transfer function>, where $K=R_2/R_1$ and $\tau_i=R_iC_i$. Its peak is at $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{\tau_1\tau_2}$ with voltage gain $K\tau_1/(\tau_1+\tau_2)$. A wide input-high-pass/feedback-low-pass separation produces a broad plateau, not a narrow <resonance>. Validity requires stable <negative feedback>, sufficient loop gain and linear output operation.

= Selectivity bound for a two-real-pole band-pass
{parent=Inverting two-pole band-pass filter}
{title2=$Q=\sqrt{\tau_1\tau_2}/(\tau_1+\tau_2)\le1/2$}

For a response proportional to $s/[(1+s\tau_1)(1+s\tau_2)]$ with positive time constants, its half-power width is $1/\tau_1+1/\tau_2$. Combining with the geometric-mean centre <frequency> gives the displayed <quality factor>; the <arithmetic-geometric mean inequality> proves the bound. Equality requires matched time constants and gives half-power <frequencies> $(\sqrt2\pm1)\omega_0$. Adjusting gains does not make this topology a high-Q resonant filter.

= Inverting low-pass amplifier
{parent=Inverting amplifier}
{title2=$G(\omega)=-R_f/[R_i(1+i\omega R_fC)]$}

An ideal <operational amplifier> with a grounded noninverting input, input <resistor> $R_i$, and parallel feedback <resistor> $R_f$ and <capacitor> $C$ implements a <low-pass filter>. The <virtual short> and <Kirchhoff's current law> give $V_{\rm out}/V_{\rm in}=-Z_f/R_i$, where $Z_f=R_f/(1+i\omega R_fC)$. The DC gain magnitude is $R_f/R_i$ and the <cutoff frequency> is $1/(2\pi R_fC)$. The <capacitance> reduces high-<frequency> feedback <electrical impedance>, causing the gain magnitude to fall.

= Virtual short
{parent=Operational amplifier}

Under stable <negative feedback> and within its linear operating range, an ideal voltage-feedback <operational amplifier> has equal input voltages. This relation is a consequence of large open-loop gain, and does not imply current flow between the inputs.

= Op amp integrator
{parent=Operational amplifier}
{wiki}

An <op amp integrator> places a <resistor> $R$ between the input source and the inverting input of an <operational amplifier>, and a feedback <capacitor> $C$ between that input and the output, with the non-inverting input grounded. For an <ideal operational amplifier>, <Kirchhoff's current law> gives $v_{\mathrm{in}}/R=-C\,dv_o/dt$, hence
$$
v_o(t)=v_o(t_0)-\frac1{RC}\int_{t_0}^t v_{\mathrm{in}}(u)\,du,\qquad H(s)=-\frac1{sRC}.
$$
The initial capacitor charge supplies the integration constant. A parallel feedback <resistor> $R_f$ replaces the transfer by $H(s)=-(R_f/R)/(1+sR_fC)$: the direct-current gain becomes finite while integration is recovered when $|sR_fC|\gg1$. This limits offset-driven saturation. https://wiki.analog.com/university/courses/electronics/text/chapter-2[Analog Devices, Chapter 2, integrator and parallel feedback resistance]{ref}

= Finite-gain operational-amplifier integrator
{parent=Op amp integrator}
{title2=$H(s)=\frac{sCr_o-A(s)}{1+sC\{r_o+R[1+A(s)]\}}$}

Consider an <op amp integrator> with infinite <input impedance>, an internal controlled voltage $u=-A(s)v$ where $v$ is the inverting-input voltage, and a series <output impedance> equal to a resistance $r_o$. Assume no external output load. Writing $y$ for the output, <Kirchhoff's current law> gives
$$
\frac{v_{\mathrm{in}}-v}{R}=sC(v-y),\qquad \frac{u-y}{r_o}=sC(y-v).
$$
Eliminating $u$ and $v$ yields
$$
\boxed{H(s)=\frac{y}{v_{\mathrm{in}}}=\frac{sCr_o-A(s)}{1+sC\{r_o+R[1+A(s)]\}}.}
$$
The expression at $r_o=0$ is obtained by its limit. In particular, constant gain $A_0$ and $r_o=0$ give $H(s)=-A_0/[1+sRC(1+A_0)]$: a finite direct-current gain followed by an integrating regime. For the dominant-pole model $A(s)=A_0/(1+s/\omega_a)$ and $r_o=0$,
$$
H(s)=\frac{-A_0}{1+s[\omega_a^{-1}+(1+A_0)RC]+s^2RC/\omega_a}.
$$
If $A_0\gg1$ and $A_0\omega_aRC\gg1$, there are separated turnover frequencies near $1/(A_0RC)$ and $A_0\omega_a$. The gain magnitude is respectively constant, proportional to $\omega^{-1}$ and proportional to $\omega^{-2}$. With the convention $e^{i\omega t}$ a continuous phase choice passes from $180^\circ$ through $90^\circ$ to $0^\circ$. If $r_o>0$ and $A(i\omega)\to0$, capacitive feedthrough instead gives the ultimate limit $r_o/(R+r_o)$. Specifying finite gain alone therefore does not specify a unique high-frequency response.

= Slew rate

The <slew rate> bounds the large-signal output-voltage change per unit time. It is distinct from a small-signal <gain-bandwidth product>. A sinusoidal output of amplitude $V_p$ and <angular frequency> $\omega$ needs $\omega V_p$ below the available <slew rate> to avoid this source of distortion.

= Input bias current
{parent=Operational amplifier}
{title2=$I_b$}

An <input bias current> is a direct-current component drawn by an input terminal of a real <operational amplifier>. Through a source resistance it produces a voltage error, and through the feedback <capacitor> of an <op amp integrator> it can produce an output ramp. Its magnitude and sign depend on the amplifier technology and operating conditions.

= Input offset voltage
{parent=Operational amplifier}

The <input offset voltage> of an <operational amplifier> is the differential direct-current input voltage needed to make its output zero at a specified operating point. Its effect resembles a small unwanted input signal and can cause sustained output drift in an <op amp integrator>.

= Open-loop gain
{parent=Operational amplifier}
{wiki}
{title2=$A(s)$}

The <open-loop gain> of an <operational amplifier> is the ratio of its output voltage to its differential input voltage before an external <feedback control> connection is applied. A common small-signal model is $A(s)=A_0/(1+s/\omega_a)$, with a large direct-current gain $A_0$ and a dominant pole at $-\omega_a$. Above $\omega_a$ its magnitude falls approximately inversely with <angular frequency>.

= Gain-bandwidth product
{parent=Open-loop gain}
{title2=$f_t$}

For a dominant-pole voltage-feedback <operational amplifier> with $A(s)=A_0/(1+s/\omega_a)$ and $A_0\gg1$, the unity-gain <angular frequency> is approximately $\omega_t=A_0\omega_a$. In the range where this pole dominates, a roughly constant closed-loop noise gain $G_N$ gives a bandwidth approximately $f_t/G_N$. This approximation depends on the amplifier model and <feedback control> circuit; it is not a universal statement about all amplifiers or all frequency-dependent feedback networks. https://wiki.analog.com/university/courses/alm1k/alm-lab-gbw[Analog Devices, Activity: Op-Amp Gain Bandwidth Product, dominant-pole gain and unity-gain frequency]{ref}

= Ideal operational amplifier
{parent=Operational amplifier}

An <ideal operational amplifier> has infinite <open-loop gain> and <input impedance>, zero <output impedance>, unlimited response speed and zero <input offset voltage> and <input bias current>. Its input currents vanish. In a stable linear circuit with <negative feedback> and a finite output, its differential input voltage tends to zero as the <open-loop gain> tends to infinity. Thus $i_+=i_-=0$ and $v_+=v_-$ are the familiar conditional circuit rules. The equality of the input voltages cannot be imposed on an open-loop, unstable or saturated circuit.

= Inverting operational amplifier
{parent=Ideal operational amplifier}
{title2=$V_o/V_i=-R_f/R_{\rm in}$}

Connect a <resistor> $R_{\rm in}$ from the input source to the inverting input, a feedback <resistor> $R_f$ from output to that same input, and ground the non-inverting input. For ideal linear operation the inverting node is a <virtual ground>. <Kirchhoff's current law> gives $V_i/R_{\rm in}+V_o/R_f=0$, yielding the displayed voltage gain. The source sees <input impedance> $R_{\rm in}$, even though the amplifier input itself has infinite <input impedance>.

= Voltage gain

The ratio of output to input <signal> <electric potential difference> for specified source and load conditions. A signed <voltage gain> records whether the output reverses polarity; its value can depend on <frequency>.

= Current gain

The ratio of output to input <signal> <electric current>, with specified reference directions and load conditions. A large <current gain> alone does not determine <power gain>.

= Power gain

The ratio of <signal> <power> delivered to a specified output load to <signal> <power> accepted at a specified input, with $P_{\rm in}>0$. An <amplifier> draws additional <energy> from its supply, so a <power gain> above one does not create <energy>.

= Lock-in amplifier

A <lock-in amplifier> measures the component of an input <signal> synchronized with a reference at a chosen <frequency>. It multiplies the input by a reference <sinusoid>, then uses a <low-pass filter> to retain the slowly varying product. Two references in <phase quadrature> recover both <amplitude> and <phase difference>. A shared timing source keeps the relative <wave phase> stable: this is coherent detection.<Model SR830 DSP Lock-In Amplifier manual by Stanford Research Systems>[Model SR830 DSP Lock-In Amplifier manual, revision 2.5, printed pp. 3-1–3-2: multiplication, filtering, reference locking and dual-phase detection]{ref}

The useful trick is to move the wanted <frequency> to zero before averaging. An <electronic amplifier> alone enlarges both the wanted <signal> and unwanted <noise>; a <lock-in amplifier> instead selects a component by its timing. A <photodiode>'s <electric current> can first be converted to an <electric potential difference>, so the same calculation can measure weak modulated <light>. The <chopped-light lock-in experiment> makes this selection tangible.

= Phase-sensitive detection
{parent=Lock-in amplifier}
{title2=$X=A\cos\phi,\quad Y=A\sin\phi$}

<phase-sensitive detection> correlates an input with a synchronized reference and filters the product. Sinusoidal quadrature references recover both <signal> <amplitude> and relative phase. Narrow detection <bandwidth> rejects incoherent <noise> but cannot reject a background coherent at the same <frequency>; changing the integration <time> also changes settling and tracking speed.<Model SR830 DSP Lock-In Amplifier manual by Stanford Research Systems>[printed pp.3-1–3-3]{ref}

= Lock-in quadratures
{parent=Lock-in amplifier}
{title2=$X=A\cos\phi,\quad Y=A\sin\phi$}

Let the input <electric potential difference> be $s(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$, with peak <amplitude> $A\geq0$, <angular frequency> $\omega>0$, and <phase difference> $\phi$ relative to $\cos\omega t$. For an observation <interval> $[0,T]$ containing an <integer> number $N\geq1$ of cycles, each lasting one <period of a function>, $T=2\pi N/\omega$, define the <lock-in quadratures> in a peak convention:
$$
X=\frac2T\int_0^T s(t)\cos\omega t\,dt,\qquad
Y=-\frac2T\int_0^T s(t)\sin\omega t\,dt.
$$
These <integrals> average the products with dimensionless references $2\cos\omega t$ and $-2\sin\omega t$; $X$ and $Y$ have the input's units. The minus sign makes positive $\phi$ give positive $Y$. The <trigonometric functions> give
$$
\begin{aligned}
2s(t)\cos\omega t&=A\cos\phi+A\cos(2\omega t+\phi),\\
-2s(t)\sin\omega t&=A\sin\phi-A\sin(2\omega t+\phi).
\end{aligned}
$$
The oscillating terms have zero <integral> over the stated <interval>, proving $X=A\cos\phi$ and $Y=A\sin\phi$. Their <orthogonality> turns an oscillating <signal> into two constant coordinates.

Represent those coordinates as a <vector>, or as the <complex number> $Z=X+\mathrm iY$. Its <modulus> and <complex argument> recover the peak <amplitude> and <phase difference>:
$$
R=\sqrt{X^2+Y^2}=A,\qquad
\phi=\operatorname{atan2}(Y,X)\pmod{2\pi},\qquad R>0.
$$
Here $\operatorname{atan2}(Y,X)$ selects the <angle> with the correct signs of both coordinates, rather than applying the <arctangent> to $Y/X$ and losing the quadrant. When $X=Y=0$, the <amplitude> is zero and the <phase difference> is undefined. When $\phi=\pi/2$, $X=0$ despite a nonzero <signal>; measuring $Y$ prevents this blind spot. Rotating both references by a fixed <angle> rotates the coordinate <vector> but preserves $R$.

The factors of two above deliberately recover peak <amplitude>. The SR830 instead reports the selected sinusoidal component in <root mean square> units: in a calibrated, zero-offset display, $R_{\mathrm{rms}}=R/\sqrt2$. For $R=0.4\,\mathrm V$ this is $0.282843\,\mathrm V$, while the peak-to-peak span of that component is $0.8\,\mathrm V$. Display normalization, reference sign, gain and <angle> units must be checked for the particular instrument.<Model SR830 DSP Lock-In Amplifier manual by Stanford Research Systems>[Model SR830 DSP Lock-In Amplifier manual, printed p. 3-3, “RMS or Peak?” and “Degrees or Radians?”; pp. 3-13–3-14, output scaling and display offsets]{ref}

= Lock-in harmonic rejection
{parent=Lock-in amplifier}

Consider this original constructed input <electric potential difference>, with $t$ in seconds and $f_0=1\,\mathrm{Hz}$:
$$
s(t)=(0.4\,\mathrm V)\cos(2\pi f_0t+\pi/3)
+(0.9\,\mathrm V)\cos(6\pi f_0t+\pi/5).
$$
The unwanted component has three times the reference <frequency> and more than twice the wanted peak <amplitude>. Nevertheless, the <lock-in quadratures> over $T=4/f_0=4\,\mathrm s$ are exactly
$$
X=0.4\cos(\pi/3)\,\mathrm V=0.2\,\mathrm V,\qquad
Y=0.4\sin(\pi/3)\,\mathrm V=0.2\sqrt3\,\mathrm V\approx0.34641016\,\mathrm V,
$$
so $R=0.4\,\mathrm V$ and $\operatorname{atan2}(Y,X)=\pi/3=60^\circ$. This is a peak <amplitude> calculation, not an SR830 display prediction.

To see why the larger component vanishes, multiply $B\cos(3\omega t+\psi)$ by each reference:
$$
\begin{aligned}
2B\cos(3\omega t+\psi)\cos\omega t
&=B\cos(2\omega t+\psi)+B\cos(4\omega t+\psi),\\
-2B\cos(3\omega t+\psi)\sin\omega t
&=B\sin(2\omega t+\psi)-B\sin(4\omega t+\psi).
\end{aligned}
$$
Each <sinusoid> has zero <average value of a function> over a whole reference cycle. More generally, a component $B\cos(n\omega t+\psi)$ with <integer> $n\geq2$ gives products at $(n-1)\omega$ and $(n+1)\omega$, whose <integrals> vanish over any <integer> number of reference cycles. This is the <orthogonality> behind selecting a <Fourier coefficient> with sinusoidal references. A nonsinusoidal reference can contain additional <frequencies> and need not have the same rejection property.

An unwanted component at the reference <frequency> survives. If $u(t)=B\cos(\omega t+\psi)$ is added to the input, its coordinate <vector> adds to the wanted one:
$$
Z=Ae^{\mathrm i\phi}+Be^{\mathrm i\psi}.
$$
For example, adding $B=0.1\,\mathrm V$ with $\psi=0$ to the wanted component gives $X=0.3\,\mathrm V$, $Y=0.2\sqrt3\,\mathrm V$, $R=\sqrt{0.21}\,\mathrm V\approx0.458258\,\mathrm V$, and <phase difference> $\operatorname{atan2}(0.2\sqrt3,0.3)\approx49.1066^\circ$. Longer averaging cannot separate these two synchronized contributions.

= Finite-window lock-in response
{parent=Lock-in amplifier}
{title2=$F_T(\nu)=e^{\mathrm i\nu T/2}\operatorname{sinc}(\nu T/2)$}

An observation <interval> of finite duration is not an infinitely sharp <frequency> selector. Combine the <lock-in quadratures> into
$$
Z_T=\frac2T\int_0^T s(t)e^{-\mathrm i\omega t}\,dt.
$$
For unwanted <electric potential difference> $s(t)=B\cos((\omega+\delta)t+\psi)$, where $\delta$ is an <angular frequency> offset and $\omega+\delta>0$, expansion into <complex exponentials> gives the exact response
$$
\begin{aligned}
Z_T&=Be^{\mathrm i\psi}F_T(\delta)
+Be^{-\mathrm i\psi}F_T(-(2\omega+\delta)),\\
F_T(\nu)&=\frac1T\int_0^T e^{\mathrm i\nu t}\,dt
=e^{\mathrm i\nu T/2}\operatorname{sinc}(\nu T/2),\\
\operatorname{sinc}(x)&=\begin{cases}\sin x/x,&x\ne0,\\1,&x=0.\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$
The <sinc function> formula follows by integrating the <exponential function> and factoring $e^{\mathrm i\nu T}-1=2\mathrm i e^{\mathrm i\nu T/2}\sin(\nu T/2)$; its value at zero follows from a <limit>. The first term changes slowly after multiplication; the second oscillates at the sum <frequency>. It vanishes at exact matching when $T$ is an <integer> number of reference cycles, each lasting one <period of a function>, but cannot generally be discarded in a short-window calculation.

For $|\delta|\ll\omega$ and many reference cycles, the first term usually dominates: its <modulus> is $B|\operatorname{sinc}(\delta T/2)|$ and its <complex argument> includes $\delta T/2$. Thus $|\delta|T\ll1$ gives almost no rejection. A longer observation separates closer <frequencies>, with the first zeros of this term at $|\delta|=2\pi/T$. Those zeros are not a brick-wall cutoff: the <sinc function> has nonzero lobes beyond them. At $\delta=0$ a synchronized contribution remains however large $T$ becomes.

For a numerical check, use $f_0=1\,\mathrm{Hz}$, $T=4\,\mathrm s$, $\psi=0$ and an unwanted <frequency> $f=1.125\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Then $(f-f_0)T=1/2$ and the exact expression gives
$$
\frac{|Z_T|}{B}=\frac2\pi-\frac2{17\pi}=\frac{32}{17\pi}\approx0.599172,
$$
whereas the difference-frequency term alone gives $2/\pi\approx0.636620$. An interference <amplitude> $B=0.9\,\mathrm V$ therefore contributes $0.539254\,\mathrm V$ to $|Z_T|$ in this constructed example. A nearby <frequency> can survive strongly even though the three-times-frequency component in <lock-in harmonic rejection> cancels exactly.

A practical <lock-in amplifier>'s <low-pass filter> weights past products differently from this rectangular observation <interval>. Its <time constant> and roll-off determine near-reference rejection and <settling time>; narrowing detection slows the response.<Model SR830 DSP Lock-In Amplifier manual by Stanford Research Systems>[Model SR830 DSP Lock-In Amplifier manual, printed p. 3-2, “Narrow band detection”; pp. 3-11–3-12, time constants and filter roll-off]{ref}

= Chopped-light lock-in experiment
{parent=Lock-in amplifier}

The University of California, Berkeley guide *LLS - Measuring the Light Signal from a Diode*, in Experimental Setup and Experiments X–XI, documents an experiment using a red <light-emitting diode> amid room-light background. An <optical chopper>, controlled by an SR540, periodically interrupts its <light>. A PIN-10DP <photodiode> sends <electric current> to an SR570 preamplifier, which converts it to an <electric potential difference> for an SR760 spectrum analyzer and an SR830 <lock-in amplifier>. The SR540 supplies the external reference. Preparation includes aligning the <photodiode> and its shielding cone and choosing gain to avoid overload. One comparison first searches for the source <frequency> without the chopper reference, then checks against that external reference; switching off the LED tests the candidate spectral peak. The measurement procedure varies chopper <frequency> and preamplifier filtering, then records repeated lock-in readings with selected averaging settings. These are prescribed observations to make, not published numerical results.<LLS - Measuring the Light Signal from a Diode, University of California, Berkeley>[LLS - Measuring the Light Signal from a Diode, Experimental Setup; Block Diagram I; Experiment X, steps 2 and 9–13; Experiment XI, steps 1–8]{ref}

The guide notes nearly triangular chopping because slit and emitter sizes are comparable. Its interpretation requires detector conversion, preamplifier gain and waveform geometry to infer <optical power> from the selected component.<LLS - Measuring the Light Signal from a Diode, University of California, Berkeley>[Experimental Setup, waveform discussion; Interpreting the Data, points 1–3 and collection-geometry discussion]{ref}

The inference is therefore specific: synchronized <electric potential difference> supports the presence of a modulated optical component at the reference <frequency>. It does not by itself give total emitted <optical power>. The <photodiode>'s <spectral responsivity>, collected fraction of the <light>, and conversion from the measured <Fourier coefficient> to the chopped waveform must enter that calibration. The <finite-window lock-in response> also explains why a frequency-search reading can drift when the reference is not synchronized. An LED-off comparison tests background at the same timing; it cannot guarantee that every synchronized disturbance disappears when the desired source is present. Overload before multiplication destroys information that later averaging cannot restore.

= Complex impedance
{synonym}

<Electrical impedance> is the ratio of terminal <electric potential difference> and <electric current> <phasors> at one specified positive <angular frequency> $\omega$. For an <electrical circuit> modeled as a <linear time-invariant system>, use the sinusoidal <steady state> after any decaying transient has settled. With the <passive sign convention>, positive <electric current> enters the terminal assigned positive <electric potential difference>. Define peak <complex amplitudes> by
$$
v(t)=\operatorname{Re}(\widehat V e^{\mathrm i\omega t}),\qquad
i(t)=\operatorname{Re}(\widehat I e^{\mathrm i\omega t}),\qquad
Z(\omega)=\frac{\widehat V}{\widehat I},\quad \widehat I\ne0.
$$
The physical $v(t)$ and $i(t)$ are real; the <complex numbers> encode their <amplitudes> and <wave phases>. The unit of <electrical impedance> is the <ohm>, $1\,\Omega=1\,\mathrm V/\mathrm A$. Both <phasors> may instead use <root mean square> magnitudes, with the same ratio $Z$. A nonlinear <diode> can generate additional <frequencies>; a single $Z(\omega)$ then does not describe the whole response.

Write the <electrical impedance> in Cartesian and <polar forms> as
$$
Z=R+\mathrm iX=|Z|e^{\mathrm i\phi},\qquad
R=\operatorname{Re}Z,\quad X=\operatorname{Im}Z,\quad
|Z|=\sqrt{R^2+X^2},\quad
\phi=\arg Z=\arg\widehat V-\arg\widehat I.
$$
Here $R$ is the resistive <real part>, $X$ the signed <electrical reactance>, and $\phi$ the voltage-minus-current <phase difference>, defined modulo a full turn. The <Pythagorean theorem> gives the <modulus> from a <right triangle> in the <complex plane>. The <complex argument> gives the rotation from the current <phasor> to the voltage <phasor>. Thus $V_{\rm rms}=|Z|I_{\rm rms}$, while negative $\phi$ means <electric current> leads terminal <electric potential difference>. For $R>0$, $\phi=\arctan(X/R)$ lies between $-\pi/2$ and $\pi/2$; outside that case, use the quadrant of the <complex number> rather than a bare <inverse tangent>. The geometric magnitude and <phase difference> are the two pieces of the AC relation.<University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others>[§15.3, figures 15.12–15.13 and equations 15.9–15.11: phasor addition, phase angle, impedance magnitude and ohm unit]{ref}

Multiplication by $Z$ acts on <phasors>, not on instantaneous physical signals. For example, when $i(t)=I_0\cos\omega t$,
$$
v(t)=I_0\bigl(R\cos\omega t-X\sin\omega t\bigr).
$$
The second term is in <phase quadrature> with <electric current>; it cannot be represented by pointwise multiplication $v(t)=Zi(t)$ when $X\ne0$. This is the useful compression: one <complex multiplication> replaces separate <amplitude> and <phase difference> calculations, while the <real part> restores the physical signal.

An ideal <resistor>, <inductor> and <capacitor> have $Z_R=R$, $Z_L=\mathrm i\omega L$ and $Z_C=1/(\mathrm i\omega C)$. Their <electrical impedances> add in a <series RLC circuit>, giving $Z=R+\mathrm i(\omega L-1/(\omega C))$. The current <amplitude> is $V_0/|Z|$, so cancellation of the <imaginary parts> explains the <resonant series RLC response>. The component-law derivation and opposite <complex exponential> convention are in <reactance phase convention>.

= Capacitor in series with a parallel RL branch
{c}
{parent=Electrical impedance}
{title2=$\omega_*^{-2}=LC-L^2/R^2$}

For an ideal <capacitor> in series with a parallel <resistor> and <inductor>, $Z=1/(i\omega C)+(R\parallel i\omega L)$. A finite positive frequency with real input impedance exists precisely when $R^2C>L$. At that frequency $\omega_*=[LC-L^2/R^2]^{-1/2}$ and $Z_*=L/(RC)$. The resistor's voltage can exceed the source voltage because the reactive voltage drops cancel in the series sum; all average dissipation is still supplied by the source.

= Parallel conjugate impedances
{parent=Electrical impedance}
{title2=$Z_\parallel=R(1+s^2)/2$}

Two parallel branches with <complex impedances> $R(1+is)$ and $R(1-is)$ have a real input impedance $R(1+s^2)/2$, since their admittances have cancelling imaginary parts. For $s=\sqrt{15}$, adding a series resistance $2R$ gives total impedance $10R$. With source voltage amplitude $V_0$, each branch-resistor drop and the series-resistor drop has amplitude $V_0/5$, despite their different <phases>.

= Impedance magnitude
{parent=Electrical impedance}
{title2=$|Z|$}

The <impedance magnitude> of a linear <electrical circuit> in sinusoidal steady state is the ratio of <voltage> amplitude to <electric current> amplitude. For <complex impedance> $Z=R+iX$, it is $|Z|=\sqrt{R^2+X^2}$, whereas the dissipative <electrical resistance> is $\operatorname{Re}Z=R$. Measuring only amplitudes does not separately determine $R$ and $X$; a <phase shift> measurement supplies the missing information.

= Input impedance
{parent=Electrical impedance}
{title2=$Z_{\mathrm{in}}$}

<Input impedance> is the ratio of a small applied input-voltage <phasor> to the resulting input-current <phasor>, with the output termination and operating point specified. A finite <input impedance> loads a voltage source. For an <ideal operational amplifier> the <input impedance> is infinite, so neither input terminal draws current.

= Impedance matching
{parent=Electrical impedance}
{wiki}

For a source with internal <electrical impedance> $Z_s$, conjugate matching $Z_L=Z_s^*$ maximizes delivered mean <power> when the source is fixed. For a purely resistive source and equal load <electrical resistance> $R$, the load sees half the open-circuit <voltage>. A <Johnson-Nyquist noise> source with open-circuit mean-square voltage $4k_BTR\,d\nu$ therefore delivers $k_BT\,d\nu$ to the matched load, rather than $4k_BT\,d\nu$.

= Matched load
{parent=Impedance matching}

A load matched to a source receives the maximum available power in the stated circuit model. For a real source resistance $R$ and open-circuit root mean square voltage $V_{\rm oc}$, a load $R$ receives voltage $V_{\rm oc}/2$ and power $V_{\rm oc}^2/(4R)$. For complex impedances, maximum power transfer instead uses conjugate matching under the usual linear-source assumptions.

= Maximum power transfer theorem
{parent=Impedance matching}
{wiki}
{title2=$R_L=R_s$}

A <Thévenin equivalent> voltage source $V_s$ with positive source resistance $R_s$ supplies load power $P_L=V_s^2R_L/(R_s+R_L)^2$ to a real resistive load. Differentiating gives its maximum at $R_L=R_s$. With reactive impedances and fixed source impedance, conjugate matching maximizes the average delivered power. Maximum delivered power is distinct from maximum efficiency; at a real resistive match half of the source's internally generated power is dissipated in $R_s$.

= Quarter-wave impedance transformer
{parent=Impedance matching}
{wiki}

A lossless intermediate section of positive real <characteristic impedance> $Z_M$ matches unequal real exterior <characteristic impedances> $Z_L,Z_R$ when $Z_M^2=Z_LZ_R$ and its propagation <wave phase> is an odd multiple of $\pi/2$. The length is an odd quarter of the internal <wavelength>. This principle also applies to the <characteristic impedance of a stretched string>.

For a uniform intermediate string of length $l$, put $\delta=k_Ml$. Continuity of displacement and transverse <force> gives the displacement <reflection coefficient>
$$
r=\frac{(Z_L-Z_R)\cos\delta+i(Z_M-Z_LZ_R/Z_M)\sin\delta}{(Z_L+Z_R)\cos\delta-i(Z_M+Z_LZ_R/Z_M)\sin\delta}.
$$
At real positive <mechanical impedances>, the denominator never vanishes. Its numerator vanishes only if $(Z_L-Z_R)\cos\delta=0$ and $(Z_M^2-Z_LZ_R)\sin\delta=0$. Thus unequal exterior strings require the quarter-wave condition. Equal exterior strings with a different middle string instead transmit perfectly at integer half-<wavelengths>; if all three <mechanical impedances> agree, every <frequency> is matched.

= Lossy parallel coil resonance
{parent=Electrical impedance}
{title2=$\omega_r^2=1/(LC)-R^2/L^2$}

A <series circuit> comprising <electrical resistance> $R>0$ and <inductance> $L>0$, placed in a <parallel circuit> with <capacitance> $C>0$, has <electrical admittance> $Y=(R+i\omega L)^{-1}+i\omega C$. Vanishing <imaginary part> at nonzero <angular frequency> gives the displayed condition, requiring $L>R^2C$. It defines zero voltage-current <phase difference>, which need not coincide exactly with the maximum of impedance magnitude when loss is present. At this frequency the <electrical impedance> is real and equals $L/(RC)$.

= Determining a lossy parallel coil from its admittance
{parent=Lossy parallel coil resonance}

For series resistance R and <inductance> L in parallel with <capacitance> C, suppose the applied <voltage> <amplitude> V is fixed. The DC current gives $R=V/I_{\rm DC}$. If the high-frequency current is $I\sim\alpha\omega$, then $C=\alpha/V$. At the nonzero zero-phase <frequency>, current <amplitude> $I_r=VRC/L$ determines $L=VRC/I_r$. The condition $R^2<L/C$ must hold. The inferred zero-phase <frequency> is $\sqrt{1/(LC)-R^2/L^2}$; it is not automatically the exact <frequency> of minimum current magnitude.

The reciprocal of <electrical impedance>, relating <electric current> and <electric potential difference> <phasors> by $\widetilde I=Y\widetilde V$. Its unit is the siemens, $\mathrm S=\Omega^{-1}$. <Electrical admittances> add for branches in a <parallel circuit>.

= Electrical susceptance

In the $e^{i\omega t}$ <phasor> convention, <electrical admittance> is $Y=G+iB$, with conductance $G$ and <electrical susceptance> $B$. An ideal <capacitor> has $B=\omega C$ and an ideal <inductor> has $B=-1/(\omega L)$. <electrical susceptances> add for parallel branches; their cancellation gives a purely resistive input admittance.

= Series RC impedance
{parent=Electrical impedance}
{title2=$Z=R-\mathrm i/(\omega C)$}

Consider a stipulated <series circuit> containing an ideal <resistor> of <electrical resistance> $R=100\,\Omega$ and an ideal <capacitor> of <capacitance> $C=1\,\mu\mathrm F$. The same <electric current> passes through both. Their terminal <electric potential differences> add by <Kirchhoff's voltage law>, so
$$
\widehat V=\widehat V_R+\widehat V_C
=R\widehat I+\frac{\widehat I}{\mathrm i\omega C},\qquad
Z=R-\frac{\mathrm i}{\omega C}.
$$
A quick derivation uses $i=C\,dv_C/dt$: taking the <derivative> of the <complex exponential> multiplies the capacitor voltage <phasor> by $\mathrm i\omega$, hence $\widehat V_C=\widehat I/(\mathrm i\omega C)$. Dimensionally, $1/(\omega C)$ has units $\mathrm{s/F}=\mathrm{V/A}=\Omega$, matching $R$.

At <frequency> $f=1000\,\mathrm{Hz}$, the <angular frequency> is $\omega=2\pi f$. The signed capacitive <electrical reactance>, <modulus> and <complex argument> of the total <electrical impedance> are
$$
X_C=-159.154943\,\Omega,\qquad
|Z|=187.963549\,\Omega,\qquad
\phi=-57.858092^\circ.
$$
Apply $V_{\rm rms}=1.00\,\mathrm V$ across the complete <series circuit>. Its <root mean square> <electric current> is
$$
I_{\rm rms}=\frac{1.00\,\mathrm V}{187.963549\,\Omega}
=5.320180\,\mathrm{mA}.
$$
With voltage as the zero-phase reference, the current <phasor> has <complex argument> $+57.858092^\circ$: <electric current> leads. The <phasor> drops have <moduli> $V_{R,\rm rms}=0.532018\,\mathrm V$ and $V_{C,\rm rms}=0.846733\,\mathrm V$; these add as <complex numbers>, not as positive magnitudes. Their squared magnitudes sum to $(1.00\,\mathrm V)^2$.

Ling and others>[§15.4, equations 15.12–15.14 and the capacitor/inductor paragraph: RMS average power and zero ideal reactive average power]{ref} To see why the phase factor appears, average $p(t)=v(t)i(t)$ over a full cycle with $v(t)=\sqrt2V_{\rm rms}\cos\omega t$ and $i(t)=\sqrt2I_{\rm rms}\cos(\omega t-\phi)$. The <cosine addition formula> reduces the average to $V_{\rm rms}I_{\rm rms}\cos\phi$; the term at $2\omega$ averages to zero. Independently, $p_C=d(Cv_C^2/2)/dt$, and the <capacitor energy> returns to its starting value after each cycle.

At fixed applied $V_{\rm rms}$, the <limit> $\omega\to0^+$ gives $|Z|\to\infty$, $\phi\to-\pi/2$ and $I_{\rm rms}\to0$: the ideal <capacitor> blocks steady <electric current>. The <limit> $\omega\to\infty$ gives $Z\to R$, $\phi\to0$ and $I_{\rm rms}\to V_{\rm rms}/R$. These are <limits> of the ideal lumped <electrical circuit>; unwanted lead <inductance> and component losses can invalidate that model at high <frequency>. At $\omega RC=1$, the equal legs of the impedance <right triangle> give $|Z|=\sqrt2R$ and $\phi=-\pi/4$ without numerical evaluation. Measuring both the magnitude and the sign of the <phase difference> distinguishes this <capacitor> response from an equally large positive <electrical reactance>.

$$
|Z|=R\frac{V_1}{V_R},\qquad
\Delta t=t_R-t_1,\qquad
\phi=\frac{2\pi\Delta t}{T},\qquad
Z=|Z|(\cos\phi+\mathrm i\sin\phi).
$$
The recovered <electrical impedance> includes the known <resistor>. For a separate unknown component in series with that <resistor>, subtract $R$ from the inferred total <electrical impedance>. Timing supplies the <complex argument> that an amplitude ratio alone cannot recover.

These controls test the signed inference rather than assuming that the scope's delay readout has the desired sign.

Measure the loaded terminal <electric potential difference> on channel 1 at every <frequency>. The generator's internal <electrical impedance> is outside the terminal ratio above, while the known sensing <resistor> is inside it. = Electrical reactance
{parent=Electrical impedance}
{title2=$X=\operatorname{Im}Z$}

= Reactance
{synonym}

Electrical reactance $X$ is the signed <imaginary part> of <electrical impedance> $Z=R+\mathrm iX$, measured in <ohms>. It describes the part of the terminal <electric potential difference> in <phase quadrature> with the <electric current> in a sinusoidal <steady state>. In the $e^{\mathrm i\omega t}$ convention, an ideal <inductor> has $X_L=\omega L>0$ and an ideal <capacitor> has $X_C=-1/(\omega C)<0$ for $\omega>0$. A large $|X|$ reduces <electric current> at fixed applied voltage, but an ideal purely reactive component returns the <energy> it receives over each cycle. This is why <electrical reactance> and <electrical resistance> have the same units yet describe different physical behavior. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_22.html#Ch22-S5[Feynman, Leighton and Sands, The Feynman Lectures on Physics, II.22.5: resistance, reactance and energy loss]{ref}.

In a <series RLC circuit>, <electrical impedances> add because the same <electric current> passes through every component. For ideal positive <inductance> $L$, <capacitance> $C$, and total <electrical resistance> $R_\Sigma>0$,
$$
X(\omega)=\omega L-\frac1{\omega C},\qquad
|Z|=\sqrt{R_\Sigma^2+X^2},\qquad
I_{\rm rms}=\frac{V_{\rm rms}}{|Z|},\qquad
\phi=\arctan\frac{X}{R_\Sigma}.
$$
Here $\phi$ is voltage <wave phase> minus current <wave phase>. Below $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$, $X<0$ and <electric current> leads terminal voltage; above it, $X>0$ and <electric current> lags. At $\omega_0$, the two signed <electrical reactances> cancel, leaving $Z=R_\Sigma$ and the maximum <electric current> at fixed terminal voltage. This is the <resonant series RLC response>. <Electrical resistance> does not cancel: the toy circuit gives $f_0=\omega_0/(2\pi)=1591.55\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and $I_{\rm rms}=9.091\,\mathrm{mA}$ there. These numbers follow directly from the stipulated component values. <University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others>[Ling, Moebs and Sanny, University Physics 2, §15.3: series impedance and resonance]{ref}.

Cancellation concerns terminal voltage, not the disappearance of stored <energy>. In the toy circuit at <resonance>, the individual <inductor> and <capacitor> voltages are each $0.9091\,\mathrm V$ in <root mean square> magnitude, with opposite <wave phases>, while their sum is zero. Their peak stored <magnetic energy> and <capacitor energy> are each $0.8264\,\mu\mathrm J$, attained at different times. With smaller positive $R_\Sigma$, either component voltage can exceed the applied voltage. This ability to select a <frequency> while sustaining internal <energy> exchange connects <electrical reactance> to tuning and <frequency response>.

For an ideal <inductor> or <capacitor>, the absorbed instantaneous <power> equals the rate of change of stored <energy>:
$$
p_L=v_Li=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12Li^2\right),\qquad
p_C=v_Ci=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12Cv_C^2\right).
$$
Positive <power> stores <energy>; negative <power> returns it. Over a periodic <steady state>, each stored <energy> returns to its initial value, so its cycle-averaged absorbed <power> is zero. An ideal <resistor> instead has $p_R=Ri^2\geq0$, transferring <energy> into <Joule heating>. More generally, the average terminal <power> of this <series RLC circuit> is
$$
\overline p=V_{\rm rms}I_{\rm rms}\cos\phi=I_{\rm rms}^2R_\Sigma.
$$
Zero average <power> in an ideal reactive component does not imply zero instantaneous <power>, zero <electric current>, or zero stored <energy>. Real coils and <capacitors> have losses, so zero ideal reactive loss is a model assumption rather than a claim about every component. <University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others>[Ling, Moebs and Sanny, University Physics 2, §15.4: instantaneous and average AC power]{ref}.

= Reactance phase convention
{parent=Electrical reactance}
{title2=$v(t)=\operatorname{Re}(\widehat V e^{\mathrm i\omega t})$}

Use <complex amplitudes> only for signals at one common positive <angular frequency> $\omega$. Define the real terminal voltage and <electric current> by
$$
v(t)=\operatorname{Re}\left(\widehat V e^{\mathrm i\omega t}\right),\qquad
i(t)=\operatorname{Re}\left(\widehat I e^{\mathrm i\omega t}\right),\qquad
Z=\frac{\widehat V}{\widehat I}.
$$
The <complex amplitudes> here use peak magnitudes; both can instead use <root mean square> magnitudes without changing their ratio. Choose positive <electric current> entering the terminal assigned positive voltage, so $p=vi$ is absorbed <power>. For an ideal linear <inductor> and <capacitor>, their terminal laws $v_L=L\,di/dt$ and $i_C=C\,dv_C/dt$ give
$$
\widehat V_L=\mathrm i\omega L\widehat I,\qquad
\widehat I_C=\mathrm i\omega C\widehat V_C,\qquad
Z_L=\mathrm i\omega L,\qquad
Z_C=\frac1{\mathrm i\omega C}=-\frac{\mathrm i}{\omega C}.
$$
The <derivative> multiplies a <complex amplitude> by $\mathrm i\omega$: this single algebraic step explains both signs. Taking $i(t)=I_0\cos\omega t$ as a reference gives $v_L(t)=-\omega LI_0\sin\omega t$ and $v_C(t)=I_0\sin\omega t/(\omega C)$ for the sinusoidal response. Thus <inductor> voltage leads <electric current> by $\pi/2$, while <capacitor> voltage lags <electric current> by $\pi/2$. A constant offset or a free transient is outside this single-<frequency> representation. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_22.html#Ch22-S1[Feynman, Leighton and Sands, II.22.1: complex sinusoidal representation and component impedances]{ref}.

With the alternative $e^{-\mathrm i\omega t}$ convention, the same real signals use conjugated <complex amplitudes> and $Z_L=-\mathrm i\omega L$, $Z_C=+\mathrm i/(\omega C)$. Their physical leads and lags stay the same; the sign of $\operatorname{Im}Z$ changes. Also, some textbooks call the positive magnitude $1/(\omega C)$ the capacitive reactance and write the series expression as $X_L-|X_C|$. This article consistently uses signed $X_C=-1/(\omega C)$ and hence $X=X_L+X_C$. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/15-2-simple-ac-circuits[Ling, Moebs and Sanny, University Physics 2, §15.2: positive capacitive-reactance magnitude and quarter-cycle phase relations]{ref}.

{parent=Electrical reactance}

The motivating observation is a pair of sinusoids on an oscilloscope: changing <frequency> changes both their relative <amplitudes> and their <phase difference>. Gary Tuttle's EE 201 laboratory uses resistor voltage as the current-phase reference, compares predictions with readings at several <frequencies>, and explicitly asks students to measure component values and account for coil <electrical resistance>. The following bench arrangement applies that method to a <series RLC circuit>; its component values and illustrated traces are our own toy example. https://gtuttle.net/circuits/lab/ac_circuits_1.pdf[Gary Tuttle, EE 201 Lab 9: AC circuits, especially the notes and RC/RL measurements]{ref}.

Connect a low-voltage sine generator to a series chain ending in a known sensing <resistor> $R_s$ at the common return. Use a <resistor> whose unwanted <inductance> is negligible over the chosen <frequency> range. An oscilloscope displays voltage against time: channel A reads the loaded voltage across the whole chain, and channel B reads the voltage $v_s$ across $R_s$. Both channel ground connections and the generator return meet at that same node. Ordinary bench oscilloscope grounds are electrically common; attaching one to an intermediate circuit node would change the circuit. A separate floating component voltage needs a suitable differential measurement, rather than moving a ground clip. https://www.tek.com/en/documents/technical-brief/floating-oscilloscope-measurements-and-operator-protection[Tektronix, Floating Oscilloscope Measurements and Operator Protection: ground-referenced inputs]{ref}.

After the free transient decays, record <frequency> $f$, period $T=1/f$, both voltage <amplitudes>, and matched rising zero-crossing times $t_A,t_B$. Check that the traces are sinusoidal. Define $\Delta t=t_B-t_A$ on the branch $|\Delta t|<T/2$. With negligible probe loading and negligible sensing-<resistor> <electrical reactance>, $v_s=R_si$, so channel B is the <electric current> reference, and in the <reactance phase convention>
$$
I_{\rm rms}=\frac{V_{s,\rm rms}}{R_s},\qquad
|Z_{\rm chain}|=\frac{V_{A,\rm rms}}{I_{\rm rms}},\qquad
\phi=\frac{2\pi\Delta t}{T},\qquad
X_{\rm chain}=|Z_{\rm chain}|\sin\phi,\qquad
R_{\rm chain}=|Z_{\rm chain}|\cos\phi.
$$
A later current crossing gives $\Delta t>0$, hence positive <electrical reactance>; an earlier crossing gives negative <electrical reactance>. Use the same <amplitude> convention for both channels. An amplitude-only ratio recovers $|Z|$, so it cannot distinguish the two signs: timing supplies the missing information. The formulas follow from $Z=|Z|e^{\mathrm i\phi}$ and the chosen terminal orientation.

Begin with a known <resistor> replacing the reactive chain: its expected <phase difference> is approximately zero, testing channel polarity and relative delay. Measure the same signal with both channels to establish the instrumental delay baseline, then exchange probes to check that a phase offset does not belong to one channel. Next test an <inductor> and a <capacitor> separately with $R_s$: after subtracting $R_s$ from the inferred real part, expect approximately $X/f=2\pi L$ and $fX=-1/(2\pi C)$, respectively. Finally combine $L,C,R,R_s$ and sweep through the predicted <resonance>. These are proposed controls and consequences of the model, not reported observations.

For the toy <series RLC circuit>, the prediction at $500\,\mathrm{Hz}$ is $X=-286.894\,\Omega$, $|Z|=307.259\,\Omega$, $\phi=-69.02^\circ$, and $V_{s,\rm rms}=32.55\,\mathrm{mV}$. At $5000\,\mathrm{Hz}$, $X=+282.328\,\Omega$ and $\phi=+68.71^\circ$. Near $1591.55\,\mathrm{Hz}$ the model predicts zero <phase difference> and $V_{s,\rm rms}=90.91\,\mathrm{mV}$. Keep actual loaded $V_{A,\rm rms}$ fixed or normalize by it at each <frequency>; the generator's display alone does not establish that voltage. The $10\,\Omega$ sensing <resistor> is part of the $110\,\Omega$ total and therefore changes the predicted peak <electric current>.

Real components add unintended <electrical impedance>: coil wire has <electrical resistance>, nearby turns add <capacitance>, and <capacitor> leads add <inductance>. These effects can produce a component's own <resonance>, beyond which the ideal $\omega L$ or $-1/(\omega C)$ approximation fails. Work in a range where that approximation is adequate, measure coil wire <electrical resistance>, and compare the inferred $R_{\rm chain}$ with the sensing <resistor>, intended <resistor>, and component losses. A measured nonzero real part is evidence of loss in the assembly, not negative or positive <electrical reactance> becoming heat. https://www.keysight.com.cn/cn/zh/assets/7018-06840/application-notes/5950-3000.pdf[Keysight, Impedance Measurement Handbook, §§1.5.1 and 5.2.1: component frequency response and parasitic elements]{ref}; https://gtuttle.net/circuits/lab/ac_circuits_1.pdf[Tuttle, EE 201 Lab 9: parasitic coil series resistance]{ref}.

The probes also add <electrical resistance> and <capacitance> to the measured nodes; their loading becomes more important as <frequency> increases. Use compensated probes with known input <electrical impedance> and short ground connections, and repeat with a smaller drive voltage to test whether the inferred $X$ depends on <amplitude>. https://www.tek.com/en/documents/application-note/how-oscilloscope-probes-affect-your-measurement[Tektronix, How Oscilloscope Probes Affect Your Measurement: resistive and capacitive loading]{ref}. Record actual voltage and phase readings separately from calculated predictions, with component tolerances and timing uncertainty. A phase error $\delta\phi$ alone produces approximately $\delta X=|Z|\cos\phi\,\delta\phi$, so near zero <electrical reactance>, a small delay can imitate a small residual $X$. Agreement within stated uncertainties tests the model; the smooth toy curves establish no experimental accuracy.

= Battery

An <electric battery> converts the available chemical <Gibbs free energy> of one or more <galvanic cells> into electrical <work> delivered to an <electrical load>. The reacting materials are a finite reservoir: a <battery> can sustain a terminal <electric potential difference> while discharging, but cannot supply <energy> indefinitely. Unlike a <capacitor>, whose <capacitor energy> resides in an <electric field>, a <battery> chiefly stores its available <energy> in the chemical state of its materials. The detailed <half-reactions> and apparatus of a <Daniell cell> explain this conversion at the <electrode> level.

During discharge, <electrons> move through metallic wires from the negative <anode>, through the <electrical load>, to the positive <cathode>; external <conventional current> points the other way. Inside the <battery>, <ions> carry <electric current> through the <electrolyte>, and <redox> <chemical reactions> connect ionic transport to electronic transport at the <electrodes>. The same <electron> does not make a lap through both wire and <electrolyte>. <Electric charge conservation> closes the circuit; changing chemical composition supplies the <energy>. See <galvanic cell> for the two transport paths and <electrolyte> for how oppositely moving <ions> contribute to one net <electric current>.

= Battery internal resistance
{parent=Electric battery}
{title2=$V_L=\mathcal E-Ir$}

A useful short-interval approximation replaces a <battery> by an ideal <electromotive force> $\mathcal E$ in series with a positive internal <electrical resistance> $r$. This is the <Thévenin terminal response> with <open-circuit voltage> $\mathcal E$ and <Thévenin resistance> $r$. The model describes the terminals without asserting that there is a separate physical <resistor> hidden inside the cell. Both $\mathcal E$ and $r$ can change with composition, <temperature>, <electric current> and history.<University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others>[§10.1, Internal Resistance and Terminal Voltage, current/temperature/history dependence and equation 10.1]{ref}

For a resistive <electrical load> $R>0$, take $I>0$ to mean discharge: <conventional current> leaves the positive terminal. <Kirchhoff's voltage law> and <Ohm's law> give
$$
\mathcal E-Ir-IR=0,\qquad
I=\frac{\mathcal E}{R+r},\qquad
V_L=IR=\mathcal E-Ir.
$$
For the stipulated $\mathcal E=3\,\mathrm V$, $r=1\,\Omega$, $R=4\,\Omega$,
$$
I=0.6\,\mathrm A,\qquad
Ir=0.6\,\mathrm V,\qquad
V_L=2.4\,\mathrm V.
$$
An <open-circuit voltage> of $3\,\mathrm V$ therefore does not promise $3\,\mathrm V$ at this <electrical load>. Increasing the load <electrical resistance> reduces <electric current> and brings $V_L$ closer to $\mathcal E$, as in <Thévenin load response>.

The signed relation also explains charging within this model. If an external source drives <electric current> into the positive terminal, $I<0$ and $V_L=\mathcal E+|I|r$. The terminal <electric potential difference> then exceeds $\mathcal E$. This algebra is not evidence that every <battery> is rechargeable.<University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others>[§10.1, paragraph accompanying Figure 10.10: reversed current and charging terminal voltage]{ref}

= Battery load power and efficiency
{parent=Electric battery}
{title2=$P_L=\mathcal E^2R/(R+r)^2$}

In the constant-parameter <battery internal resistance> model, the source's available electrical <power> splits between the <electrical load> and internal dissipation:
$$
\mathcal E I=\underbrace{V_LI}_{P_L}+\underbrace{I^2r}_{P_{\rm int}},\qquad
P_L=\frac{\mathcal E^2R}{(R+r)^2},\qquad
P_{\rm int}=\frac{\mathcal E^2r}{(R+r)^2}.
$$
The stipulated $3\,\mathrm V$, $1\,\Omega$, $4\,\Omega$ calculation gives $P_L=1.44\,\mathrm W$, $P_{\rm int}=0.36\,\mathrm W$, and $\mathcal E I=1.80\,\mathrm W$. A resistive <electrical load> converts its share to <Joule heating>; another <electrical load> might convert some of it to mechanical <work> or <light>. The source term is available electrical <work> per time, not a complete cell <heat> budget: reaction <entropy> can also matter.

Define the delivery efficiency relative to the source's available electrical <work>, rather than to all chemical <energy> or to a complete charging cycle:
$$
\eta=\frac{P_L}{\mathcal E I}=\frac{V_L}{\mathcal E}=\frac{R}{R+r}.
$$
Here $\eta=0.80$. The largest load <power> occurs at $R=r$: since $(R+r)^2\geq4Rr$,
$$
P_L\leq\frac{\mathcal E^2}{4r},\qquad
P_L\big|_{R=r}=2.25\,\mathrm W,\qquad
\eta\big|_{R=r}=\frac12.
$$
Thus maximum load <power> and maximum delivery efficiency ask different questions. As $R\to\infty$, $V_L\to\mathcal E$ and $\eta\to1$, but $I\to0$ and $P_L\to0$: a high nearly unloaded voltage supplies almost no <power>. As $R\to0$, the model gives $V_L\to0$, $P_L\to0$ and internal <power> tending to $\mathcal E^2/r$. Real high-current operation can invalidate constant $r$ and $\mathcal E$; the mathematical maximum does not establish a permissible operating point for an actual <battery>.

= Finite battery charge reservoir
{parent=Electric battery}
{title2=$Q_{\rm delivered}=\int I\,dt$}

A <battery>'s charge reservoir means a finite amount of <electric charge> can pass through the external <electrical circuit> before its usable chemical state is depleted. It does not mean a large net <electric charge> is sitting inside the casing. For one net cell <chemical reaction> transferring $n$ <electrons> per reaction event, an ideal reaction inventory obeys
$$
Q_{\rm delivered}=\int_0^t I(t')\,dt'=nF\xi,\qquad
W_L=\int_0^t V_L(t')I(t')\,dt',
$$
where $F$ is the <Faraday constant> and $\xi$ the <extent of reaction> in <moles>, assuming all the <electric current> serves that reaction. At fixed <temperature> and <pressure>, its reversible <electromotive force> obeys $\Delta_rG=-nF\mathcal E$: a decrease in <Gibbs free energy> provides the maximum reversible electrical <work> per <mole>. The <electromotive force> also depends on composition; the <Daniell cell> illustrates this with <thermodynamic activity>.<Chemistry 2e by Paul Flowers and others/17.4 Potential, Free Energy, and Equilibrium>[§17.4, E° and ΔG°, and Potentials at Nonstandard Conditions: free energy, cell potential and composition]{ref}

As an original finite-reservoir exercise, stipulate usable $Q_{\max}=360\,\mathrm C$ and hold the preceding $3\,\mathrm V$, $1\,\Omega$, $4\,\Omega$ parameters constant until an abrupt cutoff. Then $t=Q_{\max}/I=600\,\mathrm s$, available electrical <work> is $\mathcal E Q_{\max}=1080\,\mathrm J$, load <work> is $V_LQ_{\max}=864\,\mathrm J$, and internal dissipation is $216\,\mathrm J$. This explicitly finite idealization predicts ten minutes, not an experimentally measured runtime. Real accessible charge depends on discharge conditions and the chosen terminal-voltage cutoff. Charge alone is also not an <energy>: converting it to delivered <work> requires the terminal <electric potential difference> throughout discharge.

= RL circuit
{c}
{parent=Electrical circuit}
{title2=$L\,dI/dt+RI=V$}
{wiki}

An <RL circuit> combines <electrical resistance> $R$ and <self-inductance> $L$. In the series model, the same <electric current> $I$ flows through both components. An ideal <battery> maintains terminal <electric potential difference> $V$; the drops in the direction of positive <electric current> obey <Ohm's law> and the <inductor> relation:
$$
v_R=RI,\qquad v_L=L\frac{dI}{dt},\qquad
V=v_R+v_L.
$$
This is <Kirchhoff's voltage law> with the <inductor>'s terminal drop, rather than its oppositely signed induced <electromotive force>. For constant $R>0$, $L>0$, and $V$, the <first-order linear differential equation> has solution
$$
I(t)=I_\infty+(I(0)-I_\infty)e^{-t/\tau},\qquad
I_\infty=\frac VR,\qquad \tau=\frac LR.
$$
The <time constant> $\tau$ measures <exponential decay> of the difference from the final <electric current>. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/14-4-rl-circuits[OpenStax University Physics 2, §14.4: RL circuits]{ref}.

For a stipulated example, take <electrical resistance> $R=2\,\Omega$, <self-inductance> $L=0.5\,\mathrm H$, terminal <electric potential difference> $V=6\,\mathrm V$, and initial <electric current> $I(0)=0$. Then
$$
\tau=0.25\,\mathrm s,\qquad I_\infty=3\,\mathrm A,
\qquad I(t)=(3\,\mathrm A)(1-e^{-t/(0.25\,\mathrm s)}).
$$
With $t$ measured numerically in seconds, this is $I(t)=3(1-e^{-4t})$ in amperes. At one <time constant>,
$$
I(\tau)=3(1-e^{-1})\,\mathrm A\approx1.89636\,\mathrm A,
$$
and the terminal <electric potential difference> drops are
$$
v_R(\tau)=6(1-e^{-1})\,\mathrm V\approx3.79272\,\mathrm V,
\qquad v_L(\tau)=6e^{-1}\,\mathrm V\approx2.20728\,\mathrm V.
$$
Their sum is exactly $6\,\mathrm V$. At $t=0^+$, the <inductor> takes the entire terminal <electric potential difference> while the <electric current> is still zero. At steady <electric current>, the <inductor>'s terminal drop is zero and the entire drop is across the <electrical resistance>.

\Image[rl-current-and-voltage.png]
{title=<Electric current> and terminal <electric potential difference> drops during an <RL circuit> response}
{description=The <battery> maintains $6\,\mathrm V$ across <electrical resistance> $2\,\Omega$ and <self-inductance> $0.5\,\mathrm H$ in series. The dashed vertical line marks <time constant> $0.25\,\mathrm s$; $v_L$ uses the positive terminal-drop convention.}

The final <magnetic energy> stored by the <inductor> is $U_\infty=LI_\infty^2/2=2.25\,\mathrm J$. At one <time constant>, the <electric current> has reached about $63.21\%$ of its final value, but its <magnetic energy> has reached only about $39.96\%$:
$$
U(\tau)=\frac12L I(\tau)^2
=2.25(1-e^{-1})^2\,\mathrm J\approx0.899047\,\mathrm J.
$$
This difference follows from the square of <electric current> in the <inductor>'s <magnetic energy>.

For release, take $t=0$ at the start of a separate experiment with initial <electric current> $3\,\mathrm A$. Remove the <battery> from the model while maintaining a closed path through the same <electrical resistance> and <inductor>. Then
$$
L\frac{dI}{dt}+RI=0,\qquad
I(t)=(3\,\mathrm A)e^{-t/(0.25\,\mathrm s)}.
$$
Now $v_L=-v_R$: the induced <electromotive force> supports the continuing positive <electric current> as its magnitude decreases. This closed-loop model describes <exponential decay>, not opening the sole path and imposing an instantaneous jump to zero <electric current>. The <inductor>'s <magnetic energy> and the <power> of <Joule heating> are
$$
U(t)=(2.25\,\mathrm J)e^{-2t/\tau},\qquad
P_R(t)=RI(t)^2=(18\,\mathrm W)e^{-2t/\tau},\qquad
\frac{dU}{dt}=-P_R.
$$
Their <time constant> is $\tau/2=0.125\,\mathrm s$. The total <energy> dissipated by <Joule heating> is exactly the initial <magnetic energy>:
$$
\int_0^\infty P_R(t)\,dt
=(18\,\mathrm W)\frac{\tau}{2}=2.25\,\mathrm J.
$$
The second diagram in <inductor> shows this <energy> transfer. A finite closed path lets us predict both how long the <electric current> persists and how much <energy> becomes heat. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/14-4-rl-circuits[OpenStax University Physics 2, §14.4: decay in a closed RL loop]{ref}.

= Inductor branch shunted by a resistor
{parent=RL circuit}
{title2=$\tau=L/(2R)$}

An ideal source $V$ feeds a series resistance $2R$, followed by parallel branches $R+L$ and $2R$. The <Thévenin equivalent> seen by the <inductor> has voltage $V/2$ and resistance $R+(2R\parallel2R)=2R$. From zero initial current, the inductor current is $V(1-e^{-t/\tau})/(4R)$, the other branch current is $V(1+e^{-t/\tau})/(8R)$, and the source current is $V(3-e^{-t/\tau})/(8R)$. <Kirchhoff's current law> holds at every time, and the final stored <magnetic energy> is $LV^2/(32R^2)$.

= Periodically switched RL circuit
{parent=RL circuit}
{title2=$I_{\max}=(V/R)/(1+e^{-R\Delta t/L})$}

An <RL circuit> alternating for equal intervals $\Delta t$ between zero drive and constant drive $V$ has a unique attracting periodic <electric current> when $R,L,\Delta t>0$. Write $s=e^{-R\Delta t/L}$ and $J=V/R$. Sampling just before each zero-drive interval gives the <recurrence relation> $I_{n+1}=s^2I_n+J(1-s)$. Subtracting its <fixed point> yields $I_n-J/(1+s)=s^{2n}[I_0-J/(1+s)]$. Thus the limiting upper and lower <electric currents> are $J/(1+s)$ and $sJ/(1+s)$; convergence is geometric. This gives a simple exact ripple estimate for a periodically driven <inductor>.

= RLC circuit
{c}
{parent=Electrical circuit}
{wiki}

An <RLC circuit> combines <electrical resistance> $R$, <inductance> $L$, and <capacitance> $C$. The <capacitor> stores <energy> in its <electric field>; the <inductor> stores <energy> in its <magnetic field>. Their exchange can produce an <oscillation>, while <Joule heating> removes <energy>. A <series RLC circuit> gives a particularly clear example: changing $R$ turns ringing into a direct return to equilibrium, and changing $L$ or $C$ tunes a <resonance>.

These circuits connect two useful viewpoints. In time, they describe how stored <energy> is released after switching. In <frequency>, they select a range of signals: the <resonant series RLC response> explains radio tuning and simple filters. The same mathematics describes a <damped harmonic oscillator>, so insight about mechanical ringing transfers directly to an <electrical circuit>. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_23.html#Ch23-S3[Feynman lectures, I.23.3: electrical and mechanical resonance]{ref}; https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_22.html#Ch22-S7[Feynman lectures, II.22.7: frequency filters]{ref}.

= Driven RLC branch with a parallel shunt resistor
{parent=RLC circuit}
{title2=$U_{\max}/W_{\rm diss}=\omega_0L/(4\pi R)$}

An ideal sinusoidal voltage source drives a series $R,L,C$ branch and a separate resistor $R$ in parallel. At the series resonance, the reactive stored energy is $LV_0^2/(2R^2)$, while both resistors together dissipate $2\pi V_0^2/(\omega_0R)$ per cycle. Counting only the series resistor overestimates the stored-energy-to-dissipation ratio by a factor of two.

= Parallel RLC circuit
{parent=RLC circuit}
{title2=$Y(\omega)$}

An ideal <resistor>, <inductor> and <capacitor> connected across the same <voltage> have admittance $Y=1/R+i(\omega C-1/(\omega L))$ in the $e^{i\omega t}$ convention. At $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$ the reactive branch currents cancel, giving minimum source current $V_0/R$ and maximum impedance $R$. This differs from a lossy series coil placed across a <capacitor>.

= Free parallel RLC voltage decay
{parent=Parallel RLC circuit}
{title2=$V''+V'/(RC)+V/(LC)=0$}

With no external current, <Kirchhoff's current law> is $C\dot V+V/R+I_L=0$, and $L\dot I_L=V$. Differentiating gives the title equation. For $4R^2>L/C$, its real solutions are $Ae^{-t/(2RC)}\cos(\omega_dt+\phi)$ with $\omega_d^2=1/(LC)-1/(4R^2C^2)$. The original current balance fixes the compatible initial <inductor> current. A single <complex exponential> represents a physical <voltage> through its <real part>; general complex solutions require both conjugate characteristic roots.

= Resistor-referenced parallel-resonator response
{parent=Parallel RLC circuit}
{title2=$H=(R/R_s)/[1+iR(\omega C-1/(\omega L))]$}

When $R_s$ is in series with a <parallel RLC circuit>, the ratio of parallel-network <voltage> to <voltage> across $R_s$ is its impedance divided by $R_s$. Its peak is $R/R_s$, and its <phase> passes from $+\pi/2$ through zero to $-\pi/2$ as <angular frequency> rises through $1/\sqrt{LC}$. This is not the transfer ratio relative to the total input <voltage>, which includes the additional series <voltage> drop and has peak $R/(R+R_s)$. Identifying <voltage> endpoints is necessary before using the <voltage> divider formula.

= Parallel RLC bandwidth
{parent=Parallel RLC circuit}
{title2=$\Delta\omega=1/(RC)$}

For the ratio of resistor-current <amplitude> to total-current <amplitude> to exceed $1/\sqrt2$, $|\omega C-1/(\omega L)|<1/R$. The exact positive endpoint difference is $\Delta\omega=1/(RC)$ and the <quality factor> is $Q=R\sqrt{C/L}$. Greater shunt resistance means weaker dissipation, unlike increasing a series resistance.

= Series RLC circuit
{parent=RLC circuit}
{title2=$Lq''+Rq'+q/C=V(t)$}

In a <series RLC circuit>, the same <electric current> $i=q'$ flows through the <resistor> with <electrical resistance> $R$, the <inductor>, and the <capacitor>. Choose $q$ as the signed <electric charge> on the <capacitor> plate into which positive $i$ flows. With terminal drops measured in that direction, <Kirchhoff's voltage law> gives
$$
v_R=Ri,\qquad v_L=Li',\qquad v_C=q/C,\qquad
Lq''+Rq'+q/C=V(t).
$$
Here $V(t)$ is the applied <electric potential difference>; assume constant $L>0$, $C>0$, and $R>0$. With the applied source set to zero and the loop kept closed, the free response satisfies
$$
q''+2\alpha q'+\omega_0^2q=0,\qquad
\alpha=\frac{R}{2L},\qquad \omega_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}.
$$
The <angular frequency> $\omega_0$ is the value for lossless free <oscillation>; $\alpha$ is the decay parameter. The three possibilities are <underdamped RLC response> for $\alpha<\omega_0$, <critically damped RLC response> for $\alpha=\omega_0$, and <overdamped RLC response> for $\alpha>\omega_0$. https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-2/pages/14-6-rlc-series-circuits[OpenStax University Physics 2, §14.6: free RLC response]{ref}.

For a fair comparison, start each circuit with the same <electric charge> $q(0)=q_0$ and zero <electric current> $q'(0)=0$. Set $\tau=\omega_0t$ and $a=\alpha/\omega_0$; then $x=q/q_0$ obeys $d^2x/d\tau^2+2a\,dx/d\tau+x=0$. The plot compares $a=0.5,1,2$. These dimensionless curves apply to any $L,C$ after rescaling time and <electric charge>.

\Image[rlc-free-damping.png]
{title=Free <series RLC circuit> responses with identical <initial conditions>}
{description=Left: <electric charge> for <underdamped RLC response>, <critically damped RLC response>, and <overdamped RLC response>. Right: total stored <energy>, which decreases in all three cases even when <electric charge> oscillates. The normalization is $\omega_0=1$, $q_0=1$, $q'(0)=0$.}

The stored <energy> is the sum of <magnetic energy> and <capacitor energy>:
$$
E=\frac12Li^2+\frac{q^2}{2C}.
$$
Differentiating and using the circuit equation gives the complete <power> balance,
$$
E'=i\left(Li'+\frac qC\right)=V(t)i-Ri^2.
$$
In the free response, $E'=-Ri^2\leq0$: <Joule heating> accounts exactly for the lost <energy>. For the plotted <initial conditions>, $E(0)=q_0^2/(2C)$ and $E/E(0)=x^2+(dx/d\tau)^2$. Since every free solution tends to zero for $R>0$,
$$
\int_0^\infty Ri(t)^2\,dt=E(0).
$$
In the lossless limit $R=0$, <energy> stays constant and shuttles between the <capacitor> and <inductor>. Thus an <oscillation> of <electric charge> and a decrease of total <energy> are compatible statements.

The analogy with a <damped harmonic oscillator> is exact within these ideal models: <electric charge> corresponds to displacement, <electric current> to <velocity>, <inductance> to <mass>, $1/C$ to <spring constant>, and $R$ to the coefficient of a resisting force proportional to <velocity>. The two stored <energy> terms play the roles of <kinetic energy> and elastic <potential energy>. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_23.html#Ch23-S3[Feynman lectures, I.23.3: the oscillator analogy]{ref}.

Differentiating the driven equation also gives $Li''+Ri'+i/C=V'$. When using this equation to solve for <electric current>, retain the original constraint $Li'(0)+Ri(0)+q(0)/C=V(0)$: the initial <electric charge> fixes the initial rate of change of <electric current>.

= Inductor voltage magnification in a series RLC circuit
{parent=Series RLC circuit}
{title2=$H_L=\frac{i\omega L}{R+i(\omega L-1/(\omega C))}$}

= Inductor voltage magnification
{synonym}

For series resistance $R$, <inductance> $L$ and <capacitance> $C$, with output measured across the <inductor>, the $e^{i\omega t}$ transfer function is $H_L=i\omega L/[R+i(\omega L-1/(\omega C))]$. At current <resonance> $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$ its modulus is $\omega_0L/R=Q$. Large and oppositely phased <inductor> and <capacitor> <voltages> nearly cancel in the loop sum. This is <voltage> magnification in a passive circuit, not <power gain>. The maximum of $|H_L|$ need not occur exactly at the current-resonance <frequency>.

= Displacement and current bandwidths of a series RLC circuit
{parent=Series RLC circuit}

Write $\omega_0^2=1/(LC)$ and $\gamma=R/L$ for a <series RLC circuit>. The squared charge response has denominator $D=(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+\gamma^2\omega^2$. Its nonzero <resonance> peak occurs at $\omega_r^2=\omega_0^2-\gamma^2/2$. The two squared half-height frequencies, when both exist, are
$$
\omega_\pm^2=\omega_0^2-\frac{\gamma^2}{2}\pm\gamma\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\frac{\gamma^2}{4}}.
$$
Their difference in <angular frequencies> is only approximately $\gamma$ at large <quality factor>. The <electric current> instead has $|I|^2=|V|^2/[R^2+(\omega L-1/(\omega C))^2]$. Its exact half-power frequencies satisfy $|\omega L-1/(\omega C)|=R$, whose positive roots differ by $R/L$, independently of <capacitance>. Thus an exact capacitance-independent <bandwidth> applies to current or resistor-voltage output; capacitor-voltage output is a low-pass response with a possible resonant peak.

= Resonant series RLC response
{parent=Series RLC circuit}
{title2=$I_{\mathrm{amp}}(\omega_0)=V_0/R$}

Drive a <series RLC circuit> with <electric potential difference> $V(t)=V_0\cos(\omega t)$, where $V_0>0$ and $\omega>0$. After the free transient decays, the <electric current> has the same <angular frequency> as the source:
$$
i_{\mathrm{ss}}(t)=I_{\mathrm{amp}}\cos(\omega t-\phi),\qquad
I_{\mathrm{amp}}(\omega)=\frac{V_0}{\sqrt{R^2+(\omega L-1/(\omega C))^2}},\qquad
\tan\phi=\frac{\omega L-1/(\omega C)}{R},\quad -\frac\pi2<\phi<\frac\pi2.
$$
For fixed positive $R,L,C,V_0$, the <electric current> <amplitude> is largest exactly at $\omega=\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$. The two <electrical reactance> contributions cancel there, leaving only the drop from <electrical resistance>. In particular,
$$
i_{\mathrm{ss}}(t)=\frac{V_0}{R}\cos(\omega_0t),\qquad
q_{\mathrm{ss}}(t)=\frac{V_0}{R\omega_0}\sin(\omega_0t).
$$
Every solution approaches this periodic response because its difference from the displayed solution is a decaying free response. The peak remains at $\omega_0$ even for an <overdamped RLC response>; free ringing is not required for a maximum of driven <electric current>. <University Physics Volume 2 by Samuel J. Ling and others>[OpenStax University Physics 2, §15.3: current amplitude, phase, and resonance]{ref}.

For example, stipulate $L=10\,\mathrm{mH}$, $C=1\,\mu\mathrm F$, $R=20\,\Omega$, and $V_0=1\,\mathrm V$. Then $\omega_0=10^4\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, $f_0=\omega_0/(2\pi)\approx1591.55\,\mathrm{Hz}$, and the peak <electric current> <amplitude> is $50\,\mathrm{mA}$. Holding the source <amplitude> fixed is essential when comparing responses at different <frequencies>.

\Image[rlc-current-resonance.png]

Three <frequencies> must be distinguished. The free <underdamped RLC response> rings at $\omega_d=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\alpha^2}$. Driven <electric current> peaks at $\omega_0$. Driven <electric charge> has <amplitude>
$$
q_{\mathrm{amp}}(\omega)=\frac{I_{\mathrm{amp}}(\omega)}{\omega}
=\frac{V_0/L}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+4\alpha^2\omega^2}},
$$
whose peak occurs at $\sqrt{\omega_0^2-2\alpha^2}$ only if $\alpha<\omega_0/\sqrt2$. Otherwise it decreases from its zero-frequency limit $CV_0$, with no positive-frequency peak. The different peak locations follow because <electric current> is the time <derivative> of <electric charge>, multiplying a sinusoidal <amplitude> by $\omega$. The limiting responses are $I_{\mathrm{amp}}\sim V_0C\omega$ as $\omega\to0^+$ and $I_{\mathrm{amp}}\sim V_0/(L\omega)$ as $\omega\to\infty$.

The <Q factor> measures selectivity:
$$
Q=\frac{L\omega_0}{R}=\frac{\omega_0}{2\alpha}.
$$
The average <Joule heating> <power> is $RI_{\mathrm{amp}}^2/2$. Its half-maximum <angular frequencies> and their separation are exactly
$$
\omega_\pm=\sqrt{\omega_0^2+\alpha^2}\pm\alpha,\qquad
\Delta\omega=\omega_+-\omega_-=2\alpha=\frac RL=\frac{\omega_0}{Q}.
$$
These follow by setting $|\omega L-1/(\omega C)|=R$. In the numerical example, $Q=5$ and $\Delta\omega=2000\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$. Reducing $R$ makes a taller, narrower <electric current> peak; varying $C$ tunes the selected <frequency>. Measuring the drop $v_R=Ri$ gives a <band-pass filter> that suppresses very low and very high <frequencies> while passing a range near <resonance>. This is useful for selecting a radio signal, though the range must be broad enough to pass its modulation. https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_22.html#Ch22-S7[Feynman lectures, II.22.7: resonant filters and radio sidebands]{ref}.

= Overdamped RLC response
{parent=Series RLC circuit}
{title2=$\alpha>\omega_0$}

An <overdamped RLC response> is a free <series RLC circuit> response with $R>2\sqrt{L/C}$, equivalently $\alpha>\omega_0$. Put $\beta=\sqrt{\alpha^2-\omega_0^2}$. The distinct negative <characteristic roots> are $r_\pm=-\alpha\pm\beta$, so the general <electric charge> is
$$
q(t)=Ae^{r_+t}+Be^{r_-t},\qquad i(t)=q'(t).
$$
For <initial conditions> $q(0)=q_0$, $i(0)=0$,
$$
q(t)=q_0e^{-\alpha t}\left(\cosh(\beta t)+\frac\alpha\beta\sinh(\beta t)\right),\qquad
i(t)=-\frac{q_0\omega_0^2}{\beta}e^{-\alpha t}\sinh(\beta t).
$$
For $q_0>0$, this <electric charge> decreases to zero without reversing sign. In the dimensionless example $\omega_0=1$, $\alpha=2$, the <characteristic roots> are $-2\pm\sqrt3\approx-0.268,-3.732$. The slow term dominates late times. Stronger <damping> can therefore make the final return slower: for $\alpha\gg\omega_0$, $r_+\sim-\omega_0^2/(2\alpha)=-1/(RC)$. Nonoscillation alone does not imply a fast response, and monotonicity here uses the specified <initial conditions>.

= Critically damped RLC response
{parent=Series RLC circuit}
{title2=$\alpha=\omega_0,\quad R=2\sqrt{L/C}$}

A <critically damped RLC response> is the boundary between <underdamped RLC response> and <overdamped RLC response>. The free <series RLC circuit> has a repeated <characteristic root> $-\omega_0$, with general solution
$$
q(t)=(A+Bt)e^{-\omega_0t}.
$$
For <initial conditions> $q(0)=q_0$, $i(0)=0$,
$$
q(t)=q_0(1+\omega_0t)e^{-\omega_0t},\qquad
i(t)=-q_0\omega_0^2t e^{-\omega_0t}.
$$
In the plotted normalization, $q(1)=2/e\approx0.735759$ and $i(1)=-1/e\approx-0.367879$. The <electric charge> returns without ringing; the <polynomial> factor $1+\omega_0t$ matters, so a single <exponential function> does not give the whole response.

For fixed $L,C$ and these <initial conditions>, <critical damping> avoids ringing while giving a faster asymptotic return than any larger $R$: the <overdamped RLC response> has a root closer to zero. This makes <critical damping> a useful tuning target for settling after switching. An MIT laboratory exercise makes the distinction observable by asking students to compare all three cases with $L=47\,\mathrm{mH}$ and $C=47\,\mathrm{nF}$. Those values give $R_{\mathrm{crit}}=2\sqrt{L/C}=2000\,\Omega$ and $\omega_0\approx21276.6\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$. https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-071j-introduction-to-electronics-signals-and-measurement-spring-2006/491e7e50ee65521c601ee51d3c6f7e5f_lab15_transients.pdf[MIT 6.071, Spring 2006, Laboratory 15, exercise 2]{ref}.

= Underdamped RLC response
{parent=Series RLC circuit}
{title2=$\omega_d=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\alpha^2}$}

An <underdamped RLC response> is a free <series RLC circuit> response with $0<R<2\sqrt{L/C}$, equivalently $0<\alpha<\omega_0$. Its <characteristic roots> are $-\alpha\pm\mathrm{i}\omega_d$, and its general <electric charge> is
$$
q(t)=e^{-\alpha t}\left(A\cos(\omega_dt)+B\sin(\omega_dt)\right).
$$
For <initial conditions> $q(0)=q_0$, $i(0)=0$,
$$
q(t)=q_0e^{-\alpha t}\left(\cos(\omega_dt)+\frac\alpha{\omega_d}\sin(\omega_dt)\right),\qquad
i(t)=-\frac{q_0\omega_0^2}{\omega_d}e^{-\alpha t}\sin(\omega_dt).
$$
In the dimensionless example $\omega_0=1$, $\alpha=0.5$, the ringing <angular frequency> is $\omega_d=\sqrt3/2\approx0.866025$, below the driven <electric current> <resonance> at $\omega_0=1$. The <electric charge> first crosses zero at $t=4\pi/(3\sqrt3)\approx2.41840$ and reaches its first negative extremum at $t=2\pi/\sqrt3\approx3.62760$, where $q/q_0=-e^{-\pi/\sqrt3}\approx-0.163034$.

The sinusoidal factor changes sign while its envelope decays as $e^{-\alpha t}$. The total <energy> follows the exact balance $E'=-Ri^2$, rather than a pure <exponential decay> at every instant; in particular, its rate of loss is zero whenever $i=0$. As $\alpha\to0$, the displayed solution tends to the lossless <harmonic oscillator> $q=q_0\cos(\omega_0t)$. As $\alpha\to\omega_0$ from below, $\sin(\omega_dt)/\omega_d\to t$, recovering the <critically damped RLC response> continuously. The <overdamped RLC response> reaches the same limit through $\sinh(\beta t)/\beta\to t$.

= IC

An <integrated circuit> combines interconnected <electrical circuit> elements in a fabricated structure. A <monolithic integrated circuit> forms its elements in or on one piece of <semiconductor>, rather than merely putting separately manufactured components into one enclosure. <Transistors> supply control in <electronic amplifiers>; <resistors> and <capacitors> can supply bias and timing. <Jack St. Clair Kilby>'s patent describes making such functions from a common <semiconductor> body with compatible fabrication steps.<Miniaturized electronic circuits by Jack St. Clair Kilby>[Description, opening statement of the invention and discussion preceding Figs. 1–5a]{ref}

= Discrete circuit
{parent=Integrated circuit}

An <electrical circuit> assembled from separately made components, such as individual <transistors>, <resistors> and <capacitors>. Several functions can share a package without forming a <monolithic integrated circuit>; packaging alone does not establish how the components were fabricated.

= Monolithic integrated circuit
{parent=Integrated circuit}

A <monolithic integrated circuit> has its circuit elements fabricated in or on one <semiconductor die>. Their connections are essential: an unconnected collection of <transistors> does not implement the intended <electrical circuit>.

An original ideal example separates function from fabrication. Connect two <resistors>, $R_1=10\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ above $R_2=20\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$, in a <series circuit> across an ideal $3\,\mathrm V$ <voltage source>. With no load <electric current> drawn from their meeting point, <Ohm's law> and the <Kirchhoff cycle law> give
$$
I=\frac{3\,\mathrm V}{R_1+R_2}=0.1\,\mathrm{mA},\qquad
V_{\rm out}=IR_2=2\,\mathrm V.
$$
These equations apply to either a <discrete circuit> or an idealized <monolithic integrated circuit> realizing those same <electrical resistances>. Integration changes construction; the <electrical circuit> equations still determine the output. A real load, unwanted <capacitance> or finite <resistor tolerance> changes the ideal calculation. Manufacturing many functions together also couples their fate: a fatal defect can discard an entire <semiconductor die>, motivating the <Poisson defect yield> model.

= Kilby integrated circuit demonstration
{c}
{parent=Integrated circuit}

In his 2000 lecture, <Jack St. Clair Kilby> describes an August 1958 comparison circuit made from separate <silicon> elements. <Resistors> were cut and etched; <capacitors> came from diffused, metallized <semiconductor wafers>. It tested whether <semiconductor> elements could perform all the required functions, but was still a <discrete circuit>. For the September prototype he used already diffused <germanium> material with contacts, cut into bars, and hand-applied wax masks to define a <transistor> and a distributed <resistor>-<capacitor> region. An inspected eyewitness interview describes the <germanium> on a microscope slide with protruding wires; changing its connections changed the <oscilloscope> display from a straight line to a <sinusoid>. This supplied a qualitative connected/disconnected comparison, not a calibrated record of <amplitude>, loading or repeatability.<Oral History of Jay W. Lathrop>[Oral History of Jay W. Lathrop, recorded 7 May 2009, p. 11, demonstration and oscilloscope comparison]{ref}

The inference was feasibility: integrated <semiconductor> elements could cooperate as an operating <electrical circuit>. The hand-wired <germanium> prototype did not yet supply the deposited <integrated circuit interconnects> of <Robert Noyce>'s <silicon> design. = Poisson defect yield
{c}
{parent=Integrated circuit}
{title2=$Y=e^{-DA}$}

Consider a deliberately simplified model: fatal defects form a spatially uniform <Poisson point process> with intensity $D$ per unit <area>. A <semiconductor die> of <area> $A$ works exactly when it contains no fatal defect. Its defect count $N$ has a <Poisson distribution> with <expected value> $DA$, hence its probability of working, called its yield, is
$$
Y=\Pr(N=0)=\frac{(DA)^0}{0!}e^{-DA}=e^{-DA}.
$$
The product $DA$ is dimensionless. The formula concerns fatal defects, not every visible imperfection. Spatial uniformity, independent defect counts in disjoint <areas>, and failure from even one fatal defect are model assumptions. Clustering, shared processing failures, repairable regions and defects that affect only some functions can invalidate them. This is a chosen probability model, not historical yield data.

Choose $D=0.005\,\mathrm{mm}^{-2}$. For <semiconductor die> <areas> $25$, $100$ and $200\,\mathrm{mm}^2$, the model gives $Y=e^{-0.125}=0.882497$, $e^{-0.5}=0.606531$ and $e^{-1}=0.367879$. Doubling the <area> squares the yield: $Y(2A)=Y(A)^2$. This follows either from the <exponential function> or by splitting the larger <area> into two disjoint parts and using <independence>. More functions on one <semiconductor die> can save assembly while making the no-defect requirement harder to satisfy.

= Integrated circuit cost per good die
{parent=Poisson defect yield}
{title2=$C_{\rm good}=C_w/(N_gY)$}

Let a circular <semiconductor wafer> have <diameter> $d$ and processed cost $C_w$. For square <semiconductor dies> of <area> $A$, ignoring all edge losses gives the ideal gross count $N_0=\pi d^2/(4A)$. For illustration adopt the geometric edge approximation
$$
N_g\approx\frac{\pi d^2}{4A}-\frac{\pi d}{\sqrt{2A}},\qquad
\mathbb E[G]\approx N_g e^{-DA},\qquad
C_{\rm good}\approx\frac{C_w}{N_g e^{-DA}}.
$$
Here $G$ is the good-die count. The correction scales as wafer perimeter divided by die side length: only a boundary strip can lose partially fitting squares. Its coefficient is an approximation depending on layout; the expression is not an exact packing theorem and is useful only when $\sqrt A\ll d$. Counts from it need not be <integers>. Actual layouts, edge exclusion and separation lanes determine an integer gross count. The <expected value> of $G$ follows by <linearity of expectation>; independence between <semiconductor dies> is not required for that step if each has the same yield.

For the original example $d=200\,\mathrm{mm}$, $A=100\,\mathrm{mm}^2$, $D=0.005\,\mathrm{mm}^{-2}$ and $C_w=1000$ cost units, $N_0=314.159$ and $N_g\approx269.730$. The predicted good count is $163.600$ per wafer, and the processed cost is $6.11248$ units per good die. This is total processed cost divided by expected output, appropriate to a large repeated batch; it is not the <expected value> of a single-wafer random ratio $C_w/G$, which is undefined when $G=0$. Test, packaging, design and equipment costs are excluded.

A hypothetical test readout makes the inference explicit. Suppose $600$ of $1000$ tested <semiconductor dies>, each of <area> $100\,\mathrm{mm}^2$, pass a specified functional test. These are invented counts for calculation, not reported measurements. The pass fraction is $\widehat Y=0.600$; within the <Poisson defect yield> assumptions it implies
$$
\widehat D=-\frac{\ln\widehat Y}{A}=0.00510826\,\mathrm{mm}^{-2}.
$$
If pass indicators are <independent random variables> with common success probability $Y$, their sum has a <binomial distribution>. The fitted standard deviation of the pass fraction is $\sqrt{0.6(1-0.6)/1000}=0.01549$. A test readout estimates pass probability for its tested functions and conditions; it cannot alone establish spatially independent defects, detect every latent failure or distinguish fabrication losses from a faulty test. The <logarithm> only translates the observed fraction into a defect intensity after those additional assumptions are adopted.
