= 1

= Solution
{parent=1}

The conserved initial <four-momentum> of a particle with positive <rest mass> satisfies $P_\mu P^\mu=m^2c^2>0$. A single <photon> instead has null <four-momentum>, $K_\mu K^\mu=0$. <Four-momentum conservation> for an isolated one-body decay would require $P^\mu=K^\mu$, contradicting these <Lorentz invariants>. Thus **decay into a single photon is impossible in every inertial frame**.

Equivalently, the incoming <energy> and <momentum> obey $E^2-c^2|\mathbf p|^2=m^2c^4$, whereas a single outgoing <photon> obeys $E=c|\mathbf p|$. Changing the laboratory <speed> cannot change this mismatch of the invariant <mass shell>.

= 2
{parent=Section A}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=2}

For motion along one spatial axis, introduce the <rapidity> $\eta$ so that the <four-velocity> is $u^\mu=c(\cosh\eta,\sinh\eta,0,0)$. Its <four-acceleration> is
$$
a^\mu=c\frac{d\eta}{d\tau}(\sinh\eta,\cosh\eta,0,0),\qquad a_\mu a^\mu=-c^2\left(\frac{d\eta}{d\tau}\right)^2.
$$
A constant signed <proper acceleration> $g$ therefore gives $d\eta/d\tau=g/c$, hence $\eta=\eta_0+g\tau/c$. Differentiating the <four-velocity> twice yields
$$
\boxed{\frac{d^2u^\mu}{d\tau^2}=\frac{g^2}{c^2}u^\mu.}
$$
The zero transverse components also satisfy this equation. The constant acceleration measured on board is the <proper acceleration>, rather than a constant coordinate acceleration in the laboratory.

= 3
{parent=Section A}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=3}

Use the <geodesic Lagrangian> $L=\tfrac12(y^2\dot x^2+x^2\dot y^2)$, where dots denote an <affine parameter> derivative. The <Euler-Lagrange equations> give
$$
\boxed{y^2\ddot x+2y\dot y\dot x-x\dot y^2=0,\qquad
x^2\ddot y+2x\dot x\dot y-y\dot x^2=0.}
$$
These are the <geodesic equations> wherever $xy\ne0$, so the <Riemannian metric> is nondegenerate. Substitution of $y=mx$, $\dot y=m\dot x$ and $\ddot y=m\ddot x$ makes both equations proportional to
$$
x\ddot x+\dot x^2=\frac{d}{d\lambda}(x\dot x)=0.
$$
Thus $\boxed{x^2=A\lambda+B,\quad y=mx}$ is a family of affinely parametrized <geodesics>, on intervals staying within one quadrant. In particular, $m\ne0$ and $x\ne0$ keep the metric nonsingular; the coordinate axes themselves are not regular points of this metric. The coordinate $x$ need not be linear in the <affine parameter>, despite the straight coordinate-space shape of these curves.

= Solution
{parent=B4}

Use signature $(+---)$ and local inertial coordinates $x^0=ct$. Write $u^\mu=\gamma(c,\mathbf v)$ and $w=\rho c^2+p$, where $\rho$ is rest <mass density>. The <perfect fluid in general relativity> has components
$$
T^{00}=w\gamma^2-p,\qquad T^{0i}=T^{i0}=\frac{w\gamma^2v_i}{c},\qquad
T^{ij}=\frac{w\gamma^2v_iv_j}{c^2}+p\delta_{ij}.
$$
Here $T^{00}$ is <energy density>, $cT^{i0}$ is <energy flux>, $T^{0i}/c$ is <momentum density>, and $T^{ij}$ is the flux of the $j$th <momentum> component through a surface normal to $i$. In the local rest frame these reduce to <energy density> $\rho c^2$, zero energy and momentum flow, and isotropic <pressure> $p$. The equation $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu}=0$ expresses local <stress-energy conservation>. Its temporal and spatial components give energy and momentum balance; in flat coordinates, for example, $\partial_tT^{00}+\partial_i(cT^{i0})=0$.

For <relativistic dust>, $T^{\mu\nu}=\rho u^\mu u^\nu$. Contracting its divergence with $u_\nu$, and using <metric compatibility> together with $u_\nu u^\nu=c^2$, gives
$$
0=u_\nu\nabla_\mu(\rho u^\mu u^\nu)
=c^2\nabla_\mu(\rho u^\mu)+\frac\rho2u^\mu\nabla_\mu(u_\nu u^\nu)
=c^2\nabla_\mu(\rho u^\mu).
$$
Consequently $\nabla_\mu(\rho u^\mu)=0$. Substituting this continuity equation back into the divergence leaves $\rho u^\mu\nabla_\mu u^\nu=0$. Wherever matter is present,
$$
\boxed{u^\mu\nabla_\mu u^\nu=0,}
$$
so the individual particles follow <geodesics>.

The electromagnetic <four-force> law $f^\mu=qF^{\mu\nu}u_\nu$ must preserve the particle's <rest mass>. Thus $u_\mu f^\mu=0$, and requiring this for arbitrary timelike <four-velocities> makes the <electromagnetic field tensor> antisymmetric. Its six independent components comprise the <electric field> and <magnetic field>. With the stated signature and $x^0=ct$, one consistent convention is
$$
F^{i0}=E_i/c,\qquad F^{0i}=-E_i/c,\qquad F^{ij}=-\epsilon_{ijk}B_k.
$$
It gives the spatial force $d\mathbf p/dt=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)$ and energy transfer $dE/dt=q\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v$. Under a <Lorentz transformation>, $F^{\mu\nu}$ transforms as a rank-two <contravariant tensor>; its electric and magnetic components consequently mix. A four-potential supplies $F_{\mu\nu}=\nabla_\mu A_\nu-\nabla_\nu A_\mu$, unchanged by $A_\mu\mapsto A_\mu+\nabla_\mu\chi$. The <Maxwell equations> are
$$
\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=\mu_0j^\nu,\qquad
\nabla_\alpha F_{\beta\gamma}+\nabla_\beta F_{\gamma\alpha}+\nabla_\gamma F_{\alpha\beta}=0.
$$
These combine the source equations and the homogeneous equations into covariant tensor statements; $j^\mu$ is the <four-current>.

For <charged relativistic dust>, the matter tensor exchanges <four-momentum> with the electromagnetic field: $\nabla_\mu T_{\rm m}^{\mu\nu}=F^{\nu\alpha}j_\alpha$, while their total <stress-energy tensor> is conserved. Identical particles of <rest mass> $m$ and <electric charge> $q$ have $j^\alpha=(q/m)\rho u^\alpha$. Contraction with $u_\nu$ again gives mass continuity because $u_\nu F^{\nu\alpha}u_\alpha=0$. Removing that term gives
$$
\boxed{u^\mu\nabla_\mu u^\nu=\frac qmF^{\nu\alpha}u_\alpha.}
$$
Using zero divergence for the matter tensor alone in this last step would omit the electromagnetic <Lorentz force>.

= B5
{c}
{parent=Section B}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=B5}

Write $A(r)=1-2\mu/r$, with $\mu=GM/c^2$. On the equatorial plane, the <geodesic Lagrangian> is
$$
L=\frac12\left(c^2A\dot t^2-A^{-1}\dot r^2-r^2\dot\phi^2\right).
$$
The coordinates $t$ and $\phi$ are cyclic, giving $\boxed{A\dot t=k,\quad r^2\dot\phi=h}$. The conserved physical <energy> per unit <rest mass> is $c^2k$, while $h$ is the signed <angular momentum> per unit <rest mass>. The radial <Euler-Lagrange equation>, with $A'=2\mu/r^2$, is
$$
\boxed{A^{-1}\ddot r+\frac{\mu c^2}{r^2}\dot t^2
-\frac{\mu}{r^2A^2}\dot r^2-r\dot\phi^2=0.}
$$
These are the requested <geodesic equations>. The equatorial condition is consistent with the polar equation by the spherical symmetry of the <Schwarzschild metric>.

The normalization of the <four-velocity> is $c^2=c^2A\dot t^2-A^{-1}\dot r^2-r^2\dot\phi^2$. Insert the two conserved quantities and multiply by $A$ to obtain
$$
\boxed{\dot r^2+\frac{h^2}{r^2}\left(1-\frac{2\mu}r\right)-\frac{2\mu c^2}r
=c^2(k^2-1).}
$$
This is a one-dimensional radial energy equation. Half of its left side is radial kinetic energy per unit mass plus an <effective potential>: the Newtonian centrifugal and gravitational terms acquire the extra attractive contribution $-\mu h^2/r^3$.

A <timelike circular Schwarzschild geodesic> requires $\dot r=\ddot r=0$. Equivalently, set the derivative of $V(r)=h^2/r^2-2\mu h^2/r^3-2\mu c^2/r$ to zero. At $r=R$ this gives $h^2(R-3\mu)=\mu c^2R^2$, and substitution in the radial energy equation yields
$$
\boxed{h^2=\frac{\mu c^2R^2}{R-3\mu},\qquad
k^2=\frac{(R-2\mu)^2}{R(R-3\mu)}.}
$$
Such timelike circular orbits require $R>3\mu$. Their coordinate <angular velocity> satisfies $\Omega^2=\mu c^2/R^3$, and their proper clock rate is $d\tau_s/dt=A/k=\sqrt{1-3\mu/R_s}$.

For the static North-Pole clock, $d\tau_E/dt=\sqrt{1-2\mu/R_E}$. Consecutive overhead passages occur one coordinate orbital period apart, and the repeated Earth-satellite geometry gives equal signal travel delays. The <comparison of circular-orbit and static Schwarzschild clocks> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{\tau_s}{\tau_E}=\sqrt{\frac{1-3\mu/R_s}{1-2\mu/R_E}}.}
$$
This includes both gravitational and orbital-motion effects; a separate Newtonian time-dilation correction must not be added again.

For an estimate, <Kepler's third law> gives $R_s=42100\,\mathrm{km}\,(3/24)^{2/3}=10525\,\mathrm{km}$. The same approximate geostationary radius and period give $\mu=4\pi^2R_{\rm geo}^3/(c^2T_{\rm geo}^2)\simeq4.4\,\mathrm{mm}$. Expanding the ratio to first order,
$$
\frac{\tau_s}{\tau_E}\simeq1+\frac\mu{R_E}-\frac{3\mu}{2R_s}
\simeq\boxed{1+6.4\times10^{-11}}.
$$
The satellite clock consequently gains about $0.7\,\mu\mathrm s$ over a three-hour orbit in this approximation. The surface observer is taken to be static, and the spacetime model is the spherical <Schwarzschild metric> specified in the problem.

= Solution
{parent=B6}

A <fundamental observer> follows the mean cosmic matter flow and is at rest with respect to the homogeneous cosmological frame. <Cosmological isotropy> permits no preferred spatial acceleration, rotation vector or anisotropic expansion for this flow: the congruence is geodesic, irrotational and shear-free, with isotropic expansion. Its orthogonal homogeneous spatial slices define simultaneity. Choosing fixed spatial labels along these worldlines and their common <proper time> as $t$ gives <synchronous coordinates>, $g_{tt}=c^2$ and $g_{ti}=0$.

In the <Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker metric>, $t$ is this cosmic <proper time> and $\chi$ is radial <comoving coordinate>. On a constant-$t$ radial slice, the <proper distance in cosmology> from $0$ to a fixed $\chi_0$ is $D(t)=a(t)\chi_0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\dot D=H(t)D,\qquad H(t)=\frac{\dot a(t)}{a(t)}.}
$$
This is the <Hubble parameter>. The metric describes expansion when $\dot a>0$; its form alone also permits static or contracting histories, so expansion additionally requires that sign of the <scale factor> derivative.

For a radial <photon>, the metric components relevant to its motion are $g_{tt}=c^2$ and $g_{\chi\chi}=-a^2(t)$, both independent of $\chi$. The angular momentum components vanish. The covariant-momentum form of the <geodesic equation> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{dp_\chi}{d\lambda}=\frac12\partial_\chi g_{\nu\rho}p^\nu p^\rho=0.}
$$
Only this radial contraction is needed: angular metric coefficients can depend on $\chi$, but multiply zero angular components. The conserved quantity is the lower-index $p_1=p_\chi$, rather than the contravariant component $p^1$.

For outward travel put $P=-p_\chi>0$. The null condition $c^2(p^t)^2-a^2(p^\chi)^2=0$ gives
$$
p^\chi=\frac P{a^2},\qquad p^t=\frac P{ac},\qquad\boxed{p_0=p_t=c^2p^t=\frac{cP}{a(t)}.}
$$
Here the time coordinate is $t$, rather than $ct$. A <comoving observer> has $u^t=1$ and measures <photon energy> $E=p_\mu u^\mu=p_t$. Thus <energy> and <frequency> decrease as $a^{-1}$, giving the <cosmological redshift>
$$
\boxed{1+z=\frac{\nu_E}{\nu_R}=\frac{a(t_R)}{a(t_E)}.}
$$
The reciprocal relation for <wavelengths> follows from their common local propagation speed $c$.

Finally center the spatial coordinates on the receiving <comoving observer>. At the source, the transverse spatial line element is $dl=a(t_E)S_K(\chi)\,d\theta$. For a short transverse rod and a small observed <angle> $\Delta\theta$,
$$
l=a(t_E)S_K(\chi)\Delta\theta
=\boxed{\frac{a(t_R)S_K(\chi)}{1+z}\Delta\theta.}
$$
Thus the <angular diameter distance> is $d_A=a(t_E)S_K(\chi)$. Writing the reception epoch as $t_0=t_R$ gives the displayed formula in the question. The length is the proper transverse length at emission; $t_0$ cannot be replaced by an unrelated epoch while retaining that expression.
