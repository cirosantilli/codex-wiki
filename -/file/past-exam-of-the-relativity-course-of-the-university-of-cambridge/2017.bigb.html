= Solution
{parent=a}

Use the <Christoffel symbol> formula with its first index raised:
$$
\Gamma^i{}_{jk}=\frac12g^{i\ell}(\partial_jg_{\ell k}+\partial_kg_{\ell j}-\partial_\ell g_{jk}).
$$
The diagonal <metric tensor> has $g_{rr}=e^{2\chi}$ and $g_{\theta\theta}=r^2e^{2\chi}$, with inverse components $g^{rr}=e^{-2\chi}$ and $g^{\theta\theta}=r^{-2}e^{-2\chi}$. Since $\partial_r\chi=\chi'$ and all angular derivatives vanish,
$$
\boxed{\Gamma^r{}_{rr}=\chi',\qquad
\Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta}=-r-r^2\chi',\qquad
\Gamma^\theta{}_{\theta r}=\Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta}=\frac1r+\chi'.}
$$
For example, $\Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta}=-\tfrac12e^{-2\chi}\partial_r(r^2e^{2\chi})$. The other components vanish. The formula supplied in the paper gives the first-kind coefficients $\Gamma_{ijk}$; multiplication by the inverse <metric tensor> is needed for the requested second-kind coefficients. These <polar coordinates> are understood away from $r=0$.

= Solution

For a <scalar field>, $\nabla_\mu\phi=\partial_\mu\phi$. Apply the <product rule> to the <stress-energy tensor>, using <metric compatibility> to pass the <covariant derivative> through $g^{\mu\nu}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu}
={}&(\Box\phi)\nabla^\nu\phi
+\nabla^\mu\phi\,\nabla_\mu\nabla^\nu\phi\\
&-\nabla_\sigma\phi\,\nabla^\nu\nabla^\sigma\phi
+m^2\phi\nabla^\nu\phi.
\end{aligned}
$$
The middle terms cancel because the <Levi-Civita connection> gives a <symmetric Hessian of a scalar field>. Thus, with the sign conventions of this question, the <scalar stress-energy divergence identity> becomes
$$
\boxed{\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu}=(\Box\phi+m^2\phi)\nabla^\nu\phi=0.}
$$
The last equality is the <Klein-Gordon equation>. This <stress-energy conservation> follows from the field equation and <metric compatibility>; it does not require imposing the <Einstein field equations>.

Take <metric signature> $(+,-,-,-)$ and the supplied covariant <electromagnetic field tensor>, so $F_{0i}=E_i$ and $F_{ij}=-\epsilon_{ijk}B_k$. Raising both indices reverses the time-space components but leaves the spatial components unchanged. Its complete contraction is therefore
$$
F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}=2(B^2-E^2).
$$
Define the <dual electromagnetic field tensor> by $\widetilde F^{\mu\nu}=\tfrac12\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}F_{\rho\sigma}$, with $\varepsilon^{0123}=+1$. Direct contraction gives
$$
\boxed{E^2-B^2=-\frac12F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu},\qquad
\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B=-\frac14F_{\mu\nu}\widetilde F^{\mu\nu}.}
$$
These <electromagnetic field invariants> are complete tensor contractions. The second is a <pseudoscalar>: it is invariant under proper <Lorentz transformations> and changes sign under spatial inversion.

For $T^{0i}$ the metric trace term vanishes. Since $F^0{}_j=E_j$,
$$
F^0{}_{\rho}F^{i\rho}=\sum_jE_j(-\epsilon_{ijk}B_k)=-(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)_i.
$$
Consequently the <Poynting vector> is exactly the time-space row in the stated $c=1$ units:
$$
\boxed{(T^{01}_{\rm em},T^{02}_{\rm em},T^{03}_{\rm em})
=\frac1{\mu_0}\mathbf E\times\mathbf B.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=a}

The three classic tests probe orbital motion, the propagation of <light>, and clock rates in a <gravitational field>.

**<Schwarzschild perihelion precession>.** For an orbit with <semi-major axis> $a$ and <orbital eccentricity> $e$, the leading advance per revolution is
$$
\boxed{\Delta\varpi=\frac{6\pi GM}{a(1-e^2)c^2}.}
$$
The relevant observation for <Mercury (planet)> is the residual advance after the much larger ordinary perturbations have been included, approximately $43$ arcseconds per century.https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19870009701/downloads/19870009701.pdf[Physics through the 1990s: Gravitation, cosmology and cosmic-ray physics, 1986: discussion of the anomalous perihelion advance]{ref} An orbital fit must account for the other planets and reference-frame effects before interpreting that residual as a relativistic contribution.

**<Schwarzschild light deflection>.** A ray with large <impact parameter> $b$ passing a nearly spherical mass is deflected through
$$
\boxed{\alpha\simeq\frac{4GM}{bc^2}.}
$$
At the <Sun>'s limb this is about $1.75$ arcseconds. The <Eddington experiment> used the 1919 <solar eclipse> to photograph background <stars> near the obscured <Sun>, then compared their positions with reference photographs. The measured effect is an apparent outward displacement of the background <stars>, not a direct photograph of a curved light path. Telescope focus, plate scale and atmospheric effects must be controlled when extracting such small shifts.<A determination of the deflection of light by the Sun's gravitational field by Frank Watson Dyson and others>[1920 paper, observational comparison and deflection analysis]{ref}

**<Gravitational redshift>.** For a photon emitted lower in a weak static field and received a height $H$ above it,
$$
\boxed{\frac{\nu_{\rm rec}-\nu_{\rm em}}{\nu_{\rm em}}\simeq-\frac{gH}{c^2}.}
$$
The <Pound-Rebka experiment> used the narrow recoil-free nuclear resonance supplied by the <Mössbauer effect>. A controlled source motion supplies a small <Doppler shift> that compensates the gravitational shift, allowing a change in resonant absorption to reveal it across a laboratory tower.<Apparent Weight of Photons by Robert Vivian Pound and Glen Rebka>[1960 experiment: gamma-ray source and resonant absorber at different heights]{ref} The leading weak-field redshift follows from the <equivalence principle> and therefore does not, by itself, distinguish every competing metric theory of gravity.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Nearby freely falling particles can accelerate relative to one another even though each follows a <geodesic>. A local <inertial frame> removes the common gravitational acceleration at one event; it cannot remove the variation across an extended laboratory. That variation is a <tidal force>, measured covariantly by <geodesic deviation>.

Let $u^\mu$ be the reference <four-velocity> and $\xi^\mu$ an infinitesimal separation to a neighboring <geodesic>, with matching <affine parameters>. With the convention $[\nabla_\beta,\nabla_\gamma]V^\mu=R^\mu{}_{\nu\beta\gamma}V^\nu$,
$$
\boxed{\frac{D^2\xi^\mu}{D\tau^2}=R^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta\gamma}u^\alpha u^\beta\xi^\gamma.}
$$
This relates measured relative <acceleration> to the <Riemann curvature tensor>. Reversing the curvature-tensor convention reverses the displayed curvature sign, without changing the physical relative motion.

In a small freely falling frame outside a spherical mass, the weak-field <tidal tensor> has radial and transverse components proportional to $2,-1,-1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\ddot\xi_r=\frac{2GM}{r^3}\xi_r,\qquad
\ddot\xi_{\perp}=-\frac{GM}{r^3}\xi_{\perp}.}
$$
An initially spherical cloud stretches radially and compresses in both transverse directions. Two separated test masses, with their changing separation measured by light, form the basic idea of a gravity gradiometer; the common falling motion drops out. A uniform apparent acceleration in an accelerated laboratory can occur even in flat <Minkowski spacetime>, so it is the separation-dependent tidal response that diagnoses curvature.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Write $r_s=2GM/c^2$ and $f=1-r_s/r$. The <Schwarzschild tortoise coordinate> satisfies $dr_*/dr=1/f$ and can be chosen as $r_*=r+r_s\ln|r/r_s-1|$. Define the advanced time $v=t+r_*/c$. Substituting $dt=dv-dr/(cf)$ into the <Schwarzschild metric> gives the <ingoing Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates> form
$$
\boxed{ds^2=f c^2dv^2-2c\,dv\,dr-r^2d\Omega^2.}
$$
The coefficients are regular at $r=r_s$ and the radial-block determinant is $-c^2$, so the exterior Schwarzschild-time divergence is a coordinate singularity at the future <event horizon>. The physical curvature singularity at $r=0$ remains.

The two families of <radial null trajectories in ingoing Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates> obey
$$
\boxed{v=\mathrm{constant}\quad\text{(ingoing)},\qquad
\frac{dr}{dv}=\frac c2\left(1-\frac{r_s}{r}\right)\quad\text{(outgoing)}.}
$$
Outside the <event horizon>, the outgoing family increases $r$; at the horizon it generates the horizon itself; inside, even the outgoing future-directed family decreases $r$. An infaller crosses the future horizon at finite $v$ and finite <proper time>. The chart makes this causal geometry visible without mistaking Schwarzschild coordinate time for the infaller's clock.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $x^\mu(\tau)$ denote the spacetime coordinates, with dots meaning differentiation with respect to an <affine parameter>; for a massive particle take <proper time> $\tau$. The <metric tensor> determines the quadratic <geodesic Lagrangian> $L=g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu$. The <Euler-Lagrange equations> are
$$
\frac d{d\tau}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^\mu}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^\mu}=0.
$$
Symmetry of the <metric tensor> gives $\partial L/\partial\dot x^\mu=2g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\nu$. Hence the covariant <four-momentum> is $p_\mu=mg_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\nu=(m/2)\partial L/\partial\dot x^\mu$. If $x^\mu$ is a <cyclic coordinate>, its <Euler-Lagrange equation> immediately gives $\dot p_\mu=0$.

For the rotating metric, $t,\phi,z$ are all cyclic. Thus
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
p_t&=m[(c^2-\omega^2r^2)\dot t-\omega r^2\dot\phi],\\
p_\phi&=-mr^2(\dot\phi+\omega\dot t),\qquad p_z=-m\dot z.
\end{aligned}}
$$
Combining the first two equations cancels their $r$ dependence:
$$
\boxed{\dot t=\frac{p_t-\omega p_\phi}{mc^2}=\mathrm{constant}.}
$$
The radial <Euler-Lagrange equation> is $-2\ddot r=-2r(\dot\phi+\omega\dot t)^2$, so
$$
\boxed{\ddot r=r(\dot\phi+\omega\dot t)^2=\frac{p_\phi^2}{m^2r^3}.}
$$
This is the <geodesic equation in a uniformly rotating Minkowski chart>. With inertial angle $\Phi=\phi+\omega t$, $-p_\phi$ is the inertial axial <angular momentum>. The outward radial-coordinate acceleration expresses the familiar centrifugal term associated with angular motion: the underlying free trajectory is a straight line in <Minkowski spacetime>. It does not imply an external radial force. Holding a particle at rest in the <rotating reference frame> instead requires a real inward force.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Let the <inertial frame> $S'$ move at speed $v$ in the positive $x$ direction relative to $S$, with parallel axes and coincident origins at zero time. The inverse <Lorentz transformation> gives
$$
dt=\gamma_v(dt'+v\,dx'/c^2),\quad dx=\gamma_v(dx'+v\,dt'),\quad dy=dy',\quad dz=dz'.
$$
Here $\gamma_v=(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$ and $\mathbf u'=d\mathbf x'/dt'$ is the particle's <velocity> in $S'$. Dividing spatial differentials by $dt$ gives the <relativistic velocity addition> law
$$
\boxed{u_x=\frac{u_x'+v}{1+vu_x'/c^2},\qquad
u_y=\frac{u_y'}{\gamma_v(1+vu_x'/c^2)},\qquad
u_z=\frac{u_z'}{\gamma_v(1+vu_x'/c^2)}.}
$$
For the collision, set $c=1$ during the algebra. Let the incident projectile have <energy> $E>m_1$ and <momentum> $p=\sqrt{E^2-m_1^2}$. The target is initially at rest. The squared <invariant mass>, boost speed and <Lorentz factor> of the <center-of-momentum frame> are
$$
s=m_1^2+m_2^2+2m_2E,\qquad V=\frac p{E+m_2},\qquad\Gamma=\frac{E+m_2}{\sqrt s}.
$$
In that frame the projectile has $p_*=m_2p/\sqrt s$ and $E_*=(m_1^2+m_2E)/\sqrt s$. An <elastic collision> changes the direction of its <momentum>, through an angle $\varphi$, while preserving these magnitudes. Transforming the outgoing <four-momentum> back to the laboratory gives
$$
\tan\theta=\frac{\sin\varphi}{\Gamma(\cos\varphi+B)},\qquad
B=\frac{VE_*}{p_*}=\frac{m_1^2+m_2E}{m_2(E+m_2)}>1.
$$
The maximum occurs at $\cos\varphi=-1/B$. Since
$$
\Gamma^2(B^2-1)=\frac{m_1^2-m_2^2}{m_2^2},
$$
the <maximum laboratory deflection in relativistic elastic scattering> is
$$
\boxed{\tan\theta_{\max}=\frac{m_2}{\sqrt{m_1^2-m_2^2}},\qquad
\sin\theta\leq\sin\theta_{\max}=\frac{m_2}{m_1}.}
$$
The printed strict inequality needs correction: equality is kinematically attainable. For example, in $c=1$ units take $m_1=2$, $m_2=1$, and incident $E=3$. A projectile scattered through $30^\circ$ with final <energy> $16/7$ has <momentum> $(3\sqrt5/7,\sqrt{15}/7)$. The target then has <energy> $12/7$ and <momentum> $(4\sqrt5/7,-\sqrt{15}/7)$; both outgoing particles satisfy their <mass shells> and conserve total <four-momentum>. This gives $\sin\theta=1/2=m_2/m_1$ exactly. The inequality is strict only away from the maximizing direction.

= Solution
{parent=4}

**Curvature contractions.** Use the index contraction stipulated in the question. In three dimensions,
$$
R_{jk}=g^{il}K(g_{ik}g_{jl}-g_{il}g_{jk})=K(g_{jk}-3g_{jk})=-2Kg_{jk}.
$$
The same contraction in four dimensions gives
$$
R_{\beta\gamma}=-\frac\Lambda3(g_{\beta\gamma}-4g_{\beta\gamma})=\Lambda g_{\beta\gamma}.
$$
Then the <Ricci scalar> is $R=4\Lambda$ and the <Einstein tensor> is $G_{\mu\nu}=-\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$. Thus $R_{\mu\nu}=\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}$ is precisely the vacuum <Einstein field equations> $G_{\mu\nu}+\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}=0$.

**The static solution.** For $A=1+\lambda^2r^2$, $A'=2\lambda^2r$ and $A''=2\lambda^2$. The given <Ricci tensor> components become
$$
R_{00}=-3\lambda^2A,\quad R_{11}=\frac{3\lambda^2}{A},\quad
R_{22}=3\lambda^2r^2,\quad R_{33}=3\lambda^2r^2\sin^2\theta.
$$
Each equals $-3\lambda^2$ times the corresponding <metric tensor> component. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Lambda=-3\lambda^2.}
$$
This is <Anti-de Sitter spacetime> in <static coordinates on anti-de Sitter spacetime>, with curvature radius $1/\lambda$. The overall <metric signature> is opposite to the convention used in some descriptions of those coordinates; the geodesic constants below use the question's $(+,-,-,-)$ convention.

**Conserved quantities and radial motion.** In the equatorial plane, the <geodesic Lagrangian> is $L=A\dot t^2-\dot r^2/A-r^2\dot\phi^2$. Its <cyclic coordinates> give
$$
\boxed{k=A\dot t,\qquad h=r^2\dot\phi.}
$$
For rest <mass> $m$, $mk=p_t$ is the conserved Killing <energy> and $mh=-p_\phi$ is the axial <angular momentum>. Timelike normalization $L=1$ gives
$$
\boxed{\dot r^2=k^2-A\left(1+\frac{h^2}{r^2}\right)
=k^2-1-\lambda^2h^2-\lambda^2r^2-\frac{h^2}{r^2}.}
$$
For purely radial motion $h=0$, differentiating away from a turning point gives $\ddot r=-\lambda^2r$, and continuity extends that equation through the turning point. An outward launch from the origin at $\tau=0$ has
$$
\boxed{r(\tau)=\frac{\sqrt{k^2-1}}\lambda\sin(\lambda\tau),\qquad
a=\frac{\sqrt{k^2-1}}\lambda,\quad b=\lambda.}
$$
This is the <radial timelike geodesic in anti-de Sitter spacetime>. Its first return to the origin is
$$
\boxed{\tau_{\rm return}=\frac\pi\lambda,}
$$
independent of the outward launch speed for every nontrivial launch $k>1$. The coordinate $r$ is nonnegative, so this sine expression describes the excursion up to that return; subsequent passage through the origin requires changing the radial direction or using a signed diameter coordinate. The speed changes the amplitude, not the harmonic frequency.

**Circular periods.** The effective radial potential is $V_{\rm eff}=A(1+h^2/r^2)$. For a circle at $r_0$, $V_{\rm eff}'=0$ gives $h^2=\lambda^2r_0^4$, and normalization gives $k=A(r_0)$. Thus $\dot t=1$ and $\dot\phi=\pm\lambda$. A <stable circular timelike geodesic in anti-de Sitter spacetime> therefore has
$$
\boxed{\Delta t=\Delta\tau=\frac{2\pi}\lambda,}
$$
independently of radius. This is the same isochronous behavior as the central harmonic radial equation. A static clock placed at the orbit's radius instead measures $\sqrt{1+\lambda^2r_0^2}\,\Delta t$, which does depend on radius; it is a different clock from the orbiting particle's <proper time>.
