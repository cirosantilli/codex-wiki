= Solution
{parent=1}

Use <angular size under a collinear relativistic boost>. For a small distant source, the two <angular diameters> have ratio $e^{|\eta|}$, where $\eta$ is the relative <rapidity>. Thus $e^{|\eta|}=2$, and the relative <velocity> satisfies
$$
\boxed{\frac{|v|}{c}=\tanh(\log2)=\frac{2^2-1}{2^2+1}=\frac35.}
$$
The speed is therefore $0.6c$. The observers are momentarily coincident, so this is <relativistic aberration>, rather than a difference in their distance from the planet. The observer moving faster towards the planet measures the smaller angular diameter under the source-direction aberration convention.

= Solution
{parent=2}

The <metric tensor> and its inverse are diagonal: $g_{xx}=1$, $g_{yy}=f^2$, $g^{xx}=1$, $g^{yy}=f^{-2}$ on a patch where $f\ne0$. Only $\partial_xg_{yy}=2ff'$ is nonzero. The <Christoffel symbol> formula therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\Gamma^x_{yy}=-ff',\qquad\Gamma^y_{xy}=\Gamma^y_{yx}=\frac{f'}f,}
$$
with all other components zero. Using the stated <Riemann curvature tensor> convention,
$$
R^x{}_{yxy}=\partial_x\Gamma^x_{yy}-\Gamma^y_{yx}\Gamma^x_{yy}
=-(f'^2+ff'')+f'^2,
$$
so $\boxed{R^x{}_{yxy}=-ff''}$. This is the <curvature of a two-dimensional warped metric>; its <Gaussian curvature> is $-f''/f$.

{parent=Section A}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=3}

For a grazing <null geodesic>, take the <impact parameter> as $b\simeq R=6.0\times10^6\,\mathrm m$. The leading <Schwarzschild light deflection> is
$$
\delta=\frac{4GM}{bc^2}.
$$
With the <gravitational constant> $G\simeq6.6743\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}$ and <speed of light> $c\simeq2.998\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$,
$$
\boxed{\delta\simeq9.9\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{rad}\simeq0.0567^\circ\simeq204\text{ arcsec}\simeq3.4\text{ arcmin}.}
$$
Here $GM/(Rc^2)\simeq2.5\times10^{-4}\ll1$, so the weak-field approximation is appropriate. A ray passing at a larger <impact parameter> has a correspondingly smaller deflection.

= a

{scope}

= Solution
{parent=a}

A <fundamental observer> follows the coarse-grained cosmological matter, with fixed <comoving coordinates>. The <cosmological principle> asserts <spatial homogeneity> and <cosmological isotropy> on large scales for this family of observers. A moving observer generally sees a preferred direction, for example a Doppler dipole in the <cosmic microwave background>, even when the underlying cosmology is isotropic.

In an <FLRW metric>, these worldlines are orthogonal to homogeneous spatial slices. Synchronize their clocks to define <cosmic time> $t$, which is their <proper time>. Along a fixed spatial label, $ds^2=c^2dt^2$, giving $g_{tt}=c^2$. Orthogonality of the spatial coordinate directions to the observers' <four-velocity> gives $g_{ti}=0$. Thus, in the $+---$ <metric signature>,
$$
\boxed{ds^2=c^2dt^2+g_{ij}(t,\mathbf x)\,dx^idx^j,}
$$
where the spatial matrix $g_{ij}$ is negative definite. <Spatial homogeneity> and <cosmological isotropy> further restrict it to $-a(t)^2\gamma_{ij}(\mathbf x)$, with $\gamma$ a constant-curvature spatial <metric>.

= b
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

For radial motion, use the <geodesic Lagrangian>
$$
\mathcal L=\frac12(c^2\dot t^2-a(t)^2\dot\chi^2).
$$
The <Euler-Lagrange equation> for $t$ gives $c^2\ddot t+a(da/dt)\dot\chi^2=0$. Since $\chi$ is a <cyclic coordinate>, its conjugate momentum is constant. Hence
$$
\boxed{\frac d{d\tau}(c^2\dot t)+a\frac{da}{dt}\dot\chi^2=0,\qquad a^2\dot\chi=C.}
$$
For a massive particle, take $\tau$ as <proper time>; the <four-velocity> normalization is $c^2\dot t^2-a^2\dot\chi^2=c^2$. For a <photon>, use an <affine parameter> rather than proper time, and the corresponding tangent is a <null vector>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\dot t^2=1+\frac{a^2\dot\chi^2}{c^2}\quad\text{(massive)},\qquad
\dot t^2=\frac{a^2\dot\chi^2}{c^2}\quad\text{(massless)}.}
$$
The normalization of the null affine parameter remains arbitrary.

= c
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

Choose the null <affine parameter> so that the <photon> tangent is its <four-momentum> $P^\mu$. A <fundamental observer> has $U^\mu=(1,0,0,0)$ in cosmic-time coordinates, so the measured energy is $E=P_\mu U^\mu=c^2P^t$. The null normalization and conserved radial quantity in part (b) give
$$
P^t=\frac{|C|}{ac},\qquad E=\frac{c|C|}{a}.
$$
Thus <redshifting of peculiar momentum> gives $E\propto a^{-1}$. Since $E=h\nu$, with $h$ the <Planck constant>, the received-to-emitted <frequency> ratio is $\nu_R/\nu_E=a(t_E)/a(t_R)$. By the definition of <cosmological redshift>,
$$
\boxed{1+z=\frac{\nu_E}{\nu_R}=\frac{a(t_R)}{a(t_E)}.}
$$

= d
{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

A nearby <fundamental observer> measures radial proper distance $d\ell=a\,d\chi$ and proper time $dt$. Thus the signed radial <peculiar velocity> is
$$
\boxed{v=a\frac{d\chi}{dt}=\frac{a\dot\chi}{\dot t}.}
$$
Substitute $a^2\dot\chi=C$ and the massive normalization from part (b):
$$
\boxed{v(a)=\frac{C/a}{\sqrt{1+C^2/(a^2c^2)}},\qquad a\gamma v=C.}
$$
Equivalently, for velocity $v_0$ at scale factor $a_0$,
$$
v(a)=\frac{v_0a_0}{\sqrt{a^2(1-v_0^2/c^2)+a_0^2v_0^2/c^2}}.
$$
This is the exact law of <redshifting of peculiar momentum>. In the nonrelativistic limit, $v\propto a^{-1}$; an initially relativistic speed stays below $c$ and decreases as the universe expands.

{parent=Section B}
{scope}

= a
{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=a}

Write $f(r)=1-2\mu/r$. In the equatorial plane, the <geodesic Lagrangian> is $\mathcal L=\frac12(fc^2\dot t^2-f^{-1}\dot r^2-r^2\dot\phi^2)$. The <cyclic coordinates> $t$ and $\phi$ give the conserved quantities
$$
\boxed{f\dot t=k,\qquad r^2\dot\phi=h.}
$$
The squared tangent norm is constant along an affinely parametrized <geodesic>, since metric compatibility gives $d[g(\dot X,\dot X)]/d\tau=2g(D\dot X/d\tau,\dot X)=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{fc^2\dot t^2-f^{-1}\dot r^2-r^2\dot\phi^2=E.}
$$
For a massive particle parametrized by <proper time>, $E=c^2$; a <photon> has a null tangent, so $E=0$. This $E$ denotes the tangent norm, not the photon's physical energy. A future-directed exterior photon has $k>0$, proportional to its positive <Killing energy>. Its affine parameter can be rescaled, so there is no mass-shell normalization fixing $k$ to a particular positive value.

= b
{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

Set $E=0$ in part (a), substitute the two conserved quantities, and multiply the normalization by $f$:
$$
c^2k^2-\dot r^2-f\frac{h^2}{r^2}=0.
$$
Consequently the <null effective potential in Schwarzschild spacetime> is
$$
\boxed{\frac12\dot r^2+V_{\rm eff}(r)=\frac12c^2k^2,\qquad
V_{\rm eff}(r)=\frac{h^2}{2r^2}\left(1-\frac{2\mu}{r}\right).}
$$
Only radii where the total radial energy is at least this <effective potential> are accessible.

= c
{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

For $h\ne0$, the <effective potential> vanishes at $r=2\mu$, rises to a maximum, and then falls to zero as $r\to\infty$. Its <derivative> is $V'_{\rm eff}=h^2(3\mu-r)/r^4$, so the only circular <null geodesic> has
$$
\boxed{r=3\mu,\qquad V_{\rm eff}=\frac{h^2}{54\mu^2}.}
$$
The second derivative there is $V''_{\rm eff}=-h^2/(81\mu^4)<0$, proving instability by the <effective potential stability criterion>. These circular orbits make up the <photon sphere>. If the formula is continued inside the horizon, the potential is negative and diverges to $-\infty$ as $r\downarrow0$. For purely radial light, $h=0$, the potential is identically zero and there is no circular orbit.

= d
{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=d}

A photon arriving from infinity crosses the barrier precisely when $c^2k^2/2$ exceeds the maximum in part (c):
$$
\boxed{k^2>\frac{h^2}{27\mu^2c^2}.}
$$
At infinity its spatial tangent has speed in affine-parameter units $ck$, while $h$ is its angular momentum per unit affine normalization. A straight asymptotic ray with <impact parameter> $b$ therefore has $|h|=bck$. Thus
$$
\boxed{b=\frac{|h|}{ck},\qquad b<3\sqrt3\mu.}
$$
At equality, the incoming ray approaches the unstable <photon sphere> asymptotically and is not captured. A larger impact parameter gives an outer turning point and scattering back to infinity.

= e
{parent=5}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=e}

Measure $\alpha$ in the <orthonormal tetrad> of a static observer at $r>2\mu$. This specifies the local frame needed to define an angle. If $E_\infty$ is the conserved photon energy, <gravitational redshift> gives $E_{\rm loc}=E_\infty/\sqrt{1-2\mu/r}$. The tangential local <momentum> is $E_{\rm loc}\sin\alpha/c$, so the <impact parameter> is
$$
b=\frac{r\sin\alpha}{\sqrt{1-2\mu/r}}.
$$
Inside the <photon sphere>, an outward ray must have $b<3\sqrt3\mu$ to cross the potential barrier. The <Schwarzschild photon escape cone> therefore has critical half-angle
$$
\boxed{\alpha_{\rm crit}(r)=\arcsin\left[\frac{3\sqrt3\mu}{r}\sqrt{1-\frac{2\mu}{r}}\right],\qquad 0\le\alpha<\alpha_{\rm crit}.}
$$
The upper bound is strict: a critical ray only approaches the circular orbit. At $r=3\mu$, the critical half-angle is $\pi/2$, and exactly tangential light is circular. As $r\downarrow2\mu$, the cone shrinks to zero opening. No static observer exists exactly on the <Schwarzschild event horizon>, and no future-directed photon emitted there escapes to exterior infinity; the zero-angle result is an exterior limit.

= Solution
{parent=a}

At any event on a massive observer's <worldline>, choose an <orthonormal tetrad> with timelike basis vector $e_{(0)}=U/c$. Its components obey $g(e_{(a)},e_{(b)})=\eta_{ab}=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$, so locally $ds^2=\eta_{ab}\,dX^{(a)}dX^{(b)}$: the <Minkowski metric>. A <local inertial frame> can also remove the <Christoffel symbols> at that event. Curvature cannot be removed on a finite region, and a frame following an accelerated observer need not remain inertial in its neighbourhood.

In general coordinates, $X^\mu(\tau)$ labels the trajectory, while the <four-velocity> and <four-acceleration> are
$$
U^\mu=\frac{dX^\mu}{d\tau},\qquad
A^\mu=\frac{DU^\mu}{d\tau}=\frac{dU^\mu}{d\tau}+\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}U^\nu U^\rho.
$$
Coordinate values $X^\mu$ are not themselves a vector under arbitrary nonlinear coordinate changes; their tangent $U$ is. For fixed rest mass $m$, the <four-momentum> is $P^\mu=mU^\mu$ and the <four-force> is $f^\mu=DP^\mu/d\tau=mA^\mu$. With the $+---$ <metric signature>,
$$
\boxed{U^\mu U_\mu=c^2,\qquad U^\mu A_\mu=0,\qquad A^\mu A_\mu=-a^2\le0,}
$$
where $a$ is the <proper acceleration>. The middle identity follows by differentiating the constant velocity norm; in the instantaneous <rest frame>, $U=(c,\mathbf0)$ and $A=(0,\mathbf a)$, proving the last identity.

= Solution
{parent=i}

The inverse transformation is $t=(p+q)/2$, $x=(q-p)/2$. Thus
$$
ds^2=\frac14\big[(dp+dq)^2-(dq-dp)^2\big]=dp\,dq.
$$
The <metric tensor> in these <light-cone coordinates> is therefore
$$
\boxed{(g_{ab})_{(p,q)}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1/2\\1/2&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
(g^{ab})=\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\2&0\end{pmatrix}.}
$$
Both off-diagonal entries are needed, since the quadratic line element sums over both index orders.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The <Lorentz transformation> is $t'=\gamma(t-vx)$, $x'=\gamma(x-vt)$ in units $c=1$. Adding and subtracting gives
$$
p'=\gamma(1+v)(t-x),\qquad q'=\gamma(1-v)(t+x).
$$
Since $\gamma(1+v)=\sqrt{(1+v)/(1-v)}$ and the other factor is its reciprocal,
$$
\boxed{p'=\alpha p,\qquad q'=q/\alpha,\qquad\alpha=\sqrt{\frac{1+v}{1-v}}=e^\eta.}
$$
Here $\eta$ is the boost <rapidity>. Their product is unchanged, as required by invariance of the <Minkowski interval>.

= iii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The <proper time> normalization of the astronaut's <four-velocity> gives $\dot t^2-\dot x^2=1$. In the <light-cone coordinates> this is
$$
\boxed{\dot p\dot q=1.}
$$
The <four-acceleration> has squared norm $\ddot t^2-\ddot x^2=-a^2$, because $a$ is the constant <proper acceleration>. The same coordinate change therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\ddot p\ddot q=-a^2.}
$$
Equivalently write $\dot q=e^\eta$, $\dot p=e^{-\eta}$. Their second derivatives give $\ddot p\ddot q=-\dot\eta^2$, so the <rapidity form of one-dimensional proper acceleration> makes $\dot\eta=\pm a$, with sign fixing the acceleration direction.

= iv
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

For the stated outward-accelerating hyperbola, take positive signed <proper acceleration> and an initially stationary spaceship. Then $\eta=a\tau$, so $\dot p=e^{-a\tau}$ and $\dot q=e^{a\tau}$. The initial values are $p(0)=-1/a$, $q(0)=1/a$. <Integration> gives
$$
\boxed{p(\tau)=-\frac1a e^{-a\tau},\qquad q(\tau)=\frac1a e^{a\tau}.}
$$
Taking their half-sum and half-difference yields the <hyperbolic motion>
$$
\boxed{t(\tau)=\frac1a\sinh(a\tau),\qquad x(\tau)=\frac1a\cosh(a\tau).}
$$
The printed position data alone do not specify the initial velocity. If the initial <rapidity> is an arbitrary $\eta_0$, the equally valid positive-acceleration solution is
$$
t(\tau)=\frac{\sinh(\eta_0+a\tau)-\sinh\eta_0}{a},\qquad
x(\tau)=\frac1a+\frac{\cosh(\eta_0+a\tau)-\cosh\eta_0}{a}.
$$
Thus the requested special expression implicitly selects $\eta_0=0$.

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=v}

Use the specified hyperbolic trajectory. The emitted right-moving <photon> follows $x=L+t-t_0$, so its retarded <null coordinate> is $p=t_0-L$. Interception at astronaut <proper time> $\tau$ requires
$$
t_0-L=-\frac1a e^{-a\tau}.
$$
The astronaut has velocity $v=\tanh(a\tau)$ relative to the star and $\gamma=\cosh(a\tau)$. The collinear <relativistic Doppler effect> gives
$$
\boxed{\nu_{\rm obs}=\nu_0\gamma(1-v)=\nu_0e^{-a\tau}=a\nu_0(L-t_0).}
$$
This is the <Doppler shift for a uniformly accelerated receiver>. A finite reception time exists precisely when $t_0<L$, so **the limiting emission time is $L$**, approached from below. At $t_0=L$ the photon lies on the <Rindler horizon> and never reaches the astronaut. If reception is restricted to $\tau\ge0$, the full emission range is $L-1/a\le t_0<L$; for emission after $t=0$, it is $0\le t_0<L$.
