= Electronegative
{synonym}

<Electronegativity> describes a <chemical atom>'s tendency to attract <electron density> towards itself in a <chemical bond>. It helps answer a useful question: when two different <chemical atoms> share <electrons>, which end of the <chemical bond> tends to be richer in <electron density>? For <hydrogen chloride>, the answer is <chlorine>: the <covalent bond> is a <polar covalent bond>, conventionally written $\mathrm H^{\delta+}-\mathrm{Cl}^{\delta-}$. The symbols indicate <partial atomic charges>, not isolated <ions> carrying whole elementary <electric charges>.<Chemistry 2e>[§7.2, “Pure vs. Polar Covalent Bonds”, HCl example]{ref}

<Electronegativity> is a comparison scheme, not the number of <electrons> on a <chemical atom>. A <partial atomic charge> depends on how the <electron density> is partitioned; <formal charge> instead follows an integer allocation in a <Lewis structure>. Neither is numerically equal to <electronegativity>. Nor is <electronegativity> identical to <electron affinity>, which concerns adding an <electron> to an isolated species. Different <electronegativity> scales use different measured inputs and conventions; compare numbers only after specifying the scale.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry/electronegativity>{ref}

For a concrete comparison, the Royal Society of Chemistry lists <Pauling electronegativities> $\chi_{\rm P}(\mathrm H)=2.20$ and $\chi_{\rm P}(\mathrm{Cl})=3.16$. Their difference is $0.96$, so the <electronegativity> rule predicts <bond polarization> towards <chlorine> in <hydrogen chloride>. An isolated <molecular hydrogen> <molecule> has identical ends and zero <electronegativity> difference. These examples explain a direction of unequal sharing, not a quantitative <partial atomic charge>.<Periodic Table by Royal Society of Chemistry/Hydrogen — Element information, properties and uses>{ref}<Periodic Table by Royal Society of Chemistry/Chlorine — Element information, properties and uses>{ref}

\Image[electronegativity-scales-hydrogen-chlorine.png]
{id=electronegativity-scales-hydrogen-chlorine}
{title=<Pauling electronegativity> and <Mulliken electronegativity> for <hydrogen> and <chlorine>: separate scales, separate units; the <Mulliken electronegativity> values are calculated from the stated ground-state inputs}

A larger <electronegativity> difference often suggests stronger <bond polarization>, but there is no universal numerical boundary where a <covalent bond> suddenly becomes <ionic bonding>. Tables of cutoffs are classification aids with exceptions. <Hydrogen fluoride>, for example, is described by a <polar covalent bond> even when its <electronegativity> difference exceeds a textbook's nominal ionic threshold. The actual <electron density>, physical setting, and <chemical substance>'s structure matter.<Chemistry 2e>[§7.2, “Electronegativity and Bond Type”, general-guide warning and HF exception]{ref}

The distinction from <ionization energy> is equally useful: an <ionization energy> measures the cost of removing an <electron> from a specified isolated species, whereas <electronegativity> compares attraction for <electron density> in a bonded environment. Neither the removal cost alone nor the attachment release alone supplies a unique bonded-atom <electronegativity>. A scale combines selected measured inputs with a model and a convention.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry/electronegativity>[E01990: multiple definitions; Mulliken average versus Pauling bond-energy differences]{ref}

For an original relative comparison, stipulate fictitious bonded sites $A,B,C$ on one chosen <dimensionless> <electronegativity> scale:
$$
\chi(A)=\chi_0,\qquad \chi(B)=\chi_0+\Delta\chi,\qquad
\chi(C)=\chi_0+2\Delta\chi,\qquad \Delta\chi>0.
$$
The <electronegativity> heuristic predicts <bond polarization> towards $B$ in $A-B$, towards $C$ in $B-C$, and towards $C$ in $A-C$. Shifting $\chi_0$ leaves every comparison unchanged. Although the $A-C$ difference is twice the $A-B$ difference, this does not make its <partial atomic charge> or <bond dipole> twice as large: those require additional information about <electron density> and <bond length>. These symbols describe a stipulated ordering, not a table of measured <chemical elements>.

Keep the chemical context attached to that ordering. A <chemical atom> in a <molecule> has neighbours and an <electron configuration>; an isolated-atom <energy> scale and a bonded-atom comparison are different constructions. The <chemical environment dependence of electronegativity> explains why a table is a useful approximation rather than an immutable property of every site. Furthermore, an applied <electric field> can induce <bond polarization> even between identical <chemical atoms>, through <electric polarizability>; equal tabulated <electronegativity> does not forbid that response.

A scale proportional to the average spectroscopic one-electron binding energy of a free ground-state atom’s <valence electrons>. For an s,p atom, $\chi=C(n_s\varepsilon_s+n_p\varepsilon_p)/(n_s+n_p)$, with positive binding energies and a scale constant $C$. It distinguishes s and p binding rather than assigning both one Slater charge.<Electronegativity is the average one-electron energy of the valence-shell electrons in ground-state free atoms by Leland C. = Pauling scale
{c}
{synonym}

<Pauling electronegativity> is a relative, <dimensionless> scale motivated by <bond-dissociation energies>. The interesting observation is that a <chemical bond> between unlike <chemical atoms> can be stronger than a simple baseline inferred from the corresponding like-atom <chemical bonds>. An empirical excess <energy> can then be associated with an <electronegativity> difference. The 1932 paper <The Nature of the Chemical Bond. IV. The Energy of Single Bonds and the Relative Electronegativity of Atoms> built such a map; its original coordinates used <hydrogen> as zero, so those coordinates should not be mistaken for the modern tabulated <Pauling electronegativities>.<The Nature of the Chemical Bond. IV. The Energy of Single Bonds and the Relative Electronegativity of Atoms by Linus Pauling>[pp. 3576–3578, equation (1), Table III: H 0.00, Cl 0.94]{ref}

One arithmetic-baseline version of the <Pauling scale> relation is
$$
\Delta_D=D_{AB}-\frac{D_{AA}+D_{BB}}{2},\qquad
\left|\chi_{\rm P}(A)-\chi_{\rm P}(B)\right|\approx\sqrt{\frac{\Delta_D}{1\,\mathrm{eV}}}.
$$
Here $D_{AB}$, $D_{AA}$ and $D_{BB}$ are consistently defined <bond-dissociation energies> per <molecule>, and the <arithmetic mean> is the chosen baseline. With molar <energies> instead, write $\sqrt{\Delta_D/(96.485\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}})}$, approximately $0.102\sqrt{\Delta_D/(1\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}})}$. The denominator makes the result <dimensionless>; the factor $0.102$ is not a unit-independent magic constant. This is an empirical relation with a specified baseline, not a definition valid for every <chemical bond>.<Energy Electronegativity and Chemical Bonding by Stepan S. Batsanov>[§1 equation (1), coefficients for eV, kcal/mol and kJ/mol; §2.2, dependence on other bond properties]{ref}

For an explicitly synthetic example, suppose $D_{AA}=D_{BB}=300\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ and $D_{AB}=396.485\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ with the same temperature and dissociation convention. Then $\Delta_D=96.485\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ and the model gives an <electronegativity> difference of $1$. Replacing $D_{AB}$ by $300\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ gives zero; a negative excess would make this real square-root model inapplicable, not produce a negative <electronegativity> difference. These invented inputs illustrate the formula and are not measurements of named <chemical substances>.

Only differences appear, so adding the same constant to every <Pauling electronegativity> leaves this relation unchanged. A reference value must fix the origin. The <bond-dissociation energies> alone also do not tell which endpoint attracts <electron density> more strongly, because interchanging $A$ and $B$ leaves the formula unchanged. Chemical comparisons fix that direction. Thus <Pauling electronegativity> is useful as a compact ordering, while the underlying measured <energies> and approximations remain essential.

= Pauling bond-energy excess
{c}
{parent=Pauling electronegativity}
{title2=$\Delta_D$}

A <Pauling bond-energy excess> subtracts a chosen like-atom baseline from the unlike-atom <bond-dissociation energy>. For an unequal-input arithmetic example, stipulate $D_{AA}=200$, $D_{BB}=450$ and $D_{AB}=421.485\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, all with one dissociation convention. The <arithmetic mean> baseline and excess are
$$
D_{\rm base}=\frac{200+450}{2}=325\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}},\qquad
\Delta_D=421.485-325=96.485\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}.
$$
The arithmetic-baseline <Pauling scale> model therefore gives $|\chi_{\rm P}(A)-\chi_{\rm P}(B)|=\sqrt{96.485/96.485}=1$. The <bond-dissociation energy> $421.485$ is not itself the excess: subtracting the baseline is essential. All three numbers are stipulated, not experimental values for named <chemical elements>.

\Image[pauling-bond-energy-excess-construction.png]
{id=image-pauling-bond-energy-excess-construction}
{title=Constructing a <Pauling bond-energy excess> from stipulated <bond-dissociation energies> and converting it to a <dimensionless> <electronegativity> difference}

Changing the baseline changes the model. For the same stipulated inputs, a <geometric mean> baseline would be $\sqrt{200\times450}=300\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, yielding an excess of $121.485\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ and a difference of approximately $1.122$. It is a different construction, even though both start from the same <bond-dissociation energies>. State which baseline is used before comparing <electronegativity> differences.

The arithmetic-baseline <Pauling scale> also has a useful inverse: a difference of $0.5$ corresponds to an excess of $24.12125\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, and a difference of $2$ corresponds to $385.94\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. Doubling the <electronegativity> difference quadruples the modeled excess. Interchanging the endpoint labels changes neither quantity; the <square root> alone cannot choose the direction of <bond polarization>.

In <The Nature of the Chemical Bond. IV. The Energy of Single Bonds and the Relative Electronegativity of Atoms> (printed pp. 3576–3578, Table II and equation (1)), <Linus Pauling> organized excess <energies> for <chemical bonds> into an approximately additive map of their <square roots>. He also identified a limitation: his H–F map separation of $2$ predicted an excess of $4.00\,\mathrm{eV}$, exceeding his table's value by $1.23\,\mathrm{eV}$. Those are historical values and a historical model discrepancy, not modern recommended inputs.<The Nature of the Chemical Bond. IV. The Energy of Single Bonds and the Relative Electronegativity of Atoms by Linus Pauling>[printed pp. 3576–3578, Table II, equation (1), and paragraph beginning “These coördinates”]{ref}

= Mulliken scale
{c}
{synonym}

<Mulliken electronegativity> uses both the cost of removing an <electron> and the <energy> released by accepting an <electron>. For specified isolated-atom states, the common ground-state convention is
$$
\chi_{\rm M}=\frac{I+A_{\rm ea}}{2},\qquad
I=E(X^+)-E(X),\qquad A_{\rm ea}=E(X)-E(X^-).
$$
The free <electron>'s <energy> is the zero reference. Here $I$ is the <ionization energy> and $A_{\rm ea}$ is the <electron affinity>, positive for exothermic attachment. If attachment is instead tabulated as the signed <energy> change $\Delta E_{\rm attach}=E(X^-)-E(X)$, the same expression is $(I-\Delta E_{\rm attach})/2$. Stating the sign convention prevents adding the wrong quantities. The 1934 original treated suitable valence states, which are not automatically the ground states used in this example.<A New Electroaffinity Scale; Together with Data on Valence States and on Valence Ionization Potentials and Electron Affinities by Robert S. Mulliken>{ref}

<Mulliken electronegativity> has <energy> units before any additional rescaling. With $I$ and $A_{\rm ea}$ in <electronvolts> per <chemical atom>, $\chi_{\rm M}$ is in <electronvolts>; with both in $\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, it is a molar <energy>. For <hydrogen>, the RSC ground-state inputs are $I=1312.05$ and $A_{\rm ea}=72.769\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, giving $\chi_{\rm M}=692.4095\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\approx7.176\,\mathrm{eV}$ per <chemical atom>. For <chlorine>, $I=1251.186$ and $A_{\rm ea}=348.575\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ give $799.8805\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\approx8.290\,\mathrm{eV}$. These are calculated ground-state <Mulliken electronegativities>, not the RSC's tabulated <Pauling electronegativities>.<Periodic Table by Royal Society of Chemistry/Hydrogen — Element information, properties and uses>{ref}<Periodic Table by Royal Society of Chemistry/Chlorine — Element information, properties and uses>{ref}

Both schemes place <chlorine> above <hydrogen> here, but their numerical differences cannot be subtracted from one another or equated. Dividing a molar <Mulliken electronegativity> by an <energy> conversion factor changes units; it does not transform it into a <Pauling electronegativity>. A further empirical mapping would require its own calibration and stated domain.

= Electron affinity
{parent=Mulliken electronegativity}
{wiki}
{title2=$A_{\rm ea}$}

<Electron affinity> measures how strongly an isolated neutral species binds an additional <electron>. In the spectroscopic convention used here, it is the positive <energy> required to detach that <electron> from a bound <anion>. With the free <electron> at rest assigned zero <energy>,
$$
A_{\rm ea}=E(X)+E(e^-)-E(X^-),\qquad
\Delta E_{\rm attach}=-A_{\rm ea}.
$$
The <quantum states> of $X$ and $X^-$ must be specified, usually their <ground states>. Positive $A_{\rm ea}$ means that attachment lowers the <energy>; a signed attachment <energy> is then negative. This convention agrees with the IUPAC definition and with the atomic tables used for <Mulliken electronegativity>.<IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology (Gold Book) by International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry/electron affinity>{ref}

For <chlorine>, a published <photodetachment threshold> is $A_{\rm ea}/(hc)=29138.59(22)\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, approximately $3.612725\,\mathrm{eV}$ per <chemical atom>. Here $h$ is the <Planck constant>, $c$ the <speed of light>, and the inverse-length unit expresses an <energy> through a spectroscopic <wavenumber>. The parentheses give the reported uncertainty in the last digits, not a range of possible sign conventions.<Isotope shift in the electron affinity of chlorine by U. Berzinsh and others>[§2.3, printed pp. 6–7: reported threshold 29138.59(22) inverse centimetres]{ref}

\Image[electron-affinity-attachment-detachment-levels.png]
{id=image-electron-affinity-attachment-detachment-levels}
{title=<Electron affinity> of <chlorine>: positive detachment <energy> and negative attachment <energy> connect the same isolated-species levels; a finite-temperature <electron attachment enthalpy> needs additional corrections}

An <electron affinity> is neither an <electronegativity> nor a <lattice energy>. The first describes a specified isolated-species <energy> difference, <electronegativity> compares attraction for <electron density> in a <chemical bond>, and <lattice energy> refers to an entire <ionic crystal> and separated gaseous <ions>. A stable <anion> can therefore help explain <ionic bonding> without describing the full <energy> balance for making a <salt>.

= Electron attachment enthalpy
{parent=Electron affinity}
{title2=$\Delta H_{\rm attach}^{\circ}(T)$}

An <electron attachment enthalpy> is the signed molar <enthalpy> change for a stated attachment reaction,
$$
X(g)+e^-(g)\longrightarrow X^-(g).
$$
For favourable attachment such as <chlorine>'s first attachment, the <electron attachment enthalpy> is negative. Its leading isolated-ground-state <energy> contribution is $-N_{\rm A}A_{\rm ea}$, where $N_{\rm A}$ is the <Avogadro constant>. At finite <temperature>, consistent thermal populations, translational contributions and the $pV$ term in <enthalpy> must also be included, together with the same gaseous-<electron> reference used in the corresponding <ionization energy> term. Consequently a measured spectroscopic <electron affinity> is not automatically an exact $298.15\,\mathrm K$ attachment <enthalpy> with a minus sign. A <Born-Haber cycle> must use compatible <enthalpies> throughout.

= Photodetachment
{parent=Electron affinity}

<Photodetachment> removes an <electron> from an <anion> using a <photon>. For <chlorine>, the reaction is $\mathrm{Cl}^-(g)+\gamma\longrightarrow\mathrm{Cl}(g)+e^-$. Measuring the minimum <photon> <energy> for the specified final <quantum state> gives a <photodetachment threshold> and hence an <electron affinity>.

= Photodetachment threshold
{parent=Photodetachment}
{title2=$E_{\rm th}$}

In the published <chlorine> experiment, negative <ions> were accelerated and mass-selected, then overlapped with a tunable <laser> beam. A downstream <electric field> deflected charged <ions>; neutral <chemical atoms> continued to a plate, whose secondary <electrons> were counted. Scanning the <laser> <frequency> produced an onset in this neutral-atom signal. The apparatus therefore inferred an <electron affinity> from a threshold, rather than measuring the <heat> of attachment.<Isotope shift in the electron affinity of chlorine by U. Berzinsh and others>[§2, printed pp. 2–4, Figure 1: ion preparation, deflectors, neutral-atom secondary-emission detector and threshold fitting]{ref}

Reversing the <laser> direction and averaging the two thresholds corrected the first-order <Doppler shift>. A <Fabry–Pérot interferometer> and reference spectral lines calibrated the <frequency>. Finite <laser> bandwidth, unresolved <quantum state> splitting and shifts of the calibration lines limited the inference; the authors included these effects in fitting and the error estimate. The level diagram shows only the extracted gap, not a measured signal curve.<Isotope shift in the electron affinity of chlorine by U. Berzinsh and others>[§§2.1 and 2.3, printed pp. 3–7, equation (2), Figures 2 and 5: direction reversal, calibration, bandwidth and reference-line systematic shifts]{ref}

= Experimental inputs to electronegativity scales
{parent=Electronegativity}

The contrast between <Pauling electronegativity> and <Mulliken electronegativity> becomes clearer by following the measurements into the formulas. A <Pauling bond-energy excess> compares three compatible <bond-dissociation energies>; <Mulliken electronegativity> takes the <arithmetic mean> of an <ionization energy> and an <electron affinity>. The former asks how much stronger an unlike-atom <chemical bond> is than a stipulated baseline; the latter combines two different isolated-species <energy> gaps.

Actual readouts precede those model choices. In the <nitrogen dissociation threshold from fragment images>, fitted squared image radii versus assigned excitation <energies> reveal a threshold after accounting for the product <quantum states>. In the <chlorine> <photodetachment threshold> experiment, mass-selected <anions> overlap a tunable <laser>, and neutral-atom counts reveal the onset of <electron> detachment. Opposite <laser> directions control the <Doppler shift>. That second threshold supplies an <electron affinity>, not an <electronegativity>; the <Mulliken scale> still needs an independent <ionization energy>.<Accurate measurements of the bond dissociation energies of 14N2, 14N15N and 15N2 by Liya Lu and others>[§1, recoil-energy/image-radius equation; §3.2, Figure 5, squared image sizes versus term energies; §3.2, threshold channel assignment]{ref}<Isotope shift in the electron affinity of chlorine by U. Berzinsh and others>[§2, printed pp. 2–4, Figure 1: mass selection and neutral counting; §2.1, direction reversal]{ref}

For an original arithmetic comparison, stipulate isolated-species $I=(8,7,6)\,\mathrm{eV}$ and positive attachment-release $A_{\rm ea}=(0.5,3,3)\,\mathrm{eV}$ for fictitious species $A,B,C$. Their <Mulliken electronegativities> are $(4.25,5,4.5)\,\mathrm{eV}$. The largest <ionization energy> belongs to $A$, while the largest <Mulliken electronegativity> belongs to $B$: accepting an <electron> matters as well as retaining one. Using signed attachment <energies> $(-0.5,-3,-3)\,\mathrm{eV}$ in $(I-\Delta E_{\rm attach})/2$ gives exactly the same results.

Independently stipulate $D_{RR}=300$ and $D_{XX}=(200,300,450)\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ for $X=A,B,C$, with unlike-atom values
$$
D_{RX}=\frac{D_{RR}+D_{XX}}{2}+96.485\,s_X^2\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}},\qquad
(s_A,s_B,s_C)=(0.5,1,1.5).
$$
Thus $D_{RX}=(274.12125,396.485,592.09125)\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, and the <Pauling scale> differences from $R$ are $(0.5,1,1.5)$. If all three fictitious species are placed above $R$ on that scale, their ordering is $A<B<C$, whereas the stipulated <Mulliken scale> gives $A<C<B$. The <bond-dissociation energies> and isolated-species <energies> were invented independently: this demonstrates what the formulas do, not a measured disagreement for actual <chemical elements>. A unit conversion cannot force these two input models to agree.

= Chemical environment dependence of electronegativity
{parent=Electronegativity}

A tabulated <electronegativity> is useful because it compresses many comparisons, but one number per <chemical element> suppresses chemical context. Changing the bonded partners, <bond length> or <electron configuration> changes the conditions under which a <chemical atom> attracts <electron density>. The <bond-dissociation energy> can change for reasons beyond <bond polarization>, so attributing every extra unit of <energy> solely to an <electronegativity> difference is an approximation.<Energy Electronegativity and Chemical Bonding by Stepan S. Batsanov>[§2.2, opening paragraph: bond distance and atomic valence affect bond energy]{ref}

An <oxidation state> labels a formal allocation of <electrons>; it is not a measured <partial atomic charge>. State-sensitive <electronegativity> models may give different values for different <oxidation states> or <electron configurations>, while a simple <chemical element> table intentionally provides a common comparison convention. Likewise, replacing an isolated <chemical atom> by a site in an <ionic crystal> introduces a surrounding <electric field> and many neighbours. Applying the same tabulated <electronegativity> without adjustment becomes an additional assumption.<Energy Electronegativity and Chemical Bonding by Stepan S. Batsanov>[§2.1, Table 1 and valence footnotes; §2.2, Table 2; §2.3, opening paragraph on cooperative effects in solids]{ref}

For the ground-state <Mulliken scale>, the state dependence is visible directly in the formula. Replacing $X$ by $X^+$ replaces $E(X^+)-E(X)$ with $E(X^{2+})-E(X^+)$ as the removal cost, and replaces $E(X)-E(X^-)$ with $E(X^+)-E(X)$ as the attachment release. These are different <ionization energies> and <electron affinities>. This isolated-<ion> arithmetic does not claim that a bonded <chemical atom> with the same formal <oxidation state> is an isolated <ion>. In a <molecule>, the actual <electron density> and <dipole evidence for bond polarization> supply complementary information about that environment.

= Dipole evidence for bond polarization
{parent=Electronegativity}

A permanent <electric dipole moment> gives evidence of separated positive and negative <electric charge> in an overall neutral <molecule>. This is a more direct probe of <chemical polarity> than an <electronegativity> table. For <hydrogen chloride>, NIST lists about $1.109$ <debye> for the lowest vibrational state and $1.093$ <debye> for the equilibrium value. They describe different averaging conventions, not two contradictory measurements. The NIST WebBook traces the vibrational value $1.1085$ <debye> to molecular-beam electric-resonance measurements in 1970.<HCl (Hydrogen chloride): experimental data by National Institute of Standards and Technology>{ref}<Hydrogen chloride: Constants of diatomic molecules by Klaus P. Huber and others>{ref}

How does <molecular beam electric resonance> reveal an <electric dipole moment>? A source and apertures form a beam of <molecules> in <vacuum>. A first nonuniform <electric field>, A, selects <quantum states> through their different <Stark effects>. In a central region, C, electrodes supply a nearly uniform <electric field> and applied radiation drives transitions at selected <frequencies>. A second nonuniform <electric field>, B, analyzes the outgoing <quantum states>; a detector measures the rate of arriving <molecules>. In a transmission-loss arrangement, resonant radiation transfers some <molecules> to a <quantum state> rejected by B, so the measured arrival rate dips. The observable is a change in detected <molecules> as the radiation <frequency> is scanned, rather than light absorption itself. This general apparatus description is documented in a later experimental thesis; it is not a reconstruction of the exact 1970 HCl instrument.<High Resolution Molecular Beam Study of Alkali Cyanides by Johannes Jacobus van Vaals>[§2, printed pp. 11–14: fields A, C and B, detected flux, parallel plates, radiation and detector]{ref}

\Image[bond-dipole-beam-resonance.png]
{id=bond-dipole-beam-resonance}
{title=<Molecular beam electric resonance>: schematic state selection and a synthetic <Stark effect> signal from a <rigid rotor> model; neither apparatus dimensions nor signal curves are measured HCl data}

The inference comes from how transition <frequencies> change with the known <electric field>. The interaction $-\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf E$ couples the <electric dipole moment> to the <electric field>; fitting the <quantum mechanics> of the relevant rotational states extracts its magnitude. A free rotating <molecule> need not show a first-order shift proportional to $|\mathbf E|$: the weak-field <Stark effect> can be quadratic. The diagram uses a simplified <rigid rotor>, with $B$ an <energy> constant, to illustrate this behavior. It omits the fine details of actual spectra.

Useful controls include scans with the radiation off to measure the baseline arrival rate, repeated scans at several calibrated <electric fields>, and agreement between independently assigned transitions. These are methodological checks, not claims about undocumented steps of the 1970 experiment. Field calibration and state assignment limit the inferred <electric dipole moment>. The published 1970 abstract confirms analysis of HCl and DCl beam-resonance spectra and combination with <infrared spectroscopy> intensities to obtain a distance-dependent <electric dipole moment>; its full apparatus and calibration record were unavailable here.<Dipole Moment and Hyperfine Parameters of H35Cl and D35Cl by E. W. Kaiser>[pp. 1686–1703, authored abstract; full text unavailable]{ref}

Even an accurately measured <electric dipole moment> is not an absolute <partial atomic charge>. Replacing a <hydrogen chloride> <molecule> by two point <electric charges> $\pm q$ separated by <bond length> $r$ gives $|\mu|=|q|r$. Using $1.109$ <debye> and $r=1.27455\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm m$ gives $|q|\approx0.181$ times the elementary <electric charge>. This is an effective two-point model, combining a vibrationally averaged <electric dipole moment> with an equilibrium <bond length>, not a unique partition of the actual <electron density> or a measured “18.1 percent ionic bond”.<Hydrogen chloride: Constants of diatomic molecules by Klaus P. Huber and others>{ref}

For a <molecule> with several <chemical bonds>, <molecular geometry> can cancel opposing contributions to the total <electric dipole moment>. <Carbon dioxide> illustrates why an overall <nonpolar molecule> need not have unpolarized individual <chemical bonds>. An <electronegativity> comparison helps anticipate local <bond polarization>; the measured molecular <electric dipole moment> tests the entire <electric charge> distribution.

= Molecular beam electric resonance
{parent=Dipole evidence for bond polarization}

The apparatus and dipole inference are described under <dipole evidence for bond polarization>. The <rigid rotor> supplies a general starting model for rotational <quantum states>; <rotational spectroscopy> measures their transition <frequencies>, while <molecular beam electric resonance> can select and analyze states through their <Stark effects>.

An actual implementation gives substance to the apparatus description above. In <Johannes Jacobus van Vaals>'s experiments, heated samples expanded with <argon> through a nozzle, then <molecular beam skimmers> formed the <molecular beam> in <vacuum>. Nonuniform <electric fields> selected and analyzed <quantum states>; parallel plates housed the <microwave> interaction. A <surface ionization detector> supplied <electric current> to an <electronic amplifier> and a <lock-in amplifier>. Resonant transfer into a rejected <quantum state> reduced the detected flux. <Phase locking> stabilized the <microwave> <frequencies>; an absorber suppressed reflections that could distort <spectral lines>. The thesis reports preliminary <electric dipole moment> components for gaseous <potassium cyanide> from measurements in nonzero <electric fields>. Such inference uses the <Stark effect>, rather than measuring <electronegativity>. The source notes restrictions from <electric-dipole selection rules> and <molecular beam> transit time; its preliminary dipole report is not a full calibration record. These are documented features of this apparatus, not proposed controls for the earlier <hydrogen chloride> experiment.<High Resolution Molecular Beam Study of Alkali Cyanides by Johannes Jacobus van Vaals>[chapter I §2, printed pp. 11–14: source, A/C/B fields, detector, phase locking, absorber and limitations; chapter III, printed p. 49, reference note 29: preliminary dipole components measured in nonzero fields]{ref}

= Bond polarization
{parent=Electronegativity}

<Bond polarization> is unequal sharing of <electron density> across a <chemical bond>. An <electronegativity> difference is a heuristic for its direction and tendency, not a unique <partial atomic charge>.

A simple two-site model makes the notation explicit. Represent each site by effective positive <electric charge> $+e$, and allocate expected <electron> populations $1-s$ to $A$ and $1+s$ to $B$, with $0\leq s<1$. Here $e$ is the <elementary charge>; these effective sites omit the internal structure of real <chemical atoms>. Subtraction gives
$$
q_A=e-e(1-s)=se,\qquad q_B=e-e(1+s)=-se,\qquad q_A+q_B=0.
$$
For the stipulated illustration $s=0.35$, the populations are $0.65$ and $1.35$, and the effective <partial atomic charges> are $+0.35e$ and $-0.35e$. Fractional expected populations do not split an individual <electron> into pieces. The total population remains two, and the pair remains neutral. No formula connecting $s$ to $\Delta\chi$ has been assumed; many <electron density> distributions and partitioning conventions can share one qualitative <electronegativity> ordering.

= Bond dipole
{parent=Bond polarization}
{title2=$\mathbf p_b$}

A <bond dipole> represents the separation of positive and negative <electric charge> associated with a <chemical bond>. In a neutral two-<point charge> model put $+q$ at $\mathbf r_A$ and $-q$ at $\mathbf r_B$, with $q>0$. Its physical <electric dipole moment> is
$$
\mathbf p_b=q\mathbf r_A-q\mathbf r_B=-q\mathbf d,\qquad
\mathbf d=\mathbf r_B-\mathbf r_A,\qquad |\mathbf p_b|=q|\mathbf d|.
$$
If $B$ is the negative end, the physical <electric dipole moment> points from $B$ to $A$. The customary crossed-tail chemical arrow points towards the negative, more <electronegative> end, from $A$ to $B$. It therefore has the opposite direction. State the convention before doing <vector addition>; use physical <electric dipole moments> in the interaction <potential energy> $U=-\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E$, where $\mathbf E$ is the <electric field>.<Chemistry 2e>[§7.6, “Molecular Polarity and Dipole Moment”, Figure 7.26: arrow towards the more electronegative end, with a plus at the tail]{ref}

For a fixed neutral <molecule>, its complete <electric dipole moment> follows from all <atomic nuclei> and the <electron density>, not just <point charges> assigned to sites. If $n(\mathbf r)$ is the expected number of <electrons> per unit <volume>, the corresponding <charge density> is $\rho_e(\mathbf r)=-en(\mathbf r)$; the nuclear terms contribute separately. Partitioning that distribution into <bond dipoles> is a model. <Lone pairs> and <electric polarization> within a <chemical atom> can contribute as well. A measured molecular <electric dipole moment> cannot uniquely recover every individual <bond dipole> or <partial atomic charge>.

= Molecular dipole cancellation
{parent=Bond dipole}
{title2=$\mathbf p_{\rm mol}=\sum_b\mathbf p_b$}

<Molecular dipole cancellation> occurs when nonzero contributions to a <molecule>'s <electric dipole moment> cancel under <vector addition>. In a <bond dipole> model, use $\mathbf p_{\rm mol}=\sum_b\mathbf p_b$; adding magnitudes instead discards the <molecular geometry>. A symmetric <linear molecule> with two equivalent opposed <bond dipoles> has $\mathbf p_1=p_0\widehat{\mathbf x}$ and $\mathbf p_2=-p_0\widehat{\mathbf x}$, hence zero total <electric dipole moment> even though each <chemical bond> is polarized. <Carbon dioxide> provides the familiar molecular example; <water>'s <bent molecular geometry> permits a nonzero total.<Chemistry 2e>[§7.6, Figure 7.27 and accompanying paragraphs: cancellation in carbon dioxide, noncancellation in water]{ref}

For an original geometry calculation, give a fictitious three-site model $AB_2$ a central effective <electric charge> $+2q$ at the origin and two terminal <electric charges> $-q$ at
$$
\mathbf r_1=r\bigl(-\sin(\theta/2),\cos(\theta/2)\bigr),\qquad
\mathbf r_2=r\bigl(\sin(\theta/2),\cos(\theta/2)\bigr),\qquad 0<\theta\leq\pi.
$$
Here $r$ is the stipulated <bond length>, $\theta$ the <angle> between the two connections, and $q>0$ is independent of any <electronegativity> scale. Equivalently, split the central $+2q$ into two colocated $+q$ contributions, one for each neutral <bond dipole>. With $p_0=qr$, the physical <electric dipole moments> point from the terminal sites towards the central site:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf p_1&=p_0\bigl(\sin(\theta/2),-\cos(\theta/2)\bigr),\\
\mathbf p_2&=p_0\bigl(-\sin(\theta/2),-\cos(\theta/2)\bigr),\\
\mathbf p_{\rm mol}&=\mathbf p_1+\mathbf p_2
=\bigl(0,-2p_0\cos(\theta/2)\bigr).
\end{aligned}
$$
The sideways components cancel; the components along the <angle bisector> add. Thus $|\mathbf p_{\rm mol}|=2p_0\cos(\theta/2)$. At $\theta=180^\circ$ the result is zero; at the stipulated $\theta=120^\circ$ it is exactly $p_0$. This is an angle example for a model, not the measured angle or <electric dipole moment> of <water>.

Opposition alone is insufficient: a <linear molecule> with unequal opposed <bond dipoles> of magnitudes $p_1$ and $p_2$ retains magnitude $|p_1-p_2|$. Conversely, symmetry can enforce exact cancellation beyond a particular <bond dipole> partition. If the complete <charge density> invariant under <point reflection> about the origin satisfies $\rho(\mathbf r)=\rho(-\mathbf r)$, contributions to $\int\mathbf r\rho(\mathbf r)\,d^3r$ cancel in pairs. That <proof> uses the whole <electric charge> distribution, including <lone pairs> and <atomic nuclei>. It explains why molecular <symmetry> is stronger evidence for zero permanent <electric dipole moment> than a rule based only on tabulated <electronegativity> differences.

A permanent molecular <electric dipole moment> is specified relative to the <molecule>'s orientation. An ensemble with random orientations can have zero mean laboratory <vector> while each <molecule> is polar. That orientational average differs from internal <molecular dipole cancellation>. The <Stark effect> and <molecular beam electric resonance> probe molecular <electric dipole moments> through state-dependent responses to an <electric field>.

= Polar covalent bond
{parent=Bond polarization}

A <polar covalent bond> shares <electrons> unequally, as in <hydrogen chloride>.

The word “polar” qualifies the sharing within a <covalent bond>; it does not require complete transfer of an <electron> or an isolated <cation>–<anion> pair. In $A^{\delta+}-B^{\delta-}$, the signs identify the relatively electron-poor and electron-rich ends, while $\delta$ leaves the amount unspecified. A neutral <Lewis structure> can give both <chemical atoms> zero <formal charge> and still correspond to a <polar covalent bond>: equal division of the <bond pair> is a formal allocation, whereas <bond polarization> concerns the actual <electron density>.

Local and molecular descriptions answer different questions. Calling one <chemical bond> a <polar covalent bond> does not establish that the whole <molecule> is a <polar molecule>. Combine the <bond dipoles> with the <molecular geometry>, then check for <molecular dipole cancellation>. This lets a short <electronegativity> comparison explain a local tendency without confusing it with the observable <electric dipole moment> of the whole <molecule>.
