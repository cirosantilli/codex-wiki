An <arithmetic> <logical formula> is $\Sigma_0$, or bounded, if all its <quantifiers> are bounded by arithmetical <first-order terms>. A $\Sigma_1$ <logical formula> has an initial block of <existential quantifiers> followed by a bounded matrix, up to the usual logical transformations. <Soundness of an arithmetic theory> at a given level means that every <first-order sentence> of that level proved by the <first-order theory> is true in the standard <natural numbers>. Here $\Sigma_0$-completeness means that every true <bounded arithmetic sentence> is provable.

A <first-order theory> is <one-consistent> if there is no <bounded arithmetic formula> $A(x)$ for which it proves $\exists xA(x)$ while proving $\neg A(\bar n)$ for every standard <natural number> $n$. This is a restricted form of <omega-consistency>. In particular <one-consistency> implies <first-order consistency>, since an inconsistent <first-order theory> proves every <first-order sentence> and its <negation>.

Suppose a $\Sigma_0$-complete <first-order theory> $S$ is <first-order consistent>. If it proved a false <bounded arithmetic sentence> $\theta$, the true <bounded arithmetic sentence> $\neg\theta$ would also be provable by completeness, contradicting <first-order consistency>. Thus $S$ is $\Sigma_0$-sound. Conversely $\Sigma_0$-soundness excludes inconsistency because an inconsistent <first-order theory> proves the false <bounded arithmetic sentence> $0=1$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Sigma_0\text{-complete}:\quad\operatorname{Con}(S)\iff\Sigma_0\text{-soundness}.}
$$
If $S$ is <one-consistent> but proved a false <existential sentence> $\exists xA(x)$ with bounded matrix, every standard instance $\neg A(\bar n)$ would be true and hence provable. This violates <one-consistency>. Thus it is $\Sigma_1$-sound. Conversely, $\Sigma_1$-soundness implies <first-order consistency> by the previous argument, since <bounded arithmetic sentences> are included at the existential level. If $S$ proved $\exists xA(x)$, <soundness of an arithmetic theory> would give a standard witness $n$ with $A(n)$ true. <Bounded completeness of an arithmetic theory> would then give $S\vdash A(\bar n)$, so <first-order consistency> prevents simultaneous <formal proofs> of all the negated instances. Hence
$$
\boxed{\Sigma_0\text{-complete}:\quad1\text{-consistency}\iff\Sigma_1\text{-soundness}.}
$$
Multiple existential <logical variables> can be treated as a finite <tuple> of witnesses; the same reasoning applies.

\boxed{S\text{ one-consistent}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad S\nvdash\neg G_S.}
$$
The two hypotheses play different roles: ordinary <first-order consistency> excludes the positive <Gödel sentence>, while the stronger hypothesis also excludes its <negation>.

Use the <Hilbert-Bernays-Löb derivability conditions> for an <arithmetical provability predicate>. Abbreviate $P_S(\ulcorner A\urcorner)$ by $P A$. The conditions needed below are
$$
S\vdash A\Longrightarrow S\vdash P A,\qquad
S\vdash P(A\to B)\to(P A\to P B),\qquad
S\vdash P A\to P P A.
$$
These are the usual theoremhood, distribution and positive-introspection conditions intended by a standard <arithmetical provability predicate>; arbitrary <predicate symbols> merely agreeing with provability externally need not satisfy them.

Since $S\vdash\neg Y$, it proves $Y\to G$. The first two conditions give $S\vdash P Y\to P G$. For the converse, the fixed-point equivalence gives $S\vdash G\to(P G\to Y)$. Applying theoremhood and distribution gives
$$
S\vdash P G\to P(P G\to Y).
$$
<Positive introspection> also gives $P G\to P P G$, and distribution combines these to yield $S\vdash P G\to P Y$. Thus $S\vdash P G\leftrightarrow P Y$. Combining with $G\leftrightarrow\neg P G$ proves
$$
\boxed{S\vdash G\leftrightarrow\neg P Y.}
$$
If $S$ proved $\neg P Y$, it would prove $G$. The theoremhood condition would then give $P G$, while the fixed-point equivalence gives $\neg P G$. Therefore <first-order consistency> implies
$$
\boxed{S\nvdash\neg P Y.}
$$

Under the arithmetical interpretation $\square A=P_S(\ulcorner A\urcorner)$, the Löb axiom says that a <formal proof> of the local reflection implication for $A$ already entails a <formal proof> of $A$:
$$
P_S(\ulcorner P_S(\ulcorner A\urcorner)\to A\urcorner)\to P_S(\ulcorner A\urcorner).
$$
It is a formalized constraint on self-verification of a <formal proof> system. Externally, <Löb's theorem> says that if the system itself proves its own local reflection implication for $A$, it proves $A$. Distribution reflects closure of <formal proofs> under implication, necessitation reflects that a theorem has an arithmetically recognizable <formal proof>, and <positive introspection> reflects <formal proof> verification inside <arithmetic>.

Taking $A$ to be a refutable <first-order sentence>, especially a <contradiction>, recovers the obstruction behind the second incompleteness theorem: a <first-order consistent> sufficiently strong system cannot prove the corresponding claim that no <formal proof> of that <first-order sentence> exists. This does not identify provability with truth, nor grant the unrestricted factivity principle $\square A\to A$ as a theorem of the system. The Löb axiom is the <characteristic> additional scheme of Gödel–Löb <provability logic>.

An $L$-structure has a nonempty underlying <set> $A$. It assigns to each <constant symbol> $c_k$ an element $c_k^{\mathcal A}\in A$, to each $n$-ary <function symbol> $f_j$ a <function> $A^n\to A$, and to each $n$-ary <predicate symbol> $P_i$ a relation contained in $A^n$. <Logical equality>, when included as <logical equality>, is interpreted as actual identity.

The arities belong to the specification of the <first-order language>. An assignment of <logical variables> to elements of $A$ then evaluates <first-order terms> recursively and determines satisfaction of <logical formulas> through the <logical connectives> and <quantifiers>. A <first-order sentence> has no <free variables>, so its truth in the <first-order structure> is independent of the assignment.

The <set> $\Sigma$ is <satisfiable> if there is one $L$-structure $\mathcal A$ satisfying every <first-order sentence> in $\Sigma$ simultaneously. It is finitely <satisfiable> if each finite <subset> $\Sigma_0\subseteq\Sigma$ has a <first-order model>; that <first-order model> is allowed to depend on the chosen finite <subset>.

Thus the two statements have different <quantifier orders>:
$$
\exists\mathcal A\ \forall\sigma\in\Sigma\ (\mathcal A\models\sigma),
\qquad
\forall\Sigma_0\subseteq_{\rm finite}\Sigma\ \exists\mathcal A\ (\mathcal A\models\Sigma_0).
$$
Satisfiability immediately implies finite satisfiability by using the same <first-order structure> for every finite <subset>. The converse is the substantive content of <compactness theorem>.

= Solution

The <compactness theorem> states that a <set> of <first-order sentences> has a <first-order model> if and only if every finite <subset> has a <first-order model>.

For the nontrivial direction suppose every finite <subset> of $\Sigma$ is <satisfiable>. If $\Sigma$ were inconsistent, a finite formal derivation of a <contradiction> would use only finitely many premises $\Sigma_0\subseteq\Sigma$. <Soundness of an arithmetic theory> would then exclude a <first-order model> of $\Sigma_0$, contradicting finite satisfiability. Therefore $\Sigma$ is <first-order consistent>. The completeness theorem, proved by adjoining witnesses and constructing a Henkin <term model>, supplies a <first-order model> of $\Sigma$.

The reverse direction is immediate by restriction of the list of required <first-order sentences>. The key reason <compactness theorem> follows from syntactic completeness is that **every <formal proof> uses only finitely many premises**, even when the whole <first-order theory> is infinite.

Expand the <ring> <first-order language> by a new constant $c$ and consider

\operatorname{Th}(\mathbb Z)\cup\{c\ne0\}\cup
\{\exists y\ (\bar n\cdot y=c):n=1,2,3,\ldots\}.

\boxed{R\equiv\mathbb Z,\qquad c^R\ne0,\qquad
(\forall n\in\mathbb N_{>0})\ R\models\exists y\ (\bar n y=c^R).}

Such an element cannot be an ordinary nonzero <integer>, since an <integer> of absolute value less than $n$ cannot be a nonzero multiple of $n$. The statement concerns each standard <integer> index separately; it does not assert <divisibility> by every internally nonzero element of $R$.

\boxed{|N|\le\max(|S|,|L|,\aleph_0).}
$$

Choose a default element $a_0\in M$. For every <logical formula> $\exists x\phi(x,\vec y)$ choose a <Skolem function> $F_\phi$ which returns a witness in $M$ when one exists, and returns $a_0$ otherwise. Begin with $S$, the interpretations of the <first-order language>'s constants, and $a_0$. Successively adjoin the values of all the original <functions> and these <Skolem functions> on finite <tuples> already chosen. Let $N$ be the <union> of the resulting <countable> sequence of closure stages.

There are at most $\max(|L|,\aleph_0)$ <logical formulas>, and each <function> has finite arity. With $\kappa=\max(|S|,|L|,\aleph_0)$, each closure stage has size at most $\kappa$, and so does their <countable> <union>. Closure under the original <functions> makes $N$ an $L$-substructure. For the exact-cardinality version, choose an initial <set> of $\kappa$ elements, enlarged to contain any prescribed <subset> of size at most $\kappa$. The construction gives an upper bound $\kappa$ while containment gives the matching lower bound.
