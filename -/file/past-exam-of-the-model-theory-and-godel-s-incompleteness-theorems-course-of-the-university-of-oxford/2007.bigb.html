<Bounded completeness of an arithmetic theory> means that every bounded <arithmetic> <first-order sentence> true in the standard <natural numbers> is provable in $S$. If $S$ is <first-order consistent> and proves a false <bounded arithmetic sentence> $B$, <bounded completeness of an arithmetic theory> also proves its true bounded <negation>, a <contradiction>. Conversely, if every bounded theorem is true, $S$ cannot be inconsistent, because inconsistency would prove the false <bounded arithmetic sentence> $0=1$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{Con}(S)\iff\Sigma_0\text{-soundness}.}
$$
If $S$ is <one-consistent> and proves $\exists xB(x)$ with bounded matrix, that <existential sentence> cannot be false: otherwise every $\neg B(\bar n)$ would be a true <bounded arithmetic sentence> and hence provable, contrary to <one-consistency>. Thus $S$ has <Sigma-1 soundness>.

Conversely, <Sigma-1 soundness> excludes inconsistency. If $S\vdash\exists xB(x)$, it supplies a standard witness $n$ with $B(n)$ true. <Bounded completeness of an arithmetic theory> proves $B(\bar n)$, so <first-order consistency> forbids a <formal proof> of $\neg B(\bar n)$. Hence there cannot simultaneously be refutations of all standard instances. Therefore
$$
\boxed{1\text{-consistency}\iff\Sigma_1\text{-soundness}.}
$$
Finite blocks of existential <logical variables> are treated as finite <tuples> of witnesses, with the same argument.

<Omega-consistency> forbids a <logical formula> $B(x)$ of any complexity for which $S\vdash\exists xB(x)$ and $S\vdash\neg B(\bar n)$ for every standard $n$. The difference from <one-consistency> is that the matrix is unrestricted. It implies <first-order consistency>.

For a complete <first-order consistent> classical <first-order theory>, theorem membership obeys the ordinary Boolean <truth tables>. For example, it proves a <conjunction> exactly when it proves both conjuncts; completeness and <first-order consistency> also make theoremhood of a <negation> the complement of theoremhood of the <first-order sentence>. <Omega-consistency> additionally gives the witness property
$$
S\vdash\exists xB(x)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
S\vdash B(\bar n)\text{ for some standard }n.
$$
The reverse implication is existential introduction. For the forward implication, if no standard instance were provable, completeness would give a <formal proof> of each negated instance, violating <omega-consistency>. This works also after replacing additional <free variables> by numerals.

Now induct on <logical formulas> with their remaining <free variables> replaced by standard numerals. At the atomic level, the equivalence between theoremhood and standard truth follows from <bounded completeness of an arithmetic theory> and bounded <soundness of an arithmetic theory> from (b). The Boolean steps use the truth-table facts just proved. The existential step uses the witness property and the <induction> hypothesis on its matrix; <universal quantification> follows by <negation>. Hence
$$
\boxed{S\vdash X\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathbb N\models X}
$$
for every <first-order sentence> $X$. Completeness ensures it decides each <first-order sentence>, and this equivalence ensures every decision is correct.

In the function-free setting permitted here, this means every element is the interpretation of a <constant symbol>. A <full set of first-order sentences> has the witness property in its own <first-order language>: whenever it proves an <existential sentence> $\exists x\phi(x)$, some <closed term> $t$ satisfies that the <set> proves $\phi(t)$. A <prime model> of a <complete theory> $T$ is a <first-order model> admitting an elementary <embedding of first-order structures> into every <first-order model> of $T$. Let $\mathcal A\models T$ be canonical and $\mathcal B\models T$. For each <closed term> $t$, define

e(t^{\mathcal A})=t^{\mathcal B}.

\mathcal A\models\phi(\vec t^{\mathcal A})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\mathcal B\models\phi(\vec t^{\mathcal B}).

Thus $e$ is elementary. Since $\mathcal B$ was arbitrary, **every such <canonical first-order structure> is prime**. This <formal proof> also handles closed-term generation when <function symbols> are present; under the constant-name convention one simply uses constants as the <first-order terms>.

A <first-order theory> is a <set> of <first-order sentences> in a fixed <first-order language>, often identified with its deductive closure. It is $\kappa$-categorical if it has a <first-order model> of <cardinality> $\kappa$ and all <first-order models> of that <cardinality> are <isomorphic> as <first-order structures> in that same <first-order language>. In particular, an <isomorphism> must preserve the named constants as well as the order.

A <first-order theory> is complete if it is <first-order consistent> and decides every <first-order sentence>: for each $\sigma$ it entails either $\sigma$ or $\neg\sigma$. Equivalently all its <first-order models> satisfy exactly the same <first-order sentences>. <First-order consistency> is included to exclude the vacuous <first-order theory> with no <first-order models>. The distinctions matter here: naming infinitely many ordered points changes <countable> categoricity without destroying completeness.

Use two expansions of the rational order. In the first interpret $c_n=n$, so the named sequence is unbounded above. In the second interpret $c_n=-1/n$, so the named sequence is strictly increasing but has upper bounds in the rational order. Both expansions are countably infinite <first-order models> of the specified <first-order theory>.

An <isomorphism> preserving each $c_n$ would preserve the existence of an element greater than all the named constants, since any such element in one <first-order structure> would map to such an element in the other. The first <first-order structure> has none and the second has, for instance, $0$. They cannot be <isomorphic>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{DLO}_\omega\text{ is not }\aleph_0\text{-categorical}.}
$$
The property of lying above all $c_n$ is an infinite <partial type> rather than a single <first-order sentence>. This is why its realization can distinguish the two <first-order models> without contradicting the completeness established in (iv).

In a <countable> <first-order model>, the finitely many named constants split the order into the open intervals
$$
(-\infty,c_1),\ (c_1,c_2),\ldots,(c_{n-1},c_n),\ (c_n,\infty),
$$
together with the $n$ named points. Each interval is a <countable> <dense linear order without endpoints> in its induced order. It is nonempty and infinite by density and the absence of endpoints.

Use Cantor's classification of <countable> <dense linear orders without endpoints>: each such interval is <isomorphic> to the rational order. It follows by a <back-and-forth method> extending finite increasing partial <bijections> alternately along enumerations of the two orders. Given two <first-order models> of $\mathrm{DLO}_n$, choose <isomorphisms> between their corresponding intervals and send each named point to its counterpart. Their <union> is an increasing <bijection> preserving all constants, hence an <isomorphism> in $L_n$.

There are <countable> <first-order models>, for example any finite increasing list in the rational order. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{DLO}_n\text{ is }\aleph_0\text{-categorical}.}
$$
For $n=0$, the same argument is just the classification theorem for the whole order.

Use the completeness of the <first-order theory> of <dense linear orders without endpoints> and its <quantifier elimination>. After naming finitely many increasing constants, <quantifier elimination> still holds in the expanded <first-order language>: treat these constants as fixed parameters when eliminating <quantifiers>. Every <quantifier-free sentence> is a Boolean combination of <logical equalities> and order comparisons of the named constants, all decided by their stated strict increasing order. Hence $\mathrm{DLO}_n$ is complete.

An alternative <formal proof> of this finite case uses the stated general theorem that a <first-order theory> in a <countable> <first-order language> with only infinite <first-order models> and a unique <countable> <first-order model> is complete: if <first-order models> disagreed on a <first-order sentence>, downward Löwenheim-Skolem would give disagreeing <countable> <first-order models>. Part (iii) supplies the uniqueness.

Every <first-order sentence> of $L_\omega$ mentions only finitely many <constant symbols>, hence belongs to some $L_n$. Its truth is decided by $\mathrm{DLO}_n$, and every <first-order model> of $\mathrm{DLO}_\omega$ has a reduct satisfying that finite <first-order theory>. Thus the larger <first-order theory> also decides every <first-order sentence>. The rational-order examples in (ii) establish <first-order consistency>. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{DLO}_n\text{ and }\mathrm{DLO}_\omega\text{ are complete}.}
$$
There is no <contradiction> with (ii): completeness is agreement on individual finite <first-order sentences>, while categoricity requires a global <isomorphism> respecting all constants simultaneously.

An $n$-type over a <first-order theory> $T$ is a <set> of <logical formulas> in the displayed $n$ <free variables> <first-order consistent> with $T$. A <complete type> chooses exactly one of $\phi$ and $\neg\phi$ for each such <logical formula>. Equivalently it is a <maximal consistent> such <set>. For parameter-free <partial types> the <logical formulas> use the original <first-order language>; <partial types> over parameters use additional names for those parameters.

A <partial type> $p(\vec x)$ is principal if some <logical formula> $\theta(\vec x)$ <first-order consistent> with $T$ implies every member of $p$ modulo $T$:
$$
T\models\forall\vec x\,(\theta(\vec x)\to\phi(\vec x))\qquad(\phi\in p).
$$
Such a $\theta$ is a <principal formula of a type> of the <partial type>. For a <complete type> it isolates that <partial type>, so <principal complete types> are exactly <isolated types>.

For a <tuple> $\vec a$ in a <first-order structure> $\mathcal A$, its parameter-free <partial type> is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{tp}^{\mathcal A}(\vec a)=
\{\phi(\vec x):\mathcal A\models\phi(\vec a)\}.}
$$
It is a <complete type> over $\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal A)$ because the <first-order structure> decides the truth of every <logical formula> at that <tuple>.

Let $\vec a\in A^n$, with $A$ finite, and put $T=\operatorname{Th}(\mathcal A)$. There are only finitely many competing <tuples> $\vec b\in A^n$. For each whose <complete type> differs from that of $\vec a$, choose a <logical formula> $\phi_{\vec b}$ true at $\vec a$ and false at $\vec b$. Such a <logical formula> exists by the inequality of their <complete types>, negating a distinguishing <logical formula> if necessary.

Let $\theta$ be the <conjunction> of this finite list, or <logical truth> if the list is empty. It is true at $\vec a$, and every <tuple> satisfying it has the same <partial type> as $\vec a$. For each $\psi\in\operatorname{tp}(\vec a)$, the <first-order sentence>
$$
\forall\vec x\,(\theta(\vec x)\to\psi(\vec x))
$$
is therefore true in $\mathcal A$ and belongs to $T$. The <logical formula> $\theta$ is <first-order consistent> with $T$ because it is realized at $\vec a$. It is consequently a <principal formula of a type> of the <partial type>, proving **every <partial type> realized in a finite <first-order structure> is principal**. Finiteness of the <first-order structure>, rather than finiteness of the <first-order language>, is what makes the <conjunction> finite.

Every automorphism preserves all <logical formulas> by <induction> on their construction, so an automorphism taking $\vec a$ to $\vec b$ necessarily gives equal <partial types>.

For the converse, first note that a finite <first-order structure> is atomic by (ii). Thus in both cases every realized finite-tuple <partial type> has an isolating <logical formula>. Suppose two finite <tuples> $\vec u,\vec v$ have the same <complete type>, and choose a new element $x$ to extend the first <tuple>. Let $\theta(\vec z,w)$ isolate the realized <partial type> of $(\vec u,x)$. Since
$$
\mathcal A\models\exists w\,\theta(\vec u,w),
$$
<logical equality> of <partial types> gives $\mathcal A\models\exists w\,\theta(\vec v,w)$. Choose a witness $y$. Isolation ensures $(\vec u,x)$ and $(\vec v,y)$ have the same <complete type>. In particular, if $x$ is distinct from the entries already used, their inequality <logical formulas> ensure $y$ is distinct from its corresponding entries. The resulting finite map is <injective> and partial elementary.

Start from $\vec a\mapsto\vec b$, which is well-defined even for repeated coordinates because their <logical equality> patterns agree. Enumerate the carrier and extend alternately to include the next missing element in the domain and the next missing element in the range. For a range step apply the same extension argument to the inverse finite map. The <union> is an elementary <bijection> from $A$ onto itself: every finite <tuple> and every <logical formula> involving it is handled at some stage. It is therefore an automorphism taking $\vec a$ to $\vec b$. In the finite case the construction terminates once all elements have been included.

Hence
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{tp}(\vec a)=\operatorname{tp}(\vec b)
\iff\exists\pi\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal A),\ \pi(\vec a)=\vec b.}
$$
Countability makes the back-and-forth <numeralwise enumeration in arithmetic> exhaust the entire <first-order structure>; it cannot simply be dropped.

Use the <first-order language> with one <equivalence relation> $E$. Let $\mathcal A$ have exactly two <equivalence classes>, one countably infinite and one uncountable, with no further <first-order structure>. Its <complete theory> says that there are exactly two classes and both are infinite.

This <first-order theory> has <quantifier elimination>. To eliminate one existential <logical variable> from a <conjunction> of <atomic formulas> and negated <atomic formulas>, a <logical equality> either fixes its value, or finitely many inequalities exclude finitely many points. Its required equivalence-class membership is determined by the finitely many existing classes represented among the parameters. A required unused class exists exactly when fewer than two classes have already been used. Once its class is possible, infinitude supplies a point avoiding the finite exclusions. These tests are quantifier-free <logical equality> and equivalence conditions on the parameters. <Disjunctive normal form> and <induction> eliminate all <quantifiers> and also show completeness.

Thus a finite <tuple>'s <complete type> is isolated by its finite equality-and-equivalence pattern. Every realized finite-tuple <partial type> is principal, so $\mathcal A$ is atomic. In particular all individual elements have the same <complete one-type>, isolated by $x=x$.

Choose $a$ in the <countable> class and $b$ in the uncountable class. Any automorphism taking $a$ to $b$ would give a <bijection> from their whole <equivalence classes>, impossible because their cardinalities differ. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathcal A\text{ is atomic but not homogeneous}.}
$$
Its uncountability is precisely what avoids the conclusion of (iii).

Here $\mathcal B\subseteq\mathcal A$ means a <substructure>, so the smaller carrier contains all named constants, is closed under every <function symbol>, and has each relation restricted from the larger <first-order structure>. <First-order term> evaluation on <tuples> from $B$ agrees in the two <first-order structures>. Consequently <atomic formulas> agree, and <induction> on Boolean connectives makes every <quantifier-free formula> agree as well.

Write an arbitrary <universal sentence> of $T$ as $\forall\vec x\,\theta(\vec x)$ with quantifier-free matrix. Every <tuple> from $B$ is also a <tuple> from $A$, so $\theta$ holds at that <tuple> in $\mathcal A$. Quantifier-free agreement transfers it to $\mathcal B$. Since the <tuple> was arbitrary, $\mathcal B$ satisfies the <universal sentence>. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathcal A\models T,\ \mathcal B\subseteq\mathcal A
\Longrightarrow\mathcal B\models T.}
$$
An arbitrary <subset> without the <substructure> operations and relation restrictions would not be the hypothesis used by this <formal proof>.

The <set> of closed-term values is nonempty because the <first-order language> has a constant. It contains the value of every constant. If $f$ is an $r$-ary <function symbol> and $t_1,\ldots,t_r$ are <closed terms>, then
$$
f^{\mathcal A}(t_1^{\mathcal A},\ldots,t_r^{\mathcal A})
=(f(t_1,\ldots,t_r))^{\mathcal A},
$$
which is another closed-term value. Hence the <set> is closed under every operation. Restrict the operations and relations of $\mathcal A$ to it; this defines a <substructure> $\mathcal B$ whose carrier is exactly the specified <set>.

Part (i) now applies to the universal <first-order theory>, giving
$$
\boxed{\mathcal B\subseteq\mathcal A,\qquad\mathcal B\models T.}
$$
It is the <substructure> generated by the <first-order language>'s constants, often called the <ground-term substructure>. The source's constant-symbol assumption ensures its nonemptiness, even when no other elements can be generated.

Adjoin to $T$ the <negations> of all closed-term instances of $\theta$. If the resulting <set> had a <first-order model> $\mathcal A$, its <ground-term substructure> $\mathcal B$ from (ii) would still <first-order model> $T$. The assumed existential consequence therefore supplies a <tuple> $\vec b$ in $\mathcal B$ satisfying $\theta$. Each $b_j$ is the value of a <closed term> $t_j$.

Quantifier-free agreement between a <substructure> and its ambient <first-order structure> gives
$$
\mathcal A\models\theta(t_1,\ldots,t_k),
$$
contradicting the adjoined <negation> of this very ground instance. Thus
$$
T\cup\{\neg\theta(t_1,\ldots,t_k):t_1,\ldots,t_k\text{ closed terms}\}
$$
is unsatisfiable. <Compactness theorem> gives a finite unsatisfiable <subset>. Its finitely many ground <negations> imply that, for some finite list of <first-order term> <tuples>,
$$
\boxed{T\models\bigvee_{i=1}^m\theta(t_{i1},\ldots,t_{ik}).}
$$
If the finite <subset> already shows $T$ itself inconsistent and supplies no ground <negations>, choose any one closed-term <tuple> using the available constant; then any <first-order sentence> is a consequence of $T$, so $m=1$ suffices. Otherwise the selected list is nonempty. This also explains why the conclusion contains a finite disjunction rather than necessarily one universal choice of a witness <first-order term>.
