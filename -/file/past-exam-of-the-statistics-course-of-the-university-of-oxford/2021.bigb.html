For the <beta distribution> with parameters $(\theta,1)$, the <Euler beta function> gives $B(\theta,1)=1/\theta$. Its <probability density function> is therefore $\theta x^{\theta-1}$ on $(0,1)$. Direct <integration> gives the <raw moments>
$$
\mathbb E_\theta X^r=\theta\int_0^1x^{\theta+r-1}\,dx=\frac{\theta}{\theta+r},\qquad r> -\theta.
$$
In particular, its <mean> is $\mu(\theta)=\theta/(\theta+1)$. Equating this to the <sample mean> and rearranging gives
$$
\theta=\bar X(1+\theta),\qquad
\boxed{T_1=\frac{\bar X}{1-\bar X}}.
$$
The observations lie strictly between zero and one with <probability> one, so $0<\bar X<1$ and this <method of moments (statistics)> <estimator> is well defined and positive. This is an instance of <estimation of a beta shape parameter>.

Using the <raw moments> from part (i), the <variance> of one observation is
$$
\operatorname{Var}_\theta(X)=\frac{\theta}{\theta+2}-\frac{\theta^2}{(\theta+1)^2}
=\frac{\theta}{(\theta+1)^2(\theta+2)}.
$$
The <central limit theorem> for the independent <statistical sample> gives
$$
\sqrt n(\bar X-\mu)\xrightarrow d
N\left(0,\frac{\theta}{(\theta+1)^2(\theta+2)}\right).
$$
For $g(x)=x/(1-x)$, its <derivative> at the <mean> is $g'(\mu)=(1-\mu)^{-2}=(\theta+1)^2$. The <delta method> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\sqrt n(T_1-\theta)\xrightarrow d
N\left(0,\frac{\theta(\theta+1)^2}{\theta+2}\right)}.
$$
Equivalently, the large-sample <normal approximation> is
$$
T_1\ \mathrel{\dot\sim}\ N\left(\theta,\frac{\theta(\theta+1)^2}{n(\theta+2)}\right),
\qquad v(T_1)=\frac{\theta(\theta+1)^2}{n(\theta+2)}.
$$
Here $v$ denotes the leading <asymptotic variance>, rather than an assertion about an exact finite-sample <variance>.

Write $A=-\sum_i\log X_i>0$, with $\log$ the <natural logarithm>. The <likelihood function> and <log-likelihood function> are
$$
L(\theta)=\theta^n\exp\{-(\theta-1)A\},\qquad
\ell(\theta)=n\log\theta-(\theta-1)A.
$$
The <score function> is $\ell'(\theta)=n/\theta-A$ and the second <derivative> is $\ell''(\theta)=-n/\theta^2<0$. Thus the <log-likelihood function> is strictly <concave>, its stationary point is the unique global maximum, and the <maximum-likelihood estimator> is
$$
\boxed{T_2=\widehat\theta=\frac nA=-\frac n{\sum_i\log X_i}}.
$$
The one-observation <Fisher information> is $I(\theta)=1/\theta^2$. The <support of a probability distribution> here is independent of $\theta>0$, so the regular <asymptotic normality of a maximum likelihood estimator> gives
$$
\boxed{\sqrt n(T_2-\theta)\xrightarrow dN(0,\theta^2)},\qquad
T_2\ \mathrel{\dot\sim}\ N(\theta,\theta^2/n),\qquad v(T_2)=\theta^2/n.
$$
There is also a direct check: $U_i=-\log X_i$ has an <exponential distribution> with rate $\theta$, since $\mathbb P(U_i>u)=\mathbb P(X_i<e^{-u})=e^{-\theta u}$ for $u>0$. Its <mean> and <variance> are $1/\theta$ and $1/\theta^2$. Apply the <central limit theorem> to $A/n$ and the <delta method> to $h(u)=1/u$ to obtain the same result.

The specified <asymptotic relative efficiency> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{v(T_2)}{v(T_1)}
=\frac{\theta(\theta+2)}{(\theta+1)^2}
=1-\frac1{(\theta+1)^2}}.
$$
For every fixed $\theta>0$ this lies strictly between zero and one. **Prefer $T_2$ on grounds of asymptotic precision**: it has the smaller leading <asymptotic variance> and attains the inverse <Fisher information>. Both are <consistent estimators>, by the <law of large numbers> and the <continuous mapping theorem> applied respectively to $\bar X$ and $A/n$. The efficiency approaches zero as $\theta\downarrow0$ and one as $\theta\to\infty$.

This preference is an asymptotic comparison. In particular, it does not assert that $T_2$ is an <unbiased estimator>: since $A$ has a <gamma distribution> of shape $n$ and rate $\theta$, $\mathbb E T_2=n\theta/(n-1)$ for $n>1$.

The <standard error> suggested by part (iii) is $\theta/\sqrt n$. Substituting the <consistent estimator> $T_2$ gives $T_2/\sqrt n$; <Slutsky's theorem> gives
$$
\frac{\sqrt n(T_2-\theta)}{T_2}\xrightarrow dN(0,1).
$$
Using the central <standard normal quantiles> $\pm1.96$, an approximate 95% <Wald confidence interval> is
$$
\boxed{\left[T_2\left(1-\frac{1.96}{\sqrt n}\right),\quad
T_2\left(1+\frac{1.96}{\sqrt n}\right)\right]\cap(0,\infty)}.
$$
Its <coverage probability> approaches 0.95 for each fixed $\theta>0$ as $n\to\infty$. The intersection enforces positivity and does not change whether the true positive <statistical parameter> is covered. The value 1.64 would give the wrong central two-sided <coverage probability>.

Put $A=-\sum_i\log x_i>0$, $B=-\sum_j\log y_j>0$, and $N=n+m$. The two independent <statistical samples> give the joint <likelihood function>
$$
L(\theta,\tau)=\theta^n\tau^m\exp\{-(\theta-1)A-(\tau-1)B\}.
$$
The unrestricted <maximum-likelihood estimators> are $\widehat\theta=n/A$ and $\widehat\tau=m/B$. Under the <null hypothesis>, the common <statistical parameter> $\eta$ has <log-likelihood function> $N\log\eta-(\eta-1)(A+B)$, maximized at $\widehat\eta=N/(A+B)$. With the <likelihood ratio> defined as the null maximum divided by the unrestricted maximum,
$$
\lambda=\frac{(N/(A+B))^Ne^{-N+A+B}}
{(n/A)^n(m/B)^me^{-N+A+B}}
=\frac{N^N}{n^nm^m}\frac{A^nB^m}{(A+B)^N}.
$$
The statistic in the paper is $T=A/(A+B)$, since both original sums of <natural logarithms> are negative. Hence
$$
\boxed{\lambda=c(n,m)T^n(1-T)^m,\qquad
c(n,m)=\frac{(n+m)^{n+m}}{n^nm^m}}.
$$
It has maximum one at $T=n/N$, corresponding to equal fitted rates. This is the <likelihood-ratio test for equality of beta shape parameters>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}
{c}

A <likelihood-ratio test> rejects for small $\lambda$. The <derivative> of the <natural logarithm> of its variable factor is
$$
\frac d{dt}\{n\log t+m\log(1-t)\}
=\frac{n-(n+m)t}{t(1-t)}.
$$
Thus $t^n(1-t)^m$ increases up to $n/(n+m)$ and decreases thereafter, tending to zero at both endpoints. Every nontrivial lower <level set> of $\lambda$ therefore consists of two tails. For a prescribed <test size> $0<\alpha<1$, the exact <critical region> is
$$
\boxed{T<k_1\quad\text{or}\quad T>k_2},\qquad
0<k_1<\frac n{n+m}<k_2<1.
$$
Writing $F_{n,m}$ for the <cumulative distribution function> of the <beta distribution> with parameters $(n,m)$, the required two equations are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
k_1^n(1-k_1)^m&=k_2^n(1-k_2)^m,\\
F_{n,m}(k_1)+1-F_{n,m}(k_2)&=\alpha.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The first makes both cutoffs have the same <likelihood ratio>; the second calibrates the <null hypothesis> rejection <probability>. Equivalently, the second left-hand side is
$$
\frac1{B(n,m)}\left\{\int_0^{k_1}t^{n-1}(1-t)^{m-1}\,dt+
\int_{k_2}^1t^{n-1}(1-t)^{m-1}\,dt\right\}.
$$
As the common <likelihood ratio> threshold increases from zero to one, this <probability> increases continuously and strictly from zero to one; hence the equations determine unique cutoffs. **The two tail probabilities need not each equal $\alpha/2$** when $n\ne m$. Exact calibration holds for every common rate because the given <null distribution> of $T$ is independent of that rate. Boundary equalities have zero <probability>.

**True, with the test's rejection rule fixed.** If its <rejection region> in the space of the <test statistic> is $C$, its <statistical power> against the specified <simple hypothesis> $H_1$ is

Let $S=\sum_iX_i$, which has a <binomial distribution> with parameters $(n,p)$. For $0<p_1<1/2$, the alternative-to-null <likelihood ratio> of the independent <Bernoulli trials> is
$$
R(S)=\frac{p_1^S(1-p_1)^{n-S}}{(1/2)^n}
=[2(1-p_1)]^n\left(\frac{p_1}{1-p_1}\right)^S.
$$
Since $p_1/(1-p_1)<1$, this is strictly decreasing in $S$. A threshold on large $R$ is therefore a lower-tail <likelihood-ratio test>, and a threshold between $R(2)$ and $R(1)$ gives the stated <critical region> $S<2$. The <Neyman-Pearson lemma> also proves it is a <most powerful test> at its attained <test size>.

Using the <binomial probability mass>, its <test size> and <statistical power> are respectively
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P_{1/2}(S<2)=\frac{1+n}{2^n}},\qquad
\boxed{\mathbb P_{p_1}(S<2)=(1-p_1)^n+np_1(1-p_1)^{n-1}}.
$$
The associated <power function> is $\pi(p)=(1-p)^n+np(1-p)^{n-1}$. These formulas hold for positive integer $n$; when $n=1$ the test always rejects and has size one. If the boundary value $p_1=0$ is admitted, all alternative <probability> is at $S=0$; the same test still has <statistical power> one and can be represented using a threshold with ties in the <likelihood ratio>.

For any fixed $p>1/4$, the alternative-to-null <likelihood ratio> is

$$
\boxed{\text{reject }H_0\text{ exactly when }S\geq4}

is a <uniformly most powerful test> of the required exact <test size>. Its <power function> is $5p^4(1-p)+p^5=5p^4-4p^5$. At $p=1$ its <statistical power> is one, so including that boundary alternative preserves the conclusion.

Condition on the total of 400 complaints. Assume each complaint independently has the same <probability> $p$ of being received on Monday; in particular, repeated complaints from one incident must not create material dependence. The Monday count $S$ then has a <binomial distribution> with parameters $(400,p)$. Test $H_0:p=0.2$ against $H_1:p>0.2$, rejecting for large $S$ as suggested by the <monotone likelihood ratio>.

Under the <null hypothesis>, $\mathbb E_0S=80$ and $\operatorname{Var}_0(S)=400(0.2)(0.8)=64$. Both expected successes and expected failures are large, so the <central limit theorem> gives a suitable <normal approximation>:
$$
Z=\frac{S-80}{8}\ \mathrel{\dot\sim}\ N(0,1),\qquad
\boxed{z_{\rm obs}=\frac{100-80}{8}=2.5}.
$$
Using the paper's supplied rounded table, the upper-tail <p-value> is approximately
$$
\boxed{p_{\rm approx}=1-\Phi(2.5)\approx0.005}.
$$
A more accurate evaluation of the <standard normal distribution> <cumulative distribution function> gives about 0.0062. For example, choose <significance level> 0.05 before examining the data; the supplied upper 5% <standard normal quantile> is approximately 1.6, and $2.5>1.6$. **Reject $H_0$ at 5%, and also at 1%, using this approximation.** The observed <binomial proportion> is $100/400=0.25$, providing evidence that the Monday <probability> exceeds 0.2 under the sampling assumptions.

If applying a <continuity correction>, approximate $\mathbb P_0(S\geq100)$ by $1-\Phi((99.5-80)/8)=1-\Phi(2.4375)\approx0.0074$; this gives the same 5% and 1% conclusions. The uncorrected value 0.005 is approximate, so a decision exactly at the 0.5% boundary is not warranted by the supplied rounded table.

Here the <gamma distribution> uses a rate <statistical parameter> $\theta$, with known shape $\lambda$. Write $S=\sum_iX_i$ and $a=n\lambda$. For each fixed $\theta_1>\theta_0$, the alternative-to-null <likelihood ratio> is
$$
\frac{L(\theta_1)}{L(\theta_0)}
=\left(\frac{\theta_1}{\theta_0}\right)^a
\exp\{-(\theta_1-\theta_0)S\},
$$
strictly decreasing in $S$. The <Neyman-Pearson lemma> therefore gives the same lower-tail <critical region> $S<c_\alpha$ for every larger rate. Since $S$ has a continuous <gamma distribution> under the <null hypothesis>, there is a unique positive $c_\alpha$ satisfying
$$
\mathbb P_{\theta_0}(S<c_\alpha)=\alpha.
$$
Thus **reject when $S<c_\alpha$** is a <uniformly most powerful test> of exact <test size> $\alpha$. If $G_a$ denotes the unit-rate <gamma distribution> <cumulative distribution function> with shape $a$, then
$$
c_\alpha=\frac{G_a^{-1}(\alpha)}{\theta_0},\qquad
\pi(\theta)=G_a(\theta c_\alpha).
$$
This is the <uniformly most powerful test for a gamma rate>.

When $\lambda=1/n$, the sum has shape one and hence an <exponential distribution> with rate $\theta$. Therefore
$$
1-e^{-\theta_0c_\alpha}=\alpha,\qquad
\boxed{c_\alpha=-\frac{\log(1-\alpha)}{\theta_0}},\qquad
\boxed{\pi(\theta)=1-(1-\alpha)^{\theta/\theta_0}}.
$$
In particular $\pi(\theta_0)=\alpha$, and the <power function> is strictly increasing, exceeding $\alpha$ for $\theta>\theta_0$ and tending to one as $\theta\to\infty$.

Set $S=\sum_i x_i$ and $N=nm$. Conditional <independence> of the <binomial distribution> observations gives the <likelihood function>
$$
L(\theta)=\left\{\prod_{i=1}^n\binom m{x_i}\right\}
\theta^S(1-\theta)^{N-S}.
$$
The <prior distribution> is the <beta distribution> with parameters $(1,\beta)$. By <Bayes' theorem>, multiplying the <prior density> by the <likelihood function> gives a <posterior density> proportional to
$$
\theta^S(1-\theta)^{N-S+\beta-1}.
$$
Recognizing and normalizing this <beta distribution> gives
$$
\boxed{\theta\mid\boldsymbol x\sim\operatorname{Beta}(S+1,N-S+\beta)},\qquad
\boxed{\pi(\theta\mid\boldsymbol x)=
\frac{\theta^S(1-\theta)^{N-S+\beta-1}}{B(S+1,N-S+\beta)}}
$$
for $0<\theta<1$. This is <beta-binomial conjugacy>; the original binomial coefficients cancel in the normalization because they are independent of $\theta$.

The <prior mean>, <posterior mean>, and <maximum-likelihood estimate> are
$$
\widetilde\theta_0=\frac1{\beta+1},\qquad

The <prior mean> $1/(\beta+1)$ decreases as $\beta$ increases. More strongly, its <cumulative distribution function> and upper-tail <probability> are
$$
F_\beta(t)=1-(1-t)^\beta,\qquad
\mathbb P_\beta(\theta>t)=(1-t)^\beta,\qquad 0<t<1.
$$
For every fixed $t$, that upper-tail <probability> decreases with $\beta$. The statistician increasingly favors smaller values of $\theta$; as $\beta\to\infty$, the <prior distribution> concentrates near zero. For $\beta>1$, its <variance> $\beta/[(\beta+1)^2(\beta+2)]$ also decreases. This is <concentration of a beta prior near zero>.

With the data fixed, the <derivatives> are
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\lambda}{d\beta}=\frac{N}{(N+\beta+1)^2}>0},\qquad
\boxed{\frac{d\widetilde\theta_1}{d\beta}
=-\frac{S+1}{(N+\beta+1)^2}<0}.
$$
Thus **the prior weight increases and the posterior mean decreases**. Their limits are $\lambda\to1$ and $\widetilde\theta_1\to0$. Both the changing <prior mean> and the increasing weight contribute to the change in the <posterior mean>.

Let $S_{xx}=\sum_i x_i^2$, $S_{xy}=\sum_i x_iy_i$, and $\bar y=n^{-1}\sum_i y_i$. The independent observations in the <normal linear model> give
$$
L(\alpha,\beta)\propto
\exp\left\{-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(y_i-\alpha-\beta x_i)^2\right\}.
$$
The centered design condition $\sum_i x_i=0$ removes the $\alpha\beta$ term:
$$
\sum_i(y_i-\alpha-\beta x_i)^2
=\sum_i y_i^2+n\alpha^2+S_{xx}\beta^2
-2n\bar y\alpha-2S_{xy}\beta.
$$
The stated <prior density> factors into independent <normal distributions> for the <regression intercept> $\alpha$ and <regression coefficient> $\beta$. Multiplication by the <likelihood function> therefore leaves separate quadratic expressions in the two coefficients. Define the positive posterior <variances>
$$
v_\alpha=\left(\frac n{\sigma^2}+\frac1{\tau_0^2}\right)^{-1},\qquad
v_\beta=\left(\frac{S_{xx}}{\sigma^2}+\frac1{\tau_1^2}\right)^{-1},
$$
and the <posterior means>
$$
m_\alpha=v_\alpha\left(\frac{n\bar y}{\sigma^2}+\frac{\alpha_0}{\tau_0^2}\right),\qquad
m_\beta=v_\beta\left(\frac{S_{xy}}{\sigma^2}+\frac{\beta_0}{\tau_1^2}\right).
$$
By <completing the square>, the joint <posterior density> is
$$
\boxed{\pi(\alpha,\beta\mid\boldsymbol y)
=\frac1{2\pi\sqrt{v_\alpha v_\beta}}
\exp\left\{-\frac{(\alpha-m_\alpha)^2}{2v_\alpha}
-\frac{(\beta-m_\beta)^2}{2v_\beta}\right\}}.
$$
Equivalently,
$$
\boxed{\alpha\mid\boldsymbol y\sim N(m_\alpha,v_\alpha),\qquad
\beta\mid\boldsymbol y\sim N(m_\beta,v_\beta),\qquad
\alpha\perp\beta\mid\boldsymbol y}.
$$
This is <Gaussian conjugacy for a normal linear model>. Each posterior <precision parameter> is the sum of its data and prior <precision parameters>. The formulas remain valid when $S_{xx}=0$: all $x_i$ are then zero, so the <posterior distribution> of $\beta$ equals its <prior distribution>.

An unknown <variance> requires a joint <prior distribution> for $(\alpha,\beta,\sigma^2)$. **The analysis is not determined until a prior for the unknown variance, and its relation to the coefficients, is specified.** A <maximum-likelihood estimate> substituted for $\sigma^2$ does not by itself account for its posterior uncertainty.

For example, retain the independent coefficient <prior distributions> from part (b) and independently assign $s=\sigma^2$ an <inverse-gamma distribution> with shape $a_0>0$ and scale $b_0>0$, whose <probability density function> is proportional to $s^{-a_0-1}e^{-b_0/s}$. Then the joint <posterior density> is proportional to
$$
s^{-a_0-1-n/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{b_0+\frac12\sum_i(y_i-\alpha-\beta x_i)^2}{s}
-\frac{(\alpha-\alpha_0)^2}{2\tau_0^2}
-\frac{(\beta-\beta_0)^2}{2\tau_1^2}\right\}.
$$
Conditional on $s$, the two coefficient <posterior distributions> are still the independent <normal distributions> in part (b), with $s$ substituted for $\sigma^2$. The other <full conditional distribution> is
$$
s\mid\alpha,\beta,\boldsymbol y\sim
\operatorname{InvGamma}\left(a_0+\frac n2,
b_0+\frac12\sum_i(y_i-\alpha-\beta x_i)^2\right).
$$
The marginal coefficient <posterior distribution> requires <marginalization> over $s$; it is generally nonnormal and the two coefficients need not remain independent. In particular, the posterior <cumulative distribution function> of the requested <mean> becomes
$$
\mathbb P(M_0\leq t\mid\boldsymbol y)
=\int_0^\infty
\Phi\left(\frac{t-m_\alpha(s)-x_0m_\beta(s)}
{\sqrt{v_\alpha(s)+x_0^2v_\beta(s)}}\right)
\pi(s\mid\boldsymbol y)\,ds.
$$
Its shortest 95% <credible interval> must be found from this marginal <posterior distribution>, for example by numerical <integration> or posterior simulation. This is <normal regression with independent coefficient and variance priors>.

A different, convenient choice is a <normal-inverse-gamma prior>, in which the conditional coefficient prior <covariance matrix> is proportional to $\sigma^2$. That choice gives a multivariate <Student's t-distribution> after <marginalization>, so the interval uses its univariate <Student's t-distribution> <quantiles>. It changes the coefficient prior specification; a Student interval does not follow automatically from retaining the fixed-$\tau_0,\tau_1$ priors in part (b).
