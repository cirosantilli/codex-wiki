For the <independent> <statistical sample>, write the <likelihood function> and <log-likelihood function> as
$$
L_n(\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^nf(X_i;\theta),\qquad
\ell_n(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^n\log f(X_i;\theta).
$$
The expected <Fisher information> in the whole <statistical sample> is
$$
\boxed{I_n(\theta)=\mathbb E_\theta\!\left[\left\{\frac{\partial\ell_n(\theta)}{\partial\theta}\right\}^2\right]
=nI_1(\theta),\qquad
I_1(\theta)=\int\left\{\partial_\theta\log f(x;\theta)\right\}^2 f(x;\theta)\,dx.}
$$
Under the regularity conditions that permit <differentiation under the integral sign>, the <score function> has <expected value> zero and
$$
I_n(\theta)=-\mathbb E_\theta\ell_n''(\theta).
$$
For an interior <statistical parameter>, assuming <identifiability> and $0<I_1(\theta)<\infty$, the regular <asymptotic normality of a maximum likelihood estimator> states
$$
\boxed{\sqrt n(\widehat\theta_n-\theta)\xrightarrow dN\!\left(0,I_1(\theta)^{-1}\right).}
$$
Thus the large-sample <normal approximation> is $\widehat\theta_n\mathrel{\dot\sim}N(\theta,I_n(\theta)^{-1})$. The inverse information is the leading sampling <variance> approximation; the displayed convergence specifies its precise asymptotic meaning.

For $0<\sigma^2<\infty$, the <central limit theorem> gives
$$
\sqrt n(\overline X-\mu)\xrightarrow dN(0,\sigma^2).
$$
If $g$ is differentiable at $\mu$, the <delta method> applies to the first-order expansion
$$
g(\overline X)-g(\mu)=g'(\mu)(\overline X-\mu)+o_p(n^{-1/2}),
$$
where the remainder notation means that its product with $\sqrt n$ tends to zero in <probability>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\sqrt n\{g(\overline X)-g(\mu)\}\xrightarrow dN\!\left(0,\sigma^2[g'(\mu)]^2\right),\qquad
g(\overline X)\mathrel{\dot\sim}N\!\left(g(\mu),\frac{\sigma^2[g'(\mu)]^2}{n}\right).}
$$
The useful nondegenerate <normal approximation> requires $g'(\mu)\ne0$. When $g'(\mu)=0$, the first-order limit is zero and a higher-order expansion may be needed.

For a <Bernoulli distribution> with fixed $0<p<1$, the <mean> and <variance> are $p$ and $p(1-p)$. The <odds> transformation $g(x)=x/(1-x)$ has <derivative> $g'(x)=(1-x)^{-2}$. Applying the <delta method> from part (ii),
$$
\boxed{\frac{\overline X}{1-\overline X}\mathrel{\dot\sim}
N\!\left(\frac p{1-p},\frac{p}{n(1-p)^3}\right).}
$$
More precisely, $\sqrt n\{\overline X/(1-\overline X)-p/(1-p)\}$ converges to a <normal distribution> of <mean> zero and <variance> $p/(1-p)^3$, after defining the statistic arbitrarily on $\overline X=1$. That exceptional <event> has <probability> $p^n\to0$, so this extension does not change the limit. The unextended ratio is undefined there and has no finite exact <variance> if interpreted as infinite. At $p=0$ it is zero almost surely; at $p=1$ the denominator is always zero. This is the <delta method for bernoulli odds>.

Put $A=\sum_i x_i$, $B=\sum_i x_i^2$ and $M_2=B/n$. The <normal distribution> model gives
$$
L(\mu)=(2\pi)^{-n/2}\mu^{-n}\exp\!\left\{-\frac{B-2\mu A+n\mu^2}{2\mu^2}\right\},\qquad\mu>0,
$$
and, up to a constant independent of $\mu$, its <log-likelihood function> is
$$
\ell(\mu)=-n\log\mu-\frac{B}{2\mu^2}+\frac A\mu.
$$
The <score function> and its zero equation are
$$
\ell'(\mu)=\frac{B-A\mu-n\mu^2}{\mu^3},\qquad
n\mu^2+A\mu-B=0.
$$
For $B>0$, this <quadratic equation> has exactly one positive root, giving the <maximum-likelihood estimator>
$$
\boxed{\widehat\mu=\frac{-A+\sqrt{A^2+4nB}}{2n}
=\frac{-\overline X+\sqrt{\overline X^{\,2}+4M_2}}2.}
$$
The numerator $B-A\mu-n\mu^2$ of the <score function> is positive between its negative and positive roots and negative above its positive root. Thus the <likelihood function> increases up to $\widehat\mu$ and decreases afterwards: this is its unique global maximum on $(0,\infty)$. Also, $\ell(\mu)\to-\infty$ at both ends. The all-zero observation vector has <probability> zero; on that exceptional vector $L(\mu)\propto\mu^{-n}$ and there is no attained maximum.

The second <derivative> and the relevant <moments> are
$$
\ell''(\mu)=\frac n{\mu^2}-\frac{3B}{\mu^4}+\frac{2A}{\mu^3},\qquad
\mathbb E_\mu A=n\mu,\qquad
\mathbb E_\mu B=n(\mu^2+\mu^2)=2n\mu^2.
$$
Therefore the expected <Fisher information> is
$$
I_n(\mu)=-\mathbb E_\mu\ell''(\mu)=\frac{3n}{\mu^2}.
$$
The <support of a probability distribution> is the whole <real line>, independent of the positive <statistical parameter>. The <law of large numbers> gives $\overline X\to\mu$ and $M_2\to2\mu^2$ in <probability>, so the displayed <maximum-likelihood estimator> is a <consistent estimator>. The regular <asymptotic normality of a maximum likelihood estimator> now yields
$$
\boxed{\sqrt n(\widehat\mu-\mu)\xrightarrow dN(0,\mu^2/3),\qquad
\widehat\mu\mathrel{\dot\sim}N\!\left(\mu,\frac{\mu^2}{3n}\right).}
$$
Thus the requested leading <asymptotic variance> on the unscaled estimator's scale is $\mu^2/(3n)$. The <normal mean proportional to standard deviation model> provides a reusable version of this calculation.

Let $z=z_{1-\alpha/2}$ be the $(1-\alpha/2)$ <standard normal quantile>. Since $\widehat\mu$ is a <consistent estimator>, <Slutsky's theorem> gives
$$
\frac{\sqrt{3n}(\widehat\mu-\mu)}{\widehat\mu}\xrightarrow dN(0,1).
$$
Its estimated <standard error> is $\widehat\mu/\sqrt{3n}$, giving the approximate $(1-\alpha)$ <Wald confidence interval>
$$
\boxed{\left[\widehat\mu\left(1-\frac z{\sqrt{3n}}\right),\quad
\widehat\mu\left(1+\frac z{\sqrt{3n}}\right)\right]\cap(0,\infty).}
$$
For each fixed $\mu>0$, this procedure's <coverage probability> tends to $1-\alpha$. Intersecting with the positive parameter space preserves coverage of the true positive value.

Write $S^2=Q/(n-1)$ and $S=\sqrt{S^2}$, with $n\geq2$. Two exact facts about a <normal sample> are
$$
\overline X\sim N(\mu,\mu^2/n),\qquad
\frac{(n-1)S^2}{\mu^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1},
$$
and <independence of the normal sample mean and sample variance> holds. A <chi-squared distribution> with $n-1$ <statistical degrees of freedom> is a sum of $n-1$ <independent> $\chi^2_1$ variables, each with <mean> one and <variance> two. The <central limit theorem> therefore gives
$$
\sqrt{n-1}(S^2-\mu^2)\xrightarrow dN(0,2\mu^4).
$$
For $g(x)=\sqrt x$, the <derivative> at $\mu^2$ is $1/(2\mu)$ because $\mu>0$. The <delta method> gives the <asymptotic normality of a normal sample standard deviation>:
$$
\sqrt n(S-\mu)\xrightarrow dN(0,\mu^2/2).
$$
The exact independence of $\overline X$ and $S$ makes their limiting <normal distributions> independent. Consequently,
$$
\sqrt n(\widetilde\mu-\mu)
=\frac13\{\sqrt n(\overline X-\mu)+2\sqrt n(S-\mu)\}
\xrightarrow dN\!\left(0,\frac19\left\{\mu^2+4\frac{\mu^2}2\right\}\right).
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\sqrt n(\widetilde\mu-\mu)\xrightarrow dN(0,\mu^2/3),\qquad
\widetilde\mu\mathrel{\dot\sim}N\!\left(\mu,\frac{\mu^2}{3n}\right).}
$$
This has the same leading <asymptotic variance> as $\widehat\mu$. The weights $1/3$ and $2/3$ are those in the <optimal asymptotic combination of a normal sample mean and standard deviation>. This does not require $S$ to be an exactly <unbiased estimator> of $\mu$.

The <Neyman-Pearson lemma> concerns a <simple hypothesis> $H_0:\theta=\theta_0$ against another <simple hypothesis> $H_1:\theta=\theta_1$. For a continuous <statistical sample>, put $f_j(x)=\prod_i f(x_i;\theta_j)$ for the joint <probability density functions>, $j=0,1$. A <randomized statistical test> is a measurable $\phi(x)\in[0,1]$, the conditional <probability> of rejection after observing $x$. Its <test size> is $\mathbb E_0\phi$ and its <statistical power> is $\mathbb E_1\phi$.

Choose $k\geq0$ and boundary randomization so that
$$
\phi_*(x)=\begin{cases}
1,&f_1(x)>kf_0(x),\\
0,&f_1(x)<kf_0(x),\\
\gamma(x)\in[0,1],&f_1(x)=kf_0(x),
\end{cases}
\qquad\mathbb E_0\phi_* =\alpha.
$$
Then **$\phi_*$ is most powerful among all tests of size at most $\alpha$**. Thus one rejects for sufficiently large alternative-to-null <likelihood ratio>. If the equality set has zero <probability> under $H_0$, the test needs no randomization. Continuous observations alone do not guarantee that their <likelihood ratio> has a continuous <probability distribution>.

For the proof, let $\phi$ be any competing test with $\mathbb E_0\phi\leq\alpha$. At every $x$,
$$
(\phi_*(x)-\phi(x))(f_1(x)-kf_0(x))\geq0.
$$
Indeed, on $f_1>kf_0$ the first factor is $1-\phi\geq0$; on $f_1<kf_0$ it is $-\phi\leq0$; and on equality the product is zero. <Integration> gives
$$
\mathbb E_1\phi_* -\mathbb E_1\phi
\geq k(\mathbb E_0\phi_* -\mathbb E_0\phi)\geq0,
$$
which proves the claimed optimality. Moreover, when $k>0$, equality of <statistical power> forces equality of <test size> and forces $\phi=\phi_*$ almost everywhere where $f_1\ne kf_0$: otherwise the nonnegative integrand would have positive <integral>. This proves the usual necessity assertion as well, up to equality sets and sets of zero <probability> under both hypotheses.

Let $S=\sum_iX_i$. For the <exponential distribution> with <mean> $\theta$, the <likelihood function> is $L(\theta)=\theta^{-n}e^{-S/\theta}$ on positive observations. Fix any $0<\theta_1<\theta_0$. The alternative-to-null <likelihood ratio> is
$$
\frac{L(\theta_1)}{L(\theta_0)}
=\left(\frac{\theta_0}{\theta_1}\right)^n
\exp\!\left\{-\left(\frac1{\theta_1}-\frac1{\theta_0}\right)S\right\}.
$$
Since $1/\theta_1-1/\theta_0>0$, this is strictly decreasing in $S$. The <Neyman-Pearson lemma> says to reject for small $S$. Under the <null hypothesis>, the given sum rule yields $S\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(n,1/\theta_0)$, where the second argument is a rate, and hence
$$
\frac{2S}{\theta_0}\sim\chi^2_{2n}.
$$
Let $q_\alpha$ be the lower $\alpha$ <quantile> of this <chi-squared distribution>. The required <critical region> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{2\sum_iX_i}{\theta_0}<q_\alpha,\qquad
q_\alpha=F^{-1}_{\chi^2_{2n}}(\alpha).}
$$
Its <test size> is exactly $\alpha$ because the <null distribution> is continuous. Its cutoff is the same for every $\theta_1<\theta_0$, so it is simultaneously most powerful against every such value: it is a <uniformly most powerful test>. Equivalently, reject if $S$ is below the lower $\alpha$ <quantile> of the specified <gamma distribution>.

Put $A=\sum_iX_i$, $B=\sum_jY_j$ and $N=n+m$. Independence gives the <likelihood function>
$$
L(\theta,\mu)=\theta^{-n}\mu^{-m}\exp(-A/\theta-B/\mu).
$$
The unrestricted <maximum-likelihood estimators> are $\widehat\theta=A/n$ and $\widehat\mu=B/m$. Under the <null hypothesis>, the common <mean> $\eta=\theta=\mu$ maximizes $\eta^{-N}e^{-(A+B)/\eta}$ at $\widehat\eta=(A+B)/N$. These are maxima because each one-parameter <score function> has the sign of its observed sum minus its sample size times its proposed <mean>.

Define the generalized <likelihood ratio> to be the constrained maximum divided by the unrestricted maximum. The exponential factors cancel, giving
$$
\Lambda=\frac{[N/(A+B)]^Ne^{-N}}{(n/A)^n(m/B)^me^{-N}}
=\frac{N^N}{n^nm^m}\frac{A^nB^m}{(A+B)^N}.
$$
Since $T=A/(A+B)$ and $1-T=B/(A+B)$,
$$
\boxed{\Lambda=\frac{(n+m)^{n+m}}{n^nm^m}T^n(1-T)^m.}
$$
It has maximum one at $T=n/N$, when the two fitted <means> agree, and is small toward either endpoint. A <likelihood-ratio test> rejects for small $\Lambda$, or equivalently large $W=-2\log\Lambda$. This is the <likelihood-ratio test for equality of exponential means>.

= Solution

{c}

Under the <null hypothesis>, the full model has two free positive <statistical parameters> and the constrained model has one. The <Wilks theorem> therefore gives
$$
W=-2\log\Lambda\xrightarrow d\chi^2_1
$$
as both sample sizes tend to infinity, with $n/(n+m)$ tending to a number strictly between zero and one. For the observed $t$, calculate
$$
w_{\rm obs}=2\left[n\log\frac{n}{(n+m)t}+m\log\frac{m}{(n+m)(1-t)}\right].
$$
The large-sample <p-value> is the upper-tail <probability>
$$
\boxed{p_{\rm approx}=1-F_{\chi^2_1}(w_{\rm obs})
=2\{1-\Phi(\sqrt{w_{\rm obs}})\},}
$$
where $\Phi$ is the <cumulative distribution function> of the <standard normal distribution>. The last equality uses the fact that the square of a <standard normal random variable> has a <chi-squared distribution> with one <statistical degree of freedom>. Values of $T$ on either side of $n/(n+m)$ are already included by the transformation to $W$.

Let $s=\sum_i x_i$. The <Bernoulli distribution> <likelihood function> is $\theta^s(1-\theta)^{n-s}$. Multiplication by the <beta distribution> <prior density> and <Bayes' theorem> give
$$
\pi(\theta\mid x)\propto\theta^{a+s-1}(1-\theta)^{b+n-s-1},\qquad0<\theta<1.
$$
Recognizing the normalized <beta distribution>,
$$
\boxed{\theta\mid x\sim\operatorname{Beta}(a+s,b+n-s),\qquad
\pi(\theta\mid x)=\frac{\theta^{a+s-1}(1-\theta)^{b+n-s-1}}{B(a+s,b+n-s)}.}
$$
This is <Beta-binomial conjugacy>; the same update applies whether the data are recorded as the individual <Bernoulli trials> or their success count.

Conditional on $\theta$, the future <Bernoulli random variable> is independent of the observed <statistical sample> and has success <probability> $\theta$. Its <posterior predictive probability> is therefore the <posterior mean> of $\theta$. Put $A=a+s$ and $B=b+n-s$. Then
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(X_{n+1}=1\mid x)
&=\int_0^1\theta\,\pi(\theta\mid x)\,d\theta\\
&=\frac{B(A+1,B)}{B(A,B)}
=\frac{\Gamma(A+1)\Gamma(A+B)}{\Gamma(A)\Gamma(A+B+1)}
=\frac A{A+B},
\end{aligned}
$$
using the <beta-gamma identity> and the <Gamma function recurrence>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(X_{n+1}=1\mid X_1,\ldots,X_n)=\frac{a+\sum_iX_i}{a+b+n}.}
$$
The whole <posterior predictive distribution> of $X_{n+1}$ is the <Bernoulli distribution> with this success <probability>.

For a specified <posterior probability> $1-\alpha$, an interval $I=(\theta_1,\theta_2)$ is a <highest posterior density interval> if
$$
\int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2}\pi(\theta\mid x)\,d\theta=1-\alpha
$$
and every included point has <posterior density> at least as large as every excluded point, apart from boundary and null-set choices. Equivalently, $I$ is a <level set> $\{\theta:\pi(\theta\mid x)>k\}$, with possible choices on its boundary, whose <posterior probability> is $1-\alpha$.

This selects the most densely supported values of the <statistical parameter>. When the relevant <level set> is one interval and has no plateau of positive <probability>, it is the shortest <credible interval> of that <posterior probability>. With several separated peaks, the corresponding <highest density region> can instead be disconnected, so an interval need not exist. The definition is in the stated parameter coordinate: <posterior density> ordering can change under a nonlinear change of parameter.

Put $A=a+s>1$ and $B=b+n-s>1$ as in part (a), and let $F_{A,B}$ be the <cumulative distribution function> of the <beta distribution> $\operatorname{Beta}(A,B)$. The two endpoint equations are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
F_{A,B}(\theta_2)-F_{A,B}(\theta_1)&=0.95,\\
\theta_1^{A-1}(1-\theta_1)^{B-1}
&=\theta_2^{A-1}(1-\theta_2)^{B-1}.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The first equation fixes the <posterior probability>, and the second makes the two endpoint <posterior densities> equal. Their admissible ordering is
$$
0<\theta_1<\frac{A-1}{A+B-2}<\theta_2<1.
$$
To justify sufficiency and uniqueness, the <derivative> of the logarithm of the <posterior density> is
$$
\frac{A-1}{\theta}-\frac{B-1}{1-\theta},
$$
which is positive below $m=(A-1)/(A+B-2)$ and negative above it. Thus the <posterior density> increases strictly to its unique <mode> and decreases strictly thereafter, vanishing at both ends. Each density threshold $k$ between zero and the maximum determines exactly one endpoint on each side of $m$. The <probability> between these endpoints decreases continuously and strictly from one to zero as $k$ increases, so exactly one threshold gives 0.95. This is the <beta highest posterior density endpoint characterization>.

For a scalar <statistical parameter> in a regular model with <probability mass function> $f(x\mid\theta)$, define the one-observation expected <Fisher information> by
$$
I(\theta)=\mathbb E_\theta\!\left[\{\partial_\theta\log f(X\mid\theta)\}^2\right]
=\sum_x\frac{\{\partial_\theta f(x\mid\theta)\}^2}{f(x\mid\theta)}.
$$
The <Jeffreys prior> is the <prior density> specified, up to a factor independent of $\theta$, by
$$
\boxed{\pi_J(\theta)\propto\sqrt{I(\theta)}.}
$$
For $n$ independent observations the <Fisher information> is $nI(\theta)$, so the factor $\sqrt n$ does not change this <prior distribution>. If $\int\sqrt{I(\theta)}\,d\theta$ is finite, divide by this integral to normalize; otherwise the rule defines an <improper prior>.

= Solution

The <Jeffreys prior> is **invariant under smooth one-to-one reparameterization**. This means that applying the rule directly in a new parameter $\eta=h(\theta)$ gives the same <prior distribution> as first constructing the rule for $\theta$ and then transforming its <prior density> by the change-of-variable formula.

More explicitly, for a differentiable invertible change of parameter with nonzero <derivative>,
$$
I_\eta(\eta)=I_\theta(\theta)\left(\frac{d\theta}{d\eta}\right)^2,
\qquad
\pi_{J,\eta}(\eta)\propto\sqrt{I_\eta(\eta)}
=\sqrt{I_\theta(\theta)}\left|\frac{d\theta}{d\eta}\right|.
$$
Thus $\pi_{J,\eta}(\eta)\,d\eta$ and $\pi_{J,\theta}(\theta)\,d\theta$ represent the same measure, up to the normalization constant. Invariance concerns the measure: the numerical values of the <prior densities> need not be equal in the two coordinates. The same change of variables then makes the <posterior distributions> agree. This is the <reparameterization invariance of jeffreys prior>.

For one <Bernoulli random variable>,
$$
\log f(x\mid\theta)=x\log\theta+(1-x)\log(1-\theta),\qquad
\partial_\theta\log f(x\mid\theta)=\frac{x-\theta}{\theta(1-\theta)}.
$$
Its <variance> is $\theta(1-\theta)$, so the expected <Fisher information> and <Jeffreys prior> are
$$
I(\theta)=\frac1{\theta(1-\theta)},\qquad
\pi_J(\theta)\propto\theta^{-1/2}(1-\theta)^{-1/2}.
$$
The normalizer is $B(1/2,1/2)=\pi$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\pi_J(\theta)=\frac1{\pi\sqrt{\theta(1-\theta)}},\quad0<\theta<1;\qquad
\theta\sim\operatorname{Beta}(1/2,1/2).}
$$
This <Jeffreys prior for a Bernoulli parameter> is proper despite its integrable endpoint singularities. It is the <arcsine distribution>. It replaces the specified $a,b>1$ <prior distribution> in part (a) when the <Jeffreys prior> rule is chosen; it is not one of those restricted priors.

Let $y_{(n)}=\max_i y_i$ and $c=\max(\beta,y_{(n)})$. For the observed positive data, the <uniform distribution> <likelihood function> is
$$
L(\theta;y)=\theta^{-n}\mathbf1_{\{\theta>y_{(n)}\}}.
$$
Multiplying by the <Pareto distribution> <prior density> gives the <posterior density> kernel $\theta^{-(\alpha+n+1)}\mathbf1_{\{\theta>c\}}$. Since
$$
\int_c^\infty\theta^{-(\alpha+n+1)}\,d\theta
=\frac{c^{-(\alpha+n)}}{\alpha+n},
$$
the normalized <posterior distribution> is
$$
\boxed{\theta\mid y\sim\operatorname{Pareto}(\alpha+n,c),\qquad
\pi(\theta\mid y)=\frac{(\alpha+n)c^{\alpha+n}}{\theta^{\alpha+n+1}}\mathbf1_{\{\theta>c\}}.}
$$
This is <uniform-pareto conjugacy>. The <posterior density> is strictly decreasing over its support, so the <highest posterior density interval> starts at $c$, where the admissible density is largest in the limiting sense. Its upper endpoint $u$ satisfies
$$
\mathbb P(\theta>u\mid y)=\left(\frac cu\right)^{\alpha+n}=0.05.
$$
Hence a 95% <highest posterior density interval> is
$$
\boxed{\left(c,\;c\,20^{1/(\alpha+n)}\right),\qquad c=\max\!\left(\beta,\max_iY_i\right).}
$$
Inclusion of endpoints does not change its <posterior probability>. Every point inside has higher <posterior density> than every admissible point to its right, and points below $c$ have density zero. This is the <Pareto highest density interval>; equal endpoint densities are unnecessary because one endpoint is the support boundary.
