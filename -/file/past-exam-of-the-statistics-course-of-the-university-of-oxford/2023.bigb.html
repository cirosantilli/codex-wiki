= Solution
{parent=a}

Sort the observations into <order statistics> $x_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq x_{(n)}$. Choose <plotting positions> $p_i=(i-1/2)/n$ and calculate the <standard normal quantiles> $z_i=\Phi^{-1}(p_i)$. The <normal Q-Q plot> consists of the pairs $(z_i,x_{(i)})$.

If the <normal distribution> model is appropriate, the points should lie approximately on a straight line with positive slope, because its population <quantile function> is
$$
Q(p)=\mu+\sigma\Phi^{-1}(p).
$$
The intercept is approximately $\mu$ and the slope approximately $\sigma$; neither needs to be known in advance. The reference need not be the identity line. One justification is the <probability integral transform>: for an independent normal <statistical sample>, $\Phi((X_{(i)}-\mu)/\sigma)$ are uniform <order statistics>, whose typical values are near their <plotting positions>. The equality is approximate for a finite sample, particularly at the extremes. Systematic curvature, unusually heavy tails, or isolated extreme departures argue against the proposed distributional shape. A straight marginal <Q-Q plot> does not itself establish <independence> of observations.

= Solution

Expanding the exponent, the <log-likelihood function> is
$$
\ell(\theta)=C-\frac{\sum_i y_i}{2\theta^2}+\frac n\theta,
$$
where $C$ is independent of $\theta$. Its <score function> and second derivative are
$$
\ell'(\theta)=\frac{\sum_i y_i-n\theta}{\theta^3},\qquad
\ell''(\theta)=-\frac{3\sum_i y_i}{\theta^4}+\frac{2n}{\theta^3}.
$$
The first is positive below $\bar y$ and negative above it, so the unique <maximum-likelihood estimator> is
$$
\boxed{\widehat\theta=\bar Y}.
$$
Using $\mathbb E\sum_iY_i=n\theta$, the one-observation <Fisher information> is $I(\theta)=-n^{-1}\mathbb E\ell''(\theta)=\theta^{-3}$. The support does not depend on the interior parameter $\theta>0$, and the regular <asymptotic normality of a maximum likelihood estimator> gives
$$
\boxed{\sqrt n(\widehat\theta-\theta)\xrightarrow d N(0,\theta^3)},\qquad
\widehat\theta\ \mathrel{\dot\sim}\ N(\theta,\theta^3/n).
$$
This is <Inverse Gaussian mean estimation> with known shape one. Equivalently, the <Inverse Gaussian distribution> has <variance> $\theta^3$, so the same result follows directly from the <central limit theorem> applied to its <sample mean>.

= Solution

The estimated <standard error> is $\widehat\theta^{3/2}/\sqrt n$. Using the central 95% <standard normal quantiles> $\pm1.96$, a <Wald confidence interval> is
$$
\boxed{\left[\widehat\theta-1.96\frac{\widehat\theta^{3/2}}{\sqrt n},\quad\widehat\theta+1.96\frac{\widehat\theta^{3/2}}{\sqrt n}\right]\cap(0,\infty)}.
$$
Consistency of $\widehat\theta$ and <Slutsky's theorem> justify replacing the unknown scale $\theta^{3/2}$ by its estimate. The <coverage probability> is asymptotic, not exact at finite $n$. Intersecting with the positive parameter space removes impossible values without changing whether the true positive parameter is covered.

Put $S=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2$ and $q=S/\sigma_0^2$. Under the <null hypothesis>, maximizing over the nuisance <mean> gives $\widehat\mu_0=\bar x$. The unrestricted <normal mean and variance maximum-likelihood estimators> are $\widehat\mu=\bar x$, $\widehat{\sigma^2}=S/n$. Thus, for $S>0$,
$$
\lambda(\boldsymbol x)=\frac{(2\pi\sigma_0^2)^{-n/2}e^{-S/(2\sigma_0^2)}}{(2\pi S/n)^{-n/2}e^{-n/2}}
=\boxed{\left(\frac qn\right)^{n/2}e^{(n-q)/2}}.
$$
The <likelihood-ratio test statistic> is consequently
$$
\boxed{W=-2\log\lambda=q-n-n\log(q/n)}.
$$
The usual model assumes $n\geq2$. The event $S=0$ has <probability> zero under a nondegenerate <normal distribution>; at that observation the unrestricted likelihood is unbounded as the variance tends to zero, and the ratio has limiting value zero. This is the <likelihood-ratio test for a normal variance> with unknown mean.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The full parameter space has dimension two and the null space dimension one. The regular <Wilks theorem> therefore gives $W\xrightarrow d\chi_1^2$ under $H_0$ as $n\to\infty$. If $F_1$ denotes the <cumulative distribution function> of the <chi-squared distribution> with one degree of freedom, the approximate size-$\alpha$ <rejection region> is
$$
\boxed{q-n-n\log(q/n)\geq F_1^{-1}(1-\alpha)}.
$$
The corresponding approximate <p-value> is
$$
\boxed{1-F_1\!\left(q_{\mathrm{obs}}-n-n\log(q_{\mathrm{obs}}/n)\right)}.
$$
The one degree of freedom here describes the large-sample <likelihood-ratio test statistic>; it is different from the exact residual degrees of freedom $n-1$ in the next part.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The derivative of the log ratio is $\tfrac12(n/q-1)$, so $\lambda(q)$ increases from zero to one on $(0,n)$ and decreases to zero on $(n,\infty)$. Rejecting for a sufficiently small ratio therefore gives two tails with $0<q_1<n<q_2$.

Under $H_0$, <Cochran's theorem> gives the exact <sampling distribution> $Q=S/\sigma_0^2\sim\chi_{n-1}^2$, independently of the unknown mean. Writing $F_{n-1}$ for its <cumulative distribution function>, the two cutoffs are determined by
$$
\boxed{F_{n-1}(q_1)+1-F_{n-1}(q_2)=\alpha,\qquad n\log(q_2/q_1)=q_2-q_1}.
$$
The first equation calibrates the exact <size of a statistical test>. The second is $\lambda(q_1)=\lambda(q_2)$, so both tails belong to one <likelihood-ratio> cutoff. Hence the exact <critical region> is $\boxed{\{q\leq q_1\}\cup\{q\geq q_2\}}$. Its two tail probabilities are not generally equal: equal-tailed chi-squared cutoffs give a different test.

= Solution

For a <critical region> $C$, the <size of a statistical test> of the simple <null hypothesis> $P_0$ is $\alpha=P_0(\boldsymbol X\in C)$, the <Type I error> probability. Its <statistical power> against the simple <alternative hypothesis> $P_1$ is $P_1(\boldsymbol X\in C)$, the probability of correctly rejecting that null. For a randomized test function $\varphi(\boldsymbol x)\in[0,1]$, replace these by $\mathbb E_0\varphi$ and $\mathbb E_1\varphi$.

{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The <Neyman-Pearson lemma> states that a test which rejects where the joint <likelihood ratio> $L_1/L_0$ is largest is a <most powerful test> of its size: its power is at least that of any test with no larger size. More precisely, choose a threshold $k$ and reject if $L_1/L_0>k$, accept if it is smaller, and if necessary randomize on equality to attain the specified size.

For an independent and identically distributed <statistical sample>, take
$$
Y_i=\log\frac{f_0(X_i)}{f_1(X_i)}.
$$
The $Y_i$ are <independent and identically distributed random variables> under either simple hypothesis, and the log joint ratio is their sum. Thus the nonrandomized threshold form can be written
$$
\boxed{\sum_{i=1}^nY_i\leq c}
$$
with $c=-\log k$, using either strict or non-strict inequality when the boundary has probability zero. The usual finite-log expression assumes positive common support; zero densities can instead be handled by the extended likelihood ratio on the relevant supports. Continuous observations alone do not ensure a continuous likelihood-ratio statistic: ties may require the boundary randomization in the lemma. This qualification preserves the exact-size formulation.

= Solution

The joint <likelihood ratio> in favor of the alternative is
$$
\frac{L_3}{L_2}=\left(\frac32\right)^n\prod_i x_i.
$$
It is increasing in the product, so the <Neyman-Pearson lemma> rejects for large product, equivalently small $S=-\sum_i\log x_i$. Under $H_0$, the transformation $Z_i=-\log X_i$ gives
$$
\mathbb P_0(Z_i>z)=\mathbb P_0(X_i<e^{-z})=e^{-2z},\qquad z\geq0.
$$
Therefore the $Z_i$ are <independent> <exponential random variables> of rate two, $S\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(n,\text{rate }2)$, and $4S\sim\chi_{2n}^2$. An exact size-$\alpha$ <critical region> is
$$
\boxed{S\leq c_\alpha},\qquad\boxed{F_{\chi_{2n}^2}(4c_\alpha)=\alpha}.
$$
Equivalently, reject when the <geometric mean> satisfies $g\geq e^{-c_\alpha/n}$. The boundary distribution is continuous, so no randomization is needed here.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Under $H_0$, each $Z_i=-\log X_i$ has <expected value> $1/2$ and <variance> $1/4$. The <central limit theorem> therefore approximates
$$
\frac{S-n/2}{\sqrt n/2}\ \mathrel{\dot\sim}\ N(0,1).
$$
For the supplied data this standardized value is
$$
\boxed{z_{\mathrm{obs}}=\frac{6.25-12.5}{2.5}=-2.5}.
$$
Using the provided approximation $\Phi(1.6)\approx0.95$, the lower-tail 5% rule rejects when $S\leq12.5-1.6(2.5)=8.5$. The observed $6.25$ is in this region, with approximate <p-value> $\Phi(-2.5)\approx0.005$. **Reject $H_0:\theta=2$ at the 5% level in favor of the specified alternative $\theta=3$.** The unusually large <geometric mean> is consistent with the direction of that alternative. This is evidence in this comparison, not a proof that the parameter is exactly three.

= Solution
{parent=i}

Given the observed data, a $100(1-\alpha)\%$ <credible interval> $[a,b]$ for the scalar parameter satisfies
$$
\boxed{\mathbb P(a\leq\theta\leq b\mid\boldsymbol x)=1-\alpha}.
$$
The probability refers to the <posterior distribution>, proportional to $\pi(\theta)\prod_i f(x_i\mid\theta)$ on the parameter space. It conditions on the observations; it is not the repeated-sampling <coverage probability> definition of a <confidence interval>.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

An <equal-tailed credible interval> for a continuous <posterior distribution> has probability $\alpha/2$ below its lower endpoint and $\alpha/2$ above its upper endpoint. If $F$ is the posterior <cumulative distribution function>, the endpoints are $F^{-1}(\alpha/2)$ and $F^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)$.

A <highest posterior density interval> is a <credible interval> consisting of points of highest <posterior density>: every included point has density at least as high as every excluded point, apart from null-set and boundary choices. It is a posterior <highest density region> when that region is an interval. For a continuous unimodal density with two interior endpoints, their densities agree. A mode at a support boundary can instead force one endpoint to that boundary; a multimodal posterior can have a disconnected highest-density region rather than one interval. Equal tails and highest density need not give the same interval.

Writing $s=\sum_i x_i$, the <Poisson distribution> likelihood is proportional to $\theta^s e^{-n\theta}$. Multiplying by the <gamma distribution> prior gives
$$
\pi(\theta\mid\boldsymbol x)\propto\theta^{r+s-1}e^{-(\lambda+n)\theta}.
$$
This is <Poisson-gamma conjugacy>, with the rate convention made explicit:
$$
\boxed{\theta\mid\boldsymbol x\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(r+s,\text{rate }\lambda+n)}.
$$
Its normalized density is $(\lambda+n)^{r+s}\theta^{r+s-1}e^{-(\lambda+n)\theta}/\Gamma(r+s)$ for $\theta>0$.

{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The <posterior mean> is a <convex combination> of the <prior mean> and the usual <Poisson> <maximum-likelihood estimate>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E(\theta\mid\boldsymbol x)=\frac{r+s}{\lambda+n}=\frac\lambda{\lambda+n}\frac r\lambda+\frac n{\lambda+n}\bar x}.
$$
Both weights are positive and sum to one, so the mean lies between $r/\lambda$ and $\bar x$, strictly between them unless they coincide. If all counts are zero, $\bar x=0$ is the boundary estimate in the usual nonnegative Poisson parameter space; with a strictly positive parameter restriction it represents the likelihood supremum at that boundary. The displayed posterior-mean relation is valid in either interpretation.

Let $M=\max\{b,y_1,\ldots,y_n\}$. The <continuous uniform distribution> likelihood contributes $\phi^{-n}\mathbf1_{\{\phi\geq\max_i y_i\}}$, while the <Pareto distribution> prior contributes $\phi^{-a-1}\mathbf1_{\{\phi\geq b\}}$. Their product proves <Uniform-Pareto conjugacy>:
$$
\boxed{p(\phi\mid\boldsymbol y)=(a+n)M^{a+n}\phi^{-(a+n+1)}\mathbf1_{\{\phi\geq M\}}}.
$$
Thus the <posterior distribution> is Pareto with shape $a+n$ and lower bound $M$. Its density is strictly decreasing on its support, so the <highest posterior density interval> begins at $M$. Since the upper-tail probability is $(M/u)^{a+n}$, the required upper endpoint satisfies $(M/u)^{a+n}=0.05$. The <Pareto highest density interval> is therefore
$$
\boxed{[M,\ M\,20^{1/(a+n)}]}.
$$

= Solution
{parent=d}

Write $m=r+s$ and $\beta=\lambda+n$. Conditional independence of the next count given $\theta$ allows us to average its <Poisson distribution> over the <posterior distribution>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(X_{n+1}=k\mid\boldsymbol x)&=\int_0^\infty\frac{e^{-\theta}\theta^k}{k!}\frac{\beta^m}{\Gamma(m)}\theta^{m-1}e^{-\beta\theta}\,d\theta\\
&=\frac{\Gamma(k+m)}{\Gamma(m)k!}\frac{\beta^m}{(\beta+1)^{k+m}}\\
&=\binom{k+m-1}{k}\left(\frac\beta{\beta+1}\right)^m\left(\frac1{\beta+1}\right)^k.
\end{aligned}
$$
This <Poisson-gamma mixture> is the <posterior predictive distribution>. In the stated <negative binomial distribution> convention its parameters are
$$
\boxed{m=r+\sum_i x_i,\qquad p=\frac{\lambda+n}{\lambda+n+1}}.
$$
Its <expected value> $m/\beta$ equals the <posterior mean> of $\theta$, while its <variance> $m/\beta+m/\beta^2$ includes both conditional Poisson variation and uncertainty in $\theta$.
