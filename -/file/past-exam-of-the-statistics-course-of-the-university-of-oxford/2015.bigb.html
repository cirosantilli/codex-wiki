= Solution
{parent=i}

The given <probability density function> is that of a <beta distribution> with parameters $r$ and $n-r+1$. We can derive the required <moments> directly rather than assuming the beta moment formulas. For an integer $j\geq0$, the <Euler beta function> gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[U_{(r)}^j]
&=\frac{n!}{(r-1)!(n-r)!}
\int_0^1u^{r+j-1}(1-u)^{n-r}\,du\\
&=\frac{n!(r+j-1)!}{(r-1)!(n+j)!}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $B(a,b)=(a-1)!(b-1)!/(a+b-1)!$ for positive integers $a,b$; this identity also follows by repeated <integration by parts>. Taking $j=1,2$ yields
$$
\mathbb E[U_{(r)}]=\frac r{n+1},\qquad
\mathbb E[U_{(r)}^2]=\frac{r(r+1)}{(n+1)(n+2)}.
$$
Subtract the square of the first <moment> from the second to obtain the <variance>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[U_{(r)}]=\frac r{n+1},\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(U_{(r)})=
\frac{r(n-r+1)}{(n+1)^2(n+2)}.}
$$
These are exact finite-sample identities for each <uniform order statistic>.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For odd sample size, the <sample median> is the central <order statistic>, $M_U=U_{(m+1)}$. Since $n=2m+1$, its index is $r=(n+1)/2$ and $n-r+1=(n+1)/2$. Substitute these into the <expectation> and <variance> formulas from part (i):
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E[M_U]=\frac12,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(M_U)=\frac1{4(n+2)}.}
$$
In fact $M_U$ has the symmetric <beta distribution> $\operatorname{Beta}(m+1,m+1)$, explaining why its <expectation> is exactly the centre of the interval.

= Solution

Continuity and strict increase make the <quantile function> $F^{-1}$ well-defined on $(0,1)$. The endpoints of the <uniform distribution> occur with <probability> zero and do not affect the construction. For every real $x$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Pr(X_i\leq x)
&=\Pr(F^{-1}(U_i)\leq x)\\
&=\Pr(U_i\leq F(x))=F(x).
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus each $X_i$ has <cumulative distribution function> $F$. Applying a fixed measurable function separately to <independent random variables> preserves their independence; explicitly, joint lower-tail probabilities factor as
$$
\Pr(X_1\leq x_1,\ldots,X_n\leq x_n)=\prod_{i=1}^nF(x_i).
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{X_1,\ldots,X_n\text{ are independent and identically distributed with CDF }F.}
$$
This is <inverse transform sampling>. Since $F^{-1}$ preserves order, it also sends the central <order statistic> to the central <order statistic>, justifying $M_X=F^{-1}(M_U)$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

First establish the limiting law needed by the <delta method>. For fixed $t$, put $u_n=1/2+t/\sqrt n$ and let $N_n$ count how many of the $U_i$ are at most $u_n$. Then $N_n$ has a <binomial distribution> with <expectation> $n/2+t\sqrt n$ and <variance> $n/4-t^2$. The event $M_U\leq u_n$ is exactly $N_n\geq(n+1)/2$. Its standardized threshold tends to $-2t$, so the <central limit theorem> for these binomial counts gives
$$
\Pr\!\left(\sqrt n(M_U-1/2)\leq t\right)\longrightarrow\Phi(2t).
$$
Here $\Phi$ is the <cumulative distribution function> of the <standard normal distribution>. Equivalently,
$$
\sqrt n(M_U-1/2)\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1/4).
$$
Use the usual local regularity for the <delta method>: $f$ is continuous at the population <median> $\theta$ and $f(\theta)>0$. The <inverse function theorem>, applied at $F(\theta)=1/2$, gives
$$
\left.\frac{d}{du}F^{-1}(u)\right|_{u=1/2}=\frac1{f(\theta)}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\sqrt n(M_X-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}
N\!\left(0,\frac1{4f(\theta)^2}\right).}
$$
The <asymptotic normality> gives the requested leading <variance> approximation
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{avar}(M_X)=\frac1{4n f(\theta)^2}.}
$$
Here the variance parameter for the rescaled limiting law is $1/(4f(\theta)^2)$, while the source states the corresponding $1/n$ approximation for the unscaled <estimator>. Strict increase of $F$ alone does not force $f(\theta)>0$; that local condition is needed for this form of the <asymptotic distribution of a sample median>.

{scope}

= Solution
{parent=c}

For the <normal distribution>, the density at its centre is $f(\theta)=1/(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})$. The <sample mean> has exact <variance> $\sigma^2/n$, whereas part (b) gives the <sample median> the leading <variance> $\pi\sigma^2/(2n)$. Thus the requested <asymptotic relative efficiency> is
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ARE}(M_X,\overline X)=\frac2\pi\approx0.637
\quad\text{for the normal model}.}
$$
At a fixed confidence level, a <confidence interval> based on the <sample mean> has a leading half-width smaller by the factor $\sqrt{2/\pi}$. With known $\sigma$, $\overline X\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sigma/\sqrt n$ also has exact normal coverage. **Prefer the sample mean under the normal model.**

For the <Laplace distribution>, symmetry puts both the <mean> and <median> at $\theta$. Its density there is $1/(2\sigma)$, and
$$
\operatorname{Var}(X_i)=\frac1\sigma\int_0^\infty y^2e^{-y/\sigma}\,dy
=2\sigma^2.
$$
Consequently the <sample mean> has <variance> $2\sigma^2/n$, while the <sample median> has leading <variance> $\sigma^2/n$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{ARE}(M_X,\overline X)=2
\quad\text{for the Laplace model}.}
$$
A large-sample <confidence interval> based on the <sample median> has leading half-width smaller by $1/\sqrt2$; with known scale it is $M_X\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sigma/\sqrt n$. **Prefer the sample median under the Laplace model.** Consistent estimation of an unknown scale preserves these leading comparisons. The reason for the reversal is quantitative: the <median>'s precision depends on the central density $f(\theta)$, while the <sample mean>'s precision depends on the whole population <variance>.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $L(\vartheta;x)$ be the <likelihood function>, with full parameter space $\Theta$ and <null hypothesis> $\vartheta\in\Theta_0\subseteq\Theta$. The <generalized likelihood-ratio test> compares the best null fit to the best unrestricted fit:
$$
R(x)=\frac{\sup_{\vartheta\in\Theta_0}L(\vartheta;x)}
{\sup_{\vartheta\in\Theta}L(\vartheta;x)},
\qquad \Lambda(x)=-2\log R(x).
$$
The <likelihood ratio> $R$ lies between zero and one, and small values count against the <null hypothesis>; equivalently, large values of the <likelihood-ratio test statistic> $\Lambda$ count against it.

Compute restricted and unrestricted <maximum-likelihood estimates>, evaluate their <log-likelihoods>, and form $\Lambda=2(\ell(\widehat\vartheta)-\ell(\widehat\vartheta_0))$. Under the regular conditions of <Wilks theorem>, the null limit is
$$
\Lambda\xrightarrow{d}\chi_d^2,\qquad
d=\dim\Theta-\dim\Theta_0.
$$
These <statistical degrees of freedom> count independent parameter restrictions. The regularity includes a locally identifiable smooth model, nonsingular <Fisher information>, and an interior null value; parameter-dependent support can invalidate the result.

For a chosen <significance level> $\alpha$, obtain the $(1-\alpha)$ <quantile> $q_{d,1-\alpha}$ of the <chi-squared distribution> and use
$$
\boxed{\text{reject }H_0\text{ if }\Lambda_{\rm obs}>q_{d,1-\alpha}.}
$$
Equivalently, compare the asymptotic <p-value> $\Pr(\chi_d^2\geq\Lambda_{\rm obs})$ to $\alpha$. This is a large-sample calibration; part (c) requires direct treatment because its support depends on a parameter.

= Solution

The count vector has a <multinomial distribution>, with <likelihood function>
$$
L(\pi)=\frac{n!}{\prod_i n_i!}\prod_{i=1}^k\pi_i^{n_i},
\qquad\sum_i\pi_i=1.
$$
Maximize its <log-likelihood> using a <Lagrange multiplier> for the sum constraint. The equations $n_i/\pi_i=\lambda$ and $\sum_i\pi_i=1$ give $\lambda=n$ and $\widehat\pi_i=n_i/n$. Since all counts are positive and the <log-likelihood> is strictly concave on the interior of the probability simplex, this is the global <maximum-likelihood estimate>.

Under the uniform <null hypothesis>, each category has expected count
$$
\boxed{e_i=n\pi_{i,0}=\frac nk.}
$$
The common multinomial coefficient cancels from the <likelihood ratio>:
$$
R=\frac{k^{-n}}{\prod_i(n_i/n)^{n_i}}
=\prod_{i=1}^k\left(\frac{e_i}{n_i}\right)^{n_i}.
$$
Taking minus twice its logarithm gives exactly
$$
\boxed{\Lambda=2\sum_{i=1}^k n_i\log\!\left(\frac{n_i}{e_i}\right).}
$$
There are $k-1$ free category probabilities, because their sum is one, and the null fixes all of them. Thus its regular asymptotic <chi-squared distribution> has $k-1$ <statistical degrees of freedom>.

For four categories the number of <statistical degrees of freedom> is $4-1=3$. The supplied 5% critical value is therefore $q_3=7.82$, and $7.3<7.82$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\text{Do not reject equal category probabilities at the 5\% level.}}
$$
The approximate <p-value> is $\Pr(\chi_3^2\geq7.3)\approx0.063$. This conclusion assumes the large-sample multinomial approximation is adequate, in particular sufficiently large expected category counts. It means there is insufficient evidence against the uniform <null hypothesis>, rather than establishing that the probabilities are equal.

= Solution
{parent=c}

Write $a=x_{(1)}=\min_i x_i$, $T=\sum_i x_i$ and $\overline x=T/n$. For nondegenerate data from the shifted <exponential distribution>, the <likelihood function> is
$$
L(\theta,\beta)=\beta^{-n}
\exp\!\left[-\frac{T-n\theta}{\beta}\right]
\mathbf1_{\{0\leq\theta\leq a\}}.
$$
For fixed feasible $\theta$, differentiating its <log-likelihood> with respect to $\beta$ gives
$$
-\frac n\beta+\frac{T-n\theta}{\beta^2}=0,
\qquad \widehat\beta(\theta)=\overline x-\theta.
$$
The derivative changes from positive to negative there. Substituting gives the profile <likelihood> $e^{-n}(\overline x-\theta)^{-n}$, which increases with $\theta$. Therefore the unrestricted <maximum-likelihood estimates> are
$$
\widehat\theta=a,\qquad \widehat\beta=\overline x-a,
$$
while under the <null hypothesis> $\theta=0$ the estimate is $\widetilde\beta=\overline x$. Their <likelihood ratio> is
$$
R=\frac{\overline x^{-n}e^{-n}}{(\overline x-a)^{-n}e^{-n}}
=\left(1-\frac a{\overline x}\right)^n.
$$
Since the <likelihood-ratio test> rejects for small $R$, the requested form is obtained with
$$
\boxed{g(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=\frac{x_{(1)}}{\overline x}
=\frac{n\min_i x_i}{\sum_i x_i},\qquad
C=\{x:(1-g(x))^n\leq k\}.}
$$
The formulas assume $\overline x>a$, which holds almost surely for a continuous sample with $n\geq2$; coincident observations give an unbounded unrestricted <likelihood> and can be treated by its supremum.

An exact calibration is available and also explains why <Wilks theorem> should not be applied here. Under the null, the <memoryless property> makes $A=nX_{(1)}$ and $D=\sum_i(X_i-X_{(1)})$ independent <gamma distributions> with common scale $\beta$ and shapes $1$ and $n-1$. By <beta sampling by gamma ratios>,
$$
1-g=\frac{D}{A+D}\sim\operatorname{Beta}(n-1,1).
$$
Consequently, for $0\leq k\leq1$,
$$
\Pr_0(R\leq k)=k^{(n-1)/n},\qquad
\boxed{k_\alpha=\alpha^{n/(n-1)}.}
$$
This yields an exact <test size> $\alpha$, independent of the unknown scale. The support endpoint and boundary null make this a <likelihood-ratio test for an exponential support endpoint>, with a different calibration from a regular one-parameter test.

= Solution
{parent=a}

Let $p_0=\Pr(\theta\in\Theta_0)$ and $p_1=1-p_0$, assuming both are positive. The <prior odds> are $\alpha=p_0/p_1$. Given data $x$, the <posterior odds> are
$$
O_{01}(x)=\frac{\Pr(H_0\mid x)}{\Pr(H_1\mid x)}.
$$
With conditional joint <likelihood> $L(\theta;x)=\prod_i f(x_i\mid\theta)$, define the hypothesis-conditional marginal densities
$$
m_0(x)=\frac1{p_0}\int_{\Theta_0}L(\theta;x)\,\pi(d\theta),\qquad
m_1(x)=\frac1{p_1}\int_{\Theta\setminus\Theta_0}L(\theta;x)\,\pi(d\theta).
$$
The <Bayes factor> for $H_0$ relative to $H_1$ is $B=m_0(x)/m_1(x)$. The normalizations $p_0,p_1$ matter: the <prior distribution> within each hypothesis must have total probability one. <Bayes' theorem> now gives
$$
O_{01}(x)=\frac{p_0m_0(x)}{p_1m_1(x)}=\alpha B.
$$
Converting <posterior odds> to <posterior probability> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\Pr(H_0\mid x)=\frac{\alpha B}{1+\alpha B}.}
$$

= Solution
{parent=i}

Take $\sigma,\tau>0$ to be the positive standard deviations. Multiply the normal <likelihood function> by the normal <prior density>. Terms independent of $\theta$ can be absorbed into the normalizing constant:
$$
\pi(\theta\mid x)\propto
\exp\!\left[-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(x_i-\theta)^2
-\frac{\theta^2}{2\tau^2}\right].
$$
The identity $\sum_i(x_i-\theta)^2=\sum_i(x_i-\overline x)^2+n(\theta-\overline x)^2$ makes the quadratic term explicit. Completing the square gives
$$
\pi(\theta\mid x)\propto\exp\!\left[-\frac{(\theta-\mu_n)^2}{2v_n}\right],
$$
where
$$
\boxed{v_n=\left(\frac n{\sigma^2}+\frac1{\tau^2}\right)^{-1}
=\frac{\sigma^2\tau^2}{\sigma^2+n\tau^2},\qquad
\mu_n=\frac{n\tau^2}{\sigma^2+n\tau^2}\overline x.}
$$
Thus $\theta\mid x\sim N(\mu_n,v_n)$. This is <normal-normal conjugacy>: prior and data precisions add, and the <posterior mean> shrinks the <sample mean> towards the prior mean zero.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The symmetric continuous <prior distribution> gives $\Pr(\theta<0)=\Pr(\theta\geq0)=1/2$, so the <prior odds> are one. Therefore the <Bayes factor> equals the <posterior odds>. Standardizing the normal <posterior distribution>, put
$$
z=\frac{\mu_n}{\sqrt{v_n}}
=\frac{n\tau\overline x}{\sigma\sqrt{\sigma^2+n\tau^2}}.
$$
Then $\Pr(H_0\mid x)=\Phi(-z)$ and $\Pr(H_1\mid x)=\Phi(z)$. The endpoint $\theta=0$ has zero posterior probability, so the strict/non-strict split causes no additional term. Hence
$$
\boxed{B=\frac{\Phi(-z)}{\Phi(z)}
=\frac{1-\Phi(z)}{\Phi(z)},\qquad
z=\frac{n\tau\overline x}{\sigma\sqrt{\sigma^2+n\tau^2}}.}
$$
In particular, positive values of the <sample mean> make this <Bayes factor> smaller, favouring the positive-mean hypothesis.

{parent=b}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The denominator $\Phi(z)$ is positive, so
$$
B<\frac1{10}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
1-\Phi(z)<\frac1{10}\Phi(z)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\Phi(z)>\frac{10}{11}.
$$
Strict increase of the <standard normal distribution> function turns this into $z>\Phi^{-1}(10/11)$. Since the coefficient multiplying $\overline x$ in $z$ is positive, the rejection rule is precisely $\overline x>c$, with
$$
\boxed{c=\frac{\sigma\sqrt{\sigma^2+n\tau^2}}{n\tau}
\Phi^{-1}\!\left(\frac{10}{11}\right)
=\frac\sigma{\sqrt n}\sqrt{1+\frac{\sigma^2}{n\tau^2}}
\Phi^{-1}\!\left(\frac{10}{11}\right).}
$$
The <quantile> is approximately $1.33518$. Equivalently, the rule requires <posterior probability> of $H_0$ below $1/11$, because its <prior odds> are one. The calculation is an instance of the <Bayes factor for the sign of a normal mean>.
