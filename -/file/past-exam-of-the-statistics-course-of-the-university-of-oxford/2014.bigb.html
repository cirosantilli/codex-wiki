= Solution

Write $I_1(\theta)$ for the <Fisher information> in one observation:
$$
I_1(\theta)=\mathbb E_\theta\!\left[\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log f(X;\theta)\right\}^2\right]
=-\mathbb E_\theta\!\left[\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}\log f(X;\theta)\right].
$$
Under the stipulated regularity assumptions, the <maximum-likelihood estimator> is <consistent> and has <asymptotic normality>:
$$
\boxed{\sqrt n(\widehat\theta-\theta)\ \xrightarrow{d}\ N\!\left(0,I_1(\theta)^{-1}\right).}
$$
Equivalently, its large-sample <normal approximation> has <mean> $\theta$ and <variance> $1/[nI_1(\theta)]$. The information for the whole <random sample> is $nI_1(\theta)$; keeping the one-observation convention explicit prevents an extra factor of $n$.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

Let $z=\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)$, where $\Phi$ is the <standard normal distribution>'s <cumulative distribution function>. <Statistical consistency> and <Slutsky's theorem> allow replacement of $I_1(\theta)$ by $I_1(\widehat\theta)$:
$$
\sqrt{nI_1(\widehat\theta)}(\widehat\theta-\theta)\ \xrightarrow{d}\ N(0,1).
$$
Thus the probability that this standardized <estimation error> lies between $-z$ and $z$ tends to $1-\alpha$. Rearranging gives the <Wald confidence interval>
$$
\boxed{\left[\widehat\theta-\frac{z}{\sqrt{nI_1(\widehat\theta)}},\quad
\widehat\theta+\frac{z}{\sqrt{nI_1(\widehat\theta)}}\right].}
$$
The <coverage probability> is approximate, with limiting value $1-\alpha$; it is a repeated-sampling statement about the random <confidence interval>.

= Solution

Here the <exponential distribution> is parametrized by its <mean>, so $f(x;\theta)=\theta^{-1}e^{-x/\theta}$ for $x\geq0$ and $\theta>0$. Its <log-likelihood> and <maximum-likelihood estimate> are
$$
\ell(\theta)=-n\log\theta-\frac{\sum_iX_i}{\theta},\qquad \widehat\theta=\overline X.
$$
For one observation, $\partial_\theta\log f=-1/\theta+X/\theta^2$. Since $\operatorname{Var}(X)=\theta^2$, its <Fisher information> is $I_1(\theta)=1/\theta^2$. Consequently the estimated <standard error> is $\overline X/\sqrt n$, giving
$$
\boxed{\left[\overline X\left(1-\frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}}{\sqrt n}\right),\quad
\overline X\left(1+\frac{z_{1-\alpha/2}}{\sqrt n}\right)\right].}
$$
This is a large-sample <confidence interval>, not an exact one. If its lower endpoint is negative, intersecting with the <parameter space> $(0,\infty)$ removes impossible values without excluding any possible true <statistical parameter>.

Define $R=\sum_i(Y_i-\overline Y)^2$ and $s_Y^2=R/(n-1)$, with $n\geq2$. The unrestricted <maximum-likelihood estimates> are $\widehat\mu=\overline Y$ and $\widehat\sigma^2=R/n$. Under the <null hypothesis>, maximizing the <normal distribution>'s <likelihood> over the remaining <variance> gives
$$
\widehat\sigma_0^2=\frac1n\sum_i(Y_i-\mu_0)^2
=\frac Rn+(\overline Y-\mu_0)^2,
$$
because the cross term vanishes when $\sum_i(Y_i-\overline Y)=0$. At either fitted <variance>, the exponential factor in the <likelihood function> is $e^{-n/2}$, so it cancels in the ratio:
$$
\lambda=\left(\frac{\widehat\sigma^2}{\widehat\sigma_0^2}\right)^{n/2}
=\left(1+\frac{n(\overline Y-\mu_0)^2}{R}\right)^{-n/2}.
$$
The standardized <test statistic> is
$$
\boxed{t=\frac{\sqrt n(\overline Y-\mu_0)}{s_Y},\qquad
\lambda=\left(1+\frac{t^2}{n-1}\right)^{-n/2}.}
$$
The sample <variance> $s_Y^2$ uses divisor $n-1$, whereas the <maximum-likelihood estimate> uses divisor $n$; this explains the $n-1$ in the final expression. Under a nondegenerate <normal distribution>, $R>0$ with <probability> one.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For fixed $n$, the <likelihood ratio> is strictly decreasing as $t^2$ increases. A <likelihood-ratio test> rejects for small $\lambda$, so its rejection rule is equivalently large $|t|$. Under the <null hypothesis>, the standard <normal distribution> and <chi-squared distribution> results give independent quantities
$$
\frac{\sqrt n(\overline Y-\mu_0)}{\sigma}\sim N(0,1),\qquad
\frac{(n-1)s_Y^2}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-1}.
$$
Their ratio therefore has <Student's t-distribution> with $n-1$ <statistical degrees of freedom>. The exact level-$\alpha$ rule is
$$
\boxed{|t|>t_{n-1,\,1-\alpha/2}.}
$$
This is precisely the two-sided <Student's t-test>; choosing the corresponding cutoff for $\lambda$ yields the same rejection region and the same <p-value>.

= Solution
{parent=i}

The equal-<variance> two-sample <Student's t-test> pools the within-group sums of squares. If $s_A^2$ and $s_B^2$ are the two unbiased sample <variances>, the required positive square root is
$$
\boxed{S=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^m(A_i-\overline A)^2+\sum_{j=1}^n(B_j-\overline B)^2}{m+n-2}}
=\sqrt{\frac{(m-1)s_A^2+(n-1)s_B^2}{m+n-2}}.}
$$
For ten observations in each group, $S^2=(s_A^2+s_B^2)/2$ and the usual unpaired reference <Student's t-distribution> has $18$ <statistical degrees of freedom>, provided the assumed independence between groups is justified.

{parent=c}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

At the conventional $5\%$ <significance level>, Student 1's <p-value> $0.72$ gives **no rejection of equal mean wear**, while Student 2's <p-value> $0.009$ gives **rejection of equal mean wear**. The latter also rejects at the $1\%$ <significance level>. More generally, compare each <p-value> with the prespecified level $\alpha$; no decision threshold was fixed in the data supplied.

Failure to reject does not establish equality, and a <p-value> is not the posterior <probability> that the <null hypothesis> is true. These two-sided <p-values> alone also do not identify which material has larger <mean> wear.

= iii
{parent=c}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

**Prefer Student 2's paired analysis**, because the observational units are matched pairs: two shoes exposed to the same child's activity. A <paired t-test> uses $Y_i=A_i-B_i$, so shared child-to-child variation is removed before estimating the <standard error>. The independent units are the ten pairs, giving $9$ <statistical degrees of freedom> under the model of independent normally distributed pair differences.

The role of <covariance> is explicit:
$$
\operatorname{Var}(A_i-B_i)=\operatorname{Var}(A_i)+\operatorname{Var}(B_i)-2\operatorname{Cov}(A_i,B_i).
$$
With common marginal <variance> $\sigma^2$ and within-pair <correlation coefficient> $\rho$, the true <variance> of the average difference is $2\sigma^2(1-\rho)/10$. The independent-groups calculation ignores the covariance term and uses $2\sigma^2/10$. Positive within-pair association can therefore make the paired <standard error> much smaller, explaining how the reported <p-values> can differ without arithmetic mistakes. The preference follows from the paired design, not from selecting the smaller <p-value>. Marginal normality alone need not imply normally distributed differences; exact paired inference uses the normal-difference assumption, for example justified by a joint <multivariate normal distribution>.

= Solution
{parent=i}

For two simple <statistical hypotheses> with joint <probability density functions> $g_0$ and $g_1$, fix a <significance level> $\alpha$. A <randomized statistical test> $\varphi(x)$ is the conditional <probability> of rejection given the data. Choose $k\geq0$ and, if necessary, randomization on the equality set so that
$$
\varphi_*(x)=\begin{cases}1,&g_1(x)>kg_0(x),\\0,&g_1(x)<kg_0(x),\\\gamma(x)\in[0,1],&g_1(x)=kg_0(x),\end{cases}
\qquad \mathbb E_0\varphi_*=\alpha.
$$
The <Neyman-Pearson lemma> says that this is a <most powerful test> among all tests with <test size> at most $\alpha$:
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E_1\varphi\leq\mathbb E_1\varphi_*\quad\text{whenever}\quad\mathbb E_0\varphi\leq\alpha.}
$$
Thus the optimal ordering rejects where the alternative-to-null <likelihood ratio> is largest. On null-impossible observations with $g_1>0$, the rejection probability is one. Randomization is dispensable whenever an ordinary threshold already attains the required <test size>.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For a random sample, write $g_j(x)=\prod_{i=1}^nf(x_i;\theta_j)$, $j=0,1$, for its joint <probability density functions>. Let $\varphi$ be any competing <statistical test> of level at most $\alpha$. Pointwise,
$$
(\varphi_* -\varphi)(g_1-kg_0)\geq0.
$$
Indeed, where $g_1>kg_0$, $\varphi_*=1\geq\varphi$; where $g_1<kg_0$, $\varphi_*=0\leq\varphi$, so both factors have the same sign. On the equality set the product is zero, regardless of randomization. Integrate over the joint sample space using its ordinary volume measure:
$$
\begin{aligned}
0&\leq\int(\varphi_* -\varphi)(g_1-kg_0)\,dx\\
&=\mathbb E_1\varphi_* -\mathbb E_1\varphi-k(\mathbb E_0\varphi_* -\mathbb E_0\varphi).
\end{aligned}
$$
Since $k\geq0$ and $\mathbb E_0\varphi_* =\alpha\geq\mathbb E_0\varphi$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbb E_1\varphi_* -\mathbb E_1\varphi\geq k(\alpha-\mathbb E_0\varphi)\geq0.}
$$
This proves maximal <power>. A continuous sample distribution does not itself guarantee that the equality set of the <likelihood ratio> has zero probability; allowing randomization there makes the proof valid in that case too.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Put $T=\sum_iX_i$. For interior parameters $0<\theta_0<\theta_1<1$, the alternative-to-null <likelihood ratio> is
$$
\frac{L(\theta_1)}{L(\theta_0)}
=\left(\frac{1-\theta_1}{1-\theta_0}\right)^n
\left(\frac{\theta_1(1-\theta_0)}{\theta_0(1-\theta_1)}\right)^T.
$$
The second base is greater than one, so this is strictly increasing in $T$. The <Neyman-Pearson lemma> therefore gives **an upper-tail rejection rule**. Under the <null hypothesis>, $T\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n,\theta_0)$, and an ordinary conservative threshold can be chosen as
$$
C=\{x:T(x)\geq c_\alpha\},\qquad
\Pr_{\theta_0}(T\geq c_\alpha)\leq\alpha.
$$
There is an important discrete qualification: an integer cutoff need not have <test size> exactly $\alpha$. To obtain exact size, choose an integer $b$ with $\Pr_0(T>b)\leq\alpha\leq\Pr_0(T\geq b)$, reject certainly when $T>b$, and at $T=b$ reject with probability
$$
\boxed{\gamma=\frac{\alpha-\Pr_0(T>b)}{\Pr_0(T=b)}.}
$$
This <randomized statistical test> is a <most powerful test> at exact size $\alpha$; when a binomial upper tail equals $\alpha$, no randomization is needed and the stated region is literal. Otherwise the unrandomized upper-tail region has size at most $\alpha$, and the exact-size result includes the boundary randomization. Endpoint parameter cases retain the same upper-tail ordering by taking limits, with ties resolved on the null-supported outcomes.

= Solution
{parent=i}

The <sample mean> $\overline X=T/n$ has <mean> $\theta$ and <variance> $\sigma(\theta)^2=\theta(1-\theta)/n$. The <central limit theorem> gives a <normal approximation> for interior $\theta$ when both expected binomial counts are sufficiently large. Under the <null hypothesis>, a level-$\alpha$ upper-tail cutoff is approximately
$$
\overline X>\theta_0+\sigma(\theta_0)z_\alpha,\qquad\Phi(z_\alpha)=1-\alpha.
$$
To obtain the <power function>, evaluate this same cutoff under the true <statistical parameter> $\theta$, standardizing with $\sigma(\theta)$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
w(\theta)&\approx\Pr_\theta\!\left(\frac{\overline X-\theta}{\sigma(\theta)}>
\frac{\theta_0-\theta+\sigma(\theta_0)z_\alpha}{\sigma(\theta)}\right)\\
&=\boxed{1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{\theta_0-\theta}{\sigma(\theta)}+
\frac{\sigma(\theta_0)}{\sigma(\theta)}z_\alpha\right).}
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $\Phi$ is the <standard normal distribution>'s <cumulative distribution function>. The formula treats the binomial threshold as continuous; exact integer cutoffs and boundary randomization are finite-sample refinements.

Here $z_\alpha=1.96$, $\sigma(1/2)=1/(2\sqrt n)$ and $\sigma(3/4)=\sqrt3/(4\sqrt n)$. The desired approximate <power> is at least $0.975=\Phi(1.96)$ exactly when the argument of the upper tail in part (i) is at most $-1.96$:
$$
-\frac{\sqrt n}{\sqrt3}+\frac{2(1.96)}{\sqrt3}\leq-1.96.
$$
Rearranging and squaring the positive quantities yields
$$
\boxed{n\geq[1.96(2+\sqrt3)]^2\approx53.5066,\qquad n=54\text{ as the approximate design}.}
$$
This is a <normal approximation> to a <sample size> calculation. It is not a guarantee of the exact finite-sample <power> for an arbitrary unrandomized binomial cutoff; the discrete <test size> and <power> can instead be checked using binomial probabilities.

Given observed data $x$, a <credible interval> $[L(x),U(x)]$ has posterior <probability> $1-\alpha$:
$$
\boxed{\Pr\{L(x)\leq\theta\leq U(x)\mid x\}=1-\alpha.}
$$
For a continuous <posterior distribution> with <probability density function> $\pi(\theta\mid x)$, this is $\int_{L(x)}^{U(x)}\pi(\theta\mid x)\,d\theta=1-\alpha$. The probability is conditional on the observed data and concerns the <statistical parameter> under its <posterior distribution>; it does not assert frequentist <coverage probability>.

= ii
{parent=a}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

An <equal-tailed credible interval> allocates equal posterior <probability> to its two excluded tails:
$$
\Pr(\theta<L\mid x)=\Pr(\theta>U\mid x)=\alpha/2.
$$
For a continuous strictly increasing posterior <cumulative distribution function> $F$, the endpoints are $F^{-1}(\alpha/2)$ and $F^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)$.

A <highest posterior density interval> instead includes the parameter values of greatest posterior density: there is a cutoff $c$ such that the retained region is a superlevel set of $\pi(\theta\mid x)$, of posterior mass $1-\alpha$. Every interior retained value has density at least as large as every excluded value, apart from possible ties. For a unimodal continuous density this is an interval; when both endpoints lie inside the support they have equal posterior density. For a multimodal <posterior distribution>, a highest-density region may be a union of intervals, rather than one interval. **Equal tail probabilities and highest density are different criteria.**

= Solution
{parent=i}

Let $s=\sum_ix_i$, $A=a+s$ and $B=b+n$. Conditional independence gives the <Poisson distribution>'s <likelihood>
$$
L(\theta;x)=\frac{\theta^s e^{-n\theta}}{\prod_ix_i!}.
$$
Multiply by the prior <gamma distribution>'s density and discard factors independent of $\theta$:
$$
\pi(\theta\mid x)\propto\theta^{a+s-1}e^{-(b+n)\theta}.
$$
Normalizing with the <gamma function> identifies
$$
\boxed{\theta\mid x\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(A,B),\qquad
\pi(\theta\mid x)=\frac{B^A}{\Gamma(A)}\theta^{A-1}e^{-B\theta},\quad\theta>0.}
$$
Here $A=a+s$ is the shape and $B=b+n$ is the rate, not the scale. This is <conjugate prior> updating for a <Poisson distribution>.

= ii
{parent=b}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

The <mean> of a shape-rate <gamma distribution> is shape divided by rate. Hence the <posterior mean> is
$$
\mathbb E(\theta\mid x)=\frac{a+n\overline x}{b+n}
=\frac b{b+n}\frac ab+\frac n{b+n}\overline x.
$$
Both weights are positive and sum to one. This is a <convex combination> of the <prior mean> and the <sample mean>, proving
$$
\boxed{\min\{a/b,\overline x\}\leq\mathbb E(\theta\mid x)\leq\max\{a/b,\overline x\}.}
$$
For $n\geq1$ it lies strictly between them if the two means differ, and equals both if they agree. The rate $b$ acts as the prior weight relative to the $n$ observed exposure units.

Let $F$ be the <cumulative distribution function> of a <gamma distribution> with shape $A=a+\sum_ix_i$ and rate $B=b+n$; explicitly,
$$
F(t)=\frac{B^A}{\Gamma(A)}\int_0^t u^{A-1}e^{-Bu}\,du\quad(t\geq0).
$$
The <posterior distribution> is continuous and has positive density on $(0,\infty)$, so its <quantiles> give the requested <equal-tailed credible interval>:
$$
\boxed{[F^{-1}(\alpha/2),\ F^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)].}
$$
Equivalently, $2B\theta\mid x\sim\chi^2_{2A}$, so the endpoints can be written as the corresponding <chi-squared distribution> quantiles divided by $2B$. Either form excludes posterior <probability> $\alpha/2$ from each tail.

= Solution
{parent=iv}

**No.** A permitted counterexample is $a=1$ with all observed counts zero. Then $A=1$ and the <posterior distribution> is an <exponential distribution> with rate $B=b+n$, whose density decreases strictly from the left boundary. Its <equal-tailed credible interval> is
$$
[L,U]=\left[-\frac{\log(1-\alpha/2)}B,\ -\frac{\log(\alpha/2)}B\right].
$$
Here $L>0$. This excludes values just above zero whose posterior density is larger than that of retained values near $U$, violating the highest-density criterion. The actual <highest posterior density interval> starts at the support boundary and solves $1-e^{-Bh}=1-\alpha$:
$$
\boxed{[0,h],\qquad h=-\frac{\log\alpha}{B}.}
$$
Writing zero as the endpoint causes no problem because a single point has zero posterior <probability>. Thus even within the gamma family the two intervals need not agree. More generally, for an interior unimodal <gamma distribution>, highest-density endpoints have equal densities, whereas equal-tailed endpoints need not.

= Solution

Use $A=a+\sum_ix_i$ and $B=b+n$ as above. The future observation is independent of the old observations conditional on $\theta$, so the <posterior predictive probability> is obtained by integrating its <Poisson distribution> against the <posterior distribution> of $\theta$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Pr(X_{n+1}=k\mid x)
&=\int_0^\infty\frac{e^{-\theta}\theta^k}{k!}\frac{B^A}{\Gamma(A)}\theta^{A-1}e^{-B\theta}\,d\theta\\
&=\frac{B^A}{\Gamma(A)k!}\int_0^\infty\theta^{A+k-1}e^{-(B+1)\theta}\,d\theta\\
&=\boxed{\frac{\Gamma(A+k)}{\Gamma(A)k!}\left(\frac B{B+1}\right)^A
\left(\frac1{B+1}\right)^k,\quad k=0,1,\ldots.}
\end{aligned}
$$
The last integral is the normalizing <gamma function> integral, equal to $\Gamma(A+k)/(B+1)^{A+k}$. This is a <negative binomial distribution> in the count-of-failures convention with shape $A$ and success probability $B/(B+1)$; the gamma-function expression remains valid for noninteger $A>0$. As checks, its <mean> is $A/B$, and the <law of total variance> gives <variance> $A/B+A/B^2$. The additional term is the contribution of posterior uncertainty in $\theta$.
