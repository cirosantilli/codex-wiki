= Fluorescence
{wiki}

<Fluorescence> is <photon emission> following <photon absorption> by matter: an <excited state> releases some of its <energy> as <light>. A <fluorophore> can therefore mark where something is, while its <fluorescence emission spectrum> and <fluorescence lifetime> report different aspects of its environment. <Fluorescein> is one example; <FRET> exploits a competing route for excitation <energy> rather than requiring a second emitted <photon>.

The usual molecular sequence is excitation, relaxation, then emission, as in the <Jablonski diagram>. The emitted <photon> commonly has lower <energy> and longer <wavelength>, giving a <Stokes shift>; the exceptions are discussed below.<What is the Stokes Shift? by Edinburgh Instruments>[Stokes Shift in Fluorescence Spectroscopy, Figure 4 and relaxation discussion]{ref}

{parent=Fluorescence}

Spectroscopy measuring emitted light following excitation. Emission peak position and intensity can report changes of speciation, but concentration, excitation <absorption> and photochemical loss also influence the signal.

= Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching
{parent=Fluorescence}

<fluorescence recovery after photobleaching> follows fluorescence return to a bleached region as unbleached labeled molecules redistribute. Recovery depends on geometry, transport, binding and immobile fractions; it is not a direct measurement of every aspect of membrane fluidity.

= Fluorescence anisotropy
{parent=Fluorescence}
{title2=$r=(I_\parallel-I_\perp)/(I_\parallel+2I_\perp)$}

<fluorescence anisotropy> measures the polarization dependence of emitted <fluorescence> after polarized excitation. It can constrain rotational freedom on the fluorescence-lifetime timescale, but probe orientation, lifetime and partitioning also affect the signal.

= Fluorescence correlation spectroscopy
{parent=Fluorescence}

<fluorescence correlation spectroscopy> analyzes fluctuations of detected <fluorescence> to infer quantities such as molecular number and movement through an observation volume. Photophysical fluctuations and background must be distinguished from transport.

= Quenching
{synonym}

A reduction in <fluorescence> caused by a process competing with <photon emission>, for example a collision that removes excitation <energy> without emitting a <photon>. Measuring less <light> because fewer excitation <photons> reach the sample does not by itself establish fluorescence quenching.

= Static fluorescence quenching
{parent=Quenching (fluorescence)}

Loss of fluorescent population through nonemitting ground-state association. In a simple static-only case the surviving unbound emitters keep their lifetime, distinguishing it from dynamic quenching; mixtures and association-dependent spectra complicate that test.

= Dynamic fluorescence quenching
{parent=Quenching (fluorescence)}

Quenching by an excited-state encounter that introduces an additional decay rate $k_Q[Q]$. It reduces both <fluorescence quantum yield> and lifetime in the simple single-population model.

= Stern-Volmer equation
{parent=Quenching (fluorescence)}
{c}
{title2=$\Phi_0/\Phi_Q=1+k_Q\tau_0[Q]$}

For purely dynamic quenching of a single emitting population, $\Phi_0/\Phi_Q=\tau_0/\tau_Q=1+k_Q\tau_0[Q]$. The slope measures $k_Q\tau_0$, so determining a bimolecular quenching constant requires the unquenched lifetime or equivalent independent rate information.

= Fluorescence dequenching
{parent=Quenching (fluorescence)}

An increase in <fluorescence> caused by reducing a quenching interaction. Dye dilution can reduce <Förster resonance energy transfer>; the signal alone need not identify the process that diluted the dyes.

= Fluorophore
{parent=Fluorescence}
{wiki}

A <fluorophore> is a fluorescent <molecule> or fluorescent part of a <molecule>. Its usefulness as a label depends on how efficiently it absorbs the chosen excitation <light>, its <fluorescence quantum yield>, and its resistance to <photobleaching>. A large <fluorescence quantum yield> alone does not guarantee a bright image if few excitation <photons> are absorbed or collected.

= Jablonski diagram
{parent=Fluorescence}
{c}
{wiki}

A <Jablonski diagram> arranges molecular <energy eigenstates> vertically and draws arrows for changes between them. In the usual closed-shell molecular example, $S_0$ is the electronic <ground state>, $S_1$ and $S_2$ are electronic <excited states> with <singlet state> spin, and $T_1$ has <triplet state> spin. Each electronic state also has levels of <molecular vibration>. The diagram records possible processes, not molecular paths in space.<Advanced Fluorescence Microscopy Techniques—FRAP, FLIP, FLAP, FRET and FLIM by Hellen C. Ishikawa-Ankerhold and others>[§1.1.1, Figure 2B; §1.1.2, Figure 4: singlet and triplet levels]{ref}

<Photon absorption> can populate an upper level of <molecular vibration> in $S_1$. Relaxation removes excess <energy> without <photon emission>; <fluorescence> commonly starts from relaxed $S_1$.<What is the Stokes Shift? by Edinburgh Instruments>[Stokes Shift in Fluorescence Spectroscopy, Figure 4 and its discussion]{ref} The drawing also includes relaxation from a higher electronic <excited state> and a competing spin-changing route to a <triplet state>.

= Stokes shift
{parent=Fluorescence}
{c}
{wiki}
{title2=$\Delta\lambda=\lambda_{\rm em,max}-\lambda_{\rm abs,max}$}

The <Stokes shift> compares the lowest absorption-band maximum with the <fluorescence emission spectrum> maximum. Relaxation often makes $\Delta\lambda>0$. This comparison differs from using an arbitrary excitation <wavelength>. A spectral-density maximum can move when changing from per unit <wavelength> to per unit <spectroscopic wavenumber>; specify the spectral convention.<What is the Stokes Shift? by Edinburgh Instruments>[Stokes Shift in Fluorescence Spectroscopy, definition, spectral-coordinate caveat, Figures 2 and 4]{ref}

An original illustrative <energy> budget makes the distinction concrete. Excitation at $450\,\mathrm{nm}$ and emission at $520\,\mathrm{nm}$ correspond to about $2.76$ and $2.38\,\mathrm{eV}$ per <photon>, using $E=h c/\lambda$ with <Planck constant> $h$ and <speed of light> $c$ in <vacuum>. The difference, about $0.37\,\mathrm{eV}$, must go elsewhere in the complete <energy> balance. These chosen <wavelengths> are not measured band maxima for a particular <fluorophore>.

Anti-Stokes emission has shorter <wavelength> than excitation. Additional <energy>, for example from a thermally populated level of <molecular vibration> or multiple excitation <photons>, makes it possible. The one-photon, initially relaxed drawing does not cover these cases.<Advanced Fluorescence Microscopy Techniques—FRAP, FLIP, FLAP, FRET and FLIM by Hellen C. Ishikawa-Ankerhold and others>[§1.1.1, final paragraphs: anti-Stokes emission and multiphoton excitation]{ref}

= Fluorescence excitation spectrum
{parent=Fluorescence}
{title2=$F_{\rm ex}(\lambda_{\rm ex})$}

A <fluorescence excitation spectrum> scans excitation <wavelength> while detecting <fluorescence> in a fixed emission band. A <fluorescence emission spectrum> does the converse. The inspected spectrofluorometer example uses an excitation <monochromator> scan with the emission <monochromator> fixed, and reverses their roles for emission.<What are Absorption, Excitation and Emission Spectra? by Edinburgh Instruments>[What are Absorption, Excitation and Emission Spectra?, Excitation Spectra, PDF pp. 2–3, Figures 3–4; Emission Spectra, p. 4, Figures 5–6]{ref}

An operational model explains why this need not reproduce absorption. For one dilute, unsaturated emitting species, write $a(\lambda)$ for absorbed fraction, $R_{\rm in}(\lambda)$ for incident <photon> rate, $\Phi(\lambda)$ for <fluorescence quantum yield>, and $\eta_{\rm band}(\lambda)$ for the fraction of emitted <photons> counted in the selected band. The background-subtracted count rate is
$$
C_{\rm ex}(\lambda)=R_{\rm in}(\lambda)a(\lambda)\Phi(\lambda)\eta_{\rm band}(\lambda).
$$
Only after correcting incident <photon> rate, and with constant yield and emission-band collection, is this proportional to absorbed fraction. A nonfluorescent absorber contributes to absorption but not this readout. Thick samples, multiple emitting species, and reabsorption of <fluorescence> complicate the model.

= Fluorescence emission spectrum
{parent=Fluorescence}
{title2=$F_{\rm em}(\lambda_{\rm em})$}

A <fluorescence emission spectrum> resolves emitted <photons> by <wavelength> at fixed excitation settings. Subtract a matched sample-container and solvent background, and correct the wavelength-dependent throughput and <photodetector> sensitivity when estimating the emitted distribution. A camera image through one <fluorescence emission filter> instead measures a weighted band <integral>, not a whole <fluorescence emission spectrum>. A <fluorescence lifetime> resolves delay after excitation; its horizontal axis is <time>, not <wavelength>.

= Fluorescence quantum yield
{parent=Fluorescence}
{title2=$\Phi$}

<Fluorescence quantum yield> counts emitted fluorescent <photons> per absorbed excitation <photon>. It differs from <quantum efficiency>, which describes the <photodetector>.<Advanced Fluorescence Microscopy Techniques—FRAP, FLIP, FLAP, FRET and FLIM by Hellen C. Ishikawa-Ankerhold and others>[§1.1.2, Quantum yield, equation 2 and the quantum-efficiency distinction]{ref}

For an original constant-rate model, let $k_{\rm r}$ be the radiative decay rate and $k_{\rm nr}$ the sum of competing rates without fluorescent <photon emission>. An initial population $N_0$ in the emitting <excited state> obeys the <rate equation>
$$
\frac{dN}{dt}=-(k_{\rm r}+k_{\rm nr})N,\qquad
N(t)=N_0e^{-t/\tau},\qquad
\tau=\frac{1}{k_{\rm r}+k_{\rm nr}}.
$$
Integrating $k_{\rm r}N(t)$ over all <time> gives
$$
\Phi=\frac{1}{N_0}\int_0^\infty k_{\rm r}N(t)\,dt
=\frac{k_{\rm r}}{k_{\rm r}+k_{\rm nr}}=k_{\rm r}\tau.
$$
Here every absorbed excitation is assumed to reach this emitting population; additional losses before that stage require another factor. Under steady weak excitation, absorbed <photon> rate $R_{\rm abs}$ and total detection fraction $\eta$ give $C=\eta\Phi R_{\rm abs}$. The count rate therefore mixes excitation, yield, and detection, whereas $\tau$ specifies a decay timescale.

= Photobleaching
{parent=Fluorescence}
{wiki}

<Photobleaching> is an irreversible loss of fluorescent capacity caused by illumination. It changes the number of active <fluorophores> over experimental <time>; <fluorescence lifetime> concerns the delay after an individual excitation. Reversible dark states and ordinary return to the <ground state> are different processes.

In a stipulated population-loss model, let $T$ denote elapsed illumination <time> and $t$ denote delay after a pulse. If active population declines as $e^{-k_{\rm b}T}$ while surviving <fluorophores> retain the same decay rates, then $I(t;T)=A_0e^{-k_{\rm b}T}e^{-t/\tau}$. Brightness falls while $\tau$ stays fixed. This example does not assert that all bleaching follows <exponential decay>: chemical changes and selective loss from a mixture can also change the observed decay shape. The existing <FRET photobleaching controls> illustrate why deliberate bleaching requires careful interpretation.

= Fluorescence lifetime
{parent=Fluorescence}
{title2=$\tau$}

For the constant-rate model in <fluorescence quantum yield>, <fluorescence lifetime> is the mean residence <time> in the emitting <excited state> and the $1/e$ timescale of its <exponential decay>. The detected fluorescent <photon> rate after an ideal instantaneous pulse is $I(t)=\eta k_{\rm r}N_0e^{-t/\tau}$ for $t\geq0$. Its amplitude and its decay timescale are separate quantities.

For a stipulated example, rates $(k_{\rm r},k_{\rm nr})=(0.05,0.20)\,\mathrm{ns}^{-1}$ give $\tau=4\,\mathrm{ns}$ and $\Phi=0.20$. Rates $(0.20,0.05)\,\mathrm{ns}^{-1}$ give the same $\tau$ but $\Phi=0.80$. Conversely, doubling $N_0$ doubles the ideal amplitude without changing $\tau$. Equal <fluorescence lifetimes> need not imply equal brightness or equal <fluorescence quantum yields>.

Different environments can supply several populations, giving $I(t)=\sum_j A_j e^{-t/\tau_j}$ rather than one <exponential decay>. A fitted single timescale then depends on the selected model and detection band. <FRET donor lifetime> uses the change in a donor's decay when transfer introduces an additional loss route; the <FRET> branch develops that interpretation.

= TCSPC
{synonym}
{c}

<TCSPC> accumulates a <histogram> of detected <photon> delays relative to repeated excitation pulses. Each cycle supplies a timing reference; many cycles provide the decay distribution. The <fluorescence instrument response> broadens that distribution. In conventional first-event acquisition, losing later <photons> in a cycle preferentially preserves early arrivals: this pile-up can falsely shorten the inferred <fluorescence lifetime>. Keep detection probability per cycle low and check stability when the illumination is attenuated.<Time-Correlated Single Photon Counting (TCSPC) by PicoQuant>[How Does TCSPC Work?, delay histogram, instrument response and first-photon pile-up paragraphs]{ref}

= Fluorescence instrument response
{parent=Time-correlated single-photon counting}
{title2=$R(t)$}

A <fluorescence instrument response> is the distribution of recorded delays for an effectively prompt optical signal. Finite excitation-pulse width and timing variation in the <photodetector> and electronics contribute. With a normalized response $R$, intrinsic fluorescent <photon> rate $I$, and background rate $B$, the expected trace is
$$
M(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}R(u)I(t-u)\,du+B(t),\qquad
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}R(u)\,du=1.
$$
Fit a candidate decay after this <convolution> with the measured response; treating the broadened leading edge as the intrinsic decay can bias <fluorescence lifetime>. The accessible technical note describes measuring prompt scattered excitation with a nonfluorescent scattering sample.<Time-Correlated Single Photon Counting (technical note v. 2.1) by Michael Wahl>[Michael Wahl, Time-Correlated Single Photon Counting, v. 2.1, p. 3: scattering-medium response measurement and iterative reconvolution]{ref}

= Fluorescence lifetime measurement with pulsed excitation
{parent=Time-correlated single-photon counting}

The inspected apparatus sends picosecond diode-<laser> pulses into a sample cuvette. A neutral attenuation filter keeps detected events rare; an emission cut-off filter rejects scattered excitation. Collection optics deliver <fluorescence> to a <photodetector>; detector pulses and the laser-driver synchronization signal feed the timing electronics. The illustrated arrangement uses a PDL 800-B driver and TimeHarp 200 electronics. The note's recorded comparison uses two dye solutions and a prompt instrument-response trace; the dye tails have distinct slopes on a logarithmic display. Its Figure 9 measurement specifies an 80 MHz source, separately from the Figure 8 driver-setting example.<Time-Correlated Single Photon Counting (technical note v. 2.1) by Michael Wahl>[Michael Wahl, Time-Correlated Single Photon Counting, v. 2.1, pp. 7–9, Figures 8–9: cuvette/filter/detector setup; Toluidine Blue and Oxazin 4 curves; explicit 80 MHz measurement setting]{ref}

For a defensible new measurement, separately acquire detector background with excitation blocked, a solvent/container blank under excitation, and a prompt response appropriate to the timing channel. Compare successively attenuated acquisitions to expose pile-up; compare early and late acquisition intervals to expose <photobleaching>. Choose a repetition period long enough for the relevant decay or include residual population from earlier pulses in the model. These are proposed checks, not additional controls claimed for the published curves. <Photon shot noise>, background mismatch, and uncertainty in the <fluorescence instrument response> limit how uniquely multiple decay components can be inferred.

= Fluorescence microscope
{parent=Fluorescence}
{wiki}

A <fluorescence microscope> maps <photon emission> to an image. Excitation <light> passes through a <fluorescence excitation filter> and reflects from a wavelength-selective <beam splitter> into the objective <optical lens>. Returning <fluorescence> passes through that <beam splitter> and a <fluorescence emission filter> to the <photodetector>. Rejecting excitation makes faint emission visible.<Advanced Fluorescence Microscopy Techniques—FRAP, FLIP, FLAP, FRET and FLIM by Hellen C. Ishikawa-Ankerhold and others>[§1.2.1, Figure 5 and excitation/emission-filter paragraphs]{ref}

An ordinary image records emission-band counts at each <pixel>. Adding pulse-delay measurements gives a <fluorescence lifetime> map; scanning emission <wavelength> gives spectral information instead. <Confocal microscopy> adds spatial rejection and <confocal optical sectioning>, as developed in its own branch. An unlabeled specimen under the same illumination reveals intrinsic <fluorescence> and excitation leakage; excitation-blocked frames reveal detector background. A blank cannot perfectly predict background in a differently prepared specimen.

= Fluorescence excitation filter
{parent=Fluorescence microscope}

A <fluorescence excitation filter> selects the illumination <wavelength> band reaching the specimen. Its finite transmission changes the absorbed <photon> rate and thus image brightness. Specify that band when interpreting a <fluorescence excitation spectrum> or comparing images.

= Fluorescence emission filter
{parent=Fluorescence microscope}

A <fluorescence emission filter> transmits the chosen emitted <wavelength> band while rejecting excitation leakage. Finite rejection, overlap of the <fluorescence emission spectrum> with excitation, and the <photodetector>'s wavelength-dependent response can all affect the count. A darker filtered image alone does not establish a changed <fluorescence lifetime> or increased <photobleaching>.

= Confocal microscopy
{parent=Fluorescence microscope}
{wiki}

<Confocal microscopy> forms an image by scanning a localized illumination spot and preferentially detecting <light> from the same small region. In a thick specimen emitting <fluorescence>, a conventional <fluorescence microscope> also collects a haze from material away from the selected <optical focus>. <Confocal optical sectioning> suppresses that haze, revealing structure at a chosen depth without cutting the specimen. The useful idea is a paired selection: where excitation occurs, and which emitted <photons> reach the <photodetector>. <Introductory Confocal Concepts by Nikon Instruments Inc.>[Introductory Confocal Concepts, opening paragraphs and Fig. 2: out-of-focus fluorescence and optical sections]{ref}

The scope here is single-point, linear <fluorescence> imaging. <Confocal microscopy> can also use reflected <light>; its broader implementations need not all have the same scanning mechanism. The patent https://patents.google.com/patent/US3013467A/en[Microscopy apparatus, US3013467A, by Marvin Minsky] describes a point source imaged onto a specimen, a second pinhole receiving its image, specimen scanning, and a synchronized display. It was filed on 7 November 1957 and published on 19 December 1961; the filing date should not be described as the grant date. This is evidence for that apparatus and those dates, rather than a claim that all later <fluorescence> instruments were already present in it. <Microscopy apparatus by Marvin Minsky>[Front-page filing/publication metadata; description, Optical System, Fig. 1; Stage Scanning System]{ref}

= Conjugate pinholes in confocal microscopy
{parent=Confocal microscopy}

Two planes are optically conjugate when an <optical lens> system images one onto the other. In <confocal microscopy>, an illumination pinhole is imaged onto the selected specimen point, and that specimen point is imaged onto the detection pinhole. Thus the two <apertures> select corresponding positions in different planes; they need not occupy the same physical plane. Modern <laser> instruments can supply the illumination point with an <optical fiber> or a suitable focused <laser> mode instead of a separate metal illumination pinhole. <Confocal microscopy (chapter manuscript) by Brad Amos and others>[Amos, McConnell and Wilson, Confocal microscopy, manuscript pp. 5, 7–8: conjugate foci, Fig. 3, fiber-delivered illumination]{ref}

A fluorescent emitter at the selected <optical focus> forms a compact <point spread function> at the detection <aperture>. An emitter displaced axially produces a broadened spot there through <defocus>; a smaller fraction of its collected <light> passes. The emitter still exists and still emits <photons>. Rejected <photons> strike the opaque mask or other parts of the instrument instead of contributing to the accepted detector channel. Neither a finite pinhole nor <diffraction> makes the accepted out-of-focus contribution exactly zero.

= Confocal optical sectioning
{parent=Confocal microscopy}

At one depth, scan the illumination spot across $x$ and $y$ and assign the detector reading during each dwell interval to a <pixel>. Move the specimen or objective <optical lens> axially to acquire the next depth. A stack of these optical sections samples three-dimensional structure. With beam scanning, the returning <fluorescence> commonly retraces the scanning optics: this descanning holds the image of the currently illuminated point at the detection pinhole. With stage scanning, the fixed spot and pinhole remain aligned while the specimen moves. <Confocal microscopy (chapter manuscript) by Brad Amos and others>[Introduction, manuscript p. 2; Fig. 3 and caption, pp. 7–8: scanning and stationary return beam]{ref}

An optical section is a depth-weighted image of an intact specimen, with a finite axial response and tails. A physical cut actually separates a slice of material; its thickness and cutting damage are properties of the preparation. Changing <optical focus> selects another optical section without removing a slice, although illumination can still damage a specimen. Neither a thin physical slice nor a sharply displayed optical section makes the imaging <point spread function> infinitely narrow. <Introductory Confocal Concepts by Nikon Instruments Inc.>[Fig. 2 discussion: focused-light sections rather than physical sectioning]{ref}

Point response and section thickness answer different questions. For a spatially uniform, very thin fluorescent sheet, its response versus axial displacement is proportional to the lateral <integral> of the three-dimensional confocal response,
$$
R_{\rm sheet}(z)\propto\int_{\mathbb R^2}H_a(x,y,z)\,dx\,dy.
$$
Consequently a trace through a single bead, $H_a(0,0,z)$, does not by itself give the sheet's optical-section thickness. State which object and which <full width at half maximum> are being measured. A smaller axial sampling step gives more samples of the same response, rather than automatically improving <diffraction-limited resolution>. <Confocal microscopy (chapter manuscript) by Brad Amos and others>[Appendix §6, manuscript pp. 42–43: thin fluorescent sheet translated through focus]{ref}

= Confocal excitation and detection point spread function
{parent=Confocal microscopy}
{title2=$H_a(\mathbf r)=E(\mathbf r)D_a(\mathbf r)$}

For an isolated fluorescent molecule at displacement $\mathbf r$ from the scanning <optical focus>, write $E(\mathbf r)$ for relative excitation <light intensity> and $D_a(\mathbf r)$ for the fraction of its collected emission passed by a centred detection pinhole of radius $a$. The expected response, apart from a common brightness and collection factor, is
$$
H_a(\mathbf r)=E(\mathbf r)D_a(\mathbf r),\qquad
D_a(\mathbf r)=\int_{|\mathbf u|\leq a}p_{\rm em}(\mathbf u;\mathbf r)\,d^2\mathbf u,\qquad
\int_{\mathbb R^2}p_{\rm em}(\mathbf u;\mathbf r)\,d^2\mathbf u=1.
$$
Here $\mathbf u$ is position in the detection-pinhole plane and $p_{\rm em}$ is the emitted <light intensity> distribution there conditional on collection, with units of inverse <area>. This <point spread function normalization> retains throughput: $D_a$ is a fraction, not a curve rescaled to have peak one. The product expresses excitation followed by detection; it is an intensity/probability product, not multiplication of coherent <wave amplitudes> from different molecules. <Confocal microscopy (chapter manuscript) by Brad Amos and others>[Manuscript p. 16, Fig. 4 discussion; Appendix §5, p. 37, Eq. A17: illumination-times-detection response]{ref}

Assume linear, unsaturated <fluorescence>, independent emitters whose <light intensities> add, a stationary specimen, fixed collection efficiency and <quantum efficiency>, and negligible changes from scattering, absorption or bleaching during a scan. Different excitation and emission <wavelengths> generally give different excitation and detection responses. For a sufficiently small pinhole, $D_a$ is approximately its <area> times $p_{\rm em}$ at the centre; this is the familiar excitation-times-detection <point spread function>. It is not universally the square of one <point spread function>. A spatially invariant response further allows the image to be expressed as a <convolution> with the emitter distribution; depth-dependent <optical aberrations> can invalidate that approximation.

For an explicitly synthetic axial example, put $t=z/z_0$ and choose an on-axis excitation $E(t)=1/(1+t^2)$, inspired by a <Gaussian beam>. Stipulate a circular <Gaussian function> for the detection-plane <light intensity>, with standard deviation $s(t)=s_0\sqrt{1+t^2}$. In dimensionless coordinates $\mathbf v=\mathbf u/s_0$, put $\alpha=a/s_0$. Then
$$
p(\mathbf v;t)=\frac{\exp[-|\mathbf v|^2/(2(1+t^2))]}{2\pi(1+t^2)},\qquad
D_\alpha(t)=1-\exp\!\left[-\frac{\alpha^2}{2(1+t^2)}\right],\qquad
H_\alpha(t)=\frac{D_\alpha(t)}{1+t^2}.
$$
The expression for $D_\alpha$ follows by integrating $p$ over a <disk> using <polar coordinates>. The assumptions specify a toy model with matched axial scales; its parameters are not a calibration for a real objective <optical lens>. It omits <Airy disk> rings, vector <diffraction>, and unequal excitation and emission <wavelengths>.

For a vanishingly small pinhole, the peak-normalized synthetic response tends to $(1+t^2)^{-2}$, whose <full width at half maximum> is $2\sqrt{\sqrt2-1}\,z_0\simeq1.2872z_0$. = Confocal pinhole throughput
{parent=Confocal microscopy}

Opening the pinhole increases accepted in-focus and out-of-focus <photons>; closing it strengthens <confocal optical sectioning> while discarding some useful in-focus <photons> too. In the synthetic model, $\alpha=0.5,1.5,4$ transmits approximately $0.1175,0.6753,0.9997$ of the collected in-focus emission. At $t=2$ the corresponding peak-normalized signals are about $0.0420,0.0597,0.1597$. These are calculated fractions under stated assumptions, not instrument measurements.

An Airy unit conventionally specifies a pinhole diameter equal to the first-zero diameter of the emission <Airy disk> in that plane. For an ideal circular pupil,
$$
d_{\rm AU}\simeq1.22\,\frac{\lambda_{\rm em}|M|}{\mathrm{NA}},
$$
where $M$ is the total specimen-to-pinhole <magnification>, $\lambda_{\rm em}$ the emission <wavelength> in <vacuum>, and $\mathrm{NA}$ the objective <numerical aperture>. A software setting needs its specified <wavelength> and relay <magnification>; it is not a universal micrometre diameter. <Confocal microscopy (chapter manuscript) by Brad Amos and others>[Appendix §6, manuscript pp. 42–43: Airy unit defined between the focal spot's zeros and conversion to real units]{ref}

For ideal independent counting, accepted source and background means $S$ and $B$ give <photon shot noise> with <variance> $S+B$ and <signal-to-noise ratio in photon counting> $S/\sqrt{S+B}$. Closing a pinhole need not improve that ratio: it depends on how both $S$ and $B$ change. Increasing exposure or excitation to replace discarded <photons> can cause bleaching, an irreversible loss of fluorescent emission capacity, or damage. Detection rejection occurs after excitation and therefore does not undo excitation outside the selected depth. Such material can absorb illumination and emit <photons> even when their emission is omitted from the recorded image.

= Fluorescent bead experiment for confocal microscopy
{parent=Confocal microscopy}

A useful motivating experiment moves a bead emitting <fluorescence> through the selected <optical focus> and records the detector signal against position. <Measurement of point-spread function (PSF) for confocal fluorescence microscopy by InCheon Song and others> (2005, §2 and Fig. 1) describes a $543.8\,\mathrm{nm}$ <laser> delivered through a single-mode <optical fiber>, collimation, beam-size adjustment and a dichroic <beam splitter>. A $100\times$, <numerical aperture> $1.3$ oil-immersion objective <optical lens> excites slide-mounted fluorescent beads and collects their emission. Emission passes a filter and collecting <optical lens>, then a pinhole, before an avalanche <photodiode>. A three-dimensional positioning stage moves the specimen. The apparatus also contains a phase plate for deliberately modified responses.<Measurement of point-spread function (PSF) for confocal fluorescence microscopy by InCheon Song and others>[§2 and Fig. 1, pp. 2–3: apparatus and readout]{ref}

The published comparison uses nominal bead diameters $0.17,0.5,1\,\mathrm{\mu m}$: larger beads give broader measured spots. This is a control on the finite emitter, because the image is the <convolution> of bead emission with the confocal <point spread function>. The smallest bead has weaker <signal> relative to <noise>; incorrect bead size, <defocus>, bleaching and <optical aberrations> limit inference from a restored response. The reported bead-size comparison concerns lateral responses; it is not a measured pinhole-size series or a measured sheet-sectioning curve.<Measurement of point-spread function (PSF) for confocal fluorescence microscopy by InCheon Song and others>[§3.2 and Figs. 4–5, pp. 4–5; p. 6, paragraph before §3.3: error sources]{ref}

To test axial pinhole selection with this kind of apparatus, the following is a proposed extension, not an additional result from that paper. Choose an isolated bead small relative to the response, centre it laterally, then step $z$ through <optical focus> while recording background-subtracted signal at several pinhole settings. Hold excitation <power>, dwell interval, detector gain, emission band and sampling fixed. Preserve raw peaks before also plotting peak-normalized traces. A lower peak and faster relative decay with axial displacement would support the paired-selection mechanism; fitting only normalized widths would miss the throughput cost.

Measure blocked-excitation background, include a bead-free region, avoid detector saturation, and repeat scans in reversed order to reveal drift or bleaching. Check pinhole centring separately, since misalignment can mimic signal loss. Record bead diameter, immersion medium, <refractive index>, <numerical aperture>, <wavelengths> and axial step. A finite bead is an approximation to an <optical point source>; a point trace does not certify <confocal optical sectioning> of a whole fluorescent sheet. Depth-dependent <spherical aberration>, scattering and sample motion can broaden or distort the response. Increasing <magnification> or making a display brighter does not correct those effects.
