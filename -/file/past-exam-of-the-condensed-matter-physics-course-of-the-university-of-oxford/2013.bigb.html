== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The sites form a <face-centered cubic lattice> decorated by an identical two-atom <atomic basis of a crystal>, at fractional displacements $0$ and $\boldsymbol\delta=(1/4,1/4,1/4)$. This is the <diamond cubic structure>; the eight atoms in its conventional cube are not eight independent Bravais-lattice points.

For identical silicon <atomic scattering factors> $f$, the conventional-cell <structure factor> separates into the FCC factor and the two-site motif factor:
$$
F_{hkl}=f\left[1+(-1)^{h+k}+(-1)^{h+l}+(-1)^{k+l}\right]
\left[1+e^{i\pi(h+k+l)/2}\right].
$$
The first factor vanishes unless $h,k,l$ are all even or all odd. For all-even indices, the motif also vanishes if $h+k+l=4r+2$; it survives if the sum is divisible by four. All-odd indices survive, with motif modulus $\sqrt2$. Thus $(100)$ and $(110)$ are absent by centering, $(200)$ is absent by the diamond basis, and $(111)$ and $(220)$ are allowed.

For a cubic cell, $d_{hkl}=a/\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}$. At fixed <X-ray> <wavelength>, <Bragg's law> makes the smallest scattering angles correspond to the smallest allowed positive values of $h^2+k^2+l^2$. These start with $3$ and $8$, so
$$
\boxed{\text{first line }\{111\},\qquad\text{second line }\{220\}.}
$$
The braces denote whole powder families, including their symmetry-equivalent plane normals. The next family is $\{311\}$, with squared-index sum $11$; using the FCC rule alone would incorrectly insert a $(200)$ line before $(220)$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Interpret the reported angles as total scattering angles $\psi=2\theta$. The photon <energy> gives
$$
\lambda=\frac{hc}{E_\gamma}=1.23984\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm m.
$$
Combining $2d\sin\theta=\lambda$ with the two index assignments yields
$$
a_{111}=\frac{\sqrt3\lambda}{2\sin11.4^\circ}=0.54323\,\mathrm{nm},\qquad
 a_{220}=\frac{\sqrt8\lambda}{2\sin18.8^\circ}=0.54409\,\mathrm{nm}.
$$
The slight difference is consistent with the rounded angular data, giving
$$
\boxed{a\simeq0.544\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
The intensity estimate needs a stated convention. The geometric <structure factors> obey $|F_{111}|^2=32|f_{111}|^2$ and $|F_{220}|^2=64|f_{220}|^2$, so equal atomic factors give a **single-directed-reflection** ratio $1/2$. For a powder line, the <powder diffraction multiplicities> are eight for $\{111\}$ and twelve for $\{220\}$. Hence a multiplicity-and-structure-factor estimate is
$$
\boxed{\frac{I_{111}}{I_{220}}\simeq\frac{8\cdot32}{12\cdot64}=\frac13}
$$
if other angle-dependent factors are neglected.

A measured integrated powder intensity also contains the <Lorentz-polarization factor>, atomic form factor, thermal-motion factor and instrument/sample corrections. For example, in the common unpolarized symmetric powder geometry,
$$
LP(\theta)\propto\frac{1+\cos^22\theta}{\sin^2\theta\cos\theta},\qquad
\frac{LP(11.4^\circ)}{LP(18.8^\circ)}=2.9174.
$$
Including this factor but still taking equal atomic factors gives $I_{111}/I_{220}\simeq0.9725$, rather than $1/3$. More generally,
$$
\frac{I_{111}}{I_{220}}\simeq0.9725
\left|\frac{f_{111}}{f_{220}}\right|^2e^{-2(W_{111}-W_{220})}
$$
within that geometry, before other corrections. The actual form factors and measurement geometry are not supplied, so no unique precision experimental peak-height ratio follows. The values $1/2$, $1/3$ and $0.9725$ refer to distinct explicitly stated levels of approximation; a powder family must not be silently treated as one directed reflection.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

At fixed radiation <wavelength>, differentiating <Bragg's law> gives $\cot\theta\,d\theta=-da/a$. With constant linear <thermal expansion coefficient> $\alpha_L$,
$$
\frac{\Delta a}{a}\simeq\alpha_L\Delta T=0.0018,
\qquad
\boxed{\Delta\psi=2\Delta\theta\simeq-2\alpha_L\Delta T\tan\theta.}
$$
Both scattering angles decrease as the lattice expands. Substitution gives
$$
\boxed{\Delta\psi_{111}\simeq-0.0416^\circ,\qquad
\Delta\psi_{220}\simeq-0.0702^\circ.}
$$
These are shifts of $2\theta$, not of the Bragg angle alone. As a finite-step check, using $a'=a(1+0.0018)$ gives $\psi'=2\arcsin[\sin(\psi/2)/(1.0018)]$. It produces shifts $-0.04151^\circ$ and $-0.07008^\circ$, consistent with the first-order estimate. A strictly constant differential coefficient would instead give $a'/a=e^{\alpha_L\Delta T}$; its difference from the linear length approximation is second order in this small expansion.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

<Diffraction thermal-expansion thermometry> needs a stable monochromatic <X-ray> beam, controlled collimation, an accurately calibrated angular scale, and an X-ray-sensitive detector with enough counts to fit reproducible line centroids. Crystal monochromators or appropriate <energy> selection are relevant here; an apparatus for visible-light interference does not directly measure these <X-ray> peaks. Sample displacement and angular-zero drift should be checked with a suitable reference standard.

The first-order angular sensitivities are about $6.93\times10^{-5}$ and $1.17\times10^{-4}$ degrees per kelvin, respectively. A one-kelvin inference therefore requires comparable centroid stability, with high count statistics and control of systematic errors; a centroid can be fitted more precisely than the line width. Fractional <wavelength> drift also mimics lattice expansion, so the desired <temperature> precision constrains $\Delta\lambda/\lambda$ relative to $\alpha_L\Delta T$.

The powder must be at a known uniform <temperature> and allowed to equilibrate. Mechanical stress, clamping, composition changes, oxidation and phase changes can also shift or broaden peaks. Accurate work requires a calibrated $a(T)$, including the actual <temperature> dependence of the <thermal expansion coefficient>, rather than assuming the supplied constant describes every <temperature> exactly. Using both lines provides a consistency check on a common cubic expansion.

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $C=\alpha q^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$ with $\alpha=2\ln2$. Relative to a chosen positive ion, successive neighbours have alternating charge. The sum of its interactions with all other ions is $-C/R$. Summing this over all $2N$ ions would count every pair twice, so the total electrostatic <energy> is $-NC/R$, or $-C/R$ per ion pair.

There are $2N$ nearest-neighbour bonds in a periodic chain of $N$ pairs; boundary corrections are negligible for large $N$. If $A/R^n$ is the <energy> of **each** such bond, the repulsive contribution per ion pair is $2A/R^n$. Therefore the <alternating-charge chain cohesive energy> is
$$
\boxed{u(R)=\frac{U}{N}=-\frac{\alpha q^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0R}+\frac{2A}{R^n}.}
$$
The factor two in the short-range term counts two bonds per ion pair. If a repulsion parameter were instead defined as the whole pair's repulsive coefficient, it would equal $2A$ in this convention; changing that definition must not silently change the equilibrium formula. The absolute zero of <energy> is separated ions for a genuinely decaying repulsion.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For a repulsive short-range potential and a stable finite minimum, assume $A>0$ and integer $n>1$. Setting $u'(R)=C/R^2-2nA/R^{n+1}=0$ gives
$$
\boxed{R_0=\left(\frac{2nA}{C}\right)^{1/(n-1)}
=\left(\frac{8\pi\epsilon_0nA}{\alpha q^2}\right)^{1/(n-1)}.}
$$
At this point $2A/R_0^n=C/(nR_0)$, so
$$
\boxed{u_0=-\frac{C}{R_0}\left(1-\frac1n\right),\qquad
u''(R_0)=\frac{(n-1)C}{R_0^3}>0.}
$$
Here $u''$ denotes the second derivative of the <energy> $u$, not a displacement variable. The negative equilibrium <energy> is the cohesive <energy> per pair relative to infinite separation.

The qualifiers on $A,n$ matter: an arbitrary real coefficient and integer exponent do not automatically describe stabilizing repulsion. For instance $A<0,n>1$ gives collapse as $R\to0$, whereas $n=1$ gives $u=(2A-C)/R$ and has no isolated finite equilibrium. The displayed minimum therefore applies to the physically repulsive, faster-than-Coulomb branch of the stated model.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For a one-dimensional free-electron gas of length $L$, allowed <wave vectors> have spacing $2\pi/L$. Including two <spin> states and occupied <wave vectors> from $-k_F$ to $+k_F$,
$$
N_e=2\frac{L}{2\pi}(2k_F),\qquad
\boxed{k_F=\frac{\pi n_e}{2},\qquad
E_F=\frac{\hbar^2k_F^2}{2m_e}=\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2n_e^2}{8m_e}.}
$$
The ground-state <kinetic energy> is found by integrating rather than assigning every <electron> the <Fermi energy>:
$$
K_e=2\frac{L}{2\pi}\int_{-k_F}^{k_F}\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m_e}\,dk
=\frac{L\hbar^2k_F^3}{3\pi m_e}
=\boxed{\frac13N_eE_F.}
$$
<Charge neutrality> gives $N_e=ZN$ because each positive ion has charge $+Ze$. Thus $K_e=ZNE_F/3$. Since the ion separation is $2R$, $L\simeq2RN$ and $n_e=Z/(2R)$. These are one-dimensional number densities and state counts; using a three-dimensional $k_F\propto n^{1/3}$ relation here would change the model. The results concern the ground-state Fermi-gas contribution, neglecting <electron> interactions beyond the supplied electrostatic <energy>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the supplied effective electrostatic term and enforce <charge neutrality> while varying the chain length: $R=Z/(2n_e)$, with $N$ and $Z$ fixed. The total <energy> per positive ion becomes
$$
\varepsilon(n_e)=-\frac{\alpha Z^2e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0R}+\frac{ZE_F}{3}
=-\frac{\alpha Ze^2}{2\pi\epsilon_0}n_e
+\frac{Z\pi^2\hbar^2}{24m_e}n_e^2.
$$
The positive quadratic term supplies <degeneracy pressure> against compression. Differentiating gives the <neutral one-dimensional metal equilibrium density>,
$$
\boxed{n_e^*=\frac{6\alpha m_ee^2}{\pi^3\epsilon_0\hbar^2}
=\frac{24\alpha}{\pi^2a_B}\simeq6.37\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}},}
$$
where $a_B=4\pi\epsilon_0\hbar^2/(m_ee^2)$ is the <Bohr radius>. The second derivative $Z\pi^2\hbar^2/(12m_e)>0$ verifies a minimum. Within this specified toy <energy>, the minimizing <electron> density is independent of $Z$; instead the equilibrium ion spacing is $2R^*=Z/n_e^*$.

At the minimum the electrostatic contribution is minus twice the kinetic contribution, so $\varepsilon_*=-ZE_F^*/3$. These follow from the given electrostatic approximation, not from a full self-consistent calculation of a real one-dimensional metal. In particular, additional Coulomb terms cannot be appended without the corresponding neutral-background subtraction that defines the supplied finite <energy>.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $a$ be the equilibrium spacing of this monatomic chain and $u_j$ the displacement from $ja$. The right nearest-neighbour bond exerts $K(u_{j+1}-u_j)$ and the left bond $K(u_{j-1}-u_j)$. The two next-nearest-neighbour bonds similarly connect $j$ to $j\pm2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{m\ddot u_j=K(u_{j+1}+u_{j-1}-2u_j)
+G(u_{j+2}+u_{j-2}-2u_j).}
$$
Every uniform displacement gives zero force, as translation symmetry requires. There is one displacement coordinate per primitive cell. Adding further-neighbour springs does not introduce a second atom or turn this into a diatomic-chain problem.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Substitute the traveling <normal mode> $u_j=u\exp[i(kja-\omega t)]$. Combining the opposite-neighbour phases gives
$$
m\omega^2=2K(1-\cos ka)+2G(1-\cos2ka),
$$
so the positive-frequency branch is
$$
\boxed{\omega_0(k)=2\sqrt{\frac{K\sin^2(ka/2)+G\sin^2(ka)}{m}},
\qquad |k|\leq\pi/a.}
$$
For positive <spring constants> this is a stable acoustic branch. At long wavelengths,
$$
\omega_0^2\simeq\frac{a^2(K+4G)}m k^2,\qquad
\boxed{c_s=a\sqrt{\frac{K+4G}{m}}.}
$$
The next-nearest-neighbour bond spans twice the distance, hence contributes the factor four. Relative to $G=0$, positive $G$ increases the sound speed by $\sqrt{1+4G/K}$. The chain still has one acoustic branch and no optical branch.

The group speed near $k=0$ and the maximum frequency need not change in the same way. The zone-edge value is always $\omega_0(\pi/a)=2\sqrt{K/m}$, because the next-nearest-neighbour sites then move together. For $G>K/4$ the frequency maximum moves inside the zone, with $\cos(ka)=-K/(4G)$; the small-$k$ sound speed nevertheless keeps increasing.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For a <viscously damped monatomic-chain phonon>, the extra force gives $m\ddot u_j+\Gamma\dot u_j$ on the left of the preceding equation. Keep a real <wave number> $k$ and permit complex frequency in the convention $u_j\propto e^{i(kja-\omega t)}$. The dispersion equation is
$$
\boxed{m\omega^2+i\Gamma\omega-m\omega_0^2(k)=0.}
$$
Writing $\gamma=\Gamma/(2m)$, its two roots are
$$
\boxed{\omega_\pm(k)=-i\gamma\pm\sqrt{\omega_0^2(k)-\gamma^2}.}
$$
The negative imaginary part corresponds to decay with the chosen time convention. Here $\Gamma$ has units of <mass> per time, while $\gamma$ is a decay rate. A driven experiment at specified real frequency would instead solve for a generally complex <wave number>; it is a different way of specifying the response, not a contradictory sign choice.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For passive friction $\Gamma>0$, the time dependence of each spatial normal coordinate follows its damping regime:

* If $\omega_0>\gamma$, it is underdamped: $u_k(t)=e^{-\gamma t}[A\cos(\Omega t)+B\sin(\Omega t)]$, with $\Omega=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}$. The amplitude envelope has decay time $1/\gamma=2m/\Gamma$; cycle-averaged <energy> decays as $e^{-2\gamma t}$.
* If $\omega_0=\gamma$, it is critically damped: $u_k(t)=(A+Bt)e^{-\gamma t}$.
* If $0<\omega_0<\gamma$, it is overdamped: $u_k(t)=A e^{-[\gamma-\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}]t}+B e^{-[\gamma+\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}]t}$. Both terms decay without oscillation, with two different rates.

For any fixed positive damping, sufficiently small nonzero $k$ is overdamped because $\omega_0\simeq c_s|k|$. The slow branch then has $\omega\simeq-i(mc_s^2/\Gamma)k^2$, a diffusive long-wavelength relaxation. At exactly $k=0$, uniform translation has no restoring force, so $u_0(t)=A+B e^{-\Gamma t/m}$ and the constant displacement need not decay. Thus saying every amplitude simply decays as $e^{-\Gamma t/(2m)}$ is valid only for the underdamped envelope, not for all wave numbers.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

In the SI linear-response convention $\mathbf M=\chi_H\mathbf H$, a diamagnetic contribution has negative susceptibility: the induced moment opposes the applied field. Orbital motion supplies a familiar microscopic mechanism, and closed-shell atoms can be diamagnetic even without permanent moments. <Paramagnetism> has positive susceptibility, with induced <magnetization> along the field; thermal alignment of permanent localized moments is one mechanism, while conduction-electron <spin> response is another.

Neither sign alone is a definition of <ferromagnetic ordering>. An ordinary paramagnetic phase has no spontaneous zero-field <magnetization>. A diamagnetic background can coexist with a larger paramagnetic contribution in the same specimen. Curie's inverse-temperature law describes an appropriate localized-moment model, not every possible paramagnet.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Interpret the source's $\mathbf J$ as the dimensionless angular-momentum operator, with $J_z|J,m_J\rangle=m_J|J,m_J\rangle$. If <angular momentum> is written with physical units, the magnetic-moment formula instead contains $\mathbf J_{\rm phys}/\hbar$; this is a convention, not an extra factor in the requested <energy>.

The field fixes the quantization axis, so
$$
\mathcal H=-g\mu_BBJ_z,\qquad
\boxed{E_{m_J}=-g\mu_BBm_J,\quad m_J=-J,-J+1,\ldots,J.}
$$
There are $2J+1$ Zeeman levels equally spaced in <energy> by $g\mu_BB$. For positive $gB$, the state with largest positive moment has lowest <energy>. The given sign defines $\mathbf J$ through the specified moment relation; inserting an additional electron-charge minus sign into it would change the source convention.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $x=g\mu_BB/(k_BT)$. The single-ion <partition function> and mean projected <magnetic moment> are
$$
Z_J(x)=\sum_{m_J=-J}^{J}e^{xm_J},\qquad
\langle\mu_z\rangle=g\mu_B\frac{\sum m_J e^{xm_J}}{\sum e^{xm_J}}
=g\mu_B\frac{\partial\ln Z_J}{\partial x}.
$$
For $J>0$, the geometric sum also gives
$$
\langle\mu_z\rangle=g\mu_B\left[
\left(J+\tfrac12\right)\coth\!\left(\left(J+\tfrac12\right)x\right)
-\tfrac12\coth(x/2)\right].
$$
In the high-temperature, weak-polarization regime $|x|J\ll1$, expand the <Boltzmann factors>. The numerator's zero-order term vanishes because $\sum m_J=0$, and the first-order term uses $\sum m_J^2=J(J+1)(2J+1)/3$. The denominator is $2J+1+O(x^2)$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\langle\mu_z\rangle\simeq\frac{(g\mu_B)^2J(J+1)B}{3k_BT}
=\frac CT,\qquad
C=\frac{(g\mu_B)^2J(J+1)B}{3k_B}.}
$$
This <fixed-field per-ion Curie coefficient> describes **mean moment per ion at fixed field**, so it includes $B$. It is not the field-independent volume-susceptibility <Curie constant>. For <number density> $n$ and $B\simeq\mu_0H$, the latter is $C_\chi=n\mu_0(g\mu_B)^2J(J+1)/(3k_B)$, giving $\chi_H=C_\chi/T$. At $J=0$, the specified moment is zero and both expressions vanish.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Linear Curie behavior fails gradually when the Zeeman <energy> of a substantial moment is comparable to thermal <energy>. Its appropriate scale is
$$
\boxed{g\mu_BJ B\sim k_BT,\qquad
B_{\rm nl}\sim\frac{k_BT}{g\mu_BJ}.}
$$
At $T=300\,\mathrm K$, $k_BT/\mu_B=446.6\,\mathrm T$. Thus $B_{\rm nl}\sim446.6/(gJ)\,\mathrm T$: about $223\,\mathrm T$ for $g=2,J=1$, or $447\,\mathrm T$ for a spin-one-half moment with $g=2$. Larger moments saturate at smaller fields. The source does not specify numerical $g,J$, so it determines this parametric scale rather than one universal room-temperature threshold. At sufficiently large field the moment tends toward $g\mu_BJ$ and cannot keep increasing linearly with $B$.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Room-temperature iron is ferromagnetic: interactions among its electronic moments produce spontaneous local order. The independent-ion <Curie law> therefore needs an interaction and an ordered-state description. In a Weiss mean-field approximation, replace the applied field by $H_{\rm eff}=H+\Lambda M$. Above the ordering <temperature>, linearizing the single-ion response yields
$$
M=\frac{C_\chi}{T}(H+\Lambda M),\qquad
\boxed{\chi_H=\frac{C_\chi}{T-T_C},\qquad T_C=\Lambda C_\chi.}
$$
This is the Curie–Weiss approximation on the paramagnetic side. Merely substituting room <temperature> below $T_C$ into that high-temperature formula would produce a negative response and is not a valid model of stable iron.

Below $T_C$, use the full nonlinear moment response to the effective field; it has nonzero zero-field solutions. On a chosen ordered branch the local small-field response has the form $M\simeq M_s(T)+\chi_{\rm diff}(T)H$, rather than $M\propto H/T$ through the origin. Macroscopic <magnetic domains> and <hysteresis> further determine the measured net moment. Exchange interactions, arising from electronic quantum statistics and Coulomb correlations, supply the alignment <energy>; bare dipolar fields alone do not explain ordinary iron's strong <ferromagnetism>. A localized-ion Weiss model describes the qualitative ordering, while iron's itinerant electronic structure limits a literal independent-fixed-ion interpretation.
