== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <Bravais lattice> is an infinite periodic set of equivalent points $\mathbf R=n_1\mathbf a_1+n_2\mathbf a_2+n_3\mathbf a_3$, with integer $n_i$ and independent primitive translations $\mathbf a_i$. It describes translation symmetry, without assigning a chemical species to a point. An <atomic basis of a crystal> is the motif of atoms, with their species and relative positions, repeated identically at every <lattice point>. The resulting <crystal structure> consists of the lattice decorated by that basis; the basis can contain several atoms even when the lattice is primitive.

A <primitive unit cell> tiles space under lattice translations and contains exactly one <lattice point>, counting shared boundary points fractionally. Its <volume> is $|\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)|$. A <conventional unit cell> is chosen to display useful crystal symmetry and may contain several <lattice points>. Thus a centered conventional cell is not primitive, but the crystal still has a primitive cell. Neither cell size nor the number of dots drawn on its boundary should be confused with the number of atoms in the crystal basis.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Use fractional coordinates relative to conventional cubic vectors $(a\hat x,a\hat y,a\hat z)$. A <body-centered cubic lattice> has representative points
$$
(0,0,0),\quad(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12),
$$
so its conventional cell contains two <lattice points>: eight shared corners contribute one and the interior point contributes one. A <face-centered cubic lattice> has
$$
(0,0,0),\quad(0,\tfrac12,\tfrac12),\quad(\tfrac12,0,\tfrac12),\quad(\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0),
$$
so its conventional cell contains four: one from the shared corners and three from six half-shared faces.

For $N$ atoms at fractional positions $(x_j,y_j,z_j)$, the geometric <structure factor> and the atomic-factor-weighted amplitude are respectively
$$
G_{hkl}=\sum_{j=1}^N e^{2\pi i(hx_j+ky_j+lz_j)},\qquad
F_{hkl}=\sum_{j=1}^N f_j(\mathbf G)e^{2\pi i(hx_j+ky_j+lz_j)}.
$$
For identical atoms $F=fG$. Applying the conventional-cell lattice factors gives
$$
\boxed{G_{\rm BCC}=1+(-1)^{h+k+l}=\begin{cases}2,&h+k+l\text{ even},\\0,&h+k+l\text{ odd},\end{cases}}
$$
and
$$
\boxed{G_{\rm FCC}=1+(-1)^{k+l}+(-1)^{h+l}+(-1)^{h+k}
=\begin{cases}4,&h,k,l\text{ all even or all odd},\\0,&\text{mixed parity}.\end{cases}}
$$
These are lattice selection rules; an additional nontrivial <crystal basis structure factor> can extinguish further allowed reflections.

Of the eight equally represented parity triples, four satisfy the BCC condition and two the FCC condition. Thus the fractions among integer index triples are $1/2$ and $1/4$, respectively. This reflects the two and four equivalent <lattice points> in the conventional cells: their reciprocal lattices occupy corresponding subsets of the conventional reciprocal grids. In primitive reciprocal coordinates every integer triple denotes a reciprocal-lattice vector; lattice-centering absences are then built into the grid rather than an extra rule. A multi-atom chemical basis can still cause genuine additional absences. If permutations means merely reordering one fixed triple, its allowedness never changes: the quoted fractions concern all index triples, not reordering a chosen $(h,k,l)$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

After compression along $z$, let the original conventional edges be $a,a,c$ with $c=a/\sqrt2$. This gives exactly the specified <volume> reduction. The compressed FCC points admit the alternative translation vectors
$$
\mathbf b_1=\frac a2(1,1,0),\qquad
\mathbf b_2=\frac a2(-1,1,0),\qquad
\mathbf b_3=(0,0,a/\sqrt2).
$$
They are mutually perpendicular, have equal length $b=a/\sqrt2$, and form a right-handed cube. Besides its corners, the lattice has the point
$$
\frac{\mathbf b_1+\mathbf b_2+\mathbf b_3}{2}
=(0,a/2,a/(2\sqrt2)),
$$
which is the compressed image of an original FCC face centre. All compressed FCC sites are generated by the cube's integer translations and this body-centre coset. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{compressed FCC}\equiv\text{BCC with conventional edge }b=a/\sqrt2.}
$$
The old compressed tetragonal conventional cell has <volume> $a^3/\sqrt2$ and four sites; the new BCC cube has half that <volume> and two sites. This verifies the same physical site density rather than silently losing atoms during the change of cell. The continuous uniaxial deformation is the <Bain path>, named for <Edgar Bain>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The FCC parity rule forbids $(100)$ and $(110)$ and allows $(200)$ and $(111)$. To follow each same plane family through the deformation, use fractional coordinates $(x,y,z)$ in the compressed old cell and $(X,Y,Z)$ in the new cube. The preceding vectors give
$$
x=(X-Y)/2,\qquad y=(X+Y)/2,\qquad z=Z.
$$
Thus $hx+ky+lz=m$ becomes $HX+KY+LZ=m$, with
$$
\boxed{(H,K,L)=\left(\frac{h+k}{2},\frac{k-h}{2},l\right).}
$$
Crucially, this transformation retains the plane spacing and diffraction order, not just a normal direction:

|| Old FCC reflection
|| New-cell reciprocal coordinates
|| Status

| $(100)$
| $(\tfrac12,-\tfrac12,0)$
| Not a new reciprocal-lattice vector; absent

| $(110)$
| $(1,0,0)$
| Odd index sum; absent

| $(200)$
| $(1,-1,0)$
| Even index sum; allowed

| $(111)$
| $(1,0,1)$
| Even index sum; allowed

The two physically allowed reflections remain allowed, and the two absent ones remain absent. Their angles and spacings change under strain; their allowedness as tracked reciprocal vectors does not.

The half-integer entry is deliberate. It cannot be relabeled as the ordinary BCC $(1\bar10)$ reflection while claiming to track the same diffraction order. Multiplying its indices by two doubles the reciprocal vector and halves the spacing; that new allowed reflection is the image of old FCC $(200)$, not old $(100)$. If only an orientation label is wanted, the old $(100)$ and $(200)$ planes both have the new $(1\bar10)$ orientation, but are distinct orders. This is the distinction between <diffraction indexing under a cell change> and merely reducing a plane normal to the smallest integer ratio.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

In the spin-degenerate <free electron model>, a monovalent metal has conduction-electron <number density> $n$. The density of wave-vector states is $1/(2\pi)^3$ per unit real-space <volume>, with two <spin> states at each <wave vector>. Filling a sphere at zero <temperature> gives
$$
n=\frac{2}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{4\pi k_F^3}{3}=\frac{k_F^3}{3\pi^2},\qquad
\boxed{T_F=\frac{E_F}{k_B}=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_ek_B}(3\pi^2n)^{2/3}.}
$$
For the <Debye model>, there are three acoustic polarizations and $3n$ modes per unit <volume> in total. Replacing their dispersions by $\omega=ck$ with the effective <Debye sound speed>, mode counting gives
$$
3\frac{1}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{4\pi k_D^3}{3}=3n,
\qquad k_D=(6\pi^2n)^{1/3},
\qquad \boxed{\theta_D=\frac{\hbar c}{k_B}(6\pi^2n)^{1/3}.}
$$
The factor two in the <electron> count is <spin>, whereas the factor three in the <phonon> count is polarization; the two counts are not interchangeable. If branch speeds differ, the Debye effective average obeys $c^{-3}=(c_l^{-3}+2c_t^{-3})/3$ in the isotropic approximation.

Dividing gives $T_F/\theta_D=\lambda(3\pi^2n/2)^{1/3}$, where $\lambda=\hbar/(2m_ec)$. A monatomic FCC conventional cell contains four atoms, so $n=4/a^3$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{T_F}{\theta_D}=(6\pi^2)^{1/3}\frac\lambda a.}
$$
Here and below $c$ is the **speed of sound**, not the speed of light. The Debye cutoff counts acoustic modes in a spherical approximation to the <Brillouin zone>; it is not an electron-state cutoff.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Using $c=2700\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ gives the <electron-acoustic recoil length>
$$
\lambda=\frac{\hbar}{2m_ec}=2.1438\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm m.
$$
The <temperature> ratio therefore fixes
$$
\boxed{a=\frac{(6\pi^2)^{1/3}\lambda}{240}=3.482\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm m=0.3482\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
It follows that $n=4/a^3=9.48\times10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$, $k_F=1.4105\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ and $k_D=1.7771\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$. Substitution gives
$$
\boxed{\theta_D\simeq366.5\,\mathrm K,\qquad T_F\simeq8.796\times10^4\,\mathrm K.}
$$
The resulting <Fermi energy> is about $7.58\,\mathrm{eV}$ and the <temperature> ratio is indeed $240$. These values follow from the supplied effective sound speed and free-electron/Debye approximations; they are not independent precision measurements of copper's parameters.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

An <electron> losing <energy> to an acoustic <phonon> has, in a normal process, $\mathbf k'=\mathbf k_F-\mathbf k_{\rm ph}$, with possible addition of a reciprocal-lattice vector for an <Umklapp process>. The <energy> transfer is at most of order $\hbar\omega_D=k_B\theta_D$, much smaller than $E_F=k_BT_F$. Hence the magnitudes change very little even though the <momentum> direction can change substantially. At $T\sim\theta_D\ll T_F$, thermal vacancies near the <Fermi surface> allow emission into some lower-energy states; exactly at zero <temperature> emission into an occupied Fermi sea would be blocked by the <Pauli exclusion principle>.

Using $k'=(1-\delta)k_F$ and the free-electron dispersion, <energy> conservation gives
$$
\frac{\hbar^2k_F^2}{2m_e}\left[1-(1-\delta)^2\right]
=\hbar c k_{\rm ph}.
$$
Since $\delta\ll1$, the bracket is $2\delta+O(\delta^2)$. Rearranging yields
$$
\boxed{\frac1{2\delta}\frac{k_{\rm ph}}{k_F}\frac1{k_F}
\simeq\frac\hbar{2m_ec}=\lambda.}
$$
Equivalently $\delta\simeq\hbar c k_{\rm ph}/(2E_F)$. For a Debye-cutoff <phonon> in the preceding model, $\delta\simeq\theta_D/(2T_F)=1/480=0.00208$, confirming the approximation. Near-elastic scattering constrains the radial distance in wave-vector space, not the <scattering angle> alone.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The <electron-acoustic recoil length> compares electronic recoil with the acoustic quantum <energy> associated with the same <momentum>. For transferred wave number $q$,
$$
E_{\rm recoil}(q)=\frac{\hbar^2q^2}{2m_e},\qquad
E_{\rm phonon}(q)=\hbar cq,\qquad
\boxed{\frac{E_{\rm recoil}}{E_{\rm phonon}}=q\lambda.}
$$
The dispersions cross at $q=1/\lambda$; there the reduced <electron> de Broglie wavelength is $\lambda$ and its free-particle group <velocity> is $2c$. Thus $\lambda$ is the length scale at which an <electron>'s localization/recoil <energy> and a corresponding acoustic excitation <energy> become comparable. At $q\lambda\gg1$ the <electron> recoil <energy> is large compared with the <phonon> <energy>; this explains the separation of electronic and acoustic <energy> scales when lattice wave numbers exceed $1/\lambda$.

Formally it is half the <reduced Compton wavelength> with the speed of light replaced by the speed of sound. That is an analogy of scales, not a relativistic <electron> <mass> or a pair-production threshold: sound does not replace the limiting speed in <electron> dynamics. Also it is a reduced-wavelength convention; an ordinary de Broglie wavelength $2\pi/q$ at the crossing is $2\pi\lambda$. No scattering lifetime appears, so $\lambda$ is not an <electron> <mean free path>.

=== a

==== Solution

An <intrinsic semiconductor> has its carrier populations set primarily by thermal excitation across the <band gap>; in the ideal pure, neutral case its conduction-electron and hole densities are equal, $n=p=n_i$. An <extrinsic semiconductor> has carrier populations significantly controlled by donor or acceptor impurities, producing predominantly <n-type semiconductor> or <p-type semiconductor> behavior. Chemical purity alone does not fix conductivity independently of <temperature> and gap size.

The positive <charge carrier mobility> is the magnitude of mean drift speed per unit weak <electric field>. For <electrons> $\mathbf v_e=-\mu_e\mathbf E$, for holes $\mathbf v_h=\mu_h\mathbf E$, and its SI unit is $\mathrm{m^2\,V^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$. In an isotropic relaxation-time model, $\mu=|q|\tau/m^*$.

A <band effective mass> characterizes how an <electron> wave packet accelerates in an <energy band>, rather than replacing its vacuum rest <mass>. In one dimension and its tensor generalization,
$$
\boxed{\frac1{m^*}=\frac1{\hbar^2}\frac{d^2E}{dk^2},\qquad
(M^{*-1})_{ij}=\frac1{\hbar^2}\frac{\partial^2E}{\partial k_i\partial k_j}.}
$$
These follow by differentiating $v_i=\hbar^{-1}\partial E/\partial k_i$ and using $\hbar\dot k_j=F_j$. Effective masses can be anisotropic and <electron> band curvature can be negative.

=== b
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

An <electron hole> is a missing <electron> in an otherwise almost filled <valence band>. A completely filled band has no net transport current: its velocities cancel over the <Brillouin zone>. Describing a few missing states as mobile excitations is therefore simpler than following almost every occupied <electron> state.

Near an isotropic valence maximum, write the <electron> dispersion as $E_v(\mathbf k)=E_v(0)-\hbar^2k^2/(2m_h)$ with $m_h>0$. The <electron> curvature <mass> is negative there. Removing that <electron> creates an excitation with <momentum> $\hbar\mathbf k_h=-\hbar\mathbf k$ and <energy> measured upward from the filled reference,
$$
E_h(\mathbf k_h)=E_v(0)-E_v(-\mathbf k_h)=\frac{\hbar^2k_h^2}{2m_h}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{m_h^*=-m_{e,\mathrm{valence}}^*>0.}
$$
The sign refers to the positive excitation <energy> of a missing <electron> near a maximum, not to negative vacuum <mass>. A general maximum gives the corresponding positive hole-curvature tensor.<Lecture Notes for Solid State Physics by Steven H. Simon>[§16.2, printed pp. 178–179: missing-electron carriers, opposite charge and <band effective mass>]{ref}

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Removing an <electron> of charge $-e$ leaves charge $+e$ relative to the filled-band reference. This also reproduces the current. If the missing <electron> state had <velocity> $\mathbf v_e(\mathbf k)$, subtracting its contribution from the zero current of a filled band gives
$$
\Delta\mathbf j=-(-e)\mathbf v_e(\mathbf k)=+e\mathbf v_e(\mathbf k).
$$
The hole dispersion with $\mathbf k_h=-\mathbf k$ gives $\mathbf v_h(\mathbf k_h)=\mathbf v_e(\mathbf k)$, so
$$
\boxed{q_h=+e,\qquad \Delta\mathbf j=q_h\mathbf v_h.}
$$
Likewise $\hbar\dot{\mathbf k}=-e\mathbf E$ becomes $\hbar\dot{\mathbf k}_h=+e\mathbf E$. Positive hole charge and positive hole <effective mass> consistently describe drift along the <electric field>, while the valence <electrons> themselves drift through an almost full band. A hole is a quasiparticle, not a proton moving through the crystal.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

In the simple transport model, $\mu_e=e\tau_e/m_e^*$ and $\mu_h=e\tau_h/m_h^*$. Conduction bands in many common semiconductors have lighter transport masses than the dominant valence hole bands. For comparable momentum-relaxation times, this gives larger <electron mobility>. It is a common trend, not a theorem: band anisotropy, light and heavy hole populations and different scattering times can alter the comparison. The hole's <mass> is not the <mass> of an atom, and crystal translational symmetry does not make its mobility infinite.

For the intrinsic <germanium> sample, <electrons> and holes both contribute positive conventional conductivity:
$$
\sigma=e(n\mu_e+p\mu_h)=en_i(\mu_e+\mu_h),\qquad\sigma=1/\rho=2\,\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{n=p=n_i=\frac1{(0.5)e(0.36+0.18)}
=2.31\times10^{19}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}.}
$$
This is the density of each species; the combined carrier <number density> is $2n_i$. Assigning separate densities from the two mobilities would violate intrinsic charge neutrality.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

A monovalent FCC metal supplies one conduction <electron> per atom and four atoms per conventional cell. Hence
$$
n_m=\frac4{(0.36\times10^{-9})^3}=8.57\times10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}.
$$
With a one-carrier conductivity $\sigma=n_me\mu_m$,
$$
\boxed{\mu_m=\frac1{\rho_mn_me}=\frac1{(1.7\times10^{-8})(8.57\times10^{28})e}
=4.28\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^2\,V^{-1}\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
This is about $84$ times smaller than the stated germanium <electron mobility>. Nevertheless the metal is vastly more conductive because its carrier density is about $3.7\times10^9$ times larger. Resistivity depends on both density and mobility; low resistivity alone does not establish high mobility or long scattering time.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Neglect the battery's internal resistance, contact drops and the very small resistance of laboratory-length metal leads. The germanium resistance and field are
$$
R_g=\rho_g\frac{L_g}{A_g}=0.5\frac{0.01}{10^{-6}}=5000\,\Omega,
\qquad E_g=\frac2{0.01}=200\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}.
$$
Taking the positive direction along the <electric field>, the carrier drift velocities are
$$
\boxed{\mathbf v_e=-72\,\hat{\mathbf E}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},\qquad
\mathbf v_h=+36\,\hat{\mathbf E}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
Their conventional current densities add: $j_e=en_i\mu_e E_g\simeq266.7\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}$ and $j_h\simeq133.3\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}$. Thus $I=(j_e+j_h)A_g=0.400\,\mathrm{mA}$ is the same total current through either metal lead. The <current continuity between a metal and an intrinsic semiconductor> gives
$$
\boxed{|v_m|=\frac{I}{n_meA_m}
=\frac{4.00\times10^{-4}}{(8.57\times10^{28})e(0.5\times10^{-6})}
=5.82\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The metal <electrons> move opposite its conventional current. This extremely small drift speed is compatible with large microscopic Fermi velocities; drift is the small imbalance of the occupied <momentum> distribution.

The wire lengths were not specified. Their resistance per metre is $\rho_m/A_m=0.034\,\Omega\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$, so neglecting them relative to $5000\,\Omega$ is justified for an ordinary apparatus. If finite total wire length $L_w$ is retained, the exact ideal-contact circuit expression is $I=2/(5000+0.034L_w)\,\mathrm A$ with $L_w$ in metres; all three drift magnitudes above scale with $I/(0.400\,\mathrm{mA})$. Thus the approximation and its missing geometric input are explicit.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The SI <volume> <magnetic susceptibility> is the weak-field response $\chi_M=\partial M/\partial H$, or $M/H$ for a linear isotropic sample with no spontaneous moment. It is dimensionless; a molar susceptibility would be a different quantity. For weak <magnetization>, $B\simeq\mu_0H$. Neglect local-field and demagnetizing corrections in this independent-spin model.

A spin-$1/2$ ion has moment projections $\pm g\mu_B/2$. The requested coefficient assumes a spin-only $g\simeq2$, giving projections $\pm\mu_B$ and Zeeman energies $\mp\mu_BB$. With $x=\mu_BB/(k_BT)$, the one-ion <partition function> and <magnetization> are
$$
Z_1=e^x+e^{-x}=2\cosh x,\qquad
M=N\mu_B\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}{e^x+e^{-x}}=N\mu_B\tanh x.
$$
For $|x|\ll1$, $\tanh x\simeq x$, so
$$
\boxed{\chi_M\simeq\mu_0\frac{\partial M}{\partial B}
=\frac{N\mu_0\mu_B^2}{k_BT}.}
$$
Here $N$ is a <number density>, as specified, rather than total ion number. The small-field condition is
$$
\boxed{\mu_B|B|\ll k_BT,}
$$
meaning the Zeeman splitting $2\mu_BB$ is small compared with the thermal scale up to an irrelevant order-one convention. It is not a temperature-independent absolute upper bound on $B$. For a different $g$ value the coefficient contains $(g\mu_B/2)^2$ instead of $\mu_B^2$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

A point <magnetic dipole> $\boldsymbol\mu$ produces
$$
\mathbf B(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r^3}
\left[3(\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\hat r)\hat r-\boldsymbol\mu\right].
$$
For $|\mu|\sim\mu_B$ and neighbour separation $r\sim0.2\,\mathrm{nm}$, its characteristic magnitude is
$$
\boxed{B_{\rm dip}\sim\frac{\mu_0\mu_B}{4\pi r^3}\simeq0.116\,\mathrm T.}
$$
The axial field is twice this scale; the exact orientation and crystal geometry are unspecified. The corresponding pair <magnetic dipole interaction> has <energy> scale $U_{\rm dip}\sim\mu_BB_{\rm dip}\simeq1.08\times10^{-24}\,\mathrm J$, giving
$$
\boxed{T_{\rm dip}\sim\frac{U_{\rm dip}}{k_B}\simeq0.078\,\mathrm K\sim0.1\,\mathrm K.}
$$
An axial orientation changes this to about $0.16\,\mathrm K$. A real ordering <temperature> depends on a lattice sum, symmetry and fluctuations, so this is a nearest-neighbour <energy> estimate, not a complete ordering calculation. It establishes that ordinary dipolar coupling is far too weak to explain a thousand-kelvin ferromagnetic transition.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The inverse-susceptibility curve has a Curie–Weiss form above $T_C$ and vanishes on approach to it. The material can have <spontaneous magnetization> for $T<T_C$, including the zero-temperature limit, whereas for $T>T_C$ its zero-field equilibrium state is paramagnetic. At a continuous transition the spontaneous moment tends to zero at $T_C$; divergence of differential susceptibility there is not a finite saturated permanent moment.

A simple <Weiss molecular field> model takes the effective internal field intensity to be $H_{\rm eff}=H+\Lambda M$, with positive coupling $\Lambda$. Let $C$ be the independent-moment Curie constant. Linearizing the single-ion response on the high-temperature side gives
$$
M=\frac CT(H+\Lambda M),\qquad
\boxed{\chi_M=\frac C{T-T_C},\quad T_C=\Lambda C,\quad
\frac1{\chi_M}=\frac{T-T_C}{C}.}
$$
This explains the straight upper branch and its positive <temperature> intercept. At $T_C$ the zero-field linearized response diverges; finite field, finite size and critical fluctuations limit its use. For a spin-only two-state mean-field model the full equation is $m=\tanh[(T_C/T)m+\mu_B\mu_0H/(k_BT)]$, with $m=M/(N\mu_B)$; below $T_C$ it has nonzero zero-field solutions.

An ordering scale near $1000\,\mathrm K$ corresponds to $k_BT_C\simeq0.086\,\mathrm{eV}$, about four orders of magnitude larger than the preceding dipolar pair scale. <Exchange interaction> can supply such energies: the Pauli antisymmetry of overlapping <electron> states changes their spatial correlations and hence Coulomb and kinetic energies according to their <spin> configuration. An effective ferromagnetic coupling can favor parallel moments. It is not simply the classical <magnetic field> of a neighbouring dipole. For example, with the convention $\mathcal H=-J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\mathbf S_i\cdot\mathbf S_j$ and $J>0$, the localized-spin mean-field scale is $k_BT_C=zJS(S+1)/3$; this illustrates why an atomic electronic interaction, rather than a bare dipolar field, can produce a high transition <temperature>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

A bulk <ferromagnet> generally separates into <magnetic domains>. Within each domain many moments align and the local <magnetization> can be large, but different domain orientations partially or almost completely cancel the vector sum measured for the entire specimen. This reduces its external stray field and magnetostatic <energy>. Formation of <magnetic domain walls> costs exchange and anisotropy <energy>, so the actual pattern balances these costs with the reduction in stray-field <energy>.

A strong applied field can move domain walls and rotate moments toward a nearly <saturation magnetization>. When the field is removed, pinning and the sample's magnetic history determine the remanent moment. Thus a small net moment does not show that local ferromagnetic order has disappeared, or that moments within each domain are all randomly oriented. At finite <temperature>, thermal <spin> fluctuations also reduce the local ordered moment below its zero-temperature maximum even in a single-domain specimen. These two reductions—between-domain cancellation and within-domain thermal reduction—should be distinguished.
