== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <Bravais lattice> is a set of points generated by integer translations along three independent <primitive lattice translation vectors>:
$$
\mathbf R=n_1\mathbf a_1+n_2\mathbf a_2+n_3\mathbf a_3,\qquad n_i\in\mathbb Z.
$$
Every <lattice point> has the same environment under <lattice translations>. A <crystal structure> consists of this <Bravais lattice> with an <atomic basis of a crystal> repeated at every <lattice point>; the set of atomic positions need not itself be a <Bravais lattice>.

The two positions in the conventional cubic cell of alpha-<tungsten> give a <body-centred cubic lattice>, with one <tungsten> atom per primitive cell and two per conventional cell. One possible set of <primitive lattice translation vectors> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf a_1=\frac a2(-1,1,1),\quad\mathbf a_2=\frac a2(1,-1,1),\quad\mathbf a_3=\frac a2(1,1,-1).}
$$
Their primitive-cell <volume> is $a^3/2$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For the conventional <body-centred cubic lattice>, the <crystal basis structure factor> of identical atoms is
$$
F_{hkl}=f_{\mathrm W}\bigl[1+e^{i\pi(h+k+l)}\bigr].
$$
It vanishes for odd $h+k+l$ and equals $2f_{\mathrm W}$ for even $h+k+l$. Thus the first <powder diffraction> ring is the $\{110\}$ family, with <interplanar spacing> $d_{110}=a/\sqrt2$.

The measured deflection is the angle between incident and scattered rays, $2\theta=40.26^\circ$, so the <Bragg angle> is $\theta=20.13^\circ$. Using first-order <Bragg diffraction>, $2d\sin\theta=\lambda$, gives
$$
\boxed{a=\frac{\lambda}{\sqrt2\sin\theta}=0.31683\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
The <mass density> follows from two atoms per conventional <unit cell>, with molar mass $M=0.1838\,\mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}}$ and <Avogadro constant> $N_A$:
$$
\boxed{\rho=\frac{2M}{N_Aa^3}=1.9194\times10^4\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\simeq19.19\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}.}
$$
Using $40.26^\circ$ as the <Bragg angle> would confuse $\theta$ with $2\theta$ and give an incorrect <lattice constant> and <mass density>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The beta phase has a <primitive cubic lattice> with an eight-atom <atomic basis of a crystal>, rather than a <body-centred cubic lattice>. A <lattice translation> must map the entire atomic arrangement onto itself. For example, the putative body-centering vector $(1/2,1/2,1/2)$ maps $(1/4,1/2,0)$ to $(3/4,0,1/2)$ modulo integer coordinates, where the given <atomic basis of a crystal> has no atom. None of the seven nonzero basis positions generates a translation that preserves all eight sites. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{Bravais lattice: primitive cubic; basis: eight atoms.}}
$$
The presence of atoms at both the origin and the body centre alone is insufficient to establish body-centering of the full <crystal structure>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Initially approximate every <tungsten> atom by the same <atomic scattering factor> $f$. Pairing the quarter-coordinate sites in the <crystal basis structure factor> gives
$$
\frac{F_{hkl}}f=1+(-1)^{h+k+l}+2\left[(-1)^k\cos\frac{\pi h}{2}+(-1)^h\cos\frac{\pi l}{2}+(-1)^l\cos\frac{\pi k}{2}\right].
$$
For cubic <powder diffraction>, increasing deflection corresponds to increasing $h^2+k^2+l^2$, since $d_{hkl}=a/\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}$. The first possibilities are

|| Family | $h^2+k^2+l^2$ | $F/f$ \\
| $\{100\}$ | $1$ | $0$ \\
| $\{110\}$ | $2$ | $0$ \\
| $\{111\}$ | $3$ | $0$ \\
| $\{200\}$ | $4$ | $4$ \\
| $\{210\}$ | $5$ | $4$

Hence the first two strong rings have <Miller indices>
$$
\boxed{\{200\},\qquad\{210\}.}
$$
The <powder diffraction multiplicity> is six for $\{200\}$, from three choices of nonzero axis and two signs. For $\{210\}$ it is $3!\,2^2=24$, from six permutations and four sign choices. If the <atomic scattering factors> and angular corrections are taken as approximately equal for these nearby rings, their integrated <diffraction intensity> obey
$$
\boxed{I_{200}:I_{210}\simeq6|4f|^2:24|4f|^2=1:4.}
$$
This is a multiplicity-based estimate. An exact measured ratio also depends on the momentum-transfer dependence of the <atomic scattering factor>, the <Lorentz-polarization factor>, the <Debye–Waller factor>, absorption and instrument geometry. In particular, the source does not provide the beta-phase <lattice constant> or instrument corrections needed to calculate those angular factors. Peak heights additionally depend on peak widths; the estimate refers to integrated ring <intensities>.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

The two body-centre/origin sites and the other six sites have different local environments. Their effective <atomic scattering factors> therefore need not be identical even in an elemental, chemically pure <crystal>. Write them as $f_A$ and $f_B$, respectively. The <crystal basis structure factor> becomes
$$
F_{hkl}=f_A[1+(-1)^{h+k+l}]+2f_B\left[(-1)^k\cos\frac{\pi h}{2}+(-1)^h\cos\frac{\pi l}{2}+(-1)^l\cos\frac{\pi k}{2}\right].
$$
For the low-angle families this gives
$$
F_{100}=0,\qquad \boxed{F_{110}=2(f_A-f_B),}\qquad F_{111}=0.
$$
Thus $\{110\}$ can produce a weak extra <powder diffraction> ring below $\{200\}$. This is an <accidental diffraction extinction from equal atomic scattering factors>: its cancellation in part d is exact only in the identical-atom-scattering approximation. Small differences in the bonding <electron density>, or site-dependent <Debye–Waller factors>, can leave a small nonzero amplitude. For instance, taking $f_A=f+\delta f$ and $f_B=f$ gives $F_{110}=2\delta f$ while $F_{200}=4f+2\delta f$; the corresponding intensity suppression is quadratic in $|\delta f/f|$. No impurity is required. The $\{100\}$ and $\{111\}$ cancellations remain for arbitrary scalar $f_A,f_B$ in this two-site-class model.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <chemical potential> is the change in <Helmholtz free energy> when particles are added at fixed <temperature> and <volume>:
$$
\boxed{\mu=\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial N}\right)_{T,V}.}
$$
Equivalently, $dU=T\,dS-P\,dV+\mu\,dN$ identifies it as the derivative of <internal energy> at fixed <entropy> and <volume>. In equilibrium, systems that exchange particles have equal <chemical potentials>.

The <Fermi energy> is the zero-temperature <chemical potential>, or, for independent <electrons>, the highest occupied single-particle <energy> in the zero-temperature limit. Its numerical value depends on the chosen <energy> reference; the <Fermi energy> need not be measured from the bottom of a band unless that convention is specified.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Expand a single-electron <wavefunction> as $|\psi\rangle=\sum_n\psi_n|n\rangle$. Project the <Schrödinger equation> onto $\langle n|$ to obtain
$$
E\psi_n=\epsilon_0\psi_n-t\psi_{n-1}-t\psi_{n+1}.
$$
Translation <symmetry> suggests the <Bloch state> $\psi_n=N^{-1/2}e^{ikna}$ on a periodic chain of $N$ sites. Substitution gives
$$
\boxed{E(k)=\epsilon_0-t(e^{ika}+e^{-ika})=\epsilon_0-2t\cos(ka).}
$$
Take the conventional positive hopping $t>0$. The first <Brillouin zone> is $-\pi/a\leq k<\pi/a$; the band has minimum $\epsilon_0-2t$ at $k=0$ and maximum $\epsilon_0+2t$ at the zone edges. Its width is $4t$. A negative hopping interchanges the extrema but does not change the <density of states> after replacing $t$ by $|t|$.

There are $N$ allowed <wavevectors> and two <spin> states per <wavevector>. For the total spin-summed <density of states> $D(E)$, each interior band <energy> has two roots, $\pm k$. Using the spacing $2\pi/(Na)$ gives
$$
D(E)=\frac{2Na}{2\pi}\sum_{k:E(k)=E}\frac1{|dE/dk|}
=\frac{2N}{\pi\sqrt{4t^2-(E-\epsilon_0)^2}}.
$$
Thus the <density of states> per atom is
$$
\boxed{g(E)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{2}{\pi\sqrt{4t^2-(E-\epsilon_0)^2}},&|E-\epsilon_0|<2t,\\0,&|E-\epsilon_0|>2t.\end{cases}}
$$
It is symmetric about $\epsilon_0$ and has integrable inverse-square-root <van Hove singularities> at both band edges. Its <integral> over the band is two states per atom. The plot labels the dispersion extrema and both singular edges.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Monovalency gives one <electron> per atom, so the two-spin-state band is half filled. At zero <temperature>, occupying $-k_F<k<k_F$ gives $2(Na/2\pi)(2k_F)=N$. Hence
$$
\boxed{k_F=\frac{\pi}{2a},\qquad E_F=E(k_F)=\epsilon_0.}
$$
Relative to the band minimum, the <Fermi energy> is $2t$.

The <density of states> obeys $g(\epsilon_0+x)=g(\epsilon_0-x)$. At $\mu=\epsilon_0$, the <Fermi–Dirac distribution> satisfies
$$
f(\epsilon_0+x)+f(\epsilon_0-x)=1.
$$
Pairing the energies above and below the band centre therefore leaves exactly half of the two available states per atom occupied, at every positive <temperature>. Increasing $\mu$ strictly increases the mean particle number, so this solution at fixed half filling is unique. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\mu(T)=\epsilon_0\quad\text{for every }T\text{ in this isolated symmetric-band model}.}
$$
This <particle-hole symmetry of a half-filled bipartite band> argument uses the symmetric <tight-binding> band and fixed half filling; it is not a general temperature-independence property of <chemical potential> in metals with additional bands or an asymmetric <density of states>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The first <Brillouin zone> is the square
$$
-\frac\pi a\leq k_x<\frac\pi a,\qquad -\frac\pi a\leq k_y<\frac\pi a.
$$
At half filling, translation of the <wavevector> by $\mathbf Q=(\pi/a,\pi/a)$ exchanges the two band <energies>:
$$
E(\mathbf k+\mathbf Q)-\epsilon_0=-[E(\mathbf k)-\epsilon_0].
$$
Thus the same energy-pair argument as in part c gives
$$
\boxed{\mu(T)=\epsilon_0.}
$$
At zero <temperature>, the <Fermi surface> satisfies $\cos(k_xa)+\cos(k_ya)=0$. Within the square <Brillouin zone> this is the diamond
$$
\boxed{|k_x|+|k_y|=\frac\pi a,}
$$
whose vertices are $(\pm\pi/a,0)$ and $(0,\pm\pi/a)$. The occupied region is the diamond's interior, containing the band minimum at the zone centre. It has half the <Brillouin zone> area, consistent with one <electron> per atom and two <spin> states per <wavevector>.

{title=<Brillouin zones> and half-filled <Fermi surfaces> for square and rectangular models}

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

The reciprocal primitive vectors are $(2\pi/a_x,0)$ and $(0,2\pi/a_y)$, so the first <Brillouin zone> is
$$
\boxed{|k_x|\leq\pi/a_x,\qquad |k_y|\leq\pi/a_y.}
$$
It is wider in the $k_x$ direction because $a_x<a_y$.

The geometry alone does not fix the hopping amplitudes or a unique <Fermi surface>. One consistent <tight-binding model> is
$$
E(\mathbf k)=\epsilon_0-2t_x\cos(k_xa_x)-2t_y\cos(k_ya_y),\qquad t_x>t_y>0,
$$
representing larger hopping between the closer atoms. It remains a nearest-neighbour bipartite band at half filling, so its <chemical potential> is $\epsilon_0$. Its <Fermi surface> consists of the two sheets
$$
\boxed{k_x=\pm\frac1{a_x}\arccos\!\left[-\frac{t_y}{t_x}\cos(k_ya_y)\right].}
$$
These meet the top and bottom zone edges and continue periodically into the neighbouring zones. The right panel of the figure gives an explicit example with $t_x=2t_y$ and $a_x=0.65a_y$. The central region between the sheets is occupied and has half the zone area. If the hopping amplitudes instead remained equal, a geometrically stretched diamond would also satisfy the stated information. The metric inequality $a_x<a_y$ alone does not determine which of these shapes occurs.

=== a

==== Solution

A <phonon> is a quantum of a <normal mode> of <Bravais lattice> vibration. A harmonic mode of <angular frequency> $\omega$ has <energy> $\hbar\omega(n+1/2)$, and adding one <phonon> increases its <energy> by $\hbar\omega$. The <phonon> carries <crystal momentum> $\hbar\mathbf q$, defined modulo a <reciprocal lattice> vector.

The first <Brillouin zone> is the <Wigner–Seitz cell> of the <reciprocal lattice> about the origin: the set of <wavevectors> at least as close to the origin as to any other <reciprocal lattice> point. Its boundaries are reciprocal-space perpendicular bisectors. It gives one representative of each <wavevector> modulo <reciprocal lattice> translations.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $y=e^{-r/\xi}$. Differentiating the <Morse potential> gives
$$
V'(r)=\frac1\xi(-2Ay^2+By).
$$
The finite-separation minimum has $y=B/(2A)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{a=\xi\ln\frac{2A}{B}=0.36809\,\mathrm{nm},\qquad V(a)=-\frac{B^2}{4A}=-0.250\,\mathrm{eV}.}
$$
The positive <bond dissociation energy> is $D=B^2/(4A)=0.250\,\mathrm{eV}$, measured relative to the separated-atom value $V(\infty)=0$. This distinguishes the well's negative <potential energy> from the positive <energy> required to break the bond.

For the displacement $x=r-a$, the <Taylor expansion> is
$$
V(a+x)=V(a)+\frac12\kappa x^2+O(x^3),\qquad
\kappa=V''(a)=\frac{B^2}{2A\xi^2}=\frac{2D}{\xi^2}.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\kappa=70.862\,\mathrm{eV\,nm^{-2}}=11.353\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}.}
$$
The restoring <force> is $-\kappa x$ to leading order, identifying the bond's <Hooke's law> <spring constant>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The <mass> per <lead> atom is
$$
m=\frac{0.2070\,\mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}}}{N_A}=3.4373\times10^{-25}\,\mathrm{kg}.
$$
Let $u_n$ be its longitudinal displacement. <Newton's second law> gives the <harmonic chain> equation
$$
m\ddot u_n=\kappa(u_{n+1}+u_{n-1}-2u_n).
$$
Insert the <normal mode> $u_n=u\,e^{i(kna-\omega t)}$ to obtain
$$
\omega^2=\frac{4\kappa}{m}\sin^2\frac{ka}{2}.
$$
At long <wavelength>, $\omega\simeq a\sqrt{\kappa/m}\,|k|$. The <sound speed> is the corresponding low-<frequency> <group velocity>:
$$
\boxed{v_s=a\sqrt{\frac\kappa m}=2.1155\times10^3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The relevant inertia in this chain equation is one atomic <mass>. The reduced <mass> $m/2$ belongs to the relative vibration of an isolated pair and should not be substituted for $m$ in the chain's <dispersion relation>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Write the <Morse potential> relative to its minimum as $V(a+x)=D[e^{-2x/\xi}-2e^{-x/\xi}]$. Expanding one order beyond the harmonic term gives
$$
\boxed{V(a+x)=-D+\frac{D}{\xi^2}x^2-\frac{D}{\xi^3}x^3+O(x^4)
=-D+\frac\kappa2x^2-\frac{\kappa}{2\xi}x^3+O(x^4).}
$$
The negative cubic coefficient means that stretching is softer than compression. Thermal motion therefore has positive mean displacement even though the most probable minimum is at $x=0$.

For <thermal expansion of a weakly anharmonic bond>, classical <equipartition> gives $\langle x^2\rangle_0=k_BT/\kappa$. Vanishing mean restoring <force>, to first anharmonic order, requires
$$
0=\langle V'(a+x)\rangle=\kappa\langle x\rangle-\frac{3\kappa}{2\xi}\langle x^2\rangle+\cdots,
\qquad \langle x\rangle\simeq\frac{3k_BT}{2\kappa\xi}.
$$
For a bond free to expand under negligible applied tension, $L=\langle r\rangle\simeq a+\langle x\rangle$. Its <linear thermal expansion coefficient> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\alpha\sim\frac{k_B}{a\kappa\xi}\simeq3.9\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}.}
$$
Keeping the leading perturbative numerical factor gives $\alpha\simeq3k_B/(2a\kappa\xi)=5.9\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{K^{-1}}$. The question only requires the scale, not that factor. The <thermal expansion> is positive; a purely harmonic approximation would give zero mean displacement and zero <thermal expansion>. The local <Taylor expansion> describes a bound bond and is not a globally stable cubic potential.

=== e
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

The small parameter controlling the local <Taylor expansion> is the typical bond displacement divided by $\xi$. In the classical regime, the root-mean-square displacement is $x_{\mathrm{rms}}\simeq\sqrt{k_BT/\kappa}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\frac{x_{\mathrm{rms}}}{\xi}\ll1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
T\ll\frac{\kappa\xi^2}{k_B}=5.80\times10^3\,\mathrm K.}
$$
For example, the magnitude of the cubic term divided by the quadratic term is $|x|/\xi$, and subsequent terms carry further powers of this ratio. A physically safe bound-bond regime also has $k_BT\ll D$, or $T\ll2.90\times10^3\,\mathrm K$. These are the same parametric scale, differing only by a factor two. The inequalities are strong: they do not imply that a truncated local expansion is accurate close to several thousand kelvin.

At very low <temperature>, replace the classical fluctuation estimate by the <zero-point motion> as discussed in part ii. Its bond displacement is small compared with $\xi$ for the supplied parameters, so low <temperature> does not itself invalidate the local expansion. Actual melting, other neighbours and bond breaking are outside this one-dimensional bond model.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Quantum statistics matter when thermal <energy> is comparable with a characteristic vibrational level spacing. The chain's natural <angular frequency> is $\omega_0=\sqrt{\kappa/m}=5.7471\times10^{12}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, giving
$$
\boxed{T_Q\sim\frac{\hbar\omega_0}{k_B}=43.9\,\mathrm K.}
$$
Factors of order unity depend on which vibrational scale is used. The largest chain <phonon> <angular frequency> is $2\omega_0$, corresponding to $87.8\,\mathrm K$. An isolated two-atom bond has reduced <mass> $m/2$ and <angular frequency> $\sqrt2\omega_0$, corresponding to $62.1\,\mathrm K$. Thus quantum corrections become appreciable on the scale of several tens of kelvin, and all modes are classical only when $T$ is comfortably above the largest relevant vibrational scale. Below it, high-<frequency> <phonons> are progressively frozen out and <equipartition> fails.

For the isolated bond, the zero-temperature root-mean-square relative displacement is
$$
x_{\mathrm{zp}}=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2(m/2)(\sqrt2\omega_0)}}\simeq0.0061\,\mathrm{nm}\simeq0.073\xi.
$$
It is small enough for a local bond expansion. The classical weakly anharmonic estimate in part d therefore has a useful parametric window
$$
\boxed{10^2\,\mathrm K\ll T\ll\text{a few }10^3\,\mathrm K,}
$$
where the lower condition means comfortably above the vibrational scale, rather than a sharp threshold. The model's upper bound concerns local anharmonicity and binding, not a prediction of the melting temperature.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A material with <paramagnetism> develops a <magnetization> parallel to a weak applied <magnetic field intensity>, with positive <magnetic susceptibility> and no spontaneous <magnetization> in its paramagnetic phase. <Molecular oxygen> is an example; it has unpaired <electron spins>.

A material with <diamagnetism> develops an induced <magnetization> opposing the applied <magnetic field>, with negative <magnetic susceptibility>. A closed-shell atom such as <helium> is an example; its orbital response is diamagnetic.

A <ferromagnet> has a spontaneously ordered <magnetization> below its <Curie temperature>, even in the limit of vanishing applied <magnetic field>. <Iron> below its <Curie temperature> is an example. Different <magnetic domains> can cancel the net moment of an unmagnetized bulk sample, despite nonzero local spontaneous <magnetization>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

An $f$ shell has orbital quantum number $l=3$ and seven orbital states with $m_l=-3,-2,\ldots,3$. The first of <Hund's rules> maximizes total <spin> subject to the <Pauli exclusion principle>. Seven <electrons> occupy the seven orbitals singly with parallel spins, giving
$$
\boxed{S=7\times\frac12=\frac72.}
$$
Their orbital projections sum to zero, so the half-filled shell also has total orbital <angular momentum> $L=0$, total $J=7/2$, and <Landé g-factor> $g_J=2$. These further values identify the magnetic moment used below.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The specified two-direction constraint permits magnetic moments $\mu_z=\pm\mu$, with
$$
\mu=g_JS\mu_B=7\mu_B,
$$
where $\mu_B$ is the <Bohr magneton>. There are four magnetic <gadolinium> ions per conventional <face-centered cubic lattice> cell; the closed-shell nitrogen ions add no local spin moment. Hence the magnetic-ion <number density> is
$$
n=\frac4{a^3}=3.20\times10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}.
$$
At high <temperature>, neglect interactions and use $B_z\simeq\mu_0H_z$. The one-ion <partition function> and mean <magnetic dipole moment> are
$$
Z_1=2\cosh\!\left(\frac{\mu\mu_0H_z}{k_BT}\right),\qquad
\langle\mu_z\rangle=\mu\tanh\!\left(\frac{\mu\mu_0H_z}{k_BT}\right).
$$
In the weak-<magnetic field> limit, $\tanh x\simeq x$, so $M_z=n\langle\mu_z\rangle=\mu_0n\mu^2H_z/(k_BT)$. Thus the dimensionless SI <magnetic susceptibility> is $\chi=C/T$, with
$$
\boxed{C=\frac{\mu_0n(7\mu_B)^2}{k_B}=12.27\,\mathrm K.}
$$
The coefficient has units of kelvin. The unrestricted-spin expression proportional to $S(S+1)/3$ is inappropriate here: the source explicitly retains only the two orientations with $S_z=\pm S$. In this <two-state Curie law>, the squared axial moment is $(g_JS\mu_B)^2$.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Ferromagnetic interactions enhance the response above the ordering transition. The <Curie–Weiss law> improves the independent-spin approximation:
$$
\boxed{\chi(T)\simeq\frac{C}{T-\Theta},\qquad T>T_c,}
$$
with positive <Weiss temperature> $\Theta$ and the same high-<temperature> coefficient $C$. In a simple <mean-field theory>, take $\Theta=T_c=70\,\mathrm K$. More generally $\Theta$ is fitted and need not equal $T_c$, and the simple pole does not give exact critical behaviour arbitrarily close to the transition.

At zero <temperature>, a selected ferromagnetic orientation has all magnetic moments aligned. Its <saturation magnetization> is
$$
\boxed{M_s(0)=n\mu=\frac4{a^3}(7\mu_B)=2.077\times10^6\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}.}
$$
Equivalently, there are $28\mu_B$ per conventional cell. The moment points along either allowed easy-axis direction at zero <magnetic field>. This is the local or single-domain spontaneous <magnetization>; a macroscopic multidomain sample can have smaller, even zero, net <magnetization> in the absence of a selecting field.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

Choose the positive spontaneous branch for $H_z=0$. Its <magnetization> starts at $M_s(0)$, decreases with <temperature>, and reaches zero continuously at $T_c$. It remains zero for $T_c\leq T<2T_c$. There is an equally valid negative branch below $T_c$; domain averaging can instead give zero net moment.

A fixed small positive <magnetic field intensity> selects the positive branch. At zero <temperature> it still saturates at $M_s(0)$; at intermediate <temperature> it increases the <magnetization> relative to the zero-field branch. It rounds the zero-field transition and leaves a positive <magnetization> above $T_c$, tending in the linear-response region to $C H_z/(T-\Theta)$. The <magnetization> therefore does not vanish at $T_c$ in the nonzero-field curve.

For a concrete labelled sketch, the figure uses the illustrative <mean-field theory> equation
$$
m=\tanh\!\left(\frac{m+h}{t}\right),\qquad
m=\frac{M_z}{M_s(0)},\quad t=\frac{T}{T_c},\quad h=\frac{\mu\mu_0H_z}{k_BT_c}.
$$
It shows $h=0$ and a fixed small $h=0.01$. The curve is an illustrative equilibrium equation of state, not a determination of the precise critical exponents of GdN. At $h>0$ no symmetry-breaking transition remains along this temperature sweep.
