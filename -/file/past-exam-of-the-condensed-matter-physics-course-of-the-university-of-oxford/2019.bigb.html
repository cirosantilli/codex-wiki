A <Bravais lattice> is the set of points
$$
\Lambda=\left\{n_1\mathbf a_1+n_2\mathbf a_2+n_3\mathbf a_3:n_i\in\mathbb Z\right\},
$$
where the <primitive lattice translation vectors> $\mathbf a_i$ are linearly independent. Every lattice point has the same translational environment.

An <atomic basis> is the motif of atoms attached to each point of the <Bravais lattice>, specified by positions $\mathbf r_p$ and atomic species. The full crystal consists of atoms at $\mathbf R+\mathbf r_p$, with $\mathbf R\in\Lambda$. The <Bravais lattice> describes translations; the <atomic basis> describes what is repeated. A primitive cell contains one lattice point but can contain several atoms.

Put $v=\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3)\ne0$. Define
$$
\boxed{\mathbf b_1=\frac{2\pi}{v}\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3,\quad\mathbf b_2=\frac{2\pi}{v}\mathbf a_3\times\mathbf a_1,\quad\mathbf b_3=\frac{2\pi}{v}\mathbf a_1\times\mathbf a_2.}
$$
The <scalar triple product> gives $\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf b_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}$. Consequently every integer combination $\mathbf G=\sum_jm_j\mathbf b_j$ satisfies
$$
\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf R=2\pi\sum_jm_jn_j\in2\pi\mathbb Z,\qquad e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf R}=1.
$$
This proves that the proposed vectors generate points in the <reciprocal lattice>, but primitiveness also requires the converse. Let $\mathbf G$ satisfy the exponential condition for every <lattice vector>, and in particular for $\mathbf R=\mathbf a_i$. Then
$$
m_i=\frac{\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf a_i}{2\pi}\in\mathbb Z.
$$
Since the $\mathbf b_i$ form a <basis> of three-dimensional space, the vector $\sum_i m_i\mathbf b_i$ has the same dot products with each $\mathbf a_j$ as $\mathbf G$. Their difference is orthogonal to three independent vectors and is zero. Hence
$$
\boxed{\Lambda^*=\left\{\sum_i m_i\mathbf b_i:m_i\in\mathbb Z\right\},}
$$
and no additional <reciprocal lattice vectors> lie outside that integer span. Thus the $\mathbf b_i$ are primitive, not merely three possible reciprocal translations.

Translate the integration variable by an arbitrary <lattice vector> and use the periodicity of $V$:
$$
V_{\mathbf q}=\int d^3x\,e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x}V(\mathbf x)
=e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf R}\int d^3y\,e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf y}V(\mathbf y)
=e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf R}V_{\mathbf q}.
$$
Thus $(1-e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf R})V_{\mathbf q}=0$ for every $\mathbf R$. A nonzero coefficient requires $e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf R}=1$ for every lattice translation, which is precisely the defining condition for $\mathbf q\in\Lambda^*$.

For an infinite crystal the integral is a <Fourier transform of a periodic distribution>, rather than an ordinary convergent integral. More explicitly, the <Fourier series>
$$
V(\mathbf x)=\sum_{\mathbf G\in\Lambda^*}v_{\mathbf G}e^{-i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf x},\qquad
v_{\mathbf G}=\frac1{\Omega_c}\int_{\mathrm{cell}}d^3x\,e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf x}V(\mathbf x)
$$
gives
$$
\boxed{V_{\mathbf q}=(2\pi)^3\sum_{\mathbf G\in\Lambda^*}v_{\mathbf G}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf q-\mathbf G).}
$$
This is the precise meaning of zero away from the <reciprocal lattice>. A finite specimen instead has broadened <reciprocal-lattice peaks of a finite crystal>; exact delta-function support is an infinite-periodicity idealization.

Separate the entire-system atom index into a cell translation $\mathbf R$ and an index $p$ within a single <primitive unit cell>. The potential becomes
$$
V(\mathbf x)=\sum_{\mathbf R\ \mathrm{all\ cells}}\sum_{p\ \mathrm{one\ cell}}u_p(\mathbf x-\mathbf R-\mathbf r_p).
$$
The same $u_p$ is used in every translated cell because the <atomic basis> repeats. Normalize <plane waves> in volume $\Omega$ by $\langle\mathbf x|\mathbf k\rangle=\Omega^{-1/2}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x}$. For $\mathbf G=\mathbf k'-\mathbf k$, substitution and $\mathbf s=\mathbf x-\mathbf R-\mathbf r_p$ give
$$
\langle\mathbf k'|V|\mathbf k\rangle=\frac1\Omega\left[\sum_{\mathbf R\ \mathrm{all\ cells}}e^{-i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf R}\right]\left[\sum_{p\ \mathrm{one\ cell}}e^{-i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf r_p}\int d^3s\,e^{-i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf s}u_p(\mathbf s)\right].
$$
For a real static scalar potential, define the <atomic scattering factor> in the positive-exponent convention of the question by
$$
\boxed{b_p(\mathbf G)=\int d^3s\,e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf s}u_p(\mathbf s),\qquad S(\mathbf G)=\sum_{p\ \mathrm{one\ cell}}b_p(\mathbf G)e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf r_p}.}
$$
The second bracket in the matrix element is $S(\mathbf G)^*$. At a <reciprocal lattice vector> every term of the first bracket is one, giving the number of cells $N_c$. Therefore the stated transition-rate rule, equivalently the first <Born approximation> and <Fermi's golden rule> up to the final-state kinematic factors, yields
$$
\boxed{I(\mathbf G)\propto|\langle\mathbf k'|V|\mathbf k\rangle|^2=\frac{N_c^2}{\Omega^2}|S(\mathbf G)|^2\propto|S(\mathbf G)|^2.}
$$
The harmless complex conjugation records the distinction between the source's positive-exponent <Fourier transform> and the usual positive-exponent incoming <plane wave>. Reversing every Fourier sign gives the equivalent amplitude convention. Only the cell-translation sum ranges over the full crystal; the <crystal basis structure factor> contains the atoms of one cell.

In cubic coordinates write $\mathbf G=(2\pi/a)(h,k,l)$. The <face-centred cubic selection rule> allows integer triples only when $h,k,l$ are all even or all odd. One way to derive it is to sum the four conventional-cell lattice positions:
$$
F_{\mathrm{fcc}}=1+(-1)^{h+k}+(-1)^{h+l}+(-1)^{k+l}.
$$
This vanishes for mixed parity and equals four otherwise. The two-atom diamond <atomic basis> adds the factor
$$
F_{\mathrm{basis}}=b(\mathbf G)\left[1+e^{i\pi(h+k+l)/2}\right].
$$
For all-odd indices this is nonzero. For all-even indices it vanishes when $h+k+l=2\pmod4$ and survives when the sum is divisible by four: this is <diamond-basis diffraction extinction>.

Exclude the forward beam $000$ and order the remaining families by $h^2+k^2+l^2$. The first is $111$, with squared length three. The candidate $200$, with squared length four, is extinguished by the diamond <atomic basis>. The next allowed family is $220$, with squared length eight. Thus
$$
\boxed{|\mathbf G_1|=\frac{2\pi\sqrt3}{a}\quad(111),\qquad|\mathbf G_2|=\frac{2\pi\sqrt8}{a}=\frac{4\pi\sqrt2}{a}\quad(220).}
$$
For fixed incident <wavelength>, $|\mathbf G|=2k\sin\theta$ increases with scattering angle, so these are the two lowest-angle powder rings. The two atoms are counted per primitive cell; a conventional silicon cube contains four lattice points and eight atoms.

The <Einstein model of a solid> replaces the vibrations of $N$ identical atoms by $3N$ independent <quantum harmonic oscillators> with a common frequency $\omega_E$. Each atom has three displacement directions; the restoring force is harmonic, coupling between different atoms is replaced by a fixed local restoring potential, and anharmonicity and frequency dispersion are neglected. The crystal volume and $\omega_E$ are held fixed when calculating the vibrational <heat capacity at constant volume>.

The one-oscillator <partition function> is
$$
z=\sum_{n=0}^\infty e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_E(n+1/2)}=\frac{e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_E/2}}{1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_E}},\qquad Z=z^{3N}.
$$
Consequently, with $T_E=\hbar\omega_E/k_B$,
$$
U=-\frac{\partial\log Z}{\partial\beta}=3N\hbar\omega_E\left[\frac12+\frac1{e^{T_E/T}-1}\right].
$$
Differentiating at fixed volume gives
$$
\boxed{C_V=3Nk_B\left(\frac{T_E}{T}\right)^2\frac{e^{T_E/T}}{(e^{T_E/T}-1)^2}.}
$$
The <zero-point energy> does not contribute to this derivative. The result tends to the <Dulong-Petit law> $3Nk_B$ at high temperature and vanishes exponentially at low temperature; the latter is a limitation of the single-frequency model for a real acoustic spectrum.

Apply the given small-$x$ expansion with $x=T_E/T$ to the internal energy, including its <zero-point energy>:
$$
\begin{aligned}
U&=3Nk_BT_E\left[\frac12+\frac{T}{T_E}-\frac12+\frac{T_E}{12T}+O(T^{-3})\right]\\
&=3Nk_B\left[T+\frac{T_E^2}{12T}+O(T^{-3})\right].
\end{aligned}
$$
The temperature-independent terms cancel. Taking the derivative gives the <Einstein high-temperature heat-capacity expansion>
$$
C_V=3Nk_B\left[1-\frac{T_E^2}{12T^2}+O(T^{-4})\right].
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{A_0=3Nk_B,\qquad A_1=0,\qquad A_2=-\frac{Nk_BT_E^2}{4}=-\frac{A_0T_E^2}{12}.}
$$
There is no $1/T$ correction to the <heat capacity> even though the internal energy has a $1/T$ correction.

For one Cartesian displacement, the harmonic potential is $m_{\mathrm{Ar}}\omega_E^2x^2/2$. At $T\gg T_E$, the <equipartition theorem> assigns $k_BT/2$ to this quadratic potential term. Hence
$$
\boxed{\langle x^2\rangle=\frac{k_BT}{m_{\mathrm{Ar}}\omega_E^2}=\frac{\hbar^2T}{m_{\mathrm{Ar}}k_BT_E^2}.}
$$
This is a one-coordinate <mean squared displacement>, not the total three-dimensional displacement variance, which is three times larger in the isotropic model.

For comparison, the exact <Einstein mean-square displacement> is
$$
\langle x^2\rangle=\frac{\hbar}{2m_{\mathrm{Ar}}\omega_E}\coth\left(\frac{T_E}{2T}\right).
$$
Using $\coth y\simeq1/y$ recovers the requested high-temperature result. Its nonzero zero-temperature limit is the displacement contribution of the <zero-point energy>.

The <Boltzmann carrier density of a two-dimensional parabolic band> can be derived directly. Use one isotropic parabolic <conduction band>, $E(\mathbf k)=E_c+\hbar^2k^2/(2m_e^*)$, with two spin states per <wave vector>. In the nondegenerate limit the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> reduces to its Boltzmann factor. The number per area is therefore
$$
\begin{aligned}
n&=2\int\frac{d^2k}{(2\pi)^2}\exp\left[-\beta\left(E_c-\mu+\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m_e^*}\right)\right]\\
&=\frac{e^{-\beta(E_c-\mu)}}\pi\int_0^\infty k\,e^{-\beta\hbar^2k^2/(2m_e^*)}\,dk\\
&=\frac{m_e^*k_BT}{\pi\hbar^2}e^{-\beta(E_c-\mu)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
The integral uses $\int_0^\infty ke^{-ak^2}dk=1/(2a)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{C=\frac{k_B}{\pi\hbar^2},\qquad a=1,\qquad b=1.}
$$
Equivalently, the two-dimensional <density of states> is constant, $D_c=m_e^*/(\pi\hbar^2)$, and $n=N_c e^{-\beta(E_c-\mu)}$ with $N_c=D_ck_BT$. These expressions include twofold <spin degeneracy> and one valley. With a general total band degeneracy $g_c$, replace the factor $1/\pi$ by $g_c/(2\pi)$; extra valleys or occupied confinement subbands would change the coefficient.

Near a parabolic <valence band> maximum $E_v$, the missing-electron <effective mass> is $m_h^*>0$. The same two-dimensional state counting gives
$$
\boxed{p=\frac{m_h^*k_BT}{\pi\hbar^2}e^{-\beta(\mu-E_v)}\equiv N_v e^{-\beta(\mu-E_v)}.}
$$
Multiplication eliminates the <chemical potential>, giving the <semiconductor mass-action law>
$$
\boxed{np=N_cN_v e^{-E_g/(k_BT)}=m_e^*m_h^*\left(\frac{k_BT}{\pi\hbar^2}\right)^2e^{-E_g/(k_BT)},\qquad E_g=E_c-E_v.}
$$
For an <intrinsic semiconductor>, <charge neutrality> imposes $n=p=n_i$, so
$$
n_i=\frac{\sqrt{m_e^*m_h^*}\,k_BT}{\pi\hbar^2}e^{-E_g/(2k_BT)}.
$$
The intrinsic <chemical potential> is not exactly midgap when the two <effective masses> differ: $\mu_i=(E_c+E_v)/2+(k_BT/2)\log(m_h^*/m_e^*)$. The exponential suppression still justifies the assumed nondegenerate regime here.

Take the screened <Coulomb potential> of a singly ionized donor as $V(r)=-\kappa/r$, with $\kappa=e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r)$ and $r=|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0|$. The electron's motion is two-dimensional, but its electrostatic interaction is the ordinary three-dimensional $1/r$ interaction restricted to the layer; it is not a logarithmic two-dimensional electrostatic potential.

For the rotationally symmetric bound state, the two-dimensional radial <Schrodinger equation> is
$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e^*}\left(\frac{d^2\psi}{dr^2}+\frac1r\frac{d\psi}{dr}\right)-\frac\kappa r\psi=E\psi.
$$
For $\psi=Ae^{-br}$, the radial Laplacian is $(b^2-b/r)\psi$. Cancel the $1/r$ term to obtain
$$
\boxed{b=\frac{2m_e^*\kappa}{\hbar^2}=\frac2{a_B^*},\qquad E=-\frac{\hbar^2b^2}{2m_e^*}=-\frac{2m_e^*\kappa^2}{\hbar^2}.}
$$
Thus the positive <two-dimensional hydrogenic donor> binding energy is four times the three-dimensional effective Rydberg:
$$
\boxed{E_B=4(13.6057\,\mathrm{eV})\frac{0.026}{15^2}\simeq6.29\,\mathrm{meV},\qquad b\simeq6.55\times10^7\,\mathrm m^{-1}.}
$$
The decay length is $1/b=a_B^*/2\simeq15.3\,\mathrm{nm}$. Normalization, if desired, follows from $1=2\pi|A|^2\int_0^\infty re^{-2br}dr=\pi|A|^2/(2b^2)$, giving $A=b\sqrt{2/\pi}$ up to a constant phase. This ideal result neglects finite well thickness, dielectric mismatch at the interfaces and short-range impurity corrections.

With equal constant <electron mobility> and <hole mobility> $\mathcal M$, the sheet <electrical conductivity> is $\sigma=e\mathcal M(n+p)$. In the intrinsic regime, $n+p\simeq2n_i\propto T e^{-E_g/(2k_BT)}$, so
$$
\log\sigma=\mathrm{constant}+\log T-\frac{E_g}{2k_BT}.
$$
It decreases steeply with increasing $1/T$ on the high-temperature side. There is a small logarithmic curvature rather than a perfectly straight activation line.

In the fully ionized extrinsic regime, <charge neutrality> and the <semiconductor mass-action law> give $n-p=n_d$, $np=n_i^2$, hence
$$
n+p=\sqrt{n_d^2+4n_i^2},\qquad\sigma\simeq e\mathcal M n_d\quad(n_i\ll n_d).
$$
This gives a nearly horizontal region passing through $\sigma(200\,\mathrm K)=0.01\,\Omega^{-1}$. At lower temperatures <semiconductor carrier freeze-out> decreases the ionized donor density and bends the curve downward again.

To locate that bend realistically, include <donor charge-state degeneracy>. For a neutral donor with two spin states and an empty donor with one state, the ionized density is
$$
n_d^+=\frac{n_d}{1+2(n/N_c)e^{E_B/(k_BT)}}.
$$
Solve $n-n_i^2/n=n_d^+$ for the mobile density. In the asymptotic freeze-out regime, where $p$ is negligible and $n\ll n_d$,
$$
n\simeq\sqrt{\frac{n_dN_c}{2}}e^{-E_B/(2k_BT)},\qquad\log\sigma\simeq\mathrm{constant}+\frac12\log T-\frac{E_B}{2k_BT}.
$$
Its activation scale $E_B/2\simeq3.14\,\mathrm{meV}$ is much smaller than the intrinsic scale $E_g/2=180\,\mathrm{meV}$.

Within the specified interval the low-temperature downturn is modest: $N_c(30\,\mathrm K)\simeq2.81\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$, and the partial-ionization equation gives $n(30\,\mathrm K)\simeq0.65n_d$. The sample is not yet deep in the asymptotic freeze-out limit at 30 K. Calibrating the mobility to the stated 200 K value gives representative values
$$
\boxed{\sigma(30\,\mathrm K)\simeq0.0066,\quad\sigma(200\,\mathrm K)=0.010,\quad\sigma(300\,\mathrm K)\simeq0.024,\quad\sigma(600\,\mathrm K)\simeq1.39\quad\text{in }\Omega^{-1}.}
$$
The donor statistical weights were not explicitly supplied, so they are a stated assumption for these illustrative curve details; a one-state donor approximation changes the low-temperature rounding but not the three physical regimes or their activation scales.

A material showing <paramagnetism> has an induced <magnetization> in the direction of a weak applied field, giving positive <magnetic susceptibility>, and no spontaneous magnetization once the field is removed in the paramagnetic phase. Alignment of permanent atomic or nuclear moments is one mechanism.

A material showing <diamagnetism> develops an induced moment opposing the applied field, giving negative <magnetic susceptibility>. Closed electronic shells supply a familiar orbital contribution; the response need not require permanent moments.

A <ferromagnet> has a phase with a spontaneous ordered <magnetization> below its <Curie temperature>, produced by cooperative alignment of moments. Its <magnetic domains> can cancel in a macroscopic zero-field sample, so a measured zero net moment alone does not show that the material lacks <ferromagnetism>.

The <helium atom> has a closed $1s^2$ electronic shell with total electronic spin and orbital angular momentum zero. The nucleus of <helium-4> also has spin zero. At low temperature there is no Curie response from permanent atomic or nuclear moments; the leading atomic response is <diamagnetism>.

Let $n_4$ denote the number of atoms per volume and let $\langle r^2\rangle$ be the mean squared radius of one electron. Use the symmetric <magnetic vector potential> $\mathbf A=\mathbf B\times\mathbf r/2$. Expanding the electron kinetic Hamiltonian $({\mathbf p}+e\mathbf A)^2/(2m_e)$ gives the quadratic field term
$$
\Delta E_{\mathrm{atom}}=\sum_{a=1}^2\frac{e^2B^2}{8m_e}\langle x_a^2+y_a^2\rangle=\frac{2e^2B^2}{12m_e}\langle r^2\rangle,
$$
where isotropy gives $\langle x^2+y^2\rangle=2\langle r^2\rangle/3$. The term linear in orbital angular momentum vanishes in the spherical electronic ground state. Thus
$$
M=-n_4\frac{\partial\Delta E_{\mathrm{atom}}}{\partial B}=-\frac{n_4e^2\langle r^2\rangle}{3m_e}B.
$$
For weak susceptibility, $B\simeq\mu_0H$, so the <Langevin diamagnetic susceptibility> is
$$
\boxed{\chi_4=-\frac{\mu_0n_4e^2\langle r^2\rangle}{3m_e}.}
$$
If the supplied density is a mass density $\rho_4$, use $n_4=\rho_4/(4u)$, with $u$ the atomic mass unit. The result is approximately temperature independent at fixed density. The factor two for the two electrons is already included; $\langle r^2\rangle$ has not been defined as their summed radius.

Let $\mu_a=2.13\mu_N$ be the magnitude of the saturated nuclear moment of <helium-3>, with $\mu_N=e\hbar/(2m_p)$. A spin-one-half nucleus has the two Zeeman energies $\mp\mu_aB$. For independent nuclei the one-site <partition function> and magnetization density are
$$
z=2\cosh\left(\frac{\mu_aB}{k_BT}\right),\qquad M=n_3\mu_a\tanh\left(\frac{\mu_aB}{k_BT}\right).
$$
In the weak-field limit $\mu_aB\ll k_BT$, expand the hyperbolic tangent:
$$
\boxed{\chi_{3,\mathrm{nuc}}=\frac{\mu_0n_3(2.13\mu_N)^2}{k_BT}=\frac{\mu_0\rho_3(2.13\mu_N)^2}{3u\,k_BT}.}
$$
The nuclear moment's negative sign means that its moment points oppositely to its spin; it does not make the thermal <magnetic susceptibility> negative. The response is <paramagnetism> governed by <Curie's law>.

There is no extra factor $1/3$ here: the stated $2.13\mu_N$ is the moment of a fully polarized spin-one-half state. Equivalently, writing the moment operator as $g_N\mu_N\mathbf I$ requires $g_N=-4.26$, and $g_N^2\mu_N^2I(I+1)/3=(2.13\mu_N)^2$. The formula presumes independent localized nuclear moments; exchange correlations invalidate it close to the much lower ordering scale discussed in part (e).

Add the electronic <diamagnetism> of each helium atom to the nuclear <magnetic susceptibility>, using the same $n_3$ for both contributions in the <helium-3> sample:
$$
\chi_{3,\mathrm{tot}}\simeq\mu_0n_3\left[\frac{(2.13\mu_N)^2}{k_BT}-\frac{e^2\langle r^2\rangle}{3m_e}\right].
$$
The density cancels when the two terms balance. The sign-change temperature in the independent-atom approximation is therefore
$$
\boxed{T_0=\frac{3m_e(2.13\mu_N)^2}{e^2k_B\langle r^2\rangle}.}
$$
No numerical atomic radius was supplied, so the exact numerical value remains conditional on that electronic length scale. Taking $\langle r^2\rangle\sim a_0^2$, where $a_0$ is the <Bohr radius>, gives $T_0\simeq0.32\,\mathrm K$. A slightly more explicit estimate uses the <helium variational effective charge> $\zeta=27/16$, for which a hydrogenic $1s$ orbital has $\langle r^2\rangle=3a_0^2/\zeta^2\simeq1.05a_0^2$, giving
$$
\boxed{T_0\approx0.3\,\mathrm K\quad\text{as an atomic-model estimate}.}
$$
Above this scale the electronic contribution is negative and dominates; below it the positive nuclear contribution dominates within the independent-moment approximation. The estimate is not a precision prediction of a real interacting helium phase.

Let $s=\langle S_i^z\rangle$ be uniform, with dimensionless spin operators $S=1/2$. The factor $1/2$ in the source Hamiltonian removes double counting of pairs. Decoupling $\mathbf S_i\cdot\mathbf S_j$ about a uniform polarization gives the one-site <mean-field theory> Hamiltonian
$$
H_i^{\mathrm{MF}}=-sJ_0S_i^z+\mathrm{constant},\qquad J_0=\sum_jJ_{ij}.
$$
A body-centred-cubic site has eight nearest neighbors and six next-nearest neighbors, so
$$
\frac{J_0}{k_B}=8(1\,\mathrm{mK})+6(-0.5\,\mathrm{mK})=5\,\mathrm{mK}.
$$
For a spin one half in this effective field, the <mean-field self-consistency equation> is
$$
s=\frac12\tanh\left(\frac{J_0s}{2k_BT}\right).
$$
Linearizing at the continuous onset gives $s=J_0s/(4k_BT)+O(s^3)$, hence
$$
\boxed{T_C^{\mathrm{MF}}=\frac{J_0}{4k_B}=1.25\,\mathrm{mK}.}
$$
Equivalently, the general spin-$S$ result is $k_BT_C^{\mathrm{MF}}=S(S+1)J_0/3$. The antiferromagnetic next-nearest-neighbor exchange reduces the uniform ordering scale rather than contributing its absolute magnitude. The negative nuclear <g-factor> does not change this exchange instability; aligning the spins also aligns their moments with one another.

For these particular competing couplings, the uniform mode is indeed the largest exchange eigenvalue. Writing $x=\cos(q_xa/2)$, $y=\cos(q_ya/2)$ and $z=\cos(q_za/2)$ gives
$$
\frac{J(\mathbf q)}{k_B\,\mathrm{mK}}=8xyz-2(x^2+y^2+z^2)+3\le5,
$$
with equality at $\mathbf q=0$ modulo <reciprocal lattice vectors>. To see the bound, replace a negative $xyz$ by its absolute value for an upper bound, let $t=(|xyz|)^{1/3}$, and use $x^2+y^2+z^2\ge3t^2$. The expression is at most $8t^3-6t^2+3$, whose maximum on $0\le t\le1$ is five. Thus another modulation does not preempt the stated ferromagnetic onset at this level of <mean-field theory>. Fluctuations can change the actual transition temperature.
