The ordered <crystal structure> consists of a <primitive cubic lattice> with the four-site <atomic basis>
$$
\mathrm{Au}:(0,0,0),\qquad
\mathrm{Cu}:\left(0,\frac12,\frac12\right),\left(\frac12,0,\frac12\right),\left(\frac12,\frac12,0\right),
$$
in <fractional crystal coordinates>. The drawing shows all boundary representatives of these sites. Eight corner <gold> <chemical atoms>, shared among eight <unit cells> each, contribute one <gold> <chemical atom>. Six face-centred <copper> <chemical atoms>, shared between two <unit cells> each, contribute three <copper> <chemical atoms>. Thus
$$
\boxed{N_{\rm Au}=8\times\frac18=1,\qquad N_{\rm Cu}=6\times\frac12=3,}
$$
which gives the required <stoichiometry>. Although the unlabelled positions form a <face-centered cubic lattice>, a face-centering <lattice translation> exchanges <gold> and <copper> and therefore fails to preserve the ordered <crystal structure>. Its <Bravais lattice> is <primitive cubic lattice>.

For the <reciprocal lattice vector> $\mathbf G_{hkl}=(2\pi/a)(h,k,l)$, sum the <atomic scattering factors> with the <atomic basis> phase factors. Taking the positive phase convention gives the <structure factor>
$$
S_{hkl}=f_{\rm Au}(G_{hkl})+f_{\rm Cu}(G_{hkl})
\left[e^{i\pi(k+l)}+e^{i\pi(h+l)}+e^{i\pi(h+k)}\right].
$$
Changing the phase convention complex-conjugates the <structure factor> and leaves the <diffraction> <wave intensity> unchanged. Since the <Miller indices> are integers,
$$
\alpha_{hkl}=(-1)^{k+l}+(-1)^{h+l}+(-1)^{h+k}.
$$
If all three <Miller indices> have the same <integer parity>, all pairwise sums are even. Otherwise exactly one pairwise sum is even and two are odd. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\alpha_{hkl}=\begin{cases}3,&h,k,l\text{ all even or all odd},\\-1,&\text{mixed parity}.\end{cases}}
$$
The corresponding <structure factors> are $f_{\rm Au}+3f_{\rm Cu}$ and $f_{\rm Au}-f_{\rm Cu}$. Reflections with mixed <integer parity> are <superlattice reflections> that reveal <chemical ordering in alloys>.

In <elastic scattering>, the incoming and outgoing <wave vectors> have equal magnitude $k_0=2\pi/\lambda$. Their mutual angle is $2\theta_{hkl}$. The <scattering vector> $\mathbf q=\mathbf k_f-\mathbf k_i$ therefore has magnitude
$$
q^2=2k_0^2\left[1-\cos(2\theta_{hkl})\right]
=4k_0^2\sin^2\theta_{hkl}.
$$
The <elastic Bragg scattering condition> is $\mathbf q=\mathbf G_{hkl}$, giving
$$
\boxed{G_{hkl}=\frac{4\pi}{\lambda}\sin\theta_{hkl}.}
$$
Equivalently, $G_{hkl}=2\pi/d_{hkl}$ turns this relation into <Bragg's law>, $2d_{hkl}\sin\theta_{hkl}=\lambda$, with the reflection order incorporated into the <Miller indices>.

\boxed{2\theta_{110}=33.762^\circ\simeq33.8^\circ,\qquad
2\theta_{111}=41.666^\circ\simeq41.7^\circ.}
$$
These are the full scattering angles; the <Bragg angles> themselves are half as large.

f_{\rm Au}(111)&=51.5618,&f_{\rm Cu}(111)&=20.7241.

The <stoichiometry> fixes the site <probabilities> to $1/4$ <gold> and $3/4$ <copper>. Every site in the average <crystal structure> therefore has the same <atomic scattering factor>
$$
\bar f=\frac14f_{\rm Au}+\frac34f_{\rm Cu}.
$$
The average <structure factor> is
$$
\bar S_{hkl}=\bar f\left[1+(-1)^{k+l}+(-1)^{h+l}+(-1)^{h+k}\right].
$$
At $(110)$ the bracket vanishes, while at $(111)$ it is four. Hence
$$
\boxed{I_{110}^{\rm Bragg}=0,\qquad
\bar S_{111}=4\bar f=f_{\rm Au}+3f_{\rm Cu}=S_{111}^{\rm ordered}.}
$$
The $(111)$ <powder diffraction multiplicity> is unchanged, so its coherent <Bragg diffraction> <wave intensity> equals the ordered value under the stated equal-<lattice-constant> approximation.

The zero refers to the coherent <Bragg diffraction> peak of the average <crystal structure>. A random <alloy> can still give <occupational diffuse scattering> at the same <scattering vector>: the average of the squared amplitude includes fluctuations as well as the square of the average amplitude. Such background is not a surviving $(110)$ <Bragg diffraction> peak.

Replace every coloured site in the drawing by an identical site carrying $\bar f$. The face-centering <lattice translations> now preserve the average <crystal structure>, whose <Bravais lattice> is a <face-centered cubic lattice>. One choice of <primitive lattice translation vectors> is

\mathbf a_1=\frac a2(0,1,1),\qquad

A coherent <Bragg diffraction> peak requires $\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf a_j\in2\pi\mathbb Z$ for all three <primitive lattice translation vectors>. This is the <face-centred cubic selection rule>: $(110)$ is forbidden and $(111)$ is allowed. The restoration of these <lattice translations> explains the disappearance of the ordered <superlattice reflections>.

Specify the normalization of the <alloy sublattice order parameter> rather than assuming that its endpoint values uniquely define it. Let $p_c$ be the <probability> of <gold> occupation at a corner site and define
$$
\rho=\frac{p_c-1/4}{1-1/4},\qquad p_c=\frac{1+3\rho}{4}.
$$
The complementary <copper> <probability> is $3(1-\rho)/4$, so the corner-site <atomic scattering factor> is
$$
\boxed{\langle f_c\rangle=\frac14\left[(1+3\rho)f_{\rm Au}+3(1-\rho)f_{\rm Cu}\right].}
$$
The <stoichiometry> provides one <gold> <chemical atom> per four-site <unit cell>. If all three face sublattices are equivalent, their <gold> occupation <probability> $p_f$ obeys $p_c+3p_f=1$. Therefore
$$
p_f=\frac{1-\rho}{4},\qquad 1-p_f=\frac{3+\rho}{4},\qquad
\boxed{\langle f_f\rangle=\frac14\left[(1-\rho)f_{\rm Au}+(3+\rho)f_{\rm Cu}\right].}
$$
At $\rho=1$ the corner and face sites carry pure <gold> and pure <copper>; at $\rho=0$ they have identical statistical compositions. These formulas describe the branch with $0\leq\rho\leq1$ and equivalent face-site averages.

The boundary of the first <Brillouin zone> is $|k|=\pi/a$. The unperturbed <free electron model> assigns the <kinetic energy>
$$
\boxed{E_{\rm ZB}=\frac{\hbar^2\pi^2}{2m_ea^2},}
$$
where $m_e$ is the bare <electron mass> and $\hbar$ is the <reduced Planck constant>. This is the free <kinetic energy> at the <Brillouin zone> boundary, not the already split <energy band> edge. A constant mean <potential energy> may be absorbed into the choice of zero of <energy>.

Write $U(x)=-e\phi(x)$ for the <potential energy> of an <electron> in an <electrostatic potential> $\phi$, where $e>0$. With $G=2\pi/a$, the coupling in the <nearly-free electron model> is the <Fourier coefficient>
$$
\boxed{V_G=\frac1a\int_0^a U(x)e^{-iGx}\,dx
=-\frac ea\int_0^a\phi(x)e^{-iGx}\,dx.}
$$
If a symbol $V(x)$ already denotes the <electron> <potential energy>, as in part (c), it replaces $U(x)$ without another factor $-e$.

At the <Brillouin zone> boundary the two resonant <plane waves> have unperturbed <energy> $E_{\rm ZB}$. Their <Hamiltonian operator> in the two-state basis has diagonal entries $E_{\rm ZB}$ and off-diagonal entries $V_G,V_G^*$; its <eigenvalues> are $E_{\rm ZB}\pm|V_G|$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\Delta=2|V_G|=2\epsilon E_{\rm ZB}.}
$$
This is the local <band gap> in the two-wave <nearly-free electron model>.

For zero coupling, $\sqrt{4y^2}=2|y|$, and the two folded <energy bands> are
$$
E_-(y)=E_{\rm ZB}(1-|y|)^2,\qquad
E_+(y)=E_{\rm ZB}(1+|y|)^2.

They touch at $y=0$, where the underlying free <plane-wave> branches cross. A nonzero <Fourier coefficient> replaces this crossing by an <avoided crossing>, with $E_\pm(0)=E_{\rm ZB}(1\pm\epsilon)$. The lower <energy band> moves downward and the upper <energy band> upward. Both branches are smooth near $y=0$ for $\epsilon>0$.

For a long chain of length $L$ with periodic boundary conditions, each <spin> has $L\,dk/(2\pi)$ <quantum states> in a <wavenumber> interval $dk$. Including two <spin> states, a full <energy band> contains $2L/a$ <electrons>. The <state counting in a Bloch band> therefore gives the <energy density>
$$
\mathcal U(\epsilon)=\frac1\pi\int_{\text{one zone}}E_-(k)\,dk
=\frac{E_{\rm ZB}}a\int_{-1}^1
\left[1+y^2-\sqrt{4y^2+\epsilon^2}\right]dy.
$$
The integral of $1+y^2$ is $8/3$. For $\epsilon>0$, the even square-root integral is
$$
\int_{-1}^1\sqrt{4y^2+\epsilon^2}\,dy
=\sqrt{4+\epsilon^2}+\frac{\epsilon^2}{2}
\ln\frac{2+\sqrt{4+\epsilon^2}}{\epsilon}.
$$
For example, this follows by setting $u=2y$, applying the supplied antiderivative, and evaluating both endpoints; the lower-end <logarithm> contributes $\ln\epsilon$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathcal U(\epsilon)=\frac{E_{\rm ZB}}a
\left[\frac83-\sqrt{4+\epsilon^2}
-\frac{\epsilon^2}{2}\ln\frac{2+\sqrt{4+\epsilon^2}}{\epsilon}\right].}
$$
The continuous zero-coupling limit is $\mathcal U(0)=2E_{\rm ZB}/(3a)$. This agrees with directly integrating the one-dimensional <free electron model> up to $k_F=\pi/a$ at two <electrons> per <unit cell>.

At every $y$ and every $\epsilon>0$,
$$
\sqrt{4y^2+\epsilon^2}>2|y|,
$$
so the lower <energy band> lies strictly below its zero-coupling counterpart. Its <state counting in a Bloch band> and complete occupation are unchanged. Subtracting the two <energy densities> directly gives
$$
\mathcal U(\epsilon)-\mathcal U(0)
=-\frac{E_{\rm ZB}}a\int_{-1}^1
\left[\sqrt{4y^2+\epsilon^2}-2|y|\right]dy<0.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathcal U(\epsilon)<\mathcal U(0)\quad(\epsilon>0).}
$$
Equality holds at $\epsilon=0$. This is the downward displacement of the filled lower <energy band> in the sketch. The upward displacement of the empty upper <energy band> contributes nothing to the zero-<temperature> electronic <energy density>.

For $\epsilon\to0^+$, the <Taylor expansions> needed in the exact <energy density> are
$$
\sqrt{4+\epsilon^2}=2+\frac{\epsilon^2}{4}+O(\epsilon^4),\qquad
\ln\frac{2+\sqrt{4+\epsilon^2}}{\epsilon}
=\ln\frac4\epsilon+O(\epsilon^2).
$$
Substitution yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal U(\epsilon)
&=\frac{E_{\rm ZB}}a\left[\frac23-\frac{\epsilon^2}{4}
-\frac{\epsilon^2}{2}\ln\frac4\epsilon+O(\epsilon^4)\right]\\
&=\frac{E_{\rm ZB}}a\left[\frac23-\frac{\epsilon^2}{4}
\left(1-2\ln\frac\epsilon4\right)+O(\epsilon^4)\right].
\end{aligned}
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\alpha=1,\qquad\beta=2.}
$$
Here <Big O notation> describes the neglected small-coupling terms, and the <logarithm> is natural. The nonanalytic $\epsilon^2\ln\epsilon$ term comes from the formerly degenerate <quantum states> near the <Brillouin zone> boundary; a uniform nondegenerate <perturbation theory> expansion of the integrand would fail there. This is the same mechanism as the <Peierls energy gain>.

Regard $V(x)$ as an <electron> <potential energy>, and take $G=2\pi/a$. Each cosine supplies half its coefficient to each of its two <Fourier coefficients>. The nonzero coefficients are
$$
V_{\pm G}=-\pi V_0\delta,\qquad
V_{\pm2G}=\frac{V_0}{2},\qquad
V_{\pm3G}=+\pi V_0\delta.
$$
The <Fourier coefficient> connecting the two <plane waves> at $k=\pm\pi/a$ is $V_G$, so the two-wave <band gap> is
$$
\boxed{\Delta=2\pi|V_0|\,|\delta|,\qquad
\epsilon=\frac{\pi|V_0|}{E_{\rm ZB}}|\delta|.}
$$
At $\delta=0$ the actual <periodic potential> has period $a/2$ and no Fourier harmonic at $G$. The crossing at the artificially halved <Brillouin zone> boundary then has zero <band gap>. Higher-order weak-potential corrections can change the proportionality coefficient at nonzero $\delta$; the displayed coefficient belongs to the given two-wave approximation.

There is also a small-displacement qualification in the source: the claimed positions of the <potential energy> minima are correct to first order in $\delta$, not exactly. Put $t=2\pi x/a$. Then
$$
\frac1{V_0}\frac{dV}{dt}=-2\sin2t+2\pi\delta(\sin t-3\sin3t).
$$
At $\delta=0.01$ and $x/a=1/4-\delta=0.24$, this equals approximately $-2.802\times10^{-3}$, rather than zero. For $V_0>0$, expanding about $x/a=1/4$ and $3/4$ gives minima at $1/4-\delta+O(\delta^3)$ and $3/4+\delta+O(\delta^3)$. This does not alter the leading <Fourier coefficient> or the linear opening of the <band gap>.

Without the <Peierls distortion>, adjacent <chemical atoms> are separated by $a/2$. The <primitive unit cell> contains one <chemical atom> and contributes one <electron>. A <energy band> with two <spin> states accommodates two <electrons> per <primitive unit cell>, so the first <energy band> is half filled. Its true first <Brillouin zone> extends to $\pm2\pi/a$, while the <Fermi points> are at $\pm\pi/a$.

Describing the same chain with a doubled <unit cell> of length $a$ merely folds the half-filled <energy band> into two branches that touch at those <Fermi points>. The unbroken <lattice translation> by $a/2$ prevents an artificial <band gap> there. A <Peierls distortion> breaks that smaller <lattice translation> and supplies the missing <Fourier coefficient> at $2k_F=G$; the lower folded <energy band> can then be completely filled and separated from the upper <energy band>. Thus the zero-<band-gap> result at $\delta=0$ agrees with the original one-<electron> <band filling>.

Both <electrons> per doubled <unit cell> have been included. Use the same normalization for the <elastic energy> and electronic <energy density>: if $\kappa\delta^2$ is an <energy> per <unit cell>, divide it by $a$ first; if it is already per length, use it directly. Write its coefficient per length as $K>0$ and put $A=\pi^2V_0^2/(4aE_{\rm ZB})>0$. The small-distortion change in total <energy density> is
$$
\mathcal U_{\rm tot}(\delta)-\mathcal U_{\rm tot}(0)
=\delta^2\left[K-A+2A\ln\frac{C|\delta|}{4}\right]+O(\delta^4).
$$
No minimization calculation is needed. For arbitrarily small nonzero $|\delta|$, the <logarithm> is negative with arbitrarily large magnitude. Hence the negative electronic <Peierls energy gain>, proportional to $\delta^2\ln(1/|\delta|)$, eventually exceeds any fixed positive quadratic <elastic energy>. There are distorted configurations with lower <energy> than the undistorted configuration for every finite $K$ and nonzero coupling. The ideal zero-<temperature> infinite-chain model therefore has
$$
\boxed{|\delta_{\rm eq}|>0.}
$$
The source's $\delta_{\rm eq}>0$ is correct when $\delta$ denotes a nonnegative distortion amplitude. If $\delta$ is signed, the <energy density> is even and the two signs describe equally stable patterns related by a <lattice translation>. For example, $V(x+a/2;\delta)=V(x;-\delta)$, so a negative equilibrium distortion has the same <energy> as the positive one. The zero-coupling case $V_0=0$ instead has its minimum at $\delta=0$. The argument assumes a coupled chain, complete lower-<energy-band> filling and zero <temperature>; it does not by itself establish finite-<temperature> <long-range order> in an isolated one-dimensional system.

Let $\beta=1/(k_BT)$, with $k_B$ the <Boltzmann constant>, $T$ the absolute <temperature>, and $\mu$ the common equilibrium <chemical potential>. The <conduction band> minimum and <valence band> maximum are $\epsilon_c$ and $\epsilon_v$; their separation is the <band gap> $E_g=\epsilon_c-\epsilon_v$. The positive parameters $m_e,m_h$ in the supplied <density of states> functions are the <band effective masses> of <electrons> and <electron holes>, not necessarily the bare <electron mass>.

The <electron> occupation is the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> $f(\epsilon)=[e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}+1]^{-1}$. The appropriate statistical factors are $f_c=f$ for <electrons> and $f_v=1-f$ for <electron holes>. In a <nondegenerate semiconductor>, $\epsilon_c-\mu\gg k_BT$ and $\mu-\epsilon_v\gg k_BT$, so the <Boltzmann approximation> gives
$$
f_c(\epsilon)\simeq e^{-\beta(\epsilon-\mu)},\qquad
f_v(\epsilon)\simeq e^{\beta(\epsilon-\mu)}.
$$
For the <conduction band>, set $u=\epsilon-\epsilon_c$ and then $\xi=\beta u$. The supplied integral yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
n&=\frac{(2m_e)^{3/2}}{2\pi^2\hbar^3}
 e^{-\beta(\epsilon_c-\mu)}\int_0^\infty u^{1/2}e^{-\beta u}\,du\\
&=2\left(\frac{m_e}{2\pi\beta\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}
 e^{-\beta(\epsilon_c-\mu)}=N_c e^{-\beta(\epsilon_c-\mu)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
For the <valence band>, set $u=\epsilon_v-\epsilon$ to obtain
$$
p=N_v e^{-\beta(\mu-\epsilon_v)},\qquad
N_v=2\left(\frac{m_h}{2\pi\beta\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}.
$$
The <effective density of states> factors $N_c,N_v$ include the two <spin> states already present in the supplied <density of states> functions. Multiplication cancels the <chemical potential> and gives the <semiconductor mass-action law>
$$
\boxed{np=N_cN_v e^{-\beta E_g}
=4\left(\frac{\sqrt{m_em_h}\,k_BT}{2\pi\hbar^2}\right)^3
 e^{-E_g/(k_BT)}=n_i^2.}
$$
Here $n_i=\sqrt{N_cN_v}e^{-E_g/(2k_BT)}$ is the <intrinsic carrier concentration>. Equality $n=p=n_i$ additionally requires an electrically neutral <intrinsic semiconductor>. The product law also applies to an equilibrium doped <semiconductor> while both populations remain nondegenerate. The derivation assumes parabolic <energy bands>, the stated <spin degeneracy>, and a single common <chemical potential>.

For a single <impurity level in a crystal> with allowed occupations zero and one and equal occupied/empty statistical weights, the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> gives
$$
f_D=\frac1{e^{\beta(\epsilon_D-\mu)}+1}.

Since $n_{\rm full}=N_D f_D$ and $n_{\rm empty}=N_D(1-f_D)$,
$$
\boxed{\frac{n_{\rm full}}{n_{\rm empty}}=\frac{f_D}{1-f_D}
=e^{\beta(\mu-\epsilon_D)}.}
$$
The same result follows from the two <grand canonical ensemble> weights $1$ and $e^{-\beta(\epsilon_D-\mu)}$.

The equal-statistical-weight convention is necessary for the printed formula when $n_{\rm full}$ and $n_{\rm empty}$ count whole <donor impurities>. More generally, <donor charge-state degeneracy> gives $n_{\rm full}/n_{\rm empty}=(g_{\rm full}/g_{\rm empty})e^{\beta(\mu-\epsilon_D)}$. An ordinary singly occupied donor with two <spin> orientations and a nondegenerate empty state has ratio $2e^{\beta(\mu-\epsilon_D)}$; at $\mu=\epsilon_D$ its occupied-to-empty ratio is two, a concrete counterexample to treating the printed expression as universal. The following part uses the paper's ratio-one convention.

Assume uncompensated <donor impurities>, negligible intrinsic <electron holes>, and a nondegenerate <conduction band>. Then <charge neutrality> equates the conduction <electron number density> to the ionized-donor <number density>:
$$
n=n_{\rm empty},\qquad n_{\rm full}=N_D-n.
$$
The low-<temperature> condition relative to the <band gap> supports neglect of intrinsic carriers; one also needs $n_i\ll n$ and the <Boltzmann approximation> for the conduction <electrons>. Combining part (b)(i) with the <effective density of states> result gives
$$
\frac{N_D-n}{n}=e^{\beta(\mu-\epsilon_D)},\qquad
n=N_c e^{\beta(\mu-\epsilon_c)}.
$$
Eliminate the <chemical potential> by writing $e^{\beta(\mu-\epsilon_D)}=(n/N_c)e^{\beta(\epsilon_c-\epsilon_D)}$. With the donor binding <energy> $E_i=\epsilon_c-\epsilon_D>0$,
$$
\boxed{\frac{n^2}{N_D-n}=N_c e^{-\beta E_i}
=2\left(\frac{m_e}{2\pi\beta\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}e^{-\beta E_i}.}
$$
Writing $A(T)=N_c e^{-\beta E_i}$, the physical root of the resulting <quadratic equation> is
$$
n=\frac{\sqrt{A^2+4AN_D}-A}{2}.
$$
This lies between zero and $N_D$. At <semiconductor carrier freeze-out>, $A\ll N_D$, it becomes $n\simeq\sqrt{N_DN_c}\,e^{-E_i/(2k_BT)}$; at essentially complete ionization, $A\gg N_D$, it tends to $N_D$. Including <donor charge-state degeneracy> would replace $A$ by $A\,g_{\rm empty}/g_{\rm full}$.

There are three regimes. At the lowest <temperatures>, most <donor impurities> retain their <electrons>; thermal ionization gives <semiconductor carrier freeze-out>. The result from part (b) is
$$
n\simeq\sqrt{N_DN_c}\,e^{-E_i/(2k_BT)}\propto T^{3/4}e^{-E_i/(2k_BT)}.
$$
This produces the approximately straight descending low-<temperature> tail on the <logarithmic scale> plot against $1/T$. The activation <energy> in the exponential is $E_i/2$, because the <chemical potential> adjusts with donor ionization.

At intermediate <temperatures>, the <donor impurities> are essentially fully ionized while intrinsic excitation remains small. <Charge neutrality> gives $n\simeq N_D$, producing the extrinsic plateau, roughly from $20$ to $40\,\mathrm K$ in the figure.

At higher <temperatures>, excitation from the <valence band> to the <conduction band> creates <electron-hole pairs>. The <intrinsic carrier concentration> grows as $n_i\propto T^{3/2}e^{-E_g/(2k_BT)}$ for constant parabolic <band effective masses> and constant <band gap>. With ionized <donor impurities>, <charge neutrality> and the <semiconductor mass-action law> give
$$
n-p=N_D,\qquad np=n_i^2,\qquad
n=\frac{N_D+\sqrt{N_D^2+4n_i^2}}2.
$$
Thus $n\simeq n_i+N_D/2$ when $n_i\gg N_D$. This explains the renewed rapid rise and the eventual intrinsic branch; that branch is governed by the <band gap>, rather than the donor binding <energy>.

For a fixed open-shell <electron configuration> in the <LS coupling> regime, <Hund's rules> select the free-<ion> ground term in the following order:

* Maximize the total <spin> $S$, subject to the <Pauli exclusion principle>.
* Among terms with that $S$, maximize the total <orbital angular momentum> $L$.
* For a less-than-half-filled subshell choose the smallest total <angular momentum>, $J=|L-S|$; for a more-than-half-filled subshell choose the largest, $J=L+S$. A half-filled subshell has $L=0$ and $J=S$.

The first two rules come from the <Coulomb interaction> between <electrons> within the partially filled subshell, including its <exchange interaction>. Parallel <spins> require an antisymmetric spatial <wavefunction>, reducing close approach and often lowering the repulsive <Coulomb interaction> <energy>. Among the allowed high-<spin> terms, the angular dependence of the <Coulomb interaction> generally favours the largest $L$; a simple magnetic alignment argument alone does not derive this second ordering rule.

The weaker <spin-orbit coupling> then splits a selected $L,S$ term. In <LS coupling>, its term-level <energy> is proportional to
$$
\langle\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S\rangle
=\frac{\hbar^2}{2}\left[J(J+1)-L(L+1)-S(S+1)\right].
$$
The effective sign appropriate to less-than-half versus more-than-half filling gives the third rule. These rules assume the electrostatic term separation dominates <spin-orbit coupling>; strong <crystal field splitting> or other coupling regimes require further analysis.

In weak <magnetic field> and the regime of <paramagnetism>, localized <magnetic moments> with total <angular momentum> $J$ and <Landé g-factor> $\widetilde g$ have SI <volume magnetic susceptibility>
$$
\chi_v=\frac{\mu_0 n_m(\widetilde g\mu_B)^2J(J+1)}{3k_B(T-\Theta)},
$$
within the <Curie-Weiss law>. Here $n_m$ counts magnetic <ions> per volume, $\mu_0$ is the <vacuum permeability>, $\mu_B$ is the <Bohr magneton>, and $\Theta$ is the <Weiss temperature>. The factor $J(J+1)/3$ is the zero-field average of the squared angular projection over $m_J=-J,\ldots,J$.

For one magnetic <ion> per formula unit, a molar volume $V_m$ contains $N_A$ magnetic <ions>, where $N_A$ is the <Avogadro constant>. Consequently $n_mV_m=N_A$, and the <molar magnetic susceptibility> is
$$
\chi_{\rm mol}=V_m\chi_v
=\frac{\mu_0N_A\mu_B^2}{3k_B}\,
\frac{\widetilde g^{\,2}J(J+1)}{T-\Theta}.
$$
Defining the dimensionless <effective paramagnetic moment> $m_{\rm eff}$ by $\mu_{\rm eff}=m_{\rm eff}\mu_B$, numerical substitution gives
$$
\frac{\mu_0N_A\mu_B^2}{3k_B}=1.5714\times10^{-6}\,
\mathrm{m^3\,K\,mol^{-1}},\qquad
\boxed{\chi_{\rm mol}=(1.57\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^3\,K\,mol^{-1}})
\frac{m_{\rm eff}^2}{T-\Theta}.}
$$
The normalization is per mole of formula units, which here also means per mole of magnetic <ions>.

The <Weiss temperature> $\Theta$ is the extrapolated zero of $1/\chi$ in a <Curie-Weiss law> fit. It summarizes the leading high-<temperature> effect of interactions between <magnetic moments>. For the sign convention
$$
H_{\rm ex}=-\sum_{i<j}\mathcal J_{ij}\,\mathbf J_i\cdot\mathbf J_j,
$$
where $\mathbf J_i$ are dimensionless <angular momentum operators>, <mean-field theory> gives
$$
\Theta=\frac{J(J+1)}{3k_B}\sum_j\mathcal J_{ij}.
$$
Thus positive net <exchange interaction> in this convention favours parallel <magnetic moments>, giving $\Theta>0$ and tendencies towards <ferromagnetism>; negative net <exchange interaction> favours antiparallel <magnetic moments>, giving $\Theta<0$ and <antiferromagnetic> tendencies. Competing interactions can partially cancel in this sum. The <Weiss temperature> need not equal the actual ordering <temperature>, and its sign alone is not a complete determination of the low-<temperature> ordered structure.

Each $f$ <electron> has orbital <angular momentum quantum number> $l=3$. Place the two <spins> parallel, so $S=1$. The <Pauli exclusion principle> then requires distinct orbital projections. The largest allowed total projection is $M_L=3+2=5$, obtained from $m_l=3$ and $m_l=2$, giving the maximum allowed $L=5$. A larger $M_L=6$ would place two parallel-<spin> <electrons> in the same <atomic orbital> and is forbidden.

The $f$ subshell holds fourteen <electrons>; $f^2$ is less than half filled. The third of <Hund's rules> therefore selects $J=L-S=4$. Hence
$$
\boxed{L=5,\qquad S=1,\qquad J=4,\qquad \text{ground term }{}^3H_4.}
$$
The <term symbol> uses $2S+1=3$ and the spectroscopic letter $H$ for $L=5$. The <praseodymium> <ion> retains an orbital contribution, so a spin-only estimate would be inappropriate.
