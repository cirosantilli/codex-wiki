Determining <antiferromagnetic> <magnetic order> is a good reason to use <neutron scattering>. The <neutron> has a <magnetic dipole moment>, so its <scattering amplitude> includes the spatial arrangement of electronic <magnetic moments>. Alternating <magnetic moments> can produce <magnetic neutron diffraction> peaks even when there is no change of the nuclear <crystal structure>. Ordinary nonresonant <X-ray diffraction> primarily measures the <electron density>, and is much less sensitive to this <magnetic order>.

The <neutron> is nonrelativistic at this <kinetic energy>. Its <de Broglie wavelength> is
$$
\lambda=\frac{h}{\sqrt{2m_nE}}
=\frac{6.62607015\times10^{-34}}{\sqrt{2(1.67493\times10^{-27})(25\times10^{-3})(1.602176634\times10^{-19})}}
=0.180891\,\mathrm{nm}.
$$
In <elastic scattering>, <Bragg's law> gives $2d\sin\theta=\lambda$. The measured total deflection is $2\theta$, so it must be halved before taking the sine. The reflection order is absorbed into the <Miller indices> of the diffracting <lattice planes>. In the order of the four measured rings, the <interplanar spacings> are
$$
\boxed{d_1=0.42457\,\mathrm{nm},\quad d_2=0.29995\,\mathrm{nm},\quad d_3=0.24462\,\mathrm{nm},\quad d_4=0.21203\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
The last digits give the numerical calculation, rather than accuracy beyond the supplied angular precision.

For the <cubic crystal system>, the <Miller indices> satisfy
$$
\frac1{d_{hkl}^2}=\frac{h^2+k^2+l^2}{a^2}.
$$
The inverse squared <interplanar spacings> have ratios approximately $1:2.004:3.012:4.010$. With the <lattice constant> close to $0.4\,\mathrm{nm}$, the appropriate squared-index sums are $1,2,3,4$. Thus the four <powder diffraction> rings are
$$
\boxed{\{100\},\quad\{110\},\quad\{111\},\quad\{200\}.}
$$
The braces collect all symmetry-related <lattice planes> with the same <interplanar spacing>. In particular, $(100)$ is forbidden for both a <body-centered cubic lattice> and a <face-centered cubic lattice>, but occurs for a <primitive cubic lattice>. These observations identify the <Bravais lattice> as <primitive cubic lattice>, with a multi-atom <atomic basis>, consistent with a <perovskite structure>.

Each ring independently gives $a_j=d_j\sqrt{j}$:
$$
a_1=0.42457,\qquad a_2=0.42419,\qquad a_3=0.42369,\qquad a_4=0.42406\qquad (\mathrm{nm}).
$$
Their average is $0.42413\,\mathrm{nm}$. Allowing for the rounded measured angles, a suitable final value is
$$
\boxed{a\simeq0.424\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
A larger centred <unit cell> could relabel reflections, but would conflict with the stated approximate <lattice constant>.

An applied <magnetic field> changes the alignment of the manganese <magnetic moments>. The component of their <magnetic structure factor> perpendicular to the <scattering vector> contributes to <magnetic neutron diffraction>. A field-induced uniform <magnetization> has the same translational periodicity as the nuclear <crystal structure>, so its <scattering amplitude> can occur at the existing <reciprocal lattice vectors>. The <diffraction> <wave intensities> can therefore change without moving the peaks. Their angular positions still measure the same <interplanar spacings>. This mechanism does not require a measurable change of the <lattice constant>.

A consistent mechanism is a transition to G-type <antiferromagnetism> at the <Néel temperature>. On the manganese <primitive cubic lattice>, take the <magnetic moments> to be
$$
\mathbf m_{j_xj_yj_z}=\mathbf m_0(-1)^{j_x+j_y+j_z}.
$$
Every nearest neighbour has the opposite <magnetic moment>. The nuclear <crystal structure> can retain its original <lattice constant>, while the <magnetic order> introduces the propagation <wavevector>
$$
\mathbf Q=\frac{2\pi}{a}\left(\frac12,\frac12,\frac12\right).
$$
Summing the <scattering amplitude> from the <magnetic moments> over sites gives peaks at $\mathbf q=\mathbf G+\mathbf Q$, where $\mathbf G$ is a nuclear <reciprocal lattice vector>. Thus all three components of the new <Miller indices>, expressed relative to the original <unit cell>, are half-integers. The two smallest squared magnitudes in units of $(2\pi/a)^2$ are
$$
\frac34\quad\text{for }\left\{\frac12,\frac12,\frac12\right\},\qquad
\frac{11}{4}\quad\text{for }\left\{\frac32,\frac12,\frac12\right\}.
$$
The first predicts $2\theta\simeq21.29^\circ$, agreeing with the new ring. The next squared magnitude is $19/4$, outside the measured angular range. Taking the observed first magnetic ring as the calibration, <Bragg's law> predicts
$$
\sin\theta_2=\sqrt{\frac{11}{3}}\sin(10.65^\circ),\qquad
\boxed{2\theta_2\simeq41.45^\circ\simeq41.4^\circ.}
$$
Using the independently fitted <lattice constant> gives $41.42^\circ$, within the input rounding. One ring alone does not uniquely determine a <magnetic order>; this proposed nearest-neighbour <antiferromagnetic> pattern explains both the observed ring and the count of two new rings in the specified interval.

The <Bravais lattice> is a <face-centered cubic lattice>. Four inequivalent representatives of its <lattice points> in the conventional cubic <unit cell>, in <fractional crystal coordinates>, are
$$
(0,0,0),\qquad\left(0,\frac12,\frac12\right),\qquad
\left(\frac12,0,\frac12\right),\qquad
\left(\frac12,\frac12,0\right).
$$
Boundary copies in the drawing do not constitute additional independent <lattice points>. Attach the two-atom <atomic basis>
$$
\boxed{\mathrm{Al}:(0,0,0),\qquad
\mathrm{P}:\left(\frac14,\frac14,\frac14\right)}
$$
to each <lattice point>. The resulting <aluminium phosphide> <crystal structure> is the <zinc blende structure>. For example, the four <phosphorus> positions reduced into the conventional <unit cell> are $(1/4,1/4,1/4)$, $(1/4,3/4,3/4)$, $(3/4,1/4,3/4)$, and $(3/4,3/4,1/4)$, reproducing the heights in the source drawing. There are four <aluminium> and four <phosphorus> <chemical atoms> per conventional <unit cell>, but only two <chemical atoms> per <primitive unit cell>.

{c}

Given independent <primitive lattice translation vectors> $\mathbf a_1,\mathbf a_2,\mathbf a_3$, define
$$
V_p=\mathbf a_1\cdot(\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3),\qquad
\mathbf b_1=2\pi\frac{\mathbf a_2\times\mathbf a_3}{V_p},\quad
\mathbf b_2=2\pi\frac{\mathbf a_3\times\mathbf a_1}{V_p},\quad
\mathbf b_3=2\pi\frac{\mathbf a_1\times\mathbf a_2}{V_p}.
$$
Then $\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf b_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}$. The <reciprocal lattice> consists of
$$
\boxed{\mathbf G=h\mathbf b_1+k\mathbf b_2+l\mathbf b_3,\qquad h,k,l\in\mathbb Z,}
$$
equivalently all <wavevectors> for which $e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf R}=1$ for every direct <lattice vector> $\mathbf R$.

For the <face-centered cubic lattice>, choose
$$
\mathbf a_1=\frac a2(0,1,1),\quad
\mathbf a_2=\frac a2(1,0,1),\quad
\mathbf a_3=\frac a2(1,1,0).
$$
Their <scalar triple product> is $a^3/4$, and direct evaluation of the <cross products> gives
$$
\mathbf b_1=\frac{2\pi}{a}(-1,1,1),\quad
\mathbf b_2=\frac{2\pi}{a}(1,-1,1),\quad
\mathbf b_3=\frac{2\pi}{a}(1,1,-1).
$$
Every integer combination has coordinates $(2\pi/a)(H,K,L)$ with $H,K,L$ all even or all odd. Conversely, for such coordinates the coefficients $(K+L)/2,(H+L)/2,(H+K)/2$ are integers, so every such point occurs. The even-coordinate points are the corners of cubes of edge $4\pi/a$, and the odd-coordinate points are their body centres. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\text{reciprocal Bravais lattice: body-centred cubic},\qquad a_{\rm reciprocal}=\frac{4\pi}{a}.}
$$
This is a property of the <Bravais lattice>; the two-atom <atomic basis> changes <structure factors> rather than these <reciprocal lattice> translation vectors.

There are two <chemical atoms> in the <primitive unit cell>, hence six displacement coordinates and six <phonon branches> at each <wavevector>. Three rigid <translations> cost no restoring <potential energy> in the long-<wavelength> limit. Consequently
$$
\boxed{3\text{ acoustic phonon branches}.}
$$
For propagation along $[100]$, one <acoustic phonon> has <longitudinal polarization> along $x$ and two have <transverse polarization> along $y,z$. The cubic <crystal symmetry> makes those two transverse directions equivalent. In terms of the <elastic stiffness tensor> and the mass <density>,
$$
v_L=\sqrt{\frac{C_{11}}{\rho_m}},\qquad
v_{T,y}=v_{T,z}=\sqrt{\frac{C_{44}}{\rho_m}}.
$$
Thus there are **two distinct acoustic velocities** in general, one longitudinal and a doubly degenerate transverse value. This <degeneracy of normal modes> concerns the two <transverse polarizations>; it does not imply equality with the longitudinal <speed of sound>.

Subtracting the three <acoustic phonon branches> from the six displacement <normal modes> leaves
$$
\boxed{3\text{ optical phonon branches}.}

At small <wavevector>, these involve relative motion of the two <atomic basis> sites rather than a rigid <translation> of the <primitive unit cell>. Their <angular frequencies> remain nonzero at the zone centre. The count uses the <primitive unit cell>; using all eight <chemical atoms> in the conventional <unit cell> would spuriously multiply the number of distinct branches in the first <Brillouin zone>.

Use the long-<wavelength> linear <dispersion relation> $\omega\simeq v_L|\mathbf k|$ as an estimate. The <phonon> <energy> is then
$$
E=\hbar\omega\simeq\hbar v_L\frac{2\pi}{a}
=\frac{h(8433\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}})}{0.5431\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm m},

There is therefore no reason for this <acoustic phonon> <energy> to vanish.

In the <diamond cubic structure> of <silicon>, the longitudinal <acoustic phonon branch> and longitudinal <optical phonon branch> meet at $X=(2\pi/a,0,0)$. The two <atomic basis> sites are identical, and the associated <crystal symmetry> protects their boundary <degeneracy of normal modes>. In <aluminium phosphide>, the two sites contain different <chemical atoms>. The corresponding <zinc blende structure> lacks the site-exchange symmetry, and these two longitudinal branches generally split at $X$.

A simple way to see the splitting is a <diatomic-chain dispersion relation with equal springs>, representing successive $[100]$ atomic planes by alternating <masses> $M_1,M_2$ at spacing $d=a/4$. With effective <spring constant> $K$, its <angular frequencies> obey
$$
\omega_\pm^2=K\left(\frac1{M_1}+\frac1{M_2}\right)
\pm K\sqrt{\left(\frac1{M_1}+\frac1{M_2}\right)^2-\frac4{M_1M_2}\sin^2(kd)}.
$$
At $X$, $kd=\pi/2$, so the inter-sublattice coupling cancels and the two squared <angular frequencies> are $2K/M_1$ and $2K/M_2$. Equal <masses> give a contact; unequal <masses> give
$$
\boxed{\omega_{\rm acoustic}(X)\simeq\sqrt{\frac{2K}{M_{\rm heavy}}},\qquad
\omega_{\rm optical}(X)\simeq\sqrt{\frac{2K}{M_{\rm light}}}.}
$$
In <aluminium phosphide>, the heavier <phosphorus> plane moves in the lower-frequency edge mode, and the lighter <aluminium> plane in the higher-frequency mode in this model. Their similar <atomic masses> explain why the splitting is relatively small. Different electronic environments can also change the <force constant matrix>. The chain calculation illustrates the mechanism; a quantitative three-dimensional <phonon spectrum> requires its full <dynamical matrix>. It does not assert that all transverse acoustic and optical branches meet in <silicon>.

In the <harmonic approximation>, each in-plane displacement coordinate contributes one quadratic <kinetic energy> term and one quadratic restoring <potential energy> term. The <equipartition theorem> assigns $k_BT/2$ to each. There are two such coordinates per <chemical atom>, hence
$$
U\simeq2N_ak_BT,\qquad
\boxed{\frac{C_v}{N_a}\longrightarrow2k_B.}
$$
Here the fixed-size <heat capacity> is at constant area in this two-dimensional model. On cooling, <phonon> excitation freezes out and the <heat capacity> decreases. For each nonzero <angular frequency>, the <quantum harmonic oscillator> contributes
$$
c_\omega=k_B\frac{x^2e^x}{(e^x-1)^2},\qquad x=\frac{\hbar\omega}{k_BT},
$$
which is below $k_B$ at finite $x$ and approaches zero as $T\to0$. Two linearly dispersing in-plane <acoustic phonon branches> give the familiar two-dimensional <Debye model> scaling $C_v\propto T^2$ at sufficiently low <temperature>. Out-of-plane modes are excluded by the specified model.

For a nondegenerate two-dimensional <electron gas>, the two quadratic momentum terms supply $k_BT/2$ each by the <equipartition theorem>. There is no lattice restoring <potential energy> for a free <electron>. Thus
$$
U=N_ek_BT,\qquad
\boxed{\frac{C_v}{N_e}\longrightarrow k_B.}
$$
On cooling below the <Fermi temperature>, the <Pauli exclusion principle> restricts the thermally active <electrons> to within approximately $k_BT$ of the <Fermi energy>. The <linear low-temperature heat capacity of a two-dimensional Fermi gas> is
$$
\boxed{\frac{C_v}{N_e}\simeq\frac{\pi^2}{3}k_B\frac{k_BT}{E_F}\longrightarrow0.}
$$
The <heat capacity> therefore decreases from its classical value as <quantum degeneracy> becomes important. The <particle number> of <electrons> and sample area are held fixed when differentiating the <internal energy>.

Let the sample area be $A$, set the band touching <energy> to zero, and write $m^*=m_e^*=m_h^*$. Assume one <electronic valley> in each <energy band> with two <spin> states. The near-edge <parabolic dispersions> are
$$
E_c(k)=\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m^*},\qquad E_v(k)=-\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m^*}.
$$
Under <periodic boundary conditions>, each <spin> occupies reciprocal-space area $(2\pi)^2/A$. Counting the <quantum states> inside a circle gives
$$
\mathcal N_c(E)=2\frac{A}{(2\pi)^2}\pi k^2
=\frac{Am^*}{\pi\hbar^2}E\qquad(E>0).
$$
Its <derivative> is the constant <two-dimensional free-electron density of states>. Defining $D=Am^*/(\pi\hbar^2)$, the signed-<energy> <density of states> is
$$
\boxed{g_c(E)=D\Theta(E),\qquad g_v(E)=D\Theta(-E).}
$$
The total electronic <density of states> is therefore $D$ on either side of zero, not $2D$ at every signed <energy>. If instead one counts positive-<energy> excitations, the <electron> and <hole> species each have <density of states> $D$, giving $2D$ when summed. A common additional <valley degeneracy> multiplies all these quantities by that factor. The <effective mass> approximation assumes thermally sampled <energies> remain within the near-edge regions of both <energy bands>.

Equal <effective masses> and equal <spin> and <valley degeneracies> give <electron-hole symmetry>. In the <intrinsic semiconductor>, <charge neutrality> requires equal numbers of <conduction band> <electrons> and <valence band> <holes>. It therefore pins the <chemical potential> to the band touching point, $\mu=0$. The <Fermi-Dirac distribution> gives
$$
N_c=D\int_0^\infty\frac{dE}{e^{E/(k_BT)}+1}
=Dk_BT\ln2,
\qquad
\boxed{N_c(T)=\frac{Am^*k_BT}{\pi\hbar^2}\ln2.}
$$
Measured relative to the filled <valence band> at zero <temperature>, the thermal <internal energy> includes both species. Removing a <valence band> <electron> at $-E$ costs the positive <hole> <energy> $E$, just as adding a <conduction band> <electron> at $E$ does. Hence
$$
\Delta U=2D\int_0^\infty\frac{E\,dE}{e^{E/(k_BT)}+1}
=\frac{\pi^2}{6}D(k_BT)^2.
$$
Differentiate at fixed area and fixed total electronic <particle number>, allowing creation of <electron-hole pairs>:
$$
C_v^{\rm tot}=\frac{\pi^2}{3}Dk_B^2T,\qquad
\boxed{\frac{C_v^{\rm tot}}{N_c}=\frac{\pi^2}{3\ln2}k_B\simeq4.746\,k_B.}
$$
There is no conflict with the <equipartition theorem>. The normalization $N_c$ counts only the excited <conduction band> <electrons>, while the <heat capacity> includes both <electrons> and <holes>; moreover, $N_c\propto T$ is not fixed. In fact $\Delta U/N_c=\pi^2k_BT/(6\ln2)$, and differentiating $N_c(T)$ supplies another equal contribution to the <heat capacity>. Finally, $\mu=0$ gives occupation $1/2$ at the touching point, so the excitation gas is not in the classical dilute limit even though $k_BT$ exceeds the vanishing <band gap>.

For the degenerate <electron gas>, let $D_g=Am/(\pi\hbar^2)$ include both <spin> states. The <Sommerfeld expansion> gives

U_g(T)-U_g(0)=\frac{\pi^2}{6}D_g(k_BT)^2,\qquad

\boxed{\frac{C_v^{\rm tot}}{C_v}=\frac{m^*}{m};\qquad

The equality often intended in this comparison requires the same <density of states>. The source sets $m_e^*=m_h^*$ but does not explicitly equate them to the <electron gas> <mass> $m$. The filled <Fermi sea> does not add a factor $N_e/N_c$: only the thermal shell near its <Fermi energy> contributes to its <heat capacity>.

Undoped <gallium arsenide> is expected to be **diamagnetic** at zero <temperature>. Its <valence band> is filled and its <conduction band> empty, so there are no mobile <conduction band> <electrons> to give <Pauli paramagnetism>. The specified absence of localized <magnetic moments> removes a <Curie law> response and the usual mechanism for <ferromagnetic ordering>. The remaining induced orbital response is <diamagnetism>. This classification uses the ordinary weak-field <band insulator> picture; the <band gap> suppresses thermally generated carriers as $T\to0$.

The number of formula units per volume follows from the mass <density>:
$$
n_f=\frac{5.9\times10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}}{(69.7+74.9)(1.66053906892\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg})}
=2.4572\times10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}.

Each neutral formula unit contains $31+33=64$ <electrons>, so $\rho_e=64n_f=1.5726\times10^{30}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$. Let $R^2$ be the electron-weighted <mean-square orbital radius>, averaged over all these <electrons>. The supplied <Larmor-Langevin susceptibility> then gives the dimensionless SI <volume magnetic susceptibility>
$$
\chi_0=-\frac{\mu_0\rho_e e^2}{6m_e}R^2,
\qquad
\boxed{\chi_0=-2.5990\times10^{-5}\left(\frac{R}{a_0}\right)^2,}
$$
where $a_0=0.0529177\,\mathrm{nm}$ is the <Bohr radius>. An explicit rough atomic-scale assumption $R\simeq a_0$ gives $\chi_0\simeq-2.6\times10^{-5}$.

The quoted bulk data do not determine $R$: a nuclear charge does not specify an orbital radius in a screened many-<electron> <chemical atom>. Consequently the numerical estimate just made has atomic-model uncertainty; it is not a deduction with guaranteed 30 percent accuracy. For example, the alternative assumed $R=2a_0$ gives four times the magnitude while leaving every supplied bulk number unchanged. Atomic <wavefunctions>, an explicit screened-orbital model, or measured atomic <magnetic susceptibilities> of materials exhibiting <diamagnetism> would be needed to support that accuracy. Keeping $R$ explicit makes the later comparison independent of an unadvertised radius assumption. The bare <electron mass> belongs in this atomic formula; the <conduction band> <effective mass> is relevant instead to the large donor envelope in part (c).

In the <hydrogenic donor state> approximation, the screened <Coulomb potential energy> is $-e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r r)$ and the kinetic term uses $m^*=0.067m_e$. Rescaling the <hydrogen atom> gives the <effective Bohr radius of a hydrogenic donor> and the positive binding <energy>
$$
a_b=\frac{4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r\hbar^2}{m^*e^2}
=\frac{12.9}{0.067}a_0,
\qquad
E_b=\frac{\hbar^2}{2m^*a_b^2}
=13.6057\,\mathrm{eV}\frac{0.067}{12.9^2}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{a_{\rm binding}=10.19\,\mathrm{nm},\qquad E_{\rm binding}=5.48\,\mathrm{meV}.}
$$
The radius here is the exponential length of the ground-state <wavefunction>, not its root-mean-square radius. With $\psi(r)=(\pi a_b^3)^{-1/2}e^{-r/a_b}$, radial integration gives $\langle r^2\rangle=3a_b^2$ and $\langle x^2+y^2\rangle=2a_b^2$.

For dilute independent donors of number <density> $n$, a bound <spin> one-half <electron> has <magnetic moment> magnitude $\mu_s=|g|\mu_B/2$, with the <Bohr magneton> defined using the bare <electron mass>. Its weak-field <Curie law> response is
$$
\chi_{\rm spin}=\frac{\mu_0n g^2\mu_B^2}{4k_BT}.
$$
For the orbital response, expand the <effective mass> <Hamiltonian> $(\mathbf p+e\mathbf A)^2/(2m^*)$ in a uniform <magnetic field>, using $\mathbf A=\mathbf B\times\mathbf r/2$. The spherical ground state has zero orbital <angular momentum>; the quadratic term raises its <energy> by
$$
\Delta E_{\rm orb}=\frac{e^2B^2}{8m^*}\langle x^2+y^2\rangle
=\frac{e^2a_b^2}{4m^*}B^2.
$$
Differentiating this <energy> twice and using $B\simeq\mu_0H$ gives
$$
\chi_{\rm orb}=-\frac{\mu_0ne^2a_b^2}{2m^*}.
$$
The donor-only <magnetic susceptibility> cancels when
$$
\boxed{T_* =\frac{g^2\mu_B^2m^*}{2k_Be^2a_b^2}
=\frac{g^2}{4}\left(\frac{m^*}{m_e}\right)^2\frac{E_b}{k_B}
\simeq1.44\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm K.}
$$
The negative sign of $g=-0.45$ drops out of the linear <magnetic susceptibility>. This crossover is far below $E_b/k_B\simeq63.6\,\mathrm K$, consistent with donor binding. It assumes sufficiently weak <magnetic field>, negligible exchange between donors and a thermal scale above any inter-donor splitting; sufficiently dilute donors can satisfy those assumptions. The background <diamagnetism> from part (b) is excluded from this cancellation.

A <hydrogenic donor state> has an exponentially decaying envelope $\psi(r)\propto e^{-r/a_b}$. Neighbouring donors at typical separation $r_d\sim n^{-1/3}$ therefore have exponentially small orbital overlap. The corresponding <hopping integral> is of order $E_b e^{-r_d/a_b}$, apart from algebraic factors and factors from the number of neighbours. Hybridizing many such localized states produces an <impurity band> with a <bandwidth> of the same scale:
$$
\boxed{W\sim E_b\exp\left(-\alpha\frac{n^{-1/3}}{a_b}\right).}
$$
The order-one constant $\alpha$ absorbs details of the donor spacing and envelope. The exponential dependence expresses <quantum tunnelling> between weakly overlapping donor orbitals, rather than thermal excitation over the donor binding <energy>.

Use the stipulated noninteracting <impurity band> approximation, with one donated <electron> per donor and two available <spin> states per donor orbital. The <energy band> is half filled. Approximating its spin-summed <density of states> per volume near the <Fermi energy> by $D_v(E_F)\simeq2n/W$ gives the zero-<temperature> <Pauli paramagnetism>
$$
\chi_{\rm imp}=\mu_0\left(\frac{|g|\mu_B}{2}\right)^2D_v(E_F)
\simeq\frac{\mu_0g^2\mu_B^2n}{2W}.
$$
With $\alpha=1$,
$$
\boxed{\chi_{\rm imp}(n)\sim\frac{\mu_0g^2\mu_B^2}{2E_b}\,n\exp\left(\frac1{a_bn^{1/3}}\right)>0.}
$$
A factor of order one depends on the <density of states> shape; using the <conduction band> $g$-factor rather than silently setting $g=2$ matters here.

To locate the comparison with the magnitude of the undoped <Larmor-Langevin susceptibility>, put $x=n^{-1/3}/a_b>1$. Numerically,
$$
\chi_{\rm imp}\simeq P\frac{e^x}{x^3},\qquad
P=\frac{\mu_0g^2\mu_B^2}{2E_ba_b^3}=1.179\times10^{-8}.
$$
For the stated atomic-scale estimate $R=a_0$ in part (b), equality with $|\chi_0|=2.599\times10^{-5}$ requires
$$
x-3\ln x=\ln\frac{|\chi_0|}{P},\qquad x\simeq16.02.
$$
Thus the dilute-branch crossover is
$$
\boxed{n_*\simeq2.3\times10^{20}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}
=2.3\times10^{14}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}},}
$$
well below $a_b^{-3}=9.45\times10^{23}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$. Within this model the positive <spin> response exceeds the negative background magnitude for **lower** <dopant densities> on this dilute branch: the exponential narrowing of $W$ wins over the loss of donors. Indeed $d\ln(e^x/x^3)/dx=1-3/x>0$ for $x>3$. This is not a claim of <ferromagnetism>.

The degenerate <Pauli paramagnetism> calculation requires $k_BT\ll W$, with an unimportant order-one filling factor. At the estimated crossing,
$$
W=E_be^{-16.02}\simeq6.04\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm{eV},\qquad
\boxed{T\ll W/k_B\simeq7.0\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm K.}
$$
The weak-field condition also requires $|g|\mu_BB\ll W$. For a different assumed atomic $R$, use $|\chi_0|=2.599\times10^{-5}(R/a_0)^2$ in the displayed equation for $x$. An order-of-magnitude background $|\chi_0|\sim10^{-5}$ gives the same density order and a crossover scale of order $10^{-5}\,\mathrm K$. At higher <temperature> than this extremely narrow <bandwidth> permits, the $n/W$ enhancement cannot be used; the independent-donor <Curie law> is the appropriate thermal scale if interactions remain negligible. At very small <dopant density>, disorder, localization and <Coulomb interactions> can also invalidate the assumed noninteracting <impurity band>. The formal divergence as $n\to0$ is therefore a limit of the stated zero-<temperature> band model, not a prediction for isolated donors at a fixed nonzero <temperature>.
