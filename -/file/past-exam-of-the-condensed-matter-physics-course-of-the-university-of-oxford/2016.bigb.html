Take $(x_j,y_j,z_j)$ as fractional coordinates in the chosen <unit cell>. In the kinematic approximation the <crystal basis structure factor> is
$$
\boxed{F(hkl)=\sum_j f_j(Q)\exp[2\pi i(hx_j+ky_j+lz_j)]},\qquad Q=\frac{4\pi\sin\theta}{\lambda}.
$$
Equivalently, in Cartesian coordinates the phase is $\mathbf G_{hkl}\cdot\mathbf r_j$. Reversing every phase sign complex-conjugates the nonabsorbing amplitude and leaves its <intensity> unchanged. The <atomic scattering factor> is the <Fourier transform> of the <Chemical atom>[atom]'s <Electron number density>[electron density], in <electron> scattering units:
$$
f_j(\mathbf Q)=\int\rho_{e,j}(\mathbf r)e^{i\mathbf Q\cdot\mathbf r}\,d^3r.
$$
At zero <Bragg angle>[scattering angle] the normal form factor counts <Electron>[electrons]: $f_j(0)=Z_j$ for a neutral <Chemical atom>[atom], modified by the ionic charge for an <ion>. At increasing $Q$, different parts of the <electron> cloud scatter with different phases, reducing the coherent amplitude. Typical nonresonant <Atomic scattering factor>[atomic curves] fall from their forward value toward small values at large angle. A localized cloud falls more slowly in reciprocal space. Anomalous scattering near an absorption edge adds energy-dependent complex terms and is excluded from this simple sketch.

For a fixed reflection and fixed instrumental and geometric corrections,
$$
\boxed{I_{hkl}\propto|F(hkl)|^2.}
$$
The full amplitude also contains the lattice sum. At its <Reciprocal lattice>[reciprocal-lattice] peak, $N_c$ coherently illuminated identical cells give a peak amplitude $N_cF$; peak height and integrated peak <intensity> have different size dependences because the peak narrows with <crystal> size. Powder multiplicity, polarization, absorption, <Thermal fluctuation>[thermal motion] and measurement geometry also affect observed <Intensity>[intensities]. The basis factor determines relative <interference>, not every experimental prefactor.

The two sites give phases one and $\exp[i\pi(h+k+l)]=(-1)^{h+k+l}$. Thus
$$
F_{\rm CsCl}=f_{\rm Cs^+}+(-1)^{h+k+l}f_{\rm Cl^-},\qquad
\boxed{I_{hkl}=K_{hkl}\left|f_{\rm Cs^+}+(-1)^{h+k+l}f_{\rm Cl^-}\right|^2.}
$$
For real nonresonant <Atomic scattering factor>[form factors], even $h+k+l$ gives the square of the sum and odd $h+k+l$ the square of the difference. The factor $K_{hkl}$ represents the remaining experimental corrections, not another basis-interference term. Since the two <Ion>[ions] differ, a half-body-diagonal translation does not preserve the decorated <crystal>. Its <Bravais lattice> is therefore <Simple cubic lattice>[simple cubic], with a two-ion basis; <Caesium chloride structure>[CsCl] is not a monatomic <Body-centered cubic lattice>[body-centered cubic] <Bravais lattice>.

Replacing **both** sites by identical Fe <Chemical atom>[atoms] changes the amplitude to
$$
F_{\rm Fe}=f_{\rm Fe}[1+(-1)^{h+k+l}],\qquad
\boxed{I_{hkl}^{\rm Fe}=\begin{cases}4K_{hkl}|f_{\rm Fe}|^2,&h+k+l\text{ even},\\0,&h+k+l\text{ odd}.\end{cases}}
$$
Now the body-centering translation does preserve the structure. It is a <body-centered cubic lattice> with one Fe <Chemical atom>[atom] per <Primitive unit cell>[primitive cell], or two per conventional cubic cell.

For 111 the parity is odd: <Caesium chloride structure>[CsCl] generally has a nonzero amplitude $f_{\rm Cs^+}-f_{\rm Cl^-}$, whereas the identical-Fe 111 reflection is systematically absent. A numerical absolute <Caesium chloride structure>[CsCl] <intensity> requires the angle-dependent <Atomic scattering factor>[form factors] and experimental normalization; no such values are supplied. Forward <electron> counts, 54 for Cs+ and 18 for Cl-, explain why their amplitudes are substantially unequal, but are not substituted for the actual finite-angle factors without an additional approximation.

The isothermal volume <Isothermal compressibility>[compressibility] is $\kappa=-(1/V)(\partial V/\partial p)_T$. For isotropic cubic compression $V\propto a^3$ and $d_{hkl}=a/\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}$, so
$$
\frac1{d_{hkl}}\left(\frac{\partial d_{hkl}}{\partial p}\right)_T
=\frac1a\left(\frac{\partial a}{\partial p}\right)_T=-\frac\kappa3.
$$
<Derivative>[Differentiate] <Bragg's law> $2d_{hkl}\sin\theta=\lambda$ at fixed incident <wavelength> and fixed reflection:
$$
0=\sin\theta\frac{\partial d}{\partial p}+d\cos\theta\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial p},
\qquad\boxed{\left(\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial p}\right)_T=\frac\kappa3\tan\theta,\quad\alpha=\frac13.}
$$
This <derivative> uses radians. It remains locally valid with a pressure-dependent <Isothermal compressibility>[compressibility], provided the cubic isotropic phase is retained. For a constant stipulated $\kappa$, integration gives
$$
\boxed{a(p)=a_0e^{-\kappa p/3},\qquad
\sin\theta(p)=\sin\theta_0e^{\kappa p/3}.}
$$
Thus $a_0(1-\kappa p/3)$ is only the first-order approximation, not the exact consequence of a constant logarithmic <Isothermal compressibility>[compressibility]. The finite-pressure calculation also assumes that the reflection still exists, the <crystal> remains in the same structure and $\sin\theta_0e^{\kappa p/3}\leq1$. These limitations matter for a large extrapolation.

In the <free electron model>, <Conduction electron>[conduction electrons] occupy independent <Plane wave>[plane-wave] states with <energy> $\varepsilon_{\mathbf k}=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)$ in a uniform mean background. <Periodic potential>[Crystal-periodic] band structure and explicit <electron>–<electron> correlations are omitted. <Electron>[Electrons] still obey the <Pauli exclusion principle> and <Fermi-Dirac statistics>[Fermi–Dirac statistics]; free does not mean distinguishable classical particles. A scattering time can be added phenomenologically to describe transport.

The <Fermi energy> is the highest occupied single-particle <energy> at zero <temperature>, measured here from the bottom of the <Parabolic dispersion>[parabolic band]. The <Fermi temperature> is the corresponding scale $T_F=E_F/k_B$, not the actual <Temperature>[thermal temperature] of the sample. Metallic <electron> densities are large, so filling all distinct states up to the required <momentum> gives <Fermi energy>[Fermi energies] of several electronvolts, equivalent to tens of thousands of kelvin or more. At ordinary <Temperature>[temperatures], only a narrow region around the <Fermi surface> has appreciably changing occupations.

A room-temperature metal and its lattice are nevertheless at room <temperature>, not at $T_F$. High occupied <Kinetic energy>[kinetic energies] are present even in the zero-temperature ground state and cannot all be liberated as <heat> without violating the allowed occupancy structure. A metal below skin <temperature> often feels cold because it transfers <heat> rapidly out of the skin; its <thermal conductivity> and <thermal effusivity> matter, not the value of its <Fermi temperature>. At the same actual <temperature>, a poorly conducting material can therefore feel warmer while neither material is thermodynamically hotter.

Use periodic boundaries in a box of side $L$ and include two <Spin degeneracy>[spin states] for each <wavevector>. One orbital state occupies $(2\pi/L)^d$ in <wavevector> space. Let $v_d$ be the volume of the unit ball in dimension $d$, namely $2,\pi,4\pi/3$. Filling the ball of radius $k_F$ gives
$$
N=2\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)^d v_dk_F^d,
\qquad k_F^d=\frac{(2\pi)^d}{2v_d}n.
$$
Since $E_F=\hbar^2k_F^2/(2m)$,
$$
\boxed{a_d=\frac{(2\pi)^d}{2v_d},\qquad
 a_1=\frac\pi2,\quad a_2=2\pi,\quad a_3=3\pi^2.}
$$
The density $n$ means number per length, area or volume as appropriate; it is not assigned three-dimensional units in every case.

For the same spin-summed states, the cumulative number per $d$-dimensional volume below <energy> $E$ is $n(E)=2v_d(2mE/\hbar^2)^{d/2}/(2\pi)^d$. <Derivative>[Differentiating] yields $g(E)=(d/2)n(E)/E$. Evaluating at the filling <energy>,
$$
\boxed{g(E_F)=b_d\frac n{E_F},\qquad
b_1=\frac12,\quad b_2=1,\quad b_3=\frac32.}
$$
In one, two and three dimensions the <density of states> varies respectively as $E^{-1/2}$, a constant and $E^{1/2}$. The normalization counts both signs of <momentum> and both <Spin degeneracy>[spin states] exactly once. A single-spin <density of states> or extra valley degeneracy would require a different counting convention, not an extra unexplained factor in these constants.

Write the two allowed <Spin-state population and net polarization>[moment projections] as $\pm\mu$, where $\mu=g\mu_B/2$ for <spin one-half>. Their <Zeeman effect>[Zeeman energies] in the prescribed <Magnetic flux density>[flux density] are $\mp\mu B$. The single-particle <partition function> and the mean moment along the field are
$$
z=e^{\beta\mu B}+e^{-\beta\mu B}=2\cosh x,\qquad
\langle\mu_z\rangle=\mu\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}{e^x+e^{-x}}=\mu\tanh x,
\quad x=\frac{\mu B}{k_BT}.
$$
For noninteracting moments, <magnetization> is moment per volume:
$$
\boxed{M=M_s\tanh(\eta B/T),\qquad M_s=\frac{N\mu}{V},\quad
\eta=\frac\mu{k_B}.}
$$
For electron-like <Spin-only magnetic moment>[spin-only] moments $g\simeq2$, these become $M_s=N\mu_B/V$ and $\eta=\mu_B/k_B$.

<Derivative>[Differentiate] with respect to the specified $B$ to obtain
$$
\boxed{\chi_B=\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial B}\right)_T
=\frac{N\mu^2}{Vk_BT}\operatorname{sech}^2x.}
$$
This $B$ <derivative> is not a dimensionless SI <Volume magnetic susceptibility>[volume susceptibility]. In the weak-feedback approximation $B\simeq\mu_0H$, the usual dimensionless <Differential magnetic susceptibility>[differential susceptibility] is $\chi_{\rm diff}\simeq\mu_0\chi_B$. The <Linear response>[small-field limit] gives Curie's law $\chi_0=\mu_0N\mu^2/(Vk_BT)$. At finite bias a secant response $M/H\simeq\mu_0M/B=\chi_0\tanh(x)/x$ differs from the differential slope $\chi_0\operatorname{sech}^2x$. For an internal $B=\mu_0(H+M)$ retained self-consistently, the exact local conversion is $\chi_H=\mu_0\chi_B/(1-\mu_0\chi_B)$; <Demagnetizing field>[demagnetizing] geometry can further affect an applied-field measurement. Stating the field convention avoids a hidden factor of $\mu_0$ or confusion between a slope and a ratio.

A $3d^9$ shell has one hole in the otherwise filled $d$ shell. <Hund's rules> give $S=1/2$, $L=2$, and, for a more-than-half-filled shell, $J=L+S=5/2$: the free-ion term is ${}^2D_{5/2}$. Its <Landé g-factor>[Landé factor] is
$$
g_J=1+\frac{J(J+1)+S(S+1)-L(L+1)}{2J(J+1)}=\frac65.
$$
Distinguish the <Effective paramagnetic moment>[Curie effective moment] from a saturated projection:
$$
\boxed{\mu_{\rm eff}^{\rm free}=g_J\mu_B\sqrt{J(J+1)}=3.55\mu_B,
\qquad\mu_{\rm sat}^{\rm free}=g_JJ\mu_B=3\mu_B.}
$$
The question then explicitly asks us to use <orbital quenching> in the <crystal>. <Crystal field theory>[Ligand fields] split and mix orbital states, suppressing their freely orientable <angular momentum>. The <Spin-only magnetic moment>[spin-only] approximation has $g\simeq2$, $S=1/2$, so $\mu_{\rm eff}=\sqrt3\mu_B$ but the two <Spin one-half along an axis>[field-axis] projections are $\pm\mu_B$. The latter, not $\sqrt3\mu_B$, belong in the tanh argument and <Spin-one-half paramagnetic magnetization>[saturation magnetization].

The sulfate and water environment supplies mainly closed-shell, weakly magnetic surroundings, stabilizes localized Cu2+ sites and separates their moments so that <Exchange interaction>[exchange] can be weak on the <temperature> scale considered. <Paramagnetism> here is an approximation of weakly interacting local spins, not a claim that dilution guarantees no magnetic ordering at any <temperature>. Elemental <copper> has a filled $3d$ band and itinerant <Conduction electron>[conduction electrons] rather than one localized Cu2+ spin per <Chemical atom>[atom]. <Pauli paramagnetism>[Pauli spin response] and orbital/core contributions then replace a local-spin <Curie's law>[Curie law]; ordinary pure <copper> has a weak net <Diamagnetism>[diamagnetic] response.https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1090780715000774[2015 primary measurement, abstract: negative volume susceptibility of high-purity copper wire]{ref}

\qquad\boxed{\chi_0(300\ \mathrm K)=1.4390\times10^{-4},\quad
\chi_0(2\ \mathrm K)=2.1585\times10^{-2}.}
$$
These neglect <Exchange interaction>[exchange], <Anisotropy>[anisotropic] $g$ factors and background <diamagnetism>. At high field and low <temperature>, saturation requires the nonlinear response in <3/a>; a field-independent <Curie's law>[Curie value] then describes only the zero-field slope.

<Inelastic neutron scattering> measures both <momentum> and <energy> exchanged with the <crystal>. Comparing incident and outgoing neutron <Wavevector>[wavevectors] gives $\mathbf Q=\mathbf k_i-\mathbf k_f$, while their <energy> difference gives a created or annihilated <Phonon>[phonon energy] $\hbar\omega$. <Crystal momentum> conservation identifies the reduced <Wavevector>[phonon wavevector] modulo a <Reciprocal lattice vector>[reciprocal-lattice vector]. Varying scattering geometry maps <Dispersion relation>[dispersion curves] and <Phonon polarization>[polarization-dependent] <Intensity>[intensities] across a substantial part of the <Brillouin zone>.

<Inelastic X-ray scattering> provides a complementary energy- and momentum-resolved probe, particularly when small samples or high-pressure environments favor photon beams. <Raman spectroscopy>[Raman] and <infrared spectroscopy> measure optical-frequency resonances subject to different symmetry selection rules. Photon <momentum> at these frequencies is usually small on the Brillouin-zone scale, so these methods mostly sample near the <Brillouin zone>[zone center] rather than an arbitrary <Wavevector>[phonon wavevector]. <Brillouin scattering> and <Ultrasound>[ultrasonic] measurements probe low-frequency acoustic motion and infer <Speed of sound>[sound velocities] or <Elastic modulus>[elastic constants].

<Heat capacity>[Heat-capacity] measurements constrain the thermally weighted <Phonon density of states>[vibrational density of states] and its low-frequency behavior, but do not by themselves reconstruct a unique wavevector-resolved dispersion. Elastic <X-ray diffraction>[diffraction] and temperature-dependent <displacement> factors also constrain mean-square motion, while energy-resolved inelastic measurements distinguish individual excitations directly. A monatomic scalar chain has only the <Acoustic phonon branch>[acoustic branch] derived below; it should not be assigned an <Optical phonon branch>[optical branch] merely because <Raman spectroscopy>[Raman] spectroscopy exists for more complicated <Crystal>[crystals].

The right <spring> changes length by $u_{n+1}-u_n$ and pulls <Chemical atom>[atom] $n$ to the right with <force> $C(u_{n+1}-u_n)$. The left <spring> contributes $-C(u_n-u_{n-1})$. Adding these <Restoring force>[restoring forces] and applying <Newton's second law> gives
$$
m\ddot u_n=C(u_{n+1}-2u_n+u_{n-1}).
$$
Uniform <displacement> makes both <spring> extensions vanish, as <Translational symmetry>[translational invariance] requires. Search for a <normal mode> $u_n=u_0e^{i(kna-\omega t)}$. Substitution yields
$$
-m\omega^2=C(e^{ika}+e^{-ika}-2)=-4C\sin^2(ka/2),
\qquad\boxed{\omega(k)=2\sqrt{C/m}\,|\sin(ka/2)|.}
$$
One representative <first Brillouin zone> is $-\pi/a\leq k\leq\pi/a$. This is one positive-frequency <Acoustic phonon branch>[acoustic branch]: near zero <wavevector>, $\omega\simeq a\sqrt{C/m}|k|$, so the <Speed of sound>[sound speed] is $v_s=a\sqrt{C/m}$. Opposite signs of $k$ label opposite propagation directions, not a second polarization branch.

The model assumes small <Displacement>[displacements], <Harmonic oscillator>[harmonic] nearest-neighbor <Force>[forces], one scalar <displacement> per <Chemical atom>[atom] and uniform equilibrium spacing. It excludes <damping>, <Anharmonicity>[anharmonic] expansion and transverse polarizations. In a three-dimensional monatomic <crystal> there would normally be three acoustic polarizations; that counting cannot be imported into this explicitly one-dimensional scalar model.

Consequently
$$
\boxed{\omega(k)=4.0\times10^{13}|\sin(ka/2)|\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}},\quad
k_{\rm edge}=\frac\pi a=1.571\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}.}
$$
The curve is even in $k$, rises linearly from the origin with one-sided slopes $\pm4.0$ km/s, and flattens at both <Brillouin zone>[zone edges]. Its maximum is $\omega_m=4.0\times10^{13}$ rad/s, or ordinary frequency $\nu_m=\omega_m/(2\pi)=6.366$ THz. Keeping <angular frequency> distinct from cycles per second avoids a factor-of-$2\pi$ error. Repeating the curve outside the first zone is a representation of equivalent <crystal> momenta, not the discovery of additional modes.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

<Derivative>[Differentiate] the positive-frequency branch on either side of the origin:
$$
\boxed{v_g(k)=\frac{d\omega}{dk}=v_s\,\operatorname{sgn}(k)\cos(ka/2),\qquad
0<|k|<\pi/a.}
$$
It tends to $+4.0$ km/s as $k\to0^+$ and to $-4.0$ km/s as $k\to0^-$, and vanishes at either <Brillouin zone>[zone boundary]. A positive-$k$ <Wave packet>[wavepacket] therefore transports <energy> to the right, while a negative-$k$ one transports it to the left. At the zone edge, neighboring <Chemical atom>[atoms] oscillate out of phase and the dispersion is flat, giving zero <group velocity> even though the frequency is nonzero.

The graph has separate one-sided limits at zero because $\omega(k)$ has a cusp there. Assigning one differentiable slope at exactly $k=0$ would erase the distinction between the two propagation directions; a precisely uniform translation has no preferred propagation direction. Plotting only $0\leq k\leq\pi/a$ is also legitimate if the missing negative branch is stated. <Group velocity> and <phase velocity> $\omega/k$ are different except in the long-wavelength linear limit.

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For a long periodic chain of length $\mathcal L=Na$, neighboring allowed signed <Wavevector>[wavevectors] differ by $2\pi/\mathcal L$. Counting both signs once gives a <Density of states>[state density] per length $dk/\pi$ on the positive half-zone. Therefore the <Phonon density of states>[density per angular-frequency interval] and per length is
$$
g(\omega)=\frac1\pi\left|\frac{dk}{d\omega}\right|
=\boxed{\frac1{\pi v_s\sqrt{1-(\omega/\omega_m)^2}}},\qquad0<\omega<\omega_m.
$$
It is zero outside this band, starts at $g(0)=1/(\pi v_s)=7.958\times10^{-5}$ s/m and has an integrable inverse-square-root <Van Hove singularity> as $\omega\to\omega_m^-$. The state-count normalization is
$$
\int_0^{\omega_m}g(\omega)\,d\omega=\frac{\omega_m}{2v_s}=\frac1a.
$$
Thus the scalar chain has one vibrational mode per <Chemical atom>[atom], with no extra factor of two for the already counted propagation directions. If the horizontal variable were ordinary frequency $\nu$, the density would be $g_\nu(\nu)=2\pi g_\omega(2\pi\nu)$. A finite chain has discrete lines instead of an actual infinite peak; the smooth divergent curve is the large-chain continuum description.

{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

At fixed chain length, each <Normal mode>[harmonic mode] contributes a <Bose-Einstein distribution>[Bose–Einstein thermal occupation] and a temperature-independent <zero-point energy>. The latter contributes no <heat capacity>. With $x=\hbar\omega/(k_BT)$,
$$
\frac{C_V}{\mathcal L}=k_B\int_0^{\omega_m}g(\omega)
\frac{x^2e^x}{(e^x-1)^2}\,d\omega.
$$
At low <temperature> only the approximately linear part of the <Monatomic-chain dispersion relation>[acoustic dispersion] is excited, so $g(\omega)\simeq1/(\pi v_s)$. Using $\int_0^\infty x^2e^x/(e^x-1)^2dx=\pi^2/3$ gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{C_V}{\mathcal L}\simeq\frac{\pi k_B^2}{3\hbar v_s}T
=(4.732\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-2}\,m^{-1}}) T.}
$$
It is **linear in <temperature>**, rather than the three-dimensional <Debye model>[Debye] $T^3$ law. At high <temperature> every scalar oscillator tends to $k_B$, hence
$$
\boxed{\frac{C_V}{\mathcal L}\longrightarrow\frac{k_B}{a}
=6.903\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,m^{-1}},\qquad C_V/(Nk_B)\to1.}
$$
The cutoff <temperature> is $\Theta_m=\hbar\omega_m/k_B=305.5$ K. The curve rises smoothly from zero toward this plateau; $\Theta_m$ sets a crossover scale, not a sharp transition. In normalized variables its initial slope is $C_V/(Nk_B)\simeq(2\pi/3)T/\Theta_m$. The low-temperature continuum limit also requires <temperature> above the finite-size level spacing; uniform center-of-mass translation is treated separately. Below the lowest nonzero mode <energy> a finite chain does not retain a continuum linear law down to arbitrary <temperature>.
