== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The incident <X-ray> drives the <electrons> of an atom. Away from an <absorption edge>, and when the photon <energy> greatly exceeds the relevant electronic binding energies but is small compared with $m_ec^2$, each electron has approximately the same <Thomson scattering> amplitude. The much heavier nucleus makes a negligible contribution because its acceleration is much smaller.

For <elastic scattering>, the amplitudes from different bound <electrons> add coherently. In units of the single-electron amplitude, the <atomic scattering factor> is the <Fourier transform> of the bound-electron <number density>:
$$
f(\mathbf q)=\int n_e(\mathbf r)e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}\,d^3r,
\qquad f(\mathbf0)=N_e.
$$
Consequently, when the <electron> cloud is not well resolved by the <scattering vector>, its internal phase differences are small and
$$
\boxed{f\simeq N_e,\qquad I\propto N_e^2.}
$$
This is an approximation to the coherent <scattering amplitude>, not an assertion that independently scattered intensities add as $N_e$. At larger momentum transfer, destructive interference within the cloud reduces $f$; near <absorption edges>, the <atomic scattering factor> acquires dispersive corrections. <Inelastic scattering> is a separate contribution. In an <ionic crystal>, use the ion's bound-electron count rather than automatically using the neutral atom's atomic number.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the total scattering angle $\psi=2\theta$. For a cubic <unit cell>, <Bragg's law> and the <Miller indices> give
$$
\sin^2\theta=\frac{\lambda^2}{4a_0^2}(h^2+k^2+l^2).
$$
The four measured values of $\sin^2\theta$, normalized to the first, are $1,1.330,2.655,3.649$. Multiplying by three gives approximately $3,4,8,11$, the initial allowed sequence for a <face-centered cubic lattice>:
$$
\{111\},\quad\{200\},\quad\{220\},\quad\{311\}.
$$
The FCC lattice factor is $1+(-1)^{h+k}+(-1)^{h+l}+(-1)^{k+l}$; it vanishes unless all three indices have the same parity. Thus the initial ratios differ from those of a primitive or body-centered cubic lattice. This is the conventional minimal cubic indexing; arbitrarily hiding additional weak reflections could make a short list alone ambiguous.

The low-pressure <rock salt structure> has an FCC <Bravais lattice> with one NaCl <formula unit> per <primitive unit cell>, or four per conventional cube. A possible <atomic basis of a crystal> is Na at zero and Cl at $(1/2,0,0)$ in conventional fractional coordinates. The two species have unequal <atomic scattering factors>, so both the odd and even FCC families can occur.

The four independent <lattice constant> estimates are
$$
a_0=\frac{\lambda\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}}{2\sin(\psi/2)}
=(0.56312,\ 0.56386,\ 0.56436,\ 0.56446)\,\mathrm{nm}.
$$
Their small scatter reflects the rounded angular data. The appropriate reported result is
$$
\boxed{\text{FCC Bravais lattice},\qquad a_0\simeq0.564\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The high-pressure <atomic basis of a crystal> has the <caesium chloride structure>. Its chemically labelled <Bravais lattice> is <simple cubic lattice>: exchanging Na and Cl is not a <translation symmetry>. Its <structure factor> is
$$
F_{hkl}=f_{\rm Na}+(-1)^{h+k+l}f_{\rm Cl}.
$$
Because <ionic bonding> gives approximately Na$^+$ and Cl$^-$, their bound-electron counts are $10$ and $18$. In the stipulated electron-count approximation, odd-index-sum reflections have $|F|=8$, while even-index-sum reflections have $|F|=28$. In particular, the first ring $\{100\}$ is weak but present; it must not be discarded using a body-centering extinction rule.

The next ring is $\{110\}$, and <Bragg's law> gives
$$
\sin\frac{\psi_{110}}2=\sqrt2\sin\frac{29.7^\circ}2,
\qquad\boxed{\psi_{110}=42.50^\circ.}
$$
The <powder diffraction multiplicities> are six and twelve. Counting all symmetry-equivalent reflections, while neglecting other angular factors, therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\frac{I_{110}}{I_{100}}\simeq\frac{12}{6}\left(\frac{28}{8}\right)^2=24.5.}
$$
The corresponding single-directed-reflection ratio would be $12.25$. Neutral-atom counts would give the wrong contrast because the material is ionic.

For measured integrated photon counts, the geometry must also be specified. More generally the ratio is $2|F_{110}/F_{100}|^2$ times the ratio of <Lorentz-polarization factors>, thermal-motion factors and detection efficiencies. For example, the common unpolarized symmetric-powder convention has
$$
LP(\theta)\propto\frac{1+\cos^2(2\theta)}{\sin^2\theta\cos\theta},
$$
and gives $24.5\,LP(21.2506^\circ)/LP(14.85^\circ)\simeq11.18$ if all other corrections are suppressed. Thus **24.5 is the multiplicity-and-structure-factor estimate**, rather than a geometry-independent precision count ratio; actual angle-dependent ionic form factors are also not supplied.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The high-pressure <lattice constant> follows from its first $(100)$ reflection:
$$
a_P=\frac{0.154\,\mathrm{nm}}{2\sin14.85^\circ}=0.30044\,\mathrm{nm}.
$$
Conserve the number of NaCl <formula units>. The low-pressure conventional <unit cell> contains four, whereas the high-pressure cell contains one. The <volume> per <formula unit> changes from $a_0^3/4$ to $a_P^3$, so
$$
\boxed{\frac{V_P}{V_0}=4\left(\frac{a_P}{a_0}\right)^3\simeq0.605.}
$$
The sample contracts by about $39.5\%$. Comparing $a_P^3$ directly with $a_0^3$ would compare different amounts of material.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Debye model> treats a three-dimensional solid as an isotropic elastic continuum with three independent acoustic <phonon polarizations>. It assumes harmonic <normal modes>, a linear <dispersion relation> $\omega=vq$, and equilibrium <Bose-Einstein statistics> with zero <phonon> <chemical potential>. Real anisotropy and nonlinear dispersion are replaced by one effective <Debye sound speed>. A cutoff restores the finite number of atomic vibrational degrees of freedom; the continuum itself would otherwise have infinitely many modes.

For sample <volume> $V$, the <wavevector> state density is $V/(2\pi)^3$ per polarization. Counting a sphere gives
$$
\mathcal N(q)=3\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{4\pi q^3}{3}
=\frac{Vq^3}{2\pi^2}.
$$
There are $3N$ modes for $N$ atoms. Hence
$$
q_D^3=6\pi^2\frac NV,
\qquad\boxed{\omega_D=v\left(6\pi^2n_a\right)^{1/3},\quad n_a=N/V.}
$$
The associated <phonon density of states> is
$$
g(\omega)=\frac{3V\omega^2}{2\pi^2v^3}
=\frac{9N\omega^2}{\omega_D^3},\qquad0<\omega<\omega_D.
$$
If longitudinal and transverse <sound speeds> differ, use $3/v^3=1/v_L^3+2/v_T^3$ for the low-frequency count. The <Debye frequency> is an effective mode-counting cutoff, not a claim that every actual branch terminates at exactly that frequency.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The FCC conventional <unit cell> has four atoms, so the cube contains
$$
N=4\left(\frac{180}{0.409}\right)^3=3.4096\times10^8
$$
atoms, neglecting surface corrections. A <phonon> is one excitation quantum of a vibrational <normal mode>; there can be many quanta per mode, and <zero-point motion> does not add half a phonon to the thermal population.

For the <low-temperature thermal phonon number>, integrate the <Bose-Einstein distribution> against the <Debye model> <phonon density of states>:
$$
N_{\rm ph}=\int_0^{\omega_D}\frac{g(\omega)}{e^{\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}-1}\,d\omega
=9N\left(\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\right)^3
\int_0^{\Theta_D/T}\frac{x^2}{e^x-1}\,dx.
$$
At $10\,\mathrm K$, $\Theta_D/T=21.5$, so extending the upper limit to infinity has negligible effect at the precision required. The supplied integral yields
$$
\boxed{N_{\rm ph}\simeq9(3.4096\times10^8)\left(\frac{10}{215}\right)^3(2.404)
\simeq7.42\times10^5.}
$$
This is the thermally excited <phonon> number, not the number $3N$ of available modes.

The continuum approximation fails when the thermally occupied <wavelengths> become comparable with the cube size $L$. From the <Debye temperature>,
$$
v=\frac{k_B\Theta_D}{\hbar(6\pi^2n_a)^{1/3}}\simeq1.86\times10^3\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.
$$
A lowest nontrivial acoustic frequency is of order $v/L$; a standing-wave estimate $\omega_{\min}\sim\pi v/L$ corresponds to $\hbar\omega_{\min}/k_B\sim0.25\,\mathrm K$. <Periodic boundary conditions> instead give $2\pi v/L$ and $0.50\,\mathrm K$. The precise spectrum depends on mechanical boundary conditions and branch speeds, neither of which is specified.

This <finite-crystal phonon freeze-out> requires summing discrete <normal modes>. For a finite positive lowest frequency, $N_{\rm ph}\sim g_{\min}e^{-\hbar\omega_{\min}/(k_BT)}$, rather than extrapolating the bulk $T^3$ law indefinitely. Rigid translations and rotations of an isolated crystallite are not internal thermal <phonons>. Surface acoustic modes can modify the intermediate crossover, but do not justify inventing a unique boundary spectrum.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The harmonic <phonon> contribution to the <internal energy> is
$$
U=\int_0^{\omega_D}g(\omega)\hbar\omega
\left[\frac12+\frac1{e^{\hbar\omega/(k_BT)}-1}\right]d\omega.
$$
For $T\gg\Theta_D$, every retained mode has $x=\hbar\omega/(k_BT)\ll1$. Expanding $1/(e^x-1)=1/x-1/2+O(x)$ cancels the explicit half quantum and gives $k_BT$ per mode to leading order. Therefore
$$
U=3Nk_BT+O\!\left(\frac{N(k_B\Theta_D)^2}{k_BT}\right),
\qquad\boxed{C_V=\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_V\longrightarrow3Nk_B.}
$$
Equivalently, differentiating before integrating gives
$$
C_V=9Nk_B\left(\frac{T}{\Theta_D}\right)^3\int_0^{\Theta_D/T}\frac{x^4e^x}{(e^x-1)^2}\,dx.
$$
The integrand tends to $x^2$, and its integral is $(\Theta_D/T)^3/3$, producing the same <Dulong-Petit law>. In classical language, the <equipartition theorem> assigns $k_BT/2$ each to the kinetic and elastic parts of every harmonic <normal mode>. Fixed <zero-point energy> does not contribute to the <heat capacity>.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Assume <thermal equilibrium>, nondegenerate carriers, and isotropic <parabolic band dispersions>, with the supplied <effective masses> interpreted as density-of-states masses. Take one effective valley with two <electron spin> states for each band; any additional valley multiplicity must instead be absorbed into the masses or explicit degeneracy factors.

Let the <conduction band> edge be $E_c$, the <valence band> edge $E_v$, and $E_g=E_c-E_v$. The spin-summed conduction <density of states> per <volume> is
$$
g_c(E)=\frac1{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m_e^*}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt{E-E_c},\qquad E\geq E_c.
$$
Using the nondegenerate limit of the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> and the substitution $x=(E-E_c)/(k_BT)$ gives
$$
n=\int_{E_c}^\infty g_c(E)e^{-(E-\mu)/(k_BT)}dE
=N_c e^{-(E_c-\mu)/(k_BT)},
\quad N_c=2\left(\frac{m_e^*k_BT}{2\pi\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}.
$$
The factor $\sqrt\pi/2$ in the supplied integral produces the normalization. Counting missing <valence band> <electrons> similarly gives
$$
p=N_v e^{-(\mu-E_v)/(k_BT)},\qquad
N_v=2\left(\frac{m_h^*k_BT}{2\pi\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}.
$$
Multiplication cancels the <chemical potential>, establishing the <semiconductor mass-action law>:
$$
\boxed{np=N_cN_v e^{-E_g/(k_BT)}
=4\left(\frac{k_BT}{2\pi\hbar^2}\right)^3(m_e^*m_h^*)^{3/2}e^{-E_g/(k_BT)}.}
$$
The result does not require an <intrinsic semiconductor>: <semiconductor doping> changes $n$ and $p$ separately while preserving this product under the stated equilibrium and nondegeneracy assumptions.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

A substitutional group-V atom such as <phosphorus> has one more <valence electron> than <germanium> and acts as a <donor impurity>, giving an <n-type semiconductor>. Approximate its outer <electron> by a <hydrogenic donor state> with screened Coulomb attraction and <effective mass> $m_e^*$. The <ionization energy> is the positive binding-energy magnitude
$$
E_D=13.606\,\mathrm{eV}\,\frac{m_e^*/m_e}{\epsilon_r^2}
=13.606\frac{0.22}{15.8^2}\,\mathrm{eV},
\qquad\boxed{E_D\simeq0.0120\,\mathrm{eV}=12.0\,\mathrm{meV}.}
$$
Its <effective Bohr radius of a hydrogenic donor> is about $3.80\,\mathrm{nm}$, much larger than an atomic core, consistent with a shallow extended bound state.

At $300\,\mathrm K$, $k_BT=25.9\,\mathrm{meV}$, while the mean three-dimensional classical translational <kinetic energy> is $3k_BT/2=38.8\,\mathrm{meV}$. Both exceed the donor binding <energy>, so substantial thermal ionization is expected, unlike excitation across the much larger $0.661\,\mathrm{eV}$ host <band gap>. A precise ionized fraction also depends on the <chemical potential>, <dopant density> and <donor charge-state degeneracy>; comparing two energy scales alone does not prove complete ionization at arbitrary concentration.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

With the supplied fixed <band gap> and <effective masses>, the <intrinsic carrier concentration> is
$$
n_i(T)=2\left(\frac{k_BT}{2\pi\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}(m_e^*m_h^*)^{3/4}
 e^{-E_g/(2k_BT)}.
$$
Numerically,
$$
n_i(750\,\mathrm K)=8.53\times10^{22}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}},\qquad
n_i(300\,\mathrm K)=1.01\times10^{19}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}.
$$
For uncompensated, approximately ionized <donor impurities>, <charge neutrality> and the <semiconductor mass-action law> require
$$
n-p=N_D,\qquad np=n_i^2,\qquad
n=\frac{N_D+\sqrt{N_D^2+4n_i^2}}2.
$$
There is no abrupt extrinsic-to-intrinsic transition. An order-of-magnitude lower crossover density is therefore
$$
\boxed{N_D\gtrsim n_i(750\,\mathrm K)\simeq8.5\times10^{22}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}
=8.5\times10^{16}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}.}
$$
Robust extrinsic dominance requires $N_D\gg n_i$; at the nominal equality, $n=1.618N_D$ and $p=0.618N_D$, so intrinsic contributions are already appreciable. For a quantitative tolerance $p/n\leq\eta$, the minimum ionized <dopant density> is $n_i(1-\eta)/\sqrt\eta$. For example, $\eta=0.1$ would require $N_D\geq2.85n_i$ at the upper temperature.

Using the nominal crossover estimate and the complete-ionization approximation at room temperature, $n\simeq N_D$ and
$$
\boxed{p(300\,\mathrm K)\simeq\frac{n_i(300\,\mathrm K)^2}{N_D}
=1.19\times10^{15}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\simeq1.19\times10^9\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}.}
$$
For the twofold neutral-donor degeneracy, a consistency calculation gives about $91.3\%$ ionization at $300\,\mathrm K$, raising this minority density to $1.30\times10^{15}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$, about $9.6\%$ above the complete-ionization estimate. Stronger extrinsic dominance uses more donors and correspondingly fewer <electron holes>. Actual germanium has <temperature>-dependent band parameters and multiple valleys; the numerical estimate consistently uses the fixed effective data supplied rather than adding an unsupplied multiplicity.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

A <Wigner-Seitz cell> is the region of real space at least as close to a chosen <Bravais lattice> point as to any other. It is bounded by perpendicular bisectors of vectors to neighboring lattice points and is a <primitive unit cell>.

A <reciprocal lattice vector> $\mathbf G$ satisfies $e^{i\mathbf G\cdot\mathbf R}=1$ for every <lattice translation> $\mathbf R$. With primitive vectors $\mathbf a_i$, reciprocal primitive vectors satisfy $\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf b_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}$, and $\mathbf G=\sum_jn_j\mathbf b_j$ for integers $n_j$.

The <first Brillouin zone> is the <Wigner-Seitz cell> of that <reciprocal lattice>, centered at $\mathbf k=0$. Its boundaries are <Bragg planes>, $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf G=G^2/2$. Higher <Brillouin zones> are the regions separated from the origin by successively more such planes: an interior point of zone $j$ has crossed exactly $j-1$ planes. A <second Brillouin zone> is generally disconnected in this extended-zone representation; it is not just a translated first-zone rectangle.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For area $A$, allowed <wavevectors> have density $A/(2\pi)^2$. Include two <electron spin> states and fill a disk of radius $k_F$ at zero <temperature>. Then
$$
N=2\frac{A}{(2\pi)^2}\pi k_F^2,
\qquad n_s=N/A=\frac{k_F^2}{2\pi}.
$$
The <free electron model> has <parabolic dispersion> $E=\hbar^2k^2/(2m_e)$, with its minimum chosen as zero, so
$$
\boxed{k_F=\sqrt{2\pi n_s},\qquad E_F=\frac{\pi\hbar^2n_s}{m_e}.}
$$
These are the <Fermi wavevector> and <Fermi energy> for a spin-degenerate two-dimensional <free electron gas>. An imposed spin polarization or additional valley degeneracy would change the state count.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For the <first two Brillouin zones of a rectangular lattice>, write $K=\pi/a$ and $L=\pi/b$, so $0<K<L$ and the <reciprocal lattice vectors> are $(2nK,2mL)$. The perpendicular bisectors to the four nearest axial reciprocal points enclose
$$
\boxed{\mathcal B_1=\{(k_x,k_y):|k_x|<K,\ |k_y|<L\}.}
$$
The <second Brillouin zone> consists of points beyond exactly one <Bragg plane>. In the first quadrant, it is the union of the following two regions:
$$
\begin{aligned}
K<k_x<2K,&\qquad 0<k_y<\frac{K^2+L^2-Kk_x}{L},\\
0<k_x<K,&\qquad L<k_y<\frac{K^2+L^2-Kk_x}{L}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Reflect these regions in both axes. The first is a trapezoidal side lobe; the second is half of a triangular top lobe. The sloping boundary is the bisector for $\mathbf G=(2K,2L)$. On the side lobe, $k_x<2K$ is the bisector condition for $\mathbf G=(4K,0)$: crossing that plane too would enter the third or a higher zone. These additional bisectors are why four rectangles pasted onto the first zone would be incorrect.

Equivalently, the full right lobe has vertices
$$
(K,-L),\quad(2K,-L+K^2/L),\quad(2K,L-K^2/L),\quad(K,L),
$$
and the top lobe has vertices $(-K,L),(K,L),(0,L+K^2/L)$. Add their left and bottom reflections. Their total area is
$$
2\left(2KL-\frac{K^3}{L}\right)+2\frac{K^3}{L}=4KL,
$$
equal to the first-zone area, as required by reciprocal-space state counting. These formulas remain valid for every $a>b$, including very elongated rectangles; boundaries themselves can be shared by convention.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For one <electron> per <primitive unit cell>, the areal <number density> is $n_s=1/(ab)$. The <Fermi circle> has radius $k_F=\sqrt{2\pi/(ab)}$. The nearer first-zone boundary is $k_x=\pm\pi/a$, because $a>b$. The circle protrudes precisely when
$$
k_F>\frac\pi a
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\frac{2\pi}{ab}>\frac{\pi^2}{a^2}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\boxed{a>\frac{\pi b}{2}.}
$$
At equality the <Fermi surface> only touches the boundary. Crossing an extended-zone boundary alone does not make a gap: the <free electron gas> remains metallic when the ionic <periodic potential> is neglected.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

With <spin degeneracy>, each <energy band> contains two <electrons> per <primitive unit cell>. Divalency therefore permits a filled first band, but only if its maximum lies below the second band's minimum. With negligible ionic <periodic potential>, the circular <Fermi surface> and the rectangular zone have equal enclosed area, not equal shape. Electrons occupy some states outside the first zone while some higher-<energy> first-zone states remain empty. Folded bands overlap, and the material is a <metal>.

A <periodic potential> mixes <plane waves> differing by a <reciprocal lattice vector>. At a two-wave <Bragg point>, the <nearly-free electron model> gives a direct gap $2|V_{\mathbf G}|$. Increasing appropriate <Fourier coefficients> can remove the band overlap. With the first band filled, the system is then a <band insulator>, provided the <temperature> is small compared with the insulating <band gap> in energy units.

The <rectangular free-electron band-overlap scale> follows from two different points in reciprocal space. Put $C=\hbar^2/(2m_e)$. Before gap opening, the highest first-zone energy is at the corner, while the lowest second-zone energy is at the midpoint of the nearer face:
$$
E_{1,\max}^{(0)}=C(K^2+L^2),\qquad E_{2,\min}^{(0)}=CK^2.
$$
Thus the downward shift $\delta_v$ of the first-band maximum plus the upward shift $\delta_c$ of the second-band minimum must overcome
$$
\boxed{\Delta_{\rm overlap}=CL^2=\frac{\hbar^2\pi^2}{2m_eb^2},\qquad
E_g^{\rm indirect}=\delta_v+\delta_c-\Delta_{\rm overlap}>0.}
$$
In a rigid-splitting estimate, an opening $\Delta$ lowers the first band by $\Delta/2$ and raises the second by $\Delta/2$, giving the familiar threshold $\Delta>\hbar^2\pi^2/(2m_eb^2)$. Equality is only band touching, not a positive insulating gap. This expression is the **overlap that must be removed**, under that explicit band-shift approximation.

<Positive direct gaps can coexist with band overlap>: a local direct splitting and the global <indirect band gap> are not interchangeable. The shape and <Fourier coefficients> of the ionic potential are unspecified, so there is no universal exact critical direct-gap width determined by $a,b$ alone. For a concrete demonstration, a separable potential $2V_x\cos(2Kx)+2V_y\cos(2Ly)$ has axial direct gaps $\Delta_x=2|V_x|$ and $\Delta_y=2|V_y|$. To first order, the corner's lowest level is $C(K^2+L^2)-|V_x|-|V_y|$, and the two candidate second-band minima are $CK^2+|V_x|$ and $CL^2+|V_y|$. Therefore, within this model and neglecting higher-order band shifts, nonoverlap requires
$$
\Delta_x+\frac{\Delta_y}{2}>CL^2,
\qquad \frac{\Delta_x}{2}+\Delta_y>CK^2.
$$
Equal axial gaps would give the first-order estimate $\Delta>2CL^2/3$, rather than the rigid-splitting estimate. Such a large opening can exceed the quantitative range of weak-potential perturbation theory, but the example identifies the missing model assumption: corner levels feel both couplings. The exact general answer is the positive indirect-gap criterion above; a geometry-only direct-gap threshold would claim more than the supplied potential information supports.
