== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

With the convention $\mathbf a_i\cdot\mathbf b_j=2\pi\delta_{ij}$, the <reciprocal lattice> of a <face-centered cubic lattice> is a <body-centered cubic lattice>. The reciprocal conventional cube is parallel to the direct conventional cube and has side $4\pi/a$. In direct-cubic coordinates its points are
$$
\boxed{\mathbf G=\frac{2\pi}{a}(h,k,l),\qquad h,k,l\text{ all even or all odd}.}
$$
The even triples supply the corners of that reciprocal cube and the odd triples its body centres. The <atomic basis> can change <diffraction intensities> through its <structure factor>; it does not change the <reciprocal lattice> determined by the translation lattice.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The cubic <Miller indices> give an <interplanar spacing> $d_{hkl}=a/\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}$. Equivalently, the <reciprocal lattice vector> normal to these <lattice planes> has length $2\pi/d_{hkl}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{d_{111}=\frac{a}{\sqrt3},\qquad
\mathbf G_{111}=\frac{2\pi}{a}(1,1,1).}
$$
The eight $\{111\}$ <reciprocal lattice vectors> are the nearest nonzero reciprocal points. Their perpendicular bisectors form the eight hexagonal faces of the first <Brillouin zone>, a truncated octahedron. For the positive $(111)$ direction, that <Bragg plane> is $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf G_{111}=|\mathbf G_{111}|^2/2$, or $k_x+k_y+k_z=3\pi/a$. It is parallel to the direct $(111)$ <lattice planes>. The six remaining square zone faces correspond to the $\{200\}$ reciprocal points. The zone boundary is halfway to a reciprocal point, not the point itself.

=== c
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Use the <angle between two planes> defined as the acute angle between their <normal vectors>. The normals $(1,1,1)$ and $(-1,1,1)$ have dot product one and lengths $\sqrt3$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\phi=\cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac13\right)=70.5288^\circ.}
$$
Choosing an oriented rather than an acute interplanar angle could give the supplementary $109.4712^\circ$; it describes the same unoriented pair of <lattice planes>.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

The $(100)$ <normal vector> is $(1,0,0)$. Its normalized dot product with $(1,1,1)$ is $1/\sqrt3$, so
$$
\boxed{\phi=\cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac1{\sqrt3}\right)=54.7356^\circ.}
$$
This is the acute angle between the <lattice planes>, rather than the complementary angle between a plane and the other plane's normal.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The <X-ray> <wavelength> is $\lambda=hc/E=1.54980\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm m$. Write the amplitude of one conventional <unit cell> as the <face-centred cubic selection rule> times the two-atom <atomic basis> amplitude:
$$
F_{hkl}=\left[1+e^{i\pi(k+l)}+e^{i\pi(h+l)}+e^{i\pi(h+k)}\right]
\left[f_{\mathrm{Ga}}+f_{\mathrm{As}}e^{i\pi(h+k+l)/2}\right].
$$
The first bracket is four when the indices have equal parity and zero otherwise. Among the allowed <Bragg reflections>, the <zinc blende structure factor> therefore gives
$$
|F_{hkl}|^2=16\begin{cases}
f_{\mathrm{Ga}}^2+f_{\mathrm{As}}^2,&h,k,l\text{ odd},\\
(f_{\mathrm{Ga}}+f_{\mathrm{As}})^2,&h+k+l\equiv0\pmod4,\\
(f_{\mathrm{Ga}}-f_{\mathrm{As}})^2,&h+k+l\equiv2\pmod4.
\end{cases}
$$
Here the <atomic scattering factors> are taken as real. For the requested rough estimate set $f_{\mathrm{Ga}}\simeq31$ and $f_{\mathrm{As}}\simeq33$. The weak difference reflections remain allowed: unequal species do not have the extra exact extinction of the equal-species diamond limit.

The first six allowed values of $h^2+k^2+l^2$ are $3,4,8,11,12,16$. <Bragg's law> gives the measured scattering angle $2\theta=2\sin^{-1}(\lambda\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}/2a)$. For <powder diffraction>, multiply the squared amplitude by the <powder diffraction multiplicity> $m_{hkl}$. The last two columns use $I_{111}=100$:
$$
\begin{array}{c|r|r|r|r|r}
hkl&2\theta\ ({}^\circ)&m_{hkl}&|F|^2&I\propto m|F|^2&I\propto m|F|^2LP\\\hline
111&27.4693&8&32800&100&100\\
200&31.8234&6&64&0.1463&0.1068\\
220&45.6248&12&65536&299.707&98.684\\
311&54.0830&24&32800&300&67.110\\
222&56.6994&8&64&0.1951&0.03921\\
400&66.5025&6&65536&149.854&21.165
\end{array}
$$
The first intensity column is the geometry-independent multiplicity-and-structure estimate. The second additionally assumes an unpolarized beam and a common symmetric powder-diffractometer geometry, with <Lorentz-polarization factor>
$$
LP(\theta)\propto\frac{1+\cos^2(2\theta)}{\sin^2\theta\cos\theta}.
$$
An overall constant cancels in either normalization. A particular measured intensity set is not fixed by the atomic numbers alone: actual <atomic scattering factors> decrease with momentum transfer, anomalous scattering and absorption can matter, and the instrument geometry and <polarization> determine angular corrections. The two columns state the approximation rather than asserting identical peak heights for all instruments.<Quantitative powder diffraction using a (2 + 3) surface diffractometer and an area detector by Giuseppe Abbondanza and others>[Section 4, equation 5 and sections 4.1–4.2: multiplicity, structure amplitude and geometry-dependent polarization and Lorentz corrections]{ref}

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

The sixth allowed family is $400$. For the two <lattice constants>, use $2\theta_{400}=2\sin^{-1}(2\lambda/a)$:
$$
2\theta_{400}(\mathrm{GaAs})=66.50250^\circ,\qquad
2\theta_{400}(\mathrm{AlAs})=66.40961^\circ.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\Delta(2\theta)\equiv2\theta(\mathrm{AlAs})-2\theta(\mathrm{GaAs})=-0.09290^\circ.}
$$
The larger <lattice constant> shifts the AlAs reflection to lower scattering angle. As a differential check, $\Delta(2\theta)\simeq-2\tan\theta\,\Delta a/a$ in radians.

If the angular displacement is unresolved, use the intensity contrast. With the same $f\simeq Z$ model, a difference reflection has basis amplitude $13-33=-20$ for AlAs, versus $31-33=-2$ for GaAs. Its squared amplitude is therefore one hundred times greater before overall sample normalization. More robustly, compare a weak and a strong reflection within each specimen. Neglecting their almost identical angular corrections,
$$
\frac{I_{200}}{I_{220}}=\frac6{12}\left(\frac{f_A-f_{\mathrm{As}}}{f_A+f_{\mathrm{As}}}\right)^2
=\begin{cases}0.0004883,&\mathrm{GaAs},\\0.09452,&\mathrm{AlAs}.
\end{cases}
$$
The contrast in this internal ratio is about a factor $194$. Element-specific <X-ray fluorescence>, for example testing for a Ga characteristic line with a suitably energetic incident beam, or an <X-ray absorption spectroscopy> scan through an element-specific <absorption edge>, also distinguishes composition. Such scans can change incident energy; one must not assume every core shell can be excited by the original $8\,\mathrm{keV}$ beam.

== 2
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <density of states> $g(E)$ counts one-electron states per energy interval, including both <electron spin> orientations. It may be a total or a per-volume quantity; $U$ must have the matching normalization. The <Fermi-Dirac distribution>
$$
f(E,T)=\frac1{e^{(E-\mu(T))/(k_BT)}+1}
$$
is the occupation probability of such a state. In the fixed-volume <free electron model>, the integration variable $E$ and the <density of states> do not depend on $T$. The occupation depends explicitly on $T$ and implicitly through the <chemical potential> $\mu(T)$ needed to preserve electron number. Thermal expansion or a changing <electronic band structure> would require additional effects outside this model.

At $T\ll T_F$, only states within order $k_BT$ of the <Fermi energy> change their occupation. There are order $g(E_F)k_BT$ such states, each carrying an excitation energy of order $k_BT$. Thus $U(T)-U(0)\propto g(E_F)(k_BT)^2$ and the <electronic heat capacity> is proportional to $g(E_F)k_B^2T$. Using the supplied coefficient,
$$
\boxed{C_V=\gamma T,\qquad\gamma=\frac{\pi^2}{3}k_B^2g(E_F),\qquad
U(T)-U(0)=\frac12\gamma T^2.}
$$
The shell argument fixes the scaling, not the numerical coefficient; the controlled <Sommerfeld expansion> fixes that coefficient at fixed electron number. Filled states far below $E_F$ cannot accept arbitrary thermal excitations because of the <Pauli exclusion principle>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

At fixed volume and particle number, reversible heating obeys $dS=C_V\,dT/T$. Integrating the <electronic heat capacity> from zero <temperature> gives
$$
S(T)-S(0)=\int_0^T\frac{\gamma T'}{T'}\,dT'=\gamma T.
$$
With no extensive residual ground-state degeneracy, the <third law of thermodynamics> sets the electronic $S(0)=0$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{S_{\mathrm{el}}=\gamma T.}
$$
The <entropy> normalization follows that of $\gamma$: total, per volume or per mole. Lattice contributions are additional and are not part of this electronic result.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $g_v(E)$ denote the spin-summed <density of states> per volume. An electron moment with magnitude the <Bohr magneton> $\mu_B$ has <Zeeman energies> $\mp\mu_B B$. At low <temperature>, the two spin populations are
$$
n_\pm=\frac12\int_0^{\mu\pm\mu_B B}g_v(E)\,dE,
\qquad
n_+-n_-=g_v(E_F)\mu_B B+O(B^3).
$$
The common <chemical potential> changes only at order $B^2$, so it does not affect this linear imbalance. The <magnetization> is $M=\mu_B(n_+-n_-)=\mu_B^2g_v(E_F)B$. Define SI volume <magnetic susceptibility> by $M=\chi_p H$ and take the weak applied field as $B\simeq\mu_0H$. Then <Pauli paramagnetism> gives
$$
\boxed{\chi_p=\mu_0\mu_B^2g_v(E_F)=\frac{3\mu_0n\mu_B^2}{2E_F}.}
$$
The last equality uses the three-dimensional parabolic <free electron model>, $g_v(E_F)=3n/(2E_F)$. This susceptibility is nearly independent of <temperature> for $T\ll T_F$, unlike the <Curie law> for localized independent moments. The displayed result excludes orbital <diamagnetism> and interaction enhancements. If susceptibility instead denotes $\partial M/\partial B$, omit $\mu_0$; confusing that convention with $\partial M/\partial H$ changes the units.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

In the one-band electron <Hall effect> model, $R_H=-1/(ne)$, with $e>0$. The measured <Hall coefficient> therefore gives
$$
n=\frac1{e|R_H|}=2.4966\times10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}.
$$
A monovalent <body-centered cubic lattice> has two conduction electrons per conventional <unit cell>. Thus $na^3=2$, and
$$
\boxed{a=\left(\frac2n\right)^{1/3}=4.3108\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm m.}
$$
Counting two spin states within the <Fermi sphere> gives $k_F=(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}=9.0418\times10^9\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$. Using the specified <effective mass> in the dispersion,
$$
\boxed{E_F=\frac{\hbar^2k_F^2}{2m^*}=2.3960\,\mathrm{eV},\qquad
T_F=E_F/k_B=2.7805\times10^4\,\mathrm K.}
$$
There is one mole of conduction electrons per mole of sodium. Its molar <density of states> at $E_F$ is $3N_A/(2E_F)$, giving
$$
\boxed{\gamma_{\mathrm{mol}}=\frac{\pi^2N_Ak_B^2}{2E_F}
=1.4757\,\mathrm{mJ\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-2}}.}
$$
Using the volume <density of states> in <2/c> gives
$$
\boxed{\chi_p=1.0543\times10^{-5}\quad\text{(dimensionless SI volume susceptibility)}.}
$$
The <effective mass> changes the dispersion and <density of states>; the electron <Bohr magneton> used for the spin coupling retains the bare electron mass. Replacing it by $e\hbar/(2m^*)$ would be an additional, unjustified magnetic-moment assumption. A Hall factor or a multiband metal would also invalidate the simple density inference; the stated calculation uses the requested one-band approximation.

== 3
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Take two localized <spin> orientations with moments $\pm\mu_B$ along the applied <magnetic field>. This assumes an electron-like spin $g$ factor of two. Their <energies> are $\mp\mu_BB$, so the one-moment <partition function> is $Z_1=2\cosh x$, with $x=\mu_BB/(k_BT)$. The thermal mean moment is $k_BT\,\partial_B\ln Z_1=\mu_B\tanh x$, and the <spin-one-half paramagnetic magnetization> is
$$
\boxed{M=n\mu_B\tanh\!\left(\frac{\mu_BB}{k_BT}\right).}
$$
For $|x|\ll1$, $M\simeq n\mu_B^2B/(k_BT)$. With the same SI convention $\chi=\partial M/\partial H$ and $B\simeq\mu_0H$,
$$
\boxed{\chi=\frac CT,\qquad C=\frac{n\mu_0\mu_B^2}{k_B}.}
$$
This <Curie law> is a zero-field or weak-field susceptibility. At arbitrary field the differential response is $(C/T)\operatorname{sech}^2x$, and $M$ saturates at $n\mu_B$; extending the linear law to unlimited field would contradict that finite <saturation magnetization>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

In the <Weiss molecular field> approximation, the local response obeys
$$
M=n\mu_B\tanh\!\left[\frac{\mu_B(B+\lambda\mu_0M)}{k_BT}\right].
$$
Linearize around $M=B=0$ in the stable phase of <paramagnetism>. Substitution of <3/a> gives
$$
M=\frac{C}{\mu_0T}(B+\lambda\mu_0M),\qquad
(T-\lambda C)M=\frac C{\mu_0}B.
$$
Hence the <Curie-Weiss law> and <Weiss temperature> are
$$
\boxed{\chi=\frac{C}{T-\theta},\qquad
\theta=\lambda C=\frac{\lambda n\mu_0\mu_B^2}{k_B}.}
$$
For positive $\lambda$, $\theta$ is the <mean-field theory> <Curie temperature>: the zero-magnetization solution becomes unstable there, and below it a spontaneous solution appears. For negative coupling, a negative fitted <Weiss temperature> indicates predominantly opposing interactions, but this one-uniform-magnetization treatment does not determine an antiferromagnetic ordering pattern or its transition temperature. More generally the fitted <Weiss temperature> need not equal the observed ordering temperature.

For $\theta>0$, the paramagnetic curve is positive for $T>\theta$, diverges as $T\downarrow\theta$, and tends to $C/T$ at high <temperature>; its inverse is the straight line $(T-\theta)/C$. The expression is not the stable susceptibility below $\theta$. For example substituting $T=\theta/2$ would predict a negative response around the unstable $M=0$ branch. The nonlinear self-consistency equation must instead be expanded about the appropriate spontaneous <magnetization>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Within the stipulated localized, one-moment-per-atom <spin> model, the number density follows directly from mass density and molar mass:
$$
n=\frac{\rho N_A}{M_{\mathrm{mol}}}
=\frac{8908}{0.058693}N_A
=9.1400\times10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}.
$$
The <face-centered cubic lattice> is consistent with four atoms per conventional <unit cell>; no additional factor four is needed once $n$ has been obtained from the density. The <Curie constant> is $C=0.71549\,\mathrm K$. Identifying the model's $\theta=\lambda C$ with the specified <Curie temperature> gives
$$
\boxed{\lambda=\frac{627\,\mathrm K}{0.71549\,\mathrm K}=8.7632\times10^2.}
$$
At very low <temperature>, a fully ordered single domain has $M_s=n\mu_B=8.4764\times10^5\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}$. Its <Weiss molecular field> is
$$
\boxed{B_{\mathrm{eff}}=\lambda\mu_0M_s
=\frac{k_BT_C}{\mu_B}=9.3343\times10^2\,\mathrm T.}
$$
The cancellation of $n$ in the middle expression provides a useful check. The ordinary magnetostatic scale is only $\mu_0M_s\simeq1.065\,\mathrm T$, smaller by the factor $\lambda$. Thus $B_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is not a plausible ordinary self-generated dipolar <magnetic field> of the nickel moments. It represents the <exchange interaction> energy in field units, not an additional Maxwell field of that magnitude. This does not assert that such large externally generated fields are forbidden. Nickel is an itinerant <ferromagnet>; the imposed one-$\mu_B$-per-atom model is an estimate rather than its measured microscopic moment.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

A macroscopic nickel specimen can split into <magnetic domains>, each with nonzero local <spontaneous magnetization> but with different orientations. Their vector contributions can cancel, giving an almost zero measured net <magnetization> below the <Curie temperature>. This reduces the external stray-field energy, at the expense of <magnetic domain-wall> energy; the balance favours a multidomain state in many ordinary specimens.

An applied <magnetic field> can move <magnetic domain walls> and rotate the domain moments, revealing a large response and eventually <saturation magnetization>. Thus a zero average does not mean that each local moment has become thermally disordered or that the material is above its <Curie temperature>. Preparation, shape and domain-wall pinning influence remanence.

== 4
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Assume thermal equilibrium, a three-dimensional <nondegenerate semiconductor>, one parabolic conduction extremum and one parabolic valence extremum, with two spin states in each. Use the supplied <effective masses> as scalar density-of-states parameters. With conduction energy $E=E_c+\varepsilon$, state counting gives the per-volume <density of states>
$$
D_c(\varepsilon)=\frac1{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m_e^*}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt\varepsilon.
$$
Since $E_c-\mu\gg k_BT$, replace the <Fermi-Dirac distribution> by its <Boltzmann factor>. Using $x=\varepsilon/(k_BT)$ and the supplied integral,
$$
n=e^{-(E_c-\mu)/(k_BT)}\int_0^\infty D_c(\varepsilon)e^{-\varepsilon/(k_BT)}d\varepsilon
=N_c e^{-(E_c-\mu)/(k_BT)},
$$
$$
N_c=2\left(\frac{m_e^*k_BT}{2\pi\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}
=2\left(\frac{2\pi m_e^*k_BT}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}.
$$
The <electron hole> occupation is $1-f(E)$ in the <valence band>. Applying the same integration below $E_v$ gives
$$
p=N_v e^{-(\mu-E_v)/(k_BT)},\qquad
N_v=2\left(\frac{2\pi m_h^*k_BT}{h^2}\right)^{3/2}.
$$
Their product cancels the <chemical potential>, giving the <semiconductor mass-action law>
$$
\boxed{np=4\left(\frac{2\pi k_B}{h^2}\right)^3
(m_e^*m_h^*)^{3/2}T^3e^{-E_g/(k_BT)}.}
$$
Thus, for masses expressed in ordinary mass units,
$$
\boxed{A=4\left(\frac{2\pi k_B}{h^2}\right)^3
=\frac{k_B^3}{2\pi^3\hbar^6},\qquad B=\frac32,\qquad C=3.}
$$
The labels $B,C$ in this last box are exponents, unrelated to the magnetic field or <Curie constant> above. <Valley degeneracy> or multiple valence bands must be included in the <effective density of states>, or absorbed into suitable <density-of-states effective masses>, not counted twice. Real silicon has anisotropic multiple valleys, so its conductivity mass is not automatically its density-of-states mass; the numerical answers below implement the paper's supplied scalar-mass model.<ECE 340 Lecture 6 by John Dallesasse>[Slide 26, A Few Cautions: carrier counting uses the density-of-states effective mass, while silicon conductivity and cyclotron masses differ]{ref}

The formula is not an identity for arbitrarily degenerate carriers. For a concrete counterexample, take $\mu-E_c=10k_BT$ while holes remain dilute. Fermi occupation gives $n/N_c\simeq24.1$, whereas the Boltzmann replacement would give $e^{10}\simeq22026$; the predicted product then differs by about three orders of magnitude. Separate electron and hole quasi-Fermi levels out of equilibrium would also prevent the cancellation used above.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For an undoped neutral <intrinsic semiconductor>, $n=p$. Equating the two expressions from <4/a> and taking logarithms gives
$$
\ln N_c-\frac{E_c-\mu}{k_BT}
=\ln N_v-\frac{\mu-E_v}{k_BT},
$$
and hence the <intrinsic-semiconductor chemical potential>
$$
\boxed{\mu_i(T)=\frac{E_c+E_v}{2}
+\frac{k_BT}{2}\ln\frac{N_v}{N_c}
=\frac{E_c+E_v}{2}+\frac34k_BT\ln\frac{m_h^*}{m_e^*}.}
$$
Therefore $\boxed{\alpha=\tfrac34\ln(m_h^*/m_e^*)}$. The logarithm is <temperature>-independent for fixed parabolic <effective masses>. The <chemical potential> is exactly at midgap only if the two <effective density of states> values coincide; for $m_h^*>m_e^*$ it moves above midgap. Additional valley multiplicities would add their logarithmic contribution.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

A substitutional phosphorus atom supplies one more valence electron than the silicon atom it replaces. In the shallow <hydrogenic donor state> model, the remaining positive impurity attracts that extra electron through the screened <Coulomb potential>
$$
V(r)=-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0\epsilon_r r}.
$$
Rescaling the <hydrogen atom> binding energy by the <effective mass> and dielectric screening gives the ionization energy
$$
\boxed{E_D^{\mathrm{bind}}=13.6057\,\mathrm{eV}\,
\frac{m_e^*/m_e}{\epsilon_r^2}
=13.6057\frac{0.26}{11.7^2}\,\mathrm{eV}
=0.02584\,\mathrm{eV}.}
$$
The donor level lies at $E_c-E_D^{\mathrm{bind}}$; its binding magnitude is positive. The associated <effective Bohr radius of a hydrogenic donor> is $a_B^*=\epsilon_r(m_e/m_e^*)a_0\simeq2.38\,\mathrm{nm}$, consistent with a shallow state extended over several lattice spacings.

At $1000\,\mathrm K$, $k_BT=0.08617\,\mathrm{eV}$, approximately $3.3$ times this binding energy. Dilute donors are consequently expected to be largely ionized, rather than frozen out. The actual ionized fraction also depends on the <chemical potential>, available conduction states and <donor charge-state degeneracy>; comparing $k_BT$ to the binding energy alone is not a normalized ionization probability. At sufficiently high <temperature>, intrinsic electron-hole production can dominate the total carriers even though donors are ionized. Band anisotropy and short-range impurity corrections limit the numerical hydrogenic estimate.

=== d
{scope}

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

At $300\,\mathrm K$, $k_BT=0.0258520\,\mathrm{eV}$. With the scalar <effective masses> and two-spin convention of <4/a>, the <effective density of states> values are
$$
N_c=3.32684\times10^{24}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}},\qquad
N_v=6.11180\times10^{24}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}.
$$
The <intrinsic carrier concentration> follows from $n=p=n_i$:
$$
\boxed{p=n_i=\sqrt{N_cN_v}\,e^{-E_g/(2k_BT)}
=2.1405\times10^{15}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}.}
$$
From <4/b>, $\alpha=(3/4)\ln(0.39/0.26)=0.304099$. Taking $E_v=0$ as a reference,
$$
\boxed{\mu_i-E_v=0.562862\,\mathrm{eV},\qquad
E_c-\mu_i=0.547138\,\mathrm{eV}.}
$$
It lies $7.862\,\mathrm{meV}$ above the middle of the <band gap>, because the hole <effective density of states> is larger. Both band edges are more than twenty $k_BT$ away, validating the <nondegenerate semiconductor> approximation.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

Take uncompensated <donor impurities> and initially approximate them as ionized. <Charge neutrality> then gives $n-p=N_d$, while the <semiconductor mass-action law> gives $np=n_i^2$. Solving the quadratic without prematurely discarding minority carriers,
$$
n=\frac{N_d+\sqrt{N_d^2+4n_i^2}}2,\qquad
p=\frac{2n_i^2}{N_d+\sqrt{N_d^2+4n_i^2}}.
$$
Since $N_d\gg n_i$, the hole density is
$$
\boxed{p\simeq\frac{n_i^2}{N_d}
=4.5816\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}},\qquad
n\simeq1.0000\times10^{20}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}.}
$$
Use the conduction population to locate the <chemical potential>:
$$
\boxed{E_c-\mu=k_BT\ln\frac{N_c}{n}=0.269180\,\mathrm{eV},\qquad
\mu-E_v=0.840820\,\mathrm{eV}.}
$$
It moves toward the <conduction band> but remains about $10.4k_BT$ below it, so the mobile electrons are nondegenerate. Despite the donor binding energy being comparable with $k_BT$, the large number of available conduction states makes ionization almost complete at this dilute doping. For a singly occupied two-spin donor, $N_d^+/N_d=[1+2e^{(\mu-E_D)/(k_BT)}]^{-1}$, with $E_D=E_c-0.02584\,\mathrm{eV}$. The inferred value is $0.99984$, confirming the approximation to much better than one percent. Including this small correction raises the hole density only to about $4.5824\times10^{10}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$.
