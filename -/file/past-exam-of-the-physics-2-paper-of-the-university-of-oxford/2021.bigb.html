== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $k=1/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$. The separation of the two <point charges> is $2l\sin\phi$, so <Coulomb's law> gives the horizontal repulsive <force> $F=kQ^2/(4l^2\sin^2\phi)$. Resolving the rod's <tension> vertically and horizontally gives $T\cos\phi=mg$ and $T\sin\phi=F$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\sin^2\phi\tan\phi=\frac{Q^2}{16\pi\epsilon_0mgl^2}.}
$$
The left side increases from zero to infinity for $0<\phi<\pi/2$, so this determines a unique outward <equilibrium>. For a small angle, $\phi^3\simeq Q^2/(16\pi\epsilon_0mgl^2)$. With $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, direct numerical solution gives
$$
\boxed{\phi=7.71\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{rad}=0.442^\circ.}
$$

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

A large <electrical conductor> between the <point charges> provides <electrostatic shielding>: one must not retain their original mutual <Coulomb force> through an ideal infinite plate. In the usual broad-plate approximation, let $\sigma$ denote the unperturbed <surface charge density> **on each face**. The <electrostatic boundary condition> gives an outward <electric field> $E_0=\sigma/\epsilon_0$. Neglecting local <electrostatic induction>, the rod's <tension> then satisfies $mg\tan\phi=QE_0$, giving
$$
\boxed{\phi\simeq\tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{Q\sigma}{\epsilon_0mg}\right)=1.98^\circ.}
$$
There are two physical qualifications. First, if $\sigma$ means the **total charge per projected plate area**, shared equally between the two faces, then $E_0=\sigma/(2\epsilon_0)$ and the same approximation gives $\phi\simeq0.989^\circ$. The factor of two is a convention about the specified <surface charge density>, not a different <Gauss's law>.

Second, a <point charge> near an <electrical conductor> makes its <surface charge density> nonuniform. For an infinite plane with prescribed background <electric field> $E_0$, the <method of images> adds an attractive <force> $kQ^2/(4x^2)$, where $x=l\sin\phi$. The more accurate <equilibrium> equation is
$$
mg\tan\phi=QE_0-\frac{Q^2}{16\pi\epsilon_0l^2\sin^2\phi}.
$$
Its larger, stable root is approximately $1.96^\circ$ for the per-face convention, or $0.877^\circ$ for the total-area convention. This ideal-plane refinement is not an exact answer for an unspecified finite plate: its dimensions and the meaning of the quoted <surface charge density> would be needed. In particular, adding a uniform-sheet <electric field> to the original mutual repulsion would describe a different, nonshielding model.

==== Solution

At <direct current> an ideal <capacitor> is an <open circuit>. A finite measured <electrical resistance> therefore requires the <resistor> and <capacitor> to form a <parallel circuit>, with $R=100\,\Omega$. Use <phasors> proportional to $e^{i\omega t}$. The <electrical admittance> and <complex impedance> are
$$
Y=\frac1R+i\omega C,\qquad Z=\frac{R}{1+i\omega RC},\qquad |Z|=\frac{R}{\sqrt{1+(\omega RC)^2}}.
$$
Interpreting the stated AC value as the measured <impedance magnitude>, $\omega=2\pi(50)\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $|Z|=20\,\Omega$ give $\omega RC=\sqrt{24}$. Thus
$$
\boxed{R=100\,\Omega,\quad C=1.56\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm F,\quad Z(50\,\mathrm{Hz})=(4.00-19.60i)\,\Omega.}
$$
The <electric current> leads the applied <voltage> by $\tan^{-1}\sqrt{24}=78.46^\circ$. Strictly, AC “resistance” is ambiguous: if it means $\operatorname{Re}Z=20\,\Omega$, the same <parallel circuit> instead gives $\omega RC=2$, $C=63.7\,\mu\mathrm F$, and $Z=20-40i\,\Omega$. A measurement of $|V|/|I|$ selects the boxed interpretation.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

A suitable <electrical circuit> has $R$ in series with the input and a shunt branch consisting of $C$ in parallel with the lossy <inductor> $R_L+i\omega L$; take the output <voltage> across that shunt branch. This is the middle arrangement in the circuit figure. In terms of its <complex impedance>,
$$
Z_p=\left(i\omega C+\frac1{R_L+i\omega L}\right)^{-1},\qquad H(\omega)=\frac{U_2}{U_1}=\frac{Z_p}{R+Z_p}.
$$
At <direct current> the <capacitor> is an <open circuit> and the <inductor> contributes only $R_L$, so $H(0)=R_L/(R+R_L)=0.01$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{R=99\,\Omega.}
$$
When $R_L$ is neglected, $Z_p=i\omega L/(1-\omega^2LC)$. At the <antiresonance> $\omega_0^2LC=1$ its <electrical admittance> vanishes, so $|H|$ reaches its maximum value of one. Consequently
$$
\boxed{C=\frac1{L\omega_0^2}=1.78\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm F.}
$$
This value uses precisely the requested lossless approximation; keeping $R_L$ changes both the peak position and its height. A <transfer function> peak and one DC measurement do not uniquely identify every possible <two-port network>, but the displayed arrangement meets both observations to the stipulated approximation. Also, the angle brackets in the source must denote AC <wave amplitudes> or <root mean square> values: the literal time averages of sinusoidal <voltages> are zero, whose ratio would be undefined.

=== a

==== Solution

In <vacuum>, <Maxwell's equations> in differential form are
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0},\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}.
$$
Here $\rho$ is <charge density>, $\mathbf J$ is <electric current density>, $\mathbf E$ is <electric field>, and $\mathbf B$ is <magnetic flux density>. In a source-free region, taking the <curl> of the third equation and using $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)-\nabla^2\mathbf E$ gives the <wave equation>
$$
\nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}.
$$
The same argument gives the <wave equation> for $\mathbf B$. A concrete family satisfying all four <Maxwell's equations> is
$$
\mathbf E=\widehat{\mathbf x}F(z-ct),\qquad \mathbf B=\frac{\widehat{\mathbf y}}{c}F(z-ct),\qquad
\boxed{c=\frac1{\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}}.}
$$
The function $F$ may be any twice differentiable profile. These are transverse <travelling waves>; reverse the <magnetic field> direction for propagation in the opposite direction. Because the source-free <Maxwell's equations> are homogeneous <linear differential equations>, any finite <linear combination> of such paired <electric fields> and <magnetic fields> is again a solution. Infinite <superpositions> also work whenever the required derivatives converge. A solution of the component <wave equations> alone must still satisfy the divergence and mutual-curl constraints.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For a <plane electromagnetic wave> with <linear polarization>, write $\mathbf E=E_0\widehat{\mathbf x}\cos(kz-\omega t)$ and $\mathbf H=E_0\widehat{\mathbf y}\cos(kz-\omega t)/(\mu_0c)$. The <Poynting vector> is instantaneous <energy> flux:
$$
\mathbf S=\frac{E_0^2}{\mu_0c}\cos^2(kz-\omega t)\widehat{\mathbf z}.
$$
The <light intensity> is its cycle average, not its peak:
$$
\boxed{I=\langle|\mathbf S|\rangle=\frac{E_0^2}{2\mu_0c}=\frac12\epsilon_0cE_0^2.}
$$
An incident <electromagnetic wave> transports longitudinal <momentum> $\mathcal E/c$ per <energy> $\mathcal E$. Perfect reflection back along the incident direction reverses that <momentum>, transferring $2\mathcal E/c$ to the mirror. Dividing by illuminated <area> and time gives the mean <radiation pressure>
$$
\boxed{p=\frac{2I}{c}=\frac{2\langle|\mathbf S|\rangle}{c}.}
$$
Here $\mathbf S$ denotes the **incident travelling wave**. The average net <Poynting vector> of the incident-plus-reflected <standing wave> is zero, although its <radiation pressure> is nonzero.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <condenser lens> should image the point source onto the small projection <lens>. Then every illuminated LCD point directs its central <light ray> into the projection <lens>, rather than wasting light elsewhere. In the paraxial idealization this gives approximately uniform useful illumination.

Put the LCD at $z=0$, the source at $z=-100\,\mathrm{mm}$, the projection <lens> at $z=u=29.5567\,\mathrm{mm}$, and the <condenser lens> at $z=-h$. The cone from the <condenser lens>'s rim, of radius $15\,\mathrm{mm}$, to the projection <lens> has radius
$$
r_{\rm LCD}=15\frac{u}{u+h}\,\mathrm{mm}
$$
at the LCD. Maximum approximately uniform illumination without unnecessary overfilling makes this equal the half-diagonal $7.5\,\mathrm{mm}$. Thus $h=u$: place the <condenser lens> $29.56\,\mathrm{mm}$ before the LCD, or $70.44\,\mathrm{mm}$ after the source. Its object and image distances are $100-h$ and $u+h$, so
$$
\boxed{f_c=\frac{(100-h)(u+h)}{100+u}=32.14\,\mathrm{mm}.}
$$
The illuminated disk just contains the rectangular LCD. Exact spatially uniform <irradiance> is not guaranteed by a single spherical <lens> and a point source; the result uses paraxial <geometrical optics>, negligible <optical aberration>, and an approximately uniform incident cone.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

\Image[2021-operational-amplifier-stages.png]
{title=An inverting amplifier followed by an integrator, with an optional offset-correction branch}

Use the following arrangement: an <inverting operational amplifier> with input <resistor> $R_a$ and feedback <resistor> $R_f$, followed by an <op amp integrator> with input <resistor> $R_i$ and feedback <capacitor> $C_i$. Ground both non-inverting inputs. Each <ideal operational amplifier> has infinite <input impedance>, so its inputs draw no <electric current>; <negative feedback> makes the inverting input a <virtual ground>.

<Kirchhoff's current law> at the first summing junction gives $V_1=-(R_f/R_a)V_{\rm in}$. At the second summing junction it gives $V_1/R_i+C_i\,dV_{\rm out}/dt=0$. Consequently the two inversions cancel:
$$
\boxed{V_{\rm out}(t)=V_{\rm out}(t_0)+\frac{R_f}{R_aR_iC_i}\int_{t_0}^t V_{\rm in}(t')\,dt'.}
$$
The initial output <voltage> records the initial <capacitor> charge. A reset switch across $C_i$ sets it to zero. The formula holds while both stages remain within their supply rails; a persistent input <voltage> otherwise produces <amplifier saturation>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $V_{\rm in}=v(t)+V_{\rm off}$, with constant unwanted offset $V_{\rm off}$. Add an adjustable reference <voltage> $V_b$ through another <resistor> $R_b$ to the **first** inverting summing junction, as the dashed branch in the circuit figure shows. <Kirchhoff's current law> now gives
$$
V_1=-R_f\left(\frac{V_{\rm in}}{R_a}+\frac{V_b}{R_b}\right).
$$
Choose the reference polarity and magnitude so that
$$
\boxed{\frac{V_b}{R_b}=-\frac{V_{\rm off}}{R_a}.}
$$
A potentiometer between positive and negative reference supplies permits either sign. The first stage then has zero output <voltage> when $v=0$, and the <op amp integrator> integrates only $v(t)$:
$$
\boxed{V_{\rm out}(t)=V_{\rm out}(t_0)+\frac{R_f}{R_aR_iC_i}\int_{t_0}^t v(t')\,dt'.}
$$
Merely placing a large <resistor> across the integrating <capacitor> limits drift but also changes the <transfer function>; cancellation at the summing junction preserves ideal integration of the desired signal.

== 6
{scope}

=== Solution

Neglect the entrance and exit <capacitor fringing fields> and use the coaxial <electric field> between the cylindrical plates. Let $U=V(R_1)-V(R_2)$, with $R_2>R_1$. The radial <electric field> is
$$
E_r(r)=\frac{U}{r\ln(R_2/R_1)}.
$$
For an <electron>, of <electric charge> $-e$, to follow the circular path $R=(R_1+R_2)/2$, its inward <centripetal force> must be $m_ev_0^2/R$. Therefore
$$
e\frac{U}{R\ln(R_2/R_1)}=\frac{m_ev_0^2}{R},\qquad
\boxed{U=\frac{m_ev_0^2}{e}\ln\frac{R_2}{R_1}.}
$$
The **inner plate must be at higher <electric potential>**: the <electric field> points outward and the <electron>'s <electric force> inward. The <electric force> is perpendicular to the <velocity>, so no <work> is done and the speed remains $v_0$. The required <voltage> is independent of the opening angle $\phi$, which only sets the transit time $R\phi/v_0$. For a narrow gap, $\ln(R_2/R_1)\simeq(R_2-R_1)/R$. This is a nonrelativistic, negligible-<capacitor fringing field> answer; finite open cylindrical sections do not have an exactly radial <electric field> at their ends.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For a uniformly illuminated single slit of width $b$, summing the <wave amplitudes> across the slit gives the <Fraunhofer diffraction> profile
$$
I(\theta)=I(0)\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2,\qquad \beta=\frac{\pi b\sin\theta}{\lambda}.
$$
The first minimum occurs at $\sin\theta=\lambda/b$. The <Rayleigh criterion> places one source's central maximum at the other source's first minimum; hence the slit gives $\Delta\theta\simeq\lambda/b$. A circular <aperture> instead produces an <Airy disk>, whose first minimum is at $1.22\lambda/D$. For the camera's circular <lens>,
$$
\boxed{\Delta\theta=\frac{1.22\lambda}{D}=4.88\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{rad}=0.0280^\circ\simeq101\text{ arcseconds}.}
$$
The factor $1.22$ belongs to circular-<aperture> <diffraction>, rather than the single-slit profile.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Rayleigh criterion> gives a separation in the sensor plane $\delta=f\Delta\theta=2.44\,\mu\mathrm m$, using the $5\,\mathrm{mm}$ image distance. Counting one square <pixel> per such <spatial resolution element> gives
$$
\boxed{N_{\rm Rayleigh}\simeq\frac{(4\,\mathrm{mm})(3\,\mathrm{mm})}{(2.44\,\mu\mathrm m)^2}=2.02\times10^6.}
$$
This is an estimate of the number of optically distinguishable elements. To sample all the spatial information, specify a <signal sampling> criterion as well. For <incoherent imaging> through an ideal circular <aperture>, the <optical transfer function> has cutoff spatial frequency $\nu_c=D/(\lambda f)$. The <Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem> requires a <pixel> pitch
$$
p\leq\frac1{2\nu_c}=\frac{\lambda f}{2D}=1.00\,\mu\mathrm m,
\qquad \boxed{N_{\rm Nyquist}\geq4000\times3000=1.20\times10^7.}
$$
A common intermediate rule, two <pixels> per Rayleigh separation, gives about $8.1$ million <pixels>; it is a heuristic and does not quite sample the full ideal <optical transfer function> bandwidth. Thus about $2$ million is the elementary <spatial resolution element> count, while $12$ million is the ideal monochrome <Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem> answer to “full use.” Color-filter layout and finite <pixel> area would impose further practical conditions.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Take the grounded plane as $z=0$. The <method of images> replaces it, in $z>0$, by an <image charge> $-q$ at $z=-d$ and an <image charge> $+q$ at $z=-2d$. These make the <electric potential> zero on the plane; the <uniqueness theorem for the electrostatic potential> establishes the solution in the physical half-space.

At $z=3d/2$, both real <point charges> contribute upward <electric fields>, each of magnitude $kq/(d/2)^2$. The nearer <image charge> contributes downward and the farther one upward. Hence
$$
\mathbf E=\frac{kq}{d^2}\left(4+4-\frac4{25}+\frac4{49}\right)\widehat{\mathbf z}
=\boxed{\frac{9704}{1225}\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0d^2}\widehat{\mathbf z}.}
$$
This is upward for $q>0$; retaining signed $q$ automatically handles the opposite sign. No self-<electric field> is involved because the evaluation point is distinct from every <point charge>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For <image charges in a sixty-degree conducting wedge>, use <polar coordinates> in a cross-section normal to the planes' intersection, putting the physical wedge at $0<\theta<\pi/3$ and the real <point charge> at $(r,\theta)=(d,\pi/6)$. Repeated reflection in the two grounded faces produces five <image charges>, all at radius $d$ and the same coordinate along the intersection:
$$
\begin{array}{c|rrrrrr}
\theta&30^\circ&90^\circ&150^\circ&210^\circ&270^\circ&330^\circ\\
\text{charge}&q\ (\text{real})&-q&+q&-q&+q&-q
\end{array}
$$
Equivalently, the positive copies are at $\theta=\pi/6+2k\pi/3$ and the negative copies at $\theta=-\pi/6+2k\pi/3$, for $k=0,1,2$. Only the original <point charge> lies inside the physical wedge.

\Image[2021-wedge-image-charges.png]
{title=Five image charges outside the physical sixty-degree conducting wedge}

For any observation point on either face, reflection pairs every positive <point charge> with an equidistant negative one. Their <Coulomb potentials> cancel, so both grounded faces have zero <electric potential>. The <electric potential> also tends to zero at infinity. The <uniqueness theorem for the electrostatic potential> then identifies this <method of images> construction with the physical solution.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $\widehat{\mathbf e}_r$ point from the intersection to the real <point charge>. Reflection symmetry cancels the component of <electrostatic force> perpendicular to the wedge bisector. An <image charge> $q_j$ at angular separation $\Delta$ from the real <point charge> gives radial <force>
$$
F_{r,j}=kqq_j\frac{d(1-\cos\Delta)}{[2d^2(1-\cos\Delta)]^{3/2}}.
$$
The two adjacent negative <image charges> have $\Delta=\pm60^\circ$, distance $d$, and combined radial <force> $-kq^2/d^2$. The two positive <image charges> have $\Delta=\pm120^\circ$, distance $\sqrt3d$, and combined radial <force> $+kq^2/(\sqrt3d^2)$. The opposite negative <image charge> is at distance $2d$ and contributes $-kq^2/(4d^2)$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F=-\frac{q^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0d^2}\left(\frac54-\frac1{\sqrt3}\right)\widehat{\mathbf e}_r.}
$$
The <electrostatic force> is towards the intersection, independently of the sign of $q$. One sums the five <image-charge> <electric fields> and multiplies by $q$; the factor $1/2$ used in induced-charge <electrostatic energy> must not be inserted into this direct <force> calculation.

=== a

==== Solution

Let each slit alone give local <light intensity> $I_s$, and assume equal coherent illumination. In <Fraunhofer diffraction>, the two <wave amplitudes> differ in <wave phase> by $\delta=2\pi s\sin\theta/\lambda$. Adding the <wave amplitudes> before squaring gives
$$
I(\theta)=I_s|1+e^{i\delta}|^2=4I_s\cos^2\left(\frac{\pi s\sin\theta}{\lambda}\right).
$$
For a flat screen, $\sin\theta=x/\sqrt{d^2+x^2}$. In the paraxial region $|x|\ll d$, the <double-slit interference> profile is therefore
$$
\boxed{I(x)=4I_s\cos^2\left(\frac{\pi sx}{\lambda d}\right),\qquad \Delta x=\frac{\lambda d}{s}.}
$$
The given $d\gg s^2/\lambda$ justifies the far-field expansion. Slit widths have been neglected; a finite width multiplies this profile by the single-slit <diffraction envelope>. At large angles the one-slit <light intensity> and geometric spreading must also be retained, rather than treating $I_s$ as constant.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The screen now lies in the back <focal plane> of the <lens>. In paraxial <geometrical optics>, a <light ray> arriving at the <lens> at height $y$ and angle $\theta$ leaves at angle $\theta-y/f$, and reaches the screen at
$$
x=y+f(\theta-y/f)=f\theta.
$$
Thus all <light rays> with the same incident direction reach the same screen coordinate, independently of their height at the <lens>. The <Fraunhofer diffraction> pattern is an angular pattern mapped to $x=f\theta$:
$$
\boxed{I(x)=4I_{s,f}\cos^2\left(\frac{\pi sx}{\lambda f}\right),\qquad \Delta x=\frac{\lambda f}{s}.}
$$
Here $I_{s,f}$ is the one-slit <light intensity> after focusing. Its absolute value depends on collected light and the <lens> <aperture>, which are unspecified; it need not equal the no-<lens> value $I_s$. Propagation from the slits to the <lens> introduces a common angular <wave phase>, so it does not alter the relative <wave phase> responsible for these fringes, provided the <aperture> passes the required light.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The slide adds <optical path length> $\Delta=(n-1)t=0.5(0.1\,\mathrm{mm})=50\,\mu\mathrm m$ to the covered beam. Put the covered slit at transverse coordinate $+s/2$. For positive screen coordinate $x$ its geometrical path is shorter by approximately $sx/d$, so the total covered-minus-uncovered <optical path difference> is
$$
\Delta_{\rm tot}=(n-1)t-\frac{sx}{d}.
$$
The zero-order fringe follows equal total <optical path lengths>, $\Delta_{\rm tot}=0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{x_0=\frac{d(n-1)t}{s}=0.050\,\mathrm m=5.0\,\mathrm{cm},\quad\text{towards the covered slit}.}
$$
The covered beam needs a shorter geometric path to compensate its extra <optical path length>. This is the paraxial estimate; exact point-to-point distances give $x_0\simeq5.006\,\mathrm{cm}$. “Central” here follows the zero-order fringe continuously as the slide is introduced. At one fixed <wavelength>, the final <monochromatic wave> <interference> pattern is periodic, so intensity alone identifies a fringe position only modulo the fringe spacing. Slide reflections and absorption have been neglected.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The two optical <frequencies> have no persistent mutual <optical coherence> over the detector's averaging time, so their <light intensities> add. Assuming equal strengths and using $s=1.0\,\mathrm{mm}$, let $I_s$ be the one-slit <light intensity> at either <wavelength>. Then
$$
\boxed{I(\theta)=4I_s\left[\cos^2\left(\frac{\pi s\sin\theta}{\lambda_1}\right)+\cos^2\left(\frac{\pi s\sin\theta}{\lambda_2}\right)\right],\quad \lambda_1=520\,\mathrm{nm},\quad\lambda_2=540\,\mathrm{nm}.}
$$
The identity $\cos A+\cos B=2\cos[(A+B)/2]\cos[(A-B)/2]$ rewrites this as
$$
I(\theta)=4I_s\left[1+\cos\left(\pi s\sin\theta\left(\frac1{\lambda_1}+\frac1{\lambda_2}\right)\right)\cos\left(\pi s\sin\theta\left(\frac1{\lambda_1}-\frac1{\lambda_2}\right)\right)\right].
$$
The slowly varying <interferometric visibility> is approximately $\mathcal V=|\cos(\pi s\sin\theta\,[1/\lambda_1-1/\lambda_2])|$. Its first zero occurs at
$$
s\sin\theta_{\rm zero}=\frac{\lambda_1\lambda_2}{2(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)}=7.02\,\mu\mathrm m,
\qquad \theta_{\rm zero}=0.402^\circ.
$$
The rapid fringes have effective <wavelength> $\lambda_h=2\lambda_1\lambda_2/(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)=529.81\,\mathrm{nm}$, so the first contrast minimum is at order $7.02\,\mu\mathrm m/\lambda_h=13.25$. Thus the conventional estimate is **about 13 fringes on each side**, before their first washout, plus the central maximum. “Clearly” is not a specified visibility threshold: the last of those fringes already has low contrast.

\Image[2021-two-wavelength-fringes.png]
{title=Two-wavelength interference, its first visibility minima, and contrast revival over minus one to plus one degree}

The fringes do **not** disappear permanently beyond that point. Their contrast revives; the first exact common maximum satisfies $27\lambda_1=26\lambda_2=14.04\,\mu\mathrm m$, at $|\theta|=0.804^\circ$. This is a concrete counterexample to interpreting 13 as a global bound on visible fringes. Unequal spectral strengths would leave nonzero residual contrast at the nominal washout; the plotted equal-strength case is the natural interpretation in the absence of specified strengths.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

With <phasors> proportional to $e^{i\omega t}$, the <resistor>, <inductor>, and <capacitor> have <complex impedances> $R$, $i\omega L$, and $1/(i\omega C)$. In a <series circuit> they carry the same <electric current>, so their <voltage> drops add:
$$
\boxed{Z(\omega)=R+i\left(\omega L-\frac1{\omega C}\right).}
$$
The real part describes <Joule heating>; the imaginary part changes sign between capacitive and inductive response, passing through zero at $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <voltage divider> relation for the <capacitor> output gives the <transfer function>
$$
H(\omega)=\frac{V_{\rm out}}{V_{\rm in}}=\frac{1/(i\omega C)}{R+i\omega L+1/(i\omega C)}=\boxed{\frac1{1-\omega^2LC+i\omega RC}.}
$$
Consequently the relative <wave amplitude> is
$$
\boxed{|H|=\frac1{\sqrt{(1-\omega^2LC)^2+(\omega RC)^2}}.}
$$
For input $V_0\cos\omega t$, the output is $V_0|H|\cos(\omega t+\phi)$, where the continuous <phase shift> is
$$
\boxed{\phi=-\operatorname{atan2}(\omega RC,\,1-\omega^2LC).}
$$
For positive <frequency> and $R>0$, this <phase shift> goes from $0$ towards $-\pi$, passing through $-\pi/2$ at $\omega_0$. A one-argument arctangent without a quadrant correction would wrongly predict a positive <phase shift> above $\omega_0$. The <capacitor> follows a slowly varying input <voltage>, while its output falls as $1/(\omega^2LC)$ at high <frequency>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

At $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$ the inductive and capacitive <electrical reactances> cancel, so the <complex impedance> is $R$ and the <electric current> amplitude is $V_0/R$. The <capacitor> <voltage> amplitude is $V_0/(R\omega_0C)$. Thus the specified <quality factor> is
$$
\boxed{Q=|H(\omega_0)|=\frac1{R\omega_0C}=\frac{\omega_0L}{R}=\frac1R\sqrt{\frac LC}.}
$$
A <capacitor> output exceeding the input <voltage> is consistent with a passive <electrical circuit>: the <inductor> and <capacitor> <voltages> largely cancel in their sum. The expression assumes positive $R$; the ideal undamped limit $R=0$ has no finite steady resonant response.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $x=\omega/\omega_0$. Maximizing the output <wave amplitude> is equivalent to minimizing
$$
D(x)=|H|^{-2}=(1-x^2)^2+\frac{x^2}{Q^2}.
$$
Its derivative is $D'(x)=2x[2x^2-2+Q^{-2}]$. An interior minimum at positive <frequency> therefore requires $Q>1/\sqrt2$, and occurs at
$$
\boxed{\omega_{\rm res}=\omega_0\sqrt{1-\frac1{2Q^2}},\qquad Q>\frac1{\sqrt2}.}
$$
Indeed $D''(x_{\rm res})=8x_{\rm res}^2>0$, and the peak value is $|H|_{\max}=Q/\sqrt{1-1/(4Q^2)}$. For $0<Q\leq1/\sqrt2$, $D$ increases for $x>0$ and the largest output is the DC value $|H(0)|=1$. At equality the putative peak has reached zero <frequency>; there is no distinct nonzero-<frequency> <resonance>. This threshold concerns <capacitor> <voltage> peaking: the <series RLC circuit>'s <electric current> still has its maximum at $\omega_0$ for every positive $R$.

== 11
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

<Faraday's law of induction> states that the induced <electromotive force> around an oriented closed circuit equals the negative rate of change of linked <magnetic flux>. For a stationary circuit,
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E=\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=-\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt},\qquad \Phi_B=\int_A\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf A.}
$$
Here $\mathbf E$ is <electric field>, $\mathbf B$ is <magnetic flux density>, and the oriented surface $A$ has the circuit as its boundary. For $N$ identical series turns, replace $\Phi_B$ by the total <flux linkage> $N\Phi_B$. <Lenz's law> supplies the sign: an induced <electric current> opposes the **change** of linked <magnetic flux>, in agreement with <conservation of energy>.

If the wire moves with local <velocity> $\mathbf u$, the more general <electromotive force> includes the magnetic <Lorentz force> per unit <electric charge>:
$$
\mathcal E=\oint(\mathbf E+\mathbf u\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=-\frac{d}{dt}\int_{A(t)}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf A.
$$
The boundary and surface orientations in both expressions are related by the <right-hand rule>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

In the <long-solenoid approximation>, the internal <magnetic flux density> is uniform, $B=\mu_0nI$, and the external <magnetic field> is negligible. This follows from the <magnetostatic Ampère law> applied to a rectangular loop running inside and outside the <solenoid>. Its number of turns is $N=nD$ and its cross-sectional <area> is $A=\pi a^2$. Each turn links <magnetic flux> $\Phi_B=BA=\mu_0nI\pi a^2$. Therefore the <flux linkage> is
$$
\Lambda=N\Phi_B=\mu_0n^2D\pi a^2I.
$$
Using the definition of <self-inductance>, $\Lambda=LI$, gives
$$
\boxed{L=\mu_0n^2\pi a^2D.}
$$
This neglects end leakage and winding thickness and uses <vacuum permeability> for the air core.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For the imposed <electric current> $I=I_0\cos\omega t$, <Faraday's law> gives the self-induced <electromotive force>
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E_{\rm self}=-L\frac{dI}{dt}=\omega LI_0\sin\omega t.}
$$
The externally applied terminal <voltage>, with the <passive sign convention>, has the opposite sign:
$$
v_L=L\frac{dI}{dt}=-\omega LI_0\sin\omega t.
$$
Thus the terminal <voltage> leads the <electric current> by $\pi/2$. The instantaneous input <power> is
$$
p=v_LI=-\frac12\omega LI_0^2\sin2\omega t=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12LI^2\right),\qquad \boxed{\langle p\rangle=0.}
$$
An ideal <inductor> alternately stores <magnetic energy> and returns it to the source. Real winding <electrical resistance> would add mean <Joule heating> $I_0^2R_w/2$; the ideal zero result assumes that loss is neglected.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The single secondary turn links the primary <magnetic flux> $\mu_0n\pi a^2I_p$, so its <mutual inductance> with the primary is
$$
M=\mu_0n\pi a^2=\frac{L}{nD}.
$$
Choose positive winding directions so that positive secondary <electric current> would reinforce the primary <magnetic flux>. <Faraday's law> and <Kirchhoff's voltage law> then give
$$
L_s\frac{dI_s}{dt}+RI_s=-M\frac{dI_p}{dt}=\omega MI_0\sin\omega t.
$$
After the transient proportional to $e^{-Rt/L_s}$ has decayed, put $\alpha=\tan^{-1}(\omega L_s/R)$. The steady <electric current> is
$$
\boxed{I_s(t)=\frac{\omega MI_0}{\sqrt{R^2+(\omega L_s)^2}}\sin(\omega t-\alpha).}
$$
Its <wave amplitude> is $\omega MI_0/\sqrt{R^2+(\omega L_s)^2}$ and it lags the induced <electromotive force> by $\alpha$. Relative to the primary $I_0\cos\omega t$, its <phase shift> is $-\pi/2-\alpha$, modulo $2\pi$. Reversing the chosen secondary winding direction adds $\pi$ to this signed <phase shift> and leaves all <power> predictions unchanged.

The lamp's mean <Joule heating> is
$$
\boxed{\langle P_R\rangle=\frac12R|I_s|^2=\frac{R\omega^2M^2I_0^2}{2[R^2+(\omega L_s)^2]}.}
$$
For $\omega L_s\ll R$ the response is nearly in phase with the induced <electromotive force>; for $\omega L_s\gg R$ the secondary <electric current> nearly opposes the primary <electric current> in the stated winding convention.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

The positive lamp <power> comes from extra <work> by the primary current source. The secondary <electric current> creates a changing <magnetic flux> through the primary, so maintaining the same prescribed primary <electric current> requires an additional terminal <voltage>. With the preceding winding convention,
$$
v_p=L\frac{dI_p}{dt}+M\frac{dI_s}{dt}.
$$
Using <phasors>, $\widetilde I_s=-i\omega M\widetilde I_p/(R+i\omega L_s)$, hence the primary sees the <complex impedance>
$$
Z_{\rm in}=i\omega L+\frac{\omega^2M^2}{R+i\omega L_s},\qquad
\operatorname{Re}Z_{\rm in}=\frac{\omega^2M^2R}{R^2+(\omega L_s)^2}>0.
$$
It follows directly that
$$
\boxed{\langle P_{\rm source}\rangle=\tfrac12I_0^2\operatorname{Re}Z_{\rm in}=\langle P_R\rangle.}
$$
This is the <reflected impedance> of the secondary load. The <self-inductance> and <mutual inductance> terms exchange stored <magnetic energy>, while the lamp dissipates it. Multiplying the two coupled equations from <Kirchhoff's voltage law> by their respective <electric currents> also gives the instantaneous <conservation of energy> identity
$$
v_pI_p=RI_s^2+\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12LI_p^2+MI_pI_s+\frac12L_sI_s^2\right).
$$
The derivative averages to zero in periodic steady state. A fixed primary <current source> supplies the required extra <voltage>; a fixed <voltage source> would generally change its primary <electric current> when the lamp is connected.
