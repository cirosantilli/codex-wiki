{scope}

=== Solution

Let $K=eU_a$, where $U_a=100\,\mathrm V$, and let $v_z$ be the incident <electron> <speed>. Nonrelativistically, $mv_z^2=2eU_a$. Take the plate length along the beam as $\ell=0.01\,\mathrm m$, their separation as $d=0.01\,\mathrm m$, and their centre-to-screen distance as $D=0.30\,\mathrm m$. A signed deflecting <voltage> $V_d$ gives the transverse <acceleration> $a_x=eV_d/(md)$, choosing its sign so that positive $V_d$ deflects the <electron> towards positive $x$. The transit time is $t_p=\ell/v_z$. The displacement inside the plates and in the subsequent field-free drift is
$$
x=\frac12a_xt_p^2+a_xt_p\frac{D-\ell/2}{v_z}
=\frac{a_x\ell D}{v_z^2}
=\frac{V_d\ell D}{2dU_a}.
$$
Thus the two screen edges require
$$
\boxed{-166.7\,\mathrm V\ \lesssim V_d\lesssim+166.7\,\mathrm V,\qquad\text{total sweep}\simeq333\,\mathrm V.}
$$
The transverse deflection at plate exit is $x_p=x\ell/(2D)=4.17\,\mathrm{mm}$ at either extreme, smaller than the half-gap $5\,\mathrm{mm}$, so the beam clears the plates. The outgoing slope is $v_x/v_z=x/D\simeq0.833$; no small-angle replacement for the trajectory was used. The <kinetic energy> remains far below $mc^2$, so the nonrelativistic approximation is adequate. If “30 cm behind” is instead measured from the downstream plate edge, replace $D$ by $0.305\,\mathrm m$, giving approximately $\pm164\,\mathrm V$; the intended thin-deflector estimate is about $\pm170\,\mathrm V$.

=== Solution

Orient the ring's area normal with positive external <magnetic flux>. <Faraday's law> gives the applied <electromotive force>
$$
\mathcal E_{\rm ext}=-\frac{d}{dt}(AB_0\cos\omega t)
=AB_0\omega\sin\omega t.
$$
The ring's <self-inductance> opposes changes in its <electric current>. With positive <electrical resistance> $R$, <Kirchhoff's voltage law> therefore gives $L\dot I+RI=\mathcal E_{\rm ext}$. Define $\phi=\tan^{-1}(\omega L/R)$. The periodic steady state is
$$
\boxed{I(t)=\frac{AB_0\omega}{\sqrt{R^2+\omega^2L^2}}\sin(\omega t-\phi),\qquad
\langle P\rangle=\frac{R A^2B_0^2\omega^2}{2(R^2+\omega^2L^2)}.}
$$
The second result follows from <Joule heating>, $P=RI^2$, and $\langle\sin^2\rangle=1/2$. For unspecified initial conditions, add a transient $I_h=C\exp(-Rt/L)$; the displayed time average is over the periodic steady state after that transient has decayed. The <inductor> temporarily stores and returns <energy>, with no net storage change over one complete cycle.

=== Solution

Use a cross-section normal to the sheets' line of intersection: the positive sheet is $x=0$, and the negative sheet is $y=0$. A <Gaussian pillbox> gives a single infinite sheet's <electric field> magnitude $\sigma/(2\epsilon_0)$ on either side. <Superposition> consequently gives, away from the sheets,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E=\frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}\bigl(\operatorname{sgn}(x)\hat{\mathbf x}-\operatorname{sgn}(y)\hat{\mathbf y}\bigr),\qquad
|\mathbf E|=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt2\epsilon_0}.}
$$
The <electric field> points away from the positively charged sheet and towards the negatively charged sheet, bisecting each right angle. The <electric field lines> are straight within each quadrant: in the first quadrant, for example, $dy/dx=-1$, so $x+y$ is constant along a line. They start on $x=0$ and finish on $y=0$. At a sheet, the normal component has the usual surface-charge jump, so the quadrant formula specifies the appropriate one-sided limits rather than a unique <electric field> on the charged surface.

==== Solution

The six faces subtend equal <solid angles> at the central <point charge>. <Gauss's law> fixes their total outward <electric flux> at $q/\epsilon_0$. By the cube's <symmetry>,
$$
\boxed{\Phi_{\rm each\ face}=\frac{q}{6\epsilon_0}.}
$$
For negative $q$ the same signed expression represents inward <electric flux>. The result is independent of the side length.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

Place eight identical cubes around the charged corner to make one larger cube, with the <point charge> at its centre. Each large face has <electric flux> $q/(6\epsilon_0)$ and consists of four equivalent small faces. Each of the original cube's three faces opposite the charged corner therefore has
$$
\boxed{\Phi_{\rm opposite}=\frac{q}{24\epsilon_0}.}
$$
On each of its three faces containing the corner, the <electric field> of the <point charge> obeying <Coulomb's law> is tangent to the face at every regular point; its outward-normal component is zero. Defining the face integral by omitting the singular corner gives
$$
\boxed{\Phi_{\rm incident}=0,\qquad\Phi_{\rm original\ cube}=\frac{q}{8\epsilon_0}.}
$$
A boundary <point charge> does not satisfy the ordinary nonsingular hypotheses for simply counting it as an interior charge in <Gauss's law>. The $1/8$ result expresses the octant <solid angle> and the specified improper surface integrals. Moving a charge towards the corner from inside a cube before integrating is a different limiting operation; its total closed-surface <electric flux> stays $q/\epsilon_0$ until it reaches the boundary.

{scope}

=== Solution

In source-free <vacuum>, <Maxwell's equations> read
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=0,\quad\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\quad
\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf B,\quad
\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t\mathbf E.
$$
Take the <curl> of the third equation and use the <curl of the curl identity> $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)-\nabla^2\mathbf E$. Then
$$
\boxed{\nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t^2\mathbf E,\qquad
c=\frac1{\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}}\simeq2.998\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The prescribed <plane wave> travels in the $+z$ direction. Its <polarization> is along $x$, and <Faraday's law> requires $B_y=(k/\omega)E_x$ with $\omega=ck$. The wave's <magnetic flux density> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B(z,t)=\frac{E_0}{c}\cos(kz-\omega t)\hat{\mathbf y},\qquad
B_0=3.34\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm T.}
$$
The direction is also fixed by $\mathbf E\times\mathbf B$ pointing along the propagation direction. A superposed static uniform <magnetic field> is not fixed by the oscillatory <electric field>; the expression above is the <magnetic field> belonging to the wave itself.

== 6
{scope}

=== Solution

The requested source-image relation is the <thin lens> equation, using positive real-object distance $u$ and signed image distance $v$:
$$
\boxed{\frac1f=\frac1u+\frac1v.}
$$
Here $f>0$ for a converging <lens>, and $v<0$ denotes a <virtual image>. This is usually distinguished from <Lensmaker's equation>, $1/f=(n-1)(1/R_1-1/R_2)$ for a thin <lens> in air with the usual signed surface radii. The latter relates <focal length> to the <lens> material and curvature, rather than to object and image positions.<University Physics Volume 3 by Samuel J. Ling and others>[§2.4: thin-lens equation, sign conventions, and <lensmaker's equation>]{ref}

For a <magnifying glass> held close to the eye, compare the angle $h/u$ with the unaided near-point angle $h/D_n$, where $D_n=25\,\mathrm{cm}$. Maximum usable <angular magnification> occurs with the <virtual image> at the <near point>, so $v=-D_n$. Thus
$$
u=\frac{fD_n}{D_n+f}=\frac{100}{29}\,\mathrm{cm}\simeq3.45\,\mathrm{cm},\qquad
\boxed{M_{\max}=\frac{D_n}{u}=1+\frac{D_n}{f}=7.25.}
$$
The object lies inside the front <focal length>. The emerging <light rays> diverge, while their backward extensions meet at an upright enlarged <virtual image> on the object's side. With the image at infinity instead, the relaxed-eye <angular magnification> would be $D_n/f=6.25$, which is smaller. A finite eye-to-lens distance $s$ changes the near-point result to $1+(D_n-s)/f$; the quoted maximum uses the conventional $s\simeq0$ limit.<University Physics Volume 3 by Samuel J. Ling and others>[§2.7, equations 2.29–2.30: near-point <angular magnification> and lens-eye separation]{ref}

{scope}

=== Solution

The original circuit has $R_1$ and $C$ in series between the input and the inverting node, with $R_2$ providing feedback and the noninverting node grounded. An ideal <operational amplifier> in stable linear <negative feedback> draws no input <electric current> and holds the inverting node at <virtual ground>. For <phasors> proportional to $e^{i\omega t}$, the input <complex impedance> is $Z_1=R_1+1/(i\omega C)$. <Kirchhoff's current law> gives $\widetilde V_{\rm in}/Z_1=-\widetilde V_{\rm out}/R_2$, hence
$$
\boxed{H(\omega)=\frac{\widetilde V_{\rm out}}{\widetilde V_{\rm in}}
=-\frac{i\omega R_2C}{1+i\omega R_1C},\qquad
|H|=\frac{\omega R_2C}{\sqrt{1+\omega^2R_1^2C^2}}.}
$$
This is an inverting <high-pass filter>. Its <phase> can be chosen continuously as
$$
\boxed{\arg H=-\frac\pi2-\tan^{-1}(\omega R_1C).}
$$
Thus $V_{\rm out}(t)=V_0|H|\cos(\omega t+\arg H)$. The ratio above is a <phasor> ratio, not an instantaneous ratio at input zero crossings. Setting $R_1=R_2$ does not remove the <capacitor>'s <phase>: the output lags by $90^\circ+\tan^{-1}(\omega R_1C)$, between $90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$. At $\omega R_1C=1$ the lag is $135^\circ$; only for $\omega R_1C\gg1$ does it approach $180^\circ$. Being within the amplifier's own <bandwidth> does not specify the external RC corner <frequency>.

==== Solution

Define $V=\phi(r_2)-\phi(r_1)$ and $\eta=\ln(r_2/r_1)>0$. Cylindrical <symmetry> and <Gauss's law> imply a radial <electric field> proportional to $1/r$. Integrating $\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi$ fixes the sign:
$$
\phi(r)-\phi(r_1)=\frac{V\ln(r/r_1)}\eta,\qquad
\boxed{\mathbf E(r)=-\frac{V}{\eta r}\hat{\mathbf r}.}
$$
If the inner cylinder has <linear charge density> $\lambda_q$, a coaxial <Gaussian surface> gives $2\pi r E_r=\lambda_q/\epsilon_0$, so $\lambda_q=-2\pi\epsilon_0V/\eta$. <Capacitance> is positive regardless of the chosen terminal polarity:
$$
\boxed{C'=\frac{|\lambda_q|}{|V|}=\frac{2\pi\epsilon_0}{\ln(r_2/r_1)}.}
$$
End effects are neglected for the long cylinders, as required by the cylindrical approximation.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <electromagnetic energy density> in <vacuum> is $u_E=\epsilon_0E^2/2$. For a length $\ell_z$ of the coaxial <capacitor>, integrate over annular shells:
$$
U=\int_{r_1}^{r_2}\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\frac{V^2}{\eta^2r^2}(2\pi r\ell_z)\,dr
=\frac{\pi\epsilon_0\ell_zV^2}{\eta^2}\ln\frac{r_2}{r_1}.
$$
Since $C=C'\ell_z=2\pi\epsilon_0\ell_z/\eta$, this becomes
$$
\boxed{U=\frac12CV^2.}
$$
The integral is over the inter-cylinder <electric field>, and gives the stored <energy>, independently of which electrode is assigned zero <electric potential>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Choose $\mathbf B=B\hat{\mathbf z}$ and a signed tangential <velocity> $\mathbf v=u\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$, with $u>0$ counterclockwise viewed from $+z$. The magnetic part of the <Lorentz force> is $euB\hat{\mathbf r}$, while the electric part is $-eV\hat{\mathbf r}/(\eta r)$. The radial <acceleration> for a <circular orbit> is $-u^2/r$, regardless of the sign of $u$. Consequently
$$
-\frac{mu^2}{r}=e\left(-\frac{V}{\eta r}+uB\right),\qquad
\boxed{mu^2+eBr\,u-\frac{eV}{\eta}=0.}
$$
One valid signed-velocity convention therefore has $a=m$, $b=eBr$, $c=-eV/\ln(r_2/r_1)$. If $v$ is required to be a nonnegative <speed>, the two senses of rotation instead satisfy
$$
\boxed{mv^2\pm eBr\,v-\frac{eV}{\eta}=0,}
$$
with the plus sign for counterclockwise motion. For positive $V$, the electric <force> is inward, so it can supply <circular motion> in either direction; in one direction the magnetic <force> opposes it, while in the other it helps. A signed negative root must not be discarded as an unphysical <speed>; it denotes the opposite orbit direction.

==== Solution

With $V=+20\,\mathrm V$ in the outer-minus-inner convention, $\eta=\ln1.5=0.405465$ and the radial <electric field> at the orbit is $E_r=-986.52\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}$. The signed roots are
$$
u_\pm=\frac{-eBr\pm\sqrt{e^2B^2r^2+4meV/\eta}}{2m}.
$$
Using the <proton> <mass> and charge gives
$$
\boxed{u_+=4.88\times10^4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},\qquad
u_-=-9.67\times10^4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The <speeds> are therefore $48.8$ and $96.7\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, in opposite senses of rotation. Both are nonrelativistic. The slower counterclockwise orbit has an outward magnetic <force>; the faster clockwise orbit has an inward magnetic <force>. Reversing $\mathbf B$ interchanges the senses, while reversing the electrode polarity changes the quadratic and may eliminate real orbits; its sign must therefore be specified.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For a current element $I\,d\boldsymbol\ell$, the <Biot-Savart law> is
$$
d\mathbf B=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\frac{d\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf R}{R^3}.
$$
Take a straight wire along $z$, extending from $-a$ to $a$, and observe its <magnetic field> at $(s,0,0)$. All contributions point in $+\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$ for positive $I$ along $+z$, by the <right-hand rule>. Their magnitude integrates to
$$
B_\phi=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\int_{-a}^{a}\frac{s\,dz}{(s^2+z^2)^{3/2}}
=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\left[\frac{z}{s\sqrt{s^2+z^2}}\right]_{-a}^{a}
=\boxed{\frac{\mu_0Ia}{2\pi s\sqrt{s^2+a^2}}}.
$$
For $a/s\to\infty$ this becomes $\mu_0I/(2\pi s)$, so $\oint\mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=2\pi sB_\phi=\mu_0I$, exactly as <Ampère's law> requires for an infinite straight wire.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Each long wire lies in the other's <magnetic field>, $B=\mu_0I/(2\pi d)$. The magnetic <Lorentz force> per unit length has magnitude
$$
\boxed{F'=\frac{\mu_0I^2}{2\pi d},\qquad\text{opposite currents repel}.}
$$
For the two-wire circuit's <self-inductance>, a nonzero wire radius is essential. Introduce a radius $a_w\ll d$. Between the wires their <magnetic fields> add. The external <magnetic flux linkage> per unit length through the connecting ribbon is, to leading order in $a_w/d$,
$$
\Phi'=\int_{a_w}^{d-a_w}\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi}\left(\frac1x+\frac1{d-x}\right)dx
=\frac{\mu_0I}{\pi}\ln\frac{d-a_w}{a_w}.
$$
It follows that the leading thin-wire loop <self-inductance> per unit length is
$$
\boxed{L'_{\rm ext}\simeq\frac{\mu_0}{\pi}\ln\frac d{a_w}.}
$$
It is the loop value for the pair, not the contribution of just one conductor. Corrections from the wire interior depend on the current distribution: for nonmagnetic round wires with uniform direct <electric current>, the pair contributes another $\mu_0/(4\pi)$; for a surface current the internal contribution is zero. The leading logarithm is common to both models.

==== Solution

Put the three wires at $(x,y)=(-d,0),(0,0),(d,0)$ with $d=1\,\mathrm{cm}$ and each current along $+z$, out of the page. Adding their <Biot-Savart law> <magnetic fields> gives
$$
\mathbf B(x,y)=\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi}\sum_{j=-1}^{1}
\frac{-y\hat{\mathbf x}+(x-jd)\hat{\mathbf y}}{(x-jd)^2+y^2}.
$$
For $y\ne0$, all three $x$-components have the same sign, so they cannot cancel. On $y=0$, a zero of the remaining component satisfies
$$
\frac1{x+d}+\frac1x+\frac1{x-d}
=\frac{3x^2-d^2}{x(x^2-d^2)}=0.
$$
Excluding the wire positions themselves, the two and only two finite zeros are
$$
\boxed{(x,y)=\left(\pm\frac d{\sqrt3},0\right)
=\left(\pm0.577\,\mathrm{cm},0\right).}
$$
The <magnetic field lines> circulate counterclockwise about each current near that wire. Far away they circle the combined total current $3I$. The two nulls separate the nearby circulation regions in the original <magnetic field line> plot below.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For a displacement $(0,y)$ of the middle wire, the horizontal attractions towards the two fixed outer wires cancel. Each attraction has magnitude $\mu_0I^2/(2\pi\sqrt{d^2+y^2})$ per unit length. Projecting them in the $y$ direction gives
$$
\mu_m\ddot y=-\frac{\mu_0I^2}{\pi}\frac{y}{d^2+y^2}
\simeq-\frac{\mu_0I^2}{\pi d^2}y\qquad(|y|\ll d).
$$
Neglecting dissipative effects and other transverse <forces>, this is <simple harmonic motion>. Release from rest at $y_0$ gives
$$
\boxed{y(t)=y_0\cos\Omega t,\qquad
\Omega=\sqrt{\frac{\mu_0I^2}{\pi\mu_m d^2}}=2.00\,\mathrm{s^{-1}},\qquad
T=\frac{2\pi}{\Omega}=3.14\,\mathrm s.}
$$
The inverse-angular-frequency timescale is $\Omega^{-1}=0.500\,\mathrm s$; it differs from the oscillation period by $2\pi$. The assumed displacement plane matters: small displacements towards one outer wire along $x$ are unstable, rather than oscillatory.

This result concerns the specified magnetic motion with gravity absent from, or compensated in, the $y$ direction. The source diagram fixes coordinate directions, not their orientation relative to gravity. If $y$ is physically vertical with upward positive and the wire is unsupported, add $-\mu_m g$ to the <force> per unit length. The maximum restoring magnetic <force> is only $\mu_0I^2/(2\pi d)=2.0\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$, compared with weight $9.8\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$; that different setup would fall and could not oscillate about the original plane.

==== Solution

In <Fraunhofer diffraction>, the source and observation geometry make the incident and outgoing <wavefronts> effectively planar across the <aperture>. For direct propagation over distance $z$, the quadratic variation in propagation <phase> across a slit of width $w$ must be negligible, approximately $w^2/(\lambda z)\ll1$. The far-field angular <amplitude> is then the <Fourier transform> of the <aperture>'s complex illumination. A converging <lens> also produces this angular distribution in its rear focal plane for collimated illumination, without requiring a physically enormous observation distance. The approximation concerns the aperture-dependent <phase>, not the absence of <diffraction>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Let the slit run over $-w/2<x<w/2$ and let its coherent illumination have constant complex <amplitude> $A_0$. In a direction making angle $\theta$ to the normal, the relative propagation <phase> from position $x$ is $-kx\sin\theta$, with $k=2\pi/\lambda$. Summing the secondary <amplitudes> gives
$$
\mathcal A(\theta)\propto A_0\int_{-w/2}^{w/2}e^{-ikx\sin\theta}\,dx
=A_0w\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta},\qquad
\beta=\frac{\pi w\sin\theta}{\lambda}.
$$
The <intensity> is proportional to the squared modulus, hence within the usual scalar <Fraunhofer diffraction> approximation
$$
\boxed{I(\theta)=I(0)\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2.}
$$
The central value is defined by the continuous limit $\sin\beta/\beta\to1$. The nonzero minima satisfy $w\sin\theta=m\lambda$ for integer $m\ne0$. This expression neglects the slowly varying obliquity factor and uses a uniform slit; it describes the narrow-angle <diffraction> pattern for $w\gg\lambda$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The <Rayleigh criterion> declares two equal incoherent point images just resolved when the central maximum of one lies at the first minimum of the other's <point spread function>. For a single rectangular slit, the first minimum occurs at $w\sin\theta_1=\lambda$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\Delta\theta_R=\sin^{-1}(\lambda/w)\simeq\lambda/w.}
$$
This is a resolution in the direction across the slit width, and the last approximation requires $\lambda\ll w$. The central maximum's full width between its two first minima is approximately $2\lambda/w$; it is not the <Rayleigh criterion>'s separation. Two coherent sources can interfere with one another, so a resolution statement for them also needs their relative <phase>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

A feature of transverse size $s=0.03\,\mathrm m$ at height $H=2.0\times10^5\,\mathrm m$ subtends
$$
\Delta\theta\simeq\frac{s}{H}=1.5\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{rad}.
$$
Choose a representative visible <wavelength> $\lambda=550\,\mathrm{nm}$. For an unobstructed circular mirror, the first <Airy disk> minimum gives $\Delta\theta_R=1.22\lambda/D$, so
$$
\boxed{D\simeq\frac{1.22\lambda H}{s}=4.47\,\mathrm m\quad\text{(about a 4–5 m mirror)}.}
$$
Using the single-slit scaling alone would give $D\sim\lambda H/s=3.67\,\mathrm m$; it gives the same metre-scale estimate but omits the circular-aperture factor. Choosing visible <wavelengths> between $400$ and $700\,\mathrm{nm}$ changes the circular-aperture estimate to roughly $3.3$–$5.7\,\mathrm m$. These are diffraction-only estimates, not a claim that resolving one stroke guarantees reading arbitrary text with limited contrast or <noise>.

=== e
{scope}

==== Solution

At <focal length> $f=80\,\mathrm m$, the stroke maps to a focal-plane width $w_i=fs/H=12\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. A practical estimate with two detector samples across that stroke is
$$
\boxed{p\lesssim\frac{fs}{2H}=6.0\,\mathrm{\mu m},\qquad
N\gtrsim\frac{f(500\,\mathrm m)/H}{p}\simeq3.33\times10^4\ \text{pixels across}.}
$$
The required sensor extent in that direction is $f(500\,\mathrm m)/H=0.20\,\mathrm m$. One $12\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ pixel per stroke would give about $1.67\times10^4$ pixels across, but cannot reliably encode a stroke's position and contrast as the image moves relative to the pixel grid.

For a precise interpretation of “fully resolve” as sampling the entire diffraction-limited incoherent image, use the optical intensity-transfer cutoff $\nu_c=D/(\lambda f)$. The <Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem> requires $1/(2p)\ge\nu_c$. With the circular mirror chosen in 10d,
$$
\boxed{p\le\frac{\lambda f}{2D}=\frac{fs}{2(1.22)H}=4.92\,\mathrm{\mu m},\qquad
N\ge4.07\times10^4.}
$$
This stricter value retains the full circular-aperture spatial <bandwidth>; the $6\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ and $3.3\times10^4$ estimates use two samples per specified feature instead. An engineering design would provide further sampling and contrast margin. The distinction states the sampling convention rather than treating the order-of-magnitude estimates as contradictory.

==== Solution

For a long air-cored <solenoid>, <Ampère's law> applied to a rectangular loop running inside and outside the winding gives $B\ell\simeq\mu_0NI$, with the external <magnetic field> negligible away from the ends. Hence $B=\mu_0nI$ along the winding axis, where $n=N/\ell$. Each turn links <magnetic flux> $BA$ with $A=\pi a^2$, so the total <magnetic flux linkage> is $NBA=LI$. It follows that
$$
\boxed{B=\mu_0\frac N\ell I,\qquad
L=\mu_0\frac{N^2\pi a^2}{\ell}=\mu_0n^2\pi a^2\ell.}
$$
Here $n=2000\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$, $a=0.010\,\mathrm m$ and $\ell=0.50\,\mathrm m$, so $N=1000$ and
$$
\boxed{L=7.90\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm H=0.790\,\mathrm{mH}.}
$$
The value uses the stated long-solenoid approximation and ignores end corrections and winding resistance.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The original diagram places $R_2$ in parallel with the <inductor>; $R_1$ connects their common node to the source only when the switch is in position 2. Let $v(t)$ be their shared <voltage> and $i_L(t)$ the downward <inductor> <electric current>. Since $i_L(0^-)=0$ and a finite <voltage> cannot change it instantaneously, the <inductor> initially behaves as an open branch. Thus
$$
I_2(0^+)=\frac{V_0}{R_1+R_2}=\frac{100}{110}\,\mathrm A=0.909\,\mathrm A.
$$
At long times, an ideal <inductor> carrying constant <electric current> has $v=L\dot i_L=0$, shorting $R_2$. Therefore $I_2(\infty)=0$ and $i_L(\infty)=V_0/R_1=10\,\mathrm A$.

For the full transient, <Kirchhoff's current law> gives $(V_0-v)/R_1=i_L+v/R_2$ with $v=L\dot i_L$. Equivalently, the source seen by the <inductor> has the <Thévenin equivalent>
$$
V_{\rm th}=\frac{V_0R_2}{R_1+R_2},\qquad
R_{\rm th}=R_1\parallel R_2=\frac{R_1R_2}{R_1+R_2}.
$$
Solving $L\dot i_L+R_{\rm th}i_L=V_{\rm th}$ gives
$$
\boxed{i_L(t)=\frac{V_0}{R_1}(1-e^{-t/\tau}),\quad
I_2(t)=\frac{V_0}{R_1+R_2}e^{-t/\tau},\quad
\tau=\frac{L}{R_{\rm th}}=86.9\,\mathrm{\mu s}.}
$$
The relevant resistance is the parallel combination with the ideal <voltage source> suppressed, not $R_1+R_2$ and not $R_1$ alone.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

During switch-on, the <Joule heating> in $R_2$ has instantaneous <power> $P_2=R_2[V_0/(R_1+R_2)]^2e^{-2t/\tau}$. Integrating the finite transient gives
$$
Q_{2,\rm on}=\int_0^\infty P_2\,dt
=\frac{R_2V_0^2}{(R_1+R_2)^2}\frac\tau2
=\boxed{\frac{LV_0^2}{2R_1(R_1+R_2)}=3.59\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm J.}
$$
Although the source continues supplying <power> to $R_1$ indefinitely, $R_2$ is eventually shorted and its heat integral converges. It would be incorrect to identify this heat with the final stored <magnetic energy>, because the source and $R_1$ also participate during switch-on.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Opening the switch removes the source and $R_1$ but leaves a closed path through the <inductor> and $R_2$. Set the new time origin at switch-off. <Inductor> current continuity gives $i_L(0)=V_0/R_1=10\,\mathrm A$. Since the two parallel branches are now isolated, <Kirchhoff's current law> gives $I_2=-i_L$, and $L\dot i_L=-R_2i_L$. Thus
$$
i_L(t)=\frac{V_0}{R_1}e^{-R_2t/L},\qquad
\tau_{\rm off}=\frac L{R_2}=7.90\,\mathrm{\mu s}.
$$
All the initially stored <magnetic energy> is ultimately converted to <Joule heating> in $R_2$:
$$
\boxed{Q_{2,\rm off}=\frac12L\left(\frac{V_0}{R_1}\right)^2
=3.95\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm J.}
$$
Direct integration of $R_2i_L^2(t)$ gives the same result. The initial shared <voltage> is $v(0^+)=-R_2i_L(0)=-1000\,\mathrm V$; its reversal drives the return current. The ideal circuit permits this transient <voltage>; real switching would also be limited by stray <capacitance>, breakdown and other parasitic effects.
