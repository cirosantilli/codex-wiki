= Solution
{parent=1}

The <electric field> of a signed point <electric charge> in vacuum follows <Coulomb's law>:
$$
E(r)=\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{r}{|r|^3}.
$$
For the <conducting plane>, the <method of images> replaces each charge $Q_j$ at height $h_j$ by an <image charge> $-Q_j$ at height $-h_j$. The real and image <electric fields> have cancelling tangential components and equal normal components at the surface. Thus the <electric field at a conducting plane from axial point charges>, just above the point directly below them, is
$$
E_z(0^+)=-\frac{2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\sum_j\frac{Q_j}{h_j^2}
=\frac{60}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left(\frac1{5000^2}-\frac1{8000^2}\right).
$$
Numerically,
$$
\boxed{E(0^+)\simeq1.31\times10^4\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\,\hat z,\quad\text{upwards}.}
$$
The surface value means the air-side limit. Inside the ideal <conductor> the electrostatic <electric field> is zero; its discontinuity across the surface is supported by induced <surface charge>. Omitting the <image charges> would give half the external answer.

For steady currents in vacuum, <Ampère's circuital law> is
$$
\oint_C B\cdot d\ell=\mu_0I_{\mathrm{enc}}.
$$
Here $C$ is an oriented closed <curve>, $d\ell$ its tangent line element, and $I_{\mathrm{enc}}$ the signed <electric current> through a spanning <surface> oriented by the <right-hand rule>. A circular contour about a straight infinite wire has constant tangential <magnetic field>, so $B(r)=\mu_0I/(2\pi r)$. The <Lorentz force> on a length $L$ of the other wire is $IL\hat\ell\times B$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac FL=\frac{\mu_0I^2}{2\pi r},\qquad\text{the force is attractive}.}
$$
The <right-hand rule> makes the force on each parallel, same-direction current point toward the other wire.

= Solution
{parent=3}

The <continuity equation for electric charge> is $\nabla\cdot J=-\partial_t\rho$. Taking the <divergence> of the magnetostatic <Ampère's law> would instead require $\nabla\cdot J=0$. The <displacement current> repairs this conflict: using <Gauss's law>, $\nabla\cdot(\epsilon_0\partial_tE)=\partial_t\rho$, so the <Ampère-Maxwell equation> is
$$
\nabla\times B=\mu_0(J+\epsilon_0\partial_tE).
$$
In the <parallel-plate capacitor>, neglect <fringing fields> and take $E_z=V_0e^{-i\omega t}/d$. A circular <Ampère loop> of radius $s<a$ encloses <displacement current> $-i\omega\epsilon_0\pi s^2V_0e^{-i\omega t}/d$. Therefore the <magnetic field between circular capacitor plates> has phasor and amplitude
$$
\widetilde B_\phi(s)=-\frac{i\omega\mu_0\epsilon_0V_0s}{2d},\qquad
\boxed{B_{\mathrm{amp}}(s)=\frac{\omega\mu_0\epsilon_0|V_0|}{2d}\,s,\quad0\leq s<a.}
$$
It is zero on the axis, grows linearly with radius, and is azimuthal by the <right-hand rule>. This is a <quasistatic approximation>: in addition to $d\ll a$, the uniform-field model requires $\omega a/c\ll1$ and excludes the edge region. The answer gives the oscillation amplitude, rather than a time-independent instantaneous magnitude.

= Solution

The <intensity of an isotropic radiator> at distance $r$ is the radiated <power> divided by spherical area: $\mathcal I=P/(4\pi r^2)$. For a locally plane far-field <electromagnetic wave> in air, its mean <Poynting flux> is $\mathcal I=E_{\mathrm{rms}}^2/Z_0=E_{\mathrm{peak}}^2/(2Z_0)$, where the vacuum <wave impedance> is $Z_0\simeq376.7\ \Omega$. Thus
$$
\mathcal I=\frac{10^5}{4\pi(10^5)^2}\simeq7.96\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}},
$$
and
$$
\boxed{E_{\mathrm{rms}}\simeq1.73\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}},\qquad E_{\mathrm{peak}}\simeq2.45\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}.}
$$
Both conventions are given because “field strength” alone does not specify RMS or peak. This estimate treats the stated transmitter <power> as radiated power and ignores ground reflection and absorption.

A <grating spectrometer> places an illuminated narrow slit at the front focal plane of a <collimator>. The resulting parallel beam reaches the <diffraction grating> at known incidence, here taken normal. A rotatable telescope focuses each outgoing diffracted parallel beam onto its crosshair or detector. Align the slit with the grating rulings, focus the <collimator> at infinity, and illuminate many grooves across a suitable aperture. A narrow slit supplies sufficient <spatial coherence> and prevents slit width from blurring the angular separation; the telescope must also resolve the two images.

\Image[2018-grating-spectrometer.png]
{title=A <grating spectrometer> with a narrow source slit, <collimator>, normally illuminated <diffraction grating> and rotatable observing telescope}

The groove spacing is $d=(2000\times100)^{-1}\ \mathrm m=5.00\ \mu\mathrm m$. The <grating equation> at normal incidence is $d\sin\theta_m=m\lambda$. For <diffraction order> $m=2$,
$$
\theta_{579}=\arcsin(0.2316)\simeq13.3913^\circ,\qquad
\theta_{577}=\arcsin(0.2308)\simeq13.3442^\circ.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\Delta\theta\simeq8.22\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{rad}=0.0471^\circ=2.83\ \text{arcmin}.}
$$
The ideal <resolving power of a diffraction grating> is $|m|N$, so the <Rayleigh criterion> for this doublet requires about $N\geq145$ illuminated grooves, spanning roughly $0.73\ \mathrm{mm}$. This is an ideal lower bound; finite slit width, aberrations and detector resolution can require a larger usable aperture.

= Solution
{parent=6}

Use an ideal inverting <summing amplifier>, grounding the non-inverting input and connecting the two sources through resistances $R$ and $R/2$. A feedback resistance $R_f=2R$ gives the desired weights. <Operational-amplifier negative feedback> makes the inverting node a <virtual ground>, and negligible input current lets <Kirchhoff's current law> give
$$
\frac{V_a}{R}+\frac{V_b}{R/2}=-\frac{V_{\mathrm{out}}}{2R},\qquad
\boxed{V_{\mathrm{out}}=-2(V_a+2V_b).}
$$

\Image[2018-weighted-summing-amplifier.png]
{title=An inverting <summing amplifier> with input resistances $R,R/2$ and feedback resistance $2R$}

Replacing the feedback <resistor> by a <capacitor> $C$ makes a weighted <op amp integrator>:
$$
\boxed{\frac{dV_{\mathrm{out}}}{dt}=-\frac{V_a+2V_b}{RC},\qquad
V_{\mathrm{out}}(t)=V_{\mathrm{out}}(t_0)-\frac1{RC}\int_{t_0}^t[V_a(u)+2V_b(u)]\,du.}
$$
The initial capacitor charge determines the integration constant. A nonzero DC weighted input produces a ramp; a real <operational amplifier> eventually reaches its output-voltage limit. A parallel feedback resistance can limit drift while preserving integration over an appropriate frequency range.

= Solution
{parent=7}

The integral and differential forms of <Gauss's law> in vacuum are
$$
\oint_{\partial V}E\cdot n\,dA=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{enc}}}{\epsilon_0},\qquad
\nabla\cdot E=\frac\rho{\epsilon_0}.
$$
For an isolated spherical <conductor>, symmetry gives $E(r)=Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)$ outside and zero inside. Taking <electric potential> zero at infinity, $V(R)=\int_R^\infty E(r)\,dr=Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0R)$. Thus the <capacitance of an isolated conducting sphere> is
$$
\boxed{C=4\pi\epsilon_0R.}
$$
Charging from zero gives the <electrostatic potential energy>
$$
\boxed{W=\int_0^Q\frac qC\,dq=\frac{Q^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0R}=\frac12CV^2.}
$$
This is also the integral of <electric field energy density> $\epsilon_0E^2/2$ outside the sphere. The later insertion into a <coaxial cylindrical capacitor> conserves its <electric charge>, not its original isolated <electric potential>.

{parent=7}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}

Initially the isolated sphere has $Q=4\pi\epsilon_0RV$. After insertion without contact, its charge remains $Q$. In the long-cylinder approximation, with the sphere far from the openings, neglect electric flux escaping through the ends. A <Gauss surface> running through the shielding metal and closed by end sections of negligible flux then encloses zero net charge. Its cavity wall therefore carries $-Q$. Since that cylinder is isolated and initially neutral, its outside wall carries $+Q$. The grounded outer cylinder supplies charge $-Q$ on its inside wall, terminating the annular <electric field lines>.

\Image[2018-coaxial-induced-charges.png]
{title=Transverse schematic of a <charged sphere inside a neutral coaxial capacitor>: induced charges and <electric field lines>, with no field inside the conducting walls}

For positive $V$, the cavity <electric field lines> run from the sphere to the inner wall, while annular <electric field lines> run from the outside of the inner conductor to the grounded outer conductor. Negative $V$ reverses their directions. The transverse picture is schematic: exact cavity fields depend on the sphere's position and the ends. The following annular formulas neglect end effects and place the sphere well inside the long cylindrical assembly.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For $b<r<a$, a coaxial <Gauss surface> of length $l$ encloses net charge $Q$: the sphere and the cavity-wall charge cancel, leaving $+Q$ on the outside of the inner conductor. In the long-cylinder approximation,
$$
E_r(r)\,2\pi rl=\frac Q{\epsilon_0},\qquad
\boxed{E_r(r)=\frac{Q}{2\pi\epsilon_0lr}=\frac{2RV}{lr}.}
$$
The <electric field> points radially outward for $V>0$. End effects and axial nonuniformity near the openings are excluded by the ideal <coaxial cylindrical capacitor> approximation.

{parent=7}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

The grounded outer cylinder fixes $\Phi(a)=0$. Integrating the annular <electric field> inward gives
$$
\boxed{\Phi(b)=\int_b^aE_r(r)\,dr=\frac{2RV}{l}\log\frac ab.}
$$
Equivalently, the <coaxial cylindrical capacitor> has $C_{\mathrm{cyl}}=2\pi\epsilon_0l/\log(a/b)$ and carries charge $Q=4\pi\epsilon_0RV$, so $\Phi(b)=Q/C_{\mathrm{cyl}}$. The original sphere potential $V$ is used to infer its conserved charge; it is not the final potential of the sphere or of the inner cylinder.

= Solution
{parent=8}

The <Biot-Savart law> is
$$
B(r)=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\int\frac{d\ell'\times(r-r')}{|r-r'|^3}.
$$
Here $r'$ is the current-element position, $d\ell'$ follows the <electric current>, and $r$ is the observation point. For a circular <current loop>, transverse components cancel on the axis. Each element contributes axial factor $a/\sqrt{a^2+x^2}$ in addition to the inverse-square distance. Integrating around length $2\pi a$ gives the <on-axis magnetic field of a circular current loop>
$$
\boxed{B_{\mathrm{axis}}(x)=\frac{\mu_0Ia^2}{2(a^2+x^2)^{3/2}},}
$$
with axial direction set by the <right-hand rule>.

For a finite straight wire parametrized by $z\in[-b,b]$, at perpendicular distance $a$, the <Biot-Savart law> gives
$$
B=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\int_{-b}^{b}\frac{a\,dz}{(a^2+z^2)^{3/2}}
=\boxed{\frac{\mu_0Ib}{2\pi a\sqrt{a^2+b^2}}.}
$$
The direction is azimuthal about the current axis by the <right-hand rule>. In the hairpin, the semicircle contributes $\mu_0I/(4a)$ and each semi-infinite straight segment contributes $\mu_0I/(4\pi a)$. For the indicated current, all three contributions point **out of the page**. Thus the <magnetic field at the center of a hairpin wire> is
$$
\boxed{B(P)=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi a}(\pi+2),\quad\text{out of the page}.}
$$

For this question use <phasors> with time factor $e^{i\omega t}$, so an <electrical impedance> is $Z=\widetilde V/\widetilde I$. The same <electric current> flows through all components of a <series circuit>, and their <voltages> add by <Kirchhoff's voltage law>. Hence the total series <electrical impedance> is
$$
\boxed{Z_{\mathrm{series}}=R+i\left(\omega L-\frac1{\omega C}\right).}
$$
The individual steady sinusoidal currents and their phase relations are derived in the following three parts. The diagram normalizes each current by its own amplitude, so it compares <phase differences> without mixing unlike physical units.

= Solution
{parent=i}

For a <resistor>, <Ohm's law> gives
$$
\boxed{Z_R=R,\qquad I_R(t)=\frac{V_0}{R}\cos\omega t.}
$$
The <electric current> and <voltage> are **in phase**.

{parent=9}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=ii}

For a <capacitor>, $I_C=C\,dV/dt$, so
$$
\boxed{Z_C=\frac1{i\omega C},\qquad I_C(t)=-\omega CV_0\sin\omega t=\omega CV_0\cos(\omega t+\pi/2).}
$$
The <electric current> **leads the voltage by $\pi/2$**.

= iii
{parent=9}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

For an <inductor>, $V=L\,dI_L/dt$. In the steady AC solution with no added constant <electric current>,
$$
\boxed{Z_L=i\omega L,\qquad I_L(t)=\frac{V_0}{\omega L}\sin\omega t=\frac{V_0}{\omega L}\cos(\omega t-\pi/2).}
$$
The <electric current> **lags the voltage by $\pi/2$**. An ideal <inductor> can also sustain an arbitrary DC current, fixed by initial conditions; it is excluded from this steady sinusoidal comparison.

= Solution
{parent=a}

The output is measured across the <capacitor>. The <voltage divider> gives the <RC low-pass filter> transfer function
$$
H(i\omega)=\frac{Z_C}{R+Z_C}=\frac1{1+i\omega RC}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{V_{\mathrm{out}}(t)=\frac{V_0}{\sqrt{1+(\omega RC)^2}}\cos\bigl(\omega t-\arctan(\omega RC)\bigr).}
$$
The amplitude ratio tends to $1$ and the <phase shift> to zero as $\omega\to0$. For $\omega\to\infty$, the amplitude ratio is asymptotic to $1/(\omega RC)$ and the <phase shift> tends to $-\pi/2$.

\Image[2018-rc-frequency-and-square-response.png]

= Solution
{parent=b}

The half-power amplitude condition for the <RC low-pass filter> gives $1+(\omega RC)^2=2$. Since <angular frequency> is positive,
$$
\boxed{\omega_c=\frac1{RC}.}
$$
At this <cutoff angular frequency>, the output amplitude is smaller by $1/\sqrt2$ and the <phase shift> is $-\pi/4$.

= Solution
{parent=c}

The <RC low-pass filter> satisfies $RC\,\dot V_{\mathrm{out}}+V_{\mathrm{out}}=V$ for $t>0$. With zero initial capacitor voltage, the <first-order linear differential equation> gives
$$
\boxed{V_{\mathrm{out}}(t)=V\bigl(1-e^{-t/(RC)}\bigr),\quad t\geq0.}
$$
The output is continuous at switching and approaches $V$ with <time constant> $RC$.

= Solution
{parent=d}

For explicit sketches take a symmetric, 50% <duty cycle> square wave with levels $\pm V_0$. The source does not specify levels or duty cycle; a unipolar 50% wave follows by adding its mean and rescaling. Let $\tau=RC$ and $M$ be the positive output extremum in the periodic steady state. The <square-wave response of an RC low-pass filter> has
$$
M=V_0\tanh\frac{T}{4\tau},\qquad
V_{\mathrm{out}}(t)=\begin{cases}
V_0-(V_0+M)e^{-t/\tau},&0\leq t<T/2,\\
-V_0+(V_0+M)e^{-(t-T/2)/\tau},&T/2\leq t<T,
\end{cases}
$$
extended periodically. The extremum formula follows by imposing $V_{\mathrm{out}}(0)=-M$ and $V_{\mathrm{out}}(T/2)=M$. It makes the three regimes below quantitative, including their finite amplitudes.

When $\omega RC\ll1$, each half-cycle lasts many <time constants>, so $M\simeq V_0$. The output nearly follows the two input levels, with **exponentially rounded transitions** lasting order $RC$. It remains continuous rather than making ideal vertical jumps.

= Solution
{parent=ii}

When $\omega RC\gg1$, the <capacitor> voltage changes little during each half-cycle. Thus $\dot V_{\mathrm{out}}\simeq V_{\mathrm{in}}/(RC)$, giving an approximately triangular output. Its extrema are
$$
\boxed{M\simeq\frac{V_0T}{4RC}=\frac{\pi V_0}{2\omega RC}.}
$$
The finite but small triangle is centered on zero for the chosen zero-mean input. A nonzero input mean shifts the output mean by the same amount.

= Solution

The <Huygens-Fresnel principle> constructs a later wavefront as the envelope of secondary wavelets emitted from the earlier one. At a planar interface, take a time interval during which one point on the incoming front travels distance $v_1\Delta t$ before reaching the interface, while the point that reached it first generates a wavelet of radius $v_2\Delta t$. The two right triangles share the interface segment, giving $\sin i/\sin r=v_1/v_2$. Since the <refractive index> is $n=c/v$, this yields <Snell's law>:
$$
\boxed{n_1\sin i=n_2\sin r.}
$$
The angles are measured from the interface <normal>. This geometric construction assumes homogeneous media and uses the propagation speeds at the given <frequency>.

{parent=10}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=i}

Let $r=\arcsin(\sin\theta/n)$ be the angle inside the prism relative to the inward normal of face A. For the entry position shown, below the midpoint of A, and $0<\theta<90^\circ$, the transmitted ray first reaches face B. Its incidence angle there is $i_B=|\alpha-r|$. If $n\sin i_B<1$, a transmitted ray leaves B according to <Snell's law>. If $n\sin i_B>1$, it undergoes <total internal reflection> at B and then reaches C. At the equality, transmission is grazing.

On the reflected route through this equilateral prism, the incidence angle at C is $r$, so $n\sin r=\sin\theta<1$ permits emergence from C. The <one-reflection path through an equilateral prism> is drawn alongside the direct-transmission route.

\Image[2018-prism-paths.png]
{title=Principal transmitted ray paths through an <optical prism>: <total internal reflection> at B followed by exit at C, or direct refraction out of B}

Real interfaces also produce partial <Fresnel reflection> below the critical angle; these additional weaker rays are omitted from the principal-path sketch. Which face a ray first reaches also depends on its entrance position, so the route statement uses the entrance geometry illustrated in the source.

= Solution

For $n=1.5$, the <critical angle> is $i_c=\arcsin(1/1.5)\simeq41.8103^\circ$. Here $0\leq r<i_c<\alpha=60^\circ$, so the condition at B is $\alpha-r>i_c$. At the threshold,
$$
r_*=60^\circ-i_c\simeq18.1897^\circ,\qquad
\theta_*=\arcsin(1.5\sin r_*)\simeq27.9205^\circ.
$$
Thus the requested positive-angle interval for strict <total internal reflection> is
$$
\boxed{0<\theta<27.9205^\circ.}
$$
At the upper equality the transmitted ray is grazing; normal incidence $\theta=0$ also gives <total internal reflection> at B if the endpoint is included. The distinction follows the question's positive-angle convention.

{parent=10}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iii}

Take the positive horizontal direction along B. The initial direction is $(\alpha-90^\circ)-\theta$. Reflection at the horizontal face B reverses the vertical direction. At C, symmetry of the equilateral geometry gives internal incidence $r$, so <Snell's law> makes the emerging angle to C's normal equal to $\theta$. Its direction is therefore $(90^\circ-\alpha)+\theta$. Subtracting the incoming direction gives
$$
\boxed{\delta=180^\circ-2\alpha+2\theta=60^\circ+2\theta.}
$$
This is the smaller angle between the incident and emergent propagation directions in the requested reflection interval. The cancellation of $n$ occurs because the entry and exit <refraction angles> have equal magnitudes; <refractive index> still determines whether the reflected route exists and where it exits.

= iv
{parent=10}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=iv}

Assume <normal dispersion>, so shorter visible wavelengths have larger <refractive indices>. At fixed external angle $\theta$, the relevant criticality parameter is
$$
n\sin i_B=\sin\alpha\sqrt{n^2-\sin^2\theta}-\cos\alpha\sin\theta,
$$
which increases with $n$ on this route. Thus **shorter-wavelength blue light is totally reflected at B and reaches C, while longer-wavelength red light can transmit through B**; green lies at the critical threshold. The prism separates the routes by wavelength near the green cutoff.

The totally reflected rays emerging from C have the same external deviation $180^\circ-2\alpha+2\theta$ despite their different <refractive indices>; their exit positions can differ. Red light also has a partially reflected component by the <Fresnel equations>, so C is not a mathematically perfect colour cutoff. The observation concerns total versus partial reflection, with the transmitted B rays spread by wavelength. Anomalous dispersion, if present, would require the actual $n(\lambda)$ instead of the normal ordering assumed here.
