== 1
{scope}

=== Solution

In a source-free <vacuum>, the <Maxwell equations> in <SI> are
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=0,\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t},
$$
where $\epsilon_0$ and $\mu_0$ are the <vacuum permittivity> and <vacuum permeability>. Take the <curl> of the last equation, substitute <Faraday's law of induction>, and use the <curl of the curl identity>:
$$
\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf B)
=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf B)-\nabla^2\mathbf B
=-\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\mathbf B}{\partial t^2}.
$$
The zero <divergence> therefore gives the <wave equation>
$$
\boxed{\nabla^2\mathbf B=\frac1{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\mathbf B}{\partial t^2},\qquad c=\frac1{\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}}.}
$$
For a propagating <plane wave>, write $\mathbf B=\operatorname{Re}[\mathbf B_0e^{i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t)}]$. The <wave equation> gives $\omega^2=c^2|\mathbf k|^2$, and <Gauss's law for magnetism> gives $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf B_0=0$. Thus the oscillating <magnetic field> is a <transverse wave>: it is perpendicular to the propagation <wavevector>. A static background <magnetic field>, if present, is separate from this propagating component.

=== a

==== Solution

The central <point charge> and spherical boundaries give a <spherically symmetric charge distribution>. A <conductor in electrostatic equilibrium> has zero <electric field> within its material. Consequently a spherical <Gaussian surface> inside the metal encloses zero net <electric charge>, so the inner surface carries $-q$. The shell's prescribed total <electric charge> then leaves $-2q$ on its outer surface. <Gauss's law> gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(r)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^2}\,\hat{\mathbf r},&0<r<r_1,\\[4pt]
\mathbf0,&r_1<r<r_2,\\[4pt]
-\dfrac{2q}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^2}\,\hat{\mathbf r},&r>r_2.
\end{cases}}
$$
The <electric field> is singular at the ideal central <point charge>. At each charged surface the normal component has distinct one-sided limits, as required by the <electrostatic boundary conditions at a conductor>. Spherical symmetry makes the outer <surface charge density> uniform:
$$
\boxed{Q_{\rm inner}=-q,\qquad \sigma_{\rm outer}=-\frac{2q}{4\pi r_2^2}.}
$$

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For the <uniform space charge in a grounded spherical cavity>, <grounding> sets the metal's <electric potential> to zero. With no external sources and zero <electric potential> at infinity, the exterior <electric potential> is also zero by the <uniqueness theorem for the electrostatic potential>. The original shell <electric charge> is no longer fixed: <electric charge> can flow to or from earth.

Inside the cavity, <Gauss's law> gives $\mathbf E=\rho r\hat{\mathbf r}/(3\epsilon_0)$. Integrating the <electric field> inward from the zero boundary <electric potential> gives
$$
V(r)=\int_r^{r_1}\frac{\rho s}{3\epsilon_0}\,ds
=\frac{\rho(r_1^2-r^2)}{6\epsilon_0},\qquad
\boxed{V(0)=\frac{\rho r_1^2}{6\epsilon_0}.}
$$
The inner surface carries $-Q_\rho$, where $Q_\rho=4\pi\rho r_1^3/3$, and the outer surface carries zero net <electric charge>. Only the cavity contributes to the final <electrostatic energy>:
$$
U=\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int |\mathbf E|^2\,d^3r
=\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}4\pi\int_0^{r_1}\left(\frac{\rho r}{3\epsilon_0}\right)^2r^2\,dr,
\qquad
\boxed{U=\frac{2\pi\rho^2r_1^5}{45\epsilon_0}
=\frac{Q_\rho^2}{40\pi\epsilon_0r_1}.}
$$
The same result follows from $U=\tfrac12\int\rho V\,d^3r$; the conductor's <surface charge> contributes zero because its <electric potential> is zero. This is the final stored <electrostatic energy>, not the change from the earlier central-charge configuration, whose ideal <point charge> has divergent self-energy.

{scope}

=== Solution

Use the <parallel-plate capacitor> approximation, neglecting the <fringing field of a capacitor>. With plate area $A=\pi(0.10\,\mathrm m)^2$, the initial <capacitance> and opposite plate <electric charges> are
$$
C_i=\frac{\epsilon_0A}{0.010\,\mathrm m}=2.78163\times10^{-11}\,\mathrm F,
\qquad
\boxed{Q_\pm=\pm C_i(100\,\mathrm V)=\pm2.78163\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm C.}
$$
After the <electric battery> is disconnected, the isolated plate <electric charges> stay fixed. Doubling the separation halves the <capacitance>. The quasistatic pulling work is the increase of <electrostatic energy>:
$$
W=\frac{Q^2}{2C_f}-\frac{Q^2}{2C_i}
=\frac12C_i(100\,\mathrm V)^2,
\qquad
\boxed{W=1.39081\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm J.}
$$
The final <electric potential difference> is therefore
$$
\boxed{\Delta V_f=\frac Q{C_f}=200\,\mathrm V.}
$$
An <electron> released at rest gains <kinetic energy> $e\Delta V_f$, where $e$ is the positive <elementary charge>. Neglecting radiation and the negligible perturbation caused by this one <electron>, <conservation of energy> gives
$$
\frac12m_ev^2=e\Delta V_f,
\qquad
\boxed{v=\sqrt{\frac{2e(200\,\mathrm V)}{m_e}}
=8.39\times10^6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
This is $0.0280c$, so the nonrelativistic <kinetic energy> is adequate. The circular plates have finite radii; the numerical results are leading <parallel-plate capacitor> values rather than exact finite-plate <capacitances>.

== 4
{scope}

=== Solution

The <current density> is the moving <charge density> times its <velocity>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf J=\rho\mathbf v=\rho v_0\hat{\mathbf z}\quad(r<R),\qquad \mathbf J=\mathbf0\quad(r>R).}
$$
For a cylindrical <Gaussian surface> of radius $r<R$ and length $\ell$, cylindrical symmetry makes the <electric field> radial. <Gauss's law> gives $E(2\pi r\ell)=\rho\pi r^2\ell/\epsilon_0$. A circular <Ampère's circuital law> contour encloses <electric current> $I(r)=\rho v_0\pi r^2$, giving $B(2\pi r)=\mu_0I(r)$. Hence
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(r)=\frac{\rho r}{2\epsilon_0}\hat{\mathbf r},\qquad
\mathbf B(r)=\frac{\mu_0\rho v_0r}{2}\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}
=\mu_0\epsilon_0\,\mathbf v\times\mathbf E(r),\quad r<R.}
$$
The <right-hand rule> fixes the positive azimuthal direction of the <magnetic field> for positive <electric charge> moving along $+z$. Both the <electric field> and the <magnetic field> vanish on the axis. These are the steady long-cylinder <electric field> and <magnetic field>; end effects and changes of the prescribed beam distribution are excluded.

=== Solution

For normal incidence on a <diffraction grating>, adjacent grooves contribute a path difference $d\sin\theta$. <Constructive interference> occurs when the <grating equation> $d\sin\theta=m\lambda$ holds. Thus the groove spacing is
$$
d=\frac{3(589\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm m)}{\sin32^\circ}
=3.334\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm m,
\qquad
\boxed{N=\frac{10^{-3}\,\mathrm m}{d}=299.9\ \text{lines per mm}\simeq300\ \text{lines per mm}.}
$$
An allowed propagating <diffraction order> must have $|\sin\theta|\leq1$. Therefore
$$
|m|\leq\frac d\lambda=\frac3{\sin32^\circ}=5.6615,
\qquad
\boxed{|m|_{\max}=5.}
$$
The fifth <diffraction order> is the highest geometrically permitted order for this <wavelength>. Its actual brightness depends on groove shape and the <single-slit diffraction> envelope, which the source does not specify; geometric permission alone does not guarantee a bright observable maximum.

{scope}

=== Solution

The original diagram has an ideal $5\,\mathrm V$ <electric battery> feeding series <resistors> of $8\,\Omega$ above the tapping point and $2\,\Omega$ below it. The long battery bar is positive, so terminal $A$ is above terminal $B$ in <electric potential>. With an <open circuit> at the output, the internal <electric current> is $5/(8+2)=0.5\,\mathrm A$. The <voltage divider> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E_0=V_A-V_B=(0.5\,\mathrm A)(2\,\Omega)=1.0\,\mathrm V.}
$$
For the <Thévenin resistance>, suppress the ideal voltage source by replacing it with a <short circuit>. The two <resistors> are then in parallel as seen from $A,B$:
$$
\boxed{R_{\rm Th}=\frac{8\times2}{8+2}\,\Omega=1.6\,\Omega.}
$$
By <Thévenin's theorem>, the terminal <voltage> under an outward load <electric current> $I$ is $V_A-V_B=\mathcal E_0-IR_{\rm Th}$. Consequently
$$
\boxed{I_{\rm sc}=\frac{\mathcal E_0}{R_{\rm Th}}=0.625\,\mathrm A,}
$$
flowing through an external <short circuit> from $A$ to $B$. Directly shorting the original terminals bypasses the $2\,\Omega$ <resistor> and leaves $5\,\mathrm V$ across $8\,\Omega$, independently confirming the result.

==== Solution

The integral and differential forms of <Gauss's law> in <electrostatics> are
$$
\boxed{\oint_{\partial\mathcal V}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf A
=\frac{Q_{\rm enc}}{\epsilon_0},\qquad
\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}.}
$$
Here $\partial\mathcal V$ is a closed surface bounding a volume $\mathcal V$, $d\mathbf A=\hat{\mathbf n}\,dA$ is its outward area <vector>, $\mathbf E$ is the <electric field>, $\rho$ is total volume <charge density>, and $Q_{\rm enc}=\int_{\mathcal V}\rho\,d^3r$ is enclosed <electric charge>. The <divergence theorem> converts one form to the other. For ideal sheets or <point charges>, interpret the differential equation with their distributional <charge densities>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Write the uniform <surface charge density> on the <annulus> as $\sigma=Q/[\pi(b^2-a^2)]$, and let $x$ be signed distance along its axis. A ring of radius $r$ and width $dr$ contains <electric charge> $dq=2\pi\sigma r\,dr$. Its transverse <electric field> components cancel in pairs, and its <electric potential> is $dV=dq/[4\pi\epsilon_0\sqrt{x^2+r^2}]$. Setting $V(\infty)=0$ and integrating gives the <axial field of a uniformly charged annulus>:
$$
\boxed{V(x)=\frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}\left(\sqrt{x^2+b^2}-\sqrt{x^2+a^2}\right),}
$$
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(x)=-\frac{dV}{dx}\hat{\mathbf x}
=\frac{\sigma x}{2\epsilon_0}\left(\frac1{\sqrt{x^2+a^2}}-\frac1{\sqrt{x^2+b^2}}\right)\hat{\mathbf x}.}
$$
At $x=0$, each element's <electric field> cancels that of the diametrically opposite element, so $\mathbf E=0$. There is no <surface charge> at the centre because $a>0$; the axis <electric field> is continuous there.

For positive $Q$ and $x>0$, differentiate the signed axial <electric field>:
$$
\frac{dE_x}{dx}=\frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}
\left[\frac{a^2}{(x^2+a^2)^{3/2}}-\frac{b^2}{(x^2+b^2)^{3/2}}\right].
$$
Its stationary condition is $a^{4/3}(x^2+b^2)=b^{4/3}(x^2+a^2)$. Factoring with $A=a^{2/3}$ and $B=b^{2/3}$ gives
$$
\boxed{x_{\max}^2=\frac{a^{4/3}b^{4/3}}{a^{2/3}+b^{2/3}},\qquad
x_{\max}=\frac{(ab)^{2/3}}{\sqrt{a^{2/3}+b^{2/3}}}.}
$$
The derivative starts positive, has exactly one positive zero and is negative at large $x$, so this is the positive-side maximum. For either sign of $Q$, the <electric field> magnitude has equal maxima at $x=\pm x_{\max}$. As $b\to a$, the thin-ring limit is $x_{\max}\to a/\sqrt2$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $m$ be the sliding <point charge>'s <mass>. Expanding the <axial field of a uniformly charged annulus> for $|x|\ll a,b$ gives
$$
E_x=\frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}\left(\frac1a-\frac1b\right)x+O(x^3)
=\frac{Q}{2\pi\epsilon_0ab(a+b)}x+O(x^3).
$$
Its <electric charge> is $-Q$, so its <electric force> points toward the centre. <Newton's second law> becomes
$$
m\ddot x=-\frac{Q^2}{2\pi\epsilon_0ab(a+b)}x+O(x^3).
$$
To leading order this is <simple harmonic motion>, with
$$
\boxed{\omega_{\rm osc}^2=\frac{Q^2}{2\pi\epsilon_0mab(a+b)},\qquad
T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2\pi\epsilon_0mab(a+b)}{Q^2}}.}
$$
The <mass> is an additional parameter, since the source supplies no numerical value. The constraint is essential: <stability> along the axis does not imply unconstrained three-dimensional trapping by a static <electric field>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For a steady filamentary <electric current> $I$, the <Biot-Savart law> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int
\frac{I\,d\boldsymbol\ell'\times(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|^3}.}
$$
Here $\mathbf r$ is the observation position, $\mathbf r'$ the source position, $d\boldsymbol\ell'$ points in the direction of positive <electric current>, and $\mu_0$ is the <vacuum permeability>. A circular loop has centre <magnetic field> $\mu_0I/(2r)$: the cross product has magnitude $r\,d\ell'$ and a constant axial direction, and $\int d\ell'=2\pi r$.

Introduce the constant signed <angular velocity> $\Omega$ about the positive axis. A ring's <electric charge> $dq=2\pi\sigma r\,dr$ crosses any radial cut once per rotation, so its <electric current> is $dI=\Omega\,dq/(2\pi)=\sigma\Omega r\,dr$. Adding loop contributions yields the <magnetic field of a rotating charged annulus>:
$$
\mathbf B(0)=\hat{\mathbf x}\int_a^b\frac{\mu_0}{2r}\sigma\Omega r\,dr,
\qquad
\boxed{\mathbf B(0)=\frac{\mu_0\sigma\Omega(b-a)}2\hat{\mathbf x}
=\frac{\mu_0Q\Omega}{2\pi(a+b)}\hat{\mathbf x}.}
$$
The direction follows the <right-hand rule> for positive <electric charge>; reversing either $Q$ or $\Omega$ reverses the <magnetic field>. The source supplies no rotation rate, so the answer necessarily retains $\Omega$.

=== a

==== Solution

Take $\mathbf d$ from the negative to the positive <point charge>. Their <electric dipole moment> is $\mathbf p=q\mathbf d$. Place them at $\pm\mathbf d/2$ and use the <superposition principle> for the <electric potential>:
$$
V(\mathbf s)=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\left(\frac1{|\mathbf s-\mathbf d/2|}-\frac1{|\mathbf s+\mathbf d/2|}\right).
$$
For $s\gg d$, the first-order <Taylor expansion> of the reciprocal distances gives
$$
\boxed{V(s,\theta)=\frac{\mathbf p\cdot\hat{\mathbf s}}{4\pi\epsilon_0s^2}
=\frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0s^2}}
$$
to leading <electric dipole> order. In <spherical coordinates> aligned with $\mathbf p$, the <gradient> gives $E_s=-\partial_sV$, $E_\theta=-s^{-1}\partial_\theta V$ and $E_\phi=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(s,\theta)=\frac{p}{4\pi\epsilon_0s^3}
\left(2\cos\theta\,\hat{\mathbf s}+\sin\theta\,\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}\right).}
$$
Equivalently, the <electric dipole field> is $[3(\mathbf p\cdot\hat{\mathbf s})\hat{\mathbf s}-\mathbf p]/(4\pi\epsilon_0s^3)$. The omitted terms vanish relative to these leading expressions as $d/s\to0$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <method of images> replaces a conductor's induced <surface charge> by suitable fictitious <image charges> located outside the physical region in which the <electric field> is sought. Choose them so that the combined <electric potential> satisfies the specified conductor boundary values, has the required behaviour at infinity, and has the correct physical sources. The <uniqueness theorem for the electrostatic potential> then identifies this constructed <electric potential> with the physical solution in that region.

For an infinite grounded <conducting plane> at $z=0$, a <point charge> $q$ at $(x,y,z)$ with $z>0$ has an <image charge> $-q$ at $(x,y,-z)$. Distances to the pair are equal on the plane, so their <electric potentials> cancel there. This construction determines the <electric field> above the plane; below it the actual conductor has constant <electric potential> and zero <electric field>, rather than the fictitious image's continuation.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the <electric dipole above a grounded conducting plane> approximation, valid for $d\ll a$. Let $\hat{\mathbf z}$ point upward, and choose $\mathbf p=p(\sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf x}+\cos\theta\,\hat{\mathbf z})$. Reflecting the two constituent <point charges> and reversing their signs produces an image <electric dipole moment>
$$
\mathbf p'=p(-\sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf x}+\cos\theta\,\hat{\mathbf z})
$$
at height $-a$. Thus the parallel <electric dipole moment> reverses and the normal one is unchanged. At the real <electric dipole>, the separation from its image is $2a\hat{\mathbf z}$, giving the image <electric field>
$$
\mathbf E_{\rm im}=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0(2a)^3}
\left[3(\mathbf p'\cdot\hat{\mathbf z})\hat{\mathbf z}-\mathbf p'\right]
=\frac{p}{32\pi\epsilon_0a^3}
\left(\sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf x}+2\cos\theta\,\hat{\mathbf z}\right).
$$
The physical <torque> is $\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf p\times\mathbf E_{\rm im}$, so
$$
\boxed{\boldsymbol\tau=-\frac{p^2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{32\pi\epsilon_0a^3}\hat{\mathbf y}
=-\frac{p^2\sin2\theta}{64\pi\epsilon_0a^3}\hat{\mathbf y}.}
$$
For $0<\theta<\pi/2$, its direction reduces $\theta$, tending to align the <electric dipole> with the upward normal. In general it favours either normal orientation; a perfectly parallel orientation has zero <torque> but is unstable to tilting.

The conductor-response <potential energy> includes one half of the real-image interaction:
$$
U=-\frac12\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E_{\rm im}
=-\frac{p^2(1+\cos^2\theta)}{64\pi\epsilon_0a^3}.
$$
This factor accounts for the induced <surface charge> changing as the physical source is assembled. Differentiation with respect to height at fixed orientation gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F=-\frac{\partial U}{\partial a}\hat{\mathbf z}
=-\frac{3p^2(1+\cos^2\theta)}{64\pi\epsilon_0a^4}\hat{\mathbf z}.}
$$
There is no horizontal <force>, since translating the entire arrangement parallel to the plane leaves $U$ unchanged. For example, the normal and parallel orientations give respective downward <force> magnitudes $3p^2/(32\pi\epsilon_0a^4)$ and $3p^2/(64\pi\epsilon_0a^4)$.

A single physical <point charge> induces total plane <electric charge> $-q$. One can verify this by integrating its induced <surface charge density>, $-qh/[2\pi(\varrho^2+h^2)^{3/2}]$, over the plane. The two physical <electric charges> therefore induce opposite totals, giving
$$
\boxed{Q_{\rm plane}=-(+q)-(-q)=0.}
$$
The local <surface charge density> is generally nonzero. The zero total holds for the finite neutral pair as well as its ideal <electric dipole> limit.

==== Solution

<Fermat's principle> states that the travel time, or equivalently the <optical path length> $\int n\,ds$ in a stationary medium, is stationary under small variations of the path with endpoints held fixed. It need not be a global minimum.

Let a <light ray> join a point at height $h_1$ above a plane interface to a point at depth $h_2$ below it, with horizontal separation $D$. If the crossing point is horizontally $\xi$ from the first endpoint, its <optical path length> is
$$
\mathcal L(\xi)=n_1\sqrt{h_1^2+\xi^2}
+n_2\sqrt{h_2^2+(D-\xi)^2}.
$$
Setting its derivative to zero gives
$$
\frac{n_1\xi}{\sqrt{h_1^2+\xi^2}}
=\frac{n_2(D-\xi)}{\sqrt{h_2^2+(D-\xi)^2}}.
$$
The two geometric ratios are the sines of the incident and refracted <angles>, measured from the interface <normal vector>. Hence <Snell's law> is
$$
\boxed{n_1\sin\theta_1=n_2\sin\theta_2.}
$$

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <paraxial approximation> restricts <light rays> to small slopes relative to the <optical axis>, so $\sin\beta\simeq\tan\beta\simeq\beta$ and geometric lengths can be expanded to second order in height. Treat the source's <optical lens> as a <thin lens> located at $x=0$, with $|y|\ll u,|v|$ and small surface slopes.

For positive real conjugate distances $u,v$, the collapsed-plane geometric path has length
$$
S(y)=\sqrt{u^2+y^2}+\sqrt{v^2+y^2}
\simeq u+v+\frac{y^2}{2}\left(\frac1u+\frac1v\right).
$$
To thin-element accuracy, a segment $s(y)=s_0-\alpha y^2$ of this path lies inside the <optical lens>, replacing the same length of air. The geometric air path is $S-s$, the material path is $s$, and the <optical path length> and travel time are therefore
$$
\mathcal L(y)=S(y)+(n-1)s(y),\qquad
ct(y)\simeq u+v+(n-1)s_0
+y^2\left[\frac1{2u}+\frac1{2v}-(n-1)\alpha\right].
$$
This distinguishes geometric length from <optical path length>; adding $ns$ to $S$ without subtracting the replaced air would count that segment twice.

For a nonaxial <light ray>, <Fermat's principle> gives $d\mathcal L/dy=0$ and hence
$$
\frac1u+\frac1v=2(n-1)\alpha.
$$
The <focal length> is obtained with the incident object at infinity:
$$
\boxed{\frac1f=2(n-1)\alpha,\qquad f=\frac1{2(n-1)\alpha}.}
$$
The constant thickness $s_0$ supplies a common delay but does not change the leading <focal length>. Since $\alpha$ has units of inverse <length>, this formula has the correct dimensions. These are <thin lens> results; <paraxial approximation> alone does not remove finite-thickness corrections in a general <thick lens>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Substituting the stated <refractive index> and thickness coefficient, $\alpha=2\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$, gives
$$
\boxed{f=\frac1{2(1.5-1)(2\,\mathrm{m^{-1}})}=0.50\,\mathrm m.}
$$
The <thin lens> equation then gives
$$
\frac1v=\frac1f-\frac1u=2-\frac1{0.7}=\frac47\,\mathrm{m^{-1}},
\qquad
\boxed{v=1.75\,\mathrm m.}
$$
The positive image distance places a <real image> on the outgoing side of the <optical lens>, at $x=+1.75\,\mathrm m$. The object is beyond the <focal point>, so outgoing <light rays> actually converge there rather than only their backward extensions meeting.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use <phasors> with time dependence $e^{i\omega t}$. The <electrical impedance> $Z=\widetilde V/\widetilde I$ is the <complex number> relating the sinusoidal <voltage> and <electric current>: $|Z|$ is their <amplitude> ratio and $\arg Z$ is the <voltage> <phase> relative to the <electric current>. The component laws give
$$
V=RI\ \Longrightarrow\ \boxed{Z_R=R},\qquad
I=C\frac{dV}{dt}\ \Longrightarrow\ \boxed{Z_C=\frac1{i\omega C}},\qquad
V=L\frac{dI}{dt}\ \Longrightarrow\ \boxed{Z_L=i\omega L}.
$$
Thus the <resistor> has zero relative <phase>, the <capacitor> <voltage> lags its <electric current> by $\pi/2$, and the <inductor> <voltage> leads its <electric current> by $\pi/2$. The ideal <capacitor> and <inductor> store and return <energy>; the real <electrical impedance> of the <resistor> describes dissipation.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The original diagram's upper arm is a series <resistor> and <capacitor>; its lower arm is a parallel <resistor> and <capacitor>. The output is the upper-minus-lower <voltage> across that parallel arm. Assume an ideal input voltage source and an unloaded output. Their <electrical impedances> are
$$
Z_s=R+\frac1{i\omega C},\qquad Z_p=\frac{R}{1+i\omega RC}.
$$
For this <series-parallel RC band-pass filter>, the <voltage divider> gives, with $w=\omega RC$,
$$
\boxed{H(\omega)=\frac{\widetilde V_{\rm out}}{\widetilde V_{\rm in}}
=\frac{Z_p}{Z_s+Z_p}
=\frac1{3+i(w-w^{-1})}
=\frac{iw}{1-w^2+3iw}.}
$$
Taking the real part of $V_0H e^{i\omega t}$ yields the steady output
$$
\boxed{V_{\rm out}(t)=\frac{V_0}{\sqrt{9+(w-w^{-1})^2}}
\cos\!\left[\omega t-\arctan\!\left(\frac{w-w^{-1}}3\right)\right].}
$$
Initial charging contributes decaying transients if the source is switched on at a finite time; the displayed expression is the sinusoidal steady state. The output polarity matters: reversing the indicated output <voltage> would multiply $H$ by $-1$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

For positive <angular frequency>, the squared <amplitude> response of the <series-parallel RC band-pass filter> is
$$
|H|^2=\frac1{9+(w-w^{-1})^2}.
$$
Its denominator is minimized exactly when $w=w^{-1}$, so
$$
\boxed{\omega_0=\frac1{RC},\qquad H(\omega_0)=\frac13,\qquad
V_{\rm out}(t)=\frac{V_0}{3}\cos\omega_0t.}
$$
This is a <band-pass filter> maximum produced entirely by the two RC arms; the drawn <electrical circuit> contains no <inductor>. The low- and high-<frequency> limits are
$$
\boxed{H\simeq i\omega RC\quad(\omega\ll\omega_0),\qquad
H\simeq-\frac{i}{\omega RC}\quad(\omega\gg\omega_0).}
$$
Thus the output <amplitude> tends to zero linearly with $\omega$ at low <frequency> and as $1/\omega$ at high <frequency>. In real time,
$$
V_{\rm out}\simeq-V_0\omega RC\sin\omega t\quad(\omega\ll\omega_0),\qquad
V_{\rm out}\simeq\frac{V_0}{\omega RC}\sin\omega t\quad(\omega\gg\omega_0).
$$
The upper series <capacitor> suppresses low <frequencies>, while the lower shunt <capacitor> suppresses high <frequencies>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

In the chosen <phasor> convention, output <phase> minus input <phase> is
$$
\boxed{\phi(\omega)=-\arctan\!\left[\frac{\omega RC-(\omega RC)^{-1}}3\right],\qquad \omega>0.}
$$
The real part of the denominator is positive, so this principal <arctangent> is continuous throughout the positive-<frequency> range. Below $\omega_0$, the output leads the input, approaching $+\pi/2$ at low <frequency>. Above $\omega_0$, it lags, approaching $-\pi/2$ at high <frequency>. At the maximum,
$$
\boxed{\phi(\omega_0)=0.}
$$
The input and output are in <phase> there, with the positive one-third <voltage> ratio found above.
