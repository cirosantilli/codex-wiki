== 1
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

The magnetic <Lorentz force> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F=q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B.}
$$
Since the given <velocity> is perpendicular to the <magnetic flux density>, its magnitude is $|q|vB$. Its direction follows the vector product for positive charge and reverses for negative charge.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

The instantaneous power delivered by the <magnetic field> is
$$
\frac{dW}{dt}=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v=q(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\cdot\mathbf v=0.
$$
Therefore $\boxed{W=0}$ over any part of the orbit. The magnetic force changes direction of motion but leaves the speed and <kinetic energy> constant.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $\mathbf r_P$ be the initial position, $\hat{\mathbf b}=\mathbf B/B$, and $\Omega=qB/m$, including the sign of $q$. Solving $\dot{\mathbf v}=\Omega\mathbf v\times\hat{\mathbf b}$ gives
$$
\mathbf v(t)=\mathbf v_0\cos\Omega t+(\mathbf v_0\times\hat{\mathbf b})\sin\Omega t.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbf r(t)=\mathbf r_P+\frac{\mathbf v_0}{\Omega}\sin\Omega t+\frac{\mathbf v_0\times\hat{\mathbf b}}{\Omega}(1-\cos\Omega t).}
$$
The locus is a circle in the plane perpendicular to $\mathbf B$, centred at $\mathbf r_c=\mathbf r_P+(\mathbf v_0\times\hat{\mathbf b})/\Omega$, with
$$
\boxed{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_c|=\frac{m|\mathbf v_0|}{|q|B},\qquad(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_P)\cdot\mathbf B=0.}
$$
This also follows by equating the magnetic force to the <centripetal force>. The nonrelativistic angular frequency is $|q|B/m$.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

Place charges $+Q$ and $-Q$ on the inner and outer conductors. In the vacuum gap, <Gauss's law> gives $E(r)=Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)$. Their <electric potential difference> is
$$
V_a-V_b=\int_a^bE(r)\,dr=\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left(\frac1a-\frac1b\right).
$$
Consequently the <spherical capacitor> has
$$
\boxed{C=\frac{Q}{V_a-V_b}=4\pi\epsilon_0\frac{ab}{b-a}.}
$$
For a narrow gap $b-a\ll a$, this approaches $\epsilon_0(4\pi a^2)/(b-a)$, the local parallel-plate approximation. For $b\to\infty$, it becomes the capacitance $4\pi\epsilon_0a$ of an isolated sphere.

== 3
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

In the linear <inverting amplifier>, the non-inverting terminal is grounded and <negative feedback> connects the output to the inverting terminal. For large open-loop gain $A$, $V_o=A(V_+-V_-)$; a finite unsaturated output therefore requires $V_-\simeq V_+=0$. Thus
$$
\boxed{V_-\simeq0\quad\text{under stable negative feedback}.}
$$
This is a <virtual ground>: it has approximately ground potential, but it is not a conducting connection to ground. The ideal <operational amplifier> draws no input current, so input current flows through the feedback resistor. Saturation, loss of feedback, or insufficient bandwidth invalidates the approximation.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

An external source sees the input resistor between its terminal and the <virtual ground>. The input current is $I_i=V_i/R_i$, so
$$
\boxed{R_{\rm in}=R_i=1\,\mathrm{k\Omega},\qquad R_{\rm out}=0\quad\text{for an ideal amplifier}.}
$$
The infinite input resistance of the ideal <operational amplifier> itself is distinct from the finite input resistance of the complete <inverting amplifier>. Negative feedback makes the output approach an ideal <voltage source>; a real amplifier retains a small nonzero output resistance.

== 4
{scope}

=== Solution

Assume a converging <thin lens> with $f>0$, a real object, and a real image on the detector. For positive object and image distances $u,v$, the <thin lens> equation gives $uv=f(u+v)$. Put $S=u+v$. Then
$$
(u-v)^2=S^2-4uv=S(S-4f)\ge0.
$$
Since $S>0$, $S\ge4f$, with equality at $u=v=2f$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{S_{\min}=4f,\qquad u=v=2f.}
$$
Below this separation no real conjugate pair exists for one ideal thin lens; a diverging lens alone cannot form a real image of a real object on the detector.

== 5
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

<Faraday's law> states
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E=\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=-\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}}
$$
for a stationary loop, with $\Phi_B=\int\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf S$ and consistent contour and area orientations. The minus sign expresses <Lenz's law>: the induced response opposes the change of <magnetic flux>. An emf can exist across an open loop even when no conduction current flows.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

Choose the loop normal along $+z$. Rotation about $x$ gives $B_z=B\cos\omega t$, so the linked <magnetic flux> is $\Phi_B=Ba^2\cos\omega t$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E(t)=Ba^2\omega\sin\omega t.}
$$
The sign uses the contour orientation associated with $+z$. For the small open gap, this is the corresponding open-circuit terminal emf, with the stated terminal ordering. The tangential induced <electric field> is nonconservative, so its circulation is not the integral of a single-valued electrostatic potential gradient.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

If $R$ closes the break, a conduction current flows and electrical energy is dissipated. When loop self-<inductance> is negligible,
$$
\boxed{I(t)=\frac{Ba^2\omega}{R}\sin\omega t.}
$$
The externally imposed flux-change emf remains the same. If self-inductance $L_\ell$ matters, the current instead satisfies $L_\ell\dot I+RI=Ba^2\omega\sin\omega t$; its steady amplitude is $Ba^2\omega/\sqrt{R^2+\omega^2L_\ell^2}$ and it lags the emf by $\tan^{-1}(\omega L_\ell/R)$. If the break remains open after inserting a resistor elsewhere in series, the loop is still open and the conduction current remains zero.

== 6
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

Use positive current in the charging direction and passive voltage drops across $R,L,C$. The five ideal elements obey
$$
\boxed{v_{\rm battery}=V_0,\quad v_R=RI,\quad v_L=L\frac{dI}{dt},\quad I=C\frac{dv_C}{dt}.}
$$
The battery maintains its emf independently of current. An ideal open switch has $I=0$ with its voltage unconstrained; an ideal closed switch has $v_{\rm switch}=0$ with its current unconstrained. These statements are the switch's piecewise current-voltage relation. <Inductor current continuity> and capacitor-voltage continuity hold when no impulse voltage or current is imposed.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

For an initially uncharged capacitor and zero initial current, <Kirchhoff's voltage law> is $L\dot I+RI+Q/C=V_0$, with $I=\dot Q$. Let
$$
\alpha=\frac{R}{2L},\qquad\omega_n=\frac1{\sqrt{LC}},\qquad\omega_d=\sqrt{\omega_n^2-\alpha^2}.
$$
Differentiating the circuit equation at $t>0$ gives $\ddot I+2\alpha\dot I+\omega_n^2I=0$, while the original equation fixes $I(0)=0$ and $\dot I(0)=V_0/L$. The underdamped response is
$$
\boxed{I(t)=\frac{V_0}{L\omega_d}e^{-\alpha t}\sin\omega_dt.}
$$
For critical damping, $\alpha=\omega_n$, its limit is $I=(V_0/L)t e^{-\alpha t}$. For overdamping, put $\beta=\sqrt{\alpha^2-\omega_n^2}$ and obtain $I=V_0e^{-\alpha t}\sinh(\beta t)/(L\beta)$. These forms cover every positive $R,L,C$.

The source does not explicitly specify the capacitor's initial charge. If its initial voltage is $V_{C0}$, replace $V_0$ in the numerators by $V_0-V_{C0}$; an open switch alone does not discharge a capacitor. The boxed expression uses the usual uncharged initial condition.

== 7
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $q_1$ at $x=-L$, $q_2$ at $x=+L$, and $q_3$ at $x=fL$, with $-1<f<1$. For a nonzero test charge, the force vanishes when the electric field vanishes:
$$
\frac{q_1}{(1+f)^2}=\frac{q_2}{(1-f)^2}.
$$
For equal-sign nonzero $q_1,q_2$, take the positive square root $s=\sqrt{q_2/q_1}$. The distances are positive, so $1-f=s(1+f)$ and
$$
\boxed{f=\frac{1-s}{1+s}.}
$$
For $s>1$ the zero moves toward the weaker charge $q_1$; equal charges give $f=0$.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

With $q_2=-q_1$, both field contributions between the charges point the same way. Their signed force on $q_3$ is
$$
F_x(f)=\frac{k_eq_1q_3}{L^2}\left[\frac1{(1+f)^2}+\frac1{(1-f)^2}\right]
=\frac{2k_eq_1q_3}{L^2}\frac{1+f^2}{(1-f^2)^2},\qquad k_e=\frac1{4\pi\epsilon_0}.
$$
Since $f$ is dimensionless, the small-displacement expansion is $|f|\ll1$, giving
$$
\boxed{F_x(f)=\frac{2k_eq_1q_3}{L^2}\left(1+3f^2+O(f^4)\right).}
$$
The force at the midpoint is nonzero and the first variation is quadratic, not linear. In particular, the midpoint is not an equilibrium supporting a linear restoring-force approximation. The printed comparison of $f$ with $L$ is dimensionally inconsistent; the expansion above keeps their distinct roles.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

Here $q_1=3.8q_3$ and $q_2=-1.9q_3$. The part of the <electrostatic potential energy> that changes is
$$
U(f)=\frac{k_eq_3^2}{L}\left(\frac{3.8}{1+f}-\frac{1.9}{1-f}\right).
$$
At the two endpoints, $U(-0.9)=37k_eq_3^2/L$ and $U(0.9)=-17k_eq_3^2/L$. The fixed-source interaction energy is unchanged, and conservation of energy gives
$$
\frac12mv^2=U(-0.9)-U(0.9)=\frac{54k_eq_3^2}{L},\qquad
\boxed{v=\sqrt{\frac{108k_eq_3^2}{mL}}.}
$$
The force points toward increasing $f$ throughout this interval: the left charge repels $q_3$ and the right charge attracts it, so release from rest reaches the stated endpoint without a turning point.

== 8
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

In the <Fraunhofer approximation>, contributions from positions $\xi$ across the slit have relative phase $k\xi\sin\theta$. With uniform illumination, the field relative to its axial value is
$$
\frac{E(\theta)}{E(0)}=\frac1w\int_{-w/2}^{w/2}e^{ik\xi\sin\theta}\,d\xi
=\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta},\qquad\beta=\frac{\pi w\sin\theta}{\lambda}.
$$
Thus the <single-slit diffraction> intensity is
$$
\boxed{I(\theta)=I(0)\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2.}
$$
On the detector $\sin\theta=y/\sqrt{d^2+y^2}$; near the axis, $\beta\simeq\pi wy/(\lambda d)$. The central maximum takes the continuous limit $\sin\beta/\beta\to1$, and the minima satisfy $w\sin\theta=m\lambda$ for nonzero integers $m$. The stated far-field condition justifies adding the aperture contributions with a linear phase.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

The first single-slit minimum is at $\sin\theta=\lambda/w$, so the characteristic angular resolution of an aperture of width $D$ is of order $\lambda/D$. With $D=1$ m, $\lambda=550$ nm, and distance $\mathcal D=10^8$ m, this slit-based estimate is
$$
\boxed{\Delta\theta\sim5.5\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{rad},\qquad \Delta x\sim55\,\mathrm m.}
$$
For the actual circular lens, the <Rayleigh criterion> uses the first zero of the <Airy disk>, $\Delta\theta\simeq1.22\lambda/D$. It gives $6.71\times10^{-7}$ rad and a resolved separation of about $67$ m. The factor $1.22$ belongs to the circular aperture, not to the one-dimensional slit derivation.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

For normal incidence, adjacent grating lines have <optical path difference> $s\sin\theta$. Constructive interference at order $m$ requires $s\sin\theta_m=m\lambda$. Differentiating at fixed $m$ yields the <angular dispersion>
$$
\boxed{\frac{d\theta_m}{d\lambda}=\frac{m}{s\cos\theta_m}.}
$$
It is zero in the zero order and increases in magnitude toward grazing diffraction. An order exists only if $|m\lambda|\le s$. The number of illuminated lines controls peak width and resolving power, but does not alter this angle-versus-wavelength relation.

== 9
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $A_t=\pi d^2/4$ be the tube cross-section. A long uniformly wound <solenoid> with $n_1$ total turns has field $B_A=\mu_0n_1I/s$, approximately uniform inside and negligible outside. Therefore the flux through one cross-section, or one turn of either coil, is
$$
\boxed{\Phi=\frac{\mu_0n_1IA_t}{s}=\frac{\mu_0n_1I\pi d^2}{4s}.}
$$
The linked flux of coil A is $n_1\Phi$; that of coil B is $n_2\Phi$. Distinguishing flux from flux linkage prevents an extra factor of the turn count.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

For chosen positive terminal currents, define self-inductance by the coil's own flux linkage $\Lambda_j=L_jI_j$ and mutual inductance by $\Lambda_2=M I_1$ when only coil A carries current. With matching winding directions and terminal references,
$$
\boxed{L_A=\frac{\mu_0n_1^2A_t}{s},\qquad L_B=\frac{\mu_0n_2^2A_t}{s},\qquad M=\frac{\mu_0n_1n_2A_t}{s}.}
$$
The reciprocal flux-linkage relation gives the same $M$ from B to A, and $M^2=L_AL_B$ in this ideal fully coupled long-solenoid limit. The induced emf is $-d\Lambda/dt$; passive terminal voltage drops use the opposite sign, $v_A=L_A\dot I_A+M\dot I_B$ and similarly for B.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

Reversing one winding reverses its signed coupling to the other, while its self-inductance is unchanged. With the same north-to-south current references,
$$
\boxed{M\longrightarrow-M,\qquad L_A,L_B\text{ unchanged}.}
$$
Equal currents then produce opposing magnetic fields: the signed axial field is proportional to $(n_1-n_2)I$, rather than $(n_1+n_2)I$. Which direction is positive must be fixed by the right-hand rule and the actual winding orientation.

=== iv
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $B_E>0$ be the northward component of the ambient <magnetic field>. Define $\eta=+1$ if positive north-to-south current in A produces a northward field, and $\eta=-1$ otherwise. With opposite windings and the same signed current in both,
$$
B_N=B_E+\eta\frac{\mu_0(n_1-n_2)}sI.
$$
A compass reverses only when this northward component becomes negative. For current chosen to make the coil field southward,
$$
\boxed{|I|>I_{\rm crit}=\frac{B_Es}{\mu_0|n_1-n_2|}.}
$$
At equality the ideal axial field is zero and the needle has no preferred axial direction. If $n_1=n_2$, the opposing coil fields cancel and no finite current reverses it in this model. A numerical threshold cannot be given without $B_E$ and the coil parameters.

=== v
{scope}

==== Solution

Use north-minus-south terminal voltages for both oppositely wound coils, with coil A measured at high input impedance so its AC current is zero. Then $v_B=L_B\dot I_B$ and $v_A=M\dot I_B$, with $M<0$. Hence
$$
\boxed{v_A(t)=\frac{M}{L_B}v_B(t)=-\frac{n_1}{n_2}V_0\sin\omega t.}
$$
The magnitude is the turns ratio times the drive, and these terminal references give a phase reversal. Reversing one voltmeter lead reverses the displayed sign. A fixed DC bias in A does not change this incremental result, while short-circuiting A would require solving both coupled current equations.

== 10
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

Use sinusoidal <phasors>, $v(t)=\operatorname{Re}(\widetilde V e^{i\omega t})$. Differentiation becomes multiplication by $i\omega$, so the time-dependent element laws reduce to algebraic <complex impedances>:
$$
\boxed{Z_R=R,\qquad Z_L=i\omega L,\qquad Z_C=\frac1{i\omega C}.}
$$
Then $\widetilde V=Z\widetilde I$ encodes both amplitude ratio $|Z|$ and voltage-current phase difference $\arg Z$. Series impedances add, while parallel admittances add. This applies to linear sinusoidal steady state; switching transients are separate homogeneous solutions.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

The source figure has a series capacitor followed by $R$ in parallel with $L$. Its <complex impedance> is
$$
Z=-\frac{i}{\omega C}+\frac{i\omega LR}{R+i\omega L}
=\frac{R\omega^2L^2}{R^2+\omega^2L^2}+i\left[\frac{\omega LR^2}{R^2+\omega^2L^2}-\frac1{\omega C}\right].
$$
The imaginary part vanishes at a finite positive frequency only if $R^2C>L$, in which case
$$
\boxed{\omega_*^2=\frac{R^2}{L(R^2C-L)}=\frac1{LC-L^2/R^2}.}
$$
At that frequency, substituting $R^2+\omega_*^2L^2=\omega_*^2LR^2C$ into the real part gives
$$
\boxed{Z(\omega_*)=\frac{L}{RC}.}
$$
If $R^2C\le L$, no finite positive-frequency cancellation exists; the impedance merely tends toward its high-frequency limit.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

The parameters satisfy $R^2C=100\,\mu\mathrm H>L=10\,\mu\mathrm H$, and
$$
\omega_*=3.33\times10^5\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}},\qquad Z_*=\frac{L}{RC}=1\,\Omega.
$$
Thus the source current is $10$ A RMS. The ideal reactive elements dissipate no average power, so all mean input power is lost in $R$:
$$
\boxed{\overline P=\frac{V_{\rm rms}^2}{Z_*}=100\,\mathrm W,\qquad P_{\rm resistor,peak}=2\overline P=200\,\mathrm W.}
$$
The source's instantaneous input power also has maximum $200$ W because its total current is in phase with its voltage, although its power maximum need not occur at the resistor's power maximum: the reactive elements exchange stored energy.

With only $R$ across the source,
$$
\boxed{\overline P_R=10\,\mathrm W,\qquad P_{R,\rm peak}=20\,\mathrm W.}
$$
The network transforms the real input load from $10\,\Omega$ to $1\,\Omega$. At $\omega_*$ the parallel-branch impedance is $1+3i\,\Omega$, giving resistor RMS voltage $10\sqrt{10}$ V and again $(10\sqrt{10})^2/10=100$ W. The extra power is supplied by the voltage source; it is not produced by the ideal capacitor or inductor.
