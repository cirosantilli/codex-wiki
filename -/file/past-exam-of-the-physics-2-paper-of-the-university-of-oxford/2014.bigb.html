== 1
{scope}

=== Solution

Take the conducting plane as $y=0$, with the real <point charges> $Q$ at $(0,b)$ and $-2Q$ at $(a,b)$. The <method of images> replaces the plane by image charges $-Q$ at $(0,-b)$ and $+2Q$ at $(a,-b)$. This makes the plane an equipotential; the physical field above it is the sum of the real- and image-charge fields. No factor of $1/2$ enters the force on a real charge.

Set $k_e=1/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$ and $d=\sqrt{a^2+4b^2}$, and use positive $x$ from $Q$ toward $-2Q$, positive $y$ upward. The horizontal forces combine the mutual attraction and the force from the other charge's image:
$$
\boxed{F_{Q,x}=2k_eQ^2\left(\frac1{a^2}-\frac a{d^3}\right),\qquad F_{-2Q,x}=-F_{Q,x}.}
$$
For the vertical components, the own-image attractions act downward; each cross-image term acts upward. Thus
$$
\boxed{F_{Q,y}=k_eQ^2\left(-\frac1{4b^2}+\frac{4b}{d^3}\right),\qquad F_{-2Q,y}=k_eQ^2\left(-\frac1{b^2}+\frac{4b}{d^3}\right).}
$$
These are the electrostatic forces; the external forces holding the charges fixed are their negatives. The smaller positive charge's vertical force need not always point downward, because the other charge's induced surface charge also contributes. The construction assumes the usual ideal large-plane limit with no imposed background field.

== 2
{scope}

=== 1
{scope}

==== Solution

<Faraday's law> states that the induced <electromotive force> equals minus the rate of change of linked <magnetic flux>:
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E=-\frac{d(N\Phi)}{dt}.}
$$
The minus sign expresses <Lenz's law>: the induced <electric current> acts to oppose the change in <magnetic flux>. For a fixed contour this is equivalent to $\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=-d\int\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf S/dt$.

=== 2
{scope}

==== Solution

The loop area is $A=\pi(5\times10^{-3})^2\,\mathrm{m^2}$. Integrating <Ohm's law> and <Faraday's law> through the whole decay gives
$$
RQ_{\rm passed}=\int\mathcal E\,dt=NA B_0.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{B_0=\frac{RQ_{\rm passed}}{NA}=\frac{30(5\times10^{-6})}{50\pi(5\times10^{-3})^2}\simeq3.82\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm T.}
$$
The switching time cancels: a faster linear flux decay produces a larger <electric current> for a proportionally shorter interval. If the coil's self-<inductance> is retained, integrating $L\,dI/dt$ gives zero provided the initial and final currents vanish and the stated total charge includes the decay tail.

== 3
{scope}

=== Solution

Let the wire radius be $a$, and take its <electric current> along $+z$. Cylindrical symmetry makes the <magnetic flux density> azimuthal. For a circular <Amperian loop> of radius $r$, <Ampère's law> gives $2\pi rB=\mu_0 I_{\rm enclosed}$. Uniform <current density> means $I_{\rm enclosed}=Ir^2/a^2$ inside and $I$ outside. Consequently
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B(r)=\begin{cases}
\dfrac{\mu_0Ir}{2\pi a^2}\hat{\boldsymbol\phi},&0\le r\le a,\\
\dfrac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}\hat{\boldsymbol\phi},&r\ge a.
\end{cases}}
$$
Its direction follows the right-hand rule. The magnitude rises linearly from zero to $\mu_0I/(2\pi a)$ at the surface, is continuous there, and then falls as $1/r$.

== 4
{scope}

=== 1
{scope}

==== Solution

For the intended force-balanced straight-line motion, the <Lorentz force> vanishes:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B=0.}
$$
For a nonzero uniform <magnetic field>, this implies $\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B=0$ and
$$
\mathbf v_\perp=\frac{\mathbf E\times\mathbf B}{B^2},
$$
while a component parallel to $\mathbf B$ is unrestricted. A straight trajectory by itself states only that the force has no transverse component, $\mathbf v\times(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)=0$. For example, parallel electric field and motion can give straight but accelerated motion. The boxed cancellation is the usual constant-velocity interpretation of the source.

=== 2
{scope}

==== Solution

After the <electric field> is removed, the uniform <magnetic field> preserves the speed. Decompose the instantaneous initial <velocity> as $\mathbf v_0=\mathbf v_\parallel+\mathbf v_\perp$, with $\hat{\mathbf b}=\mathbf B/B$ and signed $\Omega=qB/m$. Then
$$
\mathbf v(t)=\mathbf v_\parallel+\mathbf v_\perp\cos\Omega t+(\mathbf v_\perp\times\hat{\mathbf b})\sin\Omega t,
$$
and, integrating,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf r(t)=\mathbf r_0+\mathbf v_\parallel t+\frac{\mathbf v_\perp}{\Omega}\sin\Omega t+\frac{\mathbf v_\perp\times\hat{\mathbf b}}{\Omega}(1-\cos\Omega t).}
$$
This is a <helical motion in a uniform magnetic field> of radius $m|\mathbf v_\perp|/(|q|B)$ and angular frequency $|q|B/m$. It is a circle if $\mathbf v_\parallel=0$, and a straight line if $\mathbf v_\perp=0$. The formula assumes a uniform, nonzero field and nonrelativistic motion.

== 5
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

With <phasors> proportional to $e^{i\omega t}$, the series <RLC circuit> has
$$
Z=R+i\left(\omega L-\frac1{\omega C}\right),\qquad I_0=\frac{V_0}{\sqrt{R^2+(\omega L-1/(\omega C))^2}}.
$$
For fixed $V_0$ and frequency-independent positive $R$, the maximum occurs when the reactances cancel. Thus
$$
\boxed{\omega_0=\frac1{\sqrt{LC}},\qquad I_{0,\max}=\frac{V_0}{R}.}
$$
This is the driven current <resonance>; a damped free-oscillation frequency is a different quantity.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

Define the phase difference as current phase minus voltage phase. Since $\widetilde I=\widetilde V/Z$,
$$
\boxed{\phi(\omega)=-\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\omega L-1/(\omega C)}{R}\right).}
$$
It decreases continuously from $+\pi/2$ at low frequency, through zero at $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$, to $-\pi/2$ at high frequency. Thus the current leads in the capacitive limit and lags in the inductive limit. Defining voltage phase minus current phase would reverse the graph's sign.

== 6
{scope}

=== Solution

<Huygens' principle> treats each point of a <wavefront> as the source of a secondary wavelet. The later <wavefront> is the common forward envelope of those wavelets. Consider a plane front reaching a flat interface between media with speeds $v_1$ and $v_2$. In the same interval $\Delta t$, the incident front advances $v_1\Delta t$ while the first transmitted wavelet grows to radius $v_2\Delta t$.

The two right triangles sharing the interface segment of length $d$ give $d\sin i=v_1\Delta t$ and $d\sin r=v_2\Delta t$, where $i$ and $r$ are ray angles to the normal. Hence
$$
\frac{\sin i}{\sin r}=\frac{v_1}{v_2}=\frac{n_2}{n_1},\qquad\boxed{n_1\sin i=n_2\sin r.}
$$
This is <Snell's law>. The frequency is unchanged at a stationary interface, while wavelength changes with the propagation speed.

== 7
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== 1
{scope}

===== Solution

Let $I_s$ be the intensity that either narrow slit would produce alone, and let $y$ measure screen displacement from the perpendicular bisector. The <optical path difference> is approximately $\delta=b\sin\theta\simeq by/D$. Equal coherent field amplitudes then give
$$
I=I_s+I_s+2I_s\cos\left(\frac{2\pi\delta}{\lambda}\right)
=\boxed{4I_s\cos^2\left(\frac{\pi by}{\lambda D}\right)}.
$$
The maxima are at $y_m=m\lambda D/b$; the minima are halfway between them. Here
$$
\boxed{\Delta y=\frac{\lambda D}{b}=0.532\,\mathrm{mm}.}
$$
The expression uses the paraxial approximation and neglects the slowly varying single-slit <diffraction> envelope because the slit widths are unspecified.

==== 2
{scope}

===== Solution

The slide adds an <optical path length> $\Delta=(n-1)t=50\,\mu\mathrm m$ in the covered path. If the covered slit is at positive transverse position, its geometric path to $y>0$ is shorter, giving total path difference $\Delta-by/D$. The zero-difference fringe therefore moves toward that slit:
$$
\boxed{y_0=\frac{D(n-1)t}{b}=5.0\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm m=50\,\mathrm{mm}.}
$$
This is about $94$ fringe periods. The leading grating-angle correction, $b\sin\theta=\Delta$ and $y=D\tan\theta$, gives $50.06$ mm, consistent with the paraxial result. In a strictly monochromatic periodic pattern, identifying the continuously tracked central order is essential: the final intensity pattern determines displacement only modulo one fringe spacing.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The band has mean wavelength $\bar\lambda\simeq530\,\mathrm{nm}$ and width $\Delta\lambda=20\,\mathrm{nm}$. At order $m$, the positions of the extreme-wavelength maxima differ by about $|m|D\Delta\lambda/b$. Comparing this spread with a fringe spacing $D\bar\lambda/b$ gives
$$
|m|\sim\frac{\bar\lambda}{\Delta\lambda}\simeq26.5.
$$
Equivalently, the <coherence length> scale is $\ell_c\sim\bar\lambda^2/\Delta\lambda\simeq14\,\mu\mathrm m$. Thus the usual bandwidth estimate is
$$
\boxed{\text{about }26\text{ orders on either side, or order }50\text{ fringes in total}.}
$$
This is a washout scale, not a sharp definition of “clearly”. For a spectrum uniform in inverse wavelength $\sigma=1/\lambda$, the <interference visibility> is $|\operatorname{sinc}(\pi\Delta\sigma\,\delta)|$, with $\operatorname{sinc}u=\sin u/u$; its first zero occurs near order $26.5$. Fringes well inside that limit have stronger contrast. A half-fringe-smear criterion would instead retain about $13$ orders each side. The precise visible number needs the spectral distribution and a contrast threshold, neither supplied. If the slide remains in place and dispersion is neglected, the envelope is centred on the shifted zero-path-difference position and this order-count scale is unchanged.

== 8
{scope}

=== 1
{scope}

==== Solution

In matter, <Gauss's law> for free <electric charge> is
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_{\rm free},\qquad \oint_{\partial\mathcal V}\mathbf D\cdot d\mathbf S=Q_{\rm free,enclosed}.}
$$
The area normal points out of $\mathcal V$. In vacuum $\mathbf D=\epsilon_0\mathbf E$, so these become $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\epsilon_0$ and $\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf S=Q_{\rm enclosed}/\epsilon_0$.

=== 2
{scope}

==== Solution

Put charges $+Q$ and $-Q$ on the concentric conductors. <Gauss's law> gives $E(r)=Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)$ between them, $a<r<2a$. The potential difference is
$$
\Delta V=\int_a^{2a}\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^2}\,dr
=\frac{Q}{8\pi\epsilon_0a}.
$$
The <capacitance> of this vacuum <spherical capacitor> is therefore
$$
\boxed{C=\frac{Q}{\Delta V}=8\pi\epsilon_0a.}
$$
The conductors are equipotentials; a uniform dielectric filling would replace $\epsilon_0$ by its <permittivity>.

=== 3
{scope}

==== Solution

At $(x,0)$ the distances from the two centres are equal:
$$
r=\sqrt{x^2+a^2/4},\qquad x_1=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}a,\qquad x_2=\frac{\sqrt{15}}2a.
$$
By the electrostatic <shell theorem>, a uniformly charged spherical shell gives zero field inside and its point-charge field outside. For $x<x_1$ the point is inside both shells. For $x_1<x<x_2$ only the small, negatively charged shell contributes. For $x>x_2$ both contribute, so
$$
\mathbf E(x)=\begin{cases}
0,&0\le x<x_1,\\
\dfrac{k_eq}{r^3}\left(-x\hat{\mathbf x}+\dfrac a2\hat{\mathbf y}\right),&x_1<x<x_2,\\
\dfrac{k_eqa}{r^3}\hat{\mathbf y},&x>x_2,
\end{cases}\qquad k_e=\frac1{4\pi\epsilon_0}.
$$
The requested magnitude is
$$
\boxed{|\mathbf E(x)|=\begin{cases}
0,&0\le x<x_1,\\
\dfrac{k_eq}{x^2+a^2/4},&x_1<x<x_2,\\
\dfrac{k_eqa}{(x^2+a^2/4)^{3/2}},&x>x_2.
\end{cases}}
$$
It jumps from zero to $k_eq/a^2$ at $x_1$, decreases to $k_eq/(4a^2)$ just before $x_2$, jumps down to $k_eq/(8a^2)$ just after $x_2$, and then falls as $x^{-3}$. The ideal surface charge makes the field discontinuous at each crossing, so the one-sided limits, rather than a unique value on the sheet, specify the graph. The far field is the equatorial field of an <electric dipole> with <electric dipole moment> $\mathbf p=-qa\hat{\mathbf y}$.

== 9
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

The source diagram has $2R$ in series with two parallel branches: one containing $R$ then $L$, and the other containing $2R$. Let their common voltage be $u(t)$, measured from the upper node to the lower return. <Kirchhoff's current law> and the branch laws give
$$
I_0=I_1+I_2,\qquad u=2RI_2=RI_1+L\frac{dI_1}{dt},\qquad V=2RI_0+u.
$$
Using $I_2=u/(2R)$ in the source equation yields $u=V/2-RI_1$. Substitution into the coil branch gives
$$
\boxed{L\frac{dI_1}{dt}+2RI_1=\frac V2.}
$$
The switch was open for a long time, so $I_1(0^-)=0$. <Inductor current continuity> gives $I_1(0^+)=0$ for a finite applied voltage.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

The constant particular solution is $A=V/(4R)$, and the homogeneous equation has decay rate $2R/L$. Imposing $I_1(0)=0$ gives
$$
\boxed{I_1(t)=\frac{V}{4R}\left(1-e^{-t/\tau}\right),\qquad A=\frac V{4R},\quad\tau=\frac L{2R}.}
$$
As a separate check, the <Thévenin equivalent> seen by $L$ has open-circuit voltage $V/2$ and resistance $R+(2R\parallel2R)=2R$, again giving $\tau=L/(2R)$.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $e_t=e^{-t/\tau}$. From $u=V/2-RI_1$ and $I_0=I_1+I_2$,
$$
\boxed{I_1=\frac V{4R}(1-e_t),\qquad I_2=\frac V{8R}(1+e_t),\qquad I_0=\frac V{8R}(3-e_t).}
$$
At $t=0^+$ the three values are $I_1=0$ and $I_2=I_0=V/(4R)$. At late times they are $I_1=V/(4R)$, $I_2=V/(8R)$, and $I_0=3V/(8R)$. Thus $I_1$ and $I_0$ increase monotonically while $I_2$ decreases; every transient has the same <time constant>. By $10\tau$, the exponential correction is only $e^{-10}\simeq4.54\times10^{-5}$ of its initial amplitude.

=== iv
{scope}

==== Solution

Only the ideal <inductor> stores energy in this circuit; ideal <resistors> dissipate energy as heat. With $I_1(\infty)=V/(4R)$,
$$
\boxed{U_{\rm stored}=\frac12LI_1(\infty)^2=\frac{LV^2}{32R^2}.}
$$
The question excludes the battery's internal energy, and the continuously generated resistor heat is not stored electromagnetic energy.

== 10
{scope}

=== 1
{scope}

==== Solution

<Maxwell's equations> in matter, with free charge and current densities, are
$$
\boxed{\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_{\rm free},\quad\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\quad\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},\quad\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_{\rm free}+\frac{\partial\mathbf D}{\partial t}.}
$$
For vacuum, $\mathbf D=\epsilon_0\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf H=\mathbf B/\mu_0$, so the last equation is $\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\epsilon_0\,\partial\mathbf E/\partial t$.

=== 2
{scope}

==== Solution

For a charging <parallel-plate capacitor> of area $A$, neglect fringing. The field in the gap is $E=Q/(\epsilon_0A)$, so the <displacement current> through the gap is
$$
\boxed{I_D=\epsilon_0\frac{d}{dt}\int\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf S=\epsilon_0A\frac{dE}{dt}=\frac{dQ}{dt}=I.}
$$
An <Amperian loop> around the feed wire can bound a surface cutting the wire, which has conduction current $I$, or a bulging surface passing through the dielectric gap, which has no conduction current. Including displacement current makes both surfaces give the same magnetic circulation. It is also required by <charge conservation>: the divergence of the <Ampère-Maxwell equation> and <Gauss's law> yields $\nabla\cdot\mathbf J+\partial\rho/\partial t=0$, the <continuity equation>.

=== 3
{scope}

==== Solution

In charge- and current-free vacuum, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$. Taking the curl of <Faraday's law> and using the <Ampère-Maxwell equation> gives
$$
\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=-\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}.
$$
Since $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E)-\nabla^2\mathbf E$, it follows that
$$
\boxed{\nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2},\qquad\nabla^2\mathbf B=\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\mathbf B}{\partial t^2}.}
$$
Substituting a plane wave with phase $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r-\omega t$ gives $k^2=\mu_0\epsilon_0\omega^2$ and therefore
$$
\boxed{c=\frac\omega k=\frac1{\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}}\simeq3.00\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
The divergence equations make the waves transverse, and the curl equations fix $\mathbf B=\hat{\mathbf k}\times\mathbf E/c$.

=== 4
{scope}

==== Solution

The phase $kz-\omega t$ describes propagation along $+z$. With $\psi=kz-\omega t$, the vacuum <plane electromagnetic wave> has
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B=\frac1c\hat{\mathbf z}\times\mathbf E=\frac Ac(-3\hat{\mathbf x}+2\hat{\mathbf y})\sin\psi.}
$$
Thus $B_x=-3A\sin\psi/c$, $B_y=2A\sin\psi/c$, and $B_z=0$. The field amplitudes are $E_0=\sqrt{13}|A|$ and $B_0=\sqrt{13}|A|/c$, giving
$$
\boxed{E_0/B_0=c.}
$$
The instantaneous magnitude ratio also equals $c$ wherever the fields are nonzero; at a node both vanish together. Their <Poynting vector> points along $+z$, checking the relative component signs.
