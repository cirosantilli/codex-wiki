== 1
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

Position A disconnects the <battery> and closes the <resistor>-<inductor> loop. Taking clockwise <electric current> as positive, <Kirchhoff's voltage law> for this <RL circuit> gives
$$
L\frac{dI}{dt}+RI=0.
$$
The <electric current> through an ideal <inductor> remains continuous across a switch with no impulsive <voltage>. Thus $I(0^+)=I_0$, and <separation of variables> gives
$$
\boxed{I(t)=I_0e^{-Rt/L}\quad(0<t<\Delta t).}
$$
If the switch remains at A, the same result holds for every $t>0$. The <time constant> is $L/R$; the stored <magnetic energy> is dissipated by <Joule heating>.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

Put $s=e^{-R\Delta t/L}$ and $J=V/R$, with $0<s<1$. The first interval leaves <electric current> $I_1=sI_0$. Position B reconnects the <battery>, so the driven <RL circuit> obeys $L\dot I+RI=V$. Multiplication by the <integrating factor> $e^{Rt/L}$, or subtraction of the constant solution $J$, gives
$$
I(t)=J+(sI_0-J)e^{-R(t-\Delta t)/L},\qquad \Delta t<t<2\Delta t.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{I_2=s^2I_0+J(1-s).}
$$
The same two intervals give the <recurrence relation> $I_{2n+2}=s^2I_{2n}+J(1-s)$. Its <fixed point> is $I_*=J/(1+s)$. Subtracting this <fixed point> makes the <recurrence relation> homogeneous:
$$
I_{2n}-I_*=s^{2n}(I_0-I_*),\qquad
\boxed{\lim_{n\to\infty}I_{2n}=\frac{V/R}{1+e^{-R\Delta t/L}}.}
$$
This is the upper endpoint of the eventual <periodically switched RL circuit> response, immediately before switching from B to A. The lower endpoint is $sJ/(1+s)$. Their sum is $J$, consistent with equal charging and discharging intervals.

=== i

==== Solution

An <ideal operational amplifier> has **infinite <input impedance>**, so neither input draws <electric current>, and **zero <output impedance>**, so its prescribed output <voltage> is independent of the output <electric current> within the ideal model. These are the two additional assumptions needed here.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

The non-inverting input is grounded, so the finite <voltage gain> gives $V_{\rm OUT}=-AV^-$, or $V^-=-V_{\rm OUT}/A$. Infinite <input impedance> makes <Kirchhoff's current law> at the inverting input
$$
\frac{V_{\rm IN}-V^-}{R_1}+\frac{V_{\rm OUT}-V^-}{R_2}=0.
$$
Substitute $V^-=-V_{\rm OUT}/A$ and multiply by $R_1R_2$:
$$
R_2V_{\rm IN}+\left(R_1+\frac{R_1+R_2}{A}\right)V_{\rm OUT}=0.
$$
Hence the <inverting operational amplifier> has
$$
\boxed{\frac{V_{\rm OUT}}{V_{\rm IN}}=-\frac{R_2}{R_1+(R_1+R_2)/A},\qquad B=\frac{R_1+R_2}{A}.}
$$
As $A\to\infty$, the <voltage gain> tends to $-R_2/R_1$. Setting $V^-=0$ before taking that limit would discard precisely the correction being calculated.

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

Assume the <electrical conductor> is nonmagnetic, with <magnetic permeability> $\mu_0$, and neglect end effects. <Rotational symmetry> and <translational symmetry> make the <magnetic flux density> azimuthal and constant along a coaxial circle. The <magnetostatic Ampère law> therefore gives
$$
2\pi rB(r)=\mu_0 I_{\rm enc}(r).
$$
Uniform <current density> is $j=I/(\pi a^2)$, so $I_{\rm enc}(r)=j\pi r^2=Ir^2/a^2$. Thus
$$
\boxed{B(r)=\frac{\mu_0 I r}{2\pi a^2}\quad(0\leq r\leq a).}
$$
The direction follows the <right-hand rule> around the <electric current>. For a uniform linear magnetic material, replace $\mu_0$ by its <magnetic permeability>.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

Write the axial <current density> as $j(r)=Kr$. Normalize it using the total <electric current>:
$$
I=\int_0^a Kr\,2\pi r\,dr=\frac{2\pi Ka^3}{3},\qquad K=\frac{3I}{2\pi a^3}.
$$
The enclosed <electric current> at radius $r$ is $I_{\rm enc}(r)=2\pi Kr^3/3=I(r/a)^3$. The <magnetostatic Ampère law> now yields
$$
\boxed{B(r)=\frac{\mu_0 I r^2}{2\pi a^3}\quad(0\leq r\leq a).}
$$
Both profiles join the exterior <magnetic flux density> $\mu_0 I/(2\pi a)$ at the surface, but the second vanishes quadratically at the axis because less <electric current> is concentrated near it.

== 4
{scope}

=== Solution

Choose zero <electrostatic potential> at infinity. By <Coulomb's law> and the <superposition principle>, the two <point charges> give
$$
\boxed{\Phi(r,\theta)=\frac1{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac{q_1}{\sqrt{r^2+r_1^2-2rr_1\cos\theta}}+\frac{q_2}{\sqrt{r^2+r_2^2-2rr_2\cos\theta}}\right].}
$$
Here $\epsilon_0$ is the <vacuum permittivity>. For a nondegenerate zero-<equipotential curve>, the <electric charges> must have opposite signs. Let $d_1,d_2$ be the two distances. The equation $\Phi=0$ becomes $q_1d_2=-q_2d_1$, and hence
$$
q_1^2(r^2+r_2^2-2rr_2\cos\theta)=q_2^2(r^2+r_1^2-2rr_1\cos\theta).
$$
For a circle $r=\rho>0$ centred at the origin, its $\cos\theta$ coefficient must vanish: $q_1^2r_2=q_2^2r_1$. If $r_1,r_2>0$ and $r_1\ne r_2$, this gives
$$
\boxed{q_2=-q_1\sqrt{r_2/r_1}.}
$$
Substitute $q_2^2/q_1^2=r_2/r_1$ in the constant term:
$$
r_1\rho^2+r_1r_2^2=r_2\rho^2+r_2r_1^2,
\qquad (r_1-r_2)(\rho^2-r_1r_2)=0.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\rho=\sqrt{r_1r_2}.}
$$
This is an <Apollonius circle>: the distance ratio $d_2/d_1$ is constant. The distinct-position assumption matters. For example, $r_1=r_2=1$, $q_2=-q_1$ gives zero <electrostatic potential> everywhere away from the coincident <point charges>, so there is no distinguished zero circle. If only one position is at the origin and the other is not, no nontrivial positive-radius circle centred at the origin is possible.

{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

In charge-free, current-free vacuum, <Maxwell's equations> are
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=0,\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t},\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}.
$$
Taking the <curl> of the third equation and using the fourth, the <curl of the curl identity>, and the zero <divergence> of the <electric field> gives
$$
\nabla^2\mathbf E=\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}.
$$
The <magnetic flux density> obeys the same <wave equation>. For the <plane wave> factor $e^{i(\omega t-kx)}$, the spatial <Laplacian> contributes $-k^2$ and the second time <derivative> contributes $-\omega^2$. Thus
$$
k^2=\mu_0\epsilon_0\omega^2,\qquad
\boxed{c=\frac{\omega}{k}=\frac1{\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}}}
$$
for propagation in the positive $x$ direction. The zero-<divergence> conditions require $E_{0x}=B_{0x}=0$, while the <curl> equations require $\mathbf B_0=(k/\omega)\hat{\mathbf x}\times\mathbf E_0$. These conditions show that the proposed <plane waves> satisfy all four <Maxwell's equations>, rather than only their derived <wave equations>. Physical <electric fields> and <magnetic fields> are the <real parts> of the complex expressions.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

Using $\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf B$ directly with the specified <plane wave> gives
$$
-ik\hat{\mathbf x}\times\mathbf E_0=-i\omega\mathbf B_0,
\qquad
\boxed{\mathbf B_0=\frac{E_0}{c}\hat{\mathbf z}.}
$$
The <electric field>, <magnetic flux density> and propagation direction form a right-handed triad, and for a nonzero <wave amplitude>
$$
\boxed{\frac{|\mathbf E_0|}{|\mathbf B_0|}=c.}
$$
The <electric field> and <magnetic field> have the same <wave phase> in vacuum.

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

The distance from the origin to the incident straight <light ray> is $d_a$ (taking $d_a\geq0$). At its intersection with the sphere, let $i$ and $t$ be the <angle of incidence> and <angle of refraction>, measured from the radial <normal vector>. Elementary <right triangle> geometry gives $d_a=r_b\sin i$. By <Snell's law>,
$$
n_a\sin i=n_b\sin t.
$$
The refracted straight <light ray> inside the sphere has distance $d_b=r_b\sin t$ from the origin. Therefore
$$
\boxed{d_b=\frac{n_a}{n_b}d_a,\qquad r\cos(\theta-\theta_b)=\frac{n_a}{n_b}d_a.}
$$
The angle $\theta_b$ describes the <normal vector> of the new line; it need not equal the propagation angle. The existence of a crossing requires $|d_a|<r_b$. A signed convention for $d_a$ gives the same line equation after choosing the orientation of its <normal vector> consistently.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

Approximate the atmosphere by concentric spherical layers of <refractive index>. At every boundary, <Snell's law> conserves $nr\sin\psi$, where $\psi$ is the angle between the <light ray> and the radius. Straight propagation between boundaries conserves the same quantity because $r\sin\psi$ is the distance of that line from the origin. In the smooth limit this is the <spherical ray invariant>
$$
p=n(r)r\sin\psi.
$$
At the surface, a <light ray> apparently on the horizon is tangent, so $p=n_sR$. In vacuum far from the Earth, $n=1$, and the straight incident line has distance $p$ from the centre. Removing the atmosphere while retaining that incident line gives minimum altitude
$$
\boxed{H=p-R=(n_s-1)R=0.0003(6400\,\mathrm{km})=1.92\,\mathrm{km}.}
$$
The detailed variation of <refractive index> with altitude changes the local curvature of the <light ray>, but not the conserved $p$. This conclusion assumes a spherically stratified atmosphere and a <light ray> which actually connects the surface to space; nonspherical structure or a trapping layer would require separate analysis.

== 7
{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

Take coordinate $y$ across the slit and observation angle $\theta$ from its <normal vector>. Under <Fraunhofer diffraction>, the relative <optical path difference> from $y$ is $y\sin\theta$. Uniform coherent illumination makes the <complex amplitude> proportional to the aperture <integral>:
$$
\mathcal E(\theta)=K\int_{-w/2}^{w/2}e^{-iky\sin\theta}\,dy
=Kw\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta},\qquad
\beta=\frac{\pi w\sin\theta}{\lambda},\quad k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}.
$$
The <light intensity> is proportional to $|\mathcal E|^2$, so
$$
\boxed{I(\theta)=I(0)\left[\frac{\sin(\pi w\sin\theta/\lambda)}{\pi w\sin\theta/\lambda}\right]^2.}
$$
The central value is the continuous limit of the <sinc function>. The first minima satisfy $\sin\theta=\pm\lambda/w$, if $\lambda\leq w$. Infinite slit height removes spreading in the other transverse direction; this expression describes the slit-width direction.

Strictly, distance $D\gg w$ alone does not ensure <Fraunhofer diffraction>: one also needs $w^2/(\lambda D)\ll1$, so that quadratic aperture-dependent <wave phase> is negligible, or observation in a suitable <focal plane>. For example, $w=1\,\mathrm{mm}$, $\lambda=500\,\mathrm{nm}$ and $D=1\,\mathrm m$ have $D/w=1000$ but $w^2/(\lambda D)=2$; the <Fresnel diffraction> correction is then substantial. The displayed result uses the intended far-field regime of the question and the usual scalar aperture approximation.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

For viewing near the nadir, the required <angular resolution> is
$$
\delta\theta\simeq\frac{s}{H}=\frac{100}{840000}=1.19048\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{rad}.
$$
Using the <Rayleigh criterion> with the first minimum of the <single-slit diffraction> pattern gives $\delta\theta\simeq\lambda/w$. Thus the minimum collecting width in that model is
$$
\boxed{w_{\min}=\frac{\lambda H}{s}=\frac{12\times10^{-6}\times840000}{100}\,\mathrm m=0.1008\,\mathrm m\simeq10.1\,\mathrm{cm}.}
$$
The numerical coefficient depends on the aperture shape and the chosen resolution criterion. If the mirror has an unobstructed circular aperture, the <Airy disk> version of the <Rayleigh criterion> is $\delta\theta=1.22\lambda/D$, giving $D_{\min}=0.12298\,\mathrm m\simeq12.3\,\mathrm{cm}$. The question does not specify mirror shape; $10.1\,\mathrm{cm}$ follows the slit-width model of part (i).

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the $e^{i\omega t}$ <phasor> convention and interpret <impedance matching> as conjugate matching for the <maximum power transfer theorem>. The load <electrical impedance> of the <series circuit> is
$$
Z_{RC}=R-\frac{i}{\omega C}.
$$
For $Z=A+i\omega B$, the condition $Z_{RC}=Z^*$ equates the <real parts> and <imaginary parts>:
$$
\boxed{R=A,\qquad \omega^2BC=1,\qquad \omega=\frac1{\sqrt{BC}}.}
$$
These formulas require $B>0$, $C>0$ and a passive realization with $A\geq0$, evaluated at the matching <angular frequency>; if $A,B$ depend on $\omega$, the equation for $\omega$ is implicit.

There is a convention ambiguity in the word “matched”. Literal equality $Z_{RC}=Z$, as required for equal bridge arms in part (b), instead gives $R=A$ and $\omega^2BC=-1$, requiring $B<0$. The inductive cases below use conjugate matching; literal equality is impossible for either passive inductive configuration at a finite positive <angular frequency> because its <electrical reactance> is positive while that of the <series RC impedance> is negative.

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

Let the unknown <series circuit> contain <electrical resistance> $R_s>0$ and <inductance> $L>0$. Its <electrical impedance> is $Z=R_s+i\omega L$. Thus $A=R_s$, $B=L$, and conjugate <impedance matching> is possible with
$$
\boxed{R=R_s,\qquad \omega=\frac1{\sqrt{LC}}.}
$$
At this <angular frequency>, the <inductor> and load <capacitor> have opposite <electrical reactances> of equal magnitude. Literal equality of their separate <electrical impedances> is impossible; for example, $R_s=L=C=1$ gives $1+i\omega$ versus $R-i/\omega$ for every $\omega>0$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

For <electrical resistance> $R_p>0$ in a <parallel circuit> with <inductance> $L>0$, add <electrical admittances> and invert:
$$
Z=\left(\frac1{R_p}+\frac1{i\omega L}\right)^{-1}
=\frac{R_p\omega^2L^2}{R_p^2+\omega^2L^2}
+i\omega\frac{R_p^2L}{R_p^2+\omega^2L^2}.
$$
Conjugate <impedance matching> to the <series RC impedance> requires
$$
\frac1{\omega C}=\frac{\omega R_p^2L}{R_p^2+\omega^2L^2},
\qquad R_p^2=\omega^2L(CR_p^2-L).
$$
It is possible at a finite positive <angular frequency> exactly when $CR_p^2>L$, in which case
$$
\boxed{\omega=\frac{R_p}{\sqrt{L(CR_p^2-L)}},\qquad R=\frac{L}{CR_p}.}
$$
The expression for $R$ follows by substituting this $\omega^2$ into the <real part> of $Z$. If $CR_p^2\leq L$, no such finite matching <angular frequency> exists. Under literal equality, both passive inductive configurations fail by the sign of their <imaginary parts>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Let the source's top terminal have <voltage> <phasor> $V_0$ and its bottom terminal zero. Denote the left and right junctions by $L$ and $R$. The two left <resistors> are equal, so at balance $V_L=V_0/2$. Zero <electric current> through the <ammeter> requires $V_R=V_L$, and therefore equal upper and lower right-arm <electrical impedances>:
$$
Z=\frac{R_x}{1+i\omega R_xC_x}=W,\qquad
W=R_v+\frac1{i\omega C}=R_v-\frac{i}{\omega C}.
$$
Write $x=\omega R_xC_x$. Separating <real parts> and <imaginary parts> gives
$$
R_v=\frac{R_x}{1+x^2},\qquad
\frac1{\omega C}=\frac{\omega R_x^2C_x}{1+x^2}.
$$
The second equation implies $1+x^2=\omega^2R_x^2CC_x$. Subtract $x^2=\omega^2R_x^2C_x^2$ to obtain
$$
\boxed{\omega_0=\frac1{R_x\sqrt{C_x(C-C_x)}},\qquad
\frac{C_x}{C}=1-\frac{R_v}{R_x}.}
$$
A finite balance with positive components requires $0<C_x<C$, and then $0<R_v<R_x$. This <equal-ratio RC bridge> uses equality of arm <electrical impedances>, independent of the conjugate-matching convention in part (a).

**Detector convention.** The stated negligible-<electrical impedance> <ammeter> is an ideal <short circuit>, whose actual terminal <voltage> is zero at every <angular frequency>. The nontrivial bridge output usually intended is the *open-circuit* junction difference, obtained with the detector removed or replaced by a high-<input impedance> <voltmeter>. Define its polarity by
$$
V_{\rm oc}=V_R-V_L
=V_0\left(\frac{W}{Z+W}-\frac12\right)
=\frac{V_0(W-Z)}{2(W+Z)}.
$$
For a detector of finite <electrical impedance> $Z_A$, its terminal <voltage> is instead
$$
V_A=V_{\rm oc}\frac{Z_A}{Z_A+Z_{\rm th}},\qquad
Z_{\rm th}=\frac R2+\frac{ZW}{Z+W}.
$$
Here $Z_{\rm th}$ is the <Thévenin equivalent> <electrical impedance>, found by shorting the ideal source. This exhibits why the ideal-<ammeter> limit $Z_A\to0$ has zero terminal <voltage> even when the bridge is unbalanced.

==== i
{scope}

===== Solution

At zero <angular frequency>, the lower right <capacitor> is an <open circuit>, while the upper right <electrical impedance> tends to $R_x$. With the detector removed, no <electric current> flows down that branch, so $V_R=V_0$ and $V_L=V_0/2$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{V_{\rm oc}(0)=\frac{V_0}{2},\qquad V_A(0)=0\ \text{for an ideal ammeter}.}
$$
For the connected ideal <ammeter>, the resulting <electric current> from right to left is $V_0/(R+2R_x)$, despite its zero terminal <voltage>. This follows from $I_A=V_{\rm oc}/Z_{\rm th}$ with $Z_{\rm th}=R/2+R_x$.

==== ii
{scope}

===== Solution

At the balance <angular frequency>, $Z=W$ and the two <voltage dividers> have the same ratio. Thus
$$
\boxed{V_{\rm oc}(\omega_0)=0,\qquad V_A(\omega_0)=0,\qquad I_A(\omega_0)=0.}
$$
This zero detector <electric current> is the operational condition used to find $R_v$ and $\omega_0$.

==== iii
{scope}

===== Solution

As $\omega\to\infty$, both <capacitors> approach <short circuits>. Thus $Z\to0$, $W\to R_v>0$, and the unloaded right junction tends to the source's top-terminal <voltage>. Hence
$$
\boxed{\lim_{\omega\to\infty}V_{\rm oc}=\frac{V_0}{2},\qquad V_A=0\ \text{for an ideal ammeter}.}
$$
The connected ideal <ammeter> carries limiting <electric current> $V_0/R$ from right to left because $Z_{\rm th}\to R/2$. Reversing the output polarity reverses both nonzero open-circuit answers, without changing their magnitudes.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Set the grounded plane at $z=0$ and the real <point charge> at $(0,0,h)$. The <image charge> $-q$ at $(0,0,-h)$ makes the <electrostatic potential> vanish throughout the plane. The <uniqueness theorem for the electrostatic potential> then identifies the physical solution in $z>0$:
$$
\Phi(a,z)=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac1{\sqrt{a^2+(z-h)^2}}-\frac1{\sqrt{a^2+(z+h)^2}}\right].
$$
The normal <electric field> is $E_z=-\partial_z\Phi$. Differentiation gives
$$
E_z(a,z)=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac{z-h}{[a^2+(z-h)^2]^{3/2}}-\frac{z+h}{[a^2+(z+h)^2]^{3/2}}\right],
$$
and therefore
$$
\boxed{E_z(a,0^+)=-\frac{q}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{h}{(a^2+h^2)^{3/2}}.}
$$
For $q>0$ the <electric field> points into the <electrical conductor>, consistently with negative induced <surface charge density>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Apply the <superposition principle> to the result of part (a). If $F(a,h)=-qh/[2\pi\epsilon_0(a^2+h^2)^{3/2}]$, then the exact normal <electric field> from the two real <point charges> and their <image charges> is
$$
E_z=F(a,h)-F(a,h+d)
=\frac{q}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac{h+d}{[a^2+(h+d)^2]^{3/2}}-\frac{h}{(a^2+h^2)^{3/2}}\right].
$$
For $d\ll h$, <Taylor's theorem> gives $E_z=-d\partial_hF+O(d^2)$. Since
$$
\frac{d}{dh}\frac{h}{(a^2+h^2)^{3/2}}
=\frac{a^2-2h^2}{(a^2+h^2)^{5/2}},
$$
the leading <electric dipole> result is
$$
\boxed{E_z(a,0^+)=\frac{qd}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{a^2-2h^2}{(a^2+h^2)^{5/2}}+O\!\left(\frac{qd^2}{\epsilon_0h^4}\right).}
$$
The <electric dipole moment> is $\mathbf p=-qd\hat{\mathbf z}$. The circle $a=\sqrt2h$ is the zero of the leading normal <electric field> and hence of the induced <surface charge density>. Inside it, induced <surface charge density> is negative; outside it, positive. The tangential <electric field> is zero everywhere on the grounded <electrical conductor>, so the leading full <electric field> vanishes on that circle. The corresponding <dipole-induced charge on a grounded plane> integrates to zero over the whole plane: the negative central region is balanced by the positive exterior.

For finite $d$, $a=\sqrt2h$ is only the leading-order zero. With $u=h^{2/3}$ and $v=(h+d)^{2/3}$, the exact zero satisfies $a^2=uv(u+v)$, giving $a=\sqrt2(h+d/2)+O(d^2/h)$. For example, inserting any $d>0$ in the exact expression at $a=\sqrt2h$ does not give zero. This is a controlled <electric dipole> approximation, not an exact finite-separation identity.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

The <electric field> within a <conductor in electrostatic equilibrium> vanishes. The normal <electric field> boundary condition, with outward <normal vector> $+\hat{\mathbf z}$, therefore gives $\sigma=\epsilon_0E_z(0^+)$. Directly below the monopole, $a=0$:
$$
\boxed{\sigma_{\rm mono}(0)=-\frac{q}{2\pi h^2}.}
$$
For the <electric dipole>,
$$
\boxed{\sigma_{\rm dip}(0)=-\frac{qd}{\pi h^3}+O(qd^2/h^4).}
$$
The exact finite-separation answer is
$$
\sigma(0)=\frac{q}{2\pi}\left[\frac1{(h+d)^2}-\frac1{h^2}\right],
$$
whose <Taylor series> agrees with the boxed leading term. Although the real <point charges> have zero total <electric charge>, their unequal distances leave negative induced <surface charge density> immediately beneath them.

== 10
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the source orientation, anticlockwise viewed from $+z$. The position and oriented line element are
$$
\mathbf r=a(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0),\qquad
 d\mathbf l=a(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,0)\,d\theta.
$$
The <Lorentz force> on the <electric current> element is $d\mathbf F=I\,d\mathbf l\times\mathbf B$. Evaluating the <cross product> gives
$$
\boxed{d\mathbf F=Ia\bigl(B_z\cos\theta,\ B_z\sin\theta,\ -B_y\sin\theta-B_x\cos\theta\bigr)\,d\theta.}
$$
Each component integrates to zero around $0\leq\theta<2\pi$, so $\mathbf F_{\rm net}=0$. Equivalently, uniform <magnetic flux density> permits $I\oint d\mathbf l\times\mathbf B=I(\oint d\mathbf l)\times\mathbf B=0$. Uniformity is essential; a nonuniform <magnetic field> can exert a net <force> on a <magnetic dipole>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The elemental <torque> about the origin is $d\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf r\times d\mathbf F$. Substitution of part (a) gives
$$
d\boldsymbol\tau=Ia^2\bigl(-B_y\sin^2\theta-B_x\sin\theta\cos\theta,\ B_y\sin\theta\cos\theta+B_x\cos^2\theta,\ 0\bigr)\,d\theta.
$$
Only its $x$ component drives the allowed rotation about the $x$ axis. Using $\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2\theta\,d\theta=\pi$ and $\int_0^{2\pi}\sin\theta\cos\theta\,d\theta=0$, the projected <torque> is
$$
\boxed{\boldsymbol\tau_{\text{about }x}=-I\pi a^2B_y\hat{\mathbf x}.}
$$
The <magnetic dipole moment> is $\mathbf m=I\pi a^2\hat{\mathbf z}$, so the full electromagnetic <torque> is
$$
\mathbf m\times\mathbf B=I\pi a^2(-B_y\hat{\mathbf x}+B_x\hat{\mathbf y}).
$$
Its $x$ component agrees exactly. The $y$ component is a real electromagnetic <torque>, but the bearing constraining motion to the $x$ axis supplies a cancelling reaction <torque>. For an allowed infinitesimal rotation $d\boldsymbol\alpha=\hat{\mathbf x}\,d\alpha$, only $\boldsymbol\tau\cdot d\boldsymbol\alpha=\tau_xd\alpha$ does mechanical <work>.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Define positive $\alpha$ by the <right-hand rule> about $+x$, starting from the loop in the $x$-$y$ plane at $\alpha=0$. Its <magnetic dipole moment> becomes
$$
\mathbf m(\alpha)=m(0,-\sin\alpha,\cos\alpha),\qquad m=I\pi a^2.
$$
The corresponding $x$ component of <magnetic dipole torque> is $\tau_x=-m(B_z\sin\alpha+B_y\cos\alpha)$. Integrating $\tau_x=-dE/d\alpha$ gives the mechanical <magnetic dipole potential energy>
$$
E=-\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf B=m(B_y\sin\alpha-B_z\cos\alpha),
$$
where the arbitrary additive constant is chosen as zero. Set $B_\perp=\sqrt{B_y^2+B_z^2}$. For $B_\perp>0$, a positive-amplitude form is
$$
\boxed{E=E_0\cos(\alpha-\alpha_0),\qquad E_0=mB_\perp,\qquad
\cos\alpha_0=-\frac{B_z}{B_\perp},\quad \sin\alpha_0=\frac{B_y}{B_\perp}.}
$$
Equivalently, $\alpha_0=\operatorname{atan2}(B_y,-B_z)$ modulo $2\pi$. With this positive choice, minimum <potential energy> occurs at $\alpha=\alpha_0+\pi$, where the rotating <magnetic dipole moment> aligns with the projection of $\mathbf B$ into the $y$-$z$ plane. If $B_y=B_z=0$, the allowed <torque> vanishes, $E_0=0$ and $\alpha_0$ is arbitrary. Holding <electric current> constant includes exchange with its supply; differentiating only the isolated loop's stored <magnetic energy> would not give the appropriate mechanical <potential energy>.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Write $T$ for transit time and $\ell(y)=t_0-\alpha y^2$ for lens thickness to avoid using $t$ for both. In the <thin lens> approximation, the two geometrical distances to the lens plane are $\sqrt{a^2+d^2}$ and $\sqrt{b^2+d^2}$. Their sum counts the lens segment as vacuum. Replacing that segment by material of <refractive index> $n$ adds $(n-1)\ell(d)$ to the <optical path length>. Thus, to the retained thin-element accuracy,
$$
cT=\sqrt{a^2+d^2}+\sqrt{b^2+d^2}+(n-1)(t_0-\alpha d^2).
$$
Using the <Taylor expansion> $\sqrt{1+u}=1+u/2+O(u^2)$ for $d\ll a,b$ gives
$$
\boxed{T=\frac1c\left[a\left(1+\frac{d^2}{2a^2}\right)+b\left(1+\frac{d^2}{2b^2}\right)+(n-1)(t_0-\alpha d^2)\right].}
$$
The omitted geometrical terms include $O(d^4/a^3+d^4/b^3)$ in <optical path length>, together with finite-thickness corrections discarded by the <thin lens> and <paraxial approximations>. The addition is $n-1$, because the underlying distance already includes propagation across the thickness once in vacuum.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

<Fermat's principle> makes the <optical path length>, or transit time in a stationary medium, stationary under small variations of the <light ray> with fixed endpoints. It need not be a global minimum. In the result of part (a), the coefficient of $d^2$ is
$$
\frac1{2a}+\frac1{2b}-\alpha(n-1).
$$
For a family of paraxial <light rays> from the object to its image, stationarity with respect to $d$ requires this coefficient to vanish. Therefore the <thin lens> equation is
$$
\frac1a+\frac1b=2\alpha(n-1)=\frac1f.
$$
For a distant object, $a\to\infty$, and the image position $b=f$ defines the <focal length>. Hence
$$
\boxed{f=\frac1{2\alpha(n-1)}.}
$$
The axial member $d=0$ is stationary by symmetry for arbitrary $b$; requiring the neighbouring non-axial <light rays> to meet the same image is what determines $b$.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

In surrounding <refractive index> $n'$, the geometrical segments contribute $n'[\sqrt{a^2+d^2}+\sqrt{b^2+d^2}]$ to the <optical path length>, while replacement by the lens adds $(n-n')\ell(d)$. <Fermat's principle> therefore gives
$$
n'\left(\frac1a+\frac1b\right)=2\alpha(n-n').
$$
The physical <focal length> measured in the surrounding medium is
$$
\boxed{f(n')=\frac{n'}{2\alpha(n-n')}=\frac1{2\alpha(n/n'-1)}.}
$$
For $\alpha>0$ and $n>1$, increasing $n'$ from $1$ towards $n$ makes the positive <focal length> grow monotonically from its vacuum value to infinity: $df/dn'=n/[2\alpha(n-n')^2]>0$. At $n'=n$, there is no <refraction> at the boundary, so the element has zero <optical power> and no finite <optical focus>. If $n'>n$, its <optical power> changes sign and the same shape becomes diverging.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For the convex vacuum void, use internal <refractive index> $1$, external <refractive index> $n$, and the original $\alpha>0$ in the immersed <thin lens> formula:
$$
\boxed{f_{\rm void}=\frac{n}{2\alpha(1-n)}=-\frac{n}{2\alpha(n-1)}.}
$$
A concave glass <thin lens> of the same curvature magnitude in vacuum has $\alpha_{\rm concave}=-\alpha$ and therefore
$$
\boxed{f_{\rm concave}=-\frac1{2\alpha(n-1)},\qquad f_{\rm void}=n f_{\rm concave}.}
$$
Both elements diverge, but their physical <focal lengths> differ by a factor $n$ for $n>1$. For example, $n=1.5$ makes the void's <focal length> magnitude $1.5$ times that of the concave glass element. Their reduced <optical powers> $n_{\rm outside}/f$ are equal; physical <focal length> also depends on the surrounding <refractive index>.
