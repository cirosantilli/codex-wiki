== 1
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Take the <electrostatic potential energy> to vanish for infinitely separated <point charges>, and write $k_e=1/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$. The first charge costs no work to place. Bringing the second to distance $a$ costs $k_eq^2/a$; the third is distance $a$ from both existing charges and costs $2k_eq^2/a$. Thus the external quasistatic assembly work is
$$
\boxed{W=U=\frac{3q^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0a}}.
$$
Equivalently, sum over the three distinct pairs in the <equilateral triangle>. Counting both ordered pairs without a compensating factor of one half would double the answer. The positive result applies to either sign of $q$, since all three <electric charges> have the same sign.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

By symmetry, the resultant <Coulomb force> on each charge points along the line from the <centroid> to its vertex. Their <accelerations> and subsequent <speeds> are equal, so the <centroid> remains fixed and the <equilateral triangle> expands without rotating. Each charge follows a straight radial trajectory; the separation grows and the repulsive <force> decreases.

For example, if the instantaneous side is $s$ and the centroid-to-charge distance is $r=s/\sqrt3$, the two pair forces have a $60^\circ$ angle between them. Their resultant is $\sqrt3k_eq^2/s^2=k_eq^2/(\sqrt3r^2)$, along the outward radius. This confirms that the symmetric shape persists.

Assuming release from rest, nonrelativistic motion and negligible radiation losses, <energy conservation> gives
$$
\frac32mv_\infty^2=\frac{3k_eq^2}{a},\qquad\boxed{v_\infty=\sqrt{\frac{2k_eq^2}{ma}}=\sqrt{\frac{q^2}{2\pi\epsilon_0ma}}}.
$$
The three velocity vectors sum to zero, consistent with <momentum conservation>. Their magnitudes approach the displayed value as the remaining <electrostatic potential energy> tends to zero.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

The <Lorentz force> is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F=q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)}.
$$
For $\mathbf E=0$, the magnetic part is perpendicular to the <velocity>, so its instantaneous <power> $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf v$ vanishes. The <speed> and <kinetic energy> therefore remain constant. Decompose the initial <velocity> into $v_\parallel$ along the uniform <magnetic field> and $v_\perp$ perpendicular to it. The parallel component is unchanged, while the perpendicular component rotates uniformly.

For a nonrelativistic particle with $qB\ne0$, the resulting helical motion has
$$
\boxed{r_L=\frac{mv_\perp}{|q|B},\qquad\omega_c=\frac{|q|B}{m},\qquad T=\frac{2\pi m}{|q|B},\qquad h=v_\parallel T}.
$$
Here $r_L$ is the <Larmor radius>, $\omega_c$ the <cyclotron frequency> in angular units, and $h$ the axial advance per turn. Opposite signs of <electric charge> give opposite senses of rotation. Pure perpendicular motion gives a circle; pure parallel motion gives a straight line. If $q=0$ or $B=0$, there is no magnetic deflection. At relativistic <speeds>, replace $m$ in the radius and period by $\gamma m$; $\gamma$ stays constant because the magnetic <force> does no work.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Translational and reflection symmetry parallel to the slabs imply $\mathbf E=E_x(x)\hat{\mathbf x}$. The differential <Gauss's law> is $dE_x/dx=\rho/\epsilon_0$. Starting at the left boundary with $E_x(-a)=0$ gives
$$
E_x(x)=-\frac{C}{\epsilon_0a}(x+a),\qquad -a\le x\le0.
$$
There is no singular <surface charge> at $x=0$, so the normal <electric field> is continuous there: $E_x(0)=-C/\epsilon_0$. Integrating through the positive slab gives
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(x)=\begin{cases}
-\dfrac{C(x+a)}{\epsilon_0a}\hat{\mathbf x},&-a\le x\le0,\\
-\dfrac{C(b-x)}{\epsilon_0b}\hat{\mathbf x},&0\le x\le b,\\
0,&x<-a\text{ or }x>b.
\end{cases}}
$$
The second expression indeed returns to zero at $x=b$, because the two total charges per unit area are $-C$ and $+C$. For $C>0$, the <electric field> throughout the interior points from the positive slab towards the negative slab, along $-x$; reversing $C$ reverses the directions. The piecewise linear profile is a useful consequence of the uniform volume <charge density>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Define the requested <electric potential difference> as $\Delta V=V(b)-V(-a)$. Since $E_x=-dV/dx$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta V&=-\int_{-a}^{b}E_x(x)\,dx\\
&=\frac{C}{\epsilon_0a}\int_{-a}^{0}(x+a)\,dx+\frac{C}{\epsilon_0b}\int_0^b(b-x)\,dx,
\end{aligned}
$$
so
$$
\boxed{V(b)-V(-a)=\frac{C(a+b)}{2\epsilon_0}}.
$$
Geometrically, the integral is the area of two triangles under the signed <electric field> profile. For $C>0$, the right-hand plane is at higher <electric potential>. Choosing $V(-a)=0$, a continuous interior <electric potential> is $V=C(x+a)^2/(2\epsilon_0a)$ in the first slab and $V=Ca/(2\epsilon_0)+C(x-x^2/(2b))/\epsilon_0$ in the second. These expressions also verify the boundary values directly.

{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $A=\pi r^2$ and choose the rotating area normal to coincide initially with $+z$. At time $t$ its angle to the applied <magnetic field> is $\omega t$, so the external <magnetic flux> is $\Phi_B=BA\cos\omega t$. <Faraday's law> therefore gives
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E(t)=-\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}=B\pi r^2\omega\sin\omega t}.
$$
In the high-<electrical resistance> approximation, neglecting the loop's <self-inductance>, <Ohm's law> gives
$$
\boxed{I(t)=\frac{B\pi r^2\omega}{R}\sin\omega t}.
$$
The displayed signs specify positive circulation by the chosen normal; reversing the reference circulation reverses both signs. The high-<electrical resistance> assumption is quantitatively $\omega L\ll R$ if the loop has <self-inductance> $L$. Otherwise the current lags the induced <electromotive force> and must be obtained from $L\dot I+RI=\mathcal E$.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <magnetic dipole moment> is $\boldsymbol\mu=IA\hat{\mathbf n}$ and its magnetic <torque> is $\boldsymbol\tau_{\rm mag}=\boldsymbol\mu\times\mathbf B$. Its component along the rotation axis opposes the imposed rotation. For positive $\omega$, the external driving <torque> needed to keep the <angular velocity> constant is
$$
\tau_{\rm drive}(t)=IAB\sin\omega t=\frac{B^2A^2\omega}{R}\sin^2\omega t.
$$
Averaging over a complete rotation, with $\langle\sin^2\omega t\rangle=1/2$, gives
$$
\boxed{\langle\tau_{\rm drive}\rangle=\frac{B^2\pi^2r^4\omega}{2R}}.
$$
As an independent sign and dimension check, $\omega\langle\tau_{\rm drive}\rangle=R\langle I^2\rangle$. Thus the mechanical input <power> equals the mean <Joule heating>; a passive loop cannot supply a sustained driving <torque>. This average assumes the same negligible-<self-inductance> approximation as the current expression.

== 5
{scope}

=== Solution

The magnetostatic <Ampère's law>, in vacuum, is
$$
\boxed{\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=\mu_0I_{\rm enc},\qquad\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J}.
$$
The path direction and surface normal are related by the right-hand rule. Taking the <divergence> of the differential law gives $\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0$, since the <divergence> of a <curl> vanishes. However, <charge conservation> requires the <continuity equation> $\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=-\partial\rho/\partial t$, which need not vanish during charging.

Using <Gauss's law>, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\epsilon_0$, the combination $\mathbf J+\epsilon_0\partial\mathbf E/\partial t$ has zero <divergence>. Replacing $\mathbf J$ by this conserved total current density gives the <Ampère-Maxwell equation>:
$$
\boxed{\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}}.
$$
Its integral form is $\oint\mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=\mu_0[I_{\rm enc}+\epsilon_0\,d\Phi_E/dt]$. The extra term is the <displacement current>. In a charging parallel-plate <capacitor>, a surface cutting the wire encloses a conduction current $dQ/dt$, while a surface through the vacuum gap encloses no conduction current but has $\epsilon_0d\Phi_E/dt=dQ/dt$. This modification makes the magnetic circulation independent of which spanning surface is chosen. The vacuum <displacement current> represents a changing <electric field>, not literal transport of charge across the gap.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Under the <Rayleigh criterion>, two equally bright, mutually incoherent <point sources> are just resolved when the central maximum of one <diffraction pattern> lies at the first minimum of the other. Their <intensities>, rather than their electric-field amplitudes, add. For an ideal circular aperture of diameter $D$, the first dark ring of the <Airy disk> gives
$$
\boxed{\theta_R\simeq1.22\frac{\lambda}{D}}.
$$
The angle is in radians in the small-angle limit. This is a conventional resolution criterion for a specified aperture and <wavelength>; it is not an absolute prohibition on estimating a source separation by fitting noisy data under additional assumptions.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

With $D=5\ \mathrm{mm}$, the circular-aperture <diffraction limit> is $\theta_R=244\lambda$ when $\lambda$ is in metres. No <wavelength> is specified, so a unique numerical diffraction angle cannot be deduced. Taking representative visible light at $\lambda=550\ \mathrm{nm}$ gives
$$
\theta_R=1.34\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{rad}\simeq0.46\ \mathrm{arcmin}.
$$
For a roughly square receptor arrangement with surface density $n=10^5\ \mathrm{mm}^{-2}$, the characteristic receptor pitch is $s\simeq n^{-1/2}=3.16\ \mathrm{micrometres}$. With the retina $f_{\rm eye}=17\ \mathrm{mm}$ behind the lens, that pitch subtends
$$
\boxed{\theta_{\rm pitch}\simeq\frac{s}{f_{\rm eye}}=1.86\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{rad}\simeq0.64\ \mathrm{arcmin}}.
$$
This is an order-of-magnitude estimate of the point-separation scale from retinal sampling. A periodic intensity pattern needs at least two samples per period under the <Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem>, giving a corresponding period angle of about $3.72\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{rad}$ or $1.28\ \mathrm{arcmin}$. A different receptor packing changes the pitch by an order-one geometric factor; density alone does not specify a full sampling lattice or a contrast threshold.

At a $5\ \mathrm{mm}$ pupil and the chosen visible <wavelength>, the optical <diffraction limit> is somewhat finer than the receptor pitch. The supplied model is therefore not purely diffraction limited: retinal sampling is comparable to, and slightly more restrictive than, ideal aperture diffraction. Real <optical aberrations> and neural processing can further reduce resolution. Different <wavelengths> or a smaller pupil can change which effect dominates.

==== Solution

Use an <ideal operational amplifier> with its non-inverting terminal grounded, an input <resistor> $R$ and a feedback <capacitor> $C$, as in the upper circuit. Stable unsaturated <negative feedback> holds the inverting terminal at <virtual ground>, while the input current into the amplifier itself vanishes.

\Image[2016-op-amp-integrator-differentiator.png]
{title=Inverting operational-amplifier integrator and differentiator circuits}

Applying <Kirchhoff's current law> at the inverting node gives
$$
\frac{V_{\rm in}}{R}=C\frac{d(0-V_{\rm out})}{dt},\qquad\boxed{V_{\rm out}(t)=V_{\rm out}(t_0)-\frac1{RC}\int_{t_0}^{t}V_{\rm in}(t')\,dt'}.
$$
Thus the circuit is an inverting <op amp integrator>, with its integration constant set by the initial <capacitor charge>. A constant input makes the output ramp until a real amplifier reaches a supply rail. A practical low-frequency design usually adds a large feedback <resistor> to limit drift; the ideal integral relation applies where the feedback <capacitor> dominates.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Interchange the input and feedback components: put the <capacitor> $C$ between the input and the inverting terminal, and the <resistor> $R$ between the output and the inverting terminal. Keep the non-inverting terminal grounded, as in the lower circuit above. For an <ideal operational amplifier> under unsaturated <negative feedback>, the inverting node remains at <virtual ground>.

The input <capacitor current> is $I_C=C\,dV_{\rm in}/dt$. It flows through the feedback <resistor>, giving
$$
C\frac{dV_{\rm in}}{dt}=\frac{0-V_{\rm out}}R,\qquad\boxed{V_{\rm out}=-RC\frac{dV_{\rm in}}{dt}}.
$$
This is an inverting <op amp differentiator>. Its ideal gain magnitude increases with <frequency>, so it strongly amplifies high-frequency noise. Practical differentiators include components that restrict the useful band and stabilize feedback; that qualification does not change the displayed ideal low-amplitude relation within the operating band.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <method of images> replaces a conducting boundary by fictitious <point charges> placed outside the physical region being solved. Choose their positions and strengths so that the resulting <electric potential> has the required constant value on each conductor. The <uniqueness theorem for the electrostatic potential> then guarantees that this solution equals the physical solution in the allowed region, provided the other boundary conditions are also satisfied.

For a plane held at zero <electric potential>, a real charge $q$ a height $d$ above the plane has an image $-q$ the same distance below it. The pair's <electric potential> vanishes everywhere on the plane. Its tangential <electric field> consequently vanishes there, as required in <electrostatic equilibrium>. The image is a mathematical representation of the induced <surface charge>, not a physical charge placed inside the conductor. Forces on the real charge can be computed from the image field, but total physical <electrostatic energy> must account for the conductor's induced response rather than simply counting a full real-plus-image pair energy.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the standard infinite-plane boundary condition: the sheet is an <equipotential>, with no imposed uniform background <electric field> and the <electric potential> tending to zero far from the localized charges. The images of $+Q$ at $z=d$ and $-2Q$ at $z=2d$ are $-Q$ at $z=-d$ and $+2Q$ at $z=-2d$.

At the point $z=0^+$ directly below the real charges, horizontal components vanish. The first real-image pair contributes $-2k_eQ/d^2$ along $z$, while the second contributes $+2k_e(2Q)/(2d)^2=+k_eQ/d^2$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(0,0,0^+)=-\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0d^2}\hat{\mathbf z}}.
$$
For $Q>0$, the <electric field> points downwards into the sheet. The expression is the one-sided vacuum limit; the <electric field> inside the conducting material is zero. A prescribed uniform background field would be an additional boundary condition and would change this result.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Let $x\ge0$ denote the radial distance from the axis in the plane, and write $r_h=(x^2+h^2)^{1/2}$. A charge $q$ at height $h$ and its image $-q$ give a combined normal <electric field> $-2k_eqh/r_h^3$ at $z=0^+$; the tangential components cancel. Superposing the two pairs yields
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(x,0,0^+)=\frac{Qd}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac4{(x^2+4d^2)^{3/2}}-\frac1{(x^2+d^2)^{3/2}}\right]\hat{\mathbf z}}.
$$
The conductor boundary condition gives $\sigma=\epsilon_0E_z$, so
$$
\boxed{\sigma(x)=\frac{Qd}{2\pi}\left[\frac4{(x^2+4d^2)^{3/2}}-\frac1{(x^2+d^2)^{3/2}}\right]}.
$$
To integrate the induced <surface charge>, use concentric annuli of area $2\pi x\,dx$. Since $\int_0^\infty x(x^2+h^2)^{-3/2}\,dx=1/h$,
$$
Q_{\rm sheet}=Qd\left(\frac4{2d}-\frac1d\right)=\boxed{+Q}.
$$
The local <surface charge density> is negative near the origin but positive farther out; integrating only the negative patch would miss the total. The total real charge is $-Q$, so the induced $+Q$ also agrees with the sum of the two image charges. An isolated finite plate constrained to zero net charge would require a different global boundary-value problem; the formula is the infinite-plane solution just specified.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

Set the normal <electric field> to zero and cancel its nonzero overall factor:
$$
\frac4{(x^2+4d^2)^{3/2}}=\frac1{(x^2+d^2)^{3/2}}.
$$
Taking the power $2/3$ gives $4^{2/3}(x^2+d^2)=x^2+4d^2$, hence
$$
\boxed{x=d\sqrt{\frac{4-4^{2/3}}{4^{2/3}-1}}\simeq0.98686d=0.98686\ \mathrm m}.
$$
The numerical value is independent of the nonzero magnitude of $Q$. Beyond this radius the signed normal <electric field> is positive. Thus its magnitude first falls to zero, rises again and eventually decays; it is not globally monotone.

For $x\gg d$, expanding each denominator gives
$$
E_z=\frac{Qd}{2\pi\epsilon_0x^3}\left[3-\frac{45}{2}\frac{d^2}{x^2}+O\!\left(\frac{d^4}{x^4}\right)\right],\qquad\boxed{E_z\sim\frac{3Qd}{2\pi\epsilon_0x^3}>0}.
$$
This is a dipole rather than a monopole tail. The real-plus-image charge distribution has zero total charge and dipole moment $p_z=-6Qd$; in its equatorial plane a dipole produces $E_z=-k_ep_z/x^3$, confirming the coefficient and sign.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Spherical symmetry and the vacuum <Gauss's law> imply $\mathbf E=A\hat{\mathbf r}/r^2$ in the gap, where $A$ is constant. Integrating the <electric field> between the two <equipotentials> gives
$$
V_1=V(a)-V(b)=\int_a^b\frac{A}{r^2}\,dr=A\left(\frac1a-\frac1b\right).
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(r)=\frac{V_1ab}{(b-a)r^2}\hat{\mathbf r},\qquad a<r<b}.
$$
The inner charge is $Q=4\pi\epsilon_0A$, which reproduces the <spherical capacitor> <capacitance> $4\pi\epsilon_0ab/(b-a)$. The radial direction is outward for positive $V_1$ and inward for negative $V_1$; the formula represents the signed field, not just its magnitude.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

The <electric field> magnitude falls as $1/r^2$ throughout the vacuum gap, so its maximum occurs just outside the inner sphere:
$$
E_{\max}=\frac{|V_1|b}{a(b-a)}=\frac{10^3\times0.4}{0.1(0.4-0.1)}.
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{E_{\max}=1.333\times10^4\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}},\qquad r=a^+}.
$$
Inside the metal the <electric field> is zero. The one-sided gap value is the relevant value for dielectric breakdown; the ideal zero-thickness conducting boundary itself should not be assigned a single field value across its surface-charge discontinuity.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Inserting an independently biased conducting sphere makes two vacuum gaps, each with its own constant radial <electric flux>. Let $A_1$ and $A_2$ be the coefficients of $1/r^2$. The two potential drops are
$$
V_1-V_2=A_1\left(\frac1a-\frac1c\right),\qquad V_2=A_2\left(\frac1c-\frac1b\right).
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(r)=\begin{cases}
\dfrac{(V_1-V_2)ac}{(c-a)r^2}\hat{\mathbf r},&a<r<c,\\
\dfrac{V_2bc}{(b-c)r^2}\hat{\mathbf r},&c<r<b.
\end{cases}}
$$
The middle conductor can carry net <electric charge>, so $A_1$ and $A_2$ need not be equal. Its charge is $4\pi\epsilon_0(A_2-A_1)$, consistent with the jump in the normal <electric field>. For $0<V_2<V_1$ both fields point outwards; outside that interval one of the gap fields reverses direction.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

The largest field in each gap lies at that gap's smaller radius. Thus the quantity to minimize is
$$
E_{\max}(V_2)=\max\left\{\frac{c|V_1-V_2|}{a(c-a)},\frac{b|V_2|}{c(b-c)}\right\}.
$$
For positive $V_1$, projecting any $V_2$ outside $[0,V_1]$ back to that interval reduces both absolute potential drops, so the optimum lies inside it. Within the interval, one candidate maximum decreases linearly with $V_2$ and the other increases linearly. Their maximum is minimized at their intersection, not by making the potential drops equal.

With $a=0.1\ \mathrm m$, $c=0.2\ \mathrm m$, $b=0.4\ \mathrm m$ and $V_1=1000\ \mathrm V$, the two maxima are $20(1000-V_2)$ and $10V_2$ in $\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}$. Equating them gives
$$
\boxed{V_2=\frac{2000}{3}\ \mathrm V\simeq666.7\ \mathrm V,\qquad E_{\max}=\frac{20000}{3}\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\simeq6667\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}}.
$$
The maximum is halved relative to the two-sphere system and is attained at both $a^+$ and $c^+$. At $c^-$ and $b^-$ the optimized field is only about $1667\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}$; at $c$ the charged middle shell produces a jump.

For plotting, the two-sphere <electric potential> is $V^{(2)}(r)=V_1(1/r-1/b)/(1/a-1/b)$. In the three-sphere system it is
$$
V^{(3)}(r)=\begin{cases}
V_2+(V_1-V_2)\dfrac{1/r-1/c}{1/a-1/c},&a\le r\le c,\\
V_2\dfrac{1/r-1/b}{1/c-1/b},&c\le r\le b.
\end{cases}
$$
The <electric potential> remains continuous at $c$, while its radial derivative changes there. The graphs show both the continuous potentials and the one-sided gap fields; the marked endpoints identify all maxima.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Adopt positive object and real-image distances $u$ and $v$ measured from a converging <thin lens>, with the object on the left. A ray through the lens centre is undeviated in the thin-lens approximation. For object height $h$ and signed image height $h'$, similar triangles give $h'/h=-v/u$.

A second ray leaves the object parallel to the axis, reaches the lens at height $h$ and then passes through the far <focal point>. Its height at distance $v$ is $h'=h(1-v/f)$. Equating the two descriptions gives
$$
-\frac vu=1-\frac vf,\qquad\boxed{\frac1u+\frac1v=\frac1f}.
$$
The signed transverse <magnification> is $\boxed{m=h'/h=-v/u}$. Since a real image has $v>0$ for $u>0$, $m<0$: it is inverted. Its size relative to the object is $|m|=v/u$.

\Image[2016-thin-lens-real-and-virtual-rays.png]
{title=Paraxial ray construction for real inverted and virtual upright lens images}

The derivation assumes the <paraxial approximation>, a negligible lens thickness and a <focal length> defined in the surrounding medium. The ray diagrams show actual paths as solid lines and backward extrapolations as dashed lines.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

For a real image, $u>f$ and $v=fu/(u-f)>0$. The object-to-image separation is
$$
D=u+v=\frac{u^2}{u-f}.
$$
Its excess above $4f$ factors into a nonnegative expression:
$$
D-4f=\frac{u^2-4f(u-f)}{u-f}=\frac{(u-2f)^2}{u-f}\ge0.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{D_{\min}=4f,\qquad u=v=2f}.
$$
At this minimum the image is inverted and has unit absolute <magnification>. The restriction to a real object and a real image matters: the positive separation used here is not the same quantity as a signed distance to a virtual image.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

A <real image> is a point where the actual outgoing rays converge; a screen placed there can intercept the focused light. A <virtual image> is a point from which the outgoing rays appear to diverge: only their backward extensions intersect, so a screen at that apparent point does not receive a focused real image from the same rays.

For a converging <thin lens> with $f>0$ and a real object a distance $u>0$ away, the lens relation gives $v=fu/(u-f)$. If $u>f$, then $v>0$: the image is real, on the opposite side, and inverted. If $0<u<f$, then $v<0$: the image is virtual, on the same side as the object, upright and enlarged, with $m=-v/u=f/(f-u)>1$.

At $u=f$, the emergent rays are parallel and the image is at infinity; there is no finite focused image. For an object very far away, $v\to f$ from above. These are conditions for a real object in the stated lens convention, rather than claims about all systems with virtual incident objects.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

For a lens between an object and a screen, $u+v=D$ and the <thin lens> relation requires $uv=fD$. Eliminating $v$ gives the quadratic equation
$$
u^2-Du+fD=0,\qquad u_\pm=\frac{D\pm\sqrt{D^2-4fD}}2.
$$
For $D>4f$, both roots lie between $0$ and $D$, and they exchange the object and image distances. Both therefore focus the image on the same screen, with reciprocal absolute <magnifications>. The distance between lens positions is
$$
\boxed{l=|u_+-u_-|=\sqrt{D^2-4fD}=\sqrt{D(D-4f)}}.
$$
The existence qualification is essential: for $D=4f$ the two roots coincide and there is only one distinct position; for $D<4f$ there is none. For example, $D=3f$ makes the discriminant negative, providing a concrete counterexample to an unconditional assertion of two positions. This is the basis of the <Bessel lens displacement method>, with $f=(D^2-l^2)/(4D)$ when two distinct positions can be measured.

{scope}

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

Choose the <phasor> convention $V(t)=\operatorname{Re}(\widetilde V e^{i\omega t})$ and similarly for the <electric current>. In a linear sinusoidal steady state, differentiation becomes multiplication by $i\omega$, and <complex impedance> is $Z=\widetilde V/\widetilde I$. <Kirchhoff's current law> and <Kirchhoff's voltage law> then become algebraic equations that retain both amplitude and <phase> information.

For a <resistor>, $V=RI$, so $Z_R=R$. For a <capacitor>, $I=C\dot V$, while an <inductor> obeys $V=L\dot I$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{Z_R=R,\qquad Z_C=\frac1{i\omega C}=-\frac{i}{\omega C},\qquad Z_L=i\omega L}.
$$
The resistor's <electric potential difference> and current are in <phase>. The inductor's <electric potential difference> leads its current by $\pi/2$; the capacitor's current leads its <electric potential difference> by $\pi/2$. Replacing $e^{i\omega t}$ by $e^{-i\omega t}$ conjugates every impedance but leaves these physical lead-and-lag statements unchanged. The <phasor> method describes the steady state; switch-on transients must be added separately when initial conditions are relevant.

==== a
{scope}

===== Solution

A series connection has the same <electric current> through each element, so <complex impedances> add:
$$
\boxed{Z_{\rm s}=R+i\left(\omega L-\frac1{\omega C}\right)}.
$$
For $R>0$, define $\phi=\arg\widetilde V-\arg\widetilde I=\arg Z_{\rm s}$. Then
$$
\phi=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\omega L-1/(\omega C)}R\right).
$$
Above resonance, the circuit is inductive and <electric potential difference> leads current; below resonance it is capacitive and <electric potential difference> lags current. At
$$
\boxed{\omega_0=\frac1{\sqrt{LC}},\qquad f_0=\frac1{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}},
$$
the inductive and capacitive <reactances> cancel and $Z_{\rm s}=R$ is purely resistive. The same resonance condition holds for an ideal series circuit independent of the drive amplitude.

==== b
{scope}

===== Solution

A parallel connection has the same <electric potential difference> across each element, so the <electrical admittances> add. With $G=1/R$ and $B=\omega C-1/(\omega L)$,
$$
Y_{\rm p}=G+iB,\qquad\boxed{Z_{\rm p}=\left[\frac1R+i\left(\omega C-\frac1{\omega L}\right)\right]^{-1}=\frac{G-iB}{G^2+B^2}}.
$$
For a prescribed sinusoidal current, $\widetilde V=Z_{\rm p}\widetilde I$. Thus the voltage-minus-current <phase> is
$$
\phi=-\tan^{-1}\left[R\left(\omega C-\frac1{\omega L}\right)\right].
$$
Below $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$ the circuit is inductive, so <electric potential difference> leads current; above it the circuit is capacitive, so <electric potential difference> lags current. At $\omega_0$, the reactive branch currents cancel, $Y_{\rm p}=1/R$ and $\boxed{Z_{\rm p}=R}$. This is the impedance maximum for positive finite $R$, whereas the series circuit has its impedance minimum at the same <frequency>. It does not mean that either individual reactive branch current vanishes.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

The source's variable inductor and variable resistor are in **parallel**. Let their combined <complex impedance> be
$$
Z_V=\frac{i\omega L_Vr_V}{r_V+i\omega L_V}.
$$
Use the left and right source terminals as the drive nodes. The upper divider has arms $Z_V$ and $i\omega L_1$; the lower divider has arms $R_1$ and $R+i\omega L$. At bridge balance, the detector draws no <electric current> and the divider midpoint <electric potential differences> agree, so
$$
\frac{Z_V}{i\omega L_1}=\frac{R_1}{R+i\omega L},\qquad Z_V(R+i\omega L)=i\omega L_1R_1.
$$
Substitution and cancellation at a nonzero drive <frequency> give
$$
L_Vr_V(R+i\omega L)=L_1R_1(r_V+i\omega L_V).
$$
Equating real and imaginary parts yields
$$
\boxed{R=\frac{R_1L_1}{L_V},\qquad L=\frac{R_1L_1}{r_V}}.
$$
The first expression has units of resistance and the second units of <inductance>. Both are independent of the drive amplitude and, for ideal frequency-independent components, of the chosen nonzero drive <frequency>. At zero <frequency> the ideal inductors short their arms and the cancellation would not be valid as a measurement of $L$.

Two applications are precision comparison of an unknown <electrical resistance> with calibrated standards in a <Wheatstone bridge>, and detecting small resistance changes in a <strain gauge> or resistance <thermometer>. Alternating-current bridges additionally compare <capacitance>, <inductance> and dielectric losses; this particular bridge determines the two parameters of a non-ideal <inductor> from a null measurement.
