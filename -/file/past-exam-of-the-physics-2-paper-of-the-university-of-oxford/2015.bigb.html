== 1
{scope}

=== Solution

Take the <electric potential> to vanish at infinity. The <distance> from the centre to each <point charge> is half the <diagonal of a polygon> of the <square>, namely $a/\sqrt2$. The <superposition principle> for the <electric potential> therefore gives, for four charges $+q$,
$$
\boxed{V=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{4q}{a/\sqrt2}=\frac{\sqrt2 q}{\pi\epsilon_0a}.}
$$
Strictly, specifying only the *magnitude* of each <electric charge> leaves its sign undetermined. For signed charges $q_j$, the general answer is $V=\sqrt2\sum_{j=1}^4q_j/(4\pi\epsilon_0a)$. For example, two positive and two negative charges of equal magnitude give zero <electric potential> at the centre. The boxed result uses the usual interpretation that all four charges have the same positive sign.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

<Gauss's law> states that the outward <electric flux> through a closed <surface> is the enclosed <electric charge> divided by the <vacuum permittivity>:
$$
\oint_S\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf S=\frac{Q_{\rm enc}}{\epsilon_0},\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}.
$$
In <electrostatics>, the <electric field> is the negative <gradient> of the <electrostatic potential>, $\mathbf E=-\nabla V$. Substitution into the <differential equation> form of <Gauss's law> gives <Poisson's equation> $\nabla^2V=-\rho/\epsilon_0$. Consequently, in a region containing no <charge density>,
$$
\boxed{\nabla^2V=0,}
$$
which is <Laplace's equation>. Charges on the bounding <conductors> enter through the <boundary conditions>; they need not vanish for <Laplace's equation> to hold inside the empty gap.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Neglect <fringing field> near the edges of the <parallel-plate capacitor>. Put the plates at $z=0,d$, with prescribed <electric potentials> $V_A,V_B$. <Translational symmetry parallel to a plane> makes the <electrostatic potential> independent of the coordinates parallel to the plates. Thus <Laplace's equation> reduces to $d^2V/dz^2=0$. Its <general solution> is $V=A+Bz$, and the two <boundary conditions> determine both constants:
$$
\boxed{V(z)=V_A+\frac{V_B-V_A}{d}z,\qquad \mathbf E=\frac{V_A-V_B}{d}\hat{\mathbf z}.}
$$
The <electric field> is uniform and points toward decreasing <electric potential>. No plate <voltages> are specified, so a numerical <electric potential> cannot be fixed; choosing one plate as the reference only fixes the arbitrary additive constant.

== 3
{scope}

=== Solution

Use the <geometric cross section> shown in the source: the outer <cylinder> has radius $2a$ and centre $O$, and the circular cavity has radius $a$ and centre $C=a\hat{\mathbf x}$. Let the <electric current> be in the $+z$ direction. The conducting <cross-sectional area> is $3\pi a^2$, so the uniform <electric current density> is $\mathbf J=I\hat{\mathbf z}/(3\pi a^2)$.

To obtain the <exterior magnetic field of a wire with an eccentric cylindrical cavity>, apply the <superposition principle> to a full <cylinder> of <electric current density> $\mathbf J$ centred at $O$ and a second <cylinder> of <electric current density> $-\mathbf J$ centred at $C$. Their <electric currents> are $4I/3$ and $-I/3$. This reproduces the actual <electric current density> everywhere, including zero in the cavity. Outside a long circular <cylinder>, <Ampère's law> gives $\mathbf B=\mu_0 I_{\rm cyl}\hat{\mathbf z}\times\mathbf r/(2\pi r^2)$. Every point outside the outer boundary is outside both component <cylinders>. Consequently, writing $\mathbf r=x\hat{\mathbf x}+y\hat{\mathbf y}$,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B(x,y)=\frac{\mu_0I}{6\pi}\hat{\mathbf z}\times\left(\frac{4\mathbf r}{r^2}-\frac{\mathbf r-a\hat{\mathbf x}}{|\mathbf r-a\hat{\mathbf x}|^2}\right),\qquad r\geq2a.}
$$
In <Cartesian coordinates>, the same <magnetic flux density> is
$$
B_x=\frac{\mu_0I}{6\pi}\left(-\frac{4y}{x^2+y^2}+\frac{y}{(x-a)^2+y^2}\right),\qquad B_y=\frac{\mu_0I}{6\pi}\left(\frac{4x}{x^2+y^2}-\frac{x-a}{(x-a)^2+y^2}\right),\qquad B_z=0.
$$
The cavity breaks <rotational symmetry>. Thus the <magnetic flux density> is generally neither constant in magnitude nor tangential on a circle centred at $O$; replacing its <line integral> by $2\pi rB$ would be unjustified. Its <line integral> is nevertheless $\mu_0I$, as <Ampère's law> requires. At <distances> $r\gg a$, the leading term becomes $\mu_0I\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}/(2\pi r)$, with corrections of order $\mu_0Ia/r^2$.

== 4
{scope}

=== Solution

Choose the entry point as $(0,0)$, the initial <velocity> as $v\hat{\mathbf x}$, and the slit as $(d,a)$, with $a,d>0$. A <magnetic field> perpendicular to the page changes the direction of the <velocity>, while the <Lorentz force> does no <work>. For positive $q$, an upward initial <force> requires the <magnetic flux density> to point into the page. The resulting <circular motion> has centre $(0,r)$, since its initial tangent is horizontal.

The slit must lie on the <circle>, giving $d^2+(a-r)^2=r^2$ and hence $r=(d^2+a^2)/(2a)$. Equating the <centripetal force> to the magnetic part of the <Lorentz force>, $mv^2/r=|q|vB$, gives
$$
\boxed{|B|=\frac{2mva}{|q|(d^2+a^2)},\qquad \mathbf B=-\frac{2mva}{q(d^2+a^2)}\hat{\mathbf z}.}
$$
A negative <electric charge> requires the opposite direction of <magnetic flux density>. These expressions assume nonrelativistic <motion> and negligible energy loss through <electromagnetic radiation>.

For an opaque screen, reaching the slit must be the *first* encounter with $x=d$. The <velocity> there has $v_x/v=(d^2-a^2)/(d^2+a^2)$, so passage from left to right requires $a<d$, as in the drawn arrangement. At $a=d$ the <trajectory> merely touches the screen. If $a>d$, the same <circle> first meets the screen at height $d^2/a<a$, where the particle is blocked; its later passage through $(d,a)$ does not solve the opaque-screen problem. This qualification distinguishes a geometric <circle> through two points from an accessible slit <trajectory>.

== 5
{scope}

=== Solution

Take the <divergence> of the proposed <Ampère's law>. Since the <divergence of a curl> vanishes identically, it implies $\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0$. However, local <charge conservation> requires the <continuity equation>
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=-\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}.
$$
For example, <electric current> entering a charging <capacitor> accumulates <electric charge> on its plates, so the <charge density> is time dependent. The proposed equation cannot describe that process.

From <Gauss's law>, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\epsilon_0$. Thus $\nabla\cdot(\epsilon_0\partial_t\mathbf E)=\partial_t\rho$, which supplies precisely the missing <divergence>. Adding the <displacement current density> gives the <Ampère-Maxwell equation>:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf J_D=\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t},\qquad \nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\left(\mathbf J+\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t}\right).}
$$
Its <divergence> now yields the correct <continuity equation>. Through a fixed <surface>, the associated <displacement current> is $I_D=\epsilon_0\,d\Phi_E/dt$, where $\Phi_E$ is the <electric flux>. In a vacuum <capacitor> gap, this equals the conduction <electric current> charging the plates, even though no charges cross the gap. <Charge conservation> fixes the required <divergence> of the correction; by itself it does not exclude adding a further <divergence-free vector field>.

== 6
{scope}

=== Solution

Let $Q(t)$ denote the <electric charge> on the initially positive <capacitor> plate and define the discharge <electric current> by $I=-\dot Q$. <Kirchhoff's voltage law> for the <series RLC circuit> gives the <damped harmonic oscillator> equation
$$
L\ddot Q+R\dot Q+\frac{Q}{C}=0.
$$
The initial conditions are $Q(0)=Q_0$ and $\dot Q(0)=0$: the open switch initially prevents <electric current>, and finite <inductance> prevents an instantaneous jump in it. Put $\gamma=R/(2L)$ and $\omega_0^2=1/(LC)$. The two <characteristic roots> are $s=-\gamma\pm\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}$. An oscillatory <electric current> requires a nonzero imaginary part, hence
$$
\boxed{R^2<\frac{4L}{C},\quad\text{or}\quad R<2\sqrt{\frac LC},\qquad \omega_d=\sqrt{\frac1{LC}-\frac{R^2}{4L^2}}.}
$$
For positive $L,C$ and nonnegative $R$, this is the <underdamped> regime. The initial conditions give
$$
Q(t)=Q_0e^{-\gamma t}\left(\cos\omega_dt+\frac{\gamma}{\omega_d}\sin\omega_dt\right),\qquad I(t)=\frac{Q_0}{LC\omega_d}e^{-\gamma t}\sin\omega_dt.
$$
At equality there is <critical damping>, and above it there is an <overdamped> response, with no repeated changes of sign of the discharge <electric current>. A nonzero initial <electric charge> is understood; if $Q_0=0$, the stated initial conditions give no <electric current> at all.

== 7
{scope}

=== Solution

Use <Fraunhofer diffraction>, appropriate to observation in the far field or in the focal plane of a <lens>, and uniform illumination across the slit. At observation angle $\theta$, points separated by transverse coordinate $u$ acquire a <phase difference> $ku\sin\theta$, where $k=2\pi/\lambda$ is the <wavenumber>. The <superposition principle> for the coherent <wave amplitudes> gives
$$
A(\theta)\propto\int_{-w/2}^{w/2}e^{iku\sin\theta}\,du=w\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta},\qquad \beta=\frac{\pi w\sin\theta}{\lambda}.
$$
The <intensity> is proportional to the squared modulus of the <wave amplitude>, so
$$
\boxed{I(\theta)=I(0)\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2.}
$$
The limit at $\beta=0$ is one. The noncentral zeros satisfy $w\sin\theta=m\lambda$ for nonzero integers $m$ for which the angle is physically allowed. In the <Rayleigh criterion>, two mutually incoherent point images are just resolved when the central maximum of one <diffraction pattern> lies at the first minimum of the other. For <single-slit diffraction> this gives
$$
\boxed{\Delta\theta_R=\arcsin\left(\frac\lambda w\right)\simeq\frac\lambda w\quad(w\gg\lambda).}
$$
The exact statement here takes one image on the slit normal; for nearby paraxial images the <angular resolution> is $\lambda/w$. The first propagating minimum exists only for $w\geq\lambda$. The numerical factor $1.22$ belongs to a circular aperture and does not apply to this slit.

== 8
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

For a linear element in sinusoidal steady state, the <complex impedance> is the ratio of the <voltage> <phasor> to the <electric current> <phasor>, $Z=\widetilde V/\widetilde I$. Use the convention that the physical signal is the real part of its <phasor> times $e^{i\omega t}$. Then time <differentiation> becomes multiplication by $i\omega$.

For a <resistor>, <Ohm's law> gives $\widetilde V=R\widetilde I$. For a <capacitor>, $I=C\dot V$ gives $\widetilde I=i\omega C\widetilde V$. For an <inductor>, $V=L\dot I$ gives $\widetilde V=i\omega L\widetilde I$. Hence
$$
\boxed{Z_R=R,\qquad Z_C=\frac1{i\omega C}=-\frac{i}{\omega C},\qquad Z_L=i\omega L.}
$$
The imaginary part encodes the relative <phase> of <electric current> and <voltage>: <electric current> leads <voltage> in an ideal <capacitor> and lags it in an ideal <inductor>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Assume a passive linear <electrical circuit> in sinusoidal steady state, with transients absent and $V_0$ the *peak* <voltage>. Write $Z=|Z|e^{i\phi}=R+iX$. The <electric current> is $I(t)=I_0\cos(\omega t-\phi)$, with $I_0=V_0/|Z|$. Average the instantaneous <electric power> $V(t)I(t)$ over a full period. The double-frequency term averages to zero, leaving
$$
\langle P\rangle=\frac{V_0I_0}{2}\cos\phi,\qquad |Z|^2=R^2+X^2,\qquad \cos\phi=\frac R{|Z|}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\boxed{\langle P\rangle=\frac{V_0^2R}{2(R^2+X^2)}=\frac12 I_0^2R.}
$$
Equivalently, <root mean square> values give $\langle P\rangle=V_{\rm rms}I_{\rm rms}\cos\phi$. The <reactance> exchanges stored <electric field> or <magnetic field> energy with the source, whereas the real part produces <Joule heating>. A purely imaginary <complex impedance> dissipates no mean <electric power> under these ideal assumptions.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Keep the source diagram's topology: the arm from the left supply node to $X$ contains $R_0$ and $C_0$ in series; the arm from $X$ to the right supply node is $C_1$; the arm from the left node to $Y$ is $R_1$; and the arm from $Y$ to the right node contains $R$ and $C$ in parallel. Their <complex impedances> are
$$
Z_0=R_0+\frac1{i\omega C_0},\qquad Z_1=\frac1{i\omega C_1},\qquad Z_p=\left(\frac1R+i\omega C\right)^{-1}.
$$
For this <capacitor bridge with series loss and a parallel standard arm>, the detector carries no <electric current> at balance. Taking the right supply node as zero <electric potential> and the left one as the source <voltage> $V_s$, the two <voltage dividers> give $V_X=V_sZ_1/(Z_0+Z_1)$ and $V_Y=V_sZ_p/(R_1+Z_p)$. Equality of these complex <voltages> means equality at every time, rather than merely equality of their magnitudes. Cross multiplication yields $R_1Z_1=Z_0Z_p$. Thus
$$
R_0+\frac1{i\omega C_0}=R_1\frac{Z_1}{Z_p}=\frac{R_1C}{C_1}+\frac{R_1}{i\omega RC_1}.
$$
Equating real and imaginary parts gives
$$
\boxed{R_0=\frac{R_1C}{C_1},\qquad C_0=\frac{RC_1}{R_1}.}
$$
The two adjustable standards determine the two components of the unknown <complex impedance>. Treating the lower-right arm as a series combination would give a different and incorrect balance equation for the source circuit. The result also gives the dimensionless <dielectric loss tangent> $\omega C_0R_0=\omega RC$ for the series-loss model.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

The <electric dipole moment> is $\mathbf p=2aq\hat{\mathbf z}$. By the <superposition principle>, the exact <electric potential> is
$$
V(\mathbf r)=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac1{\sqrt{r^2+a^2-2ar\cos\theta}}-\frac1{\sqrt{r^2+a^2+2ar\cos\theta}}\right].
$$
For $a/r\ll1$, the <binomial expansion> gives each reciprocal <distance> as $r^{-1}(1\pm(a/r)\cos\theta+O(a^2/r^2))$. The leading $1/r$ terms cancel because the total <electric charge> is zero. The next even terms also cancel between the two signs. Consequently
$$
V(\mathbf r)=\frac{2aq}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{\cos\theta}{r^2}+O\left(\frac{qa^3}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^4}\right),\qquad \boxed{\alpha=\frac{2aq}{4\pi\epsilon_0}=\frac{p}{4\pi\epsilon_0}.}
$$
This is the leading <electric dipole> term of the <multipole expansion>; it describes the far field, not the <electric potential> arbitrarily close to either <point charge>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Apply the negative <gradient> to the leading far-field <electric potential>. In <spherical coordinates>,
$$
E_r=-\frac{\partial V}{\partial r}=\frac{2\alpha\cos\theta}{r^3},\qquad E_\theta=-\frac1r\frac{\partial V}{\partial\theta}=\frac{\alpha\sin\theta}{r^3},\qquad E_\phi=-\frac1{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial V}{\partial\phi}=0.
$$
The <electric field> therefore has the coordinate-independent form
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E=\frac{3(\mathbf p\cdot\hat{\mathbf r})\hat{\mathbf r}-\mathbf p}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^3}.}
$$
For the <electric dipole> along $z$, use $V=\alpha z/(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}$. Direct <partial differentiation> gives the <vector components>
$$
\boxed{E_x=\frac{3\alpha xz}{r^5},\qquad E_y=\frac{3\alpha yz}{r^5},\qquad E_z=\frac{\alpha(3z^2-r^2)}{r^5},\quad r^2=x^2+y^2+z^2.}
$$
On the positive axis the <electric field> points along the <electric dipole moment>; in the equatorial plane it points oppositely, providing a useful sign check.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

First use the usual idealization of a grounded infinite <conducting plane> at $z=0$, with the real <point charge> $+q$ at $(0,0,d)$. The <method of images> replaces the metal, for purposes of calculating the exterior <electric field>, by an image charge $-q$ at $(0,0,-d)$. The exact <electric potential> in $z>0$ is
$$
V(\rho,z)=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac1{\sqrt{\rho^2+(z-d)^2}}-\frac1{\sqrt{\rho^2+(z+d)^2}}\right].
$$
It vanishes on the plane, and the <uniqueness theorem for the electrostatic potential> identifies it with the physical exterior solution. The tangential <electric field> cancels on the plane, while the normal components add:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(\rho,0^+)=-\frac{qd}{2\pi\epsilon_0(\rho^2+d^2)^{3/2}}\hat{\mathbf z}.}
$$
The <electric field> inside the <conductor> is zero, so the <electrostatic boundary condition> gives $\sigma=\epsilon_0E_z(0^+)$. Integrating the <surface charge density> over the facing plane yields
$$
Q_{\rm face}=\int_0^\infty\frac{-qd}{2\pi(\rho^2+d^2)^{3/2}}2\pi\rho\,d\rho=-qd\left[-\frac1{\sqrt{\rho^2+d^2}}\right]_0^\infty,\qquad \boxed{Q_{\rm face}=-q.}
$$
The exact image expression is essential near the charge: using the far-field <electric dipole> approximation at $\rho$ comparable with $d$ would be invalid.

**Electrical boundary condition.** The source does not specify grounding or the initial total <electric charge> of the slab. The boxed total is the grounded infinite-plane answer and the large-plane approximation to the charge on its nearby face. An initially neutral, finite, electrically isolated slab retains zero *total* <electric charge>; an approximately $-q$ accumulation near the real charge is then balanced by approximately $+q$ on its remote face and edges. This gives a concrete counterexample to interpreting $-q$ as the unconditional total charge of every large metal slab. The local image formula assumes the infinite-plane limit with no applied background <electric field>.

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

<Faraday's law of induction> states that the induced <electromotive force> around a material <electrical circuit> equals the negative rate of change of its linked <magnetic flux>:
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E=\oint_{C(t)}(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=-\frac{d}{dt}\int_{S(t)}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf S.}
$$
The <surface> orientation and direction around its boundary are related by the <right-hand rule>. <Lenz's law> supplies the physical meaning of the minus sign: the induced <electric current> produces a <magnetic field> opposing the change in linked <magnetic flux>. For a stationary contour the local form is $\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf B$; for this moving wire, the magnetic <Lorentz force> term is also needed in the <electromotive force>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Write the wire diameter as $d_w$ to distinguish it from other geometric labels. Its <cross-sectional area> is $A_w=\pi d_w^2/4$, and the total wire length is $4a$. From the <electrical resistivity>, its <electrical resistance> is
$$
R=\frac{4a\rho_c}{A_w}=\frac{16\rho_ca}{\pi d_w^2}.
$$
While the loop straddles the boundary, let $h$ be the height of each vertical side remaining in the <magnetic field>. The linked <magnetic flux> has magnitude $Bah$, with $\dot h=-v$. <Faraday's law> therefore gives $|\mathcal E|=Bav$. Neglecting the loop's <self-inductance>, <Ohm's law> gives
$$
\boxed{I=\frac{Bav}{R}=\frac{\pi d_w^2Bv}{16\rho_c}.}
$$
The into-page <magnetic flux> is decreasing, so <Lenz's law> requires a **clockwise** <electric current> as viewed in the diagram. If the loop is wholly inside a uniform <magnetic field>, or wholly outside it, its <magnetic flux> is constant and this induced <electromotive force> is zero. During a rapid transient one should instead solve $L\dot I+RI=Bav$; at the assumed steady speed the same boxed relation holds.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Use $d\mathbf F=I\,d\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf B$ for the magnetic <Lorentz force> on each wire segment. Let $+x$ point right, $+y$ upward and $+z$ out of the page, so $\mathbf B=-B\hat{\mathbf z}$. With clockwise <electric current>, the four magnetic <forces> are
$$
\boxed{\mathbf F_{\rm top}=IaB\hat{\mathbf y},\quad \mathbf F_{\rm bottom}=0,\quad \mathbf F_{\rm right}=IBh\hat{\mathbf x},\quad \mathbf F_{\rm left}=-IBh\hat{\mathbf x}.}
$$
The upper side carries <electric current> to the right and experiences an upward <force>, opposing the fall. The bottom side lies outside the <magnetic field> and has no magnetic <force>. On the right side the <electric current> is downward and the <force> is outward to the right; on the left it is upward and the <force> is outward to the left. Each side's in-field vertical length is $h$, so these horizontal <forces> cancel in the net <force> on the loop.

The wire's total <mass> is $M=4a\rho_mA_w$. Uniform wire gives each side a downward <weight> $Mg/4$. Internal mechanical <forces> at the corners transmit the unequal loads and maintain the assumed square shape; they cancel for the loop as a whole. The total external vertical <force> is therefore $Mg-IaB$ downward.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

At <terminal velocity>, the upward magnetic <force> balances the <weight>. Substitute the <electric current> from the preceding solution:
$$
Mg=IaB=\frac{B^2a^2}{R}v_s,\qquad M=\rho_m\pi a d_w^2,\qquad R=\frac{16\rho_ca}{\pi d_w^2}.
$$
The geometric factors cancel, giving
$$
\boxed{v_s=\frac{MgR}{B^2a^2}=\frac{16\rho_m\rho_cg}{B^2}=2.39\times10^{-2}\ {\rm m\,s^{-1}}.}
$$
Here $g=9.81\ {\rm m\,s^{-2}}$, $B=1\ {\rm T}$, $\rho_m=8960\ {\rm kg\,m^{-3}}$ and $\rho_c=1.7\times10^{-8}\ {\rm \Omega\,m}$. As numerical checks, the specified $a=0.08\ {\rm m}$ and $d_w=0.001\ {\rm m}$ give $M=2.252\times10^{-3}\ {\rm kg}$, $R=6.926\times10^{-3}\ {\rm \Omega}$ and $I_s=0.276\ {\rm A}$.

The gravitational <power> lost, $Mgv_s$, equals the <Joule heating> $I_s^2R$, consistent with <energy conservation>. This <terminal velocity> applies only while the top arm remains in the <magnetic field>; after it exits, the magnetic braking ceases, apart from any brief <inductive transient>.

== 11
{scope}

=== a
{scope}

==== Solution

Follow the branch of a <light ray> that undergoes one internal <specular reflection> in the spherical drop. At entry, <refraction> turns its direction by $\alpha-\beta$. The internal <specular reflection> turns it by $\pi-2\beta$, because the radius is the <normal vector> and the incidence and reflection angles are both $\beta$. At exit the second <refraction> contributes another $\alpha-\beta$. Thus the total <deviation angle> is
$$
\boxed{\delta=2(\alpha-\beta)+(\pi-2\beta)=\pi+2\alpha-4\beta.}
$$
<Snell's law> at entry gives $\sin\alpha=n\sin\beta$, taking the surrounding air's <refractive index> as one. The same internal angle at successive encounters follows from the <isosceles triangles> formed by each chord and the two radii. Internal <specular reflection> here need not be <total internal reflection>; the transmitted branch at that encounter does not belong to the one-reflection <trajectory>.

=== b
{scope}

==== Solution

Regard the <refraction angle> as a function of the <angle of incidence>, $\beta=\arcsin(\sin\alpha/n)$. <Implicit differentiation> of <Snell's law> gives $\beta'=\cos\alpha/(n\cos\beta)$. Differentiating the <deviation angle> gives $\delta'=2-4\beta'$. An interior <stationary point> therefore satisfies
$$
\boxed{\cos\alpha=\frac n2\cos\beta,\qquad A=2.}
$$
The <stationary deviation of a primary rainbow ray> is a minimum: differentiating $\cos\alpha=n\cos\beta\,\beta'$ again and using <Snell's law> gives $\beta''=\tan\beta((\beta')^2-1)$. At the <stationary point>, $\beta'=1/2$, and hence $\delta''=-4\beta''=3\tan\beta>0$ for a positive interior <refraction angle>. This minimum is responsible for the concentration of emerging <light rays> near the primary <rainbow> direction.

=== c
{scope}

==== Solution

Square the stationary condition and eliminate the <angle of incidence> using <Snell's law>:
$$
1-n^2\sin^2\beta=\cos^2\alpha=\frac{n^2}{4}\cos^2\beta=\frac{n^2}{4}(1-\sin^2\beta).
$$
Rearranging yields $3n^2\sin^2\beta=4-n^2$. Taking the positive root for a physical incident <light ray>,
$$
\boxed{\sin\beta=\sqrt{\frac{4-n^2}{3n^2}}.}
$$
An interior minimum exists for $1<n<2$, which includes water at visible <wavelengths>. For illustration, $n=4/3$ gives $\beta=40.20^\circ$, $\alpha=59.39^\circ$ and $\delta_{\min}=137.97^\circ$. The corresponding angle from the antisolar direction is $180^\circ-\delta_{\min}=42.03^\circ$. At $n=2$ the stationary solution reaches the endpoint, and for $n>2$ the displayed square root is not real; the water result is not a formula for every possible <refractive index>.

=== d
{scope}

==== Solution

With the Sun behind the observer and raindrops ahead, the one-reflection branch sends many <light rays> toward directions close to the minimum <deviation angle>. Nearby values of the <angle of incidence> produce nearly the same outgoing direction there, forming a <wave caustic>. For water the resulting cone is approximately $42^\circ$ from the antisolar direction. The observer sees the intersecting drops as a circular <rainbow>, or the part of that circle visible above the ground.

<Normal dispersion> makes the <refractive index> larger for violet than for red <light>. At the minimum, increasing the <refractive index> increases the <deviation angle>, and therefore decreases the viewing angle $\pi-\delta_{\min}$. Red is on the outer edge of the primary <rainbow>, violet on the inner edge. The internal <specular reflection> is partial: with $n=4/3$, the relevant $\beta\simeq40.2^\circ$ is below the <critical angle> $\arcsin(1/n)\simeq48.6^\circ$. <Diffraction> and the finite angular size of the Sun smooth the geometrical concentration; the physical <intensity> does not actually become infinite.
