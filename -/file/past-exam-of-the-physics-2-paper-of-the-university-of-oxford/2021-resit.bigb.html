= Solution
{parent=a}

In <vacuum>, <Gauss's law> states that the outward <electric flux> through a closed <surface> is the enclosed <electric charge> divided by the <vacuum permittivity>:
$$
\oint_S\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf A=\frac{Q_{\rm enc}}{\epsilon_0}.
$$
Take opposite <electric charges> $\pm Q$ on the two <electrical conductors>, with <surface charge densities> $\pm\sigma=\pm Q/A$. A <Gaussian pillbox> crossing one isolated large sheet gives $2ES=\sigma S/\epsilon_0$, so each sheet contributes $\sigma/(2\epsilon_0)$ on either side. By the <superposition principle>, the <electric fields> add between the sheets and cancel outside. Ignoring the <fringing field of a capacitor>, the gap <electric field> is $E=\sigma/\epsilon_0$. The <electric potential difference> and <capacitance> of this <parallel-plate capacitor> are consequently
$$
V=\int_0^d E\,dx=\frac{Qd}{\epsilon_0A},\qquad
\boxed{C=\frac QV=\frac{\epsilon_0A}{d}.}
$$
The approximation requires $d$ much smaller than both transverse sheet dimensions. A uniform filling with <permittivity> $\epsilon$ replaces $\epsilon_0$ by $\epsilon$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Put <electric charge> $+Q$ on the inner <electrical conductor> and $-Q$ on the outer one. In the gap, <spherical symmetry> makes the <electric field> radial and constant on a spherical <Gaussian surface>. <Gauss's law> gives
$$
4\pi r^2E(r)=\frac Q{\epsilon_0},\qquad a<r<b.
$$
The inner-to-outer <electric potential difference> is therefore
$$
V=\int_a^b\frac Q{4\pi\epsilon_0r^2}\,dr
=\frac Q{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left(\frac1a-\frac1b\right).
$$
Thus the <capacitance> of the <spherical capacitor> is
$$
\boxed{C=4\pi\epsilon_0\frac{ab}{b-a}.}
$$
As $b\to\infty$ this gives the <capacitance of an isolated conducting sphere>, $4\pi\epsilon_0a$; for $b-a\ll a$ it approaches the <parallel-plate capacitor> result with <area> $4\pi a^2$.

Use opposite <electric charges> $\pm Q$ over a common <length> $L$, and write the inner <linear charge density> as $\lambda=Q/L$. Away from the ends, <axisymmetry> and <Gauss's law> on a coaxial cylindrical <Gaussian surface> of <length> $\ell$ give
$$
E(r)\,2\pi r\ell=\frac{\lambda\ell}{\epsilon_0},\qquad
E(r)=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0r},\qquad a<r<b.
$$
The <electric potential difference> and <capacitance> of the <coaxial cylindrical capacitor> are
$$
V=\int_a^bE(r)\,dr=\frac Q{2\pi\epsilon_0L}\log\frac ba,
\qquad\boxed{C=\frac{2\pi\epsilon_0L}{\log(b/a)}.}
$$
This neglects the <fringing field of a capacitor> at the ends, as justified when $L\gg b$.

**The <real image> is upside down.** A converging <optical lens> produces negative <transverse magnification> on the screen: a point above the <optical axis> sends a central <light ray> through the <optical lens> centre to a point below the <optical axis>. The same happens in the horizontal direction. Viewed directly from behind, the screen therefore shows a picture rotated through $180^\circ$ relative to the scene.

= Solution
{parent=b}

**The image is upright vertically when viewed down from behind, but left and right are interchanged.** To track the <image formation>, let $+x$ point forwards and $+z$ upwards. Incoming <light> travels along $-x$. The $45^\circ$ flip <plane mirror> sends it along $+z$. Before this <reflection>, the <optical lens> has inverted the scene's top into the $-z$ direction; the <law of reflection> maps that direction into $+x$ on the horizontal screen. The screen's far edge is the top of the picture for someone looking down from behind. The horizontal inversion from the <optical lens> remains, since this <plane mirror> does not change the lateral coordinate.

= Solution
{parent=c}

The minimum is **two additional <plane mirrors>**, making three including the flip <plane mirror>. Their reflecting planes can meet at $45^\circ$ in the vertical section, as in a <pentaprism>. For example, in the $xz$ section take the first added reflecting plane at $67.5^\circ$ to $+x$ and the second at $112.5^\circ$. The upward beam first travels diagonally forwards and upwards, then horizontally backwards towards an eye looking forwards. The two <reflections> together rotate directions through $90^\circ$: the propagation direction $+z$ becomes $-x$, while the screen's top direction $+x$ becomes $+z$ at the eye. Thus the picture remains upright vertically.

One additional <plane mirror> would also send $+z$ into $-x$, but it would send the top direction $+x$ into $-z$, making the image upside down. This proves minimality for ordinary <plane mirrors>. The three-<reflection> arrangement still reverses left and right; correcting that additionally requires a roof arrangement or another suitable image-erecting system. Vertical erection alone is what is asked here.

Use a converging <thin lens> as a <magnifying glass>, with the screen at or just inside its front <focal plane>. It presents an upright <virtual image> at infinity or at the eye's <near point>, respectively. Let the <near point> distance be $D$; the conventional numerical assumption is $D=250\,\mathrm{mm}$, which is not specified in the paper.

Under the usual approximation that the eye is close to the eyepiece, interpreting $50\,\mathrm{mm}$ as the <focal length> $f$ gives the familiar <angular magnifications>
$$
M_\infty=\frac Df=5,\qquad
\boxed{M_{\rm near}=1+\frac Df=6\quad(f=50\,\mathrm{mm},\ D=250\,\mathrm{mm}).}
$$
The first is for a relaxed eye with the screen at the front <focal plane>. For the second, the <virtual image> is at signed distance $v=-D$ and the <thin lens> equation gives screen distance $u=fD/(D+f)=41.7\,\mathrm{mm}$. Thus the often intended maximum $6$ requires a screen-to-eye distance of approximately $41.7\,\mathrm{mm}$, rather than exactly $50\,\mathrm{mm}$.

If $50\,\mathrm{mm}$ instead fixes the physical screen-to-eye separation $s$, an eye adjacent to the <optical lens> sees the <angular magnification>
$$
\boxed{M=\frac Ds=5\quad(s=50\,\mathrm{mm},\ D=250\,\mathrm{mm}).}
$$
This is achieved by $f=s$ for relaxed viewing, or by $f=sD/(D-s)=62.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ for a <virtual image> at the <near point>. The latter follows from $1/f=1/s-1/D$.

More generally, the source does not specify the eyepiece-to-eye distance either. If that distance is $e$, then $u=s-e$ and a <virtual image> at the <near point> has <transverse magnification> $(D-e)/u$. Its <angular magnification> relative to unaided viewing at $D$ is
$$
M=\frac{D-e}{s-e}.
$$
For example, $s=50\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $e=25\,\mathrm{mm}$ give $M=9$, with $u=25\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $f=28.125\,\mathrm{mm}$. This is a concrete counterexample to a universal maximum of $5$ at fixed screen-to-eye separation. In ideal <geometrical optics>, $e\to s^-$ allows arbitrarily large $M$ with an arbitrarily short <focal length>; practical <aperture> and <optical aberrations> constrain this. A unique numerical maximum therefore needs the conventional near-eye eyepiece geometry and <near point> assumption. The conventional $f=50\,\mathrm{mm}$ answer is $6$, with those assumptions stated explicitly.

Let $U>0$ be the drop in <electric potential> along the positive <electric charge>'s path. By <conservation of energy>, $qU=mv^2/2$, so the nonrelativistic exit <speed> is $v=\sqrt{2qU/m}$. Apply a uniform <magnetic field> perpendicular to the incident <velocity> and to the desired orbital plane. The magnetic <Lorentz force> is perpendicular to the <velocity>, does no <work>, and supplies the <centripetal force>:
$$
qvB=\frac{mv^2}{R},\qquad
\boxed{R=\frac{mv}{qB}=\frac1B\sqrt{\frac{2U}{q/m}}.}
$$
For example, $\mathbf v=v\widehat{\mathbf x}$ and $\mathbf B=B\widehat{\mathbf z}$ give initial deflection along $-y$ for positive $q$. Reversing the <magnetic field> reverses the bending. A uniform <electric field> alone would give constant <acceleration> and a parabolic path, not uniform <circular motion>. The displayed result assumes $qU\ll mc^2$; the exact relativistic <momentum> relation would give $R=\sqrt{q^2U^2/c^2+2mqU}/(qB)$.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Add a uniform <electric field> satisfying $\mathbf E=-\mathbf v_0\times\mathbf B$. Then the <Lorentz force> $q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v_0\times\mathbf B)$ vanishes for the desired <velocity>. With the axes used above, $\mathbf E=E\widehat{\mathbf y}$, perpendicular to both $\mathbf v_0$ and $\mathbf B$, with
$$
\boxed{E=Bv_0=B\sqrt{2U(q/m)_0},\qquad (q/m)_0=\frac{E^2}{2UB^2}.}
$$
The crossed fields form a <velocity selector>. All particles must first have traversed the same <electric potential difference> from rest: that is what converts selection of <speed> into selection of <charge-to-mass ratio>. Equal <charge-to-mass ratios> yield equal <speeds> even if the separate masses and charges differ. For other ratios, the initial transverse <force> is $q(E-vB)$ and is nonzero.

Use sinusoidal <phasors> with physical values $v(t)=\operatorname{Re}(\widehat V e^{j\omega t})$, $i(t)=\operatorname{Re}(\widehat I e^{j\omega t})$, and $j^2=-1$. The <complex impedance> is $Z=\widehat V/\widehat I=X+jY$: its <modulus> is the voltage-to-current <amplitude> ratio, and its <complex argument> is the voltage-minus-current <phase difference>. Here $X$ is the <electrical resistance> part and $Y$ the signed <electrical reactance>; the source's $X,Y$ notation differs from the common $R,X$ notation.

The component laws $v_R=Ri$, $v_L=L\,di/dt$ and $i_C=C\,dv_C/dt$ give the <complex impedances> of an ideal <resistor>, <inductor> and <capacitor>:
$$
Z_R=R,\qquad Z_L=j\omega L,\qquad Z_C=\frac1{j\omega C}=-\frac j{\omega C}.
$$
In a <series circuit> the same <electric current> flows through both components, so <Kirchhoff's voltage law> adds their voltage <phasors>. The resulting <series RC impedance> is
$$
Z=R-\frac j{\omega C},\qquad
\boxed{|Z|=\sqrt{R^2+\frac1{\omega^2C^2}},\qquad
\arg Z=-\arctan\frac1{\omega CR}.}
$$
For positive $R,C,\omega$, the negative <phase difference> means that <electric current> leads the applied <electric potential difference>.

{parent=4}
{scope}

= Solution
{parent=b}

In the <parallel LC circuit> both components have the same voltage <phasor>. Adding their current <phasors> by <Kirchhoff's current law> gives the total <electrical admittance>:
$$
\frac1Z=\frac1{j\omega L}+j\omega C
=j\left(\omega C-\frac1{\omega L}\right).
$$
Thus
$$
\boxed{Z=\frac{j\omega L}{1-\omega^2LC},\qquad
|Z|=\frac{\omega L}{|1-\omega^2LC|},\qquad
\arg Z=\begin{cases}+\pi/2,&\omega<1/\sqrt{LC},\\-\pi/2,&\omega>1/\sqrt{LC}.\end{cases}}
$$
At the ideal parallel <resonance>, $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$, the two branch current <phasors> cancel. The net <electrical admittance> is zero and the magnitude of the <complex impedance> tends to infinity: the parallel combination behaves as an open circuit at that <frequency>. Its <complex argument> is undefined exactly at the pole. Finite component losses replace the infinite peak by a large finite <electrical impedance>. This conclusion concerns a voltage-driven sinusoidal response; an ideal resonant current drive does not have a bounded sinusoidal steady-state voltage.

In <SI> units, the <Maxwell equations> in <vacuum> with prescribed <charge density> $\rho$ and <current density> $\mathbf J$ are
$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac\rho{\epsilon_0},\qquad
\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf B,\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t\mathbf E.
$$
When $\rho=0$ and $\mathbf J=\mathbf0$, take the <curl> of the third equation and substitute the fourth. Using the <curl of the curl identity> and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=0$ gives the <electromagnetic wave equation>:
$$
\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)=-\nabla^2\mathbf E
=-\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t^2\mathbf E,\qquad
\boxed{\nabla^2\mathbf E-\frac1{c^2}\partial_t^2\mathbf E=0,
\quad c=(\mu_0\epsilon_0)^{-1/2}.}
$$
For example, $\mathbf E=E_0\widehat{\mathbf x}\cos(kz-\omega t)$, $\mathbf B=(E_0/c)\widehat{\mathbf y}\cos(kz-\omega t)$ is a <plane electromagnetic wave> provided $\omega=ck$. The <electric field>, <magnetic field> and propagation direction are mutually perpendicular.

The <Maxwell equations> are linear, so the <superposition principle> applies to fields. However, the <Poynting vector> is quadratic:
$$
\langle\mathbf S\rangle=\frac1{\mu_0}\langle
\mathbf E_1\times\mathbf B_1+\mathbf E_2\times\mathbf B_2
+\mathbf E_1\times\mathbf B_2+\mathbf E_2\times\mathbf B_1\rangle.
$$
One may add the average <wave intensities> only when the cross terms vanish under the actual detector average. For co-propagating <plane electromagnetic waves> of parallel <linear polarization>, averaging over optical cycles but potentially resolving the difference <frequency> gives
$$
I(t)=I_1+I_2+2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\cos[(\omega_1-\omega_2)t+\phi].
$$
If the detector integrates for $T$, the last term acquires $\operatorname{sinc}[(\omega_1-\omega_2)T/2]$, with <sinc function> convention $\operatorname{sinc}x=\sin x/x$, evaluated at the midpoint phase. Thus $T\gg1/|f_1-f_2|$ suppresses <beats>, and the infinite-time average adds the <wave intensities> exactly for distinct fixed <frequencies>. Random mutually uncorrelated <wave phases> also remove the cross terms in an ensemble average; orthogonal <linear polarizations> remove them for co-propagating waves in polarization-insensitive detection. Merely having different <frequencies> does not remove a resolved <beat> signal: two nearly equal <frequencies> with fixed relative <wave phase> are a concrete counterexample. For different propagation directions, the relevant addition is of average <Poynting vectors>, or their components through the chosen detector <surface>.

= Solution

For a sinusoidal <plane electromagnetic wave> in <vacuum>, write the maximum <electric field> as $E_0$. The <magnetic field> maximum is $B_0=E_0/c$, so the cycle-averaged <Poynting vector> magnitude, or <light intensity>, is
$$
I=\frac{E_0B_0}{\mu_0}\langle\cos^2\omega t\rangle
=\frac12\epsilon_0cE_0^2,\qquad
\boxed{E_0=\sqrt{\frac{2I}{\epsilon_0c}}.}
$$
Each <photon> carries <energy> $hf$, where $h$ is the <Planck constant>. Therefore the <photon number flux density>, namely the number per unit normal <area> per unit time, is
$$
\boxed{\Phi_N=\frac I{hf}=\frac{\epsilon_0cE_0^2}{2hf}.}
$$
Multiplying by an illuminated normal <area> gives the <photon> arrival rate. Each absorbed <photon> transfers <momentum> $hf/c$ at <normal incidence>, giving the <radiation pressure on a perfectly absorbing surface>
$$
\boxed{p=\frac Ic=\frac12\epsilon_0E_0^2.}
$$
These are time-averaged quantities. At oblique <angle of incidence> $\theta$, the normal <radiation pressure> is $I\cos^2\theta/c$; the simple displayed expression assumes <normal incidence>.

The phrase “image” does not specify whether a <real image> or a <virtual image> is required. The later mention of an image plane makes a projected <real image> a natural interpretation. Let $u>0$ be object distance and $v>0$ real-image distance from a converging <thin lens>. A <transverse magnification> of magnitude $15$ gives $v=15u$, and the object-to-image separation gives $u+v=200\,\mathrm{mm}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{u=12.5\,\mathrm{mm},\quad v=187.5\,\mathrm{mm},\quad
f=\frac{uv}{u+v}=11.71875\,\mathrm{mm}.}
$$
Place the <optical lens> $12.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ beyond the object along the illuminating beam. The <real image> is inverted and has <transverse magnification> $-15$.

For the alternative single-<optical lens> <magnifying glass> interpretation, the <virtual image> is upright and lies on the object's side, so signed $v=-15u$. Its separation from the object is $|v|-u=14u=200\,\mathrm{mm}$. The <thin lens> equation then gives
$$
\boxed{u=\frac{100}{7}\,\mathrm{mm}=14.286\,\mathrm{mm},\quad
v=-\frac{1500}{7}\,\mathrm{mm},\quad
f=\frac{750}{49}\,\mathrm{mm}=15.306\,\mathrm{mm}.}
$$
The source does not explicitly select between these two geometries; they should not be conflated with a $200\,\mathrm{mm}$ lens-to-image distance.

= Solution

Use the principle of <dark-field microscopy>: block the unscattered beam where it is concentrated, while transmitting most <light scattering> at nonzero angles. All direct <light rays> of the collimated illumination are parallel, so the <thin lens> focuses them to one point in its <back focal plane>. Put a small opaque circular stop centred on that point, one <focal length> behind the <optical lens>. In the <real image> geometry above this is $11.719\,\mathrm{mm}$ behind the <optical lens>, well before the <real image> at $187.5\,\mathrm{mm}$.

A <light ray> leaving object coordinate $y$ with a small initial direction $\theta$ reaches the <optical lens> at $y+u\theta$. The <thin lens> changes its direction to $\theta-(y+u\theta)/f$. At the <back focal plane> its coordinate is therefore
$$
y_f=y+u\theta+f\left(\theta-\frac{y+u\theta}{f}\right)=f\theta.
$$
Thus direct illumination with $\theta=0$ is concentrated at the centre, whereas scattered <light> at different directions is distributed away from it, independently of $y$. A stop of <radius> $r_s$ removes approximately $|\theta|\lesssim r_s/f$; choose it just large enough to cover the direct beam's finite <diffraction> spot, illumination divergence and alignment errors. Scattered <light> inside that small angular cone is also lost, so negligible loss requires most useful scattered <light> to lie outside it. A stop in the object's <real image> plane instead removes object detail and does not generally separate scattered and direct <light>. For the <virtual image> interpretation, the same physical <back focal plane> filtering requires a downstream observation arrangement that actually passes through this plane.

The <wavelength> at $12\,\mathrm{GHz}$ is $\lambda=c/f\simeq0.0250\,\mathrm m$. For an ideal circular <aperture> of <diameter> $D=0.850\,\mathrm m$, the <Rayleigh criterion> places one source at the first minimum of the other's <Airy disk>:
$$
\boxed{\theta_R=1.22\frac\lambda D=0.03588\,\mathrm{rad}=2.056^\circ.}
$$
The customary full-ring packing estimate for the number of resolvable positions in a <geostationary orbit> is
$$
\boxed{N_{\max}\simeq\left\lfloor\frac{2\pi}{\theta_R}\right\rfloor=175.}
$$
This is an ideal angular-packing estimate. It identifies apparent angular spacing with spacing around the orbital ring and disregards Earth's occultation and station location. An individual ground station cannot see the whole ring; exact conversion from orbital longitude to sky direction requires its location and the orbital <radius>. Also, a practical tapered <antenna> illumination changes the ideal circular-<aperture> response.

A distant satellite supplies an approximately collimated incident beam. A <concave spherical mirror> of <radius of curvature> $R_c=1.5\,\mathrm m$ has paraxial <focal length> $f_m=R_c/2$. Put the receiving feed at the principal focus, on the <optical axis> in front of the dish:

Two nearby directions separated by $\theta_R$ focus at transverse separation $s\simeq f_m\theta_R$. To avoid a receiver covering both geometric image centres at once, its <diameter> $d$ should not exceed this separation along the orbital direction:
$$
\boxed{d_{\max}\sim f_m\theta_R
=(0.750)(0.03588)\,\mathrm m=26.9\,\mathrm{mm}.}
$$
This is the usual receiver-size estimate from <angular resolution>. The <Airy disk> first-minimum <diameter> is $2f_m\theta_R=53.8\,\mathrm{mm}$, but that is a spot-size definition, not the same as the receiver's allowed angular acceptance. At the <Rayleigh criterion>, the images already overlap; integrating over a finite receiver further reduces contrast. Consequently $26.9\,\mathrm{mm}$ is an order-of-resolution upper scale, not a sharp universal boundary for a specified signal discrimination probability. A detailed limit would require the receiver response and a resolving criterion.

= Solution

Use a <two-element radio interferometer>: couple two small dishes coherently and align the projected baseline along the angular separation of the neighbouring satellites. Each dish's broad <antenna> response should include both satellites. Match the electronic delays so that the desired satellite arrives in phase at the combiner. For baseline <length> $b$ perpendicular to the central line of sight, a small angular displacement $\theta$ produces geometric path difference $b\sin\theta$ and <phase difference>
$$
\phi(\theta)=\frac{2\pi b}{\lambda}\sin\theta.
$$
For equal signal <amplitudes> from one source, the output <wave intensity> is $4I_0\cos^2(\phi/2)$. Put one satellite on a maximum and its neighbour on the first null: $\phi(\theta_R)=\pi$. The smallest useful projected baseline is therefore
$$
\boxed{b=\frac{\lambda}{2\sin\theta_R}\simeq\frac{\lambda}{2\theta_R}
=0.348\,\mathrm m.}
$$
The <angles> here are measured near the normal to the baseline. At a general pointing angle $\theta_0$, replace $b$ in the small-separation estimate by $b\cos\theta_0$. Longer odd-half-fringe baselines can also null the neighbour but create more angular ambiguity. This arrangement provides discrimination along one direction; two collectors alone have repeated <interference fringes> and do not produce an unambiguous full image.

The signal <phase velocity> in the coaxial cables is $v_c=c/2$, so the guided <wavelength> is $\lambda_c=v_c/f=12.5\,\mathrm{mm}$. The excess cable <length> is $\Delta\ell=3.125\,\mathrm{mm}=\lambda_c/4$, adding a relative delay of $20.83\,\mathrm{ps}$ and a relative <wave phase> of magnitude
$$
\delta_c=2\pi\frac{\Delta\ell}{\lambda_c}=\frac\pi2.
$$
Label the two inputs so that the first configuration has total <phase difference> $\phi-\pi/2$, where $\phi=2\pi b\sin\theta/\lambda$ is the geometric <phase difference>. Swapping the unequal cables reverses that added <wave phase>, giving $\phi+\pi/2$. For the same single-source received <wave intensity> $I_0$ in each arm,
$$
\boxed{I_A(\theta)=2I_0[1+\sin\phi(\theta)],\qquad
I_B(\theta)=2I_0[1-\sin\phi(\theta)].}
$$
The two <interference> patterns are shifted by half a fringe: maxima in one are nulls in the other. At broadside $\phi=0$ both give $2I_0$, so swapping does not change the strength of every possible source. For small <angles>, the maximum-to-maximum shift is $\lambda/(2b)\simeq\theta_R=2.056^\circ$ with the chosen baseline. Near broadside, the maxima of configuration A occur near $\theta=+\theta_R/2$ and those of B near $\theta=-\theta_R/2$; the signs are exchanged if the input labels are reversed. Independent satellites contribute separately according to these directional gains; their mutually uncorrelated signals should not be added as coherent <amplitudes>.

Let the original cylinder axis be $\mathbf r=\mathbf0$, and let the hole axis be at transverse <vector> $\mathbf a$, with $|\mathbf a|=a$. The <charge density> is reproduced by a full cylinder of <radius> $R_1$ and <charge density> $+\rho$, superposed with a cylinder of <radius> $R_2$ centred at $\mathbf a$ and <charge density> $-\rho$. This uses the <superposition principle> for fixed insulating <electric charges>; the hole is not a hole in an <electrical conductor>.

For a complete uniformly charged infinite cylinder of <radius> $R$, a coaxial <Gaussian surface> of <length> $\ell$ gives, by <Gauss's law>,
$$
2\pi s\ell E(s)=\begin{cases}\rho\pi s^2\ell/\epsilon_0,&s<R,\\
\rho\pi R^2\ell/\epsilon_0,&s\geq R.
\end{cases}
$$
Consequently define
$$
\mathbf F_R(\mathbf s)=\begin{cases}\mathbf s,&|\mathbf s|\leq R,\\
R^2\mathbf s/|\mathbf s|^2,&|\mathbf s|>R.
\end{cases}
$$
The complete <electric field>, including the exterior if desired, is
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(\mathbf r)=\frac\rho{2\epsilon_0}
\left[\mathbf F_{R_1}(\mathbf r)-\mathbf F_{R_2}(\mathbf r-\mathbf a)\right].}
$$
In the insulating material, $|\mathbf r|<R_1$ and $s=|\mathbf r-\mathbf a|>R_2$, so
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E_{\rm material}(\mathbf r)=\frac\rho{2\epsilon_0}
\left[\mathbf r-\frac{R_2^2(\mathbf r-\mathbf a)}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf a|^2}\right].}
$$
Inside the hole both contributions use their interior formulas, and all dependence on $\mathbf r$ cancels:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E_{\rm hole}=\frac\rho{2\epsilon_0}[\mathbf r-(\mathbf r-\mathbf a)]
=\frac\rho{2\epsilon_0}\mathbf a.}
$$
The <electric field> in the hole is uniform, points from the original axis towards the hole axis for $\rho>0$, and vanishes for a concentric hole. The two expressions agree at the hole wall, as expected with no imposed <surface charge density> there. Its uniformity is the useful <uniform electric field in an off-axis cylindrical cavity> result.

= Solution
{parent=b}

Take the grounded <electrical conductor> to occupy $z\leq0$ and approximate the negligible-radius charged sphere by a <point charge> $Q$ at $\mathbf r_+=D\widehat{\mathbf z}$. Put a fictitious <image charge> $-Q$ at $\mathbf r_-=-D\widehat{\mathbf z}$. The constructed <electric potential> is zero on $z=0$, has the correct <point charge> singularity, and vanishes at infinity. The <uniqueness theorem for the electrostatic potential> makes it the physical <electric potential> for $z>0$:
$$
\phi(\mathbf r)=\frac Q{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\left(\frac1{|\mathbf r-D\widehat{\mathbf z}|}
-\frac1{|\mathbf r+D\widehat{\mathbf z}|}\right).
$$
Taking its negative <gradient> gives the requested <electric field> outside the small sphere:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(\mathbf r)=\frac Q{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\left[\frac{\mathbf r-D\widehat{\mathbf z}}{|\mathbf r-D\widehat{\mathbf z}|^3}
-\frac{\mathbf r+D\widehat{\mathbf z}}{|\mathbf r+D\widehat{\mathbf z}|^3}\right],\quad z>0.}
$$
On the plane at cylindrical <radius> $r$, the tangential components cancel and the normal component is
$$
E_z(r,0^+)=-\frac{QD}{2\pi\epsilon_0(r^2+D^2)^{3/2}}.
$$
The <electrostatic boundary conditions at a conductor>, with outward <unit normal> $+\widehat{\mathbf z}$ and zero interior <electric field>, give the induced <surface charge density>
$$
\boxed{\sigma(r)=\epsilon_0 E_z(r,0^+)
=-\frac{QD}{2\pi(r^2+D^2)^{3/2}}.}
$$
Finally integrate over annuli of <area> $2\pi r\,dr$:
$$
Q_{\rm ind}=\int_0^\infty\sigma(r)2\pi r\,dr
=-QD\int_0^\infty\frac{r\,dr}{(r^2+D^2)^{3/2}}
=-QD\left[-\frac1{\sqrt{r^2+D^2}}\right]_0^\infty,
\qquad\boxed{Q_{\rm ind}=-Q.}
$$
The <image charge> is a computational device, not a real charge below the plane. For a finite conducting sphere, its induced polarization would change the point-source approximation; the stated negligible <radius> is essential.

The <Huygens-Fresnel principle> treats every point of an advancing <wavefront> as a source of secondary <spherical waves>; their forward envelope constructs the next <wavefront>. Consider two points where an oblique plane <wavefront> meets a plane interface a distance $AB$ apart. After its first point reaches $A$, let time $t$ elapse until the second reaches $B$. In medium 1, the incident travel distance is $v_1t=AB\sin i$. Meanwhile, a secondary <wave> from $A$ has travelled distance $v_2t$ in medium 2. The tangent from $B$ to that secondary <wavefront> gives $v_2t=AB\sin r$. Therefore
$$
\frac{\sin i}{\sin r}=\frac{v_1}{v_2}=\frac{n_2}{n_1},
\qquad\boxed{n_1\sin i=n_2\sin r.}
$$
Here $i,r$ are the <angle of incidence> and <angle of refraction>, measured from the interface <normal vector>, and $n_j=c/v_j$ are the <refractive indices> at the common <frequency>. This is <Snell's law>.

Let $r_1,r_2$ be the internal <angle of refraction> values measured from the two face normals, and let $i,e$ be the external incidence and emergence <angles>. Geometry of an <optical prism> gives $r_1+r_2=A$ and total deviation $\delta=(i-r_1)+(e-r_2)=i+e-A$.

For an isosceles <optical prism> whose internal beam is parallel to its base, reflection symmetry gives $r_1=r_2=A/2$. Applying <Snell's law> at both faces, with air <refractive index> approximated by one, gives $\sin i=n\sin(A/2)=\sin e$. For transmitted forward rays, $i=e$, so $i=(\delta+A)/2$. Hence
$$
\boxed{n=\frac{\sin[(\delta+A)/2]}{\sin(A/2)}.}
$$
This is the symmetric <minimum deviation> geometry at a single <wavelength>. Transmission requires $n\sin(A/2)\leq1$; otherwise the proposed symmetric path cannot enter and leave through the two faces.

Because <optical dispersion> makes the <refractive index> depend on <wavelength>, no single <angle of incidence> makes every component of white <light> travel parallel to the base. \boxed{i_0=\arcsin[1.86\sin(27.5^\circ)]=59.188^\circ,\qquad

Keep this <angle of incidence> fixed for all colours. The other components have

r_1(n)=\arcsin\frac{\sin i_0}{n},\qquad
r_2(n)=A-r_1(n),\qquad
e(n)=\arcsin[n\sin r_2(n)],\qquad
\delta(n)=i_0+e(n)-A.

Substitution gives

\begin{array}{c|c|c|c}
\lambda\ (\mathrm{nm})&n&r_1&e\\\hline
370&1.94&26.277^\circ&68.800^\circ\\
535&1.86&27.500^\circ&59.188^\circ\\
700&1.83&27.990^\circ&56.210^\circ
\end{array}

\boxed{\Delta\theta=e(1.94)-e(1.83)=12.590^\circ\simeq0.21973\,\mathrm{rad}.}

The respective deviations are $72.988^\circ$, $63.376^\circ$ and $60.398^\circ$. A simple converging <optical lens> bends different <wavelengths> through different <angles> because of <optical dispersion>. For a fixed shape in air, the <lensmaker's equation> gives $1/f\propto n(\lambda)-1$: the larger short-<wavelength> <refractive index> gives a shorter <focal length>. Different colours therefore form sharp images at different axial positions, causing <longitudinal chromatic aberration>, blur and coloured fringes in a white-light image. Off the <optical axis>, colour-dependent <transverse magnification> can also give <lateral chromatic aberration>. An <achromatic doublet> combines suitable <optical glasses> and curvatures to reduce this wavelength dependence.

For a circular <current loop>, the <Biot-Savart law> is
$$
d\mathbf B=\frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\frac{d\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf s}{s^3},
$$
where $\mathbf s$ points from a <current element> to the field point. Choose the loop in the $yz$ plane, with observation point $x\widehat{\mathbf x}$. A parametrization consistent with positive axial <magnetic field> is
$$
\mathbf r'(\varphi)=(0,a\cos\varphi,a\sin\varphi),\quad
d\boldsymbol\ell=(0,-a\sin\varphi,a\cos\varphi)d\varphi,\quad
\mathbf s=(x,-a\cos\varphi,-a\sin\varphi).
$$
Then $s=\sqrt{a^2+x^2}$ and
$$
d\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf s
=(a^2,ax\cos\varphi,ax\sin\varphi)d\varphi.
$$
The transverse contributions cancel around the <circle>, leaving the <on-axis magnetic field of a circular current loop>
$$
\boxed{\mathbf B(x)=\frac{\mu_0Ia^2}{2(a^2+x^2)^{3/2}}\widehat{\mathbf x}.}
$$
The <right-hand rule> fixes its direction; reversing the <electric current> reverses the <magnetic field>. At the centre this becomes $\mu_0I/(2a)$, and far away it decays as $|x|^{-3}$, as expected for a <magnetic dipole>.

= Solution

Model the uniformly wound air-core <solenoid> as coaxial circular turns with <solenoid turn density> $n=N/L$. Put its ends at $x'=0,L$ and an internal axial point at $x\in[0,L]$. By the <superposition principle>, integrating the <on-axis magnetic field of a circular current loop> gives
$$
B(x)=\frac{\mu_0nIa^2}{2}\int_0^L\frac{dx'}{[a^2+(x-x')^2]^{3/2}}.
$$
Since $d[u/\sqrt{a^2+u^2}]/du=a^2/(a^2+u^2)^{3/2}$, this evaluates to the <on-axis magnetic field of a finite solenoid>:
$$
B(x)=\frac{\mu_0NI}{2L}\left[
\frac{x}{\sqrt{a^2+x^2}}+
\frac{L-x}{\sqrt{a^2+(L-x)^2}}\right].
$$
The source's acute endpoint <angles> satisfy $\cos\alpha=x/\sqrt{a^2+x^2}$ and $\cos\beta=(L-x)/\sqrt{a^2+(L-x)^2}$. Hence
$$
\boxed{B=\frac{\mu_0NI}{2L}(\cos\alpha+\cos\beta).}
$$
Although the paper calls this a “flux”, $B$ is <magnetic flux density>, measured in teslas. <Magnetic flux> is an <surface integral> $\Phi_B=\int\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf A$, measured in webers, and cannot be assigned to a point. For example, two differently sized small disks at the same point have the same limiting $B$ but different <magnetic fluxes>, approximately $B$ times their normal <areas>. The displayed plus sign uses the internal-point angle convention shown in the source. Outside the coil, the <integral> expression still works, but one must retain signed axial projections rather than adding two acute-angle cosines.

= Solution

At an end, $x=0$, the <on-axis magnetic field of a finite solenoid> is
$$
B_{\rm end}=\frac{\mu_0NI}{2\sqrt{a^2+L^2}}.
$$
At its centre, $x=L/2$, it is
$$
B_{\rm centre}=\frac{\mu_0NI}{2\sqrt{a^2+L^2/4}}.
$$
The ratio is therefore
$$
\boxed{\frac{B_{\rm end}}{B_{\rm centre}}
=\frac{\sqrt{a^2+L^2/4}}{\sqrt{a^2+L^2}}
=\frac12\sqrt{\frac{1+4a^2/L^2}{1+a^2/L^2}}
\longrightarrow\frac12\quad(L\gg a).}
$$
For a long <solenoid>, $B_{\rm centre}\simeq\mu_0NI/L$ and $B_{\rm end}\simeq\mu_0NI/(2L)$. For a very short <solenoid>, the ratio tends to one, providing a separate check: its centre and end become nearly coincident compared with its <radius>.
