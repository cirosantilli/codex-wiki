== 1
{scope}

=== Solution

For one wire, <Ampère's circuital law> on a concentric <circle> of radius $r$ gives
$$
2\pi r B(r)=\mu_0I,\qquad B(r)=\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}.
$$
The other wire experiences the <Lorentz force> on its <electric current>, $d\mathbf F=I\,d\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf B$. Its direction is perpendicular to this <magnetic field>, so the <force per unit length> is
$$
\boxed{\frac FL=\frac{\mu_0I^2}{2\pi r}.}
$$
The <right-hand rule> gives attraction for parallel <electric currents> in the same direction and repulsion for opposite directions.

For the upper wire, the upward magnetic <force per unit length> balances its weight per unit length, $\lambda g$, where $g$ is the <gravitational acceleration>. Therefore
$$
\boxed{r_{\mathrm{eq}}=\frac{\mu_0I^2}{2\pi\lambda g}.}
$$
This assumes the lower wire is held fixed and both wires remain straight. Within the vertical-motion model, increasing the separation weakens the upward <magnetic force>, so this <equilibrium> is vertically restoring.

== 2
{scope}

=== Solution

Interpret the coordinates as a plane in ordinary three-dimensional <electrostatics>. Set $k_e=1/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$, where $\epsilon_0$ is the <vacuum permittivity>. The work required to bring the second <point charge> in quasistatically is its <electric charge> times the first charge's <electric potential>:
$$
\boxed{U=-\frac{k_eQ^2}{d}.}
$$
The individual point-charge self-energies are excluded; this is the <electrostatic potential energy of point charges> relative to infinite separation.

By the <superposition principle>, for $\mathbf r=(x,y)$ and $\mathbf d=(d,0)$ the <electric field> is
$$
\mathbf E(\mathbf r)=k_eQ\left(\frac{\mathbf r}{|\mathbf r|^3}-\frac{\mathbf r-\mathbf d}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf d|^3}\right).
$$
At the first position both contributions point toward positive $x$, whereas at the second they oppose one another. Thus
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(d/3,0)=\frac{45k_eQ}{4d^2}\,\hat{\mathbf x},\qquad
\mathbf E(-d/3,0)=-\frac{135k_eQ}{16d^2}\,\hat{\mathbf x}.}
$$
At $(d/2,d)$ the two distances are $\sqrt5d/2$. The vertical <electric field> components cancel and the horizontal components add:
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E(d/2,d)=\frac{8k_eQ}{5\sqrt5\,d^2}\,\hat{\mathbf x}.}
$$
**Dimensional convention.** The source's description of space as two-dimensional cannot literally mean two-dimensional <electrostatics> with the same inverse-square formula. In a genuinely two-dimensional <Gauss's law>, the circumference grows as $r$, giving an <electric field> proportional to $1/r$ and a logarithmic <electric potential>. For example, integrating $1/r$ from $d$ to infinity diverges, so the same finite reference <potential energy> at infinite separation does not exist. The displayed results use the conventional three-dimensional <Coulomb's law> restricted to the stated plane.

== 3
{scope}

=== Solution

Assemble the <spherically symmetric charge distribution> by adding thin concentric shells, with the final <charge density> kept uniform. When the assembled radius is $s$, the enclosed <electric charge> and the next increment are
$$
q(s)=Q\frac{s^3}{r^3},\qquad dq=\frac{3Qs^2}{r^3}\,ds.
$$
The <electric potential> of the already assembled material at that shell is $k_eq(s)/s$. Consequently its assembly work is $dU=k_eq(s)\,dq/s$, and the <electrostatic energy of a uniformly charged solid sphere> is
$$
U=\frac{3k_eQ^2}{r^6}\int_0^r s^4\,ds,
\qquad
\boxed{U=\frac{3k_eQ^2}{5r}=\frac{3Q^2}{20\pi\epsilon_0r}.}
$$
There is no extra factor of one half in this assembly <integral>: each pair of charge elements has already been counted exactly once, when the later element was brought in. A solid uniformly charged insulator has this value; a conducting sphere has its <electric charge> on the surface and instead has <electrostatic energy> $k_eQ^2/(2r)$.

== 4
{scope}

=== Solution

For this <space-charge-filled parallel-plate capacitor>, place the first plate at $x=0$ and the second at $x=d=x_o$, ignoring <fringing field of a capacitor>. With <vacuum permittivity> between the plates and fixed <charge density> $\rho$, <Poisson's equation> and the prescribed boundary <electric potentials> give
$$
\frac{d^2V}{dx^2}=-\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0},\qquad V(0)=V_1,\quad V(d)=V_2.
$$
Integrating twice and imposing both values yields
$$
\boxed{V(x)=V_1+\frac{V_2-V_1}{d}x+\frac{\rho}{2\epsilon_0}x(d-x).}
$$
The <electric field> is minus the <gradient> of the <electric potential>, hence
$$
\boxed{E_x(x)=\frac{V_1-V_2}{d}+\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}\left(x-\frac d2\right),\qquad
|\mathbf E(x)|=\left|\frac{V_1-V_2}{d}+\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}\left(x-\frac d2\right)\right|.}
$$
Thus the <electric potential> is a parabola, concave downward for $\rho>0$ and upward for $\rho<0$. The signed <electric field> is a straight line. Its magnitude has a V-shaped corner if its zero
$$
x_* =\frac d2-\frac{\epsilon_0(V_1-V_2)}{\rho d}
$$
lies inside the gap; otherwise its magnitude is a single straight segment. An interior zero requires $|V_1-V_2|<|\rho|d^2/(2\epsilon_0)$. At that point the <electric potential> has its interior extremum. For $\rho=0$, these reduce to a linear <electric potential> and a uniform <electric field>.

{scope}

=== Solution

Let $v_C$ denote the <capacitor> <voltage> with the same polarity as the applied <voltage>. By <Kirchhoff's voltage law>, <Ohm's law> and $I=C\,dv_C/dt$,
$$
RC\frac{dv_C}{dt}+v_C=V,\qquad v_C(0)=0.
$$
Solving this first-order <differential equation> gives the <RC low-pass step response>
$$
\boxed{v_C(t)=V\left(1-e^{-t/\tau}\right),\qquad \tau=RC=0.050\,\mathrm s.}
$$
The <capacitor> <voltage> is continuous at switching because an instantaneous finite jump would require an impulsive <electric current>.

For the <square-wave response of an RC low-pass filter>, take equal positive and negative half-periods. Let $A=10\,\mathrm V$, $T=1/f$, $h=T/2$ and $\alpha=e^{-h/\tau}$. After the transient, symmetry makes the <capacitor> <voltage> alternate between $-v_p$ and $+v_p$. Over the positive half-period,
$$
v_C(t)=A-(A+v_p)e^{-t/\tau},\quad 0\leq t<h.
$$
Requiring $v_C(h)=v_p$ gives
$$
\boxed{v_p=A\frac{1-\alpha}{1+\alpha}=A\tanh\!\left(\frac{T}{4\tau}\right).}
$$
The next half-period is the sign-reversed copy. These exact expressions explain the requested sketches:

* At $1\,\mathrm{kHz}$, $h/\tau=0.01$. The <voltage> barely changes in each half-period, so the output is nearly triangular about zero, with extrema about $\pm0.0500\,\mathrm V$. Its small-signal slope is approximately $\pm A/\tau=\pm200\,\mathrm{V\,s^{-1}}$.
* At $10\,\mathrm{Hz}$, $h=\tau$. Clearly curved exponential rises and falls reach only $\pm4.621\,\mathrm V$; the output never settles to either input level.
* At $0.1\,\mathrm{Hz}$, $h=100\tau$. The output closely follows the input plateaux at $\pm10\,\mathrm V$, joined by continuous exponential transitions with <time constant> $0.050\,\mathrm s$.

=== Solution

Treat the given orbital separation as the projected separation on the sky. The small <angular separation> is
$$
\theta\simeq\frac{1.5\times10^{11}}{2\times10^{17}}=7.5\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{rad}.
$$
For an ideal circular <telescope> aperture of diameter $D$, the <Rayleigh criterion> requires $1.22\lambda/D\leq\theta$. Therefore
$$
\boxed{D\geq\frac{1.22(500\times10^{-9})}{7.5\times10^{-7}}\,\mathrm m\simeq0.81\,\mathrm m.}
$$
This is the <diffraction> limit, assuming comparable image brightness and adequate sampling. Atmospheric image blurring can require correction or space-based observing. A smaller actual projected separation of the <binary star> would require a larger aperture.

This is a real, inverted image within a sufficiently long glass rod, before any subsequent surface.

{scope}

=== Solution

<Faraday's law of induction> states that the oriented <electromotive force> around a closed material circuit is minus the rate of change of its linked <magnetic flux>:
$$
\mathcal E=\oint_{C(t)}(\mathbf E+\mathbf u\times\mathbf B)\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell
=-\frac{d}{dt}\int_{S(t)}\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf S.
$$
The surface orientation and circuit direction obey the <right-hand rule>. The minus sign expresses <Lenz's law>: the induced <electric current>, when allowed to flow, produces a <magnetic field> opposing the change of linked <magnetic flux>. For a stationary circuit this reduces to $\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=-d\Phi_B/dt$.

The source's “Lorenz force” is the <Lorentz force>, $q(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)$. A wire element of cross-section $A_c$ contains $nA_c\,d\ell$ carriers of charge $q$ with drift <velocity> $\mathbf v_d$. Adding their magnetic <forces> and using $I=nqA_cv_d$ gives
$$
\boxed{d\mathbf F=I\,d\boldsymbol\ell\times\mathbf B,\qquad
\mathbf F=I\mathbf L\times\mathbf B}
$$
for a straight wire in a uniform <magnetic field>. The length <vector> follows conventional <electric current>, irrespective of the carrier sign.

For the unlabelled upward-motion request, define $\hat{\mathbf t}=(\cos\phi,0,-\sin\phi)$ down the slope, $\hat{\mathbf b}=(0,1,0)$ across the rails and $\hat{\mathbf n}=(\sin\phi,0,\cos\phi)$ out of their plane. Choose the vertical <magnetic field> as $\mathbf B=B\hat{\mathbf z}$. An <electric current> $I$ across the bar in direction $-\hat{\mathbf b}$ gives a horizontal <magnetic force> $-IBs\hat{\mathbf x}$. The other <forces> are weight $-mg\hat{\mathbf z}$ and the rail <normal force> $N\hat{\mathbf n}$. Uniform upward <velocity> requires
$$
mg\sin\phi-IBs\cos\phi=0,\qquad
N-mg\cos\phi-IBs\sin\phi=0.
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{I=\frac{mg\tan\phi}{Bs},\qquad
F_B=\frac{mg\sin\phi}{\cos\phi},\qquad N=\frac{mg}{\cos\phi}.}
$$
Each symmetric rail supplies half of $N$. The <electric current> must make $I\mathbf L\times\mathbf B$ point horizontally toward the uphill side; for the coordinates just defined it flows along $-\hat{\mathbf b}$. Reversing the vertical <magnetic field> reverses this current direction. The external supply must maintain that <electric current> against the motional <electromotive force> during ascent. For $\cos\phi=0$, no finite horizontal <magnetic force> can support uniform motion along the vertical rails.

=== i
{scope}

==== Solution

Use the coordinates in <8>. At downhill speed $v$, the moving bar has $\mathbf v\times\mathbf B=-Bv\cos\phi\,\hat{\mathbf b}$. The <electromotive force> and induced <electric current> are therefore
$$
\boxed{\mathcal E=Bs v\cos\phi,\qquad I=\frac{Bs v\cos\phi}{R}.}
$$
Here $I$ is positive along $-\hat{\mathbf b}$ across the sliding bar, then returns through the rails and <resistor>. This direction makes the <magnetic force> oppose downhill motion, as required by <Lenz's law>. The factor $\cos\phi$ is essential: only the component of <magnetic field> normal to the rail plane threads the circuit. Rail and bar <electrical resistances> and circuit <self-inductance> are neglected relative to the stated large $R$ model.

=== ii
{scope}

==== Solution

The component of <magnetic force> along the slope has magnitude $IBs\cos\phi$. The rate at which the sliding bar does work against this magnetic braking is
$$
P_{\mathrm{brake}}=IBs v\cos\phi=I\mathcal E.
$$
By <Ohm's law>, $\mathcal E=IR$, so
$$
\boxed{P_{\mathrm{brake}}=I^2R=\frac{B^2s^2v^2\cos^2\phi}{R}.}
$$
This exactly equals the <Joule heating> in the <resistor> in the negligible-<self-inductance> approximation. The <normal force> does no work because it is perpendicular to the <velocity>.

The gravitational <power> is instead $mgv\sin\phi$. By the <work-energy theorem>,
$$
mgv\sin\phi=I^2R+\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12mv^2\right).
$$
It equals the <Joule heating> only at constant speed. For a concrete counterexample, during the initial acceleration the <kinetic energy> increases, so some gravitational <power> remains mechanical.

=== iii
{scope}

==== Solution

Project <Newton's second law> down the slope. The normal reaction has no component along the rails, while the magnetic term opposes motion:
$$
\boxed{m\frac{dv}{dt}=mg\sin\phi-IBs\cos\phi
=mg\sin\phi-\frac{B^2s^2\cos^2\phi}{R}v.}
$$
This first-order linear <differential equation> has the release-from-rest solution
$$
v(t)=\frac{mgR\sin\phi}{B^2s^2\cos^2\phi}\left(1-e^{-t/\tau_m}\right),\qquad
\tau_m=\frac{mR}{B^2s^2\cos^2\phi}.
$$
The electrical <self-inductance> has been neglected; retaining it introduces a coupled <electric current> equation rather than this instantaneous resistive response.

=== iv
{scope}

==== Solution

At the <terminal velocity>, the acceleration in <8/iii> vanishes. Thus
$$
\boxed{v_{\mathrm t}=\frac{mgR\sin\phi}{B^2s^2\cos^2\phi}.}
$$
For $0<\phi<\pi/2$ and nonzero $B$, the magnetic drag increases linearly with speed and this terminal state is stable. Horizontal rails have no gravitational drive. Vertical rails have no linked vertical <magnetic flux> change, so the model gives free fall rather than a finite <terminal velocity>.

=== Solution

In SI units, <Maxwell's equations> in <vacuum>, allowing <charge density> $\rho$ and <electric current density> $\mathbf J$, are
$$
\boxed{\begin{aligned}
\nabla\cdot\mathbf E&=\rho/\epsilon_0,&\nabla\cdot\mathbf B&=0,\\
\nabla\times\mathbf E&=-\partial_t\mathbf B,&
\nabla\times\mathbf B&=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\epsilon_0\partial_t\mathbf E.
\end{aligned}}
$$
The source-free specialization sets $\rho=0$ and $\mathbf J=0$; it cannot describe the charged objects at their locations. Applying the <divergence theorem> to the first equation gives <Gauss's law>:
$$
\oint_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf S
=\int_\Omega\nabla\cdot\mathbf E\,d^3x
=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{enc}}}{\epsilon_0}.
$$
For an isolated <point charge> $q_1$, <spherical symmetry> makes the <electric field> radial and constant in magnitude on a concentric sphere. Therefore $4\pi r^2E_r=q_1/\epsilon_0$. Multiplying by a second <point charge> gives <Coulomb's law>,
$$
\boxed{\mathbf E=\frac{q_1}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^2}\hat{\mathbf r},\qquad
\mathbf F_{2}=\frac{q_1q_2}{4\pi\epsilon_0r^2}\hat{\mathbf r}.}
$$
The <force> points away from the first charge for like signs and toward it for opposite signs.

For the <spherical capacitor>, a Gaussian sphere between the shells encloses $q$, so $E_r=q/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)$ for $a<r<b$. Integrating the <electric field> inward from the outer shell yields
$$
V(r)-V(b)=-\int_b^r\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0s^2}\,ds
=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left(\frac1r-\frac1b\right).
$$
An arbitrary constant $V(b)$ remains unless a reference is specified. The <capacitance> is determined by the potential difference:
$$
\boxed{C=\frac{q}{V(a)-V(b)}=4\pi\epsilon_0\frac{ab}{b-a}.}
$$
For the subsequent charging operation, take the initial outer shell to be earthed, or interpret the stated $V$ as the inner-to-outer <voltage> with the outer reference zero. Isolating the inner shell fixes its <electric charge> at $q=CV$. Removing the outer shell leaves an isolated conducting sphere. Its <electric field> and <electric potential>, now with zero at infinity, are
$$
\mathbf E(r)=\begin{cases}0,&r<a,\\q\hat{\mathbf r}/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2),&r>a,\end{cases}
\qquad
V(r)=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0r}\quad(r\geq a).
$$
Consequently
$$
\boxed{V_{\mathrm{surface}}=\frac{bV}{b-a},\qquad
V_{\mathrm{old\ outer\ position}}=\frac{aV}{b-a}.}
$$
These changes of <electric potential> are compatible with constant <electric charge>. Without the initial reference convention the absolute stated $V$ does not determine $q=CV$: for example, if the outer shell were initially isolated and neutral, the absolute inner <electric potential> would instead be $q/(4\pi\epsilon_0a)$.

Finally take the connected spheres to be sufficiently far apart that the long wire's <capacitance> and their mutual influence can be neglected. Their common <electric potential> requires
$$
\frac{q_a}{a}=\frac{q_{2a}}{2a},\qquad q_a+q_{2a}=q,
$$
so $q_a=q/3$ and $q_{2a}=2q/3$. The magnitudes of the surface <electric fields> are
$$
E_a=\frac{q}{12\pi\epsilon_0a^2},\qquad E_{2a}=\frac{q}{24\pi\epsilon_0a^2},\qquad
\boxed{E_a:E_{2a}=2:1.}
$$
This is the usual widely separated-sphere approximation. A wire enforces an <equipotential>, but alone does not make this ratio exact for arbitrary arrangements. As a concrete counterexample, if the second shell enclosed the first concentrically and they were connected, all net <electric charge> would reside on the exterior and the inner sphere's surface <electric field> would be zero.

== 10
{scope}

=== Solution

In the <Huygens-Fresnel principle> construction, after a time $\Delta t$ the incident disturbance advances $v_1\Delta t$ while the secondary disturbance in the second medium advances $v_2\Delta t$. The two right triangles share the segment along the interface. Hence
$$
\frac{\sin\theta_1}{\sin\theta_2}=\frac{v_1}{v_2}=\frac{n_2}{n_1},\qquad
\boxed{\theta_2=\arcsin\!\left(\frac{n_1}{n_2}\sin\theta_1\right).}
$$
Here both <angles> are measured from the <normal vector>, and $v_j=c/n_j$ are <phase velocities>. This is <Snell's law>. For $n_1>n_2$, no real transmitted <angle> exists when
$$
\boxed{\theta_1>\theta_c=\arcsin(n_2/n_1).}
$$
This is <total internal reflection>; equality is the limiting grazing geometry. The statement concerns an ideal transparent interface, not material absorption or losses at a nearby second boundary.

Let $\beta$ be the internal <angle> to the <optical axis> in the straight <step-index optical fiber>. At its flat entrance, <Snell's law> gives $\sin\theta_i=n_1\sin\beta$. At the side wall a <meridional ray> has <angle of incidence> $\pi/2-\beta$. The limiting <total internal reflection> condition is $\cos\beta=n_2/n_1$. Thus the <optical fiber acceptance angle> satisfies
$$
\boxed{\sin\theta_{i,\max}=\sqrt{n_1^2-n_2^2}\equiv\mathrm{NA},\qquad
\theta_{i,\max}=\arcsin\sqrt{n_1^2-n_2^2}.}
$$
The <numerical aperture> expression assumes the square root is at most one for air; otherwise all forward launch <angles> are admitted by this ideal ray test. Strictly confined <meridional rays> lie inside the limiting cone.

For $n_1=1.48$ and $n_2=1.46$, $\mathrm{NA}=0.242487$ and $\theta_{i,\max}=14.0334^\circ$. A <light ray> at internal <angle> $\beta$ travels distance $L/\cos\beta$ along a guide of axial length $L$. In the nondispersive approximation its <travel time> is $t=n_1L/(c\cos\beta)$. Therefore
$$
\begin{aligned}
t_{\max}&=\frac{n_1^2L}{cn_2}=5.00438\,\mu\mathrm s,\\
t_{\mathrm{half}}&=\frac{n_1L}{c\sqrt{1-\sin^2(\theta_{i,\max}/2)/n_1^2}}
=4.95365\,\mu\mathrm s,
\end{aligned}
\qquad
\boxed{\Delta t\simeq50.7\,\mathrm{ns}.}
$$
Here $L=1000\,\mathrm m$ and $c=299792458\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$; using $3.00\times10^8\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ gives $5.001\,\mu\mathrm s$ and $4.950\,\mu\mathrm s$. The halving applies to the external launch <angle>, not to $\beta$. For actual short pulses the <group refractive index> $n_{\mathrm g}$ replaces the prefactor $n_1$ in $t=n_{\mathrm g}L/(c\cos\beta)$; the source supplies no <optical dispersion> data. The difference calculated here is geometrical <intermodal dispersion> in a nondispersive <geometrical optics> model.

Bending lowers the outer-wall <angle of incidence>, allowing a previously guided <light ray> to escape into the cladding. To calculate a definite <meridional ray bend threshold>, continue the on-axis launch assumption and take a <meridional ray> in the plane of the circular bend. Its initial internal <angle> to the centreline tangent obeys $\sin\beta=\sin10^\circ/n_1$. Its straight path has distance $b_{\mathrm{ray}}=R\cos\beta$ from the bend centre. At the outer wall of radius $R+r$, elementary right-triangle geometry gives
$$
\sin i_{\mathrm{out}}=\frac{R\cos\beta}{R+r}.
$$
At the loss threshold, this equals $n_2/n_1$. Solving for the centreline radius gives
$$
\boxed{R_c=\frac{n_2r}{n_1\cos\beta-n_2}
=\frac{n_2r}{\sqrt{n_1^2-\sin^2 10^\circ}-n_2}
=14.93\,\mathrm{mm}.}
$$
For this launch, $R<R_c$ violates the <total internal reflection> condition at the outer wall; $R=R_c$ is the limiting case. A real <optical fiber> has gradual bend loss governed by its <electromagnetic wave> modes, rather than a universal abrupt cutoff.

**Launch dependence.** The final sentence does not separately specify the entry position or orientation of the bend relative to the incident <light ray>. For a planar launch displaced by $u$ from the centreline toward the outer wall, replace the numerator of $\sin i_{\mathrm{out}}$ by $(R+u)\cos\beta$. For example, a launch at the inner edge, $u=-r$, instead gives
$$
R_{c,\mathrm{inner}}=r\frac{n_1\cos\beta+n_2}{n_1\cos\beta-n_2}=29.96\,\mathrm{mm}.
$$
Thus $14.93\,\mathrm{mm}$ is the result for the stated on-axis continuation; it is not an entry-independent threshold. No missing figure is needed for these explicitly specified ray models.

== 11
{scope}

=== Solution

Use <phasors> with time dependence $e^{i\omega t}$ and <electrical impedance> $Z=\widetilde V/\widetilde I$. The element laws $V=IR$, $I=C\,dV/dt$ and $V=L\,dI/dt$ give
$$
\boxed{Z_R=R,\qquad Z_C=\frac1{i\omega C}=-\frac{i}{\omega C},\qquad Z_L=i\omega L.}
$$
A <complex number> records both <amplitude> and <phase>, turning the linear <differential equations> of a sinusoidally driven <electrical circuit> into algebraic equations. A <resistor> has in-phase <voltage> and <electric current> and dissipates average <power>. For an ideal <capacitor>, the <electric current> leads the <voltage> by $90^\circ$; for an ideal <inductor>, it lags by $90^\circ$. Their imaginary <electrical impedances> express reversible energy storage and return, with zero net average <power> over a period. Using $e^{-i\omega t}$ would conjugate the displayed reactive signs without changing predictions.

In the illustrated <Maxwell bridge>, the left and right vertices are the supply terminals and the upper and lower vertices are detector terminals. Insert a sensitive AC null detector between the latter and drive the former with a sinusoidal source. The upper-left arm is $Z_x=R_x+i\omega L_x$, the upper-right arm is $R_3$, the lower-left arm is $R_2$, and the lower-right arm is the parallel combination
$$
Z_p=\left(\frac1{R_1}+i\omega C\right)^{-1}.
$$
At a null no detector <electric current> flows, so the two voltage dividers give
$$
\frac{R_3}{Z_x+R_3}=\frac{Z_p}{R_2+Z_p},\qquad
Z_xZ_p=R_2R_3.
$$
Multiplying by the parallel-arm <electrical admittance> yields
$$
R_x+i\omega L_x=R_2R_3\left(\frac1{R_1}+i\omega C\right).
$$
Equating real and imaginary parts therefore gives
$$
\boxed{R_x=\frac{R_2R_3}{R_1},\qquad L_x=CR_2R_3.}
$$
Two independent adjustments are needed to annul the in-phase and quadrature detector signals. For example, change $R_2$ to set $L_x$ and $R_1$ to set $R_x$, iterating until the detector reads zero. Alternatively, with fixed $R_2,R_3$, independently vary $R_1$ and $C$.

The <Maxwell bridge> compares an unknown lossy <inductor> with a stable standard <capacitor>, avoiding the need for an accurately known reference <inductor>. The ideal balance equations contain no <frequency>, and a null measurement need not rely on absolute detector calibration. In practice choose a suitable <frequency> below the coil's self-resonance, where the series $R_x,L_x$ model and the standard <capacitor> remain accurate.

If the initial setting is far from balance, the bridge as drawn needs adjustable components and a detector, not merely a more precise readout. Fit resistance boxes or switched ranges for $R_1$ and a ratio arm, and a selectable standard $C$. Choose ranges around
$$
R_2R_3=\frac{L_x}{C},\qquad R_1=\frac{L_x}{CR_x}.
$$
Then approach the null with coarse adjustments followed by finer ones. A large required $R_1$ can be brought into range by increasing $C$ and decreasing $R_2R_3$ proportionally. Start with reduced excitation or reduced detector sensitivity to avoid overload, then increase sensitivity near the null. Changing <frequency> alone cannot repair an impossible component range, since the ideal balance conditions are frequency-independent. Stray <capacitance> and lead <electrical resistance> set practical accuracy limits and may require shielding and short connections.
